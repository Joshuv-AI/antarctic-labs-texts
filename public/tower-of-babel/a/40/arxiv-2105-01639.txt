Practical Verification of Quantum Properties in Quantum Approximate Optimization Runs
M. Sohaib Alam, Filip A. Wudarski, Matthew J. Reagor, James Sud, Shon Grabbe, Zhihui Wang, Mark Hodson, P. Aaron Lott, Eleanor G. Rieffel, Davide Venturelli

Abstract
In order to assess whether quantum resources can provide an advantage over classical computation, it is necessary to characterize and benchmark the non-classical properties of quantum algorithms in a practical manner. In this paper, we show that using measurements in no more than 3 out of the possible 3 N 3^{N} bases, one can not only reconstruct the single-qubit reduced density matrices and measure the ability to create coherent superpositions, but also possibly verify entanglement across all N N qubits participating in the algorithm. We introduce a family of generalized Bell-type observables for which we establish an upper bound to the expectation values in fully separable states by proving a generalization of the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, which may serve of independent interest. We demonstrate that a subset of such observables can serve as entanglement witnesses for QAOA-MaxCut states, and further argue that they are especially well tailored for this purpose by defining and computing an entanglement potency metric on witnesses. A subset of these observables also certify, in a weaker sense, the entanglement in GHZ states, which share the ℤ 2 \mathbb{Z}_{2} symmetry of QAOA-MaxCut. The construction of such witnesses follows directly from the cost Hamiltonian to be optimized, and not through the standard technique of using the projector of the state being certified. It may thus provide insights to construct similar witnesses for other variational algorithms prevalent in the NISQ era. We demonstrate our ideas with proof-of-concept experiments on the Rigetti Aspen-9 chip for ansatze containing up to 24 qubits.



I
Introduction

At present, there is great interest in designing and validating the implementation of solvers of computational problems that can achieve a
quantum advantage
, i.e. superior performance with respect to other known classical methods. There is considerable research activity in the study of speedup metrics
Rønnow et al. 2014
and in the certification and quantification of quantum properties (which we later also refer to as
quantumness
) in the context of variational quantum algorithms
Woitzik et al. 2020
,
Wiersema et al. 2020
,
Díez-Valle et al. 2021
.
It is however of utmost importance in the NISQ era to understand the role of coherence and entanglement, as well as to guarantee that the software-hardware systems that we use for the empirical exploration exploit these resources to outperform classical competitors.
This task is distinct from evaluating the fidelity of the experimental results with respect to a simulation, and instead focuses on verifying the key non-classical properties of a solver that may achieve quantum advantage on noisy hardware whose exact mechanics may not be easily described through analytical expressions or even numerical simulations.

Bearing in mind the lively debate over approaches to verify the presence of quantum properties in experimental runs on quantum annealers

By probing single-qubit reduced density matrices (SQRDMs), we are able to identify which qubits in the chip participate in the computation with the ability to create coherent superpositions of the classical bit values 0 and 1. Moreover, using the same measurement data as required to construct the SQRDMs, we are able to infer the expectation values of observables that serve as entanglement witnesses for some quantum state(s). The ease with which these witnesses can be measured make them a practical toolkit for a quantum programmer to quickly verify if any entanglement has been generated in the executed circuit. Although such a procedure could work for arbitrary circuits in principle, we demonstrate that the introduced family of witnesses is particularly well suited to detecting entanglement in QAOA circuits. We do so by explicitly showing that such observables do indeed serve as witnesses for QAOA-MaxCut states, and by introducing and computing an

A standard method to construct an entanglement witness

While the theorems we prove only establish that bounds by fully separable states can be violated, we also provide numerical evidence that a subset of such observables can certify against bi-separable (and by extension

The remainder of this article is organized as follows. In Section
II

III

In particular, we analytically prove an upper bound to the absolute value of the expectation of a large class of observables in fully separable states using a generalized Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, which we also prove as a lemma, as well as a lower bound to the maximum eigenvalue for a subset of such observables, thus proving that they can serve as witnesses for some entangled states. We also identify a particular class of observables that are especially relevant for QAOA-MaxCut, and prove that they serve as witnesses for such states prepared via the ring cost Hamiltonian. In addition, we also provide numerical evidence for the view that similar observables constructed for other cost Hamiltonians also serve as witnesses for the corresponding QAOA-MaxCut states. We also demonstrate the relevance of the constructed observables to QAOA-MaxCut through the calculation of an entanglement potency metric, which we introduce. We finish this section by presenting proof-of-concept experimental results from the Rigetti Aspen-9 quantum chip. We conclude in Section
IV

II
Quantum Coherence

The most iconic property of a quantum system is the ability to be in a
coherent superposition
of multiple reference states. This basic distinctive property of quantum information processing and its direct manifestations (e.g. tunneling) is believed to be essential to achieving any kind of quantum advantage
DiVincenzo and Loss 1999
. Formally, a system is said to be in coherent superposition with respect to a basis of states, if its density matrix is non-diagonal when expressed in that basis
Cohen-Tannoudji 1962
. In combinatorial optimization, the specification of both the problem variables as well as the solution, or an algorithm’s approximation to it, are efficiently representable with a polynomial number of classical bits. A quantum algorithm for combinatorial optimization might in general exploit the coherent superposition of all
2^{N}
computational basis states. However, probing these coherences require a prohibitively exponential experimental cost.

Without requiring a full tomographic reconstruction of the many-body density matrix of a quantum state, we propose to use a method which allows us to extract information about off-diagonal elements of all single-qubit reduced density matrices (SQRDMs) expressed in the computational basis, which we later use as a threshold figure of merit to determine the extent of quantumness. If sizeable values of these elements are measured at any time during the execution of an algorithm, it can be concluded that the individual qubits at that time still hold the potential to exploit single-particle quantum effects for the purpose of information processing. A generalization of the arguments to multi-particle coherent effects (i.e. co-tunneling) would be straightforward in principle, though with an exponentially greater experimental cost.

II.1
Efficient SQRDM Tomography

Suppose that we have a single qubit quantum state, either pure or mixed, represented by the density matrix

\rho=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\rho_{11}&\rho_{12}\\
\rho_{21}&\rho_{22}\end{array}\right),

(3)

for which we identify the off-diagonal elements
\rho_{12}=\rho_{21}^{*}

Here, we choose to use the absolute value of coherence, i.e.

\rho=\frac{1}{2}(\mathbbm{1}+\langle X\rangle X+\langle Y\rangle Y+\langle Z\rangle Z).

(4)

One could argue that only two measurements suffice to measure

The above protocol easily generalizes

II.2
SQRDMs in QAOA for MaxCut

Now let us demonstrate how the above protocol works for a QAOA algorithm. Let

4

\displaystyle F(\rho_{QAOA},\rho)=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\langle X\rangle\cos^{d}{2\gamma}\right)+

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\cos^{2d}{2\gamma}\right)^{1/2}\cdot\left(1-|\vec{P}|^{2}\right)^{1/2},

(6)

III
Entanglement

Quantum entanglement

Horodecki et al. 2009
is a property of a physical system that relates to the intrinsic non-decomposable character of its state. It is famously manifested as a type of correlation that distinguishes classical physics, which is local and deterministic, from quantum mechanics. While it is still debated to what extent non-local correlations are necessary for quantum advantage
Jozsa and Linden 2003
,
Datta et al. 2008
, it is certainly a signature feature of the QAOA protocol and of its relationship with adiabatic quantum computation.

Methods to estimate, quantify, and detect entanglement are highly valuable assets for benchmarking quantum hardware. Measures such as bipartite and multipartite concurrence
Hill and Wootters 1997
,
Wootters 1998
,
Carvalho et al. 2004
, entanglement of formation
Wootters 1998
, von Neumann entropy and many more exist
Horodecki et al. 2009
,
Plenio and Virmani 2014
,
Horodecki 2001
. However they are typically difficult to compute and experimentally costly to estimate.

In this work, we construct a family of observables that act as
entanglement witnesses

Chruściński and Sarbicki 2014
,
Bae et al. 2020
for some quantum state(s). These are observables whose expectation value is bounded for separable states, so that a violation of such a bound provides a signature of entanglement.
Although we focus our attention on QAOA-MaxCut states, the observables we introduce below may also serve as witnesses for other types of states, such as parametric families of states found in variational quantum algorithms, e.g. variational quantum eigensolver (VQE)
Peruzzo et al. 2014
,
McClean et al. 2016
, quantum machine learning
Biamonte et al. 2017
,
Farhi and Neven 2018
, or variational Hamiltonian ansatz
Wiersema et al. 2020
,
Wecker et al. 2015
.
From the experimentalist’s perspective, it is a simple matter to run a circuit and obtain measurements in the
X
,
Y
and
Z
bases, and then to simply check whether an observable constructed as described below violates a bound (see Theorem
1
). If it does, the experimentalist has verified the presence of entanglement in the circuit, regardless of whether that circuit runs QAOA or some completely different algorithm.
3
3

3

If it fails, this method cannot say anything about whether the state is entangled or not.
In this sense, the methods we describe below can be perceived as algorithm agnostic, and can be considered to be a general purpose benchmarking tool that can be easily and efficiently deployed for an arbitrary algorithm and system size. Additionally, this method can also be deployed to noisy circuits that are prevalent in the NISQ era, and is not just restricted to detecting entanglement in pure states.

In this section, we first describe general properties of a family of observables and show that they can serve as entanglement witnesses
Chruściński and Sarbicki 2014
for some quantum states. We then show that a subset of such observables are capable of certifying entanglement in QAOA-MaxCut states. Additionally, we discuss the separability properties of these witnesses. Lastly, we introduce an
entanglement potency
metric on a witness that quantifies the relevance of a witness to a family of states. We conclude the section with experimental results from the Aspen-9 quantum chip.

III.2
Entanglement Witnesses for QAOA-MaxCut

In QAOA
Farhi et al. 2014
, we seek to find the (approximate) ground state of diagonal Hamiltonians. For the particular case of MaxCut over a graph of equal weights, we seek to maximize the cost Hamiltonian
C^{\prime}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle}\left(I-Z_{i}Z_{j}\right)

C=\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle}Z_{i}Z_{j}

H^{\otimes N}|0\rangle^{\otimes N}

\langle C\rangle

For QAOA states built out of the 2-local MaxCut cost Hamiltonian, the 2-local observable

defined over the same set of edges as the cost Hamiltonian is a natural candidate for an entanglement witness. Indeed, we can view this observable as being constructed directly out of the cost Hamiltonian

as

. Note that if
\rho(\vec{\theta}_{p})
represents the parametric QAOA state after
p
rounds, and

\max_{\theta}\mathrm{Tr}\left(A\,\rho(\vec{\theta}_{p+k})\right)\geq\max_{\theta}\mathrm{Tr}\left(A\,\rho(\vec{\theta}_{p})\right)

k\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
. In other words, if
W
is a witness for QAOA
p
states, then it is also a witness for QAOA
p+k
states. We now show that observables from the
W_{XZ}^{(2,N,\mathcal{G})}
family can serve as entanglement witnesses for QAOA states, noting a lemma before we do so.

Ring graph

Theorem 3
.

Given a ring Hamiltonian
C=\sum_{i}Z_{i}Z_{i+1}
and the standard mixer
B=\sum_{i}X_{i}
, the observable defined on the ring graph
\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{ring}}

\displaystyle W\equiv W_{XZ}^{(2,N,\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{ring}})}=\sum_{i}X_{i}X_{i+1}+Z_{i}Z_{i+1}

(18)

In particular, the gap
\Delta W=\max\langle W\rangle_{QAOA}-\max\langle W\rangle_{sep}
between the maximum expectation value for
W

\displaystyle\Delta W\geq N\frac{\sqrt{2}-1}{2}

(19)

Proof.

For ring graphs of
N
nodes, we have
d_{u}=d_{v}=1
,
f=0
for each of
N

\langle W\rangle=N\,W_{e}
, where

denotes the expectation value
\langle XX+ZZ\rangle
over a single edge in the ring graph. From Lemma
3
, we have

\displaystyle W_{e}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\cos^{2}{2\gamma}+\frac{\sin{4\gamma}\sin{4\beta}}{2}

(20)

At all local optima, we have
\partial_{\gamma}\langle W_{e}\rangle=\partial_{\beta}\langle W_{e}\rangle=0
. Simultaneously solving these equalities gives us either
\gamma_{a}=\frac{n\pi}{4}
or
\gamma_{b}=\frac{\pi}{8}\left(n-\frac{1}{2}\right)
, and correspondingly either
\sin{4\beta_{a}}=0
or
\sin{4\beta_{b}}=\pm 1
(
+1
for
n

-1
for
n

Let

. Then,

, indicating a saddle point. For

indicating a local minimum. For

indicating a local maximum. At all these local maxima,

. Since this is true of all local maxima, this also provides the global maximum.

For separable states, the maximum achievable value for

is one. Since all edges contribute the same expectation value in a ring graph, the gap

III.4
Separability

Having established an upper bound for the expectation value of a large family of observables in fully separable states, it is a natural to ask next whether the type of entanglement detected by certain witnesses is genuine
N
-partite entanglement, or could be achieved with entanglement over only a subset of those
N
qubits. In other words, one can look at the

-separability properties of the observables described above. An
N
-partite pure quantum state
|\Psi_{k_{sep}}\rangle
is
k
-separable iff it can be expressed in the form

\displaystyle|\Psi_{k_{sep}}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|\Psi_{1}\rangle\otimes|\Psi_{2}\rangle\otimes\dots\otimes|\Psi_{k}\rangle

(38)

and likewise, a mixed state
\rho_{k_{sep}}
is
k
-separable iff it can be expressed in the form

\displaystyle\rho_{k_{sep}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i}p_{i}|\Psi_{k_{sep}}^{(i)}\rangle\langle\Psi_{k_{sep}}^{(i)}|

(39)

We know from theorem
1
that the maximum expectation value achievable by a fully seperable state for any observable of the form

is
|E_{k}|
. On the other hand, we have shown that this bound is violated by

-qubit GHZ states for observables of the form
W_{XYZ}^{(N,N,\mathcal{G})}
whenever
N=4n
and for those of the form
W_{XZ}^{(N,N,\mathcal{G})}
whenever
N=2n
for some
n\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
. However, such violations do not necessarily certify genuine
N
-partite entanglement.

To illustrate, consider an
M
-separable product state of the form
|\Phi_{n}^{M}\rangle=\otimes_{m=1}^{M}|\psi_{n}^{(m)}\rangle
, where
|\psi_{n}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(|0\rangle^{\otimes n}+|1\rangle^{\otimes n}\right)
is the

W_{XYZ}^{(N,N,\mathcal{G})}
is achieved by both an
N
-qubit GHZ state
|\psi_{N}\rangle
, but also any
M
-separable state
|\Phi_{n}^{M}\rangle
whenever
N=4nM
. Likewise,
\langle\psi_{N}|W_{XZ}^{(N,N,\mathcal{G})}|\psi_{N}\rangle=\langle\Phi_{n}^{M}|W_{XZ}^{(N,N,\mathcal{G})}|\Phi_{n}^{M}\rangle=2
, the maximal value, whenever
N=2nM
.

For
W_{XZ}^{(2,N,\mathcal{G})}
, the upper bound in Lemma
2
implies that if the edge set is given by
E_{2}=\cup_{j=1}^{3}E_{2}^{(j)}
where
E_{2}^{(1)}
and
E_{2}^{(2)}
are respectively the edges contained within each halves of some partition of the graph
\mathcal{G}
, and
E_{2}^{(3)}
the edges that cross that partition, then any bi-separable state could achieve an expectation value of at most
2(|E_{2}^{(1)}|+|E_{2}^{(2)}|)+|E_{2}^{(3)}|
, which can certainly be exceeded if the upper bound of
2(|E_{2}^{(1)}|+|E_{2}^{(2)}|+|E_{2}^{(3)}|)
is saturated, in which case the observable would certify genuine
N
-partite entanglement
7
7

7

We could similarly extend this argument to
W_{M}^{(2,N,\mathcal{G})}
(see Appendix
B
), and also use the same reasoning to
W_{M}^{(k,N,\mathcal{G})}
to establish the upper bound of
M\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k}|E_{k}^{(j)}|\right)+|E_{k}^{(k+1)}|
for

k

consists of
k

.
However, such a saturation of the upper bound is not guaranteed. In principle, the generalized Cauchy-Schwarz inequality (Lemma
1
) could be employed in conjunction with the inequalities proved earlier, as well as the physicality requirement
\sum_{\alpha=1}^{4^{k}}\langle P_{\alpha}\rangle^{2}\leq 2^{k}
, where the sum runs over all
k
-qubit Pauli operators in any

-qubit state

k
-separable states. However, in practice we can find tighter bounds using numerical techniques. For each

, we compute the largest expectation value achievable by any
k
-separable pure state, setting an upper bound to the largest expectation value achievable by any
k
-separable mixed state.

Concretely, we adopt a trigonometric parametrization of a classical probability vector
\vec{p}=(p_{1},\dots,p_{2^{N}})
as

\displaystyle p_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sin^{2}{\theta_{i-1}}\prod_{j=i}^{N-1}\cos^{2}{\theta_{j}}

with
\theta_{0}=\pi/2

\sum_{i}p_{i}=1
. A pure
N
-qubit quantum state can then be parametrized in terms of this probability vector and
2^{N}-1

\displaystyle|\psi\rangle_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{p_{i}}e^{i\theta_{i}}

(41)

where
\theta_{0}=0

As an example, numerically optimizing for the expectation value of

In both these examples, there is a strict hierarchy of the maximum achievable expectation value

III.5
Entanglement Potency

Since the entanglement structure of multi-qubit states is very rich
Horodecki et al. 2009
,
Bengtsson and Życzkowski 2017
, it is relevant to ask how many entangled states can the discussed Bell-type observables detect? In order to answer this question, we introduce a metric on witnesses, called
entanglement potency
, and compute its value on the space of QAOA-MaxCut states (according to a particular distribution) as well as Haar random states. We see that these observables have non-negligible entanglement potency for QAOA-MaxCut states, but close to zero potency for Haar random states, making them a suitable choice for detecting entanglement generated by this particular ansatz.

Definition 1
.

The entanglement potency of an entanglement witness
W
with respect to some measurable set of states
\xi
is given by

P_{\xi}(W)=\frac{V^{\xi}_{d}}{V^{\xi}_{T}},

(42)

where
V^{\xi}_{T}
is the total volume of quantum states in the set
\xi
, and
V^{\xi}_{d}
is the volume of such states whose entanglement is detected by
W
.

The set
\xi
could be a parametric family of states, such as QAOA or VQE, or it could also be the set of all
N
-qubit states (e.g. with Hurwitz parametrization
Hurwitz 1897
,
Zyczkowski and Sommers 2001
). Since the set is measurable, the definition allows us the freedom to draw from arbitrary distributions over the chosen set of states. In practice, we would draw states according to some fixed distribution over some collection, and measure the fraction of drawn states that violate the separable threshold for the observable
W
. This provides an unbiased estimate of the entanglement potency defined above, and approaches its ideal value asymptotically as the number of samples becomes large.

One may expect that the potency of any
N
-qubit observable in Haar random states decreases with
N
and perhaps vanishes at large
N
, since each observable can detect only a small subset of entangled states, while the volume of the entire set of states grows exponentially. This is similar to the vanishingly small volume of separable states at large
N

Życzkowski et al. 1998
. We numerically observe that for
W_{XZ}^{(2,N,\mathcal{G})}
on fully connected and ring graphs, the potency is negligible while for
W_{XZ}^{(N,N,\mathcal{G})}
it decreases with
N
(see Table
1
). For QAOA-MaxCut, we observe a non-negligible amount of entangled states detected by the investigated observables (see Fig.
7
,
8
and Table
1

Applying this metric to QAOA-MaxCut, we consider states prepared via the ring graph Hamiltonian together with observables of the type
W_{XZ}^{(2,N,\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{ring}})}
defined on the same ring graph. The potency is then given by the volume of
(\gamma,\beta)

\displaystyle P_{\mathrm{QAOA}_{1}}(W_{XZ}^{(2,N,\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{ring}})})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\beta\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\gamma

\displaystyle\qquad\Theta\left(\cos^{2}(2\gamma)+\frac{1}{2}\sin(4\gamma)\sin(4\beta)-1\right)

(2\pi)^{2}

IV
Conclusions

In this paper, we have introduced a practical method to verify key non-classical properties of a quantum algorithm’s implementation on a physical device. Using measurements in only the three Pauli bases, we reconstruct the experimental single-qubit reduced density matrices (SQRDMs), and interpret their coherence as a basic measure of non-classicality, or quantumness. We identified a large family of obsevables, that can serve as entanglement witnesses and that could be measured using the same measurement data. Although our work focuses on QAOA-MaxCut, the same procecure could in principle be used to test the entanglement of any other state prepared on a (noisy) quantum device – since Theorem
1
provides both upper and lower bounds for separable states, the experimentalist simply has to collect bitstrings in the
X
,
Y
and
Z
bases, compute the expectation value for a suitable observable and check if either the upper or lower bound is violated. Given the measurement data, estimating the expectation value of these observables is at most polynomial in the number of qubits, and therefore efficient.

Our work has also proposed a novel generalization of Bell-type observables, for which we established a non-trivial inequality. The generalized Cauchy-Schwarz inequality we used to prove one of our main results may have independent interest in other areas of quantum information, computer science, and related fields. We showed that entanglement witnesses for variational circuits can be constructed out of the cost Hamiltonian itself, without resorting to decomposing the projector of the entangled state into a possibly exponentially large set of measurable operators. This may inspire the construction of yet more witnesses in future work. In particular, we imagine that the techniques we have outlined in this paper could provide a foundation to the construction of similar witnesses in other parameteric families of circuits, such as those found in Variational Quantum Eigensolvers (VQE) or Quantum Machine Learning (QML), prevalent in the NISQ era.

For QAOA-MaxCut in particular, we noted that the cost Hamiltonian and the corresponding 2-local witness defined on the same graph saturate in maximum expectation value at around the same
p
. Thus, even though further rounds of QAOA might generate more entanglement, the witness does not capture this extra entanglement. Instead, the observation that it tends to saturate around the same
p
hints that it may provide a measure of the amount of useful entanglement that the algorithm employs in the optimization problem. In general, it is desirable to obtain measures of and verification procedures for the amount of entanglement that is algorithmically relevant, and our work provides a step in that direction.

As gate fidelities and qubit counts and connectivities on near-term devices improve, it will become increasingly important to verify that the hardware resources are employing genuinely non-classical resources in the execution of some algorithm, without which a quantum advantage would be impossible. Our work takes an important step in the direction of such algorithmic benchmarks. Additionally, identifying the entanglement properties in quantum circuits can help us in understanding the role of entanglement as a resource for quantum computing.

Proof of Lemma
1

Proof.

We will first prove the following identity involving the Hadamard product using induction

\left\lVert\odot_{j=1}^{k}\vec{x}^{(j)}\right\rVert\leq\prod_{j=1}^{k}\left\lVert\vec{x}^{(j)}\right\rVert

(52)

where the Hadamard product is defined as

\left(\odot_{j=1}^{k}\vec{x}^{(j)}\right)_{i}=\prod_{j=1}^{k}x_{i}^{(j)}.

(53)

First, we prove the base case involving two vectors
\vec{a}
,
\vec{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and their Hadamard product
\vec{v}=\vec{a}\odot\vec{b}
. Then,

\displaystyle\left\lVert\vec{v}\right\rVert

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\left(a_{1}b_{1}\right)^{2}+\dots+\left(a_{n}b_{n}\right)^{2}}

(54)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sqrt{a_{1}^{2}\left\lVert\vec{b}\right\rVert^{2}+\dots+a_{n}^{2}\left\lVert\vec{b}\right\rVert^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\lVert\vec{a}\right\rVert\left\lVert\vec{b}\right\rVert

where the inequality follows from noting that the absolute value of any component of a vector is bounded above by the (Euclidean) norm of that vector by definition,
|b_{i}|\leq\left\lVert\vec{b}\right\rVert=\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{n}b_{j}^{2}}
for any
\vec{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
i\in[n]
.
Next, we assume that Eq. (
52
) holds true for some collection of
k-1
vectors
\vec{x}^{(1)},\dots,\vec{x}^{(k-1)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, i.e. defining
\vec{v}=\odot_{j=1}^{k-1}\vec{x}^{(j)}
, we have
\left\lVert\odot_{j=1}^{(k-1)}\vec{x}^{(j)}\right\rVert=\left\lVert\vec{v}\right\rVert\leq\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}\left\lVert\vec{x}^{(j)}\right\rVert
.
Then,

\displaystyle\left\lVert\odot_{j=1}^{k}\vec{x}^{(j)}\right\rVert

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\lVert\vec{v}\odot\vec{x}^{(k)}\right\rVert

(55)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{v_{1}^{2}\left(x_{1}^{(k)}\right)^{2}+\dots+v_{n}^{2}\left(x_{n}^{(k)}\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sqrt{\left\lVert\vec{v}\right\rVert^{2}\left(x_{1}^{(k)}\right)^{2}+\dots+\left\lVert\vec{v}\right\rVert^{2}\left(x_{n}^{(k)}\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\lVert\vec{v}\right\rVert\left\lVert\vec{x}^{(k)}\right\rVert=\prod_{j=1}^{k}\left\lVert\vec{x}^{(j)}\right\rVert

and so Eq. (
52
) follows by induction. We can now prove the main statement of our Lemma. Defining
\vec{w}=\odot_{j=1}^{m-1}\vec{x}^{(j)}
, we now have

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\odot_{j=1}^{m}\vec{x}^{(j)}\right)_{i}\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\vec{w}\odot\vec{x}^{(m)}\right)_{i}\right|

(56)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|\vec{w}\cdot\vec{x}^{(m)}|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left\lVert\vec{w}\right\rVert\left\lVert\vec{x}^{(m)}\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^{m-1}\left\lVert\vec{x}^{(j)}\right\rVert\left\lVert\vec{x}^{(m)}\right\rVert

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^{m}\left\lVert\vec{x}^{(j)}\right\rVert

which is the statement of the Lemma. In the proof above, the first inequality follows from the standard Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, while the second inequality follows from Eq. (
52
).

∎

Proof of Theorem
1

Proof.

Given a pure state
|\psi\rangle=\cos{\frac{\theta}{2}}|0\rangle+e^{i\phi}\sin{\frac{\theta}{2}}|1\rangle
, we have

\displaystyle\langle X\rangle_{\psi}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sin{\theta}\cos{\phi}

\displaystyle\langle Y\rangle_{\psi}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sin{\theta}\sin{\phi}

\displaystyle\langle Z\rangle_{\psi}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\cos{\theta}

(57)

A fully separable pure state is given by
|\Psi\rangle=\otimes_{j=1}^{N}\left(\cos{\frac{\theta_{j}}{2}}|0\rangle+e^{i\phi_{j}}\sin{\frac{\theta_{j}}{2}}|1\rangle\right)
. For
M=1
alone, it is easy to prove the the upper bound of the absolute expectation values in such states, as follows

\displaystyle\left|\langle W_{1}^{(k,N,\mathcal{G})}\rangle\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{\langle i_{1},\dots,i_{k}\rangle}\prod_{j=1}^{k}\langle\sigma_{i_{j}}^{(a_{j,1})}\rangle\right|

(58)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{\langle i_{1},\dots,i_{k}\rangle}\prod_{j=1}^{k}(1)\right|=|E_{k}|

For
1\leq M\leq 3
, we note that
\langle W_{M}^{(k,N,\mathcal{G})}\rangle
can be written as a Hadamard product of
k
vectors

\displaystyle\langle W_{M}^{(k,N,\mathcal{G})}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\langle i_{1},\dots,i_{k}\rangle}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\prod_{j=1}^{k}\langle\sigma_{i_{j}}^{a_{j,m}}\rangle

(59)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\langle i_{1},\dots,i_{k}\rangle}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\left(\odot_{j=1}^{k}\vec{v}_{i_{j}}^{(M)}\right)_{m}

where
\vec{v}_{i_{j}}^{(M)}=\left(\langle\sigma_{i_{j}}^{(a_{j,1})}\rangle,\dots,\langle\sigma_{i_{j}}^{(a_{j,M})}\rangle\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
(since expectations of Pauli operators are real) where
j
labels some index in a
k
-tuple.
Note that
\left\lVert\vec{v}_{i_{j}}^{(1)}\right\rVert\leq 1
since the maximum (absolute) expectation value of any Pauli operator is 1. Next, since
\forall j
,
a_{j,1}\neq a_{j,2}\neq a_{j,3}
, we have that
\left\lVert\vec{v}_{i_{j}}^{(2)}\right\rVert
is the square root of the sum of squares of
\langle X\rangle
and
\langle Y\rangle
,
\langle X\rangle
and
\langle Z\rangle
, or
\langle Y\rangle
and
\langle Z\rangle
. All of these cases are bounded above by
\left\lVert\vec{v}_{i_{j}}^{(3)}\right\rVert
.
Furthermore,

\displaystyle\left\lVert\vec{v}_{i_{j}}^{(3)}\right\rVert

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\sin^{2}{\theta_{i_{j}}}\cos^{2}{\phi_{i_{j}}}+\sin^{2}{\theta_{i_{j}}}\sin^{2}{\phi_{i_{j}}}+\cos^{2}{\theta_{i_{j}}}}

(60)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1

so that we have
\left\lVert\vec{v}_{i_{j}}^{(M)}\right\rVert\leq 1
for any
M\in\{1,2,3\}
. Therefore, we obtain

\displaystyle\left|\langle W_{M}^{(k,N,\mathcal{G})}\rangle\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{\langle i_{1},\dots,i_{k}\rangle}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\left(\odot_{j=1}^{k}\vec{v}_{i_{j}}^{(M)}\right)_{m}\right|

(61)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{\langle i_{1},\dots,i_{k}\rangle}\left|\sum_{m=1}^{M}\left(\odot_{j=1}^{k}\vec{v}_{i_{j}}^{(M)}\right)_{m}\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{\langle i_{1},\dots,i_{k}\rangle}\prod_{j=1}^{k}\left\lVert\vec{v}_{i_{j}}^{(M)}\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{\langle i_{1},\dots,i_{k}\rangle}\prod_{j=1}^{k}(1)=|E_{k}|

where the first inequality follows from repeated use of the triangle inequality
|a+b|\leq|a|+|b|
for any
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
, the second inequality follows from Lemma
1
and the last inequality follows from the bound
\left\lVert\vec{v}_{i_{j}}^{(M)}\right\rVert\leq 1
as noted above.
Next, suppose we are given some fully separable mixed state

\rho=\sum_{j}\alpha_{j}\rho_{j_{1}}\otimes\dots\otimes\rho_{j_{N}}

(62)

where each
\alpha_{j}>0
and
\sum_{j}\alpha_{j}=1
. Each of the density operators in the tensor product can be expressed as

\rho_{j_{k}}=\sum_{l}c_{j_{k},l}|\psi_{j_{k},l}\rangle\langle\psi_{j_{k},l}|

(63)

with
c_{j_{k},l}>0
and
\sum_{l}c_{j_{k},l}=1
. Therefore, by absorbing these constants into the definition of the
\alpha_{j}
’s, we have

\rho=\sum_{j}\alpha_{j}|\psi_{j_{1}}\rangle\langle\psi_{j_{1}}|\otimes\dots\otimes|\psi_{j_{N}}\rangle\langle\psi_{j_{N}}|

(64)

and defining the separable pure states
|\Psi_{j}\rangle\langle\Psi_{j}|=\otimes_{m=j_{1}}^{j_{n}}|\psi_{m}\rangle\langle\psi_{m}|
, we have

\displaystyle\left|\mathrm{Tr}{\left(\rho W_{M}^{(k,N,\mathcal{G})}\right)}\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|\mathrm{Tr}{\left(\sum_{j}\alpha_{j}|\Psi_{j}\rangle\langle\Psi_{j}|\cdot W_{M}^{(k,N,\mathcal{G})}\right)}\right|

(65)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{j}\alpha_{j}\mathrm{Tr}{\left(\ket{\Psi_{j}}\bra{\Psi_{j}}\cdot W_{M}^{(k,N,\mathcal{G})}\right)}\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{j}\alpha_{j}\left|\mathrm{Tr}{\left(\ket{\Psi_{j}}\bra{\Psi_{j}}\cdot W_{M}^{(k,N,\mathcal{G})}\right)}\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{j}\alpha_{j}\cdot|E_{k}|=|E_{k}|

where the first inequality follows from repeated use of the triangle inequality, and the last inequality follows from Eq. (
61
) for pure states.

Proof of Theorem
2

Proof.

We will first prove the theorem for the case of
W_{XZ}^{(k,N,\mathcal{G})}
. One way to lower bound the largest eigenvalue of
W=\sum_{\langle i_{1},\dots,i_{k}\rangle}\bigotimes_{j=i_{1}}^{i_{k}}X_{j}+\bigotimes_{j=i_{1}}^{i_{k}}Z_{j}
is to simply compute its expectation value in some state. A convenient choice is
|+\rangle^{\otimes N}
. In this state, we find
\langle W\rangle=|E_{k}|
, therefore
\lambda_{max}\geq|E_{k}|
. However, we would like to increase this lower bound, since
|E_{k}|
is met by separable states. In
Mieghem
, a sharper lower bound for the largest eigenvalue of any
m\times m
symmetric matrix is provided as

\lambda_{max}\geq\frac{N_{1}}{m}+2\left(\frac{N_{3}}{2m}-\frac{N_{1}N_{2}}{m^{2}}+\frac{N_{1}^{3}}{2m^{3}}\right)\lambda_{0}^{-2}+O(t^{-4})

(66)

where
u=[1\,1\dots 1]^{T}
is the
m\times 1
all-ones vector,
N_{k}=u^{T}A^{k}u
,
B=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\text{max}_{1\leq j\leq m}(W_{jj}+\sum_{i=1;i\neq j}^{m}|W_{ij}|)
,
\lambda_{0}=t\sqrt{m}
and
t
is any real number satisfying
t\geq B
. In our case, we have
m=2^{N}
.

We already noted that
N_{1}/m=|E_{k}|
. Now,

\displaystyle W^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{\langle i_{1},\dots,i_{k}\rangle}(\bigotimes_{m=i_{1}}^{i_{k}}X_{m}+\bigotimes_{m=i_{1}}^{i_{k}}Z_{m})\right)^{2}

(67)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\langle i_{1},\dots,i_{k}\rangle}\sum_{\langle j_{1},\dots,j_{k}\rangle}\left(X_{i_{1}}\dots X_{i_{k}}X_{j_{1}}\dots X_{j_{k}}+\right.

\displaystyle\qquad X_{i_{1}}\dots X_{i_{k}}Z_{j_{1}}\dots Z_{j_{k}}+Z_{i_{1}}\dots Z_{i_{k}}X_{j_{1}}\dots X_{j_{k}}

\displaystyle\left.\qquad+Z_{i_{1}}\dots Z_{i_{k}}Z_{j_{1}}\dots Z_{j_{k}}\right)

Under expectation in the
|+\rangle^{\otimes N}
state, the first term becomes
\left(\sum_{\langle i_{1},\dots,i_{k}\rangle}\langle X_{i_{1}}\rangle\dots\langle X_{i_{k}}\rangle\right)^{2}=|E_{k}|^{2}
. Both terms involving a product of
X\dots X
and
Z\dots Z
terms are zero under expectation, since either the
Z_{i}
terms will survive under the product, or multiply with an
X_{i}
to produce a
Y_{i}
, and since
\langle+|Z|+\rangle=\langle+|Y|+\rangle=0
, the entire term vanishes in expectation. The last term
Z_{i_{1}}\dots Z_{i_{k}}Z_{j_{1}}\dots Z_{j_{k}}
can only contribute whenever
\langle i_{1},\dots,i_{k}\rangle
and
\langle j_{1},\dots,j_{k}\rangle
specify the same
k
-tuple, so that
\left\langle\sum_{\langle i_{1},\dots,i_{k}\rangle}\sum_{\langle j_{1},\dots,j_{k}\rangle}Z_{i_{1}}\dots Z_{i_{k}}Z_{j_{1}}\dots Z_{j_{k}}\right\rangle=|E_{k}|
. Altogether, this gives us

\displaystyle\frac{N_{2}}{m}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle W^{2}\rangle=|E_{k}|^{2}+|E_{k}|

(68)

To compute the
N_{3}
term, we observe that
W^{3}
consists of four types of terms:

1.

One term of the form
X_{i_{1}}\dots X_{i_{k}}X_{j_{1}}\dots X_{j_{k}}X_{l_{1}}\dots X_{l_{k}}
, which under expectation give a total contribution of
|E_{k}|^{2}
, following the same reasoning as in the computation of
N_{2}/m
above,

2.

Three terms of the form
\left(X_{i_{1}}\dots X_{i_{k}}\right)^{2}\left(Z_{i_{1}}\dots Z_{i_{k}}\right)
, each of which give a contribution of 0 under expectation, since the
Z_{m}
’s have no choice but to either persist or multiply with an
X_{m}
to give
Y_{m}
, and
\langle Z_{m}\rangle=\langle Y_{m}\rangle=0
,

3.

Three terms of the form
\left(X_{i_{1}}\dots X_{i_{k}}\right)\left(Z_{i_{1}}\dots Z_{i_{k}}\right)^{2}
, each of which give a contribution of
|E_{k}|^{2}
since
\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}}\langle X_{i_{1}}\dots X_{i_{k}}\rangle=|E_{k}|
, and
\langle\left(\sum_{\langle i_{1},\dots,i_{k}}Z_{i_{1}}\dots Z_{i_{k}}\right)^{2}\rangle=|E_{k}|
for the same reason we noted earlier in the computation of
N_{2}/m
,

4.

One term of the form
Z_{i_{1}}\dots Z_{i_{k}}Z_{j_{1}}\dots Z_{j_{k}}Z_{l_{1}}\dots Z_{l_{k}}
, which has non-trivial contributions from triangles within a graph, and is generally tedious to compute.

Even without explicitly calculating this last term, we have

\displaystyle\frac{N_{3}}{m}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle W^{3}\rangle\geq|E_{k}|^{3}+3|E_{k}|^{2}

(69)

Combining these results, we have

\displaystyle\frac{N_{3}}{2m}-\frac{N_{1}N_{2}}{m^{2}}+\frac{N_{1}^{3}}{2m^{3}}

(70)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{|E_{k}|^{3}+3|E_{k}|^{2}}{2}-|E_{k}|^{3}-|E_{k}|^{2}+\frac{|E_{k}|^{3}}{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{|E_{k}|^{2}}{2}>0

Letting
t=\frac{\sqrt{m}}{2}B=|E_{k}|
, we have
t\geq B
for all
N\geq 2
, and
\lambda_{0}=t\sqrt{m}=|E_{k}|\sqrt{2^{N}}
. Plugging these values into Eq. (
66
), we find that

\displaystyle 2\left(\frac{N_{3}}{2m}-\frac{N_{1}N_{2}}{m^{2}}+\frac{N_{1}^{3}}{2m^{3}}\right)\lambda_{0}^{-2}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2^{N}}>0

for any finite
N
, so that

\displaystyle\lambda_{max}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle|E_{k}|+\frac{1}{2^{N}}+O\left(\frac{1}{|E_{k}|^{4}}\right)

(71)

\displaystyle>

\displaystyle|E_{k}|+O\left(\frac{1}{|E_{k}|^{4}}\right)

which shows that the theorem holds for
W_{XZ}^{(k,N,\mathcal{G})}
. Noting that the eigenspectrum of any observable
A
remains invariant under a unitary transformation
U^{\dagger}AU
, we can perform single-qubit rotations
U=\bigotimes_{q=1}^{N}R_{X}^{(q)}(\pi/2)
(where
R_{X}^{(q)}(\alpha)=\exp(-i\alpha X_{q})
) at every qubit to have Eq. (
C
) apply to observables of the form
W_{XY}^{(k,N,\mathcal{G})}
as well. Similarly, choosing
U=\bigotimes_{q=1}^{N}R_{Z}^{(q)}(\pi/2)
yields the same bound for observables of the form
W_{YZ}^{(k,N,\mathcal{G})}
. This exhausts all the possibilities for
W_{PQ}^{(k,N,\mathcal{G})}
, and the theorem holds.

∎

Proof of Lemma
3

Proof.

These expressions can be derived using essentially the procedure described in
Wang et al. 2018
. In that paper, the authors derive the expression for
\langle C_{uv}\rangle
, where
C_{uv}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathbbm{1}-Z_{u}Z_{v}\right)\rightarrow\langle Z_{u}Z_{v}\rangle=1-2\langle C_{uv}\rangle
, and where the QAOA state is built from the cost Hamiltonian
C^{\prime}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\langle u,v\rangle}\left(\mathbbm{1}-Z_{u}Z_{v}\right)
. The expression they derive for
\langle Z_{u}Z_{v}\rangle
matches our expression for
\langle Z_{u}Z_{v}\rangle
as long as one adjusts for the difference in convention by replacing
\gamma\rightarrow 2\gamma
and
\langle Z_{u}Z_{v}\rangle\rightarrow 2\langle Z_{u}Z_{v}\rangle
in going from the notation of
Wang et al. 2018
to our expression.

The expression for
\langle X_{u}X_{v}\rangle
is derived similarly in the Heisenberg picture. We first note that
e^{i\beta B}X_{u}X_{v}e^{-i\beta B}=X_{u}X_{v}
for
B=\sum_{i=1}^{N}X_{i}
, so that

\displaystyle\langle X_{u}X_{v}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}{\left(e^{i\gamma C}e^{i\beta B}X_{u}X_{v}e^{-i\beta B}e^{-i\gamma C}\rho_{0}\right)}

(72)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}{\left(e^{i\gamma C}X_{u}X_{v}e^{-i\gamma C}\rho_{0}\right)}

where
\rho_{0}=\prod_{q=1}^{N}\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathbbm{1}+X_{q}\right)
is the initial state. Further, since
[Z_{u}Z_{v},X_{u}X_{v}]=0
and
[Z_{i}Z_{j},X_{u}X_{v}]=0
whenever
i\neq u
,
j\neq v
, the only terms that survive are those involving
u
(
v
) and its neighbors excluding
v
(
u
). Denoting the number of such neighbors as
d_{u}
and
d_{v}
respectively for
u
and
v
, and defining
C_{u}=\sum_{j=1}^{d_{u}}Z_{u}Z_{a_{j}}
and
C_{v}=\sum_{i=1}^{d_{v}}Z_{v}Z_{b_{i}}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}{\left(e^{i\gamma C}X_{u}X_{v}e^{-i\gamma C}\rho_{0}\right)}

(73)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}{\left(e^{i\gamma C_{v}}e^{i\gamma C_{u}}X_{u}X_{v}e^{-i\gamma C_{u}}e^{-i\gamma C_{v}}\rho_{0}\right)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}{\left(e^{i2\gamma C_{v}}e^{i2\gamma C_{u}}X_{u}X_{v}\rho_{0}\right)}

where in the last line we have used the fact that
X_{m}Z_{m}=-Z_{m}X_{m}
to move the
e^{-i\gamma C_{u}}
and
e^{-i\gamma C_{v}}
terms past
X_{u}X_{v}
. Now,
\mathrm{Tr}{\left(A_{u}B_{v}\rho_{0}\right)}=1
only when
A,B\in\{\mathbbm{1},X\}
, so that the only terms in the product
e^{i2\gamma C_{v}}e^{i2\gamma C_{u}}
that can contribute non-trivially are those that are proportional to the identity. Therefore, using
e^{i\alpha Z_{a}Z_{b}}=\cos{\alpha}\mathbbm{1}+i\sin{\alpha}Z_{a}Z_{b}
, we see that for triangle-free edges,

\langle X_{u}X_{v}\rangle=\cos^{d_{u}+d_{v}}{2\gamma}

(74)

which agrees with the expression in Lemma
3
for the case
f=0
. In the more general case, only terms in the expansion that are proportional to even powers of
Z_{u}
and
Z_{v}
(and therefore the identity) contribute non-trivially. We then have

\displaystyle\langle X_{u}X_{v}\rangle

(75)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0,2,4,\dots}^{f}{f\choose j}\left(\cos{2\gamma}\right)^{d_{u}+d_{v}-2j}(i\sin{2\gamma})^{2j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\cos^{d_{u}+d_{v}-2f}{2\gamma}

\displaystyle\;\times\sum_{j=0,2,4,\dots}^{f}{f\choose j}(\cos^{2}{2\gamma})^{f-j}(-\sin^{2}{2\gamma})^{j}

and using the identity

\sum_{j=0,2,4,\dots}^{f}{f\choose j}a^{f-j}b^{j}=\frac{1}{2}\left[(a+b)^{f}+(a-b)^{f}\right]

(76)

we finally obtain

\langle X_{u}X_{v}\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\cos^{d_{u}+d_{v}-2f}{2\gamma}\left(1+\cos^{f}{4\gamma}\right)

(77)

as stated in the Lemma.

To derive the expression for
\langle Y_{u}Y_{v}\rangle
, we make use of the relation

SWAP_{uv}=\frac{1}{2}\left(X_{u}X_{v}+Y_{u}Y_{v}+Z_{u}Z_{v}+\mathbbm{1}\right)
and derive the expectation value for
SWAP_{uv}
first.
Notice that
[Y_{u}Y_{v},B]=-[Z_{u}Z_{v},B]
and
[X_{u}X_{v},B]=[\mathbbm{I},B]=0
, so that
[SWAP_{uv},B]=0
, and therefore the mixing unitaries do not contribute to the expectation value:

\displaystyle\langle SWAP_{uv}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}{\left(e^{i\gamma C}e^{i\beta B}X_{u}X_{v}e^{-i\beta B}e^{-i\gamma C}\rho_{0}\right)}

(78)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}{\left(e^{i\gamma C}SWAP_{uv}e^{-i\gamma C}\rho_{0}\right)}

Inside the trace, only terms involving
u
or
v
contribute, and by making use of
SWAP_{uv}e^{i\gamma Z_{u}Z_{u^{\prime}}}SWAP_{uv}=e^{i\gamma Z_{v}Z_{u^{\prime}}}
, we have

\displaystyle\langle SWAP_{uv}\rangle

(79)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\Big(e^{i\gamma\sum_{u^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}(u)}Z_{u}Z_{u^{\prime}}}e^{i\gamma\sum_{v^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}(v)}Z_{v}Z_{v^{\prime}}}

\displaystyle\;SWAP_{uv}e^{-i\gamma\sum_{u^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}(u)}Z_{u}Z_{u^{\prime}}}e^{-i\gamma\sum_{v^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}(v)}Z_{v}Z_{v^{\prime}}}\rho_{0}\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}{\left(e^{i\gamma(Z_{u}-Z_{v})\left(\sum_{u^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}^{\prime}(u)}Z_{u^{\prime}}-\sum_{v^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}^{\prime}(v)}Z_{v^{\prime}}\right)}SWAP_{uv}\rho_{0}\right)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}{\left(e^{i\gamma(Z_{u}-Z_{v})\left(\sum_{u^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}^{\prime}(u)}Z_{u^{\prime}}-\sum_{v^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}^{\prime}(v)}Z_{v^{\prime}}\right)}\rho_{0}\right)}

where

\mathcal{N}(u)
is the set of neighbors of
u
,
\mathcal{N}^{\prime}(u)
is the set of nodes that are neighbors of
u
but not
v
or neighbors of
v

and the last step is due to

\displaystyle\langle SWAP_{uv}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2^{N}}\mathrm{Tr}{\left(e^{i\gamma(Z_{u}-Z_{v})\left(\sum_{u^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}^{\prime}(u)}Z_{u^{\prime}}-\sum_{v^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}^{\prime}(v)}Z_{v^{\prime}}\right)}\right)}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2^{N}}\times

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\left(\prod_{u^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}^{\prime}(u)}\left(\cos\gamma+i\sin\gamma Z_{u}Z_{u^{\prime}}\right)\left(\cos\gamma-i\sin\gamma Z_{v}Z_{u^{\prime}}\right)\right.

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1,3,5,\dots}^{d^{\prime}_{u}}{d^{\prime}_{u}\choose i}\left(\cos^{2}{\gamma}\right)^{d^{\prime}_{u}-i}\left(\sin^{2}{\gamma}\right)^{i}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}\left(1-\cos^{d^{\prime}_{u}}{2\gamma}\right)\left(1-\cos^{d^{\prime}_{v}}{2\gamma}\right)

Appendix D
Properties of
W_{XYZ}^{(N,N,\mathcal{G})}
and
W_{PQ}^{(N,N,\mathcal{G})}

Here we present properties of the
W_{XYZ}^{(N,N,\mathcal{G})}
observable (which can later be used to infer those of
W_{PQ}^{(N,N,\mathcal{G})}
). In particular, we show that some of the GHZ-type states yield expectation values of
\pm 3
for an even number of qubits, and that they are eigenstates of the considered observable. Additionally, it follows from Theorem
1
that separable states yield expectation values that are bounded by
\pm 1
.

First, let’s calculate the expectation value with respect to the
standard
incarnation of an
N
-qubit GHZ state

\ket{\psi}=\frac{\ket{00\ldots 0}+\ket{11\ldots 1}}{\sqrt{2}}=\frac{\ket{\bar{0}}+\ket{\bar{1}}}{\sqrt{2}},

(85)

where we used
\ket{\bar{k}}=\ket{kk\ldots k}
for brevity of notation. The expectation value of
W_{XYZ}^{(N,N,\mathcal{G})}
with respect to
\ket{\psi}
is

\bra{\psi}W_{XYZ}^{(N,N,\mathcal{G})}\ket{\psi}=\frac{3}{2}+\frac{(-1)^{N}}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\big((-i)^{N}+(i)^{N}\big),

(86)

where we used

\displaystyle\bra{\bar{0}}Z^{\otimes N}\ket{\bar{0}}=1,\quad\bra{\bar{1}}Z^{\otimes N}\ket{\bar{1}}=(-1)^{N},\quad\bra{\bar{0}}Z^{\otimes N}\ket{\bar{1}}=0,

\displaystyle\bra{\bar{1}}Z^{\otimes N}\ket{\bar{0}}=0,\quad\bra{\bar{0}}X^{\otimes N}\ket{\bar{0}}=0,\quad\bra{\bar{1}}X^{\otimes N}\ket{\bar{1}}=0,

\displaystyle\bra{\bar{0}}X^{\otimes N}\ket{\bar{1}}=1,\quad\bra{\bar{1}}X^{\otimes N}\ket{\bar{0}}=1,\quad\bra{\bar{0}}Y^{\otimes N}\ket{\bar{0}}=0,

\displaystyle\bra{\bar{1}}Y^{\otimes N}\ket{\bar{1}}=0,\quad\bra{\bar{0}}Y^{\otimes N}\ket{\bar{1}}=(-i)^{N},\quad\bra{\bar{1}}\bar{Y}\ket{\bar{0}}=(i)^{N}.

For
N=4k
, Eq. (
86
) gives
\bra{\psi}W_{XYZ}^{(N,N,\mathcal{G})}\ket{\psi}=+3
. It is easy to generate other
N
-qubit GHZ states that also give an expectation value of
+3
by appropriately flipping an even number of qubits with the
X
operator. Such a transformation leaves the expectation value unchanged, since
XYX=-Y
and
XZX=-Z
. A further odd number of transformations will cancel the negative sign.
There are a total of
2^{N-2}
such GHZ-like states that produce this expectation value, which can be obtained from the following counting formula

\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}{4k\choose 2i}+\frac{1}{2}{4k\choose 2k}+1=2^{N-2}.

(87)

The first term counts the number of allowed flips
2,4,\ldots N/2-1
on all possible combinations of qubits, noting that by the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
symmetry of the GHZ state, no more than half of the qubits need be flipped.
The next term is the number of ways that we can flip exactly half of the qubits, with the factor
\frac{1}{2}
eliminating
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
symmetric duplicates, and the final addition of 1 just counts the original
\ket{\psi}
state.

Similarly, one can construct GHZ-type states for systems composed of
N=4k+2
qubits to give the expectation value
-3
.
Starting with the state

\ket{\phi}=\frac{\ket{00\ldots 01}-\ket{11\ldots 10}}{\sqrt{2}},

(88)

and by applying similar reasoning as for the case above, one can show that
\bra{\phi}W_{XYZ}^{(N,N,\mathcal{G})}\ket{\phi}=-3
.
The number of states obtainable from
\ket{\phi}
by an even number of flips, and that therefore produce the same expectation value
-3
is again
2^{N-2}
as above.

Now, we show that the states identified above are also eigenstates of
W_{XYZ}^{(N,N,\mathcal{G})}
. Knowing that

\displaystyle X\ket{0}=\ket{1}

\displaystyle X\ket{1}=\ket{0},

(89)

\displaystyle Y\ket{0}=i\ket{1}

\displaystyle Y\ket{1}=(-i)\ket{0},

(90)

\displaystyle Z\ket{0}=\ket{0}

\displaystyle Z\ket{1}=-\ket{1},

(91)

we see that

\displaystyle X^{\otimes N}\left(\ket{\bar{0}}+\ket{\bar{1}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\ket{\bar{1}}+\ket{\bar{0}}\right),

(92)

\displaystyle Y^{\otimes N}\left(\ket{\bar{0}}+\ket{\bar{1}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left((i)^{N}\ket{\bar{1}}+(-i)^{N}\ket{\bar{0}}\right),

(93)

\displaystyle Z^{\otimes N}\left(\ket{\bar{0}}+\ket{\bar{1}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\ket{\bar{0}}+(-1)^{N}\ket{\bar{1}}\right),

(94)

so that
\ket{\psi}
is an eigenstate of
W_{XYZ}^{(N,N,\mathcal{G})}
for
N=4k
, and the sum of the above terms gives its corresponding eigenvalue of
+3
. Similarly, other GHZ states that are generated by flipping an even number of qubits in
\ket{\psi}
are also eigenvectors with the same
+3
eigenvalue. One can incorporate the bit flip (
X
) operator to all
X^{\otimes N},Y^{\otimes N}
or
Z^{\otimes N}
terms and use Pauli algebra, e.g. for flipping the first two qubits we will have terms like:
(XX)\otimes(XX)=\mathbbm{1}\otimes\mathbbm{1}
,
(YX)\otimes(YX)=-Z\otimes Z
and
ZX\otimes ZX=-Y\otimes Y
. Thus, for example, the state

\ket{\psi_{1,2}}=\ket{1100\ldots 0}+\ket{0011\ldots 1}=(X_{1}X_{2})(\ket{\bar{0}}+\ket{\bar{1}}

(95)

is an eigenstate of
W_{XYZ}^{(N,N,\mathcal{G})}
, since
X^{\otimes N}\ket{\psi_{1,2}}=Y^{\otimes N}\ket{\psi_{1,2}}=\ket{\psi_{1,2}}
, and perhaps less obviously

\displaystyle Y^{\otimes N}\ket{\psi_{1,2}}=-Z_{1}Z_{2}Y^{\otimes(N-2)}\left(\ket{\bar{0}}+\ket{\bar{1}}\right)

\displaystyle=-(i)^{N-2}\ket{0011\ldots 1}-(-1)^{N-2}\ket{1100\ldots 0}

\displaystyle=-(i)^{N-2}X_{1}X_{2}\ket{1}-(-i)^{N-2}X_{1}X_{2}\ket{0}=\ket{\psi_{1,2}},

(96)

and similarly for all other states obtained by flipping an even number of qubits in
\ket{\psi}
.
For eigenstates with the
-3
eigenvalue, the reasoning follows analogous steps.

Based on Theorem
1

W_{XYZ}^{(N,N,\mathcal{G})}
are
+1

-1

Bae et al. 2020
for an even number of qubits. For odd numbers of qubits, we numerically found the expectation value to be
\pm\sqrt{3}
. Similarly, if we restrict to only two terms (e.g.
XZ
,
XY

\pm 2

\pm|E|=\pm 1
