Abstract
. We present WeaverBird, an intelligent dialogue system tailored for the finance sector. Our system harnesses a large language model of GPT architecture that has been tuned using extensive corpora of financial texts. As a result, our system possesses the capability to understand complex financial queries, such as “How should I manage my investments during inflation?”, and provide informed responses. Furthermore, our system integrates a local knowledge base and search engine to retrieve relevant information. The final system responses are conditioned on the web search and include proper references to the sources, thus enjoying enhanced credibility. Comparative evaluations on a wide range of financial question-answering highlight our system’s superior performance over other models. Experience WeaverBird for yourself through our live demo at https://weaverbird.ttic.edu and view our concise 2-minute video at https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=yofgeqnlrMc . Our code has also been made publicly available at https://github.com/ant-research/fin_domain_llm .

2.
System Design

When presented with a natural language prompt, such as “How many EVs did BYD sell in Q1 2023?”, our system employs a two-phase process. First, it transforms the prompt into queries suitable for the knowledge base and search engine. Next, it analyzes the retrieved information, identifies the most relevant pieces, and generates a comprehensive natural language response, with proper references to the original sources. The Internet’s vast resources enable us to craft responses and insights based on up-to-date information, enhancing the adaptability and reliability of our approach. The overall pipeline of WeaverBird is depicted in

)
, our system integrates our novel training and inference techniques, which significantly enhance its overall performance and efficiency. In this section, we delineate the design of each phase, including the model architecture as well as training and inference paradigms.

2.1.
Efficiency-Optimized Search and Update

The first phase is to retrieve information from our local knowledge base and the broader internet (i.e., the first step in
Figure

1
). Our knowledge base
\mathcal{K}
is a collection of financial documents
{\bm{\mathbf{d}}}^{\text{loc}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{\mathbf{d}}}^{\text{loc}}_{N}
where the number of documents
N
is large. Following
Chase 2022
, we split each document
{\bm{\mathbf{d}}}_{n}
into multiple paragraphs
{\bm{\mathbf{p}}}^{\text{loc}}_{n,1},\ldots,{\bm{\mathbf{p}}}^{\text{loc}}_{n,M_{n}}
, where
M_{n}
(and
m

n

{\bm{\mathbf{e}}}^{\text{loc}}_{n,m}
through a neural encoder
f_{\text{key}}

{\bm{\mathbf{x}}}
comes, it is embedded into a vector
{\bm{\mathbf{q}}}
through another neural encoder
f_{\text{query}}

K

{\bm{\mathbf{q}}}^{\top}{\bm{\mathbf{e}}}/\|{\bm{\mathbf{q}}}\|\|{\bm{\mathbf{e}}}\|

If the cosine similarity of the most relevant retrieval is larger than a predefined threshold

Otherwise, we start an internet search, which is slower than a local search but may yield higher-quality results.
This efficiency-optimized design is not in the original LangChain pipeline and we propose it to strike a balance between performance and efficiency.

When an internet search

As updates occur, the knowledge base will expand, but the local search is still significantly more efficient than a web search.

2.2.
Learning to Embed and Search

We confidently consider a higher similarity to signify a more relevant paragraph due to the
training of the encoders
f_{\text{key}}
and
f_{\text{query}}
. Their optimization involves maximizing a well-defined objective:

(1)

\displaystyle\ell={\bm{\mathbf{q}}}^{\top}{\bm{\mathbf{e}}}_{0}-\log\sum_{i=0}^{I}\exp\left({\bm{\mathbf{q}}}^{\top}{\bm{\mathbf{e}}}_{i}\right),

where
{\bm{\mathbf{e}}}_{0}
is the embedding of the paragraph known to contain relevant information for the query, and the other
I
embeddings
{\bm{\mathbf{e}}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{\mathbf{e}}}_{I}
belong to a set of negative paragraphs (see
Section

3.3
for how they are selected).
Intuitively, we want the dot products
{\bm{\mathbf{q}}}^{\top}{\bm{\mathbf{e}}}
to be relatively large for the query-paragraph pairs that are actually relevant. Our encoders use Multilingual-E5-base model architecture
(
Wang et al. 2022
)
.

Once we have trained the encoders, we could determine the threshold
c
by computing the cosine similarities for a holdout set of query-paragraph pairs and analyzing the histogram.

4

4

We experimented with dot product and cosine similarity and found that the latter yields a better overall performance even though our training objective has used the former
. In practice, we choose the 1% quantile to be our threshold

Another question is where our query-paragraph pairs come from. We have collected a corpus of query-paragraph pairs by leveraging financial documents and question generation methods, which we will outline in

3.2.
Collection of Query-Response Pairs

Another important use of our data collection is to create query-response pairs for tuning the language model. This step is crucial for the performance of our system: recent research has shown that language models tuned with query-response pairs have demonstrated superior performance in answering open-domain questions and engaging in conversations
(
Mangrulkar et al. 2022
)
.
We collected four kinds of query-response pairs, three of which were created from our collection of financial documents.

Table

4
shows a breakdown of the pairs into these four categories.
In each category, 60% of the pairs are in Chinese while the rest are in English.

Generation

For each document, we can construct a query-response pair that tunes the language model to perform content generation.
To create the query, we fill the template “Please write a [TYPE] on [TOPIC] with the title [TITLE]” where TYPE, TOPIC, and TITLE can be found in the corresponding text file.
The SUMMARY part of the file is used as the response.
An example of query is “Please write a review report on China economics with the title CHINA POLICY: POLITBURO MAINTAINS PRO-GROWTH STANCE” and its response is “The politburo indicates continuous pro-growth policy without big stimulus…”

Summarization

The reverse of a content generation pair can be used as a content summarization pair.
Now the query will be created by filling the template “Please summarize the following [TYPE]: [SUMMARY]” and the response is the TITLE.
An example of query is “Please summarize the following report: NIO’s 4Q22 earnings missed our prior forcast…” and its response is “NIO INC. (NIO.US): 4Q22 miss”.

Close-book QA

Given a document, we would like the model to answer any questions that could be answered by directly identifying the relevant information from the document.
This is the focus of the close-book QA research
(
Roberts et al. 2020
)
.
To achieve this goal, we need a specific kind of query-response pairs: query is the question and the document; response is the answer.
We created such pairs using the question generation method proposed by
Rajpurkar et al. 2016
.
Precisely, given a document, we used a pre-trained question generation model to identify some interesting information in the document and then generate a question whose answer should be that piece of information.

Open QA

We would also like the system to answer open questions: unlike close-book QA, the evidence is not available in the context.
We created this kind of Chinese question-answer (i.e., query-response) pairs by scrapping well-known online QA forums such as Zhihu and SinaFinance.
On such forums, users post questions, tag questions, answer questions, and upvote or downvote answers.
We identified 6,000 highly recognized questions with the “finance” tag; with each question being the query, we took the answer with the most upvotes to be its response.
We then randomly sampled 4,000 Enligsh open QA questions from the English finance QA dataset FinQA
(
Bharti 2023
)
.

3.3.
Data Collection for Training Encoders

Recall from
Section

2.2
that we need query-paragraph pairs to train the key and query encoders
f_{\text{key}}
and
f_{\text{query}}
.
The close-book QA subset available in
Section

3.2
provides a direct resource to fulfill this requirement.
First, we categorized all the close-book query-response pairs according to their topics.
Then we manually reviewed the data of each topic and selected a total of 1,000 pairs while ensuring a comprehensive coverage of all topics.
The selection of each pair was based on our anticipated likelihood that someone would ask this question during interaction with the demo.
We used these 1,000 query-response pairs as the positive pairs in training the encoders.
For each of them, we randomly sampled five negative responses from the entire pool of paragraphs.

Related Work

Large language models such as ChatGPT
(
Brown et al. 2020
)
and GPT-4
(
OpenAI 2023
)
have showcased their remarkable capabilities in engaging in human-like communication and understanding complex queries, bringing a trend of incorporating LLMs in various fields
(
Anil et al. 2023
;
Gunasekar et al. 2023
;
Jin et al. 2023a
)
.
Recent advancements have further enhanced these models by incorporating external tools, enabling them to search for relevant online information
(
Nakano et al. 2021
)
, utilize tools
(
Schick et al. 2023
)
, and create more sophisticated applications
(
Chase 2022
)
.
In this paper, we harness these advancements to build an intelligent dialogue system that specializes in the finance domain.
The most relevant work is BloombergGPT
(
Wu et al. 2023
)
, a generative Transformer trained with large corpora of finance-related text.
While BloombergGPT has only been trained with English corpora, our system is also carefully tailored to handle Chinese, a language spoken by one-fifth of the global population.
In addition, BloombergGPT is only trained to predict the next token given the context but not to engage in conversations like our WeaverBird system; nor is it integrated with knowledge bases or search engines.

Our work aligns with research on retrieval-based language generation, which involves enhancing language models by incorporating external datastores.
This area has seen advancements in new model architectures that condition the next token generation on retrieved text
(
Khandelwal et al. 2020
;
Wu et al. 2022
;
Borgeaud et al. 2022
)
, as well as developments in training and inference methods aimed at improving the quality of retrieval
(
Zhong et al. 2022
)
and the performance in downstream tasks such as question answering
(
Karpukhin et al. 2020
;
Izacard et al. 2022b
;
Shi et al. 2022
;
Menick et al. 2022
)
and code generation
(
Zhou et al. 2023
)
.
Our work aims to deliver a system that has superior performance in answering finance-related questions.
It uses the standard GPT architecture, and augments the inputs (i.e., queries) with retrieved information (i.e., online documents or KB entries): this design is similar to the approaches of
Shi et al. 2023
;
Ram et al. 2023
, but our efficiency-optimized search and update (
Section

2.1
) is novel.

5.
Models and Training

5.1.
Language Model

Architecture.

The system is based on the pre-trained ChatGLM2-6B
(
Du et al. 2022
;
Zeng et al. 2022
)
, and we fine-tune it with the data and training strategy specified below.

Dataset.

We use the collected query-response pairs clarified in
Section

3.2
for training and evaluation. The whole set of pairs are divided into sets of 17,000 training pairs, 3,000 development pairs, and 4,100 test pairs.

Training.

We apply the LoRA
(
Hu et al. 2021
)
strategy to fine-tune the base model to adapt to the finance domain. The LoRA layer details are as follows: the LoRA rank is 16, the LoRA alpha is 32, the dropout is 0.1, and other settings are default.

5.2.
Encoders

Architecture.

The key and query encoders
f_{\text{key}}
and
f_{\text{query}}
are initialized as Multilingual-E5-base (ME5) model architecture
(
Wang et al. 2022
)
.

Dataset.

Recall from
Section

2.2
that we need query-paragraph pairs to train the key and query encoders
f_{\text{key}}
and
f_{\text{query}}
. We use the collected query-response pairs clarified in
Section

2.3
for training and evaluation. The chosen pairs were then divided into sets of 700 training pairs, 100 development pairs, and 200 test pairs.

Training.

We pass query-response pairs to the
model to yield a scalar score for each of the pair
and maximize the scores for the positive pairs while minimizing the scores for the negative pairs with the cross entropy loss.

Implementation and Deployment Details.

Knowledge Base and WebUI.

For the implementation of LangChain, we use the code from the public GitHub repository at
https://github.com/imClumsyPanda/langchain-ChatGLM

(
LangChain-ChatGLM 2023
)
without any License. For the implementation of WebUI, we use the code from the public GitHub repository at
https://github.com/THUDM/WebGLM

)
with Apache 2.0 License.

Computation Cost.

All the experiments were conducted on a server with 128G RAM, a 64 logical cores CPU (Intel(R) Xeon(R) Platinum 8163 CPU @ 2.50GHz) and 8 NVIDIA A100 80G Tensor Core GPUs for acceleration. During the fine-tuning process on the finance dataset, we set the train batch size on each device equal to
8
, the gradient accumulation step equal to 100, the max step equal to
10,000
and the learning rate equal to 0.0001, and the running time of each step is close 72 seconds, the total running time is close to
200
hours.

Deployment Details.

Our system is deployed on a server on Alibaba Cloud with 30G RAM, a 8 logical cores (Intel Xeon(Ice Lake) Platinum 8369B), and a NVIDIA A100 80G Tensor Core GPU. The inference speed depends search speed on the website, searches in local knowledge only take 1 second, and the search on the website takes 10 seconds on average.

Section

5.2
for evaluation. When evaluating a method (i.e., a specific model paired with a specific similarity metric), we take the top
K=5
highest-ranked retrievals for each given query, and evaluate the mean average precision (MAP) and mean average recall (MAR) on the entire test set of query-paragraph pairs. Intuitively, MAP measures the fraction of the predictions that are correct, while MAR measures the fraction of the actual paragraphs that are covered by the predictions. Higher is better.

Results.

The results are in
Figure

3
.
As we can see, trained encoders work significantly better than pretrained encoders, indicating the effectiveness of our training method.
Moreover, our ME5-based encoders significantly outperform the Contriver-based encoders: it is perhaps because a large portion of our test cases are in Chinese and ME5 is a bilingual model.
Interestingly, cosine similarity turns out to work the best among all the similarity metrics, even though our training objective uses dot product.

7.
Conclusion

We presented an open-source, intelligent dialogue system tailored for the finance sector, which outperforms the best available solutions as evidenced by its superior capabilities in solving a wide range of finance-related questions.
Our systematic approach contributes to the line of research on building domain-specific large language models. In addition, our training and inference strategies may be useful for developing retrieval-based dialogue systems in other domains, allowing us to unlock broader real applications.

Appendix A

Ongoing and Future Work

We are currently exploring several extensions to deal with more complex dialogue cases in the finance domain. We are particularly interested in handling:

•

Multiple-turn conversations.
In such scenarios, the system has to engage in the communication and provide new responses at each turn that are consistent and coherent with previous statements.

•

External tools.
Users may want our system to interact with other external tools, such as pulling information from commercial data vendors (e.g., Bloomberg Terminal) and placing transaction orders in online brokerage software (e.g., Charles Schwab).

•

More user-friendly presentation.
Users may desire our system presenting answers in richer formats such as tables and diagrams.
