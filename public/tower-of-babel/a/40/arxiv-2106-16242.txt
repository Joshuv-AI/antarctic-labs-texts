On proportional network connectivity
Ashley Armbruster, Jieqi Di, Nicholas Hanson, Nathan Shank

Abstract




1
Introduction

The reliability of a network is an important parameter to consider when building a network. Different characteristics of the network can become unreliable over time or from outside forces. In a simple setting, we model a network as a graph where the vertices represent our objects and a connection between these objects are represented by an edge. These networks could be computer networks, but they could also represent power stations and power lines, people and friendships, or departments in a company and workflow.
Generally there are two things to consider when discussing the reliability of a network. The first is the conditions that need to be satisfied in order for the network to be operational. We can also consider what properties need to be satisfied in order to render our network inoperable. The second consideration is what properties of our network tend to fail. In certain applications edges are prone to failure and in others vertices are prone to failure.

One of the first examples of a network reliability measure is the connectivity of a graph, which measures the minimum number of vertices (or edges) which can be removed in order to disconnect the graph. This was also generalized to mixed removals where vertices are removed first followed by edges
[
1
]
. Network reliability measures have recently been generalized to other graph properties, not just connectivity. For example, in
[
3
]
the authors consider the component order vertex connectivity (COVC) parameter which is the minimum number of vertices which can be removed so that the order of all components in the resulting graph are less than some predetermined value,
k
. A similar measure was developed for edge removals (COEC) in
[
2
]
as well as neighbor removals in
[
8
]
and
[
9
]
.

In this paper we extend this idea. Instead of a network being operational if there is a component of order larger than a fixed size,
k
, we define the network to be operational if there is a component with order greater than some proportion of the original order. So as our networks become larger, we will need a component of proportionally larger order to remain if the network is to be in an operating state. This connectivity measure has been studied for
r=\frac{1}{2}
and for the purposes of VLSI circuit design
[
7
]
, but we explore the measure for all
0<r<1
.

2
Definitions

Throughout the paper we will assume that
G=(V,E)
is a simple graph with vertex set
V
and edge set
E
. We will follow the standard graph theory notation found in
[
2
]
.

Given a graph
G
define the following:

•

Let
V(G)
be the set of vertices of
G
, and
E(G)
the set of edges of
G
.

•

Let
G-V^{\prime}
be the graph obtained from removing the vertices of
V^{\prime}\subseteq V(G)
from
G
. Similarly for edges define
G-E^{\prime}
for any set
E^{\prime}\subseteq E(G).

•

Let
|G|
be the order of the graph, that is, the cardinality of the vertex set
V(G)
.

We will assume throughout that
0<r<1
and
n
is a positive integer.

As indicated previously, we are concerned with what determines if our network is operational and what elements of our graph are prone to failure. We will consider a network to be operational if there is a component of sufficiently large size. This is formalized in the following definition.

Definition 2.1
.

Given
0<r<1
, we say a graph
G
of order
n
is in an
operating state
if at least one component of
G
has order greater than
rn
. Otherwise, we will say that
G
is in a
failure state
.

We will consider vertex and edge failures. For vertex removals, if a graph
G
is in an operating state, we want to find subsets of vertices which can render our graph inoperable. These subsets of vertices will be called
vertex disconnecting sets
.

Definition 2.2
.

Given a graph
G=(V,E)
and
0<r<1
, a subset of vertices
V^{\prime}\subseteq V
is a
vertex disconnecting set
if
G-V^{\prime}
is in a failure state; that is, every component of
G-V^{\prime}
has order at most
rn
, where
n=|V|
.

The set of all vertex disconnecting sets of
G
will be denoted
\text{COPVD}(G)
. To measure the reliability of the network, we need to find the smallest set of vertices which, when removed, render our network inoperable. This will allow us to quantify the reliability of our network.

Definition 2.3
.

Given a graph
G=(V,E)
and
0<r<1
, the
Component Order Proportion Vertex Connectivity
of
G
, denoted
CO^{r}_{v}(G)
, is size of the smallest set
V^{\prime}\in\text{COPVD}(G)
. If
V^{\prime}\in\text{COPVD}(G)
is of minimum size, we will call
V^{\prime}
a
minimum vertex disconnecting set.

Now we can consider a similar reliability measure for networks where the edges are prone to failure rather than the vertices.

Definition 2.4
.

Given a graph
G=(V,E)
and
0<r<1
, a subset of edges
E^{\prime}\subseteq E
is an
edge disconnecting set
if
G-E^{\prime}
is in a failure state; that is, every component of
G-E^{\prime}
has order at most
rn
, where
n=|V|
.

The set of all edge disconnecting sets of
G
will be denoted
\text{COPED}(G)
. The size of the smallest set in
\text{COPED}(G)
will be the measure of the networks reliability.

Definition 2.5
.

Given a graph
G=(V,E)
and
0<r<1
, the
Component Order Proportion Edge Connectivity
of
G
, denoted
CO^{r}_{e}(G)
, is size of the smallest set
E^{\prime}\in\text{COPED}(G)
. If
E^{\prime}\in\text{COPED}(G)
is of minimum size, we will call
E^{\prime}
a
minimum edge disconnecting set
.

3
COPVC and COPEC in General Graph Classes

In this section we outline how
\text{CO}_{v}
and
\text{CO}_{e}
work with several general graph classes including paths, cycles, complete graphs, and complete bipartite graphs.

3.1
Vertex

For vertex removal in path graphs, we remove vertices so that the resulting graph will be the disjoint union of the maximum number of large order failure components. This will produce a failure state by removing the fewest vertices.

Theorem 3.1
.

If
P_{n}
is a path graph of order
n
and
r\in(0,1)
then

CO_{v}^{r}(P_{n})=\left\lfloor\dfrac{n}{\left\lfloor rn\right\rfloor+1}\right\rfloor.

Proof.

Let
P_{n}=(V,E)
be a path graph on
n
vertices defined in the standard way so that
v_{1}
and
v_{n}
have degree 1 and
v_{i}
is adjacent to
v_{i+1}
for all
1<i<n
.

Let
V^{\prime}=\{v_{i}\in V:i=j(\lfloor rn\rfloor+1)\text{ where }j\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}\}
. Note
|V^{\prime}|=\left\lfloor\frac{n}{\lfloor rn\rfloor+1}\right\rfloor
.

So
P_{n}-V^{\prime}=G_{1}+G_{2}+\ldots+G_{|V^{\prime}|+1}

where
G_{i}=P_{\lfloor rn\rfloor}
except possibly
G_{|V^{\prime}|+1}
which will be a subgraph of
P_{\lfloor rn\rfloor}
. So
P_{n}-V^{\prime}
is the disjoint union of paths of length at most
\lfloor rn\rfloor
. Since each graph
G_{i}
has order at most
\lfloor rn\rfloor
, we know
V^{\prime}\in\text{COPVD}(G)
.

Next we show there is no smaller set in
\text{COPVD}(G)
.
Assume there is a smaller set
V^{\prime\prime}\in\text{COPVD}(G)
so that
|V^{\prime\prime}|<|V^{\prime}|.

Since
P_{n}
is a tree of maximum degree 2, every vertex deletion creates at most one more component. Therefore,
P_{n}-V^{\prime\prime}
is the disjoint union of graphs
G^{\prime}_{1},...,G^{\prime}_{|V^{\prime\prime}|+1}
(allowing empty graphs if necessary). Then we have that

\displaystyle n=|P_{n}|=|V^{\prime\prime}|+\sum|G^{\prime}_{i}|

\displaystyle\leq|V^{\prime\prime}|+\lfloor rn\rfloor(|V^{\prime\prime}|+1)

\displaystyle<|V^{\prime}|+\lfloor rn\rfloor|V^{\prime}|

\displaystyle\leq n.

So
n<n
, a contradiction.
∎

Removing a vertex in a cycle of order
n
results in a path of order
n-1
. Therefore, we have the following immediate corollary.

Corollary 3.2
.

For all
r\in(0,1)
,

CO_{v}^{r}(C_{n})=\left\lfloor\dfrac{n-1}{\left\lfloor r(n-1)\right\rfloor+1}\right\rfloor+1.

Complete graphs are another class of graphs which are easy to understand since any number of vertex removals will not disconnect our graph.

Theorem 3.3
.

For all
r\in(0,1)
,

CO^{r}_{v}(K_{n})=n-\lfloor rn\rfloor.

Proof.

Removing any
j
vertices from a complete graph on
n
vertices gives a complete graph on
n-j
vertices. So a failure state for
K_{n}
will be a complete graph on at most
\lfloor rn\rfloor
vertices. So for
V^{\prime}
to be in
\text{COPVD}(K_{n})
, it must have at least
n-\lfloor rn\rfloor
vertices. So
CO_{v}^{r}(K_{n})=n-\lfloor rn\rfloor
.
∎

For complete bipartite graphs, we may produce a failure state by removing one of the vertex sets or a portion of a vertex set.

Theorem 3.4
.

Assume
a\leq b
. For all
r\in(0,1)
,

CO^{r}_{v}(K_{a,b})=\min\{a,a+b-\lfloor rn\rfloor\}.

Proof.

Let
K_{a,b}=(V,E)
have two parts,
A=\{a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{a}\}
and
B=\{b_{1},b_{2},\ldots b_{b}\}
. Let
n=a+b
. Notice that the deletion of any set of vertices will result in a complete bipartite graph or a set of isolated vertices.

Case 1: Suppose
rn\geq b
. Since
rn\geq b
,
a+b-\lfloor rn\rfloor\leq a
. So let
V^{\prime}\subseteq A
be a collection of
a+b-\lfloor rn\rfloor
vertices. Notice
|K_{a,b}-V^{\prime}|=\lfloor rn\rfloor
is a failure state, so
V^{\prime}\in\text{COPVD}(K_{a,b})
. There are no sets of smaller order in
\text{COPVD}(K_{a,b})
, since if
V^{\prime\prime}\in\text{COPVD}(K_{a,b})
and
|V^{\prime\prime}|<|V^{\prime}|
then
K_{a,b}-V^{\prime\prime}
is connected (since
|V^{\prime\prime}|<a
) and
|K_{a,b}-V^{\prime\prime}|>\lfloor rn\rfloor
. So
V^{\prime\prime}\notin\text{COPVD}(K_{a,b})
and
CO^{r}_{v}(K_{a,b})=a+b-\lfloor rn\rfloor
in this case.

Case 2: Suppose
rn<b.
Consider removing
A
. Then
K_{(a,b)}-A
is an empty graph with
b
vertices and is in a failure state. For any subset
V^{\prime}\subseteq V
with
|V^{\prime}|<a
we know
K_{(a,b)}-V^{\prime}
is a complete bipartite graph with order
a+b-|V^{\prime}|
. However,
a+b-|V^{\prime}|>b
and therefore,
K_{(a,b)}-V^{\prime}
can not be in a failure state.
∎

3.2
Edges

We now shift our focus to edge removals for paths, cycles, complete graphs, and complete bipartite graphs.

Theorem 3.5
.

For all
r\in(0,1)
,
CO^{r}_{e}(P_{n})=\left\lfloor\frac{n-1}{\lfloor rn\rfloor}\right\rfloor.

Proof.

Let
P_{n}=(V,E)
be a defined as in Theorem
3.1
and label the edge incident to vertices
v_{i},v_{i+1}
as edge
e_{i}
. .

Let
E^{\prime}=\{e_{i}\in E:i=j\lfloor rn\rfloor\text{, where }j\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}\}
. Note
|E^{\prime}|=\left\lfloor\frac{n-1}{\lfloor rn\rfloor}\right\rfloor
.

So
P_{n}-E^{\prime}=G_{1}+G_{2}+\ldots+G_{|E^{\prime}|+1}

where
G_{i}=P_{\lfloor rn\rfloor}
except possibly
G_{|E^{\prime}|+1}
which will be a subgraph of
P_{\lfloor rn\rfloor}
. So
P_{n}-E^{\prime}
is the disjoint union of paths of length at most
\lfloor rn\rfloor
. Since each graph
G_{i}
has order at most
\lfloor rn\rfloor
, we know
E^{\prime}\in\text{COPED}(G)
.

Next we show there is no smaller set in
\text{COPED}(G)
.
Assume there is a smaller set
E^{\prime\prime}\in\text{COPED}(G)
so that
|E^{\prime\prime}|<|E^{\prime}|.

Since
P_{n}
is a tree, every edge deletion creates at most one more component. Therefore,
P_{n}-E^{\prime\prime}
is the disjoint union of graphs
G^{\prime}_{1},...,G^{\prime}_{|E^{\prime\prime}|+1}
(allowing empty graphs if necessary). Then we have that

\displaystyle n=|E(P_{n})|+1=\sum|E(G^{\prime}_{i})|+|E^{\prime\prime}|+1

\displaystyle\leq(\lfloor rn\rfloor-1)(|E^{\prime\prime}|+1)+|E^{\prime\prime}|+1

\displaystyle<\lfloor rn\rfloor|E^{\prime}|+1

\displaystyle<n.

So
n<n
, a contradiction.
∎

Again, since any vertex remove from a
C_{n}
will produce
P_{n-1}
, we have the following:

Corollary 3.6
.

For all
r\in(0,1)
,

CO^{r}_{e}(C_{n})=\left\lfloor\frac{n-1}{\lfloor rn\rfloor}\right\rfloor+1.

Theorem 3.7
.

For any
r\in(0,1)
, let
n=p\lfloor rn\rfloor+q
with
0\leq q<\lfloor rn\rfloor
. Then

CO^{r}_{e}(K_{n})=\binom{n}{2}-p\binom{\lfloor rn\rfloor}{2}-\binom{q}{2}.

Proof.

First we prove that for optimal
E^{\prime}\in\text{COPED}(K_{n})
, each component of
K_{n}-E^{\prime}
is a complete graph. Let
G
be a component of
K_{n}-E^{\prime}
that is not complete. Let
e
be an edge not present in
E(G)
whose vertices are in
V(G)
. Since
K_{n}
is complete,
e\in E(K_{n})
and so
e\in E^{\prime}
. Let
E^{\prime\prime}=E^{\prime}-\{e\}
. Then note that the components of
K_{n}-E^{\prime\prime}
are the same as
K_{n}-E^{\prime}+e
since the only difference is the edge
e
, which is contained within the component
G
. Since no components have their order changed,
K_{n}-E^{\prime\prime}
is a failure state. So
E^{\prime\prime}\in\text{COPED}(K_{n})
and
|E^{\prime\prime}|<|E^{\prime}|
. So a disconnecting set of smaller size can always be found if one of the components of
E^{\prime}
is not complete, thus, the removal of a minimum disconnecting set will produce a failure state whose components are all complete graphs.

Let
G_{1},G_{2},\cdots G_{k}
denote the components of
K_{n}-E^{\prime}
. Assume
G_{i}
, and
G_{j}
have order strictly less than
\lfloor rn\rfloor
and let
|V(G_{i})|\leq|V(G_{j})|
. Then consider a vertex
v\in V(G_{i})
. Let
E^{\prime\prime}\subseteq E(K_{n})
be the same as
E^{\prime}
on all vertices
v^{\prime}\neq v
, but contains the edges connecting
v
to vertices in
G_{i}
and has no edges connecting
v
to
G_{j}
. Thus,

E^{\prime\prime}=\left(E^{\prime}-\{e\in E^{\prime}:v\text{ is incident to }e\}\right)\cup\{(v,v^{\prime}):v^{\prime}\in V(G_{i})\}
. Now let
G_{1}^{\prime},G_{2}^{\prime},\cdots
be the components of
G-E^{\prime\prime}
. Then
G_{k}^{\prime}=G_{k}
for
k\neq i,j
,
G_{i}^{\prime}=G_{i}-v
, and
G_{j}^{\prime}=G_{j}+v
. Note that
|G_{i}^{\prime}|<\lfloor rn\rfloor
and
|G_{j}^{\prime}|\leq\lfloor rn\rfloor
, so
E^{\prime\prime}\in\text{COPED}(K_{n})
.
There are
|G_{i}|-1
more edges in
E^{\prime\prime}
(since we sought to remove those edges from the failure state), but
|G_{j}|
less (the edges we sought to add to the failure state). So
|E^{\prime\prime}|=|E^{\prime}|-|G_{j}|+|G_{i}|-1
and so
|E^{\prime\prime}|<|E^{\prime}|
. Therefore, if there exist two components in
K_{n}-E^{\prime}
with order less than
\lfloor rn\rfloor
, then there exists
E^{\prime\prime}\in\text{COPED}(K_{n})
with strictly lesser cardinality than
E^{\prime}
.

Therefore, if
n=p\lfloor rn\rfloor+q
with
0\leq q<\lfloor rn\rfloor
our failure state will consist of
p
complete graphs of order
\lfloor rn\rfloor
and a complete graph of order
q
.
∎

The analysis of
CO^{r}_{e}(K_{a,b})
is more involved. In
[
6
]
the authors consider the
k
-component order edge connectivity of
K_{a,b}
, denoted
\lambda_{c}^{k}(K_{a,b})
, which is defined to be the minimum number of edges that need to be removed so that all of the remaining components have order at most
k-1
. Therefore, if
\lfloor rn\rfloor=k
, then
CO^{r}_{e}(K_{a,b})=\lambda_{c}^{k}(K_{a,b})
.

4
G(n,m)

We will now turn our attention to the family of graph
G(n,m)
, the set of all graphs with
n
vertices and
m
edges.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
G(n,m)=\{G:|V(G)|=n,|E(G)|=m\}
be the set of all graphs of order
n
and size
m
for
0\leq m\leq\binom{n}{2}.

In this section we will be concerned with finding the maximum and minimum values of
CO^{r}_{v}(G)
and
CO^{r}_{e}(G)
over all graphs
G\in G(n,m)
. Intuitively, a graph achieves this minimum value would be one of the least reliable networks we can construct with
n
vertices and
m
edges. Similarly, a graph achieves this maximum value would be one of the most reliable networks we can construct with
n
vertices and
m
edges.

Definition 4.2
.

If
\mathcal{G}
is a collection of graphs, then
CO^{r^{-}}_{v}(\mathcal{G})=\min\limits_{G\in\mathcal{G}}CO^{r}_{v}(G)
.

Definition 4.3
.

If
\mathcal{G}
is a collection of graphs, then
CO^{r^{+}}_{v}(\mathcal{G})=\max\limits_{G\in\mathcal{G}}CO^{r}_{v}(G)
.

We can define
CO^{r^{-}}_{e}(G(n,m))
and
CO^{r^{+}}_{e}(G(n,m))
similarly.

The following lemma gives bounds on the number of edges that we can have in a failure state. In what follows, we can consider a graph of order
n
and we define
p
to be the maximum number of failure components of maximum order. Since a component of maximum order has order
\lfloor rn\rfloor
,
p=\left\lfloor\frac{n}{\lfloor rn\rfloor}\right\rfloor
. We will let
q
denote the number of vertices which remain after we remove
p
pairwise disjoint sets of
\lfloor rn\rfloor
vertices. So an induced graph on
p
vertices will be in a failure state and an induced graph on
q
vertices will also be in a failure state.

Lemma 4.4
.

Given a graph
G
with
|V(G)|=n
, define non-negative integers
p
and
q
so that
n=\lfloor rn\rfloor p+q
with
0\leq q<\lfloor rn\rfloor
. If
G
is in a failure state then
|E(G)|\leq p{\lfloor rn\rfloor\choose 2}+{q\choose 2}
. In particular, there exists a graph that is the maximal failure state which is the disjoint union of
\lfloor rn\rfloor
copies of
K_{p}
with one copy of
K_{q}
.

Proof.

Assume
n=\lfloor rn\rfloor p+q
.
Let
G
be a graph with order
n
so that
G
is in a failure state and there does not exist a graph
G^{\prime}
of order
n
which is also in a failure state so that
|E(G^{\prime})|>|E(G)|
. This means that
G
has the maximum number of edges possible to be in a failure state. If
G
contains a component which is not a complete graph, then we can add an edge into that component and
G
will remain in a failure state. Therefore,
G
must be the union of components which are complete graphs of order at most
\lfloor rn\rfloor.
Assume
G=K_{a_{1}}\cup K_{a_{2}}\cup\ldots\cup K_{a_{c}}
. Then
|E(G)|
is maximized when
a_{1}=a_{2}=\ldots=a_{c-1}=\lfloor rn\rfloor
and
a_{c}=q
, since if this is not the case we have
K_{a}
and
K_{b}
,
a\leq b<\lfloor rn\rfloor
. But if this is the case we note

\displaystyle{b\choose 2}+{a\choose 2}

\displaystyle=\frac{b^{2}+b}{2}+\frac{a^{2}+a}{2}

\displaystyle<\frac{b^{2}+b}{2}+\frac{a^{2}+a}{2}+(b+1)-a

\displaystyle=\frac{(b+2)(b+1)}{2}+\frac{(a)(a-1)}{2}

\displaystyle<{b+1\choose 2}+{a-1\choose 2}

and so
K_{a-1}
and
K_{b+1}
has more edges than
K_{a}
and
K_{b}
. Therefore,
G
would not be maximal.
∎

4.1
CO^{r^{-}}_{v}(G(n,m))

We will establish some basic properties of
CO^{r^{-}}_{v}(G(n,m))
for a fixed
n
which will be used to find exact values for
CO^{r^{-}}_{v}(G(n,m)).

Lemma 4.5

(Basic properties of
CO^{r^{-}}_{v}(G(n,m))
)
.

Let
n=p\lfloor rn\rfloor+q
,
0\leq q<\lfloor rn\rfloor
, and let
g_{v}^{r^{-}}(m)=CO^{r^{-}}_{v}(G(n,m))
. Then the following hold:

1.

g_{v}^{r^{-}}(m)
is non-decreasing.

2.

g_{v}^{r^{-}}(m+1)-g_{v}^{r^{-}}(m)\leq 1
.

3.

g_{v}^{r^{-}}(m)
takes on the value
0
for
0\leq m\leq p{\lfloor rn\rfloor\choose 2}+{q\choose 2}
.

4.

The maximum possible value
g_{v}^{r^{-}}(m)
can take on is
n-\left\lfloor\frac{n}{\lfloor rn\rfloor}\right\rfloor
, and
g_{v}^{r^{-}}(m)
always takes on this value for some
m
.

Proof.

1.

Consider the graph
G\in G(n,m)
where
CO^{r}_{v}(G)=g_{v}^{r^{-}}(m+1)
. Then removing one edge,
e
, from
G
clearly does not increase the vertex connectivity since any vertex set whose removal creates a failure state in
G
will also create a failure state in
G-e
.
So
g_{v}^{r^{-}}(m)\leq g_{v}^{r^{-}}(m+1)
.

2.

Consider
G\in G(n,m)
. Then let
D
be a minimal component order proportion vertex connectivity set of
G
. Now consider some
G^{\prime}\in G(n,m+1)
so that
G^{\prime}=G+e
. Assume
v\in V(G)
is incident to
e
. Then
D\cup\{v\}
is a disconnecting set for
G^{\prime}
since
G^{\prime}-D-v
is a subgraph of
G-D
. Since
g_{v}^{r^{-}}(m)
is non-decreasing,
0\leq g_{v}^{r^{-}}(m+1)-g_{v}^{r^{-}}(m)\leq 1
.

3.

Consider the graph
[\cup^{p}_{i=1}K_{\lfloor rn\rfloor}]\cup K_{q}
, the maximal failure state from Lemma
4.4
. Then this graph is already in a failure state. So by (a),
g_{v}^{r^{-}}(m)=0
for
0\leq m\leq p{\lfloor rn\rfloor\choose 2}+{q\choose 2}
.

4.

Since
g_{v}^{r^{-}}(m)
is non-decreasing,
g_{v}^{r^{-}}(m)
achieves the greatest value when
m=\binom{n}{2}
, i.e. when
G=K_{n}
. So the maximum value of
g_{v}^{r^{-}}(m)
is
CO^{r}_{v}(K_{n})=n-\left\lfloor\frac{n}{\lfloor rn\rfloor}\right\rfloor
.

∎

Now using the above lemma and Lemma
4.4
, we can obtain a full characterization of
g_{v}^{r^{-}}(m)=CO^{r^{-}}_{v}(G(n,m))
for fixed
n
and
r
.

Theorem 4.6
.

Fix
n
and let
0\leq k\leq n-\lfloor rn\rfloor
. Define

f(k)=k(n-k)+\binom{k}{2}+p^{\prime}\binom{\lfloor rn\rfloor}{2}+\binom{q^{\prime}}{2}

where
n-k=\lfloor rn\rfloor p^{\prime}+q^{\prime}
with
0\leq q^{\prime}<\lfloor rn\rfloor
.

Then

CO^{r^{-}}_{v}(G(n,m))=\begin{cases}0&m\leq f(0)\\
k&f(k-1)<m\leq f(k).\\
\end{cases}.

Proof.

Fix
0\leq k<n-\lfloor rn\rfloor
. We construct the graph
G
of order
n
with maximum size such that
CO^{r}_{v}(G)=k
as follows. We partition the vertex set into two sets,
A
and
B
, with
|A|=k
and
|B|=n-k
. Connect every vertex in
A
to every other vertex in
G
. Also, make the induced subgraph on
B
, denoted
G[B]
, to be the maximal failure state as described in Lemma
4.4
. Clearly the removal of

G-A=G[B]
. If we removed some vertex set

with

from

, so that there is a vertex

in
V^{\prime}
, then there must be at least one vertex

in

. Then note that, since
k<n-\lfloor rn\rfloor
,
|G-V^{\prime}|>\lfloor rn\rfloor
. But since

is connected to every vertex in
G
, it is also connected to every vertex in
G-V^{\prime}
. So the component
u
resides in is
G-V^{\prime}
itself. But since
|G-V^{\prime}|>\lfloor rn\rfloor
,
G-V^{\prime}
is not a failure state. Thus, only removing
A
from
G
will put it into a failure state, and
CO^{r}_{v}(G)=k
.

For
G
, we can decompose the edge set of
G
into
E(G)=E(A)\cup E(B)\cup E(A\leftrightarrow B)
where
E(A\leftrightarrow B)
is the set of edges incident to one vertex in
A
and one vertex in
B
. Then consider
G^{\prime}
with
|E(G^{\prime})|>|E(G)|
and
CO^{r}_{v}(G^{\prime})=k
. Let
A^{\prime}
be the vertex disconnecting set of
G^{\prime}
and
B^{\prime}=V^{\prime}-A^{\prime}
. Then let
E(G^{\prime})=E(A^{\prime})\cup E(B^{\prime})\cup E(A^{\prime}\leftrightarrow B^{\prime})
. Since
|E(G^{\prime})|>|E(G)|
, we have that
|E(A^{\prime})|+|E(B^{\prime})|+|E(A^{\prime}\leftrightarrow B^{\prime})|>|E(A)|+|E(B)|+|E(A\leftrightarrow B)|
. But since
G[A]
is a complete graph and every vertex of
A
is adjacent to every vertex of
B
, then
E(A^{\prime})\ngtr E(A)
and
E(A^{\prime}\leftrightarrow B^{\prime})\ngtr E(A\leftrightarrow B)
. So
E(B^{\prime})>E(B)
. This contradicts the fact that
G[B]
is the failure state with the largest possible edge set. So no
G^{\prime}
of larger size exists that satisfies
CO^{r}_{v}(G^{\prime})=k

The graph
G
constructed above will have size

f(k)=|E(G)|=k(n-k)+\binom{k}{2}+p^{\prime}\binom{\lfloor rn\rfloor}{2}+\binom{q^{\prime}}{2}

where
n-k=\lfloor rn\rfloor p^{\prime}+q^{\prime}
with
0\leq q^{\prime}<\lfloor rn\rfloor
.

If
m<f(0)
, then it is clear by Lemma
4.4
there exists a graph with of size
m
which is already in a failure state.

So if
m>f(k)
, then
CO^{r^{-}}_{v}(G(n,m))>k
and since
CO^{r^{-}}_{v}(G(n,m))
is non-decreasing and can only differ by 1 as
m
increases, we know if
m=f(k),CO^{r^{-}}_{v}(G(n,m))=k
. This holds for all

, so if
f(k-1)<m\leq f(k)
the
CO^{r^{-}}_{v}(G(n,m))=k
.

∎

It can be shown that Theorem
4.6
implies the following results, which allows for a more straightforward computation of
CO^{r^{-}}_{v}(G(n,m))
.

Theorem 4.7
.

Fix
n
and define
p,q\in\mathbb{Z}
so that
n=\lfloor rn\rfloor p+q
with
0\leq q<\lfloor rn\rfloor
. Then consider
0\leq m\leq{n\choose 2}
. Let
A=p{\lfloor rn\rfloor\choose 2}+{q\choose 2}
,

, and
\displaystyle C_{i,j}=\sum^{i-1}_{t=1}{[\lfloor rn\rfloor^{2}(p-t)]}+(j-1)\lfloor rn\rfloor(p-i)+B
. Then label
m
as follows:

If
0\leq m\leq A
, then let
f(m)=m_{0}
.

If
A+tp\lfloor rn\rfloor<m\leq A+(t+1)p\lfloor rn\rfloor
for

.

If
C_{i,j}<m\leq C_{i,(j+1)}\textrm{ for }1\leq i\leq p-1
,

f(m)=m_{i,j}
.

If
C_{i,\lfloor rn\rfloor}<m\leq C_{i+1,1}\textrm{ for }1\leq i\leq p-1
, then let
f(m)=m_{i,\lfloor rn\rfloor}
.

If
C_{p,1}\leq m
, then let
f(m)=m_{p,1}
.

Then we have that

CO^{r^{-}}_{v}(G(n,m))=\begin{cases}t&f(m)=m_{t}\\
(i-1)\lfloor rn\rfloor+j+q&f(m)=m_{i,j},i\leq p-1\\
(p-1)\lfloor rn\rfloor+q&f(m)=m_{i,j},i=p.\end{cases}

4.2
CO^{r^{-}}_{e}(G(n,m))

Now we will find the minimum value of
CO^{r^{-}}_{e}(G(n,m))
which considers edge removals rather than vertex removals. Similar to Lemma
4.5
, we will first establish some basic properties of
CO^{r^{-}}_{e}(G(n,m))
.

Lemma 4.8
.

(Basic properties of
CO^{r^{-}}_{e}(G(n,m))
) Define non-negative integers
p
and
q
so that
n=\lfloor rn\rfloor p+q
with
0\leq q<\lfloor rn\rfloor
.
For a fixed
n
, let
g_{e}^{r^{-}}(m)=CO^{r^{-}}_{e}(G(n,m))
. Then the following hold:

1.

g_{e}^{r^{-}}(m)
is non-decreasing

2.

g_{e}^{r^{-}}(m+1)-g_{e}^{r^{-}}(m)\leq 1

3.

g_{e}^{r^{-}}(m)
takes on the value
0
for
0\leq m\leq p{\lfloor rn\rfloor\choose 2}+{q\choose 2}

4.

The maximum possible value of
g_{e}^{r^{-}}(m)
is
{n\choose 2}-p{\lfloor rn\rfloor\choose 2}-{q\choose 2}
, and
g_{e}^{r^{-}}(m)
always takes on this value for some
m
.

Proof.

1.

Consider the graph
G\in G(n,m+1)
that achieves
g_{e}^{r^{-}}(m+1)
. Then removing one edge from
G
clearly does not increase the edge connectivity since the same edge set that creates a failure state in
G
creates a failure state in
G-e
. So
g_{e}^{r^{-}}(m)\leq g_{e}^{r^{-}}(m+1)
.

2.

Consider
G\in G(n,m)
. Then let
D
be a minimal component order proportion edge connectivity set of
G
. Now consider some
G^{\prime}\in G(n,m+1)
so that
G^{\prime}=G+e
. Then
D\cup\{e\}
is a disconnecting set for
G^{\prime}
since
G^{\prime}-D-e=G-D
. Since
g_{e}^{r^{-}}(m)
is non-decreasing,
0\leq g_{e}^{r^{-}}(m+1)-g_{e}^{r^{-}}(m)\leq 1
.

3.

Follows directly from Lemma
4.4
.

4.

Since
g
is non-decreasing,
K_{n}
achieves the greatest value of
g_{e}^{r^{-}}(
). Since the maximum failure state is
\displaystyle[\cup^{p}_{i=1}K_{\lfloor rn\rfloor}]\cup K_{q}
, we have that the maximum value of
g_{e}^{r^{-}}(m)
is
{n\choose 2}-p{\lfloor rn\rfloor\choose 2}-{q\choose 2}
.

∎

We can now find a simple closed form for
CO^{r^{-}}_{e}(G(n,m))
.

Theorem 4.9
.

Fix
n
, let
g_{e}^{r^{-}}(m)=CO^{r^{-}}_{e}(G(n,m))
, and let
n=\lfloor rn\rfloor p+q
, with
0\leq q<\lfloor rn\rfloor
. Then we have

g_{e}^{r^{-}}(m)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0&m\leq p{\lfloor rn\rfloor\choose 2}+{q\choose 2}\\
m-p{\lfloor rn\rfloor\choose 2}-{q\choose 2}&\text{otherwise.}\\
\end{array}\right.

Proof.

The first case of this piecewise function follows from Lemma
4.8
(c). If
m>p{\lfloor rn\rfloor\choose 2}+{q\choose 2}
, then we can write
m=p{\lfloor rn\rfloor\choose 2}+{q\choose 2}+i
for
i>0
. Let
G_{0}
denote the maximal failure state as described in Lemma
4.4
. Now if
|E(G)|=m
, then if we remove
j<i
edges we are left with a graph
G^{\prime}=G-E^{\prime}
with
|E(G^{\prime})|>|E(G_{0})|
, and so
G^{\prime}
cannot be a failure state. So
CO^{r^{-}}_{v}(G(n,m))\geq i
.

Now we construct a sequence of graphs recursively: let
G_{i}=G_{i-1}+e_{i-1}
and
G_{1}=G_{0}+e_{0}
, where
e_{i-1}
is an arbitrary edge not in
G_{i-1}
and
G_{0}
is again the maximal failure state. Then
CO^{r}_{e}(G_{1})=1
since
G_{0}
is a maximal failure state. Further, if we assume
CO^{r}_{e}(G_{i-1})=i-1
, then
CO^{r}_{e}(G_{i})\leq CO^{r}_{e}(G_{i}-e_{i-1})+1=i
. However we know
CO^{r}_{e}(G_{i})\geq i
, and thus,
CO^{r}_{e}(G_{i})=i
and
CO^{r^{-}}_{e}(G(n,m))=i
in this case.

∎

4.3
CO^{r^{+}}_{v}(G(n,m))
and
CO^{r^{+}}_{e}(G(n,m))

Working with
CO^{r^{+}}_{v}(G(n,m))
and
CO^{r^{+}}_{e}(G(n,m))
is considerably more difficult than the minimum functions, and full characterizations of the functions were not found. In this section we present our results as well as routes of attack and conjectures concerning these functions.

4.3.1
CO^{r^{+}}_{v}(G(n,m))

Similar to the minimum value,
CO^{r^{-}}_{v}(G(n,m))
we have the following properties for our maximum function. These follow a similar argument found in Lemma
4.5
.

Lemma 4.10

(Basic properties of
CO^{r^{+}}_{v}(G(n,m))
)
.

Let
n=\lfloor rn\rfloor p+q
,
0\leq q<\lfloor rn\rfloor
, and let
g_{v}^{r^{+}}(m)=CO^{r^{+}}_{v}(G(n,m))
. Then the following hold:

1.

g_{v}^{r^{+}}(m)
is non-decreasing.

2.

g_{v}^{r^{+}}(m+1)-g_{v}^{r^{+}}(m)\leq 1
.

3.

The maximum possible value
g_{v}^{r^{+}}(m)
can take on is
n-\lfloor rn\rfloor
, and
g_{v}^{r^{+}}(m)
always takes on this value for some
m
.

It is clear that if
m<\lfloor rn\rfloor
, then we are already in a failure state since the largest connected component could have at most
m+1\leq\lfloor rn\rfloor
vertices.
Also, if
{n\choose 2}-\lfloor rn\rfloor<m\leq{n\choose 2}
then
CO^{r^{+}}_{v}(G(n,m))=n-\lfloor rn\rfloor
. So we have bounds on the tails of
CO^{r^{+}}_{v}(G(n,m))
as follows:

CO^{r^{+}}_{v}(G(n,m))=\begin{cases}0&m<\lfloor rn\rfloor\\
?&\lfloor rn\rfloor\leq m\leq{n\choose 2}-\lfloor rn\rfloor\\
n-\lfloor rn\rfloor,&{n\choose 2}-\lfloor rn\rfloor<m\leq{n\choose 2}.\end{cases}

However, the case when
\lfloor rn\rfloor\leq m\leq{n\choose 2}-\lfloor rn\rfloor
is not as straight forward because the particular failure state structure is unknown. We do not know if the failure state would consist of a small number of components with a few edges in each component or a single large component with more edges.

Another way to approach the problem may be to consider what is the minimum number of edge a graph can have if fix
CO^{r}_{v}(G)=k
. This could give us a way to find
CO^{r^{+}}_{v}(G(n,m))
, however, we were unsuccessful with this approach.

4.3.2
CO^{r^{+}}_{e}(G(n,m))

As in the preceding section, we once again have the following list of simple properties. The proof is essentially identical to Lemma
4.8
.

Lemma 4.11

(Basic properties of
CO^{r^{+}}_{e}(G(n,m))
)
.

Let
n=\lfloor rn\rfloor p+q
,
0\leq q<\lfloor rn\rfloor
, and let
g_{e}^{r^{+}}(m)=CO^{r^{+}}_{e}(G(n,m))
. Then the following hold:

1.

g_{e}^{r^{+}}(m)
is non-decreasing.

2.

g_{e}^{r^{+}}(m+1)-g_{e}^{r^{+}}(m)\leq 1
.

3.

The maximum possible value
g_{e}^{r^{+}}(m)
can take on is
{n\choose 2}-p{\lfloor rn\rfloor\choose 2}-{q\choose 2}
, and
g_{e}^{r^{+}}(m)
always takes on this value for some
m
.

For edge remove, it is also clear that we can figure out the tails as follows:

CO^{r^{+}}_{e}(G(n,m))=\begin{cases}0&\text{ if }m<\lfloor rn\rfloor\\
?&\lfloor rn\rfloor\leq m<{n\choose 2}\\
{n\choose 2}-\left(p{\lfloor rn\rfloor\choose 2}+{q\choose 2}\right)&m={n\choose 2}.\\
\end{cases}

If there are enough edges to guarantee that any subgraph of
G
will contain
p
disjoint copies of
K_{\lfloor rn\rfloor}
and a disjoint
K_{q}
, then we know
CO^{r^{+}}_{e}(G(n,m))={n\choose 2}-\left(p{\lfloor rn\rfloor\choose 2}+{q\choose 2}\right)
which is the maximum value possible for
CO^{r^{+}}_{e}(G(n,m)).
However, this may not happen for very many values of
m
. For example, assume
m={n\choose 2}-2
and
G=K_{n}-\{e_{1},e_{2}\}
where
e_{1}
and
e_{2}
are vertex disjoint edges. Consider the case when
\lfloor rn\rfloor=n-1
. Then there is no subgraph of
G
which contains
K_{\lfloor rn\rfloor}=K_{n-1}
. Therefore, the failure state of
G
would be
K_{\lfloor rn\rfloor}-e_{1}
and we would have to remove

edges, not
{n\choose 2}-\left(p{\lfloor rn\rfloor\choose 2}+{q\choose 2}\right)={n\choose 2}-{n-1\choose 2}=n-1.

4.3.3
Bounds

Since a general formula for
CO^{r^{+}}_{e}(G(n,m))
was not found, we would like to bound the function. Given a formula for a general class of graphs immediately gives a lower bound. As an example, Theorem
3.5
states that
CO^{r}_{e}(P_{n})=\left\lfloor\frac{n-1}{\lfloor rn\rfloor}\right\rfloor
and so
CO^{r^{+}}_{e}(G(n,m))\geq\left\lfloor\frac{n-1}{\lfloor rn\rfloor}\right\rfloor
. However, this method does not give an upper bound.

In the case of
r=\frac{1}{2}
, we attempt to find an upper bound on the
CO^{r^{+}}_{e}(G(n,m))
value by employing a method that looks at bipartite subgraphs in the complement of a graph. We begin with a conjecture about the failure state of a graph that achieves the maximum edge removal connectivity for its graph class.

Conjecture 4.12
.

Let
r=\frac{1}{k}
and let
n
be a multiple of
k
. The there exists
G\in G(n,m)
so that
CO^{1/k}_{e}(G)=CO^{1/k^{+}}_{e}(G(n,m))
and the failure state of
G
can be written as the union of
k
components of order
\frac{n}{k}
.

In the case
n=2
, this conjecture is equivalent to the statement that one of the failure states of
G
is such that the vertices of the components partitions
G
into two equally sized vertex sets.

This conjecture is fruitful when coupled with lower bounds for the largest bipartite subgraph of a graph
G
. Since an edge disconnecting set can be seen as a bipartite graph, minimizing an edge disconnecting set in
G
is dual to finding the largest bipartite graph in the complement,
\overline{G}
. We can then apply a lower bound to the complement graph class
G(n,\overline{m})
, which gives an upper bound for
G(n,m)
.

Definition 4.13
.

Let
G
be a graph. We define the function
b(G)
to be the size of the largest bipartite subgraph of
G
. For fixed
m
, we define the function
b(m)=\min_{G}b(G)
, where
G\in G(n,m)
.

The functions
b(m)
and
b(G)
have been previously studied. Some results are summarized below:

Theorem 4.14

(Bounds on the Bipartite Function)
.

Let
G\in G(n,m)
. Then

•

b(m)\geq\left\lceil{\frac{1}{2}m+\frac{\sqrt{8m+1}-1}{8}}\right\rceil
.
[
4
]

•

b(G)\geq\frac{1}{2}(m+\frac{1}{3}n)
in the case that
G
has no isolated points.
[
5
]

•

b(G)\geq\frac{1}{2}(m+\frac{1}{2}(n-1))
in the case that
G
is a connected graph.
[
5
]

These bounds could be used to provide an upper bound for
CO^{1/2^{+}}_{e}(G(n,m))
as outlined in the next conjecture.

Conjecture 4.15
.

Let
n
be even. Then
CO^{1/2^{+}}_{e}(G(n,m))\leq\frac{m}{2}+\frac{7n}{12}
.

Proof Outline:

Let
\bar{m}={n\choose 2}-m
. Then from Conjecture
4.12
, let
G\in G(n,m)
be such that
G
has maximal
CO^{1/2}_{e}
value and has a failure state of
2
identically sized components. For simplicity, we will assume that
G
has no isolated vertices. Let
C_{1},C_{2}
be these components. Then consider the subgraph
H
consisting of the vertices of
C_{1},C_{2}
and the edges between the components. Then the number of edges is precisely
CO^{1/2}_{e}(G)
.

Then since
H
is a bipartite subgraph of
G
,
CO^{1/2}_{e}(G)=|E(H)|\leq b(G)
.

Now, we define
\bar{H}
to be the bipartite complement of
B
; that is, subgraph consisting of vertex sets
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
, with edges connecting the two sets only when an edge is absent in
H
.

Since each bipartite component has order
\frac{n}{2}
, for any bipartite subgraph
B
,

|E(B)|=\frac{n^{2}}{4}-|E(\bar{B})|
, where
\bar{B}
is the bipartite complement. Then substituting this for the
|E(B)|
term in the definition of
b(G)
, we obtain
b(G)=\max_{B\subset G}\left[\frac{n^{2}}{4}-\left|E(\bar{B})\right|\right]
. But this is simply
\frac{n^{2}}{2}-b(\bar{G})
, and
CO^{1/2}_{e}(G)\leq b(G)=\frac{n^{2}}{4}-b(\bar{G})
.

We now apply the second Erdös bound from Theorem
4.14
to
b(\bar{G})
. We obtain the final inequality,
\text{CO}_{e}^{1/2^{+}}{(G(n,m))}\leq\frac{n^{2}}{4}-\frac{1}{2}(\bar{m}+\frac{1}{3}n)=\frac{m}{2}+\frac{7n}{12}.

If
G
has
l
isolated points, we can repeat the previous argument with the isolated points removed and consider
\tilde{n}=n-l
. The upper bound of
\frac{m}{2}+\frac{7n}{12}
will still hold, as long as
l
is even.

\square
