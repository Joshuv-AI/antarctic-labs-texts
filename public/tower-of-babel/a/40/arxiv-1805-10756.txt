The linearized Vlasov and Vlasov-Fokker-Planck equations in a uniform magnetic field
Jacob Bedrossian, Fei Wang

Abstract
We study the linearized Vlasov equations and the linearized Vlasov-Fokker-Planck equations in the weakly collisional limit in a uniform magnetic field. In both cases, we consider periodic confinement and Maxwellian (or close to Maxwellian) backgrounds. In the collisionless case, for modes transverse to the magnetic field, we provide a precise decomposition into a countably infinite family of standing waves for each spatial mode. These are known as Bernstein modes in the physics literature, though the decomposition is not an obvious consequence of any existing arguments that we are aware of. We show that other modes undergo Landau damping. In the presence of collisions with collision frequency ν ≪ 1 \nu\ll 1 , we show that these modes undergo uniform-in- ν \nu Landau damping and enhanced collisional relaxation at the time-scale O ( ν − 1 / 3 ) O(\nu^{-1/3}) . The modes transverse to the field are uniformly stable and exponentially thermalize on the time-scale O ⁡ ( ν − 1 ) O(\nu^{-1}) . Most of the results are proved using Laplace transform analysis of the associated Volterra equations, whereas a simple case of Yan Guo’s energy method for hypocoercivity of collision operators is applied for stability in the collisional case.

Introduction

In this paper we consider the linearized Vlasov and Vlasov-Fokker-Planck equations with a constant background magnetic field in a periodic box.

Remark 6
.

Enhanced dissipation and hypoellipticity are closely related in the context of kinetic theory.
However, they are not equivalent. Indeed, the passive transport equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}h+v\cdot\nabla_{x}h+(v\times B_{0})\cdot\nabla_{v}h=\nu\left(\Delta_{v}+\nabla_{v}\cdot(vh)\right)

2.1.2
Non-local response

Next, we will study the full linearized Vlasov equations,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}h+v\cdot\nabla_{x}h+\frac{q}{m}v\times B_{0}\nabla_{v}h=-\frac{q}{m}E(t,x)\cdot\nabla_{v}f^{0}.

(2.35)

Using the trajectories introduced above for the passive transport equation (
2.1
), the method of characteristics gives

\frac{d}{d\tau}h(t,X(\tau;t,x,v),V(\tau;t,x,v))=-\frac{q}{m}E(t,X(\tau;t,x,v))\cdot\nabla_{v}f^{0}(V(\tau;t,x,v)),

(2.36)

and hence

h(t,x,v)=h_{in}(X(0;t,x,v),V(0;t,x,v))-\int_{0}^{t}\frac{q}{m}E(t,X(\tau;t,x,v))\cdot\nabla_{v}f^{0}(V(\tau;t,x,v))\,d\tau.

(2.37)

Taking the Fourier transform both in
x
and
v
as in Section
2.1.1
gives

\displaystyle\widehat{h}(t,k,\eta)

\displaystyle=\widehat{h_{in}}\left(k,O(t)\eta+O(t)\tilde{O}^{T}(t)k\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{qi}{m}\int_{0}^{t}\hat{\rho}(\tau,k)\widehat{W}(k)k\cdot(\widehat{\nabla_{v}f^{0}})(O(t-\tau)\eta+O\tilde{O}^{T}(t-\tau)k)\,d\tau.

(2.38)

Therefore, the Volterra equation for
\rho
reads (recall
\widehat{\rho_{0}}
is the density associated with the passive transport (
2.1
)):

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}(t,k)

\displaystyle=:\hat{\rho}_{0}\left(t,k\right)+\int_{0}^{t}\hat{\rho}(\tau,k)K(t-\tau,k)d\tau

(2.39)

where

\displaystyle K(t,k):=-\frac{iq}{m}\widehat{W}(k)k\cdot(\widehat{\nabla_{v}f^{0}})(O\tilde{O}^{T}(t)k).

(2.40)

To further expand
K
, first note

\displaystyle\left|O\tilde{O}^{T}(t)k_{\perp}\right|^{2}=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{k_{2}}{\omega_{c}}\cos(\omega_{c}t)+\frac{k_{1}}{\omega_{c}}\sin(\omega_{c}t)-\frac{k_{2}}{\omega_{c}}\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{k_{2}}{\omega_{c}}\sin(\omega_{c}t)-\frac{k_{1}}{\omega_{c}}\cos(\omega_{c}t)+\frac{k_{1}}{\omega_{c}}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=2\frac{k_{1}^{2}+k_{2}^{2}}{\omega_{c}^{2}}-2\frac{k_{1}^{2}+k_{2}^{2}}{\omega_{c}^{2}}\cos(\omega_{c}t)

\displaystyle=2\frac{|k_{\perp}|^{2}}{\omega_{c}^{2}}-2\frac{|k_{\perp}|^{2}}{\omega_{c}^{2}}\cos(\omega_{c}t)

(2.41)

where recall we denote
|k_{\perp}|^{2}=k_{1}^{2}+k_{2}^{2}
and

\displaystyle k\cdot O\tilde{O}^{T}(t)k

\displaystyle=(k_{1},k_{2},k_{3})\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{\omega_{c}}\sin(\omega_{c}t)&-\frac{1}{\omega_{c}}(1-\cos(\omega_{c}t))&0\\
\frac{1}{\omega_{c}}(1-\cos(\omega_{c}t))&\frac{1}{\omega_{c}}\sin(\omega_{c}t)&0\\
0&0&t\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}k_{1}\\
k_{2}\\
k_{3}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\frac{k_{1}^{2}}{\omega_{c}}\sin(\omega_{c}t)+\frac{k_{2}^{2}}{\omega_{c}}\sin(\omega_{c}t)+k_{3}^{2}t

\displaystyle=\frac{|k_{\perp}|^{2}}{\omega_{c}}\sin(\omega_{c}t)+k_{3}^{2}t.

(2.42)

Hence, the
K(t,k)
defined in (
2.39
) is written by

\displaystyle K(t,k)

\displaystyle=-\frac{q}{m}\widehat{W}(k)k\cdot O\tilde{O}^{T}(t)k\exp{\left(2\frac{|k_{\perp}|^{2}}{\omega_{c}^{2}}-2\frac{|k_{\perp}|^{2}}{\omega_{c}^{2}}\cos(\omega_{c}t)\right)}\widehat{f^{0}_{3}}(k_{3}t)

\displaystyle=-A_{k}\sin(\omega_{c}t)\exp{\left(-2\frac{|k_{\perp}|^{2}}{\omega_{c}^{2}}\cos(\omega_{c}t)\right)}\widehat{f^{0}_{3}}(k_{3}t)

\displaystyle\quad-B_{k}t\exp{\left(-2\frac{|k_{\perp}|^{2}}{\omega_{c}^{2}}\cos(\omega_{c}t)\right)}\widehat{f^{0}_{3}}(k_{3}t),

(2.43)

where

A_{k}=\frac{q}{m}\widehat{W}(k)\frac{|k_{\perp}|^{2}}{\omega_{c}}\exp{\left(-2\frac{|k_{\perp}|^{2}}{\omega_{c}^{2}}\right)}

(2.44)

and

B_{k}=\frac{q}{m}\widehat{W}(k)k_{3}^{2}\exp{\left(-2\frac{|k_{\perp}|^{2}}{\omega_{c}^{2}}\right)}.

(2.45)

Given that (
2.39
) together with (
2.1.2
) defines a Volterra equation for each
k
, it is very natural to apply Laplace transform methods (see §
1.1
for the conventions used). It is not hard to verify that the Laplace transform of
\rho
and
\rho_{0}
are holomorphic for
\textup{Re}z>C
for some sufficiently large
C\geq 0
and that
\mathcal{L}[K](z,k)
is holomorphic over
\textup{Re}z>0
. Hence, over the half-plane
\textup{Re}z>C
, there holds

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}[\hat{\rho}](z,k)=\mathcal{L}[\hat{\rho}_{0}](z,k)+\mathcal{L}[\hat{\rho}](z,k)\mathcal{L}[K](z,k).

(2.46)

Below we also use the notation

\displaystyle L(z,k):=\mathcal{L}[K](z,k).

(2.47)

The points
z\in\mathbb{C}
where
1=L(z,k)
are called
solutions of the dispersion relation
. Points
z_{0}\in\mathbb{C}
where
\lim_{z\rightarrow z_{0}}\left|L(z,k)\right|=\infty
are known as
resonances
.
Through the inverse Laplace transform, these two sets will determine most of the important properties of
\hat{\rho}(t,k)
.

2.2
Bernstein modes: the
k_{z}=0
case

First, we consider modes with
k_{z}=0
, i.e. completely transverse to the magnetic field.
Set

a=2|k_{\perp}|^{2}/\omega_{c}^{2}

(2.48)

and let
I_{n}(x)
be the generalized Bessel functions defined by

I_{n}(x)=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{m!\Gamma(m+n+1)}\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)^{2m+n}.

(2.49)

We begin with some preliminary lemmas, the first two are due to Bernstein
[
13
]
(and the third essentially is as well), but we include the proofs for readers’ convenience.
The first lemma characterizes
L(z,k)
.

Lemma 2.2
.

For
k_{z}=0
,
L(z,k)
is holomorphic for all
z\neq\pm in\omega_{c}
and is given by the following (absolutely convergent for
z\neq\pm in\omega_{c}
)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}[K](z,k)=-A_{k}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{2n}{a}I_{n}(a)\frac{n\omega_{c}}{z^{2}+(n\omega_{c})^{2}}.

(2.50)

Proof.

In the case
k_{z}=0
, the
K(t,k)
simplifies to

\displaystyle K(t,k)=-A_{k}\sin(\omega_{c}t)\exp{\left(2\frac{|k_{\perp}|^{2}}{\omega_{c}^{2}}\cos(\omega_{c}t)\right)}.

(2.51)

By (
A.2
) we get

\displaystyle\sin(\omega_{c}t)\exp{\left(2\frac{|k_{\perp}|^{2}}{\omega_{c}^{2}}\cos(\omega_{c}t)\right)}=\sin(\omega_{c}t)(I_{0}(a)+2\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}I_{n}(a)\cos(n\omega_{c}t)

\displaystyle\quad=I_{0}(a)\sin(\omega_{c}t)+2\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}I_{n}(a)\sin(\omega_{c}t)\cos(n\omega_{c}t)

\displaystyle\quad=I_{0}(a)\sin(\omega_{c}t)+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}I_{n}(a)(\sin((n+1)\omega_{c}t)-\sin((n-1)\omega_{c}t))

\displaystyle\quad=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(I_{n-1}(a)-I_{n+1}(a))\sin(n\omega_{c}t)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{2n}{a}I_{n}(a)\sin(n\omega_{c}t),

(2.52)

where we also used the identity (
A.3
) in the last equality.
Note that by (
A.6
), the sum is absolutely convergent for all
t
.
Then, for
\textup{Re}\,z>0
, the Laplace transform is explicitly computed as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\left[\sin(\omega_{c}t)\exp{\left(2\frac{|k_{\perp}|^{2}}{\omega_{c}^{2}}\cos(\omega_{c}t)\right)}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{n}{ai}I_{n}(a)\left(\frac{1}{z-in\omega_{c}}-\frac{1}{z+in\omega_{c}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{2n}{a}I_{n}(a)\frac{n\omega_{c}}{z^{2}+(n\omega_{c})^{2}}.

(2.53)

By (
A.6
), the sum is absolutely convergent and defines a holomorphic function for all
z\neq\pm in\omega_{c}
.
∎

From (
2.50
), we see that there is a resonance at each cyclotron harmonic
z=\pm in\omega_{c}
(recall we refer to a ‘resonance’ as
z_{0}\in\mathbb{C}
such that
\lim_{z\rightarrow z_{0}}\left|L(z,k)\right|=\infty
.
The location of solutions of the dispersion relation – that is, points
z_{0}\in\mathbb{C}
such that
1=L(z_{0},k)
– is less clear.
The next lemma shows that such points can only exist on the imaginary axis.

Lemma 2.3
.

Every
z
such that
L(z,k)=1
satisfies
\textup{Re}z=0
.

Proof.

We first claim that a solution to the dispersion relation can only appear on the imaginary axis or on the real axis. In fact, if
z\in\mathbb{C}
is not in the two axes, then
\textup{Im}z^{2}\neq 0
. According to (
2.50
),
L
is given by

L(z,k)=-A_{k}\sum_{1}^{\infty}\frac{2n}{a}I_{n}(a)\frac{n\omega_{c}}{z^{2}+(n\omega_{c})^{2}}.

(2.54)

Note that if
\textup{Im}z^{2}\neq 0
, then

\textup{Im}\frac{n\omega_{c}}{z^{2}+(n\omega_{c})^{2}}\neq 0

(2.55)

and it has the opposite sign as
\textup{Im}z^{2}
. Therefore, we deduce

\textup{Im}L\neq 0,

(2.56)

hence
L\neq 1
.
Since
L<0
on the real axis (recall (
2.44
)), no solutions to the dispersion relation can occur on the real axis.
Hence, the lemma follows.
∎

The next lemma characterizes solutions to the dispersion relation as simple poles of
(1-L)^{-1}
on the imaginary axis between each cyclotron harmonic.

Lemma 2.4

(Bernstein modes)
.

For each
k_{\perp}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}_{\ast}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists a unique
b_{n}=b_{n}(\omega_{c},k)\in(n,n+1)
such that
L(\pm i\omega_{c}b_{n},k)=1
. More specifically,
(1-L)^{-1}
has a simple pole at each
\pm i\omega_{c}b_{n}
.

Proof.

On the imaginary axis, we have by (
2.2
)

\displaystyle L(iy,k)=-A_{k}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{2n}{a}I_{n}(a)\frac{n\omega_{c}}{(n\omega_{c})^{2}-y^{2}}.

(2.57)

Since
L(iy,k)
is even in
y
, we just need to consider
y\geq 0
.
Taking the derivative with respect to
y
we arrive at

\displaystyle\partial_{y}L=-A_{k}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{2n}{a}I_{n}(a)\frac{2n\omega_{c}y}{((n\omega_{c})^{2}-y^{2})^{2}}<0

(2.58)

for
y\neq n\omega_{c}
and
y>0
.
Noting that

\lim_{y\searrow n\omega_{c}}L(iy,k)=+\infty

and

\lim_{y\nearrow(n+1)\omega_{c}}L(iy,k)=-\infty,

the proof is completed.
∎

Remark 10
.

The decomposition given in
1.8
is
not
an immediate consequence of Lemma
2.4
, not even formally.
Formally, the Laplace transform of the density is given by the following formula wherever
\mathcal{L}[K]\neq 1
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}[\hat{\rho}](z,k)=\frac{\mathcal{L}[\hat{\rho}_{0}](z,k)}{1-\mathcal{L}[K](z,k)}.

(2.59)

Given the poles in
\mathcal{L}[\hat{\rho}_{0}](z,k)
(see (
2.19
)), if
\mathcal{L}[K]
were bounded, than
\hat{\rho}(t,k)
would (formally) contain oscillations at
the cyclotron harmonics
\pm in\omega_{c}

and
the Bernstein modes
\pm ib_{n}\omega_{c}
.
The decomposition in (
1.8
) is only possible due to the resonances at the cyclotron harmonics.

Next, we study the Laplace transform of
\hat{\rho}(z,k)
.
In particular, we show that the resonances cancel the poles present in
\mathcal{L}[\hat{\rho}_{0}]
from (
2.19
), ultimately making a decomposition like (
1.8
) possible.

Lemma 2.5
.

The Laplace transform of the density
\mathcal{L}[\hat{\rho}](z,k)
is holomorphic at all
z\in\mathbb{C}

except

z=\pm i\omega_{c}b_{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}_{\ast}
, where
\mathcal{L}[\hat{\rho}]
has a simple pole.

Proof.

For
\textup{Re}\,z>C
, we have the formula (provided the
g_{n,k}
are summable)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}[\hat{\rho}](z,k)

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}g_{n,k}\frac{1}{in\omega_{c}-z}\right)\left(\frac{1}{1-iA_{k}\sum_{1}^{\infty}\frac{n}{a}I_{n}(a)(\frac{1}{z-in\omega_{c}}-\frac{1}{z+in\omega_{c}})}\right).

(2.60)

We claim that
\mathcal{L}[\hat{\rho}]
is holomorphic for all
z\neq\pm i\omega_{c}b_{\ell}
.
Away from the Bernstein modes and the cyclotron harmonics, (
2.60
) is the product of two holomorphic functions, and hence by analytic continuation we can extend this formula for
\mathcal{L}[\hat{\rho}](z,k)
to all
z\neq in\omega_{c}
and
z\neq ib_{n}\omega_{c}
.
Next, we show that
\mathcal{L}[\hat{\rho}](z,k)
is actually holomorphic at the cyclotron harmonics.
Indeed, suppose that
z\rightarrow i\omega_{c}\ell
for some
\ell\in\mathbb{Z}
. We see that
\mathcal{L}[\hat{\rho}](z,k)
is continuous (i.e. the singularity is removable):

\displaystyle\lim_{z\rightarrow i\omega_{c}\ell}\mathcal{L}[\hat{\rho}](z,k)=\frac{g_{\ell,k}}{iA_{k}\frac{\ell}{a}I_{\ell}(a)}.

(2.61)

Something analogous holds for the derivatives as well, and hence
\mathcal{L}[\hat{\rho}]
is holomorphic also at the cyclotron harmonics.

Finally, it is evident from (
2.60
) that
\mathcal{L}[\hat{\rho}]
has a simple pole whenever
z=\pm i\omega_{c}b_{\ell}
for one of the Bernstein modes
b_{\ell}
.
∎

In order to make rigorous the decomposition of
\widehat{\rho}(t,k)
suggested by Lemma
2.5
, we begin by analyzing the residues at the Bernstein modes and deducing
decay estimates in terms of both spatial mode
k
and in cyclotron harmonic number.
Note,

Lemma 2.7

(Low frequency)

\displaystyle 6\left\lVert\nabla\rho\right\rVert_{L^{2}_{x}}^{2}+\left\lVert\rho\right\rVert_{L^{2}_{x}}^{2}\leq\frac{d}{dt}G(t)+C\left\lVert\nabla_{x}g\right\rVert_{L^{2}_{\mu}}^{2}+C(\nu+b)\left\lVert\rho\right\rVert_{L^{2}_{x}}\left\lVert\nabla_{x}g\right\rVert_{L^{2}_{\mu}}.

(3.30)

The lemma then follows (up to re-defining

∎

Using Lemma

).
Define the following additional ensergy

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int\frac{\left|\nabla_{x}h\right|^{2}}{\mu}dxdv+\frac{1}{2}\int\left|\nabla_{x}E\right|^{2}dx.

(3.31)

From Proposition (

) (and that

commutes with the (

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{E}_{0}

\displaystyle=-\nu\langle{g,Lg}\rangle_{\mu}\leq-\nu\lambda\left\lVert g\right\rVert_{L^{2}_{\mu}}^{2}

(3.32)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{E}_{1}

\displaystyle=-\nu\langle{\nabla_{x}h,L\nabla_{x}h}\rangle_{\mu}.

(3.33)

Note that the presence of
\nabla_{x}
does not affect the orthogonality:

\displaystyle\langle{\nabla_{x}h,L\nabla_{x}h}\rangle_{\mu}=\langle{\nabla_{x}g,L\nabla_{x}g}\rangle_{\mu}.

Let
\delta>0
be a parameter to be chosen small later. Then, by the positivity from Lemma

and the spectral gap (

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\left(\mathcal{E}_{0}+\mathcal{E}_{1}\right)

\displaystyle\leq-\nu\lambda\left\lVert g\right\rVert_{L^{2}_{\mu}}^{2}-\nu(1-\delta)\lambda\left\lVert\nabla_{x}g\right\rVert_{L^{2}_{\mu}}^{2}-\nu\delta\lambda\left\lVert\nabla_{x}g\right\rVert_{L^{2}_{\mu}}^{2}

(3.35)

\displaystyle\leq-\nu\lambda\left\lVert g\right\rVert_{L^{2}_{\mu}}^{2}-\nu(1-\delta)\lambda\left\lVert\nabla_{x}g\right\rVert_{L^{2}_{\mu}}^{2}-\frac{\nu\delta\lambda}{C}\left\lVert\nabla_{x}\rho\right\rVert_{L^{2}_{x}}^{2}+\frac{\nu\lambda\delta}{C}\frac{d}{dt}G(t).

(3.36)

Hence, for
\delta^{\prime}
small enough (depending only on

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\left(\mathcal{E}_{0}+\mathcal{E}_{1}-\frac{\nu\lambda\delta}{C}G\right)\leq-\nu\delta^{\prime}\left(\left\lVert g\right\rVert_{L^{2}_{\mu}}^{2}+\left\lVert\nabla_{x}g\right\rVert_{L^{2}_{\mu}}^{2}+\left\lVert\nabla_{x}\rho\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\right).

(3.37)

Next, we observe from (

), (

)

\displaystyle\left|G(t)\right|\lesssim\left\lVert\nabla_{x}g\right\rVert_{L^{2}_{\mu}}^{2}+\left\lVert\rho\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\lesssim\mathcal{E}_{0}+\mathcal{E}_{1},

(3.38)

and hence for
\nu\in(0,1)

such that (recall

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{0}+\mathcal{E}_{1}-\frac{\nu\lambda\delta}{C}G\leq C_{0}\left(\left\lVert g\right\rVert_{L^{2}_{\mu}}^{2}+\left\lVert\nabla_{x}g\right\rVert_{L^{2}_{\mu}}^{2}+\left\lVert\nabla_{x}\rho\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\right),

(3.39)

(recall that

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\left(\mathcal{E}_{0}+\mathcal{E}_{1}-\frac{\nu\lambda\delta}{C}G\right)\leq-\nu\frac{\delta}{C_{0}}\left(\mathcal{E}_{0}+\mathcal{E}_{1}-\frac{\nu\lambda\delta}{C}G\right),

(3.40)

which implies the exponential decay

\displaystyle\left(\mathcal{E}_{0}+\mathcal{E}_{1}-\frac{\nu\lambda\delta}{C}G\right)(t)\leq e^{-\nu\frac{\delta}{C_{0}}t}\left(\mathcal{E}_{0}+\mathcal{E}_{1}-\frac{\nu\lambda\delta}{C}G\right)(0).

Using again (

\displaystyle\left(\mathcal{E}_{0}+\mathcal{E}_{1}\right)(t)\lesssim e^{-\frac{\nu\delta}{C_{0}}t}\left(\mathcal{E}_{0}+\mathcal{E}_{1}\right)(0).

(3.42)

This completes the proof of the exponential decay estimate (

Using that

3.2.1
Volterra equation reduction

In this section, we derive a Volterra equation for the density.
This is analogous to calculations done in

As in these previous works, this uses crucially that the linear Fokker-Planck collision operator is relatively simple under the Fourier transform.

Taking the Fourier transform of (

x

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\hat{h}+\left(A\eta-k\right)\cdot\nabla_{\eta}\hat{h}+\hat{E}(t,k)\cdot i\eta\hat{f^{0}}(\eta)=-\nu\left|\eta\right|^{2}\hat{h},

where

\displaystyle A=\begin{pmatrix}\nu&\omega_{c}&0\\
-\omega_{c}&\nu&0\\

(3.44)

As in the collisionless case, we solve this via the method of characteristics.
The characteristic emanating from

\displaystyle\bar{\eta}(t;k,\eta)=e^{tA}\eta-\int_{0}^{t}e^{(t-\tau)A}kd\tau.

\displaystyle e^{tA}=e^{\nu t}\begin{pmatrix}\cos\omega_{c}t&\sin\omega_{c}t&0\\

\displaystyle\hat{h}(t,k,\eta)=\hat{f}\left(t,k,e^{-tA}\eta+\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\tau A}kd\tau\right).

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\widehat{f}+\widehat{E}(t,k)\cdot i\bar{\eta}(t;k,\eta)\widehat{f^{0}}(\bar{\eta}(t;k,\eta))=-\nu\left|\bar{\eta}(t;k,\eta)\right|^{2}\hat{f}.

(3.48)

Next, define the propagator:

\displaystyle S(t,\tau;k,\eta)=\exp\left[-\nu\int_{\tau}^{t}\left|\bar{\eta}(s;k,\eta)\right|^{2}ds\right].

(3.49)

Integrating (

\displaystyle\hat{f}(t,k,\eta)=S(t,0;k,\eta)\widehat{f_{in}}-\int_{0}^{t}S(t,\tau;k,\eta)\widehat{E}(\tau,k)\cdot i\bar{\eta}(\tau;k,\eta)\widehat{f^{0}}(\bar{\eta}(\tau;k,\eta))d\tau.

Define the Orr-critical frequency as (here

stands for ‘critical time’):

\displaystyle\eta_{CT}(t,k)=\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\tau A}kd\tau;

(3.52)

Hence, evaluating (

\eta_{CT}

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}(t,k)

\displaystyle=S(t,0;k,\eta_{CT}(t))\widehat{f_{in}}(k,\eta_{CT}(t))

\displaystyle\quad-\int_{0}^{t}S(t,\tau;k,\eta_{CT}(t))\widehat{E}(\tau,k)\cdot i\bar{\eta}(\tau;k,\eta_{CT}(t))\widehat{f^{0}}(\bar{\eta}(\tau;k,\eta_{CT}(t)))d\tau.

Note that

\displaystyle\bar{\eta}(\tau;k,\eta_{CT}(t))

\displaystyle S(t,\tau;k,\eta_{CT}(t,k))

j\leq\sigma
.

\displaystyle\sup_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}_{\ast}:k_{3}\neq 0}\sup_{\lambda\in[0,\infty)}\left|\partial_{\omega}^{j}\mathcal{L}[K^{\nu,\delta}](\lambda+i\omega,k)\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim_{j}1.

(3.102)

The next two lemmas show that any non-decaying behavior introduced for small
\nu
must occur in a fixed window in
(z,k)\in\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{Z}^{3}
.

Lemma 3.8
.

For
\nu
sufficiently small (depending on
\omega_{c}
), there holds the uniform bound

\displaystyle\sup_{\textup{Re}\,z\geq 0}\left|\mathcal{L}[K^{\nu,\delta}](z,k)\right|\lesssim\frac{1}{\left|k\right|}.

(3.103)

Proof.

First consider the case
t<\nu^{-1/2}
.
In order to obtain a decay estimate from (
3.86
) in
k
that does not depend badly on
\nu
, it is clear that the term involving
\left|k^{\perp}\right|^{2}\sin\omega_{c}t
is potentially the worst term.
For this term, we must use the exponential factor coming from the collisionless contribution. For this, first note that for
t>0
there holds

\displaystyle 1-2e^{-\nu t}\cos\omega_{c}t+e^{-2\nu t}=2e^{-\nu t}\left(\cosh\nu t-\cos\omega_{c}t\right)>0.

(3.104)

We will also separate short times (
t\lesssim\omega_{c}^{-1}
) from others in order to isolate powers of
t
, with the intention of using the smoothing from
S(t,k)
.
By
e^{-x}\lesssim x^{-1/2}
, there holds (from (
3.86
))

\displaystyle\left|K^{\nu,\delta}(t,k)\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{S(t,k)}{\left|k\right|^{2}}\left|k_{\perp}\right|^{2}\left(\mathbf{1}_{t<\omega_{c}^{-1}}(\nu t^{2}+t)+\mathbf{1}_{t>\omega_{c}^{-1}}\nu+\left|\frac{e^{-\frac{1}{2}\nu t}\sin\omega_{c}t}{\left(\cosh\nu t-\cos\omega_{c}t\right)^{1/2}\left|k_{\perp}\right|}\right|\right)

(3.105)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\times\exp\left(-\frac{\left|k_{\perp}\right|^{2}}{\nu^{2}+\omega_{c}^{2}}\mathbf{1}_{t<(\omega_{c})^{-1}}t^{2}\right)e^{-k_{3}^{2}t^{2}}+\frac{S(t,k)}{\left|k\right|^{2}}k_{3}^{2}te^{-k_{3}^{2}t^{2}}.

(3.106)

Notice for
t<\nu^{-1/2}
, where
n
is such that
t\in[2n\pi-\pi,2n\pi+\pi)
,

\displaystyle\left|\frac{e^{-\frac{1}{2}\nu t}\sin\omega_{c}t}{\left(\cosh\nu t-\cos\omega_{c}t\right)^{1/2}}\right|\lesssim\frac{\left|t-2n\pi\right|}{\left(\left|t-2n\pi\right|^{2}+(\nu t)^{2}\right)^{1/2}}\lesssim 1.

(3.107)

Therefore, it follows that for
t<\nu^{-1/2}
, using also Lemma
3.5
,

\displaystyle\left|K^{\nu,\delta}(t,k)\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{\left|k\right|}\left(1+k_{3}^{2}t)\right)e^{-k_{3}^{2}t}.

(3.108)

On the other hand, for
t\geq\nu^{-1/2}
, we can simply bound by

\displaystyle\left|K^{\nu,\delta}(t,k)\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim S(t,k)\left(1+k_{3}\right).

(3.109)

Then, by Lemma
3.5
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\left|K^{\nu,\delta}(t,k)\right|dt\lesssim\int_{0}^{\nu^{-1/2}}\left|K^{\nu,\delta}(t,k)\right|dt+\int_{\nu^{-1/2}}^{\infty}\left|K^{\nu,\delta}(t,k)\right|dt\lesssim\frac{1}{\left|k\right|}.

(3.110)

∎

The next lemma is a little more involved but is based on similar principles.

Lemma 3.9
.

Uniformly in
\nu
sufficiently small, there holds

\displaystyle\sup_{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ast}^{3}:k_{3}\neq 0}\sup_{\textup{Re}\,z\geq 0}\left|\mathcal{L}[K^{\nu,\delta}](z,k)\right|\lesssim\frac{1}{\left|\omega\right|}.

(3.111)

Proof.

As in the collisionless case (Lemma
2.13
), it suffices to obtain uniform (in
k
,
z
, and
\nu
) absolute integrability of
\left|\partial_{t}K^{\nu,\delta}(t,k)\right|
.
Consider
\partial_{t}K^{\nu,\delta}
(note the first two terms come from
\partial_{t}S
),

\displaystyle\left|\partial_{t}K^{\nu,\delta}\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim\nu^{-1}k_{3}^{2}\left|1-2e^{-\nu t}+e^{-2\nu t}\right|\left|K^{\nu,\delta}(t,k)\right|

\displaystyle\quad+\left|1-\frac{2\nu}{\nu^{2}+\omega_{c}^{2}}e^{-\nu t}\left(\nu\cos\omega_{c}t+\omega_{c}\sin\omega_{c}t\right)-\frac{2\omega_{c}}{\nu^{2}+\omega_{c}^{2}}e^{-\nu t}\left(-\nu\sin\omega_{c}t+\omega_{c}\cos\omega_{c}t\right)+e^{-2\nu t}\right|

\displaystyle\quad\quad\times\nu\left|k_{\perp}\right|^{2}\left|K^{\nu}(t,k)\right|

\displaystyle\quad+\left(\left|k_{\perp}\right|^{2}\left|2\omega_{c}\sin\omega_{c}te^{-\nu t}+2\nu\cos\omega_{c}te^{-\nu t}-2\nu e^{-2\nu t}\right|+k_{3}^{2}e^{-\nu t}\left(\frac{1-e^{-\nu t}}{\nu}\right)\right)\left|K^{\nu}(t,k)\right|

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{\left|k\right|^{2}}\left[\left[\left|k_{\perp}\right|^{2}\nu e^{-\nu t}+e^{-\nu t}\right]+k_{3}^{2}e^{-\nu t}\right]

\displaystyle\quad\quad\times S(t,k)\exp\left[-\pi\left(\frac{\left|k_{\perp}\right|^{2}}{\nu^{2}+\omega_{c}^{2}}\left(1-2\cos\omega_{c}te^{-\nu t}+e^{-2\nu t}\right)+k_{3}^{2}\left(\frac{1-e^{-\nu t}}{\nu}\right)^{2}\right)\right]

\displaystyle:=\mathcal{K}_{1}+\mathcal{K}_{2}+\mathcal{K}_{3}+\mathcal{K}_{4}.

By Lemma
3.5
, for
0<t<\nu^{-1/2}
we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{1}

\displaystyle\lesssim\nu k_{3}^{2}t^{2}\exp\left(-\delta_{0}\min(\nu k_{3}^{2}t^{3},\nu^{-1}k_{3}^{2}t)\right)\left(\nu+\frac{1}{\left|k\right|^{2}}+k_{3}^{2}t\right)\exp(-k_{3}^{2}t^{2}),

(3.112)

and hence (using Lemma
3.5
for
t>\nu^{-1/2}
as above ),

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathcal{K}_{1}(t,k)dt\lesssim 1.

(3.113)

Then, by Lemma
3.5
, for
0<t<\nu^{-1/2}
we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{2}(t,k)

\displaystyle=\nu\left|k_{\perp}\right|^{2}\left|1-2\cos\omega_{c}te^{-\nu t}+e^{-2\nu t}\right|\left|K^{\nu}(t,k)\right|

(3.114)

\displaystyle\lesssim\nu\left|k_{\perp}\right|^{2}\left(\mathbf{1}_{t<\omega_{c}/2}t^{2}+\mathbf{1}_{t>\omega_{c}/2}\right)\left|K^{\nu}(t,k)\right|

(3.115)

\displaystyle\lesssim\exp\left[-\delta_{0}\nu\left|k_{\perp}\right|^{2}\min(t^{3},t)\right]\nu\left|k_{\perp}\right|^{2}\left(\mathbf{1}_{t<\omega_{c}/2}t^{2}+\mathbf{1}_{t>\omega_{c}/2}\right)

(3.116)

\displaystyle\quad\times\frac{1}{\left|k\right|^{2}}\left(\left((\nu\left|k_{\perp}\right|^{2}t+1)\mathbf{1}_{t<\omega_{c}/2}t+\mathbf{1}_{t>\omega_{c}/2}\right)+1+k_{3}^{2}t\right)e^{-k_{3}^{2}t^{2}}

(3.117)

\displaystyle\lesssim\left(1+k_{3}^{2}t\right)e^{-k_{3}^{2}t^{2}},

(3.118)

Hence, (using Lemma
3.5
as above)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathcal{K}_{2}(t,k)dt

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{0}^{\nu^{-1/2}}\mathcal{K}_{2}(t,k)dt+O(\frac{\nu}{\left|k\right|^{2}})\lesssim 1.

(3.119)

Turn next to
\mathcal{K}_{3}
.
Here, for
t<\nu^{-1/2}
, by Lemma
3.5
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{3}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left|k_{\perp}\right|^{2}\left(\mathbf{1}_{t<\omega_{c}/2}t+\mathbf{1}_{t>\omega_{c}/2}\right)\frac{S(t,k)}{\left|k\right|^{2}}\left(\left|k_{\perp}\right|^{2}\left(\mathbf{1}_{t<\omega_{c}/2}(\nu t^{2}+t)+\mathbf{1}_{t>\omega_{c}/2}\right)+k_{3}^{2}t\right)e^{-k_{3}^{2}t^{2}}+k_{3}^{2}te^{-k_{3}^{2}t^{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim(1+k_{3}^{2}t)e^{-k_{3}^{2}t^{2}}.

Hence, using Lemma
3.5
as above for
t>\nu^{-1/2}
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathcal{K}_{3}(t,k)dt

\displaystyle\lesssim 1.

(3.120)

The last term,
\mathcal{K}_{4}
is similar but easier and is hence omitted for brevity.
∎

Proof of Theorem
2
(ii)
.

Inequality (
1.13
) will follow from Lemmas
3.7
and
3.6
provided we prove that there exists
\nu_{0}>0
such that the following holds (where
\kappa
is given in Lemma
2.11
):

\displaystyle\inf_{\nu\in(0,\nu_{0})}\inf_{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ast}^{3}:k_{3}\neq 0}\inf_{\textup{Re}\,z\geq 0}\left|1-\mathcal{L}[K^{\nu,\delta}](z,k)\right|\geq\kappa/2.

(3.121)

Lemmas
3.8
and
3.9
show that there exists an
M>0
such that for all
\nu_{0}
sufficiently small,

\displaystyle\inf_{\nu\in(0,\nu_{0})}\inf_{k:k_{3}\neq 0\&\left|k\right|\geq M}\inf_{z:\textup{Re}\,z\geq 0\,\&\,\left|\textup{Im}\,z\right|\geq M}\left|1-\mathcal{L}[K^{\nu,\delta}](z,k)\right|\geq\kappa/2.

(3.122)

Next, we will obtain (
3.121
) on
\left|\textup{Im}\,z\right|\leq M
and
\left|k\right|\leq M
by approximation from the collisionless case.
Ultimately, we will apply the dominated convergence theorem.
By Lemma
3.5
and calculations analogous to those in Lemma
3.8
,

\displaystyle\left|K^{\nu,\delta}(t,k)\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim\langle{\frac{1-e^{-\nu t}}{\nu}}\rangle\exp\left(-\delta_{0}\min(\nu k_{3}^{2}t^{3},\nu^{-1}k_{3}^{2}t)\right)

(3.123)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\times\exp\left[-\pi\left(\frac{\left|k_{\perp}\right|^{2}}{\nu^{2}+\omega_{c}^{2}}\left(1-2\cos\omega_{c}te^{-\nu t}+e^{-2\nu t}\right)+k_{3}^{2}\left(\frac{1-e^{-\nu t}}{\nu}\right)^{2}\right)\right]

(3.124)

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{\left|k_{3}\right|}\exp\left(-\delta_{0}\min(\nu k_{3}^{2}t^{3},\nu^{-1}k_{3}^{2}t)\right)\exp\left[-k_{3}^{2}\left(\frac{1-e^{-\nu t}}{\nu}\right)^{2}\right]

(3.125)

\displaystyle\lesssim\mathbf{1}_{\nu^{1/2}t<1}\frac{1}{\left|k_{3}\right|}\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}k_{3}^{2}t^{2}\right]+\mathbf{1}_{\nu^{1/2}t>1}\frac{1}{\left|k_{3}\right|}\exp\left(-\delta_{0}\min(\nu k_{3}^{2}t^{3},\nu^{-1}k_{3}^{2}t)\right).

(3.126)

Consider the following integral for
\lambda\geq 0
; we have for
\nu
sufficiently small:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}[K^{\nu,\delta}](z,k)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-(\lambda+i\omega)t}K^{\nu,\delta}(t,k)dt

(3.127)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\nu^{-1/2}}e^{-(\lambda+i\omega)t}K^{\nu,\delta}(t,k)dt+O\left(\frac{1}{k_{3}^{2}}\exp[-k_{3}^{2}\nu^{-1/12}]\right).

(3.128)

By (
3.126
), we can then apply the dominated convergence theorem to the first term and deduce for all
z,k
with
\textup{Re}\,z\geq 0
,

\lim_{\nu\rightarrow 0}\mathcal{L}[K^{\nu,\delta}](z,k)=\mathcal{L}[K^{0}](z,k)
pointwise.
Moreover, using (
3.126
), it is clear that we have uniform (in
\nu
,
k
, and
z
) bounds on the derivatives

\displaystyle\sup_{z\in\mathbb{C}:\textup{Im}\,z\leq M\,\&\,0\leq\textup{Re}\,z\leq M}\left|\partial_{z}^{j}\mathcal{L}[K^{\nu,\delta}](z,k)\right|\lesssim_{j}1.

(3.129)

Using the derivative estimates and the restrictions
\left|\textup{Im}\,z\right|\leq M
,
\left|k\right|\leq M
,
0\leq\textup{Re}\,z\leq M
(it is clear that we can add this additional restriction by the definition of the Laplace transform) imply we can upgrade the pointwise convergence to uniform convergence.
That is, for all
\epsilon>0
, there exists
\nu_{\epsilon}
such that

\displaystyle\sup_{\nu\in(0,\nu_{\epsilon})}\sup_{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ast}^{3}:k_{3}\neq 0\&\left|k\right|\leq M}\sup_{z\in\mathbb{C}:\textup{Re}\,z\geq 0\,\&\,\left|z\right|\leq M}\left|\mathcal{L}[K^{\nu}](z,k)-\mathcal{L}[K^{0}](z,k)\right|\leq\epsilon.

(3.130)

The estimate (
3.121
) then follows from Lemma
2.11
. As discussed above, this completes the proof of (
1.13
), and hence, Theorem
2
.
∎

Appendix A
Identities and estimates for the generalized Bessel functions

Recall the generalized Bessel functions

I_{\alpha}(x)=i^{-\alpha}J_{\alpha}(ix)=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{m!\Gamma(m+\alpha+1)}\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)^{2m+\alpha}

(A.1)

where
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
and
x\in\mathbb{C}
.
Theses functions enjoy the following identities:

\displaystyle\exp{(z\cos\theta)}=I_{0}(z)+2\sum_{1}^{\infty}I_{n}(z)\cos n\theta

(A.2)

\displaystyle I_{n-1}(a)-I_{n+1}(a)=\frac{2n}{a}I_{n}(a)

(A.3)

which is used crucially in the proof.
From (
A.2
), we have

\text{e}^{a}=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}I_{n}(a)=I_{0}(a)+2\sum_{1}^{\infty}I_{n}(a).

(A.4)

Note that since each
I_{n}(a)
is positive, it is straightforward to get

I_{n}(a)\leq\text{e}^{a}

(A.5)

for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
a\in\mathbb{R}
.
By summing up (
A.3
) in
n
, we get

I_{0}(a)+I_{1}(a)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(I_{n-1}(a)-I_{n+1}(a))=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{2n}{a}I_{n}(a).

(A.6)

For
I_{0}(a)
and
I_{1}(a)
, we have the following bounds.

Lemma A.1
.

The following inequalities hold

\displaystyle I_{0}(a)\lesssim\frac{1}{\sqrt{a}}\text{e}^{a};

(A.7)

\displaystyle I_{1}(a)\lesssim\frac{1}{\sqrt{a}}\text{e}^{a}.

(A.8)

Proof.

By the definition of
I_{n}(a)
, we obtain

\displaystyle I_{0}(a)=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{m!^{2}}\left(\frac{a}{2}\right)^{2m}\leq\text{e}^{a/2}\sup_{m\geq 0}\frac{1}{m!}\left(\frac{a}{2}\right)^{m}.

(A.9)

Denote
m_{0}=[a/2]
and observe that the sequence
\frac{1}{m!}\left(\frac{a}{2}\right)^{m}
is increasing for
m\leq m_{0}
and decreasing for
m\geq m_{0}
and hence,

\displaystyle\sup_{m}\frac{1}{m!}\left(\frac{a}{2}\right)^{m}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m_{0}!}\left(\frac{a}{2}\right)^{m_{0}}\lesssim\frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{m_{0}}}\left(\frac{e}{m_{0}}\right)^{m_{0}}\left(\frac{a}{2}\right)^{m_{0}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{m_{0}}}\text{e}^{a/2}\left(\frac{a}{2m_{0}}\right)^{m_{0}}\lesssim\frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{m_{0}}}\text{e}^{a/2}.

(A.10)

Therefore, we may bound
I_{0}(a)
as

I_{0}(a)\lesssim\frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{m_{0}}}\text{e}^{a}

(A.11)

from where (
A.7
) follows.
Similar argument gives

I_{1}(a)\lesssim\frac{1}{\sqrt{a}}\text{e}^{a}

(A.12)

completing the proof of the lemma.
∎
