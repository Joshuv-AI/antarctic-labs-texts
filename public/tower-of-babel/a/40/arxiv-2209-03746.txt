Coherence, superposition, and Löwdin symmetric orthogonalization
Gökhan Torun

Abstract
The notions of coherence and superposition are conceptually the same; however, an important distinction exists between their resource-theoretic formulations. Namely, while basis states are orthogonal in the resource theory of coherence, they are not necessarily orthogonal in the resource theory of superposition. Owing to the nonorthogonality, the manipulation and characterization of superposition states require significant efforts. Here, we demonstrate that the Löwdin symmetric orthogonalization (LSO) method offers a useful means for characterizing pure superposition states. The principal property of LSO is that the structure and symmetry of the original nonorthogonal basis states are preserved to the greatest extent possible, which prompts us to study the role of LSO in identifying the hierarchical relations of resource states. Notably, we reveal that the maximally coherent states turn into the states with maximal superposition with the help of LSO: in other words, they are equivalent under the action of symmetric orthogonalization. Our results facilitate further connections between coherence and superposition, where LSO is the main tool.

Background

II.1
Resource theory of superposition

The essentials of RTS was introduced and discussed in Ref.
[
18
]
. Here we give a succinct overview of RTS, including the free states (
\mathcal{F}
), resource states (
\mathcal{R}
), and the restricted set of free operations (
\mathcal{O}
), which are the three major elements of a general quantum resource theory
[
4
]
.
As in
[
18
]
, let
\mathcal{H}_{d}
be a
d
-dimensional Hilbert space and
\{\mathinner{|{c_{i}}\rangle}:i=0,1,\dots,d-1\}
be the set of nonorthogonal, normalized, and linearly independent basis of
\mathcal{H}_{d}
.
Then, states defined as
\rho=\sum_{i}\rho_{i}|{c_{i}}\rangle\langle{c_{i}}|
are called superposition-free, where
\rho_{i}\geq 0
form a probability distribution. The set of superposition-free density operators is denoted by
\mathcal{F}
and forms the set of free states. All density operators which are not an element of
\mathcal{F}
are called
superposition states and form the set of resource states
\mathcal{R}
.
A linear combination of
\{\mathinner{|{c_{i}}\rangle}\}
gives us the pure superposition states

\displaystyle\mathinner{|{\psi}\rangle}=\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}\psi_{i}\mathinner{|{c_{i}}\rangle},

(1)

where the coefficients
\{\psi_{i}\}
are complex number. Let
S
be overlap matrix of basis states
\{\mathinner{|{c_{i}}\rangle}\}
, i.e., Gram matrix,
with the elements
S_{ij}=\langle{c_{i}}|c_{j}\rangle
. Then the normalization condition reads
\langle{\psi}|\psi\rangle=\sum_{i,j=0}^{d-1}\psi_{i}^{\ast}S_{ij}\psi_{j}=1
, where
S_{ij}
are complex in general.

In the context of RTS, a Kraus operator
K_{n}
is called superposition-free if
K_{n}\rho K_{n}^{\dagger}\in\mathcal{F}

for all
\rho\in\mathcal{F}
, and is of the form

\displaystyle K_{n}=\sum_{k}c_{k,n}{\mathinner{|{c_{f_{n}(k)}}\rangle}\mathinner{\langle{c_{k}^{\perp}}|}},

(2)

where
c_{k,n}
are complex number,
{f_{n}(k)}
are arbitrary index functions
[
18
]
, and
\langle{c_{i}^{\perp}}|c_{j}\rangle=\zeta_{i}\delta_{ij}
for
\zeta_{i}\in\mathbb{C}
where the vectors
\mathinner{|{c_{k}^{\perp}}\rangle}
are normalized.
Also, a quantum operation
\Phi(\cdot)
is called superposition-free if it is trace preserving and can be written
such that
\Phi(\rho)=\sum_{n}K_{n}\rho K_{n}^{\dagger}
, where all
K_{n}
are free. For further reading and details, we refer the reader to Refs.
[
18
,
54
,
60
]
.

II.2
Löwdin symmetric orthogonalization

Recall that
\{\mathinner{|{c_{i}}\rangle}:i=0,1,\dots,d-1\}
be a set of nonorthogonal, normalized, and linearly independent basis vectors as in the previous section. We aim to construct an orthonormal basis set
\{\mathinner{|{l_{i}}\rangle}:i=0,1,\dots,d-1\}
by a suitable linear transformation.
To this end, we utilize the Löwdin’s symmetric orthogonalization
[
22
,
25
]
abbreviated by LSO, so that the orthogonal basis vectors
\{\mathinner{|{l_{i}}\rangle}\}
are called as “Löwdin basis”. We start by defining column vectors

\bm{C}:={\left(c_{0},c_{1},\dots,c_{d-1}\right)}^{\intercal},\quad\bm{L}:={\left(l_{0},l_{1},\dots,l_{d-1}\right)}^{\intercal}.

(3)

Here the components
\{c_{i}\}
and
\{l_{i}\}
represent the basis sets
\{\mathinner{|{c_{i}}\rangle}\}
and
\{\mathinner{|{l_{i}}\rangle}\}
, respectively.
We can define a general linear transformation
T
such that

\displaystyle\bm{L}=T\bm{C},\quad\Big(TT^{\dagger}=S^{-1}\Big).

(4)

In order to get the linear transformation
T
, we first construct the overlap matrix
S
, which is positive semidefinite in general, such that
S_{ij}=\langle{c_{i}}|c_{j}\rangle
for
i,j=0,1,\dots,d-1
. By a unitary matrix
U
, we can diagonalize the overlap matrix
S
, that is,
S_{\text{diag}}=U^{\dagger}SU
. Let
S
have eigenvalues
\{\lambda_{i}\}
. Since the eigenvalues of
S
are positive, the elements of
S_{\text{diag}}
can be replaced by
\{\lambda_{i}\}
, from which we can get
{S^{{1}/{2}}_{\text{diag}}}=\text{diag}(\sqrt{\lambda_{0}},\sqrt{\lambda_{1}},\dots,\sqrt{\lambda_{d-1}})
. By using this matrix, we define the matrix
{S^{{1}/{2}}}=US^{{1}/{2}}_{\text{diag}}U^{\dagger}
and finally get
{S^{-{1}/{2}}}={(S^{{1}/{2}})}^{-1}
. Then the linear transformation given by Eq. (
4
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\big[\bm{L}\big]_{i}=\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}\big[{S^{-{1}/{2}}}\big]_{ij}\big[\bm{C}\big]_{j},

(5)

for
i,j=0,1,\dots,d-1
, which is invertible. Needless to say, here
[{S^{-{1}/{2}}}]_{ij}
is the (
i,j
)-th matrix element of
{S^{-{1}/{2}}}
and
[\bm{L}]_{i}
(
[\bm{C}]_{i}
) is the
i
-th entry of
\bm{L}
(
\bm{C}
). Thus, we can obtain the orthogonal basis vectors
\{\mathinner{|{l_{i}}\rangle}\}
as a linear combination of the nonorthogonal basis vectors
\{\mathinner{|{c_{i}}\rangle}\}
, that is, from Eq. (
5
) one has
\mathinner{|{l_{i}}\rangle}=\sum_{j}[{S^{-{1}/{2}}}]_{ij}\mathinner{|{c_{j}}\rangle}
.

{c_{0}}
and
{c_{1}}
with reciprocal states
{c_{0}^{\perp}}
and
{c_{1}^{\perp}}

[
18
]
. The basis sets
\{c_{0},c_{1}\}
and
\{c_{0}^{\perp},c_{1}^{\perp}\}
can be expressed in terms of symmetrically orthonormalized vectors
{l_{0}}
and
{l_{1}}
. In order to satisfy Eq. (
6
) one needs to take
\delta_{0}=\delta_{1}=\delta
, and thus one has
\theta+2\delta={\pi}/{2}
. For normalized vectors
{c_{0}}
and
{c_{1}}
, the inner product (i.e., overlap) reads
\langle{c_{0}}|c_{1}\rangle=\cos\theta
with
\theta\in(0,\pi)
. Similarly, for normalized vectors
{c_{0}^{\perp}}
and
{c_{1}^{\perp}}
, one has
\gamma_{0}=\gamma_{1}=\delta
and
\langle{c_{0}^{\perp}}|c_{1}^{\perp}\rangle=-\cos\theta
. Also, here
\langle{c_{i}}|c_{i}^{\perp}\rangle=\sin\theta
and
\langle{c_{i}}|c_{j}^{\perp}\rangle=0
for
i,j=0,1
(
i\neq j
). Thus, the symmetric orthonormalization procedure generates new vectors
{l_{0}}
and
{l_{1}}
which are symmetrically rotated respect to
{c_{0}}
and
{c_{1}}
.

In fact, the ways of taking a nonorthogonal set into an orthonormal set are multifarious. The LSO has a meaningful feature among all possible orthogonalization methods: namely, the symmetric orthogonalization ensures

\displaystyle\sum_{i}\|c_{i}-l_{i}\|^{2}=\min,

(6)

where
\|c_{i}-l_{i}\|^{2}\equiv\langle{c_{i}-l_{i}}|c_{i}-l_{i}\rangle
. From Eq. (
6
), one can infer that the symmetrically orthogonalized vectors
\{\mathinner{|{l_{i}}\rangle}\}
are the least distant in the Hilbert space from the original nonorthogonal vectors
\{\mathinner{|{c_{i}}\rangle}\}
, i.e., LSO implies the gentlest pushing of the directions of the nonorthogonal vectors in order to get them orthogonal
[
22
,
25
]
.

One can consider two nonorthogonal states
\mathinner{|{c_{0}}\rangle}
and
\mathinner{|{c_{1}}\rangle}
, as in Fig.

\mathinner{|{c_{0}}\rangle}
and
\mathinner{|{c_{1}}\rangle}
, then orthogonal states
\mathinner{|{l_{0}}\rangle}
and
\mathinner{|{l_{1}}\rangle}
are generated such that
\|c_{0}-l_{0}\|^{2}+\|c_{1}-l_{1}\|^{2}=\min
. Moreover, another two nonorthogonal basis states
\mathinner{|{c_{0}^{\perp}}\rangle}
and
\mathinner{|{c_{1}^{\perp}}\rangle}
— reciprocal states — also exist which take part in the definition of superposition-free operations for qubit systems. Thus, it is readily apparent that the three basis sets
\{\mathinner{|{c_{0}}\rangle},\mathinner{|{c_{1}}\rangle}\}
,
\{\mathinner{|{c_{0}^{\perp}}\rangle},\mathinner{|{c_{1}^{\perp}}\rangle}\}
, and
\{\mathinner{|{l_{0}}\rangle},\mathinner{|{l_{1}}\rangle}\}
can be expressed in terms of each other, where LSO brings out these connections in the sense of Eqs. (

II.3
Resource theory of coherence

The resource theory of coherence (RTC), which is a special case of RTS, is a basis dependent concept. Importantly, one may choose the reference basis in accordance with the physics of the problem under consideration. In that connection, we here designate the Löwdin basis as our preferred (complete and orthonormal) basis. Namely, let
\{\mathinner{|{l_{k}}\rangle}:k=0,1,\dots,d-1\}
be a particular orthonormal basis of the
d
-dimensional Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{d}
; then the quantum states that are diagonal with respect to this specific basis
\{\mathinner{|{l_{k}}\rangle}\}
are called as incoherent, constituting a set labeled by
\mathcal{I}

[
6
,
7
]
. Hence, all incoherent states
\rho\in\mathcal{I}
are of the form

\rho=\sum_{k=0}^{d-1}p_{k}\mathinner{|{l_{k}}\rangle}\mathinner{\langle{l_{k}}|}
, where
p_{k}\in[0,1]
and
\sum_{k}p_{k}=1
. On the other hand, a finite
d
-dimensional pure coherent state is given by

\displaystyle\mathinner{|{\phi}\rangle}=\sum_{k=0}^{d-1}\phi_{k}e^{i\theta_{k}}\mathinner{|{l_{k}}\rangle},

(7)

where
\{\phi_{k}:k=0,1,\dots,d-1\}
are real numbers such that
\phi_{k}\geq\phi_{k+1}>0
, satisfying
\sum_{k}\phi_{k}^{2}=1
.
Note that all complex phases
\theta_{k}\in[0,2\pi)
in Eq. (
7
) can be eliminated by incoherent operations.
We denote by
\mathinner{|{\Phi_{d}}\rangle}
the maximally coherent state in the reference basis with entries
\phi_{k}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}
,
that is,

\displaystyle\mathinner{|{\Phi_{d}}\rangle}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_{k=0}^{d-1}e^{i\theta_{k}}\mathinner{|{l_{k}}\rangle}.

(8)

Moreover, since all other
d
-dimensional coherent states can be generated from
\mathinner{|{\Phi_{d}}\rangle}
by means of the free operations, this definition regards as a unit of RTC — coherence bit.

Following standard definitions of RTC, Kraus operators
\{K_{n}\}
are called incoherent operators,
which are characterized as the set of trace preserving completely positive maps, such that

\displaystyle\rho\rightarrow\rho_{n}=\frac{K_{n}\rho K_{n}^{{\dagger}}}{\text{Tr}[K_{n}\rho K_{n}^{{\dagger}}]}\in\mathcal{I}

(9)

for all
n
, where
\rho\in\mathcal{I}
and
\sum_{n}K_{n}^{{\dagger}}K_{n}=\text{1}
. Also, the quantum operations
\Lambda(\cdot)
consisting of the Kraus operators
\{K_{i}\}
satisfying Eq. (
9
) are the free operations in the context of RTC.

II.4
Quantifying superposition and coherence

At this point, we give two of superposition (and coherence) measures, where
the conditions when a function
M
is called a superposition measure were detailed in Refs.
[
6
,
18
]
.
In fact, the following two measures of superposition were introduced
[
18
]
by extending the method used for quantifying coherence
[
7
]
.
Within certain types of resource measures, the
l_{1}
measure of superposition is defined as

\displaystyle M_{l_{1}}(\rho)=\sum_{i\neq j}|\rho_{ij}|,

(10)

for
\rho=\sum_{i,j}\rho_{ij}\mathinner{|{c_{i}}\rangle}\mathinner{\langle{c_{j}}|}

[
18
]
. Also, Eq. (
10
) corresponds to the
l_{1}
norm of coherence such that
\rho=\sum_{i,j}\rho_{ij}\mathinner{|{l_{i}}\rangle}\mathinner{\langle{l_{j}}|}

[
7
]
.
The relative entropy of superposition is given by

\displaystyle M_{\text{rel.ent}}(\rho)=\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal{F}}S(\rho\|\sigma),

(11)

where
S(\rho\|\sigma)=\text{tr}[\rho\log\rho]-\text{tr}[\rho\log\sigma]
denotes the quantum relative entropy.
From the definition of (
11
), the relative entropy of coherence reads
S(\rho_{\text{diag}})-S(\rho)
, where incoherent state
\rho_{\text{diag}}
denotes the state obtained from
\rho
by deleting all off-diagonal elements
[
7
]
.

III
Setting the Scene: Two Representations of a Given Quantum state

The purpose of this section is to explicitly construct two different representations of a given pure quantum state such that one of the two representations involves a nonorthogonal basis consisting of vectors
\{\mathinner{|{c_{0}}\rangle},\dots\mathinner{|{c_{d-1}}\rangle}\}
and the other involves an orthogonal basis consisting of vectors
\{\mathinner{|{l_{0}}\rangle},\dots,\mathinner{|{l_{d-1}}\rangle}\}
, where LSO is the applied technique that relates these to each other.

III.1
Qubit systems

To formulate our approach based on LSO, it is natural to start with exploring the two-dimensional systems. Let us then consider the nonorthogonal basis states
\mathinner{|{c_{0}}\rangle}
and
\mathinner{|{c_{1}}\rangle}
with
\langle{c_{0}}|c_{1}\rangle=s\in(-1,1)
. After concluding the symmetric orthogonalization given by Eq. (
5
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathinner{|{l_{0}}\rangle}=\alpha\mathinner{|{c_{0}}\rangle}+\beta\mathinner{|{c_{1}}\rangle},\quad\mathinner{|{l_{1}}\rangle}=\beta\mathinner{|{c_{0}}\rangle}+\alpha\mathinner{|{c_{1}}\rangle},

(12)

where
\alpha=({1}/{\sqrt{\lambda_{1}}}+{1}/{\sqrt{\lambda_{0}}})/2
and
\beta=({1}/{\sqrt{\lambda_{1}}}-{1}/{\sqrt{\lambda_{0}}})/2
.
Here,
\lambda_{0}={1-s}
and
\lambda_{1}={1+s}
are the eigenvalues of the overlap matrix
S
.
Thus, from Eq. (
12
), we can complete the linear map
\{\mathinner{|{c_{0}}\rangle},\mathinner{|{c_{1}}\rangle}\}\overset{\text{LSO}^{\mapsto}}{\longrightarrow}\{\mathinner{|{l_{0}}\rangle},\mathinner{|{l_{1}}\rangle}\}
. Notice that Löwdin basis states
\mathinner{|{l_{0}}\rangle}
and
\mathinner{|{l_{1}}\rangle}
given in Eq. (

measures of superposition are equal,
M_{l_{1}}(\mathinner{|{l_{0}}\rangle})=M_{l_{1}}(\mathinner{|{l_{1}}\rangle})=|{s}/{(1-s^{2})}|
. Also, from Eq. (
12
) it is easy to obtain

\displaystyle\mathinner{|{c_{0}}\rangle}=\alpha^{\prime}\mathinner{|{l_{0}}\rangle}-\beta^{\prime}\mathinner{|{l_{1}}\rangle},\quad\mathinner{|{c_{1}}\rangle}=\alpha^{\prime}\mathinner{|{l_{1}}\rangle}-\beta^{\prime}\mathinner{|{l_{0}}\rangle},

(13)

where
\alpha^{\prime}=\sqrt{1-s^{2}}\alpha
and
\beta^{\prime}=\sqrt{1-s^{2}}\beta
. Again, notice that the nonorthogonal basis states
\mathinner{|{c_{0}}\rangle}
and
\mathinner{|{c_{1}}\rangle}
(which are pure superposition-free states as well
[
18
]
) given in Eq. (

) are coherent states in terms of Löwdin basis, and their

measures of coherence are equal,
M_{l_{1}}(\mathinner{|{c_{0}}\rangle})=M_{l_{1}}(\mathinner{|{c_{1}}\rangle})=|{s}|
.

Now consider a given resource state
\mathinner{|{\tau_{2}}\rangle}
for which the (normalized) superposition state
\mathinner{|{\psi}\rangle}=\psi_{0}\mathinner{|{c_{0}}\rangle}+\psi_{1}\mathinner{|{c_{1}}\rangle}

\langle{c_{0}}|c_{1}\rangle=s\in(-1,1)

\mathinner{|{\psi}\rangle}\overset{\text{LSO}^{\mapsto}}{\longrightarrow}\mathinner{|{\bar{\psi}}\rangle}

\displaystyle\mathinner{|{\psi}\rangle}\overset{\text{LSO}^{\mapsto}}{\longrightarrow}\mathinner{|{\bar{\psi}}\rangle}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\big(\alpha^{\prime}\psi_{0}-\beta^{\prime}\psi_{1}\big)\mathinner{|{l_{0}}\rangle}+\big(\alpha^{\prime}\psi_{1}-\beta^{\prime}\psi_{0}\big)\mathinner{|{l_{1}}\rangle}

(14)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\bar{\psi}}_{0}\mathinner{|{l_{0}}\rangle}+{\bar{\psi}}_{1}\mathinner{|{l_{1}}\rangle}.

Here in Eq. (

) one can see that
{\bar{\psi}}_{0}
(
{\bar{\psi}}_{1}
) is equal to the conjugate of

(

) satisfying

and after applying

\displaystyle\mathinner{|{\bar{\psi}}\rangle}\overset{\text{LSO}^{\mapsfrom}}{\longrightarrow}\mathinner{|{\psi}\rangle}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\big(\alpha{\bar{\psi}}_{0}+\beta{\bar{\psi}}_{1}\big)\mathinner{|{c_{0}}\rangle}+\big(\alpha{\bar{\psi}}_{1}+\beta{\bar{\psi}}_{0}\big)\mathinner{|{c_{1}}\rangle}

(15)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\psi}_{0}\mathinner{|{c_{0}}\rangle}+{\psi}_{1}\mathinner{|{c_{1}}\rangle}.

Notice that, while the linear map
\text{LSO}^{\mapsto}
seen in Eq. (
14
) corresponds the LSO itself, the linear map
\text{LSO}^{\mapsfrom}
here in Eq. (
15
) corresponds to the LSO, but in the opposite direction.
As a consequence, two pure resource states
\mathinner{|{\psi}\rangle}
and
\mathinner{|{\bar{\psi}}\rangle}
linked in this way represent the same resource state
\mathinner{|{\tau_{2}}\rangle}
. Namely, the former written in terms of nonorthogonal basis, and therefore describes a superposition state; the latter written in terms of Löwdin basis instead of nonorthogonal basis, and therefore describes a coherent state:

\mathinner{|{\tau_{2}}\rangle}\equiv\begin{cases}\mathinner{|{\psi}\rangle}:&\text{with respect to}\,\{\mathinner{|{c_{0}}\rangle},\mathinner{|{c_{1}}\rangle}\},\\
\mathinner{|{\bar{\psi}}\rangle}:&\text{with respect to}\,\{\mathinner{|{l_{0}}\rangle},\mathinner{|{l_{1}}\rangle}\},\end{cases}

(16)

which we write
\mathinner{|{\psi}\rangle}\dashleftarrow\mathinner{|{\tau_{2}}\rangle}\dashrightarrow\mathinner{|{\bar{\psi}}\rangle}
in short. In addition to this,
l_{1}
measure of superposition of
\mathinner{|{\psi}\rangle}
and
l_{1}
measure of coherence of
\mathinner{|{\bar{\psi}}\rangle}
are

\displaystyle M_{l_{1}}(\mathinner{|{\psi}\rangle})=2|\psi_{0}\psi_{1}|,\quad M_{l_{1}}(\mathinner{|{\bar{\psi}}\rangle})=2|\psi_{0}\psi_{1}(1-s^{2})+\frac{s}{2}|,\quad

(17)

where
M_{l_{1}}(\mathinner{|{\psi}\rangle})=M_{l_{1}}(\mathinner{|{\bar{\psi}}\rangle})
for
s=0
as expected.
We conclude this consideration for qubit systems here by stating that we address the practical advantages of this connection, in Section
IV
, simply expressed as given by Eq. (
16
).

III.2
Qudit systems

Having introduced our strategy for two-dimensional systems, it is now time to look at
d
-dimensional systems. In this general case, Eq. (
14
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\mathinner{|{\psi}\rangle}=\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}\psi_{j}\mathinner{|{c_{j}}\rangle}\overset{\text{LSO}^{\mapsto}}{\longrightarrow}\mathinner{|{\bar{\psi}}\rangle}=\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}{\bar{\psi}}_{i}\mathinner{|{l_{i}}\rangle}.

(18)

Here the coefficients
{\bar{\psi}}_{i}
are functions of
\psi_{j}
and
\lambda_{k}
,
that is,
{\bar{\psi}}_{i}={\bar{\psi}}_{i}(\psi_{0},\psi_{1},\dots,\psi_{d-1},\lambda_{0},\lambda_{d-1})
,
satisfying
\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}|{\bar{\psi}}_{i}|^{2}=1
, where
\{\lambda_{k}\}
are the eigenvalues of the overlap matrix
S
. As a result,
LSO given in Eq. (
18
) leads the superposition state
\mathinner{|{\psi}\rangle}
to the coherent state
\mathinner{|{\bar{\psi}}\rangle}
, basically
\mathinner{|{\psi}\rangle}\dashleftarrow\mathinner{|{\tau_{d}}\rangle}\dashrightarrow\mathinner{|{\bar{\psi}}\rangle}
.
Given the overlap matrix
S
and the superposition state
\mathinner{|{\psi}\rangle}
for dimension
d
, the explicit forms of the coefficients
{\bar{\psi}}_{i}
follow by Eq. (
5
).

For the simplicity of the discussion, we limit ourselves with the inner product setting
\langle{c_{i}}|c_{j}\rangle=s\in\mathbb{R}
. Given the linear independence of basis states, the interval
(\frac{1}{1-d},1)
encompasses the allowable values for
s
. This range signifies that
s
lies between
\frac{1}{1-d}
and
1
, exclusively. Consequently, the elements of the overlap matrix,
S_{ij}=\langle{c_{i}}|c_{j}\rangle
, are determined to be real values represented by
s
. In such an instance, the eigenvalues of the constructed overlap matrix
S
are found to be
\lambda_{0}=\lambda_{1}=\dots=\lambda_{d-2}={1-s}
and
\lambda_{d-1}={1+(d-1)s}
. This result highlights the equality of eigenvalues
\lambda_{0}
to
\lambda_{d-2}
, each equivalent to
1-s
, while the eigenvalue
\lambda_{d-1}
is determined as
1+(d-1)s
.

Now, the step-by-step derivation of the matrix
S^{-1/2}
(i.e., the transformation matrix
T
) is presented herein, demonstrating the systematic process of obtaining the matrix
S^{-1/2}
for any given
d\geq 2
level as well. The overlap matrix
S
can be diagonalized by a unitary matrix
U
, yielding the diagonal matrix
S_{\text{diag}}=U^{\dagger}SU
. Notably, the columns of the matrix
U
correspond to the eigenvectors of the overlap matrix. This diagonalization process, facilitated by the unitary transformation, enables us to express the overlap matrix
S
in a simplified and more interpretable form, with the eigenvectors serving as the basis for this transformation. Since the eigenvalues of the overlap matrix
S
are positively defined, it is feasible to replace the elements in
S_{\text{diag}}
with the corresponding eigenvalues, denoted as
\left\{\lambda_{i}\right\}
. Thus, by taking the square root of each eigenvalue, the resulting matrix
S^{1/2}_{\text{diag}}
can be obtained as
\text{diag}(\sqrt{\lambda_{0}},\sqrt{\lambda_{1}},\dots,\sqrt{\lambda_{d-1}})
. After that, utilizing the matrix
S^{1/2}_{\text{diag}}
, we proceed to establish the matrix
S^{1/2}
through the expression
{S^{{1}/{2}}}=US^{{1}/{2}}_{\text{diag}}U^{\dagger}
. Subsequently, we ultimately arrive at the matrix
{S^{{-1}/{2}}}
by computing the inverse of
{S^{{1}/{2}}}
denoted as
{\left(S^{{1}/{2}}\right)}^{-1}
. Finally, setting

\displaystyle\mu:=\frac{1}{d}\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+(d-1)s}}+\frac{d-1}{\sqrt{1-s}}\right]

(19)

and

\displaystyle\kappa:=\frac{1}{d}\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+(d-1)s}}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-s}}\right],

(20)

the
d\times d
transformation matrix
S^{-1/2}
can be brought to the following form:

\displaystyle S^{-1/2}=\begin{pmatrix}\mu&\kappa&\kappa&\dots&\kappa\\
\kappa&\mu&\kappa&\dots&\kappa\\
\kappa&\kappa&\mu&\dots&\kappa\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\kappa&\kappa&\kappa&\dots&\mu\\
\end{pmatrix}.

(21)

We here note that, by following the prescribed steps, the matrix
S^{-1/2}
can be effectively generated across varying dimensions and overlap settings
[
25
]
. With the help of (
21
), we obtain the following relations between the orthogonal basis states
\{\mathinner{|{l_{0}}\rangle},\mathinner{|{l_{1}}\rangle},\dots,\mathinner{|{l_{d-1}}\rangle}\}
and the nonorthogonal basis states
\{\mathinner{|{c_{0}}\rangle},\mathinner{|{c_{1}}\rangle},\dots,\mathinner{|{c_{d-1}}\rangle}\}
:

\displaystyle\mathinner{|{l_{i}}\rangle}=\mu\mathinner{|{c_{i}}\rangle}+\kappa\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=0\\
(j\neq i)\end{subarray}}^{d-1}\mathinner{|{c_{j}}\rangle}\ \ \big(i=0,1,\dots,d-1\big).

(22)

It is easy to check that the basis states
\{\mathinner{|{l_{0}}\rangle},\mathinner{|{l_{1}}\rangle},\dots,\mathinner{|{l_{d-1}}\rangle}\}
are orthonormal indeed. From Eq. (
22
), with some calculation, nonorthogonal basis states can also be obtained in terms of orthogonal basis states:

\mathinner{|{c_{i}}\rangle}={\sqrt{\lambda_{d-1}\lambda_{0}}}\bigg[{\Big(\mu+[d-2]\kappa\Big)\mathinner{|{l_{i}}\rangle}-\kappa\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=0\\
(j\neq i)\end{subarray}}^{d-1}\mathinner{|{l_{j}}\rangle}}\bigg],

(23)

for
i=0,1,\dots,d-1
. Thus, we now know the the orthogonal basis states
\{\mathinner{|{l_{0}}\rangle},\mathinner{|{l_{1}}\rangle},\dots,\mathinner{|{l_{d-1}}\rangle}\}
in terms of the nonorthogonal basis states
\{\mathinner{|{c_{0}}\rangle},\mathinner{|{c_{1}}\rangle},\dots,\mathinner{|{c_{d-1}}\rangle}\}
, and vice versa. Then, one can find the coefficients
{\bar{\psi}}_{i}
such that

{\bar{\psi}}_{i}={\sqrt{\lambda_{d-1}\lambda_{0}}}\bigg[{\Big(\mu+[d-2]\kappa\Big)\psi_{i}-\kappa\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=0\\
(j\neq i)\end{subarray}}^{d-1}\psi_{j}}\bigg],

(24)

for
i=0,1,\dots,d-1
. Note that, when the overlap matrix
S
is equal to the identity matrix
1
in Löwdin basis, we have
\lambda_{i}=1
,
\mu=1
, and
\kappa=0
, and therefore
\mathinner{|{c_{i}}\rangle}=\mathinner{|{l_{i}}\rangle}
and
{\bar{\psi}}_{i}=\psi_{i}
, i.e., two notions, superposition and coherence, coincide when
s=0
.

Regarding the above standpoint, we can deduce that if a representation of a given quantum state
\mathinner{|{\tau_{d}}\rangle}
is known as a linear combination of one of the two basis states
\{\mathinner{|{c_{0}}\rangle},\dots,\mathinner{|{c_{d-1}}\rangle}\}
and
\{\mathinner{|{l_{0}}\rangle},\dots,\mathinner{|{l_{d-1}}\rangle}\}
connected via LSO, then due to the invertibility of the LSO transformation, its representation in the other basis can be easily obtained. Indeed, the LSO method enables efficient conversion between the two bases and allows the original state to be recovered from its representation in either basis. Evidently, the LSO method offers a versatile tool for establishing useful connection(s) between RTC and RTS. In the following, we examine the scenarios where the state
\mathinner{|{\tau_{d}}\rangle}
is regarded as being maximally resourceful.

IV
Main Result: Equivalence of Maximally Resourceful States

We now proceed to our main result in which we focus our attention on the resource states with maximal superposition. This essential subject was particularly discussed in Ref.
[
60
]
,
where the eigensystems of the constructed overlap matrices involve information on the existence of maximal superposition states.
Here, we show that the same results
[
60
]
can be obtained forthwith by LSO.
Figure
2
illustrates the way that we apply to realize the form of the states with maximal superposition, i.e., golden states in RTS
[
60
]
.

FIG. 2:
By using the Löwdin symmetric orthogonalization it is possible to embody the profound connection between the maximally coherent states and states with maximal superposition. Here,
\Psi
and
\Phi
denote maximal superposition states (i.e., golden states) and maximally coherent states for
d
-dimensional systems, respectively. The situation is exactly as follows: With a quantum state
{\tau}
at hand, if we express
{\tau}
in terms of Löwdin basis
\{\mathinner{|{l_{0}}\rangle},\mathinner{|{l_{1}}\rangle},\dots,\mathinner{|{l_{d-1}}\rangle}\}

{\Phi}
. But if we express
{\tau}
in terms of nonorthogonal basis
\{\mathinner{|{c_{0}}\rangle},\mathinner{|{c_{1}}\rangle},\dots,\mathinner{|{c_{d-1}}\rangle}\}

{\Psi}
. Essentially, LSO is the only orthogonalization technique that reveals this structure. The detailed analysis with various examples is given in the paper.

Let us consider the two-dimensional case first. Employing the method of LSO, one obtains the Löwdin basis states
\{\mathinner{|{l_{0}}\rangle},\mathinner{|{l_{1}}\rangle}\}
in the basis
\{\mathinner{|{c_{0}}\rangle},\mathinner{|{c_{1}}\rangle}\}
with
\langle{c_{0}}|c_{1}\rangle=s\in(-1,1)
, as given in Eq. (
12
).
Let us then assume that one representation of a quantum state
\mathinner{|{\tau_{2}}\rangle}
at hand is given as
\eta_{0}\mathinner{|{l_{0}}\rangle}+\eta_{1}\mathinner{|{l_{1}}\rangle}
in terms of Löwdin basis. With the help of Eq. (
12
),
\mathinner{|{\tau_{2}}\rangle}
can also be written in terms of nonorthogonal basis states such that

\displaystyle\mathinner{|{\tau_{2}}\rangle}\equiv\eta_{0}\mathinner{|{l_{0}}\rangle}+\eta_{1}\mathinner{|{l_{1}}\rangle}\overset{\text{LSO}^{\mapsfrom}}{\longrightarrow}\zeta_{0}\mathinner{|{c_{0}}\rangle}+\zeta_{1}\mathinner{|{c_{1}}\rangle},

(25)

where
\zeta_{0}=\alpha\eta_{0}+\beta\eta_{1}
and
\zeta_{1}=\alpha\eta_{1}+\beta\eta_{0}
. In other words, if a given quantum state
\mathinner{|{\tau_{2}}\rangle}
could be represented in the basis
\{\mathinner{|{l_{0}}\rangle},\mathinner{|{l_{1}}\rangle}\}
, then it also has a representation in the basis
\{\mathinner{|{c_{0}}\rangle},\mathinner{|{c_{1}}\rangle}\}
. Concretely, we have the following

\displaystyle\zeta_{0}\mathinner{|{c_{0}}\rangle}+\zeta_{1}\mathinner{|{c_{1}}\rangle}\dashleftarrow\mathinner{|{\tau_{2}}\rangle}\dashrightarrow\eta_{0}\mathinner{|{l_{0}}\rangle}+\eta_{1}\mathinner{|{l_{1}}\rangle}.

(26)

Thus, LSO connects the two states
\eta_{0}\mathinner{|{l_{0}}\rangle}+\eta_{1}\mathinner{|{l_{1}}\rangle}
and
\zeta_{0}\mathinner{|{c_{0}}\rangle}+\zeta_{1}\mathinner{|{c_{1}}\rangle}

\zeta_{0}\mathinner{|{c_{0}}\rangle}+\zeta_{1}\mathinner{|{c_{1}}\rangle}
has if
\eta_{0}\mathinner{|{l_{0}}\rangle}+\eta_{1}\mathinner{|{l_{1}}\rangle}
is taken as the maximally coherent state. To see that, let us consider

\eta_{0}\mathinner{|{l_{0}}\rangle}+\eta_{1}\mathinner{|{l_{1}}\rangle}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\mathinner{|{l_{0}}\rangle}+\mathinner{|{l_{1}}\rangle}\right),

(27)

which is the maximally coherent state
\mathinner{|{\Phi_{2}}\rangle}
for qubit systems. From Eqs. (
25
) and (
27
), it is easy to get

\displaystyle\mathinner{|{\Phi_{2}}\rangle}\overset{\text{LSO}^{\mapsfrom}}{\longrightarrow}\mathinner{|{\Psi^{+}_{2}}\rangle}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2(1+s)}}(\mathinner{|{c_{0}}\rangle}+\mathinner{|{c_{1}}\rangle}).

(28)

The state
\mathinner{|{\Psi^{+}_{2}}\rangle}
given in Eq. (
28
) is the maximal superposition state for
d=2
with
\langle{c_{0}}|c_{1}\rangle=s\in(-1,0]

[
18
,
54
,
60
]
. Note that the state
(\mathinner{|{l_{0}}\rangle}-\mathinner{|{l_{1}}\rangle})/\sqrt{2}
is also the maximally coherent state for the qubit systems. In this case one has

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left({\mathinner{|{l_{0}}\rangle}-\mathinner{|{l_{1}}\rangle}}\right)\overset{\text{LSO}^{\mapsfrom}}{\longrightarrow}\mathinner{|{\Psi^{-}_{2}}\rangle}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2(1-s)}}(\mathinner{|{c_{0}}\rangle}-\mathinner{|{c_{1}}\rangle}).

(29)

The state
\mathinner{|{\Psi^{-}_{2}}\rangle}
given in Eq. (
29
) is the maximal superposition state for
d=2
with
s\in[0,1)

[
54
,
60
]
. In other words, states given in Eqs. (
28

29
) can be
transformed into any state in dimension two with
s\in(-1,0]
and
s\in[0,1)
, respectively, via superposition-free operations. As a consequence, LSO demonstrates that we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2(1\mp{s})}}(\mathinner{|{c_{0}}\rangle}\mp\mathinner{|{c_{1}}\rangle})\dashleftarrow\mathinner{|{\tau_{2}}\rangle}\dashrightarrow\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left({\mathinner{|{l_{0}}\rangle}\mp\mathinner{|{l_{1}}\rangle}}\right).

(30)

Indeed, the method of LSO is simple and straightforward, and exhibits a distinct feature — establishes the link between the maximally coherent states and the states with maximal superposition.
We conclude that, in the case of two-dimensional systems, maximally coherent states and states with maximal superposition are equivalent under LSO. This equivalence highlights the structural similarities between coherence and superposition and demonstrates the utility of LSO in analyzing resource states in RTC and RTS.

For the sake of clarity of the discussion given in this section, we now consider the qutrit case. Also, let us examine this for different overlaps setting, so that we can see how we should analyze the same in the higher dimensions. For instance, consider
\langle{c_{0}}|c_{1}\rangle=s

\displaystyle\mu=\frac{1}{3}\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+2s}}+\frac{2}{\sqrt{1-s}}\right],\ \ \kappa=\frac{1}{3}\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+2s}}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-s}}\right],\quad

\displaystyle\mathinner{|{l_{0}}\rangle}=\mu\mathinner{|{c_{0}}\rangle}+\kappa\big(\mathinner{|{c_{1}}\rangle}-\mathinner{|{c_{2}}\rangle}\big),\ \ \mathinner{|{l_{1}}\rangle}=\mu\mathinner{|{c_{1}}\rangle}+\kappa\big(\mathinner{|{c_{0}}\rangle}-\mathinner{|{c_{2}}\rangle}\big),

(32)

32

We believe that it is very valuable to bring such an alternative and simple solution to the problem, where orthogonalization methods other than LSO do not have such a special feature.

V
Conclusion and Outlook

To summarize, we have shown that LSO offers compelling advantages in revealing the strong connections between the notions of coherence and superposition within their resource-theoretic formulations. At the heart of our consideration is LSO
[
22
,
25
]
, which has a unique feature among all existing orthogonalization techniques. Namely, LSO is distinguished by its geometric approach, which enables it to maintain the original structure and symmetry of nonorthogonal basis states with minimal distortion. Taking cue from this, we have shown that one can obtain the states with maximal superposition (i.e., golden states) from the maximally coherent state by means of LSO. As we proceeded, we were able to completely solve the problem in two and three dimensions, using the methods we have developed along the way. We have obtained consistent results with the comprehensive study recently presented in Ref.
[
60
]
. The method we have devised allows for the realization of maximal superposition states in any dimension. We have also briefly discussed the
l_{1}
measures of the superposition states and the coherent states, where LSO connects them.

Looking forward, there could be various implications of the present results. For instance, practical and efficient strategies were presented to obtain one of a family of maximally coherent states of dimension
n=2,3,\dots,d
from any pure state
[
46
]
and mixed state
[
50
]
of dimension
d
. One direction for future work would be to explore whether we can obtain a set of states with maximal superposition of dimension
n\leq d
from a given (pure or mixed) superposition state, with the aid of LSO and tasks developed in Refs.
[
46
,
50
]
. It would be interesting to see the consequences of these for mixed superposition states. In light of another crucial aspect, namely resource quantifiers, the benefits of LSO can be further explored. For instance, it was shown
[
62
]
that the maximal value condition of coherence measures holds for mixed states if and only if it holds for a special subset of pure states. Analogous investigation employing LSO as an auxiliary tool could be undertaken to explore superposition in a similar vein. Moreover, LSO may provide further motivation to extend the role of coherence as a resource in metrology
[
10
,
11
]
to superposition. We believe that LSO positively impacts our understanding of the links between orthogonal and nonorthogonal settings, and could provide more for general quantum resource theories.
