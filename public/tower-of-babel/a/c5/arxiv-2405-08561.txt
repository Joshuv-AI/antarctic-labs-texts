Minimax and maximin problems for sums of translates on the real axis Thanks: The work was performed as part of research conducted in the Ural Mathematical Center with the financial support of the Ministry of Science and Higher Education of the Russian Federation (Agreement number 075-02-2024-1377)
Tatiana M. Nikiforova

Abstract
. Sums of translates generalize logarithms of weighted algebraic polynomials. The paper presents the solution to the minimax and maximin problems on the real axis for sums of translates. We prove that there is a unique function that is extremal in both problems. The key in our proof is a reduction to the problem on a segment. For this, we work out an analogue of the Mhaskar-Rakhmanov-Saff theorem, too.

26A51, 26D07, 49K35

1.
Introduction

1.1.
The minimax and maximin problems on a segment

In 1978, B. D. Bojanov generalized the famous Chebyshev’s theorem on polynomials least deviating from zero on a segment
[
1
]
.

Theorem A
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
\nu_{1},\ldots,\nu_{n}>0
be integers. There is a unique set of points
x^{*}_{1}\leqslant\ldots\leqslant x^{*}_{n}

such that

\|(x-x^{*}_{1})^{\nu_{1}}\cdot\ldots\cdot(x-x^{*}_{n})^{\nu_{n}}\|=\inf\limits_{x_{1}\leqslant\ldots\leqslant x_{n}}\|(x-x_{1})^{\nu_{1}}\cdot\ldots\cdot(x-x_{n})^{\nu_{n}}\|,

where
\|\cdot\|
is the sup norm over
[0,1]
. Moreover,
0<x^{*}_{1}<\ldots<x^{*}_{n}<1
, and the extremal polynomial
T(x):=(x-x^{*}_{1})^{\nu_{1}}\cdot\ldots\cdot(x-x^{*}_{n})^{\nu_{n}}
is characterized by an equioscillation property: there exists an array of points
0=t_{0}<t_{1}<\ldots<t_{n-1}<t_{n}=1
such that

T(t_{k})=(-1)^{\nu_{k+1}+\ldots+\nu_{n}}\|T\|,\quad k=0,\ldots,n.

By taking logarithm, we can write Bojanov’s problem in the following form

\mathbf{minimize}\text{ (in }x_{1}\leqslant\ldots\leqslant x_{n})\quad\max\limits_{x\in[0,1]}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}\nu_{j}\log|x-x_{j}|.

It is natural to consider weighted sup norms. That is, for a weight function
w(x)\geqslant 0
,
x\in[0,1]
, we can also consider the problem

\mathbf{minimize}\text{ (in }x_{1}\leqslant\ldots\leqslant x_{n})\quad\max\limits_{x\in[0,1]}\left(\log w(x)+\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}\nu_{j}\log|x-x_{j}|\right).

In 2000, P. C. Fenton
[
2
]
considered a generalization of the weighted Chebyshev problem and solved a dual maximization problem, too. He worked with the so-called sums of translates functions, generalizing the above weighted sum of logarithms.
Now, to formulate Fenton’s result, let us give some definitions. We use a different notation than Fenton’s original one. Instead, we use the notation from the recent papers
[
7
]
and
[
8
]
, to which we will return later.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
0<p\leqslant\infty.

A function
K\colon(-p,0)\cup(0,p)\to\mathbb{R}
is called a kernel function if
K
is concave on
(-p,0)
and on
(0,p)

and
\lim\limits_{t\downarrow 0}K(t)=\lim\limits_{t\uparrow 0}K(t),
which are either real or equal to
-\infty
.

If additionally
K
is decreasing on
(-p,0)
and increasing on
(0,p)
, then we call
K

monotone
.

We extend
K
by defining

K(0)=\lim\limits_{t\to 0}K(t),\quad K(-p)=\lim\limits_{t\downarrow-p}K(t),\quad K(p)=\lim\limits_{t\uparrow p}K(t).

If

K(0)=-\infty
, the kernel function
K
is called
singular
.

Definition 1.2
.

Let
A
be a segment, a semiaxis or
\mathbb{R}
. We call a function
J\colon A\to\underline{\mathbb{R}}:=\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty\}
an external
n
-field function or simply a field on
A
if
J
is bounded above on
A
and it assumes finite values at more than
n
different points of
A,
where in the case of a segment we count boundary points with weights
1/2.

Here we impose precisely the conditions on the weights of the points to keep consistency with the case of a segment. In this case, it is necessary that there are at least
n
interior points and some additional one anywhere in the segment, where the field is finite.

Let
r_{1},\ldots,r_{n}>0
. Denote
\mathbf{y}:=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}),
where
y_{1}\leqslant\ldots\leqslant y_{n}
. Replacing
\log|\cdot-y_{j}|
by
K(\cdot-y_{j})
and
\log w
by
J
, we obtain
the sum of translates function
:

(1)

\displaystyle F(\mathbf{y},t)=J(t)+\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}r_{j}K(t-y_{j}).

The sum of translates method originates from Fenton. Initially, Fenton’s goal was to prove a conjecture of P. D. Barry from 1962 on the growth of entire functions. Fenton succeeded in this in 1981
[
3
]
. And even though this conjecture was proved a little earlier by A. A. Goldberg
[
4
]
, Fenton got other nice results in the theory of entire functions using his approach of the sums of translates
[
5
]
,
[
6
]
.

Consider a segment
[a,b]
. In what follows, we will denote by
\overline{S^{[a,b]}}
the closed simplex

\overline{S^{[a,b]}}:=\{\mathbf{y}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\ a\leqslant y_{1}\leqslant\ldots\leqslant y_{n}\leqslant b\}.

Let
\mathbf{y}\in\overline{S^{[a,b]}}
and
F(\mathbf{y},t)
be defined for
t\in[a,b]
. Denote

\displaystyle m^{[a,b]}_{0}(\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle:=\sup\limits_{t\in[a,y_{1}]}F(\mathbf{y},t),\quad m^{[a,b]}_{n}(\mathbf{y}):=\sup\limits_{t\in[y_{n},b]}F(\mathbf{y},t),

\displaystyle m^{[a,b]}_{j}(\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle:=\sup\limits_{t\in[y_{j},y_{j+1}]}F(\mathbf{y},t),\quad j=1,\ldots,n-1,

and

\overline{m^{[a,b]}}(\mathbf{y}):=\max\limits_{j=0,\ldots,n}m^{[a,b]}_{j}(\mathbf{y})=\sup\limits_{t\in[a,b]}F(\mathbf{y},t),\quad\underline{m^{[a,b]}}(\mathbf{y}):=\min\limits_{j=0,\ldots,n}m^{[a,b]}_{j}(\mathbf{y}).

Note that for any
\mathbf{y}\in\overline{S^{[a,b]}}
the value of
\overline{m^{[a,b]}}(\mathbf{y})
is finite. Indeed, we have
\overline{m^{[a,b]}}(\mathbf{y})>-\infty
, since
J
is finite at least at
n+1
points.
Further,
\overline{m^{[a,b]}}(\mathbf{y})<\infty,
as
K
is concave and
J
is bounded above.

Next, we also define

M(\overline{S^{[a,b]}}):=\inf\limits_{\mathbf{y}\in\overline{S^{[a,b]}}}\left(\overline{m^{[a,b]}}(\mathbf{y})\right),\quad m(\overline{S^{[a,b]}}):=\sup\limits_{\mathbf{y}\in\overline{S^{[a,b]}}}\left(\underline{m^{[a,b]}}(\mathbf{y})\right).

If
a=-b,
then we will write
\overline{S^{b}},\ m^{b}_{j}(\mathbf{y}),\ \overline{m^{b}}(\mathbf{y}),\ \underline{m^{b}}(\mathbf{y}),\ M(\overline{S^{b}})
and
m(\overline{S^{b}}).

A point
\mathbf{y}\in\overline{S^{[a,b]}}
is called an equioscillation point on
[a,b]
if

m^{[a,b]}_{0}(\mathbf{y})=m^{[a,b]}_{1}(\mathbf{y})=\ldots=m^{[a,b]}_{n}(\mathbf{y}).

Now consider
[a,b]=[0,1]
. Let
K^{1}
be a kernel defined on
[-1,1]
and
J^{1}
be an
n
-field defined on
[0,1].
Fenton proved the following theorem
[
2
]
.

Theorem B
.

Let
K^{1}
be a monotone kernel defined on
[-1,1]
,
K^{1}
be twice differentiable on
[-1,1]\setminus\{0\}
,
(K^{1})^{\prime\prime}<0
and

\lim\limits_{t\to 0}|(K^{1})^{\prime}(t)|=\infty.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N},\ r_{1}=\ldots=r_{n}=1
.
Assume that
J
is a concave function on
(0,1)
and put

J(0)=\lim\limits_{t\downarrow 0}J(t),\ J(1)=\lim\limits_{t\uparrow 1}J(t).

Consider the sum of translates function (
1
).
Then there is a unique point
\mathbf{w}=(w_{1},\ldots,w_{n})\in\overline{S^{[0,1]}}
such that

\overline{m^{[0,1]}}(\mathbf{w})=M(\overline{S^{[0,1]}}),\quad\underline{m^{[0,1]}}(\mathbf{w})=m(\overline{S^{[0,1]}}).

This extremal point
\mathbf{w}
is the unique equioscillation point on
[0,1].

The problem of minimizing
\overline{m^{[0,1]}}
is called the minimax problem, and the problem of maximizing
\underline{m^{[0,1]}}
is called the maximin problem.

At present, B. Farkas, B. Nagy, and Sz. Gy. Révész have fruitfully developed the subject of sums of translates in their research. The following theorem combines the results of their articles
[
7
]
and
[
8
]
.

Theorem C
.

Let
K^{1}:(-1,0)\cup(0,1)\to\mathbb{R}
be a monotone kernel function. Let
n\in\mathbb{N},\ r_{1},\ldots,r_{n}>0
be arbitrary. Assume that
J^{1}
is an
n
-field function on
[0,1]
. Consider the sum of translates function (
1
).
Then

M(\overline{S^{[0,1]}})=m(\overline{S^{[0,1]}})

and
there exists some point
\mathbf{w}=(w_{1},\ldots,w_{n})\in\overline{S^{[0,1]}}
at which the simplex minimax is attained:

\overline{m}(\mathbf{w})=M(\overline{S^{[0,1]}}).

Furthermore, there are no
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}
with finite local maxima
m^{[0,1]}_{j}
such that

(2)

\displaystyle m^{[0,1]}_{j}(\mathbf{x})>m^{[0,1]}_{j}(\mathbf{y}),\quad j=0,\ldots,n.

If there exists an equioscillation point
\mathbf{e}
, then it is the minimax and maximin point, i.e.,

M(\overline{S^{[0,1]}})=\overline{m}(\mathbf{e})=\underline{m}(\mathbf{e})=m(\overline{S^{[0,1]}}).

Additionally,

(1)

If
J^{1}
is upper semicontinuous or
K^{1}
is singular, then there exists an equioscillation point.

(2)

If
K^{1}
is singular and strictly concave, then the equioscillation point is unique. Moreover, the so-called intertwining property holds: the strict inequality (
2

\mathbf{x}\neq\mathbf{y}.
In particular, this implies that the equioscillation point is the unique minimax and maximin point.

In conclusion, it is worth noting that the sums of translates approach has found its place not only in problems on a segment, but also on a torus. It all started in 2013 with a conjecture of G. Ambrus, K. M. Ball and T. Erdélyi
[
9
]
that for any
2\pi
-periodic, even and convex on
(0,2\pi)
function
f
the expression

\min\limits_{\theta\in[0,2\pi)}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}f(\theta-\theta_{j})

is maximized when the nodes
\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{n}
are uniformly distributed on
[0,2\pi)
(it follows that the local maxima of
\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}f(\theta-\theta_{j})
are equal). This conjecture was proved by D. P. Hardin, A. P. Kendall and E. B. Saff
[
10
]
in the same year. In 2018, Farkas, Nagy and Révész presented a solution of the minimax problem for functions
\displaystyle F(\mathbf{y},t)=K_{0}(t)+\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}K_{j}(t-y_{j})

[
11
]
. They assumed that
K_{0},\ldots,K_{n}:\ \mathbb{R}\to[-\infty,0)
are
2\pi
-periodic functions, strictly concave on
(0,2\pi)
, and either all are continuously differentiable on
(0,2\pi)
or for each
j=0,\ldots,n
we have

\lim\limits_{t\uparrow 2\pi}D_{+}K_{j}(t)=\lim\limits_{t\uparrow 2\pi}D_{-}K_{j}(t)=-\infty,\quad\text{or}\quad\lim\limits_{t\downarrow 0}D_{-}K_{j}(t)=\lim\limits_{t\downarrow 0}D_{+}K_{j}(t)=-\infty.

Here
D_{\pm}K_{j}
denote the (everywhere existing) one sided derivatives of the function
K_{j}
.

In the present paper we prove an analog of Theorem
C
on
\mathbb{R},
by reducing minimax and maximin problems on the axis to the case of a segment.

1.2.
Formulation of the problems on the real axis

Among the generalizations of Chebyshev’s alternation theorem, there exists a fairly general result for the so-called
T
-systems. However, this approach does not work for Bojanov’s case. On the other hand, the sums of translates approach successfully addresses it. Let us discuss this in detail.

Recall that a
T
-system on a set
A
is a set of continuous real-valued functions
\{u_{j}(x)\}_{j=0}^{n}
defined on
A
with the property that all the nontrivial generalized polynomials of the form
\displaystyle\sum\limits_{j=0}^{n}a_{j}u_{j}(x)
have at most
n
zeros on
A
.

In 1926, S. N. Bernstein
[
12
]
obtained the following generalization of Chebyshev’s alternation theorem for
T
-systems on the segment
[a,b]
.

Theorem D
.

If
\{u_{j}(x)\}_{j=0}^{n}
is a
T
-system on
[a,b]
, then for any continuous function
f
on
[a,b]
there exists a unique polynomial
u_{*}
that minimizes the problem of finding

\min_{a_{0},\ldots,a_{n}}\max_{x\in[a,b]}\left|f(x)-\sum\limits_{j=0}^{n}a_{j}u_{j}(x)\right|.

This polynomial is characterized by the existence of
n+2
points
a\leqslant x_{1}<\ldots<x_{n+2}\leqslant b
such that

(-1)^{j}\delta(f(x_{j})-u_{*}(x_{j}))=\max\limits_{x\in[a,b]}|f(x)-u_{*}(x)|,\quad j=1,\ldots,n+2,\quad\delta=\pm 1.

Let
\{u_{j}(t)\}_{j=0}^{n}
be a
T
-system on
\mathbb{R}
, let
w:\mathbb{R}\to(0,\infty)
and
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be continuous functions. Assume that

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{t\to\pm\infty}w(t)u_{j}(t)=0,\ j=0,\ldots,n,\quad\lim\limits_{t\to\pm\infty}w(t)f(t)=0.

It is easy to see that
\{w(t)u_{j}(t)\}_{j=0}^{n}
is a
T
-system on
\mathbb{R}
. Note that if we set
w(-\pi/2)u_{j}(-\pi/2)=w(\pi/2)u_{j}(\pi/2)=0,
then
\{w(\tan x)u_{j}(\tan x)\}_{j=0}^{n}
is a
T
-system on
[-\pi/2,\pi/2]
. This follows from the fact that the function
\tan
strictly increases on
(-\pi/2,\pi/2)
and maps this interval onto
\mathbb{R}
. Applying Theorem
D
to the system
\{w(\tan x)u_{j}(\tan x)\}_{j=0}^{n}
with the approximated function
w(\tan x)f(\tan x)
, and then making the substitution
\tan x=t
, we conclude that in the case of
T
-systems, Bernstein’s theorem for the weighted minimax problem on
\mathbb{R}
is valid. For
f(t)=t^{n+1}
,
\{u_{j}(t)\}_{j=0}^{n}=\{t^{j}\}_{j=0}^{n}
we obtain the problem for weighted algebraic polynomials of degree
n+1
.

However, attempts to apply that approach to our problem directly fail quickly because even for the Bojanov problem, the occurring polynomials do not form a vector space, as linear combinations of them can have different root multiplicities.

The sum of translates approach allows one to obtain a result for Bojanov’s case as well. We present a characterization of the extremal polynomial in the weighted Bojanov problem in Corollary
7.1
. Moreover, as in Theorem
C
, we deal with almost arbitrary weights.

To formulate our problems precisely, we need some additional preparation.

Let
K:(-\infty,0)\cup(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
be a kernel function and
J:\mathbb{R}\to\underline{\mathbb{R}}
be
an
n
-field function.
Let us introduce the set

\overline{S^{\infty}}:=\{\mathbf{y}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})\colon-\infty<y_{1}\leqslant y_{2}\leqslant\ldots\leqslant y_{n}<\infty\}.

Let
r_{1},\ldots,r_{n}>0.

Consider the sum of translates function

F(\mathbf{y},t)=J(t)+\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}r_{j}K(t-y_{j}),\quad\mathbf{y}\in\overline{S^{\infty}},\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

Definition 1.3
.

Let
R>0
and let
K
be a kernel function defined on
\mathbb{R}
. A field function
J
defined on
\mathbb{R}
is said to be
R
-admissible (for
K
) if

\lim_{|t|\to\infty}\left(J(t)+RK(t)\right)=-\infty.

Remark 1.1
.

Note that if
J
is
R
-admissible for
K
, then it is also
\widetilde{R}
-admissible for any
\widetilde{R}\in(0,R)
. Without loss of generality, consider positive
t.
If
C:=\lim\limits_{t\to\infty}K(t)\in\mathbb{R}
, then
\widetilde{R}K(t)\leqslant R(K(t)-C+1)
for large
t
, and hence
J
is
\widetilde{R}
-admissible. Otherwise, due to the concavity,
\lim\limits_{t\to\infty}K(t)=-\infty
. Since
J
is bounded above by definition, it follows that
J
is
\widetilde{R}
-admissible.

In what follows, we will consider only admissible fields. If we do not specify anything about
R
, it means that we are considering sums of translates with multiplicities
r_{1},\ldots,r_{n}
, and

R:=r_{1}+\ldots+r_{n}.

Denote

\displaystyle m_{0}(\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle:=\sup\limits_{t\in(-\infty,y_{1}]}F(\mathbf{y},t),\quad m_{n}(\mathbf{y}):=\sup\limits_{t\in[y_{n},\infty)}F(\mathbf{y},t),

\displaystyle m_{j}(\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle:=\sup\limits_{t\in[y_{j},y_{j+1}]}F(\mathbf{y},t),\quad j=1,\ldots,n-1.

Similarly to the case of
[a,b]
, we say that
\mathbf{y}\in\overline{S^{\infty}}
is an equioscillation point on
\mathbb{R}
if

m_{0}(\mathbf{y})=m_{1}(\mathbf{y})=\ldots=m_{n}(\mathbf{y}).

We study the quantities

\displaystyle\overline{m}(\mathbf{y}):=\max\limits_{j=0,\ldots,n}m_{j}(\mathbf{y})=\sup\limits_{t\in\mathbb{R}}F(\mathbf{y},t),\quad\underline{m}(\mathbf{y}):=\min\limits_{j=0,\ldots,n}m_{j}(\mathbf{y}),

\displaystyle M(\overline{S^{\infty}}):=\inf\limits_{\mathbf{y}\in\overline{S^{\infty}}}\overline{m}(\mathbf{y}),\quad m(\overline{S^{\infty}}):=\sup\limits_{\mathbf{y}\in\overline{S^{\infty}}}\underline{m}(\mathbf{y}).

It is easy to see that
\overline{m}(\mathbf{y})
is finite for all
\mathbf{y}\in\overline{S^{\infty}}
.
Indeed, by (
4
),
F(\mathbf{y},\cdot)
is bounded above outside some segment. Since
J
and
K
are bounded above on this segment,
F(\mathbf{y},\cdot)
is bounded above too. Therefore,
F(\mathbf{y},\cdot)
is bounded above on
\mathbb{R}
, so
\overline{m}(\mathbf{y})<\infty
. On the other hand,
\overline{m}(\mathbf{y})>-\infty,
since
F(\mathbf{y},\cdot)
is finite at least at one point by the definition of
J
.

Our goal is to prove the following theorem.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
K:(-\infty,0)\cup(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
be a monotone kernel function. Let
n\in\mathbb{N},\ r_{1},\ldots,r_{n}>0
be arbitrary, and define
R:=r_{1}+\ldots+r_{n}
. Assume that
J:\mathbb{R}\to\underline{\mathbb{R}}
is an
R
-admissible
n
-field function for
K
. Consider the sum of translates function (
1
). Then

M(\overline{S^{\infty}})=m(\overline{S^{\infty}})

and
there exists some point
\mathbf{w}\in\overline{S^{\infty}}
such that

\overline{m}(\mathbf{w})=M(\overline{S^{\infty}}).

Furthermore, there are no
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}
with finite local maxima
m_{j}
such that

(3)

\displaystyle m_{j}(\mathbf{x})>m_{j}(\mathbf{y}),\quad j=0,\ldots,n.

If there exists an equioscillation point
\mathbf{e}
on
\mathbb{R}
, then it is the minimax and maximin point, i.e.,

M(\overline{S^{\infty}})=\overline{m}(\mathbf{e})=\underline{m}(\mathbf{e})=m(\overline{S^{\infty}}).

Additionally,

(1)

If
J
is upper semicontinuous or
K
is singular, then there exists an equioscillation point on
\mathbb{R}
.

(2)

If
K
is singular and strictly concave, then the equioscillation point is unique. Moreover, the intertwining property holds: the strict inequality (
3
) can be replaced by the non-strict one, if
\mathbf{x}\neq\mathbf{y}.
This in particular implies that the equioscillation point is the unique minimax and maximin point.

2.
Simple lemmas

Let us formulate an auxiliary assertion, an equivalent description of concavity, which will be very useful for us below. This statement is well-known, its proof can be found e.g. in
[
18
, Lemma 10]
, but we still give it in a more convenient formulation for us.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
g
be a concave function on a segment. If
x
belongs to this segment and
k,h>0
are such that
x+k+h
also belongs to it, then

g(x+k+h)-g(x+h)\leqslant g(x+k)-g(x).

Proof.

By definition of concavity, for the point
x+k
we have

\dfrac{k}{k+h}g(x+k+h)+\dfrac{h}{k+h}g(x)\leqslant g(x+k),

and for
x+h

\dfrac{h}{k+h}g(x+k+h)+\dfrac{k}{k+h}g(x)\leqslant g(x+h).

Summing these inequalities, we obtain

g(x+k+h)+g(x)\leqslant g(x+k)+g(x+h),

and the lemma is proved.
∎

Lemma 2.2
.

If
J
is admissible, then for any
\mathbf{y}\in\overline{S^{\infty}}

(4)

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{|t|\to\infty}F(\mathbf{y},t)=-\infty.

Proof.

Let us prove the lemma for
t\to\infty
. If
t\to-\infty
, then we can consider
\widetilde{J}(t)=J(-t)
and
\widetilde{K}(t)=K(-t)
and apply what is proved for
t\to\infty
.

Firstly, let us show that for any
y\neq 0

\lim\limits_{t\to\infty}J(t)+RK(t-y)=-\infty.

We consider two cases.
First, let
K
be a kernel which is
monotonically increasing on
(0,\infty)
.
We have

J(t)+RK(t-y)=J(t)+RK(t)+R(K(t-y)-K(t)).

If
K(t-y)-K(t)\leqslant 0,
then, obviously, since
J
is admissible, we obtain our statement. Now let
K(t-y)-K(t)>0.
Hence, by monotonicity of
K
, for large
t
we have that
t-y>t>1.
Here applying Lemma
2.1
with
x=1,\ k=-y,\ h=t-1,
we obtain that

K(t-y)-K(t)\leqslant K(1-y)-K(1).

This estimate does not depend on
t.
Hence, using the admissibility of
J
, we have that

J(t)+RK(t-y)\leqslant J(t)+RK(t)+R(K(1-y)-K(1))\to-\infty,\quad t\to\infty.

Now suppose that
K
is not monotone on
(0,\infty)
. Therefore,
\lim\limits_{t\to\infty}K(t)=-\infty
. By definition of a field,
J
is bounded above, and we have that
J(t)+RK(t-y)\to-\infty
as
t\to\infty
.

Finally, we have that

F(\mathbf{y},t)=J(t)+\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}r_{j}K(t-y_{j})=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}\dfrac{r_{j}}{R}\left(J(t)+RK(t-y_{j})\right)\to-\infty,\quad t\to\infty.

∎

3.
Mhaskar-Rakhmanov-Saff theorem for sums of translates

on the real axis

The key in our proof is a reduction to the problem on a segment. For this, we work out an analogue of the Mhaskar-Rakhmanov-Saff theorem.
In 1985, H. N. Mhaskar and E. B. Saff proved the following theorem
[
13
]
.

Theorem E
.

Let
w:\mathbb{R}\to[0,\infty)
be a function with support
\Sigma
such that

(1)

\Sigma
has positive logarithmic capacity.

(2)

The restriction of
w
to
\Sigma
is continuous on
\Sigma.

(3)

The set
Z:=\{x\in\Sigma:\ w(x)=0\}
has logarithmic capacity zero.

(4)

If
\Sigma
is unbounded, then
|x|w(x)\to 0
as
|x|\to\infty,\ x\in\Sigma.

Then there exists a compact set
S_{w}
such that for any polynomial
p
of degree
n
if

|w^{n}(x)p(x)|\leqslant M

holds quasi-everywhere on
S_{w}
, i.e., except for a set of logarithmic capacity zero, then it holds quasi-everywhere on
\Sigma
.

The key to proving this theorem is to solve the weighted potential theory problem of minimizing logarithmic energy. The support of the extremal measure for this problem is
S_{w}
. The minimization of logarithmic energy has physical significance and is related to the potential energy of charged particles. The study of this problem goes back
to Gauss. In 1935, O. Frostman studied the weighted problem, assuming that
w
is continuous and superharmonic
[
16
]
. Mhaskar and Saff adapted Frostman’s approach in their proof. In addition to the original work
[
13
]
, more details can be found in
[
14
, p. 153]
.

E. A. Rakhmanov independently explored the connection between potential theory and the minimax problem in 1982
[
15
]
. Rakhmanov was interested in the asymptotic properties of orthogonal polynomials with Hermite and Laguerre weights on the real axis, including their logarithmic asymptotics and distribution of zeros. In addition, Rakhmanov investigated the continuous analogue of the minimax problem for polynomials, where logarithmic potentials are considered instead of polynomials. He showed that such a problem has a unique solution. Throughout his work, there is a clear connection between polynomials and potentials. Rakhmanov is always mentioned alongside Mhaskar and Saff because their ideas overlap.

It is natural to ask about finding
S_{w}
. This question has been extensively studied when
w
is an even differentiable function and
x\cdot\dfrac{w^{\prime}(x)}{w(x)}
is negative and decreasing on
(0,\infty).
In this case,
S_{w}
is a symmetric segment, its upper bound is called the Mhaskar-Rakhmanov-Saff number and can be found as the solution of a certain integral equation
[
14
, p. 216]
.

The minimal set on which the absolute values of any weighted polynomial attain their maxima is called the minimal essential set. It is important to note that the set
S_{w}
from the Mhaskar-Rakhmanov-Saff theorem is also the minimal essential set
[
17
]
.

In our paper, we do not use the approach of potential theory (and the concept of logarithmic capacity, respectively), and our inequalities hold everywhere.

\mathbf{y}
arbitrarily. Let
C:=\max\{|x_{1}|,|x_{n}|,|y_{1}|,|y_{n}|\}.
By Lemma
4.3
, there is
M_{C}\geqslant C
such that

\displaystyle m^{M_{C}}_{j}(\mathbf{y})=m_{j}(\mathbf{y})\text{ and }m^{M_{C}}_{j}(\mathbf{x})=m_{j}(\mathbf{x}),\ j=0,\ldots,n.

By Theorem
C
, inequalities
m^{M_{C}}_{j}(\mathbf{x})>m^{M_{C}}_{j}(\mathbf{y})
for every
j=0,\ldots,n
is impossible. So, we have that inequalities
m_{j}(\mathbf{x})>m_{j}(\mathbf{y}),\ j=0,\ldots,n,
cannot hold. If
K
is singular and strictly concave, then, also by Theorem
C
, this inequality can be replaced by the non-strict one, if
\mathbf{x}\neq\mathbf{y}.

4. Note that if
J
is upper semicontinuous or
K
is singular, then by Theorem
C
, there is an equioscillation point on
[-\ell,\ell].
By Lemma
3.1
, it is an equioscillation point on
\mathbb{R}.

If
K
is singular and strictly concave, as proven above, the intertwining property holds in this case, and this immediately implies the uniqueness of the equioscillation point.

Now, even in the most general case, assuming only that
K
is monotone and
J
is admissible, suppose there exists an equioscillation point
\mathbf{e}
on
\mathbb{R}
. Since

M(\overline{S^{\infty}})\leqslant\overline{m}(\mathbf{e})=\underline{m}(\mathbf{e})\leqslant m(\overline{S^{\infty}})\quad\text{and}\quad M(\overline{S^{\infty}})=m(\overline{S^{\infty}}),

equality holds throughout, that is,
\mathbf{e}
is an extremal point for both problems.

The theorem is proved.
∎

6.
Problems on a semiaxis

Let
J^{+}
be an admissible
n
-field on
[0,\infty)
,
K
be a monotone kernel defined on
\mathbb{R}
and

\overline{S^{[0,\infty)}}=\{\mathbf{y}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}):\ 0\leqslant y_{1}\leqslant\ldots\leqslant y_{n}<\infty\}.

For positive
r_{1},\ldots,r_{n}
consider the sum of translates on
[0,\infty)

F^{+}(\mathbf{y},t)=J^{+}(t)+\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}r_{j}K(t-y_{j}),\quad\mathbf{y}\in\overline{S^{[0,\infty)}},\quad t\in[0,\infty).

For
F^{+}
with admissible field
J^{+}
the minimax and maximin problems on
\overline{S^{[0,\infty)}}
also make sense. And it is not hard to see that these problems are equivalent to the problems for

F(\mathbf{y},t)=J(t)+\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}r_{j}K(t-y_{j}),\quad\mathbf{y}\in\overline{S^{[0,\infty)}},\quad t\in\mathbb{R},

where

J(t)=\begin{cases}-\infty,&t<0,\\
J^{+}(t),&t\geqslant 0.\end{cases}

That is, for
F
we would like to optimize
\underline{m}
and
\overline{m}
on
\overline{S^{[0,\infty)}}
instead of
\overline{S^{\infty}}.
Let us show that all statements of Theorem
1.1
are valid for these problems on
[0,\infty).

Corollary 6.1
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N},\ r_{1},\ldots,r_{n}>0
be arbitrary. Let
K:(-\infty,0)\cup(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
be a monotone kernel function and
J^{+}
be an admissible
n
-field function on
[0,\infty)
. Then

M(\overline{S^{[0,\infty)}})=m(\overline{S^{[0,\infty)}})

and
there exists some point
\mathbf{w}\in\overline{S^{[0,\infty)}}
such that

\overline{m}(\mathbf{w})=M(\overline{S^{[0,\infty)}}).

Furthermore, there are no
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\overline{S^{[0,\infty)}}
with finite local maxima
m_{j}
such that

(19)

\displaystyle m_{j}(\mathbf{x})>m_{j}(\mathbf{y}),\quad j=0,\ldots,n.

If there exists an equioscillation point
\mathbf{e}
on
[0,\infty)
, then it is the minimax and maximin point, i.e.,

M(\overline{S^{\infty}})=\overline{m}(\mathbf{e})=\underline{m}(\mathbf{e})=m(\overline{S^{\infty}}).

Additionally,

(1)

If
J^{+}
is upper semicontinuous or
K
is singular, then there exists an equioscillation point on
[0,\infty)
.

(2)

If
K
is singular and strictly concave, then the equioscillation point is unique. Moreover, the intertwining property holds: the strict inequality (
19
) can be replaced by the non-strict one, if
\mathbf{x}\neq\mathbf{y}.
This in particular implies that the equioscillation point is the unique minimax and maximin point.

Proof.

1. Let us show that there is a minimax point
\mathbf{w}\in\overline{S^{[0,\infty)}}
. By Theorem
1.1
, there is some minimax point
\mathbf{w}^{\prime}=(w^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,w^{\prime}_{n})\in\overline{S^{\infty}}
for the problem on
\mathbb{R}
with
F
. Using monotonicity of
K
, it is easy to see that

J(t)+\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}r_{j}K(t-w^{\prime}_{j})\geqslant J(t)+\sum\limits_{w^{\prime}_{j}<0}r_{j}K(t)+\sum\limits_{w^{\prime}_{j}\geqslant 0}r_{j}K(t-w^{\prime}_{j}),\quad t\in[0,\infty).

Hence

\overline{m}(\mathbf{w}^{\prime})\geqslant\overline{m}(\mathbf{w}),\text{ where }w_{j}=\begin{cases}0,&w^{\prime}_{j}<0,\\
w^{\prime}_{j},&w^{\prime}_{j}\geqslant 0.\end{cases}

So,
\mathbf{w}\in\overline{S^{[0,\infty)}}
is also a minimax point. This implies that

M(\overline{S^{\infty}})=M(\overline{S^{[0,\infty)}}).

2. Now let us prove that
M(\overline{S^{[0,\infty)}})=m(\overline{S^{[0,\infty)}}).
By Theorem
1.1
,
M(\overline{S^{\infty}})=m(\overline{S^{\infty}}).
Note that for all
\mathbf{y}\in\overline{S^{\infty}}\setminus\overline{S^{[0,\infty)}}
we have
m_{0}(\mathbf{y})=\underline{m}(\mathbf{y})=-\infty
.
Therefore, obviously,
m(\overline{S^{\infty}})=m(\overline{S^{[0,\infty)}})
.
And we obtain

(20)

\displaystyle M(\overline{S^{[0,\infty)}})=M(\overline{S^{\infty}})=m(\overline{S^{\infty}})=m(\overline{S^{[0,\infty)}}).

3. Note that if
\mathbf{e}
is an equioscillation point on
\mathbb{R}
, then
\mathbf{e}\in\overline{S^{[0,\infty)}}
. Indeed, as noted above, otherwise we have that
m_{0}(\mathbf{e})=\ldots=m_{n}(\mathbf{e})=-\infty,
and this is impossible. By Theorem
1.1
and (
20
),

\overline{m}(\mathbf{e})=M(\overline{S^{[0,\infty)}})=m(\overline{S^{[0,\infty)}})=\underline{m}(\mathbf{e}).

4. All the other statements are obvious.
∎

7.
The weighted Bojanov problem on the real axis

wLet
r_{1},\ldots,r_{n}>0
be arbitrary, and denote
\mathbf{r}:=(r_{1},\ldots,r_{n})
. Consider the following sets of monic, generalized, nonnegative polynomials
[
20
, p. 392]
of degree
R\penalty\ :=\penalty\ \sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}r_{j}

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{r}}:=\left\{\prod\limits_{j=1}^{n}|x-(x_{j}+iy_{j})|^{r_{j}}:\ -\infty<x_{1}<\ldots<x_{n}<\infty,\ y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}\in\mathbb{R}\right\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{r}}(\mathbb{R}):=\left\{\prod\limits_{j=1}^{n}|x-x_{j}|^{r_{j}}:\ -\infty<x_{1}<\ldots<x_{n}<\infty\right\}\subset\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{r}}.

Theorem
1.1
immediately implies the following version of Theorem
A
on
\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{r}}
.

Corollary 7.1
.

Let
w:\mathbb{R}\to[0,\infty)
be a bounded above function assuming non-zero values at more than
n
points. Assume that

(21)

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{|x|\to\infty}w(x)\cdot x^{R}=0.

Then there exists a unique
T\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{r}}

such that

\|w(x)T(x)\|=\inf\limits_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{r}}}\|w(x)P(x)\|,

where
\|\cdot\|
is the sup norm over
\mathbb{R}
. All roots of
T
are real and distinct. Moreover, this extremal polynomial is characterized by an equioscillation property: there exists an array of points
-\infty<t_{0}<\ldots<t_{n}<\infty
such that

(22)

\displaystyle|T(t_{k})|=\|T\|,\quad k=0,\ldots,n.

Proof.

Note that all roots of the extremal polynomials are necessarily real, since if
y_{j}\neq 0,
then

|x-(x_{j}+iy_{j})|>|x-x_{j}|,\quad x\in\mathbb{R}.

Hence we may consider the minimax problem on
\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{r}}(\mathbb{R})
instead of the original problem.

Let
K:=\log|\cdot|,\ J:=\log w.
It is clear that the original minimax problem is equivalent to the minimax problem for the sums of translates with
K
and
J.
Obviously,
K
is a strictly concave, monotone, singular kernel and
J
is an admissible
n
-field function for
K
and for
R.
Since
K
is monotone and
J
is admissible, by Theorem
1.1
, there is a minimax point. And because of
K
is singular and strictly concave, there exists a unique equioscillation point
\mathbf{x}^{*}:=(x^{*}_{1},\ldots,x^{*}_{n})
and it is the unique minimax point. Note that from singularity of
K
it follows that
x^{*}_{1}<\ldots<x^{*}_{n}
. And it is clear that the equioscillation property of
\mathbf{x}^{*}
is equivalent to (
22
).
∎

The intertwining property for generalized polynomials deserves special attention.

Corollary 7.2
.

Let
w:\mathbb{R}\to[0,\infty)
be a bounded above function assuming non-zero values at more than
n
points and satisfying (
21
).
Consider
P_{1},P_{2}\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{r}}(\mathbb{R})
. Let
x^{1}_{1}<\ldots<x^{1}_{n}
be roots of
P_{1}
and
x^{2}_{1}<\ldots<x^{2}_{n}
be roots of
P_{2}.
Then the intertwining property holds. Namely, if the inequalities

\displaystyle\max\limits_{x\in(-\infty,x^{1}_{1}]}|w(x)P_{1}(x)|\leqslant\max\limits_{x\in(-\infty,x^{2}_{1}]}|w(x)P_{2}(x)|,

\displaystyle\max\limits_{x\in[x^{1}_{j},x^{1}_{j+1}]}|w(x)P_{1}(x)|\leqslant\max\limits_{x\in[x^{2}_{j},x^{2}_{j+1}]}|w(x)P_{2}(x)|,\quad j=1,\ldots,n-1,

\displaystyle\max\limits_{x\in[x^{1}_{n},\infty)}|w(x)P_{1}(x)|\leqslant\max\limits_{x\in[x^{2}_{n},\infty)}|w(x)P_{2}(x)|.

hold simultaneously, then
P_{1}=P_{2}.

Proof.

Consider the sums of translates with
K:=\log|\cdot|,\ J:=\log w.
As mentioned in the proof of Corollary
7.1
,
K
is strictly concave, monotone, singular and
J
is admissible. By Theorem
1.1
, for these sums of translates we have the intertwining property. It remains to note that inequalities between local maxima of two generalized polynomials do not change after taking logarithms.
∎

Note that the intertwining property on the real axis is new even in classical cases, such as the Chebyshev problem for algebraic polynomials with the Hermite weight
\exp(-x^{2})
.
