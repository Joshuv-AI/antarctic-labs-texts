A Weak Galerkin Mixed Finite Element Method for second order elliptic equations on 2D Curved Domains Thanks: Liu and Wang are supported by the NSFC grant 12171244. Chen is supported by NSFC grant 12071090.
Yi Liu, Wenbin Chen, Yanqiu Wang

Abstract
. This article concerns the weak Galerkin mixed finite element method (WG-MFEM) for second order elliptic equations on 2D domains with curved boundary. The Neumann boundary condition is considered since it becomes the essential boundary condition in this case. It is well-known that the discrepancy between the curved physical domain and the polygonal approximation domain leads to a loss of accuracy for discretization with polynomial order α > 1 \alpha>1 . The purpose of this paper is two-fold. First, we present a detailed error analysis of the original WG-MFEM for solving problems on curved domains, which exhibits an O ⁡ ( h 1 / 2 ) O(h^{1/2}) convergence for all α ≥ 1 \alpha\geq 1 . It is a little surprising to see that even the lowest-order WG-MFEM ( α = 1 \alpha=1 ) experiences a loss of accuracy. This is different from known results for the finite element method (FEM) or the mixed FEM, and appears to be a combined effect of the WG-MFEM design and the fact that the outward normal vector on the polygonal approximation domain is different from the one on the curved domain. Second, we propose a remedy to bring the approximation rate back to optimal by employing two techniques. One is a specially designed boundary correction technique. The other is to take full advantage of the nice feature that weak Galerkin discretization can be defined on polygonal meshes, which allows the curved boundary to be better approximated by multiple short edges without increasing the total number of mesh elements. Rigorous analysis shows that a combination of the above two techniques renders optimal convergence for all α \alpha . Numerical results further confirm this conclusion.

1.
Introduction

Many practical problems arising in science and engineering are posed on domains with curved boundaries.
When such problems are approximated on polygonal or polyhedral computational domains,
the geometric difference between the two leads to a loss of approximation accuracy
[
34
,
35
]
for high-order elements.
To
resolve
this issue, a straight-forward idea is to reduce the geometric error down to the same level of the approximation error.
Popular methods following this track include the isoparametric finite element method
[
21
,
25
]

and the isogeometric analysis
[
20
,
24
]
. However, due to their specialized design, neither of them can
be applied to meshes consisting of polygons or polyhedra.

In the past two decades, discretizations on polygonal and polyhedral meshes have gained considerable
attention in the scientific computing community. Various numerical schemes have been proposed, including
the virtual element method (VEM) (see
[
2
]
and references therein),
the discontinuous Galerkin method (DG)
[
18
,
22
,
31
]
,
and the weak Galerkin method (WG)
[
38
,
39
,
30
]
, to name a few.
Very recently, researchers start to apply these discretizations to curved domains, which requires

innovative techniques, with its reason explained above
.
One emerging method is the boundary correction technique, which may have its root date back to a 1972 paper by Bramble, Dupont and Thomée
[
10
]
.
The idea is to use normal-directional Taylor expansion, in most cases just a linear approximation, to correct function values on the boundary.
Burman et. al.
[
13
,
14
]
proposed the technique for a CutFEM discretization in 2018.
It was soon applied to VEM by Bertoluzza et. al.
[
6
]
.
In 2019, Cheung et. al.
[
16
]
proposed a polynomial extension FEM which is based on an averaged Taylor expansion.
In a series of papers starting from 2018, Main and Scovazzi
[
26
,
27
,
1
]

designed a shifted boundary FEM on non-fitted meshes, i.e., boundary nodes of the mesh may not lie on the curved physical boundary.
However, their method uses 1st-order (linear) Taylor expansion and hence only works for linear elements.
Finally, we mention an earlier but closely related work
[
19
]
, where instead of Taylor expansion
the authors used a path integration to achieve a similar ‘boundary correction’ effect.

A totally different track, first proposed for VEM by Beirão da Veiga et. al.
[
4
]
in 2019,
is to define the discretization directly on curved mesh elements.
This is possible because of the ‘skeletal’ style design of VEM, where the degrees of freedom (dofs) in the interior and on the boundary of each
mesh element are defined separately.
In
[
4
]
, dofs as well as related shape functions on curved boundary edges
are defined using the parameter in the parametric equation of the curved boundary.
Hence the shape functions on curved edges are no longer polynomials in the physical space.
Later a modification was proposed
[
3
]

which uses the restriction of physical polynomials to define shape functions on curved boundary edges.
In 2021, Mu applied the idea to the primal WG discretization
[
28
]
.
Because of the direct use of the curved boundary, the implementation of these methods requires

numerical integration formulae on curved mesh elements, as well as
a mapping between each curved boundary segment and its flat counterpart in the parametric space
.
The theoretical analysis is also more complicated as it has to deal with the parametric mapping.

In this paper, we consider the weak Galerkin mixed finite element method (WG-MFEM) on 2D curved domains.
Since its first debut, the WG method has been quickly applied to various situations

[
15
,
29
,
36
,
37
,
39
,
40
,
41
]
,
among which
[
39
]
focuses on the WG-MFEM for second order elliptic equations and

[
15
]
uses WG-MFEM to solve coupled Darcy-Stokes equations.
However, there seems to be no rigorous analysis of the original WG-MFEM on curved domains.
The first objective of this paper is to fill this gap.
Although analysis of discretizations in the primal formulation on curved domains has long been
well-known, study of the mixed formulation is relatively rare.
We shall first clarify that in either cases, the main difficulty lies in how to impose the essential boundary condition.
For the mixed formulation of second order elliptic equations, it is the Neumann boundary condition that becomes essential.
A subtlety arises as the Neumann boundary condition involves the outward normal vector on the boundary,
which is
different
on the curved boundary and its polygonal approximation.
To our knowledge, such a problem, as well as subsequent mixed-FEM error analysis on curved domains, was first studied by
Bertrand et. al.
[
7
,
8
]
for the Raviart-Thomas element in 2014.
Both the analysis and numerical results show that the loss of accuracy only occurs for high-order elements.
Therefore it was a little surprising when we found that the original WG-MFEM yields only an
O(h^{1/2})
convergence for all
\alpha
,
which means an accuracy loss even for the lowest-order WG-MFEM discretization.
This appears to be a combined effect of the WG-MFEM design and the outward normal vector issue mentioned above.

We then propose a remedy to bring the approximation rate back to optimal, which is the second objective of this paper.

The remedy employs two techniques. One is a specially designed boundary correction technique that
treats the difference between outward normal vectors on the curved domain and the polygonal computational domain.
Unlike the boundary correction
[
13
,
14
,
16
,
26
,
27
,
1
]
designed for Dirichlet boundary conditions,
ours mainly deals with the normal component of the flux.
The implementation is easy and does not involve integration on curved regions.
But one has to be careful about the discrete compatibility condition, noticing that the solution to the pure Neumann boundary problem is not unique.
The other technique is a simple strategy proposed in
[
33
]
,
which uses multiple short straight edges to obtain a better geometric approximation to the curved boundary.
Since the WG discretizations can be defined on polygonal meshes, the curved boundary is then better approximated
by polygonal elements with multiple short edges.
Mathematically, this multiple short edge approach

is still a ‘linear’ approximation and hence the number of short edges required will definitely increase with
\alpha
.
The main advantage of this strategy
lies in
its simplicity in the implementation.

The algorithm itself remains untouched.
One only needs to provide a new polygonal mesh consisting of multiple short boundary edges
which better approximates the curved domain.
We show that
combining the above two techniques and
using suitable meshes, the modified WG-FEM reaches optimal approximation rates for all
\alpha
.

The paper is organized as follows.
In Section
2
, we introduce the model problem, the notation and mesh assumptions.
In Section
3
, we present a rigorous analysis of the original WG-MFEM on curved domains.
The modified WG-FEM and its theoretical analysis are given in Section
4
.
Finally, numerical results are presented in Section
5
.

2.
Model problem and mesh assumptions

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a general domain with Lipschitz-continuous and possibly curved boundary
\partial\Omega
.
Consider the Poisson’s equation in its mixed form:

Given
g
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
, find functions
{\bm{u}}
and
p
such that

(2.1)

\displaystyle{\bm{u}}+\nabla p

\displaystyle=\bm{0}\quad

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega,

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot{\bm{u}}

\displaystyle=g\quad

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega.

Here we conveniently use bold face characters to denote vectors or vector-valued functions.
Equip system (
2.1
) with the homogeneous Neumann boundary condition as follows:

(2.2)

{\bm{u}}\cdot\widetilde{\bf n}=0\qquad\text{on }\partial\Omega,

where
\widetilde{\bf n}
is the unit outward normal vector on

.
System (
2.1
)-(

) is well-posed as long as the following compatibility condition holds:

(2.3)

\int_{\Omega}g\,\mathrm{d}x=0.

Moreover, the solution is unique assuming
\int_{\Omega}p\,\mathrm{d}x=0
.

Denote by
H^{m}(D)
,
m\geq 0
, the usual Sobolev space defined on an open bounded domain
D\subset\Omega
,
and endow it with the inner-product
(\cdot,\cdot)_{m,D}
, the norm
\|\cdot\|_{m,D}
and the seminorm
|\cdot|_{m,D}
.
Let
H_{0}^{m}(D)=\overline{C_{0}^{\infty}}^{H^{m}(D)}
.
When
m=0
, the space
H^{0}(D)
is identical to
L^{2}(D)
.
In this case, we use
(\cdot,\cdot)_{D}
to denote the
L^{2}
inner-product on
D
.
The above notation also extends to a curve or edge

in
\overline{D}
.
Moreover, denote by
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{e}
the duality pair on
e
.
Denote by
H(\text{div},D)
the space of vector-valued functions with all its components and divergence in

.

Define spaces

\displaystyle{\bm{V}}

\displaystyle=\{{\bm{v}}\in H(\text{div},\Omega)\,|\,{\bm{v}}\cdot\widetilde{\bf n}=0\text{ on }\partial\Omega\},

\displaystyle\Psi

\displaystyle=L^{2}_{0}(\Omega)\triangleq\left\{q\in L^{2}(\Omega)\,\bigg|\,\int_{\Omega}q\,\mathrm{d}x=0\right\}.

Then, the mixed variational formulation of the Poisson’s equation with homogeneous Neumann boundary condition can be written as:
Find
({\bm{u}},p)\in{\bm{V}}\times\Psi
such that

(2.4)

\begin{cases}a({\bm{u}},{\bm{v}})+b({\bm{v}},p)=0\quad&\forall\,{\bm{v}}\in{\bm{V}},\\
b({\bm{u}},q)=-(g,q)_{\Omega}\quad&\forall\,q\in\Psi,\end{cases}

where

a({\bm{u}},{\bm{v}})=({\bm{u}},{\bm{v}})_{\Omega},\qquad b({\bm{v}},q)=-(\nabla\cdot{\bm{v}},q)_{\Omega}.

The existence and uniqueness of a weak solution to the mixed problem (
2.4
) can be found in
[
9
]
.

Let
\mathcal{T}_{h}
be a body-fitted partition of
\Omega
consisting of polygons.
By ‘body-fitted’ we mean that each boundary edge of
\mathcal{T}_{h}
has its two end points lying on
\partial\Omega
.
Let
\overline{\Omega}_{h}=\cup_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}K
.
If
\Omega
is a polygonal domain in

But when

In general,

For the sake of simplicity, we only present theoretical analysis for the case when

Generalization to non-convex domains can be done but requires a non-trivial use of extension operators.
In Section

, a numerical example in non-convex domain is presented, which exhibits the same behavior as experiments in convex domains.

Denote by

Denote by

.
Later we shall allow

to be much smaller than

.

.

Recall that

Denote by

Denote by

Remark 2.2

Weak Galerkin discretization

Now we introduce the WG-MFEM discretization for system (
2.1
)-(
2.2
)
in a form presented in
[

]
.
On each
K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
, denote by

the set of polynomials
with degree less than or equal to

.
Likewise, on each
e\in\mathcal{E}_{h}
, let
P_{j}(e)
be the set of polynomials of degree
no more than
j
. We define the weak Galerkin spaces

\displaystyle\bm{W}_{h}=\big\{

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\bm{v}}=\{{\bm{v}}_{0},{\bm{v}}_{b}\}\in[L^{2}(\Omega_{h})]^{2}\times[L^{2}(\mathcal{E}_{h})]^{2}\text{ such that}

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\bm{v}}_{0}|_{K_{0}}\in[P_{\alpha}(K_{0})]^{2}\text{ for }K\in\mathcal{T}_{h},

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\bm{v}}_{b}|_{e}=v_{b}{\bf n}_{e}\text{, where }v_{b}\in P_{\beta}(e)\text{, for }e\in\mathcal{E}_{h}\big\},

\displaystyle{\bm{V}}_{h}=\big\{

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\bm{v}}\in\bm{W}_{h}\textrm{ satisfying }{\bm{v}}_{b}|_{e}=0\text{ for }e\in\mathcal{E}_{h}^{B}\big\},

where
\alpha
and

are given non-negative integers and

is a prescribed normal direction on each edge

.
A key feature of the weak Galerkin discretization is that a function

takes separate
values
{\bm{v}}_{0}
on the interior of each
K

{\bm{v}}_{b}

Define

\Psi_{h}=\big\{q\in L^{2}_{0}(\Omega_{h})\text{ such that }q|_{K}\in P_{\sigma}(K)\ \text{for}\ K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\big\},

where
\sigma
is a given non-negative integer. Moreover, assume that

(3.1)

\beta-1\leq\sigma\leq\beta=\alpha.

Condition (
3.1
) is imposed to make sure that the weak Galerkin discretization has desired stability and approximation properties,
as will become clear in the analysis to be given later.

On each
K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
, define the weak divergence
\nabla_{w}\cdot{\bm{v}}\in P_{\beta}(K)

for
{\bm{v}}\in{\bm{V}}_{h}
by

(\nabla_{w}\cdot{\bm{v}},q)_{K}=-({\bm{v}}_{0},\nabla q)_{K}+\langle{\bm{v}}_{b}\cdot{\bf n},q\rangle_{\partial K}\qquad\forall\,q\in P_{\beta}(K).

Define the discrete bilinear forms
a_{h}:\>{\bm{V}}_{h}\times{\bm{V}}_{h}\to\mathbb{R}
and
b_{h}:\>{\bm{V}}_{h}\times\Psi_{h}\to\mathbb{R}
by

\displaystyle a_{h}({\bm{u}},{\bm{v}})

\displaystyle=\left({\bm{u}}_{0},{\bm{v}}_{0}\right)_{\Omega_{h}}+\rho\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}h_{K}^{-1}\left\langle\left({\bm{u}}_{0}-{\bm{u}}_{b}\right)\cdot{\bf n},\left({\bm{v}}_{0}-{\bm{v}}_{b}\right)\cdot{\bf n}\right\rangle_{\partial K},

\displaystyle b_{h}({\bm{v}},q)

\displaystyle=-(\nabla_{w}\cdot{\bm{v}},q)_{\Omega_{h}},

in which
\rho
is a positive constant.

Remark 3.1
.

The second part in
a_{h}(\cdot,\cdot)
is a stabilization term. However, different from the discontinuous Galerkin method,
the stabilization parameter
\rho
in the weak Galerkin discretization can be chosen arbitrarily without affecting the approximation results.
In practice, one can simply set
\rho=1
.
For convenience, we denote the stabilization part by

(3.2)

c({\bm{u}},{\bm{v}})=\rho\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}h_{K}^{-1}\left\langle\left({\bm{u}}_{0}-{\bm{u}}_{b}\right)\cdot{\bf n},\left({\bm{v}}_{0}-{\bm{v}}_{b}\right)\cdot{\bf n}\right\rangle_{\partial K}.

The weak Galerkin formulation for system (
2.1
)-(
2.2
) can now be written as follows:
Find

{\bm{u}}_{h}\in{\bm{V}}_{h}
and
p_{h}\in\Psi_{h}
such that

(3.3)

\begin{cases}a_{h}({\bm{u}}_{h},{\bm{v}})+b_{h}({\bm{v}},p_{h})=0\quad&\forall\,{\bm{v}}\in{\bm{V}}_{h},\\
b_{h}({\bm{u}}_{h},q)=-(g,q)_{\Omega_{h}}\quad&\forall\,q\in\Psi_{h}.\end{cases}

Remark 3.2
.

Recall that for the continuous problem (
2.1
)-(
2.2
) to be well-posed, a compatibility condition (
2.3
) is necessary
which requires
g
to be mean-value free on
\Omega
.
One may wonder whether the discrete problem requires a similar compatibility condition or not.
We point out that the compatibility mechanism works differently for finite-dimensional problems

and the key is to have

(3.4)

b_{h}({\bm{v}},1)=0\qquad\forall\,{\bm{v}}\in{\bm{V}}_{h},

which is obviously true in our case according to the definitions of
b_{h}(\cdot,\cdot)
and
\nabla_{w}\cdot
.
Note that
(1,q)_{\Omega_{h}}=0
for all
q\in\Psi_{h}
. Hence the second equation in (
3.3
) is the same as

(3.5)

b_{h}({\bm{u}}_{h},q)=-(\tilde{g},q)_{\Omega_{h}}\quad\forall q\in\Psi_{h},

where
\tilde{g}=g-\frac{1}{|\Omega_{h}|}\int_{\Omega_{h}}g\,\mathrm{d}x
is mean-value free on
\Omega_{h}
.
Equation (
3.5
) is now ‘compatible’ in the traditional sense as it also holds for
q\equiv const
.
From the theoretical point of view, there is no difference should one choose to use (
3.3
) or to replace its second equation by (
3.5
).

For finite-dimensional problems, what the ‘compatibility’ condition may actually affect is the implementation procedure.
In practice, it is not convenient to compute a basis for
\Psi_{h}
, which needs to be mean-value free on
\Omega_{h}
.
One usually drops the mean-value free condition while at the same time expecting the resulting stiffness matrix
M
to have a rank
1
deficiency

(under the discrete inf-sup condition to be proved later)
.
Now, if the ‘compatible’ equation (
3.5
) is used in the implementation, it guarantees that the right-hand side vector of the linear system
is orthogonal to
ker(M^{T})
. Hence the system is solvable, i.e., compatible.
Using elementary linear algebra, one immediately sees that even if the ‘non-compatible’ second equation of (
3.3
) is used in the implementation,
it just yields a right-hand side vector not orthogonal to
ker(M^{T})
.
In this case, we know that the kernel consists of exactly
q\equiv const
. Therefore a pure algebraic post-process after assembling the entire linear system,
i.e., making the right-hand vector orthogonal to
ker(M^{T})
, can easily
resolve
this issue and render the linear system ‘compatible’.

Due to the above explanation, we do not need to worry about the ‘compatibility’ of the discrete system
either theoretically or in the implementation,
as long as (
3.4
) holds
.

To analyze the well-posedness and approximation properties of the weak Galerkin discretization (
3.3
),
we first define the norm on
{\bm{V}}_{h}
as follows:

\displaystyle\|{\bm{v}}\|_{{\bm{V}}_{h}}=

\displaystyle\left(\|{\bm{v}}_{0}\|_{\Omega_{h}}^{2}+\rho\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}h_{K}^{-1}\left\|\left({\bm{v}}_{0}-{\bm{v}}_{b}\right)\cdot{\bf n}\right\|_{\partial K}^{2}\right)^{1/2}\quad\forall\,{\bm{v}}\in{\bm{V}}_{h}.

It is obvious that
a_{h}({\bm{v}},{\bm{v}})=\|{\bm{v}}\|_{{\bm{V}}_{h}}^{2}
for all
{\bm{v}}\in{\bm{V}}_{h}
.
We shall show that
\|\cdot\|_{\bm{V_{h}}}
is a well-defined norm. Indeed, if
\|{\bm{v}}\|_{{\bm{V}}_{h}}=0
for some
{\bm{v}}\in{\bm{V}}_{h}
; i.e.,

\left({\bm{v}}_{0},{\bm{v}}_{0}\right)_{\Omega_{h}}+\rho\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}h_{K}^{-1}\left\langle\left({\bm{v}}_{0}-{\bm{v}}_{b}\right)\cdot{\bf n},\left({\bm{v}}_{0}-{\bm{v}}_{b}\right)\cdot{\bf n}\right\rangle_{\partial K}=0,

one has
{\bm{v}}_{0}\equiv\bm{0}
on each element
K
and
({\bm{v}}_{0}-{\bm{v}}_{b})\cdot{\bf n}=0
on each edge
e\in\mathcal{E}_{h}
.
This leads to
0={\bm{v}}_{b}\cdot{\bf n}=v_{b}{\bf n}_{e}\cdot{\bf n}
and consequently
{\bm{v}}_{b}\equiv\bm{0}
on each edge
e\in\mathcal{E}_{h}
.
Therefore
\|\cdot\|_{\bm{V_{h}}}
is a norm on
{\bm{V}}_{h}
.

On each
K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
, denote by
{\bm{Q}}_{0},\ \mathbb{Q}_{h}
and
\pi_{h}
the
L^{2}
projections
onto
\left[P_{\alpha}(K)\right]^{2}
,
P_{\sigma}(K)
and
P_{\beta}(K)
, respectively.
On each
e\in\mathcal{E}_{h}
, denote by
Q_{b}
the
L^{2}
projection onto
P_{\beta}(e)
.
On the entire
\Omega_{h}
, we use the same notation to denote the combination of the above local projections.
For
{\bm{v}}\in[H^{1}(\Omega_{h})]^{2}
, define
{\bm{Q}}_{b}{\bm{v}}=(Q_{b}({\bm{v}}\cdot{\bf n}_{e})){\bf n}_{e}
for each
e\in\mathcal{E}_{h}
.
Then we define a projection
{\bm{Q}}_{h}:\>[H^{1}(\Omega_{h})]^{2}\to\bm{W}_{h}
by

{\bm{Q}}_{h}{\bm{v}}=\{{\bm{Q}}_{0}{\bm{v}},{\bm{Q}}_{b}{\bm{v}}\}.

One has

Lemma 3.3
.

(Lemma 3.12 in
[
15
]
) Assume that
\alpha
and
\beta
satisfy (
3.1
), the following commutative property holds:

(3.6)

\displaystyle\nabla_{w}\cdot\left({\bm{Q}}_{h}{\bm{v}}\right)

\displaystyle=\pi_{h}(\nabla\cdot{\bm{v}})\qquad\forall\,{\bm{v}}\in[H^{1}(\Omega_{h})]^{2}.

The above commutative property is one of the key features of the weak Galerkin discretization
and has played an important role in its theoretical analysis
[
15
,
39
]
.
However, the situation is quite different when
\Omega
is a curved domain,
because for
{\bm{v}}\in[H^{1}(\Omega)]^{2}
satisfying
{\bm{v}}\cdot\widetilde{\bf n}|_{\partial\Omega}=0

one does not get
{\bm{Q}}_{h}{\bm{v}}\cdot{\bf n}|_{\partial\Omega_{h}}=0
.
The projection
{\bm{Q}}_{h}
only maps
{\bm{v}}
into
\bm{W}_{h}
, but not
{\bm{V}}_{h}
.

To partly remedy this, we introduce

\widetilde{\bm{Q}}_{b}{\bm{v}}=\begin{cases}\bm{0}\quad&\textrm{on }e\in\mathcal{E}_{h}^{B},\\
{\bm{Q}}_{b}{\bm{v}}&\textrm{on all other edges},\end{cases}

and define a modified projection
\widetilde{\bm{Q}}_{h}:\>[H^{1}(\Omega_{h})]^{2}\to{\bm{V}}_{h}
by

\widetilde{\bm{Q}}_{h}{\bm{v}}=\{{\bm{Q}}_{0}{\bm{v}},\widetilde{\bm{Q}}_{b}{\bm{v}}\}.

Obviously, the price to pay is that
\widetilde{\bm{Q}}_{h}
no longer satisfies the highly desired commutative property (
3.6
).
This brings a lot trouble to the theoretical analysis of the weak Galerkin formulation (
3.3
), as we will see later.

Using the Bramble-Hilbert lemma
2.8
, we easily get the following result:

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
{\bm{u}}\in[H^{r+1}(\Omega)]^{2}
and
p\in H^{t+1}(\Omega)
, where
0\leq r\leq\alpha
and
0\leq t\leq\sigma
. Then

\displaystyle\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\|{\bm{u}}-{\bm{Q}}_{0}{\bm{u}}\|^{2}_{K}+\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}h_{K}^{2}\|\nabla({\bm{u}}-{\bm{Q}}_{0}{\bm{u}})\|^{2}_{K}\lesssim h^{2(r+1)}|{\bm{u}}|^{2}_{r+1,\Omega_{h}},

\displaystyle\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\|p-\mathbb{Q}_{h}p\|^{2}_{K}+\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}h_{K}^{2}\|\nabla(p-\mathbb{Q}_{h}p)\|^{2}_{K}\lesssim h^{2(t+1)}|p|^{2}_{t+1,\Omega_{h}}.

3.1.
Existence and uniqueness of the discrete solution

Since
a_{h}(\cdot,\cdot)
is indeed an inner-product on
{\bm{V}}_{h}
,
according to the standard theory of mixed finite element methods
[
9
]
,
System (
3.3
) admits a unique solution as long as the following discrete inf-sup condition holds:

(3.7)

\sup_{{\bm{v}}\in{\bm{V}}_{h}}\frac{|b_{h}({\bm{v}},q)|}{\|{\bm{v}}\|_{{\bm{V}}_{h}}}\gtrsim\|q\|_{0,\Omega_{h}}\qquad\forall\,q\in\Psi_{h}.

The standard way to prove the discrete inf-sup condition is to use the continuous inf-sup condition together with a stable projection onto the discrete space,
as is the case in
[
39
]
. However, following this strategy is not easy on domains with curved boundary.
Below we shall explain why. Firstly, the continuous inf-sup condition must be defined on
\Omega
instead of
\Omega_{h}

so that the constant in it does not depend on
h
or
s
. However, a function
q\in\Psi_{h}
is mean value free on
\Omega_{h}
but not necessarily on
\Omega
,
and hence can not be used directly in the continuous inf-sup condition.
Secondly, as pointed out earlier, the projection
{\bm{Q}}_{h}
satisfies the commutative property (
3.6
) but only maps functions into
\bm{W}_{h}
,
while
\widetilde{\bm{Q}}_{h}
maps functions into
{\bm{V}}_{h}
at the price of violating the commutative property.
Neither of them can serve directly in a traditional proof of the discrete inf-sup condition.
In fact, the proof of the inf-sup condition (
3.7
) turns out to be non-trivial:

Lemma 3.5
.

The discrete
\inf
-
\sup
condition (
3.7
) holds when
h
is sufficiently small.

Proof.

Each
q\in\Psi_{h}
is a function defined on
\Omega_{h}
.
Note that it can be naturally extended to
\Omega
by filling the gap
\Omega\backslash\Omega_{h}
with the same polynomial values on neighboring mesh elements.
For simplicity, we still denote this extension by
q
.
Now
q
is mean-value free on
\Omega_{h}
. Define
\bar{q}=q-\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\int_{\Omega\setminus\Omega_{h}}q\,\mathrm{d}x
.
Obviously
\bar{q}
is mean-value free on
\Omega
. Moreover, by the triangle inequality, Lemma
2.6
,
and the fact that
s\leq h\leq O(1)
, one has

\displaystyle\|\bar{q}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}

\displaystyle=\bigg(\|q\|_{0,\Omega_{h}}^{2}+\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}_{h}^{B}}\|q\|_{0,M_{e}}^{2}\bigg)+\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\left(\int_{\Omega\setminus\Omega_{h}}q\,\mathrm{d}x\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|q\|_{0,\Omega_{h}}^{2}+h^{-1}s^{2}\|q\|_{0,\Omega_{h}}^{2}+|\Omega\backslash\Omega_{h}|\int_{\Omega\backslash\Omega_{h}}|q|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\|q\|_{0,\Omega_{h}}^{2}+h^{-1}s^{2}\|q\|_{0,\Omega_{h}}^{2}+|\Omega\backslash\Omega_{h}|\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}_{h}^{B}}\|q\|_{0,M_{e}}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|q\|_{0,\Omega_{h}}^{2}+h^{-1}s^{2}\|q\|_{0,\Omega_{h}}^{2}\lesssim\|q\|_{0,\Omega_{h}}^{2}.

It can be shown (see, e.g.,
[
23
]
) that there exists a
\bm{w}\in\left[H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)\right]^{2}
such that
\nabla\cdot\bm{w}=\bar{q}

and
\|\bm{w}\|_{1,\Omega}\lesssim\|\bar{q}\|_{0,\Omega}\lesssim\|q\|_{0,\Omega_{h}}
.
By Lemma
3.3
and the fact that
\sigma\leq\beta,
we have

\left(\nabla_{w}\cdot{\bm{Q}}_{h}\bm{w},q\right)_{\Omega_{h}}=\left(\pi_{h}(\nabla\cdot\bm{w}),q\right)_{\Omega_{h}}=(\nabla\cdot\bm{w},q)_{\Omega_{h}}=(\bar{q},q)_{\Omega_{h}}=\|q\|_{0,\Omega_{h}}^{2}.

Combine the above equation with the definitions of
{\bm{Q}}_{h}
,
\widetilde{\bm{Q}}_{h}
and
\nabla_{w}\cdot
, one gets

\displaystyle\left|\left(\nabla_{w}\cdot\widetilde{\bm{Q}}_{h}\bm{w},q\right)_{\Omega_{h}}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\left(\nabla_{w}\cdot{\bm{Q}}_{h}\bm{w},q\right)_{\Omega_{h}}-\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}^{B}}\langle{\bm{Q}}_{b}\bm{w}\cdot{\bf n},q\rangle_{\partial K\cap\partial\Omega_{h}}\right|

\displaystyle\geq\|q\|_{0,\Omega_{h}}^{2}-\left(\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}^{B}}h_{K}^{-1}\|{\bm{Q}}_{b}\bm{w}\cdot{\bf n}\|_{0,\partial K\cap\partial\Omega_{h}}^{2}\right)^{1/2}\left(\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}^{B}}h_{K}\|q\|_{0,\partial K\cap\partial\Omega_{h}}^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

Check the right-hand side of the above inequality. First, by the trace inequality and the inverse inequality
(lemmas
2.4
-
2.5
), it is clear that

(3.8)

\left(\sum\limits_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}^{B}}h_{K}\|q\|_{0,\partial K\cap\partial\Omega_{h}}^{2}\right)^{1/2}\lesssim\|q\|_{0,\Omega_{h}}.

Next, using
\bm{w}|_{\partial\Omega}=\bm{0}
and Lemma
2.7
, we have

(3.9)

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}^{B}}h_{K}^{-1}\|{\bm{Q}}_{b}\bm{w}\cdot{\bf n}\|_{0,\partial K\cap\partial\Omega_{h}}^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}^{B}}h_{K}^{-1}\|\bm{w}\cdot{\bf n}\|_{0,\partial K\cap\partial\Omega_{h}}^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\lesssim h^{-\frac{1}{2}}s\|\bm{w}\|_{1,\Omega}\lesssim h^{-\frac{1}{2}}s\|q\|_{0,\Omega_{h}}.

Hence when
h
is sufficiently small,

\displaystyle\left|(\nabla_{w}\cdot\widetilde{\bm{Q}}_{h}\bm{w},q)\right|\geq\left(1-O(h^{-\frac{1}{2}}s)\right)\|q\|^{2}_{0,\Omega_{h}}\geq\left(1-O(h^{\frac{1}{2}})\right)\|q\|^{2}_{0,\Omega_{h}}\gtrsim\|q\|^{2}_{0,\Omega_{h}}.

Using the fact that
{\bm{Q}}_{0}{\bm{w}}={\bm{Q}}_{b}{\bm{Q}}_{0}{\bm{w}}
and the approximation property of
{\bm{Q}}_{0}
, one gets

\displaystyle h_{K}^{-1}\left\|\left({\bm{Q}}_{0}\bm{w}-{\bm{Q}}_{b}\bm{w}\right)\cdot{\bf n}\right\|_{0,\partial K}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq h_{K}^{-1}\left\|{\bm{Q}}_{0}\bm{w}-{\bm{Q}}_{b}\bm{w}\right\|_{0,\partial K}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq h_{K}^{-1}\left\|{\bm{Q}}_{0}\bm{w}-\bm{w}\right\|_{0,\partial K}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|\nabla\bm{w}\|_{0,K}^{2}.

We then have

\displaystyle\|\widetilde{\bm{Q}}_{h}\bm{w}\|_{{\bm{V}}_{h}}=

\displaystyle\left(\|{\bm{Q}}_{0}\bm{w}\|_{0,\Omega_{h}}^{2}+\rho\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}h_{K}^{-1}\|\left({\bm{Q}}_{0}\bm{w}-\widetilde{\bm{Q}}_{b}\bm{w}\right)\cdot{\bf n}\|_{0,\partial K}^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(\|{\bm{Q}}_{0}\bm{w}\|_{0,\Omega_{h}}^{2}+\rho\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}h_{K}^{-1}\|\left({\bm{Q}}_{0}\bm{w}-{\bm{Q}}_{b}\bm{w}\right)\cdot{\bf n}\|_{0,\partial K}^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle+\left(\rho\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}^{B}}h_{K}^{-1}\|{\bm{Q}}_{b}\bm{w}\cdot{\bf n}\|_{0,\partial K\cap\partial\Omega_{h}}^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|{\bm{w}}\|_{1,\Omega}+h^{-\frac{1}{2}}s\|{\bm{w}}\|_{1,\Omega}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|q\|_{0,\Omega_{h}}.

Combining the above gives

\sup_{{\bm{v}}\in{\bm{V}}_{h}}\frac{|b_{h}({\bm{v}},q)|}{\|{\bm{v}}\|_{{\bm{V}}_{h}}}\geq\frac{|b_{h}(\widetilde{\bm{Q}}_{h}{\bm{w}},q)|}{\|\widetilde{\bm{Q}}_{h}{\bm{w}}\|_{{\bm{V}}_{h}}}\gtrsim\|q\|_{0,\Omega_{h}}.

This completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

By the standard theory of the mixed finite elements
[
9
]
, we know that system (
3.3
) admits a unique solution.
Moreover, the discrete inf-sup condition ensures that the discrete operator in (
3.3
) is stable in the sense that that unique solution to

\begin{cases}a_{h}({\bm{u}}_{h},{\bm{v}})+b_{h}({\bm{v}},p_{h})=F({\bm{v}})\quad&\forall{\bm{v}}\in{\bm{V}}_{h},\\
b_{h}({\bm{u}}_{h},q)=G(q)\quad&\forall q\in\Psi_{h},\end{cases}

satisfies

(3.10)

\|{\bm{u}}_{h}\|_{{\bm{V}}_{h}}+\|p_{h}\|_{\Psi_{h}}\lesssim\|F\|_{{\bm{V}}_{h}^{\prime}}+\|G\|_{\Psi_{h}^{\prime}}.

The stability result (
3.10
) is essential to the error analysis to be given next.

3.2.
Error analysis

To analyze the approximation error of the weak Galerkin discretization (
3.3
),
we first give two lemmas that help to simplify the derivation of error equations.
The proof of these two lemmas use quite standard techniques and will be given in Appendix
A
.

Lemma 3.6
.

The solution
{\bm{u}}
and
p
to system (
2.1
)-(
2.2
) satisfy

a_{h}\left(\widetilde{\bm{Q}}_{h}{\bm{u}},{\bm{v}}\right)+b_{h}\left({\bm{v}},\mathbb{Q}_{h}p\right)=c({\bm{Q}}_{h}{\bm{u}},{\bm{v}})+l_{s}({\bm{v}})-l_{\mathrm{div}}({\bm{v}})\qquad\forall\,{\bm{v}}\in{\bm{v}}_{h},

where
c(\cdot,\cdot)
is the stabilization term defined in (
3.2
)
and the linear functionals
l_{s}(\cdot)
and
l_{\mathrm{div}}(\cdot)
are defined by

\displaystyle l_{s}({\bm{v}})

\displaystyle=\rho\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}^{B}}h_{K}^{-1}\left\langle{\bm{u}}\cdot{\bf n},\left({\bm{v}}_{0}-{\bm{v}}_{b}\right)\cdot{\bf n}\right\rangle_{\partial K\cap\partial\Omega_{h}},

\displaystyle l_{\mathrm{div}}({\bm{v}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\langle({\bm{v}}_{0}-{\bm{v}}_{b})\cdot{\bf n},p-\mathbb{Q}_{h}p\rangle_{\partial K}.

Lemma 3.7
.

The solution
{\bm{u}}
to problem (
2.1
)-(
2.2
) satisfies

b_{h}\left(\widetilde{\bm{Q}}_{h}{\bm{u}},q\right)=-(g,q)_{\Omega_{h}}+l_{b}(q)\qquad\forall\,q\in\Psi_{h},

where the linear functional
l_{b}(\cdot)
is defined by

l_{b}(q)=\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}^{B}}\langle{{\bm{u}}}\cdot{{\bf n}},q\rangle_{\partial K\cap\partial\Omega_{h}}.

With the aid of lemmas
3.6
-
3.7
, we can easily derive the error equations.
Let
({\bm{u}},p)
be the solution to problem (
2.1
)-(
2.2
),
and
({\bm{u}}_{h},p_{h})
be the solution to the weak Galerkin discretization (
3.3
).
Define

(3.11)

{\bm{e}}_{{{\bm{u}}}}=\widetilde{\bm{Q}}_{h}{{\bm{u}}}-{\bm{u}}_{h}=\left\{{\bm{Q}}_{0}{{\bm{u}}}-{{\bm{u}}}_{0},\widetilde{\bm{Q}}_{b}{{\bm{u}}}-{{\bm{u}}}_{b}\right\},\quad e_{p}=\mathbb{Q}_{h}p-p_{h}.

Using lemmas
3.6
-
3.7
, we clearly have

(3.12)

\begin{cases}a_{h}\left({\bm{e}}_{{{\bm{u}}}},{\bm{v}}\right)+b_{h}\left({\bm{v}},e_{p}\right)=c({\bm{Q}}_{h}{\bm{u}},{\bm{v}})+l_{s}({\bm{v}})-l_{\text{div}}({\bm{v}})\qquad&\forall\,{\bm{v}}\in{\bm{V}}_{h},\\
b_{h}\left({\bm{e}}_{{{\bm{u}}}},q\right)=l_{b}(q)\quad&\forall\,q\in\Psi_{h}.\end{cases}

We shall first derive the upper bounds for the right-hand side of (
3.12
).

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
u\in{\bm{V}}\cap[H^{r+1}(\Omega)]^{2}
and
p\in\Psi\cap H^{t+1}(\Omega)
be the solution to problem (
2.1
)-(
2.2
),
where
0\leq r\leq\alpha
and
0\leq t\leq\sigma
.
Then

\displaystyle|c({{\bm{Q}}}_{h}\bm{{u}},{\bm{v}})|

\displaystyle\lesssim h^{r}\|{\bm{u}}\|_{r+1,\Omega}\|{\bm{v}}\|_{{\bm{V}}_{h}}\quad

\displaystyle\forall\,{\bm{v}}\in{\bm{V}}_{h},

\displaystyle|l_{\operatorname{div}}({\bm{v}})|

\displaystyle\lesssim h^{t+1}\|p\|_{t+1,\Omega}\|{\bm{v}}\|_{{\bm{V}}_{h}}\quad

\displaystyle\forall\,q\in\Psi_{h}.

Proof.

Note that
{\bm{Q}}_{0}{\bm{u}}={\bm{Q}}_{b}({\bm{Q}}_{0}{\bm{u}})
on each edge in
\mathcal{T}_{h}
.
Hence by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, the trace inequality (Lemma
2.4
) and the approximation property of
{\bm{Q}}_{0}
, we have

(3.13)

\displaystyle|c\left({\bm{Q}}_{h}{\bm{u}},{\bm{v}}\right)|

\displaystyle=\left|\rho\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}h_{K}^{-1}\left\langle\left({\bm{Q}}_{0}{\bm{u}}-{\bm{Q}}_{b}{\bm{u}}\right)\cdot{\bf n},\left({\bm{v}}_{0}-{\bm{v}}_{b}\right)\cdot{\bf n}\right\rangle_{\partial K}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\rho\left(\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}h_{K}^{-1}\left\|{\bm{Q}}_{0}{\bm{u}}-{\bm{Q}}_{b}{\bm{u}}\right\|_{0,\partial K}^{2}\right)^{1/2}\left(\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}h_{K}^{-1}\left\|({\bm{v}}_{0}-{\bm{v}}_{b})\cdot{\bf n}\right\|_{0,\partial K}^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\rho\left(\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}h_{K}^{-1}\left\|{\bm{Q}}_{0}{\bm{u}}-{\bm{u}}\right\|_{0,\partial K}^{2}\right)^{1/2}\left(\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}h_{K}^{-1}\left\|\left({\bm{v}}_{0}-{\bm{v}}_{b}\right)\cdot{\bf n}\right\|_{0,\partial K}^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\lesssim h^{r}\|{\bm{u}}\|_{r+1,\Omega}\|{\bm{v}}\|_{{\bm{V}}_{h}}.

Similarly, the upper bound for
l_{\mathrm{div}}({\bm{v}})
follows directly from Lemma
2.4
and the approximation property of
\mathbb{Q}_{h}
, that is,

\displaystyle|l_{\mathrm{div}}({\bm{v}})|

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\langle({\bm{v}}_{0}-{\bm{v}}_{b})\cdot{\bf n},p-\mathbb{Q}_{h}p\rangle_{\partial K}\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim\left(\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}h_{K}^{-1}\left\|({\bm{v}}_{0}-{\bm{v}}_{b})\cdot{\bf n}\right\|_{0,\partial K}^{2}\right)^{1/2}h^{t+1}\|p\|_{t+1,\Omega_{h}}

\displaystyle\lesssim h^{t+1}\|p\|_{t+1,\Omega}\|{\bm{v}}\|_{{\bm{V}}_{h}}.

This completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

Lemma 3.9
.

Assume that
{\bm{u}}\in{\bm{V}}\cap[H^{2}(\Omega)]^{2}
is the solution to problem (

)-(

(3.14)

\displaystyle|l_{b}(q)|

\displaystyle\lesssim h^{-\frac{3}{2}}s^{2}\|{\bm{u}}\|_{2,\Omega}\|q\|_{0,\Omega_{h}}

\displaystyle|l_{s}({\bm{v}})|

\displaystyle\lesssim h^{-\frac{1}{2}}s\|{\bm{u}}\|_{2,\Omega}\|{\bm{v}}\|_{{\bm{V}}_{h}}

\displaystyle\forall\,{\bm{v}}\in{\bm{V}}_{h}.

Figure 2.
M_{e}
in the local coordinate system.

Proof.

For each

is surrounded by the straight edge

.
Let

It is just for convenience that we draw both

As mentioned in the beginning of the proof of Lemma

Using Lemma

\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}_{h}^{B}}\|q\|_{0,M_{e}}^{2}\lesssim h^{-1}s^{2}\|q\|_{0,\Omega_{h}}^{2},\qquad\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}_{h}^{B}}\|\nabla q\|_{0,M_{e}}^{2}\lesssim h^{-3}s^{2}\|q\|_{0,\Omega_{h}}^{2}.

Combining the above and noticing that the solution

to problem (

\displaystyle|l_{b}(q)|=\left|-\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}_{h}^{B}}\langle{\bm{u}}\cdot{\bf n}_{e},q\rangle_{e}\right|

For any

\displaystyle\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}_{h}^{B}}\|(({\bm{v}}_{0}-{\bm{v}}_{b})\cdot{\bf n}_{e})^{*}\|_{0,M_{e}}^{2}

\displaystyle\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}_{h}^{B}}\|\nabla(({\bm{v}}_{0}-{\bm{v}}_{b})\cdot{\bf n}_{e})^{*}\|_{0,M_{e}}^{2}

\displaystyle|l_{s}({\bm{v}})|

∎

Now, we are able to present the error estimate:

Theorem 3.10
.

Let
{\bm{u}}
and

and Lemma

The error

\left\|{\bm{e}}_{{{\bm{u}}}}\right\|_{{\bm{V}}_{h}}+\left\|e_{p}\right\|_{0,\Omega_{h}}\lesssim h^{r}\|{\bm{u}}\|_{r+1,\Omega}+h^{t+1}\|p\|_{t+1,\Omega}+h^{-\frac{1}{2}}s\|{\bm{u}}\|_{2,\Omega},

Proof.

The theorem follows immediately from the mixed finite element theory,

-

,

∎

The error bound in Theorem

consists of two parts:
an approximation error

and a consistency error
h^{-\frac{1}{2}}s\|{\bm{u}}\|_{2,\Omega}
.
The consistency error comes purely from the discrepancy between
\Omega
an
\Omega_{h}
.
It vanishes if
\Omega=\Omega_{h}
.
Here we are only interested in the case when
\Omega
has curved boundary and hence
\Omega\neq\Omega_{h}
.
To fully examine the effect of the consistency error, we set

\alpha=\beta\geq 1
,
\sigma=\alpha-1
and assume that the exact solution satisfies
{\bm{u}}\in[H^{\alpha+1}(\Omega)]^{2}
,
p\in H^{\alpha}(\Omega)
,
so that the approximation error reaches its optimal order
O(h^{\alpha})
.
Then by adjusting
s
, we get the following results:

Corollary 3.11
.

Assuming the polynomial approximation error reaches the optimal
O(h^{\alpha})
with
\alpha\geq 1
.
On domains with curved boundary, when
s=O(h)
, the errors
\bm{e}_{{\bm{u}}}
and
e_{p}
satisfy

\left\|{\bm{e}}_{{{\bm{u}}}}\right\|_{{\bm{V}}_{h}}+\left\|e_{p}\right\|_{0,\Omega_{h}}\lesssim O(h^{\frac{1}{2}}).

Numerical results in Section
5
will show that the result in Corollary
3.11
is indeed sharp.

Corollary 3.12
.

Assuming that the polynomial approximation error reaches the optimal
O(h^{\alpha})
with
\alpha\geq 1
.
On domains with curved boundary, when
s=O(h^{\alpha+\frac{1}{2}})
, the errors
\bm{e}_{{\bm{u}}}
and
e_{p}
reach an optimal

\left\|{\bm{e}}_{{{\bm{u}}}}\right\|_{{\bm{V}}_{h}}+\left\|e_{p}\right\|_{0,\Omega_{h}}\lesssim O(h^{\alpha}).

When

is small, setting

means to approximate the curved boundary

with
multiple short edges. Numerical results in Section
5

will show that for moderate

(our experiments take

), this approach seem to work quite well.

4.
A modified weak Galerkin method

In this section, we construct a modified weak Galerkin discretization for problem (
2.1
)-(
2.2
),

for which the consistency error improves from
O(h^{-\frac{1}{2}}s)
to
O(h^{-\frac{1}{2}}s^{2})
.

Previously we have defined
{\bf n}
and
\widetilde{\bf n}
as the outward normal on
e\in\mathcal{E}_{h}^{B}
and
\tilde{e}
, respectively.
By using the map
\bm{\Gamma}
as shown in Figure
1
, the definition of
\widetilde{\bf n}
can be pulled to
e
through
\widetilde{\bf n}\circ\bm{\Gamma}
.
We still denote this pullback by
\widetilde{\bf n}
, i.e.,
\widetilde{\bf n}
is now also well-defined on
e\in\mathcal{E}_{h}^{B}
.
For simplicity of notation, on interior edges
e\in\mathcal{E}_{h}^{I}
we just set
\widetilde{\bf n}={\bf n}
.
Now we introduce the following modified bilinear forms

(4.1)

\displaystyle c_{1}({\bm{u}},{\bm{v}})

\displaystyle=\rho\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}h_{K}^{-1}\left\langle\left({\bm{u}}_{0}-{\bm{u}}_{b}\right)\cdot\widetilde{\bf n},\left({\bm{v}}_{0}-{\bm{v}}_{b}\right)\cdot\widetilde{\bf n}\right\rangle_{\partial K},

\displaystyle a_{h,1}({\bm{u}},{\bm{v}})

\displaystyle=\left({\bm{u}}_{0},{\bm{v}}_{0}\right)_{\Omega_{h}}+c_{1}({\bm{u}},{\bm{v}}),

while the definition
b_{h}({\bm{v}},q)=-(\nabla_{w}\cdot{\bm{v}},q)
remains unchanged.

Remark 4.1
.

The definition of
b_{h}(\cdot,\cdot)
depends on
\nabla_{w}\cdot
which in turn depends on the choice of outward normal vectors on each
K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
.
We emphasize that the definition of
\nabla_{w}\cdot
still uses
{\bf n}
instead of
\widetilde{\bf n}
.
Hence
b_{h}(\cdot,\cdot)
remains unchanged.

The modified weak Galerkin formulation for problem (
2.1
)-(
2.2
) reads as follows:

Find
{\bm{u}}_{h}\in{\bm{V}}_{h}
and
p_{h}\in\Psi_{h}
such that

(4.2)

\begin{cases}a_{h,1}({\bm{u}}_{h},{\bm{v}})+b_{h}({\bm{v}},p_{h})=0\quad&\forall\,{\bm{v}}\in{\bm{V}}_{h},\\
b_{h}({\bm{u}}_{h},q)-\langle{\bm{u}}_{0}\cdot{\bf n}-\overline{{\bm{u}}_{0}\cdot{\bf n}},q\rangle_{\partial\Omega_{h}}=-(g,q)_{\Omega_{h}}\quad&\forall\,q\in\Psi_{h},\end{cases}

where
\langle{\bm{u}}_{0}\cdot{\bf n}-\overline{{\bm{u}}_{0}\cdot{\bf n}},q\rangle_{\partial\Omega_{h}}
in the second equation is a boundary correction term with

\overline{{\bm{u}}_{0}\cdot{\bf n}}|_{e}=\frac{1}{|e|}\int_{e}{\bm{u}}_{0}\cdot{\bf n}\,\mathrm{d}s,\quad\forall e\in\mathcal{E}_{h}^{B}.

Remark 4.2
.

The term
\overline{{\bm{u}}_{0}\cdot{\bf n}}
is added for two purposes.
First, it ensures that the left-hand side of the second equation of (
4.2
) vanishes for
q=const
.
Then, under the discrete inf-sup condition to be proved later,
the kernels of both the stiffness matrix for (
4.2
) and its transpose are
q\equiv const
.
As discussed earlier in Remark
3.2
,
one does not need to worry about the ‘compatibility’ issue for the modified problem (
4.2
).
Second, it gives an
O(s)
asymptotic rate to the boundary correction term, which will play an important role in the current proof
of the discrete inf-sup condition.

Similar to the analysis in Section
3
, its not hard to see that

\|{\bm{v}}\|_{\bm{V}_{h,1}}=\sqrt{a_{h,1}({\bm{v}},{\bm{v}})}

is a well-defined norm on
{\bm{V}}_{h}
.
Moreover, we have

Lemma 4.3
.

One has
\|{\bm{v}}\|_{\bm{V}_{h,1}}\approx\|{\bm{v}}\|_{{\bm{V}}_{h}}
for all
{\bm{v}}\in{\bm{V}}_{h}
.

Proof.

By the mesh assumptions
A4
and
A6
,
the trace and the inverse inequality (lemmas
2.4
-
2.5
),
the facts that
{\bm{v}}_{b}=\bm{0}
on
\partial\Omega_{h}
and each
K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}^{B}

contains at most
O(\frac{h}{s})
edges in
\mathcal{E}_{h}^{B}
,
one gets

\rho\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}h_{K}^{-1}\left\|\left({\bm{v}}_{0}-{\bm{v}}_{b}\right)\cdot(\widetilde{\bf n}-{\bf n})\right\|_{0,\partial K\cap\partial\Omega_{h}}^{2}\lesssim\|{\bm{v}}_{0}\|_{0,\Omega_{h}}^{2}.

The lemma then follows from the above inequality, the definitions of
\|\cdot\|_{{\bm{V}}_{h}}
and
\|\cdot\|_{\bm{V}_{h,1}}
, and the triangle inequality.
∎

Denote the left-hand side of the second equation in (

) by

b_{h,1}({\bm{u}}_{h},q)=b_{h}({\bm{u}}_{h},q)-\langle{\bm{u}}_{0}\cdot{\bf n}-\overline{{\bm{u}}_{0}\cdot{\bf n}},q\rangle_{\partial\Omega_{h}}.

System (

) is non-symmetric. Non-symmetric mixed systems have been studied in
[
5
,
17
,
32
]
.
We follow the theoretical framework in
[
32
]
. Since

is coercive on the entire

,
stability of (

) only requires that both

and

satisfy the discrete inf-sup condition
(see
[

]
and also Remark 4.2.7 in

]
), which we prove in the following lemma:

Lemma 4.4
.

When
h
and
\frac{s}{h}
are sufficiently small,

both

and
b_{h,1}(\cdot,\cdot)
satisfy the discrete
\inf
-
\sup
condition under the modified norm
\|\cdot\|_{\bm{V}_{h,1}}

(4.3)

\displaystyle\sup_{{\bm{v}}\in{\bm{V}}_{h}}\frac{|b_{h}({\bm{v}},q)|}{\|{\bm{v}}\|_{\bm{V}_{h,1}}}

\displaystyle\gtrsim\|q\|_{0,\Omega_{h}}\qquad\forall\,q\in\Psi_{h},

\displaystyle\sup_{{\bm{v}}\in{\bm{V}}_{h}}\frac{|b_{h,1}({\bm{v}},q)|}{\|{\bm{v}}\|_{\bm{V}_{h,1}}}

\displaystyle\gtrsim\|q\|_{0,\Omega_{h}}\qquad\forall\,q\in\Psi_{h}.

Proof.

Using lemmas
3.5
and
4.3
, one immediately gets the discrete inf-sup condition for
b_{h}(\cdot,\cdot)

when
h
is sufficiently small
.
As for
b_{h,1}(\cdot,\cdot)
, through a careful examination of the proof of Lemma
3.5
,

it is not hard
to see that one only needs to prove
(using the same
{\bm{w}}
as in the proof of Lemma
3.5
)

(4.4)

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}^{B}}h_{K}^{-1}\|{\bm{Q}}_{0}\bm{w}\cdot{\bf n}-\overline{{\bm{Q}}_{0}\bm{w}\cdot{\bf n}}\|_{0,\partial K\cap\partial\Omega_{h}}^{2}\right)^{1/2}\left(\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}^{B}}h_{K}\|q\|_{0,\partial K\cap\partial\Omega_{h}}^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq C(h,s)\|q\|_{0,\Omega_{h}}^{2}

\displaystyle<\|q\|_{0,\Omega_{h}}^{2},

with

strictly bounded below

.
By (
3.8
), this reduces to proving a bound for

\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}^{B}}h_{K}^{-1}\|{\bm{Q}}_{0}\bm{w}\cdot{\bf n}-\overline{{\bm{Q}}_{0}\bm{w}\cdot{\bf n}}\|_{0,\partial K\cap\partial\Omega_{h}}^{2}

which can be achieved by using the trace inequality and the inverse inequality

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}^{B}}h_{K}^{-1}\|{\bm{Q}}_{0}\bm{w}\cdot{\bf n}-\overline{{\bm{Q}}_{0}\bm{w}\cdot{\bf n}}\|_{0,\partial K\cap\partial\Omega_{h}}^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left(s^{2}\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}^{B}}h_{K}^{-1}\|\nabla({\bm{Q}}_{0}\bm{w})\|_{0,\partial K\cap\partial\Omega_{h}}^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left(s^{2}\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}^{B}}h^{-2}\|\nabla({\bm{Q}}_{0}\bm{w})\|_{0,K}^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{s}{h}\|\bm{w}\|_{1,\Omega},

Thus the coefficient

in (

The rest of the proof follows directly from the proof of Lemma
3.5
.
∎

Remark 4.5
.

When the curved boundary of

is approximated by multiple short edges,

The proof does not work for the case of

.
We suspect that a better discrete inf-sup condition, with less restrictions on

Remark 4.6
.

The proof of Lemma

depends on the

According to Theorem 3.1 in

]

Lemma 4.8
