A multipoint perturbation formula for eigenvalue problems
Geneviève Dusson, Louis Garrigue, Benjamin Stamm

Abstract
. Standard perturbation theory of eigenvalue problems consists of obtaining approximations of eigenmodes in the neighborhood of a Hamiltonian where the corresponding eigenmode is known. Nevertheless, if the corresponding eigenmodes of several nearby Hamiltonians are known, standard perturbation theory cannot simultaneously use all this knowledge to provide a better approximation. We derive a formula enabling such an approximation result, and provide numerical examples for which this method is more competitive than standard perturbation theory.



1.
Introduction

Eigenvalue problems are ubiquitous in applied mathematics, for instance in quantum physics, structural mechanics, graph theory and optimization.
Numerical methods to solve these eigenvalue problems are hence key to obtain accurate approximate solutions in an efficient and robust way. Let us take a Hilbert space, consider
H^{0}
to be a self-adjoint operator on this Hilbert space, and define a family of self-adjoint operators

\displaystyle H(G):=H^{0}+G

called Hamiltonians, where
G
is a parametrized self-adjoint operator. We consider the eigenvalue problem associated to
H(G)
under variation of
G
. A widely used approach to treat eigenvalue problems is based on perturbation theory,
where one knows or can easily compute the eigenstates of an operator
H(G_{1})
for some
G_{1}
and deduces properties of approximate solutions for the close problem
H(G_{1})+g
, where
g
is small in some sense to be made precise later, see
[
2
,
1
,
3
]
and
[
7
, Section XII]
for a presentation of perturbation theory for eigenvalue problems.
In other words, from spectral information on
H(G_{1})
, one can deduce information on the perturbed
H(G_{1})+g
for small perturbations
g
, thus locally around
H(G_{1})
.

In contrast, when the solution of an eigenvalue problem with Hamiltonian
H(G_{j})
is known at
n
points
\left(G_{j}\right)_{j=1}^{n}
, standard perturbation theory does not exploit the full approximation power of using the information at all
n
points to obtain approximations at a new point
G
as it will only use local information of the closest point
G_{j}
.
We illustrate the comparison between single point and multipoint perturbation theory on Figure
1
.

In standard perturbation theory, one gets approximations of the solution

by using only the closest

, while in multipoint perturbation we simultaneously use all the
G_{j}
’s on which we know the solutions.

Let us now detail our framework. We consider some admissible set
\mathcal{G}
of operators
G
’s, being the space of parameters for the Hamiltonian.
For
G\in\mathcal{G}
and
k\in\mathbb{N}\backslash\{0\}
, we denote by
\mathcal{F}\left(G\right)
the spectral projection onto the eigenspace corresponding to the
k^{\text{th}}
lowest eigenvalue of the operator
H(G)
, which we assume to be non-degenerate. It is clear that the knowledge of
\mathcal{F}(G)
is equivalent to know an eigenvector corresponding to the
k^{\text{th}}
eigenvalue.
Our framework can be generalized to projectors onto the space generated by several eigenvalues, and those eigenvalues could lie anywhere in the discrete spectrum since our methodology relies on spectral projectors, or, in other words, on density matrices for the selected states.
However, this goes beyond the scope of this work and we restrict our analysis within this article to the non-degenerate case.

The motivation for this work comes from
[
4
,
5
]
, where it is observed that if
G
is a linear combination of
G_{j}
’s, that is

G=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}G_{j},

then the density matrix appears to be well approximated by the same linear combination of the corresponding density matrix, i.e.

\mathcal{F}(G)\simeq\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}\mathcal{F}(G_{j}).

This amounts to say that the map
G\mapsto\mathcal{F}(G)
is locally very close to linear, under some conditions.
In this work, we aim at partly explaining this phenomenon developping a multipoint perturbation theory (a precise meaning will be given later).

This article is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we provide the mathematical setting and present a review of standard perturbation theory. In Section
3
, we present our main result, the resolvent formula (
12
), and compute the first orders in the expansion of
\mathcal{F}(G)
in Section
4
. We illustrate our work with numerical simulations on Schrödinger operators in Section
5
. We conclude that the produced approximations are particularly relevant when the
G_{j}
’s are close to each other, and when the
G
on which one wants the solution is close to the affine space spanned by the
G_{j}
’s. All the proofs are provided in Section
6
.

2.
Mathematical setting

In this section, we define the main mathematical objects that will be needed later on for the presentation of the multipoint perturbation method itself and the theoretical analysis that follows.

2.1.
Hamiltonians

First, we consider a separable Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
endowed with a scalar product
\left<\cdot,\cdot\right>
, with corresponding norm
\|\cdot\|
. Inequalities on operators will be considered in the sense of forms, meaning that for two operators
A
and
B
, we write
A\leqslant B
if there is a vector subspace
\mathcal{H}_{0}\subset\mathcal{H}
, dense in
\mathcal{H}
, such that
\left<\psi,A\psi\right>\leqslant\left<\psi,B\psi\right>
for all
\psi\in\mathcal{H}_{0}
. We consider a self-adjoint operator
H^{0}
on
\mathcal{H}
, satisfying
H^{0}\geqslant\alpha
for some
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
.
Throughout the paper,
H^{0}
will be a fixed operator, for instance
-\Delta
in the case of Schrödinger operators. Moreover, for a given operator
A
, we denote its adjoint operator by
A^{*}
.

We define the
set of admissible perturbations
by

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}:=\big\{G\text{ linear operator on }\mathcal{H},\;G^{*}=G,\;\forall\varepsilon>0\;\exists c^{G}_{\varepsilon}>0,\;\left|G\right|\leqslant\varepsilon H^{0}+c^{G}_{\varepsilon}\big\},

(1)

where
|A|:=\sqrt{A^{*}A}
is defined via the spectral theorem. For any
G\in\mathcal{G}
, by the Kato–Lions–Lax–Milgram–Nelson (KLMN) theorem
[
6
, p. 323]
, the Friedrichs extension of

\displaystyle H(G)=H^{0}+G

is a well-defined self-adjoint and bounded from below operator. Considering the set (
1
) enables to take into account Schrödinger operators perturbed by, e.g., singular potentials. We could also treat operators
H(G)
which are not bounded from below, this extension is immediate from our presentation.

The finite-dimensional case is covered by our analysis as we make no assumption whether the Hilbert space is finite or infinite-dimensional. In the finite-dimensional case,
\mathcal{G}
is the space of
m\times m
matrices, where
m
is the dimension of the considered Hilbert space.

2.2.
Non-degeneracy assumption

We use the notation
\mathbb{N}=\{0,1,\dots\}
, so
0\in\mathbb{N}
, and we will use the notation
\mathbb{N}\backslash\{0\}
for the set of strictly positive integers. Since we only consider non-degenerate eigenmodes, we take

k\in\mathbb{N}\backslash\{0\},

fixed
throughout all the document. Moreover, in all this article, for any
G\in\mathcal{G}
and any
p\in\mathbb{N}\backslash\{0\}
, we denote by
\lambda^{p}(G)
the
p^{\text{th}}
eigenvalue of
H(G)
, counting multiplicities.

For any
\left(G_{j}\right)_{j=1}^{n}\in\mathcal{G}^{n}
, we assume that the
k^{\text{th}}
eigenvalue is always separated from the rest of the spectrum, and that it remains so as we navigate on the corresponding admissible space. To translate this into mathematical terms, we first define for any
\lambda_{\textup{min}},\lambda_{\textup{max}}\in\mathbb{R}
the set

\displaystyle\Omega_{\lambda_{\textup{min}}}^{\lambda_{\textup{max}}}:=\left\{G\in\mathcal{G}\;\middle|\;\sigma(H(G))\cap[\lambda_{\textup{min}},\lambda_{\textup{max}}]=\{\lambda^{k}(G)\}\right\},

(2)

where
\sigma(A)
denotes the spectrum of
A
for any operator
A
. We then formalize the following assumption.

Assumption 2.1
.

There holds

\displaystyle\exists\lambda_{\textup{min}},\lambda_{\textup{max}}\in\mathbb{R},\;\lambda_{\textup{min}}<\lambda_{\textup{max}},\qquad\mbox{such that}\qquad\text{Conv }\left(G_{j}\right)_{j=1}^{n}\subset\Omega_{\lambda_{\textup{min}}}^{\lambda_{\textup{max}}},

(3)

where “Conv” denotes the convex hull.

In the following, we will consider
G
’s such that

\displaystyle G\in\Omega_{\lambda_{\textup{min}}}^{\lambda_{\textup{max}}}.

Moreover, in all this document,
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathbb{C}
will denote a contour, say a rectangle, such that
\mathcal{C}\cap\mathbb{R}=\{\lambda_{\textup{min}},\lambda_{\textup{max}}\}
. From Assumption
2.1
, this contour will enclose exactly one eigenvalue of
H(G)
, namely the
k^{\text{th}}
eigenvalue, for any
G\in\Omega_{\lambda_{\textup{min}}}^{\lambda_{\textup{max}}}
.

2.3.
Operator norms

Take
\mu\in\;]-\inf\sigma(H^{0}),+\infty[
, and
\kappa\in[0,1]
. For any operator on
\mathcal{H}
, we define the operator norm as

\displaystyle\left|\!\left|A\right|\!\right|:=\left|\!\left|A\right|\!\right|_{\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}}=\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}\psi\in\mathcal{H}\\
\psi\neq 0\end{subarray}}{\text{{sup}}}\quad\frac{\left|\!\left|A\psi\right|\!\right|}{\left|\!\left|\psi\right|\!\right|},

which can be equal to
+\infty
. The set
\{A:\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}\;|\;\left|\!\left|A\right|\!\right|<+\infty\}
is usually denoted by
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
. We define, for any operator
\Gamma
of
\mathcal{H}
,

\displaystyle\left|\!\left|\Gamma\right|\!\right|_{e}:=\left|\!\left|\left(H^{0}+\mu\right)^{\frac{\kappa}{2}}\;\Gamma\;\left(H^{0}+\mu\right)^{\frac{\kappa}{2}}\right|\!\right|

(4)

which will be the norm on the space of density matrices.
The norm on the parameter space is the dual one, which is given by,

\displaystyle\left|\!\left|G\right|\!\right|_{a}:=\left|\!\left|\left(H^{0}+\mu\right)^{-\frac{\kappa}{2}}\;G\;\left(H^{0}+\mu\right)^{-\frac{\kappa}{2}}\right|\!\right|,

(5)

defined for any operator
G
of
\mathcal{H}
.
Taking
\kappa=1
is the most natural choice from a theoretical point of view since
\left|\!\left|\cdot\right|\!\right|_{e}
becomes the natural energy norm in this case.
However, the following proofs work for any
\kappa\in[0,1]
.
In the numerical section, we will also take
\kappa=1
, but one could consider
\kappa=0
to simplify the implementation.

2.4.
Resolvents and density matrices

Let
\mathcal{C}
be the contour introduced in Section
2.2
relying on Assumption
2.1
and take
G\in\Omega_{\lambda_{\textup{min}}}^{\lambda_{\textup{max}}}
.
Given the resolvent
\left(z-H(G)\right)^{-1}
of
H(G)
, we define

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(G):=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{\mathcal{C}}\left(z-H(G)\right)^{-1}{\rm d}z.

(6)

By the spectral theorem, see
[
7
, Section XII]
,
\mathcal{F}(G)
is the spectral projection onto the one-dimensional space spanned by an eigenvector corresponding to the
k^{\text{th}}
eigenvalue of
H(G)
.

2.5.
Pseudo-inverses

We consider
G\in\mathcal{G}
and Assumption
2.1
of Section
2.2
, where a level
k\in\mathbb{N}\backslash\{0\}
was defined.
We now introduce the pseudo-inverse operator

\displaystyle K(G):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\left(\left(\lambda^{k}(G)-H(G)\right)_{\mkern 1.0mu\vrule height=6.02777pt\mkern 2.0mu\left(\Ker\left(\lambda^{k}(G)-H(G)\right)\right)^{\perp}}\right)^{-1}&\mbox{on }\left(\Ker\left(\lambda^{k}(G)-H(G)\right)\right)^{\perp},\\
0&\mbox{on }\Ker\left(\lambda^{k}(G)-H(G)\right),\end{array}\right.

extended on the whole of
\mathcal{H}
by linearity, that will be needed in the upcoming perturbation theory.
We recall that
\Ker\left(\lambda^{k}(G_{j})-H(G_{j})\right)
is the one-dimensional vector space spanned by an eigenvector corresponding to the
k^{\text{th}}
eigenvalue of
H(G_{j})
.

Let us illustrate this definition for a finite-dimensional Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
. In this case,
H(G)
is a hermitian
matrix, and taking
\left(u_{a}(G)\right)_{a=1}^{\dim\mathcal{H}}
a basis of
\mathcal{H}
formed by eigenvectors of
H(G)
sorted such that the corresponding eigenvalues
\left(\lambda^{a}(G)\right)_{a=1}^{\dim\mathcal{H}}
are increasing, provides the explicit representation for the pseudo-inverse

\displaystyle K(G)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant a\leqslant\dim\mathcal{H}\\
a\neq k\end{subarray}}\left(\lambda^{k}(G)-\lambda^{a}(G)\right)^{-1}P_{u_{a}(G)},

where
P_{u}
is the projection operator on the vector space spanned by
u\in\mathcal{H}
.

2.6.
Standard linear perturbation theory

We now present standard perturbation theory, in the aim of comparing it with the proposed multipoint perturbation method. Let us take
G_{1}\in\mathcal{G}
, consider the non-degeneracy assumption
2.1
and take
G\in\Omega_{\lambda_{\textup{min}}}^{\lambda_{\textup{max}}}
. We consider that the unperturbed operator is
H(G_{1})
i.e. we assume knowledge of
P_{1}:=\mathcal{F}(G_{1})
and
K_{1}:=K(G_{1})
using (
2.5
). Standard perturbation theory consists in deducing approximations of
\mathcal{F}(G)
in series of
G-G_{1}
. The base resolvent equation is

\displaystyle\left(z-H(G)\right)^{-1}=\left(z-H(G_{1})\right)^{-1}\left(1-(G-G_{1})\left(z-H(G_{1})\right)^{-1}\right)^{-1}.

(9)

We then define for
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\ell}:=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{\mathcal{C}}(z-H(G_{1}))^{-1}\left((G-G_{1})(z-H(G_{1}))^{-1}\right)^{\ell}{\rm d}z,\qquad\mathbb{P}_{\ell}:=\sum_{p=0}^{\ell}\mathcal{P}_{p},

and recall the following classical result
[
2
,
7
]
.

Proposition 2.2

(Standard linear perturbation theory bound)
.

Take
k\in\mathbb{N}\backslash\{0\}
,
G_{1},G\in\mathcal{G}
satisfying the non-degeneracy assumption
2.1
, consider the contour
\mathcal{C}
as in Section
2.2
, define
g:=G-G_{1}
and
C:=\max_{z\in\mathcal{C}}\left|\!\left|\left(z-H(G_{1})\right)^{-1}\right|\!\right|_{e}
, and assume that
\left|\!\left|g\right|\!\right|_{a}<1/(2C)
. Then there exists a constant
c>0
independent of
g
and
\ell
such that for any
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle\left|\!\left|\mathcal{F}(G)-\mathbb{P}_{\ell}\right|\!\right|_{e}\leqslant c\left(C\left|\!\left|g\right|\!\right|_{a}\right)^{\ell+1}.

(10)

For the sake of completeness, we provide a proof in Section
6
.
The terms
\mathbb{P}_{\ell}
can be obtained explicitly using Cauchy’s integration formula. For instance, the four first order terms read

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{0}

\displaystyle=P_{1},

(11)

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{1}

\displaystyle=P_{1}gK_{1}+K_{1}gP_{1},

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{2}

\displaystyle=\left(P_{1}gK_{1}gK_{1}+K_{1}gP_{1}gK_{1}+K_{1}gK_{1}gP_{1}\right)-(P_{1}gP_{1}gK_{1}^{2}+P_{1}gK_{1}^{2}gP_{1}+K_{1}^{2}gP_{1}gP_{1}),

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{3}

\displaystyle=(P_{1}gP_{1}gP_{1}gK_{1}^{3}+P_{1}gP_{1}gK_{1}^{3}gP_{1}+P_{1}gK_{1}^{3}gP_{1}gP_{1}+K_{1}^{3}gP_{1}gP_{1}gP_{1})

\displaystyle\qquad+(P_{1}gK_{1}gK_{1}gK_{1}+K_{1}gP_{1}gK_{1}gK_{1}+K_{1}gK_{1}gP_{1}gK_{1}+K_{1}gK_{1}gK_{1}gP_{1})

\displaystyle\qquad-\left(P_{1}gP_{1}gK_{1}^{2}gK_{1}+P_{1}gP_{1}gK_{1}gK_{1}^{2}+K_{1}gK_{1}^{2}gP_{1}gP_{1}+K_{1}^{2}gK_{1}gP_{1}gP_{1}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad-\left(P_{1}gK_{1}gK_{1}^{2}gP_{1}+P_{1}gK_{1}^{2}gK_{1}gP_{1}+P_{1}gK_{1}gP_{1}gK_{1}^{2}+P_{1}gK_{1}^{2}gP_{1}gK_{1}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad-\left(K_{1}gP_{1}gK_{1}^{2}gP_{1}+K_{1}^{2}gP_{1}gK_{1}gP_{1}+K_{1}gP_{1}gP_{1}gK_{1}^{2}+K_{1}^{2}gP_{1}gP_{1}gK_{1}\right).

3.
Multipoint resolvent formula

In this section, we finally introduce the multipoint perturbation theory, which, compared to standard perturbation theory, allows to retain more information of a series of density matrices related to
G_{1},\ldots,G_{n}
.

3.1.
Main formula

The following formula is the main result of this document. Its purpose is to express the resolvent
\left(z-H(G)\right)^{-1}
in terms of the known resolvents
\left(z-H(G_{j})\right)^{-1}
, so that the orthogonal projection
\mathcal{F}(G)
can also be expanded in terms of known terms
as will be shown in Section
4.2
.

Theorem 3.1

(Multipoint resolvent formula)
.

Take
\bm{\alpha}=\left(\alpha_{j}\right)_{j=1}^{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\text{\boldmath$G$}=\left(G_{j}\right)_{j=1}^{n}\in\mathcal{G}^{n}
,
G\in\mathcal{G}
,
z\in\mathbb{C}\backslash\left(\sigma(H(G))\cup_{j=1}^{n}\sigma(H(G_{j}))\right)
and
s_{\text{\boldmath$\alpha$}}:=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}
such that
s_{\text{\boldmath$\alpha$}}\neq 0
. We define the operators

\displaystyle\mathbb{H}_{z}

\displaystyle:=s_{\text{\boldmath$\alpha$}}^{-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant i<j\leqslant n\end{subarray}}\alpha_{i}\alpha_{j}\left(G_{i}-G_{j}\right)\left(z-H(G_{j})\right)^{-1}\left(G_{i}-G_{j}\right)\left(z-H(G_{i})\right)^{-1},

\displaystyle\mathbb{A}_{z}

\displaystyle:=s_{\text{\boldmath$\alpha$}}^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}G_{j}\left(z-H(G_{j})\right)^{-1},\qquad\qquad\mathbb{L}_{z}:=s_{\text{\boldmath$\alpha$}}^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}\left(z-H(G_{j})\right)^{-1}.

We also define
g:=G-\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}G_{j}
. If
{1+\mathbb{H}_{z}-(s_{\text{\boldmath$\alpha$}}-1)\mathbb{A}_{z}-g\mathbb{L}_{z}}
is invertible, there holds

\displaystyle\boxed{\left(z-H\left(G\right)\right)^{-1}=\mathbb{L}_{z}\left(1+\mathbb{H}_{z}-(s_{\text{\boldmath$\alpha$}}-1)\mathbb{A}_{z}-g\mathbb{L}_{z}\right)^{-1}.}

(12)

The proof is given in Section
6
.

Remark 3.2

(Linearity)
.

Formula (
12
) provides an explicit formula for the resolvent of the Hamiltonian
H(G)
that is expressed as a linear combination of the resolvents of
H(G_{1}),\ldots,H(G_{n})
, namely
\mathbb{L}_{z}
, times a perturbation term when
\mathbb{H}_{z}-(s_{\text{\boldmath$\alpha$}}-1)\mathbb{A}_{z}-g\mathbb{L}_{z}
is small.

Remark 3.3

(
\alpha
is our degree of freedom)
.

Let us note that the degrees of freedom to use the formula are the coefficients
\alpha_{j}
for
j
from 1 to
n
, which can be freely chosen in
\mathbb{R}
as long as
\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}\neq 0
.

Remark 3.4

(Term involving
(1-s_{\text{\boldmath$\alpha$}})
)
.

We cannot “absorb”
(s_{\text{\boldmath$\alpha$}}-1)\mathbb{A}_{z}
in
-g\mathbb{L}_{z}
in the sense that in general,
(s_{\text{\boldmath$\alpha$}}-1)\mathbb{A}_{z}+g\mathbb{L}_{z}
cannot be written as
\widetilde{g}\,\mathbb{L}_{z}
for some operator
\widetilde{g}
, i.e., the two operators can be linearly independent. Hence we a priori need to keep both terms in the expansion. Note however that if we impose
s_{\text{\boldmath$\alpha$}}=1
, then the term in
(s_{\text{\boldmath$\alpha$}}-1)\mathbb{A}_{z}
simply disappears.

Remark 3.5

(Reduction to standard linear perturbation theory)
.

If we take
\alpha_{1}=1
and
\alpha_{j}=0
for all
j\in\{2,\dots,n\}
, or
G_{j}=G_{1}
for any
j\in\{2,\dots,n\}
, formula (
12
) boils down to (
9
). Indeed, in the latter case,
\mathbb{H}_{z}=0
,
\mathbb{A}_{z}=G_{1}\left(z-H(G_{1})\right)^{-1}
and
\mathbb{L}_{z}=\left(z-H(G_{1})\right)^{-1}
from which we easily obtain (
9
). Hence we recover standard perturbation theory.

3.2.
Three perturbation parameters

The next step of this multipoint linear perturbation theory is to expand the expression

\displaystyle\left(1+\mathbb{H}_{z}-(s_{\text{\boldmath$\alpha$}}-1)\mathbb{A}_{z}-g\mathbb{L}_{z}\right)^{-1}

(13)

appearing in (
12
) in terms of a Neumann series, so the perturbative terms can be integrated over the complex contour
\mathcal{C}
and give approximations for the density matrix
\mathcal{F}(G)
. For this, we need
\mathbb{H}_{z}-(s_{\text{\boldmath$\alpha$}}-1)\mathbb{A}_{z}-g\mathbb{L}_{z}
to be small enough. Let us therefore define the three smallness parameters

\displaystyle\delta_{\text{\boldmath$\alpha$},\text{\boldmath$G$}}:=\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant i,j\leqslant n\end{subarray}}{\text{{max}}}\;\sqrt{|\alpha_{i}\alpha_{j}|}\left|\!\left|G_{i}-G_{j}\right|\!\right|_{a},\qquad\delta_{\text{\boldmath$\alpha$}}:=\left|1-\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}\right|,\qquad\delta_{g}:=\left|\!\left|g\right|\!\right|_{a},

recalling
g=G-\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}G_{j}
, and where

•

\delta_{\text{\boldmath$\alpha$},\text{\boldmath$G$}}
enters the estimation of
\mathbb{H}_{z}
, it is small when all the
G_{j}
’s are close to each others or when
\alpha_{j}
is small,

•

\delta_{\text{\boldmath$\alpha$}}
enters into
(s_{\text{\boldmath$\alpha$}}-1)\mathbb{A}_{z}
, it is small when
\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}
is close to 1,

•

\delta_{g}
allows to estimate
g\mathbb{L}_{z}
, it is small when
G
is close to
\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}G_{j}
.

All three parameters need to be small for the expansion of (
13
) to be possible. In fact, a sufficient condition is that the
G_{j}
’s are close to each other and
G
is close to the affine space

\displaystyle\Aff\left(G_{j}\right)_{j=1}^{n}:=\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}G_{j}\;\middle|\;\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}=1\right\},

which is the smallest affine subspace of
\mathcal{G}
containing all the
G_{j}
’s.

3.3.
Perturbation bound

The final step consists in integrating the multipoint perturbative resolvent formulation along the complex contour
\mathcal{C}
to obtain corresponding approximations for the density matrix
\mathcal{F}(G)
via Cauchy’s formula (
6
). We first define the perturbation terms

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{(2a,b,c)}

\displaystyle:=\frac{(-1)^{a}}{2\pi i}(s_{\text{\boldmath$\alpha$}}-1)^{b}\oint_{\mathcal{C}}\mathbb{L}_{z}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}B_{1},\dots,B_{a+b+c}\in\{\mathbb{H}_{z},\mathbb{A}_{z},g\mathbb{L}_{z}\}\\
\left|\{i\;|\;B_{i}=\mathbb{H}_{z}\}\right|=a\\
\left|\{i\;|\;B_{i}=\mathbb{A}_{z}\}\right|=b\\
\left|\{i\;|\;B_{i}=g\mathbb{L}_{z}\}\right|=c\end{subarray}}B_{1}B_{2}\cdots B_{a+b+c}{\rm d}z,

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{(2a+1,b,c)}

\displaystyle:=0,\qquad\qquad\mathcal{D}_{\ell}:=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a,b,c\in\mathbb{N}\\
a+b+c=\ell\end{subarray}}\mathcal{D}_{(a,b,c)},\qquad\qquad\mathbb{D}_{\ell}:=\sum_{p=0}^{\ell}\mathcal{D}_{p},

(14)

In the summation formula for
\mathcal{D}_{(2a,b,c)}
, we consider all the combinations of products
B_{p}
’s in
\{\mathbb{H}_{z},\mathbb{A}_{z},g\mathbb{L}_{z}\}
, each once, with the convention that the empty sum on the first line is taken as the identity ; to clarify how to compute them, particular examples will be presented in the upcoming Section
4.2
. Then we have the following result.

Corollary 3.6

(Multipoint perturbation bound)
.

Let us take

Case

3.5.
Exploiting the symmetry group of
H^{0}

If the Hamiltonian
H^{0}
is invariant with respect to some symmetry, one can exploit it to
approximate density matrices for many more admissible
G
’s, without additional knowledge. The corresponding principle is explained in the following. We introduce the symmetry group of
H^{0}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}:=\left\{U\text{ bounded linear operator of }\mathcal{H},\;U^{*}U={\mathds{1}},\;[H^{0},U]=0\right\}.

For instance if
H^{0}=-\Delta
,
\mathcal{U}
is the Galilean group, i.e. the group spanned by translations and rotations. For any
U\in\mathcal{U}
, and any
G\in\mathcal{G}
, we have

\displaystyle U\left(z-H(G)\right)^{-1}U^{*}=\left(z-UH(G)U^{*}\right)^{-1}=\left(z-H(UGU^{*})\right)^{-1},

so by integration,
U\mathcal{F}(G)U^{*}=\mathcal{F}(UGU^{*})
. Hence it is natural to use this information to extend the multipoint perturbation formula (
12
).
Thus, denoting
\text{\boldmath$U$}=(U_{1},\ldots,U_{n})
with
U_{i}\in\mathcal{U}
,
defining

\displaystyle\mathbb{H}_{z,\text{\boldmath$U$}}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant i<j\leqslant n\end{subarray}}\alpha_{i}\alpha_{j}\left(U_{i}G_{i}U_{i}^{*}-U_{j}G_{j}U_{j}^{*}\right)U_{j}\left(z-H(G_{j})\right)^{-1}U_{j}^{*}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\times\left(U_{i}G_{i}U_{i}^{*}-U_{j}G_{j}U_{j}^{*}\right)U_{i}\left(z-H(G_{i})\right)^{-1}U_{i}^{*},

\displaystyle\mathbb{A}_{z,\text{\boldmath$U$}}

\displaystyle:=s_{\text{\boldmath$\alpha$}}^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}U_{j}G_{j}\left(z-H(G_{j})\right)^{-1}U_{j}^{*},\quad\mathbb{L}_{z,\text{\boldmath$U$}}:=s_{\text{\boldmath$\alpha$}}^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}U_{j}\left(z-H(G_{j})\right)^{-1}U_{j}^{*},

and
g_{\text{\boldmath$U$}}:=G-\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}U_{j}G_{j}U_{j}^{*}
, equation (
12
) becomes

\displaystyle\left(z-H\left(G\right)\right)^{-1}=\mathbb{L}_{z,\text{\boldmath$U$}}\left(1+\mathbb{H}_{z,\text{\boldmath$U$}}-(s_{\text{\boldmath$\alpha$}}-1)\mathbb{A}_{z,\text{\boldmath$U$}}-g_{\text{\boldmath$U$}}\mathbb{L}_{z,\text{\boldmath$U$}}\right)^{-1}.

(16)

From this, the results obtained in (
15
) can easily be extended to this setting as well, and

\displaystyle\left|\!\left|\mathcal{F}(G)-\mathbb{D}_{\ell}^{\text{\boldmath$U$}}\right|\!\right|_{e}\leqslant C_{\ell}c_{\text{\boldmath$\alpha$}}^{m_{\ell}}\left(\delta_{\text{\boldmath$\alpha$},\text{\boldmath$U$}\text{\boldmath$G$}\text{\boldmath$U$}^{*}}^{\ell+1+\xi_{\ell}}+\delta_{\text{\boldmath$\alpha$}}^{\ell+1}+\delta_{g_{\text{\boldmath$U$}}}^{\ell+1}\right),

where
{\text{\boldmath$U$}\text{\boldmath$G$}\text{\boldmath$U$}^{*}:=\left(U_{j}G_{j}U_{j}^{*}\right)_{j=1}^{n}}
and where
\mathbb{D}_{\ell}^{\text{\boldmath$U$}}
is
\mathbb{D}_{\ell}
under the above transformations, which can be recast as described in the upcoming (
19
). Minimizing the previous bound over
\text{\boldmath$\alpha$}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\backslash\{0\}
and
\text{\boldmath$U$}\in\mathcal{U}^{n}
provides a better estimate of
\mathcal{F}(G)
.

4.4.
Complexity analysis

In this section, we compare the complexity of the standard perturbation approximation and the multipoint perturbation method depending on the order of the expansion
\ell
and the number of points
n
.
We denote by

•

m
the cost of applying an operator of the kind
\left|\varphi\right>\left<\psi\right|
to a vector, where
\varphi,\psi\in\mathcal{H}
,

•

q
the cost of applying
K_{ab}
or
K_{a}
to a vector in
\mathcal{H}
,

•

p
the cost of applying
g
or
G_{i}
to a vector in
\mathcal{H}
.

Of course, this is intended for the case of a finite-dimensional Hilbert space. Further, the complexity of
q,p
with respect to the dimension of the Hilbert space very much depends on the properties of the induced matrices.
If the discrete setting results from a discretization of a problem posed in an infinite-dimensional Hilbert space, then these properties also depend much on the type of discretization method that is employed. We keep it therefore abstract in this analysis such that it can be assessed for each method individually.

4.4.1.
Using the symmetries of the
\mathcal{I}
’s

To decrease the number of operations, we first exploit the symmetries of
\mathcal{I}_{i,j}
and
\mathcal{I}_{i,j,k}
and write

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{(0,1,0)}=2!s_{\text{\boldmath$\alpha$}}^{-2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant i<j\leqslant n\end{subarray}}\alpha_{i}\alpha_{j}\mathcal{I}_{i,j}(g)+s_{\text{\boldmath$\alpha$}}^{-2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}^{2}\mathcal{I}_{i,i}(g),

reducing to
J_{1}:=\frac{1}{2}n(n+1)
the number of operations in the two sums instead of
n^{2}
. Similarly,

\mathcal{D}_{(0,2,0)}=3!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant i<j<k\leqslant n\end{subarray}}\alpha_{i}\alpha_{j}\alpha_{k}\mathcal{I}_{i,j,k}(g,g)+2!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant i<k\leqslant n\end{subarray}}\alpha_{i}^{2}\alpha_{k}\mathcal{I}_{i,i,k}(g,g)\\
+2!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant i<j\leqslant n\end{subarray}}\alpha_{i}^{2}\alpha_{j}\mathcal{I}_{i,j,i}(g,g)+2!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant i<j\leqslant n\end{subarray}}\alpha_{i}\alpha_{j}^{2}\mathcal{I}_{i,j,j}(g,g)+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}^{3}\mathcal{I}_{i,i,i}(g,g).

Using that

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant i<j<k\leqslant n\end{subarray}}1=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}1=\frac{n}{6}\left(n^{2}-3n+4\right)

brings the number of operations from
n^{3}
to

\displaystyle J_{2}:=\frac{1}{6}n(n^{2}-3n+4)+3\times 2\times\frac{1}{2}n(n-1)+n=\frac{1}{6}n\left(n^{2}-15n-8\right).

Note that the leading order remains the same but the preconstant can be significantly reduced.

4.4.2.
Costs of intermediate quantities

For any operator
L
, we denote by
\mathfrak{C}(L)\in\mathbb{N}^{2}

the cost of applying
L
to a vector, the first component being the offline cost and the second one being the online cost. The offline computations are the ones that can be performed once the
G_{j}
’s are fixed and that do not depend on
G
. The online computations are the remaining ones that can only be performed once a
G
is chosen.
Note that our aim here is to apply
\mathbb{P}_{\ell}
and
\mathbb{D}_{\ell}
to a vector.
We separate the offline and online computations so that we can assess the complexity for both cases when the perturbation is applied to only one
G
as well as if it is applied for many different
G
’s, such as in a many-query context, where the
G_{j}
’s are fixed once and for all and the computations need then to be done for many sample points
G
’s.
Let us start with a typical example. For
a,b,j=1,\ldots,n
, to apply
P_{a}G_{j}K_{ab}
to a vector
\psi\in\mathcal{H}
, we write

\displaystyle P_{a}G_{j}K_{ab}\psi=\left<u_{k}(G_{a}),G_{j}K_{ab}\psi\right>u_{k}(G_{a})=\left<K_{ab}G_{j}u_{k}(G_{a}),\psi\right>u_{k}(G_{a})

so we can pre-compute
\varphi_{a,b,j}:=K_{ab}G_{j}u_{k}(G_{a})
offline which costs
p+q
. Finally, one can compute
\left<\varphi,\psi\right>
online, this costs
m
, and we write
\mathfrak{C}\left(P_{a}G_{j}K_{ab}\right)=(p+q,m)
. Therefore we obtain for the following operators

\displaystyle\mathfrak{C}\left(P_{a}G_{j}K_{ab}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{C}\left(K_{ab}G_{j}P_{a}\right)=(p+q,m)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{C}\left(P_{a}gK_{ab}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{C}\left(K_{ab}gP_{a}\right)=(0,p+q+m)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{C}\left(\mathcal{I}_{a,b}(G_{j})\right)

\displaystyle=2(p+q,m)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{C}\left(\mathcal{I}_{a,b}(g)\right)

\displaystyle=2(0,p+q+m),

using (
17
) for the expression of
\mathcal{I}_{a,b}
.
We now compute the cost of the
\mathcal{I}_{a,b,c}
’s. For instance, for the term

, we have the decomposition

\displaystyle P_{a}gP_{b}G_{j}K_{ac}K_{bc}\psi=u_{k}(G_{a})\left<gu_{k}(G_{a}),u_{k}(G_{b})\right>\left<K_{bc}K_{ac}G_{j}u_{k}(G_{b}),\psi\right>,

so that

\mathfrak{C}\left(P_{a}gP_{b}G_{j}K_{ac}K_{bc}\right)=\left(2q+p,2m+p\right).

Similarly, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathfrak{C}\left(P_{a}gK_{ab}gK_{ac}\right)

\displaystyle=(0,m+2q+2p)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{C}\left(P_{a}gP_{b}gK_{ac}K_{bc}\right)

\displaystyle=(0,2(m+q+p))

\displaystyle\mathfrak{C}\left(\mathcal{I}_{a,b,c}(g,g)\right)

\displaystyle=(0,3(m+2q+2p)+6(m+q+p))=3(0,3m+4q+4p),

then

\displaystyle\mathfrak{C}\left(P_{a}G_{j}K_{ab}gK_{ac}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{C}\left(K_{ba}G_{j}P_{b}gK_{bc}\right)=(p+q,m+p+q)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{C}\left(K_{ca}G_{j}K_{cb}gP_{c}\right)

\displaystyle=(0,m+2p+2q)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{C}\left(P_{a}G_{j}P_{b}gK_{ac}K_{bc}\right)

\displaystyle=(m+p,m+p+2q)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{C}\left(P_{a}G_{j}K_{ab}K_{cb}gP_{c}\right)

\displaystyle=(p+2q,2m+p)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{C}\left(K_{ba}K_{ca}G_{j}P_{b}gP_{c}\right)

\displaystyle=(p+2q,2m+p)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{C}\left(\mathcal{I}_{a,b,c}(G_{j},g)\right)

\displaystyle=(m+5p+6q,8m+7p+6q),

as well as

\displaystyle\mathfrak{C}\left(P_{a}gK_{ab}G_{j}K_{ac}\right)

\displaystyle=(0,m+2p+2q)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{C}\left(K_{ba}gP_{b}G_{j}K_{bc}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{C}\left(K_{ca}gK_{cb}G_{j}P_{c}\right)=(p+q,m+p+q)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{C}\left(P_{a}gP_{b}G_{j}K_{ac}K_{bc}\right)

\displaystyle=(p+2q,2m+p)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{C}\left(P_{a}gK_{ab}K_{cb}G_{j}P_{c}\right)

\displaystyle=(p+2q,2m+p)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{C}\left(K_{ba}K_{ca}gP_{b}G_{j}P_{c}\right)

\displaystyle=(m+p,m+p+2q)

\displaystyle=(m+5p+6q,8m+7p+6q),

and

\displaystyle\mathfrak{C}\left(P_{a}G_{i}K_{ab}G_{j}K_{ac}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{C}\left(K_{ba}G_{i}P_{b}G_{j}K_{bc}\right)=\mathfrak{C}\left(K_{ca}G_{i}K_{cb}G_{j}P_{c}\right)=(2p+2q,m)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{C}\left(P_{a}G_{i}P_{b}G_{j}K_{ac}K_{bc}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{C}\left(P_{a}G_{i}K_{ab}K_{cb}G_{j}P_{c}\right)=\mathfrak{C}\left(K_{ba}K_{ca}G_{i}P_{b}G_{j}P_{c}\right)=(m+2p+2q,m)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{C}\left(\mathcal{I}_{a,b,c}(G_{i},G_{j})\right)

\displaystyle=\left(3m+12p+12q,6m\right).

Now for the multipoint perturbation method, we start by giving the cost estimation for the

, as well as the computations from Section

\displaystyle\mathfrak{C}\left(\mathcal{D}_{(0,0,0)}\right)

\displaystyle=n(0,m)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{C}\left(\mathcal{D}_{(0,1,0)}\right)

\displaystyle=J_{1}\mathfrak{C}\left(\mathcal{I}_{a,b}(g)\right)=n(n+1)(0,p+q+m)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{C}\left(\mathcal{D}_{(0,0,1)}\right)

\displaystyle=n^{2}\mathfrak{C}\left(\mathcal{I}_{a,b}(G_{j})\right)=2n^{2}(p+q,m)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{C}\left(\mathcal{D}_{(2,0,0)}\right)

\displaystyle=n^{3}\mathfrak{C}\left(\mathcal{I}_{a,b,c}(G_{i},G_{j})\right)=n^{3}\left(3m+12p+12q,6m\right)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{C}\left(\mathcal{D}_{(0,2,0)}\right)

\displaystyle=J_{2}\mathfrak{C}\left(\mathcal{I}_{a,b,c}(g,g)\right)=\frac{1}{2}n\left(n^{2}-15n-8\right)(0,3m+4q+4p)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{C}\left(\mathcal{D}_{(0,0,2)}\right)

\displaystyle=n^{3}\mathfrak{C}\left(\mathcal{I}_{a,b,c}(G_{i},G_{j})\right)=3n^{3}(m+4p+4q,2m)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{C}\left(\mathcal{D}_{(0,1,1)}\right)

Therefore, we obtain for the multipoint perturbation method up to second-order

\displaystyle\mathfrak{C}\left(\mathbb{D}_{0}\right)

\displaystyle=n(0,m)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{C}\left(\mathbb{D}_{1}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{C}\left(\mathbb{D}_{0}\right)+\delta_{g\neq 0}\mathfrak{C}\left(\mathcal{D}_{(0,1,0)}\right)+\delta_{s_{\text{\boldmath$\alpha$}}\neq 1}\mathfrak{C}\left(\mathcal{D}_{(0,0,1)}\right)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{C}\left(\mathbb{D}_{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{C}\left(\mathbb{D}_{1}\right)+\mathfrak{C}\left(\mathcal{D}_{(2,0,0)}\right)+\delta_{g\neq 0}\mathfrak{C}\left(\mathcal{D}_{(0,2,0)}\right)+\delta_{s_{\text{\boldmath$\alpha$}}\neq 1}\mathfrak{C}\left(\mathcal{D}_{(0,0,2)}\right)+\delta_{g\neq 0}\delta_{s_{\text{\boldmath$\alpha$}}\neq 1}\mathfrak{C}\left(\mathcal{D}_{(0,1,1)}\right).

To lower again the computational cost of terms, one can use multipoint theory on

Lemma 6.1

(24)

We used the notation

We denote by

. Now

\displaystyle\left(H^{0}+\eta\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(H(G)-z\right)^{-1}\left(H^{0}+\eta\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad=\left(H^{0}+\eta\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(H(G)+\eta\right)^{-1}\left(H(G)+\eta\right)\left(H(G)-z\right)^{-1}\left(H^{0}+\eta\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad=D\left(1+\left(\eta+z\right)\left(H^{0}+\eta\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\left(1+\left(\eta+z\right)\left(H(G)-z\right)^{-1}\right)\left(H^{0}+\eta\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}D\right),

and we deduce that

\displaystyle\left|\!\left|\left(H^{0}+\eta\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(H(G)-z\right)^{-1}\left(H^{0}+\eta\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right|\!\right|\leqslant 2\left(1+2\left|\eta+z\right|\left(1+\frac{\left|\eta+z\right|}{{\rm d}\left(z,\sigma\left(H(G)\right)\right)}\right)\right).

Then

\displaystyle\left(H^{0}+\mu\right)\left(H^{0}+\eta\right)^{-1}=1+\left(\mu-\eta\right)\left(H^{0}+\eta\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle\left|\!\left|\left(H^{0}+\mu\right)\left(H^{0}+\eta\right)^{-1}\right|\!\right|\leqslant 1+\frac{\left|\mu-\eta\right|}{\lambda+\eta}.

\left(H^{0}+\mu\right)\left(H^{0}+\eta\right)^{-1}\geqslant 0

We deduce (

6.3.
Main formula

We will use the notation
R_{j}:=\left(z-H(G_{j})\right)^{-1}
,
G_{ij}:=G_{i}-G_{j}
so we have, by the resolvent formula,

\displaystyle\left(z-H(G)\right)^{-1}-R_{j}=\left(z-H(G)\right)^{-1}(G-G_{j})R_{j},\qquad\qquad R_{j}-R_{i}=R_{j}G_{ji}R_{i}.

(26)

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

For any
{\bm{\alpha}}=(\alpha_{i})_{i=1}^{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},
let
s_{\text{\boldmath$\alpha$}}:=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}
. There holds

\displaystyle s_{\text{\boldmath$\alpha$}}\left(1-\left(z-H(G)\right)\mathbb{L}_{z}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(z-H(G)\right)\left(\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}\right)\left(z-H(G)\right)^{-1}-\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}R_{j}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(z-H(G)\right)\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}\left(\left(z-H(G)\right)^{-1}-R_{j}\right).

Using (
26
), and recalling that
g:=G-\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}G_{j}
, we obtain

\displaystyle s_{\text{\boldmath$\alpha$}}\left(1-\left(z-H(G)\right)\mathbb{L}_{z}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}\left(G-G_{j}\right)R_{j}

\displaystyle=g\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}R_{j}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}\left(\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}G_{i}\right)-G_{j}\right)R_{j}

\displaystyle=g\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}R_{j}+\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}\alpha_{i}\left(G_{i}-G_{j}\right)R_{j}+\left(s_{\text{\boldmath$\alpha$}}-1\right)\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}G_{j}R_{j}

\displaystyle=s_{\text{\boldmath$\alpha$}}g\mathbb{L}_{z}+\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}\alpha_{i}\left(G_{i}-G_{j}\right)R_{j}+\left(s_{\text{\boldmath$\alpha$}}-1\right)s_{\text{\boldmath$\alpha$}}\mathbb{A}_{z}.

Noting that
G_{ji}=-G_{ij}
, we rearrange the second term on the right-hand side into

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant i,j\leqslant n\end{subarray}}\alpha_{i}\alpha_{j}G_{ij}R_{j}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant i,j\leqslant n\end{subarray}}\alpha_{i}\alpha_{j}G_{ij}R_{j}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant i,j\leqslant n\end{subarray}}\alpha_{j}\alpha_{i}G_{ji}R_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant i,j\leqslant n\end{subarray}}\alpha_{i}\alpha_{j}G_{ij}\left(R_{j}-R_{i}\right).

Using again (
26
), we get

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant i,j\leqslant n\end{subarray}}\alpha_{i}\alpha_{j}G_{ij}R_{j}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant i,j\leqslant n\end{subarray}}\alpha_{i}\alpha_{j}G_{ij}R_{j}G_{ij}R_{i}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant i<j\leqslant n\end{subarray}}\alpha_{i}\alpha_{j}G_{ij}R_{j}G_{ij}R_{i}

\displaystyle=-s_{\text{\boldmath$\alpha$}}\mathbb{H}_{z}.

Therefore,

\left(1-\left(z-H(G)\right)\mathbb{L}_{z}\right)=g\mathbb{L}_{z}-\mathbb{H}_{z}+(s_{\text{\boldmath$\alpha$}}-1)\mathbb{A}_{z},

which is equivalent to

\displaystyle\left(z-H(G)\right)^{-1}(1+\mathbb{H}_{z}+(1-s_{\text{\boldmath$\alpha$}})\mathbb{A}_{z}-g\mathbb{L}_{z})=\mathbb{L}_{z}.

∎

\mathcal{I}_{a,b}(A)
when
\lambda^{k}(G_{a})=\lambda^{k}(G_{b})
, we use the previous one and since the result is regular as
\left|\lambda^{k}(G_{a})-\lambda^{k}(G_{b})\right|
is arbitrarily small, we can deduce that the same formula holds in any case. We compute
\mathcal{I}_{a,b,c}(A,B)
with a similar procedure. We nevertheless give detail about the computation of terms of the following kind,

\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{\mathcal{C}}\left(z-\lambda^{k}(G_{a})\right)^{-1}\left(z-\lambda^{k}(G_{b})\right)^{-1}P_{a}AP_{b}BR_{c}^{\perp}(z){\rm d}z\\
=\left(\lambda^{k}(G_{a})-\lambda^{k}(G_{b})\right)^{-1}P_{a}AP_{b}BR_{c}^{\perp}(\lambda^{k}(G_{a}))+\left(\lambda^{k}(G_{b})-\lambda^{k}(G_{a})\right)^{-1}P_{a}AP_{b}BR_{c}^{\perp}(\lambda^{k}(G_{b}))\\
=\left(\lambda^{k}(G_{a})-\lambda^{k}(G_{b})\right)^{-1}P_{a}AP_{b}B\left(K_{ac}-K_{bc}\right).

Finally, by the resolvent formula,
K_{ac}-K_{bc}=\left(\lambda^{k}(G_{b})-\lambda^{k}(G_{a})\right)K_{ac}K_{bc}
.
∎

7.
Conclusion

In this article, we introduced a multipoint perturbation formula for eigenvalue computations.
It allows to use the density matrix for several Hamiltonians
H(G_{j})=H^{0}+G_{j}
simultaneously to obtain the solution for a nearby Hamiltonian
H(G)=H^{0}+G
.
Our formula is based on the resolvent formalism and incorporates a new resolvent identity (
12
) involving several Hamiltonians.
Based on this identity, we then derived approximations of different expansion orders with respect to smallness parameters.
We also derived a detailed complexity analysis allowing to compare the multipoint perturbation method to the standard perturbation method in terms of convergence order and complexity with the purpose to understand in which regimes multipoint perturbation is more efficient than standard perturbation theory.

We verified the asymptotic estimates (
15
) by a series of numerical results for the discretized Schrödinger equation.
We observed, as expected by the theory, that multipoint perturbation is more efficient when the new
G
is close to the affine space

\displaystyle\Aff\left(G_{j}\right)_{j=1}^{n}=\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}G_{j}\;\middle|\;\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}=1\right\},

and when the
G_{j}
’s are sufficiently close to each other. In such a case, the multipoint perturbation method is one order more accurate.

8.2.
K_{ij}
’s from
K_{i}
’s

Here we will consider that one does not know
K_{ij}
but only
{K_{j}:=K(G_{j})}
using definition (
2.5
), i.e. the quantities known in standard perturbation theory. This is not an issue since we can easily obtain approximations of
K_{ij}
by
K_{j}
if
\lambda_{i}
and
\lambda_{j}
are close, using the following lemma that is based on perturbative arguments. We recall that in numerical practice, the intermediate steps to compute
K_{i}
’s enable to compute the
K_{ij}
’s. For
j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, we define
\lambda_{j}:=\lambda^{k}(G_{j})
.

Lemma 8.1

(Deducing
K_{ij}
from
K_{j}
)
.

If

\displaystyle\left|\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}\right|<\left|\!\left|\left(H^{0}+\mu\right)^{-\frac{\kappa}{2}}K_{j}\left(H^{0}+\mu\right)^{\frac{\kappa}{2}}\right|\!\right|^{-1},

then

\displaystyle K_{ij}=K_{j}\left(1+\left(\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}\right)K_{j}\right)^{-1}=K_{j}\sum_{m=0}^{+\infty}\left(\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{i}\right)^{m}K_{j}^{m}

(28)

Proof of Lemma
8.1
.

We first show that

\displaystyle K_{j}-K_{ij}=\left(\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}\right)K_{j}K_{ij}.

(29)

We define
P^{\perp}_{j}:=1-P_{j}
, we have

\displaystyle K_{j}=P_{j}^{\perp}\left(\lambda_{j}-H(G_{j})\right)^{-1}P_{j}^{\perp},\qquad\qquad K_{ij}=P_{j}^{\perp}\left(\lambda_{i}-H(G_{j})\right)^{-1}P_{j}^{\perp},

where we used an abuse of notation because
\left(\lambda_{j}-H(G_{j})\right)^{-1}
should be defined as a pseudo-inverse, but the pseudo-inverse is equal to this quantity on
P^{\perp}_{j}\mathcal{H}
. We have

\displaystyle K_{j}-K_{ij}

\displaystyle=P^{\perp}_{j}\left(\left(\lambda_{j}-H(G_{j})\right)^{-1}-\left(\lambda_{i}-H(G_{j})\right)^{-1}\right)P^{\perp}_{j}

\displaystyle=\left(\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}\right)P^{\perp}_{j}\left(\lambda_{j}-H(G_{j})\right)^{-1}\left(\lambda_{i}-H(G_{j})\right)^{-1}P^{\perp}_{j}

\displaystyle=\left(\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}\right)P^{\perp}_{j}\left(\lambda_{j}-H(G_{j})\right)^{-1}P^{\perp}_{j}P^{\perp}_{j}\left(\lambda_{i}-H(G_{j})\right)^{-1}P^{\perp}_{j}

\displaystyle=\left(\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}\right)K_{j}K_{ij},

where we used that
P^{\perp}_{j}\left(\lambda_{j}-H(G_{j})\right)^{-1}P_{j}=P_{j}\left(\lambda_{j}-H(G_{j})\right)^{-1}P^{\perp}_{j}=0
. From (
29
), we write
K_{ij}\left(1+\left(\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}\right)K_{j}\right)=K_{j}
so
K_{ij}=K_{j}\left(1+\left(\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}\right)K_{j}\right)^{-1}
. With a manipulation similar as in (
25
), we have

\displaystyle\left|\!\left|K_{ij}\right|\!\right|_{e}\leqslant\frac{\left|\!\left|K_{j}\right|\!\right|_{e}}{1-\left|\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}\right|\left|\!\left|\left(H^{0}+\mu\right)^{-\frac{\kappa}{2}}K_{j}\left(H^{0}+\mu\right)^{\frac{\kappa}{2}}\right|\!\right|},

and we can deduce (
28
).
∎
