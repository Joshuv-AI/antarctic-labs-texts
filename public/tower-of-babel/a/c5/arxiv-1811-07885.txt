Strong solutions for the Stochastic Navier-Stokes equations on the 2D rotating sphere with stable Lévy noise
Leanne Dong 1

Abstract
In this paper we prove the existence and uniqueness of a strong solution (in PDE sense) to the stochastic Navier-Stokes equations on the rotating 2-dimensional unit sphere perturbed by stable Lévy noise. This strong solution turns out to exist globally in time.



1
Introduction

The deterministic Navier-Stokes system (NSEs) on the rotating sphere serves as a basic model in large scale ocean dynamics. Many authors have studied the NSEs on the unit spheres. Notably, Il’in and Filatov
[
17
,
15
]
tackled the well-posedness of these equations and identified the Hausdorff dimension of their global attractors
[
16
]
. Teman and Wang investigated the inertial forms of NSEs on the sphere while Teman and Ziane show that the NSEs on a 2D sphere is a limit of NSE defined on a spherical cell
[
27
]
.
Our paper is concerned with the following stochastic Navier-Stokes equations (SNSEs) on a 2D rotating sphere:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u+\nabla_{u}u-\nu\mathbf{L}u+\mathbf{\omega}\times u+\nabla p=f+\eta(x,t),\quad\text{div }u=0,\quad u(0)=u_{0},

(1.1)

where
\mathbf{L}
is the stress tensor,
\mathbf{\omega}
is the Coriolis acceleration,
f
is the external force and
\eta
is the noise process that can be informally described as the derivative of an
H
-valued Lévy process. Rigorous definitions of all relevant quantities in this equation will be given in sections 2 and 3. To the best of our knowledge, there are only three papers which discuss stochastic Navier-Stokes equations on spheres
[
3
,
4
,
29
]
. All these were concerned with the Gaussian case. In particular, the authors in
[
3
]
proved the existence and uniqueness of weak solutions to (
1.1
) with additive Gaussian noise. Moreover, they proved that the associated random dynamical system is asymptotically compact, which induced the existence of a compact random attractor and the existence of an invariant measure in their accompanying paper
[
4
]
. The author in
[
29
]
studied the Navier-Stokes system on spheres with a Gaussian kick force and a deterministic force. The main contribution was the existence and uniqueness of a time-invariant measure.

Our paper is the first paper to discuss SNSEs on the sphere with a stable Lévy noise. There are three new features which distinguish our paper from other work in the literature on SNSEs on spheres and SNSEs with Lévy noise. First, the domain of consideration is a sphere. Next, the noise is of a stable type which is ruled out by many existing studies on stochastic PDEs with Lévy noise. Third, our well-posedness result is new in the sense of a strong solution in PDE sense.

The aim of our paper is to prove the existence and uniqueness of a global strong solution to (
1.1
). In particular, we prove that
given a
\mathbb{L}^{4}
-valued noise,
H
-valued forcing
f
and small
V
-valued initial data, there exists a unique global strong solution in PDE sense for the abstract stochastic Navier-Stokes equations on the 2D unit sphere perturbed by stable Lévy noise, which depends continuously on the initial data.
The time interval of existence depends on the regularity of the forcing and the assumptions imposed on noise.

The paper is organised as follows. In section
2
, we review the fundamental mathematical theory for the deterministic Navier-Stokes equations (NSEs) on the sphere. We state some known results without proofs. In section
3
, we define the SNSEs on spheres. We start with some analytic facts; we introduce the driving noise process, which is a stable Lévy noise via subordination. The SNSEs are then decomposed into an Ornstein Uhlenbeck (OU) process (associated with the linear part of the SNSEs) and nonlinear PDEs. In section
4
, we prove strong classical solution (see the proof of Theorem 3.3.7) for smooth initial data, sufficient regular noise following the classical lines in the proof of Theorem 3.1
[
6
]
.

2
Navier-Stokes equations on a rotating 2D unit sphere

The sphere is the simplest example of a compact Riemannian manifold without boundaries, hence one may employ the well-developed tools from Riemannian geometry to study objects on such a manifold. Nevertheless, all objects of interest in this thesis are defined explicitly under the spherical coordinates. The presentation here follows closely from Goldys et al.
[
3
]
and references therein.

2.1
Preliminaries

Let
\mathbb{S}^{2}
be a 2D unit sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, that is
\mathbb{S}^{2}=\{x=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:|x|=1\}
. An arbitrary point
x
on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
can be parametrized in the spherical coordinates as

\displaystyle x=\hat{x}(\theta,\phi)=(\sin\theta\cos\phi,\sin\theta\sin\phi,\cos\theta),\quad 0\leq\theta\leq\pi,\quad 0\leq\phi\leq 2\pi.

The corresponding angle
\theta
and
\phi
will be denoted by
\theta(x)
and
\phi(x),
or simply by
\theta
and
\phi.

Let
e_{\theta}=e_{\theta}(\theta,\phi)
and
e_{\phi}=e_{\phi}(\theta,\phi)

be the standard unit tangent vectors of
\mathbb{S}^{2}
at point
\hat{x}(\theta,\phi)\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
in the spherical coordinates, that is,

\displaystyle e_{\theta}

\displaystyle=(\cos\theta\cos\phi,\cos\theta\sin\phi,-\sin\theta),\quad e_{\phi}=(-\sin\phi,\cos\phi,0).

We remark that

\displaystyle e_{\theta}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\hat{x}(\theta,\phi)}{\partial\theta},\quad e_{\phi}=\frac{1}{\sin\theta}\frac{\partial\hat{x}(\theta,\phi)}{\partial\phi},

where the second identity holds whenever
\sin\theta\neq 0
.

Our first objective is to give a meaning to all of the terms in the deterministic Navier-Stokes equations for the velocity field
u(\hat{x},t)=(u_{\theta}(\hat{x},t),u_{\phi}(\hat{x},t))
of a geophysical fluid flow on the 2D rotating unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{2}
under the external force
f=(f_{\theta},f_{\phi})=f_{\theta}e_{\theta}+f_{\phi}e_{\phi}
. The motion of the fluid is governed by the equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u+\mathbf{\nabla}_{u}u-\nu\mathbf{L}u+\omega\times u+\frac{1}{\rho}\mathbf{\nabla}p=f,\quad\text{div}\,\,u=0,\,\,u(x,0)=u_{0}.

(2.1)

Here
\nu
and
\rho
are two positive constants denoting the viscosity and the density of the fluid; the normal vector field

\displaystyle\omega=2\Omega\cos(\theta(x))x,

where
x=\hat{x}(\theta(x),\phi(x))
;
\Omega
is the angular velocity of the Earth; and
\theta
is the parameter representing the colatitude. Note that
\theta(x)=\cos^{-1}(x_{3}).
In what follows we will identify
\mathbf{\omega}
with the corresponding scalar function
\omega
defined by
\omega(x)=2\Omega\cos(\theta(x)).
We will introduce now the other terms that appear in the equation. The surface gradient for a scalar function
f
on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
is given by

\displaystyle\nabla f=\frac{\partial f}{\partial\theta}e_{\theta}+\frac{1}{\sin\theta}\frac{\partial f}{\partial\phi}e_{\phi},\quad 0\leq\theta\leq\pi,\,\,0\leq\phi\leq 2\pi.

Unless specified otherwise, by a vector field on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
we mean a tangential vector field. That is, a section of the tangent vector bundle of
\mathbb{S}^{2}.

On the other hand, for a vector field
u=(u_{\theta},u_{\phi})
on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
, that is
u=u_{\theta}e_{\theta}+u_{\phi}e_{\phi}
, one puts

\displaystyle\text{div}\,u=\frac{1}{\sin\theta}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}(u_{\theta}\sin\theta)+\frac{\partial}{\partial\phi}u_{\phi}\right).

(2.2)

Given two vector fields
u
and
v
on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
, there exist vector fields
\tilde{u}
and
\tilde{v}

defined in some neighbourhood of the surface
\mathbb{S}^{2}
and such that their restrictions to
\mathbb{S}^{2}
are equal to
u
and
v
. More precisely, see Definition 3.31 in
[
10
]
,

\left.\tilde{u}\right|_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}=u:\mathbb{S}^{2}\to T\mathbb{S}^{2},\quad\mathrm{and}\quad\left.\tilde{v}\right|_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}=v:\mathbb{S}^{2}\to T\mathbb{S}^{2}\,.

For
x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, we define the orthogonal projection
\pi_{x}:\mathbb{R}^{3}\to T_{x}\mathbb{S}^{2}
of
x
onto
T_{x}\mathbb{S}^{2}
, that is

\displaystyle\pi_{x}:\mathbb{R}^{3}\ni y\,\mapsto\,y-(x\cdot y)x=-x\times(x\times y)\in T_{x}\mathbb{S}^{2}.

(2.3)

Lemma 2.1

(
[
4
]
)
.

Suppose
\tilde{u}
and
\tilde{v}
are
\mathbb{R}^{3}
-valued vector fields on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
, and
u
,
v
are tangent vector fields on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
, defined by
u(x)=\pi_{x}(\tilde{u}(x))
and
v(x)=\pi_{x}(\tilde{v}(x))

,
x\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
. Then the following identity holds:

\displaystyle\pi_{x}(\tilde{u}(x)\times\tilde{v}(x))=u(x)\times((x\cdot v(x))x)+((x\cdot u(x))x\times v(x),\quad x\in\mathbb{S}^{2}.

(2.4)

Proof.

Let us fix
x\in\mathbb{S}^{2}.
Then one may decompose vectors
\tilde{u}
and
\tilde{v}
into tangential and normal components as follows:

\displaystyle\tilde{u}=u+u^{\perp}\quad\text{with}\quad u\in T_{x}\mathbb{S}^{2},\quad u^{\perp}=(u\cdot x)x,

\displaystyle\tilde{v}=v+v^{\perp}\quad\text{with}\quad v\in T_{x}\mathbb{S}^{2},\quad v^{\perp}=(v\cdot x)x.

Since
u\times v
is normal to
T_{x}\mathbb{S}^{2}
,
\pi_{x}(u\times v)=0.
Likewise,
u^{\perp}\times v^{\perp}=0
since the cross-product of two parallel vectors yields the 0 vector. Hence, it follows that

\displaystyle\pi_{x}(\tilde{u}\times\tilde{v})=\pi_{x}(u\times v+u\times v^{\perp}+u^{\perp}\times v)=u\times v^{\perp}+u^{\perp}\times v.

(2.5)

∎

We will denote by
\tilde{\nabla}
the usual gradient in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
and then we have

\displaystyle(\nabla f)(x)=\pi_{x}(\tilde{\nabla}\tilde{f}(x)).

(2.6)

The operator
curl
is defined by the formula

\displaystyle(\text{curl}\,u)(x)=(I-\pi_{x})((\tilde{\nabla}\times\tilde{u})(x))=(x\cdot(\tilde{\nabla}\times\tilde{u})(x))x.

(2.7)

Let
u
be a tangent vector field on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
. Applying formula (
2.5
) to the vector fields
\tilde{u}
and
\tilde{v}=\tilde{\nabla}\times\tilde{u}
, one gets

\displaystyle\pi_{x}(\tilde{u}\times(\tilde{\nabla}\times\tilde{u}))

\displaystyle=\tilde{u}\times(\tilde{\nabla}\times(u^{\perp}+u)

\displaystyle=u\times((\nabla\times u)^{\perp})+u^{\perp}\times(\nabla\times u)

\displaystyle=u\times((x\cdot(\tilde{\nabla}\times\tilde{u}))x)

\displaystyle=(x\cdot(\tilde{\nabla}\times\tilde{u}))(u\times x),\quad x\in\mathbb{S}^{2}.

(2.8)

So, we can now define the curl of the vector field
u
on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
, namely,

\displaystyle\text{curl }u:=\hat{x}\cdot(\tilde{\nabla}\times\tilde{u})|_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}.

(2.9)

Equations (
2.9
) and (
2.4
) together yield

\displaystyle\pi_{x}[\tilde{u}\times(\tilde{\nabla}\times\tilde{u})](x)=[u(x)\times x]\,\text{curl}u(x),\quad x\in\mathbb{S}^{2}.

Therefore, we have the following:

Definition 2.2
.

Let
u
be a tangent vector field on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
, and let the vector field
\psi
be normal to
\mathbb{S}^{2}
. We set

\displaystyle\text{curl}\,u

\displaystyle=(\hat{x}\cdot(\tilde{\mathbf{\nabla}}\times\tilde{u}))|_{\mathbb{S}^{2}},\quad\text{Curl}\,\psi=(\tilde{\mathbf{\nabla}}\times\mathbf{\psi})|_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}.

(2.10)

The first equation above indicates a projection of
\nabla\times\tilde{u}
onto the normal direction, while the 2nd equation means a restriction of
\nabla\times\psi
to the tangent field on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
. The definitions presented above do not depend on the extensions
\tilde{u}
and
\tilde{\psi}.
A vector field
\psi
normal to
\mathbb{S}^{2}
will often be identified with a scalar function on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
when it is convenient to do so. The following expressions describe the relationships among Curl of a scalar function
\psi
, Curl of a normal vector field
\mathbf{w}=w\hat{x}
, and curl of a vector field
v
on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
.

\displaystyle\text{Curl}\,\psi

\displaystyle=-\hat{x}\times\nabla\psi,\quad\text{Curl}\,\mathbf{w}=-\hat{x}\times\nabla w,\quad\text{curl }v=-\text{div}(\hat{x}\times v).

(2.11)

Let

\displaystyle(\mathbf{\nabla}_{v}u)(x)

\displaystyle=\pi_{x}\left(\sum^{3}_{i=1}\tilde{v}_{i}(x)\partial_{i}\tilde{u}(x)\right)=\pi_{x}\left((\tilde{v}(x)\cdot\tilde{\nabla})\tilde{u}(x)\right),\quad x\in\mathbb{S}^{2}.

(2.12)

Invoking (
2.4
) and the formula

\displaystyle(\tilde{u}\cdot\tilde{\nabla})\tilde{u}=\tilde{\nabla}\frac{|\tilde{u}^{2}|}{2}-\tilde{u}\times(\tilde{\nabla}\times\tilde{u}),

we find that the covariant derivative
\mathbf{\nabla}_{u}u
takes the form

\displaystyle\mathbf{\nabla}_{u}u=\nabla\frac{|{u}^{2}|}{2}-\pi_{x}(\tilde{u}\times(\tilde{\nabla}\times\tilde{u})).

In particular, using (
2.4
) we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbf{\nabla}_{u}u=\nabla\frac{|u|^{2}}{2}-\pi_{x}(\tilde{u}\times(\tilde{\nabla}\times\tilde{u})).

The surface diffusion operator acting on vector fields on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
is denoted by
\mathbf{\Delta}
(known as the Laplace de Rham operator) and is defined as

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Delta}v=\nabla\text{div }v-\text{Curl curl }v.

(2.13)

Using (
2.11
), one can derive the following relations connecting the above operators:

\displaystyle\text{div Curl }v

\displaystyle=0,\quad\text{curl Curl v}=-\hat{x}\Delta v,\quad\mathbf{\Delta}\text{Curl }v=\text{Curl}\Delta v.

(2.14)

Next, we recall the definition of the Ricci tensor Ric of the 2D sphere
\mathbb{S}^{2}
. Since

\text{Ric}=\begin{pmatrix}E&F\\
F&C,\end{pmatrix}

where the coefficients
E,F,G
of the first fundamental form are given by

\displaystyle E

\displaystyle=x_{\theta}\cdot x_{\theta}=1;

\displaystyle F

\displaystyle=x_{\theta}\cdot x_{\phi}=x_{\phi}\cdot x_{\theta}=0;

\displaystyle C

\displaystyle=x_{\phi}\cdot x_{\phi}=\sin^{2}\theta,

we find that

\displaystyle\text{Ric}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&\sin^{2}\theta\end{pmatrix}\,.

(2.15)

Finally we define the stress tensor
\mathbf{L}
. It is given by

\displaystyle\mathbf{L}=\mathbf{\Delta}+2\text{Ric},

where
\mathbf{\Delta}
is the Laplace-de Rham operator.

2.2
Function spaces on the sphere

In what follows we denote by
dS
the surface measure on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
. In the spherical coordinates one has locally,
dS=\sin\theta\,d\theta d\phi.
For
p\in[1,\infty)
, we denote by
L^{p}=L^{p}(\mathbb{S}^{2},\mathbb{R})
of
p
-integrable scalar function on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
, endowed with the norm

\displaystyle|v|_{L^{p}}=\left(\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}|v(x)|^{p}dS(x)\right)^{1/p}.

For
p=2
the corresponding inner product is denoted by

\displaystyle(v_{1},v_{2})=(v_{1},v_{2})_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{2})}=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}v_{1}v_{2}\,dS.

On the other hand, we denote by
\mathbb{L}^{p}=\mathbb{L}^{p}(\mathbb{S}^{2})
the space
L^{p}(\mathbb{S}^{2},T\mathbb{S}^{2})
of vector fields
v:\mathbb{S}^{2}\to T\mathbb{S}^{2}
endowed with the norm

\displaystyle|v|_{L^{p}}=\left(\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}|v(x)|^{p}dS(x)\right)^{1/p},

where, for
x\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
,
|v(x)|
denotes the length of
v(x)
in the tangent space
T_{x}\mathbb{S}^{2}.
For
p=2
the corresponding inner product is denoted by

\displaystyle(v_{1},v_{2})=(v_{1},v_{2})_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}v_{1}\cdot v_{2}\,dS.

In this paper, the induced norm on
\mathbb{L}^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{2})
is denoted by
|\cdot|
. For other inner product spaces, say
V
with the inner product
(\cdot,\cdot)_{V}
, the associated norm is denoted by
|\cdot|_{V}.

The following identities hold for appropriate real valued scalar functions and vector fields on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
, see (2.4)-(2.6)
[
15
]
:

\displaystyle(\nabla\psi,v)

\displaystyle=-(\psi,\text{div}\,v),

(2.16)

\displaystyle(\text{Curl }\psi,v)

\displaystyle=(\psi,\text{curl }v),

(2.17)

\displaystyle(\text{Curlcurl }w,z)

\displaystyle=(\text{curl }w,\text{curl }z).

(2.18)

In (
2.17
), the
\mathbb{L}^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{2})
inner product is used on the left hand side while the
L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{2})
is used on the right hand side. Throughout this paper, we identify a normal vector field
\mathbf{w}
with a scalar field
w
and by
\mathbf{w}=\hat{x}w
. We hence put

\displaystyle(\psi,\mathbf{w})

\displaystyle:=(\psi,w)_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{2})},\quad\text{if}\,\,\mathbf{w}=\hat{x}w,\quad\psi,w\in L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{2}).

(2.19)

Let us now introduce the Sobolev spaces
H^{1}(\mathbb{S}^{2})
and
\mathbb{H}^{1}(\mathbb{S}^{2})
of scalar functions and vector fields on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
.
Let
\psi
be a scalar function and let
u
be a vector field on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
, respectively. For
s\geq 0
we define

\displaystyle|\psi|^{2}_{H^{1}(\mathbb{S}^{2})}=|\psi|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{2})}+|\nabla\psi|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{2})},

(2.20)

and

\displaystyle|u|^{2}_{\mathbb{H}^{1}(\mathbb{S}^{2})}

\displaystyle=|u|^{2}+|\nabla\cdot u|^{2}+|\text{Curl}\,u|^{2}\,.

(2.21)

One has the following Poincaré inequality

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}|u|^{2}\leq|\text{div}\,u|^{2}+|\text{Curl}\,u|^{2},\quad u\in\mathbb{H}^{1}(\mathbb{S}^{2}),

(2.22)

where
\lambda_{1}>0
is the first positive eigenvalue of the Laplace-Hodge operator, see below.
By the Hodge decomposition theorem in Riemannian geometry
[
9
]
, the space of
C^{\infty}
smooth vector field on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
can be decomposed into three components:

\displaystyle C^{\infty}(T\mathbb{S}^{2})=\mathcal{G}\oplus\mathcal{V}\oplus\mathcal{H},

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}=\{\nabla\psi\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{2})\},\quad\mathcal{V}=\{\text{Curl}\psi\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{2})\},

and
\mathcal{H}
is the finite-dimensional space of harmonic vector fields. Since the sphere is simply connected, that is, the map
\mathbb{S}^{2}\to\mathbb{S}^{2}
is a diffeomorphism, we have
\mathcal{H}=\{0\}.
The condition of orthogonality to
\mathcal{H}
is dropped out. We introduce the following spaces:

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle:=\{u\in\mathbb{L}^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{2}):\nabla\cdot u=0\},

(2.23)

\displaystyle V

\displaystyle:=H\cap\mathbb{H}^{1}(\mathbb{S}^{2}).

In other words,
H
is the closure of the

\displaystyle\{u\in C^{\infty}(T\mathbb{S}^{2}):\nabla\cdot u=0\}

in the
\mathbb{L}^{2}
norm
|u|=(u,u)^{1/2}
, where
u=(u_{\theta},u_{\phi})
and

\displaystyle(u,v)=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}u_{\theta}v_{\theta}+u_{\phi}v_{\phi}dS(x).

(2.24)

The space
V
is the closure of

\displaystyle\{u\in C^{\infty}(T\mathbb{S}^{2}):\nabla\cdot u=0\}

in the norm of
\mathbb{H}^{1}\left(\mathbb{S}^{2}\right)
.
Since
V
is densely and continuously embedded into
H
, and
H
can be identified with its dual
H^{\prime}
, one has the following Gelfand triple:

\displaystyle V\subset H\cong H^{\prime}\subset V^{\prime}.

(2.25)

2.3
Stokes operator

We will recall first that the Laplace-Beltrami operator on
\mathbb{S}^{2}

\displaystyle\Delta f=\frac{1}{\sin\theta}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\left(\sin\theta\frac{\partial f}{\partial\theta}\right)+\frac{1}{\sin^{2}\theta}\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial\phi^{2}}

(2.26)

can be defined in terms of spherical harmonics
Y_{l,m}
as follows (See also
[
30
]
). For
\theta\in[0,\pi]
,
\phi\in[0,2\pi)
, we define

\displaystyle Y_{l,m}(\theta,\varphi)=\left[\frac{(2l+1)(l-|m|)!}{4\pi(l+|m|)!}\right]^{1/2}P^{m}_{l}(\cos\theta)e^{im\varphi},\quad m=-l,\cdots,l,

(2.27)

with
P^{m}_{l}
being the associated Legendre polynomials. The family
\left\{Y_{l,m}:\,l=0,1,\ldots,\,\,m=-l,\ldots,l\right\}
form an orthonormal basis in
L^{2}\left(\mathbb{S}^{2}\right)

and we then can define the well known Laplace-Beltrami operator on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
(
2.26
)
by putting

\Delta Y_{l,m}=-l(l+1)Y_{l,m}.

Then one can extend by linearity to all functions
f:L^{2}\left(\mathbb{S}^{2}\right)
such that

\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-l}^{l}l^{2}(l+1)^{2}\left(f,Y_{l,m}\right)^{2}_{L^{2}\left(\mathbb{S}^{2}\right)}<\infty\,.

We consider the following linear Stokes problem
[
3
]
. That is, given
f\in V^{\prime}
, find
v\in V
such that

\displaystyle\nu\text{Curlcurl}u-2\nu\text{Ric}(u)+\nabla p=f,\quad\text{div}\,u=0.

(2.28)

By taking the inner product of the first equation above with a test field
v\in V
, and then using (
2.18
), the pressure term drops and we obtain

\displaystyle\nu(\text{curl}\,u,\text{curl}\,v)-2\nu(\text{Ric}\,u,v)=(f,v)\quad\forall\,v\in V.

Without loss of generality, let
\nu=1
, we define a bilinear form
a:V\times V\to\mathbb{R}
by

\displaystyle a(u,v)=(-Lu,v).

(2.29)

By performing some elementary calculations, one can write (
2.29
) as follows:

\displaystyle a(u,v):=(\text{curl}\,u,\text{curl}\,v)-2(\text{Ric}\,u,v),\quad u,\,v\in V.

(2.30)

In view of (
2.21
) and formula (
2.15
) for the Ricci tensor on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
, the bilinear form
a
satisfies

\displaystyle a(u,v)\leq|u|_{\mathbb{H}^{1}}|v|_{\mathbb{H}^{1}}

(2.31)

and so it is continuous on
V
. So, by the Riesz representation theorem, there exists a unique operator
\mathcal{A}:V\to V^{\prime}
where
V^{\prime}
is the dual of
V
, such that
a(u,v)=(\mathcal{A}u,v)
, for
\{u,v\}\in V.
Let us recall that by the results in
[
26
]
, p.1446, we also have

\displaystyle a(u,u)=|\text{Def}\,u|^{2}_{2},\quad u\in V

where
Def
is the deformation tensor (See
[
26
]
for more details). Then by the Poincaré inequality (
2.22
) we find that
a(u,u)\geq\alpha|u|^{2}_{V}
, for a certain
\alpha>0
, which implies that
a
is coercive in
V
. Hence, by the Lax-Milgram theorem, the operator
\mathcal{A}:V\to V^{\prime}
is an isomorphism. Let
A
be a restriction of
\mathcal{A}
to
H
:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}D(A)&:=\{u\in V:\,\mathcal{A}u\in H\},\\
Au&:=\mathcal{A}u,\quad u\in D(A).\end{cases}

(2.32)

It is well known (see for instance
[
25
]
, Theorem 2.2.3 ) that
A
is positive definite, self-adjoint in
H
and
D(A^{1/2})=V
with equivalent norms.
Furthermore, for some positive constants
c_{1},c_{2}
we have

c_{1}|u|_{D(A)}\leq|Au|\leq c_{2}|u|_{D(A)}\,,

\displaystyle\langle Au,u\rangle=((u,u))=|u|_{V}=|\nabla u|^{2}=|Du|^{2},\quad u\in D(A).

(2.33)

The spectrum of A consists of an infinite sequence of eigenvalues
\lambda_{l}
. Using the stream function
\psi_{l}
for which
w_{l}=\text{Curl}\psi_{l,m}
and identities (
2.14
), one can show that each
\lambda_{l}
is in fact the vector of eigenvalues of the Laplace-Beltrami operator
\Delta

∎

Lemma 4.3
.

4.2

v_{0}\in H

Corollary
.

If

The idea stems from the standard approximation method commonly used in PDE theory. In view of the apriori estimate (

\displaystyle\ \left|(u_{n},u_{n})_{V}-(u,u_{n})_{V}+(u,u_{n})_{V}-(u,u)_{V}\right|

\displaystyle\ \left|(u_{n},u_{n})_{V}-(u,u_{n})_{V}\right|+\left|(u,u_{n})_{V}-(u,u)_{V}\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\ |u_{n}-u|_{V}|u_{n}|_{V}+|u|_{V}|u_{n}-u|_{V}.

Now

is Cauchy and is therefore bounded. So

converges to

in

Then using (

) one deduces that

\displaystyle\ |B(u_{n})-B(u)|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ |B(u_{n},u_{n})-B(u_{n},u)+B(u_{n},u)-B(u,u)|\leq C(|u_{n}|^{2}_{V}+|u_{n}|^{2}_{V}|u|+|u|^{2}_{V})\to C|u|^{2}_{V}.

Now analogous to the earlier proof of contraction we have,

\displaystyle\ |B(u_{n}(s))-B(u(s))|^{2}_{Y_{T}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\ \left|\int^{t}_{0}S(t-s)(B(u_{n}(s))-B(u(s)))ds\right|^{2}_{Y_{T}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\ c\int^{T}_{0}|B(u_{n}(s))-B(u(s))|^{2}ds

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\ c|u|^{2}_{T}\sqrt{T}.

Therefore,

is in

Now by the continuity argument again, one has

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\int^{T}_{0}S(t-s)B(u_{n}(s))ds=\int^{T}_{0}S(t-s)B(u(s))ds,

and

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\int^{T}_{0}S(t-s)f_{n}(s)ds=\int^{T}_{0}S(t-s)f(s)ds.

Combining the above with the assumptions that

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}S(t)u_{0,n}

\displaystyle=S(t)u_{0},

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}z_{n}(t)

\displaystyle=z(t),

and there exists a solution to (

). However, the solution constructed as the limits of
