The Influence of Urban Morphology on Water Scarcity
Rafael Prieto-Curiel, Christian Borja-Vega

Abstract




1
Abstract

Cities play a vital role in providing water to vast populations. Due to an increasing population and the urbanization process, urban water demand is expected to double by 2050. However, some cities are expanding in locations with limited water retention capacity, scarce rainfall and unfavourable shape. This study quantifies the impact of urban form on water scarcity across more than 100 cities in Latin America, Asia, and Africa. The analysis integrates indicators of urban morphology, satellite imagery for natural resources, and terrain metrics to model cities’ influence on water accessibility. Water tariffs, proximity to critical infrastructure, and access to piped water are used to assess scarcity in urban areas. Results show that locations that are far from the centre, with smaller buildings and fewer constructed surfaces, are less likely to have water access. Urban sprawl negatively impacts water availability within a city, decreases proximity to critical infrastructure and increases water tariffs. Keeping everything else constant, a location that is twice as far from the city centre has 38% less proximity to critical infrastructure. Also, water tariffs are up to 75% higher and the capacity of cities to provide water drops by half in more sparse cities.

4
Discussion

Cities are simultaneously locations where economies of scale simplify the challenge of distributing resources among large populations, but they are also where disasters magnify due to their extensive impact. Water policies are a critical step for the sustainability of growing urban areas. Policies can neutralize water scarcity by allocating resources more efficiently and promoting recycling
[
70
]
. Management of ground pollution guarantees the sustainability of most sources of fresh water
[
71
]
. Yet, another set of actionable policies is related to how cities grow. Sprawled cities require more energy for transport and require covering a larger surface with pavement
[
72
,
73
]
. Building compact, walkable neighbourhoods and taller buildings can reduce pressure on the periurban ecosystem by accommodating the growing number of people
[
49
,
74
]
.

Urban form is strongly linked with income, proximity to infrastructure and water. Our findings underscore the correlation between remoteness and socio-economic indicators, with remote areas generally experiencing higher levels of poverty and social exclusion. We explore the impact of remoteness on water tariffs, finding that more sprawling cities incur higher costs for water services, reflecting challenges in providing equitable access.

We introduce the concept of “remoteness” to quantify the distance of a location within a city to its centre, accounting for variations in city sizes. Remoteness proves to be a valuable metric, revealing distinct patterns in urban development. Central areas exhibit higher population density, more constructed surface, and increased proximity to critical infrastructure, resulting in elevated average income. Conversely, remote areas tend to be characterized by limited constructed surfaces and lower population density, both contributing to decreased access to water infrastructure. At the local level, remoteness reduces income and proximity to infrastructure, thus reducing the chances that a remote household has water supplies. High poverty levels do not mean poor hygiene practices
[
75
]
. However, a lack of reliable water access hinders essential practices like drinking and cooking and may cause health issues such as dehydration, injury, and diarrhoea
[
3
]
.

At the city level, we introduce the concept of sparseness. We show that it has critical implications. Cities where most of the population is sparse present unique challenges compared to more centrally concentrated cities. Here, we show that sparse cities tend to be poorer, critical infrastructure is more scarce, and also water tariffs tend to be higher. Further, poor households are usually not connected to piped water, and if they are, they tend to consume smaller quantities of water, so the majority of water subsidies are captured by the wealthiest households
[
69
]
. Thus, unaffordable water is a persistent struggle among poor and remote neighbourhoods. Smaller cities tend to exhibit higher sparseness, emphasizing the need for tailored urban planning strategies. Comparative analyses across continents highlight regional variations, with Asian cities generally demonstrating lower remoteness compared to counterparts in Latin America and Africa. The presence of numerous tiny buildings in some cities contributes to higher sparseness, suggesting the need for nuanced approaches to address specific urban configurations. Increasing density in the core parts of the city and designing greenbelts may help maintain specific desirable properties of the urban form whilst conserving green spaces from being built over
[
76
]
.

In some cities, access to water will take a lot of work. A mix of terrain aridity, lack of rain and nearby water sources will require a substantial budget for obtaining water from remote locations
[
22
]
. This situation will be considerably challenging for the poorest and fastest-growing cities. In some cases, the demand for water is currently increasing, and it is expected to double within the next decades. With fast population growth, cities may evolve towards dispersed and disorderly growth
[
77
]
. These cities will be in greatest need of aid.
[
22
]
.

5
Methods

A variety of models, including cellular automata models, water evolution and planning models coupled with geographic information systems, remote sensing, and criteria analytic hierarchical process, have been used to model water scarcity
[
7
]
. Also, a variety of indicators have been developed to capture different characteristics of water scarcity, including those related to population, availability, and water use
[
1
]
. Here, we use the spatial arrangement of buildings within a city to model urban forms. Then, we use the proximity to critical infrastructure, water tariffs, and the population’s access to piped water to link urban forms and water insecurity.

We consider 106 cities from Africa (45 cities), Asia (30 cities) and Latin America (31 cities). Major cities, state capitals and other medium-sized cities were selected (Figure
6
). Data availability played a restriction in terms of the cities that could be analysed. For example, data corresponding to building footprints from China or Saudi Arabia was not available and thus could not be included.

5.1
Identifying buildings and delineating cities

Buildings’ locations were extracted from the recently launched Google Open Buildings dataset,
https://sites.research.google/open-buildings/
, which contains building footprints
[
59
]
. The buildings’ footprints were obtained using a deep learning model with high-resolution satellite imagery (50 cm pixel size)
[
59
]
. This dataset contains information on the location of the building’s footprint centre in geographic coordinates (latitude and longitude), its area in m
2
, and the geometry of the building’s footprint. We keep the attributes of the building’s centre location (latitude and longitude) and the building footprint area using
[
78
]
. We obtained the location and characteristics of the buildings’ footprints of 106 urban agglomerations in Africa, Latin America and Asia. In total, we analyse the footprint of 183.4 million buildings, corresponding to a constructed surface nearly the size of Kuwait.

5.2
Delienation or urban agglomerations

Defining urban agglomerations is challenging and often depends on considerations and parameters
[
16
,
79
,
80
,
81
]
. Here, we construct the delineation of the 106 cities using the same definition across all of them, applying the same criteria. The delineation of an urban agglomeration boundary is based on a spatial approach that combines physical limits and the continuity of the built-up area. We consider the building footprint and the proximity between constructions to determine the boundaries of a city. Our criteria is to join an urban polygon to some buildings depending on whether they are less than 200 m apart. The same criteria have been applied elsewhere to construct the delineation of thousands of cities in Africa
[
82
]
. This step ensures that the same criteria to define the urban boundary are applied to cities across all countries.

We employ a geometric structure known as the alpha-convex hull. It is frequently used in computational geometry to represent the convex hull of a set of points in a plane. Here, the points represent the buildings in each city. The alpha-convex depends on a parameter,
\alpha
. Constructing the alpha-convex hull involves modifying the traditional convex hull by allowing certain concave regions to be included or removed based on the
\alpha
parameter. The process begins with computing the standard convex hull of the given buildings in a city. Then, each edge of the concave shape is examined, introducing additional edges connecting points within a certain distance. These extra edges remove and round concave portions of the original shape. The result is an urban polygon that is comparable across cities.

For each urban area, its buildings are identified by those inside the core delineation. Then, using the identified buildings, we measure the number of constructions in each city, the constructed surface, and the mean building area.

The infrastructure in some cities is formed mainly by small buildings. For example, in cities like Khartoum and Agadez, nearly two of every three buildings have a footprint smaller than 20m
2
. In Kuala Lumpur and Singapore, less than 15% of buildings are so small (Figure
7
).

Distribution of building size across selected cities.

5.3
Identifying the city centre

For each urban agglomeration, we identify the city centre as the location of the main square, main church, mosque or central transport station (Figure
8
). Based on the location of the city’s centre, two variables at the pixel level are measured: distance to the city centre and relative distance to the centre. The idea is that remote locations within the same urban agglomerations are identified. Although the precise location of the centre could be ambiguous (for example, in some cities where the location of the main square does not correspond to the location of the main religious temple or transport station), the location is used to determine the relative proximity and centrality of other places in the city. Thus, although the centre could be defined in terms of more than one location, the impact on remoteness is insignificant.

5.4
Remoteness and sparseness

For location
j
in the city
i
, we consider two metrics. First is
D_{i,j}
corresponds to the (geodesic) distance in km between the city centre
i
and location
j
. This distance, however, is relative to the population of a city. For example, in Bogota (a metropolitan area with nearly 9 million inhabitants), a location 2.5km away from the city centre is still considered a central location. However, in Manizales (a city with 434,000 inhabitants), a location within the same distance is a peripheral location. We construct the remoteness
R_{i,j}
between the city centre of
i
and location
j
given by

R_{i,j}=\frac{1000D_{i,j}}{\sqrt{P_{i}}}.

(3)

The idea is that a city with a population
P_{i}
occupies an area proportional to its population
[
49
]
. In general, distances in a city grow with the square root of its population
[
49
]
. One reason for this is the growth of distances within a circle. Consider a circle with area
A
and consider two points inside that circle. The expected distance between them is

\frac{128}{45\pi}\sqrt{A}.

(4)

Thus, in a circle, distances grow linearly with its radius. Similarly, if the area of a city grows with its population, distances in that city should grow proportional to
\sqrt{P_{i}}
. With this formula, a location in Bogota 2.5km away from its centre has
R_{B,j}=0.9
, whereas a location in Manizales 2.5km away from its centre has
R_{M,j}=3.8
, so the same distance would be considered much more remote.

The definition of remoteness depends on the identification of the centre. However, the impact is not critical. One example is Bogota. In the city, the centre could be identified as Plaza Bolívar, or Museo del Oro (500m north), or Parque Metropolitano (600m west), among other locations. The distance from Parque Soacha (a neighbourhood in the southeast part of the city) to the city centre is 16.0km, 16.4km, or 15.5km, respectively, meaning a variation of less than 3%. Parque Soacha’s remoteness is either 5.7, 5.8 or 5.5, respectively, so it is identified as a remote location, regardless of the definition of the city centre.

The remoteness applies to different locations within a city. We then construct a metric that applies at the city level that corresponds to how remote it is, on average, its population. For city
i
we measure the sparseness
S_{i}
as

S_{i}=\frac{\sum_{j}P_{i,j}R_{i,j}}{\sum_{j}P_{i,j}},

(5)

representing the average remoteness of city
i
weighted by the population of each location.

5.5
Surveying cities

We survey distinct locations within a city to detect whether remote locations alter water availability. We begin by considering the Relative Wealth Index data, a public access dataset that provides micro estimates of wealth for all low and middle-income countries
[
26
]
. Data is provided at 2.4km resolution and was constructed by merging different datasets, including DHS survey
[
83
]
, mobile phone and others. Scores cannot be compared across countries. Although the data has some limitations and it may not accurately reflect the ground truth, it provides a systemic way of comparing different regions across cities
[
84
]

We use the grid of the Relative Wealth Index data for geographically surveying cities. Thus, we take the coordinates that are inside each urban polygon and use those coordinates as “pixels” to analyse heterogeneities across cities. Depending on the size of the city, more or less pixels are considered. For instance, more than 1,000 pixels are analysed in New Delhi, more than 500 pixels are analysed in São Paulo, and more than 200 pixels are analysed in Cape Town. In total, nearly 20,000 pixels are analysed (Table 4).

5.6
Cities and water

We consider three ways to analyse water accessibility: water tariffs, proximity to critical infrastructure and access to piped water. For water tariffs, we use the IBNet Tariffs database
[
64
]
. It is a large dataset with water and wastewater tariffs in a comparable U$ format per city.

For proximity to critical infrastructure, we use the Critical Infrastructure Spatial Index, a harmonised index of critical infrastructure
[
63
]
. It uses data from OpenStreetMap to construct a harmonised index of critical infrastructure.

For each city, we estimate the total urban population with access to piped water. The estimate is obtained from national data from the Joint Monitoring Programme for Water Supply, Sanitation and Hygiene (JMP)
[
65
]
.

At the local level (considering the 19,734 pixels as observations) and at the local level (considering the 106 cities), a regression of transformed variables was computed to analyse a functional correlation between variables. Although we use GDP, critical infrastructure, water tariffs or urban population with access to piped water as the dependent variable, we cannot assume a causal relation with the independent variables (such as population size, remoteness or sparseness). The links between variables are complex, and likely, there is feedback between them (for example, between population and GDP per person). The coefficients estimated for each model are in the Supplementary materials.
