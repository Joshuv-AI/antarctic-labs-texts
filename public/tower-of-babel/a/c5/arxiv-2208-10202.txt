Asymptotics of the single-source stochastic sandpile model
Thomas Selig, Haoyue Zhu

Abstract
In the single-source sandpile model, a number N N grains of sand are positioned at a central vertex on the 2-dimensional grid ℤ 2 \mathbb{Z}^{2} . We study the stabilisation of this configuration for a stochastic sandpile model based on a parameter M ∈ ℕ M\in\mathbb{N} . In this model, if a vertex has at least 4 ​ M 4M grains of sand, it topples, sending k k grains of sand to each of its four neighbours, where k k is drawn according to some random distribution γ \gamma with support { 1 , ⋯ , M } \{1,\cdots,M\} . Topplings continue, a new random number k k being drawn each time, until we reach a stable configuration where all the vertices have less than 4 ​ M 4M grains. This model is a slight variant on the one introduced by Kim and Wang. We analyse the stabilisation process described above as N N tends to infinity (with M M fixed), for various probability distributions γ \gamma . We focus on two global parameters of the system, referred to as radius and avalanche numbers. The radius number is the greatest distance from the origin to which grains are sent during the stabilisation, while the avalanche number is the total number of topplings made. Our simulations suggest that both of these numbers have fairly simple asymptotic behaviours as functions of γ \gamma , N N and M M as N N tends to infinity, and we give heuristic arguments explaining how these asymptotics appear. We also provide a more detailed analysis in the case where γ \gamma is the binomial distribution with parameter p p , in particular when p p tends to 1 1 . We exhibit a phase transition in that limit at the scale p ∼ 1 / N p\sim 1/N . 1 Introduction 1.1 The Abelian sandpile model The Abelian sandpile model (ASM) was originally introduced by Bak, Tang and Wiesenfeld as an example of a system exhibiting the phenomenon known as self-organised criticality [ 4 ] . This means that a system tunes itself to a critical state without any need for external modification of parameters [ 11 ] . In the original ASM, grains of sand are positioned at vertices (sites) of a finite square grid. At each step, a grain of sand is added to a randomly chosen vertex on the grid. If the number of grains at a given vertex is greater than or equal to 4 4 , we say that the vertex has become unstable . Unstable vertices topple , sending one grain to each adjacent vertex on the grid. This can cause other vertices to become unstable, and these topple in turn. When a vertex at the edge of the grid topples, grains fall off the grid, exiting the system, so that this process eventually stabilises [ 22 ] . Figure gives an example of the evolution of the ASM. The ASM has been widely studied in Statistical Physics, Mathematics and Computer Science (under the name of “chip-firing game” in the latter case). It also has a number of real-world applications such as forest-fires [ 17 ] , earthquakes [ 3 ] and sediment deposits [ 18 ] . We refer the interested reader to the excellent recent survey on the topic by Klivans [ 13 ] . Among the various topics studied in sandpile research is the so-called single-source sandpile . This takes place on the grid ℤ d \mathbb{Z}^{d} . Initially, a large number N N of sand grains are placed at a single vertex (also called the origin). If N ≥ 2 ​ d N\geq 2d , the origin is unstable, and topples, yielding the toppling avalanches described above, until the process eventually stabilises. The goal is then to study this stabilisation process, usually as N N tends to infinity, and in particular obtain properties of the final stable configuration reached. In various simulations of this configuration, kaleidoscopic patterns will appear [ 5 ] , and these patterns are stated with rigourous explanations in terms of scaling limits and solutions to certain differential equations [ 16 ] . The single-source ASM has been quite extensively studied. Fey, Levine and Peres described its growth rate in [ 10 ] . In the same work, the authors gave a conjecture of dimensional reduction, which was proved by Bou-Rabee on the hypercube [ 6 ] . In [ 2 ] , a technical tool was introduced to deal with the proof of discrete quasiconcavity for the odometer function in the 2-dimensional case ( d = 2 d=2 ). In [ 21 ] , Sadhu and Dhar exhibited the formation of patterns for a slight variant of the single-source ASM, in which N N grains are dropped at a single vertex into an initial configuration satisfying some periodicity conditions. 1.2 Stochastic variants of the ASM In the ASM, the only level of randomness lies in the locations at which grains of sand are added to the system, after which the stabilisation process is deterministic. In particular, the single-source ASM is entirely deterministic. This may make it hard to match the complexity and apparent randomness of real-world situations. Therefore, to increase the randomness of the model, stochastic variations of the ASM have been introduced, in which the topplings are also made random. There are a number of such stochastic sandpile models in the literature. • In [ 14 ] , there are two different types of grain, which cannot occupy the same vertex. When they do, one of the grains is moved to a randomly chosen neighbouring vertex instead. • In [ 19 ] , unstable vertices lose all their grains, which are re-distributed at random to their neighbours: some neighbours may receive more than one grain, others none, while some grains may exit the system directly. • In [ 7 ] , unstable vertices flip a coin for each neighbour to decide which neighbours to send grains to. That is, all neighbours independently of each other receive a grain with probability p ∈ ( 0 , 1 ) p\in(0,1) (with probability ( 1 − p ) (1-p) that grain is kept by the toppling vertex). • The model in [ 15 ] essentially generalises the two previous models in [ 7 , 19 ] . • In [ 12 ] , Kim and Wang set the toppling threshold of a vertex to a (fixed) multiple M M of its degree. For each toppling, a random number k ∈ { 1 , ⋯ , M } k\in\{1,\cdots,M\} is chosen according to some probability distribution γ \gamma , and each neighbour of the toppling vertex receives the same (random) number k k of grains. This particular SSM has been linked to the LLL algorithm for lattice-basis reduction [ 9 ] . In general, the research for these stochastic variants is quite sparse compared to that of the ASM. This paper aims to remedy some of this by analysing the 2-dimensional single-source sandpile for the Kim-Wang model described above [ 12 ] . To our knowledge, this is the first study of a stochastic single-source sandpile model. Our paper is organised as follows. In the rest of this section we give a formal description of the Kim-Wang stochastic sandpile model and of the radius and avalanche numbers studied in this paper (Section ), and introduce the random toppling distributions γ \gamma considered (Section ). Recall that each time a vertex becomes unstable, we re-sample γ \gamma , so that the number of grains toppled could be different each time. Because the only condition on γ \gamma is that its support be included in { 1 , ⋯ , M } \{1,\cdots,M\} , the selection of γ \gamma could be flexible, with a number of possible distributions. In this paper, we study the single-source SSM for six different γ \gamma distributions, given as follows. • Deterministic distribution: γ = M \gamma=M a.s., so that each time a vertex topples, it sends M M grains to each of its neighbours. This is equivalent to the ASM, up to rescaling (see Section ). • Uniform distribution: γ \gamma is a uniform random variable with range from 1 1 to M M , i.e. P ⁡ ( γ = k ) = 1 M P(\gamma=k)=\frac{1}{M} for all k ∈ { 1 , ⋯ , M } k\in\{1,\cdots,M\} . • Binomial distribution: γ \gamma is a binomial random variable with parameters M , p M,p , for some p ∈ ( 0 , 1 ] p\in(0,1] , conditioned to be non-zero (so that γ ≥ 1 \gamma\geq 1 a.s.). This distribution is introduced in order to study the limit of the Binomial distribution as p p tends to 0. (see Section for more details). 2 General behaviour Recall briefly the goal of our study. We start with N N grains at the origin of ℤ 2 \mathbb{Z}^{2} , and study the stabilisation process as N N tends to infinity. The stabilisation here is the random process of the KW model: the toppling threshold at each vertex is 4 ​ M 4M for some positive integer M M , and topplings send a random number k k of grains to each neighbour according to some probability distribution γ \gamma on { 1 , ⋯ , M } \{1,\cdots,M\} . Our research focuses on two global parameters of the system: • the radius number R ⁡ ( γ , N , M ) R(\gamma;N,M) , describing how far away from the origin this process reaches; • the avalanche number A ​ v ​ ( γ , N , M ) Av(\gamma;N,M) , describing the total number of topplings that occur in this process. These parameters are random variables, whose distribution depends on the distribution γ \gamma , as well as the parameters N N and M M . We use the term “global” here in contrast to “local” parameters such as the number of times each individual vertex topples (called the odometer function in sandpile research, see e.g. [ 16 ] ). Such local phenomena may also be of interest in future study (see Section for more details on this). 2.1 The radius and avalanche constants For the model developed in this paper, we observe the following general asymptotic behaviour of the radius number, as N N tends to infinity with M M fixed: R ⁡ ( γ , N , M ) ∼ c R ​ ( γ ) ⋅ N M , R(\gamma;N,M)\sim c_{R}(\gamma)\cdot\sqrt{\frac{N}{M}}, (4) where c R ​ ( γ ) c_{R}(\gamma) is a constant whose value only depends on γ \gamma and neither on N N nor on M M . In this model, vertices topple when they have at least 4 ​ M 4M grains of sand, for some fixed parameter M M . When toppling, a vertex chooses a random number k ∈ { 1 , ⋯ , M } k\in\{1,\cdots,M\} according to some probability distribution γ \gamma , and sends k k grains to each of its four neighbours (in ℤ 2 \mathbb{Z}^{2} ). The single-source sandpile model consists in initially placing N N grains of sand at the origin of ℤ 2 \mathbb{Z}^{2} , and making these (random) topplings until no more vertices can topple. We then study the asymptotics of this stabilisation process as N N tends to infinity. In this paper, we have focused on two global parameters of the system. The first is the radius number R ⁡ ( γ , N , M ) R(\gamma;N,M) which measures the maximum distance from the origin to which grains are sent in the stabilisation. The second is the avalanche number A ​ v ​ ( γ , N , M ) Av(\gamma;N,M) which counts the total number of topplings during the stabilisation. We study these two numbers for six different distributions γ \gamma : Deterministic, Always-1, Uniform, Binomial, Log-Law, and Power-Law. Our simulations show the following asymptotics as N N tends to infinity: R ⁡ ( γ , N , M ) ∼ c R ​ ( γ ) ⋅ N M , R(\gamma;N,M)\sim c_{R}(\gamma)\cdot\sqrt{\frac{N}{M}}, (24) A ​ v ​ ( γ , N , M ) ∼ c A ​ v ​ ( γ ) ⋅ N 2 M ⋅ E ⁡ ( γ ) , Av(\gamma;N,M)\sim c_{Av}(\gamma)\cdot\dfrac{N^{2}}{M\cdot E(\gamma)}, (25) where c R ​ ( γ ) c_{R}(\gamma) and c A ​ v ​ ( γ ) c_{Av}(\gamma) are constants depending only on the toppling distribution γ \gamma and neither on N N nor on M M . We call these the radius and avalanche constants of the system, and give numerical estimates of their values for the six γ \gamma distributions. We also give heuristic arguments for the behaviours observed in Equations ( ) and ( ). Finally, we analyse in more detail the behaviour of these two constants in the case where γ \gamma is a Binomial distribution with parameters M , p M,p , viewing them as functions of p ∈ ( 0 , 1 ] p\in(0,1] . We see that the two constants are increasing in p p , and that as p p tends to 0 0 they converge to the constants of the Always-1 distribution, as expected. More precisely, we identify three regimes. • If 1 − p ≪ 1 N 1-p\ll\frac{1}{N} , the model behaves like the Deterministic distribution. • If 1 − p ≫ 1 N 1-p\gg\frac{1}{N} , the model behaves like a “standard binomial” distribution. • If 1 − p ∼ a N 1-p\sim\frac{a}{N} for a > 0 a>0 , the model behaves differently. Since this is, to our knowledge, the first study of a single-source SSM, there remains much work to be done. Firstly, we should seek to make our heuristic arguments from Sections and more rigourous. In particular, it is not completely clear to us why the correct scale for the phase transition when p p tends to 1 1 depends on the average number of non-full topplings at a given vertex. We could also analyse the radius and avalanche constants in the Power-Law case as functions of the parameter s > 0 s>0 . This would require us to improve our code (simulations for this distribution were very slow). In particular, we would expect the model to behave like the Uniform model when s s tends to 0 0 , and the Always-1 model when s s tends to infinity (as s s tends to infinity, we have P ⁡ ( Power − Law = k ) ≪ P ⁡ ( Power − Law = 1 ) P(\mathrm{Power-Law}=k)\ll P(\mathrm{Power-Law}=1) for k ≥ 2 k\geq 2 , the same behaviour as the Binomial distribution when p p tends to 0 0 ). It would be interesting to see if experimental data confirms this, and whether there are any phase transitions in these limits. Another possible avenue of future research would be to focus on more local parameters, rather than the (global) radius and avalanche numbers. For example, we could look at the odometer function, as has been done in the single-source ASM [ 16 ] . We could also try to describe the limit “shapes” of the stable configuration reached after all topplings are complete. Figure gives some examples of these, and it would be interesting to analyse them further. At first glance, the shapes in Figure show some interesting properties. Recall that we use a sliding RGB colour scale, with blue ≡ 1 \equiv 1 grain, green ≡ 2 ​ M \equiv 2M , and red ≡ 4 ​ M − 1 \equiv 4M-1 . We notice that they are largely made up of a mix of red and green. This indicates that the number of grains at any vertex in the toppling ball would be at least 2 ​ M 2M . We also see that the proportion of “red” vertices, i.e. vertices with closer to 4 ​ M − 1 4M-1 grains (vertices that are “nearly full”), is decreasing in E ⁡ ( γ ) E(\gamma) . For example, the Always-1 distribution has almost all full vertices, whereas the Binomial ⁡ ( p = 0.75 ) \mathrm{Binomial}(p=0.75) distribution, which has the highest expected value among the six examples of the figure, has the largest proportion of green (half-full) vertices. This matches with findings from Equation ( ) that the radius increases with E ⁡ ( γ ) E(\gamma) . Indeed, the balls all have N N grains in total, since the total number of grains in the system remains constant through the stabilisation process. This means that if the ball is larger (has higher radius), we should expect individual vertices to have fewer grains. It would be interesting to formalise these observations, and also to study the shapes more generally. Finally, we could attempt to extend our work to study the single-source SSM with one or more sinks. A sink is introduced to allow sand grains to exit the system, and such a model was studied for the single-source ASM in [ 20 ] . In particular, the authors showed that sinks will change the radius scale when initially placing N N sand grains at the origin: if there is a sink, the radius behaves like N / log ⁡ N \sqrt{N/\log{N}} (for large N N ); while, as we have seen, if there is no sink, the radius will behave like N \sqrt{N} . It would be interesting if a similar change of scale occurs for the SSM.
