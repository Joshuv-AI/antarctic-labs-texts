Determining the potential and the gradient coupling of two-state quantum systems in an infinite waveguide


Abstract




Mohamed Hamrouni, Imèn Rassas, Éric Soccorsi

Abstract.

We consider the inverse coefficient problem of simultaneously determining the space dependent electric potential, the zero-th order coupling term and the first order coupling vector of a two-state Schrödinger equation in an infinite cylindrical domain of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
n\geq 2
, from finitely many partial boundary measurements of the solution. We prove that these
n+1
unknown scalar coefficients can be Hölder stably retrieved by
(n+1)
-times suitably changing the initial condition attached at the system.

Keywords:
Inverse problem, stability estimate, two-state Schrödinger equation.

Mathematics subject classification 2010:
35R30.

1.
Introduction

This is the second of two papers dealing with the stability issue in the inverse problem of determining the electric potential and the coupling coefficients of a two-state quantum system, from local Neumann data. Such systems are commonly used to describe the dynamics of elementary particules like electrons, carrying a two-state (or two-level) quantum mechanical label called spin, and they can exist in any superposition of two independent states. Their dynamics are governed by Schrödinger equations bound together through a linear gradient coupling. When the two quantum states are constrained to a bounded spatial domain, it was proved in
[
17
]
that the electric potential and the coupling are stably determined by finitely many partial boundary observations of the system. In the present work we aim for the same type of identification result when the quantum motion is no longer bounded but may escape to infinity in one direction over the course of time.

1.1.
Settings

Throughout this article,

\omega
is a bounded domain of
\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
,
n\geq 2
, with smooth boundary
\gamma:=\partial\omega
, and
\Omega:=\omega\times\mathbb{R}
.
For
T\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
, we consider the following initial-boundary value problem (IBVP) with initial states
u_{0}^{\pm}
and non-homogenous Dirichlet boundary conditions
g^{\pm}
, for the coupled Schrödinger equations in the unknowns
u^{\pm}
,

(1.1)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-i\partial_{t}u^{+}-\Delta u^{+}+q^{+}u^{+}+A\cdot\nabla u^{-}+pu^{-}=0&\textrm{in}\ Q:=\Omega\times(0,T)\\
-i\partial_{t}u^{-}-\Delta u^{-}+q^{-}u^{-}-A\cdot\nabla u^{+}+pu^{+}=0&\textrm{in}\ Q\\
u^{+}(\cdot,0)=u^{+}_{0},\ u^{-}(\cdot,0)=u^{-}_{0}&\textrm{in}\ \Omega\\
u^{+}=g^{+},\ u^{-}=g^{-}&\textrm{on}\ \Sigma:=\Gamma\times(0,T),\end{array}\right.

where
\Gamma:=\gamma\times\mathbb{R}
. Since
\Gamma
is unbounded, let us make the above boundary condition more precise. For all
x\in\Omega
, we write
x=(x^{\prime},x_{n})
where
x^{\prime}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n-1})\in\omega
and
x_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
, and using a standard density argument we extend the mapping

\begin{array}[]{ccl}C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}\times(0,T),H^{2}(\omega))&\to&L^{2}(\mathbb{R}\times(0,T),H^{\frac{3}{2}}(\omega))\\
w&\mapsto&\left[(x_{n},t)\in\mathbb{R}\times(0,T)\mapsto w(\cdot,x_{n},t)_{|\gamma}\right],\end{array}

to a bounded operator
\gamma_{0}
acting from
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}\times(0,T),H^{2}(\omega))
into

L^{2}(\mathbb{R}\times(0,T),H^{\frac{3}{2}}(\gamma))
. Then, for all
u^{\pm}\in L^{2}(0,T,H^{2}(\Omega))
, the boundary condition in (
1.1
) reads
\gamma_{0}u^{\pm}=g^{\pm}
.

In the present paper we aim to stably retrieve the electric potentials
q^{\pm}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
, the zero-th order coupling term
p:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
and the first order coupling vector
A:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
, by finitely many partial boundary measurements over
the entire time-span
(0,T)
of the solution
u^{\pm}
to (
1.1
).
In contrast with
[
17
]
where the spatial domain
\Omega
is bounded, here we consider an infinitely extended cylindrical domain and we address the problem of simultaneous identification of non-compactly supported unknown coefficients
p
,
q^{\pm}
and
A
. This requires a slightly different and technically more demanding approach than the one implemented in
[
17
]
.

1.2.
Motivations

The dynamics of the two states
u^{\pm}
governed by (
1.1
) are bound together through linear gradient coupling
pu^{\mp}\pm A\cdot\nabla u^{\mp}
. We refer the reader to
[
22
]
and the references therein for the relevance of these processes in physics. Gradient coupling
appears also naturally in quantum fields theory (see
[
2
,
23
]
) or quantum cosmology (see
[
9
,
13
]
), and it is sometimes a first-order approximation of nonlinear coupling (see
[
26
]
).

Quantum wires such as carbon nanotubes are extremely narrow structures which have a length-to-diameter ratio up to
10^{8}
. They are commonly modeled by infinite three-dimensional cylindrical domains such as
\Omega
, in which electrons are essentially free to move in one direction. Quantum wires exhibit valuable physical properties for electronics, optics and other fields of materials science and technology, see e.g.,
[
1
]
, and for this reason they have attracted a lot of attention from the scientific community.

The IBVP (
1.1
) can be interpreted as the time-evolution of
the spin of a spin-
\frac{1}{2}
particle such as an electron (whose spin can have values
\pm\frac{\hbar}{2}
, where
\hbar
is the reduced Planck constant) confined in a carbon nanotube. Notice that for the sake of notational simplicity, the various physical constants such as
\hbar
, the charge and the mass of the electron, are all taken equal to one in (
1.1
).

1.3.
Bibliography

The mathematical literature devoted to inverse coefficient problems for the dynamic Schrödinger equation is so extensive that this presentation is not intended to be exhaustive, but we can mention
[
4
,
5
,
7
,
8
,
20
]
where zero-th or/and first order unknown coefficients of the Schrödinger equation are determined by the Dirichlet-to-Neumann map. These articles assume knowledge of infinitely many boundary data, but in
[
3
,
25
]
the real-valued electric potential is stably retrieved by one partial lateral observation of the solution. This result was
extended to complex-valued electric potentials in
[
15
]
. The boundary measurement in
[
3
,
15
,
25
]
is taken on a subpart of the boundary fulfilling a geometric condition related to geometric optics condition insuring observability. This condition was relaxed to arbitrarily small sub-boundaries in
[
5
]
, provided the potential is known in the vicinity of the boundary.
The inverse problem of determining the magnetic vector potential of the autonomous Schrödinger equation is addressed in
[
15
]
. The same problem for the space-varying part of the magnetic potential appearing in a non-autonomous Schrödinger equation is treated in
[
12
]
. In both cases, the
n
-th dimensional unknown magnetic vector potential,
n\geq 1
, is retrieved from
n
partial Neumann data obtained by
n
-times suitably selecting the initial condition attached at the magnetic Schrödinger equation.

The strategy of
[
3
,
12
,
15
,
25
]
relies on a Carleman inequality specifically designed for the Schrödinger equation, see
[
15
,
24
,
25
]
for actual examples of such weighted energy estimates.
The idea of using a Carleman estimate for solving inverse problems goes back to 1981 and was introduced by A. L. Bukhgeim and M. V. Klibanov in their seminal article
[
11
]
. Since then, the Bukhgeim-Klibanov approach has been successfully applied to parabolic, hyperbolic and Schrödinger systems and even to coupled systems of partial differential equations. We refer the reader to
[
16
]
and references therein, for a complete survey of multidimensional inverse problems solved by the Bukhgeim-Klibanov method.

In all the aforementioned papers, the Schrödinger equation under study is posed in a bounded spatial domain. The inverse problem of determining the electric potential of the Schrödinger equation stated in an infinite waveguide is examined in
[
6
,
19
]
. This is achieved by mean of a specifically designed Carleman estimate for the Schrödinger equation in an unbounded cylindrical domain, which is established in
[
18
]
.
All the articles listed above are concerned with the "one state" Schrödinger equation. In
[
21
]
, assuming that the gradient coupling vector is known, the authors show that the zero-th order coupling term of a two state magnetic Schrödinger equation is uniquely determined by one partial Neumann data. Recently in
[
27
]
, the electric potential of a strongly coupled Schrödinger equations in a bounded spatial domain was Lipschitz stably retrieved by one partial (internal or boundary)
measurement of the solution to the system. In
[
17
]
, the zero-th and first order coefficients of the coupling are Hölder stably recovered by finitely many partial boundary observations of the solution. The coupled Schrödinger equations under study in
in
[
17
,
21
,
27
]
are posed on a bounded spatial domain. In the present paper, we aim to extend the result of
[
17
]
to the case of an unbounded waveguide.

1.5.
Main results

Prior to investigating the inverse problem under study in this article, we examine the well-posedness issue for the forward problem associated with (
1.1
). For this purpose we introduce the Hamiltonian operator acting on
(C_{0}^{\infty}(Q)^{\prime})^{2}
,

\mathcal{H}(A,p,q^{\pm}):=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}-\Delta+q^{+}&A\cdot\nabla+p\\
-A\cdot\nabla+p&-\Delta+q^{-}\end{array}\right)

and state the following existence, uniqueness and regularity result for the solution to the IBVP
(
1.1
).

Proposition 1.1
.

Let
m\in\mathbb{N}
and assume that
\gamma
is
\mathcal{C}^{2(m+1)}
. Let
A\in W^{2m+1,\infty}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap C^{2(m-1)}(\overline{\Omega},\mathbb{R}^{n})
be such that
\nabla\cdot A=0
a.e. in
\Omega
, let

p\in W^{2m+1,\infty}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})\cap C^{2(m-1)}(\overline{\Omega},\mathbb{R})
and let
q^{\pm}\in W^{2m+1,\infty}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})\cap C^{2(m-1)}(\overline{\Omega},\mathbb{R})
satisfy

\left\|A\right\|_{W^{2m+1,\infty}(\Omega)}+\left\|p\right\|_{W^{2m+1,\infty}(\Omega)}+\left\|q^{+}\right\|_{W^{2m+1,\infty}(\Omega)}+\left\|q^{-}\right\|_{W^{2m+1,\infty}(\Omega)}\leq M,

for some a priori fixed positive constant
M
.
Then, for all

g=(g^{+},g^{-})^{T}\in H^{2(m+7/4),m+7/4}(\Sigma)^{2}
and all
u_{0}=(u_{0}^{+},u_{0}^{-})^{T}\in H^{2m+3}(\Omega)^{2}
fulfilling the following compatibility conditions

(1.2)

\partial_{t}^{\ell}g(\cdot,0)=(-i)^{\ell}\mathcal{H}(A,p,q^{\pm})^{\ell}u_{0}\ \mbox{on}\ \Gamma,\ \ell=0,\cdots,m,

the IBVP (
1.1
) admits a unique solution
u=(u^{+},u^{-})^{T}\in\cap_{\ell=0}^{m+1}H^{m+1-\ell}(0,T;H^{2\ell}(\Omega)^{2})
. Moreover, there exists a positive constant
C
, depending only on
\omega
,
T
and
M
such that

(1.3)

\sum_{\ell=0}^{m+1}\left\|u\right\|_{H^{m+1-\ell}(0,T;H^{2\ell}(\Omega)^{2})}\leq C\left(\left\|u_{0}\right\|_{H^{2m+3}(\Omega)^{2}}+\left\|g\right\|_{H^{2(m+7/4),m+7/4}(\Sigma)^{2}}\right).

Notice that the divergence-free condition on
A
requested by Proposition
1.1
is to guarantee that

\mathcal{H}(A,p,q^{\pm})
endowed with homogeneous Dirichlet boundary condition on
\Gamma
, has a self-adjoint realization
H(A,p,q^{\pm})
in
L^{2}(\Omega)^{2}
, see
[
17
, Lemma 2.1]
. As a consequence the operator
-iH(A,p,q^{\pm})
is
m
-dissipative in
L^{2}(\Omega)^{2}
, and since the IBVP (
1.1
) is equivalently rewritten as

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-i\partial_{t}u+\mathcal{H}(A,p,q^{\pm})u=0&\mbox{in}\ Q\\
u(\cdot,0)=u_{0}&\mbox{in}\ \Omega\\
u=g&\mbox{on}\ \Sigma,\end{array}\right.

the statement of Proposition
1.1
follows by arguing in the same way as in the proof of
[
17
, Lemma 2.3]
.

We point out that the regularity assumptions on the coefficients
A
,
p
and
q^{\pm}
, the initial states
u_{0}^{\pm}
and the boundary conditions
g^{\pm}
, in Proposition
1.1
, are only sufficient conditions ensuring a higher order of regularity of the solution

to (
1.1
), as requested by the analysis of the inverse problem under study in this article. As a matter of fact the Bukhgeim-Klibanov method requires
\partial_{t}u^{\pm}
and

to be bounded in

, which can be achieved upon taking

, sufficiently large relative to

. Namely, we choose

(1.4)

N\in\mathbb{N}\cap\left(\frac{n+2}{4}+1,\frac{n+2}{4}+2\right],

pick

,
\kappa
,
\varrho
,
\mathfrak{a}
,
\mathfrak{p}
and
\mathfrak{q}
in
\mathbb{R}_{+}
, and for
A_{0}\in W^{2N+1,\infty}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap C^{2(N-1)}(\overline{\Omega},\mathbb{R}^{n})
,
p_{0}\in W^{2N+1,\infty}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})\cap C^{2(N-1)}(\overline{\Omega},\mathbb{R})
and
q_{0}^{\pm}\in W^{2N+1,\infty}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})\cap C^{2(N-1)}(\overline{\Omega},\mathbb{R})

(1.5)

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\mathfrak{p}}(p_{0})

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\left\{p\in W^{2N+1,\infty}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})\cap C^{2(N-1)}(\overline{\Omega},\mathbb{R})\ \mbox{s.t.}\ \left\|p\right\|_{W^{2N+1,\infty}(\Omega)}\leq M,\right.

\displaystyle\left.\partial_{x}^{k}p=\partial_{x}^{k}p_{0}\ \mbox{on}\ \Gamma,\ k=0,\ldots,2(N-1)\ \mbox{and}\ \left|(p-p_{0})(\cdot,x_{n})\right|\leq\mathfrak{p}e^{-\kappa\langle x_{n}\rangle^{\varrho}},\ x_{n}\in\mathbb{R}\right\},

1.6.
Brief comments

Theorem
1.2
claims that
n+2
Neumann data stably determine
n+2
unknown scalar coefficients (strictly speaking there are
n+3
unknown scalar coefficients in the inverse problem that Theorem
1.2
is dealing with, but since the
n
components of the gradient coupling vector are bound together through some divergence free condition, they only amount for
n-1
free unknown scalar coefficients). From the viewpoint of the analysis of inverse problems, such a result is thus somehow optimal.

The statement and the strategy of the proof of Theorem
1.2
are very similar to the ones of
[
17
, Theorem 1.2]
, which holds for a bounded spatial domain
\Omega
. Nevertheless, there are two major differences in the derivation of Theorem
1.2
as compared to the one of
[
17
, Theorem 1.2]
. Firstly, the Carleman estimate that is used in Section
3
below is designed for a Schrödinger equation in an unbounded cylindrical domain, and it is slightly different from the one used in
[
17
]
, which is specific to the Schrödinger equation in a bounded domain. Secondly, the construction of the initial states
u_{0}
used for probing the system in the analysis of the inverse problem under examination in this article, is more delicate than in
[
17
]
. This is due to the fact that it is technically more challenging to design a suitable set of
L^{2}(\Omega)
-initial states
u_{0}
when the domain
\Omega
is infinitely extended, than in the case where
\Omega
is bounded. As can be seen from Section
3
, this
L^{2}
-condition will be satisfied by introducing an additional decay with respect to the infinite direction of the waveguide.

2.2.
Global Carleman estimate for the Schrödinger equation in
\omega\times\mathbb{R}

For further use we introduce a global Carleman estimate specifically designed for the Schrödinger equation in the unbounded cylindrical domain
\Omega
, which is borrowed from
[
18
, Proposition 3.3 and Lemma 4.2]
.

For this purpose we pick a function
\alpha\in C^{4}(\overline{\omega},\mathbb{R}_{+})
and an open subset
\gamma_{*}\subset\partial\omega
satisfying the following conditions:

Assumption 2.2
.

(i)

\exists c\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
s.t.
\left|\nabla^{\prime}\alpha(x^{\prime})\right|\geq c
for all
x^{\prime}\in\omega
.

(ii)

\forall x^{\prime}\in\gamma\setminus\gamma_{*}
,
\partial_{\nu}\alpha(x^{\prime})=\nabla^{\prime}\alpha(x^{\prime})\cdot\nu^{\prime}(x^{\prime})<0
.

(iii)

\exists\lambda_{0}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
,
\exists c\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
s.t.

\lambda\left|\nabla^{\prime}\alpha(x^{\prime})\cdot\zeta\right|^{2}+D^{2}\alpha(x^{\prime},\zeta)\geq c\left|\zeta\right|^{2},\ \zeta\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1},\ x^{\prime}\in\omega,\ \lambda\geq\lambda_{0},

where
D^{2}\alpha(x^{\prime}):=\left(\partial_{i,j}^{2}\alpha(x^{\prime})\right)_{1\leq i,j\leq n-1}
and
D^{2}\alpha(x^{\prime},\zeta)
denotes the
\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
-scalar product of
D^{2}\alpha(x^{\prime})\zeta
with
\zeta
.

We point out that there exist
\alpha
and
\gamma_{*}
fulfilling the above conditions (i), (ii) and (iii). As a matter of fact, for all

\left\|e^{-s\eta_{0}}\langle x_{n}\rangle^{-\frac{5+\epsilon}{2}}x_{k}x_{n}a_{n}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq\left|\omega\right|\left\|e^{-s\eta_{0}}\langle x_{n}\rangle^{-\frac{5+\epsilon}{2}}x_{n}a_{n}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
, it then follows that

\displaystyle\left\|e^{-s\eta_{0}}\langle x_{n}\rangle^{-\frac{1+\epsilon}{2}}a_{k}\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle Cs^{-3/2}\left(\left\|e^{-s\eta_{0}}A\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{n}}+\left\|e^{-s\eta_{0}}p\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\left\|e^{-s\eta_{0}}q^{+}\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\left\|e^{-s\eta_{0}}q^{-}\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\right.

\displaystyle\left.+s\left(\sum_{i=1}^{2}\left(\mu^{+,i}+\mu^{-,i}\right)+\mu^{+,k+2}+\mu^{-,k+2}\right)\right),\ s\geq s_{1}.

Summing up the above inequality over
k=1,\ldots,n-1
and remembering (
3.23
), we obtain

(3.25)

\displaystyle\left\|e^{-s\eta_{0}}\langle x_{n}\rangle^{-\frac{5+\epsilon}{2}}A\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{n}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle Cs^{-3/2}\left(\left\|e^{-s\eta_{0}}A\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{n}}+\left\|e^{-s\eta_{0}}p\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\left\|e^{-s\eta_{0}}q^{+}\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\left\|e^{-s\eta_{0}}q^{-}\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+s\xi\right),

for
s\geq s_{1}
, where
\xi:=\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}\left(\mu^{+,i}+\mu^{-,i}\right)
.

3.3.
End of the proof

For all
y>0
we have

(3.26)

\displaystyle\left(\langle y\rangle^{-(5+\epsilon)}-Cs^{-\frac{3}{2}}\right)\left(\left\|e^{-s\eta_{0}}A\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{n}}+\left\|e^{-s\eta_{0}}p\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{y})}+\left\|e^{-s\eta_{0}}q^{+}\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{y})}+\left\|e^{-s\eta_{0}}q^{-}\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{y})}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle Cs^{-\frac{3}{2}}\left(\left\|e^{-s\eta_{0}}A\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega\setminus\Omega)^{n}}+\left\|e^{-s\eta_{0}}p\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega\setminus\Omega_{y})}+\left\|e^{-s\eta_{0}}q^{+}\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega\setminus\Omega_{y})}+\left\|e^{-s\eta_{0}}q^{-}\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega\setminus\Omega_{y})}+s\xi\right),

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle Cs^{-\frac{3}{2}}\left(\left\|A\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega\setminus\Omega)^{n}}+\left\|p\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega\setminus\Omega_{y})}+\left\|q^{+}\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega\setminus\Omega_{y})}+\left\|q^{-}\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega\setminus\Omega_{y})}+s\xi\right),\ s\geq s_{1},

by (
3.17
), (
3.19
), (
3.21
) and (
3.25
), where
\Omega_{y}:=\omega\times(-y,y)
. Notice that in the last line of (
3.26
), we used that
\eta_{0}
is non-negative in
\Omega
.
Moreover, for all
y\geq y_{1}:=\left((2C)^{-\frac{2}{3}}s_{1}\right)^{\frac{3}{2(5+\epsilon)}}
we have
s_{y}:=(2C)^{\frac{2}{3}}\langle y\rangle^{\frac{2(5+\epsilon)}{3}}\geq s_{1}
and

2Cs_{y}^{-\frac{3}{2}}\leq\langle y\rangle^{-(5+\epsilon)}
. Therefore, applying (
3.26
) with
s=s_{y}
and using that
\eta_{0}(x)\leq\frac{e^{2K}}{T^{2}}
for all
x\in\Omega
, we obtain that

(3.27)

\Theta_{\Omega_{y}}\leq C\left(\Theta_{\Omega\setminus\Omega_{y}}+\langle y\rangle^{\frac{2(5+\epsilon)}{3}}\xi\right),\ y\geq y_{1},

where we set
\Theta_{X}:=\left\|A\right\|^{2}_{0,X}+\left\|p\right\|^{2}_{0,X}+\left\|q^{+}\right\|^{2}_{0,X}+\left\|q^{-}\right\|^{2}_{0,X}
for any subset
X\subset\Omega
.
Next, using that
p_{j}\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathfrak{p}}(p_{0})
for
j=1,2
, we infer from (
1.5
) upon writing
\left\|p\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega\setminus\Omega_{y})}\leq\sum_{j=1,2}\left\|p_{j}-p_{0}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega\setminus\Omega_{y})}
, that

(3.28)

\displaystyle\left\|p\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega\setminus\Omega_{y})}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 4\mathfrak{p}^{2}\int_{\Omega\setminus\Omega_{y}}e^{-2\kappa\langle x_{n}\rangle^{\varrho}}dx^{\prime}dx_{n}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 4\mathfrak{p}^{2}\left|\omega\right|\int_{\left|x_{n}\right|>y}e^{-2\kappa\langle x_{n}\rangle^{\varrho}}dx_{n}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 4\mathfrak{p}^{2}\left|\omega\right|\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-\delta\langle x_{n}\rangle^{\varrho}}dx_{n}\right)e^{-(2\kappa-\delta)\langle y\rangle^{\varrho}},\ \delta\in(0,2\varrho).

Similarly, since
q_{j}^{\pm}\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathfrak{q}}(q_{0}^{\pm})
and
A_{j}\in\mathcal{A}_{\mathfrak{a}}(A_{0})
for
j=1,2
, we obtain

(3.29)

\Theta_{\Omega\setminus\Omega_{y}}\leq Ce^{-(2\kappa-\delta)\langle y\rangle^{\varrho}},\ \delta\in(0,2\varrho),

from (
1.6
) and (
3.28
), where
C=4\left|\omega\right|(\mathfrak{a}^{2}+\mathfrak{p}^{2}+2\mathfrak{q}^{2})\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-\delta\langle x_{n}\rangle^{\varrho}}dx_{n}
. It follows from this and (
3.27
) that

(3.30)

\Theta_{\Omega_{y}}\leq C\left(e^{-(2\kappa-\delta)\langle y\rangle^{\varrho}}+\langle y\rangle^{\frac{2(5+\epsilon)}{3}}\xi\right),\ y\geq y_{1},\ \delta\in(0,2\varrho).

Put
\xi_{1}:=e^{-(2\kappa-\delta)\langle y_{1}\rangle^{\varrho}}
. We shall examine the two cases
\xi\in(0,\xi_{1}]
and
\xi\in(\xi_{1},+\infty)
separately. Let us start with
\xi\in(0,\xi_{1}]
. In this case, we pick

y\in[y_{1},+\infty)
so large that
e^{-(2\kappa-\delta)\langle y\rangle^{\varrho}}=\xi
, i.e.,
y=\left(\left(-\frac{\ln\xi}{2\kappa-\delta}\right)^{\frac{2}{\varrho}}-1\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}
. Thus, with reference to (
3.29
)-(
3.30
) we get for all
\xi\in(0,\xi_{1}]
that

\Theta_{\Omega\setminus\Omega_{y}}\leq C\xi_{1}^{1-2\theta}\xi^{2\theta}
and that

\Theta_{\Omega_{y}}\leq C\left(\xi_{1}^{1-2\theta}+C_{1}(\theta)\right)\xi^{2\theta}
,
where
C_{1}(\theta):=\sup_{\xi\in(0,\xi_{1}]}\left(\xi^{1-2\theta}\left(\frac{-\ln\xi}{2\kappa-\delta}\right)^{\frac{2(5+\epsilon)}{3\varrho}}\right)<\infty
from the assumption
\varrho>0
. As a consequence we have

(3.31)

\Theta_{\Omega}\leq C\left(2\xi_{1}^{1-2\theta}+C_{1}(\theta)\right)\xi^{2\theta},\ \xi\in(0,\xi_{1}],

and the desired result follows. Now, when
\xi\in(\xi_{1},+\infty)
, we infer from (
1.5
) upon majorizing

\left\|p\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}
by the sum

2\sum_{j=1,2}\left\|p_{j}-p_{0}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}
, that

\left\|p\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq 4\mathfrak{p}^{2}\left|\omega\right|\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-2\kappa\langle x_{n}\rangle^{\varrho}}dx_{n}\right)\xi_{1}^{-2\theta}\xi^{2\theta}
. Doing the same with
q^{\pm}
and
A
, with the aid of, respectively, (
1.5
) and (
1.6
), we find that
\Theta_{\Omega}\leq\tilde{C}_{1}(\theta)\xi^{2\theta}
, where the notation

\tilde{C}_{1}(\theta)
stands for the constant
4\left(\mathfrak{a}^{2}+\mathfrak{p}^{2}+2\mathfrak{q}^{2}\right)\left|\omega\right|\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-2\kappa\langle x_{n}\rangle^{\varrho}}dx_{n}\right)\xi_{1}^{-2\theta}
. This, (
3.31
) and the estimates
\mu^{\pm}_{k}\leq C\left\|\partial_{\nu}v^{\pm,k}\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\tilde{\Sigma}_{*})}
for all
k=1,\ldots,n+1
, yield (
1.9
), which completes the proof of Theorem
1.2
.
