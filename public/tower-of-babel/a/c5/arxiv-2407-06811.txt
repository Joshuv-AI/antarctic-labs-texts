Dunkl approach to slice regular functions
Giulio Binosi, Hendrik De Bie, Pan Lian

Abstract
. In this paper, we show that a Clifford algebra valued function is slice if and only if it is in the kernel of Dunkl-spherical Dirac operator and that a slice function f f is slice regular if and only if it is in the kernel of the Dunkl-Cauchy-Riemann operator of a certain parameter. Based on this observation and the inverse Dunkl intertwining operator, we present a new approach to construct a family of classical monogenic functions from a given holomorphic function.



2020 Mathematics Subject Classification
Primary 30G35; Secondary 33C52, 33C55

1.
Introduction

In the literature, there are mainly two approaches to generalize classical holomorphic function theory to higher dimensions. The first approach is the function theory of several complex variables (see e.g.
[
25
]
), while the second one revolves around the function theory of the Dirac operator, usually referred to as Clifford analysis
[
10
]
. Functions in the kernel of the Dirac operator are called monogenic. Over the last four decades, many results from complex analysis of one variable have been generalized within the latter framework. Unfortunately, the powers of the variables are not in the kernel of the classical Dirac operator. Since the 1990s, Leutwiler, Eriksson, and their collaborators have developed a modification of classical Clifford analysis that incorporates the powers of the variable into the kernel of the modified Dirac operator, see e.g.
[
26
,
27
]
. Interestingly, their adjustment is closely related with hyperbolic geometry, see e.g.
[
17
]
. Around 2005, another generalization of classical Clifford analysis is introduced in
[
2
]
by employing the influential Dunkl operators
[
12
]
, which has attracted a lot of attention in the last years. Some relations between these three kinds of monogenic functions have been discussed in e.g.
[
17
,
24
,
27
]
. See the right half part of Fig.
1
for a rough understanding.

In 2006-2007, a new hyper-complex function theory, mainly studying so-called slice regular functions, was introduced by Gentili and Struppa in
[
21
]
. This theory has attracted considerable interest and experienced a rapid growth over the last years. In fact, the theory of slice regular functions of one variable nowadays is well-established and has found significant applications in the study of quaternionic quantum mechanics and spectral theory of several operators, see e.g.
[
6
]
. Importantly, convergent power series of the form
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}x^{n}a_{n}
are slice regular on an open ball centered at the origin. There is a huge amount of publications on this topic. Without claiming completeness, we refer to
[
5
,
7
,
21
,
20
]
.

Fueter’s theorem (see e.g.
[
8
,
15
,
19
,
30
]
) establishes a link between slice regular (or slice monogenic) functions and monogenic functions. These two theories are also intertwined through the Radon transform
[
4
]
. Besides those links, the hypermonogenic function theory, the Dunkl-Clifford analysis, the slice regular function theory were all developed independently in some sense.

In this paper, we provide a link between these theories giving a new characterization of slice and slice regular functions using the Dunkl-spherical Dirac and the Dunkl-Cauchy-Riemann operators. More precisely, we find that a function defined in an axially symmetric open set is slice if and only if it is in the kernel of the Dunkl-spherical Dirac operator (Proposition
3.3
) and it is slice regular if it is in the kernel of both the Dunkl-spherical Dirac operator and the Dunkl-Cauchy-Riemann operator (Theorem
3.6
), with an appropriate condition on the multiplicity function.
By this observation, we introduce a new approach in Section
5
to construct a family of classical monogenic functions from a given slice regular function based on the inverse Dunkl intertwining operator
[
14
]
, whereas Fueter’s famous theorem only yields one axial monogenic function at one time. Moreover, our approach maps each
x^{n}
homogeneously to monogenic polynomials of the same degree and doesn’t significantly differ in even and odd dimensions. The formula bears a resemblance to the well-known Cauchy-Kovalevskaya extension
[
10
]
. We would also like to remark that the structure of the inverse Dunkl intertwining operator is much simpler than the Dunkl intertwining operator, as the latter one is explicitly known only in some special cases, see e.g.
[
9
,
14
]
. The connection with hyperbolic harmonic functions offers a local way to study slice regular functions, as shown in Theorem
3.8
. Moreover, we find that the functions produced in the Fueter’s scheme are also in the kernel of certain Dunkl-Cauchy-Riemann operators. The Laplace operator in this scheme acts as a shift operator on the index
\gamma_{\kappa}
, see Section
4
. These relations are summarized in Figure
1
.

The rest of this paper is structured as follows. In Section
2
, we recall some basic notions of Dunkl-Clifford analysis and slice regular functions. Section
3

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Dunkl operators

Suppose
\alpha
is a non-zero vector in
\mathbb{R}^{m+1}
. The reflection in the hyperplane orthogonal to
\alpha
is defined by

\sigma_{\alpha}x:=x-2\frac{\langle\alpha,x\rangle}{|\alpha|^{2}}\alpha,\qquad\mbox{for}\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{m+1},

where
\langle\alpha,x\rangle:=\sum_{j=0}^{m}\alpha_{j}x_{j}
is the standard Euclidean inner product and
|x|:=\sqrt{\langle x,x\rangle}
.
A finite set
\mathscr{R}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m+1}\backslash\{0\}
is called a root system if
\mathscr{R}\cap\mathbb{R}\cdot\alpha=\{\alpha,-\alpha\}

and
\sigma_{\alpha}\mathscr{R}=\mathscr{R}
for all
\alpha\in\mathscr{R}
.
The dimension of
{\rm span}_{\mathbb{R}}\mathscr{R}
is called the rank of
\mathscr{R}
.
For a given root system
\mathscr{R}
, the reflections
\sigma_{\alpha}
,
\alpha\in\mathscr{R}
, generate a finite group
W\subset{\rm O}(m+1)
, called the Coxeter-group associated with
\mathscr{R}
. It is known that each root system can be expressed as a disjoint union, i.e.
\mathscr{R}=\mathscr{R}_{+}\cup(-\mathscr{R}_{+})
, where
\mathscr{R}_{+}
and
-\mathscr{R}_{+}
are separated by a hyperplane through the origin. We fix the positive subsystem
\mathscr{R}_{+}
.
From now on, we assume that
\mathscr{R}
is normalized, meaning that
\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle=2
for every
\alpha\in\mathscr{R}
.
A function
\kappa:\mathscr{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
on a root system
\mathscr{R}
is called a multiplicity function if it is invariant under the action of the associated reflection group
W
. For later use, we introduce the index
\gamma_{\kappa}:=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathscr{R}_{+}}\kappa(\alpha).

The number
(m+1)+2\gamma_{\kappa}
is the generalized homogeneous dimension, see
[
14
]
.

Definition 2.1
.

[
12
]

Let
\mathscr{R}
be a root system over
\mathbb{R}^{m+1}
. For each fixed positive subsystem
\mathscr{R}_{+}
and multiplicity function
\kappa
, the Dunkl operator is defined by

T_{i}f(x)=\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}f(x)+\sum_{\alpha\in\mathscr{R}_{+}}\kappa(\alpha)\frac{f(x)-f(\sigma_{\alpha}x)}{\langle\alpha,x\rangle}\alpha_{i},\qquad i=0,1,\ldots,m,

for
f\in\mathcal{C}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{m+1})
.

Remark 2.2
.

The Dunkl operators
\{T_{j}\}_{j=0}^{m}
reduce to the classical partial derivatives when
\kappa\equiv 0
. An important property of Dunkl operators is that for fixed
\kappa
, they commute mutually, i.e.,
T_{i}T_{j}=T_{j}T_{i}
.

The classical Fischer product can be defined in the Dunkl setting. Denote
\mathcal{P}
for the space of polynomial functions on
\mathbb{R}^{m+1}
.

Definition 2.3
.

[
31
]
For any
p,q\in\mathcal{P}
, we define

(2.1)

[p,q]_{\kappa}:=(p(T)q)(0).

The set of all values
\kappa
such that the bilinear form (
2.1
) is degenerate is called the singular set of
\kappa
, denoted by
K^{\rm sing}
.

A complete description of
K^{\rm sing}
was given in
[
13
]
.

Example 2.4
.

For the Dunkl operator on the line, the singular set is given by

K^{\rm sing}=\{-1/2-n,n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}\}.

For more details on Dunkl theory, we refer to
[
14
,
31
]
. In this work, we are mainly interested in the whole sum
\gamma_{\kappa}=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathscr{R}_{+}}\kappa(\alpha)
, rather than the concrete value of the multiplicity function
\kappa
. Therefore, in the subsequent sections where we discuss the index
\gamma_{\kappa}
, the singular set of
\kappa
will be excluded for simplicity.

2.2.
Dunkl-Clifford analysis

We denote by
\mathbb{R}_{0,m}
the real universal Clifford algebra generated by the multiplication rules

\begin{split}\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}e_{j}\,e_{k}=-e_{k}\,e_{j},&1\leq j\neq k\leq m,\\
e_{j}^{2}=-1,&j=1,\ldots,m,\\
\end{array}\right.\end{split}

where
(e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{m})
is the orthonormal basis of the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{m}
. In the following, any vector
(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{m})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
will be identified with

\underline{x}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}x_{j}e_{j}
.
The Clifford product of two vectors splits into a scalar part and a bivector as follows,

(2.2)

\underline{x}\,\underline{y}=-\langle\underline{x},\underline{y}\rangle+\underline{x}\wedge\underline{y},

where

\langle\underline{x},\underline{y}\rangle=\sum_{j=1}^{m}x_{j}y_{j}\quad{\rm and}\quad\underline{x}\wedge\underline{y}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\sum_{k=j+1}^{m}e_{j}e_{k}(x_{j}y_{k}-x_{k}y_{j}).

The space of paravectors is defined by

\{x=x_{0}+e_{1}x_{1}+\cdots+e_{m}x_{m}|x_{0},\ldots,x_{m}\in\mathbb{R}\}\simeq\mathbb{R}^{m+1}.

A basis for
\mathbb{R}_{0,m}
is given by the elements
e_{A}=e_{i_{1}}\ldots e_{i_{j}}
, where
A=\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{j}\}\subset\{1,\ldots,m\}
satisfying
1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{j}\leq m
. Thus an
\mathbb{R}_{0,m}
-valued function
f
over
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{m+1}
can be written as

f=\sum_{A}e_{A}f_{A},

where each component
f_{A}:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a real valued function. If each component
f_{A}\in\mathcal{C}^{1}(\Omega)
, we say that
f
is of class
\mathcal{C}^{1}(\Omega)
. We also need the conjugate (the anti-involution) on
\mathbb{R}_{0,m}
, defined by
e_{0}^{c}=e_{0}
,
e_{j}^{c}=-e_{j}
and
(uv)^{c}=v^{c}\,u^{c}
for any
u,v\in\mathbb{R}_{0,m}
.

In this paper, we will always assume that the finite reflection group
W
associated to the root system
\mathscr{R}
leaves the
x_{0}
-axis invariant and assume that
T_{0}=\partial_{x_{0}}
. Namely, we demand that the root system
\mathscr{R}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
.

Definition 2.5
.

The Dunkl-Dirac operator on
\mathbb{R}^{m}
associated to the root system
\mathscr{R}
is defined by

\underline{D}_{\kappa}:=\sum_{j=1}^{m}e_{j}T_{j}.

Furthermore, the Dunkl-Cauchy-Riemann operator on
\mathbb{R}^{m+1}
is given by

D_{\kappa}:=\partial_{x_{0}}+\underline{D}_{\kappa}.

Functions in the kernel of
D_{\kappa}
or
\underline{D}_{\kappa}
will be called Dunkl monogenic.

Remark 2.6
.

When
\kappa\equiv 0
,
\underline{D}_{\kappa}
reduces to the Euclidean Dirac operator on
\mathbb{R}^{m}
, i.e.

\underline{D}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}e_{j}\partial_{x_{j}},

while
D_{\kappa}
reduces to the generalized Cauchy-Riemann operator
\overline{\partial}_{x}:=\partial_{x_{0}}+\underline{D}.

We consider the spherical part of the Dunkl-Dirac operator.

Lemma 2.7
.

[
18
]
The Dunkl-Dirac operator on
\mathbb{R}^{m}
in spherical coordinates is given by

\underline{D}_{\kappa}=\underline{\omega}\left(\partial_{r}+\frac{1}{r}\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\underline{\omega}}\right),

where
\underline{\omega}=\underline{x}/|\underline{x}|
,
r=|\underline{x}|
and

(2.3)

\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\underline{\omega}}:=\gamma_{\kappa}+\Gamma_{\underline{\omega}}+\Psi

is the Dunkl-spherical Dirac operator, in which

\Gamma_{\underline{\omega}}=-\sum_{i<j}e_{i}e_{j}(x_{i}\partial_{x_{j}}-x_{j}\partial_{x_{i}})

is the spherical Dirac operator and

(2.4)

\begin{split}\Psi f(\underline{x})&:=-\sum_{i<j}e_{i}e_{j}\sum_{\underline{\alpha}\in\mathscr{R}_{+}}\kappa(\underline{\alpha})\frac{f(\underline{x})-f(\sigma_{\underline{\alpha}}\underline{x})}{\langle\underline{\alpha},\underline{x}\rangle}(x_{i}\alpha_{j}-x_{j}\alpha_{i})\\
&\quad-\sum_{\underline{\alpha}\in\mathscr{R}_{+}}\kappa(\underline{\alpha})f(\sigma_{\underline{\alpha}}\underline{x})\end{split}

for any
f\in\mathcal{C}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{m})
.

In the Dunkl case, we have the following generalization of a property of the ordinary spherical Dirac operator.

Lemma 2.8
.

[
18
]
Suppose
M_{k}(\underline{x})
is a homogeneous Dunkl monogenic polynomial of degree
k
on
\mathbb{R}^{m}
, i.e.,
M_{k}\in\ker\underline{D}_{\kappa}
and
M_{k}(\underline{x})=r^{k}M_{k}(\underline{\omega})
. Then it holds

(2.5)

\begin{split}\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\underline{\omega}}(M_{j})=-jM_{j},\\
\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\underline{\omega}}(\underline{x}M_{j})=(2\gamma_{\kappa}+j+m-1)\underline{x}M_{j}.\\
\end{array}\right.\end{split}

Furthermore, for any radial function
f(\underline{x})=f(|\underline{x}|)=f(r)
and
\underline{\omega}=\underline{x}/|\underline{x}|,
we have

(2.6)

\begin{split}\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\underline{\omega}}f(r)=0,\\
\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\underline{\omega}}(\underline{\omega}f(r))=\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\underline{\omega}}(\underline{\omega})f(r),\\
\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\underline{\omega}}(\underline{\omega})=(2\gamma_{\kappa}+m-1)\underline{\omega}.\end{array}\right.\end{split}

2.3.
Slice regular functions

Let
D\subset\mathbb{C}
be an open subset symmetric with respect to the real axis. A function
F\colon D\to\mathbb{R}_{0,m}\otimes\mathbb{C}
is called stem function if it is complex intrinsic, i.e., it satisfies

F(\overline{z})=\overline{F(z)},\qquad\forall\,z\in D,

where
\overline{z}
is the complex conjugation of
z
, and
\overline{(x\otimes z)}:=x\otimes\overline{z}
, for any
x\in\mathbb{R}_{0,m}
,
z\in\mathbb{C}
. Note that, if
F=\alpha+i\beta
, it is equivalent to requiring
\alpha(\overline{z})=\alpha(z)
and
\beta(\overline{z})=-\beta(z)
.
Let
\mathbb{S}^{m-1}
be the
(m-1)
-dimensional unit sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
. Then for any vector
\underline{\omega}\in\mathbb{S}^{m-1}
, it is easy to verify that
\underline{\omega}^{2}=-1
. Given
\underline{\omega}\in\mathbb{S}^{m-1}
, let
\mathbb{C}_{\underline{\omega}}={\rm span}\{1,\underline{\omega}\}
be the complex slice of
\mathbb{R}^{m+1}
generated by
\underline{\omega}
and define the
∗
-algebra isomorphism
\phi_{\underline{\omega}}:\mathbb{C}\ni a+ib\mapsto a+\underline{\omega}b\in\mathbb{C}_{\underline{\omega}}
. We can define the circularization of a symmetric set
D
as

\Omega_{D}:=\bigcup_{\underline{\omega}\in\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}\phi_{\underline{\omega}}(D)=\{a+\underline{\omega}b:a+ib\in D,\underline{\omega}\in\mathbb{S}^{m-1}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m+1}.

Sets of the form
\Omega_{D}
are called axially symmetric and slice domains whenever
D\cap\mathbb{R}\neq\emptyset
.
Given a stem function
F=\alpha+i\beta
, we define the induced slice function
f:=\mathcal{I}(F):\Omega_{D}\to\mathbb{R}_{m}
for any
x=x_{0}+\underline{\omega}|\underline{x}|\in\Omega_{D}
as

(2.7)

f(x):=\alpha(x_{0},|\underline{x}|)+\underline{\omega}\,\beta(x_{0},|\underline{x}|).

We will say that
f
is slice regular if it is induced by a stem function which is holomorphic with respect to the complex structure on
\mathbb{R}_{0,m}\otimes\mathbb{C}
defined by left multiplication by
i
. Equivalently, a slice function
f
is slice regular on
\Omega_{D}
if and only if for any unit vector
\underline{\omega}\in\mathbb{S}^{m-1}
, the restriction
f_{\underline{\omega}}
of the function
f
to the complex plane
\mathbb{C}_{\underline{\omega}}
is holomorphic, i.e.

\overline{\partial}_{\underline{\omega}}f(x_{0}+\underline{\omega}r):=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{0}}+\underline{\omega}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\right)f_{\underline{\omega}}(x_{0}+\underline{\omega}r)=0,

on
\Omega_{D}\cap\mathbb{C}_{\underline{\omega}}
. We denote with
\mathcal{S}(\Omega_{D})
and
\mathcal{S}\mathcal{R}(\Omega_{D})
respectively the set of slice and slice regular functions on
\Omega_{D}
.

Furthermore, we introduce two notions related with slice functions, called the spherical value
f_{s}^{\circ}(x)

and the spherical derivative
f_{s}^{\prime}(x)
of
f
in literature. They are defined as

(2.8)

\begin{split}\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}f_{s}^{\circ}(x):=(f(x)+f(x^{c}))/2,\\
f_{s}^{\prime}(x):={\rm Im}(x)^{-1}(f(x)-f(x^{c}))/2,\\
\end{array}\right.\end{split}

Note that
f^{\circ}_{s}
and
f^{\prime}_{s}
are slice functions as well, induced respectively by
\alpha(z)
and
\beta(z)/\operatorname{Im}(z)
. They are constant on every sphere
\mathbb{S}_{x}=\{x_{0}+\underline{\omega}|\underline{x}|:\underline{\omega}\in\mathbb{S}^{m-1}\}
, so they depend only on
\operatorname{Re}(x)
and
|\operatorname{Im}(x)|
and they decompose
f
through the representation formula

f(x)=f^{\circ}_{s}(x)+\operatorname{Im}(x)f^{\prime}_{s}(x).

In
[
3
]
, Colombo, González-Cervantes and Sabadini introduced a global non-constant coefficients differential operator to study slice regular functions. Here, we consider a slightly different operator,

\overline{\theta}:=\partial_{x_{0}}+\frac{\underline{x}}{|\underline{x}|^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{m}x_{j}\partial_{x_{j}},

defined on the class
\mathcal{C}^{1}(\Omega_{D}\backslash\mathbb{R})
of
\mathbb{R}_{0,m}
-valued functions. The operator
\overline{\theta}
is obtained by dividing the operator in
[
3
]
by
|\underline{x}|^{2}
.
It was found that slice regularity can be characterized as follows:

Lemma 2.9
.

[
23
,
29
]
If
f\in\mathcal{C}^{1}(\Omega_{D})
is a slice function, then
f
is slice-regular if and only if
\overline{\theta}f=0

on
\Omega_{D}\backslash\mathbb{R}
. If
\Omega_{D}\cap\mathbb{R}\neq\emptyset
and
f\in\mathcal{C}^{1}(\Omega_{D})
(not a priori a slice function), then
f
is slice-regular if and only if
\overline{\theta}f=0.

3.
Characterization of slice regular functions

First, we give a compact expression for the Dunkl spherical Dirac operator
\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\underline{\omega}}
given in (
2.3
). Denote

(3.1)

\rho_{\underline{\alpha}}f(\underline{x}):=\frac{f(\underline{x})-f(\sigma_{\underline{\alpha}}\underline{x})}{\langle\underline{\alpha},\underline{x}\rangle}=\int_{0}^{1}\partial_{\alpha}f(\underline{x}-t\langle\underline{\alpha},\underline{x}\rangle\underline{\alpha})\,{\rm d}t

for
f\in\mathcal{C}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{m})
, where
\partial_{\alpha}
is the usual
directional derivative.

Proposition 3.1
.

It holds that

\gamma_{\kappa}+\Psi=-\underline{x}\sum_{\underline{\alpha}\in\mathscr{R}_{+}}\kappa(\underline{\alpha})\underline{\alpha}\,\,\rho_{\underline{\alpha}},

where
\Psi
is defined in (
2.4
).
Thus we have

\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\underline{\omega}}=\Gamma_{\underline{\omega}}-\underline{x}\sum_{\underline{\alpha}\in\mathscr{R}_{+}}\kappa(\underline{\alpha})\underline{\alpha}\,\rho_{\underline{\alpha}}.

Proof.

By definition (
2.4
), we have

\Psi f(\underline{x})=-\sum_{\underline{\alpha}\in\mathscr{R}_{+}}\kappa(\underline{\alpha})\underline{x}\wedge\underline{\alpha}\rho_{\underline{\alpha}}f(\underline{x})-\sum_{\underline{\alpha}\in\mathscr{R}_{+}}\kappa(\underline{\alpha})f(\sigma_{\underline{\alpha}}\underline{x}).

It follows that

\begin{split}(\gamma_{\kappa}+\Psi)f(\underline{x})&=\sum_{\underline{\alpha}\in\mathscr{R}_{+}}\kappa(\underline{\alpha})f(\underline{x})-\sum_{\underline{\alpha}\in\mathscr{R}_{+}}\kappa(\underline{\alpha})\underline{x}\wedge\underline{\alpha}\rho_{\underline{\alpha}}f(\underline{x})-\sum_{\underline{\alpha}\in\mathscr{R}_{+}}\kappa(\underline{\alpha})f(\sigma_{\underline{\alpha}}\underline{x})\\
&=\sum_{\underline{\alpha}\in\mathscr{R}_{+}}\kappa(\underline{\alpha})\left[f(\underline{x})-f(\sigma_{\underline{\alpha}}\underline{x})-\underline{x}\wedge\underline{\alpha}\rho_{\underline{\alpha}}f(\underline{x})\right]\\
&=\sum_{\underline{\alpha}\in\mathscr{R}_{+}}\kappa(\underline{\alpha})\left[\langle\underline{x},\underline{\alpha}\rangle\rho_{\underline{\alpha}}f(\underline{x})-\underline{x}\wedge\underline{\alpha}\rho_{\underline{\alpha}}f(\underline{x})\right]\\
&=-\underline{x}\sum_{\underline{\alpha}\in\mathscr{R}_{+}}\kappa(\underline{\alpha})\underline{\alpha}\,\rho_{\underline{\alpha}}f(\underline{x}),\end{split}

where we have used (
2.2
) in the last step.
Thus
\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\underline{\omega}}=\gamma_{\kappa}+\Gamma_{\underline{\omega}}+\Psi=\Gamma_{\underline{\omega}}-\underline{x}\sum_{\underline{\alpha}\in\mathscr{R}_{+}}\kappa(\underline{\alpha})\underline{\alpha}\rho_{\underline{\alpha}}
.
∎

For later use, we give some properties of
\rho_{\underline{\alpha}}
. We will abuse the notations
\rho_{\underline{\alpha}}f(\underline{x})
and
\rho_{\underline{\alpha}}f(x_{0}+\underline{x})
, which will not cause any confusion when
\mathscr{R}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
.

Lemma 3.2
.

For any
\underline{\alpha}\in\mathscr{R}
, the operator
\rho_{\underline{\alpha}}
in (
3.1
) has the following properties:

(1)

\displaystyle\rho_{\underline{\alpha}}f(|\underline{x}|)=0
;

(2)

\displaystyle\rho_{\underline{\alpha}}(\underline{\omega})=\frac{2}{|\underline{x}|}\frac{\underline{\alpha}}{|\underline{\alpha}|^{2}};

(3)

\displaystyle\rho_{\underline{\alpha}}(f)=\frac{2\underline{\alpha}}{|\underline{\alpha}|^{2}}f^{\prime}_{s}
for any slice function
f
;

(4)

\displaystyle\rho_{\underline{\alpha}}f(\sigma_{\underline{\alpha}}\underline{x})=\rho_{\underline{\alpha}}f(\underline{x})
;

(5)

\displaystyle\rho_{\underline{\alpha}}^{2}f=0
for any function.

Proof.

(1)

It follows from
|\sigma_{\underline{\alpha}}(\underline{x})|=|\underline{x}|
that
\displaystyle\rho_{\underline{\alpha}}f(|\underline{x}|)=\frac{f(|\underline{x}|)-f(|\sigma_{\underline{\alpha}}\underline{x}|)}{\langle{\underline{\alpha}},\underline{x}\rangle}=0
.

(2)

Since
\displaystyle\underline{\omega}(\sigma_{\underline{\alpha}}\underline{x})=\frac{\sigma_{\underline{\alpha}}\underline{x}}{|\sigma_{\underline{\alpha}}\underline{x}|}=\frac{\sigma_{\underline{\alpha}}\underline{x}}{|\underline{x}|}=\underline{\omega}(\underline{x})-\frac{2\langle{\underline{\alpha}},\underline{x}\rangle}{|\underline{\alpha}|^{2}|\underline{x}|}\underline{\alpha}
, we have

\rho_{\underline{\alpha}}\underline{\omega}=\frac{2\langle{\underline{\alpha}},\underline{x}\rangle}{|{\underline{\alpha}}|^{2}|\underline{x}|}\frac{{\underline{\alpha}}}{\langle{\underline{\alpha}},\underline{x}\rangle}=\frac{2\underline{\alpha}}{|\underline{\alpha}|^{2}|\underline{x}|}.

(3)

For a slice function
f
,

\rho_{\underline{\alpha}}(f)=\rho_{\underline{\alpha}}[f^{\circ}_{s}(x)+\operatorname{Im}(x)f^{\prime}_{s}(x)]=\rho_{\underline{\alpha}}(f^{\circ}_{s}(x))+\rho_{\underline{\alpha}}(\operatorname{Im}(x)f^{\prime}_{s}(x)).

The first term on the right side vanishes by (1). For the second term, by the facts
f_{s}^{\prime}(\sigma_{\underline{\alpha}}x)=f_{s}^{\prime}(x)
and
{\rm Im}(x)=\underline{\omega}|\underline{x}|
, we have

\rho_{\underline{\alpha}}(\operatorname{Im}(x)f^{\prime}_{s}(x))=\rho_{\underline{\alpha}}(\underline{\omega})|\underline{x}|f_{s}^{\prime}(x).

Now using (2), we obtain the formula (3).

(4)

\rho_{\underline{\alpha}}f(\sigma_{\underline{\alpha}}\underline{x})=\dfrac{f(\sigma_{\underline{\alpha}}\underline{x})-f(\sigma_{\underline{\alpha}}^{2}\underline{x})}{\langle\underline{\alpha},\sigma_{\underline{\alpha}}\underline{x}\rangle}=\dfrac{f(\sigma_{\underline{\alpha}}\underline{x})-f(\underline{x})}{-\langle\underline{\alpha},\underline{x}\rangle}=\rho_{\underline{\alpha}}f(\underline{x}).

(5)

By (4), we have

\rho_{\underline{\alpha}}^{2}f\underline{(}\underline{x})=\dfrac{\rho_{\underline{\alpha}}f(\underline{x})-\rho_{\underline{\alpha}}f(\sigma_{\underline{\alpha}}\underline{x})}{\langle\underline{\alpha},\underline{x}\rangle}=\dfrac{\rho_{\underline{\alpha}}f(\underline{x})-\rho_{\underline{\alpha}}f(\underline{x})}{\langle\underline{\alpha},\underline{x}\rangle}=0.

∎

Proposition 3.3

(Characterization of sliceness)
.

Let

for every

. In particular,
\underline{\alpha}\rho_{\underline{\alpha}}f=\underline{\beta}\rho_{\underline{\beta}}f

and slice function

In order to prove the previous Proposition, we use a deformation of Fischer decomposition with respect to the Dunkl-Dirac operator

4.
Fueter’s theorem revisited

Let
D
be an open subset of
\mathbb{C}
, symmetric with respect to the real axis and let
F:D\to\mathbb{R}_{0,m}\otimes\mathbb{C}
be a holomorphic stem function defined by

F(z)=\alpha(s,t)+i\beta(s,t),\qquad z=s+it\in D.

Then
F

induces a slice regular function

(4.1)

f(x_{0}+\underline{x})=\alpha(x_{0},|x|)+\frac{\underline{x}}{|\underline{x}|}\beta(x_{0},|\underline{x}|)

on the set
\Omega_{D}:=\{x_{0}+\underline{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{m+1}:(x_{0},|x|)\in D\}
. When
m
is odd,
the classical Fueter’s theorem (see e.g.
[
19
,
30
,
32
]
) states that the function

\Delta_{\mathbb{R}^{m+1}}^{\frac{m-1}{2}}f(x_{0}+\underline{x})

is monogenic. It was already proved in Theorem
3.6
that
f(x_{0}+\underline{x})\in\ker D_{\kappa}
with
\gamma_{\kappa}=(1-m)/2
.
In this section, we show that the functions induced in Fueter’s scheme, i.e.

\Delta_{\mathbb{R}^{m+1}}f(x_{0}+\underline{x}),\quad\Delta_{\mathbb{R}^{m+1}}^{2}f(x_{0}+\underline{x}),\quad\ldots,\quad\Delta_{\mathbb{R}^{m+1}}^{\frac{m-3}{2}}f(x_{0}+\underline{x}),

are also in the kernel of certain
D_{\kappa}
and there is an interesting shift property on the index
\gamma_{\kappa}
. Note that most of the computations have already been done in
[
18
]
. Here we only provide a different viewpoint.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
f(x_{0}+\underline{x})
be a slice function of the form

(4.2)

f(x_{0}+\underline{x})=\alpha(x_{0},r)+\underline{\omega}\,\beta(x_{0},r)

with
r=|\underline{x}|
.
We define

\begin{split}\mathcal{V}_{n}f:=&\left(\frac{1}{r}\partial_{r}\right)^{n}\alpha(x_{0},r)+\underline{\omega}\left(\partial_{r}\frac{1}{r}\right)^{n}\beta(x_{0},r)\\
:=&A(x_{0},r)+\underline{\omega}B(x_{0},r).\end{split}

Lemma 4.2
.

[
18
]
For a slice regular function
f(x)
of the form (
4.2
), we have

\begin{split}\mathcal{V}_{n}f(x_{0}+\underline{x})\sim\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\Delta_{\mathbb{R}^{m+1}}^{n}f,&0\leq n\leq(m-1)/2\in\mathbb{N},\\
\Delta_{\kappa}^{n}f,&0\leq n\leq\gamma_{\kappa}+(m-1)/2\in\mathbb{N}.\\
\end{array}\right.\end{split}

Here the notation
\sim
means that they are equal up to a non-zero real constant and
\Delta_{\kappa}=\sum_{j=0}^{m}T_{j}^{2}
is the Dunkl Laplacian on
\mathbb{R}^{m+1}
. (Note that there is an overlap for the domain of
n
above. )

Proof.

The second line is proved in Lemma 4.2 in
[
18
]
, where the first line is proved implicitly there.

∎

Lemma 4.3
.

[
18
]
A slice function
f
on
\Omega_{D}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m+1}
of the form (
4.2
)
is Dunkl monongenic, i.e.,
D_{\kappa}f=0
, if and only if
\alpha(x_{0},r)
and
\beta(x_{0},r)
satisfy the Vekua-type system

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\partial_{x_{0}}\alpha-\partial_{r}\beta=\displaystyle\frac{2\gamma_{\kappa}+m-1}{r}\beta,\\
\partial_{x_{0}}\beta+\partial_{r}\alpha=0.\end{array}\right.

Theorem 4.4
.

Suppose
f(x_{0}+\underline{x})
is a slice regular function of the form (
4.2
), then we have

\mathcal{V}_{n}f\in\ker D_{\kappa},\qquad n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\},

with
\gamma_{\kappa}=n+\frac{1-m}{2}
. In other words, for a fixed root system
\mathscr{R}
with positive multiplicity function
\kappa
such that
\gamma_{\kappa}+\left(\frac{m-1}{2}\right)\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
, we have

(4.3)

\begin{split}\mathcal{V}_{n}f(x_{0}+\underline{x})\in\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\ker\overline{\partial}_{x},&n=(m-1)/2\in\mathbb{N},\\
\ker D_{\kappa},&n=\gamma_{\kappa}+(m-1)/2\in\mathbb{N}.\\
\end{array}\right.\end{split}

Proof.

By Lemma
4.3
, it is sufficient to show that
A
and
B
(see Definition
4.1
) satisfy the Vekua-type system. Indeed, we have

\begin{split}\partial_{x_{0}}A-\partial_{r}B&=\left(\frac{1}{r}\partial_{r}\right)^{n}\{\partial_{x_{0}}\alpha(x_{0},r)\}-\partial_{r}\left(\partial_{r}\frac{1}{r}\right)^{n}\beta(x_{0},r)\\
&=\left(\frac{1}{r}\partial_{r}\right)^{n}\{\partial_{r}\beta(x_{0},r)\}-\partial_{r}\left(\partial_{r}\frac{1}{r}\right)^{n}\beta(x_{0},r)\\
&=\frac{2n}{r}\left(\partial_{r}\frac{1}{r}\right)^{n}\beta(x_{0},r)\\
&=\frac{2n}{r}B,\end{split}

where the second equality follows from the fact that
\alpha
and
\beta
satisfy the classical Cauchy-Riemann equations, and the third equality is by
[
18
, Lemma 4.1 (iv)]
.

Similarly, we have

\begin{split}\partial_{x_{0}}B+\partial_{r}A&=\left(\partial_{r}\frac{1}{r}\right)^{n}\{\partial_{x_{0}}\beta(x_{0},r)\}+\partial_{r}\left(\frac{1}{r}\partial_{r}\right)^{n}\{\alpha(x_{0},r)\}\\
&=\left(\partial_{r}\frac{1}{r}\right)^{n}\{\partial_{x_{0}}\beta(x_{0},r)\}+\left(\partial_{r}\frac{1}{r}\right)^{n}\{\partial_{r}\alpha(x_{0},r)\}\\
&=\left(\partial_{r}\frac{1}{r}\right)^{n}\{\partial_{x_{0}}\beta(x_{0},r)+\partial_{r}\alpha(x_{0},r)\}\\
&=0,\end{split}

where we have used the fact
\partial_{r}\left(\frac{1}{r}\partial_{r}\right)^{n}=\left(\partial_{r}\frac{1}{r}\right)^{n}\partial_{r}
in the second equality.

This completes the proof.
∎

Remark 4.5
.

The first formula in (
4.3
) is indeed the classical Fueter’s theorem, while the second one is the Fueter’s theorem for Dunkl-monogenics obtained in
[
18
, Theorem 1.1]
.

As a summary, the functions in Fueter’s scheme are in the kernel of the Dunkl-Cauchy-Riemann operator
D_{\kappa}
, with the index
\gamma_{\kappa}
given as follows,

\begin{matrix}&f\longrightarrow&\Delta_{\mathbb{R}^{m+1}}f\longrightarrow&\cdots&\longrightarrow\Delta_{\mathbb{R}^{m+1}}^{\frac{m-1}{2}}f&\longrightarrow\mathcal{V}_{\frac{m+1}{2}}f\longrightarrow&\mathcal{V}_{\frac{m+3}{2}}f\longrightarrow&\cdots\\
\\
\gamma_{\kappa}:&\frac{1-m}{2}\quad\quad&\frac{3-m}{2}\qquad&\cdots&\qquad 0&1&2&\cdots\\
\end{matrix}

This means that for the slice regular functions, the operator
\mathcal{V}_{n}
(i.e. Laplacian
\Delta_{\mathbb{R}^{m+1}}
or the Dunkl-Laplacian
\Delta_{\kappa}
) acts as a shift operator on the index
\gamma_{\kappa}
. Some closely related results are given recently in
[
1
]
.

5.
New approach to generate monogenic functions by the inverse intertwining operator

In this section, we always assume that the multiplicity function
\kappa\not\in K^{{\rm sing}}
in the Dunkl operator and
\gamma_{\kappa}=(1-m)/2
.

Recall that the inverse intertwining operator (see e.g.
[
14
, Definition 6.5.1]
) is defined as follows:

\mathcal{T}f(x_{0}+\underline{x}):={\rm Exp}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m}x_{j}T_{j}^{(\underline{y})}\right)f(x_{0}+\underline{y})\bigg|_{\underline{y}=0},

where in the above formula, the operator
T_{j}^{(\underline{y})}
acts on the variable
\underline{y}
of
f(x_{0}+\underline{y})
first; then we set set
\underline{y}=0
, ultimately obtaining a function in
x_{0}+\underline{x}
.

Theorem 5.1
.

Suppose
f
is a slice regular function of the form (
4.2
), then the function
\mathcal{T}f(x_{0}+\underline{x})

is monogenic, i.e. it is in the kernel of the generalized Cauchy-Riemann operator
\overline{\partial}_{x}=\partial_{x_{0}}+\sum_{j=1}^{m}e_{j}\partial_{x_{j}}
.

Proof.

It is sufficient to verify

\begin{split}\left(\partial_{x_{0}}+\sum_{j=1}^{m}e_{j}\partial_{x_{j}}\right)\mathcal{T}f(x_{0}+\underline{x})&=\mathcal{T}\left(\left(\partial_{x_{0}}+\sum_{j=1}^{m}e_{j}T_{j}\right)f(x_{0}+\underline{x})\right)\\
&=\mathcal{T}0=0.\end{split}

The first step is by the property of the inverse intertwining operator

\partial_{x_{j}}\circ\mathcal{T}=\mathcal{T}\circ T_{j}

at least formally, see
[
14
, Proposition 6.5.2]
.
The second step is by Theorem
3.6
.
∎

Some remarks are now in the order.

Remark 5.2
.

As opposed to the classical Fueter’s theorem (see e.g.
[
30
,
32
]
), there is no significant difference between even and odd dimensions in this approach. However, singular parameters
\kappa
should be excluded.

Remark 5.3
.

Our formula looks similar with the other well-known approach to construct monogenic functions, namely the
Cauchy-Kovalevskaya extension
(see e.g.
[
11
, Theorem 2]
)
, given by

f(x_{0}+\underline{x})={\rm Exp}\left(-x_{0}\sum_{j=1}^{m}e_{j}\partial_{x_{j}}\right)f(\underline{x}).

Remark 5.4
.

The formal inverse intertwining operator
\mathcal{T}
exists for any multiplicity function, see
[
14
]
.
On the other hand, it is known that the Dunkl’s intertwining operator for the rank 1 root system can be expressed explicitly using the Weyl fractional integral, see e.g.
[
9
,
14
]
. It will be interesting to write
\mathcal{T}
in terms of classical fractional integrals and ordinary derivatives for the rank 1 root system.
Then one can give more precise convergence discussions.

Remark 5.5
.

It is interesting to ask the domain of
\mathcal{T}f(x_{0}+\underline{x})
. From the proof of Theorem
5.1
, the result holds when the inverse intertwining operator is well defined and the inverse intertwining relations hold.

At the end of this section, we give some examples mainly related with the Dunkl operators on the line.

Example 5.6
.

For the polynomial
x_{0}+\underline{x}=x_{0}+e_{1}x_{1}+e_{2}x_{2}+e_{3}x_{3}
, the classical Fueter’s theorem in
[
32
]
produces a trivial monogenic function.
If we choose
D_{\kappa}=\partial_{x_{0}}+e_{1}\partial_{x_{1}}+e_{2}\partial_{x_{1}}+e_{3}T_{3}
, where

(5.1)

T_{3}f(x)=\partial_{x_{3}}f(x)-\frac{f(x)-f(\sigma_{e_{3}}x)}{x_{3}},

then we have

\mathcal{T}(x_{0}+\underline{x})=x_{0}+e_{1}x_{1}+e_{2}x_{2}-e_{3}x_{3},

which is a homogeneous monogenic polynomial of degree 1.
Similarly, if we put the Dunkl operator in the variable
x_{2}
, it yields

\mathcal{T}(x_{0}+\underline{x})=x_{0}+e_{1}x_{1}-e_{2}x_{2}+e_{3}x_{3}.

Example 5.7
.

For the polynomial

x^{2}=(x_{0}+\underline{x})^{2}=(x_{0}+e_{1}x_{1}+e_{2}x_{2}+e_{3}x_{3})^{2},

Fueter’s theorem in
[
32
]
again produces a constant, which is clearly monogenic. While choosing as before
D_{\kappa}=\partial_{x_{0}}+e_{1}\partial_{x_{1}}+e_{2}\partial_{x_{1}}+e_{3}T_{3}
, where
T_{3}
is the operator defined in (
5.1
), we obtain

\mathcal{T}(x_{0}+\underline{x})^{2}=x_{0}^{2}-x_{1}^{2}-x_{2}^{2}+x_{3}^{2}+2x_{0}(e_{1}x_{1}+e_{2}x_{2}-e_{3}x_{3}),

which is a homogeneous monogenic of degree 2.

Furthermore, if we consider the root system
\mathscr{R}=\{\pm e_{j}\}_{j=1}^{3}
, with
D_{h}=\partial_{x_{0}}+e_{1}T_{1}+e_{2}T_{2}+e_{3}T_{3}
, in which

T_{j}f(x)=\partial_{x_{j}}f(x)+\kappa(e_{j})\frac{f(x)-f(\sigma_{e_{j}}x)}{x_{j}},\qquad i=1,2,3,

our approach produces a family of monogenic functions under the condition
\gamma_{\kappa}=\sum_{i=1}^{3}\kappa(e_{i})=-1
:

\mathcal{T}(x_{0}+\underline{x})^{2}=x_{0}^{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{3}x_{i}^{2}(1+2\kappa(e_{i}))+2x_{0}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{3}e_{i}x_{i}(1+2\kappa(e_{i}))\right).

Indeed, we have

\overline{\partial}_{x}\mathcal{T}(x_{0}+\underline{x})^{2}=-4x_{0}\left(1+\sum_{i=1}^{3}\kappa(e_{i})\right)=0.

This can also be extended to the general Clifford setting. Consider the polynomial on
\mathbb{R}^{m+1}
,

x^{2}=(x_{0}+\underline{x})^{2}=x_{0}^{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{m}x_{i}^{2}+2x_{0}\sum_{i=1}^{m}e_{i}x_{i}

and the root system
\mathscr{R}=\{\pm e_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
. Then we have

\mathcal{T}x^{2}=x_{0}^{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{m}x_{i}^{2}(1+2\kappa(e_{i}))+2x_{0}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}e_{i}x_{i}(1+2\kappa(e_{i}))\right).

These are monogenic functions under the condition
\gamma_{\kappa}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\kappa(e_{i})=(1-m)/2
. Indeed, straightforward computation shows that

\overline{\partial}_{x}\mathcal{T}x^{2}=4x_{0}\left(\frac{1-m}{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{m}\kappa(e_{i})\right)=0.

6.
Concluding remarks

This paper establishes the groundwork for a research project to investigate the interactions among Dunkl, Clifford and slice analysis. As shown, the inverse intertwining operator in Dunkl theory, serves as a potent tool that naturally generates examples of monogenic functions, in a different way than Fueter’s celebrated theorem. These elucidated connections are poised to yield novel insights into slice regular functions, leveraging methodologies from Dunkl theory and hyperbolic function theory. Noteworthy outcomes will include the derivation of novel mean value properties and the Cauchy integral formula, among others. Moreover, the reflections can be shown to be involutional anti-automorphisms which is the basis of the construction of slice functions in the context of alternating
∗
-algebras in
[
22
]
. Currently, only a small part of these tools has been taken into consideration and in the future we plan to analyze them in more depth.
