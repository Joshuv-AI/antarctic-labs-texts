Minimising Numbers of Losses and Abandonments in Small Call Centres Under a Transient Regime
Hritika Gupta, Peter G. Taylor

Abstract
In this paper, we show how to calculate transient performance measures in models for small call centres that employ skills-based routing. In particular, we calculate the expected number of customer losses and call abandonments in a fixed time. We use the results to compare how call allocation policies can minimise the expected numbers of losses and abandonments, and make recommendations about which policies should be employed.

Introduction

The term
call centre
is commonly used to describe a telephone-based human-service operation. A call centre provides teleservices, namely services in which customers and service agents are remote from each other (
[
15
]
). They have become increasingly popular as service quality is now a very important factor in attracting and retaining customers.

Human resources typically account for more than
60\%
of the total operating cost of a call centre (
[
9
]
,
[
7
]
). Hence, it is not profitable to overstaff a call centre. On the other hand, understaffing a call centre leads to poor customer service which can have a negative impact on the business’s reputation. Therefore, it is important to strike a balance and appropriately staff a call centre. However, it can be hard to estimate the workload of a call centre because of the uncertainty involved in the time and number of call arrivals, type of query, service time and customers’ patience level.

Call centre service providers frequently have contracts with their clients under which they have to manage the client’s customer care department and meet certain quality standards, for example, serving at least
90\%
of the calls within 20 seconds and having less than
3\%
of call abandonments per month. A reasonable objective for a call centre service provider is to minimise the staffing requirements while meeting all the targets. This can be achieved by improving the prediction accuracy of call arrivals, using an efficient scheduling method and making the best use of resources by appropriately allocating an incoming call to an agent.

When a customer calls a call centre, they are first connected to an
Interactive Voice Response (IVR)
system, where they are required to provide some information regarding their query. In some instances the query is resolved by the IVR system itself and the call is completed. In all other cases, the customer requires human assistance. In such cases, if a suitable agent is available, the caller is transferred to that agent. Otherwise, the customer joins a queue where they wait either until they are served or abandon the queue depending on their patience level and the value of that service for them.

In many call centres, customer queries are divided among different categories depending on the level of complexity of the query. Similarly, agents are also assigned a skill level depending on their ability to solve these queries. When a call arrives, based on the caller’s initial input to the IVR, they are assigned to an agent with the appropriate skill level. Some agents are multi-skilled and can deal with more than one type of query. When a call centre has multi-skilled agents, it can lead to a number of possibilities for allocating an incoming call to an agent. We call these rules of assigning an incoming call to an agent a
policy
.

In this paper, we develop a method to calculate various performance measures related to call centres such as the expected number of call abandonments and the expected total waiting time of customers. We show how to calculate these measures over short time intervals using Markov chains under a transient setting without using any asymptotic regimes. Hence, the method is appropriate for small call centres which is important because routing policies make a bigger difference in such cases. Since, the number of agents and the arrival rate can change over a short timescale, implying that the initial distribution has a significant impact on the performance of a call centre, it is crucial to calculate these quantities in a transient regime. We can then utilise these performance measures to compare different routing policies. We have verified all the results presented using simulations.

Different policies can lead to a difference in the performance level of a call centre. Consider an example in which customers are calling with either a sales query or a technical query. Some agents, which we call level 1 agents, are only trained to answer sales queries, and other agents, which we call level 2 agents, are trained to answer both sales and technical queries. Say, a sales query is coming in and both level 1 and level 2 agents are available. The best course of action, in this case, would be to assign that query to one of the available level 1 agents. Similarly, when a technical query is coming in and a level 2 agent is available, we would assign the query to the available level 2 agent. However, what if a sales query is coming in and all level 1 agents are busy handling other calls whereas a level 2 agent is available? Should we assign this call to the available level 2 agent and block them from taking a technical query for a while, or wait for a level 1 agent to become free by making the caller wait in the queue? This decision can depend on a number of factors, for example, the number of level 2 agents available, the service rate of a sales query caller by a level 2 agent, the abandonment rate of a sales query caller and the priority of a sales query caller as compared to a technical query caller for the call centre.

The type of policy we look at is a reservation policy. Under this policy, if there is an arrival of a level
i-1
caller, we first try to assign that call to a level
i-1
agent and if they all are busy, we check the number of level
i
agents available, if there are at least
n_{i}
agents available at level
i
, we assign the call to one of the available level
i
agents, otherwise the caller has to wait in the queue.

When there is a service by a level
i
agent, the level
i
agent who becomes available will first look for level
i
calls and answer them in first-come-first-served (FCFS) order, and if no level level
i
calls are in waiting but level
i-1
calls are in waiting, then they will take the first level
i-1
call in the queue if the number of level
i
agents available is at least
n_{i}
. This implies that FCFS is followed across the same level of calls but not across all levels.

The objective is to find the optimal value of the number of agents to be reserved at each level for a given set of parameters and the initial state of the system so that the expected number of call abandonments is minimised during some future time interval.

We model the system as a continuous-time Markov chain (CTMC), assuming that the calls are arriving according to a Poisson process, and the service and abandonment times are exponentially distributed. The method we will use here was introduced in
[
8
]
.

For illustrative purposes, in Section
2

3
, we extend the method to four types of queries and agents. In Section
4
, we provide some numerical examples on how to employ the method to find the optimal allocation policy. In Section
5
, we discuss the limitations of the method and in Section
6
, we compare our optimal policy with some other policies using simulations. We conclude the paper in Section
7
.

2.2
Time-dependent transition probabilities

We have discussed how to calculate the expected number of call abandonments over a single time interval, but what if we want to calculate this measure over multiple time intervals with varying parameters? For example, say the day is divided into two shifts with varying numbers of agents and call arrival rates. In such a case, we want to be able to project over both time intervals with the initial state of the second interval being the final state of the first interval. In this subsection, we will calculate the distribution of the state of the system after
t

.

Let

at time 0. Let

\lambda_{j}=\lambda\mathbbm{1}_{0\leq j<\ell},

\mu_{j}=\min(j,k)\mu\mathbbm{1}_{0<j\leq\ell}

Let

(19)

and

\min(c-k_{3},k_{4}-d-n_{4})\mathbbm{1}_{c>k_{3},d<k_{4}-n_{4}}\leq c_{1}\leq\min(k_{4}-n_{4},c).

(20)

Hence, the state space is given by
\Omega=\{(a,a_{1},b,b_{1},c,c_{1},d)\}
such that

•

a,b,c,d\in\{0,1,\dots,l\}
,

•

a+b+c+d\leq\ell
,

•

\min(a-k_{1},k_{2}-b-n_{2})\mathbbm{1}_{a>k_{1},b<k_{2}-n_{2}}\leq a_{1}\leq\min(k_{2}-n_{2},a)
,

•

\min(b-k_{2},k_{3}-c-n_{3})\mathbbm{1}_{b>k_{2},c<k_{3}-n_{3}}\leq b_{1}\leq\min(k_{3}-n_{3},b)
and

•

\min(c-k_{3},k_{4}-d-n_{4})\mathbbm{1}_{c>k_{3},d<k_{4}-n_{4}}\leq c_{1}\leq\min(k_{4}-n_{4},c)
.

Now, for a given state
(a,a_{1},b,b_{1},c,c_{1},d)\in\Omega
at time
0
and reservation vector
\mathbf{n}
, we define
A_{a,a_{1},b,b_{1},c,c_{1},d}(\mathbf{n},t),t\geq 0
as the expected cost of abandonments and losses during the time interval
(0,t)
.

3.3
Transition rates

For transitions between the states, we can either have an arrival or a service or an abandonment at each level. We will now discuss all the possible transitions from the state
(a,a_{1},b,b_{1},c,c_{1},d)
.

•

If a level 1 caller arrives, it leads to three possibilities.

–

The first possibility is that they are allocated to a level 1 agent if at least one of them is available leading to the state
(a+1,a_{1},b,b_{1},c,c_{1},d)
.

–

The second possibility is that they are allocated to a level 2 agent (if no level 1 agent is available, no level 2 call is in waiting and there are more than
n_{2}
level 2 agents available) leading to the state
(a+1,a_{1}+1,b,b_{1},c,c_{1},d)
.

–

The third possibility is that they join the queue if neither of the first two conditions are satisfied leading to the state
(a+1,a_{1},b,b_{1},c,c_{1},d)
.

Hence, there is a transition to the state
(a+1,a_{1}+\mathbbm{1}(a-a_{1}\geq k_{1},b-b_{1}<k_{2}-n_{2}-a_{1}),b,b_{1},c,c_{1},d)
with rate
\lambda_{1}
if
a+b+c+d<\ell
, otherwise the caller is blocked, costing
\beta
.

•

Similarly, a transition to the state
(a,a_{1},b+1,b_{1}+\mathbbm{1}(b-b_{1}\geq k_{2},c-c_{1}<k_{3}-n_{3}-b_{1}),c,c_{1},d)
happens with rate
\lambda_{2}
and a transition to the state
(a,a_{1},b,b_{1},c+1,c_{1}+\mathbbm{1}(c-c_{1}\geq k_{3},d<k_{4}-n_{4}-c_{1}),d)
happens with the rate
\lambda_{3}
if
a+b+c+d<\ell
.

•

When a level 4 caller arrives, there is only one possibility, that is a transition to the state
(a,a_{1},b,b_{1},c,c_{1},d+1)
with rate
\lambda_{4}
if
a+b+c+d<\ell
.

•

A service of level 1 caller by a level 1 agent happens with rate
\min(k_{1},a-a_{1})\mu_{1}
leading to the state
(a-1,a_{1},b,b_{1},c,c_{1},d)
.

•

If there is a service of level 1 caller by a level 2 agent, it leads to two possibilities.

–

The first is that after they are served, that agent becomes free or starts serving a level 2 caller if there are any in waiting leading to the state
(a-1,a_{1}-1,b,b_{1},c,c_{1},d)
.

–

The second is that after they are served, the agent starts serving another level 1 caller leading to the state
(a-1,a_{1},b,b_{1},c,c_{1},d)
.

Hence, there is transition to the state
(a-1,a_{1}-(1-\mathbbm{1}(a-a_{1}>k_{1},b-b_{1}=k_{2}-n_{2}-a_{1}),b,b_{1},c,c_{1},d)
with rate
a_{1}\mu_{1}^{\prime}
.

•

Similarly, when there is a service of a level 2 caller by a level 2 agent, that agent can either serve another level 2 caller or depending on the conditions, serve a level 1 caller or stay available. Hence, we transition to the state
(a,a_{1}+\mathbbm{1}(a-a_{1}>k_{1},b-b_{1}=k_{2}-n_{2}-a_{1}),b-1,b_{1},c,c_{1},d)
with rate
\min(b-b_{1},k_{2}-a_{1})\mu_{2}
.

•

Similarly, we transition to

–

(a,a_{1},b-1,b_{1}-(1-\mathbbm{1}(b-b_{1}>k_{2}-a_{1},c=k_{3}-n_{3}-b_{1}),c,c_{1},d)
with rate
b_{1}\mu_{2}^{\prime}
when there is a service of level 2 caller by a level 3 agent,

–

(a,a_{1},b,b_{1}+\mathbbm{1}(b-b_{1}>k_{2},c-c_{1}=k_{3}-n_{3}-b_{1}),c-1,c_{1},d)
with rate
\min(c-c_{1},k_{3}-b_{1})\mu_{3}
when there is a service of a level 3 caller by a level 3 agent,

–

(a,a_{1},b,b_{1},c-1,c_{1}-(1-\mathbbm{1}(c-c_{1}>k_{3}-b_{1},d=k_{4}-n_{4}-c_{1}),d)
with rate
c_{1}\mu_{3}^{\prime}
when a level 3 caller is served by a level 4 agent and

–

(a,a_{1},b,b_{1},c,c_{1}+\mathbbm{1}(c-c_{1}>k_{3}-b_{1},d=k_{4}-n_{4}-c_{1}),d-1)
with rate
\min(d,k_{4}-c_{1})\mu_{4}
when there is a service of level 4 caller.

•

The transitions in case of abandonments are straightforward. There is a transition to

–

(a-1,a_{1},b,b_{1},c,c_{1},d)
when a level 1 caller abandons with rate
\max(0,a-(k_{1}+a_{1}))\theta_{1}
costing
\gamma_{1}
,

–

(a,a_{1},b-1,b_{1},c,c_{1},d)
when a level 2 caller abandons with rate
\max(0,b-(k_{2}-a_{1}+b_{1}))\theta_{2}
costing
\gamma_{2}
,

–

(a,a_{1},b,b_{1},c-1,c_{1},d)
when a level 3 caller abandons with rate
\max(0,c-(k_{3}-b_{1}+c_{1}))\theta_{3}
costing
\gamma_{3}
and

–

(a,a_{1},b,b_{1},c,c_{1},d-1)
when a level 4 caller abandons with rate
\max(0,d-(k_{4}-c_{1}))\theta_{4}
costing
\gamma_{4}
.

5
Limitations

This method works well for policies that do not require tracking of the arrival times of individual customers. For example, if we want to analyse a policy in which we first try to assign a caller to an agent at its own level, and if they are not available, then we transfer it to an agent at a higher level after waiting for say 15 seconds, this kind of policy would require a separate state variable for the arrival time of every customer. This will lead to the problem of state explosion. For such policies, we can use simulations and compare their performance with the reservation policy.

For the type of reservation policy we considered, the case with four levels of call required seven variables. This was computationally tractable only for relatively small values of the total system capacity
\ell
. With efficient state space ordering and the use of a supercomputer, we have demonstrated that it works for
\ell=15
and we expect that with some additional time, if needed, the value of
\ell
can be taken up to
20
. However, higher values might lead to memory issues.

For a small call centre with a low arrival rate, the proportion of callers blocked with
\ell=10
is very low (less than
0.3\%
for the parameter values listed in Table
1
), hence the method is usable. For cases with less than four levels of calls (or policies with less than seven state space variables), this method can be used for larger call centres.

We believe that the method can be extended to five levels of calls, but since with each level of call, we need to add two variables and state space grows exponentially with each variable, it would be hard to extend the method any more than that.

In the next section, we use simulation to check the results in Section
4
. We also compare the performance of our suggested reservation policy with some other policies that are not so analytically tractable.

7
Conclusion

In this paper, we have calculated some important transient performance measures for small call centres. We developed a general method that can be used to calculate many desirable quantities for various queueing models such as the expected number of call abandonments and expected total waiting time of the customers. In a forthcoming paper, we will describe how a modification of this method can be used to find the waiting time distribution.

From the analysis above, we discovered that out of all three policies, the reservation policy with the optimal reservation vector minimises the expected number of call abandonments.

We also observed that having an FCFS policy within a level of calls can be more beneficial than having an FCFS policy across all levels and that waiting for some time before assigning calls to the next level agent may not be a good policy in most cases.

There are many examples where the policy of zero reservation is not optimal and for those cases, the method above can be employed to find the optimal reservation vector.
