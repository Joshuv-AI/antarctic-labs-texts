High-dimensional Location Estimation via Norm Concentration for Subgamma Vectors
Shivam Gupta, Jasper C.H. Lee, Eric Price

Abstract
In location estimation, we are given n n samples from a known distribution f f shifted by an unknown translation λ \lambda , and want to estimate λ \lambda as precisely as possible. Asymptotically, the maximum likelihood estimate achieves the Cramér-Rao bound of error N ⁡ ( 0 , 1 n ​ ℐ ) N(0,\frac{1}{n\mathcal{I}}) , where ℐ \mathcal{I} is the Fisher information of f f . However, the n n required for convergence depends on f f , and may be arbitrarily large. We build on the theory using smoothed estimators to bound the error for finite n n in terms of ℐ r \mathcal{I}_{r} , the Fisher information of the r r -smoothed distribution. As n → ∞ n\to\infty , r → 0 r\to 0 at an explicit rate and this converges to the Cramér-Rao bound. We (1) improve the prior work for 1-dimensional f f to converge for constant failure probability in addition to high probability, and (2) extend the theory to high-dimensional distributions. In the process, we prove a new bound on the norm of a high-dimensional random variable whose 1-dimensional projections are subgamma, which may be of independent interest.



1
Introduction

Location estimation—a variant of mean estimation—is a fundamental problem in parametric statistics.
Suppose there is a translation-invariant model
f^{\lambda}(x)=f(x-\lambda)
for some known distribution
f
over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
The statistician receives
n
i.i.d. samples from
f^{\lambda}
for some arbitrarily chosen
true parameter

\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and the goal is to estimate
\lambda
with high accuracy, succeeding with high probability over the samples.

In contrast to general mean estimation, which aims to estimate the mean under minimal assumptions on the distribution, here we know the exact shape of the distribution up to translation. Such additional information allows us to estimate
\lambda
to higher accuracy.

The classic “textbook” theory for location estimation, and indeed for parametric estimation in general, recommends using the
Maximum Likelihood Estimate
(MLE).
The MLE enjoys asymptotic normality: if we fix a distribution
f
and take the number of samples
n
to infinity, the distribution of the MLE converges to the multivariate Gaussian
\mathcal{N}(\lambda,\frac{1}{n}\mathcal{I}^{-1})
, where
\mathcal{I}
is the
Fisher information
matrix, defined by

\mathcal{I}=\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim f}\left[\left(\nabla\log f(x)\right)\left(\nabla\log f(x)\right)^{\top}\right]

As a basic property, if we denote the covariance matrix of
f
by
\Sigma
, then we always have
\mathcal{I}^{-1}\preceq\Sigma
, implying that the asymptotic performance of the MLE is always at least as good as the sample mean, whose performance is controlled by the covariance
\Sigma
.
Furthermore, the Crámer-Rao bound states that no unbiased location estimator can have covariance smaller than
\frac{1}{n}\mathcal{I}^{-1}
, and so the MLE has the best asymptotic performance of any unbiased estimator.

Even though the textbook theory is satisfying in that the Fisher information essentially captures the information-theoretic limits of location estimation, its predictions may be misleading in practice.
Specifically, this is due to the asymptotic nature of the MLE performance guarantee: we need to take the number of samples
n
to infinity in order to achieve subgaussian estimation error.
The asymptotic result may have arbitrarily bad dependence on
n
in terms of the model
f
. While bounds exist in terms of regularity properties of
f

(
Miao 2010
;
Spokoiny 2011
;
Pinelis 2017
)
, these bounds are infinite for simple examples like the Laplace distribution. The research goal, therefore, is to establish a
finite-sample
theory of location estimation, which bounds the estimation error explicitly as a function of
n
, applies to every
f
, and ideally attains even optimal constants in the estimation error.

Recent work by Gupta et al.
(
Gupta et al. 2022
)
addressed this question in the special case of 1 dimension.
They showed that, while the MLE can have bad finite-sample performance, it is possible to improve the behavior by a simple adaptation: add Gaussian noise of some appropriately chosen radius
r
, where
r
decreases with the number of samples, to both the samples and model before performing MLE.
Accordingly, the theoretical guarantees for the
smoothed
MLE replaces the Fisher information of
f
with the Fisher information of the smoothed distribution
f_{r}
, also called the smoothed Fisher information
\mathcal{I}_{r}
.
Smoothed MLE achieves finite-sample subgaussian error bounds analogous to a Gaussian with variance
(1+o(1))\mathcal{I}_{r}^{-1}
, where the
o(1)
term can be explicitly calculated and is
independent
of
f
.

Characterization by smoothed Fisher information.

Our results will follow the approach of Gupta et al.
(
Gupta et al. 2022
)
and show finite sample bounds in terms of the smoothed Fisher information.
Here, focusing on the 1-dimensional case, we briefly discuss why Fisher information is inadequate and why smoothed Fisher information is a suitable substitute.

Gaussian+Sawtooth Distribution

Figure 2:
Constant probability error lower bound for Gaussian+Sawtooth

Consider the “Gaussian+Sawtooth” distribution shown in Figure
1
, which is a sawtooth of tooth width
w
and slope
\pm\Delta
added to the central section of the standard Gaussian density.
As
w\to 0
, the density converges to the standard Gaussian, yet the Fisher information grows to
\Theta(\Delta^{2})
as
\Delta\to\infty
.
The asymptotic theory thus predicts an error of
O(1/(\Delta\sqrt{n}))
with constant probability.

However, Gupta et al.
(
Gupta et al. 2022
)
showed that for
n\ll 1/w^{2}
, the constant probability error for
every
algorithm is in fact at least
\Omega(1/\sqrt{n})
, as if the distribution were just a standard Gaussian.
Intuitively, we need to align the model to within a single sawtooth width of
w
in order to leverage the sawtooth structure for high accuracy estimation.
For a standard Gaussian,
\Omega(1/w^{2})
samples are needed for error less than
w
.
Figure
2
shows a plot of the constant probability error lower bound for the Gaussian+Sawtooth model, with the error scaled by
\sqrt{n}
for normalization.

Since the sample threshold depends on
w
, this example shows that there is no algorithm that converges to the asymptotic error in a distribution-independent way.
Concretely, no algorithm can be within a
1+o(1)
factor of the
\mathcal{N}(0,1/(n\mathcal{I}))
error for a distribution-independent
o(1)
term.
We therefore need an alternative quantity to replace
\mathcal{I}
for finite-sample error bounds, which can capture the phase transition in Figure
2
.

Smoothed Fisher information exhibits this phase transition behavior.
Smoothing by radius
r\gg w
blurs out the sawtooth structure—
\mathcal{I}_{r}
is small and close to the standard Gaussian Fisher information of 1.
On the other hand, smoothing by radius
r\ll w
preserves the sawtooth and keeps
\mathcal{I}_{r}
close to
\mathcal{I}=\Theta(\Delta^{2})
.
Both Gupta et al.
(
Gupta et al. 2022
)
and we leverage this behavior to show finite sample bounds analogous to
(1+o(1))\mathcal{N}(0,1/(n\mathcal{I}_{r}))
, with a
o(1)
term that is distribution-independent.

We need to choose the smoothing parameter
carefully
, as the smoothed Fisher information can depend delicately on
r
.
Intuitively, we expect
r\to 0
as
n\to\infty
; however, this is not true of Gupta et al.’s results.
Their choice of smoothing vanishes only in the high-probability regime, i.e. when both
n\to\infty
and
\delta\to 0
for failure probability
\delta
.
Thus, for small constant
\delta
, their results can be very sub-optimal.
One of our new results removes the spurious dependence of
r
on
\delta
.

Our results.

In this paper, we improve and extend
the result of Gupta et al.
(
Gupta et al. 2022
)
in two ways.
First, we show that a
variant of the algorithm has a simpler and better analysis in one
dimension. This better analysis supports smaller smoothing radius

r
, and hence higher Fisher information
\mathcal{I}_{r}
:

Theorem 1.1

(1-d Smoothed MLE)
.

Given a model
f
, let the
r
-smoothed Fisher information of a distribution
f
be
\mathcal{I}_{r}
, and let
\mathrm{IQR}
be the interquartile range of
f
.
Fix the failure probability be
\delta\leq 0.5
, and assume that
n\geq c\cdot\log\frac{2}{\delta}
for some sufficiently large constant
c
.

Choose
r^{*}=\Omega((\frac{\log\frac{2}{\delta}}{n})^{1/8})\mathrm{IQR}
.
Then, with probability at least
1-\delta
, the output
\hat{\lambda}
of Algorithm
2
satisfies

|\hat{\lambda}-\lambda|\leq\left(1+O\left(\frac{\log\frac{2}{\delta}}{n}\right)^{\frac{1}{10}}\right)\sqrt{\frac{2\log\frac{2}{\delta}}{n\mathcal{I}_{r^{*}}}}

The main difference between this result and
(
Gupta et al. 2022
)
is the
dependence on
\delta
: the previous result needed

\delta\to 0
for
r
to decay to 0 and for the leading constant to decay to 1. In ours, both decay polynomially in
n
for constant
\delta
.

Consider how this result behaves on the Gaussian+Sawtooth example above (Figure
1
), for constant
\delta
. For small
n
, we will choose
r^{*}=\frac{1}{\operatorname*{\mathrm{poly}}(n)}>w
and get error within
1+\frac{1}{\operatorname*{\mathrm{poly}}(n)}
of the regular Gaussian tail; for large
n
,
r^{*}\ll w
and the error is within
1+\frac{1}{\operatorname*{\mathrm{poly}}(n)}
of the asymptotically optimal
\mathcal{N}(0,1/(n\mathcal{I}_{r}))
. Thus we get the same qualitative transition behavior as Figure
2
, albeit at a different transition point (
\frac{1}{w^{8}}
rather than
\frac{1}{w^{2}}
). The prior work
(
Gupta et al. 2022
)
additionally required vanishing
\delta
, roughly
\delta<2^{-\operatorname*{\mathrm{poly}}(n)}
, to observe this behavior.

Second, our simpler approach lets us generalize the result to high
dimensions. We show an analogous result to the one-dimensional
result. In an ideal world, since the (unsmoothed) MLE satisfies

(\widehat{\lambda}-\lambda)\to\mathcal{N}(0,\frac{1}{n}\mathcal{I}^{-1})
asymptotically, we would aim for the Gaussian tail error (
Boucheron et al. 2013
, Example 5.7)

\displaystyle\|\widehat{\lambda}-\lambda\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{\frac{\Tr(\mathcal{I}^{-1})}{n}}+\sqrt{2\|\mathcal{I}^{-1}\|\frac{\log\frac{1}{\delta}}{n}}

(1)

with probability
1-\delta
.
We show that this
almost
holds.
Let
d_{\text{eff}}(A)=\frac{\Tr(A)}{\|A\|}
denote the effective dimension of a positive semidefinite matrix
A
. If we smooth by a spherical Gaussian
R=r^{2}I_{d}
for some
r^{2}\leq\|\Sigma\|
, then for a sufficiently large
n
as a function of
\|\Sigma\|/r^{2},\log\frac{1}{\delta}
,
d_{\text{eff}}(\Sigma)
, and
d_{\text{eff}}(\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1})
, our error is close
to (
1
) replacing
\mathcal{I}
with the smoothed Fisher
information
\mathcal{I}_{R}
.

Theorem 1.2

(High-dimensional MLE, Informal; see Theorem
B.16
)
.

Let
f
have covariance matrix
\Sigma
. For any
r^{2}\leq\|\Sigma\|
, let
R=r^{2}I_{d}
and
\mathcal{I}_{R}
be the
R
-smoothed Fisher information of the distribution.
For any constant
0<\eta<1
,

\|\widehat{\lambda}-\lambda\|_{2}\leq(1+\eta)\sqrt{\frac{\Tr(\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1})}{n}}+5\sqrt{\frac{\|\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}\|\log\frac{4}{\delta}}{n}}

with probability
1-\delta
, for

n>O_{\eta}\left(\left(\frac{\|\Sigma\|}{r^{2}}\right)^{2}\left(\log\frac{2}{\delta}+d_{\text{eff}}(\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1})+\frac{d_{\text{eff}}(\Sigma)^{2}}{d_{\text{eff}}(\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1})}\right)\right)

When

d_{\text{eff}}(\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1})\gg\log\frac{1}{\delta}
, the bound is
(1+\eta+o(1))\sqrt{\Tr(\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1})}
.
This is very close to the Cramer-Rao bound for the expected error of
\sqrt{\Tr(\mathcal{I}^{-1})}
for unbiased estimators (
Bickel & Doksum 2015
, Theorem 3.4.3).

The formal version of this theorem, Theorem
B.16
, also gives bounds for general distances
\|\widehat{\lambda}-\lambda\|_{M}
induced by symmetric PSD matrices
M
; the exact bound, and the
n
required for convergence, depend on
M
.

One key piece of our proof, which may be of independent interest, is a
concentration bound for the norm of a high-dimensional vector
x
with
subgamma marginals in every direction.
If a vector is Gaussian in every direction, it is a high-dimensional Gaussian and satisfies the tail bound (
1
)
(replacing
\mathcal{I}^{-1}
by the covariance matrix
\Sigma
).
It was shown in
(
Hsu et al. 2012
)
that the same bound applies even if the marginals are merely
subgaussian
with parameter
\Sigma
.
We extend this to get a bound for
subgamma
marginals:

Theorem 1.3

(Norm concentration for subgamma random vectors; see Theorem
5.1
)
.

Let
x
be a mean-zero random vector in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
that is

(\Sigma,C)
-subgamma, i.e., it satisfies that for any vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}[e^{\lambda\langle x,v\rangle}]\leq e^{\lambda^{2}v^{T}\Sigma v/2}

for
|\lambda|\leq\frac{1}{\|Cv\|}
. Then with probability
1-\delta
,

\displaystyle\|x\|

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\Tr(\Sigma)}+4\sqrt{\|\Sigma\|\log\frac{2}{\delta}}+16\|C\|\log\frac{2}{\delta}

\displaystyle+\min\left(4\|C\|_{F}\sqrt{\log\frac{2}{\delta}},8\frac{\|C\|_{F}^{2}}{\sqrt{\Tr(\Sigma)}}\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right)

The first, trace term is the expected norm and the next two
terms are (up to constants) the tight bound from 1-dimensional subgamma concentration.
When
x
is an average of
n
samples, both
\Sigma
and
C
drop by a factor
n
; thus, the terms involving
C
decay at a rate of
1/n
, versus the terms involving only
\Sigma
, which decay at a rate of
1/\sqrt{n}
.
As
n\to\infty
, the terms involving
C
disappear compared with the Gaussian terms involving
\Sigma
.

To better understand the last term, consider
x
to be the average of
n
samples
X_{i}
drawn from the spherical case (
\Sigma=\sigma^{2}I,C=cI
).
We also focus on the high-dimensional regime where
d\geq(2/\eta^{2})\log(1/\delta)
for some small
\eta
, where the target error bound of (
1
) becomes
(1+\eta)\sqrt{\mathrm{tr}(\Sigma)/n}
, that is, within a
(1+\eta)
factor of the expected
\ell_{2}
norm error.
In the subgamma setting, the bound of Theorem
1.3
implies an error of
(1+O(\eta))\sqrt{\Tr(\Sigma)/n}
whenever
n\gtrsim(c/\sigma)^{2}d
, where the threshold for
n
is due to comparing the last “min” term in the bound with the
\sqrt{\|\Sigma\|\log\frac{2}{\delta}}
term.

Under the stronger assumption that the random vectors have distance at most
c
from their expectation, one can compare our tail bound with Talagrand’s/Bousquet’s suprema concentration inequality (
Boucheron et al. 2013
, Theorem 12.5).
Focusing again on the high-dimensional, spherical regime where
d\geq(2/\eta^{2})\log(1/\delta)
and
\Sigma=\sigma^{2}I,C=cI
, Bousquet’s inequality implies an almost-identical
\ell_{2}
error of
(1+O(\eta))\sqrt{\Tr(\Sigma)/n}
whenever
n\gtrsim(c/\sigma)^{2}d
, albeit with smaller hidden constant.
Given that the
n
threshold for our bound is due to our last “min” term, it is likely that such a term is qualitatively necessary, and that our last term is not too large at least in the relevant regimes we consider in this paper.

Notation

We denote the known distribution by
f
.
In 1 dimension,
f_{r}
is the
r
-smoothed distribution
f\ast\mathcal{N}(0,r^{2})
, with smoothed Fisher information
\mathcal{I}_{r}
.
In high dimensions,
f_{R}
is the
R
-smoothed distribution
f\ast\mathcal{N}(0,R)
with smoothed Fisher information
\mathcal{I}_{R}
—note the quadratic difference between
r
and
R
, analogous to the usual conventions for the (co)variance of 1-dimensional vs high-dimensional Gaussians.

The true parameter is denoted by
\lambda
.
Both our 1-dimensional and high-dimensional algorithms first gets an initial estimate
\lambda_{1}
, before refining it into the final estimate
\hat{\lambda}
.

Unless otherwise specified, for a given vector
x
,
\|x\|
denotes the
\ell_{2}
norm, and similarly
\|A\|
is the operator norm of a square matrix
A
.
Given a square positive semidefinite matrix
A
, we define its
effective dimension
to be
d_{\mathrm{eff}}(A)=\mathrm{tr}(A)/\|A\|
.
The effective dimension of a matrix
A
is
d
when it is spherical, but decays if one or more of its eigenvalues deviate from the maximum eigenvalue.

2
Related work

For an in-depth textbook treatment of the asymptotic theory of location estimation and parametric estimation in general, see
(
van der Vaart 2000
)
.
There have also been finite-sample analysis of the MLE (
(
Spokoiny 2011
)
in high dimensions,
(
Pinelis 2017
;
Miao 2010
)
in 1 dimension), but they require strong regularity conditions in addition to losing (at least) multiplicative constants in the estimation error bounds.
Most related to this paper is the prior work of Gupta et al.
(
Gupta et al. 2022
)
, which introduced smoothed MLE in the context of location estimation in 1 dimension, as well as formally analyzed its finite sample performance in terms of the smoothed Fisher information for large
n
and small
\delta
.

There has been a flurry of work in recent years on the closely related problem of mean estimation, under the minimal assumption of finite (co)variance. The bounds then depend on this variance, rather than the Fisher information.
In 1 dimension, the seminal paper of Catoni
(
Catoni 2012
)
initiated the search for a subgaussian mean estimator with estimation error tight to within a
1+o(1)
factor; improvements by Devroye et al.
(
Devroye et al. 2016
)
and Lee and Valiant
(
Lee & Valiant 2022a
)
have given a 1-dimensional mean estimator that works for all distributions with finite (but unknown) variance, with accuracy that is optimal to within a
1+o(1)
factor.
Crucially, the
o(1)
term is independent of the underlying distribution.

It remains an open problem to find a subgaussian mean estimator with tight constants under bounded covariance in high dimensions. A line of work
(
Lugosi & Mendelson 2017
;
Hopkins 2018
;
Cherapanamjeri et al. 2019
)
has shown how to achieve the subgaussian rate, ignoring constants, in polynomial time. More recently, Lee and Valiant
(
Lee & Valiant 2022b
)
has achieved linear time and a sharp constant, but requires the effective dimension of the distribution to be much larger than
\log^{2}\frac{1}{\delta}
.

Our other contribution is our novel norm concentration bound for subgamma random vectors.
The norm concentration for Gaussian vectors has long been understood, see for example the textbook (
Boucheron et al. 2013
, Example 5.7).
Hsu et al.
(
Hsu et al. 2012
)
generalized this bound to the case of direction-by-direction subgaussian vectors.
Norm concentration can also be viewed as the supremum of an empirical process.
Bousquet’s version
(
Bousquet 2002
;
Bousquet 2003
)
of Talagrand’s suprema concentration inequality implies a norm concentration bound for random vectors bounded within an
\ell_{2}
ball of their expectation.
Our bound generalizes this case of Bousquet’s inequality from bounded vectors to all subgamma vectors. As discussed after Theorem
1.3
, the results are quite similar for spherical
\Sigma
and
C
.

3
1-dimensional location estimation

We discuss our 1-dimensional location estimation algorithm and its analysis at a high level in this section.
See Appendix
A
for the complete analysis.

Algorithm
1
below is a
local
algorithm in the sense that it assumes we have an initial estimate
\lambda_{1}
that is within some distance
\epsilon
of
\lambda
, with the goal of refining the estimate to high accuracy.

Algorithm 1

Local smoothed MLE for one dimension

Input Parameters:

•

Description of
f
, smoothing parameter
r
, samples
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\overset{i.i.d.}{\sim}f^{\lambda}
and initial estimate
\lambda_{1}
of
\lambda

1.

Let
s(\hat{\lambda})
be the score function of
f_{r}
, the
r
-smoothed version of
f
.

2.

For each sample
x_{i}
, compute a perturbed sample
x^{\prime}_{i}=x_{i}+\mathcal{N}(0,r^{2})
where all the Gaussian noise are drawn independently across all the samples.

3.

Compute the empirical score at
\lambda_{1}
, namely
\hat{s}(\lambda_{1})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}s(x^{\prime}_{i}-\lambda_{1})
.

4.

Return
\hat{\lambda}=\lambda_{1}-(\hat{s}(\lambda_{1})/\mathcal{I}_{r})
.

Let
\mathcal{I}_{r}
be the Fisher information of
f_{r}
, the
r
-smoothed
version of
f
.
Basic facts about the score
s(x)
are:

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim f_{r}}[s(x)]

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{r}

\displaystyle=\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim f_{r}}[-s^{\prime}(x)]=\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim f_{r}}[s(x)^{2}].

First, Algorithm
1
adds
N(0,r^{2})
perturbation independently to each
x_{i}
to get
x^{\prime}_{i}
, which are drawn as
(y_{1}+\lambda,y_{2}+\lambda,\dotsc,y_{n}+\lambda)
for
y_{i}\sim f_{r}
.
It then computes

\widehat{s}(\lambda_{1}):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}s(x^{\prime}_{i}-\lambda_{1})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}s(y_{i}-\epsilon)

which is, in expectation,

\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim f_{r}}[s(x-\epsilon)]\approx\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim f_{r}}[s(x)-\epsilon s^{\prime}(x)]=\epsilon\mathcal{I}_{r}.

Thus we expect
\widehat{\lambda}=\lambda_{1}-\widehat{s}(\lambda_{1})/\mathcal{I}_{r}\approx\lambda
.

There are two sources of error in this calculation: (I) the Taylor
approximation to
s(x-\epsilon)
, and (II) the difference between the
empirical and true expectations of
s(x-\epsilon)
. When
\epsilon=0
, the Taylor error is
0
and the empirical estimator has variance

\frac{\operatorname*{\mathrm{Var}}(s(x))}{n}=\frac{\mathcal{I}_{r}}{n}.

Thus, when
\lambda_{1}=\lambda
,
\widehat{\lambda}
would be an unbiased estimator of
\lambda
with variance
\frac{1}{n\mathcal{I}_{r}}
: exactly the Cramér-Rao bound.
Moreover, one can show that
s(x)
is subgamma with variance proxy
\mathcal{I}_{r}
and tail parameter
1/r
, giving tails on
\widehat{\lambda}-\lambda
matching the
\frac{1}{n\mathcal{I}_{r}}
-variance Gaussian (up to some point depending on
r
).
All we need to show, then, is that shifting by
\epsilon
introduces little excess error in (I) and (II); intuitively, this happens for
|\epsilon|\ll r
because
f_{r}
has been smoothed by radius
r
.

In fact,
(
Gupta et al. 2022
)

already
bounded both errors: for (I), their Lemma C.2 shows that

\displaystyle\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim f_{r}}[s(x-\epsilon)]=\mathcal{I}_{r}\epsilon\pm O(\sqrt{\mathcal{I}_{r}}\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{r^{2}})

(2)

for all
|\epsilon|\leq r/2
, and for (II), their Corollary 3.3 and Lemma C.3 together imply that a subgamma concentration of

\displaystyle|\hat{s}(\lambda_{1})-\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim f_{r}}[s(x-\epsilon)]|\lesssim

\displaystyle\qquad(1+o(1))\sqrt{\frac{\mathcal{I}_{r}\log\frac{2}{\delta}}{n}}+\frac{\log\frac{2}{\delta}}{nr}

(3)

when
r\gg|\epsilon|
.

Therefore, for sufficiently large
r
,
the total error in
\widehat{s}(\lambda_{1})
is dominated by the leading
\sqrt{\frac{\mathcal{I}_{r}\log\frac{2}{\delta}}{n}}
term, giving a result within
1+o(1)
of optimal.

Getting an initial estimate.

We estimate
\lambda
by the empirical
\alpha
-quantile of a small
\kappa
fraction of the samples, for some
\alpha
; one can show that this has error at most
O(\mathrm{IQR}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{\log\frac{1}{\delta}}{\kappa n}})
with
1-\delta
probability, where
\mathrm{IQR}
denotes the interquartile range.
This strategy is essentially identical to
(
Gupta et al. 2022
)
, except we use fresh samples for the two stages while they reuse samples.

Algorithm 2

Global smoothed MLE for one dimension

Input Parameters:

•

Failure probability
\delta
, description of
f
,
n
i.i.d. samples drawn from
f^{\lambda}
for some unknown
\lambda

1.

Let
q
be
\sqrt{2}(\log\frac{2}{\delta}/n)^{2/5}
.

2.

Compute an
\alpha\in[q,1-q]
to minimize the width of interval defined by the
\alpha\pm q
quantiles of

.

3.

Take the sample
\alpha
-quantile of the first
(\log\frac{1}{\delta}/n)^{1/10}
fraction of the
n
samples.

4.

Let
r^{*}=\Omega((\frac{\log\frac{1}{\delta}}{n})^{1/8})\mathrm{IQR}
.

5.

Run Algorithm
1
on the rest of the samples, using initial estimate
\lambda_{1}=x_{\alpha}
and

-smoothing, and return the final estimate
\hat{\lambda}
.

Combining the above strategies and balancing the parameters gives Algorithm
2
as our final algorithm.

that the algorithm satisfies our 1-dimensional result, Theorem
1.1
.

Comparison to prior work.

All the properties of the score function we need for this 1-dimensional result were shown in
(
Gupta et al. 2022
)
, but that paper uses a different algorithm for which they could only prove a worse result. The
(
Gupta et al. 2022
)
algorithm looks for a root of
\widehat{s}
, while we essentially perform one step of Newton’s method to approximate the root. General root finding requires
uniform
convergence of
\widehat{s}
, which
(
Gupta et al. 2022
)
could not prove without additional loss factors. By using one step, and (a small number of) fresh samples for the initial estimate, our algorithm only needs pointwise convergence.

Norm concentration for subgamma vectors

Theorem 5.1

(Norm concentration for subgamma vectors)
.

Let
x
be a mean-zero random vector in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
that is
(\Sigma,C)
-subgamma, i.e., for all
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
v^{T}x\in\Gamma(v^{T}\Sigma v,\|Cv\|)
. In other words, it satisfies that for any vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}[e^{\lambda\langle x,v\rangle}]\leq e^{\lambda^{2}v^{T}\Sigma v/2}

for
|\lambda|\leq\frac{1}{\|Cv\|}
. Let
\gamma>0
. Then,

\operatorname*{\mathbb{P}}\left[\|x\|\geq\sqrt{\Tr(\Sigma)}+t\right]\leq 2e^{-\frac{1}{16}\min(\frac{t^{2}}{\|\Sigma\|},\frac{t}{\|C\|},\frac{2t\sqrt{\Tr(\Sigma)}+t^{2}}{\|C\|_{F}^{2}})}.

Thus, with probability
1-\delta
,

\displaystyle\|x\|

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\Tr(\Sigma)}+4\sqrt{\|\Sigma\|\log\frac{2}{\delta}}+16\|C\|\log\frac{2}{\delta}

\displaystyle\qquad+\min\left(4\|C\|_{F}\sqrt{\log\frac{2}{\delta}},8\frac{\|C\|_{F}^{2}}{\sqrt{\Tr(\Sigma)}}\log\frac{2}{\delta}\right)

The proof idea, similar to
(
Hsu et al. 2012
)
for the subgaussian case, is as follows. Define
v\sim N(0,I)
.
We relate
\operatorname*{\mathbb{P}}[\|x\|>t]
to the MGF
\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x}[e^{\lambda^{2}\|x\|^{2}}]
, which equals
\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x,v}[e^{\lambda\langle x,v\rangle}]
. If we interchange the order of expectation, as long as
\|Cv\|\leq 1/|\lambda|
, this is at most
\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{v}[e^{\lambda^{2}v^{T}\Sigma v}]
. Since
v
is Gaussian, we can compute the last MGF precisely.

To handle the subgamma setting, we need a way to control
\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x,v}[e^{\lambda\langle x,v\rangle}]
over those
v
with
\|Cv\|>1/|\lambda|
.
We do so by showing that (I) WLOG
\|x\|
is never strictly larger than the bound we want to show, and (II) then the contribution to the expectation from such cases is small.

Proof.

Define
\gamma=\frac{t}{\sqrt{\Tr(\Sigma)}}
, so we
want to bound
\operatorname*{\mathbb{P}}[\|x\|\geq(1+\gamma)\sqrt{\Tr(\Sigma)}].

We start by showing that WLOG
\|x\|
never exceeds this threshold.

Introducing a bounded norm assumption.

We first show
that, without loss of generality, we can assume

\|x\|\leq(1+\gamma)\sqrt{\Tr(\Sigma)}
always. Let

s\in\{\pm 1\}
be distributed uniformly independent of
x
, and
define

y=s\cdot x\cdot\min\left(1,\frac{(1+\gamma)\sqrt{\Tr(\Sigma)}}{\|x\|}\right).

to clip
x
’s norm and symmetrize. For any
v
and
x
,

\displaystyle\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{s}[e^{\lambda\langle y,v\rangle}]

\displaystyle=\cosh\left(\lambda\langle x,v\rangle\cdot\min\left(1,\frac{(1+\gamma)\sqrt{\Tr(\Sigma)}}{\|x\|}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\cosh(\lambda\langle x,v\rangle)

Now, since
x
is
(\Sigma,C)
-subgamma,

\displaystyle\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x}[\cosh(\lambda\langle x,v\rangle)]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x}[e^{\lambda\langle x,v\rangle}]+\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x}[e^{\lambda\langle x,-v\rangle}]\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(e^{\lambda^{2}v^{T}\Sigma v/2}+e^{\lambda^{2}(-v)^{T}\Sigma(-v)/2}\right)

\displaystyle=e^{\lambda^{2}v^{T}\Sigma v/2}

and so

\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{y}[e^{\lambda\langle y,v\rangle}]\leq e^{\lambda^{2}v^{T}\Sigma v/2}.

Thus
y
is also
(\Sigma,C)
-subgamma. The target quantity in our
theorem is the same for
y
as for
x
:

\operatorname*{\mathbb{P}}[\|x\|\geq(1+\gamma)\sqrt{\Tr(\Sigma)}]=\operatorname*{\mathbb{P}}[\|y\|\geq(1+\gamma)\sqrt{\Tr(\Sigma)}]
. Since

\|y\|\leq(1+\gamma)\sqrt{\Tr(\Sigma)}
always, by considering

y
instead of
x
, we can WLOG assume that

\|x\|\leq(1+\gamma)\sqrt{\Tr(\Sigma)}
in our theorem proof.

Relating probability to
\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x,v}[e^{\lambda\langle x,v\rangle}]
.

Define

\alpha:=\operatorname*{\mathbb{P}}\left[\|x\|\geq(1+\gamma)\sqrt{\Tr(\Sigma)}\right]

so that by Markov’s inequality applied to
e^{\lambda^{2}\|x\|^{2}/2}
,

\alpha\leq\frac{\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}[e^{\lambda^{2}\|x\|^{2}/2}]}{e^{\lambda^{2}(1+\gamma)^{2}\Tr(\Sigma)/2}}

for any
\lambda
.
Now, let
v\sim N(0,I_{d})
. For any
x
,

\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{v}[\epsilon^{\lambda\langle x,v\rangle}]=e^{\lambda^{2}\|x\|^{2}/2}

so

\alpha\leq\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x,v}[e^{\lambda\langle x,v\rangle}]e^{-\lambda^{2}(1+\gamma)^{2}\Tr(\Sigma)/2}.

(4)

Upper bounding
\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x,v}[e^{\lambda\langle x,v\rangle}]
.

We will bound the RHS above by making the inner expectation over

x
. Since
x
is
(\Sigma,C)
-subgamma, for every
v
,

\displaystyle\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x}[e^{\lambda\langle x,v\rangle}]\leq e^{\lambda^{2}v^{T}\Sigma v/2}\qquad\forall|\lambda|\leq\frac{1}{\|Cv\|},

Therefore

\displaystyle\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x,v}[e^{\lambda\langle x,v\rangle}]

\displaystyle=\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x,v}[e^{\lambda\langle x,v\rangle}1_{\|Cv\|\leq 1/|\lambda|}+e^{\lambda\langle x,v\rangle}1_{\|Cv\|>1/|\lambda|}]

\displaystyle\hskip-20.00003pt\leq\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{v}[e^{\lambda^{2}v^{T}\Sigma v/2}1_{\|Cv\|\leq 1/|\lambda|}]+\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x,v}[e^{\lambda\langle x,v\rangle}1_{\|Cv\|>1/|\lambda|}]

\displaystyle\hskip-20.00003pt\leq\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{v}[e^{\lambda^{2}v^{T}\Sigma v/2}]+\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x}[\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{v}[e^{\lambda\langle x,v\rangle}1_{\|Cv\|>1/|\lambda|}]]

(5)

We start with the first term. Let the eigenvalues of
\Sigma
be

\sigma_{1}^{2}\geq\sigma_{2}^{2}\geq\dots\geq\sigma_{d}^{2}
. Then,

v^{T}\Sigma v/2
is a generalized chi-squared distribution,
distributed as
\sum_{i}u_{i}^{2}
for independent Gaussian variables

u_{i}\sim N(0,\sigma_{i}^{2}/2)
. It is easy to check that

u^{2}
for
u\sim N(0,1)
is
(4,4)
-subgamma, i.e.,

\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}[e^{\lambda(u^{2}-\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}[u^{2}])}]=\frac{e^{-\lambda}}{\sqrt{1-2\lambda}}\leq e^{2\lambda^{2}}\qquad\forall|\lambda|\leq\frac{1}{4}.

Therefore
\sum u_{i}^{2}
is
(\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}^{4},2\max\sigma_{i}^{2})
=

(\|\Sigma\|_{F}^{2},2\|\Sigma\|)
-subgamma. Since

\|\Sigma\|_{F}^{2}\leq\|\Sigma\|\Tr(\Sigma)
,
v^{T}\Sigma v
is
also
(\|\Sigma\|\Tr(\Sigma),2\|\Sigma\|)
-subgamma.

Including the mean term as well (
\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}[v^{T}\Sigma v/2]=\Tr(\Sigma)/2
), we have

\displaystyle\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{v}[e^{\lambda^{2}v^{T}\Sigma v/2}]\leq e^{\lambda^{2}\Tr(\Sigma)/2}\cdot e^{\lambda^{4}\Tr(\Sigma)\|\Sigma\|/2}\quad\forall\lambda^{2}\leq\frac{1}{2\|\Sigma\|}.

(6)

We now bound the second term in (
5
) for each
x
. Since

v
is i.i.d. gaussian,
\|Cv\|\leq\|C\|_{F}+\|C\|\sqrt{2\log\frac{1}{\delta}}
with probability
1-\delta
(see Equation
1
). Therefore, for all

|\lambda|<\frac{1}{2\|C\|_{F}}
,

\operatorname*{\mathbb{P}}[\|Cv\|>1/|\lambda|]\leq e^{-\frac{(1/|\lambda|-\|C\|_{F})^{2}}{2\|C\|^{2}}}\leq e^{-\frac{1}{8\lambda^{2}\|C\|^{2}}}

and so by Cauchy-Schwarz, and our bound on
\|x\|
,

\displaystyle\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{v}[e^{\lambda\langle x,v\rangle}1_{\|Cv\|>1/|\lambda|}]

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{v}[e^{2\lambda\langle x,v\rangle}]\operatorname*{\mathbb{P}}[\|Cv\|>1/|\lambda|]}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{e^{2\lambda^{2}\|x\|^{2}}e^{-\frac{1}{8\lambda^{2}\|C\|^{2}}}}

\displaystyle=e^{\lambda^{2}(1+\gamma)^{2}\Tr(\Sigma)-\frac{1}{16\lambda^{2}\|C\|^{2}}}.

Therefore, as long as

\lambda^{2}\leq\min(\frac{1}{4(1+\gamma)\sqrt{\Tr(\Sigma)}\|C\|},\frac{1}{4\|C\|_{F}^{2}})
,

\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{v}[e^{\lambda\|x\|v_{1}}1_{\|Cv\|>1/|\lambda|}]\leq 1.

Combining with (
6
) (which is a bound always larger than
1
) and (
5
),

\displaystyle\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x,v}[e^{\lambda\langle x,v\rangle}]\leq 2e^{\lambda^{2}\Tr(\Sigma)/2}\cdot e^{\lambda^{4}\Tr(\Sigma)\|\Sigma\|/2}

\displaystyle\qquad\forall\lambda^{2}\leq\min(\frac{1}{2\|\Sigma\|},\frac{1}{4(1+\gamma)\sqrt{\Tr(\Sigma)}\|C\|},\frac{1}{4\|C\|_{F}^{2}})

and with (
4
),

\displaystyle\alpha\leq 2e^{\frac{1}{2}\lambda^{2}\Tr(\Sigma)(\lambda^{2}\|\Sigma\|-2\gamma-\gamma^{2})}

\displaystyle\forall\lambda^{2}\leq\min(\frac{1}{2\|\Sigma\|},\frac{1}{4(1+\gamma)\sqrt{\Tr(\Sigma)}\|C\|},\frac{1}{4\|C\|_{F}^{2}})

Final bound.

By also restricting
\lambda^{2}
to be at most
\frac{2\gamma+\gamma^{2}}{2\|\Sigma\|}
, we get:

\displaystyle\alpha\leq 2e^{-\frac{1}{4}\lambda^{2}\Tr(\Sigma)(2\gamma+\gamma^{2})}

\displaystyle\forall\lambda^{2}\leq\min(\frac{1}{2\|\Sigma\|},\frac{2\gamma+\gamma^{2}}{2\|\Sigma\|},\frac{1}{4(1+\gamma)\sqrt{\Tr(\Sigma)}\|C\|},\frac{1}{4\|C\|_{F}^{2}})

Set
\lambda^{2}
to the maximum of this range to get

\alpha\leq 2e^{-\frac{1}{4}\min(\frac{1}{2\|\Sigma\|},\frac{2\gamma+\gamma^{2}}{2\|\Sigma\|},\frac{1}{4(1+\gamma)\sqrt{\Tr(\Sigma)}\|C\|},\frac{1}{4\|C\|_{F}^{2}})(2\gamma+\gamma^{2})\Tr(\Sigma)}

The first two cases can be merged:

\min(\frac{2\gamma+\gamma^{2}}{2},\frac{(2\gamma+\gamma^{2})^{2}}{2})\geq\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}
. Thus:

\alpha\leq 2e^{-\frac{1}{16}\min(\frac{\gamma^{2}\Tr(\Sigma)}{\|\Sigma\|},\frac{\gamma\sqrt{\Tr(\Sigma)}}{\|C\|},\frac{(2\gamma+\gamma^{2})\Tr(\Sigma)}{\|C\|_{F}^{2}})}.

Plugging in
\gamma=\frac{t}{\sqrt{\Tr(\Sigma)}}
gives the first
result, and setting
t
such that the exponent is

\log\frac{2}{\delta}
gives the second.
∎

6
Conclusion and Future Work

In this paper we gave an algorithm for location estimation in high dimensions, getting non-asymptotic error bounds approaching those of
\mathcal{N}(0,\frac{\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}}{n})
, where
\mathcal{I}_{R}
is the Fisher information matrix of our distribution when smoothed using
\mathcal{N}(0,R)
for small
R
that decays with
n
. In the process of proving this result, we obtained a new concentration inequality for the norm of high-dimensional random variables whose
1
-dimensional projections are subgamma, which may be of independent interest. Even in
1
dimension, our results give improvement for constant failure probability.
For function classes such as a mixture of Laplacians, no previous work gives a rate for the asymptotic convergence to the Cramér-Rao bound as
n\to\infty
for fixed
\delta
.

This paper is one step in the finite-sample theory of parameter estimation. Our quantitative bounds could be improved: our bound on the rate of convergence to Cramér-Rao is
1+\frac{1}{\operatorname*{\mathrm{poly}}(n)}
, but one could hope for faster convergence (
1+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}
in general, and
1+\frac{1}{n}
for some specific function classes).
More generally, one can consider estimation of parameters other than location; the Cramér-Rao bound still relates the asymptotic behavior to the Fisher information, but a rate of convergence remains elusive. We believe that understanding high-dimensional location estimation is a good step toward understanding the estimation of multiple parameters.

Fact A.2

,

\displaystyle\operatorname*{\mathbb{P}}_{y_{i}\overset{i.i.d.}{\sim}f_{r}^{\lambda}}\Bigg(

\displaystyle|\hat{s}(\lambda+\epsilon)-\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x\leftarrow f_{r}}[s(x-\epsilon)]|\geq\sqrt{\frac{2\max(\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x}[s^{2}_{r}(x-\epsilon)],\mathcal{I}_{r})\log\frac{2}{\delta}}{n}}+\frac{15\log\frac{2}{\delta}}{nr}\Bigg)\leq\delta

The next two facts bound the expectation and second moment of the score.

Fact A.3
.

For any
|\epsilon|\leq r/2
, the expected score

\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim f_{r}}\left[s_{r}(x+\epsilon)\right]
satisfies

\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim f_{r}}\left[s_{r}(x+\epsilon)\right]\in\left[-\mathcal{I}_{r}\epsilon\pm O\left(\sqrt{\mathcal{I}_{r}}\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{r^{2}}\right)\right]

Fact A.4
.

For any
|\epsilon|\leq r/2
, if
r/\epsilon=\Omega(\sqrt{\log e/(r^{2}\mathcal{I}_{r})})
, the second moment of the score satisfies

\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim f_{r}}\left[s_{r}^{2}(x+\epsilon)\right]\leq\mathcal{I}_{r}\left(1+O\left(\frac{\epsilon}{r}\sqrt{\log\frac{e}{r^{2}\mathcal{I}_{r}}}\right)\right)

Furthermore, we always have
\mathcal{I}_{r}\leq 1/r^{2}
, and therefore
\sqrt{\log 1/(r^{2}\mathcal{I}_{r})}
above is well-defined.

We can now prove Lemma
A.1
using these facts.
The proof strategy is straightforward: we use Facts
A.2
and
A.4
to show that
\hat{s}(y)
concentrates close to its expectation with high probability, and we use Fact
A.3
to show that the expectation of
\hat{s}(y)
, which is
\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}[s(x-\epsilon)]
for
y=\lambda+\epsilon
, is very close to
\mathcal{I}_{r}\epsilon
.
The triangle inequality then implies that
y-(\hat{s}(y)/\mathcal{I}_{r})
must be close to
\lambda
with high probability.

Proof of Lemma
A.1
.

Let
\lambda_{1}=\lambda+\epsilon
.
By the lemma assumptions,
|\epsilon|\leq\epsilon_{\max}
.

First, we show that, under the lemma assumption that
r^{2}\sqrt{\mathcal{I}_{r}}\geq\gamma\epsilon_{\max}
, Fact
A.4
implies that the second moment of the score at
\lambda-\epsilon
, namely
\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim f_{r}}[s^{2}_{r}(x+\epsilon)]
, is upper bounded by
(1+O(1/\gamma))\mathcal{I}_{r}
.

To check that the precondition of Fact
A.4
holds, note that
r^{2}\sqrt{\mathcal{I}_{r}}\geq\gamma\epsilon_{\max}\geq\gamma\epsilon
is equivalent to
r/\epsilon\geq\gamma/\sqrt{r^{2}\mathcal{I}_{r}}
, which implies that

\displaystyle\frac{r}{\epsilon}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\gamma}{\sqrt{r^{2}\mathcal{I}_{r}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma}{\sqrt{e}}\sqrt{\frac{e}{r^{2}\mathcal{I}_{r}}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\gamma}{\sqrt{e}}\sqrt{\log\frac{e}{r^{2}\mathcal{I}_{r}}}

satisfying the precondition of Fact
A.4
.

Then, the fact implies that

\displaystyle\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim f_{r}}[s^{2}_{r}(x+\epsilon)]

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{I}_{r}\left(1+O\left(\frac{\epsilon}{r}\sqrt{\log\frac{e}{r^{2}\mathcal{I}_{r}}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{I}_{r}\left(1+O\left(\frac{\epsilon_{\max}}{r}\sqrt{\log\frac{e}{r^{2}\mathcal{I}_{r}}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{I}_{r}\left(1+O\left(\frac{\epsilon_{\max}}{r}\sqrt{\frac{e}{r^{2}\mathcal{I}_{r}}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{I}_{r}\left(1+O\left(\frac{\epsilon_{\max}}{r^{2}\sqrt{\mathcal{I}_{r}}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{I}_{r}\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{\gamma}\right)\right)

Next, we combine the concentration bound of Fact
A.2
with the second moment bound for
\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x}[s^{2}_{r}(x+\epsilon)]
we just derived to show that
\hat{s}(\lambda-\epsilon)
is close to its expectation with high probability.

\displaystyle\left|\hat{s}(y)-\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x\leftarrow f_{r}}[s(\lambda-\epsilon)]\right|\leq\;

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{2\log\frac{2}{\delta}}{n}\mathcal{I}_{r}}\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{\gamma}\right)\right)+\frac{15\log\frac{2}{\delta}}{nr}

\displaystyle\leq\;

\displaystyle\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{\gamma}\right)\right)\sqrt{\frac{2\log\frac{2}{\delta}}{n}\mathcal{I}_{r}}+\frac{15}{2\sqrt{\gamma}}\left(\frac{2\log\frac{2}{\delta}}{n}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}\sqrt{\frac{2\log\frac{2}{\delta}}{n}\mathcal{I}_{r}}\quad\text{(see below)}

\displaystyle\leq\;

\displaystyle\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{\gamma}\right)\right)\sqrt{\frac{2\log\frac{2}{\delta}}{n}\mathcal{I}_{r}}+O\left(\frac{1}{\gamma}\right)\sqrt{\frac{2\log\frac{2}{\delta}}{n}\mathcal{I}_{r}}\quad\text{since $\log\frac{2}{\delta}/n\leq 1/\gamma^{2}$}

\displaystyle=\;

\displaystyle\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{\gamma}\right)\right)\sqrt{\frac{2\log\frac{2}{\delta}}{n}\mathcal{I}_{r}}

where the second inequality is due to the assumption that
r^{2}\sqrt{\mathcal{I}_{r}}\geq\gamma\epsilon_{\max}\geq\gamma\sqrt{\frac{2\log\frac{1}{\delta}}{n}\frac{1}{\mathcal{I}_{r}}}
.

Further using Fact
A.3
, this implies that
\epsilon=y-\lambda
is well-approximated by
\hat{s}(y)/\mathcal{I}_{r}
, as follows.

\displaystyle|\epsilon-(\hat{s}(y)/\mathcal{I}_{r})|=\;

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mathcal{I}_{r}}|\mathcal{I}_{r}\epsilon-\hat{s}(y)|

\displaystyle=\;

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mathcal{I}_{r}}\left|\hat{s}(y)-\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x\leftarrow f_{r}}[s(\lambda-\epsilon)]+\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x\leftarrow f_{r}}[s(\lambda-\epsilon)]-\mathcal{I}_{r}\epsilon\right|

\displaystyle\leq\;

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mathcal{I}_{r}}\left|\hat{s}(y)-\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x\leftarrow f_{r}}[s(\lambda-\epsilon)]\right|+\frac{1}{\mathcal{I}_{r}}\left|\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x\leftarrow f_{r}}[s(\lambda-\epsilon)]-\mathcal{I}_{r}\epsilon\right|

\displaystyle=\;

\displaystyle\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{\gamma}\right)\right)\sqrt{\frac{2\log\frac{2}{\delta}}{n\mathcal{I}_{r}}}+O\left(\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{r^{2}\sqrt{\mathcal{I}_{r}}}\right)

by the previous bound and Fact
A.3

By the lemma assumption, we have
\epsilon^{2}/r^{2}\leq\epsilon^{2}_{\max}/r^{2}\leq(1/\gamma)\sqrt{\log\frac{2}{\delta}/n}
, and so we have bounded
|\epsilon-(\hat{s}(y)/\mathcal{I}_{r})|
by

|\epsilon-(\hat{s}(y)/\mathcal{I}_{r})|\leq\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{\gamma}\right)\right)\sqrt{\frac{2\log\frac{2}{\delta}}{n\mathcal{I}_{r}}}

To conclude, we have

\displaystyle|\hat{\lambda}-\lambda|=|y-(\hat{s}(y)/\mathcal{I}_{r})-\lambda|=|\lambda+\epsilon-(\hat{s}(y)/\mathcal{I}_{r})-\lambda|\leq\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{\gamma}\right)\right)\sqrt{\frac{2\log\frac{2}{\delta}}{n\mathcal{I}_{r}}}

as desired.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.1

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{2(\log\frac{2}{\delta})(\frac{n}{\log\frac{2}{\delta}})^{1/10}}{(\log\frac{2}{\delta}/n)^{1/10}\,n}}

\displaystyle\sqrt{2}\left(\frac{\log\frac{2}{\delta}}{n}\right)^{2/5}

Therefore, if the above event happens, Step 2 will yield a sample
\alpha

\alpha-\sqrt{2}(\log\frac{2}{\delta}/n)^{2/5}

.
Furthermore, by the minimality condition in the definition of

, the distance between these two quantiles is at most

.

We will apply Lemma

using failure probability

.

For the following calculations, note that

(A)

Appendix B
High dimensional location estimation

This section provides a complete analysis of our main Theorem
B.16
for estimating the location of a high-dimensional distribution. We start by providing some important definitions in Appendix
B.1
. Then, in Appendix
B.2
, we prove some key properties of the score of our smoothed distribution. In Appendix
B.3
we show that our score function is subgamma with appropriate variance and scale parameters. Then, Appendix
B.4
shows an error bound for the deviation between the empirical score estimate and true true score. Finally Appendix
B.5
and

provide analyses of our Local MLE and Global MLE algorithms respectively.

B.1
Definitions

Let
f
be an arbitrary distribution on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and let
Y\sim f
.
Let our smoothing parameter
R\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
be the covariance matrix of our noise
Z_{R}\sim w_{R}=\mathcal{N}(0,R)
sampled independently of
Y
. We define the
R
-smoothed
distribution
f_{R}
to be such that
X=Y+Z_{R}\sim f_{R}
. Thus, the pdf of
f_{R}
is given by

f_{R}(x)=\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{Z_{R}\sim w_{R}}[f(x+Z_{r})]

Let
s_{R}
be the score function of
f_{R}
. We have

s_{R}(x)=\nabla\log f_{R}(x)=\frac{\nabla f_{R}(x)}{f_{R}(x)}

Let
\mathcal{I}_{R}
be the Fisher information matrix of
f_{R}
. Then,

\mathcal{I}_{R}=\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim f_{R}}[s_{R}(x)s_{R}(x)^{T}]

We define the
M
-norm of vector
x
to be

\|x\|_{M}=\sqrt{x^{T}Mx}

B.2

In this section, we prove some properties of the score function
s_{R}
of the
R

that we make use of throughout the paper.
First, in Lemma

Lemma B.1
.

Let
f
be an arbitrary distribution on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and let
f_{R}
be the
R
-smoothed version of
f
. That is,
f_{R}(x)=\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{y\sim f}\left[(2\pi)^{-d/2}\det(R)^{-1/2}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}(x-Y)^{T}R^{-1}(x-Y)\right)\right]
. Let
s_{R}
be the score function of
f_{R}
. Let
(X,Y,Z_{R})
be the joint distribution such that
Y\sim f
,
Z_{R}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,R)
are independent, and
X=Y+Z_{R}\sim f_{R}
. We have for
\epsilon\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\frac{f_{R}(x+\epsilon)}{f_{R}(x)}=\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{Z_{R}|x}\left[e^{\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}Z_{R}-\frac{1}{2}\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon}\right]

so that

s_{R}(x)=\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{Z_{R}|x}\left[R^{-1}Z_{R}\right]

Proof.

First, we show that for
\epsilon\in\mathbb{R}

\frac{f_{R}(x+\epsilon)}{f_{R}(x)}=\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{Z_{R}|x}\left[\frac{w_{R}(Z_{R}+\epsilon)}{w_{R}(Z_{R})}\right]

Note that

p(z|x)=\frac{p(z,x)}{p(x)}=\frac{f(x-z)w_{R}(z)}{f_{R}(x)}

So,

\displaystyle f_{R}(x+\epsilon)

\displaystyle=\int_{[-\infty,\infty]^{d}}w_{R}(z)f(x+\epsilon-z)dz

\displaystyle=\int p(z|x)f_{R}(x)\frac{w_{R}(z+\epsilon)}{w_{R}(z)}dz

\displaystyle=f_{R}(x)\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{Z_{R}|x}\left[\frac{w_{R}(Z_{R}+\epsilon)}{w_{r}(Z_{R})}\right]

But now,
w_{R}(x)=(2\pi)^{-d/2}\det(R)^{-1/2}e^{-\frac{1}{2}x^{T}R^{-1}x}
So,

\frac{f_{R}(x+\epsilon)}{f_{R}(x)}=\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{Z_{R}|x}\left[e^{\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}Z_{R}-\frac{1}{2}\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon}\right]

which is the first claim.
Now, let
\epsilon=\gamma e_{i}
. We take the derivative wrt
\gamma
, and evaluate at
\gamma=0
to get

\frac{\nabla_{e_{i}}f_{R}(x)}{f_{R}(x)}=\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{Z_{R}|x}\left[(R^{-1}Z_{R})_{i}\right]

So,

s_{R}(x)=\frac{\nabla f_{R}(x)}{f_{R}(x)}=\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{Z_{R}|x}\left[R^{-1}Z_{R}\right]

∎

The next Lemma

is a utility result that we make use of in Lemma

Lemma B.2
.

Let
f_{R}
be the
R
-smoothed version of distribution
f
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. For
\epsilon\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, let

\Delta_{\epsilon}(x):=\frac{f_{R}(x+\epsilon)-f_{R}(x)-(\nabla f_{R}(x))^{T}\epsilon}{f_{R}(x)}

Then, for any
\epsilon
such that
|\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon|\leq\frac{1}{4}

\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim f_{R}}[\Delta_{\epsilon}(x)^{2}]\lesssim(\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon)^{2}

Proof.

By Lemma
B.1
, we have

\displaystyle\Delta_{\epsilon}(x)=\frac{f_{R}(x+\epsilon)-f_{R}(x)-(\nabla f_{R}(x))^{T}\epsilon}{f_{R}(x)}=\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{Z_{R}|x}\left[e^{\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}Z_{R}-\frac{1}{2}\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon}-1-Z_{R}^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon\right]

Let
\alpha_{\epsilon}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be such that

\alpha_{\epsilon}(z)=e^{\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}z-\frac{1}{2}\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon}-1-z^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon

We want to bound

\displaystyle\begin{split}\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x}[\Delta_{\epsilon}(x)^{2}]&=\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x}[\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{Z_{R}|X}[\alpha_{\epsilon}(Z_{r})]^{2}]\\
&\leq\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x,Z_{R}}[(\alpha_{\epsilon}(Z_{R}))^{2}]\\
&=\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{Z_{R}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,R)}[(\alpha_{\epsilon}(Z_{R}))^{2}]\end{split}

(7)

For the remaining proof, let
W=\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}Z_{R}
. Since
Z_{R}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,R)
, we have that
W\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon)
. When
|W|\leq 1
, by a Taylor expansion, we have

\displaystyle e^{W-\frac{1}{2}\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon}=1+W-\frac{1}{2}\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon+O\left(\left(W-\frac{1}{2}\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon\right)^{2}\right)

so that

\displaystyle|\alpha_{\epsilon}(Z_{R})|\lesssim\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon+W^{2}

This implies that
\alpha_{\epsilon}(Z_{R})^{2}\lesssim(\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon)^{2}+W^{4}
, meaning that

\displaystyle\begin{split}\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{Z_{R}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,R)}\left[(\alpha_{\epsilon}(Z_{R}))^{2}\cdot\mathds{1}_{|\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}Z_{R}|\leq 1}\right]&\lesssim\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{W\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon)}\left[\left((\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon)^{2}+W^{4}\right)\cdot\mathds{1}_{|\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon|\leq 1}\right]\\
&\lesssim(\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon)^{2}+\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{W\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon)}[W^{4}]\\
&\lesssim(\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon)^{2}\end{split}

(8)

On the other hand, when
|W|\geq 1
,

|\alpha_{\epsilon}(Z_{R})|\leq e^{|W|}

\displaystyle\begin{split}\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{Z_{R}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,R)}\left[\alpha_{\epsilon}(Z_{R})^{2}\cdot\mathds{1}_{|\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}Z_{r}|\geq 1}\right]&\leq\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{W\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon)}[e^{2|W|}\mathds{1}_{|W|\geq 1}]\\
&=2\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon}}e^{2|w|}e^{-\frac{w^{2}}{2\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon}}dw\\
&=2e^{2|\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon|}\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon}}e^{-\frac{(w-2|\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon|)^{2}}{2\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon}}dw\\
&\leq 2\sqrt{e}\operatorname*{\mathbb{P}}_{W\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon)}\left[W\geq 1-2|\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon|\right]\\
&\lesssim e^{-\frac{(1-2|\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon|)^{2}}{2\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon}}\\
&\leq e^{-\frac{1}{8\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon}}\lesssim(\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon)^{2}\end{split}

(9)

which combines with (
7
) and (
8
) to give the claim.
∎

The next Lemma
B.3
tells us that for good initial estimates
\epsilon\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, “inverting the score” by left multiplying
s_{R}
by
-\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}
provides a good estimate of
\epsilon
in expectation.

Lemma B.3

(Score Inversion)
.

Let
f_{R}
be an
R
-smoothed distribution with Fisher information matrix
\mathcal{I}_{R}
. Let
s_{R}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
be the score function of
f_{R}
. Let
M\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
be a symmetric matrix such that
M\succcurlyeq 0
. Then, for any
\epsilon\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
|\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon|\leq 1/4
, we have

\|\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim f_{R}}[-\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}s_{R}(x+\epsilon)]-\epsilon\|_{M}^{2}\lesssim\|M^{1/2}\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}M^{1/2}\|(\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon)^{2}

Proof.

By definition of
s_{R}
,

\displaystyle\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim f_{R}}[s_{R}(x+\epsilon)]

\displaystyle=\int_{[-\infty,\infty]^{d}}f_{R}(x)\frac{\nabla f_{R}(x+\epsilon)}{f_{R}(x+\epsilon)}dx

\displaystyle=\int\nabla f_{R}(x)\left(\frac{f_{R}(x-\epsilon)-f_{R}(x)}{f_{R}(x)}\right)dx

since

\int\nabla f_{R}(x)dx=0

Now, by the definition of
\mathcal{I}_{R}

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{R}

\displaystyle=\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim f_{R}}[s_{R}(x)s_{R}(x)^{T}]=\int_{[-\infty,\infty]^{d}}\frac{\nabla f_{R}(x)(\nabla f_{R}(x))^{T}}{f_{R}(x)}dx

So,

\displaystyle\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim f_{R}}[s_{R}(x+\epsilon)]+\mathcal{I}_{R}\epsilon

\displaystyle=\int_{-[\infty,\infty]^{d}}\frac{\nabla f_{R}(x)}{f_{R}(x)}\left(f_{R}(x-\epsilon)-f_{R}(x)+(\nabla f_{R}(x))^{T}\epsilon\right)dx

\displaystyle=\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim f_{R}}[\Delta_{-\epsilon}(x)s_{R}(x)]

where
\Delta_{\epsilon}(x):=\frac{f_{R}(x+\epsilon)-f_{R}(x)-(\nabla f_{R}(x))^{T}\epsilon}{f_{R}(x)}
. Now, left multiplying both sides by
-M^{1/2}\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}
,

M^{1/2}\left(\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim f_{R}}[-\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}s_{R}(x+\epsilon)]-\epsilon\right)=\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim f_{R}}\left[\Delta_{-\epsilon}(x)(-M^{1/2}\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}s_{R}(x))\right]

So, we have

\displaystyle\|\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim f_{R}}[-\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}s_{R}(x+\epsilon)]-\epsilon\|_{M}^{2}=\|\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim f_{R}}[\Delta_{-\epsilon}(x)\left(-M^{1/2}\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}s_{R}(x)\right)]\|^{2}

Now, by Cauchy-Schwarz

\displaystyle\|\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim f_{R}}\left[\Delta_{-\epsilon}(x)(-M^{1/2}\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}s_{R}(x))\right]\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sup_{w\in S^{d-1}}\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim f_{R}}[\Delta_{-\epsilon}(x)(-M^{1/2}\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}s_{R}(x))^{T}w]^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{w\in S^{d-1}}\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim f_{R}}[\Delta_{-\epsilon}(x)^{2}]\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim f_{R}}\left[(-M^{1/2}\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}s_{R}(x))^{T}w)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim f_{R}}\left[\Delta_{-\epsilon}(x)^{2}\right]\|\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim f_{R}}\left[(-M^{1/2}\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}s_{R}(x))(s_{R}(x)^{T}\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}M^{1/2})\right]\|

\displaystyle=\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim f_{R}}[\Delta_{-\epsilon}(x)^{2}]\|M^{1/2}\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}M^{1/2}\|

Using Lemma
B.2
, we finally have

\|\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim f_{R}}[-\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}s_{R}(x+\epsilon)]-\epsilon\|_{M}^{2}\lesssim\|M^{1/2}\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}M^{1/2}\|(\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon)^{2}

∎

Next, in Lemma
B.4
we prove a utility result that we make use of in Lemma
B.5
.

Lemma B.4
.

Let
f_{R}
be the
R
-smoothed version of
f
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. For
\epsilon\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, let

\zeta_{\epsilon}(x)=\frac{f_{R}(x-\epsilon)-f_{R}(x)}{f_{R}(x)}

Then, for any
\epsilon
such that
|\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon|\leq 1/4
, and for any
\alpha
such that
\alpha^{2}(\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon)\lesssim 1
we have

\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim f_{R}}[\zeta_{\epsilon}(x)^{2}]\lesssim(\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon)(\alpha^{2}e^{-\Omega(\alpha^{2})}+e^{-\Omega(\alpha^{2})})

Proof.

By Lemma
B.1
, we have

\zeta_{\epsilon}(x)=\frac{f_{R}(x-\epsilon)-f_{R}(x)}{f_{R}(x)}=\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{Z_{R}|x}\left[e^{-\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}Z_{R}-\frac{1}{2}\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon}-1\right]

For the remaining proof, let
W=\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}Z_{R}
. Since
Z_{R}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,R)
, we have that
W\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon)
. So, we have that

\zeta_{\epsilon}(x)=\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{W|x}\left[e^{-W-\frac{1}{2}\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon}-1\right]

Let
\alpha
be a parameter such that
\alpha^{2}(\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon)\lesssim 1
. Now, we have

\displaystyle\zeta_{\epsilon}(x)

\displaystyle\leq O\left(\alpha\sqrt{\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon}\right)+\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{W|x}\left[\mathds{1}_{|W|>\alpha\sqrt{\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon}}\left(e^{-W}-1\right)\right]

So,

\displaystyle\zeta_{\epsilon}(x)^{2}\lesssim\alpha^{2}(\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon)+\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{W|x}\left[\mathds{1}_{|W|>\alpha\sqrt{\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon}}(e^{-W}-1)\right]^{2}

Now, to bound the second term, by Jensen’s inequality, we have

\displaystyle\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{W|x}\left[\mathds{1}_{|W|>\alpha\sqrt{\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon}}(e^{-W}-1)\right]^{2}\leq\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{W|x}\left[\mathds{1}_{|W|>\alpha\sqrt{\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon}}(e^{-W}-1)^{2}\right]

So, we have

\displaystyle\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim f_{R}}\left[\zeta_{\epsilon}(x)^{2}\right]\lesssim\alpha^{2}(\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon)+\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{W}\left[\mathds{1}_{|W|>\alpha\sqrt{\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon}}(e^{-W}-1)^{2}\right]

We will now bound the second term above,
\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{W}\left[\mathds{1}_{|W|>\alpha\sqrt{\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon}}(e^{-W}-1)^{2}\right]
, in two separate cases, when

1.

|W|\leq 1

2.

|W|>1

When
|W|\leq 1
, by linear approximations to the exponential function, we have

(e^{-W}-1)^{2}\lesssim W^{2}

So,

\displaystyle\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{W}\left[\mathds{1}_{|W|>\alpha\sqrt{\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon}}\mathds{1}_{|W|\leq 1}(e^{-W}-1)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{W\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon)}\left[\mathds{1}_{|W|>\alpha\sqrt{\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon}}\cdot W^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim\alpha^{2}(\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon)e^{-\Omega(\alpha^{2})}

On the other hand, when
|W|>1

\displaystyle\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{W}\left[\mathds{1}_{|W|>\max(1,\alpha\sqrt{\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon})}(e^{-W}-1)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\int_{-\infty}^{-(1+\alpha\sqrt{\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon})}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon}}e^{-\frac{w^{2}}{2\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon}}(e^{-w}-1)^{2}dw+\int_{1+\alpha\sqrt{\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon}}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon}}e^{-\frac{w^{2}}{2\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon}}(e^{-w}-1)^{2}dw

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{1+\alpha\sqrt{\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon}}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon}}e^{-\frac{w^{2}}{2\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon}}(e^{w}-1)^{2}dw

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{1+\alpha\sqrt{\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon}}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon}}e^{-\frac{w^{2}}{2\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon}}e^{2w}dw

\displaystyle=e^{2(\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon)}\int_{1+\alpha\sqrt{\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon}}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon}}e^{-\frac{(w-2\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon)^{2}}{2\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon}}dw

\displaystyle\lesssim e^{-\Omega\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon}+\alpha^{2}\right)}\lesssim(\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon)e^{-\Omega(\alpha^{2})}\quad\text{since $|\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon|\leq 1/4$}

Thus, we have shown that

\displaystyle\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim f_{R}}\left[\zeta_{\epsilon}(x)^{2}\right]\lesssim\alpha^{2}(\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon)e^{-\Omega(\alpha^{2})}+(\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon)e^{-\Omega(\alpha^{2})}

The claim follows.
∎

The next Lemma
B.5
shows that for small
\epsilon
, the covariance of the appropriately transformed version of the shifted score
s_{R}(x+\epsilon)
is similar to the corresponding transformation of the Fisher information matrix
\mathcal{I}_{R}
.

Lemma B.5
.

Suppose
f_{R}
is a
R
-smoothed distribution on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with Fisher information matrix
\mathcal{I}_{R}
. Let
M\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
be a symmetric matrix such that
M\succcurlyeq 0
. Then for any
\epsilon\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
|\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon|\leq 1/4
, we have, for every
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
\|v\|=1
,

\displaystyle\left|v^{T}\left(\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim f_{R}}\left[M^{1/2}\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}s_{R}(x+\epsilon)s_{R}(x+\epsilon)^{T}\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}M^{1/2}\right]-M^{1/2}\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}M^{1/2}\right)v\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim\sqrt{\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon}\cdot(v^{T}M^{1/2}\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}M^{1/2}v)\sqrt{\log\left(\sup_{w\in S^{d-1}}\frac{w^{T}R^{-1}w}{w^{T}\mathcal{I}_{R}w}\right)}

Proof.

We have, by definition of score,

\displaystyle\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim f_{R}}[s_{R}(x+\epsilon)s_{R}(x+\epsilon)^{T}]

\displaystyle=\int_{[-\infty,\infty]^{d}}f_{R}(x)\frac{\nabla f_{R}(x+\epsilon)(\nabla f_{R}(x+\epsilon))^{T}}{f_{R}(x+\epsilon)^{2}}dx

\displaystyle=\int f_{R}(x-\epsilon)\frac{\nabla f_{R}(x)(\nabla f_{R}(x))^{T}}{f_{R}(x)^{2}}dx

\displaystyle=\mathcal{I}_{R}+\int(f_{R}(x-\epsilon)-f_{R}(x))\left(\frac{\nabla f_{R}(x)(\nabla f_{R}(x))^{T}}{f_{R}(x)^{2}}\right)dx

\displaystyle=\mathcal{I}_{R}+\int\zeta_{\epsilon}(x)\left(\frac{\nabla f_{R}(x)(\nabla f_{R}(x))^{T}}{f_{R}(x)}\right)dx

\displaystyle=\mathcal{I}_{R}+\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim f_{R}}\left[\zeta_{\epsilon}(x)\frac{\nabla f_{R}(x)(\nabla f_{R}(x))^{T}}{f_{R}(x)^{2}}\right]

where
\zeta_{\epsilon}(x)=\frac{f_{R}(x-\epsilon)-f_{R}(x)}{f_{R}(x)}
.
Now, since
s_{R}(x)=\frac{\nabla f_{R}(x)}{f_{R}(x)}
, the above is equivalent to

\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim f_{R}}\left[s_{R}(x+\epsilon)s_{R}(x+\epsilon)^{T}\right]-\mathcal{I}_{R}=\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim f_{R}}\left[\zeta_{\epsilon}(x)s_{R}(x)s_{R}(x)^{T}\right]

Left and right multiplying both sides by
M^{1/2}\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}
, this is

\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim f_{R}}\left[M^{1/2}\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}s_{R}(x+\epsilon)s_{R}(x+\epsilon)^{T}\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}M^{1/2}\right]-M^{1/2}\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}M^{1/2}=M^{1/2}\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim f_{R}}\left[\zeta_{\epsilon}(x)s_{R}(x)s_{R}(x)^{T}\right]\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}M^{1/2}

Then, for
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
\|v\|=1

\displaystyle v^{T}\left(\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim f_{R}}\left[M^{1/2}\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}s_{R}(x+\epsilon)s_{R}(x+\epsilon)^{T}\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}M^{1/2}\right]-M^{1/2}\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}M^{1/2}\right)v

\displaystyle=\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim f_{R}}\left[\zeta_{\epsilon}(x)(v^{T}M^{1/2}\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}s_{R}(x))^{2}\right]

Then, using Cauchy-Schwarz,

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\left|v^{T}\left(\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim f_{R}}\left[M^{1/2}\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}s_{R}(x+\epsilon)s_{R}(x+\epsilon)^{T}\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}M^{1/2}\right]-M^{1/2}\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}M^{1/2}\right)v\right|\\
&\leq\sqrt{\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim f_{R}}\left[\zeta_{\epsilon}(x)^{2}\right]\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim f_{R}}\left[(v^{T}M^{1/2}\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}s_{R}(x))^{4}\right]}\end{split}

(10)

To bound the second term inside the square root, recall that by Lemma
B.1
, we have

s_{R}(x)=\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{Z_{R}|x}[R^{-1}Z_{R}]

So, by Jensen’s inequality, we have

\displaystyle\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim f_{R}}[(v^{T}M^{1/2}\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}s_{R}(x))^{4}]

\displaystyle=\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim f_{R}}\left[(v^{T}M^{1/2}\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{Z_{R}|x}[R^{-1}Z_{R}])^{4}\right]

\displaystyle=\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim f_{R}}\left[\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{Z_{R}|x}[v^{T}M^{1/2}\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}R^{-1}Z_{R}]^{4}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim f_{R}}\left[\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{Z_{R}|x}[(v^{T}M^{1/2}\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}R^{-1}Z_{R})^{4}]\right]

\displaystyle=\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{Z_{R}}\left[(v^{T}M^{1/2}\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}R^{-1}Z_{R})^{4}\right]

Now, since
Z_{R}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,R)
, we have that
v^{\top}M^{1/2}\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}R^{-1}Z_{R}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,v^{\top}M^{1/2}\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}R^{-1}\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}M^{1/2}v)
is a 1-dimensional Gaussian.
Thus, using the standard fact about the
4^{\text{th}}
moment of a 1-dimensional Gaussian, we have

\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim f_{R}}\left[(v^{T}M^{1/2}\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}s_{R}(x))^{4}\right]\leq\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{Z_{R}}\left[(v^{T}M^{1/2}\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}R^{-1}Z_{R})^{4}\right]=3(v^{T}M^{1/2}\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}R^{-1}\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}M^{1/2}v)^{2}

, for any
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}

So, combining the above with (
10
), we have

\displaystyle\left|v^{T}\left(\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim f_{R}}\left[M^{1/2}\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}s_{R}(x+\epsilon)s_{R}(x+\epsilon)^{T}\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}M^{1/2}\right]-M^{1/2}\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}M^{1/2}\right)v\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim(v^{T}M^{1/2}\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}R^{-1}\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}M^{1/2}v)\sqrt{(\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon)(\alpha^{2}e^{-\Omega(\alpha^{2})}+e^{-\Omega(\alpha^{2})})}

\displaystyle\left|v^{T}\left(\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim f_{R}}\left[M^{1/2}\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}s_{R}(x+\epsilon)s_{R}(x+\epsilon)^{T}\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}M^{1/2}\right]-M^{1/2}\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}M^{1/2}\right)v\right|

B.3
SubGamma concentration of score

In this section, we establish that every one-dimensional projection of the score function
s_{R}
after applying a symmetric PSD linear transformation is subgamma with appropriate variance and scale parameters. We begin by showing a bound on the Jacobian of the score, which we make use of in future lemmas.

Lemma B.6
.

Let
s_{R}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
be the score function of
f_{R}
, the
R
-smoothed version of distribution
f
. Let
\textbf{J}_{s_{R}}
be the Jacobian of
s_{R}
. We have that

\textbf{J}_{s_{R}}\succcurlyeq-R^{-1}

Proof.

Taking the gradient in Lemma
B.1
wrt
\epsilon
, we have

\frac{\nabla f_{R}(x+\epsilon)}{f_{R}(x)}=\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{Z_{R}|x}\left[e^{\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}Z_{R}-\frac{1}{2}\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon}\left(R^{-1}Z_{R}-R^{-1}\epsilon\right)\right]

So,

\displaystyle s_{R}(x+\epsilon)=\frac{\nabla f_{R}(x+\epsilon)}{f_{R}(x+\epsilon)}\cdot\frac{f_{R}(x+\epsilon)}{f_{R}(x)}=\frac{\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{Z_{R}|x}\left[e^{\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}Z_{R}-\frac{1}{2}\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon}\left(R^{-1}Z_{R}-R^{-1}\epsilon\right)\right]}{\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{Z_{R}|x}\left[e^{\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}Z_{R}-\frac{1}{2}\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon}\right]}

Now, let
\epsilon=\gamma v
for
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
,
\gamma>0
so that
\|v\|=1
. Now,
e^{\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}Z_{R}-\frac{1}{2}\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon}
and
v^{T}R^{-1}Z_{R}-v^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon
are monotonically non-decreasing in
v^{T}R^{-1}Z_{R}
. So, by Lemma
C.1
, they are positively correlated. That is,

\displaystyle v^{T}\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{Z_{R}|x}\left[e^{\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}Z_{R}-\frac{1}{2}\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon}\left(R^{-1}Z_{R}-R^{-1}\epsilon\right)\right]\geq\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{Z_{R}|x}\left[e^{\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}Z_{R}-\frac{1}{2}\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon}\right]\cdot\left(v^{T}\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{Z_{R}|x}\left[R^{-1}Z_{R}-R^{-1}\epsilon\right]\right)

So,

\displaystyle v^{T}s_{R}(x+\epsilon)\geq v^{T}\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{Z_{R}|x}\left[R^{-1}Z_{R}-R^{-1}\epsilon\right]

(11)

Now, by definition of Jacobian

\textbf{J}_{s_{R}}v=\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial\gamma}s_{R}(x+\gamma v)\right]_{\gamma=0}

So, in (
11
), taking the derivative wrt
\gamma
and setting
\gamma=0
, we get

v^{T}\textbf{J}_{s_{R}}v\geq-v^{T}R^{-1}v

as required.
∎

The next lemma shows that every 1-dimensional projection of the score
s_{R}(x)
is subgamma with appropriate variance and scale parameters. As a corollary (Corollary
B.8
) we obtain that every
1
-dimensional projection of the score when transformed using a symmetric PSD matrix is also subgamma, with appropriately transformed variance and scale.

Lemma B.7
.

Let
s_{R}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
be the score function of an
R
-smoothed distribution
f_{R}
with Fisher information matrix
\mathcal{I}_{R}
. For any fixed
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
\|v\|=1
, we have

\displaystyle\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim f_{R}}[|v^{T}R^{1/2}s_{R}(x)|^{k}]\leq(1.6)^{k-2}k^{k/2}(v^{T}R^{1/2}\mathcal{I}_{R}R^{1/2}v)

Equivalently, for any
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
v^{T}R^{1/2}s_{R}(x)
is a subgamma random variable.

v^{T}R^{1/2}s_{R}(x)\in\Gamma(v^{T}R^{1/2}\mathcal{I}_{R}R^{1/2}v,1.6\|v\|)

Proof.

For
x,\gamma\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, by Lemma
B.1
, and Jensen’s inequality,

f_{R}(x+\gamma)\geq f_{R}(x)e^{\gamma^{T}s_{R}(x)-\frac{1}{2}\gamma^{T}R^{-1}\gamma}

Set
\gamma=R^{1/2}v
. Then,

f_{R}\left(x+\gamma\cdot\text{sign}(\gamma^{T}s_{R}(x))\right)\geq f_{R}(x)e^{|\gamma^{T}s_{R}(x)|}/\sqrt{e}

Now, by Lemma
B.6
, we have,

\displaystyle\gamma^{T}s_{R}(x+\gamma)

\displaystyle=\gamma^{T}s_{R}(x)+\gamma^{T}\textbf{J}_{s_{R}}\gamma

\displaystyle=\gamma^{T}s_{R}(x)+v^{T}R^{1/2}\textbf{J}_{s_{R}}R^{1/2}v

\displaystyle\geq\gamma^{T}s_{R}(x)-1

Similarly,

\gamma^{T}s_{R}(x-\gamma)\leq\gamma^{T}s_{R}(x)+1

Combining these two, we have

|\gamma^{T}s_{R}(x+\gamma\cdot\text{sign}(\gamma^{T}s_{R}(x))|\geq|\gamma^{T}s_{R}(x)|-1

So, for any
k\geq 2
, and
|\gamma^{T}s_{R}(x)|>\alpha
for
\alpha:=2+1.2\sqrt{k}

\displaystyle f_{R}(x+\gamma\cdot\text{sign}(\gamma^{T}s_{R}(x))|\gamma^{T}s_{R}(x+\gamma\cdot\text{sign}(\gamma^{T}s_{R}(x)))|^{k}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{e}}f_{R}(x)e^{|\gamma^{T}s_{R}(x)|}\left(|\gamma^{T}s_{R}(x)|-1\right)^{k}

\displaystyle=f_{R}(x)|\gamma^{T}s_{R}(x)|^{k}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{e}}e^{|\gamma^{T}s_{R}(x)|}\left(1-\frac{1}{|\gamma^{T}s_{R}(x)|}\right)^{k}\right)

\displaystyle\geq f_{R}(x)|\gamma^{T}s_{R}(x)|^{k}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{e}}e^{\alpha-1.4\frac{k}{\alpha}}\right)

\displaystyle\geq f_{R}(x)|\gamma^{T}s_{R}(x)|^{k}\cdot 4

Thus,

f_{R}(x)|\gamma^{T}s_{R}(x)|^{k}\leq\frac{1}{4}\left(f_{R}(x-\gamma)|\gamma^{T}s_{R}(x-\gamma)|^{k}+f_{R}(x+\gamma)|\gamma^{T}s_{R}(x+\gamma)|^{k}\right)

when
k\geq 2
and
|\gamma^{T}s_{R}(x)|\geq\alpha
. Integrating this,

\displaystyle\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim f_{R}}\left[|\gamma^{T}s_{R}(x)|^{k}\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{[-\infty,\infty]^{d}}f_{R}(x)|\gamma^{T}s_{R}(x)|^{k}dx

\displaystyle\leq 2\int f_{R}(x)|\gamma^{T}s_{R}(x)|^{k}-\frac{1}{4}f_{R}(x-\gamma)|\gamma^{T}s_{R}(x-\gamma)|^{k}-\frac{1}{4}f_{R}(x+\gamma)|\gamma^{T}s_{R}(x+\gamma)|dx

\displaystyle\leq 2\int f_{R}(x)|\gamma^{T}s_{R}(x)|^{k}\mathds{1}_{|\gamma^{T}s_{R}(x)|<\alpha}dx

\displaystyle\leq 2\int f_{R}(x)|\gamma^{T}s_{R}(x)|^{2}\alpha^{k-2}\mathds{1}_{|\gamma^{T}s_{R}(x)|<\alpha}dx

\displaystyle\leq 2\alpha^{k-2}\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}[|\gamma^{T}s_{R}(x)|^{2}]=2\alpha^{k-2}\gamma^{T}\mathcal{I}_{R}\gamma

Finally, for any
k\geq 2
,

2\alpha^{k-2}=2(1.2\sqrt{k}+2)^{k-2}\leq k^{k/2}\cdot 1.6^{k-2}

The claim follows.
∎

Lemma B.9
.

Let
s_{R}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
be the score function of an
R
-smoothed distribution
f_{R}
with Fisher information matrix
\mathcal{I}_{R}
. For any fixed
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
\|v\|=1
,
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
k\geq 3
, and
\epsilon\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
0\leq\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon\leq 1/4
, if
v^{T}R^{1/2}s_{R}(x+\epsilon)\geq\max(2\sqrt{k}+2,9.5),
then, for
\gamma=R^{1/2}v
,

f_{R}(x)|\gamma^{T}s_{R}(x+\epsilon)|^{k}\leq\frac{1}{5}\max\left(f_{R}(x-\epsilon)|\gamma^{T}s_{R}(x-\epsilon)|^{k},f_{R}(x+\epsilon+\gamma)|\gamma^{T}s_{R}(x+\epsilon+\gamma)|^{k}\right)

Proof.

Let
\alpha:=\frac{f_{R}(x)}{f_{R}(x+\epsilon)}
. By Lemma
B.1
, we have

\displaystyle\alpha=\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{Z_{R}|x+\epsilon}\left[e^{-\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}Z_{R}-\frac{1}{2}\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon}\right]

(12)

Let
\gamma=R^{1/2}v
. We will consider two cases

When
\log\alpha<\frac{3}{4}\gamma^{T}s_{R}(x+\epsilon)-2
. First, by Lemma
B.1
and Jensen’s inequality, we have

\frac{f_{R}(x+\epsilon+\gamma)}{f_{R}(x+\epsilon)}\geq e^{\gamma^{T}s_{R}(x+\epsilon)-1/2}

Also, by Lemma
B.6
, we have

\gamma^{T}s_{R}(x+\epsilon+\gamma)\geq\gamma^{T}s_{R}(x+\epsilon)-1

So,

\displaystyle f_{R}(x+\epsilon+\gamma)|\gamma^{T}s_{R}(x+\epsilon+\gamma)|^{k}

\displaystyle\geq f_{R}(x+\epsilon)|\gamma^{T}s_{R}(x+\epsilon)|^{k}e^{\gamma^{T}s_{R}(x+\epsilon)-\frac{1}{2}}\left(1-\frac{1}{\gamma^{T}(s_{R}(x+\epsilon)}\right)^{k}

\displaystyle\geq f_{R}(x+\epsilon)|\gamma^{T}s_{R}(x+\epsilon)|^{k}e^{\gamma^{T}s_{R}(x+\epsilon)-\frac{k}{\gamma^{T}s_{R}(x+\epsilon)-1}-\frac{1}{2}}

Since
\gamma^{T}s_{R}(x+\epsilon)\geq 2\sqrt{k}+2
,

f_{R}(x+\epsilon+\gamma)|s_{R}(x+\epsilon+\gamma)|^{k}\geq f_{R}(x+\epsilon)|s_{R}(x+\epsilon)|^{k}e^{\frac{3}{4}\gamma^{T}s_{R}(x+\epsilon)}

So, since

\alpha=\frac{f_{R}(x)}{f_{R}(x+\epsilon)}\leq e^{\frac{3}{4}\gamma^{T}s_{R}(x+\epsilon)-2}

we have

f(x)|s_{R}(x+\epsilon)|^{k}=\alpha f_{R}(x+\epsilon)|s_{R}(x+\epsilon)|^{k}\leq\frac{1}{5}f_{R}(x+\epsilon+\gamma)|s_{R}(x+\epsilon+\gamma)|^{k}

When
\log\alpha>\frac{3}{4}\gamma^{T}s_{R}(x+\epsilon)-2
.
Evaluating (
12
) at
x-\epsilon
gives

\frac{f_{R}(x-\epsilon)}{f_{R}(x)}=\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{Z_{R}|x}\left[e^{-\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}Z_{R}-\frac{1}{2}\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon}\right]

Taking the gradient wrt
\epsilon
, we have

\frac{\nabla f_{R}(x-\epsilon)}{f_{R}(x)}=\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{Z_{R}|x}\left[R^{-1}(Z_{R}+\epsilon)e^{-\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}Z_{R}-\frac{1}{2}\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon}\right]

so evaluating at
x+\epsilon
,

\frac{\nabla f_{R}(x)}{f_{R}(x+\epsilon)}=\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{Z_{R}|x+\epsilon}\left[R^{-1}(Z_{R}+\epsilon)e^{-\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}Z_{R}-\frac{1}{2}\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon}\right]

In particular,

\epsilon^{T}\frac{\nabla f_{R}(x)}{f_{R}(x+\epsilon)}=\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{Z_{R}|x+\epsilon}\left[\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}(Z_{R}+\epsilon)e^{-\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}Z_{R}-\frac{1}{2}\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon}\right]

Define
y=e^{-\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}Z_{R}-\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon}
so that
\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{Z_{R}|x+\epsilon}[y]=\alpha e^{-\frac{1}{2}\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon}
, and

\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}(Z_{R}+\epsilon)e^{-\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}Z_{R}-\frac{1}{2}\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon}=-e^{\frac{1}{2}\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon}y\log y

is concave, so by Jensen’s inequality,

\epsilon^{T}\frac{\nabla f_{R}(x)}{f_{R}(x+\epsilon)}\leq-e^{\frac{1}{2}\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon}\left(e^{-\frac{1}{2}\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon}\alpha\right)\log\left(e^{-\frac{1}{2}\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon}\alpha\right)=-\alpha\log\alpha+\frac{1}{2}\alpha\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon

So,

\epsilon^{T}s_{R}(x)=\epsilon^{T}\frac{\nabla f_{R}(x)}{f_{R}(x)}\leq-\log\alpha+\frac{1}{2}\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon

Finally we consider the move to
x-\epsilon
. By Lemma
B.6
, we have

\epsilon^{T}s_{R}(x-\epsilon)\leq s_{R}(x)+\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon\leq-\log\alpha+\frac{3}{2}\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon

By Lemma
B.1
,

\frac{f_{R}(x-\epsilon)}{f_{R}(x+\epsilon)}=\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{Z_{R}|x+\epsilon}\left[e^{-2\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}Z_{R}-2\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon}\right]=\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{Z_{R}|x+\epsilon}[y^{2}]\geq\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{Z_{R}|x+\epsilon}[y]^{2}=\alpha^{2}e^{-\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon}

Since
\log\alpha\geq\frac{3}{4}\gamma^{T}s_{R}(x+\epsilon)-2
,

-\epsilon^{T}s_{R}(x-\epsilon)\geq\frac{3}{4}\gamma^{T}s_{R}(x+\epsilon)-2-\frac{3}{2}\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon\geq\frac{3}{4}\gamma^{T}s_{R}(x+\epsilon)-\frac{19}{8}\geq\gamma^{T}s_{R}(x)

where the second inequality comes from the fact that
\frac{3}{4}\gamma^{T}s_{R}(x+\epsilon)-2>0
, so that the function is decreasing in
\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon
, and
\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon\leq 1/4
.
Thus,

f_{R}(x-\epsilon)|\gamma^{T}s_{R}(x-\epsilon)|^{k}\geq\alpha e^{-\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon}f_{R}(x)|s_{R}(x+\epsilon)|^{k}

Since our assumptions give
\alpha e^{-\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon}\geq 5
, we get the result.
∎

.

Let

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{e}}f_{R}(x)e^{\gamma^{T}s_{R}(x+\epsilon)-1}\left(|\gamma^{T}s_{R}(x+\epsilon)|-1\right)^{k}

\displaystyle\geq f_{R}(x)|\gamma^{T}s_{R}(x+\epsilon)|^{k}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{e}}e^{\gamma^{T}s_{R}(x+\epsilon)-1}\left(1-\frac{1}{|\gamma^{T}s_{R}(x+\epsilon)|}\right)^{k}\right)

Lemma B.13
.

Let
f
be an arbitrary distribution on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and let
f_{R}
be the
R
-smoothed version of
f
. Let
\mathcal{I}_{R}
be the Fisher information matrix of
f_{R}
. Let
\epsilon\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be such that
\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon\leq 1/4

. Suppose we have
n

. Let
M\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}

, we have

\displaystyle\|\hat{\epsilon}-\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim f_{R}}[\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}s_{R}(x-\epsilon)]\|_{M}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{\Tr(T)}{n}}+4\sqrt{\frac{\|T\|\log\frac{2}{\delta}}{n}}+16\frac{\|M^{1/2}\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}R^{-1/2}\|\log\frac{2}{\delta}}{n}+8\frac{\|M^{1/2}\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}R^{-1/2}\|_{F}^{2}}{n^{3/2}\sqrt{\Tr(T)}}\log\frac{2}{\delta}

By Corollary
B.12
,
M^{1/2}\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}s_{R}(x)
is
(T,M^{1/2}\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}R^{-1/2})
-subgamma. Then, applying our subgamma norm concentration bound from Theorem
5.1
gives

\displaystyle\|\hat{\epsilon}-\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim f_{R}}\left[\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}s_{R}(x-\epsilon)\right]\|_{M}

\displaystyle=\left\|M^{1/2}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}s_{R}(x_{i}-\lambda-\epsilon)\right)-M^{1/2}\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim f_{R}}\left[\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}s_{R}(x-\epsilon)\right]\right\|

\displaystyle=\left\|\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}M^{1/2}\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}s_{R}(x_{i}-\lambda-\epsilon)\right)-\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim f_{R}}\left[M^{1/2}\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}s_{R}(x-\epsilon)\right]\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{\Tr(T)}{n}}+4\sqrt{\frac{\|T\|\log\frac{2}{\delta}}{n}}+16\frac{\|M^{1/2}\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}R^{-1/2}\|\log\frac{2}{\delta}}{n}+8\frac{\|M^{1/2}\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}R^{-1/2}\|_{F}^{2}}{n^{3/2}\sqrt{\Tr(T)}}\log\frac{2}{\delta}

∎

Algorithm 3

Lemma B.14

(Local MLE)
.

Suppose we have a known model
f
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and that
f_{R}
is the
R
-smoothed version of
f
, for
R=r^{2}I_{d}
for scalar
r>0
. Suppose
f_{R}
has Fisher information matrix
\mathcal{I}_{R}
.
Further, suppose that the unknown true parameter is
\lambda
, and that we have access to an initial estimate
\lambda_{1}=\lambda+\epsilon
with the guarantee that
\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon\leq\tau
for
\tau\leq 1/4
. Suppose there exists a large parameter
\gamma\geq 1
such that
\tau\leq\frac{1}{\gamma^{2}\log^{2}\frac{\|\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}\|}{r^{2}}}
. Further, suppose
r^{2}\geq 4\gamma^{2}\|\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}\|\frac{\log\frac{2}{\delta}}{n}

Then, with probability
1-\delta
over
n
samples from
f^{\lambda}
, the output of Algorithm
3
satisfies

\displaystyle\|\hat{\lambda}-\lambda\|_{M}

\displaystyle\leq\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{\gamma}\right)\right)\left(\sqrt{\frac{\Tr(M^{1/2}\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}M^{1/2})}{n}}+4\sqrt{\frac{\|M^{1/2}\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}M^{1/2}\|\log\frac{2}{\delta}}{n}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+O\left(\tau\sqrt{\|M^{1/2}\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}M^{1/2}\|}\right)

Proof.

By the guarantee on
\lambda_{1}=\lambda+\epsilon
, we have that
\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon\leq\tau
. Let
T
be as defined in Lemma
B.13
. Now

\displaystyle\sup_{w\in S^{d-1}}\frac{w^{T}R^{-1}w}{w^{T}\mathcal{I}_{R}w}=\frac{\|\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}\|}{r^{2}}

so that since
\tau\leq\frac{1}{\gamma^{2}\log^{2}\frac{\|\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}\|}{r^{2}}}
,

\sqrt{\tau}\log\left(\sup_{w\sim S^{d-1}}\frac{w^{T}R^{-1}w}{w^{T}\mathcal{I}_{R}w}\right)\leq\frac{1}{\gamma}

So, we have

\Tr(T)\leq\Tr(M^{1/2}\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}M^{1/2})\left(1+\frac{1}{\gamma}\right)

and

\|T\|\leq\|M^{1/2}\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}M^{1/2}\|\left(1+\frac{1}{\gamma}\right)

So, by Lemma
B.13

\displaystyle\|\hat{\epsilon}-\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim f_{R}}[\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}s_{R}(x-\epsilon)]\|_{M}

\displaystyle\leq\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{\gamma}\right)\right)\left(\sqrt{\frac{\Tr(M^{1/2}\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}M^{1/2})}{n}}+4\sqrt{\frac{\|M^{1/2}\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}M^{1/2}\|\log\frac{2}{\delta}}{n}}+\frac{8\|M^{1/2}\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}\|_{F}^{2}}{r^{2}n^{3/2}\sqrt{\Tr(M^{1/2}\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}M^{1/2})}}\log\frac{2}{\delta}\right)

\displaystyle+16\frac{\|M^{1/2}\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}\|\log\frac{2}{\delta}}{rn}

Since
r^{2}\geq 4\gamma^{2}\|\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}\|\frac{\log\frac{2}{\delta}}{n}
,
\frac{1}{r}\leq\left(\frac{n}{\log\frac{2}{\delta}}\right)^{1/2}\frac{1}{2\gamma\sqrt{\|\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}\|}}
. So,

\displaystyle 16\frac{\|M^{1/2}\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}\|\log\frac{2}{\delta}}{rn}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{8\|M^{1/2}\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}\|}{\gamma\sqrt{\|\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}\|}}\left(\frac{\log\frac{2}{\delta}}{n}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{8}{\gamma}\sqrt{\frac{\|M^{1/2}\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}M^{1/2}\|\log\frac{2}{\delta}}{n}}

\displaystyle\quad\text{since }\frac{(\|M^{1/2}\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}\|)^{2}}{\|M^{1/2}\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}M^{1/2}\|}=\left(\frac{\|M^{1/2}\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}\|}{\|M^{1/2}\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1/2}\|}\right)^{2}\leq\|\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}\|

Similarly,
\frac{1}{r^{2}}\leq\frac{n}{4\gamma^{2}\|\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}\|\log\frac{2}{\delta}}
. So,

\displaystyle\frac{8\|M^{1/2}\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}\|_{F}^{2}}{r^{2}n^{3/2}\sqrt{\Tr(M^{1/2}\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}M^{1/2})}}\log\frac{2}{\delta}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{8\Tr(M\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-2})}{r^{2}n^{3/2}\sqrt{\Tr(M\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1})}}\log\frac{2}{\delta}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\Tr(M\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-2})}{\gamma\|\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}\|n\sqrt{\Tr(M\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1})}}\sqrt{\log\frac{2}{\delta}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{8}{\gamma}\sqrt{\frac{\Tr(M^{1/2}\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}M^{1/2})}{n}}\quad\text{using Lemma~\ref{lem:trace_AB_bound}}

So, we have

\displaystyle\left\|\hat{\epsilon}-\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim f_{R}}\left[\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}s_{R}(x-\epsilon)\right]\right\|_{M}\leq\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{\gamma}\right)\right)\left(\sqrt{\frac{\Tr(M^{1/2}\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}M^{1/2})}{n}}+\sqrt{\frac{\|M^{1/2}\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}M^{1/2}\|\log\frac{2}{\delta}}{n}}\right)

Now, using Lemma
B.3

\displaystyle\left\|\epsilon-\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim f_{R}}\left[\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}s_{R}(x-\epsilon)\right]\right\|_{M}\lesssim\sqrt{\|M^{1/2}\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}M^{1/2}\|}(\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon)\leq\tau\sqrt{\|M^{1/2}\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}M^{1/2}\|}

So, we have

\displaystyle\|\hat{\epsilon}-\epsilon\|_{M}

\displaystyle\leq\|\hat{\epsilon}-\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim f_{R}}\left[\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}s_{R}(x-\epsilon)\right]\|_{M}+\|\epsilon-\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim f_{R}}\left[\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}s_{R}(x-\epsilon)\right]\|_{M}

\displaystyle\leq\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{\gamma}\right)\right)\left(\sqrt{\frac{\Tr(M^{1/2}\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}M^{1/2})}{n}}+4\sqrt{\frac{\|M^{1/2}\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}M^{1/2}\|\log\frac{2}{\delta}}{n}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+O\left(\tau\sqrt{\|M^{1/2}\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}M^{1/2}\|}\right)

Now, since
\hat{\lambda}=\lambda_{1}-\hat{\epsilon}
and
\lambda=\lambda_{1}-\epsilon
,
\hat{\lambda}-\lambda=\hat{\epsilon}-\epsilon
. The claim follows.
∎

B.6
Global MLE

In this section, we state and prove our main theorem, which shows how to estimate the location
\lambda
on rate that depends on
\mathcal{I}_{R}
, given
n
samples from
f^{\lambda}
.

We begin by stating a result from the heavy-tailed estimation literature, which we will make use of to generate an initial estimate
\lambda+\epsilon
. We will then apply the result from the previous section to refine this estimate in order to recover our final estimate.

Theorem B.15

(
(
Hopkins 2018
;
Cherapanamjeri et al. 2019
)
)
.

There are universal constants
C_{0},C_{1},C_{2}
such that for every
n,d\in\mathbb{N}
and
\delta>2^{-n/C_{2}}
, there is an algorithm which
runs in time
O(nd)+(d\log(1/\delta))^{C_{0}}
such that for every random variable
X
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, given i.i.d. copies
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
of
X
, outputs a vector
\hat{\mu}_{\delta}(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})
such that

\operatorname*{\mathbb{P}}\left[\|\mu-\hat{\mu}_{\delta}\|>C_{1}\left(\sqrt{\frac{\Tr(\Sigma)}{n}}+\sqrt{\frac{\|\Sigma\|\log(1/\delta)}{n}}\right)\right]\leq\delta

where
\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}[X]=\mu
and
\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}\left[(X-\mu)(X-\mu)^{T}\right]=\Sigma

Theorem B.16

(Global MLE)
.

Let
f
be a given model on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and suppose we are given
n
samples from
f^{\lambda}
for unknown
\lambda
. Let
R=r^{2}I_{d}
for
0<r^{2}<\|\Sigma\|
so that
\mathcal{I}_{R}
is the
R
-smoothed Fisher information matrix of
f
, and let
\Sigma
be the covariance of
f
. Let
M\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
be any symmetric matrix with
M\succcurlyeq 0
and let
d_{R}:=d_{\text{eff}}(M^{1/2}\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}M^{1/2})
. Fix failure probability
\delta>0
and let
2\leq\gamma\leq\left(\frac{n}{d_{R}+\log\frac{1}{\delta}}\right)^{1/8-\alpha}
for some
\alpha>0
. Let
n\geq C\gamma^{4}(\frac{\|\Sigma\|}{r^{2}})^{2}\left(\log\frac{4}{\delta}+d_{R}+\left(\frac{d_{\text{eff}}(\Sigma)^{2}}{d_{R}}\right)\right)
for large enough constant
C>0
. Then, with probability
1-\delta
, the output
\hat{\lambda}
of Algorithm
4
satisfies

\displaystyle\|\hat{\lambda}-\lambda\|_{M}\leq\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{\gamma}\right)\right)\left(\sqrt{\frac{\Tr(M^{1/2}\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}M^{1/2})}{n}}+4\sqrt{\frac{\|M^{1/2}\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}M^{1/2}\|\log\frac{4}{\delta}}{n}}\right)

Proof.

By the guarantee from Theorem
B.15
, our initial estimate
\lambda_{1}=\lambda+\epsilon
from Step 1 has the property that with probability
1-\delta/2
,

\|\epsilon\|^{2}\lesssim\frac{\Tr(\Sigma)}{n/\gamma}+\frac{\|\Sigma\|\log\frac{2}{\delta}}{n/\gamma}

We condition on the success of Step 1. Let
n^{\prime}=n(1-1/\gamma)\geq n/2
be the number of samples used in Step 2 to call the Local MLE Algorithm
3
. By our lower bound on
n
,

r^{2}\geq\sqrt{C}\gamma^{2}\frac{\|\Sigma\|}{\sqrt{n}}\left(\frac{d_{\text{eff}}(\Sigma)}{\sqrt{d_{R}}}+d_{R}+\sqrt{\log\frac{4}{\delta}}\right)\geq\sqrt{C}\gamma^{2}\frac{\Tr(\Sigma)+\|\Sigma\|\log\frac{4}{\delta}}{\sqrt{2}n^{\prime}}\cdot\frac{(n^{\prime})^{1/2}}{\sqrt{d_{R}+\log\frac{4}{\delta}}}

So, for large enough
C
, since
\gamma>1
, setting

\tau=\frac{1}{\gamma}\sqrt{\frac{d_{R}+\log\frac{4}{\delta}}{n^{\prime}}}

yields that

\displaystyle\epsilon^{T}R^{-1}\epsilon=\frac{\|\epsilon\|^{2}}{r^{2}}\leq\tau

Also
\tau\leq 1/4
since
n^{\prime}\geq\frac{C}{2}(\log\frac{4}{\delta}+d_{R})
.
So, the condition on the confidence set
S
in Lemma
B.14
is satisfied.

By the constraint on
n
, we have

r^{2}\geq\sqrt{C}\gamma^{2}\|\Sigma\|\sqrt{\frac{\log\frac{4}{\delta}+d_{R}+\left(\frac{d_{\text{eff}}(\Sigma)^{2}}{d_{R}}\right)}{n}}\geq\sqrt{\frac{C}{2}}\gamma^{3}\|\Sigma\|\tau

We also have by Lemma
C.2
that
\|\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}\|/{\|\Sigma\|}\leq\frac{\|\Sigma+R\|}{\|\Sigma\|}\leq 2
, so

\log\frac{\|\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}\|}{r^{2}}\leq\log\frac{2\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{C}\gamma^{3}\tau}

so the
\tau
constraint is that

\gamma^{2}\tau\log^{2}\frac{2\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{C}\gamma^{3}\tau}\leq 1

the LHS is at most

O(\tau^{0.99}\gamma^{2.01})<1

since
\tau<1/\gamma^{5}
, with a constant that is arbitrarily small with
C
. So the constraint on
\tau
of Lemma
B.14
is satisfied.

Using the fact that
n^{\prime}\geq\frac{C}{2}\left(\log\frac{4}{\delta}\right)
,

\displaystyle\frac{r^{2}n^{\prime}}{\|\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}\|\log\frac{4}{\delta}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{C}{2}\gamma^{2}\frac{\|\Sigma\|\sqrt{n}}{\|\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}\|\log\frac{4}{\delta}}\left(\frac{d_{\text{eff}}(\Sigma)}{\sqrt{d_{R}}}+\sqrt{\log\frac{4}{\delta}}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{C}{2}\gamma^{2}\frac{\|\Sigma\|\sqrt{n}}{\|\Sigma+R\|\log\frac{4}{\delta}}\sqrt{\log\frac{4}{\delta}}\quad\text{by Lemma~\ref{lem:fisher_lower_bound}}

\displaystyle\geq\gamma^{2}\quad\text{since $R=r^{2}I_{d}$ so that $\|R\|=r^{2}<\|\Sigma\|$}

So the conditions of Lemma
B.14
are satisfied, and with probability
1-\delta/2
,

\displaystyle\|\hat{\lambda}-\lambda\|_{M}

\displaystyle\leq\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{\gamma}\right)\right)\left(\sqrt{\frac{\Tr(M^{1/2}\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}M^{1/2})}{n^{\prime}}}+4\sqrt{\frac{\|M^{1/2}\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}M^{1/2}\|\log\frac{4}{\delta}}{n^{\prime}}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+O\left(\tau\sqrt{\|M^{1/2}\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}M^{1/2}\|}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{\gamma}\right)\right)\left(\sqrt{\frac{\Tr(M^{1/2}\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}M^{1/2})}{n}}+4\sqrt{\frac{\|M^{1/2}\mathcal{I}_{R}^{-1}M^{1/2}\|\log\frac{4}{\delta}}{n}}\right)

since
n^{\prime}=n(1-1/\gamma)
and
\tau=\frac{1}{\gamma}\sqrt{\frac{d_{R}+\log\frac{4}{\delta}}{n^{\prime}}}
. So, our total failure probability is
\delta
. The claim follows.
∎

The following is a continuous version of the rearrangement inequality
(
user940 2015
)
:

Lemma C.1
.

Let
f,g:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be monotonically non-decreasing functions, and
X
be a random
variable over
\mathbb{R}
. Then

\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}[f(X)]\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}[g(X)]\leq\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}[f(X)g(X)]

Proof.

Let
Y
be an independent copy of
X
. By monotonicity,

(f(X)-f(Y))(g(X)-g(Y))\geq 0

always. Taking the expectation of both sides,

2\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}[f(X)g(X)]-2\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}[f(X)g(Y)]\geq 0.

Since
Y
is independent of
X
, this gives the result.
∎

Lemma C.3
.

Let
A,B
be symmetric PSD matrices. Then

Tr(AB)\leq Tr(A)\|B\|

Proof.

Let the eigenvectors of
B
be
v_{1},\dots,v_{d}
.
Then

\Tr(AB)=\sum_{i=1}^{d}v_{i}^{T}A(Bv_{i})\leq\|B\|\sum_{i=1}^{d}v_{i}^{T}Av_{i}=\|B\|\Tr(A).
