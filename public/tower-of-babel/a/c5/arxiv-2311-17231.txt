1.
Introduction

Let
E
be an elliptic curve over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
where
q
is a prime power.
It is of high interest to compute
\#E(\mathbb{F}_{q})
,
where
E(\mathbb{F}_{q})
denotes the group of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
points on
E
.
This is of theoretical significance in addition to its importance for cryptographic applications.
The Schoof–Elkies–Atkin (SEA) algorithm is a standard method for
accomplishing this task.
For more details see
[
12
]
,
[
7
]
,

[
4
, Algorithm 17.25]
.
One observes that the asymptotic time complexity of the algorithm depends
dramatically on the distribution of the so-called Elkies primes.

We define
t_{E}=q+1-\#E(\mathbb{F}_{q})
;
it is well known that this is the trace of the Frobenius endomorphism
on
E
. We refer to a prime
\ell
with

(\ell,q)=1

as an Elkies prime
if the Legendre symbol
(\frac{t_{E}^{2}-4q}{\ell})
equals
1
.
Primes

(\ell,q)=1

where the symbol equals
-1
or
0
are called Atkin primes.

If
t_{E}^{2}-4q=0
, then there are no Elkies primes; this is the case where
|t|=2\sqrt{q}

and hence the curve is “strongly maximal” with respect to the Hasse bound.
However, this latter condition can only happen for supersingular curves.
We will assume throughout that
t_{E}^{2}-4q\neq 0
.
Notice that if
t_{E}^{2}-4q
is a perfect square, then
any prime
\ell
with
(\ell,t_{E}^{2}-4q)=1
is an Elkies prime. We will avoid
this trivial case by assuming
t_{E}^{2}-4q
is not a perfect square.

In
[
11
]
, it is shown that the least Elkies prime is

O((\log q)^{2+\varepsilon})
.
However, it may be that explicit results are more relevant for some applications.
We give an explicit result that also offers a small asymptotic improvement.
Moreover, our proof is different from the one appearing in
[
11
]

as we follow the approach of Montgomery–Lagarias–Odlyzko–Bach, rather
than the arguments of Ankeny.

Theorem 1
.

Assume GRH. For
q\geq 10^{9}
,
the least Elkies prime is bounded above by

(2\log 4q+4)^{2}
.
In particular, the least Elkies prime is
O((\log q)^{2})
.

Let
N_{E}(X)
denote the number of Elkies primes up to
X
.
It is stated in Appendix A of
[
11
]
that estimating
N_{E}(X)
seems to be difficult
(even under GRH). We give such an estimate with error term, but will not
pursue making the constant explicit.

Theorem 2
.

Assume GRH. We have

N_{E}(X)=\frac{\pi(X)}{2}+O\left(\frac{\sqrt{X}(\log qX)^{2}}{\log X}\right)\,.

Notice that the previous theorem immediately implies (under GRH)
that
N_{E}(X)\sim\pi(X)/2
, as expected.
It is noted in
[
13
]
that this corollary follows from
[
11
]
;
however, neither paper gives a bound on the error
N_{E}(X)-\pi(X)/2
.
We note that the paper
[
13
]
studies the distribution of Elkies primes
on average over all elliptic curves, which is a different perspective than we consider here.

3.
Proof of Theorem
1

In light of our assumptions (stated in Section
1
)
we can use the Kronecker symbol to define a Dirichlet character

\chi_{E}(n)=\left(\frac{t_{E}^{2}-4q}{n}\right)\,.

Moreover,
\chi_{E}
is a non-principal character modulo
m=|t_{E}^{2}-4q|\geq 3

satisfying
\chi_{E}(-1)=-1
.
Let
\chi
denote the primitive character of modulus
m^{\prime}
that induces
\chi_{E}
.
Notice that
m^{\prime}\leq m\leq 4q
.
Set
X=(2\log 4q+4)^{2}\geq 2325
.
We will show that there is an Elkies prime less than
X
.
By direct computation, there is always an Elkies prime less than
40
when
m<2^{11}
,
and hence the theorem is trivial in this case. Henceforth, we assume
m\geq 2^{11}
.

Since our character
\chi_{E}
of modulus
m
is not primitive,
the difference between the sum

\sum_{n\leq X}\Lambda(n)\chi_{E}(n)\left(1-\frac{n}{X}\right)

and the corresponding sum where
\chi_{E}
is replaced by the primitive
character
\chi
is bounded (in absolute value) by

\sum_{p|m}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq X\\
n=p^{k}\end{subarray}}\Lambda(n)\left(1-\frac{n}{X}\right)\leq\sum_{p|m}\log p\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq X\\
n=p^{k}\end{subarray}}1\leq\omega(m)\log X\leq D\frac{\log m\log X}{\log\log m}

By
[
10
]
, we know that for
m\geq 3
, one has

\omega(m)\leq 1.3841\frac{\log m}{\log\log m}

which means we can take
D=1.3841
.

Now we begin the proof in earnest.
Suppose that the least Elkies prime is greater than
X
.
That is, we have that
\chi_{E}(p)\neq 1
for all primes
p\leq X
.
We seek a contradiction.
Consider the sum

S(X)=\sum_{n\leq X}\Lambda(n)\chi_{E}(n)\left(1-\frac{n}{X}\right)

We write
S=S_{1}+S_{2}+S_{3}
, where

\displaystyle S_{1}(X)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{n\leq X}\Lambda(n)\left(1-\frac{n}{X}\right)

\displaystyle S_{2}(X)

\displaystyle=\sum_{p\leq X}\Lambda(p)(\chi_{E}(p)+1)\left(1-\frac{p}{X}\right)

\displaystyle S_{3}(X)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq X\\
n=p^{k}\\
k\geq 2\end{subarray}}\Lambda(n)(\chi_{E}(n)+1)\left(1-\frac{n}{X}\right)

By hypothesis,

S_{2}(X)\leq\sum_{p|m}\log p\left(1-\frac{p}{X}\right)\leq\log(\prod_{p|m}p)\leq\log m

Moreover,
according to
Corollary 4.5 of
[
6
]
,

\psi(X)-\theta(X)\leq(1+1.47\cdot 10^{-7})\sqrt{X}+1.78\sqrt[3]{X}+\leq(1+\delta)\sqrt{X}

where
\delta=1/2
,
and therefore

|S_{3}(X)|\leq 2(\psi(X)-\theta(X))\leq 2(1+\delta)\sqrt{X}\,.

By Proposition
1

|S_{1}|\geq\frac{X}{2}-C_{1}\sqrt{X}

Therefore

\displaystyle|S(X)|

\displaystyle\geq\frac{X}{2}-(C_{1}+2(1+\delta))\sqrt{X}-\log m

On the other hand,

|S(X)|\leq C_{2}\sqrt{X}\log m+D\frac{\log m\log X}{\log\log m}

Hence

\frac{X}{2}\leq C_{2}\sqrt{X}\log m+(C_{1}+2(1+\delta))\sqrt{X}+\log m+D\frac{\log m\log X}{\log\log m}

Using
m\leq 4q
, this leads to

\displaystyle\sqrt{X}

\displaystyle\leq 2C_{2}\log 4q+2(C_{1}+2(1+\delta))+\frac{2\log 4q}{\sqrt{X}}+\frac{2D\log(4q)\log X}{\sqrt{X}\log\log 4q}

\displaystyle\leq 1.902\log 4q+6.094

\displaystyle<2\log(4q)+4.

This gives a contradiction,
thereby proving the theorem.

Proof of Theorem
2

We have

\displaystyle N_{E}(X)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\ell\leq X}\left[\left(\frac{t_{E}^{2}-4q}{\ell}\right)+1\right]-a_{E}(X)

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi(X)}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\ell\leq X}\left(\frac{t_{E}^{2}-4q}{\ell}\right)-a_{E}(X)

In the above

a_{E}(X)=\begin{cases}1/2&\ell\mid t\,,\;\chr(\mathbb{F}_{q})<X\\
1&\ell\nmid t\,,\;\chr(\mathbb{F}_{q})<X\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

Using the notation from the previous section, it suffices to bound the sum

\sum_{\ell\leq X}\chi_{E}(\ell)\,.

Partial summation gives

\sum_{\ell\leq X}\chi(\ell)=\frac{1}{\log X}\sum_{\ell\leq X}\chi(\ell)\Lambda(\ell)+\int_{2}^{X}\left(\sum_{\ell\leq t}\chi(\ell)\Lambda(\ell)\right)\frac{dt}{t(\log t)^{2}}\,.

Exercise 7.4.9 of
[
9
]
leads to a bound of the form:

(6)

\left|\sum_{\ell\leq X}\chi(\ell)\Lambda(\ell)\right|\leq C\sqrt{X}(\log mX)^{2}\,,\;t>2\,.

This leads to

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{\ell\leq X}\chi(\ell)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C\sqrt{X}(\log mX)^{2}}{\log X}+C\int_{2}^{X}\frac{(\log tm)^{2}dt}{\sqrt{t}(\log t)^{2}}\,.

Using
X<m
the second term is bounded by

4C(\log m)^{2}\int_{2}^{x}t^{-1/2}(\log t)^{-2}dt
.
Moreover, we have

\int_{2}^{X}\frac{dt}{\sqrt{t}(\log t)^{2}}\leq 2\frac{\sqrt{X}}{\log X}\,.

This yields

\displaystyle\frac{C\sqrt{X}(\log mX)^{2}}{\log X}+\frac{8C\sqrt{X}(\log m)^{2}}{\log X}

\displaystyle=C((\log mX)^{2}+8(\log m)^{2})\frac{\sqrt{X}}{\log X}

\displaystyle\leq 9C(\log mX)^{2}\frac{\sqrt{X}}{\log X}

Putting this all together shows

\left|N_{E}(X)-\frac{\pi(X)}{2}\right|\leq(9C/2)\left(\frac{\sqrt{X}(\log 4qX)^{2}}{\log X}\right)+1\,,

which completes the proof.

5.
Appendix: An explicit formula

Although various explicit formulas associated to Dirichlet
L
-functions are certainly well-known,
we could not find in the literature this particular explicit formula, with all terms rendered visible, for
an arbitrary Dirichlet character. As this is essential for the derivation of our results, we give a brief
argument together with relevant residue calculations.
The first of the three equations that follow appears in
[
8
]
.

Lemma 1
.

Suppose
X\geq 1
.
We have

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leq X}^{\prime}\Lambda(n)\left(1-\frac{n}{X}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{X}{2}-\sum_{\rho}\frac{X^{\rho}}{\rho(\rho+1)}+\frac{1}{X}\frac{\zeta^{\prime}(-1)}{\zeta(-1)}-\frac{\zeta^{\prime}(0)}{\zeta(0)}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\log\left(1-\frac{1}{X^{2}}\right)-\frac{1}{2x}\log\left(1+\frac{2}{X-1}\right)\,.

Suppose
\chi
is a primitive Dirichlet character modulo
m
.
If
\chi(-1)=-1
, then we have

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leq X}\Lambda(n)\chi(n)\left(1-\frac{n}{X}\right)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{\rho\text{ of }L_{\chi}}\frac{X^{\rho}}{\rho(\rho+1)}-\frac{L^{\prime}(0)}{L(0)}+\frac{b_{\chi}}{X}+\frac{\log X}{X}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\log\left(1+\frac{2}{X-1}\right)+\frac{1}{2x}\log\left(1-\frac{1}{X^{2}}\right)\,,

where
b_{\chi}
is the constant term in the Laurent series expansion of

L^{\prime}(s,\chi)/L(s,\chi)
at
s=-1
.
If
\chi(-1)=1
, then we have

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leq X}\Lambda(n)\chi(n)\left(1-\frac{n}{X}\right)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{\rho\text{ of }L_{\chi}}\frac{X^{\rho}}{\rho(\rho+1)}+\frac{1}{X}\frac{L^{\prime}(-1)}{L(-1)}-b_{\chi}-\log X

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\log\left(1-\frac{1}{X^{2}}\right)-\frac{1}{2x}\log\left(1+\frac{2}{X-1}\right)\,,

where
b_{\chi}
is the constant term in the Laurent series
expansion of
L^{\prime}(s,\chi)/L(s,\chi)
at
s=0
.

Proof.

We begin by proving the first equation that involves the zeros of
\zeta(s)
.
We are interested in the sum

(7)

g(X):=\sum_{n\leq X}\Lambda(n)(X-n)\,.

Along these lines, we consider the function

\displaystyle G(s):=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\Lambda(n)(X-n)}{n^{s}}

\displaystyle=X\sum_{n}\frac{\Lambda(n)}{n^{s}}-\sum_{n}\frac{\Lambda(n)}{n^{s-1}}

\displaystyle=X\frac{\zeta^{\prime}(s)}{\zeta(s)}-\frac{\zeta^{\prime}(s-1)}{\zeta(s-1)}\,.

We evaluate the following integral in two different ways:

\mathcal{I}(X):=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\sigma-i\infty}^{\sigma+i\infty}\left\{-G(s)\right\}X^{s}\frac{ds}{s}\,.

Integrating the Dirichlet series for
G(s)
term-by-term and using

\int_{\sigma-i\infty}^{\sigma+i\infty}y^{s}\;\frac{ds}{s}=\begin{cases}1&y>1\\
1/2&y=1\\
0&0<y<1\end{cases}

yields
