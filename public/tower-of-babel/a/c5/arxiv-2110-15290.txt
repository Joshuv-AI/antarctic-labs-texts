Learning to Control Using Image Feedback
R. Krishnan, N. Vignesh, S. Jagannathan

Abstract
Learning to control complex systems using non-traditional feedback, e.g., in the form of snapshot images, is an important task encountered in diverse domains such as robotics, neuroscience, and biology (cellular systems). In this paper, we present a two neural-network (NN)-based feedback control framework to design control policies for systems that generate feedback in the form of images. In particular, we develop a deep Q Q -network (DQN)-driven learning control strategy to synthesize a sequence of control inputs from snapshot images that encode the information pertaining to the current state and control action of the system. Further, to train the networks we employ a direct error-driven learning (EDL) approach that utilizes a set of linear transformations of the NN training error to update the NN weights in each layer. We verify the efficacy of the proposed control strategy using numerical examples.



I
Introduction

Learning to control complex systems using feedback data is an active research topic in systems and control theory. Approaches ranging from traditional adaptive control
[
1
]
to more modern techniques such as adaptive dynamic programming (ADP)
[
2
,
3
]
/reinforcement learning (RL)
[
4
]
that employ artificial neural networks (ANNs) have been proposed to steer systems without relying on complete model information. These control approaches use either the state or output feedback data generated by the system to learn policies for steering the system as desired. In many emerging systems such as robotic swarms, neural systems, and cellular populations, the feedback information is only available in the form of snapshot images
[
5
,
6
]
. Consequently, commonly used learning-based control strategies including ADP/RL techniques have to be upgraded to incorporate feedback data in the form of images and synthesize control policies for steering the system as desired, especially when no desired trajectory or (explicit) state measurements are available.

In this context, recent advancements in the deep
Q
learning, spurred by
[
6
]
, have demonstrated the capacity of RL models employing deep neural networks (DNNs) to perform such complex decision making tasks. In an RL model, an agent or a team of agents learn to achieve the desired objective by learning the best sequence of actions from any situation/state
[
7
]
using experiences (data) collected over time. The validity of such an RL model is typically quantified as a function of the temporal difference (TD) error
[
4
]
, which the agent seeks to reduce during learning.
Specifically, the deep
Q
-network (DQN) introduced in
[
6
]
was the first approach to design a deep RL model in which the TD error was used to learn the weights of a
Q-
network, a parameterized DNN, by mimicking a supervised learning procedure, where a clone of the
Q-
network (referred as target network) was used to generate the target values. In
[
6
]
, the target network was periodically updated with the latest weights of the
Q-
network, providing a stable training regime that mitigated short-term oscillations induced by the moving targets in a standard RL algorithm.
In this context, the problem of designing a learning controller boils down to learning a
Q
-function with a parameterised DNN.

In this paper, we introduce a DNN-based learning strategy to synthesize feedback policies for steering a system generating feedback in the form of images. To this end, we adopt and expand the DQN framework in
[
6
]
and propose a dual NN-driven exploratory learning approach for improving the learning time and efficiency of the DQN. In the proposed approach, two DNNs, independently initialized, take the role of the target and the
Q-
network (actor) alternatively in successive iterations. Furthermore, we propose training rules to update the weights of the DNN using a direct error-driven learning (EDL) strategy, which introduces a set of linear transformations that help compute training signals corresponding to each layer. We refer to this dual NN driven alternative update mechanism as the
cooperative update strategy
(coop). We demonstrate the convergence of EDL analytically, and show faster convergence for EDL in comparison to standard DQN, making it a feasible candidate for developing online control strategies in emerging applications.

There has been a growing interest in combining deep learning with RL and control to enable agents navigating complex and high-dimensional state-space to perform control tasks with non-traditional sensory data. For instance, a neural fitted Q-learning (NFQ) was introduced in

On the other hand, an efficient weight update rule is necessary

18
]

20

In this work, we introduce a dual NN driven exploratory learning approach. In contrast with the existing works that incorporate two NNs

III
Preliminaries

We consider applications where a sequence of images or snapshots are obtained from the system and must be used to choose a control input to the system from a discrete set of feasible actions. Therefore, consider an RL problem, where an agent interacts with an environment (simulates the system) through a sequence of control actions and rewards. With each control action
\boldsymbol{u}(k)
taken by the agent, the internal states of the environment change. Let
\boldsymbol{x}(k)\sim p(\mathcal{S})
be the state of the environment at the
k^{th}
sampling instant and
p(\mathcal{S})
be the distribution on the states with the state space denoted by
\mathcal{S}
. Define
\mathcal{A}
to denote the set of feasible actions,
\mathcal{R}
to denote the set of feasible rewards, and
\Omega
to denote a measurable space. The action

is, in general, based on a control policy
\pi:\mathcal{S}\to\mathcal{A}
, and as the agent takes an action, the internal state of the environment changes from
x(k)
to
x(k+1)
such that
\boldsymbol{x}(k+1)\sim T(\boldsymbol{x}(k),\boldsymbol{u}(k),\boldsymbol{\omega}(k)),
where
{T}:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\times\Omega\to\mathcal{S}
is the state transition function with
\boldsymbol{\omega}(k)\in\Omega
being the internal fluctuations, e.g., noise. In this process, the agent receives an external scalar reward
r(k+1)\in\mathcal{R}
from the environment. The tuple
(x(k),u(k),x(k+1),r(k+1))
is collected as an experience by the RL agent to learn the
Q
-function during its interaction with the environment at time instant
k
.

In our work, we employ NNs to approximate the unknown
Q-
function. To this end, we define a NN with
d
layers, denoted with an ideal parametric map,
y(\boldsymbol{z};\boldsymbol{\theta})
, where
\boldsymbol{z}
is the input and
\boldsymbol{\theta}=[\boldsymbol{W}^{(1)}\cdots\boldsymbol{W}^{(d)}]
is the collection of all the ideal weights for this approximation. Each element in
\boldsymbol{\theta}
, i.e.
\boldsymbol{W}^{(i)},i=1,\cdots d
denotes the weights of the DNN at layer
i.
Let
\varepsilon
denote bounded approximation error
[
23
]
incurred by the ideal map and
f^{(i)}
, for
i=1,\ldots,d
, denote the layer-wise activation functions corresponding to the
d
layers. To denote the estimated NN, we introduce the following set of notations. The estimated weights are denoted by
\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=[\hat{\boldsymbol{W}}^{(1)}\cdots\hat{\boldsymbol{W}}^{(d)}]
, and the estimate of the function
y(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{\theta})
is given by
\hat{y}(\boldsymbol{z};\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}),
where the symbol
\hat{(\cdot)}
is used to denote estimated quantities.

Our network is designed to take as input, the images, and provide as output the
Q-
function value corresponding to each feasible action to the system as in
[
6
]
. We will then employ a greedy policy and choose the control action that obtains the largest
Q-
function value. The learning objective is to learn the optimal
Q-
function. We denote the reward function as
r(\boldsymbol{x}(k),\boldsymbol{u}(k))
, a function of both the state and the action or control inputs. For a given (state, action) pair,
(\boldsymbol{x}(k),\boldsymbol{u}(k))
and an action policy
\pi,
the
Q
-function given as
Q^{\pi}(\boldsymbol{x}(k),\boldsymbol{u}(k))=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{k}r(\boldsymbol{x}(k),\boldsymbol{u}(k)),
where
\gamma\in(0,1)
is the discount factor. Additionally, we use
\mathbb{N}
and
\mathbb{R}
for denoting the set of natural and real numbers, respectively. Furthermore, we use
\|.\|
to denote Euclidean/ Frobenius norm for vectors/matrices.

IV
Methods

In the following, we first introduce the optimization problem and the overall learning architecture. We then present the details of the coop learning scheme.

IV-A

RL Approximation of
Q
-values

In our problem setup, feedback from the system is received by the RL agent in the form of images. Then, the RL agent must learn a map between the image inputs and the
Q

Q

[
4
]
provided as

\displaystyle Q^{*}(\boldsymbol{x}(k),\boldsymbol{u}(k))=max_{u(k+1)}(r(\boldsymbol{x}(k),\boldsymbol{u}(k))

\displaystyle+\gamma^{k}(Q^{*}(\boldsymbol{x}(k+1),\boldsymbol{u}(k+1))),

where

refers to the optimal
Q
-function with
\rho(\mathcal{A})
denoting the distribution over
\mathcal{A}
. A greedy policy is given as
\boldsymbol{u}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x}(k))=argmax_{\boldsymbol{u}(k)}(Q^{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x}(k),\boldsymbol{u}(k)))
and
\rho
refers to
\rho(\mathcal{A})
. In this work, we approximate the
Q
-function with a generic parametric map,
y(\boldsymbol{x}(k),\boldsymbol{u}(k);\boldsymbol{\theta})
with an ideal set of parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}
such that
{Q}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x}(k),\boldsymbol{u}(k))=y(\boldsymbol{x}(k),\boldsymbol{u}(k);\boldsymbol{\theta})
(hitherto denoted as
y
). We want to find
\hat{y}
with
\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(k)
for
y
such that
y
is approximated by DNNs. The cost is defined as the expected value over all the states and all the actions such that
\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{x}(k)\sim p(\mathcal{S}),\boldsymbol{u}(k)\sim\rho(\mathcal{A})}[J(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(k))],
where

\displaystyle J(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(k))=\big[\underbrace{\frac{1}{2}(y(\boldsymbol{x}(k),\boldsymbol{u}(k);\boldsymbol{\theta})-\hat{y}(\boldsymbol{x}(k),\boldsymbol{u}(k);\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}))^{2}}_{\text{Empirical Cost}}

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\varepsilon^{2}+\varepsilon^{T}(y(\boldsymbol{x}(k),\boldsymbol{u}(k);\boldsymbol{\theta})-\hat{y}(\boldsymbol{x}(k),\boldsymbol{u}(k);\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}))}_{\text{Approximation Error Cost}}\big],

(1)

with the target
y(\boldsymbol{x}(k),\boldsymbol{u}(k);\boldsymbol{\theta})=r(\boldsymbol{x}(k),\boldsymbol{u}(k))+\gamma^{k}{\hat{Q}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x}(k+1),\boldsymbol{u}(k+1))}
, coming from the Bellman equation with

denoting the approximation of the optimal

-value obtained by extrapolating the current
Q
-function estimate. Only the empirical cost, the first term, can be minimized as the other two terms depend on the choice of the NN, the available data, and the problem complexity, which is typically compensated via the use of regularization approaches
[
20
,
24
]
. Therefore, we minimize the empirical cost as

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(k)=arg~min_{\bar{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(k)}\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{x}(k)\in\mathcal{S},\boldsymbol{u}(k)\sim\rho(\mathcal{A})}

(2)

\displaystyle\big[J_{E}(\boldsymbol{x}(k),\boldsymbol{u}(k),\hat{y}(\boldsymbol{x}(k),\boldsymbol{u}(k);\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}))\big],

with
J_{E}(\boldsymbol{x}(k),\boldsymbol{u}(k),\hat{y}(\boldsymbol{x}(k),\boldsymbol{u}(k);\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(k)))=\frac{1}{2}\big[r_{k}+\gamma^{k}\hat{Q}_{k+1}^{*}-\hat{y}_{k}\big]^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\big[\epsilon_{k}\big]^{2}
, with
r(\boldsymbol{x}(k),\boldsymbol{u}(k))
denoted by
r_{k}
,
\hat{Q}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x}(k+1),\boldsymbol{u}(k+1))
by
\hat{Q}_{k+1}^{*}
and
\hat{y}(\boldsymbol{x}(k),\boldsymbol{u}(k);\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}})
by
\hat{y}_{k}
. The empirical cost (denoted hitherto as
J_{E}
for notational simplicity) which we seek to minimize is the squared temporal difference (TD) error (
\epsilon_{k}
).

In the following, we describe a TD error driven approach to update the NN weights. Specifically, we sketch the error driven learning rules first, and then, provide the algorithm for the proposed strategy synthesize a sequence of controls.

IV-B

Error driven TD learning

In the earlier section, the problem of obtaining a control policy was formulated as an optimization problem where we attempt to learn the optimal
Q
function through NNs by minimizing
J_{E}
. To learn the parameters of the optimal
Q
function, consider the update rule as

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(k+1)=\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(k)+\alpha\boldsymbol{\Delta}(k),

(3)

where
\alpha>0
is the learning rate,
k
is the sampling instant, and
\boldsymbol{\Delta}(k)
is the parameter adjustment. To simplify notations, we switch to a subscript notation instead of expressing the sampling instants in parenthesis so that (
3
) is rewritten as
\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k+1}=\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k}+\alpha\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{k}
. From here on, for brevity, the notation of the expected value operator is suppressed, and we refer to the expected value of the cost as just the cost. Next, add a regularization term to the cost function to get

\displaystyle{{H}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k})}=\big[J_{E}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k})+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda^{(i)}R_{k}(\hat{\boldsymbol{W}}^{(i)}_{k})\big],

(4)

where
\lambda^{(i)}>0
is the decay coefficient and
R_{k}
is the regularization function applied on
\hat{\boldsymbol{W}}^{(i)}_{k}
with
J_{E}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k})
being used in place of
J_{E}(\boldsymbol{x}(k),\boldsymbol{u}(k);\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(k))
for notational simplicity. The most common way of obtaining the control in this context is to backpropagate the TD error and we write the weight adjustment at each
k
as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{(i)}_{k}=-\big[\nabla_{\hat{\boldsymbol{W}}^{(i)}_{k}}H(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k})\big]=-\big[{\boldsymbol{\delta}}^{(i)}_{k}+\lambda^{(i)}\nabla_{\hat{\boldsymbol{W}}^{(i)}_{k}}R(\hat{\boldsymbol{W}}^{(i)}_{k})\big],

(5)

where the term
\nabla_{\hat{\boldsymbol{W}}^{(i)}_{k}}(\cdot)
denotes the gradient of
(\cdot)
with respect to the NN weight
\hat{\boldsymbol{W}}^{(i)}_{k}
, the second component depends on the choice of
R(\hat{\boldsymbol{W}}^{(i)}_{k})
, and
\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(i)}_{k}
is obtained by applying the chain rule to compute the gradient of the cost with respect to the NN weights. A generalized expression for
{\boldsymbol{\delta}}^{(i)}_{k}
can be derived using the chain rule

19
,
lecun2015deep
]
as

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\delta}}^{(i)}_{k}=f^{(i-1)}(\boldsymbol{x})\epsilon_{k}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}^{(i)}\boldsymbol{I}^{(i)}.

(6)

where
\prod_{j=d}^{i+1}(diag(\nabla f^{(j)}(\boldsymbol{x}))\hat{\boldsymbol{W}}^{(j)})diag(\nabla f^{(i)}(\boldsymbol{x}))
is denoted as
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}^{(i)}.
Observe that the error
\epsilon_{k}
has to propagate through transformation
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}^{(i)}
to impact learning. For a fixed
\boldsymbol{x},

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}^{(i)}
is a linear approximation (gradient) in the neighborhood of the weights. Since, this approximation is a matrix, the singular vectors of
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}^{(i)}
dictate the directions of learning. The magnitude of this learning depends on the singular values of
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}^{(i)}
. Each diagonal element in
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}^{(i)}
is the product of the derivative of the layer-wise activation functions. Therefore, in specific cases, when the derivative of the activation function provides singular values between 0 and 1, the singular values of
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}^{(i)}
would tend towards zero as the number of layers in the deep NN increase. The issue is otherwise known as the vanishing gradients issue (see
[
25
]
and the references therein for details).

Motivated by the success of direct error-driven learning in classification problems
[
19
]
, we introduce a user-defined feedback matrix denoted as
\boldsymbol{B}^{(i)}_{k}
(a hyperparameter), with
\boldsymbol{B}^{(i)}_{k}(\boldsymbol{B}^{(i)}_{k})^{T}
being positive definite, to take the role of
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}^{(i)}_{k}
in (
6
). The new feedback, denoted
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{k}^{(i)}
, is then given as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{k}^{(i)}=[f^{(i-1)}(\boldsymbol{x})]\epsilon_{k}\boldsymbol{B}^{(i)}_{k}.

(7)

With this definition of feedback, the new layer-wise cost function,
\mathcal{H}^{(i)}(\hat{\boldsymbol{W}}^{(i)})
, is defined to be

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{(i)}(\hat{\boldsymbol{W}}^{(i)})=\frac{1}{2}\big[tr((\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{k}^{(i)})^{T}\boldsymbol{P}{\hat{\boldsymbol{W}}^{(i)}}_{k})+\lambda^{(i)}R({\boldsymbol{W}^{(i)}_{k}})\big],

(8)

where
tr(.)
is the trace operator and
\boldsymbol{P}
is a positive definite symmetric matrix of choice. Note that for each layer
i
,
\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{(i)}_{k}
can be understood as the feedback provided by the overall cost
J(\boldsymbol{\theta})
towards controlling the layer
i
of the DNN. Therefore, the layer-wise cost can be interpreted as the minimization of the correlation between
\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{(i)}_{k}
and
\hat{\boldsymbol{W}}^{(i)}_{k}
under the constraint that
\|\hat{\boldsymbol{W}}^{(i)}_{k}\|
is bounded (due to the regularization). Finally, the overall cost,
\mathcal{H}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(k))
, may be written as the sum of layer-wise costs as

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(k))=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathcal{H}^{(i)}(\hat{\boldsymbol{W}}_{k}^{(i)}).

(9)

With the cost
\mathcal{H}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k})
defined in Eq. (
9
), the weight updates for each layer are defined as
\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{(i)}_{k}=-\nabla_{\hat{\boldsymbol{W}}^{(i)}_{k}}\mathcal{H}(\boldsymbol{x}(k),\boldsymbol{u}(k);\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(k))
, which results in an update rule for each layer similar to Eq. (
6
) with
\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(i)}_{k}
replaced by
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{k}^{(i)}
, and defined as in Eq. (
7

The training strategy called as cooperative or
coop
is described in the following and is illustrated in Fig.
1
. The training process is performed for a total of
M
episodes. At the start of episode
1
, we initialize the two NNs, i.e.,
Q_{1}
and
Q_{2}
. In each episode there are
C
plays. In each play, we provide a control action to the environment and we receive a tuple comprised of (reward, state, next-state). This tuple and the control action is stored in the experience replay buffer (denoted as
\mathcal{B}
). In this training strategy, each episode comprises in repetition of two phases. Phase 1: we first choose
Q_{2}
to be target (the target network) and
Q_{1}
to be the network model to be trained (the actor network); Phase 2: we choose
Q_{1}
to be the target network and
Q_{2}
to be the the actor network.

We first initiate phase 1, for each play, the actor network is used to gather control action. At the end of each play, the reward and the states are obtained from the environment and
Q_{1}
is repeatedly updated using the proposed updated rule. Once, the first phase is completed, we initiate phase 2. In phase 2,
Q_{2}
is used to provide control actions and the weights of
Q_{2}
are updated. We alternatively switch between phase 1 and phase 2 for every
C
plays. For each update, we sample a batch of data from the experience replay and use it to evaluate the error and update the weights. This process of learning reaches its culmination when the TD error is small, that is
Q_{1}
and
Q_{2}
have both reached a common location, where the cost is zero/minimum. At this point, there is no incentive for updating their weights and cooperative strategy has converged.

In the cooperative learning strategy, the two NNs are utilized to estimate the TD error. Therefore, the asymptotic convergence of
J_{E}
(Theorem 1) demonstrates the convergence of the TD error as
J_{E}
is squared TD error. We evaluate this learning strategy against the DQN benchmark (In our results, we refer to this as DQL) and the results are summarized in the next section.

VI
Conclusions

In this paper, a two DNN-driven learning-based control strategy is presented to synthesize feedback policies to steer systems generating feedback data in the form of images. Our approach provides the following advantages when compared to the traditional DQN-based RL strategy: (1) the two DNNs reduce the need for large buffer sizes, (2) the coop strategy improves convergence time for learning the
Q
-network. We also demonstrate the convergence of EDL algorithm. Despite significant improvements in comparison with the DQN, major challenges are still present in the context of employing the proposed approach for synthesizing learning-based online controls to steer the system as desired. For instance, a typical episodic RL setup is considered in this paper, where the RL agent learns iteratively. Secondly, the proposed strategy only considers discrete action space with only finitely many feasible control inputs.
