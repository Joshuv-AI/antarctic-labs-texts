Abstract
RCB stars are L ≈ 10 4 ​ L ⊙ L\approx 10^{4}\,L_{\odot} solar-mass objects that can exhibit large periods of extinction from dust ejection episodes. Many exhibit semiregular pulsations in the range of 30 − 50 30-50 days with semi-amplitudes of 0.05 − 0.3 0.05-0.3 magnitude. Space-based photometry has discovered that solar-like oscillations are ubiquitous in hydrogen-dominated stars that have substantial outer convective envelopes, so we explore the hypothesis that the pulsations in RCB stars and the closely related dustless hydrogen-deficient carbon (dLHdC) stars, which have large convective outer envelopes of nearly pure helium, have a similar origin. Through stellar modeling and pulsation calculations, we find that the observed periods and amplitudes of these pulsations follows the well-measured phenomenology of their H-rich brethren. In particular, we show that the observed modes are likely of angular orders l = 0 , 1 l=0,1 and 2 2 and predominantly of an acoustic nature (i.e. p p -modes with low radial order). The modes with largest amplitude are near the acoustic cut-off frequency appropriately rescaled to the helium-dominated envelope, and the observed amplitudes are consistent with that seen in high luminosity ( L > 10 3 ​ L ⊙ L>10^{3}\,L_{\odot} ) H-rich giants. We also find that for T eff ≳ 5400 ​ K T_{\mathrm{eff}}\gtrsim 5400\,\mathrm{K} , an HdC stellar model exhibits a radiative layer between two outer convective zones, creating a g g -mode cavity that supports much longer period ( ≈ 100 \approx 100 days) oscillations. Our initial work was focused primarily on the adiabatic modes, but we expect that subsequent space-based observations of these targets (e.g. with TESS or Plato) are likely to lead to a larger set of detected frequencies that would allow for a deeper study of the interiors of these rare stars.

Introduction

R Coronae Borealis (RCB) stars are a class of hydrogen-deficient carbon (HdC) stars
(
Clayton 1996

Clayton 2012

While RCB stars normally have

Closely related to RCB stars are dustless hydrogen-deficient carbon (dLHdC) stars, which show RCB-like spectra but do not show dust ejection episodes

In this work, we do not distinguish between RCB and dLHdC stars, but use HdC as an umbrella term encasing both.

Two formation channels are proposed for RCB stars: the double-degenerate merger scenario
(

Saio & Jeffery 2002

The former is favored in part because of the large ratio between

In addition, recent 3D

have shown good agreement between the nucleosynthesis and the observed abundances in RCB atmospheres.
Furthermore, the inferred number of galactic RCB stars

Karakas et al. 2015
, e.g.,)

It has been suggested that dLHdC stars are similarly formed through mergers, but with different mass ratios
(

Tisserand et al. 2022

Ground-based studies of RCB stars show semiregular variability with photometric semi-amplitudes between 0.05 mag and 0.3 mag and radial velocity semi-amplitudes between 10 and 20 km s

However,

Distinguishing between the effects of pulsations and surface convective plumes is an interesting observational question, but for the purpose of this paper, we proceed by interpreting periodic HdC variability as pulsations. The pulsation periods range from 20 days to
>100

Only 13 out of 32 dLHdC stars have detectable variability from ASAS-SN

Previous nonadiabatic calculations have suggested that the strange mode instability

We argue here that HdC pulsations are solar-like oscillations

First discovered in the Sun, solar-like oscillations exist in all subgiants and red giants up to and beyond the tip of the red giant branch

Discovery of solar-like oscillations has allowed for the asteroseismological derivation of a star’s mass and radius, as well as probes into its core structure

The power spectrum of solar-like oscillations consists of many sharp individual peaks whose heights follow a Gaussian-like envelope, with a frequency of maximum power,
\nu_{\mathrm{max}}
. Modes of the same angular degree but neighboring radial orders are separated by the large frequency separation,
\Delta\nu
. The former scales as the acoustic cutoff frequency,

\nu_{\mathrm{max}}\propto\nu_{\mathrm{ac}}\propto c_{\mathrm{s}}/H

(
Kjeldsen & Bedding 1995
, e.g.,)
, where
c_{\mathrm{s}}=\sqrt{\Gamma_{1}P/\rho}
is the adiabatic sound speed,
H=P/(\rho g)
is the pressure scale height, and
\Gamma_{1}
is the first adiabatic exponent.

HdC stars have neutral He-dominated atmospheres (mean molecular weight
\mu\approx 4

\mu\approx 1.3
for H-dominated stars. Since
c_{\mathrm{s}}\propto\sqrt{1/\mu}
and
H\propto P\propto 1/\mu
for an ideal gas
(
Yıldız et al. 2016
;
Viani et al. 2017
, e.g.,)
, we expect a correction factor
\sqrt{\mu/\mu_{\odot}}\approx\sqrt{4/1.3}\approx 1.75
for the solar-scaled
\nu_{\mathrm{max}}
relation when applied to HdC stars:

\nu_{\mathrm{max}}=0.41\,\mu\mathrm{Hz}\left(\frac{M}{0.7\,M_{\odot}}\right)\left(\frac{R}{100\,R_{\odot}}\right)^{-2}\left(\frac{T_{\mathrm{eff}}}{5000\,\mathrm{K}}\right)^{-1/2}.

(1)

which corresponds to a period of
30
d, in agreement with the observed period range for HdC stars.

For high radial order
p
-modes, the large frequency separation is given by

\Delta\nu\approx\left(2\int\frac{dr}{c_{\mathrm{s}}}\right)^{-1},

(2)

which for our models yield, to within 20%,

\Delta\nu=0.18\,\mu\mathrm{Hz}\left(\frac{M}{0.7\,M_{\odot}}\right)^{1/2}\left(\frac{R}{100R_{\odot}}\right)^{-3/2},

(3)

which is about
1.6
times the solar relation
1

1

For our fiducial model, eqn

\approx 1.4

.

These simple estimates imply that HdC stars would exhibit solar-like oscillations in the period range that is observed and with radial orders of

{\tt MESA}

We adopt the

Y=0.937
,

We adopt a mixing length parameter of

.

Our models mimic post-merger objects where the CO core has cooled after completing He burning. We accrete an envelope with the
Asplund et al. 2000

2\times 10^{7}\,\mathrm{K}
, and then inject entropy into the envelope to allow it to reach temperatures for He shell-burning and to expand to sizes typical for an HdC star.
We experiment with different mass combinations, with

0.25+0.55\,M_{\odot}

The outer surface boundary condition is taken to be zero surface pressure.

We take the inner boundary condition to be zero radial displacement. For a few models we explore the mixed gravito-acoustic modes with a full stellar model with a regularity inner boundary condition.

Our model names start with (initial He mass)+(initial CO mass), and if mass loss is involved, “ML” is added to the end.

\log_{10}(L/L_{\odot})\approx 3.95
).
Note that, as explored by
Schwab 2019
, the RCrB lifetime, total mass, and luminosity reached by the models sensitively depend on the mass loss prescription, with higher mass loss generally yielding a shorter lifetime, lower mass and lower luminosity.

Mode periods near
\nu_{\mathrm{max}}
are shown in the bottom row of Figure
7
.
Model
0.25+0.55
(left panel) shows longer periods than model
0.20+0.50
, and model
0.20+0.50
ML (right) shows shorter periods, since the global dynamical timescale
\tau_{\mathrm{dyn}}\propto\sqrt{R^{3}/M}
increases with radius.
Because of its scaling as
\propto R^{2}
, the period corresponding to
\nu_{\mathrm{max}}
shows a similar trend, with
\nu_{\mathrm{max}}
closest to the
n_{\mathrm{pg}}=2
mode in model
0.25+0.55
, and
n_{\mathrm{pg}}=3
mode in model
0.20+0.50
ML.

III.3
Summary of non-adiabatic effects

We investigate non-adiabatic effects for
l=0
modes in Appendix
A
.
Unlike previous works
(
Wood 1976
;
Weiss 1987
;
Gautschy et al. 1990
;
Saio et al. 1998
, e.g.,)
, our focus is not on the mechanism for self-excitation, but we are interested in how non-adiabaticity changes the real part of the frequency, particularly near
\nu_{\mathrm{max}}
.
For models
0.20+0.50
and
0.20+0.50
ML, the
n_{\mathrm{pg}}=2-4
mode frequencies near
\nu_{\mathrm{max}}
do not deviate from their adiabatic counterparts by more than
20\%
. At
T_{\mathrm{eff}}\lesssim 5500\,\mathrm{K}
where non-adiabatic effects are weak, the agreement is within 2%.
Model
0.25+0.55
behaves similarly at
T_{\mathrm{eff}}\lesssim 5500\,\mathrm{K}
. However, at higher
T_{\mathrm{eff}}
additional modes appear, and correspondence between the non-adiabatic and adiabatic modes becomes complicated.

IV
Conclusion

In this work, we argue that HdC pulsations are, by nature, solar-like oscillations resulting from a vigorously convecting envelope. We use the solar-scaled
\nu_{\mathrm{max}}
relation to He-rich HdC stars, with a
\mu
correction for He-rich compositions (Eqn
1
) to focus our work on those modes most likely of large amplitude.
We construct
{\tt MESA}
models with a
0.5-0.55\,M_{\odot}
CO core and
0.2-0.25\,M_{\odot}
He-rich envelopes, and perform adiabatic calculations for
l=0,1,2
using
{\tt GYRE}
.
We find that
\Delta\nu
is generally
1.4-2
times the solar relation, and suggest a new relation (Eqn
3
) that is
\approx 1.6
times the solar relation and applies within 20% for He-rich objects.
Modes near
\nu_{\mathrm{max}}
have radial orders
n_{\mathrm{pg}}=2-3
, which means a few modes are excited for HdC stars, in agreement with observations that only one or two periods are observed for HdC stars
(
Karambelkar et al. 2021
;
Percy 2023
, e.g.,)
.

Our
\nu_{\mathrm{max}}
corrected for He-rich compositions matches the
30-50
d periods typically found in RCB stars
(
Percy 2023
)
.
As HdC star models move to higher
T_{\mathrm{eff}}\gtrsim 5400\,\mathrm{K}
, a radiative zone appears in the otherwise fully convective envelope, which creates a
g
-mode cavity and may explain some of the longer periods (
\approx 100
days) observed in HdC stars
(
Lawson et al. 1990
, e.g.,)
.
As shown in Sec
III.2
, a lower mass HdC star has a lower luminosity due to its smaller core mass. Because of its smaller radius, its pulsation periods (and period corresponding to
\nu_{\mathrm{max}}
) are also shorter.
This points to a smaller mass for dLHdC stars, which have shorter pulsation periods as well as lower absolute magnitudes than RCB stars
(
Tisserand et al. 2022
,
\approx 10-40
days; )
.

Space-based observations of solar-like oscillations in sub-giants and giants
find a strong relation between the semi-amplitude of pulsations with the stellar luminosity,
extending all the way up the red giant branch
(
Huber et al. 2011
;
Yu et al. 2018
;
Yu et al. 2020
, e.g.,)
.
At the brightest end (e.g. at the tip of the RGB and beyond into the AGB)
these are fully convective, H-rich stars in the realms of the ground based studied
long-period variables
(
Mosser et al. 2013
;
Stello et al. 2014
;
Yu et al. 2020
, e.g.,)
.
Though we have little available phenomenology for the HdC stars, their
observed amplitudes (
0.05-0.3
mag) are consistent with comparably luminous H-rich counterparts
(
Yu et al. 2020
,
\approx 0.1
mag; )
,
giving us additional confidence in our solar-like oscillation hypothesis.
Whether the tendency of the observed modes in these luminous stars to be
preferentially dipolar
(
Stello et al. 2014
)
also proves true in the HdC realm remains to be seen.
HdC stars also have RV variability with peak-to-peak amplitude
\approx 10-20\,\mathrm{km}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}

(
Lawson & Cottrell 1997
;
Tisserand et al. 2023
, e.g.,)
. If interpreted as radial pulsations
(
Feast et al. 2019
, however, see)
, this would translate to a radial amplitude of
\delta R\approx\delta v/\omega\approx(10\,\mathrm{km}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1})/(2\pi/40\,\mathrm{d})\approx 8\,R_{\odot}
, which seems reasonable given that this is around 10% of the stellar radius.

In addition, dLHdC stars have lower absolute magnitudes than RCB stars with the same color and shorter pulsation periods
(
Tisserand et al. 2022
)
, which as our models show, hints that dLHdC stars may have lower masses.
If true, then dLHdC stars are ideal for studying pulsations in HdC stars, not just because they do not show dust ejection episodes, but also because non-adiabatic effects are less important.

Future photometric studies of HdC stars using e.g., TESS
(
Ricker et al. 2014
)
, Palomar Gattini IR
(
De et al. 2020
)
or Plato
(
Rauer et al. 2014
)
, may uncover more pulsation periods for HdC stars.
For example, due to revisits, TESS can observe some HdC stars over multiple pulsation periods. dLHdC stars are ideal targets for TESS since they show no declines and have shorter pulsation periods
(
Tisserand et al. 2022
,
\approx 10-40
days;)
which is shorter than or comparable to TESS observation of a single sector (27 days).
Determination of
\nu_{\mathrm{max}}
and
\Delta\nu
could then yield asteroseismic masses and radii for HdC stars.

We also perform non-adiabatic calculations for
l=0
. We find that at
T_{\mathrm{eff}}\lesssim 5500\,\mathrm{K}
, the mode frequencies do not deviate much from their adiabatic counterparts, particularly near
\nu_{\mathrm{max}}
. This remains true at higher
T_{\mathrm{eff}}
for models
0.20+0.50
and
0.20+0.50
ML, but additional modes appear for model
0.25+0.55
which complicates mode identification. Nevertheless, most HdC stars studied by
Crawford et al. 2023
have
T_{\mathrm{eff}}\lesssim 6500\,\mathrm{K}
, which means non-adiabatic effects are likely unimportant for them.

Finally, these 1D models have envelopes that are significantly radiation pressure-dominated, with radiation pressure accounting for
40-80
% of the total pressure. At the opacity peaks associated with the iron bump and the two helium ionization zones, the local luminosity is super-Eddington, creating a density inversion at the first helium ionization zone. Convection is likely inefficient in the sense of strong radiative losses. A 3D radiation hydrodynamic simulation similar to
Jiang et al. 2018
will provide much deeper insights into the envelope structure, and may furthermore clarify whether HdC variability is due to pulsations or surface convective plumes
(
Feast et al. 2019
, e.g.,)
.

Appendix A
Non-adiabatic effects

At low
T_{\mathrm{eff}}
(
\lesssim 5500\,\mathrm{K}
), the non-adiabatic modes show a clear correspondence with the adiabatic modes. The correspondence can be tracked on the complex frequency plane (i.e.,
\mathrm{Re}(\nu)
vs
\mathrm{Im}(\nu)
) by gradually scaling the local thermal timescale by a factor
\alpha_{\mathrm{th}}
, from a large number corresponding to the adiabatic limit, to
1
corresponding to the non-adiabatic limit
(
Saio et al. 1984
;
Gautschy et al. 1990
, e.g.,)
.
The low radial order (low frequency) non-adiabatic modes, especially those near
\nu_{\mathrm{max}}
, have real frequencies that agree well with their adiabatic counterparts, except for
n_{\mathrm{pg}}=1
(see later discussion).
For
n_{\mathrm{pg}}=2-4
, the agreement is within 2%.
Therefore, mode identification using adiabatic calculations should be sufficient for
l=0
modes with
\mathrm{Re}(\nu)\lesssim\nu_{\mathrm{max}}
.
Higher radial order modes have increasingly lower real frequencies relative to their adiabatic counterparts.
This is because as non-adiabaticity increases, the sound propagation time changes from the adiabatic limit to the isothermal limit, and hence becomes longer
(
Zinn et al. 2023
, e.g.,)
. With increasing radial order, heat exchange between neighboring temperature perturbations increases, enhancing the influence of non-adiabaticity.
For these high radial order modes, we find that non-adiabaticity increases the number of radial nodes, in agreement with
Goldstein & Townsend 2020
.

The picture becomes complicated as
T_{\mathrm{eff}}
increases (
\gtrsim 5500\,\mathrm{K}
).
We first discuss the
0.20+0.50\,M_{\odot}
merger models with and without mass loss.
Most non-adiabatic modes that have a correspondence with the adiabatic modes have
\mathrm{Re}(\nu)
that significantly deviate from their adiabatic counterparts and may approach that of another mode.
At very low frequencies (at or below that of the
n_{\mathrm{pg}}=1
mode), a number of modes with high mode inertia and/or high damping (
\mathrm{Im}(\nu)/\mathrm{Re}(\nu)\ll 0
) appear, which we speculate are secular in origin. In addition, we find a few damped modes that cross numerous other modes on a modal diagram (e.g.,
\mathrm{Re}(\nu)
vs
T_{\mathrm{eff}}
). These modes rapidly decrease in
\mathrm{Re}(\nu)
with
T_{\mathrm{eff}}
unlike the other modes, and we interpret these as the strange modes studied by
Wood 1976
;
Cox et al. 1980
;
Saio et al. 1984
. We do not further investigate the nature of these additional modes. Our present focus is on modes near
\nu_{\mathrm{max}}
. Intriguingly, the
n_{\mathrm{pg}}=3
non-adiabatic mode which is nearest to
\nu_{\mathrm{max}}
, shows
\lesssim 8\%
deviation from its adiabatic counterpart. The
n_{\mathrm{pg}}=1,2
non-adiabatic modes both increase in
\mathrm{Re}(\nu)
relative to their adiabatic counterparts, and the
n_{\mathrm{pg}}=4
mode shows a decrease. The relative change in
\mathrm{Re}(\nu)
for
n_{\mathrm{pg}}=2,4
is modest, increasing from
\lesssim 2\%
at
T_{\mathrm{eff}}\lesssim 5500\,\mathrm{K}
to
\approx 20\%
at
T_{\mathrm{eff}}\approx 8000\,\mathrm{K}
. Meanwhile,
\mathrm{Re}(\nu)
of the
n_{\mathrm{pg}}=1
mode increases up to
2-3
times starting from
T_{\mathrm{eff}}\approx 5000\,\mathrm{K}
. For the
0.25+0.55\,M_{\odot}
model, many additional modes appear, with comparable
\mathrm{Im}(\nu)
and mode inertia as the modes which have an adiabatic counterpart, making mode identification complicated.

In short, non-adiabatic effects are not significant for low
T_{\mathrm{eff}}
(
\lesssim 5500\,\mathrm{K}
), but at high
T_{\mathrm{eff}}
may change mode frequencies relative to their adiabatic counterparts or even introduce additional modes. However, mode frequencies near
\nu_{\mathrm{max}}
may still be well approximated by the adiabatic limit.
