A variation of Reynolds-Hurkens Paradox
Thierry Coquand, University of Gothenburg, Sweden

Abstract




Introduction

We present a variation of Hurkens paradox
[
9
]
, itself being a variation
of Reynolds “paradox”
[
11
]
, as used in
[
5
]
. We first explain
a related paradox in higher order logic, which can be seen as a variation of Russell’s paradox.
We then show how this paradox can be formulated in system
\lambda\mathsf{U}^{-}
. We finally argue that an analysis of the
computational behavior of this paradox requires to extend existing type systems with a first class
notion of definitions and head linear reductions, as advocated by N.G. de Bruijn
[
7
]
.

1
Some paradoxes in minimal Higher-Order logic

We first present some paradoxes in some extensions of the system
\lambda\mathsf{HOL}
, minimal Higher-Order logic,
described in
[
8
]
. This system can be seen as a minimal logic version of higher-order logic
introduced by A. Church
[
2
]
. With the notation of
[
8
]
, it has sorts
\mathsf{*},\square,\Delta

with
\mathsf{*}:\square
and
\square:\Delta
and the rules

(\mathsf{*},\mathsf{*}),~(\square,\square),~(\square,\mathsf{*})

We denote by
X,Y,\dots
types of this system.

We can define
\mathsf{Pow}:\square\rightarrow\square
by
\mathsf{Pow}~X=X\rightarrow\mathsf{*}

and
T:\square\rightarrow\square
by
T~X=\mathsf{Pow}~(\mathsf{Pow}~X)
.

Note that
T
defines a
judgmental
functor: if
f:X\rightarrow Y
we can define

T~f:T~X\rightarrow T~Y
by

T~f~F~q=F~(\lambda_{x:X}q~(f~x))

and we also have if furthermore
g:Y\rightarrow Z
the judgemental equality (here
\beta
-conversion
[
8
]
)

T~(g\circ f)=(T~g)\circ(T~f)
defining
g\circ f
as
\lambda_{x:X}g~(f~x)
.

We assume in this section to have a type
A:\square
together with two maps

\mathsf{intro}:T~A\rightarrow A
and
\mathsf{match}:A\rightarrow T~A
.

We explain now how to derive simple paradoxes assuming some convertibility properties of these maps.

1.1
A variation of Russell’s paradox

The first version is obtained by assuming that we have
\mathsf{match}~(\mathsf{intro}~u)
convertible to
u
, i.e.
T~A

is a judgemental retract of
A
.

Intuitively, we expect
\mathsf{Pow}~A
to be a retract of
T~A
, and this would imply that
\mathsf{Pow}~A
is a retract of
A

and we should be able to deduce a contradition by Russell’s paradox. One issue with this argument is that it holds
only using some form of
extensional
equalities, and we work in an intensional setting. One way to solve this
issue is to work with Partial Equivalence Relations; this is what was done in
[
5
]
. The work

[
9
]
, suggests that there should be a more direct way to express this idea, and this is what we present here.

The contradiction is obtained as follows. We first define a relation
C:\mathsf{Pow}~A\rightarrow\mathsf{Pow}~A

C~p~x~=~p~x\rightarrow\neg(\mathsf{match}~x~p)

where, as usual, we define
\perp:\mathsf{*}
by
\perp=\forall_{p:\mathsf{*}}p
and

\neg:\mathsf{*}\rightarrow\mathsf{*}
by
\neg~p=p\rightarrow\perp
.
We can then define
p_{0}:\mathsf{Pow}~A

p_{0}~x~=~\forall_{p:\mathsf{Pow}~A}C~p~x

We can also define
X_{0}:T~A

X_{0}~p~=~\forall_{x:A}C~p~x

and
x_{0}:A
as
x_{0}=\mathsf{intro}~X_{0}
. We can then build
l_{1}:X_{0}~p_{0}=\mathsf{match}~x_{0}~p_{0}

l_{1}~x~h~=~h~p_{0}~h

and
l_{2}:p_{0}~x_{0}
by

l_{2}~p~h~h_{1}~=~h_{1}~x_{0}~h~h_{1}

But this is a contradiction since
\mathsf{match}~x_{0}=\mathsf{match}~(\mathsf{intro}~X_{0})=X_{0}

by hypothesis, and hence
l_{2}~p_{0}~l_{2}~l_{1}
is of type
\perp
.

We can summarize this discussion as follows.

Theorem 1.1

In
\lambda\mathsf{HOL}
, we cannot have a type
A
such that
\mathsf{Pow}~(\mathsf{Pow}~A)
is a judgemental retract of
A
.

This can be seen as a variation of Russell/Cantor’s paradox, which states that
\mathsf{Pow}~A
cannot be a retract of
A
. Here we
state that
T~A
cannot be a retract of
A
.

1.2
A refinement

We define
\delta:A\rightarrow A
by
\delta=\mathsf{intro}\circ\mathsf{match}
and assume
the judgemental equality

\mathsf{match}\circ\mathsf{intro}=T~\delta

[formula]

which implies
\mathsf{match}~(\delta~x)~p=\mathsf{match}~x~(p\circ\delta)
.

We now (re)define

p_{0}:\mathsf{Pow}~A

p_{0}~x~=~\forall_{p:\mathsf{Pow}~A}~p~(\delta~x)\rightarrow\neg(\mathsf{match}~x~p)

and
X_{0}:T~A
as before

X_{0}~p~=~\forall_{x:A}~p~x\rightarrow\neg(\mathsf{match}~x~p)

and
x_{0}:A
as
x_{0}=\mathsf{intro}~X_{0}
. Using the judgemental equality
(1)
, it is possible to build

s_{1}:\forall_{x}~p_{0}~x\rightarrow p_{0}~(\delta~x)~~~~~~~~~~~~s_{2}:\forall_{p}~X_{0}~p\rightarrow X_{0}~(p\circ\delta)

by
s_{1}~x~h~p~=~h~(p\circ\delta)
and
s_{2}~p~h~x~=~h~(\delta~x)
.

We can now define
and
l_{0}:\forall_{p:\mathsf{Pow}~A}~p~x_{0}\rightarrow\neg(X_{0}~p)
by

l_{0}~p~h~h_{0}~=~h_{0}~x_{0}~h~(s_{2}~p~h_{0})

using
(1)
and
l_{1}:X_{0}~p_{0}
by

l_{1}~x~h~=~h~p_{0}~(s_{1}~x~h)

and
l_{2}:p_{0}~x_{0}
by
l_{2}~p=l_{0}~(p\circ\delta)
.

For this, we use the judgemental equality
\mathsf{match}~(\delta~x)~p=\mathsf{match}~x~(p\circ\delta)
, consequence of
(1)
.

We can then form the term
l_{0}~x_{0}~l_{2}~l_{1}
which is of type
\perp
.

We thus get the following result, using
T~X=\mathsf{Pow}~(\mathsf{Pow}~X)
.

Theorem 1.2

In
\lambda\mathsf{HOL}
, we cannot have a type
A
with two maps
\mathsf{intro}:T~A\rightarrow A
and

\mathsf{match}:A\rightarrow T~A
with
\mathsf{match}\circ\mathsf{intro}
convertible to
T~(\mathsf{intro}\circ\mathsf{match})
.

2
An encoding in
\lambda\mathsf{U}^{-}

2.1
Weak representation of data type

Using the notations of
[
8
]
the system
\lambda\mathsf{U}^{-}
has also sorts
\mathsf{*},\square,\Delta

with
\mathsf{*}:\square
and
\square:\Delta
and the rules

(\mathsf{*},\mathsf{*}),~(\square,\square),~(\square,\mathsf{*}),~(\Delta,\square)

We explain in this section why the refined paradox has a direct encoding
in the system
\lambda\mathsf{U}^{-}
.

As before,
T
defines a judgemental functor: if
f:X\rightarrow Y
we can define

T~f:T~X\rightarrow T~Y
by

T~f~F~q=F~(\lambda_{x:X}q~(f~x))

and we also have if furthermore
g:Y\rightarrow Z
the judgemental equality

T~(g\circ f)=(T~g)\circ(T~f)
defining
g\circ f
as
\lambda_{x:X}g~(f~x)
.

A
T
-algebra is a type
X:\square
together with a map
f:T~X\rightarrow X
.

Following Reynolds
[
11
,
12
]
, we represent
A:\square
by

A=\Pi_{X:\square}(T~X\rightarrow X)\rightarrow X

It can be seen as a weak representation of a data type. If we have
X:\square
and
f:T~X\rightarrow X

we can define
\iota~f:A\rightarrow X
by
\iota~f~a=a~X~f
. We can then define

\mathsf{intro}:T~A\rightarrow A
by
\mathsf{intro}~u~X~f=f~(T~(\iota~f))
, and we have the conversion

(\iota~f)\circ\mathsf{intro}=f\circ(T~(\iota~f))

[formula]

This expresses that the following diagram commutes strictly

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces T~A}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces T~X}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathsf{intro}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces T~(\iota~f)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(\iota~f)}

So
A,~\mathsf{intro}
represents a
weak
initial
T
-algebra.

We define next
\mathsf{match}:A\rightarrow T~A
by
\mathsf{match}=\iota~(T~\mathsf{intro})
.
Using the conversion
(2)
, we have

\mathsf{match}\circ\mathsf{intro}=(T~\mathsf{intro})\circ(T~\mathsf{match})=T~(\mathsf{intro}\circ\mathsf{match})

This is the required conversion
(1)
and we get in this way an encoding of Theorem
1.2
.

2.2
Some variations

In
[
9
]
, Hurkens uses instead

B=\Pi_{X:\square}(T~X\rightarrow X)\rightarrow T~X

[formula]

He then develops a short paradox using this type
B
, but with a different intuition, which comes from
Burali-Forti paradox. The variation we present in this note starts instead
from the remark that
T~A
cannot be a retract
of
A
. In
[
5
]
, we also use this idea, but with a more complex use of partial equivalence relations,
in order to build a strong initial
T
-algebra from a
weak initial
T
-algebra. This was following Reynolds’ informal argument in
[
11
]
,

The same argument from Theorem
1.2
can use the encoding

(3)
instead. We define then

\iota:\Pi_{X:\square}(T~X\rightarrow X)\rightarrow B\rightarrow X

by

\iota~X~f~b=f~(b~X~f)

and
\mathsf{intro}:T~B\rightarrow B
by

\mathsf{intro}~v~X~f=T~(\iota~f)~v

We then have the choice for defining
\mathsf{match}:B\rightarrow T~B
. We can use

\mathsf{match}=\iota~(T~B)~\mathsf{intro}

as before. Maybe surprisingly, we also can use

\mathsf{match}~b=b~B~\mathsf{intro}

In both cases, we get the judgemental equality
\mathsf{match}\circ\mathsf{intro}=T~(\mathsf{intro}\circ\mathsf{match})
required for the use
of Theorem
1.2
.

Family of looping combinators

The paradox corresponding to Theorem
1.2
does not produce a term that reduces to itself
however

l_{0}~p_{0}~l_{2}~l_{1}

\rightarrow

l_{1}~x_{0}~l_{2}~(s_{2}~p_{0}~l_{1})

\rightarrow

l_{2}~p_{0}~(s_{1}~x_{0}~l_{2})~(s_{2}~p_{0}~l_{1})

\rightarrow

l_{0}~(p_{0}\circ\delta)~(s_{1}~x_{0}~l_{2})~(s_{2}~p_{0}~l_{1})

\rightarrow

s_{2}~p_{0}~l_{1}~x_{0}~(s_{1}~x_{0}~l_{2})~(s_{2}~(p_{0}\circ\delta)~(s_{2}~p_{0}~l_{1}))

\rightarrow

l_{1}~(\delta~x_{0})~(s_{1}~x_{0}~l_{2})~(s_{2}~(p_{0}\circ\delta)~(s_{2}~p_{0}~l_{1}))

\rightarrow

\dots

Like for Hurkens’ paradox however, we obtain a term that reduces to itself if we forget types in

3.2
Definitions and Head linear Reduction

As discussed in
[
9
]
, using the notion of
definition
is essential, even for “small” terms,
for representing these paradoxes in an undertandable way. As was discovered in Automath
[
7
]
,
in a type system with
dependent
types, one cannot reduce definitions to abstractions and applications like in simply typed
lambda calculus. Indeed, the representation of

\mathsf{let}~x:A=e_{0}~\mathsf{in}~e_{1}

by
(\lambda_{x:A}e_{1})~e_{0}
can be incorrect, since the definition
x:A=e_{0}
can be used in the type-checking of
e_{1}
.

Furthermore, in order to understand the computational behavior of the paradox, the use of

head linear reduction
, which plays an important role in
[
7
]
, is convenient.
This is what was done when presenting above the computational behavior of various paradoxes, with a
periodic behavior for the first example and a non periodic behavior for the paradox in
\lambda\mathsf{U}^{-}
.
This use may also be relevant for understanding large proofs.
