Stochastic Epidemic SIR Models with Hidden States
Nguyen Du, Alexandru Hening, Nhu Nguyen, George Yin

Abstract
. This paper focuses on and analyzes realistic SIR models that take stochasticity into account. The proposed systems are applicable to most incidence rates that are used in the literature including the bilinear incidence rate, the Beddington-DeAngelis incidence rate, and a Holling type II functional response. Given that many diseases can lead to asymptomatic infections, we look at a system of stochastic differential equations that also includes a class of hidden state individuals, for which the infection status is unknown. We assume that the direct observation of the percentage of hidden state individuals that are infected, α ⁡ ( t ) \alpha(t) , is not given and only a noise-corrupted observation process is available. Using the nonlinear filtering techniques in conjunction with an invasion type analysis (or analysis using Lyapunov exponents from the dynamical system point of view), this paper proves that the long-term behavior of the disease is governed by a threshold λ ∈ ℝ \lambda\in\mathbb{R} that depends on the model parameters. It turns out that if λ < 0 \lambda<0 the number I ⁡ ( t ) I(t) of infected individuals converges to zero exponentially fast, or the extinction happens. In contrast, if λ > 0 \lambda>0 , the infection is endemic and the system is permanent. We showcase our results by applying them in specific illuminating examples. Numerical simulations are also given to illustrate our results.

1.
Introduction

The SIR epidemic models introduced first by
[
13
,
14
]
look at the dynamics of susceptible, infected, and recovered individuals, whose densities at the time
t
are denoted by
S(t)
,
I(t)
, and
R(t)
, respectively. In the absence of random effects,
the dynamics are
described
by the following system of differential equations

(1.1)

\begin{cases}dS(t)=\big[a_{1}-\mu_{S}S(t)-F(S(t),I(t))\big]dt,\\
dI(t)=\big[-(\mu_{I}+r)I(t)+F(S(t),I(t)\big]dt,\\
dR(t)=\big[-\mu_{R}R(t)+rI(t)\big]dt.\end{cases}

Here
a_{1}>0
is the recruitment rate of the population,
\mu_{S},\mu_{I},\mu_{R}>0
are the death rates of the susceptible, infected, and recovered individuals,
r>0
is the recovery rate of the infected individuals and
F(S(t),I(t))
is the incidence rate.
The dynamics of recovered individuals have no effect on that of the disease transmission.
As a result, it is the usual practice not to consider the recovered individuals as part of the problem formulation. We adopt this practice
throughout this paper.
Various types of incidence rates
F(S,I)
have been considered in the literature, for example,
the Holling type II functional response
F(S,I)=\frac{\beta SI}{m_{1}+S}
(see
[
10
]
), the bilinear functional response
F(S,I)=\beta SI
(see
[
2
,
33
]
), the nonlinear functional response
F(S,I)=\frac{\beta SI^{l}}{1+m_{2}I^{h}}
(see
[
26
,
30
]
),
and the Beddington-DeAngelis functional response
F(S,I)=\frac{\beta SI}{1+m_{1}S+m_{2}I}
(see
[
3
,
4
]
).

It is by now widely known that in order
to have
a realistic model, one cannot ignore random environmental fluctuations (temperature, the climate, the water resources, etc.). In this paper we consider stochastic epidemic models of the form

(1.2)

\begin{cases}dS(t)=\big[a_{1}-b_{1}S(t)-I(t)f(S(t),I(t))\big]dt+\sigma_{1}S(t)dB_{1}(t),\\
dI(t)=\big[-b_{2}I(t)+I(t)f(S(t),I(t))\big]dt+\sigma_{2}I(t)dB_{2}(t),\\
\end{cases}

where
B_{1}(t)
and
B_{2}(t)
are independent Brownian motions and
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}\neq 0
are the noise intensities (standard deviations).
Moreover, in the above we have rewritten the coefficients:
b_{1}:=\mu_{S},b_{2}:=\mu_{I}+r
and
F(S,I)=If(S,I)
(compare this to (
1.1
)). This system has been analyzed in a
general setting in
[
5
]
.

It is well-known that there are diseases for which certain infected individuals
are asymptomatic.
Covid-19 is one such example – there have been many reports of infections where the infected exhibit no symptoms. We intend to capture this type of behavior in our model.
In order to do this, we assume that
the group of infected individuals that has incidence rate
I(t)f(S(t),I(t))
(the rate that describes how the disease spreads from infected groups to susceptible groups) in the classical setting, now contains 2 sub-groups. The first group contains individuals who have been confirmed to be infected and
with the incidence rate
I(t)f_{1}(S(t),I(t))
(we will still denote this incidence rate by
I(t)f(S(t),I(t))

for
notational simplicity).
The second group has incidence rate
I(t)h(S(t),I(t))
and contains people whose
infection status is unknown or hidden.
Let
\alpha(t)
be a Markov process
taking values in
\mathcal{M}=[0,1]
. We suppose that
\alpha(t)

represents the percentage of
individuals in
the hidden-status class
that are infected at time
t\geq 0

and that only noise-corrupted observations of

\alpha(t)

are
available. More specifically, one can only observe
\alpha(t)
with additive white noise.

It is natural to assume that the hidden status of potentially infected individuals affects the spread of the disease. As a result,
we let
the functions
f
and
h

depend on
\alpha(t)
.
With
the hidden state dynamics in (
1.2
), we obtain

(1.3)

\begin{cases}dS(t)=\Big[a_{1}-b_{1}S(t)-I(t)f(\alpha(t),S(t),I(t))-\alpha(t)I(t)h(\alpha(t),S(t),I(t))\Big]dt+\sigma_{1}S(t)dB_{1}(t),\\[4.30554pt]
dI(t)=\Big[-b_{2}I(t)+I(t)f(\alpha(t),S(t),I(t))+\alpha(t)I(t)h(\alpha(t),S(t),I(t))\Big]dt+\sigma_{2}I(t)dB_{2}(t).\end{cases}

Remark 1
.

One can understand the dynamics by looking at individuals from group
S
, in which
susceptible individuals are infected at the rate
If(S,I)
.
We assume that
\alpha(t)
percent of the rate of the potentially infected individuals are actually infected. Then we can say that members of the hidden group infect susceptible individuals at the rate
\alpha Ih(S,I)
.

Remark 2
.

We could combine
I(t)f(\alpha(t),S(t),I(t))+\alpha(t)I(t)h(\alpha(t),S(t),I(t))
to
produce a new function. However, we choose the current setup
to make
the formulation and motivation clear. Moreover, this will also be more convenient for later discussions.

Our results can be summarized as follows.
Because the infection status of certain individuals is hidden,
and

\alpha(t)
is not directly available,
the dynamics of (
1.3
) are difficult to study.
To overcome the
difficulty, we apply the nonlinear filtering techniques by considering the conditional distribution of the process
\alpha(t)
given the observations. This enables us to
replace the hidden Markov process
\alpha(t)
in
(
2.3
)
by

We start by studying the well-posedness of the equation under consideration together with the positivity of solutions, the Markov-Feller property, and some moment estimates. Next, we study the longtime behavior of the system.
Under the assumption for ergodicity of nonlinear filtering
[
8
,
16
]
and using ideas from dynamical systems, by considering the boundary equation and growth rate (see e.g.,
[

,

there is a threshold

such that if
\lambda<0
, the number of the infected individuals

tends to zero

and if
\lambda>0
, all invariant probability measures of the system concentrate on
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2,\circ}:=(0,\infty)^{2}
, and then the systems is permanent.
We show that the threshold
\lambda
also characterizes
the permanence and extinction of the system (
1.3
).
We also study the case when the process
\alpha(t)
is a hidden Markov chain
taking values in a finite set.
Next, we demonstrate our results using
simple examples and numerical simulations.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. We give the mathematical formulation of our problem in Section
2
.
Section
3

Section

Finally, Section

Formulation

Throughout this paper we use

\mathbb{R}_{+}:=[0,\infty)
,
\mathbb{R}^{\circ}_{+}:=(0,\infty)
,

\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}:=[0,\infty)\times[0,\infty)

\mathbb{R}^{2,\circ}_{+}:=(0,\infty)\times(0,\infty)

Let

.
Moreover,

For any function

As discussed above, we consider the setting where
the precise values

\alpha(t)
are not available and only noisy observations are available.
The observation process

of the signal process
\alpha(t)

is given by

(2.1)

dy(t)=g(\alpha(t))dt+dW(t),\quad y(0)=0,

where

Let

Let

Such

The field of nonlinear filtering has a long history.
The main idea stems from replacing the unknown state by its conditional distributions. The earliest result was the well-known Kushner’s equation

[
17
]
. Subsequently, the Duncan-Mortensen-Zakai equation came into being

[
6
,
22
,
31

7

[
29
]

To proceed,
we detail the results of

[
7
]
(see also
[
12
]
), which involve
a differential equation for the nonlinear filtering

, next.
Define

and note that the process

is a one-dimensional Wiener process, see e.g.,
[
18
, Theorem 7.2]
.
Moreover,
\sigma\{\beta(t_{2})-\beta(t_{2}):t_{2}\geq t_{1}\geq t\}
and

are independent for all

If the signal process
\alpha(t)
is a Markov process with infinitesimal generator

, then

is the solution of

(2.2)

\Pi_{t}(l)=\Pi_{0}(l)+\int_{0}^{t}\Pi_{s}(Al)ds+\int_{0}^{t}\left(\Pi_{s}(lg)-\Pi_{s}(l)\Pi_{s}(g)\right)d\beta(s),\quad\forall l\in\mathcal{D}(A).

The interested reader is referred to the detailed analysis given in

We will not make use of (

The stochastic differential equation for

is
rather complex and is not the main concern of the current paper.
As will be seen in the next section, we only need
to establish the related ergodicity. Thus,
for us, it suffices to consider

\Pi_{t}(\cdot)
as a stochastic process taking values in
\mathcal{M}
.
In addition,
we use
continuous measurable modification of

such a modification always exists

Under the premise that

\begin{cases}dS(t)=\Big[a_{1}-b_{1}S(t)-\int_{0}^{1}I(t)f(x,S(t),I(t))\Pi_{t}(dx)\\

\displaystyle\Pi_{t}(A)=\mathbb{P}[\alpha(t)\in A|\mathcal{F}_{t}^{y}],\forall A\in\mathcal{B}([0,1]),

\displaystyle dy(t)=g(\alpha(t))dt+dW(t),\quad y(0)=0.

Assumption 2.1
.

The following conditions hold:

•

The function

•

, i.e., for all

•

For each

, functions

and

Remark 3
.

We note that almost all of the incidence rate functions used in the literature (such as the bilinear incidence rate, the Beddington-DeAngelis incidence rate, the Holling type II functional response, etc.) satisfy these conditions.
Recall that the incidence rate in our setting is

rather than

.

The third condition is imposed because the incidence rate and the growth rate of the hidden class should increase when

and

Assumption 2.2
.

The signal process

is a Markov-Feller process that has a unique invariant measure

and

\|P(t,x,\cdot)-\mu^{*}(\cdot)\|_{\text{TV}}=0,

where

is the transition probability and

Remark 4
.

This assumption is needed to guarantee the ergodicity of the nonlinear filtering as discussed in Section

Using this, we can then define a threshold that fully characterizes the longtime behavior of the underlying system; see Section

Discrete state space and Wonham filter.

If the Markov process

Let

\displaystyle e_{k}(t):=\mathbb{P}(\alpha(t)=m_{k}|\mathcal{F}_{t}^{y})=\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{1}_{\{\alpha(t)=m_{k}\}}|\mathcal{F}_{t}^{y}],k=1,\dots,n^{*},

\displaystyle e(t)=(e_{1}(t),\dots,e_{n^{*}}(t)),

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{n^{*}}:=\left\{e=(e_{1},\dots,e_{n^{*}})\in\mathbb{R}^{n^{*}}:e_{k}\geq 0,\sum_{k=1}^{n^{*}}e_{k}=1\right\},

\displaystyle g_{k}:=g(m_{k}),k=1,\dots,n^{*},\quad\overline{g}(e):=\sum_{k=1}^{n^{*}}g_{k}e_{k},e=(e_{1},\dots,e_{n^{*}})\in\mathcal{S}_{n^{*}}.

It was shown in
[
29
]
that the posterior probability

satisfies the following system of stochastic differential equations

\begin{cases}de_{k}(t)=\displaystyle\!\left[\!\sum_{i=1}^{n^{*}}q_{ik}e_{i}(t)-(g_{k}-\overline{g}(e(t)))\overline{g}(e(t))e_{k}(t)\!\!\right]\!\!dt\!\!+\!(g_{k}-\overline{g}(e(t)))e_{k}(t)dy(t),\ k=1,\dots,n^{*},\\

3.
Ergodicty of Nonlinear Filter and Threshold for Permanence and Extinction

3.1.
Ergodicity of Nonlinear Filter

The study of the asymptotic properties of the nonlinear filter has a long history in the literature. We briefly summarize the developments. One of the first works is Kunita’s paper
[
16
]
.
We restate the main result (Theorem 3.3) of this reference as follows.

Proposition 3.1
.

(
Kunita 1971
)

Assume that the signal process
\alpha(t)
taking values in a compact separable Hausdorff space is a Markov-Feller process with semigroup
P_{t}
that has a unique invariant measure
\mu^{*}
and

\limsup_{t\to\infty}\int|P_{t}l(x)-\mu^{*}(l)|\mu(dx)=0,\forall l\in C([0,1]).

Then the process
\Pi_{t}(\cdot)
is an
\mathcal{M}
-valued Markov-Feller process that has unique invariant measure
\Phi^{*}
. Moreover,
\mu^{*}
is the barycenter of
\Phi^{*}
, i.e.,

\mu^{*}(l)=\int_{\mathcal{M}}\nu(l)\Phi^{*}(d\nu),\forall l\in C[0,1].

Unfortunately, it was pointed out in
[
1
]
that there was a serious gap in the proof of the main result in
[
16
]
. A key role in the verification of the uniqueness for the invariant measure of
\Pi_{t}(\cdot)
is the following identity

(3.1)

\bigcap_{t\geq 0}\mathcal{F}_{[0,\infty)}^{y}\bigvee\sigma\{\alpha(s):s\geq t\}=\mathcal{F}_{[0,\infty)}^{y}\bigvee\left(\cap_{t\geq 0}\sigma\{\alpha(s):s\geq t\}\right),

where
\mathcal{F}_{[0,\infty)}^{y}:=\bigvee_{t\geq 0}\mathcal{F}_{t}^{y}
.
This identity is indispensable in the proof of the uniqueness of the invariant measure of nonlinear filtering; see the counterexample given in
[
1
]
.
Moreover, the exchange of intersection and supremum is not always permitted in general

Proposition 3.2
.

Under Assumption
2.2
, the process
\Pi_{t}(\cdot)
is an
\mathcal{M}
-valued Markov-Feller process and has a unique invariant measure
\Phi^{*}
. Moreover,
\mu^{*}
is the barycenter of
\Phi^{*}
, i.e.,

\mu^{*}(l)=\int_{\mathcal{M}}\nu(l)\Phi^{*}(d\nu),\forall l\in C[0,1].

Moreover, let
\mathcal{M}_{\Phi^{*}}\subset\mathcal{M}
be the support of the invariant measure
\Phi^{*}
of the nonlinear filter
\Pi_{t}(\cdot)
. In general, one should not expect that
\mathcal{M}_{\Phi^{*}}=\mathcal{M}
. In fact, this does not hold even in the simple setting of the Wonham filter when the state space has only 3 states
[
25
, Section 4]
.

3.2.
Threshold for Permanence and Extinction

We next use the ergodicity of the nonlinear filter developed in the previous section in conjunction with a Lyapunov exponent analysis (sometimes called invasion type analysis in population dynamics) coming from dynamical systems
[
2
,
5
,
9
]
. This allows us to introduce a threshold
\lambda
, which characterizes the longtime behavior of system (
2.3
).

Consider the equation on boundary when the infected individuals are absent, i.e.,

(3.2)

d\varphi(t)=\big(a_{1}-b_{1}\varphi(t)\big)dt+\sigma_{1}\varphi(t)dB_{1}(t),\;\;\;\varphi(0)=u\geq 0.

By solving the Fokker-Planck equation, equation (
3.2
) has
a unique stationary distribution
\widehat{\mu}
with the density given by

(3.3)

\dfrac{b^{a}}{\Gamma(a)}y^{-(a+1)}e^{-\frac{b}{y}},\;y>0,

where
c_{1}=b_{1}+\dfrac{\sigma_{1}^{2}}{2},a=\dfrac{2c_{1}}{\sigma_{1}^{2}},b=\dfrac{2a_{1}}{\sigma_{1}^{2}}
and

\Gamma(\cdot)
is the Gamma function.
The main idea is to determine whether
I(t)
converges to 0 or not by
looking at the Lyapunov exponent
\limsup_{t\to\infty}\dfrac{\ln I(t)}{t}
when
I(t)
is small. Using Itô’s formula yields

(3.4)

\displaystyle\dfrac{\ln I(t)}{t}=

\displaystyle\dfrac{\ln v}{t}+\dfrac{\sigma_{2}B_{2}(t)}{t}-c_{2}+\dfrac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{1}f(x,S(s),I(s))\Pi_{s}(dx)ds

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{1}xh(x,S(s),I(s))\Pi_{s}(dx)ds,

where
c_{2}=b_{2}+\dfrac{\sigma_{2}^{2}}{2}.

Intuitively,
\limsup_{t\to\infty}\frac{\ln I(t)}{t}<0
implies
\lim_{t\to\infty}I(t)=0
. As a result, if
I(t)
is small then
S(t)
is close to
\varphi(t)
provided
S(0)=\varphi(0)
. Therefore, when
t
is sufficiently large we have

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{t}

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{1}f(x,S(s),I(s))\Pi_{s}(dx)ds+\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{1}xh(x,S(s),I(s))\Pi_{s}(dx)ds

\displaystyle\approx\dfrac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{1}f(x,\varphi(s),0)\Pi_{s}(dx)ds+\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{1}xh(x,\varphi(s),0)\Pi_{s}(dx)ds.

By the strong law of large numbers for
\varphi(t)
and
\Pi_{t}
from (
3.4
), we obtain that the Lyapunov exponent of
I_{u,v}(t)
can be
approximated by

(3.5)

-c_{2}+\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathcal{M}}\left(f(x,y,0)\nu(dx)\right)\Phi^{*}(d\nu)\widehat{\mu}(dy)+\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathcal{M}}\left(\int_{0}^{1}xh(x,y,0)\nu(dx)\right)\Phi^{*}(d\nu)\widehat{\mu}(dy).

Since
\mu^{*}
is the barycenter of
\Phi^{*}
, the Lyapunov exponent of
I(t)
is approximated by

-c_{2}+\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{1}f(x,y,0)\mu^{*}(dx)\widehat{\mu}(dy)+\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{1}xh(x,y,0)\mu^{*}(dx)\widehat{\mu}(y).

Therefore, we define the threshold
\lambda
by

(3.6)

\lambda:=-c_{2}+\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{1}f(x,y,0)\mu^{*}(dx)\widehat{\mu}(dy)+\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{1}xh(x,y,0)\mu^{*}(dx)\widehat{\mu}(dy).

In the next section, we
prove that the sign of
\lambda

characterizes the longtime behavior of the system (
2.3
).
It is also noted that when
\alpha(t)
is available,
so is (
1.3
), and the permanence or extinction of (
1.3
) is also determined by the sign of
\lambda
defined in (
3.6
) (see e.g.,
[
24
]
).

4.
Characterization of Longtime Properties: Permanence and Extinction

4.1.
The existence and uniqueness of the solution and preliminary results

We begin with the following theorem on the existence and uniqueness of the solution of (
2.3
)
and then proceed with a complete characterization of its positivity and some other important properties.

Theorem 4.1
.

For any
(u,v,\pi)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}\times\mathcal{M}
, there exists a unique global solution
to
system (
2.3
) with initial value
(u,v,\pi)
.
The three-component process
\{(S(t),I(t),\Pi_{t}),t\geq 0\}

is a Markov process.

Proof.

We prove the existence and uniqueness of the solution of (
2.3
) first.
It is noted that although we have assumed
f(s,i)
is Lipschitz continuous, the coefficient
if(s,i)
in the system (
2.3
) is not globally Lipschitz in general.
Since the coefficients of the equation are locally Lipschitz continuous, there is a unique solution
(S(t),I(t))
with the initial value
(u,v,\pi)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}\times\mathcal{M}
, defined on maximal interval
t\in[0,\tau_{e})
,
\tau_{e}:=\inf\{t\geq 0:S(t)\vee I(t)=\infty\}
with the convention
\inf\emptyset=\infty
; see e.g.,
[
20
, Theorem 3.8 and Remark 3.10]
.
We need to show
\tau_{e}=\infty
a.s.
If we define

\tau_{k}=\inf\Big\{t\geq 0:S(t)\vee I(t)>k\Big\},

then
\tau_{e}=\lim_{k\to\infty}\tau_{k}
.
Consider
V_{1}(s,i)=s+i
, then
we have from definition of
\mathcal{L}V_{1}

that

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}V_{1}(s,i,\pi)

\displaystyle=a_{1}-b_{1}s-b_{2}i\leq a_{1}\;\forall(s,i,\pi)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}\times\mathcal{M}.

Hence, by applying Itô’s formula and taking expectation, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{u,v,\pi}V_{1}\big(S(\tau_{k}\wedge t),I(\tau_{k}\wedge t)\big)\leq V_{1}(u,v)+a_{1}t,

which together with Markov’s inequality implies that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{u,v,\pi}\big\{\tau_{k}<t\big\}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}_{u,v,\pi}\Big\{V_{1}\big(S(\tau_{k}\wedge t),I(\tau_{k}\wedge t),\alpha(\tau_{k}\wedge t)\big)\geq k\Big\}

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{V_{1}(u,v)+a_{1}t}{k}\to 0\;\text{as}\;k\to\infty.

Therefore, we have
\mathbb{P}_{u,v,\pi}\{\tau_{e}\leq t\}=0
or
\mathbb{P}_{u,v,\pi}\{\tau_{e}>t\}=1
for all
t>0
. As a consequence,
\mathbb{P}_{u,v,\pi}\{\tau_{e}=\infty\}=1
. Hence, system (
2.3
) has a unique, global, and continuous solution.

We proceed
to prove the Markov property. Since
\Pi_{t}
is a Markov process and is independent of
B_{1}(t)
and
B_{2}(t)
, the Markov property of the joint process
(S(t),I(t),\Pi_{t})
follows by standard arguments; see
for example,

[
20
, Theorem 3.27 and Lemma 3.2]
or
[
7
, Lemma 6.1]
. To see why the argument in
[
20
]
can be applied,
note that
\Pi_{t}
satisfies the stochastic equation (
2.2
) driven by
\beta(t)
and the
\sigma
-algebra generated by increments
\{\beta(t_{2})-\beta(t_{1}):t_{2}\geq t_{1}\geq t\}
is independent of
\mathcal{F}_{t}^{y}
.
∎

Next, using Lyapunov functions, we estimate the moments of
S(t)
and
I(t)
, and obtain some related results. Define
\sigma_{*}^{2}:=\max\{\sigma_{1}^{2},\sigma_{2}^{2}\}
.

Lemma 4.1
.

The following assertions hold:

(i)

For any
0<p<\frac{2\kappa}{\sigma_{*}^{2}}
there is a constant
Q_{1}
such that

\limsup\limits_{t\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{u,v,\pi}(S(t)+I(t))^{1+p}\leq Q_{1}\;\;\forall(u,v,\pi)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}\times\mathcal{M}.

(ii)

For any
\varepsilon>0
,
H>1
,
T>0
, there is
\overline{H}=\overline{H}(\varepsilon,H,T)
such that

\mathbb{P}_{u,v,\pi}\left\{\dfrac{1}{\overline{H}}\leq S(t)\leq\overline{H},\;\;\forall t\in[0,T]\right\}\geq 1-\varepsilon\;\;\text{if}\;\;\;(u,v,\pi)\in[H^{-1},H]\times[0;H]\times\mathcal{M},

and

\mathbb{P}_{u,v,\pi}\{0\leq S(t),I(t)\leq\overline{H},\;\;\forall t\in[0,T]\}\geq 1-\varepsilon\;\;\text{if}\;\;\;(u,v,\pi)\in[0,H]\times[0;H]\times\mathcal{M}.

Proof.

Consider the Lyapunov function

V_{3}(s,i)=(s+i)^{1+p}.

By directly calculation with
the differential operator
\mathcal{L}V_{3}
and using Assumption
2.1
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}V_{3}(s,i,\pi)=

\displaystyle(1+p)(s+i)^{p}(a_{1}-b_{1}s-b_{2}i)+\frac{p(1+p)}{2}(s+i)^{p-1}\Big(\sigma_{1}^{2}s^{2}+\sigma_{2}^{2}i^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle-(1+p)(s+i)^{p-1}\Big[-\frac{p}{2}\sigma_{*}^{2}(s+i)^{2}-a_{1}(s+i)\big],\;\forall(s,i,\pi)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}\times\mathcal{M}.

Let

0<C_{4}<\frac{p(1+p)}{2}\sigma_{*}^{2}
. By some standard calculations, we get

C_{5}=\sup_{(s,i,\pi)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}\times\mathcal{M}}\left\{\mathcal{L}V_{3}(s,i,\pi)+C_{4}V_{3}(s,i)\right\}<\infty.

This implies

(4.1)

\mathcal{L}V_{3}\leq C_{5}-C_{4}V_{3}.

Applying
[
19
, Theorem 5.2, p.157]
proves part (i) of the lemma. The proof of part (ii) follows
from
part (i) and standard arguments;
see
[
5
, Lemma 2.1]
.

∎

Theorem 4.2
.

The
process
(S(t),I(t),\Pi_{t})
is a strong Markov and Feller process.
Moreover, we have

\mathbb{P}_{u,v,\pi}\{S(t)>0,t>0\}=1
and

\mathbb{P}_{u,0,\pi}\{I(t)=0,t>0\}=1
,

\mathbb{P}_{u,v,\pi}\{I(t)>0,t>0\}=1
provided
v>0
.

Proof.

It is easily seen that
the solution of (
2.3
) is a homogeneous strong Markov and Feller process provided that the coefficients are globally
Lipschitz; see e.g.,
[
19
, Theorem 2.9.3]
and
[
32
, Section 2.5]
.
It is noted that the space
\mathcal{M}
of probability measures in
[0,1]
endowed with the weak topology can be metricized by the bounded Lipschitz metric defined by

\|\pi_{1}-\pi_{2}\|_{\text{BL}}:=\sup\left\{|\pi_{1}(l)-\pi_{2}(l)|:\|l\|\leq 1,\sup_{x\neq y\in[0,1]}\frac{|l(x)-l(y)|}{|x-y|}\leq 1\right\}.

Therefore, by using the results in Lemma
4.1
, we obtain from the local Lipschitz property of coefficients of (
2.3
) and a truncation
argument that
(S(t),I(t),\Pi_{t})
is a homogeneous strong Markov and Feller process.
The details of this truncated argument and this result can be found in
[
23
, Theorem 5.1]
.

Next, we establish the positivity of solutions. First, suppose that
u,v>0
.
Let us consider the Lyapunov function
V_{2}:\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}

V_{2}(s,i)=\left(s-1-\ln s\right)+(i-1-\ln i).

By direct calculations, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}V_{2}(s,i,\pi)

\displaystyle=\left(1-\frac{1}{s}\right)\left(a_{1}-b_{1}s-if(x,s,i)-i\int_{0}^{1}xh(x,s,i)\pi(dx)\right)+\frac{\sigma_{1}^{2}s^{2}}{2s^{2}}

\displaystyle+\left(1-\frac{1}{i}\right)\left(-b_{2}i+if(x,s,i)+i\int_{0}^{1}xh(x,s,i)\pi(dx)\right)+\frac{\sigma_{2}^{2}i^{2}}{2i^{2}}.

It follows from Assumption
2.1
that

f(x,s,i)=\left|f(x,s,i)-f(x,0,i)\right|\leq L_{1}s
and

h(x,s,i)=\left|h(x,s,i)-h(x,0,i)\right|\leq L_{2}s
.
Therefore, it is easily seen that

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}V_{2}(s,i)

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}+\frac{f(s,i)i}{s}+\frac{i\int_{0}^{1}h(x,s,i)\pi(dx)}{s}

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}+(L_{1}+L_{2})(s+i),

where
C_{1}=a_{1}+b_{1}+\frac{\sigma_{1}^{2}}{2}+b_{2}+\frac{\sigma_{2}^{2}}{2}.

As a result, if we let
C_{2}=L_{1}+L_{2}+1
and
C_{3}=C_{1}+2C_{2}\ln C_{2}+2C_{2}
then

(4.2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}V_{2}(s,i,\pi)-C_{2}V(s,i)

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}-s-i+C_{2}(\ln s+\ln i)+2C_{2}

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}+2C_{2}\ln C_{2}+2C_{2}=C_{3}.

For
k>1
, denote

\eta=\inf\big\{t\geq 0:S(t)\wedge I(t)\leq 0\big\},

\eta_{k}=\inf\Big\{t\geq 0:S(t)\wedge I(t)<\dfrac{1}{k}\Big\}.

Then
\eta=\lim_{k\to\infty}\eta_{k}
.
Therefore, by using the same argument as above, we obtain from (
4.2
) that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{u,v,\pi}\{\eta_{k}<t\}\leq\dfrac{V_{2}(u,v)+C_{3}t}{e^{-C_{2}t}(\ln k-1)}\to 0\;\text{as}\;k\to\infty.

As a result,
\mathbb{P}_{u,v,\pi}\{\eta_{\infty}=\infty\}=1
. This implies that

(4.3)

\mathbb{P}_{u,v,\pi}\big\{S(t)>0:t>0\big\}=\mathbb{P}_{u,v,\pi}\big\{I(t)>0:t>0\big\}=1\;\forall u,v>0.

If
u>0,v=0
, the result
\mathbb{P}_{u,v,\pi}\big\{S(t)>0:t>0\big\}=1
can be shown similarly. Moreover, it is obvious that
\mathbb{P}_{u,0,\pi}\big\{I(t)=0:t>0\big\}=1
.

We are in a position
to consider the case
u=0
and
v\geq 0
and prove the positivity of
S(t)
.
Let
\varepsilon>0
be sufficiently small such that

(4.4)

a_{1}-b_{1}\tilde{u}-\tilde{v}\sup_{x\in[0,1]}\big(f(x,\tilde{u},\tilde{v})+h(x,\tilde{u},\tilde{v})\big)\geq\frac{a_{1}}{2},

for any
(\tilde{u},\tilde{v})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
satisfying

\tilde{u}+|\tilde{v}-v|<\varepsilon
.
Such an
\varepsilon
exists due to Assumption
2.1
.
Set

\tilde{\tau}_{1}=\inf\{t>0:S(t)+|I(t)-v|\geq\varepsilon\}.

By the continuity of
(S(t),I(t))
it is clear that
\mathbb{P}_{0,v,\pi}\{\tilde{\tau}_{1}>0\}=1
.
It follows
from (
4.4
) that

a_{1}-b_{1}S(t)-I(t)\int_{0}^{1}f(x,S(t),I(t))\Pi_{t}(dx)+I(t)\int_{0}^{1}xh(x,S(t),I(t))\Pi_{t}(dx)>0\;\text{if}\;t\in(0,\tilde{\tau}_{1}].

This and the variation of constants formula (see
[
19
, Chapter 3]
)
imply that

\mathbb{P}_{0,v,\pi}\{S(t)>0,t\in(0,\tilde{\tau}_{1}]\}=1,

which combined with (
4.3
)
and the strong Markov property of
(S(t),X(t),\Pi(t))

yields that

\mathbb{P}_{0,v,\pi}\{S(t)>0,t\in(0,\infty)\}=1.

The theorem is therefore proved.
∎

4.2.
Extinction

Consider the case

\lambda<0
.
We shall show that the number of the infected individuals
I(t)
tends to zero at an exponential rate while the number of the susceptible individuals
S(t)
converges to
\varphi(t)
.

Theorem 4.3
.

Assume that
\lambda<0
. Then for any initial point
(u,v,\pi)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2,\circ}\times\mathcal{M}
,
the number of the infected individuals
I(t)
tends to zero at an exponential rate, i.e.,

\mathbb{P}_{u,v,\pi}\left\{\limsup\limits_{t\to\infty}\dfrac{\ln I(t)}{t}=\lambda\right\}=1,

and the susceptible class
S(t)
converges weakly to the solution
\varphi(t)
on the boundary.

In order to prove Theorem
4.3
, we need the following auxiliary results.

Lemma 4.1
.

For any
T,H>1,

\varepsilon>0,\theta>0
, there is a
\delta=\delta(H,T,\varepsilon,\theta)
such that

\mathbb{P}_{u,v,\pi}\{\tau_{\theta}\geq T\}\geq 1-\varepsilon,\;\;\forall\;(u,v,\pi)\in[0,H]\times(0,\delta]\times\mathcal{M},

where
\tau_{\theta}=\inf\{t\geq 0:I_{u,v}(t)>\theta\}.

Proof.

By the exponential martingale inequality
[
19
, Theorem 7.4, p. 44]
, we have
\mathbb{P}_{u,v,\pi}(\Omega_{1})\geq 1-\dfrac{\varepsilon}{2}
, where

\Omega_{1}=\left\{\sigma_{2}B_{2}(t)\leq\dfrac{\sigma_{2}^{2}t}{2}+\ln\dfrac{2}{\varepsilon}\;\forall t\geq 0\right\}.

In view of part (ii) Lemma
4.1
, there exists a
\overline{H}=\overline{H}(T,H,\varepsilon)
such that
\mathbb{P}_{u,v,\pi}(\Omega_{2})\geq 1-\dfrac{\varepsilon}{2}
, where

\Omega_{2}=\{0\leq S(t),I(t)\leq\overline{H}\;\;\forall t\in[0,T]\}.

Applying Itô’s formula to equation (
2.3
) yields
that

(4.5)

\displaystyle\ln I(t)=

\displaystyle\ln i-c_{2}t+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{1}f(x,S_{u,v}(s),I_{u,v}(s))\Pi_{s}(dx)ds

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{1}xh(x,S(s),I(s))\Pi_{s}(dx)ds+\sigma_{2}B_{2}(t).

Therefore, for any
(s,i,\pi)\in[0,H]\times(0,H]\times\mathcal{M}
and
\omega\in\Omega_{1}\cap\Omega_{2}
we have from (
4.5
) and the Lipschitz continuity of
f
and
h
that

\ln I(t)<\ln i-b_{2}T+T\big(2L_{1}\overline{H}+2L_{2}\overline{H}\big)+\ln\dfrac{2}{\varepsilon},\;\;\forall t\in[0,T].

Hence, we can choose a sufficiently small
\delta=\delta(H,T,\varepsilon,\theta)<H
such that for all
(s,i,\pi)\in[0,H]\times(0,\delta]\times\mathcal{M}
and
0\leq t\leq T
,
\ln I(t)<\ln\theta,\forall\omega\in\Omega_{1}\cap\Omega_{2}
. The proof is complete.
∎

Proposition 4.1
.

Suppose that the assumptions from Theorem
4.3
hold. For any
0<\varepsilon<\min\{\frac{1}{5},-\frac{\lambda}{5}\}
and
H>1
, there exists
\widehat{\delta}=\widehat{\delta}(\varepsilon,H)\in(0,H^{-1})
such that

\mathbb{P}_{u,v,\pi}\left\{\limsup_{t\to\infty}\dfrac{\ln I(t)}{t}=\lambda\right\}\geq 1-4\varepsilon,\;\forall(u,v,\pi)\in[H^{-1};H]\times(0;\widehat{\delta}]\times\mathcal{M}_{\Phi^{*}}.

Proof.

Let
\theta_{0}=\theta_{0}(\varepsilon)<\frac{\varepsilon}{L_{1}+L_{2}}\wedge\frac{b_{1}}{L_{1}+L_{2}}
be such that

(4.6)

\frac{a_{1}+\theta_{0}(L_{1}+L_{2}+L_{1}\theta_{0}+L_{2}\theta_{0})}{b_{1}-(L_{1}+L_{2})\theta_{0}}-\frac{a_{1}}{b_{1}}<\frac{\varepsilon}{L_{1}+L_{2}}.

Consider the following stochastic differential equation

(4.7)

d\overline{\varphi}(t)=\big(\overline{a}_{1}-\overline{b}_{1}\overline{\varphi}(t)\big)dt+\sigma_{1}\overline{\varphi}(t)dB_{1}(t),

where
\overline{a}_{1}=a_{1}+L_{1}+L_{2}+L_{1}\theta_{0}+L_{2}\theta_{0}
,
\overline{b}_{1}=b_{1}-(L_{1}+L_{2})\theta_{0}>0
.
A comparison result shows that
\varphi(t)\leq\overline{\varphi}(t),t\geq 0\text{\;a.s.\;}
provided that
\varphi(0)=\overline{\varphi}(0)
.
Moreover, the strong law of large numbers yields that

(4.8)

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}

\displaystyle\bigg|\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{1}f(x,\overline{\varphi}(s),0)\Pi_{s}(dx)ds+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{1}xh(x,\overline{\varphi}(s),0)\Pi_{s}(dx)ds

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{1}f(x,\varphi(s),0)\Pi_{s}(dx)ds-\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{1}xh(x,\varphi(s),0)\Pi_{s}(dx)ds\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}(L_{1}+L_{2})(\overline{\varphi}(s)-\varphi(s))ds

\displaystyle=(L_{1}+L_{2})\left(\frac{\overline{a}_{1}}{\overline{b}_{1}}-\frac{a_{1}}{b_{1}}\right)\text{\;a.s.\;}

Combining (
4.6
) and (
4.8
) implies that

(4.9)

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}

\displaystyle\bigg|\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{1}f(x,\overline{\varphi}(s),0)\Pi_{s}(dx)ds+\int_{0}^{1}xh(x,\overline{\varphi}(s),0)\Pi_{s}(dx)ds

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{1}f(x,\varphi(s),0)\Pi_{s}(dx)ds-\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{1}xh(x,\varphi(s),0)\Pi_{s}(dx)ds\bigg|<\varepsilon\text{\;a.s.\;}

Therefore, there exists
T_{1}=T_{1}(\varepsilon)
such that

\mathbb{P}(\Omega_{3})\geq 1-\varepsilon
, where

\displaystyle\Omega_{3}:=

\displaystyle\Bigg\{\bigg|\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{1}f(x,\overline{\varphi}(s),0)\Pi_{s}(dx)ds+\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{1}xh(x,\overline{\varphi}(s),0)\Pi_{s}(dx)ds

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{1}f(x,\varphi(s),0)\Pi_{s}(dx)ds-\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{1}xh(x,\varphi(s),0)\Pi_{s}(dx)ds\bigg|<2\varepsilon,\forall t\geq T_{1}\Bigg\}

By definition of
\lambda
and ergodicity of
\varphi(t),\Pi_{t}
, we obtain

\mathbb{P}_{H,\pi}\left\{-c_{2}+\lim_{t\to\infty}\dfrac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{1}f(x,\varphi(s),0)\Pi_{s}(dx)ds+\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{1}xh(x,\varphi(s),0)\Pi_{s}(dx)ds=\lambda\right\}=1,

where
\mathbb{P}_{H,\pi}
indicates the initial values of
(\varphi(t),\Pi_{t})
.
As a result, there exists
T_{2}=T_{2}(H,\varepsilon)>1
such that
\mathbb{P}_{H,\pi}(\Omega_{4})\geq 1-\varepsilon
, where

4.3.
Permanence

In this section, we deal with the case
\lambda>0
and prove that the system is permanent in the following sense.

Definition 4.1
.

We say that system (
2.3
) is permanent (in mean) if for any initial value
(u,v,\pi)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2,\circ}\times\mathcal{M}

\liminf_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}_{u,v,\pi}S(s)ds>0,\quad\liminf_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}_{u,v,\pi}I(s)ds>0.

Theorem 4.4
.

Assume that
\lambda>0
. Then for any initial
data

(u,v,\pi)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2,\circ}\times\mathcal{M}
, system (
2.3
) is permanent (in mean).

Proof.

We first prove that all invariant measures of
(S(t),I(t),\Pi(t)
concentrate on
\mathbb{R}^{2,\circ}\times\mathcal{M}
.
We assume by contradiction that there is no invariant measure on
\mathbb{R}^{2,\circ}_{+}
of
(S(t),I(t))
.
Therefore, there is no invariant measure on
\mathbb{R}^{2,*}_{+}
since the solutions starting in
\mathbb{R}^{2,*}_{+}
will enter and remain in
\mathbb{R}^{2,\circ}_{+}
due to Theorem
4.1
.
As a result,
\widehat{\mu}\times\boldsymbol{\delta}\times\Phi^{*}
(the unique invariant on the boundary
\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\{0\}\times\mathcal{M}
) is the unique invariant probability measure of the process
\{S(t),I(t),\Pi_{t}\}
on
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}\times\mathcal{M}
.
Therefore, by applying
[
9
, Lemma 3.4]
, we have

(4.12)

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{u,v,\pi}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{1}f(x,S(s),I(s))\Pi_{s}(dx)ds

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}\times\mathcal{M}}\int_{0}^{1}f(x,y,i)\widehat{\mu}(dy)\boldsymbol{\delta}(di)\Phi^{*}(d\nu)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{1}f(x,y,0)\mu^{*}(dx)\widehat{\mu}(dy).

and

(4.13)

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{u,v,\pi}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{1}xh(x,S(s),I(s))\Pi_{s}(dx)ds

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}\times\mathcal{M}}\int_{0}^{1}xh(x,y,i)\nu(dx)\widehat{\mu}(dy)\boldsymbol{\delta}(di)\Phi^{*}(d\nu)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathcal{M}}\int_{0}^{1}xh(x,y,0)\nu(dx)\widehat{\mu}(dy)\Phi^{*}(d\nu)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{1}xh(x,y,0)\mu^{*}(dx)\widehat{\mu}(dy).

On the other hand,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{u,v,\pi}\frac{\ln Y(t)}{t}=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x,y}\frac{\ln v}{t}-c_{2}+\frac{1}{t}\mathbb{E}_{x,y}\bigg(\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{1}f(x,S(s),I(s))\Pi_{s}(dx)ds

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{1}xh(x,S(s),I(s))\Pi_{s}(dx)ds\bigg)+\mathbb{E}_{x,y}\frac{\sigma_{2}W_{2}(t)}{t}.

As a result,
we have

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{u,v,\pi}\frac{\ln I(t)}{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-c_{2}+\lim_{t\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{x,y}\frac{1}{t}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{1}f(x,S(s),I(s))\Pi_{s}(dx)ds+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{1}xh(x,S(s),I(s))\Pi_{s}(dx)ds\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-c_{2}+\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{1}f(x,y,0)\mu^{*}(dx)\widehat{\mu}(dy)+\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{1}xh(x,y,0)\mu^{*}(dx)\widehat{\mu}(dy)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda>0.

This contradicts the fact that

\lim_{t\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{u,v,\pi}\frac{\ln I(t)}{t}\leq\lim_{t\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{u,v,\pi}\frac{I(t)}{t}=0

because
\ln y\leq y
while Lemma
4.1
implies
\lim_{t\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{u,v,\pi}\frac{I(t)}{t}=0
.
As a result, all invariant measures of
(S(t),I(t),\Pi(t))
concentrate on
\mathbb{R}^{2,\circ}_{+}\times\mathcal{M}_{\Phi^{*}}
(the existence of an invariant measure follows from Lemma
4.1
).

By the moment boundedness in Lemma
4.1
, we obtain that there exists a sequence
T_{k}\to\infty
as

such that

\liminf_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}_{u,v,\pi}S(s)ds=\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{1}{T_{k}}\int_{0}^{T_{k}}\mathbb{E}_{u,v,\pi}S(s)ds,

\liminf_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}_{u,v,\pi}I(s)ds=\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{1}{T_{k}}\int_{0}^{T_{k}}\mathbb{E}_{u,v,\pi}I(s)ds.

Therefore, by applying
[
9
, Lemma 3.4]
and the fact that all invariant measures of
(S(t),I(t),\Pi(t))
concentrate on
\mathbb{R}^{2,\circ}_{+}\times\mathcal{M}_{\Phi^{*}}
, we obtain the desired result.
∎

5.
Discussion and Interpretation

When a pandemic arises, there are usually multiple options one can take in order to control it. If we apply an extreme policy to control the disease transmission (i.e., we try to reduce the infection rate to be very small or almost
0
), we can certainly control the pandemic.
This can be seen
by looking at
\lambda
defined in the previous section and noting that if
f(S,I)\approx 0
then
\lambda\approx-c_{2}<0
.
However, this type of
highly restrictive policy
may hurt the economy.
It is
important
to
balance public health and the economic issues.
In our context, this is equivalent to ensuring that
\lambda<0
, which ensures the pandemic is under control, but not making
\lambda
too negative, since in the process of doing this (lockdowns, bankruptcy of businesses, unemployment) the economy could suffer significantly.

Definition 5.1
.

We say a proposed threshold
\lambda_{1}
is overcautious if it is greater than the exact threshold, that is
\lambda_{1}>\lambda
.
We say a proposed threshold
\lambda_{1}
is incautious if it is less than the exact threshold, that is
\lambda_{1}<\lambda
.

Remark 6
.

We say a threshold
\lambda_{1}>\lambda
(the exact one) is an overcautious proposed threshold because if this threshold is implemented,
we tend to
apply a policy to make
\lambda_{1}<0
. But this may not be necessary because the exact threshold is
\lambda<\lambda_{1}
.
Conversely, a threshold
\lambda_{1}<\lambda
is an incautious one because reducing
\lambda_{1}
to be less 0 may not be enough and the pandemic would
still not be controlled.

Let us consider the case when
\alpha(t)
takes the finitely many values
0=m_{1}<m_{2}<\dots<m_{n^{*}}=1
. We note that our analysis is still true for the general case when
\alpha
takes values in
[0,1]
.
Recall that we assume
\alpha(t)
has a unique invariant (discrete) measure
(\mu_{1}^{*},\dots,\mu_{n^{*}}^{*})
.

Since

\alpha(t)
is not directly available, we have two options.
One option is to use filtering to estimate
\alpha(t)
and then consider the corresponding system with filtering. It was shown that this method preserves the longtime behavior of the original system.
In particular, from Section
4.4

the exact threshold for this method is

\lambda=-c_{2}+\sum_{k=1}^{n^{*}}\mu^{*}_{k}\int_{0}^{\infty}f(m_{k},y,0)\widehat{\mu}(dy)+\sum_{k=1}^{n^{*}}m_{k}\mu^{*}_{k}\int_{0}^{\infty}h(m_{k},y,0)\widehat{\mu}(dy).

Another possible option is to estimate some prediction for
\alpha(t)
and give that value for
\alpha(t)
.
Let
k_{0}\in\{1,2,\dots,n^{*}\}
. Assume that we estimate
\alpha(t)=m_{k_{0}}
and consider the system

(5.1)

\begin{cases}dS(t)=\Big[a_{1}-b_{1}S(t)-I(t)f(m_{k_{0}},S(t),I(t))\\
\hskip 56.9055pt-\alpha_{0}I(t)h(m_{k_{0}},S(t),I(t))\Big]dt+\sigma_{1}S(t)dB_{1}(t),\\[4.30554pt]
dI(t)=\Big[-b_{2}I(t)+I(t)f(m_{k_{0}},S(t),I(t))\\
\hskip 56.9055pt+m_{k_{0}}I(t)h(m_{k_{0}},S(t),I(t))\Big]dt+\sigma_{2}I(t)dB_{2}(t).\\
\end{cases}

Using the results from
[
24
]
, the threshold for persistence and extinction of (
5.1
) is given by

\lambda_{pre}=-c_{2}+\int_{0}^{\infty}f(m_{k_{0}},y,0)\widehat{\mu}(dy)+m_{k_{0}}\int_{0}^{\infty}h(m_{k_{0}},y,0)\widehat{\mu}(dy).

The following
results tell us when
\lambda_{{\rm\tiny pre}}
is an incautious or overcautious threshold.

Proposition 5.1
.

(i)

If

\displaystyle\mu_{k_{0}}^{*}\int_{0}^{\infty}(f(m_{k_{0}},y,0)+m_{k_{0}}h(m_{k_{0}},y,0))\widehat{\mu}(dy)

\displaystyle<\sum_{k=1}^{n^{*}}\mu^{*}_{k}\int_{0}^{\infty}f(m_{k},y,0)\widehat{\mu}(dy)+\sum_{k=1}^{n^{*}}m_{k}\mu^{*}_{k}\int_{0}^{\infty}h(m_{k},y,0)\widehat{\mu}(dy)

then
\lambda_{{\rm\tiny pre}}<\lambda
. As a result,
\lambda_{{\rm\tiny pre}}
is an incautious threshold.

(ii)

If

\displaystyle\mu^{*}_{k_{0}}\int_{0}^{\infty}(f(m_{k_{0}},y,0)+m_{k_{0}}h(m_{k_{0}},y,0))\widehat{\mu}(dy)

\displaystyle>\frac{1}{1-\mu_{n^{*}}^{*}}\Big(\sum_{k=1}^{n^{*}-1}\mu^{*}_{k}\int_{0}^{\infty}f(m_{k},y,0)\widehat{\mu}(dy)+\sum_{k=1}^{n^{*}-1}m_{k}\mu^{*}_{k}\int_{0}^{\infty}h(m_{k},y,0)\widehat{\mu}(dy)\Big)

then
\lambda_{{\rm\tiny pre}}>\lambda
. As a result,
\lambda_{{\rm\tiny pre}}
is an overcautious threshold.

It is natural to assume that
f(x,y,0)
is an increasing function w.r.t.
x
because the higher infection rate in the group of potentially infected individuals would make the disease spread faster.
Note that this intuition is natural but not
always true because we are examining
functions at the boundary, i.e., there is no infected group.

Assumption 5.1
.

For each fixed
y
, the functions
f(x,y,0)
and
h(x,y,0)
are increasing in
x
.

Under this natural assumption, we will see that assuming that all potentially infected individuals are infected will lead to an overcautious policy. Conversely, it will be incautious if we assume that all individuals with hidden infection status are free of the disease.
We will make this analysis clearer in the following two subsections.

5.1.
The overcautious case: assuming that all individuals in the hidden group are infected

Suppose we do not use the filtering to consider the observable problem, make the assumption
\alpha(t)=1,t\geq 0
and consider the system (
5.1
) with
m_{k_{0}}=m_{n^{*}}=1
.
Under Assumption
5.1
, this is an overcautious prediction.
The following theorem is consistent with this intuition.

Proposition 5.2
.

Under Assumption
5.1
,

(5.2)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}(f(1,y,0)+h(1,y,0))\widehat{\mu}(dy)

\displaystyle>\frac{1}{1-\mu_{n^{*}}^{*}}\Big(\sum_{k=1}^{n^{*}-1}\mu^{*}_{k}\int_{0}^{\infty}f(m_{k},y,0)\widehat{\mu}(dy)+\sum_{k=1}^{n^{*}-1}m_{k}\mu^{*}_{k}\int_{0}^{\infty}h(m_{k},y,0)\widehat{\mu}(dy)\Big)

As a result,
\lambda_{{\rm\tiny pre}}>\lambda
and
\lambda_{{\rm\tiny pre}}
is an overcautious threshold.

Proof.

The estimate (
5.2
) follows immediately from Assumption
5.1
. We omit the details here.
∎

5.2.
The incautious case: assuming that all individuals in the hidden group are not infected

If we make the assumption that
\alpha(t)=0,t\geq 0
and consider the system (
5.1
) with
m_{k_{0}}=m_{1}=0
, this is an incautious prediction (under Assumption
5.1
).
The following result is consistent with this fact.

Proposition 5.3
.

Under Assumption
5.1
,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}(f(0,y,0)+h(0,y,0))\widehat{\mu}(dy)

\displaystyle<\frac{1}{1-\mu_{1}^{*}}\Big(\sum_{k=2}^{n^{*}}\mu^{*}_{k}\int_{0}^{\infty}f(m_{k},y,0)\widehat{\mu}(dy)+\sum_{k=2}^{n^{*}}m_{k}\mu^{*}_{k}\int_{0}^{\infty}h(m_{k},y,0)\widehat{\mu}(dy)\Big)

As a result,
\lambda_{{\rm\tiny pre}}<\lambda
and
\lambda_{{\rm\tiny pre}}
is an incautious threshold.

6.
Examples and Simulations

6.1.
A Simple Example

In this section, we consider a simple example.
Assume that all the individuals in the hidden class have the same status (susceptible or infected).
In other words, the signal Markov process
\alpha(t)
has only two possible states,
0
or
1
, (corresponding to the case that all individuals in hidden class are disease-free or infected, respectively).
Assume that
\alpha(t)
has the generator

Q=\begin{bmatrix}-q_{1}&q_{1}\\
q_{2}&-q_{2}\end{bmatrix}.

We can only observe
\alpha(t)
via the observation process
y(t)
given by

dy(t)=g(\alpha(t))dt+dW(t),\quad y(0)=0,

where
g:\{0,1\}\to\mathbb{R}
and set
g_{1}=g(0)
,
g_{2}=g(1)
. Let
e(t)=\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{1}_{\{\alpha(t)=0\}}|\mathcal{F}_{t}^{y}]
.

The dynamics of this epidemic system under the (Wonham) filter is described by

(6.1)

\begin{cases}dS(t)=\Big[a_{1}-b_{1}S(t)-I(t)f(0,S(t),I(t))e(t)-I(t)f(1,S(t),I(t))(1-e(t))\\
\hskip 56.9055pt-I(t)h(1,S(t),I(t))(1-e(t))\Big]dt+\sigma_{1}S(t)dB_{1}(t),\\[4.30554pt]
dI(t)=\Big[-b_{2}I(t)+I(t)f(0,S(t),I(t))e(t)+I(t)f(1,S(t),I(t))(1-e(t))\\
\hskip 56.9055pt+I(t)h(1,S(t),I(t))(1-e(t))\Big]dt+\sigma_{2}I(t)dB_{2}(t),\\
de(t)=[q_{2}-(q_{1}+q_{2})e(t)]dt+(g_{1}-g_{2})e(t)(1-e(t))d\overline{W}(t).\end{cases}

We will also assume that
q_{1},q_{2}>0
and
g:=g_{1}-g_{2}\neq 0
since the other cases are trivial.
Consider the equation of the third component

(6.2)

de(t)=[q_{2}-(q_{1}+q_{2})e(t)]dt+ge(t)(1-e(t))d\overline{W}(t),\quad e(0)\in[0,1].

By using the Lyapunov functional method as in Theorem
4.1
, we obtain the following result.

Theorem 6.1
.

For any initial value in
[0,1]
, the equation (
6.2
) has a unique solution and

\mathbb{P}\{e(t)\in(0,1),\forall t>0\}=1.

Now, by solving the Fokker-Plank equation, equation (
6.2
) has a unique invariant measure supported in
[0,1]
with density given by

\phi^{*}(x)=Ce^{-\frac{d_{1}}{1-x}}(1-x)^{2(d_{1}-d_{2}-1)}e^{-\frac{d_{2}}{x}}x^{2(d_{2}-d_{1}-1)},\;x\in(0,1),

where
d_{1}=\frac{q_{1}}{g^{2}}
,

d_{2}=\frac{q_{2}}{g^{2}}
, and

C
is a normalizing constant.

As developed in the main results, the threshold
\lambda
is defined by

(6.3)

\displaystyle\lambda=

\displaystyle-c_{2}+\frac{q_{2}}{q_{1}+q_{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}f(0,y,0)\widehat{\mu}(dy)

\displaystyle+\frac{q_{1}}{q_{1}+q_{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}f(1,y,0)\widehat{\mu}(dy)+\frac{q_{1}}{q_{1}+q_{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}h(1,y,0)\widehat{\mu}(dy),

where

\widehat{\mu}
is the invariant measure with the density given by (
3.3
).

Theorem 6.2
.

Consider system (
6.1
) and
\lambda
as in (
6.3
).

•

If
\lambda<0
then for any initial point
(u,v,e_{0})\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2,\circ}\times\mathcal{S}_{2}
,
the number of the infected individuals
I(t)
tends to zero at an exponential rate, i.e.,

\mathbb{P}_{u,v,e_{0}}\left\{\limsup\limits_{t\to\infty}\dfrac{\ln I(t)}{t}\leq\lambda\right\}=1,

and the susceptible class
S(t)
converges weakly to the solution
\varphi(t)
on the boundary.

•

If
\lambda>0
, then for any initial point
(u,v,e_{0})\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2,\circ}\times\mathcal{S}_{2}
, all invariant probability measures of
the solution
(S(t),I(t),e(t))

concentrate on
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2,\circ}\times[0,1]
.
Moreover, the system (
6.1
) is permanent.

6.2.
Numerical Examples

In this section, we consider a simple example and provide some numerical simulations.
Consider the equation (
6.1
) with
f(x,s,i)=m_{1}(x)s
,

h(x,s,i)=\frac{m_{2}(x)s}{1+s+i}
,
g(x)=x
, (
x\in\{0,1\}
), i.e., consider

(6.4)

\begin{cases}dS(t)=\Big[a_{1}-b_{1}S(t)-m_{1}(0)S(t)I(t)e(t)-m_{1}(1)S(t)I(t)(1-e(t))\\
\hskip 56.9055pt-\dfrac{m_{2}(1)(1-e(t))S(t)I(t)}{1+S(t)+I(t)}\Big]dt+\sigma_{1}S(t)dB_{1}(t),\\[8.61108pt]
dI(t)=\Big[-b_{2}I(t)+m_{1}(0)S(t)I(t)e(t)+m_{1}(1)S(t)I(t)(1-e(t))\\
\hskip 56.9055pt+\dfrac{m_{2}(1)(1-e(t))S(t)I(t)}{1+S(t)+I(t)}\Big]dt+\sigma_{2}I(t)dB_{2}(t),\\
de(t)=[q_{2}-(q_{1}+q_{2})e(t)]dt+e(t)(1-e(t))d\overline{W}(t).\end{cases}

The above is the corresponding system
with a filtering of hidden Markov chain
\alpha(t)
, whereas
the following
system is one under hidden process
\alpha(t)

(6.5)

\begin{cases}dS(t)=\Big[a_{1}-b_{1}S(t)-m_{1}(\alpha(t))S(t)I(t)\\
\hskip 56.9055pt-\dfrac{\alpha(t)m_{2}(\alpha(t))S(t)I(t)}{1+S(t)+I(t)}\Big]dt+\sigma_{1}S(t)dB_{1}(t),\\[8.61108pt]
dI(t)=\Big[-b_{2}I(t)+m_{1}(\alpha(t))S(t)I(t)\\
\hskip 56.9055pt+\dfrac{\alpha(t)m_{2}(\alpha(t))S(t)I(t)}{1+S(t)+I(t)}\Big]dt+\sigma_{2}I(t)dB_{2}(t).\\
\end{cases}

Here
\alpha(t)
is the Markov chain taking values in
\{0,1\}
with the generator
Q
.

Suppose one does not use the filtering to consider the corresponding observable system (
6.4
), and considers instead the system under some
predictions for
\alpha(t)
.
If one uses the incautious prediction and assumes that
\alpha(t)=0
(i.e., considers all individuals in the hidden group not to be infected), the corresponding system is

(6.6)

\begin{cases}dS(t)=\Big[a_{1}-b_{1}S(t)-m_{1}(0)S(t)I(t)\Big]dt+\sigma_{1}S(t)dB_{1}(t),\\[8.61108pt]
dI(t)=\Big[-b_{2}I(t)+m_{1}(0)S(t)I(t)\Big]dt+\sigma_{2}I(t)dB_{2}(t).\\
\end{cases}

If one uses the overcautious prediction and assumes that
\alpha(t)=1
(i.e., considers all individuals in the hidden group to be infected), the corresponding system is

(6.7)

\begin{cases}dS(t)=\Big[a_{1}-b_{1}S(t)-m_{1}(1)S(t)I(t)-\dfrac{m_{2}(1)S(t)I(t)}{1+S(t)+I(t)}\Big]dt+\sigma_{1}S(t)dB_{1}(t),\\[8.61108pt]
dI(t)=\Big[-b_{2}I(t)+m_{1}(1)S(t)I(t)+\dfrac{m_{2}(1)S(t)I(t)}{1+S(t)+I(t)}\Big]dt+\sigma_{2}I(t)dB_{2}(t).\\
\end{cases}

Example 6.1
.

Consider (
6.4
) with

a_{1}=0.5,b_{1}=1,\sigma_{1}=1,b_{2}=2,\sigma_{2}=0.5,m_{1}(0)=0.1,m_{1}(1)=4,m_{2}=0.1,q_{1}=5,q_{2}=25.

\lambda=-1.7252
, the (exact) threshold determining the longtime behavior (persistence and extinction) for both systems (
6.4
) and (
6.5
).

\lambda_{0}=-2.0750

6.6

\lambda_{0}
is an incautious threshold which would lead to overly optimistic expectations.

As our theoretical results show the number of infected
