Eigenvalue contour lines of Kac–Murdock–Szegő matrices with a complex parameter
George Fikioris and Christos Papapanos

Abstract
. A previous paper studied the so-called borderline curves of the Kac–Murdock–Szegő matrix K n ​ ( ρ ) = [ ρ | j − k | ] j , k = 1 n K_{n}(\rho)=\left[\rho^{|j-k|}\right]_{j,k=1}^{n} , where ρ ∈ ℂ \rho\in\mathbb{C} . These are the level curves (contour lines) in the complex- ρ \rho plane on which K n ​ ( ρ ) K_{n}(\rho) has a type-1 or type-2 eigenvalue of magnitude n n , where n n is the matrix dimension. Those curves have cusps at all critical points ρ = ρ c \rho=\rho_{c} at which multiple (double) eigenvalues occur. The present paper determines corresponding curves pertaining to eigenvalues of magnitude N ≠ n N\neq n . We find that these curves no longer present cusps; and that, when N < n N<n , the cusps have in a sense transformed into loops. We discuss the meaning of the winding numbers of our curves. Finally, we point out possible extensions to more general matrices.

2.
Useful properties of Chebyshev polynomials

We begin with some equalities and inequalities that are most concisely shown when expressed in terms of
T_{k}(z)
and
U_{k}(z)
(i.e. the first- and second-kind Chebyshev polynomials of degree
k
):

Lemma 2.1
.

(2.1)

T_{k}(\cos u)=\cos(ku),\quad u\in\mathbb{C},\quad k=0,1,2,\ldots;

(2.2)

U_{k}(\cos u)=\frac{\sin[(k+1)u]}{\sin u},\quad u\in\mathbb{C},\quad k=0,1,2,\ldots;

(2.3)

U_{k}(\cosh v)=\frac{\sinh[(k+1)v]}{\sinh v},\quad v\in\mathbb{C},\quad k=0,1,2,\ldots;

(2.4)

U_{k}(z)=zU_{k-1}(z)+T_{k}(z),\quad z\in\mathbb{C},\quad k=1,2,3\ldots;

(2.5)

-1\leq T_{k}(x)\leq 1,\quad-1\leq x\leq 1,\quad k=0,1,2,\ldots;

(2.6)

U_{k}(z)=\sum_{m=0}^{k}z^{m}\,T_{k-m}(z),\quad z\in\mathbb{C},\quad k=0,1,2,\ldots;

(2.7)

-(k+1)\leq U_{k}(x)\leq k+1,\quad-1\leq x\leq 1,\quad k=0,1,2,\ldots;

(2.8)

1\leq x_{1}<x_{2}\implies 1\leq T_{k}(x_{1})<T_{k}(x_{2}),\quad k=1,2,3,\ldots;

(2.9)

1\leq x_{1}<x_{2}\implies k+1\leq U_{k}(x_{1})<U_{k}(x_{2}),\quad k=1,2,3,\ldots.

Proof.

Eqns. (
2.1
) and (
2.2
) are definitions of the Chebyshev polynomials. Therefore with
z=\cos u
, (
2.4
) reduces to a trigonometric identity. Set
u=iv
(
v\in\mathbb{C}
) in (
2.2
) to get (
2.3
). With
\cos u=x
(
-1\leq x\leq 1
), (
2.1
) implies (
2.5
).
To verify (
2.6
), use
U_{0}(z)=T_{0}(z)=1
, (
2.4
), and induction. Eqn. (
2.7
) is trivial when
k=0
; and, for
k\geq 1
, (
2.7
) is a consequence of (
2.6
), (
2.5
), and the triangle inequality. Setting
u=iv
(
v\geq 0
) in (
2.1
) shows that, for
k\geq 1
,
T_{k}(\cosh v)
is a strictly increasing function of
v
; this fact (together with
T_{k}(1)=1
) is tantamount to (
2.8
). Setting
z=x
(
x\geq 1
) in (
2.6
) and invoking
(
2.8
), it is seen that
U_{k}(x)
is a sum of positive and strictly increasing functions, yielding (
2.9
).
∎

Corollary 2.2
.

The trigonometric/hyperbolic versions of (
2.7
)/(
2.9
) are

(2.10)

\bigg|\frac{\sin(ku)}{\sin u}\bigg|\leq k,\quad u\in\mathbb{R},\quad k=1,2,3,\ldots,

(2.11)

0\leq v_{1}<v_{2}\implies k\leq\frac{\sinh(kv_{1})}{\sinh v_{1}}<\frac{\sinh(kv_{2})}{\sinh v_{2}},\quad k=2,3,4,\ldots.

We conclude this section with a lemma on the zeros and maxima of the positive and even function
|U_{k}(z)|
.

Lemma 2.3
.

For
k=2,3,4,\ldots
, let
\alpha_{k,m}
be a zero of
U_{k}(z)
. The
\alpha_{k,m}
are all real, simple, and lie in the interval
(-1,1)
. They are given by

(2.12)

\alpha_{k,m}=\cos\frac{(k+1-m)\pi}{k+1},\quad m=1,2,\ldots,k.

Let
\beta_{k,m}
be a zero of the derivative
U^{\prime}_{k}(z)
. The
\beta_{k,m}
are also real, with

(2.13)

\alpha_{k,m}<\beta_{k,m}<\alpha_{k,m+1},\quad m=1,2,\ldots,k-1.

In (
2.12
), the indexing is such that

\alpha_{k,m}
increases when
m
increases, and ditto for the
\beta_{k,m}
in (
2.13
).

The two numbers
|U_{k}(\beta_{k,1})|
and
|U_{k}(\beta_{k,k-1})|
are equal and are the largest of the values
|U_{k}(\beta_{k,m})|
. Furthermore,

(2.14)

0<\max_{\alpha_{k,1}\leq x\leq\alpha_{k,k}}|U_{k}(x)|=|U_{k}(\beta_{k,1})|=|U_{k}(\beta_{k,k-1})|<k+1,

so that
|U_{k}(\beta_{k,1})|

is the global maximum of the even function
|U_{k}(x)|
in the interval
[\alpha_{k,1},\alpha_{k,k}]
.

When
\alpha_{k,k}\leq x\leq 1
, the function
U_{k}(x)
strictly increases from
U_{k}(\alpha_{k,k})=0
to
U_{k}(1)=k+1
. And when
-1\leq x\leq\alpha_{k,1}
,
|U_{k}(x)|
strictly decreases from
|U_{k}(-1)|=k+1
to
U_{k}(\alpha_{k,1})=0
.

Proof.

By (
2.2
), the
\alpha_{k,m}
in (
2.12
) are zeros of
U_{k}(z)
. Since there are
k
distinct zeros, all are simple. By Rolle’s theorem and for
m=1,2,\ldots,k-1
,
U^{\prime}_{k}(\beta_{k,m})=0
for some
\beta_{k,m}
satisfying (
2.13
). Therefore all zeros
\beta_{k,m}
of
U^{\prime}_{k}(z)
are real and are bracketed by the inequality in (
2.13
). By the extreme value theorem and
U_{k}(\alpha_{k,m})=U_{k}(\alpha_{k,m+1})=0
, the nonnegative function
|U_{k}(x)|
assumes its maximum value within
[\alpha_{k,m},\alpha_{k,m+1}]
iff
x=\beta_{k,m}
. As
|U_{k-1}(x)|
is even, we have
|U_{k}(\beta_{k,1})|=|U_{k}(\beta_{k,k-1})|
. As discussed in
[
9
]
, the
|U_{k}(\beta_{k,m})|
increase as
|\beta_{k,m}|
increases away from zero, and all
|U_{k}(\beta_{k,m})|
are smaller than the endpoint values
|U_{k}(\pm 1)|=k+1
, giving (
2.14
). Since no zero of
U^{\prime}_{k}(x)
lies in the interval
[\alpha_{k,k},1]
,
U_{k}(x)
increases strictly in this interval, from its value
0
at the left endpoint to its value
k+1
at the right endpoint. The statement pertaining to the interval
[-1,\alpha_{1,k}]
follows because
|U_{k}(x)|
is even.
∎

3.
The functions
h_{n,N}(v)
and
g_{n,N}(u)

This section defines and gives useful properties of the functions
h_{n,N}(v)
and
g_{n,N}(u)
, which will play an important role in our main theorem.

3.1.
The function
h_{n,N}(v)

Definition 3.1
.

For
n=3,4,\ldots
and
N\in\mathbb{R}
with
N>0
, we define

(3.1)

h_{n,N}(v)=\sinh^{2}(nv)-N^{2}\sinh^{2}{v},\quad v\geq 0.

In the case
N<n
, the behavior that interests us is straightforward. It is given by the following proposition.

Proposition 3.2
.

For
N<n
, the function
h_{n,N}(v)
equals zero when
v=0
, and
is positive and strictly increasing when
v\in(0,+\infty)
.

Proof.

The equality
h_{n,N}(0)=0
is obvious. For
v>0
, write

(3.2)

h_{n,N}(v)=[\sinh(nv)+N\sinh{v}][\sinh(nv)-N\sinh{v}].

The first factor is positive and strictly increasing. The second factor equals 0 when
v=0
, equals
+\infty
when
v=+\infty
and has a derivative equal to
n\cosh(nv)-N\cosh{v}
. As
\cosh(nv)>\cosh v>1
and
n>N
, this derivative cannot vanish. Thus the second factor is also positive and strictly increasing, proving the lemma.
∎

In the case
N>n
, the behavior of
h_{n,N}(v)
is not as simple. To describe it, we introduce an auxiliary quantity
v_{0}(n,N)
. This is defined as the solution to a transcendental equation as follows.

Lemma 3.3
.

For
N>n
, the equation

(3.3)

\frac{\sinh(nv)}{\sinh v}=N,\quad v\geq 0,

has a unique solution. We denote this solution, which is positive, by
v=v_{0}(n,N)
. Its limiting value as
N\to n
is

(3.4)

\lim_{N\to n+0}v_{0}(n,N)=0.

Proof.

The continuous function on the left-hand side of (
3.3
) is strictly increasing in
[0,+\infty)
by Corollary
2.2
, equals
n
when
v=0
, and equals
+\infty
when
v=+\infty
. Since the right-hand side
N
is greater than
n
there is a unique solution, and it is positive.
∎

We can now give our results for
N>n
.

Proposition 3.4
.

For
N>n
, the function
h_{n,N}(v)
is negative in the interval
(0,v_{0}(n,N))
, vanishes when
v=0
or
v=v_{0}(n,N)
, and is positive and strictly increasing in the interval
(v_{0}(n,N),+\infty)
.

Proof.

It is obvious that
h_{n,N}(0)=0
. By (
3.2
) and Lemma
3.3
,
h_{n,N}(v)
vanishes at exactly positive point, namely
v=v_{0}(n,N)
. The derivative
h^{\prime}_{n,N}(v)
is

h^{\prime}_{n,N}(v)=n\sinh(2nv)-N^{2}\sinh{(2v)},\quad v>0.

By Lemma
3.3
,
h^{\prime}_{n,N}(v)
also vanishes at exactly one point which, by Rolle’s theorem, must lie in the interval
(0,v_{0}(n,N))
. (This point, in fact, is
v=\frac{1}{2}v_{0}(n,\frac{N^{2}}{n})
.) Finally, we can easily show that
h_{n,N}(v)
is negative as
v\to 0+0
and positive as
v\to+\infty
.
These aforementioned properties prove the lemma.
∎

3.2.
The function

We now discuss the following function

.

Definition 3.5
.

For
n=3,4,\ldots
and
N\in\mathbb{R}
with
N>0
, we define

(3.5)

g_{n,N}(u)=N^{2}\sin^{2}{u}-\sin^{2}(nu),\quad-\pi\leq u\leq\pi.

We first assume

. In this case, what interests us is uncomplicated:

Proposition 3.6
.

For
N>n
, the function
g_{n,N}(u)
is zero when
u=0
and
|u|=\pi
, and positive when
0<|u|<\pi

Proof.

The statement about zeros is trivial. In the case

we have

(3.6)

g_{n,N}(u)>n^{2}\sin^{2}u-\sin^{2}(nu)\geq 0,

where we used (
3.5
), the inequalities

and

, and Corollary

.

When

, the behaviors of interest are best explained in terms of the auxiliary quantities

,

Lemma 3.7
.

The equation

(3.7)

has a unique solution which we denote by

defined by

(3.8)

N_{\mathrm{min}}(n)=\Big|U_{n-1}\left(x^{\prime}_{0}(n)\right)\Big|

satisfies

(3.9)

1\leq N_{\mathrm{min}}(n)<n,

and is the global maximum attained by

in the interval
[-\cos\frac{\pi}{n},\cos\frac{\pi}{n}]
.

Proof.

In Lemma
2.3
, set

to obtain
|\alpha_{k,1}|=\alpha_{k,k}=\cos\frac{\pi}{n}
,
\alpha_{k,k-1}=\cos\frac{2\pi}{n}
,
|\beta_{k,1}|=\beta_{k,k-1}=x^{\prime}_{0}(n)
, and
N_{\mathrm{min}}(n)=\big|U_{n-1}\left(x^{\prime}_{0}(n)\right)\big|
. Since
\cos\frac{3\pi}{2n}
belongs to
(\cos\frac{2\pi}{n},\cos\frac{\pi}{n})

is the maximum value, we have
N_{\mathrm{min}}(n)\geq\big|U_{n-1}\left(\cos\frac{3\pi}{2n}\right)\big|
; the first inequality in (

∎

As we will see, the value

Lemma 3.9
.

Let

, where

is defined in Lemma

|U_{n-1}(x)|=N,\quad-1<x<1,

has precisely one positive solution, to be denoted by

-x_{0}(n,N)
.
It is also true that

7.3.
An equivalent matrix problem; possible generalizations

The third-degree equation (

\mathrm{det}[\lambda I-M(\rho)]=0
of many

, an example being

(7.6)

M(\rho)=\begin{bmatrix}\rho+\alpha^{2}&0&-\frac{\pi\alpha^{2}}{2}\\
1&\rho+\alpha^{2}&1\\
1&\frac{\pi\alpha^{2}}{2}&0\\
\end{bmatrix}

The matrix
M(\rho)
is an
analytic matrix function
in the sense that all matrix elements are complex-analytic functions of
\rho
. Near any critical point

\rho_{c}(m)

[
3

. For the critical point
\rho_{c}(0)\in\mathbb{R}
, the Puiseux series is discussed in
[
13
]
and
[
15
]
; importantly, the coefficient of the square-root term (i.e., the coefficient of
[\rho-\rho_{c}(0)]^{1/2}
) is nonzero.

For a general class of analytic matrix functions, eigenvalue behaviors
near
such critical points are discussed in detail in Sections 3 and 4 of
[
3
]
, with the Puiseux series being the main tool of study. While the conclusions are directly relevant to the example analyzed herein,
3
3

3

Corollary 3.5 of
[
3
]
, for example, tells us that the cusp-like singularities in Fig.

are true cusps.
ref.
[
3
]
mainly focuses on eigenvalue bifurcations.

We believe that the behaviors exhibited by the
N
-level curves of
K_{n}(\rho)
(and
M(\rho)
) are quite general. It might be possible to extend the Puiseux-series techniques in Sections 3 and 4 of
[
3
]
so as to study the
local
behaviors (near cusps, loops, and the like) of

-level curves of the aforementioned class of analytic matrix functions. Preliminary work, however, indicates that this is not a simple task.

The type-2 eigenvalue contour lines of
K_{8}(\rho)
(Fig.
5
) and the eigenvalue contour lines of
M(\rho)
(Fig.
6
) are closed curves that share two important common features: (i) they are continuous; and (ii) they can be oriented in a natural manner. The

Kac–Murdock–Szegő matrix

(7.7)

\rho&1\\
\end{bmatrix}

whose eigenvalues are

and

, tells us that neither of these features is necessarily true in more general cases. The eigenvalue contour lines of

are two circles which intersect when

, but which are disjoint when

are discontinuous whenever

1
[consisting of both the solid (type-1) and the dashed (type-2) curves] also presents difficulty (b).

For certain types of matrices (or polynomials) for which the aforementioned conditions (i) and (ii) are satisfied, it is logical to expect that equations such as (
6.1

7.5
)—which involve the winding numbers of oriented level curves
L_{N}
in the complex-
\rho

We feel this matter is worthy of a systematic and rigorous study: It would be interesting to find matrix classes for which such equations remain true, and to state the equations in general form. To this end, a good starting point is the argument principle, see any text on functions of a single complex variable, or see the introductory discussions
[
16
]
of degree theory in two dimensions. For any fixed
\rho\notin L_{N}
, this principle can be used to express what we have called
j_{N}(\rho)
as a contour integral over the circle
|\lambda|=N
of the logarithmic derivative of the characteristic polynomial.
