The ring of differential operators on a monomial curve is a Hopf algebroid
Ulrich Krähmer, Myriam Mahaman

Abstract
. This article considers cuspidal curves whose coordinate rings are numerical semigroup algebras. Using a general result about descent of Hopf algebroid structures, their rings of differential operators are shown to be cocommutative and conilpotent left Hopf algebroids. If the semigroups are symmetric so that the curves are Gorenstein, they are full Hopf algebroids (admit an antipode).

1.
Introduction

1.1.
The smooth case

Let
k
be a field of characteristic
0,
A=k[X]
be
the coordinate ring of a
smooth
affine variety
X
,
and
\mathscr{D}_{A}
be the algebra of

k
-linear differential
operators on
A
.
As will be recalled in
Sections 2 and 3,
\mathscr{D}_{A}

is then generated as a
k
-algebra
by the subalgebra
A\subseteq\mathscr{D}_{A}
of multiplication
operators and the Lie algebra

\operatorname{Der}(A)\subseteq\mathscr{D}_{A}
of
vector fields on

X
(derivations
on
A
). This yields
an isomorphism of
A
-rings

\mathscr{D}_{A}\cong\mathscr{U}_{A}
with the
universal enveloping
algebra of the
Lie–Rinehart algebra

(A,\operatorname{Der}(A))
.

In particular,
\mathscr{D}_{A}
is
a left Hopf algebroid
over
A
(see Section 4), which means that
if
M,N
are

\mathscr{D}_{A}
-modules, then both

M\otimes_{A}N
and

\mathrm{Hom}_{A}(M,N)
carry

\mathscr{D}_{A}
-module
structures rendering

\mathscr{D}_{A}\mbox{-}\mathbf{Mod}
a closed
monoidal category.
Hence a natural question arises:

Question
.

For which
k
-algebras
A
is
\mathscr{D}_{A}
a
left Hopf algebroid?

This was one
of Sweedler’s motivations for
introducing Hopf algebroids
(called by him

\times_{A}
-Hopf algebras)
in

[
65
]
.
Theorem 8.7 therein
introduces the class of algebras

with almost finite projective differentials

for which
the answer is positive.
This class includes smooth and

purely inseparable
(see
Example

3.18
) algebras.

1.2.
Monomial curves

In the present article, we use a
descent theory approach to
answer the question for
the coordinate rings

A
of cuspidal curves given by
numerical semigroups:

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
k
be a field of
characteristic 0,

\mathcal{A}\subseteq(\mathbb{N},+)
be
a numerical semigroup, that
is, a monoid generated by

p_{1},\ldots,p_{n}\in\mathbb{N}

with

\mathrm{gcd}(p_{1},\ldots,p_{n})=1
,
and let

A
be its monoid algebra over
k
.
Then
\mathscr{D}_{A}
is canonically a
cocommutative and conilpotent left Hopf
algebroid over
A
. If

\mathcal{A}
is symmetric, it is a full Hopf
algebroid.

We refer to Section 7 for more
definitions and references
concerning numerical
semigroups.

1.3.
The basic example

When
p_{1}=2,p_{2}=3
, then

\mathscr{D}_{k[t^{2},t^{3}]}
is
generated by the coordinate
functions
t^{2},t^{3}

and the differential operators

D_{0}\coloneqq t\partial,\quad L_{-2}\coloneqq\partial^{2}-\frac{2}{t}\partial,\quad L_{-3}\coloneqq\partial^{3}-\frac{3}{t}\partial^{2}+\frac{3}{t^{2}}\partial

of orders
1,2
and
3
,
respectively,
where
\partial\coloneqq\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}

[
63
]
.
For this example, we compute the
Hopf algebroid structure explicitly:

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
k
be a field of
characteristic 0 and

A:=k[t^{2},t^{3}]
. Then
the
k
-algebra
\mathscr{D}_{A}

carries the structure of a
cocommutative, conilpotent,
and involutive
Hopf algebroid over
k[t^{2},t^{3}]

whose counit, coproduct and
antipode are determined by

\displaystyle\varepsilon(D_{0})

\displaystyle=\varepsilon(L_{-2})=\varepsilon(L_{-3})=0,

\displaystyle\Delta\left(D_{0}\right)

\displaystyle=D_{0}\otimes_{A}1+1\otimes_{A}D_{0},

\displaystyle\Delta\left(L_{-2}\right)

\displaystyle=L_{-2}\otimes_{A}1+2D_{0}\otimes_{A}(D_{0}-1)L_{-2}-2L_{1}\otimes_{A}L_{-3}+1\otimes_{A}L_{-2},

\displaystyle\Delta\left(L_{-3}\right)

\displaystyle=L_{-3}\otimes_{A}1+3L_{-2}\otimes_{A}L_{-1}-3L_{-1}\otimes_{A}L_{-2}

\displaystyle\quad\quad+6D_{0}\otimes_{A}(D_{0}-1)L_{-3}-6L_{1}\otimes_{A}L_{-2}^{2}+1\otimes_{A}L_{-3},

\displaystyle S(D_{0})

\displaystyle=-D_{0}+1,\quad S(L_{-2})=L_{-2},\quad S(L_{-3})=-L_{-3},

where

L_{1}:=t^{3}(D_{0}-1)L_{-2}-t^{4}L_{-3}
and

L_{-1}:=t^{2}(D_{0}-1)L_{-3}-t^{3}L_{-2}^{2}
.

Smith and Stafford, and,
independently, Muhasky
have shown that
\mathscr{D}_{k[t^{2},t^{3}]}
is
Morita-equivalent to the ring

\mathscr{D}_{k[t]}
of differential
operators on the
normalisation
k[t]

of
k[t^{2},t^{3}]

[
63
,
47
]
, and Ben-Zvi and Nevins
have extended this result to suitable
quotients of Cohen–Macaulay
varieties

[
5
, Theorem 1.2]
. As
k[t]

is smooth, the Weyl algebra

\mathscr{D}_{k[t]}
is a Hopf
algebroid,
and with the above Hopf algebroid
structure on
\mathscr{D}_{k[t^{2},t^{3}]}
, the
Morita-equivalence becomes
strict monoidal, see
Theorem
7.26
.

1.4.
Motivation

We find the above results
interesting for several
reasons:

First, being a Hopf algebroid has
implications for the homological
properties of
\mathscr{D}_{A}
and yields
additional algebraic structures
on
\mathrm{Ext}
-,

\mathrm{Tor}
-,
and
\mathrm{Cotor}
-groups
over
\mathscr{D}_{A}
.
For example, the derived version
of the internal
hom adjunction

\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathscr{D}_{A}}(M,N)\cong\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathscr{D}_{A}}(A,\mathrm{Hom}_{A}(M,N))
is a spectral sequence
which in good cases allows one to
compute

\mathrm{Ext}_{\mathscr{D}_{A}}(M,N)
using
a projective resolution of the

\mathscr{D}_{A}
-module
A
. See for example

[
30
,
31
,
32
]

for more information.

Second, Moerdijk and Mrčun have
obtained a Hopf algebroid version
of the Cartier–Milnor–Moore theorem

[
44
, Theorem 3.1]
.
The classical theorem
asserts that
over a field of characteristic zero,
the cocommutative
and conilpotent Hopf algebras
are precisely the
universal enveloping algebras
of Lie algebras
(see e.g.
[
35
, Theorem 3.5]

and
[
66
, Theorem
V.4]
,
neither Cartier nor Kostant had
published their original
proofs). The
Hopf algebroid version
establishes a correspondence
between
A
-projective
Lie–Rinehart algebras and
cocommutative, conilpotent
Hopf algebroids satisfying some
projectivity assumption. As
Theorem
1.1

demonstrates, a
classification beyond the
projective case could be a
non-trivial but interesting endeavour.

Third, the underlying coalgebras
over
A
are neat examples that
demonstrate some subtleties
of the theory
of coalgebras over commutative rings
that are not fields:
at first, the formulas
in Theorem
1.2

do not seem to
support the claim that
\mathscr{D}_{k[t^{2},t^{3}]}

is cocommutative and
conilpotent. On closer
inspection, one convinces
oneself of the cocommutativity
which is veiled by the
relations in the

k[t^{2},t^{3}]
-module
\mathscr{D}_{k[t^{2},t^{3}]}
.
Concerning conilpotency,
one observes
a phenomenon that cannot occur for
coaugmented coalgebras over
fields:

\mathscr{D}_{k[t^{2},t^{3}]}
is
an example of a coaugmented
coalgebra whose primitive
filtration is not a coalgebra
filtration (see

Lemma

7.41
).

Next, the question whether

\mathscr{D}_{A}
admits an antipode
aligns nicely with the
theory of symmetric numerical
semigroups. Their
monoid algebras are Gorenstein

[
34
]
,
and this yields an
isomorphism of
A
-rings

S\colon\mathscr{D}_{A}\rightarrow\mathscr{D}^{\mathrm{op}}_{A}
. See

[
53
]

for a recent elementary proof of
this fact
due to
Quinlan-Gallego and

[
77
, Corollary 4.9]

for a more general (and less
elementary) proof due to Yekutieli.

Last but not least, we present
Theorem
1.1
as an
application of a general theory of
descent of Hopf algebroid
structures that might be of interest
in itself and can be applied more
widely and in particular to
the study of differential
operators in characteristic

p
. We briefly sketch this in
the introduction for
experts on Hopf algebroids,
see Section 5 for the details.

1.5.
Descent for Hopf
algebroids

Let
k
be any commutative ring,
and assume
we are given a bialgebroid

C
over a commutative

k
-algebra
K
and
two subalgebras
A,B\subseteq K
.
The unit object in the
monoidal category of
C
-modules is

K
, with
c\in C
acting on

b\in K
as
\varepsilon(b\blacktriangleright c)=\varepsilon(cb)
where

\varepsilon
is the counit
of
C
.

Now define

\displaystyle C(B,A)

\displaystyle:=\{c\in C\mid\varepsilon(cb)\in A\,\forall b\in B\},

\displaystyle R(B,A)

\displaystyle:=\{r\colon C(B,A)\rightarrow A,c\mapsto\textstyle\sum_{i}\varepsilon(a_{i}cb_{i})\mid a_{i}\in A,b_{i}\in B\}.

Our main result provides criteria
for coalgebra, bi- and Hopf algebroid
structures to descend to

C(B,A)
if
C\cong K\otimes_{A}C(B,A)
. We are interested
especially in the case that
A\subseteq K
is a localisation
(in our application,
K
corresponds
to the smooth locus
of a singular variety with
coordinate ring
A
), so
K
is
typically not faithfully flat over

A
(which is usually assumed
in results involving descent).
However, we prove that it
suffices to verify that
C(B,A)
is

R(B,A)
-locally
projective (see
Section
5.1
for this
notion):

Theorem 1.3
.

If

C(B,A)
is

R(B,A)
-locally
projective and

\mu\colon K\otimes_{A}C(B,A)\rightarrow C,\quad x\otimes_{A}c\mapsto xc

is surjective,
then we have:

(1)

(\Delta,\varepsilon)

uniquely restricts to a

B|A
-coring structure on

C(B,A)
.

(2)

If
B=A
, this turns

C(A,A)
into a bialgebroid over

A
.

(3)

If
C
is in addition
a left Hopf
algebroid, then
C(A,A)
is a left
Hopf algebroid.

Conventions

From now on,

k
is a fixed commutative ground

over

Differential

Throughout the paper,
k
is a
commutative ring, “algebra”
means “unital associative algebra
over
k
”, and
A
is a
commutative algebra.
This section contains

A
-ring

\mathscr{D}_{A}
of

,

2.1.
Filtered and graded
(co)algebras

There will be several filtered
and graded objects in this
paper, so we begin by fixing
our notation and conventions
concerning these.

Definition 2.1
.

A
filtered object

V
in a
category
\mathbf{C}
is a chain of
monomorphisms
f_{i}\colon V_{i}\rightarrow V_{i+1}
,
i\in\mathbb{Z}
.
The graded object

{\operatorname{gr}}(V)

associated
to
a filtered object
V
is
given by
{\operatorname{gr}}(V)_{i}:=V_{i}/V_{i-1}=\mathrm{coker}\,f_{i-1}
.

Here and elsewhere, we tacitly
assume that all occurring (co)limits
such as (co)products, (co)kernels
etc. exist.

More generally, one may replace

\mathbb{Z}
by any

directed
set
, that is, a preorder
(\Lambda,\leq)
such that any two elements

\lambda,\mu\in\Lambda
have
an
upper bound

\nu\in\Lambda
with

\lambda\leq\nu,\mu\leq\nu
,
and consider functors
V\colon\Lambda\rightarrow\mathbf{C}
; these
will be called

directed systems
in

\mathbf{C}
while functors

\Lambda^{\mathrm{op}}\rightarrow\mathbf{C}

will be called
inverse
systems
. If
\Lambda
is
discrete (
\lambda\leq\mu\Leftrightarrow\lambda=\mu
), we call

V
a
\Lambda
-graded
object
in
\mathbf{C}
. As is customary,
we will often use the same symbol

V
to denote the colimit of a
directed system
V
.

Example 2.2
.

If
V
is a
\mathbb{Z}
-graded
respectively a filtered

k
-module, then we denote

\bigoplus_{i}V_{i}
respectively

\bigcup_{i}V_{i}
also by
V
.

With natural transformations as
morphisms, the directed respectively
inverse systems in
\mathbf{C}
(and in
particular filtered and graded
objects) form again categories
and
{\operatorname{gr}}
is a functor from filtered
to
\mathbb{Z}
-graded objects.
If
\Lambda
and

\mathbf{C}
are both monoidal categories,
the category of
directed systems is also
monoidal with the
Day
convolution
as monoidal structure,
see e.g.
[
37
,
Section 6.2]
.

Example 2.3
.

The tensor product
of
\mathbb{Z}
-graded
k
-modules

V,W
has

(V\otimes W)_{l}=\bigoplus_{m+n=l}V_{m}\otimes W_{n}.

In the tensor product of filtered

k
-modules,

(V\otimes W)_{l}
is the image
of the canonical morphism

\bigoplus_{m+n\leq l}V_{m}\otimes W_{n}\rightarrow V\otimes W

induced by
the tensor products of the
embeddings
V_{m}\rightarrow V,W_{n}\rightarrow W
.

Thus we may consider
graded

respectively
filtered
(co)algebras
, that is,
(co)monoids in the monoidal
categories of graded respectively
filtered
k
-modules, as well as the categories of graded
respectively filtered (co)modules
over such (co)algebras. We will
also iterate the process and
consider for example filtered
coalgebras
D
in the category of
graded modules over
a (commutative) graded algebra
A
.

Remark 2.4
.

The category of
\mathbb{Z}
-graded
k
-modules is closed
monoidal: for any graded
modules
V,W
, their
internal hom
is the graded module with
[V,W]_{d}:=\{\varphi\in\mathrm{Hom}(V,W)\mid\varphi(V_{i})\subseteq W_{i+d}\,\forall i\in\mathbb{Z}\}
.

2.2.
Almost commutative
algebras

Let
H
be a filtered algebra.

Definition 2.5
.

H
is
almost
commutative
if
{\operatorname{gr}}(H)
is
commutative.

So explicitly,
H
is almost
commutative if
for all
a\in H_{i},b\in H_{j}
, we have

ab-ba\in H_{i+j-1}
.
In this case,
the
Poisson bracket
of

[a]\in{\operatorname{gr}}(H)_{i},[b]\in{\operatorname{gr}}(H)_{j}
is

\{[a],[b]\}:=[ab-ba]\in{\operatorname{gr}}(H)_{i+j-1}.

The
following is an
immediate consequence of the
above definition; in short,
it states that

({\operatorname{gr}}(H),\{-,-\})
is a

graded Poisson algebra

(with Poisson bracket of degree
-1):

Proposition 2.6
.

If
H
is an almost
commutative filtered algebra,
then

({\operatorname{gr}}(H),\{-,-\})
is a
Lie algebra over
k
, and
for all
X,Y,Z\in{\operatorname{gr}}(H)
, we have

\{X,YZ\}=\{X,Y\}Z+Y\{X,Z\}.

The aim of this
Section
2
is to
recall that any commutative
algebra
A
defines three
almost commutative filtered algebras
with
H_{0}=A
:

(1)

the universal enveloping
algebra
\mathscr{U}_{A}
of the
Lie–Rinehart algebra

\operatorname{Der}(A)
of all
k
-linear
derivations
A\rightarrow A
,

(2)

the algebra
\mathscr{D}_{A}
of
differential operators on
A
,
and

(3)

its subalgebra
\mathcal{D}_{A}
generated by

\mathscr{D}_{A}^{1}=A\oplus\operatorname{Der}(A)
.

At the end we will recall that
in characteristic 0, they
all agree when
\operatorname{Der}(A)
is a finitely
generated projective
A
-module
(Theorem
2.22
); in
Section
3
this will
be related to the smoothness of
A
.

2.3.
Augmented

A
-rings

We adopt the following
terminology:

Definition 2.7
.

An
A
-ring
is an
algebra morphism

\eta\colon A\rightarrow H
.
An
augmented

A
-ring
is an
A
-ring

\eta\colon A\rightarrow H

together with a

k
-linear map
\varepsilon\colon H\rightarrow A
for which

\varepsilon\circ\eta=\mathrm{id}_{A}
and

\mathrm{ker}\,\varepsilon\subset H

is a left ideal.

In an augmented
A
-ring, we
usually
suppress
\eta
and
identify
A
with its image
in
H
, that is, view
A
as
a subalgebra of
H
so that
we have an
A
-module decomposition

H=A\oplus\mathrm{ker}\,\varepsilon
.

Remark 2.8
.

Even though
A
is assumed to be
commutative,

\mathrm{im}\,\eta
is not
assumed to lie in the centre of

H
. That is,

H
is a monoid in the category

A\mbox{-}\mathbf{Mod}\mbox{-}A
of
A
-
A
-bimodules
rather than a
monoid in the monoidal category

A\mbox{-}\mathbf{Mod}
of

A
-modules, which would be
an
A
-algebra.
Since
A
is commutative,
there is no actual difference
between left and right
A
-actions,
so there are
two forgetful functors

A\mbox{-}\mathbf{Mod}\mbox{-}A\rightarrow A\mbox{-}\mathbf{Mod}
. When
considering
H
as an
A
-module
with respect to left multiplication,

A\otimes H\rightarrow a\otimes h\mapsto a\smalltriangleright h:=\eta(a)h,

we will denote the resulting

A
-module
just by
H
. When forgetting the
left action, we will instead
denote the resulting

A
-module by
\bar{H}
,

A\otimes\bar{H}\rightarrow\bar{H},\quad a\otimes h\mapsto a\blacktriangleright h:=h\eta(a).

Remark 2.9
.

Note also
that an augmentation

\varepsilon
of an
A
-ring
is not an algebra map in general.
Instead, it
can be equivalently described
as a left
H
-module structure
on
A
that extends the
multiplication in
A
; in
terms of
\varepsilon
and
\eta
, the
action of
h\in H
on
a\in A
yields

\varepsilon(h\eta(a))=\varepsilon(a\blacktriangleright h),

while
\varepsilon
is by
construction
A
-linear with respect
to the left
A
-action,

\varepsilon(a\smalltriangleright h)=\varepsilon(\eta(a)h)=a\varepsilon(h)
.

Example 2.10
.

Consider the canonical
injection

\eta\colon A\hookrightarrow\operatorname{End}(A)\coloneqq\mathrm{Hom}(A,A),\quad a\mapsto(x\mapsto ax)

that assigns to each
a\in A
the corresponding
multiplication operator on
A
.
This turns
\operatorname{End}(A)
into an
augmented
A
-ring with
augmentation

\varepsilon(\varphi):=\varphi(1)
. Yet an equivalent way
to define an augmentation of an

A
-ring
H
is as an

A
-ring morphism

\hat{\varepsilon}\colon H\rightarrow\operatorname{End}(A)
.

By applying the above to
the opposite ring
H^{\mathrm{op}}
of

H
, we obtain that
right
H
-module structures on

A
that extend the multiplication
in
A
correspond bijectively to
maps

\bar{\varepsilon}\colon\bar{H}\to A
that are
A
-linear,

\bar{\varepsilon}(a\blacktriangleright h)=\bar{\varepsilon}(h\eta(a))=\bar{\varepsilon}(h)a=a\bar{\varepsilon}(h),\quad a\in A,h\in H,

satisfy
\bar{\varepsilon}\circ\eta=\mathrm{id}_{A}
, and
for which

\mathrm{ker}\,\bar{\varepsilon}

is a right ideal in
H
; the
corresponding right action of
h\in H
on
a\in A
yields

\bar{\varepsilon}(a\smalltriangleright h)
.

2.4.
Differential
operators

Now we recall the notion of
a differential operator over

A
.
We refer e.g. to

[
16
,
43
,
21
]

for more information.

By definition,
\eta

identifies
A
in
Example
2.10
with the

differential
operators of order 0
on
A
.
The differential operators of
higher order are defined
inductively as follows:

Definition 2.11
.

The
A
-ring of

differential
operators
over
A

is the almost commutative
subalgebra

\mathscr{D}_{A}=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\mathscr{D}_{A}^{n}\subseteq\operatorname{End}(A),

where

\mathscr{D}_{A}^{0}:=A
and for
n\geq 1
, we set

\mathscr{D}_{A}^{n}=\{D\in\operatorname{End}(A)\mid Da-aD\in\mathscr{D}_{A}^{n-1}\,\forall a\in A\}.

Remark 2.12
.

For
n\geq 1
,
the embedding

\mathscr{D}_{A}^{n}\hookrightarrow\mathscr{D}_{A}^{n+1}

does in general not split as a
morphism of
A
-modules, while
for
n=0
we have a canonical
isomorphism

\mathscr{D}_{A}^{1}\rightarrow A\oplus\operatorname{Der}(A)
,
D\mapsto(D(1),D-D(1))
.

The
A
-ring
\mathscr{D}_{A}
is by definition
an almost commutative filtered
algebra. We use upper indices
for the filtration given by the
order of a differential operator
since in our main examples,

A
is itself a
\mathbb{Z}
-graded algebra, and then
we use lower indices to denote the
differential operator of degree

,

\mathscr{D}_{A,d}:=\{D\in\mathscr{D}_{A}\mid D(A_{r})\subseteq A_{r+d}\forall r\in\mathbb{Z}\},\quad\mathscr{D}_{A,d}^{n}:=\mathscr{D}_{A}^{n}\cap\mathscr{D}_{A,d}.

Note that in general we have

\bigoplus_{d}\mathscr{D}_{A,d}\subsetneq\mathscr{D}_{A}
, but we will show in
Corollary
3.11

that equality holds when

A
is
essentially of finite
type
(is
a localisation of a finitely
generated algebra).

Finally, we define the following
subalgebra of
\mathscr{D}_{A}
:

Definition 2.13
.

We denote by

\mathcal{D}_{A}:=\bigcup_{n\geq 0}\mathcal{D}_{A}^{n},\quad\mathcal{D}_{A}^{n}:=\mathrm{span}_{A}\{X_{1}\cdots X_{m}\mid X_{i}\in\operatorname{Der}(A),0\leq m\leq n\}

the augmented
A
-subring of

\mathscr{D}_{A}
generated (as an algebra)
by
\mathscr{D}_{A}^{1}
.

2.5.
Principal parts

For an alternative description of

\mathscr{D}_{A}
, consider
A\otimes A
as

A
-module with multiplication in
the first tensor component as

A
-action so that
we have an isomorphism

\operatorname{End}(A)=\mathrm{Hom}(A,A)\cong(A\otimes A)^{*}=\mathrm{Hom}_{A}(A\otimes A,A).

Here and elsewhere,
M^{*}
is the
dual of a left
A
-module

M
.
This identifies
D\in\operatorname{End}(A)
with
the

A
-linear functional
on
A\otimes A
given by

a\otimes b\mapsto aD(b)
.
A straightforward computation (see
e.g.
[
24
, Proposition 2.2.3]
)
shows that

\mathscr{D}_{A}^{n}
consists under this
isomorphism
precisely
of those
A
-linear
functionals on
A\otimes A

that vanish on
I_{A}^{n+1}
, where

I_{A}
is the
kernel of the multiplication map

\mu_{A}
of
A
.
Thus we have

(2.1)

\mathscr{D}_{A}^{n}\cong(\mathscr{P}_{A}^{n})^{*}=\mathrm{Hom}_{A}(\mathscr{P}_{A}^{n},A),\text{ where }\mathscr{P}_{A}^{n}:=(A\otimes A)/I_{A}^{n+1}.

Definition 2.14
.

\mathscr{P}_{A}^{n}
is
the
module of
principal parts
of order
n
of
A
.

This terminology will be explained
further in
Remark
3.20
.

2.6.
Lie–Rinehart
algebras

Here we recall from

[
56
]

the notion of a
Lie–Rinehart algebra

over

A
and
of its universal
enveloping algebra.

Definition 2.15
.

A
Lie–Rinehart
algebra

over
A
is a Lie algebra

L
over
k
together
with an
A
-module
structure

a\otimes X\mapsto a\cdot X
on
L
and an

L
-module structure

X\otimes a\mapsto X(a)

on

which satisfy

X(ab)=aX(b)+X(a)b,\quad aX(b)=(a\cdot X)(b),

[X,a\cdot Y]=a\cdot[X,Y]+X(a)\cdot Y,

for
X,Y\in L
,

.
The Lie algebra morphism

\omega\colon L\to\operatorname{Der}(A)
,

X\mapsto X(-)
is referred
to as the

.

Remark 2.16
.

When
A
is the
\mathbb{R}
-algebra

of smooth functions
on a smooth manifold

and
L

is the module of sections of a
locally trivial real vector
bundle over

, one also
refers to

as a
Lie
algebroid
.
Rinehart himself called
Lie–Rinehart algebras

(k,A)
-Lie algebras. The
terminology Lie–Rinehart
algebra goes back to
Huebschmann

[

]
, who
however uses it for the pair

(A,L)
in which both

and

are variable.

The following
consequence of
Proposition
2.6

says that Lie–Rinehart
algebras are in a sense
infinitesimal first order
approximations
of almost commutative
algebras:

Corollary 2.17
.

If
H
is an almost
commutative algebra, then

\{-,-\}
turns

L=H_{1}/H_{0}
into a Lie–Rinehart
algebra over
H_{0}
with
anchor given by

X(a):=\{X,a\}

and
a\cdot X
given by left
multiplication in
{\operatorname{gr}}(H)
.

2.7.
Universal enveloping
algebras

Conversely, any Lie–Rinehart
algebra
L

determines an almost commutative
augmented
A
-ring with the
following universal property:

Definition 2.18
.

The
universal
enveloping algebra
of a
Lie–Rinehart algebra
L
over

A
is the universal pair

(\mathscr{U}_{A}(L),\rho)
, where

(1)

\mathscr{U}_{A}(L)
is an
A
-ring
and

(2)

\rho\colon L\rightarrow\mathscr{U}_{A}(L)

is a
k
-linear map satisfying

\displaystyle\rho([X,Y])

\displaystyle=\rho(X)\rho(Y)-\rho(Y)\rho(X),

\displaystyle\rho(a\cdot X)

\displaystyle=\eta(a)\rho(X),

\displaystyle\eta(X(a))

\displaystyle=\rho(X)\eta(a)-\eta(a)\rho(X),

where
a\in A
and
X,Y\in L
.

The algebra
\mathscr{U}_{A}(L)
is
constructed as a quotient of the
tensor algebra

T_{A}(L\oplus A)
,
of the
A
-bimodule
L\oplus A

with left and right action

a(X,b):=(a\cdot X,ab),\quad(X,b)a:=(a\cdot X,X(a)+ba).

The filtration with respect
to which
\mathscr{U}_{A}(L)
is almost
commutative is similarly to
that of
\mathcal{D}_{A}
given by

(2.2)

\mathscr{U}_{A}(L)^{n}:=\mathrm{span}_{A}\{\rho(X_{1})\cdots\rho(X_{m})\mid X_{i}\in L,m\leq n\}.

We refer to
[
56
, p197]

for the details.

Therein, Rinehart has also
extended the
Poincaré-Birkhoff-Witt
theorem from Lie to
Lie–Rinehart algebras:

Theorem 2.19

(
[
56
]
, Theorem 3.1 on
p198)
.

If the
A
-module
L
is
projective, then
\rho

induces an
A
-algebra
isomorphism

\operatorname{Sym}_{A}L\to{\operatorname{gr}}(\mathscr{U}_{A}(L))
.

Here,
\operatorname{Sym}_{A}M
denotes the
symmetric algebra of an

A
-module
M
, that is, the free
commutative
A
-algebra on

M
.

The universal property of

\mathscr{U}_{A}(L)
implies in
particular that the
anchor map
\omega
extends
uniquely to a
morphism of
A
-rings

(2.3)

\hat{\varepsilon}\colon\mathscr{U}_{A}(L)\rightarrow\mathcal{D}_{A}\subseteq\mathscr{D}_{A}\subseteq\operatorname{End}(A),

and in this way,

\mathscr{U}_{A}(L)
is canonically
an augmented

A
-ring.
By construction,
we thus have

\omega=\hat{\varepsilon}\circ\rho
,
which implies in particular:

Lemma 2.20
.

If
\omega\colon L\rightarrow\operatorname{Der}(A)
is injective, so
is
\rho\colon L\rightarrow\mathscr{U}_{A}(L)
.

Remark 2.21
.

In general,
\rho
is not
injective; in particular,
when
A=k
, then a
Lie–Rinehart algebra is just a
Lie algebra, and
\mathscr{U}_{A}(L)
is its
universal enveloping algebra.
In this setting, Cohn has
shown that
\rho
is
injective if
L
is
torsion-free as an abelian
group under addition, and
given an example
for which it is not

[
15
, Section 5 on
p202]
.

For a general Lie–Rinehart algebra,

\hat{\varepsilon}\colon\mathscr{U}_{A}(L)\rightarrow\mathscr{D}_{A}

is neither injective nor
surjective.
However, let us now focus on
the case
L=\operatorname{Der}(A)
,
\omega=\mathrm{id}_{\operatorname{Der}(A)}
, and abbreviate

\mathscr{U}_{A}:=\mathscr{U}_{A}(\operatorname{Der}(A)).

In this case, we have:

Theorem 2.22
.

If
\mathbb{Q}\subseteq A

and the
A
-module

\operatorname{Der}(A)
is finitely
generated projective, then

\hat{\varepsilon}\colon\mathscr{U}_{A}\rightarrow\mathscr{D}_{A}
is an
isomorphism. In particular,
we have
\mathscr{D}_{A}=\mathcal{D}_{A}
.

Proof.

The morphism
\mathscr{U}_{A}\rightarrow\mathscr{D}_{A}
is a morphism of almost
commutative

A
-rings, hence induces an
associated morphism

(2.4)

{\operatorname{gr}}(\mathscr{U}_{A})\rightarrow{\operatorname{gr}}(\mathscr{D}_{A})

of graded
A
-algebras.
By the
Poincaré-Birkhoff-Witt
theorem
(Theorem
2.19
),
we also have an isomorphism of

A
-algebras

\operatorname{Sym}_{A}\operatorname{Der}(A)\rightarrow{\operatorname{gr}}(\mathscr{U}_{A}).

The composition of the two is
the canonical morphism

(2.5)

\tau\colon\operatorname{Sym}_{A}\operatorname{Der}(A)\rightarrow{\operatorname{gr}}(\mathscr{D}_{A})

that results from the fact
that
{\operatorname{gr}}(\mathscr{D}_{A})^{1}=\operatorname{Der}(A)
and
that
{\operatorname{gr}}(\mathscr{D}_{A})
is a
commutative
A
-algebra (
\operatorname{Sym}_{A}\operatorname{Der}(A)
is the free commutative

A
-algebra on the

-module

\operatorname{Der}(A)
).
It was shown by Narváez-Macarro
in

[
49
, Corollary 2.17]

that

is
an isomorphism.
Therefore, we deduce
that (

) is an
isomorphism. An elementary
induction argument implies that the
original filtered morphism

\mathscr{U}_{A}\rightarrow\mathscr{D}_{A}
is thus
an isomorphism, too.
∎

Remark 2.23
.

If
\mathbb{Q}\not\subseteq A
,

.

Any

-ring

This section recalls the notions of

We also show that

As before,

3.1.
Smooth and étale

We begin by defining

(1)

(2)

(3)

algebra morphism
\rho\colon A\rightarrow B
with
\psi\circ\rho=\varphi
, and

(4)

it is

is both formally smooth and
unramified.

.

Proof.

The main claim follows
from the fact that
if
\rho\colon A\rightarrow B

lifts
\varphi\colon A\rightarrow B
, then the other

\rho+\delta
with

\delta\in\operatorname{Der}(A,\mathrm{ker}\,\psi)

(see e.g.

[

).
If

is a localisation,
it is in particular an

equivalent to

being an

.
∎

Proposition 3.13
.

A formally smooth algebra
is differentially
smooth.

Proof.

See

[

, Proposition IV.16.10.2]
.
∎

Corollary 3.14
.

If
A
is smooth, then
we have

\mathscr{P}_{A}^{n}\cong\bigoplus_{i=0}^{n}\operatorname{Sym}_{A}^{i}\Omega^{1}_{A}\cong\bigoplus_{i=0}^{n}I_{A}^{i}/I^{i+1}_{A},

and these

-modules are all
finitely generated projective.
In particular,
\operatorname{Der}(A)
is
finitely generated projective.

Proof.

By the previous proposition,

\Omega^{1}_{A}
is projective, and by

(2) it is
finitely generated.
Hence its dual

\operatorname{Der}(A)
is finitely generated

The statement about the

is shown by an easy
induction argument, using that the

\operatorname{Sym}^{n}_{A}M
of a finitely generated

are finitely
generated projective, and that

0\rightarrow I_{A}^{n+1}\rightarrow I_{A}^{n}\rightarrow I_{A}^{n}/I_{A}^{n+1}\cong\operatorname{Sym}^{n}_{A}\Omega^{1}_{A}\rightarrow 0

split.
∎

Remark 3.15
.

If

Remark 3.16
.

Let

.

In

Example 3.18
.

Among the examples studied by
Sweedler were algebras

that
are
purely inseparable

[
65
, Definition 13.14]
,
meaning that

consists of nilpotent

being nilpotent,
say

.
We then have

\mathscr{P}_{A}^{m}\cong A\otimes A
for

, and therefore

\mathscr{D}_{A}=\mathscr{D}_{A}^{n}\cong(A\otimes A)^{*}\cong\operatorname{End}(A)
.

Example 3.19
.

As a concrete example,
consider

and

, the ring of truncated
polynomials, and let

\hbar\in A

. Then

is a free

-module with

, and

(as we are in

\Omega^{1}_{A}=I_{A}\cong A
and

\operatorname{Der}(A)\cong A
(with a basis

that descends to a derivation of

\operatorname{Sym}^{n}_{A}\Omega^{1}_{A}\cong A
for all

for

.
For this algebra,
the canonical map

is
surjective, but not injective
(its kernel is the ideal
generated by

).

Remark 3.20
.

By an
affine
scheme
over
k
,
we mean a representable
functor
X
on the category of
commutative algebras, and we call

smooth if the

representing

are smooth.
In this case, the affine scheme

TX
over

represented by the

-algebra

\operatorname{Sym}_{A}\Omega^{1}_{A}
is the total space of
the

of

.
Its unit map is a morphism of

of affine schemes

over

; this is a locally trivial

(or

)

of

.

3.3.
Regular algebras

Let us briefly discuss
the relation to the more classical
concept of regularity, and that
smoothness means regularity when
A

is the coordinate ring of an affine
variety over a perfect field.

Definition 3.21
.

(1)

A commutative
ring
A
is
regular

if it is Noetherian and for all
prime ideals

\mathfrak{p}\subset A
, the
maximal ideal
\mathfrak{p}A_{\mathfrak{p}}
of the
localisation
A_{\mathfrak{p}}

can be generated by

\dim(A_{\mathfrak{p}})

elements, where

\dim(A_{\mathfrak{p}})

denotes the
Krull dimension of
A_{\mathfrak{p}}
.

(2)

An affine variety
(irreducible algebraic set)

X\subseteq k^{N}
is

non-singular
if
its ring
k[X]
of regular functions

X\rightarrow k
is regular.

Over perfect fields, this just means
smoothness:

Proposition 3.22
.

If
k
is a field and
A
is
essentially of finite type over
k
,
then
A
is smooth if and only if

A\otimes A
is regular. If
k

is perfect, this is true if and only
if
A
is regular.

Proof.

It is shown in

[
12
, Proposition X.6.5.8 on
p78]

that under the assumptions made,
the following statements are
equivalent:

(1)

A\otimes A
is regular.

(2)

A
is
absolutely regular
,
meaning that for any finite radical
extension
k^{\prime}
of
k
,

k^{\prime}\otimes A
is regular.

(3)

I_{A}
is a complete
intersection.

In

[
12
, Théorème X.7.10.4 on
p103]

it is shown that
I_{A}
is a complete
intersection if and only if
A
is
smooth. Finally, when
k
is
perfect, then

A
is absolutely regular if and
only if it is regular

[
12
, Example X.6.4.1 on
p75]

(by definition of a perfect
field,
k^{\prime}
is a separable
extension of
k
, hence

k^{\prime}\otimes A
is regular
if
A
is regular,
see
[
12
, Proposition X.6.3.5
b) on
p75]
).
∎

Corollary 3.23
.

An affine variety
X
over a perfect
field
k
is non-singular if and
only if
k[X]
is smooth.

In particular,
\Omega^{1}_{A}
is
projective in this case. However,
as we have seen in
Example
3.19
,
this is not
sufficient to imply
regularity in general.
Let us finally
point out that in the case of
integral domains, it actually
is:

Proposition 3.24
.

If
k
is a perfect field and

A
is an
integral domain and essentially of
finite type, then
\Omega^{1}_{A}

is projective if and only if

A
is regular.

Proof.

The earliest reference for
this fact that we are aware of is

[
25
]
.
Therein, the fundamental
insight that

\Omega^{1}_{A}\cong\mathrm{Tor}^{A\otimes A}_{1}(A,A)
is the first Hochschild homology
of the algebra
A
with coefficients
in itself sneaks in somewhat tacitly
on p391,
but this follows directly from the
definitions. Thus

[
25
, Theorem 2.1]

implies that if
A
is
regular, so is
A\otimes A
, and then

[
25
, Theorem 3.1]

(applied to the multiplication
map
A\otimes A\rightarrow A
) implies
that

\Omega^{1}_{A}
is
projective. The converse is

[
25
, Theorem 4.1]
.
∎

Example 3.25
.

The prototypical example of

A
we
are interested in is the cusp,

A=k[t^{2},t^{3}]\subset k[t]
.
If
x:=t^{2}
and

, then

is as an algebra isomorphic to

In particular,

\omega=2x\mathrm{d}y-3y\mathrm{d}x\neq 0

.

Smooth manifolds

For the sake of completeness, let us
also point out that
Theorem

applies equally
well in the setting of differential
rather than algebraic geometry, that

So as in
Remark
2.16
,

is the algebra

-valued functions

See any textbook on

.

In this case,

4.
Hopf
algebroids

In this
Section
4
we gather all
necessary definitions from the
theory of corings,
bialgebroids, and Hopf
algebroids over commutative
base algebras. For more information and
background, see for example

[
7
,
8
,
19
]
.
For bialgebras and Hopf
algebras, see
e.g.
[
64
,
45
,
13
,
54
]
.

We retain the assumption that

A,B
are commutative
algebras over a commutative
ring
k
.

4.1.
B|A
-corings

A Hopf algebroid over
A

is in particular an augmented

A
-ring and at the same time a

coalgebra
over
A
, that is,
an
A
-module
D
together
with
A
-module morphisms

\Delta\colon D\rightarrow D\otimes_{A}D
and
\varepsilon\colon D\rightarrow A

that satisfy

\displaystyle(\Delta\otimes_{A}\mathrm{id}_{D})\circ\Delta

\displaystyle=(\mathrm{id}_{D}\otimes_{A}\Delta)\circ\Delta,

\displaystyle(\varepsilon\otimes_{A}\mathrm{id}_{D})\circ\Delta

\displaystyle=(\mathrm{id}_{D}\otimes_{A}\varepsilon)\circ\Delta=\mathrm{id}_{D},

where we suppress both the
isomorphisms
A\otimes_{A}D\cong D\otimes_{A}A\cong D

and
(D\otimes_{A}D)\otimes_{A}D\cong D\otimes_{A}(D\otimes_{A}D)
. Recall that
a coalgebra is

cocommutative
if

\Delta=\tau\circ\Delta
, where
\tau(c\otimes_{A}d):=d\otimes_{A}c
.
As usual, we adopt Sweedler’s
notation
c_{(1)}\otimes_{A}c_{(2)}:=\Delta(c)
,

c\in D
.

Remark 4.1
.

A coalgebra is a comonoid in

A\mbox{-}\mathbf{Mod}
. In contrast to
this, an
A
-ring is a monoid
in
A\mbox{-}\mathbf{Mod}\mbox{-}A

(recall Remarks
2.8
and

2.9
). For a Hopf algebroid,
the compatibility between the coalgebra
structure and the right

A
-module structure will be the
special case of the next definition
in which
A=B
.

Definition 4.2
.

A
B|A
-coring
is an

A
-
B
-bimodule
D
with
symmetric action of
k
,
together with an
A
-coalgebra
structure
such that for all
d\in D,b\in B
, we have

(db)_{(1)}\otimes_{A}(db)_{(2)}=d_{(1)}b\otimes_{A}d_{(2)}=d_{(1)}\otimes_{A}d_{(2)}b.

There are
several motivations for this
concept;
for us, the essence is
that
B|A
-corings
D
are
precisely the bimodules for
which the functor
D\otimes_{B}-
takes

B
-coalgebras to

A
-coalgebras,
see
Theorem

4.28

below.

Remark 4.3
.

The concept of

B|A
-coring admits an
extension to noncommutative
base algebras
A,B
– in this
case,
D
is an
A\otimes A^{\mathrm{op}}
-
B\otimes B^{\mathrm{op}}
-bimodule, see

[
70
, Theorem-Definition 3.10]

or
[
68
]

for the full
definition.

Note also that

B|A
-corings are preduals of

A\otimes B
-algebras:

Lemma 4.4
.

The
A
-linear dual
D^{*}=\mathrm{Hom}_{A}(D,A)
of a
B|A
-coring
is canonically an algebra
over
A\otimes B
with unit given
by

(4.1)

\sigma\colon A\otimes B\rightarrow D^{*},\quad\sigma(a\otimes b)(d)=\varepsilon(adb)

and product

D^{*}\otimes_{A\otimes B}D^{*}\rightarrow D^{*}

given by the so-called convolution

(4.2)

(r*s)(d):=r(d_{(1)})s(d_{(2)}),\quad r,s\in D^{*},d\in D.

Proof.

This is verified by
straightforward verification,
see for
example
[
13
,
7
,
70
]
.
∎

4.2.
Conilpotency

The Hopf algebroids that we
will construct are not just
cocommutative, but also

conilpotent

(the term
cocomplete
is used instead
in
[
44
]
),
a property
that is crucial in the
structure theory especially of
cocommutative coalgebras.

Definition 4.5
.

A
coaugmentation
of a
coalgebra
D
over
A

is a coalgebra
morphism
\eta\colon A\rightarrow D
.

Remark 4.6
.

Equivalently,
a coaugmentation is given by a

group-like element

g\in D
, that is, an
element with
\varepsilon(g)=1
and
\Delta(g)=g\otimes_{A}g
(namely
g:=\eta(1)
).

A choice of
coaugmentation yields
dually to the case of augmented

A
-rings an
A
-module splitting

D\rightarrow A\oplus\mathrm{ker}\,\varepsilon,\quad c\mapsto(\varepsilon(c),c-\varepsilon(c)g)

and the (in general not
counital, but coassociative)

reduced coproduct

\bar{\Delta}\colon\mathrm{ker}\,\varepsilon\rightarrow\mathrm{ker}\,\varepsilon\otimes_{A}\mathrm{ker}\,\varepsilon,\quad c\mapsto\Delta(c)-g\otimes_{A}c-c\otimes_{A}g.

We denote by

\bar{\Delta}^{n}\colon\mathrm{ker}\,\varepsilon\rightarrow(\mathrm{ker}\,\varepsilon)^{\otimes_{A}n+1}

the map defined inductively by

\bar{\Delta}^{0}=\operatorname{id}_{\ker\varepsilon},\quad\bar{\Delta}^{1}=\bar{\Delta},\quad\bar{\Delta}^{n+1}=(\bar{\Delta}^{n}\otimes_{A}\mathrm{id}_{\mathrm{ker}\,\varepsilon})\circ\bar{\Delta}.

By construction,
the assignment
(D,\Delta,\varepsilon,\eta)\mapsto(\mathrm{ker}\,\varepsilon,\bar{\Delta})
is an
equivalence between the
categories of coaugmented
counital coalgebras and of
noncounital coalgebras.

Definition 4.7
.

(1)

The
A
-module

\operatorname{Prim}(D):=\mathrm{ker}\,\bar{\Delta}
is the set of

primitive elements
in

D
, and the
sequence
\{\mathrm{ker}\,\bar{\Delta}^{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
is the

primitive filtration
of

\mathrm{ker}\,\varepsilon
.

(2)

One calls
D

conilpotent
if
its primitive filtration is exhaustive,
that is, if for
all
c\in\mathrm{ker}\,\varepsilon
there exists

n\geq 0
such that

\bar{\Delta}^{n}(c)=0
.

(3)

One calls
D

graded projective

if the associated graded

A
-module
{\operatorname{gr}}(D)

is projective over
A
.

Remark 4.8
.

When

A
is a field, then
the primitive
filtration of
\ker\varepsilon

is easily seen to be
a coalgebra filtration,
that is,

\bar{\Delta}(\ker\bar{\Delta}^{n})\subseteq\mathrm{span}_{A}\{d_{i}\otimes_{A}d_{j}\mid d_{i}\in\ker\bar{\Delta}^{i},\,d_{j}\in\ker\bar{\Delta}^{j},\,i+j=n\}.

However, this
is not true in general,
as we will illustrate with
the next example.

Example 4.9
.

Consider
A
-modules
M,N

satisfying

M\otimes_{A}M=N\otimes_{A}N=0,\quad M\otimes_{A}N\neq 0,

and set

V:=M\oplus N
. Then we have

V\otimes_{A}V\cong M\otimes_{A}N\oplus N\otimes_{A}M\neq 0,\quad V\otimes_{A}V\otimes_{A}V=0.

Thus any
A
-linear map

\bar{\Delta}\colon V\rightarrow V\otimes_{A}V
is automatically
coassociative, and

\mathrm{ker}\,\bar{\Delta}^{2}=V
.
Here,
the primitive filtration
is

0\subseteq\mathrm{ker}\,\bar{\Delta}\subseteq V=V=V=\ldots,

and it fails to be a coalgebra filtration
whenever there exists an element

v\in V

for which
\bar{\Delta}(v)

is not in the image of

\ker\bar{\Delta}\otimes_{A}\ker\bar{\Delta}

in
V\otimes_{A}V
.

Explicit examples of this type can
be constructed by choosing two
multiplicatively closed subsets

S_{1},S_{2}\subseteq A
and by setting

M\coloneqq(S^{-1}_{1}A)/A,\quad N\coloneqq S^{-1}_{2}A/A.

Concretely, when

A=k[x,y]
and
S_{1}=\{x^{i}\mid i\geq 0\},S_{2}=\{y^{j}\mid j\geq 0\}
, then

M
and
N
have as

A
-modules a presentation in terms
of generators and relations as
follows:
M
has generators

m_{i}\in M
,

i<0
, which are the classes of

x^{i}
in
M=k[x,x^{-1},y]/k[x,y]
, and

N
has generators

n_{j}\in N
,
which are the classes of

y^{j}\in N=k[x,y,y^{-1}]/k[x,y]
.
The former are a basis of
the
k[y]
-module
M
, while the
latter are a basis of the

k[x]
-module
N
. We have

xm_{i}=\begin{cases}m_{i+1}&i<-1\\
0&i=-1\end{cases}\quad\text{and}\quad yn_{j}=\begin{cases}n_{j+1}&j<-1\\
0&j=-1.\end{cases}

So
M\otimes_{A}N

is free
as a
k
-module,
with basis

m_{i}\otimes_{A}n_{j}
,

i,j<0
.
We define
the
A
-linear map
\bar{\Delta}\colon V\rightarrow V\otimes_{A}V

given by

V\supset M\rightarrow M\otimes_{A}N\subset V\otimes_{A}V,\quad m_{i}\mapsto m_{i}\otimes_{A}n_{-1}

and

V\supset N\rightarrow N\otimes_{A}M\subset V\otimes_{A}V,\quad n_{j}\mapsto n_{j}\otimes_{A}m_{-1}.

Then

\mathrm{ker}\,\bar{\Delta}

is the
A
-submodule
of
V

spanned
by the
ym_{i}\in M

and
xn_{j}\in N
,
hence
\bar{\Delta}(m_{-1})=m_{-1}\otimes_{A}n_{-1}
is not in

\mathrm{ker}\,\bar{\Delta}\otimes_{A}\mathrm{ker}\,\bar{\Delta}
.

4.3.
Bialgebroids

Let
\eta\colon A\rightarrow H
be an

A
-ring
and consider
H
as an
A
-module
via
a\otimes h\mapsto a\smalltriangleright h=\eta(a)h
,
a\in A,h\in H
. The complication in the
definition of a bialgebroid
(in comparison to that of a
bialgebra) stems from the fact
that multiplication in
H

does not induce a well-defined
componentwise multiplication
on
H\otimes_{A}H
.
We rather have:

Lemma 4.10
.

The subset

H\times_{A}H\subseteq H\otimes_{A}H
given by

\Bigl\{\sum_{i}g_{i}\otimes_{A}h_{i}\mid\sum_{i}g_{i}\eta(a)\otimes_{A}h_{i}=\sum_{i}g_{i}\otimes_{A}h_{i}\eta(a)\forall a\in A\Bigr\}

is an
A
-ring with
respect to the componentwise
product

(g\otimes_{A}h)(x\otimes_{A}y):=gx\otimes_{A}hy,\quad g,h,x,y\in H

and the unit map

\eta\colon A\rightarrow H\times_{A}H
,

a\mapsto a\otimes_{A}1=1\otimes_{A}a.

Definition 4.11
.

The
A
-ring
H\times_{A}H
is
called the
Takeuchi
product
of
H
with itself over

A
.

Remark 4.12
.

As defined here, the
concept was introduced by
Sweedler
[
65
]
, and
Takeuchi generalised it to the
case of noncommutative
A

[
69
]
.

Now one can meaningfully
define:

Definition 4.13
.

A (left)
bialgebroid

over
A
is an augmented

A
-ring
H
together with a
morphism of
A
-rings

\Delta\colon H\rightarrow H\times_{A}H

for which
(H,\Delta,\varepsilon)
is an
A|A
-coring.

By very definition of
H\times_{A}H
, this means in
particular that

H
is not just a
coalgebra over
A
, but an

A|A
-coring.

Remark 4.14
.

When
A=k
, then
H\times_{A}H=H\otimes_{A}H

and the definition above reduces to that of a bialgebra over
A
.

Remark 4.15
.

There is a more general
definition of a left bialgebroid
over a possibly noncommutative
algebra
A

[
7
,
8
,
69
,
13
]
. This comes equipped
with both an
A
-ring and an

A^{\mathrm{op}}
-ring structure; the
two unit maps are usually
called
source
and

target
.
The bialgebroids in
Definition
4.13

are exactly those whose
base algebras are commutative
and whose source and target
maps are equal.

4.4.
Hopf
algebroids

There are several
non-equivalent definitions of
the notion of a
Hopf algebroid.
We will first recall the one
introduced by Schauenburg

[
62
]
,
and afterwards relate it to two
others due to Böhm

[
7
]

respectively
Böhm and Szlachányi

[
9
, Definition 4.1]
.
They all consider
bialgebroids over possibly
noncommutative
base algebras as in

Remark

4.15
. We recall
the definitions in the simpler
setting in which
A
is commutative
(and the source and target maps
agree). As in
Remark
2.8
, we denote
by
H\in A\mbox{-}\mathbf{Mod}
respectively

\bar{H}\in A\mbox{-}\mathbf{Mod}

the
k
-module
H
with the

A
-actions

\displaystyle A\otimes H\rightarrow H,\quad a\otimes h\mapsto a\smalltriangleright h=\eta(a)h,

\displaystyle A\otimes\bar{H}\rightarrow\bar{H},\quad a\otimes h\mapsto a\blacktriangleright h=h\eta(a).

The first notion, due to Schauenburg

[
62
, Theorem and Definition 3.5]
,
focuses on
H
being
a Galois extension of
A
:

Definition 4.16
.

A bialgebroid
H

is called a
left Hopf algebroid

(or
\times_{A}
-Hopf algebra)
if its
(left) Galois map

(4.3)

\delta\colon\bar{H}\otimes_{A}H\rightarrow H\otimes_{A}H,\quad g\otimes_{A}h\mapsto g_{(1)}\otimes_{A}g_{(2)}h

is bijective.

Remark 4.17
.

There is the analogous notion
of a
right Hopf algebroid
,
which means that
H^{\mathrm{op}}
is
a left Hopf algebroid.

The map
\delta
is
right
H
-linear (where the
right
H
-module structure is
given by multiplication from the
right in the second tensor
component), hence its inverse

\delta^{-1}
is so, too, if it exists.
The inverse
\delta^{-1}

is thus uniquely determined
by its value on the elements
of the form

h\otimes_{A}1\in H\otimes_{A}H
. This information gets stored in
the following map:

Definition 4.18
.

The
translation map

of a left Hopf algebroid
H

is the map

\tau\colon H\to\bar{H}\otimes_{A}H,\quad h\mapsto\delta^{-1}(h\otimes_{A}1).

As is customary in the literature, we
denote
\tau(h)
in a
Sweedler-type notation by

h_{+}\otimes_{A}h_{-}
.

Proposition 4.19
.

We have for any
h\in H
, and
a\in A
:

\displaystyle a\smalltriangleright(h_{+})\otimes_{A}h_{-}

\displaystyle=h_{+}\otimes_{A}a\blacktriangleright(h_{-}),

\displaystyle h_{+}\otimes_{A}h_{-(1)}\otimes_{A}h_{-(2)}

\displaystyle=h_{++}\otimes_{A}h_{-}\otimes_{A}h_{+-},

\displaystyle(hg)_{+}\otimes_{A}(hg)_{-}

\displaystyle=h_{+}g_{+}\otimes_{A}g_{-}h_{-}.

Proof.

See
[
62
, Proposition 3.7]
.
∎

4.5.
Antipodes

Assume we are given
a bialgebroid
H
in the sense
of
Definition
4.13
and
an isomorphism of
A
-rings

S\colon H\rightarrow H^{\mathrm{op}},\quad S\circ\eta=\eta.

This gives rise to
well-defined maps

\displaystyle\gamma

\displaystyle\colon H\otimes_{A}H\rightarrow H\otimes_{A}H,\quad g\otimes_{A}h\mapsto S(g)_{(2)}\otimes_{A}S(g)_{(1)}h,

\displaystyle\bar{\gamma}

\displaystyle\colon H\otimes_{A}H\rightarrow H\otimes_{A}H,\quad g\otimes_{A}h\mapsto S^{-1}(g)_{(1)}\otimes_{A}S^{-1}(g)_{(2)}h.

Definition 4.20
.

A
full Hopf algebroid
is a
bialgebroid
H
together with an isomorphism of

A
-rings

S\colon H\rightarrow H^{\mathrm{op}}

for which

\gamma\circ\bar{\gamma}=\bar{\gamma}\circ\gamma=\mathrm{id}_{H\otimes_{A}H}
.
The map
S
is called an
antipode
for
H
.

Proposition 4.21
.

A full Hopf
algebroid
is a left Hopf algebroid
with

\delta^{-1}\colon g\otimes_{A}h\mapsto S^{-1}(S(g)_{(2)})\otimes_{A}S(g)_{(1)}h.

Proof.

This is shown by
straightforward computation.
∎

For bialgebras over
A=k
,
the bijectivity of
\delta

is equivalent to the existence
of an antipode
given by
S(h)\coloneqq\varepsilon(h_{+})h_{-}.

In general, this expression is
ill-defined, though,
since the counit
\varepsilon

is not right
A
-linear, and there
are left Hopf algebroids that do not
admit an antipode

[
33
]
.

More precisely, the situation
is this:
recall from
Section
2.3
that
right
H
-module structures on

A
that extend the multiplication
in
A
correspond bijectively to

A
-linear maps

\bar{\varepsilon}\colon\bar{H}\to A

satisfying
\bar{\varepsilon}\circ\eta=\mathrm{id}_{A}
, and
for which

\mathrm{ker}\,\bar{\varepsilon}

is a right ideal in
H
, with the
corresponding right action of
h\in H
on
a\in A
given by

\bar{\varepsilon}(a\smalltriangleright h)
.
We have seen in
Section
2.8
that for
the
A
-ring
\mathscr{D}_{A}
, such

\bar{\varepsilon}
correspond
further to
A
-ring
isomorphisms
\mathscr{D}_{A}\cong\mathscr{D}^{\mathrm{op}}_{A}
.
At least when
H
is
cocommutative, one similarly
obtains that in the setting of
bialgebroids, such
\bar{\varepsilon}
correspond to antipodes:

Proposition 4.22
.

Let
H
be a left Hopf algebroid.

(1)

An augmentation
\bar{\varepsilon}\colon\bar{H}\rightarrow A
of

H^{\mathrm{op}}
defines an
A
-ring
morphism

S\colon H\rightarrow H^{\mathrm{op}},\quad h\mapsto\bar{\varepsilon}(h_{+})\smalltriangleright h_{-}.

(2)

If
H
is cocommutative,

S
is an antipode, and

S^{2}=\mathrm{id}_{H}
.

(3)

For cocommutative Hopf
algebroids, this establishes a
bijective correspondence
between antipodes and
augmentations of
H^{\mathrm{op}}
.

Proof.

(1): Clearly,
S
is
well-defined, and
Proposition
4.19

shows that
S
is an algebra
morphism
H\rightarrow H^{\mathrm{op}}
:

\displaystyle S(hg)

\displaystyle=\bar{\varepsilon}(h_{+}g_{+})\smalltriangleright(g_{-}h_{-})

\displaystyle=\bar{\varepsilon}(\bar{\varepsilon}(h_{+})\smalltriangleright g_{+})\smalltriangleright(g_{-}h_{-})

\displaystyle=\bar{\varepsilon}(g_{+})\smalltriangleright(\bar{\varepsilon}(h_{+})\blacktriangleright g_{-})h_{-}

\displaystyle=(\bar{\varepsilon}(g_{+})\smalltriangleright g_{-})\eta(\bar{\varepsilon}(h_{+}))h_{-}

\displaystyle=S(g)S(h).

In particular, one obtains
that for all
a\in A
,

S(a\smalltriangleright g)=a\blacktriangleright S(g),\quad S(a\blacktriangleright h)=a\smalltriangleright S(h),\quad S(\eta(a))=\eta(a).

(2): In any left Hopf
algebroid, one has

\delta(h_{+}h_{-+}\otimes_{A}h_{--})=h_{+(1)}h_{-}\otimes_{A}h_{+(2)}.

If
H
is cocommutative, this
reduces to
1\otimes_{A}h
,
see
e.g.
[
29
, p69]
.

Using that
\delta
and
hence
\delta^{-1}
are
right
H
-linear so that

\delta^{-1}(1\otimes_{A}h)=1\otimes_{A}h
shows

(4.4)

h_{+}h_{-+}\otimes_{A}h_{--}=1\otimes_{A}h.

From this, it follows by
straightforward computation
that

S^{2}=\mathrm{id}_{H}
.

So for cocommutative left Hopf
algebroids, we have
\gamma=\bar{\gamma}
. Furthermore,
Proposition
4.19

shows

\Delta(S(h))=h_{-}\otimes_{A}S(h_{+}).

Using this and once more
(
4.4
) one computes
directly that

\gamma
is an involution so
that
S
is an antipode.

(3): One recovers
\bar{\varepsilon}
from
S
as

\varepsilon(S(h))=\bar{\varepsilon}(h_{+})\varepsilon(h_{-})=\bar{\varepsilon}(\varepsilon(h_{-})\blacktriangleright h_{+})=\bar{\varepsilon}(h).\qed

Note that
Böhm has also given a more
general definition of full Hopf
algebroids
in which
S
is not
necessarily bijective; the
two definitions are related as
follows:

Proposition 4.23
.

A Hopf algebroid in the sense of
Definition
4.20
defines a
Hopf algebroid in the sense of

[
7
, Definition 4.1]

as follows:

(1)

The left base algebra is

L:=A
and the left
bialgebroid
H_{L}
is
the one underlying
H
:

s_{L}=t_{L}:=\eta
,

\varepsilon_{L}:=\varepsilon
,

\Delta_{L}:=\Delta
.

(2)

The right base algebra
is

R:=A
and the right bialgebroid is
given by

s_{R}=t_{R}:=\eta
,

\varepsilon_{R}:=\varepsilon\circ S
, and

\Delta_{R}(h):=S(S(h)_{(2)})\otimes_{A}S(S(h)_{(1)}).

Proof.

Straightforward computation,
cf.
[
29
, Section 2.6.8]
.
∎

Remark 4.24
.

A bialgebroid can in general
admit several antipodes, see
Example
4.40
below and

[
6
]

for a more detailed discussion.
Therefore, when
A=k
, the
definition of a full Hopf
algebroid does
not
reduce to
the definition of a Hopf algebra
(here the antipode
is unique, as it is the convolution
inverse of the identity map

\mathrm{id}_{H}
).
See e.g.
[
7
, Example
4.1.1]

for an example.

4.6.
Closed monoidal
categories

The following theorem
expresses the representation-theoretic
meaning of bialgebroid and
Hopf algebroid structures on
an augmented
A
-ring
H
:

Theorem 4.25
.

Let
\eta\colon A\rightarrow H
,
\varepsilon\colon H\rightarrow A
be an
augmented
A
-ring.

(1)

The
A
-bialgebroid
structures on
H
correspond
bijectively to monoidal
structures on the category
H\mbox{-}\mathbf{Mod}
for
which the forgetful functor

H\mbox{-}\mathbf{Mod}\rightarrow A\mbox{-}\mathbf{Mod}
is
strict monoidal.

(2)

H
is a left Hopf
algebroid
if and only if the left closed
structure on
(A\mbox{-}\mathbf{Mod},\otimes_{A},A)
lifts to
H\mbox{-}\mathbf{Mod}
.

Proof.

See
[
61
, Theorem
5.1]

for (1) and

[
62
, Theorem
3.1]

for (2).
∎

Explicitly, the unit
object of
H\mbox{-}\mathbf{Mod}
is
A
with

H
-module structure given by

\varepsilon
, and
the
H
-module structure on
M\otimes_{A}N
is given for

H
-modules
M,N
by

h(m\otimes_{A}n)=h_{(1)}m\otimes_{A}h_{(2)}n,\quad h\in H,m\in M,n\in N.

Remark 4.26
.

For any bialgebroid, the monoidal category

H\mbox{-}\mathbf{Mod}
is
left closed with internal
hom functor given by

\mathrm{Hom}_{H}(H\otimes_{A}M,N)
, where

H\otimes_{A}M
is the
monoidal product of the left

H
-modules
H
and
M
and
the left
H
-module structure
on
\mathrm{Hom}_{H}(H\otimes_{A}M,N)
is given by

(hf)(g\otimes_{A}m)=f(gh\otimes_{A}m),\quad g,h\in H,m\in M,f\in\mathrm{Hom}_{H}(H\otimes_{A}M,N).

The point in
Theorem
4.25
(2)
is that when the map
\delta

from (
4.3
)
is bijective, it induces a
natural isomorphism of

H
-modules

H\otimes_{A}M\cong\bar{H}\otimes_{A}M
, where the

H
-module structure on the
latter is given by

h(g\otimes_{A}m)=hg\otimes_{A}m,

and therefore one has

\mathrm{Hom}_{H}(H\otimes_{A}M,N)\cong\mathrm{Hom}_{H}(\bar{H}\otimes_{A}M,N)\cong\mathrm{Hom}_{A}(M,N).

4.7.
Opmonoidal functors

An
opmonoidal structure
(also called

comonoidal
) on a
functor

F\colon\mathbf{C}\rightarrow\mathbf{D}

between monoidal categories
is a monoidal structure
on its opposite

F^{\mathrm{op}}\colon\mathbf{C}^{\mathrm{op}}\to\mathbf{D}^{\mathrm{op}}

(see
e.g.
[
8
, Definition 3.5]
).
Explicitly, this is
given by natural morphisms

\mu_{M,N}\colon F(M\otimes_{\mathbf{C}}N)\rightarrow F(M)\otimes_{\mathbf{D}}F(N)

and a morphism

\eta\colon F(1_{\mathbf{C}})\rightarrow 1_{\mathbf{D}}

(where
M,N
are objects in

\mathbf{C}
and

1_{\mathbf{C}},1_{\mathbf{D}}

are the unit objects in the
two categories) which satisfy
the coassociativity and counitality
conditions.
In particular, this
means that
F
induces a
functor from comonoids in

\mathbf{C}

to comonoids in
\mathbf{D}
.

Remark 4.27
.

If a functor
F\colon\mathbf{C}\to\mathbf{D}

admits a right adjoint

G\colon\mathbf{D}\to\mathbf{C}
,
then there exists a bijective
correspondence between
opmonoidal structures on
F

and monoidal structures on
G
.
The latter is the approach used
in the sources

[
70
,
68
]

cited below.

When the categories are the
categories of modules over
commutative algebras, this leads directly to
corings:

Theorem 4.28
.

Let
D
be an

A
-
B
-bimodule. Then there
exists a bijective
correspondence
between

B|A
-coring structures on

D
and opmonoidal structures
on
D\otimes_{B}-\colon B\mbox{-}\mathbf{Mod}\rightarrow A\mbox{-}\mathbf{Mod}
via

\mu_{M,N}(d\otimes_{B}(m\otimes_{B}n)):=(d_{(1)}\otimes_{B}m)\otimes_{A}(d_{(2)}\otimes_{B}n),\quad\eta(d\otimes_{B}b):=\varepsilon(db),

where
d\in D,m\in M,n\in N,b\in B=1_{B\mbox{-}\mathbf{Mod}}
.

Proof.

See
[
70
, Theorem 3.6]
.
∎

This gets upgraded to a
statement about the monoidal
categories of modules over
bialgebroids over different
base algebras as follows:

Proposition 4.29
.

Let
H
be a
bialgebroid over
A
,
G

be a bialgebroid over
B
, and

D
be an
H
-
G
-bimodule.
Then the opmonoidal structures on

{D\otimes_{G}-}\colon G\mbox{-}\mathbf{Mod}\to H\mbox{-}\mathbf{Mod}

correspond bijectively to

B|A
-coring
structures on
D

such that for any

d\in D,g\in G

we have

(1)

\Delta_{D}(dg)=d_{(1)}g_{(1)}\otimes_{A}d_{(2)}g_{(2)}
, and

(2)

\varepsilon_{D}(dg)=\varepsilon_{D}(d\,\varepsilon_{G}(g))
.

Proof.

See
[
68
, Lemmata 2.2 and 3.3]
.
∎

4.8.
Rings of differential operators

Recall that our goal is to find
a Hopf algebroid structure on the ring of differential
operators
\mathscr{D}_{A}
.
The augmented
A
-ring structure on
\mathscr{D}_{A}
is fixed,
so the main task lies in finding a suitable
comultiplication map

\Delta\colon\mathscr{D}_{A}\to\mathscr{D}_{A}\otimes_{A}\mathscr{D}_{A}
.
In particular, the axioms of a bialgebroid imply that

(4.5)

D(ab)=D_{(1)}(a)D_{(2)}(b)

for all
a,b\in A
, and
D\in\mathscr{D}_{A}
.

Here are two known instances
where the ring of differential
operators is a bialgebroid.
The first comes from Sweedler’s seminal paper

[
65
]

and the second one from
Ben-Zvi and Nevins’ paper

[
5
]
.

Sweedler introduced the
following notion:

Definition 4.30
.

An algebra
A
has

almost finite projective differentials

if there exists a directed
system
(I_{\alpha})_{\alpha}

of ideals in
A\otimes A

which is cofinal with

(I_{A}^{n})_{n}
(for each

\alpha
there exists
n

such that
I_{A}^{n}\subseteq I_{\alpha}
and for each
n
there
exists
\alpha
such that

I_{\alpha}\subseteq I_{A}^{n}
),
and for which

(A\otimes A)/I_{\alpha}

is finitely generated projective as an
A
-module
for all
\alpha
.

Example 4.31
.

If
A
is smooth, then
A

has almost finite projective differentials,
as follows from
Corollary

3.14
.

Example 4.32
.

If
A
is purely inseparable as in
Example

3.18

and
A
is finitely generated projective over
k
,
then
A
has almost finite projective differentials.
Indeed, if
I_{A}^{n+1}=0
, then
the singleton
{0}
satisfies the desired
properties, since
(A\otimes A)/I_{A}^{n+1}=A\otimes A

is finitely generated projective
over
A

by base change.

We refer to
[
65
]

for further constructions of
algebras with almost finite projective
differentials. For such
algebras, we have:

Theorem 4.33
.

If
A
has almost finite projective differentials, then
there exists a unique left Hopf algebroid structure on
\mathscr{D}_{A}
.
This is cocommutative and
the comultiplication induces
an isomorphism
\mathscr{D}_{A}\cong\mathscr{D}_{A}\times_{A}\mathscr{D}_{A}
.

Proof.

The existence of the bialgebroid structure is shown in

[
65
, Theorem 8.7]
.
The existence of the translation map is shown in

[
65
, Theorem 12.1, Remark 12.2]

through the existence of what Sweedler calls an
Ess map
.
∎

For smooth algebras, this
implies:

Corollary 4.34
.

If
A
is smooth over
k
,
then
\mathscr{D}_{A}
is a cocommutative Hopf algebroid.

Remark 4.35
.

If
A
is smooth, then the
primitive filtration
of
\mathscr{D}_{A}
agrees with its canonical filtration.
Therefore,
\mathscr{D}_{A}
is conilpotent and graded projective.

In the next sections, we shall consider
the notion of
local projectivity

for rings of differential
operators. As will be
explained in
Example
6.16
,
this holds for algebras with almost finite
projective differentials.

The examples of monomial
curves that we consider in the
last section of this paper
could also be approached using
the results of Ben-Zvi and
Nevins, as they are
good Cohen–Macaulay varieties

[
5
, Definition 2.9]
.
We briefly outline how the existence
of a bialgebroid structure on the rings of differential
operators on such varieties can be
deduced from Theorem 3.11
therein.

To do so,
the notion of a bialgebroid
respectively Hopf algebroid
has to be formulated in a more general setting,
where one replaces the closed monoidal category
A\mbox{-}\mathbf{Mod}

with the categories of
quasi-coherent respectively

pro-coherent

sheaves
over a variety
X

(see

[
5
, Remark 2.7]

for the latter notion).
Recall
that when
X
is affine with
coordinate ring
A
, then a
quasi-coherent sheaf is
equivalent to an
A
-module; a
pro-coherent sheaf corresponds to an
inverse system of
A
-modules.
The following
theorem implies that for an
affine good Cohen–Macaulay
variety with coordinate ring

A
,
\mathscr{D}_{A}
as defined in the
present paper carries a
bialgebroid structure:

Conjecture 4.36
.

If
X
is a good Cohen–Macaulay variety,
then the sheaf of differential operators
\mathscr{D}_{X}

is a bialgebroid
in the category of
quasi-coherent sheaves over
X
.

Sketch of proof.

In their paper, the authors introduce a
pro-coherent Hopf algebroid
\mathscr{J}_{X}
,
called the
jet
algebroid
. This is commutative as an
algebra but its source and
target maps are distinct; in
the affine case, it
corresponds to the inverse
system
(\mathscr{P}^{n}_{A})_{n}
from
(
2.1
).
The sheaf of differential operators
\mathscr{D}_{X}

is its dual in the category of
pro-coherent sheaves, i.e. a
differential operator is a
continuous
\mathscr{O}_{X}
-linear functional
on
\mathscr{J}_{X}
; this sheaf
turns out to be quasi-coherent.

Theorem 3.11(2) in
[
5
]

states that the category of left
\mathscr{D}_{X}
-modules
is equivalent to the category of

\mathscr{J}_{X}
-
comodules

which are quasi-coherent.
The category of all

\mathscr{J}_{X}
-comodules
is monoidal since

\mathscr{J}_{X}
is a Hopf
algebroid
(the proof of this fact carries over
verbatim from the purely
algebraic setting, see e.g.
[
7
, Theorem 3.18]
).
The tensor product of
comodules that are
quasi-coherent can be shown
to be the usual tensor
product of quasi-coherent
sheaves. That is, the
subcategory of quasi-coherent
comodules is monoidal, and the
forgetful functor to

\mathscr{O}_{X}
-modules is
strict monoidal.
Hence we have a monoidal structure as in

Section

4.6

on the category of left
\mathscr{D}_{X}
-modules,
thus the bialgebroid structure on
\mathscr{D}_{X}

follows.
∎

Our approach to the monomial curves
relies on elementary methods, which
allow us to compute explicitly the
Hopf algebroid structure for such curves.

4.9.
The example

\mathscr{U}_{A}(L)

Universal enveloping algebras
of Lie algebras are
cocommutative and conilpotent
Hopf algebras.
Similarly, we have:

Proposition 4.37
.

The universal
enveloping algebra
\mathscr{U}_{A}(L)
of any
Lie–Rinehart algebra is a
cocommutative and conilpotent left Hopf algebroid
over
A
, whose coproduct
and counit are
determined by

\Delta(\rho(X))=1\otimes_{A}\rho(X)+\rho(X)\otimes_{A}1,\quad\varepsilon(\rho(X))=0,\quad\forall X\in L.

Its translation map
is given by

\tau(\rho(X))=\rho(X)_{+}\otimes_{A}\rho(X)_{-}=\rho(X)\otimes_{A}1-1\otimes_{A}\rho(X).

Proof.

This is well-known;
the bialgebroid structure of

\mathscr{U}_{A}(L)
has
appeared first in

[
76
]
, and
the fact that it is a left Hopf
algebroid is pointed out e.g. in

[
30
, Example 2]
.
Recall the filtration

\{\mathscr{U}_{A}(L)^{n}\}
given in
(
2.2
); it
is immediate that

\mathscr{U}_{A}(L)^{n}\cap\mathrm{ker}\,\varepsilon\subseteq\mathrm{ker}\,\bar{\Delta}^{n}
,
so
\mathscr{U}_{A}(L)
is
conilpotent (the coaugmentation
is given by
\eta

Our goal is to prove

5.1.
Local

We fix a left

-module

We will distinguish

Definition 5.1
.

We say that

\operatorname{ev}_{X,r}\colon X\otimes_{A}M\rightarrow X,\quad x\otimes_{A}m\mapsto xr(m),\quad r\in R

\bigcap_{r\in R}\mathrm{ker}\,\operatorname{ev}_{X,r}=0
.

In

[
74
]
,
such

were called

-locally
projective
. For

the literature, including

[

,

]
,

[
18
]
,

[

]
,
and
flat and strict

[

.

We will use two results that

to flatness and projectivity,
and which are
contained in most of the above

Lemma 5.2

Let

Then

for any injective map

of right

\mathrm{im}(\iota\otimes_{A}\mathrm{id}_{M})=\bigl\{y\in Y\otimes_{A}M\mid\operatorname{ev}_{Y,r}(y)\in\mathrm{im}\,\iota\,\forall r\in R\bigr\}.

Proof.

By the definition of the
evaluation maps, each

gives rise to a commutative
diagram of abelian groups

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X\otimes_{A}M}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Y\otimes_{A}M}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(Y/X)\otimes_{A}M}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Y}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(Y/X)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0,}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\iota\otimes_{A}\mathrm{id}_{M}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{ev}_{X,r}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\xi\otimes_{A}\mathrm{id}_{M}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{ev}_{Y,r}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{ev}_{Y/X,r}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\iota}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\xi}

\mathrm{im}(\iota\otimes_{A}\mathrm{id}_{M})=\mathrm{ker}(\xi\otimes_{A}\mathrm{id}_{M})

if and only if

For the second result, we
define the following generalisation of
finite-rank operators:

Definition 5.3
.

For any
A
-module
N
, we
denote by

\operatorname{hom}^{R}_{A}(M,N)\subseteq\mathrm{Hom}_{A}(M,N)

the set of all
A
-module
morphisms
M\rightarrow N

of the form

\varphi\colon M\rightarrow N,\quad m\mapsto\sum_{i=1}^{l}r_{i}(m)n_{i}

for some
r_{1},\ldots,r_{l}\in R,n_{1},\ldots,n_{l}\in N
,
l\geq 0
.

Remark 5.4
.

Clearly,
\operatorname{hom}^{R}_{A}(M,N)
is closed under
addition, and for all
\varphi\in\operatorname{hom}^{R}_{A}(M,N)

and for all
A
-module
morphisms

\psi\colon N\rightarrow P
,
we have
\psi\circ\varphi\in\operatorname{hom}^{R}_{A}(M,P)
with

(\psi\circ\varphi)(m)=\sum_{i}r_{i}(m)p_{i},\quad p_{i}:=\psi(n_{i}).

In particular,

\operatorname{hom}^{R}_{A}(M,M)\subseteq\mathrm{Hom}_{A}(M,M)
is a
left ideal. If
R
is closed
under precomposition
with arbitrary endomorphisms
of
M
, that is, is
a
\mathrm{Hom}_{A}(M,M)
-
A
-subbimodule of

M^{*}
, then
\operatorname{hom}^{R}_{A}(M,M)

is a two-sided ideal.

Remark 5.5
.

By definition,

\operatorname{hom}^{R}_{A}(M,N)
is the image of
the canonical map

R\otimes_{A}N\rightarrow M^{*}\otimes_{A}N\rightarrow\mathrm{Hom}_{A}(M,N)
.
If
M
is
R
-locally
projective, this
canonical map is for
N=M

injective, so that

\operatorname{hom}^{R}_{A}(M,M)\cong R\otimes_{A}M
.

Proposition 5.6
.

The following statements are
equivalent:

(1)

M
is
R
-locally
projective.

(2)

For each
m\in M
there
exists
\pi\in\operatorname{hom}^{R}_{A}(M,M)
with
\pi(m)=m
.

(3)

For
each finitely generated
submodule
N\subseteq M

there exists
\pi\in\operatorname{hom}^{R}_{A}(M,M)
with
\pi|_{N}=\mathrm{id}_{N}
.

Proof.

“
(3)\Rightarrow(1)
”: Let
X
be
a right
A
-module and
assume that

x=\sum_{i}x_{i}\otimes_{A}m_{i}\in X\otimes_{A}M
is an
element with
\operatorname{ev}_{X,r}(x)=\sum_{i}x_{i}r(m_{i})=0
for all

r\in R
.
Let
N\subseteq M

be the
A
-module generated by
the
m_{i}
, and
\pi\in\operatorname{hom}^{R}_{A}(M,M)
be as
in (3), with
\pi(m)=\sum_{j}r_{j}(m)n_{j}
.
Then
\pi(m_{i})=m_{i}
implies

x=\sum_{i}x_{i}\otimes_{A}m_{i}=\sum_{ij}x_{i}\otimes_{A}r_{j}(m_{i})n_{j}=\sum_{ij}x_{i}r_{j}(m_{i})\otimes_{A}n_{j}=0.

“
(1)\Rightarrow(2)
”: Assume
that
M
is
R
-locally
projective. Let
J\subseteq A
be the right
ideal generated by all
elements of the form
r(m)

with
r\in R
, and set

X:=A/J
so that

X\otimes_{A}M\cong M/JM
as
abelian group.
For all
r\in R
,
we now have

\operatorname{ev}_{A/J,r}([1]\otimes_{A}m)=[r(m)]=0,

where

is the

a\in A
.
So by assumption,
[1]\otimes_{A}m=0
. This means that

, so there exist

with

.
By the definition of

, we have

a_{i}=\sum_{j}r_{ij}(m)b_{ij}

for some

,
and we

m=\sum_{ij}r_{ij}(m)m_{ij},\quad m_{ij}:=b_{ij}m_{i}.

So
\pi(x):=\sum_{ij}r_{ij}(x)m_{ij}

defines an element of

\operatorname{hom}^{R}_{A}(M,M)
with
\pi(m)=m
.

“
(2)\Rightarrow(3)
”:
We prove by
induction on
l\geq 0
that
for any given elements

n_{1},\ldots,n_{l}\in M
, there
exists
\pi\in\operatorname{hom}^{R}_{A}(M,M)

with
\pi(n_{j})=n_{j}
for

. The claim then
follows by choosing
n_{1},\ldots,n_{l}

as a finite
set of generators of
N
.
The base case

follows
from (2), so assume that the claim is
proven for any set of
l-1

n_{1},\ldots,n_{l}\in M
are

Choose auxiliary elements

\alpha,\beta\in\operatorname{hom}^{R}_{A}(M,M)
with

\alpha(n_{j})=n_{j}
for

and

\beta(a_{l})=a_{l}
(which exist by the

so that we have

\beta(n_{l})-\beta\circ\alpha(n_{l})=\beta(a_{l})=a_{l}=n_{l}-\alpha(n_{l}).

Now define

\pi:=\alpha+\beta-\beta\circ\alpha.

Then we have

\pi(n_{j})=\alpha(n_{j})+\beta(n_{j})-\beta\circ\alpha(n_{j})=n_{j}+\beta(n_{j})-\beta(n_{j})=n_{j}

for
j<l
and

\pi(n_{l})=\alpha(n_{l})+\beta(n_{l})-\beta\circ\alpha(n_{l})=\alpha(n_{l})+n_{l}-\alpha(n_{l})=n_{l}.\qed

Remark 5.7
.

We stress that
this does not mean that
finitely generated submodules

of

are
projective, since

is usually neither

.
However, the next corollary

.

The

-module

is

-locally projective

and all

-module morphisms

\varphi\colon M\rightarrow W,\psi\colon V\rightarrow W

with
\psi

surjective, there exists

\rho\in\operatorname{hom}^{R}_{A}(M,V)
with

(\psi\circ\rho)|_{N}=\varphi|_{N}
.

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces N}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces M}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces V}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces W}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\varphi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\exists\rho}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\psi}

Proof.

“
\Rightarrow
”: Given

,

\varphi
, and

\pi(m)=\sum_{i}r_{i}(m)m_{i}
. For each

.
Consider the instance of the

5.2.
Strong local
projectivity

While
R
-local projectivity
is sufficient to
apply
Theorem
1.3
, the
modules that appear in our
applications have in
fact a stronger property:

Definition 5.9
.

We say that
M
is

strongly
R
-locally
projective

if for every finitely generated

A
-submodule
N\subseteq M

there exists an idempotent

\pi\in\operatorname{hom}^{R}_{A}(M,M)
(so

\pi=\pi\circ\pi
) and

\pi|_{N}=\mathrm{id}_{N}
.

Remark 5.10
.

(1)

By the dual basis lemma,
a submodule
Q\subseteq M

is a direct summand of
M

which is finitely generated projective
if and only if there exists
an idempotent element
\pi

\pi\in\operatorname{hom}^{M^{*}}_{A}(M,M)

such that
\operatorname{im}\pi=Q
.

(2)

In
[
39
]
,
a module is called

locally projective

if each finitely generated
submodule
N\subseteq M
is
contained in a finitely generated
projective submodule which is a direct
summand of
M
.
The definition above,
first introduced in

[
74
]
,
generalises this notion,
by restricting
the projective submodules
to those which are images
of idempotent elements

\pi\in\operatorname{hom}^{R}_{A}(M,M)
.

For the examples of rings of
differential operators,
we will verify the
strong local projectivity by
explicitly constructing a
sufficiently large system of
idempotents in
\operatorname{hom}^{R}_{A}(M,M)
:

Lemma 5.11
.

M
is strongly
R
-locally
projective if and only if
there exists a subset

S\subseteq\operatorname{hom}_{A}^{R}(M,M)

consisting of idempotent elements
with the property that

(1)

0\in S
,

(2)

for all
\alpha,\beta\in S
,

\alpha+\beta-\beta\circ\alpha\in S

if
\alpha\circ\beta=0
, and

(3)

for any
m\in M
and

\alpha\in S
,
there exists
\beta\in S

such that

\beta(m-\alpha(m))=m-\alpha(m)\text{ and }\alpha\circ\beta=0.

Proof.

“
\Rightarrow
”
We choose
S
to be the set of all
idempotent elements in
\operatorname{hom}_{A}^{R}(M,M)
.
Then
0\in S
, so (1) is satisfied.
If
\alpha,\beta\in\operatorname{hom}_{A}^{R}(M,M)

are idempotent
with
\alpha\circ\beta=0
,
then

\pi\coloneqq\alpha+\beta-\beta\circ\alpha

is again idempotent, so (2) is satisfied.
Let
m\in M
,

\alpha\in S
, and
consider
the submodule
N\subseteq M

generated by
m
and
\operatorname{im}\alpha
.
It is finitely generated,
so there exists
an element
\pi\in S
such that

\pi|_{N}=\operatorname{id}_{N}

by the strong

R
-locally projectivity of
M
.
This means in particular
that
\pi\circ\alpha=\alpha
and
\pi(m)=m
.
Simple computations then show
that
\beta\coloneqq\pi-\alpha\circ\pi

is idempotent, thus is in
S
,
that
\alpha\circ\beta=0
,
and that

\beta(m-\alpha(m))=m-\alpha(m)
,
thus (3) is satisfied.

“
\Leftarrow
”
We perform the same induction proof
as in
Proposition

(3), with the same
notation.
The only difference here is that
we use the properties of
S

to ensure that the
element
\pi\in\operatorname{hom}_{A}^{R}(M,M)

we construct
in the induction step
is in
S
, and is thus idempotent.
Let
n_{1},\ldots,n_{l}\in M
.
The base case
l=1
follows from
applying the property (3) of
S

to
n_{1}
and
\alpha=0\in S
.
Next, suppose
l>1

and let
\alpha\in S
be
such that
\alpha(n_{j})=n_{j}

for all
j<l
.
Property (3), applied
to
n_{l}
and
\alpha
,
yields an element

\beta\in S
such that

\beta(n_{l}-\alpha(n_{l}))=n_{l}-\alpha(n_{l})\text{ and }\alpha\circ\beta=0.

It follows then that

\pi\coloneqq\alpha+\beta-\beta\circ\alpha\in S
,
and that

\pi(n_{j})=n_{j}

for all
j=1,\ldots,l
.
∎

A standard situation in which
strong local projectivity holds is
the following:
assume that
P
is a right

A
-module,
\{I_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\in\Lambda}
is a directed set
of

P_{\lambda}:=P/I_{\lambda}
, and

M\coloneqq\operatorname{colim}_{\lambda\in\Lambda}P_{\lambda}^{*}
.
The inclusions

dual to the quotient

yield
a canonical morphism of left

A

, and hence a
morphism
\nu\colon P\rightarrow P^{**}\rightarrow M^{*}
. Let

R:=\mathrm{im}\,\nu\subseteq M^{*}
be the image
of this map. Then we have:

Lemma 5.12
.

If
P_{\lambda}
is finitely generated projective
for all
\lambda\in\Lambda
, then
the left
A
-module
M

is strongly
R
-locally

Conversely, any strongly

Proof.

Let
N\subseteq M

be a finitely generated

.
If we identify
P_{\lambda}^{*}

with its image in
M
, then

M=\sum_{\lambda}P_{\lambda}^{*}
because

is a
colimit. So there exist

\lambda_{i}^{1},\ldots,\lambda_{i}^{d_{i}}\in\Lambda
with

. As
\Lambda
is directed, there
exists an upper bound

\lambda\in\Lambda

so that for all

, we have

I_{\lambda_{i}^{j}}\subseteq I_{\lambda}
, hence
there exists an

-module

P_{\lambda}\rightarrow P_{\lambda_{i}^{j}}
so that

P_{\lambda_{i}^{j}}^{*}\subseteq P_{\lambda}^{*}
. It
follows that

.
∎

Corollary 6.7
.

Under the assumptions of

,
we have:

(1)

\mathscr{D}^{n}_{K}\cong\mathrm{Hom}_{K}(\mathscr{P}_{K}^{n},K)\cong\operatorname{Hom}_{A}(P_{n},K)

(2)

(3)

Proof.

(1)

Recall from

\displaystyle\mathscr{D}_{K}^{n}

\displaystyle\cong\

\displaystyle\operatorname{Hom}_{K}(\mathscr{P}^{n}_{K},K)

\displaystyle\cong\

\displaystyle\operatorname{Hom}_{K}(K\otimes_{A}P_{n},K)

\displaystyle\cong\

\displaystyle\operatorname{Hom}_{A}(P_{n},K).

(2)

Under the isomorphism

\mathscr{D}_{K}^{n}\cong\mathrm{Hom}_{K}(\mathscr{P}_{K}^{n},K)

we have

\displaystyle\mathscr{D}^{n}_{K}(B,A)

where

(3)

Since

\displaystyle\mathscr{D}_{K}=\operatorname{colim}\mathscr{D}_{K}^{n}

The inclusions

7.
Application to numerical

Finally, we
discuss a class of

for which all
conditions of
Theorem

and
Corollaries
6.8
and

6.10
are met,
namely the semigroup
algebras of
numerical semigroups
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}

(cofinite
submonoids of
(\mathbb{N},+)
)

[
1
,
3
,
57
]
. In
algebro-geometric terms, these
form a well-behaved class of
affine curves with a unique
cuspidal singularity, the
so-called monomial curves

[
52
,
17
,
4
]
.

Throughout this
Section
7
,
k

denotes a field of
characteristic 0.

7.1.
Numerical semigroups

The algebras
A,B

to which we want to
apply the general theory
developed above are associated
to the following type of monoids:

Definition 7.1
.

A
numerical semigroup

is an additive monoid

\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathbb{N}
such that

\mathbb{N}\setminus\mathcal{A}

is finite. Its
Frobenius
number
is

F(\mathcal{A}):=\max(\mathbb{N}\setminus\mathcal{A})
, and

\mathcal{A}
is
symmetric

if for any
z\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\mathcal{A}
, we have

F(\mathcal{A})-z\in\mathcal{A}
.

So a submonoid

\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathbb{N}
is
numerical if and only if it
can be generated by finitely
many elements
a_{1},\ldots,a_{l}\in\mathbb{N}
whose
greatest common divisor is 1,
or, equivalently,
if the subgroup of
\mathbb{Z}

generated by
\mathcal{A}
is all of

\mathbb{Z}
.

Remark 7.2
.

To determine whether the numerical
semigroup
generated by
a_{1},\ldots,a_{l}
is
symmetric is intricate. However,
Sylvester has shown that all
numerical semigroups generated by
two coprime numbers
a_{1},a_{2}
are
symmetric
[
67
]
.

For the remainder of this article,
we fix numerical semigroups

\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B},\mathcal{C}
,
and we denote by

A\coloneqq k[\mathcal{A}],\quad B\coloneqq k[\mathcal{B}]

the corresponding semigroup
algebras. That is,
A
is the free

k
-module with basis
\mathcal{A}

whose unit map and
multiplication are obtained by
linearisation of the monoid
structure

\{0\}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{A},\quad+\colon\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{A}\rightarrow\mathcal{A}.

Equivalently,
an element
f\in A
can be
interpreted as a finitely supported
function
\mathcal{A}\rightarrow k
. Then
the product
fg
of
f,g\in A

is given by convolution

(f*g)(a)\coloneqq\sum_{b,c\in\mathcal{A}\atop a=b+c}f(b)g(c)

and the unit element
1\in A
is
the delta-function
\delta_{0}

supported in the unit element

0\in\mathcal{A}
.
As all numerical semigroups are
submonoids of
\mathbb{Z}
, all
their convolution algebras embed
naturally into the group algebra
of
\mathbb{Z}
, that is, the
algebra of Laurent polynomials

k[\mathbb{Z}]\cong K\coloneqq k[t,t^{-1}].

We therefore view
A,B
from now on
as subalgebras of
K
, identifying

a\in\mathcal{A}
(respectively the
delta-function
\delta_{a}
) with

t^{a}\in K
.

Remark 7.3
.

The Frobenius
number
F(\mathcal{A})
is sometimes also called the

conductor
of
\mathcal{A}
.
Recall that more generally, the
conductor of an integral
domain
A
is the annihilator of the
A
-module

B/A
, where
B
is the normalisation
of
A
(the integral closure
of
A
in its field
of fractions). For any numerical
semigroup algebra
A
, its
normalisation is
B=k[t]
, and
it follows straight from the
definition that the conductor
in this sense is the ideal in

A
generated by

t^{F(\mathcal{A})+1}
.

Viewed
algebro-geometrically,
A
thus
becomes the coordinate ring of a

monomial curve
. The
properties of such curves have been
studied intensively. In particular,
one has:

Theorem 7.4
.

A
is Gorenstein if and only if

\mathcal{A}
is symmetric.
In this case,
A\cong\omega_{A}
.

Proof.

See

[
34
, Theorem]

for the main statement.
The canonical module was
described explicitly in

[
73
, proof of Theorem 4.1]
.
We have

\omega_{A}=[K/A,k]\cong k[t^{-n}\colon n\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\mathcal{A}],

where
[K/A,k]

is
the internal hom as in
Remark
2.4
.
If
\mathcal{A}
is symmetric, then the involution

\nu\colon\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}
,

a\mapsto F(\mathcal{A})-a,

maps
\mathcal{A}
bijectively to
\mathbb{Z}\setminus\mathcal{A}
,
and vice versa, so that we have
an isomorphism
of graded
A
-modules
\bar{\nu}\colon A\to\omega_{A}
,

t^{a}\mapsto t^{-\nu(a)}.

∎

In the theory of differential
operators over semigroup
algebras, the following sets play a
central role (see
e.g.
[
59
,
60
,
58
]

for some background).

Definition 7.5
.

For
d\in\mathbb{Z}
, let

\mathcal{Z}_{d}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{A})\coloneqq\mathcal{B}\setminus(-d+\mathcal{A})=\{i\in\mathcal{B}\mid i+d\not\in\mathcal{A}\}.

We shall write
\mathcal{Z}_{d}(\mathcal{A})

for
\mathcal{Z}_{d}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{A})
.

The following lemma collects some of
the properties of these sets that we
will use
below:

Lemma 7.6
.

For all
d\in\mathbb{Z}
, we have:

(1)

The set
\mathcal{Z}_{d}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{A})

is finite.

(2)

If
\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{C}
, then we have
\mathcal{Z}_{d}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{A})\subseteq\mathcal{Z}_{d}(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{A})
.

(3)

If

\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{C}
, then we
have

\mathcal{Z}_{d}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{C})\subseteq\mathcal{Z}_{d}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{A})
.

Proof.

“(1)”:
For
d\in\mathbb{Z}
, we have

\mathcal{Z}_{d}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{A})\subseteq\{i\in\mathbb{N}\mid i+d<0\}\sqcup\{i\in\mathbb{N}\mid i+d\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\mathcal{A}\},

where
\sqcup
denotes the
disjoint union.
Both sets on the right hand
side are finite, thus
so is
\mathcal{Z}_{d}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{A})
.

The claims (2) and (3) follow
directly from the definition.
∎

Lemma 7.7
.

For all
d,e\in\mathbb{Z}
and all
a\in\mathcal{A}
, we have:

(1)

\mathcal{Z}_{a}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{A})=\varnothing

if
a>F(\mathcal{A})
,

(2)

\mathcal{Z}_{a}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{A})=\varnothing

if
\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{A}
,

(3)

\mathcal{Z}_{d+e}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{A})\subseteq\mathcal{Z}_{d}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{A})\sqcup(-d+\mathcal{Z}_{e}(\mathcal{A}))
,

(4)

\mathcal{Z}_{d+a}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{A})\subseteq\mathcal{Z}_{d}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{A})
; more precisely, we
have

\mathcal{Z}_{d}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{A})=\mathcal{Z}_{d+a}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{A})\sqcup\left(-d-a+\mathcal{Z}_{-a}(\mathcal{A})\right)\cap\mathcal{B},

(5)

\mathcal{Z}_{d}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{A})=\bigcap_{b\in\mathcal{B},\,b+d\in\mathcal{A}}\mathcal{Z}_{-b}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{A})
.

Proof.

“(1)”:
If
a>F(\mathcal{A})
and
i\in\mathbb{N}
,
then
i+a>F(\mathcal{A})
, hence

i+a\in\mathcal{A}
. Thus

\mathcal{Z}_{a}(\mathbb{N},\mathcal{A})=\varnothing
. So by
Lemma
7.6
(2),

\mathcal{Z}_{a}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{A})=\varnothing
.

“(2)”:
Similarly, as
\mathcal{A}
is closed
under addition,
\mathcal{Z}_{a}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{A})=\varnothing
, so by
Lemma
7.6
(3),

\mathcal{Z}_{a}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{A})=\varnothing
if

\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{A}
.

“(3)”: If
i\in\mathcal{Z}_{d+e}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{A})
then

i\in\mathcal{B}
and

i+d+e\notin\mathcal{A}
. If in
addition
i\notin\mathcal{Z}_{d}(\mathcal{B},A)
this means
that
j:=i+d\in\mathcal{A}
, so

j\in\mathcal{Z}_{e}(\mathcal{A})
and

i\in-d+\mathcal{Z}_{e}(\mathcal{A})
.

“(4)”: If
i\in\mathcal{Z}_{d+a}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{A})
, then

i\in\mathcal{B}
and

i+d+a\notin\mathcal{A}
, so
we also have
i+d\notin\mathcal{A}

as
a\in\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{A}
is
closed under addition, that
is,
i\in\mathcal{Z}_{d}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{A})
. If
conversely
i\in\mathcal{Z}_{d}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{A})

but
i\notin\mathcal{Z}_{d+a}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{A})
, then

i\in\mathcal{B}
and
i+d\notin\mathcal{A}
but
j:=i+d+a\in\mathcal{A}
, so

j\in\mathcal{Z}_{-a}(\mathcal{A})
and

i\in\mathcal{B}\cap-d-a+\mathcal{Z}_{-a}(\mathcal{A})
.

“(5)”: The inclusion

\subseteq
follows from
(4) as
d=-b+(b+d)
, where
b+d\in\mathcal{A}
.
For the reverse inclusion

\supseteq
,
assume that

i\in\mathcal{B}\setminus\mathcal{Z}_{d}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{A})
.
Then
i+d\in\mathcal{A}
. But
for all
i\in\mathcal{B}
, we have

i\notin\mathcal{Z}_{-i}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{A})
.
Therefore,
i
is not in the
intersection on the right hand
side.
∎

Taking
\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{A}
, we in
particular obtain:

Corollary 7.8
.

For any
a,b\in\mathcal{A}
, we
have:

(1)

\mathcal{Z}_{a}(\mathcal{A})=\varnothing
,

(2)

\mathcal{Z}_{-a-b}(\mathcal{A})=\mathcal{Z}_{-a}(\mathcal{A})\sqcup(a+\mathcal{Z}_{-b}(\mathcal{A}))
.

Remark 7.9
.

\mathcal{Z}_{-a}(\mathcal{A})
is
called
the
Apéry set
of
a\in\mathcal{A}
.
This set consists of

|\mathcal{Z}_{-a}(\mathcal{A})|=a
elements
(the map
\{0,\ldots,a-1\}\rightarrow\mathcal{Z}_{-a}(\mathcal{A})
that maps

j
to the smallest element in
\mathcal{A}

of the form

for some
n\geq 0
is
a bijection). In particular,
the assignment

a\mapsto\mathcal{Z}_{-a}(\mathcal{A})

is injective and identifies

\mathcal{A}
with a set of subsets of

\mathbb{Z}
. The above
corollary describes the
resulting monoid
structure on the set of
Apéry sets.

7.2.
The Laurent
polynomials

In Example
4.39
,
we have already remarked that
for
K=k[t,t^{-1}]
, the ring of
differential operators is
the localised Weyl algebra

\mathscr{D}_{K}\cong\mathscr{U}_{K}\cong k\langle t,u,\partial\rangle/\langle\partial t-t\partial-1,tu-1,ut-1\rangle.

In particular, the
\mathbb{Z}
-grading of the Laurent polynomials
given by the degree induces a

\mathbb{Z}
-grading of

\mathscr{D}_{K}

as discussed at the end of
Section
2.4
,
with the degree
d
part of

\mathscr{D}_{K}
given by

\mathscr{D}_{K,d}=\{D\in\mathscr{D}_{K}\mid\forall r\in\mathbb{Z}\exists\lambda_{r}\in k:D(t^{r})=\lambda_{r}t^{r+d}\}.

The aim of this brief
subsection is to use this
to express
\mathscr{D}_{K}

as follows as a crossed
product:
the group
\mathbb{Z}
acts on
the ring of polynomials

k[x]
by the automorphism
given by
\sigma(x)=x+1
.
We denote by
k[x]\hash\mathbb{Z}
the resulting
crossed product
(see
e.g.
[
46
, Example 2.7]

for this notion; other authors
speak of semidirect products,
smash products, or skew-group
rings rather than crossed
products):

Definition 7.10
.

The
crossed product

k[x]\hash\mathbb{Z}
is the algebra
whose underlying vector space
is
k[x]\otimes k[t,t^{-1}]
,
endowed with the multiplication

(f\otimes t^{d})(g\otimes t^{e}):=\sigma^{e}(f)g\otimes t^{d+e},\quad f,g\in k[x],d,e\in\mathbb{Z}.

Remark 7.11
.

Note this multiplication is
the opposite of the one
usually described
in Hopf algebra
text books.

We will adopt the standard
notation and write

f\hash p
instead of
f\otimes p
for
f\in k[x]
,
p\in k[t,t^{-1}]
when
considering the element in
this crossed product.
Observe that
k[x]\hash\mathbb{Z}
is
\mathbb{Z}
-graded,
where the component of degree
d

is given by
k[x]\hash t^{d}
.

Lemma 7.12
.

Let
D_{0}\coloneqq t\partial\in\mathscr{D}_{K}
.
Then

\Phi\colon k[x]\hash\mathbb{Z}\to\mathscr{D}_{K},\quad x\hash t^{d}\mapsto t^{d}D_{0}

defines an isomorphism
of graded algebras.

Proof.

This is straightforward, see
e.g.
[
52
]
or

[
17
]
.
∎

Explicitly, if

\sum_{d\in\mathbb{Z}}f_{d}\hash t^{d}\in k[x]\hash\mathbb{Z}
,
with all but finitely many

f_{d}
being zero, then

D=\Phi\left(\sum_{d}f_{d}\hash t^{d}\right)
is given by

D(t^{a})=\sum_{d}f_{d}(a)t^{d+a}.

For the sake of readability,
we will omit the isomorphism
\Phi

and
will identify the elements
of
k[x]\hash\mathbb{Z}
with their
image in
\mathscr{D}_{K}
.

Remark 7.13
.

In particular,
\mathscr{D}_{K,d}=t^{d}k[D_{0}]
.
Furthermore,
we also have

\mathscr{D}_{K,d}=k[D_{0}]t^{d}

since
[D_{0},D]=dD
for any

D\in\mathscr{D}_{K,d}
.
Note also that the set

\mathscr{D}^{n}_{K}
of
differential operators of
order at most
n
corresponds
under
\Phi
to

k[x]_{\leq n}\hash\mathbb{Z}
.

7.3.
Semigroup algebras

We now find ourselves in the setting of
Corollary
6.10

and are able to describe

\mathscr{D}_{K,d}(B,A)\coloneqq\mathscr{D}(B,A)\cap\mathscr{D}_{K,d}

using the sets

\mathcal{Z}_{d}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{A})
.
We refer e.g. to

[
59
,
60
,
58
]

for a more general treatment of rings of
differential operators over
general semigroup algebras,
and to
[
52
,
17
,
3
]

specifically for numerical
semigroups, that is, monomial curves.

For
d\in\mathbb{Z}
, consider the
ideal

I_{d}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{A})\coloneqq\{f\in k[x]\mid f(b)=0\,\forall b\in\mathcal{Z}_{d}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{A})\}\subseteq k[x].

It follows directly
from Lemma
7.7

that we have:

Lemma 7.14
.

For
d\in\mathbb{Z}
and
a\in\mathcal{A}
,
we have:

(1)

I_{a}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{A})=k[x]
if
a>F(\mathcal{A})

or
\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{A}
,

(2)

I_{d+e}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{A})\supseteq I_{d}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{A})\sigma^{d}(I_{e}(\mathcal{A}))
,

(3)

I_{d+a}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{A})\supseteq I_{d}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{A})
,

(4)

I_{d}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{A})=\sum_{b\in\mathcal{B},b+d\in\mathcal{A}}I_{b}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{A})
.

Furthermore, we have:

Lemma 7.15
.

\Phi(I_{d}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{A})\hash t^{d})=\mathscr{D}_{K,d}(B,A).

Proof.

Let
D=\Phi(f\hash t^{d})\in\mathscr{D}_{K,d}(B,A)
.
Recall that
D(t^{a})=f(a)t^{d+a}
,
thus
D(t^{a})\in A
if and only

f(a)=0
when

d+a\not\in\mathcal{A}
.
∎

Remark 7.16
.

All statements made so far about

\mathcal{Z}_{d}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{A})

and
I_{d}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{A})

can be easily generalised to higher
dimensions, meaning to
semigroups

\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{n}

which generate
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
as an
abelian group. Geometrically, these
are higher-dimensional affine spaces
in which the origin gets replaced by
a cuspidal singularity.
Their rings of differential operators
were studied in

[
59
,
60
,
58
]
,
and we are hopeful that
the techniques introduced here
can be used to investigate
the local projectivity
in the general case.
The main differences to the
one-dimensional
case that have to be dealt with
are the facts that

\mathcal{Z}_{d}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{A})

is no longer finite and
that
the ring of polynomials in
more than one variable
is not a principal ideal
domain,
which will be crucially used
in the proof of

Lemma

7.20
.

Definition 7.17
.

For
d\in\mathbb{Z}
, we define

\ell_{d}\coloneqq\prod_{b\in\mathcal{Z}_{d}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{A})}(x-b)\quad\mbox{and}\quad L_{d}\coloneqq\Phi(\ell_{d}\hash t^{d}).

Then

I_{d}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{A})=\langle\ell_{d}\rangle

and

\mathscr{D}_{K,d}(B,A)=L_{d}k[D_{0}]=k[D_{0}]L_{d}
.
When
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{B}
, it follows from
Corollary
7.8
that
for
a,b\in\mathcal{A}
, we have

L_{-a-b}=L_{-a}L_{-b}
.
Thus we recover a result by Perkins:

Proposition 7.18

(

[
52
, Theorem 4.2]
)
.

The subalgebra of
\mathscr{D}_{A}

generated by the set

\{L_{-a}\mid a\in\mathcal{A}\}

is isomorphic to
A
.

Remark 7.19
.

By the above, we can express

\mathscr{D}_{A}
as the crossed product

k[x]\hash_{\tau}\mathbb{Z}
which is
twisted by the 2-cocycle
(we refer again
to
[
46
, Example 2.7]

for the notion of twisted
crossed products, also called cleft
extensions)

\tau\colon\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}\to k[x],\quad(d,e)\mapsto\frac{\sigma^{e}(\ell_{d})\ell_{e}}{\ell_{d+e}}.

7.4.
Strong local
projectivity

Using its description given above,
we are now able to prove that

\mathscr{D}_{K}(B,A)
is
R(B,A)
-locally
projective.

Lemma 7.20
.

For any
d\in\mathbb{Z}
and
f\in I_{d}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{A})
,
there exist

b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}\in\mathcal{B}

such that

(1)

d+b_{i}\in\mathcal{A}
and

f(b_{i})\neq 0
for all
i
,

(2)

b_{i}\in\mathcal{Z}_{-b_{j}}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{A})
if
i\neq j
, and

(3)

f\in I_{-b_{1}}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{A})+\ldots+I_{-b_{n}}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{A})
.

Proof.

Assume
f\neq 0
(otherwise
the statement is clear). Set

S_{0}:=\{b\in\mathcal{B}\mid f(b)\neq 0\}.

As
f\neq 0
,
S_{0}
is
not empty.

We inductively construct a
descending chain

S_{0}\supset S_{1}\supset\ldots\supset S_{n}\supset S_{n+1}=\varnothing

of finite subsets of
S_{0}
by
letting
b_{i+1}\in S_{i}

be the smallest element and
setting
S_{i+1}:=S_{i}\cap\mathcal{Z}_{-b_{i+1}}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{A})
.
For
i>0
,
S_{i}\subseteq\mathcal{Z}_{-b_{i}}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{A})
is finite
by Lemma
7.6
(1).
Furthermore, we have

S_{i}\supsetneq S_{i+1}
: we
have
b_{i}\in S_{i}
, but

b_{i}\notin\mathcal{Z}_{-b_{i}}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{A})

by definition, so
b_{i}\in S_{i}\setminus S_{i+1}
.
Consequently, the induction
terminates after finitely many
steps in some

S_{n+1}=\varnothing.

In this way, we have
constructed
b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}\in\mathcal{B}
. As they are all taken
from
S_{0}
, we have
f(b_{i})\neq 0
for all
i
.
Recall furthermore that

f\in I_{d}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{A})
, so

f(b)=0
if
b\in\mathcal{Z}_{d}(\mathcal{B},A)
,
that is, we have

S_{0}\cap\mathcal{Z}_{d}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{A})=\varnothing.

Thus
b_{i}\notin\mathcal{Z}_{d}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{A})
,
which means

b_{i}+d\in\mathcal{A}
and we have shown
(1).

Furthermore, the way we have
chosen
b_{j}
for
j>i
shows

b_{j}\in\mathcal{Z}_{-b_{i}}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{A})

and
b_{j}>b_{i}
. The latter
implies that we also have

b_{i}\in\mathcal{Z}_{-b_{j}}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{A})

as
b_{i}-b_{j}<0
. Thus we have
shown (2).

To show (3),
recall that

I_{d}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{A})=\langle\ell_{d}\rangle
, so

\sum_{i=1}^{n}I_{-b_{i}}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{A})=\langle\mathrm{gcd}(\ell_{-b_{1}},\ldots,\ell_{-b_{n}})\rangle.

By the Definition
7.17
of

\ell_{d}
, we have

\mathrm{gcd}(\ell_{-b_{1}},\ldots,\ell_{-b_{n}})=\prod_{b\in T}(x-b),\quad T:=\bigcap_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{Z}_{-b_{i}}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{A}).

In view of (1) which is already
shown, we have

T\supseteq\bigcap_{b\in\mathcal{B},d+b\in\mathcal{A}}\mathcal{Z}_{-b}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{A})=\mathcal{Z}_{d}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{A}),

where the last equality was
established in Lemma
7.7

(5). The fact that

S_{n+1}=\varnothing
means

S_{0}\cap T=\varnothing
.
Put differently,
if
b\in T
,
then
f(b)=0
, so

f
is divisible by

\prod_{b\in T}(x-b)
and (3) is
shown.
∎

In particular, this shows:

Proposition 7.21
.

For
d\in\mathbb{Z}

and

f\in I_{d}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{A})
, let

b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}\in\mathcal{B}
be as
in the previous lemma. Then
there exist

f_{i}\in I_{-b_{i}}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{A})
,
i=1,\ldots,n
,
such that

(1)

f_{i}(b_{j})=\delta_{ij}
,

(2)

f_{i}(b)=0
for all
b\in\mathcal{B}

such that
f(b)=0
,
and

(3)

f=\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(b_{i})f_{i}.

Proof.

Let
h\in k[x]
such that

f=\prod_{b\in T}(x-b)h
, where
T
is as in the
lemma. Using (3) from there,
we express

\prod_{b\in T}(x-b)
as

g_{1}+\cdots+g_{n}
with

g_{j}\in I_{-b_{j}}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{A})
,
so that
f=g_{1}h+\cdots+g_{n}h
.
If
b\in\mathcal{B}
such that

f(b)=0
,
then either
b\in T

or
h(b)=0
,
hence
g_{i}(b)h(b)=0

for all
i=1,\ldots,n
.
Now
(2) from the lemma
implies that
g_{j}(b_{i})=0

for
i\neq j
, hence

g_{i}(b_{i})h(b_{i})=f(b_{i})\neq 0

for all

i=1,\ldots,n
.
Rescaling
the
g_{i}h
to

f_{i}:=\frac{1}{f(b_{i})}g_{i}h

therefore yields elements as wanted.
∎

Let us introduce some notation
before proving the main result.
Let us abbreviate
M\coloneqq\mathscr{D}_{K}(B,A)

and
R\coloneqq R(B,A)
.
In order to prove that
M
is
R
-locally
projective, we introduce a subset

S\subseteq\operatorname{hom}_{A}^{R}(M,M)

which satisfies the three properties in

Lemma

5.11
:
for
b\in\mathcal{B}
, we define the functional

r_{b}\colon D\mapsto D(b)\in R(B,A)

and consider the set
S
of all elements
in
\operatorname{hom}_{A}^{R}(M,M)
of the form

\pi\coloneqq\sum_{b\in\mathcal{B}}r_{b}(-)(f_{b}\hash t^{-b}),

where all but finitely many
f_{b}

are nonzero, and we assume

(1)

f_{b}\in I_{-b}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{A})
for all
b\in\mathcal{B}
, and

(2)

f_{b}(a)=\delta_{ab}
for all
a,b\in\operatorname{supp}(\pi)\coloneqq\{b\in\mathcal{B}\mid f_{b}\neq 0\}
.

Lemma 7.22
.

Let
D\coloneqq g\hash t^{d}\in\mathscr{D}_{K,d}(B,A).

Then

\pi(D)=\sum_{b\in\mathcal{B}}g(b)f_{b}\hash t^{d}.

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle\pi(D)=\sum_{b\in\mathcal{B}}r_{b}(D)\,(f_{b}\hash t^{-b})=\sum_{b\in\mathcal{B}}g(b)t^{d+b}\,(f_{b}\hash t^{-b})=\sum_{b\in\mathcal{B}}g(b)f_{b}\hash t^{d}.

We have

g(b)f_{b}\hash t^{d}\in\mathscr{D}_{K,d}(B,A)

for all
b\in\mathcal{B}
,
since
I_{-b}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{A})\subseteq I_{d}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{A})

if
d+b\in\mathcal{B}
and

g(b)=0
otherwise.
The sum on the right hand side is thus
well-defined.
∎

The following lists the
properties of
S
that we have
to establish and will use:

Lemma 7.23
.

Let

\alpha\coloneqq\sum_{b\in\mathcal{B}}r_{b}(-)(f_{b}\hash t^{-b}),\quad\beta\coloneqq\sum_{b\in\mathcal{B}}r_{b}(-)(g_{b}\hash t^{-b})\in S.

We have

(1)

\alpha(f_{b}\hash t^{-b})=f_{b}\hash t^{-b}

for all
b\in\mathcal{B}
,

(2)

\alpha=\beta
if and only if

f_{b}=g_{b}
for all
b\in\mathcal{B}
,

(3)

we have

\beta\circ\alpha=\sum_{b\in\mathcal{B}}r_{b}(-)(h_{b}\hash t^{-b}),\text{ where }h_{b}=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{B}}f_{b}(a)g_{a}\hash t^{-b},

(4)

\alpha
and
\beta

are idempotent,

(5)

\alpha\circ\beta=0

if and only if

g_{b}(a)=0

for all
a\in\operatorname{supp}(\alpha)
, and

(6)

\alpha+\beta-\beta\circ\alpha\in S

if
\alpha\circ\beta=0
.

Proof.

(1)

We have

\alpha(f_{b}\hash t^{-b})=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{B}}f_{b}(a)f_{a}\hash t^{-b}=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{B}}\delta_{ab}f_{a}\hash t^{-b}=f_{b}\hash t^{-b}.

(2)

Suppose
\alpha=\beta

and let
\Lambda\coloneqq\operatorname{supp}(\alpha)\cup\operatorname{supp}(\beta)
.
We choose
d\in\mathbb{Z}
large enough
so that
d+b\in\mathcal{A}

for all
b\in\Lambda
.
We define
for each
b\in\Lambda

the polynomial

h_{b}\coloneqq\ell_{d}\prod_{i\in\Lambda,i\neq b}(x-i)

We chose

d
in such a way that
\ell_{d}(b)\neq 0
,
so
h_{b}(b)\neq 0
,
and we can define

\bar{h}_{b}\coloneqq\frac{1}{h_{b}(b)}h_{b}
.
Then we have
\bar{h}_{b}(a)=\delta_{ab}

for all
a,b\in\Lambda
, and
it follows that for all
b\in\mathcal{B}

f_{b}\hash t^{d}=\alpha(\bar{h}_{b}\hash t^{d})=\beta(\bar{h}_{b}\hash t^{d})=g_{b}\hash t^{d}.

(3)

Let
D\coloneqq h\hash t^{d}\in\mathscr{D}_{K,d}(B,A)
.
We have

\displaystyle\beta\circ\alpha(D)

\displaystyle=\beta\left(\sum_{b\in\mathcal{B}}h(b)f_{b}\hash t^{d}\right)=\sum_{b\in\mathcal{B}}h(b)\beta\left(f_{b}\hash t^{d}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{b\in\mathcal{B}}h(b)\left(\sum_{a\in\mathcal{B}}f_{b}(a)g_{a}\hash t^{d}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{b\in\mathcal{B}}h(b)\left(\sum_{a\in\mathcal{B}}f_{b}(a)g_{a}\right)\hash t^{d},

thus the claim follows.

(4)

We have

\displaystyle\alpha^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{b\in\mathcal{B}}r_{b}(-)\left(\sum_{a\in\mathcal{B}}f_{b}(a)f_{a}\right)\hash t^{-b}

\displaystyle=\sum_{b\in\mathcal{B}}r_{b}(-)\left(\sum_{a\in\operatorname{supp}(\alpha)}f_{b}(a)f_{a}\right)\hash t^{-b}

\displaystyle=\sum_{b\in\mathcal{B}}r_{b}(-)\left(f_{b}\hash t^{-b}\right)=\alpha.

(5)

Suppose

\alpha\circ\beta=\sum_{b\in\mathcal{B}}r_{b}(-)\left(\sum_{a\in\mathcal{B}}g_{b}(a)f_{a}\right)\hash t^{-b}=0.

Then

\sum_{a\in\mathcal{B}}g_{b}(a)f_{a}=0

for all
b\in\mathcal{B}
.
For all
c\in\operatorname{supp}(\alpha)
,
we have

f_{a}(c)=\delta_{ac}
,
hence we obtain

g_{b}(c)=\sum_{a\in\operatorname{supp}(\alpha)}g_{b}(a)f_{a}(c)=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{B}}g_{b}(a)f_{a}(c)=0.

(6)

Let
\pi\coloneqq\alpha+\beta-\beta\circ\alpha
.
Then

\pi=\sum_{b\in\mathcal{B}}r_{b}(-)(h_{b}\hash t^{-b}),\text{ where }h_{b}\coloneqq f_{b}+g_{b}-\sum_{a\in\mathcal{B}}f_{b}(a)g_{a}.

We have
h_{b}=0
if
b\not\in\operatorname{supp}(\alpha)\cup\operatorname{supp}(\beta)
.
Since
\alpha\circ\beta=0
,

g_{b}(c)=0
if
c\in\operatorname{supp}(\alpha)

so that

h_{b}(c)=\begin{cases}g_{b}(c)&\text{if }c\in\operatorname{supp}(\beta),\\
f_{b}(c)&\text{if }c\in\operatorname{supp}(\alpha)\setminus\operatorname{supp}(\beta).\end{cases}

It follows that

h_{b}(a)=\delta_{ab}\quad\forall a,b\in\operatorname{supp}(\pi)=\operatorname{supp}(\alpha)\cup\operatorname{supp}(\beta),

so
\pi\in S
.

∎

Lemma 7.24
.

Let
D\in\mathscr{D}_{K,d}(B,A)

and
\alpha\in S
.
Then, there exists
\beta\in S

such that

\beta(D-\alpha(D))=D-\alpha(D)\text{ and }\alpha\circ\beta=0.

Proof.

Suppose that
D=:g\hash t^{d}

and

\alpha=:\sum_{b\in\mathcal{B}}r_{b}(-)(f_{b}\hash t^{-b}).

Let
g^{\prime}\in I_{d}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{A})

such that

g^{\prime}\hash t^{d}=D-\alpha(D)=\left(g-\sum_{b\in\mathcal{B}}g(b)f_{b}\right)\hash t^{d}.

In particular,

\alpha(g^{\prime}\hash t^{d})=0
,
so we have

g^{\prime}(a)=r_{a}\circ\alpha(g^{\prime}\hash t^{d})=0

for all
a\in\operatorname{supp}(\alpha)
.

By Lemma
7.20
,
there exist
b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}\in\mathcal{B}

and polynomials
g_{i}\in I_{-b_{i}}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{A})
,

i=1,\ldots,n
, such that

g_{i}(b_{j})=\delta_{ij}
,

g_{i}(b)=0
for all
b\in\mathcal{B}

for which
g^{\prime}(b)=0
, and

g^{\prime}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}g^{\prime}(b_{i})g_{i},

Define

\beta\coloneqq\sum_{b\in\mathcal{B}}r_{b}(-)(g_{b}\hash t^{-b}),\text{ where }g_{b}=\begin{cases}g_{i}&\text{if }b=b_{i},\\
0&\text{else.}\end{cases}

Then straightforward computations
show that
\beta
satisfies
the desired properties.
∎

.

Let
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}
be numerical semigroups,
and
A,B\subseteq K=k[t,t^{-1}]

Then
\mathscr{D}_{K}(B,A)
is strongly

-locally projective.

Proof.

The set
S
satisfies all
the properties
in
Lemma

5.11
.
To begin with, all the elements
in
S
are idempotent
(
Lemma

7.23
4
).
Furthermore,

0\in S
which is (1) in
Lemma
5.11
,
while property (2)
was shown
in

Lemma

7.23
6
.
Finally, property (3) was
shown in

Lemma

7.24
.
Strong local projectivity thus follows.
∎

7.5.
Main result

Let us summarise our results:

Theorem 7.26
.

Let
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}
be numerical semigroups,
and
A,B\subseteq K=k[t,t^{-1}]
be their semigroup algebras.
Then, we have:

(1)

There is a canonical

B|A
-coring structure on

\mathscr{D}_{K}(B,A)
.

(2)

This turns

\mathscr{D}_{A}=\mathscr{D}_{K}(A,A)
and

\mathscr{D}_{B}=\mathscr{D}_{K}(B,B)
into left Hopf algebroids over

A
respectively
B
.

(3)

If
\mathcal{A}
is symmetric,
then
\mathscr{D}_{A}
admits an antipode.

(4)

\mathscr{D}_{K}(B,A)\otimes_{\mathscr{D}_{B}}-\colon\mathscr{D}_{B}\mbox{-}\mathbf{Mod}\rightarrow\mathscr{D}_{A}\mbox{-}\mathbf{Mod}
is a monoidal
equivalence.

Proof.

All assumptions of
Theorem
6.13

are satisfied and we deduce
(1) and (2). Theorem
7.4

stated that for a symmetric
numerical semigroup,
A
is
Gorenstein and hence satisfies all

7.6.
Computing
\Delta(D)

Finally, we describe a
technique for computing the
coproduct of a given
differential operator
explicitly.

We identify
\mathscr{D}_{K}\otimes_{K}\mathscr{D}_{K}

with
k[x,y]\hash\mathbb{Z}

via the correspondence

(f\hash t^{d})\otimes_{K}(g\hash t^{e})\mapsto f(x)g(y)\hash t^{d+e}.

This is an isomorphism of
k
-vector spaces,
where

(\mathscr{D}_{K}\otimes_{K}\mathscr{D}_{K})_{d}\cong k[x,y]\hash t^{d},\quad d\in\mathbb{Z}.

The order filtration on
\mathscr{D}_{K}
,
whose graded component is

\mathscr{D}_{K,d}^{n}\cong\{f\hash t^{d}\mid\deg f\leq n\}

induces the canonical filtration
on
\mathscr{D}_{K}\otimes_{K}\mathscr{D}_{K}
,
with graded component

(\mathscr{D}_{K}\otimes_{K}\mathscr{D}_{K})^{n}_{d}\cong\{f(x)g(y)\hash t^{d}\mid\deg f+\deg g\leq n\}.

In this presentation, the comultiplication
is given as follows:

Lemma 7.31
.

For
f\hash t^{d}\in\mathscr{D}_{K,d}
,
we have

\Delta(f\hash t^{d})=f(x+y)\hash t^{d}\in(\mathscr{D}_{K}\otimes_{K}\mathscr{D}_{K})^{n}_{d}.

Proof.

Recall,
\mathscr{D}_{K}\cong\mathscr{U}_{K}
is the
universal enveloping algebra
of a Lie–Rinehart algebra
(see
Example

4.39
).
We have

\Delta(D_{0})=1\otimes_{K}D_{0}+D_{0}\otimes_{K}1,\quad\Delta(t^{d})=t^{d}\otimes_{K}1,

where
D_{0}=t\partial

was identified with

x\hash 1\in k[x]\hash\mathbb{Z}

in
Lemma

7.12
.
The claim thus follows, since

\Delta
is multiplicative.
∎

Let us now look at
\mathscr{D}_{K}(B,A)
.
We define the set

\displaystyle I^{\otimes 2}_{d}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{A})\coloneqq

\displaystyle\{(f,g)\in k[x]\times k[y]\mid f\in I_{i}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{A}),\,g\in I_{j}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{A}),\,i,j\in\mathbb{Z},\,i+j=d\}.

Then under our identification, we have

\mathscr{D}_{K}(B,A)\otimes_{A}\mathscr{D}_{K}(B,A)\cong\mathrm{span}_{k}\{f(x)g(y)\hash t^{d}\mid(f,g)\in I^{\otimes 2}_{d}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{A})\}.

Remark 7.32
.

For a universal enveloping
algebra such as
\mathscr{U}_{K}\cong\mathscr{D}_{K}
, the primitive
filtration is also a

K
-coalgebra filtration.
As explained in

Remark

5.15
,

\mathscr{D}_{K}(B,A)\otimes_{A}\mathscr{D}_{K}(B,A)

therefore carries two filtrations:
the canonical one with graded component

(\mathscr{D}_{K}(B,A)\otimes_{A}\mathscr{D}_{K}(B,A))^{n}_{d}

which is the
k
-linear subspace
spanned by the elements

\{f(x)g(y)\hash t^{d}\mid(f,g)\in I^{\otimes 2}_{d}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{A}),\,\deg f+\deg g\leq n\},

and the induced filtration,
with graded component

(\mathscr{D}_{K}\otimes_{K}\mathscr{D}_{K})^{n}_{d}\cap(\mathscr{D}_{K}(B,A)\otimes_{A}\mathscr{D}_{K}(B,A)).

The first is included in the second one,
but this inclusion is strict in general,
due to the fact that a polynomial of degree
n

might be written as a linear combination
of polynomials of higher degree.
As mentioned before, the restriction
of
\Delta
to
\mathscr{D}_{K}(B,A)

is compatible with the induced filtration,
but not necessarily with the canonical one.
We will illustrate this with a concrete
example in
Lemma

7.41
.

The statement of

Theorem

7.26

translates into the following:

Proposition 7.33
.

For any
f\hash t^{d}\in\mathscr{D}_{K}(B,A)
,

f(x+y)\in\langle g(x)h(y)\mid(g,h)\in I^{\otimes 2}_{d}\rangle\subseteq k[x,y].

To find a presentation of

\Delta(f\hash t^{d})

in

\mathscr{D}_{K}(B,A)\otimes_{A}\mathscr{D}_{K}(B,A)

is thus equivalent to finding
a presentation for
f(x+y)

inside this ideal,
which is not
unique in general.

In concrete computations,
we can however take advantage of the
fact that
\Delta
is multiplicative
by realising
\mathscr{D}_{K}(B,A)
as a module
over a suitably chose
subring
H\subseteq\mathscr{D}_{K}

whose comultiplication is
known. Then we may use:

Lemma 7.34
.

Let
\{g_{i}\}
be a set of generators
of
\mathscr{D}_{K}(B,A)
as a module over
H
.
Suppose we have two maps
(not necessarily
k
-linear)

\Delta^{\prime}\colon\{g_{i}\}\to\mathscr{D}_{K}(B,A)\otimes\mathscr{D}_{K}(B,A),\quad\Delta_{H}\colon H\to H\otimes H

such that
\Delta|_{\{g_{i}\}}=\iota^{\prime}\circ\Delta^{\prime}

and

\Delta|_{H}=\iota_{H}\circ\Delta_{H}
,
where

\iota^{\prime}\colon\mathscr{D}_{K}(B,A)\otimes\mathscr{D}_{K}(B,A)\to\mathscr{D}_{K}\otimes_{K}\mathscr{D}_{K},\quad\iota_{H}\colon H\otimes H\to\mathscr{D}_{K}\otimes_{K}\mathscr{D}_{K}

are the canonical maps.
Then the comultiplication
of
\mathscr{D}_{K}(B,A)

is uniquely determined by

\Delta^{\prime}
and
\Delta_{H}
.

Proof.

Straightforward.
∎

So if we find a pair
(H,\Delta_{H})
,
then it suffices to determine

\Delta^{\prime}
on a set of generators.
