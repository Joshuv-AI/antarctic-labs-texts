Online Resource Sharing via Dynamic Max-Min Fairness: Efficiency, Robustness and Non-Stationarity Thanks: Part of this work was done while Giannis Fikioris was visiting Google as a Student Researcher in the Market Algorithms team.
Giannis Fikioris, Siddhartha Banerjee, Éva Tardos

Abstract
We study the allocation of shared resources over multiple rounds among competing agents, via a dynamic max-min fair (DMMF) mechanism: the good in each round is allocated to the requesting agent with the least number of allocations received to date. Previous work has shown that when an agent has i.i.d. values across rounds, then in the worst case, she can never get more than a constant strictly less than 1 1 fraction of her ideal utility – her highest achievable utility given her nominal share of resources. Moreover, an agent can achieve at least half her utility under carefully designed ‘pseudo-market’ mechanisms, even though other agents may act in an arbitrary (possibly adversarial and collusive) manner. We show that this robustness guarantee also holds under the much simpler DMMF mechanism. More significantly, under mild assumptions on the value distribution, we show that DMMF allows each agent to realize a 1 − o ⁡ ( 1 ) 1-o(1) fraction of her ideal utility, despite arbitrary behavior by other agents. We achieve this by characterizing the utility achieved under a richer space of strategies, wherein an agent can tune how aggressive to be in requesting the item. Our new strategies also allow us to handle settings where an agent’s values are correlated across rounds, thereby allowing an adversary to predict and block her future values. We prove that again by tuning one’s aggressiveness, an agent can guarantee Ω ⁡ ( γ ) \Omega(\gamma) fraction of her ideal utility, where γ ∈ [ 0 , 1 ] \gamma\in[0,1] is a parameter that quantifies dependence across rounds (with γ = 1 \gamma=1 indicating full independence and lower values indicating more correlation). Finally, we extend our efficiency results to the case of reusable resources, where an agent might need to hold the item over multiple rounds to receive utility.

Introduction

Consider a principal (e.g., a system administrator) with some public resource that needs to be repeatedly shared between a set of self-interested agents over multiple rounds.
The public resource could be a scientific instrument (e.g., mass spectrometer, gene sequencer, microscope, telescope, etc.), a computing cluster, or something more commonplace like the nice big conference room.
A principal in charge of allocating these resources has to face up to multiple competing challenges:

•

Resource constraints:

A microscope can be used for one experiment at a time; a telescope can focus on one point in the sky; the conference room cannot accommodate two meetings simultaneously.

•

Stochastic and uncertain demands:

Astronomical events like supernovae are unpredictable, appearing randomly and independently of past events.
Conversely, after using a microscope to analyze a sample, a biologist takes a random amount of time to evaluate the data before needing the microscope again; thus her next request is stochastic but not independent.
Finally, the pattern of demands for different biologists or astronomers might be completely different.

•

Inefficiency:

If, for the sake of fairness, the nice conference room cannot be booked by the same person twice a week, looking at its empty chairs while presenting from a nearby room with a faulty projector would be frustrating.

•

Unfair use:

At any point, it should not be that a team’s experiment has been rejected from the computing cluster
9
times out of
10
so far, while another team’s has been accepted
19
times out of
20
.
Situations like this must be avoided even when one team tries to game the allocation rules.

•

Simple non-monetary mechanism:

The researcher of a research lab cannot be charged real money to use the lab’s resources.
The allocation rule has to be understandable and depend only on the agents’ preferences which are not expressed in monetary value and thus cannot be compared.

We model this allocation problem over
n
agents, where agent
i
has a private value
V_{i}[t]
for being allocated in round
t
.
The principal wants to be fair and can not use money, so we cannot use auction-based mechanisms like VCG, which are truthful and efficient.
This leaves the principal with more ad-hoc approaches, which may be inefficient or unfair.
For example, priority-based policies work well if few agents have high values but are unfair to the others; round-robin allocation policies are fair but inefficient as they may allocate to an agent when she has no need.

Recent work on public resource sharing has made significant progress for the above problem.
In particular, a critical development was made by
[
GBI21
]
, who proposed a new per-agent benchmark – the
ideal utility
– for measuring the performance of any mechanism.
We formalize this in
Section

2
, but in brief, the ideal utility of an agent quantifies how much utility the agent can hope to get when no other agent is present but the agent can claim no more than a pre-specified share of the resource.

[
GBI21
]
then show that under a repeated first-price auction with artificial currencies, each agent can use a simple strategy to realize at least half of her ideal utility robustly, i.e., even under adversarial (possibly collusive) behavior by other agents.

[
BFT23
]
generalize this result to the allocation of reusable resources, i.e., those that agents might use for multiple rounds, and prove that in such settings this guarantee is tight.

Our mechanism and results

We study a simple and widely used mechanism,
Dynamic Max-Min Fair
(
DMMF
), rather than pseudo-markets with artificial currencies. The
DMMF
mechanism (
Algorithm

1
) is a dynamic version of the classical max-min fair policy:
the resource in each round is allocated to the requesting agent with the least fraction of received allocations to date
.
It is widely used as a heuristic, and has been studied before, but only for
divisible
resources
[
KPS13
,
FAT21
,
Fre+18
,
Vup+23
]
.
We note that, unlike us, these works only focus on the incentive compatibility of
DMMF
, and the outcome if agents are truthful.

First, we are given the
fair share

\alpha_{i}
of each agent
i
(with
\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}=1
).
The fair share
\alpha_{i}
is meant to represent the ‘priority’ of agent
i
in the system.
For example, given
n
research labs sharing an instrument, setting fair shares equal (
\alpha_{i}=1/n
) means the mechanism favors all agents the same.
In general, however, research labs might have different sizes and so require unequal use of the instrument; the (exogenously specified) fair shares then direct the mechanism to treat different labs unequally, proportional to their fair shares.
Given the fair shares, we can define the
ideal utility
benchmark
v^{\star}_{i}(\alpha_{i})
for any agent
i
as the maximum expected per-round utility she can get if there is no competition, but she is constrained to receive the resource only on an
\alpha_{i}
fraction of the rounds.

Next, given the agents’ fair shares, we can define the basic
DMMF
mechanism as follows: in any round, given the agents requesting for the resource in that round,
DMMF
allocates to the agent with the least number of past allocations, normalized by her fair share.
The
DMMF
mechanism is much easier to participate in than pseudo-markets mechanisms: instead of declaring a real-valued bid in every round, agents in
DMMF
declare only if they want the item in that round or not.

An important idea in
[
GBI21
,
BFT23
]
is that the ideal utility
v^{\star}_{i}(\alpha_{i})
serves a dual purpose: as a benchmark (agent
i
should expect at most
v^{\star}_{i}(\alpha_{i})
per-round utility as more would violate others’ fair shares), but also, as a guide for the agent’s strategy.
These works show that a simple scheme – where the agent competes only on rounds which she should receive when realizing
v^{\star}_{i}(\alpha_{i})
– is sufficient to guarantee that each agent receives utility at least
\frac{1}{2}v^{\star}_{i}(\alpha_{i})T
by the final round
T
, irrespective of how others bid.
It is natural to ask what happens if agent
i
uses
v^{\star}_{i}(\alpha_{i})
to determine when to request under
DMMF
; in this case we recover a similar robustness guarantee as in prior work. However, a subtle but fruitful generalization is to note that since
v^{\star}_{i}(\cdot)
only depends on agent
i
’s value distribution,
v^{\star}_{i}(\beta)
can be computed for any
\beta\in[0,1]
.
This motivates a richer space of strategies we refer to as
\beta
-aggressive strategies
(
Definition

4.1
), where an agent with fair share
\alpha_{i}
acts as if her share is
\beta
.
By setting
\beta>\alpha_{i}
(resp.
\beta<\alpha_{i}
), the agent can become more (resp. less) assertive than she is entailed; however, her fair share
\alpha_{i}
still determines her normalization in
DMMF
as well as her benchmark
v^{\star}_{i}(\alpha_{i})
, which is the maximum utility she should hope for.
Nevertheless using
\beta
-aggressive policies under
DMMF
, we get the following:

•

General robust guarantee
:
We first focus on guarantees for agents whose values are i.i.d. across rounds. We show (
Theorem

4.1
) that under
DMMF
if agent
i
uses a
\beta
-aggressive strategy, then no matter how other agents behave, she is guaranteed utility up to any round
t
satisfying

\sum_{\tau\in[t]}\E\left[\big.U_{i}[\tau]\right]\geq\frac{\alpha_{i}}{\alpha_{i}+\beta-\alpha_{i}\beta}v^{\star}_{i}(\beta)t-\order{1}

(1)

where
U_{i}[\tau]
is agent
i
’s utility in round
\tau
.
This bound is not directly useful as it is expressed in terms of
v^{\star}_{i}(\beta)
and not the ideal utility
v^{\star}_{i}(\alpha_{i})
; however, it gives us a parameterized set of guarantees, which we can then optimize over to get the following setting-specific results.

Uniform distribution
:
The simplest demonstration of the usefulness of
Eq.

1
is when agent
i
’s value is uniformly distributed and
\alpha_{i}
is small (
\leq 1/8
), then
\beta=\sqrt{2\alpha_{i}}
gives:

\sum_{\tau\in[t]}\E\left[\big.U_{i}[\tau]\right]\geq\quantity( 1 - \sqrt{2\a_i} )v^{\star}_{i}(\alpha_{i})t-\order{1}

In the large market setting, as
\alpha_{i}\to 0
, this is close to optimal, as no agent can get expected utility more than her ideal utility unless assigned more than her fair share.
Moreover, note that the agent is bidding much more aggressively than her fair share suggests (since
\sqrt{2\alpha_{i}}\gg\alpha_{i}
); by doing so, however, she guarantees a
1-\sqrt{2\alpha_{i}}
fraction of
v^{\star}_{i}(\alpha_{i})t
instead of
1/2
.

Bounded distribution
(
Corollary

4.4
): More generally, when an agent’s value distribution pdf is lower and upper bounded by
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}
, respectively, then choosing
\beta=\sqrt{\nicefrac{{2\lambda_{1}\alpha}}{{\lambda_{2}}}}
gives

\sum_{\tau\in[t]}\E\left[\big.U_{i}[\tau]\right]\geq\Bigg(1-\sqrt{\frac{2\lambda_{2}\alpha_{i}}{\lambda_{1}}}\Bigg)v^{\star}_{i}(\alpha_{i})t-\order{1}.

In large market settings, this is near optimal; more specifically, under bounded distributions and equal shares across all agents, every agent loses
\order*{\nicefrac{1}{\sqrt{n}}}
fraction of her ideal utility.

Bernoulli values
(
Corollary

4.5
): If the agent’s value in each round is Bernoulli distributed
(i.e., ON/OFF, or more generally, high/low), with probability of ON states being
p
, then by using a
p
-aggressive policy, the agent can realize utility up to every round
t

\sum_{\tau\in[t]}\E\left[\big.U_{i}[\tau]\right]\geq\frac{\max\{\alpha_{i},p\}}{\alpha_{i}+p-\alpha_{i}p}v^{\star}(\alpha_{i})t-\order{1}.

In particular, if either
p\gg\alpha_{i}
or
\alpha_{i}\gg p
(i.e., the probability of ON is higher/lower than her fair share), then the above guarantee approaches
v^{\star}(\alpha_{i})t-\order{1}
.

Worst-case distributions
(
Corollary

4.2
):
If we are agnostic of the agent’s distribution, then by setting
\beta=\alpha_{i}
in
Eq.

1
we realize a
1/(2-\alpha_{i})
fraction of
v^{\star}_{i}(\alpha_{i})
.
On the positive side, this shows that an agent can always guarantee a constant fraction of her ideal utility under
DMMF
.
On the negative side, compared to our distribution-aware guarantees, this is a much smaller fraction of ideal utility.
In addition, if every agent
i
uses an
\alpha_{i}
-aggressive strategy (as suggested by the existing worst-case robustness guarantees) then a constant fraction of rounds are uncontested for.
This highlights the importance of our distribution-specific results and justifies the use of
DMMF
in large markets.

Even in the worst-case setting (which was the sole focus of previous work), our mechanism improves on prior work in two important ways: it gives
anytime
guarantees (i.e., guarantees holding in every round, not just the final round) and has better additive error (
\order{1}
instead of
\order*{\sqrt T}
).
While these might seem negligible at first, they are important in practice since they allow
DMMF
to handle
dynamic settings/populations
.
In particular, at any round, the principal can reset everyone’s allocations and restart the system (e.g., to accommodate new agents) with minimal additional loss.
In contrast, to minimize losses in pseudo-market mechanisms, the principal can only restart after a pre-determined final round, and with higher additive loss.

•

Price of Anarchy:

In symmetric settings (i.e., equal shares and identical value distributions across agents), robust guarantees on the ideal utility also imply bounds on the Price of Anarchy – the fraction of the optimal welfare achieved under any equilibrium.
In particular, for
n
symmetric agents, the expected maximum social welfare by round
t
is at most
t\sum_{i}v^{\star}_{i}(\alpha_{i})
.
On the other hand, the strategy we suggest in each of the cases above can guarantee every agent
i
close to
v^{\star}_{i}(\alpha_{i})t
utility, irrespective of how other agents act.
This means that in any Nash equilibrium, every agent must have at least that much utility, thereby showing that
DMMF
is fair and efficient in large markets under mild assumptions on the value distributions (
Corollary

4.6
).

•

Correlated rounds
:
We also extend our guarantees to handle dependent valuations.
In particular, we consider settings where agent
i
’s values are drawn via a hidden Markov model, with the underlying Markov chain over latent states parameterized by a
decorrelation parameter

\gamma_{i}\in(0,1]
.
We define this formally in
Section

2
, but at a high level,
\gamma_{i}=1
indicates independent values, while as
\gamma_{i}
decreases, states become more correlated across rounds.
Previous work
[
GBI21
,
BFT23
,
GBI17
,
GC10
]
in this setting focuses exclusively on i.i.d. values, and to the best of our knowledge, we are the first to consider values that can be correlated across rounds.
We show that under the same
DMMF
mechanism, by using a
\beta
-aggressive policy with appropriately chosen
\beta\leq\alpha_{i}
, agent
i
can always guarantee utility in every round
t

\sum_{\tau\in[t]}\E\left[\big.U_{i}[\tau]\right]\geq\frac{\gamma_{i}}{4}v^{\star}_{i}(\alpha_{i})t-\order{1}.

(2)

The actual bounds (
Corollaries

4.7
and
4.8
; also see
Fig.

1
) are more complicated, but we note that as
\gamma_{i}\to 1
we recover the
1/(2-\alpha_{i})
guarantee of the independent across rounds values setting.
See
Theorem

4.1
for the general form of our guarantee for any
\gamma_{i}
and
\beta
.
We also provide a utility guarantee in
Theorem

4.12
that is irrespective of
\gamma_{i}
and instead depends on the similarity of agent’s values in different states.

•

Multi-Round Demands
:
In
Section

5
we consider the setting of
[
BFT23
]
with multi-round demands (agents need the resource for intervals of rounds) with independent value distribution.
We then recover our per-agent utility guarantees in
Eq.

1
(up to
\order*{\sqrt T}
terms), with two caveats:

(i)

DMMF
needs to know the horizon
T
and use a parameter
r
to limit how many multi-round demands agents get, and

(ii)
the parameter
r
is tuned according to
\beta
(see
Theorem

5.1
).
This means that to get distribution dependent guarantees, we need to have symmetric agents, and the principal needs to know the distribution and tune
r
accordingly.
Setting
r
independent of the value distributions also lets us recover the
\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
robustness guarantee under
DMMF
, matching the guarantee for pseudo-markets shown in
[
BFT23
]
(who further showed this is tight for
any
mechanism under worst-case distributions).

•

Impossibility results
: Finally in
Section

6
, we study impossibility results under the
DMMF
mechanism.
For single-round demands, we show that agent
i
cannot guarantee more than a
\gamma_{i}/(1+\gamma_{i})
fraction of her ideal utility, making our guarantee in
Eq.

2
tight up to a constant factor (
Corollary

6.2
).
For multi-round demands, we show that the use of the parameter
r
, as well as the need to tune it according to the aggressiveness level
\beta
, is necessary: our bound in
Theorem

5.1
is tight for every
r
and
\beta
, up to additive
\order*{\sqrt T}
terms (
Theorem

6.3
).

Challenges and Technical Novelty

As with most online mechanisms with state, analyzing the
DMMF
mechanism is challenging.
Even if every agent has a time-invariant strategy (like the
\beta
-aggressive strategy we propose) then the mechanism determines an unbounded random walk on the space
\mathbb{N}^{n}
of the agents’ allocations.
This random process can become even more complicated when examining robust guarantees, where the other agents act adversarially, and therefore might have strategies that depend both on the time and the agents’ current allocations.

The first step towards our results is to establish an invariant property (i.e., some ‘conservation law’) that holds under
DMMF
on
any
sample path (i.e., under any sequence of values and agent actions).
Indeed, our ideal utility guarantees stem from such a property given in
Lemma

4.11
, where we show that for any agent
i
and in any interval
[1,t]
, the ratio of the number of rounds that
i
wins to the number of rounds in which she is ‘blocked’, is at least
\alpha_{i}/(1-\alpha_{i})
(i.e., the ratio of
i^{\prime}s
fair share to that of everyone else). While this should seem natural under
DMMF
, formalizing it is subtle.
In particular, it is critical to define ‘blocked’ in a counterfactual sense: an agent is blocked in round
t
, not only if she loses upon requesting, but also if she would have lost had she requested.

The above invariant implies that agent
i
can not win rounds significantly fewer relative to the number of her requests and her share.
However, this does not imply any utility guarantees: if the other agents know agent
i
’s values, they can block only her high-valued requests, making her win rounds that have next to
0
value. When the values are independent in each iteration, the agent’s value on round
t
cannot be predicted, and so the other agents have to block her independently of her value. Selecting the right
\beta
-aggressive policy is key to getting our near-optimal guarantees.
As we increase
\beta
(starting from
\beta=\alpha_{i}
), agent
i
gains the potential to win more rounds and have higher total value across requested rounds; however, a higher
\beta
allows the other agents to block a bigger fraction of agent
i
’s requests, and also means a lower average value per request.
In
Section

4.1
we show how to pick
\beta
to get the right trade-off between the above two competing objectives.

When agent
i
’s values are correlated across rounds, the other agents might be able to infer information about her values from her requests, so their blocking may not be independent of her values.
For a fixed decorrelation parameter
\gamma_{i}
, agent
i
can use the
\alpha_{i}
-aggressive strategy to get some utility guarantees depending on
\gamma_{i}
; however, this guarantee turns out to be a
\Omega(\gamma_{i}^{2})
fraction of her ideal utility.
This is the outcome of information leakage when
\gamma_{i}<1
: the other agents can now block agent
i
’s high-valued rounds
without
blocking her low-valued rounds.
This has a twofold effect: agent
i
loses utility
and
wins rounds that are worth less. Both effects increase as
\gamma_{i}
decreases, making the dependence on
\gamma_{i}
quadratic.
We show however that agent
i
can control this leakage by following a
\beta
-aggressive strategy for
\beta<\alpha_{i}
and get utility bounds that scale linearly with
\gamma_{i}
.

Related Literature

Our work combines three separate considerations in the design of resource allocation algorithms: dynamics (the ‘online’ aspect), competing objectives (overall efficiency vs. individual guarantees), and competition (private values and robust guarantees). These topics, and combinations thereof, have a long history of study – in the interest of space, we discuss only the most relevant work here.

We build directly on the work of
[
GBI21
,
BFT23
]
who study robust guarantees of pseudo-market mechanisms when agents have i.i.d. valuations.
They only focus on guarantees for worst-case distributions and prove that an agent can always guarantee a
1/2
fraction of her ideal utility;
[
GBI21
]
study only single-round demands while
[
BFT23
]
extend this guarantee to multi-round demands which they show is tight for any mechanism.
These works themselves build on earlier work on using pseudo-markets for online allocation
[
Cav14
,
JS07
,
GC10
,
GBI17
]
.
An important distinction, though, is that these earlier works study equilibrium outcomes in Bayesian settings instead of robust outcomes; moreover, all these works require agents to have i.i.d. valuations.
More recently,
[
Elo+23
]
study pseudo-markets where agent’s values can be correlated across time; they prove the existence of a static equilibrium, and empirically study its performance in a few practical examples.
The interest in pseudo-markets stems from successful real-world deployments for course allocation
[
Bud+17
]
, food banks
[
Wal14
,
Pre22
]
and cloud computing
[
Daw+13
,
Vas+16
]
.
Our results suggest that simpler mechanisms may suffice in some of these settings, and also that strong performance guarantees are achievable under more relaxed models of agent utility.

Further afield, we have the study of fairness-efficiency tradeoffs in online allocation of divisible resources. this line of work starts with
[
KMT98
]
, and
with a recent renewal of interest due to data-centers
[
BM01
,
Bon+06
]
and cloud computing
[
Gho+11
,
SFS12
,
Gra+16
,
PPS12
]
.
Most of these works consider static settings or assume requests of equal importance.
Recent work has focused on extending these results to time-varying utilities
[
Fre+18
,
FAT21
,
Vup+23
]
, analyzing the incentive and efficiency properties of simple
DMMF
-like policies for divisible resource allocation.
While the need for rationing in our setting makes it incompatible with these works, our idea of proving robust guarantees against per-agent benchmarks may also prove useful in those models.

Another stream of work has looked at efficiency-fairness tradeoffs in online allocation problems, but without incentive constraints (i.e., where values are known to the principal at the start of a round).
In this context, prior work has considered both stochastic
[
SBY22
,
BHS23
,
YK22
]
as well as adversarial
[
FZC17
,
Ban+22
,
KS22
,
BKM22
,
Ban+23
]
utility models, and characterized various tradeoffs between different objectives.
This is also related to a much larger body of work looking at similar questions in static (i.e., one-shot) settings. A particularly relevant example of this for us is recent work by
[
BEF21
,
BEF22
]
, who study the one-shot allocation of indivisible goods among agents with exogenous shares, and arbitrary values, and prove that every agent can guarantee a constant fraction of her
Any Price Share
(APS), a quantity that could be described as a worst-case ideal utility. While their setting and results are, for the moment, incomparable to the one we consider, understanding how they are related is an important future direction.

Finally, considering incentives in online allocation places our work in the literature on dynamic mechanisms with state.
The challenge here arises from factors that couple decisions across time; e.g., incomplete information and learning
[
IJS11
,
DPS15
,
NST15
]
,
constraints like budget limits
[
GKP13
,
LST12
,
BBW15
]
,
queues in a network allocation
[
GT20
,
GT21
]
,
stochastic fluctuations
[
GM09
,
BV10
]
, etc.
Analyzing equilibria in such repeated settings can be difficult, and our work contributes to the small but growing set of techniques available for this; in particular, our idea of obtaining results that interpolate between constant factors in worst-case settings to near-optimal efficiency in more realistic settings is reminiscent of the work on Price of Anarchy in large markets
[
Fel+16
]
.

Online Public Resource Allocation – Single Round Demands

In this section, we formalize our model, mechanism and results when agents have single-round demands.
We develop our results in the general setting where an agent’s value in different rounds can be correlated.
The results for independent demands follow as special cases.

The principal has a single resource in each round to allocate between
n
agents over a horizon of
T
rounds.
Agents have
single-round demands
: in every round
t\in[T]
, each agent
i\in[n]
samples a random
value

V_{i}[t]\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
: if she is allocated the item on round
t
then she gets
V_{i}[t]
utility in that round.
In
Section

5
, we extend this to
multi-round
demands, where an agent may need to get the resource for more than one contiguous round to satisfy her demand.

We assume that each agent’s value is drawn from an underlying distribution, that is
independent across agents
but possibly
dependent across rounds
.
The dependence follows a Hidden Markov Model: agent
i
in round
t
has some latent state
S_{i}[t]
drawn from some discrete and finite state-space
\mathcal{S}_{i}
.
In round
t
, agent
i
’s latent state
S_{i}[t]
is generated based on
S_{i}[t-1]
via a time-invariant Markov chain with transition kernel
p_{i}(\cdot,\cdot)
(i.e., the transition probability from state
s^{\prime}
to
s
is given by
p_{i}(s^{\prime},s)
).
We assume that this Markov chain is ergodic (i.e., irreducible and aperiodic), thereby converging to a unique stationary distribution
\pi_{i}
from any starting state.
To simplify our results, we assume the state in the first round
S_{i}[1]
is sampled from the stationary distribution
\pi_{i}
.

Each state
s_{i}\in\mathcal{S}_{i}
is associated with a value distribution
\mathcal{F}_{i,s_{i}}
; given state
S_{i}[t]
of agent
i
in round
t
,
V_{i}[t]
is sampled from
\mathcal{F}_{i,S_{i}[t]}
.
This sampling process is done independently from other rounds: formally,
conditioned on the value of
S_{i}[t]
, the value
V_{i}[t]
is independent of
V_{1}[1],\ldots,V_{i}[t-1]
and
S_{1}[1],\ldots,S_{i}[t-1]
.
We also define
\mathcal{F}_{i}
as the induced value distribution when state
S_{i}[t]
is sampled from
\pi_{i}
, the stationary distribution of agent
i
’s Markov chain; formally,
\mathcal{F}_{i}=\sum_{s}\pi_{i}(s)\mathcal{F}_{i,s}
.

Finally, our main robustness guarantees depend on a parameter that quantifies the amount of decorrelation across rounds. For agent
i
, we define
\gamma_{i}\in[0,1]
as
\gamma_{i}=\min_{s^{\prime},s\in\mathcal{S}_{i}}\big(\nicefrac{{p_{i}(s^{\prime},s)}}{{\pi_{i}(s)}}\big)
.
Thus
\gamma_{i}=1
corresponds to the i.i.d. value distribution case, since
p_{i}(s^{\prime},s)=\pi_{i}(s)
and therefore in each round
t
the state
S[t]
is sampled from
\pi_{i}
, regardless of the state in the previous round.
In contrast, if
\gamma_{i}=0
, then some states are perfectly correlated with the following ones.

The Dynamic Max-Min Fair Mechanism

Our work focuses on understanding the performance of the
Dynamic Max-Min Fair (
DMMF
) mechanism
.
To define
DMMF
, we first assume that each agent
i
has an associated
fair share

\alpha_{i}
, with
\sum_{i\in[n]}\alpha_{i}=1
.
Fair shares are exogenously defined (i.e., before we run the mechanism) and represent the fraction of the resource that the principal wants each agent to have (which then determines her associated
ideal utility
, see
Section

3
).

The
DMMF
mechanism (
Algorithm

1
) aims to keep the rounds allocated to every agent
i
close to her fair share
\alpha_{i}
, compared to other agents.
Formally, if
A_{i}[t-1]
is the allocation of agent
i
up to round
t-1
(i.e., the number of rounds the agent has held the item), the agent with the smallest
\frac{A_{i}[t-1]+1}{\alpha_{i}}
who requests the item wins in round
t
(we break ties arbitrarily).

Reemphasizing our comment in the introduction:
DMMF
is a very simple deterministic policy that needs no knowledge of the agents’ latent state, value distribution, or even the horizon
T
.

Input:

Agents
[n]
, agents’ fair shares
\{\alpha_{i}\}_{i\in[n]}

Initialize
agent allocations
A_{i}[0]=0\quad\forall\,i\in[n]

for

t=1,2,\ldots

do

Let
\mathcal{N}_{t}=
agents who request for the item in round
t

if

|\mathcal{N}_{t}|=0

then

Set
i^{*}=\varnothing

//
No winner

else

Allocate to
i^{*}=\argmin_{i\in\mathcal{N}_{t}}\frac{A_{i}[t-1]+1}{\alpha_{i}}

//
Minimum normalized allocation

Set
A_{i}[t]=A_{i}[t-1]+\mathbb{1}\left[i=i^{*}\right]\quad\forall\,i\in[n]

//
Update agents’ total allocations

end for

The Ideal Utility Benchmark

We now motivate and define the performance benchmark we consider for our setting.

When mechanisms use real money and agents have quasi-linear utilities, monetary values provide a way to compare different agents’ values and utilities.
In contrast, in non-monetary settings, there is no way to make interpersonal comparisons between agents, and so we need to use individual welfare benchmarks (i.e., from the perspective of each agent).
The benchmark we use here is the
ideal utility
, which starts off by assuming that each agent has an (exogenous) fair share
\alpha
, and then defines an agent’s benchmark utility as the top
\alpha
fraction of items based on her own relative valuations in different rounds.
The ideal utility was introduced in
[
GBI21
]
, based on earlier ideas from the economics literature on bargaining and the Fisher market.

[
BFT23
]
subsequently extend this definition for multi-round demands, which we use in
Section

5
.

Following
[
GBI21
,
BFT23
]
, we define an agent
i
’s ideal utility to be
the highest expected per-round utility she can get while only getting her fair share of the resource
(i.e., being allocated at most an
\alpha_{i}
fraction of rounds).
Formally, for each agent
i
with fair-share
\alpha_{i}
, her ideal utility is the maximum expected per-round utility she can achieve in a simplified setting with no agents other than
i
, but where agent
i
has to request the item at most
\alpha_{i}
fraction of the rounds.

Note, though, that the definition of an agent’s ideal utility only takes as input her fair share
\alpha_{i}\in[0,1]
, and not those of other agents.
This motivates a generalization of the above definition to that of
\beta
-ideal utility
: the maximum expected per-round utility an agent can receive with no competition if she is allowed to get the item a
\beta\in[0,1]
fraction of the rounds.
We denote the
\beta
-ideal utility of agent
i
with
v^{\star}_{i}(\beta)
.
Recall we define
\mathcal{F}_{i}
to be the agent’s steady-state value distribution.
Now, extending the definition of
[
BFT23
]
, we have

Definition 3.1

(
\beta
-ideal utility)
.

For any fixed
\beta\in[0,1]
, the
\beta
-ideal utility
v^{\star}_{i}(\beta)
of an agent with valuation distribution
\mathcal{F}_{i}
is the maximum expected utility she can get under no competition, but when the probability with which she requests the item is at most
\beta
.
Formally, if
\rho_{\beta}:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to[0,1]
denotes the (stationary) probability of requesting the item given its current value, then:

\begin{split}v^{\star}_{i}(\beta)\;=\quad\max_{\rho_{\beta}:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to[0,1]}\qquad&\E_{V\sim\mathcal{F}_{i}}\left[V\,\rho_{\beta}(V)\right]\\
\textrm{such that}\qquad&\E_{V\sim\mathcal{F}_{i}}\left[\rho_{\beta}(V)\right]\leq\beta\end{split}

(3)

The
\beta
-ideal utility (and the corresponding requesting optimal policy
\rho^{\star}_{\beta}(\cdot)
) are important for our results, and so, before proceeding, we make three observations:
First, the ideal utility of agent
i
with fair share
\alpha_{i}
(as defined in
[
GBI21
,
BFT23
]
) is
v^{\star}_{i}(\alpha_{i})
.
However, the more general definition lets us consider the value of a different request frequency, i.e., more/less aggressive than what her fair share is, even though the mechanism treats them as having share
\alpha_{i}
.

Second, note that
Eq.

3
references
\mathcal{F}_{i}
, the agent’s
steady-state
value distribution; neither the policy
\rho_{\beta}
nor the expectations depend on her true latent state.
This is because, for sufficiently long horizon
T
, the value of
v^{\star}_{i}(\beta)
is roughly the same as the following:
the time-average utility of agent
i
over
T
rounds if she requests the item according to the optimal policy (that might be state, history, and time-dependent), which always gets the item at most
\beta T
rounds.
In particular, if we take
T\to\infty
, then the two are the same almost surely.

Third, note that
v^{\star}_{i}(b)
is a non-decreasing and concave function.
This is important to relate its values for different values of
\beta
.
To see this, note that we can simplify (
3
) to get that
v^{\star}_{i}(\beta)
is the expected value after replacing values in the bottom
(1-\beta)
part of the distribution with
0
.
For distributions with no atoms, we can write this as
v^{\star}(\beta)=\E\left[V\mathbb{1}\left[V\geq F^{-1}(1-\beta)\right]\right]
, where
F
is the distribution’s CDF.
This is clearly non-decreasing in
\beta
as higher
\beta
includes more non-negative values.
Further, the derivative in
\beta
is
F^{-1}(1-\beta)
, the value at the top
\beta
th fraction of the distribution, which is non-increasing in
\beta
and shows that the function is concave.

3.1
Ideal Utility and Social Welfare

As we mentioned before, the ideal utility provides a benchmark for how much utility an agent with a specific fair share can hope to achieve, irrespective of other agents’ valuations and behaviors.
This is important in our setting since in the absence of money we can not compare agent valuations.
However, in the special case when all agents have the same value distribution, even without knowing this distribution, we can reason about their relative values, and consequently, maximizing social welfare becomes a reasonable goal.
In this setting, where every agent
i
has
v^{\star}_{i}(\cdot)=v^{\star}(\cdot)
, the quantity
nv^{\star}(1/n)T
is an upper bound for the expected maximum social welfare.

This upper bound entails that every per-agent robustness guarantee for ideal utility also implies a social welfare guarantee.
When every agent receives utility at least
cv^{\star}(1/n)T
for some
c\leq 1
, this immediately implies that the resulting social welfare is at least a
c
fraction of the expected maximum social welfare.
In other words, the robustness guarantees of the
DMMF
mechanism immediately translate to Price of Anarchy bounds in symmetric settings.
We summarize the implications of our ideal utility bounds for social welfare in the case of independent value distributions in
Corollary

4.6
.

We note that the above technique can also be used to maximize social welfare for arbitrary value distributions under strong assumptions: values are expressed in a way where inter-agent comparisons are possible, the principal knows the agents’ value distributions, and can use it to determine shares. This follows from standard arguments involving ex-ante relaxations, wherein we set the fair shares according to the fractional allocation that maximizes the expected social welfare.
Subsequently, our robust guarantees would imply welfare guarantees, as in the symmetric case above.
A similar technique was used in
[
GBI17
]
, where the agents’ budgets are set according to the above idea to maximize social welfare in a pseudo-market mechanism.

4
Performance Guarantees for
DMMF
Under Single-Round Demands

In this section, we characterize the robustness of the
DMMF
mechanism, i.e., how much utility an agent can get under
DMMF
using a particular class of strategies, irrespective of the behavior of the other agents.
Because we focus on a single agent, we drop the
i
subscript through this section.

We consider an agent with fair share
\alpha
, whose latent state Markov chain has decorrelation parameter
\gamma
, and who uses one among the following set of strategies:

Definition 4.1

(
\beta
-aggressive strategies)
.

For any fixed
\beta\in[0,1]
, in a
\beta
-aggressive strategy
the agent requests the resource in round
t
with probability
\rho^{\star}_{\beta}(V[t])
, i.e., with the probability suggested by the optimal policy in (
3
) which realizes her
\beta
-ideal utility.
In other words,
\beta
parameterizes how aggressive the agent is in asking for resources.

Note that
\beta

need not equal the agent’s fair share

\alpha
.
Using
U[t]
to denote the agent’s utility in round
t
, our main guarantee for
DMMF
is as follows.

Theorem 4.1
.

Consider any agent with fair share
\alpha
, and assume that her latent-state Markov chain satisfies that for all
s,s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}
,
p(s^{\prime},s)\geq\gamma\pi(s)
.
Fix any
\beta\in(0,1]
and let
\rho^{\star}_{\beta}
be the optimal policy in optimization problem (
3
).
Under the
DMMF
mechanism, if the agent uses the
\beta
-aggressive strategy, then irrespective of how other agents behave, she is guaranteed total utility for every round
T

\sum_{t\in[T]}\E\left[\big.U[t]\right]\geq\gamma\frac{\alpha-(1-\alpha)\beta(1-\gamma)}{\alpha+(1-\alpha)\beta\gamma}v^{\star}(\beta)T-\frac{v^{\star}(\beta)}{\alpha+\beta}.

(4)

Eq.

4
provides a robust guarantee for the utility earned by an agent under
DMMF
, for any share
\alpha
, any
\beta
-aggressive strategy, and any decorrelation parameter
\gamma
.
This level of generality makes it hard to interpret directly.
Also, its proof is somewhat involved; we defer it to
Section

4.3
.

One reason the guarantee in
Eq.

4
is difficult to parse is that it is in terms of
v^{\star}(\beta)
, instead of the ideal utility
v^{\star}(\alpha)
(which is the benchmark we want to compare against).
While this is not directly a useful comparison (since the agent cannot hope utility close to this, especially for
\beta\gg\alpha
),
\beta
gives us a tuning parameter that achieves a range of different guarantees via a
single
set of strategies.
To demonstrate this, in the next two sections, we carefully choose
\beta
depending on setting-specific features to get strong performance guarantees under
(1)
i.i.d. valuations (
\gamma=1
) with structured distributions and
(2)
general correlated valuations.

4.1
Robustness and Efficiency of
DMMF
under Independent Valuations

First, we specialize the guarantees in
Theorem

4.1
to the case where agent valuations are independent across rounds (i.e.,
\gamma=1
). The corollaries mostly follow by carefully setting the parameters in
Theorem

4.1
, the detailed proofs of which are deferred to
Section

A.1
.

With independent distributions, by setting
\beta=\alpha
, we immediately get a
worst-case
robustness guarantee, i.e., one that does not depend on the agent’s value distribution.

Corollary 4.2

(Robustness under Independent Valuations)
.

If an agent with fair share
\alpha
and i.i.d. values (i.e.,
\gamma=1
) uses the
\alpha
-aggressive strategy, then under
DMMF
in every round
T

\sum_{t\in[T]}\E\left[\big.U[t]\right]\geq\frac{1}{2-\alpha}v^{\star}(\alpha)T-\order{ 1 }.

(5)

This corollary follows by substituting
\gamma=1
and
\beta=\alpha
in
Theorem

4.1
.
It recovers the guarantee of
[
GBI21
,
BFT23
]
that was obtained under the ‘repeated first-price with artificial currencies’ mechanism, but using the much simpler
DMMF
mechanism; to the best of our knowledge, this is the first robustness guarantee shown for a non-pseudo-market mechanism.
Additionally, compared to those previous results, our guarantee holds in every round (not just the final one), the additive utility error is
\order*{1}
instead of
\order*{\sqrt{T}}
, and the multiplicative factor is slightly better than
1/2
.
The first two facts have the following practical implications.

Remark 4.3

(Handling dynamic populations)
.

When agents enter or leave the mechanism, the principal might want to restart the allocation.
If the
DMMF
mechanism is restarted
m
times, the only additional error in our guarantee is an
\order*{m}
additive error.
Furthermore, these restarts can happen at arbitrary rounds.
In contrast, restarting the pseudo-market mechanism of
[
GBI21
,
BFT23
]
needs to happen at pre-decided times to avoid unnecessary losses.

The guarantee in
Eq.

5
is remarkable in that it shows that an agent, by requesting appropriately, can always realize at least
\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
her ideal utility, no matter what her value distribution is or how other agents behave.
One downside is that this may lead to the system overall losing efficiency because no agent requests the item in too many rounds.
Unfortunately, this downside is unavoidable in the worst-case: in
Theorem

6.1
we show that under a certain value distribution, the agent cannot guarantee more than half her ideal utility.

Improved guarantees

The use of
\beta
-aggressive strategies allows us to recover this efficiency loss in cases where an agent’s distribution is structured.
To this end, we next consider the case when the agent’s value is drawn i.i.d. from a uniformly continuous probability density function (pdf), which further is upper and lower bounded. For example, if the agent’s value is a uniform distribution every round, the agent can always guarantee a
1-\sqrt{2\alpha}
fraction of her total ideal utility.

Corollary 4.4

(Independent Valuations with Bounded density function)
.

Consider an agent with fair share
\alpha
, and whose value is drawn i.i.d. (i.e., with
\gamma=1
) from some interval
[\underline{v},\overline{v}]
via a probability density function
f
satisfying
\lambda_{1}\leq f(v)\leq\lambda_{2}
for all
\forall\,v\in[\underline{v},\overline{v}]
.
If
\alpha\leq\min\big\{\frac{2\lambda_{1}}{\lambda_{2}},\frac{\lambda_{2}}{2\lambda_{1}}\big\}
, by using a
\beta
-aggressive request strategy with
\beta=\sqrt{\nicefrac{{2\lambda_{1}\alpha}}{{\lambda_{2}}}}
, the agent can get

\frac{\sum_{t\in[T]}\E\left[\big.U[t]\right]}{v^{\star}(\alpha)T}\geq 1-\sqrt{\frac{2\lambda_{2}\alpha}{\lambda_{1}}}-\order{ T^{-1} }.

Another case that showcases the strength of
Theorem

4.1
and
\beta
-aggressive policies is when the agent’s values are Bernoulli random variables.
If the value’s mean is exactly
\alpha
, then we cannot improve over the guarantee of
Corollary

4.2
(in
Theorem

6.1
we show that such an agent cannot do better than
1/2
of the ideal utility if
\alpha
is small).
However, if the mean is not close to
\alpha
(either much bigger or much smaller), we prove that a
\beta
-aggressive policy guarantees almost all her ideal utility.

Corollary 4.5

(Independent Bernoulli Valuations)
.

Consider an agent with fair share
\alpha
, and whose value is drawn i.i.d. (i.e., with
\gamma=1
) from a Bernoulli distribution with mean
p
, for some
p\in(0,1]
. Then, using a
p
-aggressive request strategy, the agent can get

\frac{\sum_{t\in[T]}\E\left[\big.U[t]\right]}{v^{\star}(\alpha)T}\geq\frac{\max\{\alpha,p\}}{\alpha+p-\alpha p}-\order{ T^{-1} }\geq 1-\frac{1}{1+\lambda}-\order{ T^{-1} }

where
\lambda=\max\big\{\frac{\alpha}{p},\frac{p}{\alpha}\big\}\geq 1
.
The bigger
\lambda
becomes, the closer to optimal the previous bound becomes.

As we mentioned in
Section

3.1
, in the special case when agents are
symmetric
(same distributions and fair shares), maximizing social welfare becomes a reasonable goal.
With
n
symmetric agents, each with a fair share of
\alpha=1/n

nv^{\star}(1/n)T
.
The above corollaries show that each individual can achieve total utility close to
v^{\star}(\alpha)T
, implying close to optimal social welfare at all Nash equilibria. In the settings of
Corollaries

4.4
and
4.5

Corollary 4.6

(Social Welfare in Symmetric Settings)
.

When there are
n
agents with identical fair shares and distributions,
Corollaries

4.2
,
4.4
, and
4.5
prove that the Price of Anarchy is bounded.
More specifically, ignoring the additive

errors, we have that the Price of Anarchy is at most

•

2-1/n
for arbitrary independent valuations.

•

1+\order*{ 1/\sqrt{n} }
for valuations with bounded density functions.

•

1+\order*{ n^{-\e} }
for Bernoulli valuations with mean
p\leq 1/n^{1+\varepsilon}
or
p\geq 1/n^{1-\varepsilon}
for some
\varepsilon>0
.

Corollary 4.7

4.3
Robustness in the
DMMF
mechanism (Proof of Theorem
4.1
)

Finally, in this section we prove our guarantee in
Theorem

4.1
.
We refer to the other agents simply as the adversary.
To prove our guarantee, we first prove an invariant under
DMMF
for the normalized allocation an agent gets under any strategy.
For this, we need some additional notation: under any request strategy being used by the agent, we define

•

\texttt{Req}[t]=1
indicates that the agent requests the item in round
t
;
\texttt{Req}[t]=0
otherwise.

•

\texttt{Blk}[t]=1
indicates that the adversary wins the item in round
t
and would have done so regardless of whether the agent requests the item or not;
\texttt{Blk}[t]=0
otherwise.

•

W[t]=1
if the agent wins round
t
;
W[t]=0
otherwise. Note
W[t]=\texttt{Req}[t](1-\texttt{Blk}[t])
.

•

U[t]
is the utility of the agent in round
t
. Note
U[t]=V[t]W[t]=V[t]\texttt{Req}[t](1-\texttt{Blk}[t])
.

We start with the following simple lemma that bounds the number of times the adversary can block the agent. We do so for any strategy followed by the agent and the adversary, not only the
\beta
-aggressive strategy that we are going to consider for the agent in subsequent results.

Lemma 4.11
.

For every round
T
and strategy followed by the agent, with probability
1
,

\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\sum_{t\in[T]}\texttt{Blk}[t]\leq\frac{1}{\alpha}\left(1+\sum_{t\in[T]}W[t]\right).

(8)

The lemma above makes intuitive sense: the number of rounds where the agent gets blocked (normalized by the adversary’s fair share) cannot be more than the number of the agent’s wins (normalized by the agent’s fair share).
If the inequality in
Lemma

4.11
is violated in a round, then the agent in that round has won significantly less than the adversary.
This means that she can win the item if she requests it.
This indicates why it is important to bound rounds when
\texttt{Blk}[t]=1
, not just the rounds the adversary wins.
In contrast, the number of rounds the adversary wins might not correlate with the rounds won by the agent.

In this section, we prove a utility guarantee that does not depend on

Similar to

However, instead of

\sigma(\beta)

If these probabilities are the same for every state then

and the bound of our theorem becomes identical to the one in

The result for this special case makes sense: if the policy

requests the item with the same probability every round, even if the agent’s values are dependent across rounds, her requests are independent.
This means that the adversary cannot infer her state from the request, and so cannot take advantage of the dependence.
We therefore get results that match the independent values case.
In general, however,
\sigma(\beta)
can take values less than

We note that a simple lower bound is

Theorem 4.12

Fix
\beta\in(0,1]

The theorem’s proof is based on the agent requesting the good with probability

5
Reusable Public Resources

In this section, we study the reusable resources model, where an agent might need to use the resource for multiple rounds. We focus only on the case where values are independent across rounds. We get results similar to the ones in
Theorem

4.1
for
\gamma=1

Single Agent model

We note that the model of this section is identical to the one in
[
BFT23
]
.
In every round, in addition to the agent’s value in that round, there is also a duration for how long she wants to use the resource.
Every round
t
, the agent samples a type
(V[t],K[t])
where
K[t]
is the length of the demand and
V[t]
is the agent’s value
per-round
if she gets the item for the desired duration.
In other words, if the agent is allocated the item for rounds
t,t+1,\ldots,t+K[t]-1
, then she receives total utility
V[t]K[t]
.
In contrast, if the agent is not allocated the item for
all
of these rounds, then she cannot gain any utility from her round
t

Similar to when agents have single-round durations, we assume that
(V[t],K[t])
is sampled from some distribution
\mathcal{F}
, independently of other agents and rounds (for reusable resources, we focus only on independence across rounds).
Note that this means if the agent has
K[t]>1
but is not allocated the item in round
t
, then in round
t+1
, the earlier demand is lost, and she draws a new demand
(V[t],K[t])
.
Finally, we assume that there is a
k_{\max}
known to the mechanism such that the maximum desired duration of every agent is at most
k_{\max}
.
Once an agent is allocated the item for the rounds that correspond to her desired duration, we assume the item is unavailable for reallocation; this includes the agent who was allocated the item.
This commitment to not interrupt allocation models situations with large setup costs.

This model captures many practical examples, especially when research teams are competing for the use of a scientific instrument.
For example, teams of biologists might want to use a gene sequencer to run experiments whose durations have varying lengths.
After allocating the sequencer for a certain experiment, we cannot reallocate until that experiment is finished; otherwise, the samples and chemicals used are wasted.
We refer the reader to
[
BFT23
]
for further examples that motivate this model.

Dynamic Max-Min Fair Mechanism

We now present the
DMMF
mechanism for reusable resources.
First, note that
Algorithm

1

when the adversary can make reservations for multiple rounds that cannot be interrupted, the agent cannot get the resource unless she requests it before the adversary blocks it for a long period.
If the adversary’s demands are really long, it is unlikely that the agent has positive value in the few rounds between two different requests by the adversary.
This means the agent either has to win rounds for which she has no value or win very infrequently.

To circumvent the issue above, the mechanism of this section limits when agents get the item for multiple rounds.
In particular, if the number of rounds the agent has won is above a certain threshold, then the agent cannot make any more multi-round demands.
We quantify that threshold with the total number of rounds

and a parameter

Unfortunately, because this algorithm depends on the horizon

In

p(s^{\prime},s)\geq\gamma\pi(s)
.
This implies that when the agent stays in state
1
for roughly
1+1/\gamma
rounds in expectation.
The adversary can take advantage of this structure: whenever the agent wins a round in state
1
, because of the properties of the
DMMF
mechanism, the adversary can win the next
\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}
rounds.
This makes the agent miss out on any utility during these rounds, the first
1/\gamma
of which are state
1
rounds in expectation.
This makes the agent miss out on roughly a
\frac{1/\gamma}{1+1/\gamma}
fraction of her positive-valued rounds, no matter what strategy she follows.
We include the full proofs of both the theorem and the corollary in
Appendix

C
.

Impossibility results for reusable resources

We now show an upper bound on the amount of utility the agent can guarantee in the multi-round demand setting.
Our result is an upper bound for the expected utility of the agent in the
DMMF
mechanism relative to
v^{\star}(\beta)T
, for any
\beta\in(0,1]
and any
r\geq 1
used by the mechanism.
We note that our result holds under any strategy used by the agent, not necessarily a
\beta
-aggressive strategy.

Our bound is most interesting when
k_{\max}
is a large constant.
More specifically, if
\frac{k_{\max}-1}{k_{\max}}
is about
1
, then our result implies that the agent cannot guarantee more than
(1-\frac{1-\alpha}{r})v^{\star}(\beta)T
utility.
This makes our guarantee of

\min\big\{\frac{\alpha}{r\beta},1-\frac{1-\alpha}{r}\big\}v^{\star}(\beta)T

in
Theorem

5.1
essentially right, for any
r,\beta
(up to an additive error of
O(\sqrt{T})
):
Because an agent with only multi-round demands cannot win more than
T\alpha/r
rounds, by the definition of
\beta
-ideal utility, the largest expected utility she can guarantee is
Tv^{\star}(\alpha/r)
which by concavity is at most
Tv^{\star}(\beta)\min\{1,\frac{\alpha}{r\beta}\}
.
Combining this with the following result for large
k_{\max}
proves the tightness.

Theorem 6.3
.

In the
DMMF
mechanism, for every
\beta\in(0,1]
, there is an agent value distribution such that the agent cannot get total expected utility more than

\left(1-\frac{1-\alpha}{r}\frac{k_{\max}-1}{k_{\max}}\right)v^{\star}(\beta)T+v^{\star}(\beta)(k_{\max}-1).

The proof considers the agent having single-round demands with Bernoulli values with mean
\beta
and the adversary requesting
k_{\max}
rounds every round.
Unless the agent has high utility when the adversary makes the request, she has to either wait
k_{\max}
rounds before requesting the item again or request the item in a zero-valued round.
This essentially means that for large
k_{\max}
, the agent misses out on any utility until the adversary wins
T\frac{1-\alpha}{r}
rounds, after which she cannot make any more multi-round demands.
This leaves only
T(1-\frac{1-\alpha}{r})
rounds for the agent where she can only get
v^{\star}(\beta)=\beta
utility out of each in expectation.
We include the full proof in
Appendix

D
.

7
Conclusion

Recall our question from
Section

1
: Is there a
simple
mechanism for public resource sharing that is:

•

Individually robust
: Each agent receives a guaranteed utility, irrespective of how others behave.

•

Globally efficient
: Resource utilization and overall welfare are high.

•

Universal
: The guarantees hold under a wide range of settings.

We believe our results take a significant step towards these goals. What is more pleasing is that the answer does not involve a complex mechanism, but rather the well-loved
DMMF
heuristic. Extending this work to more general allocation problems is a promising avenue for future work.

To understand the above optimization problem, it is easiest to first define
\texttt{Req}(V,K)
as a Bernoulli random variable with mean
\rho_{\beta}(V,K)

If we let

\frac{\E\left[VK\rho_{\beta}(V,K)\right]}{1-\E\left[\rho_{\beta}(V,K)\right]+\E\left[K\rho_{\beta}(V,K)\right]}=\frac{\E\left[VK\;|\;\texttt{Req}(V,K)=1\right]}{\frac{1}{q}-1+\E\left[K\rho_{\beta}(V,K)\;|\;\texttt{Req}(V,K)=1\right]}

The denominator in the r.h.s. of (

These two quantities together define the expected number of rounds between the agent’s requests.
Given that the numerators of (

\beta

For every valuation
