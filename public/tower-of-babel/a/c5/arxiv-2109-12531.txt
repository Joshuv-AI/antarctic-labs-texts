Boundary controllability for a degenerate wave equation in non divergence form with drift
Idriss Boutaayamou

Abstract
We consider a degenerate wave equation with drift in presence of a leading operator which is not in divergence form. We provide some conditions for the boundary controllability for the solution of the associated Cauchy problem at a sufficiently large time.



Keywords:
degenerate equation, drift, boundary observability, null controllability

MSC: 35L10, 35L80, 93B05, 93B07, 93D15

1
Introduction

It is well know that standard linear theory for transverse waves in a string of length
L
under tension leads to the classical wave equation

\rho(x)\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial t^{2}}(t,x)=\frac{\partial\mathcal{T}}{\partial x}(t,x)\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}(t,x)+\mathcal{T}(t,x)\frac{\partial^{2}u(t,x)}{\partial x^{2}}.

Here
u(t,x)
denotes the vertical displacement of the string from the
x
axis at position
x\in(0,L)
and time
t>0
,
\rho(x)
is the mass density of the string at position
x
, while
\mathcal{T}(t,x)
denotes the tension in the string at position
x
and time
t
. Divide by
\rho(x)
, assume
\mathcal{T}
is independent of
t
, and set
a(x)=\mathcal{T}(x)\rho^{-1}(x)
,
b(x)=\mathcal{T}^{\prime}(x)\rho^{-1}(x)
. In this way, we obtain

\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial t^{2}}(t,x)=a(x)\frac{\partial^{2}u(t,x)}{\partial x^{2}}+b(x)\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}(t,x).

Now, suppose that the density is extremely large at some point, say
x=0
, then the previous equation
degenerates
at
x=0
, in the sense that we can consider
a(0)=0
, while the remainder term is a drift one.

Starting from this model, we consider the problem

\begin{cases}u_{tt}-a(x)u_{xx}-b(x)u_{x}=0,&(t,x)\in Q_{T},\\
u(t,0)=0,\quad u(t,1)=f(t),&t>0,\\
u(0,x)=u_{0}(x),\quad u_{t}(0,x)=u_{1}(x),&x\in(0,1),\end{cases}

(1.1)

where
Q_{T}=(0,T)\times(0,1)
,
T>0
. The main feature in this problem is that
a
degenerates at
x=0
, and the function
f
acts as a boundary control and it is used to drive the solution to 0 at a sufficiently large time
T>0
.

This problem has not been treated before, but its study seems natural because of the model above. Moreover, it is related to other ones: the problem without drift and with leading operator in divergence form was considered in
[
1
,
39
]
, but it is well known that problems in non divergence or in divergence form require different settings. Moreover, the drift term
bu_{x}
cannot be considered just as a small perturbation of the diffusion term
au_{xx}
(see
[
12
, Chapter 7.2]
). These three motivations are the main ones to investigate problem (
1.1
).

We will look for a control
f\in L^{2}(0,T)
, knowing that
a,b\in C^{0}[0,1]
,
a>0
on

(0,1]
and
a(0)=0
. Concerning
b
, it can possibly degenerate at
0
. Indeed, we will just require that
\displaystyle\frac{b}{a}\in L^{1}(0,1)
: hence, if
a(x)=x^{K}
,
K>0
, we can consider
b(x)=x^{h}
for any
h>K-1
. Finally, the initial data
u_{0}
and
u_{1}
belong to suitable weighted spaces.

As for the function
a
, we consider two cases, according to the following

Definition 1.1
.

A function
a
is
weakly degenerate at
0
, (WD) for short, if
a\in C^{0}[0,1]\cap C^{1}(0,1]
is such that

a(0)=0
,
a>0
on
(0,1]
and, if

\sup_{x\in(0,1]}\frac{x|a^{\prime}(x)|}{a(x)}=K,

(1.2)

then
K\in(0,1)
.

Definition 1.2
.

A function
a
is
strongly degenerate at
0
, (SD) for short, if
a\in C^{1}[0,1]
is such that

a(0)=0
,
a>0
on
(0,1]
and in
(
1.2
) we have
K\in[1,2)
.

We shall always assume that
K<2
, since, as in the parabolic case, observability (and thus null controllability) no longer holds true if
K\geq 2
, see Section
3
below.

Remark 1
.

Clearly, when
a
is strongly degenerate we cannot treat the case
b
strictly positive, since we would have
\displaystyle\frac{1}{a}\not\in L^{1}(0,1)
Hence, if
a
is strongly degenerate at
0
, then
b
must degenerate at the same point, as well.

Before going on, let us frame (
1.1
). Controllability issues for parabolic problems have been a mainstream topic in
recent years: starting from the
heat equation, related contributions have
been found in more general situations. A common strategy in showing controllability is to prove global Carleman estimates for
the operator which is the adjoint of the given one.
Such estimates for uniformly parabolic operators without degeneracies or
singularities have been largely developed (see, e.g.,
[
26
]
).
Recently, these estimates have been also studied for operators which are not
uniformly parabolic. Indeed, many problems
coming from Physics and Biology (see
[
29
]
), Biology (see
[
4
]
,
[
5
]
,
[
11
]
,
[
17
]
,
[
18
]
and
[
25
]
) or Mathematical Finance
(see
[
28
]
) are described by degenerate parabolic equations. In this framework, new Carleman estimates and null controllability properties have been established in
[
2
]
,
[
9
]
,
[
10
]
,
[
19
]
and
[
31
]
for regular degenerate coefficients, in
[
5
]
and
[
20
]
,
[
23
]
for non smooth degenerate coefficients and in
[
14
]
,
[
15
]
,
[
16
]
,
[
21
]
,
[
22
]
,
[
24
]
and in
[
38
]
for degenerate and singular coefficients.

On the contrary, null controllability for degenerate wave equations have received less attention. Let us recall that null controllability for the one dimensional
nondegenerate
wave equation can be attacked in several ways: for instance, consider

\begin{cases}u_{tt}-u_{xx}=f_{\omega}(t,x),&(t,x)\in Q_{T},\\
u(t,0)=0,\quad u(t,1)=f(t),&t\in(0,T),\\
u(0,x)=u_{0}(x)\in H^{1}_{0}(0,1),\quad u_{t}(0,x)=u_{1}(x)\in L^{2}(0,1),&x\in(0,1).\end{cases}

(1.3)

Here
u
is the state, while
f_{\omega}
and
f
are the controls: one may have
f=0
and
f_{\omega}
acting as a control localized in the subset
\omega
of
[0,1]
, or
f_{\omega}=0
and
f
acting as a boundary control. In any case, one looks for conditions in order to drive the solution to equilibrium at a given time
T
, i.e. given the initial data
(u_{0},u_{1})
in a suitable space, we look for a control (
f
or
f_{\omega}
) such that

u(T,x)=u_{t}(T,x)=0,\quad\text{ for all }x\in(0,1).

(1.4)

Some comments are in order. First, due to the finite speed of propagation of solutions of the wave equation, we cannot expect to have null controllability at any final time
T
(as in the parabolic case), but we need
T
sufficiently large: for equation (
1.3
) it is well known that null controllability holds if
T>2
, see
[
36
, Chapter 4]
. Second, the Hilbert Uniqueness Method (HUM) permits to characterize such a control in terms of minimum of a certain functional. In details, following
[
30
]
, in analogy with problem (
1.1
), consider (
1.3
) with
f_{\omega}=0
and its adjoint system

\begin{cases}\varphi_{tt}-\varphi_{xx}=0,&(t,x)\in Q_{T},\\
\varphi(t,0)=\varphi(t,1)=0,&t\in(0,T),\\
\varphi(0,x)=\varphi_{0}(x)\in H^{1}_{0}(0,1),\quad\varphi_{t}(0,x)=\varphi_{1}(x)\in L^{2}(0,1)&x\in(0,1).\end{cases}

(1.5)

Then, (
1.4
) is equivalent to the validity of the following
observability inequality

\|(\varphi_{0},\varphi_{1})\|^{2}_{H^{1}_{0}(0,1)\times L^{2}(0,1)}\leq C\int_{0}^{T}|\varphi_{x}(t,1)|^{2}\,dt,

(1.6)

where
C=C(T)>0
is a universal constant. Such an inequality guarantees the coercivity of the functional
J:H^{1}_{0}(0,1)\times L^{2}(0,1)\to{\mathbb{R}}
defined as

J(\varphi_{0},\varphi_{1})=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}|\varphi_{x}(t,1)|^{2}dt+\int_{0}^{1}\varphi_{1}u_{0}dx-\langle\varphi_{0},u_{1}\rangle_{H^{-1},H^{1}_{0}},

where
\varphi
solves (
1.5
) with initial data
(\varphi_{0},\varphi_{1})
. As a consequence,
J
has a unique minimizer
(\tilde{\varphi}_{0},\tilde{\varphi}_{1})
. Then, the control
f
which drives the solution of (
1.3
) to 0 at time
T
is given by

f(t)=\tilde{\varphi}_{x}(t.1),

where
\tilde{\varphi}
is the solution of (
1.5
) with initial data
(\tilde{\varphi}_{0},\tilde{\varphi}_{1})
. A related approach
for (
1.3
) with
f=0
is showed in
[
35
]
.

In
[
34
]
the authors investigate the problem of maximizing the observability constant, or its asymptotic average
in time, over all possible subsets
\omega
of
[0,\pi]
of Lebesgue measure
L\pi
.
In
[
33
]
the author considers the controllability problem of a semilinear wave equation with a control multiplying the nonlinear term.

To our knowledge,
[
27
]
is the first paper where a degenerate wave equation is considered: more precisely, the author considers the equation in divergence form

u_{tt}-(x^{\alpha}u_{x})_{x}=0

for
\alpha\in(0,1)
and the control acts in the degeneracy point
x=0
. Later on, in
[
1
]
the authors consider the equation

u_{tt}-(a(x)u_{x})_{x}=0

where
a\sim x^{K}
. In this case the authors establish observability
inequalities for weakly as well as strongly degenerate equations. They
also prove a negative result when the diffusion coefficient degenerates too violently (i.e. when
K\geq 2
) and
the blow-up of the observability time when
K\uparrow 2
. We remark that the observability inequality, and hence null controllability, in
[
27
]
is obtained via spectral analysis, while in
[
1
]
via suitable energy estimates.
We also mention the recent paper
[
37
]
, where the author studies (
1.3
) with two linearly moving endpoints, establishing observability results in a sharp time and deriving exact boundary controllability results.

As far as we know, this is the first paper where the equation is in non divergence form and
b\not\equiv 0
. Clearly the presence of the drift term leads us to use different spaces with respect to the ones in
[
1
]
and gives rise to some new difficulties. However, thanks to some suitable assumptions on the drift term, one can prove some estimates that are crucial to prove an observability similar to (
1.6
), namely

E_{\varphi}(0)\leq C_{T}\int_{0}^{T}|\varphi_{x}(t,1)|^{2}dt,

(1.7)

where
C_{T}
is a strictly positive universal constant. Here
E_{\varphi}
denotes the energy of
\varphi
, solution of (
1.5
), see (
3.16
) below for its precise definition.

We remark that in our situation, the non divergence form of the leading operator forces to use reference Hilbert spaces which are different from those in
[
1
]
. Indeed, the natural framework must take into account such a function, and so we are forced to introduce weighted spaces with a singular weight (
\sigma
), which gives some complications in the boundary behaviour (see Lemma
3.1
). It must be noticed that such a weight appears independently of the presence of the drift term and in fact it reduces to
a
when no drift is given.

As a consequence, the tools used in
[
1
]
cannot be simply adapted. For instance, the choice of the multipliers done therein in some cases doesn’t work without any further assumption. For this reason, in the (SD) case an additional assumption is in order, see Hypothesis
3.2
.

Moreover, the presence of the drift term introduces another complication. Indeed, without assuming any other special behaviour on the drift term
b
(such as sign or growth conditions), we cannot expect that necessarily it is an irrelevant lower order term. As a consequence, in order to prove controllability, we will need to assume a ”smallness” condition on
b
, which permits to balance the presence of the degenerate coefficient
a
(see the assumptions in Corollaries
3.1
and
3.2
).

The paper is organized as follows: in Section
2
we study well posedness of the original problem with Dirichlet boundary conditions; in Section
3
we consider the adjoint problem of (
1.1
) and we prove that for this kind of problem the associated energy is constant through the time. Thanks to this property and to some estimates established in Section
3
, we will prove in Section
4
that (
1.1
) is null controllable under suitable assumptions.

′

\dot{\ }
denotes the derivative of a function depending on the real time variable
t

y_{x}^{2}

2
Well posedness for the problem with homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions

We start assuming very low regularity conditions which will be assumed from now on, but for the functional setting the assumption below is sufficient, while more restrive ones will be needed below.

Hypothesis 2.1
.

Functions
a
and
b
are continuous in
[0,1]
with

\dfrac{b}{a}\in L^{1}(0,1)
.

Consider the degenerate hyperbolic problem
with Dirichlet boundary conditions

\begin{cases}y_{tt}-ay_{xx}-by_{x}=0,&(t,x)\in(0,+\infty)\times(0,1),\\
y(t,1)=y(t,0)=0,&t\in(0,+\infty),\\
y(0,x)=y^{0}_{T}(x),&x\in(0,1),\\
y_{t}(0,x)=y^{1}_{T}(x),&x\in(0,1).\end{cases}

(2.8)

We anticipate the fact that the choice of denoting initial data with
T-
dependence is related to the approach for null controllability used in Section
4
.

In order to study the well-posedness of (
2.8
), let us
recall the well-known absolutely continuous weight function

\eta(x):=\exp\left\{\int_{\frac{1}{2}}^{x}\frac{b(s)}{a(s)}ds\right\},\quad x\in[0,1],

introduced by Feller in a related context
[
13
]
and used by
several authors, see
[
8
]
,
[
12
]
and
[
32
]
. Since
\frac{b}{a}\in L^{1}(0,1)
, we find that
\eta\in C^{0}[0,1]\cap C^{1}(0,1]
is a strictly positive function. When
b
degenerates at 0 not slower than
a
, for instance if
a(x)=x^{K}
and
b(x)=x^{h}
,
K\leq h
, then
\eta
can be extended to a function of class
C^{1}[0,1]
. Now set

\sigma(x):=a(x)\eta^{-1}(x),

and observe that if
y
is sufficiently smooth, e.g.
y\in W^{2,1}_{\text{loc}}(0,1)
, then

Ay:=ay_{xx}+by_{x}=\sigma(\eta y_{x})_{x}.

(2.9)

As in
[
8
]
, we introduce
the following Hilbert spaces with related inner products:

L^{2}_{\frac{1}{\sigma}}(0,1):=\left\{u\in L^{2}(0,1)\;\big|\;\|u\|_{\frac{1}{\sigma}}<\infty\right\},\;\langle u,v\rangle_{\frac{1}{\sigma}}:=\int_{0}^{1}uv\frac{1}{\sigma}dx;

H^{1}_{\frac{1}{\sigma}}(0,1):=L^{2}_{\frac{1}{\sigma}}(0,1)\cap H^{1}_{0}(0,1),\;\langle u,v\rangle_{1}:=\langle u,v\rangle_{\frac{1}{\sigma}}+\int_{0}^{1}u^{\prime}v^{\prime}dx;

H^{2}_{\frac{1}{\sigma}}(0,1):=\Big\{u\in H^{1}_{\frac{1}{\sigma}}(0,1)\;\big|\;Au\in L^{2}_{\frac{1}{\sigma}}(0,1)\Big\},\;\langle u,v\rangle_{2}:=\langle u,v\rangle_{1}+\langle Au,Av\rangle_{\frac{1}{\sigma}}.

It is clear that the following integration by parts holds.

Lemma 2.1
.

If
u\in H^{2}_{{\frac{1}{\sigma}}}(0,1)
and
v\in H^{1}_{{\frac{1}{\sigma}}}(0,1)
, then

\int_{0}^{1}(\eta u^{\prime})^{\prime}v\,dx=-\int_{0}^{1}\eta u^{\prime}v^{\prime}dx.

It turns out that, under reasonable assumptions, the norm in
H^{1}_{\frac{1}{\sigma}}(0,1)
is equivalent to the standard norm

\int_{0}^{1}|u^{\prime}(x)|^{2}dx
for all
u\in H^{1}_{\frac{1}{\sigma}}(0,1)
(see Corollary
2.1
).

Hypothesis 2.2
.

Hypothesis
2.1
holds. In addition,
a\in C^{0}[0,1]
is such that
a(0)=0
,
a>0
on

(0,1]
and there exists
K\in(0,2)
such that the function

x\longmapsto\dfrac{x^{K}}{a(x)}

is nondecreasing in a right neighborhood of
x=0
.

Notice that here we don’t require any differentiability and that the monotonicity property (which holds globally in
(0,1]
if
a
is (WD) or (SD)) is valid only near
0
. Moreover, it is easy to see that the same monotonicity is inherited by the map

x\longmapsto\dfrac{x^{\gamma}}{a(x)}

for any
\gamma\geq K
.

The next result is related to a similar one given in
[
7
]
for problems without drift. The proof being similar to that one, we omit it.

Proposition 2.1

(Hardy-Poincaré Inequality)
.

Assume Hypothesis
2.2
. Then, there exists

C>0
such that

\int_{0}^{1}v^{2}\frac{1}{\sigma}dx\leq C\int_{0}^{1}(v^{\prime})^{2}dx\quad\forall\;v\in H^{1}_{\frac{1}{\sigma}}(0,1).

(2.10)

Remark 2
.

It is clear that the previous inequality is closely related to the classical Hardy inequality

\int_{0}^{1}\frac{y^{2}}{x^{2}}dx\leq 4\int_{0}^{1}y_{x}^{2}dx\mbox{ for any $y\in H^{1}_{0}(0,1)$,}

which clearly holds true, since
H^{1}_{\frac{1}{\sigma}}(0,1)\subset H^{1}_{0}(0,1).

Thanks to the previous proposition, one has that the
spaces
H^{1}_{0}(0,1)
and
H^{1}_{\frac{1}{\sigma}}(0,1)
algebraically coincide. In particular, we have the equivalence below.

Corollary 2.1
.

Assume Hypothesis
2.2
. Then the two norms

\|u\|_{1}^{2}:=\int_{0}^{1}u^{2}\frac{1}{\sigma}dx+\int_{0}^{1}(u^{\prime})^{2}dx\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\|u\|_{2}^{2}:=\int_{0}^{1}(u^{\prime})^{2}dx,

are equivalent in
H^{1}_{\frac{1}{\sigma}}(0,1)
.

Finally, we introduce the last Hilbert space

\mathcal{H}_{0}:=H^{1}_{{\frac{1}{\sigma}}}(0,1)\times L^{2}_{{\frac{1}{\sigma}}}(0,1),

endowed with the inner product

\langle(u,v),(\tilde{u},\tilde{v})\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{0}}:=\int_{0}^{1}\eta u^{\prime}\tilde{u}^{\prime}dx+\int_{0}^{1}v\tilde{v}\frac{1}{\sigma}dx

for every
(u,v),(\tilde{u},\tilde{v})\in\mathcal{H}_{0}
, which induces the norm

\|(u,v)\|_{\mathcal{H}_{0}}^{2}:=\int_{0}^{1}\eta(u^{\prime})^{2}dx+\int_{0}^{1}v^{2}\frac{1}{\sigma}dx.

Remark 3
.

Notice that the weighted norm in the gradient is equivalent to the usual one, since
\eta
is bounded above and below by positive constants. However, we introduce such a weight to make considerations straightforward in the steps below, in particular for proving that an involved operator is skew-adjoint.

We are now ready to define the domain
D(A)
of
the operator
A
given in (
2.9
) as

D(A)=H^{2}_{{\frac{1}{\sigma}}}(0,1).

Also,
H^{-1}_{{\frac{1}{\sigma}}}(0,1)
is the dual space of
H^{1}_{{\frac{1}{\sigma}}}(0,1)
with respect to the pivot space
L^{2}_{{\frac{1}{\sigma}}}(0,1)
.

In order to study the well posedness of problem (
2.8
), we introduce the matrix operator
{\mathcal{A}}:D({\mathcal{A}})\subset\mathcal{H}_{0}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}_{0}
, given by

{\mathcal{A}}:=\begin{pmatrix}0&Id\\
A&0\end{pmatrix},\quad D({\mathcal{A}}):=H^{2}_{{\frac{1}{\sigma}}}(0,1)\times H^{1}_{{\frac{1}{\sigma}}}(0,1).

In this way, rewrite (
2.8
) as the Cauchy problem

\begin{cases}\dot{\mathcal{Y}}(t)={\mathcal{A}}{\mathcal{Y}}(t),&t\geq 0,\\
{\mathcal{Y}}(0)={\mathcal{Y}}_{0},\end{cases}

(2.11)

with

{\mathcal{Y}}(t):=\begin{pmatrix}y\\
y_{t}\end{pmatrix}\;\text{ and }\;{\mathcal{Y}}_{0}:=\begin{pmatrix}y^{0}_{T}\\
y^{1}_{T}\end{pmatrix}.

Now, it is straightforward to see that for every
\begin{pmatrix}u\\
v\end{pmatrix}\in D({\mathcal{A}})
we have

\left<{\mathcal{A}}\begin{pmatrix}u\\
v\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}u\\
v\end{pmatrix}\right>_{\mathcal{H}_{0}}=0,

i.e.
{\mathcal{A}}
is a skew-adjoint operator. Moreover, as in the classical case,
I-{\mathcal{A}}:D({\mathcal{A}})\to L^{2}_{\frac{1}{\sigma}}(0,1)
is surjective. Hence, by the Lumer-Phillips Theorem,
{\mathcal{A}}
generates a contraction semigroup
(S(t))_{t\geq 0}
on
\mathcal{H}_{0}
.
Hence, if
{\mathcal{Y}}_{0}\in\mathcal{H}_{0}
then
{\mathcal{Y}}(t)=S(t){\mathcal{Y}}_{0}
is the mild solution of (
2.11
). Also, if
{\mathcal{Y}}_{0}\in D(\mathcal{A})
, then the solution is classical and the equation in (
2.8
) holds for all
t\geq 0
. Hence, as in
[
1
]
or in
[
3
, Proposition 3.15]
, one has

Theorem 2.1
.

Assume Hypothesis
2.2
.
If
(y^{0}_{T},y^{1}_{T})\in\mathcal{H}_{0}
, then there exists a unique mild solution

y\in C^{1}([0,+\infty);L^{2}_{\frac{1}{\sigma}}(0,1))\cap C([0,+\infty);H^{1}_{\frac{1}{\sigma}}(0,1))

of (
2.8
) which depends continuously on the initial data
(y^{0}_{T},y^{1}_{T})\in\mathcal{H}_{0}.

Moreover, if
(y^{0}_{T},y^{1}_{T})\in D(\mathcal{A})
, then the solution
y
is classical, in the sense that

y\in C^{2}([0,+\infty);L^{2}_{\frac{1}{\sigma}}(0,1))\cap C^{1}([0,+\infty);H^{1}_{\frac{1}{\sigma}}(0,1))\cap C([0,+\infty);H^{2}_{\frac{1}{\sigma}}(0,1))

and the equation of (
2.8
) holds for all
t\geq 0
.

Remark 4
.

Due to the reversibility in time of the equation, solutions exist with the same regularity for
t<0
(indeed,
{\mathcal{A}}
is skew-adjoint and it generates a
C_{0}
group of unitary operators on
\mathcal{H}_{0}
by Stone’s Theorem). This will be used in Section
4
.

Recall that if
a
is (WD) or (SD), then (
1.2
) implies that the function

x\mapsto\frac{x^{\gamma}}{a(x)}

(2.12)

is nondecreasing in
(0,1]
for all
\gamma\geq K
. In particular, Hypothesis
2.2
holds. Moreover,

\lim_{x\rightarrow 0}\frac{x^{\gamma}}{a(x)}=0

(2.13)

for all
\gamma>K
and

\left|\frac{x^{\gamma}b(x)}{a(x)}\right|\leq\frac{1}{a(1)}\|b\|_{L^{\infty}(0,1)}

(2.14)

for all
\gamma\geq K
. Let

M:=\displaystyle\frac{\|b\|_{L^{\infty}(0,1)}}{a(1)}.

(2.15)

These facts will play a crucial role in the following sections.

3
Energy estimates and boundary observability

In this section we prove estimates of the energy associated to the solution of the intial value problem both from below and from above. The former will be used in the next section to prove a controllability result, while the latter is used here to prove a boundary observability inequality.

Let
y
be a mild solution of (
2.8
) and consider its energy

E_{y}(t)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\left(\frac{1}{\sigma}y_{t}^{2}(t,x)+\eta y_{x}^{2}(t,x)\right)dx,\quad\forall\;t\geq 0.

(3.16)

With the definition above, the classical conservation of the energy still holds true also in the degenerate case with drift:

Theorem 3.1
.

Assume Hypothesis
2.1
and let
y
be a mild solution of (
2.8
). Then

E_{y}(t)=E_{y}(0),\quad\forall\;t\geq 0.

(3.17)

Proof.

First of all suppose that
y
is a classical solution. Then multiply the equation by
\displaystyle\frac{y_{t}}{\sigma}
(recall that
y_{t}\in H^{1}_{\frac{1}{\sigma}}(0,1)
by Theorem
2.1
), integrate over
(0,1)
and use the boundary conditions to get

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{y^{2}_{t}}{\sigma}\right)dx-[\eta y_{x}y_{t}]_{x=0}^{x=1}+\int_{0}^{1}\eta y_{x}y_{tx}dx

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{y^{2}_{t}}{\sigma}+\eta y^{2}_{x}\right)dx=\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}E_{y}(t),

since
y\in C^{2}([0,+\infty);L^{2}_{\frac{1}{\sigma}}(0,1))
and we can differentiate under the sign integral.

If

is a mild solution, we approximate the initial data with more regular ones, obtaining associated classical solutions (for which (

∎

For the rest of the paper the following lemma will be crucial.

Lemma 3.1
.

1.

Assume Hypothesis
2.1
. If
y\in D(A)
and
u\in H^{1}_{\frac{1}{\sigma}}(0,1)
, then

\displaystyle\lim_{x\rightarrow 0}u(x)y^{\prime}(x)=0.

2.

Assume Hypothesis
2.1
. If
u\in D(A)
, then
\displaystyle\lim_{x\rightarrow 0}x^{2}(u^{\prime}(x))^{2}=0.

3.

Assume Hypothesis
2.2
. If
u\in D(A)
and
K\leq 1
, then
\displaystyle\lim_{x\rightarrow 0}x(u^{\prime}(x))^{2}=0
.

4.

Assume Hypothesis
2.2
. If
u\in D(A)
,
K>1
and
\displaystyle\frac{xb}{a}\in L^{\infty}(0,1),
then
\displaystyle\lim_{x\rightarrow 0}x(u^{\prime}(x))^{2}=0
.

5.

Assume Hypothesis
2.2
. If
u\in H^{1}_{\frac{1}{\sigma}}(0,1)
, then
\displaystyle\lim_{x\rightarrow 0}\frac{x}{a}u^{2}(x)=0.

Proof.

1. Consider the function

z(x):=\eta(x)u(x)y^{\prime}(x),\quad x\in(0,1].

Observe that

\int_{0}^{1}|z|dx\leq C\|u\|_{L^{2}(0,1)}\|y^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}(0,1)}.

Moreover

z^{\prime}(x)=(\eta y^{\prime})^{\prime}u+\eta y^{\prime}u^{\prime}

and, by Hölder’s inequality,

\int_{0}^{1}|(\eta y^{\prime})^{\prime}u|dx=\int_{0}^{1}\sqrt{\sigma}|(\eta y^{\prime})^{\prime}|\frac{|u|}{\sqrt{\sigma}}dx\leq\|\sigma(\eta y^{\prime})^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}_{\frac{1}{\sigma}}(0,1)}\|u\|_{L^{2}_{\frac{1}{\sigma}}(0,1)}

and

\int_{0}^{1}\eta|y^{\prime}u^{\prime}|dx\leq C\|y^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}(0,1)}\|u^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}(0,1)}.

Thus
z^{\prime}
is summable on
[0,1]
. Hence
z\in W^{1,1}(0,1)\hookrightarrow C[0,1]
and there exists

\lim_{x\rightarrow 0}z(x)=L\in{\mathbb{R}}.

If
L\neq 0
there would exist a neighborhood
\mathcal{I}
of
0
such that

\frac{|L|}{2}\leq|\eta y^{\prime}u|,

for all
x\in\mathcal{I}
;
but, by Hölder’s inequality,

|u(x)|\leq\int_{0}^{x}|u^{\prime}(t)|dt\leq\sqrt{x}\|u^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}(0,1)}.

Hence

\frac{|L|}{2}\leq|\eta y^{\prime}u|\leq\|\eta\|_{\infty}|y^{\prime}|\sqrt{x}\|u^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}(0,1)}

for all
x\in\mathcal{I}
. This would imply that

|y^{\prime}|\geq\frac{|L|}{2\|u^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}(0,1)}\|\eta\|_{\infty}\sqrt{x}}

in contrast to the fact that
y^{\prime}\in L^{2}(0,1)
. Hence
L=0
and the conclusion follows since
\eta
is bounded below by a positive constant.

2. It is a straightforward consequence of the previous point. Indeed, it is enough to choose any function
u\in H^{1}_{\frac{1}{\sigma}}(0,1)
such that
u(x)=x
in a right neighborhood of 0, and squaring.

3.
Consider the function
z:=x\eta(x)(u^{\prime}(x))^{2}
. We have

z^{\prime}=\eta(u^{\prime})^{2}+x\eta^{\prime}(u^{\prime})^{2}+2x\eta u^{\prime}u^{\prime\prime}=\eta(u^{\prime})^{2}+2xu^{\prime}(\eta u^{\prime})^{\prime}-x\eta^{\prime}(u^{\prime})^{2}.

Proceeding as before one has that
x\eta^{\prime}(u^{\prime})^{2}
and
2xu^{\prime}(\eta u^{\prime})^{\prime}
belong to
L^{1}(0,1)
since
K\leq 1
. Hence
z\in W^{1,1}(0,1)
and
\lim_{x\to 0}z(x)=0.

4. Proceed as above, observing that

\left|x\eta^{\prime}(u^{\prime})^{2}\right|=\left|x\eta\frac{b}{a}(u^{\prime})^{2}\right|\leq\left\|\frac{xb}{a}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(0,1)}\left\|\eta\right\|_{L^{\infty}(0,1)}(u^{\prime})^{2}

and that

|xu^{\prime}(\eta u^{\prime})^{\prime}|=\left|\frac{x}{\sqrt{a}}\sqrt{\eta}u^{\prime}\right|\cdot\left|\sqrt{\sigma}(\eta u^{\prime})^{\prime}\right|.

5. If
K\leq 1
, the proof is straightforward, since by Hypothesis
2.2
we get that
\frac{x}{a}
is bounded. In the general case, set
\displaystyle z:=\frac{x}{a}u^{2}(x)
. Then
z\in L^{1}(0,1)
. Indeed

\int_{0}^{1}\frac{x}{a}u^{2}(x)dx\leq\frac{1}{\min_{[0,1]}\eta}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{u^{2}}{\sigma}dx.

Moreover

z^{\prime}=\frac{u^{2}}{a}+2\frac{xuu^{\prime}}{a}-\frac{a^{\prime}x}{a^{2}}u^{2};

thus, for a suitable
\varepsilon>0
given by Hypothesis
2.2
,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\varepsilon}|z^{\prime}|dx

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\min_{[0,1]}\eta}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{u^{2}}{\sigma}dx+2\left(\int_{0}^{\varepsilon}\frac{x^{2}(u^{\prime})^{2}}{a}dx\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\int_{0}^{1}\frac{u^{2}}{a}dx\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}+K\int_{0}^{1}\frac{u^{2}}{a}dx

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1+K}{\min_{[0,1]}\eta}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{u^{2}}{\sigma}dx+\frac{2\varepsilon}{\sqrt{a(\varepsilon)}\min_{[0,1]}\eta}\left(\int_{0}^{1}(u^{\prime})^{2}dx\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\int_{0}^{1}\frac{u^{2}}{\sigma}dx\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

This is enough to conclude that
z\in W^{1,1}(0,1)
and thus there exists
\lim_{x\to 0}z(x)=L\in{\mathbb{R}}
. If
L\neq 0
, sufficiently close to
x=0
we would have that

\frac{u^{2}(x)}{a}\geq\frac{|L|}{2x}\not\in L^{1}(0,1),

while
\displaystyle\frac{u^{2}}{a}\in L^{1}(0,1)
, since
\displaystyle\frac{u^{2}}{\sigma}\in L^{1}(0,1)
.
∎

\displaystyle\frac{K}{2}\left|\int_{0}^{1}\left[\frac{yy_{t}}{\sigma}\right]_{t=0}^{t=T}dx\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{K}{4}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{y_{t}^{2}}{\sigma}(T,x)dx+\frac{K}{4}\frac{\max_{[0,1]}\eta}{a(1)}\int_{0}^{1}\left(\frac{y^{2}}{x^{2}}\right)(T,x)dx

\displaystyle+\frac{K}{4}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{y_{t}^{2}}{\sigma}(0,x)dx+\frac{K}{4}\frac{\max_{[0,1]}\eta}{a(1)}\int_{0}^{1}\left(\frac{y^{2}}{x^{2}}\right)(0,x)dx

\displaystyle\leq\frac{K}{4}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{y_{t}^{2}}{\sigma}(T,x)dx+\frac{K}{4}\frac{4\max_{[0,1]}\eta}{a(1)\min_{[0,1]}\eta}\int_{0}^{1}\eta y_{x}^{2}(T,x)dx

\displaystyle+\frac{K}{4}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{y_{t}^{2}}{\sigma}(0,x)dx+\frac{K}{4}\frac{4\max_{[0,1]}\eta}{a(1)\min_{[0,1]}\eta}\int_{0}^{1}\eta y_{x}^{2}(0,x)dx

Hence

\eta(1)\int_{0}^{T}y_{x}^{2}(t,1)dt\geq\left(T(2-K-2M)-{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}8}\max\left\{1,\frac{1}{a(1)},\frac{K\max_{[0,1]}\eta}{a(1)\min_{[0,1]}\eta}\right\}\right)E_{y}(0).

Thus the conclusion holds true if

Now, if

3.2

Now, we consider the case in which

Hypothesis 3.2

Remark 7

Observe that in this case the condition

. Indeed, if

in a right neighborhood of 0, while

(see

). For instance, for the prototypes

In order to get an estimate similar to the one proved in Theorem

, namely an observability inequality, we introduce the following constant:

M_{\infty}:=\left\|\frac{xb}{a}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(0,1)}.

(3.31)

By requiring
\frac{xb}{a}\in L^{\infty}(0,1)
, all the calculations made in the proofs of Theorems
3.3
and

can be reformulated verbatim, substituting

with

. For instance, with the new assumption, in the proof of the analogous of Theorem
3.3
one can multiply again the equation by
\dfrac{xy_{x}}{\sigma}
and all integrations by parts are justified. For instance, since

is a classical solution, we know that
\sigma(\eta y_{x})_{x}\in L^{1}_{\frac{1}{\sigma}(0,1)}
, so that

\int_{Q_{T}}(\eta^{\prime}y_{x}+\eta y_{x}x)xy_{x}dxdt\in{\mathbb{R}}.

But

, and thus

is integrable under Hypothesis
3.2
. As a consequence,

is integrable, as well. Moreover, identity (

) is valid by using Lemma
3.1
.4, the subsequent integration by parts are equally justified, and the proofs still hold true.

In particular, in the (SD) case Theorem
3.4
re-writes as

Theorem 3.5
.

Assume Hypothesis
3.2
.Then, for any

Following

then (

) is observable in time

. Moreover

Observe that the assumption

is clearly satisfied if

. Indeed in this case

and

.

The analogous statement of Corollary

in the strongly degenerate case becomes

.

Assume Hypothesis

2.8
) is observable in time

\frac{1}{\eta(1)}\left(T(2-K-2M_{\infty})-8\max\left\{1,\frac{1}{a(1)},\frac{K\max_{[0,1]}\eta}{a(1)\min_{[0,1]}\eta}\right\}\right)\leq C_{T}.

As announced in the introduction, we now prove that boundary observability is no longer true when

(recall that

is the constant that appears in (

. The examples are based on those given in

Example 1

Let

and consider the equation

Then, if

4
Null controllability

In this section we study the problem of null controllability for (
1.1
). More precisely, given
(u_{0},u_{1})\in L^{2}_{{\frac{1}{\sigma}}}(0,1)\times H^{-1}_{\frac{1}{\sigma}}(0,1)
, we look for a control
f\in L^{2}(0,T)
such that the solution of (
1.1
) satisfies (
1.4
).

First of all, we give the definition of a solution for (
1.1
)
by transposition
, which permits low regularity on the notion of solution itself: such a definition is formally obtained by re-writing the equation as
u_{tt}-\sigma(\eta u_{x})_{x}
thanks to (
2.9
), multiplying by
\frac{v}{\sigma}
and integrating by parts. Precisely:

Definition 4.1
.

Let
f\in L^{2}_{\text{loc}}[0,+\infty)
and
(u_{0},u_{1})\in L^{2}_{{\frac{1}{\sigma}}}(0,1)\times H^{-1}_{{\frac{1}{\sigma}}}(0,1)
. We say that
u
is a solution by transposition of (
1.1
) if

u\in C^{1}([0,+\infty);H^{-1}_{{\frac{1}{\sigma}}}(0,1))\cap C([0,+\infty);L^{2}_{{\frac{1}{\sigma}}}(0,1))

and for all
T>0

\displaystyle\langle u_{t}(T),v^{0}_{T}\rangle_{H^{-1}_{{\frac{1}{\sigma}}}(0,1),H^{1}_{{\frac{1}{\sigma}}}(0,1)}

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{\sigma}u(T)v^{1}_{T}dx=\langle u_{1},v(0)\rangle_{H^{-1}_{{\frac{1}{\sigma}}}(0,1),H^{1}_{{\frac{1}{\sigma}}}(0,1)}

(4.37)

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{\sigma}u_{0}v_{t}(0,x)dx-\eta(1)\int_{0}^{T}f(t)v_{x}(t,1)dt

for all
(v^{0}_{T},v^{1}_{T})\in H^{1}_{{\frac{1}{\sigma}}}(0,1)\times L^{2}_{{\frac{1}{\sigma}}}(0,1)
, where
v
solves the backward problem

\begin{cases}v_{tt}-av_{xx}-bv_{x}=0,&(t,x)\in(0,+\infty)\times(0,1),\\
v(t,1)=v(t,0)=0,&t\in(0,+\infty),\\
v(T,x)=v^{0}_{T}(x),&x\in(0,1),\\
v_{t}(T,x)=v^{1}_{T}(x),&x\in(0,1).\end{cases}

(4.38)

Setting
y(t,x):=v(T-t,x)
, one has that
y
satisfies
(
2.8
) with
y^{0}_{T}(x)=v^{0}_{T}(x)
and
y^{1}_{T}(x)=-v^{1}_{T}(x)
. Hence, thanks to Theorem
2.1
(and Remark

) admits a unique solution

v\in C^{1}([0,+\infty);L^{2}_{\frac{1}{\sigma}}(0,1))\cap C([0,+\infty);H^{1}_{\frac{1}{\sigma}}(0,1))

which depends continuously on the initial data
V_{T}:=(v^{0}_{T},v^{1}_{T})\in\mathcal{H}_{0}
.

By Theorem
3.1
, the energy is preserved in our setting, as well, so that the method of transposition done in
[
1
]
continues to hold thanks to (
3.19
), and so there exists a unique solution by transposition
u\in C^{1}([0,+\infty);H^{-1}_{{\frac{1}{\sigma}}}(0,1))\cap C([0,+\infty);L^{2}_{{\frac{1}{\sigma}}}(0,1))
of (
1.1
), namely a solution of (
4.37
).

Now, we are ready to pass to null controllability, recalling that by linearity and reversibility of equation (
1.1
), null controllability for any initial data
(u_{0},u_{1})
is equivalent to exact controllability, see
[
1
]
. In order to prove that (
1.1
) is null controllable, let us start with

Hypothesis 4.1
.

Assume

•

Hypothesis
3.1
with
K<2-2M
and (
3.34
), or

•

Hypothesis
3.2
with
K<2-2M_{\infty}
and (

Now, consider the bilinear form
\Lambda:\mathcal{H}_{0}\times\mathcal{H}_{0}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
defined as

\Lambda(V_{T},W_{T}):=\eta(1)\int_{0}^{T}v_{x}(t,1)w_{x}(t,1)dt,

where
v
and
w
are the solutions of (
4.38
) associated to the final data
V_{T}:=(v^{0}_{T},v^{1}_{T})
and
W_{T}:=(w^{0}_{T},w^{1}_{T})
, respectively. The following lemma holds.

Lemma 4.1
.

Assume Hypothesis
4.1
. Then, the bilinear form
\Lambda
is continuous and coercive.

Proof.

By Theorem
3.1
,
E_{v}
and
E_{w}
are constant in time and thanks to (
3.19
), one has that
\Lambda
is continuous. Indeed, by Hölder’s inequality, Theorem
3.2
, we get

\displaystyle|\Lambda(V_{T},W_{T})|

\displaystyle\leq\eta(1)\int_{0}^{T}\left|v_{x}(t,1)w_{x}(t,1)\right|dt

\displaystyle\leq\left(\eta(1)\int_{0}^{T}v_{x}^{2}(t,1)dt\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\eta(1)\int_{0}^{T}w_{x}^{2}(t,1)dt\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq CE^{\frac{1}{2}}_{v}(T)E^{\frac{1}{2}}_{w}(T)

\displaystyle=C\left(\int_{0}^{1}\frac{(v^{1}_{T})^{2}(x)}{\sigma}dx+\int_{0}^{1}\eta v_{x}^{2}(T,x)dx\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\times

\displaystyle\left(\int_{0}^{1}\frac{(w^{1}_{T})^{2}(x)}{\sigma}dx+\int_{0}^{1}\eta w_{x}^{2}(T,x)dx\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=C\|(v(T),v_{t}(T))\|_{\mathcal{H}_{0}}\|(w(T),w_{t}(T))\|_{\mathcal{H}_{0}}=C\|V_{T}\|_{\mathcal{H}_{0}}\|W_{T}\|_{\mathcal{H}_{0}}

for a positive constant
C
independent of
(V_{T},W_{T})\in\mathcal{H}_{0}\times\mathcal{H}_{0}
.

In a similar way, one can prove that
\Lambda
is coercive. Indeed, by Theorem
3.4
or
3.5
, for all
V_{T}\in\mathcal{H}_{0}
, one immediately has

\displaystyle\Lambda(V_{T},V_{T})=\int_{0}^{T}\eta(1)v_{x}^{2}(t,1)dt\geq C_{T}E_{v}(0)=C_{T}E_{v}(T)\geq C\|V_{T}\|_{\mathcal{H}_{0}}^{2},

for a positive constant
C
.
∎

Function

is used to prove the null controllability for the original problem (

as the lower bound found in Corollaries

Theorem 4.1
.

Assume Hypothesis

\displaystyle=\langle u_{1},w(0)\rangle_{H^{-1}_{{\frac{1}{\sigma}}}(0,1),H^{1}_{{\frac{1}{\sigma}}}(0,1)}-\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{\sigma}u_{0}w_{t}(0,x)dx
