VC dimensions of group convolutional neural networks
Philipp Christian Petersen, Anna Sepliarskaia

Abstract
We study the generalization capacity of group convolutional neural networks. We identify precise estimates for the VC dimensions of simple sets of group convolutional neural networks. In particular, we find that for infinite groups and appropriately chosen convolutional kernels, already two-parameter families of convolutional neural networks have an infinite VC dimension, despite being invariant to the action of an infinite group.



Keywords:
Convolutional neural networks, group convolutional neural networks, sample complexity, generalization, VC dimension

Mathematics Subject Classification:
68T07, 68Q32, 68T05

1
Introduction

Due to impressive results in image recognition, convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have become one of the most widely-used neural network architectures
[
12
,
13
]
.
It is believed that one of the main reasons for the efficiency of CNNs is their ability to convert translation symmetry of the data into a built-in translation-equivariance property of the neural network without exhausting the data to learn the equivariance
[
4
,
15
]
.
Based on this intuition, other data symmetries have recently been incorporated into neural network architectures.
Group convolutional neural networks (G-CNNs) are a natural generalization of CNNs that can be equivariant with respect to rotation
[
5
,
24
,
23
,
9
]
, scale
[
21
,
20
,
1
]
, and other symmetries defined by matrix groups
[
7
]
.
Moreover, every neural network that is equivariant to the action of a group on its input is a G-CNN, where the convolutions are with respect to the group,
[
11
]
(see Theorem
2.10
below).

Although one of the main reasons for constructing equivariant neural networks is their ability to generalize better than neural networks without built-in symmetries, the theoretical understanding of this phenomenon still needs to be better developed. Here, we say that a neural network generalizes if it achieves comparable performance on unseen data compared to the performance on the training data.

The most studied direction in the analysis of equivariant and invariant models is the analysis of sample complexity.
Several results show that the sample complexity of learning problems is improved if the objective function and the learned algorithm maintain symmetry.
For example, sample complexity is improved by a factor equal to the group size when using an invariant kernel over the group, compared to the corresponding non-invariant kernel
[
3
]
.
In
[
18
]
, it was observed that a larger volume of a group leads to a smaller generalization error.
In addition,
[
6
]
analyzes sample complexity by calculating a covering number of the input set and concluding that the sample complexity is much smaller for a neural network with invariances because, in general, the covering number of the orbit space representatives is much smaller than the covering number of the original input set.

Overall, the previous work suggests that assuming more invariances of a model implies better generalization behavior.

This work can be considered a counter-point to that intuition in the context of G-CNNs.

Our main contributions are the lower and upper bounds of the VC dimension for a simple two-layer neural network consisting of one convolutional layer and one pooling layer.

If one fixes the convolutional kernel, then
the set of associated G-CNNs has only two-free parameters which stem from the involved bias parameters.
Nonetheless, we found that in the case of an infinite group, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, there is a fixed convolution kernel such that the VC dimension of the proposed elementary neural network is at least
n
.
More precisely, when the symmetry group has size
n\in\mathbb{N}
, then there is a convolutional kernel such that the VC dimension of the proposed neural network is at least
\log_{2}(n)-2\log_{2}(\log_{2}(n))-4
. The details are given in Theorems
3.5
and Theorem
3.6
as well as in Corollary
3.7
.
The VC dimension of the neural networks with the identified kernel almost matches an associated upper bound, which is
\log_{2}(n)+9\log_{2}(\log_{2}(n))
(see Corollary
3.4
).

Our result allows a significant, potentially counter-intuitive conclusion. While a larger group implies more invariances of the associated group-invariant classifiers, it nonetheless yields a higher VC dimension for an appropriately chosen kernel.

This paper is organized as follows: In Section
2
, we introduce the model for group convolutional neural networks that will be analyzed in the rest of the work. Thereafter, in Section
3
, we present our main results. First, Theorem
3.1
yields an upper bound for the VC dimension of a G-CNN with a finite group. Second, in Theorem
3.5
, we demonstrate an associated lower bound for a carefully chosen kernel
\mathcal{K}
.

2
Group convolutional neural networks

2.1
Preliminaries

This paper studies the relationship between the symmetry group and the generalization capabilities of associated group convolutional neural networks.
First, we formally introduce all necessary concepts. We start with the definition of the type of group used in this work.
This group later encodes the symmetries of the associated group convolutional neural networks. To stress this point, we will henceforth often call it symmetry group.

Definition 2.1

(Symmetry group)
.

A
group

(G,\cdot)
is a set with an operation that satisfies the following three properties. First
associativity
holds:

\displaystyle g_{1}\cdot(g_{2}\cdot g_{3})=(g_{1}\cdot g_{2})\cdot g_{3},\ \text{ for all }g_{1},g_{2},g_{3}\in G.

Second, there exists an
identity element

e\in G
such that

\displaystyle g\cdot e=e\cdot g=g,\ \text{ for all }g\in G.

Third, for all
g\in G
there exists a unique
inverse

g^{-1}\in G
such that

\displaystyle g^{-1}\cdot g=g\cdot g^{-1}=e.

Finally, a group is a
topological group
if it is equipped with a topology such that multiplication with an element and inversion of elements are continuous operations. We will assume all groups in the sequel to be topological groups. Moreover, we assume the topology to be Hausdorff and first-countable.

In the sequel, groups act on certain sets, and we expect the group convolutional neural networks to interact appropriately with the corresponding action. To formalize this, we proceed by defining an action.

Definition 2.2

(Action of a symmetry group)
.

Let
G
be a symmetry group. An
action
of
G
on a set
\mathcal{X}
is a map
a\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G\times\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{X}
such that for all
g_{1},g_{2}\in G
and for all
x\in\mathcal{X}

\displaystyle a(g_{1}\cdot g_{2},x)=a(g_{1},a(g_{2},x)).

We are interested in neural networks that are invariant to specific group actions.
This means that the output of a neural network does not change if a group action is applied to the input.
The standard architecture of an invariant neural network consists of two parts: First, a so-called equivariant neural network, and second, a pooling operation.
To clarify this concept, we first recall equivariant maps.

Definition 2.3

(Equivariant map)
.

Let
\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}
be sets, let
G
be a group, and
a_{X}
,
a_{Y}
be group actions on
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
, respectively.
A map
f\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{Y}
is
equivariant
to the actions
a_{X},a_{Y}
if for all
g\in G,x\in\mathcal{X}
it holds that

\displaystyle f(a_{X}(g,x))=a_{Y}(g,f(x)).

Definition
2.3
requires that the group transformations commute with the application of
f
. In other words, transforming the input before the application of
f
is equivalent to transforming the output after the application of
f
.

Equivariant neural networks define parametric families of equivariant maps by composing layers that are individually equivariant with respect to the same group.

Definition 2.4

(Equivariant neural network
[
14
]
)
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and
\mathcal{X}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{X}_{n}
be sets. Let
G
be a group, and let
a_{j}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G\times\mathcal{X}_{j}\rightarrow\mathcal{X}_{j}
for
j=1,\dots,n
be actions of
G
.
An
equivariant neural network
with respect to the actions
(a_{j})_{j=1}^{n}
is a function
f\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{X}_{1}\rightarrow\mathcal{X}_{n}

which can be described as the composition of linear maps

f_{i}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{X}_{i}\rightarrow\mathcal{X}_{i+1}
that are equivariant with respect to the actions
a_{i},a_{i+1}
, and coordinate-wise nonlinearity
\rho_{i}\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R},\ \rho_{i}(x)=\rho(x+b_{i})
, for
b_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
:

\displaystyle f(x)=\rho_{n}\circ f_{n}\circ\rho_{n-1}\circ f_{n-1}\cdots\rho_{1}\circ f_{1}(x),\text{ for }x\in\mathcal{X}_{1}.

In this definition, for
i\in[n]
, the function
f_{i}
is called the
i
-th layer of the neural network
f
.

For comparison, we also recall the classical notion of feed-forward neural networks.

Definition 2.5

(Feed-forward neural network)
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
\mathcal{X}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{X}_{n}
be sets.
A
feed-forward neural network
is a function
f\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{X}_{1}\rightarrow\mathcal{X}_{n}

which can be described as the composition of affine maps
f_{i}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{X}_{i}\rightarrow\mathcal{X}_{i+1}
and coordinate-wise nonlinearity
\rho\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
:

\displaystyle f(x)=\rho\circ f_{n}\circ\rho\circ f_{n-1}\cdots\rho\circ f_{1}(x),\text{ for }x\in\mathcal{X}_{1}.

In this definition, for
i\in[n]
, the function
f_{i}
is called the
i
-th layer of the neural network
f
.

Equivalently to Definition
2.4
, we can define an equivariant neural network as a feed-forward neural network in which each layer is an equivariant function.
Moreover, it is convenient to describe equivariant neural networks through generalized convolutions.
To this end, we introduce the Haar measure on a compact group.

Theorem 2.6

(
[
17
, Theorem 8.1.2]
)
.

Let
G
be a compact group. Then, there is a left-invariant finite Borel measure, i.e., a finite Borel measure
\mu
, such that
\mu(A)=\mu(g\cdot A)
for any measurable set
A\subset G
. This measure is called a Haar measure. The Haar measure is unique up to scaling.

Remark 2.7
.

Every finite topological group is compact. Hence, there exists a Haar measure on every finite group. A Haar measure on the finite group is its counting measure. Since scaling does not affect the results in the rest of the manuscript, we will always choose the counting measure as the Haar measure on a finite group.

On compact groups, we can define the generalized convolution.
We will typically use the Haar measure as a measure in the convolution. However, in a couple of special cases later, we require a bit more generality, which is why we state the definition for general finite measures.

Definition 2.8

(Generalized convolution)
.

Let
G
be a compact group, let
\mu
be a finite measure on
G
, and let
f_{1},f_{2}\colon G\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be functions.

Then, the convolution of
f_{1}
with
f_{2}
is defined by

\displaystyle(f_{1}*_{G}f_{2})(g)=\int_{G}f_{1}(g\cdot h^{-1})\cdot f_{2}(h)d\mu(h)\quad\text{ for }g\in G.

Remark 2.9
.

For the generalized convolution to be sensible, we need for every
g\in G
, the integral
\int_{G}f_{1}(g\cdot h^{-1})\cdot f_{2}(h)d\mu(h)
to be well defined. This is guaranteed, for example, if
f_{1},f_{2}
are both
\mu
-measurable and bounded, which we will assume in the sequel.

Theorem 2.10

(
[
11
]
)
.

A feed-forward neural network
f
is equivariant to the action of a compact group
G
on its inputs if and only if each layer of
f
implements a generalized form of convolution with respect to
G
.

Thanks to Theorem
2.10
, each equivariant neural network is based on repeated generalized convolutions and hence can also be called
Group Convolutional Neural Network (G-CNN)
.
The standard way of designing an invariant classifier or regressor corresponding to an action
a\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G\times\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{X}
using neural networks is to compose an equivariant neural network with a global pooling operation
[
11
,
10
,
2
]
.
Concretely, if
f\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is an equivariant neural network, then we define
\widetilde{f}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
as

\displaystyle\widetilde{f}(x)=\int_{G}f(a(g,x))d\mu(g),\text{ for }x\in X.

To illustrate the concepts described above we give an example.

Example 2.11
.

Let
\mathcal{X}\coloneqq\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, which is a homogeneous space under the standard action of the group of integer translations:

\displaystyle T((t_{1},t_{2}),(x_{1},x_{2}))=(x_{1}+t_{1},x_{2}+t_{2}).

An image, such as an image of a handwritten digit, can be thought of as a function on
\mathcal{X}
that, given coordinates, returns the pixel value at the corresponding location:
\mathcal{I}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
.
The standard action of the group of integer translations can be extended to the action on images by

\displaystyle T((t_{1},t_{2}),\mathcal{I})(x_{1},x_{2})=\mathcal{I}(x_{1}-t_{1},x_{2}-t_{2}).

The generalized convolution, in this case, is a standard convolution layer, and the classifier, which is invariant to the action of
T
, can be designed by first having
n
convolution layers and then composing it with an averaging pooling operator.

Similar examples can be constructed with respect to group actions
of
compact groups
incorporating rotations, shearings, or general affine transformations.

2.2
Introduction of the G-CNN model

In this paper, we consider the situation already encountered in Example
2.11
, where the input space for a neural network is a subset of functions on a homogeneous space
\mathcal{X}
.
As mentioned in
[
11
]
in this case, after fixing an origin
y\in\mathcal{X}
, we can map a
function
f\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
to a function
\bar{f}\colon G\to\mathbb{R}
, by

\displaystyle\bar{f}(g)

\displaystyle\coloneqq f(a(g,y))\text{ for }g\in G.

(1)

Now
\bar{f}
can be convolved with a kernel
\mathcal{K}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G\to\mathbb{R}
if both
\bar{f}
and
\mathcal{K}
are measurable and bounded. Clearly,
\bar{f}
is bounded if
f
was. Hence, for convenience, we define

\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X},\mu)\coloneqq\{f\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}\colon f\text{ bounded and }\bar{f}\text{ is }\mu-\text{measurable}\},

Now the generalized convolution between a function
f\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X},\mu)
and a kernel
\mathcal{K}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G\to\mathbb{R}
can be defined as

\displaystyle f*_{G}\mathcal{K}\coloneqq\bar{f}*_{G}\mathcal{K}.

(2)

In this work, we study the generalization capabilities of invariant classifiers with architecture described in the previous subsection.
These classifiers are a composition of a G-CNN and a pooling operation, followed by the application of a sign function before the output.
Typically, the hypothesis class of this type of classifier consists of a neural network with several layers, where each layer is a generalized convolution with a kernel taken from a linear space with a predetermined basis.
The learning procedure selects suitable kernels by finding their coordinates in a given basis, i.e., the coordinates are the learnable parameters of the training algorithm.
In our analysis, we consider neural networks that have the fewest training parameters, i.e., neural networks with only one convolutional layer and with a fixed kernel.
We state the corresponding definition below.

Definition 2.12

(G-CNNs with fixed kernel)
.

Let
G
be a compact group acting on
\mathcal{X}
. Let
\mathcal{K}\colon G\to\mathbb{R}
be a bounded kernel. Let
\mu
be the Haar measure on
G
. Let
c_{1},c_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
and set

\displaystyle H_{c_{1},c_{2}}(\mathcal{K})\colon\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X},\mu)

\displaystyle\to\{-1,1\}

\displaystyle H_{c_{1},c_{2}}(\mathcal{K})(f)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{sign}\left(\int_{G}\mathrm{ReLU}((f*_{G}\mathcal{K})(g)+c_{1})d\mu(g)+c_{2}\right).

(3)

Here
\mathrm{sign}=2\mathds{1}_{(0,\infty)}-1
.
We denote the
set of G-CNNs with kernel
\mathcal{K}
by
\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{K})\coloneqq\{H_{c_{1},c_{2}}(\mathcal{K})\colon c_{1},c_{2}\in\mathbb{R}\}
.

We note that the set
\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{K})
has only two scalar parameters since the kernel
\mathcal{K}
is fixed for the whole class.
Even though this neural network contains only two scalar parameters and, in addition, is constrained to be invariant, we will demonstrate in the next section that when the group
G
contains an infinite number of elements, there exists, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, a convolution kernel
\mathcal{K}
such that the VC dimension of
\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{K})
is at least
n
.
By the fundamental theorem of learning (
[
16
, Theorem 3.20]
or
[
19
, Theorem 6.7]
), this implies that successful learning of these neural networks from finitely many samples is impossible in general.
On the other hand, we will see that the VC dimension can be upper-bounded for finite groups.

3
VC dimension of a G-CNN associated with a finite group

To study the generalization capacity of sets of G-CNNs of the form
\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{K})
, we compute the so-called
VC dimension
of these sets, which we denote by
\mathrm{VCdim}(\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{K}))
. We refer to
[
19
, Definition 6.5]
for a formal definition of the VC dimension as well as for the concept of
shattering
. We recall that, by the fundamental theorem of learning
[
19
, Theorem 6.7]
, a finite VC dimension facilitates learning, whereas an infinite VC dimension prohibits it.

3.1
Upper bound on VC dimension

For finite groups, we have the following upper bound on the VC dimension of
\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{K})
.

Theorem 3.1

(Upper bound on VC dimension)
.

Let
G
be a group acting on
\mathcal{X}
, let
|G|=n
, and let
\mu
be the Haar measure on
G
. Let
\mathcal{K}\colon G\to\mathbb{R}
be a bounded kernel.
Then,
\mathrm{VCdim}(\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{K}))=m<\infty
.
Moreover,

\displaystyle m-3\log_{2}(m+1)\leq\log_{2}(n).

(4)

Proof.

For
f\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X},\mu)
, we consider the function
F_{\mathcal{K},f}
defined by

\displaystyle F_{\mathcal{K},f}\colon\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle\to\mathbb{R}

(5)

\displaystyle F_{\mathcal{K},f}(c)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\int_{G}(f*_{G}\mathcal{K})(g)\mathds{1}_{(f*_{G}\mathcal{K})(g)>-c}d\mu(g).

Let
\{g_{1},\dots,g_{n}\}=G
be such that
\kappa_{j}\coloneqq(f*_{G}\mathcal{K})(g_{j})\mu(g_{j})\geq(f*_{G}\mathcal{K})(g_{j+1})\mu(g_{j+1})\eqqcolon\kappa_{j+1}
for all
j<n
. Then, the function
F_{\mathcal{K},f}
is a step function with values

\displaystyle 0,\kappa_{1},\kappa_{1}+\kappa_{2},\dots,\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\kappa_{j},\sum_{j=1}^{n}\kappa_{j}.

(6)

In particular,
|F_{\mathcal{K},f}(\mathbb{R})|\leq n+1
, where we denote by
|A|
the cardinality of a finite set
A
.

Assume that, for
m\in\mathbb{N}
,
\mathrm{VCdim}(\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{K}))\geq m
, then there are
m
functions
f_{1},\dots,f_{m}
which are shattered by
\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{K})
.
We observe with the help of (
6
) that
F_{\mathcal{K},f_{k}}
has at most
n
break points—the points where the functions are not affine—for each
f_{k}
,
k\in[m]
. This yields that the
f_{k}
collectively have not more than
mn
break points. Hence,
F_{\mathcal{K},f_{1}},\dots,F_{\mathcal{K},f_{m}}
have at most
mn+1
constant pieces.
Consequently, the constant regions of
F_{\mathcal{K},f_{1}},\dots,F_{\mathcal{K},f_{m}}
divide the real line into no more than
mn+1
segments, i.e., there exist
(\Lambda_{i})_{i=1}^{mn+1}
such that
\Lambda_{i}
is an interval and
c\mapsto(F_{\mathcal{K},f_{1}}(c),\dots,F_{\mathcal{K},f_{m}}(c))
is constant on
\Lambda_{i}
for all
i\in[mn+1]
.

For
i\in[mn+1]
and

, we define maps

\displaystyle\gamma_{i,k}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\Lambda_{i}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\mathbb{R},

(7)

\displaystyle\gamma_{i,k}(c)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\int_{G}\mathrm{ReLU}((f_{k}*_{G}\mathcal{K})(g)+c)d\mu(g)

\displaystyle=F_{\mathcal{K},f_{k}}(c)+\int_{G}c\cdot\mathds{1}_{(f_{k}*_{G}\mathcal{K})(g)>-c}d\mu(g).

Note that
\gamma_{i,k}
is an affine function as
F_{\mathcal{K},f_{k}}
is constant on
\Lambda_{i}
. We proceed by estimating the number of possible classifications of
f_{1},\dots,f_{m}
by
H_{c_{1},c_{2}}(\mathcal{K})
if
(c_{1},c_{2})
can be chosen in
\Lambda_{i}\times\mathbb{R}
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
i\in[nm+1]
. The classifications of
f_{1},\dots,f_{m}
by
H_{c_{1},c_{2}}(\mathcal{K})
with

\displaystyle\Lambda_{i}\times\mathbb{R}\ni(c_{1},c_{2})\mapsto\mathcal{H}_{c_{1},c_{2}}(f_{1},\dots,f_{m})\coloneqq\left(H_{c_{1},c_{2}}(\mathcal{K})(f_{1}),\dots,H_{c_{1},c_{2}}(\mathcal{K})(f_{m})\right).

(8)

Then, the number of elements of
\{\mathcal{H}_{c_{1},c_{2}}(f_{1},\dots,f_{m}),\ c_{1}\in\Lambda_{i},\ c_{2}\in\mathbb{R}\}
is not more than
{m(m-1)}/{2}+m
.

Proof.

Note that, per Definition
2.12
and (

H_{c_{1},c_{2}}(\mathcal{K})(f_{k})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}1&\text{ if }\gamma_{i,k}(c_{1})>-c_{2}\\

\end{array}\right.

To improve readability in the remainder of the proof, we omit the explicit reference to the kernel

Let

, and

\displaystyle(p_{j})_{j=1}^{l}\coloneqq\{p\in\Lambda_{i}\colon\exists r,s\in[m]\colon\gamma_{i,r}(p)=\gamma_{i,s}(p)\text{ and }\gamma_{i,r}\neq\gamma_{i,s}\text{ on }\Lambda_{i}\}.

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{j+1}\setminus\mathcal{H}_{j}

\displaystyle\subset\{2\mathds{1}_{u(k,c^{*})}-1\colon k\in[m]\}\cup\{2\mathds{1}_{u(k,p_{j})}-1\colon k\in[m]\}

is a vector which equals
1
in coordinate
q
if
q\in u(k,c^{*})
and
0
else. The last inclusion holds because only the order of the
(\gamma_{i,k}(c_{1}))_{k=1}^{m}
influences the classifications that can be produced with all
c_{2}
and that order is, as explained before, the same for all
c_{1}\in(p_{j},p_{j+1})
.

We set for
c^{*}\in(p_{0},p_{1})

\displaystyle J_{1}\coloneqq\{2\mathds{1}_{u(k,c^{*})}-1\colon k\in[m]\}\supset\mathcal{H}_{1}.

Note that, by construction for
j=1,\dots,l

\displaystyle\{2\mathds{1}_{u(k,p_{j})}-1\colon k\in[m]\}\subset J_{j}\cap J_{j+1}.

(10)

Due to (
9
) and (
10
), we have that

\displaystyle\bigcup_{j=2}^{l+1}(\mathcal{H}_{j}\setminus\mathcal{H}_{j-1})\subset\bigcup_{j=2}^{l+1}J_{j}.

Moreover, we set
J_{1}\coloneqq\mathcal{H}_{1}
.

Let
k\in[m]
be such that
\gamma_{k}
intersects no
\gamma_{q}
with
q\neq k
on
(p_{j-1},p_{j+1})
, i.e.,

\{q\in[m]\colon\gamma_{q}(p_{j})\geq\gamma_{k}(p_{j})\}=\{q\in[m]\colon\gamma_{q}\geq\gamma_{k}\text{ on }(p_{j-1},p_{j+1})\}.

Then, it is clear that
u(k,c)
is constant on
(p_{j-1},p_{j+1})
. Therefore,
J_{j}\setminus J_{j-1}
contains no more than
l_{j}
vectors, where
l_{j}
is the number of
\gamma_{r}^{\prime}s
that intersect another
\gamma_{r^{\prime}}
with
r\neq r^{\prime}
in
p_{j}
.
We conclude that

\displaystyle|\mathcal{H}_{l+1}|

\displaystyle\leq|\mathcal{H}_{1}\cup\bigcup_{j=2}^{l+1}(\mathcal{H}_{j}\setminus\mathcal{H}_{j-1})|

\displaystyle\leq|J_{1}\cup\bigcup_{j=2}^{l+1}J_{j}|

\displaystyle\leq|J_{1}\cup\bigcup_{j=2}^{l+1}(J_{j}\setminus J_{j-1})|

\displaystyle\leq|J_{1}|+\sum_{j=1}^{l}l_{j}\leq m+\frac{m\cdot(m-1)}{2}.

Where the last inequality follows since the maximum number of intersections between
m
affine lines is equal to
{m\cdot(m-1)}/{2}
and by using the trivial estimate of
m
for
J_{1}
.
∎

From Lemma
3.2
it follows that the number of classifications of
m
functions
f_{1},\dots,f_{m}
by
H_{c_{1},c_{2}}(\mathcal{K})
with
c_{1},c_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
is not more than
(m+m\cdot(m-1)/{2})\cdot(mn+1)
.
Since
\mathrm{VCdim}(\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{K}))\geq m
, we conclude that

2^{m}\leq\left(m+\frac{m\cdot(m-1)}{2}\right)\cdot(mn+1)

which, after taking dyadic logarithms, implies

\displaystyle m

\displaystyle\leq\log_{2}\left(\frac{m\cdot(m+1)}{2}\right)+\log_{2}(mn+1)

\displaystyle\leq 2\log_{2}\left(m+1\right)+\log_{2}((m+1)n)

\displaystyle\leq 3\log_{2}(m+1)+\log_{2}(n).

(11)

Since
3\log_{2}(m+1)\leq m/2
for all
m\geq 50
we conclude that
m<\max\{2\log_{2}(n),50\}
. Hence
\mathrm{VCdim}(\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{K}))<\infty
.
Equation (
11
) now yields the claim.
∎

We can state two immediate consequences of Theorem
3.1
, which remove the
\log
terms in
m
. First, noticing that

6\log(m+1)\leq m,\text{ for all }m\geq 30,

yields the following corollary.

Corollary 3.3
.

Let
G
be a group acting on
\mathcal{X}
, let
|G|=n
, and let
\mu
be the Haar measure on
G
. Let
\mathcal{K}\colon G\to\mathbb{R}
be a kernel. Then,
\mathrm{VCdim}(\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{K}))\leq\max\{30,2\log_{2}(n)
}
.

Based on Corollary
3.3
, we can now simplify the estimate of Theorem
3.1
if the group
G
is not too small.

Corollary 3.4
.

Let
G
be a group acting on
\mathcal{X}
, let
|G|=n\geq 16
, and let
\mu
be the Haar measure on
G
. Let
\mathcal{K}\colon G\to\mathbb{R}
be a kernel.
Then,
\mathrm{VCdim}(\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{K}))\leq\log_{2}(n)+9\log_{2}(\log_{2}(n))
.

Proof.

Thanks to Corollary
3.3
, we have that, for
\mathrm{VCdim}(\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{K}))=m
,

\displaystyle m\leq\max\{30,2\log_{2}(n)\}\leq 8\log_{2}(n)

if
n\geq 16
.
Hence Theorem
3.1
yields that for
n\geq 16

\displaystyle m

\displaystyle\leq\log_{2}(n)+3\log_{2}(m+1)

\displaystyle\leq\log_{2}(n)+3\log_{2}(8\log_{2}(n)+1)

\displaystyle\leq\log_{2}(n)+3\log_{2}(16\log_{2}(n))

\displaystyle\leq\log_{2}(n)+3(\log_{2}(\log_{2}(n))+4)\leq\log_{2}(n)+9\log_{2}(\log_{2}(n)).\qed

3.2
Lower bound on VC dimension

In this subsection, we provide the complement to Theorem
3.1
in the form of a lower bound on the VC dimension of
\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{K})
for an appropriately chosen kernel
\mathcal{K}
. The result requires the underlying group
G
to act on
\mathcal{X}
with an action
a
that
has a trivial kernel
, i.e., for an origin
y\in\mathcal{X}
it holds that
a(g,y)=a(g^{\prime},y)
for
g,g^{\prime}\in G
only if
g=g^{\prime}
.

We state two lower bounds in Theorems
3.5
and
3.6
. The first result uses a specific assumption on the group, which allows a larger lower bound on the VC dimension of
\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{K})
in terms of the group size. Concretely, we assume the group to contain an element
g\neq e
of
order two
, i.e.
g\cdot g=e
, where
e
is the identity element of the group. E.g., for a rotation group, the element that corresponds to a rotation by
\pi
is an element of order two.

Theorem 3.5

(Lower bound on VC dimension)
.

Let
m\in\mathbb{N}
, let
G
be a compact group acting on
\mathcal{X}
via an action with trivial kernel, let
G
contain an element of order two, let
|G|\geq 2m\cdot\binom{m}{\lfloor m/2\rfloor}
, and let
\mu
be the Haar measure on
G
.
Then, there is a bounded kernel
\mathcal{K}\colon G\to\mathbb{R}
such that
\mathrm{VCdim}(\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{K}))\geq m
.

Proof.

The theorem follows from the results in Subsections
3.2.1
and
3.2.2
below.
Concretely, in Definition
3.8
, we introduce the notion of a set of orders associated with a kernel and a set of
m\in\mathbb{N}
functions
F
. Orders are injective maps from
[m]
to
[m]
.

For finite groups, we show in Lemma
3.10
that a necessary and sufficient condition for
\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{K})
to shatter
F
is that the associated set of orders contains a so-called
complete
set of orders, (Definition
3.9
).
We demonstrate in Lemma
3.14
that a complete set of orders of size
\binom{m}{\lfloor m/2\rfloor}
exist, let us call it
\mathcal{O}_{1}
.
In Lemma
3.19
, we provide, for every set
\mathcal{O}
of
r\in\mathbb{N}
injective maps from
[m]
to
[m]
, a set of
m
functions, and a kernel
\mathcal{K}
such that the associated set of orders of
\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{K})
contains
\mathcal{O}
. This requires
|G|\geq 2rm
. Applying Lemma
3.19
with
r=\binom{m}{\lfloor m/2\rfloor}
to
\mathcal{O}_{1}
yields that
\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{K})
contains a complete set of orders and finishes the proof if the group is finite.

For infinite groups, the result is shown in Theorem
3.22
.
∎

In the general case, when the assumption that the group contains an element of order two is dropped, we have the following result.

Theorem 3.6

(Lower bound on VC dimension in general case)
.

Let
m\in\mathbb{N}
, let
G
be a compact group acting on
\mathcal{X}
via an action with trivial kernel, let
|G|\geq 9m\cdot\binom{m}{\lfloor m/2\rfloor}
, and let
\mu
be the Haar measure on
G
.
Then, there is a bounded kernel
\mathcal{K}
such that
\mathrm{VCdim}(\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{K}))\geq m
.

Proof.

The proof of the theorem repeats the proof of Theorem
3.5
, but in the case that the group is finite, we use the Lemma
3.20
instead of Lemma
3.19
to prove that
\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{K})
contains a complete set of orders. The case of infinite groups is again addressed by Theorem
3.22
.
∎

Next, we state some immediate consequences of Theorem
3.5
, which yield a more explicit lower bound on the VC dimension of
\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{K})
.

Corollary 3.7
.

Let
m\in\mathbb{N}
, let
G
be a group acting on
\mathcal{X}
via an action with trivial kernel, let
|G|=n>1
, and let
\mu
be the Haar measure on
G
.

Then, there is a bounded kernel
\mathcal{K}\colon G\to\mathbb{R}
such that
\mathrm{VCdim}(\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{K}))\geq\log_{2}(n)-2\log_{2}(\log_{2}(n))-4
. Moreover, if
G
contains an element of order two, then there is a bounded kernel
\mathcal{K}\colon G\to\mathbb{R}
such that
\mathrm{VCdim}(\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{K}))\geq\log_{2}(n)-2\log_{2}(\log_{2}(n))-1
.

Proof.

We start with the second part of the assertion, i.e., the case where
G
has an element of order two.
Note that, we can assume
n\geq 2^{8}
since otherwise

\log_{2}(n)-2\log_{2}(\log_{2}(n))-1<1,

where the result is trivial.
Wallis’ formula
[
22
]
yields that for all
m\in\mathbb{N}

\displaystyle\binom{2m}{m}<\frac{4^{m}}{\sqrt{\pi m}}.

(12)

Hence, if

\displaystyle 4m\cdot\binom{2m}{m}<4m\frac{4^{m}}{\sqrt{\pi m}}<n,

(13)

then, by Theorem
3.5
, there is a bounded kernel
\mathcal{K}\colon G\to\mathbb{R}
such that
\mathrm{VCdim}(\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{K}))\geq 2m
.
Taking logarithms, we conclude that the second inequality of (
13
) is equivalent to

\displaystyle 2+\frac{1}{2}\log_{2}(m)+2m-\frac{1}{2}\log_{2}(\pi)<\log_{2}(n).

(14)

We pick
m\in\mathbb{N}
, such that
2m\leq\log_{2}(n)-2\log_{2}(\log_{2}(n))\leq 2m+1
.
Then,

\displaystyle 2m+\frac{1}{2}\log_{2}(m)+2-\frac{1}{2}\log_{2}(\pi)

\displaystyle\leq\log_{2}(n)-2\log_{2}(\log_{2}(n))+\frac{1}{2}\log_{2}\left(\log_{2}(n)-2\log_{2}(\log_{2}(n))\right)-\frac{1}{2}+2-\frac{1}{2}\log_{2}(\pi)

\displaystyle\leq\log_{2}(n)-2\log_{2}(\log_{2}(n))+\frac{1}{2}\log_{2}\left(\log_{2}(n)\right)+\frac{3}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\log_{2}(\pi)

\displaystyle\leq\log_{2}(n)-\log_{2}(\log_{2}(n))+\frac{3}{2}<\log_{2}(n),

which implies (
14
) and hence, by (
13
) we conclude that

\displaystyle\mathrm{VCdim}(\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{K}))\geq 2m\geq\log_{2}(n)-2\log_{2}(\log_{2}(n))-1.

The general case follows by a similar argument. We note that we can assume
n\in\mathbb{N}
to be such that

\displaystyle\log_{2}(n)-2\log_{2}(\log_{2}(n))-4\geq 1

(15)

since the result is trivial otherwise.

Then, instead of (
13
) we use the estimate

\displaystyle 18m\cdot\binom{2m}{m}<18m\frac{4^{m}}{\sqrt{\pi m}}<n,

(16)

which yields with Theorem
3.6
the existence of a kernel
\mathcal{K}\colon G\to\mathbb{R}
such that
\mathrm{VCdim}(\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{K}))\geq 2m
. Then (
16
)
holds if

\displaystyle 5+\frac{1}{2}\log_{2}(m)+2m-\frac{1}{2}\log_{2}(\pi)<\log_{2}(n).

(17)

Choosing now
m\in\mathbb{N}
such that
2m\leq\log_{2}(n)-2\log_{2}(\log_{2}(n))-3\leq 2m+1
, which is possible because of (
15
), we conclude with the same computation as above, that

\displaystyle\mathrm{VCdim}(\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{K}))\geq 2m\geq\log_{2}(n)-2\log_{2}(\log_{2}(n))-4.

∎

In the following subsections, we provide the auxiliary results used to prove Theorems
3.5
and
3.6
.

3.2.1
Complete sets of orders

To continue further, we need to introduce some additional notation. We will introduce auxiliary variables
\nu_{\mathcal{K},k}
, which are very closely related to the
\gamma_{i,k}
of (
7
) but defined on the whole domain
\mathbb{R}
and hence not globally affine.

Definition 3.8
.

Let
G
be a compact group acting on
\mathcal{X}
, let
\mu
be a finite measure on
G
, let
\mathcal{K}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G\to\mathbb{R}
be a bounded kernel and let
\{f_{1},\dots,f_{m}\}\subset\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X},\mu)
.
We define

\nu_{\mathcal{K},k}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R},\ \nu_{\mathcal{K},k}(c)\coloneqq\int_{G}\mathrm{ReLU}((f_{k}*_{G}\mathcal{K})(g)+c)d\mu(g).

For
c\in\mathbb{R}
, we denote by
\sigma(\mathcal{K},c)
the
order
of
(\nu_{\mathcal{K},k}(c))_{k=1}^{m}
,

\sigma(\mathcal{K},c)(k)\coloneqq 1+|\{l\in[m]\colon\nu_{\mathcal{K},l}(c)<\nu_{\mathcal{K},k}(c)\}|.

We denote by
\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{K})\coloneqq\{\sigma(\mathcal{K},c),\ c\in\mathbb{R}\}
the
set of orders of
(\nu_{\mathcal{K},k}(c))_{k=1}^{m}
obtained by varying
c
.

Requiring that
\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{K})
shatters
\{f_{1},\dots,f_{m}\}
imposes restrictions on the set of orders
\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{K})
.
Vice versa, there is a set
\mathcal{O}\subset\mathcal{P}([m])
such that if
\mathcal{O}\subset\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{K})
, then
\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{K})
shatters
\{f_{1},\dots,f_{m}\}
.
Specifically, we call such sets of orders
\mathcal{O}

complete
sets of orders.

Definition 3.9

(Complete set of orders)
.

Let
m\in\mathbb{N}
. A set
\mathcal{O}\subset\{\sigma\colon[m]\to[m]\}
is a
complete set of orders
if for each
A\subset[m]
, there exists
\sigma_{A}\in\mathcal{O}
such that
\sigma_{A}(a)<\sigma_{A}(b)
for all
a\in A,\ b\in A^{c}
.

Next, we relate the shattering properties of G-CNNs for a given set of functions with a certain kernel
\mathcal{K}
to the property that
\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{K})
contains a complete set of orders.

Lemma 3.10
.

Let
G
be a group acting on
\mathcal{X}
, let
\mu
be the Haar measure on
G
, let
\mathcal{K}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G\to\mathbb{R}
be a bounded kernel and let
\{f_{1},\dots,f_{m}\}\subset\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X},\mu)
.

Then,
\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{K})
contains all functions from
\{f_{1},\dots,f_{m}\}
to
\{-1,1\}
if and only if
\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{K})
contains a complete set of orders.

Proof.

First, we prove that if
\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{K})
shatters
F\coloneqq\{f_{1},\dots,f_{m}\}
, then
\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{K})
contains a complete set of orders.
Indeed, if
\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{K})
shatters
F
, then for every
A\subset[m]
there is a classifier
cl\in\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{K})
,
cl\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}F\rightarrow\{-1,1\}
, such that
cl(f_{j})=-1,
if
j\in A,
and
cl(f_{j})=1
if
f_{j}\in A^{c}
.
That means we can pick
c_{1},c_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
, such that
cl
is equal to
H_{c_{1},c_{2}}(\mathcal{K})
.
Invoking the definition of
H_{c_{1},c_{2}}(\mathcal{K})
, we conclude that
\nu_{\mathcal{K},j}(c_{1})<-c_{2}
if
f_{j}\in A
and

\nu_{\mathcal{K},j}(c_{1})\geq-c_{2}
if
f_{j}\in A^{c}
.
As a consequence
\nu_{\mathcal{K},j}(c_{1})>\nu_{\mathcal{K},k}(c_{1})
for all
f_{j}\in A^{c}
and
f_{k}\in A
. Hence,
\sigma(\mathcal{K},c_{1})(i)<\sigma(\mathcal{K},c_{1})(j)
for all
i\in A,\ j\in A^{c}
.

Let us now prove that if
\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{K})
contains a complete set of orders, then
\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{K})
shatters
F
.
Let
cl\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}F\rightarrow\{-1,1\}
be arbitrary and choose
A=cl^{-1}(\{-1\})
to be the set of elements where the classifier has value
-1
.
By assumption, we can find
\sigma\in\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{K})
, such that
\sigma(i)<\sigma(j)
for all
i\in A,\ j\in A^{c}
.
As a consequence, there exists
c^{*}
such that

\nu_{\mathcal{K},i}(c^{*})<\nu_{\mathcal{K},f_{j}}(c^{*})

for all
i\in A
and
j\in A^{c}
.
Therefore, we have that

\nu_{1}\coloneqq\max_{i\in A}\nu_{\mathcal{K},i}(c^{*})<\min_{j\in A^{c}}\nu_{\mathcal{K},j}(c^{*})\eqqcolon\nu_{2}.

Setting
c_{2}=-(\nu_{1}+\nu_{2})/2
, we get that

H_{c^{*},c_{2}}(\mathcal{K})(f_{j})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}-1&\text{ if }j\in A\\
1&\text{ else. }\end{array}\right.

∎

Based on the connection between
\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{K})
being shattering to properties of sets of orders established in Lemma
3.10
, we can now state a lower bound on the size of
\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{K})
.

Lemma 3.11
.

Let
G
be a group acting on
\mathcal{X}
, let
\mu
be the Haar measure on
G
, let
\mathcal{K}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G\to\mathbb{R}
be a bounded kernel and let
\{f_{1},\dots,f_{m}\}\subset\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X},\mu)
.

If
\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{K})
shatters
\{f_{1},\dots,f_{m}\}
, then
\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{K})
contains at least
\binom{m}{\lfloor m/2\rfloor}
elements, where
\lfloor m/2\rfloor
is the largest integer part of
m/2
.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.10
if
\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{K})
shatters
F\coloneqq\{f_{1},\dots,f_{m}\}
, then for every set
A\subset[m]
containing
\lfloor m/2\rfloor
elements there is an order
\sigma_{A}\in\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{K})
such that
\sigma_{A}(i)<\sigma_{A}(j)
for all
i\in A,\ j\in A^{c}
.
Moreover, it is easy to see that for two different sets
A
and
B
containing
\lfloor m/2\rfloor
elements necessarily
\sigma_{A}\neq\sigma_{B}
.
Consequently, the number of permutations in
\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{K})
is not smaller than the number of different sets containing
\lfloor m/2\rfloor
elements, which is equal to
\binom{m}{\lfloor m/2\rfloor}
.
∎

The rest of this section shows how to construct a complete set of orders
\mathcal{O}
, containing no more than the necessary number of
\binom{m}{\lfloor m/2\rfloor}
elements established in Lemma
3.11
.

Lemma 3.12
.

Let
S(q,l)\coloneqq\{A\subset[l]\colon|A|=q\}
for
q,l\in\mathbb{N}
.
Then, there is a bijective map

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{q,2q-1}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}S(q,2q-1)\rightarrow S(q-1,2q-1)

such that
\mathcal{F}_{q,2q-1}(A)\subset A
for every set
A\in S(q,2q-1)
.

Proof.

For
q=2
, we define

F_{2,3}(\{1,2\})=\{2\},\ F_{2,3}(\{1,3\})=\{1\},\ F_{2,3}(\{2,3\})=\{3\}.

Clearly,
F_{2,3}
satisfies the requirements of the lemma.

Next, we inductively define
F_{q+1,2q+1}
, by assuming
F_{q,2q-1}
exists with the required property.
For
A\coloneqq\{q,q+1,\dots,2q+1\}
, we define
F_{q,2q-1}(A)=\{q+1,\dots,2q+1\}
.
For all other
A\in S(q+1,2q+1)
, we let

\displaystyle j

\displaystyle\coloneqq\max\{a\in A\text{ such that }a+1\notin A\}.

(18)

Define

\displaystyle F_{q+1,2q+1}(A)\coloneqq\{j\}\cup T_{j}^{-1}(F_{q,2q-1}(T_{j}(A\setminus\{j\})),

(19)

where

T_{j}(q)\coloneqq\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}q&\text{ if }q<j\\
q-2&\text{ if }q>j+1.\\
\end{array}\right.

Per induction assumption, it holds that
F_{q+1,2q+1}(A)\subset A
since
(F_{q,2q-1}(T_{j}(A\setminus\{j\}))\subset T_{j}(A\setminus\{j\})
.

Now we show that
j
, which is defined in (
18
) is equal to
\max\{i\in F_{q+1,2q+1}(A)\text{ such that }i+1\notin F_{q+1,2q+1}(A)\}
.
First we note, that by construction
j
is in
F_{q+1,2q+1}(A)
.
Moreover, as
F_{q+1,2q+1}(A)\subset A
and
j+1\notin A
, it holds that
j+1\notin F_{q+1,2q+1}(A)
.
We will prove the maximality of such
j
by contradiction.
Let

\displaystyle j_{1}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\min\{j^{\prime}>j\colon j^{\prime}\in F_{q+1,2q+1}(A),\text{ and }j^{\prime}+1\notin F_{q+1,2q+1}(A)\},

\displaystyle A_{1}

\displaystyle\coloneqq A\setminus\{j\}.

Then, it follows from (
19
) that

j_{1}\in T_{j}^{-1}(F_{q,2q-1}(T_{j}(A_{1}))\text{ and }j_{1}+1\not\in T_{j}^{-1}(F_{q,2q-1}(T_{j}(A_{1})).

Hence,
j_{1}-2=T_{j}(j_{1})\in F_{q,2q-1}(T_{j}(A_{1}))
and
j_{1}-1=T_{j}(j_{1}+1)\notin F_{q,2q-1}(T_{j}(A_{1}))
.
Consequently,

j_{2}\geq T_{j}(j_{1}),\text{ where }j_{2}\coloneqq\max\{i\in F_{q,2q-1}(A_{1})\text{ such that }i+1\notin F_{q,2q-1}(T_{j}(A_{1}))\}.

By induction assumption
j_{2}=\max\{a\in T_{j}(A_{1})\text{ such that }a+1\notin T_{j}(A_{1})\}
.
Consequently,
j_{2}+2=T_{j}^{-1}(j_{2})\in A_{1}\subset A
and
T_{j}^{-1}(j_{2}+1)=(T_{j}^{-1}(j_{2})+1)\notin A_{1}
.
Therefore,
T_{j}^{-1}(j_{2})\geq j_{1}>j
and
T_{j}^{-1}(j_{2})\in A,\ (T_{j}^{-1}(j_{2})+1)\notin A
, which contradicts the definition of
j
.

Now we are ready to show the injectivity of
F_{q+1,2q+1}
. Let
A,B\in S(q+1,2q+1)
with
A\neq B
such that

F_{q+1,2q+1}(A)=F_{q+1,2q+1}(B).

As
A\neq B
one of these sets not equal to
\{q,\dots,2q+1\}
.
Without loss of generality, we assume that
A\neq\{q+1,\dots,2q+1\}
.
Then, we can define
j_{A}
as in (
18
).
By the statement above it holds that

\displaystyle j_{A}

\displaystyle=\max\{i\in F_{q+1,2q+1}(A)\text{ such that }i+1\notin F_{q+1,2q+1}(A)\}

\displaystyle=\max\{i\in F_{q+1,2q+1}(B)\text{ such that }i+1\notin F_{q+1,2q+1}(B)\}

\displaystyle=\max\{b\in B\text{ such that }b+1\notin B\}=j_{B}.

That means that
A\setminus\{j_{A}\}\neq B\setminus\{j_{B}\}
, but, by (

F_{q,2q-1}(T_{j_{A}}(A\setminus\{j_{A}\}))=F_{q,2q-1}(T_{j_{B}}(B\setminus\{j_{B}\})).

The last equation is a contradiction to the induction assumption.
Since
|S(q,2q-1)|=|S(q-1,2q-1)|
, it follows from the injectivity that
\mathcal{F}_{q,2q-1}
is also surjective.
∎

Lemma 3.13
.

Let
m\in\mathbb{N}
.
Then, for all
q\in[m]
there exists a map
\mathcal{F}_{q,m}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}S(q,m)\to S(q-1,m)
such that for every
A\in S(q,m)
, it holds that
\mathcal{F}_{q,m}(A)\subset A
.
Moreover, if
\binom{m}{q}\geq\binom{m}{q-1}
, then
\mathcal{F}_{q,m}
is surjective.
If
\binom{m}{q}\leq\binom{m}{q-1}
, then
\mathcal{F}_{q,m}
is injective.

Proof.

We prove this statement by induction over
m\in\mathbb{N}
.
It is easy to see that the statement holds for all
q\leq m
for
m=3
.
Let us assume that the statement holds for all
m<r
; we will prove the statement for
m=r
.
We denote

\displaystyle S_{q,m}\coloneqq\{A\in S(q,m)\colon m\in A\},

and

\displaystyle S_{q,m}^{c}\coloneqq\{A\in S(q,m)\colon m\notin A\}.

Let
\mathcal{P}([m])
be the power set of
\{1,2,\dots,m\}
, we define three maps:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}_{m}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{P}([m])\to\mathcal{P}([m-1]),

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}_{m}(A)=\{a\in A,\ a<m\},

\displaystyle\mathrm{Inv}_{m}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\ \mathcal{P}([m-1])\to\mathcal{P}([m]),

\displaystyle\mathrm{Inv}_{m}(A)=A,

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathrm{Inv}}_{m}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{P}([m-1])\to\mathcal{P}([m]),

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathrm{Inv}}_{m}(A)=A\cup\{m\}.

We prove the statement of Lemma
3.13
by considering three cases.

Case 1:

m\geq 2q
.
In this case, it holds that

\displaystyle\binom{m-1}{q}\geq\binom{m-1}{q-1}\text{ and }\binom{m-1}{q-1}>\binom{m-2}{q-1}.

Consequently, by the induction hypothesis, there exists

\mathcal{F}_{q,m-1}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}S(q,m-1)\rightarrow S(q-1,m-1),

such that for all
A\subset S(q,m-1)
it holds that
\mathcal{F}_{q,m-1}(A)\subset A
, and
\mathcal{F}_{q,m-1}
is surjective.

Also, by the induction hypothesis, there exists
\mathcal{F}_{q-1,m-1}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}S(q-1,m-1)\rightarrow S(q-2,m-1),

such that for all
A
in
S(q-1,m-1)
it holds that
\mathcal{F}_{q-1,m-1}(A)\subset A
, and
\mathcal{F}_{q-1,m-1}
is surjective.
We define
\mathcal{F}_{q,m}(A)
using
\mathcal{F}_{q-1,m-1}
and
\mathcal{F}_{q,m-1}
by

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{q,m}(A)\coloneqq\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathrm{Inv}_{m}\circ\mathcal{F}_{q,m-1}\circ\mathrm{Pr}_{m}(A)&\text{ if }A\in S_{q,m}^{c}\\
\widehat{\mathrm{Inv}}_{m}\circ\mathcal{F}_{q-1,m-1}\circ\mathrm{Pr}_{m}(A)&\text{ if }A\in S_{q,m}.\end{array}\right.

It is clear that
\mathcal{F}_{q,m}
satisfies all the requirements of the theorem by construction.

Case 2:

m=2q-1
.
This case was proved in Lemma
3.12
.

Case 3:

m\leq 2q-2
.
In this case, it holds that

\displaystyle\binom{m-1}{q}<\binom{m-1}{q-1}\text{ and }\binom{m-1}{q-1}\leq\binom{m-2}{q-1}.

By the induction hypothesis, there exists

\mathcal{F}_{q,m-1}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}S(q,m-1)\rightarrow S(q-1,m-1),

such that for all
A\subset S(q,m-1)
it holds that
\mathcal{F}_{q,m-1}(A)\subset A
, and
\mathcal{F}_{q,m-1}
is injective.

Also, by the induction hypothesis, there exists

\mathcal{F}_{q-1,m-1}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}S(q-1,m-1)\rightarrow S(q-2,m-1),

such that for all
A\subset S(q-1,m-1)
it holds that
\mathcal{F}_{q-1,m-1}(A)\subset A
, and
\mathcal{F}_{q-1,m-1}
is injective.
We define
\mathcal{F}_{q,m}(A)
using
\mathcal{F}_{q-1,m-1}
and
\mathcal{F}_{q,m-1}
by

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{q,m}(A)\coloneqq\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathrm{Inv}_{m}\circ\mathcal{F}_{q,m-1}\circ\mathrm{Pr}_{m}(A)&\text{ if }A\in S_{q,m}^{c}\\
\widehat{\mathrm{Inv}}_{m}\circ\mathcal{F}_{q-1,m-1}\circ\mathrm{Pr}_{m}(A)&\text{ if }A\in S_{q,m}.\end{array}\right.

It holds that
\mathcal{F}_{q,m}
satisfies all the requirements of the theorem by construction.
∎

In the following lemma, we now establish the existence of relatively small complete sets of orders. The proof of this lemma is based on Lemma

.
More specifically, the maps
(\mathcal{F}_{q,m})_{q=1}^{m}
are used to define an order on

.

Lemma 3.14
.

Let
m\in\mathbb{N}
. There is a complete set of orders
\mathcal{O}
, containing no more than
\binom{m}{\lfloor m/2\rfloor}
elements.

Proof.

Let
A\in S(q,m)
. We define
F_{1,m}\coloneqq\mathcal{F}_{q,m}
and for
k\in\{2,\dots,q\}
,

\displaystyle F_{k,m}\coloneqq\mathcal{F}_{q-k+1,m}\circ\cdots\circ\mathcal{F}_{q,m}.

We define the order
\tilde{\sigma}_{A}
on
A
by

\displaystyle\tilde{\sigma}_{A}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\ A

\displaystyle\to\{1,2,\dots,q\},

\displaystyle\tilde{\sigma}_{A}(a)

\displaystyle=k,\ \text{ if }a\in F_{k-1,m}(A)\setminus F_{k,m}(A)\text{ for }k\in\{1,\dots,q\},

(20)

where
F_{0,m}(A)\coloneqq A
. We have that
\tilde{\sigma}_{A}
is well defined since, for
k\in[q]
, if
a\in F_{k-1,m}(A)\setminus F_{k,m}(A)
, then
a\not\in F_{k,m}(A)
and hence
a\not\in F_{k^{\prime},m}(A)
for every
k^{\prime}>k
. Similarly, since
a\in F_{k-1,m}(A)
it follows that
a\in F_{k^{\prime\prime},m}(A)
for all
k^{\prime\prime}<k
. Hence, there exists exactly one
k\in[q]
such that
a\in F_{k-1,m}(A)\setminus F_{k,m}(A)
.

We note that by construction:

\displaystyle\tilde{\sigma}_{A}(a)>\tilde{\sigma}_{A}(b),\text{ for every }a\in F_{k,m}(A),\ \text{and every }b\in A\setminus F_{k,m}(A).

(21)

We say that an order
\sigma

separates
a set
B_{1}
from a set
B_{2}
if

\displaystyle\sigma(a)>\sigma(b),\text{ for every }a\in B_{1},\ \text{and every }b\in B_{2}.

(22)

In particular, (
21
) yields that
\tilde{\sigma}_{A}
separates
F_{k,m}(A)
from
A\setminus F_{k,m}(A)
.

We construct a complete set of orders
\mathcal{O}=\bigcup_{i=0}^{q}\mathcal{O}_{m-i}
, with

\mathcal{O}_{m}\subset\mathcal{O}_{m-1}\subset\cdots\subset\mathcal{O}_{m-q},

where
q=\lfloor m/2\rfloor
.
We start by defining for
A=[m]\in S(m,m)
the set of orders
\mathcal{O}_{m}=\{\tilde{\sigma}_{A}\}
.

Next, we assume that, in the
i
-th step, for each subset
A\in S(m-i+1,m)
, there is exactly one order
\sigma\in\mathcal{O}_{m-i+1}
that separates
A
from
A^{c}\coloneqq[m]\setminus A
.
We proceed with the
i+1
-st step.
We note, that as
i\leq\lfloor m/2\rfloor
it holds that
|S(m-i+1,m)|<|S(m-i,m)|
.
We define
\mathcal{O}_{m-i}
as the union of
\mathcal{O}_{m-i+1}
with the following orders: for each
A\in S(m-i,m)
, such that there is no order in
\mathcal{O}_{m-i+1}
separating
A
from
A^{c}
, we construct the following order

\displaystyle\sigma_{A}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\{1,2,\dots,m\}

\displaystyle\to\{1,2,\dots,m\},

(23)

\displaystyle\sigma_{A}(a)

\displaystyle=\tilde{\sigma}_{A}(a),\text{ for every }a\in A,\ \text{ and }

\displaystyle\sigma(a^{\prime})

\displaystyle=i-1+\tilde{\sigma}_{A^{c}}(a^{\prime}),a^{\prime}\in A^{c}.

We make the following two observations about
\mathcal{O}_{m-i}
:

1.

Each
\tilde{A}\in S(m-i,m)
is separated from
\tilde{A}^{c}
by some
\sigma\in\mathcal{O}_{m-i}
.

2.

If
\tilde{A}\in S(m-i,m)
is separated from
\tilde{A}^{c}
by
\sigma\in\mathcal{O}_{m-i}
, then
\sigma
is of the form (
23
) for some
Q\subset[m]
with
|Q|\geq m-i
.

Next, we will show that for
\bar{A}\in S(m-i,m)
, the separating order is unique. Assume that
\bar{A}\in S(m-i,m)
is separated from
\bar{A}^{c}
by
\sigma
and
\sigma^{\prime}
. By the previous observation, we have that
\sigma=\sigma_{U}
and
\sigma^{\prime}=\sigma_{Q}
for some
U,Q\subset[m]
with
|U|,|Q|\geq m-i
.

Assume towards a contradiction that
\sigma_{Q}\neq\sigma_{U}
. Then, we conclude that
\sigma_{V}
separates for
V=Q,U
the sets
\sigma_{V}^{-1}(\{1,\dots,m-i+1\})
from
\sigma_{V}^{-1}(\{m-i+2,\dots,m\})
. By induction assumption, we have that

\displaystyle\sigma_{Q}^{-1}(\{1,\dots,m-i+1\})\neq\sigma_{U}^{-1}(\{1,\dots,m-i+1\}).

(24)

However, by (
20
) it holds that

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{m-i+1,m}(\sigma_{Q}^{-1}(\{1,\dots,m-i+1\}))=A=\mathcal{F}_{m-i+1,m}(\sigma_{U}^{-1}(\{1,\dots,m-i+1\})).

Since
\mathcal{F}_{m-i+1,m}
is injective, we arrive at a contradiction to (
24
). This yields that
\sigma_{Q}=\sigma_{U}
, and hence we observe that for each
A\in S(m-i,m)
, there is exactly one order from
\mathcal{O}_{m-i}
, that separates it. Hence, we conclude that
|\mathcal{O}_{m-i+1}|=|S(m-i,m)|=\binom{m}{m-i}
. In particular,
|\mathcal{O}_{m-q+1}|=\binom{m}{\lceil m/2\rceil}=\binom{m}{\lfloor m/2\rfloor}
.

Now, we will prove that every set
A\in\mathcal{P}([m])
is separated from
A^{c}
by some order from
\mathcal{O}_{m-q}
.
We prove this statement by contradiction.

Let
A\in S(k,m)
with the biggest
k
such that
A
is not separated from
A^{c}
by an order from
\mathcal{O}_{m-q}
. By the previous part of the proof, we can assume that

.

In this case, as
|S(k+1,m)|>|S(k,m)|
it holds that
\mathcal{F}_{k+1,m}
is surjective by Lemma
3.13
.
Therefore, there exists

, such that
\mathcal{F}_{k+1,m}(B)=A
.
Moreover, as

it holds that
B
is separated from

by
\sigma\in\mathcal{O}_{m-q}
.
By construction, this implies that
A
is separated from
A^{c}
by
\sigma
, which produces a contradiction.

We conclude that
\mathcal{O}
is a complete set of orders, containing
\binom{m}{\lfloor m/2\rfloor}
elements.
∎

3.2.2
Construction of an expressive kernel

Let
G
be a group with
|G|\geq n
. In this subsection, we construct a kernel
\mathcal{K}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G\to\mathbb{R}
such that
\mathrm{VCdim}(\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{K}))
is close to the upper bound of Theorem
3.1
.
We construct for every
m\in\mathbb{N}
with
2m\cdot\binom{m}{\lfloor m/2\rfloor}\leq n
a bounded kernel
\mathcal{K}
, such that
\mathrm{VCdim}(\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{K}))\geq m
.
We also identify the functions
f_{1},\dots,f_{m}
that are shattered by
\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{K})
.

Recall that an action has trivial kernel if for an origin
y\in\mathcal{X}
as in (
1
) the action
a
on
G
satisfies that
a(g,y)=a(g^{\prime},y)
only if
g=g^{\prime}
. Therefore, for a function
u\colon G\to\mathbb{R}
, we can define

\displaystyle f\colon\mathcal{X}

\displaystyle\to\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle f(x)

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}u(g)&\text{ if }x=a(g,y)\text{ for a }g\in G\\
0&\text{ else.}\end{array}\right.

It is not hard to see that, in this case,
\bar{f}=u
. Hence, if we find
m\in\mathbb{N}
real-valued functions
u_{1},\dots,u_{m}
on
G
which are shattered by

\displaystyle\left\{u\mapsto\mathrm{sign}\left(\int_{G}\mathrm{ReLU}((u*_{G}\mathcal{K})(g)+c_{1})d\mu(g)+c_{2}\right)\colon c_{1},c_{2}\in\mathbb{R}\right\},

then there also exist real-valued functions
f_{1},\dots,f_{m}
on
\mathcal{X}
that are shattered by
\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{K})
.

Specifically, for
g\in G
with
g\neq e
, we will choose the functions
u_{1},\dots,u_{m}
from the two-dimensional space of functions
U
generated by the functions
\mathds{1}_{e},\ \mathds{1}_{g}
.
In this case, every function
u\in U
can be written as a linear combination of
\mathds{1}_{e},\ \mathds{1}_{g}
:

\displaystyle u\coloneqq a\mathds{1}_{e}+b\mathds{1}_{g},\ a,b\in\mathbb{R}.

(25)

To simplify the notation, we define for
u
as in (
25
),

\displaystyle\widetilde{u}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R},\ \widetilde{u}(\mathbf{k})=k_{1}a+k_{2}b,\text{ where }\mathbf{k}=(k_{1},k_{2}).

In the following lemma, we construct for
A>0
functions
u_{0},\dots,u_{2m}\in U
and associated vectors
\mathbf{k}^{2i,A}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
,

such that
\widetilde{u_{2i}}(\mathbf{k}^{2i,A})=A
and
\widetilde{u_{l}}(\mathbf{k}^{2i,A})<B<A
for all

,

. This choice of
u_{0},\dots,u_{2m}
and
\mathbf{k}^{i,A}
is essential for the construction of a kernel that scatters a subset of
u_{0},\dots,u_{2m}
.

Lemma 3.15
.

Let
m\in\mathbb{N}
, let
G
be a group acting on
\mathcal{X}
, and let
g\in G
be such that
g\neq e
. Let
C>B>0
,
p\in\mathbb{N}
, and let for

\displaystyle\epsilon_{i}

\displaystyle\coloneqq 4\frac{C}{B}+1+(p-i)\left(\frac{B}{C}+1\right).

For
i\in[2p+1]
, let
u_{i}\colon G\to\mathbb{R}
be defined by

\displaystyle u_{0}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathds{1}_{e},

\displaystyle u_{1}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathds{1}_{g},

\displaystyle u_{2}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\epsilon_{1}u_{0}+u_{1}\eqqcolon a_{2,1}\mathds{1}_{e}+a_{2,2}\mathds{1}_{g},

\displaystyle u_{3}

\displaystyle u_{2q}

\displaystyle u_{2q+1}

\displaystyle\coloneqq u_{2q-2}+\epsilon_{q}u_{2q-1}\eqqcolon a_{2q+1,1}\mathds{1}_{e}+a_{2q+1,2}\mathds{1}_{g},\text{ for }q\in[p].

A

\mathbf{k}^{i,A}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}

\displaystyle\widetilde{u_{i}}(\mathbf{k}^{i,A})=A\text{ and }\widetilde{u_{l}}(\mathbf{k}^{i,A})<B,\text{ for all }l\in[2p+1]\text{ with }l\neq i.

Note that the definition of
\epsilon_{i}

\displaystyle\epsilon_{i}

\displaystyle>\frac{4C}{B}>\frac{2C}{B}+\frac{1}{2}>2,\text{ for all }i\in[p],

\displaystyle\epsilon_{i+1}

\displaystyle<\epsilon_{i}-\frac{B}{C},\text{ for all }i\in[p-1].

and

. Note first that for all

\displaystyle\alpha u_{2m-2}+\beta u_{2m-1}

\displaystyle=\alpha\epsilon_{m-1}u_{2m-4}+\alpha u_{2m-3}+\beta u_{2m-4}+\beta\epsilon_{m-1}u_{2m-3}

As a consequence, we have that
\alpha(a_{2m-2,1},a_{2m-2,2})\neq\beta(a_{2m-1,1},a_{2m-1,2})

and we conclude that the matrix

\displaystyle Q\coloneqq\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}a_{2m-2,1}&a_{2m-2,2}\\
a_{2m-1,1}&a_{2m-1,2}\end{array}\right)

As a consequence, we have that there exist

\displaystyle\widetilde{u}_{2m-2}(\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle=2\epsilon_{m}^{-1}A,

(29)

\displaystyle\widetilde{u}_{2m-1}(\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle\epsilon_{m-1}u_{2m-2}-u_{2m-1}

\displaystyle\frac{\epsilon_{m-1}C}{\epsilon_{m-1}^{2}-1}<\frac{B}{2}.

Plugging (

\displaystyle\widetilde{u}_{2m-4}(\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\epsilon_{m-1}^{2}-1}\left(\epsilon_{m-1}\widetilde{u}_{2m-2}(\mathbf{k})-\widetilde{u}_{2m-1}(\mathbf{k})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\epsilon_{m-1}^{2}-1}\left(\epsilon_{m-1}2\epsilon_{m}^{-1}A+A\right)

Also, by a similar argument as above
|\widetilde{u}_{2m-3}(\mathbf{k})|<B
.

Next, we note, that if

and

t<j
.
Indeed,
|\widetilde{u}_{2t}(\mathbf{k})|<\frac{\epsilon_{t}|\widetilde{u}_{2t+2}(\mathbf{k})|+|\widetilde{u}_{2t+3}(\mathbf{k})|}{\epsilon_{t}^{2}-1}<\frac{B}{\epsilon_{t}-1}<B
.
The same argument can be made to bound
|\widetilde{u}_{2t+1}(\mathbf{k})|
by
B
.
Thus,
|\widetilde{u_{i}}(\mathbf{k})|<B
for all
i<2m-2
.

Let us collect what we have proved so far:

1.

It holds that
|\widetilde{u_{i}}(\mathbf{k})|<B
for all
i<2m-2
.

2.

For
i=2m-2
or
i=2m-1
, it follows directly from (
29
) and (
30
), that
\widetilde{u_{i}}(\mathbf{k})<B
.

3.

For
i=2m
, it holds that
\widetilde{u_{i}}(\mathbf{k})=\epsilon_{i}2\epsilon_{i}^{-1}A-A=A
.

4.

For
i=2m+1
, it holds that
\widetilde{u_{i}}(\mathbf{k})=2\epsilon_{i}^{-1}A-\epsilon_{i}A<0
by (
26
).

Hence, the proof is complete if we show that
\widetilde{u_{i}}(\mathbf{k})<0
for all
i>2m+1
.
We have that

\displaystyle\widetilde{u}_{2m+2}(\mathbf{k})=\epsilon_{m+1}A+2\epsilon_{m}^{-1}A-\epsilon_{m}A.

Moreover,

\displaystyle\epsilon_{m+1}A-\epsilon_{m}A=(\epsilon_{m+1}-\epsilon_{m})A<-\frac{B}{C}A.

Consequently,

\displaystyle\widetilde{u}_{2m+2}(\mathbf{k})<-\frac{B}{C}A+2\epsilon_{m}^{-1}A<0,

since
2\epsilon_{m}^{-1}-B/C<B/(2C)-B/C<0
by (
26
). Moreover,

\displaystyle\widetilde{u}_{2m+3}(\mathbf{k})=A+2\epsilon_{m}^{-1}\epsilon_{m+1}A-\epsilon_{m}\epsilon_{m+1}A<A-\epsilon_{m+1}A<0.

Finally, it is not hard to see that, if
\widetilde{u}_{2m+3}(\mathbf{k})<0
and
\widetilde{u}_{2m+2}(\mathbf{k})<0
, then
\widetilde{u}_{i}(\mathbf{k})<0
for all
i>2m+3
.
∎

(34)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{u}_{2p}(\mathbf{k}^{o_{1}(i),C-(m-i+1)\epsilon})<B,\text{ for all }p\in[m]\ and\ p\neq i\text{ and }

\displaystyle u_{2i}*_{G}\mathcal{K}_{l}(h_{1,i})

\displaystyle=\widetilde{u}_{2i}(\mathbf{k}^{o_{1}(i),C-(m-i+1)\epsilon})=C-(m-i+1)\epsilon.

Next, we obtain

3.15

(38)

We will prove that lemma

Lemma 3.17
.

For every

it holds that

\displaystyle B<m_{l}-m\cdot(M_{l}+\epsilon)\text{ and }M_{l}\leq\epsilon\cdot(m^{l}-1).

(39)

Proof.

For every

\displaystyle(u*_{G}\mathcal{K}_{l})(h)

\displaystyle(u_{2o_{p}(j)}*_{G}\mathcal{K}_{l})(h_{p,i})<B,\text{ for all }j\neq i\text{ and }u_{2o_{p}(i)}*_{G}\mathcal{K}_{l}(h_{p,i})=m_{p-1}-(m-i+1)(M_{p-1}+\epsilon).

In addition, as

for every

(40)

Next, we check (

\displaystyle m_{p}-(m-i+1)(M_{p}+\epsilon)

Now, we are ready to estimate

\displaystyle\hat{\nu}_{\mathcal{K}_{p+1},{2o_{p+1}(i)}}(-m_{p+1}+\epsilon)

\displaystyle=\left((u_{2o_{p+1}(i)}*_{G}\mathcal{K}_{p+1})(h_{p+1,i})-m_{p+1}+\epsilon\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+\hat{\nu}_{\mathcal{K}_{p+1},{2o_{p+1}(i)}}(-m_{p}+\epsilon)+(m_{p}-m_{p+1})\sum_{g\in\widetilde{H}_{r}}\mathds{1}_{(u_{2o_{p+1}(i)}*_{G}\mathcal{K}_{p+1})(g)>m_{p}-\epsilon}

\displaystyle M_{p+1}\leq|(m-1)(M_{p}+\epsilon)|+M_{p}=mM_{p}+(m-1)\epsilon\leq\left(m\cdot(m^{p}-1)+(m-1)\right)\epsilon=(m^{p+1}-1)\epsilon.

\displaystyle\{g^{-2}\cdot h_{i,p},\ g^{-1}\cdot h_{i,p},\ h_{i,p},\ g\cdot h_{i,p},\ g^{2}\cdot h_{i,p}\}\cap\{g^{-2}\cdot h_{i,t},\ g^{-1}\cdot h_{i,t},\ h_{i,t},\ g\cdot h_{i,t},\ g^{2}\cdot h_{i,t}\}=\varnothing.

To see that

as above exist we observe that we can build them sequentially: For

,
p=1,\dots,m
, because of the size of

there exists

such that

g^{\prime}\not\in\bigcup_{i=1}^{l-1}\left(g^{-4}\cdot H_{i}\cup g^{-3}\cdot H_{i}\cup\cdots\cup\ g^{4}\cdot H_{i}\right)\cup\bigcup_{r=1}^{p-1}\{g^{-4}\cdot h_{l,r},\ g^{-3}\cdot h_{l,r},\dots,\ g^{3}\cdot h_{l,r},\ g^{4}\cdot h_{l,r}\}.

Then, we set

.

Let
\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}
be the kernel defined in Lemma
3.16
. As
\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}
has non zero values only on the elements from
\widetilde{H}_{r}\cup(g^{-1}\cdot\widetilde{H}_{r})
, we can define

\displaystyle K

\displaystyle\coloneqq\max_{g\in G}{|\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}(g)|},

\displaystyle s

\displaystyle\coloneqq\min_{i\in[m],j\in[2]}a_{2i,j},

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle\coloneqq\max_{i\in[m],j\in[2]}{a_{2i,j}},

where by definition of
u_{i},\ u_{i}\coloneqq a_{i,1}\mathds{1}_{e}+a_{i,2}\mathds{1}_{g}
for
i\in[2m]
.
Note that by construction,
a_{2i,1}>0
and
a_{2i,2}>0
holds for all
i\in[m]
, hence
s
is positive.

Now we define the kernel
\mathcal{K}
:

1.

The kernel
\mathcal{K}
is equal to zero on

.

2.

For every

and

,

.

3.

For every

and

As a consequence of the construction, we have for all

that

\displaystyle(u_{j}*_{G}\mathcal{K})(h_{l,p})=(u_{j}*_{G}\widetilde{\mathcal{K}})(h_{l,p}).

(45)

Moreover, for all
i\in[r],\ p,j\in[m]

\displaystyle(u_{2j}*_{G}\mathcal{K})(h)=a_{2j,1}\mathcal{K}(h)+a_{2j,2}\mathcal{K}(g^{-1}\cdot h)\leq-sK\frac{S}{s}+KS=0,

as

, and
h
or

, where we defined the kernel to be equal to

.

On all elements

from

\displaystyle(u_{2j}*_{G}\mathcal{K})(h)=a_{2j,1}\mathcal{K}(h)+a_{2j,2}\mathcal{K}(g^{-1}\cdot h)=0,

To summarize, the values of

and all

.
Hence, for

,

\sum_{g\in G}\mathrm{ReLU}((u_{2k}*_{G}\mathcal{K})(g)+c)=\sum_{g\in\widetilde{H}_{r}}\mathrm{ReLU}((u_{2k}*_{G}\mathcal{K})(g)+c)=\hat{\nu}_{\mathcal{K},k}(c)=\hat{\nu}_{\widetilde{\mathcal{K}},k}(c),

for

, where the last equality follows by (

Thus, for

, the order

associated to

as defined in Definition

Remark 3.21

The kernel
\mathcal{K}

•

for all

with

).

Moreover, for an infinite group
G
, we can choose
(H_{l})_{l=1}^{r}\subset G
like in the proof of Lemma
3.20
such that
\widetilde{H}_{r}=\bigcup_{l\leq r}H_{l}\subset G
.
Moreover, for a measure
\mu
such that
\mu(h)=1
for all
h\in H
, and
\mu(h)=0
for every
h\in G\setminus H
where

H\coloneqq\{e,g\}\bigcup\left((g^{-2}\cdot\widetilde{H}_{r})\cup(g^{-1}\cdot\widetilde{H}_{r})\cup\widetilde{H}_{r}\cup(g\cdot\widetilde{H}_{r})\right),

the statement of Lemma
3.20
holds.
Indeed, in this case, the proof can be carried out mutatis mutandis.

In the Lemma
3.20
, we assumed that the group contains a finite number of elements. The following theorem treats the case of infinite groups.

Theorem 3.22
.

Let
m\in\mathbb{N}
, let
G
be an infinite compact group acting on
\mathcal{X}
, and let
\mu
be the Haar measure on
G
.
Then, for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
there is a bounded kernel
\mathcal{K}\colon G\to\mathbb{R}
such that
\mathrm{VCdim}(\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{K}))\geq n
.

Proof.

We fix
m\in\mathbb{N}
,
g\in G,\ g\neq e
and
u_{0},\dots,u_{2m}
as in Lemma
3.15
, and let
\mathcal{O}\coloneqq\{o_{1},\dots,o_{r}\}
be a complete set of orders.

Next, we show how to construct a kernel
\mathcal{K}
, such that
\mathcal{O}\subset\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{K})
, where
\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{K})
is the set of all orders associated to
u_{2},\dots,u_{2m}
as in Definition
3.8
.

As the first step of the proof, we introduce an auxiliary measure
\mu_{1}
.
This will be the counting measure on a finite subset of
G
.
To clarify which measure is used in the convolutions in the sequel, we will use in this proof the notation
f*_{\mu_{1},G}\mathcal{K}
for the convolution of a function
f\colon G\to\mathbb{R}
with the kernel
\mathcal{K}\colon G\to\mathbb{R}
using the measure
\mu_{1}
.

As we already mentioned in Remark
3.21
, since
G
is infinite, we can choose finite sets
H_{l}\subset G,\ l=1,\dots,r
, define

\displaystyle H\coloneqq\{e,g\}\bigcup\left(\bigcup_{i=1,p=1}^{i=r,p=m}\{g^{-2}\cdot h_{i,p},\ g^{-1}\cdot h_{i,p},\ h_{i,p},\ g\cdot h_{i,p}\}\right),\text{ where }h_{i,p}\in H_{i},

and choose
\mu_{1}
to be the counting measure on
H
. Then, Lemma
3.20
can be applied with
\mu_{1}
to yield the filter
\widehat{\mathcal{K}}
, such that
\mathcal{O}\subset\mathcal{O}(\widehat{\mathcal{K}})
, where all convolutional operations are integration by measure
\mu_{1}
.

However, since
\mu\neq\mu_{1}
, it is not necessarily the case that
\mathcal{H}(\widehat{\mathcal{K}})
shatters
u_{2},\dots,u_{2m}
. To correct this, we choose an open neighborhood of the identity
U
such that
h\cdot U\cap h^{\prime}\cdot U=\varnothing
for all
h\neq h^{\prime},\ h,h^{\prime}\in H
. The existence of
U
follows from the Hausdorff property of
G
. Indeed, we can construct disjoint open sets
(U_{h})_{h\in H}
such that
h\in U_{h}
for all
h\in H
. Then, we set

\displaystyle U\coloneqq\bigcap_{h\in H}h^{-1}\cdot(h\cdot U_{e}\cap U_{h}).

Since we only take finitely many intersections in the construction of
U
, it is clear that
U
is open. Moreover, we directly see that
e\in U
. Assuming there exists
h,h^{\prime}\in H
such that
h\cdot U\cap h^{\prime}\cdot U\neq\varnothing
implies by construction that

\displaystyle h\cdot U_{e}\cap U_{h}\cap h^{\prime}U_{e}\cap U_{h^{\prime}}\neq\varnothing.

(46)

Clearly, (
46
) can only hold if
h=h^{\prime}
. This shows that
U
as desired exists.

We define a kernel
\mathcal{K}
as a modification of the kernel
\widehat{\mathcal{K}}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}\coloneqq\sum_{h\in H}\widehat{\mathcal{K}}(h)\cdot\mathds{1}_{h\cdot U}.

(47)

To continue further, we introduce additional notation.
We define
a_{i}
and
b_{i}
as the coefficients
a_{i}\mathds{1}_{e}+b_{i}\mathds{1}_{g}\coloneqq u_{2i}
for
i\in[2m]
. Moreover, we define for
h^{\prime}\in G

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{a_{i},b_{i}}(h^{\prime})\coloneqq\sum_{h\in H}(a_{i}\widehat{\mathcal{K}}(h)+b_{i}\widehat{\mathcal{K}}(g^{-1}\cdot h))\cdot\mathds{1}_{h\cdot U}(h^{\prime}).

We set

\displaystyle\tilde{\nu}_{\mathcal{K},i}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R},\ \tilde{\nu}_{\mathcal{K},i}(c)\coloneqq\int_{G}\mathrm{ReLU}(\mathcal{K}_{a_{2i},b_{2i}}(h)-c)d\mu(h).

For
(c_{j})_{j=1}^{r}
defined as in Remark
3.21
, we will show now, that for
i\in[m]
the newly defined
\tilde{\nu}_{\mathcal{K},i}(c_{j})
are equal to
\mu(U)\cdot\nu_{\widehat{\mathcal{K}},i}(c_{j})
.
Indeed,

\displaystyle\tilde{\nu}_{\mathcal{K},i}(c_{j})

\displaystyle=\int_{G}\mathrm{ReLU}(\mathcal{K}_{a_{2i},b_{2i}}(h_{1})-c_{j})d\mu(h_{1})

\displaystyle=\int_{G}\sum_{h\in H}\left(\mathrm{ReLU}\left(\left(a_{2i}\widehat{\mathcal{K}}(h)\mathds{1}_{h\cdot U}(h_{1})+b_{2i}\widehat{\mathcal{K}}(g^{-1}\cdot h)\cdot\mathds{1}_{h\cdot U}(h_{1})\right)-c_{j}\right)\right)d\mu(h_{1})

\displaystyle=\sum_{h\in H}\left(\int_{G}\mathrm{ReLU}\left(\left(a_{2i}\widehat{\mathcal{K}}(h)+b_{2i}\widehat{\mathcal{K}}(g^{-1}\cdot h)\right)\cdot\mathds{1}_{h\cdot U}(h_{1})-c_{j}\right)d\mu(h_{1})\right)

\displaystyle=\mu(U)\int_{G}\mathrm{ReLU}(u_{2i}*_{\mu_{1},G}\widehat{\mathcal{K}}-c_{j})(h)d\mu_{1}(h)=\mu(U)\cdot\nu_{\widehat{\mathcal{K}},i}(c_{j}),

where
\nu_{\widehat{\mathcal{K}},i}
is as in Definition
3.8
. The second equality holds since
a_{2i}\widehat{\mathcal{K}}(h)+b_{2i}\widehat{\mathcal{K}}(g^{-1}\cdot h)
is greater than
c_{j}
only for
h\in\widetilde{H}_{r}=\bigcup_{l\in[r]}H_{l}
;
the fourth equality holds since for a Haar measure
\mu(U)=\mu(h\cdot U)
.

From the proven equality, we can conclude that the order of
(\tilde{\nu}_{\mathcal{K},i}(c_{j}))_{i\in[m]}
is
o_{j}
for every
j\in[r]
.
Moreover, let

\displaystyle\tilde{m}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\min_{l\in[r],\ i,j\in[m],i\neq j}|\tilde{\nu}_{\mathcal{K},i}(c_{l})-\tilde{\nu}_{\mathcal{K},j}(c_{l})|

\displaystyle=\min_{l\in[r],\ i,j\in[m],i\neq j}|\nu_{\widehat{\mathcal{K}},i}(c_{l})-\nu_{\widehat{\mathcal{K}},j}(c_{l})|\cdot\mu(U)

\displaystyle=\min_{l\in[r],\ i,j\in[m],i\neq j}|\hat{\nu}_{\widehat{\mathcal{K}},i}(c_{l})-\hat{\nu}_{\widehat{\mathcal{K}},j}(c_{l})|\cdot\mu(U),

where
\hat{\nu}
is defined in Lemma
3.16
.

It holds that
\tilde{m}>0
, since by Remark
3.21

\min_{l\in[r],\ i,j\in[m],i\neq j}|\hat{\nu}_{\widehat{\mathcal{K}},i}(c_{l})-\hat{\nu}_{\widehat{\mathcal{K}},j}(c_{l})|>0.

We finish the proof, by modifying
u_{i},\ i\in[2m]
to yield
\widetilde{u}_{i}
,
i\in[2m]
, and showing that
\widetilde{u}_{2i}
,
i\in[m]
are shattered by
\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{K})
.

We claim that for every
\delta>0
there exists open sets
\widetilde{U}_{\delta}
such that for all
i\in[2m]

\displaystyle\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mu(\widetilde{U}_{\delta})^{-1}(a_{i}\mathds{1}_{\widetilde{U}_{\delta}^{-1}}+b_{i}\mathds{1}_{(g\cdot\widetilde{U}_{\delta})^{-1}})*_{G}\mathcal{K}-\mathcal{K}_{a_{i},b_{i}}\right\rVert}_{L^{1}}\leq\delta.

(48)

Before we prove (
48
), we show how it yields the claim. We fix

\delta\coloneqq\tilde{m}/{4},

and denote the functions
\mu(\widetilde{U}_{\delta})^{-1}(a_{i}\mathds{1}_{\widetilde{U}_{\delta}^{-1}}+b_{i}\mathds{1}_{(g\cdot\widetilde{U}_{\delta})^{-1}})*_{G}\mathcal{K}
as
\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}_{a_{i},b_{i}}
, and
\widetilde{u}_{i}\coloneqq\mu(\widetilde{U}_{\delta})^{-1}(a_{i}\mathds{1}_{\widetilde{U}_{\delta}^{-1}}+b_{i}\mathds{1}_{(g\cdot\widetilde{U}_{\delta})^{-1}})
.

We prove that
\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{K})
shatters
(\widetilde{u}_{2i})_{i\in[m]}
by showing that for each
j\in[r]
the order of
(\int_{G}\mathrm{ReLU}(\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}_{a_{2i},b_{2i}}(g)-c_{j})d\mu(g))_{i\in[m]}
is equal to
o_{j}
.
Let
i,j\in[m]
, and
l\in[r]
, such that
\tilde{\nu}_{\mathcal{K},i}(c_{l})-\tilde{\nu}_{\mathcal{K},j}(c_{l})\geq\tilde{m}>0
.
Then, we can conclude that

\displaystyle\int_{G}\mathrm{ReLU}(\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}_{a_{2i},b_{2i}}-c_{l})(h)d\mu(h)-\int_{G}\mathrm{ReLU}(\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}_{a_{2j},b_{2j}}-c_{l})(h)d\mu(h)

\displaystyle=\left(\int_{G}\mathrm{ReLU}(\mathcal{K}_{a_{2i},b_{2i}}-c_{l})(h)d\mu(h)-\int_{G}\mathrm{ReLU}(\mathcal{K}_{a_{2j},b_{2j}}-c_{l})(h)d\mu(h)\right)

\displaystyle\qquad-\left(\int_{G}\mathrm{ReLU}(\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}_{a_{2j},b_{2j}}-c_{l})(h)d\mu(h)-\int_{G}\mathrm{ReLU}(\mathcal{K}_{a_{2j},b_{2j}}-c_{l})(h)d\mu(h)\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\left(\int_{G}\mathrm{ReLU}(\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}_{a_{2i},b_{2i}}-c_{l})(h)d\mu(h)-\int_{G}\mathrm{ReLU}(\mathcal{K}_{a_{2i},b_{2i}}-c_{l})(h)d\mu(h)\right)

\displaystyle\geq\tilde{\nu}_{\mathcal{K},i}(c_{l})-\tilde{\nu}_{\mathcal{K},j}(c_{l})-\|\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}_{a_{2j},b_{2j}}-\mathcal{K}_{a_{2j},b_{2j}}\|_{L^{1}}-\|\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}_{a_{2i},b_{2i}}-\mathcal{K}_{a_{2i},b_{2i}}\|_{L^{1}}

\displaystyle\geq\tilde{m}-\frac{\tilde{m}}{4}-\frac{\tilde{m}}{4}=\frac{\tilde{m}}{2}>0,

where we used the linearity and monotonicity of the integral and the 1-Lipschitz property of the ReLU in the first inequality.

Thus, for each
j\in[r]
the order of
(\int_{G}\mathrm{ReLU}(\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}_{a_{2i},b_{2i}}(g)-c_{j})d\mu(g))_{i\in[m]}
is equal to the order of
(\tilde{\nu}_{\mathcal{K},i}(c_{j}))_{i\in[m]}
, which is equal to
o_{j}
.

We complete the proof by showing (
48
).
First note, that since
G
is assumed to be first-countable, there exists a sequence of neighborhoods of
e
, denoted by
(N_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
, such that

N_{1}\supset N_{2}\supset N_{3}\supset\dots

and, for all neighborhoods
Z
of
e
, there exists
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
N_{k}\subset Z
. As a consequence, we have that

\displaystyle\bigcap_{i=1}^{\infty}N_{i}=\{e\}.

(49)

Indeed, assuming that there exists
g\in G,g\neq e
with

g\in N_{i}
for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
yields a contradiction by invoking the Hausdorff property of
G
.
Concretely, by the Hausdorff property, we have that there exists
U
with
e\in U
,
g\not\in U
. Hence, there exists
k
such that
N_{k}\subset U
which implies
g\not\in N_{k}
and produces the contradiction.

Since
\mu
is finite, we conclude that
\mu(\{e\})=0
. Moreover, since
\mu
is a Borel measure, we have by (
49
) that

\mu(N_{k})\to 0
for
k\to\infty
.

In addition, it follows from the continuity of the multiplication that there exists
(Q_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
Q_{k}
is open,
Q_{k}\subset N_{k}
and
Q_{k}\cdot Q_{k}\subset N_{k}
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

Next, observe that to show (
48
), it follows by the triangle inequality, linearity of the convolution, and the translation invariance of the Haar measure, that it suffices to show that for every
\delta>0
there exists
\widetilde{U}_{\delta}
such that for all
|a|\leq A\in\mathbb{R}^{+}

\displaystyle\mathinner{\!\left\lVert a\mu(\widetilde{U}_{\delta})^{-1}\mathds{1}_{\widetilde{U}_{\delta}^{-1}}*_{G}\mathcal{K}-a\mathcal{K}\right\rVert}_{L^{1}}\leq\delta/2.

(50)

Since for finite Borel measures on a compact set
G
, the set of continuous functions is dense in
L^{1}(G)
(see
[
8
, Theorem 7.9]
), we can replace (also using Young’s convolution inequality)
\mathcal{K}
by a continuous approximation
\mathcal{K}^{\rm cont}\colon G\to\mathbb{R}
such that

\displaystyle\mathinner{\!\left\lVert a\mu(\widetilde{U}_{\delta})^{-1}\mathds{1}_{\widetilde{U}_{\delta}^{-1}}*_{G}\mathcal{K}-a\mathcal{K}\right\rVert}_{L^{1}}\leq\mathinner{\!\left\lVert a\mu(\widetilde{U}_{\delta})^{-1}\mathds{1}_{\widetilde{U}_{\delta}^{-1}}*_{G}\mathcal{K}^{\rm cont}-a\mathcal{K}^{\rm cont}\right\rVert}_{L^{1}}+\delta/4.

(51)

The proof is completed by observing that

\displaystyle\mathinner{\!\left\lVert a\mu(\widetilde{U}_{\delta})^{-1}\mathds{1}_{\widetilde{U}_{\delta}^{-1}}*_{G}\mathcal{K}^{\rm cont}-a\mathcal{K}^{\rm cont}\right\rVert}_{L^{1}}

\displaystyle=|a|\int_{G}\left|\mu(\widetilde{U}_{\delta})^{-1}\int_{\widetilde{U}_{\delta}\cdot g}\mathcal{K}^{\rm cont}(h)-\mathcal{K}^{\rm cont}(g)d\mu(h)\right|d\mu(g).

(52)

Since
\mathcal{K}^{\rm cont}
is continuous there exists for all
g\in G
an open set set
Q_{k,g}\ni e
such that
|\mathcal{K}^{\rm cont}(h)-\mathcal{K}^{\rm cont}(g)|\leq\delta/(8A\mu(G))
for all
h\in Q_{k,g}\cdot Q_{k,g}\cdot g
.
Since
(Q_{k,g}\cdot g)_{g\in G}
is an open cover of
G
, we can choose a finite subcover,
(Q_{k_{i},g_{i}}\cdot{g_{i}})_{i=1}^{K}
for
K\in\mathbb{N}
.

We set

\widetilde{U}_{\delta}=\bigcap_{i=1}^{K}Q_{k_{i},g_{i}}

and observe that
\widetilde{U}_{\delta}
is an open neighborhood of
e
. Moreover, per construction
\widetilde{U}_{\delta}\cdot{g}\subset Q_{k_{i},g_{i}}\cdot Q_{k_{i},g_{i}}\cdot{g_{i}}
for some
i\in[K]
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle|\mathcal{K}^{\rm cont}(h)-\mathcal{K}^{\rm cont}(g_{i})|\leq\delta/(8A\mu(G))

(53)

for all
h\in\widetilde{U}_{\delta}\cdot g
. Since (
53
) holds in particular for
h=g
, we conclude by the triangle inequality that

|\mathcal{K}^{\rm cont}(h)-\mathcal{K}^{\rm cont}(g)|\leq\delta/(4A\mu(G))

for all
h\in\widetilde{U}_{\delta}\cdot g
.

We conclude with (
52
) that

\displaystyle\mathinner{\!\left\lVert a\mu(\widetilde{U}_{\delta})^{-1}\mathds{1}_{\widetilde{U}_{\delta}^{-1}}*_{G}\mathcal{K}^{\rm cont}-a\mathcal{K}^{\rm cont}\right\rVert}_{L^{1}}\leq\delta/4,

which yields (
51
), and hence (
50
) which implies (
48
) and completes the proof.
∎
