Preparation of Fe STM tips for room-temperature spin-polarized tunneling spectroscopy
Jędrzej Tepper, Jan M. van Ruitenbeek

Abstract
We present a recipe based on field-directed Ar ion etching of iron tips that results in probes suitable for stable spin-polarized scanning tunneling spectroscopy. This is illustrated by a measurement of the spectroscopic mapping of the magnetization of Co islands on Cu(111).



I
Introduction

The data recorded during the operation of a scanning tunneling microscope (STM) critically depends on the atomic-scale structure and chemical composition of the outermost layer of the tip apex. Therefore, the procedure used to prepare the probe influences the probability of performing a successful STM experiment. Such procedures depend on the metal one selects as the tip material. Conventional STM probes are often made out of tungsten, due to its mechanical stability stemming from strong chemical bonds inside the material. Another popular choice is a platinum/iridium alloy, which is well suited for microscopes operating in non-vacuum conditions, due to its chemical stability. One of the more recent extensions of the original method conceived by Binning and Rohrer
Binnig et al. 1982
consists of using magnetic tips to study local spin-dependent properties.
Wiesendanger et al. 1990
;
Wulfhekel and Kirschner 1999
This work presents a method of preparing bulk iron tips for spin-polarized scanning tunneling spectroscopy (SP-STS) at room temperature.

II
Room-temperature SP-STS requirements

STM probes with straight tip apices with a radius below 50 nm can be considered an adequate structure that excludes any detrimental tip effects on the acquired topography in the greater majority of STM experiments. However, the requirements towards reproducible spectroscopy measurements extend beyond this geometrical prerequisite. Scanning tunneling spectroscopy (STS) measurements are especially demanding when performed at room temperature due to the increased mobility of the surface atoms constituting the probe. Despite using metal wires of high purity in the electrochemical etching, the external layer of the tip responsible for conducting current in STM consists of oxides and non-metallic residues. This can be inspected at the relevant scale using transmission electron microscopy (TEM), which is often not trivial. However, using distance/bias dependence (z/V) spectroscopy in STM, one can easily verify that tips etched electrochemically out of iron wire, upon introduction to an ultra-high vacuum (UHV) system and after water removal, do not form junctions exhibiting metal-vacuum-metal tunneling characteristics. Instead, significant semi-conducting behavior is observed. Widely used tip-sample interaction procedures inside the UHV STM, such as indenting of the sample surface with the tip,
Tewari et al. 2017
or field evaporation,
Chen 2007
can be used to expose a clean metal apex. However, our experience shows that the stability of the junctions formed in such a way is often unsuitable for spectroscopy measurements at room temperature. We suspect that remnants of the electrochemical process migrate towards the apex during the operation of the STM.

III
Dry etching results

Various techniques have been developed to mitigate this issue, such as ion milling of a rotating tip at an angle,
Biegelsen et al. 1987
similar to a pencil sharpener. In the recent work by Schmucker
et al.

Schmucker et al. 2012
, the effects are studied of on-axis Ar ion bombardment on the electrochemically etched tungsten and platinum/iridium tips, kept at different potentials. This approach allows for spatially selective deflection of the incoming ions, as the electric field gradient around the sharpest structure will be higher than that around the tip shaft. The effectiveness of this method applied to the tips made out of iron is studied here. Whereas Schmucker
et al.
optimized the technique for sharpening the tip apex that can be employed for nanopatterning, in our work we focus on preserving the apex while simultaneously removing remnants of the etching process to produce stable junctions suitable for SP-STS at room temperature.

Our first study was aimed at determining the scale at which material removal takes place during the process. Tips, produced by means of electrochemical etching,

). At the lowest magnification, the results for the dry etching are similar, and any influence of the applied deflection potential cannot be distinguished. We only observe a general smoothing of the electrochemically etched surface. However, the effects of the deflection potential are clear at ten times higher magnification, where the tip etched without deflection can no longer be considered a good candidate for STM experiments, given its increased apex radius. It can be concluded that for 5keV Ar ions, 500V is enough to protect the sharpest structure formed in the electrochemical process against blunting by the ion bombardment.

This observation was utilized in the next experiment (Fig.
3
), where subsequent 5keV Ar ion treatments were applied with a gradual reduction of deflection. Surprisingly, the initially cleaned apex structure survived all subsequent etching steps with only minor changes, and no significant blunting or sharpening was observed, indicating mechanical stability of the apex cleaned with the initial deflection potential.

The amount of material displaced in the sputtering process depends on the kinetic energy of the ions. Gradual reduction of this parameter, akin to grit sizes of sanding paper, removes smaller and smaller volumes of material upon impact. This property was exploited in a subsequent experiment, where an electrochemically etched apex is subjected to ion bombardment with gradually decreasing kinetic energy and no deflection (Fig.
4
). Not only did this treatment preserve the majority of the apex but also created nano-asperities. Subsequent etching with high deflecting potential and again gradually decreasing energy preserved and smoothened the entire nano-tip created during the first etching. Although this procedure produces sharp apex structures which may be useful for applications such as tip-induced sample modifications, the stability of junctions formed with such tips is insufficient for our purposes.

Another promising approach could be to first blunt or remove the stressed apex created during the drop-off phase of electrochemical etching and afterward use dry etching to sharpen it in a controlled manner. An experiment utilizing 2.2µA of 2keV Ar ions applied for 2 hours without deflecting potential showed that removal of the thin and poorly supported apex is possible. However, efforts to sharpen such created blunt tip using 1µA of 5keV Ar ions with 100V deflection potential for an hour showed little promise for this approach. The subsequent treatment with the same parameters except for the lowered kinetic energy of ions (2.5keV) did not change the outlook, and the tip did not perform well in STM experiments.

IV
Conclusions

Our investigations show that for dry etching applied after electrochemical etching without deflecting potential and with decreasing energies ultra-sharp asperities can be created. Those can be smoothed with the same approach but this time utilizing deflection. Dry etching with sufficient deflecting potential always results in, both, preservation of the electrochemically etched apex as well as cleaning of its outer surface. The best results have been obtained with the highest available ion energy, that is 5keV, and 500V deflecting potential. The structure formed by such a process exhibited only minor changes in subsequent dry etchings without deflection and no detrimental apex blunting could be observed.

In summary, on-axis Ar ion etching of electrochemically etched iron tips kept at different potentials has been studied. We obtain an improvement of the junction stability for the tips that have been subjected to a dry etching procedure before introduction to UHV-STM. This treatment removes the semiconducting tunneling behavior observed after electrochemical etching. Our finding suggests that any residue left by the electrochemical etching is more problematic than the one left after dry etching and subsequent transfer through ambient conditions.
