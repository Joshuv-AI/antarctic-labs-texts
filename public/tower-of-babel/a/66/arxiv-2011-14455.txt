On the Density arising from the Domain of Attraction of an Operator interpolating between Sum and Supremum: the α \alpha -Sun Operator
N.S. Witte, P.E. Greenwood

Abstract
We explore the analytic properties of the density function h ⁡ ( x , γ , α ) h(x;\gamma,\alpha) , x ∈ ( 0 , ∞ ) x\in(0,\infty) , γ > 0 \gamma>0 , 0 < α < 1 0<\alpha<1 which arises as a normed limit from the domain of attraction problem for an operator interpolating between the supremum and sum as applied to a sequence of i.i.d. non-negative random variables. The parameter α \alpha controls the interpolation between these two cases, while γ \gamma further parametrises the type of distribution from which the underlying random variables are drawn. It is known that in the normed limit, for α = 0 \alpha=0 the Fréchet density arises, whereas for α = 1 \alpha=1 the limit is a stable random variable which has the well-known identification with a particular Fox H-function. It is known [ 21 ] that for intermediate α \alpha an entirely new distribution function appears, which is not one of the extensions to the hypergeometric function considered to date. Here we derive series, integral and continued fraction representations of this latter function.

Our Motivation

Consider a sequence of non-negative independent identically distributed random variables
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots
.
Define the sequence
Y_{0},Y_{1},Y_{2},\ldots
by

Y_{n}=\max\{Y_{n-1},\alpha Y_{n-1}+X_{n}\},\quad n\geq 1,\quad Y_{0}=X_{0}\in\mathbb{R}.

(1.1)

For
\alpha=0
we have the supremum,
Y_{n}=\max\{X_{0},X_{1},X_{2},\ldots,X_{n}\}
,
while for
\alpha=1
we have the sum,
Y_{n}=X_{0}+\sum^{n}_{j=1}X_{j}\mathbb{I}_{[0,\infty)}(X_{j})
.
We see that the sequence (
1.1
), termed an
\alpha
-Sun sequence, interpolates between the sum and the supremum
of a sequence of i.i.d. random variables via the parameter
\alpha
.
When
\alpha=1
, if a normed limit of the sequence
Y_{n}
exists, the limit is a stable law,
and we say the underlying distribution is in the domain of attraction of a stable distribution.
When
\alpha=0
, if a normed limit of
Y_{n}
exists, the limit is a Fréchet extreme value distribution,
and we say the underlying distribution is in the "sup" domain of attraction of a Fréchet law,
\Phi_{\gamma}(x)=\exp(-x^{-\gamma})\mathbb{I}_{[0,\infty)}(x)
.
In their study of (
1.1
), Greenwood and Hooghiemsta [
[
21
]
, Theorem 2, Eq. 2.4]
have shown that the domain of attraction of the
\alpha
-Sun operator is the same as that for the sup operator,
i.e. it is using the same collection of underlying distributions and the same norming sequences as for the sup operator,
that the normed
\alpha
-Sun sequences converges weakly.
The non-degenerate limiting law satisfies a limiting distribution with a density
h
.

There is a compelling motivation for our model (
1.1
).
Many decisions which we take are of the following form:
Accept the status quo, in which case
Y_{n}=Y_{n-1}
,
or risk losing some portion of what we have, in the process of taking some action,
so that the value of our current state is reduced to a portion
\alpha Y_{n-1}
,
in which case we can increase our value by a stochastic
X_{n}
to obtain
Y_{n}=\alpha Y_{n-1}+X_{n}
.
Suppose we are so lucky or wise that we can evaluate
X_{n}
before taking the decision whether to act or not.
Then we can maximize the outcome by formulating our decision as per (
1.1
).
Historically this model first arose in descriptions of the storage of energy in a solar collection system,
see Haslett
[
24
]
and the Introduction of
[
30
]
for some of the early work on this problem.

In their study of this model - the
\alpha
-Sun process - Greenwood and Hooghiemstra
[
21
, Theorem 2, Eq. (2.4)]

have shown that there exist normalising constants
a_{n}>0
,
b_{n}\in\mathbb{R}

such that
(Y_{n}-b_{n})/a_{n}
converges weakly to a non-degenerate limiting distribution which
has a density
h(x;\gamma,\alpha)
satisfying a linear, homogeneous integral equation

h(x)=\frac{\gamma}{x}\int_{0}^{x}du\;\frac{h(u)}{(x-\alpha u)^{\gamma}},

(1.2)

subject to the conditions:
\alpha\in(0,1)
,
\gamma\in(0,\infty)
identifies the domain of attraction as above,
and
h(x)
is a real, normalised probability density on
x\in(0,\infty)
.
In our study we investigate the solutions
h(x)=h(x;\gamma,\alpha)
of this integral equation.
We will see towards the end of this paper (see Remark
3.5
) that the
\alpha
-Sun process,
for all
\alpha
in
(0,1)
, behaves in a way that is much more like the supremum of the input variables than like the sum.
There is a discontinuity of behaviour exactly when
\alpha
becomes unity.

Our central result gives a Mellin-Barnes integral representation for
h(x)
, (
3.41
) in Prop.
3.9
,
which is a standard way of defining many special functions, such as the Meijer-G and Fox-H functions.
Under the conditions
0\leq\alpha<1
,
\gamma>0
,
0<x<\infty
the density
h(x;\gamma,\alpha)
has the representation for any

h(x)=\frac{\gamma}{x}\int_{c-i\infty}^{c+i\infty}\frac{dt}{2\pi i}x^{-\gamma t}\Gamma(1-t)(1-\alpha)^{-\gamma t}\prod_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{{}_{2}F_{1}(\gamma,(j-t)\gamma;1+(j-t)\gamma;\alpha)}{{}_{2}F_{1}(\gamma,j\gamma;1+j\gamma;\alpha)}.

(1.3)

In a subsequent work
[
29

, Hooghiemstra and Greenwood took up the problem of finding the remaining normed limit distributions and their domains of
attraction for the
\alpha
-Sun operator applied to sequences of i.i.d. random sequences on all of

They found that there are two additional possibilities, corresponding to the two additional extreme value distributions.
The two classes of functions and the norming constants are again the same as for the two additional extreme value distributions,

2.1
Case
\alpha=0
: the Fréchet and Weibull distributions

In this case (

(2.1)

A simple differentiation of this yields the ordinary differential equation

The solution for the case

In the general case we recall the Mellin transform of the density

Proposition 3.1
.

For
0<\alpha<1
,
\gamma>0
and
\Re(s)<1+\gamma
the retarded Mellin transform
H(s;\gamma,\alpha)
satisfies the linear, homogeneous functional equation

H(s)=\frac{\gamma}{1+\gamma-s}{}_{2}F_{1}(\gamma,1+\gamma-s;2+\gamma-s;\alpha)H(s-\gamma),

(3.2)

where
{}_{2}F_{1}(a,b;c;z)
is the Gauss hypergeometric function
[
53
, §15]
.

Proof.

Taking the Mellin transform of both sides of (
1.2
) we compute

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}dx\;x^{s-1}h(x)

\displaystyle=\gamma\int_{0}^{\infty}dx\;x^{s-2}\int_{0}^{x}du\;\frac{h(u)}{(x-\alpha u)^{\gamma}},

\displaystyle=\gamma\int_{0}^{\infty}du\;h(u)\int_{u}^{\infty}dx\;\frac{x^{s-2}}{(x-\alpha u)^{\gamma}},

\displaystyle=\gamma\int_{0}^{\infty}du\;h(u)u^{s-1-\gamma}\int_{1}^{\infty}dx\;\frac{x^{s-2}}{(x-\alpha)^{\gamma}}.

The nested integral exists only for
\Re(s)<1+\gamma
and the integrand permits an expansion about
\alpha=0
,
which can be integrated term-by-term to yield

\int_{1}^{\infty}dx\;\frac{x^{s-2}}{(x-\alpha)^{\gamma}}=\frac{1}{1+\gamma-s}\sum^{\infty}_{k=0}\frac{(\gamma)_{k}(1+\gamma-s)_{k}}{k!(2+\gamma-s)_{k}}\alpha^{k}.

This allows us to make the identification with the stated Gauss hypergeometric function and (
3.2
).

From its definition (
1.12
)
H(s)
exists for
s
in the strip
a<\Re(s)<b
, for some
a<b
.
Due to the existence of the norm we have
a<1<b
.
In addition
H(s-\gamma)
exists for
a<\Re(s)-\gamma<b
, or
a+\gamma<\Re(s)<b+\gamma
.
There must be a common overlap of these two intervals so we conclude
a+\gamma<b
.
Exploiting all of the above inequalities we find that
b<1+\gamma
and
1-\gamma<a
and so
in summary we deduce
1-\gamma<a<1<b<1+\gamma
.
∎

3.1
On the nature of the
{}_{2}F_{1}(\gamma,1+\gamma-s;2+\gamma-s;\alpha)

Firstly with regard to the analytical character of this hypergeometric function with respect to

(a)

For

{}_{2}F_{1}
is convergent for all
\Re(s)<1+\gamma
.
The
{}_{2}F_{1}
has simple poles on the right at

.

(b)

For
|\alpha|=1
we require in addition that
\Re(\gamma)<1

The poles are the same as for
|\alpha|<1
,

(c)

Our
{}_{2}F_{1}

\alpha=0

that is bounded as

(by
(1-\alpha)^{-\gamma}
).
This distinguishes itself from the other member of the pair which has the simple algebraic form

\alpha^{s-\gamma-1}
, and which diverges as
s\to-\infty

This hypergeometric function can be linearly mapped into other forms via the Kummer or linear fractional transformations of the
\alpha
-plane,
and we record three versions
[

, 15.8.1]

\displaystyle{}_{2}F_{1}(\gamma,1+\gamma-s;2+\gamma-s;\alpha)=(1-\alpha)^{-\gamma}

\displaystyle{}_{2}F_{1}(\gamma,1;2+\gamma-s;\tfrac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}),

\displaystyle=(1-\alpha)^{s-\gamma-1}

Proposition 3.2

.
The finite product
G_{l}
, defined by (
3.24
), can be written as an analytic function of complex
\Re(l)>1+\gamma^{-1}
in an infinite product form

G_{l}=(1-\alpha)^{\gamma l}\prod_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{F_{j+l}}{F_{j}},

(3.37)

and its interpolating function is

G(-t)=(1-\alpha)^{-\gamma t}\prod_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{F_{j-t}}{F_{j}}.

(3.38)

A useful relation, to be used later, is

F_{-t}\prod_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{F_{j-t}}{F_{j}}=F_{\infty}\prod_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{F_{j-t}}{F_{j}}.

(3.39)

Proof.

To show convergence of the product,
[
34
, §29, Satz 5, s. 228]
, we compute bounds for

\left|\frac{F_{j+l}}{F_{j}}-1\right|
.
The factors can be written (see (
3.5
))

F_{j}=(1-\alpha)^{-\gamma}-\alpha\gamma\int_{0}^{1}du\;u^{j\gamma}(1-\alpha u)^{-\gamma-1}.

Then we note

\displaystyle\left|\frac{F_{j+l}}{F_{j}}-1\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha\gamma(1-\alpha)^{\gamma}\int_{0}^{1}du\;(1-\alpha u)^{-\gamma-1}[u^{j\gamma}-u^{(j+l)\gamma}]}{\left|1-\alpha\gamma(1-\alpha)^{\gamma}\int_{0}^{1}du\;(1-\alpha)^{-\gamma-1}u^{j\gamma}\right|},

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\alpha\gamma(1-\alpha)^{-1}\int_{0}^{1}du\;[u^{j\gamma}-u^{(j+l)\gamma}]}{\left|1-\alpha\gamma(1-\alpha)^{-1}\int_{0}^{1}du\;u^{j\gamma}\right|},

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha\gamma^{2}l}{\left[1+(j+l)\gamma\right]\left[1-\alpha(1+\gamma)+j\gamma(1-\alpha)\right]}

\displaystyle=\text{${\rm O}(j^{-2})$ as $j\to\infty$},

uniformly for
l,\alpha,\gamma
under the conditions stated.

We recall
\lim_{j\to\infty}F_{j}=F_{\infty}=(1-\alpha)^{-\gamma}
is well defined.
The situation we have is that we take
N\to\infty
for fixed
l
so

\displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^{N}\frac{F_{j+l}}{F_{j}}

\displaystyle=\frac{F_{N+1}\ldots F_{N+l}}{F_{1}\ldots F_{l}},\quad\text{as $N\geq l+1$},

\displaystyle\mathop{\to}\limits_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{F_{1}\ldots F_{l}}\lim_{N\to\infty}F_{N+1}\ldots F_{N+l},

\displaystyle=F_{\infty}^{l}G_{l},

and (
3.37
) follows.
Now set
l\mapsto l-1
or
t\mapsto t+1
in (
3.38
)

\displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^{N}\frac{F_{j-1+l}}{F_{j}}

\displaystyle=\frac{F_{N+1}\ldots F_{N+l-1}}{F_{1}\ldots F_{l-1}},\quad\text{as $N\geq l$},

\displaystyle\mathop{\to}\limits_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{F_{1}\ldots F_{l-1}}\lim_{N\to\infty}F_{N+1}\ldots F_{N+l-1},

\displaystyle=F_{\infty}^{l-1}G_{l-1},

\displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{F_{j-1+l}}{F_{j}}

\displaystyle=\frac{F_{l}}{F_{\infty}}\prod_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{F_{j+l}}{F_{j}}.

Thus (
3.39
) also follows.
∎

In summary we have our penultimate result where the generating function and density are shown to possess integral representations.

Proposition 3.9
.

Let
0\leq\alpha<1-\delta
,
\delta>0
.
The generating function
g(x;\gamma,\alpha)
has the Mellin-Barnes integral representation
0<c<1+\gamma^{-1}

g(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{c-i\infty}^{c+i\infty}dt\;x^{-t}\Gamma(t)(1-\alpha)^{-\gamma t}\prod_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{{}_{2}F_{1}(\gamma,(j-t)\gamma;1+(j-t)\gamma;\alpha)}{{}_{2}F_{1}(\gamma,j\gamma;1+j\gamma;\alpha)},

(3.40)

and consequently the density
h(x;\gamma,\alpha)
has a similar representation
c<1

h(x)=\frac{\gamma}{x}\int_{c-i\infty}^{c+i\infty}\frac{dt}{2\pi i}x^{-\gamma t}\Gamma(1-t)(1-\alpha)^{-\gamma t}\prod_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{{}_{2}F_{1}(\gamma,(j-t)\gamma;1+(j-t)\gamma;\alpha)}{{}_{2}F_{1}(\gamma,j\gamma;1+j\gamma;\alpha)}.

(3.41)

The Mellin transform of the density
H(s;\gamma,\alpha)
is

H(s)=(1-\alpha)^{1-s}\Gamma\left(\frac{1+\gamma-s}{\gamma}\right)\prod_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{{}_{2}F_{1}(\gamma,1-s+j\gamma;2-s+j\gamma;\alpha)}{{}_{2}F_{1}(\gamma,j\gamma;1+j\gamma;\alpha)}.

(3.42)

Proof.

The exponential generating function sum can be written as a Mellin-Barnes inversion integral of (
3.27
)

g(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{c-i\infty}^{c+i\infty}dt\;x^{-t}\Gamma(t)G(-t),

(3.43)

given convergence of the integrand as
\Im(t)\to\pm\infty
on the vertical contour
\Re(t)=c
.
The second formula follows from the first under the absolute convergence of both integrals and the validity of the integral

\int^{\infty}_{0}du\;u^{1-2t}J_{0}(zu)=2^{1-2t}z^{2t-2}\frac{\Gamma(1-t)}{\Gamma(t)},

when
\frac{3}{4}<\Re(t)<1
.
∎

Second Proof.

There is a simple and direct way to verify (
3.41
).
For generic

the full set of singularities of the integrand in (

a sequence of simple poles due to

at

and a double sequence of simple poles due to the denominator factor in

Now we substitute (

{\rm RHS}=\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2\pi ix}\int_{c-i\infty}^{c+i\infty}dt\;\Gamma(1-t)(1-\alpha)^{-\gamma t}\prod_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{F_{j-t}}{F_{j}}\int_{0}^{x}du\;(x-\alpha u)^{-\gamma}u^{-1-\gamma t},

where we have abbreviated the parameter dependence of the hypergeometric functions and employed the absolute and uniform convergence of the

The

Because

∎

Remark 3.5
.

The observations on the large
N
behaviour of the products in Prop.
3.5
and the integral representations given above
show that the previous Proposition does not hold in the neighbourhood of
\alpha=1
.
When
\alpha=1
the finite product has the following evaluation

\displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^{L}\frac{{}_{2}F_{1}(\gamma,(j-t)\gamma;1+(j-t)\gamma;1)}{{}_{2}F_{1}(\gamma,j\gamma;1+j\gamma;1)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-t\gamma)}\frac{\Gamma(L\gamma+1-t\gamma)}{\Gamma(L\gamma+1)},

\displaystyle\mathop{\sim}\limits_{L\to\infty}\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-t\gamma)}\left(L\gamma+\frac{1-t\gamma}{2}\right)^{-t\gamma},

(3.44)

and so the limit vanishes.
Furthermore we know from (
3.13
) that the inverse Mellin transform shows, for
c<1+\gamma
,

h_{1}(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{c-i\infty}^{c+i\infty}ds\;x^{-s}\left(\Gamma(1-\gamma)\right)^{(s-1)/\gamma}\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{\displaystyle 1+\gamma-s}{\displaystyle\gamma}\right)}{\Gamma(2-s)}.

(3.45)

Clearly there is a transition regime from
\alpha<1
to
\alpha=1
where the limiting behaviour as
L\to\infty

crosses over from one to the other - a so-called "double scaling limit".
We can explicitly demonstrate this with the following matching procedure: using (
3.44
) the relevant part of the integrand of (
3.41
) is

(1-\alpha)^{-\gamma t}\prod_{j=1}^{L}\frac{F_{j-t}}{F_{j}}\mathop{\sim}\limits_{L\to\infty\atop\alpha\uparrow 1}\frac{\left[\gamma(1-\alpha)L\right]^{-\gamma t}}{\Gamma(1-t\gamma)}.

(3.46)

Now if we change variables
t=(s-1)/\gamma
the integrand of (
3.41
) will match that of (
3.45
) if we simultaneously take

L\to\infty
with
\alpha\to 1^{\uparrow}
such that the product

(1-\alpha)L=\frac{1}{\gamma\left(\Gamma(1-\gamma)\right)^{1/\gamma}},

is fixed.

3.6
The large
\gamma
case

A particularly instructive case of our foregoing analysis is when
\gamma
is real and large whilst
0<\alpha<1
.
In this way we have a simple example where
\alpha
is explicitly exhibited.
It is apparent that in the opposite case of
\gamma\to 0
only the trivial solution of
h(x)
with Dirac-atomic measure at the origin is admissible.
In this regime the leading order behaviour of the hypergeometric function is
[
48
]
,
[
11
]
is

{}_{2}F_{1}(a+\epsilon\lambda,b;c+\lambda;z)\sim\left(1-\epsilon z\right)^{-b},\quad|\lambda|\to\infty,

(3.47)

excluding the region around the singular point
z_{S}=1/\epsilon
.
In our application we require the connection formula

{}_{2}F_{1}(\gamma,(k-t)\gamma;1+(k-t)\gamma;\alpha)=(1-\alpha)^{-\gamma}{}_{2}F_{1}(\gamma,1;1+(k-t)\gamma;-\tfrac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}),

(3.48)

with
\lambda=(k-t)\gamma
and
0<\epsilon=1/(k-t)<1
so our singular point is

\alpha_{S}=1+1/(k-1-t)>1,

(3.49)

which is excluded from our consideration. Using the definition
3.1
we have

F_{j}\mathop{\sim}\limits_{\gamma\to\infty}(1-\alpha)^{-\gamma}\left[1+\frac{1}{j}\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\right]^{-1},\quad j\geq 1.

(3.50)

However in the works cited above, and other literature treating the asymptotic expansions of this hypergeometric function,
no bounds or estimates are given for the error term in (
3.50
).
We address this gap with the following lemma.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
0\leq\alpha<1
,
j\in\mathbb{N}
. Then for
\gamma\in[0,\infty)
the hypergeometric function
F_{j}
possesses the asymptotic expansion
as
\gamma\to\infty

F_{j}=(1-\alpha)^{-\gamma}\left\{\left[1+\frac{1}{j}\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\right]^{-1}+{\rm O}(\gamma^{-1})\right\}.

(3.51)

Proof.

Using the Apostol-Lerch integral representation of our hypergeometric function (
3.3
), (
3.4
),
or making the change of variable
u=e^{-x}
in Euler’s integral formula for the hypergeometric function, we observe

F_{j}=j\gamma\int_{0}^{\infty}dx\;e^{-j\gamma x}\left(1-\alpha e^{-x}\right)^{-\gamma}.

(3.52)

Defining
e^{\phi(x)}:=e^{-jx}\left(1-\alpha e^{-x}\right)^{-1}
we note that
e^{\phi(x)}
is real and monotone, decreasing on
[0,\infty)

so that
\phi(x)
has no stationary points on this interval. The only one occurs at
x_{0}=-\log(\frac{j}{\alpha(j-1)})<0
.
Integrating by parts we find

F_{j}=(1-\alpha)^{-\gamma}\left[1+\frac{1}{j}\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\right]^{-1}+j\int_{0}^{\infty}dx\;e^{\gamma\phi(x)}\frac{\phi^{{}^{\prime\prime}}(x)}{\left(\phi^{{}^{\prime}}(x)\right)^{2}},

(3.53)

along with

\frac{\phi^{{}^{\prime\prime}}(x)}{\left(\phi^{{}^{\prime}}(x)\right)^{2}}=\frac{\alpha e^{-x}}{\left[j-(j-1)\alpha e^{-x}\right]^{2}}.

Employing the bounds

e^{\phi(x)}\leq(1-\alpha)^{-1}e^{-jx},\qquad\frac{\phi^{{}^{\prime\prime}}(x)}{\left(\phi^{{}^{\prime}}(x)\right)^{2}}\leq\alpha e^{-x}\left[j-(j-1)\alpha\right]^{-2},

in the second term of (
3.53
), we deduce that this error term is bounded by

\frac{\alpha j}{\left[j-(j-1)\alpha\right]^{2}}\frac{(1-\alpha)^{-\gamma}}{1+j\gamma}.

Thus (
3.51
) follows.
∎

With this knowledge one can easy verify the following results.

Proposition 3.10
.

For
0\leq\alpha<1
and

the hypergeometric product

Consequently the generating function is given by the confluent hypergeometric function at leading order in the asymptotic expansion

This hypergeometric function is an entire function of

and

with exponential decay as

.
The Mellin transform of the generating function is

\mathcal{M}[g(x);t]=(1-\alpha)^{-\gamma t}\Gamma(t)\left\{\frac{\Gamma(\frac{1}{1-\alpha}-t)}{\Gamma(1-t)\Gamma(\frac{1}{1-\alpha})}+{\rm O}(\gamma^{-1})\right\},

for

, using Oberhettinger’s tables
[

G(t)=(1-\alpha)^{\gamma t}\left\{\frac{\Gamma(\frac{1}{1-\alpha}+t)}{\Gamma(1+t)\Gamma(\frac{1}{1-\alpha})}+{\rm O}(\gamma^{-1})\right\}.

Proof.

The hypergeometric product (

)
and the generating function result (

in the error term.

One can also solve the functional equation (

Expanding the coefficient of this equation to sufficient order for large

\frac{\gamma}{1+\gamma-s}{}_{2}F_{1}(\gamma,1+\gamma-s;2+\gamma-s;\alpha)\\

∎

Remark 3.6
.

Inverting the Mellin transform (

h_{\gamma}(x)=\frac{\gamma(1-\alpha)^{-\frac{\gamma-\alpha}{1-\alpha}}}{\Gamma\left(\frac{1-\alpha/\gamma}{1-\alpha}\right)}x^{-1-\frac{\gamma-\alpha}{1-\alpha}}\exp\left[-(1-\alpha)^{-\gamma}x^{-\gamma}\right].

Clearly this result is invalid for
\gamma\leq\alpha
which, of course, is expressly ruled out by virtue of our assumptions.

The density exhibits a tail as

with algebraic decay, having the leading form

for all

and

.
For non-rational

with computable coefficients

The coefficients

3.7
Continued Fraction Forms

Our final representation of
g(x)
is as a continued fraction,
the utility of which can lie in the fact that they may have better convergence properties than series or products forms.
In our case the continued fraction form is best expressed in terms of a related function rather than
g

Definition 3.3
.

Let
L(z;\gamma,\alpha)
denote the Laplace transform of the generating function
g(x)
, for
\Re(z)>0

L(z):=\mathcal{L}\left[g(x);z\right]=\int_{0}^{\infty}dx\;e^{-zx}g(x).

(3.65)

Thus we have the convergent expansion due to (
3.25
) for
|z|>(1-\alpha)^{\gamma}

L(z)=\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{l}}{\prod_{j=0}^{l}F_{j}}z^{-l-1}.

(3.66)

Our definition is also motivated by a relation to the Stieltjes moment problem.
Setting
X
for the positive random variable with density
h(x)
, the power transform
Y=X^{-\gamma}
has the exponential moment generating function

\mathbb{E}[e^{zY}]=1+\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{H_{k}z^{k}}{k!}

and in particular the random variable
Y
is characterized by its integer moments. The above moment generating function is given by

\mathbb{E}[e^{zY}]=-z^{-1}L(-z^{-1}),

as follows from (
3.66
).

Proposition 3.11
.

L(z)
is a special
M
-continued fraction, see
[
10
, pg. 38, Chap. 1]

\frac{1}{L(z)}=z+\vbox{\hbox{\huge$\mathrm{K}$}}_{m=1}^{\infty}\cfrac{\displaystyle F_{m-1}z}{\displaystyle F_{m}z-1},

(3.67)

where our notation is equivalent to the alternative forms

b_{0}+\vbox{\hbox{\huge$\mathrm{K}$}}^{\infty}_{j=1}\frac{a_{j}}{b_{j}}=b_{0}+\cfrac{a_{1}}{b_{1}+\cfrac{a_{2}}{b_{2}+\cfrac{a_{3}}{b_{3}+\ldots}}}=b_{0}+\frac{a_{1}}{b_{1}}\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{}{+}\frac{a_{2}}{b_{2}}\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{}{+}\frac{a_{3}}{b_{3}}\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{}{+\ldots}.

\gamma\to\infty
the leading term of the Laplace transform has the algebraic form

L_{\gamma}(z)=z^{-1}\left(1+(1-\alpha)^{\gamma}z^{-1}\right)^{-\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}+z^{-1}e^{-(1-\alpha)^{\gamma}z^{-1}}{\rm O}(\gamma^{-1}).

(3.72)

This can be established from the convergent expansion (
3.66
) about
z=\infty
using (
3.54
) or
computing the Laplace transform of (
3.55
) with the assistance of
[
14
, pg. 215, §2.2 (11)]
assuming
\Re(z)>0
.
Therefore we note the M-continued fraction form

\frac{1}{L_{\gamma}(z)}=z+\vbox{\hbox{\huge$\mathrm{K}$}}_{m=1}^{\infty}\cfrac{\frac{(m-1)}{(m-1+\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha})}(1-\alpha)^{-\gamma}z}{\frac{m}{(m+\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha})}(1-\alpha)^{-\gamma}z-1}.

(3.73)

3.41

5
Discussion

We pick up here the thread in Section §
1
concerning related works in the probability literature.
Schlather
[
58
]
treats a different operation from the one considered in
[
21
]
,
[
29
]
or here
in that he takes a
1/p
root of a sum of
p^{\rm th}
powers.
In addition to being an altogether different problem producing a very different solution, he employs a different method.
For example he finds a different family of limit distributions parametrised by
c
with
0\leq c\leq\infty
.
And he discusses domains of attraction,
but with norming depending on
c
(unlike in the foregoing works) and of course different limits.

We recently learned
[
60
]
that the
\alpha
-Sun limit distributions
on
\mathbb{R}^{+}
satisfying (
1.1
) and represented by (
1.3
),
0<\alpha<1
, are infinitely divisible.
This is not unexpected since this property is long-known for the Fréchet (
\alpha=0
) and for the stable (
\alpha=1
) distributions of normed limits,
and for the generalised stable laws.
The ingenious proof in
[
60
]
for the
\alpha
-Sun case uses the relatively recent result of
[
4
]
that the class (GGC) of generalized gamma
convolutions is stable under independent multiplication.
A remaining challenge would be to find a more straight-forward proof, see e.g.
[
19
]
.
A useful overview of infinite divisibility arguments for classes of density functions up to 2014 is in
[
47
]
.

For
\alpha=1
we have recovered a known identification of
h(x;\gamma,\alpha)
as a Fox
H
-function,
and we have mentioned some previous works
[
59
]
,
[
47
]
,
[
33
]
where there is a nice statistical interpretation of this fact.
However the sequence (
1.1
) is in the domain of attraction of the normal
distribution for
\alpha=1
, so there is a “discontinuity":
(
1.1
) interpolates between Fréchet and normal, whereas (
1.2
) interpolates between Fréchet and
this Fox
H
-function.

Regarding the nature of the generic solution
h(x;\gamma,\alpha)
we have found here,
it appears that this is a novel extension of the hypergeometric function to the best of our knowledge.
Standard extensions through the Mittag-Leffler, Wright-Bessel and Meijer-
G
functions to the Fox H-functions clearly appear when

\alpha=1
, but not for
0<\alpha<1
.
The clearest way to distinguish the generic functions found here is to think of the standard hypergeometric or Fox-H function as series

H\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}(A_{1},a_{1})&\ldots&(A_{P},a_{P})\\
(B_{1},b_{1})&\ldots&(B_{Q},b_{Q})\end{array};x\right)=\sum_{n\geq 0}C_{n}x^{n},

and that the coefficients
C_{n}
satisfy a first order difference equation with respect to
n
,
in unit steps in the forward direction
n\mapsto n+1
,

C_{n+1}=\frac{(A_{1}+a_{1}n)\ldots(A_{P}+a_{P}n)}{(B_{1}+b_{1}n)\ldots(B_{Q}+b_{Q}n)}C_{n},\\
\left\{A_{1},a_{1},\ldots,A_{P},a_{P},B_{1},b_{1},\ldots,B_{Q},b_{Q}\right\}\in\mathbb{C},

i.e. rational coefficients in
n
.
Such equations can be solved in terms of products of gamma functions or Pochhammer symbols given suitable boundary conditions.
However in our generic case the coefficient of the first order difference equation is itself a hypergeometric function of
n
,
which is the solution of a second-order difference equation with respect to
n
, but as
n\mapsto n+1/\gamma
.
Alternatively the original coefficients
C_{n}
, or effectively
H(s=1-n\gamma)
,
would satisfy a non-linear difference equation with rational coefficients in
n
but with
incommensurate steps of
1
and
\gamma^{-1}

0=\left[1+\gamma-s+\alpha(1-s)\right]\frac{H(s)}{H(s-\gamma)}\\
+\alpha(s-2)\frac{H(s-1)}{H(s-1-\gamma)}+(s-\gamma)\frac{H(s+1)}{H(s+1-\gamma)}.

(5.1)

This is an entirely higher level of special function.

It may be of interest to note that another family of extensions to the Gauss hypergeometric functions has been explored recently,
under the monikers of

extended hypergeometric functions

[
8
]
,

extended
\tau
-Gauss hypergeometric functions

[
62
]
,
[
50
]
,

extended, generalised
\tau
-Gauss hypergeometric functions

[
37
]
.
The common theme here is that a pair of Pochhammer symbol ratios in
C_{n}
,
which are equivalent to the beta function
B(a,b)

[
53
, §5.12]
, are replaced by

B(a,b;\sigma)=\int_{0}^{1}dt\;t^{a-1}(1-t)^{b-1}\exp\left[-\frac{\sigma}{t(1-t)}\right],\Re(\sigma)>0,

(5.2)

along with

F_{\sigma}(a,b;c;z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(a)_{n}\frac{B(b+n,c-b;\sigma)}{B(b,c-b)}\frac{z^{n}}{n!},

or ultimately

B(a,b;\sigma;\alpha,\beta,\rho,\lambda)=\int_{0}^{1}dt\;t^{a-1}(1-t)^{b-1}{}_{1}F_{1}\left(\alpha;\beta;-\frac{\sigma}{t^{\rho}(1-t)^{\lambda}}\right),\Re(\sigma)>0.

There is no evidence that the extended beta function (
5.2
) satisfies a difference equation like that of (
5.1
).
