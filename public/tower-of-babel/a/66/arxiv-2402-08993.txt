The polyhedral type of a polynomial map on the plane Thanks: For this work, the first author was partially supported by the DFG Walter Benjamin Programme: EL 1092/1-1 Thanks: MSC 2020: Primary 14D06, Secondary: 14T20, 58K15
Boulos El Hilany, Kemal Rose

Abstract
. Two continuous maps f , g : ℂ 2 → ℂ 2 f,g:\mathbb{C}^{2}\to\mathbb{C}^{2} are said to be topologically equivalent if there exist homeomorphisms φ , ψ : ℂ 2 → ℂ 2 \varphi,\psi:\mathbb{C}^{2}\to\mathbb{C}^{2} satisfying ψ ∘ f ∘ φ = g \psi\circ f\circ\varphi=g . It is known that there are finitely many topologically non-equivalent polynomial maps ℂ 2 → ℂ 2 \mathbb{C}^{2}\to\mathbb{C}^{2} with any given degree d d . The number T ⁡ ( d ) T(d) of these topological types is known only whenever d = 2 d=2 . In this paper, we describe the topology of generic complex polynomial maps on the plane using the corresponding pair of Newton polytopes and establish a method for constructing topologically non-equivalent maps of degree d d . We furthermore provide a software implementation of the resulting algorithm, and present lower bounds on T ⁡ ( d ) T(d) whenever d = 3 d=3 and d = 4 d=4 .

1.
Introduction

Polynomial maps
f:=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}):\mathbb{C}^{m}\to\mathbb{C}^{n}
are some of the most classical objects in algebraic geometry and singularity theory.
Questions regarding their stability, singularities, and classifications find their roots in the pioneering works of Whitney, Thom, Boardmann, and Mather
[
35
,
3
,
34
,
28
,
27
]
on smooth maps between Euclidean spaces. One such problem, initiated by Thom
[
33
,
34
]
, asks for a topological characterization: Two continuous maps
f,g:\mathbb{C}^{m}\to\mathbb{C}^{n}
are said to be
topologically equivalent
if there exist homeomorphisms
\varphi:\mathbb{C}^{m}\to\mathbb{C}^{m}
and
\psi:\mathbb{C}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}^{n}
satisfying

\psi\circ f\circ\varphi=g.

Fukuda
[
12
]
confirmed Thom’s famous conjecture that there are finitely many topological types of polynomial functions
\mathbb{C}^{m}\to\mathbb{C}
with a given degree
d
. Soon after, Aoki, Noguchi, and Sabbah
[
1
,
31
]
generalized this result for polynomial maps
\mathbb{C}^{2}\to\mathbb{C}^{n}
with fixed
d=\deg f:=\max(\deg f_{1},\ldots,\deg f_{n})
. Nakai then showed
[
29
]
that all the remaining cases for
m\geq 3
admit infinitely many topological types if
\deg f\geq 4
.

The ensuing classification problem of topological types is still open for polynomial functions with a given degree, as well as for maps on the plane. Nevertheless, it becomes tractable in some instances, such as proper, or quadratic maps. In the former case, this amounts to counting degree-
d
ramified covers of
\mathbb{C}^{m}
(c.f.
[
17
, Proof of Theorem 3.5]
), and in the latter, a full classification has been achieved through case-by-case analysis
[
9
,
10
]
. In general, however, the difficulty emanates from the lack of accessible tools for describing those properties of polynomial maps that are invariant under homeomorphisms.

Our main result concerns polynomial maps on the plane.

Theorem 1.1
.

The number of topological types of polynomial maps
\mathbb{C}^{2}\to\mathbb{C}^{2}
of degree smaller or equal to
3
and
4
are at least
26
and

3\penalty\ 217
respectively.

To achieve the lower bounds in Theorem
1.1
, we developed a tool for constructing many topological types that relies on polyhedral invariants of polynomials; the
support
of a polynomial
P
in
n
variables is the set
\supp(P)\subset\mathbb{N}^{n}
of exponent vectors of the monomials of
P
. Its
Newton polytope

\mathsf{NP}(P)
is the convex hull in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
of
\supp(P)
.
Let
\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{N},2)
be the set of all pairs
A:=(A_{1},A_{2})
of lattice polytopes in the non-negative orthant of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. For any
A\in\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{N},2)
, we use
\mathbb{C}^{A}
to denote the vector space consisting of polynomial maps
f:\mathbb{C}^{2}\to\mathbb{C}^{2}
that satisfy
\mathsf{NP}(f_{1})\subset A_{1}
and
\mathsf{NP}(f_{2})\subset A_{2}
.

As a consequence of Sabbah’s results in
[
31
]
(see Lemma
2.1
below), there exists a Zariski open subset
\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}^{A}
in which any two polynomial maps are topologically equivalent. Accordingly, a map
f\in\mathbb{C}^{A}
is called
generic
if
f\in\Omega
. Unsurprisingly, all generic maps in
\mathbb{C}^{A}
share a list of invariants, which can be computed using computer algebra methods (see Corollary
2.9
below). This list consists of algebraic identities from three curves: the
critical points
,
\mathcal{C}_{f}
, of
f
, its
discriminant

\mathcal{D}_{f}:=f(\mathcal{C}_{f})
, and its
non-properness
set
{\mathcal{S}}_{f}
, which is the set

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}_{f}

\displaystyle:=\left\{w\in\mathbb{C}^{2}\penalty\ \left|\penalty\ \exists\penalty\ \{z^{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathbb{C}^{2},\penalty\ \|z^{k}\|\underset{k\rightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}\infty,\penalty\ f(z^{k})\longrightarrow w\right\}\right.

Whitney studied in
[
35
]
smooth maps of the plane into the plane, and showed that
\mathcal{C}_{f}
is smooth, and a singularity of
\mathcal{D}_{f}
is either a
simple node
or an
ordinary cusp
. In other words, either
p
is a normal self-crossing of
\mathcal{D}_{f}
, or, for some
a\in\mathbb{C}^{2}
with
f(a)=p
, the germ
(f,a):(\mathbb{C}^{2},a)\to(\mathbb{C}^{2},f(a))
is biholomorphically equivalent to
(\mathbb{C}^{2},0)\ni(x,\penalty\ y)\mapsto(x,\penalty\ xy+y^{3})\in(\mathbb{C}^{2},0)
.

A shift from analytic to polynomial maps arises as a highly challenging task due to the latter forming a more rigid class. Nevertheless, Farnik, Jelonek and Ruas
[
11
]
recently extended Whitney’s results for the space
\Omega(d_{1},d_{2})
of polynomial maps on the plane
f:\mathbb{C}^{2}\to\mathbb{C}^{2}
satisfying
\deg f_{1}=d_{1}
and
\deg f_{2}=d_{2}
. Namely, they showed that for any given pair
(d_{1},d_{2})\in\mathbb{N}^{2}
, a generic map in
\Omega(d_{1},d_{2})
is proper (i.e.,
{\mathcal{S}}_{f}=\emptyset
), the curve
\mathcal{C}_{f}
is smooth, a singular point of
\mathcal{D}_{f}
can either be a simple node or an ordinary cusp, and their respective numbers are computed in terms of
d_{1}
and
d_{2}
.

In this paper, we extend here some of the above results in
[
11
]
to maps in
\mathbb{C}^{A}
for all
A
in a large family
\mathscr{C}(\mathbb{N},2)\subset\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{N},2)
of pairs of polytopes. The family
\mathscr{C}(\mathbb{N},2)
strictly contains all
(A_{1},A_{2})
where, for each
i=1,2
, there is a non-singular plane conic passing through five points in
A_{i}\cap\mathbb{N}^{2}\setminus\{(0,0)\}
. Accordingly, all pairs in
\mathscr{C}(\mathbb{N},2)
will be called “conical” (Definition
2.13
). Our central result, Theorem
2.19
, on generic maps
f\in\mathbb{C}^{A}
states that
\mathcal{C}_{f}
is smooth outside
\{(0,0)\}
, and if
p\in\mathbb{C}^{2}\setminus\{(0,0)\}
is a singular point of
\mathcal{D}_{f}
, then it is either a simple node or an ordinary cusp. We also show that a singular point of
{\mathcal{S}}_{f}\setminus\{(0,0)\}
is always a simple node. Furthermore, we express the number of the above singularities in terms of the combinatorics of
A
, and we present formulas for other topologically relevant data of
f
such as the number of components of
\mathcal{C}_{f}
,
\mathcal{D}_{f}
, and
{\mathcal{S}}_{f}
, together with their multiplicities and geometric genera. Consequently, we obtain a list of numerical invariants that distinguish the topological type of
f
, and represent it as a vector

, which we call the “polyhedral type” of
f
(see Definition
2.21
), given by a map
\Psi:\mathscr{C}(\mathbb{N},2)\to\mathbb{N}^{12}
. To this end, we show (Theorem
2.23
) that for any
A,B\in\mathscr{C}(\mathbb{N},2)
, two generic maps
f\in\mathbb{C}^{A}
and
g\in\mathbb{C}^{B}
are not topologically equivalent whenever
f
and
g
have different polyhedral types.

Corollary 1.2
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
be any positive integer, and let
\mathscr{C}_{d}
be the subset of conical pairs
A\in\mathscr{C}(\mathbb{N},2)
for which any
f\in\mathbb{C}^{A}
satisfies
\deg f=d
. Then, the value
\#\Psi(\mathscr{C}_{d})
bounds from below the number of topological types of polynomial maps
\mathbb{C}^{2}\to\mathbb{C}^{2}
of degree
d
.

Theorem
1.1
is obtained by counting images
\Psi(A)
over the all conical pairs
A
from
\mathscr{C}_{3}
and
\mathscr{C}_{4}
.

Remark 1.3
.

The sharpness of the lower bound in Theorem
1.1
is expected to deteriorate significantly for large degrees. Indeed, on the one hand, thanks to the properties of the well-known Lyashko-Looijenga map
[
24
,
25
]
, there are at least
d^{d-3}
topological types of polynomial functions
g:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
of degree
d

[
22
, §5.1.3]
. By considering non-dominant maps of the form
(x,\penalty\ y)\mapsto(0,\penalty\ g(x))
, we deduce that the lower bound on the number of topological types of polynomial maps
\mathbb{C}^{2}\to\mathbb{C}^{2}
of degree
d
is at least exponential in
d
. On the other hand, the lower bound in Theorem
1.1
is lower than the number of pairs
A\subset\mathscr{C}_{d}
, which is at most polynomial in
d

[
5
]
.

Idea of proof and organization of the paper

§
2
is devoted to elaborating on topological invariants (Definition
2.11
), polyhedral type of a generic map (Definition

), and detailing the main result, Theorem
2.19
, for conical pairs

.
The proof of Theorem

is achieved in §
6
; it requires three key results: Theorems
3.5
,
4.11
, and
5.8
in §
3
, §
4
, and §

respectively.

The main result of §

is Theorem

; it states that the critical locus

of a generic map

is smooth outside
\{(0,0)\}
, and that
\mathcal{D}_{f}
has at most mild singularities. For its proof, we show that the restricted Thom-Boardman map sending
\mathbb{C}^{A}
to the first few jet-spaces is smooth with respect to the Thom-Boardman strata describing the singularities of

. It turns out that the conical property of
A
is necessary for smoothness. Then, similarly as in
[
11
, Sections 2, 3 and 4]
, we apply classical results in the theory of jet spaces (see e.g.,
[
15
]
) to describe the singularities of
\mathcal{D}_{f}
in
\mathbb{C}^{2}\setminus\{(0,0)\}
. As for counting their number, one necessitates an adjunction-type formula relating the singularities of

to its geometric genus, together with singular points “at infinity”. The latter are sent to the boundary of the compactification of
\mathcal{D}_{f}
in the toric variety
X_{\Delta}
corresponding to the Newton polytope
\Delta:=\mathsf{NP}(\mathcal{D}_{f})
. Theorem
4.11
in §
4
states that there are no boundary singular points under this compactification. To prove this, we use the fact that if
\Sigma
is the Newton polytope of
\mathcal{C}_{f}
, then its toric compactification in
X_{\Sigma}
is transversal to all the orbits of
X_{\Sigma}
.

The last ingredient for proving Theorem

requires describing the non-properness set
{\mathcal{S}}_{f}
; it was shown in
[
7
]
that for a generic

, the polynomial defining

depends only on those coefficients of

whose exponent vectors appear at pairs of faces of

(see Theorem

below). In Theorem

, we use this description to show that

has only simple nodes as singularities in

is the Newton polytope of

, then its toric compactification in

is transversal to all the one-dimensional orbits of

.

Altogether the above descriptions of

,

, and

imply that one can use classical results of Bernstein, Kouchnirenko, and Khovanskii, presented in §

, to express the singularities and the geometric genera in terms of lattice points and volumes obtained from

,
\Delta
, and

Polytopes

are determined solely from the pair

are made available in the

Topological invariants from Newton pairs

This section is divided into two parts. In the first part, we prove Corollary

2.1.
The polyhedral type of generic maps

We start with the following result.

Lemma 2.1
.

For any pair of polytopes
A\in\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{N},2)
, there exists a Zariski open subset
\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}^{A}
in which any two elements
f,g\in\Omega
represent two topologically equivalent polynomial maps
\mathbb{C}^{2}\to\mathbb{C}^{2}
.

Proof.

Consider the space
\mathbb{C}^{N_{d}}
of polynomials maps
f:\mathbb{C}^{2}\to\mathbb{C}^{2}
having degree at most
d
. On the one hand, for every
A\in\mathscr{C}(\mathbb{N},2)
, the space
\mathbb{C}^{A}
has a canonical embedding
\mathbb{C}^{A}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{C}^{N_{d}}
, as the set of all maps
g
with some coefficients are substituted by zero. We identify
\mathbb{C}^{A}
with a coordinate linear subspace of
\mathbb{C}^{N_{d}}
. On the other hand it is known (see e.g.,
[
31
, pp. 5]
) that for every affine subset
S\subset\mathbb{C}^{N_{d}}
, there exists a Zariski open
\Omega\subset S
in which any two polynomial maps
f,g\in\Omega
in a connected component of
\Omega
will be topologically equivalent. To conclude the proof, notice that every Zariski open of
\mathbb{C}^{A}
is connected.
∎

Definition 2.2
.

In the notation of Lemma
2.1
, a map

is called
generic
if it belongs to
\Omega
.
∎

Next, we describe the numerical data that are shared among topologically equivalent maps. Let us first introduce some notations and definitions.

Definition 2.3

Let

be a planar algebraic curve given by a bivariate polynomial

A

For any convenient bivariate complex polynomial

Theorem 6.2

Let

be two polynomials such that

forms a Newton non-degenerate pair of polynomials.

Theorem 6.3

(Khovanskii)

and Newton polytope

We follow similar steps as in

satisfying

. Then, for any

Theorem 7.5
.

Let
A\in\mathscr{C}(\mathbb{N},2)
be a dicritical pair, and let
\Sigma
and
\Delta
be the two polytopes, corresponding to
A
, appearing in Theorem
2.19
. For any lattice polytope
\Pi\subset\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{2}
, the following equality holds

(7.1)

\MV(\Delta,\penalty\ \Pi)=\MV(\Sigma,\penalty\ \Lft(\Pi))-{\mathcal{T}}_{A}(\Pi).

Proof.

We follow similar steps to those in the proof of
[
11
, Lemma 4.3]
. Let
f\in\mathbb{C}^{A}
be a generic map
\mathbb{C}^{2}\to\mathbb{C}^{2}
, and consider any polynomial
P\in\mathbb{C}[w_{1},w_{2}]
satisfying

•

\Pi=\mathsf{NP}(P)
,

•

the pair of curves
(\mathbb{V}_{\!*}(P),\mathcal{D}_{f}^{*})
is Newton non-degenerate, and

•

\mathbb{V}_{\!*}(P)\cap\mathcal{D}_{f}^{*}
consists of transversal intersections.

Then, the lifted curve
{\mathcal{L}}^{*}:=f^{-1}(\mathbb{V}_{\!*}(P))
intersects
\mathcal{C}_{f}^{*}
transversally in
(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{2}
, and thus

(7.2)

\displaystyle\#\mathbb{V}_{\!*}(P)\cap\mathcal{D}_{f}^{*}

\displaystyle=\#{\mathcal{L}}^{*}\cap\mathcal{C}_{f}^{*}.

Since
\#\mathbb{V}_{\!*}(P)\cap\mathcal{D}_{f}^{*}=\MV(\Delta,\penalty\ \Pi)
according to Theorem
6.2
, it is enough to show

(7.3)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}^{*}\cap\mathcal{C}_{f}^{*}

\displaystyle=\MV(\Sigma,\penalty\ \Lft(\Pi))-{\mathcal{T}}_{A}(\Pi).

Choosing
P
generic enough, and making the substitution
(w_{1},w_{2})\leftrightarrow(f_{1}(z),f_{2}(z))
in
P
, we get

\mathsf{NP}({\mathcal{L}}^{*})=\Lft(\Pi),

where
P
is expressed as
\sum c_{b}\penalty\ w^{b}
. Then, results in
[
30
]
(see also
[
13
, Section 5.5, pp. 121]
) show that
\MV(\Sigma,\penalty\ \Lft(\Pi))
equals the total number of isolated intersection points (counted with multiplicities) of the two compactifications
\overline{L}:=\varsigma\circ\Phi_{\Theta}({\mathcal{L}}^{*})
and
\overline{C}:=\varsigma\circ\Phi_{\Theta}(\mathcal{C}_{f}^{*})
in the toric variety
X_{\Theta}
corresponding to the polytope
\Theta:=\Sigma\oplus\Lft(\Pi)
(see §
4.2.3
). The above-referenced result can be viewed as a more detailed version of Bernstein’s Theorem
[
2
]
. Therefore, since
\#\overline{C}\cap\overline{L}=\#\partial C\cap\partial L+\#{\mathcal{L}}^{*}\cap\mathcal{C}_{f}^{*}
, it is enough to show the following statement:

(7.4)

{\mathcal{T}}_{A}(\Pi)
is equal to the weighted number of intersection points of
\overline{{\mathcal{L}}}\cap\overline{C}
at the
1
-orbits of
X_{\Theta}
.

Note that edges
\eta:=(\sigma,\penalty\ \ell)
of the pair of polytopes
(\Sigma,\penalty\ \Lft(\Pi))
are in bijection with edges of
\Sigma+\Lft(\Pi)
. Then, Theorem
4.6
shows that above edges
\eta
correspond to
1
-orbits
V(\eta)
of
X_{\Theta}
. Furthermore, if
\overline{{\mathcal{L}}}\cap\overline{C}
has a solution at some
V(\eta)
, then

(7.5)

\det\Jac_{z}(f)_{\sigma}=P(f(z))_{\ell}=0

has a solution
z\in(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{2}
. It follows from the definitions that the restricted polynomial
P(f(z))_{\ell}
can be expressed as

(7.6)

P(f(z))_{\ell}=\sum_{b\in e\cap\mathbb{N}^{2}}c_{b}\penalty\ f_{\gamma_{1}}^{b_{1}}\penalty\ f_{\gamma_{2}}^{b_{2}},

for some face
e\prec\Pi
, and a face
\gamma:=(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})\prec A^{0}
. One can choose the coefficients of

,
f_{2}
, and

to be generic enough so that the system (

) has a solution in

if ad only if

has a factor of the form

for some

To finish proving Statement (

\displaystyle\det\Jac_{z}(f)_{\sigma}

\displaystyle=f_{\gamma_{1}}\cdot h(z^{v})

\displaystyle P(f(z))_{\ell}

\displaystyle=f_{\gamma_{1}}^{\beta}\penalty\ \big(\sum_{b:=(\beta,k)\in\Ver(\Pi)}c_{b}f_{\gamma_{2}}^{k}\big),

where

. Note that each of

Corollary 7.6
.

Let
A\in\mathscr{C}(\mathbb{N},2)
be a dicritical pair, and let
\Sigma
and
\Delta
be the two polytopes, corresponding to
A
, appearing in Theorem
2.19
. Then, for any
rational
polytope
\Pi\subset\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{2}
, the following equality holds

(7.7)

\MV(\Delta,\penalty\ \Pi)=\MV(\Sigma,\penalty\ \Lft(\Pi))-{\mathcal{T}}_{A}(\Pi).

Proof.

Let
\lambda\in\mathbb{Q}_{>0}
be any value such that the scaled polytope
\lambda\cdot\Pi\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a lattice polytope. Since
\Lft(\lambda\cdot\Pi)
will also be a lattice polytope, Theorem
7.5
shows that

(7.8)

\MV(\Delta,\penalty\ \lambda\cdot\Pi)=\MV(\Delta,\penalty\ \Lft(\lambda\cdot\Pi))-{\mathcal{T}}_{A}(\lambda\cdot\Pi).

On the one hand, as scaling subsets in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
commutes with the operations of taking the union, Minkowski sums, and convex hulls, we get

(7.9)

\Lft(\lambda\cdot\Pi)=\lambda\cdot\Lft(\Pi).

Similarly, from the definition of the correction term, we get

{\mathcal{T}}_{A}(\lambda\cdot\Pi)=\lambda\penalty\ {\mathcal{T}}_{A}(\Pi).

On the other hand, since the Mixed volume commutes with scaling of polytopes, we get
\MV(\Delta,\penalty\ \lambda\cdot\Pi)=\lambda\penalty\ \MV(\Delta,\penalty\ \Pi)
, and
\MV(\Sigma,\penalty\ \lambda\cdot\Lft(\Pi))=\lambda\penalty\ \MV(\Sigma,\penalty\ \Lft(\Pi))
. We obtain (
7.7
) by combining the above identities to form the equality

\lambda\penalty\ \MV(\Delta,\penalty\ \Pi)=\lambda\penalty\ \MV(\Sigma,\penalty\ \Lft(\Pi))-\lambda\penalty\ {\mathcal{T}}_{A}(\Pi).

∎

7.2.2.
Correction term from mixed volumes

Let
P,\Pi\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be full dimensional polytopes having the same inner normal fan
{\mathcal{F}}
, and let
\rho_{1},\dots,\rho_{h}
be the rays in
{\mathcal{F}}
(i.e. its one-dimensional cones). For any

we set
\Pi_{i}\subset\Pi
to be the polytope below (see the red polytope in Figure
6
):

\Pi_{i}:=\{q\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\mid\penalty\ \langle q,\rho_{i}\rangle\geq\langle r,\rho_{i}\rangle+1,\penalty\ \forall r\in\Pi\}.

Lemma 7.7
.

The difference of volumes
\Vol(\Pi)-\Vol(\Pi_{i})
is equal
to the lattice length
\#\penalty\ \Pi^{\rho_{i}}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{2}-1
plus an error term
which only depends on the fan.

Proof.

We display the difference of
\Vol(\Pi)
and
\Vol(\Pi_{i})
in Figure
6
. On the right, it is split into a yellow part of volume equal to the lattice length
\#\penalty\ \Pi^{\rho_{i}}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{2}-1
, and a green part.
The green part constitutes a (possibly negative) error term equal to

\frac{1}{2\|\rho_{i}\|}(\operatorname{tan}(\alpha)+\operatorname{tan}(\beta)
.
Here
\alpha
and
\beta
denote the angles

\alpha=\sphericalangle(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})+\frac{\pi}{2}
,

\beta=\sphericalangle(\rho_{2},\rho_{3})-\frac{\pi}{2}
.
In particular, the error term
depends only on the vectors
\rho_{1},\dots,\rho_{h}
.
∎

Lemma 7.8
.

The lattice length of the face
P^{\rho_{i}}
is equal to
the difference of mixed volumes

\#\penalty\ P^{\rho_{i}}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{2}-1=\MV(P,\Pi)-\MV(P,\Pi_{i}).

Proof.

Using the properties of the mixed volume, we obtain the expansion

(7.10)

\MV(P,\Pi)-\MV(P,\Pi_{i})=\Vol(P\oplus\Pi)-\Vol(P\oplus\Pi_{i})-\left(\Vol(\Pi)-\Vol(\Pi_{i})\right).

Applying Lemma
7.7
to the right side of (
7.10
) yields

(7.11)

\#\penalty\ \left(P\oplus\Pi\right)^{\rho_{i}}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{2}-1-\left(\#\penalty\ \Pi^{\rho_{i}}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{2}-1\right).

Lattice lengths of faces are additive in Minkowski sums, so we have

\#\penalty\ \left(P\oplus\Pi\right)^{\rho_{i}}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{2}-1=\#\penalty\ P^{\rho_{i}}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{2}-1+\#\penalty\ \Pi^{\rho_{i}}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{2}-1.

Substituting this into (
7.11
), and (
7.11
) into (
7.10
)
finishes the proof.
∎
