L 2 \mathrm{L}^{2} Hypocoercivity methods for kinetic Fokker-Planck equations with factorised Gibbs states
Emeric Bouin, Jean Dolbeault, Luca Ziviani

Abstract
This contribution deals with L 2 \mathrm{L}^{2} hypocoercivity methods for kinetic Fokker-Planck equations with integrable local equilibria and a factorisation property that relates the Fokker-Planck and the transport operators. Rates of convergence in presence of a global equilibrium, or decay rates otherwise, are estimated either by the corresponding rates in the diffusion limit, or by the rates of convergence to local equilibria, under moment conditions. On the basis of the underlying functional inequalities, we establish a classification of decay and convergence rates for large times, which includes for instance sub-exponential local equilibria and sub-exponential potentials. Introduction

Hypocoercivity
refers to the method developed by C. Villani in order to capture large time asymptotics in kinetic equations, see
Mouhot-Neumann
;
Mem-villani
, which borrows ideas from Hörmander’s
hypoelliptic
theory and from the
carré du champ
method introduced by D. Bakry and M. Emery in
Bakry1985
. For this reason, the Fisher information plays an important role and, to some extent, we can consider it as an
\mathrm{H}^{1}
-theory. Here we shall focus more on the notion of
\mathrm{L}^{2}
-hypocoercivity inspired by
MR2215889
and introduced in
Dolbeault2009511
;
DMS
in a simple case of a kinetic Fokker-Planck equation, which puts the emphasis on the underlying diffusion limit. The heuristic idea is simple: while the Fokker-Planck diffusion operator controls the rate of convergence towards local equilibria in the velocity space, the equilibration of the spatial density (its convergence to the spatial density of a global equilibrium or its decay when no such equilibrium exists) can be interpreted as a diffusion in the position space, at least in a certain parabolic scaling, which results of the interplay of the diffusion in the velocity direction and the transport and the mixing in the phase space induced by the transport operator. The advantage of this approach is that rates are fully determined by the functional inequalities associated to the diffusion operator on the velocity space and to the diffusion limit in the position space.

This paper is organized as follows. We start by recalling the abstract
\mathrm{L}^{2}
hypocoercivity method in Theorem
2.1
before applying it to the framework of non-Maxwellian local equilibria and a compatible transport operator in Corollary
1
. Although an adaptation of the standard theory in presence of microscopic and macroscopic coercivity associated respectively to the Fokker-Planck operator and the diffusion limit, this result is new and covers for instance the case of relativistic transport. This framework is also well adapted to situations with weaker notions of coercivity corresponding to either an external potential with slower growth at infinity or to local equilibria with fatter tails than the Maxwellian. After reviewing various families of interpolation inequalities which dictate the asymptotic behaviours of the solutions to the Fokker-Planck equations, we extend the
\mathrm{L}^{2}
hypocoercivity method to the kinetic Fokker-Planck equations and establish a classification in terms the (microscopic) local equilibria and the (macroscopic) equilibria associated with the Fokker-Planck diffusion limit.

Reviewing extensively the literature on the asymptotic behaviour of the solutions of Fokker-Planck, degenerate Fokker-Planck and kinetic Fokker-Planck equations goes beyond our scope. Let us simply quote some papers directly related to our methods, with more details to be given later.

Concerning
Fokker-Planck
equations, coercivity for the diffusion operator means spectral gap and Poincaré inequality, and thus exponential decay of the solutions. This is a basic example of application of the carré du champ method: see
MR3155209
.
Weak Poincaré
inequalities are natural in the absence of spectral gaps as explained in
MR4056271
and have been quite systematically explored: see
MR1856277
;
MR2381160
;
MR4265692
and earlier references therein. However, such methods require strong assumptions on the initial data. This is the reason why we adopt an alternative approach based on moments and weighted functional inequalities, where the extreme case corresponds to Nash’s inequality in absence of an external potential. See Table
1
.

As for
kinetic Fokker-Planck
equations, hypocoercivity primarily refers to the method exposed in
Mem-villani
. We can also quote
piazzoli2019relaxation
for a detailed presentation of the commutator method and of Bakry-Emery type computations applied to estimates of the relaxation to equilibrium. More results and further references can also be found in
doi:10.1142/S0218202518500574
. In
MR3892316
S. Hu and X. Wang introduced a weak hypocoercivity approach
à la
Villani, using a weak Poincaré inequality, and proved subexponential convergence to equilibrium. This was later extended to a class of degenerate diffusion processes in
MR4021241
by M. Grothaus and F.-Y. Wang using weak Poincaré inequalities for the symmetric and antisymmetric part of the generator, with non-exponential rates of convergence. In the same vein, C. Cao proved quantitative convergence rates for the kinetic Fokker-Planck equation with more general confinement forces in
MR4069622
;
MR4278431
.

Alternatively the method of
DMS
was extended to cases without potentials in
BDMMS
while cases of weak or very weak potentials were considered respectively in
BDLS
and
BDS-very-weak

In these papers the effort has been mostly focused on the role of the external potential and fat tail local equilibria were not taken into consideration. However, it is known from

that fat-tail local equilibria can be responsible of a fractional diffusion limit, which may govern decay rates in the case without external potential as shown in

From microscopic and macroscopic coercivity to hypocoercivity

Let us start by an expository section which collects some known results and introduces our present purpose. Let us consider the general evolution equation

\frac{dF}{dt}+\mathsf{T}F=\mathsf{L}F

(1)

where
F
is the density of a probability distribution defined on a real or complex Hilbert space
\mathscr{H}
with scalar product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
and norm
\|\cdot\|
. We assume that
\mathsf{T}
and
\mathsf{L}
are two linear operators, respectively anti-Hermitian and Hermitian:
\mathsf{T}^{*}=-\,\mathsf{T}
and
\mathsf{\mathrm{}}L^{*}=\mathsf{L}
, where
∗
denotes the adjoint with respect to
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
. We are interested in the decay rate of
F
or in the convergence to a steady state
F_{\star}
. We assume that
F_{\star}
is unique, up to normalization. Since (
1
) is linear, we can always replace
F
by
F-F_{\star}
and study the convergence to
0
of an eventually sign changing function
F
. We have in mind that
\mathsf{L}
is an elliptic degenerate operator. If
\Pi
is the orthogonal projection onto the kernel of
\mathsf{L}
, we assume that
\mathsf{L}
has the
microscopic coercivity
property in the sense that it is
coercive
on
(1-\Pi)\,\mathscr{H}
, where
1
is here a shorthand notation for the identity that will make sense in the functional setting of interest. In other words, we claim that

\frac{1}{2}\,\frac{d}{dt}\,\|F\|^{2}=\langle\mathsf{L}F,F\rangle\leq-\,\lambda_{m}\,\|(1-\Pi)F\|^{2}

(H1)

for some
\lambda_{m}>0
. This is not enough to conclude that
\|F(t,\cdot)\|
decays exponentially as we have no decay rate on
\mathrm{Ker}(\mathsf{L})
, but if the operators
\mathsf{L}
and
\mathsf{T}
do not commute, we can hope that some of the decay properties on
(1-\Pi)\,\mathscr{H}
are transferred on
\Pi\,\mathscr{H}
. This points towards the computation of various commutators and the whole machinery of Hörmander’s hypoellipticity theory. A micro/macro approach offers a simpler framework, that has the advantage of clarifying the role played by various functional inequalities in estimating decay rates of
F
. The underlying ideas rely on the formal
macroscopic limit
of the scaled evolution equation

\varepsilon\,\frac{dF}{dt}+\mathsf{T}F=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\,\mathsf{L}F

on the Hilbert space
\mathscr{H}
, which is a typical parabolic scaling when
\varepsilon
is a small parameter. Using a formal expansion of a solution
F_{\varepsilon}=F_{0}+\varepsilon\,F_{1}+\varepsilon^{2}\,F_{2}+{\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{3})}
as
\varepsilon\to 0_{+}
and solving the equation order by order, we obtain

\begin{array}[]{ll}\mbox{at order}\,O\big(\varepsilon^{-1}\big):&\quad\mathsf{L}F_{0}=0\,,\\[4.0pt]
\mbox{at order}\,O\big(\varepsilon^{0}\big):&\quad\mathsf{T}F_{0}=\mathsf{L}F_{1}\,,\\[4.0pt]
\mbox{at order}\,O\big(\varepsilon^{1}\big):&\quad\frac{dF_{0}}{dt}+\mathsf{T}F_{1}=\mathsf{L}F_{2}\,.\end{array}

The first and second equation respectively read as
F_{0}=\Pi F_{0}
and
F_{1}=-\,(\mathsf{T}\Pi)\,F_{0}
. After projection on
\mathrm{Ker}(\mathsf{L})
, the third equation becomes
\frac{d}{dt}\left(\Pi F_{0}\right)-\,\Pi\mathsf{T}\,(\mathsf{T}\Pi)\,F_{0}=\Pi\mathsf{L}F_{2}=0
that we can also write as

\frac{\partial F_{0}}{\partial t}+(\mathsf{T}\Pi)^{*}\,(\mathsf{T}\Pi)\,F_{0}=0\,.

(2)

Assuming
macroscopic coercivity
,
i.e.
, the property that the operator
(\mathsf{T}\Pi)^{*}\,(\mathsf{T}\Pi)
is coercive on
(1-\Pi)\,\mathscr{H}
, we obtain

\frac{1}{2}\,\frac{d}{dt}\,\|F_{0}\|^{2}=-\,\|(\mathsf{T}\Pi)\,F_{0}\|^{2}\leq-\,\lambda_{M}\,\|F_{0}\|^{2}

(H2)

for some
\lambda_{M}>0
. In order to derive (
2
), we implicitly used the fact that all terms are of order
\varepsilon
, which relies on the
parabolic macroscopic dynamics
condition

\Pi\mathsf{T}\Pi\,F=0\,.

(H3)

As in the
hypocoercivity
method of
DMS
, let us consider the operator

\mathsf{A}:=\big(1+(\mathsf{T}\Pi)^{*}(\mathsf{T}\Pi)\big)^{-1}(\mathsf{T}\Pi)^{*}

where the
(\mathsf{T}\Pi)^{*}(\mathsf{T}\Pi)
term is of course reminiscent of (
2
), and, the Lyapunov functional, or
entropy
,

\mathsf{H}[F]:=\frac{1}{2}\,\|F\|^{2}+\delta\,\mathrm{Re}\langle\mathsf{A}F,F\rangle\,.

(3)

The parameter
\delta>0
, to be determined, as to be thought as a small parameter so that
\mathsf{H}[F]
is a perturbation of
\frac{1}{2}\,\|F\|^{2}
. The following estimate is by now classical but deserves some emphasis. Let us consider
G=\mathsf{A}F
,
i.e.
, the solution of
(\mathsf{T}\Pi)^{*}F=G+(\mathsf{T}\Pi)^{*}\,\mathsf{T}\Pi\,G
. As in
(
DMS
, Lemma 1)
, by a Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we learn that

\langle\mathsf{TA}F,F\rangle=\langle G,(\mathsf{T}\Pi)^{*}\,F\rangle=\|G\|^{2}+\|\mathsf{T}\Pi G\|^{2}=\|\mathsf{A}F\|^{2}+\|\mathsf{TA}F\|^{2}\\
\leq\|\mathsf{TA}F\|\,\|(\mathrm{Id}-\Pi)F\|\leq\frac{1}{2\,\mu}\,\|\mathsf{TA}F\|^{2}+\frac{\mu}{2}\,\|(\mathrm{Id}-\Pi)F\|^{2}\,.

Applied with either
\mu=1/2
or
\mu=1
, this estimate proves that
\|\mathsf{A}F\|\leq\frac{1}{2}\,\|(\mathrm{Id}-\Pi)F\|
and
\|\mathsf{TA}F\|\leq\|(\mathrm{Id}-\Pi)F\|
.
Incidentally, this proves that

\displaystyle\left|\langle\mathsf{TA}F,F\rangle\right|=\left|\langle\mathsf{TA}F,(\mathrm{Id}-\Pi)F\rangle\right|\leq\|(\mathrm{Id}-\Pi)F\|^{2}\,,

(4a)

\displaystyle|\langle\mathsf{A}F,F\rangle|\leq\frac{1}{2}\,\|\Pi F\|\,\|(\mathrm{Id}-\Pi)F\|\leq\frac{1}{4}\,\|F\|^{2}\,.

(4b)

We read from (
4b
) that
\mathsf{H}[F]
and
\|F\|^{2}
are equivalent with

\frac{2-\delta}{4}\,\|F\|^{2}\leq\mathsf{H}[F]\leq\frac{2+\delta}{4}\,\|F\|^{2}\,.

However, the twist introduced in
\mathsf{H}[F]
by
\langle\mathsf{A}F,F\rangle
makes it exponentially decaying in
t
if
F
solves (
1
). We can indeed compute

-\,\frac{d}{dt}\mathsf{H}[F]=\mathsf{D}[F]

where

\mathsf{D}[F]:=-\,\langle\mathsf{L}F,F\rangle+\delta\,\langle\mathsf{AT}\Pi F,F\rangle-\,\delta\,\Big(\mathrm{Re}\langle\mathsf{TA}F,F\rangle-\mathrm{Re}\langle\mathsf{AT}(1-\Pi)F,F\rangle+\mathrm{Re}\langle\mathsf{AL}F,F\rangle\Big)\,.

(5)

By (
H1
), we know that
-\,\langle\mathsf{L}F,F\rangle\geq\lambda_{m}\,\|(1-\Pi)F\|^{2}
. On the other hand, (
H2
) amounts to

\big\langle(\mathsf{T}\Pi)^{*}\,(\mathsf{T}\Pi)\,F,F\big\rangle\geq\lambda_{M}\,\|F\|^{2}\quad\mbox{if}\quad F\in\mathrm{Ker}(\mathsf{L})

and, by construction, the operator
\mathsf{A}
is therefore such that

\langle\mathsf{AT}\Pi F,F\rangle\geq\frac{\lambda_{M}}{1+\lambda_{M}}\,\|\Pi F\|^{2}\,.

(6)

The first two terms in the definition of
\mathsf{D}[F]
can be combined to prove that

\mathsf{D}[F]\geq\lambda_{m}\,\|(1-\Pi)F\|^{2}+\frac{\delta\,\lambda_{M}}{1+\lambda_{M}}\,\|\Pi F\|^{2}-\,\delta\,\Big(\mathrm{Re}\langle\mathsf{TA}F,F\rangle-\mathrm{Re}\langle\mathsf{AT}(1-\Pi)F,F\rangle+\mathrm{Re}\langle\mathsf{AL}F,F\rangle\Big)\,.

(7)

Under the additional assumption that the last term in the above identity involves only
bounded auxiliary operators
in the sense that

\|\mathsf{AT}(1-\Pi)F\|+\|\mathsf{AL}F\|\leq C_{M}\,\|(1-\Pi)F\|\,,

(H4)

\mathsf{D}[F]\geq\lambda\,\mathsf{H}[F]

for some explicit constant
\lambda>0
. The precise statement goes as follows. It has been established in

and extended to complex Hilbert spaces in

based on various functional inequalities.

3.1
Strong confinement case: Poincaré inequality

If
\phi(x)=\frac{1}{\alpha}\,\langle x\rangle^{\alpha}
with
\alpha\geq 1
, then (
14
) holds with
\lambda_{\phi}=\lambda_{M}>0
. We apply it to
u=\rho/e^{-\phi}
. A solution
\rho
of (
16
) with initial datum
\rho_{0}
at
t=0
satisfies

\frac{d}{dt}\|\rho(t,\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},\,e^{\phi}\,dx)}^{2}=-\,2\,\sigma\,\|\nabla\rho(t,\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},\,e^{\phi}\,dx)}^{2}\leq-\,2\,\lambda_{M}\,\sigma\,\|\rho(t,\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},\,e^{\phi}\,dx)}^{2}\,,

which yields the estimate

\|\rho(t,\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},\,e^{\phi}\,dx)}^{2}\leq\|\rho_{0}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},\,e^{\phi}\,dx)}^{2}\,e^{-2\,\lambda_{M}\,\sigma\,t}\quad\forall\,t\geq 0\,.

3.2
Weak confinement case: weighted Poincaré inequality

The following results are taken from
(
BDLS
, Appendices A and B)
. We assume here that
\alpha\in(0,1)
and consider a solution of (
16
) with nonnegative initial datum
\rho_{0}\in\mathrm{L}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},dx)
such that
\left\|{\rho_{0}}\right\|_{\mathrm{L}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}=1
. The function
u=\rho\,e^{\phi}
is a solution of the
Ornstein-Uhlenbeck
equation (also known as the
backward Kolmogorov
equation)

\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}=\sigma\,\big(\Delta u-\nabla\phi\cdot\nabla u\big)\,.

(18)

With
k\geq 0
, let us compute

\frac{d}{dt}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{|u(t,x)|^{2}\,\langle x\rangle^{k}\,e^{-\phi}}\,dx+2\,\sigma\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{|\nabla_{x}u(t,x)|^{2}\,\langle x\rangle^{k}\,e^{-\phi}}\,dx\leq\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{\big(a_{k}-b_{k}\,\langle x\rangle^{\alpha-2}\big)\,|u(t,x)|^{2}\,\langle x\rangle^{k}\,e^{-\phi}}\,dx

for some
a_{k}\in{\mathbb{R}}
,
b_{k}\in(0,+\infty)
. As a consequence, there exists a constant
\mathcal{K}(k)>0
such that

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{\langle x\rangle^{k}\,|\rho(t,x)|^{2}\,e^{\phi}}\,dx\leq\mathcal{K}(k)\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{\langle x\rangle^{k}\,|\rho_{0}|^{2}\,e^{\phi}}\,dx\quad\forall\,t\geq 0\,.

See
(
BDLS
, Proposition 4 and Appendix B.2)
for details. With
k=0
, we notice that
a_{0}=b_{0}=0
and use the
weighted Poincaré inequality

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{|\nabla_{x}u(t,x)|^{2}\,e^{-\phi}}\,dx\geq\mathscr{C}_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{wP}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{|u(t,x)-\bar{u}|^{2}\,\frac{e^{-\phi}}{\langle x\rangle^{2\,(1-\alpha)}}}\,dx\quad\mbox{where}\quad\bar{u}=\frac{\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{u\,e^{-\phi}}\,dx}{\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{e^{-\phi}}\,dx}

(19)

(notice that the average
\bar{u}
is computed with respect to the measure of the l.h.s.) to prove that

\frac{d}{dt}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{|u(t,x)-\bar{u}|^{2}\,e^{-\phi}}\,dx=-\,2\,\sigma\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{|\nabla_{x}u(t,x)|^{2}\,e^{-\phi}}\,dx\leq-\,2\,\sigma\,\mathscr{C}_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{wP}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{|u(t,x)-\bar{u}|^{2}\,\frac{e^{-\phi}}{\langle x\rangle^{2\,(1-\alpha)}}}\,dx\,.

With
k\geq 2\,(1-\alpha)
and
\theta=k/\big(k+2\,(1-\alpha)\big)
, Hölder’s inequality

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{|u-\bar{u}|^{2}\,e^{-\phi}}\,dx\leq\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{|u-\bar{u}|^{2}\,\frac{e^{-\phi}}{\langle x\rangle^{2\,(1-\alpha)}}}\,dx\right)^{\theta}\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{|u-\bar{u}|^{2}\,\langle x\rangle^{k}\,e^{-\phi}}\,dx\right)^{1-\theta}

allows us to prove that

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{|\rho(t,x)-\rho_{\star}(x)|^{2}\,e^{\phi}}\,dx\leq\left(\|\rho_{0}-\rho_{\star}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},\,e^{\phi}\,dx)}^{-\,4\,(1-\alpha)/k}+\frac{4\,(1-\alpha)\,\sigma\,\mathscr{C}_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{wP}}}{k\,\mathcal{K}_{*}^{\,2\,(1-\alpha)/k}}\,t\right)^{-\frac{k}{2\,(1-\alpha)}}\quad\forall\,t\geq 0

with
\mathcal{K}_{*}:=\mathcal{K}(k)^{2}\,\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{\langle x\rangle^{k}\,|\rho_{0}|^{2}\,e^{\phi}}\,dx+\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{\langle x\rangle^{k}\,e^{-\phi}}\,dx\left\|{\rho_{0}}\right\|_{\mathrm{L}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}^{2}
.

3.3
Weak confinement, a limit case: Hardy-Poincaré inequality

The results in this case are new. In the limit as
\alpha\to 0_{+}
, we can assume that
\phi(x)=\gamma\,\log\langle x\rangle
with
\gamma>d
so that

defined by (

be a solution of (

\frac{d}{dt}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{|u(t,x)|^{2}\,\langle x\rangle^{k}\,e^{-\phi}}\,dx+2\,\sigma\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{|\nabla_{x}u(t,x)|^{2}\,\langle x\rangle^{k}\,e^{-\phi}}\,dx\\
=k\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{|u(t,x)|^{2}\,\langle x\rangle^{k-2}\left(d+(k-\gamma-2)\,\tfrac{|x|^{2}}{\langle x\rangle^{2}}\right)e^{-\phi}}\,dx\\

for an appropriate choice of

Lemma 1

For any
\beta>0

\mathsf{D}[f]\geq\kappa\left(\|(1-\Pi)f\|_{\beta}^{2}+\langle\mathsf{AT}\Pi f,\Pi f\rangle\right)\,.

Proof

We recall that by (

\mathsf{D}

In order to prove Lemma

and
\langle\mathsf{AT}\Pi f,\Pi f\rangle
. We obtain these estimates in four steps, as follows.

Step 1.
Expressions of
\langle\mathsf{AT}\Pi f,\Pi f\rangle
.
We consider the function
u=u(x)
implicitly defined by
u\,f_{\star}=\big(1+(\mathsf{T}\Pi)^{\ast}(\mathsf{T}\Pi)\big)^{-1}\,\Pi f

u-\frac{\sigma}{\rho_{\star}}\,\nabla_{x}\cdot\big(\rho_{\star}\,\nabla_{x}u\big)=u_{f}=\frac{\rho_{f}}{\rho_{\star}}\,.

We deduce from

\mathsf{AT}\Pi f=\big(1+(\mathsf{T}\Pi)^{\ast}(\mathsf{T}\Pi)\big)(\mathsf{T}\Pi)^{\ast}(\mathsf{T}\Pi)f=\Pi f-\big(1+(\mathsf{T}\Pi)^{\ast}(\mathsf{T}\Pi)\big)^{-1}f=\Pi f-u\,f_{\star}=\left(\frac{\rho_{f}}{\rho_{\star}}-u\right)f_{\star}

and (

\langle\mathsf{AT}\Pi f,\Pi f\rangle=-\,\sigma\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{\big(\nabla_{x}\cdot\big(\rho_{\star}\,\nabla_{x}u\big)\big)\,\rho_{f}}\,dx=-\,\sigma\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{\nabla_{x}\cdot\big(\rho_{\star}\,\nabla_{x}u\big)\left(u-\frac{\sigma}{\rho_{\star}}\,\nabla_{x}\cdot\big(\rho_{\star}\,\nabla_{x}u\big)\right)}\,dx\,,

that is,

\langle\mathsf{AT}\Pi f,\Pi f\rangle=\sigma\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{|\nabla_{x}u|^{2}\,\rho_{\star}}\,dx+\sigma^{2}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{\big|\nabla_{x}\cdot\big(\rho_{\star}\,\nabla_{x}u\big)\big|^{2}\,\rho_{\star}^{-1}}\,dx\,.

(26)

Testing (
25
) with
u\,\rho_{\star}
, we learn after an integration by parts that

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{|u|^{2}\,\rho_{\star}}\,dx+\sigma\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{|\nabla u|^{2}\,\rho_{\star}}\,dx=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{u\,\rho_{f}}\,dx\leq\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{|u|^{2}\,\rho_{\star}}\,dx\right)^{1/2}\,\|\Pi f\|

\|\Pi f\|^{2}=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{|\rho_{f}|^{2}\,\rho_{\star}^{-1}}\,dx
. Hence

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{|u|^{2}\,\rho_{\star}}\,dx\leq\|\Pi f\|^{2}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\sigma\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{|\nabla u|^{2}\,\rho_{\star}}\,dx\leq\|\Pi f\|^{2}\,.

Step 2.
An estimate of
|\langle\mathsf{TA}f,f\rangle|
.
We know from (
4a
) that
|\langle\mathsf{TA}f,f\rangle|\leq\|(1-\Pi)f\|_{2}^{2}
if
\beta\geq 1
. With
\sigma
defined by (

), we claim that

\big|\mathrm{Re}\langle\mathsf{TA}f,f\rangle\big|\leq\frac{1}{\sigma}\,\|(1-\Pi)f\|_{\beta}^{2}

(28)

also holds if
\beta\in(0,1)
. In this later case, let us consider the function
w=w(x)
implicitly defined by
w\,f_{\star}=\mathsf{A}f
, that is, the solution of

w-\frac{\sigma}{\rho_{\star}}\,\nabla_{x}\cdot(\rho_{\star}\,\nabla_{x}w)=-\,\frac{1}{\rho_{\star}}\nabla_{x}\cdot\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{v\,\langle v\rangle^{\beta-2}f}\,dv\,.

Testing with

we obtain

\sigma\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{|\nabla_{x}w|^{2}\,\rho_{\star}}\,dx\leq\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{|w|^{2}\,\rho_{\star}}\,dx+\sigma\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{|{\nabla_{x}w}|^{2}\,\rho_{\star}}\,dx=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{\nabla_{x}w\cdot\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{v\,\langle v\rangle^{\beta-2}f}\,dv\right)}\,dx\,.

By applying the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and after squaring, we obtain

\sigma^{2}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{|\nabla_{x}w|^{2}\,\rho_{\star}}\,dx\leq\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{v\,\langle v\rangle^{\beta-2}(1-\Pi)f}\,dv\right)^{2}\,\rho_{\star}^{-1}}\,dx\leq\|(1-\Pi)f\|_{\beta}^{2}

We claim that, for some explicit constant

. Notice that

\displaystyle-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{\partial_{x_{i}}\phi\,\partial_{x_{j}}u\,\big(\partial_{x_{i}}(\rho_{\star}\,\partial_{x_{j}}u)\big)}\,dx=

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{\partial_{x_{i}x_{i}}^{2}\phi\,\partial_{x_{j}}u\,\big(\rho_{\star}\,\partial_{x_{j}}u\big)}\,dx+\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{\partial_{x_{i}}\phi\,\partial_{x_{i}x_{j}}^{2}u\,\big(\rho_{\star}\,\partial_{x_{j}}u\big)}\,dx

0\leq f_{0}\leq C\,f_{\star}
, for a certain
C>0
. As sign plays no role, up to replacing
f
with
f-f_{\star}
when
f_{\star}
is integrable, we may assume that

\iint_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f(t,x,v)\,dx\,dv=0\quad\forall\,t\geq 0\,.

(36)

We distinguish various cases depending on the values of
\beta
and
\alpha
.

\bullet
Case
\beta\geq 1
and
\alpha\geq 1

Thanks to Lemma
1
, we have

\mathsf{D}[f]\geq\kappa\left(\|(1-\Pi)f\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\,d\mu)}^{2}+\langle\mathsf{AT}\Pi f,\Pi f\rangle\right)\,.

for some
\kappa>0
. Because of the assumption
\alpha\geq 1
the operator
(\mathsf{T}\Pi)^{\ast}(\mathsf{T}\Pi)
is coercive, that is (
H2
) hold. As a consequence we also have (
6
),
i.e.
,

\langle\mathsf{AT}\Pi f,\Pi f\rangle\geq\frac{\lambda_{M}}{1+\lambda_{M}}\,\|\Pi f\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\,d\mu)}^{2}\,.

Therefore

\mathsf{D}[f]\geq\frac{\kappa\,\lambda_{M}}{1+\lambda_{M}}\,\|f\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\,d\mu)}^{2}

holds for some
\kappa>0
, which gives exponential convergence:

\|f(t,\cdot,\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\,d\mu)}^{2}\leq\frac{4}{2-\delta}\,\mathsf{H}[f(t,\cdot,\cdot)]\leq\frac{4}{2-\delta}\,\mathsf{H}[f_{0}]\,e^{-\lambda t}\quad\mbox{where}\quad\lambda=\frac{\kappa\,\lambda_{M}}{1+\lambda_{M}}\,.

Exactly the same proof applies in the case of Corollary
1
, with
\lambda_{M}
now given by the Poincaré inequality associated with the measure
e^{-\phi}\,dx
, of which the case
\phi(x)=\frac{1}{\alpha}\,\langle x\rangle^{\alpha}
with
\alpha\geq 1
is a special case.

\bullet
Case
\beta\in(0,1)
and
\alpha\geq 1

In this case, on the one hand we still have macroscopic coercivity (
H2
) due to the fact that
\alpha\geq 1
, but on the other hand, a loss of weight now appears for the microscopic component because of
\beta\in(0,1)
. Inequality (
24
) now reads as

\mathsf{D}[f]\geq\kappa\left(\|(1-\Pi)f\|^{2}_{\mathrm{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\langle v\rangle^{-2(1-\beta)}\,d\mu)}+\frac{\lambda_{M}}{1+\lambda_{M}}\,\|f\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\,d\mu)}^{2}\right)\,.

In order to recover the
\mathrm{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d},d\mu)
norm we need to interpolate with the conservation of moments. Let
\ell>0
and notice that
f_{0}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\langle v\rangle^{\ell}d\mu)
by our assumption on the initial datum (
23
). Setting
a=\ell/\big(\ell+2(1-\beta)\big)
, by Hölder’s inequality and Lemma
3
, we have

\displaystyle\|(1-\Pi)f\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\,d\mu)}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|(1-\Pi)f\|^{2a}_{\mathrm{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\langle v\rangle^{-2(1-\beta)}\,d\mu)}\,\|(1-\Pi)f\|^{2(1-a)}_{\mathrm{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\langle v\rangle^{\ell}\,d\mu)}

\displaystyle\leq\|(1-\Pi)f\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\langle v\rangle^{-2(1-\beta)}\,d\mu)}^{2a}\,K_{\ell}(t)^{1-a}

\displaystyle\leq C_{\ell}^{1-a}\,\|(1-\Pi)f\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\langle v\rangle^{-2(1-\beta)}\,d\mu)}^{2a}\,.

As a consequence, for a certain constant
C>0
we have

\mathsf{D}[f]\geq C\left(\|(1-\Pi)f\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\,d\mu)}^{2\left(1+\frac{2(1-\beta)}{\ell}\right)}+\|\Pi f\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\,d\mu)}^{2}\right)\geq C\,\|f\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\,d\mu)}^{2\left(1+\frac{2(1-\beta)}{\ell}\right)}\,.

By the Bihari-LaSalle estimate, we finally have

\mathsf{H}[f]\leq\mathsf{H}[f_{0}]\left(1+C\,\mathsf{H}[f_{0}]^{\frac{2(1-\beta)}{\ell}}\,t\right)^{-\frac{\ell}{2(1-\beta)}}\,.

\bullet
Case
\beta\geq 1
and
\alpha\in(0,1)

In this case we have the symmetrical situation compared to the previous one. The dissipation of entropy is

\mathsf{D}[f]\geq\kappa\left(\|(1-\Pi)f\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\,d\mu)}^{2}+\langle\mathsf{AT}\Pi f,\Pi f\rangle\right)

where now
\langle\mathsf{AT}\Pi f,\Pi f\rangle
does not produce macroscopic coercivity, but is given in terms of
u
by (
26
). Fix
k>0
and assume that
f_{0}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\langle x\rangle^{k}\,d\mu)
, then from Lemma
2
and
3
, we have that the moments
M_{k}(t)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{|u|^{2}\,\langle x\rangle^{k}\,\rho_{\star}}\,dx
are uniformly bounded in time. With
b=k/\big(k+2(1-\alpha)\big)\in(0,1)
, using Hölder’s inequality and the weighted Poincaré inequality with non-classical average of
(
BDLS
, Cor. 10)
, we obtain

\displaystyle\|\Pi f\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d},d\mu)}^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{|u|^{2}\,\rho_{\star}}\,dx+2\,\sigma\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{|\nabla_{x}u|^{2}\,\rho_{\star}}\,dx+\sigma^{2}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{|\nabla_{x}\cdot(\rho_{\star}\,\nabla_{x}u)|^{2}\,\rho_{\star}}\,dx

\displaystyle\leq\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{|u|^{2}\,\langle x\rangle^{-2(1-\alpha)}\,\rho_{\star}}\,dx\right)^{b}\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{|u|^{2}\,\langle x\rangle^{k}\,\rho_{\star}}\,dx\right)^{1-b}+2\,\langle\mathsf{AT}\Pi f,\Pi f\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\big(\mathscr{C}_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{wP}}\big)^{b}\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{|\nabla_{x}u|^{2}\,\rho_{\star}}\,dx\right)^{b}\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{|u|^{2}\,\langle x\rangle^{k}\,\rho_{\star}}\,dx\right)^{1-b}+2\,\langle\mathsf{AT}\Pi f,\Pi f\rangle

\displaystyle\leq C\,\langle\mathsf{AT}\Pi f,\Pi f\rangle^{b}+2\,\langle\mathsf{AT}\Pi f,\Pi f\rangle=:\Phi\big(\langle\mathsf{AT}\Pi f,\Pi f\rangle\big)

where
C
depends on
\mathscr{C}_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{wP}}
and the bound on
\|f_{0}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\langle x\rangle^{k}\,d\mu)}
. In the weighted Poincaré inequality we used (
36
), hence
\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{u\,\rho_{\star}}\,dx=\iint_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f\,dx\,dv=0
. Now we have

\displaystyle\mathsf{H}[f]\leq\frac{2+\delta}{4}\,\|f\|^{2}_{\mathrm{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d},d\mu)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2+\delta}{4}\left(\|(1-\Pi)f\|^{2}_{\mathrm{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d},d\mu)}+\Phi\big(\langle\mathsf{AT}\Pi f,\Pi f\rangle\big)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2+\delta}{4}\,\Phi\left(\|(1-\Pi)f\|^{2}_{\mathrm{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d},d\mu)}+\langle\mathsf{AT}\Pi f,\Pi f\rangle\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2+\delta}{4}\,\Phi\left(\kappa^{-1}\,\mathsf{D}[f]\right)\,.

Therefore the decay of
\mathsf{H}[f]
is estimated by the decay of the solution
z(t)
of

z^{\prime}=\frac{dz}{dt}=-\,\kappa\,\Phi^{-1}\left(\frac{4\,z}{2+\delta}\right)\,,\quad z(0)=\mathsf{H}[f_{0}]\,.

In view of the expression of
\Phi
, we conclude that
z
monotonically converges to
0
as
t\to+\infty
and, as a consequence
z^{\prime}
also converges to
0
. This implies that after some time
t_{0}\geq 0
, we have

\Phi\left(-\,\kappa^{-1}\,z^{\prime}\right)\leq C\left(-\,\kappa^{-1}\,z^{\prime}\right)^{b}\,,

Where
C
denotes a positive constant that may change from line to line. Altogether, we end up with the differential inequality

z^{\prime}\leq\,-\,C\,z^{1/b}\,.

Integrating and using
\frac{b}{1-b}=\frac{k}{2(1-\alpha)}
, we obtain that
z(t)\leq C\left(1+t\right)^{-\frac{k}{2(1-\alpha)}}
.

\bullet
Case
\beta\in(0,1)
and
\alpha\in(0,1)

If
\beta\in(0,1)
and
\alpha\in(0,1)
we have neither microscopic coercivity nor macroscopic coercivity. The dissipation of entropy is

\mathsf{D}[f]\geq\kappa\left(\|(1-\Pi)f\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\langle v\rangle^{-2(1-\beta)}\,d\mu)}^{2}+\langle\mathsf{AT}\Pi f,\Pi f\rangle\right)

and we have to interpolate with moments in both variables
x
and
v
. As in the previous cases, we have

\|\Pi f\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\,d\mu)}^{2}\leq C\,\langle\mathsf{AT}\Pi f,\Pi f\rangle^{b}+2\,\langle\mathsf{AT}\Pi f,\Pi f\rangle=\Phi\big(\langle\mathsf{AT}\Pi f,\Pi f\rangle\big)

and

\displaystyle\|(1-\Pi)f\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\,d\mu)}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\,\|(1-\Pi)f\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\langle v\rangle^{-2(1-\beta)}\,d\mu)}^{2a}=:\Psi\left(\|(1-\Pi)f\|^{2}_{\mathrm{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\langle v\rangle^{-2(1-\beta)}\,d\mu)}\right)

with
a=\frac{\ell}{\ell+2(1-\beta)}
and
b=\frac{k}{k+2(1-\alpha)}
. As above we have

\displaystyle\mathsf{H}[f]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2+\delta}{4}\left(\Psi\left(\|(1-\Pi)f\|^{2}_{\mathrm{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\langle v\rangle^{-2(1-\beta)}\,d\mu)}\right)+\Phi\big(\langle\mathsf{AT}\Pi f,\Pi f\rangle\big)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2+\delta}{4}\left(\Psi\big(\kappa^{-1}\,\mathsf{D}[f]\big)+\Phi\big(\kappa^{-1}\,\mathsf{D}[f]\big)\right)\,.

Notice that the function
t\mapsto\Psi(t)+\Phi(t)
is increasing, concave and
\Psi(0)+\Phi(0)=0
. Moreover we have

\frac{d}{dt}\mathsf{H}[f(t,\cdot,\cdot)]\leq-\,\kappa\,(\Psi+\Phi)^{-1}\left(\frac{4}{2+\delta}\,\mathsf{H}[f(t,\cdot,\cdot)]\right)\,.

As a consequence,
\mathsf{H}[f(t,\cdot,\cdot)]
can be estimated by the solution
z
of

z^{\prime}=-\,\kappa\,(\Psi+\Phi)^{-1}\left(\frac{4\,z}{2+\delta}\right)\,.

For the same reasons as before,
z^{\prime}
converges to
0
as
t\to+\infty
. Using the explicit expressions of
\Phi
and
\Psi
, we see that there exists some
t_{0}\geq 0
such that, for any
t\geq t_{0}
,

(\Psi+\Phi)\left(-\,\kappa^{-1}\,z^{\prime}\right)\leq C\left(-\,\kappa^{-1}\,z^{\prime}\right)^{\zeta}

for some
C>0
, where
\zeta=\min\{a,b\}
. This inequality leads to
z^{\prime}\leq-\,C\,z^{1/\zeta}
and therefore to

z(t)\leq C\,(1+t)^{-\min\big\{\frac{k}{2(1-\alpha)},\frac{\ell}{2(1-\beta)}\big\}}\quad\forall\,t\geq 0

by the Bihari-LaSalle estimate.

\bullet
Case
\beta\geq 1
and
\phi=0

In absence of a global equilibrium, we can still consider (
24
) written with
\rho_{\star}=1
. Identity (
26
) now reads as

\langle\mathsf{AT}\Pi f,\Pi f\rangle=\sigma\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{|\nabla_{x}u|^{2}}\,dx+\sigma^{2}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{|\Delta_{x}u|^{2}}\,dx\,.

Because of Nash’s inequality and the conservation of mass, we have

\displaystyle\|\Pi f\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d},d\mu)}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|u\|^{2}_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(dx)}+2\,\sigma\|\nabla_{x}u\|^{2}_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(dx)}+\sigma^{2}\|\Delta_{x}u\|^{2}_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(dx)}

\displaystyle\leq C_{\mathrm{Nash}}\,\|u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{1}(dx)}^{\frac{4}{d+2}}\,\|\nabla_{x}u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(dx)}^{\frac{2d}{d+2}}+2\,\langle\mathsf{AT}\Pi f,\Pi f\rangle

\displaystyle\leq C\,\langle\mathsf{AT}\Pi f,\Pi f\rangle^{\frac{d}{d+2}}+2\,\langle\mathsf{AT}\Pi f,\Pi f\rangle\,.

In the asymptotic regime of small
\|\Pi f\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\,d\mu)}^{2}
, we have

\langle\mathsf{AT}\Pi f,\Pi f\rangle\geq C\,\|\Pi f\|^{2\left(1+\frac{2}{d}\right)}

for some suitable constant
C>0
, and

\mathsf{D}[f]\geq C\left(\|(1-\Pi)f\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d},d\mu)}^{2}+\|\Pi f\|^{2\left(1+\frac{2}{d}\right)}_{\mathrm{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d},d\mu)}\right)\geq C\,\|f\|^{2\left(1+\frac{2}{d}\right)}\,.

By the Bihari-LaSalle estimate, we can finally conclude

\mathsf{H}[f(t,\cdot,\cdot)]\leq\mathsf{H}[f_{0}]\left(1+C\,\mathsf{H}[f_{0}]^{\frac{2}{d}}\,t\right)^{-\frac{d}{2}}\forall\,t\geq 0\,.

\bullet
Case
\beta\in(0,1)
and
\phi=0

We proceed as in the previous case. The macroscopic part obeys the same estimate

\langle\mathsf{AT}\Pi f,\Pi f\rangle\gtrsim C\,\|\Pi f\|^{2\left(1+\frac{2}{d}\right)}\,.

The microscopic component has to be interpolated with moments:

\|(1-\Pi)f\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\,d\mu)}^{2}\leq C\,\|(1-\Pi)f\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\langle v\rangle^{-2(1-\beta)}\,d\mu)}^{2b}\,.

We conclude that, as
\|f\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\,d\mu)}\to 0
,

\mathsf{D}[f]\geq C\left(\|(1-\Pi)f\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\,d\mu)}^{2\left(1+\frac{2(1-\beta)}{k}\right)}+\|\Pi f\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\,d\mu)}^{2\left(1+\frac{2}{d}\right)}\right)\geq C\,\|f\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\,d\mu)}^{2\left(1+\frac{1}{\zeta}\right)}

where
\zeta=\min\{\frac{k}{2(1-\beta)},\frac{d}{2}\}
. By the Bihari-LaSalle estimate we conclude

\mathsf{H}[f]\leq\mathsf{H}[f_{0}]\left(1+C\,\mathsf{H}[f_{0}]^{\frac{1}{\zeta}}\,t\right)^{-\zeta}\,.
