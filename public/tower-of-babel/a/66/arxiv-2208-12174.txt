Abstract

Introduction

We consider the measure space
(\mathbb{T}^{2},\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{T}^{2}),|\cdot|)
where the torus
\mathbb{T}^{2}
is the unit square
[0,1]^{2}
with periodic boundary conditions,
\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
denotes the Borel subsets of
\mathbb{T}^{2}
and
|\cdot|
represents the normalized Lebesgue measure. We consider the group of automorphisms on the torus, that is

G(\mathbb{T}^{2})=\{T:\mathbb{T}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{T}^{2}\text{ invertible and measure-preserving}\},

made of all measurable and invertible maps that preserve the Lebesgue measure, in symbols:

|T^{-1}(A)|=|A|,\quad\forall A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{T}^{2}).

Among automorphisms, we look for those that
mix
sets, that is

•

T
is
weakly mixing
if
\forall A,B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{T}^{2})

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\left[|T^{-j}(A)\cap B|-|A||B|\right]^{2}=0;

•

T
is
(strongly) mixing
if
\forall A,B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{T}^{2})

\lim_{n\to\infty}|T^{-n}(A)\cap B|=|A||B|.

One can easily observe that the strong mixing implies the weak mixing, while there are counterexamples of weakly mixing automorphisms that are not strongly mixing (see for instance
[
4
]
).

These objects are well understood from the point of view of Ergodic Theory, whereas their counterpart in the dynamics of incompressible fluids is a recent research topic that presents many challenging open questions. In Fluid Dynamics one focuses mostly on (divergence-free) vector fields
b=b(t,x)
with
t\in[0,1]
,
x\in\mathbb{T}^{2}
, whose flow
X(t)
, that is the solution of the following ODE system

\begin{cases}\dot{X}(t,x)=b(t,X(t,x)),\\
X(0,x)=x,\end{cases}

is an automorphism of
G(\mathbb{T}^{2})
when evaluated at time
t=1
. Indeed, thanks to the incompressibility condition (
\text{div }b=0
), at any instant of time the flow
X(t)
can be regarded as an invertible and measure-preserving map from
\mathbb{T}^{2}
into itself.
The theory of DiPerna and Lions
[
6
]
and Ambrosio
[
9
]
establishes existence, uniqueness and stability for the (Regular Lagrangian) flow of divergence-free vector fields living in
L^{\infty}([0,1],W^{1,1}_{\text{loc}}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))
or
L^{\infty}([0,1],\BV_{\text{loc}}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))
, giving the possibility to exploit on the one side the rough regularity in space of the vector field, on the other side to establish deep connections with Ergodic Theory. To be more precise,

Definition 1.1
.

Let
b\in L^{1}([0,1]\times\mathbb{T}^{2};\mathbb{T}^{2})
. A map
X:[0,1]\times\mathbb{T}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{T}^{2}
is a
Regular Lagrangian Flow
(RLF) for the vector field
b
if

1.

for a.e.
x\in\mathbb{T}^{2}
the map
t\rightarrow X(t,x)
is an absolutely continuous integral solution of

\begin{cases}\frac{d}{dt}x(t)=b(t,x(t));&\\
x(0)=x.\end{cases}

2.

there exists a positive constant
C
independent of
t
such that

|X(t)^{-1}(A)|\leq C|A|,\quad\forall A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{T}^{2}).

The above discussion motivates the following

Definition 1.2
.

Let
b\in L^{\infty}([0,1],\BV_{\text{loc}}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))
be a divergence-free vector field. We say that
b
is weakly mixing (resp. strongly mixing) if its unique RLF
X(t)
, when evaluated at
t=1
, is a weakly mixing automorphism (resp. strongly mixing).

The definition of mixing vector fields was first given in
[
3
]
, but there are examples of strongly mixing vector fields in previous literature. For instance, in
[
7
]
the authors give an explicit example of a strongly mixing vector field
b\in L^{\infty}([0,1],W^{s,p}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))
for
s<\frac{1+\sqrt{5}}{2}
and
p\in[1,\frac{1}{2s+1-\sqrt{5}})
whose RLF at time
t=1
is the
Folded Baker’s map
.
We should remark that the advantage of the rough regularity of the vector field, especially in the case of the
\BV
regularity, is that it allows for rigid
cut and paste
motions, since the flows originated by these vector fields do not preserve the property of a set to be connected. These constructions would be hard to reproduce for vector fields with higher regularity in time and space. Nevertheless, a stochastic approach (see for example
[
2
]
) investigates mixing vector fields with higher regularity, but does not furnish deterministic examples of vector fields with the desired mixing properties.

The aim of this paper is the construction of a vector field
b\in L^{\infty}([0,1],\BV(\mathbb{T}^{2}))
, which is weakly mixing but not strongly mixing. As proved in
[
12
]
for automorphisms and in
[
3
]
for vector fields, the weakly mixing behaviour is
typical
, while strongly mixing vector fields are
few
in the sense of Baire Category Theorem (see also
[
1
]
,
[
11
]
,
[
8
]
,
[
10
]
,
[
13
]
,
[
15
]
). Nevertheless it is hard to give examples of weakly mixing automorphisms/vector fields that are not strongly mixing.
As written above, Zlatoš and Elgindi construct a strongly mixing vector field in
[
7
]
, while there are no examples in literature (up to our knowledge) of vector fields that are weakly mixing but not strongly mixing. We want especially to underline that the parallelism with Ergodic Theory, which has investigated different stages of the mixing behaviour (see for instance
mild mixing
,
light mixing
,
partial mixing
), can give new insights from the point of view of the dynamics of an incompressible fluid.

Our examples is based on a work of Chacon
[
4
]
, who constructed a weakly mixing automorphism that is not strongly mixing, in the one-dimensional space
([0,1],\mathcal{B}([0,1]),|\cdot|)
, where
|\cdot|
is the one-dimensional Lebesgue measure. The importance of his work is that a general procedure to build up weakly mixing automorphisms that are not strongly mixing is given. Indeed his example easily extends to multiple dimensions, but we will focus on the dimension
d=2
to avoid technicalities and to provide some
visual
expressions of the vector fields under consideration. In particular we will construct a divergence-free vector field
b\in L^{\infty}([0,1],\BV(\mathbb{T}^{2}))
whose Regular Lagrangian Flow
X(t)
, when evaluated at time
t=1
, is a weakly mixing automorphism that is not strongly mixing.

The idea of the paper is the definition of a setting (see
Configurations
in Section
3
) that helps to relate an automorphism, call it
U
, to the vector field
b^{U}
whose RLF at time
t=1
is
U
.

A
configuration

\gamma
is a
n\times n
matrix that takes values in the set
\{1,2,\dots,n^{2}\}
. Moreover we ask that
\gamma_{ij}=\gamma_{hk}
iff
i=h,j=k
. Every configuration indeed represents an enumeration of the subsquares of the torus of the grid
\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}\frac{1}{n}
(see Figures
1
,
2
). A
movement

T:\mathcal{C}(n)\rightarrow\mathcal{C}(n)
is a one-to-one map from the space of configurations
\mathcal{C}(n)
into itself, that is, it is a
permutations
of the subsquares of the grid
\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}\frac{1}{n}
. Among movements the following are relevant for Chacon’s example: simple exchange, sort, rotation (Section
3
), and each one of them can be realized as the flow, evaluated at time
t=1
, of some
\BV
divergence-free vector field (see Subsection
3.1
and Figure
3
).

k=4
and an enumeration of subsquares.

sends the starting configuration into this final one.

The key example that one has to keep in mind while approaching this problem is the
15 puzzle
where one performs rigid movements on subsquares in order to reach the desired configuration (see Figure
4
).

Chacon’s method provides a sequence of automorphisms
\{U_{k}\}
that are permutations of the subsquares of the grid
\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}\frac{1}{k}
. To give an intuition, we fix for example
k=4
and we assume to enumerate squares as in Figure
1
. Chacon’s automorphism
U_{4}\in G(\mathbb{T}^{2})
moves the previous enumeration of squares (the starting configuration) into the final configuration in Figure

. Then he considers the limit map
U=\lim_{k\to\infty}U_{k}
, which is a weakly mixing automorphism that is not strongly mixing (see Sections
2.3
,
4
).

Using Chacon’s construction and the setting of configurations, we prove in particular the following result (Section
5
):

Theorem 1.3
.

There exists a divergence-free vector field
b^{U}\in L^{\infty}_{t}\BV_{x}
whose RLF
X^{U}(t)
when evaluated at time
t=1
is the Chacon’s map
U
, that is
b^{U}
is a weakly mixing vector field that is not strongly mixing.

We conclude remarking that all these constructions are possible assuming to work in
[0,1]^{d}
instead of
\mathbb{T}^{d}
.

Preliminaries

For notations and preliminaries we refer mainly to
[
3
]
. Throughout the paper we will consider divergence-free vector fields
b:[0,1]\times\mathbb{T}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
in the space
L^{\infty}([0,1],\BV(\mathbb{T}^{2}))
(or alternatively in
L^{\infty}([0,1],\BV(\mathbb{R}^{2}))
with
\text{supp }b\subset\subset[0,1]^{2}
), in short
b\in L^{\infty}_{t}\BV_{x}
. The measure space under interest is
(\mathbb{T}^{2},\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{T}^{2}),|\cdot|)
where
|\cdot|
is the two dimensional Lebesgue measure, and
\mathbb{T}^{2}
is the unit square
[0,1]^{2}
with periodic boundary conditions. We remark that the analysis of the next sections can be easily extended to higher dimensions, but we will focus on the two dimensional case to avoid technical issues.

2.1
Mixing automorphisms

We call
G(\mathbb{T}^{2})
the group (w.r.t. the composition) of automorphisms of the torus, namely:

Definition 2.1
.

An
automorphism
of the measure space
(\mathbb{T}^{2},\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{T}^{2}),|\cdot|)
is an invertible map
T:\mathbb{T}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{T}^{2}
measurable and measure-preserving, that is

|A|=|T(A)|=|T^{-1}(A)|,\quad\forall A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{T}^{2}).

From now on we will say that
T
is an automorphism of the torus meaning that it is an automorphism of the measure space
(\mathbb{T}^{2},\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{T}^{2}),|\cdot|)
. We remark that the definitions and propositions that we give in this section hold true for
(\Omega,\Sigma,\mu)
where
\Omega
is locally compact and separable, while
\mu
is a complete and normalized measure on
\Omega
.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
T\in G(\mathbb{T}^{2})
be an automorphism. Then

•

T
is
ergodic
if
\forall A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
such that
T^{-1}(A)=A
then
|A|=0
or
1
;

•

T
is
weakly mixing
if
\forall A,B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{T}^{2})

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\left[|T^{-j}(A)\cap B|-|A||B|\right]^{2}=0;

(2.1)

•

T
is
(strongly) mixing
if
\forall A,B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{T}^{2})

\lim_{n\to\infty}|T^{-n}(A)\cap B|=|A||B|.

(2.2)

It is easy to see that strong mixing implies weak mixing which implies the ergodicity. Let now
T\in G(\mathbb{T}^{2})
, then the Kolmogorov Koopman operator (see Chapter 1 in
[
5
]
)
U_{T}:L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})\rightarrow L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
is defined as

U_{T}f(x)\doteq f(T(x)),\quad\forall f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2}).

(2.3)

We observe that any operator
U_{T}
of the form considered has eigenfunctions
f=\text{const}
corresponding to the eigenvalue
1
. Then we have the following:

Theorem 2.3

(Mixing, Theorem 2
[
5
]
)
.

T
is weakly mixing iff
U_{T}
has no eigenfunctions which are not constants.

2.2
Mixing vector fields

We are interested in divergence-free vector fields
b
whose flow
t\rightarrow X(t)\in C([0,1];G(\mathbb{T}^{2}))
when evaluated at time
t=1
is a weakly/strongly mixing automorphism. When the velocity field
b:[0,1]\times\mathbb{T}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{T}^{2}
is Lipschitz in space, uniformly in time, then its
flow
is well-defined in the classical sense, indeed it is the map
X:[0,1]\times\mathbb{T}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{T}^{2}
satisfying

\begin{cases}\frac{d}{dt}X(t,x)=b(t,X(t,x)),&\\
X_{0}(x)=x.\end{cases}

In the presence of discontinuities of the vector field (as the
\BV
regularity in space) we can still give a notion of a flow known as the
Regular Lagrangian Flow
associated with the vector field
b
.

Definition 2.4

(Regular Lagrangian Flow)
.

Let
b\in L^{1}([0,1]\times\mathbb{T}^{2};\mathbb{T}^{2})
. A map
X:[0,1]\times\mathbb{T}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{T}^{2}
is a
Regular Lagrangian Flow
(RLF) for the vector field
b
if

1.

for a.e.
x\in\mathbb{T}^{2}
the map
t\rightarrow X(t)(x)
is an absolutely continuous integral solution of

\begin{cases}\frac{d}{dt}x(t)=b(t,x(t)),&\\
x(0)=x.\end{cases}

2.

there exists a positive constant
C
independent of
t
such that

|X(t)^{-1}(A)|\leq C|A|,\quad\forall A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{T}^{2}).

Existence, uniqueness and stability for Regular Lagrangian Flows were established in
[
6
]
for the Sobolev case, and in
[
9
]
for the
\BV
regularity of the vector field. In particular, if
b\in L^{\infty}_{t}\BV_{x}
is a divergence-free vector field (that is, the distributional divergence
D\cdot b(t)=0

\mathcal{L}^{1}
-a.e.
t
), then its unique RLF
t\rightarrow X(t)
is a flow of automorphisms.

Definition 2.5
.

Let
b\in L^{\infty}([0,1],\BV_{\text{loc}}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))
be a divergence-free vector field. We say that
b
is weakly mixing (strongly mixing) if its unique RLF
X(t)
when evaluated at
t=1
is a weakly mixing (respectively strongly mixing) automorphism of
\mathbb{T}^{2}
.

The above definition is motivated by the discussion in
[
3
]
(see page 13, Lemma 3.7). The main point of this work, as in
[
3
]
,
[
7
]
, is the construction of a vector field whose RLF, when evaluated at time
t=1
, is an invertible and measure-preserving map which is weakly mixing but not strongly mixing.
In the sequel we will build flows of measure-preserving maps originating from divergence-free vector fields; more precisely, if a flow
X:[0,1]\times\mathbb{T}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{T}^{2}
is invertible, measure-preserving for
\mathcal{L}^{1}
-a.e.
t
and the map
t\rightarrow X(t)
is differentiable for
\mathcal{L}^{1}
-a.e.
t
and
\dot{X}(t)\in L^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
, then the
vector field associated with
X(t)
is the divergence-free vector field defined by

b(t)(x)=b(t,x)=\dot{X}(t)(X^{-1}(t,x)).

(2.4)

2.3
Canonical Chacon’s Transformation

We present here the one dimensional canonical Chacon’s Transformation
[
4
]
, that can be easily extended to higher dimension (see for example Section
4
for the two dimensional): indeed, this construction is based on a general geometric approach which consists in mapping subintervals of the same length linearly onto each other (see also
[
14
]
for further reference). Let us consider
I=[0,1]
and let
|\cdot|
be the Lebesgue measure. The aim is to construct a weakly mixing automorphism
T\in G(I)
which is not strongly mixing.

Definition 2.6
.

A
column

C
is a finite sequence of disjoint subintervals
J\subset I
called
levels
. The number of levels in a column is its
height

h
.

We define a family of automorphisms
\{T_{k}\}_{k}\subset G(I)
by induction.
Let
C_{0}
be the column
C_{0}\doteq I_{0,1}=\left[0,\frac{2}{3}\right)
, and let the
remaining set
be
R_{0}=\left[\frac{2}{3},1\right]
. The height of the column
C_{0}
is
h_{0}=1
, since it has a unique level
I_{0,1}
. We divide
C_{0}
into three disjoint subintervals with same length:
I_{0,1}(1)=\left[0,\frac{2}{9}\right)
,
I_{0,1}(2)=\left[\frac{2}{9},\frac{4}{9}\right)
,
I_{0,1}(3)=\left[\frac{4}{9},\frac{2}{3}\right)
. We call
spacer
the interval
S_{0}=\left[\frac{2}{3},\frac{8}{9}\right)
and
R_{1}=R_{0}\setminus S_{0}
. We observe that the spacer has the same length of
I_{0,1}(j)
for
j=1,2,3
. We put the spacer on the top of the middle interval
I_{0,1}(2)
(see Figure
5
). We define the piecewise linear map
T_{1}:I\rightarrow I
in the following way:

\begin{cases}T_{1}(I_{0,1}(1))=I_{0,1}(2),\\
T_{1}(I_{0,1}(2))=S_{0},\\
T_{1}(S_{0})=I_{0,1}(3).\end{cases}

In the left figure the Column
C_{0}

T_{1}
.

In the set
I\setminus(I_{0,1}(1)\cup I_{0,1}(2)\cup S_{0})
the map
T_{1}

T_{1}

T_{1}(I_{0,1}(3))=I_{0,1}(1)

A useful notation to represent

T_{1}\llcorner_{[0,1]\setminus R_{1}}=\left(\begin{matrix}I_{0,1}(1)&I_{0,1}(2)&I_{0,1}(3)&S_{0}\\
I_{0,1}(2)&S_{0}&I_{0,1}(1)&I_{0,1}(3)\end{matrix}\right).

(2.5)

We construct the column
C_{1}
of height
h_{1}=3h_{0}+1=4
putting one on the top of the other the intervals in the order
I_{0,1}(1),I_{0,1}(2),S_{0},I_{0,1}(3)
: the intervals are arranged so that each point is located below its image (see Figure
6
). We rename the levels
I_{1,1}=I_{0,1}(1),I_{1,2}=I_{0,1}(2),I_{1,3}=S_{0},I_{1,4}=I_{0,1}(3)
.

Figure 6:
The column
C_{1}
in which the levels are arranged one on top the other.

The inductive step is performed in the following way: we start with a column
C_{n}
made of
h_{n}=3h_{n-1}+1
levels
I_{n,1},I_{n,2},\dots,I_{n,h_{n}}
. We divide each level into three disjoint consecutive subintervals of the same length, that is
I_{n,j}=I_{n,j}(1)\cup I_{n,j}(2)\cup I_{n,j}(3)
for
j=1,\dots,h_{n}
. We consider the set
R_{n}=\left[\frac{3^{n+1}-1}{3^{n+1}},1\right]
and we construct

,

and we put it on the top of the middle interval
I_{n,h_{n}}(2)
of the bottom level
I_{n,h_{n}}
(see Figure
7
). We finally define the piecewise linear map
T_{n+1}
in the following way:

\begin{cases}T_{n+1}(I_{n,j}(i))=I_{n,j+1}(i)\quad\forall j=1,\dots h_{n}-1,\quad\forall i\in\{{1,2,3}\},\\
T_{n+1}(I_{n,h_{n}}(1))=I_{n,1}(2),\\
T_{n+1}(I_{n,h_{n}}(2))=S_{n},\\
T_{n+1}(I_{n,h_{n}}(3))=I_{n,1}(1),\\
T_{n+1}(S_{n})=I_{n,1}(3),\\
T_{n+1}=\text{id}\quad\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

A column
C_{n}
and the action of the automorphism
T_{n+1}
.

The new column
C_{n+1}
of height

Definition 2.7
.

The Canonical Chacon’s map is the automorphism

.

Observe that the limit map

is well defined, invertible and measure-preserving, indeed one can easily check that

.

Theorem 2.8

(Chacon
[

)
.

We will give a proof of this theorem in the case of two dimensional examples (see Proposition

3
Configurations and movements

In this subsection we introduce the notion of rows, columns and configurations. The advantage of this abstract description is to simplify the action of two-dimensional automorphisms on the torus as the composition of a finite number of
movements
, where each one can be described as the flow (evaluated at time
t=1
) of a divergence-free
\BV
vector field.

Definition 3.1
.

A row
r(i)
of
length

n\in\mathbb{N}
and index
i\in\mathbb{N}
is a integer-valued
1\times n
matrix

r(i)=\begin{matrix}(i,j_{1}&i,j_{2}&\dots&i,j_{n})\end{matrix},

where
j_{1},j_{2},\dots,j_{n}\in\mathbb{N}
.

Definition 3.2
.

A column
c(j)
of
length

n\in\mathbb{N}
and index
j\in\mathbb{N}
is a integer-valued
n\times 1
matrix

c(j)=\left(\begin{matrix}i_{1},j&i_{2},j&\dots&i_{n},j\end{matrix}\right)^{T},

where
i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{n}\in\mathbb{N}
.

Definition 3.3
.

A configuration
\gamma
of
size

n\in\mathbb{N}
is a integer-valued

matrix, where the entries
\gamma_{i,j}\in\{{1,2,\dots,n^{2}\}}

\gamma_{i,j}=\gamma_{h,k}
iff

. We can denote
\gamma
both
by rows
as
\gamma=(r(1),r(2),\dots,r(n))^{T}
and
by columns

\gamma=(c(1),c(2),\dots,c(n))

of size

Remark 3.4

One can easily observe that

, being
\gamma

\{1,2,\dots,n^{2}\}

Definition 3.5
.

Let

be a configuration. Two entries

or

Definition 3.6
.

A movement is a bijective map

.

Definition 3.7

(Simple exchange)
.

Let
\gamma\in\mathcal{C}(n)
and let
\gamma_{i,j}
,
\gamma_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}
be two adjacent entries. For simplicity we can assume

according to Definition

. A

that exchanges the two entries, that is

\displaystyle E_{s}(i,j;i+1;j)\left(\begin{matrix}\dots&\dots&\dots&\dots&\dots\\
i,1&\dots&i,j&\dots&i,n\\
i+1,1&\dots&i+1,j&\dots&i+1,n\\
\dots&\dots&\dots&\dots&\dots\end{matrix}\right)=\left(\begin{matrix}\dots&\dots&\dots&\dots&\dots\\
i,1&\dots&i+1,j&\dots&i,n\\
i+1,1&\dots&i,j&\dots&i+1,n\\
\dots&\dots&\dots&\dots&\dots\end{matrix}\right)

Definition 3.8

(Sort)
.

Let
\gamma\in\mathcal{C}(n)

be some fixed row index. Let

with

\displaystyle S_{c}(i;j,j^{\prime})

\displaystyle\left(\begin{matrix}\dots&\dots&\dots&\dots&\dots&\dots&\dots\\
\dots&i,j&i,j+1&\dots&i,j^{\prime}-1&i,j^{\prime}&\dots\\
\dots&\dots&\dots&\dots&\dots&\dots&\dots\end{matrix}\right)=

\displaystyle\left(\begin{matrix}\dots&\dots&\dots&\dots&\dots&\dots&\dots\\
\dots&i,j^{\prime}&i,j&i,j+1&\dots&i,j^{\prime}-1&\dots\\
\dots&\dots&\dots&\dots&\dots&\dots&\dots\end{matrix}\right).

Similarly, if
i,i^{\prime},j\in\mathbb{N}
and
i<i^{\prime}

\dots&i,j&\dots\\
\dots&i+1,j&\dots\\
\dots&\dots&\dots\\
\dots&i^{\prime},j&\dots\\
\dots&\dots&\dots\\
\end{matrix}\right)=\left(\begin{matrix}\dots&\dots&\dots\\
\dots&i^{\prime},j&\dots\\
\dots&i,j&\dots\\
\dots&\dots&\dots\\
\dots&i^{\prime}-1,j&\dots\\
\dots&\dots&\dots\\
\end{matrix}\right).

(3.1)

.

Definition 3.11

(Rotation)
.

(3.2)

\end{matrix}\right)=\left(\begin{matrix}\dots&\dots&\dots&\dots\\

\end{matrix}\right).

3.1
Flows of vector fields associated with movements

From now on we will consider the two dimensional torus
\mathbb{T}^{2}=[0,1]^{2}
with periodic boundary conditions. The idea of this subsection is to apply the previous description via configurations, rows and columns to the two dimensional torus. In particular our aim is to find vector fields whose flow acts on the torus as the movements previously defined (Sort, Exchange, Rotation) and we want to give some estimates on their total variation. We remark here that the
\BV
regularity of the vector fields make possible all these constructions, allowing for rigid
cut and paste motions
, in the spirit of
[
3
]
and
[
7
]
.

Following the notation introduced in
[
3
]
we define a flow that rotates subrectangles of
\mathbb{T}^{2}
and the vector field associated with it. More precisely we define the
rotation flow

r_{t}:\mathbb{T}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{T}^{2}
for
t\in[0,1]
in the following way: call

V(x)=\max\left\{\left|x_{1}-\frac{1}{2}\right|,\left|x_{2}-\frac{1}{2}\right|\right\}^{2},\quad(x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathbb{T}^{2}.

Then the
rotation field
is
r:\mathbb{T}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}

{r}(x)=\nabla^{\perp}V(x),

(3.4)

where
\nabla^{\perp}=(-\partial_{x_{2}},\partial_{x_{1}})
is the orthogonal gradient. Finally the rotation flow
r_{t}
is the flow of the vector field
r
, i.e. the unique solution to the following ODE system:

\begin{cases}\dot{r}(t,x)={r}(r(t,x)),\\
r(0,x)=x.\end{cases}

(3.5)

This flow rotates the unit square
[0,1]^{2}
counterclockwise of an angle
\frac{\pi}{2}
in a unit interval of time. We recall here Lemma 2.6 in
[
3
]
that gives the cost, in terms of the total variation, of the rotation of a rectangle:

Lemma 3.13
.

Let
Q\subset[0,1]^{2}
be a rectangle of sides
a,b>0
. Consider the rotating flow on the torus

(R_{Q})(t,x)=\chi_{Q}^{-1}\circ r(t)\circ\chi_{Q}(x),\quad\text{if }x\in Q.

where
\chi_{Q}:Q\rightarrow[0,1]^{2}
is the affine map sending
Q
into
[0,1]^{2}
and
r(t)
is the rotation flow defined in (
3.5
). Let
b(t)^{R_{Q}}
the divergence-free vector field associated with
(R_{Q})(t)
(extended to
0
outside the rectangle
Q
). Then

\text{\rm Tot.Var.}(b^{R}(t))(\mathbb{T}^{2})=4a^{2}+4b^{2},\quad\forall t\in[0,1].

Let us fix now some
k\in\mathbb{N}
giving the size of the partition, and let us consider the grid given by
\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}\frac{1}{k}
made of squares of side
\frac{1}{k}
. We consider the subsquares
Q_{i,j}=[\frac{j-1}{k},\frac{j}{k}]\times[\frac{k-i}{k},\frac{k-i+1}{k}]
and we identify each subsquare with an entry
\gamma_{ij}
of some configuration
\gamma\in\mathcal{C}(k)
. A horizontal stripe
H_{i}=[0,1]\times[\frac{k-i}{k},\frac{k-i+1}{k}]
is a row, while a vertical stripe
V_{j}=[\frac{j-1}{k},\frac{j}{k}]\times[0,1]
is a column. We observe that if
Q_{i,j}
and
Q_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}
are adjacent subsquares (that is, they share a common side), then
\gamma_{i,j}
and
\gamma_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}
are adjacent entries (Definition
3.5
). From now on we will identify every
\gamma\in\mathcal{C}(k)
with an enumeration of the subsquares of the torus, and any movement
T:\mathcal{C}(k)\rightarrow\mathcal{C}(k)
with an automorphism
T:\mathbb{T}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{T}^{2}
that sends rigidly every subsquare of the grid
\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}\frac{1}{k}
into another one of the same grid. We start introducing the vector fields whose RLF, when evaluated at time
t=1
, are movements.

We first recall the transposition vector field, first introduced in
[
3
]
.
Let
\kappa_{i},\kappa_{j}
be two adjacent squares of size
\frac{1}{k}
and let
Q=\kappa_{i}\cup\kappa_{j}
, then the
transposition flow
between
\kappa_{i},\kappa_{j}
is
T(t)(\kappa_{i},\kappa_{j}):[0,1]\times\mathbb{T}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{T}^{2}
defined as

T(t)(\kappa_{i},\kappa_{j})=\begin{cases}\chi^{-1}\circ r(4t)\circ\chi&x\in\mathring{Q},\ t\in\left[0,\frac{1}{2}\right],\\
\chi_{i}^{-1}\circ r(4t)\circ\chi_{i}&x\in\mathring{\kappa}_{i},\ t\in\left[\frac{1}{2},1\right],\\
\chi_{j}^{-1}\circ r(4t)\circ\chi_{j}&x\in\mathring{\kappa}_{j},\ t\in\left[\frac{1}{2},1\right],\\
x&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(3.6)

where the map
\chi:Q\rightarrow\mathbb{T}^{2}
is the affine map sending the rectangle
Q
into the torus
\mathbb{T}^{2}
,
\chi_{i},\chi_{j}
are the affine maps sending
\kappa_{i},\kappa_{j}
into the torus
\mathbb{T}^{2}
and
r
is the rotation flow (
3.5
). This invertible measure-preserving flow has the property to exchange the two subsquares in the unit time interval (Figure
8
). Moreover, by the computations done in Lemma

, we can estimate the total variation of the vector field
b^{T}(t)(\kappa_{i},\kappa_{j})
associated with

(recall
2.4
) as

\text{\rm Tot.Var.}(b^{T}(t)(\kappa_{i},\kappa_{j}))(\mathbb{T}^{2})\leq 4\frac{20}{k^{2}}.

(3.7)

We also observe that also
L^{\infty}
estimates on the vector field are easily available: indeed

\|b^{T}(\kappa_{i},\kappa_{j})\|_{L^{\infty}_{t.x}}\leq 4\frac{2}{k}.

(3.8)

A transposition between two squares is an exchange.

Definition 3.14

(Simple exchange vector field)
.

Let us fix
i,i^{\prime}\leq k
with
|i-i^{\prime}|=1
and
j\leq k
(or alternatively
i\in\mathbb{N}
and
j,j^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}
with
|j-j^{\prime}|=1

vector field
is
b(t)(i,j;i^{\prime},j)\in L^{\infty}_{t}\BV_{x}
such that, if
X(i,j;i^{\prime},j)
is its RLF evaluated at time
t=1
, then

X(i,j;i^{\prime},j)(\mathbb{T}^{2})=E_{s}(i,j;i^{\prime},j)(\gamma),\quad\forall\gamma\in\mathcal{C}(k).

(3.9)

The construction of this vector field is easy: fix for example
i^{\prime}=i+1
. Then take the two adjacent subsquares
Q_{ij}
and
Q_{i+1,j}
and perform a transposition between them
T(t)(Q_{i,j},Q_{i+1,j})
. Then define

b(t)(i,j;i+1,j)=b^{T}(t)(Q_{ij},Q_{i+1,j}).

Clearly one has

\|b(i,j;i+1,j)\|_{L^{\infty}_{t,x}}\leq\frac{8}{k},\qquad\text{\rm Tot.Var.}(b(t)(i,j;i+1,j))(\mathbb{T}^{2})\leq 4\frac{20}{k^{2}}.

(3.10)

Similarly we have

Definition 3.15

(Sort vector field)
.

Let
i\leq k
be some fixed row index. Let
j,j^{\prime}\leq k
with
j<j^{\prime}
. Then the
sort vector field
(on columns) is
b^{S_{c}}(i;j,j^{\prime})(t)\in L^{\infty}_{t}\BV_{x}
such that, if

X^{S_{c}}(i;j,j^{\prime})(\mathbb{T}^{2})=S_{c}(i;j,j^{\prime})(\gamma),\quad\forall\gamma\in\mathcal{C}(k).

(3.11)

Remark 3.16
.

Similarly one has the sort vector field on rows
b^{S_{r}}(i,i^{\prime};j)(t)
for some
i<i^{\prime}\leq k
,
j\leq k
.

To construct a sort vector field (on columns, but the construction for rows is identical), we fix
\gamma\in\mathcal{C}(k)
, a row index
i
and two columns indices
j<j^{\prime}
. The idea is to perform
j^{\prime}-j
exchanges between squares (recalling that an exchange is a transposition). For clarity see Figure

We define the flow in the following way:

{X}^{S_{c}}(i;j,j^{\prime})(t)=\begin{cases}T_{(j^{\prime}-j)t}\left(Q_{i(j^{\prime}-1)},Q_{ij^{\prime}}\right)\quad\text{for }t\in\left[0,\frac{1}{j^{\prime}-j}\right],\\

\dots\\

By definition one has

so that

being the rotation vector field of the order of the side of subsquares. This implies that

\|b^{S_{c}}(i;j,j^{\prime})\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}L^{\infty}_{x}}\leq 8.

(3.14)

Similarly one has, for every

\text{\rm Tot.Var.}(b^{S_{c}}(t)(i;j,j^{\prime}))(\mathbb{T}^{2})\leq(j^{\prime}-j)\cdot 4\cdot 4\left(\frac{4}{k^{2}}+\frac{1}{k^{2}}\right)\leq\frac{80}{k}.

(3.15)

Remark 3.17
.

Recalling Remark

and

(3.16)

So we have instead the following estimates:

(3.17)

This tells us that both the shift vector field and the sort vector field have total variation of the order of the side of the squares of the grid.

4
Two dimensional construction

Our aim is the construction of a weakly mixing vector field which is not strongly mixing, that is a divergence-free vector field
b\in L^{\infty}([0,1],\BV(\mathbb{T}^{2}))
whose RLF
X\llcorner_{t=1}
when evaluated at time
t=1
is a weakly mixing automorphism of
\mathbb{T}^{2}
but not a strongly mixing automorphism. The idea is to adapt Chacon’s one-dimensional construction of Subsection
2.3
and decompose the map into simple movements (Section
3
), described as the flow of some divergence-free BV vector field (Subsection
3.1
).
Also in this case the fundamental idea is to define
U
as the limit of a family of automorphisms
\{U_{k}\}_{k}
.

Let us consider the two dimensional torus
\mathbb{T}^{2}
as the unit square
Q_{1}=Q_{1,1}=[0,1]^{2}
with the canonical identification of boundaries. Using the notation via configurations (see Section
3
) we say that
Q_{1}
can be identified with the configuration
\gamma_{1}=(1)\in\mathcal{C}(1)
. We define
h_{1}=0
and we divide the square
Q_{1}=Q_{1,1}
into four identical subsquares each one of side
\frac{1}{2}
, more precisely:
Q_{1,1}=Q_{1,1}(1)\cup Q_{1,1}(2)\cup Q_{1,1}(3)\cup R_{1}
where
Q_{1,1}=[0,\frac{1}{2}]\times[\frac{1}{2},1]
,
Q_{1,1}(2)=[\frac{1}{2},1]\times[\frac{1}{2},1]
and
Q_{1,1}(3)=[0,\frac{1}{2}]\times[0,\frac{1}{2}]
. Clearly
R_{1}=[\frac{1}{2},1]\times[0,\frac{1}{2}]
. We define
U_{1}
on these subsquares in such a way that

U_{1}(Q_{1,1}(1))=Q_{1,1}(2),\quad U_{1}(Q_{1,1}(2))=Q_{1,1}(3)

(4.1)

and
U_{1}
is measure-preserving and invertible on
Q_{1,1}
. More precisely, we define
U_{1}
as

U_{1}(x)=\begin{cases}x+(\frac{1}{2},0)\quad\text{if}\quad x\in\mathring{Q}_{1,1}(1),\\
x+(-\frac{1}{2},-\frac{1}{2})\quad\text{if}\quad x\in\mathring{Q}_{1,1}(2),\\
x+(0,\frac{1}{2})\quad\text{if}\quad x\in\mathring{Q}_{1,1}(3),\\
x\quad\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(4.2)

We put
h_{2}=4h_{1}+3
and we rename the subsquares
Q_{1,1}(i)
as
Q_{2,1}=Q_{1,1}(1),Q_{2,2}=Q_{1,1}(2),Q_{2,3}=Q_{1,1}(3)
. We look at this via configurations: let us take
\gamma_{2}\in\mathcal{C}(2)
, for example

\gamma_{2}=\left(\begin{matrix}1&2\\
3&4\end{matrix}\right).

(4.3)

Then we can easily represent
U_{1}(\gamma_{2})
as

U_{1}(\gamma_{2})=\left(\begin{matrix}3&1\\
2&4\end{matrix}\right).

(4.4)

Here we see the advantage of the representation via configurations, which is more immediate. We continue our construction dividing each subsquare
Q_{2,i}
with
i\in\{{1,2,3}\}
into
4
subsquares of side equal to
\frac{1}{4}
:
Q_{2,i}=Q_{2,i}(1)\cup Q_{2,1}(2)\cup Q_{2,1}(3)\cup Q_{2,i}(4)
and we divide also
R_{1}=R_{1}(1)\cup R_{1}(2)\cup R_{1}(3)\cup R_{2}
into
4
subsquares (see Figure
11
).

We define the map
U_{2}
in the following way

U_{2}(x)=\begin{cases}U_{1}(x)\quad\text{if}\quad x\in\mathring{Q}_{2,1}\cup\mathring{Q}_{2,2},\\
