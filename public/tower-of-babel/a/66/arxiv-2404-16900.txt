Space-Variant Total Variation boosted by learning techniques in few-view tomographic imaging
Elena Morotti, Davide Evangelista, Andrea Sebastiani, Elena Loli Piccolomini

Abstract
This paper focuses on the development of a space-variant regularization model for solving an under-determined linear inverse problem. The case study is a medical image reconstruction from few-view tomographic noisy data. The primary objective of the proposed optimization model is to achieve a good balance between denoising and the preservation of fine details and edges, overcoming the performance of the popular and largely used Total Variation (TV) regularization through the application of appropriate pixel-dependent weights. The proposed strategy leverages the role of gradient approximations for the computation of the space-variant TV weights. For this reason, a convolutional neural network is designed, to approximate both the ground truth image and its gradient using an elastic loss function in its training. Additionally, the paper provides a theoretical analysis of the proposed model, showing the uniqueness of its solution, and illustrates a Chambolle-Pock algorithm tailored to address the specific problem at hand. This comprehensive framework integrates innovative regularization techniques with advanced neural network capabilities, demonstrating promising results in achieving high-quality reconstructions from low-sampled tomographic data.

Introduction

In this work, we consider linear inverse problems of the form:

\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta}=\boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{x}^{GT}+\boldsymbol{e},\quad||\boldsymbol{e}||_{2}\leq\delta,

(1)

where
\boldsymbol{x}^{GT}\in\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the (unknown) ground truth image,
\boldsymbol{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
is an under-sampled linear operator, i.e.
m\leq n
,
\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
is the acquired, noisy datum, and
\boldsymbol{e}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
is the noise, whose norm is bounded by
\delta
.
Note that, since
m\leq n
, the kernel of
\boldsymbol{K}
is generally non-trivial, which implies that (
1
) admits infinite solutions. Moreover, if
\boldsymbol{K}
comes from the discretization of an ill-posed integral operator (as it typically happens in imaging), the solutions of (
1
) do not continuously depend on the data
\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta}
. To handle the scarcity of data and the ill-posedness of the problem, a good solution of (
1
) can be computed by minimizing a regularized problem of the form:

\boldsymbol{x}^{*}=\arg\min_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta}):=\mathcal{F}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta})+\lambda\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{x}),

(2)

where
\mathcal{F}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta})
is the data-fidelity term
is utilized to ensure that
x^{*}

aligns with the acquisition model (
1
)
and
\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{x})
is the regularization term, enforcing stability and uniqueness of the solution by considering prior knowledge on
\boldsymbol{x}^{*}
. The scalar
\lambda>0
controls the contribution of the regularization term over the fidelity one in the overall objective function
\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta})
.

In traditional regularization approaches,
\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{x})
is applied uniformly to the whole image, i.e. its formulation equally weights the contributions across the pixels. We refer to this case as
global regularization
.
Global regularization is simple to implement and computationally low demanding;
on the other hand, it treats the entire image as a homogeneous entity making the setting the optimal weight
\lambda
not trivial conceptually, because a value may not adapt well to varying characteristics within the image.
For instance, a considerably high value of
\lambda
may effectively denoise the resulting image within flat regions, yet it could excessively smooth it, potentially leading to the loss or blurring of crucial details in certain areas. Conversely, a slightly lower value might adequately preserve details but fail to sufficiently eliminate noise in uniform areas.

To increase the flexibility of the regularization and better balance the trade-off between denoising and preserving fine details, a good strategy is the
space-variant regularization
. It allows for adapting regularization strength based on local image characteristics. This can enhance the preservation of fine details and edges without losing denoising efficacy, but its implementation is often more complex and computationally intensive than for global methods. In addition, its performance might be more sensitive to noise and artifacts, particularly if the local characteristics are not accurately estimated. In particular, the selection of optimal local regularization parameters is very challenging.

In this work, we explore an imaging application focused on reconstructing medical images from few tomographic measurements.
In this case,
\boldsymbol{K}
comes from the discretization of the 2-dimensional fan-beam Radon transform and fits the aforementioned properties of ill-posedness.
The data perturbations can be modeled with a white additive Gaussian noise, as in (
2
), hence it is common to set
\mathcal{F}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta})=\frac{1}{2}||\boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta}||_{2}^{2}
as the data-fitting term.
Our task-oriented regularizing prior should preserve diagnostically significant features as well as remove noise. As medical images often contain relevant information in the form of low-contrast objects and boundaries between different anatomical tissues, a common choice for
\mathcal{R}
is the Total Variation (TV) function. In fact, TV is known to be effective in preserving edges because it penalizes the variation or changes in pixel intensity by promoting sparsity in the gradient image domain
[
28
,
25
,
16
]
.
When globally applied, the drawback of TV regularization is the potential introduction of piecewise constant artifacts, which could impact the interpretation of medical information. It is particularly noticeable in regions with smooth intensity variations, as TV regularization tends to oversimplify these areas, resulting in blocky or stair-step artifacts.
Furthermore, considering the very restricted number of projections acquired in few-view X-ray examinations (in contrast to conventional imaging protocols), the blocky counteraction can severely promote streaking artifacts caused by the lack of views in the acquisition process.

Contributions.

The contributions of this paper are twofold, both theoretical and algorithmic.

Firstly, we demonstrate the uniqueness of the TV-based regularized solution, in the special case where
\boldsymbol{K}
is under-determined. It is widely acknowledged that the solution is unique when
\boldsymbol{K}
is over-determined; however, to the best of our knowledge, there is no proof in the under-determined case.

Secondly, we introduce a novel model for space-variant Total Variation (TV) regularization. In this model, the TV function’s weights are determined by evaluating the gradient magnitudes of a pre-computed image. We demonstrate that this image should closely approximate the ground truth, particularly within its gradient domain, to get an accurate final solution.

On the algorithmic front, we present several methods for generating the pre-calculated image as a coarse reconstruction from the given observations.
When provided with only a single datum
\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta}
, we can employ either an analytical or a variational approach for a tomographic problem.
Conversely, in cases where the medical scenario provides a dataset comprising consistent pairs of data and accurate reconstructions, we can harness the power of neural networks to generate images with desired features. We have developed a convolutional neural network trained to approximate both the ground truth image and its gradient, utilizing an elastic loss function.

Finally, we tailor the widely recognized Chambolle-Pock algorithm to address the resulting minimization problem.
Extensive numerical simulations conducted on both a synthetic image and a real dataset assess the relevance of the model for this application of tomographic imaging.

The paper is organized as follows.
In Section
2
, we initially establish the uniqueness of the solution to a weighted Total Variation regularization model, followed by an exposition of our approach for determining the weights. Subsequently, we outline the algorithmic aspects in Section
3
and
we conduct numerical experiments in Section
4
to evaluate the effectiveness of our proposal across various implementations.
Finally, we discuss our approach and set the conclusions in Section
5
, whereas we refer to the Appendix
A
for further theoretical aspects.

2
Space-Variant regularization models

In this Section, we state our regularized model and derive important theoretical results about its convergence to a unique optimal solution.

To simplify the readability of the upcoming results, we introduce here some notations.
When not specified, we always assume vectors
\boldsymbol{x}
to lie in
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, which we assume to be convex. Thus, for any
\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathcal{X}
, the symbol
\langle\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\rangle
represents the standard inner product in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Moreover, if
\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we say that
\boldsymbol{x}\leq\boldsymbol{y}
if and only if
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\leq\boldsymbol{y}_{i}
for any
i=1,\dots,n
. If
\boldsymbol{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
is a linear operator, then
\boldsymbol{A}^{T}
represents the transpose of
\boldsymbol{A}
. If
C\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then
\iota_{C}(\boldsymbol{x})
is the characteristic function of
C
, defined as:

\displaystyle\iota_{C}(\boldsymbol{x})=\begin{cases}0\quad&\mbox{if }\boldsymbol{x}\in C,\\
+\infty\quad&\mbox{if }\boldsymbol{x}\notin C.\end{cases}

(3)

Finally, we define the 2-dimensional discrete gradient operator
\boldsymbol{D}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{2n}
, such that:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{D}_{h}\boldsymbol{x}\\
\boldsymbol{D}_{v}\boldsymbol{x}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n},

(4)

where
\boldsymbol{D}_{h},\boldsymbol{D}_{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
are the discrete central differences operators associated with the horizontal and vertical derivatives, respectively, and
|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}|\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the gradient-magnitude image of
\boldsymbol{x}
, defined as:

\displaystyle\left(|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}|\right)_{i}=\sqrt{\left(\boldsymbol{D}_{h}\boldsymbol{x}\right)_{i}^{2}+\left(\boldsymbol{D}_{v}\boldsymbol{x}\right)_{i}^{2}}.

(5)

Note that, from (
4
), it holds that
(\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x})_{i}=(\boldsymbol{D}_{h}\boldsymbol{x})_{i}
for
i=1,\dots,n
, while
(\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x})_{i}=(\boldsymbol{D}_{v}\boldsymbol{x})_{i-n}
for
i=n+1,\dots,2n
.

2.1
Regularization based on Total Variation

Space-variant (or adaptive) regularization functions, which operate distinctively within each pixel of the image, have been proposed in the literature, primarily grounded in the Total Variation approach.
The popular isotropic Total Variation operator
TV(\boldsymbol{x})
is the Rudin, Osher, Fatemi prior
[
27
]
defined as:

\displaystyle TV(\boldsymbol{x})=||\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}||_{2,1}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sqrt{\left(\boldsymbol{D}_{h}\boldsymbol{x}\right)_{i}^{2}+\left(\boldsymbol{D}_{v}\boldsymbol{x}\right)_{i}^{2}}.

(6)

In a recent review by
[
26
]
, the authors demonstrate the efficacy of space-variant TV-based regularization in addressing the limitations of TV in characterizing local features. Indeed, a widely employed technique to derive space-variant models involves weighting the regularization function pixel-wise. They suggest various sparsifying regularization functions with weights derived from a Bayesian interpretation of the model.
In other papers, these weights are typically determined based on either an estimation of the data noise
[
18
,
19
,
3
,
6
]
, by an assessment of the image scaling
[
17
,
10
]
or by means of specifically trained neural networks
[
11
]
.
An alternative approach entails utilizing diverse measures of the image gradient, as exemplified in the Total p-Variation (TpV) regularization method, which typically is:

\displaystyle||\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}||_{2,p}^{p}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\sqrt{\left(\boldsymbol{D}_{h}\boldsymbol{x}\right)_{i}^{2}+\left(\boldsymbol{D}_{v}\boldsymbol{x}\right)_{i}^{2}}\right)^{p}

(7)

with
0<p<1
.
In this scenario, the parameter
p
can be adapted for each pixel of the image
[
10
,
9
]
.

All the referenced papers employ regularization techniques to address denoising and deblurring problems, wherein the observed image (datum) and the reconstructed image (solution) belong to the same space. However, our application differs in that the data (sinogram) resides in a lower-dimensional space of the solution; thus, the noise present in the sinogram varies significantly from the noise inherent in the reconstructed image. Indeed, streaking artifacts also impact the reconstructions, originating from the sparsity of the views.
Given that several of the referenced algorithms adjust adaptive weights at each iteration of the solver, leading to heightened computational overhead, they are unsuitable for the application under consideration, where optimizing execution time is paramount for practical purposes.

For these reasons, we propose a new space-variant approach wherein pixel-dependent weights are customized to eliminate noise and streaking artifacts, remaining constant throughout all iterations of the solver.
In our variational model (
2
), the space-variant regularizer
\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{x})=TV_{\boldsymbol{w}}(\boldsymbol{x})
is defined as:

\displaystyle TV_{\boldsymbol{w}}(\boldsymbol{x}):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{w}_{i}\sqrt{\left(\boldsymbol{D}_{h}\boldsymbol{x}\right)_{i}^{2}+\left(\boldsymbol{D}_{v}\boldsymbol{x}\right)_{i}^{2}}=||\boldsymbol{w}\odot|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}|||_{1},

(8)

where
\boldsymbol{w}=(\boldsymbol{w}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{w}_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the vector of weights, and
\odot
is the element-wise product.

2.2
Uniqueness of the solution

In this Section we establish the existence and uniqueness of the solution of problem (
2
) when
\mathcal{F}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta})=\frac{1}{2}||\boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta}||_{2}^{2}
and
\mathbb{R}(x)=TV_{\boldsymbol{w}}(x)
. To accomplish this, we initially focus on demonstrating the case where the regularization function
\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{x})
correspnds to the TV prior defined in (
6
).
In the following, as it is common in imaging science, we will always refer to
\mathcal{X}
as the non-negative subspace
\mathcal{X}=\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n};\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\geq\boldsymbol{0},\>\forall i=1,\dots n\}
of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

In this setting, proving the existence of (possibly many) solutions to the problem is trivial, as the objective function is convex on the convex feasible set
\mathcal{X}
.

On the contrary, the uniqueness of the optimal solution needs to be investigated more carefully and it passes through some preliminary propositions
[
20
]
. In this Section, we denote as
\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{x}):=\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta})
when the explicit dependence on
\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta}
is not relevant. Moreover, we denote as
\mathcal{J}^{*}=\min_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{x})
, whereas
\mathcal{M}:=\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}|\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{x})=\mathcal{J}^{*}\}
represents the set of the global minimizers, which is not empty since
\mathcal{J}
is convex and coercive. We can now state the first important results.

Proposition 1
.

For any
\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\boldsymbol{x}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, it holds:

\mathcal{J}\left(\frac{\boldsymbol{x}_{1}+\boldsymbol{x}_{2}}{2}\right)\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{x}_{1})+\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{x}_{2})\right)-\frac{1}{8}||\boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{x}_{1}-\boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{x}_{2}||_{2}^{2}.

Proof.

The proof is a simple algebraic manipulation of the objective function. Indeed, since
TV(\boldsymbol{x})=||\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}||_{2,1}
is a norm, by the triangular inequality we get:

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}\left(\frac{\boldsymbol{x}_{1}+\boldsymbol{x}_{2}}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left\|\boldsymbol{K}\left(\frac{\boldsymbol{x}_{1}+\boldsymbol{x}_{2}}{2}\right)-\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\mathcal{R}\left(\frac{\boldsymbol{x}_{1}+\boldsymbol{x}_{2}}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\left\|\boldsymbol{K}\left(\frac{\boldsymbol{x}_{1}+\boldsymbol{x}_{2}}{2}\right)-\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{\lambda}{2}\left(\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{x}_{1})+\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{x}_{2})\right).

Moreover, the linearity of
\boldsymbol{K}
gives:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left\|\boldsymbol{K}\left(\frac{\boldsymbol{x}_{1}+\boldsymbol{x}_{2}}{2}\right)-\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta}\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left\|\frac{\boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{x}_{1}+\boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{x}_{2}-2\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta}}{2}\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{8}\left\|\left(\boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{x}_{1}-\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta}\right)+\left(\boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{x}_{2}-\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta}\right)\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{8}\left(\left\|\boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{x}_{1}-\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\left\|\boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{x}_{2}-\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta}\right\|_{2}^{2}+2\langle\boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{x}_{1}-\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta},\boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{x}_{2}-\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta}\rangle\right).

Note that, by the bilinearity of the scalar product:

\displaystyle\langle\boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{x}_{1}-\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta},\boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{x}_{2}-\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta}\rangle=\langle\boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{x}_{2}\rangle+\langle\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta},\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta}\rangle-\langle\boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta}\rangle-\langle\boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{x}_{2},\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta}\rangle.

Now we can complete the square of
\langle\boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{x}_{2}\rangle
,
\langle\boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta}\rangle
, and
\langle\boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{x}_{2},\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta}\rangle
by adding and subtracting
\frac{||\boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{x}_{1}||_{2}^{2}}{2}
and
\frac{||\boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{x}_{2}||_{2}^{2}}{2}
to obtain:

\displaystyle 2\langle\boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{x}_{1}-\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta},\boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{x}_{2}-\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta}\rangle=-||\boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{x}_{1}-\boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{x}_{2}||_{2}^{2}+||\boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{x}_{1}-\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta}||_{2}^{2}+||\boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{x}_{2}-\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta}||_{2}^{2}.

Substituting this value in the inequality above gives:

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}\left(\frac{\boldsymbol{x}_{1}+\boldsymbol{x}_{2}}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\left\|\boldsymbol{K}\left(\frac{\boldsymbol{x}_{1}+\boldsymbol{x}_{2}}{2}\right)-\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{\lambda}{2}\left(\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{x}_{1})+\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{x}_{2})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1}{2}\left\|\boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{x}_{1}-\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\left\|\boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{x}_{2}-\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta}\right\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{1}{4}\left\|\boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{x}_{1}-\boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{x}_{2}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right)+\frac{\lambda}{2}\left(\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{x}_{1})+\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{x}_{2})\right).

Grouping together the fidelities with the corresponding regularization terms to form
\mathcal{J}
, we finally obtain:

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}\left(\frac{\boldsymbol{x}_{1}+\boldsymbol{x}_{2}}{2}\right)\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{x}_{1})+\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{x}_{2})\right)-\frac{1}{8}||\boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{x}_{1}-\boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{x}_{2}||_{2}^{2}.

∎

An important consequence of this Proposition is that the difference of any two minimizers of
\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{x})
must lie in the kernel of
\boldsymbol{K}
.

Proposition 2
.

For any
\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\boldsymbol{x}_{2}\in\mathcal{M}
, it holds
\boldsymbol{x}_{1}-\boldsymbol{x}_{2}\in\ker(\boldsymbol{K})
.

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{x})
is convex, the set
\mathcal{M}
of its minima is convex. Consequently, if both
\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\boldsymbol{x}_{2}\in\mathcal{M}
, also
\frac{\boldsymbol{x}_{1}+\boldsymbol{x}_{2}}{2}\in\mathcal{M}
.
However, by Proposition
1
, we get:

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}\left(\frac{\boldsymbol{x}_{1}+\boldsymbol{x}_{2}}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{x}_{1})+\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{x}_{2})\right)-\frac{1}{8}||\boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{x}_{1}-\boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{x}_{2}||_{2}^{2},

where:

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{x}_{1})=\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{x}_{2})=\mathcal{J}\left(\frac{\boldsymbol{x}_{1}+\boldsymbol{x}_{2}}{2}\right)=\mathcal{J}^{*}.

Consequently, we get:

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}^{*}\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathcal{J}^{*}+\mathcal{J}^{*}\right)-\frac{1}{4}||\boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{x}_{1}-\boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{x}_{2}||_{2}^{2}=\mathcal{J}^{*}-\frac{1}{8}||\boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{x}_{1}-\boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{x}_{2}||_{2}^{2}

and it holds if and only if
||\boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{x}_{1}-\boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{x}_{2}||_{2}^{2}=0
, i.e. if
\boldsymbol{K}\left(\boldsymbol{x}_{1}-\boldsymbol{x}_{2}\right)=\boldsymbol{0}
which concludes the proof.
∎

Note that, since
\boldsymbol{K}
is undersampled by assumption,
\ker(\boldsymbol{K})
is not trivial in general, which implies that Proposition
2
is not sufficient to prove that the solution of (
2
) is unique. However, Proposition
2
implies an interesting property of the TV regularizer.

Proposition 3
.

For any
\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\boldsymbol{x}_{2}\in\mathcal{M}
, it holds
\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{x}_{1})=\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{x}_{2})
.

Proof.

Since we assume
\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\boldsymbol{x}_{2}\in\mathcal{M}
, Proposition
2
suggests that
\boldsymbol{x}_{1}-\boldsymbol{x}_{2}\in\ker(\boldsymbol{K})
, i.e.
\boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{x}_{1}=\boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{x}_{2}
and it implies that
\mathcal{F}(\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta})=\mathcal{F}(\boldsymbol{x}_{2},\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta})
. Moreover, by convexity,
\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{x}_{1})=\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{x}_{2})=\mathcal{J}^{*}
, hence the result trivially follows.
∎

A last step to prove the uniqueness of the solution to the TV-grounded Problem (
2
) requires fixing some preliminary notations.
Denoting by
I_{h}=\{1,\dots,n\}
and
I_{v}=\{n+1,\dots,2n\}
the sets of indices of
\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}
related to the horizontal and vertical derivatives of
\boldsymbol{x}
, the operator
\hat{\boldsymbol{D}}\boldsymbol{x}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{2n}
is defined as follows:

\displaystyle\forall\ i\in I_{h},\quad(\hat{\boldsymbol{D}}\boldsymbol{x})_{i}=(\hat{\boldsymbol{D}}_{h}\boldsymbol{x})_{i}:=\begin{cases}\frac{\left(\boldsymbol{D}_{h}\boldsymbol{x}\right)_{i}}{\left(|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}|\right)_{i}}&\mbox{if}\left(|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}|\right)_{i}\neq 0,\\
\frac{1}{2}&\mbox{otherwise},\end{cases}

(9)

\displaystyle\forall\ i\in I_{v},\quad(\hat{\boldsymbol{D}}\boldsymbol{x})_{i}=(\hat{\boldsymbol{D}}_{v}\boldsymbol{x})_{i-n}:=\begin{cases}\frac{\left(\boldsymbol{D}_{v}\boldsymbol{x}\right)_{i-n}}{\left(|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}|\right)_{i-n}}&\mbox{if}\left(|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}|\right)_{i-n}\neq 0,\\
\frac{1}{2}&\mbox{otherwise},\end{cases}

(10)

where
\left(|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}|\right)_{i}
is the
i
-th element of the gradient magnitude of
\boldsymbol{x}
, as defined in (
5
). The importance of
\hat{\boldsymbol{D}}
resides in the following property.

Proposition 4
.

For any
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}
, it holds
||\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}||_{2,1}=\langle\hat{\boldsymbol{D}}\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}\rangle
.

Proof.

First of all, by definition, we get:

\displaystyle||\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}||_{2,1}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}|\right)_{i}.

whereas:

\displaystyle\langle\hat{\boldsymbol{D}}\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{2n}(\hat{\boldsymbol{D}}\boldsymbol{x})_{i}\left(\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}\right)_{i}.

Note that, for any
i\in I_{h}
such that
\left(|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}\right|)_{i}\neq 0
it holds
(\hat{\boldsymbol{D}}\boldsymbol{x})_{i}\left(\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}\right)_{i}=\frac{\left(\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}\right)_{i}^{2}}{\left(|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}|\right)_{i}}
, and similarly
(\hat{\boldsymbol{D}}\boldsymbol{x})_{i}\left(\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}\right)_{i}=\frac{\left(\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}\right)_{i}^{2}}{\left(|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}|\right)_{i-n}}
for any
i\in I_{v}
such that
\left(|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}\right|)_{i-n}\neq 0
.
On the contrary,
(\hat{\boldsymbol{D}}\boldsymbol{x})_{i}\left(\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}\right)_{i}=0
whenever
\left(|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}\right|)_{i}=0
for
i\in I_{h}
and whenever
\left(|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}\right|)_{i-n}=0
for
i\in I_{v}
.
Consequently:

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{2n}(\hat{\boldsymbol{D}}\boldsymbol{x})_{i}\left(\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}\right)_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i:\left(|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}\right|)_{i}\neq 0}\frac{\left(\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}\right)_{i}^{2}}{\left(|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}|\right)_{i}}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in I_{h}\\
\left(|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}\right|)_{i}\neq 0\end{subarray}}\frac{\left(\boldsymbol{D}_{h}\boldsymbol{x}\right)_{i}^{2}}{\left(|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}|\right)_{i}}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in I_{v}\\
\left(|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}\right|)_{i-n}\neq 0\end{subarray}}\frac{\left(\boldsymbol{D}_{v}\boldsymbol{x}\right)_{i-n}^{2}}{\left(|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}|\right)_{i-n}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in I_{h}\\
\left(|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}\right|)_{i}\neq 0\end{subarray}}\frac{\left(\boldsymbol{D}_{h}\boldsymbol{x}\right)_{i}^{2}+\left(\boldsymbol{D}_{v}\boldsymbol{x}\right)_{i}^{2}}{\left(|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}|\right)_{i}}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in I_{h}\\
\left(|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}\right|)_{i}\neq 0\end{subarray}}\frac{\left(|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}|\right)_{i}^{2}}{\left(|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}|\right)_{i}}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in I_{h}\\
\left(|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}\right|)_{i}=0\end{subarray}}\left(|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}\right|)_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}\right|)_{i}=||\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}||_{2,1},

which concludes the proof.
∎

With these tools, we can now prove the last preliminary propositions, which will allow to prove the main result for this Section.

Proposition 5
.

Let
\boldsymbol{x}_{1}\in\mathcal{M}
and
\bar{I}=\{i\in\{1,\dots,n\}\ |\ \left(|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}_{1}|\right)_{i}=0\}
. Considering the set
S_{1}:=\{\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\>|\>\ \left(|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}|\right)_{i}=0\ \forall\ i\in\bar{I}\}
. Then
S_{1}
is a vectorial space and
\boldsymbol{x}_{1}\in S_{1}
.

Proof.

Clearly,
\boldsymbol{x}_{1}\in S_{1}
since
\left(|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}_{1}|\right)_{i}=0
for any
i\in\bar{I}
. Moreover,
\boldsymbol{0}\in S_{1}
since
\left(|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{0}|\right)_{i}
equals
0
for any
i=1,\dots,n
and in particular for any
i\in\bar{I}
. Now, let
\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w}\in S_{1}
and
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
. Then
\boldsymbol{t}:=\alpha\boldsymbol{v}+\beta\boldsymbol{w}\in S_{1}
since
\left(|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{t}|\right)_{i}=\alpha\left(|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}|\right)_{i}+\beta\left(|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{w}|\right)_{i}=0
for any
i\in\bar{I}
. This concludes the proof.
∎

We can now prove the uniqueness of the solution of an underdetermined inverse problem with TV regularization, with the following Theorem.

Theorem 6

(Unicity theorem)
.

Let
\boldsymbol{x}_{1}\in\mathcal{M}
and assume:

(i)

\exists\ \boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
s.t.
\boldsymbol{D}^{T}\hat{\boldsymbol{D}}\boldsymbol{x}_{1}=\boldsymbol{K}^{T}\boldsymbol{z}
,

(ii)

\ker(\boldsymbol{K})\cap S_{1}=\{\boldsymbol{0}\}
.

Then the solution of Problem (
2
) with
\mathcal{F}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta})=\frac{1}{2}||\boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta}||_{2}^{2}
and
\mathbb{R}(x)=TV(x)
is unique, i.e.
\mathcal{M}=\{\boldsymbol{x}_{1}\}
.

Proof.

Let
\boldsymbol{x}_{1}\in\mathcal{M}
and we consider
\boldsymbol{x}_{2}\in\mathcal{M}
to show that necessarily
\boldsymbol{x}_{1}=\boldsymbol{x}_{2}
. To this aim, we consider the two complementary cases of
\boldsymbol{x}_{2}\in S_{1}
and
\boldsymbol{x}_{2}\notin S_{1}
.

If
\boldsymbol{x}_{2}\in S_{1}
, then
\boldsymbol{x}_{1}-\boldsymbol{x}_{2}\in S_{1}
as well, since
S_{1}
is a vectorial space by Proposition
5
and it is closed by sum. Moreover, by Proposition
2
,
\boldsymbol{x}_{1}-\boldsymbol{x}_{2}\in\ker(\boldsymbol{K})
. Consequently, for the second assumption of this Theorem,
\boldsymbol{x}_{1}=\boldsymbol{x}_{2}
.

If
\boldsymbol{x}_{2}\notin S_{1}
, there must exist at least an index
i_{0}\in\bar{I}
such that
\left(|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}_{2}|\right)_{i_{0}}\neq 0
. In addition, according to the definition of
\hat{\boldsymbol{D}}
given in (
9
) and (
10
),
\left(\boldsymbol{D}_{h}\boldsymbol{x}_{1}\right)_{i_{0}}=\left(\boldsymbol{D}_{v}\boldsymbol{x}_{1}\right)_{i_{0}}=0
and
(\hat{\boldsymbol{D}}_{h}\boldsymbol{x}_{1})_{i_{0}}=(\hat{\boldsymbol{D}}_{v}\boldsymbol{x}_{1})_{i_{0}}=\frac{1}{2}
, .

Now, recalling the result in Proposition
4
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{x}_{1})=||\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}_{1}||_{2,1}=\langle\hat{\boldsymbol{D}}\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}_{1}\rangle,

and by means of adjoint matrix, if
\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the variable defined by the first hypothesis:

\displaystyle\langle\hat{\boldsymbol{D}}\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}_{1}\rangle=\langle\boldsymbol{D}^{T}\hat{\boldsymbol{D}}\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\boldsymbol{x}_{1}\rangle=\langle\boldsymbol{K}^{T}\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{x}_{1}\rangle.

Thus, by Propositions
2
and
4
, we get:

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{x}_{1})

\displaystyle=\langle\boldsymbol{K}^{T}\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{x}_{1}\rangle=\langle\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{x}_{1}\rangle=\langle\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{x}_{2}\rangle=\langle\boldsymbol{K}^{T}\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{x}_{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\boldsymbol{D}^{T}\hat{\boldsymbol{D}}\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\boldsymbol{x}_{2}\rangle=\langle\hat{\boldsymbol{D}}\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}_{2}\rangle.

In addition, we can derive the following inequality:

\displaystyle\langle\hat{\boldsymbol{D}}\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}_{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{2n}(\hat{\boldsymbol{D}}\boldsymbol{x}_{1})_{i}(\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}_{2})_{i}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sqrt{(\hat{\boldsymbol{D}}_{h}\boldsymbol{x}_{1})_{i}^{2}+(\hat{\boldsymbol{D}}_{v}\boldsymbol{x}_{1})_{i}^{2}}\cdot\sqrt{(\boldsymbol{D}_{h}\boldsymbol{x}_{2})_{i}^{2}+(\boldsymbol{D}_{v}\boldsymbol{x}_{2})_{i}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sqrt{(\hat{\boldsymbol{D}}_{h}\boldsymbol{x}_{1})_{i}^{2}+(\hat{\boldsymbol{D}}_{v}\boldsymbol{x}_{1})_{i}^{2}}\cdot\left(|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}_{2}|\right)_{i},

for the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality applied onto the 2-dimensional vectors
[(\hat{\boldsymbol{D}}_{h}\boldsymbol{x}_{1})_{i},(\hat{\boldsymbol{D}}_{v}\boldsymbol{x}_{1})_{i}]
and
[(\boldsymbol{D}_{h}\boldsymbol{x}_{2})_{i},(\boldsymbol{D}_{v}\boldsymbol{x}_{2})_{i}]
, for all the indexes
i
separately.
To conclude, note that by definition of
\hat{\boldsymbol{D}}
, it holds:

\displaystyle\sqrt{(\hat{\boldsymbol{D}}_{h}\boldsymbol{x}_{1})_{i}^{2}+(\hat{\boldsymbol{D}}_{v}\boldsymbol{x}_{1})_{i}^{2}}=\begin{cases}1\quad&\mbox{if }\left(|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}_{1}|\right)_{i}\neq 0,\\
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\quad&\mbox{if }\left(|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}_{1}|\right)_{i}=0.\end{cases}

(11)

Consequently,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sqrt{(\hat{\boldsymbol{D}}_{h}\boldsymbol{x}_{1})_{i}^{2}+(\hat{\boldsymbol{D}}_{v}\boldsymbol{x}_{1})_{i}^{2}}\left(|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}_{2}|\right)_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}\setminus\{i_{0}\}}\sqrt{(\hat{\boldsymbol{D}}_{h}\boldsymbol{x}_{1})_{i}^{2}+(\hat{\boldsymbol{D}}_{v}\boldsymbol{x}_{1})_{i}^{2}}\left(|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}_{2}|\right)_{i}+\sqrt{(\hat{\boldsymbol{D}}_{h}\boldsymbol{x}_{1})_{i_{0}}^{2}+(\hat{\boldsymbol{D}}_{v}\boldsymbol{x}_{1})_{i_{0}}^{2}}\left(|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}_{2}|\right)_{i_{0}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}\setminus\{i_{0}\}}\left(|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}_{2}|\right)_{i}+\sqrt{(\hat{\boldsymbol{D}}_{h}\boldsymbol{x}_{1})_{i_{0}}^{2}+(\hat{\boldsymbol{D}}_{v}\boldsymbol{x}_{1})_{i_{0}}^{2}}\left(|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}_{2}|\right)_{i_{0}}

\displaystyle<\sum_{i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}\setminus\{i_{0}\}}\left(|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}_{2}|\right)_{i}+\left(|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}_{2}|\right)_{i_{0}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}_{2}|\right)_{i}=||\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}_{2}||_{2,1}=\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{x}_{2}).

Bringing together the previous inequalities, we have demonstrated that
\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{x}_{1})<\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{x}_{2})
, which is a contradiction with Proposition
3
, where we proved that if
\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\boldsymbol{x}_{2}\in\mathcal{M}
, then
\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{x}_{1})=\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{x}_{2})
. Thus, every element of
\mathcal{M}
must be in
S_{1}
and it implies that
\mathcal{M}=\{\boldsymbol{x}_{1}\}
.
∎

Note that the first condition of Theorem
6
is satisfied whenever
\boldsymbol{D}^{T}\hat{\boldsymbol{D}}\boldsymbol{x}_{1}\in Rg(\boldsymbol{K}^{T})
, the range of
\boldsymbol{K}^{T}
. We recall that
Rg(\boldsymbol{K}^{T})=\ker(\boldsymbol{K})^{\perp}
, whose dimension depends on the number of CT acquisitions performed by
\boldsymbol{K}
. Consequently, Theorem
6
implies that, whenever the number of acquisitions is sufficiently large, then the solution to Problem (
2
) is unique.

In addition, Theorem
6
can be simply extended to prove the uniqueness of the solution of any space-variant TV regularized model, i.e. when
\mathbb{R}(x)=TV_{\boldsymbol{w}}(x)
.
To achieve this, consider the matrix
\mathcal{W}
defined as a
2n\times 2n
diagonal matrix with two equal diagonal blocks of size
n\times n
, where the elements
\left(\boldsymbol{w}\right)_{i}
reside on the diagonal. Observing that:

\displaystyle||\boldsymbol{w}\odot|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}|||_{1}=||\mathcal{W}\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}||_{2,1},

we can state the following Corollary.

Corollary 7
.

Replacing
\boldsymbol{D}
with
\mathcal{W}\boldsymbol{D}
in the assumptions of Theorem
6
, then the problem:

\displaystyle\min_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta}):=\frac{1}{2}||\boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta}||_{2}^{2}+\lambda||\boldsymbol{w}\odot|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}|||_{1}

(12)

always admits a unique solution in
\mathcal{X}=\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n};\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\geq\boldsymbol{0}\ \forall i=1,\dots n\}
.

The proof is trivial by replacing
\boldsymbol{D}
with
\mathcal{W}\boldsymbol{D}
in the proof of Theorem
6
.

In the following, we assume that these hypotheses are satisfied by our operator
\boldsymbol{K}
.

2.3
On the choice of the weights

In this Section we focus on the selection of the weights
\boldsymbol{w}
in the TV model (
12
), which is a matter of high delicacy.

Ideally,
w_{i}
should be small if the corresponding pixel
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
of image to be restored lies either close to an edge of a low-contrast region or on a small detail, so that the regularization strength on that pixel is moderate and the fidelity term recovers the information provided in the data
\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta}
. Similarly, to maximize the regularization effect,
\boldsymbol{w}_{i}
should be high on large and uniform regions of
\boldsymbol{x}
so that the emerging of streaking artifacts and the noise propagation get removed or hindered.

To determine the weights
\boldsymbol{w}
, we drew inspiration from the paper
[
28
]
, where the authors successfully utilize the TpV regularization (
7
) in few-view CT. This regularization method efficiently promotes sparsity in the gradient domain and mitigates the undesired blurring effects often associated with Total Variation regularization, particularly on critical details and edges. In particular, we establish the weighting factors motivated by the iterative reweighting
\ell_{1}
-norm strategy
[
5
,
12
]
, commonly utilized in solving TpV problems, as:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{w}_{\eta}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}):=\left(\frac{\eta}{\sqrt{\eta^{2}+|\boldsymbol{D}\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}|^{2}}}\right)^{1-p},

(13)

where
\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a suitable image and
\eta\geq 0
is a scalar parameter.

The following statements serve to elucidate the conceptual underpinnings of our weight parameters, formally.

Proposition 8
.

For any
\eta>0
, any
0<p<1
, and any
\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\left(w_{\eta}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}})\right)_{i}\in(0,1]
,
\forall i=1,\dots,n
. Moreover,
\left(\boldsymbol{w}_{\eta}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}})\right)_{i}=1
on a pixel
i\in 1,\dots,n
if and only if
|\boldsymbol{D}\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}|_{i}=0
.

Proof.

Note that, for any
0<p<1
,
1-p>0
. Consequently,

\displaystyle\left(\boldsymbol{w}_{\eta}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}})\right)_{i}=\left(\frac{\eta}{\sqrt{\eta^{2}+|\boldsymbol{D}\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}|^{2}}}\right)^{1-p}\in(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{1}]\iff\frac{\eta}{\sqrt{\eta^{2}+|\boldsymbol{D}\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}|^{2}}}\in(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{1}].

Moreover, since both
\eta
and
\sqrt{\eta^{2}+|\boldsymbol{D}\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}|^{2}}
are strictly positive, their ratio is necessarily strictly positive. On the other side,

\displaystyle\frac{\eta}{\sqrt{\eta^{2}+|\boldsymbol{D}\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}|^{2}}}\leq\boldsymbol{1}\iff\eta\leq\sqrt{\eta^{2}+|\boldsymbol{D}\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}|^{2}}\iff|\boldsymbol{D}\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}|^{2}\geq\boldsymbol{0},

which holds for any
\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and
|\boldsymbol{D}\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}|_{i}^{2}=0
on a pixel
i
if and only if
|\boldsymbol{D}\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}|_{i}=0
.
∎

Now we observe that whenever
|\boldsymbol{D}\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}|_{i}=0
(i.e., if
\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}
is on a flat region), then
\left(\boldsymbol{w}_{\eta}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}})\right)_{i}=1
, and the Total Variation is exactly weighted by
\lambda
. Conversely, whenever
|\boldsymbol{D}\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}|_{i}\gg 0
(indicating that pixel
i
is either on an edge or a small detail), then
\left(\boldsymbol{w}_{\eta}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}})\right)_{i}<1
, signifying that the Total Variation regularization is weaker, and the details are preserved.

Focusing now on the role of
\eta
in the computation of the weights
\boldsymbol{w}_{\eta}
, Figure
1
shows the value of
\left(\boldsymbol{w}_{\eta}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}})\right)_{i}
at increasing values of
|\boldsymbol{D}\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}|_{i}
, for different values of
\eta>0
.
It is evident that, for very small values of
\eta
,
\left(\boldsymbol{w}_{\eta}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}})\right)_{i}
rapidly decreases to zero (i.e., the solution of the problem is not regularized around pixel
i
). On the other side, for large values of
\eta
,
\left(\boldsymbol{w}_{\eta}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}})\right)_{i}
decreases very slowly as a function of
|\boldsymbol{D}\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}|_{i}
, which implies that small details will be flattened out.
Wrapping up the analysis of weights, we emphasize that since the optimal values of
\boldsymbol{w}_{\eta}
are linked to the contrasts and the arrangement of details in
\boldsymbol{x}^{GT}
, we strive for
|\boldsymbol{D}\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}|
to closely resemble
|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}^{GT}|
, especially near the edges.

Figure 1:
A plot of
\left(\boldsymbol{w}_{\eta}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}})\right)_{i}
for different values of
\eta
, over
|\boldsymbol{D}\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}|_{i}
.

To provide a more precise specification on how to derive
\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}
from
\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta}
we consider
\Psi:\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
as a Lipschitz-continuous function that maps
\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta}
to an approximate reconstruction
\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}=\Psi(\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta})
of
\boldsymbol{x}^{GT}
. In accordance with the nomenclature introduced in
[
15
]
, we denote such mappings as
reconstructors
.

Hence, given a reconstructor
\Psi:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\lambda>0
and
\eta>0
, we can formalize the proposed weighted TV model as:

\displaystyle\arg\min_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\frac{1}{2}||\boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta}||_{2}^{2}+\lambda||\boldsymbol{w}_{\eta}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}})\odot|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}|||_{1}

(14)

where
\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}=\Psi(\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta})
, the space-variant weights are computed as in Equation (
13
) and
\mathcal{X}=\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n};\ \boldsymbol{x}_{i}\geq\boldsymbol{0},\ \forall i=1,\dots n\}
, representing the non-negative subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
In the following, we denote the solution of (
14
) as
\boldsymbol{x}^{*}_{\Psi,\delta}
and
we refer to this model as
\Psi
-W
\ell_{1}
to remark its reliance on
\Psi
in the weighted
\ell_{1}
-norm regularization (as further elaborated upon in Section
2.4
).

We have already intuitively observed that
\boldsymbol{x}^{GT}
would be a suitable candidate for
\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}
. This intuition is further confirmed by the numerical results obtained from simulations (presented in Section
4
). Indeed, if we use the ground truth image to compute the space-variant weights, we can achieve highly accurate solutions (that will be referred to as
\boldsymbol{x}^{*}_{GT,\delta}
, with a minor notational adaptation).

However, the ground truth images are not available in real cases, hence we need to use a reconstructor working on the noisy data.
Under a few assumptions, we can demonstrate that
if
|\boldsymbol{D}\Psi(\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta})|
provides a good approximation of
|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}^{GT}|
, then the solution obtained considering
\boldsymbol{w}_{\eta}(\Psi(\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta}))
is a reliable approximation of
\boldsymbol{x}^{*}_{GT,\delta}
.
To this aim, we need to build a sequence of reconstructors
\Psi_{k}:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
indexed by
k
, and demonstrate the following theorem.

Theorem 9
.

Given
\delta>0
, let
\{\Psi_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of reconstructors such that:

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}|||\boldsymbol{D}\Psi_{k}(\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta})|-|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}^{GT}|||_{1}=0,\quad\forall\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta}=\boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{x}^{GT}+\boldsymbol{e},||\boldsymbol{e}||_{2}\leq\delta,

then:

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}||\boldsymbol{x}^{*}_{\Psi_{k},\delta}-\boldsymbol{x}^{*}_{GT,\delta}||_{1}=0.

The verification of this theorem necessitates numerous observations, the proofs of which, along with the theorem’s proof itself, are meticulously expounded in the Appendix
A
. This organizational choice is made to uphold the overall readability and fluidity of the entire manuscript.

At last, we observe that the above theorem leads to the following result, straightforwardly.

Corollary 10
.

Given
\delta>0
, let
\{\Psi_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of reconstructor such that:

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}||\Psi_{k}(\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta})-\boldsymbol{x}^{GT}||_{1}=0,\quad\forall\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta}=\boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{x}^{GT}+\boldsymbol{e},||\boldsymbol{e}||_{2}\leq\delta,

then:

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}||\boldsymbol{x}^{*}_{\Psi_{k},\delta}-\boldsymbol{x}^{*}_{GT,\delta}||_{1}=0.

The Corollary suggests that if we can fix a reconstructor whose output is sufficiently close to
\boldsymbol{x}^{GT}
for any noisy datum,
the optimal images
\boldsymbol{x}^{*}_{\Psi_{k},\delta}
and
\boldsymbol{x}^{*}_{GT,\delta}
(computed with the respective weights) will be sufficiently close to each other.

2.4
Proposed
\Psi
reconstructors

We can now propose various possible choices to define the reconstructor
\Psi(\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta})
and compute
\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}
from the sinogram
\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta}
.

Dealing with tomographic imaging from X-ray measurements, a simple yet fast reconstructor is the Filtered Back Projection (FBP) solver
[
21
]
. As an analytical algorithm, it operates by applying a filter to the back-projected data, disregarding the inverse problem formulation of the imaging task. Even if FBP may not address all reconstruction challenges, its speed and simplicity make it a pragmatic choice for many medical imaging scenarios, particularly when balancing computational resources and reconstruction quality is essential.

A further option is setting
\Psi(\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta})
as an early-stopped variational solver. For example, if we still consider the global TV regularization (
6
) and stop the iterative algorithm for the solution of the corresponding problem (
2
) according to computational constraints.
While in few-view CT the FBP solution is typically characterized by neat details but also by noise and streaking artifacts, the solution computed by an early stopped TV solver is expected to be a blurred version of the ground truth image, with moderate few-view-caused artifacts.
However, we observe that these reconstructors can only provide images approximating
\boldsymbol{x}^{GT}
in a classical interpretation.

Figure 2:
Visual depictions of the proposed
\Psi
-W
\ell_{1}
method, illustrating the key computational steps of our proposal, spanning from the sinogram to the final reconstruction. On the top, the scenario where
\Psi
is implemented by a single solver; on the bottom, the case where it is formed through a composition of two functions.

Another method entails the use of a composition of two reconstructors. We can in fact define
\Psi(\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta})=\Psi_{2}(\Psi_{1}(\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta}))
, where
\Psi_{1}(\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta})
can be one of the previously described reconstructors applied to map the data
\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta}
to the image domain, whereas
\Psi_{2}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is designed to enhance the quality of
\Psi_{1}(\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta})
.
In Figure
2
, we visually illustrate the contrast between the current and the previous methods for the establishing of
\Psi
in the
\Psi
-W
\ell_{1}
model.

In our specific case, we have chosen
\Psi_{2}
to be a neural network (NN).
As extensively documented in the literature, NNs possess the capability to execute efficient image-processing computations through pre-trained deep architectures, offering the flexibility to estimate various image transformations, such as the image gradients
[
22
,
7
]
or the image shearlets coefficients
[
4
]
. However, it is crucial to note that the accuracy of NN reconstructors is contingent upon the quality of the training samples and a considerable computational effort is required during their offline training phase.

It is noteworthy that, in the context of medical imaging, the collection of data from the same clinical device allows for the construction of a coherent dataset for training a network.
Therefore, we can presume the availability of the training set
\mathcal{D}
, constituted by
M
couples
(\boldsymbol{x}^{GT}_{j},\Psi_{1}(\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta}_{j}))
of high-quality reconstructed images
\boldsymbol{x}^{GT}_{j}
(to be used as ground truth images) and the corresponding low-quality reconstructions
\Psi_{1}(\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta}_{j})
, for all
j=1,\dots,M
.

We utilize in our experiments the renowned Residual U-Net architecture as described in
[
24
,
14
]
.
A neural network-based reconstructor
\Psi_{2}
can be seen as a
\Theta
parameter-dependent solver, where
\Theta
represents the high-dimensional vector of the NN inner weights; therefore we will denote it as
\Psi_{\Theta}
in the following.
To set these weights, we need to train the network having established a loss function to be minimized.
Inspired by the convergence results in Theorem
9
and Corollary
10
where we respectively assume that
|\boldsymbol{D}\Psi_{k}(\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta})|\to|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}^{GT}|
and
\Psi_{k}(\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta})\to\boldsymbol{x}^{GT}\ \mbox{for\ }k\to\infty
, we have designed a loss function such that each
\Psi_{\Theta}(\Psi_{1}(\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta}_{j}))
image approximates not only the ground truth one but also the magnitude images
|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}^{GT}_{j}|
.
To this aim, inspired by the regularization technique proposed in
[
2
]
, we train
\Psi_{\Theta}
with the
elastic loss
:

\Theta^{*}\in\arg\min_{\Theta}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\alpha|||\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}^{GT}_{j}|-|\boldsymbol{D}\Psi_{\Theta}(\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta}_{j})|||_{2}^{2}\ +\ (1-\alpha)||\boldsymbol{x}^{GT}_{j}-\Psi_{\Theta}(\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta}_{j})||_{2}^{2}

(15)

where
\alpha\in[0,1]
.
Setting
\alpha=0
yields a network aimed at achieving the best image approximation, whereas
\alpha=1
directs the training process to focus solely on learning gradient images.
We anticipate that, in our experimental setting, we will consider
\alpha=\{0,0.5,1\}
to test the effectiveness of the elastic loss in these extreme
\alpha
values as well as in its central elastic coefficient.
In addition, we will use
\Psi_{1}
as the FBP algorithm, to get a comprehensive reconstructing framework of very fast execution.
As training samples, we use
M
=3305 couples
(\boldsymbol{x}^{GT}_{j},\Psi_{1}(\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta}_{j}))
of real chest images extracted from the Low Dose CT Grand Challenge data set by the Mayo Clinic
[
23
]
. They are
256\times 256
pixel resolution and are normalized in the unitary interval.
We perform 50 epochs with Adam optimizer with a learning rate equal to
10^{-3}
and
\beta_{1}=0.9
,
\beta_{2}=0.9999
to complete the training phase.

3
Chambolle-Pock algorithm

To solve the optimization problem (
14
), we consider the Chambolle-Pock (CP) algorithm
[
8
]
, that is a popular iterative method used to solve the
TV
-regularized inverse problem
[
25
,
28
]
in few-view CT. In its original formulation, it can be employed to minimize an objective function of the form:

\displaystyle\min_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}F(\boldsymbol{M}\boldsymbol{x})+G(\boldsymbol{x}),

(16)

where both
F
and
G
are real-valued, proper, convex, lower semi-continuous functions and
\boldsymbol{M}
is a linear operator from
\mathbb{R}^{n}
to
\mathbb{R}^{s}
.
Note that there are no constraints on the smoothness of either
F
and
G
; therefore, the method can be applied to our problem by setting:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}G(\boldsymbol{x})=\iota_{\mathcal{X}}(\boldsymbol{x}),\\
F(\boldsymbol{M}\boldsymbol{x})=\mathcal{J}_{\Psi}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta})=\frac{1}{2}||\boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta}||_{2}^{2}+\lambda||\boldsymbol{w}_{\eta}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}})\odot|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}|||_{1},\end{cases}

(17)

where
\iota_{\mathcal{X}}(\boldsymbol{x})
is the indicator function of the feasible set
\mathcal{X}
. As already stated, we consider
\mathcal{X}
as the non-negative subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

To apply the CP method to our problem, we define the linear operator
\boldsymbol{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{s\times n}
by concatenating row-wise
\boldsymbol{K}
and
\boldsymbol{D}
, namely
\boldsymbol{M}=\left[\boldsymbol{K};\boldsymbol{D}\right]
and
s=(m+2n)
. The CP algorithm considers the primal-dual formulation of (
16
), that reads:

\displaystyle\min_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\max_{\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{m+2n}}\boldsymbol{z}^{T}\boldsymbol{M}\boldsymbol{x}+G(\boldsymbol{x})-F^{*}(\boldsymbol{z}),

(18)

where
F^{*}
is the convex conjugate of
F

[
1
]
, defined as:

\displaystyle F^{*}(\boldsymbol{z}^{*}):=\sup_{\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{m+2n}}\left\{\boldsymbol{z}^{T}\boldsymbol{z}^{*}-F(\boldsymbol{z})\right\}.

(19)

Given a starting guess for both the primal variable
\boldsymbol{x}^{(0)}
and the dual variable
\boldsymbol{z}^{(0)}
, the update rule is the following:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\boldsymbol{z}^{(k+1)}=\mathrm{prox}_{\sigma F^{*}}\left(\boldsymbol{z}^{(k)}+\sigma\boldsymbol{M}\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}^{(k)}\right),\\
\boldsymbol{x}^{(k+1)}=\mathrm{prox}_{\tau G}\left(\boldsymbol{x}^{(k)}-\tau\boldsymbol{M}^{T}\boldsymbol{z}^{(k+1)}\right),\\
\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}^{(k+1)}=\boldsymbol{x}^{(k+1)}+\beta\left(\boldsymbol{x}^{(k+1)}-\boldsymbol{x}^{(k)}\right),\end{cases}

(20)

where
\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}^{(0)}=\boldsymbol{0}
,
\beta\in[0,1]
is a parameter that we set equal to 1 in the experiments, while
\sigma>0
,
\tau>0
are computed as
\sigma=\tau\approx\frac{1}{||\boldsymbol{M}||_{2}}
. A reliable approximation of
||\boldsymbol{M}||_{2}
can be computed by means of the power method
[
13
]
.

Focusing on
G(\boldsymbol{x})
, its proximal operator corresponds to the projection
\mathcal{P}_{+}
over the non-negative subspace
\mathcal{X}
. Consequently the updating rule of the primal variable
\boldsymbol{x}^{(k)}
becomes:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}^{(k+1)}=\mathcal{P}_{+}\left(\boldsymbol{x}^{(k)}-\tau\boldsymbol{M}^{T}\boldsymbol{z}^{(k+1)}\right).

(21)

The explicit derivation of
\mathrm{prox}_{\sigma F^{*}}
requires the introduction of two dual variables,
\boldsymbol{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
\boldsymbol{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
, such that:

\displaystyle F(\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q})=\underbrace{\frac{1}{2}||\boldsymbol{p}-\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta}||_{2}^{2}}_{:=F_{1}(\boldsymbol{p})}+\underbrace{\lambda||\boldsymbol{w}_{\eta}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}})\odot|\boldsymbol{q}|||_{1}}_{:=F_{2}(\boldsymbol{q})}.

(22)

Denominating
F_{1}(\boldsymbol{p})=\frac{1}{2}||\boldsymbol{p}-\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta}||_{2}^{2}
, its convex conjugate
F_{1}^{*}(\boldsymbol{p})
can be easily computed as:

\displaystyle F_{1}^{*}(\boldsymbol{p})

\displaystyle=\sup_{\boldsymbol{p}^{\prime}}\left\{\boldsymbol{p}^{T}\boldsymbol{p}^{\prime}-\frac{1}{2}||\boldsymbol{p}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta}||_{2}^{2}\right\}

(23)

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{p}^{T}\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta}+\frac{3}{2}||\boldsymbol{p}||_{2}^{2},

(24)

since the problem defining
F_{1}^{*}
is quadratic in
\boldsymbol{p}^{\prime}
and it can be solved by imposing the optimality condition of null gradient. Consequently, the proximal map of
F_{1}^{*}
gets:

\displaystyle\mathrm{prox}_{\sigma F_{1}^{*}}(\boldsymbol{p})

\displaystyle=\arg\min_{\boldsymbol{p}^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{p}^{\prime})^{T}\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta}+\frac{3}{2}||\boldsymbol{p}^{\prime}||_{2}^{2}+\frac{1}{2\sigma}||\boldsymbol{p}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{p}||_{2}^{2}

(25)

\displaystyle=\frac{\boldsymbol{p}-\sigma\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta}}{1+3\sigma}.

(26)

Similarly, calling
F_{2}(\boldsymbol{q})=\lambda||\boldsymbol{w}_{\eta}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}})\odot|\boldsymbol{q}|||_{1}
, we derive:

\displaystyle F_{2}^{*}(\boldsymbol{q})=\begin{cases}0\quad&\text{if }\boldsymbol{q}\leq\lambda\boldsymbol{w}_{\eta}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}),\\
\infty\quad&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(27)

which implies that:

\displaystyle\mathrm{prox}_{\sigma F_{2}^{*}}(\boldsymbol{q})

\displaystyle=\arg\min_{\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime}}F_{2}^{*}(\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime})+\frac{1}{2\sigma}||\boldsymbol{q}-\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime}||_{2}^{2}

(28)

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda\boldsymbol{w}_{\eta}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}})\odot\boldsymbol{q}}{\max\left(\lambda\boldsymbol{w}_{\eta}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}),\boldsymbol{q}\right)},

(29)

where both the maximum and the division are taken element-wise. More details on the computation of the proximal operator of
F_{2}^{*}
can be found in
[
28
]
.

Plugging these results into the CP scheme (
20
) with
\boldsymbol{z}^{(k)}=(\boldsymbol{p}^{(k)},\boldsymbol{q}^{(k)})
leads to the following updating rules:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\boldsymbol{p}^{(k+1)}=\frac{\boldsymbol{p}^{(k)}+\sigma\left(\boldsymbol{K}\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}^{(k)}-\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta}\right)}{1+3\sigma},\\
\boldsymbol{q}^{(k+1)}=\frac{\lambda\boldsymbol{w}_{\eta}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}})\odot\left(\boldsymbol{q}^{(k)}+\sigma|\boldsymbol{D}\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}^{(k)}|\right)}{\max\left(\lambda\boldsymbol{w}_{\eta}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}),\boldsymbol{q}^{(k)}+\sigma|\boldsymbol{D}\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}^{(k)}|\right)},\\
\boldsymbol{x}^{(k+1)}=\mathcal{P}_{+}\left(\boldsymbol{x}^{(k)}-\tau\boldsymbol{M}^{T}\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{p}^{(k+1)}\\
\boldsymbol{q}^{(k+1)}\end{bmatrix}\right),\\
\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}^{(k+1)}=\boldsymbol{x}^{(k+1)}+\beta\left(\boldsymbol{x}^{(k+1)}-\boldsymbol{x}^{(k)}\right).\end{cases}

(30)

The corresponding CP algorithm is reported in Algorithm

.

The tests on synthetic images primarily aim to assess the potential of the model and analyze the method’s behavior in an optimal scenario. Real images, as such, inherently contain artifacts and some noise, which may not necessarily be desirable to reproduce in the reconstruction. On the other hand, they possess characteristics and areas of interest that cannot be perfectly replicated in a simulation.

In both cases we compute a test problem, by setting a very sparse fan beam geometry with 45 angles in the range

5
Conclusion

In this paper, we propose a space-variant weighted Total Variation regularization method for reconstructing tomographic images from sparse views. Sparse-view CT constitutes an under-determined linear inverse problem, the solution of which is marked by noise and prominent artifacts.
The proposed weighting strategies is obtained by linearizing a smoothed Total p-Variation, and by exploiting an image that we called
\tilde{x}
, without any prior knowledge of the noise characteristics in the data.
We have used a data driven approach to effectively approximate the ground truth image or its gradients. Finally we have applied the space-variant Total Variation to a sinogram from very sparse geometry both of a synthetic and real image. The numerical results show that our model outperforms the global Total Variation, which uniformly acts on all the pixels of the image. Moreover, the best result is obtained when the information of the weights is retrieved from the ground truth, as demonstrated in the theoretical analysis. The approximation achieved by the neural network demonstrates its effectiveness, particularly when it learns to reconstruct the gradients of the images.
The proposed spatially variant weighted approach can be easily extended to regularization methods other than Total Variation. In addition, we also intend to adaptively select the value of
p
in (
14
), in order to appropriately regularize both smooth and non-smooth regions of the image.

Appendix A
Theoretical derivation of convergence results

In the following, we prove a stability result showing that
||\boldsymbol{x}^{*}_{GT,\delta}-\boldsymbol{x}^{*}_{\Psi,\delta}||_{1}
, i.e. the error introduced by using
\Psi(\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta})
instead of
\boldsymbol{x}^{GT}
into the computation of
\boldsymbol{w}_{\eta}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}})
, converges to
0
as the distance
||\Psi(\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta})-\boldsymbol{x}^{GT}||_{1}
goes to 0.

We first need to introduce a preliminary result.

Proposition 11
.

Let
\{f_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of functions converging uniformly to
f^{*}
on any compact subset of
\mathcal{X}
. Let us assume that for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
it holds:

(i)

\min_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}}f_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})
has a unique solution
\boldsymbol{x}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

(ii)

\min_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}}f^{*}(\boldsymbol{x})
has a unique solution
\boldsymbol{x}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Then:

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}||\boldsymbol{x}_{k}-\boldsymbol{x}^{*}||_{1}=0.

Proof.

Let
\boldsymbol{x}_{k}
be the unique minimizer of
f_{k}
for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
and let
C\subseteq\mathcal{X}
be any compact set such that
\boldsymbol{x}_{k}\in C
for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Since
\{\boldsymbol{x}_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is a sequence in a compact set, there exists a convergent subsequence
\{\boldsymbol{x}_{k_{j}}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
. Let
\boldsymbol{x}^{*}
be the limit of this subsequence. By definition, for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
, it holds:

\displaystyle f_{k}(\boldsymbol{x}_{k})\leq f_{k}(\boldsymbol{x}),\quad\forall\boldsymbol{x}\in C,

and in particular:

\displaystyle f_{k_{j}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{k_{j}})\leq f_{k_{j}}(\boldsymbol{x}),\quad\forall\boldsymbol{x}\in C.

Since
f_{k}
converges uniformly to
f^{*}
and
\boldsymbol{x}_{k_{j}}
converges to
\boldsymbol{x}^{*}
, it holds that:

\displaystyle\lim_{j\to\infty}f_{k_{j}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{k_{j}})=f^{*}(\boldsymbol{x}^{*}),

\displaystyle\lim_{j\to\infty}f_{k_{j}}(\boldsymbol{x})=f^{*}(\boldsymbol{x}),\quad\forall\boldsymbol{x}\in C.

Consequently,

\displaystyle f^{*}(\boldsymbol{x}^{*})\leq f^{*}(\boldsymbol{x}),\quad\forall\boldsymbol{x}\in C,

i.e.
\boldsymbol{x}^{*}
is a minimizer of
f^{*}
. Since
f^{*}
has a unique minimizer, then all the subsequences of
\boldsymbol{x}_{k}
converge to the same element
\boldsymbol{x}^{*}
, which implies that
\boldsymbol{x}_{k}
converges to
\boldsymbol{x}^{*}
as
k\to\infty
. In particular:

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}||\boldsymbol{x}_{k}-\boldsymbol{x}^{*}||_{1}=0.

∎

Definition 1
.

Let

be an

-accurate reconstructor in

-norm. Then, for any

is the

\displaystyle C^{\epsilon}_{\Psi,p}=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}\\
||\boldsymbol{e}||_{p}\leq\epsilon\end{subarray}}\frac{||\Psi(\boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{e})-\boldsymbol{x}||_{p}-\eta_{p}}{||\boldsymbol{e}||_{p}}.

For an analysis of the accuracy and the stability constant of a reconstructor, see
[

]
where the case

can be easily extended to

In the context of this study, we emphasize that

p
-norm) incurred by the reconstructor
\Psi

\delta=0
). Conversely,

serves as a metric quantifying the amplification (or reduction), provided by
\Psi
, of input noise, which is upper bounded within a
p
-norm by
\epsilon
in the data, since Definition

can be rewritten as:

\displaystyle||\Psi(\boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{e})-\boldsymbol{x}||_{p}\leq\eta_{p}+C^{\epsilon}_{\Psi,p}||\boldsymbol{e}||_{p},\quad\forall\>||\boldsymbol{e}||_{p}\leq\epsilon,\>\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}.

(32)

Now, we need to prove a couple of results.

Proposition 12
.

For any
\delta>0
and any
\eta>0
,
\boldsymbol{w}_{\eta}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}})
defined in Equation (
13
) is a Lipschitz continuous function of
|\boldsymbol{D}\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}|
in 1-norm, i.e. there exists
L(\boldsymbol{w}_{\eta})\geq 0
, such that for any
\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}},\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
:

\displaystyle||\boldsymbol{w}_{\eta}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}})-\boldsymbol{w}_{\eta}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\prime})||_{1}\leq L(\boldsymbol{w}_{\eta})|||\boldsymbol{D}\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}|-|\boldsymbol{D}\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\prime}|||_{1}.

Proof.

If we consider:

\displaystyle f(\alpha)=\left(\frac{\eta}{\sqrt{\eta^{2}+\alpha^{2}}}\right)^{1-p},

the proof trivially follows from the observation that
f^{\prime}(\alpha)
is a continuous function of
\alpha
. Indeed:

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(\alpha)=(p-1)\frac{\alpha}{\sqrt{\eta^{2}+\alpha^{2}}}\left(\frac{\eta}{\sqrt{\eta^{2}+\alpha^{2}}}\right)^{p-2},

which is continuous everywhere if
\eta\neq 0
.
∎

From now on, we denote as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{GT}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta}):=\frac{1}{2}||\boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta}||_{2}^{2}+\lambda||\boldsymbol{w}_{\eta}(\boldsymbol{x}^{GT})\odot|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}|||_{1}.

(33)

the objective function of our regularized model, where the ground truth image
\boldsymbol{x}^{GT}
is used in place of
\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}
in the weighted strategy.
We can thus prove the following inequality.

Proposition 13
.

For any
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}
, any fixed
\delta>0
and any
\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, it holds:

\displaystyle|\mathcal{J}_{GT}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta})-\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta})|\leq\lambda L(\boldsymbol{w}_{\eta})||\boldsymbol{D}||_{2,1}\left(\eta_{1}+C^{\epsilon}_{\Psi,1}||\boldsymbol{e}||_{1}\right)||\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}||_{2,1},

(34)

where
\eta_{1}
and
C^{\epsilon}_{\Psi,1}
are the accuracy and the
\epsilon
-stability constant in 1-norm of
\Psi
, respectively,
\epsilon>0
is the maximum norm of the noise
\boldsymbol{e}
in
\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta}
, and
\mathcal{J}_{GT}
is given by Equation (
33
).

Proof.

We first note that:

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{GT}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta})-\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta})=\lambda\left(||\boldsymbol{w}_{\eta}(\boldsymbol{x}^{GT})\odot|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}|||_{1}-||\boldsymbol{w}_{\eta}(\Psi(\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta}))\odot|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}|||_{1}\right).

Thus,

\displaystyle|\mathcal{J}_{GT}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta})-\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta})|

\displaystyle=\lambda|\ ||\boldsymbol{w}_{\eta}(\boldsymbol{x}^{GT})\odot|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}|||_{1}-||\boldsymbol{w}_{\eta}(\Psi(\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta}))\odot|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}|||_{1}\ |

\displaystyle\leq\lambda||\boldsymbol{w}_{\eta}(\boldsymbol{x}^{GT})\odot|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}|-\boldsymbol{w}_{\eta}(\Psi(\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta}))\odot|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}|||_{1}

by the reverse triangular inequality.

Now, let
\boldsymbol{W}_{\eta}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}})
be the
n\times n
diagonal matrix, whose diagonal entries are the elements of
\boldsymbol{w}_{\eta}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}})
, so that
\boldsymbol{w}_{\eta}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}})\odot|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}|=\boldsymbol{W}_{\eta}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}})|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}|
.
Consequently, it holds:

\displaystyle|\mathcal{J}_{GT}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta})-\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta})|

\displaystyle\leq\lambda\left\|\boldsymbol{W}_{\eta}(\boldsymbol{x}^{GT})|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}|-\boldsymbol{W}_{\eta}(\Psi(\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta}))|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}|\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\lambda\left\|\left(\boldsymbol{W}_{\eta}(\boldsymbol{x}^{GT})-\boldsymbol{W}_{\eta}(\Psi(\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta}))\right)|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}|\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\lambda\left\|\boldsymbol{W}_{\eta}(\boldsymbol{x}^{GT})-\boldsymbol{W}_{\eta}(\Psi(\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta}))\right\|_{1}\left\||\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}|\right\|_{1}.

From Proposition
12
, it follows:

\displaystyle\left\|\boldsymbol{W}_{\eta}(\boldsymbol{x}^{GT})-\boldsymbol{W}_{\eta}(\Psi(\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta}))\right\|_{1}\leq L(\boldsymbol{w}_{\eta})\left\||\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}^{GT}|-|\boldsymbol{D}\Psi(\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta})|\right\|_{1},

where
L(\boldsymbol{w}_{\eta})
is the Lipschitz constant of
\boldsymbol{w}_{\eta}
.

To go on, note that the reverse triangular inequality gives:

\displaystyle\left\||\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}^{GT}|-|\boldsymbol{D}\Psi(\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta})|\right\|_{1}\leq\left\||\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}^{GT}-\boldsymbol{D}\Psi(\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta})|\right\|_{1}=\left\|\boldsymbol{D}\left(\boldsymbol{x}^{GT}-\Psi(\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta})\right)\right\|_{2,1},

where
||\cdot||_{2,1}
is defined in (
6
). Now, let
||\boldsymbol{D}||_{2,1}
be the matrix norm induced by the
||\cdot||_{2,1}
vector norm, i.e.:

\displaystyle||\boldsymbol{D}||_{2,1}:=\max_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{||\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}||_{2,1}}{||\boldsymbol{x}||_{1}}.

We get:

\displaystyle||\boldsymbol{D}\left(\boldsymbol{x}^{GT}-\Psi(\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta})\right)||_{2,1}\leq||\boldsymbol{D}||_{2,1}||\boldsymbol{x}^{GT}-\Psi(\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta})||_{1}.

To conclude, let
\epsilon>0
be the maximum norm of
\boldsymbol{e}
in
\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta}=\boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{x}^{GT}+\boldsymbol{e}
. By Equation (
32
), following from Definition
2
:

\displaystyle||\boldsymbol{x}^{GT}-\Psi(\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta})||_{1}=||\boldsymbol{x}^{GT}-\Psi(\boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{x}^{GT}+\boldsymbol{e})||_{1}\leq\eta_{1}+C^{\epsilon}_{\Psi,1}||\boldsymbol{e}||_{1},

where
\eta_{1}
and
C^{\epsilon}_{\Psi,1}
are the accuracy and the
\epsilon
-stability constant in 1-norm of
\Psi
, respectively.
In conclusion, we have obtained the following inequality:

\displaystyle|\mathcal{J}_{GT}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta})-\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta})|\leq\lambda L(\boldsymbol{w}_{\eta})||\boldsymbol{D}||_{2,1}\left(\eta_{1}+C^{\epsilon}_{\Psi,1}||\boldsymbol{e}||_{1}\right)\left\||\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}|\right\|_{1},

which proves the result.
∎

To proceed, we first consider the noiseless case (i.e.
\delta=0
), for which it holds the following Proposition.

Proposition 14
.

Let
\boldsymbol{x}^{*}_{GT,0}
be the unique minimum of
\mathcal{J}_{GT}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta})
, defined in (
33
), with
\delta=0
. Let
\{\Psi_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of reconstructor, each with accuracy
\eta_{1,k}^{-1}
in 1-norm. Let
\boldsymbol{x}^{*}_{k,0}

\mathcal{J}_{k}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}^{0}):=\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta})

. Then, if

\displaystyle||\boldsymbol{x}^{*}_{GT,0}-\boldsymbol{x}^{*}_{k,0}||_{1}\to 0.

If
\delta=0

\displaystyle|\mathcal{J}_{GT}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}^{0})-\mathcal{J}_{k}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}^{0})|\leq\eta_{1,k}L(\boldsymbol{w}_{\eta})||\boldsymbol{D}||_{2,1}||\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}||_{2,1}

C

Proposition 15
.

Let
\{\delta_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}

\mathcal{J}_{GT}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta_{k}})
converges uniformly to
\mathcal{J}_{GT}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}^{0})
in
\boldsymbol{x}

\boldsymbol{x}^{*}_{GT,\delta_{k}}
converges to
\boldsymbol{x}^{*}_{GT,0}
as

. To this aim, we observe that for any

\displaystyle\left|\mathcal{J}_{GT}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta_{k}})-\mathcal{J}_{GT}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}^{0})\right|

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}||\boldsymbol{x}^{*}_{GT,\delta_{k}}-\boldsymbol{x}^{*}_{GT,0}||_{1}=0.

Proposition 16
.

For any reconstructor
\Psi:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
, let
\{\delta_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be any sequence of positive noise levels such that
\delta_{k}\to 0
as

. For any

, let

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}||\boldsymbol{x}^{*}_{\Psi,\delta_{k}}-\boldsymbol{x}^{*}_{\Psi,0}||_{1}\to 0.

We proceed as in the proof of Proposition
15
, denoting by
\mathcal{R}_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})
the value of the regularizer with weights computed by

The second term can be rearranged by means of Proposition

,

\displaystyle\left|\mathcal{R}_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})-\mathcal{R}_{0}(\boldsymbol{x})\right|

\displaystyle=\left|||\boldsymbol{w}_{\eta}(\Psi(\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta_{k}}))\odot|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}|||_{1}-||\boldsymbol{w}_{\eta}(\Psi(\boldsymbol{y}^{0}))\odot|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}|||_{1}\right|
