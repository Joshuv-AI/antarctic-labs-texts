Decay rates of convergence for Fokker-Planck equations with confining drift
Alessio Porretta

Abstract
We consider Fokker-Planck equations in the whole Euclidean space, driven by Levy processes, under the action of confining drifts, as in the classical Ornstein-Ulhenbeck model. We introduce a new PDE method to get exponential or sub-exponential decay rates, as time goes to infinity, of zero average solutions, under some diffusivity condition on the Levy process, which includes the fractional Laplace operator as a model example. Our approach relies on the long time oscillation estimates of the adjoint problem and applies to (the possible superposition of) both local and nonlocal diffusions, as well as to strongly or weakly confining drifts. Our results extend, with a unifying perspective, many previous works based on different analytic or probabilistic methods, with several interesting connections. On one hand, we make a link between the (nonlinear) PDE methods used for the long time behavior of Hamilton-Jacobi equations and the decay estimates of Fokker-Planck equations; on another hand, we give a purely analytical approach towards some oscillation decay estimates which were obtained so far only with probabilistic coupling methods.



Keywords:

Fokker-Planck equations, long time decay, rate of convergence, fractional Laplacian, drift-diffusion equations, coupling methods.

Introduction

This article is concerned with Fokker-Planck equations in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and with the issue of time-decay rates of zero average solutions, which results as effect of the presence of a confining drift. To fix the ideas, the model problem that we will discuss is the following

\begin{cases}m_{t}+\left(-\Delta\right)^{\frac{\sigma}{2}}m-{\rm div}(b(t,x)m)=0&\text{ in }(0,T)\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\\
m(0,x)=m_{0}(x),\qquad\,&\text{ in }{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\end{cases}

(1.1)

where

\left(-\Delta\right)^{\frac{\sigma}{2}}m
is the usual Laplace operator for
\sigma=2
or the fractional Laplacian for
\sigma\in(0,2)
.

If
b(t,x)=b(x)
, the decay of zero average solutions is equivalent, in particular, to the problem of long time convergence of unit mass solutions towards stationary invariant measures. This is a fundamental question in the study of Fokker-Planck equations, which attracted a huge interest all over the years. As is well-known, the confining property of the drift plays a crucial role in this convergence, being related to the ergodicity property of the underlying stochastic process.
The typical result which is proved in this context, for
m_{0}
with zero average, is that

\|m(t)\|_{X}\leq\varpi(t)\|m_{0}\|_{X}\,,\qquad\forall t>0\,,\quad\hbox{with $\varpi(t)\mathop{\to}\limits^{t\to\infty}0$,}

(1.2)

where
\varpi(t)
decays exponentially if the confinement force is sufficiently strong (
b\cdot x\gtrsim|x|^{2}
at infinity), or it possibly decays sub-exponentially if
b\cdot x\gtrsim|x|^{\gamma}
with
\gamma\in(0,2)
. The space
X
is typically a
L^{p}
weighted space, with
p=1
being the most natural choice, in a way that
(
1.2
) implies decay of the total mass.

It is impossible here to mention all relevant contributions in the literature, where an estimate such as (
1.2
) has been proved. Let us just point out two main approaches that were developed so far for this kind of analysis.

Starting from the case of Laplace operator and drift
b=\nabla V(x)
, a first approach relies on functional analytic methods, exploiting spectral gap results in
L^{2}
(weighted) spaces, logarithmic Sobolev (and Poincaré) inequalities and, more generally, entropy-energy methods. We refer the reader to many influential papers such as
[
2
]
,
[
26
]
,
[
34
]
,
[
41
]
,
[
42
]
. Further refinements of spectral gap methods, relying on ideas in semigroup theory, were developed more recently, see e.g.
[
25
]
,
[
31
]
. Even if the above results are mostly related to local operators,
methods based on entropy decay or spectral gaps were also used for nonlocal diffusions, see
[
11
]
,
[
24
]
,
[
43
]
.

A second approach, which is different in spirit, stands on ideas and probabilistic methods developed in the study of recurrence and decay properties of Markov processes.
This is often referred to as the Meyn & Tweedie approach (see e.g.
[
21
]
,
[
38
]
) and this is intrinsically related to Harris’ ergodic theorem on Markov chains, as presented in the enlightening version of Hairer and Mattingly
[
27
]
. The main tools in this approach are the existence of a Lyapunov function at infinity and the
(local) strict positivity of the operator, see e.g.
[
18
]
,
[
12
]
. Extensions to nonlocal diffusions were recently developed in
[
32
]
.
We also address the reader to the influential paper
[
3
]
for a comparison between the different approaches mentioned above.
Finally, it is to mention that similar estimates can be also deduced from bilateral bounds on the fundamental solution (see e.g.
[
36
]
,
[
39
]
,
[
46
]
for divergence free drift terms) or, with a probabilistic approach, from the study of decay rates of the transition probabilities associated to the process, see e.g.
[
19
]
,
[
20
]
,
[
37
]
. We will comment more about that, in what follows.

The purpose of this article is to introduce a new method for proving decay estimates such as (
1.2
), for solutions of (
1.1
). This is an entirely PDE method, relatively simple, which applies to a variety of different regimes, ranging from local to nonlocal diffusions and including both geometrical and subgeometrical rates of convergence.

The motivation of our work comes from the study of the long time behavior of mean-field game systems
[
22
]
; those are systems of PDEs coupling Fokker-Planck and Hamilton-Jacobi equations, which describe Nash equilibria in large populations of interacting agents (see
[
33
]
,
[
28
]
,
[
13
]
). In that context, the Fokker-Planck equation is driven by the optimal strategies chosen by the agents, which results in the fact that the drift term in (
1.1
) depends on the solution of the coupled Hamilton-Jacobi equation. With this in mind, the goal is to obtain decay estimates for Fokker-Planck equations under hopefully cheap conditions on the drift term
b(t,x)
; in particular, our results apply to time-dependent fields
b
and avoid any direct assumption on
{\rm div}(b)
.

Before explaining the novelty of our approach, let us give a sample of the kind of results that we prove, when specialized to the model problem (
1.1
). We start with the case of purely local diffusion, where the results are slightly more general.

Theorem 1.1

Let us set
\langle x\rangle:=\sqrt{1+|x|^{2}}
. For
k>0
, assume that
m_{0}\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},\langle x\rangle^{k}\,dx)
be such that
{\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}m_{0}\,dx=0
. Let
m
be a weak solution to the Cauchy problem

\begin{cases}m_{t}-\Delta m-{\rm div}(b(t,x)m)=0&\text{ in }(0,T)\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\\
m(0,x)=m_{0}(x),\qquad\,&\text{ in }{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\end{cases}

(1.3)

where we assume that
b(t,x)
is a locally bounded function satisfying

\exists\,\,\alpha\,,\,R>0\,:\,\quad b(t,x)\cdot x\geq\alpha\,|x|^{\gamma}\qquad\forall x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}:\,|x|\geq R\,,\,\forall t>0\,,

(1.4)

and

\exists\,\,c_{0}>0\,:\,\quad(b(t,x)-b(t,y))\cdot(x-y)\geq-c_{0}|x-y|\qquad\forall x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\,,\,\forall t>0\,.

(1.5)

Then we have:

(a)

If
\gamma\geq 2
in (
1.4
), then

\|m(t)\|_{L^{1}(\langle x\rangle^{k})}\leq K\,e^{-\omega t}\,\|m_{0}\|_{L^{1}({\langle x\rangle^{k}})}

for some constant
K,\omega
only depending on
\alpha,c_{0},k,d
.

(b)

If
\gamma\in(0,2)
in (
1.4
), then for any
\bar{k}>k
such that
m_{0}\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},\langle x\rangle^{\bar{k}}\,dx)
we have

\|m(t)\|_{L^{1}(\langle x\rangle^{k})}\leq K\,(1+t)^{-q}\,\|m_{0}\|_{L^{1}({\langle x\rangle^{\bar{k}}})}\qquad\hbox{where $q=\frac{\bar{k}-k}{2-\gamma}$.}

for some
K
depending on
\alpha,\gamma,c_{0},k,\bar{k},d
.

Theorem
1.1
recovers, with a unifying approach, the results which are known in the literature, including the case of slow, or degenerate, confining drift, which was studied previously both with the entropy method by Toscani and Villani
[
42
]
and with more sophisticated tools of semigroup theory by Kavian, Mischler and Dao
[
31
]
. We stress however that we avoid any
C^{1}
regularity of the drift
b
and any requirement on
{\rm div}(b)
or
Db
; the one-sided condition (
1.5
) plays somehow a similar role, although it appears to be a milder requirement which, for instance, allows for any (space-time) bounded perturbation of dissipative vector fields.
We also mention that, at the expense of a mild restriction on the parameter
k
in the weight function, the previous result holds even replacing (
1.5
) with more general conditions, e.g. assuming
(b(t,x)-b(t,y))\cdot(x-y)\geq-c_{0}(|x-y|\vee|x-y|^{2})
, see Remark
3.7
.

Whilst Theorem
1.1
only gives a sample of the results that we prove, the main outcome of our approach appears in the extension of this kind of estimates to general Levy processes, including possibly nonlocal diffusions. This is the model result that we prove for the specific example of the fractional Laplacian, to be compared with
[
32
, Thm 1.4]
.

Theorem 1.2

Let
\sigma\in(0,2)
and
k\in(0,\sigma)
. Assume that
m_{0}\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},\langle x\rangle^{k}\,dx)
be such that
{\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}m_{0}\,dx=0
. Let
m
be a weak solution to the Cauchy problem (
1.1
), where we assume that
b
is a locally Lipschitz vector field satisfying (
1.4
) and
either of the following conditions:

(i)
\sigma\in(1,2)
and (
1.5
) holds true.

(ii)
\sigma\in(0,1]
and
(b(t,x)-b(t,y))\cdot(x-y)\geq-c_{0}|x-y|(|x-y|\wedge 1)^{1-\sigma+\delta}
, for some
\delta>0
,
c_{0}>0
.

Then we have:

(a)

If
\gamma\geq 2
in (
1.4
), then

\|m(t)\|_{L^{1}(\langle x\rangle^{k})}\leq K\,e^{-\omega t}\,\|m_{0}\|_{L^{1}({\langle x\rangle^{k}})}

for some constant
K,\omega
only depending on
\alpha,\sigma,\delta,c_{0},k,d
.

(b)

If
\gamma\in(((2-\sigma)\vee 1),2)
in (
1.4
), then for any
k<\bar{k}<\sigma
such that
m_{0}\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},\langle x\rangle^{\bar{k}}\,dx)
we have

\|m(t)\|_{L^{1}(\langle x\rangle^{k})}\leq K\,(1+t)^{-q}\,\|m_{0}\|_{L^{1}({\langle x\rangle^{\bar{k}}})}\qquad\hbox{where $q=\frac{\bar{k}-k}{2-\gamma}$.}

for some
K
depending on
\alpha,\gamma,\sigma,\delta,c_{0},k,\bar{k},d
.

Let us stress that the local Lipschitz character of the drift, which is assumed in the above statement, was only used to make sure that
m(t)\in L^{1}
.
More general statements, which extend Theorem
1.2
, will be given in Section
4
for Levy operators: we refer the reader to Theorem
4.2
, in Section 4. In that case, the decay is formulated in the total variation norm for measure-valued solutions. Correspondingly, we will adopt a suitable formulation of solution which can be applied to a far more general setting; as an example, one can consider the case of fractional Laplacian with additional second order degenerate diffusions.

Compared with the existing literature, our strategy is certainly close to (and even inspired from) the probabilistic approach mentioned above, although we develop a purely PDE method which has an interest in its own. There are two main cornerstones in the approach that we suggest:

(i) the decay estimates for the Fokker-Planck equation (
1.1
) are entirely deduced by duality from oscillation decay estimates obtained on the solutions of the adjoint problem

\begin{cases}u_{t}+\left(-\Delta\right)^{\frac{\sigma}{2}}u+b(t,x)\cdot Du=0&\text{ in }(0,T)\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\\
u(0,x)=u_{0}(x),\qquad\,&\text{ in }{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\,.\end{cases}

(1.6)

(ii) for solutions of (
1.6
), we prove decay estimates which are dual to estimates of the type (
1.2
). Namely, we show that

\begin{split}&\qquad[u(t)]_{\langle x\rangle^{k}}\leq\varpi(t)\,[u_{0}]_{\langle x\rangle^{k}}\qquad\hbox{with $\varpi(t)\mathop{\to}\limits^{t\to\infty}0$,}\\
\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt\cr&\hbox{where $[u]_{\langle x\rangle^{k}}=\sup\limits_{x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\,\,\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|}{\langle x\rangle^{k}+\langle y\rangle^{k}}$.}\end{split}

(1.7)

Estimates such as (
1.7
) are an evidence of the ergodicity of the underlying process. In the PDE perspective, global (in time) bounds for the oscillation of solutions are usually exploited in the study of long time behavior of Hamilton-Jacobi equations and the convergence to the ergodic problem (see e.g.
[
10
]
,
[
6
]
,
[
9
]
,
[
14
]
). Those results typically stand on either classical gradient estimates (through the celebrated Bernstein method) or doubling variables techniques developed in the theory of viscosity solutions, specifically by Ishii and Lions
[
29
]
. This kind of methods was later extended to nonlocal operators in
[
5
]
,
[
30
]
. However, quantitative estimates like (
1.7
) are new in this framework, compared to those earlier works. Otherwise, estimates similar to (
1.7
) can be found in the probabilistic literature in the form of contraction estimates for transition probabilities in Wasserstein’s metrics, see e.g.
[
19
]
,
[
20
]
,
[
37
]
,
[
44
]
. In that context, the proofs rely on probabilistic tools based on coupling methods (
[
15
]
,
[
35
]
).

In our approach, the key idea is to obtain (
1.7
) by proving that

u(t,x)-u(t,y)\leq\varpi(t)\,[u_{0}]_{\langle x\rangle^{k}}\big[\langle x\rangle^{k}+\langle y\rangle^{k}+\psi(|x-y|)\big]

(1.8)

for some bounded function
\psi(r)
which is locally Hölder near
r=0
.
Notice that this estimate combines the effects of the Lyapunov function at infinity (used to control long-range interactions) with the local ellipticity used to control short-range interactions, when
|x-y|
is small. This is the role of the function
\psi
, namely to handle the small oscillations; at the same time, since
\psi
is bounded, (
1.8
) readily implies (
1.7
) up to multiplying the decay rate
\varpi(t)
by a bounded factor. Finally, by a simple duality argument, an estimate like (
1.7
) yields the decay
\|m(t)\|_{L^{1}(\langle x\rangle^{k})}\leq\varpi(t)\,\|m_{0}\|_{L^{1}({\langle x\rangle^{\bar{k}}})}
for the solution of (
1.1
).

It seems remarkable that
the decay of Fokker-Planck equations will be deduced from the single estimate (
1.8
)
proved on the dual problem (
1.6
). This is the main difference of our method compared to the Meyn-Tweedy, or Harris-type approach, where the local ellipticity is exploited directly in Harnack-type properties of the Fokker-Planck equation (or strong positivity, say irreducibility conditions, on the associated semigroup).
By contrast, (
1.8
) is a pointwise estimate that we derive from maximum principle, to be applied to a suitable operator in the product space for the variables
(x,y)
. This kind of application of the maximum principle contains ideas that connect the doubling variables methods of viscosity solutions to probabilistic coupling methods. We will explain more about that in Section
3.1
, where the key steps are given in Lemmas
3.3
and
3.4
.

Let us mention that the idea of an analytic version of the coupling method was already suggested in
[
40
]
for local diffusions (some related ideas appeared later in
[
23
]
, in a rather informal way). On that occasion, we explained that the doubling variables approach developed in the theory of viscosity solutions was parallel, and conceptually equivalent, to the coupling method used in probability (see
[
40
, Appendix]
). In the present article, we come back on this viewpoint and introduce similar ideas to handle nonlocal diffusions as well, getting a crucial improvement in the doubling variable method which is used for Levy operators.
This allows us to give entirely PDE proofs of some contraction estimates that were only proved with probabilistic methods so far, with the advantage of relaxing the assumptions and making them ready to several possible extensions.

In the present paper, in order not to increase the level of technicalities which could be induced by generalizations, we confine ourselves to the study of Levy processes, which contain both local diffusion and jump processes, giving evidence of the generality of the method.

We also show how the suggested strategy can be adapted to different
Lyapunov functions in order to obtain exponential or sub-exponential rates of convergence.
We refer the reader to Theorem
5.5
for a general version of our results
, which is not limited to power-type Lyapunov functions. In particular, we show how different decay rates are proved for the weighted norm
\|m(t)\|_{{\mathcal{M}}_{\varphi}}
(the total variation of
m(t)\,\varphi
) according to whether the Lyapunov function
\varphi
satisfies stronger, or weaker, conditions of super-solution. This way, Theorem
5.5
will provide with a general recipe which may please the readers inclined towards statements in a more abstract form. As an example of application of similar generalizations, we will obtain exponential convergence of solutions to (
1.3
) even for slowly confining drifts, up to using stronger Lyapunov weights rather than powers: we refer to Corollary
5.6
, which extends recent results obtained in
[
31
]
.

To conclude, let us briefly mention the organization of the paper. In Section 2, we introduce the notation and the assumptions that will be used, as well as the notions of solutions for the evolution problems to be considered. Section 3 is devoted to the oscillation decay estimates for problems like (
1.6
); this is where we use doubling variables methods (with coupling ideas embedded) to derive estimates as (
1.7
), with power-type weight functions. In Section 4, we deduce by duality the decay of Fokker-Planck equations, which generalize Theorem
1.1
and
1.2
. Section 5 is devoted to a more general version of the results, where we consider Lyapunov functions of general type, and we give exponential or sub-exponential decay rates accordingly. We also mention the application of our results to the convergence of unit mass solutions towards stationary measures. Finally, in the Appendix, Section 6, we give the proof of the existence and uniqueness of solutions for the Fokker-Planck equation, under the (non standard) setting of assumptions which is used in the paper.

Notation, assumptions and preliminary tools

We denote by
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,
d\geq 1
, the
d
-dimensional Euclidean space, with Lebesgue measure
dx
.
I_{d}
denotes the identity matrix in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. Given two real numbers
a,b
, we use the standard notation
a\vee b=\max(a,b)
and
a\wedge b=\min(a,b)
. For a positive continuous function
\varphi(x)
, we denote by
L^{1}(\varphi(x))
the usual Lebesgue space
L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},\mu)
defined in terms of the measure
\mu=\varphi(x)dx
, and by
L^{\infty}(\varphi(x))
the space of measurable functions
u:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
such that
u\,\varphi
is essentially bounded (i.e.
u\varphi\in L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
).

We will use systematically the weight function

\langle x\rangle:=\sqrt{1+|x|^{2}}\,

replacing the role of
|x|
. Notice that
D\langle x\rangle=\frac{x}{\langle x\rangle}
.

The space
{\mathcal{M}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
denotes the space of finite (signed) Borel measures
m
endowed with the total variation norm
\|m\|_{TV}=|m|({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
; here
|m|=m^{+}+m^{-}
, where
m^{\pm}
are positive measures giving the Hahn decomposition of
m
. The set
{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
denotes the space of probability measures, where we consider the Kantorovich-Rubinstein distance

d_{1}(m,\tilde{m}):=\sup\{{\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}\varphi\,d(m-\tilde{m})\,,\,\,\varphi\,\hbox{Lipschitz,}\,\,\|\varphi\|_{\infty},\|D\varphi\|_{\infty}\leq 1\}\,.

Finally, we denote by
{\mathcal{M}}_{k}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
the subset of measures
m
with finite
k
moments, i.e.
\langle x\rangle^{k}\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},d|m|)
. For
m\in{\mathcal{M}}_{k}
, we use the notation

\|m\|_{{\mathcal{M}}_{k}}:={\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}\langle x\rangle^{k}\,d|m|

to denote the total variation of
\langle x\rangle^{k}\,m
. We use a similar notation with possibly different weights, setting
{\mathcal{M}}_{\varphi}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
the subset of measures such that
\varphi\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},d|m|)
for some positive function
\varphi
, with
\|m\|_{{\mathcal{M}}_{\varphi}}={\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}\varphi\,d|m|
.

For given
T>0
, we set
Q_{T}:=(0,T)\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. We denote by
C^{1,2}(Q_{T})
the functions defined on
Q_{T}
which are
C^{1}
in
t
and
C^{2}
in
x
. The notation
C_{b}(U)
refers to continuous bounded functions on a set
U
, and
C_{c}(U)
to continuous compactly supported functions. We denote by
C([0,T];{\mathcal{M}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{*})
the functions
m
from
[0,T]
into
{\mathcal{M}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
which are continuous with respect to the weak
∗
topology of measures. When working with probability measures, we denote
C([0,T];{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}))
the functions
m
which are continuous from
[0,T]
into
{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, endowed with the
d_{1}
distance.

Associated to a continuous function
\varphi
such that
\varphi(x)\geq 1
for all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,
we introduce suitable weighted seminorms, defined as

[u]_{\varphi}=\sup_{x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\,\,\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|}{\varphi(x)+\varphi(y)}\,.

In particular, we will set

[u]_{\langle x\rangle^{k}}:=\sup_{x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\,\,\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|}{\langle x\rangle^{k}+\langle y\rangle^{k}}\,.

With a slight abuse of notation, when considering the case
k=0
, we denote

[u]_{0}:=\sup_{x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\,\,\left(|u(x)-u(y)|\right)\,

the seminorm measuring the oscillation of a bounded function
u
.

Given a continuous matrix
\Sigma\,:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times\ell}
,
\Sigma^{*}
denotes its transposed matrix; then we define the second order operator

\begin{split}{\mathcal{L}}_{0}(u):&=-\lambda_{0}\Delta u-{\rm tr}(\Sigma(x)\Sigma^{*}(x)D^{2}u)\\
&=-\lambda_{0}\sum_{i=1}^{d}u_{x_{i}x_{i}}-\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}\Sigma_{ik}(x)\Sigma_{jk}(x)u_{x_{i}x_{j}}\ \end{split}

(2.1)

where
\lambda_{0}\geq 0
. Without going in search of the most generality, we will assume
\Sigma
to be bounded and Lipschitz continuous:

\exists\,\,\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1}>0\,:\quad\|\Sigma(x)\|\leq\sigma_{0}\,,\qquad\|\Sigma(x)-\Sigma(y)\|\leq\sigma_{1}|x-y|\quad\forall x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\,.

(2.2)

Now we define a pure jump Levy operator as

\mathcal{I}(x,[u]):=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\{u(x+z)-u(x)-(Du(x)\cdot z)\mathds{1}_{|z|\leq 1}\}\nu(dz)

(2.3)

in which the measure
\nu
is absolutely continuous and satisfies

\exists\lambda,\Lambda\geq 0\,,\sigma\in(0,2)\,:\,\quad\,\frac{\lambda}{|z|^{d+\sigma}}\leq\frac{d\nu}{dz}\leq\,\frac{\Lambda}{|z|^{d+\sigma}}\qquad\forall z\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\,.

(2.4)

Typical examples of operators which satisfy (
2.3
)-(
2.4
) are given by the fractional Laplacian
(-\Delta)^{\frac{\sigma}{2}}
(
\sigma\in(0,2)
) or more generally by the generators of
\alpha
-stable jump processes.
We define the operator
{\mathcal{L}}
as

{\mathcal{L}}[u]:={\mathcal{L}}_{0}(u)-\mathcal{I}(x,[u])\,.

(2.5)

It is well known (see e.g.
[
1
]
) that
{\mathcal{L}}[u]+b\cdot Du
is the infinitesimal generator of a general Levy process where the drift-diffusion part is generated by
{\mathcal{L}}_{0}+b\cdot D
and the jump part is represented by
{\mathcal{I}}
. This is associated with the stochastic SDE

\begin{cases}dX_{t}=-b(X_{t})dt+\sqrt{2\lambda_{0}}dB_{t}+\sqrt{2}\Sigma(X_{t})dB_{t}+dP_{t}&\\
X_{0}=x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}&\end{cases}

(2.6)

where
B_{t}
is a standard Brownian motion and
P_{t}
is a compound Poisson process with measure
\nu
.

We point out that more general conditions upon the diffusion matrix
\Sigma(x)
and the Levy measure
\nu(z)
would be possible, but we keep things simpler here for the reader’s convenience.

Under the assumptions (
2.2
), (
2.4
), and for a continuous vector field
b(t,x)
, we consider the evolution problem

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u+{\mathcal{L}}[u]+b(t,x)\cdot Du=0&\text{ in }(0,T)\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\\
u(0,x)=u_{0}(x),\qquad\,&\text{ in }{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\end{cases}

(2.7)

It is well known that, if
b
is sufficiently regular, then the unique solution of (
2.7
) is characterized as

u(t,x)={\mathbb{E}}(X_{t})
, where
X_{t}
is the solution of (
2.6
).

The associated Fokker-Planck equation is defined through the adjoint problem of (
2.7
). To this purpose, we set

\mathcal{I}^{*}(x,[m]):=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\{m(x+z)-m(x)-(Dm(x)\cdot z)\mathds{1}_{|z|\leq 1}\}\nu^{*}(dz)

(2.8)

where we define
\nu^{*}(B):=\nu(-B)
, for every Borel set
B\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. Then the operator
{\mathcal{L}}^{*}
is defined as

\begin{split}{\mathcal{L}}^{*}[m]:&={\mathcal{L}}_{0}^{*}(m)-\mathcal{I}^{*}(x,[m])\\
&=-\lambda_{0}\Delta m-\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}\left(\Sigma_{ik}\Sigma_{jk}m\right)_{x_{i}x_{j}}-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\{m(x+z)-m(x)-(Dm(x)\cdot z)\mathds{1}_{|z|\leq 1}\}\nu^{*}(dz)\,.\end{split}

(2.9)

This allows us to introduce the Fokker-Planck equation

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}m+{\mathcal{L}}^{*}[m]-{\rm div}(b(t,x)\,m)=0&\text{ in }(0,T)\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\\
m(0)=m_{0},\qquad\,&\text{ in }{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\end{cases}

(2.10)

where
m_{0}\in{\mathcal{M}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
.

Suitable formulations of problems (
2.7
) and (
2.10
) are given below.
In order to ease notations, in the following we set, for
\varphi\in C^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
,

{\mathcal{L}}^{b}[\varphi]:={\mathcal{L}}[\varphi]+b(t,x)\cdot D\varphi\qquad\,;\qquad\qquad{\mathcal{L}}_{0}^{b}[\varphi]:={\mathcal{L}}_{0}[\varphi]+b(t,x)\cdot D\varphi\,.

(2.11)

The key-assumption, throughout the whole paper, is the following confining condition on the drift:

\exists\,\,\alpha\,,\,R>0\,:\,\quad b(t,x)\cdot x\geq\alpha\,|x|^{\gamma}\qquad\forall x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}:\,|x|\geq R\,,\,\forall t>0\,,

(2.12)

where we assume that
\gamma>0
.
Three different regimes will be considered:
\gamma\geq 2
, which is the coercive
case,
\gamma\in[1,2)
, which is often referred to in the literature as a slowly confining case, and
\gamma\in(0,1)
, which may be called a degenerate confining case.
Our goal is to give a unifying approach which provides with optimal decay estimates in all such ranges of
\gamma
.

We also assume a one-sided control for the accretivity of
b
. Two slightly different conditions will be assumed depending on the ellipticity of the nonlocal operator, say whether
\sigma
in (
2.4
) is bigger or smaller than
1
. Namely, we suppose
that
b
satisfies either

\exists\,\,c_{0}>0\,:\,\quad(b(t,x)-b(t,y))\cdot(x-y)\geq-c_{0}|x-y|\qquad\forall x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\,,\,\forall t>0\,,

(2.13)

or the strongest condition (when
\sigma\leq 1
):

\sigma\in(0,1]\,\,\hbox{and $\exists\,\delta\in(0,1)$: \quad$(b(t,x)-b(t,y))\cdot(x-y)\geq-c_{0}|x-y|(|x-y|\wedge 1)^{1-\sigma+\delta}$,}

(2.14)

for some
c_{0}>0
. Let us mention that further generalizations of (
2.13
), (
2.14
) are also possible; we refer to Remark
3.7
.

2.1
Viscosity solutions

It is convenient to use the framework of viscosity solutions for problem (
2.7
). In our setting, where the operator is defined through (
2.5
), we could use slightly different formulations of the notion of viscosity solution, which all turn out to be equivalent; we refer the reader to
[
8
]
for a discussion of this issue, and we pick just one among those possible versions. Moreover, since our focus is on regularity estimates, we will simplify some requirement by only considering continuous solutions. For the interested reader, upper semi-continuous (USC) subsolutions, and lower semi-continuous (LSC) supersolutions, are defined accordingly in
[
8
]
.

Definition 2.1

A continuous function
u\in C^{0}([0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d})
is a viscosity solution of (
2.7
) if
u\in L^{\infty}((0,T);L^{\infty}(\langle x\rangle^{-m}))
for some
m\in(0,\sigma)
, if
u(0,x)=u_{0}(x)
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and if
u
satisfies the following two requirements:

(i)

for every
\varphi\in C^{1,2}(Q_{T})
such that
\varphi\in L^{\infty}((0,T);L^{\infty}(\langle x\rangle^{-k}))
for some
k\in(0,\sigma)
, if
(t_{0},x_{0})\in Q_{T}
is a global maximum point of
u-\varphi
, then

\partial_{t}\varphi(t_{0},x_{0})+{\mathcal{L}}[\varphi](t_{0},x_{0})+b(t_{0},x_{0})\cdot D\varphi(t_{0},x_{0})\leq 0\,.

(ii)

for every
\varphi\in C^{1,2}(Q_{T})
such that
\varphi\in L^{\infty}((0,T);L^{\infty}(\langle x\rangle^{-k}))
for some
k\in(0,\sigma)
, if
(t_{0},x_{0})\in Q_{T}
is a global minimum point of
u-\varphi
, then

\partial_{t}\varphi(t_{0},x_{0})+{\mathcal{L}}[\varphi](t_{0},x_{0})+b(t_{0},x_{0})\cdot D\varphi(t_{0},x_{0})\geq 0\,.

Remark 2.2

We stress that the requirement made in Definition
2.1
concerning the growth at infinity, for both the solution and the test function, is only due to the nonlocal term
{\mathcal{I}}
. Due to assumption (
2.4
), some limitation in the growth at infinity is required in order that
{\mathcal{I}}(x,[u])
,
{\mathcal{I}}(x,[\varphi])
be well-defined. Alternative definitions, formulated with a more local character, are suggested in
[
8
]
, which turn out to be equivalent.

Another alternative definition of solution can be given using sub-jets and super-jets of the function
u
, as introduced in
[
17
]
, denoted by
{J}^{2,-}_{Q_{T}}u
,
{J}^{2,+}_{Q_{T}}u
respectively. The notation
{\overline{J}}^{2,\pm}_{Q_{T}}u
denotes the closure of elements in the sub/super-jets.

The following lemma is the extension to nonlocal operators of the classical Ishii-Jensen’s lemma in viscosity solutions’s theory
[
17
, Thm 8.3]
. The following nonlocal version follows from
[
8
, Corollary 2]
; we use here a slightly more readable version, which is obtained asymptotically, see e.g.
[
8
, Proposition 2]
.

Theorem 2.3

Let
u
be an USC sub-solution of (
2.7
), let
v
be a LSC super-solution of (
2.7
), and let

(\hat{t},\hat{x},\hat{y})\in(0,T)\times({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{2}
be a local maximum point of the map
(t,x,y)\mapsto[u(t,x)-v(t,y)-\Phi(t,x,y)]
, for some function
\Phi\in C^{1,2}((0,T)\times({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{2})
.

Then, for every
n
sufficiently large, there exist real numbers
a,b\in{\mathbb{R}}
and matrices
X,Y\in{\mathcal{S}}_{d}
such that

\displaystyle a-b=\partial_{t}\Phi(\hat{t},\hat{x},\hat{y})

\displaystyle\,,\,\,(a,D_{x}\Phi(\hat{t},\hat{x},\hat{y}),X)\in{\overline{J}}^{2,+}_{Q_{T}}u(\hat{t},\hat{x}),\,\,(b,-D_{y}\Phi(\hat{t},\hat{x},\hat{y}),Y)\in{\overline{J}}^{2,-}_{Q_{T}}v(\hat{t},\hat{y})

\displaystyle-(n+c_{d}\|A\|)I\leq\begin{pmatrix}X&0\\
0&-Y\end{pmatrix}\leq A+\frac{1}{n}A^{2}

where
A=D^{2}_{x,y}\Phi(\hat{t},\hat{x},\hat{y})
. Moreover, we have

\begin{split}&a-{\rm tr}(Q(\hat{x})X)+b(\hat{t},\hat{x})\cdot D_{x}\Phi(\hat{t},\hat{x},\hat{y})-{\mathcal{I}}(\hat{x},u(\hat{t}),D_{x}\Phi(\hat{t},\hat{x},\hat{y}))\leq 0\\
&b-{\rm tr}(Q(\hat{y})Y)-b(\hat{t},\hat{y})\cdot D_{y}\Phi(\hat{t},\hat{x},\hat{y})-{\mathcal{I}}(\hat{y},v(\hat{t}),-D_{y}\Phi(\hat{t},\hat{x},\hat{y}))\geq 0\end{split}

(2.15)

where we have denoted, for
x,p\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
:

\begin{split}&Q(x)=\lambda_{0}I_{d}+\Sigma\Sigma^{*}(x)\\
&{\mathcal{I}}(x,u,p)={\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}[u(x+z)-u(x)-(p\cdot z)\mathds{1}_{{|z|\leq 1}}]d\nu(z)\,.\end{split}

(2.16)

Let us stress that the previous theorem also implies the consistency of the viscosity formulation; if
u\in C^{1,2}(Q_{T})
is a viscosity solution, then it is also a classical solution. Otherwise, if
u_{0}
is a continuous function with polynomial growth, problem (
2.7
) admits a unique viscosity solution; we refer to Proposition
6.1
for a statement of this kind.

Remark 2.4

The reader which is not familiar with viscosity solutions should not be puzzled by the statement of Theorem
2.3
. In fact, if
u,v
were smooth, the above statement would be straightforward: if
u(t,x)-v(t,y)-\Phi(t,x,y)
has a local maximum at
(\hat{t},\hat{x},\hat{y})
, then by elementary calculus one has

\partial_{t}u-\partial_{t}v=\partial_{t}\Phi\,,\quad\begin{cases}D_{x}u=D_{x}\Phi&\\
D_{y}v=-D_{y}\Phi&\end{cases}\,,\quad\begin{pmatrix}D^{2}_{x}u&0\\
0&-D^{2}_{y}v\end{pmatrix}\leq\ D^{2}_{(x,y)}\Phi

where everything is computed on
(\hat{t},\hat{x},\hat{y})
. In this case the statement obviously holds (and can be read for
n\to\infty
) with
a=\partial_{t}u
,
b=\partial_{t}v
,
X=D^{2}_{x}u
and
Y=D^{2}_{y}v
, where the two inequalities (
2.15
) are just the conditions
[\partial_{t}u+{\mathcal{L}}[u]+b\cdot Du]_{|(\hat{t},\hat{x})}\leq 0
(respectively,
[\partial_{t}v+{\mathcal{L}}[v]+b\cdot Dv]_{|(\hat{t},\hat{y})}\geq 0
) having replaced
D_{x}u(\hat{t},\hat{x}),D_{y}v(\hat{t},\hat{y})
by
D_{x}\Phi
,
-D_{y}\Phi
respectively.

The relevance of Theorem
2.3
is to guarantee that a similar conclusion holds for functions
u,v
that are not differentiable (even possibly just semi-continuous); in that case,
X,Y
should be considered as approximations (as
n\to\infty
) of the second derivatives
D^{2}_{x}u,D^{2}_{y}v
, and similarly
a,b
as replacements of
\partial_{t}u\,,\partial_{t}v
. Let us mention here that
X,Y
in principle depend on
n
, but a standard compactness argument (see e.g.
[
40
, Rmk 2.3]
) allows eventually to let
n\to\infty
and to obtain new matrices
X,Y
that satisfy the upper bound
\begin{pmatrix}X&0\\
0&-Y\end{pmatrix}\leq A
in the limit. Thus, there is no loss in comprehension for a reader that would consider
n\to\infty
in a rough reading of the above statement.

2.2
Duality solutions of Fokker-Planck equations

Solutions of the Fokker-Planck equation (
2.10
) will be defined here in duality with the notion of viscosity solution for
{\mathcal{L}}^{b}
.

Definition 2.5

Let
m_{0}\in{\mathcal{M}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
. A function
m\in C^{0}([0,T);{\mathcal{M}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{*})
is a solution to (
2.10
) if

\displaystyle{\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}\xi\,dm(t)+\int_{0}^{t}{\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}f\,dm(\tau)\,d\tau={\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}\varphi(0,x)\,dm_{0}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\forall\,t\in(0,T)\,,\,\xi\in C_{b}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}),\,f\in C_{b}(Q_{t})\,,\varphi\in C_{b}([0,t]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d})\quad\mbox{such that}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\hbox{$\varphi$ is a viscosity solution of }\quad\mbox{\bf(B)}\,\,\begin{cases}-\partial_{t}\varphi+{\mathcal{L}}^{b}[\varphi]=f&\text{ in }(0,t)\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\\
\varphi(t,x)=\xi(x),\qquad\,&\text{ in }{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\end{cases}

Let us point out that, since classical solutions are viscosity solutions, then a solution
m
according to Definition
2.5
is also a weak solution
in the usual distributional sense, which means

\int_{0}^{T}{\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}[-\partial_{t}\varphi+{\mathcal{L}}^{b}[\varphi]]\,dm(t)\,dt={\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}\varphi(0,x)\,dm_{0}\qquad\forall\varphi\in C^{1,2}_{c}([0,T)\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d})\,.

The converse statement, namely that weak solutions enjoy the duality property of Definition
2.5
, is more delicate under the general setting in which
we consider the drift term
b
and the nonlocal diffusion
{\mathcal{I}}
. However, this is certainly true for second order operators (i.e. when
\lambda_{0}>0
in (
2.1
)) or for locally Lipschitz vector fields
b
.
In fact, weak solutions satisfy Definition
2.5
whenever they are unique. This is a consequence of the following existence and uniqueness result, which fully
justifies the previous notion of solution in the present setting.
We recall that
{\mathcal{M}}_{k}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
denotes the subset of measures in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
having finite
k
-th moment.

Theorem 2.6

Let
m_{0}\in{\mathcal{M}}_{k}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, for some
0<k<\sigma
. Let
{\mathcal{L}}
be defined by (
2.5
) where
\Sigma
satisfies (
2.2
), the Levy measure in
{\mathcal{I}}
satisfies (
2.4
), and
\lambda_{0}+\lambda>0
. Assume that
b\in C^{0}(Q_{T})
is such that
b(t,x)\cdot x
is bounded below
and either of the following conditions hold:

(i)
\lambda_{0}>0
or
\sigma\in(1,2)
, and (
2.13
) holds true whenever
|x-y|\leq 1
.

(ii)
\lambda_{0}=0
,
\sigma\in(0,1]
and (
2.14
) holds true whenever
|x-y|\leq 1
.

Then there exists a unique solution
m
of (
2.10
) in the sense of Definition
2.5
, and
m(t)\in{\mathcal{M}}_{k}
for all
t>0
. Moreover,
m
is obtained as the limit of solutions
m_{\varepsilon}
corresponding to the operator
({\mathcal{L}}^{b})^{*}-\varepsilon\Delta
.

Finally, the application
m_{0}\mapsto m(t)
preserves positivity and mass.

The proof of Theorem
2.6
will be given in the Appendix, for the sake of completeness. It stems from the simple idea that, in duality by what happens for problem (B), the notion of solution given in Definition
2.5
is stable by vanishing viscosity limits. Thus, even if at first sight the formulation of Definition
2.5
sounds a bit unusual, the reader can see that this is actually a quite natural setting in which problem (

) can be formulated (and proved to be well-posed) outside a standard set of assumptions.
In fact, the uniqueness result, and the
characterization of the solution as the limit of Fokker-Planck equations with vanishing viscosity, show the consistency of this formulation with all known results in terms of weak or classical solutions.
In particular, one can readily deduce that
m\in C([0,T];L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}))
when
\lambda_{0}>0
(second order problems), or for the case of fractional Laplacian (or more generally, symmetric kernels) under some extra condition on
{\rm div}(b)
(see e.g.
[
32

).

Finally, we point out that the Definition

has been given for signed measures, because we will deal with zero average measures. Due to the Hahn decomposition, and the linearity of the formulation, every solution

can be split as

, where
m_{1},m_{2}
are the solutions corresponding to
m_{0}^{+}
,
m_{0}^{-}
respectively.

3
Oscillation decay estimates

In this Section we give the oscillation estimates for problem (
2.7
), where we assume that
u_{0}
is a continuous function in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, and

{\mathcal{L}},b
satisfy the assumptions (
2.2
), (
2.4
), (
2.12
) and (
2.13
) (or (
2.14
)).

A crucial role here is played by the explicit Lyapunov functions
\langle x\rangle^{\beta}:=(\sqrt{1+|x|^{2}})^{\beta}
.
The properties of those super-solutions are listed below.

Lemma 3.1

Assume (
2.12
). Let us denote
\varphi(x)=\langle x\rangle^{\beta}
, with
\beta>0
. Then we have:

(i)

for every
\varepsilon>0
there exists
K_{\varepsilon}>0
such that

\mathcal{L}_{0}[\varphi]+b(t,x)\cdot D\varphi\geq(\alpha-\varepsilon)\beta\,\frac{\varphi(x)}{\langle x\rangle^{2-\gamma}}-K_{\varepsilon}\qquad\forall x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\,,t>0\,.

(3.1)

(ii)

Let the Levy measure
\nu
satisfy (
2.4
). If
0<\beta<\sigma
, then for every
\varepsilon>0
there exists
K_{\varepsilon}>0
such that

\mathcal{L}[\varphi]+b(t,x)\cdot D\varphi\geq(\alpha-\varepsilon)\beta\,\frac{\varphi(x)}{\langle x\rangle^{2-\gamma}}-K_{\varepsilon}\qquad\forall x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\,,t>0,

(3.2)

provided
\beta\leq 1
or
\gamma>1
in (
2.12
).

Proof
.  We compute
D\varphi=\beta\langle x\rangle^{\beta-1}\frac{x}{\langle x\rangle}
,
D^{2}\varphi=\beta\langle x\rangle^{\beta-2}\left(I_{d}+(\beta-1)\frac{x}{\langle x\rangle}\otimes\frac{x}{\langle x\rangle}\right)
and so, if
Q(x)=\lambda_{0}I_{d}+\Sigma\Sigma^{*}(x)
, we have from (
2.2
)

\left|{\rm tr}(Q(x)D^{2}\varphi)\right|\leq\beta\,c_{\beta}\,(\sigma_{0}^{2}+\lambda_{0})\langle x\rangle^{\beta-2}\,

for some
c_{\beta}
only depending on
\beta
and
d
. Hence

{\mathcal{L}}_{0}^{b}[\varphi]\geq\beta\langle x\rangle^{\beta-2}\left(b(t,x)\cdot x-c_{\beta}(\sigma_{0}^{2}+\lambda_{0})\right)\,.

Using (
2.12
), we get (
3.1
).

As for the Levy operator
{\mathcal{I}}
, the difference is that we need to handle the nonlocal term for large values of
|z|
. Indeed, we have

\displaystyle{\mathcal{I}}(x,[\varphi])

\displaystyle=\int_{|z|>1}\{\varphi(x+z)-\varphi(x)\}\nu(dz)+\int_{|z|\leq 1}\{\varphi(x+z)-\varphi(x)-(D\varphi(x)\cdot z)\}\nu(dz)

\displaystyle\leq c_{\beta}\,\int_{|z|>1}\max(\langle x\rangle^{\beta-1}\,|z|\,,\,|z|^{\beta})\nu(dz)+c_{\beta}\langle x\rangle^{\beta-2}\int_{|z|\leq 1}|z|^{2}\,\nu(dz)

and, due to (
2.4
), the first term is integrable provided
\beta<\sigma
.
If
\sigma\leq 1
, this also implies that
\beta\leq 1
, so we deduce that
{\mathcal{I}}(x,[\varphi])
is bounded above. Hence we get

{\mathcal{L}}^{b}[\varphi]\geq\beta\langle x\rangle^{\beta-2}\,b(t,x)\cdot x-C_{\beta}

for some different constant
C_{\beta}
, possibly depending on
\lambda_{0}
and
\sigma_{0}
as well. Using (
2.12
), we conclude with (
3.2
).
The same remains true if
\sigma>1
and
\beta\leq 1
.

If
\sigma>1
and
\beta>1
, we estimate, for some
c_{\beta}>0
,

{\mathcal{I}}(x,[\varphi])\leq c_{\beta}(\langle x\rangle^{\beta-1}+1)

and we get

{\mathcal{L}}^{b}[\varphi]\geq\beta\langle x\rangle^{\beta-2}\,b(t,x)\cdot x-C_{\beta}(1+\langle x\rangle^{\beta-1})\,.

(3.3)

Using (
2.12
), the conclusion follows provided
\gamma>1
.

Remark 3.2

Let us observe, from the previous Lemma, the coercive case given by
\gamma\geq 2
. In this range, we have that
\liminf_{|x|\to\infty}\frac{{\mathcal{L}}^{b}[\varphi]}{\varphi}>0
. This holds for every
\beta>0
in the local case
{\mathcal{L}}={\mathcal{L}}_{0}
, otherwise it holds for every
\beta<\sigma
if
{\mathcal{I}}
satisfies (
2.4
).

By contrast, if
\gamma<2
, then
{\mathcal{L}}^{b}[\varphi]\to+\infty
as
|x|\to\infty
only for
\beta>2-\gamma
. This introduces a natural threshold for nonlocal operators satisfying (
2.4
): since
\beta<\sigma
is required for the behavior of
\nu
at infinity, this will lead to require
\gamma>2-\sigma
for the confinement force to play a significant role.

Notice that
\gamma+\beta-2>0
already implies
\gamma>1
whenever
\beta\leq 1
. This always occurs if
\sigma\leq 1
, since
\beta<\sigma
. Otherwise, when
\sigma>1
, it is possible to consider
\beta>1
; nevertheless the restriction
\gamma>1
is still needed to obtain (
3.2
), because one needs the drift term to dominate the diffusion coming from the jumps involving large values of
x
(see (
3.3
)). An interpretation is that the jump part contains terms which are comparable to drift components; so they would be controlled by a confining force only if this blows-up at infinity (say,
\gamma>1
).

To sum up this remark, we observe that requiring
\langle x\rangle^{\beta}
to be a Lyapunov function (i.e.
{\mathcal{L}}^{b}[\langle x\rangle^{\beta}]\to+\infty
as
|x|\to\infty
) leads to the restriction that
\gamma>1
and
\gamma>2-\sigma
.

However, we stress that the restriction
\beta<\sigma
in the nonlocal case only comes from the behavior at infinity of the Levy measure
\nu(z)
, and the reader may easily think at generalizing this result to different settings for
\nu
.

3.1
Doubling variables methods

As we mentioned in the Introduction, two ingredients yield the oscillation estimates of this Section. While Lemma
3.1
exploits the existence of Lyapunov functions using the confinement property of the drift, the second ingredient comes out by handling the local oscillation of solutions through the maximum principle. Here we exploit in full strength the Ishii-Lions doubling variables method, revisited through the ideas of coupling along the lines suggested in
[
40
]
.

Since this is the main ingredient in our approach, let us give first a rough idea of how it works, letting aside any technicality. As we explained in the Introduction, our purpose is to show an oscillation estimate of the form (
1.8
), for some quantitative rate
\varpi(t)
. Rephrasing (
1.8
), this amounts to show that

{\mathcal{U}}(t,x,y)\leq{\mathcal{\zeta}}(t,x,y)

(3.4)

where

{\mathcal{U}}(t,x,y):=u(t,x)-u(t,y)\,,\quad\hbox{and}\quad{\mathcal{\zeta}}(t,x,y)=K\,\varpi(t)\left[[\varphi(x)+\varphi(y)]+\psi(|x-y|)\right],

for some constant
K>0
, some Lyapunov function
\varphi(x)
and some (modulus of continuity)
\psi(|x-y|)
.

Now, (
3.4
) is nothing but a form of comparison, that can be established via maximum principle applied in the product space for
(x,y)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. In fact, if
u
solves (
2.7
), then
{\mathcal{U}}
satisfies

\partial_{t}{\mathcal{U}}-{\rm tr}(Q(x)D^{2}u(x)-Q(y)D^{2}(u(y))-\left[\mathcal{I}(x,[u(x)])-\mathcal{I}(y,[u(y)])\right]+{\mathcal{B}}(x,y)\cdot D_{(x,y)}{\mathcal{U}}=0

(3.5)

where
{\mathcal{B}}(x,y)=(b(x),b(y))
.
Now we observe that if

{\mathcal{U}}-{\mathcal{\zeta}}
has a maximum, then
D^{2}_{(x,y)}{\mathcal{U}}\leq D^{2}_{(x,y)}{\mathcal{\zeta}}
, which implies that

{\rm Tr}\left({\mathcal{A}}D^{2}_{(x,y)}{\mathcal{U}}\right)(x,y)\leq{\rm Tr}\left({\mathcal{A}}D^{2}_{(x,y)}{\mathcal{\zeta}}\right)(x,y)

(3.6)

for any matrix
\mathcal{A}\geq 0
. Such matrix is a degree of freedom to use in order to optimize the estimate on the maximum point. Since
Q
is the diffusion matrix of the operator
{\mathcal{L}}
, it is natural to choose
\mathcal{A}
of the form

\mathcal{A}=\begin{pmatrix}Q(x)&C\\
C^{*}&Q(y)\end{pmatrix}

where the crossed terms are given by a generic matrix
C
with the only constraint that
\mathcal{A}\geq 0
. With this choice (
3.6
) becomes

{\rm tr}(Q(x)D^{2}u(x)-Q(y)D^{2}(u(y))\leq{\rm Tr}\left({\mathcal{A}}D^{2}_{(x,y)}{\mathcal{\zeta}}\right)(x,y)

(3.7)

and a clever choice of the matrix
C
can provide with a useful estimate of the right-hand side. This matrix
C
plays the role of the coupling in the probabilistic approach. On account of the special form of
\zeta
, a suitable choice of
C
will give the estimate (
3.9
) of Lemma
3.3
below, where
X_{n},Y_{n}
should be meant as approximations of
D^{2}u(x),D^{2}u(y)
(in the viscosity solutions’ approach). This is entirely borrowed from
[
40
]

If we also estimate
\psi^{\prime}(r)\leq\psi^{\prime}(r_{1})

\psi(r)\leq C_{1}
,
we deduce from (

K-2\varepsilon k_{0}-(c_{0}+2\sigma_{0}\sigma_{1})\psi^{\prime}(r_{1})-\omega C_{1}<0\qquad\forall r\geq r_{1}.

(3.24)

We now fix

K:=\omega C_{1}+(c_{0}+2\sigma_{0}\sigma_{1})\psi^{\prime}(r_{1})+2\varepsilon k_{0}+[u_{0}]_{\langle x\rangle^{k}}

(3.25)

and we see that inequality (
3.24
) cannot hold. Therefore, we are left with the possibility that

(A) Case

or (

) with

Here we use condition (

\begin{split}&-(2Kk_{0}+2\varepsilon k_{0})<4\lambda_{0}\psi^{\prime\prime}(r)+(c_{0}+2\sigma_{0}\sigma_{1})\psi^{\prime}(r)+\omega\,\psi(r)\\
&\qquad+4\lambda\int_{0}^{1}(1-s)\int_{B}\psi^{\prime\prime}(r+2s(\widehat{x-y}\cdot z))\,|\widehat{x-y}\cdot z|^{2}\frac{dz}{|z|^{d+\sigma}}ds\,.\end{split}

We observe that, using once more the concavity of

Therefore (

\begin{split}&-(2k_{0}(\omega C_{1}+[u_{0}]_{\langle x\rangle^{k}})+2\varepsilon k_{0}(1+2k_{0}))<4\lambda_{0}\psi^{\prime\prime}(r)+(1+2k_{0})(c_{0}+2\sigma_{0}\sigma_{1})\psi^{\prime}(r)+\omega\,\psi(r)\\
&\qquad+4\lambda\int_{0}^{1}(1-s)\int_{B}\psi^{\prime\prime}(r+2s(\widehat{x-y}\cdot z))\,|\widehat{x-y}\cdot z|^{2}\frac{dz}{|z|^{d+\sigma}}ds\,.\end{split}

\begin{split}&-((1+2k_{0})\omega C_{1}+2k_{0}[u_{0}]_{\langle x\rangle^{k}}+2\varepsilon k_{0}(1+2k_{0}))<(1+2k_{0})(c_{0}+2\sigma_{0}\sigma_{1})\psi^{\prime}(r)\\
&\qquad+4\lambda\int_{0}^{1}(1-s)\int_{B}\psi^{\prime\prime}(r+2s(\widehat{x-y}\cdot z))\,|\widehat{x-y}\cdot z|^{2}\frac{dz}{|z|^{d+\sigma}}ds\,.\end{split}

(3.28)

Now we estimate the nonlocal term as follows:

\int_{0}^{1}(1-s)\int_{B}\psi^{\prime\prime}(r+2s(\widehat{x-y}\cdot z))\,|\widehat{x-y}\cdot z|^{2}\frac{dz}{|z|^{d+\sigma}}ds\leq\int_{0}^{1}(1-s)\int_{B^{-}}\psi^{\prime\prime}(r+2s(\widehat{x-y}\cdot z))\,|\widehat{x-y}\cdot z|^{2}\frac{dz}{|z|^{d+\sigma}}ds

Setting

, and using polar coordinates, we observe that

\psi

\begin{split}\psi^{\prime}(r)&\leq\frac{2}{C_{d,\sigma}}\int_{0}^{1}(1-s)\int_{B^{-}}\frac{\psi^{\prime}(r)}{(r\wedge 1)^{2-\sigma}}|e\cdot z|^{2}\frac{dz}{|z|^{d+\sigma}}ds\\

where we also used, in the last step, that for

.

Using (
3.30

3.28

\begin{split}&-((1+2k_{0})\omega C_{1}+2k_{0}[u_{0}]_{\langle x\rangle^{k}}+2\varepsilon k_{0}(1+2k_{0}))\\
&\qquad\leq\int_{0}^{1}(1-s)\int_{B^{-}}\left\{4\lambda\psi^{\prime\prime}(\xi_{s,z})+C\frac{\psi^{\prime}(\xi_{s,z})}{(\xi_{s,z}\wedge 1)^{2-\sigma}}\right\}|e\cdot z|^{2}\frac{dz}{|z|^{d+\sigma}}ds\end{split}

(3.31)

where
\xi_{s,z}=r+2s(e\cdot z)
, and
C=\frac{2}{C_{d,\sigma}}(1+2k_{0})(c_{0}+2\sigma_{0}\sigma_{1})
.

Recall that for
z\in B^{-}
we have
r/2\leq\xi_{s,z}\leq r
. In particular, we have
\xi_{s,z}\leq r_{1}
whenever
r<r_{1}
. Using the definition of
\psi
we compute

\displaystyle 4\lambda\psi^{\prime\prime}(\xi)+C\,\frac{\psi^{\prime}(\xi)}{(\xi\wedge 1)^{2-\sigma}}=-C_{1}\,\theta\,C_{2}\,e^{-C_{2}\xi^{\theta}}\xi^{\theta-2}\left\{4\lambda(1-\theta)+4\lambda\theta C_{2}\xi^{\theta}-C(\xi^{\sigma-1}\vee\xi)\right\}\,.

sufficiently large so that

4\lambda\theta C_{2}\xi^{\theta}-C(\xi^{\sigma-1}\vee\xi)\geq 0\quad\forall\xi\in(0,r_{1})\,.

With this choice of

(which only depends on
\lambda,\theta,\sigma,C,r_{1}
), we finally estimate

\displaystyle\forall\xi\leq r\leq r_{1},\qquad 4\lambda\psi^{\prime\prime}(\xi)+C\,\frac{\psi^{\prime}(\xi)}{(\xi\wedge 1)^{2-\sigma}}

\displaystyle\leq-C_{1}\,\theta\,C_{2}\,e^{-C_{2}\xi^{\theta}}\xi^{\theta-2}4\lambda(1-\theta)

\displaystyle\leq-C_{1}\,L\,r^{\theta-2}\qquad\forall r<r_{1}

for some constant

independent of

Then from (

C_{1}\left(c_{d,\sigma}\,L\,r_{1}^{\theta-\sigma}-\omega(1+2k_{0})\right)-2k_{0}[u_{0}]_{\langle x\rangle^{k}}-2\varepsilon k_{0}(1+2k_{0})\leq 0\,.

Choosing

sufficiently small and
C_{1}
suitably large (

for some large

This shows that (

) holds true; then letting
\varepsilon\to 0
and recalling our choices of the parameters

, we proved that

\displaystyle u(t,x)-u(t,y)

\displaystyle\leq e^{-\omega t}\left\{K\,(\varphi(x)+\varphi(y))+\psi(|x-y|)\right\}

\displaystyle\quad\leq e^{-\omega t}\,(K+C_{1})(\varphi(x)+\varphi(y))\leq e^{-\omega t}\,\tilde{K}\,[u_{0}]_{\langle x\rangle^{k}}(\varphi(x)+\varphi(y))

for some constant

,

(

(B) Case
\lambda_{0}=0

In this case we use (

\begin{split}&-(2Kk_{0}+2\varepsilon k_{0})\leq(c_{0}(r\wedge 1)^{1-\sigma+\delta}+\sigma_{1}(\sigma_{1}r\wedge 2\sigma_{0}))\psi^{\prime}(r)+\omega\,\psi(r)\\
&\qquad+4\lambda\int_{0}^{1}(1-s)\int_{B}\psi^{\prime\prime}(r+2s(\widehat{x-y}\cdot z))\,|\widehat{x-y}\cdot z|^{2}\frac{dz}{|z|^{d+\sigma}}ds\,.\end{split}

Similarly as in (

\displaystyle(c_{0}(r\wedge 1)^{1-\sigma+\delta}+\sigma_{1}(\sigma_{1}r\wedge 2\sigma_{0}))\psi^{\prime}(r)\leq C\int_{0}^{1}(1-s)\int_{B^{-}}\frac{\psi^{\prime}(r+2s(e\cdot z))}{(r+2s(e\cdot z))\wedge 1)^{1-\delta}}|e\cdot z|^{2}\frac{dz}{|z|^{d+\sigma}}ds

for some constant

where

Now we compute

4\lambda\psi^{\prime\prime}(\xi)+C\,\frac{\psi^{\prime}(\xi)}{(\xi\wedge 1)^{1-\delta}}=-C_{1}\,\theta\,C_{2}\,e^{-C_{2}\xi^{\theta}}\xi^{\theta-2}\left\{4\lambda(1-\theta)+4\lambda\theta C_{2}\xi^{\theta}-C(\xi^{\delta}\vee\xi)\right\}\,.

Remark 3.7

We point out that the conditions assumed on the drift
b(t,x)
can be slightly relaxed, at the expense of a mild restriction on the power
\langle x\rangle^{k}
which is used in the weighted seminorm. To be precise, we can replace assumptions (
2.13
) with

\exists\,\,c_{0}>0\,:\,\quad(b(t,x)-b(t,y))\cdot(x-y)\geq-c_{0}|x-y|(1\vee|x-y|)^{q}\qquad\forall x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\,,\,\forall t>0\,,

(3.35)

for some
c_{0}>0,q>0
, and respectively (
2.14
) with

\sigma\in(0,1]\,\hbox{and $\exists\,\delta\in(0,1)$: \, $(b(t,x)-b(t,y))\cdot(x-y)\geq-c_{0}|x-y|(|x-y|\wedge 1)^{1-\sigma+\delta}(1\vee|x-y|)^{q}$.}

(3.36)

Then the conclusion of Theorems
3.5
and Theorem
3.6
remain valid for any
k
such that
\gamma+k-2\geq q

(notice that the above Theorems correspond to
q=0
, where any
k>0,\gamma\geq 2
satisfy this condition).

To provide with this generalization, the only difference in the proof occurs for large values of
|x-y|
.
This is a straightforward variation, since using

\mathcal{L}[\varphi](x)+b(t,x)\cdot D\varphi(x)-\omega\varphi(x)\geq\frac{\alpha k}{2}|x|^{\gamma-2}\varphi(x)\qquad\hbox{for large $|x|$}

one can change (
3.24
) into the following estimate

K\,\frac{\alpha\,k}{2}\left(\frac{r}{2}\right)^{\gamma-2+k}-2\varepsilon k_{0}-(c_{0}\,r^{q}+2\sigma_{0}\sigma_{1})\psi^{\prime}(r_{1})-\omega C_{1}<0\qquad\forall r\geq r_{1}.

which, whenever
\gamma+k-2\geq q
, still implies a choice of
K
of the same order as (
3.25
).

This remark shows that the classical condition which is mostly used in the probabilistic approach, say

(b(t,x)-b(t,y))\cdot(x-y)\geq-c_{0}|x-y|^{2}\qquad\forall x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\,,\,\forall t>0

is not only allowed in our setting, but can be improved e.g. into

(b(t,x)-b(t,y))\cdot(x-y)\geq-c_{0}(|x-y|\vee|x-y|^{2})\qquad\forall x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\,,\,\forall t>0\,.

Remark 3.8

An inspection of the proof of Theorem
3.5
and
3.6
may give some information on how
K,\omega
in (
3.19
) depend on the constants appearing in assumptions (
2.2
), (
2.4
), (
2.12
)-(
2.14
), as well as on other relevant parameters such as the dimension
d
. In fact, an explicit choice of
K
is provided by (
3.25
) and a quantitative estimate of
K
, as well as of
\omega
, will mostly depend on the parameter
r_{1}
and on the choice of the function
\psi(\cdot)
.

Notice that the parameter
r_{1}
only relies on the Lyapunov function
\varphi
, and possibly depends on the dimension
d
(in the model case with Ornstein-Ulhenbeck drift
b(x)=x
, we have
r_{1}=O(\sqrt{d})
). However, the explicit choice of
\psi
made in (
3.20
) may not lead to optimal estimates of
\omega
(neither of
K
, in turn). For example, in case that
{\mathcal{L}}
is the Laplace operator and
b=x
is the Ornstein-Ulhenbeck drift, a better choice for
\psi
would be to take
the solution of the ODE
4\psi^{\prime\prime}(r)+\omega\psi(r)+\omega L=0
for
r\in(0,r_{1})
, where
L
is some suitable constant and
\psi(0)=0,\psi^{\prime}(r_{1})\simeq 0
. This would lead to
\omega=O(r_{1}^{-2})=O(d^{-1})
, as it is expected for this model case, but the same estimate would not be achieved if
\psi
is given by (
3.20
).

This is to mention that, while we opted for the explicit choice (
3.20
) in order to give a unifying proof, sufficiently simple, which works in all different settings (local and nonlocal operators, diffusive or sub-diffusive, slowly and strongly confining drifts, etc…), the same approach could yield better bounds if the auxiliary function
\psi
is built through more sophisticated constructions (which involve possibly auxiliary eigenvalue problems).
A similar discussion is done in
[
20
]
, where quantitative explicit bounds are obtained using the probabilistic coupling method. This could also be possible here, but at the expense of either restricting to more specific examples or adding an extra source of technical details.

3.3
Sub-exponential rate

We now come at the slowly confining case, where, for Lyapunov functions
\varphi
of power type, we have
\mathcal{L}^{b}(\varphi)\to\infty
but
\frac{\mathcal{L}^{b}(\varphi)}{\varphi}\to 0
. This occurs when
\gamma<2
in (
2.12
). In this case one can prove a sub-exponential decay rate (so-called sub-geometrical decay, from discrete models). We start with a general statement of polynomial decay of the weighted oscillation. A similar strategy as in Theorem
3.6
will work, but provided the weight is sufficiently strong.
As in Section
3.2
, we give first a separate statement for the purely local case: indeed, if the diffusion is local the full range of
\gamma\in(0,2)
can be exploited and we have estimates even in the regime of
degenerate confining
, say when
\gamma\in(0,1)
in (
2.12
).

As a first step, we recall the following characterization of weighted seminorms. The following lemma is taken from
[

Lemma 4.1

Let
\varphi
be a continuous function such that
\varphi(x)\geq 1
for all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. For any function
u
such that
u\in L^{\infty}(\varphi^{-1})
, we have

\sup_{x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\,\,\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|}{\varphi(x)+\varphi(y)}=\inf_{c\in{\mathbb{R}}}\,\,\|u+c\|_{L^{\infty}(\varphi^{-1})}

Proof
.  Let us call
M:=\sup\limits_{x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\,\,\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|}{\varphi(x)+\varphi(y)}
and
m:=\inf\limits_{c\in{\mathbb{R}}}\,\,\|u+c\|_{L^{\infty}(\varphi^{-1})}
.
Since

\displaystyle|u(x)-u(y)|

\displaystyle\leq|u(x)+c|+|u(y)+c|

\displaystyle\leq\varphi(x)\|u+c\|_{L^{\infty}(\varphi^{-1})}+\varphi(y)\|u+c\|_{L^{\infty}(\varphi^{-1})}

we immediately deduce that
M\leq m
.

For the reverse inequality, define
c:=\inf_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\,[M\varphi-u]
.
Notice that
c
is finite because
M\varphi(x)-u(x)\geq-M\,\varphi(y)-u(y)
for any
y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.
By definition, we have

\frac{u(x)+c}{\varphi(x)}\leq M\,.

We also estimate, using the definition of
M
and
c
,

\displaystyle\frac{u(x)+c}{\varphi(x)}

\displaystyle=\inf_{y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\,\left[\frac{u(x)+M\varphi(y)-u(y)}{\varphi(x)}\right]

\displaystyle\geq\inf_{y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\left[\frac{M\varphi(y)}{\varphi(x)}-M\left(\frac{\varphi(x)+\varphi(y)}{\varphi(x)}\right)\right]=-M\,.

Hence we deduce that
\|u+c\|_{L^{\infty}(\varphi^{-1})}\leq M
, which yields
m\leq M
.

Thanks to Lemma
4.1
, the decay estimates follow by duality using the results of the previous Section.

5
Generalizations and further results

We presented so far the results that can be obtained using Lyapunov functions of power type, resulting in moments decay of measure-valued solutions. Here we review our strategy in a more abstract form, showing that it can be applied to different choices of Lyapunov functions. The Lyapunov function will determine the weighted space for the oscillation estimate and, what is more important, the rate of convergence is consequential to the weighted norm which is used.

There are two main settings that we consider, corresponding to exponential or sub-exponential decay. Here and below,
\varphi
denotes a generic positive smooth function such that
\varphi(x)\to\infty
as
|x|\to\infty
. There is no loss of generality in assuming that
\varphi(x)\geq 1
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.
We first consider the simplest case of exponential decay in
some
weighted norm, namely when some
\varphi
exists satisfying

\exists\,\,\omega_{0}>0\,:\quad\liminf_{|x|\to\infty}\,\frac{{\mathcal{L}}^{b}[\varphi]}{\varphi}\geq\omega_{0}\,.

(H1)

We denote

\Lambda_{1}:=\{\varphi\in C^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}):\,\,\varphi(x)\mathop{\longrightarrow}^{|x|\to\infty}\infty,\,\,\hbox{${\mathcal{I}}(x,[\varphi])$ is well defined and $\varphi$ satisfies (\ref{H1})}\}.

Clearly, for every
\varphi\in\Lambda_{1}
, one can choose
\omega
sufficiently small so that

{\mathcal{L}}^{b}[\varphi]-\omega\varphi\geq\frac{\omega_{0}}{2}\varphi-k_{0}\qquad\forall x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}

for some
k_{0}>0
. Using the above property, the proof of Theorem
3.6
follows replacing
\langle x\rangle^{k}
with
\varphi(x)
, so that we can generalize the exponential decay estimate for the oscillation, as follows.

Proof

\bar{m}\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
such that
m_{n}\to\bar{m}
in
{\mathcal{M}}_{\varphi}
(or in
{\mathcal{M}}_{\varphi^{\theta}}
if
\varphi\in\Lambda_{2}
). In fact,
\bar{m}
is itself a probability measure. Now we observe that, for any stationary
\xi
, we have

{\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}\xi\,\,d(m_{n}-m_{n-1})+\int_{n-1}^{n}{\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}{\mathcal{L}}^{b}[\xi]\,dm(t)\,dt=0

which implies

{\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}{\mathcal{L}}^{b}[\xi]\,dm_{n}=\int_{n-1}^{n}{\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}{\mathcal{L}}^{b}[\xi]\,d(m_{n}-m(t))\,dt-{\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}\xi\,d(m_{n}-m_{n-1})\,.

(5.13)

If
\varphi\in\Lambda_{1}
, by (
5.11
) we have
\|m_{n}-m_{n-1}\|_{{\mathcal{M}}_{\varphi}}\to 0
. The first integral in the right-hand side also goes to zero; indeed, again by (
5.11
) we have

\left|\int_{n-1}^{n}{\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}{\mathcal{L}}^{b}[\xi]\,d(m(t)-m_{n})\,dt\right|\leq\|{\mathcal{L}}^{b}[\xi]\|_{L^{\infty}(\varphi^{-1})}\int_{n-1}^{n}\|m(t)-m_{n}\|_{{\mathcal{M}}_{\varphi}}dt\leq C\,\int_{n-1}^{n}e^{-\omega t}dt\to 0\,.

If
\varphi\in\Lambda_{2}
, the same argument applies in
{\mathcal{M}}_{\varphi^{\theta}}
and replacing

with
\varpi(t)
, according to (
5.11
).
Therefore, passing to the limit in (
5.13
), recalling the convergence of
m_{n}
, we conclude that
\bar{m}
is a stationary solution. Finally, we can now apply Theorem
5.5
to
m-\bar{m}
, and we deduce that the whole sequence
m(t)
converges to
\bar{m}
. Let us point out that, in case
\varphi\in\Lambda_{2}
, one should apply Theorem
5.5
between the spaces
{\mathcal{M}}_{\varphi^{\theta}}
and
{\mathcal{M}}_{\varphi^{\theta+\varepsilon}}
, since a priori one only knows that
\bar{m}\in{\mathcal{M}}_{\varphi^{\theta}}
for any
\theta<1
.

We point out that, even in the case when the rate function
\varpi(t)
in (
5.2
) is not integrable at infinity, the above argument produces the existence of a stationary solution
\bar{m}\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
. Indeed, in the inequality (
5.13
) it is enough to use that
\varpi(t)\to 0
as
t\to\infty
. By the compactness of
{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, the sequence
m_{n}
will converge to a stationary solution. What is less obvious, in this case, is to show that the stationary solution enjoys
the further regularity
\bar{m}\in{\mathcal{M}}_{\varphi^{\theta}}
for any
\theta<1
. In fact, we don’t know that the sum (
5.12
) is converging in
{\mathcal{M}}_{\varphi^{\theta}}
, if
\varpi
is no longer in
L^{1}(0,\infty)
. In specific cases, one should use extra arguments
to show first that
m(t)
is bounded in
{\mathcal{M}}_{\varphi^{\theta}}
(uniformly in time). We mention that, for the case of power weights
\varphi=\langle x\rangle^{k}
and fractional Laplace operator, one can use e.g.
[

to deduce that
\bar{m}\in L^{1}(\langle x\rangle^{\bar{k}})
for all

the decay estimate (

6
Appendix: existence and uniqueness of solutions

In this section we give a proof of the existence and uniqueness of solutions of the Fokker-Planck equation (
2.10
). Once more, this can be essentially deduced by duality from the well-posedness of viscosity solutions of the adjoint problem. This is why we start by giving a proof of the comparison principle, and uniqueness, of viscosity solutions.

Proposition 6.1

Let
u_{0}\in C({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\cap L^{\infty}(\langle x\rangle^{-k})
,
f\in C(Q_{T})\cap L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{\infty}(\langle x\rangle^{-k}))
, for some
k<\sigma
.
Let
{\mathcal{L}}
be defined by (
2.5
) where
\Sigma
satisfies (
2.2
), the Levy measure in
{\mathcal{I}}
satisfies (
2.4
), and

is such that

is bounded below
and either of the following conditions hold:

(i)
\lambda_{0}>0
or
\sigma\in(1,2)
, and (
2.13
) holds true whenever
|x-y|\leq 1
.

(ii)
\lambda_{0}=0
,
\sigma\in(0,1]
and (
2.14
) holds true whenever
|x-y|\leq 1

Then there exists a unique viscosity solution of

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u+{\mathcal{L}}[u]+b(t,x)\cdot Du=f&\text{ in }(0,T)\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\\
u(0,x)=u_{0}(x),\qquad\,&\text{ in }{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\end{cases}

(6.1)

such that
|u(t,x)|\leq C(1+|x|)^{m}
for some
m<\sigma
.

Proof
.  We prove first the uniqueness of solutions.
Let us take some
\beta<\sigma
such that
\beta\geq k\vee m
. We first remark that (see the computations in Lemma
3.1
)
{\mathcal{L}}^{b}[\langle x\rangle^{\beta}]\geq\beta\langle x\rangle^{\beta-2}(b\cdot x)-c_{\beta}(1+\langle x\rangle^{\beta-1})
; hence there exists

such that

\langle x\rangle^{\beta}+{\mathcal{L}}^{b}[\langle x\rangle^{\beta}]\geq\frac{1}{2}\langle x\rangle^{\beta}-K_{0}

where

only depends on
\sigma,d,\|\Sigma(x)\|_{\infty},\Lambda
and the lower bound of

. We deduce that, choosing

large enough, the function

is a supersolution, provided
\langle x\rangle^{\beta}+K_{1}\geq|u_{0}|
. Let now
u,v
be, respectively, a sub and super-solution of (
6.1
). By linearity, we have that

and
v_{\varepsilon}:=v+\varepsilon W
are still sub/supersolutions, and, if
\beta>m
, there exists a compact set

such that

for

,

. Of course, if we prove the comparison for

, then we get the comparison for

by letting

. Therefore, up to replacing
u,v
with
u_{\varepsilon},v_{\varepsilon}
, we can assume that

outside a compact set

.
Now, suppose that

, which means that

is attained at some point in
(0,T)\times K
, with
t>0
. Doubling variables, we consider

M_{\varepsilon}:=\sup_{(x,y)}\,\,\{u(t,x)-v(t,y)-\frac{|x-y|^{2}}{2\varepsilon}-A_{\varepsilon}t^{\eta}\}

for some
\eta,A_{\varepsilon}>0
to be chosen, with

. Since

, then

is positive for

, this implies that

is assumed at points

which lie in a compact set, which is actually an
\varepsilon

,

\frac{|x_{\varepsilon}-y_{\varepsilon}|^{2}}{2\varepsilon}\leq\hat{M}=\sup u-\inf v<\infty
. In particular we have

|x_{\varepsilon}-y_{\varepsilon}|\leq\sqrt{2\varepsilon\,\hat{M}}

and by usual arguments, since

as

, we also get

\frac{|x_{\varepsilon}-y_{\varepsilon}|^{2}}{\varepsilon}\to 0
. Using Theorem
2.3
, the Lipschitz character of
\Sigma(x)
and standard arguments in the doubling variable method, we get

\eta A_{\varepsilon}\,t^{\eta-1}+(b(t,x_{\varepsilon})-b(t,y_{\varepsilon}))\cdot\frac{(x_{\varepsilon}-y_{\varepsilon})}{\varepsilon}\leq f(t,x_{\varepsilon})-f(t,y_{\varepsilon})+C\,\sigma_{1}\frac{|x_{\varepsilon}-y_{\varepsilon}|^{2}}{\varepsilon}\,.

(6.3)

At this stage we use the following regularity result, which can be obtained with similar arguments as used in Section 3 (see the Appendix in
[
22
]
): using conditions (
2.13
), or (
2.14
), for

, every viscosity solution of (

\begin{split}&|u(t,x)-u(t,y)|\leq C_{T}\frac{[\langle x\rangle^{k}+\langle y\rangle^{k}]}{t^{\frac{1}{\sigma}}}\,|x-y|\,,\quad\forall x,y\,,\qquad\hbox{if $\sigma\in(1,2)$,}\\
&|u(t,x)-u(t,y)|\leq C_{T}\frac{[\langle x\rangle^{k}+\langle y\rangle^{k}]}{t^{\frac{\theta}{\sigma}}}\,|x-y|^{\theta}\,,\quad\forall x,y\,,\qquad\hbox{if $\sigma\in(0,1]$, $\theta<\sigma$.}\end{split}

lie in a compact set, we deduce that they satisfy, if

\frac{|x-y|^{2}}{2\varepsilon}\leq\frac{C_{T,K}}{t^{\frac{1}{\sigma}}}|x-y|\leq C\frac{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}{t^{\frac{1}{\sigma}}}\,.

Notice that this constant

\displaystyle\eta A_{\varepsilon}\,t^{\eta-1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2C_{T,K}}{t^{\frac{1}{\sigma}}}\,|b(t,x_{\varepsilon})-b(t,y_{\varepsilon})|+f(t,x_{\varepsilon})-f(t,y_{\varepsilon})+C\,\sigma_{1}\frac{|x_{\varepsilon}-y_{\varepsilon}|^{2}}{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\leq C\,\omega(\varepsilon)\left(\frac{1}{t^{\frac{1}{\sigma}}}+1\right)+\frac{C}{t^{\frac{1}{\sigma}}}\,\sqrt{\varepsilon}

If

\frac{|x-y|^{2}}{2\varepsilon}\leq\frac{C}{t^{\frac{\theta}{\sigma}}}|x-y|^{\theta}

then we
use (

\displaystyle\eta A_{\varepsilon}\,t^{\eta-1}

\displaystyle\leq c_{0}\,|x_{\varepsilon}-y_{\varepsilon}|^{1-\sigma+\delta}\,\frac{|x_{\varepsilon}-y_{\varepsilon}|}{\varepsilon}+f(t,x_{\varepsilon})-f(t,y_{\varepsilon})+C\,\sigma_{1}\frac{|x_{\varepsilon}-y_{\varepsilon}|^{2}}{\varepsilon}

The existence of solutions can be proved through the vanishing viscosity method (using (
