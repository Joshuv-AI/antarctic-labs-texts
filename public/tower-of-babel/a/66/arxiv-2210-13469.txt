Symmetric function generalizations of the q q -Baker–Forrester ex-conjecture and Selberg-type integrals Thanks: *Corresponding author
Guoce Xin, Yue Zhou*

Abstract
. It is well-known that the famous Selberg integral is equivalent to the Morris constant term identity. In 1998, Baker and Forrester conjectured a generalization of the q q -Morris constant term identity. This conjecture was proved and extended by Károlyi, Nagy, Petrov and Volkov in 2015. In this paper, we obtain two symmetric function generalizations of the q q -Baker–Forrester ex-conjecture. These includes: (i) a q q -Baker–Forrester type constant term identity for a product of a complete symmetric function and a Macdonald polynomial; (ii) a complete symmetric function generalization of KNPV’s result. Keywords: Constant term identities, Selberg integrals, q q -Morris identity, q q -Baker–Forrester conjecture, Macdonald polynomials, complete symmetric functions.

Introduction

In 1944, Atle Selberg
[
21
]
gave the following remarkable multiple integral:

\int_{0}^{1}\cdots\int_{0}^{1}\prod_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{\alpha-1}(1-x_{i})^{\beta-1}\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq n}|x_{i}-x_{j}|^{2\gamma}\mathrm{d}x_{1}\cdots\mathrm{d}x_{n}\\
=\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+j\gamma)\Gamma(\beta+j\gamma)\Gamma(1+(j+1)\gamma)}{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta+(n+j-1)\gamma)\Gamma(1+\gamma)},

where
\alpha,\beta,\gamma
are complex parameters such that

\mathrm{Re}(\alpha)>0,\quad\mathrm{Re}(\beta)>0,\quad\mathrm{Re}(\gamma)>-\min\{1/n,\mathrm{Re}(\alpha)/(n-1),\mathrm{Re}(\beta)/(n-1)\}.

When
n=1
, the above Selberg integral reduces to the Euler beta integral.
For the importance of the Selberg integral, one can refer
[
6
]
.

It is well-known that the Selberg integral is equivalent to the Morris constant term identity
[
19
]

(1.1)

\CT_{x}\prod_{i=1}^{n}(1-x_{0}/x_{i})^{a}(1-x_{i}/x_{0})^{b}\prod_{1\leq i\neq j\leq n}(1-x_{i}/x_{j})^{c}=\prod_{i=0}^{n-1}\frac{(a+b+ic)!\big((i+1)c\big)!}{(a+ic)!(b+ic)!c!}

for nonnegative integers
a,b,c
,
where
\CT\limits_{x}
denotes taking the constant term with respect to
x:=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
.
Note that we can set
x_{0}=1
in (
1.1
) without changing the constant term, since we take the constant term in a homogeneous Laurent polynomial.
The same principle applies to all the homogeneous Laurent polynomials in this paper.
In his Ph.D. thesis
[
19
]
, Morris also conjectured the following

q
-analogue constant term identity

(1.2)

\CT_{x}\prod_{i=1}^{n}(x_{0}/x_{i})_{a}(qx_{i}/x_{0})_{b}\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq n}(x_{i}/x_{j})_{c}(qx_{j}/x_{i})_{c}=\prod_{i=0}^{n-1}\frac{(q)_{a+b+ic}(q)_{(i+1)c}}{(q)_{a+ic}(q)_{b+ic}(q)_{c}},

where
(z)_{c}=(z;q)_{c}:=(1-z)(1-zq)\cdots(1-zq^{c-1})
is the
q
-factorial for a positive integer

(1)

3.1.
Plethystic notation and substitution

Plethystic or
\lambda
-ring notation is a device to facilitate computations in the ring of symmetric functions.
In this subsection, we briefly introduce plethystic notation and substitution.
The reader can refer
[
11
,
14
,
20
]
for more details.

Let
\mathbb{F}
be a field and
\Lambda_{\mathbb{F}}
be the ring of symmetric functions in countably many variables with coefficients in
\mathbb{F}
.
For a set of countably many variables
X=\{x_{1},x_{2},\dots\}
(we refer
X
as an alphabet),
we additively write
X:=x_{1}+x_{2}+\cdots
, and
use plethystic brackets to indicate this additive notation:

f(X)=f(x_{1},x_{2},\dots)=f[x_{1}+x_{2}+\cdots]=f[X],\quad\text{for $f\in\Lambda_{\mathbb{F}}$.}

Let
p_{r}
be the power sum
symmetric function in the alphabet
X
, defined by

p_{r}=\sum_{i\geq 1}x_{i}^{r}

for
r
a positive integer and
p_{0}=1
.
For a partition
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\dots)
, define

p_{\lambda}=p_{\lambda_{1}}p_{\lambda_{2}}\cdots.

The
p_{r}
are algebraically independent over

\mathbb{Q}
, and the
p_{\lambda}
form a basis of
\Lambda_{\mathbb{Q}}

[
18
]
.
Hence, we can write

\Lambda_{\mathbb{Q}}=\mathbb{Q}[p_{1},p_{2},\dots].

A power sum whose argument is the sum, difference or Cartesian product
of two alphabets
X
and
Y
is defined as

(3.1a)

\displaystyle p_{r}[X+Y]

\displaystyle=p_{r}[X]+p_{r}[Y],

(3.1b)

\displaystyle p_{r}[X-Y]

\displaystyle=p_{r}[X]-p_{r}[Y],

(3.1c)

\displaystyle p_{r}[XY]

\displaystyle=p_{r}[X]p_{r}[Y].

Occasionally we need to use an ordinary minus sign in plethystic notation.
To distinguish this from a plethystic minus sign, we denote by
\epsilon
the alphabet consisting of the single letter
-1
, so that for
f\in\Lambda_{\mathbb{F}}

f(-x)=f(-x_{1},-x_{2},\dots)=f[\epsilon x_{1}+\epsilon x_{2}+\cdots]=f[\epsilon X].

Hence

p_{r}[\epsilon X]=(-1)^{r}p_{r}[X],\quad p_{r}[-\epsilon X]=(-1)^{r-1}p_{r}[X].

If
f
is a homogeneous symmetric function of degree
k
then

(3.2)

f[aX]=a^{k}f[X]

for a single-letter alphabet
a
.
In particular,

(3.3)

f[\epsilon X]=(-1)^{k}f[X].

From (
3.1a
), we also have

p_{r}[2X]:=p_{r}[X+X]=2p_{r}[X].

We extend the above to any
k\in\mathbb{F}
via

(3.4)

p_{r}[kX]=kp_{r}[X].

Note that this leads to some notational ambiguities,
and whenever not clear from the context we will indicate if a symbol such as
a
or
k

represents a letter or a binomial element
1
1

1

In
[
14
, p. 32]
Lascoux refers to
k\in\mathbb{F}
as a binomial element.
.
Suppose
t
is a single-letter alphabet such that
|t|<1
. By (
3.1a
) and (
3.1c
)

p_{r}\Big[\frac{X}{1-t}\Big]=p_{r}[X(1+t+t^{2}+\cdots)]=p_{r}[X]\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}p_{r}[t^{k}]=p_{r}[X]\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}t^{kr}=\frac{p_{r}[X]}{1-t^{r}}.

Hence, we have the next division rule:

(3.5)

p_{r}\Big[\frac{X}{1-t}\Big]=\frac{p_{r}[X]}{1-t^{r}}.

Note that we can not define the plethystic division by an arbitrary
alphabet.

The plethystic substitution greatly simplifies our computation of constant terms because of the following rules:

1. We can simplify and compute the alphabets in the bracket.
For instance
f[X-X]=f[0]=f(0,0,\dots)
and
f[(1-q^{k})/(1-q)]=f[1+q+\cdots+q^{k-1}]
for

k
a nonnegative integer and any
f\in\Lambda_{\mathbb{F}}
, as this is easily checked for the
f=p_{r}
case.

2. We can make the substitution inside the bracket if we substitute
an
x
(or
y
)-variable by a one-letter alphabet (or a product of finitely many one-letter alphabets). For instance, if we want to substitute
Y
by
M

in
f[X-Y]
, then we can check that

p_{r}[X-Y]\big|_{Y\to M}=p_{r}[X]-p_{r}[Y]\big|_{Y\to M}=p_{r}[X]-p_{r}[M]=p_{r}[X-M]
.
In fact, we can summarize this rule simply by:
a variable can only be substituted by a one-letter alphabet
(or a product of finitely many one-letter alphabets), not a signed one-letter alphabet.

Let the elementary symmetric
function be defined as

e_{r}=\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<\dots<i_{r}}x_{i_{1}}x_{i_{2}}\cdots x_{i_{r}}

for
r
a positive integer and
e_{0}=1
.
The next simple fact plays an important role in proving vanishing
properties of the constant terms
B_{n,n_{0}}(a,b,c,l,\mu)
and
C_{n,n_{0}}(a,b,c,l,m)
.
By the definition of the elementary symmetric function,

(3.6)

e_{r}[X]=0\qquad\text{if $|X|<r$,}

for
r
a positive integer and
X
an alphabet of finitely many variables.
Here
|X|
denotes the cardinality of
X
.

Finally, we need the following two basic plethystic identities.
One can find proofs in
[

, Theorem 1.27]
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
X
and
Y
be two alphabets. For

a nonnegative integer,

(3.7)

\displaystyle h_{r}[X+Y]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{r}h_{i}[X]h_{r-i}[Y],

(3.8)

\displaystyle h_{r}[-X]

3.2.
Constant term evaluations using iterated Laurent series

In this subsection we present a basic lemma for extracting
constant terms from rational functions in the framework of the field of iterated Laurent series.

Throughout this paper we let
K=\mathbb{C}(q)
and work in the field of iterated
Laurent series
K\langle\!\langle x_{n},x_{n-1},\dots,x_{0}\rangle\!\rangle=K(\!(x_{n})\!)(\!(x_{n-1})\!)\cdots(\!(x_{0})\!)
.
Elements of
K\langle\!\langle x_{n},x_{n-1},\dots,x_{0}\rangle\!\rangle

are regarded first as Laurent series in
x_{0}
, then as
Laurent series in
x_{1}
, and so on.
In
[
9
]
, the authors showed that the field
K\langle\!\langle x_{n},x_{n-1},\dots,x_{0}\rangle\!\rangle
is highly suitable for proving the
q
-Dyson style constant term identities.
The reader may refer
[
24
]
and
[
25
]
for more detailed
account of the properties of this field.
The most applicable fact in what is to follow is that the field
K(x_{0},\dots,x_{n})
of
rational functions in the variables
x_{0},\dots,x_{n}
with coefficients in
K

forms a subfield of
K\langle\!\langle x_{n},x_{n-1},\dots,x_{0}\rangle\!\rangle
, so that every rational function can be identified with its unique Laurent
series expansion.

The following series expansion of
1/(1-cx_{i}/x_{j})

for
c\in K\setminus\{0\}
forms a key ingredient in our approach:

\frac{1}{1-cx_{i}/x_{j}}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\sum_{l\geq 0}c^{l}(x_{i}/x_{j})^{l}&\text{if $i<j$},\\[14.22636pt]
\displaystyle-\sum_{l<0}c^{l}(x_{i}/x_{j})^{l}&\text{if $i>j$}.\end{cases}

Thus,

(3.9)

\CT_{x_{i}}\frac{1}{1-cx_{i}/x_{j}}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if $i<j$},\\
0&\text{if $i>j$},\\
\end{cases}

where, for
f\in K\langle\!\langle x_{n},x_{n-1},\dots,x_{0}\rangle\!\rangle
,
we also use the notation
\displaystyle\CT_{x_{i}}f
to denote taking the constant term
of
f
with respect to
x_{i}
.
The constant term operators defined in
K\langle\!\langle x_{n},\dots,x_{0}\rangle\!\rangle
have the property of commutativity:

\CT_{x_{i}}\CT_{x_{j}}f=\CT_{x_{j}}\CT_{x_{i}}f

for any
i
and
j
.
This means that we are taking constant term in a set of variables.
Then
\CT\limits_{x}
is well-defined in this field.

The following lemma has appeared previously in
[
9
]
.
It is a basic tool for
extracting constant terms from rational functions.

Lemma 3.2
.

For a positive integer
m
, let
p(x_{k})
be a Laurent polynomial
in
x_{k}
of degree at most
m-1
with coefficients in

K\langle\!\langle x_{n},\dots,x_{k-1},x_{k+1},\dots,x_{0}\rangle\!\rangle
.
Let
0\leq i_{1}\leq\dots\leq i_{m}\leq n
such that all
i_{r}\neq k
,
and define

(3.10)

f=\frac{p(x_{k})}{\prod_{r=1}^{m}(1-c_{r}x_{k}/x_{i_{r}})},

where
c_{1},\dots,c_{m}\in K\setminus\{0\}
such that
c_{r}\neq c_{s}
if
x_{i_{r}}=x_{i_{s}}
.
Then

(3.11)

\CT_{x_{k}}f=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r=1\\[1.0pt]
i_{r}>k\end{subarray}}^{m}\big(f\,(1-c_{r}x_{k}/x_{i_{r}})\big)\Big|_{x_{k}=c_{r}^{-1}x_{i_{r}}}.

Note that we extended Lemma
3.2
to
[
16
, Lemma 3.1]
,
in which
p(x_{k})
is a Laurent polynomial in
x_{k}
of degree at most
m
.
In this paper, we find a way to deal with the cases when the degree in
x_{k}
of
p(x_{k})
is larger than
m
.
In our application of plethystic substitution, a crucial fact is that every
c_{r}
is of the form
q^{u}
for

u\in\mathbb{Z}
. In other words,
q
and
q^{-1}
are treated as single letter alphabets in plethystic notation,
and each
c_{r}
is a product of finite many
q
or
q^{-1}
.

The next proposition presents an equivalence between two kinds of constant terms.

Proposition 3.3
.

For a positive integer
n
, a nonnegative integer
c
and
x:=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
, let
f(x)
be a rational function invariant under any permutation of
x
. Then

(3.12)

\CT_{x}f(x)\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq n}(x_{i}/x_{j})_{c}(qx_{j}/x_{i})_{c}=\frac{1}{n!}\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}\frac{1-q^{(i+1)c}}{1-q^{c}}\times\CT_{x}f(x)\prod_{1\leq i\neq j\leq n}(x_{i}/x_{j})_{c}.

Proof.

For
w:=(w_{1},\dots,w_{n})\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
and any function
g(x)
, define

w\circ g(x):=g(x_{w_{1}},\dots,x_{w_{n}}).

Since the action of
w
on any Laurent polynomial (or Laurent series) does not change the constant term, we have

(3.13)

\CT_{x}f(x)\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq n}(x_{i}/x_{j})_{c}(qx_{j}/x_{i})_{c}\\
\quad=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{w\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}\CT_{x}w\circ\bigg(f(x)\prod_{1\leq i\neq j\leq n}(x_{i}/x_{j})_{c}\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\frac{1-q^{c}x_{j}/x_{i}}{1-x_{j}/x_{i}}\bigg).

Because
f(x)\prod_{1\leq i\neq j\leq n}(x_{i}/x_{j})_{c}
is invariant under the action of
w
, the right-hand side of (
3.13
) becomes

\frac{1}{n!}\CT_{x}f(x)\prod_{1\leq i\neq j\leq n}(x_{i}/x_{j})_{c}\cdot\sum_{w\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}w\circ\bigg(\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\frac{1-q^{c}z_{j}/x_{i}}{1-x_{j}/x_{i}}\bigg),

which is the right-hand side of (
3.12
) by the next identity
[
18
, III, (1.4)]
.

(3.14)

\sum_{w\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}w\circ\bigg(\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\frac{1-q^{c}x_{j}/x_{i}}{1-x_{j}/x_{i}}\bigg)=\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}\frac{1-q^{(i+1)c}}{1-q^{c}}.\qed

3.3.
A splitting formula

In this subsection, we give a splitting formula for
S_{n,n_{0}}(x)
defined in (
3.16
) below.
The idea first appeared in Cai’s proof of Kadell’s
q
-Dyson orthogonality conjecture
[
5
]
.

For nonnegative integers
n,n_{0}
and a positive integer
c
, let

(3.15)

D_{n,n_{0}}(x)=D_{n,n_{0}}(x,c):=\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\big(x_{i}/x_{j}\big)_{c-\chi(i\leq n_{0})}\big(qx_{j}/x_{i}\big)_{c-\chi(i\leq n_{0})},

where
x:=(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n})
.
Recall the definition of
F_{n,n_{0}}(x;a,b,c,m)
in (
1.5
). It is clear that

D_{n,n_{0}}(x,c)=F_{n,n_{0}}(x;0,0,c,0).

Denote

(3.16)

S_{n,n_{0}}(x)=S_{n,n_{0}}(x,c):=D_{n,n_{0}}(x,c)\times\prod_{i=1}^{n_{0}}(x_{i}/w)^{-1}_{c-1}\prod_{i=n_{0}+1}^{n}(x_{i}/w)^{-1}_{c},

where
w
is a parameter such that
all terms of the form
q^{u}x_{i}/w
in (
3.16
)
satisfy
|q^{u}x_{i}/w|<1
. Hence,

\frac{1}{1-q^{u}x_{i}/w}=\sum_{j\geq 0}(q^{u}x_{i}/w)^{j}.

Then, it is easy to see that

(3.17)

\CT_{x}x^{v}D_{n,n_{0}}(x)=\CT_{x,w}x^{v}S_{n,n_{0}}(x),

where
v:=(v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
and

x^{v}
denotes the monomial
x_{1}^{v_{1}}\cdots x_{n}^{v_{n}}
.
Note that if
|v|:=v_{1}+\cdots+v_{n}\neq 0
then

\CT\limits_{x}x^{v}D_{n,n_{0}}(x)=0
by the homogeneity of
D_{n,n_{0}}(x)
.

We need the following simple result in the proof of the splitting formula mentioned above.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
i,j
be nonnegative integers and
t
be an integer. Then, for
0\leq t\leq j

(3.18a)

\frac{(1/y)_{i}(qy)_{j}}{(q^{-t}/y)_{i}}=q^{it}(q^{1-i}y)_{t}(q^{t+1}y)_{j-t};

for
-1\leq t\leq j-1

(3.18b)

\frac{(y)_{j}(q/y)_{i}}{(q^{-t}/y)_{i}}=q^{i(t+1)}(q^{-i}y)_{t+1}(q^{t+1}y)_{j-t-1};

and for
0\leq t\leq j-1

(3.18c)

\frac{(y)_{j}(q/y)_{i}}{(q^{-t}/y)_{i+1}}=-yq^{(i+1)t}(q^{-i}y)_{t}(q^{t+1}y)_{j-t-1}.

Note that the
t=j
case of (
3.18a
) (taking
y\mapsto y/q
) is the standard fact in
[
7
, Equation (I.13)]
.
The condition
0\leq t\leq j-1
in (
3.18c
) forces
j
to be a positive integer.

Proof.

For
0\leq t\leq j
,

\frac{(1/y)_{i}(qy)_{j}}{(q^{-t}/y)_{i}}=\frac{(q^{i-t}/y)_{t}(qy)_{j}}{(q^{-t}/y)_{t}}=\frac{(-1/y)^{t}q^{it-\binom{t+1}{2}}(q^{1-i}y)_{t}(qy)_{j}}{(-1/y)^{t}q^{-\binom{t+1}{2}}(qy)_{t}}=q^{it}(q^{1-i}y)_{t}(q^{t+1}y)_{j-t}.

Taking
y\mapsto y/q
and
t\mapsto t+1
in (
3.18a
) yields
(
3.18b
) for
-1\leq t\leq j-1
.

For
0\leq t\leq j-1
,

\frac{(y)_{j}(q/y)_{i}}{(q^{-t}/y)_{i+1}}=\frac{(y)_{j}(q^{i-t+1}/y)_{t}}{(q^{-t}/y)_{t+1}}=\frac{(y)_{j}(-1/y)^{t}q^{it-\binom{t}{2}}(q^{-i}y)_{t}}{(-1/y)^{t+1}q^{-\binom{t+1}{2}}(y)_{t+1}}=-yq^{(i+1)t}(q^{-i}y)_{t}(q^{t+1}y)_{j-t-1}.\qed

The rational function

admits the following partial fraction expansion.
We refer it as the splitting formula for

Proposition 3.5
.

Let

\times\prod_{l=i+1}^{n_{0}}\big(q^{1-c}x_{i}/x_{l}\big)_{j+1}\big(q^{j+1}x_{i}/x_{l}\big)_{c-j-2}\prod_{l=n_{0}+1}^{n}(-x_{i}/x_{l})\big(q^{1-c}x_{i}/x_{l}\big)_{j}\big(q^{j+1}x_{i}/x_{l}\big)_{c-j-2},

where

.

Note that the

. If

Proof.

By partial fraction decomposition of

with respect to
w^{-1}

and

(3.23)

3.4.
Consequences of the splitting formula

In this subsection, we obtain several vanishing constant terms by the splitting formula for
S_{n,n_{0}}(x)
.

We begin with the next vanishing lemma.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
D_{n,n_{0}}(x;c)
be defined in (
3.15
),

v=(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, and
\lambda
be a partition such that
|v|=|\lambda|
.
If
\lambda_{1}>\max\{v_{i}\mid i=1,\dots,n\}

(3.29)

\CT_{x}x^{-v}h_{\lambda}\Big[\sum_{t=1}^{n}\frac{1-q^{c-\chi(t\leq n_{0})}}{1-q}x_{t}\Big]D_{n,n_{0}}(x;c)=0.

Proof.

By the generating function for complete symmetric functions (

for a nonnegative integer

.
Thus

(3.30)

\CT_{x}x^{-v}h_{\lambda}\Big[\sum_{t=1}^{n}\frac{1-q^{c-\chi(t\leq n_{0})}}{1-q}x_{t}\Big]D_{n,n_{0}}(x;c)\\
=\CT_{x,w}\frac{w^{\lambda_{1}}h_{\lambda^{(1)}}\big[\sum_{t=1}^{n}\frac{1-q^{c-\chi(t\leq n_{0})}}{1-q}x_{t}\big]D_{n,n_{0}}(x;c)}{x^{v}\prod_{i=1}^{n}(x_{i}/w)_{c-\chi(i\leq n_{0})}},

where
\lambda^{(1)}=(\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3},\dots)
.
By the splitting formula (
3.19
) for
S_{n,n_{0}}(x)
,

(3.31)

\CT_{x,w}\frac{w^{\lambda_{1}}h_{\lambda^{(1)}}\big[\sum_{t=1}^{n}\frac{1-q^{c-\chi(t\leq n_{0})}}{1-q}x_{t}\big]D_{n,n_{0}}(x;c)}{x^{v}\prod_{i=1}^{n}(x_{i}/w)_{c-\chi(i\leq n_{0})}}=\CT_{x,w}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{0}}\sum_{j=0}^{c-2}\frac{w^{\lambda_{1}}h_{\lambda^{(1)}}\big[\sum_{t=1}^{n}\frac{1-q^{c-\chi(t\leq n_{0})}}{1-q}x_{t}\big]A_{ij}}{x^{v}(1-q^{j}x_{i}/w)}\\
+\CT_{x,w}\sum_{i=n_{0}+1}^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{c-1}\frac{w^{\lambda_{1}}h_{\lambda^{(1)}}\big[\sum_{t=1}^{n}\frac{1-q^{c-\chi(t\leq n_{0})}}{1-q}x_{t}\big]B_{ij}}{x^{v}(1-q^{j}x_{i}/w)}.

Here the
A_{ij}
and the
B_{ij}
are polynomials in
x_{i}
.
Taking the constant term with respect to

in the right-hand side of (

) yields

(3.32)

\displaystyle\CT_{x,w}\frac{w^{\lambda_{1}}h_{\lambda^{(1)}}\big[\sum_{t=1}^{n}\frac{1-q^{c-\chi(t\leq n_{0})}}{1-q}x_{t}\big]D_{n,n_{0}}(x;c)}{x^{v}\prod_{i=1}^{n}(x_{i}/w)_{c-\chi(i\leq n_{0})}}

\displaystyle\quad=\CT_{x}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{0}}\sum_{j=0}^{c-2}q^{j\lambda_{1}}x^{-v}x_{i}^{\lambda_{1}}h_{\lambda^{(1)}}\Big[\sum_{t=1}^{n}\frac{1-q^{c-\chi(t\leq n_{0})}}{1-q}x_{t}\Big]A_{ij}

\displaystyle\quad+\CT_{x}\sum_{i=n_{0}+1}^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{c-1}q^{j\lambda_{1}}x^{-v}x_{i}^{\lambda_{1}}h_{\lambda^{(1)}}\Big[\sum_{t=1}^{n}\frac{1-q^{c-\chi(t\leq n_{0})}}{1-q}x_{t}\Big]B_{ij}.

Since

, and

Thus

\CT_{x,w}\frac{w^{\lambda_{1}}h_{\lambda^{(1)}}\big[\sum_{t=1}^{n}\frac{1-q^{c-\chi(t\leq n_{0})}}{1-q}x_{t}\big]D_{n,n_{0}}(x;c)}{x^{v}\prod_{i=1}^{n}(x_{i}/w)_{c-\chi(i\leq n_{0})}}=0.

Together with (
3.30
), the lemma follows.
∎

4.
Macdonald polynomials

In this section, we give several essential results for Macdonald polynomials
[
17
,
18
]
.

Given a sequence
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\dots)
of nonnegative integers such that
|\alpha|=\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}+\cdots
is finite.
Let
X=\{x_{1},x_{2},\dots\}
be an alphabet of countably many variables. Then the monomial symmetric function indexed by a partition
\lambda
is defined as

m_{\lambda}=m_{\lambda}(X):=\sum x_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}x_{2}^{\alpha_{2}}\cdots,

where the sum is over all distinct permutations
\alpha
of
\lambda
.
Denote
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{Q}(q,t)
.
Let
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle:\Lambda_{\mathbb{F}}\times\Lambda_{\mathbb{F}}\rightarrow\mathbb{F}

be the
q,t
-Hall scalar product on
\Lambda_{\mathbb{F}}
given by
[
18
, page 306]

\langle p_{\lambda},p_{\mu}\rangle:=\delta_{\lambda,\mu}z_{\lambda}\prod_{i\geq 1}\frac{1-q^{\lambda_{i}}}{1-t^{\lambda_{i}}},

where
z_{\lambda}:=\prod_{i\geq 1}i^{m_{i}}m_{i}!
if
m_{i}
is the number of parts
i
in
\lambda
,
and the Kronecker symbol
\delta_{\lambda,\mu}=1
if
\lambda=\mu
and 0 otherwise.
The Macdonald polynomials

P_{\lambda}=P_{\lambda}(q,t)=P_{\lambda}(X;q,t)
are the unique symmetric functions
[
18
, VI, (4.7)]
such that

\langle P_{\lambda},P_{\mu}\rangle=0\quad\text{if}\quad\lambda\neq\mu

and

(4.1)

P_{\lambda}=m_{\lambda}+\sum_{\mu<\lambda}u_{\lambda\mu}m_{\mu},\quad u_{\lambda\mu}\in\mathbb{F}.

Let

b_{\lambda}=b_{\lambda}(q,t)=\langle P_{\lambda},P_{\lambda}\rangle^{-1},

and

(4.2)

Q_{\lambda}=b_{\lambda}P_{\lambda},

so that

\langle P_{\lambda},Q_{\mu}\rangle=\delta_{\lambda\mu}

for all partitions
\lambda
and
\mu
. See
[
18
, VI, (4.11), (4.12)]
.
For any three partitions
\lambda,\mu,\nu
let

f_{\mu\nu}^{\lambda}=f_{\mu\nu}^{\lambda}(q,t)=\langle Q_{\lambda},P_{\mu}P_{\nu}\rangle\in\mathbb{F}.

The coefficient
f_{\mu\nu}^{\lambda}
is called the
q,t
-Littlewood–Richardson coefficient.
Let
\lambda,\mu
be partitions and define skew functions

Q_{\lambda/\mu}\in\Lambda_{\mathbb{F}}

by

(4.3)

Q_{\lambda/\mu}=\sum_{\nu}f_{\mu\nu}^{\lambda}Q_{\nu}

so that

(4.4)

\langle Q_{\lambda/\mu},P_{\nu}\rangle=\langle Q_{\lambda},P_{\mu}P_{\nu}\rangle.

Likewise we define the skew Macdonald polynomial
P_{\lambda/\mu}
by interchanging the
P
’s and
Q
’s in (
4.4
)

(4.5)

\langle P_{\lambda/\mu},Q_{\nu}\rangle=\langle P_{\lambda},Q_{\mu}Q_{\nu}\rangle.

Since
Q_{\lambda}=b_{\lambda}P_{\lambda}
, it follows that

(4.6)

Q_{\lambda/\mu}=b_{\lambda}b_{\mu}^{-1}P_{\lambda/\mu}.

See
[
18
, VI, (7.8)]
.
For
u,v\in\mathbb{F}
such that
v\neq\pm 1
, let
\omega_{u,v}
denote the
\mathbb{F}
-algebra
endomorphism of
\Lambda_{\mathbb{F}}
defined by

\omega_{u,v}(p_{r})=(-1)^{r-1}\frac{1-u^{r}}{1-v^{r}}p_{r}

for all
r\geq 1
, so that

\omega_{u,v}(p_{\lambda})=(-1)^{|\lambda|-\ell(\lambda)}p_{\lambda}\prod_{i=1}^{\ell(\lambda)}\frac{1-u^{\lambda_{i}}}{1-v^{\lambda_{i}}}.

Then we have the duality
[
18
, VI, (7.16)]

(4.7)

\omega_{q,t}P_{\lambda/\mu}(q,t)=Q_{\lambda^{\prime}/\mu^{\prime}}(t,q).

For any alphabet
X
, the endomorphism
\omega_{q,t}
can be interpret plethystically.
That is

(4.8)

X\mapsto\epsilon X\frac{1-q}{t-1}.

For two alphabets
X
and
Y
,

(4.9)

P_{\lambda}[X+Y]=\sum_{\mu\subset\lambda}P_{\lambda/\mu}[X]P_{\mu}[Y].

See
[
18
, VI, (7.9’)]
.
If
n<\ell(\lambda)
then

(4.10)

P_{\lambda}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n};q,t)=0.

See
[
18
, VI, (4.10)]
.
If
\lambda
is a partition of length
n
, then

(4.11)

P_{\lambda}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n};q,t)=x_{1}\dots x_{n}P_{\mu}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n};q,t),

where
\mu=(\lambda_{1}-1,\dots,\lambda_{n}-1)
.
See
[
18
, VI, (4.17)]
.
For the skew functions

(4.12)

Q_{\lambda/\mu}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n};q,t)=0

unless
0\leq\lambda^{\prime}_{i}-\mu^{\prime}_{i}\leq n
for each
i\geq 1
.
See
[
18
, VII, (7.15)]
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\lambda
and
\mu
be partitions such that
\mu\subset\lambda
.
Then for
l
a positive integer

(4.13)

P_{\lambda/\mu}\Big(\Big[\frac{1-q}{t-1}\big(m_{1}+m_{2}+\cdots+m_{l}\big)\Big];q,t\Big)=(-1)^{|\lambda|-|\mu|}Q_{\lambda^{\prime}/\mu^{\prime}}\Big(\big[m_{1}+m_{2}+\cdots+m_{l}\big];t,q\Big),

where the

Proof.

We prove by straightforward calculation.

\displaystyle\quad=P_{\lambda/\mu}\Big(\Big[\big(\epsilon\frac{1-q}{t-1}\big)\big(m_{1}+m_{2}+\cdots+m_{l}\big)\Big];q,t\Big)\quad

\displaystyle\quad=(-1)^{|\lambda|-|\mu|}P_{\lambda/\mu}\Big(\Big[\frac{1-q}{t-1}\big(m_{1}+m_{2}+\cdots+m_{l}\big)\Big];q,t\Big)\quad

\displaystyle\text{by \eqref{e-Mac3}}.

By Lemma

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
\lambda
be a partition and

For any nonzero part

Proof.

By (

(4.15)

P_{\lambda}\Big(\Big[\frac{1-q}{t-1}\big(m_{1}+m_{2}+\cdots+m_{\lambda_{i}-1}\big)+n_{1}+\cdots+n_{i-1}\Big];q,t\Big)\\
=\sum_{\mu\subset\lambda}P_{\lambda/\mu}\Big(\Big[\frac{1-q}{t-1}\big(m_{1}+m_{2}+\cdots+m_{\lambda_{i}-1}\big)\Big];q,t\Big)P_{\mu}\big([n_{1}+\cdots+n_{i-1}];q,t\big).

Define the modified complete symmetric function
g_{n}(X;q,t)
by its generating function

\prod_{i\geq 1}\frac{(tx_{i}y;q)_{\infty}}{(x_{i}y;q)_{\infty}}=\sum_{n\geq 0}g_{n}(X;q,t)y^{n},

Note that

For

See

The next result is called the Pieri formula for Macdonald polynomials

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
\mu
be a partition. For

(4.18)

P_{\mu}g_{r}=\sum_{\lambda}\varphi_{\lambda/\mu}P_{\lambda},

where the sum is over partitions

The explicit expression for

[

We do not need the explicit form of

in this paper.

The symmetric functions

form a basis of

\Lambda_{\mathbb{F}}

[
18
, VI, (2.19)]
.
Then the Macdonald polynomials can be written as a linear combination of
modified complete symmetric functions.
The formula is referred as the generalized Jacobi-Trudi expansions for Macdonald polynomials.
We state that in the next theorem.

Theorem 4.4
.

[
15
]

Let
\lambda
and
\mu
be partitions with length at most
n
. Then

(4.19)

P_{\lambda}=\sum_{\mu\geq\lambda}c_{\mu}g_{\mu}.

Note that the explicit expression for

is quite complicated, we refer the reader to

[
15
, Theorem 3]
. When

, (

) reduces to the Jacobi-Trudi identity

where

is the Schur function.

We can express the skew Macdonald polynomials as (usual) Madonald polynomials.

Proposition 4.5
.

For partitions
\lambda
and
\mu
such that
\mu\subset\lambda
and
\ell(\mu)<\ell(\lambda)
,
let

(4.20)

P_{\lambda/\mu}=\sum_{\nu}r_{\mu\nu}^{\lambda}\cdot P_{\nu},

where
\nu
is over all partitions such that
|\nu|=|\lambda|-|\mu|
.
Then
r_{\mu\nu}^{\lambda}=0
for
\nu_{1}<\lambda_{\ell(\mu)+1}
, or equivalently,

\nu_{1}\geq\lambda_{\ell(\mu)+1}
.

Proof.

Substituting (
4.2
) into (

) yields

Q_{\lambda/\mu}=\sum_{\nu}f_{\mu\nu}^{\lambda}Q_{\nu}=\sum_{\nu}f_{\mu\nu}^{\lambda}b_{\nu}P_{\nu}.

Together with
Q_{\lambda/\mu}=b_{\lambda}b_{\mu}^{-1}P_{\lambda/\mu}
in (

), we have

P_{\lambda/\mu}=\sum_{\nu}f_{\mu\nu}^{\lambda}b_{\mu}b_{\lambda}^{-1}b_{\nu}P_{\nu}.

Since the
P_{\lambda}
form a basis of
\Lambda_{\mathbb{F}}

[
18
]
,
comparing this with (
4.20
), we have

r_{\mu\nu}^{\lambda}=b_{\mu}b_{\nu}b_{\lambda}^{-1}f_{\mu\nu}^{\lambda}.

Hence, to prove the proposition it suffices to prove that a family of
q,t
-Littlewood–Richardson coefficients are zeros. That is

(4.21)

f_{\mu\nu}^{\lambda}=\langle Q_{\lambda},P_{\mu}P_{\nu}\rangle=0

for
\nu_{1}<\lambda_{\ell(\mu)+1}
,
\mu\subset\lambda
and
\ell(\mu)<\ell(\lambda)
.

By (
4.19
), we can write

P_{\mu}=\sum_{\omega\geq\mu}c_{\omega}g_{\omega}.

Using this and by the linearity of the
q,t
-Hall scalar product, we have

(4.22)

f_{\mu\nu}^{\lambda}=\langle Q_{\lambda},P_{\mu}P_{\nu}\rangle=\sum_{\omega\geq\mu}c_{\omega}\langle Q_{\lambda},P_{\nu}g_{\omega}\rangle.

We can get
\ell(\omega)\leq\ell(\mu)
by
\omega\geq\mu
.
Consequently,
\ell(\omega)<\ell(\lambda)
.
Then we prove (
4.21
) by showing that each term in the sum of (

)

(4.23)

\langle Q_{\lambda},P_{\nu}g_{\omega}\rangle=0

for
\nu_{1}<\lambda_{\ell(\mu)+1}
,
\ell(\omega)<\ell(\lambda)
.
Repeatedly using of the Pieri formulas for Macdonald polynomials (Proposition
4.3
) for

\ell(\omega)
times, we have

P_{\nu}g_{\omega}=P_{\nu}\cdot g_{\omega_{1}}\cdot g_{\omega_{2}}\cdots=\sum_{\lambda^{*}}\overline{c}_{\lambda^{*}}P_{\lambda^{*}},

where
\overline{c}_{\lambda^{*}}
is the coefficient for

.
Since
\nu_{1}<\lambda_{\ell(\mu)+1}
and
\ell(\omega)<\ell(\lambda)
,
none of the partitions

can be
\lambda
.
(It needs at least
(\ell(\mu)+1)
-th use of the Pieri formula.)
It follows that

\langle Q_{\lambda},P_{\nu}g_{\omega}\rangle=\sum_{\lambda^{*}}\overline{c}_{\lambda^{*}}\langle Q_{\lambda},P_{\lambda^{*}}\rangle=0.\qed

The next proposition is concerned about the degrees of the Macdonald polynomials.

Proposition 4.6
.

For positive integers
s
and
n
, let
\lambda
be a partition and
u:=(u_{1},u_{2},\dots,u_{s})
be a vector of integers such that

. For a polynomial
f(x)=f(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
and

, we define a substitution
T_{u}\big(f(x)\big)
by replacing

for

in

.
Then the degree in

of

is at most
\lambda_{1}+\cdots+\lambda_{s}
.

Proof.

By (
4.1
), the Macdonald polynomial

is a linear combination of those monomial symmetric functions

for

.
Hence, to prove the proposition, it suffices to prove that for

,
the degree in

of

is no more than

.

∎

5.
Polynomiality and rationality

The aim of this section is to show that
B_{n,n_{0}}(a,b,c,l,\mu)
and

C_{n,n_{0}}(a,b,c,l,m)
satisfy the following two properties: i) they are polynomials in
q^{a}
;
ii) if the expressions for them hold for sufficiently many integers
c
,
then they hold for all positive integers
c
.

We begin with the next polynomiality lemma.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
L(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
be an arbitrary Laurent polynomial independent of
a
and
x_{0}
.
Then, for fixed nonnegative integers
b
and
t
such that
t\leq nb
,

(5.1)

\CT_{x}x_{0}^{t}L(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\prod_{i=1}^{n}(x_{0}/x_{i})_{a}(qx_{i}/x_{0})_{b}

is a polynomial in
q^{a}
of degree at most
nb-t
.
Moreover, if
t>nb
, then the constant term (
5.1
) vanishes.

Proof.

The proof is almost the same as that of
[
26
, Lemma 2.2]
.
∎

By Lemma
5.1
, it is not hard to conclude that
B_{n,n_{0}}(a,b,c,l,\mu)
and
C_{n,n_{0}}(a,b,c,l,m)

are polynomials in
q^{a}
.

Corollary 5.2
.

The constant terms
B_{n,n_{0}}(a,b,c,l,\mu)
and

C_{n,n_{0}}(a,b,c,l,m)
are polynomials in
q^{a}
of degree at most
nb+l+|\mu|
and
nb+l+m
respectively, assuming all the parameters but
a
are fixed.

Proof.

We can write
C_{n,n_{0}}(a,b,c,l,m)
as

(5.2)

\CT_{x}x_{0}^{-l}\prod_{i=n-m+1}^{n}(1-q^{b+1}x_{i}/x_{0})\prod_{i=1}^{n}(x_{0}/x_{i})_{a}(qx_{i}/x_{0})_{b}L(x_{1},\dots,x_{n}),

where

(5.3)

L(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=h_{l}\Big[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1-q^{c-\chi(i\leq n_{0})}}{1-q}x_{i}\Big]\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq n}(x_{i}/x_{j})_{c-\chi(i\leq n_{0})}(qx_{j}/x_{i})_{c-\chi(i\leq n_{0})}.

Expanding the first product in (
5.2
), we can see that
C_{n,n_{0}}(a,b,c,l,m)
is a polynomial in
q^{a}
of degree at most
nb+l+m
by Lemma
5.1
.

By (
4.9
) and (
3.2
), we can write
B_{n,n_{0}}(a,b,c,l,\mu)
as

(5.4)

\sum_{\nu\subset\mu}P_{\nu}\big(\big[\frac{q^{c-b-1}-q^{a}}{1-q^{c}}\big];q,q^{c}\big)\CT_{x}x_{0}^{-l-|\mu|+|\nu|}P_{\mu/\nu}\Big(\Big[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1-q^{c-\chi(i\leq n_{0})}}{1-q^{c}}x_{i}\Big];q,q^{c}\Big)\\
\times\prod_{i=1}^{n}(x_{0}/x_{i})_{a}(qx_{i}/x_{0})_{b}L(x_{1},\dots,x_{n}),

where
L(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
is given by (
5.3
).
We write

\displaystyle P_{\nu}\big(\big[\frac{q^{c-b-1}-q^{a}}{1-q^{c}}\big];q,q^{c}\big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda\geq\nu}c_{\lambda}g_{\lambda}\Big(\Big[\frac{q^{c-b-1}-q^{a}}{1-q^{c}}\Big];q,q^{c}\Big)\quad

by (
4.19
)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda\geq\nu}c_{\lambda}h_{\lambda}\Big[\frac{q^{c-b-1}-q^{a}}{1-q}\Big]\quad

by (
4.16
)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda\geq\nu}c_{\lambda}q^{(c-b-1)|\lambda|}h_{\lambda}\Big[\frac{1-q^{a+b+1-c}}{1-q}\Big]\quad

by (

)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda\geq\nu}c_{\lambda}q^{(c-b-1)|\lambda|}\prod_{i\geq 1}\frac{(q^{a+b+1-c})_{\lambda_{i}}}{(q)_{\lambda_{i}}}.

The right-most equality holds by
h_{r}[(1-z)/(1-q)]=(z)_{r}/(q)_{r}
, see e.g.,
[
18
, page 27]
.
Then one can see that
P_{\nu}\big[\frac{q^{c-b-1}-q^{a}}{1-q^{c}}\big]

is a polynomial in
q^{a}
of degree at most

. Together with the fact that
the constant term in (

) is a polynomial in
q^{a}
of degree at most

by Lemma

, we conclude that
B_{n,n_{0}}(a,b,c,l,\mu)
is a polynomial in
q^{a}
of degree
at most
nb+l+|\mu|
.
∎

The following rationality result, which is implicitly due to
Stembridge
[
22
]
, as can be seen from the proof. One can also see this result in

[
26
, Proposition 3.1]
and
[
13
, Lemma 7.5]
.
The

case of this result
is the equal parameter case of
[
8
, Proposition 2.4]
.

Proposition 5.3
.

Let
n
be a positive integer and
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
such that

|\alpha|=0
. For

a nonnegative integer

(5.5)

\displaystyle\CT_{x}x_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots x_{n}^{\alpha_{n}}\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\Big(\frac{x_{i}}{x_{j}}\Big)_{c}\Big(\frac{x_{j}}{x_{i}}q\Big)_{c}=\frac{(q)_{nc}}{(q)_{c}^{n}}\cdot R_{n}(q^{c};q,\alpha),

where
R_{n}(q^{c};q,\alpha)
is a rational function in
q^{c}
and
q
.

A straightforward consequence of Proposition
5.3
is the next result.

Corollary 5.4
.

Let
c,d
be nonnegative integers,
n
be a positive integer, and
H
be a homogeneous Laurent polynomial in
x_{0},x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
of degree 0. If an expression for

\CT_{x}H\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\Big(\frac{x_{i}}{x_{j}}\Big)_{c}\Big(\frac{x_{j}}{x_{i}}q\Big)_{c}

holds for

, then it holds for all nonnegative integers
c
.
Here

is independent of

, but it may depend on

.

Proof.

By Proposition
5.3
, it exists a rational function
R_{n}(q^{c};q,H)
such that

\CT_{x}H\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\Big(\frac{x_{i}}{x_{j}}\Big)_{c}\Big(\frac{x_{j}}{x_{i}}q\Big)_{c}=\CT_{x}H|_{x_{0}=1}\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\Big(\frac{x_{i}}{x_{j}}\Big)_{c}\Big(\frac{x_{j}}{x_{i}}q\Big)_{c}=\frac{(q)_{nc}}{(q)_{c}^{n}}\cdot R_{n}(q^{c};q,H).

Here the first equality holds by the homogeneity of

.
Then

(5.6)

\frac{(q)_{c}^{n}}{(q)_{nc}}\CT_{x}H\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\Big(\frac{x_{i}}{x_{j}}\Big)_{c}\Big(\frac{x_{j}}{x_{i}}q\Big)_{c}=R_{n}(q^{c};q,H).

It follows that if (

) holds for

for some nonnegative integer

,
then it holds for all nonnegative integers

since the both sides are rational functions in
q^{c}
.
∎

6.
Preliminaries for determination of the roots

In this section, we present several essential results to determine the roots
of
B_{n,n_{0}}(a,b,c,l,\mu)
and

6.1.
The cardinality of an alphabet

The main observation in this subsection is Proposition
6.7
, which
concerns the cardinality of an alphabet in
B_{n,n_{0}}(a,b,c,l,\mu)

under certain substitutions.

For a positive integer
s
, let
\mathfrak{S}_{s}
be the set of all the permutations of
\{1,2,\dots,s\}
.
For
w\in\mathfrak{S}_{s}
and
r
an integer such that
0\leq r<s
,
define

(6.1)

N_{w,r}:=\sum_{j=1}^{s}u_{j}(w)+\chi(w(j)>r)\chi(w(j-1)>r),

where
w(0):=0
and

(6.2)

u_{j}(w)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if $w(j-1)<w(j)$,}\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

For example, if
s=7
,
r=3
and
w=(3147526)
, then
N_{w,r}=6
.
Let

(6.3)

w(0)\mathop{\longrightarrow}^{e_{1}}w(1)\mathop{\longrightarrow}^{e_{2}}\cdots\mathop{\longrightarrow}^{e_{s-1}}w(s-1)\mathop{\longrightarrow}^{e_{s}}w(s)

be a weighted directed path on
w\in\mathfrak{S}_{s}
,
where

(6.4)

e_{j}:=u_{j}(w)+\chi(w(j)>r)\chi(w(j-1)>r).

Then we can view
N_{w,r}
as the sum of the weights on this directed path.
Note that
e_{j}
is determined by
w(j),w(j-1)
and the integer
r
,
and
e_{1}=1
.
For
r\in\{0,1,\dots,s-1\}
, denote
I_{1}:=\{1,2,\dots,r\}
and
I_{2}:=\{r+1,r+2,\dots,s\}
.
Let
\mathfrak{S}_{s,r}
be the subset of
\mathfrak{S}_{s}
that includes all the permutations
that all the elements of
I_{1}
and
I_{2}
are in a decreasing order respectively.
We define
\varepsilon_{1}:\mathfrak{S}_{s}\mapsto\mathfrak{S}_{s,r}

to be the map of permuting the elements of
I_{1}
and
I_{2}
in
w\in\mathfrak{S}_{s}
respectively
such that all the elements of
I_{1}
and
I_{2}
in
w
are decreasing.
For example, if
s=7
,
r=3
and
w=(3147526)
, then
I_{1}=\{1,2,3\},I_{2}=\{4,5,6,7\}
,

\varepsilon_{1}\circ w=(3276514)
.
Having the above notation, we obtain the following property for
the number
N_{w,r}
.

Lemma 6.1
.

For a positive integer
s
, let
w\in\mathfrak{S}_{s}
and
r
be an integer such that
0\leq r<s
.
Let
\varepsilon_{1}
be defined as above. Then

(6.5)

N_{w,r}\geq N_{\varepsilon_{1}\circ w,r}.

Furthermore, if there is an

such that
w(i-1),w(i)\in I_{1}
(resp.
I_{2}
) and
w(i-1)<w(i)
,
then

(6.6)

N_{w,r}>N_{\varepsilon_{1}\circ w,r}.

Proof.

Let
w_{1}=\varepsilon_{1}\circ w
.
We will show that for

(6.7a)

\chi(w(j)>r)=\chi(w_{1}(j)>r),

and

(6.7b)

u_{j}(w)\geq u_{j}(w_{1}).

Then it is clear that
N_{w,r}\geq N_{\varepsilon_{1}\circ w,r}
from the definition of
N_{w,r}
in (

)
and the fact that

u_{1}(w)+\chi(1>r)\chi(0>r)=u_{1}(w_{1})+\chi(1>r)\chi(0>r)=1.

Since
\varepsilon_{1}
permutes the elements of
I_{1}
and
I_{2}
in
w
respectively,
for some

if
w(j)\in I_{1}
(resp.
I_{2}
) then
w_{1}(j)\in I_{1}
(resp.
I_{2}
).
Thus (
6.7a
) holds.

Since
u_{j}(w)
is determined by
w(j)
,
w(j-1)
and

, we prove (

) by discussing the following four cases:

(1)

;

(2)

w(j-1)\in I_{2},w(j)\in I_{1}
;

(3)

w(j-1)\in I_{1},w(j)\in I_{1}
;

(4)

w(j-1)\in I_{2},w(j)\in I_{2}
.

If
w(j-1)\in I_{1}
and
w(j)\in I_{2}
for some

,
then by the definition of
\varepsilon_{1}
,
w_{1}(j-1)\in I_{1}
and
w_{1}(j)\in I_{2}
.
It follows that
w(j-1)<w(j)
and
w_{1}(j-1)<w_{1}(j)
. Then
u_{j}(w)=u_{j}(w_{1})=1
.
Similarly, if
w(j-1)\in I_{2}
and
w(j)\in I_{1}
for some
j
,
then
u_{j}(w)=u_{j}(w_{1})=0
.
If
w(j-1),w(j)\in I_{1}
for some

,
then
w_{1}(j-1),w_{1}(j)\in I_{1}
.
By the definition of
\varepsilon_{1}
,
all the elements of

in

Thus

.
Then by the definition of
u_{j}
in (

.
It follows that

(6.8)

u_{j}(w)\geq u_{j}(w_{1}).

For the case

).
In conclusion, (

) holds for all the four cases.
Then
N_{w,r}\geq N_{\varepsilon_{1}\circ w,r}
by (

).

If for some

such that

(or

) and

,
then
u_{i}(w)>u_{i}(w_{1})
. Thus, in this case the equality of (
6.5
) can not hold
and we obtain (
6.6
).
∎

We define another map
\varepsilon_{2}:\mathfrak{S}_{s,r}\mapsto\mathfrak{S}_{s,r}
.
If
w=(s,s-1,\dots,1)
, then
\varepsilon_{2}\circ w=w
.
If
w\in\mathfrak{S}_{s,r}
but
w\neq(s,s-1,\dots,1)
, then
it exists an integer
1\leq j<s
such that
w(i)=s-i+1
for
i=1,\dots,j-1
but
w(j)\neq s-j+1
.
The map
\varepsilon_{2}
acts on
w
by putting
w(t)=s-j+1
for some
t>j
between
w(j-1)
and
w(j)
in
w
, and other entries of
w
keep their original order in
w
.
That is

(6.9)

\varepsilon_{2}\circ w=\big(w(1),\dots,w(j-1),w(t),w(j),\dots,w(t-1),w(t+1),\dots,w(s)\big).

In particular, if
j=1
then
w(t)=s
and
\varepsilon_{2}\circ w=\big(s,w(1),\dots,w(t-1),w(t+1),\dots,w(s)\big)
.
Note that if
w\neq(s,s-1,\dots,1)
then
w(t)>r
since
w\in\mathfrak{S}_{s,r}
.
For example, if
s=7
,
r=3
and
w=(3276514)
, then
w_{1}:=\varepsilon_{2}\circ(3276514)=(7326514)
.
In this case,
N_{w,r}=5
and
N_{w_{1},r}=4
.
We can decrease the number
N_{w,r}
further by acting
\varepsilon_{2}
on
w
.
This is the next lemma.

Lemma 6.2
.

For a positive integer
s
and
r\in\{0,1,\dots,s-1\}
, let
w\in\mathfrak{S}_{s,r}
,
N_{w,r}
be defined
in (
6.1
) and
\varepsilon_{2}
be defined as above.
Then

(6.10)

N_{w,r}\geq N_{\varepsilon_{2}\circ w,r}.

In particular, if the equality of (
6.10
) holds, then
w(1)=s
.

Proof.

If
w=(s,s-1,\dots,1)
then
\varepsilon_{2}\circ w=w
and the lemma holds. Hence, we assume

w\neq(s,s-1,\dots,1)
in the following of the proof.
By the definition of
\varepsilon_{2}
,
N_{w,r}
and
N_{\varepsilon_{2}\circ w,r}
only differ in two parts (view them as weighted directed paths as in (
6.3
)). That is

\displaystyle\big(w(j-1)\mathop{\longrightarrow}^{e_{j}}w(j)\big)

\displaystyle\mapsto\big(w(j-1)\mathop{\longrightarrow}^{e^{\prime}_{j}}w(t)\mathop{\longrightarrow}^{e^{\prime}_{j+1}}w(j)\big),

\displaystyle\big(w(t-1)\mathop{\longrightarrow}^{e_{t}}w(t)\mathop{\longrightarrow}^{e_{t+1}}w(t+1)\big)

\displaystyle\mapsto\big(w(t-1)\mathop{\longrightarrow}^{e^{\prime}_{t+1}}w(t+1)\big),

where
j
is the integer such that

w(i)=s-i+1
for
i=1,\dots,j-1
but
w(j)\neq s-j+1
, and
w(t)=s-j+1
for some
t>j
.
Recall that
N_{w,r}
equals the sum of the weights on its corresponding directed path.
Thus, to prove
N_{w,r}\geq N_{\varepsilon_{2}\circ w,r}
it is sufficient to prove

e_{j}+e_{t}+e_{t+1}\geq e^{\prime}_{j}+e^{\prime}_{j+1}+e^{\prime}_{t+1}
.
We will prove this by showing that

(6.11a)

e_{j}+1\geq e^{\prime}_{j}+e^{\prime}_{j+1},

and

(6.11b)

e_{t}+e_{t+1}=e^{\prime}_{t+1}+1.

If
j=1
then
e_{j}=e^{\prime}_{j}=1
, and
e^{\prime}_{j+1}=1
for
w(j)\in I_{2}
and
e^{\prime}_{j+1}=0
for
w(j)\in I_{1}
. It follows that (
6.11a
) holds for
j=1
.
By the definition of
\varepsilon_{2}
, for
j\geq 2
we have
w(j-1),w(t)\in I_{2}
and
w(j-1)=w(t)+1=s-j+2
.
We then prove (
6.11a
) for
j\geq 2
by discussing the two cases:
w(j)\in I_{1}
and
w(j)\in I_{2}
.
The case
w(j)\in I_{2}
can not occur. Otherwise, since
w(j-1)\in I_{2}
and
w\in\mathfrak{S}_{s,r}
,
all the elements of
I_{2}
in
w
are in a decreasing order,
w(j)=w(j-1)-1=s-j+1
. This contradicts the assumption
w(j)\neq s-j+1
in (
6.9
).
If
w(j)\in I_{1}
and
j\geq 2
then
e_{j}=e^{\prime}_{j+1}=0
. Together with the fact
e^{\prime}_{j}=1
by
w(j-1)>w(t)
and
w(j-1),w(t)\in I_{2}
, we have
e_{j}+1=e^{\prime}_{j}+e^{\prime}_{j+1}=1
.
Therefore (
6.11a
) holds for
j=1,2,\dots,s-1
.

By the definition of
\varepsilon_{2}
in (
6.9
) again, we have
w(t)\in I_{2},w(t)>w(t-1)
and
w(t)>w(t+1)
. Since
w\in\mathfrak{S}_{s,r}
and all the elements of
I_{2}
in
w
are in a decreasing order,
w(t-1)\in I_{1}
.
We then prove (
6.11b
) by discussing the following two cases:

(1)

w(t-1)\in I_{1},w(t+1)\in I_{2}
;

(2)

w(t-1)\in I_{1},w(t+1)\in I_{1}
.

If
w(t-1)\in I_{1},w(t+1)\in I_{2}
then
e_{t}=e_{t+1}=e^{\prime}_{t+1}=1
and
e_{t}+e_{t+1}=e^{\prime}_{t+1}+1=2
.
If
w(t-1),w(t+1)\in I_{1}
then
e_{t}=1,e_{t+1}=0
. Since all the elements of
I_{1}
in
w
are in a decreasing order,
e^{\prime}_{t+1}=0
. Then
e_{t}+e_{t+1}=e^{\prime}_{t+1}+1=1
.
Therefore (
6.11b
) holds.

We prove the particular case by showing that
N_{w,r}>N_{\varepsilon_{2}\circ w,r}
for
w(1)\neq s
.
If
w(1)\neq s
then
j=1
and
w(1)\in I_{1}
.
Correspondingly,
e_{1}=e^{\prime}_{1}=1
and
e^{\prime}_{2}=0
.
Hence,
e_{1}+1=2>e^{\prime}_{1}+e^{\prime}_{2}=1
. Together with (
6.11b
) and the above discussion,

N_{w,r}>N_{\varepsilon_{2}\circ w,r}
.
∎

By Lemma
6.1
and Lemma
6.2
, we obtain the next proposition.

Proposition 6.3
.

For a positive integer
s
, let
w\in\mathfrak{S}_{s}
,
w(0):=0
and
d_{1},\dots,d_{s}
be nonnegative integers such that
d_{j}\geq 1
if
w(j)>w(j-1)
for
j=1,\dots,s
.
For an integer
r
such that
0\leq r\leq s
,

(6.12a)

\sum_{j=1}^{s}\big(d_{j}+\chi(w(j)>r)\chi(w(j-1)>r)\big)\geq s-r,

and for
i\in\{1,2,\dots,s\}
,

(6.12b)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}^{s}\big(d_{j}+\chi(w(j)>r)\chi(w(j-1)>r)\big)\geq s-r-1.

In particular, if the equality of (
6.12a
) holds, then
w(1)>r
and
d_{1}=1
.

Proof.

By the definition of
u_{j}(w)
in (
6.2
), we have
d_{j}\geq u_{j}(w)
.
It follows that

(6.13a)

\sum_{j=1}^{s}\big(d_{j}+\chi(w(j)>r)\chi(w(j-1)>r)\big)\geq\sum_{j=1}^{s}\big(u_{j}(w)+\chi(w(j)>r)\chi(w(j-1)>r)\big)=N_{w,r},

and

(6.13b)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}^{s}\big(d_{j}+\chi(w(j)>r)\chi(w(j-1)>r)\big)\geq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}^{s}u_{j}(w)+\chi(w(j)>r)\chi(w(j-1)>r).

We then prove (
6.12a
) by showing that
N_{w,r}\geq s-r
,
and prove (
6.12b
) by showing that the right-hand side of (
6.13b
)
is no less than
s-r-1
.

For any permutation
w\in\mathfrak{S}_{s}
, let
\varepsilon_{1}
act on
w
and then let

\varepsilon_{2}
act on
\varepsilon_{1}\circ w
recursively until the resulting permutation
is
w_{1}:=(s,s-1,\dots,1)
.
By the definition of
\varepsilon_{1}
and
\varepsilon_{2}
, these operations are valid.
By Lemma
6.1
and Lemma
6.2
,

(6.14)

N_{w,r}\geq N_{\varepsilon_{1}\circ w,r}\geq N_{\varepsilon_{2}\circ(\varepsilon_{1}\circ w),r}\geq N_{\varepsilon_{2}\circ\cdots\circ\varepsilon_{2}\circ(\varepsilon_{1}\circ w),r}=N_{w_{1},r}=s-r.

Together with (
6.13a
) we obtain (
6.12a
).

For a fixed
i\in\{1,2,\dots,s\}
,
it is clear that

u_{i}(w)+\chi(w(i)>r)\chi(w(i-1)>r)\in\{0,1,2\}
.
If
u_{i}(w)+\chi(w(i)>r)\chi(w(i-1)>r)\leq 1
,
then

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}^{s}u_{j}(w)+\chi(w(j)>r)\chi(w(j-1)>r)\\
=N_{w,r}-u_{i}(w)-\chi(w(i)>r)\chi(w(i-1)>r)\geq s-r-1

using
N_{w,r}\geq s-r
.
Together with (
6.13b
) gives (
6.12b
).
In particular, (
6.12b
) holds for
i=1
since in this case

u_{1}(w)+\chi(w(1)>r)\chi(w(0)>r)=1
.
If
u_{i}(w)+\chi(w(i)>r)\chi(w(i-1)>r)=2
for some
i\in\{2,\dots,s\}
,
then
w(i)>w(i-1)>r
, i.e.,
w(i-1),w(i)\in I_{2}
and
w(i-1)<w(i)
.
Let
\varepsilon_{1}
act on
w
and then let

\varepsilon_{2}
act on
\varepsilon_{1}\circ w
recursively again until the resulting permutation
is
w_{1}:=(s,s-1,\dots,1)
. Using (
6.6
) we have

N_{w,r}>N_{w_{1},r}=s-r.

It follows that

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}^{s}u_{j}(w)+\chi(w(j)>r)\chi(w(j-1)>r)\\
>N_{w_{1},r}-u_{i}(w)-\chi(w(i)>r)\chi(w(i-1)>r)=s-r-2.

Hence, in all the cases the right-hand side of (
6.13b
) is no less than
s-r-1
and we obtain (
6.12b
).

Let

S:=\sum_{j=1}^{s}\big(d_{j}+\chi(w(j)>r)\chi(w(j-1)>r)\big).

Finally, we prove that if
S=s-r
then
w(1)>r
and
d_{1}=1
.
By the argument in the above, we know that
S\geq N_{w,r}\geq s-r
.
If
S=s-r
then it forces
N_{w,r}=s-r
. It follows that
d_{j}=u_{j}(w)
for
j=1,\dots,s
.
In particular,
d_{1}=u_{1}(w)=1
.
We then prove
w(1)>r
by contradiction.
If
w(1)\leq r\neq s
, then

N_{w,r}\geq N_{\varepsilon_{1}\circ w,r}>N_{\varepsilon_{2}\circ(\varepsilon_{1}\circ w),r}\geq N_{w_{1},r}=s-r.

Here
N_{\varepsilon_{1}\circ w,r}\neq N_{\varepsilon_{2}\circ(\varepsilon_{1}\circ w),r}

by Lemma
6.2
.
Consequently,

S\geq N_{w,r}>s-r
. This contradicts the fact
S=s-r
.
Hence,
w(1)>r
for
S=s-r
.
∎

Now we can prove the key lemma in this paper, which plays an important role in proving the vanishing properties
of
B_{n,n_{0}}(a,b,c,l,\mu)
and
C_{n,n_{0}}(a,b,c,l,m)
.

Lemma 6.4
.

For
s,c
positive integers, let
b,t
and
k_{1},\dots,k_{s}
be nonnegative integers such that

1\leq k_{i}\leq(s-1)(c-1)+b+t
for
1\leq i\leq s
.
Then for an integer
r
such that
0\leq r\leq s
, at least one of the following holds:

(1)

1\leq k_{i}\leq b
for some
i
with
1\leq i\leq s
;

(2)

-c+1\leq k_{i}-k_{j}\leq c-2
for some
(i,j)
such that
1\leq i<j\leq s
and
i\leq r
;

(3)

-c\leq k_{i}-k_{j}\leq c-1
for some
(i,j)
such that
r<i<j\leq s
;

(4)

there exists a permutation
w\in\mathfrak{S}_{s}
and nonnegative integers
d_{1},\dots,d_{s}

such that

(6.15a)

k_{w(1)}=b+d_{1},

and

(6.15b)

k_{w(j)}-k_{w(j-1)}=c-1+\chi(w(j)>r)\chi(w(j-1)>r)+d_{j}\quad\text{for $2\leq j\leq s$.}

Here the
d_{j}
satisfy

(6.16)

s-r\leq\sum_{j=1}^{s}\big(d_{j}+\chi(w(j)>r)\chi(w(j-1)>r)\big)\leq t,

w(0):=0
, and
d_{j}>0
if
w(j-1)<w(j)
for
1\leq j\leq s
.
In particular, if
t=s-r
and
r<s
, then

(6.17)

k_{i}=b+1\quad\text{for some $i>r$}.

Proof.

We prove the lemma by showing that if (1)–(3) fail then (4) must hold.

Assume that (1)–(3) are all false.
Then we construct a weighted tournament
T
on a complete graph
on
s
vertices, labelled
1,\dots,s
, as follows.
For the edge
(i,j)
with
1\leq i<j\leq s
and
i\leq r
, we draw an arrow from
j
to
i
and attach a weight
c-1
if
k_{i}-k_{j}\geq c-1
.
If, on the other hand,
k_{i}-k_{j}\leq-c
then we draw an arrow
from
i
to
j
and attach the weight
c
.
Similarly, for the edge
(i,j)
with
1\leq i<j\leq s
and
i>r
,
we draw an arrow from
j
to
i
and attach a weight
c
if
k_{i}-k_{j}\geq c
,
and we draw an arrow from
i
to
j
and attach the weight
c+1

if
k_{i}-k_{j}\leq-c-1
.
Note that the weight of each edge of a tournament is nonnegative since
c
is a positive integer.

We call a directed edge from
i
to
j
ascending if
i<j
.
It is immediate from our construction that
(i) the weight of the edge
i\to j
is less than or equal
k_{j}-k_{i}
, and
(ii) the weight of an ascending edge is positive.

We will use (i) and (ii) to show that any of the above-constructed
tournaments is acyclic and hence transitive.
As consequence of (i), the weight of a directed path from
i
to
j
in
T
,
defined as the sum of the weights of its edges, is at most
k_{j}-k_{i}
.
Proceeding by contradiction, assume that
T
contains a cycle
C
.
By the above, the weight of
C
must be non-positive, and hence
0
.
Since
C
must have at least one ascending edge, which by (ii) has positive
weight, the weight of
C
is positive, a contradiction.

Since each
T
is transitive, there is exactly one directed Hamilton path
P
in
T
,
corresponding to a total order of the vertices.
Assume
P
is given by

P=w(1)\rightarrow w(2)\rightarrow\cdots\rightarrow w(s-1)\rightarrow w(s),

where we have suppressed the edge weights.
Then

k_{w(s)}-k_{w(1)}\geq(s-1)(c-1)+\sum_{j=2}^{s}\chi(w(j)>r)\chi(w(j-1)>r),

and thus

\displaystyle k_{w(s)}

\displaystyle\geq k_{w(1)}+(s-1)(c-1)+\sum_{j=2}^{s}\chi(w(j)>r)\chi(w(j-1)>r)

(6.18)

\displaystyle\geq b+1+(s-1)(c-1)+\sum_{j=2}^{s}\chi(w(j)>r)\chi(w(j-1)>r).

Together with the assumption that
k_{w(s)}\leq(s-1)(c-1)+b+t

this implies that
P
has at most
t-1
ascending edges.
Let
d_{1},\dots,d_{s}
be nonnegative integers such that (
6.15
) holds.
Since (1) does not hold and by (
6.15a
),
k_{w(1)}=b+d_{1}\geq b+1
, so that
d_{1}\geq 1
.
For
2\leq j\leq s
, if
w(j-1)\to w(j)
is an ascending edge, then
d_{j}
is a positive integer.
That is, for
2\leq j\leq s
if
w(j-1)<w(j)
then
d_{j}\geq 1
.
Set
k_{0}:=0
.
Since

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{s}(k_{w(j)}-k_{w(j-1)})=k_{w(s)}

\displaystyle=b+(s-1)(c-1)+\sum_{j=1}^{s}\big(d_{j}+\chi(w(j)>r)\chi(w(j-1)>r)\big)

\displaystyle\leq b+(s-1)(c-1)+t,

we have

\sum_{j=1}^{s}\big(d_{j}+\chi(w(j)>r)\chi(w(j-1)>r)\big)\leq t.

By Proposition
6.3
,

\sum_{j=1}^{s}\big(d_{j}+\chi(w(j)>r)\chi(w(j-1)>r)\big)\geq s-r.

This completes the proof of the assertion that (4) must hold if (1)–(3) fail.

If
t=s-r
and
r<s
, then

\sum_{j=1}^{s}\big(d_{j}+\chi(w(j)>r)\chi(w(j-1)>r)\big)=s-r.

By Proposition
6.3
we have
w(1)>r
,
d_{1}=1
and
k_{w(1)}=b+1
.
That is the assertion (
6.17
).
∎

For
r=s
and
t=1
, we have the next corollary of Lemma
6.4
.

Corollary 6.5
.

For
s,c
positive integers, let
b
and

be nonnegative integers such that

for

.
Then, at least one of the following holds:

for some

(2)

.

with minor modification.

By Lemma
6.4
, we obtain another corollary.

Corollary 6.6
.

For positive integers
s,c
, let

and

be nonnegative integers such that

.

or

.
Then, for an integer

, at least one of the following four cases holds:

(i)

1\leq k_{i}\leq b
for some
i
with
1\leq i\leq s
;

(ii)

-c+1\leq k_{i}-k_{j}\leq c-2
for some
(i,j)
such that
1\leq i<j\leq s
and
i\leq r
;

(iii)

-c\leq k_{i}-k_{j}\leq c-1
for some
(i,j)
such that

;

(iv)

if
m>0
then
s>n-m
and

for some

.

Note that only the first three cases can occur for

.

Proof.

The cases (i)–(iii) of this proposition are exactly the same as (1)–(3) of Lemma
6.4
respectively.
Hence, we only need to show the next two claims:
(a) For the
m=0
case, take
t=\chi(s>n_{0}+1)(s-n_{0}-1)
in Lemma
6.4
, then the case (4) of Lemma
6.4
can not occur;
(b) For the
0\leq n-n_{0}\leq m\leq n
case, take
t=\chi(s>n_{0}+1)(s-n_{0}-1)+\chi(s>n-m)
in Lemma
6.4
, then (4) of Lemma
6.4
leads to (iv).

Carrying out the substitution
t=\chi(s>n_{0}+1)(s-n_{0}-1)+\chi(s>n-m)
in (
6.16
) gives

(6.19)

s-r\leq\sum_{j=1}^{s}\big(d_{j}+\chi(w(j)>r)\chi(w(j-1)>r)\big)\leq\chi(s>n_{0}+1)(s-n_{0}-1)+\chi(s>n-m).

Here
w\in\mathfrak{S}_{s}
and
d_{1},\dots,d_{s}
are nonnegative integers such that

w(0):=0
, and
d_{j}>0
if
w(j-1)<w(j)
for
1\leq j\leq s
.
Notice that
d_{1}\geq 1
since

.
It follows that

(6.20)

\sum_{j=1}^{s}\big(d_{j}+\chi(w(j)>r)\chi(w(j-1)>r)\big)\geq 1.

We first prove the claim (a). If
s\leq n_{0}+1
then
t=\chi(s>n_{0}+1)(s-n_{0}-1)=0
.
Substituting this and

into (

) gives

\sum_{j=1}^{s}\big(d_{j}+\chi(w(j)>r)\chi(w(j-1)>r)\big)\leq 0.

This contradicts (
6.20
).
On the other hand, if
s>n_{0}+1
and

then

(6.22)

t=\chi(s>n_{0}+1)(s-n_{0}-1)=s-n_{0}-1.

By (

), we have

.
Together with

gives

.
But this contradicts

in (

).
Therefore, the claim (a) holds.

To prove the claim (b), we first show that it holds for the
r=s
case.
For

Lemma 9.1
.

Let
(u_{1},u_{2},\dots,u_{s})
be an integer sequence such that
1\leq u_{1}<u_{2}<\cdots<u_{s}\leq n
and set
U=\{u_{1},u_{2},\dots,u_{s}\}
. Denote
r=\big|\{u_{i}\>|\>u_{i}\leq n_{0},i=1,\dots,s\}\big|
.
For
0\leq r\leq n_{0}-2
and
1\leq d\leq s(c-1)+b+\chi(s>n_{0})(s-n_{0})
,

Q(d\>|\>u;k)
is either 0, or can be written as a Laurent polynomial of the form

(9.1)

x_{u_{s}}^{l}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\\
i\notin U\end{subarray}}^{n}\Big(p_{i}(x_{i}/x_{u_{s}})x_{i}^{d-s(c-1)-\chi(i>n_{0})(s-r)}\Big)\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq i<j\leq n\\
i,j\notin U\end{subarray}}\big(x_{i}/x_{j}\big)_{c-\chi(i\leq n_{0})}\big(qx_{j}/x_{i}\big)_{c-\chi(i\leq n_{0})},

where
p_{i}(z)
is a polynomial in
z
and

Recall that
Q(d\>|\>u;k)
can be written as (
6.33
).
We can further write it as

(9.2)

Q(d\>|\>u;k)=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq i<j\leq n\\
i,j\notin U\end{subarray}}\big(x_{i}/x_{j}\big)_{c-\chi(i\leq n_{0})}\big(qx_{j}/x_{i}\big)_{c-\chi(i\leq n_{0})}\times H\times C\times V^{\prime}\times L^{\prime},

where

H=E_{u,k}\big(x_{0}^{-\mathfrak{r}}\times M_{\mathfrak{r}}\big),

\displaystyle C=

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{s}(q^{k_{i}-d})^{-1}_{d-k_{i}}(q)^{-1}_{k_{i}-1}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq i<j\leq s\\
i\leq r\end{subarray}}(-1)^{c-1}q^{\binom{c}{2}+(c-1)(k_{i}-k_{j})}\prod_{r+1\leq i<j\leq s}(-1)^{c}q^{\binom{c+1}{2}+c(k_{i}-k_{j})}

\displaystyle\ \times\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\\
i\notin U\end{subarray}}^{n}(-1)^{d}q^{\binom{d+1}{2}-dk_{s}}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\\
i\notin U\end{subarray}}^{n}\prod_{j=1}^{r}(-1)^{c-1}q^{(c-1)\big(k_{s}-k_{j}+\chi(u_{j}>i)\big)+\binom{c-1}{2}}

\displaystyle\ \times\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\\
i\notin U\end{subarray}}^{n}\prod_{j=r+1}^{s}(-1)^{c-\chi(i\leq n_{0})}q^{(c-\chi(i\leq n_{0}))\big(k_{s}-k_{j}+\chi(u_{j}>i)\big)+\binom{c-\chi(i\leq n_{0})}{2}},

V^{\prime}=\prod_{i=1}^{s}(q^{1-k_{i}})_{b}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq i<j\leq s\\
i\leq r\end{subarray}}(q^{k_{j}-k_{i}-c+1})_{2c-2}\prod_{r+1\leq i<j\leq s}(q^{k_{j}-k_{i}-c})_{2c},

and

(9.4)

\displaystyle L^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\\
i\notin U\end{subarray}}^{n}\frac{(q^{1-k_{s}}x_{i}/x_{u_{s}})_{b}}{(x_{u_{s}}/x_{i})^{d}\big(q^{1-k_{s}}x_{i}/x_{u_{s}}\big)_{d}}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\\
i\notin U\end{subarray}}^{n}\prod_{j=1}^{r}(x_{u_{s}}/x_{i})^{c-1}\big(q^{k_{j}-k_{s}-c+2-\chi(u_{j}>i)}x_{i}/x_{u_{s}}\big)_{2c-2}

\displaystyle\ \ \times\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\\
i\notin U\end{subarray}}^{n}\prod_{j=r+1}^{s}(x_{u_{s}}/x_{i})^{c-\chi(i\leq n_{0})}\big(q^{k_{j}-k_{s}-c+1+\chi(i\leq n_{0})-\chi(u_{j}>i)}x_{i}/x_{u_{s}}\big)_{2c-2\chi(i\leq n_{0})}.

For certain conditions,
L^{\prime}
can be written as a Laurent polynomial. That is, the product
\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\\
i\notin U\end{subarray}}^{n}(q^{1-k_{s}}x_{i}/x_{u_{s}})_{d}
in the denominator of

is in fact a factor of the numerator of

.

Lemma 9.2
.

For
s\in\{1,2,\dots,n\}
, let
0\leq r\leq\min\{n_{0}-2,s\}
,
1\leq d\leq s(c-1)+b+\chi(s>n_{0})(s-n_{0})
.
For

the

, and
there exists a permutation

and nonnegative integers

such that

k_{w(1)}=b+t_{1},

and

(9.6)

k_{w(j)}-k_{w(j-1)}=c-1+\chi(w(j)>r)\chi(w(j-1)>r)+t_{j}\quad\text{for $2\leq j\leq s$.}

Here the

(9.7)

s-r\leq\sum_{j=1}^{s}\big(t_{j}+\chi(w(j)>r)\chi(w(j-1)>r)\big)\leq c-1+\chi(s>n_{0})(s-n_{0}),

for

Then the rational function

defined in (

x_{u_{s}}^{l}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\\
i\notin U\end{subarray}}^{n}\Big(p_{i}(x_{i}/x_{u_{s}})x_{i}^{d-s(c-1)-\chi(i>n_{0})(s-r)}\Big),

where

l=(n-s)\big(s(c-1)-d\big)+(s-r)(n-n_{0}-s+r),

and the

are polynomials in

.

Proof.

Let

\displaystyle S_{0}:=

\displaystyle\{1-k_{s},2-k_{s},\dots,d-k_{s}\},\quad B:=\{1-k_{s},2-k_{2},\dots,b-k_{s}\},

\displaystyle S_{j}:=

\displaystyle\{k_{j}-k_{s}-c+2-\chi(u_{j}>i),\dots,k_{j}-k_{s}+c-1-\chi(u_{j}>i)\}

for
j=1,\dots,r
and
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}\setminus U
,
and

S_{j}:=\{k_{j}-k_{s}-c+1+\chi(i\leq n_{0})-\chi(u_{j}>i),\dots,k_{j}-k_{s}+c-\chi(i\leq n_{0})-\chi(u_{j}>i)\}

for
j=r+1,\dots,s
and
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}\setminus U
.
Assuming the conditions of the lemma, we show that the product

\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\\
i\notin U\end{subarray}}^{n}\big(q^{1-k_{s}}x_{i}/x_{u_{s}}\big)_{d}

in the denominator of
L^{\prime}
is in fact a factor of its numerator. Then
L^{\prime}
can be written as a Laurent polynomial, rather than a rational function.
To achieve this, it is equivalent to showing that

(9.9)

S_{0}\subseteq\bigcup_{j=1}^{s}S_{j}\bigcup B.

Since
S^{\prime}_{j}:=\{k_{j}-k_{s}-c+2,\dots,k_{j}-k_{s}+c-2\}\subseteq S_{j}
for
j=1,\dots,s
,
(
9.9
) holds if we show that

(9.10)

S_{0}\subseteq\bigcup_{j=1}^{s}S^{\prime}_{j}\bigcup B.

To obtain (
9.10
), it suffices to prove

(1)

b\geq k_{w(1)}-c+1
;

(2)

k_{w(s)}+c-2\geq d
;

(3)

k_{w(j-1)}+c-2\geq k_{w(j)}-c+1
for
j=2,\dots,s
.

To prove (1), we need to find the upper bound for
k_{w(1)}
.
By (
9.6
) we have

(9.11)

\sum_{j=2}^{s}\big(k_{w(j)}-k_{w(j-1)}\big)=k_{w(s)}-k_{w(1)}=(s-1)(c-1)+\sum_{j=2}^{s}\big(t_{j}+\chi(w(j)>r)\chi(w(j-1)>r)\big).

Then

(9.12)

k_{w(1)}=k_{w(s)}-(s-1)(c-1)-\sum_{j=2}^{s}\big(t_{j}+\chi(w(j)>r)\chi(w(j-1)>r)\big).

Since the

satisfy Proposition

(9.13)

\sum_{j=2}^{s}\big(t_{j}+\chi(w(j)>r)\chi(w(j-1)>r)\big)\geq s-r-1

for

.
Hence, substituting (
9.13

(9.14)

k_{w(1)}\leq k_{w(s)}-(s-1)(c-1)-(s-r-1).

Since

,

k_{w(1)}\leq c-1+b+(s-n_{0})-\big(s-(n_{0}-2)-1\big)=c+b-2

for

. Thus (1) holds for
s>n_{0}
.
If
s\leq n_{0}
, then
k_{w(s)}\leq d\leq s(c-1)+b
. Substituting this into (
9.12
) yields

k_{w(1)}\leq c-1+b-\sum_{j=2}^{s}\big(t_{j}+\chi(w(j)>r)\chi(w(j-1)>r)\big)\leq c-1+b.

Consequently, (1) holds if
s\leq n_{0}
.
In conclusion, (1) holds for all the
s
.

To prove (2), we need to find the lower bound for
k_{w(s)}
.
By (
9.11
)

k_{w(s)}=k_{w(1)}+(s-1)(c-1)+\sum_{j=2}^{s}\big(t_{j}+\chi(w(j)>r)\chi(w(j-1)>r)\big).

Substituting (
9.5
) into the above equation gives

If

then by (

) and

Hence, (2) holds for
s>n_{0}
.
If
s\leq n_{0}
, then by the fact that

k_{w(s)}\geq b+(s-1)(c-1)+1=s(c-1)+b-c+2\geq d-c+2.

Thus (2) also holds for
s\leq n_{0}
. In conclusion, (2) holds for all
s
.

If (3) fails for some
i\in\{2,\dots,s\}
, then

k_{w(i)}-k_{w(i-1)}\geq 2(c-1).

Together with (

) gives

(9.15)

k_{w(s)}-k_{w(1)}=\sum_{j=2}^{s}\big(k_{w(j)}-k_{w(j-1)}\big)\geq s(c-1)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=2\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}^{s}\big(t_{j}+\chi(w(j)>r)\chi(w(j-1)>r)\big).

Substituting (

) into the above equation, we have

k_{w(s)}\geq s(c-1)+b+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}^{s}\big(t_{j}+\chi(w(j)>r)\chi(w(j-1)>r)\big).

We conclude contradictions by discussing the following cases:

•

For

, using (

) again and

This contradicts the assumption
k_{i}\leq d
for all

.

•

If
r=s>n_{0}
then it contradicts to the condition

in the lemma.

•

If
s\leq n_{0}
then
d\leq s(c-1)+b
. By (

) and

, we have

k_{w(s)}\geq s(c-1)+k_{w(1)}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=2\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}^{s}\big(t_{j}+\chi(w(j)>r)\chi(w(j-1)>r)\big)\geq s(c-1)+b+1>d.

This also contradicts the assumption
k_{i}\leq d
for all

.

Therefore, (3) holds.

Since (1)-(3) hold, we obtain (

).
It follows that
L^{\prime}
is a Laurent polynomial of the form

\displaystyle\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\\
i\notin U\end{subarray}}^{n}\frac{p_{i}(x_{i}/x_{u_{s}})}{(x_{u_{s}}/x_{i})^{d}}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\\
i\notin U\end{subarray}}^{n}\bigg(\prod_{j=1}^{r}(x_{u_{s}}/x_{i})^{c-1}\prod_{j=r+1}^{s}(x_{u_{s}}/x_{i})^{c-\chi(i\leq n_{0})}\bigg)

\displaystyle\ \ =\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\\
i\notin U\end{subarray}}^{n}\bigg(\frac{p_{i}(x_{i}/x_{u_{s}})}{(x_{u_{s}}/x_{i})^{d}}\prod_{j=1}^{s}(x_{u_{s}}/x_{i})^{c-1}\prod_{j=r+1}^{s}(x_{u_{s}}/x_{i})^{\chi(i>n_{0})}\bigg)

\displaystyle\ \ =x_{u_{s}}^{l}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\\

By Lemma

9.2.

In this subsection, we give a proof of the Case (3) of Lemma

Lemma 9.3
.

Assume the conditions of Lemma
6.8
.
If
s\neq n

s(c-1)+\chi(s>n_{0})(s-n_{0})+1\leq d\leq s(c-1)+(s-r)(n-n_{0}-s+r)/(n-s),

\CT_{x}Q(d\>|\>u;k)=0.

Proof.

Recall that
r
is defined as

r=\big|\{u_{i}\>|\>u_{i}\leq n_{0},i=1,2,\dots,s\}\big|.

Then
0\leq r\leq\min\{n_{0},s\}
.
We first show that
r\neq n_{0}-1,n_{0}
in this lemma.

Since

\frac{(s-r)(n-n_{0}-s+r)}{n-s}\bigg|_{r=n_{0}-1}=\frac{(s-n_{0}+1)(n-s-1)}{n-s}<s-n_{0}+1\leq\chi(s>n_{0})(s-n_{0})+1,

the lower bound of
d
exceeds its upper bound in (
9.17
) for
r=n_{0}-1
.
Hence,
r\neq n_{0}-1
.
Since

\frac{(s-r)(n-n_{0}-s+r)}{n-s}\bigg|_{r=n_{0}}=s-n_{0}<\chi(s>n_{0})(s-n_{0})+1,

the lower bound of
d
also exceeds its upper bound in (
9.17
) for
r=n_{0}
.
Hence,
r\neq n_{0}
.
Therefore, we conclude that (
9.17
) implies
0\leq r\leq n_{0}-2
. Then
0\leq r\leq\min\{n_{0}-2,s\}
.

Since
0\leq r\leq\min\{n_{0}-2,s\}
, we can apply Lemma
9.1
to obtain that

Q(d\>|\>u;k)
is either 0, or can be written as a Laurent polynomial of the form

L:=x_{u_{s}}^{l}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\\
i\notin U\end{subarray}}^{n}\Big(p_{i}(x_{i}/x_{u_{s}})x_{i}^{d-s(c-1)-\chi(i>n_{0})(s-r)}\Big)\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq i<j\leq n\\

where
p_{i}(z)
is a polynomial in
z
,
l=(n-s)\big(s(c-1)-d\big)+(s-r)(n-n_{0}-s+r)
and
U=\{u_{1},\dots,u_{s}\}
.
Notice that by taking the constant term of
L
with respect to
x_{u_{s}}
,
we can write
\CT\limits_{x_{u_{s}}}L
as a finite sum of the form

P:=c\times\frac{\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\\
i\notin U\end{subarray}}^{n_{0}}x_{i}^{d-s(c-1)}}{\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=n_{0}+1\\

i\notin U\end{subarray}}^{n}x_{i}^{t_{i}}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq i<j\leq n\\
i,j\notin U\end{subarray}}\big(x_{i}/x_{j}\big)_{c-\chi(i\leq n_{0})}\big(qx_{j}/x_{i}\big)_{c-\chi(i\leq n_{0})},

where
c\in K
and the

are nonnegative integers such that

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\\
i\notin U\end{subarray}}^{n}t_{i}=l
. We will show that
\CT\limits_{x}P=0
.

Let

\displaystyle(x_{1},\dots,\hat{x}_{u_{1}},\dots,\hat{x}_{u_{s}},x_{n})

\displaystyle\mapsto z:=(z_{1},\dots,z_{n-s})\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle(t_{1},\dots,\hat{t}_{u_{1}},\dots,\hat{t}_{u_{s}},t_{n})

\displaystyle\mapsto(t^{\prime}_{1},\dots,t^{\prime}_{n-s}).

Then

(9.18)

\CT_{x}P=c\times\CT_{z}\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{n_{0}-r}z_{i}^{d-s(c-1)}}{\prod_{i=n_{0}-r+1}^{n-s}z_{i}^{s(c-1)-d+s-r}}\prod_{i=1}^{n-s}z_{i}^{t^{\prime}_{i}}\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq n-s}\big(z_{i}/z_{j}\big)_{c-\chi(i\leq n_{0}-r)}\big(qz_{j}/z_{i}\big)_{c-\chi(i\leq n_{0}-r)},

where
\sum_{i=1}^{n-s}t^{\prime}_{i}=l

If we further take

,

\CT_{x}P=c\times\CT_{z}\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{n^{\prime}_{0}}z_{i}}{\prod_{i=n^{\prime}_{0}+1}^{n^{\prime}}z_{i}^{h}}\prod_{i=1}^{n^{\prime}}z_{i}^{t^{\prime}_{i}+d-s(c-1)-1}\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq n^{\prime}}\big(z_{i}/z_{j}\big)_{c-\chi(i\leq n^{\prime}_{0})}\big(qz_{j}/z_{i}\big)_{c-\chi(i\leq n^{\prime}_{0})}.
