MARIO: MAth Reasoning with code Interpreter Output – A Reproducible Pipeline
Minpeng Liao, Wei Luo, Chengxi Li, Jing Wu, Kai FanAlibaba Group {minpeng.lmp,muzhuo.lw,xiji.lcx,wj334275,k.fan}@alibaba-inc.com

Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) have seen considerable advancements in natural language understanding tasks, yet there remains a gap to bridge before attaining true artificial general intelligence, especially concerning shortcomings in mathematical reasoning capabilities. We postulate that the inherent nature of LLM training, which focuses on predicting probabilities of next token, presents challenges in effectively modeling mathematical reasoning that demands exact calculations, both from data-driven and theoretical standpoints. In this paper, we address this challenge by enriching the data landscape and introducing a novel math dataset, enhanced with a capability to utilize a Python code interpreter. This dataset is derived from GSM8K [ 7 ] and MATH [ 15 ] and has been further refined through a combination of GPT-4 annotations, human review, and self-training processes, where the errors in the original GSM8K training set have been fixed. Additionally, we propose a tentative, easily replicable protocol for the fine-tuning of math-specific LLMs, which has led to a significant improvement in the performance of a 7B-parameter LLM on the GSM8K and MATH datasets. We are committed to advancing the field of mathematical reasoning in LLMs and, to that end, we have made source code for data generation / training / inference, and the model checkpoints publicly available at https://github.com/MARIO-Math-Reasoning/MARIO . We hope this will facilitate further research and development within the community.

Introduction

The Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting technique
[
34
]
has been empirically shown to enhance the complex reasoning capabilities of large language models (LLMs) by generating a sequence of intermediate reasoning steps.
Commercial or proprietary LLMs, including GPT-4
[

]
and Claude-2
[

, are designed to produce CoT responses by default, leading to improved mathematical reasoning performance, as evidenced by a 50.36% accuracy rate on the MATH dataset with GPT-4
[
44

.
Moreover, when these LLMs are augmented with a plugin capable of executing code snippets, their accuracy in arithmetic computations—which are typically challenging for LLMs—is further enhanced.
For instance, GPT-4-Code achieved a 69.69% accuracy rate on the MATH dataset
[
44
]
.
This underscores the efficacy of integrating text analysis with code execution in datasets designed for mathematical reasoning tasks.

Recently,
Yang et al. 2023
demonstrated that, despite extensive fine-tuning on arithmetic operations, large language models (LLMs) are still unable to achieve perfect accuracy.
Consequently, integrating code for precise numerical computation has become an inevitable trend.
The Program-of-Thought (PoT) framework
[
6
]
and Program-Aided Language (PAL) models
[
12
]
represent seminal efforts in generating code-centric datasets for academic research.
Building on this trend, ToRA
[
14
]
advances the field by employing a proprietary annotation methodology along with GPT-4, setting a new benchmark for state-of-the-art performance in solving mathematical problems via code interpretation.
However, it is noteworthy that the solutions contained within these datasets predominantly consist of code snippets, with minimal accompanying textual analysis.
An obvious shortcoming of a code-centric solution is that it can overlook common sense in math word problems.
For instance, as shown in Figure
1(b)
, the PoT solutions disregard the fact that the quantity of the food taken cannot be negative.
MathCoder
[
32
]
represents an initiative that emulates the response patterns of GPT-4, integrating a plugin proficient in both code generation and natural language reasoning.
This approach harnesses the capabilities of GPT-4, equipped with a code interpreter, to automate the annotation process, producing a blend of text analyses and code snippets, guided by appropriate instructions.

Our data generation approach generally mirrors that of MathCoder, but with the enhancement of an extra layer of human verification specifically for the correction of easily rectifiable errors.
Therefore, all erroneous solutions in the original GSM8K training set have been manually fixed.
In contrast, the MATH dataset encompasses more challenging problems and necessitates the expertise of professional annotators.
To address this, we utilize self-training and knowledge distillation techniques to selectively identify correct solutions, in conjunction with more samplings.

Furthermore, we introduce a tentative and easily replicable protocol for the fine-tuning of math-specific Large Language Models (LLMs).
To ensure the reproducibility of our experiments, we begin with the extensively studied LLM, Llama-2
[
31
]
, and utilize its math-oriented continual pre-training variant, Llemma
[
4
]
.
Subsequently, we apply supervised fine-tuning to our annotated dataset to establish a baseline model.

Unlike text generation tasks such as summarization or translation, mathematical reasoning usually yields a unique answer, which simplifies the verification of its correctness.
However, assessing the reasoning process that leads to the final answer remains challenging.
To address this issue,
Lightman et al. 2023
introduced a model supervised by processes and a corresponding dataset with manually labeled solution procedures.
Yet, from a replication standpoint, creating these annotations is both labor-intensive and costly.
As a compromise, we recommend training a straightforward outcome-supervised model with both correct and incorrect solutions as described by
[
8
,
40
]
, to serve as an auxiliary tool for comparing and selecting the best among various solutions.
Our outcome value model (OVM) is fine-tuned using the efficient LoRA training
[
18
]
in a multi-task setting, which enables the model to conserve computational resources while maintaining its generative capabilities.

In summary, our main contributions are in three-fold.

1.

We create a math reasoning format that integrates both text analyses and code snippets, leveraging logical reasoning and precise computation.

2.

We introduce a reproducible pipeline for fine-tuning a large language model in the mathematical domain, which includes Continual Pre-training (CPT), Supervised Fine-tuning (SFT), and Multi-Task OVM Fine-tuning.

3.

Both our SFT and Value LLMs can generate solutions, and the value LLM can play the role of outcome value model. Our experiments demonstrate that our approach can significantly enhance performance on math reasoning tasks, as highlighted in Figure
1(a)
.

2
Dataset

In this section, we will provide a detailed description of our methodology and the steps involved in constructing the complete corpus.
As mentioned earlier, the solution we aim to develop must integrate text analysis and code snippets seamlessly.
The textual content should articulate the problem-solving process, while the code snippets should perform precise computations, which may encompass symbolic simplification, differentiation, numerical analysis, equation solving, and handling inequalities, among other tasks.
Our starting point will be math problems at the grade school level, specifically the dataset GSM8K.

GSM8K
To create a solution in the desired format, we utilize instructions inspired by REACT
[
39
]
to ensure GPT recognizes when to employ an external tool, such as an offline Python code interpreter, when needed.
In addition to the REACT instruction in the prompt, we provide two manually crafted demonstration examples within the prompt for the language model to emulate.
For further details on the prompt setup, please see Appendix
7.1.1
.

Given that the problems in GSM8K are relatively straightforward, we initially prompt both GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 to tackle each question with at maximum 5 code snippets in the training set using a temperature 0, which is intended to simulate greedy decoding.

For questions that remain unsolved after the initial two attempts, we address the potential requirement for more creative solutions by re-prompting GPT-4 with a temperature 0.6 for another two attempts.
This approach is intended to enhance the diversity of the generated responses.
Following this method, we successfully obtain at least one correct solution for 98.3% of the questions in the GSM8K training set.

Approximately 100 questions remain for which the answers generated by GPT do not align with the answers from the original training set.
Given the manageable number, we have conducted a manual review of the discrepancies and corrected any inaccuracies found in either the LLM-generated or the original answers.
This ensures that each question within the GSM8K training set is associated with at least one correct solution in required format.

MATH
The foundational approach to constructing solutions for the MATH dataset continues to rely on the REACT instruction, with the specifics of the prompt setup available for reference in Appendix
7.1.1
.
However, two primary distinctions exist.
First, in the initial four attempts employing GPT, we exclusively utilize GPT-4 with maximum 8 code snippets allowed due to the substantially higher difficulty of the MATH questions.
Following this process, a mere 71.8% of the questions in the MATH training set are provided with at least one correct solution.

Secondly, we forgo manual solutions for all remaining 1,208 questions due to the significant burden it imposes, requiring both specialized knowledge and an excessive budget.
Instead, human verification is applied for the correction of easily rectifiable errors.
Consequently, 83.9% of MATH questions are correctly solved, then combined with previously created GSM8K dataset into a unified dataset with size 26.9K.
This was fine-tuned on a well-pretrained MATH LLM, Llemma-34B
[
4
]
.
Using the obtained teacher model, we generated up to 100 samples with a temperature of 0.6 for each unsolved question, continuing until at most 4 correct solution were identified.
Through this approach, we achieved a coverage of 93.8% of the questions in the MATH training set with correct solutions.

Augmentation
To further augment the scale and variety of questions in our dataset, we incorporate about 240K novel questions sourced from MetaMath
[
41
]
.
These questions, which are transformations of those found in the original GSM8K and MATH datasets, allow us to gather a richer set of sampled solutions including both positive and negative examples.
We anticipate that this strategy of data augmentation will significantly boost the model’s performance and serve the training signals for outcome value model.

Re-formatting
The data generation pipeline for REACT is depicted in Figure
2
, resulting in a consolidated dataset comprising approximately 28.8K solutions.
However, the data we obtained using the REACT template’s keywords was not used directly.
Instead, we converted the REACT data into an HTML-like format, using
<p></p>
to encapsulate text analyses and
<code></code>
to encapsulate code snippets, as demonstrated in the Figure
5
in Appendix
7.3
.
We observed that employing REACT instructions typically yielded higher quality output from GPT models.
Yet, when it comes to fine-tuning a pre-trained Large Language Model (LLM), utilizing an HTML-like format for the initial iteration results in a training loss that is, on average, approximately 20% lower.
A thorough comparison is available in Section
4
.
Our hypothesis is that the HTML-like format may bridge the gap between the pre-training corpus and the fine-tuning corpus, leading to improved initialization performance.

Fine-Tuning

To enhance a large language model’s mathematical reasoning capabilities, we propose utilizing the foundational model Llemma
[
4
]
.
There are two primary reasons for choosing Llemma.
First, Llemma represents a continuation of the pre-training process initiated by Llama-2
[
31
]
, extending its proficiency into both mathematical and coding domains, which aligns seamlessly with our requirements.
Second, it has been demonstrated that neither Llama-2 nor Llemma exhibit excessive overfitting on the GSM8K or MATH dataset, as confirmed by
[
35
]
.

3.1
Supervised Fine-Tuning with Full Parameters

The supervised fine-tuning closely mirrors the data generation step that serves to expand the coverage of the MATH dataset.
During the SFT stage, we tune the entire set of parameters of the LLM using our specially curated dataset.
For each given question
\mathbf{q}
and its corresponding correct solution
\mathbf{s}^{+}
, we optimize the model by minimizing the cross-entropy loss by masking out the tokens of questions.

\min-\log p(\mathbf{s}^{+}|\mathbf{q})

3.2
Multi-Task Fine-Tuning with LoRA

During solution generation, the Language Model (LLM) is capable of effortlessly creating both correct (positive) and incorrect (negative) samples
\mathbf{s}^{-}
.
This duality enables us to train the LLM to discern the validity of a solution by predicting whether the final answer is accurate.
To achieve this, we add a light-weighted binary classifier (
i.e.
, a linear layer parameterized by
W\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times 1}
and
b\in\mathbb{R}
with sigmoid activation), alongside the existing softmax layer responsible for token prediction.
We maintain a roughly equal ratio of positive to negative examples for balanced training.
The overall loss follows the multi-task setting in
[
8
]
, while the main difference is that our LLM is fine-tuned using the efficient training method known as LoRA
[
18
]
.

\min-\log p\left(\mathbf{s}^{+}|\mathbf{q}\right)-\log p\left(\mathbf{y}^{+}|(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{s}^{+})\right)-\log p\left(\mathbf{y}^{-}|(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{s}^{-})\right)

(2)

where the first term represents the traditional generation loss of training LLM on correct solutions, while the latter two terms correspond to the classification loss calculated for each token of either correct or incorrect solution.
Therefore,
\mathbf{y}
is a binary vector whose length is equal to the number of tokens in the solution
\mathbf{s}
.
Similar to
[
8
]
, the label of each token is completely determined by the correctness of solution,
i.e.
,
\mathbf{y}^{+}=\mathbf{1}
; and
\mathbf{y}^{-}=\mathbf{0}
.

Given the significantly larger data size required for training the Value LLM, we employ LoRA during fine-tuning to be more computationally efficient.
In addition, the LoRA training also retrains the model’s generative abilities,
i.e.
, playing a dual role on generation solutions and evaluating them.
The primary benefits of this feature is practicality, as it requires the deployment of only a single LLM for the entire inference process.
This ability, maintained along with the prediction of token-level values, allows for straightforward modifications to the decoding algorithm used in the transformer decoder implementation.
For instance, the beam search mechanism could combine the original log-likelihood with the predicted value.
This potential direction is what we intend to explore in future work.

3.3
Inference

Both SFT LLM and Value LLM are capable of directly generating solutions.
The solution-generating LLM can be paired with the Value LLM, which serves as an outcome value model (OVM).
The Value LLM primarily serves to evaluate outcomes, that is, to estimate the likelihood of the final answer being correct.
To improve the quality of solutions generated by the SFT LLM, one might consider re-ranking multiple solutions sampled from the SFT LLM.
However, we suggest employing an outlier-free OVM selection algorithm to identify the best answer.
Specifically, given
K
sampled solutions
\{\mathbf{s_{i}}\}_{i=1}^{K}
resulting in
k
distinct final answers
\{\mathbf{a}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{k}
, the frequency of each answer is represented as
n_{1},\cdots,n_{k}
, such that
\sum_{j=1}^{k}n_{j}=K
.
The optimal answer is selected according to the criterion
\kappa=\arg\max_{\{j|n_{j}>\delta_{K}\}}\max_{\mathbf{s}_{i}\in\mathbf{a}_{j}}\text{OVM}(\mathbf{s}_{i})
.
In our experiments, for
K=20
, we set
\delta_{K}=1
.
This is because a small number of samples, such as 20 in our example, might lead to a situation where a random sample yields an anomalously high outcome prediction, making it crucial to exclude outlier solutions.
In the rare case that all
K
sampled solutions are unique, we simply choose the solution with the highest predicted outcome value.

Implementation Details

We train the Llemma series
[
4
]
through fine-tuning with our curated corpus, resulting in the development of our SFT LLM series, because, on GSM8K and MATH, Llama-2 was verified
[
35
]
to have no data leakage issue, and its CPT model Llemma had undergone data overlapping checking
[
4
]
.
During this optimization phase, we generally employed a learning rate of 5e-5, with the exception of the 7B and 34B models, for which we reduced the rate to 4e-5.
We set the global batch size at 512 and used a linear learning rate scheduler that included a warm-up phase constituting 3% of the total training duration, spread over 3 epochs.
Training for all models was launched with the accelerate
2
2

2

https://github.com/huggingface/accelerate
in DeepSpeed ZeRO Stage2
[
26
]
and Flash-Attention 2 mechanism
[
9
]
.

When fine-tuning the value LLM with LoRA, we configure the hyper-parameters with a rank of 4096 and an alpha of 2048 for the attention parameters
W_{q}
and
W_{v}
.
In the context of the Llama-2-7B architecture, 2B model parameters are trainable.
We employ a learning rate of 5e-5, which is progressively adjusted using a cosine decay scheduler.

During inference, the maximum number of code snippets permitted remains consistent with the prepared training data, with a limit of 5 for GSM8K and 8 for MATH.
For the more challenging out-of-domain datasets, the number is also set at 8 code snippets.

Main Results

SFT Model
Table
2
demonstrates the performance of our model across four datasets that encompass both in-domain and out-of-domain problems.
Our 34B teacher model, MARIO-34B, which is fine-tuned exclusively on the 26.9K dataset, does not match the performance of MathCoder or ToRA at the grade school math level.
In contrast, for more complex problems in the MATH dataset, or even for challenging out-of-domain problems, our 34B model consistently outperforms others.
Fewer training data may be one reason, but the main reason should be its capability to perform text analysis, which breaks down problems into manageable code snippets, thus enhancing its problem-solving effectiveness.
This is verified by the similar pattern observed with our 7B model that was trained on an 82K dataset, which is in line with past state-of-the-art (SOTA) methods.
So we conclude that such a model achieves superior results on more complex problems, likely because these problems demand more than simple logic and a few arithmetic steps — scenarios where models with a code-centric approach typically have an edge.

In Table

We perform the first ablation study to examines the impact of each data source by incrementally adding more training examples, with the primary findings detailed in Table

Overall, the advancements in MATH are more pronounced.
We ascribe this trend to three main factors.
First, the GSM8K dataset, synthesized by GPT, encompasses 98.3% of the questions, in contrast to the MATH dataset’s 71.8% coverage.
Secondly, the selection criterion for the MATH dataset hinges on an exact match between GPT’s generated final answer and the dataset’s provided answer, which could lead the fine-tuned model to overfit specific questions that have straightforward answers.
Human intervention has the potential to enhance the variability of the answers.
Lastly, the teacher model’s generated solutions concentrate more heavily on the MATH dataset.
This is due to the fact that we have extracted a greater number of multi-step reasoning solutions according to MetaMath questions, which are likely better suited to the difficulty of the MATH dataset.

Why GSM-Hard is not a good testset for MATH LLM?

The GSM-Hard dataset, introduced by
Gao et al. 2023
, is akin to the original GSM8K test set, with the sole distinction being the alteration of numbers in the original problem statements.
However, as illustrated in the introduction, these modifications to the numbers do not always align with the common sense of real physical world; for example, ages cannot be negative, and the number of people cannot be fractional.
Methods following the PAL or PoT paradigm tend to generate code without verifying the sensibility of their output.
In contrast, our approach incorporates a textual analysis that ensures the results derived from code execution are consistent with the constraints of the physical world.
As a result, our LLM will opt not to produce an illogical final answer or to arbitrarily round fractions, even if the so-called correct answer has been computed during the problem-solving process.
This accounts for the lower accuracy observed with our method on this dataset, as shown in Table
6
.
In addition, we found the solutions in the GSM-Hard remain the same as the original GSM8K, even the numbers have changed.
For more intricate examples, please refer to the case study provided in Appendix
7.4
.
In summary, we suggest not the use GSM-Hard dataset in this community unless the mentioned errors have been fixed.

Why re-formatting to HTML?

The standard template for REACT examplified in Appendix
7.1.5
employs a key-value pair format represented as strings, with each step comprising elements like “
Thought:text analysis
", “
Action: tool name
", “
Action Input: code snippet
", and “
Observation: execution output
".

Figure 4
:
Train loss of different formats within first 100 iterations when fine-tuning on Llemma-34B.

REACT
[
39
]
is an excellent approach to empower the LLM the capability of using external tool.
However, our analysis of the log-likelihood for REACT examples suggests that such data formats are rare in the corpora used for pre-training LLMs.
In contrast, when converting REACT data into an HTML format, using tags such as “
<p>text analysis</p>
" and “
<code>code snippet</code>
", we noted a substantial decrease in log-likelihood.
In our data pipeline of Figure
2
, when training the Llemma-34B on the 26.9K dataset with different formats, this reduction was evident from the initial loss observed in the first iteration.

As Figure
4
demonstrates, the loss for fine-tuning with HTML-formatted data is 20% lower than when using the original REACT format.
However, upon evaluating the performance on the test sets after 3 epochs of SFT, we observed only a marginal improvement ( 1%) on MATH test set.
Given the substantially reduced initial loss, we hypothesize that careful hyper-parameter tuning could potentially enhance the performance of data formatted in HTML.
We intend to explore this possibility in future work.

Conclusion

This paper introduces a reproducible pipeline that covers both the construction of a math-specific dataset and the fine-tuning of a large language model (LLM) with an emphasis on leveraging a Python code interpreter.
To support further research and development in this field, we first have made our model checkpoints available as an open-source resource and provide a detailed description of our three-stage fine-tuning process.
Our results demonstrate that integrating text analyses with code snippets enhances the model’s capabilities for common sense reasoning and precise computation in mathematical reasoning tasks.
Moreover, our fine-tuning method enhances model performance by incorporating a verifier model that requires only a negligible number of additional parameters.
To the best of our knowledge, our approach sets a new state-of-the-art benchmark for LLMs with a size around 7 billion parameters on the MATH datasets, and it exhibits notable generalization ability on challenging out-of-domain math datasets.

Question: Alex, Stan, and Adelwolfe are trying to catch them all, Pokemon that is.

has Stan caught?

# write values for alex and adelwolfe in terms of stan

9 * stan + 33 == 4 * total
9 * stan == 4 * 339 - 33

stan = (4 * 339 - 33) / 9

7.4.2
Incorrect or unreasonable intermediate step

In this case, the PoT solution offered by GSM-Hard calculates an intermediate variable cows as -3016191, which is not feasible.
Even though the final answer representing the number of chickens is positive, the question is deemed invalid because the quantity of cows cannot be a negative value.
Regarding the ToRA solution, it arrives at the same answer as GSM-Hard despite generating an incorrect program output.
Our model’s program produces identical intermediate results to what is considered the correct answer, yet upon textual analysis, it recognizes that the count of animals must not be negative.
Consequently, the model persists in self-verification up to a predetermined maximum number of steps and ultimately yields ’None’ as the output.

Question: Farmer Brown has 3016226 animals on his farm, all either chickens or cows.
They have a total of 70 legs, all together. How many of the animals are chickens?

PoT Solution from GSM-Hard:

def solution():
total_animals = 3016226
total_legs = 70
chicken_legs = 2
cow_legs = 4
cows = (total_legs - (total_animals * chicken_legs)) / (cow_legs - chicken_legs)
chickens = total_animals - cows
result = chickens
return result
