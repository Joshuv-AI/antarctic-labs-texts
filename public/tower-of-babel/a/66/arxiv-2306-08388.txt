Skill-Critic: Refining Learned Skills for Reinforcement Learning
Ce Hao, Catherine Weaver, Chen Tang, Kenta Kawamoto, Masayoshi Tomizuka, Wei Zhan

Abstract
Hierarchical reinforcement learning (RL) can accelerate long-horizon decision-making by temporally abstracting a policy into multiple levels. Promising results in sparse reward environments have been seen with skills , i.e. sequences of primitive actions. Typically, a skill latent space and policy are discovered from offline data, but the resulting low-level policy can be unreliable due to low-coverage demonstrations or distribution shifts. As a solution, we propose fine-tuning the low-level policy in conjunction with high-level skill selection. Our Skill-Critic algorithm optimizes both the low and high-level policies; these policies are also initialized and regularized by the latent space learned from offline demonstrations to guide the joint policy optimization. We validate our approach in multiple sparse RL environments, including a new sparse reward autonomous racing task in Gran Turismo Sport. The experiments show that Skill-Critic’s low-level policy fine-tuning and demonstration-guided regularization are essential for optimal performance. Images and videos are available at https://sites.google.com/view/skill-critic . We plan to open source the code with the final version.

Introduction

Figure 1:
Our Skill-Critic approach leverages low-coverage demonstrations to facilitate hierarchical reinforcement learning by (1) acquiring a basic skill-set from demonstrations that (2) guides learning online skill selection and skill improvement

Reinforcement learning (RL) has demonstrated remarkable success across various domains
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
]
.
However, standard RL algorithms often lack the ability to incorporate prior structure, knowledge, or experience, which may be necessary for complex tasks
[
5
,
6
,
7
,
8
]
. Incorporating prior experience by learning from demonstration can facilitate efficient exploration in complex environments
[
9
]
. For example, statistical methods can infer the hidden structure of offline data and inform the decision-making process
[
6
,
7
]
. However, offline data alone may not suffice for determining an optimal policy, particularly when the data originates from simpler environments or pertains to intricate or stochastic tasks. In such cases, online policy optimization is imperative to refine suboptimal policies. In this work, we present a hierarchical RL framework that can leverage offline data to accelerate RL training without limiting its performance by the quality of offline data.

Our framework employs
skills
, temporally extended sequences of primitive actions
[
10
]
. Previous works extract skills from unstructured data and transfer them to downstream RL tasks with a skill selection policy whose action space is the skill itself
[
11
]
. Skill-Prior RL (SPiRL)
[
6
]
found learning a set of skills may not be adequate to guide skill selection; rather, exploration is improved when high-level skill selection is regularized by a data-informed prior distribution known as the skill prior. In SPiRL, the skill prior informs high-level policy selection, but the low-level policy, i.e. the skill, is
stationary
. However, when offline data is low-coverage or low-quality,
stationary
low-level skills may not suffice in complicated downstream environments.

Our
Skill-Critic
approach aims to leverage joint high-level and low-level policy optimization to critique the skills themselves during skill selection. Intuitively, agents could use their experience to
improve
their skill set, rather than being constrained to select skills from a stationary, offline library.
We show this problem can be formulated as the joint optimization of a high-level (HL) policy to select a skill and a low-level (LL) policy to select an action based on that skill. We guide HL skill selection with an offline data-informed skill prior
[
6
]
, and we extend this notion to initialize and regularize the LL skills using an action prior informed by offline data. Skill-Critic is reminiscent of the discrete
options
framework
[
12
,
13
]
; however, the continuous skill space that is initialized and regularized by offline data adds a unique structure for guiding and stabilizing the joint policy optimization.

Our contributions are as follows: (1) We formulate the joint optimization of the HL and LL policies to simultaneously select skills and improve the skill set, (2) We use an action prior to guide LL policy fine-tuning to improve a skill set learned from offline data, and (3) We show our method can
improve
the skill set and performance in simulated navigation tasks, and solve a new sparse reward autonomous racing task in the challenging Gran Turismo Sport environment.

Related Works

Offline RL.

Offline RL algorithms use pre-collected demonstrations to learn optimal policies without environment interaction
[
14
,
15
,
16
,
17
,
18
]
. When environment interaction is limited, offline RL can warm-start and accelerate online RL training
[
19
,
20
,
21
,
22
,
23
]
, which is referred to as
fine-tuning
. However, the quality of offline RL priors highly relies on the demonstration. The performance of RL fine-tuning can deteriorate when attempting to
transfer
prior experience from one task to another
[
14
]
.

Skill-transfer RL

Skill-transfer RL reuses pre-trained skills, i.e. sequences of actions, to accelerate RL training for downstream tasks
[
6
,
7
,
24
,
25
]
. One commonly used approach is to learn a skill latent space from offline data using variational autoencoder (VAE)
[
10
]
. In the downstream RL task, an HL skill-selection policy is then trained to select the optimal skill from the learned skill space. In this way, RL only needs to explore how to stitch temporally-extended action sequences, instead of searching for the optimal action for each single time step. Furthermore, a prior for the HL policy can be obtained from VAE training to guide and further accelerate RL training. Prior works often consider a
stationary
skill space. Thus, the RL agent’s performance is constrained by the skills contained in the offline data. Adaptive Skill-Prior RL (ASPiRe)
[
26
]
alleviates this issue by enabling the RL agent to compose skill priors learned from multiple datasets. Nevertheless, the LL policies are still stationary, and the RL agent may still fail when the downstream task requires skills unseen in any available datasets. It motivates us to propose Skill-Critic, which can effectively leverage low-coverage or low-quality data for skill-transfer RL.

Hierarchical RL.
Hierarchical RL breaks long-horizon tasks down into simpler sub-tasks, enhancing training performance
[
27
,
28
,
5
,
29
,
30
]
. Commonly, algorithms employ intermediate variables, such as goals, options, or skills, to define subdomains that bridge high and low levels
[
18
,
31
,
32
,
33
,
12
,
25
]
.
Single-layer hierarchical RL (such as skill-transfer RL) may fail to adequately adjust to the downstream environment.
Joint policy training can alleviate this issue, but may be unstable or more sample intensive than single-layer RL because the agent must explore both the intermediate space and action space
[
33
]
. Joint policy learning can be accelerated by pre-trained goal-conditioned
policies from demonstrations
[
34
,
5
]
that require structured goal states to be defined. Conversely, we leverage
skills
, thereby adding structure and prior experience to guide the joint policy optimization problem.

3
Approach

Our goal is to employ demonstration-informed, temporally abstracted skills in hierarchical RL to facilitate the learning of complex long-horizon tasks
[
6
]
. Ultimately, we aim to solve the task with a hierarchical policy, consisting of 1) an HL policy,
\pi_{z}(z_{t}|s_{t})
, that selects the best skill,
z_{t}
, given the current state,
s_{t}
; and 2) a LL policy,
\pi_{a}(a_{t}|s_{t},z_{t})
, selects the optimal action,
a_{t}
, based on the current state and selected skill. The HL policy selects skills from a learned continuous skill set
\mathcal{Z}
, i.e.,
z_{t}\in\mathcal{Z}
. As in SPiRL
[
6
]
, we consider the setting we can extract an initial skill set from offline demonstrations to accelerate downstream RL training. We further consider the case when the extracted skill set is
insufficient
for solving the downstream tasks, which motivates our
Skill-Critic
framework. The proposed framework is detailed in Figure
2
, which consists of two stages. In Stage 1, we leverage demonstrations to learn a skill decoder and skill prior that can accelerate RL training. In Stage 2, we leverage hierarchical RL to fine-tune
both
the HL and LL policies, thus, further improving the inadequate offline learned skill set.

3.1
Offline Skill Prior and Embedding Pre-Training (Stage 1)

We assume access to demonstrations in the training environment consisting of trajectories
\mathcal{D}=(\tau_{0},\tau_{1},...,\tau_{N})
, which each include states and actions at every time step
\tau=(s_{t},a_{t},...)
. The demonstrations may not contain complete solutions for the downstream task; this motivates transferring skills by training an HL policy to select the best skills for a new task. We furthermore consider the case when demonstrations include only a subset of potential skills or suboptimal skills, motivating the improvement and discovery of skills with RL fine-tuning of the LL policy.

In Stage 1, we train a Variational Auto-Encoder (VAE)
[
10
]
to abstract
skills
from the demonstrations; each skill consists of an action sequence of a fixed horizon
H
. We denote the action sequence
a_{0:H-1}
with state sequence
s_{0:H-1}
. We use precisely the method in
[
6
]
. The output of this stage is a trained skill encoder
q_{\zeta}(z|s_{0:H-1},a_{0:H-1})
, skill decoder
g_{\psi_{a}}(a|s,z)
, and skill prior
p_{\psi_{z}}(z|s)
. The skill prior is used to initialize and regularize a downstream HL policy
[
6
]
. We employ a state-dependent skill decoder,
g_{\psi_{a}}(a|s,z)

[
7
]
and also assume that the state is augmented with a one-hot vector corresponding to the time since the last skill was selected for a more informative policy.

In our framework, we aim to use the skill decoder to guide the training of a LL policy, in contrast to directly using the skill decoder as the
stationary
and
deterministic
LL policies in prior works
[
6
,
7
]
. To enable efficient exploration in LL policy training, we adopt a stochastic LL policy. Thus, to effectively guide the LL policy with the pre-trained skill decoder, we define an action prior denoted as
p_{\bar{\psi}_{a}}(a|z,s)=\mathcal{N}(\mu_{\hat{a}},\sigma_{\hat{a}})
, where
\mu_{\hat{a}}
is the output of the skill decoder
g_{\psi_{a}}
and
\sigma_{\hat{a}}
is a hyperparameter we set. Appendix
D
provides an ablation study on the effect of
\sigma_{\hat{a}}
on the performance. Later, we will introduce how we may regularize the LL policy with this action prior.

3.2
Problem Formulation: Hierarchical RL Fine-tuning with Skills

To optimize
\pi_{a}
and
\pi_{z}
, we show the skill framework is a continuous version of a semi-MDP similar to option-critic
[
33
,
35
]
. However, the semi-MDP formulation uses inter-related value functions that require special algorithms. In contrast, Double-actor-critic
[
13
]
casts the semi-MDP as two parallel augmented MDPs, and any policy optimization algorithms can be used to learn each policy. We show this is applicable even for our continuous skills - thus we can use any policy optimization algorithm to optimize
\pi_{a}
and
\pi_{z}
,
including
optimization algorithms with non-uniform prior regularization
[
6
]
.

Semi-MDP with skills.
We argue that the MDP endowed with skills is a semi-MDP similar to the one defined in the options framework
[
35
]
, as skills are continuous, fixed-duration options. The state space
\mathcal{S}
consists of the environment states that are augmented with a one-hot encoding of the time index since the beginning of the active skill,
k_{t}\doteq(t\mod H)\in\mathcal{K}\doteq\{0,1,...H-1\}
. Following options notation
[
13
]
, the skill can be defined as a triple
(\mathcal{I},\pi_{a},\beta)
, with initiation set
\mathcal{I}
, intra-skill policy
\pi_{a}:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{Z}\rightarrow\mathcal{A}
, and termination function
\beta:\mathcal{S}\rightarrow[0,1]
. In our case, we set
\mathcal{I}
to the subset of states in
\mathcal{S}
where
k=0
, so that skills are only initiated after a fixed horizon
H
since the previous skill was initiated. The termination function is likewise
\beta(s_{t})\doteq\mathbb{I}_{k_{t}=0}
, which takes value
\beta(s_{t})=1
when
k_{t}=0
and
\beta(s_{t})=0
otherwise.
The semi-MDP, therefore, consists of the tuple
\{\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{Z},R,p,p_{0},\gamma\}
, of states, actions, skills, reward, transition probability, initial state distribution, and
discount factor. The RL objective,
\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{a},\pi_{z},\beta}[\Sigma_{t=0}^{T-1}\gamma^{t}r_{t}]
, maximizes the expected discounted rewards induced by
\pi_{a}
,
\pi_{z}
, and
\beta
.

Reformulation as two parallel MDPs.

We adapt DAC
[
13
]
for continuous, fixed-horizon skills to formulate two parallel augmented MDPs. The HL policy
\pi_{z}
makes decisions (selects skills) in the high-MDP
M^{\mathcal{H}}
, and the LL policy
\pi_{a}
makes decisions (selects actions) in the low-MDP
M^{\mathcal{L}}
.
To account for continuous skills, we define the skill transition and reward function:

p_{z}\left(s_{t+1}|s_{t},z_{t}\right)\doteq\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi_{a}\left(a|s_{t},z_{t}\right)}\left[p\left(s_{t+1}|s_{t},a\right)\right],\quad r_{z}\left(s_{t},z_{t}\right)\doteq\mathbb{E}_{a_{t}\sim\pi_{a}\left(a\mid s_{t},z_{t}\right)}\left[r\left(s_{t},a_{t}\right)\right].

(1)

In
M^{\mathcal{H}}
, a state-skill pair is interpreted as the new state, and the skill is interpreted as the new action:

\displaystyle M^{\mathcal{H}}

\displaystyle\doteq\left\{\mathcal{S}^{\mathcal{H}},\mathcal{A}^{\mathcal{H}},p^{\mathcal{H}},p_{0}^{\mathcal{H}},r^{\mathcal{H}},\gamma\right\},\quad\mathcal{S}^{\mathcal{H}}\doteq\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{S},\quad\mathcal{A}^{\mathcal{H}}\doteq\mathcal{Z}

(2)

\displaystyle p^{\mathcal{H}}\left(S_{t+1}^{\mathcal{H}}\mid S_{t}^{\mathcal{H}},A_{t}^{\mathcal{H}}\right)

\displaystyle\left.\doteq p^{\mathcal{H}}\left((z_{t},s_{t+1})\mid(z_{t-1},s_{t}),A_{t}^{\mathcal{H}}\right)\right)\doteq\mathbb{I}_{A_{t}^{\mathcal{H}}=z_{t}}p_{z}\left(s_{t+1}\mid s_{t},z_{t}\right)

\displaystyle p_{0}^{\mathcal{H}}\left(S_{0}^{\mathcal{H}}\right)\doteq p_{0}^{\mathcal{H}}\left((z_{-1},s_{0})\right)

\displaystyle\doteq p_{0}\left(s_{0}\right),\quad r^{\mathcal{H}}\left(S_{t}^{\mathcal{H}},A_{t}^{\mathcal{H}}\right)\doteq r^{\mathcal{H}}\left(s_{t},z_{t}\right)\doteq r_{z}\left(s_{t},z_{t}\right),

(4)

We define a Markov policy

\pi^{\mathcal{L}}\left(A_{t}^{\mathcal{L}}\mid S_{t}^{\mathcal{L}}\right)\doteq\pi^{\mathcal{L}}\left(a_{t}\mid\left(s_{t},z_{t}\right)\right)\doteq\pi_{a}\left(a_{t}\mid s_{t},z_{t}\right).

\pi_{z}
and optimize

3.3
Hierarchical Skill-Prior and Action-Prior Regularized RL (Stage 2)

We now present the Skill-Critic algorithm, which optimizes the HL policy
\pi^{z}
for the high-MDP
M^{\mathcal{H}}
and the LL policy
\pi^{a}
for the low-MDP
M^{\mathcal{L}}
with guidance from the pre-trained skill prior
p_{\psi_{z}}(z|s)
and action prior
p_{\bar{\psi}_{a}}(a|s,z)
. We will introduce the high-MDP and low-MDP policy optimization problems respectively in the following subsections. In each policy optimization problem, we initialize the trained policy with the corresponding pre-trained prior policy and augment the objective function with the KL divergence between the trained policy and its corresponding prior. We essentially adapt the HL policy training algorithm in SPiRL
[
6
]
, which can be interpreted as soft actor-critic (SAC)
[
36
]
with non-uniform priors, to optimize both the HL and LL policies.

High-MDP Policy Optimization.
To optimize
\pi^{\mathcal{H}}
on
M^{\mathcal{H}}
, a policy
\pi_{\theta_{z}}
is parameterized by
\theta_{z}
, and we fix
\pi^{\mathcal{L}}
. Since
\pi^{\mathcal{H}}
executes a skill for
H
time steps, at which point
\pi_{\theta_{z}}
selects a new skill, we employ an
H
-step reward:
\tilde{r}_{H}(s_{t},z_{t}):=\Sigma_{\tau=t}^{t+H-1}r(s_{\tau},a_{\tau})
.
The skill prior,
p_{\psi_{z}}
, is a prior distribution for
\pi_{\theta_{a}}
, and
\theta_{z}
is initialized with
\psi_{z}
. The regularized objective with temperature,
\alpha_{z}
, is

\underset{\theta_{z}}{\operatorname{argmax}}\;\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta_{z}},\pi^{\mathcal{L}}}\left[\sum_{t=\{0,H,2H,\ldots\}}^{\infty}\gamma_{z}^{t}\Big(\tilde{r}_{H}(s_{t},z_{t})-\alpha_{z}D_{KL}[\pi_{\theta_{z}}(z_{t}|s_{t})\|p_{\psi_{z}}(z_{t}|s_{t})]\Big)\right].

(6)

Eqn. (
6
) corresponds to the
HL update
in Fig.
2
and is identical to SPiRL
[
6
]
. By using the

-step reward,

, decays exponentially only every

can be represented with
r^{\mathcal{H}}\doteq\tilde{r}_{H}
when evaluated only at instances when the skills change (
\beta(s)=1
)
[
6
]
. Practically, the
H
-step reward allows the effect of long-horizon, sparse rewards to have a greater signal in value estimation.

To optimize

on

We evaluate Skill-Critic in three tasks: maze navigation, maze trajectory planning, and autonomous racing (Fig.

Maze Navigation.

The maze navigation task is based on the D4RL point maze environment
[
37
]
. We use the same planner-based demonstrations as
[
6
]
, consisting of 85000 goal-reaching trajectories in randomly generated, small maze layouts. We create a new maze navigation task with multiple diagonal tunnels (see Fig.

) for downstream learning. The agent has 2000 steps in each episode and receives a binary reward for each time-step that the distance to the goal is below a threshold, encouraging the agent to reach the goal in fewer steps. The agent can transfer skills such as traversing hallways or passing through doors, but must learn to rapidly navigate the diagonal maze.

Maze Trajectory Planning.

The maze trajectory planning task uses the same skills, simulation engine, episode length, and reward as the maze navigation task. The difference is that the downstream environment has a curvy tunnel with corners of varying shapes, offering the agent multiple options for position, heading, and velocity. To minimize time to reach the goal, the agent must learn to create a smooth trajectory specialized to the full course.

Autonomous Racing.

We employ the Gran Turismo Sport (GTS) high-fidelity racing simulator
[
38
]
to test a new, sparse reward autonomous racing task. The low-dimensional state representation includes pose, velocity, and track information, and actions are steering and throttle/brake command. Unlike previous works in GTS
[
38
,
39

, we create a sparse reward for trajectory planning in a single corner. The agent starts at a low speed in the center of the track and has 600-step (60-sec) episodes; the agent receives a binary +1 reward for every time step it is past the finish line. The game’s rule-based built-in AI controller creates 40000 trajectories from random, low-speed starting points in the corner, each 200 steps in length. These demonstrations navigate the corner, but are suboptimally cautious and slow. The agent can transfer skills such as speeding up, turning, and slowing down, but must improve these skills to complete the corner as fast as possible.

Baselines.
We compare Skill-Critic with existing methods to show the necessity of fine-tuning a temporally abstracted LL policy.

[

without offline experience or temporal abstraction.

uses offline experience to initialize a policy with behavioral cloning (BC), then fine-tune the policy via SAC.

Maze Navigation and Trajectory Planning.

We analyze the maze tasks in Figure

In comparison, the temporally abstracted methods (SPiRL and Skill-Critic) can effectively utilize offline demonstrations to successfully find and reach the goal. However, SPiRL plans slow, jagged trajectories because it cannot improve the offline-learned skills.

Conversely, Skill-Critic learns to optimize its path in sharp turns and adjust heading and velocity, resulting in an optimal trajectory planning. Skill-Critic
discovers
diagonal motion, but it does not forget the skills to solve the maze due to the LL policy regularization. Ablations in Appendix

Autonomous Racing

The autonomous racing task is evaluated with episode reward, time to task completion, and time in contact with the wall on the edge of the track (Figure

SAC sufficiently explores to find trajectories that eventually reach the goal, but is slow to improve its policy with the sparse-reward; it gains only a low reward and is relatively prone to hitting the wall. Warm-starting the SAC policy appears to hinder exploration, and BC+SAC is unable to find the goal.
In comparison, SPiRL leverages the temporally extended low-speed skills and finds the reaches the goal. However, SPiRL’s high-level policy cannot use the stationary, slow skills to navigate the corner at higher speeds, resulting in a slow trajectory. Also, SPiRL’s offline skills may not adjust to unseen states, similar to BC’s distribution shift problem; as a result, SPiRL consistently collides with the corner wall.

Skill-Critic addresses these issues with online LL policy training, achieving higher episode rewards, quickly navigating the corner, and reducing contact time with course walls. Skill-Critic still exploits offline pre-training and skill regularization to guide exploration. Furthermore, the LL fine-tuning adjusts to distribution shift and learns the appropriate maneuvers, e.g. accelerating at the start, slowing and turning hard in the corner, and avoiding collision. These results show Skill-Critic can effectively leverage and adjust offline skills to achieve higher rewards in challenging autonomous racing tasks.

5
Conclusion

In this work, we propose Skill-Critic, a hierarchical skill-transfer RL algorithm, which performs two parallel policy optimization updates for skill selection and skill fine-tuning. We show that Skill-Critic can effectively leverage low-coverage and low-quality demonstrations to accelerate RL training, which is impossible with existing skill-transfer RL methods with stationary LL policies. In our experiments, we demonstrate that our method can solve maze navigation tasks that require exploring new skills in online fine-tuning. Also, we show that Skill-Critic can solve a challenging sparse-reward autonomous racing task with the help of low-quality non-expert demonstrations.

Limitation and Future Work.
Skill-Critic relies on reformulating the hierarchical RL problem into two parallel MDPs. Alternating between optimizing the HL and LL policies for the two parallel MDPs does not necessarily lead to a joint policy that is optimal for the original semi-MDP. The two separate augmented KL regularization terms make it more difficult to theoretically evaluate the gap between the learned and optimal joint policies. In future work, we are interested in exploring an alternative theoretical framework where the HL and LL policies are directly optimized for a single KL-regularized semi-MDP. Furthermore, we are interested in alleviating the restriction of fixed skill horizons and enabling adaptive and dynamic skill horizons.

We formulate the skill inference network (encoder) as

Pre-Training Details and Summary.

We use the same network structure as SKilD
[
7
]
, except for the decoder policy. The encoder is a 1-layer LSTM with 128 hidden units. The skill prior is a 5-layer MLP with 128 hidden units per layer. We use a horizon

and 10-dimensional skill representations,

. We tune the regularization weight

The decoder is a 3-layer MLP with 128 hidden units per layer. Unlike SKilD, to help with downstream learning, for the skill decoder only, we extend the state with a one-hot variable

The outputs of this stage are the encoder and decoder mapping the skill embedding space,
\mathcal{Z}
and the skill prior,

, that characterizes a prior probability for the skill

Variance of the Action Prior.

The decoder parameters,
\psi_{a}
, are used to initialize the LL policy
\pi_{\theta_{a}}
, which is made possible since the decoder outputs
(\mu_{\hat{a}},\sigma_{0})
, where
\sigma_{0}

\mu_{\hat{a}}

We manually set the value of the decoder variance,
\sigma_{\hat{a}}
, and only the output corresponding to
\mu_{\hat{a}}

p_{\bar{\psi}_{a}}(a|s,z)=\mathcal{N}(\mu_{\hat{a}},\sigma_{\hat{a}})

is the output of the policy decoder
g_{\psi_{a}}

\bar{\psi}_{a}\doteq[\psi_{a},\sigma_{a}]

During downstream training, the LL policy network,
\pi_{\theta_{a}}
outputs the values
\mu_{\pi_{a}}
and
\sigma_{\pi_{a}}
. These values parameterize a Gaussian distribution for action selection,
\mathcal{N}(\mu_{\pi_{a}},\sigma_{\pi_{a}}+\sigma_{\hat{a}})

\sigma_{\hat{a}}

\sigma_{\pi_{a}}

, where
\mu_{\pi_{a}}
and
\sigma_{\pi_{a}}
are the outputs of the network
\pi_{\theta_{a}}
and
\sigma_{\hat{a}}
is the fixed-value hyperparameter. We denote the LL policy regularization by

D_{KL}\left(\pi_{\theta_{a}}\left(a\mid s,z\right)\|p_{\bar{\psi}_{a}}\left(a\mid s,z\right)\right)\doteq D_{KL}\left(\mathcal{N}(\mu_{\pi_{a}},\sigma_{\pi_{a}}+\sigma_{\hat{a}})\|\mathcal{N}(\mu_{\hat{a}},\sigma_{\hat{a}})\right).

This procedure allows us to ensure the action prior has a non-zero variance. This is important since policy variance plays a pivotal role in balancing action exploration and exploitation

After 1M steps of HL policy training, we perform the full Skill-Critic Algorithm 2 with the iterative HL and LL policy updates. \pi_{z}

Otherwise, if the HL and LL policy are jointly optimized immediately before the goal state is discovered, the LL policy may deviate too much from the offline skills and the goal may not be found (red line in Fig.
7
). We do not apply the SPiRL warm-start to the racing environment in Fig.

Appendix C
Environment and Data Collection

We run the experiments with 3 different random seeds and present the average and standard deviation across seeds in all of our results.

Maze Demonstrations.

The maze navigation environment utilized in this study is based on the point maze environment from the D4RL framework
[
37
]
. To generate the training data, we use the exact demonstrations from SPiRL
[
6
]
. These demonstrations consist of randomly generated, simple maze layouts consisting of walls and doorways placed at random positions. The start and goal positions are randomly positioned within the empty spaces of the maze. The RL agent perceives the environment through an agent-centric top-down view with dimensions of
32\times 32\times 3
pixels. For the image-based state inputs, we first pass the state through a convolutional encoder network with three layers, a kernel size of three and (8, 16, 32) channels respectively. Additionally, to capture the agent’s velocity, we incorporate two consecutive observations as input (this is also done in SPiRL). The RL agent’s actions are represented as two-dimensional vectors, indicating the velocity in the horizontal and vertical directions. The demonstrations are collected using the D4RL planner that uses tabular Q-learning with actions limited to four directions: up, down, left, and right. Consequently, the learned skills cannot effectively handle diagonal movements or execute smooth curves.

Maze Navigation Task.

We manually design the target maze navigation environment using the D4RL simulator, and it is different than the manual maze environment employed by SPiRL
[
6
]
. In this environment, the agent starts at the bottom left corner and must navigate through a series of tunnels at each intersection to reach the goal point located at the top center. The width of both horizontal and vertical tunnels is 1 unit. Additionally, the maze has
diagonal
tunnels that are composed of
1\times 1
empty spaces. The agent is rewarded when its distance to the goal point is less than 1 unit, and it can continue earning rewards until the end of the episode, with a maximum number of steps of 2000.

Maze Trajectory Planning Task.

We likewise manually design the maze trajectory planning task using the D4RL simulator. There is a curvy tunnel with a width of 2 units that extends from the start to the goal point. The agent has more choices to plan trajectories with higher velocity and fewer steps, and can obtain greater rewards. In the D4RL environment, the agent is discouraged from wall contact because the velocity perpendicular to the wall is set to zero whenever the agent is in contact with the wall. It is important to note that the actions (velocities) and acceleration of the agent are limited, which means that the time-optimal trajectory may not necessarily be the minimum distance path. The ultimate objective in this environment is to plan time-optimal trajectories, namely trajectories that require the least time steps to reach the goal.

Autonomous Racing.

For the autonomous racing experiment, we conduct the data collection and experiments in the Gran Turismo Sport (GTS) high-fidelity racing simulation game. GTS provides highly complex vehicle dynamics models and boasts a large player base
2

The actions taken by the agent are the steering wheel angle and the combined throttle/brake pedal command. The agent can observe states and send actions to the environment at a frequency of 10Hz. The agent selects actions between [-1, 1]. For the steering command, the agent’s action is then scaled to the max steering value of
30^{\circ}
. For the throttle/brake command, if the agent’s action is positive, the agent’s action is the throttle opening fraction from 0 to 1. Otherwise, if the agent’s action is negative, the agent’s action is the brake pedal fraction from 0 to 1. See

Unlike

Specifically, the agent is initialized at the course centerline with a low velocity (10 m/s); its progress along the track is randomly initialized within the corner. Its initial pose is parallel to the heading of the centerline. We create 40000 demonstration trajectories, each 200 steps in length. During the downstream racing task, the agent has episode lengths of 600 steps (60 seconds) and will receive a reward of +1 for as long as it is
after

In this section, we examine the effect of the regularization for the LL policy and its effect on the overall performance of the skill critic algorithm. As we have described in Section

In the experiments in Fig.

LL Target KL Divergence.

\delta_{a}

\delta_{a}
is used to determine the parameter,
\alpha_{a}

36

. We compare
\delta_{a}
values 10, 20, 50 and 80 for the maze navigation experiment corresponding to Fig.

. All methods are warm-started with SPiRL for 1M steps. Then they are trained via Skill-Critic with the varying value for

. The results show that when target divergence increases, the episode rewards increase correspondingly, because the LL policy has more freedom to deviate from the prior policy. LL Prior Distribution.
Skill-Prior RL
[
6
]
extends the uniform prior in SAC
[
36
]
to the non-uniform HL skill prior. Skill-Critic likewise employs a non-uniform action prior learned from demonstration to regularize the LL policy exploration. We conduct an ablation study in the maze navigation task using a uniform LL prior, which replaces the KL divergence regularization term with LL policy entropy. We apply the auto entropy adjustment and use the target entropy value of -2
[
42
]
. However, the uniform prior regularizes the LL policy to execute random actions, leading to inefficient exploration in the long-horizon environment.

Variance of the Action Prior.
We change the variance of the action prior,
\sigma_{\hat{a}}
to values
\log\sigma_{\hat{a}}=-1
,
-3
, and
-5
in the maze navigation task. All methods are warm-started with SPiRL for 1M steps. Then they are trained via Skill-Critic with the varying value for
\sigma_{\hat{a}}
. When the variance is too small (
\log\sigma_{\hat{a}}=-5
), the LL policy is unable to sufficiently explore to improve its skills. Conversely, larger variance, i.e.
\log\sigma_{\hat{a}}=-1
, means the low-level policy explores with weaker regularization to the action prior, so the agent forgets previous skills and fails to reach the goal. We found a suitable value for the action-prior variance is
\log\sigma_{\hat{a}}=-3
, which promotes reasonable exploitation of the action prior and policy exploration to improve the skill set.
