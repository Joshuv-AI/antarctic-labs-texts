Generalized solutions of Riccati equalities and inequalities
D. Z. Arov, M. A. Kaashoek, D. R. Pik

Abstract
. The Riccati inequality and equality are studied for infinite dimensional linear discrete time stationary systems with respect to the scattering supply rate. The results obtained are an addition to and based on our earlier work on the Kalman–Yakubovich–Popov inequality in [ 6 ] . The main theorems are closely related to the results of Yu. M. Arlinskiĭ in [ 3 ] . The main difference is that we do not assume the original system to be a passive scattering system, and we allow the solutions of the Riccati inequality and equality to satisfy weaker conditions.

\Sigma\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}x_{n+1}&=&Ax_{n}+Bu_{n}\\
\vskip 4.0pt\cr y_{n}&=&Cx_{n}+Du_{n}\end{array}\right.\quad(n=0,1,2,\ldots).

Here

,

,

and

,

, and

,

With the system
\Sigma=(A,B,C,D;\mathcal{X},\mathcal{U},\mathcal{Y})
we associate the linear manifolds

which are defined as follows

{\rm Im\,}(A|B)={\rm span}\,\{{\rm Im\,}A^{n}B\mid{n\geq 0}\},\quad{\rm Ker\,}(C|A)=\bigcap_{n\geq 0}{\rm Ker\,}CA^{n}.

(1.2)

Recall that
\Sigma

(i.e.,
\Sigma
is

) and
{\rm Ker\,}(C|A)=\{0\}
( i.e.,
\Sigma

[
6
]
. Finally, we denote by
M(\Sigma)
the
system matrix associated with

\Sigma
, that is,
M(\Sigma)
is the
2\times 2
operator matrix defined by

M(\Sigma):=\begin{bmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{bmatrix}:\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{X}\\
\mathcal{U}\end{bmatrix}\to\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{X}\\
\mathcal{Y}\end{bmatrix}.

(1.3)

In this paper we are interested in systems that are
passive
(or, in an other terminology,
dissipative
) with respect to the scattering supply rate function
w(u,y)=\|u\|^{2}-\|y\|^{2}
. The latter means that for each initial condition
x_{0}
and each input sequence

we have

\|x_{n+1}\|^{2}-\|x_{n}\|^{2}\leq\|u_{n}\|^{2}-\|y_{n}\|^{2},\quad n=0,1,2,\dots,

where
x_{n+1}
and

are determined from
u_{n}
and
x_{n}
via the system equations in (
1.1

\mathcal{X}\oplus\mathcal{U}
into
\mathcal{X}\oplus\mathcal{Y}
. The converse is also true. In other words, the system
\Sigma
is passive if and only if the operator
M_{\Sigma}
is a contraction. Moreover, in that case its transfer function
\theta_{\Sigma}
is a Schur class function.

Our main theorems given below concern the Riccati equality and Riccati inequality for discrete time systems with a scattering supply rate. Analogous results may be obtained for other supply rates, e.g., impedance and transmission supply rates, and for continuous time systems. For these different supply rate functions see, e.g., the papers

Definition 1.1

Let
\Sigma=(A,B,C,D;\mathcal{X},\mathcal{U},\mathcal{Y})
. A
(

)

(C1)

the operator

is positive as a selfadjoint operator, i.e,
\langle Hx,x\rangle>0
for each

;

(C2)

A\mathcal{D}(H^{1/2})\subset\mathcal{D}(H^{1/2})
and
B\mathcal{U}\subset\mathcal{D}(H^{1/2})
;

(C3)

the operator
\delta_{\Sigma}(H)=I_{\mathcal{U}}-D^{*}D-(H^{1/2}B)^{*}H^{1/2}B
is bounded and nonnegative, and

\Big(D^{*}C+(H^{1/2}B)^{*}H^{1/2}A\Big)\mathcal{D}(H^{1/2})\subset\delta_{\Sigma}(H)^{1/2}\mathcal{U};

(1.4)

(C4)

for each

we have

\displaystyle\|H^{1/2}x\|^{2}

\displaystyle-\|H^{1/2}Ax\|^{2}-\|Cx\|^{2}

(1.5)

\displaystyle=\|\Big(\delta_{\Sigma}(H)^{1/2}\Big){}^{[-1]}\Big(D^{*}C+(H^{1/2}B)^{*}H^{1/2}A\Big)x\|^{2}.

Here and in the sequel
\mathcal{D}(H)
stands for the

of the operator
H
. Since

is a positive selfadjoint operator, we know from the theory of operators (possibly unbounded) on Hilbert spaces (see, e.g., Chapter XII in
[

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(H)

\displaystyle=\{x\in\mathcal{D}(H^{1/2})\mid H^{1/2}x\in\mathcal{D}(H^{1/2})\},

\displaystyle Hx

\displaystyle=H^{1/2}\big(H^{1/2}x\big)\quad\big(x\in\mathcal{D}(H)\big).

The latter two properties define

Note that (C1) and (C2) imply that the operator

Moore–Penrose pseudo-inverse.
Let
A
be a
bounded selfadjoint operator on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{X}
. Put

\mathcal{X}_{1}=\overline{A\mathcal{X}}
and
\mathcal{X}_{2}=\mathcal{X}\ominus\mathcal{X}_{1}
. Since
A
is
selfadjoint,
\mathcal{X}_{2}
is the null space of
A
. It follows that
relative to the Hilbert space orthogonal direct sum

\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{X}_{1}\oplus\mathcal{X}_{2}
the operator
A
has the following
2\times 2
operator matrix representation:

A=\begin{bmatrix}A_{1}&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}:\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{X}_{1}\\
\mathcal{X}_{2}\end{bmatrix}\to\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{X}_{1}\\
\mathcal{X}_{2}\end{bmatrix}.

(1.9)

The fact that
\mathcal{X}_{2}
is the null space of
A
, implies that the operator
A_{1}
maps
\mathcal{X}_{1}
in one-to-one way into itself and
A_{1}\mathcal{X}_{1}
is equal to the range of
A
which is dense in
\mathcal{X}_{1}
. By
A^{[-1]}
we denote the closed linear operator given by

A^{[-1]}=\begin{bmatrix}A_{1}^{-1}&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}:\begin{bmatrix}{\rm Im\,}A_{1}\\
\mathcal{X}_{2}\end{bmatrix}\to\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{X}_{1}\\
\mathcal{X}_{2}\end{bmatrix}.

We call
A^{[-1]}
the Moore–Penrose pseudo-inverse of
A
. Its
domain
\mathcal{D}(A^{[-1]})
is the linear space
{\rm Im\,}A_{1}\oplus\mathcal{X}_{2}
. Note that
A^{[-1]}
is a selfadjoint operator, possibly
unbounded. Furthermore,
A^{[-1]}
is a zero operator if and
only if
A
is a zero operator.

Now assume that
A
is a bounded selfadjoint operator on
\mathcal{X}
which is nonnegative. Then
A^{[-1]}
is nonnegative too, and the square roots
A^{1/2}
and
(A^{[-1]})^{1/2}
are well-defined. Note that the spaces
\overline{A\mathcal{X}}
and
\overline{A^{1/2}\mathcal{X}}
coincide. Using the latter and the operator matrix representation (
1.9
), it is not difficult to show that

(A^{1/2})^{[-1]}=(A^{[-1]})^{1/2}.

(1.10)

In particular, these two operators have the same domain.

Part 1.

Part 2.

3.

The Riccati inequality is closely related to the

. Using the norm (
4.2
) and items (b) and (c) above it follows (see, e.g.,
[
4
, Lemma 5.2]
) that the range of the Hankel operator
G_{\theta}
is contained in the model space
\mathcal{H}(\theta)
.

We are now ready to define the system
\Sigma_{\circ}
. By definition, the state space
\mathcal{X}_{\circ}
is the closure of
{\rm Im\,}G_{\theta}
in
\mathcal{H}(\theta)
and

\begin{array}[]{lll}A_{\circ}:\mathcal{X}_{\circ}\to\mathcal{X}_{\circ},&(A_{\circ}x)(z)=z^{-1}(x(z)-x(0))\quad(x\in\mathcal{X}_{\circ});\\
B_{\circ}:\mathcal{U}\to\mathcal{X}_{\circ},&(B_{\circ}u)(z)=z^{-1}(\theta(z)-\theta(0))u\quad(u\in\mathcal{U});\\
C_{\circ}:\mathcal{X}_{\circ}\to\mathcal{Y},&C_{\circ}x=x(0)\quad(x\in\mathcal{X}_{\circ});\\
D:\mathcal{U}\to\mathcal{Y},&Du=\theta(0)u\quad(u\in\mathcal{U}).\end{array}

These operators are all well defined, and
\Sigma_{\circ}=(A_{\circ},B_{\circ},C_{\circ},D,\mathcal{X}_{\circ},\mathcal{U},\mathcal{Y})
is the minimal and optimal realization of
\theta
given by Theorem 5.1 in
[
4
]
.

Now let us prove Lemma
4.2
with
\Sigma_{\circ}
in place of
\Sigma
. Let
\eta\in H^{2}(\mathcal{U})
. We decompose
\eta
as
\eta(z)=u+z\tilde{\eta}(z)
, where
u=\eta(0)
and
\tilde{\eta}(z)=z^{-1}\left(\eta(z)-\eta(0)\right)
. Note that the constant function
u
and the function
z\tilde{\eta}(z)
are perpendicular in
H^{2}(\mathcal{U})
, and thus

\|\eta\|_{H^{2}(\mathcal{U})}^{2}=\|u\|^{2}+\|\tilde{\eta}\|_{H^{2}(\mathcal{U})}^{2}.

(4.3)

Next observe that

\displaystyle(x+\theta\eta)(z)=x(0)+\theta(0)\eta(0)

\displaystyle+\big(x(z)-x(0)\big)

\displaystyle+\big(\theta(z)-\theta(0)\big)\eta(0)+\theta(z)\big(\eta(z)-\eta(0)\big).

Furthermore, using
\eta(z)=u+z\tilde{\eta}(z)
and the definitions of the operators
A_{\circ},B_{\circ},C_{\circ},D
given above we see that

\displaystyle x(0)+\theta(0)\eta(0)

\displaystyle=C_{\circ}x+Du,\quad x(z)-x(0)=z(A_{\circ}x)(z),

\displaystyle\big(\theta(z)-\theta(0)\big)\eta(0)

\displaystyle=z(B_{\circ}u)(z),

\displaystyle\theta(z)\big(\eta(z)-\eta(0)\big)

\displaystyle=z(\theta\tilde{\eta})(z),\quad z\in{\mathbb{D}}.

It follows that

\|x+\theta\eta\|_{H^{2}(\mathcal{Y})}^{2}=\|C_{\circ}x+Du\|^{2}+\|A_{\circ}x+B_{\circ}u+\theta\tilde{\eta}\|_{H^{2}(\mathcal{Y})}^{2}.

(4.4)

Using the identities (
4.3
) and (
4.4
) we see that

\displaystyle\|x+\theta\eta\|_{H^{2}(\mathcal{Y})}^{2}

\displaystyle-\|\eta\|_{H^{2}(\mathcal{U})}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(\|C_{\circ}x+Du\|^{2}-\|u\|^{2}\right)+\left(\|A_{\circ}x+B_{\circ}u+\theta\tilde{\eta}\|_{H^{2}(\mathcal{Y})}^{2}-\|\tilde{\eta}\|_{H^{2}(\mathcal{U})}^{2}\right).

But then, using the definition of the norm
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{H}(\theta)}
in (
4.2
), we obtain

\displaystyle\|x\|_{\mathcal{H}(\theta)}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sup\Big\{\left(\|C_{\circ}x+Du\|^{2}-\|u\|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\left(\|A_{\circ}x+B_{\circ}u+\theta\tilde{\eta}\|_{H^{2}(\mathcal{Y})}^{2}-\|\tilde{\eta}\|_{H^{2}(\mathcal{U})}^{2}\right){\mid}u\in\mathcal{U},\ \tilde{\eta}\in H^{2}(\mathcal{U})\Big\}

\displaystyle=\sup\Big\{\|C_{\circ}x+Du\|^{2}+\|A_{\circ}x+B_{\circ}u\|_{\mathcal{H}(\theta)}^{2}-\|u\|^{2}\mid u\in\mathcal{U}\Big\}.

We conclude that

\inf\Big\{\|x\|_{\mathcal{H}(\theta)}^{2}+\|u\|^{2}-\|M(\Sigma_{\circ})\begin{bmatrix}x\\
u\end{bmatrix}\|_{\mathcal{H}(\theta)\oplus\,\mathcal{U}}^{2}\mid u\in\mathcal{U}\Big\}=0.

This proves the lemma for
\Sigma_{\circ}
, and hence we are done.
∎

5.
A criterion for uniqueness and inner functions

Let
\Sigma
be a minimal realization of an inner function
\theta
. In this section we show that in that case
\textup{RI}_{\Sigma}^{\circ}
consists of a single element,
H_{\circ}
say, and we prove that
\delta_{\Sigma}(H_{\circ})=0
. Since
\textup{RI}_{\Sigma}^{\circ}
is equal to the set
\mathcal{G}\mathcal{K}_{\Sigma,\textup{core}}^{\textup{min}}
appearing in
[
6
]
, we shall show that the first statement can be obtained as a corollary of the final part of Theorem 7.1 in
[
6
]
. The second statement is proved in the second part of this section.

Let us recall the final part of
[
6
, Theorem 7.1]
. This requires some preliminaries, which we take from
[
8
, pages 164, 165]
with some minor changes. Let
\theta
be an arbitrary function in
\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{Y})
, not necessarily inner. It is known
[
21
, Section V.4]
that there exist a Hilbert space
\mathcal{F}_{r}\subset\mathcal{U}
and a function
\varphi_{r}\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{F}_{r})
with the following three properties:

(a)

\varphi_{r}(z)^{*}\varphi_{r}(z)\leq I_{\mathcal{U}}-\theta(z)^{*}\theta(z)
for each
z\in{\mathbb{D}}
;

(b)

for any Schur class function
\varphi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{G})
, where
\mathcal{G}
is a Hilbert space, such that
\varphi(z)^{*}\varphi(z)\leq I_{\mathcal{U}}-\theta(z)^{*}\theta(z)
for each
z\in{\mathbb{D}}
, we have

\varphi(z)^{*}\varphi(z)\leq\varphi_{r}(z)^{*}\varphi_{r}(z)\quad{\text{for each}}\quad z\in{\mathbb{D}};

(c)

\overline{{\rm Im\,}\varphi_{r}(0)}=\mathcal{F}_{r}
.

Here, the inequalities are understood in the sense of bounded selfadjoint operators on Hilbert spaces. The function
\varphi_{r}
can be normalized by the condition
\varphi_{r}(0)|_{\mathcal{F}_{r}}
is positive. With this additional normalization, the function
\varphi_{r}
is uniquely defined (see
[
21
]
). From
[
21
]
we also know that properties (a), (b), (c) imply that the function
\varphi_{r}(z)
is outer.

In a similar way one defines a maximal factor
\varphi_{l}
from the left. Indeed, there exist a Hilbert space
\mathcal{F}_{l}\subset\mathcal{Y}
and a function
\varphi_{l}\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{F}_{l},\mathcal{Y})
with the following three properties:

(a’)

\varphi_{l}(z)\varphi_{l}(z)^{*}\leq I_{\mathcal{Y}}-\theta(z)\theta(z)^{*}
for each
z\in{\mathbb{D}}
;

(b’)

for any Schur class function
\psi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{G}^{\prime},\mathcal{Y})
, where
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
is a Hilbert space, such that
\psi(z)\psi(z)^{*}\leq I_{\mathcal{Y}}-\theta(z)\theta(z)^{*}
for each
z\in{\mathbb{D}}
, we have

\psi(z)\psi(z)^{*}\leq I_{\mathcal{Y}}-\theta(z)\theta(z)^{*}\quad{\text{for
each}}\quad z\in{\mathbb{D}};

(c’)

\overline{{\rm Im\,}\varphi_{l}(0)^{*}}=\mathcal{F}_{l}
.

In this case the function
\varphi_{l}(\bar{z})^{*}
is an outer function, and normalization is obtained by requiring
\varphi_{l}(0)^{*}|_{\mathcal{F}_{l}}
to be a positive operator.

The functions
\varphi_{r}
and
\varphi_{l}
are called the
right and
left defect functions
of
\theta
; see
[
2
, page 213]

and the references given therein.

Given
\theta\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{Y})
and the defect functions
\varphi_{r}\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{F}_{r})
and
\varphi_{l}\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{F}_{l},\mathcal{Y})
, we know from
[
13
]
that there exists a function
h_{0}
in the space
L^{\infty}(\mathcal{F}_{l},\mathcal{F}_{r})
of bounded measurable operator-valued functions defined on the unit circle with values in
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{F}_{l},\mathcal{F}_{r})
such that the block operator matrix

\Theta(\zeta)=\begin{bmatrix}\varphi_{l}(\zeta)&\theta(\zeta)\\
h_{0}(\zeta)&\varphi_{r}(\zeta)\end{bmatrix}:\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{F}_{l}\\
\mathcal{U}\end{bmatrix}\to\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{Y}\\
\mathcal{F}_{r}\end{bmatrix}

(5.1)

is contractive almost everywhere for
\zeta\in{\mathbb{T}}
. Moreover, according to
[
13
]
, the operator function
h_{0}
defined above is unique. We call
h_{0}
the
coupling function
defined by
\theta
.

Let
\theta\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{Y})
, and let
h_{0}
be the coupling
function defined above. Using
[
8
, Theorem 1.1]
and

\textup{RI}_{\Sigma}^{\circ}=\mathcal{G}\mathcal{K}_{\Sigma,\textup{core}}^{\textup{min}}
,
the final part of Theorem 7.1 in
[
6
]
yields the following
theorem.

Theorem 5.1

Let
\theta\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{Y})
, and let
\Sigma
be a minimal realization of
\theta
. Then
\textup{RI}_{\Sigma}^{\circ}
consists of a single element if and only if the following condition is satisfied:

(C)

the coupling function
h_{0}
defined by
\theta
is the boundary value of a function from the Schur class
\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{F}_{l},\mathcal{F}_{r})
.

Here
\mathcal{F}_{l}
and
\mathcal{F}_{r}
are the Hilbert space appearing in (
5.1
).

Condition (C) above is item (iii) in
[
8
, Theorem 1.1]
. Note that condition (C) does not depend on the particular choice of the minimal system
\Sigma
. As the proof of Theorem 7.1 in
[
6
]
shows, Theorem
5.1
above can be viewed as a corollary of the equivalence of items (i) and (iii) in
[
8
, Theorem 1.1]
.

Remark 5.2

If one of the spaces
\mathcal{F}_{l}
and
\mathcal{F}_{r}
consists of the zero vector only, then the coupling function
h_{0}
defined by
\theta
is zero. Hence condition
(C)
is trivially satisfied and, by Theorem
5.1
, the set
\textup{RI}_{\Sigma}^{\circ}
consists of one element only for any minimal realization of
\theta
.

Corollary 5.3

Let
\theta
be a scalar Schur class function. Then the defect functions
\varphi_{r}
and
\varphi_{l}
coincide. Furthermore,
\varphi_{r}=\varphi_{l}=0
if and only if the function
\log(1-|\theta(\cdot)|)
is not Lebesgue integrable on the unit circle, and in that case the set
\textup{RI}_{\Sigma}^{\circ}
consists of one element only for any minimal realization of
\theta
.

Proof.

The fact that
\varphi_{r}
and
\varphi_{l}
coincide follows directly from
the fact that scalar functions commute. Now assume that

\varphi_{r}\not=0
. Then
\log|\varphi_{r}(\cdot)|\in L^{1}({\mathbb{T}})
; see,
e.g.,
[
14
, Theorem 1.2]
. Using
\log|\varphi(\cdot)|^{2}=2\log|\varphi(\cdot)|
for any
\varphi
, we see that

\log|\varphi_{r}(\cdot)|^{2}\in L^{1}({\mathbb{T}})
. But then, since

|\varphi_{r}(z)|^{2}\leq 1-|\theta(z)|^{2}\hskip 8.5359pt(z\in{\mathbb{D}})\Longrightarrow\ |\varphi_{r}(\zeta)|^{2}\leq 1-|\theta(\zeta)|^{2}\hskip 8.5359pt(\zeta\in{\mathbb{T}}\ a.e.),

it follows that
\log(1-|\theta(\cdot)|^{2})
belongs to
L^{1}({\mathbb{T}})
. Next use

\log(1-|\theta(\cdot)|^{2})=\log(1-|\theta(\cdot)|)+\log(1+|\theta(\cdot)|).

The preceding identity together with the fact that
\log(1+|\theta(\cdot)|)
belongs to
L^{1}({\mathbb{T}})
shows that
\log(1-|\theta(\cdot)|)\in L^{1}({\mathbb{T}})
.

Conversely, assume that
\log(1-|\theta(\cdot)|)\in L^{1}({\mathbb{T}})
. Then
the factorization problem
|\varphi(z)|^{2}\leq 1-|\theta(z)|^{2}
has a
nonzero solution
\varphi
in
H^{\infty}
by Theorem 1.2 in
[
14
]

or Proposition V.7.1 (b) in
[
21
]
, and hence,
\varphi_{r}

is not zero.

We conclude that
\varphi_{r}=0
if and only if
\log(1-|\theta(\cdot)|)\not\in L^{1}({\mathbb{T}})
. The final part of the corollary now follows directly from Remark
5.2
above.
∎

Now assume that
\theta\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{Y})
is inner. Then
I_{\mathcal{U}}-\theta(\zeta)^{*}\theta(\zeta)=0
almost everywhere for
\zeta\in{\mathbb{T}}
, and hence the space
\mathcal{F}_{r}
consists of the zero element only. Thus, by the above remark, the set
\textup{RI}_{\Sigma}^{\circ}
consists of one element only. This proves the first part of the following theorem.

Theorem 5.4

Let
\Sigma
be a minimal realization of the inner function
\theta\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{Y})
. Then
\textup{RI}_{\Sigma}^{\circ}
consists of a single element,
H_{\circ}
say, and
\delta_{\Sigma}(H_{\circ})=0
.

Proof.

It remains to prove
\delta_{\Sigma}(H_{\circ})=0
. Since the function
\theta

is inner, we know from the Sz-Nagy–Foias model theory

[
21
]
that
\theta
has an observable realization

\Sigma_{1}=(A_{1},B_{1},C_{1},D;\mathcal{X}_{1},\mathcal{U},\mathcal{Y})

such that
its system matrix
M(\Sigma_{1})
is unitary. Now put
\mathcal{X}_{10}=\overline{{\rm Im\,}(A_{1}|B_{1})}
. Relative to the Hilbert space direct
sum
\mathcal{X}_{1}=\mathcal{X}_{10}\oplus\mathcal{X}_{10}^{\perp}
the operators
A_{1},B_{1},C_{1}
admit the following block matrix representations:

\displaystyle\hskip 51.21504ptA_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}A_{10}&\star\\
0&\star\end{bmatrix}:\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{X}_{10}\\
\mathcal{X}_{10}^{\perp}\end{bmatrix}\to\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{X}_{10}\\
\mathcal{X}_{10}^{\perp}\end{bmatrix},

(5.2)

\displaystyle B_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}B_{10}\\
0\end{bmatrix}:\mathcal{U}\to\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{X}_{10}\\
\mathcal{X}_{10}^{\perp}\end{bmatrix},\quad C_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}C_{10}&\star\end{bmatrix}:\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{X}_{10}\\
\mathcal{X}_{10}^{\perp}\end{bmatrix}\to\mathcal{Y}.

(5.3)

Put
\Sigma_{10}=(A_{10},B_{10},C_{10},D;\mathcal{X}_{10},\mathcal{U},\mathcal{Y})
. The above construction implies that
\Sigma_{10}
is controllable. Furthermore, since
\Sigma_{1}
is observable, the same holds true for
\Sigma_{10}
. Thus
\Sigma_{10}
is a minimal system. Moreover , the transfer function of
\Sigma_{10}
is equal to the transfer function of
\Sigma_{1}
. Thus
\Sigma_{10}
is a minimal realization of
\theta
.

Using the terminology of Section 2.1 in
[
4
]
, the system
\Sigma_{10}
is the first minimal restriction of the system
\Sigma
. But then, by
[
4
, Theorem 3.2]
, the system
\Sigma_{10}
is a minimal and optimal realization of
\theta
.

We claim that
M(\Sigma_{10})
is an isometry. To see this note that

\displaystyle I_{\mathcal{X}_{1}}-A_{1}^{*}A_{1}-C_{1}^{*}C_{1}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}I_{\mathcal{X}_{10}}-A_{10}^{*}A_{10}-C_{10}^{*}C_{10}&\star\\
\star&\star\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle A_{1}^{*}B_{1}+C_{1}^{*}D

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}A_{10}^{*}B_{10}+C_{10}^{*}D\\
\star\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle I_{\mathcal{U}}-B_{1}^{*}B_{1}-D^{*}D

\displaystyle=I_{\mathcal{U}}-B_{10}^{*}B_{10}-D^{*}D.

Since
M_{\Sigma_{1}}
is unitary, the operators in the left hand side of the three identities above are all zero. Thus

I_{\mathcal{X}_{10}}-A_{10}^{*}A_{10}-C_{10}^{*}C_{10}=0,\quad A_{10}^{*}B_{10}+C_{10}^{*}D=0,\quad I_{\mathcal{U}}-B_{10}^{*}B_{10}-D^{*}D=0.

This shows that
M(\Sigma_{10})
is an isometry.

Now use that the systems
\Sigma_{10}
and
\Sigma_{H_{\circ}}
are
unitarily equivalent (see Theorem 3.2 in
[
4
]
). It follows
that
M(\Sigma_{H_{\circ}})
is an isometry which implies that

\delta_{\Sigma}(H_{\circ})=0
.
∎

Corollary 5.6

Let
\theta\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{Y})
be co-inner, and let
\Sigma
be a minimal realization of
\theta
. Then
\textup{RI}_{\Sigma}^{\circ}
consists of a single element,
H_{\bullet}
say, and
\delta_{\Sigma^{*}}(H_{\bullet}^{-1})=0
.

Proof.

Assume
\theta\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{Y})
is co-inner. Then
I_{\mathcal{Y}}-\theta(\zeta)\theta(\zeta)^{*}=0
almost everywhere for
\zeta\in{\mathbb{T}}
, and hence the space
\mathcal{F}_{l}
consists of the zero element only. The latter implies (see Remark
5.2
) that
\textup{RI}_{\Sigma}^{\circ}
consists of a single element.

Next we use Theorem
4.3
. Recall that
\theta^{\sim}(\lambda)=\theta(\bar{\lambda})^{*}
for
\lambda\in{\mathbb{D}}
. The fact that
\theta
is co-inner, implies that
\theta^{\sim}
is inner. Indeed, we have

\theta
is co-inner

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\ \theta(\zeta)\theta(\zeta)^{*}=I\ \mbox{almost everywhere on ${\mathbb{T}}$}

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\ \theta(\bar{\zeta})\theta(\bar{\zeta})^{*}=I\ \mbox{almost everywhere on ${\mathbb{T}}$}

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\ \theta^{\sim}(\zeta)^{*}\theta^{\sim}(\zeta)=I\ \mbox{almost everywhere on ${\mathbb{T}}$}

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\ \mbox{$\theta^{\sim}$ is inner}.

Since
\Sigma
is a minimal realization of
\theta
, the system

\Sigma^{*}
is a minimal realization for
\theta^{\sim}
. Now let
H_{\bullet}

be the (unique) element in
\textup{RI}_{\Sigma}^{\circ}
. From
(
4.5
) it follows that
H_{\bullet}^{-1}
belongs to

\textup{RI}_{\Sigma^{*}}^{\circ}
. But
\Sigma^{*}
is a minimal realization
of an inner function. Hence,
\delta_{\Sigma^{*}}(H_{\bullet}^{-1})=0
by
Theorem
5.4
.
∎

Examples

In this section we present a few examples. Throughout
\theta
is a Schur class function and
\Sigma=(A,B,C,D;\mathcal{X},\mathcal{U},\mathcal{Y})
is a minimal realization of
\theta
. In the first three examples the state space
\mathcal{X}
will be finite dimensional. In that case a positive operator on
\mathcal{X}
will be bounded and boundedly invertible, and the Riccati equality can be rewritten as

\alpha_{\Sigma}(H)-\beta_{\Sigma}(H)^{*}\delta_{\Sigma}(H)^{[-1]}\beta_{\Sigma}(H)=0,

(6.1)

where

\displaystyle\alpha_{\Sigma}(H)=H-A^{\ast}HA-C^{\ast}C,\quad\beta_{\Sigma}(H)=D^{\ast}C+B^{\ast}HA,

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt\delta_{\Sigma}(H)=I-D^{\ast}D-B^{\ast}HB.

Furthermore, if
\mathcal{X}
is finite dimensional, then

if and only if
H
is a positive operator on
\mathcal{X}
, the operator
\delta_{\Sigma}(H)
is nonnegative, and
H
satisfies (
6.1
). Similarly, if
\mathcal{X}
is finite dimensional, then
H\in\textup{RI}_{\Sigma}
if and only if
H
is a positive operator on
\mathcal{X}
, the operator
\delta_{\Sigma}(H)
is nonnegative, and

\alpha_{\Sigma}(H)-\beta_{\Sigma}(H)^{*}\delta_{\Sigma}(H)^{[-1]}\beta_{\Sigma}(H)\geq 0.

(6.2)

As before, the symbol
{[-1]}
denotes the Moore–Penrose inverse.

Example 6.1

We present a simple scalar example showing that the maximal solution
in
\textup{RI}_{\Sigma}
may not belong to
\textup{RE}_{\Sigma}
. To do
this we use the scalar function
\theta
given by

[
4
, eq. (3.3)]
, i.e.,

\theta(\lambda)=(2\lambda+4)(\lambda+8)^{-1}.

From
[
4
]

Lemma 6.6

Let

. Then the following three statements are equivalent:

\displaystyle\textup{(a)}\ \tau_{\mathcal{Y}}^{*}\Pi_{2}^{*}a=x,\quad\textup{(b)}\ \tau_{\mathcal{Y}}^{*}\Pi_{1}^{*}Sa=x,

\displaystyle\textup{(c)}\ x\in\mathcal{D}(SS^{*})\quad\text{and}\quad(I+SS^{*})x=Sa.

\tau_{\mathcal{Y}}^{*}\Pi_{2}^{*}a=\tau_{\mathcal{Y}}^{*}\Pi_{1}^{*}Sa

Proof.

We split the proof into two parts.

Part 1.
We prove the equivalence of items (a) and (c). To do this we use the fact (see formula (5.9) in
[
19
, page 168]

x_{1}\in\mathcal{D}(S^{*})
and

such that

\Pi_{2}^{*}a=\begin{bmatrix}0\\
a\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}\\
S^{*}x_{1}\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}-Sx_{2}\\
x_{2}\end{bmatrix}.

(6.16)

Note that
\tau_{\mathcal{Y}}^{*}\Pi_{2}^{*}a=x_{1}
. The identity (
6.16
) is equivalent to

x_{1}=Sx_{2}\quad\mbox{and}\quad a=S^{*}x_{1}+x_{2}.

(6.17)

Since

and
x_{2}\in\mathcal{D}(S)
, the second identity in (
6.17
) shows that
S^{*}x_{1}=a-x_{2}\in\mathcal{D}(S)
. Thus
x_{1}\in\mathcal{D}(SS^{*})
and using the first identity in (
6.17
) we obtain

Sa=SS^{*}x_{1}+Sx_{2}=(I+SS^{*})x_{1}.

We conclude that
(I+SS^{*})x=Sa
with
x=x_{1}
.

Conversely, assume

satisfies
(I+SS^{*})x=Sa
. Put
x_{1}=x
and define
x_{2}=a-S^{*}x_{1}
. Then

and

Sx_{2}=Sa-SS^{*}x_{1}=Sa-\left(I+SS^{*}\right)x_{1}+x_{1}=x_{1}.

Thus the two identities in (
6.17
) are satisfied which implies that (
6.16
) holds. Hence
\tau_{\mathcal{Y}}^{*}\Pi_{2}^{*}a=x_{1}=x
.
\Box

Part 2.
We prove
\tau_{\mathcal{Y}}^{*}\Pi_{1}^{*}Sa=\tau_{\mathcal{Y}}^{*}\Pi_{2}^{*}a
.
Again using formula (5.9) in
[
19
, page 168]
, there exist (unique) vectors
x_{1}\in\mathcal{D}(S^{*})
and
x_{2}\in\mathcal{D}(S)
such that

\Pi_{1}^{*}Sa=\begin{bmatrix}Sa\\
0\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}\\
S^{*}x_{1}\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}-Sx_{2}\\
x_{2}\end{bmatrix}.

(6.18)

The latter identity is equivalent to

(6.19)

Since

Appendix A

In this appendix we review a number of results regarding
2\times 2
nonnegative operator matrices that are used in the present paper. In particular, we shall consider Schur complements for such operators. Throughout we assume that
\alpha:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{X}
,
\beta:\mathcal{U}\to\mathcal{X}
,
\delta:\mathcal{U}\to\mathcal{U}
are bounded Hilbert space operators and
T
is the bounded operator defined by

T=\begin{bmatrix}\alpha&\beta\\
\beta^{*}&\delta\end{bmatrix}:\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{X}\\
\mathcal{U}\end{bmatrix}\to\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{X}\\
\mathcal{U}\end{bmatrix}.

(A.1)

Proposition A.1

The operator
T
is nonnegative if and only if
\alpha
and
\delta
are nonnegative and there exists a contraction
\Gamma:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{U}
such that

(a)

{\rm Ker\,}\Gamma\supset{\rm Ker\,}\alpha
and
{\rm Im\,}\Gamma\subset\overline{{\rm Im\,}\delta}
,

(b)

\beta^{*}=\delta^{1/2}\Gamma\alpha^{1/2}
.

Moreover, in that case
\Gamma
is uniquely determined by conditions
(a)
and
(b)
.

If
T
is nonnegative and
\Gamma
is the contraction satisfying the two conditions in the above proposition, then we call
\Gamma
the
minimal contraction
determined by
T
.
For the proof of the proposition see the proof of
[
17
, Theorem XVI.1.1]
, of
[
11
, Lemma 2.4.4]
or of
[
15
, Lemma A.1]
.

Assume
T
is nonnegative, and let
\Gamma
be the minimal contraction determined by
T
. Then the operator
\Delta
on
\mathcal{X}
given by

\Delta=\alpha^{1/2}(I-\Gamma^{*}\Gamma)\alpha^{1/2}

(A.2)

is called the
Schur complement
of
T
supported by
\mathcal{X}
. If
\delta
is invertible, then
\Delta=\alpha-\beta\delta^{-1}\beta^{*}
, which is the classical Schur complement formula (see, e.g.,
[
18
, Lemma A.1.2]
). From formula (
A.2
) it follows that the Schur complement
\Delta=0
if and only if
\Gamma
is a partial isometry with initial space equal to
\overline{{\rm Im\,}\alpha}
.

Proposition A.2

Let
T
be nonnegative. The Schur complement of
T
supported by
\mathcal{X}
is also given by

\langle\Delta x,x\rangle=\inf\left\{\langle T\begin{bmatrix}x\\
u\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}x\\
u\end{bmatrix}\rangle\mid{u\in\mathcal{U}}\right\},\quad x\in\mathcal{X}.

(A.3)

Proof.

By direct checking one proves that

T=\begin{bmatrix}I_{\mathcal{X}}&\alpha^{1/2}\Gamma^{*}\\
0&\delta^{1/2}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\Delta&0\\
0&I_{\mathcal{U}}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}I_{\mathcal{X}}&0\\
\Gamma\alpha^{1/2}&\delta^{1/2}\end{bmatrix}.

Using this identity we see that

\displaystyle\langle T\begin{bmatrix}x\\
u\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}x\\
u\end{bmatrix}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\begin{bmatrix}\Delta&0\\
0&I_{\mathcal{U}}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}I_{\mathcal{X}}&0\\
\Gamma\alpha^{1/2}&\delta^{1/2}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}x\\
u\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}I_{\mathcal{X}}&0\\
\Gamma\alpha^{1/2}&\delta^{1/2}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}x\\
u\end{bmatrix}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\begin{bmatrix}\Delta&0\\
0&I_{\mathcal{U}}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}x\\
\Gamma\alpha^{1/2}x+\delta^{1/2}u\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}x\\
\Gamma\alpha^{1/2}x+\delta^{1/2}u\end{bmatrix}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\Delta x,x\rangle+\|\Gamma\alpha^{1/2}x+\delta^{1/2}u\|^{2}.

Thus for
x\in\mathcal{X}
and
u\in\mathcal{U}
we have

\langle\Delta x,x\rangle\leq\langle T\begin{bmatrix}x\\
u\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}x\\
u\end{bmatrix}\rangle\leq\langle\Delta x,x\rangle+\|\Gamma\alpha^{1/2}x+\delta^{1/2}u\|^{2}.

(A.4)

Now fix
x\in\mathcal{X}
. Recall that
{\rm Im\,}\Gamma\subset\overline{{\rm Im\,}\delta}=\overline{{\rm Im\,}\delta^{1/2}}
. Thus
