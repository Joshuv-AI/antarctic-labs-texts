Optimal estimation of Gaussian (poly)trees
Yuhao Wang, Ming Gao, Wai Ming Tai, Bryon Aragam, Arnab Bhattacharyya

Abstract
We develop optimal algorithms for learning undirected Gaussian trees and directed Gaussian polytrees from data. We consider both problems of distribution learning (i.e. in KL distance) and structure learning (i.e. exact recovery). The first approach is based on the Chow-Liu algorithm, and learns an optimal tree-structured distribution efficiently. The second approach is a modification of the PC algorithm for polytrees that uses partial correlation as a conditional independence tester for constraint-based structure learning. We derive explicit finite-sample guarantees for both approaches, and show that both approaches are optimal by deriving matching lower bounds. Additionally, we conduct numerical experiments * * * https://github.com/YohannaWANG/Polytree to compare the performance of various algorithms, providing further insights and empirical evidence.

One of our main contributions is to study all three problems in a single unified setting, allowing for apples-to-apples comparisons of the assumptions required, and the resulting (optimal) sample complexity for each.

Although faithfulness can be a strong assumption in practice, we emphasize that to the best of our knowledge, no optimality results under this assumption are known. Gaussian Bayesian Networks

Given a random vector

\displaystyle P(X)=\prod_{k=1}^{d}P(X_{k}\,|\,X_{\pa(k)})\,.

\displaystyle P_{T}\vcentcolon=\argmin_{\text{$T$-structured distribution }Q}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\;\|\;Q),

Faithfulness and Markov Equivalence Class

For the purpose of structure learning, a common assumption is faithfulness, under which the DAG is identified up to its Markov equivalence class (MEC). We assume the reader is familiar with standard graphical concepts such as
d
-separation;
see
[
48
]

Definition 2.1

(Faithfulness)
.

We say a distribution
P
is faithful to a DAG
G
if for any

and
S\subseteq V\setminus\{j,k\}
,

\displaystyle X_{j}\perp\mkern-9.5mu\perp X_{k}\,|\,X_{S}\Rightarrow j\text{ and }k\text{ are d-separated by }S\,.

Equivalently, for any two nodes
j
and
k
not
d
-separated by set

, faithfulness requires
X_{j}\not\perp\mkern-9.5mu\perp X_{k}\,|\,X_{S}
.
The MEC of a DAG

is the set of DAGs that encode the same set of conditional independencies as
G
, which is usually represented by a CPDAG, denoted by
\overline{G}
.
A standard approach to learning a CPDAG under faithfulness is the PC algorithm
[
72
]
, which relies on conditional independence testing to recover the skeleton and orient the edges.
While faithfulness can be a strong assumption
[
86
]
, it is known that weaker assumptions suffice. For example:

Definition 2.2

(Restricted faithfulness)
.

We say a distribution
P
is restricted faithful to a DAG
G
if

1.

For any
(j,k)\in E
,
S\subseteq V\setminus\{j,k\}
,
X_{j}\not\perp\mkern-9.5mu\perp X_{k}\,|\,X_{S}
;

2.

For any unshielded triple

, if this is a v-structure, then
X_{j}\not\perp\mkern-9.5mu\perp X_{k}\,|\,S
for any
S\subseteq V\setminus\{j,k\}
with
\ell\in S
; if not, then
X_{j}\not\perp\mkern-9.5mu\perp X_{k}\,|\,X_{S}
for any
S\subseteq V\setminus\{j,k,\ell\}
.

Under general faithfulness, all conditional independence relationships imply
d
-separations in a DAG. In other words, all instances of
d
-connections lead to conditional dependence. On the contrary, restricted faithfulness requires only a subset of
d
-connections to imply conditional dependence.
Conventionally, the first part of
Definition

2.2
is also named
adjacency-faithfulness
and the second part is named

. With our focus on the setup where the underlying DAG is a polytree, restricted faithfulness can be further relaxed as we will discuss in
Section

4
.

4
Optimal Faithful Tree Learning

In the preceding section, we learned a tree-structured distribution under the KL distance, without concern for the learned tree structure. This viewpoint primarily pertains to
distribution learning
. This section adopts an different approach, emphasizing the aspect of
structure learning
.
Specifically, we assume the underlying graph structure is indeed a tree, more generally, a polytree. We introduce an estimator based on the classic PC algorithm
[
72
]
and analyze its sample complexity under faithfulness.
Crucially, we provide a matching lower bound to conclude the minimax optimality of the algorithm, which offers insights into the difficulty of structure learning under faithfulness.

4.1
Tree-Faithfulness

As alluded to in
Section

2

Definition 4.1

(Tree-faithfulness)
.

We say distribution
P
is tree-faithful to a polytree

1.

For any two nodes connected

, we have
X_{k}\not\perp\mkern-9.5mu\perp X_{j}\,|\,X_{\ell}
for all
\ell\in V\cup\{\emptyset\}\setminus\{k,j\}

2.

For any

X_{k}\rightarrow X_{\ell}\leftarrow X_{j}
, we have
X_{k}\not\perp\mkern-9.5mu\perp X_{j}\,|\,X_{\ell}
.

Tree-faithfulness comprises two components, each corresponding to adjacency-faithfulness and orientation-faithfulness respectively in restricted faithfulness (cf.
Definition

2.2
).
In comparison to adjacency-faithfulness, tree-faithfulness solely requires conditional dependence for neighbouring nodes with conditioning sets of size at most one. Likewise, compared to orientation faithfulness, tree-faithfulness only needs conditional dependence for
v
-structures given the the collider.
Let
\rho(X_{j},X_{k}\,|\,X_{\ell})
be the conditional correlation coefficient between
X_{k}
and
X_{j}
given
X_{\ell}
.
As usual, in order to establish uniform, finite-sample results, we need the following concept of
c
-strong tree-faithfulness:

Definition 4.2

(
c
-strong tree-faithfulness)
.

We say that
P
is
c
-strong tree-faithful to a polytree
T
if

1.

For any two nodes connected
X_{j}-X_{k}
, we have
\rho(X_{k},X_{j}\,|\,X_{\ell})\geq c
for
\ell\in V\cup\{\emptyset\}\setminus\{k,j\}
;

2.

For any
v
-structure
X_{k}\rightarrow X_{\ell}\leftarrow X_{j}
, we have
\rho(X_{k},X_{j}\,|\,X_{\ell})\geq c
.

Under strong tree-faithfulness, we can now establish how the sample complexity depends on both the dimension
d
and the signal strength
c
.

4.3
Structure Learning Lower Bounds

Having provided the sample complexity upper bound, we continue to derive a matching lower bound:

Theorem 4.4
.

Assuming
c
-strong tree-faithfulness, and
c^{2}\leq 1/5
,

, if the sample size is bounded as

\displaystyle n\leq\frac{1-2\delta}{8}\times\frac{\log d}{c^{2}}\,,

then for any estimator
\widehat{T}
for
\overline{T}
,

\displaystyle\inf_{\widehat{T}}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}T\in\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}\\
P\text{ is }c\text{-strong}\\
\text{tree-faithful to }T\end{subarray}}\mathrm{Pr}(\widehat{T}\neq\overline{T})\geq\delta-\frac{\log 2}{\log d}\,.

The lower bound in
Theorem

4.4
implies the optimal sample complexity is
\Theta(\log d/c^{2})
, where the dependence on

term characterizes the hardness from “how (Tree-)faithful” the distribution is; and

term comes from the cardinality of all polytrees, which is much smaller compared to number of all DAGs.

To prove this lower bound, we employee Fano’s inequality
[
92
]
and consider a subclass of
\mathcal{T}
to exploit the property that any node in directed tree has at most one parent.
This subclass of directed trees has large enough cardinality by Cayley’s formula of undirected trees.
With the parametrization of edge weights appropriately calibrated, we show the KL divergence between the distributions consistent with any two instances from the subclass is well controlled, which leads to the final lower bound.
The detailed proof can be found in
Section

C.4
.

Remark 4.5
.

The optimality results in this section also extend to directed tree, polyforest and Markov chain.
Since the lower bound is constructed using directed trees, the optimality applies.
For polyforest, which is essentially polytree but allows for disconnected component,
PC-Tree
algorithm is able to identify the correct skeleton. On the other hand, polytree is a subclass of polyforest, thus the lower bound in
Theorem

4.4
applies.
For Markov chain, the algorithm is modified to dismiss marginal independence test, and the lower bound construction considers all Markov chains with the same way of parametrization as in
Theorem

4.4
.
All these graphical models share the optimal sample complexity
\Theta(\log d/c^{2})
.

6
Comparison and Discussion

The literature on distribution learning and structure learning have largely evolved separate from one another. An interesting aspect of our results is that they consider both problems in a unified setting, allowing for an explicit comparison of these problems.

First, it is clear that the non-realizable setting should not be compared to structure learning, since in the former setting there is no structure to speak of. In the realizable setting, however, it is reasonable to ask for a comparison. Comparing (
1.2
) and (
1.3
), it is easy to see that there is a phase transition when
\varepsilon\asymp dc^{2}
.
Focusing on directed trees for an apple-to-apple comparison, if the SEM parameters, e.g.
\beta_{k},\sigma^{2}_{k}
in (
2.2
) are bounded, then strong tree-faithfulness holds with
c\asymp 1
, see
Lemma

A.1
.
In this case, the optimal sample complexity for structure learning is
\log d
and
(d\log d)/\varepsilon
for distribution learning, which has an additional factor of
d/\varepsilon
. Thus, as long as
\varepsilon=o(d)
, which is typical, structure learning is easier than distribution learning.

Another interesting scenario arises when
\varepsilon\ll dc^{2}
: Here, distribution learning is harder, however, we might hope to learn the structure of
T
“for free” by first learning the distribution to within KL accuracy
\varepsilon
.
This is because, as
\varepsilon
goes to zero,
\widehat{P}
converges to
P
, which implies we can use
\widehat{P}
directly to estimate partial correlations for structure learning. Then the question boils down to whether there exists a good estimator of the structure that exploits
\widehat{P}
when
\varepsilon\ll dc^{2}
.

Lemma

A.2
shows that as long as the estimator is agnostic to
\widehat{P}
(in the sense that it treats
\widehat{P}
as a black-box), then we must have at least
\varepsilon\ll c^{2}
. Thus, there is a regime
c^{2}\ll\varepsilon\ll dc^{2}
where distribution learning does not automatically imply structure learning, at least in general.
It remains as an interesting open question how small
\varepsilon
must be for
\widehat{P}
to be efficiently used for structure learning, or whether or not there exist
specific
estimators
\widehat{P}
that can be used for structure learning when
c^{2}\ll\varepsilon\ll dc^{2}
.

Extending these results beyond the Gaussians we consider here (and finite alphabets as in previous work) is a promising direction for future research. Especially interesting would be bounds in a non-parametric setting.

\displaystyle\E(e^{\lambda\frac{1}{n}X^{2}})

x<\frac{1}{2}

\displaystyle\E(e^{\lambda\frac{1}{n}XY})

\displaystyle\leq e^{\frac{1}{2}(\frac{2\rho_{xy}\lambda}{n}+\frac{\lambda^{2}\Delta}{n^{2}})+(\frac{2\rho_{xy}\lambda}{n}+\frac{\lambda^{2}\Delta}{n^{2}})^{2}}\qquad\text{as long as $\frac{2\rho_{xy}\lambda}{n}+\frac{\lambda^{2}\Delta}{n^{2}}<\frac{1}{2}$}

\displaystyle\leq e^{\frac{\rho_{xy}\lambda}{n}+\frac{\lambda^{2}\sigma_{x}^{2}\sigma_{y}^{2}}{2n^{2}}+(\frac{2\sigma_{x}\sigma_{y}\lambda}{n}+\frac{\lambda^{2}\sigma_{x}^{2}\sigma_{y}^{2}}{n^{2}})^{2}}\qquad\text{using $\rho_{xy}\leq\sigma_{x}\sigma_{y}$ and $\Delta\leq\sigma_{x}^{2}\sigma_{y}^{2}$}

\displaystyle\leq e^{\frac{\rho_{xy}\lambda}{n}+\frac{19\lambda^{2}\sigma_{x}^{2}\sigma_{y}^{2}}{2n^{2}}}\qquad\text{as long as $\frac{\lambda\sigma_{x}\sigma_{y}}{n}<1$}

(B.4)

Plugging (
B.4
) into (
B.3
), the probability of
\widehat{\rho}_{xy}>\rho_{xy}+t\sigma_{x}\sigma_{y}
is bounded by

\displaystyle(e^{\frac{\rho_{xy}\lambda}{n}+\frac{19\lambda^{2}\sigma_{x}^{2}\sigma_{y}^{2}}{2n^{2}}})^{n}/e^{\lambda(\rho_{xy}+t\sigma_{x}\sigma_{y})}

\displaystyle=e^{-\frac{19\sigma_{x}^{2}\sigma_{y}^{2}}{2n}\lambda^{2}+t\sigma_{x}\sigma_{y}\lambda}=e^{-\frac{19\sigma_{x}^{2}\sigma_{y}^{2}}{2n}(\lambda-\frac{tn}{19\sigma_{x}\sigma_{y}})^{2}+\frac{t^{2}n}{38}}

and, by taking
\lambda=\frac{tn}{19\sigma_{x}\sigma_{y}}
, it becomes
e^{-\frac{t^{2}n}{38}}
.

∎

Corollary B.3
.

Suppose
(X_{1},\dots,X_{d})
is the random variable of
\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma)
for some positive definite
\Sigma
where
\rho_{ij}:=\Sigma_{ij}
and
\sigma_{i}^{2}:=\Sigma_{ii}
for
i,j=1,\dots,d
.
Let
(X_{1}^{(1)},\dots,X_{d}^{(1)}),\dots,(X_{1}^{(n)},\dots,X_{d}^{(n)})
be the i.i.d. samples of

and

\displaystyle\widehat{\rho}_{jk}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{j}^{(i)}X_{k}^{(i)}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\widehat{\sigma}_{j}^{2}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_{j}^{(i)})^{2}.

.

B.2
Conditional Mutual Information Tester

In this subsection, we define the conditional mutual information tester used in our main algorithm.

Suppose
(X,Y,Z)
is the random variable of
\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma)
for some positive definite
\Sigma=\begin{bmatrix}\sigma_{x}^{2}&\rho_{xy}&\rho_{xz}\\
\rho_{xy}&\sigma_{y}^{2}&\rho_{yz}\\
\rho_{xz}&\rho_{xy}&\sigma_{z}^{2}\end{bmatrix}
.
WLOG, we can express
(X,Y,Z)
as

\displaystyle Y

\displaystyle=\beta_{xy}X+\eta_{y}

\displaystyle Z

\displaystyle=\gamma_{xz}X+\gamma_{yz}Y+\eta_{z}

for some random variables
\eta_{y},\eta_{z}
where

\displaystyle\beta_{xy}=\frac{\rho_{xy}}{\sigma_{x}^{2}}\qquad

\displaystyle\text{and}\qquad\begin{bmatrix}\gamma_{xz}\\
\gamma_{yz}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\sigma_{x}^{2}&\rho_{xy}\\
\rho_{xy}&\sigma_{y}^{2}\end{bmatrix}^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}\rho_{xz}\\
\rho_{yz}\end{bmatrix}.

Let
\sigma_{y\mid x}^{2}
be
\E(\eta_{y}^{2})
and
\sigma_{z\mid x,y}^{2}
be
\E(\eta_{z}^{2})
.
Recall that the mutual information
I(X;Y)
and the conditional mutual information
I(Y;Z\mid X)
are defined (equivalently) as

\displaystyle I(X;Y)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2}\log(1+\frac{\beta_{xy}^{2}\sigma_{x}^{2}}{\sigma_{y\mid x}^{2}})\qquad\text{and}\qquad I(Y;Z\mid X):=\frac{1}{2}\log(1+\frac{\gamma_{yz}^{2}\sigma_{y\mid x}^{2}}{\sigma_{z\mid x,y}^{2}})

Let
(X^{(1)},Y^{(1)},Z^{(1)}),\dots,(X^{(n)},Y^{(n)},Z^{(n)})
be the i.i.d. samples of
(X,Y,Z)
.
Then we define the empirical mutual information
\widehat{I}(X;Y)
and the empirical mutual information
\widehat{I}(Y;Z\mid X)
to be

\displaystyle\widehat{I}(X;Y)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2}\log(1+\frac{\widehat{\beta}_{xy}^{2}\widehat{\sigma}_{x}^{2}}{\widehat{\sigma}_{y\mid x}^{2}})\qquad\text{and}\qquad\widehat{I}(Y;Z\mid X):=\frac{1}{2}\log(1+\frac{\widehat{\gamma}_{yz}^{2}\widehat{\sigma}_{y\mid x}^{2}}{\widehat{\sigma}_{z\mid x,y}^{2}})

(B.5)

where the
\widehat{\cdot}
mark indicates the empirical version of the quantity.
Namely,

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\widehat{\sigma}_{x}^{2}&:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X^{(i)})^{2},\qquad\widehat{\sigma}_{y}^{2}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(Y^{(i)})^{2},\qquad\widehat{\sigma}_{z}^{2}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(Z^{(i)})^{2},\\
\widehat{\rho}_{xy}&:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X^{(i)}Y^{(i)},\qquad\widehat{\rho}_{xz}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X^{(i)}Z^{(i)},\qquad\widehat{\rho}_{yz}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y^{(i)}Z^{(i)},\\
\widehat{\beta}_{xy}&:=\frac{\widehat{\rho}_{xy}}{\widehat{\sigma}_{x}^{2}},\qquad\begin{bmatrix}\widehat{\gamma}_{xz}\\
\widehat{\gamma}_{yz}\end{bmatrix}:=\begin{bmatrix}\widehat{\sigma}_{x}^{2}&\widehat{\rho}_{xy}\\
\widehat{\rho}_{xy}&\widehat{\sigma}_{y}^{2}\end{bmatrix}^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}\widehat{\rho}_{xz}\\
\widehat{\rho}_{yz}\end{bmatrix},\\
\widehat{\sigma}_{y\mid x}^{2}&:=\widehat{\sigma}_{y}^{2}-\widehat{\beta}_{xy}^{2}\widehat{\sigma}_{x}^{2}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\widehat{\sigma}_{z\mid x,y}^{2}:=\widehat{\sigma}_{z}^{2}-\widehat{\gamma}_{xz}^{2}\widehat{\sigma}_{x}^{2}-\widehat{\gamma}_{yz}^{2}\widehat{\sigma}_{y\mid x}^{2}.\end{cases}

(B.6)

Note that the above quantities depend on the samples but we will not emphasize it if the set of samples is clear in the context.
Also, it is known that, by the chain rule of mutual information,

\displaystyle I(X;Y)-I(X;Z)

\displaystyle=I(X;Y\mid Z)-I(X;Z\mid Y)

(B.7)

\displaystyle\widehat{I}(X;Y)-\widehat{I}(X;Z)

\displaystyle=\widehat{I}(X;Y\mid Z)-\widehat{I}(X;Z\mid Y).

(B.8)

From now on, when we have a
d
-dimensional random variable
(X_{1},\dots,X_{d})
, we abuse the notations defined in (
B.6
) by replacing
x,y,z
with
i,j,k
for
i,j,k=1,\dots,d
.

Theorem B.5

(Conditional Mutual Information Tester)
.

Suppose
(X_{1},\dots,X_{d})
is the random variable of
\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma)
for some positive definite
\Sigma
.
Let
(X_{1}^{(1)},\dots,X_{d}^{(1)}),\dots,(X_{1}^{(n)},\dots,X_{d}^{(n)})
be the i.i.d. samples of
(X_{1},\dots,X_{d})

For any sufficiently small
\varepsilon,\delta>0
, if

n=\Theta(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\log\frac{d}{\delta}),

the following results hold for all
i,j,k=1,\dots,d
with probability
1-\delta
:

1.

If
I(X_{i};X_{j}\mid X_{k})=0
, then
\widehat{I}(X_{i};X_{j}\mid X_{k})\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{100}
.

2.

If
I(X_{i};X_{j}\mid X_{k})\geq\varepsilon
, then
\widehat{I}(X_{i};X_{j}\mid X_{k})>\frac{1}{20}I(X_{i};X_{j}\mid X_{k})-\frac{\varepsilon}{40}
.

Combining these two cases, we have

\displaystyle\widehat{I}(X_{i};X_{j}\mid X_{k})>\frac{1}{20}I(X_{i};X_{j}\mid X_{k})-\frac{\varepsilon}{40}.

Proof.

By Lemma
B.4
, with
\Theta(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\log\frac{d}{\delta})
, we have the following properties for all
i,j,k=1,\dots,d
with probability
1-\delta
:

\displaystyle|\widehat{\gamma}_{ij}-\gamma_{ij}|<\frac{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}{100}\frac{\sigma_{j\mid i,k}}{\sigma_{i\mid k}},\qquad|\widehat{\sigma}_{i\mid j}^{2}-\sigma_{i\mid j}^{2}|<\frac{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}{100}\sigma_{i\mid j}^{2}\qquad\text{and}\qquad|\widehat{\sigma}_{i\mid j,k}^{2}-\sigma_{i\mid j,k}^{2}|<\frac{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}{100}\sigma_{i\mid j,k}^{2}

(B.9)

We express

\displaystyle\widehat{I}(X_{i};X_{j}\mid X_{k})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\log\left(1+\widehat{\gamma}_{ij}^{2}\frac{\widehat{\sigma}_{i\mid k}^{2}}{\widehat{\sigma}_{j\mid i,k}^{2}}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\log\left(1+\widehat{\gamma}_{ij}^{2}\frac{\sigma_{i\mid k}^{2}}{\sigma_{j\mid i,k}^{2}}\cdot\frac{\widehat{\sigma}_{i\mid k}^{2}}{\sigma_{i\mid k}^{2}}\cdot\frac{\sigma_{j\mid i,k}^{2}}{\widehat{\sigma}_{j\mid i,k}^{2}}\right)

(B.10)

We bound each term
\widehat{\gamma}_{ij}^{2}\frac{\sigma_{i\mid k}^{2}}{\sigma_{j\mid i,k}^{2}}
,
\frac{\widehat{\sigma}_{i\mid k}^{2}}{\sigma_{i\mid k}^{2}}
and
\frac{\sigma_{j\mid i,k}^{2}}{\widehat{\sigma}_{j\mid i,k}^{2}}
for the cases of
I(X_{i};X_{j}\mid X_{k})=0
and
I(X_{i};X_{j}\mid X_{k})\geq\varepsilon
.

We first prove if
I(X_{i};X_{j}\mid X_{k})=0
then
\widehat{I}(X_{i};X_{j}\mid X_{k})\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{100}
.
Since
I(X_{i};X_{j}\mid X_{k})=0
, it means that
X_{i}
and
X_{j}
are independent conditioned on
X_{k}
and hence
\gamma_{ij}=0
.
We have
\widehat{\gamma}_{ij}^{2}\frac{\sigma_{i\mid k}^{2}}{\sigma_{j\mid i,k}^{2}}\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{100}
.
For the term
\frac{\widehat{\sigma}_{i\mid k}^{2}}{\sigma_{i\mid k}^{2}}
, we have
\frac{\widehat{\sigma}_{i\mid k}^{2}}{\sigma_{i\mid k}^{2}}\leq 1+\frac{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}{100}
by (
B.9
).
For the term
\frac{\sigma_{j\mid i,k}^{2}}{\widehat{\sigma}_{j\mid i,k}^{2}}
, we have
\frac{\sigma_{j\mid i,k}^{2}}{\widehat{\sigma}_{j\mid i,k}^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{1-\frac{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}{100}}
by (
B.9
).
Plugging these three inequalities into (
B.10
), we have

\displaystyle\widehat{I}(X_{i};X_{j}\mid X_{k})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\log\left(1+\widehat{\gamma}_{ij}^{2}\frac{\sigma_{i\mid k}^{2}}{\sigma_{j\mid i,k}^{2}}\cdot\frac{\widehat{\sigma}_{i\mid k}^{2}}{\sigma_{i\mid k}^{2}}\cdot\frac{\sigma_{j\mid i,k}^{2}}{\widehat{\sigma}_{j\mid i,k}^{2}}\right)\leq\frac{1}{2}\log\left(1+\frac{\varepsilon}{100}\cdot\frac{1+\frac{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}{100}}{1-\frac{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}{100}}\right)\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{100}

for any sufficiently small
\varepsilon>0
.

We now prove if
I(X_{i};X_{j}\mid X_{k})\geq\varepsilon
, then
\widehat{I}(X_{i};X_{j}\mid X_{k})>\frac{1}{20}I(X_{i};X_{j}\mid X_{k})-\frac{\varepsilon}{40}
.
Since
I(X_{i};X_{j}\mid X_{k})\geq\varepsilon
, it means that
I(X_{i};X_{j}\mid X_{k})=\frac{1}{2}\log(1+\gamma_{ij}^{2}\frac{\sigma_{i\mid k}^{2}}{\sigma_{j\mid i,k}^{2}})\geq\varepsilon
and hence
\gamma_{ij}^{2}\frac{\sigma_{i\mid k}^{2}}{\sigma_{j\mid i,k}^{2}}\geq e^{2\varepsilon}-1\geq 2\varepsilon
.
We have
\widehat{\gamma}_{ij}^{2}\frac{\sigma_{i\mid k}^{2}}{\sigma_{j\mid i,k}^{2}}\geq\gamma_{ij}^{2}\frac{\sigma_{i\mid k}^{2}}{\sigma_{j\mid i,k}^{2}}-\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon}{100}}\geq 0
.
For the term
\frac{\widehat{\sigma}_{i\mid k}^{2}}{\sigma_{i\mid k}^{2}}
, we have
\frac{\widehat{\sigma}_{i\mid k}^{2}}{\sigma_{i\mid k}^{2}}\geq 1-\frac{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}{100}
by (
B.9
).
For the term
\frac{\sigma_{j\mid i,k}^{2}}{\widehat{\sigma}_{j\mid i,k}^{2}}
, we have
\frac{\sigma_{j\mid i,k}^{2}}{\widehat{\sigma}_{j\mid i,k}^{2}}\geq\frac{1}{1+\frac{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}{100}}
by (
B.9
).
Plugging these three inequalities into (
B.10
), we have

\displaystyle\widehat{I}(X_{i};X_{j}\mid X_{k})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\log\left(1+\widehat{\gamma}_{ij}^{2}\frac{\sigma_{i\mid k}^{2}}{\sigma_{j\mid i,k}^{2}}\cdot\frac{\widehat{\sigma}_{i\mid k}^{2}}{\sigma_{i\mid k}^{2}}\cdot\frac{\sigma_{j\mid i,k}^{2}}{\widehat{\sigma}_{j\mid i,k}^{2}}\right)\geq\frac{1}{2}\log\left(1+\bigg(\gamma_{ij}^{2}\frac{\sigma_{i\mid k}^{2}}{\sigma_{j\mid i,k}^{2}}-\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon}{100}}\bigg)^{2}\cdot\frac{1-\frac{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}{100}}{1+\frac{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}{100}}\right).

Note that, for any
a,b
, we have
(a-b)^{2}\geq\frac{1}{2}a^{2}-b^{2}
which implies the term
\bigg(\gamma_{ij}^{2}\frac{\sigma_{i\mid k}^{2}}{\sigma_{j\mid i,k}^{2}}-\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon}{100}}\bigg)^{2}
is larger than
\frac{1}{2}\gamma_{ij}^{2}\frac{\sigma_{i\mid k}^{2}}{\sigma_{j\mid i,k}^{2}}-\frac{\varepsilon}{100}
.
Namely, we have

\displaystyle\widehat{I}(X_{i};X_{j}\mid X_{k})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\log\left(1+\bigg(\frac{1}{2}\gamma_{ij}^{2}\frac{\sigma_{i\mid k}^{2}}{\sigma_{j\mid i,k}^{2}}-\frac{\varepsilon}{100}\bigg)\cdot\frac{1-\frac{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}{100}}{1+\frac{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}{100}}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\log\left(1+\frac{1}{3}\gamma_{ij}^{2}\frac{\sigma_{i\mid k}^{2}}{\sigma_{j\mid i,k}^{2}}-\frac{\varepsilon}{100}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\log\left(1+\frac{1}{3}\gamma_{ij}^{2}\frac{\sigma_{i\mid k}^{2}}{\sigma_{j\mid i,k}^{2}}\right)-\frac{\varepsilon}{40}

for any sufficiently small
\varepsilon>0
.
Note that, for any
a>0
,
\log(1+\frac{1}{3}a)\geq\frac{1}{10}\log(1+a)
.
Namely, we have

\displaystyle\widehat{I}(X_{i};X_{j}\mid X_{k})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\log\left(1+\frac{1}{3}\gamma_{ij}^{2}\frac{\sigma_{i\mid k}^{2}}{\sigma_{j\mid i,k}^{2}}\right)-\frac{\varepsilon}{40}\geq\frac{1}{20}\log\left(1+\gamma_{ij}^{2}\frac{\sigma_{i\mid k}^{2}}{\sigma_{j\mid i,k}^{2}}\right)-\frac{\varepsilon}{40}=\frac{1}{20}I(X_{i};X_{j}\mid X_{k})-\frac{\varepsilon}{40}.\qed

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{k}I(X_{i};Y_{i})-\sum_{i=1}^{k}I(W_{i};Z_{i})=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left(I(X_{i};Y_{i})-I(X_{i};Z_{i})+I(X_{i};Z_{i})-I(W_{i};Z_{i})\right)

Recall that we have
I(X_{i};Z_{i}\mid Y_{i})=0
and
I(W_{i};Z_{i}\mid X_{i})=0
.
Combining with (

\displaystyle D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_{T^{*}}\;\|\;P_{\widehat{T}})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{9d\varepsilon^{\prime}}{10}

with probability at least

.
By picking
\varepsilon^{\prime}=\frac{10\varepsilon}{9d}
, we conclude our result.
∎

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{V(v,h)}\bigg[\begin{pmatrix}\beta^{2(h+2)}v^{2}+\beta^{2(h+1)}v+\cdots+\beta^{2}v+v&\beta^{2(h+2)+1}v^{2}+\beta^{2(h+1)+1}v+\cdots+\beta^{3}v+\beta v\\
\beta^{2(h+2)+1}v^{2}+\beta^{2(h+1)+1}v+\cdots+\beta^{3}v+\beta v&(\beta^{2(h+2)+2}v^{2}+\beta^{2(h+1)+2}v+\cdots+\beta^{4}v+\beta^{2}v\\
&+\beta^{2(h+2)}v+\beta^{2(h+1)}+\cdots+\beta^{2}+1)\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle-\begin{pmatrix}\beta^{2(h+2)}v^{2}&\beta^{2h+3}v(\beta^{2}v+1)\\
\beta^{2h+3}v(\beta^{2}v+1)&\beta^{2(h+1)}(\beta^{2}v+1)^{2}\end{pmatrix}\bigg]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{V(v,h)}\begin{pmatrix}(\beta^{2(h+1)}+\beta^{2h}+\cdots+\beta^{2}+1)v&(\beta^{2h}+\beta^{2(h-1)}\cdots+\beta^{2}+1)\beta v\\
(\beta^{2h}+\beta^{2(h-1)}\cdots+\beta^{2}+1)\beta v&(\beta^{2h}+\beta^{2(h-1)}+\cdots+\beta^{2}+1)(\beta^{2}v+1)\end{pmatrix}

Thus the conditional correlation is

\displaystyle\rho(X_{j},X_{k}\,|\,X_{\ell})

\displaystyle=\frac{\beta v\times\frac{1-\beta^{2(h+1)}}{1-\beta^{2}}}{\sqrt{v\times\frac{1-\beta^{2(h+2)}}{1-\beta^{2}}\times(1+\beta^{2}v)\times\frac{1-\beta^{2(h+1)}}{1-\beta^{2}}}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{\beta^{2}v}{1+\beta^{2}v}\times\frac{1-\beta^{2(h+1)}}{1-\beta^{2(h+2)}}}

Denote
f(h)=\frac{1-\beta^{2(h+1)}}{1-\beta^{2(h+2)}}=1-\frac{(1-\beta^{2})\beta^{2(h+1)}}{1-\beta^{2(h+2)}}
, which is increasing in
h
with minimum value being
f(0)=\frac{1}{1+\beta^{2}}
. Therefore,
\rho(X_{j},X_{k}\,|\,X_{\ell})\geq c\Leftrightarrow\beta^{2}v\geq\frac{c^{2}}{f(h)-c^{2}}
. Since
v=\E[X_{j}^{2}]\geq 1
for all
j\in[d]
, then
\beta^{2}v\geq\beta^{2}=2c^{2}\geq\frac{c^{2}}{1/(1+2c^{2})-c^{2}}\geq\frac{c^{2}}{f(h)-c^{2}}
when
c^{2}\leq 1/5
, which yields the bound.

•

For the forth case, analogously, denote
v=\E[X_{j}^{2}]
, let’s compute the covariance matrix of
(X_{k},X_{k},X_{\ell})
:

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}v&\beta v&\beta^{h+1}v\\
\beta v&\beta^{2}v+1&\beta^{h+2}v\\
\beta^{h+1}v&\beta^{h+2}v&\beta^{2(h+1)}+\beta^{2h}+\cdots+\beta^{2}+1\end{pmatrix}

Denote
W(v,h)=\beta^{2(h+1)}+\beta^{2h}v++\cdots+\beta^{2}+1
. The covariance matrix of
(X_{j},X_{k})
given
X_{\ell}
is

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}v&\beta v\\
\beta v&\beta^{2}v+1\end{pmatrix}-\frac{1}{W(v,h)}\begin{pmatrix}\beta^{2(h+1)}v^{2}&\beta^{2h+3}v^{2}\\
\beta^{2h+3}v^{2}&\beta^{2(h+2)}v^{2}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{W(v,h)}\bigg[\begin{pmatrix}\beta^{2(h+1)}v^{2}+\beta^{2h}v+\cdots+\beta^{2}v+v&\beta^{2(h+1)+1}v^{2}+\beta^{2h+1}v+\cdots+\beta^{3}v+\beta v\\
\beta^{2(h+1)+1}v^{2}+\beta^{2h+1}v+\cdots+\beta^{3}v+\beta v&(\beta^{2(h+1)+2}v^{2}+\beta^{2h+2}v+\cdots+\beta^{4}v+\beta^{2}v\\
&+\beta^{2(h+1)}v+\beta^{2h}+\cdots+\beta^{2}+1)\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle-\begin{pmatrix}\beta^{2(h+1)}v^{2}&\beta^{2h+3}v^{2}\\
\beta^{2h+3}v^{2}&\beta^{2(h+2)}v^{2}\end{pmatrix}\bigg]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{W(v,h)}\begin{pmatrix}(\beta^{2h}+\beta^{2(h-1)}+\cdots+\beta^{2}+1)v&(\beta^{2h}+\beta^{2(h-1)}\cdots+\beta^{2}+1)\beta v\\
(\beta^{2h}+\beta^{2(h-1)}\cdots+\beta^{2}+1)\beta v&(\beta^{2h}+\beta^{2(h-1)}+\cdots+\beta^{2}+1)(\beta^{2}v+1)+\beta^{2h+2}v\end{pmatrix}

Thus the conditional correlation is

\displaystyle\rho(X_{j},X_{k}\,|\,X_{\ell})

\displaystyle=\frac{\beta v\times\frac{1-\beta^{2(h+1)}}{1-\beta^{2}}}{\sqrt{v\times\frac{1-\beta^{2(h+1)}}{1-\beta^{2}}\times\bigg[(1+\beta^{2}v)\times\frac{1-\beta^{2(h+1)}}{1-\beta^{2}}+\beta^{2h}\times\beta^{2}v\bigg]}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{\beta^{2}v}{1+\big(1+\frac{\beta^{2h}}{g(h)}\big)\beta^{2}v}}

where
g(h)=\frac{1-\beta^{2(h+1)}}{1-\beta^{2}}\geq 1
for
h\geq 0
. Since
\beta^{2}=2c^{2}\leq 1
, then
1+\beta^{2h}/g(h)\leq 2
. Since
\rho(X_{j},X_{k}\,|\,X_{\ell})\geq c\Leftrightarrow\beta^{2}v\geq\frac{c^{2}}{1-\big(1+\frac{\beta^{2h}}{g(h)}\big)c^{2}}
, and
v=\E[X_{j}^{2}]\geq 1
for all
j\in[d]
, then
\beta^{2}v\geq\beta^{2}=2c^{2}\geq\frac{c^{2}}{1-2c^{2}}\geq\frac{c^{2}}{1-\big(1+\frac{\beta^{2h}}{g(h)}\big)c^{2}}
when
c^{2}\leq 1/5
, which completes the proof.

∎
