Abstract
. In [ KRRGR18 ] Keating, Rodgers, Roditty-Gershon and Rudnick established relationships of the mean-square of sums of the divisor function d k ​ ( f ) d_{k}(f) over short intervals and over arithmetic progressions for the function field 𝔽 q ​ [ T ] \mathbb{F}_{q}[T] to certain integrals over the ensemble of unitary matrices. We consider similar problems leading to distributions over the ensemble of symplectic matrices. We also consider analogous questions involving convolutions of the von Mangoldt function.

1.
Introduction

The goal of this paper is to study the connection between certain arithmetic sums in function fields and integrals over the ensembles of unitary and unitary symplectic matrices. We consider the
k
-th divisor function over
\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]
and study two problems: the average over all the monic polynomials of fixed degree that yield a quadratic residue when viewed modulo a fixed monic irreducible polynomial
P
, and the average over all the monic polynomials of fixed degree satisfying certain condition that is analogous to having an argument (in the sense of complex numbers) lying at certain specific sector of the unit circle. In both cases, we compute asymptotics for the average and the variance as
q\rightarrow\infty
and we prove that the variance is described by a unitary symplectic matrix integral involving sum of products of secular coefficients. Our work is analogous to that of
[
KRRGR18
]
, but with problems that yield symplectic regimes, rather than unitary. We also consider the same setup with von Mangoldt convolutions instead of the
k
th divisor function and obtain similar results, connecting problems about the variance of sums of von Mangoldt convolutions to appropriate matrix integrals of sums of products of traces.

The
k
-th divisor function
d_{k}(n)
over
\mathbb{Z}
is the number of ways of writing a positive integer
n
as a product of
k
positive integers. It provides the coefficients of the
k
th power of the Riemann zeta function:

\zeta(s)^{k}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{d_{k}(n)}{n^{s}},\qquad\re(s)>1.

Now consider the remainder term of partial sums of the divisor function

\Delta_{k}(x):=\sum_{n\leq x}d_{k}(n)-\mathrm{Res}_{s=1}\frac{x^{s}\zeta(s)^{k}}{s}=\sum_{n\leq x}d_{k}(n)-xP_{k-1}(\log x),

where
P_{k-1}(T)
is certain polynomial of degree
k-1
(see
[
Tit86
, Chapter XII]
). The mean square of
\Delta_{k}(x)
has been computed by Cramér
[
Cra22
]
for
k=2
and by Tong
[
Ton56
]
for
k\geq 3
(assuming the Riemann Hypothesis for
k\geq 4
) and was found to be

\frac{1}{X}\int_{X}^{2X}\Delta_{k}(x)^{2}dx\sim c_{k}X^{1-\frac{1}{k}},

for certain constants
c_{k}
. Heath-Brown
[
HB92
]
proved that
\Delta_{k}(x)/x^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2k}}
has a non-Gaussian limiting value distribution.

Two particular problems have attracted attention: the distribution of the divisor function in short intervals and the distribution of sums of the divisor function over arithmetic progressions.

The distribution in short intervals problem involves studying

\Delta_{k}(x;H):=\Delta_{k}(x+H)-\Delta_{k}(x),

where
H<X^{1-1/k}
.

In
[
KRRGR18
, Conjecture 1.1]
, Keating, Rodgers, Roditty-Gershon, and Rudnick conjectured, based on their results over function fields, that if

0<\delta<1-\frac{1}{k}
is fixed, then for
H=X^{\delta}
, as
X\rightarrow\infty
,

(1)

\frac{1}{X}\int_{X}^{2X}\Delta_{k}(x,H)^{2}dx\sim a_{k}{\mathcal{P}}_{k}(\delta)H(\log H)^{k^{2}-1},

where

(2)

a_{k}=\prod_{p}\left(\Big(1-\frac{1}{p}\Big)^{k^{2}}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\Big(\frac{\Gamma(k+j)}{\Gamma(k)j!}\Big)^{2}\frac{1}{p^{j}}\right)

and
{\mathcal{P}}_{k}(\delta)
is a piecewise polynomial function of
\delta
, of degree
k^{2}-1
, given by

{\mathcal{P}}_{k}(\delta)=(1-\delta)^{k^{2}-1}\gamma_{k}\left(\frac{1}{1-\delta}\right).

Here

(3)

\gamma_{k}(c)=\frac{1}{k!G(1+k)^{2}}\int_{[0,1]^{k}}\delta_{c}(w_{1}+\cdots+w_{k})\prod_{i<j}(w_{i}-w_{j})^{2}d^{k}w,

where
\delta_{c}(w)=\delta(w-c)
is the delta distribution translated by
c
, and
G
is the Barnes
G
-function.

This conjecture is consistent with a Theorem of Lester
[
Les16
]
for the interval
1-\frac{1}{k-1}<\delta<1-\frac{1}{k}
. Further evidence was found by Bettin and Conrey
[
BC21
]
who proved that the Shifted moments conjecture for the Riemann zeta function implies that the main term of the variance of
\Delta_{k}(x,H)
has coefficient
a_{k}\gamma_{k}(c)
, as predicted by (
1
).

The distribution over arithmetic progressions involves studying

\mathcal{S}_{d_{k};X;Q}(A)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq X\\
n\equiv A\,(\mathrm{mod}\,Q)\end{subarray}}d_{k}(n).

The variance
\mathrm{Var}(\mathcal{S}_{d_{k};X;Q})
has been studied by Motohashi
[
Mot73
]
, Blomer
[
Blo08
]
, and Lau and Zhao
[
LZ12
]
, for
k=2
and Kowalski and Ricotta
[
KR14b
]
for
k\geq 3
.

In
[
KRRGR18
, Conjecture 3.3]
Keating, Rodgers, Roditty-Gershon, and Rudnick also conjectured, based on results that they obtained over function fields, that for
Q
prime,
Q^{1+\varepsilon}<X<Q^{k-\varepsilon}
, as
X\rightarrow\infty
,

(4)

\mathrm{Var}(\mathcal{S}_{d_{k};X;Q})\sim\frac{X}{Q}a_{k}\gamma_{k}\left(\frac{\log X}{\log Q}\right)(\log Q)^{k^{2}-1},

where
a_{k}
is given by (
2
) and
\gamma_{k}
is given by (
3
).

Bettin and Conrey
[
BC21
]
proved that the Shifted moments conjecture for Dirichlet
L
-functions implies that the main term of
\mathrm{Var}(\mathcal{S}_{d_{k};X;Q})
has coefficient
a_{k}\gamma_{k}(c)
, as predicted by (
4
). In sum,
[
BC21
]
shows that a uniform version of the Shifted moments conjecture implies both Conjectures 1.1 and 3.3. from
[
KRRGR18
]
.

The same problems can be posed in the ring of polynomials
\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]
, where
q
is an odd prime power. The benefit of working in the function field setting is that many of the analogous conjectures are provable. In
\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]
,
d_{k}(f)
is defined for a monic polynomial
f
to be

d_{k}(f):=\#\{(f_{1},\dots,f_{k})\,:\,f=f_{1}\cdots f_{k},\,f_{j}\mbox{ monic}\}.

Let
U
be an
N\times N
matrix. The secular coefficients
\mathrm{Sc}_{j}(U)
are the coefficients of the characteristic polynomial of
U
:

\det(1+xU)=\sum_{j=0}^{N}\mathrm{Sc}_{j}(U)x^{j}.

Keating, Rodgers, Roditty-Gershon, and Rudnick prove in Theorem 1.2 of
[
KRRGR18
]
that for
0\leq h\leq\min\{n-5,(1-\frac{1}{k})n-2\}
, as
q\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{q^{n}}\sum_{A\in\mathcal{M}_{n}}

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{f\in I(A;h)}d_{k}(f)-q^{h+1}\binom{n+k-1}{k-1}\right|^{2}

(5)

\displaystyle=q^{h}\int_{\mathrm{U}(n-h-2)}\left|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1}+\cdots+j_{k}=n\\
0\leq j_{1},\dots,j_{k}\leq n-h-2\end{subarray}}\mathrm{Sc}_{j_{1}}(U)\cdots\mathrm{Sc}_{j_{k}}(U)\right|^{2}dU+O(q^{h-\frac{1}{2}}).

Here the integral is over the group
U(N)
of
N\times N
unitary matrices with respect to the Haar probability measure. They also showed in Theorem 3.1 of
[
KRRGR18
]
that for
Q
squarefree and
n\leq k(\deg(Q)-1)
,

\displaystyle\lim_{q\to\infty}\frac{|Q|}{q^{n}}\frac{1}{\Phi(Q)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}A\,(\mathrm{mod}\,Q)\\
\gcd(A,Q)=1\end{subarray}}

\displaystyle\left|\mathcal{S}_{d_{k},n,Q}(A)-\langle\mathcal{S}_{d_{k},n,Q}\rangle\right|^{2}

(6)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathrm{U}(\deg(Q)-1)}\left|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1}+\cdots+j_{k}=n\\
0\leq j_{1},\dots,j_{k}\leq\deg(Q)-1\end{subarray}}\mathrm{Sc}_{j_{1}}(U)\cdots\mathrm{Sc}_{j_{k}}(U)\right|^{2}dU.

The connection with Conjectures 1.1 and 3.3 is outlined in Theorem 1.5 of
[
KRRGR18
]
, which says that if
c=m/N
, for
c\in[0,k]
,

(7)

\int_{\mathrm{U}(N)}\left|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1}+\cdots+j_{k}=m\\
0\leq j_{1},\dots,j_{k}\leq N\end{subarray}}\mathrm{Sc}_{j_{1}}(U)\cdots\mathrm{Sc}_{j_{k}}(U)\right|^{2}dU=\gamma_{k}(c)N^{k^{2}-1}+O_{k}(N^{k^{2}-2}).

Here again, the coefficient
\gamma_{k}(c)
is given by equation
3
.

Both arithmetic questions above lead to distributions over the whole unitary group
U(N)
. Our aim is to present two similar questions which instead lead to distributions on the unitary symplectic group
\mathrm{Sp}(N)
.

The first question that we consider is the distribution of
d_{k}(f)
when restricted to quadratic residues modulo an irreducible polynomial
P
. We use the notation that
\mathcal{P}_{2g+1}
is the set of monic irreducible polynomials of degree
2g+1
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
n\leq 2gk
. Let

\mathcal{S}^{S}_{d_{k},n}(P):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\,\text{monic},\deg(f)=n\\
f\equiv\square\,(\mathrm{mod}\,P)\\
P\nmid f\end{subarray}}d_{k}(f),

where
P
is a monic irreducible polynomial of degree
2g+1
. As
q\rightarrow\infty
,

\mathcal{S}^{S}_{d_{k},n}(P)\sim\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in\mathcal{M}_{n}\\
P\nmid f\end{subarray}}d_{k}(f)\sim\frac{q^{n}}{2}\binom{k+n-1}{k-1},

and

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\#\mathcal{P}_{2g+1}}\sum_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{2g+1}}\left|\mathcal{S}^{S}_{d_{k},n}(P)-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in\mathcal{M}_{n}\\
P\nmid f\end{subarray}}d_{k}(f)\right|^{2}\sim

\displaystyle\frac{q^{n}}{4}\int_{\mathrm{Sp}(2g)}\left|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1}+\cdots+j_{k}=n\\
0\leq j_{1},\dots,j_{k}\leq 2g\end{subarray}}\mathrm{Sc}_{j_{1}}(U)\cdots\mathrm{Sc}_{j_{k}}(U)\right|^{2}dU.

The restriction to quadratic residues modulo an irreducible polynomial
P
can be detected by twisting by a quadratic character. The presence of these quadratic twists ultimately yields the connection to a symplectic matrix integral. One could also consider the sum over twists by a quadratic character to a not-necessarily-irreducible modulus (as opposed to our sums, where the modulus must be an irreducible polynomial
P
). These sums yield similar results, although for simplicity we restrict our attention here to the sums with irreducible modulus.

One quirk of this choice is that its proof relies on the fact that the family
y^{2}=P(x)
, where
P(x)
is a monic irreducible polynomial of fixed odd degree
2g+1
, has monodromy group
\mathrm{Sp}(2g)
. This is a more restrictive family than the somewhat more standard family
y^{2}=f(x)
, for
f
a monic squarefree polynomial of fixed odd degree
2g+1
. This is derived from the monodromy group of the hyperelliptic ensemble, by an argument that was provided to us by Katz; the monodromy arguments involved are presented in Section
3
.

Analogously to the distribution over short intervals, we consider

\mathcal{N}^{S}_{d_{\ell},k,\nu}(v)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\,\text{monic},\deg(f)=\nu\\
f(0)\not=0\\
U(f)\in\mathrm{Sect}(v,k)\end{subarray}}d_{\ell}(f),

where the sum is taken over monic polynomials of fixed degree with certain condition (see (
17
)) that can be interpreted as the function field analogue of having the argument of a complex number lying in certain specific sector of the unit circle. This follows the model of Gaussian integers in the function field context that was considered by Rudnick and Waxman in
[
RW19
]
and initially developped by Bary-Soroker, Smilansky, and Wolf in
[
BSSW16
]
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\nu\leq\ell(2\kappa-2)
with
\kappa=\left\lfloor\frac{k}{2}\right\rfloor
. As
q\rightarrow\infty
,

\langle\mathcal{N}^{S}_{d_{\ell},k,\nu}\rangle\sim q^{\nu-\kappa}\binom{\ell+\nu-1}{\ell-1},

and

\frac{1}{q^{\kappa}}\sum_{u\in\mathbb{S}_{k}^{1}}\left|\mathcal{N}^{S}_{d_{\ell},k,\nu}(u)-\langle\mathcal{N}^{S}_{d_{\ell},k,\nu}\rangle\right|^{2}\sim\frac{q^{\nu}}{q^{\kappa}}\int_{\mathrm{Sp}(2\kappa-2)}\left|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1}+\cdots+j_{\ell}=\nu\\
0\leq j_{1},\dots,j_{\ell}\leq 2\kappa-2\end{subarray}}\mathrm{Sc}_{j_{1}}(U)\cdots\mathrm{Sc}_{j_{\ell}}(U)\right|^{2}dU.

This result also rests on a monodromy theorem due to Katz
[
Kat17
]
.

We also study parallel results coming from the distribution of
k
-fold convolutions of the von Mangoldt function rather than from the distribution of the divisor function
d_{k}
. The
von Mangoldt function

\Lambda(d)
over
\mathbb{Z}
is defined as
\Lambda(n)=\log p
if
n=p^{k}
for
p
a prime number, and
0
otherwise. It arises as the coefficients of the logarithmic derivative of the Riemann zeta function:

-\frac{\zeta^{\prime}(s)}{\zeta(s)}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\Lambda(n)}{n^{s}}.

The Prime Number Theorem says that

\psi(x):=\sum_{n\leq x}\Lambda(n)\sim x\mbox{ as }x\rightarrow\infty.

The distribution of primes in short intervals involves studying

\psi(x,H):=\sum_{x-\frac{H}{2}\leq n\leq x+\frac{H}{2}}\Lambda(n),

for
1\leq H\leq x
, which due to the Prime Number Theorem has expected value
H
. The Riemann Hypothesis gives the estimate
\psi(x;H)\sim H
for
H>x^{\frac{1}{2}+o(1)}
. Goldston and Montgomery
[
GM87
]
proved that for
X^{\delta}<H<X^{1-\delta}
,

(8)

\frac{1}{X}\int_{2}^{X}|\psi(x;H)-H|^{2}dx\sim H(\log X-\log H),

under the Riemann Hypothesis and the strong pair correlation conjecture.

Meanwhile the distribution of primes in arithmetic progressions involves studying

\psi(X;Q,A):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq X\\
n\equiv A\,(\mathrm{mod}\,Q)\end{subarray}}\Lambda(n).

The Prime Number Theorem in arithmetic progressions states that for
Q
fixed,

\psi(X;Q,A)\sim\frac{X}{\phi(Q)},\mbox{ as }X\rightarrow\infty,

so the variance is given by

G(X,Q)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}A\,(\mathrm{mod}\,Q)\\
(A,Q)=1\end{subarray}}\left|\psi(X;Q,A)-\frac{X}{\phi(Q)}\right|^{2}.

The value of
G(X,Q)
has been widely studied. Hooley
[
Hoo75
]
conjectured that, under certain unspecified conditions,

(9)

G(X,Q)\sim X\log Q.

Friedlander and Goldston
[
FG96
]
conjectured that (
9
) holds for
X^{1/2+\varepsilon}<Q<X
and proved it assuming a Hardy-Littlewood conjecture with small reminders. The barrier of
X\leq Q^{1/2}
seems to be hard to overcome.

Keating and Rudnick
[
KR14a
]
prove analogues of both (
8
) and (
9
) for function fields. Rudnick and Waxman
[
RW19
]
study analogues in the number field case, considering the variance of primes in sectors.

For a monic polynomial
f\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]
monic, we define

\Lambda_{k}(f):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}f_{1}\cdots f_{k}=f\\
f_{i}\text{ monic}\end{subarray}}\Lambda(f_{1})\cdots\Lambda(f_{k}),

where
\Lambda(f)
is the von Mangoldt function

\Lambda(f)=\begin{cases}\deg(P)&f=P^{k},\\
0&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

and we let
\Lambda_{k}(cf):=\Lambda_{k}(f)
for
f\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]
monic and
c\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
.

The following result in function fields corresponds to equation (
1
).

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
A\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]
be a monic polynomial of degree
n
, let
0\leq h\leq n-2
, and define

\mathcal{N}^{U}_{0,\Lambda_{k}}(A;h):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\text{ monic}\\
|f-A|\leq q^{h}\\
f(0)\neq 0\end{subarray}}\Lambda_{k}(f).

Assume
k\leq n
. Then as
q\rightarrow\infty
,

\langle\mathcal{N}^{U}_{0,\Lambda_{k}}\rangle\sim q^{h+1}\binom{n-1}{k-1},

and

\frac{1}{q^{n}}\sum_{A\in\mathcal{M}_{n}}\left|\mathcal{N}^{U}_{0,\Lambda_{k}}(A;h)-\langle\mathcal{N}^{U}_{0,\Lambda_{k}}\rangle\right|^{2}\sim q^{h+1}\int_{\mathrm{U}(n-h-2)}\left|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1}+\cdots+j_{k}=n\\
1\leq j_{1},\dots,j_{k}\end{subarray}}\mathrm{Tr}(U^{j_{1}})\cdots\mathrm{Tr}(U^{j_{k}})\right|^{2}dU.

In
[
Rod15
, Theorem 1.3]
, Rodgers established a more general result about a slightly different function. He considered the covariance of higher-order von Mangoldt functions, defined recursively by

\Lambda^{0}_{k}(f):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\,\text{monic}\\
d\mid f\end{subarray}}\Lambda^{0}_{k-1}(d)\Lambda(f/d)+\Lambda^{0}_{k-1}(f)\deg(f),

and later developed a more general method to compute the variances of general arithmetic functions in short intervals
[
Rod18
]
.

Looking at the
k
-fold convolution in arithmetic progressions instead of short intervals, we get the following result, corresponding to equation (
1
).

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
Q\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]
be square-free and
A\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]
be coprime to
Q
, and define

\mathcal{S}^{U}_{\Lambda_{k},n,Q}(A):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\text{monic},\,\deg(f)=n\\
f\equiv A\,(\mathrm{mod}\,Q)\end{subarray}}\Lambda_{k}(f).

Then, for
k\leq n
and as
q\rightarrow\infty
,

\mathcal{S}^{U}_{\Lambda_{k},n,Q}(A)\sim\frac{1}{\Phi(Q)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in\mathcal{M}_{n}\\
(f,Q)=1\end{subarray}}\Lambda_{k}(f)\sim\frac{q^{n}}{\Phi(Q)}\binom{n-1}{k-1},

and

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\Phi(Q)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}A\,(\mathrm{mod}\,Q)\\
(A,Q)=1\end{subarray}}

\displaystyle\left|\mathcal{S}^{U}_{\Lambda_{k},n,Q}(A)-\frac{1}{\Phi(Q)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in\mathcal{M}_{n}\\
(f,Q)=1\end{subarray}}\Lambda_{k}(f)\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\sim\frac{q^{n}}{|Q|}\int_{\mathrm{U}(\deg(Q)-1)}\left|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1}+\cdots+j_{k}=n\\
1\leq j_{1},\dots,j_{k}\end{subarray}}\mathrm{Tr}(U^{j_{1}})\cdots\mathrm{Tr}(U^{j_{k}})\right|^{2}dU.

Meanwhile we can also consider the following two problems, which connect arithmetic questions about the
k
-fold convolutions of the von Mangoldt function to symplectic matrix integrals.

Theorem 1.5
.

For an irreducible polynomial
P
of degree
2g+1
, let

\mathcal{S}^{S}_{\Lambda_{k},n}(P):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\text{ monic},\,\deg f=n\\
f\equiv\square\,(\mathrm{mod}\,P)\\
P\nmid f\end{subarray}}\Lambda_{k}(f).

Assume that
k\leq n
. As
q\rightarrow\infty
,

\mathcal{S}^{S}_{\Lambda_{k},n}(P)\sim\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in\mathcal{M}_{n}\\
P\nmid f\end{subarray}}\Lambda_{k}(f)\sim\frac{q^{n}}{2}\binom{n-1}{k-1},

and

\frac{1}{\#\mathcal{P}_{2g+1}}\sum_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{2g+1}}\left|\mathcal{S}^{S}_{\Lambda_{k},n}(P)-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in\mathcal{M}_{n}\\
P\nmid f\end{subarray}}\Lambda_{k}(f)\right|^{2}\sim\frac{q^{n}}{4}\int_{\mathrm{Sp}(2g)}\left|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1}+\cdots+j_{k}=n\\
1\leq j_{1},\dots,j_{k}\end{subarray}}\mathrm{Tr}(U^{j_{1}})\cdots\mathrm{Tr}(U^{j_{k}})\right|^{2}dU.

Theorem 1.6
.

Let

\mathcal{N}^{S}_{\Lambda_{\ell},k,\nu}(v)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\text{ monic},\,\deg f=\nu\\
f(0)\neq 0\\
U(f)\in\mathrm{Sect}(v,k)\end{subarray}}\Lambda_{\ell}(f).

Assume that
\ell\leq\nu
. Then as
q\rightarrow\infty
,

\langle\mathcal{N}^{S}_{\Lambda_{\ell},k,\nu}\rangle\sim q^{\nu-\kappa}\binom{\nu-1}{\ell-1},

and

\frac{1}{q^{\kappa}}\sum_{u\in\mathbb{S}_{k}^{1}}\left|\mathcal{N}^{S}_{d_{\ell},k,\nu}(u)-\langle\mathcal{N}^{S}_{\Lambda_{\ell},k,\nu}\rangle\right|^{2}\sim\frac{q^{\nu}}{q^{\kappa}}\int_{\mathrm{Sp}(2\kappa-2)}\left|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1}+\cdots+j_{\ell}=\nu\\
1\leq j_{1},\dots,j_{\ell}\end{subarray}}\mathrm{Tr}(U^{j_{1}})\cdots\mathrm{Tr}(U^{j_{\ell}})\right|^{2}dU,

where
\kappa:=\left\lfloor\frac{k}{2}\right\rfloor
.

As we will explore in Section
2
, these two functions are tractable because they appear naturally as coefficients of Dirichlet
L
-functions. More specifically, the divisor function
d_{k}
appears as coefficients of the
k
th power
\mathcal{L}(u,\chi)^{k}
while the von Mangoldt
convolution
\Lambda_{k}
appears as coefficients of the
k
th power of the logarithmic derivative
\left(\frac{u\mathcal{L}^{\prime}(u,\chi)}{\mathcal{L}(u,\chi)}\right)^{k}
. In this way the divisor function and the
k
-fold von Mangoldt convolution arise naturally in the study of moments and of low level densities.

This paper is organized as follows. Section
2
includes some necessary background on Dirichlet characters and
L
-functions over function fields. Sections
3
and
4
describe two problems that will lead to symplectic distributions in the limit when
q\to\infty
, as we will discuss in Sections
5
and
6
. Section
3
presents an equidistribution result of Katz, while Section
4
describes the framework of short intervals on the unit circle in the function field case. Sections
5
and
6
discuss averages of the divisor function and the von Mangoldt convolution, respectively; in each section, we study these averages over quadratic residues modulo a monic irreducible polynomial
P
and over short intervals on the unit circle. Finally, Section
7
treats the distribution of von Mangoldt convolutions over short intervals and over arithmetic progressions, which leads to unitary distributions in the limit when
q\rightarrow\infty
. In each of Sections
5
,
6
, and
7
,
we include a subsection discussing the random matrix theory point of view, which includes the computation of some lower cases of relevant integrals.

2.
Preliminaries on Dirichlet characters and
L
-functions

In this section we describe a general framework of
L
-functions over function fields that will be used as generating series for the arithmetic functions under consideration. For a general reference, see
[
Ros02
]
. We consider the ring of polynomials
\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]
, where
q
is an odd prime power. Let
\mathcal{M}
denote the set of monic polynomials in
\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]
, and
\mathcal{M}_{n}
the subset of
\mathcal{M}
of polynomials of degree
n
. Similarly, let
\mathcal{P}
denote the set of monic irreducible polynomials over
\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]
, and
\mathcal{P}_{n}
the corresponding subset of irreducible polynomials of degree
n
. The norm of a polynomial
f\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]
is defined as
|f|:=q^{\deg(f)}
. The zeta function for
\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]
is given by

\zeta_{q}(s)=\sum_{f\in\mathcal{M}}\frac{1}{|f|^{s}}=\prod_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\left(1-\frac{1}{|P|^{s}}\right)^{-1}=\frac{1}{1-q^{1-s}}.

While the initial sum and Euler product converge for
\re(s)>1
, the right-hand side identity provides a meromorphic continuation with a single pole at
s=1
.
By applying the change of variables
u=q^{-s}
, we can also write

\mathcal{Z}(u)=\sum_{f\in\mathcal{M}}u^{\deg(f)}=\frac{1}{1-qu}.

Let
\chi
be a Dirichlet character on
\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]
of conductor
m\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]
.
\chi
is said to be even if
\chi(\alpha f)=\chi(f)
for all
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
and odd otherwise.

The
L
-function corresponding to
\chi
is then given by

L(s,\chi):=\sum_{f\in\mathcal{M}}\frac{\chi(f)}{|f|^{s}}=\prod_{p\in\mathcal{P}}\left(1-\frac{\chi(P)}{|P|^{s}}\right)^{-1},

which converges for
\re(s)>1
. As before, we can also write

\mathcal{L}(u,\chi)=\sum_{f\in\mathcal{M}}\chi(f)u^{\deg(f)}.

Then
\mathcal{L}(u,\chi)
is a polynomial of degree
\delta\leq\deg(m)-1

(
[
Ros02
, Proposition 4.3]
). The Riemann hypothesis implies that

(10)

\mathcal{L}(u,\chi)=(1-u)^{\lambda}\det(1-uq^{1/2}\Theta_{\chi}),

where
\lambda=0
if
\chi
is odd and
1
if
\chi
is even, and
\Theta_{\chi}
is a unitary matrix of dimension
N=\delta-\lambda
.

For an
N\times N
matrix
U
, the secular coefficients
\mathrm{Sc}_{j}(U)
are defined by

(11)

\det(I+xU)=\sum_{j=0}^{N}\mathrm{Sc}_{j}(U)x^{j}.

Thus, the coefficients of
\mathcal{L}(u,\chi)
can be expressed in terms of the secular coefficients of
\Theta_{\chi}
.

The divisor function arises naturally via

(12)

\mathcal{L}(u,\chi)^{k}=\sum_{f\in\mathcal{M}}d_{k}(f)\chi(f)u^{\deg(f)}.

Note that
\mathcal{L}(u,\chi)^{k}
is a polynomial of degree
k\delta
.

We define

M(n;d_{k}\chi)=\sum_{f\in\mathcal{M}_{n}}d_{k}(f)\chi(f).

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\chi
be odd. For
n\leq k\delta
we have

M(n;d_{k}\chi)=(-1)^{n}q^{n/2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1}+\cdots+j_{k}=n\\
0\leq j_{1},\dots j_{k}\leq\delta\end{subarray}}\mathrm{Sc}_{j_{1}}(\Theta_{\chi})\cdots\mathrm{Sc}_{j_{k}}(\Theta_{\chi})

and
M(n;d_{k}\chi)=0
otherwise.

Let
\chi
be even. For
n\leq k\delta
, as
q\to\infty
we have

M(n;d_{k}\chi)=(-1)^{n}q^{n/2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1}+\cdots+j_{k}=n\\
0\leq j_{1},\dots j_{k}\leq\delta\end{subarray}}\mathrm{Sc}_{j_{1}}(\Theta_{\chi})\cdots\mathrm{Sc}_{j_{k}}(\Theta_{\chi})+O_{k,\delta}\left(q^{\frac{n-1}{2}}\right).

For
k\delta<n\leq k(\delta+1)
, as
q\to\infty
,

|M(n;d_{k}\chi)|\ll_{k,\delta}q^{\frac{n-1}{2}}.

Finally,
M(n;d_{k}\chi)=0
for
n>k(\delta+1)
.

Proof.

First consider the case of
\chi
odd. This result is analogous to Lemma 2.2 of
[
KRRGR18
]
.

From (
10
) (with
\lambda=0
) and (
11
) we get

\mathcal{L}(u,\chi)^{k}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq j_{1},\dots,j_{k}\leq\delta\end{subarray}}(-1)^{j_{1}+\cdots+j_{k}}q^{(j_{1}+\cdots+j_{k})/2}\mathrm{Sc}_{j_{1}}(\Theta_{\chi})\cdots\mathrm{Sc}_{j_{k}}(\Theta_{\chi})u^{j_{1}+\cdots+j_{k}}.

Comparing coefficients with equation (
12
), we obtain the conclusion in the case
n\leq\delta k
. If
n>k\delta
, the coefficients must be
0
, because
\deg\mathcal{L}(u,\chi)^{k}=k\delta
.

Now consider the case of
\chi
even. By (
10
) (with
\lambda=1
) and (
11
) we get

\frac{\mathcal{L}(u,\chi)^{k}}{(1-u)^{k}}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq j_{1},\dots,j_{k}\leq\delta\end{subarray}}(-1)^{j_{1}+\cdots+j_{k}}q^{(j_{1}+\cdots+j_{k})/2}\mathrm{Sc}_{j_{1}}(\Theta_{\chi})\cdots\mathrm{Sc}_{j_{k}}(\Theta_{\chi})u^{j_{1}+\cdots+j_{k}}.

and

(13)

\mathcal{L}(u,\chi)^{k}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq j_{1},\dots,j_{k}\leq\delta\\
0\leq h\leq k\end{subarray}}(-1)^{j_{1}+\cdots+j_{k}+h}\binom{k}{h}q^{(j_{1}+\cdots+j_{k})/2}\mathrm{Sc}_{j_{1}}(\Theta_{\chi})\cdots\mathrm{Sc}_{j_{k}}(\Theta_{\chi})u^{j_{1}+\cdots+j_{k}+h}.

By combining (
13
) and the definition of
M(n;d_{k}\chi)
,

(14)

M(n;d_{k}\chi)=(-1)^{n}q^{n/2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1}+\cdots+j_{k}+h=n\\
0\leq j_{1},\dots,j_{k}\leq\delta\\
0\leq h\leq k\end{subarray}}\binom{k}{h}q^{-h/2}\mathrm{Sc}_{j_{1}}(\Theta_{\chi})\cdots\mathrm{Sc}_{j_{k}}(\Theta_{\chi}).

By the Riemann hypothesis,
|\mathrm{Sc}_{r}(\Theta_{\chi})|\leq\binom{\delta-1}{r}
. Thus when
n\leq k\delta
, the dominating term in equation (
14
) is the one corresponding to
h=0
, leading to the estimate in this case.

When
k\delta<n\leq k(\delta+1)
, each term in (
14
) must have
h\geq 1
, leading to the bound. Finally, the case when
k(\delta+1)<n
follows from the bound on the degree of
\mathcal{L}(u,\chi)
.
∎

We now turn our attention to the
k
-fold convolution of the von Mangoldt function. Just as we did for the divisor function, we can consider

(15)

\left(\frac{u\mathcal{L}^{\prime}(u,\chi)}{\mathcal{L}(u,\chi)}\right)^{k}=\sum_{f\in\mathcal{M}}\Lambda_{k}(f)\chi(f)u^{\deg(f)},

where for
f
monic,

\Lambda_{k}(f)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}f_{1}\cdots f_{k}=f\\
f_{i}\text{ monic}\end{subarray}}\Lambda(f_{1})\cdots\Lambda(f_{k})

is the convolution of the von Mangoldt function

\Lambda(f)=\begin{cases}\deg(P)&f=P^{k},\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Note that while
\mathcal{L}(u,\chi)^{k}
is a polynomial,
\left(\frac{u\mathcal{L}^{\prime}(u,\chi)}{\mathcal{L}(u,\chi)}\right)^{k}
is not.

We define

M(n;\Lambda_{k}\chi)=\sum_{f\in\mathcal{M}_{n}}\Lambda_{k}(f)\chi(f).

Previously we could express
M(n;d_{k}\chi)
as a sum of secular coefficients; here in turn we express
M(n;\Lambda_{k}\chi)
as a sum of traces.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
\chi
be odd. For
k\leq n
, we have

M(n;\Lambda_{k}\chi)=(-1)^{k}q^{n/2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1}+\cdots+j_{k}=n\\
1\leq j_{1},\dots j_{k}\end{subarray}}\mathrm{Tr}(\Theta_{\chi}^{j_{1}})\cdots\mathrm{Tr}(\Theta_{\chi}^{j_{k}})

and
M(n;\Lambda_{k}\chi)=0
for
1\leq n<k
.

Let
\chi
be even. For
k\leq n
, as
q\to\infty
we have

M(n;\Lambda_{k}\chi)=(-1)^{k}q^{n/2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1}+\cdots+j_{k}=n\\
1\leq j_{1},\dots j_{k}\end{subarray}}\mathrm{Tr}(\Theta_{\chi}^{j_{1}})\cdots\mathrm{Tr}(\Theta_{\chi}^{j_{k}})+O_{n,k,\delta}\left(q^{\frac{n-1}{2}}\right)

and
M(n;d_{k}\chi)=0
for
1\leq n<k
.

Proof.

First suppose that
\chi
is odd. By taking the logarithm in equation (
10
), we have

\log\mathcal{L}(u,\chi)=\sum_{\theta\in\mathrm{Spec}(\Theta_{\chi})}\log(1-uq^{1/2}\theta).

The logarithmic derivative then gives

\frac{u\mathcal{L}^{\prime}(u,\chi)}{\mathcal{L}(u,\chi)}=-\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\sum_{\theta\in\mathrm{Spec}(\Theta_{\chi})}\theta^{j}q^{j/2}u^{j}=-\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\mathrm{Tr}(\Theta_{\chi}^{j})q^{j/2}u^{j}.

Finally, the
k
-th power yields

\left(\frac{u\mathcal{L}^{\prime}(u,\chi_{D})}{\mathcal{L}(u,\chi_{D})}\right)^{k}=(-1)^{k}\sum_{n=k}^{\infty}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1}+\cdots+j_{k}=n\\
1\leq j_{1},\dots,j_{k}\end{subarray}}\mathrm{Tr}(\Theta_{C_{D}}^{j_{1}})\cdots\mathrm{Tr}(\Theta_{C_{D}}^{j_{k}})q^{n/2}u^{n}.

The result follows by comparing the coefficients of
u^{n}
in the above equation with those of equation (
15
).

Now suppose that
\chi
is even.
Taking the logarithm in (
10
), we obtain,

\log\mathcal{L}(u,\chi)=\log(1-u)+\sum_{\theta\in\mathrm{Spec}(\Theta_{\chi})}\log(1-uq^{1/2}\theta).

Then taking the derivative, we get

\frac{u\mathcal{L}^{\prime}(u,\chi)}{\mathcal{L}(u,\chi)}=-\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\left(1+\sum_{\theta\in\mathrm{Spec}(\Theta_{\chi})}\theta^{j}q^{j/2}\right)u^{j}=-\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}(1+\mathrm{Tr}(\Theta_{\chi}^{j})q^{j/2})u^{j}.

Finally, we obtain for the
k
-th power,

\displaystyle\left(\frac{u\mathcal{L}^{\prime}(u,\chi)}{\mathcal{L}(u,\chi)}\right)^{k}=

\displaystyle(-1)^{k}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}(1+\mathrm{Tr}(\Theta_{\chi}^{j})q^{j/2})u^{j}\right)^{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-1)^{k}\sum_{n=k}^{\infty}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1}+\cdots+j_{k}=n\\
1\leq j_{1},\dots j_{k}\end{subarray}}(1+\mathrm{Tr}(\Theta_{\chi}^{j_{1}})q^{j_{1}/2})\cdots(1+\mathrm{Tr}(\Theta_{\chi}^{j_{k}})q^{j_{k}/2})u^{n}

By comparing coefficients with those of equation (
15
), we obtain

\displaystyle M(n;\Lambda_{k}\chi)=

\displaystyle(-1)^{k}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1}+\cdots+j_{k}=n\\
1\leq j_{1},\dots j_{k}\end{subarray}}(1+\mathrm{Tr}(\Theta_{\chi}^{j_{1}})q^{j_{1}/2})\cdots(1+\mathrm{Tr}(\Theta_{\chi}^{j_{k}})q^{j_{k}/2}).

Expanding the product of traces yields the main term,
q^{n/2}\mathrm{Tr}(\Theta_{\chi}^{j_{1}})\cdots\mathrm{Tr}(\Theta_{\chi}^{j_{k}})
; every other term in the expansion will be
O_{k,\chi,n}(q^{\frac{n-1}{2}}).

∎

3.
An equidistribution result

In this section we present an equidistribution result for the family of
Y^{2}=P(T)
, where
P\in\mathcal{P}
. Denote by
\mathcal{H}
the set of monic, square-free polynomials over
\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]
and by
\mathcal{H}_{n}
the corresponding subset of polynomials of degree
n
. In this section we will let
n=2g+1
be an odd number.

Katz and Sarnak
[
KS99
, Theorems 9.2.6, 10.1.18.3]
showed that for any continuous
\mathbb{C}
-valued central function
F
on the symplectic group
\mathrm{Sp}(2g)
,

\lim_{q\to\infty}\langle F(\Theta_{Q})\rangle=\int_{\mathrm{Sp}(2g)}F(U)dU,

where

\langle F\rangle:=\frac{1}{\#\mathcal{H}_{2g+1}}\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{H}_{2g+1}}F(Q).

We will be interested in working with the family
\mathcal{P}_{2g+1}
, rather than
\mathcal{H}_{2g+1}
, so we need a variant of their result. In order to state the next result, we fix some notation. Let
(d_{1},d_{2},\dots,d_{n})
be a partition of
2g+1
, where we use the convention
d_{1}\geq\cdots\geq d_{n}
.

We define the sets

\mathcal{H}_{d_{1},\dots,d_{n}}=\left\{f\in\mathcal{H}_{2g+1}:f=\prod_{i}^{n}f_{d_{i}},f_{d_{i}}\mbox{ monic, }\deg(f_{d_{i}})=d_{i}\right\}

and

\mathcal{P}_{d_{1},\dots,d_{n}}=\left\{f\in\mathcal{H}_{2g+1}:f=\prod_{i}^{n}f_{d_{i}},f_{d_{i}}\mbox{ monic and irreducible }\deg(f_{d_{i}})=d_{i}\right\}.

That is,
\mathcal{P}_{d_{1},\dots,d_{n}}
is the family of monic, square-free polynomials of degree
2g+1
with factorization pattern
(d_{1},\dots,d_{n})
, while
\mathcal{H}_{d_{1},\dots,d_{n}}
is the family of monic, square-free polynomials of degree
2g+1
with factorization pattern
(d_{1},\dots,d_{n})
as well as its refinements. More precisely, let
\rho,\sigma
be two partitions of
2g+1
. We write
\sigma\leq\rho
if
\sigma
refines
\rho
. Then

\mathcal{H}_{\rho}=\bigsqcup_{\sigma\leq\rho}\mathcal{P}_{\sigma}.

We will prove that the family
y^{2}=f(x)
with
f(x)\in\mathcal{P}_{d_{1},\dots,d_{n}}
also has monodromy group
\mathrm{Sp}(2g)
, and therefore satisfies the same equidistribution result as
\mathcal{H}_{2g+1}
.
To do this, we will first show that the family
y^{2}=f(x)
with
f(x)\in\mathcal{H}_{d_{1},\dots,d_{n}}
has monodromy group
\mathrm{Sp}(2g)
.

Theorem 3.1

(Katz, personal communication)
.

Let
F
be a continuous
\mathbb{C}
-valued central function on
\mathrm{Sp}(2g)
and let
\sigma
be any fixed partition of
2g+1
. Then

\lim_{q\to\infty}\frac{1}{\#\mathcal{H}_{\sigma}}\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{H}_{\sigma}}F(Q)=\int_{\mathrm{Sp}(2g)}F(U)dU.

Proof.

We know that the monodromy group of the space of
y^{2}=f(x)
with
f(x)\in\mathcal{H}_{d_{1},\dots,d_{n}}
is a subgroup of
\mathrm{Sp}(2g)
. We would like to show that this monodromy group is in fact the full group. However, the monodromy group of a pullback is a subgroup of the original monodromy group, so it suffices to show that after an appropriate choice of pullback, we have the full monodromy group
\mathrm{Sp}(2g)
.

To do that, we fix a polynomial
f_{2g}(x)\in\mathcal{H}_{d_{1}-1,\dots,d_{n}}
. Consider the space of
y^{2}=f(x)
with
f(x)=(x-t)f_{2g}(x)
, with a single parameter
t
. We restrict this space to the open subset of
t
where
f_{2g}(t)\neq 0
. We now would like to map this restricted space to the space of
y^{2}=f(x)
with
f(x)\in\mathcal{H}_{d_{1},\dots,d_{n}}
, which we do by multiplying the first component by the
(x-t)
factor. As mentioned above, the monodromy group of the space of
y^{2}=f(x)
with
f(x)=(x-t)f_{2g}(x)
is a subgroup of the monodromy group that we would like to understand. However, by
[
KS99
, Theorem 10.1.16]
(see also
[
Yu96
]
and
[
Hal08
, Theorem 4.1]
), the monodromy group of our one-parameter space is already the full group
\mathrm{Sp}(2g)
.

This implies that the family
y^{2}=f(x)
with
f(x)\in\mathcal{H}_{d_{1},\dots,d_{n}}
has monodromy group
\mathrm{Sp}(2g)
. By Theorem 9.2.6 in
[
KS99
]
, the large monodromy of this family implies the equidistribution statement.

∎

We now consider the case of
\mathcal{P}_{d_{1},\dots,d_{n}}
using an inclusion-exclusion argument.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
F
be a continuous
\mathbb{C}
-valued central function on
\mathrm{Sp}(2g)
and let
\sigma
be any fixed partition of
2g+1
. Then

\lim_{q\to\infty}\frac{1}{\#\mathcal{P}_{\sigma}}\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{P}_{\sigma}}F(Q)=\int_{\mathrm{Sp}(2g)}F(U)dU.

Proof.

The cardinality of the sets
\mathcal{P}_{\sigma}
can be estimated by

\#\mathcal{P}_{\sigma}=\frac{s_{\sigma}(m)}{m!}q^{m}+O(q^{m-1}),

where
s_{\sigma}(m)
denotes the number of elements with cycle pattern
\sigma

in the permutation group
\mathbb{S}_{m}
(see for example
[
Coh70
]
). In our case,
m=2g+1
.

Recall that

\mathcal{H}_{\rho}=\bigsqcup_{\sigma\leq\rho}\mathcal{P}_{\sigma},

which implies that

\#\mathcal{H}_{\rho}=\frac{q^{2g+1}}{(2g+1)!}\sum_{\sigma\leq\rho}s_{\sigma}(2g+1)+O(q^{2g}).

In particular, for any two partitions
\sigma
and
\rho
(not necessarily related by refinement),

(16)

\lim_{q\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\#\mathcal{H}_{\rho}}{\#\mathcal{P}_{\sigma}}=\frac{1}{s_{\sigma}(2g+1)}\sum_{\tau\leq\rho}s_{\tau}(2g+1).

Theorem
3.1
states that for a fixed factorization pattern
\rho
,

\lim_{q\to\infty}\frac{1}{\#\mathcal{H}_{\rho}}\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{H}_{\rho}}F(Q)=\int_{\mathrm{Sp}(2g)}F(U)dU.

In the formula above we have

\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{H}_{\rho}}F(Q)=\sum_{\sigma\leq\rho}\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{P}_{\sigma}}F(Q),

so applying Möbius inversion on the poset of partitions of
2g+1
yields

\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{P}_{\sigma}}F(Q)=\sum_{\rho\leq\sigma}\mu(\sigma,\rho)\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{H}_{\rho}}F(Q).

Plugging this expression in, applying (
16
), and expanding yields

\displaystyle\lim_{q\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{\#\mathcal{P}_{\sigma}}\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{P}_{\sigma}}F(Q)=

\displaystyle\lim_{q\rightarrow\infty}\sum_{\rho\leq\sigma}\frac{\#\mathcal{H}_{\rho}}{\#\mathcal{P}_{\sigma}}\mu(\sigma,\rho)\frac{1}{\#\mathcal{H}_{\rho}}\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{H}_{\rho}}F(Q)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{s_{\sigma}(2g+1)}\sum_{\rho\leq\sigma}\sum_{\tau\leq\rho}s_{\tau}(2g+1)\mu(\sigma,\rho)\int_{\mathrm{Sp}(2g)}F(U)dU

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{s_{\sigma}(2g+1)}\sum_{\tau\leq\sigma}s_{\tau}(2g+1)\sum_{\tau\leq\rho\leq\sigma}\mu(\sigma,\rho)\int_{\mathrm{Sp}(2g)}F(U)dU

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{s_{\sigma}(2g+1)}\sum_{\tau\leq\sigma}s_{\tau}(2g+1)\delta(\sigma,\tau)\int_{\mathrm{Sp}(2g)}F(U)dU

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathrm{Sp}(2g)}F(U)dU.

This concludes the proof of the statement.

∎

4.
Short intervals on the unit circle in the function field setting

In
[
RW19
]
, Rudnick and Waxman study the distribution of angles
\theta_{\mathfrak{p}}
, where
\mathfrak{p}=\langle\alpha\rangle
is an ideal of the Gaussian integers
\mathbb{Z}[i]
, and
\theta_{\mathfrak{p}}=\arg\alpha
, namely,

u(\alpha):=\left(\frac{\alpha}{\overline{\alpha}}\right)^{2}=e^{4i\theta_{\mathfrak{p}}}.

More precisely, they study

\mathcal{N}_{K,x}(\theta)=\#\{\mathfrak{p}\mbox{ prime },\mathrm{Norm}(\mathfrak{p})\leq x,\theta_{\mathfrak{p}}\in I_{K}(\theta)\},

where
I_{K}(\theta)=[\theta-\frac{\pi}{4K},\theta+\frac{\pi}{4K}]
, and they prove

Theorem 4.1
.

[
RW19
, Theorem 1.1]

Assume GRH (for the family of Hecke
L
-functions). Then almost all arcs of length
1/K
contain
at least one angle
\theta_{\mathfrak{p}}
for a prime ideal with
\mathrm{Norm}(\mathfrak{p})\leq K(\log K)^{2+o(1)}
.

This is achieved by expressing
\mathrm{Var}(\mathcal{N}_{K,x})
in terms of zeroes of the family of Hecke
L
-functions corresponding to the characters given by
\Xi_{k}(\alpha)=(\alpha/\overline{\alpha})^{2k}
,
k\in\mathbb{Z}
for an ideal
(\alpha)
in
\mathbb{Z}[i]
. The result then follows from an upper bound for said variance. The distribution of values of this family of Hecke
L
-functions was studied by Duke, Friedlander, and Iwaniec in
[
DFI95
]
. Their results suggest that this family can be modeled with a symplectic regime. Indeed, Rudnick and Waxman develop a random matrix model that they use to conjecture the following.

Conjecture 4.2
.

[
RW19
, Conjecture 1.2]
For
1\ll K\ll N^{1-o(1)}

\mathrm{Var}(\mathcal{N}_{K,x})\sim\frac{N}{K}\min\left\{1,2\frac{\log K}{\log N}\right\}.

In addition, they consider the function field analogue to this problem. In order to state this problem and to recall background that we will use in later sections, we present here the function field analogue of the Gaussian integers initially developed by Bary-Soroker, Smilansky, and Wolf in
[
BSSW16
]
, as well as the framework considered by Rudnick and Waxman in
[
RW19
]
.

For
P(T)\in\mathcal{P}
, there are
A(T),B(T)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]
with

P(T)=A(T)^{2}+TB(T)^{2}

if and only if
P(0)
is a square in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. Let
S:=\sqrt{-T}
. This defines an embedding
\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}[S]
.
Thus we can write

P(T)=(A+BS)(A-BS)=\mathfrak{p}\overline{\mathfrak{p}}

in
\mathbb{F}_{q}[S]
. There are two automorphisms of
\mathbb{F}_{q}[S]
fixing
\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]
. The nontrivial one corresponds to complex conjugation, which may be extended to the ring of formal power series:

\sigma:\mathbb{F}_{q}[[S]]\rightarrow\mathbb{F}_{q}[[S]],\qquad\sigma(S)=-S.

The norm map is then given by

\mathrm{Norm}:\mathbb{F}_{q}[[S]]^{\times}\rightarrow\mathbb{F}_{q}[[T]]^{\times},\qquad\mathrm{Norm}(f)=f\sigma(f)=f(S)f(-S).

We then have the following analogue of the unit circle:

\mathbb{S}^{1}:=\{g\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[[S]]^{\times}\,:\,g(0)=1,\mathrm{Norm}(g)=1\},

the group of formal power series with constant term 1 and unit norm. By Hensel’s Lemma the map
v\mapsto v^{2}
is an automorphism of
\mathbb{S}^{1}
.

For
f\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[[S]]
let
|f|:=q^{-\mathrm{ord}(f)}
, where
\mathrm{ord}(f)=\max\{j\,:\,S^{j}\mid f\}
, be the absolute value associated with the place at infinity. Now consider the sectors in the circle:

(17)

\mathrm{Sect}(v;k)=\{w\in\mathbb{S}^{1}\,:\,|w-v|\leq q^{-k}\}.

The sector
\mathrm{Sect}(v;k)
can be described modulo
S^{k}
. Indeed,

v\in\mathrm{Sect}(v;k)
if and only if
w\equiv v\,(\mathrm{mod}\,S^{k})
. This leads us to consider the following modular group.

\mathbb{S}^{1}_{k}:=\{f\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[S]/(S^{k})\,:\,f(0)=1,\,\mathrm{Norm}(f)\equiv 1\,(\mathrm{mod}\,S^{k})\}.

In particular, the group
\mathbb{S}^{1}_{k}
parametrizes the different sectors.

Lemma 4.3
.

[
Kat17
, Lemma 2.1]
,
[
RW19
, Lemma 6.1]
,

(1)

The cardinality of
\mathbb{S}^{1}_{k}
is

\#\mathbb{S}^{1}_{k}=q^{\kappa},\mbox{ with }\kappa:=\left\lfloor\frac{k}{2}\right\rfloor.

(2)

We have a direct product decomposition

\left(\mathbb{F}_{q}[S]/(S^{k})\right)^{\times}=H_{k}\times\mathbb{S}^{1}_{k},

where

H_{k}:=\{f\in\left(\mathbb{F}_{q}[S]/(S^{k})\right)^{\times}\,:\,f(-S)\equiv f(S)\,(\mathrm{mod}\,S^{k})\},

and

|H_{k}|=(q-1)q^{\left\lfloor\frac{k-1}{2}\right\rfloor}.

Define for
f\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[S]
coprime to
S
,

U(f):=\sqrt{\frac{f}{\sigma(f)}}.

This uses the fact that
v\mapsto v^{2}
is an automorphism of
\mathbb{S}^{1}
and therefore the square-root is well defined for

\frac{f}{\sigma(f)}\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
. Notice that
U(cf)=U(f)
for scalars
c\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
.

The modular counterpart of
U
is given by

U_{k}:\left(\mathbb{F}_{q}[S]/(S^{k})\right)^{\times}\rightarrow\mathbb{S}^{1}_{k},\qquad f\mapsto\sqrt{\frac{f}{\sigma(f)}}\,(\mathrm{mod}\,S^{k})

and is a surjective homomorphism whose kernel is
H_{k}
(
[
RW19
, Lemma 6.2]
).

We are now ready to definte the analogues of the Hecke characters in this setting. We continue to follow
[
RW19
]
as well as Katz
[
Kat17
]
. A super-even character modulo
S^{k}
is a Dirichlet character

\Xi:\left(\mathbb{F}_{q}[S]/(S^{k})\right)^{\times}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{\times}

which is trivial on
H_{k}
. Therefore, super-even characters modulo
S^{k}
are the characters of

\left(\mathbb{F}_{q}[S]/(S^{k})\right)^{\times}/H_{k}\cong\mathbb{S}_{k}^{1}
.

Proposition 4.4
.

[
RW19
, Proposition 6.3]

For
f\in\left(\mathbb{F}_{q}[S]/(S^{k})\right)^{\times}
and
v\in\mathbb{S}_{k}^{1}
, the following are equivalent:

(1)

U_{k}(f)\in\mathrm{Sect}(v;k)
,

(2)

U_{k}(f)=U_{k}(v)
,

(3)

fH_{k}=vH_{k}
,

(4)

\Xi(f)=\Xi(v)
for all super-even characters
\,(\mathrm{mod}\,S^{k})
.

The Swan conductor of
\Xi
is the maximal integer
d=d(\Xi)<k
such that
\Xi
is nontrivial on the subgroup

\Gamma_{d}:=\left(1+(S^{d})\right)/(S^{k})\subset\left(\mathbb{F}_{q}[S]/(S^{k})\right)^{\times}.

Then
\Xi
is a primitive character modulo
S^{d(\Xi)+1}
. The Swan conductor of a super-even character is necessarily odd, since these characters are automatically trivial on
\Gamma_{d}
for
d
even.

The
L
-function associated to
\Xi
is given by

\mathcal{L}(u,\Xi)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in\mathcal{M}\\
f(0)\not=0\end{subarray}}\Xi(f)u^{\deg(f)}=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}P\in\mathcal{P}\\
P(0)\not=0\end{subarray}}\left(1-\Xi(P)u^{\deg(P)}\right)^{-1},\quad|u|<1/q.

This is a polynomial of degree
d(\Xi)
when
\Xi
is nontrivial. One can write

\mathcal{L}(u,\Xi)=(1-u)\det\left(I-uq^{1/2}\Theta_{\Xi}\right)

with
\Theta_{\Xi}\in\mathrm{U}(N)
and
N=d(\Xi)-1
.

Katz
[
Kat17
, Theorem 5.1]
showed that for
q\rightarrow\infty
the set of Frobenius classes

\{\Theta_{\Xi}\,:\,\Xi\mbox{ primitive super-even }\,(\mathrm{mod}\,S^{k})\}

becomes uniformly distributed in
\mathrm{Sp}(2\kappa-2)
provided that
2\kappa-2\geq 4
, and that the same holds for
2\kappa-2=4
provided that the characteristic is coprime to 10.

Rudnick and Waxman study the function that count prime ideals with a certain direction:

(18)

\mathcal{N}_{k,\nu}(v):=\#\{(\mathfrak{p})\mbox{ prime },\mathfrak{p}(0)\not=0,\deg(\mathfrak{p})=\nu,U(\mathfrak{p})\in\mathrm{Sect}(v,k)\},

and prove an analogue to Conjecture
4.2
in this setting. This rests on studying the distribution of the von Mangoldt function. It is therefore natural for us to consider von Mangoldt convolutions, in addition to the divisor function, in this context.

5.
Symplectic averages of the divisor function

In this section we consider two different problems involving sums of the divisor function over
\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]
which lead to symplectic distributions when
q\rightarrow\infty
.

5.1.
Average of the divisor function over the quadratic residues modulo
P

The first problem we consider concerns the distribution of the divisor function over quadratic residues. We first build framework for the hyperelliptic ensemble, with a particular focus on the covers defined by
Y^{2}=P
with
P
irreducible.

Let
P(T)\in\mathcal{P}
and
f\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]
. If
P\nmid f
, the quadratic residue symbol is defined by

\left(\frac{f}{P}\right)\equiv f^{\frac{|P|-1}{2}}\,(\mathrm{mod}\,P).

If
P\mid f
, we set
\left(\frac{f}{P}\right)=0
. If
Q=P_{1}^{e_{1}}\cdots P_{r}^{e_{r}}
with each
P_{j}
irreducible, then the Jacobi symbol is given by

\left(\frac{f}{Q}\right)=\prod_{j=1}^{r}\left(\frac{f}{P_{j}}\right)^{e_{j}}.

From now on we will assume that
q\equiv 1\,(\mathrm{mod}\,4)
; in this case, quadratic reciprocity implies
\left(\frac{A}{B}\right)=\left(\frac{B}{A}\right)
for any
A,B\in\mathcal{M}
non-zero such that
(A,B)=1
.

For
D\in\mathcal{H}
, we consider the quadratic character

\chi_{D}(f)=\left(\frac{D}{f}\right).

If we consider the hyperelliptic curve with model
C_{D}:y^{2}=D
, then the zeta function associated to
C_{D}
is given by

\mathcal{Z}_{C_{D}}=\exp\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}N_{n}(C_{D})\frac{u^{n}}{n}\right).

The Weil conjectures then imply that

\mathcal{Z}_{C_{D}}=\frac{\mathcal{L}^{*}(u,\chi_{D})}{(1-u)(1-qu)},

where
\mathcal{L}^{*}(u,\chi_{D})
is the completed
L
-function given by

\mathcal{L}(u,\chi_{D})=(1-u)^{\lambda}\mathcal{L}^{*}(u,\chi_{D})

with
\lambda=1
if
\deg(D)
even, and 0 if
\deg(D)
odd. We also have that

\mathcal{L}^{*}(u,\chi_{D})
is a polynomial of even degree
\deg(D)-1-\lambda
.

For simplicity we will restrict ourselves to the case
\deg(D)
odd (so the character
\chi_{D}
is odd) and we will write
\deg(D)=2g+1
, where
g
is the genus of
C_{D}
. This does not restrict the family if we think in terms of hyperelliptic covers, since we can always find a model with
D
of degree
2g+1
. Therefore we can also assume that
\mathcal{L}^{*}(u,\chi_{D})=\mathcal{L}(u,\chi_{D})
.

By the Riemann Hypothesis all the zeroes of
\mathcal{L}^{*}(u,\chi_{D})
satisfy
|u|=\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}
. Thus, we can write

\mathcal{L}^{*}(u,\chi_{D})=\det(I-uq^{1/2}\Theta_{C_{D}})

where
\Theta_{C_{D}}\in\mathrm{Sp}(2g)
. Its conjugacy class is known as the unitarized Frobenius class.

Lemma
2.1
in this case gives the following statement.

Lemma 5.1
.

For
D\in\mathcal{H}_{2g+1}
, and

M(n;d_{k}\chi_{D})=\sum_{f\in\mathcal{M}_{n}}d_{k}(f)\chi_{D}(f),

we have for
n\leq 2gk
,

M(n;d_{k}\chi_{D})=(-1)^{n}q^{n/2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1}+\cdots+j_{k}=n\\
0\leq j_{1},\dots,j_{k}\leq 2g\end{subarray}}\mathrm{Sc}_{j_{1}}(\Theta_{C_{D}})\cdots\mathrm{Sc}_{j_{k}}(\Theta_{C_{D}}),

and
M(n;d_{k}\chi_{D})=0
otherwise.

We could then use the connection between
M(n;d_{k}\chi_{D})
and secular coefficients to study the distribution of
d_{k}(f)
among quadratic residues modulo
D
. However, for an arbitrary
D
,
\chi_{D}
does not directly detect quadratic residues; we would need to rely on an inclusion-exclusion argument as well. Specifically, we would want to consider the sum

\frac{1}{2^{\Omega(D)}}\sum_{D_{0}\mid D}M(n;d_{k}\chi_{D_{0}}\chi_{D}),

which then becomes unwieldy in the general case. Instead, we restrict to the case when
D
is a monic irreducible polynomial
P
, so that this sum becomes much simpler. In exchange, we end up with a sum over irreducible polynomials
P
of
M(n;d_{k}\chi_{P})
. In particular, in order to connect our sum to a symplectic matrix integral, we will need an equidistribution result about
\Theta_{C_{P}}
for
P
ranging over monic irreducible polynomials, instead of one about
\Theta_{C_{D}}
, for
D
ranging over monic square-free polynomials. This result was proven in Section
3
. Notice that to achieve this, we returned to an inclusion-exclusion argument at the end of our computation.

Let
P\in\mathcal{P}_{2g+1}
. We consider the question of computing the mean and variance of the following function as
q\rightarrow\infty
:

\mathcal{S}^{S}_{d_{k},n}(P)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in\mathcal{M}_{n}\\
f\equiv\square\,(\mathrm{mod}\,P)\\
P\nmid f\end{subarray}}d_{k}(f),

where the
\square
denotes a square. In other words, the sum take places over the quadratic residues modulo
P
of fixed degree
n
.

Recalling that
P\nmid f
is a square modulo
P
iff
1+\chi_{P}(f)=2
, we obtain

(19)

\mathcal{S}^{S}_{d_{k},n}(P)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in\mathcal{M}_{n}\\
P\nmid f\end{subarray}}d_{k}(f)+\frac{1}{2}M(n;d_{k}\chi_{P}).

The main term of
\mathcal{S}^{S}_{d_{k},n}(P)
comes from the first sum in (
19
).

Lemma 5.2
.

We have that

\mathcal{S}^{S}_{d_{k},n}(P)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in\mathcal{M}_{n}\\
P\nmid f\end{subarray}}d_{k}(f)\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{q}\right)\right)=\frac{q^{n}}{2}\binom{k+n-1}{k-1}+O(q^{n-1}).

Proof.

We can estimate the first term in (
19
) by considering its generating function:

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in\mathcal{M}\\
P\nmid f\end{subarray}}d_{k}(f)u^{\deg(f)}=

\displaystyle\left(\mathcal{Z}(u)(1-u^{\deg(P)})\right)^{k}=\left(\frac{1-u^{2g+1}}{1-qu}\right)^{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=0}^{k}\binom{k}{m}\binom{-k}{n-(2g+1)m}q^{n-(2g+1)m}(-1)^{n}u^{n}.

By focusing on the coefficient of
u^{n}
, we obtain

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in\mathcal{M}_{n}\\
P\nmid f\end{subarray}}d_{k}(f)=(-1)^{n}\sum_{m=0}^{k}\binom{k}{m}\binom{-k}{n-(2g+1)m}q^{n-(2g+1)m}=q^{n}\binom{k+n-1}{k-1}+O(q^{n-1}).

The above estimate is independent of
P
.

For the second term in (
19
), the Riemann Hypothesis gives

\left|M(n;d_{k}\chi_{P})\right|\ll q^{n/2}.

∎

We consider the variance

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(\mathcal{S}^{S}_{d_{k},n})=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\#\mathcal{P}_{2g+1}}\sum_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{2g+1}}\left|\mathcal{S}^{S}_{d_{k},n}(P)-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in\mathcal{M}_{n}\\
P\nmid f\end{subarray}}d_{k}(f)\right|^{2},

which by Lemma
5.2
is given by

(20)

\mathrm{Var}(\mathcal{S}^{S}_{d_{k},n})=\frac{1}{4\#\mathcal{P}_{2g+1}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}P\in\mathcal{P}_{2g+1}\end{subarray}}|M(n;d_{k}\chi_{P})|^{2}\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{q}\right)\right).

By combining equation (
20
) and Lemma
5.1
, we obtain

(21)

\mathrm{Var}(\mathcal{S}^{S}_{d_{k},n})=\frac{q^{n}}{4\#\mathcal{P}_{2g+1}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}P\in\mathcal{P}_{2g+1}\end{subarray}}\left|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1}+\cdots+j_{k}=n\\
0\leq j_{1},\dots,j_{k}\leq 2g\end{subarray}}\mathrm{Sc}_{j_{1}}(\Theta_{C_{P}})\cdots\mathrm{Sc}_{j_{k}}(\Theta_{C_{P}})\right|^{2}\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{q}\right)\right).

We set
\sigma=(2g+1)
in Theorem
3.2
and conclude that

\displaystyle\lim_{q\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{\#\mathcal{P}_{2g+1}}\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{P}_{2g+1}}F(Q)=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathrm{Sp}(2g)}F(U)dU.

Applying this limit to equation (
21
), we obtain the statement of Theorem
1.1
.

Theorem 5.3
.

Let
n\leq 2gk
. As
q\rightarrow\infty

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(\mathcal{S}^{S}_{d_{k},n})\sim

\displaystyle\frac{q^{n}}{4}\int_{\mathrm{Sp}(2g)}\left|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1}+\cdots+j_{k}=n\\
0\leq j_{1},\dots,j_{k}\leq 2g\end{subarray}}\mathrm{Sc}_{j_{1}}(U)\cdots\mathrm{Sc}_{j_{k}}(U)\right|^{2}dU.

5.2.
Average of the divisor function over short arcs on the unit circle

The second problem we study is the variance of the following sum:

\mathcal{N}^{S}_{d_{\ell},k,\nu}(v)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in\mathcal{M}_{\nu}\\
f(0)\not=0\\
U(f)\in\mathrm{Sect}(v,k)\end{subarray}}d_{\ell}(f).

Many of the ideas and techniques that we discuss here come from
[
RW19
]
.

As in Section
2
we set

M_{0}(\nu;d_{\ell}\Xi)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in\mathcal{M}_{\nu}\\
f(0)\not=0\end{subarray}}d_{\ell}(f)\Xi(f),

where the subindex
0
indicates that we perform the sum over
f(0)\not=0
.

Lemma
2.1
becomes the following statement in this setting.

Lemma 5.4
.

We have for

\nu\leq\ell(d(\Xi)-1)
,

M_{0}(\nu;d_{\ell}\Xi)=(-1)^{\nu}q^{\nu/2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1}+\cdots+j_{\ell}=\nu\\
0\leq j_{1},\dots,j_{\ell}\leq d(\Xi)-1\end{subarray}}\mathrm{Sc}_{j_{1}}(\Theta_{\Xi})\cdots\mathrm{Sc}_{j_{\ell}}(\Theta_{\Xi})+O_{\ell,k}\left(q^{\frac{\nu-1}{2}}\right).

If
\ell(d(\Xi)-1)<\nu\leq\ell d(\Xi)
,

|M_{0}(\nu;d_{\ell}\Xi)|\ll_{\ell,k}q^{\frac{\nu-1}{2}}.

Finally, if
\ell d(\Xi)<\nu
,
M_{0}(\nu;d_{\ell}\Xi)=0
.

We start our analysis by looking at the mean value of
\mathcal{N}^{S}_{d_{\ell},k,\nu}
averaged over all the directions of
v\in\mathbb{S}_{k}^{1}
.

Lemma 5.5
.

We have that

\langle\mathcal{N}^{S}_{d_{\ell},k,\nu}\rangle=\frac{1}{q^{\kappa}}\sum_{v\in\mathbb{S}_{k}^{1}}\mathcal{N}^{S}_{d_{\ell},k,\nu}(v)=\frac{1}{q^{\kappa}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in\mathcal{M}_{\nu}\\
f(0)\not=0\end{subarray}}d_{\ell}(f)=q^{\nu-\kappa}\binom{\ell+\nu-1}{\ell-1}+O\left(q^{\nu-\kappa-1}\right).

Proof.

Notice that we can count polynomials with the condition
f(0)\not=0
by removing the factor
(1-u)^{-1}
from the Euler product of the zeta function:

\mathcal{Z}(u)^{\ell}(1-u)^{\ell}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in\mathcal{M}\\
f(0)\not=0\end{subarray}}d_{\ell}(f)u^{\deg(f)}.

We use the formula for the zeta function to find a closed expression for the left-hand side term.

\mathcal{Z}(u)^{\ell}(1-u)^{\ell}=\left(\frac{1-u}{1-qu}\right)^{\ell}=\sum_{\nu=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=0}^{\ell}\binom{\ell}{m}\binom{-\ell}{\nu-m}q^{\nu-m}(-1)^{\nu}u^{\nu}.

The result follows by comparing the coefficients of
u^{\nu}
.
∎

Our next goal is to obtain a formula for
\mathcal{N}^{S}_{d_{\ell},k,\nu}(v)
in terms of the super-even characters
\Xi
. By Proposition
4.4
and the orthogonality relations, we find, for
f\in\mathcal{M}_{\nu}
,

\frac{1}{q^{\kappa}}\sum_{\Xi\text{ super-even}\,(\mathrm{mod}\,S^{k})}\overline{\Xi(v)}\Xi(f)=\begin{cases}1&U(f)\in\mathrm{Sect}(v;k),\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Hence

\mathcal{N}^{S}_{d_{\ell},k,\nu}(v)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in\mathcal{M}_{\nu}\\
f(0)\not=0\\
U(f)\in\mathrm{Sect}(u,k)\end{subarray}}d_{\ell}(f)=\frac{1}{q^{\kappa}}\sum_{\Xi\text{ super-even}\,(\mathrm{mod}\,S^{k})}\overline{\Xi(u)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in\mathcal{M}_{\nu}\\
f(0)\not=0\end{subarray}}d_{\ell}(f)\Xi(f).

The contribution from the trivial character
\Xi_{0}
is precisely

\frac{1}{q^{\kappa}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in\mathcal{M}_{\nu}\\
f(0)\not=0\end{subarray}}d_{\ell}(f)=\langle\mathcal{N}^{S}_{d_{\ell},k,\nu}\rangle.

Thus

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}^{S}_{d_{\ell},k,\nu}(v)-\langle\mathcal{N}^{S}_{d_{\ell},k,\nu}\rangle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{q^{\kappa}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\Xi\text{ super-even}\,(\mathrm{mod}\,S^{k})\\
\Xi\not=\Xi_{0}\end{subarray}}\overline{\Xi(v)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in\mathcal{M}_{\nu}\\
f(0)\not=0\end{subarray}}d_{\ell}(f)\Xi(f)

(22)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{q^{\kappa}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\Xi\text{ super-even}\,(\mathrm{mod}\,S^{k})\\
\Xi\not=\Xi_{0}\end{subarray}}\overline{\Xi(v)}M_{0}(\nu;d_{\ell}\Xi).

Recall that we want to compute the variance

(23)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(\mathcal{N}^{S}_{d_{\ell},k,\nu})=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{q^{\kappa}}\sum_{u\in\mathbb{S}_{k}^{1}}\left|\mathcal{N}^{S}_{d_{\ell},k,\nu}(u)-\langle\mathcal{N}^{S}_{d_{\ell},k,\nu}\rangle\right|^{2}.

By applying the orthogonality relations

(24)

\sum_{u\in\mathbb{S}_{k}^{1}}\overline{\Xi_{1}(u)}\Xi_{2}(u)=\begin{cases}q^{\kappa}&\Xi_{1}=\Xi_{2},\\
0&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

to equations (
6.2
) and (
23
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(\mathcal{N}^{S}_{d_{\ell},k,\nu})=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{q^{\kappa}}\sum_{u\in\mathbb{S}_{k}^{1}}\frac{1}{q^{2\kappa}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\Xi_{1},\Xi_{2}\text{ super-even}\,(\mathrm{mod}\,S^{k})\\
\Xi_{1},\Xi_{2}\not=\Xi_{0}\end{subarray}}\overline{\Xi_{1}(u)}M_{0}(\nu;d_{\ell}\Xi_{1})\Xi_{2}(u)\overline{M_{0}(\nu;d_{\ell}\Xi_{2})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{q^{2\kappa}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\Xi_{1},\Xi_{2}\text{ super-even}\,(\mathrm{mod}\,S^{k})\\
\Xi_{1},\Xi_{2}\not=\Xi_{0}\end{subarray}}M_{0}(\nu;d_{\ell}\Xi_{1})\overline{M_{0}(\nu;d_{\ell}\Xi_{2})}\frac{1}{q^{\kappa}}\sum_{u\in\mathbb{S}_{k}^{1}}\overline{\Xi_{1}(u)}\Xi_{2}(u)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{q^{2\kappa}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\Xi\text{ super-even}\,(\mathrm{mod}\,S^{k})\\
\Xi\not=\Xi_{0}\end{subarray}}|M_{0}(\nu;d_{\ell}\Xi)|^{2}.

Combining the above with Lemma
5.4
yields

(25)

\mathrm{Var}(\mathcal{N}^{S}_{d_{\ell},k,\nu})=\frac{q^{\nu}}{q^{2\kappa}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\Xi\text{ super-even}\,(\mathrm{mod}\,S^{k})\\
\Xi\not=\Xi_{0}\end{subarray}}\left|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1}+\cdots+j_{\ell}=\nu\\
0\leq j_{1},\dots,j_{\ell}\leq d(\Xi)-1\end{subarray}}\mathrm{Sc}_{j_{1}}(\Theta_{\Xi})\cdots\mathrm{Sc}_{j_{\ell}}(\Theta_{\Xi})\right|^{2}\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}\right)\right).

We are now ready to prove Theorem
1.2
.

Theorem 5.6
.

Let
\nu\leq\ell(2\kappa-2)
with
\kappa=\left\lfloor\frac{k}{2}\right\rfloor
. As
q\rightarrow\infty
,

\mathrm{Var}(\mathcal{N}^{S}_{d_{\ell},k,\nu})\sim\frac{q^{\nu}}{q^{\kappa}}\int_{\mathrm{Sp}(2\kappa-2)}\left|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1}+\cdots+j_{\ell}=\nu\\
0\leq j_{1},\dots,j_{\ell}\leq 2\kappa-2\end{subarray}}\mathrm{Sc}_{j_{1}}(U)\cdots\mathrm{Sc}_{j_{\ell}}(U)\right|^{2}dU.

Proof.

In equation (
25
), we separate the characters according to their Swan conductor, which is necessarily an odd integer
d(\Xi)<k
with maximal value
2\kappa-1
. The characters with maximal conductor are primitive; the contribution from the others is negligible. Thus, we can consider the sum only over the primitive characters, and the result follows from Katz
[
Kat17
, Theorem 5.1]
.
∎

5.3.
Relationship with Random Matrix Theory

Our goal here is to discuss what is known about the integral

(26)

I_{d_{k}}^{S}(n;N):=\int_{\mathrm{Sp}(2N)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1}+\cdots+j_{k}=n\\
0\leq j_{1},\dots,j_{k}\leq 2N\end{subarray}}\mathrm{Sc}_{j_{1}}(U)\cdots\mathrm{Sc}_{j_{k}}(U)dU.

Observe that the integral
I_{d_{k}}^{S}(n;N)
given by (
26
) measures the discrepancy of the objects discussed previously, rather than the variance. However, from the point of view of random matrix theory,
I_{d_{k}}^{S}(n;N)
is a more natural object to consider. The methods used to prove Theorem
1.1
apply to obtain a similar statement for the discrepancy.

Theorem 5.7
.

Assume that
n\leq 2gk
. As
q\rightarrow\infty
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\#\mathcal{P}_{2g+1}}\sum_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{2g+1}}

\displaystyle\left(\mathcal{S}^{S}_{d_{k},n}(P)-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in\mathcal{M}_{n}\\
P\nmid f\end{subarray}}d_{k}(f)\right)

\displaystyle\sim\frac{(-1)^{n}q^{n/2}}{2}\int_{\mathrm{Sp}(2g)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1}+\cdots+j_{k}=n\\
0\leq j_{1},\dots,j_{k}\leq 2g\end{subarray}}\mathrm{Sc}_{j_{1}}(U)\cdots\mathrm{Sc}_{j_{k}}(U)dU.

Remark 5.8
.

Note that
\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in\mathcal{M}_{n}\\
P\nmid f\end{subarray}}d_{k}(f)
is the main term of
\mathcal{S}^{S}_{d_{k},n}(P)
, so Theorem
5.7
picks up on a contribution from the second-order terms. In the case of
\mathcal{N}_{d_{\ell},k,\nu}^{S}(v)
,
\langle\mathcal{N}_{d_{\ell},k,\nu}^{S}\rangle
is the limiting mean value rather than the main term, so the discrepancy above goes to zero as
q\rightarrow\infty
.

In
[
MR21
]
Medjedovic and Rubinstein prove the following result.

Theorem 5.9
.

[
MR21
, Theorem 7]

Let

P_{k,N}(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{2kN}I_{d_{k}}^{S}(n,N)x^{n}.

Then

P_{k,N}(x)=\frac{1}{(1-x^{2})^{\binom{k+1}{2}}}\det_{1\leq i,j\leq k}\left[\binom{j-1}{i-1}x^{j-i}-\binom{2N+2k+1-j}{i-1}x^{2N+2k+2-j-i}\right].

Corollary 5.10
.

If
k=2
, then
I_{d_{2}}^{S}(n,N)
is given by

\int_{\mathrm{Sp}(2N)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1}+j_{2}=n\\
0\leq j_{1},j_{2}\leq 2N\end{subarray}}\mathrm{Sc}_{j_{1}}(U)\mathrm{Sc}_{j_{2}}(U)dU=\begin{cases}\binom{\frac{n}{2}+2}{2}\eta_{n}&0\leq n\leq N,\\
\binom{2N-\frac{n}{2}+2}{2}\eta_{n}&N+1\leq n\leq 2N,\end{cases}

where

(27)

\eta_{j}=\begin{cases}1&j\mbox{ even},\\
0&j\mbox{ odd}.\\
\end{cases}

As
N\to\infty
,
I_{d_{2}}^{S}(n,N)
is asymptotic to

I_{d_{2}}^{S}(n,N)\sim\gamma_{d_{2}}^{S}(c)N^{2},

where
c=n/N
and

\gamma_{d_{2}}^{S}(c)=\begin{cases}\frac{c^{2}}{2}&0\leq c\leq 1,\\
\frac{(2-c)^{2}}{2}&1\leq c\leq 2.\end{cases}

Proof.

If
k=2
, Theorem
5.9
gives

\displaystyle P_{2,N}(x)=

\displaystyle\frac{1-x^{4N+6}-(2N+3)x^{2N+2}(1-x^{2})}{(1-x^{2})^{3}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{\ell=0}^{2N+2}x^{2\ell}-(2N+3)x^{2N+2}}{(1-x^{2})^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=0}^{N}\binom{\ell+2}{2}x^{2\ell}+\sum_{\ell=N+1}^{2N}\binom{2N-\ell+2}{2}x^{2\ell}.

By looking at the coefficient of
x^{2\ell}
, we obtain the statement.
∎

As noted in Remark
5.8
, the discrepancy may be negligible in some cases, forcing us to study the variance. From the random matrix theory point of view, this leads to the study of the integral

(28)

I_{d_{k},2}^{S}(n;N):=\int_{\mathrm{Sp}(2N)}\left|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1}+\cdots+j_{k}=n\\
0\leq j_{1},\dots,j_{k}\leq 2N\end{subarray}}\mathrm{Sc}_{j_{1}}(U)\cdots\mathrm{Sc}_{j_{k}}(U)\right|^{2}dU.

Here we will compute
I_{d_{1},2}^{S}(n;N)
. The following result is due to Conrey, Farmer, Keating, Rubinstein, and Snaith
[
CFK+03
]
. We state here the version of Bump and Gamburd.

Proposition 5.11
.

[
BG06
, Proposition 11]

(29)

\int_{\mathrm{Sp}(2N)}\prod_{j=1}^{k}\det(I+x_{j}U)dU=\sum_{\varepsilon\in\{-1,1\}}\prod_{j=1}^{k}x_{j}^{N(1-\varepsilon_{j})}\prod_{i\leq j}(1-x_{i}^{\varepsilon_{i}}x_{j}^{\varepsilon_{j}})^{-1}.

Corollary 5.12
.

If
k=1
, then
I_{d_{1},2}^{S}(n,N)
is given by

\int_{\mathrm{Sp}(2N)}\left|\mathrm{Sc}_{n}(U)\right|^{2}dU=\begin{cases}\left\lfloor\frac{n+2}{2}\right\rfloor&0\leq n\leq N,\\
\left\lfloor\frac{2N-n+2}{2}\right\rfloor&N+1\leq n\leq 2N.\end{cases}

As
N\to\infty
,
I^{S}_{d_{1},2}(n,N)
is asymptotic to

I^{S}_{d_{1},2}(n,N)\sim\gamma_{d_{1},2}^{S}(c)N,

where
c=n/N
and

\gamma_{d_{1},2}^{S}(c)=\begin{cases}\frac{c}{2}&0\leq c\leq 1,\\
\frac{2-c}{2}&1\leq c\leq 2.\end{cases}

Proof.

In order to compute
I_{d_{1},2}^{S}(n;N)
, we consider the case of two variables in order to obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\mathrm{Sp}(2N)}

\displaystyle\det(I+x_{1}U)\det(I+x_{2}U)dU

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1-x_{1}^{2N+3}x_{2}^{2N+3}}{(1-x_{1}x_{2})(1-x_{1}^{2})(1-x_{2}^{2})}-\frac{x_{1}^{2N+3}-x_{2}^{2N+3}}{(x_{1}-x_{2})(1-x_{1}^{2})(1-x_{2}^{2})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{m=0}^{N}x_{1}^{m}(x_{2}^{m}-x_{2}^{2N+2-m})+\sum_{m=N+2}^{2N+2}x_{1}^{m}(x_{2}^{m}-x_{2}^{2N+2-m})}{(1-x_{1}^{2})(1-x_{2}^{2})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{m=0}^{N}x_{1}^{m}(x_{2}^{m}-x_{2}^{2N+2-m})+\sum_{\ell=0}^{N}x_{1}^{2N+2-\ell}(x_{2}^{2N+2-\ell}-x_{2}^{\ell})}{(1-x_{1}^{2})(1-x_{2}^{2})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{m=0}^{N}(x_{1}^{m}-x_{1}^{2N+2-m})(x_{2}^{m}-x_{2}^{2N+2-m})}{(1-x_{1}^{2})(1-x_{2}^{2})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{m=0}^{N}x_{1}^{m}x_{2}^{m}\left(\sum_{\ell_{1}=0}^{N-m}x_{1}^{2\ell_{1}}\sum_{\ell_{2}=0}^{N-m}x_{2}^{2\ell_{2}}\right).

I_{d_{1},2}^{S}(n;N)
is then given by the coefficient of
x_{1}^{n}x_{2}^{n}
, which gives the corollary.
∎

In the above proof, the fact that the exponents of
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
are equal guarantees that we integrate
\mathrm{Sc}_{n}(U)^{2}
, and not just any mixed product.
On the other hand, the method described above to obtain
I_{d_{1},2}^{S}(n;N)
does not extend to
I_{d_{k},2}^{S}(n;N)
in an obvious way. The integral
I_{d_{k},2}^{S}(n;N)
remains a problem to be explored.

6.
Symplectic averages of the von Mangoldt convolution

In this section we study the mean and variance of quantities that are very similar to
\mathcal{S}^{S}_{d_{k},n}
and
\mathcal{N}^{S}_{d_{\ell},k,\nu}
from Section
5
, but with the convolution of the von Mangoldt function
\Lambda_{k}
in place of the divisor function
d_{k}
.

6.1.
Average of the von Mangoldt convolution function over the quadratic residues modulo
P

Let
P\in\mathcal{P}_{2g+1}
. We consider the question of studying the distribution of the von Mangoldt convolution over square residues modulo
P
, as
q\rightarrow\infty
:

\mathcal{S}^{S}_{\Lambda_{k},n}(P)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in\mathcal{M}_{n}\\
f\equiv\square\,(\mathrm{mod}\,P)\\
P\nmid f\end{subarray}}\Lambda_{k}(f).

Our goal will be to understand the mean value and variance of
\mathcal{S}^{S}_{\Lambda_{k},n}
.

Let
D\in\mathcal{H}_{2g+1}
as before. We also consider

M(n;\Lambda_{k}\chi_{D})=\sum_{f\in\mathcal{M}_{n}}\Lambda_{k}(f)\chi_{D}(f).

Lemma
2.2
becomes the following statement.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
D\in\mathcal{H}_{2g+1}
. For
k\leq n
, we have

M(n;\Lambda_{k}\chi_{D})=(-1)^{k}q^{n/2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1}+\cdots+j_{k}=n\\
1\leq j_{1},\dots,j_{k}\end{subarray}}\mathrm{Tr}(\Theta_{C_{D}}^{j_{1}})\cdots\mathrm{Tr}(\Theta_{C_{D}}^{j_{k}}),

and
M(n;\Lambda_{k}\chi_{D})=0
for
1\leq n<k
.

As in the case of
d_{k}
, we have, for
\deg(P)=2g+1
,

(30)

\mathcal{S}^{S}_{\Lambda_{k},n}(P)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in\mathcal{M}_{n}\\
P\nmid f\end{subarray}}\Lambda_{k}(f)+\frac{1}{2}M(n;\Lambda_{k}\chi_{P}).

The main term of
\mathcal{S}^{S}_{\Lambda_{k},n}(P)
comes from the first term in (
30
).

Lemma 6.2
.

As
q\to\infty
,

\mathcal{S}^{S}_{\Lambda_{k},n}(P)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in\mathcal{M}_{n}\\
P\nmid f\end{subarray}}\Lambda_{k}(f)\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{q}\right)\right)=\frac{q^{n}}{2}\binom{n-1}{k-1}+O(q^{n-1}).

Proof.

We estimate the first term in (
30
) by considering its generating function:

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in\mathcal{M}\\
P\nmid f\end{subarray}}\Lambda_{k}(f)u^{\deg(f)}=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{u\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}(u)}{\mathcal{Z}(u)}-\frac{\deg(P)u^{\deg(P)}}{1-u^{\deg(P)}}\right)^{k}=\left(\frac{qu}{1-qu}-\frac{(2g+1)u^{2g+1}}{1-u^{2g+1}}\right)^{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{k}\binom{k}{j}q^{j}u^{j}(-1)^{k-j}(2g+1)^{k-j}u^{(2g+1)(k-j)}(1-qu)^{-j}(1-u^{2g+1})^{j-k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{j=0}^{k}\binom{k}{j}q^{j}(-1)^{k-j}(2g+1)^{k-j}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m=0\\
((k-j)+m)(2g+1)+j\leq n\end{subarray}}\binom{j-k}{m}(-1)^{m}

\displaystyle\times\binom{-j}{n-((k-j)+m)(2g+1)-j}(-q)^{n-((k-j)+m)(2g+1)-j}u^{n}.

Taking the coefficient of
u^{n}
yields a formula for the sum of
\Lambda_{k}
over monic polynomials of fixed degree
n
, coprime to a fixed
P
:

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in\mathcal{M}_{n}\\
P\nmid f\end{subarray}}\Lambda_{k}(f)=\sum_{j=0}^{k}

\displaystyle\binom{k}{j}(2g+1)^{k-j}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m=0\\
((k-j)+m)(2g+1)+j\leq n\end{subarray}}\binom{j-k}{m}

(31)

\displaystyle\times\binom{-j}{n-((k-j)+m)(2g+1)-j}(-1)^{n-j}q^{n-((k-j)+m)(2g+1)}.

To maximize the power of
q
, we must take
j=k
(which implies
m=0
), thus leading to

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in\mathcal{M}_{n}\\
P\nmid f\end{subarray}}\Lambda_{k}(f)=

\displaystyle q^{n}(-1)^{n-k}\binom{-k}{n-k}+O(q^{n-1})=q^{n}\binom{n-1}{k-1}+O(q^{n-1}).

Now we examine the second term in (
30
). By Lemma
6.1
, we deduce

|M(n;\Lambda_{k}\chi_{P})|\ll\binom{n-1}{k-1}(2g)^{k}q^{n/2}.

∎

We can now turn to the problem of finding the variance

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(\mathcal{S}^{S}_{\Lambda_{k},n})=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\#\mathcal{P}_{2g+1}}\sum_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{2g+1}}\left|\mathcal{S}^{S}_{\Lambda_{k},n}(P)-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in\mathcal{M}_{n}\\
P\nmid f\end{subarray}}\Lambda_{k}(f)\right|^{2}.

By the previous discussion the variance is given by

(32)

\mathrm{Var}(\mathcal{S}^{S}_{\Lambda_{k},n})=\frac{1}{4\#\mathcal{P}_{2g+1}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}P\in\mathcal{P}_{2g+1}\end{subarray}}|M(n;\Lambda_{k}\chi_{P})|^{2}\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{q}\right)\right).

By combining with Lemma
6.1
, we get

(33)

\mathrm{Var}(\mathcal{S}^{S}_{\Lambda_{k},n})=\frac{q^{n}}{4\#\mathcal{P}_{2g+1}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}P\in\mathcal{P}_{2g+1}\end{subarray}}\left|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1}+\cdots+j_{k}=n\\
1\leq j_{1},\dots,j_{k}\end{subarray}}\mathrm{Tr}(\Theta_{C_{P}}^{j_{1}})\cdots\mathrm{Tr}(\Theta_{C_{P}}^{j_{k}})\right|^{2}\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{q}\right)\right).

Applying Katz’s Theorem
3.2
, we obtain the statement of Theorem
1.5
.

Theorem 6.3
.

Let
k\leq n
. As
q\rightarrow\infty
,

\mathrm{Var}(\mathcal{S}^{S}_{\Lambda_{k},n})\sim\frac{q^{n}}{4}\int_{\mathrm{Sp}(2g)}\left|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1}+\cdots+j_{k}=n\\
1\leq j_{1},\dots,j_{k}\end{subarray}}\mathrm{Tr}(U^{j_{1}})\cdots\mathrm{Tr}(U^{j_{k}})\right|^{2}dU.

6.2.
Average of the von Mangoldt convolution function over short arcs on the unit circle

Now we consider the question of the distribution of convolutions of the von Mangoldt function over sectors of the unit circle. Our goal is to study the mean and variance of the following sum:

\mathcal{N}^{S}_{\Lambda_{\ell},k,\nu}(v)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in\mathcal{M}_{\nu}\\
f(0)\not=0\\
U(f)\in\mathrm{Sect}(v,k)\end{subarray}}\Lambda_{\ell}(f).

Rudnick and Waxman
[
RW19
]
study this question for the case
\ell=1
as an intermediate step to estimating (
18
).

As before, let

M_{0}(\nu;\Lambda_{\ell}\Xi)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in\mathcal{M}_{\nu}\\
f(0)\not=0\end{subarray}}\Lambda_{\ell}(f)\Xi(f).

Lemma
2.2
becomes the following statement.

Lemma 6.4
.

We have, for
\ell\leq\nu
,

\displaystyle M_{0}(\nu;\Lambda_{\ell}\Xi)=

\displaystyle(-1)^{\ell}q^{\nu/2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1}+\cdots+j_{\ell}=\nu\\
1\leq j_{1},\dots,j_{\ell}\end{subarray}}\mathrm{Tr}(\Theta_{\Xi}^{j_{1}})\cdots\mathrm{Tr}(\Theta_{\Xi}^{j_{\ell}})+O\left(q^{\frac{\nu-1}{2}}\right),

and
M_{0}(\nu;\Lambda_{\ell}\Xi)=0
for
1\leq n<\ell
.

We start our analysis by looking at the mean value averaging over all the directions of
v\in\mathbb{S}_{k}^{1}
.

Lemma 6.5
.

We have

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{N}^{S}_{\Lambda_{\ell},k,\nu}\rangle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{q^{\kappa}}\sum_{v\in\mathbb{S}_{k}^{1}}\mathcal{N}^{S}_{\Lambda_{\ell},k,\nu}(v)=\frac{1}{q^{\kappa}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in\mathcal{M}_{\nu}\\
f(0)\not=0\end{subarray}}\Lambda_{\ell}(f)=q^{\nu-\kappa}\binom{\nu-1}{\ell-1}+O\left(q^{\nu-\kappa-1}\right).

Proof.

We have computed this term before in (
6.1
). Since this case is particularly simple, we redo it here. The generating function is

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in\mathcal{M}\\
f(0)\not=0\end{subarray}}\Lambda_{\ell}(f)u^{\nu}=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{u\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}(u)}{\mathcal{Z}(u)}-\frac{u}{1-u}\right)^{\ell}=\left(\frac{qu}{1-qu}-\frac{u}{1-u}\right)^{\ell}=\left(\frac{u(q-1)}{(1-u)(1-qu)}\right)^{\ell}

(34)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(q-1)^{\ell}\sum_{\nu=\ell}^{\infty}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\binom{-\ell}{m}\binom{-\ell}{\nu-\ell-m}(-1)^{n-\ell}q^{\nu-\ell-m}u^{\nu}.

Comparing coefficients, we get

(35)

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in\mathcal{M}_{\nu}\\
f(0)\not=0\end{subarray}}\Lambda_{\ell}(f)=

\displaystyle q^{\nu}\binom{-\ell}{\nu-\ell}(-1)^{\nu-\ell}+O(q^{\nu-1})=q^{\nu}\binom{\nu-1}{\ell-1}+O(q^{\nu-1}).

∎

By orthogonality of super-even characters,

\mathcal{N}^{S}_{\Lambda_{\ell},k,\nu}(v)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in\mathcal{M}_{\nu}\\
f(0)\not=0\\
U(f)\in\mathrm{Sect}(u,k)\end{subarray}}\Lambda_{\ell}(f)=\frac{1}{q^{\kappa}}\sum_{\Xi\text{ super-even}\,(\mathrm{mod}\,S^{k})}\overline{\Xi(u)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in\mathcal{M}_{\nu}\\
f(0)\not=0\end{subarray}}\Lambda_{\ell}(f)\Xi(f).

The contribution from the trivial character
\Xi_{0}
is precisely

\frac{1}{q^{\kappa}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in\mathcal{M}_{\nu}\\
f(0)\not=0\end{subarray}}\Lambda_{\ell}(f)=\langle\mathcal{N}^{S}_{\Lambda_{\ell},k,\nu}\rangle.

Thus

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}^{S}_{\Lambda_{\ell},k,\nu}(v)-\langle\mathcal{N}^{S}_{\Lambda_{\ell},k,\nu}\rangle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{q^{\kappa}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\Xi\text{ super-even}\,(\mathrm{mod}\,S^{k})\\
\Xi\not=\Xi_{0}\end{subarray}}\overline{\Xi(v)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in\mathcal{M}_{\nu}\\
f(0)\not=0\end{subarray}}\Lambda_{\ell}(f)\Xi(f)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{q^{\kappa}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\Xi\text{ super-even}\,(\mathrm{mod}\,S^{k})\\
\Xi\not=\Xi_{0}\end{subarray}}\overline{\Xi(v)}M_{0}(\nu;\Lambda_{\ell}\Xi).

We consider the variance

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(\mathcal{N}^{S}_{\Lambda_{\ell},k,\nu})=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{q^{\kappa}}\sum_{u\in\mathbb{S}_{k}^{1}}\left|\mathcal{N}^{S}_{d_{\ell},k,\nu}(u)-\langle\mathcal{N}^{S}_{\Lambda_{\ell},k,\nu}\rangle\right|^{2}.

By applying the orthogonality relations (
24
) we obtain

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(\mathcal{N}^{S}_{\Lambda_{\ell},k,\nu})=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{q^{\kappa}}\sum_{u\in\mathbb{S}_{k}^{1}}\frac{1}{q^{2\kappa}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\Xi_{1},\Xi_{2}\text{ super-even}\,(\mathrm{mod}\,S^{k})\\
\Xi_{1},\Xi_{2}\not=\Xi_{0}\end{subarray}}\overline{\Xi_{1}(u)}M_{0}(\nu;\Lambda_{\ell}\Xi_{1})\Xi_{2}(u)\overline{M_{0}(\nu;\Lambda_{\ell}\Xi_{2})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{q^{2\kappa}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\Xi_{1},\Xi_{2}\text{ super-even}\,(\mathrm{mod}\,S^{k})\\
\Xi_{1},\Xi_{2}\not=\Xi_{0}\end{subarray}}M_{0}(\nu;\Lambda_{\ell}\Xi_{1})\overline{M_{0}(\nu;\Lambda_{\ell}\Xi_{2})}\frac{1}{q^{\kappa}}\sum_{u\in\mathbb{S}_{k}^{1}}\overline{\Xi_{1}(u)}\Xi_{2}(u)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{q^{2\kappa}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\Xi\text{ super-even}\,(\mathrm{mod}\,S^{k})\\
\Xi\not=\Xi_{0}\end{subarray}}|M_{0}(\nu;\Lambda_{\ell}\Xi)|^{2}.

Finally, we apply Lemma
6.4
in order to obtain,

(36)

\mathrm{Var}(\mathcal{N}^{S}_{\Lambda_{\ell},k,\nu})=\frac{q^{\nu}}{q^{2\kappa}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\Xi\text{ super-even}\,(\mathrm{mod}\,S^{k})\\
\Xi\not=\Xi_{0}\end{subarray}}\left|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1}+\cdots+j_{\ell}=\nu\\
1\leq j_{1},\dots,j_{\ell}\end{subarray}}\mathrm{Tr}(\Theta_{\Xi}^{j_{1}})\cdots\mathrm{Tr}(\Theta_{\Xi}^{j_{\ell}})\right|^{2}\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}\right)\right).

We have now all the elements to prove Theorem
1.6
.

Theorem 6.6
.

Let
\ell\leq\nu
. As
q\rightarrow\infty
,

\mathrm{Var}(\mathcal{N}^{S}_{\Lambda_{\ell},k,\nu})\sim\frac{q^{\nu}}{q^{\kappa}}\int_{\mathrm{Sp}(2\kappa-2)}\left|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1}+\cdots+j_{\ell}=\nu\\
1\leq j_{1},\dots,j_{\ell}\end{subarray}}\mathrm{Tr}(U^{j_{1}})\cdots\mathrm{Tr}(U^{j_{\ell}})\right|^{2}dU.

Proof.

As in the proof of Theorem
5.6
, we separate the characters according to their Swan conductor, which is an odd integer
d(\Xi)<k
with maximal value
2\kappa-1
. The characters with maximal conductor are primitive, and the contribution from the others is negligible. So, we consider the sum over the primitive characters. By applying the result by Katz
[
Kat17
, Theorem 5.1]
, we obtain the desired result.
∎

6.3.
Relationship with Random Matrix Theory

In this section we discuss the computation of the integral

(37)

I_{\Lambda_{k}}^{S}(n;N):=\int_{\mathrm{Sp}(2N)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1}+\cdots+j_{k}=n\\
1\leq j_{1},\dots,j_{k}\end{subarray}}\mathrm{Tr}(U^{j_{1}})\cdots\mathrm{Tr}(U^{j_{k}})dU.

Note that
I_{\Lambda_{k}}^{S}(n;N)
measures the discrepancy of the objects that we discuss above, rather than the variance. However, from the point of view of random matrix theory, this is a more natural object to consider. The methods that we used to prove Theorem
1.5

apply to obtain a similar statement for the discrepancy:

Theorem 6.7
.

Assume that
k\leq n
. As
q\rightarrow\infty
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\#\mathcal{P}_{2g+1}}\sum_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{2g+1}}

\displaystyle\left(\mathcal{S}^{S}_{\Lambda_{k},n}(P)-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in\mathcal{M}_{n}\\
P\nmid f\end{subarray}}\Lambda_{k}(f)\right)

\displaystyle\sim\frac{(-1)^{k}q^{n/2}}{2}\int_{\mathrm{Sp}(2g)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1}+\cdots+j_{k}=n\\
1\leq j_{1},\dots,j_{k}\end{subarray}}\mathrm{Tr}(U^{j_{1}})\cdots\mathrm{Tr}(U^{j_{k}})dU.

Remark 6.8
.

Note that
\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in\mathcal{M}_{n}\\
P\nmid f\end{subarray}}\Lambda_{k}(f)
denotes the main term of
\mathcal{S}^{S}_{\Lambda_{k},n}(P)
, so Theorem
6.7
picks up on a contribution from the second-order terms. In the case of

\mathcal{N}_{\Lambda_{\ell},k,\nu}^{S}(v)
,
\langle\mathcal{N}_{\Lambda_{\ell},k,\nu}^{S}\rangle
is the limiting mean value rather than the main term, so the discrepancy above goes to zero as
q\rightarrow\infty
.

Back to integral (
37
),
Diaconis and Shahshahani
[
DS94
]
prove the following result (see also
[
DE01
]
).

Theorem 6.9
.

[
DS94
, Theorem 6]
Let
U
be Haar distributed on
\mathrm{Sp}(2N)
. Let
{\bf a}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{h})
with
a_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
. Then for
N\geq\sum_{j=1}^{h}a_{j}j
,

(38)

\mathbb{E}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{h}(\mathrm{Tr}(U^{j}))^{a_{j}}\right)=\prod_{j=1}^{h}(-1)^{(j-1)a_{j}}g_{j}(a_{j}),

where if
j
is odd,

(39)

g_{j}(a)=\begin{cases}0&a\mbox{ odd},\\
j^{a/2}(a-1)!!&a\mbox{ even},\end{cases}

and if
j
is even,

(40)

g_{j}(a)=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\lfloor a/2\rfloor}\binom{a}{2\ell}j^{\ell}(2\ell-1)!!

The above result is limited by the condition
N\geq\sum_{j=1}^{h}a_{j}j
.
Keating and Odgers
[
KO08
]
give a precise statement over a larger interval for the product of one and two traces. (See also Hughes and Rudnick
[
HR03
]
.)

Lemma 6.10
.

[
KO08
, Lemma 2]

Over
\mathrm{Sp}(2N)
, we have

\mathbb{E}(\mathrm{Tr}(U^{j}))=\begin{cases}2N&j=0,\\
-\eta_{j}&1\leq|j|\leq 2N,\\
0&\mbox{ otherwise,}\end{cases}

where we recall that
\eta_{j}
is given by equation (
27
).
For
0<j_{1}\leq j_{2}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\mathrm{Tr}(U^{j_{1}})\mathrm{Tr}(U^{j_{2}}))=

\displaystyle j_{1}(\mbox{if }1\leq j_{1}=j_{2}\leq 2N)

\displaystyle+2N(\mbox{if }j_{1}=j_{2}>2N)

\displaystyle-1(\mbox{if }N+1\leq j_{1}=j_{2}\leq 2N)

\displaystyle+\eta_{j_{1}}\eta_{j_{2}}(\mbox{if }1\leq j_{1}\leq j_{2}\leq 2N)

\displaystyle-1(\mbox{if }j_{1}=n-m,j_{2}=n+m,1\leq m\leq N,n\geq N+1).

As observed in Remark
6.8
, there are cases in which it would be more natural to consider the integral

(41)

I_{\Lambda_{k},2}^{S}(n;N):=\int_{\mathrm{Sp}(2N)}\left|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1}+\cdots+j_{k}=n\\
1\leq j_{1},\dots,j_{k}\end{subarray}}\mathrm{Tr}(U^{j_{1}})\cdots\mathrm{Tr}(U^{j_{k}})\right|^{2}dU.

In fact, Lemma
6.10
automatically gives a formula for
I_{\Lambda_{1},2}^{S}(n;N)
(also stated in
[
RW19
]
), below.

Corollary 6.11
.

If
k=1
, then
I_{\Lambda_{k},2}^{S}(n;N)
is given by

\int_{\mathrm{Sp}(2N)}\left|\mathrm{Tr}(U^{n})\right|^{2}dU=\begin{cases}n+\eta_{n}&1\leq n\leq N,\\
n-1+\eta_{n}&N+1\leq n\leq 2N,\\
2N&2N<n.\end{cases}

As
N\to\infty
,
I^{S}_{\Lambda_{k},2}(n;N)
is asymptotic to

I^{S}_{\Lambda_{1},2}(n;N)\sim\gamma_{\Lambda_{1},2}(c)N,

where
c=n/2N
and

\gamma_{\Lambda_{1},2}^{S}(c)=\begin{cases}c&0\leq c\leq 2,\\
2&2\leq c.\end{cases}

We study a more general setting for
I_{\Lambda_{k}}^{S}(n;N)
. For a matrix
U\in\mathrm{Sp}(2N)
, consider

\mathcal{L}_{U}(s):=\det(I-sU)=\prod_{n=1}^{2N}(1-se^{-i\theta_{n}}).

This
L
-function satisfies the functional equation

\mathcal{L}_{U}(s)=\det U^{*}s^{N}\mathcal{L}_{U^{*}}(1/s).

Mason and Snaith
[
MS18
]
prove the following result.

Theorem 6.12
.

[
MS18
, Theorem 3.3]
Let
A=\{\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{k}\}
, such that
\re(\alpha_{j})>0
, then

(42)

\int_{\mathrm{Sp}(2N)}\prod_{\alpha\in A}(-e^{-\alpha})\frac{\mathcal{L}_{U}^{\prime}}{\mathcal{L}_{U}}(e^{-\alpha})dU

is equal to

(43)

\sum_{S\subseteq A}e^{-2N\sum_{\hat{\alpha}\in S}\hat{\alpha}}(-1)^{|S|}\sqrt{\frac{Z(S,S)Z(S^{-},S^{-})Y(S^{-})}{Y(S)Z^{\dagger}(S^{-},S)^{2}}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}A-S=W_{1}+\cdots+W_{R}\\
|W_{r}|\leq 2\end{subarray}}\prod_{r=1}^{R}H_{S}(W_{r}).

Here

S^{-}=\{-\hat{\alpha},\hat{\alpha}\in S\},

Y(A)=\prod_{\alpha\in A}z(2\alpha),\quad\mbox{ and }\quad Z(A,B)=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha\in A\\
\beta\in B\end{subarray}}z(\alpha+\beta),

where
z(\alpha)=\frac{1}{1-e^{-\alpha}}
, and the dagger imposes the additional restriction that a factor
z(\alpha)
is omitted when the argument is zero.

Finally, the sum over
W_{r}
is a sum over all the different set partitions of
A-S
and

H_{S}(W)=\begin{cases}\left(\sum_{\hat{\alpha}\in S}\frac{z^{\prime}}{z}(\alpha-\hat{\alpha})-\frac{z^{\prime}}{z}(\alpha+\hat{\alpha})\right)+\frac{z^{\prime}}{z}(2\alpha)&W=\{\alpha\}\subseteq A-S,\\
\left(\frac{z^{\prime}}{z}\right)^{\prime}(\alpha+\beta)&W=\{\alpha,\beta\}\subseteq A-S,\\
1&W=\varnothing.\end{cases}

Note that
\frac{z^{\prime}}{z}(\alpha)=-\frac{e^{-\alpha}}{1-e^{-\alpha}}
,
\left(\frac{z^{\prime}}{z}\right)^{\prime}(\alpha)=\frac{e^{-\alpha}}{(1-e^{-\alpha})^{2}}
.

We separate the sum over
S\subset A
according to
|S|
. We refer to the terms arising from
|S|=\ell
as
\ell
-swap terms. From now on, we will set
x_{i}:=e^{-\alpha_{i}}
. The integral then gives a generating series for (
37
). Since the goal is to have a fixed total sum of exponents, we can set all
x_{i}=x
(provided that there are no poles) and search for the coefficient of
x^{n}
.
After doing this, the
\ell
-swap terms are coefficients of
x^{2\ell N}
. Therefore, they only appear for
n\geq 2\ell N
. For example, it is immediate to see from formula (
43
) that when
n<2N
, we only get the
0
-swap terms. In fact, this is the case when
n<2N+2
. The
0
-swap terms in this should give the same result as Theorem
6.9
.

6.3.1.
0
-swap terms

The 0-swap terms arise from taking
S
empty. The
0
-swap terms of the integral in (
42
) are then given by terms of the form

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}A=W_{1}+\cdots+W_{R}\\
|W_{r}|\leq 2\end{subarray}}\prod_{r=1}^{R}H_{\varnothing}(W_{r}).

Each factor
H_{\varnothing}(W)
is either of the form
\frac{-x^{2}}{1-x^{2}}
when
|W|=1
or of the form
\frac{x_{i}x_{j}}{(1-x_{i}x_{j})^{2}}
when
|W|=2
.

First we will see how this compares with Theorem 6.9. Let
s
be the number of subsets
W_{r}
with cardinality
2
, so that
s+R=k
. For each
W_{r}
of cardinality
2
, let
P_{2,r}=\prod_{\alpha_{i}\in W_{r}}x_{i}
, and for each
W_{r}
of cardinality
1
, let
P_{1,r}=x_{i}
, where
\alpha_{i}\in W_{r}
. Summing over
s
, we get that the final generating function is given by

(44)

(-1)^{k}\sum_{s\leq\lfloor k/2\rfloor}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}A=W_{1}+\cdots+W_{k-s}\\
1\leq|W_{r}|\leq 2\end{subarray}}\prod_{|W_{r}|=2}\frac{P_{2,r}}{(1-P_{2,r})^{2}}\prod_{|W_{r}|=1}\frac{P_{1,r}^{2}}{1-P_{1,r}^{2}}.

Expanding the products above gives

\displaystyle(-1)^{k}\sum_{s\leq\lfloor k/2\rfloor}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}A=W_{1}+\cdots+W_{k-s}\\
1\leq|W_{r}|\leq 2\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{r}\geq 1\\
|W_{r}|=2\end{subarray}}m_{r}P_{2,r}^{m_{r}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{r}\geq 1\\
|W_{r}|=1\end{subarray}}P_{1,r}^{2m_{r}}.

In order to recover Theorem
6.9
, we focus on the coefficient of

X_{{\bf a}}:=x_{1}\cdots x_{a_{1}}x_{a_{1}+1}^{2}\cdots x_{a_{1}+a_{2}}^{2}x_{a_{1}+a_{2}+1}^{3}\cdots

In other words, we have a monomial where the first
a_{1}
variables have exponent
1
, then the next
a_{2}
variables have exponent
2
, and so on. We have that
a_{1}+\cdots+a_{k}=k
, and if any
a_{j}
is
0
, then there are no variables with exponent
j
. We want to check that the coefficient of
X_{{\bf a}}
is the same as the one predicted by formula (
38
). Note that the ordering of the variables
x_{i}
does not affect the coefficient either in our calculation or in formula (
38
), which is why it suffices to check the coefficient when the variables are ordered according to their exponent, as above.

We first focus on those variables with a fixed exponent
j
. If
j
is odd, then the only contributions to the coefficient of
X_{{\bf a}}
coming from the factors
x_{a_{j-1}+1}^{j}\cdots x_{a_{j}}^{j}
arise from terms of the form
m_{r}P_{2,r}^{m_{r}}
, where
|W_{r}|=2
and
\alpha_{j}\in W_{r}
. Since
W_{r}
also contains a second variable, the total number of variables with exponent
j
in
X_{\bf a}
must be even, or in other words
a_{j}
is even. In this case, we have

\frac{a_{j}!}{2^{a_{j}/2}\left(\frac{a_{j}}{2}\right)!}=(a_{j}-1)!!

ways of partitioning the indices of the variables
x_{a_{j-1}+1},\dots,x_{a_{j}}
into pairs; each partition has a coefficient
j^{a_{j}/2}
. This is precisely formula (
39
).

Meanwhile if
j
is even, then the contributions to the coefficient of
x_{a_{j-1}+1}^{j}\cdots x_{a_{j}}^{j}
arise from cases where, for some
\ell\leq a_{j}/2
,
\ell
pairs of the variables appear in two-element subsets
W_{r}
, and the remaining
a_{j}-2\ell
variables appear in single-element subsets. The coefficient coming from the
\ell
pairs of variables is
(2\ell-1)!!j^{\ell}
, as before, whereas the single-element subsets do not change the coefficient of
X_{\bf a}
. For each
\ell
, there are
\binom{a_{j}}{2\ell}
ways of choosing which variables lie in two-element subsets, so the coefficient is given by

\sum_{\ell=0}^{\lfloor a_{j}/2\rfloor}\binom{a_{j}}{2\ell}j^{\ell}(2\ell-1)!!,

which is precisely formula (
40
).

Proceeding inductively on
j
, we must multiply all values of
g_{j}(a_{j})
in order to obtain the coefficient of
X_{{\bf a}}
. Note that
g_{j}(0)
is always
1
. The coefficient is identical up to the sign; in particular, we must verify that the coefficient of
X_{{\bf a}}
in formula (
38
) has sign
(-1)^{k}
. But the sign in this formula is

(-1)^{\sum_{j=1}^{h}ja_{j}-a_{j}}=(-1)^{k}(-1)^{\sum_{j=1}^{h}ja_{j}}=(-1)^{k},

since
a_{1}+\cdots+a_{h}=k
, and since for a nonzero coefficient,
j
and
a_{j}
cannot both be odd. Thus we have recovered the result of Theorem
6.9
from Theorem
6.12
.

For the question of computing the
0
-swap terms in integral (
37
), we can let all the variables in (
44
) be the same, as previously discussed. We get

\displaystyle(-1)^{k}\sum_{s\leq\lfloor k/2\rfloor}\binom{k}{2s}\frac{(2s)!}{2^{s}s!}\left(\frac{x^{2}}{(1-x^{2})^{2}}\right)^{s}\left(\frac{x^{2}}{1-x^{2}}\right)^{k-2s}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{-1}{1-x^{2}}\right)^{k}\sum_{s\leq\lfloor k/2\rfloor}\binom{k}{2s}(2s-1)!!x^{2k-2s},

where the coefficient of each term in the first sum is counting the number of ways of choosing
s
sets
W
of cardinality 2 and
k-2s
sets
W
of cardinality
1
.

This gives

\displaystyle(-1)^{k}\sum_{j\geq 0}\binom{k+j-1}{j}x^{2j}\sum_{s\leq\lfloor k/2\rfloor}\binom{k}{2s}(2s-1)!!x^{2k-2s}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-1)^{k}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\geq k\end{subarray}}x^{2n}\sum_{s\leq\lfloor k/2\rfloor}\binom{n+s-1}{n+s-k}\binom{k}{2s}(2s-1)!!

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-1)^{k}k\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\geq k\end{subarray}}x^{2n}\sum_{s\leq\lfloor k/2\rfloor}\frac{(n+s-1)!}{2^{s}s!(n+s-k)!(k-2s)!}

for the final contribution of the
0
-swaps terms.

6.3.2.
1
-swap terms

The
1
-swap terms are more difficult to compute than the
0
-swap terms. In this section we do this computation for
k=2
. First notice that the 1-swap terms are given by

\displaystyle-\frac{x_{1}^{2N+2}}{x_{1}^{2}-1}\left(-\frac{x_{2}}{x_{1}-x_{2}}+\frac{x_{1}x_{2}}{1-x_{1}x_{2}}-\frac{x_{2}^{2}}{1-x_{2}^{2}}\right)-\frac{x_{2}^{2N+2}}{x_{2}^{2}-1}\left(-\frac{x_{1}}{x_{2}-x_{1}}+\frac{x_{1}x_{2}}{1-x_{1}x_{2}}-\frac{x_{1}^{2}}{1-x_{1}^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle x_{1}x_{2}\frac{x_{1}^{2N+1}(x_{1}x_{2}^{3}-2x_{2}^{2}+x_{1}x_{2}-x_{1}^{2}+1)-x_{2}^{2N+1}(x_{2}x_{1}^{3}-2x_{1}^{2}+x_{1}x_{2}-x_{2}^{2}+1)}{(x_{1}^{2}-1)(x_{2}^{2}-1)(x_{1}-x_{2})(x_{1}x_{2}-1)}

By factoring
(x_{1}-x_{2})
from the numerator and taking the limit as
x_{2}\to x_{1}
, we obtain

\displaystyle-x^{2}\frac{(2N-1)x^{2N+4}-4Nx^{2N+2}+(2N+1)x^{2N}}{(1-x^{2})^{3}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-x^{2N+2}\frac{2N(1-x^{2})^{2}+(1-x^{4})}{(1-x^{2})^{3}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-x^{2N+2}\left(\frac{2N}{1-x^{2}}+\frac{(1+x^{2})}{(1-x^{2})^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2Nx^{2N+2}\sum_{j\geq 0}x^{2j}-x^{2N}\sum_{j\geq 1}jx^{2j}-x^{2N+2}\sum_{j\geq 0}jx^{2j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-x^{2N}\sum_{j\geq 0}2Nx^{2j+2}-x^{2N}\sum_{j\geq 1}jx^{2j}-x^{2N}\sum_{j\geq 0}jx^{2j+2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sum_{j\geq 1}(2N+2j-1)x^{2N+2j}.

6.3.3.
2
-swap terms

For
k=2
, the
2
-swap terms are

\displaystyle x_{1}^{2N+1}

\displaystyle x_{2}^{2N+1}\frac{x_{1}x_{2}(x_{1}-x_{2})^{2}}{(1-x_{1}x_{2})^{2}(1-x_{1}^{2})(1-x_{2}^{2})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle x_{1}^{2N+1}x_{2}^{2N+1}(x_{1}^{2}-2x_{1}x_{2}+x_{2}^{2})\sum_{j_{0}\geq 0}x_{1}^{2j_{0}}\sum_{h_{0}\geq 0}x_{2}^{2h_{0}}\sum_{j_{1}\geq 1}x_{1}^{j}x_{2}^{j},

which yields 0 when
x_{2}\rightarrow x_{1}
.

When
k=2
, the following corollary is a result of the above computations.

Corollary 6.13
.

For
k=2
, the integral
I_{\Lambda_{2}}^{S}(n;N)
is given by

\int_{\mathrm{Sp}(2N)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1}+j_{2}=n\\
1\leq j_{1},j_{2}\end{subarray}}\mathrm{Tr}(U^{j_{1}})\mathrm{Tr}(U^{j_{2}})dU=\begin{cases}\left(n-1\right)\eta_{n}&2\leq n\leq 2N+1,\\
0&2N+2\leq n.\end{cases}

As
N\to\infty
and for
n
even,
I_{\Lambda_{2}}^{S}(n;N)
is asymptotic to

I_{\Lambda_{2}}^{S}(n;N)\sim\gamma_{\Lambda_{2}}^{S}(c)N,

where
c=n/2N
and

\gamma_{\Lambda_{2}}^{S}(c)=\begin{cases}c&0\leq c\leq 1,\\
0&1\leq c.\end{cases}

Proof.

By gathering together the contributions from the
0
-swap,
1
-swap, and
2
-swap terms in the case
k=2
, we get

\sum_{n\geq 2}I_{\Lambda_{2}}^{S}(n;N)x^{n}=2\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\geq 2\end{subarray}}\left(m-\frac{1}{2}\right)x^{2m}-\sum_{j\geq 1}(2N+2j-1)x^{2N+2j}.

∎

Note that we could have recovered this formula from Lemma
6.10
as well.

7.
Unitary averages of the von Mangoldt convolution

The goal of this section is to recover the results of
[
KRRGR18
]
for the convolution of the von Mangoldt function. These results were obtained by Keating and Rudnick
[
KR14a
]
for the von Mangoldt function itself; here we consider
k
-fold convolutions of the von Mangoldt function, as in the previous section. We will follow several of the ideas and arguments from
[
KR14a
]
.

7.1.
Average of the von Mangoldt convolution function over the short intervals

We start by recalling the notation of short intervals in
\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]
. Let
A\in\mathcal{M}_{n}
and let
0\leq h\leq n-2
. Then let

I(A;h)=\{f\in\mathcal{M}\,:\,|f-A|\leq q^{h}\}.

We are interested in studying the following sum, which is the von Mangoldt convolution analogue to the problem in Section 2 of

[
KR14a
]
:

\mathcal{N}^{U}_{0,\Lambda_{k}}(A;h)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in I(A;h)\\
f(0)\not=0\end{subarray}}\Lambda_{k}(f).

First we consider

M_{0}(n;\Lambda_{k}\chi)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in\mathcal{M}_{n}\\
f(0)\not=0\end{subarray}}\Lambda_{k}(f)\chi(f).

Lemma
2.2
becomes the following statement.

Lemma 7.1
.

Let
\chi
be even.
We have, for
k\leq n
,

\displaystyle M_{0}(n;\Lambda_{k}\chi)=

\displaystyle(-1)^{k}q^{n/2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1}+\cdots+j_{k}=n\\
1\leq j_{1},\dots,j_{k}\end{subarray}}\mathrm{Tr}(\Theta_{\chi}^{j_{1}})\cdots\mathrm{Tr}(\Theta_{\chi}^{j_{k}})+O\left(q^{\frac{n-1}{2}}\right),

and
M_{0}(n;\Lambda_{k}\chi)=0
for
1\leq n<k
.

Now we compute the mean.

Lemma 7.2
.

We have

\langle\mathcal{N}^{U}_{0,\Lambda_{k}}\rangle=\frac{1}{q^{n}}\sum_{A\in\mathcal{M}_{n}}\mathcal{N}^{U}_{0,\Lambda_{k}}(A;h)=\frac{q^{h+1}}{q^{n}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in\mathcal{M}_{n}\\
f(0)\not=0\end{subarray}}\Lambda_{k}(f)=q^{h+1}\binom{n-1}{k-1}+O\left(q^{h}\right).

Proof.

See Lemma
6.5
.
∎

We remark that Keating and Rudnick have a more precise expression in
[
KR14a
]
. Indeed, by (
6.2
),

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in\mathcal{M}_{n}\\
f(0)\not=0\end{subarray}}\Lambda_{k}(f)=(q-1)^{k}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\binom{-k}{m}\binom{-k}{n-k-m}(-1)^{n-k}q^{n-k-m}.

By setting
k=1
, we get

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in\mathcal{M}_{n}\\
f(0)\not=0\end{subarray}}\Lambda(f)=q^{n}-1,

and this results in

\langle\mathcal{N}^{U}_{0,\Lambda}\rangle=q^{h+1}\left(1-\frac{1}{q^{n}}\right),

as in
[
KR14a
]
.

Our next goal is to find the variance

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(\mathcal{N}^{U}_{0,\Lambda_{k}})=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{q^{n}}\sum_{A\in\mathcal{M}_{n}}\left|\mathcal{N}^{U}_{0,\Lambda_{k}}(A;h)-\langle\mathcal{N}^{U}_{0,\Lambda_{k}}\rangle\right|^{2}.

Next we will follow several ideas of
[
KR14a
]
. Define

\tilde{\Psi}_{k}(n;Q,A)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\deg(f)=n\\
f\equiv A\,(\mathrm{mod}\,Q)\end{subarray}}\Lambda_{k}(f),

where the sum takes places over all the polynomials of degree
n
, not necessarily monic.

For a
f\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]
with
f(0)\not=0
, we consider the following involution:

f^{*}(T)=T^{\deg(f)}f\left(\frac{1}{T}\right).

Then we have that
(fg)^{*}=f^{*}g^{*}
and
\Lambda_{k}(f^{*})=\Lambda_{k}(f)
, with a proof very similar to
[
KR14a
, Lemma 4.1]
.

Notice that, for a fixed
h
, every
f\in\mathcal{M}_{n}
with
n\geq h+1
can be written uniquely as

f=T^{h+1}B+g,\quad B\in\mathcal{M}_{n-(h+1)},\quad\deg(g)\leq h.

We can then decompose
\mathcal{M}_{n}
as

(45)

\mathcal{M}_{n}=\bigsqcup_{B\in\mathcal{M}_{n-(h+1)}}I(T^{h+1}B;h).

The involution
*
gives a bijection

\displaystyle*:\mathcal{M}_{n-(h+1)}

\displaystyle\rightarrow

\displaystyle\{\deg(B^{*})\leq n-h-1\,:\,B^{*}(0)=1\}

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle\mapsto

\displaystyle B^{*}.

Lemma 7.3
.

Let
B\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]
such that
\deg(B)=n-h-1
. Then

\mathcal{N}^{U}_{0,\Lambda_{k}}(T^{h+1}B;h)=\tilde{\Psi}_{k}(n;T^{n-h},B^{*}).

Proof.

This is an extension of
[
KR14a
, Lemma 4.2]
. It is proven that

f\in I(T^{h+1}B;h)\Leftrightarrow f^{*}\equiv B^{*}\,(\mathrm{mod}\,T^{n-h}).

The result then follows because if
f
runs over
I(T^{h+1}B;h)
such that
f(0)\not=0
, then
f^{*}
runs over all polynomials of degree exactly
n
satisfying
f^{*}\equiv B^{*}\,(\mathrm{mod}\,T^{n-h})
, and for these,
\Lambda_{k}(f^{*})=\Lambda_{k}(f)
.
∎

By the orthogonality relations for Dirichlet characters,

(46)

\tilde{\Psi}_{k}(n;T^{n-h},B^{*})=\frac{1}{\Phi(T^{n-h})}\sum_{\chi\,(\mathrm{mod}\,T^{n-h})}\overline{\chi}(B^{*})\sum_{\deg(f^{*})=n}\Lambda_{k}(f^{*})\chi(f^{*}).

Notice that only even characters contribute, since
\Lambda_{k}(cf)=\Lambda_{k}(f)
for
c\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
, and therefore, an odd character produces a factor of the form
\sum_{c\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}}\chi(c)=0
in the inner sum.
When the character is even, it contributes with a term of the form

\overline{\chi}(B^{*})\frac{q-1}{\Phi(T^{n-h})}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in\mathcal{M}_{n}\\
f(0)\not=0\end{subarray}}\Lambda_{k}(f)\chi(f)=\frac{\overline{\chi}(B^{*})}{q^{n-h-1}}M_{0}(n;\Lambda_{k}\chi).

The number of even characters modulo
T^{n-h}
is
\frac{\Phi(T^{n-h})}{q-1}=q^{n-h-1}
.
The trivial character
\chi_{0}
contributes the term

\frac{q-1}{\Phi(T^{n-h})}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in\mathcal{M}_{n}\\
f(0)\not=0\end{subarray}}\Lambda_{k}(f)=\frac{(q-1)q^{n-h-1}}{\Phi(T^{n-h})}\langle\mathcal{N}^{U}_{0,\Lambda_{k}}\rangle=\langle\mathcal{N}^{U}_{0,\Lambda_{k}}\rangle.

Combining (
46
) with Lemma
7.3
, and subtracting the mean, we obtain,

(47)

\mathcal{N}^{U}_{0,\Lambda_{k}}(T^{h+1}B;h)-\langle\mathcal{N}^{U}_{0,\Lambda_{k}}\rangle=\frac{1}{q^{n-h-1}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\chi\not=\chi_{0},\,(\mathrm{mod}\,T^{n-h})\\
\text{ even}\end{subarray}}\overline{\chi}(B^{*})M_{0}(n;\Lambda_{k}\chi).

Applying (
45
) to equation (
47
),

\mathrm{Var}(\mathcal{N}^{U}_{0,\Lambda_{k}})=\frac{1}{q^{n-h-1}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}B^{*}\,(\mathrm{mod}\,T^{n-h})\\
B^{*}(0)=1\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{q^{2(n-h-1)}}\left|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\chi\not=\chi_{0},\,(\mathrm{mod}\,T^{n-h})\\
\text{ even}\end{subarray}}\overline{\chi}(B^{*})M_{0}(n;\Lambda_{k}\chi)\right|^{2}.

Expanding, and using orthogonality relations, we get

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(\mathcal{N}^{U}_{0,\Lambda_{k}})=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{q^{2(n-h-1)}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\chi_{1},\chi_{2}\not=\chi_{0},\,(\mathrm{mod}\,T^{n-h})\\
\text{ even}\end{subarray}}M_{0}(n;\Lambda_{k}\chi_{1})\overline{M_{0}(n;\Lambda_{k}\chi_{2})}

\displaystyle\times\frac{1}{q^{n-h-1}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}B^{*}\,(\mathrm{mod}\,T^{n-h})\\
B^{*}(0)=1\end{subarray}}\overline{\chi_{1}}(B^{*})\chi_{2}(B^{*})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{q^{2(n-h-1)}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\chi\not=\chi_{0},\,(\mathrm{mod}\,T^{n-h})\\
\text{ even}\end{subarray}}\left|M_{0}(n;\Lambda_{k}\chi)\right|^{2}.

Therefore, by Lemma
7.1
, we get

\mathrm{Var}(\mathcal{N}^{U}_{0,\Lambda_{k}})=\frac{q^{h+1}}{q^{n-h-1}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\chi\not=\chi_{0},\,(\mathrm{mod}\,T^{n-h})\\
\text{even}\end{subarray}}\left|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1}+\cdots+j_{k}=n\\
1\leq j_{1},\dots,j_{k}\end{subarray}}\mathrm{Tr}(\Theta_{\chi}^{j_{1}})\cdots\mathrm{Tr}(\Theta_{\chi}^{j_{k}})\right|^{2}\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}\right)\right).

We have now all the elements to prove Theorem
1.3
.

Theorem 7.4
.

Let
k\leq n
.
As
q\rightarrow\infty
,

\mathrm{Var}(\mathcal{N}^{U}_{0,\Lambda_{k}})\sim q^{h+1}\int_{\mathrm{U}(n-h-2)}\left|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1}+\cdots+j_{k}=n\\
1\leq j_{1},\dots,j_{k}\end{subarray}}\mathrm{Tr}(U^{j_{1}})\cdots\mathrm{Tr}(U^{j_{k}})\right|^{2}dU.

Proof.

In order to apply the equidistribution result of Katz
[
Kat13b
]
, we need to take the sum over the primitive characters. There are
q^{n-h-1}\left(1-\frac{1}{q}\right)
of those. For the non-primitive characters, we bound
|M(n;\Lambda_{k}\chi)|\ll q^{n/2}
, and there are
O(q^{n-h-2})
of these. This gives the desired result.
∎

7.2.
Average of the von Mangoldt convolution function over arithmetic progressions

We are interested in studying the following sum, which is the von Mangoldt convolution analogue to the problem in Section 5 of

[
KR14a
]
:

\mathcal{S}^{U}_{\Lambda_{k},n,Q}(A)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in\mathcal{M}_{n}\\
f\equiv A\,(\mathrm{mod}\,Q)\end{subarray}}\Lambda_{k}(f),

where
Q
is square-free and
A
is coprime to
Q
.
First we consider

M(n;\Lambda_{k}\chi)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in\mathcal{M}_{n}\end{subarray}}\Lambda_{k}(f)\chi(f).

Lemma
2.2
becomes the following statement.

Lemma 7.5
.

Let
\chi
be odd. For
k\leq n
, we have

M(n;\Lambda_{k}\chi)=(-1)^{k}q^{n/2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1}+\cdots+j_{k}=n\\
1\leq j_{1},\dots,j_{k}\end{subarray}}\mathrm{Tr}(\Theta_{\chi}^{j_{1}})\cdots\mathrm{Tr}(\Theta_{\chi}^{j_{k}}),

and
M(n;\Lambda_{k}\chi)=0
for
1\leq n<k
.

Following a similar argument to
[
KR16
, Section 4.1]
, which uses the orthogonality relations of Dirichlet characters to detect the arithmetic progression,

(48)

\mathcal{S}^{U}_{\Lambda_{k},n,Q}(A)=\frac{1}{\Phi(Q)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in\mathcal{M}_{n}\\
(f,Q)=1\end{subarray}}\Lambda_{k}(f)+\frac{1}{\Phi(Q)}\sum_{\chi\not=\chi_{0}}\overline{\chi(A)}M(n;\Lambda_{k}\chi).

Lemma 7.6
.

We have that

\mathcal{S}^{U}_{\Lambda_{k},n,Q}(A)=\frac{1}{\Phi(Q)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in\mathcal{M}_{n}\\
(f,Q)=1\end{subarray}}\Lambda_{k}(f)\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{q}\right)\right)=\frac{q^{n}}{\Phi(Q)}\binom{n-1}{k-1}+O(q^{n-1}).

Proof.

The proof is identical to that of Lemma
6.2
.
∎

As usual, we are interested in the variance.

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(\mathcal{S}^{U}_{\Lambda_{k},n,Q})=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\Phi(Q)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}A\,(\mathrm{mod}\,Q)\\
(A,Q)=1\end{subarray}}\left|\mathcal{S}^{U}_{\Lambda_{k},n,Q}(A)-\frac{1}{\Phi(Q)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in\mathcal{M}_{n}\\
(f,Q)=1\end{subarray}}\Lambda_{k}(f)\right|^{2}.

Applying this to equation (
48
), we get

(49)

\mathrm{Var}(\mathcal{S}^{U}_{\Lambda_{k},n,Q})=\frac{1}{\Phi(Q)^{2}}\sum_{\chi\not=\chi_{0}}|M(n;\Lambda_{k}\chi)|^{2}.

From the bound of
M(n;\Lambda_{k}\chi)
provided by the Riemann Hypothesis, we obtain that the contribution from the even characters is

\ll\frac{\Phi_{\text{ev}}(Q)}{\Phi(Q)^{2}}q^{n}\ll\frac{q^{n-1}}{\Phi(Q)},

where
\Phi_{\text{ev}}(Q)=\frac{\Phi(Q)}{q-1}
is the number of even characters. This allows us to rewrite (
49
) as

\mathrm{Var}(\mathcal{S}^{U}_{\Lambda_{k},n,Q})=\frac{1}{\Phi(Q)^{2}}\sum_{\chi\text{ odd and primitive }}|M(n;\Lambda_{k}\chi)|^{2}+O\left(\frac{q^{n-1}}{\Phi(Q)}\right).

By Lemma
7.5
, we get

\mathrm{Var}(\mathcal{S}^{U}_{\Lambda_{k},n,Q})=\frac{q^{n}}{\Phi(Q)^{2}}\sum_{\chi\text{ odd and primitive }}\left|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1}+\cdots+j_{k}=n\\
1\leq j_{1},\dots,j_{k}\end{subarray}}\mathrm{Tr}(\Theta_{\chi}^{j_{1}})\cdots\mathrm{Tr}(\Theta_{\chi}^{j_{k}}),\right|^{2}+O\left(\frac{q^{n-1}}{\Phi(Q)}\right).

By applying Katz’s equidistribution Theorem from
[
Kat13a
]
, we obtain the statement of Theorem
1.4
.

Theorem 7.7
.

For
k\leq n
, as
q\rightarrow\infty
,

\mathrm{Var}(\mathcal{S}^{U}_{\Lambda_{k},n,Q})\sim\frac{q^{n}}{|Q|}\int_{\mathrm{U}(\deg(Q)-1)}\left|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1}+\cdots+j_{k}=n\\
1\leq j_{1},\dots,j_{k}\end{subarray}}\mathrm{Tr}(U^{j_{1}})\cdots\mathrm{Tr}(U^{j_{k}})\right|^{2}dU.

7.3.
Relationship with Random Matrix Theory

In this section we discuss what is known about the computation of the integral

(50)

I_{\Lambda_{k}}^{U}(n;N):=\int_{\mathrm{U}(N)}\left|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1}+\cdots+j_{k}=n\\
1\leq j_{1},\dots,j_{k}\end{subarray}}\mathrm{Tr}(U^{j_{1}})\cdots\mathrm{Tr}(U^{j_{k}})\right|^{2}dU.

In
[
DS94
]
Diaconis and Shahshahani prove the following result (see also
[
DE01
, Theorem 2.1]
and
[
DG06
]
).

Theorem 7.8
.

[
DS94
, Theorem 2]
Let
U
be Haar distributed on
\mathrm{U}(N)
. Let
{\bf a}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{k})
,
{\bf b}=(b_{1},\dots,b_{k})
, with
a_{i},b_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
. Then for
N\geq\max\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{k}a_{j}j,\sum_{j=1}^{k}b_{j}j\right\}
,

\mathbb{E}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{k}(\mathrm{Tr}(U^{j}))^{a_{j}}\overline{(\mathrm{Tr}(U^{j}))}^{b_{j}}\right)=\delta_{\bf ab}\prod_{j=1}^{n}j^{a_{j}}a_{j}!

Also, from
[
DE01
, Theorem 2.1]
, we have

\mathbb{E}\left(\mathrm{Tr}(U^{j_{1}})\overline{\mathrm{Tr}(U^{j_{2}})}\right)=\delta_{j_{1},j_{2}}\max\{j_{1},N\}.

The cases
k=1,2
of the above result were also known to Dyson
[
Dys62
]
.

In order to compute the integral from (
50
), we are interested in the case when
{\bf a}={\bf b}
, so that the formula is nonzero. In the integral setting, Theorem
7.8
implies that when
n\leq N
,

(51)

\int_{\mathrm{U}(N)}\left|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1}+\cdots+j_{k}=n\\
1\leq j_{1},\dots,j_{k}\end{subarray}}\mathrm{Tr}(U^{j_{1}})\cdots\mathrm{Tr}(U^{j_{k}})\right|^{2}dU=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a_{1}+\cdots+a_{n}=k\\
a_{1}+a_{2}2+\cdots+a_{n}n=n\end{subarray}}\prod_{j=1}^{n}j^{a_{j}}a_{j}!

The product inside the sum is the number of permutations in
\mathbb{S}_{n}
that commute with a permutation that has
a_{1}
cycles of length
1
,
a_{2}
cycles of length
2
, etc. In other words, we are summing the orders of all the possible centralizers of permutations with exactly
k
cycles.

The above results are restricted to
n\leq N
or to
k=1
. If we want to consider other possibilities we must work with a more general setting. Consider for a matrix
U\in\mathrm{U}(N)

\mathcal{L}_{U}(s):=\det(I-sU)=\prod_{n=1}^{N}(1-se^{-i\theta_{n}}).

This
L
-function satisfies the functional equation

\mathcal{L}_{U}(s)=(-1)^{N}\det U^{*}s^{N}\mathcal{L}_{U^{*}}(1/s).

Conrey and Snaith
[
CS08
]
prove the following result.

Theorem 7.9
.

[
CS08
, Theorem 3]
Let
A=\{\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{k}\},B=\{\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{k}\}
, such that
\re(\alpha_{j}),\re(\beta_{j})>0
, then

(52)

\int_{\mathrm{U}(N)}\prod_{\alpha\in A}(-e^{-\alpha})\frac{\mathcal{L}_{U}^{\prime}}{\mathcal{L}_{U}}(e^{-\alpha})\prod_{\beta\in B}(-e^{-\beta})\frac{\mathcal{L}_{U^{*}}^{\prime}}{\mathcal{L}_{U^{*}}}(e^{-\beta})dU

is equal to

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subseteq A,T\subseteq B\\
|S|=|T|\end{subarray}}e^{-N(\sum_{\hat{\alpha}\in S}\hat{\alpha}+\sum_{\hat{\beta}\in T}\hat{\beta})}\frac{Z(S,T)Z(S^{-},T^{-})}{Z^{\dagger}(S,S^{-})Z^{\dagger}(T,T^{-})}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(A-S)+(B-T)=W_{1}+\cdots+W_{R}\\
|W_{r}|\leq 2\end{subarray}}\prod_{r=1}^{R}H_{S,T}(W_{r}).

Here

S^{-}=\{-\hat{\alpha},\hat{\alpha}\in S\},T^{-}=\{-\hat{\beta},\hat{\beta}\in S\},

and

Z(A,B)=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha\in A\\
\beta\in B\end{subarray}}z(\alpha+\beta),

where
z(\alpha)=\frac{1}{1-e^{-\alpha}}
, and the dagger imposes the additional restriction that a factor
z(\alpha)
is omitted when the argument is zero.

Finally, the sum over
W_{r}
is a sum over all the different set partitions of
A-S
and
B-T
and

H_{S,T}(W)=\begin{cases}\sum_{\hat{\alpha}\in S}\frac{z^{\prime}}{z}(\alpha-\hat{\alpha})-\sum_{\hat{\beta}\in T}\frac{z^{\prime}}{z}(\alpha+\hat{\beta})&W=\{\alpha\}\subseteq A-S,\\
\sum_{\hat{\beta}\in T}\frac{z^{\prime}}{z}(\beta-\hat{\beta})-\sum_{\hat{\alpha}\in S}\frac{z^{\prime}}{z}(\beta+\hat{\alpha})&W=\{\beta\}\subseteq B-T,\\
\left(\frac{z^{\prime}}{z}\right)^{\prime}(\alpha+\beta)&W=\{\alpha,\beta\},\alpha\in A-S,\beta\in B-T,\\
1&W=\varnothing.\end{cases}

Recall that, as in the symplectic case of Theorem
6.12
,
\frac{z^{\prime}}{z}(\alpha)=-\frac{e^{-\alpha}}{1-e^{-\alpha}}
,
\left(\frac{z^{\prime}}{z}\right)^{\prime}(\alpha)=\frac{e^{-\alpha}}{(1-e^{-\alpha})^{2}}
.

From now on, we will set
x_{i}:=e^{-\alpha_{i}}
and
y_{i}:=e^{-\beta_{i}}
.
We will proceed to investigate some particular swaps of the above result. Recall that our goal is to evaluate integral (
50
). Notice that Theorem
7.9
provides a generating function for
\mathbb{E}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{k}(\mathrm{Tr}(M^{j}))^{a_{j}}\overline{(\mathrm{Tr}(M^{j}))}^{b_{j}}\right)
and therefore, we are interested in the case in which the exponent of
x_{i}
is matched with the exponent of
y_{i}
, with a fixed total weight.

7.3.1.
0
-swap terms

The
0
-swap terms arise from taking
S
and
T
empty. We will recover equation (
51
) in this way. The
0
-swap terms of integral (
52
) are then given by terms of the form

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}A+B=W_{1}+\cdots+W_{R}\\
|W_{r}|\leq 2\end{subarray}}\prod_{r=1}^{R}H_{\varnothing,\varnothing}(W_{r}).

Notice that these terms contribute when
A+B=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\{\alpha_{j},\beta_{\sigma(j)}\}
. In this way we find the terms

\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma\in\mathbb{S}_{k}}\prod_{j=1}^{k}\frac{x_{j}y_{\sigma(j)}}{(1-x_{j}y_{\sigma(j)})^{2}}=

\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma\in\mathbb{S}_{k}}\prod_{j=1}^{k}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\ell x_{j}^{\ell}y_{\sigma(j)}^{\ell}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma\in\mathbb{S}_{k}}\sum_{\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{k}=1}^{\infty}\ell_{1}x_{1}^{\ell_{1}}y_{\sigma(1)}^{\ell_{1}}\cdots\ell_{k}x_{k}^{\ell_{k}}y_{\sigma(k)}^{\ell_{k}}.

The above can be more clearly written as

\sum_{\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{k}=1}^{\infty}\ell_{1}\cdots\ell_{k}x_{1}^{\ell_{1}}\cdots x_{k}^{\ell_{k}}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathbb{S}_{k}}y_{\sigma(1)}^{\ell_{1}}\cdots y_{\sigma(k)}^{\ell_{k}}.

We regroup all the
x_{i}
’s according to their common exponent in the above sum. More precisely, say that for exponent
j
, we have
x_{i_{j,1}}\cdots x_{i_{j,a_{j}}}
and those are the only
x_{i}
’s with exponent
j
. The terms that contribute to integral (
50
) are those that are multiplied by
y_{i_{j,1}}\cdots y_{i_{j,a_{j}}}
. But this means that we must have

y_{i_{j,1}}\cdots y_{i_{j,a_{j}}}=y_{\sigma(i_{j,1})}\cdots y_{\sigma(i_{j,a_{j}})}.

In sum, the
\sigma\in\mathbb{S}_{k}
that contribute to the final sum giving the value of integral (
50
) are exactly those that satisfy
\sigma\in\mathbb{S}_{\{i_{j,1},\cdots i_{j,a_{j}}\}}
. There are
a_{j}!
of those. To get (
50
), we must isolate the coefficient corresponding to the restriction
a_{1}+a_{2}2+\cdots+a_{n}n=n
. This recovers formula (
51
). We remark that the 0-swaps terms contribute to (
50
) not only when
n\leq N
, but also when
n>N
. In this latter case, the final value has also contributions from other terms.

7.3.2.
1
-swap terms

The 1-swap terms are more difficult to compute than the 0-swap terms. In this section we do this computation for
k=2
, when
n\leq 2N+1
.

We first consider
S=\{\alpha_{1}\}
,
T=\{\beta_{1}\}
. The term corresponding to these
S
and
T
is given by

-x_{1}^{N}y_{1}^{N}\frac{x_{1}y_{1}}{(1-x_{1}y_{1})^{2}}\left(\frac{x_{2}y_{2}}{(1-x_{2}y_{2})^{2}}+\frac{x_{2}y_{2}(1-x_{1}y_{1})^{2}}{(x_{2}-x_{1})(1-x_{2}y_{1})(y_{2}-y_{1})(1-x_{1}y_{2})}\right).

The remaining three terms are similar; adding all four together, with a little computation, yields

\displaystyle-\frac{(x_{1}^{N}y_{1}^{N}+x_{2}^{N}y_{2}^{N})x_{1}y_{1}x_{2}y_{2}}{(1-x_{1}y_{1})^{2}(1-x_{2}y_{2})^{2}}-\frac{(x_{1}^{N}y_{2}^{N}+x_{2}^{N}y_{1}^{N})x_{1}y_{1}x_{2}y_{2}}{(1-x_{1}y_{2})^{2}(1-x_{2}y_{1})^{2}}

\displaystyle-\frac{x_{1}y_{1}x_{2}y_{2}}{(x_{2}-x_{1})(y_{2}-y_{1})}\left(\frac{x_{1}^{N}y_{1}^{N}+x_{2}^{N}y_{2}^{N}}{(1-x_{2}y_{1})(1-x_{1}y_{2})}-\frac{x_{1}^{N}y_{2}^{N}+x_{2}^{N}y_{1}^{N}}{(1-x_{1}y_{1})(1-x_{2}y_{2})}\right).

The denominator
(x_{2}-x_{1})(y_{2}-y_{1})
divides the numerator in parentheses, and we can expand the remaining denominators into power series, yielding

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-(x_{1}^{N}y_{1}^{N}+x_{2}^{N}y_{2}^{N})\sum_{j,h\geq 1}jhx_{1}^{j}y_{1}^{j}x_{2}^{h}y_{2}^{h}-(x_{1}^{N}y_{2}^{N}+x_{2}^{N}y_{1}^{N})\sum_{j,h\geq 1}jhx_{1}^{j}y_{2}^{j}x_{2}^{h}y_{1}^{h}

\displaystyle-\left(\sum_{j,h\geq 1}x_{1}^{j}y_{1}^{j}x_{2}^{h}y_{2}^{h}\right)\left(\sum_{j,h\geq 1}x_{1}^{j}y_{2}^{j}x_{2}^{h}y_{1}^{h}\right)

(53)

\displaystyle\times\left(-\sum_{j=0}^{N}x_{1}^{j}x_{2}^{N-j}\sum_{h=0}^{N}y_{1}^{h}y_{2}^{N-h}+\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}x_{1}^{j}x_{2}^{N-1-j}\sum_{h=0}^{N-1}y_{1}^{h}y_{2}^{N-1-h}+\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}x_{1}^{j+1}x_{2}^{N-j}\sum_{h=0}^{N-1}y_{1}^{h+1}y_{2}^{N-h}\right.

\displaystyle\left.-\sum_{j=0}^{N-2}x_{1}^{j+1}x_{2}^{N-1-j}\sum_{h=0}^{N-2}y_{1}^{h+1}y_{2}^{N-1-h}\right).

Our goal now is to find the sum of all coefficients of terms of the form
x_{1}^{a}x_{2}^{b}y_{1}^{a}y_{2}^{b}
, where
a+b=n
. Our strategy for each term in the sum above is to isolate terms where the exponent of
x_{1}
is equal to that of
y_{1}
and the same is true for
x_{2}
and
y_{2}
before finding the coefficient sum. We first address the first two sums above, which are similar to the 0-swap terms:

-(x_{1}^{N}y_{1}^{N}+x_{2}^{N}y_{2}^{N})\sum_{j,h\geq 1}jhx_{1}^{j}y_{1}^{j}x_{2}^{h}y_{2}^{h}-(x_{1}^{N}y_{2}^{N}+x_{2}^{N}y_{1}^{N})\sum_{j,h\geq 1}jhx_{1}^{j}y_{2}^{j}x_{2}^{h}y_{1}^{h}

For these sums, in line with formula (
51
), we get

\displaystyle-\frac{(n-N-1)(n-N)(n-N+1)}{3}-\delta_{n\equiv 0\,(\mathrm{mod}\,2)}\delta_{n\geq 2N+2}\frac{(n-N)^{2}-N^{2}}{2}.

Since
n\leq 2N+1
by assumption, we will ignore the second term.

We now turn our attention to the sums in parentheses (
53
). We first consider the first double sum:

\left(\sum_{j_{0},h_{0}\geq 1}x_{1}^{j_{0}}y_{1}^{j_{0}}x_{2}^{h_{0}}y_{2}^{h_{0}}\right)\left(\sum_{j_{1},h_{1}\geq 1}x_{1}^{j_{1}}y_{2}^{j_{1}}x_{2}^{h_{1}}y_{1}^{h_{1}}\right)\left(\sum_{j_{2}=0}^{N}x_{1}^{j_{2}}x_{2}^{N-j_{2}}\sum_{h_{2}=0}^{N}y_{1}^{h_{2}}y_{2}^{N-h_{2}}\right).

As above, our goal is to isolate terms where the exponent of
x_{1}
is equal to that of
y_{1}
and the same for
x_{2}
and
y_{2}
. These are precisely the terms where

j_{1}+j_{2}=h_{1}+h_{2}.

We now take the limit as
x_{1}\to x_{2}
and
y_{1}\to y_{2}
; we will call these
x
and
y
going forward. We need to determine the coefficient of the term
x^{n}y^{n}
; we call this coefficient
f(n,N)
. Note that

j_{0}+h_{0}+j_{1}+h_{1}+j_{2}+N-j_{2}=n,

or equivalently

(54)

j_{0}+h_{0}+j_{1}+h_{1}=n-N.

For each tuple
j_{0},h_{0},j_{1},h_{1}
satisfying (
54
), we first count the number of options of
j_{2},h_{2}
satisfying
j_{1}-h_{1}=h_{2}-j_{2}
.

Write
k:=j_{1}-h_{1}
(where
k
can be negative, and
|k|\leq N
since otherwise there are no choices of
j_{2}
and
h_{2}
with
k=j_{2}-h_{2}
). For a fixed
k
, there are
N-|k|+1
ways to choose
j_{2}
and
h_{2}
with
j_{2}-h_{2}=k
. It remains to count solutions to

(55)

j_{0}+h_{0}+2h_{1}=n-N-k,

keeping in mind that
j_{0},h_{0},h_{1}\geq 1
. The number of solutions to (
55
) is

\begin{cases}\left(\frac{n-N-k-2}{2}\right)^{2}&n-N-k-4\geq 0\mbox{ even},\\
\left(\frac{n-N-k-3}{2}\right)\left(\frac{n-N-k-1}{2}\right)&n-N-k-4\geq 0\mbox{ odd}.\\
\end{cases}

Thus the final coefficient of
x^{n}y^{n}
is

\displaystyle f(n,N)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}-N\leq k\leq N\\
k\leq n-N-4\end{subarray}}

\displaystyle(N-|k|+1)\left(\frac{n-N-k-2}{2}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{4}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}-N\leq k\leq N\\
k\leq n-N-4\end{subarray}}(N-|k|+1)\left(\frac{1+(-1)^{n-N-k+1}}{2}\right).

Since
n\leq 2N+1
, the restriction that
k\leq n-N-4
is more stringent than the restriction that
k\leq N
.
We also need to assume that
n\geq N+2
, since otherwise the contribution is empty.

Thus after expanding and simplifying, the coefficient of
x^{n}y^{n}
is given by

\displaystyle f(n,N)=

\displaystyle\frac{-n^{4}+4(2N+3)n^{3}-2(6N^{2}+24N+19)n^{2}+(8N^{3}+48N^{2}+80N+36)n}{48}

\displaystyle-\frac{4N^{4}+32N^{3}+80N^{2}+64N+9}{96}

\displaystyle+\frac{(-1)^{n}+2(-1)^{n+N}}{32}.

The remaining sums contribute coefficients
-f(n,N-1)
,
-f(n-2,N-1)
, and
f(n-2,N-2)
. Returning our attention to the full sum, and combining with our computation above, the 1-swap terms are then given by

\displaystyle-\frac{(n-N-1)(n-N)(n-N+1)}{3}

\displaystyle+f(n,N)-f(n,N-1)-f(n-2,N-1)+f(n-2,N-2)

Plugging in our expression for
f(n,N)
gives

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{-4(n-N)^{3}+6(n-N)^{2}-20(n-N)+21+3(-1)^{N+n}}{12}

which is the final result for the 1-swap terms.

For the final result for
N+2\leq n\leq 2N+1
we would need to consider the sum with the 0-swap terms, which gives

\frac{n(4n^{2}+3n-4)}{24}+\frac{(-1)^{n}n^{2}}{8}+\frac{-4(n-N)^{3}+6(n-N)^{2}-20(n-N)+21+3(-1)^{N+n}}{12}

In the case when
k=2
, we have shown the following corollary.

Corollary 7.10
.

Let
k=2
. The integral
I_{\Lambda_{2}}^{U}(n,N)
is given by

\displaystyle\int_{U(n)}

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1}+j_{2}=n\\
1\leq j_{1},j_{2}\end{subarray}}\mathrm{Tr}(U^{j_{1}})\mathrm{Tr}(U^{j_{2}})\right|^{2}dU

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\frac{n(4n^{2}+3n-4)}{24}+\frac{(-1)^{n}n^{2}}{8}&2\leq n\leq N+1,\\
\frac{n(4n^{2}+3n-4)}{24}+\frac{(-1)^{n}n^{2}}{8}+\frac{-4(n-N)^{3}+6(n-N)^{2}-20(n-N)+21+3(-1)^{N+n}}{12}&N+2\leq n\leq 2N+3.\end{cases}

In the limit as
N\to\infty
,
I_{\Lambda_{2}}^{U}(n,N)
is asymptotic to

I_{\Lambda_{2}}^{U}(n,N)\sim\gamma_{\Lambda_{2}}^{U}(c)N^{3},

where
c=n/N
and

\gamma_{\Lambda_{2}}^{U}(c)=\begin{cases}\frac{c^{3}}{6}&0\leq c\leq 1,\\
\\
\frac{c^{3}}{6}+\frac{(1-c)^{3}}{3}&1\leq c\leq 2.\\
\end{cases}
