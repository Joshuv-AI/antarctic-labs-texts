Reduced order models for Lagrangian Hydrodynamics
Dylan Matthew Copeland, Siu Wun Cheung, Kevin Huynh, Youngsoo Choi

Abstract
As a mathematical model of high-speed flow and shock wave propagation in a complex multimaterial setting, Lagrangian hydrodynamics is characterized by moving meshes, advection-dominated solutions, and moving shock fronts with sharp gradients. These challenges hinder the existing projection-based model reduction schemes from being practical. We develop several variations of projection-based reduced order model techniques for Lagrangian hydrodynamics by introducing three different reduced bases for position, velocity, and energy fields. A time-windowing approach is also developed to address the challenge imposed by the advection-dominated solutions. Lagrangian hydrodynamics is formulated as a nonlinear problem, which requires a proper hyper-reduction technique. Therefore, we apply the over-sampling DEIM and SNS approaches to reduce the complexity due to the nonlinear terms. Finally, we also present both a posteriori and a priori error bounds associated with our reduced order model. We compare the performance of the spatial and time-windowing reduced order modeling approaches in terms of accuracy and speed-up with respect to the corresponding full order model for several numerical examples, namely Sedov blast, Gresho vortices, Taylor-Green vortices, and triple-point problems.

Introduction

Physical simulations are key to developments in science, engineering, and
technology. Many physical processes are mathematically modeled by
time-dependent nonlinear partial differential equations. In many applications,
the analytical solution of such problems can not be obtained, and numerical
methods are developed to approximate the solutions efficiently. However,
subject to the complexity of the model problem and the size of the problem
domain, the computational cost can be prohibitively high. It may take a long
time to run one forward simulation even with high performance computing. In
decision-making applications where multiple forward simulations are needed, such

Many model reduction schemes have been developed to reduce the computational
cost of simulations while minimizing the error introduced in the reduction
process. Most of these approaches seek to extract an intrinsic solution subspace
for condensed solution representation by a linear combination of reduced basis
vectors. The reduced basis vectors are extracted from performing proper
orthogonal decomposition (POD) on the snapshot data of the FOM simulations. The
number of degrees of freedom is then reduced by substituting the ROM solution
representation into the (semi-)discretized governing equation. These approaches

In this paper, we present an alternative reduced order model technique for
advection-dominated problems. We consider the advection-dominated problems
arising in compressible gas dynamics. The Euler equation is used to model the
high-speed flow and shock wave propagation in a complex multimaterial setting,
and numerically solved in a moving Lagrangian frame, where the computational
mesh is moved along with the fluid velocity. In computational fluid dynamics,
the Lagrangian method is widely used to model different fluid phenomena, for
instance, the immersed boundary method

general high-order polynomial basis functions for approximating the state
variables, and curvilinear meshes for capturing the geometry of the flow and
maintaining robustness with respect to mesh motion, the method achieves
high-order accuracy. On the other hand, a modification is made to the
second-order Runge-Kutta method to compensate for the lack of total energy
conservation in standard high-order time integration techniques. The
introduction of an artificial viscosity tensor further generates the appropriate
entropy and ensures the Rankine-Hugoniot jump conditions at a shock boundary.
Although the method shows great capability and lots of advantages, forward
simulations can be computationally very expensive, especially in
three-dimensional applications with high-order finite elements over fine meshes.
It is therefore desirable to develop efficient ROM techniques for the Lagrangian
hydrodynamics simulation.

We adopt a time-windowing approach for reduced order
modeling of Lagrangian hydrodynamics. The time-windowing approach is introduced to
handle the difficulties arising from advection-dominated problems. Two
different time window division mechanisms will be considered, namely by the physical time
or the number of snapshots. Several techniques of
construction of offset variables which serve as reference points in the time
windows will be introduced. For a given time window, proper orthogonal
decomposition is used to extract the dominant modes in solution
representability, and an oversampling hyper-reduction technique is employed to
reduce the complexity due to the nonlinear terms in the governing equations. We
will present error estimates to theoretically justify our method and numerical
examples to exhibit the capabilities of our method. For the purpose of
reproducible research, open source codes are available as a branch of the Laghos

2
Lagrangian hydrodynamics

We consider the system of Euler equations of gas dynamics in a Lagrangian
reference frame
[
72
]
, assuming no external body force is
exerted:

(2.1)

\displaystyle\text{momentum conservation}:

\displaystyle\rho\frac{dv}{dt}

\displaystyle=\nabla\cdot\sigma

\displaystyle\text{mass conservation}:

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{\rho}\frac{d\rho}{dt}

\displaystyle=-\nabla\cdot v

\displaystyle\text{energy conservation}:

\displaystyle\rho\frac{de}{dt}

\displaystyle=\sigma:\nabla v

\displaystyle\text{equation of motion}:

\displaystyle\frac{dx}{dt}

\displaystyle=v.

Here,
\frac{d}{dt}=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}+v\cdot\nabla
is the material derivative,

\rho
denotes the density of the fluid,
x
and

v
denote the position and the velocity of the particles in a
deformable medium
\Omega(t)
in the Eulerian coordinates,

\sigma
denotes the deformation stress tensor, and
e

denotes the internal energy per unit mass. These physical quantities can be
treated as functions of the time
t
and the particle

\widetilde{x}\in\widetilde{\Omega}=\Omega(0)
. In gas dynamics,
the stress tensor is isotropic, and we write
\sigma=-pI+\sigma_{a}
, where
p
denotes the
thermodynamic pressure, and
\sigma_{a}
denotes the artificial
viscosity stress. The thermodynamic pressure is described by the equation of
state, and can be expressed as a function of the density and the internal
energy. In our work, we focus on the case of polytropic ideal gas with an
adiabatic index
\gamma>1
, which yields the equation of state

(2.2)

p=(\gamma-1)\rho e.

The system is prescribed with an initial condition and a boundary condition

v\cdot n=g
, where
n
is
the outward normal unit vector on the domain boundary. Moreover, a set of
problem parameters
\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\mathsf{D}
determines certain physical data in
the system of Euler equations (
2.1
), and therefore the physical
quantities are parametrized with the data
\boldsymbol{\mu}
.

2.1
Spatial discretization

Following
[
71
]
, we adopt a spatial discretization for
(
2.1
) using a kinematic space
\mathcal{V}\subset[H^{1}(\widetilde{\Omega})]^{d}
for approximating the position and the
velocity, and a thermodynamic space
\mathcal{E}\subset L_{2}(\widetilde{\Omega})
for approximating the energy. The density can be
eliminated, and the equation of mass conservation can be decoupled from
(
2.1
). We assume high-order finite element (FEM) discretization in
space, so that the finite dimensions
N_{\mathcal{V}}
and

N_{\mathcal{E}}
are the global numbers of FEM degrees of freedom in the
corresponding discete FEM spaces. For more details, see
[
71
]
.
The FEM coefficient vector functions for velocity and position are denoted as

\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{x}:[0,t_{f}]\times\mathsf{D}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathcal{V}}}
,
and the coefficient vector function for energy is denoted as

\boldsymbol{e}:[0,t_{f}]\times\mathsf{D}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathcal{E}}}
.
The semidiscrete Lagrangian conservation laws can be expressed as a nonlinear
system of differential equations in the coefficients with respect to the bases
for the kinematic and thermodynamic spaces:

(2.3)

\displaystyle\text{momentum conservation}:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{M}_{\mathcal{V}}\frac{d\boldsymbol{v}}{dt}

\displaystyle=-\boldsymbol{F}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{e},\boldsymbol{x};\boldsymbol{\mu})\cdot\boldsymbol{1}

\displaystyle\text{energy conservation}:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{M}_{\mathcal{E}}\frac{d\boldsymbol{e}}{dt}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{F}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{e},\boldsymbol{x};\boldsymbol{\mu})^{T}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}

\displaystyle\text{equation of motion}:

\displaystyle\frac{d\boldsymbol{x}}{dt}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{v}.

Let
\boldsymbol{w}\equiv(\boldsymbol{v};\boldsymbol{e};\boldsymbol{x})^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
,

N=2N_{\mathcal{V}}+N_{\mathcal{E}}
, be the hydrodynamic
state vector. Then the semidiscrete conservation equation of (
2.3
) can
be written in a compact form as

(2.4)

\frac{d\boldsymbol{w}}{dt}=\mathsf{F}(\boldsymbol{w},t;\boldsymbol{\mu}),

where the nonlinear force term,
\mathsf{F}:\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathsf{D}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{N}
, is defined as

(2.5)

\displaystyle\mathsf{F}(\boldsymbol{w};\boldsymbol{\mu})

\displaystyle\equiv\begin{pmatrix}\mathsf{F}_{v}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{e},\boldsymbol{x})\\
\mathsf{F}_{e}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{e},\boldsymbol{x})\\
\mathsf{F}_{x}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{e},\boldsymbol{x})\end{pmatrix}\equiv\begin{pmatrix}-\boldsymbol{M}_{\mathcal{V}}^{-1}\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}\\
\boldsymbol{M}_{\mathcal{E}}^{-1}\mathsf{F}^{tv}\\
\boldsymbol{v}\end{pmatrix},

where
\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}:\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathsf{D}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathcal{V}}}
and
\mathsf{F}^{tv}:\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathsf{D}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathcal{E}}}
are
nonlinear vector functions that are defined respectively as

(2.6)

\displaystyle\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}

\displaystyle\equiv\boldsymbol{F}\cdot\boldsymbol{1},

\displaystyle\mathsf{F}^{tv}

\displaystyle\equiv\boldsymbol{F}^{T}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}.

2.2
Time integrators

In order to obtain a fully discretized system of equations, one needs to apply a
time integrator. We consider two different explicit Runge-Kutta schemes: the
RK2-average and RK4 schemes. The temporal domain is discretized as
\{t_{n}\}_{n=0}^{N_{t}}
, where
t_{n}
denotes
a discrete moment in time with
t_{0}=0
,
t_{N_{t}}=t_{f}
, and
t_{n-1}<t_{n}
for

n\in{\rm Nature}{N_{t}}
, where
{\rm Nature}{N}\equiv\{1,\ldots,N\}
. The
computational domain at time
t_{n}
is denoted as

\Omega^{n}\equiv\Omega(t_{n})
. We denote the
quantities of interest defined on
\Omega^{n}
with a subscript

n
.

2.2.1
The RK2-average scheme

The midpoint Runge–Kutta second-order scheme is written as

(2.7)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{w}_{n+\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{w}_{n}+\frac{\Delta t_{n}}{2}\mathsf{F}(\boldsymbol{w}_{n}),

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{w}_{n+1}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{w}_{n}+\Delta t_{n}\mathsf{F}(\boldsymbol{w}_{n+\frac{1}{2}}),

where
\Delta t_{n}\equiv t_{n+1}-t_{n}
.
In practice, the midpoint RK2 scheme can be unstable even for simple test
problems. Therefore, the following RK2-average scheme is used:

(2.8)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{v}_{n+\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{v}_{n}-(\Delta t_{n}/2)\boldsymbol{M}_{\mathcal{V}}^{-1}\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}_{n},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{v}_{n+1}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{v}_{n}-\Delta t_{n}\boldsymbol{M}_{\mathcal{V}}^{-1}\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}_{n+\frac{1}{2}},

(2.9)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{e}_{n+\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{e}_{n}+(\Delta t_{n}/2)\boldsymbol{M}_{\mathcal{E}}^{-1}\mathsf{F}^{tv}_{n},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{e}_{n+1}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{e}_{n}+\Delta t_{n}\boldsymbol{M}_{\mathcal{E}}^{-1}\bar{\mathsf{F}}^{tv}_{n+\frac{1}{2}},

(2.10)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}_{n+\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{x}_{n}+(\Delta t_{n}/2)\boldsymbol{v}_{n+\frac{1}{2}},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}_{n+1}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{x}_{n}+\Delta t_{n}\bar{\boldsymbol{v}}_{n+\frac{1}{2}},

where the state
\boldsymbol{w}_{n}=(\boldsymbol{v}_{n};\boldsymbol{e}_{n};\boldsymbol{x}_{n})^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}

is used to compute the updates

(2.11)

\displaystyle\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}_{n}

\displaystyle=\left(\boldsymbol{F}(\boldsymbol{w}_{n})\right)\cdot\boldsymbol{1},

\displaystyle\mathsf{F}^{tv}_{n}

\displaystyle=\left(\boldsymbol{F}(\boldsymbol{w}_{n})\right)^{T}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}_{n+\frac{1}{2}},

in the first stage. Similarly,
\boldsymbol{w}_{n+\frac{1}{2}}=(\boldsymbol{v}_{n+\frac{1}{2}};\boldsymbol{e}_{n+\frac{1}{2}};\boldsymbol{x}_{n+\frac{1}{2}})^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}

is used to compute the updates

(2.12)

\displaystyle\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}_{n+\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\left(\boldsymbol{F}(\boldsymbol{w}_{n+\frac{1}{2}})\right)\cdot\boldsymbol{1},

\displaystyle\bar{\mathsf{F}}^{tv}_{n+\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\left(\boldsymbol{F}(\boldsymbol{w}_{n+\frac{1}{2}})\right)^{T}\cdot\bar{\boldsymbol{v}}_{n+\frac{1}{2}},

with
\bar{\boldsymbol{v}}_{n+\frac{1}{2}}=(\boldsymbol{v}_{n}+\boldsymbol{v}_{n+1})/2
in the second stage. Note that the RK2-average
scheme is different from the midpoint RK2 scheme in the updates for energy and
position. The RK2-average scheme uses the midpoint velocity

\boldsymbol{v}_{n+\frac{1}{2}}
and the average velocity

\bar{\boldsymbol{v}}_{n+\frac{1}{2}}
to update energy and position in the
first stage and the second stage respectively, while the midpoint RK2 uses the
initial velocity
\boldsymbol{v}_{n}
and the midpoint velocity

\boldsymbol{v}_{n+\frac{1}{2}}
respectively. The RK2-average scheme is
proved to conserve the discrete total energy exactly (see Proposition 7.1 of

[
71
]
).

2.2.2
The RK4 scheme

The traditional RK4 scheme can be applied to the Lagrangian hydrodynamics in
(
2.4
) as

(2.13)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{w}_{n+1}=\boldsymbol{w}_{n}+\frac{1}{6}\Delta t_{n}(\mathsf{Y}_{1}+2\mathsf{Y}_{2}+2\mathsf{Y}_{3}+\mathsf{Y}_{4}),

where

(2.14)

\displaystyle\mathsf{Y}_{1}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{F}(\boldsymbol{w}_{n}),

\displaystyle\mathsf{Y}_{2}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{F}(\boldsymbol{w}_{n}+\frac{\Delta t_{n}}{2}\mathsf{Y}_{1}),

\displaystyle\mathsf{Y}_{3}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{F}(\boldsymbol{w}_{n}+\frac{\Delta t_{n}}{2}\mathsf{Y}_{2}),

\displaystyle\mathsf{Y}_{4}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{F}(\boldsymbol{w}_{n}+\Delta t_{n}\mathsf{Y}_{3}).

2.2.3
Adaptive time stepping

Since explicit Runge-Kutta methods are used, we need to control the time step
size in order to maintain the stability of the fully discrete schemes. We
follow the automatic time step control algorithm described in Section 7.3 of

[
71
]
, which we briefly describe here. At the time step

n
, the algorithm starts with a time step estimate

\tau_{n}
defined as

(2.15)

\tau_{n}=\min_{x}\alpha\left(\dfrac{c_{s}(x)}{h_{\text{min}}(x)}+\alpha_{\mu}\dfrac{\mu_{s}(x)}{\rho(x)h^{2}_{\text{min}}(x)}\right)^{-1},

where the minimum is taken over all quadrature points used in the evaluation of
the local force matrices and over all Runge-Kutta stages. Here,
\alpha
and

\alpha_{\mu}
are certain Courant–Friedrichs–Lewy (CFL) constants. The
default values we use are
\alpha=0.5
and
\alpha_{\mu}=2.5
.
Moreover,
c_{s}
is the speed of sound,
\mu_{s}
is the viscosity coefficient, and

h_{\text{min}}
is the minimal singular value of the zone Jacobian. These
quantities are used for the evaluation of the artificial stress tensor for
modeling shock wave propagation. The discussion of artificial viscosity is beyond
the scope of this paper, and the reader is referred to Section 6 of

[
71
]
for more details. With the estimate

\tau_{n}
, we use the following algorithm to control the
time-step:

Step 1:

Given a time-step
\Delta t
and state
\boldsymbol{w}_{n}
, evaluate
the state
\boldsymbol{w}_{n+1}
and the corresponding time step estimate

\tau_{n}
.

Step 2:

If
\Delta t\geq\tau_{n}
, set
\Delta t\leftarrow\beta_{1}\Delta t
and go to Step 1.

Step 3:

If
\Delta t\leq\gamma\tau_{n}
, set

\Delta t\leftarrow\beta_{2}\Delta t
.

Step 4:

Set
\Delta t_{n}=\Delta t
,

n\leftarrow n+1
and continue with the next time step.

Here,
\beta_{1}
,
\beta_{2}
, and
\gamma
denote given constants. The default
values we use are
\beta_{1}=0.85
,
\beta_{2}=1.02
, and
\gamma=0.8
. We
remark that, due to the automatic time step control, the temporal discretization
is not determined a-priori and depends on various model inputs, such as the
underlying state space, the CFL constant and the time integrator.

2.3
). We
start with the usage of the reduced order model, which we refer to as the online phase.
We sequentially discuss the solution representation by a subspace in
Section
3.1
, Galerkin projection in Section
3.2
and
hyper-reduction in Section
3.3
, resulting in a continuous-in-time reduced order
model. Using a time integrator in Section
3.4
,
we obtain a fully discrete reduced order model.
Then we move on to discuss the construction of the reduced order model,

3.1
Solution representation

We restrict our solution space to a subspace spanned by a reduced basis for each
field. That is, the subspace for velocity, energy, and position fields are
defined as

(3.1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{v}

\displaystyle\equiv\text{Span}\{\mathbf{\phi}_{v}^{i}\}_{i=1}^{n_{v}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathcal{V}}},

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{e}

\displaystyle\equiv\text{Span}\{\mathbf{\phi}_{e}^{i}\}_{i=1}^{n_{e}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathcal{E}}},

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{x}

\displaystyle\equiv\text{Span}\{\mathbf{\phi}_{x}^{i}\}_{i=1}^{n_{x}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathcal{V}}},

with
\dim(\mathcal{S}_{v})=n_{v}\ll N_{\mathcal{V}}
,

\dim(\mathcal{S}_{e})=n_{e}\ll N_{\mathcal{E}}
, and

\dim(\mathcal{S}_{x})=n_{x}\ll N_{\mathcal{V}}
. Using these
subspaces, each discrete field is approximated in trial subspaces,
\boldsymbol{v}\approx\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}}\in\boldsymbol{v}_{\text{os}}+\mathcal{S}_{v}
,
\boldsymbol{e}\approx\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}}\in\boldsymbol{e}_{\text{os}}+\mathcal{S}_{e}
, and
\boldsymbol{x}\approx\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\boldsymbol{x}_{\text{os}}+\mathcal{S}_{x}
, or equivalently

(3.2)

\displaystyle\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}}(t;\boldsymbol{\mu})

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{v}_{\text{os}}(\boldsymbol{\mu})+\mathbf{\Phi}_{v}\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}}(t;\boldsymbol{\mu}),

(3.3)

\displaystyle\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}}(t;\boldsymbol{\mu})

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{e}_{\text{os}}(\boldsymbol{\mu})+\mathbf{\Phi}_{e}\widehat{\boldsymbol{e}}(t;\boldsymbol{\mu}),

(3.4)

\displaystyle\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}(t;\boldsymbol{\mu})

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{x}_{\text{os}}(\boldsymbol{\mu})+\mathbf{\Phi}_{x}\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}(t;\boldsymbol{\mu}),

where

\boldsymbol{v}_{\text{os}}(\boldsymbol{\mu})\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathcal{V}}}
,

\boldsymbol{e}_{\text{os}}(\boldsymbol{\mu})\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathcal{E}}}
, and

\boldsymbol{x}_{\text{os}}(\boldsymbol{\mu})\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathcal{V}}}
denote the prescribed offset
vectors for velocity, energy, and position fields respectively; the orthonormal basis
matrices

\mathbf{\Phi}_{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathcal{V}}\times n_{v}}
,

\mathbf{\Phi}_{e}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathcal{E}}\times n_{e}}
, and

\mathbf{\Phi}_{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathcal{V}}\times n_{x}}

are defined as

(3.5)

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Phi}_{v}

\displaystyle\equiv\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{\phi}_{v}^{1}&\cdots&\mathbf{\phi}_{v}^{n_{v}}\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Phi}_{e}

\displaystyle\equiv\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{\phi}_{e}^{1}&\cdots&\mathbf{\phi}_{e}^{n_{e}}\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Phi}_{x}

\displaystyle\equiv\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{\phi}_{x}^{1}&\cdots&\mathbf{\phi}_{x}^{n_{x}}\end{bmatrix};

and
\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}}:[0,t_{f}]\times\mathsf{D}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n_{v}}
,
\widehat{\boldsymbol{e}}:[0,t_{f}]\times\mathsf{D}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n_{e}}
, and
\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}:[0,t_{f}]\times\mathsf{D}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}}
denote the time-dependent generalized coordinates for
velocity, energy, and position fields, respectively. One natural choice of the
offset vectors is to use the initial values, i.e.
\boldsymbol{v}_{\text{os}}(\boldsymbol{\mu})=\boldsymbol{v}(0;\boldsymbol{\mu})
,
\boldsymbol{e}_{\text{os}}(\boldsymbol{\mu})=\boldsymbol{e}(0;\boldsymbol{\mu})
, and
\boldsymbol{x}_{\text{os}}(\boldsymbol{\mu})=\boldsymbol{x}(0;\boldsymbol{\mu})
. Replacing
\boldsymbol{v}
,
\boldsymbol{e}
, and
\boldsymbol{x}
with

\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}}
,
\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}}
, and
\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}
in
Eqs. (
2.3
), the semi-discretized Lagrangian hydrodynamics system
becomes the following over-determined semi-discrete system:

(3.6)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{M}_{\mathcal{V}}\mathbf{\Phi}_{v}\frac{d\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}}}{dt}

\displaystyle=-\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}(\boldsymbol{v}_{\text{os}}+\mathbf{\Phi}_{v}\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}},\boldsymbol{e}_{\text{os}}+\mathbf{\Phi}_{e}\widehat{\boldsymbol{e}},\boldsymbol{x}_{\text{os}}+\mathbf{\Phi}_{x}\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}},t;\boldsymbol{\mu})

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{M}_{\mathcal{E}}\mathbf{\Phi}_{e}\frac{d\widehat{\boldsymbol{e}}}{dt}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{F}^{tv}(\boldsymbol{v}_{\text{os}}+\mathbf{\Phi}_{v}\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}},\boldsymbol{e}_{\text{os}}+\mathbf{\Phi}_{e}\widehat{\boldsymbol{e}},\boldsymbol{x}_{\text{os}}+\mathbf{\Phi}_{x}\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}},t;\boldsymbol{\mu})

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Phi}_{x}\frac{d\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}}{dt}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{v}_{\text{os}}+\mathbf{\Phi}_{v}\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}}.

The system of ordinary differential equations is closed by defining the initial
condition at
t=0
. With the initial values as the offset vectors,
using the solution representation (
3.2
), one can derive
the initial values of the ROM coefficient vectors given by zero vectors in the
corresponding ROM spaces, i.e.
\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}}(0;\boldsymbol{\mu})=\boldsymbol{0}_{n_{v}}
,
\widehat{\boldsymbol{e}}(0;\boldsymbol{\mu})=\boldsymbol{0}_{n_{e}}
,
and
\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}(0;\boldsymbol{\mu})=\boldsymbol{0}_{n_{x}}
, where
\boldsymbol{0}_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
is the zero vector in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
.

3.2
Galerkin projection

Note that Eqs. (
3.6
) has more equations than unknowns (i.e., an
over-determined system). It is likely that there is no solution satisfying
Eq. (
3.6
). The system must be closed in order to obtain a solution.
One can invert the mass matrices in the momentum conservation and energy
conservation of Eqs. (
3.6
) and apply Galerkin projection to obtain
the following reduced system of semi-discrete Lagrangian hydrodynamics
equations:

(3.7)

\displaystyle\frac{d\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}}}{dt}

\displaystyle=-\mathbf{\Phi}_{v}^{T}\boldsymbol{M}_{\mathcal{V}}^{-1}\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}(\boldsymbol{v}_{\text{os}}+\mathbf{\Phi}_{v}\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}},\boldsymbol{e}_{\text{os}}+\mathbf{\Phi}_{e}\widehat{\boldsymbol{e}},\boldsymbol{x}_{\text{os}}+\mathbf{\Phi}_{x}\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}},t;\boldsymbol{\mu})

\displaystyle\frac{d\widehat{\boldsymbol{e}}}{dt}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{\Phi}_{e}^{T}\boldsymbol{M}_{\mathcal{E}}^{-1}\mathsf{F}^{tv}(\boldsymbol{v}_{\text{os}}+\mathbf{\Phi}_{v}\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}},\boldsymbol{e}_{\text{os}}+\mathbf{\Phi}_{e}\widehat{\boldsymbol{e}},\boldsymbol{x}_{\text{os}}+\mathbf{\Phi}_{x}\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}},t;\boldsymbol{\mu})

\displaystyle\frac{d\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}}{dt}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{\Phi}_{x}^{T}\boldsymbol{v}_{\text{os}}+\mathbf{\Phi}_{x}^{T}\mathbf{\Phi}_{v}\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}}.

Here, we use the assumption of basis orthonormality.
There is one major issue with this Galerkin formulation. The nonlinear term
involves the inverse of the mass matrices, which are dense matrices. This makes
each component of the nonlinear term vector dependent on potentially every state
variable component. Even though a gappy POD is used with a small number of sample points,
the evaluation of each point requires possibly all the state variables,
resulting in inefficient computational complexity. Therefore, we choose an
alternative Galerkin projection, in which we do not invert mass matrices.
Instead, we form reduced mass matrices by applying Galerkin projection directly
to Eqs. (
3.6
), resulting in

(3.8)

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{M}}_{\mathcal{V}}\frac{d\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}}}{dt}

\displaystyle=-\mathbf{\Phi}_{v}^{T}\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}(\boldsymbol{v}_{\text{os}}+\mathbf{\Phi}_{v}\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}},\boldsymbol{e}_{\text{os}}+\mathbf{\Phi}_{e}\widehat{\boldsymbol{e}},\boldsymbol{x}_{\text{os}}+\mathbf{\Phi}_{x}\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}},t;\boldsymbol{\mu})

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{M}}_{\mathcal{E}}\frac{d\widehat{\boldsymbol{e}}}{dt}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{\Phi}_{e}^{T}\mathsf{F}^{tv}(\boldsymbol{v}_{\text{os}}+\mathbf{\Phi}_{v}\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}},\boldsymbol{e}_{\text{os}}+\mathbf{\Phi}_{e}\widehat{\boldsymbol{e}},\boldsymbol{x}_{\text{os}}+\mathbf{\Phi}_{x}\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}},t;\boldsymbol{\mu})

\displaystyle\frac{d\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}}{dt}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{\Phi}_{x}^{T}\boldsymbol{v}_{\text{os}}+\mathbf{\Phi}_{x}^{T}\mathbf{\Phi}_{v}\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}},

where the reduced kinematic matrix,
\widehat{\boldsymbol{M}}_{\mathcal{V}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{v}\times n_{v}}
, and thermodynamic mass matrix,

\widehat{\boldsymbol{M}}_{\mathcal{E}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{e}\times n_{e}}
, are
defined as

(3.9)

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{M}}_{\mathcal{V}}

\displaystyle\equiv\mathbf{\Phi}_{v}^{T}\boldsymbol{M}_{\mathcal{V}}\mathbf{\Phi}_{v},

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{M}}_{\mathcal{E}}

\displaystyle\equiv\mathbf{\Phi}_{e}^{T}\boldsymbol{M}_{\mathcal{E}}\mathbf{\Phi}_{e}.

Note that the reduced mass matrices can be precomputed once, since the mass
matrices are constant in time (cf.
[
71
]
). Furthermore, the
dimensions of the mass matrices are small, so computing their factorizations,
e.g., LU or Cholesky factorizations, can be done efficiently and once for all
time. Alternatively, one can orthogonalize
\mathbf{\Phi}_{v}
and
\mathbf{\Phi}_{e}

with respect to
\boldsymbol{M}_{\mathcal{V}}
and
\boldsymbol{M}_{\mathcal{E}}
, respectively,
i.e.,
\widehat{\boldsymbol{M}}_{\mathcal{V}}=\boldsymbol{I}_{n_{v}}
and

\widehat{\boldsymbol{M}}_{\mathcal{E}}=\boldsymbol{I}_{n_{e}}
, where

\boldsymbol{I}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is an
identity matrix. In conclusion, the handling of the reduced mass matrices is
straightforward and efficient.

3.3
Hyper-reduction

The nonlinear matrix function,
\boldsymbol{F}
, changes every time the state
variables evolve. Additionally, it needs to be multiplied by the
basis matrices whenever the update in the nonlinear term occurs, which scales
with the full-order model (FOM) size. Therefore, we cannot expect any speed-up
without special treatment of the nonlinear terms. To overcome this issue,
a hyper-reduction technique needs to be applied, where
\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}
and

\mathsf{F}^{tv}
are approximated as

(3.10)

\displaystyle\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}

\displaystyle\approx\mathbf{\Phi}_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}\widehat{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}},

\displaystyle\mathsf{F}^{tv}

\displaystyle\approx\mathbf{\Phi}_{\mathsf{F}^{tv}}\widehat{\mathsf{F}^{tv}}.

That is,
\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}
and
\mathsf{F}^{tv}
are projected onto subspaces

\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}\equiv\text{Span}\{\mathbf{\phi}_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}^{i}\}_{i=1}^{n_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}}
and

\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{F}^{tv}}\equiv\text{Span}\{\mathbf{\phi}_{\mathsf{F}^{tv}}^{i}\}_{i=1}^{n_{\mathsf{F}^{tv}}}
, where

\mathbf{\Phi}_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}\equiv\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{\phi}_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}^{1}&\ldots&\mathbf{\phi}_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}^{n_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathcal{V}}\times n_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}}
,
n_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}\ll N_{\mathcal{V}}
and
\mathbf{\Phi}_{\mathsf{F}^{tv}}\equiv\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{\phi}_{\mathsf{F}^{tv}}^{1}&\ldots&\mathbf{\phi}_{\mathsf{F}^{tv}}^{n_{\mathsf{F}^{tv}}}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathcal{E}}\times n_{\mathsf{F}^{tv}}}
,
n_{\mathsf{F}^{tv}}\ll N_{\mathcal{E}}
, denote the nonlinear term basis matrices, and
\widehat{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}}
and
\widehat{\mathsf{F}^{tv}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathsf{F}^{tv}}}
denote the
generalized coordinates of the nonlinear terms. Following

[
73
]
, the nonlinear term bases
\mathbf{\Phi}_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}
and

\mathbf{\Phi}_{\mathsf{F}^{tv}}
can be constructed by applying another POD on the nonlinear term
snapshots obtained from the FOM simulation at every time step. This implies
that additional snapshot storage and basis construction are required for the
nonlinear terms. An alternative approach of avoiding the extra cost without
losing the quality of the hyper-reduction is discussed in
[
13
]
,
which will be discussed in more detail in Section
3.5
and Section
3.6
.

Now we will show how the generalized coordinates,
\widehat{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}
, can be
determined by the following interpolation:

(3.11)

\displaystyle\mathbf{Z}_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}^{T}\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}=\mathbf{Z}_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}^{T}\mathbf{\Phi}_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}\widehat{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}},

where
\mathbf{Z}_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}\equiv\begin{bmatrix}\mathfrak{e}_{p_{1}},\ldots,\mathfrak{e}_{p_{n_{\breve{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}}}}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathcal{V}}\times n_{\breve{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}}}
,
n_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}\leq n_{\breve{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}}\ll N_{\mathcal{V}}
, is the sampling matrix and
\mathfrak{e}_{p_{i}}
is the
p_{i}
-th
column vector of the identity matrix

\boldsymbol{I}_{N_{\mathcal{V}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathcal{V}}\times N_{\mathcal{V}}}
.
Note that Eq. (
3.11
) is an over-determined system. Thus,
we solve the least-squares problem, i.e.,

(3.12)

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}

\displaystyle=\argmin_{\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}}}\|\mathbf{Z}_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}^{T}(\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}-\mathbf{\Phi}_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}\boldsymbol{a})\|_{2}^{2}.

The solution to the least-squares problem (
3.12
) is

(3.13)

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}=(\mathbf{Z}_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}^{T}\mathbf{\Phi}_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}})^{\dagger}\mathbf{Z}_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}^{T}\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}},

where the Moore–Penrose inverse of a matrix
\boldsymbol{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{I\times J}
,

I\geq J
, with full column rank is defined as
\boldsymbol{A}^{\dagger}:=(\boldsymbol{A}^{T}\boldsymbol{A})^{-1}\boldsymbol{A}^{T}
. Therefore, Eq. (
3.10
)
becomes
\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}\approx\mathcal{P}_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}
, where

(3.14)

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}=\mathbf{\Phi}_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}\left(\mathbf{Z}_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}^{T}\mathbf{\Phi}_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}\right)^{\dagger}\mathbf{Z}_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathcal{V}}\times N_{\mathcal{V}}}

is the oblique projection matrix. Instead of constructing the sampling matrix

\mathbf{Z}_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}
, for efficiency we simply store the sampling indices

\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{n_{\breve{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}}}\}\subset{\rm Nature}{N_{\mathcal{V}}}
.
More precisely, the reduced matrix
\left(\mathbf{Z}_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}^{T}\mathbf{\Phi}_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}\right)^{\dagger}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}\times n_{\breve{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}}}

can be precomputed and stored in the offline phase,
and is multiplied to the sampled entries

\mathbf{Z}_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}^{T}\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\breve{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}}}

to obtain
\widehat{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}
by (
3.13
) in the online phase.
The sampling indices are generated by the discrete
empirical interpolation method (DEIM), which is explained in
Section
3.7
. Similarly, for the nonlinear term of the energy
conservation equation, we have

(3.15)

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathsf{F}^{tv}}=(\mathbf{Z}_{\mathsf{F}^{tv}}^{T}\mathbf{\Phi}_{\mathsf{F}^{tv}})^{\dagger}\mathbf{Z}_{\mathsf{F}^{tv}}^{T}\mathsf{F}^{tv}.

We denote the sampling matrix by
\mathbf{Z}_{\mathsf{F}^{tv}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathcal{E}}\times n_{\breve{\mathsf{F}^{tv}}}}
and the oblique
projection matrix by

(3.16)

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\mathsf{F}^{tv}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{\Phi}_{\mathsf{F}^{tv}}\left(\mathbf{Z}_{\mathsf{F}^{tv}}^{T}\mathbf{\Phi}_{\mathsf{F}^{tv}}\right)^{\dagger}\mathbf{Z}_{\mathsf{F}^{tv}}^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathcal{E}}\times N_{\mathcal{E}}}.

Then, the hyper-reduced system is obtained by replacing
\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}
and

\mathsf{F}^{tv}
in Eq. (
3.8
) with
\mathcal{P}_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}
and

\mathcal{P}_{\mathsf{F}^{tv}}\mathsf{F}^{tv}
, respectively:

(3.17)

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{M}}_{\mathcal{V}}\frac{d\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}}}{dt}

\displaystyle=-\mathbf{\Phi}_{v}^{T}\mathcal{P}_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}(\boldsymbol{v}_{\text{os}}+\mathbf{\Phi}_{v}\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}},\boldsymbol{e}_{\text{os}}+\mathbf{\Phi}_{e}\widehat{\boldsymbol{e}},\boldsymbol{x}_{\text{os}}+\mathbf{\Phi}_{x}\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}},t;\boldsymbol{\mu})

(3.18)

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{M}}_{\mathcal{E}}\frac{d\widehat{\boldsymbol{e}}}{dt}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{\Phi}_{e}^{T}\mathcal{P}_{\mathsf{F}^{tv}}\mathsf{F}^{tv}(\boldsymbol{v}_{\text{os}}+\mathbf{\Phi}_{v}\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}},\boldsymbol{e}_{\text{os}}+\mathbf{\Phi}_{e}\widehat{\boldsymbol{e}},\boldsymbol{x}_{\text{os}}+\mathbf{\Phi}_{x}\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}},t;\boldsymbol{\mu})

(3.19)

\displaystyle\frac{d\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}}{dt}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{\Phi}_{x}^{T}\boldsymbol{v}_{\text{os}}+\mathbf{\Phi}_{x}^{T}\mathbf{\Phi}_{v}\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}},

Let
\widehat{\boldsymbol{w}}\equiv(\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}};\widehat{\boldsymbol{e}};\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}})^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
n=n_{v}+n_{e}+n_{x}
, be the reduced order hydrodynamic state
vector. Then the semidiscrete hyper-reduced system (
3.17
) can
be written in a compact form as

(3.20)

\frac{d\widehat{\boldsymbol{w}}}{dt}=\widehat{\mathsf{F}}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{w}},t;\boldsymbol{\mu}),

where the nonlinear force term,
\mathsf{F}:\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathsf{D}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{N}
, is defined as

(3.21)

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathsf{F}}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{w}};\boldsymbol{\mu})

\displaystyle\equiv\begin{pmatrix}\widehat{\mathsf{F}}_{v}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}},\widehat{\boldsymbol{e}},\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}})\\
\widehat{\mathsf{F}}_{e}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}},\widehat{\boldsymbol{e}},\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}})\\
\widehat{\mathsf{F}}_{x}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}},\widehat{\boldsymbol{e}},\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}})\end{pmatrix}\equiv\begin{pmatrix}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{M}}_{\mathcal{V}}^{-1}\mathbf{\Phi}_{v}^{T}\mathcal{P}_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}\\
\widehat{\boldsymbol{M}}_{\mathcal{E}}^{-1}\mathbf{\Phi}_{e}^{T}\mathcal{P}_{\mathsf{F}^{tv}}\mathsf{F}^{tv}\\
\mathbf{\Phi}_{x}^{T}\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}}\end{pmatrix}.

3.4
Time integrators

Applying the RK2-average scheme in Section
2.2.1
to the
hyper-reduced system (
3.17
), the RK2-average fully discrete
hyper-reduced system reads:

(3.22)

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}}_{n+\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}}_{n}-(\Delta t_{n}/2)\widehat{\boldsymbol{M}}_{\mathcal{V}}^{-1}\mathbf{\Phi}_{v}^{T}\mathcal{P}_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}\tilde{\mathsf{F}}^{\mathfrak{1}}_{n},

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}}_{n+1}

\displaystyle=\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}}_{n}-\Delta t_{n}\widehat{\boldsymbol{M}}_{\mathcal{V}}^{-1}\mathbf{\Phi}_{v}^{T}\mathcal{P}_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}\tilde{\mathsf{F}}^{\mathfrak{1}}_{n+\frac{1}{2}},

(3.23)

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{e}}_{n+\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\widehat{\boldsymbol{e}}_{n}+(\Delta t_{n}/2)\widehat{\boldsymbol{M}}_{\mathcal{E}}^{-1}\mathbf{\Phi}_{e}^{T}\mathcal{P}_{\mathsf{F}^{tv}}\tilde{\mathsf{F}}^{tv}_{n},

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{e}}_{n+1}

\displaystyle=\widehat{\boldsymbol{e}}_{n}+\Delta t_{n}\widehat{\boldsymbol{M}}_{\mathcal{E}}^{-1}\mathbf{\Phi}_{e}^{T}\mathcal{P}_{\mathsf{F}^{tv}}\bar{\tilde{\mathsf{F}}}^{tv}_{n+\frac{1}{2}},

(3.24)

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n+\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n}+(\Delta t_{n}/2)\mathbf{\Phi}_{x}^{T}\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}}_{n+\frac{1}{2}},

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n+1}

\displaystyle=\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n}+\Delta t_{n}\mathbf{\Phi}_{x}^{T}\bar{\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}}}_{n+\frac{1}{2}},

where the lifted ROM approximation
\tilde{\boldsymbol{w}}_{n}=(\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}}_{n};\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}}_{n};\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n})^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
given by

(3.25)

\displaystyle\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}}_{n}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{v}_{\text{os}}+\mathbf{\Phi}_{v}\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}}_{n},

\displaystyle\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}}_{n}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{e}_{\text{os}}+\mathbf{\Phi}_{e}\widehat{\boldsymbol{e}}_{n},

\displaystyle\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{x}_{\text{os}}+\mathbf{\Phi}_{x}\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n},

is used to compute the updates

(3.26)

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathsf{F}}^{\mathfrak{1}}_{n}

\displaystyle=\left(\boldsymbol{F}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{w}}_{n})\right)\cdot\boldsymbol{1},

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathsf{F}}^{tv}_{n}

\displaystyle=\left(\boldsymbol{F}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{w}}_{n})\right)^{T}\cdot\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}}_{n+\frac{1}{2}},

in the first stage. Similarly,
\tilde{\boldsymbol{w}}_{n+\frac{1}{2}}=(\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}}_{n+\frac{1}{2}};\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}}_{n+\frac{1}{2}};\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n+\frac{1}{2}})^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}

is used to computed the updates

(3.27)

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathsf{F}}^{\mathfrak{1}}_{n+\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\left(\boldsymbol{F}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{w}}_{n+\frac{1}{2}})\right)\cdot\boldsymbol{1},

\displaystyle\bar{\tilde{\mathsf{F}}}^{tv}_{n+\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\left(\boldsymbol{F}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{w}}_{n+\frac{1}{2}})\right)^{T}\cdot\bar{\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}}}_{n+\frac{1}{2}},

with
\bar{\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}}}_{n+\frac{1}{2}}=(\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}}_{n}+\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}}_{n+1})/2
in the second
stage. The lifting is computed only for the sampled degrees of freedom, avoiding
full order computation. Here, the time step size
\Delta t_{n}
is determined
adaptively using the automatic time step control algorithm described in
Section
2.2.3
, with the state
\boldsymbol{w}_{n}
replaced
by the lifted ROM approximation
\tilde{\boldsymbol{w}}_{n}
. We remark that the
RK4 scheme in Section
2.2.2
can also be applied in a similar manner to
derive the RK4 fully discrete hyper-reduced system. As noted in
Section
2.2.3
, the temporal discretization depends on the
state space, which implies that it is very likely that the temporal
discretization used in the hyper-reduced system is different from the full
order model even with the same problem setting. To this end, we denote by

\widetilde{N}_{t}
the number of time steps in the fully discrete hyper-reduced
system, to differentiate it from the notation
N_{t}
for the full order
model.

3.5
Proper orthogonal decomposition

This section describes how we obtain the reduced basis matrices, i.e.,

\mathbf{\Phi}_{v}
,
\mathbf{\Phi}_{e}
,
\mathbf{\Phi}_{x}
,
\mathbf{\Phi}_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}
, and

\mathbf{\Phi}_{\mathsf{F}^{tv}}
. Proper orthogonal decomposition (POD) is commonly used to
construct a reduced basis. It suffices to describe how to construct
the reduced basis for the energy field only, i.e.,
\mathbf{\Phi}_{e}
, because other
bases will be constructed in the same way using POD. POD

[
74
]
obtains
\mathbf{\Phi}_{e}
from a truncated singular
value decomposition (SVD) approximation to a FOM solution snapshot matrix. It is
related to principal component analysis in statistical analysis

[
75
]
and Karhunen–Loève expansion
[
76
]

in stochastic analysis. In order to collect solution data for performing POD,
we run FOM simulations on a set of problem parameters, namely

\{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{n_{\mu}}
. For

k\in{\rm Nature}{n_{\mu}}
, let
N_{t}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k})
be the
number of time steps in the FOM simulation with the problem parameter

\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k}
. By choosing
\boldsymbol{e}_{\text{os}}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k})=\boldsymbol{e}(0;\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k})
, a solution snapshot matrix is formed by
assembling all the FOM solution data including the intermediate
Runge-Kutta stages, i.e.

(3.28)

\boldsymbol{E}\equiv\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{e}_{1}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1})-\boldsymbol{e}_{\text{os}}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1})&\cdots&\boldsymbol{e}_{N_{t}^{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{n_{\mu}}}}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{n_{\mu}})-\boldsymbol{e}_{\text{os}}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{n_{\mu}})\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathcal{E}}\times N_{E}},

where
\boldsymbol{e}_{n}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k})
is the energy state at

n
th time step with problem parameter
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k}
for

n\in{\rm Nature}{N_{t}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k})}
computed from the FOM
simulation, e.g. the fully discrete RK2-average scheme (
2.8
), and

N_{E}=r\sum_{k=1}^{n_{\mu}}N_{t}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k})
with
r
being the number of
Runge-Kutta stages in a time step.
Then, POD computes its thin SVD:

(3.29)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{E}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{U}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{V}^{T},

where
\boldsymbol{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathcal{E}}\times N_{E}}
and

\boldsymbol{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{E}\times N_{E}}
are orthogonal matrices,
and
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{E}\times N_{E}}
is the diagonal
singular value matrix. Then POD chooses the leading

n_{e}
columns of
\boldsymbol{U}
to set

\mathbf{\Phi}_{e}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{u}_{1}&\ldots&\boldsymbol{u}_{n_{e}}\end{bmatrix}
, where
\boldsymbol{u}_{i}
is

i
-th column vector of
\boldsymbol{U}
. The basis size,

, is determined by the energy criteria, i.e., we find the
minimum

such that the following
condition is satisfied:

(3.30)

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n_{e}}\sigma_{i}}{\sum_{i=1}^{N_{E}}\sigma_{i}}

\displaystyle\geq\epsilon_{\sigma},

where
\sigma_{i}
is a
i
-th largest singular
value in the singular matrix,
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
, and
\epsilon_{\sigma}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
denotes a threshold.
3
3

3

We use the default value
\epsilon_{\sigma}=0.9999
unless stated otherwise.

The POD basis minimizes
\|\boldsymbol{E}-\mathbf{\Phi}_{e}\mathbf{\Phi}_{e}^{T}\boldsymbol{E}\|_{F}^{2}
over all
\mathbf{\Phi}_{e}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathcal{E}}\times n_{e}}
with orthonormal columns, where
\|\boldsymbol{A}\|_{F}
denotes the
Frobenius norm of a matrix
\boldsymbol{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{I\times J}
, defined as

\|\boldsymbol{A}\|_{F}=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{I}\sum_{j=1}^{J}a_{ij}^{2}}

with
a_{ij}
being the
(i,j)
element of
\boldsymbol{A}
. Since
the objective function does not change if
\mathbf{\Phi}_{e}
is post-multiplied by
an arbitrary
n_{e}\times n_{e}
orthogonal matrix, the POD
procedure seeks the optimal
n_{e}
-dimensional subspace that
captures the snapshots in the least-squares sense. For more details on POD, we
refer to
[
77
,
78
]
.

The same procedure can be used to construct the other bases
\mathbf{\Phi}_{v}
,

\mathbf{\Phi}_{x}
,
\mathbf{\Phi}_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}
, and
\mathbf{\Phi}_{\mathsf{F}^{tv}}
.
Then the reduced mass matrices in (

3.7
Discrete Empirical Interpolation Method

This section describes how we obtain the sampling matrices, i.e.

\mathbf{Z}_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}
and
\mathbf{Z}_{\mathsf{F}^{tv}}
. The discrete empirical
interpolation method (DEIM) is a popular choice for nonlinear model reduction.
It suffices to describe how to construct the sampling matrix for the
momentum nonlinear term only, i.e.,
\mathbf{Z}_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}
, as the other
matrix will be constructed in the same way. The sampling matrix

\mathbf{Z}_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}
is characterized by the sampling indices

\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{n_{\breve{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}}}\}
, which can be found either by a row
pivoted LU decomposition
[
73
]
or the strong column
pivoted rank-revealing QR (sRRQR) decomposition
[
79
,
80
]
. Algorithm 1 of
[
73
]
uses the
greedy algorithm to sequentially seek additional interpolating indices
corresponding to the entry with the largest magnitude of the residual of
projecting an active POD basis vector onto the preceding basis vectors at the
preceding interpolating indices. The number of interpolating indices
returned is the same as the number of basis vectors, i.e.
n_{\breve{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}}=n_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}
. Algorithm 3 of
[
81
]
and Algorithm 5 of

[
82
]
use the greedy procedure to minimize the error in
the gappy reconstruction of the POD basis vectors
\mathbf{\Phi}_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}
. These
algorithms allow over-sampling, i.e.
n_{\breve{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}}\geq n_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}
, and can be regarded as extensions of Algorithm 1 of

[
73
]
. Instead of using the greedy algorithm,
Q-DEIM is introduced in
[
79
]
as a new framework for constructing
the DEIM projection operator via the QR factorization with column pivoting.
Depending on the algorithm for selecting the sampling indices, the DEIM
projection error bound is determined. For example, the row pivoted LU
decomposition in
[
73
]
results in the following
error bound:

(3.34)

\displaystyle\|\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}-\mathcal{P}_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}\|_{2}\leq\kappa\|(\boldsymbol{I}_{N_{\mathcal{V}}}-\mathbf{\Phi}_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}\mathbf{\Phi}_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}^{T})\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}\|_{2},

where
\|\cdot\|_{2}
denotes
\ell_{2}
norm of a vector, and
\kappa
is
the condition number of
(\mathbf{Z}_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}^{T}\mathbf{\Phi}_{\mathsf{F}^{tv}})^{-1}
, bounded by

(3.35)

\displaystyle\kappa\leq(1+\sqrt{2N_{\mathcal{V}}})^{n_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}-1}\|\mathbf{\phi}_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}^{1}\|_{\infty}^{-1}.

On the other hand, the sRRQR factorization in
[
80
]
reveals a
tighter bound than (
3.35
):

(3.36)

\displaystyle\kappa\leq\sqrt{1+\eta^{2}n_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}(N_{\mathcal{V}}-n_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}})}

where
\eta
is a tuning parameter in the sRRQR factorization (i.e.,

f
in Algorithm 4 of
[
83
]
).
Once the sampling indices are determined, the reduced matrix
\left(\mathbf{Z}_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}^{T}\mathbf{\Phi}_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}\right)^{\dagger}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}\times n_{\breve{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}}}

can be precomputed and stored.

4
Time-windowing approach

Section
3
presented a spatial ROM, in which the spatial unknowns are
reduced to small subspaces. For time-dependent advection-dominated problems, the
dimensions of the ROM spaces required to maintain accuracy grow with the
simulation time. This is intuitively clear, considering that the solution
changes over time, across many elements in the domain, resulting in many linearly
independent snapshots. Consequently, the computational cost of the ROM
simulation grows with the simulation time, so that straightforward use of ROM
may not be faster than the FOM simulation. In order to ensure
adequate speed-up for the ROM simulation, in this section we introduce a
time-windowing approach that decomposes the time domain
[t_{0},t_{f}]

into small windows with temporally-local ROM spaces defined on each window, such
that each ROM space is small but accurate for its window. The time windows can
be prescribed for some given times
T_{w}
, i.e. window

w
is
[T_{w-1},T_{w}]
for
1\leq w<N_{w}
, with
T_{0}=0
and
T_{N_{w}}=t_{f}
. The time integration should solve for the windows

1\leq w\leq N_{w}
sequentially. First, we
discuss the solution representation and the hyper-reduced system in a time window
in Sections
4.1
and
4.2
, respectively.
We complete the discussion of the online phase with the initial conditions in a time window in
Section
4.3
. Then we move on to discuss the offline phase in the time windowing approach.
We present the mechanisms of decomposing the temporal domain into time windows
in Section
4.4
and the details
of construction of the temporally local solution subspaces by proper orthogonal
decomposition on snapshot matrices in Section
4.5
.
The sampling indices in a time window can be constructed by DEIM,
described in Section
3.7
.
Finally, we discuss the choices of offset vectors in each
time window and the corresponding online computation in Section
4.6
.

4.1
Solution representation

In the time window
w
, suppose a basis is
constructed for each variable and nonlinear term by using FOM snapshots from
that window. Then we restrict our solution space
to a subspace spanned by a reduced basis for
each field. That is, the subspace for velocity, energy, and position fields are
defined as

(4.1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{v}^{w}

\displaystyle\equiv\text{Span}\{\mathbf{\phi}_{v}^{w,i}\}_{i=1}^{n_{v}^{w}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathcal{V}}},

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{e}^{w}

\displaystyle\equiv\text{Span}\{\mathbf{\phi}_{e}^{w,i}\}_{i=1}^{n_{e}^{w}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathcal{E}}},

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{x}^{w}

\displaystyle\equiv\text{Span}\{\mathbf{\phi}_{x}^{w,i}\}_{i=1}^{n_{x}^{w}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathcal{V}}},

with
\dim(\mathcal{S}_{v}^{w})=n_{v}^{w}\ll N_{\mathcal{V}}
,

\dim(\mathcal{S}_{e}^{w})=n_{e}^{w}\ll N_{\mathcal{E}}
, and

\dim(\mathcal{S}_{x}^{w})=n_{x}^{w}\ll N_{\mathcal{V}}
. Using these subspaces, each discrete field is approximated in trial subspaces,

\boldsymbol{v}\approx\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}}\in\boldsymbol{v}_{\text{os}}^{w}+\mathcal{S}_{v}^{w}
,

\boldsymbol{e}\approx\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}}\in\boldsymbol{e}_{\text{os}}^{w}+\mathcal{S}_{e}^{w}
, and

\boldsymbol{x}\approx\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\boldsymbol{x}_{\text{os}}^{w}+\mathcal{S}_{x}^{w}
, or equivalently

(4.2)

\displaystyle\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}}(t;\boldsymbol{\mu})

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{v}_{\text{os}}^{w}(\boldsymbol{\mu})+\mathbf{\Phi}_{v}^{w}\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}}^{w}(t;\boldsymbol{\mu}),

(4.3)

\displaystyle\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}}(t;\boldsymbol{\mu})

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{e}_{\text{os}}^{w}(\boldsymbol{\mu})+\mathbf{\Phi}_{e}^{w}\widehat{\boldsymbol{e}}^{w}(t;\boldsymbol{\mu}),

(4.4)

\displaystyle\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}(t;\boldsymbol{\mu})

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{x}_{\text{os}}^{w}(\boldsymbol{\mu})+\mathbf{\Phi}_{x}^{w}\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}^{w}(t;\boldsymbol{\mu}),

where

\boldsymbol{v}_{\text{os}}^{w}(\boldsymbol{\mu})\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathcal{V}}}
,

\boldsymbol{e}_{\text{os}}^{w}(\boldsymbol{\mu})\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathcal{E}}}
, and

\boldsymbol{x}_{\text{os}}^{w}(\boldsymbol{\mu})\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathcal{V}}}

denote the prescribed offset vectors for velocity, energy, and position fields respectively,
which will be discussed in detail in Section
4.6
.
The basis matrices

\mathbf{\Phi}_{v}^{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathcal{V}}\times n_{v}^{w}}
,

\mathbf{\Phi}_{e}^{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathcal{E}}\times n_{e}^{w}}
, and

\mathbf{\Phi}_{x}^{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathcal{V}}\times n_{x}^{w}}

are defined as

(4.5)

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Phi}_{v}^{w}

\displaystyle\equiv\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{\phi}_{v}^{w,1}&\cdots&\mathbf{\phi}_{v}^{w,n_{v}^{w}}\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Phi}_{e}^{w}

\displaystyle\equiv\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{\phi}_{e}^{w,1}&\cdots&\mathbf{\phi}_{e}^{w,n_{e}^{w}}\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Phi}_{x}^{w}

\displaystyle\equiv\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{\phi}_{x}^{w,1}&\cdots&\mathbf{\phi}_{x}^{w,n_{x}^{w}}\end{bmatrix},

and

\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}}^{w}:[T_{w-1},T_{w}]\times\mathsf{D}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n_{v}^{w}}
,

\widehat{\boldsymbol{e}}^{w}:[T_{w-1},T_{w}]\times\mathsf{D}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n_{e}^{w}}
, and

\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}^{w}:[T_{w-1},T_{w}]\times\mathsf{D}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}^{w}}

denote the time-dependent generalized coordinates for velocity, energy, and
position fields, respectively. Replacing
\boldsymbol{v}
,
\boldsymbol{e}
, and
\boldsymbol{x}
with

\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}}
,
\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}}
, and
\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}
in
Eqs. (
2.3
), the semi-discretized Lagrangian hydrodynamics system
in the time window
[T_{w-1},T_{w}]

becomes the following over-determined semi-discrete system:

(4.6)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{M}_{\mathcal{V}}\mathbf{\Phi}_{v}^{w}\frac{d\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}}^{w}}{dt}

\displaystyle=-\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}(\boldsymbol{v}_{\text{os}}^{w}+\mathbf{\Phi}_{v}^{w}\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}}^{w},\boldsymbol{e}_{\text{os}}^{w}+\mathbf{\Phi}_{e}^{w}\widehat{\boldsymbol{e}}^{w},\boldsymbol{x}_{\text{os}}^{w}+\mathbf{\Phi}_{x}^{w}\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}^{w},t;\boldsymbol{\mu})

(4.7)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{M}_{\mathcal{E}}\mathbf{\Phi}_{e}^{w}\frac{d\widehat{\boldsymbol{e}}^{w}}{dt}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{F}^{tv}(\boldsymbol{v}_{\text{os}}^{w}+\mathbf{\Phi}_{v}^{w}\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}}^{w},\boldsymbol{e}_{\text{os}}^{w}+\mathbf{\Phi}_{e}^{w}\widehat{\boldsymbol{e}}^{w},\boldsymbol{x}_{\text{os}}^{w}+\mathbf{\Phi}_{x}^{w}\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}^{w},t;\boldsymbol{\mu})

(4.8)

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Phi}_{x}^{w}\frac{d\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}^{w}}{dt}

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{\Phi}_{x}^{w})^{T}\boldsymbol{v}_{\text{os}}^{w}+\mathbf{\Phi}_{v}^{w}\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}}^{w}.

The system (
4.6
) of ordinary differential equations is closed by defining the initial
condition at
t=T_{w-1}
,
which will be discussed in Section
4.3
.

4.2
Hyper-reduction

Following Section
3.2
and Section
3.3
,
we derive the hyper-reduced system in a time window
w
.
We approximate
\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}
and
\mathsf{F}^{tv}
as

(4.9)

\displaystyle\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}

\displaystyle\approx\mathbf{\Phi}_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}^{w}\widehat{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}^{w},

\displaystyle\mathsf{F}^{tv}

\displaystyle\approx\mathbf{\Phi}_{\mathsf{F}^{tv}}^{w}\widehat{\mathsf{F}^{tv}}^{w}.

That is,
\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}
and
\mathsf{F}^{tv}
are projected onto subspaces

\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}^{w}\equiv\text{Span}\{\mathbf{\phi}_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}^{w,i}\}_{i=1}^{n_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}^{w}}
and

\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{F}^{tv}}^{w}\equiv\text{Span}\{\mathbf{\phi}_{\mathsf{F}^{tv}}^{w,i}\}_{i=1}^{n_{\mathsf{F}^{tv}}^{w}}
,
where
\mathbf{\Phi}_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}^{w}\equiv\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{\phi}_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}^{w,1}&\ldots&\mathbf{\phi}_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}^{w,n_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}^{w}}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathcal{V}}\times n_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}^{w}}
,

n_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}^{w}\ll N_{\mathcal{V}}
and

\mathbf{\Phi}_{\mathsf{F}^{tv}}^{w}\equiv\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{\phi}_{\mathsf{F}^{tv}}^{w,1}&\ldots&\mathbf{\phi}_{\mathsf{F}^{tv}}^{w,n_{\mathsf{F}^{tv}}^{w}}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathcal{E}}\times n_{\mathsf{F}^{tv}}^{w}}
,

n_{\mathsf{F}^{tv}}^{w}\ll N_{\mathcal{E}}
denote the nonlinear term basis matrices and

\widehat{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}^{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}^{w}}
and
\widehat{\mathsf{F}^{tv}}^{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathsf{F}^{tv}}^{w}}
denote the generalized coordinates of the nonlinear
terms. Then, the hyper-reduced system for
[T_{w-1},T_{w}]
is given by

(4.10)

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{M}}_{\mathcal{V}}^{w}\frac{d\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}}^{w}}{dt}

\displaystyle=-(\mathbf{\Phi}_{v}^{w})^{T}\mathcal{P}_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}^{w}\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}(\boldsymbol{v}_{\text{os}}^{w}+\mathbf{\Phi}_{v}^{w}\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}}^{w},\boldsymbol{e}_{\text{os}}^{w}+\mathbf{\Phi}_{e}^{w}\widehat{\boldsymbol{e}}^{w},\boldsymbol{x}_{\text{os}}^{w}+\mathbf{\Phi}_{x}^{w}\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}^{w},t;\boldsymbol{\mu})

(4.11)

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{M}}_{\mathcal{E}}^{w}\frac{d\widehat{\boldsymbol{e}}^{w}}{dt}

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{\Phi}_{e}^{w})^{T}\mathcal{P}_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}^{w}\mathsf{F}^{tv}(\boldsymbol{v}_{\text{os}}^{w}+\mathbf{\Phi}_{v}^{w}\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}}^{w},\boldsymbol{e}_{\text{os}}^{w}+\mathbf{\Phi}_{e}^{w}\widehat{\boldsymbol{e}}^{w},\boldsymbol{x}_{\text{os}}^{w}+\mathbf{\Phi}_{x}^{w}\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}^{w},t;\boldsymbol{\mu})

(4.12)

\displaystyle\frac{d\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}^{w}}{dt}

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{\Phi}_{x}^{w})^{T}\boldsymbol{v}_{\text{os}}^{w}+(\mathbf{\Phi}_{x}^{w})^{T}\mathbf{\Phi}_{v}^{w}\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}}^{w},

where the reduced kinematic matrix,
\widehat{\boldsymbol{M}}_{\mathcal{V}}^{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{v}^{w}\times n_{v}^{w}}
, and thermodynamic mass matrix,

\widehat{\boldsymbol{M}}_{\mathcal{E}}^{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{e}^{w}\times n_{e}^{w}}
, are defined as

(4.13)

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{M}}_{\mathcal{V}}^{w}

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{\Phi}_{v}^{w})^{T}\boldsymbol{M}_{\mathcal{V}}\mathbf{\Phi}_{v}^{w},

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{M}}_{\mathcal{E}}^{w}

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{\Phi}_{e}^{w})^{T}\boldsymbol{M}_{\mathcal{E}}\mathbf{\Phi}_{e}^{w},

the oblique projection matrices
\mathcal{P}_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}^{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathcal{V}}\times N_{\mathcal{V}}}
and

\mathcal{P}_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}^{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathcal{E}}\times N_{\mathcal{E}}}
are defined as

(4.14)

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}^{w}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{\Phi}_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}^{w}((\mathbf{Z}_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}^{w})^{T}\mathbf{\Phi}_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}^{w})^{\dagger}(\mathbf{Z}_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}^{w})^{T},

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\mathsf{F}^{tv}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{\Phi}_{\mathsf{F}^{tv}}^{w}\left((\mathbf{Z}_{\mathsf{F}^{tv}}^{w})^{T}\mathbf{\Phi}_{\mathsf{F}^{tv}}^{w}\right)^{\dagger}(\mathbf{Z}_{\mathsf{F}^{tv}}^{w})^{T},

with the sampling matrices

\mathbf{Z}_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}^{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathcal{V}}\times n_{\breve{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}}^{w}}
and

\mathbf{Z}_{\mathsf{F}^{tv}}^{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathcal{E}}\times n_{\breve{\mathsf{F}^{tv}}}^{w}}
.
As in the case of spatial ROM, the reduced mass matrices

\widehat{\boldsymbol{M}}_{\mathcal{V}}^{w}
and

\widehat{\boldsymbol{M}}_{\mathcal{E}}^{w}
and
the reduced matrices
\mathbf{\Phi}_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}^{w}((\mathbf{Z}_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}^{w})^{T}\mathbf{\Phi}_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}^{w})^{\dagger}

and
\mathbf{\Phi}_{\mathsf{F}^{tv}}^{w}\left((\mathbf{Z}_{\mathsf{F}^{tv}}^{w})^{T}\mathbf{\Phi}_{\mathsf{F}^{tv}}^{w}\right)^{\dagger}
can be precomputed and stored.

4.3
Initial condition of window

In this subsection, we discuss the initial conditions of
the variables in a time window.
The corresponding fully discretized system can be obtained by the time
integrators introduced in Section
3.4
.
Using the relation (

), one can derive the initial condition by
lifting the ROM solution to the FOM spaces, using the ROM bases in the time
window
w-1
, and then projecting onto the ROM spaces in the time window
w
, i.e.

(4.15)

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}}^{w}(T_{w-1})

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{\Phi}_{v}^{w})^{T}(\boldsymbol{v}_{\text{os}}^{w-1}+\mathbf{\Phi}_{v}^{w-1}\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}}^{w-1}(T_{w-1})-\boldsymbol{v}_{\text{os}}^{w}),

(4.16)

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{e}}^{w}(T_{w-1})

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{\Phi}_{e}^{w})^{T}(\boldsymbol{e}_{\text{os}}^{w-1}+\mathbf{\Phi}_{e}^{w-1}\widehat{\boldsymbol{e}}^{w-1}(T_{w-1})-\boldsymbol{e}_{\text{os}}^{w}),

(4.17)

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}^{w}(T_{w-1})

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{\Phi}_{x}^{w})^{T}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\text{os}}^{w-1}+\mathbf{\Phi}_{x}^{w-1}\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}^{w-1}(T_{w-1})-\boldsymbol{x}_{\text{os}}^{w}).

However, in view of Theorem

(4.18)

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}}^{w}(T_{w-1})

\displaystyle=(\widehat{\boldsymbol{M}}_{\mathcal{V}}^{w})^{-1}(\mathbf{\Phi}_{v}^{w})^{T}\boldsymbol{M}_{\mathcal{V}}(\boldsymbol{v}_{\text{os}}^{w-1}+\mathbf{\Phi}_{v}^{w-1}\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}}^{w-1}(T_{w-1})-\boldsymbol{v}_{\text{os}}^{w}),

(4.19)

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{e}}^{w}(T_{w-1})

\displaystyle=(\widehat{\boldsymbol{M}}_{\mathcal{E}}^{w})^{-1}(\mathbf{\Phi}_{e}^{w})^{T}\boldsymbol{M}_{\mathcal{E}}(\boldsymbol{e}_{\text{os}}^{w-1}+\mathbf{\Phi}_{e}^{w-1}\widehat{\boldsymbol{e}}^{w-1}(T_{w-1})-\boldsymbol{e}_{\text{os}}^{w}),

(4.20)

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}^{w}(T_{w-1})

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{\Phi}_{x}^{w})^{T}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\text{os}}^{w-1}+\mathbf{\Phi}_{x}^{w-1}\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}^{w-1}(T_{w-1})-\boldsymbol{x}_{\text{os}}^{w}).

We remark that for either case, the determination of the initial condition
only involves inexpensive operations in reduced dimensions in the online case,
since the contribution of offset vectors can be precomputed and stored in the offline phase.

4.4
Mechanism of decomposing time domain

The remainder of this section is devoted to discussing the
offline computations of the reduced order model.
It should be noted that the time windows are set
before constructing the reduced bases in the offline phase,
which will be discussed in Section
4.5
.
We consider two approaches of decomposing the time domain into windows.

4.4.1
Physical time windowing

A natural approach of decomposing the domain into windows is to use a fixed
partition, that is, the end points
\{T_{w}\}_{w=1}^{N_{w}}
are fixed and user-defined. Using this naive
approach, the number of snapshots for a variable in a window is proportional to
the window size if a uniform time step is used in the FOM simulation. In
addition, if a uniform partition is used to decompose the domain into windows,
then the basis sizes will be heuristically balanced among the windows. However,
in the setting of adaptive time stepping, it becomes unclear how to
prescribe window sizes to balance the ROM basis size among time windows.

4.4.2
Time windowing by number of samples

We consider an alternative way to divide the time domain into windows using a
prescribed number of snapshots per window, which can be heuristically related to
the basis size. Let
N_{\text{sample}}
be the number of FOM solution
snapshots per window. Recall that
for
k\in{\rm Nature}{n_{\mu}}
,
N_{t}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k})
is the
number of time steps in the FOM simulation with the problem parameter

\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k}
, and the temporal domain is discretized as

\{t_{n}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k})\}_{n=0}^{N_{t}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k})}
. We determine the end
points of the time windows sequentially. Given the end point

T_{w-1}
of the time window
w-1
, we denote by

n_{w-1}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k})
the latest time instance
in the FOM simulation with the problem parameter
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k}
which lies
in the time window
w-1
, i.e.

t_{n_{w-1}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k})}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k})<T_{w-1}\leq t_{n_{w-1}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k})+1}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k})
. Then we determine the end
point of the time window
w
as

(4.21)

T_{w}=\min\left\{t_{n_{w-1}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k})+N_{\text{sample}}+1}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k}):k\in{\rm Nature}{n_{\mu}}\right\},

i.e. the shortest time among all the problem parameters
\{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{n_{\mu}}
such that
N_{\text{sample}}+1
new time steps are
taken. Here, we extend the notation
t_{n}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k})
to
denote
t_{n}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k})=t_{f}
for all
n\geq N_{t}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k})
. The iterative process is terminated when

T_{w}=t_{f}
, at which we set
N_{w}=w
.

4.5
Temporally local solution subspaces

In this subsection, we discuss the construction of the solution subspaces in a
time window. Again, it will be sufficient to discuss how to construct the
reduced basis for the energy field only, i.e.,
\mathbf{\Phi}_{e}^{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathcal{E}}\times n_{e}^{w}}
, because
other bases will be constructed in the same way. In order to collect solution
data for performing POD, we run FOM simulations on a set of problem parameters,
namely
\{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{n_{\mu}}
.
Using the mechanisms described in
Section
4.4.1
or Section
4.4.2
, the temporal
domain is decomposed into time windows. The collected FOM solution vectors are
then clustered into time windows according to the time instance when the sample
was taken. By choosing
\boldsymbol{e}_{\text{os}}^{w}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k})

appropriately, a solution snapshot matrix is formed by assembling all the FOM
solution data, i.e.

(4.22)

\boldsymbol{E}^{w}\equiv\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{e}_{n_{w-1}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1})}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k})-\boldsymbol{e}_{\text{os}}^{w}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1})&\cdots&\boldsymbol{e}_{n_{w}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{n_{\mu}})+1}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{n_{\mu}})-\boldsymbol{e}_{\text{os}}^{w}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{n_{\mu}})\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathcal{E}}\times N_{S}^{w}},

where
N_{S}^{w}=\sum_{k=1}^{n_{\mu}}\left[r\left(n_{w}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k})-n_{w-1}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k})\right)+1\right]
, and then
its thin SVD is computed to obtain
\mathbf{\Phi}_{e}^{w}
as in
Section
3.5
. Similar to the discussion in Section
3.5
and Section
3.6

for the spatial ROM, the nonlinear term bases
\mathbf{\Phi}_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}^{w}
and

\mathbf{\Phi}_{\mathsf{F}^{tv}}^{w}
in the time window
w
can
also be obtained either by POD on nonlinear term snapshots or the SNS method in

[
13
]
with the subspace relations

\mathbf{\Phi}_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}^{w}=\boldsymbol{M}_{\mathcal{V}}\mathbf{\Phi}_{v}^{w}
and
\mathbf{\Phi}_{\mathsf{F}^{tv}}^{w}=\boldsymbol{M}_{\mathcal{E}}\mathbf{\Phi}_{e}^{w}
.

4.6
Window offset vectors

To end this section, we discuss the choices of the offset vectors in each of the
time windows. Again, we present how to construct the offset vectors for the
energy field only, i.e.
\boldsymbol{e}_{\text{os}}^{w}
, as the velocity and position
fields are treated similarly.
We remark that, in obtaining the reduced basis matrices from the problem
parameters
\{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{n_{\mu}}
, the offset
vector
\boldsymbol{e}_{\text{os}}^{w}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k})
has to be determined
and subtracted from the FOM solution vectors in (
4.22
). On
the other hand, in employing the reduced order model (
4.10
) for a
generic problem parameter
\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\mathsf{D}
, the offset vector

\boldsymbol{e}_{\text{os}}^{w}(\boldsymbol{\mu})
with the same physical meaning has to be
calculated or approximated.

4.6.1
Initial states

The first choice is to use the initial states as in Section
3
, i.e.
we set
\boldsymbol{e}_{\text{os}}^{w}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k})=\boldsymbol{e}(0;\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k})
for all
w\in{\rm Nature}{N_{w}}
and
k\in{\rm Nature}{n_{\mu}}
. Then, for a generic problem parameter
\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\mathsf{D}
, we simply take
\boldsymbol{e}_{\text{os}}^{w}(\boldsymbol{\mu})=\boldsymbol{e}(0;\boldsymbol{\mu})
for all
w\in{\rm Nature}{N_{w}}
.

4.6.2
Previous window

Another approach is to choose the offset vectors
depending on the window. A natural choice is to choose
the offset as an approximate solution around the time

T_{w-1}
, i.e.

\boldsymbol{e}_{\text{os}}^{w}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k})=\boldsymbol{e}_{n_{w-1}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k})}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k})

for all
w\in{\rm Nature}{N_{w}}
and
k\in{\rm Nature}{n_{\mu}}
.
In that case, in employing the reduced order model in the time window
w
for a
generic problem parameter
\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\mathsf{D}
, an approximation of the
energy field
\boldsymbol{e}(T_{w-1};\boldsymbol{\mu})
at the time

t=T_{w-1}
can be used as the offset vector

\boldsymbol{e}_{\text{os}}^{w}(\boldsymbol{\mu})
. One such approximation is given by
using the final state in the previous window. More precisely, we lift the ROM
solution in the time window
w-1
to the FOM spaces by using the ROM
bases, i.e.

(4.23)

\boldsymbol{e}_{\text{os}}^{w}(\boldsymbol{\mu})=\boldsymbol{e}_{\text{os}}^{w-1}(\boldsymbol{\mu})+\mathbf{\Phi}_{e}^{w-1}\widehat{\boldsymbol{e}}^{w-1}(T_{w-1};\boldsymbol{\mu}).

The advantage of this approach is that it provides the best approximation of the
final state in the previous window in the solution subspace of the current
window. However, the drawback of this approach is that it cannot be precomputed,
and it involves the lifting operation which scales with the dimension of the FOM
state space. In the case of advection-dominated problems, time windows are
typically small and change frequently, so the lifting operation
may limit the overall speed-up.

4.6.3
Parametric interpolation

The last choice we present in this section is to make use of the available data
from the problem parameters
\{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{n_{\mu}}
. We consider the choice of the offset
vector
\boldsymbol{e}_{\text{os}}^{w}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k})=\boldsymbol{e}_{n_{w-1}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k})}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k})

for all
w\in{\rm Nature}{N_{w}}
and
k\in{\rm Nature}{n_{\mu}}
.
In order to approximate the offset vector

\boldsymbol{e}_{\text{os}}^{w}(\boldsymbol{\mu})
for a generic parameter
\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\mathsf{D}
, we can interpolate the known offset vectors

\{\boldsymbol{e}_{\text{os}}^{w}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k})\}_{k=1}^{n_{\mu}}
,
which are computed and stored in the process of snapshot collection. Here, we
present an interpolation procedure using the inverse distance weighting (IDW).
Suppose
d:\mathsf{D}\times\mathsf{D}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
is a
distance metric on the domain
\mathsf{D}
and
r\geq 1
is a given number. The interpolated value is given by the convex combination

(4.24)

\boldsymbol{e}_{\text{os}}^{w}(\boldsymbol{\mu})=\begin{cases}\boldsymbol{e}_{\text{os}}^{w}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k})&\text{ if }d(\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k})=0\text{ for some }k\in{\rm Nature}{n_{\mu}},\\
\dfrac{\sum_{k=1}^{n_{\mu}}q_{k}(\boldsymbol{\mu})\boldsymbol{e}_{\text{os}}^{w}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k})}{\sum_{k=1}^{n_{\mu}}q_{k}(\boldsymbol{\mu})}&\text{ otherwise},\end{cases}

where, for
k\in{\rm Nature}{n_{\mu}}
, the coefficients

q_{k}(\boldsymbol{\mu})\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
are given by

(4.25)

q_{k}(\boldsymbol{\mu})=\dfrac{1}{d(\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k})^{r}}.

In our work, we use the Euclidean distance as the metric
d
and
r=2
.
One advantage of the IDW interpolation
scheme is that the offset vectors are exactly the stored interpolating values in
the reproductive cases, which provides the most accurate approximations.
Another advantage is that the interpolation can be precomputed and is
inexpensive as the interpolating coefficients depend solely on the problem
parameters, which typically lie in low-dimensional structures. However, the IDW
interpolation scheme lacks the ability to extrapolate. In practice, other
schemes allowing inexpensive and accurate extrapolation can be considered.

5
Error bounds

In this section, we present error estimates for our proposed reduced order model
in Section
3
. Due to the use of adaptive time-step control, we
cannot directly compare the temporally discrete full order model solution and
the reduced order approximation. Instead, we will use the continuous-in-time
full order model solution in (
2.3
) as the reference solution.
Throughout the section, when there is no ambiguity, we drop the time symbol

t
and the problem parameter symbol
\boldsymbol{\mu}
for simplifying the
notations. The error analysis in decomposed into two parts. The first part
accounts for the approximation error of the reduced order model. More
specifically, we analyze the error between the continuous-in-time full order
model solution in (
2.3
) and the continuous-in-time reduced order
approximation in (
3.17
), defined by

(5.1)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{v}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{v}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}}=\boldsymbol{v}-(\boldsymbol{v}_{\text{os}}+\mathbf{\Phi}_{v}\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}})

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{e}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{e}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}}=\boldsymbol{e}-(\boldsymbol{e}_{\text{os}}+\mathbf{\Phi}_{e}\widehat{\boldsymbol{e}})

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{x}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{x}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}=\boldsymbol{x}-(\boldsymbol{x}_{\text{os}}+\mathbf{\Phi}_{x}\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}).

The second part accounts for the truncation error of the temporal
discretization. More specifically, we analyze the error between the
continuous-in-time reduced order approximation in (
3.17
) and the
RK2-average fully discrete reduced order approximation in
(
3.22
), defined by

(5.2)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\delta}_{v,n}

\displaystyle=\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}}(t_{n})-\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}}_{n}=\mathbf{\Phi}_{v}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}}(t_{n})-\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}}_{n})

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\delta}_{e,n}

\displaystyle=\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}}(t_{n})-\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}}_{n}=\mathbf{\Phi}_{e}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{e}}(t_{n})-\widehat{\boldsymbol{e}}_{n})

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\delta}_{x,n}

\displaystyle=\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}(t_{n})-\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n}=\mathbf{\Phi}_{x}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}(t_{n})-\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n}).

We remark that the error analysis can be extended to the time windowing approach
in Section
4
by viewing the final solution in the previous
window as the initial condition and stacking up the error in a sequence of time
windows.

Before we begin with the analysis, we introduce a few tools and notations that
will facilitate our discussion. Since the mass matrices
\boldsymbol{M}_{\mathcal{V}}
and

\boldsymbol{M}_{\mathcal{E}}
are symmetric and positive definite, they possess the
Cholesky factorizations

(5.3)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{M}_{\mathcal{V}}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{L}_{\mathcal{V}}\boldsymbol{L}_{\mathcal{V}}^{T}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{M}_{\mathcal{E}}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{L}_{\mathcal{E}}\boldsymbol{L}_{\mathcal{E}}^{T}.

The mass matrices also induce the weighted functional
L^{2}
norm
on the kinematic and thermodynamic finite element space respectively:

(5.4)

\displaystyle\left\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\right\rVert_{\boldsymbol{M}_{\mathcal{V}}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{M}_{\mathcal{V}}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}

\displaystyle\left\lVert\boldsymbol{e}\right\rVert_{\boldsymbol{M}_{\mathcal{E}}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{e}\cdot\boldsymbol{M}_{\mathcal{E}}\cdot\boldsymbol{e}.

On the Cartesian product space
\mathcal{V}\times\mathcal{E}\times\mathcal{V}
, we define the product norm

(5.5)

{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{e},\boldsymbol{x})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}^{2}=\left\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\right\rVert_{\boldsymbol{M}_{\mathcal{V}}}^{2}+\left\lVert\boldsymbol{e}\right\rVert_{\boldsymbol{M}_{\mathcal{E}}}^{2}+\left\lVert\boldsymbol{x}\right\rVert_{2}^{2},

where
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{2}
denotes the standard Euclidean norm.

5.1
A-priori estimate for approximation error

We start with providing an a-priori error estimate between the
continuous-in-time full order model solution in (
2.3
) and the
continuous-in-time reduced order approximation in (
3.17
). The error
bound depends on the full order model solution and is controlled by a
combination of several quantities, namely the mismatch of initial generalized
coordinates in the reduced subspaces, the oblique projection error of the
solution onto the reduced subspaces over time, and the projection error of the
nonlinear terms in the Euclidean norm.

Theorem 1
.

Assume there holds the following Lipchitz continuity conditions for the force
matrix
\boldsymbol{F}
: there exists
K_{1},K_{2}>0
such that for any

(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{e},\boldsymbol{x}),(\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{e}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})\in\mathcal{V}\times\mathcal{E}\times\mathcal{V}
,

(5.6)

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{e},\boldsymbol{x})-\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}(\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{e}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq K_{1}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{e}-\boldsymbol{e}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathsf{F}^{tv}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{e},\boldsymbol{x})-\mathsf{F}^{tv}(\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{e}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq K_{2}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{e}-\boldsymbol{e}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}.

\displaystyle\leq Ce^{Ct}\Big[{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{v},\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{e},\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{x})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}+\max_{0\leq s\leq t}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{v}(s),\boldsymbol{\theta}_{e}(s),\boldsymbol{\theta}_{x}(s)\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}+

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\left\lVert(\mathbb{I}_{\mathcal{V}}-\mathcal{P}_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}})\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{e},\boldsymbol{x})\right\rVert_{2}+\left\lVert((\mathbb{I}_{\mathcal{E}}-\mathcal{P}_{\mathsf{F}^{tv}})\mathsf{F}^{tv}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{e},\boldsymbol{x})\right\rVert_{2}ds\Big],

\begin{split}\mathcal{P}_{v}&=\boldsymbol{L}_{\mathcal{V}}^{T}\mathbf{\Phi}_{v}\widehat{\boldsymbol{M}}_{\mathcal{V}}^{-1}\mathbf{\Phi}_{v}^{T}\boldsymbol{L}_{\mathcal{V}}\\

\mathcal{P}_{x}&=\mathbf{\Phi}_{x}\mathbf{\Phi}_{x}^{T}\end{split}

are orthogonal projection matrices and hence have norm
1
. Therefore, we have
the following estimates for the residuals

(5.21)

\displaystyle\left\lVert\boldsymbol{L}_{\mathcal{V}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}_{v}^{(2)}\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\boldsymbol{L}_{\mathcal{V}}^{-1}(\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{e},\boldsymbol{x})-\mathcal{P}_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}},\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}},\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}))\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\left\lVert\boldsymbol{L}_{\mathcal{E}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}_{e}^{(2)}\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\boldsymbol{L}_{\mathcal{E}}^{-1}(\mathsf{F}^{tv}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{e},\boldsymbol{x})-\mathcal{P}_{\mathsf{F}^{tv}}\mathsf{F}^{tv}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}},\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}},\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}))\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}_{x}^{(2)}\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\boldsymbol{L}_{\mathcal{V}}^{-T}\boldsymbol{L}_{\mathcal{V}}^{T}(\boldsymbol{v}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}})\right\rVert_{2}.

We further invoke the fact that the norm of a symmetric and positive definite
matrix is the square of that of its Cholesky factor and its square root, to
obtain

(5.22)

\displaystyle\left\lVert\boldsymbol{L}_{\mathcal{V}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}_{v}^{(2)}\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\boldsymbol{M}_{\mathcal{V}}^{-1/2}\right\rVert_{2}\left\lVert\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{e},\boldsymbol{x})-\mathcal{P}_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}},\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}},\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}})\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\left\lVert\boldsymbol{L}_{\mathcal{E}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}_{e}^{(2)}\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\boldsymbol{M}_{\mathcal{E}}^{-1/2}\right\rVert_{2}\left\lVert\mathsf{F}^{tv}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{e},\boldsymbol{x})-\mathcal{P}_{\mathsf{F}^{tv}}\mathsf{F}^{tv}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}},\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}},\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}})\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}_{x}^{(2)}\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\boldsymbol{M}_{\mathcal{V}}^{-1/2}\right\rVert_{2}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{L}_{\mathcal{V}}^{T}(\boldsymbol{v}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}})\right\rVert_{2}.

Using triangular inequalities, we get

(5.23)

\displaystyle\left\lVert\boldsymbol{L}_{\mathcal{V}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}_{v}^{(2)}\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\boldsymbol{M}_{\mathcal{V}}^{-1/2}\right\rVert_{2}\left(\left\lVert(\mathbb{I}_{\mathcal{V}}-\mathcal{P}_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}})\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{e},\boldsymbol{x})\right\rVert_{2}+\left\lVert\mathcal{P}_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}(\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{e},\boldsymbol{x})-\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}},\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}},\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}))\right\rVert_{2}\right)

\displaystyle\left\lVert\boldsymbol{L}_{\mathcal{E}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}_{e}^{(2)}\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\boldsymbol{M}_{\mathcal{E}}^{-1/2}\right\rVert_{2}\left(\left\lVert(\mathbb{I}_{\mathcal{E}}-\mathcal{P}_{\mathsf{F}^{tv}})\mathsf{F}^{tv}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{e},\boldsymbol{x})\right\rVert_{2}+\left\lVert\mathcal{P}_{\mathsf{F}^{tv}}(\mathsf{F}^{tv}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{e},\boldsymbol{x})-\mathsf{F}^{tv}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}},\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}},\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}))\right\rVert_{2}\right)

\displaystyle\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}_{x}^{(2)}\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\boldsymbol{M}_{\mathcal{V}}^{-1/2}\right\rVert_{2}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{v}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\rVert_{\boldsymbol{M}_{\mathcal{V}}}.

With the assumptions (
5.6
), we substitute (
5.23
) into
(
5.18
) and obtain

(5.24)

\displaystyle\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{v}(t)\right\rVert_{\boldsymbol{M}_{\mathcal{V}}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{v}\right\rVert_{\boldsymbol{M}_{\mathcal{V}}}+\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\theta}_{v}(t)\right\rVert_{\boldsymbol{M}_{\mathcal{V}}}+\left\lVert\boldsymbol{M}_{\mathcal{V}}^{-1/2}\right\rVert_{2}\int_{0}^{t}\left\lVert(\mathbb{I}_{\mathcal{V}}-\mathcal{P}_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}})\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{e},\boldsymbol{x})\right\rVert_{2}+\left\lVert\mathcal{P}_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}\right\rVert_{2}K_{1}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{v}(s),\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{e}(s),\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{x}(s))\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}ds

\displaystyle\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{e}(t)\right\rVert_{\boldsymbol{M}_{\mathcal{E}}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{e}\right\rVert_{\boldsymbol{M}_{\mathcal{E}}}+\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\theta}_{e}(t)\right\rVert_{\boldsymbol{M}_{\mathcal{E}}}+\left\lVert\boldsymbol{M}_{\mathcal{E}}^{-1/2}\right\rVert_{2}\int_{0}^{t}\left\lVert((\mathbb{I}_{\mathcal{E}}-\mathcal{P}_{\mathsf{F}^{tv}})\mathsf{F}^{tv}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{e},\boldsymbol{x})\right\rVert_{2}+\left\lVert\mathcal{P}_{\mathsf{F}^{tv}}\right\rVert_{2}K_{2}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{v}(s),\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{e}(s),\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{x}(s))\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}ds

\displaystyle\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{x}(t)\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{x}\right\rVert_{2}+\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\theta}_{x}(t)\right\rVert_{2}+\left\lVert\boldsymbol{M}_{\mathcal{V}}^{-1/2}\right\rVert_{2}\int_{0}^{t}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{v}(s),\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{e}(s),\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{x}(s))\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}ds.

which further implies

(5.25)

\displaystyle{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{v}(t),\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{e}(t),\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{x}(t))\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{3}\Big[{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{v},\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{e},\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{x})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}+\max_{0\leq s\leq t}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{v}(s),\boldsymbol{\theta}_{e}(s),\boldsymbol{\theta}_{x}(s)\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}+

\displaystyle\left\lVert\boldsymbol{M}_{\mathcal{V}}^{-1/2}\right\rVert_{2}\int_{0}^{t}\left\lVert(\mathbb{I}_{\mathcal{V}}-\mathcal{P}_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}})\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{e},\boldsymbol{x})\right\rVert_{2}ds+

\displaystyle\left\lVert\boldsymbol{M}_{\mathcal{E}}^{-1/2}\right\rVert_{2}\int_{0}^{t}\left\lVert((\mathbb{I}_{\mathcal{E}}-\mathcal{P}_{\mathsf{F}^{tv}})\mathsf{F}^{tv}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{e},\boldsymbol{x})\right\rVert_{2}ds+

\displaystyle C_{1}\int_{0}^{t}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{v}(s),\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{e}(s),\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{x}(s))\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}ds\Big],

where
C_{1}=\max\left\{\left\lVert\boldsymbol{M}_{\mathcal{V}}^{-1/2}\right\rVert_{2}\left\lVert\mathcal{P}_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}\right\rVert_{2}K_{1},\left\lVert\boldsymbol{M}_{\mathcal{E}}^{-1/2}\right\rVert_{2}\left\lVert\mathcal{P}_{\mathsf{F}^{tv}}\right\rVert_{2}K_{2},\left\lVert\boldsymbol{M}_{\mathcal{V}}^{-1/2}\right\rVert_{2}\right\}
.
By Gronwall’s inequality, we conclude

(5.26)

\displaystyle{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{v}(t),\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{e}(t),\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{x}(t))\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{3}\exp\left(C_{1}t\right)\Big[{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{v},\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{e},\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{x})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}+\max_{0\leq s\leq t}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{v}(s),\boldsymbol{\theta}_{e}(s),\boldsymbol{\theta}_{x}(s)\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}+

\displaystyle\left\lVert\boldsymbol{M}_{\mathcal{V}}^{-1/2}\right\rVert_{2}\int_{0}^{t}\left\lVert(\mathbb{I}_{\mathcal{V}}-\mathcal{P}_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}})\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{e},\boldsymbol{x})\right\rVert_{2}ds+

\displaystyle\left\lVert\boldsymbol{M}_{\mathcal{E}}^{-1/2}\right\rVert_{2}\int_{0}^{t}\left\lVert((\mathbb{I}_{\mathcal{E}}-\mathcal{P}_{\mathsf{F}^{tv}})\mathsf{F}^{tv}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{e},\boldsymbol{x})\right\rVert_{2}ds\Big],

which provides the desired result.
∎

5.2
A-posteriori estimate for approximation error

Next, we present an a-posteriori error estimate between the continuous-in-time
full order model solution in (
2.3
) and the continuous-in-time reduced
order approximation in (
3.17
). The error bound depends on the
reduced order approximation and is controlled by a combination of the mismatch
of initial condition and the oblique projection error of the nonlinear terms in
the Euclidean norm.

Theorem 2
.

Assume the Lipchitz continuity conditions (
5.6
) hold. Then
there exists a generic constant
C>0
such that for
t>0
, we have

(5.27)

\displaystyle{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{v}(t),\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{e}(t),\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{x}(t))\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}

\displaystyle\leq C\exp\left(Ct\right)\Big[{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{v}(0),\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{e}(0),\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{x}(0))\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}+

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\left\lVert(\mathbb{I}_{\mathcal{V}}-\boldsymbol{M}_{\mathcal{V}}\mathbf{\Phi}_{v}\widehat{\boldsymbol{M}}_{\mathcal{V}}^{-1}\mathbf{\Phi}_{v}^{T}\mathcal{P}_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}})\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}},\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}},\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}})\right\rVert_{2}ds+

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\left\lVert(\mathbb{I}_{\mathcal{E}}-\boldsymbol{M}_{\mathcal{E}}\mathbf{\Phi}_{e}\widehat{\boldsymbol{M}}_{\mathcal{E}}^{-1}\mathbf{\Phi}_{e}^{T}\mathcal{P}_{\mathsf{F}^{tv}})\mathsf{F}^{tv}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}},\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}},\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}})\right\rVert_{2}ds\Big]

where
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{v}(t)
,

\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{e}(t)
, and

\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{x}(t)
are defined in
Eq. (
5.1

Proof.

Instead of decomposing the residuals as in (
5.13
), we define

(5.28)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}_{v}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=-(\mathbb{I}_{\mathcal{V}}-\boldsymbol{M}_{\mathcal{V}}\mathbf{\Phi}_{v}\widehat{\boldsymbol{M}}_{\mathcal{V}}^{-1}\mathbf{\Phi}_{v}^{T}\mathcal{P}_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}})\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}},\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}},\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}})

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}_{v}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=-(\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{e},\boldsymbol{x})-\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}},\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}},\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}))

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}_{e}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=(\mathbb{I}_{\mathcal{E}}-\boldsymbol{M}_{\mathcal{E}}\mathbf{\Phi}_{e}\widehat{\boldsymbol{M}}_{\mathcal{E}}^{-1}\mathbf{\Phi}_{e}^{T}\mathcal{P}_{\mathsf{F}^{tv}})\mathsf{F}^{tv}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}},\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}},\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}})

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}_{e}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{F}^{tv}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{e},\boldsymbol{x})-\mathsf{F}^{tv}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}},\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}},\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}})

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}_{x}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=(\mathbb{I}_{\mathcal{V}}-\mathbf{\Phi}_{x}\mathbf{\Phi}_{x}^{T})\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}_{x}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{v}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}}.

Then (
5.14
) still holds true. By triangle inequality, we have

(5.29)

\displaystyle\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{v}(t)\right\rVert_{\boldsymbol{M}_{\mathcal{V}}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{v}(0)\right\rVert_{\boldsymbol{M}_{\mathcal{V}}}+\int_{0}^{t}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{L}_{\mathcal{V}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}_{v}^{(1)}(s)\right\rVert_{2}ds+\int_{0}^{t}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{L}_{\mathcal{V}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}_{v}^{(2)}(s)\right\rVert_{2}ds

\displaystyle\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{e}(t)\right\rVert_{\boldsymbol{M}_{\mathcal{E}}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{e}(0)\right\rVert_{\boldsymbol{M}_{\mathcal{E}}}+\int_{0}^{t}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{L}_{\mathcal{E}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}_{e}^{(1)}(s)\right\rVert_{2}ds+\int_{0}^{t}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{L}_{\mathcal{E}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}_{e}^{(2)}(s)\right\rVert_{2}ds

\displaystyle\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{x}(t)\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{x}(0)\right\rVert_{2}+\int_{0}^{t}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}_{x}^{(1)}(s)\right\rVert_{2}ds+\int_{0}^{t}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}_{x}^{(2)}(s)\right\rVert_{2}ds.

Using the fact that the norm of a symmetric and positive definite matrix is the
square of that of its Cholesky factor and its square root, and invoking the
assumptions (
5.6
), we have

(5.30)

\displaystyle\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{v}(t)\right\rVert_{\boldsymbol{M}_{\mathcal{V}}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{v}(0)\right\rVert_{\boldsymbol{M}_{\mathcal{V}}}+\left\lVert\boldsymbol{M}_{\mathcal{V}}^{-1/2}\right\rVert_{2}\left[\int_{0}^{t}\left\lVert(\mathbb{I}_{\mathcal{V}}-\boldsymbol{M}_{\mathcal{V}}\mathbf{\Phi}_{v}\widehat{\boldsymbol{M}}_{\mathcal{V}}^{-1}\mathbf{\Phi}_{v}^{T}\mathcal{P}_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}})\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}},\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}},\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}})\right\rVert_{2}+K_{1}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{v}(s),\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{e}(s),\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{x}(s))\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}ds\right]

\displaystyle\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{e}(t)\right\rVert_{\boldsymbol{M}_{\mathcal{E}}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{e}(0)\right\rVert_{\boldsymbol{M}_{\mathcal{E}}}+\left\lVert\boldsymbol{M}_{\mathcal{E}}^{-1/2}\right\rVert_{2}\left[\int_{0}^{t}\left\lVert(\mathbb{I}_{\mathcal{E}}-\boldsymbol{M}_{\mathcal{E}}\mathbf{\Phi}_{e}\widehat{\boldsymbol{M}}_{\mathcal{E}}^{-1}\mathbf{\Phi}_{e}^{T}\mathcal{P}_{\mathsf{F}^{tv}})\mathsf{F}^{tv}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}},\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}},\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}})\right\rVert_{2}+K_{2}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{v}(s),\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{e}(s),\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{x}(s))\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}ds\right]

\displaystyle\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{x}(t)\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{x}(0)\right\rVert_{2}+\int_{0}^{t}\left\lVert(\mathbb{I}_{\mathcal{V}}-\mathbf{\Phi}_{x}\mathbf{\Phi}_{x}^{T})\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\rVert_{2}+\left\lVert\boldsymbol{M}_{\mathcal{V}}^{-1/2}\right\rVert_{2}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{v}(s),\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{e}(s),\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{x}(s))\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}ds.

This implies

(5.31)

\displaystyle{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{v}(t),\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{e}(t),\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{x}(t))\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}

\displaystyle\left\lVert\boldsymbol{M}_{\mathcal{V}}^{-1/2}\right\rVert_{2}\int_{0}^{t}\left\lVert(\mathbb{I}_{\mathcal{V}}-\boldsymbol{M}_{\mathcal{V}}\mathbf{\Phi}_{v}\widehat{\boldsymbol{M}}_{\mathcal{V}}^{-1}\mathbf{\Phi}_{v}^{T}\mathcal{P}_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}})\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}},\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}},\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}})\right\rVert_{2}ds+

\displaystyle\left\lVert\boldsymbol{M}_{\mathcal{E}}^{-1/2}\right\rVert_{2}\int_{0}^{t}\left\lVert(\mathbb{I}_{\mathcal{E}}-\boldsymbol{M}_{\mathcal{E}}\mathbf{\Phi}_{e}\widehat{\boldsymbol{M}}_{\mathcal{E}}^{-1}\mathbf{\Phi}_{e}^{T}\mathcal{P}_{\mathsf{F}^{tv}})\mathsf{F}^{tv}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}},\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}},\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}})\right\rVert_{2}ds+

\displaystyle C_{2}\int_{0}^{t}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{v}(s),\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{e}(s),\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{x}(s)\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}ds\Big],

where

(5.32)

\displaystyle{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{v}(t),\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{e}(t),\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{x}(t))\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{3}\exp\left(C_{2}t\right)\Big[{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{v}(0),\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{e}(0),\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{x}(0))\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}+

\displaystyle\left\lVert\boldsymbol{M}_{\mathcal{V}}^{-1/2}\right\rVert_{2}\int_{0}^{t}\left\lVert(\mathbb{I}_{\mathcal{V}}-\boldsymbol{M}_{\mathcal{V}}\mathbf{\Phi}_{v}\widehat{\boldsymbol{M}}_{\mathcal{V}}^{-1}\mathbf{\Phi}_{v}^{T}\mathcal{P}_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}})\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}},\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}},\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}})\right\rVert_{2}ds+

\displaystyle\left\lVert\boldsymbol{M}_{\mathcal{E}}^{-1/2}\right\rVert_{2}\int_{0}^{t}\left\lVert(\mathbb{I}_{\mathcal{E}}-\boldsymbol{M}_{\mathcal{E}}\mathbf{\Phi}_{e}\widehat{\boldsymbol{M}}_{\mathcal{E}}^{-1}\mathbf{\Phi}_{e}^{T}\mathcal{P}_{\mathsf{F}^{tv}})\mathsf{F}^{tv}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}},\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}},\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}})\right\rVert_{2}ds\Big],

∎

5.3
Estimate for truncation error

Finally, we analyze the error between the continuous-in-time reduced order
approximation in (
3.17
) and the RK2-average fully discrete reduced
order approximation in (
3.22
). The error bound is controlled
by a combination of the mismatch of initial condition and the maximum time step
size.

Theorem 3
.

Assume
\boldsymbol{F}
is of class
C^{2}
on
\mathcal{V}\times\mathcal{E}\times\mathcal{V}
and the Lipchitz continuity conditions
(
5.6
) holds. In addition, assume there holds the following
Lipchitz continuity conditions for the Jacobian of the force matrix

\boldsymbol{F}
: there exists
K_{3},K_{4}>0
such that for any

\boldsymbol{w}=(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{e},\boldsymbol{x}),\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime}=(\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{e}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})\in\mathcal{V}\times\mathcal{E}\times\mathcal{V}
,

(5.33)

\displaystyle\left\lVert\boldsymbol{J}_{w}\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}(\boldsymbol{w})\tilde{\mathsf{F}}(\boldsymbol{w})-\boldsymbol{J}_{w}\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}(\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime})\tilde{\mathsf{F}}(\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime})\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq K_{3}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{e}-\boldsymbol{e}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}

\displaystyle\left\lVert\boldsymbol{J}_{w}\mathsf{F}^{tv}(\boldsymbol{w})\tilde{\mathsf{F}}(\boldsymbol{w})-\boldsymbol{J}_{w}\mathsf{F}^{tv}(\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime})\tilde{\mathsf{F}}(\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime})\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq K_{4}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{e}-\boldsymbol{e}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|},

where
\boldsymbol{J}_{w}\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}
and
\boldsymbol{J}_{w}\mathsf{F}^{tv}
are the Jacobian
matrix of the vector-valued functions
\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}(\boldsymbol{w})
and

\mathsf{F}^{tv}(\boldsymbol{w})
respectively, and
\tilde{\mathsf{F}}
denotes the lifted
Euler update

(5.34)

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathsf{F}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{w}})

\displaystyle\equiv\begin{pmatrix}-\mathbf{\Phi}_{v}\widehat{\boldsymbol{M}}_{\mathcal{V}}^{-1}\mathbf{\Phi}_{v}^{T}\mathcal{P}_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{w}})\\
\mathbf{\Phi}_{e}\widehat{\boldsymbol{M}}_{\mathcal{E}}^{-1}\mathbf{\Phi}_{e}^{T}\mathcal{P}_{\mathsf{F}^{tv}}\mathsf{F}^{tv}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{w}})\\
\mathbf{\Phi}_{x}\mathbf{\Phi}_{x}^{T}\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}}\end{pmatrix}.

Then there exists a constant
C>0
such that for
n\geq 0
, we have

(5.35)

\displaystyle{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\boldsymbol{\delta}_{v,n},\boldsymbol{\delta}_{e,n},\boldsymbol{\delta}_{x,n})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}\leq e^{Ct_{n}}({\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\boldsymbol{\delta}_{v,0},\boldsymbol{\delta}_{e,0},\boldsymbol{\delta}_{x,0})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}+C(\Delta t)^{2}),

where
\Delta t=\max_{0\leq j\leq\widetilde{N}_{t}-1}\Delta t_{j}
and
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{v,n}
,

\boldsymbol{\delta}_{e,n}
, and
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{x,n}

are defined in Eq. (
5.2
).

Proof.

First, we note that

(5.36)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\delta}_{v,n+1}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\delta}_{v,n}+\mathbf{\Phi}_{v}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}}(t_{n+1})-\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}}(t_{n}))-\mathbf{\Phi}_{v}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}}_{n+1}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}}_{n})

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\delta}_{e,n+1}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\delta}_{e,n}+\mathbf{\Phi}_{e}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{e}}(t_{n+1})-\widehat{\boldsymbol{e}}(t_{n}))-\mathbf{\Phi}_{e}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{e}}_{n+1}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{e}}_{n})

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\delta}_{x,n}+\mathbf{\Phi}_{x}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}(t_{n+1})-\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}(t_{n}))-\mathbf{\Phi}_{x}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n+1}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n}).

We will estimate the second term and the last term
on the right hand side of each equation in (
5.36
).
By Taylor’s remainder theorem, we have

(5.37)

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}}(t_{n+1})

\displaystyle=\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}}(t_{n})+\Delta t_{n}\frac{d\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}}}{dt}(t_{n})+\dfrac{(\Delta t_{n})^{2}}{2}\frac{d^{2}\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}}}{dt^{2}}(t_{n})+(\Delta t_{n})^{3}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}_{v}^{(1)}

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{e}}(t_{n+1})

\displaystyle=\widehat{\boldsymbol{e}}(t_{n})+\Delta t_{n}\frac{d\widehat{\boldsymbol{e}}}{dt}(t_{n})+\dfrac{(\Delta t_{n})^{2}}{2}\frac{d^{2}\widehat{\boldsymbol{e}}}{dt^{2}}(t_{n})+(\Delta t_{n})^{3}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}_{e}^{(1)}

6.1

In this subsection, we introduce the settings of several benchmark

6.1.1
Gresho vortex problem

The Gresho vortex problem is a two-dimensional standard benchmark test
for the incompressible inviscid Navier–Stokes equations
[
85
]
.
In this problem, we consider a manufactured smooth solution from extending the
steady state Gresho vortex solution to the compressible Euler equations. The
computational domain is the unit square
\widetilde{\Omega}=[-0.5,0.5]^{2}
with wall
boundary conditions on all surfaces, i.e.
v\cdot n=0
. Let
(r,\phi)
denote the polar coordinates of a particle

\widetilde{x}\in\widetilde{\Omega}
. The initial angular velocity is given by

(6.2)

v_{\phi}=\begin{cases}5r&\text{ for }0\leq r<0.2\\
2-5r&\text{ for }0.2\leq r<0.4\\
0&\text{ for }r\geq 0.4.\end{cases}

The initial density is given by
\rho=1
.
The initial thermodynamic pressure is given by

(6.3)

p=\begin{cases}5+\frac{25}{2}r^{2}&\text{ for }0\leq r<0.2\\
9-4\log(0.2)+\frac{25}{2}-20r+4\log(r)&\text{ for }0.2\leq r<0.4\\
3+4\log(2)&\text{ for }r\geq 0.4.\end{cases}

The initial energy is related to the pressure and the density by (
2.2
).
The adiabatic index in the ideal gas equations of state is set to be a constant

\gamma=5/3
. The initial mesh is a uniform Cartesian
hexahedral mesh, which deforms over time. No artificial viscosity stress is
added. We compute the resultant source terms for driving the time-dependent
simulation up to some point in time, and perform normed error analysis on the
final computational mesh. The visualized solution of the Gresho vortex problem
is given in the first column of Fig.
1
.

6.1.2
Sedov Blast problem

The Sedov blast problem is a three-dimensional standard shock hydrodynamic benchmark test

[
86
]

internal energy initially deposited at the origin of a three-dimensional cube,
analogous to the two-dimensional test in
[
71
]
, to which we
refer for further details. The computational domain is the unit cube

\widetilde{\Omega}=[0,1]^{3}
with wall boundary conditions on all surfaces, i.e.

v\cdot n=0
. The initial velocity is given by

v=0
. The initial density is given by
\rho=1
.

\gamma=1.4

6.1.3
Taylor–Green vortex problem

The Taylor–Green vortex problem is a three-dimensional standard flow benchmark test
for the incompressible inviscid Navier–Stokes equations
[
87
]
.
In this problem, we consider a manufactured smooth solution from extending the
steady state Taylor–Green vortex solution to the compressible Euler equations,
analogous to the two-dimensional test in
[
71
]
, to which we
refer for further details. The computational domain is the unit cube

\widetilde{\Omega}=[0,1]^{3}
with wall boundary conditions on all surfaces, i.e.

v\cdot n=0
. The initial velocity is given by

(6.4)

v=(\sin(\pi x)\cos(\pi y)\cos(\pi z),-\cos(\pi x)\sin(\pi y)\cos(\pi z),0).

The initial density is given by
\rho=1
.
The initial thermodynamic pressure is given by

(6.5)

p=100+\dfrac{(\cos(2\pi x)+\cos(2\pi y))(\cos(2\pi z)+2)-2}{16}.

The initial energy is related to the pressure and the density by (
2.2
).
The adiabatic index in the ideal gas equations of state is set to be a constant

\gamma=5/3
. The initial mesh is a uniform Cartesian
hexahedral mesh, which deforms over time. Artificial viscosity stress is added.
We compute the resultant source terms for driving the time-dependent simulation
up to some point in time, and perform normed error analysis on the final
computational mesh.
The visualized solution of the Taylor–Green vortex problem is given in the
third column of Fig.
1
.

6.1.4
Triple–point problem

The triple-point problem is a three-dimensional shock test with two materials in
three states
[
88
]
. In this problem, we consider a three-state,
two-material, 2D Riemann problem which generates vorticity, analogous to the
two-dimensional test in
[
71
]
, to which we refer for further
details. The computational domain is
\widetilde{\Omega}=[0,7]\times[0,3]\times[0,1.5]

v\cdot n=0
. The initial velocity is given by

v=0
. The initial density is given by

(6.6)

\rho=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if }x\leq 1\text{ or }y\leq 1.5,\\
1/8&\text{ if }x>1\text{ and }y>1.5.\end{cases}

The initial thermodynamic pressure is given by

(6.7)

p=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if }x\leq 1,\\
0.1&\text{ if }x>1.\end{cases}

The initial energy is related to the pressure and the density by (
2.2
).
The adiabatic index in the ideal gas equations of state is set to be

(6.8)

\gamma=\begin{cases}1.5&\text{ if }x\leq 1\text{ or }y>1.5,\\
1.4&\text{ if }x>1\text{ and }y\leq 1.5.\end{cases}

6.2
Short-time ROM simulation

In this section, we use the spatial ROM introduced in Section
3
to
perform short-time simulation in various benchmark problems. Only reproductive
cases are considered, i.e. the problem setting in the ROM is identical to that
in the FOM. First, in the offline phase, the fully discrete FOM scheme in
Section
2.2
is used to compute snapshot solution for
performing POD discussed in Section
3.5
. The reduced basis matrices
for the solution variables are then used to formulate the reduced order model
(
3.8
) in the online phase. In the case of hyper-reduction, we also
obtain the reduced basis matrices for the nonlinear terms using SVD or the SNS
relation, and then follow the DEIM procedure discussed in Section
3.7

to obtain sampling matrices and formulate the hyper-reduced system
(
3.17
). Using a time integrator again, the fully discrete
(hyper-)reduced order model is used to compute an approximate solution. The ROM
solution is compared with the FOM solution at the final time
t_{f}
with
respect to the relative errors (
6.1
). Also, the wall time of ROM
simulation is compared with that of the FOM simulation. We will study the
dependence of the accuracy and speed-up of ROM on the ratios of dimension
reduction

(6.9)

\zeta_{v}=\dfrac{n_{v}}{N_{\mathcal{V}}},\quad\zeta_{e}=\dfrac{n_{e}}{N_{\mathcal{E}}},\quad\zeta_{x}=\dfrac{n_{x}}{N_{\mathcal{V}}},\quad\zeta_{\breve{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}}=\dfrac{n_{\breve{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}}}{N_{\mathcal{V}}},\quad\zeta_{\breve{\mathsf{F}^{tv}}}=\dfrac{n_{\breve{\mathsf{F}^{tv}}}}{N_{\mathcal{E}}}.

On the other hand, for hyper-reduction, the numbers of sampling indices

\{n_{\breve{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}},n_{\breve{\mathsf{F}^{tv}}}\}
are controlled by the product of
the over-sampling factors
\{\lambda_{\breve{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}},\lambda_{\breve{\mathsf{F}^{tv}}}\}\in[1,\infty)

and the reduced basis dimensions
\{n_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}},n_{\mathsf{F}^{tv}}\}
, i.e.

(6.10)

n_{\breve{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}}=\min\left\{N_{\mathcal{V}},\,\lambda_{\breve{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}}n_{\mathsf{F}^{\mathfrak{1}}}\right\},\quad n_{\breve{\mathsf{F}^{tv}}}=\min\left\{N_{\mathcal{E}},\,\lambda_{\breve{\mathsf{F}^{tv}}}n_{\mathsf{F}^{tv}}\right\}.

The resultant reduced system of semi-discrete Lagrangian hydrodynamics equations
is numerically solved by the RK2-average scheme
introduced in Section
3.4
.

7
Conclusion

In this paper, we develop an efficient reduced order modeling approach for
Lagrangian hydrodynamics simulations. A time-windowing approach is introduced to
construct temporally-local ROM spaces which are small but accurate within a
short period in advection-dominated problems. Different techniques of window
division and construction of offset variables are discussed. Over-sampling
hyper-reduction on the nonlinear terms are introduced to ensure adequate
speed-up. Error bounds for the fully discrete reduced order model with adaptive
time-step control are developed using the continuous-in-time full order model
solution as the reference. The error bounds are controlled by several
quantities, including the oblique projection error of the solution and the
nonlinear terms onto reduced subspaces, the mismatch of initial condition, an
exponential factor of the time of evaluation, and the maximum time step size.
Numerical examples are shown to verify the capability of our method. For the
long-time simulation in benchmark advection-dominated problems, the time
windowing reduced order model successfully captures the extreme mesh distortion
exhibited in the Lagrangian full order model, and attains remarkable speed-up of
25 to 75 times and outstanding solution accuracy with the order of -9
to -4 in the reproductive cases. As suggested by the numerical results, the
subspace relation is useful for extracting quality nonlinear term bases and
improving the solution accuracy and overall speed-up. Various offset
approaches in the time windowing reduced order model are compared.
In the reproductive case, using the interpolation scheme is Pareto optimal
in the region of higher solution accuracy, while using the initial states is
Pareto optimal in the region of smaller relative clock time. In the predictive
cases, using the initial states provides uniformly improved accuracy
when compared with serial ROM and a speed-up of around 15 times at all the
tested parameter values.
