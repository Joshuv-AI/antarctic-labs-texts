Wigner–Weyl description of massless Dirac plasmas
José L. Figueiredo, João P. S. Bizarro, Hugo Terças

Abstract
We derive a quantum kinetic model describing the dynamics of graphene electrons in phase space based on the Wigner–Weyl formalism. To take into account the quantum nature of the carriers, we make use of the quantum Liouville equation for the density matrix. By relating the density matrix elements with the Wigner function, the equation of motion for the latter is established, with the Coulomb interaction being introduced self-consistently (i.e., in the Hartree approximation). The long-wavelength limit for the plasmon dispersion relation is obtained, for both ungated and gated situations. As an application, we derive the corresponding fluid equations from first principles and discuss the correct value of the effective hydrodynamic mass of the carriers. This constitutes a crucial point in establishing the appropriate fluid description of Dirac electrons, thus paving the way to a more comprehensive description of graphene plasmonics.



I
Introduction

In recent years, graphene has been extensively studied due to its outstanding optical, electronic and mechanical properties
Castro Neto et al. 2009
. In addition to its two-dimensional (2D) nature, the elementary electronic excitations are described by a Dirac-like dispersion in the low-energy limit
Wallace 1947
. The relativistic nature of graphene electrons, resulting from the cone-like dispersion relation near the Dirac points, makes it useful for transparent electronic devices, ultra-sensitive photodetectors, and other high-performance optoelectronic structures
Rodrigo et al. 2015
;
Chen et al. 2012
;
Zeng et al. 2015
. Furthermore, graphene possesses extremely high quantum efficiency for light-matter interactions
Koppens et al. 2011
. Also, the collective oscillations of the electron and hole densities lead to the formation of plasmons (or plasma waves)
Agarwal et al. 2018
. In fact, plasmonic of 2D materials, such as graphene, transition metal dichalcogenides (TMDCs), and hexagonal boron nitride (hBN)
Dai et al. 2014
;
Lin et al. 2009
, is nowadays a very prominent field of research
Li et al. 2017
. Graphene-based plasmonics finds a variety of applications, as the versatility of graphene enables the manufacture of optical devices working in different frequency ranges, namely in the terahertz (THz) and the infra-red domains
Grigorenko et al. 2012
. While metal plasmonics exhibit large Ohmic losses, which limits their applicability to optical processing devices, doped graphene emerges as an alternative. Its large conductivity, in part due to the zero-mass character of the carriers, encloses a wide range of potential applications, such as high-frequency nanoelectronics, nanomechanics, transparent electrodes, and composite materials
Geim and Novoselov 2007
. For this reason, the possibility of electric gating has been extensively studied in graphene, allowing for the manipulation of the Fermi level
Yang et al. 2016
. Recently, gating with a solid electrolyte allowed carrier concentrations as large as
\displaystyle 10^{14}\ \text{cm${}^{-2}$}
to be achieved, which results in a Fermi energy of
\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\text{F}}\simeq 1
eV, such that a modulation of optical transmission in the visible spectrum is possible
Wang et al. 2008
;
Laboratory et al. 2008
. The potentiality for THz emission, by making use of a graphene field-effect transistor (gFET) and by controlling the applied gate voltage and injected current, has also been recently pointed out as a possible application
Cosme and Terças 2020
.

From the theoretical point of view, a variety of techniques have been developed to establish the dynamics of Dirac fermions in graphene, ranging from semiclassical hydrodynamical models
Ryzhii et al. 2007
;
Bistritzer and MacDonald 2009
;
Svintsov et al. 2012
to quantum formulations that involve collective Green’s functions, such as the time-dependent Hartree-Fock approximation
Schwengelbeck et al. 2000
;
Jasiak et al. 2009
, or the time-dependent density functional theory
Teperik et al. 2013
. Whilst it is often the case that cumbersome equations, of very reduced utility, crop up when going for a complete quantum description, it is also true that the semiclassical approach, based on the Vlasov equation, is inadequate in the low temperature or high density regimes
Dugaev and Katsnelson 2013
.

In this work, we establish a kinetic formalism based on the Wigner–Weyl formulation of quantum mechanics to study a Dirac plasma in phase space
Wigner 1932
. We start by setting an equation of motion for the density matrix in the conduction and valence basis, derived from a microscopic tight-binding model for the low-energy electrons. Then, we derive a kinetic equation for the Wigner function components, which are related with the density matrix and incorporate the pseudo-spin degrees of freedom. Moreover, the interaction is introduced self-consistently via the Hartree approximation, which obeys the Poisson equation. Note that, despite previous works have already been focused on the Wigner representation for the case of graphene
Morandi and Schuerrer 2011
;
Zamponi and Barletti 2011
, our approach manages to treat the potential term as a perturbation, by making use of the conduction and valence eigen-states as the natural basis. By following this strategy, we are able to obtain the plasmon dispersion relation, recovering the usual result based on the random-phase approximation (RPA). By performing averages over the phase-space distributions (more precisely, by taking the moments of the evolution equation for the Wigner equation), hydrodynamical equations are obtained allowing for a fluid description of the Dirac particles in graphene. As a consequence, we are able to derive microscopically the effective mass of a fluid particle and relate it to Drude’s mass, thus contributing to the understanding of a still-open question in graphene hydrodynamics. We show as well that the classical limit appropriately recovers results that have been previously obtained based on the Vlasov equation for the classical distribution function.

So, the paper is organised as follows: in Sec.
II
, we review the basic properties of graphene carriers and corresponding low-energy Hamiltonian. In Sec.
III
, we derive the quantum kinetic equation based on the Wigner–Weyl approach for the Dirac plasma. In Sec.
IV
, we obtain the fluid equations, valid at the macroscopic scale, by taking the moments from the previously obtained kinetic equation, and discriminate its classical and quantum contributions. As a consequence, we put forward an expression for the hydrodynamical mass relating the fluid momentum and velocity fields. As an example, we correct the plasmon dispersion relation and show the emergence of a purely quantum contribution, not revealed by RPA calculations. Finally, in Sec.
V
, some conclusions are drawn and future perspectives are outlined.

\displaystyle s=\pm 1

\displaystyle(+1)

\displaystyle(-1)

\displaystyle\widehat{H}_{0}\ket{s}_{\bm{k}}=\mathcal{E}_{\bm{k}}^{s}\ket{s}_{\bm{k}}

\displaystyle\bra{\bm r}\ket{\bm k}=e^{i\bm{r}\cdot\bm{k}}/\sqrt{\mathcal{A}}

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}

\displaystyle\bra{\bm k s}\ket{\bm k' s'}=\delta_{\bm{k}\bm{k}^{\prime}}\delta_{ss^{\prime}}

\widehat{H}(t)=\widehat{H}_{0}+\widehat{V}(t)\otimes\mathbb{1},

(5)

where
\displaystyle\widehat{V}(t)

\displaystyle V(\bm{r},t)\doteq\bra{\bm r}\widehat{V}(t)\ket{\bm r}

\bm{\nabla}^{2}V=-\frac{e^{2}}{\varepsilon}n,

\displaystyle n

\displaystyle e

\displaystyle\varepsilon

\widehat{V}(t)=\frac{e^{2}}{4\pi\varepsilon}\int d\bm{r}^{\prime}\ \frac{n(\bm{r}^{\prime},t)}{\absolutevalue{\widehat{\bm r}-\bm r'}},

(7)

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{r}}

\displaystyle\widehat{V}(t)

\displaystyle r_{\text{s}}\sim\alpha_{\text{s}}/\varepsilon_{\text{r}}

\displaystyle\alpha_{\text{s}}=e^{2}/(4\pi\varepsilon_{0}\hbar v_{\text{F}})\simeq 2.2

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{0}
the vacuum permittivity and
\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\text{r}}=\varepsilon/\varepsilon_{0}
. As a result, the Hartree approximation remains reliable as long as
\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\text{r}}\gtrsim 2.2

\displaystyle\widehat{\rho}(t)

\frac{\partial\widehat{\rho}}{\partial t}+\frac{i}{\hbar}[\widehat{H},\widehat{\rho}]=\mathcal{S}\{\widehat{\rho}\},

(8)

(9)

and we recast Eq. (

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\rho_{\bm{k}\bm{k}^{\prime}}^{ss^{\prime}}=

\displaystyle\displaystyle-\frac{i}{\hbar}(\mathcal{E}_{\bm{k}}^{s}-\mathcal{E}_{\bm{k}^{\prime}}^{s^{\prime}})\rho_{\bm{k}\bm{k}^{\prime}}^{ss^{\prime}}-\frac{i}{\hbar}\sum_{\bm{k}^{\prime\prime}s^{\prime\prime}}\Big(V_{\bm{k}\bm{k}^{\prime\prime}}^{ss^{\prime\prime}}\rho_{\bm{k}^{\prime\prime}\bm{k}^{\prime}}^{s^{\prime\prime}s^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle-\rho_{\bm{k}\bm{k}^{\prime\prime}}^{ss^{\prime\prime}}V_{\bm{k}^{\prime\prime}\bm{k}^{\prime}}^{s^{\prime\prime}s^{\prime}}\Big)+\mathcal{S}_{\bm{k}\bm{k}^{\prime}}^{ss^{\prime}},

(10)

where we have set
\displaystyle O_{\bm{k}\bm{k}^{\prime}}^{ss^{\prime}}\doteq\bra{\bm k s}{\widehat{O}(t)}\ket{\bm k' s'}
.

III
Wigner–Weyl formalism

A very handy way to treat the electronic system of Eq (
10
) is to use the Wigner–Weyl picture of quantum mechanics
Wigner 1932
;
Weyl 1927
, which allows for a fully phase-space description, in close analogy with the classical case. In the classical limit, the Wigner function denotes the probability density of finding a particle in a given infinitesimal phase-space volume
\displaystyle d\bm{r}d\bm{k}
centred in
\displaystyle(\bm{r},\bm{k})
. In the quantum case, due to the commutation relation between
\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{r}}
and
\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{k}}
, the Heisenberg uncertainty principle prevents particles to localise in a specific phase-space point, and a proper distribution function (i.e., non-negative everywhere) is not possible
Moyal 1949
. However, we can still construct the much renowned Wigner function
\displaystyle W(\bm{r},\bm{k},t)
, whose properties are similar to those of a classical distribution function, i.e., it is a function of both a spatial coordinate
\displaystyle\bm{r}
and a wave-vector
\displaystyle\bm{k}
. Although
\displaystyle\bm{r}
and
\displaystyle\bm{k}
are classical-like variables and not conjugate quantum mechanical operators, they both give information about the spatial and momentum distributions of the system, which is described by a wave function
\displaystyle\psi(\bm{r},t)
. Consequently, we may have
\displaystyle W(\bm{r},\bm{k},t)<0
, with the phase-space regions where it takes negative values being purely quantum and having no classical analogue. For this reason, the Wigner function is often referred to as a quasiprobability function.

The mathematical definition of the Wigner function is given by the Weyl transform of the density operator
\displaystyle\widehat{\rho}(t)

Weyl 1927
,

W(\bm{r},\bm{k},t)=\int d\bm{s}\ e^{i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{s}}\bra{\bm r - \bm s/2}\widehat{\rho}(t)\ket{\bm r + \bm s/2}.

(11)

The expectation value of any operator can be computed by integrating its Weyl transform multiplied by
\displaystyle W(\bm{r},\bm{k},t)
over phase space, very much like in the classical case
Hillery et al. 1984
. Introducing the completeness relation for Dirac states
\displaystyle\ket{\bm k s}
, the Wigner function becomes

W(\bm{r},\bm{k},t)=\sum_{\bm{q}ss^{\prime}}\ e^{i\bm{q}\cdot\bm{r}}\ v^{ss^{\prime}}_{\bm{k}-\frac{\bm{q}}{2},\bm{k}+\frac{\bm{q}}{2}}\ \rho_{\bm{k}+\frac{\bm{q}}{2},\bm{k}-\frac{\bm{q}}{2}}^{ss^{\prime}}(t),

(12)

from which we immediately get the Fourier transform

W(\bm{q},\bm{k},t)=\mathcal{A}\sum_{ss^{\prime}}\ v^{ss^{\prime}}_{\bm{k}-\frac{\bm{q}}{2},\bm{k}+\frac{\bm{q}}{2}}\ \rho_{\bm{k}+\frac{\bm{q}}{2},\bm{k}-\frac{\bm{q}}{2}}^{ss^{\prime}}(t).

(13)

We stress the fact that
\displaystyle W(\bm{q},\bm{k},t)
carries the dynamical information contained in the matrix elements of
\displaystyle\widehat{\rho}
, plus the lattice information contained in the overlap of the pseudo-spinors
\displaystyle v^{ss^{\prime}}
. In the case of pure plane waves (e.g., a single-band massive plasma,
\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\bm{k}}\sim k^{2}
), the lattice information would be absent. By defining the matrix element
\displaystyle W^{ss^{\prime}\!}(\bm{q},\bm{k},t)=\mathcal{A}v^{ss^{\prime}}_{\bm{k}-\frac{\bm{q}}{2},\bm{k}+\frac{\bm{q}}{2}}\ \rho_{\bm{k}+\frac{\bm{q}}{2},\bm{k}-\frac{\bm{q}}{2}}^{ss^{\prime}}(t)
, we have

W(\bm{q},\bm{k},t)=\sum_{ss^{\prime}}W^{ss^{\prime}\!}(\bm{q},\bm{k},t).

(14)

When written in this manner, it becomes evident that the Wigner function correctly incorporates the dynamics of each band plus the contribution of the mixing terms, thus accounting for intra- and inter-band excitations. It is indeed known that the introduction of the spin (or pseudo-spin) makes us move to a tensorial formulation
Groot et al. 1974
.

One can show that the total density, as defined in Eq. (
9
), has a simple form in terms of the Wigner function, namely

\displaystyle\displaystyle n(\bm{r},t)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathcal{A}}\sum_{\bm{k}}W(\bm{r},\bm{k},t)=\sum_{ss^{\prime}}n^{ss^{\prime}\!}(\bm{r},t),

(15)

where
\displaystyle n^{ss^{\prime}\!}(\bm{r},t)\doteq\mathcal{A}^{-1}\sum_{\bm{k}}W^{ss^{\prime}\!}(\bm{r},\bm{k},t)
. As discussed before, the diagonal elements correspond to the density of electrons in the conduction
\displaystyle(s=+1)
and valence
\displaystyle(s=-1)
bands, whereas the off-diagonal elements represent the coherence between both populations, i.e., the fraction of the electronic population that is constantly changing between the conduction and valence bands.

Next, we derive an equation of motion for the Wigner elements in momentum space. Upon time differentiating
\displaystyle W^{ss^{\prime}}
and using Eq. (
10

(16)

where
\displaystyle\Delta\mathcal{E}^{ss^{\prime}}_{\bm{q}\bm{k}}\doteq\mathcal{E}_{\bm{k}+\frac{\bm{q}}{2}}^{s}-\mathcal{E}_{\bm{k}-\frac{\bm{q}}{2}}^{s^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}\{W^{ss^{\prime}}\}
is defined as

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{C}\{W^{ss^{\prime}}\}={\sum}_{s^{\prime\prime}}\ \Bigg[

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{v^{ss^{\prime\prime}}_{\bm{k}+\frac{\bm{q}}{2},\bm{k}+\frac{\bm{q}}{2}-\bm{q}^{\prime}}}{v^{s^{\prime\prime}s^{\prime}}_{\bm{k}-\frac{\bm{q}}{2},\bm{k}+\frac{\bm{q}}{2}-\bm{q}^{\prime}}}W^{s^{\prime}s^{\prime\prime}}(\bm{q}\!-\!\bm{q}^{\prime},\bm{k}\!-\!\bm{q}^{\prime}/2,t)-\frac{v^{s^{\prime}s^{\prime\prime}}_{\bm{k}-\frac{\bm{q}}{2}+\bm{q}^{\prime},\bm{k}-\frac{\bm{q}}{2}}}{v^{s^{\prime\prime}s}_{\bm{k}-\frac{\bm{q}}{2}+\bm{q}^{\prime},\bm{k}+\frac{\bm{q}}{2}}}W^{ss^{\prime\prime}}(\bm{q}\!-\!\bm{q}^{\prime},\bm{k}\!+\!\bm{q}^{\prime}/2,t)\Bigg]

(17)

V_{\bm{k}\bm{k}^{\prime}}^{ss^{\prime}}(t)=\frac{1}{\mathcal{A}}v^{ss^{\prime}}_{\bm{k}\bm{k}^{\prime}}V(\bm{k}-\bm{k}^{\prime},t),

(18)

with
\displaystyle V(\bm{k},t)
the Fourier transform of
\displaystyle V(\bm{r},t)
. By Fourier transforming Eq. (
7

\displaystyle V(\bm{k},t)=\mathcal{U}(\bm{k})n(\bm{k},t)

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}(\bm{k})=e^{2}/(2\varepsilon k)
accounting for the (2D) time-independent Coulomb potential.

We now consider small perturbations around an equilibrium configuration, keeping the lowest order contributions to the Wigner components. As such, we expand the density matrix as
\displaystyle\widehat{\rho}(t)=g_{s}g_{v}f(\widehat{H}_{0})+\widehat{\delta\rho}(t)
, where
\displaystyle f(\mathcal{E})=[e^{\beta(\mathcal{E}-\mu)}+1]^{-1}
is the Fermi–Dirac distribution,
\displaystyle k_{\rm B}
the Boltzmann constant,
\displaystyle T
the absolute temperature,
\displaystyle\mu
the chemical potential and
\displaystyle\beta=(k_{\rm B}T)^{-1}
. Besides,
\displaystyle g_{s}g_{v}
accounts for the spin (
\displaystyle g_{s}=2
) and valley (
\displaystyle g_{v}=2
) degeneracy. The former results from the degeneracy of the spin populations in each energy band, which we have neglected in our treatment so far, and the latter should be incorporated to consistently include the two minima in the first Brillouin zone (BZ)
Das Sarma et al. 2009
. The perturbation
\displaystyle\widehat{\delta\rho}(t)
is assumed to be small,
\displaystyle\widehat{\delta\rho}(t)\sim\order{r_\text{s}}
, such that higher powers in the coupling parameter are discarded. Consequently, the Wigner elements give

W^{ss^{\prime}}(\bm{q},\bm{k},t)\simeq g_{s}g_{v}f(\mathcal{E}_{\bm{k}}^{s})\delta(\bm{q})\delta_{ss^{\prime}}+\delta W^{ss^{\prime}}(\bm{q},\bm{k},t),

(19)

with
\displaystyle\delta W^{ss^{\prime}}(\bm{q},\bm{k},t)
a small perturbation. Similarly, the density and potential are perturbed as

\displaystyle\displaystyle n(\bm{q},t)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\simeq n_{0}\delta(\bm{q})+\delta n(\bm{q},t),

(20)

\displaystyle\displaystyle V(\bm{q},t)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\simeq\mathcal{U}(\bm{q})\left[n_{0}\delta(\bm{q})+\delta n(\bm{q},t)\right],

(21)

where
\displaystyle n_{0}=g_{s}g_{v}{\mathcal{A}}^{-1}\sum_{\bm{k}s}f(\mathcal{E}_{\bm{k}}^{s})
is the equilibrium density and
\displaystyle\delta n(\bm{q},t)
a small perturbation verifying
\displaystyle|\delta n(\bm{q},t)|\ll 1
. Introducing Eqs. (
19
)-(
21

16
), and Fourier transforming the latter in the time domain, yields

\displaystyle\displaystyle\delta W^{ss^{\prime}}(\bm{q},\bm{k},\omega)=\ g_{s}g_{v}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{U}(\bm{q})\delta n(\bm{q},\omega)\mathcal{F}_{\bm{k}+\frac{\bm{q}}{2},\bm{k}-\frac{\bm{q}}{2}}^{ss^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\times\frac{f(\mathcal{E}_{\bm{k}-\frac{\bm{q}}{2}}^{s^{\prime}})-f(\mathcal{E}_{\bm{k}+\frac{\bm{q}}{2}}^{s})}{\hbar\omega-\mathcal{E}_{\bm{k}+\frac{\bm{q}}{2}}^{s}+\mathcal{E}_{\bm{k}-\frac{\bm{q}}{2}}^{s^{\prime}}},

(22)

where
\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\bm{k},\bm{k}^{\prime}}^{ss^{\prime}}\doteq|v_{\bm{k},\bm{k}^{\prime}}^{ss^{\prime}}|^{2}
is the chirality factor, and a second-order term has been neglected, as well as the collision term. Equation (

and reproduces the features contained in the RPA for the collisionless limit
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\bm{k}\bm{k}^{\prime}}^{ss^{\prime}}\rightarrow 0

Das Sarma and Hwang 2009
;
Wunsch et al. 2006a
. This formalism is specially advantageous to describe the dynamics of electrons that are far from equilibrium, such as the case of plasma instabilities, with the configuration being solely defined by the equilibrium distributions. Upon summing both sides of Eq. (

\displaystyle\bm{k}
,
\displaystyle s
and
\displaystyle s^{\prime}
, we find the plasmon dispersion to be given by

\epsilon(\bm{q},\omega)=0,

(23)

with
\displaystyle\epsilon(\bm{q},\omega)=1+\mathcal{U}(\bm{q})\Pi(\bm{q},\omega)
the dielectric function and
\displaystyle\Pi(\bm{q},\omega)
the polarizability,

\Pi(\bm{q},\omega)=\frac{g_{s}g_{v}}{\mathcal{A}}{\sum}_{\bm{k}ss^{\prime}}\mathcal{F}^{ss^{\prime}}_{\bm{k},\bm{k}+\bm{q}}\ \frac{f(\mathcal{E}_{\bm{k}+\bm{q}}^{s^{\prime}})-f(\mathcal{E}_{\bm{k}}^{s})}{\hbar\omega+\mathcal{E}_{\bm{k}}^{s}-\mathcal{E}_{\bm{k}+\bm{q}}^{s^{\prime}}}.

The formal result of Eq. (

\displaystyle r_{\text{s}}\rightarrow 0

In what follows, we consider the case of negatively doped graphene, with the conduction band filled up to the Fermi level
\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\text{F}}=\hbar v_{\rm{F}}k_{\rm{F}}\simeq\mu\gg 0
. The Fermi level defines the Fermi wave-number
\displaystyle k_{\text{F}}
, and the latter is related with the doping density
\displaystyle n_{0}
by

k_{\text{F}}=\sqrt{\frac{4\pi n_{0}}{g_{s}g_{v}}},

(25)

Typical experimental values of
\displaystyle n_{0}
in the range
\displaystyle 10^{9}-5\times 10^{12}~\text{cm}^{-2}
are achievable in graphene
Adam et al. 2007
. In the case
\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\text{F}}\gg k_{\rm B}T
, the presence of holes (i.e., vacancies of valence electrons) is negligible, which allows us to set
\displaystyle f(\mathcal{E}_{\bm{k}+\bm{q}}^{-})-f(\mathcal{E}_{\bm{k}}^{-})=0

IV
Fluid model

One of the major advantages of the present description is the possibility of computing the average values of operators, which are naturally given in terms of the Wigner function. Their time evolution can be computed using Eq. (
31
), and a set of coupled equations can be put forward. As an example, we calculate the average momentum density

\overline{\bm{\mathcal{P}}}(\bm{r},t)=\tr[\widehat{\bm{\mathcal{P}}}\widehat{\rho}(t)\big],

(38)

where

\widehat{\bm{\mathcal{P}}}=\frac{1}{2}\anticommutator{\widehat{\bm p}}{\delta(\bm r - \bm{\widehat r})},

(39)

is the momentum density operator,
\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{p}}=\hbar\widehat{\bm{k}}
, and
\displaystyle\{,\}
denotes symmetrisation. It follows that

\overline{\bm{\mathcal{P}}}(\bm{r},t)=\frac{1}{\mathcal{A}}\sum_{\bm{k}ss^{\prime}}\hbar\bm{k}W^{ss^{\prime}}(\bm{r},\bm{k},t)\doteq\sum_{ss^{\prime}}\overline{\bm{\mathcal{P}}}^{ss^{\prime}\!\!}(\bm{r},t),

(40)

with the tensor
\displaystyle\overline{\bm{\mathcal{P}}}^{ss^{\prime}\!\!}
containing both the contribution of the particles in each band (
\displaystyle s=s^{\prime}
) and of the particles which are in mixing states
\displaystyle(s\neq s^{\prime}
). It is natural to define the average momentum
\displaystyle\overline{\bm{p}}^{ss^{\prime}}
through the relation
\displaystyle n^{ss^{\prime}}\overline{\bm{p}}^{ss^{\prime}\!\!}=\overline{\bm{\mathcal{P}}}^{ss^{\prime}\!\!}
, thus setting the relevant macroscopic variables to be

(41)

and

\overline{\bm{p}}^{ss^{\prime}}(\bm{r},t)=\frac{1}{n^{ss^{\prime}}}\int\frac{d\bm{k}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\ \hbar\bm{k}\ W^{ss^{\prime}\!}(\bm{r},\bm{k},t),

(42)

after replacing the sums with integrals.

Henceforth, and in order to simplify our analysis, we neglect the off-diagonal contributions, in as much as we are interested in the semiclassical limit of negatively doped graphene, for which case they play a minimal role. We are, thus, allowed to drop the double index to facilitate the notation (as, e.g.,
\displaystyle W^{s}\doteq W^{ss}
). The time evolution equations for the diagonal quantities in Eqs. (

IV.1
Calculation of the macroscopic variables

Having set the relevant transport equations, we move now to a more detailed discussion concerning the averaged momentum and velocity fields. Being interested in the case of full valence band and doped conduction band (
\displaystyle\mu\gg k_{\rm B}T
), we concentrate on the conduction electrons only, thus dropping the band index (
\displaystyle s=1
). As one can readily observe by comparing Eqs. (
42
) and (
45
), a proportionality relation of the form
\displaystyle\overline{\bm{p}}=m\overline{\bm{v}}
does not hold for massless particles, with a constant
\displaystyle m

Chaves et al. 2017
. However, by allowing a space and time dependence on the mass, we are able to define an effective “mass tensor”,
\displaystyle m_{ij}\doteq m_{ij}(\bm{r},t)
, as

\overline{p}_{i}=\sum_{j}m_{ij}\overline{v}_{j},

(53)

The meaning of such field should be clear: although the carriers have no mass, since they are Dirac particles, the fluid velocity and momentum fields can be related via an effective mass, providing a measure of the inertia of a fluid element. A tensorial structure such as that of Eq. (
53
) is required since, at a first glance, we should anticipate the components of
\displaystyle\overline{\bm{p}}
in a given direction
\displaystyle i
to be given by a linear combination of the velocity components in each direction
\displaystyle j
, with coefficients
\displaystyle m_{ij}
. Nevertheless, if one is dealing with a rotationally invariant system, the mass tensor becomes diagonal,
\displaystyle m_{ij}=\delta_{ij}m
.

Let us now modify the equilibrium used in the last section in order to accommodate for a (possibly local) average velocity of the electronic fluid, namely,

W(\bm{r},\bm{k},t)=\frac{g_{s}g_{v}}{1+e^{\beta(\mathcal{E}_{\bm{k}}-\hbar\bm{k}\cdot\bm{u}-\mu)}},

(54)

where
\displaystyle\mu\doteq\mu(\bm{r},t)
,
\displaystyle\bm{u}\doteq\bm{u}(\bm{r},t)
and
\displaystyle\beta\doteq 1/[k_{\rm B}T(\bm{r},t)]
are local variables to be calculated by imposing the conservation of number of particles, momentum and energy, respectively
Atwal and Ashcroft 2002
. Additionally, this particular choice for the Wigner function cancels the collision term on the Wigner equation
Svintsov et al. 2013
;
Svintsov 2018
. Computations with Eq. (
54
) are valid under the assumptions that the macroscopic quantities are slow-varying in both space and time, which is the natural requirement to go from a microscopic field theory to a macroscopic fluid model. The fluid description is valid if changes in the macroscopic quantities take place on large spacial and temporal scales, i.e., if
\displaystyle q\ll k_{\text{F}}
and
\displaystyle\omega\ll\omega_{p}
, respectively. This requirement is fulfilled if the characteristic time of the kinematic processes,
\displaystyle t\sim 1/\omega
, is much longer than the inverse collision frequency,
\displaystyle 1/\nu_{c}
, and the typical length
\displaystyle L
is much greater than the mean free path
\displaystyle l\sim v_{\text{F}}/\nu_{c}
, so that the plasma can be regarded locally as in a quasi-equilibrium configuration. However, due to the quantum nature of the model, we were able to capture the relativistic Dirac structure in a rigorous way. Actually, in the context of 2D quantum plasmas achieved in semiconductor structures, the De Broglie wavelength is replaced by the Thomas-Fermi screening length
\displaystyle\lambda_{\rm TF}=2\pi/(g_{s}g_{v}r_{\text{s}}k_{\text{F}})
. This is the analogue of the classical Debye length in plasmas, and differs from the Fermi wavelength,
\displaystyle\lambda_{\text{F}}=2\pi/k_{\text{F}}
(
\displaystyle\sim 10~{\rm nm}-10\mu{\rm m}
for typical graphene parameters). Consequently, the fluid limit is expected to be valid provided the conditions

(55)

\displaystyle\lambda_{\text{F}}/\lambda_{\rm TF}\simeq 8.8/\varepsilon_{\text{r}}

\displaystyle k_{\text{F}}
are found between
\displaystyle 10^{3}
and
\displaystyle 10^{6}

\displaystyle\text{cm}^{-1}

V
Conclusions and outlook

Using the Wigner–Weyl description, we have derived a quantum fluid model for a Dirac plasma in single-layer graphene. The massless nature of the quasi-particles was captured by the low-energy limit of the many-body Hamiltonian, which served as the basis for the construction of the Wigner matrix. Then, the equation of motion for the Wigner matrix elements in phase space was established, with the interacting potential being given by the Hartree approximation. The latter turns out to be an excellent approximation for graphene, as the coupling constant is very small,
\displaystyle r_{s}\sim\alpha_{s}/\varepsilon_{\text{r}}\ll 1
, and independent of the electron density. Additionally, a closed set of generalized quantum fluid equations was derived by taking the moments of the quantum kinetic equation. We found an infinite number of
\displaystyle\hbar
-dependent terms arising in both the continuity and force equations as a consequence of the linear dispersion relation of the carriers, and that introduced corrections to the current density and pressure. As it is shown, in the conventional case of parabolic systems (i.e. for which the single-particle dispersion relation is
\displaystyle\sim p^{2}
), these contributions vanish. To the best of our knowledge, such corrections have not been discussed so far in the literature. By neglecting the quantum corrections, our model reduces to existing semiclassical models for Dirac fermions
Svintsov et al. 2012
;
Ryzhii et al. 2007
. Moreover, a nonlinear relation between the fluid velocity and momentum fields has been put forward in order to construct a hydrodynamical mass. For small fluid velocity, we found a relation of the form
\displaystyle\overline{\bm{p}}=\gamma\mathcal{M}\overline{\bm{v}}
, where
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}
is Drude’s mass and
\displaystyle\gamma\simeq(1+\overline{v}^{2}/v^{2}_{\rm F})
is a local Lorentz-like factor. We stress the fact that a quantitative description for the hydrodynamical mass is a major issue in the current research, therefore making our findings on the hydrodynamical mass an important result of the current kinetic description. We have also computed higher-order corrections to the plasmon dispersion relation (
\displaystyle\sim q^{4}
) by taking into account the first quantum corrections to the fluid equations.

We expect our formalism to be particularly suited to describe out-of-equilibrium quantum dynamics of Dirac electrons in a more systematic manner. While the linear response can be indeed described within the framework of the RPA (which we recover upon linearization of the equations for the Wigner function), the nonlinear regimes deserve a more complete description, which are captured by the kinetic equation that we have proposed here. This is crucial in the description of saturation in the late stages of dynamical instabilities, for example. Moreover, (quantum) diffusive processes may be captured by adapting the strategy of quasilinear theories accounting for particle-wave interactions
Nicholson 1983
; by working out the collision integral at different levels of approximation, we may obtain quantum versions of the Fokker-Planck equation for relativistic-like particles. Another interesting aspect of our work is the contribution to the hydrodynamical modeling of graphene plasmas. With the
ab initio
establishment of a hydrodynamical mass, we are able to simulate experimentally relevant configurations, such as hydrodynamical instabilities in field-effect transistor THz emitters
Cosme and Terças 2020
. Moreover, at a phenomenological level, our hydrodynamical model is particularly suited to accommodate both shear and odd (or Hall) viscosity terms. In the presence of strong magnetic fields, the Chapman-Enskog approach to hydrodynamics yields a term in the fluid equations of the form
\displaystyle\sim\nu_{o}\nabla^{2}\epsilon_{ij}v_{j}
, where
\displaystyle\nu_{o}
is some function of the magnetic field and temperature and
\displaystyle\epsilon_{ij}
is the Levi-Civita symbol
Avron 1998
. The appearance of
\displaystyle\epsilon_{ij}
term makes the viscosity tensor off-diagonal and, thus, dissipation free, contrary to what happens in the case of even viscosity. Odd viscosity has been identified to play a special role in the topology of acoustic waves
Souslov et al. 2019
, and we hope that plasmons may also feature such topological transitions, opening the venue for a range of applications.
