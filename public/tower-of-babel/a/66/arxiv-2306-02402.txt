Abstract
. We study the SK model at inverse temperature β > 0 {\beta}>0 and strictly positive field h > 0 h>0 in the region of ( β , h ) ({\beta},h) where the replica-symmetric formula is valid. An integral representation of the partition function derived from the Hubbard-Stratonovitch transformation combined with a duality formula is used to prove that the infinite volume free energy of the SK model can be expressed as a variational formula on the space of magnetisations, m m . The resulting free energy functional differs from that of Thouless, Anderson and Palmer (TAP) by the term − β 2 4 ​ ( q − q EA ​ ( m ) ) 2 -\frac{{\beta}^{2}}{4}\left(q-q_{\text{EA}}(m)\right)^{2} where q EA ​ ( m ) q_{\text{EA}}(m) is the Edwards-Anderson parameter and q q is the minimiser of the replica-symmetric formula. Thus, both functionals have the same critical points and take the same value on the subspace of magnetisations satisfying q EA ​ ( m ) = q q_{\text{EA}}(m)=q . This result is based on an in-depth study of the global maximum of this near-TAP free energy functional using Bolthausen’s solutions of the TAP equations, Bandeira & van Handel’s bounds on the spectral norm of non-homogeneous Wigner-type random matrices, and Gaussian comparison techniques. It holds for ( β , h ) ({\beta},h) in a large subregion of the de Almeida and Thouless high-temperature stability region.

1.4.
Comments on stability

The issue of stability within the TAP approach has been extensively addressed in the physics literature
[
16
]
,
[
39
]
,
[
42
]
,
[
43
]
,
[
44
]
,
[
5
]
. From these works, condition (
1.14
) emerges as the main stability condition. It is also believed to coincide with the AT-condition (
1.9
). Eq. (
1.14
) was originally derived in two different ways, as a convergence condition for Plefka’s expansion
[
42
]
and as a divergence condition for the spin glass susceptibility in
[
16
]
. Both conditions ultimately reduce to conditions on the eigenvalues of the Hessian matrix of
F_{N,{\beta},h}^{TAP}
, and are formulated as conditions on the
empirical spectral measure
of the Hessian. We will not question the validity of these approaches and results here (see
[
32
]
for recent, partial but rigorous results). We simply ask what would become of these results if we replaced
F_{N,{\beta},h}^{TAP}
by
F_{N,{\beta},h}^{HT}
.

By (
1.15
), the Hessians of
NF_{N,{\beta},h}^{HT}
and
NF_{N,{\beta},h}^{TAP}
, denoted by
{\mathcal{H}}^{HT}
and
{\mathcal{H}}^{TAP}
respectively, have spectral norm of order one as
N\rightarrow\infty
. Moreover, on the subspace of magnetisations satisfying
q_{\text{EA}}(m)=q
(to which the iterative solution
m^{(k)}
asymptotically belongs if the AT-condition (
1.9
) is satisfied),
these Hessians differ by a rank-one projector of non-null eigenvalue
q{\beta}^{2}
. It is known that the
extreme eigenvalues
of certain Hermitian random matrices, such as Wigner matrices, can be strongly influenced by rank-one deformations, i.e., they can be detached from the spectrum for sufficiently large deformations (see, e.g., the survey paper
[
17
]
). Since in the present case the deformation is proportional to
q{\beta}^{2}
, this effect is expected to be present at sufficiently low temperatures. The case of spectral measures is completely different. As a direct consequence of the so-called rank inequalities (see
[
6
]
, Appendix A.6, and again
[
17
]
), the limiting behaviour of the spectral measure of a Hermitian random (or non-random) matrix is not modified by a finite-rank deformation. Accordingly,
{\mathcal{H}}^{HT}
and
{\mathcal{H}}^{TAP}
have the same limiting spectral measure, and will thus give the same condition (
1.14
) in the limit
N\rightarrow\infty
.

The proof of Theorem
1.4
described in Section
1.3
is also a stability analysis. It differs from the approaches of
[
16
]
and
[
42
]
in that the functional
F_{N,{\beta},h}^{HT}
is not examined in a single
m
, but globally over the whole hypercube. Indeed, as in the Laplace method for approximating integrals, we need to establish that the solution
m^{(k)}
of the iterative scheme (1.22)-(1.23) is (a good ansatz for) the global maximum of
F_{N,{\beta},h}^{HT}
. To do this, it is not enough to know the nature of the Hessian
{\mathcal{H}}^{HT}
at just this point. This global control is achieved at the expense of the precision of the constants in (
1.18
), i.e., in (
1.38
). We also note that our analysis, via Theorem 1.6, centers on the largest eigenvalue of
{\mathcal{H}}^{HT}
, not on the spectral measure as in
[
16
]
,
[
42
]
.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let

and

be two sequences of random variables depending on a parameter

. Assume that

in the sense of (

), namely, with a constant

depending a priori on

but not on

. Then, there exists a subset

with
{\mathbb{P}}\left({\Omega}^{*}\right)=1
, that does not depend on

and such that on

, the following holds: for all

\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\left|X^{(k)}_{N}-Y^{(k)}_{N}\right|=0,

and

(3.26)

The limits in (

) are iterated, as opposed to joint. If the constant

depends on other parameters (e.g.,

,

also depends on these parameters. The lemma

holds in the case of sequences

and

in

, with

.

Proof of Lemma
3.5
.

Given
\varepsilon>0
, define the collections of sets

\begin{split}{\Omega}^{(k)}_{N}(\varepsilon)&=\left\{{\omega}\in{\Omega}\,:\,\left|X^{(k)}_{N}({\omega})-Y^{(k)}_{N}({\omega})\right|\leq\varepsilon\right\},\quad\forall N\geq 1,k\geq 1,\\

{\Omega}^{(k)}_{0}&=\cap_{\varepsilon>0}{\Omega}^{(k)}(\varepsilon).\end{split}

(3.27)

Further introduce the quantity

{\delta}_{k,N}^{2}\equiv\frac{2C(k)}{N}\left(\log\bigl(k\sqrt{|\log C(k)|}\bigr)+\log N\right)

(3.28)

and note that by (
3.17
) (with

),

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\Bigl({\Omega}^{(k)}_{N}({\delta}_{k,N})\Bigr)^{c}\right)\leq\frac{1}{N^{2}k^{2}}.

(3.29)

Let now

be fixed. Then,

is a decreasing function of

that decays to zero as

. Hence, for all
\varepsilon>0
, there exists

such that

for all

and, for all

(3.30)

where we used (
3.29
). By Borel-Cantelli lemma, for all

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\left({\Omega}^{(k)}(\varepsilon)\right)^{c}\right)=\lim_{{N^{*}\rightarrow\infty}\atop{N^{*}\geq N(k,\varepsilon)}}{\mathbb{P}}\left({\textstyle\bigcup_{N\geq N^{*}}\bigl({\Omega}^{(k)}_{N}(\varepsilon)\bigr)^{c}}\right)=0.

From this and the monotony of

it then follows in a standard way that

{\mathbb{P}}\bigl({\Omega}^{(k)}_{0}\bigr)={\mathbb{P}}\left(\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\left|X^{(k)}_{N}({\omega})-Y^{(k)}_{N}({\omega})\right|=0\right)=1.

(3.32)

Since the above holds true for any given

,

{\mathbb{P}}\left({\textstyle\bigcap_{k\geq 1}}{\Omega}^{(k)}_{0}\right)={\mathbb{P}}\left(\forall{k\geq 1}\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\left|X^{(k)}_{N}({\omega})-Y^{(k)}_{N}({\omega})\right|=0\right)=1,

(3.33)

which proves (
3.25
).

In order to prove (
3.26
) we must take the additional limit
k\rightarrow\infty
. Set

{\Omega}_{0}=\textstyle{\bigcup_{k^{*}\geq 1}\bigcap_{k\geq k^{*}}}{\Omega}^{(k)}_{0}

(3.34)

and observe that

. Thus, by (

)

and so,

{\mathbb{P}}\bigl({\Omega}_{0}\bigr)={\mathbb{P}}\left(\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\left|X^{(k)}_{N}({\omega})-Y^{(k)}_{N}({\omega})\right|=0\right)=1.

(3.36)

This proves (
3.26
). (Note that alternatively, we could have taken this second

limit by summing (

) over

and using Borel-Cantelli lemma.) It is clear from the proof that the limits in (

) cannot be interchanged. Taking

completes the proof of the lemma in the case of sequences that depend on a single parameter,

. The extension of the proof to the case of sequences depending on finitely many parameters

is straightforward.

5.
Analysis of the Hessian of
\Psi_{N,{\beta},{\bf{h}}}

5.1.
Main results and strategy

Let
{\mathcal{H}}_{N}(m)\equiv{\mathcal{H}}_{N,{\beta},{\bf{h}}}(m)
denote the Hessian matrix of
\Psi_{N,{\beta},{\bf{h}}}
at
m
. Given
\sqrt{3/4}\leq\varrho\leq 1
, consider the set

{\mathcal{B}}^{c}_{N,{\epsilon}}(\varrho)=\left\{m\in[-1,1]^{N}:q_{\text{EA}}(m)\geq\varrho-\varrho(1-\varrho){\epsilon}\right\}.

(5.1)

The main result of this section establishes that
{\mathcal{H}}_{N}(m)
is strictly negative definite on
{\mathcal{B}}^{c}_{N,{\epsilon}}(\varrho)
for all
({\beta},h)

in
{\mathcal{D}}^{(1)}\cap{\mathcal{D}}^{(2)}_{\varrho}
, where
{\mathcal{D}}^{(1)}
and
{\mathcal{D}}^{(2)}_{\varrho}
are defined in (
1.28
) and (
1.30
), respectively.

Theorem 5.1
.

(i) Let
\sqrt{3/4}\leq\varrho
be given. For all
({\beta},h)
in
{\mathcal{D}}^{(2)}_{\varrho}

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\bigcup_{N_{0}}\bigcap_{N\geq N_{0}}\left\{\sup_{{\epsilon}\in[0,1]}\sup_{m\in{\mathcal{B}}^{c}_{N,{\epsilon}}(\varrho)}{\lambda}_{max}({\mathcal{H}}_{N}(m))<0\right\}\right)=1,

(5.2)

(ii) For all
({\beta},h)
in
{\mathcal{D}}^{(1)}

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\bigcup_{N_{0}}\bigcap_{N\geq N_{0}}\left\{\sup_{m\in[-1,1]^{N}}{\lambda}_{max}({\mathcal{H}}_{N}(m))<0\right\}\right)=1.

(5.3)

The proofs of the two items of Theorem
5.1
follow very different strategies. Item (ii) is based on elementary arguments and is proved at the very end of the section, while the proof of item (i) occupies most of it.

First, we set up the necessary matrix notation. Let
M_{N}
be an
N\times N
real symmetric matrix. We write
M_{N}<0
(resp.,
M_{N}>0
) when
M_{N}
is strictly negative (resp., positive) definite. The notation
M_{N}=\textrm{diag}(\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{N})
indicates that
M_{N}
is diagonal with diagonal entries
M_{ii}=\mu_{i}
. We denote by
{\lambda}_{max}(M_{N})
and
{\lambda}_{min}(M_{N})
the largest and smallest eigenvalues of
M_{N}
, and by
r(M_{N})
its spectral radius, i.e. the largest absolute value of its eigenvalues. The operator norm of
M_{N}
is denoted by
\|M_{N}\|
and defined by

\|M_{N}\|=\sup_{x:\|x\|_{2}=1}\|M_{N}x\|_{2}.

(5.4)

Finally, we recall that eigenvalues, spectral radius and operator norm are related through

\textstyle\sup_{x:\|x\|_{2}=1}|(x,M_{N}x)|=r(M_{N})=\sqrt{r(M^{2}_{N})}=\|M_{N}\|.

(5.5)

The central idea behind the proof of item (i) of Theorem
5.1
is to replace the condition on the negative definiteness of the Hessian matrix
{\mathcal{H}}_{N}(m)
, which, as we will see, is of additive form, by a condition on the spectrum of a matrix, which takes the form of a product and is easier to study. To this end, we go back to the function
\Psi_{N,{\beta},{\bf{h}}}
and, taking second partial derivatives, we get

{\mathcal{H}}_{N}(m)={\beta}\frac{J_{N}}{\sqrt{N}}-B_{N}(m)

(5.6)

where
B_{N}(m)=\textrm{diag}(b_{1}(m),\dots,b_{N}(m))
and

b_{i}(m)={\beta}^{2}(1-q)+\frac{1}{1-m_{i}^{2}},\quad 1\leq i\leq N.

(5.7)

Since
b_{i}(m)>0
for each
1\leq i\leq N
,
B_{N}(m)
is strictly positive definite and has rank
N
.
Denoting by
B^{1/2}_{N}(m)
its strictly unique positive definite square root and by
B^{-1/2}_{N}(m)
its inverse, (
5.6
) may be written as

{\mathcal{H}}_{N}(m)=B^{1/2}_{N}(m)\left({\beta}C_{N}(m)-I_{N}\right)B^{1/2}_{N}(m)

(5.8)

where
I_{N}
is the identity matrix in
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
and

C_{N}(m)\equiv B^{-1/2}_{N}(m)\frac{J_{N}}{\sqrt{N}}B^{-1/2}_{N}(m).

(5.9)

By definition,
{\mathcal{H}}_{N}(m)
is strictly negative definite if
(x,{\mathcal{H}}_{N}(m)x)<0
for all non-zero
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
, and this is true if and only if all eigenvalues of
{\mathcal{H}}_{N}(m)
are strictly negative. Since
\mathop{\rm rank}\nolimits B^{1/2}_{N}(m)=\mathop{\rm rank}\nolimits B_{N}(m)=N
, it follows from (
5.8
) that
{\mathcal{H}}_{N}(m)<0
if and only if

{\beta}C_{N}(m)-I<0
.
Thus, a necessary and sufficient condition for
{\mathcal{H}}_{N}(m)
to be strictly negative definite is

{\beta}{\lambda}_{max}(C_{N}(m))<1.

(5.10)

Theorem
5.1
will then be deduced from the following proposition.
Let
{\mathcal{S}}_{N,{\epsilon}}(\varrho)
denote the spherical shell

{\mathcal{S}}_{N,{\epsilon}}(\varrho)=\left\{m\in[-1,1]^{N}:|q_{\text{EA}}(m)-\varrho|\leq\varrho(1-\varrho){\epsilon}\right\}\subset{\mathcal{B}}^{c}_{N,{\epsilon}}(\varrho).

(5.11)

For
0\leq\varrho\leq q
, recalling the definition of
\vartheta(\varrho)
from (
1.29
), set

\displaystyle f_{1}(\varrho)\hskip-6.0pt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hskip-6.0pt\vartheta(\varrho)

(5.12)

\displaystyle f_{2}({\beta},q)\hskip-6.0pt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hskip-6.0pt2{\beta}-\left({\beta}^{2}(1-q)+1\right).

(5.13)

Proposition 5.2
.

(i) For all
({\beta},h)
,
h>0
, all
\varrho\geq\sqrt{3/4}
and all

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\sup_{{\epsilon}\in[0,1]}\sup_{m\in{\mathcal{S}}_{N,{\epsilon}}(\varrho)}{\lambda}_{max}(C_{N}(m))\geq f_{1}(\varrho)+16\sqrt{{\textstyle{\log N\over\sqrt{N}}}}\right)\leq 6e^{-\sqrt{N}}.

(ii) For all
({\beta},h)
,
h>0
, and all

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\sup_{m\in[-1,1]^{N}}{\lambda}_{max}({\mathcal{H}}_{N}(m))\geq f_{2}({\beta},q)+{\beta}N^{-1/4}\right)\leq 2e^{-\sqrt{N}/4}.

Again, the bulk of the proof of Proposition

\|C_{N}(m)\|
. From (

) and (

and (

, into a condition on the spectrum of the sole Wigner-type Gaussian random matrix

whose entries,

, are also independent centred Gaussians, but now have non-identical variances. More specifically, setting

a_{i}(m)\equiv b^{-1}_{i}(m),\quad 1\leq i\leq N,

we have

C_{ij}(m)=J_{ij}v_{ij}(m),\quad v_{ij}(m)\equiv\sqrt{\frac{a_{i}(m)a_{j}(m)}{N}}.

(5.17)

While there are few tools available to deal with non-finite rank perturbations of Wigner random matrices (see
[
18
]
,
[
19
]
), the question of finding bounds on the norm or largest eigenvalue of non-homogeneous Wigner-type random matrices such as

has recently witnessed significant developments

]
,
[

]
. The proof of Proposition

is based on results of

which, for the convenience of the reader, we state below in a version specialised to the matrices (

Theorem 5.3

(Theorem 1.1 and Corollary 3.9 of
[
7
]
)
.

Given

Then, for any

(5.19)

In addition, for any

). However, since we want to bound the supremum of

, we also have to deal with matrices with highly inhomogeneous coefficients. To explain the difficulty we face, let us first state a useful lemma.
Set

\varrho^{\pm}_{{\epsilon}}\equiv\varrho\pm\varrho(1-\varrho){\epsilon},

.

\displaystyle(1-\varrho^{+}_{{\epsilon}})c_{0}(1)\leq\hskip-6.0pt

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sup_{m\in{\mathcal{S}}_{N,{\epsilon}}(\varrho)}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j}a_{j}(m)

\displaystyle\hskip-6.0pt\leq c_{0}(1-\varrho^{-}_{{\epsilon}})

\displaystyle c_{0}(1-\varrho^{-}_{{\epsilon}})\leq\hskip-6.0pt

\textstyle\sup_{m\in{\mathcal{S}}_{N,{\epsilon}}(\varrho)}{\sigma}(m)\leq\sqrt{c_{0}(1)c_{0}(1-\varrho^{-}_{{\epsilon}})},\quad\sup_{m\in{\mathcal{S}}_{N,{\epsilon}}(\varrho)}{\sigma_{\star}(m)}\leq c_{0}(1)/\sqrt{N}.

with “tamed coefficient”, that are neither too large nor too small compared to the average
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j}a_{j}(m)

An integral representation formula

In this section, an integral representation of the partition function (

) derived from the Hubbard-Stratonovitch transformation
[

,
46
]
is used to prove Theorem
1.5
. This transformation has proved to be a useful tool for identifying the free energy functionals of mean-field models, from the early work of Kac
[
35
]
on the Curie-Weiss model to the more recent analysis of the Hopfield model
[
15
,
12
,
13
,
14
]
.
Here, it allows us to identify the function
F_{N,{\beta},h}^{HT}
in (
1.15
) as the free energy functional of the SK model at high temperature

Consider the matrix
A\equiv A_{N}
defined in (
2.1
). Since
M
is real symmetric, there exists an orthogonal matrix
{\mathcal{O}}
and a diagonal matrix
{\Lambda}=\textrm{diag}({\lambda}_{1},{\lambda}_{2},\dots,{\lambda}_{N})
such that
A={\mathcal{O}}^{t}{\Lambda}{\mathcal{O}}
. Let
\sqrt{A}
denote the matrix
\sqrt{A}\equiv{\mathcal{O}}^{t}\sqrt{{\Lambda}}{\mathcal{O}}
where
\sqrt{{\Lambda}}\equiv\textrm{diag}(\sqrt{{\lambda}_{1}},\sqrt{{\lambda}_{2}},\dots,\sqrt{{\lambda}_{N}})
with the convention that if
{\alpha}
is a real number,

\sqrt{{\alpha}}=\zeta\sqrt{|{\alpha}|}\quad\text{where}\quad\zeta=\begin{cases}i&\mbox{if}\,\,{\alpha}<0,\\
1&\mbox{if}\,\,{\alpha}\geq 0,\end{cases}

(7.1)

where
i
is the unit imaginary number. Thus
\sqrt{A}
is a complex symmetric matrix
that satisfies
\sqrt{A}\sqrt{A}=A
. Let
{\mathcal{F}}_{N,{\beta},h}:{\mathbb{C}}^{N}\mapsto{\mathbb{C}}
be the function

{\mathcal{F}}_{N,{\beta},h}(x)=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{N}x_{j}^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{N}\log\cosh\left(\sqrt{{\beta}}(\sqrt{A}x)_{j}+h\right)+N\log 2

(7.2)

where the log function is defined in the principal branch (it is therefore continuous in any open set in the complex plane from which the negative axes and zero have been removed).

Lemma 7.1
.

Z_{N,{\beta},h}=e^{\frac{1}{2}N{\beta}^{2}(1-q)}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{N}\frac{dx_{j}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\right)\,e^{{\mathcal{F}}_{N,{\beta},h}(x)}.

(7.3)

To evaluate such an integral, one usually starts by looking for a critical point that maximises the real part of
{\mathcal{F}}_{N,{\beta},h}
and, if this point is unique, one tries to deform the integration path so that it passes through this point in the direction of steepest descent (equivalently, a direction of constant phase). On such a contour, in the vicinity of the critical point, the integral should resemble a Laplace integral. Although our attempts to compute this integral failed, part of this programme can be carried out as we now explain.

Our first lemma links the critical points of
{\mathcal{F}}_{N,{\beta},h}
to the solutions of the TAP equations.
Recall the definitions of
\Psi_{N,{\beta},{\bf{h}}}
and
\Phi_{N,{\beta},{\bf{h}}}
from (
2.2
). Observe that for all
z\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
,

{\mathcal{F}}_{N,{\beta},h}\bigl(\sqrt{{\beta}}\sqrt{A}z\bigr)=\Phi_{N,{\beta},{\bf{h}}}(z).

(7.4)

.

If

is a solution of the TAP equations (

) satisfying

,
then

{\mathcal{F}}_{N,{\beta},h}
, and for each such pair of critical points (

) holds.

Points of the form

,

Thus the prospective maximiser of

is in
{\mathbb{C}}^{N}
while the integration contour

N
real axes.

\Xi_{N,{\beta},h}(x)
is holomorphic and continuous, it follows from Osgood’s lemma that it is holomorphic in each variable separately.
We can thus apply the one variable Cauchy-Goursat integral formula to each of the variables
x_{j}
,
j=1,2,\dots,N
, successively.

We begin with the variable
x_{1}
. Let
\theta_{1}(x_{1}):{\mathbb{C}}\mapsto{\mathbb{C}}
be the function defined by integrating
\Xi_{N,{\beta},h}
over all variables except
x_{1}
, which is kept fixed

\theta_{1}(x_{1})=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N-1}}\left(\prod_{j=2}^{N}\frac{dx_{j}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\right)\,\Xi_{N,{\beta},h}(x).

(7.25)

According to the Cauchy-Goursat integral formula

\int_{{\mathcal{C}}_{1}}\frac{dx_{1}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\theta_{1}(x_{1})=0

(7.26)

where, writing
x_{1}=u_{1}+iv_{1}
,
{\mathcal{C}}_{1}
is the rectangular closed path in the plane
(u_{1},v_{1})
defined as the boundary of the rectangle of vertices
A=(-R_{N},0)
,
B=(R_{N},0)
,
C=(R_{N},b_{1})
, and
D(-R_{N},b_{1})
, oriented counter-clockwise. Here
R_{N}=NR
,
R>0
, and
b_{1}>0
so that the rectangle lies in the upper half-plane (the case
b_{1}<0
, which corresponds to a rectangle in the lower half-plane, is treated in the same way). The left-hand side of (
7.26
) naturally decomposes into four integrals, each of them along the path that follows a given side of the rectangle

\int_{{\mathcal{C}}_{1}}\frac{dx_{1}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\theta_{1}(x_{1})={\mathcal{I}}_{AB}+{\mathcal{I}}_{BC}+{\mathcal{I}}_{CD}+{\mathcal{I}}_{DA}.

(7.27)

Let us establish that

\lim_{R\rightarrow\infty}{\mathcal{I}}_{BC}\equiv\int_{R_{N}+i0}^{R_{N}+ib_{1}}\frac{dx_{1}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\theta_{1}(x_{1})=0.

(7.28)

By a change of variables

{\mathcal{I}}_{BC}=2^{N}\int_{0}^{b_{1}}\frac{dv_{1}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}(R_{N}+iv_{1})^{2}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N-1}}\left(\prod_{j=2}^{N}\frac{du_{j}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\right)e^{-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=2}^{N}u_{j}^{2}}\Theta(u+iv)

(7.29)

where
u=(R_{N},u_{2},\dots,u_{N})
and
v=(v_{1},0,\dots,0)
are vectors in
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
and

\Theta(u+iv)=\prod_{j=1}^{N}\cosh\left(\sqrt{{\beta}}(\sqrt{A}(u+iv))_{j}+h\right).

(7.30)

Taking the modulus,

\left|{\mathcal{I}}_{BC}\right|\leq 2^{N}\int_{0}^{b_{1}}\frac{dv_{1}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}(R^{2}_{N}-v_{1}^{2})}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N-1}}\left(\prod_{j=2}^{N}\frac{du_{j}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\right)e^{-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=2}^{N}u_{j}^{2}}\left|\Theta(u+iv)\right|.

(7.31)

To bound
\left|\Theta(u+iv)\right|
, first note that

\left|\cosh(u_{0}+iv_{0})\right|=\left|\cosh(u_{0})\right|\sqrt{\cos^{2}(v_{0})+\tanh^{2}(u_{0})\sin^{2}(v_{0})}\leq\left|\cosh(u_{0})\right|\leq e^{\left|u_{0}\right|}.

Now, writing
\sqrt{A}=U+iV
where
U={\mathcal{O}}^{t}\Re(\sqrt{{\Lambda}}){\mathcal{O}}
and
V={\mathcal{O}}^{t}\Im(\sqrt{{\Lambda}}){\mathcal{O}}
, and using the above bound

\left|\Theta(u+iv)\right|\leq e^{\sqrt{{\beta}}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left|(Uu)_{j}-(Vv)_{j}\right|+Nh}.

(7.32)

Observing that
UV=VU=0
,

\displaystyle\textstyle\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left|(Uu)_{j}-(Vv)_{j}\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\textstyle\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{N}((Uu)_{j}-(Vv)_{j})^{2}}

(7.33)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\textstyle\sqrt{(uU^{2}u)+(vV^{2}v)}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\textstyle\sqrt{\max_{j}|{\lambda}_{j}|(\left\|u\right\|_{2}^{2}+\left\|v\right\|_{2}^{2})}\leq\textstyle\sqrt{\max_{j}|{\lambda}_{j}|}(\left\|u\right\|_{1}+\left\|v\right\|_{1})

where
\max_{j}|{\lambda}_{j}|
is bounded in (
7.24
) and, by definition of
u
and
v
,
\left\|u\right\|_{1}=R_{N}+\sum_{j=2}^{N}|u_{j}|
and
\left\|v\right\|_{1}=b_{1}
.
Thus, recalling the definition of
c_{q}
from (
7.24
)

\left|\Theta(u+iv)\right|\leq e^{\sqrt{{\beta}c_{q}}\left(R_{N}+\sum_{j=2}^{N}|u_{j}|+b_{1}\right)+Nh}.

(7.34)

Inserting this bound in (
7.31
),

\displaystyle\left|{\mathcal{I}}_{BC}\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2^{N}\int_{0}^{b_{1}}\frac{dv_{1}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}(R^{2}_{N}-v_{1}^{2})+\sqrt{{\beta}c_{q}}(R_{N}+b_{1})+Nh}\prod_{j=2}^{N}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\frac{du_{j}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}u_{j}^{2}+\sqrt{{\beta}c_{q}}|u_{j}|}

(7.35)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{2b_{1}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}(R^{2}_{N}-b_{1}^{2})+\sqrt{{\beta}c_{q}}(R_{N}+b_{1})+Nh}\left(4e^{\frac{1}{2}{\beta}c_{q}}\right)^{N-1},

and taking the limit
R\rightarrow\infty
of both sides of (
7.35
), we obtain (
7.28
).
We prove in exactly the same way that

\lim_{R\rightarrow\infty}{\mathcal{I}}_{DA}\equiv\int_{-R_{N}+ib_{1}}^{-R_{N}+i0}\frac{dx_{1}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\theta_{1}(x_{1})=0.

(7.36)

Passing to the limit
R\rightarrow\infty
in (
7.27
), it follows from (
7.26
), (
7.28
) and (
7.36
) that

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dx_{1}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\theta_{1}(x_{1})=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dx_{1}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\theta_{1}(x_{1}+ib_{1}).

(7.37)

By definition of
\theta_{1}(x_{1})
(see (
7.25
)), setting
b^{(1)}=(b_{1},0,\dots,0)
, this is equivalent to

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{N}\frac{dx_{j}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\right)\,\Xi_{N,{\beta},h}(x)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{N}\frac{dx_{j}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\right)\,\Xi_{N,{\beta},h}(x+ib^{(1)}).

(7.38)

To deal with the next variable,
x_{2}
, we start from the right-hand side of (
7.38
) and, in complete analogy to (
7.25
),
we let
\theta_{2}(x_{2}):{\mathbb{C}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{C}}
be the function defined by integrating
\Xi_{N,{\beta},h}
over all variables except
x_{2}
, which is kept fixed

\theta_{2}(x_{2})=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N-1}}\left(\prod_{1\leq j\leq N:j\neq 2}\frac{dx_{j}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\right)\,\Xi_{N,{\beta},h}(x+b^{(1)}).

(7.39)

We then consider the Cauchy-Goursat integral formula

\int_{{\mathcal{C}}_{2}}\frac{dx_{1}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\theta_{2}(x_{2})=0

(7.40)

where
{\mathcal{C}}_{2}
is defined as
{\mathcal{C}}_{1}
, replacing
b_{1}
by
b_{2}
,
and use it, proceeding exactly as in the proof of (
7.28
) to prove that

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dx_{2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\theta_{2}(x_{2})=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dx_{2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\theta_{2}(x_{2}+ib_{2}).

(7.41)

We omit the details of the straightforward adaptation of the bounds (
7.31
)-(
7.35
).
Setting
b^{(2)}=(b_{1},b_{2},\dots,0)
, (
7.41
) is equivalent to

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{N}\frac{dx_{j}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\right)\,\Xi_{N,{\beta},h}(x)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{N}\frac{dx_{j}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\right)\,\Xi_{N,{\beta},h}(x+ib^{(2)}).

(7.42)

Iterating this procedure over the variable
x_{j}
, we obtain (
7.5
). Lemma
7.3
is proven.
∎

We conclude this section with the

Proofs of Theorem
1.9
and
1.2
.

Theorem
1.9
follows from Theorem
1.5
and Theorem
1.8
. Theorem
1.2
follows from Theorem
1.9
.
∎
