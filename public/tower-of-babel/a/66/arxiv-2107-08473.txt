Elliptic Curve Fast Fourier Transform (ECFFT) Part I: Fast Polynomial Algorithms over all Finite Fields
Eli Ben-Sasson, Dan Carmon, Swastik Kopparty, David Levit

Abstract
Over finite fields 𝔽 q \mathbb{F}_{q} containing a root of unity of smooth order n n (smoothness means n n is the product of small primes), the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) leads to the fastest known algebraic algorithms for many basic polynomial operations, such as multiplication, division, interpolation and multi-point evaluation. These operations can be computed by constant fan-in arithmetic circuits over 𝔽 q \mathbb{F}_{q} of quasi-linear size; specifically, O ⁡ ( n ​ log ⁡ n ) O(n\log n) for multiplication and division, and O ⁡ ( n ​ log 2 ​ n ) O(n\log^{2}n) for interpolation and evaluation. However, the same operations over fields with no smooth order root of unity suffer from an asymptotic slowdown, typically due to the need to introduce “synthetic” roots of unity to enable the FFT. The classical algorithm of Schönhage and Strassen [ SS71 ] incurred a multiplicative slowdown factor of log ⁡ log ⁡ n \log\log n on top of the smooth case. Recent remarkable results of Harvey, van der Hoeven and Lecerf [ HvdHL17 , HvdH19a ] dramatically reduced this multiplicative overhead to exp ⁡ ( log ∗ ⁡ ( n ) ) \exp(\log^{*}(n)) . We introduce a new approach to fast algorithms for polynomial operations over all large finite fields. The key idea is to replace the group of roots of unity with a set of points L ⊂ 𝔽 q L\subset\mathbb{F}_{q} suitably related to a well-chosen elliptic curve group over 𝔽 q \mathbb{F}_{q} (the set L L itself is not a group). The key advantage of this approach is that elliptic curve groups can be of any size in the Hasse–Weil interval [ q + ± 2 ​ q + 1 ] [q+\pm 2\sqrt{q}+1] and thus can have subgroups of large, smooth order, which an FFT-like divide and conquer algorithm can exploit. Compare this with multiplicative subgroups over 𝔽 q \mathbb{F}_{q} whose order must divide q − 1 q-1 . By analogy, our method extends the standard, multiplicative FFT in a similar way to how Lenstra’s elliptic curve method [ Len87 ] extended Pollard’s p − 1 p-1 algorithm [ Pol74 ] for factoring integers. For polynomials represented by their evaluation over subsets of L L , we show that multiplication, division, degree-computation, interpolation, evaluation and Reed–Solomon encoding (also known as low-degree extension) with fixed evaluation points can all be computed with arithmetic circuits of size similar to what is achievable with the classical FFTs when the field size q q is special. For several problems, this yields the asymptotically smallest known arithmetic circuits even in the standard monomial representation of polynomials. The efficiency of the classical FFT follows from using the 2 2 -to- 1 1 squaring map to reduce the evaluation set of roots of unity of order 2 k 2^{k} to similar groups of size 2 k − i , i > 0 2^{k-i},i>0 . Our algorithms operate similarly, using isogenies of elliptic curves with kernel size 2 2 as 2 2 -to- 1 1 maps to reduce L L of size 2 k 2^{k} to sets of size 2 k − i 2^{k-i} that are, like L L , suitably related to elliptic curves, albeit different ones.

Remark 1.1

(Computational Model)

Unless explicitly specified otherwise, we use the word “algorithm” to mean an algebraic algorithm that uses only field operations and field constants. In particular, we do not consider any precision issues or the cost of computing the constants used by the computation. This computational model is more commonly known as an arithmetic circuit or a straight-line program. 1.1
Our Results

The main contribution of our paper is a new approach to fast polynomial algorithms via a new polynomial
representation that works over all large finite fields. The approach is very closely related to the
classical FFT algorithm, but instead of working with subgroups of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
of smooth order (be they multiplicative or additive), it works with
elliptic curve groups
with large, smooth order subgroups,

which exist for all
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.

Our approach is unrelated to all the recent results mentioned above, and unconditionally yields some
new results that would follow if
M_{q}(n)=O(n\log n)
were true.

The new representation for polynomials suggested here is essentially the evaluation tables of the
polynomials at carefully chosen subsets of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. These sets are related to some subgroup of some elliptic curve over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.
This is the analogue of representing polynomials by evaluations at multiplicative/additive subgroups of large, smooth order, which is only possible when
q
is
special—either a power of a constant prime or such that
q-1
is divisible by a large smooth factor.

In the classical multiplicative subgroup based FFT, we can convert the evaluation table representation into the
standard coefficient representation in time
O(n\log n)
via the classical inverse FFT.
Unfortunately, in our elliptic curve group case, we do not know how to do this conversion as fast.
What we can do instead is to quickly
extend
the evaluation of the polynomial on our chosen subset
S
to
another subset
S^{\prime}
of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. This is the analogue of using a combination of FFT and inverse-FFT
(with some scaling) to use the evaluations of some low degree polynomial at a multiplicative
subgroup
S
to deduce the evaluations of that low degree polynomial at some coset of
S
. In fact, the way we
compute the low degree extension to the subset
S^{\prime}
is also a combination of some FFT-like
transform (which we call the ECFFT) and the inverse transform.
It just so happens that the intermediate representation, i.e., the result of our iFFT-analogue, is not the standard monomial expansion of the polynomial, but some other representation. In this respect, our approach resembles the additive FFT-like transforms of
[
LCH14
]
which also lead to non-monomial representations supporting fast operations (see also
[
GM10
,
Can89
]
); however, their algorithms have
S,S^{\prime}
being additive subgroups of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, and require
\mathbb{F}_{q}
to have constant characteristic to have
O(n\log n)
running time.

We systematically exploit the above-mentioned fast algorithm for extending
polynomial evaluations on special sets to
develop fast algorithms

2

n

q

]
.
for a variety of polynomial computation problems, giving the following results, defined formally in

Armed with these tools for working with polynomials in the new representation, we get the following new results for

1.2
ECFFT – Informal Explanation

The standard FFT algorithm exploits the structure of the group of
2^{\sf{k}}
-th roots of
unity and its subgroups, using the squaring map
x\mapsto x^{2}
to simultaneously (i) project the
group of size
n
to a subgroup of half the size and (ii) split a polynomial of degree
n
into two
polynomials of half the degree, expressed using the squaring map.

Let
n=2^{\sf{k}}
, and suppose we are working in a field
\mathbb{F}

which contains all
n
of the
n
th roots of
1
. Let
L^{(0)}\subseteq\mathbb{F}

denote all the
n
th roots of
1
, assuming we wish
to represent polynomials of degree
<n

by evaluating them on
L^{(0)}
.
Let
\psi(X)=X^{2}
be the squaring map. For each
i
, let

L^{(i+1)}=\psi(L^{(i)})
. Thus
L^{(i)}
is the set of
\frac{n}{2^{i}}
th roots of
unity in
\mathbb{F}
and
\psi
is a
2
-to-
1
map of degree
2
from
L^{(i)}
onto
L^{(i+1)}
. Thus far we have
described how
\psi
is used to “compress” an evaluation set
L^{(i)}
to a smaller evaluation set
L^{(i+1)}

of half the size. Simultaneously,
\psi
can be used to “split” a polynomial presented in the standard
monomial basis thus:

P(X)=\sum_{i<n}a_{i}\cdot X^{i}=\left(\sum_{i<n/2}a_{2i}\cdot\psi(X)^{i}\right)+X\cdot\left(\sum_{i<n/2}a_{2i+1}\cdot\psi(X)^{i}\right)=P_{0}(\psi(X))+X\cdot P_{1}(\psi(X)).

The FFT evaluates
P
on
L^{(0)}
by recursively evaluating both
P_{0}(Y)
and
P_{1}(Y)
on
y\in\psi(L^{(0)})=L^{(1)}

and then combining the results using
O(n)
operations via the formula above. The running time
F(n)

satisfies the recursive formula
F(n)=2\cdot F(n/2)+O(n)
leading to
O(n\log n)
running time.

The essential elements we preserve in our ECFFT are the usage of degree-
2
maps
\psi^{(i)}
that are
2
-to-
1
maps on special sets of points
L^{(i)}
of size
\frac{n}{2^{i}}
, along with the ability to express a polynomial

P(X)
of degree
<n
in terms of two other polynomials
P_{0}(\psi^{(i)}(X)),P_{1}(\psi^{(i)}(X))
of degree
<n/2
,
such that the value of
P(x),x\in L^{(i)}
can be obtained “locally” from the values of
P_{0}(\psi^{(i)}(x)),P_{1}(\psi^{(i)}(x))
. Thus, we use such maps and sets of points to describe new
\mathsf{FFTrees}

. An
\mathsf{FFTree}
(see

Definition

3.3
) is an “FFT-inspired” object that is a layered binary tree whose nodes residing at the

i
th layer are labeled by the members of
L^{(i)}
, and such that the
2
-to-
1
map
\psi^{(i)}
defines directed
edges from two elements
s_{0},s_{1}\in L^{(i)}
to
t=\psi^{(i)}(s_{0})=\psi^{(i)}(s_{1})\in L^{(i+1)}
.

So far we have listed similarities between the FFT and our new ECFFT, so let us now describe the differences.
First, our set
L^{(i)}
is not a multiplicative group, and in fact it is not a group at all (soon, in

Section

1.3
, we will explain what
L^{(i)}
actually is). But examining the classical FFT, we could do
its first step using
any
degree-
2
polynomial
\psi(X)
which is
2
-to-
1
on some set of points

L^{(0)}
(mapping it to an arbitrary set of points
L^{(1)}
of size
n/2
). The group structure is useful for
knowing, recursively, that we can find further
2
-to-
1
maps from
L^{(1)}
to
L^{(2)}
and so on.
A second
point of difference is that our
2
-to-
1
maps may vary with
i
, whereas the classical FFT uses only squaring
4
4

4

When
n
is factored into different prime factors (say,
n=2^{a}\cdot 3^{b}
)
one would also use different maps in the FFT (say, squaring and cubing) to move between
L^{(i)}
and
L^{(i+1)}
, and varying maps are also used in additive FFTs
[
GM10
,
Can89
,
LCH14
]
.

to move from
L^{(i)}
to
L^{(i+1)}
.
Finally, the maps
\psi^{(i)}
we use are not degree-
2
polynomials but rather degree-
2
rational maps, ratios
of two degree-
2
polynomials. We show that any such map is just as good for the purpose of splitting a
polynomial into two subpolynomials of half the degree (see

maps rather than polynomials gives us more degrees of freedom when searching for
2
-to-
1
maps on
special sets of points. These points, and the way they are obtained, are our next, and main, point
in this intuitive description of the ECFFT.

1.3
Elliptic Curves as a Source for

Elliptic curves are a vast topic of study, with wide-ranging impact across mathematics (e.g.,
[

An elliptic curve

,

]

]
;
Couveignes and Lercier’s randomized algorithm for finding irreducible polynomials over finite
fields
[

]

cryptosystems, starting with Miller’s EC Diffie–Hellman (ECDH) key exchange
[

]

and Koblitz’s EC integrated encryption scheme (ECIES)

ABR99
]
and including Vanstone’s EC digital signature algorithm (ECDSA)

We remark that Lenstra’s method for factoring integers using elliptic curves

]

Lenstra’s method extends the

\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\times}

with elliptic curves. Very similarly, the standard FFT works inside

The main properties of elliptic curves that we use are:

•

The number of points on the curve

•

These points

[q\pm 2\sqrt{q}+1]
. In particular, we can find subgroups

•

If

\mathbb{F}_{q}

|H|

1
map
\phi
(called an isogeny) with kernel
H
from the points of the curve
E
to points on a different curve
E^{\prime}
over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. Thus, the image of
G
under the isogeny is of size
|G|/|H|
.

The observations above give us nearly all that we need. We can find a set of points
G^{(0)}
inside a curve
E^{(0)}
that
is a group of size
2^{\sf{k}}\leq O(\sqrt{q})
irrespective of the exact nature of
q
, and we have at our disposal
isogenies that “compress” groups of points
G^{(i)}
to groups
G^{(i+1)}
half the size via
2
-to-
1
maps
\phi^{(i)}
,
where the new group
G^{(i+1)}
belongs to a different curve
E^{(i+1)}
. The only remaining gap is that elements in the groups

G^{(i)}
are
pairs

(x,y)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{2}
whereas we are interested in univariate polynomials and evaluation sets over

\mathbb{F}_{q}
. The final ingredient is to pick curves represented in a certain format (extended Weierstrass form) such that
suitably shifting and then projecting
G^{(i)}
to the

that is the same size as

and, crucially, the

gives rise to a degree-

(see

and

).

Remark 1.2
.

The degree-
2
(or higher degree) maps so obtained are generalizations of
Lattés maps

[
Lat18
]
(see
[
Sil07
]
).
Lattés maps are the rational maps arising from the
x
-coordinate mapping of isogenies from an elliptic curve to
itself
. The
rational maps that underlie the
\mathsf{FFTree}
construction arise from the
x
-coordinate mapping of isogenies from an elliptic curve
E
to some other elliptic curve
E^{\prime}
, which may or may not equal
E
.

Summarizing, the abundance of elliptic curve groups of various sizes over any large finite field assures us that
we will find a subgroup of smooth size; isogenies and their projections give
2
-to-
1
degree-
2
rational maps from sets of size
2^{\sf{k}}

(in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
) to sets of size
2^{{\sf{k}}-1}
for all needed
{\sf{k}}
, and thereby we have the needed
\mathsf{FFTree}
structure which
leads to efficient FFT-like running times for all finite fields.

The paper of Chudnovsky and Chudnovsky
[
CC89
]

Some of the core ideas appearing in this paper were first suggested, in brief,
in the final section of a paper by Chudnovsky and Chudnovsky
[
CC89
, Section 6]
.
The main claim from
[
CC89
]
relevant for us is an “Elliptic Interpolation Algorithm” called
FENTT (Fast Elliptic Number Theoretic Transform), which
describes how to use elliptic curve groups over finite fields
to solve a certain rational function interpolation problem via
an FFT-type algorithm.

We were made aware of this paper by an anonymous reviewer,
who further remarked that the authors only sketch
their idea. Indeed, the writing is extremely succinct, and many details are omitted or only
hinted at. Furthermore, we believe there is at least one critical point which was overlooked in that
work, which significantly limits the applicability of the FENTT.

As a consequence of this error, it turns out (via a result published independently by Ruck
[
Rüc87
]

and Voloch
[
Vol88
]
)
that the instance size
n
of an FENTT over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
is bounded from above in terms of the prime
factorization of
q-1
(as is the case for FFTs).
The net result is that an FENTT of size
n
can be computed over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
only if
an FFT of size
\Theta(\sqrt{n})
can be computed over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
— thus, finite fields
that do not support large FFTs also do not support large FENTTs. In contrast, large ECFFTs
(of size
q^{\Omega(1)}
) are supported by all finite fields
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. This fact was crucial to our
faster algorithms for working with polynomials of degree
n
over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
for all
n\leq q^{O(1)}
.

We give details about the ideas, methods, and limitations of
[
CC89
]
in relation to ours in Appendix
A
.

For a set

B(X)\mathsf{\>rem\>}A(X)

2.2
Projective Space

We denote by
\mathbb{P}^{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
(or simply
\mathbb{P}^{n}
) the
n
-dimensional projective
space
over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
; only
\mathbb{P}^{1}
and
\mathbb{P}^{2}
will appear in the paper.
Points in
\mathbb{P}^{n}
are given by homogenized coordinates
[x_{1}:x_{2}:\dots:x_{n+1}]

where at least one

is non-zero, and with the equivalence relation

[x_{1}:x_{2}:\dots:x_{n+1}]\sim[cx_{1}:cx_{2}:\dots:cx_{n+1}],\quad\forall c\neq 0.

Points in the

space
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
are given by affine coordinates
(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
,
and in this paper we equate such points with their standard embedding into projective space, i.e.

(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=[x_{1}:\dots:x_{n}:1].

Thus,
\mathbb{P}^{n}
is the disjoint union of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
and a copy of
\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
“at infinity”, i.e. with
an additional
x_{n+1}=0
coordinate. In particular,
\mathbb{P}^{1}(\mathbb{F}_{q})=\mathbb{F}_{q}\cup\{\infty\}
, where

\infty
denotes the unique point at infinity,
[1:0]
.

We will refer to the two coordinates of the affine plane
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{2}
as
x
and
y
. For a point

P\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{2}
, we will denote its
x,y
coordinates by
P_{x},P_{y}
, respectively. For a point

P\in\mathbb{P}^{2}
, the coordinates
P_{x},P_{y}
will only be defined if it is an affine point, according
to the above notation.

2.3
Rational functions

Rational functions
over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
are quotients
R(X)=P(X)/Q(X)
where
P(X),Q(X)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[X]

are coprime polynomials and
Q
is non-zero. Rational functions form a field, denoted by

\mathbb{F}_{q}(X)
.

Rational functions can be considered as maps from
\mathbb{P}^{1}

to itself, where zeros of
Q
are mapped to
\infty
and are called
poles
of the rational
function, with multiplicity equal to their multiplicity as zeros of
Q
. Depending on whether

\deg(P)-\deg(Q)
is positive, negative, or zero, the point
\infty
is
either a pole of multiplicity
\deg(P)-\deg(Q)
, a zero of multiplicity
\deg(Q)-\deg(P)
, or mapped
to the ratio between the leading coefficients of
P
and
Q
, correspondingly.

The
degree
of
R
is defined as
\deg(R)\coloneqq\max(\deg(P),\deg(Q))
, and is equal
to both the total number of zeros and the total number of poles of
R
, including at
\infty
,
counted with multiplicity.

3
Polynomial decompositions and
\mathsf{FFTrees}

In this section we show that any rational map can be used to decompose a polynomial
into lower degree polynomials, in a way similar to how the squaring map is used in FFTs
(see
Lemma

3.1
). We then define a generalized notion of FFT-like sets of evaluation
points (
Section

3.2
). In the next section we shall instantiate both of
these—rational maps and
\mathsf{FFTrees}
—using elliptic curve groups.

3.1
Polynomial decompositions based on rational functions

Let
V_{d}
be the
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-linear subspace of
\mathbb{F}_{q}[X]
consisting of
polynomials of degree strictly less than
d
. A crucial component in the standard FFT is the decomposition of a polynomial
P(X)=\sum_{i<d}a_{i}X^{i}\in V_{d}
into two polynomials in
V_{d/2}
, one containing the terms of even degree and the other containing the terms of odd degree:

P(X)=\left(\sum_{i<d/2}a_{2i}\left(X^{2}\right)^{i}\right)+X\cdot\left(\sum_{i<d/2}a_{2i+1}\cdot\left(X^{2}\right)^{i}\right)=P_{0}(X^{2})+X\cdot P_{1}(X^{2}).

(1)

The results of this section generalize this partition by replacing
X^{2}
with any rational function. Later, we shall instantiate the results of this section with rational functions coming from projections of isogenies of elliptic curves. We state the decomposition lemma next; its proof appears
in Appendix
B
.

Lemma 3.1

(Decomposition)
.

Let
\psi(X)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}(X)
be a rational map given by:

\psi(X)=\frac{u(X)}{v(X)},

where
u(X),v(X)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[X]
are relatively prime polynomials.
Let
\delta=\deg(\psi)=\max\{\deg(u),\deg(v)\}
.
Let
d
be a multiple of
\delta
.
Then for every
P(X)\in V_{d}
, there is a unique tuple:

\left(P_{0}(X),P_{1}(X),\ldots,P_{\delta-1}(X)\right)\in(V_{d/\delta})^{\delta}

such that:

P(X)=\left(\sum_{i=0}^{\delta-1}X^{i}\cdot P_{i}(\psi(X))\right)\cdot v(X)^{\frac{d}{\delta}-1}.

(2)

The next statement says that, as in the case of the standard FFT,
moving between the two representations of
Eqs.

1
and
2
is done via a set of
\delta
-local invertible linear transformations.

Lemma 3.2

(Locality and invertibility)
.

Let
t\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
. Keeping the notation of the previous lemma,
suppose
\psi^{-1}(t)=\{s_{0},\ldots,s_{\delta-1}\}
is a set of elements of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
of size exactly
\delta
.
Then the transformation

M_{t}:\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\delta}\to\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\delta},\quad M_{t}(P(s_{0}),\ldots,P(s_{\delta-1}))\mapsto(P_{0}(t),\ldots,P_{\delta-1}(t))

(3)

is linear and invertible.

Proof.

The assumption
t\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
and, in particular,
t\neq\infty
, implies
v(s_{j})\neq 0
for each
s_{j}
.
The relationship between the
P(s_{j})
and the
P_{i}(t)
is captured by the following system of linear equations:

P(s_{j})=\left(\sum_{i=0}^{\delta-1}s_{j}^{i}\cdot P_{i}(t)\right)\cdot v(s_{j})^{\frac{d}{\delta}-1}.

Inspection shows that the underlying matrix is a nonsingular Vandermonde matrix with rows scaled by nonzero scalars.
∎

For the rest of this paper, we will focus on the
\delta=2
case, although
everything generalizes to larger
\delta
. We briefly instantiate the above lemmas in this case,
to expose the similarity to the classical FFT.

Let
\psi(X)
be a degree
2
rational function. Suppose
d
is even.
Fix any
P(X)\in V_{d}
, and consider the two polynomials
P_{0}(X),P_{1}(X)
given by
Lemma

3.1
.
Then we have the following decomposition that resembles the classical FFT case of
Eq.

1
:

P(X)=\left(P_{0}(\psi(X))+XP_{1}(\psi(X))\right)\cdot(v(X))^{\frac{d}{2}-1},

and so, for any
s\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
:

\displaystyle P(s)=\left(P_{0}(\psi(s))+sP_{1}(\psi(s))\right)\cdot(v(s))^{\frac{d}{2}-1}.

(4)

Let
s_{0},s_{1},t\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
be such that
\psi(s_{0})=\psi(s_{1})=t
with

s_{0}\neq s_{1}
. Then Lemma
3.2
implies that the values
P(s_{0}),P(s_{1})
determine
P_{0}(t),P_{1}(t)

and vice versa (this uses the fact that
s_{0}\neq s_{1}
), and the transformation between the two pairs of values
is computed by multpilication by an invertible
2\times 2
matrix, whose coefficients depend only on
the values of
s_{0},s_{1},v(s_{0})
, and
v(s_{1})
.

Thus, when we have a degree 2 rational function
\psi
that is
2
-to-
1
from
S

to
\psi(S)=T
, finding evaluations of a polynomial
P(X)
at the points of
S
is equivalent to finding
evaluations of
P_{0}(X)
and
P_{1}(X)
at the points of
T
.

3.2
\mathsf{FFTrees}

We now define
\mathsf{FFTrees}
, a structure abstracting out relevant properties of evaluation sets and maps between them, which suffice to simulate
an FFT-like algorithm.

Definition 3.3

(
\mathsf{FFTrees}
)
.

Let
q
be a prime power, and let
{\sf{k}}
be an integer.
An
\mathsf{FFTree}
over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
of
depth

{\sf{k}}

is a collection of subsets
L^{(0)},L^{(1)}\ldots,L^{({\sf{k}})}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}
along
with degree
2
rational functions
\psi^{(i)}(X)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}(X)
such that:

1.

|L^{(i)}|=2^{{\sf{k}}-i}
.

2.

\psi^{(i)}(L^{(i)})=L^{(i+1)}
(and so
\psi^{(i)}
is a
2
-to-
1
map from
L^{(i)}
to
L^{(i+1)}
).

Let
\mathcal{F}
denote the rooted, layered, binary tree, whose layers are indexed by
i\in\{0,1,\ldots,{\sf{k}}\}
. The set of vertices in layer
i
is
L^{(i)}
. The root
of
\mathcal{F}
is the unique element of
L^{({\sf{k}})}
.
The leaves of
\mathcal{F}
are all the vertices in
L^{(0)}
.
For each
i<{\sf{k}}
, the parent of the vertex
s\in L^{(i)}
of the
i
-th layer is the vertex
\psi^{(i)}(s)\in L^{(i+1)}
of the
(i+1)
st layer.

Because of the decomposition lemma, evaluations of a polynomial on
L^{(i)}
can be deduced from evaluations of 2 related lower degree polynomials on
L^{(i+1)}
, and this serves as the basis for fast “divide and conquer” algorithms.

Our eventual use of
\mathsf{FFTrees}
will be as follows. We will first fix an
\mathsf{FFTree}
over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. We will use
L
to denote
L^{(0)}
.
Let
{\sf{K}}=|L|=2^{{\sf{k}}}
.
Then for any
n\leq{\sf{K}}
, polynomials of degree
<n

will be represented by evaluations at specific subsets of
L
of size
O(n)
.
The
\mathsf{FFTree}
structure will then enable fast algorithms for working with these representations.

Thus any given
\mathsf{FFTree}
will be useful for working with polynomials of degree up to
2^{{\sf{k}}}-1
.
Therefore it is interesting to find
\mathsf{FFTrees}
with as large depth
{\sf{k}}
as possible.

In the next section, we use elliptic curves to show the existence
of
\mathsf{FFTrees}
over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
with depth
\Omega(\log q)
.

4

from Elliptic Curves

In this section we prove the existence of
\mathsf{FFTrees}
of depth
\Omega(\log q)
in any finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. Specifically, we show that there exist
\mathsf{FFTrees}
over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
whose base set

has size
\Omega(\sqrt{q})
.
We start by recounting the necessary definitions and results regarding elliptic curves.
In
Section

4.2
we then prove our main results about existence of

using rational

4.1
Background on elliptic curves and isogenies

In this subsection we provide a brief overview of the necessary definitions and theorems regarding
elliptic curves.
Further details and proofs can be found in most basic texts on the subject. Except where
specifically noted, all results can be found in
[
Sil09
]
or
[
Was08
]
.

4.1.1
Elliptic curve in Weierstrass form

An
elliptic curve

E
is a smooth, projective, algebraic curve of genus 1, with a special
marked point
O
, defined over a field. In this paper all curves will be defined over the finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.
Every elliptic curve can be presented in
extended Weierstrass form

as the set of planar points
(x,y)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{2}
satisfying a cubic equation

Y^{2}+a_{1}XY+a_{3}Y=X^{3}+a_{2}X^{2}+a_{4}X+a_{6}

(5)

or equivalently

F(X,Y):=Y^{2}+a_{1}XY+a_{3}Y-X^{3}-a_{2}X^{2}-a_{4}X-a_{6}=0

(6)

parameterized by
a_{1},a_{2},a_{3},a_{4},a_{6}
,
together with the marked point
O=[0:1:0]\in\mathbb{P}^{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
, called its
point at infinity
.

4.1.2
The group law

The points of an elliptic curve
E
form an abelian group, in which
O
is the neutral element, and
any three distinct points
P,Q,R\in E
satisfy
P+Q+R=O
iff they are colinear.
If
P=Q\neq R
, the condition
is that the tangent to
E
at
P
passes through
R
, and if
P=Q=R
the condition is that the tangent
at
P
to
E
is doubly tangent at the point.

Lines passing through
O
are either the line at
infinity (which is doubly tangent to
E
at
O
), or lines of the form
X=c
. Thus
P+Q=O
,
i.e.
P=-Q
, iff their coordinates satisfy
P_{x}=Q_{x}
and
P_{y}\neq Q_{y}
; or
P=Q\neq O
and the line
X=P_{x}

is tangent to
E
at
P
; or
P=Q=O
. Note that in both affine cases, we also have

Q_{y}=-a_{1}P_{x}-a_{3}-P_{y}
, since
P_{y},Q_{y}
are the two (not necessarily distinct) roots of
a monic quadratic in
y
with linear coefficient
a_{1}P_{x}+a_{3}
.

4.1.3
Isogenies and
x
-projection

For a curve
E
in extended Weierstrass form, let
\pi:E\to\mathbb{P}^{1}
denote the projection to the
x
-coordinate,
defined by
\pi(O)=\infty\in\mathbb{P}^{1}
and
\pi(P)=P_{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
for
P\in E\setminus\{O\}
.
Additionally,
as noted in
Section

4.1.2
, for any
P,Q\in E
,
\pi(P)=\pi(Q)
if and only if
P=\pm Q
,
thus the preimages
\pi^{-1}(\pi(P))=\{\pm P\}
are either sets of size two, or
a singleton
\{P\}
when
2P=O
. In particular, it follows that for any subset
C\subset E

such that
C
is disjoint from
-C=\{-P:P\in C\}
, the map
\left.\pi\right|_{C}

is
1
-to-
1
from
C
to
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.

Let
E,E^{\prime}
be elliptic curves over the same field. An
isogeny
between the curves is a
rational map
\phi:E\to E^{\prime}
satisfying
\phi(O)=O^{\prime}
, where
O^{\prime}
is the neutral element of
E^{\prime}
.
We follow
[
Was08
, Chapters 2.9, 12.2]
to give an algebraic, rather than geometric, description
of isogenies. When
E,E^{\prime}
are in extended Weierstrass form,
\phi
can be expressed in a

standard form
:

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
\phi:E\to E^{\prime}
be an isogeny between two curves in extended Weierstrass form. Then, in coordinates,
we may write

\phi(x,y)=(\psi(x),\xi(x,y)),

where
\psi:\mathbb{P}^{1}\to\mathbb{P}^{1}
is a rational function. Equivalently, if
\pi:E\to\mathbb{P}^{1},\pi^{\prime}:E^{\prime}\to\mathbb{P}^{1}

are the
x
-projection maps in each curve, then there exists a unique
rational function
\psi
such that the diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces E}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces E^{\prime}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{P}^{1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{P}^{1}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\phi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi^{\prime}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\psi}

is commutative.

This fact appears to be folklore, and is most commonly discussed only in the special case of curves
in
short
Weierstrass form
E:y^{2}=x^{3}+Ax+B
, where
\xi(x,y)
can also be expressed as

y
times a rational function—see
[
Was08
, Chapter 2.9]
for a discussion of this case.
When focusing only on the
x
-coordinate, the same proof is valid also for the extended Weierstrass form.
For completeness, a full proof of this fact is included in
Section

C.1
.

Definition 4.2
.

Let
\phi:E\to E^{\prime}
be an isogeny between two curves in extended Weierstrass form,
and let
\psi
be as in
Proposition

4.1
. We define
\deg\phi\coloneqq\deg\psi
,
i.e. the degree of the isogeny
\phi
is defined to be equal to the degree of
\psi
as a rational
function.
The isogeny
\phi
is called
separable
if the derivative (in
x
) of
\psi
is not identically zero.

The term
d
-isogeny
is shorthand for degree
d
isogeny.

An important property of isogenies is that they are also group homomorphisms, with finite kernels.
If
\phi
is separable, then
|\ker\phi|=\deg\phi
.
The converse is also true, and is a crucial part of our
construction:

Proposition 4.3

(
[
Sil09
, III.4.12]
)
.

Let
E
be an elliptic curve and let
H<E
be a finite subgroup of
E
. There is a unique
elliptic curve
E^{\prime}
and a separable
|H|
-isogeny
\phi:E\to E^{\prime}
with
\ker\phi=H
.

See also
[
Vél71
]
for an explicit construction of such isogenies. We will apply the proposition
for groups
H
with
|H|=2
, but all our results generalize to larger
H
. In this case

\phi
is 2-isogeny, meaning
\psi
is a degree 2 rational function.

4.1.4
Group size and structure

The group
E
is abelian, and it is always of rank at most 2, i.e. it is isomorphic to a product of
at most 2 cyclic groups

E\simeq{\mathbb{Z}}/m_{1}{\mathbb{Z}}\times{\mathbb{Z}}/m_{2}{\mathbb{Z}}

with
m_{1}\mid m_{2}
and
m_{1}\cdot m_{2}=|E|
.

Hasse’s theorem states that for every elliptic curve
E
, the order of the group
|E|

belongs to a range
of length
4\sqrt{q}
centered at
q+1
, that is,

q-2\sqrt{q}+1\leq|E|\leq q+2\sqrt{q}+1.

By a theorem of Deuring
[
Deu41
]
, any number in this range is indeed attainable as the size
of an elliptic curve, in the case where

is prime. Waterhouse
[

, Theorem 4.1]
provides
the complete characterization of achievable sizes for the prime power case.
We will require a much weaker form, about possible factors of
|E|
. The following is the
simplest case of Waterhouse’s theorem:

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
N=q+1-t
be an integer such that
|t|\leq 2\sqrt{q}
and
t
is coprime to
q
.
Then there exists an elliptic curve

with

.

4.2
An FFT-friendly sequence of rational maps coming from elliptic curves

As noted in
Section

3
, the depth (or size) of an

limits the degrees of the
polynomials which it can be used to evaluate. Thus, we would like to find the largest
\mathsf{FFTree}

possible: if smaller degrees are sufficient, we can always use a subtree instead. We will denote by

\widehat{{\sf{K}}}_{q}
the largest possible size of an

which can be obtained by our method. More
rigorously, we define

Definition 4.5
.

Let

be a prime power. Define
\widehat{{\sf{K}}}_{q}
to be the largest power of 2 such that there exists an
elliptic curve

defined over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
whose size satisfies
\widehat{{\sf{K}}}_{q}\mid|E|
and
|E|>2\widehat{{\sf{K}}}_{q}
.

We claim that
\widehat{{\sf{K}}}_{q}
is in fact fairly large with respect to

:

Claim 4.6

Let
q\geq 7
be a prime power. Then
\widehat{{\sf{K}}}_{q}>\sqrt{q}
. Equivalently,
for any
{\sf{K}}=2^{\sf{k}}\leq 2\sqrt{q}
,
there exists an elliptic curve
E
defined over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
with
{\sf{K}}\mid|E|
and
|E|>2{\sf{K}}
.
If

is even, then
\widehat{{\sf{K}}}_{q}\geq\frac{q}{4}
.

Before we prove

, having defined and bounded

, we are now able to
precisely state the main theorem of this section:

Theorem 4.7

(Existence of large
\mathsf{FFTrees}
)

Let

be a prime power, and let the integer
{\sf{k}}
be such that
{\sf{K}}=2^{\sf{k}}\leq\widehat{{\sf{K}}}_{q}
; in particular,

{\sf{K}}
to be any power of two up to

over

with depth
{\sf{k}}
.

We now proceed with building up the infrastructure towards proving
Theorem

4.7
.

Proof of
4.6
.

We will ignore at first the condition

.
By

4.4

Since the closed interval

{\sf{K}}
.
Note that at least one of

Finally, to assert

Remark 4.12
.

Not to miss the forest for the trees, we clarify some features of this

5
Representing polynomials via
\mathsf{FFTrees}

In this section, we show how to use
\mathsf{FFTrees}
to
get a nice representation for polynomials that supports
fast operations.

We begin by fixing an
\mathsf{FFTree}
for the rest of this section.
Thus we have sets
L^{(0)},L^{(1)},\ldots,L^{({\sf{k}})}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}
,
and degree-
2
rational functions
\psi^{(i)}:L^{(i)}\to L^{(i+1)}
.
We let
L=L^{(0)}
and let
{\sf{K}}=|L|=2^{\sf{k}}
.
Also recall the associated binary tree
\mathcal{F}
whose set of leaves is
L
.

All the data structures and algorithms for polynomials
that we describe will be in the context of this
\mathsf{FFTree}
.
While the exact details of how this
\mathsf{FFTree}
is obtained are not important for anything in this section,
it will be helpful to recall the parameters of
\mathsf{FFTrees}
that are achievable via
Theorem

4.7
.

5.1
Evaluation tables

We shall represent polynomials by their evaluations on various special sets of points, so we
introduce a special notation that will emphasize the sets of evaluation points used. Concretely, an
evaluation table
is specified by the following data:

•

a set
S\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}
,

•

a function
f:S\to\mathbb{F}_{q}
.

We denote the associated evaluation table by
\left\langle{f\wr S}\right\rangle
, pronounced “
f
on
S
”.

For a polynomial or rational function
P(X)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}(X)
with
P(X)
defined on
S
,
we define the associated evaluation table

\left\langle{P\wr S}\right\rangle
to be the evaluation table
\left\langle{\left.P\right|_{S}\wr S}\right\rangle
, where

\left.P\right|_{S}
is the function from
S
to
\mathbb{F}_{q}
given by evaluation of
P
. Looking ahead,
we shall use evaluation tables for operations like

•

Adding, multiplying and dividing, as in this example: given
\left\langle{f\wr S}\right\rangle
,
\left\langle{g\wr S}\right\rangle
,
\left\langle{h\wr S}\right\rangle
,
\left\langle{P\wr S}\right\rangle
for some
P(X)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[X]
, we can compute
\left\langle{\frac{f+P(X)g}{h}\wr S}\right\rangle
.

•

Restricting an evaluation table
\left\langle{f\wr S}\right\rangle
to a subset
S_{0}\subseteq S
,
denoting the restricted table by
\left\langle{f\wr S_{0}}\right\rangle

•

Partitioning a set
S
into
S=S_{0}\cup S_{1}
, and “splitting”
\left\langle{f\wr S}\right\rangle
into
\left\langle{f_{0}\wr S_{0}}\right\rangle
and
\left\langle{f_{1}\wr S_{1}}\right\rangle
, as well as doing the inverse operation of forming the combined evaluation table
\left\langle{f\wr S}\right\rangle=\left\langle{f_{0}\wr S_{0}}\right\rangle\cup\left\langle{f_{1}\wr S_{1}}\right\rangle,

where
f:S\to\mathbb{F}_{q}
is given by:

\left.f\right|_{S_{0}}=f_{0},

\left.f\right|_{S_{1}}=f_{1}.

5.2
Basic sets and moieties

We now identify some important subsets of
L
.

Definition 5.1

(Basic sets)
.

We define a
basic
set to be a subset
S
of
L

which is the set of all descendants in
L
of some vertex of
\mathcal{F}
.

Equivalently, it is a set of size
2^{a}
for some integer

,
such that if we let
g
denote the
composed function
\psi^{(a-1)}\circ\psi^{(a-2)}\circ\cdots\circ\psi^{(1)}\circ\psi^{(0)}

S=g^{-1}(u)

We have the following important property of basic sets: they can be
partitioned into two basic sets of equal size.

.

Any basic set
S
of size

can be partitioned to two basic sets

, where each

has size
2^{a-1}
.

The proof is immediate from

and
5.1
: if

is the set of all descendants in
L
of the vertex
u\in\mathcal{F}
,
then letting
\{u_{0},u_{1}\}
be the children of
u

S_{i}
to be the set of all descendants in
L
of
u_{i}
.
We shall call
S_{0}
and
S_{1}
the

of
S
. Note that the two moieties are equivalent,
and can be labeled
S_{0},S_{1}
or
S_{1},S_{0}
interchangeably.

The following property of sets and polynomials
with respect to moieties of basic sets will also prove to be important in the paper, especially for
algorithms related to modular arithmetic:

Definition 5.3
.

Let
S
be a basic set, and let

be an arbitrary set. We say

from

is half-disjoint from

.

We now consider representations of polynomials by evaluation tables.
Since nonzero polynomials of degree
<n

immediately get the following fundamental fact.
For distinct polynomials

and a set

with

, we have

\left\langle{P\wr S}\right\rangle\neq\left\langle{Q\wr S}\right\rangle.

Thus, for a fixed set

with
|S|=n
,

\left\langle{P\wr S}\right\rangle
is a way of representing a polynomial

with

<n

to be a basic set.

We now define a standard representation for polynomials (in the context of the fixed
\mathsf{FFTree}

For each

For a polynomial

For a polynomial

, we define

to be the

This standard representation will be our data structure for representing polynomials. In the next section, we show how the
\mathsf{FFTree}

6
Fast polynomial algorithms from
\mathsf{FFTrees}

As in the previous section, we assume that we have fixed an
\mathsf{FFTree}
.
Again, the exact details of how this
\mathsf{FFTree}
is obtained is not important for anything in this section, but it will be helpful to recall the parameters
of
\mathsf{FFTrees}
that are achievable via
Theorem

4.7
.

In this section we give a number of fast algorithms for working with
polynomials
P(X)
represented using evaluation tables
\left\langle{P\wr S}\right\rangle
, where

S
is a basic set. Inspection will reveal that nearly all of these algorithms can
be converted to arithmetic circuits over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
with constant fan-in and size that matches the proclaimed
running time (the only exception is the computation of polynomial degree, which outputs an integer, not a
field element). Thus, henceforth when we say an algorithm “runs in time
t(n)
” we shall allow it to receive
advice that will be explicitly stated,
and also mean that it can be computed by an arithmetic circuit over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
with
t(n)
gates (and constant fan-in).
In particular, we assume each basic arithmetic operation (
+,-,\times,/
) over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
has constant computational
cost. While the algorithms of this section use division of elements in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
for clarity, by inspecting the
details it can be seen that they can be reformulated to avoid division by taking advice in a different form
(for example, taking
\left\langle{\frac{1}{f}\wr S}\right\rangle
as advice instead of
\left\langle{f\wr S}\right\rangle
as advice).

Algorithmic notations

We use the notation
\mathsf{ALG}_{P_{1},P_{2},\ldots}(I_{1},I_{2},\ldots)
for our algorithms/circuits.
\mathsf{ALG}
is the name of
the algorithm, the subscript elements
P_{1},P_{2},\ldots
denote fixed parameters that affect
constants of the algorithm/circuit and the inputs
(I_{1},I_{2},\ldots)
are given inside the parenthesis, and
are variables.
In particular, any data which depends only on
P_{1},P_{2},\ldots
can be assumed to be included
as part of the circuit, or given by a precomputation advice, and our running times exclude the time required
to obtain these parameters and constants. Furthermore,
q
and the
\mathsf{FFTree}
that we fixed are always assumed
to be part of the fixed parameters of the algorithm.

Directory of algorithms

Below we give a list of the algorithms in this section.

1.

\mathsf{EXTEND}_{S,S^{\prime}}
which does low degree extension of polynomial evaluations
from a basic set
S
to another basic set
S^{\prime}
.
\mathsf{EXTEND}
is the basis for all the remaining algorithms
in this section.

2.

\mathsf{MULT}
, which multiplies polynomials in the new representation (allowing for the possibility of
the degree growing). Addition is trivially done in linear time so we do not explicitly describe it.

3.

\mathsf{MEXTEND}
, a version of
\mathsf{EXTEND}
for monic polynomials of known, fixed degree.

4.

\mathsf{DEGREE}
, which computes the degree of a polynomial given in the new representation.

5.

\mathsf{REDC}
, which performs Montgomery reduction—a technical operation that helps with the remaining operations.

6.

\mathsf{MOD}
, which performs modular reduction, reducing a given polynomial in the new representation modulo a fixed polynomial.

7.

\mathsf{DIV}
, which finds the quotient after division by a fixed polynomial.

8.

\mathsf{ENTER}
and
\mathsf{EXIT}
, which convert between the new representation and the standard monomial representation.

9.

\mathsf{CRT}
which computes one direction of the Chinese Remainder Theorem, constructing a polynomial
from its residues modulo two fixed and relatively prime polynomials. (The other direction of the CRT
can be done by
\mathsf{MOD}
.)

6.1
Low degree extension

Our first primitive extends the evaluation of
P
from one basic set to another basic set of the same size in time
O(n\log n)
(i.e., via an arithmetic circuit over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
with constant fan-in and
O(n\log n)
gates). In other words, the algorithm performs Reed–Solomon encoding in quasi-linear time, as long as the message is provided by the evaluation of
P
on a basic set, and is encoded by evaluating
P
on a constant collection of basic sets. Such low-degree extensions are often used to produce interactive proofs and interactive oracle proofs.

Theorem 6.1

(Low-degree extension)
.

For any two basic sets
S,S^{\prime}\subset\mathbb{F}_{q}
with
|S|=|S^{\prime}|=n
, there is
an algorithm that runs in time

O(n\log n)
, denoted
\mathsf{EXTEND}_{S,S^{\prime}}
, which when given as input:

•

\left\langle{P\wr S}\right\rangle
, where
P(X)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[X]
with
\deg(P)<n
,

outputs
\left\langle{P\wr S^{\prime}}\right\rangle
.

For the proof of this theorem (and only for this proof) we need a generalization of basic sets:

Definition 6.2

(
i
-basic sets)
.

We define an
i
-
basic
set to be a subset
S
of
L^{(i)}

which is the set of all descendants in
L^{(i)}
of some vertex of
\mathcal{F}
.

Equivalently, an
i
-basic set is a subset
S
of
L^{(i)}

of size
2^{a}
for some integer

, such that if we let

denote the function

\psi^{(a+i-1)}\circ\psi^{(a+i-2)}\circ\cdots\circ\psi^{(i+1)}\circ\psi^{(i)},

then
S=g^{-1}(u)
for some
u\in L^{(a+i)}
.

Notice that
0
-basic sets are simply basic sets per
Definition

5.1
. In our proof, stated next, we shall use the property that
for every
i
-basic set
S
,

\psi^{(i)}(S)
is an
(i+1)
-basic set
T
of size
|S|/2
, and we say
T

lies above

S
and is
induced by

\psi^{(i)}
.

Proof of
Theorem

6.1
.

We give a more general algorithm
\mathsf{EXTEND}_{S,S^{\prime},i}
to solve
the analogous extension problem where

and
S^{\prime}
are
i
-basic sets with

. The algorithm
\mathsf{EXTEND}_{S,S^{\prime}}
claimed in

is obtained by fixing

\mathsf{EXTEND}_{S,S^{\prime}}(\left\langle{\pi\wr S}\right\rangle)=\mathsf{EXTEND}_{S,S^{\prime},0}(\left\langle{\pi\wr S}\right\rangle)
.

The
\mathsf{EXTEND}_{S,S^{\prime},i}
algorithm uses the map
\psi^{(i)}
to reduce the extension problem for
i
-basic sets of size

to two analogous extension problems for
(i+1)
-basic sets of
size
n/2
,
and then proceeds recursively, by induction on

.

Let
T=\psi^{(i)}(S)
,
T^{\prime}=\psi^{(i)}(S^{\prime})
be the
(i+1)
-basic sets above
S,S^{\prime}
, respectively, which are induced by
\psi^{(i)}
.
By
Lemma

, there are unique polynomials

P_{0}(X),P_{1}(X)
of degree
<n/2
with:

\displaystyle P(X)=\left(P_{0}(\psi^{(i)}(X))+XP_{1}(\psi^{(i)}(X))\right)(v^{(i)}(X))^{\frac{n}{2}-1}.

(8)

\mathsf{EXTEND}_{S,S^{\prime},i}
first computes
\left\langle{P_{0}\wr T}\right\rangle
and
\left\langle{P_{1}\wr T}\right\rangle
.
(Since
|T|=n/2
, these uniquely determine
P_{0}(X)
and
P_{1}(X)
).
Then it runs
\mathsf{EXTEND}_{T,T^{\prime},i+1}
on this to get

\left\langle{P_{0}\wr T^{\prime}}\right\rangle
and
\left\langle{P_{1}\wr T^{\prime}}\right\rangle
, and combines the results
to get
\left\langle{P\wr S^{\prime}}\right\rangle
.

The algorithm takes as advice
\left\langle{(v^{(i)}(X))^{\frac{n}{2}-1}\wr S}\right\rangle
, which can be precomputed since it only depends on
S
and
\psi^{(i)}
, along with
whatever advice is needed in the recursive calls.

Algorithm
\mathsf{EXTEND}_{S,S^{\prime},i}
:

Input:

\left\langle{\pi\wr S}\right\rangle

1.

If
n=1
(recall that

), then

(a)

Let
S=\{s\}
and
S^{\prime}=\{s^{\prime}\}
.

(b)

Define

\pi^{\prime}:S^{\prime}\to\mathbb{F}_{q}

by

\pi^{\prime}(s^{\prime})=\pi(s)
.

(c)

Return
\left\langle{\pi^{\prime}\wr S^{\prime}}\right\rangle
.

2.

Let
T=\psi^{(i)}(S),T^{\prime}=\psi^{(i)}(S^{\prime})
be the sets that lie above
S
and
S^{\prime}
respectively.

3.

For each

(a)

Define

to be the
\psi^{(i)}

t
(noticing they are distinct because

is a basic set)

(b)

Compute
(\pi_{0}(t),\pi_{1}(t))=M_{t}(\pi(s_{0}),\pi(s_{1})))
where

is defined in
Eq.

3
.

4.

Form the evaluation tables
\left\langle{\pi_{0}\wr T}\right\rangle
and
\left\langle{\pi_{1}\wr T}\right\rangle

5.

Let
\left\langle{\pi_{0}^{\prime}\wr T^{\prime}}\right\rangle

\left\langle{\pi_{1}^{\prime}\wr T^{\prime}}\right\rangle
be the evaluation tables

\mathsf{EXTEND}_{T,T^{\prime},i+1}(\left\langle{\pi_{0}\wr T}\right\rangle)

\mathsf{EXTEND}_{T,T^{\prime},i+1}(\left\langle{\pi_{1}\wr T}\right\rangle)

6.

For each

, define

\displaystyle\pi^{\prime}(s^{\prime})=\left(\pi_{0}^{\prime}(\psi^{(i)}(s^{\prime}))+s^{\prime}\cdot\pi_{1}^{\prime}(\psi^{(i)}(s^{\prime}))\right)v^{(i)}(s^{\prime})^{\frac{n}{2}-1}.

(9)

\left\langle{\pi^{\prime}\wr S^{\prime}}\right\rangle
.

Correctness:

Suppose
P(X)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[X]
is a polynomial of degree
<n
.
We want to show that
\mathsf{EXTEND}_{S,S^{\prime},i}(\left\langle{P\wr S}\right\rangle)
returns
\left\langle{P\wr S^{\prime}}\right\rangle

The main claim is that when the input
\left\langle{\pi\wr S}\right\rangle

is
\left\langle{P\wr S}\right\rangle
, the functions

\pi_{0},\pi_{1}:T\to\mathbb{F}_{q}

\left\langle{\pi_{0}\wr T}\right\rangle=\left\langle{P_{0}\wr T}\right\rangle,

\left\langle{\pi_{1}\wr T}\right\rangle=\left\langle{P_{1}\wr T}\right\rangle,

are as in Equation (

This is trivially correct for

Take any

.
Using the fact that

, and the definition of

\pi_{0}(t)=P_{0}(t)

\pi_{1}(t)=P_{1}(t)

Thus

\left\langle{\pi_{0}\wr T}\right\rangle=\left\langle{P_{0}\wr T}\right\rangle,

\left\langle{\pi_{1}\wr T}\right\rangle=\left\langle{P_{1}\wr T}\right\rangle.

By induction on

, we conclude that
\mathsf{EXTEND}_{T,T^{\prime},i+1}

on
\left\langle{P_{0}\wr T}\right\rangle
and
\left\langle{P_{1}\wr T}\right\rangle

\left\langle{P_{0}\wr T^{\prime}}\right\rangle

\left\langle{P_{1}\wr T^{\prime}}\right\rangle

Thus

\left\langle{\pi_{0}^{\prime}\wr T^{\prime}}\right\rangle=\left\langle{P_{0}\wr T^{\prime}}\right\rangle,

\left\langle{\pi_{1}^{\prime}\wr T^{\prime}}\right\rangle=\left\langle{P_{1}\wr T^{\prime}}\right\rangle,

Using this along with Equations (

\left\langle{\pi^{\prime}\wr S^{\prime}}\right\rangle=\left\langle{P\wr S^{\prime}}\right\rangle,

as desired. This completes the proof of correctness.

Running time:

By inspection, we see that our algorithm uses

arithmetic operations over

to reduce an instance of
\mathsf{EXTEND}
of size

to two instances of size

.
(Recall that the algorithm fixes various constants, like

and the values of the matrix

Thus the total running time

of this algorithm
satisfies the recursion:

We conclude the running time (or circuit size) is

and this completes our proof.
∎

Remark 6.3
.

The
\mathsf{EXTEND}
algorithm as described is defined for

which are basic sets of the same
size in the same

. However, we note that it works just as well when
S,S^{\prime}
are
basic sets of the same size from two different

in the same

(see also

).

6.2
Multiplication

We give a quick application of the previous algorithm to multiplication of polynomials in the new representation.

Theorem 6.4

(Multiplication)
.

Let
S
be a basic set with
|S|=n
.
Let
S_{0}\subseteq S
be a moiety of
S
.

There is an algorithm
\mathsf{MULT}_{S,S_{0}}
, which when given as input:

•

\left\langle{P\wr S_{0}}\right\rangle
, where
P(X)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[X]
with
\deg(P)<n/2
, and

•

\left\langle{Q\wr S_{0}}\right\rangle
, where
Q(X)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[X]
with
\deg(Q)<n/2
,

runs in time

O(n\log n)

and computes
\left\langle{P\cdot Q\wr S}\right\rangle
.

Proof.

The algorithm is basically immediate given
\mathsf{EXTEND}
.
Let
S_{1}
be the other moiety of
S
.
We first run
\mathsf{EXTEND}_{S_{0},S_{1}}
on
\left\langle{P\wr S_{0}}\right\rangle
and
\left\langle{Q\wr S_{0}}\right\rangle

to get
\left\langle{P\wr S_{1}}\right\rangle
and
\left\langle{Q\wr S_{1}}\right\rangle
. Combining these, we get

\left\langle{P\wr S}\right\rangle
and
\left\langle{Q\wr S}\right\rangle
, and by pointwise multiplication we get

\left\langle{P\cdot Q\wr S}\right\rangle
. The running time comes from two invocations of
\mathsf{EXTEND}

and
O(n)
other operations, and is thus
O(n\log n)
.
∎

6.3
Monic polynomial extension

As noted before, for a set
S
of size
n
, the linear space of all possible evaluation tables
\left\langle{P\wr S}\right\rangle

is in one-to-one correspondence with the space of all polynomials
P(X)
of degree
<n
. It is also
interesting to note that these spaces are in one-to-one correspondence with the set of all
monic

polynomials of degree
exactly

n
. In fact, if
Z(X)
is the vanishing polynomial of
S
,
and
P(X),Q(X)
are polynomials with
\deg(Q)<n=\deg(P)
and
P
is monic, then

\left\langle{P\wr S}\right\rangle=\left\langle{Q\wr S}\right\rangle
if and only if
P(X)=Q(X)+Z(X)
.

This property allows us to easily adapt the
\mathsf{EXTEND}
algorithm into an extension algorithm for
monic polynomials, which we call
\mathsf{MEXTEND}
.

Theorem 6.5

(Monic polynomial extension)
.

For any two basic sets
S,S^{\prime}\subset\mathbb{F}_{q}
with
|S|=|S^{\prime}|=n
, there is
an algorithm that runs in time

O(n\log n)
, denoted
\mathsf{MEXTEND}_{S,S^{\prime}}
, which when given as input:

•

\left\langle{P\wr S}\right\rangle
, where
P(X)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[X]
is monic with
\deg(P)=n
,

outputs
\left\langle{P\wr S^{\prime}}\right\rangle
.

Proof.

Let
Z(X)
be the vanishing polynomial of
S
.
As noted above, for such polynomials
P(X)
, we have
\left\langle{P\wr S}\right\rangle=\left\langle{P-Z\wr S}\right\rangle
, and

\deg(P(X)-Z(X))<n
. By the properties of
\mathsf{EXTEND}
it thus follows that

\mathsf{EXTEND}_{S,S^{\prime}}(\left\langle{P\wr S}\right\rangle)=\mathsf{EXTEND}_{S,S^{\prime}}(\left\langle{P-Z\wr S}\right\rangle)=\left\langle{P-Z\wr S^{\prime}}\right\rangle

and adding
\left\langle{Z\wr S^{\prime}}\right\rangle
pointwise yields

\mathsf{MEXTEND}_{S,S^{\prime}}(\left\langle{P\wr S}\right\rangle)\coloneqq\mathsf{EXTEND}_{S,S^{\prime}}(\left\langle{P\wr S}\right\rangle)+\left\langle{Z\wr S^{\prime}}\right\rangle=\left\langle{P\wr S^{\prime}}\right\rangle

as needed. The algorithm takes
\left\langle{Z\wr S^{\prime}}\right\rangle
as advice, calls
\mathsf{EXTEND}
once and does an additional

O(n)
operations, thus runs in time
O(n\log n)
.
∎

This algorithm can replace
\mathsf{EXTEND}
in applications where the polynomials are known to be monic
and of known degrees, with more efficient run times.
For example, it can be used to multiply two monic polynomials of degree
n/2
, represented as
evaluation tables on a set of size
n/2
, with the product similarly being a monic polynomial of degree
n
,
represented as an evaluation table on a set of size
n
. If we were instead to multiply such polynomials
using the standard
\mathsf{EXTEND}
algorithm, we would have to represent each polynomial by its values on a
set of size
n
, and their product on a set of size
2n
, and use extensions from
n
to
2n

instead of extensions from
n/2
to
n
, which would more than double the required run-time.

6.4
Degree Computation

The next operation we describe is that of computing the degree of a polynomial
P
represented by
its evaluation on a basic set.

Theorem 6.6

(Degree Computation)
.

Let
S
be a basic set of size
|S|=n
.
There is an algorithm
\mathsf{DEGREE}_{S}
, which when given as input:

•

\left\langle{P\wr S}\right\rangle
, where
P(X)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[X]
with
\deg(P)<n
,

runs in time

O(n\log n)

and
computes
\deg(P)
.

Proof.

Let
S_{0},S_{1}
be the moieties of
S
, and let

Z_{0}(X)
be the vanishing polynomial of
S_{0}
.
The algorithm we give will assume that
\left\langle{Z_{0}\wr S_{1}}\right\rangle
is given as advice: this is a fixed
precomputation that depends only on
S
.

Algorithm
\mathsf{DEGREE}_{S}
:

Input:
An evaluation table
\left\langle{\pi\wr S}\right\rangle

1.

If
|S|=1
with
S=\{s\}
, then

•

if
\pi(s)\neq 0
return
0
; else, return
-\infty
.

2.

Let
\left\langle{g\wr S_{1}}\right\rangle=\mathsf{EXTEND}_{S_{0},S_{1}}\left(\left\langle{\pi\wr S_{0}}\right\rangle\right)
.

3.

If
\left\langle{g\wr S_{1}}\right\rangle=\left\langle{\pi\wr S_{1}}\right\rangle
, then return
\mathsf{DEGREE}_{S_{1}}(\left\langle{\pi\wr S_{1}}\right\rangle)
.

4.

Otherwise, using
\left\langle{\pi\wr S_{1}}\right\rangle
,
\left\langle{g\wr S_{1}}\right\rangle
and

\left\langle{Z_{0}\wr S_{1}}\right\rangle
, compute:

\left\langle{\frac{\pi-g}{Z_{0}}\wr S_{1}}\right\rangle,

and return

\frac{n}{2}+\mathsf{DEGREE}_{S_{1}}\left(\left\langle{\frac{\pi-g}{Z_{0}}\wr S_{1}}\right\rangle\right).

Correctness:

The case
n=1
is trivial, and when
P
is the zero polynomial notice by inspection the result will be
-\infty
, as required.

Suppose
n>1
.
Let
P(X)
be a polynomial with
0\leq\deg(P)<n
.
Let us consider the execution of the above algorithm on input
\left\langle{P\wr S}\right\rangle
.

•

Case 1:

\deg(P)<n/2
. Then by the defining property of
\mathsf{EXTEND}_{S_{0},S_{1}}
,
we have that

\mathsf{EXTEND}_{S_{0},S_{1}}\left(\left\langle{P\wr S_{0}}\right\rangle\right)=\left\langle{P\wr S_{1}}\right\rangle.

Thus in the execution of the algorithm, we will have
\left\langle{g\wr S_{1}}\right\rangle=\left\langle{P\wr S_{1}}\right\rangle
,
and thus in Step
3
the algorithm will return

\mathsf{DEGREE}_{S_{1}}\left(\left\langle{P\wr S_{1}}\right\rangle\right),

which equals
\deg(P)
by induction, as desired.

•

Case 2:

\deg(P)\geq n/2
. Let
P(X)=R(X)+Z_{0}(X)\cdot Q(X)
, where
\deg(R)<n/2
.
Thus
\deg(P)=n/2+\deg(Q)
.

By the above relation between
P
and
R
, we have

\left\langle{R\wr S_{0}}\right\rangle=\left\langle{P\wr S_{0}}\right\rangle=\left\langle{\pi\wr S_{0}}\right\rangle.

By the defining property of
\mathsf{EXTEND}_{S_{0},S_{1}}
, we get that
\left\langle{g\wr S_{1}}\right\rangle=\left\langle{R\wr S_{1}}\right\rangle
.
Thus

\left\langle{\frac{\pi-g}{Z_{0}}\wr S_{1}}\right\rangle=\left\langle{\frac{P-R}{Z_{0}}\wr S_{1}}\right\rangle=\left\langle{Q\wr S_{1}}\right\rangle,

which implies, by induction, that Step
4
returns

n/2+\mathsf{DEGREE}_{S_{1}}\left(\left\langle{Q\wr S_{1}}\right\rangle\right)=n/2+\deg(Q)=\deg(P),

as desired.

Running time:

The algorithm calls one instance of
\mathsf{EXTEND}
on an instance of size
O(n)
,
does
O(n)
operations,
and makes one recursive call to itself on an instance of size
n/2
.
Thus the running time
F(n)
satisfies:

F(n)\leq O(n\log n)+F(n/2),

and thus
F(n)\leq O(n\log n)
, as claimed.
∎

6.5
Modular and Montgomery Reduction

6.5.1
Modular Reduction—theorem statement

The goal of this chapter is to present an algorithm that computes the remainder of the division
of an input polynomial
P
(in the new representation) by a fixed polynomial
A
:

Theorem 6.7

(Modular Reduction)
.

Let
S
be a basic set of size
n
, and let
A(X)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[X]
be a polynomial of degree at most

n/2
which is half-disjoint from
S
, i.e.
A(X)
has no zeroes in at least one moiety of
S
.

There is an algorithm running in time
O(n\log n)
, denoted
\mathsf{MOD}_{S,A}
, which when given as input:

•

\left\langle{P\wr S}\right\rangle
, where
P(X)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[X]
with
\deg(P)<n
,

computes
\left\langle{Q\wr S}\right\rangle
, where
Q(X)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[X]
is given by:

Q(X)=P(X)\mathsf{\>rem\>}A(X).

Before presenting the proof and the algorithm, we introduce an auxiliary algorithm, which we call

Montgomery reduction
, inspired by Montgomery’s
[
Mon85
]
algorithm for “modulo-free”
modular multiplication, which we also describe briefly.

6.5.2
Montgomery Reduction

Montgomery’s algorithm for multiplication is motivated by the observation that while the operation

a\!\!\mod N
for a generic (odd) integer
N
might be computationally expensive,
the operation
a\!\!\mod R
where
R=2^{r}\gtrsim N
is very efficient, in computing systems based on
binary representations.

In Montgomery’s method, each residue
x\pmod{N}
is represented instead by
xR\!\!\mod N
.
To get the representation of the product
xy
, i.e.
xyR\!\!\mod N
, we first multiply the two
representations to get an integer equivalent to
xyR^{2}\pmod{N}
, and then apply the
reduction

algorithm
\mathsf{REDC}
, which efficiently maps an integer
t
to
tR^{-1}\!\!\mod N
, without explicitly computing
the division by
N
. The reduction algorithm relies on having the constant number
(-N^{-1})\!\!\mod R
as
advice.

The representation
xR\!\!\mod N
can be transformed back to
x\!\!\mod N
by simply applying reduction.
In the other direction,
x\!\!\mod N
can be transformed into
xR\!\!\mod N
by performing the
full Montgomery multiplication (i.e. integer multiplication + reduction) between
x\!\!\mod N

and the constant
R^{2}\!\!\mod N
, which is again given as advice.

For our purposes, we want to perform modular arithmetic of polynomials. We observe that the
vanishing polynomial
Z(X)
of a basic set
S
is a natural analogue to the radix
R=2^{r}
, as
arithmetic operations on the tables
\left\langle{P\wr S}\right\rangle
are equivalent to arithmetic operations
on polynomials modulo
Z(X)
. Thus, we can attempt to create a version of
\mathsf{REDC}
which
transforms
\left\langle{P\wr S}\right\rangle
into
\left\langle{P\cdot Z^{-1}\wr S}\right\rangle
, and then use this algorithm to perform
general modular operations, such as
\mathsf{MOD}
. In fact, we apply
\mathsf{REDC}
directly only inside

\mathsf{MOD}
.

Theorem 6.8

(Montgomery Reduction)
.

Let
S
be a basic set with
|S|=n
. Let
S_{0}\subseteq S
be a moiety of
S
.
Let
A(X)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[X]
be a polynomial of degree at most
n/2

having no zeroes in
S_{0}
. Let
Z_{0}(X)
be the vanishing polynomial
of
S_{0}
.

There is an algorithm running in time
O(n\log n)
, denoted
\mathsf{REDC}_{S,S_{0},A}
,
which when given as input:

•

\left\langle{P\wr S}\right\rangle
, where
P(X)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[X]
satisfies
\deg(P)<n
,

computes
\left\langle{Q\wr S}\right\rangle
, where
Q(X)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[X]
is a polynomial such that

•

Q(X)\equiv P(X)\cdot Z_{0}(X)^{-1}\pmod{A(X)}
, and

•

\deg(Q)\leq\max(\deg(P)-n/2,\ \deg(A)-1)<n/2
.

Remark 6.9
.

If
\deg(P)<n/2+\deg(A)
, then it follows that
\deg(Q)<\deg(A)
, and therefore

Q(X)=P(X)\cdot(Z_{0}(X))^{-1}_{A(X)}\mathsf{\>rem\>}A(X).

However, the last identity is not true in general when
n/2+\deg(A)\leq\deg(P)<n
, since

Q
might not be of degree less than
\deg(A)
.

Before giving the algorithm, we give some high level motivation for it.
Suppose for simplicity that
A(X)
has degree exactly
n/2
.
Observe that
A(X)
and
Z_{0}(X)
are relatively prime.
Thus there exist
G(X),H(X)
of degree
<n/2
such that:

G(X)A(X)+H(X)Z_{0}(X)=P(X).

From this identity, if we are given the evaluation of one of
G(X)
at a point
x\in S\setminus S_{0}
,
we can compute the evaluation of
H(X)
at that point
x
.
Finally, we observe that:

\displaystyle G(X)=(P(X)/A(X))\mathsf{\>rem\>}Z_{0}(X),

(10)

\displaystyle H(X)=(P(X)/Z_{0}(X))\mathsf{\>rem\>}A(X).

(11)

The first equation tells us how to compute
G(X)
at any
x\in S_{0}
, which we can then extend to
compute evaluations of
G(X)
at
x\in S\setminus S_{0}
.
The second equation tells us that
H(X)
equals
Q(X)
, the polynomial whose evaluations we seek.
Putting these together, we get the algorithm.

Proof.

Let
S_{1}
be the other moiety of
S
.
The algorithm uses the values of
\left\langle{Z_{0}\wr S_{1}}\right\rangle
,
\left\langle{A\wr S_{0}}\right\rangle
,
\left\langle{A\wr S_{1}}\right\rangle
,
which depend only on
S
,
S_{0}
and
A
.

Algorithm
\mathsf{REDC}_{S,S_{0},A}
:

Input:
an evaluation table
\left\langle{\pi\wr S}\right\rangle

1.

From
\left\langle{\pi\wr S_{0}}\right\rangle
and
\left\langle{A\wr S_{0}}\right\rangle
, compute

\left\langle{\frac{\pi}{A}\wr S_{0}}\right\rangle.

2.

Let
\left\langle{g\wr S_{1}}\right\rangle=\mathsf{EXTEND}_{S_{0},S_{1}}(\left\langle{\frac{\pi}{A}\wr S_{0}}\right\rangle)
.

3.

From
\left\langle{\pi\wr S_{1}}\right\rangle
,
\left\langle{g\wr S_{1}}\right\rangle
,
\left\langle{A\wr S_{1}}\right\rangle
,
\left\langle{Z_{0}\wr S_{1}}\right\rangle
,
compute:

\left\langle{h_{1}\wr S_{1}}\right\rangle=\left\langle{\frac{\pi-gA}{Z_{0}}\wr S_{1}}\right\rangle.

4.

Compute:

\left\langle{h_{0}\wr S_{0}}\right\rangle=\mathsf{EXTEND}_{S_{1},S_{0}}(\left\langle{h_{1}\wr S_{1}}\right\rangle).

5.

Return
\left\langle{h_{0}\wr S_{0}}\right\rangle\cup\left\langle{h_{1}\wr S_{1}}\right\rangle
.

Proof of correctness:

Let
P(X)
be a polynomial of degree
<n
.
We will analyze the above algorithm when its input
\left\langle{\pi\wr S}\right\rangle
is taken to be
\left\langle{P\wr S}\right\rangle
.
Let
G(X)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[X]
be the unique polynomial of degree
<n/2
interpolating
\frac{\pi}{A}
on
S_{0}
; namely:

\left\langle{G\wr S_{0}}\right\rangle=\left\langle{\frac{\pi}{A}\wr S_{0}}\right\rangle.

Then by the defining property of
\mathsf{EXTEND}
, we get that:

\mathsf{EXTEND}_{S_{0},S_{1}}(\left\langle{\frac{\pi}{A}\wr S_{0}}\right\rangle)=\mathsf{EXTEND}_{S_{0},S_{1}}(\left\langle{G\wr S_{0}}\right\rangle)=\left\langle{G\wr S_{1}}\right\rangle.

Thus in Step
2
of the algorithm, we will have

\left\langle{g\wr S_{1}}\right\rangle=\left\langle{G\wr S_{1}}\right\rangle.

By definition of
G(X)
, we have that
\frac{P(X)}{A(X)}-G(X)
vanishes on
S_{0}
.
Therefore
P(X)-G(X)A(X)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[X]
vanishes on
S_{0}
, and so
Z_{0}(X)
divides
P(X)-G(X)A(X)
.
Let
H(X)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[X]
be given by:

H(X)=\frac{P(X)-G(X)A(X)}{Z_{0}(X)}.

Note that

\deg(H)\leq\max\{\deg(P),\deg(A)+\deg(G)\}-\deg(Z_{0})\leq\max(\deg(P)-n/2,\deg(A)-1)<n/2.

(12)

The second inequality follows from the fact that
\deg(G)<\deg(Z_{0})=n/2
, and the final inequality from
the assumptions
\deg(P)<n
and
\deg(A)\leq n/2
.

We have

\left\langle{h_{1}\wr S_{1}}\right\rangle=\left\langle{\frac{\pi-gA}{Z_{0}}\wr S_{1}}\right\rangle=\left\langle{\frac{P-GA}{Z_{0}}\wr S_{1}}\right\rangle=\left\langle{H\wr S_{1}}\right\rangle.

Thus in Step
4

\mathsf{EXTEND}_{S_{1},S_{0}}
yields

\left\langle{h_{0}\wr S_{0}}\right\rangle=\left\langle{H\wr S_{0}}\right\rangle,

and so the algorithm returns:

\left\langle{h_{0}\wr S_{0}}\right\rangle\cup\left\langle{h_{1}\wr S_{1}}\right\rangle=\left\langle{H\wr S_{0}}\right\rangle\cup\left\langle{H\wr S_{1}}\right\rangle=\left\langle{H\wr S}\right\rangle

and we have already shown in
Eq.

12
that
H(X)=Q(X)
is of the claimed degree.

Finally, from the definition of
H(X)
we get

H(X)Z_{0}(X)=P(X)-G(X)A(X)\equiv P(X)\pmod{A(X)},

which after dividing by
Z_{0}(X)
is equivalent to

H(X)\equiv P(X)\cdot Z_{0}(X)^{-1}\pmod{A(X)}.

This completes the proof of correctness.

Running time:

The algorithm does
O(n)
operations and invokes
\mathsf{EXTEND}
twice on instances of size
n/2
. Thus the total running time is
O(n\log n)
.
∎

6.5.3
Modular Reduction—algorithm and proof

Proof of
Theorem

6.7
.

Let
S_{0},S_{1}
be the moieties of
S
, and suppose without loss of generality that
A(X)

has no zeros in
S_{0}
(otherwise, it has no
zeros in
S_{1}
by assumption, and we may swap the labeling of the moieties).

Let
C(X)=Z_{0}(X)^{2}\mathsf{\>rem\>}A(X)
, which has degree
\deg(C)<\deg(A)
.
The algorithm uses the values of
\left\langle{C\wr S}\right\rangle
, that depend only on
A(X)
,
S
and
S_{0}
, as
well as values used internally by the
\mathsf{REDC}_{S,S_{0},A}
sub-circuit.

Algorithm
\mathsf{MOD}_{S,A}
:

Input:
an evaluation table
\left\langle{\pi\wr S}\right\rangle

1.

From
\left\langle{\pi\wr S}\right\rangle
, compute

\left\langle{h\wr S}\right\rangle=\mathsf{REDC}_{S,S_{0},A}(\left\langle{\pi\wr S}\right\rangle).

2.

From
\left\langle{h\wr S}\right\rangle
and
\left\langle{C\wr S}\right\rangle
, compute

\left\langle{h\cdot C\wr S}\right\rangle.

3.

Compute:

\left\langle{g\wr S}\right\rangle=\mathsf{REDC}_{S,S_{0},A}(\left\langle{h\cdot C\wr S}\right\rangle).

4.

Return
\left\langle{g\wr S}\right\rangle
.

Proof of correctness:

Suppose
P(X)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[x]
with
\deg(P)<n
.
We will analyze the above computation when its input
\left\langle{\pi\wr S}\right\rangle
is taken to be

\left\langle{P\wr S}\right\rangle
.
By
Theorem

6.8
about
\mathsf{REDC}
, we get that Step
1
computes
\left\langle{h\wr S}\right\rangle=\left\langle{H\wr S}\right\rangle
,
where
H(X)
is a polynomial with
\deg H(X)<n/2
and satisfying

H(X)\equiv P(X)\cdot Z_{0}(X)^{-1}\pmod{A(X)}.

Thus Step
2
computes

\left\langle{H\cdot C\wr S}\right\rangle,

where
H(X)\cdot C(X)
is a polynomial with

\deg(H\cdot C)=\deg(H)+\deg(C)<n/2+\deg(A)

and

H(X)\cdot C(X)\cdot Z_{0}(X)^{-1}\equiv P(X)\cdot Z_{0}(X)^{-1}\cdot Z_{0}(X)^{2}\cdot Z_{0}(X)^{-1}\equiv P(X)\pmod{A(X)}.

Thus in Step
3
, as noted in
Remark

6.9
, the algorithm returns
\left\langle{Q\wr S}\right\rangle
,
where

Q(X)=\left(H\cdot C\cdot(Z_{0}(X))^{-1}_{A(X)}\right)\mathsf{\>rem\>}A(X)=P(X)\mathsf{\>rem\>}A(X),

as desired.

Running time:

The algorithm invokes
\mathsf{REDC}
twice on instances of size
n
, and does
O(n)
other operations.
Thus the running time is
O(n\log n)
.
∎

Remark 6.10
.

As noted earlier in this section, we have no further direct applications of
\mathsf{REDC}
in this paper,
and all calls to it are mediated by calls to
\mathsf{MOD}
. Nonetheless, we note that it may hold individual
interest for real-world applications, as it is naturally more than twice as fast as
\mathsf{MOD}
, due to

\mathsf{MOD}
containing two calls to
\mathsf{REDC}
. Thus, applying
\mathsf{REDC}
directly might be more efficient in
certain situations.

.

.

Let
A(X)

•

\left\langle{P\wr S}\right\rangle
, where

6.7
Exiting to Standard Polynomial Representation

The next computation transforms a polynomial represented by its evaluation on a basic set to the set of coefficients that
form the standard representation as
\sum_{i}a_{i}X^{i}
.

Theorem 6.12

(Exit to Standard Polynomial Representation)
.

Let
S
be a basic set with
|S|=n
.

There is an algorithm
\mathsf{EXIT}_{S}
, which when given as input:

•

\left\langle{P\wr S}\right\rangle
, where
P(X)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[X]
with
\deg(P)<n
,

runs in time

O(n\log^{2}n)

and computes the coefficients
a_{i}
of
P(X)
in the standard expansion

P(X)=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}a_{i}X^{i}.

Proof.

Let
S_{0},S_{1}
be the moieties of
S
, and note that we may assume without loss of generality that

0\notin S_{0}
.
Thus
X^{n/2}
has no roots in
S_{0}
, and in particular an algorithm
\mathsf{MOD}_{S,X^{n/2}}
exists.

On input
\left\langle{P\wr S}\right\rangle
, the algorithm will compute
\left\langle{U\wr S_{0}}\right\rangle
and
\left\langle{V\wr S_{0}}\right\rangle
,
where
P(X)=U(X)+X^{n/2}\cdot V(X)
with
\deg(U),\deg(V)<n/2
,
in time
O(n\log n)
. Then by recursively calling
\mathsf{EXIT}_{S_{0}}
on these two smaller instances
and combining the results in the obvious way, we get the coefficients of
P(X)
in time

O(n\log^{2}n)
.

The algorithm uses as advice the values
\left\langle{X^{n/2}\wr S_{0}}\right\rangle
, which depend only on
S
, as well as auxiliary values used by the
\mathsf{MOD}
algorithm (namely,
\left\langle{Z_{0}\wr S_{1}}\right\rangle
,
\left\langle{Z_{0}^{2}\mathsf{\>rem\>}X^{n/2}\wr S}\right\rangle
).

Algorithm
\mathsf{EXIT}_{S}
:

Input:
an evaluation table
\left\langle{\pi\wr S}\right\rangle

1.

If
|S|=1
with
S=\{s\}
, return
(\pi(s))
.

2.

Let
\left\langle{u\wr S}\right\rangle=\mathsf{MOD}_{S,X^{n/2}}(\left\langle{\pi\wr S}\right\rangle)
.

3.

Let

(a_{0},a_{1},\ldots,a_{\frac{n}{2}-1})=\mathsf{EXIT}_{S_{0}}(\left\langle{u\wr S_{0}}\right\rangle).

4.

From
\left\langle{\pi\wr S_{0}}\right\rangle
,
\left\langle{u\wr S_{0}}\right\rangle
and
\left\langle{X^{n/2}\wr S_{0}}\right\rangle
, compute:

\left\langle{v\wr S_{0}}\right\rangle=\left\langle{\frac{\pi-u}{X^{n/2}}\wr S_{0}}\right\rangle.

5.

Let

(b_{0},b_{1},\ldots,b_{\frac{n}{2}-1})=\mathsf{EXIT}_{S_{0}}(\left\langle{v\wr S_{0}}\right\rangle).

6.

Return

(a_{0},a_{1},\ldots,a_{\frac{n}{2}-1},b_{0},b_{1},\ldots,b_{\frac{n}{2}-1}).

Correctness:

Suppose
P(X)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[X]
with
\deg(P)<n
.
We will analyze what the above algorithm does when its input
\left\langle{\pi\wr S}\right\rangle
is taken
to be
\left\langle{P\wr S}\right\rangle
.

If
n=1
the algorithm is clearly correct.

Now assume
n>1
.
Write
P(X)=U(X)+X^{n/2}\cdot V(X)
, where
\deg(U),\deg(V)<n/2
.

Then
U(X)=P(X)\mathsf{\>rem\>}X^{n/2}
.
By properties of
\mathsf{MOD}
, we get that Step
2
computes
\left\langle{u\wr S}\right\rangle=\left\langle{U\wr S}\right\rangle
. Thus
\left\langle{u\wr S_{0}}\right\rangle=\left\langle{U\wr S_{0}}\right\rangle
.

Also note that
V(X)=\frac{P(X)-U(X)}{X^{n/2}}
.
Then

\left\langle{v\wr S_{0}}\right\rangle=\left\langle{\frac{\pi-u}{X^{n/2}}\wr S_{0}}\right\rangle=\left\langle{\frac{P-U}{X^{n/2}}\wr S_{0}}\right\rangle=\left\langle{V\wr S_{0}}\right\rangle.

By induction, we get that the algorithm correctly computes the coefficients of
U(X)
and
V(X)
, and by
concatenating them together, it computes the coefficients of
P(X)
, as desired.

Running time:

The algorithm makes one call to
\mathsf{MOD}
on an instance of size
n
and two recursive calls to
\mathsf{EXIT}
on instances of size
n/2
.
Thus the running time
F(n)
satisfies the recurrence:

F(n)\leq 2F(n/2)+O(n\log n),

and thus

F(n)\leq O(n\log^{2}n).

∎

6.8
Entering from Standard Polynomial Representation

The next algorithm is the inverse of
\mathsf{EXIT}
, it transforms a polynomial given in standard representation to its evaluation
over a basic set.

Theorem 6.13

(Entering from Standard Polynomial Representation)
.

Let
S
be a basic set with
|S|=n
.

There is an algorithm
\mathsf{ENTER}_{S}
, which when given as input:

•

a_{0},a_{1},\ldots,a_{n-1}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
,

runs in time

O(n\log^{2}n)

and computes
\left\langle{P\wr S}\right\rangle
, where

P(X)=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}a_{i}X^{i}.

Proof.

If
|S|=1
, the task is trivial.

Otherwise, let
S_{0},S_{1}
be the moieties of
S
.
The algorithm is based on writing the polynomial
P(X)

as:

P(X)=U(X)+X^{n/2}\cdot V(X),

where
\deg(U),\deg(V)<n/2
, and finding the evaluation tables of
U,V
on both
S_{0},S_{1}
.
A priori, this seems like reducing an
\mathsf{ENTER}
instance of size
n
to
4

\mathsf{ENTER}
instances of size
n/2
(leading to a quadratic running time), but in fact this can be done by
2
recursive calls to
\mathsf{ENTER}
and
2
invocations of
\mathsf{EXTEND}
.

The algorithm below takes
\left\langle{X^{n/2}\wr S}\right\rangle
as advice. This can be precomputed since it only depends on
S
.

Algorithm
\mathsf{ENTER}_{S}
:

Input:

(a_{0},a_{1},\ldots,a_{n-1})\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
.

1.

If
|S|=1
with
S=\{s\}

•

Define
g:S\to\mathbb{F}_{q}
by
g(s)=a_{0}

•

Return
\left\langle{g\wr S}\right\rangle

2.

Let
\left\langle{u_{0}\wr S_{0}}\right\rangle=\mathsf{ENTER}_{S_{0}}(a_{0},\ldots,a_{\frac{n}{2}-1})
.

3.

Let
\left\langle{u_{1}\wr S_{1}}\right\rangle=\mathsf{EXTEND}_{S_{0},S_{1}}(\left\langle{u_{0}\wr S_{0}}\right\rangle)
.

4.

Let
\left\langle{v_{0}\wr S_{0}}\right\rangle=\mathsf{ENTER}_{S_{0}}(a_{n/2},\ldots,a_{n-1})
.

5.

Let
\left\langle{v_{1}\wr S_{1}}\right\rangle=\mathsf{EXTEND}_{S_{0},S_{1}}(\left\langle{v_{0}\wr S_{0}}\right\rangle)
.

6.

Let

\left\langle{\pi\wr S}\right\rangle=\left\langle{u_{0}+X^{n/2}v_{0}\wr S_{0}}\right\rangle\cup\left\langle{u_{1}+X^{n/2}v_{1}\wr S_{1}}\right\rangle.

7.

Return
\left\langle{\pi\wr S}\right\rangle
.

Correctness:

The correctness follows immediately from the discussion preceding the algorithm.

Running time:

This algorithm makes two recursive calls to
\mathsf{ENTER}
on instances of half the size, makes two invocations of
\mathsf{EXTEND}
on instances of half the size, along with
O(n)
other operations.
Thus the running time
F(n)
satisfies:

F(n)\leq 2F(n/2)+O(n\log n)+O(n),

and thus
F(n)\leq O(n\log^{2}n)
, as claimed.
∎

6.9
Chinese Remaindering

The following operation receives as input two polynomials
P,Q
and computes the polynomial
R
whose remainders modulo two relatively prime polynomials
A,B
are
P
and
Q
, respectively.

Theorem 6.14

(Chinese Remaindering)
.

Let
S
be a basic set with
|S|=n
.
Let
S_{0}\subseteq S
be a moiety of
S
.

Let
A(X),B(X)
be relatively prime polynomials with degrees at most
n/2
.
Suppose that both
A(X)
and
B(X)
are half-disjoint from
S
; the moieties
having no zeroes of
A
and
B
may be the same moiety for both or a different one for each.

There is an algorithm
\mathsf{CRT}_{S,S_{0},A,B}
, which when given as input:

•

\left\langle{P\wr S_{0}}\right\rangle
, where
P(X)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[X]
with
\deg(P)<n/2
, and

•

\left\langle{Q\wr S_{0}}\right\rangle
, where
Q(X)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[X]
with
\deg(Q)<n/2
,

runs in time

O(n\log n)

and computes
\left\langle{R\wr S}\right\rangle
where
R
is the unique polynomial of degree
<\deg(A)+\deg(B)

such that
R\equiv P\pmod{A}
and
R\equiv Q\pmod{B}
.

Proof.

By the usual proof of the Chinese Remainder Theorem, the desired

R(X)
is of the form:

((P(X)\cdot G(X))\mathsf{\>rem\>}A(X))\cdot B(X)+((Q(X)\cdot H(X))\mathsf{\>rem\>}B(X))\cdot A(X),

where
G(X)=(B(X)^{-1})_{A(X)}
,
H(X)=(A(X)^{-1})_{B(X)}

depend only on
A
and
B
, and have degrees

\deg(G),\deg(H)<n/2.

Thus the algorithm simply extends
\left\langle{P\wr S_{0}}\right\rangle
and
\left\langle{Q\wr S_{0}}\right\rangle

to find
\left\langle{P\wr S}\right\rangle
and
\left\langle{Q\wr S}\right\rangle
. Then, using
\left\langle{G\wr S}\right\rangle
and
\left\langle{H\wr S}\right\rangle

as advice (which can be precomputed, since they only depend on
A
,
B
and
S
), as well
as
\left\langle{A\wr S}\right\rangle
and
\left\langle{B\wr S}\right\rangle
, we compute:

\mathsf{MOD}_{S,A}(\left\langle{P\cdot G\wr S}\right\rangle)\cdot\left\langle{B\wr S}\right\rangle+\mathsf{MOD}_{S,B}(\left\langle{Q\cdot H\wr S}\right\rangle)\cdot\left\langle{A\wr S}\right\rangle

which is the desired output. Note that
\deg(P\cdot G),\deg(Q\cdot H)<n
, as
\mathsf{MOD}
requires.

The run-time comes from two invocations of
\mathsf{EXTEND}
, two invocations of
\mathsf{MOD}
, and
O(n)
other operations,
and is thus
O(n\log n)
overall.
∎

7
Applications to classical problems

The previous
Section

6
presented fast algorithms (and arithmetic circuits) for manipulating polynomials represented by their evaluations on basic sets. This section uses those results to
efficiently solve “classical” problems of algebraic computation, in which the polynomials are represented in the “classical” way, as
sums of monomials. In all cases below we shall transition to
a representation of polynomials by their evaluations
on basic sets, and this will result in running times,
over any polynomially large field
, that are
as good as those of special, classical-FFT-friendly, finite fields.

7.1
Elementary Symmetric Polynomial Evaluation

Theorem 7.1

(Evaluating Elementary Symmetric Polynomials)
.

Let
t<n<q^{O(1)}
. There is an arithmetic circuit over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
of size

O(n\log^{2}n)

which takes as input variables
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}
and computes

{\sf{Sym}}_{n,t}(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}):=\sum_{J\subseteq[n],|J|=t}\prod_{j\in J}\alpha_{j}.

Proof.

We follow the classical approach of computing elementary symmetric polynomials as coefficients of a certain product,
except that we work with polynomials in the new representation.

The idea is to compute the coefficients, in the standard monomial representation, of the polynomial

P(X)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}(X-\alpha_{i})=\sum_{i=0}^{n}(-1)^{n-i}\cdot{\sf{Sym}}_{n,n-i}(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n})X^{i}

We do this by first computing
\left\langle{P\wr S}\right\rangle
for a big enough basic set
S
, and then running

\mathsf{EXIT}_{S}(P)
to compute the coefficients of
P
. Details follow.

By adding some dummy
0
inputs
\alpha_{i}
and increasing
n
by at most a factor
2
, we may assume
that
n
is a power of
2
.
Next, we claim that
\mathbb{F}_{q}
can be assumed to contain a basic set of size at least
2n
. Indeed, this
can be done by replacing
\mathbb{F}_{q}
by an
O(1)
-degree extension of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
which is sufficiently large,
of size
O(n^{2})
, as needed for a basic set of size at least
2n
to exist in
\mathbb{F}_{q}

(cf.
Section

4.2
). Moving to a larger
q
increases the number of arithmetic
operations by a factor of at most
O(1)
because we assume
n<q^{O(1)}
.

Let
m=\log_{2}(2n)
and fix arbitrary basic sets
U_{0}\subseteq U_{1}\ldots\subseteq U_{m}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}

with
|U_{j}|=2^{j}
. We shall
compute
\left\langle{P\wr U_{m}}\right\rangle
in a bottom-up manner by computing products of
terms
P_{i}(X):=X-\alpha_{i},i\in[n]
, of increasing size.
We start by computing

\left\langle{P_{1}\wr U_{1}}\right\rangle,\ldots,\left\langle{P_{n}\wr U_{1}}\right\rangle

which takes time
O(1)
for each term
P_{i}
(and total time
O(n)
).

Let
Q(X)=\prod_{i=i_{0}}^{i_{0}+2^{j}-1}P_{i}(X)

7.2
Multipoint evaluation over general sets of points

Previously we evaluated polynomials over basic sets in quasi-linear time (see
Theorem

6.13
).
The next result shows that evaluating polynomials over general sets of points can also be done in (slightly worse) quasi-linear time.

Theorem 7.2

(Multipoint polynomial evaluation)
.

Assume
n<q
. Given any set
B
of
m
points in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, there exists an arithmetic circuit over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
(that depends on
B
) of size

O(n\log^{2}n+m\log^{2}m)

which takes as input
(a_{0},\ldots,a_{n-1})\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
and computes

\left\langle{P\wr B}\right\rangle

for
P(X)=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}a_{i}X^{i}
.

Remark 7.3
.

Note that if
n\geq q
then we can first reduce
P(X)
modulo
X^{q}-X
trivially in
n
steps and get
back to the case
n<q
.
Moreover, if
m>n
, then we can partition
B
into
O(m/n)
sets of size at most
O(n)
,
getting a run-time of
O(m\log^{2}n)
,
whereas if
m<n
, then we can decompose
P
into
O(n/m)
polynomials of degree at most
O(m)
,
getting a run-time of
O(n\log^{2}m)
.

Proof.

Let
B=\{b_{1},\ldots,b_{m}\}
.
The idea of the algorithm is based on the fact that

P(b_{i})=P(X)\mathsf{\>rem\>}(X-b_{i}).

To find
P(X)\mathsf{\>rem\>}(X-b_{i})
, we start with
P(X)\mathsf{\>rem\>}\prod_{i=1}^{m}(X-b_{i})
, and successively
compute
P(X)\mathsf{\>rem\>}\prod_{i\in I}(X-b_{i})
for smaller and smaller sets
I\subseteq[m]
. Details follow.

The algorithm starts by running
\mathsf{ENTER}_{U_{a}}
to find

\left\langle{P\wr U_{a}}\right\rangle,

where
a=\log_{2}n+O(1)
. This step runs in
O(n\log^{2}n)
time.

Next, we tweak
B
and
U_{a}
until they are of similar sizes, specifically,
2^{a-2}<|B|\leq 2^{a-1}
.

In the case
|B|\leq 2^{a-2}
, let
a^{\prime}=\lceil\log_{2}m\rceil+1<a
.
We wish to assume that
B
is half-disjoint from
U_{a}
: if it is not the case,
we may simply split
B
into two parts that are each half-disjoint, e.g.
B\cap U_{a-1}
and

B\setminus U_{a-1}
.
Then, assuming half-disjointness, we may run

\mathsf{MOD}_{U_{a},\prod_{b\in B}(X-b)}(\left\langle{P\wr U_{a}}\right\rangle)
in
O(n\log n)
time to obtain

\left\langle{P\mathsf{\>rem\>}\prod_{b\in B}(X-b)\wr U_{a}}\right\rangle.

The resulting polynomial will have degree strictly less than

|B|\leq 2^{a^{\prime}-1}
, and we may restrict its evaluation table

\left\langle{P\mathsf{\>rem\>}\prod_{b\in B}(X-b)\wr U_{a}}\right\rangle
to

\left\langle{P\mathsf{\>rem\>}\prod_{b\in B}(X-b)\wr U_{a^{\prime}-1}}\right\rangle

at no cost while maintaining the fact that it represents
P\mathsf{\>rem\>}\prod_{b\in B}(X-b)
.
We then continue to evaluate this polynomial on
B
, replacing
a
with
a^{\prime}
, and noting that

2^{a^{\prime}-2}<|B|\leq 2^{a^{\prime}-1}
, and
a^{\prime}\leq\min(\log_{2}(n),\log_{2}(m))+O(1)
.

In the case
|B|>2^{a-1}
, split
B
arbitrarily into
l
disjoint parts, each of size at
most
2^{a-1}=O(n)
, and proceed on each part separately. As in the previous case we further require
that each part be half-disjoint from
U_{a}
, and observe that again this requires at most one additional
part (e.g. by taking one of the parts equal to
B\cap U_{a-1}
), and can be achieved using only

l=O(\frac{m}{n}+1)
parts, and note that this bound also covers the previous case (with 1 or 2
parts).
The complexity of the remaining work done on each part will be multiplied by
l
to obtain the total
complexity. We continue now with

|B|
denoting a single part, of size at most
2^{a-1}
, and half-disjoint from
U_{a}
.
Again we have
a\leq\min(\log_{2}(n),\log_{2}(m))+O(1)
. As in the previous case, the next step
is to run
\mathsf{MOD}_{U_{a},\prod_{b\in B}(X-b)}(\left\langle{P\wr U_{a}}\right\rangle)
and restrict to
U_{a-1}
, yielding

\left\langle{P\mathsf{\>rem\>}\prod_{b\in B}(X-b)\wr U_{a-1}}\right\rangle

in
O(n\log n)
time.

We can now get the desired result by applying the following recursive step, for
j=a-1,a-2,\dots,1
:
Suppose
A(X)
is a product of
\leq 2^{j}
different linear factors. Then we may write

A(X)=A^{\prime}(X)\cdot A^{\prime\prime}(X)
, where
\deg(A^{\prime}),\deg(A^{\prime\prime})\leq 2^{j-1}
, and both
A^{\prime},A^{\prime\prime}
are
half-disjoint from
U_{j}
. Then given
\left\langle{P\mathsf{\>rem\>}A\wr U_{j}}\right\rangle
,
we can compute

\left\langle{P\mathsf{\>rem\>}A^{\prime}\wr U_{j}}\right\rangle=\mathsf{MOD}_{U_{j},A^{\prime}}(\left\langle{P\mathsf{\>rem\>}A\wr U_{j}}\right\rangle)

\left\langle{P\mathsf{\>rem\>}A^{\prime\prime}\wr U_{j}}\right\rangle=\mathsf{MOD}_{U_{j},A^{\prime\prime}}(\left\langle{P\mathsf{\>rem\>}A\wr U_{j}}\right\rangle)

in time
O(|U_{j}|\log|U_{j}|)
, and then restrict the tables to
\left\langle{P\mathsf{\>rem\>}A^{\prime}\wr U_{j-1}}\right\rangle
,

\left\langle{P\mathsf{\>rem\>}A^{\prime\prime}\wr U_{j-1}}\right\rangle
.

At layer
j
of the recursion we perform
2^{a-j}

\mathsf{MOD}_{U_{j}}
operations, taking a total run time
of
O(|U_{a}|\log|U_{j}|)
, and summing over all layers
j
we get a run time of

O(|U_{a}|\log^{2}|U_{a}|)=O(\min(n\log^{2}n,m\log^{2}m))

per part. Multiplying by the number of parts
l=O(\frac{m}{n}+1)
and adding
the
O(n\log^{2}n)
from
\mathsf{ENTER}
, we get that the total run time is

O(n\log^{2}n+m\log^{2}m),

as claimed.
∎

In

The following result, the converse of the previous

Theorem 7.4

(Polynomial interpolation from general evaluation sets)
.

Let

Appendix A
The paper of Chudnovsky and Chudnovsky
[
CC89
]

Some of the core ideas appearing in this paper were first suggested, in brief,
in the final section of a paper by Chudnovsky and Chudnovsky
[
CC89
, Section 6]
.

The main claim from
[
CC89
]
relevant for us is an “Elliptic Interpolation Algorithm”, which
describes how to use elliptic curve groups over
finite fields to solve a certain rational function interpolation problem via an FFT-type algorithm.
They call this rational function interpolation problem and the algorithm for it
the Fast Elliptic Number Theoretic Transform (FENTT).
Specifically for any prime
p

and for a certain
n
and certain fixed
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n},\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n}\in\mathbb{F}_{p}

the following holds:

•

Given as input
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
, consider the rational function

F(Z)=\sum_{i}\frac{x_{i}}{Z-\alpha_{i}}.

There is an algorithm to compute
\langle F(\beta_{j})\rangle_{j\in[n]}
in
O(n\log n)
operations.

Succinctly, the claim is that there exists an
n\times n
Cauchy matrix
M
with entries in
\mathbb{F}_{p}

such that multiplying by
M
can be done in
O(n\log n)
operations. The authors go on to suggest
that
n
can be taken to be
\Omega(p)
whenever there is a power of
2
within
p+1\pm 2\sqrt{p}
, but we
believe there is an error in the analysis of their algorithm, and that in fact, there are no elliptic curves
that can make their algorithm achieve such large
n
uniformly for all
p
— like the FFT, the

n
in the FENTT is limited by the factorization of
p-1
.

Technically, the proposal is based on the doubling map, which is a specific
4
-isogeny mapping an
elliptic curve
E
to itself (where we use general 2-isogenies between different curves).
The overall scheme then follows the high level outline of
Section

1.3
,
albeit with a more direct translation, leading to a solution of the rational function interpolation
problem rather than the polynomial extension problems that we aim for (with an eye on applications).

A key point that
[
CC89
]
overlooked is that the kernel of the doubling map is
isomorphic to
{\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}}\times{\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}}
, not
{\mathbb{Z}}/4{\mathbb{Z}}
. Thus the doubling map (which is a degree
4
map) will be
a
4
-to-
1
map on a subgroup
G\subseteq E(\mathbb{F}_{p})
if and only if
{\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}}\times{\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}}
is a subgroup of
G
.
Thus if we seek a sequence
G_{0},G_{1},\ldots,G_{i},\ldots,G_{t}
of subgroups of
E(\mathbb{F}_{p})

such that each
G_{i+1}
is the image of
G_{i}
under doubling, with
|G_{i+1}|=|G_{i}|/4
,
then we need
{\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}}\times{\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}}
to be a subgroup of each
G_{i}
.
Since
E(\mathbb{F}_{p})
is a rank 2 abelian group, the above sequence of groups can exist only if

{\mathbb{Z}}/2^{t}{\mathbb{Z}}\times{\mathbb{Z}}/2^{t}{\mathbb{Z}}
is a subgroup of
E(\mathbb{F}_{p})
.
This is a much more stringent requirement on
E(\mathbb{F}_{p})
than merely having size divisible by
4^{t}
(which is what one can get out of theorems of Deuring/Waterhouse).
In fact, a theorem about the possible structures of an elliptic curve group, proved independetly by
Ruck
[
Rüc87
]
and Voloch
[
Vol88
]
, implies that an elliptic curve over
\mathbb{F}_{p}
can contain a
subgroup isomorphic to
{\mathbb{Z}}/2^{t}{\mathbb{Z}}\times{\mathbb{Z}}/2^{t}{\mathbb{Z}}
only if
p\equiv 1\pmod{2^{t}}
.

Thus, the size of the largest FENTT supported by
\mathbb{F}_{p}
is bounded in terms of the factorization of
p-1
,
and is at most quadratically larger than the size of the largest FFT supported by
\mathbb{F}_{p}
.

This is quite restrictive; for example, if
p\equiv 3\pmod{4}
, then the largest FENTT
supported by
\mathbb{F}_{p}
has instance size
4
. In contrast, our approach works with

\Omega(\sqrt{p})
instance size over
\mathbb{F}_{p}
for all primes
p
.

The necessity and feasibility of this product subgroup structure condition are not mentioned in
[
CC89
]
;
in fact, the paper erroneously states that every elliptic curve with order divisible by
2^{t}
will
have points of order
2^{t}
, i.e. a
cyclic
subgroup of order
2^{t}
,
which is not necessarily the case, and further, because of the confusion regarding
{\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}}\times{\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}}
and
{\mathbb{Z}}/4{\mathbb{Z}}

described above, is not relevant to success of the FENTT.

In summary, the idea of using elliptic curve groups in place of multiplicative groups for computing
FFT-like transforms to all prime fields is not new to our paper, but we believe that an implementation
that supports transforms with large instance sizes over
all
primes
p
is.
Furthermore, our approach is intentionally adapted to a
certain flexible polynomial extension problem (rather than the specific rational function evaluation
considered in
[
CC89
]
), and this is what enables our applications to data structures and
algorithms for intrinsically interesting classical problems involving polynomials.
