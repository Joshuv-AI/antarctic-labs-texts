Merlin-Arthur with efficient quantum Merlin and quantum supremacy for the second level of the Fourier hierarchy
Tomoyuki Morimae, Yuki Takeuchi, Harumichi Nishimura

Abstract
We introduce a simple sub-universal quantum computing model, which we call the Hadamard-classical circuit with one-qubit (HC1Q) model. It consists of a classical reversible circuit sandwiched by two layers of Hadamard gates, and therefore it is in the second level of the Fourier hierarchy FH . We show that output probability distributions of the HC1Q model cannot be classically efficiently sampled within a multiplicative error unless the polynomial-time hierarchy collapses to the second level. The proof technique is different from those used for previous sub-universal models, such as IQP, Boson Sampling, and DQC1, and therefore the technique itself might be useful for finding other sub-universal models that are hard to classically simulate. We also study the classical verification of quantum computing in the second level of the Fourier hierarchy. To this end, we define a promise problem, which we call the probability distribution distinguishability with maximum norm (PDD-Max). It is a promise problem to decide whether output probability distributions of two quantum circuits are far apart or close. We show that PDD-Max is BQP-complete, but if the two circuits are restricted to some types in the second level of the Fourier hierarchy, such as the HC1Q model or the IQP model, PDD-Max has a Merlin-Arthur system with quantum polynomial-time Merlin and classical probabilistic polynomial-time Arthur.

I.2
The verification of quantum computing in the
second level of the Fourier hierarchy

In this paper, we also study the classical verification of quantum
computing.
It is a long-standing open problem
whether quantum computing is classically verifiable.
More precisely, it is open whether
any problem
L
in BQP has an
interactive proof system with a quantum polynomial-time
prover and a classical probabilistic polynomial-time verifier.

Definition 9

We say that a problem

has
an interactive proof system with a quantum
polynomial-time prover if there exists a classical
probabilistic polynomial-time verifier such that

•

If
x\in L
then there exists a quantum polynomial-time prover
such that the verifier accepts
with probability at least
2/3
.

•

If
x\notin L
then for any prover
the verifier accepts
with probability at most
1/3
.

(Note that in this definition,
the prover is in quantum polynomial-time
only for yes instances, i.e., when the prover is honest.
For no instances,
the computational power of the malicious prover is unbounded.)

Answering the open question is
important not only for practical applications of cloud quantum computing
but also for foundations of computer science
and quantum physics

.
In fact, several partial solutions to the open problem
have been obtained. They are categorized into the following four types:

1.

Several verification protocols

;

and verifiable blind quantum computing

;

demonstrate that if the verifier has a weak quantum ability,

2.

If multiple entangling

3.

Since BQP is contained in IP

In fact, recently, a step in this line

The authors of Ref.

have constructed a new interactive proof system

It has been shown recently that the classical verification

Actually, the answer to the open problem is unconditionally yes

q_{0}
is the probability that
the classical algorithm outputs 0 (accepts).
Then, we can show that
A
is in NP. In fact,
if
x\in A_{yes}
then

\displaystyle q_{0}\geq(1-\epsilon)p_{\Lambda}>0.

If
x\in A_{no}
then

\displaystyle q_{0}\leq(1+\epsilon)p_{\Lambda}=0.

According to Definition
16
,
A
is therefore in NP.

IV
Proof of Theorem
6

In this section, we show Theorem
6
.
For simplicity, we assume that the first

k
qubits are measured, where

k=O(\log N)
.
Generalizations to other

k
qubits are the same.

The outline of our proof is as follows.
We first construct a probability distribution
\{q_{z}\}_{z}

that can be calculated in classical polynomial time, and
is close to
\{p_{z}\}_{z}
within a
1/poly
L1-norm.
We then show that we can sample
\{q_{z}\}_{z}
in classical polynomial time.

Let us consider the circuit of Fig.
1
(a).
As is shown in Appendix
B
,
the probability of obtaining
the measurement result
z\in\{0,1\}^{k}

for the first
k
qubits
is

\displaystyle p_{z}=\frac{1}{2^{N-1}}\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{N-1}}f(x),

(4)

where

\displaystyle f(x)

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2^{k}}\sum_{y\in\{0,1\}^{N-1}}(-1)^{z\cdot C_{1,...,k}(x0)+z\cdot C_{1,...,k}(y0)}\delta_{C_{k+1,...,N}(x0),C_{k+1,...,N}(y0)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2^{k}}\sum_{y\in S}(-1)^{z\cdot C_{1,...,k}(x0)+z\cdot C_{1,...,k}(y0)},

C_{1,...,k}(x0)
is the first
k
bits of
C(x0)
,

C_{k+1,...,N}(x0)
is the last
N-k
bits of
C(x0)
,
and
S\subseteq\{0,1\}^{N-1}
is defined by

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\{y\in\{0,1\}^{N-1}~|~C_{k+1,...,N}(x0)=C_{k+1,...,N}(y0)\}.

The subset
S
can be obtained in polynomial time in the following
way:

1.

Set
S=\{\}
.

2.

Repeat the following for all
\alpha\in\{0,1\}^{k}
.

2-1.

Calculate
C^{-1}(\alpha C_{k+1,...,N}(x0))
.

2-2.

If
C^{-1}(\alpha C_{k+1,...,N}(x0))=y0

for certain
y\in\{0,1\}^{N-1}
,
add
y
to
S
.

3.

End.

From the construction,

|S|\leq 2^{k}=poly(N)
.
Therefore, the value of
f(x)
is exactly computable
in polynomial time for each
x
.
Furthermore,
|f(x)|\leq 1
because

\displaystyle|f(x)|\leq\frac{1}{2^{k}}\sum_{y\in S}|(-1)^{z\cdot C_{1,...,k}(x0)+z\cdot C_{1,...,k}(y0)}|\leq 1.

Let us generate random bit strings
x_{1},...,x_{T}\in\{0,1\}^{N-1}
.
We then calculate

\displaystyle\tilde{p}_{z}\equiv\frac{1}{T}\sum_{i=1}^{T}f(x_{i}).

Note that if we take
X_{i}=f(x_{i})
, then

E(X_{i})=p_{z}
, and therefore from
the Chernoff-Hoeffding bound,

\displaystyle{\rm Pr}\Big[|\tilde{p}_{z}-p_{z}|\leq\epsilon\Big]\geq 1-2e^{-\frac{T\epsilon^{2}}{2}}.

For any polynomial
r
, let us take
\epsilon=\frac{1}{5\times 2^{2k}r}
.
Given
\{\tilde{p}_{z}\}_{z}
, define the probability
distribution
\{q_{z}\}_{z}
by

\displaystyle q_{z}\equiv\frac{|\tilde{p}_{z}|}{\sum_{z\in\{0,1\}^{k}}|\tilde{p}_{z}|}.

Note that it is well defined, because

\displaystyle\sum_{z\in\{0,1\}^{k}}|\tilde{p}_{z}|>0,

which is shown as follows:

\displaystyle\sum_{z\in\{0,1\}^{k}}|\tilde{p}_{z}|\geq\sum_{z\in\{0,1\}^{k}}\tilde{p}_{z}\geq\sum_{z\in\{0,1\}^{k}}(p_{z}-\epsilon)=1-2^{k}\epsilon=1-\frac{1}{5\times 2^{k}r}>0.

Furthermore,
\{q_{z}\}_{z}
is obtained in polynomial time.
The distance between
\{p_{z}\}_{z}
and
\{q_{z}\}_{z}
is

\displaystyle\sum_{z\in\{0,1\}^{k}}|p_{z}-q_{z}|\leq 5\times 2^{2k}\epsilon=\frac{1}{r}.

It is shown as follows. First,

\displaystyle q_{z}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{|\tilde{p}_{z}|}{\sum_{z\in\{0,1\}^{k}}|\tilde{p}_{z}|}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{p_{z}+\epsilon}{1-2^{k}\epsilon}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(p_{z}+\epsilon)(1+2^{k}\epsilon+o(2^{k}\epsilon))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle p_{z}+p_{z}2^{k}\epsilon+p_{z}o(2^{k}\epsilon)+\epsilon+2^{k}\epsilon^{2}+\epsilon o(2^{k}\epsilon)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle p_{z}+5\times 2^{k}\epsilon.

Second,

\displaystyle q_{z}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{|\tilde{p}_{z}|}{\sum_{z\in\{0,1\}^{k}}|\tilde{p}_{z}|}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{p_{z}-\epsilon}{1+2^{k}\epsilon}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(p_{z}-\epsilon)(1-2^{k}\epsilon+o(2^{k}\epsilon))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle p_{z}-p_{z}2^{k}\epsilon+p_{z}o(2^{k}\epsilon)-\epsilon+2^{k}\epsilon^{2}-\epsilon o(2^{k}\epsilon)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle p_{z}-5\times 2^{k}\epsilon.

In this way, we have shown that we can calculate
in classical polynomial time the probability distribution
\{q_{z}\}_{z}
that is close to
\{p_{z}\}_{z}
.
Our final task is to
show
that
\{q_{z}\}_{z}
can be sampled in classical
polynomial time.
For simplicity,
let us assume that each
q_{z}
is represented in the

m
-bit binary:

\displaystyle q_{z}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}2^{-j}a_{z,j},

where
a_{z,j}\in\{0,1\}
for
j=1,2,...,m
.
(Otherwise, by polynomially
increasing the size of
m
, we obtain exponentially
good approximations.)
The following algorithm samples
the probability distribution
\{q_{z}\}_{z}
.

1.

Randomly generate an
m
-bit string

(w_{1},...,w_{m})\in\{0,1\}^{m}
.

2.

Output
z\in\{0,1\}^{k}
such that

\displaystyle\sum_{y<z}q_{y}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{m}2^{-j}w_{j}<\sum_{y\leq z}q_{y},

where
y<z
and
y\leq z
mean the standard dictionary order.
(For example, for three bits,

000<001<010<011<100<101<110<111
.)

In summary, we have shown that

\{p_{z}\}_{z}
can be sampled in polynomial time within a

1/poly(N)
L1-norm error.

Proof of Theorem

In this section, we show Theorem
12
.
Our proof consists of two parts. In the first
subsection, we show that
PDD-Max is BQP-hard. In the second subsection,
we show that PDD-Max is in BQP.

V.1
BQP-hardness

Let
A=(A_{yes},A_{no})
be a promise problem in BQP.
It means
that there exists a uniform family
\{V_{x}\}_{x}
of polynomial-size
quantum circuits such that if
x\in A_{yes}
then

V_{x}
accepts with probability at least
1-2^{-r}
,
and if
x\in A_{no}
then
V_{x}
accepts with probability
at most
2^{-r}
,
where
r
is any polynomial.
More precisely,
let
V_{x}
be the
polynomial-size quantum circuit, which acts on
n=poly(|x|)
qubits,
corresponding to the instance

x
.
If we write
V_{x}|0^{n}\rangle
as

\displaystyle V_{x}|0^{n}\rangle=\sqrt{\alpha}|0\rangle\otimes|\phi_{0}\rangle+\sqrt{1-\alpha}|1\rangle\otimes|\phi_{1}\rangle

with certain
(n-1)
-qubit states
|\phi_{0}\rangle
and

|\phi_{1}\rangle
, we have

\alpha\geq 1-2^{-r}
when
x\in A_{yes}
,
and
\alpha\leq 2^{-r}
when
x\in A_{no}
.
Let us consider the circuit of Fig.
3
.
We call it
U_{1}
.
We also define

\displaystyle U_{2}\equiv H^{\otimes n+m+1}.

We now show that deciding
x\in A_{yes}
or
x\in A_{no}

can be reduced to
a PDD-Max problem with
U_{1}
and
U_{2}
,
which means that PDD-Max is BQP-hard.
In fact, note that

\displaystyle U_{1}|0^{n+m+1}\rangle=\sqrt{\alpha}|0\rangle\otimes|0^{m}\rangle\otimes V_{x}^{\dagger}(|0\rangle\otimes|\phi_{0}\rangle)+\sqrt{1-\alpha}|1\rangle\otimes|+\rangle^{\otimes m}\otimes V_{x}^{\dagger}(|1\rangle\otimes|\phi_{1}\rangle).

Let

p_{z}\equiv|\langle z|U_{1}|0^{n+m+1}\rangle|^{2}

be the probability that we obtain
z\in\{0,1\}^{n+m+1}

when we measure all qubits of
U_{1}|0^{n+m+1}\rangle

in the computational basis.
When
x\in A_{yes}
,

\displaystyle p_{0^{n+m+1}}=|\langle 0^{n+m+1}|U_{1}|0^{n+m+1}\rangle|^{2}=\alpha|\langle 0^{n}|V_{x}^{\dagger}(|0\rangle\otimes|\phi_{0}\rangle)|^{2}=\alpha^{2}\geq(1-2^{-r})^{2}.

When
x\in A_{no}
,

\displaystyle p_{0y}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|\langle 0y|U_{1}|0^{n+m+1}\rangle|^{2}=\alpha\Big|\langle y|\Big[|0^{m}\rangle\otimes V_{x}^{\dagger}(|0\rangle\otimes|\phi_{0}\rangle)\Big]\Big|^{2}\leq\alpha\leq 2^{-r}

\displaystyle p_{1y}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|\langle 1y|U_{1}|0^{n+m+1}\rangle|^{2}=2^{-m}(1-\alpha)|\langle y_{m+1},...,y_{n+m}|V_{x}^{\dagger}(|1\rangle\otimes|\phi_{1}\rangle)|^{2}\leq 2^{-m}

for any
y\in\{0,1\}^{n+m}
.
Let
q_{z}\equiv|\langle z|U_{2}|0^{n+m+1}\rangle|^{2}
,
where
z\in\{0,1\}^{n+m+1}
.
Since
U_{2}=H^{\otimes n+m+1}
, it is obvious that

q_{z}=\frac{1}{2^{n+m+1}}
for any
z
.
Therefore, when
x\in A_{yes}
,

\displaystyle|p_{0^{n+m+1}}-q_{0^{n+m+1}}|=\Big|p_{0^{n+m+1}}-\frac{1}{2^{n+m+1}}\Big|\geq(1-2^{-r})^{2}-2^{-(n+m+1)},

and when
x\in A_{no}
,

\displaystyle|p_{z}-q_{z}|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Big|p_{z}-\frac{1}{2^{n+m+1}}\Big|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle p_{z}+\frac{1}{2^{n+m+1}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\max(2^{-m},2^{-r})+2^{-(n+m+1)}

for any
z\in\{0,1\}^{n+m+1}
.
In this way, we have shown that PDD-Max is BQP-hard.

U_{1}

V.2
In BQP

We next show that PDD-Max is in BQP.
We show that the following BQP algorithm solves PDD-Max
with a
1/poly(N)
completeness-soundness gap
(i.e., the gap of acceptance probabilities between the yes-instances
and the no-instances is lowerbounded by
1/poly(N)
):

1.

Flip a fair coin
s\in\{0,1\}
. Generate
U_{s+1}|0^{N}\rangle
,
and measure each qubit in the computational basis.
Let
z\in\{0,1\}^{N}
be the measurement result.

2.

Repeat the following for
i=1,2,...,T
,
where
T
is a polynomial of
N
specified later.

2-a.

Generate
U_{1}|0^{N}\rangle
, and measure all qubits
in the computational basis. If the result is
z
, then
set
X_{i}=1
. Otherwise, set
X_{i}=0
.

3.

Calculate

\displaystyle\tilde{p}_{z}\equiv\frac{1}{T}\sum_{i=1}^{T}X_{i}.

Note that
E(X_{i})=1\times p_{z}+0\times(1-p_{z})=p_{z}
,
where
p_{z}\equiv|\langle z|U_{1}|0^{N}\rangle|^{2}
.
Therefore, from the Chernoff-Hoeffding bound,

|\tilde{p}_{z}-p_{z}|\leq\epsilon
with probability larger
than
1-2e^{-\frac{T\epsilon^{2}}{2}}
.
In a similar way, calculate the estimator
\tilde{q}_{z}

of
q_{z}\equiv|\langle z|U_{2}|0^{N}\rangle|^{2}
.
From the Chernoff-Hoeffding bound,

|\tilde{q}_{z}-q_{z}|\leq\epsilon
with probability larger
than
1-2e^{-\frac{T\epsilon^{2}}{2}}
.

4.

Calculate
|\tilde{p}_{z}-\tilde{q}_{z}|
.
If
|\tilde{p}_{z}-\tilde{q}_{z}|\geq a-\frac{a-b}{4}
, accept.
Otherwise, reject.

The intuitive idea of this algorithm is as follows.
By the definition of the PDD-Max,
if the answer of the PDD-Max is yes, there exists
z

such that
|p_{z}-q_{z}|
is large. In this case, the probability
of obtaining such
z
in step 1 is large.
Therefore let us assume that we obtain such
z
in step 1.
The step 1 is, in other words, the process to find a
candidate of the solution.
In steps 2 and 3, probabilities
p_{z}
and
q_{z}
are estimated
by using the Chernoff-Hoeffding bound.
Finally, in step 4, we check whether
|p_{z}-q_{z}|
is indeed large,
and accept with high probability since it is actually large.
If the answer of the PDD-Max is no, on the other hand,
there is no

z
such that
|p_{z}-q_{z}|
is large, and therefore in step 4 we do not
conclude that
|p_{z}-q_{z}|
is large except for some failure probability.
(Note that for general

U_{1}
and
U_{2}
, estimating
p_{z}
and
q_{z}
seems to require BQP power,
since it seems to be necessary to generate

U_{1}|0^{N}\rangle
and
U_{2}|0^{N}\rangle
.
We will see in the next section
that if
U_{1}
and
U_{2}
are restricted in FH
2
, the estimation
of
p_{z}
and
q_{z}
can be done in classical polynomial time,
and therefore PDD-Max has a Merlin-Arthur system
with quantum
polynomial-time Merlin.)

Now let us give a more precise proof that
PDD-Max is in BQP.
First, let us consider the case when
the answer to PDD-Max is YES.
If
z
obtained in step 1 satisfies
|p_{z}-q_{z}|\geq a
, and
if
\tilde{p}_{z}
and
\tilde{q}_{z}

calculated in steps 2 and 3 satisfy

|\tilde{p}_{z}-p_{z}|\leq\frac{a-b}{8}
and

|\tilde{q}_{z}-q_{z}|\leq\frac{a-b}{8}
,
we definitely accept in step 4, because

\displaystyle a

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle|p_{z}-q_{z}|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle|p_{z}-\tilde{p}_{z}|+|\tilde{p}_{z}-q_{z}|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle|p_{z}-\tilde{p}_{z}|+|\tilde{p}_{z}-\tilde{q}_{z}|+|\tilde{q}_{z}-q_{z}|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle|\tilde{p}_{z}-\tilde{q}_{z}|+\frac{a-b}{4},

and therefore

\displaystyle|\tilde{p}_{z}-\tilde{q}_{z}|\geq a-\frac{a-b}{4}.

The probability
\eta
of occurring such an event is
calculated to be

\displaystyle\eta

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle{\rm Pr}[\mbox{$z$ obtained in step 1 satisfies $|p_{z}-q_{z}|\geq a$}]

\displaystyle\times{\rm Pr}\Big[|\tilde{p}_{z}-p_{z}|\leq\frac{a-b}{8}\Big]\times{\rm Pr}\Big[|\tilde{q}_{z}-q_{z}|\leq\frac{a-b}{8}\Big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Big(\sum_{z:|p_{z}-q_{z}|\geq a}\frac{p_{z}+q_{z}}{2}\Big)\times{\rm Pr}\Big[|\tilde{p}_{z}-p_{z}|\leq\frac{a-b}{8}\Big]\times{\rm Pr}\Big[|\tilde{q}_{z}-q_{z}|\leq\frac{a-b}{8}\Big]

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{a}{2}\Big(1-2e^{-\frac{T(a-b)^{2}}{128}}\Big)^{2}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{a}{2}(1-2e^{-k})^{2},

where we have taken
T\geq\frac{128k}{(a-b)^{2}}
and
k
is any polynomial
of
N
.
Therefore,
the acceptance probability
p_{acc}
of our protocol is
lowerbounded as

\displaystyle p_{acc}\geq\eta\geq\frac{a}{2}(1-2e^{-k})^{2}\equiv\alpha.

Next, let us consider the case when
the answer to PDD-Max is NO.
If
|\tilde{p}_{z}-p_{z}|\leq\frac{a-b}{8}

and
|\tilde{q}_{z}-q_{z}|\leq\frac{a-b}{8}
,
which occurs with probability
\geq(1-2e^{-k})^{2}
,
we definitely reject,
because

\displaystyle|\tilde{p}_{z}-\tilde{q}_{z}|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle|\tilde{p}_{z}-p_{z}|+|p_{z}-\tilde{q}_{z}|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle|\tilde{p}_{z}-p_{z}|+|p_{z}-q_{z}|+|q_{z}-\tilde{q}_{z}|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{a-b}{4}+b

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle a-\frac{a-b}{4}.

The rejection probability
p_{rej}
is therefore lowerbounded
as

\displaystyle p_{rej}\geq(1-2e^{-k})^{2}.

Therefore,
the acceptance probability
p_{acc}
is
upperbounded as

\displaystyle p_{acc}=1-p_{rej}\leq 1-(1-2e^{-k})^{2}=4e^{-k}-4e^{-2k}\equiv\beta.

The completeness-soundness gap is
therefore

\displaystyle\alpha-\beta=\frac{a}{2}(1-2e^{-k})^{2}-(4e^{-k}-4e^{-2k})\geq\frac{1}{poly(N)}

for sufficiently large
k
,
which shows that PDD-Max is in BQP.

Proof of Theorem

Now let us show Theorem

\displaystyle|\tilde{p}_{z}-\tilde{q}_{z}|\geq a-\frac{a-b}{4},

Note that

\displaystyle p_{z}\equiv|\langle z|U_{1}|0^{N}\rangle|^{2}=\frac{1}{2^{2(N-1)}}\sum_{x,y\in\{0,1\}^{N-1}}f(x,y).

(5)

Its derivation is given in Appendix
C
.
If we set
X_{i}=f(x^{i},y^{i})
, we have

\displaystyle E(X_{i})=\frac{1}{2^{2(N-1)}}\sum_{x,y\in\{0,1\}^{N-1}}f(x,y)=p_{z}.

Therefore, from the Chernoff-Hoeffding bound,

\tilde{p}_{z}
satisfies

with probability
\geq 1-2e^{-\frac{T\epsilon^{2}}{2}}
.
For
\tilde{q}_{z}
, a similar result holds.

Now we show that the above protocol can verify PDD-Max.
The proof is similar to that for the BQP case given in

V
.
First,
let us consider the case when the answer to

If the

that Merlin sends to Arthur satisfies

|p_{z}-q_{z}|\geq a
,
and the estimators
\tilde{p}_{z}
and
\tilde{q}_{z}

that Arthur calculates satisfy

|\tilde{p}_{z}-p_{z}|\leq\frac{a-b}{8}

and
|\tilde{q}_{z}-q_{z}|\leq\frac{a-b}{8}

\displaystyle|\tilde{p}_{z}-\tilde{q}_{z}|

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle|p_{z}-q_{z}|-|\tilde{p}_{z}-p_{z}|-|\tilde{q}_{z}-q_{z}|

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle a-\frac{a-b}{4}.

Taking the honest prover in step 1,
the probability
\eta
of occurring such an event is

\displaystyle\eta

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle{\rm Pr}[\mbox{Merlin obtains $z$ such that $|p_{z}-q_{z}|\geq a$}]

\displaystyle\times{\rm Pr}\Big[|\tilde{p}_{z}-p_{z}|\leq\frac{a-b}{8}\Big]\times{\rm Pr}\Big[|\tilde{q}_{z}-q_{z}|\leq\frac{a-b}{8}\Big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Big(\sum_{z:|p_{z}-q_{z}|\geq a}\frac{p_{z}+q_{z}}{2}\Big)\times{\rm Pr}\Big[|\tilde{p}_{z}-p_{z}|\leq\frac{a-b}{8}\Big]\times{\rm Pr}\Big[|\tilde{q}_{z}-q_{z}|\leq\frac{a-b}{8}\Big]

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{a}{2}\Big(1-2e^{-\frac{T(a-b)^{2}}{128}}\Big)^{2}\geq\frac{a}{2}(1-2e^{-k})^{2},

where we have taken

and

is any polynomial
of

.
Therefore,
the probability
p_{acc}
that Arthur accepts in our protocol is
lowerbounded as

\displaystyle p_{acc}\geq\frac{a}{2}(1-2e^{-k})^{2}\equiv\alpha.

Next, let us consider the case when
the answer to PDD-Max is no.
If
|\tilde{p}_{z}-p_{z}|\leq\frac{a-b}{8}

and
|\tilde{q}_{z}-q_{z}|\leq\frac{a-b}{8}
,
which occurs with probability
\geq(1-2e^{-k})^{2}
,
Arthur definitely rejects,
because

\displaystyle|\tilde{p}_{z}-\tilde{q}_{z}|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle|\tilde{p}_{z}-p_{z}|+|p_{z}-q_{z}|+|q_{z}-\tilde{q}_{z}|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle b+\frac{a-b}{4}<a-\frac{a-b}{4}.

Therefore,
the probability
p_{acc}
that Arthur accepts in our protocol is
upperbounded as

\displaystyle p_{acc}\leq 1-(1-2e^{-k})^{2}=4e^{-k}-4e^{-2k}\equiv\beta.

The completeness-soundness gap is
therefore

\displaystyle\alpha-\beta=\frac{a}{2}(1-2e^{-k})^{2}-(4e^{-k}-4e^{-2k})\geq\frac{1}{poly(N)}

for sufficiently large
k
,
which shows that PDD-Max has a Merlin-Arthur
system with quantum polynomial-time
Merlin.

Remarks
.
To conclude this section, we provide
some remarks. Note that the fact that
p_{z}
and
q_{z}
can be estimated in
classical polynomial-time
seems to be a special property only for some circuits (such as those
in FH
2
),
and we do not know how to do that for other general circuits.
(In particular, in Sec.
VII.2
,
we will explain why the technique we use for FH
2
circuits
will not work
for other circuits, such as FH
3
circuits.)
Furthermore, note that what Arthur can do is to estimate

p_{z}
within an additive error given
z
: he cannot sample
\{p_{z}\}_{z}
.
(If he can do it, he can solve factoring and Simon’s problem,
for example.)

VII
Discussion

VII.1
Generalizations to other circuits

In the proof of Theorem
13
(Sec.
VI
),
we have considered the HC1Q model.
Similar proofs hold for other circuits in FH
2
.
For example, let us consider the IQP model.
For the IQP model,
f(x,y)
is a complex-valued function,
but we can use the Chernoff-Hoeffding bound for complex
random variables introduced in Ref.
NestSchwarz
.

The other example is the HC
m
Q model.
We can show a similar result for it.
In fact, the probability
p_{s,t}
of
obtaining the result
(s,t)\in\{0,1\}^{N-m}\times\{0,1\}^{m}

is

\displaystyle p_{s,t}=\frac{1}{2^{2(N-m)}}\sum_{x,y\in\{0,1\}^{N-m}}(-1)^{C_{1,...,N-m}(x0^{m})\cdot s+C_{1,...,N-m}(y0^{m})\cdot s}\delta_{t,C_{N-m+1,...,N}(x0^{m})}\delta_{t,C_{N-m+1,...,N}(y0^{m})},

and therefore the
Chernoff-Hoeffding bound argument can be used.
Here,
C_{1,...,N-m}(x0^{m})
is the first
N-m
bits of

C(x0^{m})
, and

C_{N-m+1,...,N}(x0^{m})
is the last
m
bits of

C(x0^{m})
.

Furthermore, we would like to point out that PDD-Max
can has a Merlin-Arthur system
with quantum polynomial-time Merlin for other circuits
outside of FH
2
. The essential point of our proof is that
the output probability distributions,
p_{z}
and
q_{z}
,
of
U_{1}
and
U_{2}
can be classically efficiently estimated.
This property itself is not restricted to circuits
in FH
2
, and therefore we believe that our results should hold
for many other circuits outside of FH
2
.

VII.2
FH
3

FH
2
circuits have nice structures such that

p_{z}
and
q_{z}
can be estimated in classical polynomial-time.
We do not know how to do the same thing for other circuits such
as those in FH
3
. The reason is as follows.
From a similar calculation given in Appendix
C
, we can
show that the probability
p_{z}\equiv|\langle z|U|0^{N}\rangle|^{2}

for an FH
3
circuit
U
satisfies

\displaystyle p_{z}=\frac{1}{2^{M}}\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{M}}f(x)

for certain
M=poly(N)
and a certain function
f
.
However, in this case, we no longer have
|f(x)|\leq 1
,
but
f
is exponentially increasing as a function of
N
:

f(x)=2^{s(N)}g(x)
, where

|g(x)|\leq 1
and
s
is a polynomial.
Then, by using the Chernoff-Hoeffding bound,
we can obtain an estimator
\xi
that satisfies

\displaystyle\Big|\xi-\frac{1}{2^{M}}\sum_{x}g(x)\Big|\leq\epsilon

with probability larger than
1-2e^{-\frac{T\epsilon^{2}}{2}}
.
The above inequality means

\displaystyle\Big|\xi-\frac{p_{z}}{2^{s(N)}}\Big|\leq\epsilon,

which also means

\displaystyle|2^{s(N)}\xi-p_{z}|\leq 2^{s(N)}\epsilon.

In order to make
2^{s(N)}\epsilon=O(1/poly(N))
,

\epsilon
must be exponentially small, which means
that
T
must be exponentially large.
Therefore, we cannot obtain a
1/poly(N)
-precision estimator
of
p_{z}
in classical polynomial time.
Note that it is known that as long as we use
f
as a black box,
the Chernoff-Hoeffding type bounds are optimal
(up to some factors)
Canetti
.
This is the reason why our previous proof does not work for
other circuits.

Appendix B
Derivation of Eq. (
4
)

In this appendix, we show Eq. (
4

The initial state is
|0^{N}\rangle
.
We first apply
H^{\otimes(N-1)}\otimes I
.
Then the state becomes

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^{N-1}}}\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{N-1}}|x\rangle|0\rangle.

We apply the classical gate
C
to obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^{N-1}}}\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{N-1}}|C(x0)\rangle.

If we project this state onto

(H^{\otimes k}|z\rangle\langle z|H^{\otimes k})\otimes I^{\otimes N-k}
, where

z\in\{0,1\}^{k}
,
the state becomes

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^{N-1}}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^{k}}}\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{N-1}}(-1)^{C_{1,...,k}(x0)\cdot z}(H^{\otimes k}|z\rangle)\otimes|C_{k+1,...,N}(x0)\rangle.

If we calculate its norm,
which is the probability
p_{z}
,
we obtain Eq. (
4
).
