Peano Arithmetic and μ ​ MALL \mu\mbox{\rm MALL}
Matteo Manighetti Inria Saclay Saclay, France, Dale Miller Inria-Saclay and LIX, Institut Polytechnique de Paris Palaiseau, France

Abstract
Formal theories of arithmetic have traditionally been based on either classical or intuitionistic logic, leading to the development of Peano and Heyting arithmetic, respectively. We propose to use μ ​ MALL \mu\mbox{\rm MALL} as a formal theory of arithmetic based on linear logic. This formal system is presented as a sequent calculus proof system that extends the standard proof system for multiplicative-additive linear logic (MALL) with the addition of the logical connectives universal and existential quantifiers (first-order quantifiers), term equality and non-equality, and the least and greatest fixed point operators. We first demonstrate how functions defined using μ ​ MALL \mu\mbox{\rm MALL} relational specifications can be computed using a simple proof search algorithm. By incorporating weakening and contraction into μ ​ MALL \mu\mbox{\rm MALL} , we obtain μ ​ LK p + \mu\hbox{LK}_{p}^{+} , a natural candidate for a classical sequent calculus for arithmetic. While important proof theory results are still lacking for μ ​ LK p + \mu\hbox{LK}_{p}^{+} (including cut-elimination and the completeness of focusing), we prove that μ ​ LK p + \mu\hbox{LK}_{p}^{+} is consistent and that it contains Peano arithmetic. We also prove some conservativity results regarding μ ​ LK p + \mu\hbox{LK}_{p}^{+} over μ ​ MALL \mu\mbox{\rm MALL} .

1
Introduction

First-order logic formulas are built from propositional connectives,
first-order quantifiers, first-order terms, and the class of
non-logical constants called
predicates
that denote relations
between terms. Moving from first-order logic to first-order
arithmetic, one introduces induction principles and banishes the
predicate constants by formally defining relations between terms using
those inductive principles. When moving from classical logic to
arithmetic in this fashion, one arrives at a presentation of Peano
Arithmetic. In this paper, we continue the project of studying
arithmetic based instead on linear logic that was initiated
in
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
where this linearized
version of arithmetic was called
\mu\mbox{\rm MALL}
. Since that earlier work,
many researchers (
e.g.
[
4
,
5
]
) have adopted
this name to refer to the propositional fragment of this logic,
i.e.
,
the fragment without first-order terms, quantification, and
equality. To emphasize our focus on first-order structures, we will
use the name
MALL
(and
\hbox{LK}_{p}
) in the rest of this paper.

Linear logic has played various roles in computational logic. Many
applications rely on the ability of linear logic to capture the
multiset rewriting paradigm
[
6
]
, which, in turn, can
encode Petri nets
[
7
]
, process
calculi
[
8
,
9
]
, and stateful
computations
[
10
,
11
]
. Our use of linear logic
here will have none of that flavor. While the sequent calculus we use
to present
MALL
in Section
3
is based on multisets
of formulas, we shall not model computation as some rewriting of
multisets of atomic-formulas-as-tokens. In contrast, when we use
linear logic connectives within arithmetic, we capture computation and
deduction via familiar means that rely on relations between numerical
expressions. We propose linearized arithmetic not to build a
non-standard arithmetic but to better understand computation and
reasoning in arithmetic.

Since we are interested in using
MALL
to study
arithmetic
,
we use first-order structures to encode natural numbers and fixed
points to encode relations among numbers. This focus contrasts the
uses of the propositional subset of
MALL
as a typing system (see,
for example,
[
12
]
). We shall limit ourselves to using
invariants to reason inductively about fixed points instead of
employing other methods, such as infinitary proof systems (e.g.,

[
13
]
) and cyclic proof systems (e.g.,

[
14
,
15
]
).

We begin our analysis of arithmetic by demonstrating that functions
defined relationally in
MALL
can be directly computed from their
specifications using unification and backtracking search
(Section
4
). We then introduce a new proof
system,
\hbox{LK}_{p}^{+}
, which extends
MALL
with the weakening and
contraction rules. While the addition of these rules provides a
natural foundation for classical logic, the precise nature of
\hbox{LK}_{p}^{+}
is
not well understood. In particular, we do not yet know if
\hbox{LK}_{p}
, the
cut-free version of
\hbox{LK}_{p}^{+}
, is equivalent to
\hbox{LK}_{p}^{+}
or if it admits a
complete focusing proof system. In this paper, we establish the
consistency of
\hbox{LK}_{p}^{+}
, demonstrate its capacity to encode Peano
Arithmetic (Section
6
), and prove specific
conservativity results of
\hbox{LK}_{p}
over
MALL
(Section
7
).

2.1
Logical connectives involving type
\iota

We first present the logical connectives that relate to first-order
structures. The two quantifiers
\forall
and
\exists
are both
given the type
(\iota\rightarrow o)\rightarrow o
: the terms
\forall(\lambda x.B)

and
\exists(\lambda x.B)
of type
o
are abbreviated as
\forall x.B
and
\exists x.B
, respectively. Equality
=
and non-equality

\neq
are both of the type
\iota\rightarrow\iota\rightarrow o
. For
n\geq 0
, the
least fixed point operator of arity

is written as
\mu_{n}
and the

\nu_{n}

\iota\rightarrow\cdots\rightarrow\iota\rightarrow o

Our formalizations of arithmetic do not contain predicate constants:
we do not admit any non-logical symbols of type

\iota\rightarrow\cdots\rightarrow\iota\rightarrow o

i.e.
, formulas headed by non-logical
symbols. Equality, non-equality, and fixed-point operators are treated

We shall use the usual rules for
\lambda
-conversion, namely,

\alpha
,
\beta
, and
\eta

[
16
]
, as equality
on both terms and formulas. In general, we assume that terms and
formulas are in
\beta

2.3
Polarized and unpolarized formulas

The connectives of linear logic are given a
polarity
as
follows. The
negative
connectives are
\mathrel{\invamp}
,
\bot
,

\mathrel{\&}
,
\top
,
\forall
,
\neq
, and
\nu
while their De Morgan
duals—namely,
\mathrel{\otimes}
,
1
,
\mathrel{\oplus}
,
0
,
\exists
,

=
, and
\mu
—are positive. A
MALL
formula is positive or
negative depending only on the polarity of its topmost connective.
The polarity flips between
B
and
\overline{B}
.
We shall also call
MALL
formulas
polarized formulas
.

Unpolarized formulas
are built using the four usual classical
propositional logic connectives
\land
,
tt
,
\lor
,
ff
plus

=
,
\not=
,
\forall
,
\exists
,
\mu
, and
\nu
. Thus,
the six connectives with
i
in their typing can appear in
polarized and unpolarized formulas. Unpolarized formulas are
also called
classical logic formulas
. Note that unpolarized
formulas do not contain negations. We shall extend the notation

\overline{B}
to unpolarized formulas
B
in the same sense
as used with polarized formulas. For convenience, we will
occasionally allow implications in unpolarized formulas: in those
cases, we treat
P\supset Q
as
\overline{P}\lor Q
.

A polarized formula
\hat{Q}
is a
polarized version
of the
unpolarized formula
Q
if every occurrence of
\mathrel{\&}
and
\mathrel{\otimes}

in
\hat{Q}
is replaced by
\wedge
in
Q
, every occurrence of

\mathrel{\invamp}
and
\mathrel{\oplus}
in
\hat{Q}
is replaced by
\vee
in
Q
, every
occurrence of
1
and
\top
in
\hat{Q}
is replaced by
tt
in

Q
, and every occurrence of
0
and
\bot
in
\hat{Q}
is
replaced by
ff
in
Q
. Notice that if
Q
has
n
occurrences of
propositional connectives, then there are
2^{n}
formulas
\hat{Q}
that
are polarized versions of
Q
.

Fixed point expressions, such as
((\mu\lambda P\lambda x(B~P~x))~t)
, introduce variables of predicate type (here,
P
) into
their scope. In the case of the
\mu
fixed point, any formula built
using that predicate variable as its topmost symbol will be
considered positively polarized. Dually, if the
\nu
operator is
used instead, any formula built using the predicate variable it
introduces is considered negatively polarized. For example,
expression
(p~y)
in
[\mu\lambda p\lambda x.x=z\mathrel{\oplus}\exists y.x=(s~(s~y))\mathrel{\otimes}p~y]
is polarized positively while that
same expression in
[\nu\lambda p\lambda x.x\neq z\mathrel{\&}\forall y.x\neq(s~(s~y))\mathrel{\invamp}p~y]
is polarized negatively.

2.4
The polarization hierarchy

A formula is
purely positive
(resp.,
purely negative
) if
every logical connective it contains is positive (resp., negative).
Taking inspiration from the familiar notion of the arithmetical
hierarchy, we define the following collections of formulas. The
formulas in
{\bf P}_{1}
are the purely positive formulas, and the
formulas in
{\bf N}_{1}
are the purely negative formulas. More
generally, for
n\geq 1
, the
{\bf N}_{n+1}
-formulas are those negative
formulas for which every occurrence of a positive subformula is a

{\bf P}_{n}
-formula. Similarly, the
{\bf P}_{n+1}
-formulas are those
positive formulas for which every occurrence of a negative subformula
is a
{\bf N}_{n}
-formula. A formula in
{\bf P}_{n}
or in
{\bf N}_{n}
has at
most
n-1
alternations of polarity. Clearly, the dual of a

{\bf P}_{n}
-formula is a
{\bf N}_{n}
-formula, and vice versa. We shall also
extend these classifications of formulas to abstractions over terms:
thus, we say that the term
\lambda x.B
of type
i\rightarrow o
is in

{\bf P}_{n}
if
B
is a
{\bf P}_{n}
-formula.

Note that for all
n\geq 1
, if
B
is an unpolarized
\Pi_{n}^{0}
-formula
(in the usual arithmetic hierarchy) then there is a polarized version of

B
that is
{\bf N}_{n}
. Similarly, if
B
is an unpolarized

\Sigma_{n}^{0}
-formula then there is a polarized version of
B
that is

{\bf P}_{n}
.

1.

\displaystyle[\forall x.

\displaystyle{\rm Nature}~x\supset\exists y.{\rm Nature}~y\land\phi(x,y)]\;\land

\displaystyle[\forall x.

\displaystyle{\rm Nature}~x\supset\forall y_{1}.{\rm Nature}~y_{1}\supset\forall y_{2}.{\rm Nature}~y_{2}\supset\phi(x,y_{1})\supset\phi(x,y_{2})\supset y_{1}=y_{2}].

If this formula is polarized so that the two implications are encoded
using
\mathrel{\invamp}
, the conjunction is replaced by
\with
, and the
expression
\phi
is
{\bf P}_{1}
, then this formula is a
{\bf N}_{2}

Given the definition of addition on natural numbers above, the
following totality and determinacy formulas

\displaystyle[\forall x_{1}\forall x_{2}.

\displaystyle~{\rm Nature}~x_{1}\supset{\rm Nature}~x_{2}\supset\exists y.(\hbox{\sl plus\/}(x_{1},x_{2},y)\land{\rm Nature}~y)]

\displaystyle[\forall x_{1}\forall x_{2}.

\displaystyle~{\rm Nature}~x_{1}\supset{\rm Nature}~x_{2}\supset\forall y_{1}\forall y_{2}.~\hbox{\sl plus\/}(x_{1},x_{2},y_{1})\supset\hbox{\sl plus\/}(x_{1},x_{2},y_{2})\supset y_{1}=y_{2}]

can be proved in
MALL
where
\supset
is polarized using
\mathrel{\invamp}

and the one occurrence of conjunction above is polarized using

\with
. These proofs require both induction and the
\mu\nu
rule.

The direct connection between proof search in
MALL
and the model
checking problems of reachability and bisimilarity (and their
negations) has been demonstrated in
[
19
]
. In particular,
reachability problems were encoded as
{\bf P}_{1}
-formulas, while
non-reachability problems were encoded as
{\bf N}_{1}
-formulas. The
paper
[

]
also showed that the specification of

{\bf N}_{2}
- formulas.
Another common form of
{\bf P}_{1}
-formulas arises when applying the Clark
completion
[
20
]
to Horn clause specifications.

3.3
Some known results concerning
MALL

While
MALL
does not contain the contraction rule, it is still
possible for the number of occurrences of logical connectives to grow
in sequents when searching for a proof. In particular, the unfolding
rule (when read from conclusion to premise) can make a sequent
containing
(\mu B\vec{t})
into a sequent containing
(B(\mu B)\vec{t})
: here,
the abstracted formula
B
is repeated. Surprisingly, however,
the subset of
MALL
that does not contain occurrences of fixed
points is still undecidable. In particular, consider the
following two sets of inductively defined classes of
MALL
formulas.

\begin{array}[]{cl}\Phi~::=&\Phi\with\Phi\;|\;\exists x.\Phi\;|\;\forall x.\Phi\;|\;\Psi\\
\Psi~::=&t_{1}=t_{1}^{\prime}\multimap\cdots\multimap t_{n}=t_{n}^{\prime}\multimap t_{0}=t_{0}^{\prime}\quad(n\geq 0)\end{array}

If we also assume that there are exactly three constructors, one each
of type
\iota\rightarrow\iota
,
\iota\rightarrow\iota\rightarrow\iota
, and

\iota\rightarrow\iota\rightarrow\iota\rightarrow\iota
, then it is undecidable whether or not
a given formula
\Phi
is provable in
MALL

[
21
]
.

The two main proof-theoretic results concerning
MALL
are the admissibility of the cut rule (in Figure
3
)
and the completeness of a focusing proof system
[
3
]
.

3.4
Definable exponentials

As Baelde showed in
[
3
]
, the following definitions

\mathord{\mathop{?}}P=\mu(\lambda p.\bot\oplus(p\mathrel{\invamp}p)\oplus P)\qquad\mathord{\mathop{!}}P={\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\overline{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathord{\mathop{?}}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\overline{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}}}}

approximate the exponentials of linear logic in the sense that the
following four rules—dereliction, contraction, weakening, and
promotion—are admissible in
MALL
.

\vdash\mathord{\mathop{?}}B,\Gamma\vdash\Gamma\qquad\vdash\mathord{\mathop{?}}B,\Gamma\vdash\mathord{\mathop{?}}B,\mathord{\mathop{?}}B,\Gamma\qquad\vdash\mathord{\mathop{?}}B,\Gamma\vdash B,\Gamma\qquad\vdash\mathord{\mathop{!}}B,\mathord{\mathop{?}}\Gamma\vdash B,\mathord{\mathop{?}}\Gamma

In particular, we use
\hbox{MALL}(\mathord{\mathop{!}},\mathord{\mathop{?}})
to denote the extension of
MALL
with
the two exponentials
\mathord{\mathop{!}}
and
\mathord{\mathop{?}}
and the above four proof
rules. Thus, every
\hbox{MALL}(\mathord{\mathop{!}},\mathord{\mathop{?}})
-provable sequent can be mapped to a

MALL
-provable sequent by simply replacing the exponentials for
their corresponding fixed point definition.

4
Using proof search to compute functions

We say that a binary relation
\phi
encodes a function
f
if

\phi(x,y)
holds if and only if
f(x)=y
. Of course, this
correspondence is only well defined if we know that the
totality

and
determinacy
properties hold for
\phi
.
For example, let
plus
be the definition of addition on natural
numbers given in Section
3
. The following polarized
formulas encoding totality and determinacy are
{\bf N}_{2}
-formulas.

\displaystyle[\forall x_{1}\forall x_{2}.

\displaystyle~{\rm Nature}~x_{1}\multimap{\rm Nature}~x_{2}\multimap\exists y.{\rm Nature}~y\mathrel{\otimes}\hbox{\sl plus\/}~x_{1}~x_{2}~y]

\displaystyle[\forall x_{1}\forall x_{2}.

\displaystyle~{\rm Nature}~x_{1}\multimap{\rm Nature}~x_{2}\multimap\forall y_{1}\forall y_{2}.~\hbox{\sl plus\/}~x_{1}~x_{2}~y_{1}\multimap\hbox{\sl plus\/}~x_{1}~x_{2}~y_{2}\multimap y_{1}=y_{2}]

These formulas can be proved in
MALL
.

One approach to computing the function that adds two natural numbers
is to follow the Curry-Howard approach of relating proof theory to
computation
[
22
]
. First, extract from a natural
deduction proof of the totality formula above a typed
\lambda
-term.
Second, apply that
\lambda
-term to the
\lambda
-terms representing
the two proofs of, say,
{\rm Nature}~n
and
{\rm Nature}~m
. Third, use a
nondeterministic rewriting process that iteratively selects

\beta
-redexes for reduction. In most typed
\lambda
-calculus
systems, all such sequences of rewritings will end in the same normal
form, although some sequences of rewrites might be very long, and
others can be very short. The resulting normal
\lambda
-term should
encode the proof of
{\rm Nature}~p
, where
p
is the sum of
n
and
m
.
In this section, we will present an alternative mechanism for
computing functions from their relational specification that relies on
using proof search mechanisms instead of this proof-normalization
mechanism.

The totality and determinacy properties of some binary relation
\phi

can be expressed equivalently as: for any natural number
n
, the
expression
\lambda y.\phi(n,y)
denotes a singleton set. Of course,
the sole member of that singleton set is the value of the function it
encodes. If our logic contained a choice operator, such as Church’s

definite description
operator
\iota

[
16
]
,
this function can be represented as
\lambda x.\iota y.\phi(x,y)
. The search for proofs can, however, be used to provide
a more computational approach to computing the function encoded by

\phi
. Assume that
P
and
Q
are predicates of arity one and that

P
denotes a singleton. In this case, the (unpolarized) formulas

\exists x[Px\land Qx]
and
\forall x[Px\supset Qx]
are
logically equivalent, although the proof search semantics of these
formulas are surprisingly different. In particular, if we attempt to
prove
\exists x[Px\land Qx]
, then we must
guess
a term
t

and then
check
that
t
denotes the element of the singleton
(by proving
P(t)
). In contrast, if we attempt to prove
\forall x[Px\supset Qx]
, we allocate an eigenvariable
y
and attempt to
prove the sequent
\vdash Py\supset Qy
. Such an attempt at
building a proof might
compute
the value
t
(especially if we
can restrict proofs of that implication not to involve the general
form of induction). This difference was illustrated in
Section
3
with the proof of
\vdash\exists p.\hbox{\sl plus\/}~{\bf 2}~{\bf 2}~p\mathrel{\otimes}{\rm Nature}~p
(which guesses and checks that
the value of 2 plus 2 is 4) versus the proof of
\forall u(\overline{\hbox{\sl plus\/}~{\bf 2}~{\bf 2}~u}\mathrel{\invamp}{\rm Nature}~u)
(which incrementally
constructs the sum of 2 and 2).

Assume that
P
is a
{\bf P}_{1}
predicate expression of type

i\rightarrow o
and that we have a
MALL
proof of
\forall x[Px\supset{\rm Nature}~x]
. If this proof does not contain the induction rule, then
that proof can be seen as computing the sole member of
P
. As the
following example shows, it is not the case that if there is a
MALL
proof of
\forall x[Px\supset{\rm Nature}~x]
then it has a proof in which
the only form of the induction rule is unfolding.
To illustrate this point,
let
P
be
\mu(\lambda R\lambda x.x={\bf 0}\oplus(R~(s~x)))
.
Clearly,
P
denotes the singleton set containing zero. There is also
a
MALL
proof that
\forall x[Px\supset{\rm Nature}~x]
, but there is no
(cut-free) proof of this theorem that uses unfolding instead of the
more general induction rule: just using
unfoldings leads to an unbounded proof search attempt, which
follows the outline

\vdash\overline{P~y},{\rm Nature}~y\lx@proof@logical@and\vdash{\rm Nature}~{\bf 0}\vdash\overline{P~(s~y)},{\rm Nature}~y\vdash\overline{P~(s~(s~y))},{\rm Nature}~y\vdots

Although proof search can contain potentially unbounded branches, we
can still use the proof search concepts of unification and
nondeterministic search to compute the value within a singleton. We
now define a nondeterministic algorithm to do exactly that. The

state
of this algorithm is a triple of the form

\langle x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\,;~B_{1},\ldots,B_{m}\,;~t\rangle,

where
t
is a term,

B_{1},\ldots,B_{m}
is a multiset of
{\bf P}_{1}
-formulas, and all
variables free in
t
and in the formulas
B_{1},\ldots,B_{m}
are in the set of
variables
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
. A
success state
is one of the form

\langle\cdot\,;~\cdot\,;~t\rangle
(that is, when
n=m=0
): such a state is
said to have
value

t
.

Given the state
S=\langle\Sigma\,;~B_{1},\ldots,B_{m}\,;~t\rangle
with

m\geq 1
, we can nondeterministically select one of the
B_{i}

formulas. For the sake of simplicity, assume that we have selected

B_{1}
. We define the transition
S\Rightarrow S^{\prime}
of state
S
to state

S^{\prime}
by a case analysis of the top-level structure of
B_{1}
.

•

If
B_{1}
is
u=v
and the terms
u
and
v
are unifiable with
most general unifier
\theta
, then we transition to

\langle\Sigma\theta\,;~B_{2}\theta,\ldots,B_{m}\theta\,;~t\theta\rangle.

•

If
B_{1}
is
B\mathrel{\otimes}B^{\prime}
then we transition to

\langle\Sigma\,;~B,B^{\prime},B_{2},\ldots,B_{m}\,;~t\rangle
.

•

If
B_{1}
is
B\mathrel{\oplus}B^{\prime}
then we transition to either

\langle\Sigma\,;~B,B_{2},\ldots,B_{m}\,;~t\rangle
or

\langle\Sigma\,;~B^{\prime},B_{2},\ldots,B_{m}\,;~t\rangle
.

•

If
B_{1}
is
\mu B\vec{t}
then we transition to

\langle\Sigma\,;~B(\mu B)\vec{t},B_{2},\ldots,B_{m}\,;~t\rangle
.

•

If
B_{1}
is
\exists y.~B~y
then we transition to

\langle\Sigma,y\,;~B~y,B_{2},\ldots,B_{m}\,;~t\rangle
assuming that
y

is not in
\Sigma
.

This nondeterministic algorithm is essentially applying
left-introduction rules (assuming a two-sided sequent calculus) in a
bottom-up fashion and, if there are two premises, selecting
(nondeterministically) just one premise to follow.

Lemma 4.1

Assume that
P
is a
{\bf P}_{1}
expression of type
i\rightarrow o
and
that
\exists y.Py
has a
MALL
proof. There is a
sequence of transitions from the initial state

\langle y\,;~P~y\,;~y\rangle
to a success state with value
t

such that
P~t
has a
MALL
proof.

Proof 4.2

An
augmented state
is a structure of the form

\langle\Sigma{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\,\mathrel{|}\,\theta}\,;~B_{1}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\,\mathrel{|}\,\Xi_{1}},\ldots,B_{m}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\,\mathrel{|}\,\Xi_{m}}\,;~t\rangle,

where

•

\theta
is a substitution with domain equal to
\Sigma
and
which has no free variables in its range, and

•

for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
,
\Xi_{i}
is a (cut-free)
MALL
proof of
\theta(B_{i})
.

Note that we are left with a regular state if we strike out the augmented items.
Given that we have a
MALL
proof of
\exists y.Py
, we must have a
MALL
proof
\Xi_{0}
of
P~t
for some term

t
. Note that there is no occurrence of the induction rule in

\Xi_{0}
. We now set the initial augmented state to

\langle y{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\,\mathrel{|}\,[y\mapsto t]}\,;~Py{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\,\mathrel{|}\,\Xi_{0}}\,;~y\rangle
. As we detail
now, the proof structures
\Xi_{i}
provide oracles that steer this
nondeterministic algorithm to a success state with value
t
. Given
the augmented state

\langle\Sigma{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\,\mathrel{|}\,\theta}\,;~B_{1}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\,\mathrel{|}\,\Xi_{1}},\ldots,B_{m}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\,\mathrel{|}\,\Xi_{m}}\,;~s\rangle,

we consider selecting the first pair
B_{1}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\,\mathrel{|}\,\Xi_{1}}
and consider
the structure of
B_{1}
.

•

If
B_{1}
is
B^{\prime}\mathrel{\otimes}B^{\prime\prime}
then the last inference rule of

\Xi_{1}
is
\mathrel{\otimes}
with premises
\Xi^{\prime}
and
\Xi^{\prime\prime}
, and we make a
transition to

\langle\Sigma{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\,\mathrel{|}\,\theta}\,;~B^{\prime}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\,\mathrel{|}\,\Xi^{\prime}},B^{\prime\prime}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\,\mathrel{|}\,\Xi^{\prime\prime}},\ldots,B_{m}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\,\mathrel{|}\,\Xi_{m}}\,;~s\rangle
.

•

If
B_{1}
is
B^{\prime}\mathrel{\oplus}B^{\prime\prime}
then the last inference rule of

\Xi_{1}
is
\mathrel{\oplus}
, and that rule selects either the first or the second
disjunct. In either case, let
\Xi^{\prime}
be the proof of its premise.
Depending on which of these disjuncts is selected, we make a
transition to either

\langle\Sigma{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\,\mathrel{|}\,\theta}\,;~B^{\prime}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\,\mathrel{|}\,\Xi^{\prime}},B_{2}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\,\mathrel{|}\,\Xi_{2}},\ldots,B_{m}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\,\mathrel{|}\,\Xi_{m}}\,;~s\rangle
or

\langle\Sigma{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\,\mathrel{|}\,\theta}\,;~B^{\prime\prime}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\,\mathrel{|}\,\Xi^{\prime}},B_{2}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\,\mathrel{|}\,\Xi_{2}},\ldots,B_{m}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\,\mathrel{|}\,\Xi_{m}}\,;~s\rangle
,
respectively.

•

If
B_{1}
is
\mu B\vec{t}
then the last inference rule of
\Xi_{1}
is

\mu
. Let
\Xi^{\prime}
be the proof of the premise of that inference
rule. We make a transition to

\langle\Sigma{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\,\mathrel{|}\,\theta}\,;~B(\mu B)\vec{t}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\,\mathrel{|}\,\Xi^{\prime}},B_{2}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\,\mathrel{|}\,\Xi_{2}},\ldots,B_{m}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\,\mathrel{|}\,\Xi_{m}}\,;~s\rangle
.

•

If
B_{1}
is
\exists y.~B~y
then the last inference rule of

\Xi_{1}
is
\exists
. Let
r
be the substitution term used to
introduce this
\exists

\Xi^{\prime}
be the proof of
the premise of that inference rule. Then, we make a transition to

\langle\Sigma,w{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\,\mathrel{|}\,\theta\mathbin{\circ}\varphi}\,;~B~w{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\,\mathrel{|}\,\Xi^{\prime}},B_{2}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\,\mathrel{|}\,\Xi_{2}},\ldots,B_{m}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\,\mathrel{|}\,\Xi_{m}}\,;~s\rangle
,
where
w
is a variable not in
\Sigma
and
\varphi
is the
substitution
[w\mapsto r]
. Here, we assume that the composition
of substitutions satisfies the equation
(\theta\mathbin{\circ}\varphi)(x)=\varphi(\theta(x))
.

•

If
B_{1}
is
u=v
and the terms
u
and
v
are unifiable with
most general unifier
\varphi
, then we make a transition to

\langle\Sigma\varphi{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\,\mathrel{|}\,\rho}\,;~\varphi(B_{2}){\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\,\mathrel{|}\,\Xi_{2}},\ldots,\varphi(B_{m}){\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\,\mathrel{|}\,\Xi_{m}}\,;~(\varphi t)\rangle

where
\rho
is the substitution such that
\theta=\varphi\mathbin{\circ}\rho
.

We must show that the transition is made to an augmented state in each
of these cases. This is easy to show in all but the last two rules
above. In the case of the transition due to
\exists
, we know that

\Xi^{\prime}
is a proof of
\theta(B~r)
, but that formula is simply

\varphi(\theta(B~w))
since
w
is new and
r
contains no variables
free in
\Sigma
. In the case of the transition due to equality, we
know that
\Xi_{1}
is a proof of the formula
\theta(u=v)
, which means
that
\theta u
and
\theta v
are the same terms and, hence, that
u

and
v
are unifiable and that
\theta
is a unifier. Let
\varphi

be the most general unifier of
u
and
v
. Thus, there is a
substitution
\rho
such that
\theta=\varphi\mathbin{\circ}\rho
and, for

,

is a proof of

. Finally, termination of this algorithm
is ensured since the number of occurrences of inference rules in the
included proofs decreases at every step of the transition. Since we
have shown that there is an augmented path that terminates, we have
that there exists a path of states to a success state with value
t
.

This lemma ensures that our search algorithm can compute a member from
a non-empty set, given a
MALL
proof that that set is non-empty. We
can now prove the following theorem about singleton sets. We
abbreviate
(\exists x.P~x)\land(\forall x_{1}\forall x_{2}.P~x_{1}\supset P~x_{2}\supset x_{1}=x_{2})
by
\exists!x.P~x
in the
following theorem.

Theorem 4.3

Assume that
P
is a
{\bf P}_{1}
expression of type
i\rightarrow o
and that

\exists!y.Py
has a
MALL
proof. There is a sequence of
transitions from the initial state
\langle y\,;~P~y\,;~y\rangle
to a success
state of value
t
if and only if
P~t
has a
MALL
proof.

Proof 4.4

The forward direction is immediate: given a sequence of transitions
from the initial state
\langle y\,;~P~y\,;~y\rangle
to the success state

\langle\cdot\,;~\cdot\,;~t\rangle
, it is easy to build a
MALL
proof of

P~t
. Conversely, assume that there is a
MALL
proof of
P~t
for
some term
t
and, hence, of
\exists y.P~y
. By
Lemma
4.1
, there is a sequence of transitions from the
initial state
\langle y\,;~P~y\,;~y\rangle
to the success state

\langle\cdot\,;~\cdot\,;~s\rangle
, where
P~s
has a
MALL
proof. Given a
(cut-free)
MALL
proof of
\exists!y.Py
, that proof contains a

MALL
proof of
\forall x_{1}\forall x_{2}.P~x_{1}\supset P~x_{2}\supset x_{1}=x_{2}
, which, when combined using cut with the proofs of the
formulas
Pt
and
Ps
(and the admissibility of cut for

allows us to conclude that

.

Thus, a (naive) proof-search algorithm involving both unification and
nondeterministic search is sufficient for computing the functions
encoded in relations in this setting. This result puts the
computation of such functions inside the domain of logic programming,
where relations, unification, and nondeterministic proof search are
routinely encountered. As a result, deploying any number of
Prolog-style implementation strategies, such as those found in

[
23
,
24

, can make the search for such proofs
more effective.

5
The totality of the Ackermann function

The question of the expressivity of
MALL
has been analyzed by
Baelde
[
2
, Section 3.5]
, who provided a lower bound to
it by characterizing a subset of
MALL
where proofs can be
interpreted as primitive recursive functions, and cut elimination
corresponds to computing those functions. The ideas behind the
encoding can be used in order to express primitive recursive functions
as fixpoints and provide proofs of the totality of these functions in
a similar fashion to what we did for the
plus
relation. However,
Baelde noted that this encoding is insufficient for a computational
interpretation of Ackermann’s function. We can add that it is also
insufficient to obtain a proof that the underlying relation represents
a total function.

We show here a different method, based on the extension to
\hbox{MALL}(\mathord{\mathop{!}},\mathord{\mathop{?}})
provided in Section
3.4
, that allows us to prove
the totality of Ackermann’s function. The encoding of Ackermann’s
function in
MALL
is based on the following relational
specification.

\begin{split}\hbox{\sl ack\/}=\mu(\lambda\hbox{\sl ack\/}\lambda m\lambda n\lambda a.~&(m=0\mathrel{\otimes}a=s~n)\mathrel{\oplus}\\
&\exists p(m=s~p\mathrel{\otimes}n=0\mathrel{\otimes}\hbox{\sl ack\/}~p~(s~0)~a)\mathrel{\oplus}\\
&\exists p\exists q\exists b(m=s~p\mathrel{\otimes}n=s~q\mathrel{\otimes}\hbox{\sl ack\/}~m~q~b\mathrel{\otimes}\hbox{\sl ack\/}~p~b~a))\end{split}

In order to prove that this three-place relation determines a total
function, we need to prove
determinacy
(the first two
arguments uniquely determine the third argument) and
totality

(for every choice of the first two arguments, there exists a
value for the third argument). The proof of determinacy, that is, of
the formula

\forall x\forall y\forall a_{1}\forall a_{2}.(\hbox{\sl ack\/}~x~y~a_{1}\multimap\hbox{\sl ack\/}~x~y~a_{2}\multimap a_{1}=a_{2}),

proceeds simply as follows: first, do induction on
\hbox{\sl ack\/}~x~y~a_{1}

using the rest of the context as an invariant; second, do a case
analysis on the three ways that
\hbox{\sl ack\/}~x~y~a_{1}
is defined, and, third,
in each case use the inductive assumption to complete the proof. We
have described an
MALL
proof since neither contraction nor
weakening is needed in this proof. (This proof outline also applies
to proving the determinacy of the
plus
relation in
Section
3.2
.) In the rest of this section, we prove
the following formula regarding the totality of this relation.

\forall m\forall n(nat~m\multimap nat~n\multimap\exists a.~(\hbox{\sl ack\/}~m~n~a\mathrel{\otimes}nat~a))

We now illustrate a proof of this formula.
1
1

1

prover
[
25
]
is available at

https://www.lix.polytechnique.fr/Labo/Dale.Miller/papers/PA-and-muMALL/
.

In doing so, we will highlight the crucial use of the encoded
exponentials in
\hbox{MALL}(\mathord{\mathop{!}},\mathord{\mathop{?}})
. For greater clarity, we will use

\multimap
as a shorthand, and we will retain the overline syntax for
negation instead of computing the explicit De Morgan duality. The
proof begins by introducing the universal quantifiers and then
applying twice the
\mathrel{\invamp}
rule:

\forall m\forall n(nat~m\multimap nat~n\multimap\exists a.~\hbox{\sl ack\/}~m~n~a)\vdash\overline{nat~m},\overline{nat~n},\exists a.~\hbox{\sl ack\/}~m~n~a

At this point, we need to use the coinduction rule twice, once with

\overline{nat~n}
and once with
\overline{nat~m}

8
Related and future work

The main difference between the hierarchy of
{\bf P}_{n}/{\bf N}_{n}
-formulas
used in this paper and the familiar classes of formulas in the
arithmetic hierarchy (based on quantifier alternations) is the
occurrences of fixed points within formulas. In that regard,
MALL
and
\hbox{LK}_{p}
are probably more easily compared to the extension of Peano
Arithmetic based on general inductive definitions found in

[
36
]
.

Circular proof systems for logics with fixed points have received much
attention in recent years, especially within the context of
linear logic
[
37
,
38
,
12
]

and intuitionistic logic
[
39
]
. Such proof systems
generally eschew all first-order term structures (along with
first-order quantification). They also eschew the use of explicit
invariants and use cycles within proofs as an implicit
approach to discovering invariants.

Historically speaking, the logic
MALL
was developed along with the
construction of the Bedwyr model checker
[
40
]
.
Although that model checker was designed to prove judgments in
classical logic, it became clear that only linear logic principles
were needed to describe most of its behaviors. The paper

[
19
]
illustrates how
MALL
and its (partial)
implementation in Bedwyr can be used to determine standard
model-checking problems such as reachability and simulation. A small
theorem-proving implementation based on the focused proof system

MALL
is described in
[
41
]
: that prover was capable
of proving automatically many of the theorems related to establishing
determinacy and totality of
{\bf P}_{1}
relational specifications.

As mentioned above, whether or not
\hbox{LK}_{p}
satisfies a cut-elimination
theorem or has a (relatively) complete focused proof system are open
questions. Resolving both of these questions is an
important research problem to consider next.

When we know that the rules for contraction and induction are not
involved (as in Section
4
), then proof search in

MALL
resembles computation in the logic programming setting (
i.e.
,
involving unification and nondeterministic search). In
MALL
(in
contrast to
\hbox{LK}_{p}
), contraction is not available, leaving the
generation of coinvariants as the key feature to concentrate on for
automation. A possibly fruitful application of our work on
MALL
would be to organize a theorem prover in such a way that the
cleverness involved with discovering induction coinvariants could be
organized differently: one could always choose to use the “obvious
coinvariant” when attempting a coinductive proof, much as was
done in the prover described in
[
41
]
. The cleverness
that proofs need could be left to the discovery of applicable lemmas:
initially, this discovery would be left to the user of the prover. If
appropriately organized, such a proof would only require the user to
supply a sequence of lemmas; all the remaining details, such as case
analysis and coinvariant generation, would be automated.

Many of the results in this paper are based on Chapter 3 of the first
author’s Ph.D. dissertation
[
42
]
and the technical
report
[
43
]
.

9
Conclusions

In this paper, we have started exploring
MALL
as a linearized
version of arithmetic in a way similar to using Heyting
Arithmetic as a constructive version of arithmetic. In particular, we
have considered three different proof systems. The first is
MALL
,
for which a cut-admissibility theorem is known. The other two are
natural variants of
MALL
that introduce into
MALL
the rules of
contraction and weakening, yielding
\hbox{LK}_{p}
, as well as cut, yielding

\hbox{LK}_{p}^{+}
. We demonstrate that the third proof system is consistent and
powerful enough to encompass all Peano Arithmetic. While it is
known that
MALL
can prove the totality of primitive recursive
function specifications, we demonstrate that the non-primitive
recursive Ackermann function can also be proved total in
MALL
. We
have also demonstrated that if we can prove in
\hbox{LK}_{p}
that a certain

{\bf P}_{1}
relational specification defines a function; then a simple
proof search algorithm can compute that function using unification and
backtracking search. This approach differs from the proof-as-program
interpretation of a constructive proof of the totality of a relational
specification. We have also shown a few simple cases when
\hbox{LK}_{p}
is
conservative over
MALL
.
