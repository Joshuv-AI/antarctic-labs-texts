Correcting Convexity Bias in Function and Functional Estimate
Chao Ma, Lexing Ying

Abstract
A general framework with a series of different methods is proposed to improve the estimate of convex function (or functional) values when only noisy observations of the true input are available. Technically, our methods catch the bias introduced by the convexity and remove this bias from a baseline estimate. Theoretical analysis are conducted to show that the proposed methods can strictly reduce the expected estimate error under mild conditions. When applied, the methods require no specific knowledge about the problem except the convexity and the evaluation of the function. Therefore, they can serve as off-the-shelf tools to obtain good estimate for a wide range of problems, including optimization problems with random objective functions or constraints, and functionals of probability distributions such as the entropy and the Wasserstein distance. Numerical experiments on a wide variety of problems show that our methods can significantly improve the quality of the estimate compared with the baseline method.

Introduction

In this paper, we study the problem of estimating function/functional values when uncertainty exists on the input value. Specifically, let
F
be a function or functional, and
\Omega
be its input domain. We want to estimate
F(\mathbf{x}^{*})
for some
\mathbf{x}^{*}\in\Omega
, while only having access to a set of observations sampled from a probability distribution
\mu
on
\Omega
with
\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{x}^{*}
. Let
\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2},...,\mathbf{x}_{n}
be the observations. By the law of large numbers, the average of the observations,
\bar{\mathbf{x}}:=(\mathbf{x}_{1}+\cdots+\mathbf{x}_{n})/n
, is close to
\mathbf{x}
when
n
is large. Therefore, a straightforward estimate of
F(\mathbf{x}^{*})
is to use
F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})
. However, since
F
is not necessarily linear, biases may exist when
F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})
is used as an estimate. For example, when
F
is convex, the Jensen’s inequality

F(\mathbf{x}^{*})=F(\mathbb{E}\bar{\mathbf{x}})\leq\mathbb{E}F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})

reveals positive bias when
F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})
is used to estimate
F(\mathbf{x}^{*})
. Taking into consideration the special properties of
F
(such as convexity/concavity), it is possible to find estimate methods that have less bias and outperform the straightforward estimate using the sample average.

The function/functional value estimate problem appears in many applications, such as the estimate of functionals of probability distributions (e.g. expectation, entropy, distance etc) or the estimate of optimal value of optimization problems with noisy constraints. Some of these problems have been under investigation for a long time, with many methods proposed. For instance, the James-Stein estimator of the expectation of Gaussian distributions

, the estimate of entropy

,

, mutual information
[

,

,

]
, other functionals of probability distributions
[

,

,

,

]
, etc. Besides, the estimate of the minimizers of quadratic functions has also been studied in recent works
[

. These works usually study a specific class of problems and propose methods that perform well on these problems. Besides methods, related theoretical analysis are also rich in the literature

. The works mentioned here are not meant to be comprehensive. For more detailed discussion of the literature, readers can refer to the reviews in

In this work, instead, we propose a general framework that can improve the estimate for all convex or concave functions/functionals. Our methods produce improved estimate for
F(\mathbf{x}^{*})
by estimating the bias introduced by convexity/concavity using noisy observations and removing that bias from the naive estimate
F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})
. Concretely, we either
shift

F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})
by an appropriate amount
c
and use
F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})+c
as a new estimate, or
scale

F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})
by a factor
s
and use
sF(\bar{\mathbf{x}})
as a new estimate. These “debiasing quantities”
c
and
s
are derived to minimize the square error of the estimate, i.e.

\mathbb{E}\big(F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})+c-F(\mathbf{x}^{*})\big)^{2}\ \ \textrm{or}\ \ \mathbb{E}\big(sF(\bar{\mathbf{x}})-F(\mathbf{x}^{*})\big)^{2}.

The so derived debiasing quantities
c
and
s
are in population sense, i.e. they inevitably depend on unknown quantities such as
\mathbf{x}^{*}
and
\mathbb{E}F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})
. With only the observations
\mathbf{x}_{1},\cdots\mathbf{x}_{n}
in hand, we use bootstrap to approximate these population debiasing quantities and obtain “empirical” debiasing quantities
\hat{c}
and
\hat{s}
(See Section
2
for details). Theoretical analysis are conducted to show that the
\hat{c}
and
\hat{s}
obtained using bootstrap appropriately can indeed reduce the square error of the estimate. In the following is an informal statement of our main theorems
2
and
3
.

Theorem 1
.

(Informal main theorem)
Let
F
be a convex function on
\Omega
and
\mu
be a probability distribution on
\Omega
with
\mathbf{x}^{*}=\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\mathbf{x}
. Let
\hat{c}
and
\hat{s}
be the shifting and scaling debiasing quantities computed using samples
\mathbf{x}_{1},\cdots\mathbf{x}_{n}
drawn from
\mu
. Under some assumptions on
F
,
\mu
, and the bootstrap method, when
n
is sufficiently large, we have

\mathbb{E}\big(F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})+\hat{c}-F(\mathbf{x}^{*})\big)^{2}<\mathbb{E}\big(F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})-F(\mathbf{x}^{*})\big)^{2},

and

\mathbb{E}\big(\hat{s}F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})-F(\mathbf{x}^{*})\big)^{2}<\mathbb{E}\big(F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})-F(\mathbf{x}^{*})\big)^{2}.

Besides the bootstrap method, other method can be employed to estimate the debiasing quantities when more information of
F
is available. One “covariance estimate” method that makes use of
F
’s second-order curvature is also introduced in Section
2
.

Finally, extensive numerical experiments are conducted on a series of problems, from simple convex functions, to optimization problems with stochastic objective function and constrains, and then to the estimate of entropy and Wasserstein distance of probability distributions. The same framework is applied to all the problems, with the only difference being the methods used to solve the problems themselves. In the experiments, our methods can reduce both the expected bias and the expected square error by a significant amount compared with the naive estimate. This shows that the proposed framework can serve as a handy and convenient method to improve the estimate of functions/functionals without requiring specific domain knowledge except convexity/concavity.

Problem settings and methods

2.1
Problem settings

Without loss of generality, we introduce our method using convex functions on Euclidean spaces.
Let
F:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a convex function with
\Omega\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Let
\mu
be a probability distribution on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
that satisfies
\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{x}^{*}
. Given noisy observations
\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2},...,\mathbf{x}_{n}
sampled i.i.d from
\mu
, our goal is to estimate
F(\mathbf{x}^{*})
using only the observations.
As mentioned in the introduction, a naive estimate of
F(\mathbf{x}^{*})
is to use the function value at the sample average of the observations,
F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})
, where
\bar{\mathbf{x}}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{x}_{i}
.
When the function
F
is continuous at
\mathbf{x}^{*}
, this estimate is consistent. However, bias exists due to convexity. By the Jensen’s inequality we have
F(\mathbf{x}^{*})\leq\mathbb{E}F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})
, where the expectation is taken over the sampling of
\mathbf{x}_{1},\cdots,\mathbf{x}_{n}
. This bias may be large if the curvature of
F
is big or the number of available observations
n
is small.
To reduce the convexity bias, we design methods to correct the naive estimate
F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})
. We explore two possible ways of changing
F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})
:
the shifting method
and
the scaling method
. The shifting method takes an additive approach. We estimate a debiasing quantity
c
, add it to
F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})
, and use
F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})+c
as an improved estimate for
F(\mathbf{x}^{*})
. On the other hand, the scaling method takes a multiplicative approach. We estimate another debiasing quantity
s
, multiply it to
F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})
, and use
sF(\bar{\mathbf{x}})
as an improved estimate for
F(\mathbf{x}^{*})
. In the following subsections we introduce the two methods in detail.

2.2
The shifting method

In the shifting method, we find an additive debiasing quantity
c
such that
F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})+c
is a better estimate for
F(\mathbf{x}^{*})
than
F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})
. We measure the quality of the estimate using the squared error

\mathbb{E}\left(F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})+c-F(\mathbf{x}^{*})\right)^{2},

(1)

and find
c
to minimize (
1
). Treating
c
as a constant and expanding (
1
), we obtain a quadratic function of
c
,

c^{2}+2c\mathbb{E}(F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})-F(\mathbf{x}^{*}))+\mathbb{E}(F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})-F(\mathbf{x}^{*}))^{2},

whose minimum is achieved at

c=F(\mathbf{x}^{*})-\mathbb{E}F(\bar{\mathbf{x}}).

(2)

Hence, ideally we can use the
c
defined in (
2
) as the debiasing quantity.

In practise, however,
\mathbf{x}^{*}
and
\mathbb{E}F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})
are unknown, and can only be approximated using noisy observation
\mathbf{x}_{1},\cdots,\mathbf{x}_{n}
. For general
F
and
\mu
, we can use bootstrap
[
5
]
to estimate the
c
in (
2
). bootstrap uses a uniform distribution on
\mathbf{x}_{1},\cdots,\mathbf{x}_{n}
to approximate
\mu
. Denote
\mathcal{X}=\{\mathbf{x}_{1},...,\mathbf{x}_{n}\}
and let
\mu_{\mathcal{X}}
be the uniform distribution on
\mathcal{X}
. For
k=1,2,...,K
, define

\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{k}=\frac{1}{n}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{x}_{k,i},

(3)

where
\{\mathbf{x}_{k,i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
are i.i.d. sampled from
\mu_{\mathcal{X}}
. Then, the empirical distribution of
\{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{k}\}
is an estimate of the distribution of
\bar{\mathbf{x}}
. Hence, the shifting debiasing quantity
c
can be approximated by

c=F(\mathbf{x}^{*})-\mathbb{E}F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})\approx F\left(\frac{1}{K}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{K}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{k}\right)-\frac{1}{K}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{K}F(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{k}).

Since
\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{\mathcal{X}}}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{k}=\bar{\mathbf{x}}
, we have
F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})\approx F\big(\frac{1}{K}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{K}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{k}\big)
when
K
is large. Therefore, we can use

\hat{c}:=F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})-\frac{1}{K}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{K}F(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{k}),

(4)

as an approximation of
c
. With this definition of

The covariance estimate method

bootstrap is not the only approach to estimate the debiasing quantity
c
, especially when more knowledge about the problem is available.
For example, when the distribution
\mu
is concentrated in a region where
F
is close to a quadratic function, we can estimate
c
by estimating the covariance of
\mathbf{x}

F
is close to its second Taylor polynomial at
\mathbf{x}^{*}

F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})\approx F(\mathbf{x}^{*})+\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{*})^{T}(\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^{*})+\frac{1}{2}(\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^{*})^{T}\nabla^{2}F(\mathbf{x}^{*})(\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^{*}).

(5)

Taking expectation, note that
\mathbb{E}(\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^{*})=0
, we have

\mathbb{E}\big(F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})-F(\mathbf{x}^{*})\big)\approx\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}(\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^{*})^{T}\nabla^{2}F(\mathbf{x}^{*})(\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^{*})=\frac{1}{2}\tr(\bar{C}\nabla^{2}F(\mathbf{x}^{*})),

\bar{\mathbf{x}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2n(n-1)}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}(\mathbf{x}_{i}-\bar{\mathbf{x}})^{T}H(\mathbf{x}_{i}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}).

The scaling method

2.4
Dealing with functionals

The methods above can be easily applied to the cases where
F
is a convex/concave functional.
Taking a functional of probability distribution,
F[p]
, as an example. Suppose
p\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
is a distribution on
\Omega\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
F
is convex with respect to
p
. Such functional can be the entropy of
p
, or the distance from
p
to some other probability distributions (e.g. the KL divergence, the Wasserstein distance). Let
\mathbf{x}_{1},...,\mathbf{x}_{n}
be points in
\Omega
i.i.d. sampled from

. Then, the Dirac delta distributions
\delta_{\mathbf{x}_{1}},...,\delta_{\mathbf{x}_{n}}
can be understood as noisy observations of
p
. In this case, the naive estimate of
F[p]
is

, where
\bar{p}
is the empirical distribution

\bar{p}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{\mathbf{x}_{i}}
. To estimate the debiasing quantities with bootstrap, for
k=1,2,...,K
and

, let
\mathbf{x}_{k,i}
be i.i.d. samples taken uniformly from

\tilde{p}_{k}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{\mathbf{x}_{k,i}}.

Then, for the shifting method we can take

\hat{c}=F[\bar{p}]-\frac{1}{K}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{K}F[\tilde{p}_{k}],

3
Theoretical results

In this section, we provide theoretical results which show that our debiasing methods can reduce the expected squared error of the estimate. In the statement of the theorems, without loss of generality, we consider
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
F
as a convex function of
\mathbf{x}
. In the theorems and the proofs, we use Einstein notations to represent tensor contractions involving tensors with rank
\geq 3
. For some examples, if
A,B\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
are matrices,
A_{ab}B^{bc}
represents the matrix product
AB
; if
A\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d\times d\times d}
and
B\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
,
A_{abcd}B^{cd}
gives a
d\times d
matrix by contracting the last two dimensions of
A
with the two dimensions of
B
; if
F:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a function and
\mathbf{x}=(x^{1},\cdots,x^{d}),\bm{y}=(y^{1},\cdots,y^{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
are vectors,
\partial_{abc}^{3}F(\mathbf{x})y^{a}y^{b}y^{c}
means the sum

\sum\limits_{i,j,k=1}^{d}\frac{\partial^{3}F(\mathbf{x})}{\partial x^{i}\partial x^{j}\partial x^{k}}y^{i}y^{j}y^{k}.

The shifting method.

We first make some assumptions on the input distribution
\mu
and the function
F
. In the following, the norm
\|\cdot\|
is by default the
\ell_{2}
norm for vectors and matrices.

Assumption 1
.

The probability distribution
\mu
has up to 8-th finite moments.

Assumption 2
.

F
has finite fourth-order derivatives.

Under the assumptions above, the following main result shows that the shifting method can strictly reduce the expected squared error, as long as
K
is at least at the same order of
n
.

Theorem 2
.

Suppose Assumption
1
and
2
hold. Consider the shifting debiasing method using the debiasing quantity
\hat{c}
defined in (
4
).
Denote

M_{2}=\mathbb{E}_{\mu}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{*})(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{*})^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
,
M_{3}=\mathbb{E}_{\mu}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{*})^{\otimes 3}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d\times d}
be the second and third centered moment tensors of
\mu
. Define

\displaystyle\sigma_{1}

\displaystyle=\partial_{a}F(\mathbf{x}^{*})\partial_{b}F(\mathbf{x}^{*})(M_{2})^{ab}=\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{*})^{T}M_{2}\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{*}),

\displaystyle\sigma_{2}

\displaystyle=\partial^{2}_{ab}F(\mathbf{x}^{*})(M_{2})^{ab}=\tr(M_{2}\nabla^{2}F(\mathbf{x}^{*})),

\displaystyle\sigma_{3}

\displaystyle=\partial_{a}F(\mathbf{x}^{*})\partial^{2}_{bc}F(\mathbf{x}^{*})(M_{3})^{abc},

\displaystyle\sigma_{4}

\displaystyle=\partial_{a}F(\mathbf{x}^{*})(M_{2})^{ab}\partial^{3}_{bcd}F(\mathbf{x}^{*})(M_{2})^{cd}.

Then, if
n
is sufficiently large,
K\geq C_{K}n
for some constant
C_{K}
, and

\frac{\sigma_{2}^{2}}{4}+\sigma_{3}+\sigma_{4}-\frac{\sigma_{1}}{C_{K}}>0,

(11)

we have

\mathbb{E}(F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})+\hat{c}-F(\mathbf{x}^{*}))^{2}<\mathbb{E}(F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})-F(\mathbf{x}^{*}))^{2},

(12)

where the expectation is taken on both the sampling of
\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
and the bootstrap.

Actually, the assumptions for the theorem above do not require
F
to be convex or concave. The debiasing method is effective as long as the quantity
\sigma_{2}^{2}
is larger than the other three terms in the condition
11
. Roughly speaking, this requires the second derivative of
F
is large compared with its first and third derivatives. Though, the condition is easier to hold when
F
is convex or concave, otherwise
\sigma_{2}
might be very small.

The condition (
11
) can be simplified if we problems with certain properties. for example, if
\mu
is symmetric with respect to
\mathbf{x}^{*}
, then we have
M_{3}=0
, and hence
\sigma_{3}=0
. If we further assume that
F
is quadratic, we have the following corollary derived from Theorem
2
:

Corollary 1
.

Let
F(\mathbf{x})=\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{x}^{T}A\mathbf{x}+b
be a quadratic function with
A\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
being a positive definite matrix and
b\in\mathbb{R}
being a scalar. Let
\mu
be a probability distribution on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
that satisfies
\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{x}^{*}
. Suppose
\mu
is symmetric with respect to
\mathbf{x}^{*}
. Then, the shifting debias method can reduce the expected squared error as long as

\frac{\tr(AM_{2})^{2}}{4}>\frac{(\mathbf{x}^{*})^{T}A^{T}M_{2}A\mathbf{x}^{*}}{C_{K}}.

By this corollary, the shifting method works everywhere for quadratic functions as long as
C_{K}
is large enough. This is possible because
C_{K}
is a hyperparameter that we can choose freely as long as the computational resource is sufficient. The following corollary states the condition for the shifting method to work in the limit case in which
C_{K}
is pushed to infinity:

Corollary 2
.

Let
F
satisfy the assumptions in Theorem
2
, and
\mu
be a probability distribution on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
that satisfies
\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{x}^{*}
and is symmetric with respect to
\mathbf{x}^{*}
. Suppose
C_{K}
is sufficiently large. Then, the shifting debias method can reduce the expected squared error as long as

\sigma_{2}^{2}>-4\sigma_{4},

(13)

where
\sigma_{2}
and
\sigma_{4}
are defined the same way as in Theorem
2
.

This corollary shows more clearly that the condition on
F
generally requires the second derivative to be large compared with the first and third derivatives. Quadratic function under the conditions in Corollary
2
always satisfy (
13
) because
\sigma_{4}
vanishes.

A sketch of the proof of Theorem
2
is shown in the next subsection. The full proof is provided in Section
6

and
4
hold. Consider the scaling debiasing method using the debiasing quantity
\hat{s}
defined in (
8
).
Let
M_{2}
,
M_{3}
,
\sigma_{1}
,
\sigma_{2}
,
\sigma_{3}
,
\sigma_{4}
be defined in the same as those in Theorem
2
. Define

\sigma^{\prime}_{3}=\partial_{a}F(\mathbf{x}^{*})\partial_{b}F(\mathbf{x}^{*})\partial_{b}F(\mathbf{x}^{*})(M_{3})^{abc}.

Then, if
n
is sufficiently large,
K\geq C_{K}n
for some constant
C_{K}
, and

\frac{\sigma_{2}^{2}}{4}+\sigma_{3}+\sigma_{4}+\frac{2\sigma^{\prime}_{3}}{F(\mathbf{x}^{*})}+\frac{4\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}}{F(\mathbf{x}^{*})}-\frac{3\sigma_{1}^{2}}{F(\mathbf{x}^{*})^{2}}-\frac{\sigma_{1}}{C_{K}}>0,

(14)

we have

\mathbb{E}(\hat{s}F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})-F(\mathbf{x}^{*}))^{2}<\mathbb{E}(F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})-F(\mathbf{x}^{*}))^{2},

(15)

where the expectation is taken on both the sampling of
\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}
and the bootstrap.

3.1
Proof sketch of Theorem
2

In this section, we show the idea of the proof for Theorem
2
. The proof for Theorem
3
takes a similar approach, with more involved analysis. As an illustration, some arguments in this section might not be rigorous. Strict proofs for both Theorem
2
and
3
are given in later sections.

Notations

In this proof sketch, we write
h=O(g)
if there exists a constant
C
independent with
n
such that
|h|<C|g|
always holds. We use
\textrm{hot}(k)
to denote higher-order-terms containing
\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^{*}
or (and)
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}
with total orders
\geq k
. Given observations
\mathbf{x}_{1},...,\mathbf{x}_{n}
, we use
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}
to denote the random variable given by the average of
n
uniformly drawn samples from
\mathbf{x}_{1},...,\mathbf{x}_{n}
.
When taking expectation, we use
\mathbb{E}
to denote the expectation over both the sampling of
\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}
and the choice of
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}
during bootstrap, and use
\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}
to denote the expectation over
\{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\}
based on a fixed set of
\mathbf{x}_{1},...,\mathbf{x}_{n}
. Let
H(\mathbf{x}):=\nabla^{2}F(\mathbf{x})
. For any matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
and vector
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, denote
\|\mathbf{x}\|^{2}_{A}=\mathbf{x}^{T}A\mathbf{x}
. We note that this is not a norm when
A
is not positive definite.

To prove Theorem
2
, first notice that

E(F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})+\hat{c}-F(\mathbf{x}^{*}))^{2}=\mathbb{E}(F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})-F(\mathbf{x}^{*}))^{2}+\mathbb{E}\hat{c}^{2}+2\mathbb{E}(F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})-F(\mathbf{x}^{*}))\hat{c}.

Hence, we only need to show
\mathbb{E}\big(\hat{c}^{2}+2(F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})-F(\mathbf{x}^{*}))\hat{c}\big)<0
. Let

\bar{c}=\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}\hat{c}=F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})-\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}F(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}),\ \ \textrm{and}\ \delta=\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}F(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})-\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}F(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{k}).

Then, we have
\hat{c}=\bar{c}+\delta
and
\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}\delta=0
, and

\mathbb{E}\big(\hat{c}^{2}+2(F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})-F(\mathbf{x}^{*}))\hat{c}\big)=\mathbb{E}\big(\bar{c}^{2}+2\bar{c}\delta+\delta^{2}+2(F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})-F(\mathbf{x}^{*}))\bar{c}+2(F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})-F(\mathbf{x}^{*}))\delta\big).

Since
\bar{c}
and
F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})-F(\mathbf{x}^{*})
do not depend on the sampling of
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{k}
, by the law of total expectation, we have

\mathbb{E}\bar{c}\delta=\mathbb{E}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}\bar{c}\delta\right)=\mathbb{E}\left(\bar{c}\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}\delta\right)=0,

and similarly
\mathbb{E}(F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})-F(\mathbf{x}^{*}))\delta=0
. Therefore,

\mathbb{E}\big(\hat{c}^{2}+2(F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})-F(\mathbf{x}^{*}))\hat{c}\big)=\mathbb{E}\big(\bar{c}^{2}+\delta^{2}+2(F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})-F(\mathbf{x}^{*}))\bar{c}\big).

(16)

Among the three terms on the right hand side,
\mathbb{E}\bar{c}^{2}
and
\mathbb{E}\delta^{2}
are positive. As an expected debiasing quantity,
\bar{c}
has a negative correlation with
F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})-F(\mathbf{x}^{*})
, hence
2\mathbb{E}(F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})-F(\mathbf{x}^{*}))\bar{c}
is negative.
Next, we will estimate the three terms, and show that under the condition
11
the negative term
\mathbb{E}(F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})-F(\mathbf{x}^{*}))\bar{c}
has larger absolute value than the first two terms. Hence, (
16
) is negative in total.

Estimate of
\mathbb{E}\bar{c}^{2}

Taking a Taylor expansion for
F(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})
at
\bar{\mathbf{x}}
, by Assumption
2
, we have

F(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})-F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})=\nabla F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})^{T}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\bar{\mathbf{x}})+\frac{1}{2}\|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|^{2}_{H(\bar{\mathbf{x}})}+\frac{1}{6}\partial^{3}_{abc}F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})(\tilde{x}^{a}-\bar{x}^{a})(\tilde{x}^{b}-\bar{x}^{b})(\tilde{x}^{c}-\bar{x}^{c})+O(\|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|^{4}).

Taking expectation over
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}
, noting that
\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\bar{\mathbf{x}})=0
, we have

-\bar{c}=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}\|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|^{2}_{H(\bar{\mathbf{x}})}+\textrm{hot}(3)=\frac{1}{2n^{2}}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\|\mathbf{x}_{i}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|^{2}_{H(\bar{\mathbf{x}})}+\textrm{hot}(3).

(17)

The second equality above follows the Lemma
2
. Then, a Taylor expansion of
H(\bar{\mathbf{x}})
at
\mathbf{x}^{*}
further gives

\displaystyle-\bar{c}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2n^{2}}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\|\mathbf{x}_{i}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|^{2}_{H(\mathbf{x}^{*})}+\frac{1}{2n^{2}}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\|\mathbf{x}_{i}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|^{2}_{H(\bar{\mathbf{x}})-H(\mathbf{x}^{*})}+\textrm{hot}(3)

(18)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2n^{2}}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\|\mathbf{x}_{i}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|^{2}_{H(\mathbf{x}^{*})}+\frac{\textrm{hot}(1)}{n}+\textrm{hot}(3).

(19)

In Lemma
6
, we show that generally we have
\mathbb{E}\textrm{hot}(k)=O(n^{-k/2})
(strictly speaking, we need to take the expectation of its square). Also,
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\mathbf{x}_{i}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|^{2}_{H(\mathbf{x}^{*})}\sim O(n)
. Therefore, from
19
we can obtain

\mathbb{E}\bar{c}^{2}=\frac{1}{4n^{4}}\mathbb{E}\left(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\|\mathbf{x}_{i}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|^{2}_{H(\mathbf{x}^{*})}\right)^{2}+O(\frac{1}{n^{2.5}}).

Finally, by showing

\frac{1}{4n^{4}}\mathbb{E}\left(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\|\mathbf{x}_{i}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|^{2}_{H(\mathbf{x}^{*})}\right)^{2}=\frac{\tr(M_{2}H(\mathbf{x}^{*}))^{2}}{4n^{2}}+O(\frac{1}{n^{3}})=\frac{\sigma_{2}^{2}}{4n^{2}}+O(\frac{1}{n^{3}}),

we have the following estimate for
\mathbb{E}\bar{c}^{2}
:

\mathbb{E}\bar{c}^{2}=\frac{\sigma_{2}^{2}}{4n^{2}}+O(\frac{1}{n^{2.5}}).

(20)

Note that the leading term in the estimate has order
O(\frac{1}{n^{2}})
. This is also the leading term in all the following estimates. Finally we compare the coefficients of the leading terms and complete the proof by showing that the total coefficient is negative. The higher-order-terms
O(\frac{1}{n^{2.5}})
can be made sufficiently small when
n
is sufficiently large.

Estimate of
\mathbb{E}\delta^{2}

First, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\delta^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}F(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})-\frac{1}{K}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{K}F(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{k})\right)^{2}=\mathbb{E}\textrm{Var}_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}\left(\frac{1}{K}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{K}F(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{k})\right)=\frac{1}{K}\mathbb{E}\textrm{Var}_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}(F(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{K}\mathbb{E}\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}\big(F(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})-\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}F(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})\big)^{2}\leq\frac{1}{K}\mathbb{E}\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}\big(F(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})-F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})\big)^{2}=\frac{1}{K}\mathbb{E}(F(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})-F(\bar{\mathbf{x}}))^{2}.

Still using a Taylor expansion of
F(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})
at
\bar{\mathbf{x}}
, substituting
K=C_{K}n
, we have

\mathbb{E}\delta^{2}\leq\frac{1}{C_{K}n}\mathbb{E}\left(\nabla F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})^{T}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\bar{\mathbf{x}})+\textrm{hot}(2)\right)^{2}.

Then, expanding
\nabla F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})
at
\mathbf{x}^{*}
gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\delta^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{C_{K}n}\mathbb{E}\left(\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{*})^{T}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\bar{\mathbf{x}})+\textrm{hot}(2)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{C_{K}n}\left(\mathbb{E}\big(\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{*})^{T}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\bar{\mathbf{x}})\big)^{2}+\textrm{hot}(3)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{C_{K}n}\mathbb{E}\big(\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{*})^{T}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\bar{\mathbf{x}})\big)^{2}+O(\frac{1}{n^{2.5}}).

By Lemma
2
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big(\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{*})^{T}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\bar{\mathbf{x}})\big)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\mathbb{E}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\|\mathbf{x}_{i}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|_{\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{*})\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{*})^{T}}^{2}=\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\|\mathbf{x}_{1}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|_{\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{*})\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{*})^{T}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\|\mathbf{x}_{1}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\|_{\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{*})\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{*})^{T}}^{2}+O(\frac{1}{n^{1.5}}).

Therefore,

\mathbb{E}\delta^{2}\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}\|\mathbf{x}_{1}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\|_{\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{*})\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{*})^{T}}^{2}}{C_{K}n^{2}}+O(\frac{1}{n^{2.5}})=\frac{\sigma_{1}}{C_{K}n^{2}}+O(\frac{1}{n^{2.5}}).

(21)

Estimate of
\mathbb{E}(F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})-F(\mathbf{x}^{*}))\bar{c}

A Taylor expansion of
F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})
at
\mathbf{x}^{*}
gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})-F(\mathbf{x}^{*}))\bar{c}=\mathbb{E}\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{*})^{T}(\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^{*})\bar{c}+\mathbb{E}\frac{1}{2}\|\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\|^{2}_{H(\mathbf{x}^{*})}\bar{c}+\mathbb{E}\textrm{hot}(3)\bar{c}.

(22)

For the third term on the right hand side of
22
, recall that the estimate for
\bar{c}
gives
\mathbb{E}\bar{c}^{2}=O(\frac{1}{n^{2}})
, which implies
\mathbb{E}\ \textrm{hot}(3)\bar{c}\leq O(\frac{1}{n^{2.5}})
via the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality.

For the second term, note that the leading term of
\bar{c}
has a negative sign, this second term can be shown to be negative. This term represents the goodness of the debiasing quantity, and is used to offset all other positive terms, including
\mathbb{E}\bar{c}^{2}
and
\mathbb{E}\delta^{2}
. Specifically, by (
19
), we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\frac{1}{2}\|\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\|^{2}_{H(\mathbf{x}^{*})}\bar{c}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{4n^{2}}\mathbb{E}\|\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\|^{2}_{H(\mathbf{x}^{*})}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\mathbf{x}_{i}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|^{2}_{H(\mathbf{x}^{*})}+O(\frac{1}{n^{2.5}}).

Then, we show that

\mathbb{E}\|\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\|^{2}_{H(\mathbf{x}^{*})}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\mathbf{x}_{i}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|^{2}_{H(\mathbf{x}^{*})}=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\left(\sum\limits_{i,j=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\|\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\|_{H(\mathbf{x}^{*})}\right)^{2}+O(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}})=\sigma_{2}^{2}+O(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}),

(details in Section
6
). Therefore, we have

\mathbb{E}\frac{1}{2}\|\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\|^{2}_{H(\mathbf{x}^{*})}\bar{c}=-\frac{\sigma_{2}^{2}}{4n^{2}}+O(\frac{1}{n^{2.5}}).

(23)

The leading term of the estimate above has the same magnitude as the estimate for
\mathbb{E}\bar{c}^{2}
. However, this negative part will take over
\mathbb{E}\bar{c}^{2}
considering the factor
2
before
\mathbb{E}(F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})-F(\mathbf{x}^{*}))\bar{c}
.

Next, we consider the first term on the right hand side of
22
. We need a finer representation for
\bar{c}
. By Lemma
3
, we can improve (
17
) into

\displaystyle-\bar{c}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2n^{2}}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\|\mathbf{x}_{i}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|^{2}_{H(\bar{\mathbf{x}})}+\frac{1}{6n^{3}}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\partial^{3}_{abc}F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})(x_{i}^{a}-\bar{x}^{a})(x_{i}^{b}-\bar{x}^{b})(x_{i}^{c}-\bar{x}^{c})+\textrm{hot}(3)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2n^{2}}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\|\mathbf{x}_{i}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|^{2}_{H(\bar{\mathbf{x}})}+\frac{\textrm{hot}(0)}{n^{2}}+\textrm{hot}(3).

(24)

Taylor expansions of
H(\bar{\mathbf{x}})
and
\nabla^{3}F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})
at
\mathbf{x}^{*}
further give

\bar{c}=-\frac{1}{2n^{2}}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\|\mathbf{x}_{i}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|^{2}_{H(\mathbf{x}^{*})}-\frac{1}{2n^{2}}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\partial^{3}_{abc}F(\mathbf{x}^{*})(x_{i}^{a}-\bar{x}^{a})(x_{i}^{b}-\bar{x}^{b})(\bar{x}^{c}-(x^{*})^{c})+\frac{\textrm{hot}(0)}{n^{2}}+\textrm{hot}(3)

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})-F(\mathbf{x}^{*}))\bar{c}

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{*})^{T}(\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^{*})\frac{1}{2n^{2}}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\|\mathbf{x}_{i}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|^{2}_{H(\mathbf{x}^{*})}

\displaystyle\ \ \ -\mathbb{E}\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{*})^{T}(\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^{*})\frac{1}{2n^{2}}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\partial^{3}_{abc}F(\mathbf{x}^{*})(x_{i}^{a}-\bar{x}^{a})(x_{i}^{b}-\bar{x}^{b})(\bar{x}^{c}-(x^{*})^{c})

\displaystyle\ \ \ +\mathbb{E}\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{*})^{T}(\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^{*})\left(\frac{\textrm{hot}(0)}{n^{2}}+\textrm{hot}(3)\right).

(25)

The last term in (
25
) is obviously
O(\frac{1}{n^{2.5}})
. For the other two terms, we have the following estimate (see the details in Section
6
):

-\mathbb{E}\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{*})^{T}(\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^{*})\frac{1}{2n^{2}}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\|\mathbf{x}_{i}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|^{2}_{H(\mathbf{x}^{*})}=-\frac{\sigma_{3}}{2n^{2}}+O(\frac{1}{n^{2.5}}),

-\mathbb{E}\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{*})^{T}(\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^{*})\frac{1}{2n^{2}}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\partial^{3}_{abc}F(\mathbf{x}^{*})(x_{i}^{a}-\bar{x}^{a})(x_{i}^{b}-\bar{x}^{b})(\bar{x}^{c}-(x^{*})^{c})=-\frac{\sigma_{4}}{2n^{2}}+O(\frac{1}{n^{3}}).

Altogether, we have

\mathbb{E}\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{*})^{T}(\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^{*})\bar{c}\leq-\frac{\sigma_{3}+\sigma_{4}}{2n^{2}}+O(\frac{1}{n^{2.5}}).

And for
\mathbb{E}(F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})-F(\mathbf{x}^{*})\bar{c})
we have

\mathbb{E}(F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})-F(\mathbf{x}^{*})\bar{c})\leq-\frac{\sigma_{2}^{2}}{4n^{2}}-\frac{\sigma_{3}+\sigma_{4}}{2n^{2}}+O(\frac{1}{n^{2.5}}).

(26)

3.1.1
Putting together

Finally, combining the estimates (
20
), (
21
), and (
26
), we have

\mathbb{E}\left(\bar{c}^{2}+\delta^{2}+2(F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})-F(\mathbf{x}^{*})\bar{c})\right)\leq-\frac{\sigma_{2}^{2}}{4n^{2}}+\frac{\sigma_{1}}{C_{K}n^{2}}-\frac{\sigma_{3}+\sigma_{4}}{n^{2}}+O(\frac{1}{n^{2.5}}).

(27)

The coefficient of
\frac{1}{n^{2}}
is negative when condition
11
is satisfied. This completes the proof.

4
Numerical experiments

In this section we show numerical results of the debiasing methods proposed in Section
2
. As a general framework of debiasing convex/concave functions, we test our methods on a wide variety
of problems ranging from simple convex functions, optimization problems, to functionals of probability distributions.
Specifically, we test the following seven problems:
(P1) Quadratic functions,
(P2) Fourth-order polynomials,
(P3) Rational functions,
(P4) Unconstrained optimization problems with random objective functions,
(P5) Constrained optimization problems with random constraints,
(P6) Entropy of discrete probability distribution,
(P7) Wasserstein distance between two probability distributions.
For all the problems, we apply both the shifting and scaling methods using bootstrap. The covariance estimate method is tested on a subset of problems for which it is easy to obtain an estimate of the Hessian at
\mathbf{x}^{*}
.

4.1
P1: Quadratic functions

We consider simple multivariate quadratic functions

F(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{x}^{T}A\mathbf{x},

(P1)

with
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
A\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
being a positive definite matrix. Obviously,
F
is a convex function with respect to
\mathbf{x}
. As described in previous sections, given
A
and a probability distribution
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
which satisfies
\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{x}^{*}
, we estimate
F(\mathbf{x}^{*})
using samples from
\mu
. The debiasing methods are tested for many different
A
’s and
\mu
’s. For each
A
and
\mu
, the experiment is repeated by
R=1000
times, and in each experiment we first sample a new set of noisy observations and then run the debiasing methods.

To measure and compare the performance of our debiasing methods, we compute the root mean squared error (RMSE) and the average bias of the estimates across
1000
experiments, and compare the values with that of the naive estimate. Concretely, let
F_{\textrm{debias}}^{i}
and
F_{\textrm{naive}}^{i}
be the estimate given by the debiasing method and the naive method in the
i
-th experiment, respectively, we compute and compare the following two relative quantities:

\textrm{RMSE}_{r}=\frac{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{1000}(F_{\textrm{debias}}^{i}-F(\mathbf{x}^{*}))^{2}}}{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{1000}(F_{\textrm{naive}}^{i}-F(\mathbf{x}^{*}))^{2}}},\qquad\textrm{Bias}_{r}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{1000}(F_{\textrm{debias}}^{i}-F(\mathbf{x}^{*}))}{\sum_{i=1}^{1000}(F_{\textrm{naive}}^{i}-F(\mathbf{x}^{*}))}.

(28)

By the definitions, when the debiasing method is effective, we expect
\textrm{RMSE}_{r}\in[0,1)
and
\textrm{Bias}_{r}\in(-1,1)
. The closer these quantities are to zero, the more effective the debiasing method. These two errors will also be studied in the experiments for other problems.

Before the debiasing methods are applied, a generic positive definite matrix
A
is generated by its eigenvalue decomposition. Concretely, we generate a diagonal matrix
\Lambda
with positive entries, and an orthogonal matrix
Q
, and then let
A=Q\Lambda Q^{T}
. We take
\mu
as an isotropic Gaussian distribution centered at
\mathbf{x}^{*}
, i.e.
\mu=N(\mathbf{x}^{*},\sigma^{2}I)
.
The experiment results for (P1) are shown in Figure
1
and
2
. In the experiments, we study the performance of all the three debiasing methods (shifting, scaling, covariance estimate) for problems with different dimensions (Figure
1
left), different condition numbers of
A
(Figure
1
middle), different noisy strength
\sigma
(Figure
1
right), and different norm of
\mathbf{x}^{*}
(Figure
2
left). Throughout these experiments, we take
n=10
noisy observations of
\mathbf{x}^{*}
, and do
K=10
rounds of resampling when bootstrap is applied. The figures show that the shifting debiasing method and the covariance estimate method can usually significantly reduce the estimate error and bias, while the scaling method does not perform as well when the dimension is large or the noise is strong. The debiasing methods become less effective when the noise level is low or
\|\mathbf{x}^{*}\|
is large, in which case the noise observations are (effectively) close to
\mathbf{x}^{*}
and the naive estimate is already good. The experiments on the condition number of
A
show that our methods are not sensitive to the spectrum of
A
. Here we remark that the covariance estimate method performs especially well because the function
F
is quadratic, hence the Hessian matrix has all information about the function.

4.5
P5: Constrained optimization problem

Convex debiasing methods can also be applied to constrained optimization problems with randomness. The randomness can appear on the constraints. Consider

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{x}}\ f(\mathbf{x})\ \ \ \ s.t.\ A\mathbf{x}=\bm{b}.

5
Summary

In this work, we propose a general framework to correct the bias introduced by convexity/concavity when estimating the value of functions or functionals. Our methods do not require domain knowledge of the objective functions, and can be applied as long as noisy observations of the groundtruth input are available. Numerical experiments on a wide range of problems show the effectiveness of our methods.

While our methods have general applicability, their performance may not be optimal on specific problems. For example, extensive researches are conducted on the estimate of entropy, and some proposed methods might have better empirical or theoretical properties than our methods. We emphasize that the value of our approach lies mostly on its generality—it can serve as an off-the-shelf tool to debias the estimate of convex functions and obtain improvement upon the naive estimate using the sample mean.

Nevertheless, the gain is not free—it comes with a price of additional computational cost. Compared with the naive estimate, our methods with bootstrap requires evaluating the function for multiple times. For some applications, such as optimization problems, evaluating the function can be expensive. Though, what to blame is not the methods, but the limited observations and knowledge on the problem. This is the cost of generality. If we have more domain knowledge, the covariance estimate method can be applied without high computational cost.

\displaystyle=\frac{d^{3}\max\limits_{i,j,k}\partial_{ijk}F(\mathbf{x}^{*})^{2}}{4n^{4}}\cdot n^{2}O(\frac{1}{n})

\displaystyle=O(\frac{1}{n^{3}}),

(36)

where the fourth to the fifth line is given by Lemma
6
.
Similarly, we can obtain
\mathbb{E}c_{2}^{2}=O(\frac{1}{n^{4}})
. And by Lemma
7
and similar derivations, we can obtain
\mathbb{E}c_{4}^{2}=O(\frac{1}{n^{4}})
. For
c_{3}
, the Taylor expansion
62
gives

\displaystyle|c_{3}|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{1}{6n^{3}}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\partial^{3}_{abc}F(\mathbf{x}^{*})(x_{i}^{a}-\bar{x}^{a})(x_{i}^{b}-\bar{x}^{b})(x_{i}^{c}-\bar{x}^{c})+\frac{1}{6n^{3}}O(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\|\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\|\|\mathbf{x}_{i}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|^{3})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{d^{3}\max\limits_{a,b,c}\partial^{3}_{abc}|F(\mathbf{x}^{*})|}{6n^{3}}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\|\mathbf{x}_{i}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|^{3}+\frac{1}{6n^{3}}O(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\|\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\|\|\mathbf{x}_{i}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|^{3}).

Taking square and by Lemma
6
we have
\mathbb{E}c_{3}^{2}=O(\frac{1}{n^{4}})
.
Therefore, considering
(|c_{1}|+|c_{2}|+|c_{3}|+|c_{4}|)^{2}\leq 4(c_{1}^{2}+c_{2}^{2}+c_{3}^{2}+c_{4}^{2})
, back to (
34
) we have

\mathbb{E}\bar{c}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{4n^{4}}\mathbb{E}\left(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\|\mathbf{x}_{i}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|^{2}_{H(\mathbf{x}^{*})}\right)^{2}+O(\frac{1}{n^{2.5}}).

(37)

Next, by
\mathbf{x}_{i}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}=(\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{x}^{*})+(\mathbf{x}^{*}-\bar{\mathbf{x}})
we obtain

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{x}_{i}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|^{2}_{H(\mathbf{x}^{*})}=\|\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\|^{2}_{H(\mathbf{x}^{*})}+2(\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{x}^{*})^{T}H(\mathbf{x}^{*})(\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^{*})+\|\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\|^{2}_{H(\mathbf{x}^{*})}.

Note that the terms with powers of
\|\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\|
become small quantities (no bigger than
O(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}})
) after taking expectation, we have

\frac{1}{4n^{4}}\mathbb{E}\left(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\|\mathbf{x}_{i}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|^{2}_{H(\mathbf{x}^{*})}\right)^{2}=\frac{1}{4n^{4}}\mathbb{E}\left(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\|\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\|^{2}_{H(\mathbf{x}^{*})}\right)^{2}+O(\frac{1}{n^{2.5}}).

Thus,

\mathbb{E}\bar{c}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{4n^{4}}\mathbb{E}\left(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\|\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\|^{2}_{H(\mathbf{x}^{*})}\right)^{2}+O(\frac{1}{n^{2.5}}).

Further notice that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4n^{4}}\mathbb{E}\left(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\|\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\|^{2}_{H(\mathbf{x}^{*})}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4n^{4}}\sum\limits_{i,j=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\|\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\|^{2}_{H(\mathbf{x}^{*})}\|\mathbf{x}_{j}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\|^{2}_{H(\mathbf{x}^{*})}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4n^{4}}\sum\limits_{i,j=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\|\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\|^{2}_{H(\mathbf{x}^{*})}\mathbb{E}\|\mathbf{x}_{j}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\|^{2}_{H(\mathbf{x}^{*})}

\displaystyle\ \ \ +\frac{1}{4n^{4}}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\left(\mathbb{E}\|\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\|^{4}_{H(\mathbf{x}^{*})}-\big(\mathbb{E}\|\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\|^{2}_{H(\mathbf{x}^{*})}\big)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4n^{2}}\big(\mathbb{E}\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\|^{2}_{H(\mathbf{x}^{*})}\big)^{2}+O(\frac{1}{n^{3}}).

Finally, since
\mathbb{E}\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\|^{2}_{H(\mathbf{x}^{*})}=\tr(M_{2}H(\mathbf{x}^{*}))
, we have the following estimate for
\mathbb{E}\bar{c}^{2}
:

\mathbb{E}\bar{c}^{2}\leq\frac{\tr(M_{2}H(\mathbf{x}^{*}))^{2}}{4n^{2}}+O(\frac{1}{n^{2.5}})=\frac{\sigma_{2}^{2}}{4n^{2}}+O(\frac{1}{n^{2.5}}).

(38)

6.2.2
Estimate of
\mathbb{E}\delta^{2}
.

For
\mathbb{E}\delta^{2}
, first notice that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\delta^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}F(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})-\frac{1}{K}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{K}F(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{k})\right)^{2}=\mathbb{E}\textrm{Var}_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}\left(\frac{1}{K}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{K}F(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{k})\right)=\frac{1}{K}\mathbb{E}\textrm{Var}_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}(F(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{K}\mathbb{E}\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}\big(F(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})-\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}F(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})\big)^{2}\leq\frac{1}{K}\mathbb{E}\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}\big(F(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})-F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})\big)^{2}=\frac{1}{K}\mathbb{E}(F(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})-F(\bar{\mathbf{x}}))^{2}.

Hence, taking the Taylor expansion for
F(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})
at
\bar{\mathbf{x}}
, the last term above gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\delta^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{C_{K}n}\mathbb{E}\left(\nabla F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})^{T}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\bar{\mathbf{x}})+\frac{1}{2}\|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|^{2}_{H(\bar{\mathbf{x}})}\right.

\displaystyle\ \ \ +\left.\frac{1}{6}\partial^{3}_{abc}F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})(\tilde{x}^{a}-\bar{x}^{a})(\tilde{x}^{b}-\bar{x}^{b})(\tilde{x}^{c}-\bar{x}^{c})+O(\|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|^{4})\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{C_{K}n}\mathbb{E}\left(\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{*})^{T}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\bar{\mathbf{x}})+O(\|\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\|\|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|)+O(\|\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\|^{2}\|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|)\right.

\displaystyle\ \ \ +O(\|\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\|^{3}\|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|)+\frac{1}{2}\|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|^{2}_{H(\mathbf{x}^{*})}+O(\|\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\|\|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|^{2})

\displaystyle\ \ \ \left.+O(\|\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\|^{2}\|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|^{2})+\frac{1}{6}\nabla^{3}F(\mathbf{x}^{*})[\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}]+O(\|\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\|\|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|^{3})+O(\|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|^{4})\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{C_{K}n}\left(\mathbb{E}\big(\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{*})^{T}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\bar{\mathbf{x}})\big)^{2}+\textrm{hot}(\|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|,\|\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\|;3)\right).

(39)

where the second equality follows from the Taylor expansions of
\nabla F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})
,
H(\bar{\mathbf{x}})
, and
\nabla^{3}F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})
at
\mathbf{x}^{*}
.
The highest-order terms in
\textrm{hot}(\|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|,\|\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\|;3)
have order
8
. Therefore, by Lemma
6
and
7
, we have
\mathbb{E}\textrm{hot}(\|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|,\|\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\|;3)=O(\frac{1}{n^{1.5}})
, and thus

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\delta^{2}\leq\frac{1}{C_{K}n}\mathbb{E}\big(\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{*})^{T}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\bar{\mathbf{x}})\big)^{2}+O(\frac{1}{n^{2.5}})=\frac{1}{C_{K}n}\mathbb{E}\|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|^{2}_{\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{*})\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{*})^{T}}+O(\frac{1}{n^{2.5}}).

Next, by Lemma
2
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|_{\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{*})\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{*})^{T}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\mathbb{E}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\|\mathbf{x}_{i}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|_{\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{*})\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{*})^{T}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\|\mathbf{x}_{1}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|_{\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{*})\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{*})^{T}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\|\mathbf{x}_{1}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\|_{\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{*})\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{*})^{T}}^{2}+O(\frac{1}{n^{1.5}}).

(40)

Hence,
\mathbb{E}\delta^{2}
can be bounded by:

\mathbb{E}\delta^{2}\leq\frac{1}{C_{K}n^{2}}\mathbb{E}\big(\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{*})^{T}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{*})\big)^{2}+O(\frac{1}{n^{2.5}})=\frac{\sigma_{1}}{C_{K}n^{2}}+O(\frac{1}{n^{2.5}}).

(41)

6.2.3
Estimate of
\mathbb{E}(F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})-F(\mathbf{x}^{*}))\bar{c}

Taking Taylor expansion for
F
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})-F(\mathbf{x}^{*}))\bar{c}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\big(\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{*})^{T}(\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^{*})+\frac{1}{2}\|\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\|^{2}_{H(\mathbf{x}^{*})}

\displaystyle\ \ \ +\frac{1}{6}\partial^{3}_{abc}F(\mathbf{x}^{*})(\bar{x}^{a}-(x^{*})^{a})(\bar{x}^{b}-(x^{*})^{b})(\bar{x}^{c}-(x^{*})^{c})+O(\|\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\|^{4})\big)\bar{c}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{*})^{T}(\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^{*})\bar{c}+\mathbb{E}\frac{1}{2}\|\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\|^{2}_{H(\mathbf{x}^{*})}\bar{c}+\mathbb{E}O(\|\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\|^{3})\bar{c}

(42)

We will estimate the three terms above using the estimate (
33
) for
\bar{c}
.

For the first term on the right hand side of (
42
), there exists a constant
C
, such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{*})^{T}(\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^{*})\bar{c}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{*})^{T}(\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^{*})\left(-\frac{1}{2n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\mathbf{x}_{i}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|^{2}_{H(\mathbf{x}^{*})}+c_{1}+O(c_{2})+c_{3}+O(c_{4})\right)

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{1}{2n^{2}}\mathbb{E}\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{*})^{T}(\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^{*})\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\mathbf{x}_{i}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|^{2}_{H(\mathbf{x}^{*})}+\mathbb{E}\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{*})^{T}(\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^{*})c_{1}

\displaystyle\ \ +C\|\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{*})\|\mathbb{E}\|\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\|(|c_{2}|+|c_{3}|+|c_{4}|).

(43)

We first consider the first term on the right hand side of (
43
). For convenience, let
\bm{z}_{i}=\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{x}^{*}
, and
\bar{\bm{z}}=\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^{*}
. Then, we have
\mathbb{E}\bm{z}_{i}=0
, and

\mathbb{E}\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{*})^{T}(\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^{*})\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}(\mathbf{x}_{i}-\bar{\mathbf{x}})^{T}H(\mathbf{x}^{*})(\mathbf{x}_{i}-\bar{\mathbf{x}})=\mathbb{E}\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{*})^{T}\bar{\bm{z}}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}(\bm{z}_{i}-\bar{\bm{z}})^{T}H(\mathbf{x}^{*})(\bm{z}_{i}-\bar{\bm{z}}).

For the right hand side, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{*})^{T}\bar{\bm{z}}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}(\bm{z}_{i}-\bar{\bm{z}})^{T}H(\mathbf{x}^{*})(\bm{z}_{i}-\bar{\bm{z}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{*})^{T}\bar{\bm{z}}\left(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\bm{z}_{i}^{T}H(\mathbf{x}^{*})\bm{z}_{i}-n\bar{\bm{z}}^{T}H(\mathbf{x}^{*})\bar{\bm{z}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{*})^{T}\frac{1}{n}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\bm{z}_{i}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\bm{z}_{i}^{T}H(\mathbf{x}^{*})\bm{z}_{i}-n\mathbb{E}\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{*})^{T}\bar{\bm{z}}\cdot\bar{\bm{z}}^{T}H(\mathbf{x}^{*})\bar{\bm{z}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum\limits_{i,j=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{*})^{T}\bm{z}_{i}\bm{z}_{j}^{T}H(\mathbf{x}^{*})\bm{z}_{j}-n\mathbb{E}\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{*})^{T}\bar{\bm{z}}\cdot\bar{\bm{z}}^{T}H(\mathbf{x}^{*})\bar{\bm{z}}.

(44)

For the first term above, because of the independence of
\bm{z}_{1},...,\bm{z}_{n}
and
\mathbb{E}\bm{z}_{i}=0
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum\limits_{i,j=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{*})^{T}\bm{z}_{i}\bm{z}_{j}^{T}H(\mathbf{x}^{*})\bm{z}_{j}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{*})^{T}\bm{z}_{i}\bm{z}_{i}^{T}H(\mathbf{x}^{*})\bm{z}_{i}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{*})^{T}\bm{z}_{1}\bm{z}_{1}^{T}H(\mathbf{x}^{*})\bm{z}_{1}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{3}

(45)

For the second term in (
44
), by Lemma
6
we have

\displaystyle\big|n\mathbb{E}\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{*})^{T}\bar{\bm{z}}\cdot\bar{\bm{z}}^{T}H(\mathbf{x}^{*})\bar{\bm{z}}\big|

\displaystyle\leq n\|F(\mathbf{x}^{*})\|\lambda_{\max}\mathbb{E}\|\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\|^{3}=O(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}).

(46)

Combining (
45
) and (
46
) gives

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2n^{2}}\mathbb{E}\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{*})^{T}(\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^{*})\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\mathbf{x}_{i}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|^{2}_{H(\mathbf{x}^{*})}

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{\sigma_{3}}{2n^{2}}+O(\frac{1}{n^{2.5}}).

(47)

Next, we consider the second term on the right hand side of (
43
). Using the same
\bm{z}
notations as above, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{*})^{T}(\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^{*})c_{1}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2n^{2}}\mathbb{E}\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{*})^{T}\bar{\bm{z}}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\partial_{abc}^{3}F(\mathbf{x}^{*})(z_{i}^{a}-\bar{z}^{a})(z_{i}^{b}-\bar{z}^{b})\bar{z}^{c}.

For the right hand side, we have

\displaystyle-\mathbb{E}\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{*})^{T}\bar{\bm{z}}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\partial_{abc}^{3}F(\mathbf{x}^{*})(z_{i}^{a}-\bar{z}^{a})(z_{i}^{b}-\bar{z}^{b})\bar{z}^{c}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\mathbb{E}\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{*})^{T}\bar{\bm{z}}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\partial_{abc}^{3}F(\mathbf{x}^{*})z_{i}^{a}z_{i}^{b}\bar{z}^{c}+n\mathbb{E}\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{*})^{T}\bar{\bm{z}}\partial_{abc}^{3}F(\mathbf{x}^{*})\bar{z}^{a}\bar{z}^{b}\bar{z}^{c}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum\limits_{i,j,k=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{*})^{T}\bm{z}_{i}\big(\partial^{3}_{abc}F(\mathbf{x}^{*})z_{j}^{a}z_{j}^{b}z_{k}^{c}\big)+n\mathbb{E}\textrm{hot}(\bar{\bm{z}};4)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum\limits_{i,j,k=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{*})^{T}\bm{z}_{i}\big(\partial^{3}_{abc}F(\mathbf{x}^{*})z_{j}^{a}z_{j}^{b}z_{k}^{c}\big)+O(\frac{1}{n}).

(48)

By the independence of
\bm{z}_{i}
and
\mathbb{E}\bm{z}_{i}=0
, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum\limits_{i,j,k=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{*})^{T}\bm{z}_{i}\big(\partial^{3}_{abc}F(\mathbf{x}^{*})z_{j}^{a}z_{j}^{b}z_{k}^{c}\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum\limits_{i,j=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{*})^{T}\bm{z}_{i}\big(\partial^{3}_{abc}F(\mathbf{x}^{*})z_{j}^{a}z_{j}^{b}z_{i}^{c}\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum\limits_{i,j=1}^{n}(\mathbb{E}\bm{z}_{i}^{T}\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{*})\bm{z}_{i})^{T}(\mathbb{E}\partial^{3}_{abc}F(\mathbf{x}^{*})z_{j}^{b}z_{j}^{c})

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\left(\mathbb{E}\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{*})^{T}\bm{z}_{i}\partial_{abc}^{3}F(\mathbf{x}^{*})z_{i}^{a}z_{i}^{b}z_{i}^{c}-(\mathbb{E}\bm{z}_{i}^{T}\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{*})\bm{z}_{i})^{T}(\mathbb{E}\partial_{abc}^{3}F(\mathbf{x}^{*})z_{i}^{b}z_{i}^{c}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\mathbb{E}\bm{z}^{T}\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{*})\bm{z})^{T}(\mathbb{E}\partial_{abc}^{3}F(\mathbf{x}^{*})z^{b}z^{c})+O(\frac{1}{n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{*})^{T}M_{2})(\partial_{abc}^{3}F(\mathbf{x}^{*})(M_{2})^{bc})+O(\frac{1}{n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sigma_{4}+O(\frac{1}{n}).

(49)

Therefore, we have

\mathbb{E}\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{*})^{T}(\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^{*})c_{1}=-\frac{\sigma_{4}}{2n^{2}}+O(\frac{1}{n^{3}}).

(50)

For the third term in (
43
), recall that
\mathbb{E}c_{2}^{2}=O(\frac{1}{n^{4}})
,
\mathbb{E}c_{3}^{2}=O(\frac{1}{n^{4}})
, and
\mathbb{E}c_{4}^{2}=O(\frac{1}{n^{4}})
, by Hölder’s inequality we have

\mathbb{E}\|\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\|(|c_{2}|+|c_{3}|+|c_{4}|)=O(\frac{1}{n^{2.5}}).

(51)

Combining (
47
), (
50
), and (
51
), we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{*})^{T}(\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^{*})\bar{c}

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{\sigma_{3}+\sigma_{4}}{2n^{2}}+O(\frac{1}{n^{2.5}}).

(52)

For the second term on the right hand side of (
42
), there exists a constant
C
, such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\frac{1}{2}\|\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\|^{2}_{H(\mathbf{x}^{*})}\bar{c}

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{1}{4n^{2}}\mathbb{E}\|\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\|^{2}_{H(\mathbf{x}^{*})}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\mathbf{x}_{i}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|^{2}_{H(\mathbf{x}^{*})}+\mathbb{E}\left|\frac{1}{2}\|\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\|^{2}_{H(\mathbf{x}^{*})}\right|C\sum\limits_{i=1}^{4}|c_{i}|

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{4n^{2}}\mathbb{E}\|\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\|^{2}_{H(\mathbf{x}^{*})}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\mathbf{x}_{i}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|^{2}_{H(\mathbf{x}^{*})}+O(\frac{1}{n^{2.5}}),

(53)

where the estimate of
\mathbb{E}\|\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\|_{H(\mathbf{x}^{*})}(|c_{1}|+|c_{2}|+|c_{3}|+|c_{4}|)
follows similarly the estimate of

\frac{1}{2n^{2}}\mathbb{E}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\|\mathbf{x}_{i}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|^{2}_{H(\mathbf{x}^{*})}(|c_{1}|+|c_{2}|+|c_{3}|+|c_{4}|)
when we deal with
\mathbb{E}\bar{c}^{2}
.
For the first term on the right hand side of (
53
), we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^{*})^{T}H(\mathbf{x}^{*})(\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^{*})\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\mathbf{x}_{i}-\bar{\mathbf{x}})^{T}H(\mathbf{x}^{*})(\mathbf{x}_{i}-\bar{\mathbf{x}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^{*})^{T}H(\mathbf{x}^{*})(\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^{*})\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{x}^{*})^{T}H(\mathbf{x}^{*})(\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{x}^{*})+O(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum\limits_{i,j,k=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{x}^{*})^{T}H(\mathbf{x}^{*})(\mathbf{x}_{j}-\mathbf{x}^{*})(\mathbf{x}_{k}-\mathbf{x}^{*})^{T}H(\mathbf{x}^{*})(\mathbf{x}_{k}-\mathbf{x}^{*})+O(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum\limits_{i,k=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{x}^{*})^{T}H(\mathbf{x}^{*})(\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{x}^{*})(\mathbf{x}_{k}-\mathbf{x}^{*})^{T}H(\mathbf{x}^{*})(\mathbf{x}_{k}-\mathbf{x}^{*})+O(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum\limits_{i,k=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{x}^{*})^{T}H(\mathbf{x}^{*})(\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{x}^{*})\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{x}_{k}-\mathbf{x}^{*})^{T}H(\mathbf{x}^{*})(\mathbf{x}_{k}-\mathbf{x}^{*})+O(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sigma_{2}^{2}+O(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}).

Note that from the fourth to the fifth line we are moving terms with
i=k
into the
O(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}})
term, and adding
\frac{1}{n^{2}}\big(\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{x}^{*})^{T}H(\mathbf{x}^{*})(\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{x}^{*})\big)^{2}
terms, which are also no bigger than
O(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}})
. Hence, back to (
53
) we have

\mathbb{E}\frac{1}{2}\|\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\|^{2}_{H(\mathbf{x}^{*})}\bar{c}\leq-\frac{\sigma_{2}^{2}}{4n^{2}}+O(\frac{1}{n^{2.5}}).

(54)

For the third term on the right hand side of (
42
), recall that
\mathbb{E}\bar{c}^{2}=O(\frac{1}{n^{2}})
, we have

\mathbb{E}\|\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\|^{3}\bar{c}\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\|\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\|^{6}\mathbb{E}\bar{c}^{2}}=O(\frac{1}{n^{2.5}}).

(55)

Finally, combining (
52
), (
54
) and (
55
), we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})-F(\mathbf{x}^{*}))\bar{c}

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{\sigma_{2}^{2}}{4n^{2}}-\frac{\sigma_{3}+\sigma_{4}}{2n^{2}}+O(\frac{1}{n^{2.5}}).

(56)

6.2.4
Putting together.

Finally, putting the estimates (
38
), (
41
), (
56
) together, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\hat{c}^{2}+2(F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})-F(\mathbf{x}^{*}))\hat{c})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\sigma_{2}^{2}}{4n^{2}}+\frac{\sigma_{1}}{C_{K}n^{2}}-\frac{\sigma_{2}^{2}}{2n^{2}}-\frac{\sigma_{3}}{n^{2}}-\frac{\sigma_{4}}{n^{2}}+O(\frac{1}{n^{2.5}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\left(-\frac{\sigma_{2}^{2}}{4}+\frac{\sigma_{1}}{C_{K}}-\sigma_{3}-\sigma_{4}\right)+O(\frac{1}{n^{2.5}}).

(57)

By the condition (
11
), the coefficient for
\frac{1}{n^{2}}
is negative. Hence, when
n
is sufficiently large, we have

\mathbb{E}(\hat{c}^{2}+2(F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})-F(\mathbf{x}^{*}))\hat{c})<0.

(58)

This completes the proof.

Lemma 1
.

Let
F:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a function satisfying Assumption

\mathbf{x},\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

F
and its derivatives:

\displaystyle F(\bm{y})

\displaystyle=F(\mathbf{x})+\nabla F(\mathbf{x})^{T}(\bm{y}-\mathbf{x})+\frac{1}{2}(\bm{y}-\mathbf{x})^{T}\nabla^{2}F(\mathbf{x})(\bm{y}-\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle\ \ \ +\frac{1}{6}\partial^{3}_{abc}F(\mathbf{x})(y^{a}-x^{a})(y^{b}-x^{b})(y^{c}-x^{c})+O(\|\bm{y}-\mathbf{x}\|^{4}),

(59)

\displaystyle\nabla F(\bm{y})

\displaystyle=\nabla F(\mathbf{x})+\nabla^{2}F(\mathbf{x})(\bm{y}-\mathbf{x})+\frac{1}{2}\partial^{3}_{abc}F(\mathbf{x})(y^{b}-x^{b})(y^{c}-x^{c})+O(\|\bm{y}-\mathbf{x}\|^{3}),

(60)

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}F(\bm{y})

\displaystyle=\nabla^{2}F(\mathbf{x})+\partial^{3}_{abc}F(\mathbf{x})(y^{c}-x^{c})+O(\|\bm{y}-\mathbf{x}\|^{2}),

(61)

\displaystyle\nabla^{3}F(\bm{y})

\displaystyle=\nabla^{3}F(\mathbf{x})+O(\|\bm{y}-\mathbf{x}\|).

(62)

The next two lemmas deal with the expectation of quadratic forms and third order monomials over the choice of
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}

Lemma 2
.

Let
\nu
be the uniform distribution on
n
points
\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2},...,\mathbf{x}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
\bar{\mathbf{x}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{x}_{i}=\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\mathbf{x}
. Let
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}
be the empirical mean of
m
samples i.i.d. sampled from
\nu
, i.e.
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}
,
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle iid}}{{\sim}}\nu
. Let
A\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
be an arbitrary matrix. Then,

\mathbb{E}_{\nu}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\bar{\mathbf{x}})^{T}A(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\bar{\mathbf{x}})=\frac{1}{nm}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}(\mathbf{x}_{i}-\bar{\mathbf{x}})^{T}A(\mathbf{x}_{i}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}).

Proof.

We ignore the subscript
\nu
in the expectation. Obviously, we have
\mathbb{E}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}=\bar{\mathbf{x}}
and
\mathbb{E}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}=\bar{\mathbf{x}}
. Hence,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\bar{\mathbf{x}})^{T}A(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\bar{\mathbf{x}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m^{2}}\sum\limits_{i,j=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}-\bar{\mathbf{x}})^{T}A(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{j}-\bar{\mathbf{x}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m^{2}}\left(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}-\bar{\mathbf{x}})^{T}A(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}-\bar{\mathbf{x}})+\sum\limits_{i\neq j}\mathbb{E}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}-\bar{\mathbf{x}})^{T}A(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{j}-\bar{\mathbf{x}})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m^{2}}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}-\bar{\mathbf{x}})^{T}A(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}-\bar{\mathbf{x}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m}\frac{1}{n}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}(\mathbf{x}_{i}-\bar{\mathbf{x}})^{T}A(\mathbf{x}_{i}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}).

∎

Lemma 3
.

Let
\nu
be the uniform distribution on
n
points
\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2},...,\mathbf{x}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
\bar{\mathbf{x}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{x}_{i}=\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\mathbf{x}
. Let
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}
be the empirical mean of
m
samples i.i.d. sampled by
\nu
, i.e.
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}

\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle iid}}{{\sim}}\nu
. Let

be an arbitrary

tensor. Then,

\mathbb{E}_{\nu}T_{abc}(\tilde{x}^{a}-\bar{x}^{a})(\tilde{x}^{b}-\bar{x}^{b})(\tilde{x}^{c}-\bar{x}^{c})=\frac{1}{m^{2}n}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}T_{abc}(x_{i}^{a}-\bar{x}^{a})(x_{i}^{b}-\bar{x}^{b})(x_{i}^{c}-\bar{x}^{c}).

Proof.

The proof is similar to that of Lemma
2
. Still ignore the subscript
\nu
in the expectation. We have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}T_{abc}(\tilde{x}^{a}-\bar{x}^{a})(\tilde{x}^{b}-\bar{x}^{b})(\tilde{x}^{c}-\bar{x}^{c})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}T_{abc}\left(\frac{1}{m}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\tilde{x}^{a}_{i}-\bar{x}^{a}\right)\left(\frac{1}{m}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\tilde{x}^{b}_{i}-\bar{x}^{b}\right)\left(\frac{1}{m}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\tilde{x}^{c}_{i}-\bar{x}^{c}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m^{3}}\sum\limits_{i,j,k=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}T_{abc}(\tilde{x}^{a}_{i}-\bar{x}^{a})(\tilde{x}^{b}_{j}-\bar{x}^{b})(\tilde{x}^{c}_{k}-\bar{x}^{c})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m^{3}}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}T_{abc}(\tilde{x}^{a}_{i}-\bar{x}^{a})(\tilde{x}^{b}_{i}-\bar{x}^{b})(\tilde{x}^{c}_{i}-\bar{x}^{c})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m^{2}}\frac{1}{n}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}T_{abc}(x_{i}^{a}-\bar{x}^{a})(x_{i}^{b}-\bar{x}^{b})(x_{i}^{c}-\bar{x}^{c}).

∎

The next lemma deals with the residual term
\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}\|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|^{3}
of
\bar{c}
.

Lemma 4
.

Let
\nu
be the uniform distribution on
n
points
\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2},...,\mathbf{x}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
\bar{\mathbf{x}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{x}_{i}=\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\mathbf{x}
. Let
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}
be the empirical mean of
n
samples i.i.d. sampled by
\nu
, i.e.
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}
,
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle iid}}{{\sim}}\nu
. Then,

\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|^{3}\leq\frac{1}{n^{3}}\left(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\|\mathbf{x}_{i}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|^{2}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\|\mathbf{x}_{i}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|^{4}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}+\frac{\sqrt{3}}{n^{3}}\left(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\|\mathbf{x}_{i}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|^{2}\right)^{\frac{3}{2}}.

Proof.

We ignore the subscript
\nu
in the expectation. By Cauchy-Schwartz inequality, we have

\mathbb{E}\|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|^{3}\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|^{2}\mathbb{E}\|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|^{4}}.

(63)

For
\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|^{2}
, by Lemma
2
, we have

\mathbb{E}\|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|^{2}=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\|\mathbf{x}_{i}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|^{2}.

(64)

For
\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|^{4}
, noting the independency of
\{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}\}
, we expand the expression and drop the zero terms, and obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|^{4}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{4}}\sum\limits_{i,j,k,l=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}-\bar{\mathbf{x}})^{T}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{j}-\bar{\mathbf{x}})(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{k}-\bar{\mathbf{x}})^{T}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{l}-\bar{\mathbf{x}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{4}}\left(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|^{4}+\sum\limits_{i\neq j}\mathbb{E}\|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|^{2}\|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{j}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|^{2}+2\sum\limits_{i\neq j}\mathbb{E}\big((\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}-\bar{\mathbf{x}})^{T}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{j}-\bar{\mathbf{x}})\big)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n^{3}}\mathbb{E}\|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{1}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|^{4}+\frac{3}{n^{2}}\big(\mathbb{E}\|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{1}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|^{2}\big)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{4}}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\|\mathbf{x}_{i}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|^{4}+\frac{3}{n^{4}}\left(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\|\mathbf{x}_{i}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|^{2}\right)^{2}.

(65)

Substituting (
64
) and (
65
) into (
63
), and use the result
\sqrt{a+b}\leq\sqrt{a}+\sqrt{b}
completes the proof.
∎

The next two lemmas estimates the moments of
\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{*}
,
\mathbf{x}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}
and
\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^{*}
. The first one is a general result for moments of the average of i.i.d random variables.

Lemma 5
.

Let
X
be a random variable taking values on
\mathbb{R}
, and
X_{1},X_{2},...,X_{n}
are i.i.d copies of
X
. Let
\bar{X}=\frac{1}{n}(X_{1}+X_{2}+\cdots+X_{n})
. Let
k
be a positive integer. Assume
\mathbb{E}X=0
, and
X
has up to
2k
-th order finite moments. Then, for any
l\leq 2k
, we have
\mathbb{E}|\bar{X}|^{l}=O(\frac{1}{n^{l/2}})
.

Proof.

We first show the results for even
l
. Consider the set

\mathcal{S}_{l}=\left\{(r_{1},r_{2},...,r_{p}):\ r_{1}\geq r_{2}\geq...\geq r_{p}>0,\ \sum\limits_{i=1}^{p}r_{i}=l\right\}.

Let
|\mathcal{S}_{l}|
be the number of elements in
\mathcal{S}_{l}
. Then,
|\mathcal{S}_{l}|
is a number that depends only on
l
. When
l
is an even number, we have

\mathbb{E}|\bar{X}|^{l}=\frac{1}{n^{l}}\mathbb{E}\big(X_{1}+...+X_{n}\big)^{l}=\frac{1}{n^{l}}\mathbb{E}\left(\sum\limits_{(r_{1},...,r_{p})\in\mathcal{S}}C_{(r_{1},...,r_{p})}\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}i_{1},...,i_{p}=1\\
i_{1}\neq...\neq i_{p}\end{subarray}}^{n}X_{i_{1}}^{r_{1}}X_{i_{2}}^{r_{2}}\cdots X_{i_{p}}^{r_{p}}\right).

(66)

The right hand side of (
66
) is the multinomial expansion of
(X_{1}+...+X_{n})^{l}
, organized according to the powers of different
X_{i}
’s in each term, and
C_{(r_{1},...,r_{p})}
are positive integers related with multinomial coefficients.
C_{(r_{1},...,r_{p})}
depends on
l
and
r_{1},...,r_{p}
, but is independent with
n
. Since
\mathbb{E}X=0
, the expectation of
X_{i_{1}}^{r_{1}}X_{i_{2}}^{r_{2}}\cdots X_{i_{p}}^{r_{p}}
is nonzero only when there is no
r_{i}
that equals to
1
. Hence, (
66
) equals to

\frac{1}{n^{l}}\left(\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}(r_{1},...,r_{p})\in\mathcal{S}\\
r_{p}\geq 2\end{subarray}}C_{(r_{1},...,r_{p})}\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}i_{1},...,i_{p}=1\\
i_{1}\neq...\neq i_{p}\end{subarray}}^{n}\mathbb{E}\big(X_{i_{1}}^{r_{1}}X_{i_{2}}^{r_{2}}\cdots X_{i_{p}}^{r_{p}}\big)\right).

(67)

Because
X_{i_{1}},...,X_{i_{p}}
are independent, we have
\mathbb{E}\big(X_{i_{1}}^{r_{1}}X_{i_{2}}^{r_{2}}\cdots X_{i_{p}}^{r_{p}}\big)=\prod_{j=1}^{p}\mathbb{E}X_{i_{j}}^{r_{j}}
. By the finite moment assumption of
X
, we can find a constant
C
such that
|\mathbb{E}X^{j}|\leq C
holds for any
1\leq j\leq 2k
. Then, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n^{l}}\left(\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}(r_{1},...,r_{p})\in\mathcal{S}\\
r_{p}\geq 2\end{subarray}}C_{(r_{1},...,r_{p})}\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}i_{1},...,i_{p}=1\\
i_{1}\neq...\neq i_{p}\end{subarray}}^{n}\mathbb{E}\big(X_{i_{1}}^{r_{1}}X_{i_{2}}^{r_{2}}\cdots X_{i_{p}}^{r_{p}}\big)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n^{l}}\left(\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}(r_{1},...,r_{p})\in\mathcal{S}\\
r_{p}\geq 2\end{subarray}}C_{(r_{1},...,r_{p})}\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}i_{1},...,i_{p}=1\\
i_{1}\neq...\neq i_{p}\end{subarray}}^{n}C^{p}\right).

Moreover, since
r_{1}\geq r_{2}\geq...\geq r_{p}\geq 2
, we have
p\leq l/2
, and hence

\frac{1}{n^{l}}\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}(r_{1},...,r_{p})\in\mathcal{S}\\
r_{p}\geq 2\end{subarray}}C_{(r_{1},...,r_{p})}\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}i_{1},...,i_{p}=1\\
i_{1}\neq...\neq i_{p}\end{subarray}}^{n}C^{l}\leq\frac{1}{n^{l}}\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}(r_{1},...,r_{p})\in\mathcal{S}\\
r_{p}\geq 2\end{subarray}}C_{(r_{1},...,r_{p})}n^{\frac{l}{2}}C^{l}\leq\frac{|S_{l}|\max C_{(r_{1},...,r_{p})}C^{l}}{n^{l/2}}.

This completes the proof for even
l
.

For odd
l
, note that
2k\geq l+1
. The result follows by a Cauchy-Schwartz inequality:

\mathbb{E}|\bar{X}|^{l}\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}|\bar{X}|^{l+1}\mathbb{E}|\bar{X}|^{l-1}}.

∎

Lemma 6
.

Let
\mu
be a probability distribution on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
that satisfies Assumption
1
, and
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2},...,\mathbf{x}_{n}
be i.i.d samples from

, we have

Proof.

The first result is given by Assumption
1
. The third result is a direct corollary of Lemma
5
. The second results follows the triangle inequality:

\|\mathbf{x}_{i}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|^{k}\leq\big(\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\|+\|\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\|\big)^{k}\leq 2^{k}\big(\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\|^{k}+\|\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\|^{K}\big).

∎

Lemma 7
.

Let
\mu
be a probability distribution on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
that satisfies Assumption
1
, and
\nu
is the uniform ditribution on
[n]

\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2},...,\mathbf{x}_{n}

7.1
Additional notations

In this section, we will expand our use of the
hot
notations. We use
\textrm{hot}_{\bar{\mathbf{x}}}
to denote higher-order-terms with coefficients depending on
F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})
or derivatives of
F
evaluated at
\bar{\mathbf{x}}
. For example,

\big(\nabla F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})^{T}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\bar{\mathbf{x}})\big)^{2}+F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})\|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|^{2}_{H(\bar{\mathbf{x}})}

can be represented by
\textrm{hot}_{\bar{\mathbf{x}}}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\bar{\mathbf{x}};2)
. By Lemma
1
,
F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})
,
\nabla F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})
,
\nabla^{2}F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})
and
\nabla^{3}F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})
can be represented by
\textrm{hot}(\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^{*};0)
. (Note that in the Taylor expansion the coefficients depends on
\mathbf{x}^{*}
, which is treated as a constant.) Hence, any
\textrm{hot}_{\bar{\mathbf{x}}}(f_{1},\cdots,f_{k};r)
can be represented by
\textrm{hot}(f_{1},\cdots,f_{k},\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^{*};r)
. Moreover, we use
\textrm{hot}_{\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}}
to denote higher-order-terms that expectations with respect to
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}
are taken for some parts. For example,

\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{*})^{T}(\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^{*})\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}\|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|^{2}_{H(\mathbf{x}^{*})}

can be represented by
\textrm{hot}_{\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}}(\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^{*},\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\bar{\mathbf{x}};3)
. Using the Hölder’s inequality and Jensen’s inequality, it is easy to show that the
\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}
does not change the order of the expectation of the higher-order-terms. For any
r>0
, we have

\mathbb{E}\textrm{hot}_{\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}}(\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^{*},\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\bar{\mathbf{x}};r)=O(\frac{1}{n^{r/2}}).

7.2
Main proof

We take a similar path as the proof of Theorem
2
. Specifically, in this section we consider another
\hat{c}
defined as
\hat{c}=(\hat{s}-1)F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})
, then we have

\mathbb{E}(\hat{s}F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})-F(\mathbf{x}^{*}))^{2}=\mathbb{E}(F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})+\hat{c}-F(\mathbf{x}^{*}))^{2},

which gives the same form as the shifting debiasing, but with a different debiasing quantity. To prove the theorem, we still show
\mathbb{E}\hat{c}^{2}+2\hat{c}(F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})-F(\mathbf{x}^{*}))<0
.

We first decompose
\hat{c}
to separate the effect of the sampling of
\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}
and the sampling of
\{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{k}\}
. Recall that

\hat{s}=\frac{F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})\sum\limits_{k=1}^{K}F(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{k})}{\sum\limits_{k=1}^{K}F(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{k})^{2}}=\frac{F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})\frac{1}{K}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{K}F(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{k})}{\frac{1}{K}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{K}F(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{k})^{2}}.

Define

,
\bar{c}=(\bar{s}-1)F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})
, and

Then, we have

\displaystyle\hat{c}

\displaystyle=\bar{c}+(\hat{s}-\bar{s})F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})=\bar{c}+\frac{F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})^{2}\big(s_{1}\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}F(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})^{2}-s_{2}\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}F(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})\big)}{(\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}F(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})^{2}+s_{2})\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}F(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})^{2}}

\displaystyle=\bar{c}+\frac{F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})^{2}\big(s_{1}\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}F(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})^{2}-s_{2}\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}F(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})\big)}{(\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}F(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})^{2})^{2}}-\frac{F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})^{2}\big(s_{1}\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}F(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})^{2}-s_{2}\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}F(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})\big)s_{2}}{(\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}F(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})^{2}+s_{2})(\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}F(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})^{2})^{2}}.

Denote

\delta_{1}=\frac{F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})^{2}\big(s_{1}\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}F(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})^{2}-s_{2}\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}F(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})\big)}{(\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}F(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})^{2})^{2}},\quad\delta_{2}=-\frac{F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})^{2}\big(s_{1}\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}F(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})^{2}-s_{2}\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}F(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})\big)s_{2}}{(\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}F(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})^{2}+s_{2})(\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}F(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})^{2})^{2}}.

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left(\bar{c}^{2}+\delta_{1}^{2}+\delta_{2}^{2}+2\bar{c}\delta_{1}+2\bar{c}\delta_{2}+2\delta_{1}\delta_{2}\right.

\displaystyle+\left.2\bar{c}(F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})-F(\mathbf{x}^{*})+2\delta_{1}(F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})-F(\mathbf{x}^{*})+2\delta_{2}(F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})-F(\mathbf{x}^{*})\right)

7.2.5

\bar{\mathbf{x}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{x}_{i}
, and
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{x}_{k_{i}}
. Then, for any positive integer
k
, we have

\mathbb{E}\|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|^{k}=O(\frac{1}{n^{k/2}}),

where the expectation is taken on both
\mu
and
\nu
.

The next two lemmas characterize the moments of
s_{1}
,
s_{2}
, and
F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})
,
F(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})
. The
s_{1}
and
s_{2}
are defined in (
69
).

Lemma 10
.

Under the same assumptions in Theorem
3
, for any positive integer
l
, we have

\mathbb{E}|s_{1}|^{l}\leq O(\frac{1}{(Kn)^{\frac{l}{2}}}),\ \textrm{and}\ \ \mathbb{E}|s_{2}|^{l}\leq O(\frac{1}{(Kn)^{\frac{l}{2}}})

Proof.

First, we consider
s_{1}
. Without loss of generality, assume
l
is an even number. By the law of total expectation, we have

\mathbb{E}|s_{1}|^{l}=\mathbb{E}\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}\left|\frac{1}{K}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{K}F(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{k})-\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}F(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})\right|^{l}.

(103)

For
k=1,2,...,K
, let
Y_{k}=F(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{k})-\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}F(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})
, and
Y
be an i.i.d. copy of
Y_{k}
. Then,
\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}Y=0
. Since
\mathbf{x}_{1},\cdots,\mathbf{x}_{n}
are finite, we have
\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}|Y|^{l}<\infty
. Let
\hat{Y}=\frac{Y}{\big(\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}|Y|^{l}\big)^{1/l}}
and
\hat{Y}_{k}=\frac{Y_{k}}{\big(\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}|Y|^{l}\big)^{1/l}}
. Then, by the Hölder’s inequality, for any
r\leq l
, we have
\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}|\hat{Y}|^{r}\leq 1
. Therefore, applying Lemma
5
, we have

\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}\left|\frac{1}{K}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{K}\hat{Y}_{k}\right|^{l}\leq O(\frac{1}{K^{l/2}})\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}|\hat{Y}|^{l}\leq O(\frac{1}{K^{l/2}}),

which implies

\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}\left|\frac{1}{K}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{K}Y_{k}\right|^{l}\leq O(\frac{1}{K^{l/2}})\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}|Y|^{l}.

Back to (
103
), we have

\mathbb{E}|s_{1}|^{l}\leq O(\frac{1}{K^{l/2}})\mathbb{E}\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}\left|F(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})-\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}F(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})\right|^{l}.

(104)

By Hölder’s inequality and Jensen’s inequality, we have

\displaystyle\left|F(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})-\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}F(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})\right|^{l}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{l-1}\left(|F(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})-F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})|^{l}+|\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}F(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})-F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})|^{l}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2^{l-1}\left(|F(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})-F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})|^{l}+\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}|F(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})-F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})|^{l}\right).

Hence,

\mathbb{E}\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}\left|F(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})-\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}F(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})\right|^{l}\leq 2^{l}\mathbb{E}|F(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})-F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})|^{l}

For
F(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})-F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})
, by Taylor expansion, we have

\displaystyle F(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})-F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\nabla F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})^{T}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\bar{\mathbf{x}})+\frac{1}{2}\|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|_{H(\bar{\mathbf{x}})}+\frac{1}{6}\partial_{abc}^{3}F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})(\tilde{x}^{a}-\bar{x}^{a})(\tilde{x}^{b}-\bar{x}^{b})(\tilde{x}^{c}-\bar{x}^{c})+O(\|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|^{4})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{*})^{T}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\bar{\mathbf{x}})+(\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^{*})^{T}H(\mathbf{x}^{*})(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\bar{\mathbf{x}})+\frac{1}{2}\partial^{3}_{abc}F(\mathbf{x}^{*})(\bar{x}^{a}-(x^{*})^{a})(\bar{x}^{b}-(x^{*})^{b})(\tilde{x}^{c}-\bar{x}^{c})

\displaystyle+O(\|\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\|^{3}\|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|)+\frac{1}{2}\|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|_{H(\mathbf{x}^{*})}+\frac{1}{2}\partial_{abc}^{3}F(\mathbf{x}^{*})(\bar{x}^{a}-(x^{*})^{a})(\tilde{x}^{b}-\bar{x}^{b})(\tilde{x}^{c}-\bar{x}^{c})

\displaystyle+O(\|\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\|^{2}\|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|^{2})+\frac{1}{6}\partial_{abc}^{3}F(\mathbf{x}^{*})(\tilde{x}^{a}-\bar{x}^{a})(\tilde{x}^{b}-\bar{x}^{b})(\tilde{x}^{c}-\bar{x}^{c})+O(\|\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\|\|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|^{3})

\displaystyle+O(\|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|^{4})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\textrm{hot}(\|\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\|,\|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|;1),

in which the second equality is obtained by Taylor expansions of
\nabla F(\bar{bx})
,
H(\bar{\mathbf{x}})
, and
\nabla^{3}F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})
at
\mathbf{x}^{*}
.
Therefore, by Lemma
8
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}\left|F(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})-\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}F(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})\right|^{l}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{l}\mathbb{E}\big(\textrm{hot}(\|\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\|,\|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|;1)\big)^{l}\leq O(\frac{1}{n^{l/2}}).

(105)

Substituting (
105
) back to (
104
) completes the proof for
s_{1}
. The proof for
s_{2}
is similar.
∎

Lemma 11
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
3
, for any positive integer
l
, we have

\mathbb{E}F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})^{l}=O(1),\ \textrm{and}\ \ \mathbb{E}F(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})^{l}=O(1).

Proof.

For
F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})
, by the Taylor expansion at
\mathbf{x}^{*}
, we have

F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})=F(\mathbf{x}^{*})+\textrm{hot}(\|\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\|;1).

Therefore,

F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})^{l}=(F(\mathbf{x}^{*})+\textrm{hot}(\|\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\|;1))^{l}=F(\mathbf{x}^{*})^{l}+\textrm{hot}(\|\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\|;1).

Taking expectation, we have

\mathbb{E}F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})^{l}=F(\mathbf{x}^{*})^{l}+\mathbb{E}\big(\textrm{hot}(\|\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\|;1)\big)=F(\mathbf{x}^{*})^{l}+O(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}})=O(1).

For
F(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})
, still expanding
F
at
\mathbf{x}^{*}
, we have

F(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})^{l}=F(\mathbf{x}^{*})^{l}+\textrm{hot}(\|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\|;1).

By Lemma
8
and
7
, we have

\mathbb{E}\|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\|^{l}\leq 2^{l-1}\big(\mathbb{E}\|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|^{l}+\mathbb{E}\|\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\|^{l}\big)=O(\frac{1}{n^{l/2}}).

Hence, we still have

\mathbb{E}\big(\textrm{hot}(\|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\|;1)\big)=O(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}),

which completes the proof.
∎
