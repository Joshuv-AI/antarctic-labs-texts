An arithmetic variant of Raynaud’s theorem
Jonathan Love, Libby Taylor

Abstract
It is well known that for a regular semistable curve 𝔛 \mathfrak{X} over a DVR with algebraically closed residue field, the spanning trees of the dual graph of the special fiber of 𝔛 \mathfrak{X} are in bijection with components of the special fiber of the Néron model of the Jacobian of 𝔛 \mathfrak{X} . We prove a generalization of this fact that does not require the residue field to be algebraically closed, using a combinatorially enriched version of the dual graph to encode arithmetic information about divisors on 𝔛 \mathfrak{X} .



1
Introduction

Kirchhoff’s matrix-tree theorem states that for any graph
G
, the set of spanning trees of
G
is in bijection with the elements of
\operatorname{Pic}^{0}(G)
(degree
0
divisors on
G
modulo principal divisors; see Section
2
for a full definition). The original proof
[
9
]
is almost entirely linear-algebraic, and does not give a combinatorially defined bijection between these sets. Bernardi provided such a combinatorial interpretation
[
4
,
5
]
, in which he produces a (non-canonical) action of
\operatorname{Pic}^{0}(G)
on the set of spanning trees (discussed in Section
2
).

On the algebrogeometric side, Raynaud’s theorem gives a description of the component group of the generalized Jacobian of a curve. For any smooth curve
X
over the fraction field of a DVR
R
with algebraically closed residue field, let
\mathfrak{X}
denote a regular semistable model of
X
over
R
. Then
\Phi_{X}
, the geometric component group of the special fiber of the Néron model of the Jacobian of
X
, admits a canonical isomorphism with
\operatorname{Pic}^{0}(G)
, where
G
is the dual graph of the special fiber of
\mathfrak{X}
. (For a proof of this fact, see Appendix A of
[
3
]
, in which the relation between the arithmetic and tropical geometry of this problem is spelled out in detail.)

By combining the two theorems above, we get a relationship between divisors of
X
and spanning trees of the dual graph
G
of the special fiber of
\mathfrak{X}
. We will investigate this relationship directly, giving new proofs for some known results and providing analogues of these results in the case that the residue field of the DVR is not algebraically closed. Specifically, using intersection theory on
\mathfrak{X}
, we define a subgroup
\operatorname{Pic}^{(0)}(X)
of
\operatorname{Pic}^{0}(X)
and a subgroup
\operatorname{Pic}_{b}^{0}(G)
of
\operatorname{Pic}^{0}(G)
, and prove the following result:

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
R
be a henselian DVR with fraction field
K
and residue field
k
. Let
X
be a smooth curve over
K
, and
\mathfrak{X}
a regular semistable model for
X
over
R
. Then

\operatorname{Pic}^{0}(X)/\operatorname{Pic}^{(0)}(X)\cong\operatorname{Pic}_{b}^{0}(G).

This can be thought of as an “arithmetic variant” of Raynaud’s Theorem, which can be stated and proved any reference to Néron models or Jacobians. We then use the theory of Néron models to establish an isomorphism
\operatorname{Pic}^{0}(X)/\operatorname{Pic}^{(0)}(X)\cong\Phi_{X}
under appropriate conditions, allowing us to prove the following (geometric) variant of Raynaud’s Theorem that does not require
k
to be algebraically closed:

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
R
be a DVR with fraction field
K
and residue field
k
. Let
X
be a smooth, geometrically irreducible curve over
K
, and
\mathfrak{X}
a regular semistable model for
X
over
R
. Suppose every irreducible component of
\mathfrak{X}_{k}
has a smooth
k
-point. Then
\operatorname{Pic}^{0}(G)\cong\Phi_{X}
, where
\Phi_{X}
is the component group of the special fiber of the Néron model of the Jacobian of
X
.

Here “every irreducible component has a smooth
k
-point” means smooth as a point in the component, not necessarily as a point in
\mathfrak{X}_{k}
(that is, the
k
-point may be an intersection point with another component).

It should be noted that Theorem
1.2
is not new; it was originally proved in
[
6
]
, and we have recreated their results in somewhat different language. We give a new application of this theorem, though, and use it to define a set of combinatorial objects which are a torsor under the arithmetic component group.

In general, the relationship between line bundles on the curve and divisors on
G
is not as straightforward as it is when
k
is algebraically closed. In particular, arithmetic information about
X
will translate into “weights” that are attached to the vertices and edges of
G
. We study these weighted graphs in Section
3
, and prove a weighted analogue of the matrix-tree theorem (Theorem
3.2
), allowing us to establish a torsor action of
\operatorname{Pic}^{0}(X)/\operatorname{Pic}^{(0)}(X)
on a set of combinatorially enriched spanning trees (Corollary
5.1
).

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we introduce the required background in graph theory. In Section
3
, we introduce weighted graphs and a number of related notions, and prove some results about them, including the matrix-tree analogue Theorem
3.2
. In Section
4
we begin discussing semistable curves over a DVR, focusing on properties of the specialization map, a map from divisors on the curve to divisors on the dual graph
G
of the special fiber. We apply these properties in Section
5
to prove Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
, and combine these results with the results about weighted graphs to prove Corollary
5.1
.

2
Background on chip-firing

In this section, we will review the necessary graph theory; these theorems will later be applied to the dual graph of the special fiber of a regular proper model of a curve.

Let
G
be a directed graph on
n
vertices. Its
n\times n
signed adjacency matrix
A
is defined to to have its
(i,j)
-th entry in
A
equal to
1
if there is a directed edge from
v_{i}
to
v_{j}
;
-1
if there is a directed edge from
v_{j}
to
v_{i}
; and
0
if no edge exists between the two vertices. If there are multiple edges between
v_{i}
and
v_{j}
, then the matrix entry is equal to the number of edges oriented
v_{i}
to
v_{j}
minus the number of edges oriented
v_{j}
to
v_{i}
.

A divisor on a graph
G=(V,E)
is a function
D:V\to\mathbb{Z}
, which we write in the form
\sum_{v\in V}a_{v}[v]
for
a_{v}\in\mathbb{Z}
. The set of all divisors of a graph
G
is denoted
\operatorname{Div}(G)
. The degree of the divisor
D
is defined as
\deg(D)=\sum_{v\in V}a_{v}
. The set of all divisors of degree
k
is denoted
\operatorname{Div}^{k}(G)
. There is a (non-canonical) bijection
\operatorname{Div}^{k}(G)\to\operatorname{Div}^{0}(G)
defined by
D\mapsto D-D_{0}
for some fixed reference divisor
D_{0}\in\operatorname{Div}^{k}(G)
.

The
Laplacian operator
on a graph
G
, denoted
\Delta:\operatorname{Div}(G)\to\operatorname{Div}(G)
, is defined by

\Delta(f):=\sum_{v\in V}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}e\in E\\
e=\{v,w\}\end{subarray}}(f(v)-f(w))\right)[v].

If
f
is
1
at a single vertex
v
and
0
everywhere else,
\Delta(f)
is called a “chip-firing move at
v
.” The group
\text{Prin}(G)
of principal divisors is the image of the Laplacian operator; note that it is generated by chip-firing moves. We have
\text{Prin}(G)\subseteq\operatorname{Div}^{0}(G)
, and both
\text{Prin}(G)
and
\operatorname{Div}^{0}(G)
are free abelian groups of rank
n-1
, so

\operatorname{Pic}^{0}(G)=\operatorname{Div}^{0}(G)/\text{Prin}(G)

is a finite group called the
Jacobian
of
G
.

The Jacobian of
G
is in bijection with the set
T(G)
of spanning trees of
G
; this is (one form of) the classical matrix-tree theorem. While the theorem was originally proved in
[
9
]
using linear algebra (hence the name), it was proved much more recently that there is a combinatorially-defined bijection between these two sets. This result, due to Bernardi
[
5
]
, follows by establishing a bijection between each of these sets and a particular set of divisors on
G
. We summarize this construction below.

We assign to
G
a
ribbon structure
: that is, for each vertex
v
, we order the edges incident to
v
up to cyclic permutation. Intuitively, we may consider drawing
G
in the plane (with edges allowed to cross each other), and define a ribbon structure by the counterclockwise ordering of edges around each vertex. Now fix a vertex
q\in G
and an edge
e_{0}
incident to
q
. An orientation of
G
is
q
-connected
if for every vertex
v\in G
, there is a directed path from
q
to
v
.

Given a spanning tree
T
of
G
, we can define a
q
-connected orientation of
T
using the following procedure (called a “tour” of the spanning tree), beginning with
(v,e):=(q,e_{0})
:

•

If
e
is not in
T
:

1.

If
e
has not been assigned an orientation yet, orient
e
towards
v
.

2.

Re-assign
e
to be the next edge in the ordering around

.

•

Otherwise (if

is in
T
):

1.

If
e
has not been assigned an orientation yet, orient
e
away from
v
.

2.

Re-assign

to be the other vertex incident to

.

The set of

Combining Proposition

Theorem 2.2

(

)
.

If

, then there is a unique

The map from
\operatorname{Div}^{g-1}(G)
to
\operatorname{Pic}^{0}(G)
is defined by subtracting a reference divisor
D_{0}\in\operatorname{Div}^{g-1}(G)
from each
D\in\operatorname{Div}^{g-1}(G)
. The reference divisor is taken to be the
q
-orientable divisor associated to some spanning tree
T_{0}
of
G
, which allows
T_{0}
to be considered as an “identity element” of

in this bijection. This produces a bijection between
\operatorname{Div}^{g-1}(G)/\text{Prin}(G)
and
\operatorname{Div}^{0}(G)/\text{Prin}(G)\cong\operatorname{Pic}^{0}(G)
.

Thus the Bernardi map gives a combinatorially defined bijection between spanning trees of
G
and elements of
\operatorname{Pic}^{0}(G)
, which factors as
T(G)\to\operatorname{Div}^{g-1}(G)/\operatorname{Prin}(G)\to\operatorname{Pic}^{0}(G)
. Note that this bijection is not canonical, as it depends on the choice of
T_{0}
and the choice of
q
.

If
R
is a DVR with algebraically closed residue field, and
\mathfrak{X}
is a regular semistable curve over
R
, then we can study properties of
\mathfrak{X}
by taking the dual graph
G
of the special fiber. In particular, there is a
specialization map
(Section
4
) from divisors on
\mathfrak{X}
to divisors on
G
, so the graph theory described above can be used to study the geometry of
\mathfrak{X}
.

If the residue field is not algebraically closed, however, there may not be as nice of a relationship between divisors on
\mathfrak{X}
and on
G
; for instance, there may be no divisor on
\mathfrak{X}
that has degree
1
when restricted to a particular component of the special fiber. In the following section, we develop a theory of weighted graphs that can be used to resolve some of these issues.

3.2
Spanning Trees and Sub-weightings

Given a spanning tree
T=(V,E_{T})
of
G
, we define an
edge sub-weighting
of
T
to be a function
\sigma:E\to\mathbb{Z}
with the property that
\sigma(e)=\omega(e)
if
e\notin E_{T}
, and
1\leq\sigma(e)\leq\omega(e)
for all
e\in E_{T}
. The
trivial sub-weighting
is the one with
\sigma(e)=\omega(e)
for all
e\in E
.

Assume for the sake of simplicity that
G
is connected; we explain how to remove this hypothesis at the end of this section. Fix a vertex
q\in V
and a ribbon structure of
G
. Given a spanning tree
T
of
G
, we can construct a
q
-connected orientation of

as described in Section
2

\displaystyle D_{T,\sigma}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{e:w\to v\in E}\Big(\sigma(e)[v]+(\omega(e)-\sigma(e))[w]\Big)-\sum_{v\in V}\omega(v)[v]

\displaystyle=\sum_{v\in V}\left(\sum_{e\text{ to }v}\sigma(e)+\sum_{e\text{ from }v}(\omega(e)-\sigma(e))-\omega(v)\right)[v].

See Figure
3

Note that

\left.\left(\sum_{e\text{ to }v}\sigma(e)-\sum_{e\text{ from }v}\sigma(e)\right)\right|\omega(v).

If this condition is satisfied, we call
\sigma
a

for

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
(G,\omega)
be a pleasantly weighted graph. Then

\{D_{T,\sigma}:\text{$T$ a spanning tree for $G$, $\sigma$ an edge sub-weighting for $T$}\}

forms a complete set of coset representatives for
\operatorname{Prin}(G)
in
\operatorname{Div}^{g-1}(G)
, and

\{D_{T,\sigma}:\text{$T$ a spanning tree for $G$, $\sigma$ a balanced edge sub-weighting for $T$}\}

forms a complete set of coset representatives for
\operatorname{Prin}(G)
in
\operatorname{Div}_{b}^{g-1}(G)
.

Remark
.

Theorem
3.2
is a direct generalization of the classical matrix-tree theorem that the set of spanning trees of
G
is in bijection with elements of
\operatorname{Pic}^{0}(G)
. The classical theorem arises when we take
\omega
to be a trivial weighting (i.e. every vertex and edge has weight
1
).

Proof.

Let
\widehat{G}=(V,\widehat{E})
be the unweighted graph obtained by replacing each edge
e
of
G
by a collection of
\omega(e)
parallel edges
e_{1},\ldots,e_{\omega(e)}
. Under the natural identification
\operatorname{Div}(G)\cong\operatorname{Div}(\widehat{G})
, the principal divisors are also identified.

To each spanning tree
\widehat{T}
of
\widehat{G}
, we will define a spanning tree
T
of
G
together with an edge sub-weighting of
T
. For each edge
e
of
G
, which has been replaced with the parallel edges
e_{1},\ldots,e_{\omega(e)}
in
\widehat{G}
, note that
\widehat{T}
can include at most one of these edges; include
e
in
T
if and only if some
e_{i}
is in
\widehat{T}
. Now consider the
q
-connected orientation of
\widehat{G}
defined by
\widehat{T}
. If
e\in T
, let
\sigma(e)
be the number of edges
e_{1},\ldots,e_{\omega(e)}
pointing in the same direction as
e_{i}
in the
q
-connected orientation resulting from
\widehat{T}
; this means that there will be
\omega(e)-\sigma(e)
edges pointing in the opposite direction.

Now set

D_{\widehat{T}}:=\sum_{v\in V}(\text{indeg}(v)-1)[v]=\sum_{e:w\to v\in\widehat{E}}[v]-\sum_{v\in V}[v].

By Theorem
2.2
, the divisors
D_{\widehat{T}}
give a complete set of coset representatives for
\operatorname{Prin}(\widehat{G})
in
\operatorname{Div}^{\widehat{g}-1}(\widehat{G})
, where
\widehat{g}
is the unweighted first betti number of
\widehat{G}
. But by construction of
T
and
\sigma
from
\widehat{T}
, we can see that

D_{T,\sigma}=D_{\widehat{T}}-\sum_{v\in V}(\omega(v)-1)[v].

Since
\sum_{v\in V}(\omega(v)-1)[v]
is constant for all trees and sub-weightings, this gives us a complete set of
\operatorname{Prin}(G)
–coset representatives in
\operatorname{Div}^{g-1}(G)
.

Finally, since every element of
\operatorname{Prin}(G)
is balanced, we can obtain
\operatorname{Prin}(G)
–coset representatives in
\operatorname{Div}_{b}^{g-1}(G)
simply by taking those edge sub-weighted spanning trees for which
D_{T,\sigma}
is a balanced divisor.
∎

Corollary 3.3
.

The number of elements of
\operatorname{Div}^{0}(G)/\operatorname{Prin}(G)
is

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}T\text{ spanning}\\
\text{tree of }G\end{subarray}}\ \prod_{e\in E_{T}}\omega(e),

and the number of elements of
\operatorname{Div}_{b}^{0}(G)/\operatorname{Prin}(G)
is

\frac{\gcd_{v\in V}\omega(v)}{\prod_{v\in V}\omega(v)}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}T\text{ spanning}\\
\text{tree of }G\end{subarray}}\prod_{e\in E_{T}}\omega(e)\right).

Proof.

Note that
\operatorname{Div}^{g-1}(G)
is a
\operatorname{Div}^{0}(G)
–torsor, and so the number of elements of
\operatorname{Div}^{0}(G)/\operatorname{Prin}(G)
is equal to the number of
\operatorname{Prin}(G)
–cosets contained in
\operatorname{Div}^{g-1}(G)
. By Theorem
3.2
, this is equal to the number of edge sub-weighted spanning trees. Given a spanning tree, we can define an edge sub-weighting on each edge independently, giving us the desired count.

Now observe that

\frac{\operatorname{Div}^{0}(G)/\operatorname{Prin}(G)}{\operatorname{Div}_{b}^{0}(G)/\operatorname{Prin}(G)}\cong\frac{\operatorname{Div}^{0}(G)}{\operatorname{Div}_{b}^{0}(G)}=\operatorname{Div}_{u}^{0}(G),

where
\operatorname{Div}_{u}^{0}(G)
is the kernel of the surjective map
\operatorname{Div}_{u}(G)\to\mathbb{Z}/\omega(G)\mathbb{Z}
and hence has
\frac{1}{\omega(G)}\prod_{v\in V}\omega(v)
elements. This implies the desired conclusion.
∎

Remark
.

We can generalize these results to the case that
G
is disconnected. Given a graph
G
, a
maximal spanning forest
of
G
is a subgraph
F
with the same vertex set as
G
, such that for all edges of
G
not in
F
, adding the edge to
F
will result in a subgraph with a loop. Equivalently,
F
is the union of spanning trees on each component of
G
. Instead of
q
-connected orientations, we will have
\{q_{1},\ldots,q_{r}\}
-connected orientations, where
\{q_{1},\ldots,q_{r}\}
contains one vertex from each component of
G
. We can define edge sub-weightings
\sigma
for a maximal spanning forest
F
in the same way as for spanning trees, and divisors
D_{F,\sigma}
can also be defined analogously.

To generalize Theorem
3.2
and Corollary
3.3
, it suffices to replace spanning trees with maximal spanning forests. The groups
\operatorname{Div}^{g-1}(G)
,
\operatorname{Div}^{g-1}_{b}(G)
, and
\operatorname{Prin}(G)
each decompose naturally as a direct sum of subgroups on each component of
G
, so choosing a coset representative for
\operatorname{Prin}(G)
in
\operatorname{Div}^{g-1}(G)
(resp.
\operatorname{Div}^{g-1}_{b}(G)
) is equivalent to choosing a coset representative for
\operatorname{Prin}(G_{i})
in
\operatorname{Div}^{g-1}(G_{i})
(resp.
\operatorname{Div}^{g-1}_{b}(G_{i})
) for each component
G_{i}
of
G
. Under this decomposition, each divisor
D_{F,\sigma}
can be written as a sum of elements
D_{T_{i},\sigma_{i}}
by restriction to each component
G_{i}
.

4
Intersection Theory and the Specialization Map

For the rest of the paper, we assume the following setup. Let
R
be a discrete valuation ring with field of fractions
K
and residue field
k
. Let
X
be a smooth curve over
K
, and
\mathfrak{X}
a regular semistable model for
X
over
R
. By semistable, we mean that the special fiber is reduced with only ordinary double point singularities.
2
2

2

It is more common to define a semistable curve as one whose special fiber is geometrically reduced. However, one may blow up any such curve to get one whose special fiber is a normal crossing divisor. Since blowups do not affect the combinatorics of the special fiber, as per the next lemma, this distinction will be irrelevant for us.
Let
G=(V,E)
the dual graph of the special fiber
\mathfrak{X}_{k}
, so that
V=\{C_{1},\ldots,C_{\gamma}\}
is the set of components of
\mathfrak{X}_{k}
, and
E
is the set of nodes.

We will be working with
\operatorname{Pic}^{0}(G)
; we claim that this group is independent of the choice of semistable regular proper model. This fact is well-known, but the authors were unable to find a reference, so a proof is given here.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
G
be the dual graph of
\mathfrak{X}_{k}
, the special fiber of the minimal regular proper model, and let
G^{\prime}
be the dual graph of
\mathfrak{X}^{\prime}_{k}
for
\mathfrak{X}^{\prime}
any semistable proper regular model. Then
\operatorname{Pic}^{0}(G)\cong\operatorname{Pic}^{0}(G^{\prime})
.

Proof.

It suffices to show that
\operatorname{Pic}^{0}(G)
is unchanged upon blowing up a point on the special fiber to obtain another semistable model. There are two cases to consider, corresponding to whether the point
p\in\mathfrak{X}_{k}
to be blown up is a smooth or nodal point. If
p
is a smooth point on some component
C_{i}
and
P
denotes the exceptional divisor that comes from blowing up
p
, then
P\cdot C_{i}=1
and
P\cdot C_{k}=0
for all
k\neq i
. This means that
P
adds a leaf to the dual graph, which does not change
\operatorname{Pic}^{0}(G)
.

On the other hand, suppose
p
is the intersection of two components
C_{i}
and
C_{j}
. Then we have
P\cdot\mathfrak{X}_{k}=0
,
P\cdot C_{i}=1
and
P\cdot C_{j}=1
, so that
P^{2}=-2
. But
P
is a copy of
\mathbb{P}^{1}
, therefore it must be nonreduced. But this means that the model is no longer semistable.
∎

Let
\operatorname{Div}(X)
and
\operatorname{Div}(\mathfrak{X})
denote the set of Cartier divisors on
X
and
\mathfrak{X}
respectively (since
X
and
\mathfrak{X}
are both regular, we can equivalently consider Weil divisors). Given
D\in\operatorname{Div}(X)
, we can define
\overline{D}\in\operatorname{Div}(\mathfrak{X})
, the “Zariski closure” of
D
, by defining it first on prime divisors in the natural way, and then extending linearly. Since
D
can be recovered from
\overline{D}
by restriction to the generic fiber, this allows us to identify
\operatorname{Div}(X)
with a subgroup of
\operatorname{Div}(\mathfrak{X})
, the group of “horizontal divisors” on
\mathfrak{X}
. Every divisor on
\mathfrak{X}
can be written uniquely as a sum of a horizontal divisor and a “vertical divisor,” that is, a divisor supported on
\mathfrak{X}_{k}
.

Define the
specialization map

\rho:\operatorname{Div}(\mathfrak{X})\to\operatorname{Div}(G)
by

\rho(\mathcal{D}):=\sum_{C_{i}\in V}(C_{i}\cdot\mathcal{D})[C_{i}],

where
C_{i}\cdot\mathcal{D}=\deg(\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{X}}(\mathcal{D})|_{C_{i}})
is the intersection pairing. The map
\rho
is evidently a homomorphism, and it preserves degrees because the degree of the line bundle
\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{X}}(\mathcal{D})
on
\mathfrak{X}
is preserved by base change to the special fiber. Since the intersection pairing is invariant under linear equivalence,
\operatorname{Prin}(\mathfrak{X})
is contained in
\ker\rho
.

We will prove that
\rho
maps
\operatorname{Prin}(X)
surjectively onto
\operatorname{Prin}(G)
(Lemma
4.3
). On the other hand,
\operatorname{Div}(X)
does not necessarily map surjectively onto
\operatorname{Div}(G)
; we will compute the image of
\operatorname{Div}(X)
in
\operatorname{Div}(G)
(Lemma
4.4
). Both results require the following lemma.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
S
be the spectrum of a field or of a DVR. For any divisor on a regular proper curve over
S
, and any finite set of closed points
\{p_{1},\dots,p_{m}\}
on the curve, there exists a linearly equivalent divisor with support disjoint from
\{p_{1},\dots,p_{m}\}
.

Proof.

A proper curve over a field is projective over the field
[
12
, 0A26, 0B45]
, and a regular proper curve over a DVR is projective over the DVR
[
12
, 0C5P]
. The desired result is proved in
[
11
, Theorem 3.1]
for smooth projective varieties over a field, but the proof holds for regular projective schemes over a field or a DVR as well.
∎

Lemma 4.3
.

The specialization map
\rho
induces a surjection
\operatorname{Prin}(X)\to\operatorname{Prin}(G)
.

Remark
.

Baker provides a proof for this fact in the case that
k
is algebraically closed
[
3
]
. The fact that
\operatorname{Prin}(X)
maps into
\operatorname{Prin}(G)
is his Lemma 2.1; we reproduce the argument here, essentially unchanged. The proof he provides for surjectivity (Corollary A.9), however, follows from the surjectivity of
\operatorname{Div}(X)\to\operatorname{Div}(G)
, which will not hold for general
k
. Baker’s proof is also a consequence of more powerful results about the Jacobian variety. We provide a more elementary proof that does not depend on
k
being algebraically closed.

Proof.

Suppose we are given
D\in\operatorname{Prin}(X)
, so
D=\text{div}(f)
for some rational function
f
on
X
. The open inclusion
X\to\mathfrak{X}
induces an isomorphism of function fields, so
f
extends uniquely to a rational function
\widehat{f}
on
\mathfrak{X}
. Then
\overline{D}-\text{div}(\widehat{f})
will be a vertical divisor. Now for each irreducible component
C_{j}
of
\mathfrak{X}_{k}
,
\rho([C_{j}])
is the divisor corresponding to a chip-firing move at the vertex
C_{j}
(this follows from the fact that
(C_{j}\cdot\mathfrak{X}_{k})=0
). This shows that
\rho
maps the set of vertical divisors surjectively onto
\operatorname{Prin}(G)
, so

\rho(D)=\rho(D-\text{div}(\widehat{f}))\in\operatorname{Prin}(G).

Now suppose we are given an element in
\operatorname{Prin}(G)
, which by the preceding discussion must equal
\rho(\mathcal{V})
for some vertical divisor
\mathcal{V}
. Let
x_{1},\dots,x_{m}
denote closed points on the components
C_{1},\dots,C_{m}
of
\mathfrak{X}_{k}
respectively. Applying Proposition
4.2
to the divisor
\mathcal{V}
and the points
x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}
, we can find a divisor
D=\mathcal{V}+\text{div}(f)
for some rational function
f
on
\mathfrak{X}
, such that
D
does not contain any irreducible component of
\mathfrak{X}_{k}
in its support. Then
D
is a horizontal divisor, and we have
\rho(D)=\rho(\mathcal{V})
because
\rho
annihilates
\operatorname{Prin}(\mathfrak{X})
.
∎

We now describe how to measure the failure of surjectivity of
\operatorname{Div}(X)\to\operatorname{Div}(G)
. Following
[
8
, Section 1.5]
, we define the
index
of a smooth curve
C/k
,
\operatorname{ind}(C)
, to be the greatest common divisor of the degrees of field extensions
k^{\prime}/k
such that
C(k^{\prime})\neq\emptyset
. The degree of any divisor on
C
will be a multiple of the index. If
C
is not smooth, we define
\operatorname{ind}(C)
to be the index of the normalization
\widehat{C}
of
C
.

Lemma 4.4
.

Assume
R
is henselian. The specialization map
\rho
induces a surjection
\operatorname{Div}(X)\to\operatorname{Div}_{b}(G)
, where
\operatorname{Div}_{b}(G)\leq\operatorname{Div}(G)
consists of the divisors on
G
such that the coefficient of
[C_{i}]
is a multiple of
\operatorname{ind}(C_{i})
.

Remark
.

If we define a weighting on
G
such that
\omega(C_{i}):=\operatorname{ind}(C_{i})
, then
\operatorname{Div}_{b}(G)
is the set of balanced divisors on
G
, as defined in Section
3
.

Proof.

Given
D\in\operatorname{Div}(X)
and any component
C_{i}
of
\mathfrak{X}_{k}
, observe that
\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{X}}(\overline{D})|_{C_{i}}
will be a line bundle on
C_{i}
, and so its degree,
C_{i}\cdot\overline{D}
, will be a multiple of
\operatorname{ind}(C_{i})
. This proves that
\operatorname{Div}(X)
maps into
\operatorname{Div}_{b}(G)
.

To show surjectivity, it suffices to show that for any
i
,
\operatorname{ind}(C_{i})[C_{i}]
is in the image of
\operatorname{Div}(X)
. Let
\widehat{C_{i}}
denote the normalization of
C_{i}
. By definition of index, we can find closed points
\widehat{P_{1}},\ldots,\widehat{P_{m}}
on
\widehat{C_{i}}
and integers
a_{1},\ldots,a_{m}
such that
\sum a_{\ell}[k(\widehat{P_{\ell}}):k]=\operatorname{ind}(C_{i})
. Applying Proposition
4.2
to choose a linearly equivalent divisor if necessary, we can ensure that none of the
\widehat{P_{\ell}}
map to singularities in
\mathfrak{X}_{k}
. Let
P_{\ell}
denote the image of
\widehat{P_{\ell}}
in
C_{i}
.

Since
R
is henselian, the reduction map from closed points of
X
to closed points of
\mathfrak{X}_{k}
is surjective
[
10
, Corollary 10.1.38]
. That is, for each
P_{\ell}
, there exists a closed point
Q_{\ell}
of
X
such that
\overline{\{Q_{\ell}\}}\cap\mathfrak{X}_{k}=\{P_{\ell}\}
. Define the divisor

on

. Since the Zariski closure of each

only intersects
\mathfrak{X}_{k}
at

, we have

for all

, and
C_{i}\cdot\overline{\{Q_{\ell}\}}=[k(P_{\ell}):k]
. This implies
\rho(D)=\operatorname{ind}(C_{i})[C_{i}]
as desired.
∎

\displaystyle=D_{\widehat{T^{\prime}}}-\sum_{v\in V}(\omega(v)-1)[v]

\displaystyle\sim\left(R+\sum_{v\in V}(\omega(v)-1)[v]\right)-\sum_{v\in V}(\omega(v)-1)[v]

\displaystyle=\rho(D)+D_{T,\sigma},

so this agrees with the torsor action as described in Corollary
5.1
.

5.2
Comparing the Arithmetic and Geometric Component Groups

So far, we have established bijections between
\Phi_{X}^{a}
,
\operatorname{Pic}_{b}^{0}(G)
, and the set of spanning trees of
G
with balanced edge sub-weightings. It is worth considering how each of these is affected by base change. Let
R^{\prime}/R
be an extension of DVRs; for now, we will only consider the case that
\mathfrak{X}^{\prime}=\mathfrak{X}_{R^{\prime}}
is a regular semistable model of its smooth generic fiber
X^{\prime}
. Let
k^{\prime}=R^{\prime}\otimes_{R}k
, and let
G^{\prime}
be the dual graph of the special fiber
\mathfrak{X}_{k^{\prime}}
.

There are three ways that
G^{\prime}
may differ from
G
as a weighted graph. First, a weighted edge
e
of
G
may split into multiple parallel edges in
G^{\prime}
, with the total weight preserved (this occurs if a node over
k
splits over
k^{\prime}
). This operation preserves both
\operatorname{Prin}(G)
and
\operatorname{Div}_{b}^{0}(G)
, and as the proof of Theorem
3.2
indicates, this does not change the size of the set of spanning trees with balanced edge sub-weightings. Hence
\Phi_{X^{\prime}}^{a}\cong\Phi_{X}^{a}
.

Second, the index of a component may shrink, so that
G^{\prime}
is isomorphic to
G
as a graph, but has a lower weight at some vertex and hence more balanced divisors (this can occur because there are more line bundles defined over
k^{\prime}
than over
k
). Third, a vertex of
G
may split into multiple vertices in
G^{\prime}
(this occurs if a component of
\mathfrak{X}_{k}
splits into multiple components over
k^{\prime}
). We will show that in both of these cases,
\Phi_{X}^{a}
injects into
\Phi_{X^{\prime}}^{a}
.

Note that we have an injection
\Psi:\operatorname{Div}(\mathfrak{X})\to\operatorname{Div}(\mathfrak{X}^{\prime})
, which can be defined on prime divisors by base change to
R^{\prime}
and extended by linearity. By restricting to the set of horizontal divisors and to the degree
0
part, we get a map
\operatorname{Div}^{0}(X)\to\operatorname{Div}^{0}(X^{\prime})
.

Proposition 5.2
.

The map
\Psi
induces an injection
\Phi_{X}^{a}\to\Phi_{X^{\prime}}^{a}
.

Proof.

By Theorem
1.1
, the specialization map
\rho:\operatorname{Div}^{0}(\mathfrak{X})\to\operatorname{Div}_{b}^{0}(G)
induces an isomorphism

\Phi^{a}_{X}=\operatorname{Div}^{0}(X)/(\operatorname{Prin}(X)+\operatorname{Div}^{(0)}(X))\xrightarrow{\cong}\operatorname{Div}_{b}^{0}(G)/\operatorname{Prin}(G),

and we have an analogous result for the specialization map
\rho^{\prime}:\operatorname{Div}^{0}(\mathfrak{X}^{\prime})\to\operatorname{Div}_{b}^{0}(G^{\prime})
.

Now suppose an irreducible component
C_{i}
of
\mathfrak{X}_{k}
splits into irreducible components
C_{i}^{(1)},\ldots,C_{i}^{(r_{i})}
over
k^{\prime}
. These
k^{\prime}
-components are permuted transitively by
\text{Gal}(k^{\prime}/k)
, and since
\Psi(\mathcal{D})
is preserved by the Galois action, the value of
\Psi(\mathcal{D})\cdot C_{i}^{(j)}
must be invariant for all
j=1,\ldots r
. This proves that
\operatorname{ind}(C_{i})
is a multiple of
r_{i}
, and that a divisor on
\mathfrak{X}
with degree
r_{i}d
on
C_{i}
will have degree
d
on each
C_{i}^{(j)}
. In particular, if
\mathcal{D}\in\ker\rho
, then
(\rho^{\prime}\circ\Psi)(\mathcal{D})=0
, which implies that
\Psi
descends to a map
\psi
:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{Div}^{0}(X)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{Div}^{0}(\mathfrak{X})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{Div}^{0}_{b}(G)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{Div}^{0}(X^{\prime})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{Div}^{0}(\mathfrak{X}^{\prime})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{Div}^{0}_{b}(G^{\prime})}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Psi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\rho}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Psi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\psi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\rho^{\prime}}

where
\psi
is defined by

\psi:\operatorname{ind}(C_{i})[C_{i}]\mapsto\sum_{j=1}^{r_{i}}\frac{\operatorname{ind}(C_{i})}{r_{i}}[C_{i}^{(j)}].

It now suffices to prove that
\psi:\operatorname{Div}^{0}_{b}(G)\to\operatorname{Div}^{0}_{b}(G^{\prime})
descends to an injection
\operatorname{Div}^{0}_{b}(G)/\operatorname{Prin}(G)\to\operatorname{Div}^{0}_{b}(G^{\prime})/\operatorname{Prin}(G^{\prime})
.

To show this map is well-defined, consider an element of
\operatorname{Prin}(G)
.
It was observed in the proof of Lemma
4.3
that the set of vertical divisors on
\mathfrak{X}
map surjectively onto
\operatorname{Prin}(G)
, so we can write this element as
\rho(\mathcal{V})
for some vertical divisor
\mathcal{V}\in\operatorname{Div}(\mathfrak{X})
. Then
\psi(\rho(\mathcal{V}))=\rho^{\prime}(\Psi(\mathcal{V}))
, which is in
\operatorname{Prin}(G^{\prime})
because the base change of a vertical divisor is vertical.

To show the map is injective, suppose
D\in\operatorname{Div}_{b}^{0}(G)
satisfies
\psi(D)\in\operatorname{Prin}(G^{\prime})
. Choosing
\mathcal{D}\in\operatorname{Div}(\mathfrak{X})
mapping to
D
via
\rho
, we have
\rho^{\prime}(\Psi(\mathcal{D}))=\psi(\rho(\mathcal{D}))\in\operatorname{Prin}(G^{\prime})
, which implies that
\Psi(\mathcal{D})
is a vertical divisor. Since a divisor is vertical if and only if its base change is, we can conclude that
\mathcal{D}
is a vertical divisor, and hence that
D=\rho(\mathcal{D})
is in
\operatorname{Prin}(G)
.
∎

Thus we have established that
\Phi_{X}^{a}
embeds naturally into
\Phi_{X^{\prime}}^{a}
, assuming that there is a semistable model of
X^{\prime}
which is the base change of a semistable model of
X
. We now show that
\Phi_{X}^{a}
embeds naturally into the component group
\Phi_{X}
of the special fiber of the Néron model of the Jacobian of
X
, and provide a condition under which the two are isomorphic.

Proposition 5.3
.

Assume
R
is henselian and
X
is geometrically irreducible. Suppose also that either
k
is perfect or that
\mathfrak{X}
admits an étale quasi-section. Then there is an injective homomorphism
\Phi_{X}^{a}\to\Phi_{X}
. If every irreducible component of
\mathfrak{X}_{k}
has index
1
, then this injection is an isomorphism.

Remark
.

The technical condition requiring
k
to be perfect, or
\mathfrak{X}
to admit an étale quasi-section, is just for the existence of a Néron model of a particular form. As discussed in
[
7
, Remark 9.5/5]
, the assumption that
\mathfrak{X}
admits an étale quasi-section is satisfied if any irreducible component of
\mathfrak{X}_{k}
is geometrically reduced (note that
\mathfrak{X}
is semi-stable, so irreducible components are necessarily reduced).

Proposition

is originally proved in

. We provide a proof here as well, which contains many of the same ideas but uses different language. For definitions and background on the material used in the following proof, see

Proof.

Let

over

given by line bundles of total degree

, and let
P^{\circ}

with
