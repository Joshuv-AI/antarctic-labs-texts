Faster Spectral Density Estimation and Sparsification in the Nuclear Norm
Yujia Jin, Ishani Karmarkar, Christopher Musco, Aaron Sidford, Apoorv Vikram Singh

Abstract
We consider the problem of estimating the spectral density of the normalized adjacency matrix of an n n -node undirected graph. We provide a randomized algorithm that, with O ⁡ ( n ​ ε − 2 ) O(n\varepsilon^{-2}) queries to a degree and neighbor oracle and in O ⁡ ( n ​ ε − 3 ) O(n\varepsilon^{-3}) time, estimates the spectrum up to ε \varepsilon accuracy in the Wasserstein-1 metric. This improves on previous state-of-the-art methods, including an O ⁡ ( n ​ ε − 7 ) O(n\varepsilon^{-7}) time algorithm from [Braverman et al., STOC 2022] and, for sufficiently small ε \varepsilon , a 2 O ⁡ ( ε − 1 ) 2^{O(\varepsilon^{-1})} time method from [Cohen-Steiner et al., KDD 2018]. To achieve this result, we introduce a new notion of graph sparsification, which we call nuclear sparsification . We provide an O ⁡ ( n ​ ε − 2 ) O(n\varepsilon^{-2}) -query and O ⁡ ( n ​ ε − 2 ) O(n\varepsilon^{-2}) -time algorithm for computing O ⁡ ( n ​ ε − 2 ) O(n\varepsilon^{-2}) -sparse nuclear sparsifiers. We show that this bound is optimal in both its sparsity and query complexity, and we separate our results from the related notion of additive spectral sparsification. Of independent interest, we show that our sparsification method also yields the first deterministic algorithm for spectral density estimation that scales linearly with n n (sublinear in the representation size of the graph).

Introduction

We study the fundamental problem of estimating the spectrum of an (undirected) graph. This problem has received significant attention due to applications in visualizing, classifying, and understanding large networks
[
FDBV01
,
EG17
,
CKSV18
,
DBB19
,
CTU21
,
BKM22
]
. Concretely, we consider a standard version of the problem based on the graph’s normalized adjacency matrix:

Problem 1.1

(Spectral Density Estimation (SDE))
.

Given an
n
-node undirected graph
G=(V,E,w)
with positive edge weights
w\in\mathbb{R}^{E}_{>0}
, return eigenvalue estimates
\widehat{\lambda}_{1}\leq\cdots\leq\widehat{\lambda}_{n}
such that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}}|\widehat{\lambda}_{i}-\lambda_{i}(N_{G})|\leq\varepsilon,

(1)

where
{\lambda}_{1}(N_{G})\leq\cdots\leq{\lambda}_{n}(N_{G})
are the eigenvalues of
G
’s normalized adjacency matrix,
N_{G}\in\mathbb{R}^{V\times V}

Note that (
1

\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}
and the uniform distribution on
\widehat{\lambda}_{1},\ldots,\widehat{\lambda}_{n}
is less than
\varepsilon
.
We also consider a more general problem, which we call
the matrix SDE problem
, where the goal is to estimate eigenvalues of a given symmetric matrix with spectral norm bounded by
1
, in the same metric, (
1
).

Outside of applications in network science, SDE has seen recent interest from the machine learning community due to its connections to learning distributions based on moment estimates
[
KV17
,
JMSS23
]
and learning graph structure from random walk traces

[
CKSV18
]
. The matrix SDE problem also has applications in deep learning
[
GKX19
,
PSG18
]
. Algorithms for related (often stronger) notions of spectral approximation, like relative-error eigenvalue histograms, or point-wise eigenvalue estimates, have been widely studied for applications like approximating matrix norms and spectral sums
[

,
Bha+23
,
SW23
,
Bha+24
]
.

Excitingly, at least for unweighted graphs, it has been shown that
Problem

1.1
can be solved in time that is
sublinear
in the size of
G
’s representation, which can be as large as
\Omega(n^{2})
. Given a “neighbor” oracle that when queried with
a\in V
and

outputs the degree of
a
and the
i
-th vertex incident to
a
(if there is one) in
O(1)
time, there are randomized SDE algorithms running in
2^{O(\varepsilon^{-1})}

[
CKSV18
]
and
O(n\varepsilon^{-7})
time
[
BKM22
]
.
1
1

1

The runtime of the method from
[

is independent of

; instead of outputting a list of

distinct eigenvalue magnitudes and corresponding multiplicities.

Recent work also studies query complexity lower bounds for different oracles
[
JMSS23
]
.
However, large gaps remain: The best query lower bound for neighbor oracles is just

Our work is motivated by these advances, the general importance of sublinear time graph algorithms

,

, and progress on sublinear time algorithms for other spectral problems like expander testing

.
We ask: Is it possible to improve on existing SDE algorithms? Is it possible to efficiently obtain new sparse approximations, i.e.,
sparsifications
, of

that facilitate more efficient spectral density estimation?
Finally, all previous sublinear time SDE algorithms make critical use of randomness. Are there sublinear time

In this paper, we provide an affirmative answer to each of these questions.
For unweighted graphs, we provide a randomized
O(n\varepsilon^{-3})
-time algorithm for solving
Problem

and a deterministic

Moreover, we obtain an algorithm with the same complexity for weighted graphs provided that, when queried with

1.1
A Sparsification Approach to Spectral Density Estimation

Our results follow a natural
two-stage
approach to approximating the spectral density of graphs. First, in sublinear time, we construct a
sparse matrix
that approximates
N_{G}\in\mathbb{R}^{V\times V}
, the normalized adjacency matrix of
G
, and has
o(n^{2})
entries. Second, we apply and adapt existing spectral density estimation methods to efficiently approximate the spectrum of the sparse approximation. This approach decouples the information theoretic problem of how to approximate
N_{G}
using a small number of queries from the computational problem of how to efficiently approximate eigenvalues.

Concretely, our approach motivates the following natural question: What notions of graph approximation (ultimately, sparsification) are sufficient for preserving the spectrum in the sense of (
1
)
and
can be obtained in sublinear time?
We make progress on this problem by introducing a new notion of
\varepsilon
-additive nuclear approximation
and presenting algorithms that obtain near-optimal sparsity and query complexity for producing such approximations. Formally, we define:

Definition 1.2

(Additive Nuclear Approximation and Sparsification)
.

M\in\mathbb{R}^{V\times V}
is an
\varepsilon
-additive
nuclear approximation
to
n
-vertex graph
G=(V,E,w)
if
\left\lVert N_{G}-M\right\rVert_{*}\leq\varepsilon n
.
M
is an
\varepsilon
-additive
nuclear sparsifier
of
G
if it is also
s
-sparse, i.e., has at most
s
non-zero entries, for
s=o(n^{2})
.
2
2

2

Note that
M
itself is not required to be the adjacency matrix of a graph and our algorithms will in general return matrices that are not. However, in all cases, we show that one can modify our algorithm to ensure that the output is indeed a normalized adjacency matrix. See
Lemma

7.1
in
Section

7
for details.

In
Definition

1.2
,
\left\lVert N_{G}-M\right\rVert_{*}
denotes the nuclear norm, i.e., the sum of the singular values of
N_{G}-M
. Importantly, additive nuclear approximation is sufficient for solving the SDE problem. For symmetric matrices,
A,B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, let
W_{1}(A,B)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|{\lambda}_{i}(A)-\lambda_{i}(B)|
denote Wasserstein-
1
distance between the probability distributions
p
and
q
induced by the real-valued eigenvalues of
A
and of
B
. A short proof (see
Section

1.6
,
Fact

1.17
) establishes that
W_{1}(A,B)\leq\frac{1}{n}\left\lVert A-B\right\rVert_{*}
.
3
3

3

When
A
and
B
are diagonal with monotonically decreasing diagonal entries then
W_{1}(A,B)=\frac{1}{n}\|A-B\|_{*}
. In this sense, nuclear approximation is a natural strengthening of approximation in the Wasserstein-1 distance.

As a result, if
\|M-N_{G}\|_{*}\leq\varepsilon n/2
and we find eigenvalues
\widehat{\lambda}_{1},\ldots,\widehat{\lambda}_{n}
that are
\varepsilon/2
close in Wasserstein distance to those of
M
, then by triangle inequality, those eigenvalues solve
Problem

1.1
.

Our main algorithmic result is that nuclear sparsifiers with
O(n\varepsilon^{-2})
non-zero entries can be computed
deterministically
in
O(n\varepsilon^{-2})
time. Formally, we assume the following access to
G
:

Definition 1.3

(Adjacency query model)
.

We say we have
adjacency query access
to a weighted graph
G=(V,E,w)
if
V
is known and there is an
O(1)
time procedure,
\mathsf{GetNeighbor}(a,i)
, that when queried with any
a\in V
and
i\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{>0}
outputs
\deg_{G}(a)
and, if there is one, the
i
-th largest edge
\{a,b\}
and
w_{\{a,b\}}
(with ties broken arbitrarily). If the
a
has
<i
edges, the oracle returns
\emptyset
.

Adjacency queries are easily supported by standard graph data structures. For example, an adjacency list where each node’s neighbors are stored in an array supports adjacency queries for unweighted graphs. To support weighted graphs, it suffices to sort these arrays by edge weight.
4
4

4

While this assumption on the ordering of the array might be considered non-standard, it is easy to support using a basic data structure for storing the graph provided in which we sort the edges by weight. We also show in Theorem
1.13
that we can extend our result to a more standard random walk model, still obtaining a nuclear sparsifier with just
O(n\varepsilon^{-2})
queries.

Within the query model established, we prove the following main result on constructing sublinear size nuclear sparsifiers in
Section

2
. Our method works even for weighted graphs.

Theorem 1.4

(Sublinear Time Nuclear Sparsification)
.

There is a
deterministic
method (Algorithm
1
) that, for any
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, returns a
2n\varepsilon^{-2}
-sparse
\varepsilon
-additive nuclear sparsifier for any undirected weighted graph
G
, and runs in
O(n\varepsilon^{-2})
time in the adjacency query model (
Definition

1.3
).

We obtain
Theorem

1.4
using a greedy approach that deterministically adds edges from
G
to the sparsifier based on their weight and end-point degrees. Importantly, we leverage that this approach is deterministic to obtain the first
deterministic sublinear time SDE algorithms
.
Before discussing these SDE algorithms, in
Section

1.2
we highlight that deterministic sublinear time methods like
Theorem

1.4
are
not possible
for stronger and more well-studied notions of graph sparsification.

1.2
Comparison to Prior Work on Sparsification

A significant line of work studies
spectral graph sparsification
, which generalizes cut sparsification
[
BK96
]
, and has applications in linear system solving, combinatorial graph algorithms, and beyond
[
ST11
,
SS11
,
BSS12
,
Kap+17
,
LS18
]
. Concretely, a spectral sparsifier is defined as:

Definition 1.5

(Spectral Sparsifier,
[
ST11
]
)
.

Given
\varepsilon>0
and a graph
G
with (unnormalized) Laplacian matrix
L
, a symmetric matrix
\widetilde{L}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is an
\varepsilon
-spectral sparsifier of
L
if, for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
(1-\varepsilon)x^{\top}Lx\leq x^{\top}\widetilde{L}x\leq(1+\varepsilon)x^{\top}Lx
.

Definition

1.5
involves the Laplacian, whereas we focus on the (normalized) adjacency matrix. Nevertheless, it is straightforward to show that the spectrum of the normalized adjacency matrix of an
\varepsilon
-spectral sparsifier is
\varepsilon
-close to that of
G
in Wasserstein distance.
However, it is impossible to obtain
\varepsilon
-spectral sparsifiers with
o(n^{2})
adjacency queries (see Theorem 11 of
[
Lee13
]
with
\delta=1/n
). Consequently, we consider notions of spectral sparsification that are weaker than
Definition

1.5
, but stronger than nuclear sparsification (
Definition

1.2
). In particular, we introduce the following notion of
additive error
spectral sparsification, which strictly strengthens
Definition

1.2
:
5
5

5

Definition
1.6
is related to two notions of sparsification previously studied by
[
Lee13
]
and
[
AKLP22
]
. Additive spectral sparsification is stronger than probabilistic
(\varepsilon,\varepsilon)
-spectral sparsification
[
Lee13
]
because we require
x^{\top}\tilde{N}_{G}x-\varepsilon\cdot x^{\top}x\leq x^{\top}Mx\leq x^{\top}\tilde{N}_{G}x+\varepsilon\cdot x^{\top}x
to hold
simultaneously for all vectors

x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with high probability. Additive spectral sparsification is also a stronger notion than
(\varepsilon,\varepsilon)
-cut sparsification
[
AKLP22
]
, because we require the additive guarantee to hold for all vectors
x
rather than just for cuts.

Definition 1.6

(Additive Spectral Sparsifier)
.

A symmetric matrix
M\in\mathbb{R}^{V\times V}
is an
\varepsilon
-additive spectral sparsifier of
G
if
\|M-\widetilde{N}_{G}\|_{2}\leq\varepsilon
.

We show in
Section

6
that an
O(n\varepsilon^{-2}\log n)
-sparse
\varepsilon
-additive spectral sparsifier can also be obtained for all weighted graphs in
O(n\varepsilon^{-2}\log n)
time using standard random sampling methods.
6
6

6

The
O(n\varepsilon^{-2}\log n)
sparsity can be improved to
O(n\varepsilon^{-2})
at the cost of additional
{\sf poly}(\log n/\varepsilon)
runtime factors using near-linear time
\varepsilon
-spectral sparsification methods
[
JRT23
,
LS18
]
.

Given this result,
it is natural to ask whether we can obtain a
deterministic
algorithm for additive spectral approximation with a linear dependence on
n
, as we do for nuclear sparsification in
Theorem

1.4
.
Interestingly, we prove that this is impossible:

Theorem 1.7
.

Any deterministic algorithm requires
\Omega(n^{2})
Adjacency Queries (
Definition

1.3
) to compute a
\frac{1}{4}
-additive spectral sparsifier (
Definition

1.6
), even for unweighted graphs.

Theorem

1.7
highlights a strong separation between our new notation of nuclear sparsification, for which we have a sublinear time deterministic algorithm, and stronger notions of sparsification.

1.3
Applications to Sublinear Time Spectral Density Estimation

A main application of our result on nuclear sparsification (
Theorem

1.4
) is faster deterministic and randomized sublinear time SDE algorithms for graphs. In the randomized setting, a long line of work in computational chemistry, applied math, and, recently, computer science
[
Ski89
,
SR94
,
WWAF06
,
LSY16
]
studies
linear time
methods for spectral density estimation. In
[
CTU21
]
and
[
BKM22
]
it was proven that common randomized algorithms like the stochastic Lanczos quadrature method and moment matching can approximate the spectral density of
any
symmetric matrix
A
up to
\varepsilon
-accuracy in Wasserstein distance using roughly
O(\varepsilon^{-1})
matrix-vector multiplications with
A
.
7
7

7

The method from
[
BKM22
]
uses
O(\min(\varepsilon^{-1},\varepsilon^{-2}\log^{4}(\varepsilon^{-1})/n))
matrix-vector multiplications, which is
O(\varepsilon^{-1})
for sufficiently large
n
.

At a high level, these methods solve the SDE problem by approximating the first
q=O(\varepsilon^{-1})

moments
of
A
’s eigenvalue density, which can be expressed as
\mathrm{Tr}(A),\mathrm{Tr}(A^{2}),\ldots,\mathrm{Tr}(A^{q})
. They return a distribution that matches or nearly matches those moments, so will be increasingly close to the true spectral density as
q
increases. For each
i\in 1,\ldots,q
, estimating
\mathrm{Tr}(A^{i})
can be reduced to roughly
O(i)
matrix-vector products with
A
through the use of stochastic trace estimation methods like Hutchinson’s estimator
[
Hut90
,
MMMW21
]
.

We obtain our main result on the SDE problem by simply applying such methods (concretely, Theorem 1.4 from
[
BKM22
]
) to our nuclear norm sparsifier, which can be multiplied by a vector in
O(n\varepsilon^{-2})
time. Formally, we obtain the following result (proven in
Section

2
):

Theorem 1.8

(Randomized Sublinear Time SDE)
.

There is a randomized algorithm that solves the SDE problem (Problem
1.1
) with probability
99/100
in
O\!\left(n\varepsilon^{-3}\right)
time in the adjacency query model (
Definition

1.3
).

Theorem

1.8
directly improves on an
O(n\varepsilon^{-7})
time method from
[
BKM22
]
, which only applied to unweighted graphs, and takes a different approach from ours. That work leverages randomized methods for approximating matrix-vector products instead of sparsification.
For sufficiently small
\varepsilon
, we also improve on the
2^{O(\varepsilon^{-1})}
time method from
[
CKSV18
]
, which has no dependence on
n
.
8
8

8

Initial evidence (lower bounds in restricted models) suggests that it may not be possible to improve the
\varepsilon
dependence in
[
CKSV18
]
to sub-exponential while maintaining no dependence on
n

[
JMSS23
]
.

In addition to a faster randomized algorithm, in
Section

2
we show how to use our deterministic nuclear norm sparsifiers to obtain the first sublinear time deterministic SDE method for graphs:

Theorem 1.9

(Deterministic Sublinear Time SDE)
.

There is a deterministic algorithm that solves the SDE problem (Problem
1.1
) in
n\cdot 2^{O(\varepsilon^{-1}\log(\varepsilon^{-1})}
time in the adjacency query model (
Definition

1.3
).

We prove

Theorem

1.9
by showing that it is possible to exactly compute the
i^{\text{th}}
eigenvalue moment,
\mathrm{Tr}\left(M^{i}\right)
, for our particular nuclear norm sparsifier
M
in
O(2^{i\log(\varepsilon^{-1})}))
time via direct computation of the diagonal entries of
\mathrm{Tr}\left(M^{i}\right)
. Crucially, we leverage the fact that our nuclear norm sparsifiers guaranteed by
Theorem

1.4
are
uniformly
sparse: not only is the total number of non-zero entries in
M
small, but every
row
of the sparsifier has a bounded number of non-zero entries (specifically,
O(1/\varepsilon^{2})
). Once the eigenvalue moments are computed, we again appeal to the moment matching method from
[
BKM22
]
, as in the proof of
Theorem

1.8
.
Previously, the best deterministic algorithm for spectral density estimation was to perform an eigendecomposition of
N_{G}
in time
O\!\left(n^{\omega}\right)
, where
\omega\leq 2.371552
is the current fast matrix multiplication exponent
[
WXXZ24
]
.

1.4
Lower Bounds for Nuclear Sparsification

Given that nuclear approximation is a natural relaxation of widely studied graph sparsification notions like spectral sparsification, it is desirable to fully understand the complexity of the problem. We complement our algorithmic result from
Theorem

1.4
with nearly matching sparsity and query lower bounds. First, we show that the sparsity of our sparsifiers is near-optimal:

Theorem 1.10

(Sparsity Lower Bound)
.

For any
\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{0}
, where
\varepsilon_{0}\in(0,1)
is a fixed constant and any integer
n\geq 1/\varepsilon^{2}
, there is a graph
G
on
n
nodes, with normalized adjacency matrix
N_{G}
, such that any matrix
M
satisfying
\left\lVert N_{G}-M\right\rVert_{*}\leq n\varepsilon
must have
\Omega(n\varepsilon^{-2}/\log^{2}\varepsilon^{-1})
non-zero entries.

We prove
Theorem

1.10
by considering the extreme case when
\varepsilon=1/\sqrt{cn}
for a small constant
c
. This is the smallest value of
\varepsilon
for which
Theorem

1.4
gives a non-trivial result (i.e., a matrix with less than
n^{2}
entries). Using the probabilistic method, we show that there are
2^{O(n^{2})}
graphs whose adjacency matrices are all
\varepsilon
-far in the nuclear norm. A pigeonhole argument is then used to show that not all of these matrices can be well approximated by
O(n^{2}/\log^{2}n)
-sparse matrices. We prove the result for general
\varepsilon
via a reduction to the
\varepsilon=1/\sqrt{cn}
case.

We also show that any algorithm for constructing an
\varepsilon
-additive nuclear sparsifier must, in the worst case, make
\widetilde{\Omega}(n\varepsilon^{-2})

\mathsf{GetNeighbor}
queries, matching
Theorem

1.4
up to log factors:

Theorem 1.11

(Query Lower Bound)
.

For any
\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{0}
and any integer
n\geq C/\varepsilon^{2}
where
\varepsilon_{0}\in(0,1)
and
C>1
are fixed constants, any algorithm that returns an
\varepsilon
-additive nuclear sparsifier for any input
G
with probability
\geq 3/4
requires
\Omega(n\varepsilon^{-2}\log^{-2}(\varepsilon^{-1}))

\mathsf{GetNeighbor}
queries.

Notably,
Theorem

1.11
even applies to randomized algorithms.

It is interesting to ask if the same query complexity is optimal for spectral density estimation itself (
Problem

1.1
). Currently, the best query lower bound, due to a recent result of
[
JMSS23
]
, is just
\Omega(1/\varepsilon^{2})
. That work also proves a lower bound of
\Omega(2^{1/\varepsilon})
under a more restrictive random walk query model (see
Section

1.5
). However, large gaps still remain in understanding the optimal query complexity and running times for spectral density estimation. Addressing these gaps is an exciting direction for future work.

1.5
Random Walk Query Model

Finally, motivated by the SDE algorithms of
[
BKM22
]
and
[
CKSV18
]
, we consider a weaker graph access model than the adjacency query model. The algorithms in
[
BKM22
]
and
[
CKSV18
]
assume access to
G
via random walks, i.e., that in
O(k)
time we can sample a random walk
v_{0},v_{1},...,v_{k}
, where
v_{0}
is a uniformly random node in
G
and
v_{i}
is chosen from the neighbors of
v_{i-1}
with probability proportional to edge weight.

Interestingly, we show that it is possible to construct nuclear norm sparsifiers in an even weaker model where only one-step walks are allowed:

Definition 1.12

(One-step Random Walk Query Model)
.

We say we have
one-step random walk query access
to a graph
G=(V,E,w)
if there is an
O(1)
time procedure,
\mathsf{RandomNeighbor}
, that returns a uniformly random vertex
a\in V
and
\emptyset
if
a
has no neighbors, or an edge
\{a,b\}
selected with probability proportional to its weight, along with the degree of
a
and
b
.

While our algorithm discussed in
Section

1.1
is not directly implementable in this more restrictive query model, in
Section

6
, we present an alternative, randomized algorithm that achieves identical query and time-complexities in the model:

Theorem 1.13
.

There is an algorithm (Algorithm
3
) that, for any
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, returns with probability
2/3
an
O(n\varepsilon^{-2}
)-sparse
\varepsilon
-additive nuclear sparsifier for any undirected weighted graph
G
using
O(n\varepsilon^{-2})
queries in the one-step random walk query model (Definition
1.12
).

We also obtain an algorithm for
\varepsilon
-additive spectral sparsification in the one-step random walk query model. We obtain the following via a natural application of matrix concentration. Specifically, we directly use an algorithm and theorem from
[
Coh+17
]
.

Theorem 1.14
.

There is an algorithm (Algorithm
4
) that, for any
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, returns with probability
2/3
an
O(n\varepsilon^{-2}\log n
)-sparse
\varepsilon
-additive spectral sparsifier for any undirected weighted graph
G
using just
O(n\varepsilon^{-2}\log n)
queries in the one-step random walk query model (Definition
1.12
).

Theorem
1.14
achieves the stronger notion of
\varepsilon
-additive spectral sparsification, at the cost of an extra factor of
\log n
in the sparsity, query complexity, and runtime as compared to Theorem
1.13
.
We show that this
\log n
factor is unavoidable in the following sense:

Theorem 1.15
.

For a fixed constant
\varepsilon\in(0,1/8)
and
c\in(0,1)
any algorithm requires
\Omega(n\log n)
one-step random walk model queries to output an
\varepsilon
-additive spectral sparsifier with probability
c
.

We prove
Theorem

1.15
via a coupon collector argument. Consider a graph of size
2n
with
n
isolated pairs of nodes, each of which is either connected by an edge or not. To obtain a constant factor additive spectral sparsifier, we must observe one node in each pair, which takes

Preliminary Facts.

We rely on the following basic facts about the nuclear norm.

Fact 1.16

(Nuclear Norm Duality)
.

For any

,

\left\lVert A\right\rVert_{*}=\max_{Y\in\mathbb{S}^{n\times n}:\left\lVert Y\right\rVert_{\mathrm{2}}=1}\left\langle A,Y\right\rangle
.

Fact 1.17
.

For
A,B\in\mathbb{S}^{n\times n}
, if
\left\lVert A-B\right\rVert_{*}\leq n\varepsilon
, then
W_{1}(A,B)\leq\varepsilon
.

Proof.

Let
\lambda_{\min}
be the smallest eigenvalue among the eigenvalues of
A
and
B
, so that
A-\lambda_{\min}I
and
B-\lambda_{\min}I
are both positive semidefinite, where

denotes an
n\times n
identity matrix. The singular values of

and
B-\lambda_{\min}I
equal the eigenvalues of those matrices. So, we can directly apply Mirsky’s singular value perturbation inequality
[

, Theorem 5]
to conclude that:

\displaystyle\left\lVert A-B\right\rVert_{*}=\left\lVert A-\lambda_{\min}I-(B-\lambda_{\min}I)\right\rVert_{*}

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{i\in[n]}|\lambda_{i}(A-\lambda_{\min}I)-\lambda_{i}(B-\lambda_{\min}I)|

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in[n]}\left|\lambda_{i}(A)-\lambda_{i}(B)\right|=nW_{1}(A,B).\qed

∎

Let

Proof.

\ProbOp\left\{\left\lVert A_{1}-A_{2}\right\rVert_{*}\leq\frac{n^{1.5}}{200}\right\}\leq 4\exp\left(\frac{-n^{2}}{4\cdot 10^{6}}\right).

\displaystyle\ProbOp\left\{\exists i\neq j:\left\lVert A_{i}-A_{j}\right\rVert_{*}\leq\frac{n^{1.5}}{200}\right\}\leq 2\exp\left(\frac{-n^{2}}{8\cdot 10^{6}}\right)\,.

(3)

The above equation implies that, with high probability,

\|B_{i}-B_{k}\|_{*}>n^{1.5}/1000

Then, applying

3.2
Proof of Theorem

Section

, but for unnormalized adjacency matrices. In this section, we extend the

In the following lemma, we show that the nuclear norm of a block diagonal matrix is at least the sum of the nuclear norms of the blocks of the matrix.

Lemma 3.5

(Block-wise Nuclear Norm Bound)
.

For any symmetric
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
and
S_{1},\ldots,S_{k}\subseteq[n]
that partition
[n]
, i.e.
\cup_{i\in[k]}S_{i}=[n]
and
S_{i}\cap S_{j}=\emptyset
for all
i\neq j
, it is the case that
\left\lVert A\right\rVert_{*}\geq\sum_{i\in{k}}\left\lVert A_{S_{i},S_{i}}\right\rVert_{*}
, where
A_{S_{i},S_{i}}
denotes the principal submatrix of
A
indexed by
S_{i}
.

Proof.

By
Fact

1.16
we know that there are symmetric
X_{i}^{*}\in\mathbb{S}^{\left\lvert S_{i}\right\rvert\times\left\lvert S_{i}\right\rvert}
for all
i\in[k]
such that
\left\lVert X_{i}\right\rVert_{2}\leq 1
and
\left\langle A_{S_{i},S_{i}},X_{i}^{*}\right\rangle=\left\lVert A_{S_{i},S_{i}}\right\rVert_{*}
for all
i\in[k]
. Let
\hat{X}\in\mathbb{S}^{n\times n}
be the block-diagonal matrix defined as
\hat{X}_{S_{i},S_{i}}=X_{i}
for all
i\in[k]
and all other entries of
Y
set to
0
. Note that for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

|x^{\top}\hat{X}x|=\left|\sum_{i\in[k]}x^{\top}_{S_{i}}X_{i}x_{S_{i}}\right|\leq\sum_{i\in[k]}\left\lVert x_{S_{i}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}=\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\,

where we used that
|x^{\top}_{S_{i}}X_{i}x_{S_{i}}|\leq\left\lVert x_{S_{i}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}
since
\left\lVert X_{i}\right\rVert_{2}\leq 1
. Consequently,
\left\lVert\hat{X}\right\rVert_{2}\leq 1
and the result follows from
Fact

1.16
as

\left\lVert A\right\rVert_{*}=\max_{X\in\mathbb{S}^{n\times n}:\left\lVert X\right\rVert_{\mathrm{2}}\leq 1}\left\langle A,X\right\rangle\leq\left\langle A,\hat{X}\right\rangle=\sum_{i\in[k]}\left\langle A_{S_{i},S_{i}},X_{i}^{*}\right\rangle=\sum_{i\in[k]}\left\lVert A_{S_{i},S_{i}}\right\rVert_{*}\,.\qed

Next, we show that if the blocks of a block diagonal matrix cannot be well approximated by a sparse matrix, then the block diagonal matrix also cannot be well approximated by a sparse matrix.

Corollary 3.6

(Corollary of

)
.

Let

be such that

be a block diagonal matrix

and

is an arbitrary matrix of size
(n-kb)
. For any pair of constants

, let

, for each

with

Then, for any matrix

\left\lVert E-B\right\rVert_{*}>n\tau/(4b)

Proof.

Let

. For

, define

as the submatrix of

Let

∎

4

In this section, we prove

Interestingly, our lower bound applies in an even more general query model which supports edge queries in addition to neighbor queries. We formalize this model below:

Definition 4.1

(Generalized adjacency query model)

We say we have

to a weighted graph

1.10

. Then, we will show that we can solve the coupon collector problem in
T\leq n\log n-\log(1/c)n
queries with constant probability
c
, which leads to a contradiction.

This follows because we can determine all the edges in the graph
G_{X}
, and hence all the coupons
(a_{i},b_{i})
for
i\in[n]
, from
\widetilde{A}
by taking
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
indexed by
a_{i}
and
b_{i}
, for
i\in[n]
. We take
x
to be the all
0
vector except at two coordinates where it is
1
, i.e.,
x_{a_{i}}=x_{b_{i}}=1
, and
x_{k}=0
for
k\neq a_{i}\text{ or }b_{i}
.
We get that if
3/4\leq x^{\top}\widetilde{A}x\leq 5/4
then
(a_{i},b_{i})\in E
and if
-1/4\leq x^{\top}\widetilde{A}x\leq 2/4
, then
(a_{i},b_{i})\not\in E
.

Therefore, any algorithm that takes
T\leq n\log n-\log(1/c)n
oracle queries, fails to produce a spectral sparsifier, with probability greater than
c
.
∎

Note that for the random graphs
G_{X}
considered in the proof of Theorem
1.15
, the degree of each node is at most
1
; consequently, on these random graphs, the
k
-step random walk query model is no more informative than a
1
-step random walk model. Therefore, the lower bound of Theorem
1.15
holds for the
k
-step query model as well.

7
From Nuclear Sparsifiers to Graphical Nuclear Sparsifiers

Throughout this paper, we have constructed nuclear sparsifiers that are matrices (Definition
1.2
) but may not necessarily be the normalized adjacency matrices of any graph. In this section, we prove the following lemma, which shows how to convert our nuclear sparsifiers into an
graphical nuclear sparsifier
(i.e., a nuclear sparsifier which is the normalized adjacency matrix of some graph) using no additional queries and only
O(n/\varepsilon^{2})
additional runtime. We leverage the fact that a closer analysis of the proofs of Theorems
2.1
and
1.13
actually achieve an
stronger
notion of sparsification than nuclear sparsification (Definition
1.2
) in that they output
\varepsilon
-additive nuclear sparsifiers
M
such that
\left\lVert M-N_{G}\right\rVert_{*}^{2}\leq\left\lVert M-N_{G}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}\leq n\varepsilon^{2}
.

In the following, for a matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, we let
A_{[1:j]}
denote the leading principal submatrix of order
j
.

Lemma 7.1
.

Let
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and
G=(V,E,w)
be a graph on
n
nodes. Without loss of generality, assume the vertices are ordered such that
\deg(i)\geq\deg(j)
for
i<j
. Let
M\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be an matrix such that
\left\lVert N_{G}-M\right\rVert_{F}^{2}\leq n\varepsilon^{2}
. Let
Q\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}D_{G}^{1/2}MD_{G}^{1/2}
and
\mathbf{e}\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
be a vector with
e_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\deg(i)-\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}(Q)_{i,j}
. Let
G^{\prime}
be a graph with adjacency and degree matrix

\displaystyle A_{G^{\prime}}=\begin{pmatrix}{Q}_{[1:n-1]}&\mathbf{e}\\
\mathbf{e}^{\top}&0\end{pmatrix},\text{ and }D_{G^{\prime}}=\begin{pmatrix}{D_{G}}_{[1:n-1]}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}^{\top}&\left\lVert\mathbf{e}\right\rVert_{1}\end{pmatrix}.

Then,
\left\lVert N_{G}-N_{G^{\prime}}\right\rVert_{*}\leq 3n\varepsilon
whenever
\varepsilon^{-2}\leq n
.

Proof.

Recall that for any matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
,
\left\lVert A\right\rVert_{F}\leq\left\lVert A\right\rVert_{*}\leq\sqrt{n}\left\lVert A\right\rVert_{F}
. Consequently, note that it suffices to show that

\displaystyle\left\lVert N_{G}-D_{G^{\prime}}^{-1/2}A_{G^{\prime}}D_{G^{\prime}}^{-1/2}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}\leq 9n\varepsilon^{2}.

By assumption,

\displaystyle\left\lVert\left(N_{G}-M\right)_{1:n-1}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}=\left\lVert\left(N_{G}-D_{G^{\prime}}^{-1/2}A_{G^{\prime}}D_{G^{\prime}}^{-1/2}\right)_{1:n-1}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}\leq n\varepsilon\leq n\varepsilon^{2}.

Consequently,

\displaystyle\left\lVert N_{G}-D_{G^{\prime}}^{-1/2}A_{G^{\prime}}D_{G^{\prime}}^{-1/2}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq n\varepsilon^{2}+2\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\left(\frac{w_{\{i,n\}}}{\sqrt{\deg(i)}\sqrt{\deg(n)}}-\frac{e_{i}}{\sqrt{\deg(i)}\sqrt{\left\lVert\mathbf{e}\right\rVert_{1}}}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq n\varepsilon^{2}+2\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\frac{w_{\{i,n\}}^{2}}{{\deg(i)}{\deg(n)}}+2\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\frac{e_{i}^{2}}{{\deg(i)}\left\lVert\mathbf{e}\right\rVert_{1}}.

(8)

We can bound the first term in (
8
) by using the fact that
\deg(n)\leq\deg(i)
:

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\frac{w_{\{i,n\}}^{2}}{{\deg(i)}{\deg(n)}}\leq\frac{1}{{\deg(n)}^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}w_{\{i,n\}}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{{\deg(n)}^{2}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}w_{\{i,n\}}\right)^{2}=1.

We can bound the second term by observing that, since since
0\leq e_{i}\leq\deg(i)
,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\frac{e_{i}^{2}}{\deg(i)\left\lVert\mathbf{e}\right\rVert_{1}}\leq\frac{1}{\left\lVert\mathbf{e}\right\rVert_{1}}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}e_{i}=1.

Substituting into (
8
), we conclude that
\left\lVert N_{G}-D_{G^{\prime}}^{-1/2}A_{G^{\prime}}D_{G^{\prime}}^{-1/2}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}\leq n\varepsilon^{2}+4
, which is less than
9n\varepsilon^{2}
whenever
\varepsilon^{-2}\leq n
.
∎
