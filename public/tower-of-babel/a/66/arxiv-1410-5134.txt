A gap theorem for the ZL-amenability constant of a finite group
Yemon Choi

Abstract
It was shown in [ 2 ] that the ZL-amenability constant of a finite group is always at least 1 1 , with equality if and only if the group is abelian. It was also shown in [ 2 ] that for any finite non-abelian group this invariant is at least 301 / 300 301/300 , but the proof relies crucially on a deep result of D. A. Rider on norms of central idempotents in group algebras. Here we show that if G G is finite and non-abelian then its ZL-amenability constant is at least 7 / 4 7/4 , which is known to be best possible. We avoid use of Rider’s result, by analyzing the cases where G G is just non-abelian, using calculations from [ 1 ] , and establishing a new estimate for groups with trivial centre. MSC 2010 classification: 20C15 (primary); 43A20, 43A62 (secondary). Keywords: amenability constant, character degrees, just non-abelian groups.

Introduction

Given a finite group
G
, consider its complex group algebra
\mathbb{C}G
, and recall that the centre
{\rm Z}\mathbb{C}G
is isomorphic to
\bigoplus_{p}\mathbb{C}p
where the sum is over all minimal idempotents in
{\rm Z}\mathbb{C}G
. Now consider

\Delta_{\rm Z}(G)=\sum_{p}p\otimes p\in{\rm Z}\mathbb{C}G\mathbin{\otimes}{\rm Z}\mathbb{C}G\,.

(1.1)

{\rm Z}\mathbb{C}G\mathbin{\otimes}{\rm Z}\mathbb{C}G
sits naturally inside
\mathbb{C}G\mathbin{\otimes}\mathbb{C}G\equiv\mathbb{C}(G\times G)
, and there is a natural
\ell^{1}
-norm
\|{\cdot}\|_{1}
that we can put on
\mathbb{C}(G\times G)
. We define the
ZL-amenability constant
of
G
, denoted by
\operatorname{\rm AM}_{\rm Z}(G)
, to be
\|{\Delta_{\rm Z}(G)}\|_{1}
.
The original reasons for studying
\operatorname{\rm AM}_{\rm Z}(G)
arose in connection with Banach algebras and (non-abelian) Fourier analysis; however, the problem which we address in this paper can be stated purely in terms of finite groups and the behaviour of their irreducible characters.

\operatorname{\rm AM}_{\rm Z}(G)
seems to have first been studied explicitly in work of A. Azimifard, E. Samei and N. Spronk, although they used different terminology and notation. One of their observations, paraphrased into our notation, is that
\operatorname{\rm AM}_{\rm Z}(G)\geq 1
with equality if and only if
G
is abelian; this is proved using the Schur orthogonality relations and a clever use of an “associated minorant”
\operatorname{ass}(G)
. (See Example
2.7
and Proposition
2.9

Theorem
(Azimifard–Samei–Spronk,
[
2
]

There exists
\delta>0

\operatorname{\rm AM}_{\rm Z}(G)\geq 1+\delta
for every finite non-abelian group
G
.

The purpose of the present paper is to determine

Preliminaries

2.1

Notation and terminology

We assume familiarity with basic character theory of finite groups over the complex field
\mathbb{C}
, up to the Schur orthogonality relations.
All of this can be found in Isaacs’s book
[
6
]
; alternatively, see
[
4
, Chapter 7]
or
[
7
]
.

Given a finite group
G
, we denote by
\operatorname{Conj}(G)
the set of conjugacy classes of
G
, and we write
\operatorname{Irr}(G)
for the set of irreducible characters of
G
(working over
\mathbb{C}
). The
degree
of a character
\phi
is just
\phi(e)
(which equals the dimension of any representation affording that character) and we say that
\phi
is
linear
if
\phi(e)=1
.
If
C\in\operatorname{Conj}(G)
and
\phi
is a character on
G
, we write
\phi(C)
for the value of
\phi
on any element of
C
; thus
|\phi(C)|\leq\phi(e)
.

The element
\Delta_{\rm Z}(G)
mentioned in the introduction is the
diagonal element
or
separability idempotent
for the commutative
\mathbb{C}
-algebra
Z(\mathbb{C}G)
. For the definition of these terms for general algebras over a field, see e.g.
[
5
, Section 6.7]
; further explanation in the special case of the centre of a group algebra, and discussion of the connection with amenability for Banach algebras, can be found in
[
1
, Section 2.1]
.

To keep our presentation self-contained, we will not use general properties of separability idempotents, and instead adopt a “hands-on” definition. First some terminology: if
{\mathbb{I}}
is a non-empty finite indexing set, then by the “natural”
\ell^{1}
-norm on the vector space
\mathbb{C}^{\mathbb{I}}
we simply mean
\|{a}\|_{1}\equiv\|{a}\|_{\ell^{1}({\mathbb{I}})}:=\sum_{i\in{\mathbb{I}}}|a_{i}|
.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
G
be a finite group. If
\phi\in\operatorname{Irr}(G)
, we may regard
\phi\mathbin{\otimes}\phi
as an element of
\mathbb{C}(G\times G)
(since the underlying vector space of
\mathbb{C}(G\times G)
is
\mathbb{C}^{G\times G}
).
Let

\Delta_{\rm Z}(G):=\sum_{\phi\in\operatorname{Irr}(G)}\frac{\phi(e)}{|G|}\phi\mathbin{\otimes}\frac{\phi(e)}{|G|}\phi\in\mathbb{C}(G\times G)\,.

Then, equipping
\mathbb{C}^{G\times G}
with its natural
\ell^{1}
-norm, define the
ZL-amenability constant of
G
to be the
\ell^{1}
-norm of
\Delta_{\rm Z}(G)
.

We could of course define
\operatorname{\rm AM}_{\rm Z}(G)
without explicitly naming
\Delta_{\rm Z}(G)
, but some hereditary properties of
\operatorname{\rm AM}_{\rm Z}
with respect to taking products and quotients of groups are best understood in terms of corresponding properties of
\Delta_{\rm Z}
. This will be done in the next section.

2.2

Hereditary properties of the ZL-amenability constant

The next two lemmas are known results, if one uses the definition of
\Delta_{\rm Z}(G)
that is given in
[
2
]
and
[
1
]
. We shall give character-theoretic proofs that just use Definition
2.1
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
G
and
H
be finite groups. If we identify
\mathbb{C}(G\times H\times G\times H)
with
\mathbb{C}(G\times G)\mathbin{\otimes}\mathbb{C}(H\times H)
in the natural way, then
\Delta_{\rm Z}(G\times H)
is identified with
\Delta_{\rm Z}(G)\mathbin{\otimes}\Delta_{\rm Z}(H)
.

Proof.

This is a straightforward calculation using
\operatorname{Irr}(G\times H)=\{\phi\mathbin{\otimes}\psi\mathbin{\colon}\phi\in\operatorname{Irr}(G),\psi\in\operatorname{Irr}(H)\}
and
(\phi\mathbin{\otimes}\psi)(e_{G\times H})=\phi(e_{G})\psi(e_{H})
.
∎

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
G
and
H
be finite groups, and suppose
q:G\to H
is a surjective group homomorphism. Extend
q
to an algebra homomorphism
Q:\mathbb{C}G\to\mathbb{C}H
in the natural way. Then
(Q\mathbin{\otimes}Q)(\Delta_{\rm Z}(G))=\Delta_{\rm Z}(H)
.

Proof.

The map
\phi\mapsto\phi\circ q
defines an injection
q^{*}:\operatorname{Irr}(H)\hookrightarrow\operatorname{Irr}(G)
.
Now

Q\left(\sum_{y\in H}\sum_{x\in q^{-1}(y)}a_{x}\delta_{x}\right)=\sum_{y\in H}\left(\sum_{x\in q^{-1}(y)}a_{x}\right)\delta_{y}\qquad(a\in\mathbb{C}G).

In particular, for each
\phi\in\operatorname{Irr}(H)
,

Q\left(\frac{q^{*}(\phi)(e_{G})}{|G|}q^{*}(\phi)\right)=\frac{\phi(e_{H})}{|G|}|\ker(q)|\phi=\frac{\phi(e_{H})}{|H|}\phi\;.

Let
{\mathcal{R}}
denote the set
\operatorname{Irr}(G)\setminus q^{*}(\operatorname{Irr}(H))
. Then

Q\left(\sum_{\psi\in\operatorname{Irr}(G)\setminus{\mathcal{R}}}\frac{\psi(e_{G})}{|G|}\psi\mathbin{\otimes}\frac{\psi(e_{G})}{|G|}\psi\right)=\Delta_{\rm Z}(H)\,;

so to complete the proof, it suffices to show that
{\mathcal{R}}\subseteq\ker(Q)
.

Our argument is indirect. Given
f\in{\rm Z}\mathbb{C}G
, for each
y\in H
we pick
x\in q^{-1}(y)
and note that

\delta_{y}*Q(f)=Q(\delta_{x}*f)=Q(f*\delta_{x})=Q(f)*\delta_{y}\,.

Hence
Q({\rm Z}\mathbb{C}G)\subseteq{\rm Z}\mathbb{C}H
. Since
\operatorname{Irr}(H)
spans
{\rm Z}\mathbb{C}H
, it now suffices to prove that

\sum_{y\in H}Q(\theta)(y)\ \overline{\phi(y)}=0\qquad\text{for all $\theta\in{\mathcal{R}}$ and all $\phi\in\operatorname{Irr}(H)$.}

Equivalently, it suffices to prove that for each
\theta\in{\mathcal{R}}
and each
\phi\in\operatorname{Irr}(H)
,

0=\sum_{y\in H}\left(\sum_{x\in q^{-1}(y)}\theta(x)\right)\overline{\phi(y)}=\sum_{x\in G}\theta(x)\overline{(\phi\circ q)(x)}\,.

But this is immediate from Schur orthogonality for
\operatorname{Irr}(G)
.
∎

Corollary 2.4

(
{\rm ZL}
-amenability constants of products and quotients)
.

(i)

Let
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
be finite groups. Then
\operatorname{\rm AM}_{\rm Z}(G_{1}\times G_{2})=\operatorname{\rm AM}_{\rm Z}(G_{1})\operatorname{\rm AM}_{\rm Z}(G_{2})
.

(ii)

Let
G
be a finite group and let
N\unlhd G
. Then
\operatorname{\rm AM}_{\rm Z}(G/N)\leq\operatorname{\rm AM}_{\rm Z}(G)
.

Proof.

For any finite indexing sets
{\mathbb{I}}
and
{\mathbb{J}}
, the
\ell^{1}
-norm has the following property: for each
a\in\mathbb{C}^{\mathbb{I}}
and
b\in\mathbb{C}^{\mathbb{J}}
,
we have
\|{a\mathbin{\otimes}b}\|_{\ell^{1}({\mathbb{I}}\times{\mathbb{J}})}=\|{a}\|_{\ell^{1}({\mathbb{I}})}\|{b}\|_{\ell^{1}({\mathbb{J}})}
.
Now, using Lemma
2.2
, part
(i)
follows.

To prove part
(ii)
, note that
Q:\mathbb{C}G\to\mathbb{C}H
does not increase the
\ell^{1}
-norm of elements. The same is true for
Q\mathbin{\otimes}Q:\mathbb{C}(G\times G)\to\mathbb{C}(H\times H)
. Therefore
\|{(Q\mathbin{\otimes}Q)(\Delta_{\rm Z}(G))}\|_{1}\leq\|{\Delta_{\rm Z}(G)}\|_{1}
, and applying Lemma
2.3
does the rest.
∎

Remark 2.5
.

Both parts of Corollary
2.4

were already proved in
[
2
, §1]
, using the abstract characterization of
\Delta_{\rm Z}(G)
as the unique “diagonal element” or “separability idempotent” for the algebra
{\rm Z}\mathbb{C}G
. Our approach has the benefit of just using Definition
2.1
and Schur orthogonality for finite groups, but the approach in
[
2
]
works in a somewhat broader context.

2.3

An explicit formula and some comments

In
[
2
, Theorem 1.8]
the authors give the following concrete formula for the ZL-amenability constant of a finite group:

\operatorname{\rm AM}_{\rm Z}(G)=\frac{1}{|G|^{2}}\sum_{C\in\operatorname{Conj}(G)}\sum_{D\in\operatorname{Conj}(G)}|C||D|\left|\sum_{\phi\in\operatorname{Irr}(G)}\phi(e)^{2}\phi(C)\overline{\phi(D)}\right|\;.

(2.1)

We could have taken this as the
definition
of
\operatorname{\rm AM}_{\rm Z}(G)
, if we wished to avoid discussing
\Delta_{\rm Z}(G)
and
\ell^{1}
-norms, but then the proof of Corollary
2.4
would become even more opaque.

Remark 2.6
.

The presence of complex conjugates in Equation (
2.1
) may need some explanation. First, note that the same expression without any complex conjugates follows immediately from Definition
2.1
and the definition of the
\ell^{1}
-norm on
\mathbb{C}(G\times G)
. Then observe that there is a bijection on
\operatorname{Conj}(G)
which exchanges the conjugacy class of
x
with that of
x^{-1}
; and if we denote this bijection by
D\longleftrightarrow D^{-1}
, then
|D^{-1}|\phi(D^{-1})=|D|\overline{\phi(D)}
for each
D\in\operatorname{Conj}(G)
and each character
\phi
.

Example 2.7

(ZL-amenability constants of finite abelian groups)
.

Let
G
be a finite abelian group. It was noted in
[
2
]
that in this case

\Delta_{\rm Z}(G)=|G|^{-1}\sum_{x\in G}\delta_{x}\mathbin{\otimes}\delta_{x^{-1}}
, which clearly has
\ell^{1}
-norm exactly
1
. (See the remarks in
[
1
, Section 2.1]
for further explanation of this identity.) Here we give a more direct proof.
By Equation (
2.1
)

\operatorname{\rm AM}_{\rm Z}(G)=\frac{1}{|G|^{2}}\sum_{x,y\in G}\left|\sum_{\phi\in\widehat{G}}\phi(x)\overline{\phi(y)}\right|\,.

By Schur (column) orthogonality, most terms in this sum vanish and summing the remaining ones gives

\operatorname{\rm AM}_{\rm Z}(G)=|G|^{-2}\sum_{x\in G}\sum_{\phi\in\widehat{G}}1=1,

as required.

In general it seems hard to get good
lower
bounds on the expression in (
2.1
), since there could be considerable cancellation when we sum over
\phi
, as in Example
2.7
. A key idea in
[
2
]
is to introduce a minorant for
\operatorname{\rm AM}_{\rm Z}(G)
that is easier to estimate well from below. The authors of
[
2
]
do not give this invariant a name, but since it is the main object of study in Section
3
it deserves some
ad hoc
notation.

Definition 2.8

(An auxiliary minorant)
.

For
G
a finite group, we define

\operatorname{ass}(G)=\frac{1}{|G|^{2}}\sum_{C\in\operatorname{Conj}(G)}\sum_{\phi\in\operatorname{Irr}(G)}|C|^{2}|\phi(C)|^{2}\phi(e)^{2}\ .

(2.2)

Proposition 2.9

(Azimifard–Samei–Spronk,
[
2
]
)
.

\operatorname{\rm AM}_{\rm Z}(G)\geq\operatorname{ass}(G)\geq 1
for every finite group
G
. Moreover, if
G
is non-abelian then
\operatorname{ass}(G)>1
.

Proof.

This is demonstrated in the proof of Theorem 1.8 and Corollary 1.9 in
[
2
]
, but for sake of completeness we include the argument here.
Note that
\operatorname{ass}(G)
is obtained by considering the right-hand side of (
2.1
) and only summing over the terms indexed by
\{(C,C)\mathbin{\colon}C\in\operatorname{Conj}(G)\}\subset\operatorname{Conj}(G)\times\operatorname{Conj}(G)
. Thus
\operatorname{\rm AM}_{\rm Z}(G)\geq\operatorname{ass}(G)
.
Moreover, since
|C|^{2}\geq|C|
, we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{ass}(G)

\displaystyle\geq|G|^{-2}\sum_{C\in\operatorname{Conj}(G)}\sum_{\phi\in\operatorname{Irr}(G)}|C||\phi(C)|^{2}\phi(e)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{\phi\in\operatorname{Irr}(G)}\phi(e)^{2}\sum_{C\in\operatorname{Conj}(G)}\frac{|C|}{|G|}|\phi(C)|^{2}

\displaystyle=1\,,

where the final equality follows from the Schur orthogonality relations (row and column).
Finally, if
G
is non-abelian, then there is at least one
C\in\operatorname{Conj}(G)
and
\phi\in\operatorname{Irr}(G)
such that
|C|^{2}|\phi(C)|^{2}>|C||\phi(C)|^{2}
, so that running the argument above we see that the inequality is strict.
∎

Remark 2.10
.

Combining Proposition
2.9
with Rider’s hard result (Theorem
1
) one sees that if
G
is non-abelian then
\operatorname{\rm AM}_{\rm Z}(G)\geq 1+1/300
, i.e. we get a proof of Theorem
1
.
However, it is important to note that this does not tell us anything about

\inf\{\operatorname{ass}(G)\mathbin{\colon}\text{$G$ is finite and non-abelian}\}.

Indeed, the present paper is unable to determine if this infimum is strictly greater than
1
.

A closer inspection of the proof of Proposition
2.9
yields the inequality

\operatorname{ass}(G)-1\geq\frac{s(G)^{2}-s(G)}{|G|}

where
s(G)=\min\{|C|\mathbin{\colon}C\in\operatorname{Conj}(G),|C|>1\}
.
However, in cases where
Z(G)=\{e\}
, we can do much better. This will be the topic of the next section.

Bounds on
\operatorname{ass}
for groups with trivial centre

In this section we obtain a new lower bound for
\operatorname{ass}(G)
when
G
has trivial centre. This is the key idea needed for a new proof of Theorem
1
with a much better constant than that of
[
2
]
.

Theorem 3.1

(Lower bound on
\operatorname{ass}(G)
when
Z(G)=\{e\}
)
.

Let
G
be a finite group with trivial centre, and let
s
be the smallest size of its non-trivial conjugacy classes. Then

\operatorname{ass}(G)-1\geq\frac{s-1}{s+1}\cdot\frac{2s}{s+1}\geq\frac{4}{9}\ .

Remark 3.2
.

The author has not found any groups with trivial centre for which
\operatorname{ass}(G)=13/9
. Probably one can improve on this lower bound by investigating the examples with
s=2
more closely.

The proof of Theorem
3.1
will occupy the rest of this section.
To streamline some of the formulas which follow, we
introduce the matrix
A:\operatorname{Irr}(G)\times\operatorname{Conj}(G)\to[0,1]
defined by

A_{\phi,C}=|G|^{-1}|C|\ |\phi(C)|^{2}\qquad(\phi\in\operatorname{Irr}(G),C\in\operatorname{Conj}(G)).

(3.1)

Note that rows and columns of
A
add up to
1
.
We define

\mu:=\max_{\phi\in\operatorname{Irr}(G)}A_{\phi,e}=\max_{\phi\in\operatorname{Irr}(G)}|G|^{-1}\phi(e)^{2}\in(0,1).

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
G
be a finite group with trivial centre, and let
s=s(G)
be as defined in the statement of Theorem
3.1
.
Then

\frac{\operatorname{ass}(G)-1}{s-1}\geq\sum_{\phi\in\operatorname{Irr}(G)}A_{\phi,e}(1-A_{\phi,e})\geq\max(2\mu,1)(1-\mu).

(3.2)

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle\operatorname{ass}(G)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\phi\in\operatorname{Irr}(G)}\sum_{C\in\operatorname{Conj}(G)}|C|A_{\phi,C}A_{\phi,e}

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{\phi\in\operatorname{Irr}(G)}\left(A_{\phi,e}^{2}+\sum_{C\in\operatorname{Conj}(G)\setminus\{e\}}sA_{\phi,C}A_{\phi,e}\right)

\displaystyle\text{(definition of $s$)}

\displaystyle=(1-s)\sum_{\phi\in\operatorname{Irr}(G)}A_{\phi,e}^{2}+s\sum_{\phi\in\operatorname{Irr}(G)}\sum_{C\in\operatorname{Conj}(G)}A_{\phi,C}A_{\phi,e}

\displaystyle=(1-s)\sum_{\phi\in\operatorname{Irr}(G)}A_{\phi,e}^{2}+s\sum_{\phi\in\operatorname{Irr}(G)}A_{\phi,e}

\displaystyle\text{(rows of $A$ sum to $1$)}

\displaystyle=(s-1)\sum_{\phi\in\operatorname{Irr}(G)}A_{\phi,e}\left(1-A_{\phi,e}\right)+\sum_{\phi\in\operatorname{Irr}(G)}A_{\phi,e}\,.

Since columns of
A
sum to
1
, we obtain
the first inequality in (
3.2
).

To get the second inequality: fix
\psi\in\operatorname{Irr}(G)
with
A_{\psi,e}=\mu=\max_{\phi\in\operatorname{Irr}(G)}A_{\phi,e}
. For each
\phi\in\operatorname{Irr}(G)\setminus\{\psi\}
we have
1-A_{\phi,e}\geq 1-\mu
; also, since columns of
A
sum to
1
,

1-A_{\phi,e}=\sum_{\gamma\in\operatorname{Irr}(G)\setminus\{\phi\}}A_{\gamma,e}\geq A_{\psi,e}=\mu\,.

Hence

\displaystyle\sum_{\phi\in\operatorname{Irr}(G)}A_{\phi,e}\left(1-A_{\phi,e}\right)

\displaystyle=\mu(1-\mu)+\sum_{\phi\in\operatorname{Irr}(G)\setminus\{\psi\}}A_{\phi,e}\left(1-A_{\phi,e}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\mu(1-\mu)+\sum_{\phi\in\operatorname{Irr}(G)\setminus\{\psi\}}A_{\phi,e}\max(\mu,1-\mu)

\displaystyle=\mu(1-\mu)+\max(\mu,1-\mu)(1-\mu)

\displaystyle\text{(rows of $A$ sum to $1$)}

\displaystyle=(1-\mu)\max(2\mu,1),

as required.
∎

Example 3.4
.

Let
q
be a prime power
\geq 3
and consider

\mu=|G|^{-1}(q-1)^{2}=1-q^{-1}

For this group

\operatorname{ass}(\operatorname{Aff}({\mathbf{F}}_{q}))-1\geq(s-1)(1-\mu)\max(2\mu,1)=(q-2)\cdot\frac{1}{q}\cdot 2\left(1-\frac{1}{q}\right);

(3.3)

and the right-hand side of (

(with minimum value

). In fact an explicit but tedious calculation shows that

Example
3.4
shows that even within the class of groups with trivial centre,

can be arbitrarily small. However, note that in this example, when

is small

is large. The following result shows that this happens more generally.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
G
be a finite group. Then

1\leq(1+s)(1-\mu)
.

Proof.

Choose
\psi
with
A_{\psi,e}=\mu
and choose
C
with
|C|=s
.
Then
A_{\psi,C}\leq 1-\mu
(since rows of

sum to

), while

A_{\phi,C}=\frac{s}{|G|}|\phi(C)|^{2}\leq\frac{s}{|G|}\phi(e)^{2}=sA_{\phi,e}\quad\text{for all $\phi\in\operatorname{Irr}(G)$.}

Since the columns of

sum to
1
, this gives

1=A_{\psi,C}+\sum_{\phi\neq\psi}A_{\phi,C}\leq(1-\mu)+s\sum_{\phi\neq\psi}A_{\phi,e}=(1-\mu)+s(1-\mu)

as required.
∎

Remark 3.6
.

(i)

The idea behind the proof of Lemma

•

(iii)

Proof of Theorem

Bounds on
\operatorname{\rm AM}_{\rm Z}
for just non-abelian groups

4.1

JNA groups: definitions and two results

Throughout, we denote the
derived subgroup
(or
commutator subgroup
) of
G
by
[G,G]
rather than
G^{\prime}
.

Definition 4.1
.

We say that a quotient of a group

is
proper
if it is not equal to

itself. A group

is
just non-abelian
(JNA for short) if it is non-abelian yet all its proper quotients are abelian.

Remark 4.2
.

Since every homomorphism from
G
to an abelian group factors through the quotient homomorphism
G\to G/[G,G]
, a group
G
is JNA if and only if all its non-trivial normal subgroups contain
[G,G]
. In alternative terminology: if
G
is JNA then
[G,G]
is the minimal non-trivial normal subgroup of
G
.

Recall that by Corollary
2.4
(ii)
, if
H
is a quotient of
G
then
\operatorname{\rm AM}_{\rm Z}(G)\geq\operatorname{\rm AM}_{\rm Z}(H)
. Now if
G
is a finite, non-abelian group, there exists a quotient of
G
which is JNA. (The proof is a routine induction which we omit.) Hence

\inf\{\operatorname{\rm AM}_{\rm Z}(G)\mathbin{\colon}\text{$G$ is finite and non-abelian}\}=\inf\{\operatorname{\rm AM}_{\rm Z}(G)\mathbin{\colon}\text{$G$ is finite and JNA}\}.

Therefore Theorem
1.2
will follow from the following special case.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
G
be a finite JNA group. Then
\operatorname{\rm AM}_{\rm Z}(G)\geq 7/4
.

Our proof of Theorem
4.3
works by looking at various cases.
The main point is that if

is finite JNA and has non-trivial centre, then it can be handled by the main result of
[
1
]
. For this we use the following lemma, which is a simpler (but weaker) version of
[

, Lemma 12.3]
.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let

be a finite JNA group with non-trivial centre.
Then every non-linear

(i.e. is linear) or has degree

We will also need the following result, which the author was unable to find stated explicitly in the literature.

4.2

The ZL-amenability constant in the JNA cases

To fully exploit Lemma

Theorem 4.9

(
[
1

)
.

Let

is a finite non-abelian group such that
\phi(e)\in\{1,m\}
for all

\operatorname{\rm AM}_{\rm Z}(G)-1=2(m^{2}-1)\sum_{C\in\operatorname{Conj}(G)}\frac{|C|}{|G|}\left(1-\frac{|C|}{|[G,G]|}\right).

The following calculation was done in
[
1
, Example 3.13]
for extraspecial
p

Proposition 4.10
.

Let
G
be a finite JNA group with non-trivial centre. Then there is a prime
p
and an integer
k\geq 1
such that

\operatorname{\rm AM}_{\rm Z}(G)-1=2\left(1-\frac{1}{p^{2k}}\right)\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right).

(4.1)

In particular,
\operatorname{\rm AM}_{\rm Z}(G)\geq 7/4
.

Proof.

Let
p
be the prime from Lemma
4.4
and let
k
satisfy
|G:Z(G)|^{1/2}=p^{k}
. By the lemma every
\phi\in\operatorname{Irr}(G)
has degree

or

, so

is a positive integer (strictly positive since

is non-abelian). Moreover, Lemma
4.4
also implies

\frac{|C|}{|[G,G]|}=\begin{cases}p^{-1}&\quad\text{if $C\in Z(G)$}\\
1&\quad\text{if $C\in\operatorname{Conj}(G)\setminus Z(G)$.}\end{cases}

Applying Theorem
4.9
yields

\displaystyle\operatorname{\rm AM}_{\rm Z}(G)-1

\displaystyle=2(p^{2k}-1)\sum_{C\in\operatorname{Conj}(G)}\frac{|C|}{|G|}\left(1-\frac{|C|}{|[G,G]|}\right)

\displaystyle=2(p^{2k}-1)|Z(G)|\cdot\frac{1}{|G|}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)

\displaystyle=2\frac{(p^{2k}-1)}{|G:Z(G)|}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)

\displaystyle=2\left(1-\frac{1}{p^{2k}}\right)\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right).

To finish: note that the right-hand side of Equation (
4.1
) is an increasing function of

and of
p
. Taking

and

gives the minimum value

, as required.
∎

5

A lower bound for perfect groups

Since every non-abelian simple group is JNA, and since we proved Theorem
1.2
by passing from the given non-abelian finite group to some JNA quotient, it is natural to wonder what can be said about the ZL-amenability constant of a finite simple group. In this section we obtain a lower bound that is far better than what we obtain from Theorem
3.1
, although it is probably far short of the true infimum.
In fact, our bound holds for all finite perfect groups, and our approach does not require any structure theory.

Proposition 5.1

(An alternative bound for
\operatorname{ass}
)
.

Let
G
be a finite group, and let
m=\min\{\phi(e)\mathbin{\colon}\phi\in\operatorname{Irr}(G)\text{ and }\phi(e)>1\}
. Then

\operatorname{ass}(G)-1\geq(m^{2}-1)\sum_{C\in\operatorname{Conj}(G)}\frac{|C|}{|G|}\left(1-\frac{|C|}{|[G,G]|}\right).

Equality holds if every non-linear irreducible character of
G
has degree
m
.

Proof.

This is like the proof of
[
1
, Theorem 2.4]
, only simpler; in some sense it is dual to the argument used in the proof of Proposition
3.3
.

As in Equation
3.1
, let
A_{\phi,C}:=|G|^{-1}|C||\phi(C)|^{2}
. Let
\varepsilon
denote the trivial character of
G
, sometimes called the
augmentation character
, which takes the value
1
on every conjugacy class. Then

\operatorname{ass}(G)=\sum_{C\in\operatorname{Conj}(G)}A_{\varepsilon,C}\sum_{\phi\in\operatorname{Irr}(G)}\phi(e)^{2}A_{\phi,C}\,.

Let
{\mathcal{L}}=\{\phi\in\operatorname{Irr}(G)\mathbin{\colon}\phi(e)=1\}
.
Then for each
C\in\operatorname{Conj}(G)
,

\displaystyle\sum_{\phi\in\operatorname{Irr}(G)}\phi(e)^{2}A_{\phi,C}

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{\phi\in{\mathcal{L}}}A_{\phi,C}+m^{2}\sum_{\phi\in\operatorname{Irr}(G)\setminus{\mathcal{L}}}A_{\phi,C}

\displaystyle\text{(definition of $m$)}

\displaystyle=(1-m^{2})\sum_{\phi\in{\mathcal{L}}}A_{\phi,C}+m^{2}\sum_{\phi\in\operatorname{Irr}(G)}A_{\phi,C}

\displaystyle=(1-m^{2})\sum_{\phi\in{\mathcal{L}}}A_{\phi,C}+m^{2}

\displaystyle\text{(columns of $A$ sum to $1$)}

\displaystyle=(1-m^{2})|{\mathcal{L}}|A_{\varepsilon,C}+m^{2}

\displaystyle\text{(definition of ${\mathcal{L}}$).}

Multiplying this inequality by
A_{\varepsilon,C}
and summing over all
C
,

\operatorname{ass}(G)\geq\sum_{C\in\operatorname{Conj}(G)}A_{\varepsilon,C}\;+\;(m^{2}-1)\sum_{C\in\operatorname{Conj}(G)}A_{\varepsilon,C}(1-|{\mathcal{L}}|A_{\varepsilon,C}).

Since rows of
A
sum to
1
,
and
|{\mathcal{L}}|
equals the order of the quotient group
G/[G,G]
, the result follows.
∎

The following result is surely folklore, but we include a proof since it is straightforward.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
G
be a finite group. Suppose it has a conjugacy class
C
of size
|G|/2
. Then
K=G\setminus C
is an index
2
subgroup of
G
.
In particular,
G
is not perfect.

Proof.

We divide the proof into several steps.

Step 1.

Let
t\in C
. Then its centralizer
Z_{G}(t)
has order
|G|/|C|=2
. Since
\langle t\rangle\subseteq Z_{G}(T)
,
t
is an involution and
Z_{G}(t)=\{1,t\}
.

Step 2.

If
x\in G
is an involution, and
y\in C
and
xy
is an involution, then
x=e
or
x=y
.

[
Proof
:
we have
xy=(xy)^{-1}=y^{-1}x^{-1}=yx
, so
x\in Z_{G}(y)
; now use Step 1.]

Step 3.

Let
x,y\in C
. Then
xy\in K
.

[
Proof
:
if
x=y
then
xy=e
; while if
x
and
y
are distinct elements of
C
, then by Step 2,
xy
cannot be an involution, so in particular
xy\notin C
.]

Step 4.

Let
t\in C
. Then the functions
L_{t},R_{t}:G\to G
defined by
L_{t}(x)=tx
and
R_{t}(x)=xt
are involutive bijections
C\leftrightarrow K
.

[
Proof
:
by Step 3,
L_{t}(C)\subseteq K
; and since
t
is an involution,
L_{t}
is injective. So
|L_{t}(C)|\leq K
. But
|K|=|G\setminus C|=|C|
, hence
L_{t}:C\to K
is surjective. The argument for
R_{t}
is similar.]

Step 5.

K
is closed under multiplication.

[
Proof
:
let
a,b\in K
. Pick
t\in C
; by Step 4,
at,tb\in C
. Since
t
is an involution,
ab=(at)(tb)\in C\cdot C\subseteq K
by Step 3.]

Final step.

Since
C
is the conjugacy class of an involution, it is closed under inversion. Hence
K=G\setminus C
is also closed under inversion. Combined with Step 5, this shows
K
is a subgroup of
G
; and since
K
is the union of conjugacy classes, it is normal. Finally, since
G/K
is abelian of order
2
,
G
is not perfect.
∎

Remark 5.3
.

In Step 1 of the proof we showed, in effect, that
C
consists of self-centralizing involutions. So if
t\in C
then
\langle t\rangle
is
malnormal
in

, hence by Frobenius’s theorem (
[

, Theorem 7.2]
or
[

, Chapter 7, Theorem 8.6]
) it admits a complement, and Lemma

follows.
However, Frobenius’s theorem remains quite a deep result and seems excessive in this instance.

Theorem 5.4
.

Let
G
be a finite perfect group. Then
\operatorname{ass}(G)\geq 3
.

Proof.

If
C\in\operatorname{Conj}(G)
then
|C|\leq|G|/3
(since

is a proper factor of

, and it cannot be

Applying Proposition

[G,G]=G
yields

\operatorname{ass}(G)-1\geq(m^{2}-1)\sum_{C\in\operatorname{Conj}(G)}\frac{|C|}{|G|}\left(1-\frac{1}{3}\right)=\frac{2(m^{2}-1)}{3}\,.

Since
m\geq 2
the result follows.
∎

Remark 5.5
.

If
G
is simple one can improve this lower bound. A finite simple group has no irreducible
2
-dimensional representations over
\mathbb{C}
(see e.g.
[
7
, Corollary 22.13]
);
hence
m\geq 3
when
m
is as defined in Proposition
5.1
. Running the argument in the proof of Theorem
5.4
then yields
\operatorname{ass}(G)\geq 19/3
.

Moreover: since quotients of perfect groups are perfect, every finite perfect group
G
has a quotient group
K
which is non-abelian and simple. It follows that if
G
is a finite perfect group,

\operatorname{\rm AM}_{\rm Z}(G)\geq\operatorname{\rm AM}_{\rm Z}(K)\geq\operatorname{ass}(K)\geq\frac{19}{3}\,.

While this argument does not imply
\operatorname{ass}(G)\geq 19/3
for all finite perfect groups, we know of no finite perfect group whose associated minorant is less than
19/3
.

6

Questions for future investigation

Our first two questions concern the associated minorant
\operatorname{ass}(G)
. Although it is technically easier to deal with than
\operatorname{\rm AM}_{\rm Z}(G)
for particular groups, we do not know if it has the same good hereditary properties.

Question 0.

Is there a finite group
G
and
N\lhd G
such that
\operatorname{ass}(G)<\operatorname{ass}(G/N)
?

If the answer is negative, then we can obtain lower bounds on
\operatorname{ass}(G)
by passing to JNA quotients, and hence the next question would have a
positive
answer.

Question 1.

Is
\inf\{\operatorname{ass}(G)\colon\text{$G$ non-abelian and finite}\}
strictly greater than
1
?

To motivate the next two questions, we present a small consequence of our earlier work on the JN2 cases.

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
G
be a finite nilpotent group. If
\operatorname{\rm AM}_{\rm Z}(G)<59/27
then
G
is the product of a
2
-group with an abelian group.

Proof.

Since
G
is nilpotent it factorizes as a direct product of finite
p
-groups, say
G=H_{1}\times\dots\times H_{m}
.
(More precisely, it is the direct product of its Sylow
p
-subgroups; see e.g.
[
3
, Section 9.8, Theorem 5]
.)

Suppose at least one of these groups, say
H_{1}
, is a non-abelian
p
-group where
p\geq 3
. Then
H_{k}
admits a JNA quotient
K
, and
K
must also be a
p
-group; the proof of Proposition
4.10
then implies that

\operatorname{\rm AM}_{\rm Z}(K)-1\geq 2(1-p^{-2})(1-p^{-1})\geq 2\left(1-\frac{1}{9}\right)\left(1-\frac{1}{3}\right)=\frac{32}{27}\,.

But since
\operatorname{\rm AM}_{\rm Z}(G)\geq\operatorname{\rm AM}_{\rm Z}(H_{1})\geq\operatorname{\rm AM}_{\rm Z}(K)
, this contradicts the assumption on
\operatorname{\rm AM}_{\rm Z}(G)
.
Thus each of the groups
H_{1},\dots,H_{m}
is either abelian or a
2
-group, and the result follows.
∎

Question 2.

Does Theorem
6.1
remain true if one drops the word “nilpotent”?

Note that a positive answer to Question
6
would yield a positive answer to the next question.

Question 3.

Is
\inf\{\operatorname{\rm AM}_{\rm Z}(G)\colon\text{$G$ a finite, non-nilpotent group}\}
strictly greater than
7/4
? Can we at least show that a group with ZL-amenability constant
7/4
has to be nilpotent?

Remark 6.2
.

An explicit calculation shows that
\operatorname{ass}(C_{3}\rtimes C_{2})=5/3
. Therefore, with our current methods we have no hope of proving results of the form “if
G
has trivial centre then
\operatorname{\rm AM}_{\rm Z}(G)\geq 2
”, because without the JNA condition the best we can do for groups with trivial centre is to use the lower bound provided by
\operatorname{ass}(G)
.

Moving away from questions of lower bounds, we could look at the structure of the set

\mathcal{ZL}=\{\operatorname{\rm AM}_{\rm Z}(G)\colon\text{$G$ a finite group}\}.

of possible values for ZL-amenability constants.

Question 4.

Is
\mathcal{ZL}
closed? If not, does it contain any non-isolated points?

The results summarized in
[
1
, Section 3.4]
show that there exist strictly increasing sequences in
\mathcal{ZL}
which converge. The same results suggest that these limiting values are limit points of
\mathcal{ZL}
, and hence that
\mathcal{ZL}
is not closed, but they are not conclusive.

Question 5.

Does
\mathcal{ZL}
contain any strictly decreasing sequences? That is, does there exist a sequence
(G_{n})
of finite groups such that
\operatorname{\rm AM}_{\rm Z}(G_{n+1})<\operatorname{\rm AM}_{\rm Z}(G_{n})
for all
n
?

Our final question connects the study of
\operatorname{\rm AM}_{\rm Z}(G)
back to the world of infinite-dimensional Banach algebras.

Question 6.

Consider an inverse system of finite groups

\dots G_{3}\to G_{2}\to G_{1}\to G_{0}

in which all connecting maps are surjective group homomorphisms.
If
\sup_{n}\operatorname{\rm AM}_{\rm Z}(G_{n})<\infty
, is the inverse limit
\varprojlim_{n}G_{n}
always virtually abelian?

If the answer is positive, then as noted in
[
2
]
, we could prove the following: if
G
is a profinite group which is not virtually abelian, then the centre of
L^{1}(G)
is non-amenable.

Appendix A
The proofs of Lemma
4.4
and Theorem
4.5

These proofs are included for the convenience of readers who are more familiar with harmonic analysis and Banach algebras than finite group theory.
To clarify the proof of Lemma
4.4
, it is helpful to divide the statement of the lemma into smaller pieces.

Restatement of Lemma
4.4
.

Let
G
be a finite JNA group which has non-trivial centre.
Then there is a prime
p
such that:

(i)

|G|
is a power of
p
;

(ii)

|[G,G]|=p
;

(iii)

every conjugacy class has size
1
or size
p
;

(iv)

every non-linear
\phi\in\operatorname{Irr}(G)
has degree
|G:Z(G)|^{1/2}
.

Proof of
(i)
.

Since
Z(G)
is non-trivial,
G/Z(G)
is abelian. So
G
is
2
-step nilpotent. Hence there exist subgroups
H_{1},\dots,H_{s}
which each have prime power order, such that
G\cong H_{1}\times\dots\times H_{s}
. (More precisely,
G
factorizes as the direct product of Sylow subgroups, see e.g.
[
3
, Section 9.8, Theorem 5]
.)
At least one of these subgroups, say
H_{1}
, must be non-abelian; but then since
G
quotients onto
H_{1}
, the JNA condition forces
G=H_{1}
.

Proof of
(ii)
.

By
(i)
,
G
has order
p^{N}
for some prime
p
and some
N\geq 2
. So
|Z(G)|=p^{m}
for some
1\leq m\leq N-1
. By the JNA condition
[G,G]
is contained in every non-trivial subgroup of
Z(G)
. Hence
[G,G]
is an abelian
p
-group with no non-trivial proper subgroups, so it must be cyclic of order
p
.

Proof of
(iii)
.

Let
C\in\operatorname{Conj}(G)\setminus Z(G)
. Since
|C|
divides
|G|
it is a power of
p
. On the other hand, there is an injection
C\to[G,G]
(fix
x\in C
and consider the map
C\to[G,G]
,
y\mapsto yx^{-1}
). By part
(ii)
,
|[G,G]|=p
, and so
|C|=1
or
|C|=p
.

Proof of
(iv)
.

Let
{\mathcal{L}}
denote the set of linear characters on
G
, and let
C\in\operatorname{Conj}(G)\setminus Z(G)
. By Schur orthogonality (column sums)

\frac{|G|}{|C|}-\sum_{\phi\in\operatorname{Irr}(G)\setminus{\mathcal{L}}}|\phi(C)|^{2}=\sum_{\psi\in{\mathcal{L}}}|\psi(C)|^{2}=|{\mathcal{L}}|\,.

On the other hand: since any finite abelian group has the same cardinality as its dual group,

|{\mathcal{L}}|=|(G/[G,G])^{\wedge}|=|G/[G,G]|=\frac{|G|}{|[G,G]|}.

By
(ii)
and
(iii)
,
|C|=p=|[G,G]|
. Combining this with the previous two identities forces

\sum_{\phi\in\operatorname{Irr}(G)\setminus{\mathcal{L}}}|\phi(C)|^{2}=0,

which in turn forces

\phi(C)=0
for all
\phi\in\operatorname{Irr}(G)\setminus{\mathcal{L}}
.

Now let
\phi\in\operatorname{Irr}(G)\setminus{\mathcal{L}}
. By the previous paragraph,
\phi(C)=0
for all
C\in\operatorname{Conj}(G)\setminus Z(G)
. Hence, by Schur orthogonality (row sums),

|G|=\sum_{x\in Z(G)}|\phi(x)|^{2}=|Z(G)|\phi(e)^{2}\,.

Rearranging yields the desired identity, and the proof of the lemma is complete.
\Box

Now we turn to Theorem
4.5
, which we paraphrase as follows.

Theorem
4.5
, paraphrased.

Let
G
be a finite JNA group which has trivial centre and has a conjugacy class of size
2
. Then
G
contains an involution
t
and an element
r
of odd prime order
p
, such that
|G|=2p
and
rt=tr^{-1}
.

Originally the present author re-invented a proof of this result, but the argument which follows is streamlined slightly using some ideas from
Newman’s paper
[
8
]
.
To illustrate where we use the various hypotheses on
G
, the proof is broken up into several lemmas.

Lemma A.1
.

Let
G
be a finite group with a conjugacy class
\{a,\overline{a}\}
of size
2
. Then for each
g\in G
, conjugation by
g
either fixes both
a
and
\overline{a}
or it swaps them. In particular,
a
and
\overline{a}
commute.

Proof.

When a group acts on a set of size two, each element either acts trivially or swaps the two points. This gives us the first claim in the lemma.
The second claim follows by considering the conjugation action of
a
on
\{a,\overline{a}\}
.
∎

Lemma A.2
.

Let
G
be a finite JNA group with a conjugacy class
\{a,\overline{a}\}
of size
2
.
Put
r=\overline{a}a^{-1}\neq\{e\}
. Then
[G,G]=\langle r\rangle
, and the order of
r
is prime. Moreover,
r
has order
2
if and only if it is central.

Proof.

Let
g\in G
. By Lemma
A.1
, either
grg^{-1}=r
or
grg^{-1}=a(\overline{a})^{-1}=(\overline{a})^{-1}a^{-1}=r^{-1}
. Thus the conjugation map
x\mapsto gxg^{-1}
sends
r
to
r^{\pm 1}
, and since
r^{-1}\in\langle r\rangle
this shows
\langle r\rangle\lhd G
.
So by the JNA condition
[G,G]\subseteq\langle r\rangle
. On the other hand, let
g\in G
be such that
gag^{-1}=\overline{a}
; then
r=gag^{-1}a^{-1}\in[G,G]
. Thus
[G,G]=\langle r\rangle
.

Let
p
be a prime which divides the order of
r
. Then
\langle r^{p}\rangle<\langle r\rangle=[G,G]
. Moreover
\langle r^{p}\rangle\lhd G
, since the only conjugates of
r^{p}
are
r^{p}
itself and
r^{-p}
, which both belong to
\langle r^{p}\rangle
. So by the JNA condition,
r^{p}=e
. Thus
r
has order
p
.

Finally: since the conjugacy class of
r
is
\{r^{\pm 1}\}
,
r
is central if and only if
r=r^{-1}
, i.e. if and only if
r^{2}=e
.
∎

Choose some
t\in G
such that
\overline{a}=tat^{-1}
. Let
H=Z_{G}(a)=Z_{G}(\overline{a})
: this has index
2
in
G
, so is normal, and
G=H\sqcup Ht
. Note that
\langle r\rangle\subseteq H
, by another application of Lemma
A.1
.

Lemma A.3
.

Let
G
be as above, and suppose in addition that
Z(G)=\{e\}
. Then
H=[G,G]
.

Proof.

By our previous remarks,
[G,G]=\langle r\rangle\subseteq H
.
So it suffices to prove
|H|\leq|[G,G]|
, and we can do this by exhibiting an injection
H\to[G,G]
. (Note that this injection will not be a left inverse to the inclusion of sets
[G,G]\hookrightarrow H
.)

Consider the function
\theta:H\to[G,G]
defined by
\theta(h)=tht^{-1}h^{-1}
. Since
[G,G]\subseteq Z_{G}(H)
,

t(hk)t^{-1}=(tht^{-1})(tkt^{-1})=\theta(h)h\theta(k)k=\theta(h)\theta(k)hk\quad\text{for all $h,k\in H$.}

Hence
\theta(hk)=\theta(h)\theta(k)
for all
h,k\in H
, i.e.
\theta
is a homomorphism.
Now consider the subgroup

K=\ker(\theta)=\{h\in H\colon ht=th\}=H\cap Z_{G}(t).

Since
K
is the kernel of a homomorphism
H\to G
,
K\unlhd H
, and so
K\lhd G
since
G=H\cup Ht
and
K\subseteq Z_{G}(t)
.
Suppose
K\neq\{e\}
. Then
K\supseteq[G,G]
, by the JNA condition, and so
r\in K
by Lemma
A.2
. But this would imply
rt=tr
, and since
r\in Z_{G}(H)
it would follow from
G=H\cup Ht
that
r\in Z(G)
, contradicting the assumption that
Z(G)=\{e\}
. Thus
K=\{e\}
, so
\theta
is injective, as required.
∎

Finishing the proof of Theorem
4.5
.

Let
G
be finite JNA with trivial centre and a conjugacy class of size
2
. Combining the previous lemmas, we obtain
r,t\in G
such that:

trt^{-1}=r^{-1}
;

r
has odd prime order;
and
G=\langle r,t\rangle
(this follows from Lemma
A.3
and the fact
H=\langle r\rangle
).
It only remains to show that
t^{2}=e
.
Since
trt^{-1}=r^{-1}
,
t^{2}
commutes with
r
, and hence with everything in
G=\langle r,t\rangle
. So
t^{2}\in Z(G)=\{e\}
, and the proof of Theorem
4.5
is complete.
∎
