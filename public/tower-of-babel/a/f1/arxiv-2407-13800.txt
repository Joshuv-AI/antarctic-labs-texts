Representation of continuum equations in physical components for arbitrary curved surfaces
Sujit Kumar Nath

Abstract
Continuum equations are ubiquitous in physical modelling of elastic, viscous, and viscoelastic systems. The equations of continuum mechanics take nontrivial forms on curved surfaces. Although the curved surface formulation of the continuum equations are derived in many excellent references available in the literature, they are not readily usable for solving physical problems due to the covariant, contravariant or mixed nature of the stress and strain tensors in the equations. We present the continuum equations in terms of physical components in a general differentiable manifold. This general formulation of the continuum equations can be used readily for modelling physical problems on arbitrary curved surfaces. We demonstrate this with the help of some examples.

2
The concept of physical components of vectors and tensors

Intuitively, the physical components of any scalar, vector, or tensor
are the components whose dimensions are same as their dimensions while measured during
any physical/experimental measurement. The continuum mechanical equations
are generally represented in covariant, contravariant or mixed tensor notations when
the equations are derived for arbitrary curved manifolds. Therefore, these equations are
not readily usable for physical modelling as the dimensions of the covariant, contravariant,
or mixed tensors do not match with their physically measured counterparts. Hence, it is
important to represent these equations in their correct physical components which can be
used for physical modelling purpose, being immune to any dimensional mismatch.

2.1
Physical components of vectors

Let
\mathbf{v}={v}^{i}\mathbf{g}_{i}={v}_{i}\mathbf{g}^{i}
be the representation of a
vector
\mathbf{v}
in its covariant and contravariant bases
\{\mathbf{g}_{i}\}
and
\{\mathbf{g}^{i}\}

with components
{v}^{i}
and

\mathbf{P}=(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta)

\displaystyle{a}_{r}=\frac{d^{2}r}{dt^{2}}-r\left(\frac{d\theta}{dt}\right)^{2},\,\,\,{a}_{\theta}=r^{2}\frac{d^{2}\theta}{dt^{2}}+2r\frac{dr}{dt}\frac{d\theta}{dt}.

\displaystyle\bigl[{a}_{r}\bigr]_{\mathcal{E^{*}}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{{g}_{rr}}}~{a}_{r}={a}_{r},\,\,\bigl[{a}_{\theta}\bigr]_{\mathcal{E^{*}}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{{g}_{\theta\theta}}}~{a}_{\theta}=\frac{{a}_{\theta}}{r},

and

\bigl[{a}_{\theta}\bigr]_{\mathcal{F^{*}}}
. In the next section we show that for a general tensor of any
arbitrary order all the physical components carry the same physical dimension.

2.2
Physical components of tensors

We shall discuss here the physical components of second order tensors only. The case of tensors
of any arbitrary order can easily be generalised from this discussion. Let

\mathbb{T}={\tau}^{ij}\mathbf{g}_{i}\otimes\mathbf{g}_{j}={\tau}_{ij}\mathbf{g}^{i}\otimes\mathbf{g}^{j}={\tau}^{i}_{\cdot j}\mathbf{g}_{i}\otimes\mathbf{g}^{j}={\tau}_{i}^{\cdot j}\mathbf{g}^{i}\otimes\mathbf{g}_{j}
be a second order tensor, represented
in the covariant, contravariant, and mixed bases. We can represent the physical components
in the same way we did for vectors by representing the tensor components in unit bases.
Therefore, the
16
physical components of the tensor
\mathbb{T}
are given by

\displaystyle\bigl[{\tau}^{ij}\bigr]_{\mathcal{E\otimes E}}=\sqrt{{g}_{ii}{g}_{jj}}~{\tau}^{ij},\,\,\bigl[{\tau}^{ij}\bigr]_{\mathcal{E\otimes F}}=\sqrt{\frac{{g}_{ii}}{{g}^{jj}}}~{\tau}^{ij},

\displaystyle\bigl[{\tau}^{ij}\bigr]_{\mathcal{F\otimes E}}=\sqrt{\frac{{g}_{jj}}{{g}^{ii}}}~{\tau}^{ij},\,\,\bigl[{\tau}^{ij}\bigr]_{\mathcal{F\otimes F}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{{g}^{ii}{g}^{jj}}}~{\tau}^{ij},

\displaystyle\bigl[{\tau}^{i}_{\cdot j}\bigr]_{\mathcal{E\otimes E^{*}}}=\sqrt{\frac{{g}_{ii}}{{g}_{jj}}}~{\tau}^{i}_{\cdot j},\,\,\bigl[{\tau}^{i}_{\cdot j}\bigr]_{\mathcal{E\otimes F^{*}}}=\sqrt{{g}_{ii}{g}^{jj}}~{\tau}^{i}_{\cdot j},

\displaystyle\bigl[{\tau}^{i}_{\cdot j}\bigr]_{\mathcal{F\otimes E^{*}}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{{g}^{ii}{g}_{jj}}}~{\tau}^{i}_{\cdot j},\,\,\bigl[{\tau}^{i}_{\cdot j}\bigr]_{\mathcal{F\otimes F^{*}}}=\sqrt{\frac{{g}^{jj}}{{g}^{ii}}}~{\tau}^{i}_{\cdot j},

\displaystyle\bigl[{\tau}_{i}^{\cdot j}\bigr]_{\mathcal{E^{*}\otimes E}}=\sqrt{\frac{{g}_{jj}}{{g}_{ii}}}~{\tau}_{i}^{\cdot j},\,\,\bigl[{\tau}_{i}^{\cdot j}\bigr]_{\mathcal{E^{*}\otimes F}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{{g}_{ii}{g}^{jj}}}~{\tau}_{i}^{\cdot j},

\displaystyle\bigl[{\tau}_{i}^{\cdot j}\bigr]_{\mathcal{F^{*}\otimes E}}=\sqrt{{g}^{ii}{g}_{jj}}~{\tau}_{i}^{\cdot j},\,\,\bigl[{\tau}_{i}^{\cdot j}\bigr]_{\mathcal{F^{*}\otimes F}}=\sqrt{\frac{{g}^{ii}}{{g}^{jj}}}~{\tau}_{i}^{\cdot j},

\displaystyle\bigl[{\tau}_{ij}\bigr]_{\mathcal{E^{*}\otimes E^{*}}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{{g}_{ii}{g}_{jj}}}~{\tau}_{ij},\,\,\bigl[{\tau}_{ij}\bigr]_{\mathcal{E^{*}\otimes F^{*}}}=\sqrt{\frac{{g}^{jj}}{{g}_{ii}}}~{\tau}_{ij},

\displaystyle\bigl[{\tau}_{ij}\bigr]_{\mathcal{F^{*}\otimes E^{*}}}=\sqrt{\frac{{g}^{ii}}{{g}_{jj}}}~{\tau}_{ij},\,\,\bigl[{\tau}_{ij}\bigr]_{\mathcal{F^{*}\otimes F^{*}}}=\sqrt{{g}^{ii}{g}^{jj}}~{\tau}_{ij}.

(9)

It can be shown that all the physical components of a tensor have the same dimension
which is same as their dimension in Cartesian coordinate
[
13
]
.
However, the method opted in
[
13
]
is not always applicable for tensor
fields on surfaces of codimension one, as there may not always exist a suitable Cartesian
coordinate for representing a tensor field on such a surface. Here we present an alternative
way to prove that all the physical components of a tensor have the same dimension with
the help of intrinsic geometry of the surface. We again demonstrate this with the
help of second order tensors, which can be easily generalised for tensors of arbitrary
order.

We first derive a few results relating the physical dimensions of the covariant and
contravariant metric tensor and vector components. Considering the squared arc length

(10)

we obtain

\displaystyle\dim\bigl[{{g}_{ij}}\bigr]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{L^{2}}{\dim\bigl[{{dx}^{i}}\bigr]\dim\bigl[{{dx}^{j}}\bigr]},

(11)

\displaystyle\dim\bigl[{{g}^{ij}}\bigr]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{L^{2}}{\dim\bigl[{{dx}_{i}}\bigr]\dim\bigl[{{dx}_{j}}\bigr]},

(12)

\displaystyle\dim\bigl[{{dx}^{i}}\bigr]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{L^{2}}{\dim\bigl[{{dx}_{i}}\bigr]},

(13)

where

denotes the dimension of arc length

(14)

Again, from (

\dim\bigl[{\sqrt{{g}_{ii}}}\bigr]=\frac{L}{\dim\bigl[{{dx}^{i}}\bigr]},

(15)

We now show the dimensional equality of the physical tensor components represented in different

3
Equation of motion of an elastic body in physical components

We have seen in Sec.
2
that all the physical components
of a tensor carry the same physical dimension irrespective of their representative bases. However, the basis vectors in
\mathcal{E^{*}}=\{\mathbf{e}^{i}\}
and

\mathcal{F}=\{\mathbf{f}_{i}\}
do not have unit norm in general, unlike the vectors in the bases

\mathcal{E}=\{\mathbf{e}_{i}\}
and
\mathcal{F^{*}}=\{\mathbf{f}^{i}\}
which have unit norm. Therefore,
it is customary to use the bases
\mathcal{E}=\{\mathbf{e}_{i}\}
and

\mathcal{F^{*}}=\{\mathbf{f}^{i}\}
for defining the components of physically measurable vectors
or tensors.

We now propose a method to obtain the continuum equations in physical components,
directly from their general covariant or contravariant form. Here we use the bases

\mathcal{E}=\{\mathbf{e}_{i}\}
and
\mathcal{F^{*}}=\{\mathbf{f}^{i}\}
(with basis vectors of
unit norm) to obtain the physical components. Our formalism here is motivated by the previous work
due to Altman & Oliveira
[
12
]
.
We know from standard elasticity theory
that the equation relating the strain and displacement (for small displacements, neglecting the nonlinear terms) is given by

{\epsilon}_{ij}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial u_{i}}{\partial x^{j}}+\frac{\partial u_{j}}{\partial x^{i}}\right)-\Gamma^{\alpha}_{ij}u_{\alpha},

(24)

where
{\epsilon}_{ij}
is the strain component,
{u}_{i}
is the displacement component,
and
\Gamma^{\alpha}_{ij}
is the Christoffel symbol of second kind. We also have the equation of motion (EOM)
in a general coordinate as

\nabla_{j}{{\tau}^{ij}}+{F}^{i}=\rho\,{a}^{i},

(25)

where
{\tau}^{ij}
is the stress,
{F}^{i}
is the force per unit volume,

{a}^{i}
is the acceleration, and
\nabla
denotes the covariant derivative.
Finally, we also have the constitutive relation between stress and strain which, in case of linear
elasticity of homogeneous and isotropic media, can be written as

{\tau}^{i}_{\cdot j}=\lambda\,\Theta\,{\delta}^{i}_{\cdot j}+2\mu{\epsilon}^{i}_{\cdot j},

(26)

where
\lambda,\mu
are the Lamé’s constants and
\Theta={\epsilon}^{i}_{\cdot i}
is the dilation.
However, as we have discussed in Sec.
2.1
, the dimensions of the
vector or tensor quantities
{u}_{i},{a}^{i},{\epsilon}_{ij},{\tau}^{ij},{\tau}^{i}_{\cdot j}

etc. are not the same as they are realised during physical/experimental measurements. Hence,
it is necessary to convert the equations (
24
)- (
26
) in terms
of physical components so that they can be applied to model physical systems. It can be
easily understood that for rectangular Cartesian coordinate this conversion is not necessary
as the covariant, contravariant or mixed components of tensors coincide with their physical
components automatically. However, in general, even in the cases of orthogonal curvilinear
coordinates, the conversion of the equations (
24
)-(
26
)
in physical components is necessary to avoid any dimensional mismatch.

Denoting the physical components by
{u}({i}),{F}({i}),{\epsilon}({ij}),{\tau}({ij})
etc. with respect to the bases
\mathcal{E}=\{\mathbf{e}_{i}\}
,

\mathcal{F^{*}}=\{\mathbf{f}^{i}\}
and using the relations in (
6
) and
(
9
) we obtain the strain-displacement relation (
24
) in
their corresponding physical components as

{\epsilon}({ij})=\frac{\sqrt{{g}^{ii}{g}^{jj}}}{2}\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{j}}\left(\frac{{u}({i})}{\sqrt{{g}^{ii}}}\right)+\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{i}}\left(\frac{{u}({j})}{\sqrt{{g}^{jj}}}\right)\right]-\sqrt{{g}^{ii}{g}^{jj}}\,\Gamma^{\alpha}_{ij}\frac{{u}({\alpha})}{\sqrt{{g}^{\alpha\alpha}}},

(27)

where no summation is implied over the same type of indices, i.e., the metric tensor components
{g}^{ii},{g}^{jj}
etc.
are not summed over
i
or
j
.
Next, expanding the covariant derivative in (
25
) we obtain

\frac{\partial{\tau}^{ij}}{\partial{x}^{j}}+\Gamma^{i}_{j\alpha}{\tau}^{\alpha j}+\Gamma^{j}_{j\alpha}{\tau}^{i\alpha}+{F}^{i}=\rho\,{a}^{i}.

(28)

Replacing the contravariant type components of the stress tensor
{\tau}^{ij},{\tau}^{i\alpha}
etc. in terms of
physical components (using the relations in (
9
)) we obtain the equation of
motion as

\frac{\partial}{\partial{x}^{j}}\left(\frac{{\tau}({ij})}{\sqrt{{g}_{ii}{g}_{jj}}}\right)+\Gamma^{i}_{j\alpha}\left(\frac{{\tau}({\alpha j})}{\sqrt{{g}_{\alpha\alpha}{g}_{jj}}}\right)+\Gamma^{j}_{j\alpha}\left(\frac{{\tau}({i\alpha})}{\sqrt{{g}_{ii}{g}_{\alpha\alpha}}}\right)+\frac{{F}({i})}{\sqrt{{g}_{ii}}}=\rho\,\frac{{a}({i})}{\sqrt{{g}_{ii}}},

(29)

where the summations are applicable only over common indices of different type (covariant or
contravariant as determined by (
28
)). Similarly, we obtain the constitutive relation
between stress and strain in terms of physical components as

{\tau}({ij})=\lambda\,\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{{\epsilon}({ii})}{\sqrt{{g}_{ii}{g}^{ii}}}\right]\,{\delta}({ij})+2\,\mu\,{\epsilon}({ij}),

(30)

where
n
is the dimension of the manifold. It is to be noted that for orthogonal coordinates
the constitutive relation in physical components retains the same form as its
mixed tensor form (
26
).
Equations (
27
),
(
29
), and (
30
) are the general form of the
strain-displacement relation, EOM, and constitutive relation of an elastic body in
physical components in any arbitrary curvilinear coordinate, which are not only limited to orthogonal coordinates.

0&\,\frac{1}{r^{2}}\end{array}}\right).

Therefore, the dual basis vectors are given by

\mathbf{g}^{r}={g}^{rj}\mathbf{g}_{j}=\left(\cos\theta,\sin\theta\right)\,\,\,\mathrm{and}\,\,\,\mathbf{g}^{\theta}={g}^{\theta j}\mathbf{g}_{j}=\left(-\frac{\sin\theta}{r},\frac{\cos\theta}{r}\right).

(41)

The Christoffel symbols are calculated to be

\Gamma^{r}_{\theta\theta}=-r,\Gamma^{\theta}_{r\theta}=\frac{1}{r}\,\,\,\mathrm{and}\,\,\,\Gamma^{r}_{rr}=\Gamma^{r}_{r\theta}=\Gamma^{\theta}_{\theta\theta}=\Gamma^{\theta}_{rr}=0.

(42)

Therefore, using (
27
), we obtain the strain-displacement relations
in physical components for plane polar coordinate as

\displaystyle{\epsilon}({rr})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\partial{u}({r})}{\partial r},

(43)

\displaystyle{\epsilon}({r\theta})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2r}\left[\frac{\partial{u}({r})}{\partial\theta}-{u}({\theta})+r\frac{\partial{u}({\theta})}{\partial r}\right],

(44)

\displaystyle{\epsilon}({\theta\theta})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{r}\left[\frac{\partial{u}({\theta})}{\partial\theta}-{u}({r})\right].

(45)

Similarly, we obtain the EOM in physical components directly from (
29
)
as

\displaystyle\frac{\partial{\tau}({rr})}{\partial r}+\frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial{\tau}({r\theta})}{\partial\theta}+\frac{{\tau}({rr})-{\tau}({\theta\theta})}{r}+{F}({r})=\rho{a}({r}),

(46)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial{\tau}({r\theta})}{\partial r}+\frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial{\tau}({\theta\theta})}{\partial\theta}+\frac{2{\tau}({r\theta})}{r}+{F}({\theta})=\rho{a}({\theta}).

(47)

We obtain the constitutive relations in physical components from (
30
)
as

\displaystyle{\tau}({rr})=\lambda\,\left[{\epsilon}({rr})+{\epsilon}({\theta\theta})\right]+2\,\mu\,{\epsilon}({rr}),

(48)

\displaystyle{\tau}({r\theta})=2\,\mu\,{\epsilon}({r\theta}),

(49)

\displaystyle{\tau}({\theta\theta})=\lambda\,\left[{\epsilon}({rr})+{\epsilon}({\theta\theta})\right]+2\,\mu\,{\epsilon}({\theta\theta}).

(50)

We see from (
43
)-(
50
) that
the strain-displacement relations, EOM, and the constitutive relations in plane polar coordinate obtained from
(
27
), (
29
), and (
30
) clearly match with the results
in the literature
[
17
]
but obtained via different procedures.
The advantage of having equations (
27
), (
29
), and
(
30
)
is that they can be applied to any curvilinear coordinate or curved surfaces readily,
which are also not limited to the orthogonal coordinates.
The strain-displacement relationship and EOM have been derived for orthogonal
curvilinear coordinates via different methods in seminal works by Sokolnikoff, Timoshenko etc.
[
17
,
18
]
. However,
the general form of strain-displacement relationship, EOM, and the constitutive relation in physical components as given in (
27
), (
29
), and
(
30
) are very scarce in the previous literature.

4
Conclusion

In this article we have summarized different types of physical components of vectors and tensors, scattered in earlier works in the literature
[
14
,
13
,
16
,
15
]
. We have provided an intrinsic proof
to show that all the physical components of a vector or tensor have the same physical dimension. Finally,
we use these physical components to derive the general form of the equations of elasticity which are readily
applicable for physical modelling purpose. The extension of this study to derive the hydrodynamic
equations in physical components is straightforward.
