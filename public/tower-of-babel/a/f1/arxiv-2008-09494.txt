Zenon Jan Jabłoński, Il Bong Jung, Jan Stochel

Abstract
. In this paper we extensively investigate the class of conditionally positive definite operators, namely operators generating conditionally positive definite sequences. This class itself contains subnormal operators, 2 2 - and 3 3 -isometries, complete hypercontractions of order 2 2 and much more beyond them. Quite a large part of the paper is devoted to the study of conditionally positive definite sequences of exponential growth with emphasis put on finding criteria for their positive definiteness, where both notions are understood in the semigroup sense. As a consequence, we obtain semispectral and dilation type representations for conditionally positive definite operators. We also show that the class of conditionally positive definite operators is closed under the operation of taking powers. On the basis of Agler’s hereditary functional calculus, we build an L ∞ ​ ( M ) L^{\infty}(M) -functional calculus for operators of this class, where M M is an associated semispectral measure. We provide a variety of applications of this calculus to inequalities involving polynomials and analytic functions. In addition, we derive new necessary and sufficient conditions for a conditionally positive definite operator to be a subnormal contraction (including a telescopic one).

If

\widetilde{\varPsi}_{\mathcal{H}}^{-1}(\{c\delta_{1}I\})=\emptyset
). To compare the surjectivity of

\varPsi_{\mathcal{H}}
and
\widetilde{\varPsi}_{\mathcal{H}}
,
note that if
\varPsi_{\mathcal{H}}^{-1}(\{(B,C,F)\})\neq\emptyset
, then by Theorem
1.3.1

any
T\in\varPsi_{\mathcal{H}}^{-1}(\{(B,C,F)\}
is CPD
and
T\in\widetilde{\varPsi}_{\mathcal{H}}^{-1}(\{M\})
,
where
M
is defined by (
1.3.4
) and
(
1.3.5
). And
vice versa
, if

\widetilde{\varPsi}_{\mathcal{H}}^{-1}(\{M\})\neq\emptyset
, then any
T\in\widetilde{\varPsi}_{\mathcal{H}}^{-1}(\{M\})
is CPD and
T\in\varPsi_{\mathcal{H}}^{-1}(\{(B,C,F)\})
, where
B
,
C

and
F
are defined by
(
1.3.3
)-(
1.3.5
). Note also that if

(B,C,F)=(0,0,0)
, then

corresponds to

(B,C,F)
via (
1.3.4
) and (

) and
by (
1.3.7
) and (

), we have

\displaystyle\varPsi_{\mathcal{H}}^{-1}(\{(0,0,0)\})\varsubsetneq\widetilde{\varPsi}_{\mathcal{H}}^{-1}(\{0\})\quad\text{(provided $\dim\mathcal{H}\geqslant\aleph_{0}$).}

The reader must be aware of the fact that there
are operators

such that
T\in\varPsi_{\mathcal{H}}^{-1}(\{(B,0,0)\})
with

, where
(B,0,0)
corresponds to

M=0
via (
1.3.3
)-(
1.3.5
). These are
exactly non-isometric 2-isometries. It is worth
mentioning that the ranges of the mappings

\varPsi_{\mathcal{H}}
and
\widetilde{\varPsi}_{\mathcal{H}}

when restricted to operators of class
\mathcal{Q}
can
be described explicitly (see
Remark
3.3.7
). However, the problem of
describing the ranges of the mappings

\varPsi_{\mathcal{H}}
and
\widetilde{\varPsi}_{\mathcal{H}}
in

The organization of this paper is as follows. We
begin by introducing notation and terminology in
Subsection
1.4
and collecting more or
less known facts about PD and CPD (scalar)
sequences in Subsection
2.1
. The

). We also compare the

sequences (see Theorem

).
Theorem
2.2.13
, which is the main result of

\{\gamma_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
of exponential
growth is PD if and only if
0
is an
accumulation point of the set of all
\theta\in(0,\infty)
for which the sequence
\{\theta^{n}\gamma_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
is CPD. In
Subsection
2.3
we characterize CPD
sequences of exponential growth for which the
sequence of consecutive differences is either
convergent or bounded from above plus some
additional constraints (see
Theorems
2.3.2
and

2.3.3
). As a consequence, we
show that, subject to some mild constraints,
convergent CPD sequences of exponential growth
are PD (see Corollary
2.3.4
).

Starting from Section
3
, we begin the
study of CPD operators. In

3.1
we give a semispectral
integral representation for such operators and
relate their spectral radii to the closed

3.1.1
). Certain semispectral
integral representations for completely
hypercontractive and completely hyperexpansive

[

,
21
,
22
]
with the

. In our case

3.4.6
).
Theorem
3.1.6
offers yet another

3.2.5
, which is the

3.2
, provides

3.3

contains simplified semispectral and dilation
representations of CPD operators (see

3.3.1
). As an application, we

3.3.2
). We also completely
characterize CPD operators of class
\mathcal{Q}
(see

). In both cases, we describe

.

, which is the main

3.4
, provides

. On the basis of

on

by subnormality of (in general

,

1.4.
Notation and terminology

We denote by
\mathbb{R}
and
\mathbb{C}
the fields of real
and complex numbers, respectively. Since we consider
suprema of subsets of
\mathbb{R}
which may be empty, we
adhere to the often-used convention that

\displaystyle\sup{\emptyset}=\sup_{x\in\emptyset}f(x):=-\infty\quad\text{whenever $f\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$.}

(1.4.1)

We write
\mathbb{N}
,
\mathbb{Z}_{+}
and
\mathbb{R}_{+}
for the sets of
positive integers, nonnegative integers and
nonnegative real numbers, respectively. As usual,

\mathbb{C}[X]
stands for the ring of all polynomials in
indeterminate
X
with complex coefficients. We
customarily identify members of
\mathbb{C}[X]
with
polynomial functions of one real variable. The unique
involution on
\mathbb{C}[X]
which sends
X
to itself is
denoted by
∗
, that is, if
p=\sum_{i\geqslant 0}\alpha_{i}X^{i}\in\mathbb{C}[X]
, then
p^{*}=\sum_{i\geqslant 0}\overline{\alpha_{i}}X^{i}
, or in the language of
polynomial functions
p^{*}(x)=\overline{p(x)}
for all

x\in\mathbb{R}
. If no ambiguity arises, the
characteristic function of a subset
\varOmega_{1}
of a
set
\varOmega
is denoted by
\chi_{\varOmega_{1}}
.
Given a compact topological Hausdorff space

\varOmega,
let
C(\varOmega)
stand for the Banach
space of all continuous complex functions on

\varOmega
with the supremum norm

\displaystyle\|f\|_{C(\varOmega)}=\sup_{x\in\varOmega}|f(x)|,\quad f\in C(\varOmega).

We write
{\mathfrak{B}}(\varOmega)
for the
\sigma
-algebra
of all Borel subsets of a topological Hausdorff space

\varOmega.
If not stated otherwise, measures
considered in this paper are assumed to be positive.
The
closed support
of a finite Borel measure

\mu
on
\mathbb{R}
(or
\mathbb{C}
) is denoted by
\mathrm{supp}(\mu)

(recall that
\mathrm{supp}(\mu)
exists because
\mu
is
automatically regular, see

[
59
, Theorem 2.18]
). Given
x\in\mathbb{R}
, we
write
\delta_{x}
for the Borel probability measure on

\mathbb{R}
such that
\mathrm{supp}(\delta_{x})=\{x\}
.

All Hilbert spaces considered in this paper are
assumed to be complex. Given Hilbert spaces

\mathcal{H}
and
\mathcal{K}
, we denote by
\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{K})

the Banach space of all bounded linear operators
from
\mathcal{H}
to
\mathcal{K}
. We abbreviate

\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{H})
to
\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{H})
and denote by

\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{H})_{+}
the convex cone
\{T\in\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{H})\colon T\geqslant 0\}
of nonnegative
operators on
\mathcal{H}
. We write
I_{\mathcal{H}}
(or
simply
I
if no ambiguity arises) for the
identity operator on
\mathcal{H}
. Let
T\in\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{H})
. In what follows,
\mathscr{N}(T)
,
{\mathscr{R}}(T)
,

\sigma(T)
,
\sigma_{\mathrm{p}}(T)
,
r(T)

and
|T|
stand for the kernel, the range, the
spectrum, the point spectrum, the spectral
radius and the modulus of
T
, respectively. To
comply with Gelfand’s formula for spectral
radius, we adhere to the convention that
r(T)=0
if
\mathcal{H}=\{0\}.
We say that
T
is
normaloid
if
r(T)=\|T\|
, or equivalently, by
Gelfand’s formula for spectral radius, if and
only if
\|T^{n}\|=\|T\|^{n}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Let us recall the following basic fact (see

[
25
, Proposition II.4.6]
, see also

[
31
, p. 116]
).

Any subnormal operator is normaloid.

(1.4.2)

This will be used several times in this
article. Given an operator
T\in\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{H})
, we set

\displaystyle\mathscr{B}_{m}(T)=\sum_{k=0}^{m}(-1)^{k}{m\choose k}{T^{*}}^{k}T^{k},\quad m\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}.

(1.4.3)

Recall that if
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
\mathscr{B}_{m}(T)=0
,
then
T
is called an
m
-isometry
(see

[
2
, p. 11]
and

[
3
,
4
,
5
]
). An
m
-isometry

T
is said to be
strict
if
m=1
and

\mathcal{H}\neq\{0\}
, or
m\geqslant 2
and
T
is not an

(m-1)
-isometry; in both cases
\mathcal{H}\neq\{0\}

(see
[
16
]
). Examples of strict

m
-isometries for each
m\geqslant 2
are given in

[
7
, Proposition 8]
. We say that
T
is

2
-hyperexpansive
if
\mathscr{B}_{2}(T)\leqslant 0

(see
[
56
]
). We call
T

completely
hyperexpansive
if
\mathscr{B}_{m}(T)\leqslant 0
for all

m\in\mathbb{N}
(see
[
8
]
).

Let
F\colon\mathscr{A}\to\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{H})
be a
semispectral measure
on a
\sigma
-algebra
\mathscr{A}
of
subsets of a set
\varOmega
, i.e.,
F
is

\sigma
-additive in the weak operator topology
(briefly,
wot
) and
F(\varDelta)\geqslant 0
for all

\varDelta\in\mathscr{A}
. Denote by
L^{1}(F)
the linear
space of all complex
\mathscr{A}
-measurable functions

\zeta
on
\varOmega
such that
\int_{\varOmega}|\zeta(x)|\langle F(\mathrm{d}x)h,h\rangle<\infty
for all
h\in\mathcal{H}
. Then for every
\zeta\in L^{1}(F)
, there exists a
unique operator
\int_{\varOmega}\zeta\mathrm{d}F\in\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{H})
such that (see e.g.,
[
71
, Appendix]
)

\displaystyle\Big\langle\int_{\varOmega}\zeta\mathrm{d}Fh,h\Big\rangle=\int_{\varOmega}\zeta(x)\langle F(\mathrm{d}x)h,h\rangle,\quad h\in\mathcal{H}.

(1.4.4)

If
\varOmega=\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}
and
F\colon{\mathfrak{B}}(\varOmega)\to\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{H})
is a semispectral
measure, then its closed support is denoted by

\mathrm{supp}(F)
(recall that such
F
is automatically
regular so
\mathrm{supp}(F)
exists). By a
semispectral
measure
of a subnormal operator
T\in\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{H})
we
mean a normalized compactly supported semispectral
measure
G\colon{\mathfrak{B}}(\mathbb{C})\to\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{H})
defined
by
G(\varDelta)=PE(\varDelta)|_{\mathcal{H}}
for

\varDelta\in{\mathfrak{B}}(\mathbb{C})
, where
E\colon{\mathfrak{B}}(\mathbb{C})\to\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{K})
is the spectral measure of
a minimal normal extension
N\in\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{K})
of
T
and

P\in\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{K})
is the orthogonal projection of
\mathcal{K}

onto
\mathcal{H}
(the minimality means that
\mathcal{K}
has no
proper closed vector subspace that reduces
N
and
contains
\mathcal{H}
). It follows from
[
44
, Proposition
5]
and
[
25
, Proposition II.2.5]
that a
subnormal operator has exactly one semispectral
measure. It is also easily seen that
T^{*n}T^{n}=\int_{\mathbb{C}}|z|^{2n}G(\mathrm{d}z)
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
.
Applying (
1.4.4
) and the measure transport
theorem (cf.
[
6
, Theorem 1.6.12]
) yields

\displaystyle T^{*n}T^{n}=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}x^{n}G\circ\phi^{-1}(\mathrm{d}x),\quad n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+},

(1.4.5)

where
\phi\colon\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
is defined by

\phi(z)=|z|^{2}
for
z\in\mathbb{C}
and

G\circ\phi^{-1}\colon{\mathfrak{B}}(\mathbb{R}_{+})\to\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{H})

is the semispectral measure defined by

G\circ\phi^{-1}(\varDelta)=G(\phi^{-1}(\varDelta))

for
\varDelta\in{\mathfrak{B}}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
. We refer the
reader to
[
25
]
for the foundations of the
theory of subnormal operators.

2.
Conditionally positive definite sequences

2.1.
Basic facts

Let
\boldsymbol{\gamma}=\{\gamma_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
be a
sequence of real numbers. Recall that
\boldsymbol{\gamma}

is PD if (
1.1.1
) holds for all finite
sequences
\{\lambda_{i}\}_{i=0}^{k}\subseteq\mathbb{C}
;
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
is CPD if (
1.1.1
) holds
for all finite sequences

\{\lambda_{i}\}_{i=0}^{k}\subseteq\mathbb{C}
such
that
\sum_{i=0}^{k}\lambda_{i}=0
. It is a matter
of routine to verify that
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
is PD
(resp., CPD) if and only if (
1.1.1
) holds
for all finite sequences

\{\lambda_{i}\}_{i=0}^{k}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
(resp.,
for all finite sequences

\{\lambda_{i}\}_{i=0}^{k}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
such
that
\sum_{i=0}^{k}\lambda_{i}=0
). Another
important observation (cf.
[
10
, Remark 3.1.2]
) is that
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
is
PD (resp., CPD) if and only if

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j=1}^{k}\gamma_{n_{i}+n_{j}}\lambda_{i}\bar{\lambda}_{j}\geqslant 0

for all finite sequences
\{n_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{+}
and
\{\lambda_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}\subseteq\mathbb{C}
(resp., for all finite sequences

\{n_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{+}
and

\{\lambda_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}\subseteq\mathbb{C}
such that

\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}=0
). This shows that
our definitions of positive definiteness and
conditional positive definiteness are consistent
with those in
[
10
, Section 3.1]
. Let us
mention further that according to the
terminology in
[
10
]
,
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
is CPD if
and only if
-\boldsymbol{\gamma}
is “negative definite”.
It follows from the definition that if
\boldsymbol{\gamma}

is PD (resp., CPD), then so is the sequence

\{\gamma_{n+2k}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
for every

k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
. However, it may happen that

\boldsymbol{\gamma}
is PD but

\{\gamma_{n+1}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
is not (e.g.,

\gamma_{n}=(-1)^{n}
for
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
).

The following fundamental characterization of
conditional positive definiteness in terms of positive
definiteness is essentially due to Schoenberg.

Lemma 2.1.1

(
[
55
, Lemma 1.7]
,

[
10
, Theorem 3.2.2]
)
.

If

\boldsymbol{\gamma}=\{\gamma_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
is a sequence of
real numbers, then the following conditions are
equivalent:

(i)

\boldsymbol{\gamma}
is CPD,

(ii)

\{\E^{t\gamma_{n}}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
is
PD for every positive real number
t
.

A sequence
\boldsymbol{\gamma}=\{\gamma_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
of
real numbers is said to be a
Hamburger
(resp.,

Stieltjes
,
Hausdorff
)
moment
sequence
if there exists a Borel measure
\mu
on

\mathbb{R}
(resp.,
\mathbb{R}_{+}
,
[0,1]
) such that

\displaystyle\gamma_{n}=\int t^{n}d\mu(t),\quad n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}.

(2.1.1)

A Borel measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}
satisfying
(
2.1.1
) is called a
representing measure
of

\boldsymbol{\gamma}
. If
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
is a Hamburger moment sequence
which has a unique representing measure on
\mathbb{R}
,
then we say that
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
is
determinate
. Note
that by
[
59
, Ex. 4(e), p. 71]
, the
Weierstrass theorem (see
[
58
, Theorem 7.26]
)
and the Riesz representation theorem (see

[
59
, Theorem 2.14]
) the following holds.

Lemma 2.1.2
.

A Hamburger moment sequence

\boldsymbol{\gamma}=\{\gamma_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
of real numbers
has a compactly supported representing measure if and
only if
\theta:=\limsup_{n\to\infty}|\gamma_{n}|^{1/n}<\infty
. Moreover, if this is the
case, then
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
is determinate and
\mathrm{supp}(\mu)\subseteq[-\theta,\theta]
, where
\mu
is a unique
representing measure of
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
.

In particular, a Hausdorff moment sequence is always
determinate. For our later needs, we recall a theorem
due to Stieltjes.

Theorem 2.1.3

(
[
66
]
,
[
10
, Theorem 6.2.5]
)
.

A sequence
\{\gamma_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
is a Stieltjes moment sequence
if and only if the sequences

\{\gamma_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
and

\{\gamma_{n+1}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
are PD.

We refer the reader to
[
10
,
65
]
for the
fundamentals of the theory of moment problems.

2.2.
Exponential growth

In this
subsection we give an integral representation
for CPD sequences of (at most) exponential
growth (see Theorem
2.2.5
). PD
sequences of exponential growth are
characterized by means of parameters appearing
in the above-mentioned integral representation
(see Theorem
2.2.12
). Theorem

2.2.13
states that a sequence

\{\gamma_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
of exponential
growth is PD if and only if the sequences

\{\theta^{n}\gamma_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
,
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
, are CPD.

We begin by introducing the difference transformation

\triangle
which plays an important role in further
considerations. Denote by
\mathbb{C}^{\mathbb{Z}_{+}}
the complex
vector space of all complex sequences

\{\gamma_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
with linear operations
defined coordinatewise. The difference transformation

\triangle\colon\mathbb{C}^{\mathbb{Z}_{+}}\to\mathbb{C}^{\mathbb{Z}_{+}}
is
given by

\displaystyle(\triangle\boldsymbol{\gamma})_{n}=\gamma_{n+1}-\gamma_{n},\quad n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+},\,\boldsymbol{\gamma}=\{\gamma_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}\in\mathbb{C}^{\mathbb{Z}_{+}}.

Clearly,
\triangle
is a linear. Denote by
\triangle^{k}

the
k
th composition power of
\triangle
, i.e.,

\triangle^{0}
is the identity transformation of

\mathbb{C}^{\mathbb{Z}_{+}}
and
\triangle^{k+1}\boldsymbol{\gamma}=\triangle^{k}(\triangle\boldsymbol{\gamma})
for
\boldsymbol{\gamma}=\{\gamma_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}\in\mathbb{C}^{\mathbb{Z}_{+}}
.

Given
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, we define the polynomial
Q_{n}\in\mathbb{C}[X]
by

\displaystyle Q_{n}(x)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }x\in\mathbb{R}\text{ and }n=0,1,\\
\sum_{j=0}^{n-2}(n-j-1)x^{j}&\text{if }x\in\mathbb{R}\text{ and }n=2,3,4,\dots.\end{cases}

(2.2.1)

Below, for fixed
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
x\in\mathbb{R}
, we
write
\triangle^{k}Q_{(\cdot)}(x)
to denote the
action of the transformation
\triangle^{k}
on
the sequence
\{Q_{n}(x)\}_{n=0}^{\infty}.

Lemma 2.2.1
.

The polynomials
Q_{n}
have the following properties:

\displaystyle Q_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{x^{n}-1-n(x-1)}{(x-1)^{2}},\quad n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+},\,x\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{1\},

(2.2.2)

\displaystyle Q_{n+1}(x)

\displaystyle=xQ_{n}(x)+n,\quad n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+},\,x\in\mathbb{R},

(2.2.3)

\displaystyle\frac{Q_{n}(x)}{n}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{Q_{n+1}(x)}{n+1},\quad n\in\mathbb{N},\,x\in[0,1],

(2.2.4)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{Q_{n}(x)}{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-x},\quad x\in[0,1),

(2.2.5)

\displaystyle(\triangle Q_{(\cdot)}(x))_{n}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }n=0,\,x\in\mathbb{R},\\
\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}x^{j}&\text{if }n\in\mathbb{N},\,x\in\mathbb{R},\end{cases}

(2.2.6)

\displaystyle(\triangle^{2}Q_{(\cdot)}(x))_{n}

\displaystyle=x^{n},\quad n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+},\,x\in\mathbb{R}.

(2.2.7)

Proof.

Suppose
n\geqslant 2
. Then

\displaystyle\frac{x^{n}-1-n(x-1)}{(x-1)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{(\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}x^{i})-n}{x-1}=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\frac{x^{i}-1}{x-1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\sum_{j=0}^{i-1}x^{j}=\sum_{j=0}^{n-2}(n-j-1)x^{j},\quad x\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{1\}.

This implies (

Below, we denote by

Lemma 2.2.2
.

Suppose

and
\mu
is a complex Borel
measure on
\mathbb{R}
such that
\mu(\{1\})=0
, the

is compactly supported and

\displaystyle a+bn+cn^{2}+\int_{\mathbb{R}}Q_{n}(x)\mathrm{d}\mu(x)=0,\quad n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}.

Proof.

Define
\boldsymbol{\gamma}\in\mathbb{C}^{\mathbb{Z}_{+}}

\gamma_{n}=a+bn+cn^{2}+\int_{\mathbb{R}}Q_{n}(x)\mathrm{d}\mu(x)
for

. It follows
from (

\displaystyle 0=(\triangle^{2}\boldsymbol{\gamma})_{n}=\int_{K}x^{n}\mathrm{d}(\mu+2c\delta_{1})(x),\quad n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+},

where

is a compact subset
of
\mathbb{R}
. This implies that

\displaystyle\int_{K}p(x)\mathrm{d}(\mu+2c\delta_{1})(x)=0,\quad p\in\mathbb{C}[X].

Applying the Weierstrass theorem and the uniqueness

for all
\varDelta\in{\mathfrak{B}}(\mathbb{R})
. Substituting

. Putting all this together gives

∎

Now, for the reader’s convenience we state
explicitly the fundamental characterization of
CPD sequences. Recall that a Borel measure on a
Hausdorff topological space is said to be
Radon
if it is finite on compact sets and inner
regular with respect to compact sets.

Theorem 2.2.3

(
[
10
, Theorem 6.2.6]
)
.

A sequence

\boldsymbol{\gamma}=\{\gamma_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
is CPD if and only if it has a
representation of the form

\displaystyle\gamma_{n}=\gamma_{0}+bn+cn^{2}+\int_{\mathbb{R}\setminus\{1\}}(x^{n}-1-n(x-1))\mathrm{d}\mu(x),\quad n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+},

where
b\in\mathbb{R}
,
c\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
\mu
is a Radon
measure on
\mathbb{R}\setminus\{1\}
such that

\displaystyle\int_{0<|x-1|<1}(x-1)^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu(x)<\infty,

\displaystyle\int_{|x-1|\geqslant 1}|x|^{n}\mathrm{d}\mu(x)<\infty,\quad n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}.

For our purpose, we need the following equivalent
variant of Theorem
2.2.3
.

Theorem 2.2.4
.

A sequence

\boldsymbol{\gamma}=\{\gamma_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}

and define the

4.1.
A functional calculus

We begin by discussing the space

L^{\infty}(M)
. Suppose
M\colon{\mathfrak{B}}(\mathbb{R}_{+})\to\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{H})
is a semispectral measure. We
denote by
L^{\infty}(M)
the Banach space of
all equivalence classes of
M
-essentially
bounded complex Borel functions on
\mathbb{R}_{+}

equipped with the
M
-essential supremum norm
(see
[
71
, Appendix]
; see also

[
57
, Section 12.20]
). We customarily
regard elements of
L^{\infty}(M)
as functions
that are identified by the equality a.e.
[M]
,
the latter meaning “almost everywhere with
respect to
M
”. In particular, the norm on

L^{\infty}(M)
takes the form

\displaystyle\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(M)}=\min\{\alpha\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\colon M(\{x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\colon|f(x)|>\alpha\})=0\},\quad f\in L^{\infty}(M).

The relationship between
L^{\infty}(M)
and the classical

L^{\infty}(\mu)
is explained below.

If
\mu
is a Borel measure on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
, then

L^{\infty}(M)=L^{\infty}(\mu)
if and only if
M
and

\mu
are mutually absolutely continuous; if this is
the case, then
\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(M)}=\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(\mu)}
for every
f\in L^{\infty}(M)
.

(4.1.1)

As shown in Example
4.1.1
below, it may not be
possible to find a Borel probability measure on

\mathbb{R}_{+}
with respect to which a given semispectral
measure is absolutely continuous.

Example 4.1.1
.

Let
\varOmega
be any uncountable bounded subset of

\mathbb{R}_{+}
and let
E\colon{\mathfrak{B}}(\mathbb{R}_{+})\to\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{H})
be the spectral measure given by

\displaystyle E(\varDelta)=\bigoplus_{x\in\varOmega}\chi_{\varDelta}(x)I_{\mathcal{H}_{x}},\quad\varDelta\in{\mathfrak{B}}(\mathbb{R}_{+}),

where each
\mathcal{H}_{x}
is a nonzero Hilbert space.
Clearly, the following holds.

If
\varDelta\in{\mathfrak{B}}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
, then

E(\varDelta)=0
if and only if
\varDelta\cap\varOmega=\emptyset
.

(4.1.2)

Suppose to the contrary that
E
is absolutely
continuous with respect to a finite Borel measure

\mu
on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
. Then by (
4.1.2
),
\mu(\{x\})>0
for every
x\in\varOmega
, which is impossible
because
\mu
is finite and
\varOmega
is uncountable
(see
[
6
, Problem 12, p. 12]
). Plainly,
E
is
compactly supported and
\mathrm{supp}(E)=\bar{\varOmega}
.

\diamondsuit

The situation described in Example
4.1.1

cannot happen when
\mathcal{H}
is separable. What is more,
the following statement holds.

Suppose
\mathcal{H}
is separable and
M\colon{\mathfrak{B}}(\mathbb{R}_{+})\to\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{H})
is a nonzero semispectral measure. Then
there exists a Borel probability measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}_{+}

such that
M
and
\mu
are mutually absolutely
continuous.

(4.1.3)

To see this, take an orthonormal basis
\{e_{j}\}_{j\in J}

of
\mathcal{H}
, where
J
is a countable index set. Let

\{a_{j}\}_{j\in J}
be any system of positive real numbers
such that

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in J}a_{j}\langle M(\mathbb{R}_{+})e_{j},e_{j}\rangle=1.

(4.1.4)

(This is possible because
M\neq 0
.) Define the Borel
measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
by

\displaystyle\mu(\varDelta)=\sum_{j\in J}a_{j}\langle M(\varDelta)e_{j},e_{j}\rangle,\quad\varDelta\in{\mathfrak{B}}(\mathbb{R}_{+}).

By (

),
\mu
is a probability measure.
If

is such that

, then

\displaystyle 0=\langle M(\varDelta)e_{j},e_{j}\rangle=\|M(\varDelta)^{1/2}e_{j}\|^{2},\quad j\in J,

which implies that

. Thus
E
is
absolutely continuous with respect to
\mu

is absolutely continuous with respect to

is
immediate.

We now prove the following fact.

If

is a
nonzero compactly supported semispectral measure, then

\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(M)}=\|f|_{\varOmega}\|_{C(\varOmega)}

for every

such that

, where

\varOmega:=\mathrm{supp}(M)
.

(4.1.5)

Indeed, the inequality “
\leqslant
” is obvious. If

is such that

\displaystyle M(\{x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\colon|f(x)|>\alpha\})=0,

then

As a consequence of (

The above discussion shows that (still under the

In Subsection

we were

on polynomials. In

Let

,

and

be as in

4.1.2
. Assume that

Suppose that

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}|a_{n}|^{1/n}<\frac{1}{\sup{\varOmega}}\qquad\Big(\text{with

Let
(\mathcal{K},R,S)

\displaystyle\varLambda_{T}(f)\overset{\eqref{dilat-R}}{=}R^{*}f(S)R.

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}T^{*n}\mathscr{B}_{2}(T)T^{n}=R^{*}f(S)R.

(4.2.3)

It follows from Theorem
4.1.2
(i) and
(

) that (

) holds. According to
the proof of the implication
(ii
′
)
\Rightarrow
(i) of

, (

) holds, so by

and

), we get (see also (

)
and (

\displaystyle f(S)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}S^{n}.

(4.2.4)

Concerning (

), note that

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}T^{*n}\mathscr{B}_{2}(T)T^{n}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}\nabla_{T}^{n}(\mathscr{B}_{2}(T)),

(a general fact which

\displaystyle\frac{1}{r(T)^{2}}\leqslant\frac{1}{\sup\varOmega}.

Let us now discuss two important cases. We begin with

, where

Then the above considerations lead to

),

\displaystyle(\boldsymbol{I}-z\nabla_{T})^{-1}(\mathscr{B}_{2}(T))=R^{*}(I-zS)^{-1}R,\quad z\in\mathbb{C},\,|z|<\frac{1}{r(T)^{2}},

where

is the identity map on

\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{H})
. Note also that if (

)
holds, then by differentiating the operator
valued functions appearing on both sides of the

)
n

It is a matter of routine to show that for an

In other words, we have proved that

such that the analytic function

associated with

In turn, if

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{z^{n}}{n!}T^{*n}\mathscr{B}_{2}(T)T^{n}\overset{({\ddagger})}{=}R^{*}\E^{zS}R,\quad z\in\mathbb{C},

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{{\mathrm{i}\hskip 0.43057pt}^{n}x^{n}}{n!}T^{*n}\mathscr{B}_{2}(T)T^{n}=R^{*}\E^{{\mathrm{i}\hskip 0.3014pt}xS}R,\quad x\in\mathbb{R}.

\displaystyle\bigg\|\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{{\mathrm{i}\hskip 0.43057pt}^{n}x^{n}}{n!}T^{*n}\mathscr{B}_{2}(T)T^{n}\bigg\|\overset{\eqref{dur-b}}{\leqslant}\|R\|^{2}\overset{\eqref{dzi-d}}{=}\|\mathscr{B}_{2}(T)\|,\quad x\in\mathbb{R},

at

is CPD.

h\in\mathcal{H}
,

\displaystyle\|T^{n}h\|^{2}=\langle(I-\mathscr{B}_{2}(T))h,h\rangle+n\langle(\mathscr{B}_{2}(T)-\mathscr{B}_{1}(T))h,h\rangle,\quad n\in\mathbb{N}.

(4.3.5)

By Theorem
1.1.1
,
\{\|T^{n+1}h\|^{2}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
is a Stieltjes
moment sequence for every
h\in\mathcal{H}
.
Combined with (
4.3.5
) and

[
20
, Lemma 4.7]
, this implies
that

\displaystyle\langle(\mathscr{B}_{2}(T)-\mathscr{B}_{1}(T))h,h\rangle=0,\quad h\in\mathcal{H},

or equivalently that
T^{*}\mathscr{B}_{1}(T)T=0
. By (a) and
(
1.4.2
),
T
is a contraction (in fact,

\|T\|=1
if
T\neq 0
), so
\mathscr{B}_{1}(T)\geqslant 0
and
consequently
\mathscr{B}_{1}(T)T=0
.

In turn, if
\|T\|\leqslant 1
and
\mathscr{B}_{1}(T)T=0
, then

T
is a contraction whose restriction to

\overline{{\mathscr{R}}(T)}
is an isometry, so an application
of Lemma
4.3.4
with
\mu_{h}:=\|Th\|^{2}\delta_{1}
shows that
T
is subnormal. This completes
the proof.
∎

Now we give an example of an operator satisfying
condition (i) of Proposition
4.3.5
. In
particular, we show that the class of operators
satisfying this condition can contain both
(non-isometric) subnormal and non-subnormal
operators.

Example 4.3.6
.

Fix real numbers
a\in(0,\infty)
and
b\in[1,\infty)
such that

\displaystyle\theta:=1-2a+ab>0.

(4.3.6)

Define the sequence
\{\lambda_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}\subseteq(0,\infty)
by

\displaystyle\lambda_{n}=\begin{cases}\sqrt{a}&\text{ if }n=0,\\[2.15277pt]
\sqrt{\frac{1+n(b-1)}{1+(n-1)(b-1)}}&\text{ if }n\geqslant 1.\end{cases}

Let
W_{a,b}
be the unilateral weighted shift
on
\ell^{2}
with weights

\{\lambda_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
. It follows from

[
43
, Lemma 6.1 & Proposition 6.2(iii)]

that
W_{a,b}\in\boldsymbol{B}(\ell^{2})
and

\displaystyle\|W_{a,b}\|^{2}=\max\big\{a,b\big\}.

(4.3.7)

One can also verify that
\mathscr{B}_{2}(W_{a,b})
is
the diagonal operator (with respect to the the
standard orthonormal basis of
\ell^{2}
) with the
diagonal
(\theta,0,0,\ldots)
. This together
with (
4.3.6
) implies that

\mathscr{B}_{2}(W_{a,b})W_{a,b}=0
,
\mathscr{B}_{2}(W_{a,b})\geqslant 0
and
\|\mathscr{B}_{2}(W_{a,b})\|=\theta>0
. In
view of Proposition
4.3.5
, the operator

W_{a,b}
satisfies condition (i) of this
proposition. From Propositions
4.3.1

and
4.3.5
it follows that
W_{a,b}
is
a CPD operator which is not
m
-isometric for
any
m\in\mathbb{N}
. If
a>1
, we see that

\|W_{a}\|=\sqrt{a}>1
and

\|\mathscr{B}_{2}(W_{a})\|=(a-1)^{2}
, where

W_{a}:=W_{a,a}
. Since, by Proposition

4.3.5
(a),
r(W_{a})=1
for every
a\in(1,\infty)
, we deduce that

W_{a}
is not normaloid for all
a>1
and
\lim_{a\to\infty}\|W_{a}\|=\lim_{a\to\infty}\|\mathscr{B}_{2}(W_{a})\|=\infty
.

In turn, if
a\in(0,1)
and
b=1
, then one can
verify that
\mathscr{B}_{1}(W_{a,1})W_{a,1}=0
and by
(
4.3.7
),
\|W_{a,1}\|=1
, so by
Proposition
4.3.5
, the operator

W_{a,1}
is subnormal and
r(W_{a,1})=1
.

\diamondsuit

We conclude this subsection with a remark
related to Proposition
4.3.5
and
Example
4.3.6
.

Remark 4.3.7
.

Suppose that
T\in\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{H})
is nonzero and
satisfies condition (i) of
Proposition
4.3.5
(the zero operator on
nonzero
\mathcal{H}
does satisfy (i)). By Proposition

4.3.1
,
T
is not an
m
-isometry
for any
m\in\mathbb{N}
. According to condition
(iii) of Proposition
4.3.5
,
T^{*n}T^{n}

is a polynomial in
n
if
n
varies over
\mathbb{N}

however not when
n
varies over
\mathbb{Z}_{+}
.
Indeed, otherwise, since a nonzero polynomial
may have only finite number of roots, we deduce
from (iii) that
I=I-\mathscr{B}_{2}(T)
, which
contradicts
\mathscr{B}_{2}(T)\neq 0
. In other words,
in view of
[
3
, p. 389]
(see also

[
41
, Corollary 3.5]
), the requirement
that
T^{*n}T^{n}
be a polynomial in
n
if
n

varies over
\mathbb{N}
is not enough for
T
to be
an
m
-isometry no matter what is
m
. Finally
note that
T
falls under Case 3 of the proof of
Theorem
3.2.5
(c) and the discussion
performed in Remark
3.2.8
a). Indeed, by
Theorem
3.2.5
(b),
Proposition
4.3.5
(a) and
Corollary
3.2.7
, we see that

B=-\mathscr{B}_{1}(T)
,
C=0
,
F=M
,
\vartheta:=\sup\mathrm{supp}(F)=0
,
r(T)=1
and

\displaystyle D:=B+\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\frac{1}{1-x}F(\mathrm{d}x)=\mathscr{B}_{2}(T)-\mathscr{B}_{1}(T).

Moreover, in view of Example
4.3.6
, both
cases
D=0
and
D\neq 0
can appear.
\diamondsuit
