Alternative proofs of Mandrekar’s theorem
Linus Bergqvist

Abstract
. We present two alternative proofs of Mandrekar’s theorem, which states that an invariant subspaces of the Hardy space on the bidisc is of Beurling type precisely when the shifts satisfy a doubly commuting condition. The first proof uses properties of Toeplitz operators to derive a formula for the reproducing kernel of certain shift invariant subspaces, which can then be used to characterize them. The second proof relies on the reproducing property in order to show that the reproducing kernel at the origin must generate the entire shift invariant subspace.

32A10, 32A35, 46E22

1.
Background

In this note we give two alternative proofs of Mandrekar’s theorem on shift invariant subspaces of the Hardy space on the bidisc. For the first proof we will use the main idea from the alternative proof of Beurling’s theorem given by Karaev in
[
3
]
, and use it to characterize certain closed, shift invariant subspaces through their reproducing kernels. The second proof is a modified version of the classical proof of Beurling’s theorem given in
[
2
]
. We can already here point out that the proofs given in this article work just as well for
\displaystyle H^{2}(\mathbb{D}^{n})
, though certain calculations become a bit more involved.

As usual, we say that a closed subspace
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}\subset H^{2}(\mathbb{D}^{2})
is
shift invariant
if
\displaystyle S_{j}\mathcal{M}\subset\mathcal{M}
for
\displaystyle j=1,2
, where

S_{j}:H^{2}(\mathbb{D}^{2})\mapsto H^{2}(\mathbb{D}^{2}),\quad f(z)\mapsto z_{j}f(z),\quad\text{for }j=1,2.

We want to prove the following.

Theorem 1

(Theorem 2 from
[
4
]
)
.

An invariant subspace
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}\neq\{0\}
of
\displaystyle H^{2}(\mathbb{D}^{2})
is of the form
\displaystyle\varphi H^{2}
with
\displaystyle\varphi
an inner function if and only if the shift operators
\displaystyle S_{1}
and
\displaystyle S_{2}
are
doubly commuting
on
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}
.

That
\displaystyle\{S_{j}\}_{j=1,2}
is doubly commuting means that the operators commute with each other, and with each other’s adjoints; that is
\displaystyle S_{i}S_{j}=S_{j}S_{i}
and
\displaystyle S_{i}S_{j}^{*}=S_{j}^{*}S_{i}
for
\displaystyle i\neq j
. As always, a function
\displaystyle\varphi
is inner if
\displaystyle\varphi\in H^{\infty}(\mathbb{D}^{2})
and
\displaystyle|\varphi(z)|=1
almost everywhere on
\displaystyle\mathbb{T}^{2}
.

Since we will characterize closed invariant subspaces through their reproducing kernels, we will begin by going through some general theory for the reproducing kernels of operator range spaces and how Toeplitz operators act on such spaces. For more details see
[
1
]
and chapter
\displaystyle 1
of
[
7
]
.

A Hilbert space
\displaystyle H
with inner product
\displaystyle\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{H}
consisting of functions defined on a domain
\displaystyle D
is called a reproducing kernel Hilbert space if point evaluations are bounded linear functionals. By Riesz representation theorem, this means that for every point
\displaystyle z_{0}\in D
there is a function
\displaystyle k_{z_{0}}\in H
, called the
reproducing kernel at
\displaystyle z_{0}
, such that

f(z_{0})=\langle f,k_{z_{0}}\rangle_{H}

for all
\displaystyle f\in H
. The reproducing kernel of
\displaystyle H^{2}(\mathbb{D}^{n})
is the Cauchy kernel

C_{\lambda}(z)=\prod_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{(1-\overline{\lambda_{j}}z_{j})},

and throughout this note, we will denote by
\displaystyle k_{\lambda}^{\mathcal{M}}
the reproducing kernel of a closed subspace
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}\subset H^{2}(\mathbb{D}^{n})
.

If
\displaystyle T:H\mapsto H
is a partial isometry on a reproducing kernel Hilbert space
\displaystyle H
with reproducing kernel
\displaystyle k_{\lambda}(z)
, then
\displaystyle T(H)
and
\displaystyle T(H)^{\perp}
are closed subspaces of
\displaystyle H
, and their reproducing kernels are given by
\displaystyle TT^{*}k_{\lambda}(z)
and
\displaystyle(1-TT^{*})k_{\lambda}(z)
respectively. In particular, we are interested in Toeplitz operators

T_{\varphi}:H\mapsto H,\quad f\mapsto\varphi f,

with symbol
\displaystyle\varphi
belonging the multiplier algebra of
\displaystyle H
. In the case of
\displaystyle H^{2}(\mathbb{D}^{n})
, the multiplier algebra is
\displaystyle H^{\infty}(\mathbb{D}^{n})
, the space of bounded analytic functions on
\displaystyle\mathbb{D}^{n}
.

If
\displaystyle T_{\varphi}
is an isometry, then the above means that
\displaystyle\varphi H
and
\displaystyle(\varphi H)^{\perp}
will have reproducing kernels

(1)

\varphi(z)\overline{\varphi(\lambda)}k_{\lambda}(z)\quad\text{ and }\quad(1-\varphi(z)\overline{\varphi(\lambda)})k_{\lambda}(z)

respectively. In order to arrive at (
1
) we have used that

(2)

T_{\varphi}^{*}=T_{\overline{\varphi}}\quad\text{ and }\quad T_{\overline{\varphi}}k_{\lambda}(z)=\overline{\varphi(\lambda)}k_{\lambda}(z).

These two equalities always hold for Toeplitz operators on reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces, and will be used frequently in this note.

Note that
\displaystyle T_{\varphi}
is an isometry whenever
\displaystyle\varphi
is an inner function and
\displaystyle H
is the Hardy space or a closed subspace of the Hardy space, and thus (
1
) holds in these cases.

2.
First proof

The new idea in the proof given here is that we show that an invariant subspace on which the shift operators are doubly commuting must be of the form
\displaystyle\varphi H^{2}
for
\displaystyle\varphi
inner by characterizing the reproducing kernel of such a subspace, instead of using the wandering subspace theorem from
[
10
]
as is done in the original proof. For some other parts of the theorem, we will still need Mandrekar’s original proof. For completeness and to get a self-contained proof, we will present the proofs from Mandrekar’s article of certain statements that we need to use here as well, and furthermore we will add proofs of statements from
[
10
]
which are used in the proof.

First proof of Theorem
1
.

First of all, if
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}=\varphi H^{2}
for an inner function
\displaystyle\varphi
, then
\displaystyle S_{1}
and
\displaystyle S_{2}
are commuting on
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}
since
\displaystyle z_{1}z_{2}\varphi(z)f(z)=z_{2}z_{1}\varphi(z)f(z)
for all
\displaystyle f\in H^{2}
. Then Mandrekar refers to Theorem
\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle(i)\Rightarrow(ii)
in
[
10
]
to show that
\displaystyle S_{1}
and
\displaystyle S_{2}
are in fact doubly commuting on such a subspace
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}
if
\displaystyle\varphi
is inner (or more generally, of constant modulus on
\displaystyle\mathbb{T}^{2}
).

Another way of showing that
\displaystyle S_{1}
and
\displaystyle S_{2}
are doubly commuting on such a subspace
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}
is by considering their action on the reproducing kernel. From the first formula of (
1
) we know that the reproducing kernel of
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}
is

k^{\mathcal{M}}_{\lambda}(z)=\frac{\overline{\varphi(\lambda)}\varphi(z)}{(1-\overline{\lambda_{1}}z_{1})(1-\overline{\lambda_{2}}z_{2})}.

Since the reproducing kernels are dense in
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}
, we only need to show that

S_{1}S_{2}^{*}k^{\mathcal{M}}_{\lambda}(z)=S_{2}^{*}S_{1}k^{\mathcal{M}}_{\lambda}(z)

for the kernels. By again using equation (
2
), we immediately see that the left hand side is
\displaystyle z_{1}\overline{\lambda_{2}}k^{\mathcal{M}}_{\lambda}(z)
. To see that the same holds for the right hand side we will use that
\displaystyle k^{\mathcal{M}}_{\lambda}(z)=\varphi(z)\overline{\varphi(\lambda)}C_{\lambda_{1}}(z_{1})C_{\lambda_{2}}(z_{2})
, to get

S_{2}^{*}(z_{1}k^{\mathcal{M}}_{\lambda}(z))=\left\langle w_{1}\overline{w_{2}}k_{\lambda}^{\mathcal{M}}(w),k_{z}^{\mathcal{M}}(w)\right\rangle_{H^{2}(\mathbb{D}^{2})}\\
=\left\langle w_{1}\overline{w_{2}}\varphi(z)\overline{\varphi(\lambda)}C_{\lambda_{1}}(z_{1})C_{\lambda_{2}}(z_{2}),\varphi(z)\overline{\varphi(\lambda)}C_{\lambda_{1}}(z_{1})C_{\lambda_{2}}(z_{2})\right\rangle_{H^{2}(\mathbb{D}^{2})}\\
=\left\langle\varphi(z)\overline{\varphi(\lambda)}w_{1}C_{\lambda_{1}}(w_{1})\langle\overline{w_{2}}C_{\lambda_{2}}(w_{2}),C_{z_{2}}(w_{2})\rangle_{H^{2}(\mathbb{D})},C_{z_{1}}(w_{1})\right\rangle_{H^{2}(\mathbb{D})}\\
=\left\langle\varphi(z)\overline{\varphi(\lambda)}w_{1}C_{\lambda_{1}}(w_{1})\overline{\lambda_{2}}C_{\lambda_{2}}(z_{2}),C_{z_{1}}(w_{1})\right\rangle_{H^{2}(\mathbb{D})}\\
=\varphi(z)\overline{\varphi(\lambda)}\overline{\lambda_{2}}C_{\lambda_{2}}(z_{2})\left\langle w_{1}C_{\lambda_{1}}(w_{1}),C_{z_{1}}(w_{1})\right\rangle_{H^{2}(\mathbb{D})}\\
=\varphi(z)\overline{\varphi(\lambda)}\overline{\lambda_{2}}C_{\lambda_{2}}(z_{2})z_{1}C_{\lambda_{1}}(z_{1})=z_{1}\overline{\lambda_{2}}k^{\mathcal{M}}_{\lambda}(z).

Note that in the second equality we have used that
\displaystyle\varphi
is inner in order to get rid of a factor
\displaystyle|\varphi(w)|^{2}
, and in the third equality we have used (
2
) to conclude that
\displaystyle\langle\overline{w_{2}}C_{\lambda_{2}}(w_{2}),C_{z_{2}}(w_{2})\rangle_{H^{2}(w_{2}\in\mathbb{T})}=\overline{\lambda_{2}}C_{\lambda_{2}}(z_{2})
.

This proves one direction of the theorem. It remains to show the other direction.

One proof that we will essentially get from
[
4
]
is the claim that if
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}
is an invariant subspace on which
\displaystyle\{S_{j}\}_{j=1,2}
is doubly commuting, then the closed subspace
\displaystyle O_{1}(\mathcal{M})\cap O_{2}(\mathcal{M})
either has dimension
\displaystyle 1
and contains an inner function, or it only contains the zero function. Here

O_{j}(\mathcal{M})=\mathcal{M}\ominus S_{j}\mathcal{M}.

In order to show that the dimension is smaller than or equal to
\displaystyle 1
, we must use the fact that

(3)

S_{2}(O_{1})\subset O_{1}.

Mandrekar proves this by simply referring to Theorem
\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle(iii)
in
[
10
]
. However, this can be shown with elementary arguments using that
\displaystyle\{S_{j}\}_{j=1,2}
are doubly commuting on
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}
. Namely, since
\displaystyle S_{1}
and
\displaystyle S_{2}^{*}
are commuting, we have that for every
\displaystyle g\in\mathcal{M}\ominus S_{1}\mathcal{M}

0=\langle g,S_{1}(S_{2}^{*}f)\rangle=\langle g,S_{2}^{*}(S_{1}f)\rangle=\langle S_{2}g,S_{1}f\rangle

for all
\displaystyle f\in\mathcal{M}
, and so
\displaystyle S_{2}g\in\mathcal{M}\ominus S_{1}\mathcal{M}
. Again, here we regard
\displaystyle S_{j}
and
\displaystyle S_{j}^{*}
as operators on
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}
.

With this result at hand, we can go on with presenting the way Mandrekar proves that the dimension of
\displaystyle O_{1}(\mathcal{M})\cap O_{2}(\mathcal{M})
is
\displaystyle 1
unless the intersection only contains the zero function.

Let
\displaystyle g_{1},g_{2}\in O_{1}(\mathcal{M})\cap O_{2}(\mathcal{M})
. Then for all
\displaystyle m,n>0

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}z_{1}^{m}z_{2}^{n}g_{1}(z)\overline{g_{2}(z)}dz=0,

and by (
3
) we also have that

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}z_{2}^{n}g_{1}(z)\overline{z_{1}^{m}g_{2}(z)}dz=0

for all
\displaystyle m,n>0
.
By symmetry and since
\displaystyle\overline{z_{1}}^{m}=z_{1}^{-m}
, this means that

(4)

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}z_{1}^{m}z_{2}^{n}g_{1}(z)\overline{g_{2}(z)}dz=0,

for all
\displaystyle(m,n)\neq(0,0)
, which means that
\displaystyle g_{1}(z)\overline{g_{2}(z)}=c
a.e. on
\displaystyle\mathbb{T}^{2}
.

Now suppose that there are
\displaystyle g_{1},g_{2}\in O_{1}(\mathcal{M})\cap O_{2}(\mathcal{M})\setminus\{0\}
with
\displaystyle g_{1}\perp g_{2}
.
In this case
\displaystyle|g_{1}|^{2}=c_{1}\neq 0
and
\displaystyle|g_{2}|^{2}=c_{2}\neq 0
a.e. on
\displaystyle\mathbb{T}^{2}
by (
4
), but
\displaystyle g_{1}\overline{g_{2}}=0
a.e. on
\displaystyle\mathbb{T}^{2}
since
\displaystyle g_{1}\overline{g_{2}}
is a.e. constant and
\displaystyle g_{1}\perp g_{2}
.

This is impossible, and hence
\displaystyle O_{1}(\mathcal{M})\cap O_{2}(\mathcal{M})
is one-dimensional. Furthermore, from the above arguments it follows that
\displaystyle O_{1}(\mathcal{M})\cap O_{2}(\mathcal{M})
contains, and hence be generated by an inner function, which we denote by
\displaystyle\varphi
. All that remains is to show that this implies that
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}=\varphi H^{2}(\mathbb{D}^{2})
for this inner function
\displaystyle\varphi
.

The next step in Mandrekar’s proof relies on a wandering subspace theorem for commuting isometries due to Słociński from
[
10
]
. Here, we instead use an argument with reproducing kernels similar to that in
[
3
]
.

Since
\displaystyle S_{1}
and
\displaystyle S_{2}
are partial isometries on any closed invariant subspace of
\displaystyle H^{2}(\mathbb{D}^{2})
, applying the second formula of (
1
) shows that the reproducing kernel of
\displaystyle O_{2}(\mathcal{M})
is given by

(1-\overline{\lambda_{2}}z_{2})k_{\lambda}^{\mathcal{M}}(z),

and since
\displaystyle O_{2}(\mathcal{M})
is invariant under
\displaystyle S_{1}
by (
3
), applying the second formula of (
1
) again shows that the reproducing kernel for
\displaystyle O_{1}(\mathcal{M})\cap O_{2}(\mathcal{M})
is given by

(5)

(1-\overline{\lambda_{1}}z_{1})(1-\overline{\lambda_{2}}z_{2})k_{\lambda}^{\mathcal{M}}(z).

If
\displaystyle O_{1}(\mathcal{M})\cap O_{2}(\mathcal{M})=\{0\}
, then

(1-\overline{\lambda_{1}}z_{1})(1-\overline{\lambda_{2}}z_{2})k_{\lambda}^{\mathcal{M}}(z)=0\Rightarrow k_{\lambda}^{\mathcal{M}}(z)=0,

and so
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}
is trivial.

If
\displaystyle O_{1}(\mathcal{M})\cap O_{2}(\mathcal{M})\neq\{0\}
, then we know that
\displaystyle O_{1}(\mathcal{M})\cap O_{2}(\mathcal{M})
is one-dimensional, and so its reproducing kernel will be given by
\displaystyle\overline{\varphi(\lambda)}\varphi(z)
for some
\displaystyle\varphi
with
\displaystyle H^{2}
-norm equal to
\displaystyle 1
. From the previous arguments, we know that this
\displaystyle\varphi
will in fact be inner. Put together, this means that

(1-\overline{\lambda_{1}}z_{1})(1-\overline{\lambda_{2}}z_{2})k_{\lambda}^{\mathcal{M}}(z)=\overline{\varphi(\lambda)}\varphi(z)\\
\iff k_{\lambda}^{\mathcal{M}}(z)=\frac{\overline{\varphi(\lambda)}\varphi(z)}{(1-\overline{\lambda_{1}}z_{1})(1-\overline{\lambda_{2}}z_{2})}.

By (
1
) we recognize the right hand side of the last equation as the reproducing kernel of
\displaystyle\varphi H^{2}(\mathbb{D}^{2})
. Since a Hilbert space is uniquely determined by its reproducing kernel, this finishes the proof.

∎

A somewhat informal, but perhaps helpful way to think about the derivation of formula (
5
) for the reproducing kernel of
\displaystyle O_{1}(\mathcal{M})\cap O_{2}(\mathcal{M})
is to note that the orthogonal projection onto
\displaystyle O_{j}(\mathcal{M})\subset\mathcal{M}
is given by
\displaystyle P_{j}:=(I-S_{j}S_{j}^{*})
, and using that since
\displaystyle\{S_{j}\}_{j=1,2}
are doubly commuting on
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}
, the projections
\displaystyle P_{1}
and
\displaystyle P_{2}
commute. As a consequence, the orthogonal projection onto
\displaystyle O_{1}(\mathcal{M})\cap O_{2}(\mathcal{M})
is given by
\displaystyle P_{1}P_{2}
, and the reproducing kernel of
\displaystyle O_{1}(\mathcal{M})\cap O_{2}(\mathcal{M})
is

P_{1}P_{2}k_{\lambda}^{\mathcal{M}}(z).

Furthermore, it is worth noting that our argument for why

O_{1}(\mathcal{M})\cap O_{2}(\mathcal{M})=\{0\}\Rightarrow\mathcal{M}=\{0\},

is actually not very different from the argument given in
[
4
]
. For this Mandrekar essentially refers to the wandering subspace theorem from
[
10
]
, and concludes that if
\displaystyle O_{1}(\mathcal{M})\cap O_{2}(\mathcal{M})=\{0\}
then
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}=\{0\}
, since clearly the wandering subspace
\displaystyle O_{1}(\mathcal{M})\cap O_{2}(\mathcal{M})
can’t generate anything else.

Note that the wandering subspace argument using reproducing kernels given above works exactly the same way in
\displaystyle n
variables, and Mandrekar’s argument for showing that the wandering subspace is one-dimensional also works in
\displaystyle n
variables, although in that case the argument will become slightly more technical. But since
[
10
]
only deals with a pair of doubly commuting isometries, Mandrekar’s original theorem only concerns functions of two complex variables. However, more recently the main results in
[
10
]
have been generalized by Sarkar in
[
8
]
to deal with an
\displaystyle n
-tuple of pairwise doubly commuting isometries, and thus Mandrekar’s original argument can be applied directly to show the corresponding theorem for
\displaystyle H^{2}(\mathbb{D}^{n})
. Though, it is worth pointing out that Mandrekar’s theorem on
\displaystyle H^{2}(\mathbb{D}^{n})
has already been proved by Seto in
[
9
]
by using the Wold decompositions from
[
10
]
in a slightly different way.

3.
Second proof

In this section we give another proof of the second direction of Mandrekar’s theorem, which is instead based on the proof idea of Beurling’s theorem provided in
[
2
]
.

We begin by proving a weaker version of the second direction of Mandrekar’s theorem, where we assume that the origin is not a common zero for all functions in
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}
. The exact statement we prove is as follows.

Theorem 2
.

If the shift operators
\displaystyle S_{1}
and
\displaystyle S_{2}
are
doubly commuting
on an invariant subspace
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}\neq\{0\}
of
\displaystyle H^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
, which has the additional property that it contains an element which does not vanish at the origin, then
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}
is of the form
\displaystyle\varphi H^{2}
for an inner function
\displaystyle\varphi
.

Proof.

As always we denote by
\displaystyle k_{\lambda}^{\mathcal{M}}(z)
the reproducing kernel of
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}
. Now, denote by
\displaystyle\varphi(z)
the reproducing kernel at the origin,
\displaystyle k_{0}^{\mathcal{M}}(z)
. Note that
\displaystyle k_{0}(z)\neq 0
since we assume that some function in
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}
does not vanish at the origin. We will show that
\displaystyle\varphi(z)
is inner by showing that all Fourier coefficients of
\displaystyle|\varphi(z)|^{2}
apart from the constant term are zero.

By the reproducing property of
\displaystyle\varphi

(6)

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}|\varphi(z)|^{2}z_{1}^{k}z_{2}^{n}|dz|=\langle z_{1}^{k}z_{2}^{n}\varphi(z),\varphi(z)\rangle=0

for all
\displaystyle(k,n)\neq(0,0)
with
\displaystyle k,n\geq 0
, and by taking complex conjugates, we also see that

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}|\varphi(z)|^{2}z_{1}^{k}z_{2}^{n}|dz|=0

when
\displaystyle(k,n)\neq(0,0)
, and
\displaystyle k,n\leq 0
.

If
\displaystyle(k,n)\neq(0,0)
with
\displaystyle k,n\geq 0
then

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}|\varphi(z)|^{2}z_{1}^{k}z_{2}^{-n}|dz|=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}(\varphi(z)z_{1}^{k})\overline{(\varphi(z)z_{2}^{n})}|dz|=\langle S_{1}^{k}\varphi(z),S_{2}^{n}\varphi(z)\rangle,

and since
\displaystyle S_{1}
and
\displaystyle S_{2}
are assumed to be doubly commuting

\langle S_{1}^{k}\varphi(z),S_{2}^{n}\varphi(z)\rangle=\langle\varphi(z),(S_{1}^{*})^{k}S_{2}^{n}\varphi(z)\rangle=\langle\varphi(z),S_{2}^{n}(S_{1}^{*})^{k}\varphi(z)\rangle=0,

where the last equality is again a consequence of the reproducing property of
\displaystyle\varphi
.

By using the same argument with
\displaystyle(-k,n)
instead of
\displaystyle(k,-n)
, we finally see that

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}|\varphi(z)|^{2}z_{1}^{k}z_{2}^{n}|dz|=0

for all
\displaystyle(k,n)\neq(0,0)
, which shows that
\displaystyle|\varphi(z)|
is constant almost everywhere on
\displaystyle\mathbb{T}^{2}
, and since
\displaystyle\varphi
is normalized, this means that
\displaystyle\varphi
is inner.

It remains to show that
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}=\varphi H^{2}(\mathbb{D}^{2})
.

Since
\displaystyle\varphi
has constant modulus on the boundary and since the polynomials are dense in
\displaystyle H^{2}(\mathbb{D}^{2})
, we have that

\varphi H^{2}(\mathbb{D}^{2})=\overline{\{\varphi(z)p(z):p(z)\in\mathbb{C}[z_{1},z_{2}]\}}\subset\mathcal{M}.

Now let
\displaystyle f\in\mathcal{M}
be orthogonal to all elements in
\displaystyle\{\varphi(z)p(z):p(z)\in\mathbb{C}[z_{1},z_{2}]\}
. We will show that
\displaystyle f=0
by showing that all the Fourier coefficients of
\displaystyle\varphi\overline{f}
are zero.

By the orthogonality assumption on
\displaystyle f

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\varphi(z)\overline{f(z)}z_{1}^{k}z_{2}^{n}|dz|=0,

for all
\displaystyle n,k\geq 0
. Furthermore, by again using the reproducing property of
\displaystyle\varphi
, and since
\displaystyle S_{1}
and
\displaystyle S_{2}
are doubly commuting on
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}
, we have that

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\varphi(z)\overline{f(z)}z_{1}^{k}z_{2}^{-n}|dz|=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\varphi(z)z_{1}^{k}\overline{f(z)z_{2}^{n}}|dz|\\
=\langle z_{1}^{k}\varphi(z),z_{2}^{n}f(z)\rangle=\langle\varphi(z),S_{2}^{n}(S_{1}^{*})^{k}f(z)\rangle=0,

for
\displaystyle n\geq 1
and
\displaystyle k\geq 0
. The same argument for
\displaystyle(-k,n)
instead of
\displaystyle(k,-n)
shows that

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\varphi(z)\overline{f(z)}z_{1}^{-k}z_{2}^{n}|dz|=0

when
\displaystyle n\geq 0
and
\displaystyle k\geq 1
.

It remains to show that

(7)

\langle\varphi,f(z)z_{1}^{k}z_{2}^{n}\rangle=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\varphi(z)\overline{f(z)}z_{1}^{-k}z_{2}^{-n}|dz|=0

for
\displaystyle k,n\geq 1
.

But this is an immediate consequence of the reproducing property of
\displaystyle\varphi
since
\displaystyle f(z)z_{1}^{k}z_{2}^{n}
belong to
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}
and vanish at the origin for all
\displaystyle k,n\geq 1
.

It follows that all Fourier coefficients of
\displaystyle\varphi\overline{f}
vanish, and so
\displaystyle\varphi\overline{f}=0
. Since
\displaystyle|\varphi|=1
a.e. on
\displaystyle\mathbb{T}^{2}
, this means that
\displaystyle f=0
a.e. on
\displaystyle\mathbb{T}^{2}
, and thus
\displaystyle f
is identically equal to zero.
∎

For functions of one variable, the general case — in which all function in
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}
might have a common zero at the origin — is easily reduced to the case above by, if necessary, simply factoring out
\displaystyle z^{k}
for some
\displaystyle k\geq 1
. For functions of several variables this is no longer possible since there is no canonical factor corresponding to zeros at the origin. Instead, we modify the argument given above as follows.

Second proof of the second implication of Theorem
1
.

We will show that if
\displaystyle S_{1}
and
\displaystyle S_{2}
are doubly commuting on
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}
, then
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}=\varphi H^{2}(\mathbb{D}^{2})
for some inner function
\displaystyle\varphi
.

If there is some
\displaystyle f\in\mathcal{M}
which does not vanish at the origin, we can just apply Theorem
2
, and then there is nothing more to be done.

Now suppose
\displaystyle d\geq 1
is the smallest integer such that all partial derivatives of total degree less than
\displaystyle d
of all functions
\displaystyle f\in\mathcal{M}
vanish at the origin. That is, for all
\displaystyle j_{1},j_{2}\in\mathbb{N}
with
\displaystyle j_{1}+j_{2}<d

(8)

\left(\frac{\partial^{j_{1}+j_{2}}f}{\partial z_{1}^{j_{1}}\partial z_{2}^{j_{2}}}\right)(0,0)=0

for all
\displaystyle f\in\mathcal{M}
. We may assume that
\displaystyle d<\infty
since otherwise
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}=\{0\}
.

Let
\displaystyle(d_{1},d_{2})\in\mathbb{N}^{2}
with
\displaystyle d_{1}+d_{2}=d
be any pair of integers such that

\left(\frac{\partial^{d_{1}+d_{2}}f}{\partial z_{1}^{d_{1}}\partial z_{2}^{d_{2}}}\right)(0,0)\neq 0

for some
\displaystyle f\in\mathcal{M}
.
Consider the bounded linear functional
\displaystyle E^{(d_{1},d_{2})}_{0}
on
\displaystyle H^{2}(\mathbb{D}^{2})
defined by

E^{(d_{1},d_{2})}_{0}:f\mapsto\left(\frac{\partial^{d_{1}+d_{2}}f}{\partial z_{1}^{d_{1}}\partial z_{2}^{d_{2}}}\right)(0,0).

That this functional is indeed bounded on
\displaystyle H^{2}(\mathbb{D}^{2})
is clear since it maps a function
\displaystyle f
to a fixed constant multiple of its Fourier coefficient with index
\displaystyle(d_{1},d_{2})
.

By the Riesz representation theorem there exists a unique function, which we will denote by
\displaystyle k^{(d_{1},d_{2})}_{0}
, such that

E^{(d_{1},d_{2})}_{0}(f)=\left\langle f,k^{(d_{1},d_{2})}_{0}\right\rangle

for all
\displaystyle f\in H^{2}(\mathbb{D}^{2})
. This is kind of a reproducing kernel at the origin, only it gives the value for the
\displaystyle(d_{1},d_{2})
:th partial derivative instead of for the function.

Just as for the ordinary reproducing kernel, we have that
\displaystyle P^{\mathcal{M}}k^{(d_{1},d_{2})}_{0}\in\mathcal{M}
is the unique function in
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}
such that

E^{(d_{1},d_{2})}_{0}(f)=\left\langle f,P^{\mathcal{M}}k^{(d_{1},d_{2})}_{0}\right\rangle

for all
\displaystyle f\in\mathcal{M}
. From now on we will denote the normalized function
\displaystyle P^{\mathcal{M}}k^{(d_{1},d_{2})}_{0}/\|P^{\mathcal{M}}k^{(d_{1},d_{2})}_{0}\|
by
\displaystyle\varphi
.

Since
\displaystyle\varphi
reproduces a rescaling of the
\displaystyle(d_{1},d_{2})
:th partial derivative at the origin and since equation (
8
) holds for all
\displaystyle f\in\mathcal{M}
, we have that

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}|\varphi(z)|^{2}z_{1}^{k}z_{2}^{n}|dz|=\langle z_{1}^{k}z_{2}^{n}\varphi(z),\varphi(z)\rangle=0,

for all
\displaystyle(n,k)\neq(0,0),n,k\in\mathbb{N}
. That is
\displaystyle\varphi
satisfies equation (
6
) from the proof of Theorem
2
.

In fact, as a consequence of the reproducing property of
\displaystyle\varphi
and the fact that (
8
) holds for all
\displaystyle f\in\mathcal{M}
, we have that

(9)

\langle\varphi,f(z)z_{1}^{n}z_{2}^{k}\rangle=0

for all
\displaystyle f\in\mathcal{M}
and all
\displaystyle n,k\in\mathbb{N}
with
\displaystyle(n,k)\neq(0,0)
. To see that (
9
) holds, note that when we evaluate the terms of the partial derivatives of
\displaystyle f(z)z_{1}^{n}z_{2}^{k}

at the origin, either they will vanish because of a monomial factor
\displaystyle z_{1}^{l}z_{2}^{m}
still being left after differentiation, or they will vanish because of (
8
).

Now in order to finish the proof we can just use the proof of Theorem
2
from equation (
6
) verbatim, if we just replace any reference to "the reproducing property of
\displaystyle\varphi
" with a reference to equation (
9
).

∎

The function
\displaystyle\varphi
used above can be obtained as the unique minimizer of a suitable extremal problem. When obtained in this way, one instead shows that equation (
6
) holds through a variational argument. This is of course more complicated than the argument given above, but in the one variable setting this approach has been successfully applied to extend results whose usual proofs rely heavily on Hilbert space techniques to Banach spaces like
\displaystyle H^{p}(\mathbb{D})
and
\displaystyle A^{p}(\mathbb{D})
. In this specific context though it might be worth to point out that Mandrekar’s theorem for
\displaystyle H^{p}(\mathbb{D}^{2})
for
\displaystyle p\geq 1
has already been proved by Redett in
[
5
]
. The argument used in that article is a modification of the idea of considering the intersection of the invariant subspace with
\displaystyle H^{2}(\mathbb{D}^{2})
, applying Mandrekar’s theorem, and using a density argument.
