Swarm Synergy: A Silent and Anonymous Way of Forming Community
Sweksha Jain, Rugved Katole, Leena Vachhani

Abstract
In this paper, we present a novel swarm algorithm, swarm synergy, designed for robots to form communities within a swarm autonomously and anonymously. These communities, characterized as clusters of robots, emerge without any (pre-defined or communicated) specific locations. Each robot operates as a silent agent, having no communication capability, making independent decisions based on local parameters. The proposed algorithm allows silent robots to achieve this self-organized swarm behavior using only sensory inputs from the environment. The robots intend to form a community by sensing the neighbors, creating synergy in a bounded environment. We further infer the behavior of swarm synergy to ensure the anonymity/untraceability of both robots and communities and show the results on dynamicity of various parameters relevant to swarm communities such as community size, community location, number of community, no specific agent structure in the community, etc. The results are further analysed to observe the effect of sensing limitations posed by the onboard sensor’s field of view. Simulations and experiments are performed to showcase the algorithm’s scalability, robustness, and fast convergence. Compared to the state-of-art with similar objectives, the proposed communication-free swarm synergy shows comparative time to synergize or form communities. The proposed algorithm finds applications in studying crowd dynamics under high-stress scenarios such as fire, attacks, or disasters.

Introduction

The swarm robotics paradigm mostly draws inspiration from collective behaviors observed in various biological organisms, such as honey bees, ants, birds, and fishes
Brambilla et al. 2013
. The collective behaviors are primarily clustering
Tan and Slotine 2013
, aggregation
Shah and Vachhani 2019
, flocking
Moeslinger et al. 2009
, collaborative search
Yang et al. 2019
, pattern formation
Sahin et al. 2002
, and foraging
Zhang and Pan 2022
.
These swarm behaviors are typically seen in miniature biological organisms and are beneficial with small/mini robots. These robots like Kilobots
Rubenstein et al. 2012
and SWARM-BOT
Sahin et al. 2002
have limited computational power, memory, and sensing capability with little or no environmental knowledge.

The proposed work is motivated by a specific behavior of the human crowd dynamics under high-stress scenarios, such as fires, attacks, or disasters. The chaotic crowd under stress conditions typically forms clusters/communities. Unlike clustering, these communities are not predefined (neither size nor members nor respective locations).
Furthermore, some intriguing questions investigated in this work are: Can the behavior be mimicked where each robot, with the intention of being part of a community, senses the neighbors’ presence and eventually forms communitie(s) even without inter-robot communication? In contrast to clustering, can this evolving behavior lead to community formation anonymously without the need for similarities, predefined community counts, sizes, or specific spatial arrangements?
In particular, the work focuses on the self-organized sensing-based behavior of robots forming communities or groups, where each robot in the swarm intends to be a member of a community silently (or without establishing communication) and anonymously. Each robot independently having the intention of being a member of the community eventually synergizes the swarm to form communiti(es).

The simplest collective behavior in literature is a spatial organization or aggregation: a group of robots spatially close to each other. The aggregation has been based on either cues in the environment
Baldassarre et al. 2003
;
Arvin et al. 2011
or self-organized
Correll and Martinoli 2011
;
Bakhshipour et al. 2017
. In cue-based aggregation
Baldassarre et al. 2003
, the robots aggregate near cues such as light, heat, or sound sources. Self-organized aggregation considers the interaction of robots with the environment and each other to achieve swarm aggregation
Correll and Martinoli 2011
. It is interesting to explore a swarm behavior when the interactions are purely based on limited Field-of-View (FoV) sensing of each robot and without having explicit objectives related to environment parameters/cues.

In another collective behavior, multiple aggregates or clusters are formed at different spatial locations. The clusters are created based on similarity, aiming to optimize the resources among robots. Clustering within the realm of swarm robotics has consistently drawn inspiration from the self-organized collective behaviors observed in natural biological swarms, particularly those of ants and termites
Bayındır 2016
. In the domain of swarm robotics, swarm clustering is predominantly categorized into two primary types: (i) Robot clustering and (ii) token clustering. In token clustering, robots create clusters of objects/tokens with similar features
Cruz et al. 2017
;
Aguzzi et al. 2023
, and this can involve both homogeneous and heterogeneous groups of robots. The clustering algorithms like k-means clustering and DBSCAN (density-based spatial clustering of applications with noise)
Anikin and Gazimov 2017
aim for spatial clustering, not necessarily aimed at robotic swarms. On the other hand, in robot clustering, robots must move from their initial positions to form clusters when the number of clusters is known. The robots form clusters based on the specified number of clusters and the positions of their respective centroids, typically involving homogeneous robots
McCune and Madey 2014
. Furthermore, the segregation
Kumar et al. 2010
;
Santos et al. 2020
of similar robots in a heterogeneous swarm form spatial patterns. However, clustering is purely based on intention, and local sensing.

Further, the methods to exhibit these collective behaviors by the swarm have been classified into three approaches
Brambilla et al. 2013
: Virtual Physics-based, Probabilistic, and Evolutionary approaches. The collective behavior can be exhibited by utilizing easily deployable (small-size) autonomous robots. In the virtual physics approach, the robots respond and sense the virtual forces driven by the laws of physics
Khaldi et al. 2018
. Due to these described forces, the robots experience an attractive force to reduce the distance between their position and the goal position, while repulsive force becomes dominant when any obstacle is sensed in the environment. The probabilistic approaches to the robot dynamics are described by Markov chains, where probabilities of joining or leaving the aggregate are calculated
Bayindir and Sahin 2009
. Evolutionary approaches include the methods where the collective behavior is evolved by selecting parameters using the process of artificial evolution
Yang et al. 2019
;
Santos and Egerstedt 2018
;
Hereford 2011
. Even though the proposed swarm synergy intends to be self-organized, the existing approaches based on optimal parameter selection can not be applied. Hence, we investigate an algorithmic way, as conveyed in Figure
1
, to exhibit synergy behavior. The proposed swarm synergy creates a novel framework to analyze swarm behaviors of wandering robots (homogeneous/heterogeneous).

Figure 1
:
Swarm synergy in the context of the existing literature

In the past, to achieve these collective behaviors, various communication methods have been applied to share intent and/or environment information. The robots use local communication to establish a connection with their neighboring robots
Bodi et al. 2012
, whereas global communication is used to establish a connection with any robot present in the environment. Using the communication protocols is not only susceptible to attacks by malicious agents
Gandhe and Otte 2022
but also network hijacking, data interception, spoofing, or security breaches
Kolias et al. 2011
. The current challenge is to achieve collective behavior without any exchange of global or local information.

The swarm synergy algorithm fosters self-organizing behavior even when the spatial locations and size of the formed communities are unknown. The trajectories of robots generated in each run are distinct, posing a challenge for tracing the path of each robot in the event of malicious actions. Consequently, the future cannot be estimated or generated using historical data. Hence, we show an inherent property of the swarm synergy, called untraceability. The contributions of this work are summarized as follows:

•

A novel swarm algorithm, swarm synergy, where each agent silently (without communicating) intends to form a community to enhance synergy. The self-organizing clustering/community-forming behavior is obtained without pre-defining community count, specific members in the community, and any spatial arrangement.

•

The features of formed communities are derived using various analytical, simulation, and experimental results. Parameters like sensor’s Field-of-View (FoV), sensing range accounted in effective sensing area, swarm-specific area (area per robot), and minimum community size are used for the analysis. Features like untraceability are validated using numerical, experimental, and statistical analyses like ANOVA tests.

The problem statement is formulated as follows:

Problem 2.1
.

Devise a communication-free swarm algorithm for a specified environment and a swarm of independent robots capable of sensing the relative position of nearby members with the objective of evolving the synergistic formation of communities among robots by leveraging sensing abilities of the robots.

3
Swarm Synergy Algorithm

The swarm synergy approach aims to develop a methodology independently applied to each robot and achieves a self-organizing swarm behavior. The robot forms a community when it is close enough to its neighbors. Given the minimum community size
M
, the community is formed when each robot has number of neighbors equal to or more than
M-1
. Each swarm robot is in one of the following three distinct states:

S1

already a member of a community.

S2

is in the process of forming a new community.

S3

wandering and seeking to be in either S1 or S2.

The key concept of community formation can be achieved by assigning a common goal to a set of robot and navigate these robots to the goal location without colliding with other robots. However, in the absence of global location and inter-robot communication, assigning a common goal is not possible.
We identify seminal tasks executed by each robot continuously striving to reach state S1 independent of other robots. These tasks are (i)
Neighbor Detection
, (ii)
Navigation
, and (iii)
Goal Assignment
. The algorithm pipeline is explained using Figure
3
.

Figure 3
:
Proposed Swarm Synergy with Algorithm Pipeline for Each Robot

The proposed algorithm is designed to allow each robot not to stop in state S2 or S3 and stop only if it perceives to be in a community. The community is formed when the robots in the neighborhood are close enough. Instead of describing the maximum distance between robots in a community, we describe the closeness by reaching to the goal identified by the locations of neighbor robots. Since goal assignment of each robot is allowing each robot to navigate to either detect neighbors or move closer to its neighbors. Each robot is allowed to achieve the minimum distance
D_{g}
with respect to the position of its assigned goal. In particular, the condition of
community formed
is to satisfy both 1) number of neighbors at least
M
and 2) Distance to the goal is less than or equal to
D_{g}
. The parameter,
D_{g}
, also avoids any infinite looping between tasks of inter-robot collision avoidance and reaching the goal position. More behavioural benefits related to parameter
D_{g}
are discussed in description of
Goal Assignment
task.

3.1
Neighbor Detection

After initialization, the swarm starts to detect its neighbors instantly. This task helps each robot to form its neighbor set. A robot is detected in other robots’ FoV via an embedded range sensor.
All the following conditions (in line with the range sensing) should be satisfied for the robot

•

Distance between two robots/agents is less than the sensing range of the robot, i.e.,

•

The bearing of the robot

•

Since the objective is to develop an independent motion strategy for each robot based on local sensing, the proposed approach uses relative information to avoid global robot localization. Each robot

\displaystyle x_{j}=d_{ij}cos(\delta_{ij})

(2)

\displaystyle y_{j}=d_{ij}sin(\delta_{ij})

Hence, the relative location of each robot

3.2
Navigation

The navigation controller commands the robot to navigate to the goal position, avoiding collision with other robots within the environment boundaries. The underlying objective of each robot is to reach the goal safely. Each robot uses a navigation controller to assign linear and angular velocities, commanding it to move toward its goal position while avoiding the inter-robot collision. This can be achieved by implementing any navigation controller that ensures collision-free motion to reach the goal. The collision avoidance scheme ensures a safe distance from other robots, maintaining a minimum inter-robot separation
D_{s}
. The function Navigation_Controller() takes coordinates of goal position as arguments and returns linear and angular velocities for navigation.

3.3
Goal Assignment

The most critical task of
Goal Assignment
is achieved by aiming not to rest (or stop the robot) until it is a member of a community.
The key contribution of goal assignment for each robot is based on the available sensor-based information on relative locations. To bring the robots closer to each other, we consider finding the centroid of neighbor set
N_{i}
for the robot
i
. The goal position is calculated using goal update rule given by:

\displaystyle x_{g_{i}}=\frac{(\Sigma_{j\in N_{i}}x_{j})}{|N_{i}|+1}

(3)

\displaystyle y_{g_{i}}=\frac{(\Sigma_{j\in N_{i}}y_{j})}{|N_{i}|+1}

where (
x_{g_{i}},y_{g_{i}}
) denotes the goal position for the robot
i
while (
x_{j},y_{j}
) are the position coordinates of robot
j
in neighbor set
N_{i}
.

The goal update rule given by (
3

In such a scenario, the neighbor set and the goal positions of all the robots in that community get updated again. Still, the robot in the already existing community would move only if its updated distance to the goal is more than the distance
D_{g}

The proposed approach assigns the goal location to the robot

The untraceability of the robots can also be verified statistically using the trajectories. We performed an Analysis of Variance (ANOVA)
Cuevas et al. 2004
test for all the simulations of each robot at a time to check whether the trajectories of the same robot are significantly related. The ANOVA test is a statistical test used to determine whether a statistically significant difference exists between the mean values of more than one group. The probability of rejecting the null hypothesis, or p-value, is considered at level 0.05. If the p-value is less than the predetermined level of 0.05, then the groups have significant differences. The p-value in every test conducted using all the trajectories, came equal to 0.00, concluding that the relationship is insignificant. It shows that the proposed algorithm forms swarm communities that are untraceable.

5
Conclusions and Future Work

This work explores a novel swarm algorithm incorporating silent (non-communicating) and anonymous agents. The proposed swarm synergy algorithm forms communitie(s) of the non-communicating agents in the swarm by detecting other agents in restricted FoV using only a range sensor, making independent decisions. The algorithm enables agents to achieve synergy using neither any identity nor localization nor parameters relevant to community formation such as number of communities, community size, and specific groups of agents in a community. Some compelling features of the swarm synergy strategy are investigated that show results on accommodating new members in the community, scalability, and swarm untraceability in number of communities being formed. The properties and features of the algorithm are supported by extensive numerical, statistical, and experimental analysis.

While this work silently and anonymously formed communitie(s), the possibilities of silent swarm aggregation, swarm formation, etc., will open up enormous applications in the underwater world where communication is challenging. The silent swarm applications can avoid communication related hacking issues. Future work will include extending this work to real-world scenarios with obstacles. The swarm size is very important in getting lesser ST for a larger area. The improvement in reducing this dependency can be another interesting direction. The improvement in synergy time reduction for single community formation is another direction to be focused in future.
