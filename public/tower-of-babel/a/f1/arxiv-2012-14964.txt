Control Barriers in Bayesian Learning of System Dynamics
Vikas Dhiman, Mohammad Javad Khojasteh, Massimo Franceschetti, and Nikolay Atanasov

Abstract
This paper focuses on learning a model of system dynamics online while satisfying safety constraints. Our objective is to avoid offline system identification or hand-specified models and allow a system to safely and autonomously estimate and adapt its own model during operation. Given streaming observations of the system state, we use Bayesian learning to obtain a distribution over the system dynamics. Specifically, we propose a new matrix variate Gaussian process (MVGP) regression approach with an efficient covariance factorization to learn the drift and input gain terms of a nonlinear control-affine system. The MVGP distribution is then used to optimize the system behavior and ensure safety with high probability, by specifying control Lyapunov function (CLF) and control barrier function (CBF) chance constraints. We show that a safe control policy can be synthesized for systems with arbitrary relative degree and probabilistic CLF-CBF constraints by solving a second order cone program (SOCP). Finally, we extend our design to a self-triggering formulation, adaptively determining the time at which a new control input needs to be applied in order to guarantee safety.

We generalize the CBF control synthesis techniques to handle probabilistic safety constraints induced by the dynamics distribution. Our work makes the following
contributions

First, we develop a Matrix Variate Gaussian Process (MVGP) regression to estimate the unknown system dynamics and formulate probabilistic safety and stability constraints for systems with arbitrary relative degree. III-B

Stability and Safety with Known Dynamics

We first review key results
[
25
]
on control Lyapunov functions for enforcing stability and control barrier functions for enforcing safety of control-affine systems with
known dynamics
. System stability may be asserted as follows.

Definition 1
.

A function
V\in\mathbb{C}^{1}({\cal X},\mathbb{R})
is a
control Lyapunov function
(CLF) for the system in (
1
) if it is positive definite,
V(\mathbf{x})>0
,
\forall\mathbf{x}\in{\cal X}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}
,
V(\mathbf{0})=0
, and satisfies:

\inf_{\mathbf{u}\in{\cal U}}\mbox{CLC}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u})\leq 0,\quad\forall\mathbf{x}\in{\cal X},

(2)

where
\mbox{CLC}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u})\triangleq\mathcal{L}_{f}V(\mathbf{x})+\mathcal{L}_{g}V(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{u}+\gamma(V(\mathbf{x}))
is a
control Lyapunov condition
(CLC) defined for some class
K
function
\gamma
.

Proposition 1

(Sufficient Condition for Stability
[
25
]
)
.

If there exists a CLF
V(\mathbf{x})
for system (
1
), then any Lipschitz continuous control policy
\pi(\mathbf{x})\in\left\{\mathbf{u}\in{\cal U}\mid\mbox{CLC}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u})\leq 0\right\}
asymptotically stabilizes the system.

Let
{\cal C}\triangleq\left\{\mathbf{x}\in{\cal X}\mid h(\mathbf{x})\geq 0\right\}
be a
safe set
of system states, defined implicitly by a function
h\in\mathbb{C}^{1}({\cal X},\mathbb{R})
. System (
1
) is
safe
with respect to
{\cal C}
if
{\cal C}
is
forward invariant
, i.e., for any
\mathbf{x}(0)\in{\cal C}
,
\mathbf{x}(t)
remains in
{\cal C}
for all
t\geq 0
. System safety may be asserted as follows.

Definition 2
.

A function
h\in\mathbb{C}^{1}({\cal X},\mathbb{R})
is a
control barrier function
(CBF) for the system in (
1
) if

\sup_{\mathbf{u}\in{\cal U}}\mbox{CBC}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u})\geq 0,\quad\forall\mathbf{x}\in{\cal X},

(3)

where
\mbox{CBC}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u})\triangleq\mathcal{L}_{f}h(\mathbf{x})+\mathcal{L}_{g}h(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{u}+\alpha(h(\mathbf{x}))
is a
control barrier condition
(CBC) defined for some extended class
K_{\infty}
function
\alpha
.

Proposition 2

(Sufficient Condition for Safety
[
25
]
)
.

Consider a set
{\cal C}
defined implicitly by
h\in\mathbb{C}^{1}({\cal X},\mathbb{R})
. If
h
is a CBF and
\nabla h(\mathbf{x})\neq 0
for all
\mathbf{x}
when
h(\mathbf{x})=0
, then any Lipschitz continuous control policy
\pi(\mathbf{x})\in\left\{\mathbf{u}\in{\cal U}\mid\mbox{CBC}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u})\geq 0\right\}
renders the system in (
1
) safe with respect to
{\cal C}
.

Prop.
1
and Prop.
2
provide sufficient conditions for a control policy
\pi(\mathbf{x})
applied to the system in (
1
) to guarantee stability and safety. Note that the conditions are defined by affine constraints in
\mathbf{u}
. This allows the formulation of control synthesis as a quadratic program (QP) in which stability and safety properties are captured by the linear CLC and CBC constraints, respectively:

\displaystyle\pi(\mathbf{x})\in

\displaystyle\argmin_{\mathbf{u}\in{\cal U},\delta\in\mathbb{R}}

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{u}\|^{2}+\lambda\delta^{2}

(4)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle\mbox{CLC}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u})\leq\delta,\;\mbox{CBC}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u})\geq 0,

where
\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{x})\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
is a matrix penalizing control effort and
\delta
is a slack variable that ensures feasibility of the QP by giving preference to safety over stability, controlled by the scaling factor
\lambda>0
. If a stabilizing control policy
\hat{\pi}(\mathbf{x})
is already available, it may be modified minimally online to ensure safety:

\displaystyle\pi(\mathbf{x})\in

\displaystyle\argmin_{\mathbf{u}\in{\cal U}}

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{x})\left(\mathbf{u}-\hat{\pi}(\mathbf{x})\right)\|^{2}

(5)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle\mbox{CBC}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u})\geq 0.

In practice, the QPs above cannot be solved infinitely fast. Optimization is typically performed at triggering times
t_{k},t_{k+1},\ldots
, providing control input
\mathbf{u}_{k}\triangleq\pi(\mathbf{x}_{k})
when the system state is
\mathbf{x}_{k}\triangleq\mathbf{x}(t_{k})
. Ames et al.
[
21
, Thm. 3]
show that if
f
,
g

are locally Lipschitz, then
\pi(\mathbf{x})
and
\mbox{CBC}(\mathbf{x},\pi(\mathbf{x}))
are locally Lipschitz. Thus, for sufficiently small inter-triggering times
\tau_{k}\triangleq t_{k+1}-t_{k}
, solving (
5
) at
\{t_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
ensures safety during the inter-triggering intervals
[t_{k},t_{k+1})
as well.

IV
Matrix Variate Gaussian Process Regression of System Dynamics

This section presents our novel Matrix Variate Gaussian Process (MVGP) solution to Problem
1
. We aim to estimate the unknown drift
f
and input gain
g
of system (
1
) using a state-control dataset
(\mathbf{X}_{1:k},\mathbf{U}_{1:k},\dot{\mathbf{X}}_{1:k})
. To simplify the derivation, we assume that
\mathbf{X}_{1:k}
and
\mathbf{U}_{1:k}
are observed without noise, butt each measurement
\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t_{k})
in
\dot{\mathbf{X}}_{1:k}
is corrupted by zero-mean Gaussian noise
{\cal N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{S})
that is independent across time steps. Treating
f(\mathbf{x})+g(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{u}
as a single vector-valued function with input
[\mathbf{x}^{\top},\mathbf{u}^{\top}]^{\top}
may seem natural from a function approximation perspective, but this approach ignores the control-affine structure. Encoding this structure in the learning process not only increases the learning efficiency, but also ensures that the control Lyapunov and control barrier conditions remain linear in
\mathbf{u}
. Hence, we focus on learning the matrix-valued function
F(\mathbf{x})=[f(\mathbf{x})\;g(\mathbf{x})]
defined in (
1
).

The simplest approach is to use
n(1+m)
decoupled GPs for each element of
F(\mathbf{x})
. This approach ignores the dependencies among the components of
f(\mathbf{x})
and
g(\mathbf{x})
. Furthermore, since the outputs of
f(\mathbf{x})
and
g(\mathbf{x})
are observed together via
\dot{\mathbf{X}}_{1:k}
, training data dimensions are still mutually correlated and cannot be treated as decoupled GPs denying the efficiency advantage. At the other extreme, treating
\vect(F(\mathbf{x}))
as a single vector-valued function and using a single GP distribution will enable high estimation accuracy but specifying an effective matrix-valued kernel function
\mathbf{K}_{0}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})
and optimizing its hyperparameters is challenging. A promising approach is offered by the Coregionalization GP (CoGP) model
[
65
]
, where the kernel function is decomposed as
\mathbf{K}_{0}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\triangleq\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\kappa_{0}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})
into a scalar kernel
\kappa_{0}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})
and a covariance matrix parameter
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\in\mathbb{R}^{n(1+m)\times(1+m)n}
. Estimating
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
may still require a lot of training data. Moreover, the matrix-times-scalar-kernel structure is not preserved in the posterior of the Coregionalization model, preventing its effective use for incremental learning when new data is received over time.

We propose an alternative factorization of
\mathbf{K}_{0}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})
inspired by the Matrix Variate Gaussian distribution
[
66
,
67
]
. The strengths of our factorization are that it models the correlation among the elements of
F(\mathbf{x})
, preserves its structure in the posterior GP distribution, and has similar training and testing complexity as the decoupled GP approach.

Definition 3
.

The Matrix Variate Gaussian (MVG) distribution is a three-parameter distribution
{\cal M}{\cal N}(\mathbf{M},\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B})
describing a random matrix
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
with probability density function:

\displaystyle{p(\mathbf{X};\mathbf{M},\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B})\triangleq\frac{\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\tr\left[\mathbf{B}^{-1}(\mathbf{X}-\mathbf{M})^{\top}\mathbf{A}^{-1}(\mathbf{X}-\mathbf{M})\right]\right)}{(2\pi)^{nm/2}\det(\mathbf{A})^{m/2}\det(\mathbf{B})^{n/2}}}

where
\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
is the mean. Up to a scaling factor, each column of
\mathbf{X}
has the same covariance
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, and each row has the same covariance
\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
.

Additional properties of the MVG distribution are provided in Appendix
A
. Note that if
\mathbf{X}\sim{\cal M}{\cal N}(\mathbf{M},\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B})
, then
\vect(\mathbf{X})\sim\mathcal{N}(\vect(\mathbf{M}),\mathbf{B}\otimes\mathbf{A})
. Based on this observation, we propose the following decomposition of the GP prior:

\vect(F(\mathbf{x}))\sim\mathcal{GP}(\vect(\mathbf{M}_{0}(\mathbf{x})),\mathbf{B}_{0}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\otimes\mathbf{A}),

(7)

where
\mathbf{B}_{0}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\in\mathbb{R}^{(m+1)\times(m+1)}
and
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
. We call such a process
F(\mathbf{x})
with kernel structure defined in (
7
) a Matrix Variate Gaussian Process (MVGP).

f(\mathbf{x})
and the columns of

We assume that the measurement noise covariance matrix satisfies
\mathbf{S}=\sigma^{2}\mathbf{A}

To simplify notation, let
\boldsymbol{{\cal B}}_{1:k}^{1:k}\in\mathbb{R}^{k(m+1)\times k(m+1)}
be a matrix with
k\times k

\left[\boldsymbol{{\cal B}}_{1:k}^{1:k}\right]_{i,j}\triangleq\mathbf{B}_{0}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{j})

\boldsymbol{{\cal M}}_{1:k}\triangleq\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{M}_{0}(\mathbf{x}_{1})&\cdots&\mathbf{M}_{0}(\mathbf{x}_{k})\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times k(m+1)}
,
\boldsymbol{{\cal B}}_{1:k}(\mathbf{x})\triangleq\left[\mathbf{B}_{0}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}_{1}),\dots,\mathbf{B}_{0}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}_{k})\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{(m+1)\times k(m+1)}
and
\underline{\boldsymbol{{\cal U}}}_{1:k}\triangleq\diag(\underline{\mathbf{\mathbf{u}}}_{1},\dots,\underline{\mathbf{\mathbf{u}}}_{k})\in\mathbb{R}^{k(m+1)\times k}
. Consider an arbitrary test point
\mathbf{x}_{*}
. The train and test data are jointly Gaussian:

\begin{bmatrix}\vect(\dot{\mathbf{X}}_{1:k})\\
\vect(F(\mathbf{x}_{*}))\end{bmatrix}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\begin{bmatrix}\vect(\boldsymbol{{\cal M}}_{1:k}\underline{\boldsymbol{{\cal U}}}_{1:k})\\
\vect(\mathbf{M}_{0}(\mathbf{x}_{*}))\end{bmatrix},\right.\\

Prop.

A key property of the MVGP model in Prop.

is that the posterior preserves the kernel decomposition. In the special case of,
\mathbf{B}_{0}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\triangleq\mathbf{B}\kappa_{0}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})
, in addition to the kernel parameters, our model requires learning of
\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{(1+m)\times(1+m)}
and, if the measurement noise statistics are unknown,
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
and
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}
. Thus, the MVGP model has fewer parameters than a CoGP model, which requires
(1+m)^{2}n^{2}
parameters for the full covariance decomposition
\mathbf{K}_{0}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\triangleq\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\kappa_{0}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})
. For a data set with
k
examples, the training computational complexity of our MVGP approach is
O(k^{3})
, while the same for the CoGP model is
O(k^{3}n^{3})
. This is because our model inverts only a
(k\times k)
matrix while the CoGP model inverts a
(kn\times kn)
matrix (see Appendix
C
). Fig.
1
shows a comparison of the covariances obtained from an MVGP model (with
\mathbf{B}_{0}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})=\mathbf{B}\kappa_{0}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})
) and from a CoGP model. The shapes of the MVGP covriances for different columns of
F(\mathbf{x})
are the same by construction, while those of CoGP model may be arbitrary. Thus, the CoGP model is more expressive than our MVGP model. However, MVGP model offers considerable computational advantages especially for high-dimensional systems without significant loss in accuracy. The accuracy and computational complexity of the MVGP and CoGP models are compared in Sec.
VIII-A

V
Self-triggered Control with Probabilistic Safety Constraints

The MVGP model developed in Sec.
IV
addresses Problem
1
and provides a probabilistic estimate of the unknown system dynamics
F(\mathbf{x})\underline{\mathbf{u}}
in (
11
). In this section, we consider Problem
2
. We extend the optimization-based control synthesis approach in (
4
) to handle probabilistic stability and safety constraints induced by the posterior distribution of
F(\mathbf{x})\underline{\mathbf{u}}
. We focus on handling probabilistic safety constraints of the form
\mathbb{P}(\mbox{CBC}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u})\geq 0)\geq p
. Since the control Lyapunov and control barrier conditions have the same form, involving the Lie derivative of a known function
V(\mathbf{x})
or
h(\mathbf{x})
with respect to the system dynamics, our approach can be used for stability constraints as well, as demonstrated in Sec.
VIII-B
. Furthermore, we develop self-triggering conditions to adaptively decide the times
t_{k}
,
t_{k+1}
,
\ldots
, at which the system inputs
\mathbf{u}
should be recomputed in order to guarantee safety with high probability along the continuous-time system trajectory, instead of instantaneously in time.

Our approach requires the stochastic system dynamics
F(\mathbf{x})\underline{\mathbf{u}}
to be locally Lipschitz continuous with high probability.
This is necessary to ensure that for any MVGP sample
\hat{F}(\mathbf{x})
, the ordinary differential equation
\dot{\mathbf{x}}=\hat{F}(\mathbf{x})\underline{\mathbf{u}}
in (
1
) has a unique solution
[
68
, Thm. 3.20]
. Without such an assumption, the system would be able to instantaneously move from a safe state with a large safety margin to an unsafe state, making it impossible to guarantee safety for any time duration after a control triggering instant.
The next Lemma shows constructively how to compute a Lipschitz constant
L_{\mathbf{f}}
which holds with a user-specified probability
1-\delta_{L}
for a vector-valued GP model with kernel whose fourth-order partial derivatives are Lipschtz continuous. The proof follows, with minor modifications, the argument in
[
69
, Thm 3.2]
.

Lemma 1
.

Consider the vector-valued function
\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x})\triangleq F(\mathbf{x})\underline{\mathbf{u}}
in (
11
) with distribution
\mathcal{GP}(\boldsymbol{\mu}(\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{A}\kappa_{\mathbf{B}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}))
, where
\boldsymbol{\mu}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{M}_{k}(\mathbf{x})\underline{\mathbf{u}}
and
\kappa_{\mathbf{B}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})=\underline{\mathbf{u}}^{\top}\mathbf{B}_{k}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\underline{\mathbf{u}}
. Suppose that
\kappa_{\mathbf{B}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})
has continuous partial derivatives of fourth order. For
j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, let
\kappa^{\partial j}_{\mathbf{B}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\triangleq\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\mathbf{x}_{j}\partial\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{j}}\kappa_{\mathbf{B}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})
with Lipschitz constant
L_{\kappa}^{\partial j}
over a compact set
{\cal X}
with maximal extension
r=\max_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in{\cal X}}\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\|
. Then, each element of the Jacobian of
\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x})
is bounded with probability of at least
1-\frac{\delta_{L}}{n^{2}}
:

\left|\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in{\cal X}}\frac{\partial\mathbf{f}_{i}(\mathbf{x})}{\partial\mathbf{x}_{j}}\right|\leq L_{i,\partial j},\;\;\forall i,j\in\{1,\dots,n\},

(12)

where

\displaystyle{L_{i,\partial j}\triangleq\sqrt{2\log\left(\frac{2n^{2}}{\delta_{L}}\right)}\kappa^{i,\partial j}_{{\cal X}}+12\sqrt{6n}\max\left\{\kappa^{i,\partial j}_{{\cal X}},\sqrt{r\mathbf{A}_{ii}L_{\kappa}^{\partial j}}\right\}}

and
\kappa^{i,\partial j}_{{\cal X}}\triangleq\max_{\mathbf{x}\in{\cal X}}\sqrt{\mathbf{A}_{ii}\kappa^{\partial j}_{\mathbf{B}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})}
. Furthermore, a sample function
\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x})
of the given GP is almost surely continuous on
{\cal X}
and with probability of at least
1-\delta_{L}
,
L_{\mathbf{f}}\triangleq\sqrt{\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}L_{i,\partial j}^{2}}
is a Lipschitz constant of
\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x})
on
{\cal X}
.

Proof.

See Appendix
D
.
∎

The assumptions of Lemma
1
are mild and reasonable. The assumption about kernel continuity can be satisfied by an appropriate choice of a kernel function. For example, commonly used radial basis functions are infinitely differentiable and continuous. The assumption that the space
{\cal X}
is bounded is reasonable for physical systems. For example, the region that an Ackermann vehicle can cover can be bounded based on the maximum velocity and acceleration of the vehicle.

Probabilistic Safety Constraints

Consider probabilistic stability and safety constraints in the CLF-CBF QP in (
4
), induced by the MVGP distribution of
F(\mathbf{x})\underline{\mathbf{u}}
at time
t_{k}
in (

):

\displaystyle\pi(\mathbf{x}_{k})

\displaystyle\in\argmin_{\mathbf{u}_{k}\in{\cal U},\delta\in\mathbb{R}}\|\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{x}_{k})\mathbf{u}_{k}\|^{2}+\lambda\delta^{2}

(13)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mbox{CLC}(\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{u}_{k})\leq\delta\mid\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{u}_{k})\geq\tilde{p}_{k}

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mbox{CBC}(\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{u}_{k})\geq\zeta\mid\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{u}_{k})\geq\tilde{p}_{k},

where
\tilde{p}_{k}\triangleq p_{k}/(1-\delta_{L})

Remark 1
.

Given a desired safety probability
p_{k}
for the interval
[t_{k},t_{k}+\tau_{k})
, defined in (
6
), we choose the Lipschitz continuity probability
1-\delta_{L}
in Lemma
1
to ensure instantaneous safety at time
t_{k}
with higher probability
\tilde{p}_{k}\in[0.5,1)
. The choice of
\delta_{L}
affects the Lipschitz constant
L_{\mathbf{f}}
and in turn the duration
\tau_{k}
, as discussed in Sec.
V-B
. By ensuring safety at time instant
t_{k}
with probability at least
\tilde{p}_{k}
, we guarantee safety with probability at least
p_{k}=\tilde{p}_{k}(1-\delta_{L})
during the interval
[t_{k},t_{k}+\tau_{k})
(see Proposition
5

2
for further discussion on feasibility.

\bullet

To ensure that the safety constraint does not only hold instantaneously at
t_{k}
but over an interval
[t_{k},t_{k}+\tau_{k})
, we enforce a tighter constraint via
\zeta>0
and determine the time
\tau_{k}
for which it remains valid. The choice of
\zeta
and its effect on
\tau_{k}
are discussed next. First, we obtain the mean and variance of the
CBC
constraint, which is an affine transformation of the system dynamics.

Lemma 2
.

Consider the dynamics in (
1
) with posterior distribution in (
11
). Given
\mathbf{x}_{k}
and
\mathbf{u}_{k}
,
\mbox{CBC}_{k}\triangleq\mbox{CBC}(\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{u}_{k})
is a Gaussian random variable with the following parameters:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mbox{CBC}_{k}]

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}h(\mathbf{x}_{k})^{\top}\mathbf{M}_{k}(\mathbf{x}_{k})\underline{\mathbf{u}}_{k}+\alpha(h(\mathbf{x}_{k})),

(14)

\displaystyle\Var[\mbox{CBC}_{k}]

\displaystyle=(\underline{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{B}_{k}(\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{x}_{k})\underline{\mathbf{u}}_{k})(\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}h(\mathbf{x}_{k})^{\top}\mathbf{A}\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}h(\mathbf{x}_{k})).

Proof.

We start by rewriting the definition of CBC as:

\displaystyle\mbox{CBC}_{k}

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}h(\mathbf{x}_{k})^{\top}F(\mathbf{x}_{k})\underline{\mathbf{\mathbf{u}}}_{k}+\alpha(h(\mathbf{x}_{k})).

(15)

Then, the mean and variance of
\mbox{CBC}_{k}
are:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mbox{CBC}_{k}]

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}h(\mathbf{x}_{k})^{\top}\mathbb{E}[F(\mathbf{x}_{k})\underline{\mathbf{u}}_{k}]+\alpha(h(\mathbf{x}_{k})),

(16)

\displaystyle\Var[\mbox{CBC}_{k}]

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}h(\mathbf{x}_{k})^{\top}\Var[F(\mathbf{x}_{k})\underline{\mathbf{\mathbf{u}}}_{k}]\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}h(\mathbf{x}_{k}),

where the mean and variance of
F(\mathbf{x}_{k})\underline{\mathbf{\mathbf{u}}}_{k}
are from (
11
).
∎

Using Lemma
2
, we can rewrite the safety constraint as

\mathbb{P}(\mbox{CBC}_{k}\geq\zeta|\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{u}_{k})=1-\Phi\left(\frac{\zeta-\mathbb{E}[\mbox{CBC}_{k}]}{\sqrt{\Var[\mbox{CBC}_{k}]}}\right)\geq\tilde{p}_{k},

(17)

where
\Phi(\cdot)
is the cumulative distribution function of the standard Gaussian.
Note that if the control input is chosen so that
\frac{\zeta-\mathbb{E}[\mbox{\tiny{CBC}}_{k}]}{\sqrt{\Var[\mbox{\tiny{CBC}}_{k}]}}\to-\infty
, as the posterior variance of
\mbox{CBC}_{k}
tends to zero, the probability
\mathbb{P}(\mbox{CBC}_{k}\geq\zeta|\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{u}_{k})
tends to one.
Namely, as the uncertainty about the system dynamics tends to zero, our results reduce to the setting of Sec.
III-B
, and safety can be ensured with probability one. Using (
17
) and noting that
\Phi^{-1}(1-\tilde{p}_{k})=-\sqrt{2}\mbox{erf}^{-1}(2\tilde{p}_{k}-1)
, where
\mbox{erf}(\cdot)
is the Gauss error function, the optimization problem in (
13

\displaystyle\pi(\mathbf{x}_{k})

\displaystyle\in\argmin_{\mathbf{u}_{k}\in{\cal U},\delta\in\mathbb{R}}\;\|\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{x}_{k})\mathbf{u}_{k}\|^{2}+\lambda\delta^{2}

(18)

\displaystyle\text{s.t. }

\displaystyle\delta-\mathbb{E}[\mbox{CLC}_{k}]\geq c(\tilde{p}_{k})\sqrt{\Var[\mbox{CLC}_{k}]}

\displaystyle\zeta-\mathbb{E}[\mbox{CBC}_{k}]\leq-c(\tilde{p}_{k})\sqrt{\Var[\mbox{CBC}_{k}]},

where
c(\tilde{p}_{k})\triangleq\sqrt{2}\mbox{erf}^{-1}(2\tilde{p}_{k}-1)
.
Using the reformulation in (
18

13

Proposition 4
.

The chance-constrained optimization problem in (
13

11

\displaystyle\pi(\mathbf{x}_{k})

\displaystyle\in\argmin_{y\in\mathbb{R},\delta\in\mathbb{R},\mathbf{u}\in{\cal U}}\quad y

(19a)

s.t.

\displaystyle y-\left\|\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{x}_{k})\mathbf{u}+\sqrt{\lambda}\delta\right\|_{2}\geq 0

(19b)

\displaystyle\mathbf{q}_{k}(\mathbf{x}_{k})^{\top}\underline{\mathbf{u}}-\delta+c(\tilde{p}_{k})\left\|\mathbf{P}_{k}(\mathbf{x}_{k})\underline{\mathbf{u}}\right\|_{2}\leq 0

(19c)

\displaystyle\mathbf{e}_{k}(\mathbf{x}_{k})^{\top}\underline{\mathbf{u}}-\zeta-c(\tilde{p}_{k})\left\|\mathbf{V}_{k}(\mathbf{x}_{k})\underline{\mathbf{u}}\right\|_{2}\geq 0,

(19d)

where

\displaystyle\mathbf{q}_{k}(\mathbf{x}_{k})

\displaystyle\triangleq\mathbf{M}_{k}(\mathbf{x}_{k})^{\top}\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}V(\mathbf{x}_{k})+\begin{bmatrix}\gamma(V(\mathbf{x}_{k}))\\
\mathbf{0}_{m}\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{k}(\mathbf{x}_{k})

\displaystyle\triangleq\sqrt{\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}V(\mathbf{x}_{k})^{\top}\mathbf{A}\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}V(\mathbf{x}_{k})}\mathbf{B}_{k}^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{x}_{k}),

\displaystyle\mathbf{e}_{k}(\mathbf{x}_{k})

\displaystyle\triangleq\mathbf{M}_{k}(\mathbf{x}_{k})^{\top}\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}h(\mathbf{x}_{k})+\begin{bmatrix}\alpha(h(\mathbf{x}_{k}))\\
\mathbf{0}_{m}\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}_{k}(\mathbf{x}_{k})

\displaystyle\triangleq\sqrt{\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}h(\mathbf{x}_{k})^{\top}\mathbf{A}\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}h(\mathbf{x}_{k})}\mathbf{B}_{k}^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{x}_{k}),

and
\mathbf{B}_{k}^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{x}_{k})
is the Cholesky factorization of
\mathbf{B}_{k}(\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{x}_{k})

Proof.

Substituting the mean and variance of
\mbox{CLC}_{k}
and
\mbox{CBC}_{k}
(Lemma
2

y
to express the quadratic objective as a SOCP constraint leads to the result.
∎

Remark 2
.

If the SOCP program in (

Self-triggering Design

The safety constraint in (
19
) ensures safety with high probability at the triggering times
\{t_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
. Here, we extend our analysis to the inter-triggering invervals
[t_{k},t_{k}+\tau_{k})
. We restate
[
30
, Prop. 1]
in our setting, showing that when the system dynamics are Lipschitz continuous (Lemma
1
), then the change in the state is bounded.

Lemma 3
.

Consider the GP model of the system dynamics in (
11
) with zero-order hold control for
[t_{k},t_{k}+\tau_{k})
. If the assumptions of Lemma
1
are satisfied, i.e., with high probability the system dynamics are Lipschitz continuous with Lipschitz constant
L_{\mathbf{f}_{k}}
, then for all
s\in[0,\tau_{k})
the total change in the system state is bounded with probability at least
1-\delta_{L}
:

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{x}(t_{k}+s)-\mathbf{x}_{k}\|\leq\overline{r}_{k}(s)\triangleq\frac{1}{L_{\mathbf{f}_{k}}}\|\dot{\mathbf{x}}_{k}\|\left(e^{sL_{\mathbf{f}_{k}}}-1\right).

(20)

Recall from Sec.
III-B
that
h
is a continuously differentiable function. Thus, using Lemma
3
, for any inter-triggering time
\tau_{k}
, there exist a constant
L_{h_{k}}>0
such that:

\displaystyle\sup_{s\in[0,\tau_{k})}\|\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}h(\mathbf{x}(t_{k}+s))\|\leq L_{h_{k}}.

(21)

This is used in the next proposition which concerns Problem
2
. The next proposition guarantees safety during the inter-triggering intervals as long as the controller ensures safety at the triggering time using the SOCP in (
19
).

Proposition 5
.

Consider the GP model of the system dynamics in (
11
) with safe set
\mathcal{C}
. Assume the system dynamics have Lipschitz constant
L_{\mathbf{f}_{k}}
with probability at least
1-\delta_{L}
(Lemma
1
), the system is safe with margin
\zeta
at triggering time

with probability at least
\tilde{p}_{k}=p_{k}/(1-\delta_{L})

4

. Then, the system is safe according to the constraint in (
6
) with probability at least
p_{k}
for time duration
\tau_{k}\leq\frac{1}{L_{\mathbf{f}_{k}}}\ln\left(1+\frac{L_{\mathbf{f}_{k}}\zeta}{(L_{\mathbf{f}_{k}}+L_{\alpha})L_{h_{k}}\|\dot{\mathbf{x}}_{k}\|}\right)

Proof.

Since the program (
13
) has a solution at the triggering time

\mathbb{P}(\mbox{CBC}_{k}\geq\zeta|\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{u}_{k})\geq\tilde{p}_{k}
.
Thus, conditioned

and the Lipschitz continuity of the system dynamics, if we prove
\mbox{CBC}(s+t_{k})\geq 0

, the result follows. Using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, Lipschitz continuity of the system dynamics and Lipschitz continuity of
\alpha

\displaystyle|\mbox{CBC}(\mathbf{x}(s+t_{k}),\mathbf{u}_{k})-\mbox{CBC}_{k}|

(22)

\displaystyle\quad\leq\left(\sup_{s\in[0,\tau_{k})}\|\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}h(\mathbf{x}(s+t_{k}))\|L_{\mathbf{f}_{k}}+L_{\alpha}L_{h_{k}}\right)\overline{r}_{k}(s).

Thus, using (
20
) and (
21

\frac{L_{h_{k}}L_{\mathbf{f}_{k}}+L_{\alpha}L_{h_{k}}}{L_{\mathbf{f}_{k}}}\|\dot{\mathbf{x}}_{k}\|\left(e^{L_{\mathbf{f}_{k}}s}-1\right),

which, in turn, is less than or equal to
\zeta
for

.
∎

VI-C

Self-triggering Design

In this section, we extend the time-triggered formulation for probabilistic safety of high relative-degree systems to a self-triggering setup. Our extension to self-triggering control in the relative-degree-one case in Proposition
5
, relied on Lipshitz continuity of the CBC components. To simplify the analysis for arbitrary relative degree
r
, we temporarily introduce a modified CBF
h_{b}(\mathbf{x})\triangleq h(\mathbf{x})-\zeta_{b}
with
\zeta_{b}>0
. We solve the SOCP in (
6
) with
\zeta=0
but replace
h(\mathbf{x})
with
h_{b}(\mathbf{x})
. Enforcing the safety constraint in (
6
) for
h_{b}(\mathbf{x}_{k})
, ensures that with probability
\tilde{p}_{k}
,
h(\mathbf{x}_{k})\geq\zeta_{b}
at the sampling times. We find an upper bound on the sampling time
\tau_{k}
that ensures
h(\mathbf{x}(t))
remains non-negative during the inter-triggering intervals
[t_{k},t_{k}+\tau_{k})
. As shown in the next proposition, this is sufficient to guarantee safety during the inter-triggering interval as long as the system using the SOCP controller (
6
) is safe at the triggering time
t_{k}
.

Proposition 7
.

Consider the system in (
1
) with stochastic dynamics and safe set
\mathcal{C}=\left\{\mathbf{x}\in{\cal X}\mid h(\mathbf{x})\geq 0\right\}

r\geq 1
with respect to
h(\mathbf{x})
and the SOCP in (

) has a solution at triggering time
t_{k}
with

\zeta=0

IX
Conclusion

Allowing artificial systems to safely adapt their own models during online operation will have significant implications for their successful use in unstructured changing real-world environments. This paper developed a Bayesian inference approach to approximate system dynamics and their uncertainty from online observations. The posterior distribution over the dynamics was used to specify probabilistic constraints that guarantee safe and stable online operation with high probability. Our results offer a promising approach for controlling complex systems in challenging environments. Future work will focus on applications of the proposed approach to real robot systems.

Recall the following result about the mean and cumulants of a quadratic form of Gaussian random vectors.

Lemma 10

(

)
.

Let
\mathbf{x}

and covariance matrix

\mathbf{x}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\mathbf{x}

\mathbf{x}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\mathbf{x}

\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\mathbf{x}]=\bar{\mathbf{x}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\bar{\mathbf{x}}+\tr(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}).

The
r

\mathbf{x}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\mathbf{x}

{\cal K}_{r}(\mathbf{x}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\mathbf{x})=2^{r-1}(r-1)![\tr(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\boldsymbol{\Sigma})^{r}+r\bar{\mathbf{x}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{\Lambda})^{r-1}\bar{\mathbf{x}}].

The variance of
\mathbf{x}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\mathbf{x}
is the second cumulant:

\Var[\mathbf{x}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\mathbf{x}]={\cal K}_{2}(\mathbf{x}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\mathbf{x})=2\tr(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\boldsymbol{\Sigma})^{2}+4\bar{\mathbf{x}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\bar{\mathbf{x}}.

The covariance between
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{x}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\mathbf{x}
is:

\cov(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\mathbf{x})=2\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\bar{\mathbf{x}}

\mathbf{y}\\
\mathbf{z}\end{bmatrix}.

(63)

\displaystyle\quad+\bar{\mathbf{x}}^{\top}\Var[\mathbf{y}]\bar{\mathbf{x}}+\bar{\mathbf{y}}^{\top}\cov(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\bar{\mathbf{x}}+\bar{\mathbf{x}}^{\top}\cov(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x})\bar{\mathbf{y}}
