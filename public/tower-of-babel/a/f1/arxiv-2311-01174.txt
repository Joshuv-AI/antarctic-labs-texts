Online Multivariate Changepoint Detection: Leveraging Links With Computational Geometry
Liudmila Pishchagina, Gaetano Romano, Paul Fearnhead, Vincent Runge, Guillem Rigaill

Abstract
The increasing volume of data streams poses significant computational challenges for detecting changepoints online. Likelihood-based methods are effective, but a naive sequential implementation becomes impractical online due to high computational costs. We develop an online algorithm that exactly calculates the likelihood ratio test for a single changepoint in p p -dimensional data streams by leveraging fascinating connections with computational geometry. This connection straightforwardly allows us to recover sparse likelihood ratio statistics exactly: that is assuming only a subset of the dimensions are changing. Our algorithm is straightforward, fast, and apparently quasi-linear. A dyadic variant of our algorithm is provably quasi-linear, being 𝒪 ⁡ ( n ​ log ⁡ ( n ) p + 1 ) \mathcal{O}(n\log(n)^{p+1}) for n n data points and p p less than 3 3 , but slower in practice. These algorithms are computationally impractical when p p is larger than 5 5 , and we provide an approximate algorithm suitable for such p p which is 𝒪 ⁡ ( n ​ p ​ log ⁡ ( n ) p ~ + 1 ) , \mathcal{O}(np\log(n)^{\tilde{p}+1}), for some user-specified p ~ ≤ 5 . \tilde{p}\leq 5. We derive some statistical guarantees for the proposed procedures in the Gaussian case, and confirm the good computational and statistical performance, and usefulness, of the algorithms on both empirical data and on NBA data.

Ideally, a changepoint procedure should be linear or quasi-linear in the size of the data and, for online applications, should also be able to process new observations sequentially, and as quickly as they arrive. The pDPA, FPOP, and FOCuS algorithms rely on functionalisation of the problem, that is they consider the likelihood as a function of the last segment parameter. A direct extension of these ideas to a higher dimension (

In this paper, we study online models with one changepoint, where the likelihood can be algebraically written as a log-likelihood from the

2
Maximum Likelihood Methods for Online Single Changepoint Detection

2.1
Problem set-up

We consider observations
\{y_{t}\}_{t=1,2,\dots}
in
\mathbb{R}^{p^{\prime}}
and wish to detect the possible presence of a changepoint and estimate its location. If there is a change, we denote the time of the change as
\tau
. We will consider likelihood-based methods for doing this, which involve postulating a model for the data within each segment between consecutive data points. Our model will be that the data point
y_{t}
is independent and identically distributed from a distribution with density
f_{Y}(y_{t}|\theta_{1})
before the change and
f_{Y}(y_{t}|\theta_{2})
after the change.

At time
n
twice the log-likelihood for the time series
\{y_{t}\}_{t=1,\dots,n}
, in terms of the pre-changepoint parameter,
\theta_{1}
in
\mathbb{R}^{p^{\prime\prime}}
, the post-changepoint parameter,
\theta_{2}
in
\mathbb{R}^{p^{\prime\prime}}
, and the location of a change,
\tau
, is

\ell_{\tau,n}(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})=2\left(\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\log f_{Y}(y_{t}|\theta_{1})+\sum_{t=\tau+1}^{n}\log f_{Y}(y_{t}|\theta_{2})\right).

(1)

Depending on the application one may assume that the pre-changepoint parameter
\theta_{1}
is either known or unknown. In either case, we can get the log-likelihood for a changepoint at
\tau
by maximising this equation over the unknown parameter, so
\theta_{2}
if the pre-changepoint parameter is known or both
\theta_{1}
and
\theta_{2}
otherwise. In practice we do not know the changepoint location, so we would maximise this log-likelihood over
\tau
. We can define the maximum likelihood estimator for
\tau
, for the pre-changepoint parameter being either known or unknown respectively, as

\displaystyle\hat{\tau}(\theta_{1})=

\displaystyle\arg\max_{\tau}(\max_{\theta_{2}}\ell_{\tau,n}(\theta_{1},\theta_{2}))\,,

\displaystyle\hat{\tau}(.)=

\displaystyle\arg\max_{\tau}(\max_{\theta_{1},\theta_{2}}\ell_{\tau,n}(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})).

We then obtain a Generalized Likelihood Ratio (GLR) statistic
[
21
]
for a change
\tau
by comparing with twice the log-likelihood for a model with no change. That is
2\sum_{t=1}^{n}f_{Y}(y_{t}|\theta_{1})
if
\theta_{1}
is known and
\max_{\theta_{1}}2\sum_{t=1}^{n}f_{Y}(y_{t}|\theta_{1})
if it is not.
Often it is appropriate (see for example
[
38
]
) to consider a sparse version of the likelihood that allows only
s
coordinates to jump that is
s=||\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}||_{0}
, where
||\mathbf{v}||_{0}
is equal to the number of non-zero entries of
\mathbf{v}
.

\displaystyle\hat{\tau}^{s}(\theta_{1})=

\displaystyle\arg\max_{\tau}\left(\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\theta_{2}\\
||\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}||_{0}=s\end{subarray}}\ell_{\tau,n}(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})\right)\,,

(2)

\displaystyle\hat{\tau}^{s}(.)=

\displaystyle\arg\max_{\tau}\left(\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\\
||\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}||_{0}=s\end{subarray}}\ell_{\tau,n}(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})\right).

(3)

It should be clear that
\hat{\tau}^{p}(\theta_{1})=\hat{\tau}(\theta_{1})
and
\hat{\tau}^{p}(.)=\hat{\tau}(.).

Offline, optimising Equation (
1
) is simple. Indeed assuming we have access to all the data it suffices to compute, for every possible changepoint location from
1
to
n-1
, the likelihood (which can be done efficiently using summary statistics) and then pick the best value. Online, the problem is substantially more difficult as one needs to recompute the maximum for every new observation. Applying the previous offline strategy for every new observation is inefficient as it takes order
n
to process the
n
th observation.
It is infeasible for an online application, where
n
grows unbounded, and for this reason various heuristic strategies have been proposed (see
[
39
]
for a recent review). From a computational perspective, we roughly identify two main types of heuristics.
The first idea is to restrict the set of possible
(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})
pairs to be discrete. The simplest case is to consider just one pair of
(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})
as in the Page CUSUM strategy
[
23
]
but one typically considers a grid of values
[
38
]
. The second idea is to restrict the set of changepoint locations one considers. The simplest case is arguably to consider a grid of window lengths
w_{1},\ldots,w_{M}
and at time
n
compute the GLR statistic only for changes at
\tau=n-w_{1},\ldots,n-w_{M}

[
40
,
41
]
.
But this naturally introduces a trade-off between computational efficiency and statistical power: the larger the grid of windows, the larger the computational overhead
[
39
]
.

Recently, the FOCuS algorithm has been introduced that calculates the maximum likelihood and likelihood ratio test exactly but with a per-iteration cost that increases only logarithmically with the amount data
[
34
,
42
]
- however this algorithm is only applicable for detecting changes in a univariate feature of a data stream. A key insight from the FOCuS algorithm is that to maximise the likelihood one only needs to consider changes that correspond to points on a particular convex hull in the plane. This link to geometrical features of the data is one that we leverage later in developing algorithms for detecting changes in multivariate features.

2.2
Detection of a single change in the parameter of distribution in the exponential family

We briefly introduce our basic notations. We use
\mathbf{1}_{p}
to denote a vector of length
p
in which all elements are set to one. For any vector
\mu=(\mu^{1},\dots,\mu^{p})
in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
, we define its
q
-norm as
||\mu||_{q}:=\left(\sum_{k=1}^{p}|\mu^{k}|^{q}\right)^{1/q}
, where
(q>1)
in
\mathbb{R}
. For two vectors,
\mu_{1}=(\mu^{1}_{1},\dots,\mu^{p}_{1})
and
\mu_{2}=(\mu^{1}_{2},\dots,\mu^{p}_{2})
in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
, we define their scalar product as
\langle\mu_{1},\mu_{2}\rangle=\sum_{k=1}^{p}\mu_{1}^{k}\mu_{2}^{k}
. The symbol
|\cdot|
signifies the cardinality of
\cdot
depending on the context.

We are now ready to describe our model. We assume that the density
f_{Y}(y_{t}|\theta_{k})
, for observation
y_{t}
in segment
k\in\{1,2\}
which has segment-specific parameter
\theta_{k}
, can be written in the form

f_{Y}(y_{t}|\theta_{k})=\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2}\left[A^{\prime}(r(\theta_{k}))-2\langle r(\theta_{k}),s(y_{t})\rangle+B^{\prime}(s(y_{t}))\right]\right\}\,,

(4)

where
s
and
r
are functions mapping respectively the observations,
y_{t}
, and the parameters,
\theta_{k}
, to
\mathbb{R}^{p}
,
A^{\prime}
is some convex function from
\mathbb{R}^{p}
to
\mathbb{R}
, and
B^{\prime}
from
\mathbb{R}^{p}
to
\mathbb{R}
. Defining the natural parameter
\eta_{k}
as
\eta_{k}=r(\theta_{k}),
and
x_{t}
as
x_{t}=s(y_{t})
we rewrite this density as a function of
\eta_{k}
and
x_{t}
:

f_{X}(x_{t}|\eta_{k})=\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2}\left[A^{\prime}(\eta_{k})-2\langle\eta_{k},x_{t}\rangle+B^{\prime}(x_{t})\right]\right\}\,.

(5)

This class encompasses many common exponential family models, see Table
1
for examples of several models of changes in the mean of
p
-variate data (where
p=p^{\prime}=p^{\prime\prime}
) and the corresponding functions
s
,
r
,
A^{\prime}
and
B^{\prime}
. Later we will model a change in the mean (
\theta_{1}
) and variance (
\theta_{2}
) (so
p^{\prime\prime}=2
) of a Gaussian signal in
\mathbb{R}
(so
p^{\prime}=1
). In this slightly more complex case we have
p^{\prime}\neq p=2
and we can define

,
r
,
A^{\prime}
and
B^{\prime}
as follows

\displaystyle\theta=(\theta_{1},\theta_{2}),\quad\eta=\left(\frac{\theta_{1}}{\theta_{2}},-\frac{1}{2\theta_{2}}\right),\quad s(y)=(y,y^{2}),\quad A^{\prime}(\eta)=\frac{\theta_{1}^{2}}{\theta_{2}}-\log(\theta_{2}),\quad B^{\prime}(x)=\log(2\pi).

Let us denote the pre-changepoint natural parameter as
\eta_{1}
and the post-changepoint natural parameter by
\eta_{2}
. We can then define the log-likelihood of our (transformed) data
x_{1},\ldots,x_{t}
, under a model with a change at
\tau
as

\displaystyle\ell_{\tau,n}(\eta_{1},\eta_{2})=-\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}[A^{\prime}(\eta_{1})-2\langle x_{t},\eta_{1}\rangle+B^{\prime}(x_{t})]-\sum_{t=\tau+1}^{n}[A^{\prime}(\eta_{2})-2\langle x_{t},\eta_{2}\rangle+B^{\prime}(x_{t})].

(6)

The log-likelihood estimator for
\tau
, for the pre-change parameter being either known or unknown respectively, is

\displaystyle\hat{\tau}(\eta_{1})=

\displaystyle\arg\max_{\tau}(\max_{\eta_{2}}\ell_{\tau,n}(\eta_{1},\eta_{2}))\,,

\displaystyle\hat{\tau}(.)=

\displaystyle\arg\max_{\tau}(\max_{\eta_{1},\eta_{2}}\ell_{\tau,n}(\eta_{1},\eta_{2})).

The GLR statistic for a change
\tau
is obtained by comparing with
\sum_{t=1}^{n}[A^{\prime}(\eta_{1})-2\langle x_{t},\eta_{1}\rangle+B^{\prime}(x_{t})]
if
\eta_{1}
is known and with
\max_{\eta_{1}}{\sum_{t=1}^{n}[A^{\prime}(\eta_{1})-2\langle x_{t},\eta_{1}\rangle+B^{\prime}(x_{t})]}
if it is not.

For any fixed
\eta_{1}
and any
\eta_{2}
the addition of
\sum_{t=1}^{n}\left[A^{\prime}(\eta_{2})-2\langle x_{t},\eta_{2}\rangle+B^{\prime}(x_{t})\right]
to (
6
) does not change the arg-maximiser:
\arg\max_{\tau}\ell_{\tau,n}(\eta_{1},\eta_{2})
. Moreover, this addition eliminates the dependence on
n
. Hence when searching for the maximum likelihood change we can redefine
\ell_{\tau,n}(\eta_{1},\eta_{2})
as follows:

\ell_{\tau}(\eta_{1},\eta_{2})=\tau\left[A^{\prime}(\eta_{2})-A^{\prime}(\eta_{1})\right]-2\left\langle\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}x_{t},\eta_{2}-\eta_{1}\right\rangle,

(7)

and be sure that the optimal change optimises
\ell_{\tau}(\eta_{1},\eta_{2})
at least for a pair
(\eta_{1},\eta_{2}).

\Omega(\eta_{1},\eta_{2})

\eta_{1}

is known.
Our results for the unknown case (on

and rewrite the function

as a function of

\ell_{\tau}(\mu)=\tau A(\mu)-2\left\langle\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}x_{t},\mu\right\rangle.

3
Linear Relaxation of the Functional Pruning Problem

3.1
Mathematical Formulation

Functional pruning ideas give the potential of an efficient approach to recursively calculate the GLR statistic for a change at time
n+1
given calculations at time
n

[
34
]
. The idea is to restrict the set of possible changepoints prior to
n+1
that we need to consider. Working out which changepoint locations we need to consider is obtained by viewing the GLR statistic as a function of the post-changepoint parameter difference
\mu=\eta_{2}-\eta_{1}
(see Equation (
8
)), and finding the set of changepoint locations which contribute to this, i.e. for which the GLR statistic is maximum for some value of
\mu
.

In more details, at step
n
of a functional pruning algorithm, we consider a list of possible changepoints
\tau
of the data from
x_{1}
,
x_{2}
…to
x_{n}
. Each changepoint is represented by a function
f_{\tau}
of the last segment parameter
\mu
and a set of
\mu
values for which it is maximising the likelihood:
Set_{\tau}=\{\mu|\forall\tau^{\prime}\neq\tau,\ f_{\tau}(\mu)>f_{\tau^{\prime}}(\mu)\}
. To proceed to the next data point
n+1
the update is informally done as follows.

1.

We include in the list of segmentations the one with a last change at
n
which is represented by a function
f_{n}
.

2.

We compare all other functions to this new function and accordingly restrict the set of
\mu
for which they are optimal. That is we compute the intersection
Set_{\tau}\leftarrow Set_{\tau}\cap\{\mu|f_{\tau}(\mu)>f_{n}(\mu)\}
for all
\tau<n,
and the intersection
Set_{n}\leftarrow\cap_{\tau}\{\mu|f_{n}(\mu)>f_{\tau}(\mu)\}.

3.

We discard all changepoints whose set
Set_{\tau}
is empty.

The left column of Figure
1
provides a graphical representation of these steps. The pruning of the third step is valid because whatever the data points after
n+1
might be these functions (or equivalently changepoints) can never maximise the log-likelihood. Formally, we now consider a set
\mathcal{T}
indexing functions
f_{\tau}
such that

f_{\tau}(\mu)=a_{\tau}A(\mu)-2\langle\mu,b_{\tau}\rangle+c_{\tau}\,,

(9)

where
a_{\tau}
,
c_{\tau}
are in
\mathbb{R}
, and
b_{\tau}
is in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
and
A
is a convex function from
\mathcal{D}_{A}
, a subset of
\mathbb{R}^{p}
. We define
Im(A)
as the image of the function
A
. The idea is that at the current step of a functional pruning algorithm we are storing functions of the post-changepoint parameter difference, here denoted by
\mu
. Function
f_{\tau}
corresponds to the log-likelihood for a segmentation with a change at
\tau
and with parameter difference
\mu
. This function will depend on
\mu
through the log-likelihood contribution of the data in this segment, which will impact all the coefficients and the maximum log-likelihood for data in earlier segments.
The functional form of
f_{\tau}
will depend on the specific likelihood model. As an example, for detecting a change in the mean of Gaussian data with
\eta_{1}=0
, we would have
A(\mu)=||\mu||^{2}
, with
Im(A)=\mathbb{R}^{+}
and
\mathcal{D}_{A}=\mathbb{R}^{p}
.

We then define

F_{\mathcal{T}}(\mu)=\max_{\tau\in\mathcal{T}}\{f_{\tau}(\mu)\}\,,

and, as informally stated, our goal is to recover all the indices
\tau
maximising
F_{\mathcal{T}}
for at least one value of
\mu
. Formally, we define:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{T}}=\{\tau|\exists\mu\in\mathcal{D}_{A}\subset\mathbb{R}^{p},\forall\tau^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}\text{ with }\tau^{\prime}\neq\tau:\ f_{\tau}(\mu)>f_{\tau^{\prime}}(\mu)\}.

(10)

Remark 2
.

Index set
\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{T}}
is defined using a strict inequality. As function
A
is strictly convex, (and assuming that for all
\tau\neq\tau^{\prime}
we cannot have simultaneously
a_{\tau}=a_{\tau^{\prime}}
,
b_{\tau}=b_{\tau^{\prime}}
and
c_{\tau}=c_{\tau^{\prime}}
) equality can be obtained only over a set of zero measure, consequently this case can be ignored.

3.2
Linearisation of the Functional Pruning Problem

The size of the set
\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{T}}
depends on the nature of the function
A
. In essence, to consider any possible function
A
, we introduce a new parameter
\lambda
to replace
A(\mu)
. This way we get a problem that does not depend on the form of
A
and, as we will see, is easier to solve, albeit at the cost of an additional dimension. To be specific, for any function
\mu\mapsto f_{\tau}(\mu)
we associate an (augmented) function
(\lambda,\mu)\mapsto g_{\tau}(\lambda,\mu)
defined as:

g_{\tau}(\lambda,\mu)=a_{\tau}\lambda-2\langle\mu,b_{\tau}\rangle+c_{\tau}.

As for the
f_{\tau}
, we define the maximum
G_{\mathcal{T}}(\lambda,\mu)
as

G_{\mathcal{T}}(\lambda,\mu)=\max_{\tau\in\mathcal{T}}\{g_{\tau}(\lambda,\mu)\}.

Again our goal will be to recover the index
\tau
strictly maximising
G_{\mathcal{T}}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{T}}=\{\tau|\exists(\lambda,\mu)\in Im(A)\times\mathcal{D}_{A},\forall\tau^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}\text{ with }\tau^{\prime}\neq\tau:\ g_{\tau}(\lambda,\mu)>g_{\tau^{\prime}}(\lambda,\mu)\}.

In the previous definition, the parameter
\lambda
is constrained to be in the image of the function
A
.
The values attained by
f_{\tau}
are also attained by
g_{\tau}
on a parametric curve of the
(\lambda,\mu)
space given by equation
\lambda=A(\mu)
, so that

This trivially leads to the following result that the set of
\tau

must contain the set strictly maximising

.

Theorem 1
.

\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{T}}\subseteq\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{T}}\,.

Without any further assumptions on the function

\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{T}}
could be much larger than
\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{T}}
. The following theorem demonstrates that equality holds for strictly convex power functions.

Theorem 2
.

If for all
\tau\in\mathcal{T}
,
c_{\tau}=0
and
A(\mu)=\|\mu\|^{q}
with
q>1
, then
\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{T}}=\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{T}}
.

The proof of this Theorem can be found in Supplementary Material
A.1
.

Remark 3
.

In particular, with

we get the Gaussian loss function with
\eta_{1}=0
. Assuming
\eta_{1}=0

\eta_{1}
is not

Remark 4
.

A more general approach with milder conditions for function

3.3
Half-space intersections and functional pruning

All
g_{\tau}
functions are linear in
(\lambda,\mu)
. Considering yet another variable

If

the hyperplane defined by

\cap_{\tau}H_{\tau}

The set

4
On The Detection of a Single Changepoint Online

4.1
From Functional Description to Convex Hull

We now apply the results of Section
3
to the problem of detecting a single changepoint described in Section
2.2
, to develop an efficient online algorithm. To do this, it is helpful to define a one-to-one mapping
P
between any changepoint
\tau
and a point in dimension
p+1
:

P(\tau)=\left(\tau,\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}x_{t}\right).

The following theorem, based on Theorem
4
essentially states that the changepoint maximising the likelihood (
6
)
for some value
\eta_{1}
and
\eta_{2}
corresponds to a points on a particular convex hull.

Theorem 5
.

Assume the log-likelihood,
\ell_{\tau,n}(\eta_{1},\eta_{2})
of a change at
\tau
in
\{1,\ldots,n-1\}
can be written as in Equation (
6
). Then, for any
\eta_{1}
and
s\leq p
taking
\hat{\tau}=\hat{\tau}^{s}(\eta_{1})
or
\hat{\tau}^{s}(.)
we have that
P(\hat{\tau})

is on the convex hull of
\{P(\tau)\}_{\tau\in\{1,\ldots,n-1\}}
.

The proof of this theorem can be found in Supplementary Material
A.3
.

Theorem
5
is coherent with the notion of pruning as in the FOCuS algorithm
[
34
]
.
If
P(\tau_{0})
is strictly inside the hull (i.e. not on the boundary of the hull
\{P(\tau)\}_{\tau\in\{1,\ldots,n-1\}}
) at time
n
, then it is certainly inside the hull at any future time
n^{\prime}\geq n
(i.e. not on the boundary of the hull
\{P(\tau)\}_{\tau\in\{1,\ldots,n^{\prime}-1\}}
). Thus based on Theorem
5
the likelihood associated with the change at time
\tau_{0}
will never be optimal after time
n
.

From a computational perspective, Theorem
5
is useful only if the set of points on the convex hull is significantly smaller than
n
.
In the following subsections, we will prove using
[
57
]
that, under natural assumptions, the number of vertices of the convex hull of
\{P(\tau)\}_{\tau\in\{1,\ldots,n-1\}}
is of order
2(\log n)^{p}
. This shows that for sufficiently large
n
the number of candidate changepoints one needs to store to optimise the likelihood (or the likelihood ratio test) is much smaller than
n
.

4.2
Bound on The Number of Changepoint Candidates

In this subsection using the recent paper
[
57
]
, we provide the expected number of faces and vertices of the convex hull
\{P(\tau)\}_{\tau\in\{1,\ldots,n-1\}}
, assuming that the components of
x_{\tau}
,
x_{\tau}^{d}
(with
d=1,\dots,p
), are independent and identically distributed with a continuous distribution. In turn, using Theorem
4
this gives us an upper bound on the expected number of changepoints that can maximise the likelihood function (
6
) for some value of
\eta_{1}
and
\eta_{2}
.
From a computational perspective, this bound will be useful to derive guarantees on the computational complexity of the procedure (see Algorithms
1
and
3
).

To apply the result of
[
57
]
we need to prove that the random walk
\{P(\tau)\}_{\tau\in\{1,\ldots,n-1\}}=\{(\tau,\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}x_{t})\}_{\tau\in\{1,\ldots,n-1\}}
satisfies two properties,
(Ex)
and
(GP)
defined below.

•

(
Ex
) For any permutation
\sigma
of the set
\{1,\dots,n-1\}
, we have the distributional equality

\{(1,x_{\tau})\}_{\tau\in\{1,\ldots,n-1\}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle D}}{{=}}\{(1,x_{\tau^{\prime}})\}_{\tau^{\prime}\in\sigma}

•

(
GP
) Any
p+1
vectors of the form
(\tau,\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}x_{t})
are almost surely linearly independent.

Lemma 1
.

Assuming that all
x_{\tau}^{d}
are independent and identically distributed with a continuous distribution, the properties
(Ex)
and
(GP)
of
[
57
]
are satisfied.

The proof of this lemma can be found in Supplementary Material
A.4
.

We can now state the expected number of faces and vertices of the convex hull of
\{P(\tau)\}_{\tau\in\{1,\ldots,n-1\}}
.

Theorem 6
.

Assuming all
x_{\tau}^{d}
(with
d=1,\dots,p
) are independent and identically distributed with a continuous distribution, then the expected number of faces (denoted as
U_{n}^{p}
) and vertices (denoted as
V_{n}^{p}
) of the convex hull of
\{P(\tau)\}_{\tau\in\{1,\ldots,n-1\}}
are the following:

\mathbb{E}(U_{n}^{p})=\frac{2p!}{(n-1)!}\begin{bmatrix}n\\
p+1\\
\end{bmatrix},\quad\mathbb{E}(V_{n}^{p})=\frac{2}{(n-1)!}\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\begin{bmatrix}n\\
p+1-2l\\
\end{bmatrix},

where
\begin{bmatrix}n\\
m\\
\end{bmatrix}

are the Stirling numbers of the first kind
[
58
]
.

Proof.

Based on Lemma
1
properties
(Ex)
and
(GP)
hold and we can apply formula (2) from
[
57
]
on the expected values of
U_{n}^{p}
and
V_{n}^{p}.

∎

Corollary 1
.

We have the following equivalents for the expected numbers of faces
U_{n}^{p}
and vertices
V_{n}^{p}
:

\mathbb{E}(U_{n}^{p})\sim 2\left(\log(n)\right)^{p}\,,\quad\mathbb{E}(V_{n}^{p})\sim\frac{2}{p!}\left(\log(n)\right)^{p}\,,\quad n\rightarrow\infty.

The proof of Corollary
1
follows from Remark 1.4 of
[
57
]
.
The theorem and corollary provide an expected upper limit on the number of points, or candidate locations for changepoints, that need to be evaluated to compute the CUSUM likelihood ratio test (
6
) exactly without resorting to approximations. Although this limit increases as
\mathcal{O}(\log(n)^{p})
and thus as a power of
p
, it is not overly restrictive. In some online applications, we often encounter situations where
n>>p
, making an exact implementation feasible.
In the following paragraph,
we will evaluate scenarios up to
5
dimensions.

Remark 6
.

Theorem
6
gives the expectation for i.i.d. observations. One might wonder what happens if there is a changepoint in the distribution at
i<n.
Note that the points on the hull
\{P(\tau)\}_{\tau\in\{1,\ldots,n-1\}}
are necessarily on the hull of
\{P(\tau)\}_{\tau\in\{1,\ldots,i\}}
or on the hull of
\{P(\tau)\}_{\tau\in\{i+1,\ldots,n-1\}}.
Thus applying the theorem on all points from
1
to
i
and from
i+1
to
n-1
we expect at most
\mathbb{E}(V_{i+1}^{p})+\mathbb{E}(V_{n-i}^{p})\leq 2\mathbb{E}(V_{n}^{p})
points on the hull (as the expectation of
V_{i}^{p}
is increasing with
i
). Thus if there is a changepoint (and assuming we haven’t stop because the GLR statistics is lower than the specified threshold) the expected number of candidate changepoints is still in
\mathcal{O}(\log(n)^{p}).

Empirical validation of the bound

We empirically tested the validity of Theorem
6
. We use the Gaussian distribution as an example of a distribution with continuous density and independent dimensions.
To be specific, for
p
from
1
to
5
, we simulated time series
x_{1:n}
, with
x_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}_{p}(0,I_{p})
i.i.d. We varied the time series length from
2^{10}+1(\approx 10^{3})
to
2^{23}+1(\approx 8\times 10^{6})
and simulated
100
data sets for each length. We used the
QuickHull
algorithm
[
48
]
implemented in the R package
geometry

[
59
]
to evaluate the number of faces and vertices of the convex hull of
\{P((\tau)\}_{\tau\in\{1,\ldots,n-1\}}
.
It can be seen in Figure
4
that the observed number of faces and vertices are close to their theoretical expectations (from Theorem
6
). Additional details on the calculations of the Stirling numbers are provided in Supplementary Material
C
.

The proof of Theorem
6
depends on the fact that the distribution is continuous. We thus empirically tested the robustness of the expectations to this assumption by considering time series drawn with a discrete i.i.d. Poisson distribution. The results closely resemble the Gaussian case and details can be found in Supplementary Material
E.2
.

4.3
MdFOCuS(0): a Simple Online Algorithm

In this section we propose an algorithm to compute the GLR statistics at every time step
n
for a known and unknown
\eta_{1}.
In both cases, based on
Theorem
5
we only need to consider the changepoints
\tau
such that
P(\tau)
is on the hull of
\left\{P(\tau)\right\}_{\tau\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}}.

Let us call
\mathcal{T}_{n}
the set of index
\tau
such that
P(\tau)=(\tau,\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}x_{t})
is on the convex hull of all
\{P(\tau)\}_{\tau\in\{1,\ldots,n-1\}}
. Restating Theorem
4
, the changepoint
\hat{\tau}
maximising the likelihood (
6
) (i.e
\hat{\tau}(\eta_{1})
if
\eta_{1}
is known or
\hat{\tau}(.)
if it is not) is in
\mathcal{T}_{n}
. Hence, computationally speaking, at step
n
we only need to compute the likelihood (and likelihood ratio statistics) of changepoints
\tau
in
\mathcal{T}_{n}
. Furthermore, as already explained after Theorem
5
we can prune all
\tau
that are not in
\mathcal{T}_{n}
as they will never be in
\mathcal{T}_{n^{\prime}}
for any
n^{\prime}\geq n
.

Computing the convex hull from scratch at every time step
n
is inefficient: it would require at least
\mathcal{O}(n)
operations and lead to an overall complexity at least quadratic in the number of data points. Ideally, we would like to update the hull using an efficient online or dynamic convex hull algorithm as the one in
[
60
]
. There exists implementations of such an algorithm in dimension
p=2

[
61
]
and
p=3

[
62
]

However, out of simplicity, here we rely on an iterative use of the offline
QuickHull
algorithm
[
48
]
that also works for larger dimensions and is available in the geometry R package
[
59
]
.

In detail, for any
n^{\prime}>n
we have
\mathcal{T}_{n^{\prime}}\subset\mathcal{T}_{n}\cup\{n,\ldots,n^{\prime}-1\}
. Therefore if we have already computed
\mathcal{T}_{n}
, with
QuickHull
, when we compute
\mathcal{T}_{n^{\prime}}
we need not restart from scratch but simply apply
QuickHull
to the points
P(\tau)
with
\tau
in
\mathcal{T}_{n}\cup\{n,\ldots,n^{\prime}-1\}
. A first idea would be to apply the
QuickHull
algorithm at every time step
n,n+1,\ldots
, that is compute

\mathcal{T}_{n}
as the hull of all
P(\tau)
with
\tau
in
\mathcal{T}_{n-1}\cup\{n\}
. In practice, we discovered that running the
QuickHull
algorithm intermittently, rather than at every iteration, resulted in faster run times. Specifically, we maintain a set of linearly increasing indices, denoted as
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}_{n}
, and only periodically execute
QuickHull
to reconstruct the true
\mathcal{T}_{n}
.
More precisely, to decide when to run
QuickHull
we update a maximum size variable for
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}_{n}
(denoted
maxSize
). Then, at step
n+1
:

•

either the size of
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}_{n}
,
|\mathcal{T}^{\prime}_{n}|
, is smaller than
maxSize
in that case we set
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}_{n+1}
to
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}_{n}\cup\{n\}
,

•

or it is larger and in that case we compute
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}_{n+1}
as the convex hull of all
P(\tau)
with
\tau
in
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}_{n}\cup\{n\}
.

In the later case we also update
maxSize
to
maxSize=\lfloor\alpha|\mathcal{T}^{\prime}_{n+1}|+\beta\rfloor
, with
\alpha\geq 1
and
\beta\geq 0
. It is important to note that, in this way, we always retain a few more possible changepoints than necessary:
\mathcal{T}_{n+1}\subseteq\mathcal{T}^{\prime}_{n+1}
.

Taking
\beta=1
and
\alpha=1
, we run the
QuickHull
algorithm at every time step. Increasing the value of
\alpha
allows to run it less often at the cost of having a longer list of indices
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}_{n}
. In Figure
11
we considered different value for
\alpha
with
\beta=1
and found that a value of
\alpha=2
gives good practical performances (see Supplementary Material
E.1
).

A formal description of our procedure, called MdFOCuS if
\eta_{1}
is unknown and MdFOCuS0 if it is, is available in Algorithm
1
. The only difference between MdFOCuS and MdFOCuS0 variants is in the calculation of the likelihood (lines 9 and 10). Therefore the two variants store the same number of candidate changepoints and have the same time and memory complexities. In detail, computing the full likelihood ratio statistics for any particular changepoint can be done in
\mathcal{O}(p)
and therefore lines 9 and 10 in Algorithm
1
can be done
\mathcal{O}(|\mathcal{T}|p).
Recovering the maximum is then in
\mathcal{O}(|\mathcal{T}|).
Overall, this is
\mathcal{O}(|\mathcal{T}|p).

Algorithm
1
is exact and empirically fast
but it is non-trivial to derive its worst or expected time complexity as the time at which we run
QuickHull
is stochastic. In Supplementary Material
F
we propose Algorithm
3
that is closely related and easier to analyse theoretically. This algorithm updates the set
\mathcal{T}
(lines 11 to 14 in Algorithm
1
) in a deterministic manner on predetermined subsets or chunks of the observations. This deterministic choice of chunks is likely improvable but it allows for a precise quantification of the expected complexity of the algorithm under the assumption of Theorem
6
(or Corollary
1
) and under the assumption that the convex hull algorithm is at worst quadratic.
To be specific here are our assumptions.

Assumption 1
.

In any dimension
p\geq 1
, there are positive constants
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
for which the expected number of vertices,
\mathbb{E}[V_{n}^{p}]
, can be bounded as follows:

\mathbb{E}[V_{n}^{p}]\leq c_{1}\log(n)^{p}+c_{2}.

This is true under the conditions of Corollary
1
.

Assumption 2
.

We assume that the convex hull algorithm we are using is at worst quadratic in time and assume there exist two positive constants
c_{3}
and
c_{4}
such that for
n
points the time complexity is
Time(n)\leq c_{3}n^{2}+c_{4}
.

The assumption on the quadratic complexity is true for
QuickHull
in dimensions 2 and 3 (so for
p\leq 2
)
[
48
,
63
]
and using the algorithm of Chazelle
[
64
]
it is also true in dimension 4 (so for
p\leq 3
). Still following Chazelle this is no longer true for any dimension strictly larger than
4
and therefore
p>3
.

In essence, our deterministic Algorithm
3
computes the convex hull using a divide and conquer strategy. Out of simplicity let us consider the offline setting and assume
n-1
is a power of
2
, that is
n-1=2^{\log_{2}(n-1)}.
Rather than running the hull algorithm on all points
\{P(\tau)\}_{\tau\in\{1,\ldots,n-1\}}
we first run the algorithm on smaller chunks of size
2^{q_{min}}
:
\tau\in\{2^{q_{min}}j+1,\ldots,2^{q_{min}}j+2^{q_{min}}\},
with
q_{min}
a constant chosen by the user and
j
a number smaller than
(n-1)/2^{q_{min}}
, and then dyadically merge the results. To be specific for each scale
q
with
q_{min}<q<\log_{2}(n-1)
we get the point on chunk
\{2^{q+1}j+1,\ldots,2^{q+1}j+2^{q+1}\}
, by merging the results of the two smaller chunks
\{2^{q+1}j+1,\ldots,2^{q+1}j+2^{q}\},
and
\{2^{q+1}j+2^{q}+1,\ldots,2^{q+1}j+2^{q+1}\}.
This merging is computationally efficient because, under Assumption
1
, the number of points on the hull of a chunk of size
2^{q}
is expected to be small (of order
(\log(2^{q}))^{p}=(q\log(2))^{p}
).

One difficulty in Algorithm
3
is to make this dyadic or divide and conquer merging in an online fashion. To the best of our efforts, this makes the formal description a bit tedious, and we refer the interested reader to the Supplementary Material
F
for a precise description. We now state our bound on the expected complexity of this algorithm.

Lemma 2
.

Under the assumption of Corollary
1
(see Assumption
1
) and Assumption
2
,
the expected time complexity of Algorithm
2
is

\mathbb{E}\left(Time(\text{Algorithm \ref{dyadic_online}})\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(n\log(n)^{p+1}\right).

(11)

We empirically study the complexity of Algorithm
3
in Figure
16
of Supplementary Material
F
. It can be seen that it is slower than Algorithm
1
. Algorithm
3
considers at least all points on the hull to maximise the likelihood for every new data point. For a fixed
p
following, Theorem
6
and its corollary the number of points on the hull at step
i
is in
\Omega((\log(i))^{p}).

To recover a lower bound on the complexity, we first sum overall
i\leq n
, and then consider only
i\geq n/2
to recover that the overall number of points we will consider is in
\Omega(n/2(\log(n/2))^{p})
and thus
\Omega(n(\log(n))^{p}).
Relative to that Algorithm
3
loose just one
\log(n)
term, we thus argue our bound is relatively tight.

4.4.2
MdFOCuS(0) Sp: A simple online algorithm with sparse statistics

Out of simplicity in lines 9 and 10 of Algorithm
1
we only consider the full (or dense) max-likelihood ratio statistics, as in Equation (
6
), that is assuming all coordinates are changing. However, based on Theorem
5
the changepoints maximising sparse likelihood ratio statistics, that is assuming only a subset of
s
coordinates are changing (see Equation (
2
)), also correspond to points on the hull.

Computing all sparse max-likelihood ratio statistics, as in Equation (
2
), is a bit more difficult than the full likelihood ratio statistics.
To be specific, for each candidate changepoint we sort the per-dimension ratio statistics (this can be done in
\mathcal{O}(p\log(p))
) and we then sum these from the largest to the smallest in
\mathcal{O}(p)
to iteratively recover the best for all sparsity level
s.
Once this is done for all candidates, we recover the maximum for each sparsity level in
\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{T}).
Adding these up, even if we consider all sparsity levels (that is all
s
between 1 and
p
) we are overall in
\mathcal{O}(|\mathcal{T}|p\log(p)).

In practice, it makes sense to consider a subset of all sparsity levels. In our simulations we consider:
s\in\{1,2,\ldots,2^{\lfloor\log_{2}(p)\rfloor},p\}.

In the spirit of our FOCuS approximation
[
34
]
we also consider the sum of the max statistics: that is we get for each coordinate the max likelihood ratio statistics and sum these. We call these procedures respectively MdFOCuS0 Sp if we know the pre-change parameter and MdFOCuS Sp if not. As can be seen in Figure
5
(left), the empirical runtime of MdFOCuS Sp is slower than that of MdFOCuS and comparable to that of the ocd R package
[
38
]
.

4.4.3
MdFOCuS(0)
\tilde{p}
d Sp: An approximate algorithm for large
p

Under the condition of Theorem
6
, Algorithm
1
is expected to store
\mathcal{O}(\log^{p}(n))
candidates and the
\log^{p}(n)
terms means that the algorithm is impractical for
p
larger than
5,
as exemplified in Figure
4
. In Supplementary Material
G
we propose an approximate algorithm by recovering only a subset of the points on the hull. We obtain this subset by considering projections on at most
\tilde{p}
variates, running the hull algorithm on each projection, and taking the union of the time-points (of the vertices) retained for each projection.
For each projection or group of variates, and under the condition of Theorem
6
we thus store
\mathcal{O}(\log^{\tilde{p}}(n))
candidates. Considering
p
such groups we thus store
\mathcal{O}(p\log^{\tilde{p}}(n))
candidates in total thus effectively controlling the quality of the approximation and reducing the computational complexity.

This heuristic for
\tilde{p}=2
, implemented in
R
, runs on average in about 100 seconds for
p=100
and
n=10000
. We further explore the run times of this heuristic in Figure
5
and show that its run time is comparable to ocd
[
38
]
.
Recovering just
\mathcal{O}(p\log^{2}(n))
points of the hull out
\mathcal{O}(p\log^{{p}}(n))
is arguably a crude approximation, but as shown in Section
4.6
it already provides good statistical performance relative to ocd or the FOCuS(0) multivariate approximation.

4.5
A Note on Statistical Theoretical Guarantees

In Corollary
2
, in Supplementary Material
H
, we provide time-varying thresholds (i.e. increasing with the number of observations
n
) that control the probability of incorrectly detecting a change (i.e. probability of false alarms) for the i.i.d. Gaussian setting. These results are for considering only a sparse max likelihood ratio statistics with
s=1
and the full max likelihood ratio statistics (that is with
s=p
). We also provide fixed thresholds to control the average run length. Further, in Lemma
3
we derive a bound on the average detection delay (ADD) for a fixed threshold.

We derive the thresholds following the peeling approach of
[
65
]
combined with the Chi-square concentration bound of
[
66
]
. For the sparse statistics our threshold is
8\log(np/\alpha)
and for the full or dense statistics it is
p+2\sqrt{p(4\log(n)-\log(\alpha)+1)}+4\log(n)-\log(\alpha)+1,
where
2\alpha
is the desired false positive detection rate. These thresholds are comparable to the
T^{diag}
and
T^{off}
thresholds of ocd
[
38
]
.

Note that, in Supplementary Material, we only consider two sparsity levels:
s=1
and
s=p.

In MdFOCuS Sp we typically consider more, say
s
in
1,2,\dots,2^{\lfloor\log_{2}(p)\rfloor}
. Here is one way to extend the thresholds to this case. First, for any sparsity
s
strictly larger than 1 and smaller than
p
, we consider a union bound over all possible choices of
s
coordinates among
p
, which is
{p\choose s}
(see the partial norm statistics in
[
67
]
). Second, we consider a union bound over all
\log_{2}(p)
sparsity levels. Combining these, we recover a
\log(\log_{2}(p))
term in all thresholds (as in the
T^{\text{diag}}
and
T^{\text{off}}
thresholds of ocd) and a
\log({p\choose s})\leq s\log(ep/s)
term in thresholds for sparsity
s
strictly larger than
1
and smaller than
p.

For the ADD our bound is inversely proportional to the size of the change. Combined with the threshold, this supports the intuition that if a change is in just one dimension it should be detected by a partial statistics (
s=1
) while if it is dense it should be detected by full maximum likelihood ratio statistics (
s=p
). In the dense case, our bound on the ADD is proportional to the threshold and therefore includes a term linear in
p.
This is likely sub-optimal and due to our proof technique based on a martingale argument. We show in Subsection
H.2.1
in Supplementary Material exploiting the peeling argument of
[
65
]
and
[
68
]
that with probability at least
1-\alpha
the detection delay is smaller than a constant only involving a
\sqrt{p}
terms divided by the size of the change.

4.6
Empirical statistical performances

We evaluate the empirical performance of the R implementation of MdFOCuS Sp (which computes the sparse likelihood ratio statistics) on synthetic data (the link to the code is available in the
Supplementary Material
). In this section, we analyse 3, 5, and 100-dimensional time series under the Gaussian change-in-mean setting, with an additional simulation study for 50-dimensional time series presented in Supplementary Material
E.3
. We consider two scenarios: pre-change mean known and unknown. For each experiment, we report results aggregated from 300 data sets.

For MdFOCuS Sp for dimension
p=3
and
5
, we consider sparsity level
s=1,
the full max-likelihood ratio (either
s=3
or
s=5
depending on the dimension), and the sum of the max likelihood ratio statistics per dimension as proposed in
[
34
]
(see Subsection
4.4
). For higher dimensions (
p=100
and
50
), we set the sparsity levels as in Section
4.4
. We compare the performance of MdFOCuS with the high-dimensional online changepoint detection method ocd from
[
38
]
and a naive multivariate implementation of FOCuS, obtained by running FOCuS independently for each dimension and summing or taking the maximum of the resulting traces (see Section 6 of
[
34
]
) at each iteration. Additionally, for the pre-change mean unknown case, we report results of FOCuS and ocd where a plug-in estimate of the pre-change parameter was obtained over a probation (training) period of 250 observations.

Thresholds were selected under a Monte-Carlo simulation to achieve an average run length of
5\times 10^{3}
observations with no change, consistent with the empirical evaluation studies of both
[
34
]
and
[
38
]
. An additional study controlling the false positive rate can be found in Supplementary Material
E.3
. For a change at
t=1000
, we evaluate performances for a range of different change magnitudes and density regimes, e.g., for a change affecting only a portion of the time series.
To compare performances across different density regimes, changes in the affected time series were normalised such that the change direction lies in the
p
-dimensional sphere, i.e., (
||\eta_{1}-\eta_{2}||^{2}_{2}=\delta
), where
\delta
is the overall change magnitude and
\eta_{1},\eta_{2}
are the pre- and post-change means, respectively.

We report below tables summarising the ADD over various magnitudes and for different change densities across all replicates, respectively for 3-dimensional time series (in Table
2
), 5-dimensional time series (in Table
3
), and finally 100-dimensional time series (in Table
4
).

In all tables, the two leftmost columns indicate the scenario in terms of change magnitude and density. In the pre-change parameter known scenario, on the left, with FOCuS0, MdFOCuS0 Sp, and ocd (oracle), we indicate the respective algorithms with plug-in oracle pre-changepoint parameter. On the right, for the pre-change mean unknown scenario, with FOCuS0 (est 250) and ocd (est 250), we report the pre-change mean known methods, using a plug-in pre-change mean estimate, and with FOCuS, the independent FOCuS algorithm, and finally MdFOCuS Sp, the procedure introduced in this manuscript. Finally, for the 100-dimensional profiles, with MdFOCuS0 2d Sp and MdFOCuS 2d Sp, we indicate that the procedure is employing the 2d approximation for the convex hull.

Globally, for all dimensions, in both pre-change parameter known and unknown scenarios, we observe that MdFOCuS Sp and MdFOCuS0 Sp generally achieve smaller detection delays in cases where the change affects all the time series and over changes of larger magnitudes. In scenarios with density 1, the simple FOCuS aggregation tends to be slightly better. This is expected as if we only have a change in one dimension, both the sparse statistics of FOCuS and MdFOCuS Sp will be the same, but MdFOCuS Sp will have a slightly larger threshold, resulting in a slower delay.

Focusing on the pre-change mean known case, over changes of smaller magnitudes, we find MdFOCuS0 Sp to be relatively close to the best competitor in terms of ADD, while it generally outperforms the other methods over changes of larger magnitudes. Furthermore, MdFOCuS0 Sp is seen to have quite consistent performances across different sparsity regimes over the same change magnitudes. As ocd uses a grid for the possible size of change for each variate, its performance is affected by how close the true change size is to the nearest point on the grid. In particular, its performance deteriorates when the true change is outside the set of grid values. We observe this in its deterioration of performance for low magnitudes (0.125) and large magnitudes (1 or 2), in particular over dimensions
p=3,5
. This effect emerging from a grid-based approximation has already been observed thoroughly, albeit in the 1-d case, in the main simulation study of
[
34
]
.

Moving to the pre-change mean unknown scenario, we observe that MdFOCuS Sp generally outperforms competitor methods for magnitudes larger than 0.250 in most scenarios, with the exception of scenarios with a change in a single dimension, as explained above. Moreover, in dimensions
p=5
and
100
, we can clearly see the advantages of performing the likelihood ratio test for a change when the pre-change mean is unknown over FOCuS0 or ocd, where the pre-change mean unknown parameter needs to be estimated.
The only exception occurs in the case of FOCuS (est 250) for
p=3
, where in the lowest magnitude, it is seen outperforming all other methods. Whilst this might seem counter-intuitive, as the error from the estimation should propagate, in dimension
p=3
for a small signal-to-noise ratio, the estimation is good enough to not impair the average run length and increase the thresholds, while benefiting from an easier optimisation (as we see in the pre-change mean known case). However, as we move to higher dimensions
p=5
and
100
, it is more unlikely to obtain a better estimate of the true pre-change parameter simultaneously in all dimensions. This results in larger thresholds to control the desired run length, and therefore in a global loss of power for FOCuS0 (est) and ocd (est). Of course, having more training observations improves the results, in particular for
p=3
, however, this still has little to no effect in higher dimensions (we report results with more training observations in Supplementary Material
E.4
). Moreover, in Supplementary Material
E.3
, where we control for false positives and have a much later change, it can be seen in Table
5
, that our 2d approximation MdFOCuS 2d Sp is actually better than the 1d approximation of FOCuS
[
34
]
for dense changes showing the advantage of considering larger
\tilde{p}.

Application on NBA Plus-Minus

In this section, we illustrate our methodology to detect a change in the Cleveland Cavaliers (an NBA team) Plus-Minus score from season 2010-11 to 2017-18. This example was proposed in
[
37
]
.
We use this example for two reasons.
First, it highlights that our computational framework is also applicable to CUSUM Exponential baseline e-detectors proposed in
[
37
]
which in essence are a non parametric generalization of generalized likelihood ratio rules.
Second, it illustrates how modelling not only the signal’s mean but also its variance can simplify the calibration of the detection threshold, without necessarily resorting to a non-parametric approach.

We recovered the Plus-Minus score using the nbastatR package
[
69
]
available on GitHub
https://github.com/abresler/nbastatR
. The Cavaliers Plus-Minus is represented in Figure
6
.
This score represents how well the Cavaliers fared against their opponent.
In this subsection, we assume that we are observing these matches one after the other and we are interested in detecting a changepoint online.

Plus-Minus score of the Cavaliers from season 2010-11 to season 2017-18 as a bar plot. Losses are in dark red and wins are in dark blue. Ends of seasons are represented with vertical dashed lines and seasons are shown on the bottom with grey arrows.

As detailed in
[
37
]
it is nontrivial to fit this problem into commonly used parametric sequential changepoint detection procedures for two main reasons.
First, it is not easy to choose a parametric model to fit the score. In particular, we would like to take into account that we have integer-valued data, and we expect some form of dependency.
Second, assuming we have chosen a model or likelihood function, calibrating the threshold to detect a changepoint is not trivial. A small change in the distribution we use as a reference can substantially change the threshold and as a consequence increase the detection time or lead to false detections.
E-detectors of
[
37
]
, are an interesting non-parametric solution to these two problems assuming some knowledge of the pre-change distribution.

Convex hull for CUSUM e-detectors

Following equations (27) of

5.2
A parametric approach to the Plus-Minus score

Considering a change in the mean of the Gaussian model to fit the Plus-Minus score of the Cavaliers seems rather naive, as it does not take into account the discrete nature of the data and possible dependencies. In this context, we argue that it is also not clear how one could get a valid estimation of the pre-changepoint parameter. Finally, as argued in
[
37
]
and shown a bit later calibrating the threshold to detect a changepoint is not trivial. Roughly speaking the threshold depends on the choice of the null distribution and this largely affects the position at which a change is detected. Here as a robust parametric solution to these problems, we consider a change in the mean and variance of a univariate Gaussian signal with unknown pre-changepoint parameters.

For a change in mean and variance using our convex hull framework Equation (
9
) and following its natural parametrisation presented in Section
2.2
we need to keep track of the vertices on the hull of a 3-dimensional set of points

\left(\tau,\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}x_{t},\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}x_{t}^{2}\right)_{\tau<n}.

(13)

As we will see this second model, although still parametric, is much more robust to the choice of the null distribution.

Interestingly, this mean and variance model is invariant to shift and scaling. Based on that, we see that all the points on the convex hull of the Plus-Minus e-detector are on the hull of the change in mean and variance model. Indeed for this second e-detector the raw Plus-Minus score was normalized as follows
x^{\prime}_{t}=(x_{t}+80)/160
and then
s
and
v
were chosen to be
s(x^{\prime})=(x^{\prime}/m-1)
and
v(x^{\prime})=s(x^{\prime})^{2})
. Therefore all changepoints tested by the Plus-Minus e-detector will also be tested by our mean and variance model.

As the data is discrete it is not unlikely that two consecutive scores are exactly equal (this happens 19 times out of 639 in the Plus-Minus score of the Cavaliers from 2010 to 2018). In fact, we also observed 2 stretches of 3 equal scores. For such stretches the estimated variance would be
0
and the likelihood would blow up to infinity. To get around this problem we considered a minimum value for the variance. We computed the per-season variance of all NBA teams from season 2003-2004 to season 2018-2019 and observed a minimum of
97.02
, a maximum of
247.38
and a mean of
170.68
. Hence, we argue that setting a minimum variance of 1 (which is roughly a hundred times smaller) should not hamper our ability to detect changes, while also limiting the impact of small segments with equal values.

5.2.1
Simulating NBA Plus-Minus scores without changepoint

Based on Figure
7
it is clear that for a particular range of the threshold both the mean and mean and variance model with unknown pre-changepoint parameter can detect a changepoint after the beginning of season 2014-15. To calibrate this threshold one typically needs a simulation model without a changepoint. Here, we will set the CUSUM threshold such that under our null simulation over 1000 matches we would detect a change only in 5% of the cases.

Our idea here is not to precisely model the Plus-Minus score of the Cavaliers but rather explore a few natural optimistic and pessimistic options to model it and check how this affects the threshold of the two models and in turns the time at which they detect the change.

The Plus-Minus score is integer-valued. A straightforward, albeit maybe simple, choice would be to consider the difference of two independent Poisson distributions. This is the so-called Skellam distribution. It has two parameters (the mean of the first and second Poisson
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
).
We simulated data using values of
\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{2}
both equal to 95, 105, and 115.
We considered mean values around
100
as the points per team of an NBA match from season 1999-00 to season 2021-22 range from
93.40
to
112.09
with a mean of
101.07
.

A slightly more complex choice is to consider the difference of two independent Negative Binomial distributions with equal scale parameters,
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
. As for the Poisson we considered
\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{2}
, which were set to
95
,
105
, and
115
, along with scale parameters
r_{1}=r_{2}
at values of
500
,
100
,
50
, and
10
.
This makes a total of
12
combinations.

A data-driven way to model the Plus-Minus score without changepoint is to re-sample past games. We did this sampling uniformly at random past matches of the Cavaliers from season 1999-2000 to 2009-2010 or uniformly at random past matches of any NBA team from season 1999-2000 to 2009-2010.
Finally, following the argument of
[
37
]
and noting that the Plus-Minus
score
is never larger than
80
we simulated integer valued score uniformly at random between
-80
and
80
.

In the Poisson and Negative Binomial based model, we could further model dependencies in the data. To do this we varied the mean of the first Poisson (or Negative Binomial) over time. We considered two scenarios. In the first, we added to the mean an auto-regressive process of order 1 and coefficient
0.6
multiplied by either 1, 2, 4, or 8. In the second we added sin waves with a period of 20 matches multiplied by either 1, 2, 4, or 8. We present in Figure

6
Discussion

In this paper, we establish a connection between the detection of a single changepoint in
p
-dimensional data streams and a convex hull problem in dimension
p+1
. Assuming independence of all observations and dimensions and a continuous distribution we demonstrate using the recent probabilistic paper
[
57
]
that the expected number of faces and vertices of the convex hull is of order
2(\log(n))^{p}
where
n
is the number of data points.

Based on this, we derive a simple yet efficient algorithm, MdFOCuS Sp, that allows maximising the full or restricted likelihood-ratio test statistics (that is allowing only
s
coordinates to change with
s\leq p
) over all possible window sizes. MdFOCuS Sp generalises the FOCuS algorithm
[
34
]
to the multivariate case. We show that MdFOCuS Sp is empirically fast for up to
p=5
and allows to process more than
10^{5}
data points in a matter of minutes.
For larger
p
we provide an approximation storing only
\mathcal{O}(p\log^{\tilde{p}}(n))
vertices of the convex hull where
\tilde{p}\leq p.
For
\tilde{p}=2
this approximation is suitable for high-dimensional data.
We also show using simulation that MdFOCuS Sp and its approximation (MdFOCuS
\tilde{p}
d Sp) are statistically competitive in particular when the change affects all dimensions or when the parameter prior to the change is unknown.

A few improvements to the computational complexity of the procedure could be made to allow the procedure to scale to higher dimensions and longer sequences. The most computationally expensive components of the procedure are respectively the
QuickHull
algorithm, for pruning the list of indices, and the maximisation step.
We mentioned already how further research could go towards a sequential update of the boundary of the convex hull of our points, to improve on the complexity of
QuickHull
. The idea would be to employ information from the hull at the previous iteration, evaluate the inclusion of the most recent point, and update the faces of the neighbouring vertex consequently.

Recently,
[
71
]
offered a solution to improve on the maximisation step. Instead of looking at the maxima of all indices to calculate the test statistic, the authors exploit a bound on the maxima of the differences of contiguous indices. The main idea is that the overall maximum, our test statistics, is bounded by the sum of those maxima of the differences. If this sum does not pass the threshold there is no need to compute the likelihood ratio-statistics for all unpruned changepoints. Fast sequential updates of the sum of the maxima of the difference exists, effectively rendering the univariate algorithm constant per iteration
[
71
]
. Whilst this bound translates exactly to the multivariate case, it is easily much larger than the threshold, given that we need to store and sum a large amount of indices even in dimension 2. A less conservative bound could be the object of future study to improve on the maximisation step in higher dimensions.

To conclude, our mathematical argument is valid for a large collection of parametric online single changepoint models with distributions taken from the exponential family (or more generally with a likelihood that can be algebraically written as the natural exponential family). Note that this includes models where data are of different types (e.g. with
p=2
a first Poisson component, and a second Gaussian component). We also show in Supplementary Material
D
that there is a connection between multiple changepoint models and the half-space intersection problem of Theorem
3
.
Furthermore, we show in Section
5
that Theorem
5
could be beneficial to speed the calculation of the non-parametric e-detectors.
Finally, note that no assumptions are made on the set of indices
\mathcal{T}
in Equation (
9
) and for example, our theory also applies to changepoint models with observations on a tree rather than a chain
[
72
]
.
For all these reasons we argue that this connection between changepoints and convex hull (or half-space intersection) sheds light on the geometric nature of the changepoint detection problem and is a promising line for future research.

to consider, and their probabilities satisfy
(GP)
, ensuring that property
(GP)
holds.
∎

Appendix B
Index Set Equality

The equality
\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{T}}=\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{T}}
can be proved with weaker conditions on
A
than those of Theorem
2
. This equality has a simple geometric interpretation: to be true, any half-line in
Im(A)\times\mathcal{D}_{A}
has to intersect the manifold defined by
\{(A(\mu),\mu),\mu\in\mathcal{D}_{A}\}
.

Theorem 7
.

If for all
\tau\in\mathcal{T}
,
c_{\tau}=0
and
A
is continuous defined on a cone with
Im(A)=\mathbb{R}^{+}
such that
A(0)=0
,
\nabla A(0)=0
and
\lim_{\|\mu\|\to\infty}\frac{A(\mu)}{\|\mu\|}=+\infty
we have
\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{T}}=\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{T}}
.

Proof.

Consider
\tau
in
\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{T}}
. For all
\tau^{\prime}\neq\tau
, the inverse image of the open set
(0,+\infty)
by linear functions
g_{\tau}-g_{\tau^{\prime}}
is a non-empty intersection of half-spaces. Moreover, there exists
(\lambda_{0},\mu_{0})\in Im(A)\times\mathcal{D}_{A}
such that for all
\tau^{\prime}\neq\tau
in
\mathcal{T}
we have:
g_{\tau}(\lambda_{0},\mu_{0})>g_{\tau^{\prime}}(\lambda_{0},\mu_{0})
. Using condition
c_{\tau}=c_{\tau^{\prime}}=0
and
\alpha>0
we get by linearity

g_{\tau}(\alpha\lambda_{0},\alpha\mu_{0})=\alpha g_{\tau}(\lambda_{0},\mu_{0})>\alpha g_{\tau^{\prime}}(\lambda_{0},\mu_{0})=g_{\tau^{\prime}}(\alpha\lambda_{0},\alpha\mu_{0}).

Therefore, as all points of the half-line
\{\alpha(\lambda_{0},\mu_{0}),\alpha>0\}
belong to this set, it shows that index
\tau
is optimal on an open polyhedral cone.

Suppose that for the considered
(\lambda_{0},\mu_{0})
, there exists
\alpha_{0}>0
such that
\lambda_{0}=\frac{A(\alpha_{0}\mu_{0})}{\alpha_{0}}
then
\alpha_{0}\mu_{0}
is a value in the cone
\mathcal{D}_{A}
verifying all the constraints
f_{\tau}(\alpha_{0}\mu_{0})>f_{\tau^{\prime}}(\alpha_{0}\mu_{0})
, so that
\tau
also belongs to
\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{T}}
. In geometric terms, this means that any half-line in the polyhedral cone associated with index
\tau
intersects the manifold defined by
\{(A(\mu),\mu),\mu\in\mathcal{D}_{A}\}
.

It remains to prove that the proposed assumptions over
A
are sufficient to find such an alpha proving the set equality. We consider the same half-line
\{\alpha(\lambda_{0},\mu_{0}),\alpha>0\}
. By linear approximation in
0
, we have
A(\epsilon)=A(0)+\langle\epsilon,\nabla A(0)\rangle+o(\|\epsilon\|)=o(\|\epsilon\|)
and locally, we get
A(\alpha\mu_{0})=o(\|\alpha\mu_{0}\|)<\alpha\lambda_{0}
for small
\alpha
. Notice that as
Im(A)=\mathbb{R}^{+}
and the cone is open, we have
\lambda_{0}>0
and can choose
\|\mu_{0}\|>0
. The half-line is therefore above the manifold ("
(\alpha\lambda_{0},\alpha\mu_{0})>(A(\alpha\mu_{0}),\alpha\mu_{0})
").

Using condition
\lim_{\alpha\to\infty}\frac{A(\alpha\mu_{0})}{\|\alpha\mu_{0}\|}=+\infty
, continuity of
A
and previous local result
\frac{A(\alpha\mu_{0})}{\|\alpha\mu_{0}\|}=o(1)
for small
\alpha
, it shows that there exists
\alpha_{0}
such that
\frac{A(\alpha_{0}\mu_{0})}{\|\alpha_{0}\mu_{0}\|}=\frac{\lambda_{0}}{\|\mu_{0}\|}
(as
\frac{\lambda_{0}}{\|\mu_{0}\|}>0
). Thus
(\alpha_{0}\lambda_{0},\alpha_{0}\mu_{0})=(A(\alpha_{0}\mu_{0}),\alpha_{0}\mu_{0})
which leads to
f_{\tau}(\alpha_{0}\mu_{0})>f_{\tau^{\prime}}(\alpha_{0}\mu_{0})
and index
\tau
is in
\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{T}}
.
∎

Appendix C
Stirling numbers of the first kind in terms of the harmonic series

To obtain numerical values of
U^{p}_{n}
and
V_{n}^{p}
of the convex hull of
\{P(\tau)\}_{\tau\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}}
for
1\leq p\leq 5
we need to know the expressions of
\begin{bmatrix}n\\
m\\
\end{bmatrix}
for
m=0,\dots,6
. As shown in
[
58
]
, the general formula for the Stirling numbers of the first kind in terms of the harmonic series is

\begin{bmatrix}n\\
m\end{bmatrix}=\frac{(n-1)!}{(m-1)!}\omega(n,m-1)\,.

(14)

The
\omega
-sequence is defined recursively by

\omega(n,m)=\mathbbm{1}_{m=0}+\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}(1-m)_{k}\sigma_{k+1}\omega(n,m-1-k)\,,

where
\sigma_{k+1}=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\frac{1}{i^{k+1}}
is the partial sums of the harmonic series
\left\{\frac{1}{i^{k+1}}\right\}_{i=1,2,\dots}
and
(1-m)_{k}
are the Pochhammer symbols. Applying the formula (
14
) for
m=0,\dots,6
, we get

\begin{bmatrix}n\\
0\\
\end{bmatrix}=1\,,\quad\begin{bmatrix}n\\
1\\
\end{bmatrix}=(n-1)!\,,\\
\begin{bmatrix}n\\
2\\
\end{bmatrix}=(n-1)!\sigma_{1}\,,

\begin{bmatrix}n\\
3\\
\end{bmatrix}=\frac{(n-1)!}{2}(\sigma_{1}^{2}-\sigma_{2})\,,\quad\begin{bmatrix}n\\
4\\
\end{bmatrix}=(n-1)!\left(\frac{\sigma_{1}^{3}}{6}-\frac{\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}}{2}+\frac{\sigma_{3}}{3}\right)\,,

\begin{bmatrix}n\\
5\\
\end{bmatrix}=(n-1)!\left(\frac{\sigma_{1}^{4}}{24}-\frac{\sigma_{1}^{2}\sigma_{2}}{4}+\frac{\sigma_{1}\sigma_{3}}{3}+\frac{\sigma_{2}^{2}}{8}-\frac{\sigma_{4}}{4}\right)\,,

\begin{bmatrix}n\\
6\\
\end{bmatrix}=(n-1)!\left(\frac{\sigma_{1}^{5}}{120}-\frac{\sigma_{1}^{3}\sigma_{2}}{12}+\frac{\sigma_{1}^{2}\sigma_{3}}{6}+\frac{\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}^{2}}{8}-\frac{\sigma_{1}\sigma_{4}}{4}-\frac{\sigma_{2}\sigma_{3}}{6}+\frac{\sigma_{5}}{5}\right).

Appendix D
Detection of multiple changes in the parameter of a distribution in the exponential family

We define the set
\mathcal{M}_{n}^{K}
of all possible segmentations in
K
changepoints of time series
\{y_{t}\}_{t=1,\dots,n}
as:

\mathcal{M}_{n}^{K}=\{\tau=(\tau_{0},\tau_{1},\dots,\tau_{K},\tau_{K+1})\in\mathbb{N}^{K+2}|0=\tau_{0}<\tau_{1}<\dots<\tau_{K}<\tau_{K+1}=n\}.

For any segmentation
\tau
in
M_{n}^{K}
we define its size as
|\tau|=K
.
We denote
\mathcal{M}_{n}^{\infty}
as the set of all possible segmentations of
\{y_{t}\}_{t=1,\dots,n}
:

\mathcal{M}_{n}^{\infty}=\bigcup_{K}M_{n}^{K}\,,

(15)

and take the convention that
M_{n}^{\infty-1}=M_{n}^{\infty}\,
.

From a statistical perspective given a subset of all segmentations
\mathcal{M}_{n}^{K}
for
K
in
\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
it makes sense to recover the segmentation maximising a penalised likelihood of our (transformed as in Section
2.2
) data
\{x_{t}\}_{t=1,\dots,n}
:

C_{n}^{K}=\max_{\tau\in\mathcal{M}_{n}^{K}}\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{|\tau|+1}\max_{\eta_{k}}\left[\sum_{t=\tau_{k-1}+1}^{\tau_{k}}2\log(f_{X}(x_{t}|\eta_{k}))-\beta\right]\right\}\,,

(16)

where
\beta
is a penalty value in
\mathbb{R}^{+}
.

The previous equation encompasses two common multiple changepoints strategies. In the first, which we call Segment Neighbourhood (SN)
[
22
]
the user is given a maximum number of changepoints
K_{max}
and solve Equation (
16
) for all
K\leq K_{max}
(setting
\beta
to
0
). In the second step all these solutions are compared using an appropriate model selection criteria such as
[
28
,
73
]
to select the number of changepoints. In the second strategy, which we call Optimal Partitioning (OP)
[
25
]
the position and number of changepoints are estimated in one go solving (
16
) for

K=\infty
and picking an appropriate value for
\beta
such as
2\hat{\sigma}^{2}\log(n)
in a Gaussian setting with
p=1

[
74
]
where
\hat{\sigma}^{2}
is an estimate of the Gaussian variance.

Finding the maximum of (
16
) in either case can be done using dynamic programming.
For any segmentation
\tau
with
|\tau|>1
, observe that the first
|\tau|
terms in the previous sum are by definition smaller than
C_{\tau_{|\tau|}}^{K-1}
. Hence, we only need to consider the position of the last changepoint and we recover the update rule of the SN
[
22
]
and OP
[
25
]
algorithms:

C_{t}^{K}=\max_{i<t}\left\{C_{i}^{K-1}+\max_{\eta}\left[\sum_{s=i+1}^{t}2\log(f_{X}(x_{s}|\eta))-\beta\right]\right\}\ .

(17)

With this recursion solved for increasing
t
, and, in the case of SN, increasing
K
.

Directly solving this recursion has a computational cost that is quadratic in
n
. This has led to pruning ideas that speed up the computation whilst still solving the maximisation problem exactly. The idea of these pruning rules is that at any time
t
we need only consider a subset of possible changepoint locations. The most efficient pruning rules are based on so-called functional pruning (as in the pDPA or FPOP algorithms
[
32
,
33
]
). The idea is to consider the best segmentation of the data to time
t
as a function of the parameter of the final segment. For each value of this parameter there will be an optimal position of the most recent changepoint prior to
t
, and at the next iteration, we need only consider these positions and
t
for the possible position of the most recent changepoint prior to
t+1
. Implementing this algorithm requires calculating regions of the parameter space that are optimal for each value of the most recent changepoint. When
p=1
this is straightforward as we are manipulating intervals, but for
p>1
this becomes challenging, and often computationally prohibitive, as it involves computing the intersection and subtraction of many convex sets in
\mathbb{R}^{p}

[
35
,
36
]
.

D.1
Connection between the Detection of Multiple changepoints and the half-space intersection problem

In Section
4
, we established that the maximum likelihood estimator of a single changepoint problem corresponds to a vertex on the hull of a particular set of points. This result is primarily based on Theorem
4
, which follows from the Half-space Intersection Theorem
3
.

In the following theorem, we establish that the last changepoint of the maximum likelihood segmentation (see Equation (
16
)) of a multiple changepoint problem corresponds to a supporting hyperplane of a particular half-space intersection problem defined as in Theorem
3
.

Theorem 8
.

The last changepoint
\hat{\imath}
of the segmentation
\hat{\tau}
maximising the likelihood (
16
) is such that
H_{\hat{\imath}}
is a supporting hyperplane of
\cap_{i}H_{i}
where
i
is in
\{1,\ldots,n-1\}
and
H_{i}
is defined as the hyperplane

H_{i}=\{\kappa,\lambda,\mu\ |\ h_{i}(\kappa,\lambda,\mu)=a_{i}\lambda-2\langle b_{i},\mu\rangle+c_{i}-\kappa\leq 0\},

with
a_{i}=i
,
b_{i}=\sum_{t=1}^{i}x_{t}
and
c_{i}=C_{i}^{K-1}-\sum_{t=1}^{i}B^{\prime}(x_{t})
.

Proof.

We start from Equation (
16
). There is a value
\hat{\eta}
for which
\hat{\tau}
is optimal. Now if we subtract
\sum_{t=1}^{n}\Big\{2\log(f_{X}(x_{t}|\eta))-\beta\Big\}
,
\hat{\eta}
is still optimal for
\eta=\hat{\eta}
. Now applying Theorem
3
and restricting our attention to the last changepoint as explained for Equation (
9
)
we get the desired result.
∎

Contrary to the single changepoint case, we do not propose an algorithm based on this connection. Nonetheless, in a manner similar to the MdFOCuS algorithm, we think one could restrict the set of last changepoints to consider in the standard SN or OP algorithm using the output of a half-space intersection algorithm.

Appendix F
Dyadic MdFOCuS : an algorithm with a quasi-linear expected complexity

In this appendix we present an algorithm for online changepoint detection. In essence, as in the MdFOCuS algorithm of the main text, it maintains a set of points that is slightly larger than the set of points on the hull. It does so applying the
QuickHull
(or any convex hull) algorithm on successive and non-overlapping deterministic chunks of the points.
This deterministic choice of chunks is likely sub-optimal but it allows for a precise quantification of the expected complexity of the algorithm under the assumption of Theorem
6
(or Corollary
1
)
and under the assumption that the convex hull algorithm is at worst quadratic. To be specific we obtain a
\mathcal{O}(n\log(n)^{p+1})
bound on the expected time complexity.

The algorithm we propose (as one of the main text) considers at least all points on the hull to maximise the likelihood for every new data point. For a fixed
p
following, Theorem
6
and its corollary the number of points on the hull at step
i
is in
\Omega((\log(i))^{p}).

To recover a lower bound on the complexity, we first sum overall
i\leq n
, and then consider only
i\geq n/2
to recover that the overall number of points we will consider is in
\Omega(n/2(\log(n/2))^{p})
and thus
\Omega(n(\log(n))^{p}).
Relative to that our algorithm loses just one
\log(n)
term, we thus argue our bound is relatively tight.

The assumption on the quadratic complexity is true for
QuickHull
in dimensions 2 and 3 (so for
p\leq 2
)
[
48
,
63
]
and using the algorithm of Chazelle
[
64
]
it is also true in dimension 4 (so for
p\leq 3
). Still following Chazelle this is no longer true for any dimension strictly larger than
4
and therefore
p>3
.

We begin with two preliminary results. The first is a crude bound on the expectation of the square of the number of points on the convex hull of
n
points (that is
(V_{n}^{p})^{2}
following the notation of Section
4.2
) under the assumptions of Theorem
6
.
The second result bounds the expected complexity of computing the hull of
2n
points if we already know the points on the hull of the first
n
points and the last
n
points.
In all that follows we will assume that Assumptions
1
and
2
described in the main text are true. For ease of reading, we repeat them here.

Assumption 1
.

In any dimension
p\geq 1
, there are positive constants
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
for which the expected number of vertices,
\mathbb{E}[V_{n}^{p}]
, can be bounded as follows:

\mathbb{E}[V_{n}^{p}]\leq c_{1}\log(n)^{p}+c_{2}.

This is true under the conditions of Corollary
1
.

Assumption 2
.

We assume that the convex hull algorithm we are using is at worst quadratic in time and assume there exist two positive constants
c_{3}
and
c_{4}
such that for
n
points the time complexity is
Time(n)\leq c_{3}n^{2}+c_{4}
.

We now state our bound on the expectation of the square of the number of points on the hull
V_{n}^{p}
.

Lemma 3
.

\mathbb{E}[(V_{n}^{p})^{2}]\leq c_{1}n\log(n)^{p}+c_{2}n.

(18)

Proof.

V_{n}^{p}
is bounded by
0
and
n-1
. It follows that

\mathbb{E}[(V_{n}^{p})^{2}]=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\mathbb{P}(V_{n}^{p}=i)i^{2}\leq n\sum_{i=0}^{n}\mathbb{P}(V_{n}^{p}=i)i\leq n\mathbb{E}[V_{n}^{p}]\leq c_{1}n\log(n)^{p}+c_{2}n.

(19)

We get the last inequality with Assumption
1
.
∎

We now state our bound on the complexity of merging the hull of two successive chunks.

Lemma 4
.

Consider we have
2n
data points. Assume we know
\mathcal{W}_{1}
the index of the points on the hull of
\{P(\tau)\}_{1\leq\tau\leq n}
and
\mathcal{W}_{2}
the index of the points on the hull of
\{P(\tau)\}_{n+1\leq\tau\leq 2n}
. Under the condition of Corollary
1
and Assumption
2
computing the index of the points on the hull of
\{P(\tau)\}_{1\leq\tau\leq 2n}
can be done in less than
4c_{1}c_{3}n(\log n)^{p}+4c_{2}c_{3}n+c_{4}
time.

Proof.

We will compute those points as the points on the hull of
\{P(\tau)\}_{\tau\in\mathcal{W}_{1}\cup\mathcal{W}_{2}}
.
By Assumption
2
the time complexity of processing these
|\mathcal{W}_{1}|+|\mathcal{W}_{2}|
points is

Time\left(|\mathcal{W}_{1}|+|\mathcal{W}_{2}|\right)\leq c_{3}\left(|\mathcal{W}_{1}|+|\mathcal{W}_{2}|\right)^{2}+c_{4}\leq 2c_{3}\left(|\mathcal{W}_{1}|^{2}+|\mathcal{W}_{2}|^{2}\right)+c_{4}.

Now taking the expectation and noting that under the assumption of Corollary
1
the distribution of
|\mathcal{W}_{1}|
is the same as the distribution of
|\mathcal{W}_{2}|
we get

\mathbb{E}\left(Time\left(|\mathcal{W}_{1}|+|\mathcal{W}_{2}|\right)\right)\leq 4c_{3}\mathbb{E}\left(|\mathcal{W}_{1}|^{2}\right)+c_{4}.

Using (
18
)
we get that the expected complexity is

\mathbb{E}\left(Time\left(|\mathcal{W}_{1}|+|\mathcal{W}_{2}|\right)\right)\leq 4c_{1}c_{3}n(\log n)^{p}+4c_{2}c_{3}n+c_{4}.

(20)

∎

\displaystyle 2c_{1}(\log n)^{p+1}+2c_{2}\log n+2^{q_{min}}.

\mathcal{U}_{n+1}^{q}=\mathcal{U}_{n}^{q}
.
Therefore we directly get the indices of the point on the hull of
\mathcal{U}_{n+1}^{q}
as
\mathcal{U}_{n}^{q}\cap\mathcal{T}_{n}
because

\mathcal{W}_{n}^{q}=\text{{Hull}}\left(\{P(\tau)\}_{\tau\in\mathcal{U}_{n}^{q}}\right)=\mathcal{U}_{n}^{q}\cap\mathcal{T}_{n}\,.

In Algorithm

\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
.
If we choose
q_{min}
such that
2^{q_{min}}
is larger than
p+2
this is unlikely to happen for data drawn from a distribution with a continuous density.

We now continue with an informal description of Algorithm

.
Looking at line 4 when we include a new data point
n+1

•

n

. In that case for all

\mathcal{U}_{n+1}^{q}=\mathcal{U}_{n}^{q}
. We just need to add
n
as a possible changepoint to
\mathcal{T}_{n}
to recover
\mathcal{T}_{n+1}
. This was done in line 2.

•

n
is a multiple of
2^{q_{min}}.
In that case for some
q\geq q_{min}
we have that
\mathcal{U}_{n+1}^{q}
is different from
\mathcal{U}_{n}^{q}.
We therefore need to update the set of points using the hull algorithm.

For the later case let us define
q_{n}\geq q_{min}
as the largest integer such that
2^{q_{n}}

. Before we explain the update of lines 5, 6, and 7 note the following facts.

•

In base 2,
n

q_{n}

\overline{n}_{q_{n}}=1
and for all
q<q_{n}

\overline{n}_{q}=0
. Thus all
\mathcal{U}_{n+1}^{q}
with
q<q_{n}
should be empty and
\mathcal{U}_{n+1}^{q_{n}}
is not.

•

In base
2
,
n-1

q_{n}
ones. In other words
\overline{(n-1)}_{q_{n}}=0
and for all
q<q_{n}

\overline{(n-1)}_{q}=1
. Thus all
\mathcal{U}_{n}^{q}
with
q<q_{n}
are not empty.

•

We know the index of the points on the hull for all sets of indices
\mathcal{U}_{n}^{q}
with
q<q_{n}
:

\mathcal{W}_{n}^{q}=\text{{Hull}}\left(\{P(\tau)\}_{\tau\in\mathcal{U}_{n}^{q}}\right)=\mathcal{U}_{n}^{q}\cap\mathcal{T}_{n}.

•

Finally,
\mathcal{U}_{n+1}^{q_{n}}=(\cup_{q_{min}\leq q<q_{n}}\mathcal{U}_{n}^{q})\cup\{\tau|n-2^{q_{min}}<\tau\leq n\}

P(\tau)
for
\tau

\mathcal{U}_{n+1}^{q_{n}}

1.

Appendix G
An approximation for large
p

Based on Theorem
6
and its corollary at step

we expect Algorithm

\mathcal{O}(\log^{p}(n))

\tilde{p}

More formerly for any ordered subset

Appendix H
On the probability of false alarms, average-run-length and average detection delay in the multivariate Gaussian case

In the section we consider the multivariate Gaussian case. That is we consider a sequence of
\{y_{t}\}_{t=1,\ldots}
with elements
in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
, with
y_{t}=\mu_{t}+\varepsilon_{t}
, and
\varepsilon_{t}
in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
i.i.d.
\mathcal{N}_{p}(0,I_{p}).

We consider three types of statistics: a sparse statistics
S_{s,n}
, a dense statistics
D_{s,n}
and a one-dimensional statistics
O_{s,n}^{i}
defined as follow:

\displaystyle S_{s,n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\max_{i}\lvert O_{s,n}^{i}\rvert)^{2}\,,

\displaystyle D_{s,n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\biggl\lVert\frac{1}{\sqrt{(n-s)}}\sum_{t=s+1}^{n}\varepsilon_{t}\biggl\rVert_{2}^{2}\,,

\displaystyle O_{s,n}^{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{(n-s)}}\sum_{t=s+1}^{n}\varepsilon^{i}_{t}\,.

where
\varepsilon^{i}_{t}
is the
i-
th coordinate of
\varepsilon_{t}
in
\mathbb{R}^{p}.

In this appendix, using the arguably standard peeling arguments
[
65
,
68
]
on all three statistics we demonstrate (1) that by using a time-varying threshold (i.e. increasing with the number of observations
n
), we can control the probability of detecting a change when there is none, that is the probability of false alarms, (2) that by using some fixed (independent of time) threshold we can control the average run length (ARL), and (3) that for a fixed threshold we can control the difference between the detection time
T
and the true location of the change (that is the detection delay) in expectation and probability.

H.1
Probability of false alarms in the dense and sparse case

Corollary 2
.

Considering a time-varying threshold of
b^{\text{sparse}}_{n,\alpha}=8\log(np/\alpha)
for the sparse statistics and
b^{\text{dense}}_{n,\alpha}=p+2\sqrt{px_{n,\alpha}}+2x_{\alpha,n}
with
x_{n,\alpha}=4\log(n)-log(\alpha)+1
for the dense statistics we get that

1.

Assuming there is no change, the probability of detecting a change is bounded by
2\alpha
. More precisely

\mathbb{P}(\exists s,n\in\mathbb{N}^{2}\ |\ S_{s,n}>\ b^{\text{sparse}}_{n,\alpha})\leq\alpha\quad\text{, and}\quad\mathbb{P}(\exists s,n\in\mathbb{N}^{2}\ |\ D_{s,n}>\ b^{\text{dense}}_{n,\alpha}))\leq\alpha\,.

2.

Assuming there is a change at
\tau
the probability of detecting a change prior to
\tau
is bounded by
2\alpha.
More precisely

\mathbb{P}(\exists s,n\in\{1,\ldots,\tau\}^{2}\ |\ S_{s,n}>\ b^{\text{sparse}}_{n,\alpha})\leq\alpha\quad\text{, and}\quad\mathbb{P}(\exists s,n\in\{1,\ldots,\tau\}^{2}\ |\ D_{s,n}>\ b^{\text{dense}}_{n,\alpha}))\leq\alpha\,.

Proof.

We follow step 1 and step 2 of the proof of Theorem 1 in
[
65
]
using peeling concentration bounds of our Lemma
4
.
∎

Remark 8
.

We can also control the average run length with a fixed threshold. Let’s assume we want an average run length of

. Noting that
b^{\text{sparse}}_{n,\alpha}
and

are increasing with

H.2
Average detection delay for a fixed threshold

Assume that a change in mean occurs immediately after time
\tau
. Assume we are using a test statistic with a fixed threshold (following Remark
8
) and let
T
be the first time that our test statistic exceeds the threshold. The average detection delay is defined as

\mbox{ADD}=\sup_{y_{1},\ldots,y_{\tau}}\mathbb{E}(T-\tau|T\geq\tau).

That is, it is the worst-case expected time from the change until it is detected, conditional on no detection prior to
\tau
.

Lemma 3
.

For a change of size
\delta
, with
\lVert\delta\rVert_{2}^{2}
its square norm, the average detection delay for the dense statistic and threshold
c
satisfies

\mbox{ADD}\leq\frac{c+\lVert\delta\rVert_{2}\sqrt{8/\pi}}{\lVert\delta\rVert_{2}^{2}}+1.

Proof.

Let
\tau
be the time of the change, and
T
the time at which the dense statistic first exceeds
c
. Consider the test statistic for a change at
\tau
of size
\delta
, which at time
\tau+t
, for
t=1,2,\ldots
is of the form:

A_{t}=\sum_{i=1}^{t}\left(2\delta^{T}y_{\tau+i}-\lVert\delta\rVert_{2}^{2}\right).

(23)

Define
A_{0}=0
.
Now for any data
y_{1:t+\tau}
we have
A_{t}\leq\max_{s=0}^{\tau+t-1}D_{s,\tau+t}
. Let
T^{\prime}
be the time that
A_{t}
first exceeds
c
. It follows that conditional on
T>\tau
, we have
T^{\prime}\geq T-\tau
. Thus we can bound the
ADD
by
\mathbb{E}(T^{\prime})
.

Denote each term in the sum on the right-hand side of (
23
) by
a_{i}
. We have these terms are independent, normally distributed with mean
\mathbb{E}(a_{i})=\delta^{T}\delta=\lVert\delta\rVert_{2}^{2}
and variance
4\lVert\delta\rVert_{2}^{2}
. Let
\mu=\lVert\delta\rVert_{2}^{2}
. For each
t
it is trivial to see that
\mathbb{E}(|A_{t}|)<\infty
, and thus
A_{t}-t\mu
is a Martingale. It is also straightforward to show that
T^{\prime}
is a stopping time and
\mathbb{E}(T^{\prime})<\infty
so we can apply the optional stopping theorem. This gives

\mathbb{E}\left(A_{T^{\prime}}-T^{\prime}\mu\right)=0.

Thus
\mathbb{E}(T^{\prime})=\mathbb{E}(A_{T^{\prime}})/\mu
. We can bound
\mathbb{E}(A_{T^{\prime}})
from above by considering the distribution of
A_{T^{\prime}}
conditional on
A_{T^{\prime}-1}
. We have

\mathbb{E}(A_{T^{\prime}}|A_{T^{\prime}-1}=A)=\mathbb{E}(A+\mu+\sqrt{4\mu}\epsilon|A+\mu+\sqrt{4\mu}\epsilon\geq c),

where
\epsilon
has a standard normal distribution.
This expectation is monotone increasing in
A
, and as
A<c
we can bound
\mathbb{E}(A_{T^{\prime}})
by the value of the conditional expectation in the limit as
A\rightarrow c
from below. This gives

\mathbb{E}(A_{T^{\prime}})\leq\mathbb{E}(c+\mu+\sqrt{2\mu}|\epsilon|)=c+\mu+\sqrt{2\mu}\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}.

The result follows.
∎

Remark 9
.

It is simple to extend this result to get a bound on the average detection delay of the sparse statistic; as the same argument applies to the statistic that considers any given variate changing.

\lVert\delta\rVert_{2}
would simplify to
|\delta_{j}|
with
j
the variate that is changing.

\displaystyle 2\sqrt{x^{\prime}_{2\gamma,\alpha}}+\sqrt{4x^{\prime}_{2\gamma,\alpha}+2\sqrt{px_{2\gamma,\alpha}}+2\sqrt{px_{2\gamma,.25}}+2x_{2\gamma,.25}}.

Taking the square and using the fact that
(a+b)^{2}\leq 2a^{2}+2b^{2}
we get with probability at least
1-2\alpha
that the detection time is such that

\displaystyle T-\tau

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\lVert\delta\rVert_{2}^{2}}\left(16x^{\prime}_{2\gamma,\alpha}+4\sqrt{px_{2\gamma,\alpha}}+4\sqrt{px_{2\gamma,.25}}+4x_{2\gamma,.25}\right)+1\,.

Observe that numerator is in
\lVert\delta\rVert_{2}^{2}
and the denominator has no linear term in
p.

Lemma 4
.

Assume
\varepsilon_{t}
are i.i.d. Gaussian with identity variance matrix. For
\alpha
in
(0,1)
we have:

•

Sparse statistic

\mathbb{P}(\exists s,n\in\mathbb{N}\ |\ S_{s,n}>8\log(np/\alpha))\leq\alpha.

(24)

•

Dense statistic

Taking
x_{n,\alpha}=4\log(n)-log(\alpha)+1,
we have

\mathbb{P}(\exists s,n\in\mathbb{N}\ |\ D_{s,n}>p+2\sqrt{px_{n,\alpha}}+2x_{n,\alpha}))\leq\alpha\,;

(25)

\mathbb{P}(\exists s,n\in\mathbb{N}\ |\ D_{s,n}<p-2\sqrt{px_{n,\alpha}}))\leq\alpha.

(26)

Proof.

For the dense case, the proof follows the peeling argument of Lemma 8 in
[
65
]
replacing the tail bound from their sub-Gaussianity assumption with the upper and lower bound on the tail probability of a chi-square distribution with
p
degrees of freedom from Lemma 1 of
[
66
]
. For any non-decreasing sequence
b_{n}
applying a union bound (that is for any
n
events
A_{i}
with
i
in
\{1,\ldots n\}

\mathbb{P}(\cup_{i}A_{i})\leq max_{i}n\mathbb{P}(A_{i})
) several times we get:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\exists s,n\in\mathbb{N}\ |\ D_{s,n}>b_{n}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{2^{j}\leq n<2^{j+1}}\max_{1\leq s<n}D_{s,n}>b_{n}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}2^{j}\max_{2^{j}\leq n<2^{j+1}}\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{1\leq s<n}D_{n,s}>b_{n}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}2^{j}\max_{2^{j}\leq n<2^{j+1}}n\mathbb{P}\left(W^{2}>b_{n}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}2^{2j+1}\mathbb{P}\left(W^{2}>b_{2^{j}}^{2}\right),

where
W^{2}
has a chi-square distribution with
p
degrees of freedom. We then take
b_{n}^{2}=(p+2\sqrt{px^{\prime}_{\alpha,n}}+2x^{\prime}_{\alpha,n})

with
x^{\prime}_{\alpha,n}=2\log(n)+\log(\log(2n))+\log(\log(4n))-\log(2)-2\log(\log(2))-\log(\alpha)
. Using the tail-bound from

]
we get that

\displaystyle 2^{2j+1}\mathbb{P}(W^{2}>b_{2^{j}}^{2})\leq 2^{2j+1}\exp(-x^{\prime}_{\alpha,2^{2j}})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\exp((2j+1)\log(2)-2j\log(2)-\log(2)-\log((j+1))-\log((j+2))+\log(\alpha))

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha}{(j+1)(j+2)}.

Summing over all

\mathbb{P}(\exists s,n\in\mathbb{N}\ |\ S_{s,n}>b_{n}^{2})\leq\alpha.

Out of simplicity we consider

1\geq-\log(2)-2\log(\log(2)).

We get the lower bound (

]

For the sparse case we apply the peeling argument of Lemma 8 of

65
]

p

∎

Lemma 5
.

For a given
s
in
\mathbb{N}
and
i\in\{1,,\ldots,p\}
,

\mathbb{P}\biggl(\exists n\in\mathbb{N}\ |\ O_{s,n}^{i}>2\sqrt{2\log(\log(3(n-s)))-\log(\alpha)+1}\biggl)\leq\alpha.

Proof.

We follow the proof of Lemma 5 in
[
68
]
.
We highlight below the key steps.
First, based on their Lemma 4 we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\biggl(\max_{s+2^{j}\leq n<s+2^{j+1}}O_{s,n}^{i}>2x\biggl)\leq\exp{(-x^{2}/4)}\,.

(27)

Second, we apply a union bound over all

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\exists j\ \bigg|\ \sup_{s+2^{j}\leq n<s+2^{j+1}}O_{s,n}^{i}>2\sqrt{\log[(j+1)(j+2)]-\log(\alpha)}\right)\leq\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{\alpha}{(j+1)(j+2)}=\alpha.
