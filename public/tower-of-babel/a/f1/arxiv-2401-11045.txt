A new look to branching Brownian motion from a particle-based reaction–diffusion dynamics point of view
Alberto Lanconelli, Berk Tan Perçin

Abstract
Aim of this note is to analyse branching Brownian motion within the class of models introduced in the recent paper [ 4 ] and called chemical diffusion master equations. These models provide a description for the probabilistic evolution of chemical reaction kinetics associated with spatial diffusion of individual particles. We derive an infinite system of Fokker-Planck equations that rules the probabilistic evolution of the single particles generated by the branching mechanism and analyse its properties using Malliavin Calculus techniques, following the ideas proposed in [ 13 ] . Another key ingredient of our approach is the McKean representation for the solution of the Fisher-Kolmogorov-Petrovskii-Piskunov equation and a stochastic counterpart of that equation. We also derive the reaction-diffusion partial differential equation solved by the average concentration field of the branching Brownian system of particles.



Key words and phrases: Branching Brownian Motion, particle-based reaction-diffusion dynamics, Malliavin calculus.

AMS 2020 classification: 60H07; 60H30; 92E20.

Introduction and statement of the main results

Let
\{X_{k}(t),k\in\{1,...,\mathtt{n}(t)\}\}_{t\geq 0}
be a (binary) branching Brownian motion. That means, at time zero a single particle
\{X(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
starting at the origin begins to perform Brownian motion in
\mathbb{R}
; after an exponential time
\tau
of parameter one, the particle splits into two identical, independent copies of itself that start Brownian motion at
X(\tau)
. This process is repeated ad infinitum, producing a collection of
\mathtt{n}(t)
particles
\{X_{1}(t),X_{2}(t),...,X_{\mathtt{n}(t)}(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
(we refer the reader to
[
3
]
and the references quoted there for more details on the subject). For
t\geq 0
,
n\geq 1
and
A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
we set

\displaystyle\int_{A}\rho_{n}(t,y_{1},...,y_{n})dy_{1}\cdot\cdot\cdot dy_{n}

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{P}\left(\{\mathtt{n}(t)=n\}\cap\{(X_{1}(t),...,X_{n}(t))\in A\}\right);

(1.1)

notice that by construction the sequence
\{\rho_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
fulfils the constraint

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\rho_{n}(t,y_{1},...,y_{n})dy_{1}\cdot\cdot\cdot dy_{n}=1.

(1.2)

Furthermore, for all
n\geq 2
and
t\geq 0
the functions

\displaystyle(y_{1},...,y_{n})\mapsto\rho_{n}(t,y_{1},...,y_{n})

are symmetric in their arguments: this formalizes the indistinguishability of the different Brownian particles generated by the branching mechanism.

Our first main result reads as follows.

Theorem 1.1
.

The sequence
\{\rho_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
solves the system of equations

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}\rho_{n}(t,y_{1},...,y_{n})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k,l=1}^{n}\partial^{2}_{y_{k}y_{l}}\rho_{n}(t,y_{1},...,y_{n})+\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\rho_{k}\hat{\otimes}\rho_{n-k}(t,y_{1},...,y_{n})\\
\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\mbox{-}\rho_{n}(t,y_{1},...,y_{n}),\quad\mbox{ for }n\geq 1,t>0,y_{1},...,y_{n}\in\mathbb{R};\\
\rho_{1}(0,y_{1})=\delta_{0}(y_{1}),\quad y_{1}\in\mathbb{R};\\
\rho_{n}(0,y_{1},...,y_{n})=0,\quad y_{1},...,y_{n}\in\mathbb{R};\end{cases}

(1.3)

here,
\delta_{0}
stands for the Dirac delta function concentrated at the origin while
\hat{\otimes}
denotes symmetric tensor product with respect to the variables
(y_{1},...,y_{n})
. Moreover, we have the representation

\displaystyle\rho_{n}(t,y_{1},...,y_{n})=g_{n}(t,x;x-y_{1},...,x-y_{n}),\quad t\geq 0,x,y_{1},...,y_{n}\in\mathbb{R}

(1.4)

where
\{g_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
is a sequence of functions with

\displaystyle g_{n}:[0,+\infty[\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}

\displaystyle\to\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle(t,x,y_{1},...,y_{n})

\displaystyle\mapsto g_{n}(t,x;y_{1},...,y_{n})

and defined recursively as

\displaystyle\begin{cases}g_{1}(t,x;y_{1}):=e^{-t}\mathtt{p}_{t}(x-y_{1}),\quad t\geq 0,x,y_{1}\in\mathbb{R};\\
g_{n}(t,x;y_{1},...,y_{n}):=e^{-t}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathtt{p}_{t-s}(x-z)e^{s}\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}g_{k}\hat{\otimes}g_{n-k}(s,z;y_{1},...,y_{n})dzds,\\
\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad n\geq 2,t\geq 0\mbox{ and }x,y_{1},...,y_{n}\in\mathbb{R}.\end{cases}

The symbol
\mathtt{p}_{t}(x-z)
stands for the one dimensional heat kernel
(2\pi t)^{-\frac{1}{2}}e^{-\frac{(x-z)^{2}}{2t}}
.

Remark 1.2
.

If we view branching Brownian motion as a system of particles undergoing Brownian diffusion and splitting chemical reactions (as described above), then following
[
4
]
,
[
5
]
(see also
[
6
]
) we can interpret system (
1.3
) within the class of chemical diffusion master equations. Equation (
1.3
) has been previously derived in
[

]
and further developed in
[

]
but not treated as presented here.

Now, let
\{\rho_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
be solution to (

) and consider for
0\leq k\leq n
the quantity

\displaystyle p^{\varepsilon}_{k,n-k}(t,x):={n\choose k}\int_{B_{\varepsilon}(x)^{k}\times B^{c}_{\varepsilon}(x)^{n-k}}\rho_{n}(t,y_{1},...,y_{n})dy_{1}...dy_{n}.

(1.5)

This represents the probability of having
n
particles at time
t
with exactly
k
of the them in the ball centred at
x\in\mathbb{R}
and radius

; moreover,
writing

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}^{\varepsilon}(t,x):=\sum_{n\geq 1}\sum_{k=0}^{n}kp^{\varepsilon}_{k,n-k}(t,x)

(1.6)

we obtain the average number of particles that are located at time
t
in the ball
B_{\varepsilon}(x)
. Lastly, the average concentration of particles at

is obtained through the limit

\displaystyle c(t,x):=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{\mathcal{X}^{\varepsilon}(t,x)}{\mathtt{vol}(B_{\varepsilon}(x))},

and combining (
1.5
) with (

\{c(t,x)\}_{t\geq 0,x\in\mathbb{R}}
, which we call

, can be directly related to the sequence
\{\rho_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
via the formula

\displaystyle c(t,x)=\sum_{n\geq 1}n\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}\rho_{n}(t,y_{1},...,y_{n-1},x)dy_{1}...dy_{n-1},\quad t\geq 0,x\in\mathbb{R};

(1.7)

(for such derivation we refer to
[
4
]
). We are now ready to state our second main result.

Theorem 1.3
.

The average concentration field (
1.7
) associated with the system (
1.3
) solves the reaction-diffusion partial differential equation

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}c(t,x)=\frac{1}{2}\partial_{x}^{2}c(t,x)+c(t,x),\quad t>0,x\in\mathbb{R};\\
c(0,x)=\delta_{0}(x),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}.\end{cases}

(1.8)

The paper is organized as follows: Section 3 contains the proof of Theorem
1.1

Here, we also collect some notions and results from Malliavin Calculus needed for the proof of our main theorems: the implementation of these techniques represents one of the principal novelties of our contribution; then, in Section 4 we describe the proof of Theorem
1.3
which is inspired by some ideas from
[
13
]
.

Proof of Theorem
1.1

It is well known
[
17
,
3
]
that, for a continuous function
f:\mathbb{R}\to[0,1]
and branching Brownian motion
\{X_{k}(t):k\leq\mathtt{n}(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
, the function

(t,x)\mapsto u(t,x):=\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{k=1}^{\mathtt{n}(t)}f(x-X_{k}(t))\right]

(2.1)

is solution to the Fisher-Kolmogorov-Petrovskii-Piskunov equation
[
7
,
11
]

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u(t,x)=\frac{1}{2}\partial_{xx}u(t,x)+u(t,x)^{2}-u(t,x),&t>0,x\in\mathbb{R};\\
u(0,x)=f(x),&x\in\mathbb{R}.\end{cases}

(2.2)

According to such representation and in view of (
1.1
) we can write by means of the Law of Total Probability that

\displaystyle u(t,x)=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{k=1}^{\mathtt{n}(t)}f(x-X_{k}(t))\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{k=1}^{\mathtt{n}(t)}f(x-X_{k}(t))\Bigg|\mathtt{n}(t)=n,X(t)=y\right]\rho_{n}(t,y)dy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\prod_{k=1}^{n}f(x-y_{k})\rho_{n}(t,y)dy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\prod_{k=1}^{n}f(y_{k})\rho_{n}(t,x\mathtt{1}_{n}-y)dy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f^{\otimes n}(y)\rho_{n}(t,x\mathtt{1}_{n}-y)dy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\langle\hat{U}(t,x),\mathtt{E}(f)\rangle\rangle,

(2.3)

where for
n\geq 1
we used the notation
\mathtt{1}_{n}:=(1,1,...,1)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and set

\displaystyle\hat{U}(t,x):=\sum_{n\geq 1}I_{n}(\rho_{n}(t,x\mathtt{1}_{n}-\cdot)),\quad t\geq 0,x\in\mathbb{R}.

(2.4)

In (
2.4
) the notation
I_{n}
stands for
n
-th order multiple Itô integral with respect to an auxiliary two sided Brownian motion, say
\{B_{y}\}_{y\in\mathbb{R}}
. Moreover, in (
2
) the brackets
\langle\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle\rangle
denote dual pairing between the generalized random variable
\hat{U}(t,x)
and the
stochastic exponential

\mathtt{E}(f)
, which is the smooth random variable defined by

\displaystyle\mathtt{E}(f):=\sum_{n\geq 0}I_{n}\left(\frac{f^{\otimes n}}{n!}\right)=\exp\left\{I_{1}(f)-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}f^{2}(y)dy\right\},\quad f\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}).

For more details on smooth and generalized random variables we refer the reader to
[
8
]
and
[
12
]
.

We now want to relate the expression in (
2.4
) with the solution to a Wick-type stochastic partial differential equation investigated in
[
16
]
. In the sequel, the symbol
\diamond
will denote the so-called
Wick product
: given two generalized random variables
X
and
Y
, their Wick product is defined to be the unique element
X\diamond Y
such that

\displaystyle\langle\langle X\diamond Y,\mathtt{E}(f)\rangle\rangle=\langle\langle X,\mathtt{E}(f)\rangle\rangle\langle\langle Y,\mathtt{E}(f)\rangle\rangle,\quad\mbox{for all }f\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}).

Alternatively, if

\displaystyle X=\sum_{n\geq 0}I_{n}(h_{n})\quad\mbox{ and }\quad X=\sum_{n\geq 0}I_{n}(l_{n}),

then

\displaystyle X\diamond Y=\sum_{n\geq 0}I_{n}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{n}h_{k}\hat{\otimes}l_{n-k}\right).

The transformation

\displaystyle X\mapsto\mathcal{S}(X)(f):=\langle\langle X,\mathtt{E}(f)\rangle\rangle=\sum_{n\geq 0}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}h_{n}(x)f^{\otimes n}(x)dx,\quad f\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}),

is injective and called
\mathcal{S}
-transform of
X
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\{U(t,x)\}_{t\geq 0,x\in\mathbb{R}}
be the unique solution to

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}U(t,x)=\frac{1}{2}\partial^{2}_{x}U(t,x)+U(t,x)^{\diamond 2}-U(t,x),&t>0,x\in\mathbb{R};\\
U(0,x)=W_{x},&x\in\mathbb{R};\end{cases}

(2.5)

here,
W_{x}
denotes the white noise at
x
, i.e.
W_{x}=I_{1}(\delta_{0}(x-\cdot))
. Then,

\displaystyle U(t,x)=\sum_{n\geq 1}I_{n}(g_{n}(t,x;\cdot)),\quad t\geq 0,x\in\mathbb{R}

where
\{g_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
is the sequence of functions

\displaystyle g_{n}:[0,+\infty[\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}

\displaystyle\to\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle(t,x,y_{1},...,y_{n})

\displaystyle\mapsto g_{n}(t,x;y_{1},...,y_{n})

solving the system of partial differential equations

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}g_{n}(t,x;y)=\frac{1}{2}\partial^{2}_{x}g_{n}(t,x;y)+\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}g_{k}\hat{\otimes}g_{n-k}(t,x;y)\\
\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad-g_{n}(t,x;y),\quad n\geq 1,t>0,x\in\mathbb{R},y\in\mathbb{R}^{n};\\
g_{1}(0,x;y_{1})=\delta_{0}(x-y_{1}),\quad x,y_{1}\in\mathbb{R};\\
g_{n}(0,x;y)=0,\quad n\geq 2,x\in\mathbb{R},y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.\end{cases}

(2.6)

Moreover, for
f\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
the function

\displaystyle(t,x)\mapsto(\mathcal{S}U(t,x))(f)=\langle\langle U(t,x),\mathtt{E}(f)\rangle\rangle

solves equation (
2.2
).

Proof.

For the notion of solution to the problem (
2.5
) we refer the reader to
[
16
]
. Projecting equation (
2.5
) onto chaos spaces of different orders yields a system of partial differential equations solved by the sequence of kernels
\{g_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
of
U(t,x)
; more precisely, recalling the definition of Wick product and the fact that the initial condition in (
2.5
) is made of a first order Wiener chaos one easily see that (
2.6
) corresponds to the aforementioned system of partial differential equations. On the other hand, thanks to the interplay between Wick product and
S
-transform, i.e.

\displaystyle\langle\langle U(t,x)^{\diamond 2},\mathtt{E}(f)\rangle\rangle=\langle\langle U(t,x),\mathtt{E}(f)\rangle\rangle^{2},\quad t\geq 0,x\in\mathbb{R},f\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}),

and the identity

\displaystyle\langle\langle W_{x},\mathtt{E}(f)\rangle\rangle=f(x),\quad x\in\mathbb{R},f\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}),

we see that an application of the
S
-transform to both sides of (
2.5
) reduces this problem to (
2.2
).
∎

Remark 2.2
.

The triangular structure of system (
2.6
) allows for an explicit recursive representation of its solution; namely,

\displaystyle\begin{cases}g_{1}(t,x;y_{1}):=e^{-t}\mathtt{p}_{t}(x-y_{1}),\quad t\geq 0,x,y_{1}\in\mathbb{R};\\
g_{n}(t,x;y_{1},...,y_{n}):=e^{-t}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathtt{p}_{t-s}(x-z)e^{s}\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}g_{k}\hat{\otimes}g_{n-k}(s,z;y_{1},...,y_{n})dzds,\\
\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad n\geq 2,t\geq 0\mbox{ and }x,y_{1},...,y_{n}\in\mathbb{R}.\end{cases}

Now, employing the conclusion of Lemma
2.1
in combination with (
2
) we can affirm that

\displaystyle\langle\langle\hat{U}(t,x),\mathtt{E}(f)\rangle\rangle=\langle\langle U(t,x),\mathtt{E}(f)\rangle\rangle\quad\mbox{for all $t\geq 0$, $x\in\mathbb{R}$ and $f\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})$};

from the invertibility of the
S
-transform and uniqueness of chaos expansion’s kernels we then deduce

\displaystyle\rho_{n}(t,x\mathtt{1}_{n}-y)=g_{n}(t,x;y),\quad\mbox{ for all }n\geq 1,t\geq 0,x\in\mathbb{R},y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

and thus

\displaystyle\rho_{n}(t,y_{1},...,y_{n})=

\displaystyle\rho_{n}(t,x-(x-y_{1}),...,x-(x-y_{n}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle g_{n}(t,x;x-y_{1},...,x-y_{n}),\quad n\geq 1,t\geq 0,x,y_{1},...,y_{n}\in\mathbb{R},

which corresponds to (
1.4
). Lastly, recalling that the sequence
\{g_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
solves the system of equations (
2.6
) we have

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho_{n}(t,x\mathtt{1}_{n}-y)=

\displaystyle\partial_{t}g_{n}(t,x;y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\partial^{2}_{x}g_{n}(t,x;y)+\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}g_{k}\hat{\otimes}g_{n-k}(t,x;y)-g_{n}(t,x;y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\partial^{2}_{x}(\rho_{n}(t,x\mathtt{1}_{n}-y))+\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\rho_{k}\hat{\otimes}\rho_{n-k}(t,x\mathtt{1}_{n}-y)-\rho_{n}(t,x\mathtt{1}_{n}-y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j,k=1}^{n}\partial^{2}_{y_{j}y_{k}}\rho_{n}(t,x\mathtt{1}_{n}-y)+\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\rho_{k}\hat{\otimes}\rho_{n-k}(t,x\mathtt{1}_{n}-y)-\rho_{n}(t,x\mathtt{1}_{n}-y);

if we now replace
x\mathtt{1}_{n}-y
with
y
we obtain the desired system (
1.3
), completing the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Remark 2.3
.

One can see the interplay between the different Brownian particles generated by the branching mechanism in the diffusion term

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k,l=1}^{n}\partial^{2}_{y_{k}y_{l}}\rho_{n}(t,y_{1},...,y_{n}).

This is due to the fact that the newly created particles do not emerge at an independent random location but enter to the system at the same exact location of the parent particle. Such feature is in contrast with the models considered in
[
4
]
and
[
5
]
where diffusion is described by a simple Laplace operator.

Proof of Theorem
1.3

Inspired by the ideas presented in
[

]
and further developed in
[
15
]
and
[
14
]
we now rewrite the system of equations (
1.3
) as a single abstract equation containing Malliavin Calculus operators. For details on the subject the reader is referred to the books
[
9
]
,
[
10
]
and
[
18
]
; here, we recall few basic definitions and properties needed for proving our result. In the sequel, we denote by
\{D_{x}\Phi\}_{x\in\mathbb{R}}
the
Malliavin derivative
of
\Phi=\sum_{n\geq 0}I_{n}(h_{n})
defined as

\displaystyle D_{x}\Phi:=\sum_{n\geq 1}nI_{n-1}(h_{n}(\cdot,x)),\quad x\in\mathbb{R};

notice that

\displaystyle D_{x}\mathtt{E}(f)=f(x)\mathtt{E}(f),\quad\mbox{ for all }f\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}).

Moreover, for a possibly unbounded
A:L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
we define its
differential second quantization operator
as

\displaystyle d\Gamma(A)\Phi:=\sum_{n\geq 1}I_{n}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}h_{n}\right),

where
A_{i}
stands for the operator
A
acting on the
i
-th variable of
h_{n}
.
The following useful identities hold true:

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\langle\langle d\Gamma(A)\Phi,1\rangle\rangle=0;\quad\langle\langle d\Gamma(A)\Phi,\Psi\rangle\rangle=\langle\langle\Phi,d\Gamma(A^{\star})\Psi\rangle\rangle;\\
&d\Gamma(A)\mathtt{E}(f)=\mathtt{E}(f)\diamond I_{1}(Af);\quad D_{x}(\Phi\diamond\Psi)=D_{x}\Phi\diamond\Psi+\Phi\diamond D_{x}\Psi.\end{split}

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\{\rho_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
be solution to (
1.3
). Then, the generalized stochastic process

\displaystyle\Phi(t):=\sum_{n\geq 0}I_{n}(\rho_{n}(t,\cdot)),\quad t\geq 0,

(3.1)

solves the abstract equation

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}\Phi(t)=\frac{1}{2}d\Gamma(\partial)^{2}\Phi(t)+\Phi(t)^{\diamond 2}-\Phi(t),\quad t>0;\\
\Phi(0)=W_{0}.\end{cases}

(3.2)

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\Phi(t)=

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\sum_{n\geq 0}I_{n}(\rho_{n}(t,\cdot))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 0}I_{n}(\partial_{t}\rho_{n}(t,\cdot))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 0}I_{n}\left(\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k,l=1}^{n}\partial^{2}_{y_{k}y_{l}}\rho_{n}(t,\cdot)\right)+\sum_{n\geq 0}I_{n}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\rho_{k}\hat{\otimes}\rho_{n-k}(t,\cdot)\right)

\displaystyle-\sum_{n\geq 0}I_{n}(\rho_{n}(t,\cdot))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n\geq 0}d\Gamma(\partial)^{2}I_{n}\left(\rho_{n}(t,\cdot)\right)+\sum_{n\geq 0}I_{n}(\rho_{n}(t,\cdot))\diamond\sum_{n\geq 0}I_{n}(\rho_{n}(t,\cdot))

\displaystyle-\sum_{n\geq 0}I_{n}(\rho_{n}(t,\cdot))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}d\Gamma(\partial)^{2}\Phi(t)+\Phi(t)^{\diamond 2}-\Phi(t).

Here, we utilized (
1.3
) and the definitions of differential second quantization operator and Wick product. Moreover,

\displaystyle\Phi(0)=\sum_{n\geq 0}I_{n}(\rho_{n}(0,\cdot))=I_{1}(\delta_{0})=W_{0}.

∎

Remark 3.2
.

Notice that using the notation introduced in the previous lemma condition (
1.2
) reads

\displaystyle\langle\langle\Phi(t),\mathtt{E}(1)\rangle\rangle=1,\quad\mbox{ for all }t\geq 0.

The usefulness of switching from system (

) to equation (

Lemma 3.3
.

The average concentration field (
1.7
) enjoys the representation

\displaystyle c(t,x)=\langle\langle D_{x}\Phi(t),\mathtt{E}(1)\rangle\rangle,\quad t\geq 0,x\in\mathbb{R}.

(3.3)

Proof.

From (
3.1
) we can write

\displaystyle D_{x}\Phi(t)=D_{x}\sum_{n\geq 0}I_{n}(\rho_{n}(t,\cdot))=\sum_{n\geq 1}nI_{n-1}(\rho_{n}(t,\cdot,x));

therefore,

\displaystyle\langle\langle D_{x}\Phi(t),\mathtt{E}(1)\rangle\rangle=\sum_{n\geq 1}n\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}\rho_{n}(t,y_{1},...,y_{n-1},x)dy_{1}...dy_{n-1},

and since the last member above agrees with (
1.7
) the validity of formula (

) is proven.
∎

We are now ready to prove Theorem
1.3
. By means of the two previous lemmas we get

\displaystyle\partial_{t}c(t,x)=

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\langle\langle D_{x}\Phi(t),\mathtt{E}(1)\rangle\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\langle\partial_{t}D_{x}\Phi(t),\mathtt{E}(1)\rangle\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\langle D_{x}\partial_{t}\Phi(t),\mathtt{E}(1)\rangle\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\langle D_{x}\left(\frac{1}{2}d\Gamma(\partial)^{2}\Phi(t)+\Phi(t)^{\diamond 2}-\Phi(t)\right),\mathtt{E}(1)\rangle\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\langle\langle D_{x}d\Gamma(\partial)^{2}\Phi(t),\mathtt{E}(1)\rangle\rangle+\langle\langle D_{x}\Phi(t)^{\diamond 2},\mathtt{E}(1)\rangle\rangle-\langle\langle D_{x}\Phi(t),\mathtt{E}(1)\rangle\rangle.

(3.4)

Moreover,

•

the following commutation relation holds:

\displaystyle D_{x}d\Gamma(\partial)=\left(d\Gamma(\partial)+\frac{d}{dx}\right)D_{x}.

(3.5)

In fact, checking its validity on stochastic exponentials we get

\displaystyle d\Gamma(\partial)D_{x}\mathtt{E}(h)=d\Gamma(\partial)\mathtt{E}(h)h(x)=h(x)\mathtt{E}(h)\diamond I_{1}(h^{\prime})

and

\displaystyle D_{x}d\Gamma(\partial)\mathtt{E}(h)=D_{x}\mathtt{E}(h)\diamond I_{1}(h^{\prime})=\mathtt{E}(h)h(x)\diamond I_{1}(h^{\prime})+\mathtt{E}(h)h^{\prime};
