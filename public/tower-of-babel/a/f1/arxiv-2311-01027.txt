Optimal L 2 L^{2} -growth of the generalized Rosenau equation Thanks: Corresponding author:ikehatar@hiroshima-u.ac.jp
Xiaoyan Li

Abstract
We report that the quantity measured in the L 2 L^{2} norm of the solution itself of the generalized Rosenau equation, which was completely unknown in this equation, grows in the proper order at time infinity. It is also immediately apparent that this growth aspect does not occur in three or more spatial dimensions, so we will apply the results obtained in this study to provide another proof that Hardy-type inequalities do not hold in the case of one or two spatial dimensions.

We consider the Cauchy problem of of the generalized Rosenau equation:

\displaystyle u_{tt}+\delta(-\Delta)^{\theta}u_{tt}+\mu\Delta^{2}u-\kappa\Delta u=0,\ \ \ (t,x)\in(0,\infty)\times{\bf R}^{n},

(1.1)

\displaystyle u(0,x)=u_{0}(x),\quad u_{t}(0,x)=u_{1}(x),\ x\in{\bf R}^{n},

(1.2)

where
\delta>0
,
\mu>0
,
\kappa>0
, and
0<\theta\leq 2
. Incidentally, we call it so because our target is the case
\theta=2
. We assume that, for the moment,
[u_{0}(x),u_{1}(x)]\in H^{2}({\bf R}^{n})\times H^{\theta}({\bf R}^{n})
.

The total energy
E(t)
for the solution to problem (
1.1
)-(
1.2
) is defined by

E(t):=\frac{1}{2}\left(\|u_{t}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}({\bf R}^{n})}^{2}+\delta\|(-\Delta)^{\frac{\theta}{2}}u_{t}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}({\bf R}^{n})}^{2}+\mu\|\Delta u(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}({\bf R}^{n})}^{2}+\kappa\|\nabla u(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}({\bf R}^{n})}^{2}\right).

(1.3)

Taking derivative of
t
on the both sides of (
1.3
), we see that

\frac{d}{dt}E(t)=0,

which implies the solution of (
1.1
)-(
1.2
) satisfies energy conservation law
E(t)=E(0)
for
t\geq 0
. However, we are very interested in the time behavior of the solution itself, since energy itself does not directly contain information about the solution itself.

Let us start with the Rosenau equation proposed by P. Rosenau
[
35
,
36
]
, which is one of main topics for mathematicians and physicists. The global existence and uniqueness of solution to the first order in time Rosenau equation were demonstrated by
[
30
]
(see also
[
31
]
). Concerning the second order in time Rosenau equation

\displaystyle u_{tt}+u_{xxxx}+u_{xxxxtt}-\gamma u_{xx}=f(u)_{xx},

(1.4)

the authors in
[
40
]
proved the existence and uniqueness of global solution under the assumption
f(u)=-\beta|u|^{p}u
(
\beta>0,~p>0
), and discussed the finite-time blow-up of solution with the aid of potential well method. When the hydrodynamical friction was taken into consideration, more general Rosenau equation

\displaystyle u_{tt}-\Delta u-\nu\Delta u_{t}+\Delta^{2}u+\Delta^{2}u_{tt}=\Delta f(u)

(1.5)

appeares. For (
1.5
), the global
existence and asymptotic behavior of the solution were showed in the time-weighted Sobolev spaces under smallness condition on the initial data
[
41
]
. Note that (
1.5
) and (
1.4
) are both nonlinear problems. Originally, Rosenau equations are devoted to some high
order nonlinear systems
[
35
]
.
However, it is natural that the results of corresponding linear part (i.e.
f(u)=0
) have some influence on the behavior of solutions to nonlinear problem because of Duhamel’s principle, such as the analyses in
[
41
]
.
Therefore, it is worth to consider the linear Rosenau equation (i.e.
f(u)=0
) and there are some interesting consequences related to linear Rosenau equation. For example, the author in
[
27
]
considers the initial value problem for

\displaystyle u_{tt}-\Delta u-\nu\Delta u_{t}+\Delta^{2}u+\Delta^{2}u_{tt}=0,

(1.6)

and presented the asymptotic estimates of solutions. Note that the equation (
1.6
) has a dissipative structure. In some literatures, (
1.6
) is also called the plate equation with rotational structure inertia (see
[
11
]
) or generalized Boussinesq equation with
double dispersive term (see
[
34
]
).
Such higher order hyperbolic equations with some dissipative structures are also studied by
many mathematicians (see
[
4
,
12
,
25
,
21
]
for plate equations and
[
38
,
24
]
for beam equations).

In this paper, we consider the Cauchy problem for the linear Rosenau equation (
1.1
)
and establish the optimal

-growth property of the solution itself in the case of
n=1,2
. To the best of our knowledge, to capture the optimal
L^{2}
-growth estimate of the solution itself in (
1.1
) is still an open problem, and it shows a singularity of the solution itself near low frequency zone. The Rosenau equation without any dissipative terms is a class of important partial differential equations, whatever the case, it should be studied.

The motivation of this paper includes two aspects. On the one hand, some traditional

-

On the other hand, in a series of papers (see, for example,

The Fourier transform

{\cal F}_{x\to\xi}(f)(\xi)=\hat{f}(\xi):=\displaystyle{\int_{{\bf R}^{n}}}e^{-ix\cdot\xi}f(x)dx,\hskip 9.24994pt\xi\in{\bf R}^{n},

as usual with

We denote the surface area of the

\omega_{n}:=\displaystyle{\int_{|\omega|=1}}d\omega
.
For each

I_{0,n}:=\|u_{1}\|_{L^{2}({\bf R}^{n})}+\|u_{1}\|_{L^{1}({\bf R}^{n})}.

The inner product of

is defined by

(f,g)=\int_{{\bf R}^{n}}f(x)\overline{g(x)}dx,~~~f,g\in L^{2}({\bf R}^{n}).

The equivalent inner products of

are denoted by

(u,v)_{H^{\theta}}=\int_{{\bf R}^{n}_{\xi}}(1+\delta|\xi|^{2\theta})\hat{u}\bar{\hat{v}}~d\xi\cong(u,v)+\delta\big((-\Delta)^{\frac{\theta}{2}}u,(-\Delta)^{\frac{\theta}{2}}v\big),

v(t,x)

\displaystyle v_{tt}-\delta\Delta v_{tt}+\mu\Delta^{2}v-\kappa\Delta v=u_{1},\ \ \ (t,x)\in(0,\infty)\times{\bf R}^{2},

(5.8)

\displaystyle v(0,x)=0,\quad v_{t}(0,x)=0,\ x\in{\bf R}^{2}.

(5.9)

This idea comes from
[
19
]
again. By multiplying both sides of (
5.8
) by
v_{t}(t,x)
, and integrating over
[0,t]\times{\bf R}^{2}
it follows that

\frac{1}{2}\|v_{t}(t,\cdot)\|^{2}+\frac{\delta}{2}\|\nabla v_{t}(t,\cdot)\|^{2}+\frac{\mu}{2}\|\Delta v(t,\cdot)\|^{2}+\frac{\kappa}{2}\|\nabla v(t,\cdot)\|^{2}=\int_{{\bf R}^{2}}u_{1}(x)v(t,x)dx.

(5.10)

Now, let us estimate the last term of (
5.10
) by using the inequality (
5.4
) whose formation is assumed to be.
Indeed, for any
\varepsilon>0
it follows that

\displaystyle\left|\int_{{\bf R}^{2}}u_{1}(x)v(t,x)dx\right|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{{\bf R}^{2}}w(x)|u_{1}(x)|\frac{|v(t,x)|}{w(x)}dx

\displaystyle\leq C_{\varepsilon}\int_{{\bf R}^{2}}w(x)^{2}|u_{1}(x)|^{2}dx+\varepsilon\int_{{\bf R}^{2}}\frac{|v(t,x)|^{2}}{w(x)^{2}}dx

\displaystyle\leq C_{\varepsilon}\int_{{\bf R}^{2}}w(x)^{2}|u_{1}(x)|^{2}dx+\varepsilon(C^{*})^{2}\|\nabla v(t,\cdot)\|^{2}

(5.11)

with some constant
C_{\varepsilon}>0
depending on
\varepsilon>0
. Thus, from (
5.10
) and (
5.1
) we find that

\frac{1}{2}\|v_{t}(t,\cdot)\|^{2}+\left(\frac{\kappa}{2}-\varepsilon(C^{*})^{2}\right)\|\nabla v(t,\cdot)\|^{2}\leq C_{\varepsilon}\int_{{\bf R}^{2}}w(x)^{2}|u_{1}(x)|^{2}dx<+\infty.

(5.12)

By choosing
\varepsilon>0
in (
5.12
) sufficiently small, one can arrive at the following crucial estimate:

\frac{1}{2}\|v_{t}(t,\cdot)\|^{2}\leq C_{\varepsilon}\int_{{\bf R}^{2}}w(x)^{2}|u_{1}(x)|^{2}dx.

(5.13)

Since
v_{t}=u
, one has the bounded estimate of the solution itself:

\|u(t,\cdot)\|^{2}\leq 2C_{\varepsilon}\int_{{\bf R}^{2}}w(x)^{2}|u_{1}(x)|^{2}dx<+\infty.

(5.14)

The estimate (
5.14
) contradicts (
5.7
) in the case of
n=2
. This shows the validity of the statement of Proposition
5.1
.

Incidentally, the above proof can be expressed in the form of a motto: ”If the inequality (
5.4
) is correct, then the
L^{2}
-boundedness of the solution itself can be derived”.

\Box

Remark 5.3

When we choose
w(x)=|x|(1+|\log|x||)
as the weight function, even if
u_{1}\in C_{0}^{\infty}({\bf R}^{2})
, it is not trivial to check
\int_{{\bf R}^{2}}w(x)^{2}|u_{1}(x)|^{2}dx<+\infty
. In this case it suffices to start with the proof by choosing
u_{1}\in C_{0}^{\infty}({\bf R}^{2})
satisfying
u_{1}(x)=0
near
x=0
and
\int_{{\bf R}^{2}}u_{1}(x)dx\neq 0
. This is feasible.

Remark 5.4

By basing on the similar argument above and using the growth results derived in
[
18
]

]
) in the case of

Corollary 5.1

n\geq 5
. Then there exists a constant
C^{*}>0
such that

\left\|\frac{u}{|x|^{2}}\right\|\leq C^{*}\|\Delta u\|

for all

.

The following proof is a slight modification of
[

.

Considering the energy space
\mathcal{H}:=H^{2}({\bf R}^{n})\times H^{\theta}({\bf R}^{n})
(
0\leq\theta\leq 2
), the inner product in
\mathcal{H}
is defined by

\big([u^{1},v^{1}],~[u^{2},v^{2}]\big)_{\mathcal{H}}=(u^{1},u^{2})_{H^{2}}+(v^{1},v^{2})_{H^{\theta}}.

Setting
v=u_{t}
and
A=-\Delta
, it follows from (
1.1
) that

(I+\delta A^{\theta})v_{t}=-(\mu A^{2}+\kappa A+I)u+u,

where
I
is the identity operator.
Then the problem (
1.1
)-(
1.2
) can be written as

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{d}{dt}U=\mathcal{A}U+\mathcal{B}U,\\
&U(0,x)=U_{0},\end{aligned}\right.

(6.1)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}=\begin{pmatrix}0&I\\
-P&0\end{pmatrix},~~~P=(I+\delta A^{\theta})^{-1}(\mu A^{2}+\kappa A+I),~~~~~~~

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
(I+\delta A^{\theta})^{-1}&0\end{pmatrix},~~~U=\begin{pmatrix}u\\
v\end{pmatrix}\in\mathcal{H},~~~U_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}u_{0}\\
u_{1}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathcal{H}.

Here
\mathcal{D}(P)
is defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(P)=\biggl\{

\displaystyle u\in H^{2}({\bf R}^{n}):\text{There exists}~y=y_{u}\in H^{\theta}({\bf R}^{n})~\text{such that}~

\displaystyle(u,\phi)+\kappa(A^{\frac{1}{2}}u,A^{\frac{1}{2}}\phi)+\mu(Au,A\phi)=(y,\phi)+\delta(A^{\frac{\theta}{2}}y,A^{\frac{\theta}{2}}\phi),~~\forall\phi\in H^{2}({\bf R}^{n})\biggr\}.

(6.2)

Note that
\mathcal{D}(P)
is not empty because
0\in\mathcal{D}(P)
when
y=0
. If
u\in\mathcal{D}(P)
, then
y\in H^{\theta}({\bf R}^{n})
such that (
6.2
) holds is uniquely determined. Otherwise, at least there exists
y^{1}
and
y^{2}\in H^{\theta}({\bf R}^{n})
satisfying

\displaystyle(u,\phi)+\kappa(A^{\frac{1}{2}}u,A^{\frac{1}{2}}\phi)+\mu(Au,A\phi)=(y_{1},\phi)+\delta(A^{\frac{\theta}{2}}y_{1},A^{\frac{\theta}{2}}\phi)

(6.3)

\displaystyle(u,\phi)+\kappa(A^{\frac{1}{2}}u,A^{\frac{1}{2}}\phi)+\mu(Au,A\phi)=(y_{2},\phi)+\delta(A^{\frac{\theta}{2}}y_{2},A^{\frac{\theta}{2}}\phi)

(6.4)

for all
\phi\in H^{2}({\bf R}^{n})
. Taking
\bar{y}=y^{1}-y^{2}\in H^{\theta}({\bf R}^{n})
, (
6.3
) and (
6.4
) imply

(\bar{y},\phi)+\delta(A^{\frac{\theta}{2}}\bar{y},A^{\frac{\theta}{2}}\phi)=0.

(6.5)

Owing that
C_{0}^{\infty}({\bf R}^{n})
is dense in
H^{2}({\bf R}^{n})
, (
6.5
) is still correct for all
\phi\in C_{0}^{\infty}({\bf R}^{n})
. Because
C_{0}^{\infty}({\bf R}^{n})
is also dense in
H^{\theta}({\bf R}^{n})
, we choose a sequence
\{y_{k}\}\in C_{0}^{\infty}({\bf R}^{n})
such that
\lim\limits_{k\rightarrow\infty}y_{k}\rightarrow\bar{y}
in
H^{\theta}({\bf R}^{n})
, that is

\displaystyle\|y_{k}-\bar{y}\|_{H^{\theta}}^{2}=\|\bar{y}\|_{H^{\theta}}^{2}-2(\bar{y},y_{k})_{H^{\theta}}+\|y_{k}\|_{H^{\theta}}^{2}\rightarrow 0,\hskip 9.24994pt(k\rightarrow\infty).

(6.6)

It follows from
\big|\|y_{k}\|_{H^{\theta}}-\|\bar{y}\|_{H^{\theta}}\big|\leq\|y_{k}-\bar{y}\|_{H^{\theta}}\rightarrow 0
that

\displaystyle\|y_{k}\|_{H^{\theta}}\rightarrow\|\bar{y}\|_{H^{\theta}},\hskip 9.24994pt(k\rightarrow\infty).

(6.7)

Combining (
6.6
) and (
6.7
) yields

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{k\rightarrow\infty}(\bar{y},y_{k})_{H^{\theta}}=\|\bar{y}\|_{H^{\theta}}^{2}.

(6.8)

By (
6.5
) and the definition of inner product in
H^{\theta}({\bf R}^{n})
, we have

\displaystyle 0=(\bar{y},y_{k})+\delta(A^{\frac{\theta}{2}}\bar{y},A^{\frac{\theta}{2}}y_{k})=(\bar{y},y_{k})_{H^{\theta}}

(6.9)

We conclude by (
6.8
) and (
6.9
) that

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{k\rightarrow\infty}(\bar{y},y_{k})_{H^{\theta}}=\|\bar{y}\|_{H^{\theta}}^{2}=0,

(6.10)

which implies
\bar{y}=0
in
H^{\theta}({\bf R}^{n})
, that is,
y^{1}=y^{2}
.

The above arguments show that the linear operator
P:u\rightarrow y=y_{u}
is well defined for each
u\in\mathcal{D}(P)
.

Next we demonstrate the fact

\mathcal{D}(P)=H^{4-\theta}({\bf R}^{n})

(6.11)

for
0\leq\theta\leq 2
by Lemma
6.1
and
6.2
below.

Lemma 6.1

If
0\leq\theta\leq 2
, it holds that
\mathcal{D}(P)\subset H^{4-\theta}({\bf R}^{n})
and there exists a constant
C>0
such that

\|u\|_{H^{4-\theta}}\leq C\|Pu\|_{H^{\theta}}

for all
u\in\mathcal{D}(P)
.

Proof.

For any given
u\in\mathcal{D}(P)
, there exists
y=y_{u}\in H^{\theta}
such that

(u,\phi)+\kappa(A^{\frac{1}{2}}u,A^{\frac{1}{2}}\phi)+\mu(Au,A\phi)=(y,\phi)+\delta(A^{\frac{\theta}{2}}y,A^{\frac{\theta}{2}}\phi),~~\forall\phi\in H^{2}({\bf R}^{n}).

(6.12)

Taking the Fourier transform on both sides of (
6.12
), we have

\int_{{\bf R}^{n}_{\xi}}(1+\kappa|\xi|^{2}+\mu|\xi|^{4})\hat{u}\bar{\hat{\phi}}~d\xi=\int_{{\bf R}^{n}_{\xi}}(1+\delta|\xi|^{2\theta})\hat{y}\bar{\hat{\phi}}~d\xi.

Due to the arbitrariness of
\phi
, we can obtain
(1+\kappa|\xi|^{2}+\mu|\xi|^{4})\hat{u}=(1+\delta|\xi|^{2\theta})\hat{y}
, that is

\hat{y}=\widehat{Pu}=\frac{1+\kappa|\xi|^{2}+\mu|\xi|^{4}}{1+\delta|\xi|^{2\theta}}\hat{u}.

(6.13)

Calculating the
L^{2}
-norm about
\xi
on both sides of (
6.13
) yields

\int_{{\bf R}^{n}_{\xi}}(1+\delta|\xi|^{2\theta})|\hat{y}|^{2}~d\xi=\int_{{\bf R}^{n}_{\xi}}\frac{(1+\kappa|\xi|^{2}+\mu|\xi|^{4})^{2}}{(1+\delta|\xi|^{2\theta})}|\hat{u}|^{2}~d\xi.

(6.14)

Note that
1+|\xi|^{2(4-\theta)}
can be controlled by
\frac{(1+\kappa|\xi|^{2}+\mu|\xi|^{4})^{2}}{(1+\delta|\xi|^{2\theta})}
.

In fact, if we set
r:=|\xi|
and

h(r):=\frac{(1+\kappa|\xi|^{2}+\mu|\xi|^{4})^{2}}{(1+\delta|\xi|^{2\theta})}=\frac{(1+\kappa r^{2}+\mu r^{4})^{2}}{(1+\delta r^{2\theta})},

we easily see that

\lim_{r\to 0}\frac{h(r)}{1+r^{2(4-\theta)}}=1,

and

\lim_{r\to+\infty}\frac{h(r)}{1+r^{2(4-\theta)}}=\frac{\mu^{2}+2\kappa\mu}{1+\delta}:=c_{0}>0.

(At this stage, it must be
\mu>0
.) Thus, there are positive constants
r_{0}\ll 1
and
r_{\infty}\gg 1
satisfying
0<r_{0}<1<r_{\infty}
such that

\frac{1}{2}\leq\frac{h(r)}{1+r^{2(4-\theta)}}\hskip 9.24994pt(0\leq\forall r\leq r_{0}),

\frac{c_{0}}{2}\leq\frac{h(r)}{1+r^{2(4-\theta)}}\hskip 9.24994pt(r_{\infty}\leq\forall r).

Furthermore, because of the continuity of the function
r\mapsto\frac{h(r)}{1+r^{2(4-\theta)}}
there is a constant
m>0
such that

m\leq\frac{h(r)}{1+r^{2(4-\theta)}}\hskip 9.24994pt(r_{0}\leq\forall r\leq r_{\infty}).

Set

M:=\min\{\frac{1}{2},m,\frac{c_{0}}{2}\}.

Then, one can get the lower bound estimate:

\frac{(1+\kappa|\xi|^{2}+\mu|\xi|^{4})^{2}}{(1+\delta|\xi|^{2\theta})}\geq M(1+|\xi|^{2(4-\theta)})\hskip 9.24994pt(\forall\xi\in{\bf R}_{\xi}^{n}).

Therefore, by (
6.14
), we have

\int_{{\bf R}^{n}_{\xi}}(1+\delta|\xi|^{2\theta})|\hat{y}|^{2}~d\xi\geq M\int_{{\bf R}^{n}_{\xi}}(1+|\xi|^{2(4-\theta)})|\hat{u}|^{2}~d\xi,

(6.15)

which implies

\|y\|_{H^{\theta}}^{2}\geq M\|u\|_{H^{4-\theta}}^{2}.

(6.16)

Thanks to
y=Pu\in H^{\theta}({\bf R}^{n})
, we can obtain
u\in H^{4-\theta}({\bf R}^{n})
.

\Box

Lemma 6.2

If
0\leq\theta\leq 2
, it holds that
H^{4-\theta}({\bf R}^{n})\subset\mathcal{D}(P)
.

Proof.

For any given
u\in H^{4-\theta}({\bf R}^{n})
, we define the linear functional
G_{u}:H^{\theta}({\bf R}^{n})\rightarrow{\bf R}
as

\left\langle G_{u},\psi\right\rangle=(u,\psi)+\kappa(Au,\psi)+\mu(A^{2-\frac{\theta}{2}}u,A^{\frac{\theta}{2}}\psi),~~~~\forall\psi\in H^{\theta}({\bf R}^{n}).

(6.17)

It is not difficult to see that the linear functional
G_{u}
is bounded. In fact, it follows from
u\in H^{4-\theta}({\bf R}^{n})
and
\psi\in H^{\theta}({\bf R}^{n})
that

\displaystyle\left|\left\langle G_{u},\psi\right\rangle\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|(u,\psi)\right|+\kappa\left|(Au,\psi)\right|+\mu\left|(A^{2-\frac{\theta}{2}}u,A^{\frac{\theta}{2}}\psi)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\|\hat{u}\|~\|\hat{\psi}\|+\kappa\|\>|\xi|^{2}\hat{u}\|~\|\hat{\psi}\|+\mu\|\>|\xi|^{4-\theta}\hat{u}\|~\|\>|\xi|^{\theta}\hat{\psi}\|

\displaystyle\leq(1+\kappa+\mu)\|u\|_{H^{4-\theta}}\|\psi\|_{H^{\theta}}.

Therefore, we have
G_{u}\in H^{-\theta}{({\bf R}^{n})}
.

We also need to define a bilinear functional
a(\cdot,\cdot)
:
H^{\theta}({\bf R}^{n})\times H^{\theta}({\bf R}^{n})\rightarrow{\bf R}
as follows

a(\psi_{1},\psi_{2})=(\psi_{1},\psi_{2})+\delta(A^{\frac{\theta}{2}}\psi_{1},A^{\frac{\theta}{2}}\psi_{2}),~~~~~~~~\forall~\psi_{1},\psi_{2}\in H^{\theta}({\bf R}^{n}).

(6.18)

Note that the bilinear functional
a(\cdot,\cdot)
are continuous and coercive because

|a(\psi_{1},\psi_{2})|\leq\|\hat{\psi}_{1}\|~\|\psi_{2}\|+\|\;|\xi|^{\theta}\hat{\psi}_{1}\|~\|\;|\xi|^{\theta}\hat{\psi}_{2}\|\leq 2\|\psi_{1}\|_{H^{\theta}}\|\psi_{2}\|_{H^{\theta}},

and

a(\psi_{1},\psi_{1})=\|\psi_{1}\|_{H^{\theta}}^{2}.

Thus, by the Lax-Milgram theorem, for any given
u\in H^{4-\theta}({\bf R}^{n})
, there exists a unique
y=y_{u}\in H^{\theta}({\bf R}^{n})
such that (
6.19
) below holds:

\left\langle G_{u},\psi\right\rangle=(y,\psi),\forall u\in H^{4-\theta}({\bf R}^{n}).

(6.19)

Next it is necessary to modify the regularity of
\psi
. Due to
0\leq\theta\leq 2
and
H^{2}({\bf R}^{n})\subset H^{\theta}({\bf R}^{n})
, we can require test functions
\psi\in H^{2}({\bf R}^{n})
such that

(Au,\psi)=(A^{\frac{1}{2}}u,A^{\frac{1}{2}}\psi),~~~~~~~~~(A^{2-\frac{\theta}{2}}u,A^{\frac{\theta}{2}}\psi)=(Au,A\psi).

(6.20)

Therefore, under the condition
\psi\in H^{2}({\bf R}^{n})
, substituting (
6.20
) to (
6.19
) yields

(u,\psi)+\kappa(A^{\frac{1}{2}}u,A^{\frac{1}{2}}\psi)+\mu(Au,A\psi)=(y,\psi)+\delta(A^{\frac{\theta}{2}}y,A^{\frac{\theta}{2}}\psi).

(6.21)

This implies
u\in\mathcal{D}(P)
, which completes the proof of the statement
H^{4-\theta}({\bf R}^{n})\subset\mathcal{D}(P)
.

\Box

The fact (
6.11
) can be guaranteed by Lemmas
6.1
and
6.2
. Basing on this fact one can prove the crucial lemma.

Lemma 6.3

The linear operator

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}=\begin{pmatrix}0&I\\
-P&0\end{pmatrix}:\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})\rightarrow\mathcal{H}

generates a strongly continuous contraction semigroup
T(t)
on
\mathcal{H}
, where

\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})=H^{4-\theta}({\bf R}^{n})\times H^{2}({\bf R}^{n})=\mathcal{D}(P)\times H^{2}({\bf R}^{n}).

Proof.

First, we prove that the operator
\mathcal{A}
is dissipative. For any given
U=[u,v]^{T}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})
, it follows from
(
6.13
)
that

\displaystyle\big(\mathcal{A}U,U\big)_{\mathcal{H}}

\displaystyle=\big([v,-Pu],~[u,v]\big)_{\mathcal{H}}

\displaystyle=(v,u)_{H^{2}}-(Pu,v)_{H^{\theta}}

\displaystyle=\int_{{\bf R}^{n}}(1+\kappa|\xi|^{2}+\mu|\xi|^{4})\hat{v}\bar{\hat{u}}~d\xi-\int_{{\bf R}^{n}}(1+\delta|\xi|^{2\theta})\widehat{Pu}\bar{\hat{v}}~d\xi

\displaystyle=\int_{{\bf R}^{n}}(1+\kappa|\xi|^{2}+\mu|\xi|^{4})(\hat{v}\bar{\hat{u}}-\hat{u}\bar{\hat{v}})~d\xi

\displaystyle=2i\int_{{\bf R}^{n}}(1+\kappa|\xi|^{2}+\mu|\xi|^{4})\text{Img}(\hat{u}\bar{\hat{v}})~d\xi,

(6.22)

which implies

{\text{R}e}\big(\mathcal{A}U,U\big)_{\mathcal{H}}=0.

Next we prove that
\mathcal{I}-\mathcal{A}
is surjective, that is, for any given
[f,g]^{T}\in\mathcal{H}
, we need to find
[u,v]^{T}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})
such that

\displaystyle(\mathcal{I}-\mathcal{A})\begin{pmatrix}u\\
v\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}f\\
g\end{pmatrix},

(6.23)

which is equivalent to

\displaystyle u-v=f,

(6.24)

\displaystyle Pu+v=g.

(6.25)

Substituting (
6.24
) into (
6.25
) leads to

Pu+u=f+g\in H^{\theta}({\bf R}^{n}).

(6.26)

Define the bilinear functional on
H^{2}({\bf R}^{n})
as follows

~~~~~~~B(\psi_{1},\psi_{2})=(\psi_{1},\psi_{2})_{H^{2}}+(\psi_{1},\psi_{2})_{H^{\theta}},~~~~\forall~\psi_{1},\psi_{2}\in H^{2}({\bf R}^{n}).

It is not difficult to see that the bilinear functional
B(\psi_{1},\psi_{2})
is continuous and coercive, that is,

|B(\psi_{1},\psi_{2})|\leq\|\psi_{1}\|_{H^{2}}\|\psi_{2}\|_{H^{2}}+\|\psi_{1}\|_{H^{\theta}}\|\psi_{2}\|_{H^{\theta}}\leq C\|\psi_{1}\|_{H^{2}}\|\psi_{2}\|_{H^{2}},

B(\psi_{1},\psi_{1})|=\|\psi_{1}\|_{H^{2}}^{2}+\|\psi_{1}\|_{H^{\theta}}^{2}\geq\|\psi_{1}\|_{H^{2}}^{2}

with some constant
C>0
. Meanwhile, we define a bounded linear functional
F
in
H^{2}({\bf R}^{n})

~~~~~\left\langle F,\psi\right\rangle=(f+g,\psi)_{H^{\theta}},~~~~\forall~\psi\in H^{2}({\bf R}^{n})\subset H^{\theta}({\bf R}^{n}).

By relying on the Lax-Milgram theorem, there exists a unique (weak) solution
u\in H^{2}({\bf R}^{n})
satisfying

~~~~~B(u,\psi)=\left\langle F,\psi\right\rangle,~~~~\forall~\psi\in H^{2}({\bf R}^{n})\subset H^{\theta}({\bf R}^{n}),

which is consistent with the weak form of (
6.26
). Here we just proved the existence of the (weak) solution
u\in H^{2}({\bf R}^{n})
of (
6.26
).

Finally, we have to show that
u\in H^{4-\theta}({\bf R}^{n})=\mathcal{D}(P)
.
Taking the Fourier transform on both sides of (
6.26
), we have

\widehat{Pu}=\hat{g}+\hat{f}-\hat{u}.

(6.27)

Calculating the
H^{\theta}
-norm of (
6.27
) yields

\int_{{\bf R}^{n}_{\xi}}(1+\delta|\xi|^{2\theta})|\widehat{Pu}|^{2}~d\xi=\int_{{\bf R}^{n}_{\xi}}(1+|\xi|^{2\theta})|\hat{f}+\hat{g}-\hat{u}|^{2}~d\xi,

(6.28)

which implies

\|Pu\|_{H^{\theta}}^{2}=\|f+g-u\|_{H^{\theta}}^{2}\leq C(\|f\|_{H^{\theta}}^{2}+\|g\|_{H^{\theta}}^{2}+\|u\|_{H^{\theta}}^{2})

with some constant
C>0
. By Lemma
6.1
, we can obtain

\|u\|_{H^{4-\theta}}\leq C\|Pu\|_{H^{\theta}},

(6.29)

with some constant
C>0
, that is,
u\in H^{4-\theta}({\bf R}^{n})
(
\theta\in[0,2]
). Then
v
can be determined by
v=u-f\in H^{2}({\bf R}^{n})
.
Therefore, we complete the proof of the surjectivity of the mapping
\mathcal{I}-\mathcal{A}
.

By the Lumer-Phillips theorem, we conclude that the operator
\mathcal{A}
generates a strongly continuous contraction semigroup on
\mathcal{H}
.

\Box

Let us finalize the proof of Proposition
1.1
.

Proof of Proposition
1.1
.

By (
6.1
), Lemma
6.3
and semigroup theory of linear operators, we just need to prove that
\mathcal{B}U
is bounded in
\mathcal{H}
. In fact, it follows from the definition of
\mathcal{B}
that

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{B}U\|^{2}_{H^{2}\times H^{\theta}}

\displaystyle=\|(I+\delta A^{\theta})^{-1}v\|_{H^{\theta}}^{2}

(6.30)

\displaystyle=\int_{{\bf R}^{\xi}_{n}}(1+\delta|\xi|^{2\theta})\left|\frac{\hat{v}}{1+\delta|\xi|^{2\theta}}\right|^{2}d\xi

\displaystyle=\int_{{\bf R}^{\xi}_{n}}\frac{|\hat{v}|^{2}}{1+\delta|\xi|^{2\theta}}d\xi

\displaystyle\leq\int_{{\bf R}^{\xi}_{n}}{|\hat{v}|}^{2}d\xi

\displaystyle\leq\|v\|_{H^{\theta}}^{2},

(6.31)

which completes the proof of the unique existence of mild solutions. The existence of so-called strong solutions is a consequence of standard semigroup theory.
