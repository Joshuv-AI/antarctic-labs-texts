Abstract
We report on the annual variation of quiet-time suprathermal ion composition for C through Fe using Advanced Composition Explorer (ACE)/Ultra-Low Energy Isotope Spectrometer (ULEIS) data over the energy range 0.3 MeV/nuc to 1.28 MeV/nuc from 1998 through 2019, covering solar cycle 23’s rising phase through Solar Cycle 24’s declining phase. Our findings are (1) quiet time suprathermal abundances resemble CIR-associated particles during solar minima; (2) quiet time suprathermals are M / Q \mathrm{M/Q} fractionated in a manner that is consistent with M / Q \mathrm{M/Q} fractionation in large gradual solar energetic particle events (GSEP) during solar maxima; and (3) variability within the quiet time suprathermal pool increases as a function of M / Q \mathrm{M/Q} and is consistent with the analogous variability in GSEP events. From these observations, we infer that quiet time suprathermal ions are remnants of CIRs in solar minima and GSEP events in solar maxima. Coincident with these results, we also unexpectedly show that S \mathrm{S} behaves like a low FIP \mathrm{FIP} ion in the suprathermal regime and therefore drawn from low FIP \mathrm{FIP} solar sources.

II.1
Quiet Time Selection

Desai et al. 2006c
define quiet times in ACE/ULEIS and the Wind Suprathermal through Energetic Particle Telescope (STEP)
(
von Rosenvinge et al. 1995
)
as intervals with a count threshold below a certain value, irrespective of solar activity.
Dayeh et al. 2009
build on their work and determine a distinct quiet time threshold for each year by examining the cumulative
\mathrm{C}
through
\mathrm{Fe}
intensity.
They found that the lowest 20% to 60% of the intensity values corresponded to quiet times; this percentage depends on the year.
Dayeh et al. 2017
refined this method, calculating the mean (
\mu
) and variance (
\sigma^{2}
) of this cumulative
\mathrm{C}
-
\mathrm{Fe}
intensity in consecutive 24-hour intervals when the hourly intensity is sorted in increasing order.

7

These intervals do not correspond to chronological days, but rather consecutive 24-hour periods when the data is sorted as described.

By manually inspecting the data,
Dayeh et al. 2017
found an inflection point in
\sigma^{2}
plotted as a function of
\mu
.
They determined that intervals with
\mu
less than this inflection point’s correspond to quiet times.
We refer to the inflection point as the
\mathrm{QT}
threshold.

We build on

Along with the
\mathrm{QT}
threshold, our method provides an uncertainty metric, which allows us to quantify the sensitivity of our analysis to the selection of quiet times.
We start by generating plots of
\sigma^{2}(\mu)
for each year in the manner of
Figure

1

We have binned
\sigma^{2}
in a fixed number of logarithmically-spaced
\mu
-intervals and calculated the maximum in each bin plotted as a solid orange line.
For 2018, there are 54 bins, 62 for 2019 and 2020, and 72 otherwise.
We then select a subset of these binned valued indicated with vertical black dashes and fit them in log-space with the maximum

\mathrm{QT}(\mu)=\max(\sigma^{2}_{1},\sigma^{2}_{2})

(1)

of two power laws

\sigma^{2}(\mu)=\sigma^{2}_{0}\mu^{\epsilon}

(2)

subject to the condition that the power laws
\sigma^{2}_{1}
and
\sigma^{2}_{2}
are equal at the quiet time threshold
\mathrm{QT}

\sigma^{2}_{1}(\mathrm{QT})=\sigma^{2}_{2}(\mathrm{QT}).

(3)

In effect, this method balances the weight given to the data below and above the inflection point so that we can properly identify the change in slope corresponding to the
\mathrm{QT}
threshold.
The
1\sigma
fit uncertainty for the
\mathrm{QT}
threshold is then the interval over which we can test the sensitivity of our results.
That being said, because quiet times inherently involve small numbers, we chose to use the interval
\mathrm{QT}\pm 1.25\sigma
to provide additional confidence that our fitting methods do not inadvertently include active periods that will dominate our statistics.

III.2
Abundances During Solar Cycle Extrema
\mathrm{M/Q}

ST ions are likely associated with CIRs in solar minima
(
Desai et al. 2006c
;
Dayeh et al. 2009
;
Dayeh et al. 2017
;
Zel’dovich et al. 2011
;
Zel’dovich et al. 2018
;
Zel’dovich et al. 2021
)
.
CIRs are formed when coronating fast solar wind streams from coronal holes overtake slow wind streams.
In the outer heliosphere and occasionally at
1\,\mathrm{AU}
, the boundaries of CIRs steepen into forward and reverse shocks.
Solar wind abundances are governed by their associated sources on the Sun’s surface.
Solar sources along with the occurrence rate of flares and CMEs
(
Webb & Howard 1994
)
change with the solar activity cycle.

Alterman et al. 2021
showed that the solar cycle driving solar wind abundances changes
\sim 250\,\mathrm{days}
prior to sunspot minima, likely due to changes in the solar magnetic field that impact solar source regions
(
McIntosh et al. 2014
;
McIntosh & Leamon 2017
;
McIntosh et al. 2015
)
.

To compare abundances across solar cycle extrema, we follow
Zhao et al. 2013
and select time periods around each extremum based on the normalized
\mathrm{SSN}
(
\mathrm{NSSN}
).

\mathrm{NSSN}
is the
\mathrm{SSN}
in each solar cycle normalized to that cycle’s maximum
\mathrm{SSN}
.
This feature scaling accounts for
\mathrm{SSN}
’s variable amplitude and transforms it into an amplitude-independent clock.

Allen et al. 2019
used a similar method to assign CIRs to solar cycle extrema.
The years we consider to have abundances representative of solar minima have an annual
\mathrm{NSSN}\leq 0.15
. Years with abundances representative of solar maxima correspond to
\mathrm{NSSN}\geq 0.7
.
This corresponds to approximately 4 years per extrema and, during years representative of solar minima, primarily selects those from the declining phase of solar activity.

Figure

4
highlights solar cycle extrema in the bottom panel and partially shades the corresponding
X/\mathrm{O}
data points.
Later sections use these intervals to compare ST properties during solar cycle extrema.
By biasing our solar minima selection to the declining phase of solar activity, we ensure that our Minima 24 and 25 intervals are weighted toward ST ions that are generated by a single solar cycle, not an admixture of two
(
Alterman et al. 2021
;
McIntosh et al. 2014
;
McIntosh & Leamon 2017
;
McIntosh et al. 2015
)
.

V
Discussion

Quiet times are periods when the intensity of the suprathermal population is low.
While the suprathermal intensity naturally varies with solar activity, quiet times are nevertheless present throughout the solar cycle
(
Desai et al. 2006c
;
Dayeh et al. 2009
;
Dayeh et al. 2017
;
Zel’dovich et al. 2011
;
Zel’dovich et al. 2018
;
Zel’dovich et al. 2021
)
.
We study these suprathermal ions in quiet times across solar activity to characterize their origin.

V.1
Quiet Time Selection and Annual Quiet Hours

The definition of quiet times is still unsettled in the literature and remains
ad hoc
.
For example,
Zel’dovich et al. 2014
;
Zel’dovich et al. 2018
;
Zel’dovich et al. 2021
select data based on multiple criteria from several instruments across a few spacecraft.
In contrast,
Desai et al. 2006c
;
Dayeh et al. 2009
;
Dayeh et al. 2017
set thresholds for the
\mathrm{Fe}
or total
\mathrm{C}
through
\mathrm{Fe}
intensity and allow for intensities that report zero counts, i.e. below the instrument detection threshold.
Both selection methods rely on one or more manually identified thresholds without a metric to quantify the sensitivity of the results to that selection.

As such, quiet time selection necessarily leads to inconsistent labeling of some intervals as quiet or not.
For the purposes of this study, a false positive would be a not-quiet interval labeled as quiet and a false negative would be a quiet interval labeled as not-quiet.
Quantifying the sensitivity of our result to our quiet time selection criteria provides evidence that such false positives and false negatives do not impact our results.

Section

II.1
utilizes non-linear fitting to identify the
\mathrm{QT}
threshold using the definition described by
Dayeh et al. 2017
and provide an accompanying 1
\sigma
fit uncertainty.
To study the sensitivity of our results to the fit
\mathrm{QT}
threshold, we have calculated 21 uniformly spaced steps over the range (
\mathrm{QT}\pm 1.25\sigma
), centered on the
\mathrm{QT}
fit result.
We repeat our analysis for each of these 21 thresholds and, where applicable, representing the variation as the standard deviation of our results as error bars centered on the
\mathrm{QT}
threshold
(
Alterman & Kasper 2019
)
.

Section

analyzes each year’s
\mathrm{QT}
threshold and the number of quiet hours, both as a function of time.
In general, the
\mathrm{QT}
threshold is weakly correlated in time with
\mathrm{SSN}
(
\rho=0.51
).
However, the number of quiet hours anti-correlates with
\mathrm{SSN}
at the
\rho=-0.95
level, likely because solar activity drives the occurrence of non-quiet time periods.
It is unsurprising that our correlation is stronger than that derived by
Dayeh et al. 2009
;
Dayeh et al. 2017
because their time series are 13 years

V.2
Annual Abundances: Suprathermal Sources

Studying 41 CIRs,
Mason et al. 2008
infer that energetic particles (EPs) associated in CIRs are drawn from a ST pool composed of solar wind, pickup ions, and SEP remnants.

Filwett et al. 2017
;
Filwett et al. 2019
came to similar conclusions about ST ions accelerated by CIRs.

Figure

5

X/\mathrm{O}
to an abundance characteristic of CIRs observed at
0.385\;\mathrm{MeV}\!/\mathrm{nuc}
removes the
\mathrm{M/Q}
-dependence during solar minima.

Studying 72 interplanetary shocks at
0.75\;\mathrm{MeV}\!/\mathrm{nuc}
in solar cycle 23,
Desai et al. 2003
suggest that the suprathermal pool from which shocks accelerate energetic particles is composed of 70% GSEP and 30% ISEP remnant material, both from the
5-12\;\mathrm{MeV}\!/\mathrm{nuc}
range.
Studying 64 LSEP events from solar cycle 23 at
0.38\;\mathrm{MeV}\!/\mathrm{nuc}
,
Desai et al. 2006a
suggest that GSEP events are
\mathrm{M/Q}
-fractionated in because of the ambient
0.38\;\mathrm{MeV}\!/\mathrm{nuc}
suprathermal seed population from which they accelerate ions and this suprathermal population is itself primarily drawn from
0.385\;\mathrm{MeV}\!/\mathrm{nuc}
ISEP events.

Figure

X/\mathrm{O}
to reference abundances from ISEP does not remove the
\mathrm{M/Q}
-dependence, but normalizing it to a GSEP-representative abundance does remove the
\mathrm{M/Q}
-dependence during solar maxima.
A mixture of GSEP and ISEP abundances may not be necessary to characterize the quiet time
X/\mathrm{O}
fractionation and
Desai et al. 2003

Desai et al. 2003
) observation that suprathermals are composed of 70% GSEP + 30% ISEP remnant particles may be due to their comparison of
0.75\;\mathrm{MeV}\!/\mathrm{nuc}
ions with reference abundances at
5-12\;\mathrm{MeV}\!/\mathrm{nuc}
.

Suprathermal ions have been observed to suffuse the heliosphere
(
Tsurutani & Lin 1985
;
Desai et al. 2003
;
Wiedenbeck et al. 2003
)
.
Long term studies of ST ions across solar cycles 23 and 24 also related differences in ST observations at
1\,\mathrm{AU}
to different solar source regions
(
Zel’dovich et al. 2018
;
Zel’dovich et al. 2014
)
.
That quiet time
X/\mathrm{O}
is most similar to CIRs during solar minima and GSEP abundances during solar maxima further substantiates observations their dominant source changes with solar activity.
That these observations are taken during quiet times also substantiates that suprathermals suffice the heliosphere across solar activity.
The presence of
\mathrm{M/Q}
-fractionation points to the impact of acceleration on the observed abundances.
That the fractionation disappears when normalized to CIR abundances in solar minima and GSEP abundances in solar maxima may imply that SEPs simply decrease in intensity as they spread out through the heliosphere.

V.4
Annual Abundance Correlation with SSN: Suprathermal
\mathrm{S}
is low
\mathrm{FIP}

Reames 2018a
;
Reames 2018b
argue that the difference in CIR and SEP abundances is a result of the solar source regions from which their ions are released into the heliosphere, in particular the first ionization potential (
\mathrm{FIP}
) effect.
The
\mathrm{FIP}
effect is the observation that the abundance of low
\mathrm{FIP}
ions is enhanced relative to their photospheric abundances and high
\mathrm{FIP}
ions are depleted
(
Laming 2015
)
.

Reames 2018b
argue that this low/high
\mathrm{FIP}
separation occurs at
\sim 10\;\mathrm{eV}
in SEP events and
\mathrm{S}
is a high
\mathrm{FIP}
SEP element.
In particular,
Reames 2018a
argues that
\mathrm{S}
behaves like higher
\mathrm{FIP}
elements, of which
\mathrm{C}
is commonly treated as a genetic example.
As such, we would expect
\mathrm{S}/\mathrm{O}
to follow the same temporal variations as
\mathrm{C}/\mathrm{O}
.

This work aggregates ST measurements into annual bins, which are orders of magnitude longer in duration than events that accelerate SEPs or the processes related to injection, acceleration, and transport.

Table

1
gives the correlation coefficient
\rho(X/\mathrm{O},\mathrm{SSN})
.
Given the annual duration of our ST aggregations, these
\rho
are necessarily related to either how the source from which the ST population is drawn or how the prevalence of different acceleration, injection, and transport mechanisms changes with solar activity.
As such, we can use them to test the results of
Reames 2018a
;
Reames 2018b
.

Many acceleration and transport processes that impact the solar wind and SEPs depend on
\mathrm{M/Q}

If changes in the relative occurrence of these processes drive changes in

First, we will show that there is insufficient evidence that
\rho

\mathrm{M/Q}
.
Then we will discuss that changes in source regions may be related to changes in
\rho

Let us assume that
\rho
is tied to changes in the prevalence of a rigidity-dependent process.
Then
\rho
should be ordered by
\mathrm{M/Q}
.
Two observations indicate this is not the case.

(1)

Ions with

(2)

High
\mathrm{FIP}

While only there are only three points high
\mathrm{FIP}

\mathrm{C}

\mathrm{N}
, and
\mathrm{Ne}

\mathrm{C}/\mathrm{O}
and its anti-correlation because
\mathrm{C}

In other words,
\mathrm{C}
is not necessarily a generic example of high
\mathrm{FIP}
elements.
The two remaining points (
\mathrm{N}
and
\mathrm{Ne}

\mathrm{FIP}
ions.
In short, there is insufficient evidence for an
\mathrm{M/Q}

\rho

Let us assume the opposite, that changes in
\rho
are tied to source region effects at the Sun.
The
\mathrm{FIP}
effect yields abundance differences based on how the combination of source region temperatures and height in the solar atmosphere determine when an element ionizes.
These temperatures and heights are related to solar source region type (e.g. coronal hole, active region, streamer belt, etc.) from which the ions emanates.
Because
\mathrm{FIP}

X/\mathrm{O}
are possible based on the source region driving each abundance.

\mathrm{S}

VI
Conclusion

We have developed a method for analyzing long term trends in
0.3\;\mathrm{MeV}\!/\mathrm{nuc}
to
1.28\;\mathrm{MeV}\!/\mathrm{nuc}
ions during quiet times that includes tests for the sensitivity of our results to our quiet time selection criteria.
We refer to these as suprathermals and have shown that the statistical uncertainty in our results dominates any uncertainty due to our quiet time selection criterion.
As such, our results and
\mathrm{QT}
threshold proposed by
Dayeh et al. 2017
’s (
Dayeh et al. 2017
) are robust.

We have confirmed that the annual number of quiet hours decreases with increasing solar activity
(
Dayeh et al. 2017
)
.
This is likely because the occurrence of phenomena like flares and CMEs that accelerate SEPs increases with
\mathrm{SSN}
.
Our results also support prior conclusions that the populations from which ST ions are drawn change with solar activity
(
Desai et al. 2006c
;
Dayeh et al. 2009
;
Dayeh et al. 2017
;
Zel’dovich et al. 2011
;
Zel’dovich et al. 2018
;
Zel’dovich et al. 2021
)
.
In particular, normalizing quiet time
X/\mathrm{O}
to CIR abundances during solar minima and GSEP abundances during solar maxima removes the trends with
\mathrm{M/Q}
.
This suggests that the quiet time suprathermals we observe are remnants of material that was previously accelerated by the dominant energetic particle producer during that epoch, that these energetic particles decay in intensity to the suprathermal regime, and that they are not accelerated out of the solar wind nor decelerated from SEPs.
That these observations are made during quiet times further substantiates observations that suprathermals suffuse the interplanetary medium
(
Tsurutani & Lin 1985
;
Desai et al. 2003
;
Wiedenbeck et al. 2003
)
.

Coincident to these broad findings, our analysis has also revealed that suprathermal
\mathrm{S}
is a low
\mathrm{FIP}
ion.
Given that high and low
\mathrm{FIP}
ions are from distinct sources on the Sun, this means that
\mathrm{S}
must be treated like a low
\mathrm{FIP}
ion when tracing SEP events back to their solar sources and modeling SEP acceleration mechanisms.
