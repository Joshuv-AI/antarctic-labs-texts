Gibbs point processes on path space: existence, cluster expansion and uniqueness
Alexander Zass

Abstract
We present general existence and uniqueness results for marked models with pair interactions, exemplified through Gibbs point processes on path space. More precisely, we study a class of infinite-dimensional diffusions under Gibbsian interactions, in the context of marked point configurations: the starting points belong to ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} , and the marks are the paths of Langevin diffusions. We use the entropy method to prove existence of an infinite-volume Gibbs point process and use cluster expansion tools to provide an explicit activity domain in which uniqueness holds. Keywords: marked Gibbs point processes, DLR equations, uniqueness, cluster expansion, infinite-dimensional diffusions MSC 2020: 60K35, 60G55, 60G60, 82B21, 82C22



Introduction

The motivating example for this work is a system of infinitely many Langevin diffusions in interaction: through the lens of Gibbs point process theory, we first see each diffusion – starting in
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and with displacement
\big(m(s),\ s\in[0,1]\big)
– as a marked point
\mathsfbf{x}=(x,m)\in\mathscr{E}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\mathbb{R}^{d}\times C_{0}
, where
C_{0}
is the normed space of continuous paths starting at
0
. We then consider an infinite collection of them, in terms of a marked point process on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times C_{0}
. Finally,
we add, on the space of marked point configurations, a Gibbsian energy functional
H
, with (finite but) not uniformly bounded interaction range. In such a framework, the questions of existence and uniqueness of Gibbs point processes are far from trivial; in particular, we note how the norm of the random marks is a priori unbounded.

The interest in point processes on path space can be traced back to the works of R. Feynman and M. Kač for quantum particle systems (
[
8
,
4
]
). In this context, by applying a functional integration to the operator
e^{-\beta H}
, D. Ruelle obtained in
[
25
]
the integration over a random number of Brownian bridges of length
\beta
. This representation was interpreted by J. Ginibre (
[
6
]
, and more recently, e.g.
[
21
,
27
]
) as a point processes on the space of composite loops.
The first description of a quantum system in terms of a marked point processes is due to K.-H. Fichtner (
[
5
]
), in the non-interacting case: the marks, elements of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, are given by the sequence of positions (at discrete time points) of the gas particles. In the interacting Gibbsian framework, in
[
1
]
, the authors use marked point processes to provide a variational formula for the free energy of a system of repulsive bosons.
Using the Gibbsian framework to describe the interaction between open paths (and not only loops) is then highly sought-after, as Gibbs point processes could be seen to be the solutions of a variational problem. This is connected to the open question of Bose–Einstein condensation (see the discussion in
[
1
]
). A complete analysis of this phenomenon is missing at the moment, and this work hopes to contribute towards an understanding of the Gibbsian nature of processes on path space.

We are interested here in open trajectories, i.e. when the final position is not a priori fixed. The best setting for this is that of marked points, where the starting location of the path is a point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and its mark is given by a (centred) path
m\in C_{0}
.

In this work we present general existence and uniqueness results (Theorem
1
and Theorem
2
) for marked models with pair interactions, which we exemplify through Gibbs point processes on path space (Section
1.1
).
In this setting, we consider interactions between two trajectories given as time integrals of (instantaneous) pair potentials which are usual for classical systems in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, like the Lennard–Jones pair potential. Note that we allow for potentials that are not (fully) repulsive, which induces technical complexities. Nevertheless, we require – in order to satisfy the stability conditions that are needed in the method (Assumption
2
) – a hard-core component near the origin (see Example
1
).

After presenting the general setting in Section
1.2
, we tackle the existence question in Section
2
, via the Dobrushin–Lanford–Ruelle description of Gibbs point processes. Under some stability assumptions for the energy functional
H
, we are able to prove (in Theorem
1
) the existence, for any activity
z
and inverse temperature
\beta
, of at least one infinite-volume marked Gibbs point process
P^{z,\beta}
with energy functional
H
, by applying the entropy method presented for the general marked setting in
[
22
]
.

Moreover, we also show that, for any
N\geq 1
, the
N
-point correlation function
\rho_{N}
of these Gibbs point processes satisfy a (point-dependent)
Ruelle bound
of the following form: there exists a function
\mathsfbf{c}\colon\mathscr{E}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that, for almost any finite path configuration
(\mathsfbf{x}_{1},\dots,\mathsfbf{x}_{N})\in\mathscr{E}^{N}
,

\rho_{N}(\mathsfbf{x}_{1},\dots,\mathsfbf{x}_{N})\leq\prod_{i=1}^{N}\mathsfbf{c}(\mathsfbf{x}_{i}).

In Sections
3
and
4
we present, as a novel result, an explicit activity domain where uniqueness of the Gibbs point process holds. This is obtained with the approach of cluster expansion and the Kirkwood–Salsburg equations – a method which was first developed for lattice systems in the 1980s (see e.g.
[
13
]
) and then extended to the continuous case (see e.g.
[
14
,
16
]
).

In the case of unmarked continuous point processes, the technique relies on considering a series expansion of the correlation functions. As presented by D. Ruelle in
[
25
]
, one first shows that the correlation functions of a Gibbs point process can be expressed as an absolutely converging series of cluster terms, and then proves uniqueness by considering a system of integral equations – the so-called Kirkwood–Salsburg equations – that the correlation functions satisfy. In fact, these equations can be reformulated as a fixed-point problem for an operator
\mathsfbf{K}_{z}
in an appropriately chosen Banach space, having therefore a unique solution.

The cluster expansion approach is actually well adapted to the marked setting. Indeed, S. Poghosyan and D. Ueltschi develop, in
[
19
]
, abstract finite-volume techniques that can be used both in the classical and in the marked setting, under assumptions of so-called
modified regularity
of the interaction. These assumptions and techniques are further developed in
[
20
]
by S. Poghosyan and H. Zessin, proving uniqueness of infinite-volume Gibbs point processes for potentials satisfying a certain stability condition (which they refer to as
Penrose stability
). Some similar result is presented by S. Jansen in
[
7
]
, but making strong use of the repulsive nature of the interaction she considers.

A key tool in the Kirkwood–Salsburg proof of uniqueness is a Ruelle bound for the correlation functions of any Gibbs point process, as this shows that they belong to a certain Banach space
\mathbb{X}_{\mathsfbf{c}}
(see Subsection
4.2
).
The techniques used in
[
20
]
are too restrictive if applied in our setting of unbounded marks,
so we use here a different approach, inspired by the work
[
10
]
of T. Kuna: in Section
3
, under a different set of assumptions than that of Section
2
, we rely on some tree-graph estimates, to prove a Ruelle bound for the correlation functions of infinite-volume Gibbs point processes.
In particular, under an additional regularity assumption for the interaction potential
\Phi
, we show that there exists an activity threshold
{\swabfamily z}_{Ru}(\beta)>0
such that, for any
z\in(0,{\swabfamily z}_{Ru}(\beta))
, the correlation functions
\rho_{N}^{(P)}
of any Gibbs point process
P
with activity
z
and inverse temperature
\beta
satisfy a Ruelle bound as above, but with a constant
\mathsfbf{c}_{z}
: for almost any
(\mathsfbf{x}_{1},\dots,\mathsfbf{x}_{N})\in\mathscr{E}^{N}
,
\rho_{N}^{(P)}(\mathsfbf{x}_{1},\dots,\mathsfbf{x}_{N})\leq\mathsfbf{c}_{z}^{N}
.

In Section
4
, after showing that there exists an activity threshold
{\swabfamily z}_{\text{crit}}(\beta)>0
such that, for any
z\in(0,{\swabfamily z}_{\text{crit}}(\beta))
, the norm of the Kirkwood–Salsburg operator
\mathsfbf{K}_{z}
in
\mathbb{X}_{\mathsfbf{c}}
is strictly smaller than
1
, we show that the associated equations have a unique solution and obtain the following uniqueness domain (in Theorem
2
): for any
\beta>0
and
z\in(0,{\swabfamily z}_{\text{crit}}(\beta))
, there exists a unique infinite-volume marked Gibbs point process
P
with activity
z
and inverse temperature
\beta
, associated to the energy functional
H
.

An important note is that, by using the entropy method, and not Kirkwood–Salsburg, as an existence method, we are able to obtain the existence for any activity
z>0
, and have therefore a restriction on the activity regime that is only due to the requirements for uniqueness.

1

The Gibbsian setting

Before presenting the general setting of this work, we present its main motivation, that is a marked point process representation for interacting diffusions.

1.1

A point process on path space

We consider infinitely-many independent gradient diffusions and add a dependence between them by introducing an
interaction energy
in the context of marked Gibbs point processes.

The basic mathematical object of this work is the following Langevin dynamics on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
:

dX(s)=dB(s)-\frac{1}{2}\nabla V\big(X(s)\big)ds,\quad s\in[0,1],

(1)

where
B
is a standard
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued Brownian motion, and
V\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a smooth potential satisfying, outside of some compact subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\exists\delta^{\prime},b_{1},b_{2}>0,\quad V(x)\geq b_{1}\lvert x\rvert^{d+\delta^{\prime}}\text{ and }\varDelta V(x)-\frac{1}{2}\lvert\nabla V(x)\rvert^{2}\leq-b_{2}|x\rvert^{2+2\delta^{\prime}}.

(2)

It is a known result (see e.g.
[
23
]
) that, under these conditions, there exists a unique solution to the SDE (
1
), which generates an
ultracontractive
semigroup (see
[
9
,
3
]
). Moreover, for any
\delta<\delta^{\prime}/2
,

\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\sup_{s\in[0,1]}\lvert X(s)-X(0)\rvert^{d+2\delta}}\right]<+\infty.

(3)

For the rest of this work, let
\delta>0
be fixed.

Consider now that any (continuous) path
\mathsfbf{x}
on
[0,1]
can be decomposed into its initial location
x
and a (shifted) path
m
starting from
0
. In other words, we identify
\mathsfbf{x}
with a marked point given by the pair
(x,m)\in\mathscr{E}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\mathbb{R}^{d}\times C_{0}
, where
\mathscr{S}
is the space of continuous paths on
[0,1]
starting at
0
. The space
C_{0}
, endowed with the norm
\lVert m\rVert
given by the maximum displacement of the trajectory
m
, that is
\lVert m\rVert\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\sup_{s\in[0,1]}\lvert m(s)\rvert
, is a normed space.

On
C_{0}
, we consider the measure
\mathbf{R}
, given by the law of the process
X
solution of (
1
) starting at
X(0)=0
. Notice that, thanks to (
3
), for any
\delta<\delta^{\prime}/2
,

\int_{C_{0}}e^{\lVert m\rVert^{d+2\delta}}\mathbf{R}(dm)<+\infty.

(4)

Two interacting paths of a Langevin diffusion in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Each circle is centred in the starting point, while the radii of the coloured circles correspond to their maximum displacement in the time interval
[0,1]
; the dotted circles represent the security distance
a_{0}/2
introduced in (
11
).

We can now add an interaction between two paths
\mathsfbf{x}_{1}=(x_{1},m_{1})
and
\mathsfbf{x}_{2}=(x_{2},m_{2})
, by considering the following class of interactions, described by a path pair potential of the form

\Phi(\mathsfbf{x}_{1},\mathsfbf{x}_{2})=\Big(\int_{0}^{1}\phi(\lvert x_{1}-x_{2}+m_{1}(s)-m_{2}(s)\rvert)ds\Big)\mathds{1}_{[0,a_{0}+\lVert m_{1}\rVert+\lVert m_{2}\rVert]}(\lvert x_{1}-x_{2}\rvert).

We provide precise definitions for the objects above in Example
1
, after introducing the general setting in the next subsection.

1.2

The general setting

We consider here point measures on the product state space
\mathscr{E}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathscr{S}
, where
(\mathscr{S},\lVert\cdot\rVert)
is a general normed space. On
\mathscr{E}
, we take the following product measure

\lambda(dx,dm)=dx\otimes\mathbf{R}(dm),

where
\mathbf{R}
is a probability measure on
\mathscr{S}
such that (
4
) holds.

We denote by
\mathscr{M}
the space of simple point measures (
configurations
) on
\mathscr{E}
, i.e. of all
\sigma
-finite measures of the form

\gamma=\sum_{i}\delta_{\mathsfbf{x}_{i}},\ \mathsfbf{x}_{i}=(x_{i},m_{i})\in\mathscr{E},\text{ with }\mathsfbf{x}_{i}\neq\mathsfbf{x}_{j}\text{ if }i\neq j.

Since the configurations are simple, we identify them with the subset of their atoms:

\gamma\equiv\big\{\mathsfbf{x}_{1},\dots,\mathsfbf{x}_{n},\dots\big\}\subset\mathscr{E}.

Moreover, for two disjoint configurations
\gamma,\xi\in\mathscr{M}
, we denote by
\gamma\xi
their concatenation:
\gamma\xi\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\gamma\cup\xi
. For
\gamma\in\mathscr{M}
,
\lvert\gamma\rvert
denotes the number of its points, and
\mathscr{M}_{f}\subset\mathscr{M}
is the subset of
finite
configurations, i.e. with
\lvert\gamma\rvert<+\infty
. We denote by
\underline{o}
the configuration supported on the empty set.

For any
\varLambda\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\mathscr{M}_{\varLambda}\subset\mathscr{M}
denotes the subset of point measures with support in
\varLambda\times\mathscr{S}
, and
\gamma_{\varLambda}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\gamma\cap(\varLambda\times\mathscr{S})
. Let
\mathscr{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
denote the Borel
\sigma
-algebra on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
\mathscr{B}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
the set of bounded Borel subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, which we often call
finite volumes
. For
\varLambda\in\mathscr{B}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
\lvert\varLambda\rvert
denotes its volume.

We denote by
\mathscr{P}(\mathscr{M})
(resp.
\mathscr{P}(\mathscr{M}_{\varLambda})
) the set of probability measures (or
point processes
) on
\mathscr{M}
(resp.
\mathscr{M}_{\varLambda}
). Finally, let
\mathbb{N}^{*}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\mathbb{N}\setminus\{0\}
.

We consider the following measure (of infinite mass):

Definition 1.1
.

Fix
z>0
, and define the measure
\tilde{\pi}^{z}=1+\sum_{N=1}^{+\infty}\dfrac{z^{N}}{N!}\lambda^{\otimes N}
on
\mathscr{M}_{f}
. For any finite volume
\varLambda
, we consider as reference probability measure the
marked Poisson point process

\pi^{z}_{\varLambda}\in\mathscr{P}(\mathscr{M}_{\varLambda})
with intensity parameter
z
, defined by normalising the restriction
\tilde{\pi}^{z}_{\varLambda}
of
\tilde{\pi}^{z}
to
\mathscr{M}_{\varLambda}
as follows:

\pi^{z}_{\varLambda}(d\gamma)=e^{-z\lvert\varLambda\rvert}\tilde{\pi}^{z}_{\varLambda}(d\gamma).

As a modification of the Poisson point process, we introduce an interaction between the marked points by considering the finite-volume Gibbs point process associated to an energy functional
H
. More precisely:

Definition 1.2
.

An
energy functional

H\colon\mathscr{M}_{f}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}

is a measurable functional on the set of finite configurations, with
H(\underline{o})=0
by convention. In this work, we consider the energy of a finite number
N\geq 1
of marked points to be defined, for any
\gamma=\{\mathsfbf{x}_{1},\dots,\mathsfbf{x}_{N}\}\in\mathscr{M}_{f}
, by the sum of a self-interaction (
external field
) term and a pair-potential term:

H(\gamma)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Psi(\mathsfbf{x}_{i})+\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq N}\Phi(\mathsfbf{x}_{i},\mathsfbf{x}_{j})\in\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}.

(5)

We specify later a growth condition on the self potential
\Psi
, and consider different sets of assumptions on the pair potential
\Phi\colon\mathscr{E}\times\mathscr{E}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
. In particular, in Section
2
we use the notion of tempered stability (Assumption
1
), in Section
3
we use the stronger notion of classical stability (Assumption
1∗
), and in Section
4
a weak stability condition (Assumption
1′
). We comment on the relation between them in Remark
4.3
.

We denote the
pair-interaction
component of the energy as

E_{\Phi}(\gamma)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq N}\Phi(\mathsfbf{x}_{i},\mathsfbf{x}_{j}),

and the
conditional energy
of any marked point
\mathsfbf{x}\in\mathscr{E}
given any
\xi\in\mathscr{M}
as

E_{\Phi}\left(\mathsfbf{x}\,|\,\xi\right)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\sum_{\mathsfbf{y}\in\xi}\Phi(\mathsfbf{x},\mathsfbf{y}).

(6)

Finally, for any
\gamma\in\mathscr{M}
, let

E_{\Phi}\left(\gamma\,|\,\xi\right)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\sum_{\mathsfbf{x}\in\gamma}E_{\Phi}\left(\mathsfbf{x}\,|\,\xi\right).

be the conditional energy of the configuration
\gamma
given the configuration
\xi
.

Remark 1.3
.

As the infinite sum in (
6
) is not always well defined (see Remark
2.1
), in order for the Definition
1.5
of an infinite-volume Gibbs point process
P
to be well posed, one needs to require the conditional energy be well defined for
P
-almost all
\xi\in\mathscr{M}
. We note however that this is not a restriction for Gibbs measures supported on the space of tempered configurations (see Remark
2.1
).

Definition 1.4
.

Let
H
be an energy functional as in (
5
). For any
\varLambda\in\mathscr{B}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, the free-boundary-condition
finite-volume Gibbs point process
on
\varLambda
with energy functional
H
, activity
z>0
and inverse temperature
\beta>0
is the probability measure
P^{z,\beta}_{\varLambda}
on
\mathscr{M}_{\varLambda}
defined by

P^{z,\beta}_{\varLambda}(d\gamma)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\dfrac{1}{Z^{z,\beta}_{\varLambda}}e^{-\beta H(\gamma_{\varLambda})}\,\pi^{z}_{\varLambda}(d\gamma),

(7)

where the
partition function

Z_{\varLambda}^{z,\beta}
is the normalisation constant.

In this work we investigate the existence and uniqueness of an infinite-volume Gibbs point process, in the following sense:

Definition 1.5
.

Let
H
be an energy functional as in (
5
). A probability measure
P
on
\mathscr{M}
is said to be an
infinite-volume Gibbs point process
with energy functional
H
, activity
z>0
and inverse temperature
\beta>0
, denoted
P\in\mathscr{G}_{z,\beta}(H)
, if:

For any
\gamma\in\mathscr{M}_{f}
and
P
-almost all
\xi\in\mathscr{M}
, the conditional energy
E_{\Phi}(\gamma_{\varLambda}|\xi_{\varLambda^{c}})
of
\gamma
given
\xi
is well defined , and

P
satisfies, for any
\varLambda\in\mathscr{B}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and any positive, bounded, and measurable functional
F:\mathscr{M}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, the following
DLR equation
(for Dobrushin–Lanford–Ruelle)

\int_{\mathscr{M}}F(\gamma)\,P(d\gamma)=\int_{\mathscr{M}}\dfrac{1}{Z^{z,\beta}_{\varLambda}(\xi)}\int_{\mathscr{M}_{\varLambda}}F(\gamma_{\varLambda}\xi_{\varLambda^{c}})e^{-\beta\big(H(\gamma_{\varLambda})+E_{\Phi}(\gamma_{\varLambda}|\xi_{\varLambda^{c}})\big)}\pi^{z}_{\varLambda}(d\gamma)\ P(d\xi),

(DLR)

where the partition function
Z_{\varLambda}^{z,\beta}(\xi)
depends on the boundary condition
\xi
, the inverse temperature
\beta
, and the activity
z
.

A concept that will help in showing that such an infinite-volume measure exists is that of tempered configuration. For such a configuration
\gamma
, the number
\lvert\gamma_{\varLambda}\rvert
of its points in any finite volume
\varLambda
, should grow sublinearly w.r.t. the volume, while the norm
\lVert m\rVert
of its marks should grow as a fractional power of it. More precisely,

Definition 1.6
.

The set of
tempered configurations
is given by the increasing union
\mathscr{M}^{\text{temp}}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\bigcup_{{\swabfamily t}\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}\mathscr{M}^{\swabfamily t}
, where

\mathscr{M}^{\swabfamily t}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\big\{\gamma\in\mathscr{M}:\forall l\in\mathbb{N}^{*},\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(x,m)\in\gamma\\
\lvert x\rvert\leq l\end{subarray}}(1+\lVert m\rVert^{d+2\delta})\leq{\swabfamily t}l^{d}\big\}.

(8)

We denote by
\mathscr{G}^{\text{temp}}_{z,\beta}(H)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\mathscr{G}_{z,\beta}(H)\cap\mathscr{P}(\mathscr{M}^{\text{temp}})
the set of
tempered marked Gibbs point processes
, i.e. those whose support is included in the tempered configurations.

2

Existence via the entropy method

The proof of the existence of an infinite-volume Gibbs point process that we describe here makes use of the specific entropy functional as a tightness tool, as in the general approach presented in
[
22
]
. In what follows we show that a tempered marked Gibbs point process
exists as soon as the interaction energy satisfies some quite natural assumptions.

In order for our model (and the example of interacting Langevin diffusions) to fit the setting of the aforementioned paper, in this section we consider energy functionals
H
that satisfy Assumptions
1
+
2
.

Assumption 1

(Self-interaction growth and stability)
.

(\mathscr{H}_{\text{self}})

The self-interaction potential
\Psi:\mathscr{E}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
acting on each marked point
\mathsfbf{x}=(x,m)
is bounded from below by the opposite of a power of the norm of its mark, i.e.

\exists A_{\Psi}>0:\inf_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Psi(x,m)\geq-A_{\Psi}\lVert m\rVert^{d+\delta}.

(9)

(\mathscr{H}_{\text{t.st.}})

The pair potential
\Phi:\mathscr{E}\times\mathscr{E}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
between two marked points is a symmetric functional that satisfies the following (mark-dependent)
tempered stability
condition: there exists a constant
B_{\Phi}\geq 0
such that for any finite configuration
\gamma=\{\mathsfbf{x}_{1},\dots,\mathsfbf{x}_{N}\}\in\mathscr{M}_{f}
,

E_{\Phi}(\gamma)=\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq N}\Phi(\mathsfbf{x}_{i},\mathsfbf{x}_{j})\geq-B_{\Phi}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(1+\lVert m_{i}\rVert^{d+\delta}).

(10)

Assumption 2

(Range and conditional stability)
.

The pair potential
\Phi:\mathscr{E}\times\mathscr{E}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
is such that

(\mathscr{H}_{\text{r}})

Two diffusions
\mathsfbf{x}_{i},\mathsfbf{x}_{j}
do not interact whenever they start
too far away
: there exists a constant
a_{0}\geq 0
such that

\Phi(\mathsfbf{x}_{i},\mathsfbf{x}_{j})=0\text{ whenever }\lvert x_{i}-x_{j}\rvert>a_{0}+\lVert m_{i}\rVert+\lVert m_{j}\rVert.

(11)

(\mathscr{H}_{\text{t.cond.st.}})

There exists a constant
\bar{B}_{\Phi}\geq 0
such that, for any
\mathsfbf{x}=(x,m)\in\mathscr{E}
, for any configuration
\xi\in\mathscr{M}^{\text{temp}}
,

E_{\Phi}\left(\mathsfbf{x}\,|\,\xi\right)=\sum_{\mathsfbf{y}\in\xi}\Phi(\mathsfbf{x},\mathsfbf{y})\geq-\bar{B}_{\Phi}(1+\lVert m\rVert^{d+\delta}).

(12)

Remark 2.1
.

We briefly comment on these assumptions.

•

The summation in (
12
) for the conditional energy is well defined since, as we will see in the proof of Theorem
1
, the range assumption
(\mathscr{H}_{\text{r}})
implies that the infinite sum of the conditional energy of
\mathsfbf{x}
given
\xi
is actually given by a random but finite number of terms.

•

The tempered stability
(\mathscr{H}_{\text{t.st.}})
is needed in order to obtain the entropy bounds presented in Subsection 3.2 of
[
22
]
, and therefore the existence of an infinite-volume measure. On the other hand, the
conditional
tempered stability

(\mathscr{H}_{\text{t.cond.st.}})
– which is only on
tempered
boundary conditions – is enough to recover the Gibbsianity of this object (see Subsection 3.4 of
[
22
]
).

It is easy to show that the following Lemma holds for the support of any Gibbs point process:

Lemma 2.2
.

For any activity
z>0
and inverse temperature
\beta>0
, any infinite-volume Gibbs point process
P\in\mathscr{G}^{\text{temp}}_{z,\beta}(H)
is supported on configurations with locally finite energy, that is configurations
\gamma\in\mathscr{M}^{\text{temp}}
such that, for any
\varDelta\in\mathscr{B}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
E_{\Phi}(\gamma_{\varDelta})<+\infty
. Note that this is true also whenever
\Phi
takes infinite values.

Example 1
.

On
\mathscr{E}=\mathbb{R}^{d}\times C_{0}
, consider the following class of interactions, described by a path pair potential of the form

\Phi(\mathsfbf{x}_{i},\mathsfbf{x}_{j})=\Big(\int_{0}^{1}\phi(\lvert x_{i}-x_{j}+m_{i}(s)-m_{j}(s)\rvert)ds\Big)\mathds{1}_{[0,a_{0}+\lVert m_{i}\rVert+\lVert m_{j}\rVert]}(\lvert x_{i}-x_{j}\rvert),

(13)

with
\phi
given by the sum of two potentials on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
:
\phi=\phi_{hc}+\phi_{l}
, where

•

The potential
\phi_{hc}
is pure

The potential

\phi(\lvert y_{i}-y_{j}\rvert)<+\infty

\forall N\geq 1,\,\forall\{y_{1},\dots,y_{N}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d},\quad\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq N}\phi(\lvert y_{i}-y_{j}\rvert)\geq-B_{\phi}N.

Note also how the coefficient

here plays the role of a

(see Figure

is repulsive (i.e. positive), then

can take any finite positive value. If instead on some region

Finally, note that, if

Proof.

Firstly, thank to the previous Lemma, we can actually restrict our study to the admissible configurations. Setting

In order to obtain the lower bound for the conditional energy in this context, it is unfortunately not true – as used instead in Section 4 of

Ruelle bounds for correlation functions

At the end of the previous section, we have seen how a Ruelle bound for the correlation functions of a Gibbs point process holds. This is an essential estimate when proving uniqueness of such a process, as we will see in Section
4

Suppose now that you have a pair potential
\Phi
– not necessarily satisfying the assumptions of the previous section – and that you already have an infinite-volume Gibbs point process

[

such that, for any

satisfy a Ruelle bound. An important tool is given by the Ursell kernel (see the work by R.A. Minlos and S. Poghosyan in
[

), introduced here in Subsection

.

In this section we work under Assumptions

+
3
, i.e. the classical stability assumption (

) and an additional regularity condition, introduced in the next subsection.

We consider here

\Phi:\mathscr{E}\times\mathscr{E}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
such that there exists a function
\mathsfbf{b}\colon\mathscr{E}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
where, for any

and some

(w.l.o.g.
\mathsfbf{x}_{0}
), the following holds

\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Phi(\mathsfbf{x}_{0},\mathsfbf{x}_{i})\geq-\mathsfbf{b}(\mathsfbf{x}_{0}).

(35)

Remark 4.3
.

Note that
(\mathscr{H}_{\text{w.st.}})
is actually implied by the conditional stability condition (
12
).
Moreover, if
\Phi
satisfies

[resp.
(\mathscr{H}_{\text{st.}})

\Rightarrow

(\mathscr{H}_{\text{t.st.}})

\Rightarrow

(\mathscr{H}_{\text{w.st.}})

\Leftarrow

(\mathscr{H}_{\text{t.cond.st.}})
.

Conversely, if

for

], then is also satisfies

[resp.
(\mathscr{H}_{\text{st.}})
] for the same function [resp. constant].

While in the previous section we assumed that the pair potential

regularity condition (Assumption

with

Under Assumptions

.

The Ruelle bound assumption guarantees that the sequence

Proposition 4.6

Proposition 4.7
.

Let
\Phi
such that Assumptions
1′
+
3′
+
4′
hold, and set
\mathsfbf{c}(\mathsfbf{x})\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=e^{\mathsfbf{a}(\mathsfbf{x})+\mathsfbf{b}(\mathsfbf{x})}
. For any
\beta>0
and

is a contraction in

. For such activities there exists then at most one solution of
(KS)z
in
\mathbb{X}_{\mathsfbf{c}}

Proof.

For any

, we estimate

\begin{split}\lvert(\mathsfbf{K}_{z}r)_{N+1}&(\mathsfbf{x}_{0},\dots,\mathsfbf{x}_{N})\rvert\leq e^{-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Phi(\mathsfbf{x}_{0}-\mathsfbf{x}_{i})}\bigg(\prod_{i=1}^{N}\mathsfbf{c}(\mathsfbf{x}_{i})\\
&+\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}\frac{z^{k}}{k!}\int\prod_{j=1}^{k}\lvert e^{-\Phi(\mathsfbf{x}_{0},\mathsfbf{y}_{j})}-1\rvert\prod_{i=1}^{N}\mathsfbf{c}(\mathsfbf{x}_{i})\prod_{j=1}^{k}\mathsfbf{c}(\mathsfbf{y}_{j})\ \sigma^{\otimes k}(d\mathsfbf{y}_{1},\dots,d\mathsfbf{y}_{k})\bigg)\\

4.2.2

The case of uniform Ruelle bounds

In Subsection
3.3
we presented techniques to obtain, under relatively weak assumptions for the interaction, an activity regime in which a uniform Ruelle bound holds for a constant
\mathsfbf{c}_{z}>0
. A natural question is then whether the framework presented above applies to this case as well.

We work here with energy functionals
E_{\Phi}
and activities
z>0
such that Assumptions
1∗
+
3
+
4
hold, i.e. with the classical stability
(\mathscr{H}_{\text{st.}})
.

We consider the case of a Ruelle bound that holds for a constant
\mathsfbf{c}
,
uniformly
in the points
\mathsfbf{x}_{1},\dots,\mathsfbf{x}_{N}
.
Note that, although under Assumptions
1∗
+
3
one can compute some constant
\mathsfbf{c}_{z}
such that the Ruelle bound (
41
) is satisfied (Proposition
3.14
), this constant may not be optimal. Indeed, in some cases one already has a Ruelle bound – not obtained via the techniques presented in Subsection
3.3
– which yields a larger uniqueness regime (see Example
1
at the end of this subsection). For this reason, we introduce the following:

Assumption 4

(Uniform Ruelle bound)
.

Assume there exist a constant
\mathsfbf{c}>0
such that, for any
P\in\mathscr{G}_{z,\beta}(H)
, for any
N\geq 1
, for
\sigma^{\otimes N}
-almost all
\{\mathsfbf{x}_{1},\dots,\mathsfbf{x}_{N}\}\subset\mathscr{E}^{N}
, its correlation function
\rho_{N}^{(P)}
satisfy, uniformly in
\{\mathsfbf{x}_{1},\dots,\mathsfbf{x}_{N}\}
, the following Ruelle bound:

\rho_{N}^{(P)}(\mathsfbf{x}_{1},\dots,\mathsfbf{x}_{N})\leq\mathsfbf{c}^{N}.

(41)

We want to show that this set of assumptions yields results which are consistent with those presented in the non-uniform case, in particular Propositions
4.6
and
4.7
.

Indeed, the computations of Propositions
4.6
and
4.7
are the same, except for using Assumption
3
to prove the absolute convergence of the series in (
40
). To prove that the operator is a contraction, following the proof of Proposition
4.7
with
\mathsfbf{b}(\mathsfbf{x})\equiv 2\beta B_{\Phi}
, and using the uniform Ruelle bound (
41
) in place of (
38
) yields

\begin{split}\lvert(\mathsfbf{K}_{z}r)_{N+1}&(\mathsfbf{x}_{0},\dots,\mathsfbf{x}_{N})\rvert\leq\mathsfbf{c}^{N+1}\mathsfbf{c}^{-1}e^{2\beta B_{\Phi}+z{C}(\beta)\mathsfbf{c}}.\end{split}

Hence, if there exists
{\swabfamily z}_{\text{crit}}(\beta)>0
such that

\mathsfbf{c}^{-1}e^{2\beta B_{\Phi}+{\swabfamily z}_{\text{crit}}(\beta){C}(\beta)\mathsfbf{c}}<1,

the operator is a contraction. Note that this condition is equivalent to Assumption

for constant values of

. More precisely: since we want a Ruelle bound to hold for

, set

, if
\mathsfbf{c}>e^{b}
. Condition (

) then reads

\mathsfbf{c}^{-1}e^{2\beta B_{\Phi}+{\swabfamily z}_{\text{crit}}(\beta){C}(\beta)\mathsfbf{c}}<1\iff b+{\swabfamily z}_{\text{crit}}(\beta){C}(\beta)\mathsfbf{c}<\log(\mathsfbf{c})\iff{\swabfamily z}_{\text{crit}}(\beta)\leq a({C}(\beta)\mathsfbf{c})^{-1},

which corresponds to (

) for this choice of constants.

Remark 4.8
.

If
\mathsfbf{c}\leq e^{b}
, e.g. for non-negative potentials, one can optimise the value of
\mathsfbf{c}
. See Example

below.

Without (

), one can only prove that the norm of the Kirkwood–Salsburg operator is bounded, but not that it is smaller than

. We present below two examples in which such a

The uniform Ruelle bound holds, under Assumption

, with

{\swabfamily z}_{\text{crit}}(\beta)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\inf\{z>0:\mathsfbf{c}_{z}^{-1}e^{2\beta B_{\Phi}+z\mathsfbf{c}_{z}{C}(\beta)}>1\}>0,

we have that, for any
z<{\swabfamily z}_{\text{crit}}(\beta)
, the norm of
\mathsfbf{K}_{z}
in
\mathbb{X}_{\mathsfbf{c}_{z}}
is smaller than
1
, i.e. is a contraction in
\mathbb{X}_{\mathsfbf{c}_{z}}
.

Finally, note that, since
\lim_{z\to{\swabfamily z}_{Ru}(\beta)^{-}}\mathsfbf{c}_{z}=+\infty
and
\mathsfbf{c}_{z}^{-1}e^{2\beta B_{\Phi}+z\mathsfbf{c}_{z}{C}(\beta)}=+\infty
for
z\geq{\swabfamily z}_{Ru}(\beta)
, we have that
{\swabfamily z}_{\text{crit}}(\beta)\leq{\swabfamily z}_{Ru}(\beta)
.

Example 1

(continued)
.

Consider a potential
\Phi
in the class of Example

. The Ruelle bound is satisfied, for any
z>0
, for
\mathsfbf{c}=e^{3\beta B_{\Phi}}
(see (

)).

Let
B_{\Phi}>0
. For such a value of
\mathsfbf{c}
, the Kirkwood–Salsburg operator
\mathsfbf{K}_{z}
on
\mathbb{X}_{\mathsfbf{c}}
is a contraction as soon as
\mathsfbf{c}^{-1}e^{2\beta B_{\Phi}+z\mathsfbf{c}{C}(\beta)}<1
, that is for

. With the choice of constants of Figure

. Notice how in this case, as we predicted at the beginning of this subsection, Assumption

with
\mathsfbf{c}=e^{3\beta B_{\Phi}}
yields a larger uniqueness regime than by just using the Ruelle bound with
\mathsfbf{c}_{z}
.

\mathsfbf{c}=1
trivially holds, so one can actually find that the optimal domain is actually obtained for
\mathsfbf{c}=e

\beta=1
,
{C}(1)=1
, the critical activity

\mathsfbf{a}=1
and

4.3

Uniqueness domain

We can now state the main result of this section. Recall that, for any
\beta>0
, we have a critical threshold
{\swabfamily z}_{\text{crit}}(\beta)>0
such that for any
z\in(0,{\swabfamily z}_{\text{crit}}(\beta))
, the Kirkwood–Salsburg operator
\mathsfbf{K}_{z}
is a contraction in
\mathbb{X}_{\mathsfbf{c}}
.

Theorem 2
.

Let
H
be an energy functional as in (
5
), satisfying Assumptions
1′
+
3′
+
4′
. For any fixed inverse temperature
\beta>0
, there exists a critical activity
{\swabfamily z}_{\text{crit}}(\beta)>0
such that, for any
z\in(0,{\swabfamily z}_{\text{crit}}(\beta))
, there exists at most one infinite-volume Gibbs point process
P
in
\mathscr{G}_{z,\beta}(H)
.

Proof.

Let
\beta>0
,
z\in(0,{\swabfamily z}_{\text{crit}}(\beta))
, and consider two Gibbs point processes
P,\hat{P}\in\mathscr{G}_{z,\beta}(H)
.

(i)

We know from Proposition
4.6
, that the correlation functions
\rho^{(P)}
and
\rho^{(\hat{P})}
both satisfy the Kirkwood–Salsburg equations
(KS)z
.

(ii)

By assumption,
\rho^{(P)}
and
\rho^{(\hat{P})}
satisfy a Ruelle bound for the same
\mathsfbf{c}
, and are therefore both elements of
\mathbb{X}_{\mathsfbf{c}}
.

(iii)

For
z<{\swabfamily z}_{\text{crit}}(\beta)
,
(KS)z
has a unique solution, so that the correlation functions of
\hat{P}
– and therefore its factorial moment measures
(\alpha^{(\hat{P})}_{N})_{N}
– must coincide with those of
P
.

(iv)

For any
N\geq 1
and any bounded
\Gamma\subset\mathscr{E}
, we compute

\begin{split}\alpha^{(P)}_{N}(\Gamma^{N})&=\mathbb{E}\left[\lvert\gamma_{\Gamma}\rvert\big(\lvert\gamma_{\Gamma}\rvert-1\big)\dots\big(\lvert\gamma_{\Gamma}\rvert-N+1\big)\right]\\
&=\int_{\Gamma^{N}}\rho_{N}(\mathsfbf{x}_{1},\dots,\mathsfbf{x}_{N})z^{N}\sigma(d\mathsfbf{x}_{1})\dots\sigma(d\mathsfbf{x}_{N})\\
&\leq\int_{\Gamma^{N}}\prod_{i=1}^{N}(z\mathsfbf{c}(\mathsfbf{x}_{i}))\sigma(d\mathsfbf{x}_{1})\dots\sigma(d\mathsfbf{x}_{N})=(zc_{\Gamma})^{N},\end{split}

with
c_{\Gamma}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\int_{\Gamma}\mathsfbf{c}(\mathsfbf{x})\sigma(d\mathsfbf{x})<+\infty
, since
\mathsfbf{c}
is locally integrable (under Assumptions
3′
+
4′
).

We can then conclude that
P=\hat{P}
(see
[
12
]
, and Proposition 4.12 of
[
11
]
); in other words,
\mathscr{G}_{z,\beta}(H)=\{P\}
.
∎

Example 1

(continued)
.

Consider here a potential
\phi=\phi_{hc}
with a pure hard core at some diameter
R>0
, i.e.
\phi_{l}\equiv 0
.
Taking
a_{0}=R
in the range Assumption
2
yields a path potential
\Phi
(stable, with stability constant
B_{\Phi}=0
) of the form

\Phi(\mathsfbf{x}_{1},\mathsfbf{x}_{2})=(+\infty)\,\mathds{1}_{[0,R)}\big(\inf_{s\in[0,1]}\lvert x_{1}+m_{1}(s)-x_{2}-m_{2}(s)\rvert\big).

Under this interaction, two Langevin diffusions are forbidden from coming closer than
R
to each other, at any given time
s\in[0,1]
.

For such a choice of
\Phi
– which satisfies Assumptions
1
+
2
+
3
+
4
– for any inverse temperature
\beta>0
and for any activity
z<(e{C}(\beta))^{-1}
, the Gibbs point process
P^{z,\beta}
constructed in Theorem
1
is the unique element of
\mathscr{G}^{\text{temp}}_{z,\beta}(H)
.
