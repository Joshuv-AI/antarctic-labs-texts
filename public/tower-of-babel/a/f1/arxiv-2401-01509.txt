Rigorous uniaxial limit of the Qian–Sheng inertial Q-tensor hydrodynamics for liquid crystals
Sirui Li, Wei Wang, Qi Zeng

Abstract
This article is concerned with the rigorous connections between the inertial Qian–Sheng model and the Ericksen–Leslie model for the liquid crystal flow, under a more general condition of coefficients. More specifically, in the framework of Hilbert expansions, we show that: (i) when the elastic coefficients tend to zero (also called the uniaxial limit), the smooth solution to the inertial Qian–Sheng model converges to that to the full inertial Ericksen–Leslie model; (ii) when the elastic coefficients and the inertial coefficient tend to zero simultaneously, the smooth solution to the inertial Qian–Sheng model converges to that to the noninertial Ericksen–Leslie model. Keywords. Liquid crystals, Ericksen–Leslie model, Qian–Sheng model, Q Q -tensor theory AMS subject classifications. Primary, 35Q35; Secondary, 35Q30, 76D05

Introduction

Liquid crystals are a kind of complex fluids with local orientational order, in which physical properties lie between liquid and solid. Its local anisotropy typically originates from the nonuniform distribution of nonspherical rigid molecules. The classification of liquid-crystal theories is mainly based on different characterizations of the local anisotropy. As for the uniaxial nematic phase formed by rodlike molecules, the local anisotropy can be described by an orientational distribution function, a second-order symmetric traceless tensor, or a unit vector. The resulting hydrodynamics are molecular theories (such as the Doi–Onsager model
[
6
]
), tensor theories (such as the Beris–Edwards model
[
3
]
and the Qian–Sheng model
[
37

in the Landau–de Gennes framework and the molecular-theory-based tensor model
[
12
]
obtained by closure approximations) and vector theories (such as the Ericksen–Leslie model
[
10
,
22
]
), respectively.
Since they are derived from different physical considerations and are investigated widely in liquid crystals, it is important to explore the connection between different theories. In this article, we are concerned with the rigorous connection between the Ericksen–Leslie model and the Qian–Sheng model–a typical example of
Q
-tensor hydrodynamics.

We turn to the connection between different liquid-crystal theories. The fundamental subjection usually involves the singular limit problem.
From molecular theories or tensor theories, one could derive the Ericksen–Leslie theory with its coefficients expressed by those in the molecular models or tensor models. Such derivations are based on the fact that the minimum of the bulk energy must be uniaxial and the whole procedure is done by the Hilbert expansion. In this aspect, recent research has produced important progress, but many issues still remain.
The connection between the Doi–Onsager model and the noninertial Ericksen–Leslie model has been investigated for formal derivations

The aim of this article is to rigorously justify connections between the inertial Qian–Sheng model and the Ericksen–Leslie model in the framework of smooth solutions. The main novelty of our works is stated as follows:

•

Under a more general condition of coefficients, we rigorously prove that the smooth solution to the inertial Qian–Sheng model converges to that of the full inertial Ericksen–Leslie model. The previous work
[
23
]
established this result based on a rather strong assumption:
\mu_{1}\gg J
, i.e., the damping coefficient is much greater than the inertia one. In this article, we remove the strong assumption and obtain an extension of the work
[
23
]
, see Section

•

We investigate the rigorous singular limit of the inertial Qian–Sheng model as two small parameters tend to zero simultaneously. Some attempts have been carried out when the inertial coefficient is regarded as the unique small parameter. The zero inertia limit has been studied for the inertial Ericksen–Leslie model
[
18
,
20

and for the inertial Qian–Sheng model
[

]

\varepsilon
and the inertial coefficient
\eta
are all considered as small parameters satisfying
\eta=\varepsilon^{m}
with
m

5

The main strategy of our proof is to construct approximate solutions near the solution to the limit model by the Hilbert expansion, and then derive the uniform estimates for the difference between true solutions and certain approximate ones. Some new issues and challenges will arise since the
Q
-tensor system enjoys the nonlinear hyperbolic structure and the strong condition of coefficients is discarded. To deal with these difficulties, we will give a new and general way to obtain the desired estimates for the singular terms in the remainder system. A more delicate modified energy with a key additional term is provided to handle the loss of the strong assumption for coefficients. We also deeply study the intrinsic symmetric structure of the remainder system to eliminate higher-order derivative terms, and then provide relatively simple estimates compared with the previous work
[
23
]
. We will clarify the main challenges of our work at the end of Theorem
1.1
.

Notation and Conventions.

The space of symmetric traceless tensors is defined by

\displaystyle\mathbb{S}^{3}_{0}\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{\hbox{\footnotesize def}}}\{Q\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}:Q_{ij}=Q_{ji},~~Q_{ii}=0\},

which is endowed with the inner product
Q_{1}:Q_{2}=Q_{1ij}Q_{2ij}
. Here, we have adopted the Einstein summation convention on repeated indices and will assume it throughout the article. The matrix norm on the five-dimensional space
\mathbb{S}^{3}_{0}
is defined by
|Q|\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{\hbox{\footnotesize def}}}\sqrt{Q_{ij}Q_{ij}}
. For any two tensors
A,B\in\mathbb{S}_{0}^{3}
, we denote
(A\cdot B)_{ij}=A_{ik}B_{kj}
and
A:B=A_{ij}B_{ij}
, and their commutator
[A,B]=A\cdot B-B\cdot A
. For any
Q_{1},Q_{2}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3},\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3})
, their inner product is defined as

\displaystyle\langle Q_{1},Q_{2}\rangle\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{\hbox{\footnotesize def}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}Q_{1ij}(\mathbf{x}):Q_{2ij}(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}.

The notation
\mathbf{n}_{1}\otimes\mathbf{n}_{2}
represents the tensor product of two vectors
\mathbf{n}_{1}
and
\mathbf{n}_{2}
, and the symbol
\otimes
is usually omitted for simplicity. We utilize
f_{,j}
to denote
\partial_{j}f
and
\mathbf{I}
to denote the second-order identity tensor.

1.1
Ericksen–Leslie theory

The uniaxial hydrodynamics for nematic phases is called the Ericksen–Leslie theory
[
10
,
22
]
, which is a coupled system between the evolution equation of the unit vector field
\mathbf{n}(t,\mathbf{x})
and the Navier–Stokes equation of the velocity field
\mathbf{v}(t,\mathbf{x})
. The full form of the Ericksen–Leslie model is given by

\displaystyle\mathbf{n}\times(I\ddot{\mathbf{n}}-\mathbf{h}+\gamma_{1}\mathbf{N}+\gamma_{2}\mathbf{D}\cdot\mathbf{n})=0,

(1.1)

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{v}}=-\nabla p+\nabla\cdot(\sigma^{L}+\sigma^{E}),

(1.2)

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v}=0,

(1.3)

where the notation
\dot{f}=(\partial_{t}+\mathbf{v}\cdot\nabla)f
stands for the material derivative, and
I
is
the moment of inertial density
which is usually small. The inertial term
\ddot{\mathbf{n}}
is the material derivative of
\dot{\mathbf{n}}
. The symmetric and skew-symmetric parts of the velocity gradient are denoted as, respectively,

\displaystyle\mathbf{D}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\nabla\mathbf{v}+(\nabla\mathbf{v})^{T}\right),~~\boldsymbol{\Omega}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\nabla\mathbf{v}-(\nabla\mathbf{v})^{T}\right).

The co-rotational time flux of the director
\mathbf{n}
is defined by
\mathbf{N}=\dot{\mathbf{n}}-\boldsymbol{\Omega}\cdot\mathbf{n}
. The molecular field
\mathbf{h}
is given by

\displaystyle\mathbf{h}=-\frac{\delta E_{F}}{\delta\mathbf{n}}=-\frac{\partial E_{F}}{\partial\mathbf{n}}+\nabla\cdot\frac{\partial E_{F}}{\partial(\nabla\mathbf{n})}.

Here,
E_{F}
is called the Oseen–Frank distortion energy, i.e.,

\displaystyle E_{F}(\mathbf{n},\nabla\mathbf{n})=

\displaystyle\frac{k_{1}}{2}(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{n})^{2}+\frac{k_{2}}{2}(\mathbf{n}\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf{n}))^{2}+\frac{k_{3}}{2}|\mathbf{n}\times(\nabla\times\mathbf{n})|^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{k_{2}+k_{4}}{2}(\text{Tr}(\nabla\mathbf{n})^{2}-(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{n})^{2}),

where the constants
k_{1},k_{2}
and
k_{3}
represent deformation moduli of
splay
,
twist
and
bend
, respectively, and the last term is a null Lagrangian which is related to boundary terms.

In the equation of
\mathbf{v}
, the pressure
p
ensures the incompressible condition (
1.3
). The constitutive equations for the viscous (Leslie) stress
\sigma^{L}
and the elastic (Ericksen) stress
\sigma^{E}
are given by, respectively,

\displaystyle\sigma^{L}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{1}(\mathbf{n}\mathbf{n}:\mathbf{D})\mathbf{n}\mathbf{n}+\alpha_{2}\mathbf{n}\mathbf{N}+\alpha_{3}\mathbf{N}\mathbf{n}+\alpha_{4}\mathbf{D}+\alpha_{5}\mathbf{n}\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{D}+\alpha_{6}\mathbf{D}\cdot\mathbf{n}\mathbf{n},

(1.4)

\displaystyle\sigma^{E}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\partial E_{F}}{\partial(\nabla\mathbf{n})}\cdot(\nabla\mathbf{n})^{T},

(1.5)

where the constants
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{6}
, called the Leslie viscosities, together with the coefficients
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}
satisfy

\displaystyle\alpha_{2}

\displaystyle+\alpha_{3}=\alpha_{6}-\alpha_{5},

(1.6)

\displaystyle\gamma_{1}=\alpha_{3}

\displaystyle-\alpha_{2},\quad\gamma_{2}=\alpha_{6}-\alpha_{5}.

(1.7)

The equality (
1.6
) is the so-called Parodi relation. The relations (
1.6
)–(
1.7
) will ensure a basic energy law of the system (
1.1
)–(
1.3
):

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{{\mathbb{R}^{3}}}\Big(

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}|\mathbf{v}|^{2}+\frac{I}{2}|\dot{\mathbf{n}}|^{2}+E_{F}\Big)\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}=-\int_{{\mathbb{R}^{3}}}\bigg(\Big(\alpha_{1}+\frac{\gamma_{2}^{2}}{\gamma_{1}}\Big)(\mathbf{D}:\mathbf{n}\mathbf{n})+\alpha_{4}|\mathbf{D}|^{2}

\displaystyle+\Big(\alpha_{5}+\alpha_{6}-\frac{\gamma_{2}^{2}}{\gamma_{1}}\Big)|\mathbf{D}\cdot\mathbf{n}|^{2}+\frac{1}{\gamma_{1}}|\mathbf{n}\times(\mathbf{h}-I\ddot{\mathbf{n}})|^{2}\bigg)\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}.

(1.8)

Here, the coefficients in (
1.8
) satisfy

\displaystyle\alpha_{4}>0,\quad 2\alpha_{4}+\alpha_{5}+\alpha_{6}-\frac{\gamma_{2}^{2}}{\gamma_{1}}>0,\quad\alpha_{1}+\frac{3}{2}\alpha_{4}+\alpha_{5}+\alpha_{6}>0,

(1.9)

which implies that the energy in (
1.8
) is dissipated (see Lemma
4.4
for details).

It is worth emphasizing that (
1.1
)–(
1.3
) is a hyperbolic-parabolic system due to the appearance of the inertial term
I\ddot{\mathbf{n}}
. If the inertial term is neglected, then the system (
1.1
)–(
1.3
) becomes immediately a parabolic Ericksen–Leslie system. In general, the inertial system with the hyperbolic feature will bring more challenges in analysis, compared with its noninertial counterpart.

1.2
Landau–de Gennes theory

In this theory, the local orientational order of nematic phases is described by a second-order symmetric traceless tensor
Q(\mathbf{x})
. Physically,
Q(\mathbf{x})
can be understood as the second-order moment of
f
:

\displaystyle Q(\mathbf{x})=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\Big(\mathbf{m}\mathbf{m}-\frac{1}{3}\mathbf{I}\Big)f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{m})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{m},

where
f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{m})
represents the distribution function on
{\mathbb{S}^{2}}
with the orientation parallel to
\mathbf{m}
at material point
\mathbf{x}
. We could classify
Q(\mathbf{x})
into three classes.
The tensor
Q(\mathbf{x})
is said to be
isotropic
if it has three equal eigenvalues, i.e.,
Q(\mathbf{x})=0
,
uniaxial
if it has two equal nonzero eigenvalues, and
biaxial
if it has three distinct eigenvalues.

The Landau–de Gennes free energy contains two parts, the bulk energy and the elastic energy, which is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(Q,\nabla Q)

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}^{3}}}\bigg\{-\frac{a}{2}\text{Tr}(Q^{2})-\frac{b}{3}\text{Tr}(Q^{3})+\frac{c}{4}(\text{Tr}(Q^{2}))^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad\quad+\frac{1}{2}\Big(L_{1}|\nabla Q|^{2}+L_{2}Q_{ij,j}Q_{ik,k}+L_{3}Q_{ij,k}Q_{ik,j}\Big)\bigg\}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{\hbox{\footnotesize def}}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}^{3}}}\Big(f_{b}(Q)+f_{e}(\nabla Q)\Big)\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x},

(1.10)

where the bulk energy density
f_{b}
describes transitions between homogeneous phases, and nonnegative parameters
a,b,c
depend on the material and temperature.
The elastic energy density
f_{e}
penalizing spatial nonhomogeneity contains some quadratic terms of
\nabla Q
, and the material dependent elastic constants
L_{i}(i=1,2,3)
are usually small. More detailed introductions can be referred to
[
8
,
34
]
.

To ensure the strict positiveness of the elastic energy, we assume that the elastic coefficients
L_{i}(i=1,2,3)
satisfy

\displaystyle L_{1}>0,\quad L_{1}+L_{2}+L_{3}>0,

i.e., there exists a constant
L_{0}=L_{0}(L_{1},L_{2},L_{3})>0
, such that

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}^{3}}}f_{e}(\nabla Q)\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\geq L_{0}\|\nabla Q\|^{2}_{L^{2}}.

(1.11)

The inertial Qian–Sheng model
[
37
]
is a representative
Q
-tensor hydrodynamics based on the Landau–de Gennes framework, which is given by

\displaystyle J\ddot{Q}+\mu_{1}\dot{Q}=

\displaystyle~\mathbf{H}-\frac{\mu_{2}}{2}\mathbf{D}+\mu_{1}[\boldsymbol{\Omega},Q],

(1.12)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{v}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{v}=

\displaystyle-\nabla{p}+\nabla\cdot\big(\sigma+\sigma^{d}\big),

(1.13)

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v}=

\displaystyle~0,

(1.14)

where
\dot{Q}=(\partial_{t}+\mathbf{v}\cdot\nabla)Q
and
\ddot{Q}=(\partial_{t}+\mathbf{v}\cdot\nabla)\dot{Q}
. The constant
J
represents the
inertial density
which is responsible for the hyperbolic feature of the system. In this article, we will consider the case that
J
is not a small parameter, and the case that
J
is very small, respectively. Moreover, the viscous stress
\sigma
, the distortion stress
\sigma^{d}
and the molecular field
\mathbf{H}
are expressed by

\displaystyle\sigma(Q,\mathbf{v})=

\displaystyle~\beta_{1}Q(Q:\mathbf{D})+\beta_{4}\mathbf{D}+\beta_{5}\mathbf{D}\cdot Q+\beta_{6}Q\cdot\mathbf{D}+\beta_{7}(\mathbf{D}\cdot Q^{2}+Q^{2}\cdot\mathbf{D})

\displaystyle+\frac{\mu_{2}}{2}(\dot{Q}-[\boldsymbol{\Omega},Q])+\mu_{1}\big[Q,(\dot{Q}-[\boldsymbol{\Omega},Q])\big],

(1.15)

\displaystyle\sigma^{d}=

\displaystyle~\sigma^{d}(Q,Q),

(1.16)

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}_{ij}=

\displaystyle-\Big(\frac{\delta{\mathcal{F}(Q,\nabla Q)}}{\delta Q}\Big)_{ij}=-\frac{\partial f_{b}}{\partial Q_{ij}}+\partial_{k}\Big(\frac{\partial f_{e}}{\partial Q_{ij,k}}\Big),

where the tensor
\sigma^{d}(Q,\widetilde{Q})
is defined as

\displaystyle\sigma^{d}_{ji}(Q,\widetilde{Q})\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{\hbox{\footnotesize def}}}-\frac{\partial f_{e}}{\partial Q_{kl,j}}\partial_{i}\widetilde{Q}_{kl}=-(L_{1}Q_{kl,j}\widetilde{Q}_{kl,i}+L_{2}Q_{km,m}\widetilde{Q}_{kj,i}+L_{3}Q_{kj,l}\widetilde{Q}_{kl,i}).

The viscosity coefficients
\beta_{1},\beta_{4},\beta_{5},\beta_{6},\beta_{7},\mu_{1}
, and
\mu_{2}
in (
1.2
) can be linked by

\displaystyle\beta_{6}-\beta_{5}=\mu_{2},

(1.17)

which corresponds to the Parodi relation (
1.6
). We also assume that the coefficients in the system (
1.12
)–(
1.14
) satisfy

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\beta_{1},\beta_{4},\mu_{1}>0,~\beta_{4}-\frac{\mu_{2}^{2}}{4\mu_{1}}>0,~\beta_{7}\geq 0;\\
&(\beta_{5}+\beta_{6})^{2}<8\beta_{7}\left(\beta_{4}-\frac{\mu_{2}^{2}}{4\mu_{1}}\right)~\text{if }~\beta_{7}\neq 0;~\beta_{5}+\beta_{6}=0~\text{if }\beta_{7}=0.\end{aligned}\right.

(1.18)

The relations (
1.17
)–(
1.18
) will guarantee that the system (
1.12
)–(
1.14
) enjoys the following energy dissipation law
[
23
]
:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\bigg(\int_{{\mathbb{R}^{3}}}\frac{1}{2}(|\mathbf{v}|^{2}+J|\dot{Q}|^{2})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}+\mathcal{F}(Q,\nabla Q)\bigg)

\displaystyle\quad=-\beta_{1}\|Q:\mathbf{D}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}-\left(\beta_{4}-\frac{\mu_{2}^{2}}{4\mu_{1}}\right)\|\mathbf{D}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}-(\beta_{5}+\beta_{6})\langle\mathbf{D}\cdot Q,\mathbf{D}\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad-2\beta_{7}\|\mathbf{D}\cdot Q\|^{2}_{L^{2}}-\mu_{1}\left\|\dot{Q}-[\boldsymbol{\Omega},Q]+\frac{\mu_{2}}{2\mu_{1}}\mathbf{D}\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}}.

(1.19)

1.3
Main results

This subsection will be devoted to formulating our main results. In the inertial Qian–Sheng model, two different small parameters originate from the elastic coefficients
L_{i}(i=1,2,3)
and the inertial constant
J
, respectively. Since the elastic coefficients are very small, we consider the rescaled energy functional with a small parameter
\varepsilon
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{\varepsilon}(Q,\nabla Q)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}^{3}}}\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}f_{b}(Q)+f_{e}(\nabla Q)\right)\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x},

where the coefficients satisfy
a,b,c,L_{i}(1\leq i\leq 3)\sim O(1)
. Another is the small inertial density parameter denoted by
\eta
. The relationship between
\varepsilon
and
\eta
determines the type of problem we have. In this article, we take
\eta=\varepsilon^{m}
with
m
being a nonnegative integer.

The main aim of this article is to study the singular limit problem of the inertial Qian–Sheng system with small parameter
\varepsilon
, which is given by

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{m}J\ddot{Q}^{\varepsilon}+\mu_{1}\dot{Q}^{\varepsilon}=

\displaystyle~\mathbf{H}^{\varepsilon}-\frac{\mu_{2}}{2}\mathbf{D}^{\varepsilon}+\mu_{1}[\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{\varepsilon},Q^{\varepsilon}],

(1.20)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}{\mathbf{v}}^{\varepsilon}+\mathbf{v}^{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla{\mathbf{v}}^{\varepsilon}=

\displaystyle-\nabla{p^{\varepsilon}}+\nabla\cdot\Big(\sigma(Q^{\varepsilon},\mathbf{v}^{\varepsilon})+\sigma^{d}(Q^{\varepsilon},Q^{\varepsilon})\Big),

(1.21)

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v}^{\varepsilon}=

\displaystyle~0,

(1.22)

where

\dot{Q}^{\varepsilon}=(\partial_{t}+\mathbf{v}^{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla)Q^{\varepsilon}
,
\ddot{Q}^{\varepsilon}=(\partial_{t}+\mathbf{v}^{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla)Q^{\varepsilon}

and

\displaystyle\mathbf{D}^{\varepsilon}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\nabla\mathbf{v}^{\varepsilon}+(\nabla\mathbf{v}^{\varepsilon})^{T}\right),~\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{\varepsilon}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\nabla\mathbf{v}^{\varepsilon}-(\nabla\mathbf{v}^{\varepsilon})^{T}\right).

The viscous stress
\sigma(Q^{\varepsilon},\mathbf{v}^{\varepsilon})
and the distortion stress
\sigma^{d}(Q^{\varepsilon},Q^{\varepsilon})
can be expressed by (
1.2
) and (
1.16
), respectively.
The molecular field
\mathbf{H}^{\varepsilon}
is given by

\mathbf{H}^{\varepsilon}=-\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}^{\varepsilon}}{\delta Q^{\varepsilon}}=-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\mathcal{J}(Q^{\varepsilon})-\mathcal{L}(Q^{\varepsilon}),

where two operators are defined as, respectively,

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}(Q)=

\displaystyle-aQ-bQ^{2}+c|Q|^{2}Q+\frac{b}{3}|Q|^{2}\mathbf{I},

(1.23)

\displaystyle(\mathcal{L}(Q))_{kl}=

\displaystyle-\Big(L_{1}\Delta Q_{kl}+\frac{1}{2}(L_{2}+L_{3})\Big(Q_{km,ml}+Q_{lm,mk}-\frac{2}{3}\delta_{kl}Q_{ij,ij}\Big)\Big).

(1.24)

We denote by
{\chi}
the characteristic function given by

\displaystyle\chi(m)=\begin{cases}1,\quad m=0,\\
0,\quad m\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}.\end{cases}

(1.25)

The relationship between coefficients in the Qian–Sheng model and those in the corresponding limit model can be expressed by

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\alpha_{1}=\beta_{1}s^{2},\quad\alpha_{2}=\frac{1}{2}\mu_{2}s-\mu_{1}s^{2},\\
&\alpha_{3}=\frac{1}{2}\mu_{2}s+\mu_{1}s^{2},\quad\alpha_{4}=\beta_{4}-\frac{s}{3}(\beta_{5}+\beta_{6})+\frac{2}{9}\beta_{7}s^{2},\\
&\alpha_{5}=\beta_{5}s+\frac{1}{3}\beta_{7}s^{2},\quad\alpha_{6}=\beta_{6}s+\frac{1}{3}\beta_{7}s^{2},\\
&\gamma_{1}=2\mu_{1}s^{2},\quad\gamma_{2}=\mu_{2}s,\quad I={{\chi}}(m)2s^{2}J,\\
&k_{1}=k_{3}=(2L_{1}+L_{2}+L_{3})s^{2},\quad k_{2}=2L_{1}s^{2},\quad k_{4}=L_{3}s^{2}.\end{aligned}\right.

(1.26)

If
\chi(m)=1
, then (
1.26
) are defined as the coefficients in the full inertial Ericksen–Leslie model. If
\chi(m)=0
, i.e., the inertial constant
I=0
, then (
1.26
) becomes the coefficients in the noninertial Ericksen–Leslie model.

\displaystyle(\mathbf{v},\nabla\mathbf{n},{{\chi}}(m)\partial_{t}\mathbf{n})\in L^{\infty}([0,T];H^{\ell}),\quad{\ell\geq 20.}

(1.27)

Let
Q_{0}(t,\mathbf{x})=s(\mathbf{n}(t,\mathbf{x})\mathbf{n}(t,\mathbf{x})-\frac{1}{3}\mathbf{I})
be the uniaxial minimizer of the bulk energy

Remark 1.2

The proof of Theorem

is based on mainly two ingredients: the existence of smooth solutions to the equations of

The first ingredient relies on the local existence of smooth solution to the Ericksen–Leslie system on

The second ingredient is to prove the uniform boundedness of the remainders
(Q_{R},\mathbf{P}_{R},\mathbf{v}_{R})

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{m}J(\partial_{t}+\mathbf{v}^{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{P}_{R}=-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{n}}^{\varepsilon}(Q_{R})-\frac{\mu_{2}}{2}\mathbf{D}_{R}-\mu_{1}(\mathbf{P}_{R}-[\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{R},Q_{0}])+\cdots,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}{\mathbf{v}}_{R}=-\nabla p_{R}+\nabla\cdot\Big\{\beta_{4}\mathbf{D}_{R}+\frac{\mu_{2}}{2}(\mathbf{P}_{R}-[\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{R},Q_{0}])+\mu_{1}\big[Q_{0},(\mathbf{P}_{R}-[\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{R},Q_{0}])\big]\Big\}+\cdots,

[

.

The main obstacle towards the uniform estimate comes from the singular term
\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\mathcal{H}^{\varepsilon}_{\mathbf{n}}(Q_{R})

Since the remainder system is a hyperbolic system, the term

[

is imposed. Therefore, a new difficulty of this article is how to derive the uniform estimate for the remainder system when the strong assumption of coefficients is removed.

For this purpose, we introduce a suitable energy functional

in (

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left\langle\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{n}}^{\varepsilon}(Q_{R}),\mathbf{P}_{R}\right\rangle\leq\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\Big(-\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\langle\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{n}}^{\varepsilon}(Q_{R}),Q_{R}\rangle-\varepsilon^{m}\mathcal{A}(Q_{R},\mathbf{P}_{R})\Big)+\cdots,

At last, we show that

\displaystyle~C(\varepsilon{\mathfrak{E}}+\varepsilon{\mathfrak{E}}^{\frac{3}{2}}),

\displaystyle\mathfrak{y}_{5}\leq

\displaystyle~C\varepsilon\|\mathbf{v}_{R}\|_{L^{2}}\|Q_{R}\|_{L^{2}}+C\varepsilon^{4}\|\mathbf{v}_{R}\|_{H^{2}}\|\nabla\partial_{i}Q_{R}\|_{L^{2}}\|Q_{R}\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle~C(\varepsilon{\mathfrak{E}}+\varepsilon{\mathfrak{E}}^{\frac{3}{2}}).

Summarizing the estimates yields

W_{1}\leq C({\mathfrak{E}}+\varepsilon{\mathfrak{E}}^{\frac{3}{2}}+{\mathfrak{E}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathfrak{F}^{\frac{1}{2}}+\varepsilon{\mathfrak{E}}\mathfrak{F}^{\frac{1}{2}}).

(A.1)

Using Lemma
A.1
again, we infer that

\displaystyle S_{1}=

\displaystyle-\varepsilon\Big\langle\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{n}}^{\varepsilon}(\partial_{i}Q_{R}),\partial_{i}\dot{Q}_{R}\Big\rangle+\varepsilon\Big\langle[\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{n}},\partial_{i}]Q_{R},\partial_{i}\dot{Q}_{R}\Big\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\varepsilon\Big\langle\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{n}}^{\varepsilon}(\partial_{i}Q_{R}),\dot{\overline{\partial_{i}{Q}_{R}}}+\partial_{i}\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\cdot\nabla Q_{R}\Big\rangle+\varepsilon\Big\langle[\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{n}},\partial_{i}]Q_{R},\partial_{i}\dot{Q}_{R}\Big\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\varepsilon^{2}\langle\mathcal{L}(\partial_{i}Q_{R}),(\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\cdot\nabla)\partial_{i}Q_{R}\rangle+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\langle[\partial_{t}+\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\cdot\nabla,\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{n}}]\partial_{i}Q_{R},\partial_{i}Q_{R}\rangle

\displaystyle-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\Big\langle\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{n}}^{\varepsilon}(\partial_{i}Q_{R}),\partial_{i}{Q}_{R}\Big\rangle

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle~C\Big(\|\nabla Q_{R}\|_{L^{2}}\|\varepsilon\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{n}}^{\varepsilon}(\partial_{i}Q_{R})\|_{L^{2}}+\|Q_{R}\|_{L^{2}}\|\varepsilon\partial_{i}\dot{Q}_{R}\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle+\varepsilon^{2}\|\partial_{i}Q_{R}\|^{2}_{H^{1}}+\varepsilon\|\partial_{i}Q_{R}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\Big)-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\Big\langle\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{n}}^{\varepsilon}(\partial_{i}Q_{R}),\partial_{i}{Q}_{R}\Big\rangle

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\Big\langle\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{n}}^{\varepsilon}(\partial_{i}Q_{R}),\partial_{i}{Q}_{R}\Big\rangle+C({\mathfrak{E}}+\varepsilon{\mathfrak{E}}^{\frac{3}{2}}).

Combining (

) with (

Thus, we obtain the desired estimate.

Proof of (

).

Similarly, we have

\displaystyle-\varepsilon^{3}\langle\Delta\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{n}}^{\varepsilon}(Q_{R}),\Delta\mathbf{P}_{R}\rangle

\displaystyle=-\varepsilon^{3}\langle\Delta\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{n}}^{\varepsilon}(Q_{R}),\Delta(\mathbf{v}_{R}\cdot\nabla Q^{\varepsilon})\rangle-\varepsilon^{3}\langle\Delta\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{n}}^{\varepsilon}(Q_{R}),\Delta\dot{Q}_{R}\rangle

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{\hbox{\footnotesize def}}}W_{2}+S_{2}.

By a straightforward computation, the term
W_{2}
can be expressed by

\displaystyle W_{2}=

\displaystyle-\varepsilon^{3}\langle\Delta(\mathbf{v}_{R}\cdot\nabla Q^{\varepsilon}),\Delta\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{n}}(Q_{R})+\varepsilon\mathcal{L}(\Delta Q_{R})\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\varepsilon^{3}\langle\mathbf{v}_{R}\cdot\nabla\Delta Q^{\varepsilon},\varepsilon\mathcal{L}(\Delta Q_{R})\rangle-\varepsilon^{3}\langle\Delta\mathbf{v}_{R}\cdot\nabla Q^{\varepsilon},\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{n}}(\Delta Q_{R})\rangle

\displaystyle-\varepsilon^{3}\langle\Delta\mathbf{v}_{R}\cdot\nabla Q^{\varepsilon},[\Delta,\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{n}}]Q_{R}\rangle-\varepsilon^{3}\langle\Delta\mathbf{v}_{R}\cdot\nabla Q^{\varepsilon},\varepsilon\mathcal{L}(\Delta Q_{R})\rangle

\displaystyle-\varepsilon^{3}\langle\partial_{i}\mathbf{v}_{R}\cdot\nabla\partial_{i}Q^{\varepsilon},\Delta\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{n}}(Q_{R})\rangle-\varepsilon^{3}\langle\mathbf{v}_{R}\cdot\nabla\Delta Q^{\varepsilon},\Delta\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{n}}(Q_{R})\rangle

\displaystyle-\varepsilon^{3}\langle\partial_{i}\mathbf{v}_{R}\cdot\nabla\partial_{i}Q^{\varepsilon},\varepsilon\mathcal{L}(\Delta Q_{R})\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum^{7}_{k=1}\mathfrak{m}_{k}.

From integration by parts and Lemma
A.1
, we infer that

\displaystyle\mathfrak{m}_{1}

\displaystyle=-\varepsilon^{4}\langle\mathbf{v}_{R}\cdot\nabla\Delta\widetilde{Q},\varepsilon\mathcal{L}(\Delta Q_{R})\rangle-\varepsilon^{7}\langle\mathbf{v}_{R}\cdot\nabla\Delta Q_{R},\mathcal{L}(\Delta Q_{R})\rangle

\displaystyle=\varepsilon^{4}\langle\partial_{i}(\mathbf{v}_{R}\cdot\nabla\Delta\widetilde{Q}),\varepsilon\mathcal{L}(\partial_{i}Q_{R})\rangle-\varepsilon^{7}\langle\mathbf{v}_{R}\cdot\nabla\Delta Q_{R},\mathcal{L}(\Delta Q_{R})\rangle

\Delta\mathbf{v}_{R}\cdot\nabla Q_{0}\in{\rm Ker}\mathcal{H}
, it follows that

\displaystyle\mathfrak{m}_{2}

\mathfrak{m}_{i}(i=3,4,\dots,6)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{m}_{3}\leq

\displaystyle\mathfrak{m}_{4}\leq

\displaystyle\mathfrak{m}_{6}\leq
