Parabolic Lusztig varieties and chromatic symmetric functions
Alex Abreu, Antonio Nigro

Abstract
. The characters of Kazhdan–Lusztig elements of the Hecke algebra over S n S_{n} (and in particular, the chromatic symmetric function of indifference graphs) are completely encoded in the (intersection) cohomology of certain subvarieties of the flag variety. Considering the forgetful map to some partial flag variety, the decomposition theorem tells us that this cohomology splits as a sum of intersection cohomology groups with coefficients in some local systems of subvarieties of the partial flag variety. We prove that these local systems correspond to representations of subgroups of S n S_{n} . An explicit characterization of such representations would provide a recursive formula for the computation of such characters/chromatic symmetric functions, which could settle Haiman’s conjecture about the positivity of the monomial characters of Kazhdan–Lusztig elements and Stanley–Stembridge conjecture about e e -positivity of chromatic symmetric function of indifference graphs. We also find a connection between the character of certain homology groups of subvarieties of the partial flag varieties and the Grojnowski–Haiman hybrid basis of the Hecke algebra.

The connection between chromatic symmetric functions and geometry goes back to Stanley (

The Shareshian–Wachs (

[

]
) and Guay-Paquet (

]

Namely, the chromatic quasisymmetric function of an indifference graph is the omega-dual Frobenius character of the cohomology of an associated Hessenberg variety. This motivated a flurry of work trying to better understand the cohomology of Hessenberg varieties, including
[

]

,

The chromatic symmetric function is also related to the characters of the Kazhdan–Lusztig basis elements
C^{\prime}_{w}

For general

also have geometric meaning. Namely, they are encoded in the geometry of certain subvarieties of the flag variety
\mathcal{B}:=GL_{n}/B

\mathcal{Y}_{w}(X)

\mathcal{Y}_{w}(X)=\{V_{\bullet}=(0\subset V_{1}\subset V_{2}\subset\ldots\subset V_{n}=\mathbb{C}^{n});\dim XV_{i}\cap V_{j}\geq r_{i,j}(w)\},

where

If

2.2.
Hecke Algebras in type A

The Hecke algebra
H_{n}
of the symmetric group
S_{n}
is a
q
-deformation of the group algebra
\mathbb{C}[S_{n}]
. It is defined as the algebra over
\mathbb{C}(q^{\pm\frac{1}{2}})
generated by
T_{s_{1}},\ldots,T_{s_{n-1}}
such that

\displaystyle T_{s_{i}}^{2}=

\displaystyle(q-1)T_{s_{i}}+q

\displaystyle T_{s_{i}}T_{s_{i+1}}T_{s_{i}}=

\displaystyle T_{s_{i+1}}T_{s_{i}}T_{s_{i+1}}

\displaystyle T_{s_{i}}T_{s_{j}}=

\displaystyle T_{s_{j}}T_{s_{i}}\quad\quad\quad\text{ if }|i-j|>1.

If
w=\prod_{j=1}^{\ell(w)}s_{i_{j}}
is a reduced expression for
w\in S_{n}
, we define
T_{w}=\prod_{j=1}^{\ell(w)}T_{s_{i_{j}}}
. These
T_{w}
form a basis for
H_{n}
as a
\mathbb{C}(q^{\pm\frac{1}{2}})
vector space, which has an involution
\iota\colon H_{n}\to H_{n}
given by
\iota(T_{w})=T_{w^{-1}}^{-1}
and
\iota(q^{\frac{1}{2}})=q^{-\frac{1}{2}}
. The Kazhdan–Lusztig basis
\{C^{\prime}_{w}\}_{w\in S_{n}}
of
H_{n}
is defined by the following properties:

(1)

\iota(C^{\prime}_{w})=C^{\prime}_{w}
,

(2)

there exists polynomials
P_{z,w}(q)
, for each
z,w\in S_{n}
, such that:

(a)

the degree of
P_{z,w}(q)
is at most
\frac{\ell(w)-\ell(z)-1}{2}
if
z<w
(in the Bruhat order),

(b)

if
z=w
we have that
P_{w,w}(q)=1
,

(c)

if
z\not\leq w
, then
P_{z,w}(q)=0
,

(d)

we have an equality
q^{\frac{\ell(w)}{2}}C^{\prime}_{w}=\sum_{z\leq w}P_{z,w}(q)T_{z}
.

The polynomials
P_{z,w}
are the so-called Kazhdan–Lusztig polynomials.

, denote by

Now, we turn our attention to small maps. A proper morphism

with

, we have that

(
[

]
)

If

An important example of a small map is the natural map

2.4.
Character Sheaves

Every element
g
of a Bruhat stratum
BwB
can be written uniquely as
u\dot{w}b
, with
u\in U_{w}
and
b\in B
. Since
B=TU
, we have a projection
p\colon B\to T
, which induces a projection
p_{w}\colon BwB\to T
given by
p_{w}(u\dot{w}b)=p(b)
. The map
p_{w}
is
B
-equivariant, where
B
acts on
B\dot{w}B
by conjugation and
B
acts on
T
as
tu\cdot t^{\prime}=t^{\prime}t\dot{w}t^{-1}\dot{w}^{-1}
. If
L
is a local system on
T
, we can pull it back via

to obtain a local system on
BwB
. Note that
p_{w}^{*}(L)
is
B
-invariant. We can identify
\mathcal{Y}_{w}^{\circ}:=\{(X,gB);g^{-1}Xg\in B\dot{w}B\}
with
\frac{G\times B\dot{w}B}{B}
(where
B
acts on

by conjugation and on

by

) via the isomorphism

\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}_{w}^{\circ}

\displaystyle\to\frac{G\times B\dot{w}B}{B}

\displaystyle(X,gB)

\displaystyle\to(g,g^{-1}Xg).

Since
p_{w}^{*}(L)
is
B
-invariant,
p_{w}^{*}(L)
will induce a local system
L_{w}
on
\mathcal{Y}_{w}^{\circ}
. We let
f\colon\mathcal{Y}_{w}^{\circ}\to G
be the projection onto the first factor, that is
f(X,gB)=X
.

When
L
is good enough, that is, when there exists a positive integer

such that

is the trivial sheaf on

(these are called Kummer local systems), Lusztig proved that
f_{!}(L_{w})
is a semisimple complex, that is, it is a sum of shifted simple perverse sheaves. Each simple perverse sheaf that is a summand of

for some

and some

is called a

.

The study of character sheaves, which includes their classification, was done by Lusztig in
[
Lus85a
,
Lus85b
,
Lus86a
,
Lus86b
]
. See also
[
MS89
]
. As an example, when

is the identity and

is the trivial sheaf, then the simple summands appearing in

correspond to the irreducible representations of

. Indeed, there is a map

(see
[

,
[

), and each irreducible representation

on

is induced by the regular representation of

. In the case that

As noted by Lusztig, we could also work with the closure

to

In this work we are mostly concerned with the case that

3.
Parabolic varieties

In this section we introduce the parabolic Lusztig varieties. We saw in the introduction that for a permutation
w
and a matrix
X
, we can define

\mathcal{Y}_{w}(X)=\{V_{\bullet};XV_{i}\cap V_{j}\geq r_{ij}(w)\}

by analogy with the Schubert variety

\Omega_{w,F_{\bullet}}=\{V_{\bullet};F_{i}\cap V_{j}\geq r_{ij}(w)\}.

The parabolic Schubert varieties are also defined in terms of a fixed flag
F_{\bullet}=F_{1}\subset\ldots\subset F_{n}=\mathbb{C}^{n}
. Given
J\subset\{1,\ldots,n-1\}
a subset of the set of simple transpositions of
S_{n}
and
w
a permutation in
{}^{J}S_{n}
, we define

\Omega_{w,J,F_{\bullet}}=\{V_{\bullet};\dim F_{i}\cap V_{j}\geq r_{i,j}(w),j\notin J\}.

The flag
V_{\bullet}
above is a partial flag

0=V_{0}\subset V_{i_{1}}\subset\cdots\subset V_{i_{m}}

where
\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{m}\}=J^{c}
, thereby comparing how the partial flag
V_{\bullet}
intersects the flag
F_{\bullet}
. If we try to extend this idea to construct parabolic Lusztig varieties by simply comparing
XV_{\bullet}
and
V_{\bullet}
, we will get fewer varieties than needed.

Example 3.1
.

Consider
\Gr(1,n)=\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
. The only possible relative position of
XV_{1}
and
V_{1}
are

(1)

\{V_{1};\dim XV_{1}\cap V_{1}\geq 1\}
, which is the set of the points
(0:\ldots:0:1:0:\ldots:0)
.

(2)

\{V_{1};\dim XV_{1}\cap V_{1}\geq 0\}
, which is the whole
\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
.

However, as we saw in Example
1.2
, there are other varieties that appear in the decomposition theorem, namely the varieties
H_{i}
, which are the union of the coordinates planes of codimension
i
.

The definition of a parabolic Lusztig variety is given in Definition
3.10
below. The main idea is to construct a flag
V^{\prime}_{\bullet}
from
V_{\bullet}
and
X
(Construction
3.7
), compare the flags
XV^{\prime}_{\bullet}
and
V_{\bullet}
, and iterate this construction until it stabilizes.

Let us begin with the general construction for a linear algebraic group (following
[
Lus04
]
). Let
G
be an linear algebraic group and
W
its Weyl group with set of simple reflections
S
. An infinite sequence of pairs
(J_{n},w_{n})_{n\geq 0}
, with
J_{n}\subset S
and
w_{n}\in W
is called
admissible
if the following conditions are satisfied for every
n\geq 0

(1)

J_{n+1}=J_{n}\cap w_{n}J_{n}w_{n}^{-1}
,

(2)

w_{n}\in{}^{J_{n}}W^{J_{n}}
,

(3)

w_{n+1}\in W_{J_{n}}w_{n}W_{J_{n}}
.

Here, as usual, we write
W^{J}
,
{}^{J}W
, and
{}^{J}W^{J}
for the subsets of elements
w
of
W
such that
w
is minimal in
wW_{J}
,
W_{J}w
, and
W_{J}wW_{J}
, respectively.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
(J_{n},w_{n})_{n\geq 0}
be an admissible sequence of pairs. If
J_{n}=w_{n}J_{n}w_{n}^{-1}
for some
n\geq 0
, then
(J_{n+1},w_{n+1})=(J_{n},w_{n})
.

{proof}

By item (1) we have
J_{n+1}=J_{n}
, so that items (2) and (3) imply

w_{n+1}\in{}^{J_{n}}W^{J_{n}}\cap W_{J_{n}}w_{n}W_{J_{n}}=\{w_{n}\}.

Since
J_{n+1}\subset J_{n}
, there exists
n_{0}
such that
J_{n+1}=J_{n}=:J_{\infty}
for every
n\geq n_{0}
, which is to say that
J_{n}=w_{n}J_{n}w_{n}^{-1}
for every
n\geq n_{0}
. In particular,
w_{n+1}=w_{n}=:w_{\infty}
by the lemma above.
Thus the sequence must stabilize at some point,
(J_{n+1},w_{n+1})=(J_{n},w_{n})
for every
n\geq n_{0}
. In fact,
n_{0}
can be chosen as the smallest index such that
(J_{n+1},w_{n+1})=(J_{n},w_{n})
. Now let
\Adm_{W}(J)
be the set of admissible sequences starting with
J_{0}=J
and consider the function

\displaystyle\Gamma_{J}\colon\Adm_{W}(J)

\displaystyle\to W

\displaystyle(J_{n},w_{n})_{n\geq 0}

\displaystyle\mapsto w_{\infty}.

Proposition 3.3

(
[
Lus04
, Proposition 2.5]
)
.

The function
\Gamma_{J}
is injective and its image is precisely
{}^{J}W
.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
J\subset S
and
w\in{}^{J}W^{J}
, then

(1)

\Phi_{J}\cap w\Phi_{J}=\Phi_{J\cap wJw^{-1}}
.

(2)

(\Phi_{J}\cap w(\Phi^{+}\setminus\Phi_{J}))\cup\Phi^{+}\setminus\Phi_{J}=\Phi^{+}\setminus\Phi_{J\cap wJw^{-1}}
.

(3)

(P_{J}\cap\dot{w}P_{J}\dot{w}^{-1})U^{J}=P_{J\cap wJw^{-1}}
.

{proof}

Since
w\in{}^{J}W^{J}
, for each
\beta\in\Phi_{J}\cap\Phi^{+}
we have
w(\beta)\in\Phi^{+}
and
w^{-1}(\beta)\in\Phi^{+}
. Hence, if
\beta\in\Phi_{J}\cap w(\Phi_{J})\cap\Phi^{+}
, then
\beta=w(\beta^{\prime})
with
\beta^{\prime}\in\Phi_{J}\cap\Phi^{+}
. Let
\alpha\in\Delta
be a simple root satisfying
\alpha<\beta
. Since
\beta\in\Phi_{J}
, we have
\alpha\in\Delta_{J}\subset\Phi_{J}\cap\Phi^{+}
, hence
w(\alpha)\in\Phi^{+}
. Moreover, since
\beta=w(\beta^{\prime})
,
\alpha<w(\beta^{\prime})
and both are in
\Phi_{J}
. Hence,
w(\beta^{\prime})=\alpha+\gamma
with
\gamma\in\Phi_{J}\cap\Phi^{+}
. This means that
w^{-1}(\alpha)+w^{-1}(\gamma)=\beta^{\prime}
, where
\beta^{\prime},w^{-1}(\gamma)\in\Phi_{J}\cap\Phi^{+}
in particular
w^{-1}(\alpha)\in\Phi_{J}\cap\Phi^{+}
. We claim that
w^{-1}(\alpha)\in\Delta_{J}
. Indeed, if there exists
\alpha^{\prime}\in\Phi_{J}\cap\Phi^{+}
such that
\alpha^{\prime}<w^{-1}(\alpha)
, then applying
w
, we would have
w(\alpha^{\prime})\in\Phi^{+}
and
w(\alpha^{\prime})<\alpha
, which contradicts the fact that
\alpha\in\Delta_{J}
.

This proves that if
\beta\in\Phi_{J}\cap w\Phi_{J}\cap\Phi^{+}
, then
\beta
is a sum of simple roots in
\Delta_{J\cap wJw^{-1}}
. Arguing analogously for
\beta\in\Phi_{J}\cap w\Phi_{J}\cap\Phi^{-}
we see that
\Phi_{J}\cap w\Phi_{J}\subset\Phi_{J\cap wJw^{-1}}
. The reverse inclusion is straight-forward. This proves item (1).

To prove item (2), we need only show
\Phi_{J}\cap w(\Phi^{+})=\Phi_{J}\cap\Phi^{+}
. Let
\beta\in\Phi_{J}\cap\Phi^{+}
. Since
w\in{}^{J}W^{J}
, we have
w^{-1}(\beta)\in\Phi^{+}
and hence
\beta\in w(\Phi^{+})
. This proves that
\Phi_{J}\cap\Phi^{+}\subset\Phi_{J}\cap w(\Phi^{+})
. Vice versa, assume that
\beta\in\Phi_{J}\cap\Phi_{-}
. Then
w^{-1}(\beta)\in\Phi_{-}
, which implies that
\Phi_{J}\cap\Phi_{-}\subset\Phi_{J}\cap w(\Phi_{-})
. This finishes the proof of item (2).

To prove item (3), write
P_{J}=L_{J}U^{J}
. By
[
DM20
, Proposition 2.1]
we have that

\displaystyle(P_{J}\cap\dot{w}P_{J}\dot{w}^{-1})U^{J}=(L_{J}\cap\dot{w}L_{J}\dot{w}^{-1})(L_{J}\cap\dot{w}U^{J}\dot{w}^{-1})U^{J}

By
[
BT65
]
(see also
[
DM20
, Proposition 0.34]
) we have that

L_{J}\cap\dot{w}L_{J}\dot{w}^{-1}=L_{J\cap wJw^{-1}}

and

L_{J}\cap\dot{w}U^{J}\dot{w}^{-1}=<U_{\beta}>_{\beta\in\Phi_{J}\cap w(\Phi^{+}\setminus\Phi_{J})}.

By item (2), we have

(L_{J}\cap\dot{w}U^{J}\dot{w}^{-1})U^{J}=<U_{\beta}>_{\beta\in\Phi^{+}\setminus\Phi_{J\cap wJw^{-1}}}=U^{J\cap wJw^{-1}}.

Hence
(P_{J}\cap\dot{w}P_{J}\dot{w}^{-1})U^{J}=P_{J\cap wJw^{-1}}
.

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
g=p_{1}\dot{w}p_{2}
for
p_{1},p_{2}\in P_{J}
and
w\in{}^{J}W^{J}
then

(P_{J}\cap gP_{J}g^{-1})U^{J}=p_{1}P_{J\cap wJw^{-1}}p_{1}^{-1}.

{proof}

Since
p_{1}^{-1}U^{J}=U^{J}p_{1}^{-1}
, because
U^{J}
is normal in
P_{J}
(see
[
AN22a
]
), we have that

\displaystyle(P_{J}\cap gP_{J}g^{-1})U^{J}=

Proposition 5.5

Proposition 5.8

\displaystyle\to\mathcal{Y}_{wz}^{\circ}

\displaystyle(g_{0},(X,gB))

\displaystyle\mapsto(g_{0}Xg_{0}^{-1},g_{0}gB).

We begin by noticing that the map is well defined. Indeed

\displaystyle X^{\prime}

\displaystyle=g_{0}^{-1}Xg_{0}

\displaystyle=u_{0}^{\prime-1}u_{0}^{-1}\ell_{0}^{-1}\dot{w}pg_{0}

(Chromatic symmetric function of weighted graphs following Gasharov

Let

Let

8.
Further directions

We assume that
G=GL_{n}
in all the discussions below.

8.1.
Kazhdan–Lusztig polynomials

We have that
(IC_{\mathcal{Y}_{w}(X)})_{p}
for
p\in\mathcal{Y}_{z}(X)^{\circ}
has Poincaré polynomial precisely the Kazhdan–Lusztig polynomial
P_{z,w}(q)
. In other words, the singularity of
\mathcal{Y}_{w}
at
\mathcal{Y}_{z}^{\circ}
is the same as the singularity of the Schubert variety
\Omega_{w}
at
\Omega_{z}^{\circ}
.

The picture for parabolic Lusztig varieties is not so clear. For instance,
\Omega_{2134,\{2,3\}}
is smooth, while
\mathcal{Y}_{2134,\{2,3\}}
is singular at
\mathcal{Y}_{1234,\{2,3\}}
. In fact, for an invertible regular semisimple matrix
X
, we have that
\mathcal{Y}_{2134,\{2,3\}}(X)\subset\mathbb{P}^{3}
is the union of the
6
coordinate lines, while
\mathcal{Y}_{1234,\{2,3\}}(X)
is the set of the coordinate points of
\mathbb{P}^{3}
. So
(IC_{\mathcal{Y}_{2134,\{2,3\}}})|_{\mathcal{Y}_{1234,\{2,3\}}}
is a local system on
\mathcal{Y}_{1234,\{2,3\}}
of rank
3
. More than that, it is associated to the representation of
S_{3}
whose character is
h_{2,1}
(a proper proof of this statement will be given in a subsequent work).

It is not clear to us how the parabolic Kazhdan–Lusztig polynomials (introduced by Deodhar in
[
Deo87
]
) relate to the singularities of the parabolic Lusztig varieties, or if new polynomials (or symmetric functions/graded representations) will have to be defined.

8.2.
Positivity

The natural extension of the Stanley–Stembridge and Haiman’s conjecture to the parabolic case is:

Conjecture 8.1
.

The Frobenius character
\ch(IH^{*}(\mathcal{Y}_{w,J}(X))
is
h
-positive for every
w\in S_{n}
and for every
J\subset\{1,\ldots,n-1\}
.

It is important to notice that
\ch(IH^{*}(\mathcal{Y}_{w,J}(X),L))
may fail to be
h
-positive (see Example
5.12
