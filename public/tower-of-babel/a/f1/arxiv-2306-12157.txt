Abstract
. We consider a system of three identical bosons in ℝ 3 \mathbb{R}^{3} with two-body zero-range interactions and a three-body hard-core repulsion of a given radius a > 0 a>0 . Using a quadratic form approach we prove that the corresponding Hamiltonian is self-adjoint and bounded from below for any value of a a . In particular this means that the hard-core repulsion is sufficient to prevent the fall to the center phenomenon found by Minlos and Faddeev in their seminal work on the three-body problem in 1961. Furthermore, in the case of infinite two-body scattering length, also known as unitary limit, we prove the Efimov effect, i.e. , we show that the Hamiltonian has an infinite sequence of negative eigenvalues E n E_{n} accumulating at zero and fulfilling the asymptotic geometrical law E n + 1 / E n → e − 2 ​ π s 0 ​ for ​ n → + ∞ \;E_{n+1}/E_{n}\;\,\rightarrow\,\;e^{-\frac{2\pi}{s_{0}}}\,\;\,\text{for}\,\;n\,\rightarrow\,+\infty holds, where s 0 ≈ 1.00624 s_{0}\approx 1.00624 . Keywords: zero-range interactions, three-body Hamiltonians, Efimov effect. MSC 2020: 81Q10; 81Q15; 70F07; 46N50.



1.
Introduction

The Efimov effect is an interesting physical phenomenon occurring in three-particle quantum systems in dimension three (
[
10
,
11
]
, see also
[
25
]
).
It consists in the appearing of an infinite sequence of negative eigenvalues
E_{n}
, with
E_{n}\,\rightarrow\,0
for
n\,\rightarrow\,\infty
, of the three-body Hamiltonian if the two-particle subsystems do not have bound states and at least two of them exhibit a zero-energy resonance (or, equivalently, an infinite two-body scattering length).
A remarkable feature of the effect is that the distribution of eigenvalues satisfies
the universal geometrical law

(1.1)

\lim_{n\,\rightarrow\,+\infty}\frac{E_{n+1}}{E_{n}}\>=\>e^{-\frac{2\pi}{s}}\,,

where the parameter
s>0
depends only on the mass ratios and, possibly, on the statistics of the particles.

According to an intuitive physical picture, the three-particle bound states (or trimers) associated to the eigenvalues are determined by a long range, attractive effective interaction of kinetic origin, which is produced by the resonance condition and does not depend on the details of the two-body potentials.
Roughly speaking, in a trimer the attraction between two particles is mediated by the third one, which is moving back and forth between the two.
It should also be stressed that the Efimov effect disappears if the two-body potentials become more attractive causing the destruction of the zero-energy resonance.
For interesting experimental evidence of Efimov quantum states see,
e.g.
,
[
19
]
.

The first mathematical result on the Efimov effect was obtained by Yafaeev in 1974
[
33
]
.
He studied a symmetrized form of the Faddeev equations for the bound states of the three-particle Hamiltonian and proved the existence of an infinite number of negative eigenvalues.
In 1993 Sobolev
[
29
]
used a slightly different symmetrization of the equations and proved the asymptotics

(1.2)

\lim_{z\,\rightarrow\,0^{-}}\frac{N(z)}{\lvert\log\lvert z\rvert\mspace{0.75mu}\rvert}=\frac{s}{2\pi}\,,

where
N(z)
denotes the number of eigenvalues smaller than
z<0
.
Note that (
1.2
) is consistent with the law (
1.1
).
In the same year Tamura
[
31
]
obtained the same result under more general conditions on the two-body potentials.
Other mathematical proofs of the effect were obtained by Ovchinnikov and Sigal in 1979
[
27
]
and Tamura in 1991
[
30
]
using a variational approach based on the Born-Oppenheimer approximation.
For more recent results on the subject, see
[
5
]
(for the case of two identical fermions and a different particle),
[
15
]
(for a two-dimensional variant of the problem) and
[
16
]
.

We notice that in the above mentioned mathematical results a rigorous derivation of the law (
1.1
) is lacking.

It is also worth observing that, before the seminal works of Efimov, Minlos and Faddeev
[
23
,
24
]
studied the problem of constructing the Hamiltonian for a system of three bosons with zero-range interactions in dimension three. It was known that such Hamiltonian cannot be defined considering only pairwise zero-range interactions. Minlos and Faddeev showed that a self-adjoint Hamiltonian can be constructed by imposing suitable two-body boundary conditions at the coincidence hyperplanes,
i.e.
, when the positions of two particles coincide, and also a three-body boundary condition at the triple-coincidence point, when the positions of all the three particles coincide.
They also proved that the Hamiltonian is unbounded form below, due to the presence of an infinite sequence of negative eigenvalues diverging to
-\infty
.
Such instability property can be seen as a fall to the center phenomenon and it is due to the fact that the interaction becomes too strong and attractive when the three particles are very close to each other.
A further interesting result of the analysis of Minlos and Faddeev, even if it is not explicitly emphasized, is the proof of the Efimov effect in the case of infinite two-body scattering length (corresponding to the resonant case), with a rigorous derivation of the law (
1.1
). This in particular shows that the occurrence of the Efimov effect can be obtained also with zero-range interactions, the only crucial condition being the presence of an infinite two-body scattering length.
Such a result is somewhat tainted by the fact that the Hamiltonian in unbounded from below and therefore unsatisfactory from the physical point of view.

Our aim is to present a mathematical proof of the Efimov effect and law (
1.1
) for a bounded from below Hamiltonian obtained by a slight modification of the Minlos and Faddeev Hamiltonian.
We mention that the problem of constructing a lower bounded Hamiltonian for a three-body system with zero-range interactions has been recently approached in the literature (see,
e.g.
,
[
4
,
13
,
12
,
22
]
).
The idea is to introduce an effective three-body force acting only when the three particles are close to each other, preventing the fall to the center phenomenon.

In the present work we consider a Hamiltonian with two-body zero-range interactions and another type of three-body interaction.
More precisely, the effective three-body force is replaced by a three-body hard-core repulsion.
We shall prove that such Hamiltonian is self-adjoint and bounded from below and then prove the Efimov effect,
i.e.
, the existence of an infinite sequence of negative eigenvalues satisfying (
1.1
) when the two-body scattering length is infinite.

Our work can be viewed as an attempt to make rigorous the original physical argument of Efimov. Indeed, Efimov takes into account three identical bosons and his approach is based on the replacement of the two-body potential with a boundary condition, which is essentially equivalent to consider a two-body zero-range interaction. Then, he introduces hyper-spherical coordinates and shows that if the two-body scattering length is infinite then the problem becomes separable and in the equation for the hyper-radius
R
the long range, attractive effective potential
-(s_{0}^{2}+1/4)/R^{2}
appears. The behavior for small
R
of this potential is too singular and an extra boundary condition at short distance must be imposed. After this ad hoc procedure, he obtains the infinite sequence of negative eigenvalues satisfying the law (
1.1
) as a consequence of the large
R
behavior of the effective potential.

The self-adjoint and bounded from below Hamiltonian constructed in this paper can be considered as the rigorous counterpart of the ad hoc regularization scheme mentioned above. Furthermore, we show that the eigenvalues and eigenvectors found in a formal way in the physical literature are in fact eigenvalues and eigenvectors of our Hamiltonian in a rigorous sense and, accordingly, we obtain a mathematical proof of (
1.1
).

Let us introduce some notation. Here and in the sequel:
\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\boldsymbol{x}_{2},\boldsymbol{x}_{3}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
are the coordinates of the three bosons in a fixed inertial reference frame; the units of measure employed are such that
\hbar=m_{1}=m_{2}=m_{3}=1
. It is convenient to introduce the system of Jacobi coordinates
\boldsymbol{r}_{cm},\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
defined as

\boldsymbol{r}_{cm}:={\boldsymbol{x}_{1}+\boldsymbol{x}_{2}+\boldsymbol{x}_{3}\over 3}\,,\qquad\boldsymbol{x}:=\boldsymbol{x}_{2}-\boldsymbol{x}_{1}\,,\qquad\boldsymbol{y}:={2\over\sqrt{3}}\left(\boldsymbol{x}_{3}-{\boldsymbol{x}_{1}+\boldsymbol{x}_{2}\over 2}\right).

Correspondingly, we have
\boldsymbol{x}_{1}=\boldsymbol{r}_{cm}-{1\over 2}\,\boldsymbol{x}-{1\over 2\sqrt{3}}\,\boldsymbol{y}
,
\boldsymbol{x}_{2}=\boldsymbol{r}_{cm}+{1\over 2}\,\boldsymbol{x}-{1\over 2\sqrt{3}}\,\boldsymbol{y}
,
\boldsymbol{x}_{3}=\boldsymbol{r}_{cm}+{1\over\sqrt{3}}\,\boldsymbol{y}
.
The transpositions
\sigma_{ij}
(
i,j\in\{1,2,3\}
) exchanging the
i
th
and the
j
th
particles are represented by the following changes of coordinates

\;\displaystyle{\sigma_{12}:(\boldsymbol{r}_{cm},\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\,\rightarrow\,(\boldsymbol{r}_{cm},-\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\,,}

\;\displaystyle{\sigma_{23}:(\boldsymbol{r}_{cm},\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\,\rightarrow\,(\boldsymbol{r}_{cm},\tfrac{1}{2}\,\boldsymbol{x}+\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\,\boldsymbol{y},\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\,\boldsymbol{x}-\tfrac{1}{2}\,\boldsymbol{y}),}\;
and

\displaystyle{\sigma_{31}:(\boldsymbol{r}_{cm},\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\,\rightarrow\,(\boldsymbol{r}_{cm},\tfrac{1}{2}\,\boldsymbol{x}-\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\,\boldsymbol{y},-\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\,\boldsymbol{x}-\tfrac{1}{2}\,\boldsymbol{y}).}

Upon factorizing the center of mass coordinate
\boldsymbol{r}_{cm}
(
i.e.
, adopting the center-of-mass reference frame) the heuristic Hamiltonian describing our three-boson system is expressed by

(1.3)

H=-\Delta_{\boldsymbol{x}}-\Delta_{\boldsymbol{y}}+V^{\mspace{1.5mu}hc}_{a}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})+\delta(\boldsymbol{x})+\delta\!\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\,\boldsymbol{x}-\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\,\boldsymbol{y}\right)+\delta\!\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\,\boldsymbol{x}+\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\,\boldsymbol{y}\right),

where, at a formal level,
V^{\mspace{1.5mu}hc}_{a}
indicates a hard-core potential corresponding to a Dirichlet boundary condition on the hyper-sphere of radius
a
in
\mathbb{R}^{6}
, centered at
(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})=(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{0})
and the “
\delta
-potentials” represent the zero-range interactions between the pair of particles
(1,2)
,
(2,3)
and
(3,1)
respectively.
Notice that

(\boldsymbol{x}_{1}-\boldsymbol{x}_{2})^{2}+(\boldsymbol{x}_{2}-\boldsymbol{x}_{3})^{2}+(\boldsymbol{x}_{3}-\boldsymbol{x}_{1})^{2}=\tfrac{3}{2}\,(\lvert\boldsymbol{x}\rvert^{2}+\lvert\boldsymbol{y}\rvert^{2}),

therefore the hard-core potential
V^{\mspace{1.5mu}hc}_{a}
plays the role to prevent the three bosons from reaching the triple-coincidence point
\boldsymbol{x}_{1}=\boldsymbol{x}_{2}=\boldsymbol{x}_{3}\mspace{1.5mu}
, avoiding the above mentioned fall to the center phenomenon.

The bosonic Hilbert space of states for our system is

(1.4)

L^{2}_{s}(\Omega_{a}):=\left\{\psi\!\in\mspace{-1.5mu}L^{2}(\Omega_{a})\left|\>\psi(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})=\psi(-\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})=\psi\!\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\,\boldsymbol{x}+\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\,\boldsymbol{y},\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\,\boldsymbol{x}-\tfrac{1}{2}\,\boldsymbol{y}\!\right)\right.\!\right\},

where

(1.5)

\Omega_{a}:=\left\{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\in\mathbb{R}^{6}\,\left|\;\lvert\boldsymbol{x}\rvert^{2}+\lvert\boldsymbol{y}\rvert^{2}>a^{2}\right.\right\}.

Definition (
1.4
) encodes the symmetry by exchange given by
\sigma_{12}
and
\sigma_{23}
which clearly imply also the condition corresponding to the exchange performed by
\sigma_{31}
,
i.e.

\psi(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})=\psi\!\left(\frac{1}{2}\,\boldsymbol{x}-\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\,\boldsymbol{y},-\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\,\boldsymbol{x}-\frac{1}{2}\,\boldsymbol{y}\!\right)
.
In the following we shall construct the rigorous counterpart of (
1.3
) as a self-adjoint and bounded from below operator in
L^{2}_{s}(\Omega_{a})
. The first step is to interpret the formal unperturbed operator
\;-\Delta_{\boldsymbol{x}}-\Delta_{\boldsymbol{y}}+V^{\mspace{1.5mu}hc}_{a}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\;
as the Dirichlet Laplacian in
\Omega_{a}
, namely

(1.6)

\mbox{dom}\big(H_{D}\big)=L^{2}_{s}(\Omega_{a})\cap H^{1}_{0}(\Omega_{a})\cap H^{2}(\Omega_{a})\,,\qquad H_{D}\psi=(-\Delta_{\boldsymbol{x}}-\Delta_{\boldsymbol{y}})\psi\,.

It is well known that (
1.6
) is the self-adjoint and positive operator uniquely defined by the positive quadratic form

(1.7)

\mbox{dom}\big(Q_{D}\big):=L^{2}_{s}(\Omega_{a})\cap H^{1}_{0}(\Omega_{a})\,,\qquad Q_{D}[\psi]:=\int_{\Omega_{a}}\hskip-5.69046ptd\boldsymbol{x}\,d\boldsymbol{y}\,\Big(|\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}\psi|^{2}+|\nabla_{\boldsymbol{y}}\psi|^{2}\Big)\,.

The second, and more relevant, step is to define a self-adjoint perturbation of the Dirichlet Laplacian
H_{D}
supported by the coincidence hyperplanes

\pi_{12}\mspace{-1.5mu}=\mspace{-1.5mu}\left\{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\!\in\mspace{-1.5mu}\Omega_{a}\,\Big|\>\boldsymbol{x}\mspace{-1.5mu}=0\right\}\mspace{-1.5mu},\mspace{30mu}\pi_{23}\mspace{-1.5mu}=\mspace{-1.5mu}\left\{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\!\in\mspace{-1.5mu}\Omega_{a}\,\Big|\>\boldsymbol{x}\mspace{-1.5mu}=\mspace{-1.5mu}\sqrt{3}\,\boldsymbol{y}\right\}\mspace{-1.5mu},\mspace{30mu}\pi_{31}\mspace{-1.5mu}=\mspace{-1.5mu}\left\{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\!\in\mspace{-1.5mu}\Omega_{a}\,\Big|\>\boldsymbol{x}\mspace{-1.5mu}=\mspace{-1.5mu}-\mspace{1.5mu}\sqrt{3}\,\boldsymbol{y}\right\}\mspace{-1.5mu}.

Following the analogy with the one particle case
[
1
]
, a natural attempt is to construct an operator which, roughly speaking, acts as the Dirichlet Laplacian
H_{D}
outside the hyperplanes and it is characterized by a (singular) boundary condition on each hyperplane. Specifically, given
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
, we demand that

(1.8)

\psi(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})={\xi(\boldsymbol{y})\over 4\pi\,\lvert\boldsymbol{x}\rvert}+\alpha\,\xi(\boldsymbol{y})+o(1)\,,\qquad\mbox{for fixed\, $\boldsymbol{y}\in B_{a}^{c}$\, and\, $\lvert\boldsymbol{x}\rvert\,\rightarrow\,0$}.

where
B_{a}^{c}:=\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus B_{a}
and
B_{a}=\big\{\boldsymbol{y}\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{3}\,\big|\,\lvert\boldsymbol{y}\rvert\!<\!a\big\}
.
The above condition describes the interaction between particles 1 and 2. Due to the bosonic symmetry requirements, it also accounts for the interactions between the other two admissible pairs of particles. We recall that
-\alpha^{-1}
has the physical meaning of two-body scattering length.
The strategy for the mathematical proof is based on a quadratic form approach, similar to the one adopted for the construction of singular perturbations of a given self-adjoint and positive operator in analogous contexts (see,
e.g.
,
[
32
,
8
,
4
]
).

More precisely, starting from the formal Hamiltonian (
1.3
) characterized by the boundary condition (
1.8
), we construct the corresponding quadratic form
Q_{D,\alpha}
and we formulate our main results (section 2).

The main technical part of the paper is the proof that
Q_{D,\alpha}
is closed and bounded from below in
L^{2}_{s}(\Omega_{a})
(section 3).

Then, we define the Hamiltonian
H_{D,\alpha}
of our system as the unique self-adjoint and bounded from below operator associated to
Q_{D,\alpha}
and we give an explicit characterization of the domain and action of
H_{D,\alpha}
, providing also an expression for the associated resolvent operator (section 4).

Finally we show that for
\alpha=0
the Efimov effect occurs and the law (
1.1
) holds (section 5).

In Appendix
A
we collect some useful representation formulas for the integral kernel of the resolvent of the free Laplacian in
\mathbb{R}^{6}
and for the integral kernel of the resolvent of the Dirichlet Laplacian in
\Omega_{a}
.

In Appendix
B
we recall the solution of the eigenvalue problem in the case
\alpha=0
following the treatment usually given in the physical literature.

2.
Formulation of the main results

In this section we first give a heuristic argument to derive the quadratic form associated to the formal Hamiltonian (
1.3
) and then we formulate our main results.

Let us introduce the potentials
G^{\lambda}_{ij}\xi_{ij}
(
\lambda>0
) produced by a suitable charge
\xi_{ij}
with support concentrated on the hyperplane
\pi_{ij}
:

(2.1)

\big(G^{\lambda}_{ij}\xi_{ij}\big)(\boldsymbol{X})=\int_{\Omega_{a}}\!\!\!d\boldsymbol{X}^{\prime}\;R^{\lambda}_{D}(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{X}^{\prime})\;\xi_{ij}(\boldsymbol{X}^{\prime})\,\delta_{\pi_{ij}}(\boldsymbol{X}^{\prime})\,.

Here, for the sake of brevity, we have introduced the notation

\boldsymbol{X}=(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\,,\qquad\boldsymbol{X}^{\prime}=(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\!,\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime})\,,

and we have denoted by
R^{\lambda}_{D}(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{X}^{\prime})
the integral kernel associated to the resolvent operator
R_{D}^{\lambda}:=(H_{D}+\lambda)^{-1}
.

For later convenience, we write the kernel
R_{D}^{\lambda}\big(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{X}^{\prime}\big)
as

(2.2)

R_{D}^{\lambda}\big(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{X}^{\prime}\big)=R_{0}^{\lambda}\big(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{X}^{\prime}\big)+g^{\lambda}\big(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{X}^{\prime}\big)\,,

where
R_{0}^{\lambda}\big(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{X}^{\prime}\big)
is the integral kernel associated to the resolvent operator of the free Laplacian in
\mathbb{R}^{6}
and the function
g^{\lambda}\big(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{X}^{\prime}\big)
is a reminder term, solving the following elliptic problem for any fixed
\boldsymbol{X}^{\prime}\in\Omega_{a}
and
\lambda>0
:

(2.3)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\!\displaystyle{(-\Delta_{\boldsymbol{X}}+\lambda)g^{\lambda}\big(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{X}^{\prime}\big)=0}&\displaystyle{\mbox{for\; $\boldsymbol{X}\in\Omega_{a}$}}\,,\\
\displaystyle{g^{\lambda}\big(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{X}^{\prime}\big)=-\,R_{0}^{\lambda}\big(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{X}^{\prime}\big)}&\displaystyle{\mbox{for\; $\boldsymbol{X}\in\partial\Omega_{a}$}}\,,\\
\displaystyle{g^{\lambda}\big(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{X}^{\prime}\big)\longrightarrow 0}&\displaystyle{\mbox{for\; $\lvert\boldsymbol{X}\rvert\!\,\rightarrow\,\!+\infty$}}\,.\end{array}\right.

In Appendix
A
we give explicit expressions for
R_{0}^{\lambda}\big(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{X}^{\prime}\big)
,
g^{\lambda}\big(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{X}^{\prime}\big)
and then for
R_{D}^{\lambda}\big(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{X}^{\prime}\big)
.

Furthermore, the potentials
G^{\lambda}_{ij}\xi_{ij}
fulfills the following equation in distributional sense

(2.4)

(H_{D}+\lambda)G^{\lambda}_{ij}\xi_{ij}=\xi_{ij}\,\delta_{\pi_{ij}}\,.

By a slight abuse of notation, we set

(2.5)

G^{\lambda}\xi:=G^{\lambda}_{12}\xi_{12}+G^{\lambda}_{23}\xi_{23}+G^{\lambda}_{31}\xi_{31}\,.

In order to ensure that
G^{\lambda}\xi
actually meets the bosonic symmetries encoded in
L^{2}_{s}(\Omega_{a})
, we must require

(2.6)

\xi_{12}(\boldsymbol{X})=\xi(\boldsymbol{y})\,,\qquad\quad\xi_{23}(\boldsymbol{X})=\xi_{31}(\boldsymbol{X})=\xi(-2\boldsymbol{y})\,.

Taking this into account and noting that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\hskip-5.69046ptd\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\,R^{\lambda}_{0}\big(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y};\boldsymbol{x^{\prime}}\!,\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\big)

\displaystyle={1\over(2\pi)^{6}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\hskip-5.69046ptd\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}\hskip-17.07182ptd\boldsymbol{h}\,d\boldsymbol{k}\;{e^{i\boldsymbol{h}\cdot(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})+i\boldsymbol{k}\cdot(\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime})}\over|\boldsymbol{h}|^{2}+|\boldsymbol{k}|^{2}+\lambda}

\displaystyle={1\over(2\pi)^{3}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\!\!\!\!d\boldsymbol{h}\;{e^{i\boldsymbol{h}\cdot(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})}\over|\boldsymbol{h}|^{2}+\lambda}={e^{-\sqrt{\lambda}\,|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}|}\over 4\pi\,|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}|}\;,

from Eq. (
2.1
) we infer the following:

\displaystyle\big(G^{\lambda}_{12}\xi_{12}\big)(\boldsymbol{X})=\int_{B_{a}^{c}}\!\!\!d\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\,R^{\lambda}_{D}\big(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y};\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\big)\;\xi(\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\xi(\boldsymbol{y})\int_{B_{a}^{c}}\!\!\!d\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\,R^{\lambda}_{0}\big(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y};\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\big)+\int_{B_{a}^{c}}\!\!\!d\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\,R^{\lambda}_{0}\big(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y};\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\big)\,\big[\xi(\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime})-\xi(\boldsymbol{y})\big]+\int_{B_{a}^{c}}\!\!\!d\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\,g^{\lambda}\big(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y};\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\big)\,\xi(\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\xi(\boldsymbol{y})\,{e^{-\sqrt{\lambda}\,\lvert\boldsymbol{x}\rvert}\over 4\pi\lvert\boldsymbol{x}\rvert}-\xi(\boldsymbol{y})\int_{B_{a}}\!\!\!d\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\,R^{\lambda}_{0}\big(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y};\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\big)

\displaystyle\hskip 113.81102pt+\int_{B_{a}^{c}}\!\!\!d\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\,R^{\lambda}_{0}\big(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y};\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\big)\,\big[\xi(\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime})-\xi(\boldsymbol{y})\big]+\int_{B_{a}^{c}}\!\!\!d\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\,g^{\lambda}\big(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y};\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\big)\,\xi(\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime})\,;

\displaystyle\big(G^{\lambda}_{23}\xi_{23}\big)(\boldsymbol{X})=\int_{B_{a}^{c}}\!\!d\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\,R^{\lambda}_{D}\!\left(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y};-\,\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\,\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\!,-\,\tfrac{1}{2}\,\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\right)\xi(\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime})\,;

\displaystyle\big(G^{\lambda}_{31}\xi_{31}\big)(\boldsymbol{X})=\int_{B_{a}^{c}}\!\!d\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\,R^{\lambda}_{D}\!\left(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y};\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\,\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\!,-\,\tfrac{1}{2}\,\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\right)\xi(\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime})\,.

Notice that, due to the singularity for

, the potential
G^{\lambda}_{12}\xi_{12}
does not belong to

. Of course, the same is true for

and

\psi=\varphi^{\lambda}+G^{\lambda}\xi\,,\qquad\mbox{with $\varphi^{\lambda}\in\mbox{dom}\big(H_{D}\big)$}\,.

In view of the previous arguments, Eq. (

\displaystyle\varphi^{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{y})=\left(\!\alpha+\tfrac{\sqrt{\lambda}}{4\pi}\right)\!\xi(\boldsymbol{y})+\xi(\boldsymbol{y})\int_{B_{a}}\!\!\!d\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\,R^{\lambda}_{0}\big(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{y};\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\big)-\int_{B_{a}^{c}}\!\!\!d\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\,R^{\lambda}_{0}\big(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{y};\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\big)\,\big[\xi(\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime})-\xi(\boldsymbol{y})\big]

\displaystyle-\int_{B_{a}^{c}}\!\!\!d\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\,g^{\lambda}\big(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{y};\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\big)\xi(\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime})-\int_{B_{a}^{c}}\!\!\!d\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\left[R^{\lambda}_{D}\!\left(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{y};-\,\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\,\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\!,-\,\tfrac{1}{2}\,\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\right)\!+R^{\lambda}_{D}\!\left(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{y};\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\,\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\!,-\,\tfrac{1}{2}\,\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\right)\right]\!\xi(\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime})\,.

\displaystyle\big\langle\psi\big|(H+\lambda)\psi\big\rangle

\displaystyle=\lim_{\varepsilon\,\rightarrow\,0^{+}}\int_{\Omega_{a,\varepsilon}}\hskip-11.38092ptd\boldsymbol{X}\;\overline{\psi}\,\big(H_{D}+\lambda\big)\psi=\lim_{\varepsilon\,\rightarrow\,0^{+}}\int_{\Omega_{a,\varepsilon}}\hskip-11.38092ptd\boldsymbol{X}\;\overline{(\varphi^{\lambda}+G^{\lambda}\xi)}\>\big(H_{D}+\lambda\big)(\varphi^{\lambda}+G^{\lambda}\xi)

\displaystyle=\big\langle\varphi^{\lambda}\big|(H_{D}+\lambda)\varphi^{\lambda}\big\rangle+\lim_{\varepsilon\,\rightarrow\,0^{+}}\int_{\Omega_{a,\varepsilon}}\hskip-11.38092ptd\boldsymbol{X}\;\overline{(G^{\lambda}\xi)}\>\big(H_{D}+\lambda\big)\varphi^{\lambda}\,,

where

By means of Eqs. (

), (

), (

1) Estimates for

Making reference to the definition (

\displaystyle\Phi_{1}^{\lambda}[\xi]=\int_{B_{b}\,\cap\,B_{a}^{c}}\mspace{-27mu}d\boldsymbol{y}\left(\int_{B_{a}}\!\!d\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\,R^{\lambda}_{0}\big(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{y};\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\big)\!\right)\lvert\xi(\boldsymbol{y})\rvert^{2}+\int_{B_{b}^{c}}\!\!d\boldsymbol{y}\left(\int_{B_{a}}\!\!d\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\,R^{\lambda}_{0}\big(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{y};\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\big)\!\right)\lvert\xi(\boldsymbol{y})\rvert^{2}.

Taking into account that the integral kernel

can be written explicitly in terms of the modified Bessel function of second kind

(
a.k.a.
Macdonald function), see (

) in Appendix

, by direct evaluation we get

R^{\lambda}_{0}\big(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{y};\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\big)={\lambda\over(2\pi)^{3}}\,{K_{2}\big(\sqrt{\lambda}\,\left|\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\right|\big)\over|\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}|^{2}}\,.

Since

is a positive definite function for any fixed
\nu\geqslant 0

[
26
, §10.37]
, it is evident that
\Phi_{1}^{\lambda}[\xi]
is non-negative.
Moreover, from the basic relation
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\big(t^{\nu}K_{\nu}(t)\big)=-t^{\nu}K_{\nu-1}(t)\!<\!0

[
26
, Eq. 10.29.4]
, we deduce that
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\mapsto t^{\nu}\,K_{\nu}(t)
is a continuous and decreasing function.
In particular, we have
\inf_{t\in[0,c]}t^{2}\,K_{2}(t)=c^{2}\,K_{2}(c)
for any

and
\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\!}t^{2}\,K_{2}(t)=\lim_{t\,\rightarrow\,0^{+}\!}t^{2}\,K_{2}(t)=2
, see
[
26
, Eq. 10.30.2]

On one side, these arguments suffice to infer that

{\lambda(a+b)^{2}\,K_{2}\big(\sqrt{\lambda}\,(a+b)\big)\over(2\pi)^{3}\,|\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}|^{4}}\leqslant R^{\lambda}_{0}\big(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{y};\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\big)\leqslant{2\over(2\pi)^{3}\,|\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}|^{4}}\,,\quad\;\mbox{for all\, $\boldsymbol{y}\!\in\!B_{b}\!\cap\!B_{a}^{c}$, $\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\!\in\mspace{-1.5mu}B_{a}$}\,.

For any
\boldsymbol{y}\in B_{a}^{c}
, a direct computation yields

\displaystyle\int_{B_{a}}\!\!d\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\,{1\over|\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}|^{4}}=2\pi\int_{0}^{a}\!d\rho\int_{-1}^{1}\!\!\!du\;\frac{\rho^{2}}{(\lvert\boldsymbol{y}\rvert^{2}+\rho^{2}-2\lvert\boldsymbol{y}\rvert\,\rho\,u)^{2}}={2\pi\over\lvert\boldsymbol{y}\rvert\!-\!a}\left[{1\over t\!+\!1}+{t\!-\!1\over 2t}\log\!\left({t\!-\!1\over t\!+\!1}\right)\right]_{t\,=\,\lvert\boldsymbol{y}\rvert/a}\!.

It is easy to check that the expression between square brackets appearing above is a positive and decreasing function of

. As a consequence, for all

we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{2\pi}{\lvert\boldsymbol{y}\rvert-a}\left[\frac{1}{t+1}+\frac{t-1}{2t}\,\log\!\left(\frac{t-1}{t+1}\right)\right]_{t\,=\,b/a}\!\leqslant\,\int_{B_{a}}\!\!d\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\,\frac{1}{\lvert\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\rvert^{4}}\,\leqslant\,\frac{\pi}{\lvert\boldsymbol{y}\rvert-a}\,.

In view of the previous considerations, this implies

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}\left[a+\frac{b^{2}-a^{2}}{2b}\,\log\!\left(\frac{b-a}{b+a}\right)\right]\lambda\,(a+b)\,K_{2}\big(\sqrt{\lambda}\,(a+b)\big)\int_{B_{b}\,\cap\,B_{a}^{c}}\mspace{-27mu}d\boldsymbol{y}\;{\lvert\xi(\boldsymbol{y})\rvert^{2}\over\lvert\boldsymbol{y}\rvert-a}

\displaystyle\hskip 113.81102pt\leqslant\,\int_{B_{b}\,\cap\,B_{a}^{c}}\mspace{-27mu}d\boldsymbol{y}\left(\int_{B_{a}}\mspace{-18mu}d\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\,R^{\lambda}_{0}\big(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{y};\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\big)\right)\!\lvert\xi(\boldsymbol{y})\rvert^{2}\leqslant\,\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}\int_{B_{b}\,\cap\,B_{a}^{c}}\mspace{-27mu}d\boldsymbol{y}\;\frac{\lvert\xi(\boldsymbol{y})\rvert^{2}\!}{\lvert\boldsymbol{y}\rvert-a}\,.

On the other side, noting that

0\leqslant R^{\lambda}_{0}\big(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{y};\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\big)\leqslant{\lambda(b-a)^{2}\,K_{2}\big(\sqrt{\lambda}\,(b-a)\big)\over(2\pi)^{3}\,|\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}|^{4}}\,,\qquad\mbox{for all\, $\boldsymbol{y}\!\in\!B_{b}^{c}$, $\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\!\in\!B_{a}$}\,,

by computation similar to those outlined above (recall, in particular, that

\displaystyle 0\leqslant\int_{B_{b}^{c}}\!\!\!d\boldsymbol{y}\left(\int_{B_{a}}\!\!\!d\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\,R^{\lambda}_{0}\big(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{y};\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\big)\right)\lvert\xi(\boldsymbol{y})\rvert^{2}\leqslant{1\over 4\pi^{2}(b-a)}\int_{B_{b}^{c}}\hskip-5.69046ptd\boldsymbol{y}\;\lvert\xi(\boldsymbol{y})\rvert^{2}\,.

Summing up, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}\left[a+\frac{b^{2}-a^{2}}{2b}\,\log\!\left(\frac{b-a}{b+a}\right)\right]\lambda\,(a+b)\,K_{2}\big(\sqrt{\lambda}\,(a+b)\big)\int_{B_{b}\,\cap\,B_{a}^{c}}\mspace{-27mu}d\boldsymbol{y}\;{\lvert\xi(\boldsymbol{y})\rvert^{2}\over\lvert\boldsymbol{y}\rvert-a}

\displaystyle\hskip 142.26378pt\leqslant\Phi_{1}^{\lambda}[\xi]\leqslant\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}\!\left(\int_{B_{b}\,\cap\,B_{a}^{c}}\mspace{-27mu}d\boldsymbol{y}\;\frac{\lvert\xi(\boldsymbol{y})\rvert^{2}\!}{\lvert\boldsymbol{y}\rvert-a}+{1\over b-a}\int_{B_{b}^{c}}\hskip-5.69046ptd\boldsymbol{y}\;\lvert\xi(\boldsymbol{y})\rvert^{2}\right).

(B.2)

Using polar coordinates
(r,\omega)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times(0,\pi/2)
, the above problem can be solved by separation of variables. Indeed, defining
\eta(R,\omega)=\zeta(R\sin\omega,R\cos\omega)
, one finds

(B.4)

\displaystyle-\frac{\partial^{2}\eta}{\partial R^{2}}-\frac{1}{R}\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial R}-\frac{1}{R^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2}\eta}{\partial\omega^{2}}+\mu\,\eta=0\hskip 42.67912pt\text{for\, $R>a$,\, $\omega\in(0,\tfrac{\pi}{2})$}\,,

(B.5)

\displaystyle\eta\left(R,\tfrac{\pi}{2}\right)=0\quad\text{for\, $R\geq a$}\,,\hskip 91.04872pt\eta(a,\omega)=0\quad\text{for\, $\omega\in(0,\tfrac{\pi}{2})$}\,,

(B.6)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial\omega}(R,0)+\frac{8}{\sqrt{3}}\,\eta\left(R,\tfrac{\pi}{3}\right)=0\hskip 78.24507pt\text{for\, $R>a$}\,.

Let us now look for solutions in the product form
\eta(R,\omega)=f(R)\,g(\omega)
. Then

(B.7)

\displaystyle g^{\prime\prime}-\nu g=0\,,\qquad g(\tfrac{\pi}{2})=0\,,\qquad g^{\prime}(0)+\frac{8}{\sqrt{3}}\,g\left(\tfrac{\pi}{3}\right)=0\,,

and

(B.8)

\displaystyle f^{\prime\prime}+\frac{1}{R}\,f^{\prime}+\left(\frac{\nu}{R^{2}}-\mu\right)f=0\,,\qquad f(a)=0\,,

where
\nu
is a real separation constant. We are interested in the case
\nu>0
and it turns out that in this case the only solution (apart from a multiplicative factor) of problem (
B.7
) is

(B.9)

\displaystyle g_{0}(\omega)=\sinh\left[s_{0}\left(\omega-\tfrac{\pi}{2}\right)\right]\,,

where
\nu=s_{0}^{2}
and
s_{0}>0
is the only positive solution of Eq. (
2.30
).
Next, one solves problem (
B.8
) with
\nu=s_{0}^{2}
. The only solution (apart from a multiplicative factor) of the differential equation going to zero for
R\,\rightarrow\,\infty
is the modified Bessel function of imaginary order
K_{is_{0}}\big(\sqrt{\mu}\,R\big)
. It remains to impose the Dirichlet boundary condition
K_{is_{0}}\big(\sqrt{\mu}\,a\big)=0
, which dictates the choice
\mu=\mu_{n}
as in (
5.8
).
Accordingly, the problem (
B.8
) for
\mu=\mu_{n}
has a solution going to zero for
R\,\rightarrow\,\infty

(B.10)

\displaystyle f_{n}(R)=K_{is_{0}}\!\left(\frac{t_{n}}{a}\,R\right).

By (

B.10
