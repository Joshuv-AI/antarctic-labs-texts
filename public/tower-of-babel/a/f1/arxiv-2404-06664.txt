CulturalTeaming: AI-Assisted Interactive Red-Teaming for Challenging LLMs’ (Lack of) Multicultural Knowledge
Yu Ying Chiu, Liwei Jiang, Maria Antoniak, Chan Young Park, Shuyue Stella Li, Mehar Bhatia, Sahithya Ravi, Yulia Tsvetkov, Vered Shwartz, Yejin Choi

Abstract
Frontier large language models (LLMs) are developed by researchers and practitioners with skewed cultural backgrounds and on datasets with skewed sources. However, LLMs’ (lack of) multicultural knowledge cannot be effectively assessed with current methods for developing benchmarks. Existing multicultural evaluations primarily rely on expensive and restricted human annotations or potentially outdated internet resources. Thus, they struggle to capture the intricacy, dynamics, and diversity of cultural norms. LLM-generated benchmarks are promising, yet risk propagating the same biases they are meant to measure. To synergize the creativity and expert cultural knowledge of human annotators and the scalability and standardizability of LLM-based automation, we introduce CulturalTeaming 1 1 1 Our platform CulturalTeaming is available at https://cultural-norms-demo.apps.allenai.org/ . , an interactive red-teaming system that leverages human-AI collaboration to build truly challenging evaluation dataset for assessing the multicultural knowledge of LLMs, while improving annotators’ capabilities and experiences. Our study reveals that CulturalTeaming’s various modes of AI assistance support annotators in creating cultural questions, that modern LLMs fail at, in a gamified manner. Importantly, the increased level of AI assistance (e.g., LLM-generated revision hints) empowers users to create more difficult questions with enhanced perceived creativity of themselves, shedding light on the promises of involving heavier AI assistance in modern evaluation dataset creation procedures. Through a series of 1-hour workshop sessions, we gather CulturalBench-v0.1 2 2 2 Our evaluation dataset CulturalBench-v0.1 is available at https://huggingface.co/datasets/kellycyy/CulturalBench . , a compact yet high-quality evaluation dataset with users’ red-teaming attempts, that different families of modern LLMs perform with accuracy ranging from 37.7% to 72.2%, revealing a notable gap in LLMs’ multicultural proficiency.

Uneven cultural representation has been a notorious recurrent limitation of LLMs
(
Santy et al., 2023
;
Cao et al., 2023
;
Arora et al., 2023
)
. Yet, establishing a quality benchmark to gauge LLMs’ nuanced multicultural knowledge remains a formidable challenge
(
Hershcovich et al., 2022
)
.
Conventional human-written benchmarks are static and often fail to keep pace with the evolving capabilities of LLMs
(
Yang et al., 2023
)
.
Notably, crafting standardized forms of evaluation tasks, e.g., multiple-choice questions (MCQ), for such a demanding task imposes considerable cognitive and intellectual burdens on annotators.
They must simultaneously demonstrate expert cultural knowledge, creativity in crafting novel culturally significant scenarios, familiarity of models’ capabilities to pinpoint their vulnerabilities, and meticulous attention to ensure logical and format coherence within the questions.

Alternatively, automatic benchmark creation presents unique challenges. Evaluations relying on scraped online resources like bilingual content from Wikipedia
(
Naous et al., 2023
;
Yao et al., 2023
)
, webpages or news articles
(
Nguyen et al., 2023
)
are susceptible to perpetuating skewed or outdated culture representations due to their limited forms and coverage of source media. Recently, established socio-cultural surveys originally designed for humans have been used to audit LLMs’ cultural abilities
(
Durmus et al., 2023
;
Santurkar et al., 2023
)
. This fails to capture the models’ deficiencies. The latest approaches of creating multicultural evaluations with LLMs generations
(
Li et al., 2024
;
Fung et al., 2024
)
, despite their scalability, risk propagating existing cultural biases that they are meant to measure. It is crucial to investigate alternative data annotation strategies that harness the valuable cultural insights of humans while streamlining mundane tasks with LLMs (e.g., data formatting) to allow people to direct their cognitive resources toward substantive tasks that demand creative thinking.

Drawing insights from recent red-teaming approaches on LLMs’ safety
(
Ganguli et al., 2022
;
Radharapu et al., 2023
)
and interactive model evaluation and data collection efforts
(
Talmor et al., 2022
;
Kiela et al., 2021
;
Chiang et al., 2024
;
Zheng et al., 2023
)
, we introduce

CulturalTeaming, an
AI-assisted
interactive red-teaming system that guides users in iteratively creating challenging evaluation datasets for testing the multicultural proficiency of modern LLMs. To understand how effective AI assistance may boost annotators’ ability to create challenging test cases, we design two variants of CulturalTeaming systems (Fig.
1
)—
Verifier-Only
that simply involves LLMs for in-the-loop testing, and
AI-Assisted
that engages LLM assistance more intensively, including drafting and scaffolding initial MCQ candidates based on high-level topics provided by users and suggesting hints and directions during the iterative revision process.

With the two variants of CulturalTeaming systems, we recruited 45 annotators to participate in immersive 1-hour workshop sessions for red-teaming LLMs’ (lack of) multicultural knowledge. The user study reveals positive insights about users’
behaviors
and
perception
during red-teaming with AI assistance.
User-behavior-wise
, LLM-generated hints help users construct more challenging questions for LLMs, especially when they are depleted of ideas after three rounds of revision. In particular, with LLM-generated hints (
AI-Assisted
), users are more willing to spend effort crafting a question with deliberation that yields more difficult questions eventually, rather than taking a rather arbitrary “hit-or-miss” red-teaming strategy to start over (
Verifier-Only
).
User-perception-wise
, users with more LLM assistance report a significantly higher level of agreement that the system sparked their
creativity
than those without assistance (4.19 vs. 3.58). These human insights shed light on future data annotation practices where more AI assistance could be involved to improve annotators’ ability to create high-quality data, as well as their annotating experiences.

Beyond user insights, throughout the workshop sessions, we compile users’ red-teaming attempts into a compact yet high-quality MCQ dataset, namely
CulturalBench-v0.1
, of 252 carefully-reviewed challenging questions spanned across 34 distinct cultures for testing LLMs’ multicultural knowledge. Evaluation with
CulturalBench-v0.1
reveals that CulturalTeaming yields difficult test cases to a variety of frontier LLM families (e.g., GPT, Claude, Mistral) across various sizes, with model performances ranging from 37.7% to 72.2%. This reveals a notable multicultural knowledge gap even for the most capable LLMs to date. Overall, our user study of CulturalTeaming demonstrates the promise of collecting high-quality, challenging datasets for multicultural knowledge by better optimizing users’ cognitive resources with human-AI collaboration, laying a novel foundation for future evaluation construction to effectively assess LLMs’ multicultural proficiency and beyond.

Our goal is to build a data annotation platform to help human annotators create a challenging and structured dataset based on their expert cultural knowledge.

2.1
Overall System Workflow

This system consists of three steps, as demonstrated in Fig.
2
– 1) Question Formulation 2) Question Verification and Revision 3) Feedback Collection. The first two steps involve a red-teaming exercise to formulate a challenging question step-by-step. The last step involves collecting feedback from users through a cultural contextualization survey based on their posed questions, as well as a usability survey regarding their user experience.

Step 1: Question Formulation

The goal is to facilitate users in brainstorming culturally relevant situations based on their personal experiences. A step-by-step guideline with detailed examples is provided to inspire them, as shown in Appendix
A.5
. Users formulate a multiple-choice question (MCQ), which comprises one correct and culturally appropriate option.

Step 2: Question Verification & Revision

This step provides an interactive and iterative red-teaming platform that allows users to verify their culturally sensitive MCQs. The platform assists them in revising the question and the options to make it more challenging by providing descriptions of various common revision strategies with drafted examples (e.g., “Negate the Question”), as stated in Fig.
2
and Appendix
A.5
.

Step 3: Feedback Collection

After finalizing a question, we collected users’ feedback on the question posed using a cultural contextualization survey and also their user experience by usability survey as depicted in Fig.
2
.
The cultural context survey asked users to provide their gold standard (correct option), its rationale, and several questions about their levels of particular cultural knowledge and the commonness of the specific cultural norm described in the question. We also gathered their hands-on experience regarding the functionality and user-friendliness of our interface. Survey questions can be found in Appendix
A.6
.

2.2
Varying Levels of AI Assistance: Verifier-Only & AI-Assisted

To study the effect of different AI-assistance modules, we have implemented two settings with varying AI Assistance levels in our experiment: (1) Verifier-Only (2) AI-Assisted, as presented in Fig.
1
. The first setting includes the basic element (Verifier) for the red-teaming exercise. The second setting aims to explore question formulation and hints on revision powered by LLMs.

Verifier-Only
The users formulate their MCQ in Step 1 (Question formulation). They then present their MCQ to LLM Verifier and revise their question iteratively by themselves. More specifically, the Verifier responds with one option coupled with the corresponding confidence score (which is the top-1 linear probability of its log probability). This aids users in judging the question’s difficulty. In our system, GPT-3.5-turbo (0125) served as the Verifier to reduce cost and time latency
(
Brown et al., 2020
)
.

AI-Assisted
Users benefit from extensive AI-assistance in question formation and revision, as illustrated in Fig.
2
. In Step 1, users convert their short cultural-relevant scenarios into MCQs using LLM-powered question formation. In Step 2, users verify and revise their questions with LLM-powered suggestions. The Verifier remains consistent with the Verifier-Only setting.

2.3
User Experiments with Red-teaming Workshops

We recruit annotators from an academic institution to participate in our 1-hour workshop. Annotators are randomly assigned to use either Verifier-Only or AI-Assisted. After finishing the self-contained tutorial, we encourage annotators to interact with the system for at least 45 minutes, allowing them to leave at their own will. Additional details regarding the user demographic description, and experiment setup can be found in Appendix
A.2
and
A.3
.

3
Promising AI-Assisted Benchmark Creation with CulturalTeaming

This section aims to provide insights on how to make use of human-AI collaboration to produce a challenging dataset on multicultural knowledge.

We analyze (1) AI-assistance contribution to different system modules, (2) existing LLMs’ cultural awareness using
CulturalBench-v0.1
, and (3) behavior and perception of users with varying levels of AI-assistance.

3.1
How does AI-assistance contribute to each module in our system?

We study each LLM-powered module to investigate the effect of human-AI collaboration on each type of task in our CulturalTeaming system.

3.1.1
LLM assistance on question formulation

Question formulation entails brainstorming a culturally relevant scenario and transforming it into a multiple choice question (MCQ). Users with LLM-powered question formulation are only tasked to provide a sentence-length scenario description, whereas users without LLM-assistance need to formulate the MCQ question and answer options themselves. We first compare the time difference in the question formulation between the two groups.

LLM assistance on question formulation
does not significantly improve
the time needed on formulating questions.

The time needed to form the initial MCQ template before editing for both users with more AI-assistance on question formulation (AI-Assisted setting) and less AI-assistance (Verifier-Only setting) are shown in Appendix
A.8
. The time needed for the initial MC questions created by Verifier-Only and AI-Assisted settings show no significant difference. Specifically, the former needs 183.4 seconds (SD=105.2) on average while the latter needs 161.8 (SD=109.4) on average.

Our formulation of red teaming tasks by writing structured MCQs appears to be relatively easy for human users without AI assistance. They effectively create their initial question template and attain equally impressive performance in terms of efficiency. One possible reason could be users without AI assistance formulate both questions and options concurrently when they are brainstorming. As we move forward in our future work, we should also consider how AI can aid people in brainstorming culturally relevant scenarios, rather than solely helping them in formulating structured questions.

3.1.2
LLM assistance on Question Verifier

After the MCQ is initially formulated, we attempt to use GPT-3.5-turbo as our Verifier
(
Brown et al., 2020
)
. Users revise and verify the MCQ iteratively based on the Verifier response.
In this section, we evaluate the viability of this setup by investigating whether the Verifier serves as a good estimator for the degree of challenge for other LLMs. We assume that users revise their MCQs based on the Verifier response and its corresponding confidence score.

Our LLM Verifier
is able to create challenging questions
for all tested models.
To delineate the effect of LLM assistance, we analyze the MCQs created by users without LLM assistance on other LLMs in Fig.
3
(Left). We found that LLM performance decreases with the number of revisions, especially for llama-70b by 38.73% and yi-34b by 23.82%. GPT-3.5-turbo (our Verifier model) decreased from 48.91% to 37.5%. GPT-4-turbo, which is the best performance model among all, decreased from 60.87% to 50.00% on the 10th revision. This shows that our LLM Verifier is capable of increasing the difficulty of questions for most models, as the number of revisions increases based on the response of LLM Verifier.

It implies that using only one model (GPT-3.5-turbo) as the Verifier can effectively construct challenging data for most of the models. However, if the goal of future work is to improve the stronger model (GPT-4-turbo), the relatively small decrease in it suggests that we need to randomly select different models as verifier, including GPT-4 model to help constructing a more challenging dataset.

3.1.3
LLM assistance on Question Revision

GPT-4 is used to generate LLM-powered hints based on the work-in-progress MCQ to provide revision suggestions with common revision strategies. Users without LLM-powered hints, on the other hand, are provided with static descriptions of those strategies. We now investigate the relationship between the number of revisions and the success attack rate of the finalized question (final success rates) for both users with LLM-assistance (AI-Assisted setting) and users without LLM-assistance (Verifier-Only settings).

LLM assistance on Hints helps users to construct
more challenging questions
after several rounds of revision.

For the initial three revisions, the final success attack rate of questions without LLM-powered suggestions (Verifier-Only) increases. Conversely, questions with LLM-powered suggestions (AI-Assisted) remain steady as they are initially created by the LLM-powered question formulation and are of good quality. Beyond four revisions, questions created in AI-Assisted begin to show an increasing trend. On the other hand, those in Verifier-Only require more revisions to match similar performance at 10 revisions, relative to those in AI-Assisted. We can see the potential benefits of LLM suggestions become more apparent, especially after several rounds of revisions.

One reason is that individuals start with their initial ideas, which may deplete after a few attempts. LLM suggestions can therefore serve as an idea pool, providing diverse editing ideas that are grounded in their input question template. This illustrates the use of AI in facilitating creative thought and efficient question revision among individuals.

With our high-quality and small-scale dataset, we evaluate several state-of-the-art LLMs to assess their cultural awareness and conduct qualitative analysis on factors that contribute to the difficulty of questions for LLMs.

In Table

CulturalBench-v0.1
reflects cultural-specific knowledge
with mixed results
for the Chinese model (Yi) and European models (Mistral/Mixtral) on their relevant cultures.
Contrary to the intuitive belief that models trained with a special focus on specific languages (e.g., Yi for Chinese and Mistral large and Mixtral MoE models for European languages) will perform better on cultures associated with the specialized language, we surprisingly observe mixed results. While Yi demonstrates lower-than-average performance (41.00 vs. 49.72), Mistral Large and Mixtral MoE (54.3 and 52.2 vs. 51.21) show significantly higher performance than average.

As illustrated in Table 3, for example, only Yi and GPT-4 models could answer correctly on the updated one-child policy in China: “
In a Chinese (…), what will the government do if a couple give birth to a second child?
” when other models still believed only one child is allowed in China. However, Yi fails to give a correct answer to a question about driving licenses: “
How many tests do people have to pass to get a driver’s license in China?
” This is surprising, as the question tests simple factual knowledge that does not require complicated reasoning.
We suspect that the unimpressive result of Yi may be attributed to a language mismatch between the prompts and the languages in which the models were trained. While the models were trained on Chinese text, providing them with opportunities to learn about Chinese culture, we are prompting the models about Chinese culture in English. Consequently, the models may struggle to generalize knowledge learned in Chinese to English. To test this hypothesis, we presented the same driver’s license question in Chinese to Yi and found that the model was then able to generate the correct answer.
Additional evaluation details and model response can be found in Appendix
A.7
. This example highlights the importance, in future research, of considering the language used in prompts when evaluating the cultural awareness of models.

CulturalBench-v0.1
is
challenging to modern LLMs
, with varying difficulties.

Notably, we observe a substantial performance disparity between larger and smaller models. For example, GPT-4-turbo achieves the highest accuracy at 72.2%, closely followed by Claude 3 Opus at 70.6%. Conversely, relatively smaller models such as Mistral 7b and GPT-3.5-turbo attain lower accuracy scores of 37.7% and 39.7%, respectively.

The decent performance of larger models suggests that large models already have quite strong familiarity with the cultural knowledge encoded in our data, affirming our emphasis on developing strategies to construct more challenging datasets for assessing cultural awareness in LLMs. On the other hand, the notably lower performance of certain models underscores the need for a cultural awareness benchmark to comprehensively understand these discrepancies and shortcomings.

3.3
How users’ behaviors and perception differ with varying levels of AI-assistance

We compare and analyze annotators’ behaviors and their perception. We used a two-sided Student t-test for the following analysis.

3.3.1
Behaviors

Users with more AI-assistance spend
more time
on revising and make
more revisions
, compared with those with less AI-assistance.

As shown in Table
10
, users with LLM-generated hints (AI-Assisted setting) require 152.4 more seconds, compared to users without LLM-generated Hints (Verifier-Only) with
p=0.1
.
Similarly, users in AI-Assisted setting also make 7.19 more revisions than users in Verifier-Only. One possible reason is that the LLM-generated hints provide more information and inspiration for users to revise their questions. Users with LLM-generated hints tend to try and adopt various existing hints whereas users without LLM-generated hints tend to give up on revising and restart another round due to a lack of revision ideas and less guidance.

User with more AI-assistance write
more Hard questions
, relative to users with less AI-assistance.

Following the Section
3.2
questions categorization, we found that each user with AI-Assisted created 15.7% more
Hard
questions (0-1 models we selected in Table
2
correct correctly) more than each user without AI-Assisted at p=0.05.

This finding could indicate that revision based on posed questions attributed to the creation of more Hard questions for LLMs.
This demonstrates that encouraging users to continue revising rather than restarting a new scenario could be a key to creating challenging LLM benchmarks.

3.3.2
Perception

Users with more AI-assistance expressed
more positive agreement on the system capability to spark their creativity
, relative to those with less AI-assistance.

On a scale of 1 (very limiting to creativity) to 5 (very conductive to creativity), users with more AI-assistance (AI-Assisted setting) reported a mean of 4.19 (SD=0.66) whereas users with less AI-assistance (Verifier-Only) reported a mean of 3.58 (SD=0.79).
The difference between these scores (0.61) is significant (
p=0.05
).
This suggests that the inclusion of LLMs in the initial question forming or revision could also potentially boost the system’s capacity to stimulate creativity.
One user expressed similar thoughts: “
The ability to generate multiple iterations of questions using AI was helpful to my creativity, as well as the hints provided.
”

Nine
users with both more and less AI-assistance report
positive impressions
of the annotation system and task design.

9 out of 27 users (5 from Verifier-Only; 4 from AI-Assisted) expressed their enthusiasm. One user from Verifier-Only said “
I enjoyed learning how much the AI actually knew about culture,
” Another user from AI-Assisted liked the gamified elements: “
I liked how we could run the model again and again, …, making this process a lot more fun and gamified.
”
Users from both settings positively referred to “the confidence score” helping them understand the effectiveness of their revisions.

Some
users with both more and less AI-assistance
enjoy
the LLM-powered modules.

Some users appreciate knowing the confidence score along with the chosen option by our Verifier (3 from Verifier-Only, 1 from AI-Assisted). This allows them to gauge how close they are to ’winning’ (trick the AI successfully). Users with more AI-Assistance (6 from AI-Assisted) enjoyed various LLM-powered assistance, emphasizing “
I liked how it generated a question based on your observation of a culture
”(4 from AI-Assisted) and “
AI-generated hints
(2 from AI-Assisted).”

One possible reason is that the LLM-powered modules reduce users’ cognitive load when formulating questions and revisions.
They provide grounded ideas based on users’ inputs (e.g. their scenario, their posed question), rather than purely text guidance. Users engaged more by trying the LLM-powered modules, possibly increasing question revision time.
When designing similar annotation systems, the trade-off between engagement and time spent should be considered.

Six
users with both more and less AI-assistance report
difficulty in deceiving the LLM
.

11 out of 27 users (6 from Verifier-Only; 5 from AI-Assisted) believed that nothing posed a challenge.
However, 6 users (2 from Verifier-Only; 4 from AI-Assisted) expressed difficulty in deceiving the LLM: “
Refine the questions was a bit challenging. That might be due to the nature of the question rather than the platform itself.
”. Despite all our design modifications, such as incorporating a confidence score along with the response option, this remains difficult for some users, as evidenced by their feedback. This underscores the importance of designing novel annotation systems to assist human annotators in brainstorming scenarios rather than purely formulating questions.

Related Work

AI-augmented Data Collection

Prior works have investigated on how to incorporate AI into different subtasks during data curation including writing
(
Shen et al., 2023
)
and auto-crowdsourcing pipelines
(
Wu et al., 2023
)
. One popular approach to improve LLMs is through red teaming to reveal models’ weaknesses. Researchers recruited human annotators to attack their LLMs to reduce models’ harmful outputs
(
Ganguli et al., 2022
)
. Some works have also been done on constructing adversarial datasets more efficiently with AI massive generations
(
Radharapu et al., 2023
)
.

Cultural Bias on LLMs
To evaluate using grounded theory, some studies focus on auditing LLMs and comparing them to humans through existing socio-cultural surveys
(
Li et al., 2024
;
Tao et al., 2023
;
Ramezani & Xu, 2023
)
. People also investigate language diversity by scaling up to multilingual settings
(
Mukherjee et al., 2023
;
Yue et al., 2023
)
, with a particular focus on low-resource languages
(
Deas et al., 2023
;
Wibowo et al., 2023
)
. Some recent works have also been conducted to construct schemas for generating massive datasets with AI, which aims to better capture LLMs’ cultural bias
(
Fung et al., 2024
;
Huang & Yang, 2023
;
Ziems et al., 2023
)
.

5
Conclusion

We present CulturalTeaming, an interactive red-teaming system that leverages the synergy of human-AI collaboration to collect a truly challenging dataset to assess LLMs’ multicultural knowledge. Through workshop sessions in our user studies, we gather users’ red-teaming attempts to form a compact yet high-quality evaluation dataset
CulturalBench-v0.1
. With varying AI-assistance levels of our system, we compared and analyzed our user studies’ results in terms of system performance, data quality, and users’ behaviors and perception.

Ethics Statement

Our study recruited students from a Natural Language Processing class at an academic institution. We provided participation class credits to participants who joined the experiment. These credits can also be gained through other methods e.g., writing essays. Therefore, the participation is optional. Before joining the workshop,
we provide consent materials to explain the experiment’s setup and their rights, including the ability to ask for clarification anytime and leave at their own will during the experiment. This study was reviewed by the same academic institution’s IRB committee.

The findings of the user studies on our CulturalTeaming system are based on this limited user demographics and designed task guidance. Our users were mostly from the computer science department and may not represent the general public, as shown by the skewed population in Table
3
. Additionally, our users were more familiar with interacting with LLM-based systems when compared with the general public. In this study, we prepared detailed guidance and tutorials for users to do their red-teaming tasks. This approach may not be natural, and we would need to consider such differences when employing our system with other public user groups in the future.

Moreover, the
CulturalBench-v0.1
is a small-scale dataset. Although it covers a wide range of cultures, many of them only contain a few data points. We have also chosen to collect English datasets this time instead of multilingual datasets. Therefore, the dataset is not sufficient to evaluate the multicultural knowledge of LLMs. However, we believe that CulturalTeaming has huge potential of collecting various cultural norms by expanding this platform to different locations and languages in the future.

A.2
Additional Details on User Experiment

Recruitment Eligibility Criteria

We recruit students from a natural language processing class in an academic institution. All students, regardless of their cultural backgrounds (including students with a background in the U.S. culture), are invited to participate.

User Demographic Description

We have collected self-reported demographic information from users.

In our study, we have 46 users in total (pivot round: 11; main round: 35).
It covers diverse ethnic groups, for example, East Asian with the highest proportion (48.1%), White with 17.3%, South Asian with 13.5%, and Middle East with 9.6%.

The population skewed towards the age group of 18-24 with 93.5%, education level of Bachelor’s degree with 80.0%, and gender identity of man with 71.7%.
The demographic information is in Appendix
3
. We also collected the number of years in their longest-living countries apart from the US to understand if our users have personal living experiences in corresponding regions in Fig.
5
. More than half (30 out of 46) have at least 1 year living in the longest-living country outside US.

Experiment Setup

Experiments consist of a series of 1-hour workshops, each accommodating up to 10 users.
In each session, users are randomly grouped into pairs for the trial of the two system variants.
Users are provided with self-contained instructions outlining the workshop timeline, tutorial material, and access to the annotation platform.
In the workshop, we recommend each user spend about 10 minutes on the tutorial and then start their trial, leaving about 45 minutes for interaction with the system.
They are then asked to complete a post-workshop survey for 5 minutes, capturing their demographic information along with their overall experience feedback.

The tutorial material for each of the system variants includes a 5-minute tutorial video demonstrating a full system trial and detailed instructions to use the system, including brainstorming a culturally sensitive scenario and common editing strategies with examples.
After the video, each user’s starting time was logged and they were asked to interact with the platform and create challenging questions without external resources such as ChatGPT.
We encouraged annotators to interact with the system for at least 45 minutes and leave or extend their interaction at their own will.

However, for the sake of fair comparative analysis between the two variants, we only included data from annotators who actively engaged with the system for at least 35 minutes, resulting in 12 annotators for the Verifier-Only variant and 15 annotators for the AI-Assisted variant.

Our user studies have two rounds (pivot and main round). Both rounds provide the same task design and platform. We added more instructions to make it more self-contained after this round to reduce the questions raised by annotators during the experiment. For system performance and annotator performance analysis, the additional instruction is sensitive to annotators therefore we only included the main round user performance. For our end product (dataset), the task design and schema do not change so we included both pivot and main round data.

A couple of users with both more and less AI-assistance complain about LLMs.

Two participants (1 from Verifier-Only; 1 From AI-Assisted) complained about the time latency, and the inconsistent output of the LLM model respectively.
Our system used GPT-4 to provide higher-quality hints on their posed questions. To give convenience to users, we decided to generate all the hints once they submitted their posed questions and entered the panel of revising. The multiple API requests could be a reason for the time latency problem. To address this time latency problem, we instructed models to give a short length of output and give only one example at a time in our prompt shown in Appendix Table
9
e.g. ”Give one example in 8 words”. Future work should also balance between details of responses and waiting time on API calls. For inconsistent output, our system used the default setting on temperature and top-p to provide a relatively diverse answer to users. In future work, we could consider using greedy decoding to give consistent output to avoid confusion to users.
