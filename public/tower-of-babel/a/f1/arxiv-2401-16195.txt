Dot-depth three, return of the 𝒥 \mathrel{\mathscr{J}} -class
Thomas Place and Marc Zeitoun

Abstract
. We look at concatenation hierarchies of classes of regular languages. Each such hierarchy is determined by a single class, its basis: level n n is built by applying the Boolean polynomial closure operator (BPol), n n times to the basis. A prominent and difficult open question in automata theory is to decide membership of a regular language in a given level. For instance, for the historical dot-depth hierarchy, the decidability of membership is only known at levels one and two . We give a generic algebraic characterization of the operator BPol . This characterization implies that for any concatenation hierarchy, if n n is at least two, membership at level n n reduces to a more complex problem, called covering , for the previous level, n − 1 n-1 . Combined with earlier results on covering, this implies that membership is decidable for dot-depth three and for level two in most of the prominent hierarchies in the literature. For instance, we obtain that the levels two in both the modulo hierarchy and the group hierarchy have decidable membership.



1.
Introduction

Context.

This article addresses one of the most fundamental questions in automata theory, which emerged in the 1970s: the dot-depth problem. It originates from an algorithm by Schützenberger
[
45
]
that determines whether a regular language is
star-free
. Star-free languages are specific regular languages obtained from singletons using three regularity-preserving operations: union, concatenation, and complement (on the other hand, star-free expressions disallow Kleene star). Schützenberger’s result initiated a line of research aiming to understand subclasses of regular languages defined by high-level formalisms (
e.g.
, a restricted set of regular expressions). Moreover, it highlighted a natural parameter of star-free languages, which measures their complexity: the number of alternations between complement and concatenation required to express them.

Schützenberger’s work led Brzozowski and Cohen
[
10
]
to define a hierarchy of classes of languages known as the
dot-depth
. Roughly speaking, languages at level
n
in this hierarchy are those that can be defined by a star-free expression with at most
n-1
alternations between complement and concatenation. Brzozowski and Knast showed that the dot-depth is infinite (
i.e.
, it does not collapse)
[
11
]
. The significance of the dot-depth hierarchy is further evidenced by its logical formulation, discovered by Thomas
[
59
]
: level
n
in the dot-depth corresponds to the class
\mathscr{B}\Sigma_{n}
of all languages that can be defined in first-order logic under an appropriate signature, with at most
n
blocks of quantifiers of the form
\exists^{*}
or
\forall^{*}
.

The dot-depth actually inspired a general family of hierarchies called the
concatenation hierarchies
. The simplest way to define them is through
operators
. An operator
Op
takes as input a class
\mathscr{C}
of regular languages and produces as output a larger class
\mathop{\textup{Op}(\mathscr{C})}
. In order to build a concatenation hierarchy, we require two of them. First, the
polynomial closure

\textup{Pol}(\mathscr{C})
of a class
\mathscr{C}
consists of all finite unions of concatenation products
L_{1}a_{1}L_{1}\cdots a_{n}L_{n}
where the languages
L_{i}
belong to
\mathscr{C}
and the
a_{i}
’s are letters. Second, the
Boolean closure

\textup{Bool}(\mathscr{C})
of a class
\mathscr{C}
is its closure under all Boolean operations. Finally, “
BPol
” is the composition of these two operators:
\textup{BPol}(\mathscr{C})=\textup{Bool}(\textup{Pol}(\mathscr{C}))
for each class
\mathscr{C}
. All concatenation hierarchies follow the same construction pattern: it starts from a base class, level zero; then, level
n+1
is built from level
n
by applying the “
BPol
” operator once. Thus, each hierarchy is entirely determined by its level zero. For the dot-depth hierarchy, level zero consists of four languages: the empty one, the singleton consisting of the empty word, and their complements. Other prominent hierarchies include the
Straubing-Thérien hierarchy

[
49
,
56
]
whose basis consists of only the empty and universal languages, the
group hierarchy

[
23
]
whose basis consists of the languages recognized by a permutation automaton
[
58
]
or the
modulo hierarchy

[
12
]
whose basis consist of the “modulo languages”, which are those for which membership of a word is determined by its length modulo some integer.

The logical characterization by quantifier alternation hierarchies is also a generic feature of concatenation hierarchies. Each such hierarchy corresponds to the alternation hierarchy in first-order logic equipped with a signature determined by its basis
[
39
]
. For instance, the Straubing-Thérien hierarchy
[
25
]
is associated with the signature consisting only of the linear order “
<
” on positions and the unary alphabetical predicates
a(x)
,
b(x)
,… In order to capture the dot-depth, one needs to add the successor predicate “
+1
”
[
59
]
. The modulo hierarchy
[
12
]
is also a natural example. Its signature consists of the linear order, the alphabetical predicates and the modular predicates, which test position’s
values modulo an integer
.

In summary, the levels of concatenation hierarchies have a dual characterization: by operators on the one hand, by logic on the other. This underscores that the minimum level containing a language is a
robust
parameter. Intuitively, it measures the complexity required to express a language. This intuition is formalized by the fact that the non-elementary lower bound for checking satisfiability in first-order logic comes from quantifier alternation
[
48
]
. This has motivated researchers to tackle the original Schützenberger membership problem for individual level of concatenation hierarchies: do we have an algorithm to test whether an input language belongs to such a level? If so, we say that the level is
decidable
.

State of the art.

Historically, this question has drawn the attention of numerous researchers, see
[
29
]
for a survey. Initially, it was mostly addressed for
specific levels
in the dot-depth or Straubing-Thérien hierarchies. Nevertheless, due to its inherent complexity, advances have been gradual. Simon
[
46
,
47
]
proposed an algorithm for level one in the Straubing-Thérien hierarchy. This is an influential result and there are many alternate proofs
[
26
,
54
,
1
,
18
,
19
]
. Knast
[
20
]
also tackled level one, but for the dot-depth hierarchy (see also
[
57
,
2
,
21
]
for alternate proofs). Straubing then designed a key reduction
[
50
]
that shows that a level
n
is decidable in the Straubing-Thérien hierarchy if and only if the same holds for the dot-depth. Additionally, level one has been proven to be decidable in the group hierarchy. This breakthrough was achieved
[
16
,
17
]
in a purely algebraic context, leveraging a challenging result by Ash
[
7
]
(see
[
27
]
for a survey). Finally, there are also several results concerning the lower levels of the modulo hierarchy
[
12
,
22
]

Following these first results, the decidability of dot-depth
two
became a prominent open problem in automata theory. Many partial results, conjectures and over-approximations were proposed
[
8
,
9
,
60
,
13
,
51
,
52
,
61
,
55
,
62
,
33
,
34
,
3
,
4
]
until decidability was finally proved in
[
36
,
40
]
. The algorithms presented in
[
36
]
and
[
40
]
differ slightly, but they both rely on a more general problem than membership, called
separation
, which is decidable for a subclass of dot-depth two. Intuitively, solving
\mathscr{C}
-separation for a class
\mathscr{C}
, brings information that can be exploited for studying classes built on top of
\mathscr{C}
(typically through operators). A striking example of this claim is the
generic
reduction
[
39
]
from
\textup{Pol}(\mathscr{C})
-
membership
to
\mathscr{C}
-
separation
(when
\mathscr{C}
has mild properties). It is based on a generic characterization of
\textup{Pol}(\mathscr{C})
on the
syntactic monoid
of the input language (a canonical algebraic recognizer that can be computed from the input). The class
\mathscr{C}
defines a binary relation on the syntactic monoid whose computation boils down to
\mathscr{C}
-separation. The characterization states that the input language belongs to
\textup{Pol}(\mathscr{C})
if and only if every pair of this relation satisfies a generic equation depending only on
Pol
(and not on
\mathscr{C}
).

Contributions.
We investigate the
BPol

operator
, as opposed to focusing on
\textup{BPol}(\mathscr{C})
for a
specific
class
\mathscr{C}
. This is desirable in order to obtain generic results and to pinpoint which hypotheses on the input class
\mathscr{C}
are necessary for deciding
\textup{BPol}(\mathscr{C})
-membership (thus abstracting away the irrelevant peculiarities of
\mathscr{C}
). This approach is not new, see for instance
[
34
]
. Our aim is to obtain a transfer result from
\mathscr{C}
-separation to
\textup{BPol}(\mathscr{C})
-membership, similar to the one existing for
Pol
. Unfortunately,
BPol
is much harder to deal with than
Pol
. Indeed, in practice, the classes which are closed under
language concatenation
(such as
\textup{Pol}(\mathscr{C})
) are simpler to handle than those which are not (such as
\textup{BPol}(\mathscr{C})
in general).

Our first contribution is a
generic
algebraic characterization of
BPol
(for input classes satisfying mild properties). Its presentation is similar to the aforementioned characterization of
Pol
. It uses two equations that the syntactic monoid of a language satisfies if and only if the language in is
\textup{BPol}(\mathscr{C})
. Each one parameterized by a relation defined from
\mathscr{C}
. The first of these relations is the one used in the characterization of
\textup{Pol}(\mathscr{C})
: its computation boils down to
\mathscr{C}
-separation. Unfortunately, the second relation is based on a new
ad hoc
problem for
\mathscr{C}
whose decidability status is not clear in general. Consequently, our first characterization does not immediately yield a
\textup{BPol}(\mathscr{C})
-membership procedure for most classes
\mathscr{C}
. Nonetheless, we do get an interesting simple corollary: a new effective characterization of level
two
in the Straubing-Thérien hierarchy. While such a characterization was known
[
40
]
, this new one is much simpler. In particular, this yields a new proof for the decidability of dot-depth
two
by the aforementioned reduction of Straubing
[
50
]
.

Our second contribution is a specialized characterization of
BPol
for the particular inputs
\mathscr{C}
which are themselves of the form
\textup{BPol}(\mathscr{D})
, for some other class
\mathscr{D}
. In other words,
\mathscr{C}
is a
non-zero
level in an arbitrary concatenation hierarchy and
\textup{BPol}(\mathscr{C})
is a level above
two
in the same hierarchy. In this case, we are able to reformulate the second equation of our first generic characterization. We end-up with a new equation parameterized by a relation based on the
\mathscr{C}
-
covering
problem (a generalization of separation to more than just two input languages). Therefore, the main corollary of this second characterization is that
\textup{BPol}(\textup{BPol}(\mathscr{D}))
-
membership
reduces to
\textup{BPol}(\mathscr{D})
-
covering
. As the latter problem has already been extensively studied for many classes
\textup{BPol}(\mathscr{D})
, we obtain as a corollary new positive decidability results of membership for several levels.

Applications.
When
\mathscr{G}
consists only of
group languages
and has decidable
separation
, it is shown in
[
41
,
35
]
that level
one
in the hierarchies of bases
\mathscr{G}
and
\mathscr{G}^{+}
has decidable
covering
. Here,
\mathscr{G}^{+}
consists of all languages of the form
L\setminus\{\varepsilon\}
and
L\cup\{\varepsilon\}
, with
L\in\mathscr{G}
. Combined with our results, this implies that for such classes
\mathscr{G}
, level
two
in the hierarchies of bases
\mathscr{G}
and
\mathscr{G}^{+}
has decidable
membership
. Most of the bases that are considered in the literature are of this kind. In particular, since their bases have decidable separation
[
7
,
44
]
, we obtain that level
two
in both the
modulo hierarchy
and the
group hierarchy
has decidable membership.

Our other main applications concern the Straubing-Thérien hierarchy and the dot-depth. It was shown in
[
42
]
that level
two
has decidable
covering
in both hierarchies. Combined with our results, this implies that level
three
in both the Straubing-Thérien hierarchy and the dot-depth has decidable membership.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Words, classes and regular languages

We fix an arbitrary finite alphabet
A
for the whole paper. As usual,
A^{*}
denotes the set of all words over
A
, including the empty word
\varepsilon
. We let
A^{+}=A^{*}\setminus\{\varepsilon\}
. For
u,v\in A^{*}
, we write
uv
the word obtained by concatenating
u
and
v
. A
language
is a subset of
A^{*}
. Abusing terminology, we often write
u
for the singleton
\{u\}
. We lift concatenation to languages: for
K,L\subseteq A^{*}
, we let
KL=\{uv\mid u\in K\text{\;and\;}v\in L\}
.

A
class of languages

\mathscr{C}
is a set of languages. We say that
\mathscr{C}
is a
lattice
when it is closed under union and intersection,
\emptyset\in\mathscr{C}
and
A^{*}\in\mathscr{C}
. Also, a
Boolean algebra
is a lattice closed under complement. Finally, if for all
L\in\mathscr{C}
and
w\in A^{*}
, the languages
w^{-1}L=\{u\in A^{*}\mid wu\in L\}
and
Lw^{-1}=\{u\in A^{*}\mid uw\in L\}
both belong to
\mathscr{C}
, we say that
\mathscr{C}
is closed under
quotients
.

A
positive prevariety
(resp.
prevariety
) is a lattice (resp. Boolean algebra) closed under quotients, containing only
regular languages
. These are the languages that can be equivalently defined by finite automata or finite monoids. We use the latter definition.

Monoids.
A
monoid
is a set
M
endowed with an associative multiplication
(s,t)\mapsto st
which has an identity
1_{M}
,
i.e.
, such that
{1_{M}}\cdot s=s\cdot{1_{M}}=s
for every
s\in M
. A
group
is a monoid
G
such that for all
g\in G
, there exists
g^{-1}
such that
gg^{-1}=g^{-1}g=1_{G}
.

An
idempotent
of a monoid
M
is an element
e\in M
such that
ee=e
. We write
E(M)\subseteq M
for the set of all idempotents in
M
. It is folklore that for each
finite
semigroup
M
, there is a natural number
\omega(M)
(denoted by
\omega
when
M
is understood) such that for every
s\in M
, the element
s^{\omega}
is an idempotent.

An
ordered monoid
is a pair
(M,\leq)
where
M
is a monoid and
\leq
is a partial order on
M
, compatible with multiplication: for all
s,s^{\prime},t,t^{\prime}\in M
, if
s\leq t
and
s^{\prime}\leq t^{\prime}
, then
ss^{\prime}\leq tt^{\prime}
. A subset
F\subseteq M
is an upper set (for
\leq
) if it is upward closed for
\leq
: for all
s,t\in M
, if
s\in F
and
s\leq t
, then
t\in F
. By convention, we view every
unordered
monoid
M
as the ordered one
(M,=)
whose ordering is
equality
. In this case,
all
subsets of
M
are upper sets. Thus, a definition for ordered monoids makes sense for unordered ones.

Finally, we use the Green relations
[
15
]
defined on monoids. We briefly recall them. Given a monoid
M
and
s,t\in M
,

\begin{array}[]{ll@{\ }l}s\leqslant_{\mathscr{J}}t&\text{when}&\text{there exist $x,y\in M$ such that $s=xty$},\\
s\leqslant_{\mathscr{L}}t&\text{when}&\text{there exists $x\in M$ such that $s=xt$},\\
s\leqslant_{\mathscr{R}}t&\text{when}&\text{there exists $y\in M$ such that $s=ty$},\\
s\leqslant_{\mathscr{H}}t&\text{when}&\text{$s\leqslant_{\mathscr{L}}t$ and $s\leqslant_{\mathscr{R}}t$}.\end{array}

These relations are preorders (
i.e.
, reflexive and transitive). We write
<_{\mathscr{J}}
,
<_{\mathscr{L}}
,
<_{\mathscr{R}}
and
<_{\mathscr{H}}
for their strict variants (
e.g.
,
s<_{\mathscr{J}}t
when
s\leqslant_{\mathscr{J}}t
but
t\not\leqslant_{\mathscr{J}}s
). We write
\mathrel{\mathscr{J}}
,
\mathrel{\mathscr{L}}
,
\mathrel{\mathscr{R}}
and
\mathrel{\mathscr{H}}
for the associated equivalences (
e.g.
,
s\mathrel{\mathscr{J}}t
when
s\leqslant_{\mathscr{J}}t
and
t\leqslant_{\mathscr{J}}s
). The following lemmas are standard (see
e.g.
,
[
30
]
).

Lemma 1
.

Let
M
be a finite monoid and
s,t\in M
such that
t\leqslant_{\mathscr{J}}s
. If
s\leqslant_{\mathscr{R}}t
, then
s\mathrel{\mathscr{R}}t
. Moreover, if
s\leqslant_{\mathscr{L}}t
then
s\mathrel{\mathscr{L}}t
.

Lemma 2
.

Let
M
be a finite monoid,
e\in E(M)
and
s\in M
be such that
e\mathrel{\mathscr{H}}s
. Then,
s^{\omega}=e
.

Regular languages.
Note that
A^{*}
is a monoid: its multiplication is word concatenation, and its identity is
\varepsilon
. We consider morphisms
\alpha:A^{*}\to(M,\leq)
where
(M,\leq)
is an arbitrary ordered monoid. That is,
\alpha:A^{*}\to M
is a map such that
\alpha(\varepsilon)=1_{M}
and
\alpha(uv)=\alpha(u)\alpha(v)
for all
u,v\in A^{*}
. We say that
L\subseteq A^{*}
is
recognized
by the morphism
\alpha
when there is an
upper set

F\subseteq M
for
\leq
such that
L=\alpha^{-1}(F)
.

Remark 3
.

Recall that we view any unordered monoid
M
as the ordered one
(M,=)
. Thus, a language
L\subseteq A^{*}
is recognized by a morphism
\alpha:A^{*}\to M
if there exists an
arbitrary
set
F\subseteq M
(all subsets are upper sets for “
=
”) such that
L=\alpha^{-1}(F)
.

It is standard that the regular languages are those which can be recognized by a morphism into a
finite
monoid.

Remark 4
.

The only infinite monoid that we consider is
A^{*}
. We shall implicitly assume that every other monoid
M,N,\dots
is finite.

2.2.
Membership, separation and covering

We define three decision problems. Each of them depends on an arbitrary fixed class
\mathscr{C}
and take regular languages as input. We use them as mathematical tools for investigating
\mathscr{C}
.

The first problem is
\mathscr{C}
-membership
. It takes a regular language
L
as input and asks whether
L
belongs to
\mathscr{C}
.

The second problem is
\mathscr{C}
-separation
. It takes as input two regular languages
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
and asks if
L_{1}
is
\mathscr{C}
-separable
from
L_{2}
: does there exist
K\in\mathscr{C}
such that
L_{1}\subseteq K
and
L_{2}\cap K=\emptyset
?

Remark 5
.

Separation generalizes membership. Indeed, for any regular language
L
, we have
L\in\mathscr{C}
if and only if

L
is
\mathscr{C}
-separable from
A^{*}\setminus L
(which is also regular).

The third problem is
\mathscr{C}
-covering
. It takes as input a regular language
L_{1}
and a finite set
\mathbf{L}_{2}
of regular languages, and asks whether the pair
(L_{1},\mathbf{L}_{2})
is
\mathscr{C}
-coverable
: does there exist a
\mathscr{C}
-cover
\mathbf{K}
of
L_{1}
(
i.e.
,
\mathbf{K}
is a
finite
set of languages in
\mathscr{C}
such that
L\subseteq\bigcup_{K\in\mathbf{K}}K
) such that for all
K\in\mathbf{K}
, there exists
L\in\mathbf{L}_{2}
satisfying
K\cap L=\emptyset
?

Remark 6
.

When
\mathscr{C}
is a lattice, this generalizes separation:
L_{1}
is
\mathscr{C}
-separable from
L_{2}
if and only if
(L_{1},\{L_{2}\})
is
\mathscr{C}
-coverable.

Finally, the definition of
\mathscr{C}
-covering is simplified when
\mathscr{C}
is a
Boolean algebra
. Given a finite set of languages
\mathbf{L}
, we say that
\mathbf{L}
is
\mathscr{C}
-coverable
to mean that
(A^{*},\mathbf{L})
is
\mathscr{C}
-coverable. The following simple lemma is proved in
[
37
]
.

Lemma 7
.

Let
\mathscr{C}
be a Boolean algebra,
L_{1}\subseteq A^{*}
be a language and
\mathbf{L}_{2}
be a finite set of languages. Then,
(L_{1},\mathbf{L}_{2})
is
\mathscr{C}
-coverable if and only if
\{L_{1}\}\cup\mathbf{L}_{2}
is
\mathscr{C}
-coverable.

2.3.
\mathscr{C}
-morphisms

We turn to a key mathematical tool (see
[
38
,
43
]
for the proofs). Given a positive prevariety
\mathscr{C}
, a morphism
\eta:A^{*}\to(N,\leq)
into a finite ordered monoid
is a
\mathscr{C}
-morphism
if it is
surjective
and all languages recognized by
\eta
belong to
\mathscr{C}
. If
\mathscr{C}
is a prevariety (
i.e.
,
\mathscr{C}
is also closed under complement), it suffices to consider
unordered
monoids (by convention, we view them as ordered by equality).

Lemma 8
.

Let
\mathscr{C}
be a prevariety and let
\eta:A^{*}\to(N,\leq)
be a morphism. The two following conditions are equivalent:

(1)

\eta:A^{*}\to(N,\leq)
is a
\mathscr{C}
-morphism.

(2)

\eta:A^{*}\to(N,=)
is a
\mathscr{C}
-morphism.

Remark 9
.

Another formulation of Lemma
8
is that when
\mathscr{C}
is a prevariety, whether a morphism
\eta:A^{*}\to(N,\leq)
is a
\mathscr{C}
-morphism does not depend on the ordering
\leq
. Thus, we shall implicitly use Lemma
8
and drop the ordering when dealing with prevarieties.

We use
\mathscr{C}
-morphisms as mathematical tools. In particular, we need the following result.

Proposition 10
.

Let
\mathscr{C}
be a positive prevariety and let
L_{1},\dots,L_{k}\in\mathscr{C}
. There exists a
\mathscr{C}
-morphism
\eta:A^{*}\to(N,\leq)
recognizing
L_{1},\dots,L_{k}
.

We also use
\mathscr{C}
-morphisms to tackle membership. It is known that every regular language
L
is recognized by a canonical morphism
\alpha_{L}:A^{*}\to(M_{L},\leq_{L})
, called its
syntactic morphism
, which can be computed from any representation of
L
(see
e.g.
,
[
30
]
). It is standard that for every positive prevariety
\mathscr{C}
, we have
L\in\mathscr{C}
if and only if
\alpha_{L}
is a
\mathscr{C}
-morphism. Altogether, this yields the following proposition.

Proposition 11
.

Let
\mathscr{C}
be a positive prevariety. There is an effective reduction from
\mathscr{C}
-membership to the problem of deciding whether a morphism
\alpha:A^{*}\to(M,\leq)
is a
\mathscr{C}
-morphism.

3.
Concatenation hierarchies

Concatenation hierarchies are built uniformly from a single input class using two operators, which we first present.

3.1.
Polynomial closure and Boolean closure

The
polynomial closure of a class
\mathscr{C}

is the class
\textup{Pol}(\mathscr{C})
consisting of all
finite unions
of
marked products

L_{0}a_{1}L_{1}\cdots a_{n}L_{n}
where
n\in\mathbb{N}

a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\in A
and
L_{0},\dots,L_{n}\in\mathscr{C}
. It is not clear from the definition whether the class
\textup{Pol}(\mathscr{C})
has robust properties, even when
\mathscr{C}
does. It was shown by Arfi
[
5
,
6
]
that if
\mathscr{C}
is a prevariety, then
\textup{Pol}(\mathscr{C})
is a positive prevariety. This result was later strengthened by Pin
[
28
]
: if
\mathscr{C}
is a positive prevariety, then so is
\textup{Pol}(\mathscr{C})
(see also
[
39
]
).

Theorem 12
.

Let
\mathscr{C}
be a positive prevariety. Then,
\textup{Pol}(\mathscr{C})
is a positive prevariety closed under language concatenation.

In general, the classes
\textup{Pol}(\mathscr{C})
are
not
closed under complement. Hence, it is natural to combine polynomial closure with another operator. Given a class
\mathscr{D}
, the
Boolean closure
of
\mathscr{D}
, written
\textup{Bool}(\mathscr{D})
, is the
least
Boolean algebra containing
\mathscr{D}
. For each input class
\mathscr{C}
, we write
\textup{BPol}(\mathscr{C})
for
\textup{Bool}(\textup{Pol}(\mathscr{C}))
. Since quotients commute with Boolean operations, we have the following corollary of Theorem
12
.

Corollary 13
.

If
\mathscr{C}
is a positive prevariety,
\textup{BPol}(\mathscr{C})
is a prevariety.

Observe that by definition, a language in
\textup{Bool}(\mathscr{C})
is built from finitely many languages in
\mathscr{C}
. This yields the following lemma for
\textup{Bool}(\mathscr{C})
-morphisms.

Lemma 14
.

Let
\mathscr{C}
be a positive prevariety and
\alpha:A^{*}\to M
be a
\textup{Bool}(\mathscr{C})
-morphism. There exists a morphism
\beta:Q\to M
and a
\mathscr{C}
-morphism
\gamma:A^{*}\to(Q,\leq)
such that
\alpha=\beta\circ\gamma
.

Proof.

By hypothesis on
\alpha
, there exists a finite set
\mathbf{L}
of languages in
\mathscr{C}
such that every language recognized by
\alpha
is a Boolean combination of languages in
\mathbf{L}
. Since
\mathscr{C}
is a positive prevariety, Proposition
10
yields a
\mathscr{C}
-morphism
\gamma:A^{*}\to(Q,\leq)
recognizing every language
L\in\mathbf{L}
. It remains to construct
\beta:Q\to M
. For every
q\in Q
, we fix
w_{q}\in\gamma^{-1}(q)
and define
\beta(q)=\alpha(w_{q})
. We prove that
\beta
is a morphism. This boils down to proving that
\gamma(u)=\gamma(v)\Rightarrow\alpha(u)=\alpha(v)
for all
u,v\in A^{*}
. Indeed, since
\gamma(w_{1_{Q}})=1_{Q}=\gamma(\varepsilon)
, we would get
\alpha(w_{1_{Q}})=\alpha(\varepsilon)
, and therefore
\beta(1_{Q})=1_{M}
. Similarly, for
q,r\in Q
, since
\gamma(w_{qr})=qr=\gamma(w_{q}w_{r})
, we would get
\alpha(w_{qr})=\alpha(w_{q}w_{r})
, which yields
\beta(qr)=\beta(q)\beta(r)
.

Let now
u,v\in A^{*}
such that
\gamma(u)=\gamma(v)
, and let us prove that
\alpha(u)=\alpha(v)
. Since
\gamma
recognizes every language
L\in\mathbf{L}
, we have
u\in L\Leftrightarrow v\in L
for all
L\in\mathbf{L}
. Hence, for every Boolean combination
H
of languages of
\mathbf{L}
, we have
u\in H\Leftrightarrow v\in H
. Since every language recognized by
\alpha
is such a Boolean combination, we obtain
\alpha(u)=\alpha(v)
.

It remains to prove that
\alpha=\beta\circ\gamma
. Let
w\in A^{*}
and
q=\gamma(w)
. We have
\gamma(w)=\gamma(w_{q})
. Therefore,
\alpha(w)=\alpha(w_{q})=\beta(q)=\beta(\gamma(w))
, as desired.
∎

, there exists

We shall also need the following variant of Lemma

, which considers

Lemma 16

Let
\mathscr{C}
be a prevariety,

. For

Lemmas
15
and
16
only apply to very specific languages to be covered: such a language must be a concatenation of languages in
\textup{Pol}(\mathscr{C})
that are themselves already covered. To build these specific languages, we shall use another result which we present now.

We start with definitions. Let
\eta:A^{*}\to N
be a morphism (such as a
\mathscr{C}
-morphism) and
n\in\mathbb{N}
. An
\eta
-pattern of length
n
is a tuple
\widebar{p}=(w_{1},e_{1},\dots,w_{n},e_{n},w_{n+1})
of
(A^{+}\times E(N))^{n}\times A^{*}
satisfying the three following conditions:

(1)

If
n\geq 1
, then
w_{1},\dots,w_{n+1}\in A^{+}
are
all nonempty
,

(2)

e_{i}\mathrel{\mathscr{J}}\eta(w_{1}\cdots w_{n+1})
for
1\leq i\leq n
,

(3)

\eta(w_{i})e_{i}\!=\!\eta(w_{i})
and
e_{i}\eta(w_{i+1})\!=\!\eta(w_{i+1})
for
1\!\leq\!i\!\leq\!n
.

If
\alpha:A^{*}\to M
is another morphism, we say that
\widebar{p}
is
\alpha
-guarded
if for all
1\leq i\leq n
, there exists
g_{i}\in E(M)
such that
\eta^{-1}(e_{i})\cap\alpha^{-1}(g_{i})\neq\emptyset
,
\alpha(w_{i})g_{i}=\alpha(w_{i})
and
g_{i}\alpha(w_{i+1})=\alpha(w_{i+1})
. Note that
\widebar{p}
is
\eta
-guarded if
\eta
is surjective. Note also that when
n=0
, Conditions (
1
) to (
3
) hold trivially. Therefore,
(w)
is an
\eta
-pattern for every
w\in A^{*}
.

We now introduce a notation. Let
\widebar{p}=(w_{1},e_{1},\dots,w_{n},e_{n},w_{n+1})
be an
\eta
-pattern. We write
\pi(\widebar{p})=w_{1}\cdots w_{n+1}\in A^{*}
. Also, we let
\uclos_{\eta}\widebar{p}\subseteq A^{*}
be the following language:

\uclos_{\eta}\widebar{p}=w_{1}\ \eta^{-1}(e_{1})\ \cdots\ w_{n}\ \eta^{-1}(e_{n})\ w_{n+1}.

When
\widebar{p}
is of length
n=0
, we let
\uclos_{\eta}\widebar{p}=\{w_{1}\}
.

We turn to the statement. Given a morphism
\eta:A^{*}\to N
and a
\mathrel{\mathscr{J}}
-class

, it can be used to build a cover of a language included in
\eta^{-1}(J)
. Yet, we need a strong hypothesis on

. We say that a
\mathrel{\mathscr{J}}
-class

is
\eta
-alphabetic to indicate that for every

, if
J\leqslant_{\mathscr{J}}\eta(a)
, then
\eta(a)\in J
.

Proposition 17
.

Let
\eta:A^{*}\to N
and
\alpha:A^{*}\to M
be two morphisms,
J\subseteq N
be an
\eta
-alphabetic
\mathrel{\mathscr{J}}
-class and
H\subseteq\eta^{-1}(J)
. Then, there exists a finite set
P
of
\alpha
-guarded
\eta
-patterns such that
\pi(\widebar{p})\in H
for all
\widebar{p}\in P
and
\{\uclos_{\eta}\widebar{p}\mid\widebar{p}\in P\}
is a cover of
H
.

Remark 18
.

The proof of Proposition
17
is not immediate. The core idea is to define an ordering on a suitable set of
\eta
-patterns and to show that this ordering is well. The proof is inspired by the “minimal bad sequence” argument of Nash-Williams
[
24
]
. The set
P
is then chosen as a set of minimal patterns for this ordering.

Before proving Proposition
17
, we introduce some terminology on
\eta
-patterns. Let
\eta:A^{*}\to N
be a morphism and
\widebar{p}
be an
\eta
-pattern. For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we define the
n
-splits
of
\widebar{p}
. Intuitively, they are tuples
(\widebar{p}_{1},e_{1},\dots,\widebar{p}_{n},e_{n},\widebar{p}_{n+1})
where

and
\widebar{p}_{1},\dots,\widebar{p}_{n}
are
\eta
-patterns, which are such that “flattening”

\widebar{p}
. Let us formally define
n
-splits, by induction on
n
. If
n=0
,
(\widebar{p})
is the only
0
-split of
\widebar{p}
. If

, we say that
(\widebar{p}_{1},e_{1},\widebar{p}_{2})
is a
1
-split of
\widebar{p}
if
\widebar{p}_{1}=(u_{1},f_{1},\dots,u_{k},f_{k},u_{k+1})

\widebar{p}_{2}=(v_{1},g_{1},\dots,v_{\ell},g_{\ell},v_{\ell+1})
and,

\widebar{p}=(u_{1},f_{1},\dots,u_{k},f_{k},u_{k+1},e_{1},v_{1},g_{1},\dots,v_{\ell},g_{\ell},v_{\ell+1}).

Finally, when

, we say that
(\widebar{p}_{1},e_{1},\dots,\widebar{p}_{n},e_{n},\widebar{p}_{n+1})
is an
n
-split of
\widebar{p}
when there exists an
\eta
-pattern
\widebar{q}
such that
(\widebar{q},e_{n},\widebar{p}_{n+1})
is a

-split of
\widebar{p}
and

(n-1)
-split of
\widebar{q}
.

Finally, a
split
of
\widebar{p}
is an
n
-split of
\widebar{p}
for some

. Abusing terminology, we often write
\widebar{p}=(\widebar{p}_{1},e_{1},\dots,\widebar{p}_{n},e_{n},\widebar{p}_{n+1})
to indicate that
(\widebar{p}_{1},e_{1},\dots,\widebar{p}_{n},e_{n},\widebar{p}_{n+1})
is a split of
\widebar{p}
. We may now recall the statement of Proposition
17
.

When
\widebar{p}
is an
\eta
-pattern, we abuse notation and write
\eta(\widebar{p})
instead of
\eta(\pi(\widebar{p}))
. Clearly, when
(\widebar{p}_{1},e_{1},\dots,\widebar{p}_{n},e_{n},\widebar{p}_{n+1})
is a split of
\widebar{p}
, we have
\eta(\widebar{p})=\eta(\widebar{p}_{1})\cdots\eta(\widebar{p}_{n})\eta(\widebar{p}_{n+1})
.

Proof.

We work with a slightly different property than the one of the statement. Observe that given an
\eta
-pattern
\widebar{p}
, it may happen that

\widebar{p}
an alternate language
\uclos_{*}\widebar{p}
containing

. It will be easier to manipulate in the proof. Let
\widebar{p}=(w_{1},e_{1},\dots,w_{n},e_{n},w_{n+1})
be an
\eta

\uclos_{*}\widebar{p}\subseteq A^{*}

\uclos_{*}\widebar{p}=w_{1}(\eta^{-1}(e_{1})\cup\{\varepsilon\})\ \cdots\ w_{n}(\eta^{-1}(e_{n})\cup\{\varepsilon\})w_{n+1}.

This definition ensures that
\pi(\widebar{p})\in\uclos_{*}\widebar{p}
for every
\eta
-pattern
\widebar{p}
. We connect this notion to the original languages

Fact 19
.

Let
\widebar{p}
be an
\alpha
-guarded
\eta
-pattern. There exists finitely many
\alpha
-guarded
\eta
-patterns
\widebar{p}_{1},\dots,\widebar{p}_{k}

for every

\uclos_{*}\widebar{p}=\bigcup_{0\leq i\leq k}\uclos_{\eta}\widebar{p}_{i}

We write
\widebar{p}=(w_{1},e_{1},\dots,w_{n},e_{n},w_{n+1})
. If
n=0
, then
\uclos_{*}\widebar{p}=\uclos_{\eta}\widebar{p}=\{w_{1}\}
. Hence, it suffices to chose

and
\widebar{p}_{1}=\widebar{p}
. Assume now that
n\geq 1
. Let

. We associate an
\eta
-pattern
\widebar{p}_{I}
that we build from

. Let

with
i_{1}<\cdots<i_{h}

and

We construct a finite set
P

\alpha

\widebar{p}

\eta

as a subset of

. Let

-guarded

Lemma 20

Let

. There is
\widebar{p}\in Q

Proof.

We prove will a stronger result. We define

as the set of all
\eta
-patterns of the form
\widebar{p}=(w_{1},e_{1},\dots,w_{n},e_{n},w_{n+1})\in Q

. We prove that for every

\widebar{p}\in R
such that

. In the proof, we consider the monoid

equipped with the componentwise multiplication. We write
\gamma:A^{*}\to M\times N

\gamma(u)=(\alpha(u),\eta(u))

We proceed by induction on the length of

\widebar{p}=(w)\in R

and

such that

\gamma(a_{1}\cdots a_{i})=\gamma(a_{1}\cdots a_{j})

\gamma(a_{i+1}\cdots a_{k})=\gamma(a_{j+1}\cdots a_{k})

and
|y|\leq k

(g,e)=(\gamma(a_{i+1}\cdots a_{j}))^{\omega}\in E(M\times N)
. By definition of

and

, we have
\gamma(x)(g,e)=\gamma(x)
and

. Clearly,

is a nonempty prefix of

is
\eta

-guarded

-guarded

(2)

(3)

\widebar{q}=(\widebar{p}_{1},f_{1},\widebar{q}_{1},f_{1},\ \dots\ ,\widebar{p}_{\ell},f_{\ell},\widebar{q}_{\ell},f_{\ell},\widebar{p}_{\ell+1})

. It can be verified that

Fact 21
.

Let
\widebar{p},\widebar{q}\in Q
such that
\widebar{p}\preceq\widebar{q}
. Then,
\uclos_{*}\widebar{q}\subseteq\uclos_{*}\widebar{p}

Let
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
,
\widebar{p}_{1},\dots,\widebar{p}_{\ell+1}\in Q
,
\widebar{q}_{1},\dots,\widebar{q}_{\ell}\in Q
and

be idempotents of

, as in the definition of
\widebar{p}\preceq\widebar{q}
. Now consider

. By definition, this yields

for

3.2.
Concatenation hierarchies

Let
\mathscr{C}
be a prevariety. For all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we define two classes
\textup{Pol}_{n}(\mathscr{C})
and
\textup{BPol}_{n}(\mathscr{C})
. We let
\textup{Pol}_{0}(\mathscr{C})\!=\!\textup{BPol}_{0}(\mathscr{C})\!=\!\mathscr{C}
. Then, for every
n\geq 1
, we define inductively
\textup{Pol}_{n}(\mathscr{C})=\textup{Pol}(\textup{BPol}_{n-1}(\mathscr{C}))
and
\textup{BPol}_{n}(\mathscr{C})=\textup{BPol}(\textup{BPol}_{n-1}(\mathscr{C}))=\textup{Bool}(\textup{Pol}_{n}(\mathscr{C}))
. We say that the class
\textup{BPol}_{n}(\mathscr{C})
is
level
n
in the concatenation hierarchy of basis
\mathscr{C}

. Note that we have
\textup{BPol}_{n}(\mathscr{C})\subseteq\textup{Pol}_{n+1}(\mathscr{C})\subseteq\textup{BPol}_{n+1}(\mathscr{C})
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Remark 23
.

We are mainly interested in “full” levels
\textup{BPol}_{n}(\mathscr{C})
. However, the classes
\textup{Pol}_{n}(\mathscr{C})
are important tools in the paper.

Historically, the first concatenation hierarchy is the dot-depth (Brzozowski, Cohen
[
10
]
) of basis
\textup{DD}=\{\emptyset,\{\varepsilon\},A^{+},A^{*}\}
. Thomas
[
59
]
proved that the dot-depth corresponds exactly to the
quantifier alternation hierarchy
of first-order logic with the linear order and the successor (
\textup{FO}(<,+1)
). More precisely,
\textup{Pol}_{n}(\textup{DD})=\Sigma_{n}(<,+1)
and
\textup{BPol}_{n}(\textup{DD})=\mathscr{B}\Sigma_{n}(<,+1)
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. This logical characterization extends to arbitrary concatenation hierarchies
[
39
]
: for every prevariety
\mathscr{C}
, there exists a set
\mathbb{I}_{\mathscr{C}}
of first-order predicates such that
\textup{Pol}_{n}(\mathscr{C})=\Sigma_{n}(\mathbb{I}_{\mathscr{C}})
and
\textup{BPol}_{n}(\mathscr{C})=\mathscr{B}\Sigma_{n}(\mathbb{I}_{\mathscr{C}})
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Group based hierarchies.

Prevarieties
consisting of group languages are prominent bases.
Group languages
are those that can be recognized by a morphism into a finite
group
. A
group prevariety
is a prevariety
\mathscr{G}
that contains only group languages. We use bases that are group prevarieties as well as slight extensions thereof. For each class
\mathscr{G}
, we let
\mathscr{G}^{+}
be the class consisting of all languages
\{\varepsilon\}\cup L
and
A^{+}\cap L
for
L\in\mathscr{G}
. One may verify that if
\mathscr{G}
is a prevariety, then so is
\mathscr{G}^{+}
. Also,
\mathscr{G}^{+}
contains the languages
\{\varepsilon\}
and
A^{+}
.

Let us present three standard group prevarieties. The trivial prevariety
\textup{ST}=\{\emptyset,A^{*}\}
yields a hierarchy introduced by Straubing
[
49
]
and Thérien
[
56
]
. It characterizes the alternation hierarchy of first-order logic equipped with only the linear order (
\textup{FO}(<)
). Also,
\textup{ST}^{+}=\textup{DD}
: the hierarchy of basis
\textup{ST}^{+}
is the dot-depth. Another example is the prevariety
MOD
of
modulo languages
(membership of a word in the language depends only on its length modulo some fixed integer). The hierarchies of bases
MOD
and
\textup{MOD}^{+}
characterize the alternation hierarchies of
\textup{FO}(<,MOD)
and
\textup{FO}(<,+1,MOD)
, where “
MOD
” is the set of modular predicates, see
e.g.
,
[
12
]
. Finally, let us mention the
group hierarchy
of Margolis and Pin
[
23
]
. Its basis is the class
GR
of
all
group languages.

There are many papers investigating membership, separation and covering for particular hierarchies of this kind (see
e.g.
,
[
47
,
20
,
16
,
33
,
12
,
14
,
40
,
63
]
). Yet, proving that they are decidable for all levels remains a longstanding open question in automata theory. In the paper, we shall use the following generic theorem of
[
41
,
35
]
, which applies to level one in an arbitrary group based hierarchy.

Theorem 24
.

Let
\mathscr{G}
be a group prevariety with decidable separation. Then,
\textup{BPol}(\mathscr{G})
and
\textup{BPol}(\mathscr{G}^{+})
have decidable covering.

This result can be pushed further for the Straubing-Thérien hierarchy and the dot-depth. The following is proved in
[
42
]
.

Theorem 25
.

\textup{BPol}_{2}(\textup{ST})
and
\textup{BPol}_{2}(\textup{DD})
have decidable covering.

We prove a generic characterization of
BPol
. It implies that if a
non-zero
full level has decidable
covering
, then the next full level has decidable
membership
. By Theorem
24
, it follows that if
\mathscr{G}
is a group prevariety with decidable separation, then
\textup{BPol}_{2}(\mathscr{G})
and
\textup{BPol}_{2}(\mathscr{G}^{+})
have decidable membership. Also, Theorem
25
implies that
\textup{BPol}_{3}(\textup{ST})
and
\textup{BPol}_{3}(\textup{DD})
have decidable membership.

\mathscr{C}
-pairs,

We present tools used in our characterization. With a class

and a morphism

\mathscr{C}
-

in

, the
\mathscr{C}
-

in

and the

4.1.
The
\mathscr{C}
-pairs associated to a morphism

Let
\mathscr{C}
be a class and
\alpha:A^{*}\to M
be a morphism. We say that

is a
\mathscr{C}
-pair for
\alpha
when
\alpha^{-1}(s)
is
not

\mathscr{C}
-separable from
\alpha^{-1}(t)
. When
\alpha
is understood, we simply say that
(s,t)
is a
\mathscr{C}
-pair. Since the set of
\mathscr{C}
-pairs is a subset of

, it is finite. Also, having a
\mathscr{C}
-separation algorithm in hand suffices to compute all
\mathscr{C}
-pairs associated with an input morphism.

Lemma 26
.

Let
\mathscr{C}
a class with decidable separation. Given a morphism
\alpha:A^{*}\to M
, one can compute all
\mathscr{C}
-pairs for
\alpha
.

The
\mathscr{C}
-pairs for
\alpha
define a
binary relation
on
M
, which is reflexive when
\alpha
is surjective (this is always the case in the paper). Also, it is symmetric if
\mathscr{C}
is closed under complement. On the other hand, the
\mathscr{C}
-pairs relation is
not
transitive in general.

We turn to a simple but crucial lemma. When
\mathscr{C}
is a
positive prevariety
, it characterizes the
\mathscr{C}
-pairs in terms of
\mathscr{C}
-morphisms. Let
\eta:A^{*}\to(N,\leq)
and
\alpha:A^{*}\to M
be two morphisms. We say that
(s,t)\in M^{2}
is an
\eta
-pair
for
\alpha
when there exist
u,v\in A^{*}
such that
\eta(u)\leq\eta(v)
,
s=\alpha(u)
and
t=\alpha(v)
. The statement is as follows.

Lemma 27
.

Let
\mathscr{C}
be a positive prevariety and let
\alpha:A^{*}\to M
be a morphism. For every
\mathscr{C}
-morphism
\eta:A^{*}\to(N,\leq)
, each
\mathscr{C}
-pair for
\alpha
is also an
\eta
-pair. Moreover, there exists a
\mathscr{C}
-morphism
\eta:A^{*}\to(N,\leq)
such that the
\mathscr{C}
-pairs for
\alpha
are exactly the
\eta
-pairs.

Proof.

For the first assertion, let
\eta:A^{*}\to(N,\leq)
be a
\mathscr{C}
-morphism and let
(s,t)\in M^{2}
be a
\mathscr{C}
-pair for
\alpha
. Let
F\subseteq N
be the set of all elements
r\in N
such that
\eta(u)\leq r
for some
u\in\alpha^{-1}(s)
. By definition,
F
is an upper set for the ordering
\leq
on
N
. Hence, we have
\eta^{-1}(F)\in\mathscr{C}(A)
since
\eta
is a
\mathscr{C}
-morphism. Moreover, it is immediate from the definition of

that
\alpha^{-1}(s)\subseteq\eta^{-1}(F)
. Since
(s,t)
is a
\mathscr{C}
-pair (which means that
\alpha^{-1}(s)
cannot be separated from
\alpha^{-1}(t)
by a language of
\mathscr{C}
), it follows that
\eta^{-1}(F)\cap\alpha^{-1}(t)\neq\emptyset
. This yields

such that
\eta(v)\in F
and
\alpha(v)=t
. Finally, the definition of
F
yields
u\in A^{*}
such that
\eta(u)\leq\eta(v)
and
\alpha(u)=s
, concluding the proof of the first assertion.

We turn to the second assertion. Let
P\subseteq M^{2}
be the set of all pairs
(s,t)\in M^{2}
that are
not

\mathscr{C}
-pairs. For each
(s,t)\in P
, there exists
K_{s,t}\in\mathscr{C}(A)
which separates
\alpha^{-1}(s)
from
\alpha^{-1}(t)
. Proposition
10
yields a
\mathscr{C}
-morphism
\eta:A^{*}\to(N,\leq)
such that every language

for
(s,t)\in P
is recognized by
\eta
. It remains to prove that for every
u,v\in A^{*}
, if
\eta(u)\leq\eta(v)
, then
(\alpha(u),\alpha(v))
is a
\mathscr{C}
-pair. We prove the contrapositive. Assuming that
(\alpha(u),\alpha(v))
is a
not
a
\mathscr{C}
-pair, we show that
\eta(u)\not\leq\eta(v)
. By hypothesis,
(\alpha(u),\alpha(v))\not\in P
which means that

is defined and separates
\alpha^{-1}(\alpha(u))
from
\alpha^{-1}(\alpha(v))
. Thus,

and

. Since

is recognized by

, this implies
\eta(u)\not\leq\eta(v)
, concluding the proof.

r=\gamma(v)
. By definition,
(q,r)\in Q^{2}
is an
\eta
-pair for
\gamma
, which means that it is a
\mathscr{C}
-pair by definition of
\eta
. Since
\gamma
is a
\textup{Pol}(\mathscr{C})
-morphism, Theorem
29
yields
q^{\omega+1}\leq q^{\omega}rq^{\omega}
,
i.e.
,
\gamma(u^{k+1})\leq\gamma(u^{k}vu^{k})
for
k=\omega(Q)
. By definition of
\gamma
, it follows that
(\alpha(u^{k+1}),\alpha(u^{k}vu^{k}))
is a
\textup{Pol}(\mathscr{C})
-pair. Since
e\in E(M)
, this exactly says that
(e,ese)
is a
\textup{Pol}(\mathscr{C})
-pair for
\alpha
, as desired.
∎

4.2.
The
\mathscr{C}
-sets associated to a morphism

Roughly speaking,
\mathscr{C}
-sets are the generalization of
\mathscr{C}
-pairs adapted to covering. We only use
\mathscr{C}
-sets when
\mathscr{C}
is a
Boolean algebra
. In this case, by Lemma
7
, we may consider the variant in which the language that needs to be covered is
A^{*}
.

Consider a class
\mathscr{C}
and a morphism
\alpha:A^{*}\to M
. We say that a subset
T\subseteq M
is a
\mathscr{C}
-set
for
\alpha
if and only if the set
\{\alpha^{-1}(t)\mid t\in T\}
is
not

\mathscr{C}
-coverable. As for
\mathscr{C}
-pairs, the set of all
\mathscr{C}
-sets for
\alpha
is finite: it is a subset of
2^{M}
. Furthermore, having a
\mathscr{C}
-covering algorithm in hand is clearly enough to compute all
\mathscr{C}
-sets for an input morphism
\alpha
.

Lemma 31
.

Let
\mathscr{C}
be a class with decidable covering. Given a morphism
\alpha:A^{*}\to M
, one can compute all
\mathscr{C}
-sets for
\alpha
.

Let us present a few properties. First, we have the following fact which is immediate from the definition of covering.

Fact 32
.

Let
\mathscr{C}
be a class,
\alpha:A^{*}\to M
be a morphism and
T\subseteq M
be a
\mathscr{C}
-set for
\alpha
. Every
S\subseteq T
is also a
\mathscr{C}
-set for
\alpha
.

When
\mathscr{C}
is a prevariety, we can characterize the
\mathscr{C}
-sets associated to a morphism with a single
\mathscr{C}
-morphism. Consider two morphisms
\eta:A^{*}\to N
and
\alpha:A^{*}\to M
. We say that
T\subseteq M
is an
\eta
-set
for
\alpha
to indicate that there exists
F\subseteq A^{*}
such that
\eta(w)=\eta(w^{\prime})
for every
w,w^{\prime}\in F
and
T=\alpha(F)
.

Lemma 33
.

Let
\mathscr{C}
be a prevariety and
\alpha:A^{*}\to M
be a morphism. For every
\mathscr{C}
-morphism
\eta:A^{*}\to N
, each
\mathscr{C}
-set for
\alpha
is also an
\eta
-set. There exists a
\mathscr{C}
-morphism
\eta:A^{*}\to N
such that the
\mathscr{C}
-sets for
\alpha
are exactly the
\eta
-sets.

Proof.

For the first assertion, we fix a
\mathscr{C}
-set
T\subseteq M
and a
\mathscr{C}
-morphism
\eta:A^{*}\to N
. We prove that
T
is an
\eta
-set. We define
\mathbf{K}=\{\eta^{-1}(s)\mid r\in N\}
. Since
\eta
is a
\mathscr{C}
-morphism,
\mathbf{K}
is a
\mathscr{C}
-cover of
A^{*}
. Hence, since
T
is a
\mathscr{C}
-set (which means that
\{\alpha^{-1}(t)\mid t\in T\}
is
not

\mathscr{C}
-coverable), there exists
K\in\mathbf{K}
such that
K\cap\alpha^{-1}(t)\neq\emptyset
for every
t\in T
. Thus, for each
t\in T
, we get
w_{t}\in K\cap\alpha^{-1}(t)
. Let
F=\{w_{t}\mid t\in T\}
. By definition
T=\alpha(F)
. Finally, by definition of
\mathbf{K}
, we have
K=\eta^{-1}(r)
for some
r\in N
. Hence,
\eta(w)=r
for every
w\in F
, which completes the proof of the first assertion.

We turn to the second assertion. We first introduce some terminology: given two sets of languages
\mathbf{K}
and
\mathbf{L}
, we say that
\mathbf{K}
is
separating
for
\mathbf{L}
when for every
K\in\mathbf{K}
, there exists
L\in\mathbf{L}
such that
K\cap L=\emptyset
. Note that by definition,
\mathbf{L}
is
\mathscr{C}
-coverable if and only if there exists a
\mathscr{C}
-cover of
A^{*}
that is separating for
\mathbf{L}
.

Let now
P\subseteq 2^{M}
be the set of all subsets
T
of
M
that are
not

\mathscr{C}
-sets. By definition, for every
T\in P
, there exists a
\mathscr{C}
-cover
\mathbf{K}_{T}
of
A^{*}
that is separating for
\{\alpha^{-1}(t)\mid t\in T\}
. Proposition
10
yields a
\mathscr{C}
-morphism
\eta:A^{*}\to N
recognizing all languages
K\in\mathbf{K}_{T}
, for
T\in P
. It remains to prove that if
T\subseteq M
is an
\eta
-set for
\alpha
, then it is a
\mathscr{C}
-set for
\alpha
(the converse implication is immediate from the first assertion). By contradiction, assume that
T
is
not
a
\mathscr{C}
-set,
i.e.
, that
T\in P
. By definition of covering, it follows that
T\neq\emptyset
. Also, since
T\in P
, there exists a
\mathscr{C}
-cover
\mathbf{K}_{T}
of
A^{*}
that is separating for
\{\alpha^{-1}(t)\mid t\in T\}
. Moreover, since
T
is an
\eta
-set, we get
F\subseteq A^{*}
such that
\eta(w)=\eta(w^{\prime})
for every
w,w^{\prime}\in F
and
T=\alpha(F)
. Since
T\neq\emptyset
, we have
F\neq\emptyset
as well. Since
\mathbf{K}_{T}
is a cover of
A^{*}
, we get
K\in\mathbf{K}_{T}
such that
K\cap F\neq\emptyset
. Thus, since
K
is recognized by
\eta
and
\eta(w)=\eta(w^{\prime})
for every
w,w^{\prime}\in F
, it follows that
F\subseteq K
. Since
T=\alpha(F)
, we obtain
K\cap\alpha^{-1}(t)\neq\emptyset
for every
t\in T
. It follows that
\mathbf{K}_{T}
is
not
separating for
\{\alpha^{-1}(t)\mid t\in T\}
, contradicting our hypothesis.
∎

4.3.
The
\mathscr{C}
-swaps associated to a morphism

This last relation is
ad hoc
. It is not based on a “Boolean problem” such as separation or covering. We directly use
\mathscr{C}
-morphisms.

Given two morphisms
\alpha:A^{*}\to M
and
\eta:A^{*}\to N
, we define tuples
(q,r,s,t,e,f)\in M^{6}
called the
\eta
-swaps for
\alpha
. When using these objects later, the elements
e,f\in M
will be idempotents (hence the notation). However, this is not required for the definition. We say that
(q,r,s,t,e,f)\in M^{6}
is an
\eta
-swap for
\alpha
when there exist words
u_{q}\in\alpha^{-1}(q)
,
u_{r}\in\alpha^{-1}(r)
,
u_{s}\in\alpha^{-1}(s)
,
u_{t}\in\alpha^{-1}(t)
,
u_{e}\in\alpha^{-1}(e)
and
u_{f}\in\alpha^{-1}(f)
satisfying the two following conditions:

(1)

The elements
\eta(u_{e})
and
\eta(u_{f})
are idempotents of
N
.

(2)

We have
\eta(u_{r}u_{e}u_{s})=\eta(u_{f})
and
\eta(u_{q}u_{f}u_{t})=\eta(u_{e})
.

Consider a prevariety
\mathscr{C}
. We define the
\mathscr{C}
-swaps for
\alpha
as the tuples
(q,r,s,t,e,f)\in M^{6}
that are
\eta
-swaps for
every
\mathscr{C}
-morphism
\eta:A^{*}\to N
. We conclude this section with a technical result.

Lemma 34
.

Let
\mathscr{C}
be a prevariety and let
\alpha:A^{*}\to M
be a morphism. There xs a
\mathscr{C}
-morphism
\eta:A^{*}\to N
such that the
\mathscr{C}
-swaps for
\alpha
are exactly the
\eta
-swaps and the
\mathscr{C}
-pairs for
\alpha
are exactly the
\eta
-pairs.

Proof.

Let
P\subseteq M^{6}
be the set of all tuples that are
not
a
\mathscr{C}
-swap and
\{\widebar{p}_{1},\dots,\widebar{p}_{k}\}=P
. By definition, we know that for each
i\leq k
, there exists a
\mathscr{C}
-morphism
\eta_{i}:A^{*}\to N_{i}
such that
\widebar{p}_{i}\in M^{6}
is not an
\eta_{i}
-swap. Moreover, Lemma
27
yields a
\mathscr{C}
-morphism
\gamma:A^{*}\to Q
such that the
\mathscr{C}
-pairs are exactly the
\gamma
-pairs. Consider the monoid
N_{1}\times\cdots N_{k}\times Q
equipped with the componentwise multiplication. Let now
\beta:A^{*}\to N_{1}\times\cdots N_{k}\times Q
be the morphism defined by
\beta(w)=(\eta_{1}(w),\dots,\eta_{k}(w),\gamma(w))
for
w\in A^{*}
. Finally, let
\eta:A^{*}\to N
be the surjective restriction of
\beta
. One may now verify that
\eta
satisfies the desired properties.
∎

5.
General characterization

In this section, we characterize the
\textup{BPol}(\mathscr{C})
-morphisms.

Theorem 35
.

Let
\mathscr{C}
be a prevariety and let
\alpha:A^{*}\to M
be a surjective morphism. Then,
\alpha
is a
\textup{BPol}(\mathscr{C})
-morphism if and only if the two following equations hold:

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{c}(eset)^{\omega+1}=(eset)^{\omega}et(eset)^{\omega}\text{ for every $t\in M$}\\
\text{and every $\mathscr{C}$-pair $(e,s)\in E(M)\times M$}.\end{array}

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{c}(eqfre)^{\omega}(esfte)^{\omega}=(eqfre)^{\omega}qft(esfte)^{\omega}\text{ for every}\\
\text{$\mathscr{C}$-swap $(q,r,s,t,e,f)\in M^{6}$ such that $e,f\in E(M)$}.\end{array}

Theorem
35
does
not
have immediate general consequences for
\textup{BPol}(\mathscr{C})
-membership. It implies that the problem is decidable when both
\mathscr{C}
-pairs and
\mathscr{C}
-swaps can be computed. On the one hand, the calculation of
\mathscr{C}
-pairs boils down to
\mathscr{C}
-separation. On the other hand, we do not know whether the computation of
\mathscr{C}
-swaps reduces to a natural problem. This is why we cannot deduce a general corollary from Theorem
35
. Yet, we prove in Section
6
that if
\mathscr{C}
is itself of the form
\textup{BPol}(\mathscr{D})
for a prevariety
\mathscr{D}
, then (
35
) is equivalent to an equation depending on
\mathscr{C}
-sets
only (which can be computed when
\mathscr{C}
-covering is decidable, by Lemma
31
).

Remark 36
.

Equation (
35
) was previously used in
[
43
]
: alone, it characterizes the larger class
\textup{Pol}_{2}(\mathscr{C})\cap\textit{co{-}}\!\textup{Pol}_{2}(\mathscr{C})
consisting of all languages
L
such that
L\in\textup{Pol}_{2}(\mathscr{C})
and
A^{*}\setminus L\in\textup{Pol}_{2}(\mathscr{C})
.
The inclusion
\textup{BPol}(\mathscr{C})\subseteq\textup{Pol}_{2}(\mathscr{C})\cap\textit{co{-}}\!\textup{Pol}_{2}(\mathscr{C})
implies that all
\textup{BPol}(\mathscr{C})
-morphisms indeed satisfy (
35
). We shall give a direct proof in the paper.

5.1.
A first application: the class
\textup{BPol}(\textup{AT})

We may already get an effective characterization for a key class: level
two
in the Straubing-Thérien hierarchy (
\textup{BPol}_{2}(\textup{ST})
). Obtaining such a characterization remained an important open problem for many years until it was finally solved in
[
36
]
. Theorem
35
yields a new simpler characterization of
\textup{BPol}_{2}(\textup{ST})
. Let
AT
be the class of
alphabet testable languages
: the Boolean combinations of languages
A^{*}aA^{*}
where
a\in A
is a letter. Pin and Straubing
[
31
]
proved that
\textup{BPol}_{2}(\textup{ST})=\textup{BPol}(\textup{AT})
(see also
[
39
]
for a more recent proof). Hence, we apply Theorem
35
in the special case when
\mathscr{C}=\textup{AT}
.

For
w\in A^{*}
, let
\mathit{alph}(w)\subseteq A
be the set of all letters occurring in
w
(
i.e.
, the least
B\subseteq A
such that
w\in B^{*}
). It is folklore and simple to verify that a morphism
\eta:A^{*}\to N
is an
AT
-morphism if and only if
\mathit{alph}(u)=\mathit{alph}(v)\Rightarrow\eta(u)=\eta(v)
for all
u,v\in A^{*}
. With this result in hand, one can check that for
\mathscr{C}
=
AT
, Equation (
35
) is equivalent to Equation (
37
) below, which uses
AT
-sets instead of
AT
-swaps. More precisely, Theorem
35
yields the following corollary.

Corollary 37
.

Let
\alpha:A^{*}\to M
be a surjective morphism. Then,
\alpha
is a
\textup{BPol}(\textup{AT})
-morphism if and only if the following equations hold:

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{c}(eset)^{\omega+1}=(eset)^{\omega}et(eset)^{\omega}\text{ for every $t\in M$}\\
\text{and every $\textup{AT}$-pair $(e,s)\in E(M)\times M$}.\end{array}

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{c}(eqfre)^{\omega}(esfte)^{\omega}=(eqfre)^{\omega}qft(esfte)^{\omega}\text{ for every}\\
\text{$\textup{AT}$-set $\{q,r,s,t,e,f\}\subseteq M$ such that $e,f\in E(M)$}.\end{array}

Thus,
\textup{BPol}(\textup{AT})
-membership boils down to
AT
-covering, which suffices to compute
AT
-pairs and
AT
-sets. Deciding
AT
-covering is simple: a finite set of languages
\mathbf{L}
is
not

AT
-coverable if and only if there is a sub-alphabet
B\subseteq A
such that each
L\in\mathbf{L}
contains a word
w
satisfying
\mathit{alph}(w)=B
. Hence, the class
\textup{BPol}_{2}(\textup{ST})=\textup{BPol}(\textup{AT})
is decidable. This reproves the result of
[
36
]
.

Remark 38
.

Interestingly, the properties in Corollary
37
appeared in previous papers (though precisely establishing the link involves quite a bit of technical work). Equation (
37
) is equivalent to a property that Pin and Weil
[
33
,
32
]
conjectured to be a characterization of
\textup{BPol}_{2}(\textup{ST})
. Equation (
37
) is equivalent to an upper bound of
\textup{BPol}_{2}(\textup{ST})
proved by Almeida and Klíma
[
4
]
(the link is made with Lemma
39
below which reformulates (
37
)). Actually, it was conjectured in
[
4
]
that the two put together characterize
\textup{BPol}_{2}(\textup{ST})
(a conjecture proved by Corollary
37
).

We now turn to the proof of Theorem
35
.

5.2.
Proof of Theorem
35
: “only if” direction

Assume that
\alpha
is a
\textup{BPol}(\mathscr{C})
-morphism. We show that it satisfies (
35
) and (
35
). That it satisfies (
35
) follows from
[

\textup{Pol}_{2}(\mathscr{C})\cap\textup{co-}\textup{Pol}_{2}(\mathscr{C})

and

\textup{Pol}_{2}(\mathscr{C})

\textup{BPol}(\mathscr{C})\subseteq\textup{Pol}_{2}(\mathscr{C})\cap\textup{co-}\textup{Pol}_{2}(\mathscr{C})

Since
\mathscr{C}
is a prevariety,
\textup{Pol}(\mathscr{C})
is a positive prevariety by Theorem

. Therefore, we may apply Lemma

\textup{Pol}(\mathscr{C})

\mathscr{C}

\gamma

-pairs for
\gamma

Equation (

).
We fix a
\mathscr{C}

for

and

5.3.
Proof of Theorem

Proof: main argument

Proposition 45

For every
\mathrel{\mathscr{J}}

-cover of
\eta^{-1}(J)

We first explain how to conclude the proof using Proposition

For each
\mathrel{\mathscr{J}}

We now focus on the proof of Proposition

\mathrel{\mathscr{J}}
-class of

of

as the number of
\mathrel{\mathscr{J}}
-classes

of

(2)

q=s_{1}es_{2}

For

6.
Handling full levels above two

We specialize Theorem

to characterize the classes
\textup{BPol}(\mathscr{C})
when
\mathscr{C}
is itself of the form
\mathscr{C}=\textup{BPol}(\mathscr{D})
. More precisely, we prove that in this case, Equation (

) (which is based on
\mathscr{C}
-swaps) is equivalent to a property based on
\mathscr{C}
-

Proposition 56
.

Let
\mathscr{D}
be a prevariety and
\mathscr{C}=\textup{BPol}(\mathscr{D})
. Let
\alpha:A^{*}\to M
be a surjective morphism. Then,
\alpha
satisfies (
35
) if and only if it satisfies the following condition:

(11)

\begin{array}[]{c}(eqfre)^{\omega}(esfte)^{\omega}=(eqfre)^{\omega}qft(esfte)^{\omega}\text{ for every}\\
\text{$\mathscr{C}$-set $\{q,r,s,t,e,f\}\subseteq M$ such that $e,f\in E(M)$}.\end{array}

Before we prove Proposition
56
, let us combine it with Theorem
35
. This yields the following theorem.

Theorem 57
.

Let
\mathscr{D}
be a prevariety, let
\mathscr{C}=\textup{BPol}(\mathscr{D})
and let
\alpha:A^{*}\to M
be a surjective morphism. Then,
\alpha
is a
\textup{BPol}(\mathscr{C})
-morphism if and only if it satisfies the two following equations:

(
35
)

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{c}(eset)^{\omega+1}=(eset)^{\omega}et(eset)^{\omega}\text{ for every $t\in M$}\\
\text{and every $\mathscr{C}$-pair $(e,s)\in E(M)\times M$}.\end{array}

(
11
)

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{c}(eqfre)^{\omega}(esfte)^{\omega}=(eqfre)^{\omega}qft(esfte)^{\omega}\text{ for every}\\
\text{$\mathscr{C}$-set $\{q,r,s,t,e,f\}\subseteq M$ such that $e,f\in E(M)$}.\end{array}

Given a prevariety
\mathscr{C}
with decidable
covering
, Lemmas
26
and
31
imply that the
\mathscr{C}
-pairs and the
\mathscr{C}
-sets associated to an input morphism can be computed. Hence, one may decide (
35
) and (
11
) in this case. We get the following corollary.

Corollary 58
.

Let
\mathscr{D}
be a prevariety and let
\mathscr{C}=\textup{BPol}(\mathscr{D})
. If
\mathscr{C}
-covering is decidable, then so is
\textup{BPol}(\mathscr{C})
-membership.

Corollary
58
implies that given a concatenation hierarchy, if a
non-zero full
level has decidable
covering
, then the next full level has decidable
membership
. Together with Theorem
24
(which is proved in
[
41
,
35
]
), this yields the following.

Corollary 59
.

If
\mathscr{G}
is a group prevariety with decidable separation, the classes
\textup{BPol}_{2}(\mathscr{G})
and
\textup{BPol}_{2}(\mathscr{G}^{+})
have decidable membership.

Also, applying Theorem
25
(which is proved in
[
42
]
) yields the decidability of membership for the levels
three
in both the Straubing-Thérien hierarchy and the dot-depth.

Corollary 60
.

The classes
\textup{BPol}_{3}(\textup{ST})
and
\textup{BPol}_{3}(\textup{DD})
have decidable membership.

Remark 61

\eta(w_{k,h-1})p_{t}g_{e}\leq\eta(v_{k,h})

\beta(u_{f})\in E(R)
and
\beta(u_{f})=\beta(u_{r}u_{e}u_{s})
by hypothesis. A symmetrical argument yields words
w_{1,h},\dots,w_{k,h}
such that
u_{f}=w_{1,h}\cdots w_{k,h}
,
g_{f}p_{r}\eta(v_{1,h})\leq\eta(w_{1,h})
,
\eta(v_{i,h})\leq\eta(w_{i,h})
for
2\leq i<k
and
\eta(v_{k,h})p_{s}g_{f}\leq\eta(w_{k,h})
. This concludes the construction of the
2k
words satisfying the desired construction.

Let
d=\omega(M)\times\omega(N)
. Since
N
is finite, there are two indices
m<m^{\prime}
and
\eta(w_{i,dm})=\eta(w_{i,dm^{\prime}})
for
2\leq i<k
. We fix these two numbers
m,m^{\prime}\geq 1
. Since
k-2=|M|^{2}\times|N|^{2}
, the pigeonhole principle yields
1\leq i<j<k
such that,

\begin{array}[]{lll}\alpha(w_{2,dm}\cdots w_{i,dm})&=&\alpha(w_{2,dm}\cdots w_{j,dm}),\\
\alpha(v_{2,dm+1}\cdots v_{i,dm+1})&=&\alpha(v_{2,dm+1}\cdots v_{j,dm+1}),\\
\eta(w_{2,dm}\cdots w_{i,dm})&=&\eta(w_{2,dm}\cdots w_{j,dm}),\\
\eta(v_{2,dm+1}\cdots v_{i,dm+1})&=&\eta(v_{2,dm+1}\cdots v_{j,dm+1}).\end{array}

We now define,

\begin{array}[]{ll}x_{e}=v_{1,dm+1}\cdots v_{i,dm+1}&x_{f}=w_{1,dm}\cdots w_{i,dm},\\
y_{e}=(v_{i+1,dm+1}\cdots v_{j,dm+1})^{d}&y_{f}=(w_{i+1,dm}\cdots w_{j,dm})^{d},\\
z_{e}=v_{j+1,dm+1}\cdots v_{k,dm+1}&z_{f}=w_{j+1,dm}\cdots w_{k,dm}.\end{array}

It remains to prove that the conditions in the lemma are satisfied. For the first two, we have
\alpha(y_{e}),\alpha(y_{f})\in E(M)
by definition of
d
. In addition, by definition of
i
and
j
, we know that we have

\alpha(x_{e}y_{e}z_{e})=\alpha(v_{1,dm+1}\cdots v_{k,dm+1})=\alpha(u_{e})=e,

as well as

\alpha(x_{f}y_{f}z_{f})=\alpha(w_{1,dm}\cdots w_{k,dm})=\alpha(u_{f})=f.

Furthermore,
\eta(x_{e}y_{e}z_{e})=\eta(u_{e})=g_{e}
and
\eta(x_{f}y_{f}z_{f})=\eta(u_{f})=g_{f}
.

We turn to the third assertion. Define
g^{\prime}=\eta(y_{e})
. By choice of
d
, we have
g^{\prime}\in E(N)
. Moreover, for
2\leq\ell<k
, we have
\eta(w_{\ell,0})\leq\eta(w_{\ell,dm})\leq\eta(v_{\ell,dm+1})\leq\eta(w_{\ell,dm^{\prime}})
. Hence, since
w_{i,0}=u_{f}
,
g_{f}=\eta(u_{f})
and
\eta(w_{\ell,dm})=\eta(w_{\ell,dm^{\prime}})
for
2\leq\ell<k
, we obtain that
g_{f}\leq\eta(w_{\ell,dm})=\eta(v_{\ell,dm+1})
. One can multiply these inequalities for
i+1\leq\ell\leq j
. Since
g_{f}\in E(N)
, this yields
g_{f}\leq\eta(y_{e})=\eta(y_{f})
by definition. Hence,
g^{\prime}=\eta(y_{e})=\eta(y_{f})
and
g_{f}\leq g^{\prime}
. We get
g^{\prime}=g^{\prime}g_{f}g^{\prime}
from Lemma
62
.

For the remaining assertions, we only show that
g_{f}\leq\eta(x_{f})
and
g_{e}p_{q}g_{f}\leq\eta(x_{e})
. The proof of the other inequalities is symmetrical. By definition, we have
w_{1,0}=u_{f}
,
g_{e}p_{q}\eta(w_{1,h-1})\leq\eta(v_{1,h})
and
g_{f}p_{r}\eta(v_{1,h})\leq\eta(w_{1,h})
for all
h\geq 1
. Since
g_{\smash{f}}\in E(N)
, we get
g_{e}p_{q}(g_{f}p_{r}g_{e}p_{q}g_{f})^{h-1}\leq\eta(v_{1,h})
and
(g_{f}p_{r}g_{e}p_{q}g_{f})^{h}\leq\eta(w_{1,h})
by a simple induction. Also,
g_{f}=(g_{f}p_{r}g_{e}p_{q}g_{f})^{\omega}
by Fact
64
. Thus, we have
g_{f}\leq\eta(w_{1,dm})
and
g_{e}p_{q}g_{f}\leq\eta(v_{1,dm+1})
by definition of
d
. Finally, we have
g_{f}\leq\eta(w_{\ell,dm})=\eta(v_{\ell,dm+1})
for
2\leq i<k
. Hence,
g_{f}\leq\eta(x_{f})
and
g_{e}p_{q}g_{f}\leq\eta(x_{e})
.
∎

We fix the words
x_{e},y_{e},z_{e},x_{f},y_{f},z_{f}\in A^{*}
and the element
g^{\prime}\in E(N)
given by Lemma
65
. Let
h=\omega(M)\times\omega(N)
. We let,

\begin{array}[]{ll}y_{q}=y_{e}z_{e}u_{q}x_{f}y_{f},&y_{r}=y_{f}z_{f}(u_{r}u_{e}u_{q}u_{f})^{h-1}u_{r}x_{e}y_{e},\\
y_{t}=y_{f}z_{f}u_{t}x_{e}y_{e},&y_{s}=y_{e}z_{e}u_{s}(u_{f}u_{t}u_{e}u_{s})^{h-1}x_{f}y_{f}.\end{array}

Let
\hat{x}=\alpha(y_{x})
for
x\in\{q,r,s,t,e,f\}
. Recall that in order to apply Equation (
11
), we need to construct a
\mathscr{C}
-set. We will show below that
\{\hat{q},\hat{r},\hat{s},\hat{t},\hat{e},\hat{f}\}
is such a
\mathscr{C}
-set.
Note that
\hat{e},\hat{f}\in E(M)
by Lemma
65
. Since
\alpha(y_{f}),\alpha(y_{e})\in E(M)
and
\alpha(x_{f}y_{f}z_{f})=\alpha(u_{f})=f
by Lemma
65
, one can verify that,

\begin{array}[]{lll}\hat{e}\hat{q}\hat{f}\hat{r}\hat{e}&=&\alpha(y_{e}z_{e})qf(reqf)^{h-1}r\alpha(x_{e}y_{e}),\\
&=&\alpha(y_{e}z_{e})(qfre)^{h-1}qfr\alpha(x_{e}y_{e}).\end{array}

Symmetrically, one can verify that,

\begin{array}[]{lll}\hat{e}\hat{s}\hat{f}\hat{t}\hat{e}&=&\alpha(y_{e}z_{e})s(ftes)^{h-1}ft\alpha(x_{e}y_{e}),\\
&=&\alpha(y_{e}z_{e})sft(esft)^{h-1}\alpha(x_{e}y_{e}).\end{array}

Since
\alpha(x_{e}y_{e}z_{e})=e
by Lemma
65
, it follows that,

\alpha(x_{e})(\hat{e}\hat{q}\hat{f}\hat{r}\hat{e})^{\omega}(\hat{e}\hat{s}\hat{f}\hat{t}\hat{e})^{\omega}\alpha(z_{e})=(eqfre)^{\omega}(esfte)^{\omega}.

Moreover, the definitions yield
\hat{q}\hat{f}\hat{t}=\alpha(y_{e}z_{e})qft\alpha(x_{e}y_{e})
. Hence, using again the equality
\alpha(x_{e}y_{e}z_{e})=e
of Lemma
65
, we get,

\alpha(x_{e})(\hat{e}\hat{q}\hat{f}\hat{r}\hat{e})^{\omega}\hat{q}\hat{f}\hat{t}(\hat{e}\hat{s}\hat{f}\hat{t}\hat{e})^{\omega}\alpha(z_{e})=(eqfre)^{\omega}qft(esfte)^{\omega}.

We prove that
(\hat{e}\hat{q}\hat{f}\hat{r}\hat{e})^{\omega}(\hat{e}\hat{s}\hat{f}\hat{t}\hat{e})^{\omega}=(\hat{e}\hat{q}\hat{f}\hat{r}\hat{e})^{\omega}\hat{q}\hat{f}\hat{t}(\hat{e}\hat{s}\hat{f}\hat{t}\hat{e})^{\omega}
. In view of the above, this will imply that (
13
) holds as desired (namely that
(eqfre)^{\omega}(esfte)^{\omega}=(eqfre)^{\omega}qft(esfte)^{\omega}
). Since
\alpha
satisfies (
11
), it suffices to prove that
\{\hat{q},\hat{r},\hat{s},\hat{t},\hat{e},\hat{f}\}\subseteq M
is a
\mathscr{C}
-set. We show that
\{\hat{q},\hat{r},\hat{s},\hat{t},\hat{e},\hat{f}\}
is a subset of
\alpha(\eta^{-1}(g^{\prime}))
, which is a
\mathscr{C}
-set by Lemma
62
. Thus,
\{\hat{q},\hat{r},\hat{s},\hat{t},\hat{e},\hat{f}\}
is itself a
\mathscr{C}
-set by Fact
32
.

By definition, we have to prove that for
y\in\{y_{q},y_{r},y_{s},y_{t},y_{e},y_{f}\}
, we have
\eta(y)=g^{\prime}
. By Lemma
65
, we already know this for
y=y_{e}
and
y=y_{f}
. By symmetry, we only treat the cases
y=y_{q}
and
y=y_{r}
. It suffices to prove that
\eta(y)\mathrel{\mathscr{H}}g^{\prime}
and
g^{\prime}\leq\eta(y)
. Indeed, since
g^{\prime}\in E(N)
, Lemma
2
and
\eta(y)\mathrel{\mathscr{H}}g^{\prime}
imply
(\eta(y))^{\omega}=g^{\prime}
. Moreover,
g^{\prime}\leq\eta(y)
yields
g^{\prime}\leq(\eta(y))^{\omega-1}
. Multiplying by
\eta(y)
yields
g^{\prime}\eta(y)\leq(\eta(y))^{\omega}=g^{\prime}
. Since
g^{\prime}\eta(y)=\eta(y)
(by definition of
y_{q},y_{r}
as
\eta(y_{e})=\eta(y_{f})=g^{\prime}
), we get
\eta(y)\leq g^{\prime}
. Altogeter, we obtain
g^{\prime}=\eta(y)
, as desired.

We first show that
\eta(y_{q})\mathrel{\mathscr{H}}g^{\prime}
and
g^{\prime}\leq\eta(y_{q})
. We know that
y_{q}=y_{e}z_{e}u_{q}x_{f}y_{f}
and Lemma
65
yields
g_{f}\leq\eta(x_{f})
and
g_{f}p_{t}g_{e}\leq\eta(z_{e})
. We get
g^{\prime}g_{f}p_{t}g_{e}p_{q}g_{f}g^{\prime}\leq\eta(y_{q})
. Thus, since
g_{f}p_{t}g_{e}p_{q}g_{f}=g_{f}
by Fact
64
and
g^{\prime}g_{f}g^{\prime}=g^{\prime}
by Lemma
65
, we get
g^{\prime}\leq\eta(y_{q})
. Moreover, since
g^{\prime}=\eta(y_{e})=\eta(y_{f})
, it is clear that
\eta(y_{q})\leqslant_{\mathscr{H}}g^{\prime}
. Thus, we show that
g^{\prime}\leqslant_{\mathscr{J}}\eta(y_{q})
. This will imply
\eta(y_{q})\mathrel{\mathscr{H}}g^{\prime}
by Lemma
1
. By definition,
y_{q}=y_{e}z_{e}u_{q}x_{f}y_{f}
. Hence, Lemma
65
yields,

\eta(x_{e}y_{q}z_{f})=\eta(x_{e}(y_{e}z_{e}u_{q}x_{f}y_{f})z_{f})=\eta(u_{e}u_{q}u_{f})=g_{e}p_{q}g_{f}.

Since
g_{f}p_{t}g_{e}p_{q}g_{f}=g_{f}
by Fact
64
, we get
g_{f}\leqslant_{\mathscr{J}}\eta(y_{q})
. Also,
g^{\prime}\leqslant_{\mathscr{J}}g_{f}
since
g^{\prime}=g^{\prime}g_{f}g^{\prime}
. We get
g^{\prime}\leqslant_{\mathscr{J}}\eta(y_{q})
as desired.

We now prove that
\eta(y_{r})\mathrel{\mathscr{H}}g^{\prime}
and that
g^{\prime}\leq\eta(y_{r})
. We have
y_{r}=y_{f}z_{f}(u_{r}u_{e}u_{q}u_{f})^{h-1}u_{r}x_{e}y_{e}
. Lemma
65
yields
g_{f}\leq\eta(z_{f})
and
g_{e}p_{q}g_{f}\leq\eta(x_{e})
. Thus,
g^{\prime}(g_{f}p_{r}g_{e}p_{q}g_{f})^{h}g^{\prime}\leq\eta(y_{r})
. Since
h
is a multiple of
\omega(N)
, this yields
g^{\prime}g_{f}g^{\prime}\leq\eta(y_{r})
by Fact
64
. Thus,
g^{\prime}\leq\eta(y_{r})
by Lemma
65
. Also, the definition of
y_{r}
and the fact that
g^{\prime}=\eta(y_{e})=\eta(y_{f})
yield
\eta(y_{r})\leqslant_{\mathscr{H}}g^{\prime}
. Thus, it suffices to show that
g^{\prime}\leqslant_{\mathscr{J}}\eta(y_{r})
. This implies
\eta(y_{r})\mathrel{\mathscr{H}}g^{\prime}
by Lemma
1
. By definition,
y_{r}=y_{f}z_{f}(u_{r}u_{e}u_{q}u_{f})^{h-1}u_{r}x_{e}y_{e}
. Thus, Lemma
65
yields,

\eta(x_{f}y_{r}z_{e})=(g_{f}p_{r}g_{e}p_{q}g_{f})^{h}p_{r}g_{e}.

Moreover,
(g_{f}p_{r}g_{e}p_{q}g_{f})^{h}=g_{f}
by Fact
64
. Thus,
g_{f}\leqslant_{\mathscr{J}}\eta(y_{r})
. Finally, since
g^{\prime}=g^{\prime}g_{f}g^{\prime}
, we also have
g^{\prime}\leqslant_{\mathscr{J}}g_{f}
. We get
g^{\prime}\leqslant_{\mathscr{J}}\eta(y_{r})
, which completes the proof.

7.
Conclusion

In this paper, we studied the
BPol
operator used in the construction of concatenation hierarchies. We proved a generic characterization of the classes
\textup{BPol}(\mathscr{C})
that depends on an
ad hoc
property of the class
\mathscr{C}
. While it is not clear when this property on
\mathscr{C}
is decidable in general, a first consequence is a new effective characterization of levels two in the dot-depth and Straubing-Thérien hierarchies, which is simpler than the previously known ones.

When
\mathscr{C}
is itself of the form
\textup{BPol}(\mathscr{D})
, our characterization simplifies and yields a reduction from
\textup{BPol}(\mathscr{C})
-membership to
\mathscr{C}
-covering. The latter problem was known to be decidable for level one in several hierarchies whose bases consist of group languages such as the group and modulo hierarchies. Combined with our results, this implies that level two is decidable in these hierarchies. Finally, it was known that level
two
has decidable covering in the Straubing-Thérien hierarchy and the dot-depth. Hence, we obtain that level
three
is decidable in these hierarchies.
