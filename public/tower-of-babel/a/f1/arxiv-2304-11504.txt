Abstract
We present a model that investigates preference evolution with endogenous matching. In the short run, individuals’ subjective preferences simultaneously determine who they choose to match with and how they behave in the social interactions with their matched partners, which result in material payoffs for them. Material payoffs in turn affect how preferences evolve in the long run. To properly model the “match-to-interact” process, we combine stable matching and equilibrium concepts. Our analysis unveils that endogenous matching gives rise to the “we is greater than me” moral perspective. This perspective is underpinned by a preference that exhibits both homophily and efficiency, which enables individuals to reach a consensus of a collective “we” that transcends the boundaries of the individual “I” and “you.” Such a preference stands out in the evolutionary process because it is able to force positive assortative matching and efficient play among individuals carrying the same preference type. Under incomplete information, a strong form of homophily, which we call parochialism , is necessary for a preference to prevail in evolution, because stronger incentives are required to engage in self-sorting with information friction. Keywords: Preference Evolution, Stable Matching, Evolutionary Stability, Matching with Incomplete Information, Homophily, Parochialism JEL Codes: C73, C78, Z10

Introduction

In the realm of contemporary economic analysis, decision makers’ preferences are commonly taken as exogenously given and fixed. However, preferences themselves can be the products of a lengthy evolutionary process. A significant query arises regarding why certain preferences persist while others dissipate throughout human history.

The objective of this paper is to propose a general model of preference evolution, which formally incorporates endogenous matching given an arbitrary space of preference types, any underlying game that is finite, and any information available to the individuals in the population. Our approach to model the match-to-play-game process follows the works of
Jackson and
Watts 2010

Garrido-Lucero and
Laraki 2021

On one hand, equilibrium ensures that the play between two matched individuals is self-enforcing once the matching is formed. On the other hand, stable matching captures the idea that unsatisfied pairs of individuals can communicate and jointly deviate to form new pairs in a credible manner. Alternatively, endogenous matching can be modeled in a completely non-cooperative fashion.

4

4

For instance, consider the following decentralized matching process that occurs among individuals within a generation. Individuals are temporarily matched to play the stage game and derive flow utilities at the end of each period. Anyone in the population can propose to another individual or take an outside offer. If two individuals reach a rematching agreement, they abandon their previous partners, form a new temporary match, and play according to the agreement. A stable outcome is achieved when no further rematching occurs. The time horizon within each generation is long enough so that only the flow utilities (and material payoffs) attained in the stable outcome are relevant in the long run.

Nevertheless, this approach may result in a complex and intractable extensive form that is difficult to analyze, and the solution can be sensitive to the assumed protocol. Thus, we adopt the protocol-free cooperative approach, which is robust to the details of the extensive form.

We commence our analysis by examining the scenario of complete information, where the preference type of each individual is publicly observable. While acknowledging that the assumption of complete information rarely holds in reality, we deem it a useful benchmark. To investigate the stability of matching outcomes in this context, we adapt the concept of
Nash stability
by

Laraki 2021

The rationales for these preferences to be evolutionarily stable are twofold: First, homophily or parochialism fosters positive assortative matching, ensuring that all individuals carrying this preference type are matched with one another. Second, playing the efficient strategy profile among themselves guarantees that their average fitness exceeds that of any other types that do not play efficiently. Furthermore, we show that if a preference type does not always induce efficient play or fails to exhibit plasticity, then it is evolutionarily unstable.

Next, we turn our attention to the case of incomplete information in which individuals’ preference types are their private information. Following the recent literature on stable matching with incomplete information
(
Liu
et al. 2014
;
Liu 2020
;
Chen and
Hu 2023
;
Wang 2022
)
, we develop a stability concept called Bayes-Nash stability in our model. It requires that each matched pair plays a Bayes-Nash equilibrium (internal stability), and that there is no incomplete information blocking pair (external stability). Intuitively, an incomplete information blocking pair exists when (1) two individuals agree on a rematching proposal that specifies how each side is supposed to play in the deviation, and (2) both sides strictly benefit from the deviation given that the proposal will be honored. Notice that such blocking may be accompanied by information revelation, so the requirement of external stability imposes restrictions on the form of incomplete information in a stable outcome. This highlights that information is an
endogenous
variable in our setting.

In our setting, information is not only revealed by agents’ strategies against their opponents, but, more importantly, also shaped by their decisions to rematch based on the current outcome.

We show that individuals with the stronger
parochial efficient
preference type are able to cooperate with each other through rematching and force efficient play even in the presence of incomplete information.
10
10

10

While our focus is on the stable outcome rather than explicitly modeling the dynamic matching process converging to it, we can imagine that individuals with the parochial efficient preference type engage in a self-sorting process that involves information unraveling. We demonstrate that whenever individuals with unobservable types are not matched among themselves playing an efficient strategy pair, a profitable rematching opportunity arises. Carrying out such deviations can reveal more information to the population.
Consequently, the parochial efficient preference type can ensure the highest average fitness and thus resist invasion of any other type that does not always induce efficient play. We also show through an example that the weaker homophilic efficient type may not resist invasion (Example
4
), and formally prove that it is indeed evolutionarily
unstable
for a large class of material games. The intuition is as follows: Although the homophilic efficient types prefer to play with their own type, their utility still depends on the behavior of the opponent when matched with another type; therefore, they may be reluctant to block an unfavorable matching outcome if they cannot distinguish an (unobservable) alien type that tends to minimize total material payoffs.

We argue that the combination of efficient play and homophily engenders the “we is greater than me” morality. This assertion is substantiated by two primary reasons. First, the preference for efficiency allows individuals to prioritize mutual benefits over individual material gains. Second, the homophily manifested by our key preference types makes them all possess a fixed-point feature, which involves infinite recursive reasoning about an agent’s preferences towards the opponent’s preferences. For example, an agent has parochial efficient preferences if she maximizes total material payoffs and derives a positive utility only when matched with another agent, who maximizes total material payoffs and derives a positive utility only when matched with another agent, who maximizes total material payoffs and so on. Hence, a collective sentiment of “we” emerges from a repeated layering of individual “I” and “you” perspectives. Our paper, therefore, lays down a rigorous evolutionary groundwork underscoring the potential influence of partner choice on the genesis of morality, a significant area of inquiry in evolutionary psychology and anthropology (see for example
Baumard
et al. 2013
and
Tomasello 2016
).

In traditional economic models, individuals are generally assumed to be self-interested and strive to maximize their own material payoffs. Therefore, it is important to investigate whether selfishness can be evolutionarily stable in the context of preference evolution. To this end, we examine two types of selfish preferences that exhibit homophily: the weaker
homophilic selfish
and the stronger
parochial selfish
types. Our findings indicate that with complete information, for these types to be stable, additional conditions must be met for the material game. For instance, for the weaker homophilic selfish type to be evolutionarily stable, the degree of homophily must be sufficiently high. This is because if not, they may be stuck in a cross-type match that puts them in a disadvantageous position.

As previous works in the literature suggest, selfishness may be favored by natural selection under incomplete information when the matching process is assumed to be exogenous
(
Ely and
Yilankaya 2001
;
Dekel
et al. 2007
)
. In contrast, our results imply that with endogenous matching, a preference for efficiency still dominates selfishness, even when incomplete information is present. In fact, incomplete information makes it even more challenging for selfish types to be evolutionarily stable. Specifically, we show that
all
Nash equilibria in the material game must be efficient to ensure the evolutionary stability of the parochial selfish type, which is a more stringent condition than with complete information.

Related Literature

In what follows, we review the literature related to the current work. The concern of how incomplete information affects preference evolution in a random matching environment dates back to
Güth and
Kliemt 1998
, who demonstrates that conditioner cooperators cannot survive when preference types are unobservable because they cannot behave differently according to their opponents’ types.
Dekel
et al. 2007

11
11

11

See also

They find that when preference is perfectly observable, efficiency is the driving force behind the selection of behavior. When preference types are completely unobservable, selfishness is instead evolutionarily stable. They also consider the intermediate case that the individuals’ types are partially observable, where the degree of observability is exogenously given. They find that efficiency force matters for any positive degree of observability; only when preferences are completely unobservable does this force disappear. We show that efficiency force joint with parochialism prevails regardless of the initial degree of observability before matching.

Population, Strategies and Preference Types

Consider a continuum of agents constituting a population who are matched in pairs to engage in symmetric two-person simultaneous game
\Gamma
with a common strategy set
X
. We assume
X
is finite and allow the agents to choose from the set of mixed strategies denoted by
\Delta(X)
. An agent playing pure strategy
x\in X
against another agent playing pure strategy
y\in X
receives a
material payoff
(or
fitness
)
\pi(x,y)
, where
\pi:X^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. The payoff function
\pi
is naturally extended to the domain of mixed strategy profiles
(\Delta(X))^{2}
. Because all agents have to be matched and play the material game in our model (i.e. there is no outside option), we normalize the material payoff function so that
\pi(x,y)>0
for all
(x,y)\in X^{2}
without loss of generality.
13
13

13

Alternatively, we can consider an environment where individuals do have the option to stay unmatched, but if so, they derive an extremely low material payoff. In that case, all our analysis and results apply.

Write
\Theta
for the set of
preference types
an agent can possess. Each preference type
\theta\in\Theta
defines a utility function (and its affine transformations)
U_{\theta}:X^{2}\times\Theta\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, which depends on the pure strategies played by the pair and the matched partner’s preference type. For example,
U_{\theta}(x,y,t)
denotes the utility of an agent with preference type
\theta
playing pure strategy
x
against another agent with preference type
t
playing pure strategy
y
. For each
\theta\in\Theta
,
U_{\theta}
is naturally extended to the domain
(\Delta(X))^{2}\times\Theta
. Assume
\Theta
is rich enough so that any utility function is possessed by some preference type. Our specification of the utility function is more general than those considered in most works in the literature on preference evolution as we allow it to depend on the matched partner’s preference type. This dependency potentially makes an individual less exploitable against another individual carrying a different preference type
(
Alger and
Lehmann 2023
)
. When considering endogenous matching, such a dependency becomes even more crucial, because it is possible for an individual to further avoid being exploited by trying to match only with another individual possessing the same preference type.

We impose no relation between
U_{\theta}
and the material payoff function
\pi
. Special examples include the
selfish
type that only cares about the material payoff, i.e.
U_{\theta}(x,y,t)=\pi(x,y)
, and the
efficient
type that cares about the total material payoffs in a matched pair, i.e.
U_{\theta}(x,y,t)=\pi(x,y)+\pi(y,x)
.

For the subsequent analysis, we shall only consider a population with two different preference types
\theta
and
\tau
, where
\theta,\tau\in\Theta
. A proportion
1-\varepsilon
of the agents carry
\theta
and the remainder carry
\tau
, where
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
. Following the convention in the literature, we sometimes call
\theta
the incumbent type, while
\tau
the mutant type, although it is possible to have more mutants than incumbents in our setting. We refer to the tuple
(\theta,\tau,\varepsilon)
as a
population state
.

3
Preference Evolution with Complete Information

In this section, we assume that each agent observes the preference types of all other agents. Hence, when two agents are matched, they play
\Gamma
with complete information.

For any pair
a,b\in\{\theta,\tau\}
, we let
\mu_{a,b}(\varepsilon)\in[0,1]
denote the proportion of type-
a
agents that are matched with type-
b
agents. A
matching configuration
is a vector
\mu(\varepsilon)=(\mu_{a,b}(\varepsilon))
that satisfies the following requirements:

\displaystyle\mu_{\theta,\theta}(\varepsilon)+\mu_{\theta,\tau}(\varepsilon)=1,\ \mu_{\tau,\theta}(\varepsilon)+\mu_{\tau,\tau}(\varepsilon)=1,\ \text{and }(1-\varepsilon)\mu_{\theta,\tau}(\varepsilon)=\varepsilon\mu_{\tau,\theta}(\varepsilon).

(1)

This definition of matching configuration is suitable for the analysis under complete information. When we turn to incomplete information in Section
4
, a more elaborate definition will be introduced.

Fixing a matching configuration
\mu(\varepsilon)
, define a
strategy profile
associated with
\mu(\varepsilon)
as
\Sigma=\{(x,y)_{a,b}\in(\Delta(X))^{2}:a,b\in\{\theta,\tau\}\text{~and~}\mu_{a,b}(\varepsilon)>0\}
, where each pair
(x,y)_{a,b}\in\Sigma
indicates that a type-
a
agent and a type-
b
agent play (possibly mixed) strategies
x
and
y
, respectively, when they are matched under
\mu(\varepsilon)
. For
a,b\in\{\theta,\tau\}
, we identify
(x,y)_{a,b}
with
(y,x)_{b,a}
; therefore, a strategy profile
\Sigma
contains at most three elements.

We call the combination of a matching configuration and an associated strategy profile
(\mu(\varepsilon),\Sigma)
a
matching profile
.

Remark 1
.

An implicit assumption we make is that the strategies played in a matching profile are
pairwise-homogeneous
. That is, the strategy pair played by two matched agents only depends on the types of those agents. However, when two agents of the same type are matched, they are allowed to play different strategies. This is more general than the standard assumption in the literature on preference evolution, where the strategy pair has to be symmetric when same-type agents are matched.
14
14

14

The rationale for the standard assumption in the literature is as follows. Since the underlying two-person game
\Gamma
is simultaneous and the matching process is exogenous, there is no opportunity for the agents to condition their strategies on their matched partners’ strategies, but only their types. Therefore, given that
\Gamma
is symmetric, if
x_{\theta,\theta}\in X
denotes the strategy chosen by a
\theta
-type agent against another
\theta
-type agent, then for a pair of
\theta
-type agents, they both necessarily play
x_{\theta,\theta}
.

When we take into account endogenous matching, we assume that an agent has the ability to observe their opponent’s strategy during repeated interactions and can make pairwise deviations with other agents.
15
15

15

Assuming observability of strategies played by opponents is inconsequential when matching is exogenous as in the literature.

This implies that same type agents are able to play different strategies when being matched in an outcome. More importantly, as shown in Example
1
, for some underlying games, there cannot be a stable matching profile in which same-type agents play the same strategy. Hence, the possibility of asymmetry is critical for our analysis.

3.1
Stable Matching

Given a population state
(\theta,\tau,\varepsilon)
, our next goal is to identify the matching profiles
(\mu(\varepsilon),\Sigma)
that can be deemed
stable
. The requirement of stability has two layers. First, holding the matching configuration
\mu(\varepsilon)
fixed, agents do not want to change their strategies as specified by
\Sigma
. In other words, the strategy profile should constitute a Nash equilibrium. Second, given the utilities agents derive in a matching profile, there should not exist a pair of agents who want to form a pairwise deviation and be rematched with each other. To formalize this idea, we extend the notion of Nash stability by
Garrido-Lucero and
Laraki 2021
to a continuous population. See also
Jackson and
Watts 2010
. We assume that agents cannot commit to a strategy via forces such as binding contracts or the possibility of future punishment in repeated interactions. This assumption restricts the set of pairwise deviations that are viable.

We now formally define these two layers of stability. A strategy profile
\Sigma
associated with
\mu(\varepsilon)
is a
Nash equilibrium profile
if it satisfies the following: For
a,b\in\{\theta,\tau\}
, if
\mu_{a,b}(\varepsilon)>0
and
(x^{*},y^{*})_{a,b}\in\Sigma
, we have
x^{*}\in\arg\max_{x\in\Delta(X)}\,U_{a}(x,y^{*},b)
and
y^{*}\in\arg\max_{y\in\Delta(X)}\,U_{b}(y,x^{*},a)
. That is, every matched pair is playing a Nash equilibrium under
\Sigma
. For any matching configuration
\mu(\varepsilon)
, the existence of a Nash equilibrium profile
\Sigma
is guaranteed by the standard argument of Kakutani’s fixed point theorem. Note that there can be multiple Nash equilibrium profiles associated with the same matching configuration.

Definition 1
.

Fix a matching profile
(\mu(\varepsilon),\Sigma)
. We say there is a
blocking pair
if there exist
a,b,c,d\in\{\theta,\tau\}
and some strategy pair
(\widehat{x},\widehat{y})
such that

(i)

\mu_{a,c}(\varepsilon)>0
,
\mu_{b,d}(\varepsilon)>0
, and
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})_{a,c},(x^{\prime\prime},y^{\prime\prime})_{b,d}\in\Sigma
;

(ii)

\widehat{x}\in\arg\max_{x\in\Delta(X)}U_{a}(x,\widehat{y},b)
and
\widehat{y}\in\arg\max_{y\in\Delta(X)}U_{b}(y,\widehat{x},a)
;

(iii)

U_{a}(\widehat{x},\widehat{y},b)>U_{a}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},c)
and
U_{b}(\widehat{y},\widehat{x},a)>U_{b}(x^{\prime\prime},y^{\prime\prime},d)
.

This notion of blocking pair for aggregate matching is analogous to the one in
Echenique
et al. 2013
, which serves as a natural generalization of the blocking concept proposed by

Shapley 1962

Condition (ii) requires the deviating agents agree on a Nash equilibrium strategy pair due to the lack of commitment power. Finally, condition (iii) means the deviating agents strictly prefer to rematch. An important implicit assumption we make here is that agents are able to observe not only others’ types but also their current moves, so that a blocking pair is formed based on both.

As we argued above, for a matching profile to be stable, the status quo should constitute a Nash equilibrium for each pair of matched agents, and there should be no profitable blocking pair under the matching profile. Therefore, we have the following definition of Nash stability.

\Sigma
is a Nash equilibrium profile
(internal stability)

3.2
Evolutionary Stability

As a shorthand notation, when
(x,y)_{a,b}\in\Sigma
, we write
\Sigma_{1}(a,b)=x
and
\Sigma_{2}(a,b)=y
. Given a Nash stable matching profile
(\mu(\varepsilon),\Sigma)
, the average material payoffs for type-
\theta
and type-
\tau
agents are given by

\displaystyle G_{\theta}(\mu(\varepsilon),\Sigma)

\displaystyle=\mu_{\theta,\theta}(\varepsilon)\left[\frac{1}{2}\pi(\Sigma_{1}(\theta,\theta),\Sigma_{2}(\theta,\theta))+\frac{1}{2}\pi(\Sigma_{2}(\theta,\theta),\Sigma_{1}(\theta,\theta))\right]+\mu_{\theta,\tau}(\varepsilon)\pi(\Sigma_{1}(\theta,\tau),\Sigma_{2}(\theta,\tau)),

\displaystyle G_{\tau}(\mu(\varepsilon),\Sigma)

\displaystyle=\mu_{\tau,\tau}(\varepsilon)\left[\frac{1}{2}\pi(\Sigma_{1}(\tau,\tau),\Sigma_{2}(\tau,\tau))+\frac{1}{2}\pi(\Sigma_{2}(\tau,\tau),\Sigma_{1}(\tau,\tau))\right]+\mu_{\tau,\theta}(\varepsilon)\pi(\Sigma_{1}(\tau,\theta),\Sigma_{2}(\tau,\theta)).

Notice that a uniform average is taken for matched agents of the same type due to the possibility of asymmetry. We now define the notion of evolutionary stability as follows.

Definition 4
.

A preference type
\theta\in\Theta
is
evolutionarily stable against
another type
\tau\in\Theta
if for every
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
,
G_{\theta}(\mu(\varepsilon),\Sigma)\geq G_{\tau}(\mu(\varepsilon),\Sigma)
for all Nash stable matching profiles
(\mu(\varepsilon),\Sigma)
in population state
(\theta,\tau,\varepsilon)
, while the inequality is strict for some Nash stable matching profile. A preference type
\theta
is
evolutionarily unstable
if there exists another type
\tau
that is evolutionarily stable against
\theta
.

Remark 2
.

Our definition of evolutionary stability is neither stronger nor weaker than the one in
Alger and
Weibull 2013
. First, they require strict inequality for all Nash equilibria under their exogenous matching process, while we only require it for some Nash stable matching profile. On the other hand, their notion of evolutionary stability is defined in a local sense, while ours is a global one as the inequality should hold regardless of the proportion
\varepsilon
of the mutants.

We only define evolutionary stability against a particular type
\tau
because requiring the condition to hold against all possible types would be too stringent given that
\Theta
is rich: For example, if another type
\tau
never wants to match with
\theta
and behave just like
\theta
among themselves, then the average material payoffs would be the same across the two types. Next, we introduce a related notion called neutral stability:

Definition 5
.

A preference type
\theta\in\Theta
is
neutrally stable
if for every
\tau\in\Theta
and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
,
G_{\theta}(\mu(\varepsilon),\Sigma)\geq G_{\tau}(\mu(\varepsilon),\Sigma)
for all Nash stable matching profiles
(\mu(\varepsilon),\Sigma)
in population state
(\theta,\tau,\varepsilon)
.

Neutral stability is weak because only weak inequality is required for all Nash stable matching profiles. However, it is also strong in the sense that the inequality should hold for all types
\tau\in\Theta
. By definition, if a type is neutrally stable, then it is not evolutionarily unstable. Given the definitions of evolutionary (un)stability, we now proceed with the analysis.

As a standard terminology, we say a strategy pair
(\widetilde{x},\widetilde{y})

(\widetilde{x},\widetilde{y})\in\argmax_{(x,y)\in(\Delta(X))^{2}}\,\pi(x,y)+\pi(y,x).

Note that this definition also imposes an implicit but weak assumption on the material game

Definition 7

For

U_{\theta}(x,y,t)=\pi(x,y)+\pi(y,x)+\lambda\cdot\mathmybb{1}_{\{t=\theta\}}.

(2)

In the network literature, the tendency of people to interact with similar people is referred to as homophily (see
Jackson 2014

\lambda

\lambda

\lambda

Definition 8
.

A preference type
\theta
is called the
parochial efficient
type if the corresponding utility function takes the form

U_{\theta}(x,y,t)=[\pi(x,y)+\pi(y,x)]\cdot\mathmybb{1}_{\{t=\theta\}}.

(3)

A parochial efficient agent has a strong tendency to be associated with another parochial efficient agent because it is the only possibility that she can derive a positive utility. Hence, one can consider parochialism as a strong form of homophily.
Newton 2017
also considers parochialism in preference evolution. He defines parochialism on the matching level, meaning that the parochial agents are only matched with one another. On the contrary, we define parochialism on the preference level, and how parochial agents are matched is determined by stable matching.

Remark 3
.

A parochial efficient agent cares about the aggregate material payoffs received by both agents in the match. Moreover, this mindset is activated only when she is teamed up with another parochial efficient agent, who in turn values total material payoffs solely when paired with yet another parochial efficient agent, and this reasoning continues. A similar argument applies to the homophilic efficient types as well. We posit that the fixed-point feature of such an infinite recursive reasoning process, combined with the preference for efficiency, is a shared feeling of “we” between two matched agents. This collective sentiment gives birth to the moral principle of “we is great than me.” Our subsequent exploration reveals that this principle is pivotal in fostering positive assortative matching and efficient play, which subsequently reinforces and perpetuates itself.

Our first result shows that a preference for efficiency with any level of homophily or with parochialism is likely to be the type that prevails in the long run.

4
Preference Evolution with Incomplete Information

In this section, we turn our attention to the case of incomplete information. Suppose that in the population, every agent knows her own preference type, but may not observe the types of other agents.
22
22

22

Recall that our model allows plasticity, meaning an agent’s utility function can depend on the preference type of her matched partner. The interpretation is that an agent may value certain characteristics of her opponent, which are assumed to be perfectly correlated with preferences. When these characteristics are readily observable (e.g. physical appearance), a model with complete information suffices. However, if these characteristics are intrinsically hidden (e.g. empathy or sense of responsibility), we must employ a model that accounts for incomplete information. Relaxing the assumption of perfect correlation between characteristics and preferences is beyond the scope of this paper. See a discussion in the concluding section.

Fixing a population state
(\theta,\tau,\varepsilon)
, we need to generalize the notion of matching configuration to encompass incomplete information.

A

(with incomplete information) is a pair
(p,\mu(\varepsilon))
. The first component

is a vector that satisfies

p_{\theta}+p_{\tau}+p_{u}=1,~0\leq p_{\theta}\leq 1-\varepsilon,0\leq p_{\tau}\leq\varepsilon\text{~and~}p_{u}\geq 0.

We denote by
p_{\theta}
and
p_{\tau}
the fraction of type-
\theta
and type-
\tau
agents in the population whose types are publicly observable. The types of the remaining agents cannot be recognized, and we call them agents with unobservable types or “label-
u
agents’’ without any confusion.

23

23

Note that such “label” is purely informational and does not affect utilities.
To ease our exposition, we henceforth refer to the observable type-
\theta
agents simply as “label-
\theta
agents,” and those unobservable ones as “label-
u\theta
agents.” Similar terminologies apply to the type-
\tau
agents.
24
24

24

Therefore, the set of label-
u
agents consists of label-
u\theta
and label-
u\tau
agents.
When

, we let
q_{u\theta}>0
and
q_{u\tau}>0
denote the proportions of label-
u\theta
and label-
u\tau

u
agents, i.e.

q_{u\theta}=\frac{1-\varepsilon-p_{\theta}}{p_{u}}\quad\text{and}\quad q_{u\tau}=\frac{\varepsilon-p_{\tau}}{p_{u}}.

Remark 5
.

We can interpret the “labels”
\theta
,
\tau
, and
u
as three signals that reveal information about agent types. The labels
\theta
and
\tau
perfectly reveal the types, whereas the label
u
pools the remaining population and serves as a noisy signal regarding types. To make our model more general, we can consider multiple noisy signals that convey distinct informational contents. This amounts to assuming
n>1

u
and
u^{\prime}
such that the proportions of label-
u\theta
and label-
u^{\prime}\theta
agents are different. Our focus on the case

is a simplification, but all subsequent results carry over to the general case with appropriate changes to the definitions.

Analogous to the case of complete information, for any
a,b\in\{\theta,\tau,u\}
, we let
\mu_{a,b}(\varepsilon)\in[0,1]
be the proportion of label-
a
agents that are matched with label-
b
agents. The second component of a matching configuration is therefore a vector
\mu(\varepsilon)=(\mu_{a,b}(\varepsilon))
that satisfies the following requirements

\displaystyle\mu_{a,\theta}(\varepsilon)+\mu_{a,\tau}(\varepsilon)+\mu_{a,u}(\varepsilon)=1,\text{~for $a\in\{\theta,\tau,u\}$;}

\displaystyle p_{a}\mu_{a,b}(\varepsilon)=p_{b}\mu_{b,a}(\varepsilon),\text{~for $a,b\in\{\theta,\tau,u\}$.}

Given a matching configuration
(p,\mu(\varepsilon))
, we define a
strategy profile
associated with
(p,\mu(\varepsilon))
as
\Sigma=\{(x,y)_{a,b}\in(\Delta(X))^{2}:a,b\in\{\theta,\tau,u\}\text{~and~}p_{a}\mu_{a,b}(\varepsilon)>0\}
, where each pair
(x,y)_{a,b}
indicates the (possibly mixed) strategies played by a label-
a
and a label-
b
agent when being matched under
(p,\mu(\varepsilon))
. Since there are three possible labels and we identify
(x,y)_{a,b}
with
(y,x)_{b,a}
,
\Sigma
contains at most six elements. As before, the combination of a matching configuration and an associated strategy profile
(p,\mu(\varepsilon),\Sigma)
is called a
matching profile
(with incomplete information).

Remark 6
.

As in the recent literature on matching with incomplete information, the amount of information (
p_{u}
in our case) in a “stable” matching profile should be understood as an
endogenous
variable. This is because agents can obtain information as they observe the matching pattern and the strategy profile.
25
25

25

As an extreme example, if type
\theta
has a dominant strategy
x
and type
\tau
has a dominant strategy
y\neq x
, then incomplete information can never persist because individuals will immediately tell the type of their opponents by their strategies.
Moreover, pairwise deviations under incomplete information are often accompanied by information revelation, so information is endogenously determined if we think of the stable outcome as the reduced-form limit of a dynamic procedure. Of course, one can impose exogenous restrictions on the amount of incomplete information in a population state; for example,
p_{u}\leq\bar{p}_{u}
for some
\bar{p}_{u}\in(0,1)
. However, we do not make such assumptions and consider all possible matching profiles with incomplete information.

4.1
Stable Matching with Incomplete Information

To simplify our notation, for
a\in\{\theta,\tau\}
, if
p_{a}\mu_{a,u}(\varepsilon)>0
, we write

U_{a}(x,y,u)=\,q_{u\theta}U_{a}(x,y,\theta)+q_{u\tau}U_{a}(x,y,\tau),

which is the expected utility of a label-
a
agent when playing
(x,y)
with a label-
u
opponent. Fixing a population state
(\theta,\tau,\varepsilon)
, a strategy profile
\Sigma
associated with
(p,\mu(\varepsilon))
is a
Bayes-Nash equilibrium profile
if the following conditions are satisfied:

(i)

For
a,b\in\{\theta,\tau\}
, if
p_{a}\mu_{a,b}(\varepsilon)>0
and
(x^{*},y^{*})_{a,b}\in\Sigma
, we have
x^{*}\in\arg\max_{x}U_{a}(x,y^{*},b)
and
y^{*}\in\arg\max_{y}U_{b}(y,x^{*},a)
;

(ii)

If
p_{u}\mu_{u,u}(\varepsilon)>0
and
(x^{*},y^{*})_{u,u}\in\Sigma

x^{*}\in\arg\max_{x}U_{a}(x,y^{*},u)
and
y^{*}\in\arg\max_{y}U_{a}(y,x^{*},u)
for

;

(iii)

For
a\in\{\theta,\tau\}
, if
p_{a}\mu_{a,u}(\varepsilon)>0
and
(x^{*},y^{*})_{a,u}\in\Sigma
, we have
x^{*}\in\arg\max_{x}U_{a}(x,y^{*},u)
and
y^{*}\in\arg\max_{y}U_{b}(y,x^{*},a)
for
b\in\{\theta,\tau\}
.

All argmax’s above are taken over the set of mixed strategies
\Delta(X)
. When
p_{u}=0
, conditions (ii) and (iii) are vacuously true so the definition reduces to the notion of Nash equilibrium profile defined in Section

. When incomplete information is present, i.e.

, an agent playing against a type-
u
opponent makes a best response with the belief that the opponent is of type-
\theta
with probability
q_{u\theta}
and of type-
\tau
with
q_{u\tau}
.

Because agents cannot recognize the unobservable types, we need another definition to properly define pairwise deviations under incomplete information.

Definition 11
.

A pair
(D,\boldsymbol{x})
is a
deviation plan
for a label-
u
agent if (i)
D
is a nonempty subset of
\{\theta,\tau\}
, and (ii)
\boldsymbol{x}:D\rightarrow\Delta(X)
.

In words,

is the set of unobservable types that are willing to participate in a deviation, while
\boldsymbol{x}
is a “plan” that specifies a strategy played by each deviating type.

Definition 12
.

Fix a matching profile with incomplete information
(p,\mu(\varepsilon),\Sigma)
. We say an
incomplete information blocking pair
exists if for
c,d\in\{\theta,\tau,u\}

(I)

(Complete) For

(\widehat{x},\widehat{y})
,

(i)

p_{a}\mu_{a,c}(\varepsilon)>0
,
p_{b}\mu_{b,d}(\varepsilon)>0

(ii)

\widehat{x}\in\arg\max_{x}U_{a}(x,\widehat{y},b)

;

(iii)

(II)

(One-sided) For

\widehat{x}
, and a deviation plan
(D,\boldsymbol{y})

(i)

p_{a}\mu_{a,c}(\varepsilon)>0

p_{u}\mu_{u,d}(\varepsilon)>0
, and
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})_{a,c},(x^{\prime\prime},y^{\prime\prime})_{u,d}\in\Sigma

(ii)

\widehat{x}\in\arg\max_{x}\mathbb{E}_{q}[U_{a}(x,\boldsymbol{y}(t),t)\,|\,D]

(iii)

.

(III)

(Two-sided with conditional incentives) For deviation plans
(D,\boldsymbol{x})
and
(D^{\prime},\boldsymbol{y})

(i)

p_{u}\mu_{u,c}(\varepsilon)>0

p_{u}\mu_{u,d}(\varepsilon)>0

(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})_{u,c},(x^{\prime\prime},y^{\prime\prime})_{u,d}\in\Sigma

(ii)

\boldsymbol{x}(t)\in\arg\max_{x}\mathbb{E}_{q}[U_{t}(x,\boldsymbol{y}(t^{\prime}),t^{\prime})\,|\,D^{\prime}]
for each

(iii)

\boldsymbol{y}(t^{\prime})\in\arg\max_{y}\mathbb{E}_{q}[U_{t^{\prime}}(y,\boldsymbol{x}(t),t)\,|\,D]

.

(III*)

(Two-sided with strong incentives) For

(i)

(ii)

and
\mathbb{E}_{q}[U_{a}(\widehat{x},\boldsymbol{y}(t^{\prime}),t^{\prime})\,|\,D^{\prime}]>U_{a}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},c)

(iii)

\widehat{y}\in\arg\max_{y}\mathbb{E}_{q}[U_{b}(y,\boldsymbol{x}(t),t)\,|\,D]

In the definition above, for given type
a\in\{\theta,\tau\}
, her strategy
x\in\Delta(X)
, and a deviation plan
(D,\boldsymbol{y})
of the opponent, the conditional expected utility
\mathbb{E}_{q}[U_{a}(x,\boldsymbol{y}(t),t)\,|\,D]
is evaluated by using probabilities
q_{u\theta}
and
q_{u\tau}
of

being
\theta
and
\tau
, conditional on the subset of types
D
. If
D
is a singleton, then the expectation is degenerated. Definition
12
describes four cases where an incomplete information blocking can happen, and we provide a more detailed explanation below:

•

In case (I), two deviating agents have observable types. Thus, the requirement for a viable blocking pair is the same as with complete information.

•

In case (II), one of the deviating agents has unobservable type. However, a label-
a
agent can
infer
some information about her opponent’s type from the fact that the pairwise deviation is viable — i.e. it should be incentive compatible for her opponent to participate in the deviation. We describe such inference by the set
D\subseteq\{\theta,\tau\}
in a deviation plan
(D,\boldsymbol{y})
, which contains
exactly
the type(s) that can benefit from playing a best response according to
\boldsymbol{y}
against a label-
a
agent who promises to play
\widehat{x}
.

•

In case (III), both deviating agents have unobservable types. This means two-sided incomplete information is present, and both agents can potentially infer from a viable pairwise deviation. We use two subsets of types
D
,
D^{\prime}
and a fixed-point property to capture such a (conditional) inference: For two label-
u
agents, given that
D^{\prime}
is the set of opponent types that participate and play according to
\boldsymbol{y}
, exactly those types in
D
benefit from participating and playing according to
\boldsymbol{x}
as a best response, and vice versa.
26
26

26

The idea of a fixed-point description of inference is adopted from the recent literature on matching with two-sided incomplete information. See
Liu 2017
and
Chen and
Hu 2023
.

•

Case (III*) still considers a situation with two-sided incomplete information, but the inference is based on a strong form of incentives of the deviating agents: For a pair of agents with labels
ua
and
ub
, given that a label-
ub
opponent participates and plays
\widehat{y}
, the label-
ua
agent is certain to benefit from participating and playing
\widehat{x}
as a best response
regardless of whether and how the other type of label-
u
opponent would join the deviation
, and vice versa.

Remark 7
.

One may wonder whether the seemingly stronger (III*) (blocking with strong incentives) is redundant in Definition
12
, as it would imply case (III), i.e. a blocking with conditional incentives. This conjecture turns out to be incorrect. It is possible to construct a matching profile where a viable blocking pair with strong incentives exists, but deviation plans that satisfy the fixed-point property, which define blocking with conditional incentives, do
not
exist. The non-existence issue stems from the discontinuity in payoffs due to opponents’ incentives to join or exit the deviation. An example is provided in Online Appendix
B.2
.
27
27

27

Case (II) in Definition
12
corresponds to blocking with
conditional
incentives when only one side has unobservable types. One can similarly define a notion of blocking with
strong
incentives. However, under one-sided incomplete information, the latter is strictly stronger than the former and thus redundant.

Remark 8
.

In our model, agents are assumed to be Bayesian and thus the incentives to participate in a pairwise deviation should be determined by expected utilities conditional on the available information. An agent may find out that she did not benefit from the deviation
ex post
because strategies are observable
after
the rematch, but this possibility does not prevent the uninformed agents from participating. Also, as most of works in the matching literature, we assume agents are short-sighted so they do not take into account what will happen after a pairwise deviation is carried out.

We can now extend the notion of stable matching profile to the case of incomplete information.

.

A matching profile with incomplete information
(p,\mu(\varepsilon),\Sigma)
is
Bayes-Nash stable
if it satisfies:

(i)

\Sigma
is a Bayes-Nash equilibrium profile
(internal stability)
.

(ii)

There is no incomplete information blocking pair
(external stability)

The existence of Bayes-Nash stable matching profile is straightforward: As shown in Online Appendix

p_{u}=0
, all definitions regarding Bayes-Nash stability reduce to those defining Nash stability. Thus, the existence of a Bayes-Nash stable matching profile is ensured. This argument is analogous to those in the recent development in matching theory with incomplete information
(

4.2
Evolutionary Stability with Incomplete Information

As before, for
a,b\in\{\theta,\tau,u\}
, when
(x,y)_{a,b}\in\Sigma
, we write
\Sigma_{1}(a,b)=x
and
\Sigma_{2}(a,b)=y
. Given a Bayes-Nash stable matching profile
(p,\mu(\varepsilon),\Sigma)
, the average material payoffs for agents of type
\theta
is given by

\displaystyle G_{\theta}(p,\mu(\varepsilon),\Sigma)=\frac{p_{\theta}}{1-\varepsilon}G^{\theta}_{\theta}(\mu(\varepsilon),\Sigma)+\left(1-\frac{p_{\theta}}{1-\varepsilon}\right)G^{u}_{\theta}(\mu(\varepsilon),\Sigma),

where
\frac{p_{\theta}}{1-\varepsilon}
and
1-\frac{p_{\theta}}{1-\varepsilon}
are the respective proportions of observable and unobservable type-
\theta
agents in the population, and

\displaystyle G^{\theta}_{\theta}(\mu(\varepsilon),\Sigma)=\mu_{\theta,\theta}(\varepsilon)\bigg[\frac{1}{2}\pi(\Sigma_{1}(\theta,\theta),\Sigma_{2}(\theta,\theta))

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\pi(\Sigma_{2}(\theta,\theta),\Sigma_{1}(\theta,\theta))\bigg]

\displaystyle+\mu_{\theta,\tau}(\varepsilon)\pi(\Sigma_{1}(\theta,\tau),\Sigma_{2}(\theta,\tau))

\displaystyle+\mu_{\theta,u}(\varepsilon)\pi(\Sigma_{1}(\theta,u),\Sigma_{2}(\theta,u))

and

\displaystyle G^{u}_{\theta}(\mu(\varepsilon),\Sigma)=\mu_{u,u}(\varepsilon)\bigg[\frac{1}{2}\pi(\Sigma_{1}(u,u),\Sigma_{2}(u,u))

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\pi(\Sigma_{2}(u,u),\Sigma_{1}(u,u))\bigg]

\displaystyle+\mu_{u,\theta}(\varepsilon)\pi(\Sigma_{1}(u,\theta),\Sigma_{2}(u,\theta))

\displaystyle+\mu_{u,\tau}(\varepsilon)\pi(\Sigma_{1}(u,\tau),\Sigma_{2}(u,\tau))

are the respective average material payoffs of these agents. The definition of the average material payoffs for agents of type
\tau
is analogous. Our notions of stability and unstability can be naturally extended to incorporate incomplete information by replacing “Nash stable matching profiles (with complete information)
(\mu(\varepsilon),\Sigma)

(p,\mu(\varepsilon),\Sigma)
” in Definitions

and

.

5
Discussion and Concluding Remarks

In this paper, we consider preference evolution with endogenous matching by marrying the concepts of stable matching and equilibrium play. We find that the primary forces driving preference evolution are homophily and a preference for efficiency. Specifically, homophily leads to positive assortative matching, while a preference for efficiency drives the efficient play. Preferences that combine these two traits may have a fitness advantage over other preferences. Our results hold under both complete and incomplete information, although only a strong form of homophily survives in the latter case.

In what follows, we engage in a concise discourse aimed at elucidating the connotations of several preference types that we have identified in this paper and draw comparisons between them and those found in the extant literature. In the end, we mention some possible extensions of the current model.

First, we make a comparison between the preference for efficiency and
Kantian
preference type, which has been discussed in
Alger and
Weibull 2013
. The latter is deemed evolutionary stable provided that the exogenous matching process’s degree of assortativity is 1, i.e., positive assortative matching. The preference for efficiency, akin to most of the distributional social preferences explored in economics, is rooted in consequentialist motivations. Hypothetical imperatives, preferences over strategies due to their consequences, characterize this preference, in contrast to categorical imperatives, preferences over strategies irrespective of their consequences, which
Kant 1785
called deontological motivations
(
Chen and
Schonger 2022
)
. The
Kantian
preference is represented by the utility function
u(x)=\pi(x,x)
, implying that an agent assesses different courses of action by considering their own material payoff if the course of action were universalized to all other agents
(
Alger 2022
)
. It is deontological because a
Kantian
agent does not care about what other agents choose in the underlying game. In games where a symmetric efficient strategy profile exists, it may not be possible to distinguish the preference for efficiency from the
Kantian
preference based on observable behavior. However, when all the efficient strategy profiles are asymmetric, two matched agents with a preference for efficiency would obtain a greater total material payoff than two matched
Kantian
agents.

Second, we make a comparison between the two variants of homophily we discuss in this paper. One may be inclined to view the parochial variant as the limit case of the weak variant. However, this supposition is untenable not only mathematically (the parochial variant is lexicographic while the weak variant is not), but also philosophically. The weak variant of homophily is consequentialist in nature since the preference for matching with agents of the same type is contingent upon the outcomes that transpire in the underlying game. By contrast, the parochial variant of homophily is deontological in character, as it prefers the act of interacting with agents of the same type irrespective of the consequences that might ensue from the underlying game.

There are numerous intriguing avenues for extending our work, some of which we briefly discuss here. There exists an alternative interpretation of our model: Each agent’s preferences depend on how the game is played and an individual characteristic (observable or unobservable) that is perfectly correlated with the opponent’s preferences. In general, there may not be a perfect correlation between the preferences and the characteristics to which agents attach value, and such correlation may also be subject to natural selection. This issue can be addressed by adopting a more general model of “type space.” Briefly, a type space consists of a finite set of characteristics (or types)
\mathcal{C}
, a finite set of preferences
\mathcal{P}
, and a prior distribution on
\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{P}
. The preferences are represented by a real-valued function of the strategies played and the opponent’s characteristic; therefore, we have
\mathcal{P}\subset\mathbb{R}^{X^{2}\times\mathcal{C}}
. It is important to note that this generalization differs from our model of incomplete information.

Our model of incomplete information only allows for the identification of unobservable types through strategic interactions in the matching-to-interact process. However, in real-life situations, individuals may employ costly signals to reveal their types to others, which could be either strategic or genetic. Despite the issue of mimicry and deception associated with signaling, scholars have put forth the argument that certain emotions and physical states, such as uncontrollable anger or blushing, can serve as sincere indications of one’s preferences
(
Frank 1987
;
Frank 1988
;
Hirshleifer 2001
)
. See
Alger and
Weibull 2019
and
Alger 2022
for more discussions. Equipping individuals in the matching process with the ability to signal and ability to detect others’ preferences through their signals
(
Hopkins 2014
;
Heller and
Mohlin 2019
)
may alter the matching patterns and consequently the evolution of preferences.

In order to comprehend how human preferences evolve over time, it is important to acknowledge the role of institutions. These entities can have a significant impact on people’s behavior during social interactions by modifying the material benefits of the game being played and adjusting their motivations for matching through various policy instruments, such as tax and subsidies
(
Hiller and
Touré 2021
)
, the protection of property rights
(
Bisin and
Verdier 2021
)
, the establishment and maintenance of religious infrastructures
(
Bisin
et al. 2021
)
, and plans for segregation and integration
(
Wu 2017b
;
Wu 2023
)
. Institutions are endogenous because they are collectively determined by individuals, and as a result, preferences and institutions naturally co-evolve. A potential avenue for future research is to incorporate the approach suggested by
Bisin and
Verdier 2021
for modeling endogenous institutions into models of preference evolution.

Finally,
Nunn 2021
highlights the advantages of viewing history through an evolutionary lens. Considering the increasing amount of historical and pre-historical data available, we hope that the influence of partner choice on shaping human preferences can be empirically examined.

B.1
Existence of Stable Matching Profile

Since we consider a pairwise matching problem with a continuum of population where agents are matched to play games, our model does not fit into any of the existing frameworks in the literature. However, our focus is on a simple case where only two types of agents co-exist in the population. This allows us to establish the existence of a stable matching profile in a straightforward manner.

Take an arbitrary population state
(\theta,\tau,\varepsilon)
and suppose
\varepsilon\leq\frac{1}{2}
without loss of generality. The case when
\varepsilon>\frac{1}{2}
is omitted since it can be proved by switching the roles of
\theta
and
\tau
. For

for the highest utility a type-
a

L_{a,a}=\max_{(x,y)\in\mathit{NE}_{a}}\min\{U_{a}(x,y,a),U_{a}(y,x,a)\}.

In other words,
L_{a,a}
is the loser’s utility in any loser-best Nash equilibrium between type-
a
agents. Let
\mathit{NE}_{\theta,\tau}
be the set of Nash equilibria in the game played by a type-
\theta

\tau

\theta

\tau

Proof.

Proof of Proposition

(i) By Proposition

, if
\theta

. Thus,
\theta

(ii) Let
\theta
be a type such that
U_{\theta}(x,y,t)=f(x,y)
. If
\theta
exhibits same-type inefficiency, then part (i) applies. Thus, we suppose all strategy pairs in
\mathit{NE}^{\mathit{lb}}_{\theta}
are efficient. Now consider another type
\tau
that has the following utility function:

U_{\tau}(x,y,t)=\begin{cases}\pi(x,y)+\pi(y,x)&\text{if $t=\tau$,}\\
[\pi(x,y)+\pi(y,x)]\cdot\mathmybb{1}_{\{\pi(x,y)\geq\pi(y,x)\}}\cdot\mathmybb{1}_{\{(x,y)\text{ is efficient}\}}&\text{if $t=\theta$.}\end{cases}

We now show that
\tau
is evolutionarily stable against
\theta
.

First, we argue that
G_{\tau}(p,\mu(\varepsilon),\Sigma)\geq G_{\theta}(p,\mu(\varepsilon),\Sigma)
for all Bayes-Nash stable matching profiles. This is because by construction of type
\tau
’s utility function, the following properties hold for any Bayes-Nash stable matching profile:

•

If
p_{\tau}>0
and
(x^{*},y^{*})_{\tau,\tau}\in\Sigma
, then
(x^{*},y^{*})
is efficient;

•

If
p_{\tau},p_{\theta}>0
and
(x^{*},y^{*})_{\tau,\theta}\in\Sigma
, then
(x^{*},y^{*})
is efficient and
\pi(x^{*},y^{*})\geq\pi(y^{*},x^{*})
;

•

If
p_{u}\mu_{u,\tau}>0
and
(x^{*},y^{*})_{u,\tau}\in\Sigma
, then
(x^{*},y^{*})
is efficient and
\pi(y^{*},x^{*})\geq\pi(x^{*},y^{*})
;

•

If
p_{u}\mu_{u,u}>0
and
(x^{*},y^{*})_{u,u}\in\Sigma
, then
(x^{*},y^{*})
is efficient;

•

If
p_{u}\mu_{u,\theta}>0
and
(x^{*},y^{*})_{u,\theta}\in\Sigma
, then
(x^{*},y^{*})
is efficient and
\pi(x^{*},y^{*})\geq\pi(y^{*},x^{*})
.

When the first three properties do not hold, two label-
\tau

u\tau
agents can form a blocking pair with strong incentives. The argument for the validity of blocking pairs is similar to that in the proof of Proposition
6
and thus omitted. Write

G_{\tau}(p,\mu(\varepsilon),\Sigma)\geq\frac{1}{2}\pi(\widetilde{x},\widetilde{y})+\frac{1}{2}(\widetilde{y},\widetilde{x})\geq G_{\theta}(p,\mu(\varepsilon),\Sigma).

because type-
\tau

\theta
agents in all possible matches. (In particular, if
p_{u}\mu_{u,\theta}>0
, some type-
\theta
agents (label-
u\theta

payoff of type
\theta
is lower than
\frac{1}{2}\pi(\widetilde{x},\widetilde{y})+\frac{1}{2}(\widetilde{y},\widetilde{x})
.)

It remains to show that the inequality is strict for some (a large set of) Bayes-Nash stable matching profiles. Recall that we assume
\pi(\widetilde{x},\widetilde{y})\neq\pi(\widetilde{y},\widetilde{x})
for all efficient strategy pairs
(\widetilde{x},\widetilde{y})
, so we have the following three possibilities

(i)

p_{\tau},p_{\theta}>0
,
(\widetilde{x},\widetilde{y})_{\tau,\theta}\in\Sigma
,
(\widetilde{x},\widetilde{y})\in\mathit{NE}^{\mathit{lb}}_{\theta}
, and
\pi(\widetilde{x},\widetilde{y})>\pi(\widetilde{y},\widetilde{x})
;

(ii)

p_{u}\mu_{u,\tau}>0
,
(\widetilde{y},\widetilde{x})_{u,\tau}\in\Sigma
,
(\widetilde{y},\widetilde{x})\in\mathit{NE}^{\mathit{lb}}_{\theta}
, and
\pi(\widetilde{x},\widetilde{y})>\pi(\widetilde{y},\widetilde{x})

(iii)

p_{u}\mu_{u,\theta}>0
,

u\tau
