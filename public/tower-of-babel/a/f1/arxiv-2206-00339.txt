Adaptive time integration of mechanical forces in center-based models for biological cell populations
Per Lötstedt, Sonja Mathias Department of Information Technology. Uppsala University, SE-751 05 Uppsala, Sweden

Abstract
Center-based models are used to simulate the mechanical behavior of biological cells during embryonic development or cancer growth. To allow for the simulation of biological populations potentially growing from a few individual cells to many thousands or more, these models have to be numerically efficient, while being reasonably accurate on the level of individual cell trajectories. In this work, we increase the robustness, accuracy, and efficiency of the simulation of center-based models by choosing the time steps adaptively in the numerical method. We investigate the gain in using single rate time stepping for the forward and backward Euler methods, based on local estimates of the numerical errors and the stability of the method in the case of the explicit forward Euler method. Furthermore, we propose a multirate time stepping scheme that simulates regions with high local force gradients (e.g. as they happen after cell division) with multiple smaller time steps within a larger single time step for regions with smoother forces. These methods are compared for different model systems in numerical experiments. We conclude that the adaptive single rate forward Euler method results in significant gains in terms of reduced wall clock times for the simulation of a linearly growing tissue, while at the same time eliminating the need for manual determination of a suitable time step size.



Keywords
: center-based model, time integration, adaptivity, gradient system, numerical methods

Introduction

In computational models of tissue mechanics, biological cells grow, divide, migrate, and die.
They are exposed to mechanical forces from other cells and the environment during their life time. In off-lattice cell-based models, each cell is represented individually and moves due to these forces in continuous two or three dimensional space. Off-lattice cell-based models are used in simulations to study e.g. tumours
[
22
,
23
,
26
]
, morphogenesis
[
11
]
, and colonies of bacteria
[
15
]
.
The governing equations for the motion of the aggregation of cells constitute a system of ordinary differential equations (ODEs) derived from Newton’s second law
[
1
,
4
,
22
,
28
]
.
These ODEs are satisfied by the coordinates of the cell centers in a cell-centered or center-based model (CBM) and the forces between the cells depend on their relative distance
[
8
]
. Another type of off-lattice model based on the time integration of such an ODE system is a vertex-based model (VBM)
[
22
]
. There the forces are applied in the vertices of the cell boundary.
In either case the system needs to be solved numerically
at discrete time points.

In addition to the cell movement cell-based models incorporate the aforementioned individual cell behaviors such as proliferation, migration and apoptosis which affect the mechanics of the population. As a result, there are different time scales in the ODE system. After cell proliferation or division for example, force magnitudes are large and short time steps are necessary in the numerical solution to resolve a fast time scale. Long time steps are possible between cell divisions when the cells move on a slow scale.
However, in a fixed time stepping scheme the time step size is constant and hence dictated by the minimal step size required after proliferation. This makes it very inefficient if the population behavior is highly dynamic. In fact, adaptive time stepping is mentioned as one of the challenges in the simulation of multicellular tissues in a recent survey
[
9
]
. To make things worse, choosing too large time step sizes potentially results in numerically instable solutions when using explicit schemes. And even if the time step ensures stability, cell trajectories of daughter cells after division may be physically incorrect leading to geometrical differences on the population level indistinguishable from parameter value effects unless the time step size is reduced further
[
28
]
. As such the correct choice of time step size is very important for the model behavior and consequently for the validity of model conclusions.
The effects of constant time steps on the quality of the solutions of a VBM are reported in
[
19
]
.

A simple way of ensuring numerical stability in CBMs is adjusting the time step size based on a threshold on the spatial displacement of the cells between time steps
[
2
,
17
,
21
,
36
]
.
This threshold is usually chosen significantly smaller than the cellular radius. If it is violated, the time step is decreased and the cell positions are recalculated.
Additionally, one can also define a minimum spatial step size which if fulfilled will lead to the time step being increased
[
21
]
.
The implementation of this simple heuristic approach (even without a minimal spatial displacement) can already lead to significant reduction in computational cost, as discussed in
[
2
]
.
Two time steps are determined in
[
17
]
where a short step used to integrate the rapidly moving cells and the majority of the cells are advanced in time by a longer step.
By limiting the spatial displacement in each time step, the drawback is that the modeler needs to choose the exact threshold value for this displacement.
The correct values may need to be determined by trial-and-error until the simulation results no longer show any change.
Another method for CBMs avoiding the need for the spatial displacement threshold was proposed in
[
2
]
based on an embedded Runge-Kutta scheme, in particular the Dormand-Prince 853 scheme
[
13
, p. 181]
.
Here an order eight Runge-Kutta method was used to calculate the solution itself, along with two lower order methods to provide an estimate of the numerical error. Such a high order, however, may not be necessary for cell simulations and its computational cost may be prohibitive.

In this paper, we compare methods to choose the time step in the numerical solution of the ODE system governing the motion of CBMs. The methods we propose vary the time step size not by checking a threshold, but by directly calculating the time step size necessary to satisfy a desired accuracy on the level of the cell trajectories.
As such, the time step size is determined by the properties of the ODE system and the numerical method instead of the physical properties of the cells or geometric conditions.
More specifically, the numerical solution is advanced by the backward and forward Euler methods with time steps such that the local estimate of the discretization error is bounded by a given parameter.
In addition, there is a bound on the time step in the forward Euler method for the solution to remain stable.
As a result the need to guess a constant time step for the whole time interval of interest is eliminated and at the same time the numerical errors are controlled by changing the time step sizes, thereby increasing the robustness of center-based model simulations.

The forward and backward Euler methods are of first order accuracy and the former is commonly used in CBMs
[
7
,
31
]
, both due to its simplicity and the fact that for cell-based models the modeling errors usually dominate and hence high order accuracy of the ODE solver is not required.
This is also the conclusion drawn in
[
19
]
.
The backward Euler method is an implicit method and as such associated with a higher computational cost per time step than the explicit forward Euler method. This prohibits its use in combination with a fixed time stepping for many practical experimental setups
[
2
,
29
]
. However, its improved stability properties are suitable for stiff systems as they promise larger step sizes at mechanical equilibrium. We will investigate whether the use of an adaptive time step size can render the backward Euler method computationally beneficial as well.

Based on these methods, we consider two different types of adaptive schemes.
In a globally adaptive single rate method, the same time step is used for all cells.
In a locally adaptive multirate method, on the other hand, the time step is shorter for cells subject to a rapid change and longer for cells moving more slowly closer to a mechanical equilibrium configuration.
This multirate method is suitable for cell simulations with spatially distributed cell divisions where short time steps are necessary after proliferation but only for the limited number of cells affected by the strong local forces.
In this setting, the coordinates of the dividing cell and its adjacent cells are the fast variables that are integrated with multiple small time steps. The coordinates of the majority of the cells on the other hand are the slow variables and are integrated with a single larger time step chosen as an integer multiple of the small time step such that all cells end up at the same time after the update.
The dynamic partitioning of the variables into slow and fast is easy in a cell system compared to a general system of ODEs treated in e.g.
[
10
,
24
]
. Missing values of the slow variables during the short time steps are not interpolated in the multirate method. The method is still of order one
[
10
]
.

While these kinds of methods have not been applied to CBMs before, they have been studied in the context of general systems of ODEs. A systematic variation of the time steps to keep the local discretization errors small for general systems of ODEs is surveyed in
[
37
]
for single rate methods.
An early multirate method is found in
[
10
]
where different time steps are taken for different equations in the ODE system.
The variables are partitioned into slow and fast variables with synchronization of the time steps such that the long steps for the slow variables are an integer multiple of the short ones for the fast variables. A conclusion is that the method is competitive for systems with many slow variables.
The global error is bounded adaptively in the multirate method in
[
24
,
25
]
derived from a Galerkin formulation.
The order of accuracy of the method for different equations may also vary. Multirate time stepping is also developed in
[
35
]
for systems with different time scales for different sets of equations. The time steps are successively refined by a factor two to satisfy a criterion on the local error.
For the components integrated by short time steps in a high order method, there will be missing values of the variables advanced by long time steps. These values are obtained by interpolation.
A recent Runge-Kutta method designed for a fast and a slow time step is found in
[
34
]
.

In this study the following four numerical schemes are compared:

1.

Single rate forward Euler method

2.

Single rate forward Euler method with stability check

3.

Multirate forward Euler method (with stability check)

4.

Single rate backward Euler method

In order to investigate their properties and the gain from adaptive time stepping in general we apply these schemes to three different cell configurations.
With the same error tolerance for all methods, one method is more efficient than the other ones measured in wall clock time and compared to a method with a constant time step.

The contents of the paper are as follows. The center-based model and the cell forces are given and analyzed in Section
2
. The numerical methods for time integration of the equations are developed in Section
3
and the conditions for first order accuracy and stability are derived.
Numerical results for three dimensional cell populations are presented in Section
4
and conclusions are drawn in Section
5
.

2
Analysis of forces in center-based models

In a CBM, cells are modelled as intervals, circles, and spheres in one, two and three dimensions (1D, 2D, and 3D). A force on a cell due to the contact with another cell is applied in the center and depends on the distance to the other cell. The system of ODEs defined by these forces governs the motion of the cells. Its properties are investigated in this section. The numerical methods in the next section utilize these properties to obtain accurate and stable time integration of the ODEs.

Matrices and vectors are written in boldface
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{x}
. The vector norms are the max norm
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
and the Euclidean norm
\|\cdot\|_{2}
and their associated matrix norms. A time derivative
\text{d}\mathbf{x}/\text{d}t
of
\mathbf{x}
is written
\dot{\mathbf{x}}
.

Introduce in
d
dimensions (
d=1,2,
or
3
) for
N
cells the coordinates of the center of cell
i

\mathbf{x}_{i}=(x_{i1},\ldots,x_{id})^{T},\;i=1,\ldots,N,

the relative position
\mathbf{r}_{ij}
to cell
j,j=1,\ldots,N,
for
j\neq i
, the relative
distance
r_{ij}
, and the direction of the center of the neighbor
\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{ij}

\mathbf{r}_{ij}=\mathbf{x}_{j}-\mathbf{x}_{i},\;r_{ij}=\|\mathbf{r}_{ij}\|_{2}=\left(\sum_{k=1}^{d}(x_{jk}-x_{ik})^{2}\right)^{1/2},\;\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{ij}=r_{ij}^{-1}\mathbf{r}_{ij}.

(1)

When
d=3
,
\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{ij}
between two cells is defined by two angles
\phi\in[0,2\pi]
and
\theta\in[-\pi/2,\pi/2]
in

\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{ij}=(\cos(\theta)\cos(\phi),\;\cos(\theta)\sin(\phi),\;\sin(\theta))^{T}.

(2)

For 2D, let
\theta=0
in (
2
). The full coordinate vector for the whole cell system is
\mathbf{x}^{T}=(\mathbf{x}_{1}^{T},\mathbf{x}_{2}^{T},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{N}^{T})
.

2.1
A gradient system

The force on cell
i
caused by cell
j
in a CBM is
\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{ij}g(r_{ij})
where
\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{ij}
is the direction of the force and
\|\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{ij}g(r_{ij})\|_{2}=|g(r_{ij})|
is the modulus of it.
The strength of the force
g(r)
is continuous and defined for
r\geq 0
with the properties

0\leq r\leq s:g(r)\leq 0,\quad s<r<r_{A}:g(r)>0,\quad r\geq r_{A}:g(r)=0,

(3)

for some positive rest length
s
and a maximum interaction distance between any two cells
r_{A}
. The repelling force when
r<s
is often chosen such that
|g(r)|
increases when
r
approaches 0 and
g
vanishes at
r=s
.
The force is attracting when the distance
r
is between
s
and
r_{A}
.

As an example, take the cubic force defined in
[
7
]

g^{\text{cubic}}(r)=\begin{cases}\mu\left(r-r_{A}\right)^{2}\left(r-s\right)&\text{if }\,0\leq r\leq r_{A},\\
0&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(4)

where
\mu
denotes the spring stiffness. Other examples are found in
[
28
]
.

The potential
G(r)
for a pair of cells with the force
\hat{\mathbf{r}}g(r)
is given by

G(r)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-\int_{r}^{s}g(\rho)\,d\rho\geq 0,&0\leq r\leq s,\\
\int_{s}^{r}g(\rho)\,d\rho>0,&s<r\leq r_{A},\\
\int_{s}^{r_{A}}g(\rho)\,d\rho=G_{A}>0,&r>r_{A}.\end{array}\right.

(5)

The short range potential
G
is continuously differentiable and
G(s)=0
. The force
\mathbf{f}_{ij}
on cell
i
due to cell
j
is

\mathbf{f}_{ij}(\mathbf{x})=-\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{i}}G(r_{ij})=-\left(\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{i}}r_{ij}\right)g(r_{ij})=\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{ij}g(r_{ij}).

(6)

The simple Hooke’s law with
G(r)=\frac{1}{2}(r-s)^{2}
and a linear relation between force and distance
g(r)=r-s
does not satisfy (
5
) for
r>r_{A}
. The forces in VBMs are usually defined by the gradient of a potential
[
22
]
.

The cell system has
N
free cells and
N_{0}
stationary, immobile cells.
The free cells can move continuously in any direction. The stationary cells do not move in space and have a constant coordinate vector
\mathbf{x}_{0}
. They could be part of a boundary and interact with the free cells via forces as in (
3
).

Introduce the potential
V
including the forces between the free cells,
j=1,\ldots,N,
and the stationary ones,
j=N+1,\ldots,N+N_{0},

V(\mathbf{x})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1,j\neq i}^{N+N_{0}}G(r_{ij}).

(7)

The sum in
V
has a lower bound 0 due to the properties in (
5
).
Many terms in the sum are equal to
G_{A}
because
r_{ij}>r_{A}
.

The contribution of the inertia term in Newton’s second law is assumed to be small in a CBM
[
22
]
but the viscosity term cannot be neglected.
Then the system of
d
ODEs for the center coordinates
\mathbf{x}_{i}
of a free cell
i
is

\dot{\mathbf{x}}_{i}=-\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{i}}V(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{j=1,j\neq i}^{N+N_{0}}\mathbf{f}_{ij}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{j=1,j\neq i}^{N+N_{0}}\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{ij}g(r_{ij}).

(8)

Since
\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{ij}=-\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{ji}
and
g(r_{ij})=g(r_{ji})
we have
\mathbf{f}_{ij}=-\mathbf{f}_{ji}
following Newton’s third law.
There are a limited number of
g(r_{ij})\neq 0
in (
8
) depending on the number of close neighbors to cell
i
with
r_{ij}<r_{A}
that contribute to the total force on the cell.

The ODE system for all free cells is

\dot{\mathbf{x}}=-\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}V(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x}).

(9)

This is a
gradient
system with certain properties
[
38
]
and we will see that these properties are inherited by the numerical solution. The force vector
\mathbf{F}
is continuous in
\mathbf{x}
and a continuously differentiable solution
\mathbf{x}
exists and is unique.

A solution
\mathbf{x}_{\ast}
is an equilibrium or steady state solution if
\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x}_{\ast})=0
. An equilibrium solution is not unique for a potential such as (
7
). It belongs to a subspace
\mathcal{D}
with
\mathbf{x}_{\ast}\in\mathcal{D}
. As an example, consider two free cells,
N=2,\,N_{0}=0
, with center coordinates
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
with
x_{1}<x_{2}
. Then
\mathbf{x}_{\ast}^{T}=(x_{1},x_{1}+s)\in\mathcal{D}
and
\mathbf{x}_{\ast}^{T}=(x_{1},x_{1}+\sigma)\in\mathcal{D}
for any
x_{1}
and any
\sigma>r_{A}
.

The following theorem characterizes the dynamical system (
9
) as
t\rightarrow\infty
.

Theorem 1.
Assume that
V(\mathbf{x})\geq V_{\min}
,
V
in (
9
) is continuously differentiable,
and that
\mathcal{D}
is the non-empty set of solutions
\mathbf{x}_{\ast}
such that
\nabla V(\mathbf{x}_{\ast})=\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x}_{\ast})=0
.
Then as
t\rightarrow\infty
there is no limit cycle and
\mathbf{x}(t)\rightarrow\mathbf{x}^{\infty}\in\mathcal{D}
.
If
\mathbf{x}(0)\in\mathcal{D}
, then the solution is constant
\mathbf{x}(t)=\mathbf{x}(0)
for
t\geq 0
.

Proof.
By (
9
) we have

\dot{V}=\dot{\mathbf{x}}\cdot\nabla V=-\|\dot{\mathbf{x}}\|_{2}^{2}=-\nabla V\cdot\nabla V<0,

unless
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{D}
. Since
V\geq V_{\min}
, eventually when
t\rightarrow\infty
,
\nabla V(\mathbf{x})\rightarrow 0
, and
\mathbf{x}\rightarrow\mathbf{x}^{\infty}\in\mathcal{D}
.
There is no closed orbit according to
[
38
, Thm 7.2.1]
. When
\mathbf{x}(0)\in\mathcal{D}
then
\dot{\mathbf{x}}=-\nabla V(\mathbf{x}(0))=0
and the solution is constant.

\blacksquare

Remark.

The initial condition
\mathbf{x}(0)
determines which
\mathbf{x}_{\ast}
and constant potential
V_{\ast}\geq V_{\min}
the solution converges to.

2.2
Frame invariance

The equations in (
9
) are frame invariant. To see this, transform the
\mathbf{x}_{i}
coordinates by a rotation with a
d\times d
orthonormal matrix
\mathbf{Q}
and a translation
\mathbf{c}
and let
\mathbf{y}_{i}=\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{x}_{i}+\mathbf{c}
. Then define

\boldsymbol{\rho}_{ij}=\mathbf{y}_{j}-\mathbf{y}_{i}=\mathbf{Q}(\mathbf{x}_{j}-\mathbf{x}_{j}),\;\rho_{ij}=\|\mathbf{Q}(\mathbf{x}_{j}-\mathbf{x}_{j})\|_{2}=r_{ij},\;\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{ij}=\mathbf{Q}\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{ij}.

The form of the equations is the same after the transformation

\dot{\mathbf{y}}=\mathbf{Q}\dot{\mathbf{x}}=\sum_{j=1,j\neq i}^{N+N_{0}}\mathbf{Q}\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{ij}g(r_{ij})=\sum_{j=1,j\neq i}^{N+N_{0}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{ij}g(\rho_{ij}),

(10)

avoiding dependence of the particular coordinate system chosen for the equations. Our numerical methods will also be frame invariant.

2.3
Linearization of the forces

Next, we consider the linearization of the forces.
Assume that
V
is twice continuously differentiable and let
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{dN\times dN}
be the Jacobian matrix of
\mathbf{F}
in (
9
) with elements
A_{kl}=\partial F_{k}/\partial x_{l}=-\partial^{2}V/\partial x_{k}\partial x_{l},\;k,l=1,\ldots,dN
. The properties of
\mathbf{A}
will be used to ensure stability of our numerical schemes.

After linearization about
\mathbf{x}
for the free cells and
\mathbf{x}_{0}
for the fixed cells, small perturbations
\delta\mathbf{x}
of the free cells and
\delta\mathbf{x}_{0}
of the fixed cells satisfy

\dot{\delta\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{x})\delta\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{A}_{F}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}_{0})\delta\mathbf{x}_{0}.

(11)

The Jacobian matrix
\mathbf{A}
consists of submatrices
\mathbf{A}^{ij}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d},\,i,j=1,\ldots,N,
for the interaction between the free cells
i
and
j
. The matrix
\mathbf{A}_{F}\in\mathbb{R}^{dN\times dN_{0}}
consists of submatrices
\mathbf{A}^{ij},\,i=1,\ldots,N,\,j=N+1,\ldots,N+N_{0},
for the interaction between the free cells
i
and the stationary cells
j
.
The matrices
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{A}_{F}
are

\mathbf{A}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccccc}-\sum_{j=1,j\neq 1}^{N+N_{0}}\mathbf{A}^{1j}&\ldots&\mathbf{A}^{1j}&\ldots&\mathbf{A}^{1N}\\
&&\vdots&&\\
\mathbf{A}^{i1}&\ldots&-\sum_{j=1,j\neq i}^{N+N_{0}}\mathbf{A}^{ij}&\ldots&\mathbf{A}^{iN}\\
&&\vdots&&\\
\mathbf{A}^{N1}&\ldots&\mathbf{A}^{Nj}&\ldots&-\sum_{j=1,j\neq N}^{N+N_{0}}\mathbf{A}^{Nj}\end{array}\right),

(12)

and

\mathbf{A}_{F}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}\ldots&\mathbf{A}^{1j}&\ldots\\
&\vdots&\\
\ldots&\mathbf{A}^{ij}&\ldots\\
&\vdots&\\
\ldots&\mathbf{A}^{Nj}&\ldots\end{array}\right),\;j=N+1,\ldots,N+N_{0}.

(13)

Each submatrix
\mathbf{A}^{ij}
depends on the force definition as follows

\mathbf{A}^{ij}=\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{ij}\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{ij}^{T}g^{\prime}(r_{ij})+(\mathbf{I}-\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{ij}\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{ij}^{T})\frac{g(r_{ij})}{r_{ij}},

(14)

where
\mathbf{I}
is the
d\times d
identity matrix. The Jacobian submatrix depends on the relative positions of the cells and is scaled by the force function and its derivative.
In (
2
),
\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{ij}
is represented in 3D by two angles between cells
i
and
j
.
Since
\mathbf{A}^{ij}
is symmetric
\mathbf{A}^{ij}=\mathbf{A}^{ji}=(\mathbf{A}^{ij})^{T}
,
\mathbf{A}
in (
12
) is symmetric with real eigenvalues
\lambda(\mathbf{A})
.
This is also a consequence of the fact that the equations form a gradient system (
9
).

A perturbation of cell
j
in the direction to the cell
i
,
\delta\mathbf{x}_{j}=\delta x_{j}\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{ij}
, induces a correction in the same direction proportional to
g^{\prime}

\mathbf{A}^{ij}\delta\mathbf{x}_{j}=g^{\prime}(r_{ij})\delta x_{j}\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{ij}.

With a perturbation in the orthogonal direction,
\delta\mathbf{x}_{j}=\delta x_{j}(\mathbf{I}-\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{ij}\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{ij}^{T})\mathbf{e}
with an arbitrary vector
\mathbf{e}
of unit length, the correction in the orthogonal direction is

\mathbf{A}^{ij}\delta\mathbf{x}_{j}=\frac{g(r_{ij})}{r_{ij}}\delta x_{j}(\mathbf{I}-\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{ij}\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{ij}^{T})\mathbf{e}.

If the coordinates
\mathbf{x}_{j},\,j=N+1,\ldots,N+N_{0},
in
\mathbf{x}_{0}
are fixed then the perturbation
\delta\mathbf{x}_{0}=\mathbf{0}
and only
\mathbf{A}
has an effect on
\dot{\delta\mathbf{x}}
in (
11
).

If
N_{0}=0
there are at least
d
eigenvectors with eigenvalues 0 of
\mathbf{A}
corresponding to rigid body translation of the system. Let
\mathbf{x}^{T}_{\rm trans}=(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{T},\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{T},\ldots,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{T})
with

different linearly independent
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then with
\mathbf{A}
in (
12
)
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}_{\rm trans}=\mathbf{0}
and
\lambda(\mathbf{A})=0

with the associated eigenvector
\mathbf{x}_{\rm trans}
.
Another transformation of the system without changing the forces between the cells is a rigid body rotation of the whole system or an isolated part of the system as in (

2.4
Conservation of the center of gravity of a system of free cells

The center of gravity
\mathbf{x}_{G}
of a system of free cells is defined by

\mathbf{x}_{G}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{x}_{i}.

(15)

The behavior of the center of gravity is derived in the next proposition.

Proposition 1.

Assume that the cells in
\mathcal{C}
are free and isolated without external forces (
N_{0}=0
in (
8
)) such that

\dot{\mathbf{x}}_{i}=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{C}}\mathbf{f}_{ij},\;i\in\mathcal{C},\;t\geq 0.

(16)

Then the sum of the coordinates is constant when
t\geq 0

\sum_{i\in\mathcal{C}}\sum_{l=1}^{d}x_{il}(t)=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{C}}\sum_{l=1}^{d}x_{il}(0).

(17)

The center of gravity of free cells in (
15
) is independent of
t

\mathbf{x}_{G}(t)=\mathbf{x}_{G}(0).

(18)

Proof.
The equation (
16
) can be written

\dot{\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{H}\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{C}},

(19)

where
\mathbf{H}\in\mathbb{R}^{dN\times dN}
and
\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{C}}\in\mathbb{R}^{dN}
are defined by

\mathbf{H}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccccc}\mathbf{O}&\ldots&\mathbf{F}_{1j}&\ldots&\mathbf{F}_{1N}\\
&&\vdots&&\\
\mathbf{F}_{i1}&\ldots&\mathbf{O}&\ldots&\mathbf{F}_{iN}\\
&&\vdots&&\\
\mathbf{F}_{N1}&\ldots&\mathbf{F}_{Nj}&\ldots&\mathbf{O}\end{array}\right),\quad\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{C}k}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1,&k=d(i-1)+l,\\
&i\in\mathcal{C},\;l=1,\ldots,d,\\
0,&{\rm otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

The diagonal matrix
\mathbf{F}_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
consists of the
d
elements of
\mathbf{f}_{ij}
in the diagonal and
\mathbf{O}
is the
d\times d
zero matrix.
Since
\mathbf{F}_{ij}=-\mathbf{F}_{ji}
according to Newton’s third law,
\mathbf{H}
is antisymmetric and
\mathbf{y}^{T}\mathbf{H}\mathbf{y}=0
for any
\mathbf{y}
. Multiply (
16
) by
\mathbf{1}^{T}_{\mathcal{C}}
from the left to
obtain
\mathbf{1}^{T}_{\mathcal{C}}\dot{\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{1}^{T}_{\mathcal{C}}\mathbf{H}\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{C}}=0
. Thus,
\frac{d}{dt}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{C}}\sum_{l=1}^{d}x_{il}=0
and (
17
) follows.

Let
\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{C}k}^{l}=1
when
k=d(i-1)+l,\;\mathcal{C}\in\{1,2,\ldots,N\},\;l=1,2,\;{\rm or}\;3
. Since

(\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{C}}^{l})^{T}\dot{\mathbf{x}}=\frac{d}{dt}\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{il}=(\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{C}}^{l})^{T}\mathbf{H}\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{C}}^{l}=0,

we have

x_{Gl}(t)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{il}(t)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{il}(0)=x_{Gl}(0),\quad l=1,\ldots,d.

\blacksquare

An arbitrary sum of coordinates is also preserved by the Euler methods in the next section.

2.5
Forces after cell division

At a cell proliferation in cell
i
, a new cell is introduced at a given distance from cell
i
. Proliferation at time
t_{p}
will make
V
discontinuous

V(t_{p}^{+})=V(t_{p}^{-})+\Delta V,\;\Delta V>0.

(20)

After
t_{p}^{+}
the cells will move toward an equilibrium as in Theorem 1.
Suppose that the system is at rest at
t_{p}^{-}
such that
r_{ij}=s
for all
i
and
j\in\mathcal{J}_{i}
. The set
\mathcal{J}_{i}
contains the indices of the close neighbors of cell
i
, i.e. if
j\in\mathcal{J}_{i}
then
r_{ij}<r_{A}
.
Let cell
i
divide into
i
and
N+1
. Then at
t_{p}^{+}
,
\Delta V
in (
20
) is

\Delta V=G(r_{i,N+1})+G(r_{N+1,i})+\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{i}}G(r_{ij})+\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{i}}G(r_{N+1,j}).

(21)

After proliferation of the cell at
\mathbf{x}_{i}
at time
t_{p}
as in (
20
), a new cell
N+1
is placed at
\mathbf{x}_{N+1}=\mathbf{x}_{i}-\delta\mathbf{r}
and cell
i
is moved a short distance to
\mathbf{x}_{i}+\delta\mathbf{r}
at
t_{p}^{+}
.
Then
\mathbf{r}_{i,N+1}=-2\delta\mathbf{r}
and
r_{i,N+1}=2\|\delta\mathbf{r}\|_{2}=2\delta r
. Assume that the cells were in equilibrium before the proliferation with vanishing forces as in (
21
). The distances
r_{ij}
between cell centers are then equal to
s
in the force model in (
3
) and (
4
).
Furthermore, assume that the distance
2\delta r
between the two new cells is short compared to the distances
r_{ij}
to the other cells.
Then the forces between cells
i
and
N+1
and the other cells in
\mathcal{J}_{i}
in the sums in (
8
) after
t_{p}
are to first order in
\delta r
according to (
11
) and (
12
)

\begin{array}[]{rl}\dot{\mathbf{x}}_{i}&=\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{i,N+1}g(2\delta r)+\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{i}}\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{ij}g(r_{ij})+\mathbf{A}^{ij}\delta\mathbf{r}\\
&=\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{i,N+1}g(2\delta r)-\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{i}}\mathbf{A}^{ij}\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{i,N+1}\delta r,\\
\dot{\mathbf{x}}_{N+1}&=-\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{i,N+1}g(2\delta r)+\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{i}}\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{ij}g(r_{ij})-\mathbf{A}^{ij}\delta\mathbf{r}\\
&=-\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{i,N+1}g(2\delta r)+\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{i}}\mathbf{A}^{ij}\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{i,N+1}\delta r.\end{array}

(22)

Since
|g(2\delta r)|
increases with the force function in (
4
) and the sums in (

) decreases when
\delta r
is reduced, the forces in the system are dominated by the force between the two cells involved in the proliferation. This dominance increases the smaller the initial distance
2\delta r
is between the proliferating cells.

3
Numerical methods for adaptive time integration

The ODE system (
9
) is solved by the forward Euler and the backward Euler methods for approximations
\mathbf{x}^{n}
of
\mathbf{x}(t^{n})
at the time points
t^{n+1}=t^{n}+\Delta t,\;n=0,1,2,\ldots,
with
t^{0}=0
.
The global time step for all cells is
\Delta t
in a single rate algorithm and the initial positions at
t^{0}
are
\mathbf{x}^{0}
. The length of the time step is chosen adaptively. Parts of the cell system are integrated by shorter time steps in the multirate algorithm in order to improve the efficiency in particular after proliferation.

The solution is advanced in time by the forward Euler method in

\mathbf{x}^{n+1}=\mathbf{x}^{n}+\Delta t\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x}^{n}).

(23)

The equation is frame invariant as in (
10
). By multiplying with
\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{C}}
as in the proof of the proposition in Section
2.4
in a case with
N_{0}=0
, we find that the coordinate
vector and the center of gravity are conserved by the Euler forward method

\sum_{i\in\mathcal{C}}\sum_{l=1}^{d}x_{il}^{n}=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{C}}\sum_{l=1}^{d}x_{il}^{0},\quad\mathbf{x}_{G}^{n}=\mathbf{x}_{G}^{0},

(24)

cf. (
17
). If
\mathbf{x}^{n}\in\mathcal{D}
, the space of stationary solutions, then
\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x}^{n})=0
and
\mathbf{x}^{n+1}=\mathbf{x}^{n}\in\mathcal{D}
in (
23
). When
\mathbf{x}^{n}
has entered
\mathcal{D}
, it stays there.

3.1
Single rate time stepping with the forward Euler method

The time steps in this single rate method are controlled such that an accuracy requirement is satisfied in the max norm
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
and stability is monitored in the Euclidean norm
\|\cdot\|_{2}
.

3.1.1
Time step selection

The leading term in the local error in every time step is at
t^{n}

\mathbf{e}^{n+1}=\mathbf{x}(t^{n+1})-\mathbf{x}^{n+1}=\frac{1}{2}\Delta t^{2}\ddot{\mathbf{x}}^{n}=\frac{1}{2}\Delta t^{2}\dot{\mathbf{F}}(\mathbf{x}^{n})=\frac{1}{2}\Delta t^{2}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x}^{n}),

(25)

proportional to the acceleration of the cells
\ddot{\mathbf{x}}
. When the acceleration vanishes any
\Delta t
will result in a small error.

A numerical approximation of
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{F}
in (
25
) is obtained by one extra evaluation of
\mathbf{F}
in

\mathbf{A}\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x}^{n})\approx\frac{1}{\epsilon}(\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x}^{n}+\epsilon\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x}^{n}))-\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x}^{n})).

(26)

The parameter
\epsilon
is small. A discussion how to find it is found in
[
18
]
.

Let the error tolerance be
\varepsilon
. Then
\Delta t
is chosen such that the max norm of the local error estimate in each time step in (
25
) is below this tolerance

\frac{1}{2}\Delta t^{2}\|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x}^{n})\|_{\infty}\leq\varepsilon.

(27)

The error in each cell coordinate in every time step due to the discretization is then less than
\varepsilon
. The bound on the global time step is then

\Delta t\leq\sqrt{\frac{2\varepsilon}{\|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{F}\|_{\infty}}}.

(28)

With
g
in (
4
) and by (
8
) and (
12
), we observe that both
\mathbf{A}\propto\mu
and
\mathbf{F}\propto\mu
.
Hence,
\Delta t\propto\mu^{-1}
in (
28
).

When a limited displacement is allowed in each time step as in
[
2
,
17
,
21
,
36
]
, then
\Delta t
satisfies

\|\Delta t\dot{\mathbf{x}}\|_{\infty}=\Delta t\|\mathbf{F}\|_{\infty}\leq\varepsilon.

(29)

If
\|\mathbf{F}\|_{\infty}
is small then
\Delta t
will be long. This may result in a large error in (
26
) if
\|\ddot{\mathbf{x}}\|_{\infty}
is not small simultaneously. If
\|\mathbf{F}\|_{\infty}
is large but
\|\ddot{\mathbf{x}}\|_{\infty}
is small, then unnecessarily short steps are chosen with (
29
).

The procedure is summarized in Algorithm
1
.

Input:

Right-hand side
F
of Equation (
9
) defining the ODE system, start time
t^{0}
, final time
T
, initial coordinates
\textbf{x}^{0}
, absolute accuracy
\varepsilon
, approximation parameter for Jacobian-force product
\epsilon

Initialize
t=t^{0}
;
\textbf{x}=\textbf{x}^{0}
;

while

t<T

do

Evaluate force for current coordinates
\hat{\textbf{F}}=\textbf{F}(\textbf{x})
;

Approximate Jacobian-force product by
\textbf{AF}=\frac{1}{\epsilon}\left(\textbf{F}(\textbf{x}+\epsilon\,\hat{\textbf{F}})-\hat{\textbf{F}}\right)
;

Calculate time step size
\Delta t=\sqrt{\frac{2\,\varepsilon}{\|\textbf{AF}\|_{\infty}}}
;

Update
\textbf{x}\leftarrow\textbf{x}+\Delta t\,\hat{\textbf{F}}
;
t\leftarrow t+\Delta t
;

end while

Algorithm 1

Single rate forward Euler method (SRFE)

3.1.2
Explicit consideration of the stability bound

The difference between
\mathbf{x}^{n}
and
\mathbf{x}^{n+1}
fulfills

\begin{array}[]{rll}\mathbf{x}^{n+1}-\mathbf{x}^{n}&=\mathbf{x}^{n}-\mathbf{x}^{n-1}+\Delta t(\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x}^{n})-\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x}^{n-1}))\\
&=\mathbf{x}^{n}-\mathbf{x}^{n-1}+\Delta t\tilde{\mathbf{A}}(\mathbf{x}^{n}-\mathbf{x}^{n-1})\\
&=(\mathbf{I}+\Delta t\tilde{\mathbf{A}})(\mathbf{x}^{n}-\mathbf{x}^{n-1}).\end{array}

(30)

The matrix
\tilde{\mathbf{A}}
depends on
\mathbf{x}^{n-1},\mathbf{x}^{n},
and
\boldsymbol{\theta}
with components
\theta_{k},\;k=1,\ldots,dN,
between 0 and 1.
By the mean value theorem, each row
k
of
\tilde{\mathbf{A}}
is evaluated as
\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}F_{k}(\theta_{k}\mathbf{x}^{n-1}+(1-\theta_{k})\mathbf{x}^{n})
.

The symmetric
\mathbf{A}
in (
12
) has a factorization

\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{Q}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\mathbf{Q}^{T}=(\mathbf{Q}_{+},\mathbf{Q}_{-})\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{+}&0\\
0&\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{-}\end{array}\right)(\mathbf{Q}_{+},\mathbf{Q}_{-})^{T}.

(31)

The positive eigenvalues of
\mathbf{A}
are in the diagonal of
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{+}
and the non-positive eigenvalues in the diagonal of
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{-}
. The square, orthonormal eigenvector matrix
\mathbf{Q}
is partitioned into
(\mathbf{Q}_{+},\mathbf{Q}_{-})
corresponding to the partitioning of the diagonal eigenvalue matrix
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
.

In the difference between successive solutions in (
30
),
\tilde{\mathbf{A}}
is approximated by
\mathbf{A}
at
\mathbf{x}^{n}
. If
\Delta t
is small then the difference
\tilde{\mathbf{A}}-\mathbf{A}
is small. For the components corresponding to non-positive eigenvalues, we have

\begin{array}[]{rl}\mathbf{Q}_{-}^{T}(\mathbf{x}^{n+1}-\mathbf{x}^{n})&=\mathbf{Q}_{-}^{T}(\mathbf{I}+\Delta t\mathbf{A})(\mathbf{x}^{n}-\mathbf{x}^{n-1})\\
&=(\mathbf{I}+\Delta t\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{-})\mathbf{Q}_{-}^{T}(\mathbf{x}^{n}-\mathbf{x}^{n-1}).\end{array}

(32)

A stability requirement on the numerical integration is that

\|\mathbf{Q}_{-}^{T}(\mathbf{x}^{n+1}-\mathbf{x}^{n})\|_{2}\leq\|\mathbf{Q}_{-}^{T}(\mathbf{x}^{n}-\mathbf{x}^{n-1})\|_{2}

(33)

in the Euclidean norm.
A sufficient condition for stability in (
33
) is then that
\Delta t
is such that

\|\mathbf{I}+\Delta t\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{-}\|_{2}\leq 1.

(34)

The equivalent condition on
\Delta t
is that for all non-positive eigenvalues
\lambda_{k-}
of
\mathbf{A}
in
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{-}

-2\leq\Delta t\lambda_{k-}\leq 0.

(35)

Either
\Delta t
is restricted by the stability constraint in
\ell_{2}
(
35
) or the accuracy constraint in
\ell_{\infty}
(
28
).

The eigenvalues of
\mathbf{A}
in the stability bound in (
35
) can be estimated by Gershgorin’s theorem
[
20
, 7.2.1]
. The eigenvalues are located in the union of the intervals
[\xi_{k}-\rho_{k},\xi_{k}+\rho_{k}],\,k=1,\ldots,dN,

where

\xi_{k}=A_{kk}=-\sum_{j=1,j\neq i}^{N}A^{ij}_{ll},\quad\rho_{k}=\sum_{m=1,m\neq k}^{dN}|A_{km}|,

(36)

according to (
12
). The indices in (
36
) are related by
k=d(i-1)+l,\;i=1,\ldots,N,\;l=1,\ldots,d
.

The leftmost eigenvalue
\lambda_{L}=\min_{k}\lambda_{k}(\mathbf{A})<0
constrains
\Delta t
in (
35
) such that
\Delta t\leq 2/|\lambda_{L}|
and
can be estimated by
\min_{k}\xi_{k}-\rho_{k}\leq\lambda_{L}(\mathbf{A})
with a tighter bound
2/|\min_{k}\xi_{k}-\rho_{k}|\leq 2/|\lambda_{L}|
on
\Delta t
.
Some eigenvalues lie on the positive real axis corresponding to growing modes.
There the accuracy puts a bound on
\Delta t
such that the local error in each step in (
25
)
is less than some
\varepsilon
.

Gershgorin’s estimate in (
36
) depends on the number of non-zero terms
A_{km}
. That number depends on the number of neighboring cells in
\mathcal{J}_{i}
. Its maximum
N_{d}
in a cell system depends on
d
but is independent of
N
when
N
is large.
In a crowded aggregation in 2D, the maximum number of circles of equal size touching a circle in the middle is six (the kissing number
[
5
]
). At most twelve surrounding spheres touch a sphere in the middle in 3D.
The elements
A_{km}
depend on the direction angles of
\hat{\mathbf{r}}
,
g^{\prime}(r)
, and
g(r)/r
.
In general, the Gershgorin estimate of the minimal eigenvalue
\min_{k}\xi_{k}-\rho_{k}
decreases when the dimension increases because of an increasing number of non-zero terms in the sums.

The eigenvalue of largest modulus of the Jacobian matrix
\mathbf{A}
is estimated in
[
14
,
32
]
in numerical methods for ODEs where several, successive evaluations of
\mathbf{F}
are available in each step mimicking a power iteration. With the forward Euler method we have only a few evaluations at our disposal.

Algorithm
2
is the integration method with a global time step adapted to the solution as in Algorithm
1
but with a stability constraint.

For certain geometries of the cell system, the eigenvalues are available explicitly. Consider a Cartesian configuration of cells in dD with
N
cells in each coordinate direction with
\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{ij}
pointing along the coordinate axes.
The system is close to steady state.
Then in 3D we have
\theta=-\frac{\pi}{2},0,\frac{\pi}{2}
, and for
\theta=0
we let
\phi=0,\frac{\pi}{2},\pi,\frac{3\pi}{2}
in (
2
) and in 2D

with the same
\phi
as in 3D. The outer layer of cells has fixed positions.
Linearize around the equilibrium
r_{ij}=s
to obtain
\mathbf{A}
.
The coordinates in
\mathbf{x}
are ordered such that all
x
coordinates are first, then all
y
coordinates come, and finally in 3D the
z
coordinates.
Then
\mathbf{A}
consists of
d
tridiagonal submatrices
g^{\prime}(s)\mathbf{B}
on the diagonal, one for each coordinate. Each
\mathbf{B}
has
N^{(d-1)}
tridiagonal matrices
\mathbf{B}_{0}
on
the diagonal where
\mathbf{B}_{0}
has
-2
on the diagonal and

on the subdiagonal and the superdiagonal.
The eigenvalues of
\mathbf{B}_{0}
(and
\mathbf{B}
) are
\lambda_{j}=-2(1-\cos(j\pi/(N+1)))<0,\;j=1,2,\ldots,N
. The eigenvalue of
\mathbf{A}
of largest modulus is therefore for large

\lambda_{N}=\left(-2+2\cos(\frac{N\pi}{N+1})\right)g^{\prime}(s)\approx\left(-4+\frac{\pi^{2}}{(N+1)^{2}}\right)g^{\prime}(s).

(37)

The estimate with Gershgorin’s theorem is
\lambda_{N}=-4g^{\prime}(s)
which is the limit in (
37
) for large
N
. The time step restriction for stability in (
35
) is then
\Delta t\leq 1/(2g^{\prime}(s))
.
The eigenvalue of smallest modulus and closest to zero is

\lambda_{1}=-2\left(1-\cos(\frac{\pi}{N+1})\right)g^{\prime}(s)\approx-\frac{\pi^{2}}{(N+1)^{2}}g^{\prime}(s).

(38)

of Equation (

) defining the ODE system, start time

, final time

, initial coordinates

, absolute accuracy
\varepsilon
, Jacobian

of

3.1.3
Restriction on the time step size after cell proliferation

The linearization matrix
\mathbf{A}
after proliferation as in (
22
) consists of a matrix
\mathbf{A}_{0}
with contribution from all forces except for the force between the divided cell
i
and the new cell
N+1
and a matrix
\mathbf{A}_{p}
such that
\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{A}_{0}+\mathbf{A}_{p}
.
Then the non-zero submatrices of the proliferation matrix
\mathbf{A}_{p}
are

\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}-\mathbf{A}^{i,N+1}&\mathbf{A}^{i,N+1}\\
\mathbf{A}^{N+1,i}&-\mathbf{A}^{N+1,i}\end{array}\right).

(39)

At cell proliferation in (
8
) with
\mathbf{n}=\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{i,N+1}
and

),
\mathbf{F}_{i}\approx\mathbf{n}g(r_{0})

\mathbf{F}_{N+1}\approx-\mathbf{n}g(r_{0})

is small. Moreover,

\mathbf{A}^{i,N+1}=\mathbf{A}^{N+1,i}=\mathbf{n}\mathbf{n}^{T}g^{\prime}(r_{0})+(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{n}\mathbf{n}^{T})g(r_{0})/r_{0}

in (

(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{F})_{i}\approx-2\mathbf{n}g^{\prime}(r_{0})g(r_{0}),\;(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{F})_{N+1}\approx 2\mathbf{n}g^{\prime}(r_{0})g(r_{0})

and

\mathbf{A}_{0}

When

3.2
Multirate adaptive time stepping with the forward Euler method

The time steps are chosen differently in different parts of the cell system in this multirate method. They satisfy an accuracy bound (
28
) locally with small steps close to a proliferation and larger steps in quiescent parts of the system. In addition, they also satisfy the stability bound in (
35
).

3.2.1
Time step selection

Let the acceleration in the leading term in the local error in (
25
) be denoted by
\boldsymbol{\eta}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{F}
and let
\mathcal{K}
be the set of equations
\mathcal{K}=\{1,2,\ldots,dN\}
.
Introduce the two subsets
\mathcal{K}_{\kappa},\,\kappa=0,1,
and the time steps
\Delta\tau_{\kappa}
for all equations in each
\mathcal{K}_{\kappa}
. The method is generally applicable but is suitable for ODE systems where the error estimate requires small time steps occasionally and for a limited number of equations. The sets
\mathcal{K}_{\kappa}
are disjunct and cover
\mathcal{K}
,
\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{K}_{0}\bigcup\mathcal{K}_{1}
. The relation between the time steps is chosen to be

\Delta\tau_{1}=m\Delta\tau_{0},

(45)

with an integer
m>1
such that
\Delta\tau_{0}<\Delta\tau_{1}
. The coordinate of a cell with an equation
k
in
\mathcal{K}_{\kappa}
is advanced in time by
\Delta\tau_{\kappa}
. After
m
steps with
\Delta\tau_{0}
in
\mathcal{K}_{0}
and one step with
\Delta\tau_{1}
in
\mathcal{K}_{1}
from
t^{n}
, all cell coordinates have reached the same
t^{n+1}=t^{n}+\Delta\tau_{1}
. Let
\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\kappa}=\{\eta_{k}|k\in\mathcal{K}_{\kappa}\}
. Then the local errors
\mathbf{e}_{\kappa}
in
\mathcal{K}_{\kappa}
in this step are by (
25
) approximately

Algorithm 4

Single rate backward Euler method (SRBE)

Assume that there is no proliferation and large time steps are possible to satisfy the accuracy requirements. The computational work per time step for the forward Euler method SRFE in Algorithm
1
and the backward Euler method SRBE in Algorithm
4
are denoted by
W_{\rm SRFE}
and
W_{\rm SRBE}
and are measured by the number of evaluations of
\mathbf{F}
.
The maximum time step for backward Euler is
\Delta t_{a}
given by accuracy and the maximum time step for forward Euler due to stability is
\Delta t_{s}
.
The time step for accuracy is the same for both forward and backward Euler.

Let
k_{N}
be the number of steps in the Newton iteration and let
k_{G}
be the number of iterations in GMRES to solve (
67
).
The work to compute
\mathbf{A}
and multiplication by
\mathbf{A}
in GMRES is assumed to be about the same as one evaluation of
\mathbf{F}
.
Thus, there are the equivalent to
k_{NG}=k_{N}(k_{G}+2)
evaluations of
\mathbf{F}
in the iterations in one time step of SRBE. Assuming
\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x}^{n})
to be known, one evaluation is needed to estimate the local error in (
26
) and one to compute
\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x}^{n+1})
in SRFE. Then

W_{\rm SRFE}=2,\quad W_{\rm SRBE}=k_{NG}+3=k_{N}(k_{G}+2)+3,

and the total work in an interval
[0,T]
with constant time steps is

W_{\rm SRFE,tot}=\frac{2T}{\Delta t_{s}},\quad W_{\rm SRBE,tot}=\frac{(k_{NG}+3)T}{\Delta t_{a}}.

Then backward Euler is the best choice if
W_{\rm SRBE,tot}<W_{\rm SRFE,tot}
, i.e. if

\frac{k_{N}(k_{G}+2)+3}{2}<\frac{\Delta t_{a}}{\Delta t_{s}}.

(77)

As an example, take 3 Newton iterations and 3 GMRES iterations in each Newton step. Then backward Euler is more efficient if
{\Delta t_{a}}/{\Delta t_{s}}>9
. If
\Delta t_{a}/\Delta t_{s}<1

then forward Euler is always the preferred method.

5
Summary and Conclusions

Center-based models for the forces between biological cells are analyzed and numerical methods for simulation of the models are proposed and compared. The analytical equations form a system of ODEs of first order.
The forces are obtained as the gradient of a potential. The numerical methods are developed for center-based models for cell simulations but are generally applicable to gradient systems of ODEs (
9
), for example vertex-based models of the cell forces, and to other force functions than (
4
).

The equations are approximated by the forward Euler and backward Euler methods of first order accuracy.
The backward Euler method has similar stability properties as the analytical problem with a solution converging to an equilibrium which is constant in time.
The equilibrium is not unique but depends on the initial conditions.
If the solution reaches an equilibrium solution it remains there in the analytical solution and the Euler solutions.
The center of gravity is constant in the analytical and numerical solutions.
The eigenvalues of the Jacobian matrix of the forces are real. Close to an equilibrium point they are non-positive, but at least after cell proliferation there are positive eigenvalues.

The local error in a time step is estimated. An error bound or a stability bound determines the time step for the forward Euler method.
The eigenvalues of the Jacobian matrix are estimated for the stability bound. Only the local error bounds the time step for the backward Euler method.
A multirate time stepping method is proposed. The positions of some cells are integrated by shorter time steps while longer time steps are taken for the majority of the cells. A bound on the global discretization error is derived for the forward Euler method using the bound on the local error.
The performance of four methods with time step adaptation are compared in numerical experiments: the single rate forward Euler method with and without a stability check (SRFES and SRFE), the multirate forward Euler method with local time steps (MRFE), and the single rate backward Euler method (SRBE).

We have shown that Algorithm I, the single rate forward Euler method, leads to a strong decrease in computational cost — a reduction of 70% to 90% in terms of wall time for the simulation of a linearly growing spheroid— compared to a fixed time stepping forward Euler method that uses the same initial time step (restricted by strong repulsive forces between daughter cells after proliferation) at the cost of a slightly decreased accuracy.
There are oscillations in the time step sequence and the global errors but at a low level. We hence propose the SRFE algorithm for general use since it is easy to implement, eliminates the need to manually determine the correct time step size, and offers a more intuitive absolute error in each time step on the spatial scale instead.

If accuracy is of a higher concern, our results confirm that unless cell division on the population level happens very much faster than the mechanical relaxation between two individual daughter cells, both Algorithms II (SRFES) and III (MRFE) beat the fixed time stepping algorithm in terms of wall time and computational cost. Note that our findings are mostly independent of the exact system size.
The exact value of how much time needs to pass between cell division events at the population level so that the overhead of either algorithm pays off is of course implementation dependent. For our results it lies at roughly
0.5\tau_{\text{relax}}
. Typical cell cycle durations depend strongly on the cell type, for human cells 22-24 hours are common
[
6
]
. On the other hand the mechanical relaxation time can be considered to be on the order of a few minutes or even less. Consequently, there will be a range of population sizes for which the cell division frequencies we consider here are applicable.
An alternative to the Euler methods is to upgrade the order of accuracy with an explicit Runge-Kutta method of order two. Theorem 2 is still valid and the error estimate in (
26
) should be changed to an estimate of
\text{d}^{3}\mathbf{x}/\text{d}t^{3}
.

From Figures
1
,
3
and
6
we see that the SRBE method in Algorithm IV is able to take longer steps while the other three algorithms are restricted by stability concerns, which for both configurations (i) and (ii) occurs shortly after the relaxation time. Nevertheless, the SRBE method achieves its improved stability properties at an increased computational cost per step.
It is therefore not straightforward to know if there is a gain using the SRBE method in terms of a reduced wall clock time for the simulation as discussed in Section
3.3
. In fact, in
[
2
,
29
]
it was shown for different population configurations that the performance of a fixed time stepping backward Euler method was not better than a fixed time stepping forward Euler method.
In this study, we showed that using adaptive time stepping for the backward Euler method makes it more efficient than a fixed time stepping forward Euler method when cell proliferation is sufficiently rare and the system spends long duration limited by stability. However, SRBE is less efficient than the adaptive time stepping methods based on the forward Euler method for the cell proliferation frequencies we consider.

The efficiency of the algorithms depends to some extent on the implementation of them. There is a large difference in computational cost between the algorithms that rely on explicit calculation of the stability bound (SRFES and MRFE) and SRFE that does not. It is expensive to assemble the Jacobian necessary to calculate or estimate its eigenvalues which determine the stability bound. In our Python implementation we assemble the complete Jacobian in order to benefit as much as possible from NumPy’s routines. It is, however, possible —and highly advisable for a compiled language such as C/C++— to apply Gershgorin’s estimate by only calculating relative parts of the Jacobian on the fly. Similarly, the computational cost can be reduced by not recalculating the Jacobian (or Gershgorin’s estimate) unnecessarily once the system is in a steady state, but “freezing” it until the next cell proliferation happens. These modifications would increase the gain of SRFES and MRFE compared to fixed time stepping.

Other possibilities to extend our work include its application to a variant of the center-based model where the neighborhood definition is based on the Voronoi tesselation given by the cell midpoints
[
22
,
30
]
.
In this case, cells only interact with direct neighbors sharing an edge in the Voronoi tesselation. Movement of the cells and resulting rearrangement of neighbors potentially leads to discontinuous changes in the right hand side of the ODE system but this should not cause any problems for the adaptive algorithms based on the Euler method.
