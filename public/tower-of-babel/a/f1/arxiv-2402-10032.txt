Dimension-free Structured Covariance Estimation
Nikita Puchkin, Maxim Rakhuba

Abstract
Given a sample of i.i.d. high-dimensional centered random vectors, we consider a problem of estimation of their covariance matrix Σ \Sigma with an additional assumption that Σ \Sigma can be represented as a sum of a few Kronecker products of smaller matrices. Under mild conditions, we derive the first non-asymptotic dimension-free high-probability bound on the Frobenius distance between Σ \Sigma and a widely used penalized permuted least squares estimate. Because of the hidden structure, the established rate of convergence is faster than in the standard covariance estimation problem.



keywords
Kronecker rank, effective rank, rearrangement operator, penalized permuted least squares estimate.

Introduction

Let
{\mathbf{X}},{\mathbf{X}}_{1},\dots,{\mathbf{X}}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be i.i.d. centered random vectors. We are interested in estimation of the covariance matrix
\Sigma=\mathbb{E}{\mathbf{X}}{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}
from observations
{\mathbf{X}}_{1},\dots,{\mathbf{X}}_{n}
. This classical problem is of a great significance and it arises in various domains such as wireless communications
(
Werner and Jansson 2007
)
, economics and finance
(
Bai and Shi 2011
)
, biology and medicine (for example, functional MRI
(
Derado et al. 2010
)
, genomics
(
Xie and Bentler 2003
)
, MEG/EEG
(
De Munck et al. 2004
)
). A standard approach to this problem is to consider the sample covariance

\widehat{\Sigma}=\frac{1}{n}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}{\mathbf{X}}_{i}{\mathbf{X}}_{i}^{\top}.

Unfortunately, if the number of available samples
n
is much less than the ambient dimension
d
(which is often the case in practical scenarios), the estimate
\widehat{\Sigma}
suffers from the curse of dimensionality. To overcome this issue, researchers have explored leveraging structural properties of covariance matrices to reduce their total number of degrees of freedom. This includes assumptions on the covariance matrix like sparsity
(
Banerjee et al. 2008
;
Ravikumar et al. 2011
)
, low-rank
(
Fan et al. 2008
)
, Toeplitz
(
Fuhrmann 1991
)
and bandable model
(
Bickel and Levina 2008a
;
Bickel and Levina 2008b
)
. A reader is referred to the survey
(
Cai et al. 2016
)
for further examples. In situations when data can be represented as tensors, a popular modelling assumption is based on expansion of
\Sigma
into a Kronecker product of two smaller matrices
(
Wang et al. 2022
)
:

\Sigma=\Phi\otimes\Psi,\quad\text{where $\Phi\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}$, $\Psi\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times q}$, and $pq=d$.}

(1)

Each of the matrices
\Phi,\Psi
corresponds to a certain index (mode) of the multivariate array and can be interpreted as a mode-wise covariance. For instance, when researchers deal with spatio-temporal data, they use Kronecker product to separate spatial and temporal dimensions. Despite its simplicity, the Kronecker product model (
1
) appears to be useful in various applications
(
Werner and Jansson 2007
;
Werner et al. 2008
;
Allen and Tibshirani 2010
;
Guggenberger et al. 2023
)
.
In the present paper, we are concerned with a more expressive model that approximates the covariance matrix as a sum of Kronecker products:

\Sigma=\Phi_{1}\otimes\Psi_{1}+\dots+\Phi_{K}\otimes\Psi_{K},

(2)

where
\Phi_{1},\Psi_{1},\dots,\Phi_{K},\Psi_{K}
are symmetric positive semidefinite matrices, such that
\Phi_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
,
\Psi_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times q}
for all
j\in\{1,\dots,K\}
and
pq=d
. In this case, one says that
\Sigma
has a
Kronecker rank
equal to
K
.
By summing multiple Kronecker products, the model can capture complex interactions and dependencies that may not be adequately represented by a single Kronecker product. We would like to note that any symmetric matrix of size
(pq\times pq)
admits an expansion of the form (
2
) with
K=\min\{p^{2},q^{2}\}
. However, even with a small number of Kronecker product terms, the model (
2
) has shown promising results in applications such as clutter covariance estimation for airborne STAP
(
Sun et al. 2018
)
, MEG/EEG
(
De Munck et al. 2002
;
De Munck et al. 2004
;
Bijma et al. 2005
)
, video modelling
(
Greenewald et al. 2013
)
, and SAR data analysis
(
Rucci et al. 2010
)
. Notably, accurate approximations with a small number of Kronecker product terms have been demonstrated for the Matérn family of covariances
(
Litvinenko et al. 2019
)
.

The rates of convergence in the covariance estimation problem with
\Sigma
of the form (
1
) or (
2
) were studied in numerous papers (see, for example,
Werner et al. 2008
;
Tsiligkaridis and Hero 2013
;
Zhou 2014
;
Leng and Pan 2018
;
Masak et al. 2022
). However, the existing state-of-the-art results are either dimension-dependent or based on unsuitable for high-dimensional setting assumptions. At the same time, the recent advances in covariance estimation
(
Bunea and Xiao 2015
;
Puchkin et al. 2023
;
Zhivotovskiy 2024
)
allow to establish non-asymptotic high-probability bounds on the Frobenius and operator norm of
(\widehat{\Sigma}-\Sigma)
in terms of the effective rank
\mathtt{r}(\Sigma)=\mathrm{Tr}(\Sigma)/\|\Sigma\|
under mild assumptions, completely eliminating dependence on the ambient dimension
d
. For example,
(
Bunea and Xiao 2015
, Proposition A.3)
prove that, for any
\delta\in(0,1)
, with probability at least
1-\delta
, we have

\left\|\widehat{\Sigma}-\Sigma\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}\lesssim\mathrm{Tr}(\Sigma)\left(\sqrt{\frac{\log(2/\delta)}{n}}\vee\frac{\log(2/\delta)}{n}\right).

(3)

Here and further in the paper, the sign
\lesssim
stands for an inequality up to an absolute multiplicative constant. This brings us to a question, whether it is possible to obtain sharper dimension-free bounds when
\Sigma
admits a Kronecker product expansion (
2
). The following motivating example gives a positive answer in the simple case
K=1
.

Example 1.1
.

Let
\Sigma
have a Kronecker rank one, that is, it admits a representation (
1
) for some
positive semidefinite matrices
\Phi\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
and
\Psi\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times q}
. Let
\mathbb{X}_{1},\dots,\mathbb{X}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times p}
be the matrices obtained by reshaping
1
1

1

More precisely, it holds that
\mathrm{\mathbf{vec}}(\mathbb{X}_{i})={\mathbf{X}}_{i}
for all
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, where
\mathrm{\mathbf{vec}}
is the vectorization operator, stacking the matrix columns together (see the definition in Appendix
A
).
of the vectors
{\mathbf{X}}_{1},\dots,{\mathbf{X}}_{n}
, respectively. Consider the estimates

\widehat{\Phi}=\frac{1}{n}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{X}_{i}^{\top}\mathbb{X}_{i}\quad\text{and}\quad\widehat{\Psi}=\frac{1}{n}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{X}_{i}\mathbb{X}_{i}^{\top}.

As shown by
Leng and Pan 2018
,
\widehat{\Phi}
and
\widehat{\Psi}
are unbiased estimates of
\mathrm{Tr}(\Psi)\Phi
and
\mathrm{Tr}(\Phi)\Psi
, respectively. If, for any vectors
{\mathbf{u}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
,
{\mathbf{v}}\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
and any
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, it holds that

\frac{1}{\mathrm{Tr}(\Psi)}\left\|{\mathbf{u}}^{\top}\mathbb{X}_{i}^{\top}\mathbb{X}_{i}{\mathbf{u}}\right\|_{\psi_{1}}\lesssim{\mathbf{u}}^{\top}\Phi{\mathbf{u}}\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{1}{\mathrm{Tr}(\Phi)}\left\|{\mathbf{v}}^{\top}\mathbb{X}_{i}\mathbb{X}_{i}^{\top}{\mathbf{v}}\right\|_{\psi_{1}}\lesssim{\mathbf{v}}^{\top}\Psi{\mathbf{v}},

where
\|\cdot\|_{\psi_{1}}
denotes the Orlicz norm (see the notation section below), then, similarly to the proof of Proposition A.3 from
(
Bunea and Xiao 2015
)
, we can show that

\left\|\widehat{\Phi}/\mathrm{Tr}(\Psi)-\Phi\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}\lesssim\mathrm{Tr}(\Phi)\left(\sqrt{\frac{\log(2/\delta)}{n}}\vee\frac{\log(2/\delta)}{n}\right)

and

\left\|\widehat{\Psi}/\mathrm{Tr}(\Phi)-\Psi\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}\lesssim\mathrm{Tr}(\Psi)\left(\sqrt{\frac{\log(2/\delta)}{n}}\vee\frac{\log(2/\delta)}{n}\right)

simultaneously on an event with probability at least
1-\delta
. The trace product
\mathrm{Tr}(\Phi)\mathrm{Tr}(\Psi)=\mathrm{Tr}(\Sigma)
can be easily estimated by
(\|{\mathbf{X}}_{1}\|^{2}+\dots+\|{\mathbf{X}}_{n}\|^{2})/n
. Then the estimate

\breve{\Sigma}=\frac{\widehat{\Phi}\otimes\widehat{\Psi}}{\frac{1}{n}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\|{\mathbf{X}}_{i}\|^{2}},

(4)

suggested by
Leng and Pan 2018
, satisfies the following high probability bound:

\displaystyle\left\|\breve{\Sigma}-\Sigma\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left(\|\Psi\|\mathrm{Tr}(\Phi)+\|\Phi\|\mathrm{Tr}(\Psi)\right)\left(\sqrt{\frac{\log(2/\delta)}{n}}\vee\frac{\log(2/\delta)}{n}\right)

\displaystyle=\|\Sigma\|\left(\mathtt{r}(\Phi)+\mathtt{r}(\Psi)\right)\left(\sqrt{\frac{\log(2/\delta)}{n}}\vee\frac{\log(2/\delta)}{n}\right),

where
\mathtt{r}(\Phi)=\mathrm{Tr}(\Phi)/\|\Phi\|
and
\mathtt{r}(\Psi)=\mathrm{Tr}(\Psi)/\|\Psi\|
, while (
3
) implies that

\displaystyle\left\|\widehat{\Sigma}-\Sigma\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\mathrm{Tr}(\Sigma)\left(\sqrt{\frac{\log(2/\delta)}{n}}\vee\frac{\log(2/\delta)}{n}\right)

\displaystyle=\|\Sigma\|\mathtt{r}(\Phi)\mathtt{r}(\Psi)\left(\sqrt{\frac{\log(2/\delta)}{n}}\vee\frac{\log(2/\delta)}{n}\right).

Hence, the Kronecker structural assumption helped us to significantly improve the upper bound (
3
) by replacing the product of effective ranks with their sum.

Unfortunately, the estimate
\breve{\Sigma}
from Example
1.1
significantly exploits the product structure (
1
) of
\Sigma
, which allows to estimate
\Phi
and
\Psi
separately. This approach has no direct extension on the case of larger Kronecker rank. For this reason, the case, when
\Sigma
has a form (
2
) with
K>1
, requires much more efforts.

Contribution.
Our main contribution is a sharper upper bound on the performance of a regularized permuted least squares estimate
\widetilde{\Sigma}
(defined in (
6
)), which is widely used for covariance estimation in the presence of a Kronecker product structure. Under reasonable assumptions on the distribution of
{\mathbf{X}},{\mathbf{X}}_{1},\dots,{\mathbf{X}}_{n}
, we show that, if
\Sigma
has a form (
2
), then

\left\|\widetilde{\Sigma}-\Sigma\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\lesssim K\left(\sum\limits_{j=1}^{K}\|\Phi_{j}\|\|\Psi_{j}\|\right)^{2}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{n}\max\limits_{1\leqslant j\leqslant K}\mathtt{r}(\Phi_{j})^{2}+\frac{1}{n}\max\limits_{1\leqslant j\leqslant K}\mathtt{r}(\Psi_{j})^{2}+\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{n}\right)

with probability at least
(1-\delta)
. To our knowledge, this is the first dimension-free upper bound, capturing the Kronecker structure of the population covariance.

2
Main results

This section contains the main result of this paper, but first, we have to introduce auxiliary definitions. One of the central objects when dealing with a covariance
\Sigma
of the form (
2
) is the rearrangement operator
\mathcal{R}
, defined below.

Definition 2.1

(rearrangement operator)
.

Let
M
be a matrix of size
pq\times pq
with a block structure

M=\begin{pmatrix}M(1,1)&\dots&M(1,p)\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
M(p,1)&\dots&M(p,p)\end{pmatrix},\quad\text{where $M(i,j)\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times q}$ for all $i,j\in\{1,\dots,p\}$.}

The rearrangement operator
\mathcal{R}:\mathbb{R}^{pq\times pq}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{p^{2}\times q^{2}}
is defined by the following identity: the
((j-1)p+i)
-th row of
\mathcal{R}(M)
is
\mathrm{\mathbf{vec}}(M(i,j))^{\top}
.

The importance of the rearrangement operator becomes clear from the properties formulated in the next proposition.

Proposition 2.2
.

The following holds.

•

\mathcal{R}
is a linear operator.

•

\mathcal{R}
defines an isometry between the Euclidean spaces
(\mathbb{R}^{pq\times pq},\|\cdot\|_{\mathrm{F}})
and
(\mathbb{R}^{p^{2}\times q^{2}},\|\cdot\|_{\mathrm{F}})
. As a consequence, there exists an inverse map
\mathcal{R}^{-1}
.

•

If
M=A\otimes B
, where
A\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
and
B\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times q}
, then
\mathcal{R}(M)=\mathrm{\mathbf{vec}}(A)\mathrm{\mathbf{vec}}(B)^{\top}
.

The first and the second properties follow immediately from the definition of
\mathcal{R}
. The third one is well-known in the literature (see, for instance, the proof of Theorem 1 in
(
Van Loan and Pitsianis 1993
)
). The rearrangement operator reduces our problem to low-rank matrix estimation. Indeed, if the covariance matrix
\Sigma
can be represented a sum of Kronecker products (
2
), then, due to Proposition
2.2
,

\mathcal{R}(\Sigma)=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{K}\mathcal{R}(\Phi_{j}\otimes\Psi_{j})=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{K}\mathrm{\mathbf{vec}}(\Phi_{j})\mathrm{\mathbf{vec}}(\Psi_{j})^{\top}.

In other words, if
\Sigma
has a Kronecker rank
K
, then
\mathcal{R}(\Sigma)
has the same algebraic rank. This observation allows us to construct estimates based on the singular value decomposition of
\mathcal{R}(\widehat{\Sigma})
, as in
(
Tsiligkaridis and Hero 2013
;
Masak et al. 2022
)
.
For instance,
Masak et al. 2022
considered an estimate based on
K
principal components of
\mathcal{R}(\widehat{\Sigma})
:

\widecheck{\Sigma}=\mathcal{R}^{-1}(\widecheck{R}),\quad\text{where}\quad\widecheck{R}=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{K}\widehat{\sigma}_{j}\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{j}\widehat{\mathbf{v}}_{j}^{\top},

(5)

where the values
\widehat{\sigma}_{1}\geqslant\dots\geqslant\widehat{\sigma}_{K}
and the vectors
\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{1},\widehat{\mathbf{v}}_{1},\dots,\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{K},\widehat{\mathbf{v}}_{K}
are defined from the singular value decomposition

\mathcal{R}(\widehat{\Sigma})=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{p^{2}\land q^{2}}\widehat{\sigma}_{j}\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{j}\widehat{\mathbf{v}}_{j}^{\top}.

Let us note that the estimate (
5
) solves the optimization problem

\widecheck{R}\in\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}\limits_{R\in\mathbb{R}^{p^{2}\times q^{2}}}\left\|R-\mathcal{R}(\widehat{\Sigma})\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}+\lambda\,\mathrm{rank}(R),\quad\text{where $\widehat{\sigma}_{K+1}^{2}\leqslant\lambda\leqslant\widehat{\sigma}_{K}^{2}$.}

In the present paper, we study the permuted least squares estimate suggested in
(
Tsiligkaridis and Hero 2013
)
, where the authors replaced
\mathrm{rank}(R)
by nuclear norm penalization:

\widetilde{\Sigma}=\mathcal{R}^{-1}(\widetilde{R}),\quad\text{where}\quad\widetilde{R}\in\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}\limits_{R\in\mathbb{R}^{p^{2}\times q^{2}}}\left\{\left\|R-\mathcal{R}(\widehat{\Sigma})\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}+\lambda\|R\|_{*}\right\}.

(6)

Similarly to
\widecheck{R}
, the estimate
\widetilde{R}
admits an explicit representation given by soft thresholding:

\widetilde{R}=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{p^{2}\land q^{2}}\big(\widehat{\sigma}_{j}-\lambda/2\big)_{+}\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{j}\widehat{\mathbf{v}}_{j}^{\top}.

Our goal is to establish a dimension-free upper bound on the Frobenuis norm of
(\widetilde{\Sigma}-\Sigma)
. Usually, results of such kind require some additional properties of the distribution of
{\mathbf{X}},{\mathbf{X}}_{1},\dots,{\mathbf{X}}_{n}
. We impose the following assumption.

Assumption 2.3
.

There exists
\omega>0
, such that the standardized random vector
\Sigma^{-1/2}{\mathbf{X}}
satisfies the inequality

\log\mathbb{E}\exp\left\{(\Sigma^{-1/2}{\mathbf{X}})^{\top}V(\Sigma^{-1/2}{\mathbf{X}})-\mathrm{Tr}(V)\right\}\leqslant\omega^{2}\|V\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}

(7)

for all
V\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
, such that
\|V\|_{\mathrm{F}}\leqslant 1/\omega
.

An equivalent condition appeared in the paper
(
Puchkin et al. 2023
)
, where the authors assumed that

\left\|(\Sigma^{-1/2}{\mathbf{X}})^{\top}V(\Sigma^{-1/2}{\mathbf{X}})-\mathrm{Tr}(V)\right\|_{\psi_{1}}\lesssim\|V\|_{\mathrm{F}}\quad\text{for all $V\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$.}

(8)

The fact that (
8
) yields (
7
) follows from, for instance,
(
Zhivotovskiy 2024
, Lemma 2)
. In
(
Puchkin et al. 2023
)
, the authors argued that
Assumption
2.3
holds for a large class of distributions. In particular, they discussed that it is fulfilled for all random vectors, satisfying the Hanson-Wright inequality. The examples include the Gaussian distribution
\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{0}},\Sigma)
and, more generally, all distributions, such that
\Sigma^{-1/2}{\mathbf{X}}
has independent components with finite
\psi_{2}
-norms. Besides, random vectors satisfying log-Sobolev inequality or having the convex concentration property
(
Adamczak 2015
)
also meet Assumption
2.3
. Since a covariance matrix with a Kronecker product structure (
2
) often appears in the context of matrix models, we would like to give relevant examples from this field. For instance, if we have a matrix model

\mathbb{X}=B\mathbb{Y}A^{\top},

where
B\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times q}
,
A\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
, and
\mathbb{Y}\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times p}
is a random matrix with i.i.d. standard Gaussian entries, then
\mathrm{\mathbf{vec}}(\mathbb{X})\sim\mathcal{N}\big({\boldsymbol{0}},(AA^{\top})\otimes(BB^{\top})\big)
and, consequently, it fulfils Assumption
2.3
. In the proposition below, we provide another, more general example when the assumption is satisfied.

Proposition 2.4
.

Let

\mathbb{X}=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{K}B_{j}\mathbb{Y}_{j}A_{j}^{\top},

(9)

where
\mathbb{Y}_{1},\dots,\mathbb{Y}_{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times p}
are independent centered random matrices. Assume that there exist positive numbers
\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{K}
, such that for any
j\in\{1,\dots,K\}

\log\mathbb{E}\exp\left\{\mathrm{\mathbf{vec}}(\mathbb{Y}_{j})^{\top}V\mathrm{\mathbf{vec}}(\mathbb{Y}_{j})-\mathrm{Tr}(V)\right\}\leqslant\omega_{j}^{2}\|V\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}

(10)

for all
V\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{2}\times d^{2}}
, satisfying the inequality
\|V\|_{\mathrm{F}}\leqslant 1/\omega_{j}
. Then
\mathrm{\mathbf{vec}}(\mathbb{X})
fulfils Assumption
2.3
with

\Sigma=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{K}(A_{j}A_{j}^{\top})\otimes(B_{j}B_{j}^{\top})\quad\text{and}\quad\omega\leqslant\left(2\omega_{\max}\vee 16\sqrt{\frac{2+2\omega_{\max}}{\log 2}}\right),

where
\omega_{\max}=\max\limits_{1\leqslant j\leqslant K}\omega_{j}
.

The proof of Proposition
2.4
is quite technical. For this reason, we postpone it to Appendix
B
and move to the main result.

Theorem 2.5
.

Assume that the centered random vector
{\mathbf{X}}
meets Assumption (
2.3
) and that its covariance
\Sigma
has a form (
2
). Let us fix any
\delta\in(0,1)
, satisfying the constraint

\frac{1}{2n}\left(\max\limits_{1\leqslant j\leqslant K}\mathtt{r}(\Phi_{j})^{2}+\max\limits_{1\leqslant j\leqslant K}\mathtt{r}(\Psi_{j})^{2}\right)+\frac{\log(4/\delta)}{n}\leqslant 1,

and take any
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, such that

\lambda\geqslant 2\omega\left(\sum\limits_{j=1}^{K}\|\Phi_{j}\|\|\Psi_{j}\|\right)\sqrt{\frac{13}{2n}\left(\max\limits_{1\leqslant j\leqslant K}\mathtt{r}(\Phi_{j})^{2}+\max\limits_{1\leqslant j\leqslant K}\mathtt{r}(\Psi_{j})^{2}\right)+\frac{13\log(4/\delta)}{n}}.

Then, with probability at least
(1-\delta)
, the permuted nuclear-norm penalized least squares estimate
\widetilde{\Sigma}
, defined in (
6
), fulfils the inequality

\left\|\widetilde{\Sigma}-\Sigma\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}<\frac{3\lambda^{2}K}{2}.

Remark 2.6
.

Theorem
2.5
requires a proper choice of
\lambda>0
. To tune the parameter, we can fix a finite family
\Lambda=\{\lambda_{0}\cdot 2^{-m}:0\leqslant m\leqslant M\}
, where
\lambda_{0}>0
and
M\in\mathbb{N}
are some constants, and perform model selection, using cross-validation or the random split procedure described in
(
Bickel and Levina 2008b
, Section 3)
. If the chosen
\lambda
meets the inequality

\lambda\leqslant C\omega\left(\sum\limits_{j=1}^{K}\|\Phi_{j}\|\|\Psi_{j}\|\right)\sqrt{\frac{13}{2n}\left(\max\limits_{1\leqslant j\leqslant K}\mathtt{r}(\Phi_{j})^{2}+\max\limits_{1\leqslant j\leqslant K}\mathtt{r}(\Psi_{j})^{2}\right)+\frac{13\log(4/\delta)}{n}}

with an absolute constant
C>2
, then, under the conditions of Theorem
2.5
, it holds that

\left\|\widetilde{\Sigma}-\Sigma\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\lesssim\omega^{2}K\left(\sum\limits_{j=1}^{K}\|\Phi_{j}\|\|\Psi_{j}\|\right)^{2}\left(\frac{1}{n}\max\limits_{1\leqslant j\leqslant K}\mathtt{r}(\Phi_{j})^{2}+\frac{1}{n}\max\limits_{1\leqslant j\leqslant K}\mathtt{r}(\Psi_{j})^{2}+\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{n}\right)

with probability at least
(1-\delta)
.

To our knowledge, Theorem
2.5
provides the first dimension-free high-probability upper bound on the accuracy of covariance estimation with a Kronecker product structure. The most relevant papers to ours are
(
Masak et al. 2022
)
and
(
Tsiligkaridis and Hero 2013
)
, where the authors considered the estimates
\widecheck{\Sigma}
and
\widetilde{\Sigma}
(see (
5
), (
6
)), based on the hard and soft thresholding, respectively. In
(
Masak et al. 2022
)
, the authors assumed only the existence of the fourth moment of
\|{\mathbf{X}}\|
and proper decay of the singular values of
\mathcal{R}(\Sigma)
. Under these mild conditions, they
proved the consistency of
\widecheck{\Sigma}
. Unfortunately, the established rate of convergence includes the Frobenius norm of
(\widehat{\Sigma}-\Sigma)
, which is of order
\mathtt{r}(\Sigma)/\sqrt{n}
in the worst-case scenario and, in contrast to Theorem
2.5
, shows no improvements compared to the unstructured case. In
(
Tsiligkaridis and Hero 2013
)
, the authors derived a non-asymptotic bound on the performance of the permuted least-squares estimate
\widetilde{\Sigma}
. Assuming the observations
{\mathbf{X}}_{1},\dots,{\mathbf{X}}_{n}
to have a Gaussian distribution
\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{0}},\Sigma)
, they showed that

\left\|\widetilde{\Sigma}-\Sigma\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\lesssim\frac{K(p^{2}+q^{2}+\log M)}{n}\vee\frac{K(p^{2}+q^{2}+\log M)^{2}}{n^{2}}

with probability at least
1-M^{-c}
, where
c>0
is an absolute constant. In our paper, we derive a sharper dimension-free bound for a broader class of distributions, which remains valid in the extremely high-dimensional setup.

Let us elaborate on implications of Theorem
2.5
in the simpler case
K=1
, which also got a considerable attention in the literature. In
(
Werner et al. 2008
)
, the authors focused on the Gaussian case and showed the consistency of the maximum likelihood estimate and of non-iterative flip-flop algorithm. Later,
Zhou 2014
suggested the Gemini method and obtained high-probability dimension-dependent bounds on the accuracy of covariance estimation, improving over the results of
Werner et al. 2008
. The Gemini estimator also exploits the fact that
{\mathbf{X}}_{1},\dots,{\mathbf{X}}_{n}
have the Gaussian distribution. In contrast,
Leng and Pan 2018
considered a much broader class of distributions with finite
48
-th moment. Under some technical assumptions, they derived a high-probability upper bound on the accuracy of covariance estimation in terms of both operator and Frobenius norms. Unfortunately, the analysis of
Zhou 2014
and
Leng and Pan 2018
imposes a severe restriction that the lowest eigenvalues of
\Phi
and
\Psi
from the decomposition (
1
) should be bounded away from zero. This makes their theoretical guarantees vacuous in the high-dimensional setting. On the other hand, if the Kronecker rank of
\Sigma
is equal to one, then Theorem
2.5
yields that

\left\|\widetilde{\Sigma}-\Sigma\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\lesssim\omega^{2}\|\Sigma\|^{2}\cdot\frac{\mathtt{r}(\Phi)^{2}+\mathtt{r}(\Psi)^{2}+\log(1/\delta)}{n}

with probability at least
(1-\delta)
. Note that this bound has a slightly better dependence on
\log(1/\delta)
, than the rate of convergence from Example
1.1
.

3
Proof of Theorem
2.5

The first step in the proof of the rate of convergence of the permuted least squares estimate
\widetilde{\Sigma}
is identical to the one of
Tsiligkaridis and Hero 2013
. With a proper choice of
\lambda
, we have the following oracle inequality.

Theorem 3.1

(
Tsiligkaridis and Hero 2013
, Theorem 2)
.

If
\lambda\geqslant 2\|\mathcal{R}(\widehat{\Sigma}-\Sigma)\|
, then

\left\|\widetilde{R}-\mathcal{R}(\Sigma)\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\leqslant\inf\limits_{R}\left\{\left\|R-\mathcal{R}(\Sigma)\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}+\frac{(1+\sqrt{2})^{2}}{4}\lambda^{2}\mathrm{rank}(R)\right\}.

(11)

Obviously, since
\Sigma
admits a representation (
2
), the rank of the matrix

\mathcal{R}(\Sigma)=\mathcal{R}\left(\sum\limits_{j=1}^{K}\Phi_{j}\otimes\Psi_{j}\right)=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{K}\mathcal{R}\left(\Phi_{j}\otimes\Psi_{j}\right)=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{K}\mathrm{\mathbf{vec}}(\Phi_{j})\mathrm{\mathbf{vec}}(\Psi_{j})^{\top}

(12)

is equal to
K
. In view of (
12
), the oracle inequality (
11
) can be rewritten in the form

\left\|\widetilde{R}-\mathcal{R}(\Sigma)\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\leqslant\frac{(1+\sqrt{2})^{2}\lambda^{2}K}{4},\quad\text{provided that $\lambda\geqslant 2\|\mathcal{R}(\widehat{\Sigma}-\Sigma)\|$.}

Our main technical result, leading to superior guarantees on the performance of
\widetilde{\Sigma}
is the following dimension-free bound on the operator norm of
\mathcal{R}(\widehat{\Sigma}-\Sigma)
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let Assumption
2.3
hold and let us fix any
\delta\in(0,1)
, satisfying the inequality

\frac{1}{2n}\left(\max\limits_{1\leqslant j\leqslant K}\mathtt{r}(\Phi_{j})^{2}+\max\limits_{1\leqslant j\leqslant K}\mathtt{r}(\Psi_{j})^{2}\right)+\frac{\log(4/\delta)}{n}\leqslant 1.

(13)

Then, with probability at least
(1-\delta)
, it holds that

\left\|\mathcal{R}(\widehat{\Sigma}-\Sigma)\right\|\leqslant\omega\left(\sum\limits_{j=1}^{K}\|\Phi_{j}\|\|\Psi_{j}\|\right)\sqrt{\frac{13}{2n}\left(\max\limits_{1\leqslant j\leqslant K}\mathtt{r}(\Phi_{j})^{2}+\max\limits_{1\leqslant j\leqslant K}\mathtt{r}(\Psi_{j})^{2}\right)+\frac{13\log(4/\delta)}{n}}.

We present the proof of Lemma
3.2
in Section
4
below. With Lemma
3.2
, we easily finish the proof of Theorem
2.5
. Let
\lambda
be any number, satisfying the inequality

\lambda\geqslant 2\omega\left(\sum\limits_{j=1}^{K}\|\Phi_{j}\|\|\Psi_{j}\|\right)\sqrt{\frac{13}{2n}\left(\max\limits_{1\leqslant j\leqslant K}\mathtt{r}(\Phi_{j})^{2}+\max\limits_{1\leqslant j\leqslant K}\mathtt{r}(\Psi_{j})^{2}\right)+\frac{13\log(4/\delta)}{n}}.

Then, according to Lemma
3.2
,
\lambda\geqslant 2\|\mathcal{R}(\widehat{\Sigma}-\Sigma)\|
on an event of probability at least
(1-\delta)
. Hence, on this event, it holds that

\left\|\widetilde{\Sigma}-\Sigma\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}=\left\|\widetilde{R}-\mathcal{R}(\Sigma)\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\leqslant\frac{(1+\sqrt{2})^{2}\lambda^{2}K}{4}=\frac{(3+2\sqrt{2})\lambda^{2}K}{4}<\frac{3\lambda^{2}K}{2}.

Proof of Lemma
3.2

The present section is devoted to the proof of our main technical result. For convenience, we split it into several steps, deferring auxiliary derivations to Appendix.

Step 1: reduction to an empirical process.

We start with representing the operator norm of
\mathcal{R}(\widehat{\Sigma}-\Sigma)
in an appropriate form.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\mathbb{X},\mathbb{X}_{1},\dots,\mathbb{X}_{n}
be random matrices of size
(q\times p)
, such that
\mathrm{\mathbf{vec}}(\mathbb{X})={\mathbf{X}}
and
\mathrm{\mathbf{vec}}(\mathbb{X}_{i})={\mathbf{X}}_{i}
for all
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
. Then it holds that

\left\|\mathcal{R}(\widehat{\Sigma}-\Sigma)\right\|=\sup\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}U\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p},V\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times q}\\
\|U\|_{\mathrm{F}}=\|V\|_{\mathrm{F}}=1\end{subarray}}\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{Tr}\left(\mathbb{X}_{i}^{\top}V^{\top}\mathbb{X}_{i}U\right)-\mathbb{E}\mathrm{Tr}\left(\mathbb{X}^{\top}V^{\top}\mathbb{X}U\right)\right\}.

(14)

The proof of Lemma
4.1
is postponed to Appendix
C
. Now we are in position to use powerful tools from the empirical process theory to derive a dimension-free bound on the supremum in the right-hand side of (
14
). The main ingredient of our proof is the following PAC-Bayesian variational inequality (see, for instance,
(
Catoni and Giulini 2017
, Proposition 2.1)
).

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
{\mathbf{X}},{\mathbf{X}}_{1},\dots,{\mathbf{X}}_{n}
be i.i.d. random elements on a measurable space
{\mathcal{X}}
. Let
\Theta
be a parameter space equipped with a measure
\mu
(which is also referred to as prior). Let
f:{\mathcal{X}}\times\Theta\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. Then, with probability at least
1-\delta
, it holds that

\mathbb{E}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\sim\rho}\frac{1}{n}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}f({\mathbf{X}}_{i},{\boldsymbol{\theta}})\leqslant\mathbb{E}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\sim\rho}\log\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}}e^{f({\mathbf{X}},{\boldsymbol{\theta}})}+\frac{{\mathcal{KL}}(\rho,\mu)+\log(1/\delta)}{n}

simultaneously for all
\rho\ll\mu
.

Recently,
Zhivotovskiy 2024
used a similar approach to prove that the operator norm of
(\widehat{\Sigma}-\Sigma)
is of order
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\mathtt{r}(\Sigma)/n})
with high probability. Unfortunately, the rate of convergence
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\mathtt{r}(\Sigma)/n})
is suboptimal in our case, because of the additional structure of the population covariance. In the rest of the proof, we deduce a sharper high-probability upper bound

\|\mathcal{R}(\widehat{\Sigma}-\Sigma)\|\lesssim\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\left(\max\limits_{1\leqslant j\leqslant K}\mathtt{r}(\Phi_{j})^{2}+\max\limits_{1\leqslant j\leqslant K}\mathtt{r}(\Psi_{j})^{2}\right)},

leading to better guarantees on the accuracy of estimation of
\Sigma
.

Step 2: a variational inequality.
On this step, we specify the prior and posterior distributions in order to apply Lemma
4.2
to our problem. In our case, both
\mu
and
\rho
will be absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure, so we identify them with the corresponding densities. Let
\mu
be the density of a Gaussian measure on
\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}\times\mathbb{R}^{q\times q}

\mu(X,Y)=\frac{\alpha^{p^{2}/2}\beta^{q^{2}/2}}{(2\pi)^{p^{2}/2+q^{2}/2}}\exp\left\{-\frac{\alpha}{2}\|X\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}-\frac{\beta}{2}\|Y\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\right\},\quad\text{where $X\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}$, $Y\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times q}$,}

(15)

and

\alpha=\max\limits_{1\leqslant j\leqslant K}\mathtt{r}(\Phi_{j})^{2},\quad\beta=\max\limits_{1\leqslant j\leqslant K}\mathtt{r}(\Psi_{j})^{2}.

(16)

For any
U\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
and
V\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times q}
, define a set

\displaystyle\Upsilon(U,V)

\displaystyle=\Bigg\{(X,Y)\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}\times\mathbb{R}^{q\times q}:\left\|\Sigma^{1/2}\big((X-U)\otimes(Y-V)\big)\Sigma^{1/2}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\leqslant\frac{4\mathrm{Tr}(\Sigma)^{2}}{\alpha\beta},

\displaystyle\qquad\left\|\left(\sum\limits_{j=1}^{K}\|\Psi_{j}\|\Phi_{j}\right)^{1/2}(X-U)\left(\sum\limits_{j=1}^{K}\|\Psi_{j}\|\Phi_{j}\right)^{1/2}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\leqslant\frac{4}{\alpha}\left(\sum\limits_{j=1}^{K}\|\Psi_{j}\|\mathrm{Tr}(\Phi_{j})\right)^{2},

(17)

\displaystyle\qquad\left\|\left(\sum\limits_{j=1}^{K}\|\Phi_{j}\|\Psi_{j}\right)^{1/2}(Y-V)\left(\sum\limits_{j=1}^{K}\|\Phi_{j}\|\Psi_{j}\right)^{1/2}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\leqslant\frac{4}{\beta}\left(\sum\limits_{j=1}^{K}\|\Phi_{j}\|\mathrm{Tr}(\Psi_{j})\right)^{2}\Bigg\}

and a corresponding posterior density
\rho_{U,V}
, supported on

\rho_{U,V}(X,Y)=\begin{cases}\mu(X-U,Y-V)/\mathtt{Z},\quad\text{if $(X,Y)\in\Upsilon(U,V)$},\\
0,\quad\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

(18)

where
\mathtt{Z}
is a normalizing constant. It is not hard to show that
\mathtt{Z}
is at least
1/4
.

Lemma 4.3
.

With the notations introduced above, it holds that
\mathtt{Z}\geqslant 1/4
.

The proof of Lemma
4.3
is deferred to Appendix
D
. Let
\nu>0
be a positive constant to be defined later. Considering a pair

we conclude that, according to Lemma

,

\displaystyle\sup\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}U\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p},V\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times q}\\

with probability at least

Our next goal is to bound the exponential moment

Let us fix any

for all

,

.

\displaystyle\nu\left\|\mathcal{R}(\widehat{\Sigma}-\Sigma)\right\|

5
Conclusion and open problems

We showed that, if the covariance matrix
\Sigma
can be represented in the form (
2
), then it is possible to obtain a non-asymptotic dimension-free upper bound on the Frobenius distance between
\Sigma
and the penalized permuted least squares estimate
\widetilde{\Sigma}
under reasonable conditions. The rate of convergence is sharper compared to the standard unstructured case. There is an open question whether similar improvements are possible if we measure the estimation performance in terms of the operator norm. In particular, we are not aware of any (even dimension-dependent) bounds on
\|\widetilde{\Sigma}-\Sigma\|
. We also leave open the question if the rate of convergence in Theorem
2.5
is optimal in the minimax sense. Another interesting question is how the result of Theorem
2.5
will change if we consider a misspecified model

\Sigma=\Phi_{1}\otimes\Psi_{1}+\ldots+\Phi_{K}\otimes\Psi_{K}+E,

where
E\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is a (possibly unstructured) remainder. In
(
Greenewald and Hero 2015
)
, the authors studied the case when
E
is a sparse matrix and suggested a robust Kronecker PCA procedure for estimation of
\Sigma
in this situation. Finally, one can consider more complex models with higher-order Kronecker products
(
Pouryazdian et al. 2016
;
Hafner et al. 2020
;
McCormack and Hoff 2023
)
.

(A\otimes B)(C\otimes D)=(AC)\otimes(BD),

(A\otimes B)\mathrm{\mathbf{vec}}(U)=\mathrm{\mathbf{vec}}(BUA^{\top}),

We move to the analysis of the second term in the right-hand side of (

\mathcal{R}

\displaystyle\left\|\mathcal{R}(\widehat{\Sigma}-\Sigma)\right\|

(q\times p)

Let

be i.i.d. Bernoulli random variables. Then

\sum\limits_{j\neq k}{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}_{j}^{\top}M_{jk}{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}_{k}=4\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon}\sum\limits_{j\neq k}\varepsilon_{j}(1-\varepsilon_{k}){\boldsymbol{\zeta}}_{j}^{\top}M_{jk}{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}_{k}\quad\text{almost surely,}

where
\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon}
stands for the expectation with respect to
\varepsilon_{1},\dots,\varepsilon_{K}
conditionally on
{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}_{1},\dots,{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}_{K}
. Let us define a random set
\mathcal{I}\subseteq\{1,\dots,K\}
as follows:

\mathcal{I}=\left\{j\in\{1,\dots,K\}:\varepsilon_{j}=1\right\}.

Then we can rewrite the sum

\sum\limits_{j\neq k}\varepsilon_{j}(1-\varepsilon_{k}){\boldsymbol{\zeta}}_{j}^{\top}M_{jk}{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}_{k}

in the form

\sum\limits_{j\neq k}\varepsilon_{j}(1-\varepsilon_{k}){\boldsymbol{\zeta}}_{j}^{\top}M_{jk}{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}_{k}=\sum\limits_{j\in\mathcal{I}}\sum\limits_{k\in\mathcal{I}^{c}}{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}_{j}^{\top}M_{jk}{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}_{k},

\mathcal{I}^{c}=\{1,\dots,K\}\backslash\mathcal{I}
denotes the complement of
\mathcal{I}
. With the notation introduced above, we obtain that

\mathbb{E}G\left(\sum\limits_{j\neq k}{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}_{j}^{\top}M_{jk}{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}_{k}\right)=\mathbb{E}_{{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}}G\left(4\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon}\sum\limits_{j\in\mathcal{I}}\sum\limits_{k\in\mathcal{I}^{c}}{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}_{j}^{\top}M_{jk}{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}_{k}\right).

Here
\mathbb{E}_{{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}}
stands for the expectation with respect to
{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}_{1},\dots,{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}_{K}
. Applying Jensen’s inequality and taking into account that, for any realization of
\varepsilon_{1},\dots,\varepsilon_{K}

\{{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}_{j}:j\in\mathcal{I}\}

\{{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}_{k}:k\in\mathcal{I}\}

\sum\limits_{j\in\mathcal{I}^{c}}\sum\limits_{k\in\mathcal{I}}{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}_{j}^{\top}M_{jk}{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}_{k}^{\prime}\quad\text{is independent of}\quad\sum\limits_{j\in\mathcal{I}}\sum\limits_{k\in\mathcal{I}^{c}}{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}_{j}^{\top}M_{jk}{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}_{k}^{\prime}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{{\boldsymbol{\zeta}},{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}^{\prime}}G\left(4\sum\limits_{j\in\mathcal{I}}\sum\limits_{k\in\mathcal{I}^{c}}{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}_{j}^{\top}M_{jk}{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}_{k}^{\prime}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathbb{E}_{{\boldsymbol{\zeta}},{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}^{\prime}}G\left(4\sum\limits_{j\in\mathcal{I}}\sum\limits_{k\in\mathcal{I}^{c}}{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}_{j}^{\top}M_{jk}{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}_{k}^{\prime}+4\sum\limits_{j\in\mathcal{I}^{c}}\sum\limits_{k\in\mathcal{I}}{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}_{j}^{\top}M_{jk}{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}_{k}^{\prime}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon}\mathbb{E}_{{\boldsymbol{\zeta}},{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}^{\prime}}G\left(4\sum\limits_{j\in\mathcal{I}}\sum\limits_{k\in\mathcal{I}^{c}}{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}_{j}^{\top}M_{jk}{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}_{k}^{\prime}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{{\boldsymbol{\zeta}},{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}^{\prime}}G\left(4\sum\limits_{j\neq k}{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}_{j}^{\top}M_{jk}{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}_{k}^{\prime}\right).

Proof of Lemma
B.3

Step 1: a bound on
\mathbb{E}\eta^{2k}
.

The goal of this step is to prove that

\mathbb{E}\eta^{2k}\leqslant 2\cdot k!\cdot\sigma^{2k}.

Note that Markov’s inequality and the definition of the Orlicz norm yield that

\mathbb{P}\left(|\eta|\geqslant t\right)\leqslant e^{-t^{2}/\sigma^{2}}\mathbb{E}e^{\eta^{2}/\sigma^{2}}\leqslant 2e^{-t^{2}/\sigma^{2}}\quad\text{for all $t>0$.}

Then it holds that

\mathbb{E}\eta^{2k}=\int\limits_{0}^{+\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\eta^{2k}\geqslant t\right)\mathrm{d}t=\int\limits_{0}^{+\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(|\eta|\geqslant t^{1/(2k)}\right)\mathrm{d}t\leqslant 2\int\limits_{0}^{+\infty}\exp\left\{-\frac{t^{1/k}}{\sigma^{2}}\right\}\mathrm{d}t.

Substituting
t
with
\sigma^{2k}u^{k}
, we obtain that

\mathbb{E}\eta^{2k}\leqslant 2k\,\sigma^{2k}\int\limits_{0}^{+\infty}u^{k-1}e^{-u}\mathrm{d}u=2k\,\sigma^{2k}\,\Gamma(k)=2\cdot k!\cdot\sigma^{2k}.

(48)

Step 2: a bound on
\mathbb{E}\eta^{2}
.

For the second moment, it is possible to prove a bit better bound, than (
48
). It holds that

\mathbb{E}\eta^{2}=\int\limits_{0}^{+\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\eta^{2}\geqslant t\right)\mathrm{d}t\leqslant\int\limits_{0}^{\sigma^{2}\log 2}\mathrm{d}t+\int\limits_{0}^{\sigma^{2}\log 2}\mathbb{P}\left(\eta^{2}\geqslant t\right)\mathrm{d}t\leqslant\sigma^{2}\log 2+2\int\limits_{\sigma^{2}\log 2}^{+\infty}e^{-t/\sigma^{2}}\mathrm{d}t.

Let us replace
t
with
\sigma^{2}(u+\log 2)
, where
u\in(0,+\infty)
. Then

\mathbb{E}\eta^{2}\leqslant\sigma^{2}\log 2+\sigma^{2}\int\limits_{0}^{+\infty}e^{-u}\mathrm{d}u=\sigma^{2}(1+\log 2).

(49)

Step 3: a bound on exponential moments.

Let us fix any
\lambda>0
and consider
\mathbb{E}e^{\lambda\eta}
. Using Taylor’s expansion and taking into account that
\mathbb{E}\eta=0
, we obtain that

\mathbb{E}e^{\lambda\eta}=1+\sum\limits_{k=2}^{\infty}\frac{\lambda^{k}\mathbb{E}\eta^{k}}{k!}=1+\sum\limits_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{\lambda^{2k}\mathbb{E}\eta^{2k}}{(2k)!}+\sum\limits_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{\lambda^{2k+1}\mathbb{E}\eta^{2k+1}}{(2k+1)!}.

According to the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we have

\lambda^{2k+1}\mathbb{E}\eta^{2k+1}\leqslant\frac{\lambda^{2k}\mathbb{E}\eta^{2k}}{2}+\frac{\lambda^{2k+2}\mathbb{E}\eta^{2k+2}}{2},

and thus,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}e^{\lambda\eta}

\displaystyle=1+\sum\limits_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{\lambda^{2k}\mathbb{E}\eta^{2k}}{(2k)!}+\sum\limits_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{\lambda^{2k+1}\mathbb{E}\eta^{2k+1}}{(2k+1)!}

\displaystyle\leqslant 1+\frac{7\lambda^{2}\mathbb{E}\eta^{2}}{12}+\sum\limits_{k=2}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{(2k)!}+\frac{1}{2(2k-1)!}+\frac{1}{2(2k+1)!}\right)\lambda^{2k}\,\mathbb{E}\eta^{2k}.

Due to (
48
), it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}e^{\lambda\eta}

\displaystyle\leqslant 1+\frac{7\lambda^{2}\mathbb{E}\eta^{2}}{12}+\sum\limits_{k=2}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{(2k)!}+\frac{1}{2(2k-1)!}+\frac{1}{2(2k+1)!}\right)\lambda^{2k}\cdot\left(2\cdot k!\cdot\sigma^{2k}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant 1+\frac{7\lambda^{2}\mathbb{E}\eta^{2}}{12}+\sum\limits_{k=2}^{\infty}\frac{2(k!)^{2}}{(2k)!}\left(1+k+\frac{1}{2(2k+1)}\right)\cdot\frac{\lambda^{2k}\sigma^{2k}}{k!}.

Note that, for any integer
k\geqslant 2
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{2(k!)^{2}}{(2k)!}\left(1+k+\frac{1}{2(2k+1)}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(2+2k+\frac{1}{2k+1}\right)\prod\limits_{j=1}^{k}\frac{j\cdot j}{(2j-1)\cdot 2j}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{k}}\left(2+2k+\frac{1}{2k+1}\right)\prod\limits_{j=1}^{k}\frac{j}{(2j-1)}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{2k+3}{2^{k}}\prod\limits_{j=1}^{k}\frac{j}{(2j-1)}\leqslant\frac{2k+3}{2^{k}}\cdot\frac{1}{3}<1.

Hence, it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}e^{\lambda\eta}

\displaystyle\leqslant 1+\frac{7\lambda^{2}\mathbb{E}\eta^{2}}{12}+\sum\limits_{k=2}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{(2k)!}+\frac{1}{2(2k-1)!}+\frac{1}{2(2k+1)!}\right)\lambda^{2k}\cdot\left(2\cdot k!\cdot\sigma^{2k}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant 1+\frac{7\lambda^{2}\mathbb{E}\eta^{2}}{12}+\sum\limits_{k=2}^{\infty}\frac{\lambda^{2k}\sigma^{2k}}{k!}.

The sharper bound (
49
) on
\mathbb{E}\eta^{2}
implies that

\frac{7\lambda^{2}\mathbb{E}\eta^{2}}{12}\leqslant\frac{7\lambda^{2}\sigma^{2}(1+\log 2)}{12}\leqslant\lambda^{2}\sigma^{2}.

Thus, we finally obtain that

\mathbb{E}e^{\lambda\eta}\leqslant 1+\lambda^{2}\sigma^{2}+\sum\limits_{k=2}^{\infty}\frac{\lambda^{2k}\sigma^{2k}}{k!}\leqslant e^{\lambda^{2}\sigma^{2}}\quad\text{for any $\lambda\geqslant 0$.}

The proof for negative
\lambda
is absolutely similar.
