DC conductance and memory in 3D gravity
Márcela Cárdenas, Hernán A. González, Kristiansen Lara, Miguel Pino

Abstract
Transport properties are investigated in the two-dimensional dual dynamics of AdS 3 gravity. By providing boundary conditions that deform the ADM lapse and shift functions, we construct a lower dimensional model comprising two copies of chiral boson excitations with anisotropic scaling symmetry. Using bosonization, an electric current is identified. By means of the Kubo formula, we find a DC conductance depending on the level of the theory and the dynamical exponents. The bulk realization of the linear response is related to a type of gravitational memory emerging in the context of near-horizon boundary conditions. The process is adiabatic and represents a permanent spacetime deformation parametrized by anisotropic chiral bosons through a large gauge transformation.

2
Anisotropic chiral bosons from a Hamiltonian reduction

This section reviews the construction of the boundary dynamics in three-dimensional gravity using the Chern-Simons formulation. By imposing appropriate boundary conditions, we make contact with a recently introduced model dubbed
anisotropic chiral boson

[
10
]
. The derivation generalizes the seminal example presented in
[
21
]
, where Brown-Henneaux boundary conditions give rise to a theory of two decoupled chiral bosons.

As it will be shown below, the boundary dynamics will be completely defined by the specification of a symplectic structure and a Hamiltonian obtained from a well-posed variational principle.

2.1
Kinetic term

The starting point of the construction considers the action principle given by the difference of two Chern-Simons functionals
I_{CS}[A^{+}]-I_{CS}[A^{-}]
, where the connections
A^{\pm}
are
\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})
valued one-forms. The spacetime has the topology
\mathbb{R}\times\Sigma
, where the real line is parameterized by time
t
, while
\Sigma
is a disc endowed with coordinates
r>0
and
0\leq x<2\pi l
, where
l
stands for the AdS radius. The level is
K=\frac{l}{4G}
where
G
is the Newton constant. See appendix
A
, for details on conventions and explicit formulas.

The boundary symplectic structure can be obtained by solving the bulk constraints and replacing them back in the three-dimensional action. Since the theory is devoid of local degrees of freedom, the Chern-Simons kinetic term becomes a total derivative that can be expressed as a local two-dimensional functional
[
22
,
21
]
.

Here, an alternative path is taken for constructing the reduced kinetic term of the model. In doing so, it is necessary to study the symmetries allowed by the field configurations satisfying a specific boundary condition at constant time slice. In the Chern-Simons formulation, asymptotic conditions are encoded in the spatial component of the gauge field

\displaystyle A^{\pm}_{x}=a^{\pm}_{x}+\cdots\,,

(2.1)

where the ellipsis represents subleading terms close to the boundary, capturing the radial dependence. The set of gauge transformations
\varepsilon^{\pm}
preserving the asymptotic form of
A^{\pm}_{x}
are global as long as they produce non-vanishing surfaces charges
[
23
]
. In that case, they are given by

\displaystyle\delta Q(\varepsilon^{+},\varepsilon^{-})=-\frac{K}{2\pi}\int dx\,\langle\varepsilon^{+}\delta a^{+}_{x}-\varepsilon^{-}\delta a^{-}_{x}\rangle\,.

(2.2)

Without imposing any further restriction on
a^{\pm}_{x}
, the asymptotic symmetries are generated by the
\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})
current algebra
[
24
,
25
]
. We consider the simplest set of connections within the
\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})
family spanned by only one generator
[
16
]

\displaystyle a^{\pm}_{x}=\pm\frac{4\pi}{K}\mathcal{J}_{\pm}(x,t)\;L_{0}\,.

(2.3)

Gauge parameters respecting the above boundary conditions are of the form
\varepsilon^{\pm}=\varepsilon^{\pm}(x,t)L_{0}
. The corresponding charge densities are
\mathcal{J}_{\pm}
, giving rise to
\mathfrak{u}(1)
current algebras under Dirac brackets

\displaystyle\left\{\mathcal{J}_{\pm}(x),\mathcal{J}_{\pm}(x^{\prime})\right\}_{\rm D.B.}=\pm\frac{K}{4\pi}\partial_{x}\delta(x-x^{\prime})\,.

(2.4)

The latter algebra can be used to define the canonical variables at the boundary. In fact, as shown in
[
26
]
, the kinetic term producing commutation relations (
2.4
) is

\displaystyle I_{\rm kinetic}[\mathcal{J}_{+},\mathcal{J}_{-}]=\frac{2\pi}{K}\int dxdydt\;\epsilon(x-y)\left[\mathcal{J}_{+}(x)\partial_{t}\mathcal{J}_{+}(y)-\mathcal{J}_{-}(x)\partial_{t}\mathcal{J}_{-}(y)\right]\,,

(2.5)

where
\epsilon(x)
is the inverse of operator
\partial_{x}
. In order to obtain a local description, we use fields
\phi_{\pm}
that are defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\pm}=\pm\frac{K}{4\pi}\partial_{x}\phi_{\pm}\,,

(2.6)

and the kinetic term becomes

\displaystyle I_{\rm kinetic}[\phi_{+},\phi_{-}]=\frac{K}{8\pi}\int dxdt\;\left[\partial_{x}\phi_{+}\partial_{t}\phi_{+}-\partial_{x}\phi_{-}\partial_{t}\phi_{-}\right]\,.

(2.7)

2.2
Boundary Hamiltonian

To define the time evolution generator of the system, we consider boundary charges conjugated to the gauge transformations
\varepsilon^{\pm}=-a^{\pm}_{t}
, where
a^{\pm}_{t}
is the leading term of the temporal component
A^{\pm}_{t}
. By using (
2.2
), we find that

\displaystyle\delta H=\frac{K}{2\pi}\int dx\langle a^{+}_{t}\,\delta a^{+}_{x}-a^{-}_{t}\,\delta a^{-}_{x}\rangle\,.

(2.8)

A local expression for the Hamiltonian can be obtained provided the leading terms
a^{\pm}_{t}
fulfill a condition of the form

\displaystyle a^{\pm}_{t}=\frac{\delta\mathcal{H}_{\pm}(a^{\pm}_{x})}{\delta a^{\pm}_{x}}\,,

(2.9)

where
\mathcal{H}_{\pm}(a^{\pm}_{x})
is local functional of
a^{\pm}_{x}
. However, the choice of the latter functional is not completely arbitrary since time evolution of the system must also preserve the asymptotic behavior of
A^{\pm}_{x}
. To ensure this condition, one then chooses
a^{\pm}_{t}
to have the same functional form than the allowed gauge parameters
\varepsilon^{\pm}

[
3
]
.
For instance, in the case of Brown-Henneaux boundary conditions without chemical potentials
[
21
]
, the choice
a^{\pm}_{t}=\pm a^{\pm}_{x}
singles out a Hamiltonian that is quadratic in
a^{\pm}_{x}
and produces modes evolving in the
t\pm x
directions. Another possible choice has been considered in
[
4
,
6
]
in connection with integrable systems. In that case, the selected functional
\mathcal{H}_{\pm}(a^{\pm}_{x})
is given by AKNS charges and the corresponding Hamiltonian evolution of the modes encompasses the AKNS hierarchy
[
27
]
.

With the purpose of studying time-dependent perturbations in a family of 2D field theories, we focus on the particular class

\displaystyle a^{\pm}_{t}=a_{0}^{\pm}\pm v(-1)^{\frac{z-1}{2}}\partial^{z-1}_{x}a^{\pm}_{x}\,,

(2.10)

where
a_{0}^{\pm}
are arbitrary functions of
t
without variation. This term has been added in order to guarantee the existence of a non-degenerate background metric field
2
2

2

The metric field can be constructed from

g_{\mu\nu}=\frac{l^{2}}{2}\left\langle(A^{+}_{\mu}-A^{-}_{\mu}),(A^{+}_{\nu}-A^{-}_{\nu})\right\rangle\,.

. It is important to stress that the deformation generated by (
2.10
) produces a field-dependent asymptotic behavior of the lapse and shift functions in the ADM metric formulation
[
4
,
6
]
.

The dynamical exponent
z
is an odd number, that can take different values for each copy, while
v>0
is a coupling constant with dimensions of
[{\rm length}]^{z-1}
.
In the upcoming section, we will use distinct dynamical exponents
z_{\pm}
on each Chern-Simons copy. Notice that only the particular choice
z_{+}=z_{-}
confers Lifshitz symmetry to the boundary dynamics of the gravitational model. In particular, the Brown-Henneaux case is contained in the choice
z_{\pm}=1
, where the scaling symmetry becomes isotropic.

The Hamiltonian (
2.8
) can be readily integrated using the condition (
2.10
). Additionally, when implementing
a^{\pm}_{x}=\partial_{x}\phi_{\pm}L_{0}
, its expression depends on the local fields
\phi_{\pm}
, yielding

\displaystyle H=H_{0}[\phi_{+}]+H_{0}[\phi_{-}]\,,

(2.11)

where

\displaystyle H_{0}[\phi]=\frac{Kv}{8\pi}\int dx\,\left(\partial^{\frac{z_{\pm}+1}{2}}_{x}\phi\right)^{2}\,.

(2.12)

2.3
Anisotropic chiral boson spectrum

Let us describe the action for each chirality

\displaystyle I[\phi_{\pm}]=\pm\frac{K}{8\pi}\int dxdt\;\left[\partial_{x}\phi_{\pm}\partial_{t}\phi_{\pm}\mp v\left(\partial^{\frac{z+1}{2}}_{x}\phi_{\pm}\right)^{2}\right].

(2.13)

For sake of simplicity, here we only consider one odd dynamical exponent
z
. The theory realizes anisotropic scaling symmetry
t\to\lambda^{z}t
and
x\to\lambda x
. Excitations are chiral in the sense that parity transformation (
x\to-x
) swaps the dynamics of
\phi_{\pm}
into
\phi_{\mp}
.

The equations of motion corresponds to

\displaystyle\partial_{x}\left(\partial_{t}\phi_{\pm}\pm(-1)^{\frac{z+1}{2}}v\,\partial_{x}^{z}\phi_{\pm}\right)=0\,.

(2.14)

Since this is a linear equation, solutions are given by plane wave modes

\displaystyle\phi_{\pm}=\exp[i(kx\pm\omega_{k}t)]\,,\quad\omega_{k}=vk^{z}\,.

(2.15)

Furthermore, due to parameterization (
2.6
) the system inherits zero modes responsible for the gauge symmetry
\phi_{\pm}\to\phi_{\pm}+f_{\pm}(t)
. In what follows, we consider these to be gauge fixed and therefore they will not be present in the subsequent analysis.

Another important feature is that the model (
2.13
) exhibits a
\mathfrak{u}(1)
current symmetry on each sector. Under the infinitesimal transformation
\delta\phi_{\pm}=\eta^{\pm}_{k}\exp[i(kx\pm\omega_{k}t)]
, with
\eta^{\pm}_{k}\ll 1
, the Noether theorem yields the expected currents (
2.6
).

For the forthcoming analysis, we describe the fields in terms of their Fourier modes.
In the remainder of the paper, we will be concerned with local properties of the theory, hence
x
is regarded as a coordinate defined on the whole real line. This allows to expand the field operator as

\displaystyle\phi_{\pm}(x,t)=\int^{+\infty}_{-\infty}\frac{dk}{k}e^{i(kx\pm\omega_{k}t)}b_{\pm,k}\,,

(2.16)

where the condition
b_{\pm,k}^{\dagger}=-b_{\pm,-k}
ensures hermiticity of
\phi_{\pm}
.

The Dirac bracket can be read off from the kinetic term in (
2.13
). The quantum version yields the equal time commutation relation
3
3

3

In the quantum theory we use the rule
\left[,\right]\to i\{,\}_{\rm DB}
.

\displaystyle\left[\phi_{\pm}(t,x),\partial_{x^{\prime}}\phi_{\pm}(t,x^{\prime})\right]=\pm\frac{4\pi}{K}i\delta(x-x^{\prime})\,.

(2.17)

In terms of the
b_{\pm,k}
operators, they become

\displaystyle\left[b_{\pm,k},b_{\pm,k^{\prime}}\right]=\pm\frac{2}{K}k\delta(k+k^{\prime})\,.

(2.18)

3
Linear response and the DC limit

This section explores the dynamical evolution of the boundary fields
\phi_{\pm}
driven by a perturbation to the free Hamiltonian of the boundary theory. At the level of the Lagrangian density (
2.11
), such deformation is chosen as,

\displaystyle\Delta H=\int\,dx\,\mu(x,t)\,\left[\mathcal{J}_{+}(x,t)+\mathcal{J}_{-}(x,t)\right]\,,

(3.1)

where
\mathcal{J}_{\pm}
are the Noether currents expressed in terms of the fields
\phi_{\pm}
by (
2.6
) and
\mu
is an external source localized in time, that will model an evolution process between vacuum solutions of (
2.14
). As shown below, this will allow us to calculate transport coefficients by means of the Kubo formula.

3.1
Response function

We start by identifying a conservation law using the charges expressed in terms of the chiral boson (
2.6
)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathcal{J}_{\pm}+\partial_{x}\mathcal{I}_{\pm}=0\,,

(3.2)

implying that
\mathcal{I}_{\pm}
is

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\pm}=\mp\frac{K}{4\pi}\partial_{t}\phi_{\pm}\,.

(3.3)

Equation (
3.2
) associates
\mathcal{J}_{\pm}
with a density and
\mathcal{I}_{\pm}
with a current.
In the context of bosonization, they can be understood as carriers of fermionic degrees of freedom, so that
\mathcal{J}_{\pm}
is associated to an electric charge density and
\mathcal{I}_{\pm}
corresponds to an electric current flowing along the boundary. For details on the precise relation between
\mathcal{J}_{\pm}
and fermionic operators see appendix
B
.

We wish to evaluate the linear response of the net current
\mathcal{I}_{\rm tot}\equiv\mathcal{I}_{+}+\mathcal{I}_{-}
, induced by the deformation (
3.1
), which is interpreted as an electric potential energy. In this sense, the source
\mu
produces a voltage creating a current in a one-dimensional conductor. In the next subsection, we will be interested in a source
\mu
creating a flow between two reservoirs. This has been interpreted by
[
12
]
as edge states flowing at the boundary of a two-dimensional material.

We analyze the expectation value of the variation of the current using the Kubo formula in frequency space (we refer the reader to appendix
C
for conventions)

\displaystyle\delta\langle\widetilde{\mathcal{I}}_{\pm}(\omega,x)\rangle

\displaystyle=\int\,dx^{\prime}\,\widetilde{\mu}(x^{\prime},\omega)\widetilde{\chi}^{\pm}_{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J}}(\omega;x,x^{\prime})\,,

(3.4)

where the susceptibility
\widetilde{\chi}^{\pm}_{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J}}
is

\displaystyle\widetilde{\chi}^{\pm}_{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J}}(\omega;x,x^{\prime})=-i\int dt\,\Theta(t)e^{i\omega t}\left\langle\left[\mathcal{I}_{\pm}(x,t),\mathcal{J}_{\pm}(x^{\prime},0)\right]\right\rangle\,.

(3.5)

After using (
2.17
) and its Fourier mode expansion, the suceptibility can be expressed as

\displaystyle\widetilde{\chi}^{\pm}_{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J}}(\omega;x,x^{\prime})=\frac{K}{4\pi}\left[\pm\delta(x-x^{\prime})\mp(\omega+i\epsilon)F^{\pm}(x-x^{\prime},\omega)\right]\,.

(3.6)

The functions
F^{\pm}
have been obtained after expressing the Heaviside function
\Theta(t)
as an integral in frequency space, inducing the shift
\omega\to\omega+i\epsilon
. They become

\displaystyle(\omega+i\epsilon)F^{\pm}(y,\omega)=i\Theta(-y)\sum_{k^{\pm}_{p}\in{\rm Im}_{<}}\frac{\omega(k^{\pm}_{p})e^{ik^{\pm}_{p}y}}{\omega^{\prime}(k^{\pm}_{p})}-i\Theta(y)\sum_{k^{\pm}_{p}\in{\rm Im}_{>}}\frac{\omega(k^{\pm}_{p})e^{ik^{\pm}_{p}y}}{\omega^{\prime}(k^{\pm}_{p})}\,,

(3.7)

where
{\rm Im}_{\gtrless}
correspond to the upper and lower complex plane. The values
k^{\pm}_{p}=k^{\pm}_{p}(\omega)
are solutions to the equation
\omega+i\epsilon\pm\omega(k^{\pm}_{p})=0
.

3.2
Two-terminal conductance

The situation of interest is modeled by an external source that generates a flow between two reservoirs located at positions
x=x_{L}
and
x=x_{R}

[
12
]
, that perturbs the system for a very short time interval.
In order to reproduce these features we use

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mu}(x,\omega)=V_{L}\,\Theta(x_{L}-x)+V_{R}\,\Theta(x-x_{R})\,.

(3.8)

Notice that time localization of the perturbation is ensured as the inverse Fourier transform of
\widetilde{\mu}
yields a temporal Dirac delta.

In the presence of the source
\widetilde{\mu}
, the variation of the electric current on each sector becomes

\delta\langle\widetilde{\mathcal{I}}_{\pm}\rangle=\mp\frac{K}{4\pi}\Bigg\{V_{L}\left[\Theta(x-x_{L})\Delta^{\pm}_{>}(x-x_{L})-\Theta(x_{L}-x)\Delta^{\pm}_{<}(x-x_{L})\right]\\
-V_{R}\left[\Theta(x-x_{R})\Delta^{\pm}_{>}(x-x_{R})-\Theta(x_{R}-x)\Delta^{\pm}_{<}(x-x_{R})\right]\Bigg\}\,,

(3.9)

where

\displaystyle\Delta^{\pm}_{\gtrless}(x)=\frac{1}{z^{\pm}}\sum_{k^{\pm}_{p}\in{\rm Im}_{\gtrless}}e^{ik^{\pm}_{p}x}\,.

(3.10)

As we previously mentioned, we have considered a Hamiltonian (
2.12
) with different dynamical exponents on each sector, given by
z_{+}
and
z_{-}
respectively.

In the zero frequency limit, we obtain the DC current contribution. Noticing that
k^{\pm}_{p}(\omega\to 0)=0
, we see that
\Delta^{\pm}_{\gtrless}
becomes a constant that essentially counts the number of zeros of the equation

\displaystyle\omega+i\epsilon\pm v(k^{\pm}_{p})^{z^{\pm}}=0\,,

on the upper/lower complex plane. Causality, represented by the
i\epsilon
-prescription, implies that the
\pm
sector has
\tfrac{z^{\pm}\mp 1}{2}
zeros on the upper plane and
\tfrac{z^{\pm}\pm 1}{2}
zeros on the lower one. Therefore, functions
\Delta^{\pm}_{\gtrless}
in DC limit read

\displaystyle\lim_{\omega\to 0}\Delta^{\pm}_{>}=\frac{z^{\pm}\mp 1}{2z^{\pm}}\,,\quad\lim_{\omega\to 0}\Delta^{\pm}_{<}=\frac{z^{\pm}\pm 1}{2z^{\pm}}\,.

(3.11)

Using these expressions, the variation of the net current
\langle\widetilde{\mathcal{I}}_{\rm tot}\rangle
satisfies Ohm’s law. Indeed, by denoting the voltage as
\Delta V=V_{L}-V_{R}
, we find that the two-terminal conductance
\sigma

[
12
]
is given by

\displaystyle\quad\sigma\equiv\frac{\delta\langle\widetilde{\mathcal{I}}_{\rm tot}\rangle}{\Delta V}=\frac{K}{8\pi}\left(\frac{1}{z_{+}}+\frac{1}{z_{-}}\right)\,.

(3.12)

The above expression have two contributions that are worth commenting on. Firstly, there is a factor coming from the level
K=\frac{l}{4G}
associated to the infinite-dimensional
\mathfrak{u}(1)
current symmetry (
2.4
). This symmetry is universal in the sense that is controlled by the residual transformation of the
a^{\pm}_{x}
. Lastly, the conductivity is also modified by the dynamical exponents of the theory
(z_{+},z_{-})
that are associated to the higher derivative Hamiltonians (
2.12
). This contribution is model dependent as it emerges from the choice of
a^{\pm}_{t}
.

4
The gravitational side

We wish to understand gravitational implications associated to the DC response observed on the boundary description. For that purpose, we construct the bulk observables that were identified with electric properties in the preceding calculation and probe the effect of the perturbation on a near horizon metric field. We will show that from a bulk perspective, the source
\mu
induces a permanent deformation on the spacetime that can be interpreted with a type of gravitational memory effect.

We start by defining the Chern-Simons fields
A^{\pm}
for the three-dimensional theory, whose field equations are modified by external sources as follows
4
4

4

The action principle giving rise to these field equations is discussed in appendix
A
. The orientation of the manifold is fixed by
\epsilon^{rxt}=1
.

\displaystyle\frac{K}{4\pi}\epsilon^{\alpha\mu\nu}F^{\pm}_{\mu\nu}=J^{\alpha}_{\pm}\,.

(4.1)

The gauge connections are related with their asymptotic values
a^{\pm}
through the decomposition,

\displaystyle A^{\pm}=b_{\pm}^{-1}a^{\pm}b_{\pm}+b_{\pm}^{-1}db_{\pm}\,.

(4.2)

Here,
b_{\pm}
is an SL
(2,\mathbb{R})
group element depending on the radial coordinate
r
. The longitudinal gauge connections are
a_{\pm}=a^{\pm}_{x}dx+a^{\pm}_{t}dt
, with
a^{\pm}_{x}
given by (
2.3
), while the temporal component is

\displaystyle a^{\pm}_{t}\equiv\mp\frac{4\pi}{K}\mathcal{I}_{\pm}\,L_{0}\,.

(4.3)

The source is defined through a current pointing in the radial direction

\displaystyle J^{r}_{\pm}=-\frac{K}{2\pi}\partial_{x}\mu\,\,b_{\pm}^{-1}L_{0}b_{\pm}\,,\quad J^{t}_{\pm}=J^{x}_{\pm}=0\,,

(4.4)

where its precise dependence on the group element
b_{\pm}
ensures the conservation law
\partial_{\alpha}J^{\alpha}_{\pm}+[A^{\pm}_{\alpha},J^{\alpha}_{\pm}]=0
. The functional dependence of
\mu
is chosen so that (
4.1
) describes the boundary perturbation of the previous section. Using (
3.8
), the radial component reads

\displaystyle J^{r}_{\pm}=\frac{K}{2\pi}\delta(t)\Big[V_{L}\,\delta(x-x_{L})-V_{R}\,\delta(x-x_{R})\Big]\,b_{\pm}^{-1}L_{0}b_{\pm}\,.

(4.5)

The temporal localization of the external source models a process where the initial and final configurations correspond to solutions of vacuum Einstein’s equation.
To construct those solutions in terms of a metric field, we consider a particular radial dependence of the group element
b_{\pm}
,

\displaystyle b_{\pm}(r)=\exp\left[{\pm\frac{r}{2l}(L_{1}-L_{-1})}\right]\,.

(4.6)

Since the spatial component of the gauge field (
2.3
) lies on the hyperbolic conjugacy class, the resulting gravitational configurations are given by black hole solutions
[
15
,
16
]
. Their geometry close to the Rindler horizon
r=0
is given by

\displaystyle ds^{2}=\left(\frac{2\pi l}{K}\right)^{2}(\mathcal{I}_{\rm tot}^{2}dt^{2}-2\mathcal{I}_{\rm tot}\rho_{\rm tot}dtdx+\rho_{\rm tot}^{2}dx^{2})+dr^{2}+O(r^{2})\,,

(4.7)

which is expressed in terms of the net electric current
\mathcal{I}_{\rm tot}=\mathcal{I}_{+}+\mathcal{I}_{-}
and the net charge density has been defined as
\rho_{\rm tot}\equiv\mathcal{J}_{+}+\mathcal{J}_{-}
. The

terms stand for subleading components. One can see that
\mathcal{I}_{\rm tot}
and
\rho_{\rm tot}
are related to the angular velocity and the horizon area, measured by an observer placed in a rotating frame close to
r=0
.

4.1
Linear response for near horizon boundary conditions

We discuss the effect of external source on the spacetime metric (
4.7
), by calculating the retarded responses of the operators
\mathcal{I}_{\rm tot}
and
\rho_{\rm tot}
. The evolution of the metric field is controlled by the equations

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathcal{J}_{\pm}+\partial_{x}\mathcal{I}_{\pm}=\pm\frac{K}{4\pi}\delta(t)\Big[V_{L}\,\delta(x-x_{L})-V_{R}\,\delta(x-x_{R})\Big]\,,

(4.8)

that has been obtained after using the explicit form of connections (
4.2
). Now, we make use of the boundary condition (
2.10
), yielding

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\pm}=\mathcal{I}_{\pm}^{\rm in}\pm v(-)^{\frac{z_{\pm}+1}{2}}\partial_{x}^{z_{\pm}-1}\mathcal{J}_{\pm}\,,

(4.9)

where we have identified
\mathcal{I}_{\pm}^{\rm in}
with
a^{\pm}_{0}
. Using (
4.8
) and (
4.9
) subject to initial conditions
\mathcal{J}_{\pm}(x,t=-\infty)=\mathcal{J}^{\rm in}_{\pm}
, with
\mathcal{J}^{\rm in}_{\pm}

\displaystyle\delta\widetilde{\mathcal{I}}_{\rm tot}=\int dx^{\prime}\,\widetilde{\mu}(x^{\prime},\omega)(\widetilde{\chi}^{+}_{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J}}+\widetilde{\chi}^{-}_{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J}})\,,

(4.10)

where the susceptibilities
\widetilde{\chi}^{\pm}_{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J}}
are shown to coincide with the boundary computation (

4.2
Near horizon memory and chiral bosons

The spacetime metric (
4.7

3
spacetime in a particular frame.

Let us consider a situation where the source
\mu

\displaystyle\pm\frac{4\pi}{K}\left(\mathcal{J}_{\pm}dx-\mathcal{I}_{\pm}dt\right)=d\phi_{\pm}\,,

\displaystyle ds^{2}=-\frac{l^{2}}{4}\sinh^{2}\left(\frac{r}{l}\right)(d\phi_{+}+d\phi_{-})^{2}+dr^{2}+\frac{l^{2}}{4}\cosh^{2}\left(\frac{r}{l}\right)(d\phi_{+}-d\phi_{-})^{2}\,.

(4.15)

This foliation corresponds to a double-Wick rotated version of global AdS
3
. Furthermore, the diffeomorphism (
4.14

\mathcal{J}_{\pm}
and
\mathcal{I}_{\pm}
belong to the set of large gauge transformations.

We are now in a position to analyze a process where the spacetime metric evolves from an initial to a final configuration, both of which satisfy Einstein’s equation in vacuum. We start by setting an initial state characterized by

\displaystyle\phi^{\rm in}_{\pm}(x,t)=\frac{4\pi}{K}(t\pm x)\mathcal{J}^{\rm in}_{\pm}\,,

(4.16)

representing a BTZ black hole with inner and outer horizons respectively denoted by
\frac{2\pi l^{2}}{K}(\mathcal{J}^{\rm in}_{+}\pm\mathcal{J}^{\rm in}_{-})
(see e.g.
[
29

4.9

\mathcal{I}_{\pm}^{\rm in}=\mp\mathcal{J}_{\pm}^{\rm in}

In presence of the source
\mu

4.8

\phi^{\rm out}_{\pm}
parameterizing the charge density and current after the perturbation
\mu

t>0

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}^{\rm out}_{\pm}=\pm\frac{K}{4\pi}\partial_{x}\phi^{\rm out}_{\pm}\,,\quad\mathcal{I}^{\rm out}_{\pm}=\mp\frac{K}{4\pi}\partial_{t}\phi^{\rm out}_{\pm}\,.

(4.17)

The field
\phi^{\rm out}_{\pm}
can be found after solving the equation (
4.8

\displaystyle\phi^{\rm out}_{\pm}(x,t)=\phi^{\rm in}_{\pm}(x,t)\mp V_{L}\,{\psi}_{z_{\pm}}\left(\pm\frac{x-x_{L}}{(vz_{\pm}t)^{1/z_{\pm}}}\right)\pm V_{R}\,{\psi}_{z_{\pm}}\left(\pm\frac{x-x_{R}}{(vz_{\pm}t)^{1/z_{\pm}}}\right)\,,

\displaystyle{\psi}_{z}(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\mathcal{P}\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}\frac{du}{u}\exp\left[i\left(\frac{u^{z}}{z}+xu\right)\right]\,,

(4.19)

{\rm Ai}_{z}

The final state is given again by the AdS

4.15

x

Another appealing feature codified in the chiral bosons is that the electric current DC response can be recovered from (

\displaystyle\sigma=-\frac{K}{4\pi\Delta V}\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}dt\,\partial_{t}(\delta\phi_{+}-\delta\phi_{-})=\frac{K}{4\pi}\left[{\psi}_{z^{+}}(0)+{\psi}_{z^{-}}(0)\right]\,,

\delta\phi_{\pm}=\phi^{\rm out}_{\pm}-\phi^{\rm in}_{\pm}

We have found that chiral bosons act as reparameterization modes on AdS space, modifying the charges and storing DC information of the boundary electric current. These permanent imprints in the solution space emerge as a three-dimensional version of the 4D gravitational memory, where the passage of a gravitational wave induces a permanent displacement of the detectors
[

18

.

It is interesting to compare the present process to the one studied in
[

32

5
Concluding remarks

We have identified the boundary degrees of freedom of a
\mathfrak{so}(2,2)
Chern-Simons theory characterized by temporal connections that depend on higher order derivatives of the fields (
2.10
). This leads to a generalized action principle for two copies of anisotropic chiral bosons with Lifshitz symmetry, including the self-dual action as a particular case
[
26
]
. Inspired by bosonization, we define an electric charge and study the linear response of the associated net current. It is found that the conductance in the DC limit is give by (
3.12
)

\sigma=\frac{K}{8\pi}\left(\frac{1}{z_{+}}+\frac{1}{z_{-}}\right)\,,

showing an explicit dependence on the level of the theory inherited from the Chern-Simons terms and also, a non-trivial dependence on the dynamical exponents of the Lifshitz scaling from each sector
z_{\pm}
.

This boundary transport phenomena have a reinterpretation in terms of gravitational observables. We construct a near horizon metric subject to the same dynamics and boundary perturbation. The disturbance on the spacetimes manifest in two quantities, the total current density
\mathcal{I}_{\rm tot}
and the total charge
\rho_{\rm tot}
. The computation of their retarded responses characterize the DC effect in a twofold manner. First, through the horizon area operator
\mathcal{A}
(
4.12
), where the variation
\delta\rho_{\rm tot}
implies

\delta\mathcal{A}=0\,,

meaning that the process is adiabatic in the thermodynamic sense. On the other hand, the variation
\delta\mathcal{I}_{\rm tot}
leads to define a conductivity as the time-average value of the electric current over a voltage, recovering the boundary result (
3.12
). The conductivity at the bulk becomes then a new observable, one that emerges as a result of a type of memory effect. Here, the source marks the transition between two gravitational configurations evolving from a stationary Rindler horizon to a spacetime endowed with soft charges.

The process can be understood as a large gauge transformation controlled by the chiral bosons, acting as reparametrization modes acting on a global AdS
3
spacetime. Indeed, this means that memory effects are not necessarily the result of the passage of gravitational radiation, as three-dimensional gravity is devoid of gravitational waves.

An important lesson that can be drawn from this work is the construction of a holographic framework to describe transport properties in 1+1 systems as an effect of 3D gravitational memory. To do so, one starts from three-dimensional gravity with a given set of boundary conditions, providing key ingredients allowing to determine the evolution process. The required information to reconstruct a boundary description is summarized in different components of the gauge fields and sources

•

The asymptotic conditions, expressed in the spatial components
a^{\pm}_{x}
, can be used to construct the symplectic kinetic term of the boundary model.

•

The specification of the temporal components
a^{\pm}_{t}
yields the Hamiltonian of the boundary theory.

•

The source triggers the linear response in the boundary, corresponding to the external currents coupled to the Chern-Simons bulk equations. In terms of the memory effects, it probes the transition between initial and the final states through the retarded response function.

Another avenue of investigation comes from the inclusion of more boundaries to capture global properties of black holes. As it has been pointed out in
[
36
]
, to properly describe the boundary dynamics of this kind of spacetimes it is necessary to include two boundaries in the Hamiltonian reduction. When the boundary dynamics is computed for topologies with various boundaries, the zero modes couple to the degrees of freedom on each boundary.
This setup provides a potential mechanism to intertwine transport phenomena on both boundaries.

Finally, it is desirable to extend the results of this work to different choices of asymptotic conditions. In particular, those describing non-linear dynamics at the boundary

Gravity as a Chern-Simons theory

General Relativity can be described in terms of the difference of two Chern-Simons actions
[
1
,
2
]

I=I_{CS}[A^{+}]-I_{CS}[A^{-}]\,,\quad I_{CS}[A^{\pm}]=\frac{K}{2\pi}\int\left\langle A^{\pm},dA^{\pm}+\frac{2}{3}A^{\pm 2}\right\rangle\,,

(A.1)

where the gauge fields
A^{\pm}
are valued in
\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})
algebra. Its generators are

\displaystyle\begin{split}L^{\pm}_{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}\;,\;L^{\pm}_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}-1/2&0\\
0&1/2\end{pmatrix},\;L^{\pm}_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},\end{split}

(A.2)

whose commutation relations read

\left[L^{\pm}_{n},L^{\pm}_{m}\right]=(n-m)L^{\pm}_{n+m}\,.

(A.3)

The bilinear invariant form is given by

\displaystyle\left\langle L^{\pm}_{1},L^{\pm}_{0}\right\rangle=0\quad\mbox{and}\quad\left\langle L^{\pm}_{0},L^{\pm}_{0}\right\rangle=1/2\,.

(A.4)

The connection to three-dimensional gravity is achieved by the identification

\displaystyle A^{\pm}

\displaystyle=\omega\pm\frac{e}{l},

(A.5)

where
e
is the vielbein and
\omega
is the spin connection.

In section (
4

\displaystyle I_{CS}[A;J]=I_{CS}[A]-\int d^{3}x\langle A_{\mu},J^{\mu}\rangle\,.

(A.6)

with

\displaystyle I=I_{CS}[A^{+};J_{+}]-I_{CS}[A^{-};J_{-}]\,.

(A.7)

Here,

are
\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})
valued currents. Then, the variation of the action (

\displaystyle\frac{K}{4\pi}\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho}F^{\pm}_{\nu\rho}=J_{\pm}^{\mu}\,.

It is worth noticing that the conservation of the external current

Appendix B
Bosonization and normal ordering

The interpretation of the charges
\mathcal{J}_{\pm}
as electronic charge densities goes as follows. Following

\displaystyle c_{\pm}(x)=:e^{-i\sqrt{\frac{K}{2}}\phi_{\pm}(x)}:\quad\text{and}\quad c^{\dagger}_{\pm}(x)=:e^{i\sqrt{\frac{K}{2}}\phi_{\pm}(x)}:\,,

(B.1)

where
:\bf{\mathcal{O}}:
denotes the normal ordering of the operator
\bf{\mathcal{O}}
. It is assumed all the fields are on the same time slice, so the temporal dependence is omitted.
The commutation relation (

\displaystyle\left[\phi_{\pm}(x),\phi_{\pm}(x^{\prime})\right]=\mp\frac{2\pi}{K}i\,{\rm sign}(x-x^{\prime})\,.

(B.2)

In order to properly deal with the normal order prescription, it is useful to write down the boson fields
\phi_{\pm}
as

\phi_{\pm}=\theta_{\pm}+\theta^{\dagger}_{\pm}\,,

(B.3)

where
\theta_{\pm}
and
\theta^{\dagger}_{\pm}
satisfy the commutation algebra,

\left[\theta_{\pm}(x),\theta^{\dagger}_{\pm}(x^{\prime})\right]=\pm\frac{2}{K}\log(-2\pi i(x-x^{\prime}+i\eta))\,,

(B.4)

and
\eta
is an infinitesimal positive regulator. Notice that given the sign difference in the algebra (
B.4
) for each copy, the creation and annihilation operators are interchanged. The pair
(\theta^{\dagger}_{-},\theta_{-})
acts following the standard convention, namely, as creation and annihilation operators. On the other hand, creation and annihilation operators corresponds to
(\theta_{+},\theta^{\dagger}_{+})
in the plus sector, respectively. The normal order rule imposes creation operators to be placed to the left of all annihilation operators in the product. Hence, one should consider normal-ordering the operators as follows

\displaystyle c_{+}(x)=e^{-i\sqrt{\frac{K}{2}}\theta_{+}(x)}e^{-i\sqrt{\frac{K}{2}}\theta^{\dagger}_{+}(x)}\quad\text{and}\quad c_{-}(x)=e^{-i\sqrt{\frac{K}{2}}\theta^{\dagger}_{-}(x)}e^{-i\sqrt{\frac{K}{2}}\theta_{-}(x)}.

(B.5)

Considering the above and (
B.2
), one can show that the fields
c_{\pm}
satisfy the anticommutation relations

\{c_{\pm}(x),c_{\pm}^{\dagger}(x^{\prime})\}=\{c_{\pm}(x),c_{\pm}(x^{\prime})\}=\{c_{\pm}^{\dagger}(x),c_{\pm}^{\dagger}(x^{\prime})\}=0
for
x\neq x^{\prime}
.
Using the latter, one can express
\mathcal{J}_{\pm}
defined in (
2.6
), as fermionic occupation number operators

\mathcal{J}_{+}(x)=\lim_{x\to x^{\prime}}\sqrt{\frac{K}{2}}:c_{+}(x)c_{+}^{\dagger}(x^{\prime}):\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{J}_{-}(x)=\lim_{x\to x^{\prime}}\sqrt{\frac{K}{2}}:c_{-}^{\dagger}(x)c_{-}(x^{\prime}):.

(B.6)

This justifies calling
\mathcal{J}_{\pm}
an electric charge density.

Appendix C
Kubo Formula

Here we rederive the expectation value of an operator due to a small perturbation. We consider the following Hamiltonian

\displaystyle H=H_{0}+\lambda\int dx\,\mu(x)B(x)\,,

(C.1)

where
\lambda
is a small dimensionless quantity,
B
is a time-independent deformation and
\mu
is an external source. In the Heisenberg picture, operators evolve with the full Hamiltonian
H

\displaystyle B(t,x)=e^{iH(t-t_{0})}B(x)e^{-iH(t-t_{0})}\equiv U^{\dagger}(t,t_{0})B_{I}(t,x)U(t,t_{0})\,,

(C.2)

where we have expressed the perturbation in terms of the operators
U
and
B_{I}
given by

\displaystyle U(t,t_{0})=e^{iH_{0}(t-t_{0})}e^{-iH(t-t_{0})},\quad B_{I}(x,t)=e^{iH_{0}(t-t_{0})}B(x)e^{-iH_{0}(t-t_{0})}\,.

(C.3)

The subscript
I
stands for “interaction picture” which defines the evolution in terms of the free theory. In our setting, these are the operators we control in perturbation theory.

In order to compute the expectation value, we need to find the evolution of the quantum state. Assuming that for
t\leq t_{0}
the state is given by
\left|0\right.\rangle
, the wave function for
t>t_{0}
is dictated by

\displaystyle\left|\Psi\right.\rangle=e^{-iH(t-t_{0})}\left|0\right.\rangle=e^{-iH_{0}(t-t_{0})}U\left|0\right.\rangle\,.

(C.4)

Now we consider
\langle\left.\Psi\right|A\left|\Psi\right.\rangle
and we compute it to first order in
\lambda
. This can be done by noticing that
U
satisfies the equation

\displaystyle i\frac{\partial}{\partial t}U=\lambda\left(\int dx^{\prime}\,\mu(t,x^{\prime})B_{I}(t,x^{\prime})\right)U\,,

(C.5)

therefore it can be perturbatively solved in terms of
\lambda
,

\displaystyle U(t,t_{0})=1-i\lambda\int^{t}_{t_{0}}dt^{\prime}dx^{\prime}\mu(t,x^{\prime})B_{I}(t,x^{\prime})+O(\lambda^{2})\,.

(C.6)

Then

\displaystyle\langle A\rangle=\langle A_{I}\rangle_{0}+i\lambda\int dx^{\prime}\int^{t}_{t_{0}}dt^{\prime}\mu(t,x)\langle[A_{I}(t,x),B_{I}(t^{\prime},x^{\prime})]\rangle_{0}+O(\lambda^{2})\,.

(C.7)

Placing the initial state in the far past,
t_{0}\to-\infty
, dropping
O(\lambda^{2})
terms and setting
\lambda=1
, we get

\displaystyle\delta\langle A\rangle=\int dx^{\prime}\,dt^{\prime}\,\,\chi_{AB}(t-t^{\prime};x,x^{\prime})\mu(t^{\prime},x^{\prime})\,,

(C.8)

with the response function
\chi_{AB}
given by

\displaystyle\chi_{AB}(t-t^{\prime};x,x^{\prime})=-i\Theta(t-t^{\prime})\langle[A_{I}(t,x),B_{I}(t^{\prime},x^{\prime})]\rangle\,.

(C.9)

Through out this work we have used (
C.8
) in terms of the frequency. By Taking Fourier transform one finds

\displaystyle\delta\widetilde{\langle A\rangle}=\int dx^{\prime}\,\widetilde{\mu}(\omega,x^{\prime})\,\widetilde{\chi}_{AB}(\omega;x,x^{\prime})\,,

(C.10)

where the Fourier transform of a function
O
is defined by
\widetilde{O}\equiv\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}\,dte^{i\omega t}O
.
