A simple model of radiating solitary waves
J. Douglas Wright

Abstract
. To understand an oft-observed but poorly understood phenomenon in which a solitary wave in a dispersive equation slowly deteriorates due a persistent emission of radiation ( i.e. a “radiating solitary wave”), we propose a bare-bones model which captures many essential features and which we are capable of analyzing completely by way of the Laplace transform. We find that wave amplitude decreases at an exponential rate but with a decay constant that is (in many cases) small beyond all orders of the frequency.



1.
Introduction

In the articles
[
27
,
32
,
14
]
, simulations of solitary waves in spatially heterogeneous variants of the Fermi-Pasta-Ulam-Tsingou (FPUT) and Toda lattices demonstrated that such waves do not propagate without change of form but instead continuously emit a small trailing ripple. The systems conserve energy and consequently the solitary waves experience a commensurate and extremely slow attenuation in amplitude. See Figure
1
for a representative depiction.

Figure 1.

[

]
.

A similar phenomena occurs in simulations of mass-in-mass lattices
[
16
,
9
]
, as well as in a variety of models for the evolution of capillary-gravity waves
[
4
,
5
,
33
]
, though in this latter case the emission runs ahead of the solitary wave. Simulations of water waves over variable bottom topography evoke a similar dynamic
[
13
,
25
,
22
,
26
]
. These sorts of waves, often referred to as
radiating solitary waves
, are examples of metastable structures in nonlinear dispersive systems.

The observed
attenuation is so slow that one of the major tools for analyzing the dynamics of solitary wave-like solutions, namely approximations of the problem with the Korteweg-de Vries (KdV) (or similar) equations
[
12
,
30
,
15
,
6
,
29
]
,
is incapable of capturing the phenomena.
Such approximations are valid over very long but nevertheless finite time intervals; the erosion is so subtle during the period of good approximation that it falls within the natural error bounds.
Moreover, radiating solitary waves
very often occur in problems where the construction of genuinely localized solitary waves fails and what is found instead are
generalized solitary waves
(also known as a
nanopterons
)
[
2
,
1
,
31
,
10
,
7
,
17
,
8
,
23
,
19
,
20
,
24
]
.
These traveling wave solutions are asymptotic at spatial infinity to very small amplitude co-propagating periodic waves and are consequently of infinite energy, further evidence that the dynamics of finite energy solitary wave-like solutions is subtle.

Unlike their steady counterparts the nanopterons,
there is at this time no fully rigorous mathematical explanation or description of these radiating solitary waves in any of the many problems in which they arise.
There are some careful non-rigorous treatments (especially
[
4
]
), but in the main investigations are heuristic or numerical.
The phenomenon is usually attributed to an excitation of some sort of high-frequency oscillation
by the solitary wave, due, for instance, to band structure considerations
[
14
]
, Bragg scattering
[
22
]
, internal resonances
[
9
]
or non-monotone dispersion relations.
Notably, the amplitude decays so slowly that in most cases it is very hard to determine the rate with any level of precision and only a few articles hazard a prediction, not all of which agree. For instance, the formal analysis in
[
4
]
predicts the decay is exponential with a very small decay rate whereas
[
14
]
(co-authored by the author of this paper) conjectures an algebraic rate of decay based on numerical evidence.

To better understand radiating solitary waves, in this article
we formulate a rather bare-bones model which captures many essential features at play and which we can rigorously analyze. For our system we are able to exactly pin down the rate at which the solitary wave decays as well as a rather complete description of the radiating tail.
Note that our model is decidedly not meant to quantitatively portray any of the specific systems mentioned but instead provide a partial skeleton for the rigorous analysis of such systems down the line.

Here are the main ingredients of our model:

•

A spatial variable
x\in{\bf{R}}
and time variable
t\in{\bf{R}}
.

•

A solitary wave of fixed speed and profile but variable amplitude:
a(t)q(x-t)
.
Here
q(x)
is the profile and
a(t)\in{\bf{R}}
is the amplitude. We assume that
q(x)
is real-valued
and satisfies some decay/regularity conditions we make precise below.

•

A high-frequency simple harmonic oscillator located at each point
x\in{\bf{R}}
.
We represent each oscillator by its complex amplitude
\psi(x,t)\in{\bf{C}}
. This field of oscillators is driven by
the solitary wave in a naive way:

(1.1)

\psi_{t}=i\omega\psi+\omega a(t)q(x-t).

In the above, the oscillators’ natural frequency is
\omega\gg 1.

•

A conserved energy

(1.2)

E:={1\over 2}a(t)^{2}+{1\over 2\omega^{2}}\int_{\bf{R}}\left|\psi(x,t)\right|^{2}dx.

The first term is proportional to the square of the
L^{2}
-norm (
i.e.
energy) of the solitary wave and the second is the energy of the oscillator field.

Together (
1.1
) and (
1.2
) form a closed system for the variables
a(t)
and
\psi(x,t)
.
Our main result states that
a(t)\approx a_{0}e^{-\theta_{\omega}t}
where
\theta_{\omega}
is all but equal to
1
1

1

The normalization/notation for the Fourier transform of a function
f:{\bf{R}}\to{\bf{C}}
we use is:
\displaystyle{\mathfrak{F}}[f](k):=\widehat{f}(k):={1\over 2\pi}\int_{\bf{R}}e^{-ikx}f(x)dx
.

2\pi^{2}|\widehat{q}(\omega)|^{2}
. Weak assumptions on
q(x)
tell us that
\widehat{q}(\omega)\to 0
as
\omega\to\infty
and as such
the rate of decay is very slow when
\omega
is big.
In particular, if
q(x)
analytic then one knows that
|\widehat{q}(\omega)|\leq c_{1}e^{-c_{2}|\omega|}
for some positive constants
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
.
As such the decay rate of
a(t)
is incredibly small, beyond all algebraic orders of
\omega
. For instance, we find that if
\omega=10
and
q(x)=\sech(x)
then the time it will take for the amplitude to deteriorate to
95\%
of its original amplitude is on the order of
10^{11}
.

We also prove several results about the asymptotics of the oscillator field, the most salient of which is that
e^{\theta_{\omega}t}\psi(x,t)
converges to a scalar multiple of a specific profile function

\sigma_{\omega}(x-t)
. This convergence is pointwise in the moving reference frame
x-t
.
We have an explicit formula for this asymptotic profile which ultimately leads to the description

\sigma_{\omega}(x-t)\approx iq(x-t)/\omega+\text{trailing periodic wave of extremely small amplitude}.

That is to say, when viewed in a frame moving along with the solitary wave the oscillator field looks like roughly like a copy of the solitary wave plus a tiny periodic tail, exactly the qualitative behavior seen in the systems we are hoping to understand.

1.1

.
In Section

. Lastly, in Section

2.
Reformulations and reductions

2.1.
An ODE on a Banach space

Differentiation of (
1.2
) with respect to time and the use of (
1.1
) to eliminate
\psi_{t}
get us:

0=a(t)\dot{a}(t)+{1\over\omega}\int_{\bf{R}}\Re\left[\psi^{*}(x,t)(i\psi(x,t)+a(t)q(x-t))\right]dx.

The quantity
\psi^{*}i\psi
is purely imaginary whereas
a
and
q
are purely real
so we can simplify this to:

(2.1)

\dot{a}(t)=-{1\over\omega}\int_{\bf{R}}\Re\left[\psi(x,t)\right]q(x-t)dx.

Note that now (
1.1
) and (
2.1
) are an ODE for the variables
(\psi,a)
on the Banach space
L^{2}({\bf{R}};{\bf{C}})\times{\bf{R}}
. Under the rather weak assumption that
q(x)\in L^{2}({\bf{R}};{\bf{R}})
it is simple enough to show that the mapping defined by the right hand sides of these equations is smooth from
L^{2}({\bf{R}};{\bf{C}})\times{\bf{R}}
into itself and as such Picard’s Theorem gives the existence of solutions for short times. The conservation law (
1.2
) then allows us to extend the solution for all times. In short, we can prove:

Theorem 2.1
.

Fix
q(x)\in L^{2}({\bf{R}};{\bf{R}})
and
\omega>0
. Then for any
\psi_{0}(x)\in L^{2}({\bf{R}};{\bf{C}})
and
a_{0}\in{\bf{R}}
there exists
unique

(\psi,a)\in C^{1}({\bf{R}};L^{2}({\bf{R}};{\bf{C}})\times{\bf{R}})

which satisfy (
1.1
), (
2.1
),
\psi(x,0)=\psi_{0}(x)
and
a(0)=a_{0}
.
The map carrying
(\psi_{0},a_{0})
to
(\psi,a)
is continuous (for
t
in compact sets). Additionally for all
t

{1\over 2}a(t)^{2}+{1\over 2\omega^{2}}\int_{\bf{R}}\left|\psi(x,t)\right|^{2}dx={1\over 2}a_{0}^{2}+{1\over 2\omega^{2}}\int_{\bf{R}}\left|\psi_{0}(x)\right|^{2}dx.

Before moving on, note that formulating our system as in (
1.1
) and (
2.1
) is a convenient starting point for performing simulations, which we do in Section
5
.

2.2.
A scalar delay differential equation

We can eliminate the oscillatory field from the governing equations entirely.
We solve (
1.1
) by Duhamel’s formula:

(2.2)

\psi(x,t)=e^{i\omega t}\psi_{0}(x)+\omega\int_{0}^{t}e^{i\omega(t-s)}a(s)q(x-s)ds.

Putting this into (
2.1
):

\dot{a}(t)=\begin{array}[t]{@{}c@{}}\underbrace{-{1\over\omega}\int_{\bf{R}}\Re\left[e^{i\omega t}\psi_{0}(x)\right]q(x-t)dx}\\
j_{\omega}(t)\end{array}-\int_{\bf{R}}\Re\left[\int_{0}^{t}e^{i\omega(t-s)}a(s)q(x-s)ds\right]q(x-t)dx.

Rearranging the order of integration in the second term plus some algebra yields

(2.3)

\dot{a}(t)=j_{\omega}(t)-\int_{0}^{t}\cos(\omega(t-s))q\star q(t-s)a(s)ds

where

\displaystyle q\star q(t):=\int_{\bf{R}}q(x+t)q(x)dx

is the autocorrelation of
q
.
It is worth pointing out here that
q\star q(t)
is an even function of
t
.

The scalar continuous delay differential equation (
2.3
) is equivalent to our original system and will be the formulation
on which we do our analysis. Nevertheless there is one more change we make
that permits
a short formal analysis.

2.3.
A scalar renewal equation

Integrating (
2.3
)
from
0
to
t
gives

a(t)=a_{0}+\begin{array}[t]{@{}c@{}}\underbrace{\int_{0}^{t}j_{\omega}(\tau)d\tau}\\
f_{\omega}(t)\end{array}-\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{\tau}\cos(\omega(\tau-s))q\star q(\tau-s)a(s)dsd\tau.

Exchanging the order of integration in the second term leads to

(2.4)

a(t)=a_{0}+f_{\omega}(t)+\int_{0}^{t}\phi_{\omega}(t-s)a(s)ds

where

\phi_{\omega}(t):=-\int_{0}^{t}\cos(\omega s)q\star q(s)ds.

Equation (
2.4
) is a renewal equation of the sort studied in
[
3
]
, though in most applications of renewal equations the function
\phi_{\omega}(t)
is positive with finite first moment (neither of which is the case for us).

We do a quick non-rigorous analysis of (
2.4
) now, in the case where
f_{\omega}(t)=0
.
Since
q\star q(t)
is even, the Fourier convolution theorem implies
\phi_{\omega}(t)\to-2\pi^{2}|\widehat{q}(\omega)|^{2}

as
t\to\infty
. If we simply replace
\phi_{\omega}
with this asymptotic state in (
2.4
) we have

a(t)=\displaystyle a_{0}-2\pi^{2}|\widehat{q}(\omega)|^{2}\int_{0}^{t}a(s)ds.

Differentiation of this gives
\dot{a}=-2\pi^{2}\pi^{2}|\widehat{q}(\omega)|^{2}a
and so
the solution of this simplified equation is
a_{0}e^{-2\pi^{2}|\widehat{q}(\omega)|^{2}t}
. This gives us our first glimpse as to what the rate of decay for
a(t)
is going to be.
Of course this replacement of
\phi_{\omega}
is completely unjustified which is why we now move on to rigorous analysis.

3.
The fate of
a(t)

In this section we determine the long time asymptotics of
a(t)
using (
2.3
) as the starting point.
The integral on the right hand side of that equation is a time-domain convolution of
a(t)
and

k_{\omega}(t):=\cos(\omega t)q\star q(t).

So if we apply the Laplace transform
2
2

2

Here, we use the notation that the Laplace
transform of a function
f:{\bf{R}}^{+}\to{\bf{C}}
is
{\mathcal{L}}[f](z):=F(z):=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-zt}f(t)dt.
to (
2.3
) we get

(3.1)

zA(z)-a_{0}=J_{\omega}(z)-K_{\omega}(z)A(z).

We have made use of a number of well-known properties of the Laplace transform here. We adhere
to the usual convention that
the Laplace transform of a variable whose name is in lower case is represented by the corresponding capital.

We isolate
A(z)
in (
3.1
):

(3.2)

A(z)={a_{0}\over z+K_{\omega}(z)}+{J_{\omega}(z)\over z+K_{\omega}(z)}.

From this we ascertain that the
key to understanding the evolution of
a(t)
is the function
v_{\omega}(t)
whose Laplace transform
is

(3.3)

V_{\omega}(z):={1\over z+K_{\omega}(z)}.

The following result tells us everything we need to know about
V_{\omega}(z)
:

Lemma 3.1
.

Suppose that, for some
\rho>0
,
e^{\rho t}q\star q(t)\in W^{1,\infty}({\bf{R}}^{+};{\bf{R}})
.
Then, for all
\omega
sufficiently large,
V_{\omega}(z)
has exactly one
pole in the set
\Re(z)\geq-\rho/2
. This pole is simple and located at
z=-\theta_{\omega}\in{\bf{R}}
where

(3.4)

\theta_{\omega}=2\pi^{2}|\widehat{q}(\omega)|^{2}(1+{\mathcal{O}}(1/\omega)).

Proof.

Preliminary estimates:

For functions
f:{\bf{R}}^{+}\to{\bf{C}}
we put:

\|f\|_{\rho}:=\sup_{t\geq 0}e^{\rho t}|f(t)|.

For functions with
\|f\|_{\rho}<\infty
we have the following elementary estimate, which holds for
\Re(z)>-\rho
:

(3.5)

|{\mathcal{L}}[f](z)|=\left|\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-zt}f(t)dt\right|\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\Re(z)t}\|f\|_{\rho}e^{-\rho t}dt\leq{1\over\Re(z)+\rho}\|f\|_{\rho}.

If we have
\|\dot{f}(t)\|_{\rho}<\infty
then an integration by parts in the above gets:

(3.6)

\begin{split}|{\mathcal{L}}[f](z)|=&\left|{1\over z}f(0)+{1\over z}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-zt}\dot{f}(t)dt\right|\leq{1\over|z|}\left(|f(0)|+{1\over\Re(z)+\rho}\|\dot{f}\|_{\rho}\right).\end{split}

This again holds for
\Re(z)>-\rho
. Lastly, the famous identity
\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}[tf](z)=-{d\over dz}{\mathcal{L}}[f](z),
combined with (
3.6
) implies

(3.7)

\left|{d\over dz}{\mathcal{L}}[f](z)\right|\leq{1\over|z|}{1\over\Re(z)+\rho}\left\|{d\over dt}\left(tf\right)\right\|_{\rho}.

There are no poles far away from the origin:

Note that the poles of
V_{\omega}(z)
are precisely the zeros of
z+K_{\omega}(z)
and consequently our analysis
will focus on
K_{\omega}(z)
.
The assumptions on
q\star q
and the definition of
k_{\omega}
imply that
K_{\omega}(z)
is analytic in the set
\Re(z)>-\rho
.
Moreover we have

\|k_{\omega}\|_{\rho}\leq\|q\star q\|_{\rho}<\infty
. Importantly,
\|k_{\omega}\|_{\rho}
can be controlled independently of
\omega
.
Using (
3.5
) we have

|K_{\omega}(z)|\leq\|q\star q\|_{\rho}/(\Re(z)+\rho).

The restriction that
\Re(z)\geq-\rho/2
tells us that

|K_{\omega}(z)|\leq 2\|q\star q\|_{\rho}/\rho
. Therefore

z+K(z)\neq 0
when
|z|>2\|q\star q\|_{\rho}/\rho
and the only place to look for the zeros is in inside the
set

U_{\rho}:=\left\{|z|\leq 2\|q\star q\|_{\rho}/\rho\right\}\cap\left\{\Re(z)\geq-\rho/2\right\}.

There is just one pole near the origin:

We use the fact that
\cos(\omega t)
is of high-frequency; the frequency shifting formula for the Laplace transform tells us

(3.8)

K_{\omega}(z)={1\over 2}{\mathcal{L}}[q\star q](z+i\omega)+{1\over 2}{\mathcal{L}}[q\star q](z-i\omega).

Using (
3.6
) on the right hand side we have:

(3.9)

|K_{\omega}(z)|\leq\left({1\over 2|z+i\omega|}+{1\over 2|z+i\omega|}\right)\left(q\star q(0)+{1\over\Re(z)+\rho}\left\|{d\over dt}q\star q\right\|_{\rho}\right).

We have assumed that
\left\|({d/dt})q\star q\right\|_{\rho}<\infty
and this quantity is clearly independent of
\omega
, as is
q\star q(0)
. In
U_{\rho}
we have
\Re(z)+\rho\geq\rho/2
and
|z\pm i\omega|\geq\omega-|z|\geq\omega-2\|q\star q\|_{\rho}/\rho
. All these together tell us that there are constants
\omega_{0}>0
and
C_{0}>0
for which

(3.10)

\omega\geq\omega_{0}{\text{ and }}z\in U_{\rho}\implies|K_{\omega}(z)|\leq{C_{0}/\omega}.

Now let
r:=\min\left\{|z|:z\in\delta U_{\rho}\right\}>0
, which is independent of
\omega
. Thus the preceding estimate allows us to find
\omega_{1}>0
so that

\omega>\omega_{1}{\text{ and }}z\in U_{\rho}\implies|K_{\omega}(z)|\leq r/2.

Thus we have
|K_{\omega}(z)|<|z|
on the boundary of
U_{\rho}
. Since
z
and
K_{\omega}(z)
are analytic in
U_{\rho}
we conclude, by Rouche’s Theorem, that
z
and
z+K_{\omega}(z)
have the same number of zeros (counted with multiplicity) inside
U_{\rho}
. Which is to say
z+K_{\omega}(z)
has one simple zero in that set.

And so, as foretold in Lemma
3.1
, we have shown that
V_{\omega}(z)=1/(z+K_{\omega}(z))
has exactly one simple pole (whose
location is denoted by
-\theta_{\mu}
) in the set
\Re(z)\geq-\rho/2
. What remains is to further pin down this pole as described in (
3.4
).

Characterizing the pole:

Since
q\star q(t)
is even in
t
we have

(3.11)

K_{\omega}(0)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\cos(\omega t)q\star q(t)dt=\pi{\mathfrak{F}}[q\star q](\omega)=2\pi^{2}|\widehat{q}(\omega)|^{2}.

The final equality is due to the convolution theorem.
The regularity/decay condition on
q\star q(t)
implies

{\mathfrak{F}}[q\star q](\omega)\to 0
as
\omega\to\infty
, and thus so does
K_{\omega}(0)
.

Differentiating (
3.8
) we have:

K^{\prime}_{\omega}(z)={1\over 2}{d\over dz}{\mathcal{L}}[q\star q](z+i\omega)+{1\over 2}{d\over dz}{\mathcal{L}}[q\star q](z-i\omega).

Then we use (
3.7
) to get

|K^{\prime}_{\omega}(z)|\leq{1\over 2}\left({1\over|z+i\omega|}+{1\over|z-i\omega|}\right){1\over\Re(z)+.9\rho}\left\|{d\over dt}\left(tq\star q\right)\right\|_{.9\rho}.

Note that we have used the fact that
\|f\|_{\rho}<\infty
implies
\|tf\|_{\rho^{\prime}}<\infty
for
\rho^{\prime}<\rho
.
Much as in the run up to (
3.10
), the above estimate implies the existence of
\omega_{2}>0
and
C_{2}>0
such that

(3.12)

\omega\geq\omega_{2}{\text{ and }}z\in U_{\rho}\implies|K^{\prime}_{\omega}(z)|\leq{C_{2}/\omega}.

The end approaches. Note that
K(z)
is real-valued if
z=s\in{\bf{R}}
. The fundamental theorem of calculus implies that

|K_{\omega}(s)-K_{\omega}(0)|=\left|\int_{0}^{s}K^{\prime}(s^{\prime})ds^{\prime}\right|

and so (
3.12
) leads to:

K_{\omega}(0)+(1-C_{2}/\omega)s\leq s+K_{\omega}(s)\leq K_{\omega}(0)+(1+C_{2}/\omega)s.

The function on the right is zero at
s=-K_{\omega}(0)/(1+C_{2}/\omega)
and the function on the left at
s=-K_{\omega}(0)/(1-C_{2}/\omega)
.
The intermediate value theorem then tells us that
s+K_{\omega}(s)
has a zero somewhere between these two points, which, because
K_{\omega}(0)\to 0
as
\omega\to\infty
, are both in
U_{\rho}
.
Consequently that zero
is
-\theta_{\omega}
, the unique zero of
z+K_{\omega}(z)
in
U_{\rho}
we found earlier.
All this, and (
3.11
), give:

{2\pi^{2}|\widehat{q}(\omega)|^{2}\over 1+C_{2}/\omega}\leq\theta_{\omega}\leq{2\pi^{2}|\widehat{q}(\omega)|^{2}\over 1-C_{2}/\omega}.

This is equivalent to (
3.4
) and the proof is complete.

Lemma
3.1
tell us that the rightmost pole of
V_{\omega}(z)
is located at
-\theta_{\omega}
. The common wisdom that

Theorem 3.2
.

Suppose that, for some
\rho>0
,
e^{\rho|x|}q(x)\in W^{1,\infty}({\bf{R}};{\bf{R}})
.
Then the the solution of (

) and (

where
\theta_{\omega}=2\pi^{2}|\widehat{q}(\omega)|^{2}(1+{\mathcal{O}}(1/\omega))
,
r_{\omega}=1+{\mathcal{O}}(1/\omega)
and
\|b_{\omega}\|_{\rho^{\prime}}<\infty

\rho^{\prime}<\rho/2
.

Additionally if
e^{\rho|x|}\psi_{0}(x)\in L^{\infty}({\bf{R}};{\bf{C}})
then the solution

) and (

Proof.

From Lemma

V_{\omega}(z)

r_{\omega}:=\textrm{Res}(V_{\omega},-\theta_{\omega})=\lim_{z\to-\theta_{\omega}}(z+\theta_{\omega})V_{\omega}=\lim_{z\to-\theta_{\omega}}{z+\theta_{\omega}\over z+K_{\omega}(z)}={1\over 1+K^{\prime}(-\theta_{\omega})}.

Estimate (

With this we now know that

W_{\omega}(z):=V_{\omega}(z)-{r_{\omega}\over z+\theta_{\omega}}

Because of (

\lim_{z\to\infty}zW_{\omega}(z)={z\over z+K_{\omega}(z)}-{r_{\omega}z\over z+\theta_{\omega}}=1-r_{\omega}

. Thus we have
|W_{\omega}(z)|\leq C/|z|

To get around this we let

B_{\omega}(z):=V_{\omega}(z)-{r_{\omega}\over z+\theta_{\omega}}-{1-r_{\omega}\over z+2\rho}.

The final term there is a sort of “fudge factor.” Note that
B_{\omega}(z)
is analytic for
\Re(z)>-\rho/2
. It is easy to see that
\lim_{z\to\infty}zB_{\omega}(z)=0
provided
\Re(z)>-\rho/2
.
Moreover a routine computation gives:

(3.15)

\begin{split}&\lim_{z\to\infty}z^{2}B_{\omega}(z)=(-2r_{\omega}+2)\rho+r_{\omega}\theta_{\omega}.\end{split}

Thus
|B_{\omega}(z)|<C/|z|^{2}
for
|z|
big enough. This rate is fast enough to use Lemma 76.4 in
[
21
]
and conclude that

e^{\rho^{\prime}t}{\mathcal{L}}^{-1}[B_{\omega}](t)\to 0
as
t\to\infty
for any
\rho^{\prime}<\rho/2
.

And so all together we find that

\begin{split}v_{\omega}(t)={\mathcal{L}}^{-1}\left[{r_{\omega}\over z+\theta_{\omega}}+{1-r_{\omega}\over z+2\rho}+B_{\omega}\right](t)=r_{\omega}e^{-\theta_{\omega}t}+(1-r_{\omega})e^{-2\rho t}+{\mathcal{L}}^{-1}[B_{\omega}](t).\end{split}

If
\psi_{0}(x,0)=0
then from (
3.2
) we have
a(t)=a_{0}v_{\omega}(t)
and the above leads directly to (
3.13
).

Now for (
3.14
). If
\psi_{0}(x)\neq 0

the decay conditions placed upon it imply that
\|j_{\omega}\|_{\rho}\leq C/\omega
; here is the calculation:

|e^{\rho t}j_{\omega}(t)|\leq{1\over\omega}e^{\rho t}\int_{\bf{R}}|\psi_{0}(x)||q(x-t)|dx\leq{C\over\omega}e^{\rho t}\int_{\bf{R}}e^{-\rho|x|}e^{-\rho|x-t|}dx\leq{C\over\omega}.

This tells us that
J_{\omega}(z)
is analytic for
\Re(z)>-\rho
. Thus the term
J_{\omega}(z)V_{\omega}(z)
in (
3.2
)
is analytic in
\Re(z)>-\rho/2
except for the pole of
V_{\omega}(z)
at
-\theta_{\omega}
. The same sorts of steps as above can be repeated to show that

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}e^{\theta_{\omega}t}{\mathcal{L}}^{-1}[J_{\omega}V_{\omega}](t)

exists, from which (
3.14
) follows.
∎

4.
The fate of
\psi(x,t)

Now that we have determined the dynamics of
a(t)
for large values of
t
, we do the same for
\psi(x,t)
. We begin by
observing
that because
a(t)\to 0
and we have the conservation of the energy (
1.2
) we know that
\psi(x,t)
does not converge to zero
in the
L^{2}({\bf{R}};{\bf{C}})
norm. This is not much of a statement, but it does indicate that the ultimate behavior of
\psi(x,t)
is
not disintegration.

For a more refined analysis, our starting point is
(
2.2
) which expresses
\psi(x,t)
explicitly in terms of
a(t)
.
Here is our first result:

Corollary 4.1
.

If
e^{\rho|x|}q(x)\in W^{1,\infty}({\bf{R}};{\bf{R}})
and
e^{\rho|x|}\psi_{0}(x)\in L^{\infty}({\bf{R}};{\bf{C}})
then the solution
of (
1.1
) and (
2.1
) with initial data
\psi(x,0)=\psi_{0}(x)
and
a(0)=a_{0}
satisfies (for
\omega
sufficiently large)

\lim_{t\to\infty}e^{-i\omega t}\psi(x,t)=\psi_{0}(x)+\omega\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-i\omega s}a(s)q(x-s)ds

pointwise in
x
.

Proof.

Divide both sides of (
2.2
) by
e^{i\omega t}
and take the limit. The integral converges due to the restrictions placed upon
\psi_{0}
and
q
.
∎

The most important takeaway from this result is that it indicates that
\psi(x,t)
does not decay to zero (in the supremum norm)
in the large time limit.
This can be make rigorous by drilling
down into the integral term above to get a more refined picture, but it turns out it is more interesting to view
\psi(x,t)
in a frame that moves along with the solitary wave
q(x-t)
. And so we put
x-t=l
and

\varphi(l,t)=\psi(x,t).

This converts (
2.2
) to

(4.1)

\varphi(l,t)=e^{i\omega t}\psi_{0}(t+l)+\omega\int_{0}^{t}e^{i(t-s)}a(s)q(t+l-s)ds.

We begin by considering the situation where
\psi_{0}(x)=0
and
a_{0}=1
, in which case
\varphi(l,t)=\gamma_{\omega}(l,t)
with

\gamma_{\omega}(l,t):=\omega\int_{0}^{t}e^{i\omega(t-s)}v_{\omega}(s)q(t+l-s)ds.

If we fix
l
and take the Laplace transform of the above with respect to
t
, the convolution and frequency shifting identities get
us

\Gamma_{\omega}(l,z)=\omega Q_{l}(z-i\omega)V_{\omega}(z)

where

Q_{l}(z):={\mathcal{L}}[q(\cdot+l)](z).

With our standard assumption that
e^{\rho|x|}q(x)\in W^{1,\infty}({\bf{R}};{\bf{R}})
we have

\|q(\cdot+l)\|_{\rho}<\infty
for any
l\in{\bf{R}}
. Thus
Q_{l}(z)
will be analytic when
\Re(z)>-\rho
. In turn this implies that
\Gamma_{\omega}(l,z)
will inherit the simple pole at
z=-\theta_{\omega}
from
V_{\omega}(z)
and that this is the only singularity when
\Re(z)\geq-\rho/2
.
It is easy to compute that

(4.2)

\textrm{Res}(\Gamma_{\omega}(l,z),-\theta_{\omega})=\omega r_{\omega}\begin{array}[t]{@{}c@{}}\underbrace{Q_{l}(-\theta_{\omega}-i\omega)}\\
\sigma_{\omega}(l)\end{array}.

Thus the common wisdom implies that
\gamma_{\omega}(l,t)
looks like
\sigma_{\omega}(l)e^{-\theta_{\omega}t}
as
t\to\infty
. We codify this in the following result, whose proof is so close to that of Theorem
3.2
we omit it:

Theorem 4.2
.

Suppose that, for some
\rho>0
,
e^{\rho|x|}q(x)\in W^{1,\infty}({\bf{R}};{\bf{R}})
.
Then the the solution of (
1.1
) and (
2.1
)
with initial data
\psi(x,0)=0
and
a(0)=a_{0}
satisfies (for
\omega
sufficiently large)

(4.3)

\psi(t+l,t)=a_{0}\omega r_{\omega}\sigma_{\omega}(l)e^{-\theta_{\omega}t}+\eta(l,t).

Here
\sigma_{\omega}(l)
is given in (
4.2
),
r_{\omega}=1+{\mathcal{O}}(1/\omega)
and
\|\eta(l,\cdot)\|_{\rho^{\prime}}<\infty
for all
\rho^{\prime}<\rho/2
and every
l\in{\bf{R}}
.

Additionally if
e^{\rho|x|}\psi_{0}(x)\in L^{\infty}({\bf{R}};{\bf{C}})
then the solution
of (
1.1
) and (
2.1
) with initial data
\psi(x,0)=\psi_{0}(x)
and
a(0)=a_{0}
satisfies (for
\omega
sufficiently large)

(4.4)

\psi(t+l,t)=\beta\omega r_{\omega}\sigma_{\omega}(l)e^{-\theta_{\omega}t}+\eta(l,t)

for some finite constant
\beta=\beta(\psi_{0},a_{0})\in{\bf{R}}
;
r_{\omega}
and
\eta(l,t)
satisfy the same estimates as above.

Remark 1
.

The result above is pointwise in
l
.
It is quite possible that a stronger mode of convergence holds here, though the technical difficulty
in establishing this is motivation enough to leave that for another article.

To close out this section, we now describe the “asymptotic profile”
\sigma_{\omega}(l)
in greater detail.
The analysis here is formal though it could be made rigorous if we stack enough hypotheses on
q(x)
.
Recall that
\sigma_{\omega}(l)=Q_{l}(-\theta_{\omega}-i\omega).

Since
\theta_{\omega}\to 0
as
\omega\to\infty
and
Q_{l}(z)
is analytic in
z
we have

Q_{l}(\theta_{\omega}-i\omega)=Q_{l}(-i\omega)+{\mathcal{O}}(\theta_{\omega}).

Then by definition we have

Q_{l}(-i\omega)=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{i\omega t}q(t+l)dt=e^{-i\omega l}\int_{l}^{\infty}e^{i\omega x}q(x)dx.

And so we see that

e^{i\omega l}Q_{l}(-i\omega)\to 2\pi\widehat{q}(-\omega)

as
l\to-\infty
and
e^{i\omega l}Q_{l}(-i\omega)\to 0
as
l\to\infty
. This latter convergence will be exponentially fast.
Lastly the same sort of calculation that led to (
3.6
) gives

Q_{l}(-i\omega)=iq(l)/\omega+{\mathcal{O}}(1/\omega^{2}).

Putting everything together we have

\sigma_{\omega}(l)\sim\begin{cases}2\pi\widehat{q}(-\omega)e^{-i\omega l}&l\ll 0\\
{i}q(l)/\omega&l\sim 0\\
\text{exponential decay}&l\gg 0.\end{cases}

In short,
\sigma_{\omega}(l)
looks like a scalar multiple of the solitary wave profile plus a trailing periodic wave of small amplitude and frequency
\omega
.

5.
Simulations

We have simulated solutions of our model with a variety choices of the profile
q(x)
and frequency
\omega
.
We always take
\psi_{0}(x)=0
since our analytic results indicate the effects of this part of the initial data are transient and do not alter the long time behavior (or at least the rate of decay of
a(t)
). Likewise
a_{0}=1
in all cases.

Our method is straightforward:
we treat the system as an ODE for
(\psi,a)
as in
(
1.1
) and (
2.1
) and simulate using an RK4 algorithm. We implement the integrals in (
2.1
) via Simpson’s rule.
We also compute
\sigma_{\omega}(l)
numerically, as for most functions closed expressions are hard to obtain. We again use Simpson’s rule for the computation. All simulations were done in MATLAB.

Before we get into the results we note that with our method high accuracy/long time simulations of the problem are challenging to obtain even for modestly large values of
\omega
. Roughly speaking, to accurately resolve a decay rate like
e^{-\theta_{\omega}t}
we would need to simulate out to times of
{\mathcal{O}}(1/\theta_{\omega})
and (for the RK4 method we use) have a temporal step size which is
{\mathcal{O}}(\theta_{\omega})
. The long time of integration in turn
implies a large spatial domain (also
{\mathcal{O}}(1/\theta_{\omega})
) is needed, since
q(x-t)
propagates in space. And we need to resolve that spatial domain at the same step size as the temporal one. All these considerations tell us that we need
{\mathcal{O}}(1/\theta_{\omega}^{4})
operations, at a bare minimum, to experimentally determine
\theta_{\omega}
in a quantitatively reliable fashion. And in the most interesting cases,
\theta_{\omega}
is exponentially small in
\omega
, meaning that we quickly reach a computational bottleneck.

In light of these inherent difficulties we simply integrate out to
t=1000
and choose our step size to be
\pi(10\omega)^{-1}
.
For the largest values of
\omega
, our simulations have been pushed past the point at which we can be convinced of their quantitative reliability and instead we view them as a qualitative illustration of our results (which are, after all, fully justified) and the phenomena they describe.

a(t)

where
\alpha
is just a scaling factor used to make the graph readable. Theorem
3.2
tells us that these should be nearly parallel when
\omega
is large and in fact we do see this.

a(t)

\omega
, as predicted by Theorem
3.2
.

5.1.
q(x)=\sech(x)

We lead off with this choice for
q(x)
because of the ubiquity of hyperbolic secant in profiles for solitary waves.
One has
\widehat{q}(\omega)=\sech(\pi\omega/2)/2
and consequently we have

\theta_{\omega}={\pi^{2}\over 2}\sech^{2}\left({\pi\omega\over 2}\right)(1+{\mathcal{O}}(1/\omega)).

We take
\omega=2,\ 4
and
8
. Results are shown in Figures
2
-
4
.
When
\omega=8
both the decay of
a(t)
and the trailing oscillatory tails of
\psi(x,1000)
and
\sigma_{\omega}(l)
have vanished to the naked eye.

5.2.
q(x)=e^{-x^{2}}

We selected this because the ultra-rapid decay of its Fourier transform renders the decay rate incredibly small
even at modest values of
\omega
. We have

\theta_{\omega}={\pi\over 2}e^{-\omega^{2}/2}(1+{\mathcal{O}}(1/\omega)).

We show results for
\omega=4
and
\omega=6
, in Figures
5
and
6
. Note how there is essentially no decay even at
\omega=6
. Likewise at this value the trailing oscillatory waves in
\psi(x,1000)
and
\sigma_{\omega}(l)
are invisible.

5.3.
q(x)=e^{-|x|}
.

This “peakon” profile
is not analytic and consequently the decay rate merely goes to zero algebraically fast and as such the decay is more obvious at larger values of
\omega
. To wit

\theta_{\omega}={2\over(1+\omega^{2})^{2}}(1+{\mathcal{O}}(1/\omega)).

We show results for
\omega=4,\ 8
and
16
in Figures
7
-
9
.
Both the decay and the tails are visible at
\omega=16
.

5.4.
q(x)=(1-|x|)_{+}
.

The subscript “
+
” mean to take the positive part, which is to say that
q(x)
is the “tent” map.
This is also non-analytic and its Fourier transform is sometimes zero (unlike the others) and as such if we fine tune
\omega
we can get solutions which do not decay at all. Specifically we have

\theta_{\omega}={1\over 2}\textrm{sinc}(\omega/2)^{4}

so if we take
\omega
to be an even multiple of
\pi
we should see no decay/no tails. We show results here (Figures
10
-
13
) for

\omega=2\pi,\ 3\pi,\ 4\pi
and
5\pi
and we see exactly this behavior.

6.
Conclusions, remarks and future directions

A key takeaway of this article is that in this simple model of radiating solitary waves the rate of attenuation in the amplitude
is very slow, but nevertheless exponential. This is in line with the results of
[
4
]
and not
the algebraic decay rate the author predicted in
[
14
]
. The extremely slow rate of decay predicted by the main results provides further evidence that radiating solitary waves present challenging complications both on the numerical and analytic sides. Indeed, even in this simple model really capturing the rate of decay numerically for large values of
\omega
would require a much more sophisticated approach than is used here.

One can reasonably ask, however, whether or not our model here will truly be reflective of the systems which possess radiating solitary waves. After all, the derivation of (
1.1
)-(
1.2
) is
ad hoc
and (as is always the case in such models) important features/considerations have been omitted. Indeed, in our model only the amplitude of the solitary wave is variable and it has fixed speed. In nearly all systems with solitary waves, the speed, amplitude
and wavelength
of the solitary wave are linked. This is particularly important in the proofs of stability of solitary waves in KdV
[
28
]
and FPUT
[
11
]
and in the derivation of effective equations for solitary waves in potentials
[
18
]
. By restricting to a fixed speed and width, our model is ultimately linear in its unknowns and this in turn makes the analysis by Laplace transform possible. Preliminary attempts at incorporating variable speed/wavelength result in nonlinear systems and, consequently, there are substantial technical challenges that make the application of the methods used here non-obvious. One avenue is to adapt the refined asymptotics for stationary problems in
[
1
]
to this time-dependent setting. Success in that venture will lead to the next, and most exciting challenge:
connecting such results rigorously to a full system with radiating solitary waves. Work is underway.
