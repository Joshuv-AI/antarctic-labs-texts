Approximating the Longest Common Subsequence problem within a sub-polynomial factor in linear time
Negev Shekel Nosatzki

Abstract
The Longest Common Subsequence ( LCS ) of two strings is a fundamental string similarity measure with a classical dynamic programming solution taking quadratic time. Despite significant efforts, little progress was made in improving the runtime. Even in the realm of approximation, not much was known for linear time algorithms beyond the trivial n \sqrt{n} -approximation. Recent breakthrough result provided a n 0.497 n^{0.497} -factor approximation algorithm [ HSSS19 ] , which was more recently improved to a n 0.4 n^{0.4} -factor one [ BCD21 ] . The latter paper also showed a n 2 − 2.5 ​ α n^{2-2.5\alpha} time algorithm which outputs a n α n^{\alpha} approximation to the LCS , but so far no sub-polynomial approximation is known in truly subquadratic time. In this work, we show an algorithm which runs in O ⁡ ( n ) O(n) time, and outputs a n o ⁡ ( 1 ) n^{o(1)} -factor approximation to LCS ​ ( x , y ) \textsc{LCS}(x,y) , with high probability, for any pair of length n n input strings. Our entire algorithm is merely an efficient black-box reduction to the Block-LIS problem, introduced very recently in [ ANSS21 ] , and solving the Block-LIS problem directly.



1
Introduction

The Longest Common Subsequence problem (
LCS
) is a classical string alignment problem. Formally, given two strings
x,y
of length
n
over alphabet
\Sigma
, the longest common subsequence between
x
and
y
is the largest string which appears as a subsequence in both
x
and
y
. The (length of the)
LCS
has a classical dynamic programming solution in quadratic time
[
WF74
]
.

LCS
is also a fundamental string similarity measure of key importance both in theory and practice, and therefore has been extensively studied during the course of the past 50 years
[
UAH76
,
Hir77
,
HS77
,
AG87
,
EGGI92
,
SW07
,
RSSS19
,
RS20
]
, with the main goal of improving the run-time from
polynomial towards close(r) to linear.

Despite significant attention, little progress was made, and the runtime has improved by logarithmic factors only
[
MP80
]
. More recently, fine-grained hardness results helped explaining the lack of progress, showing that any truly
subquadratic solution for LCS would refute the Strong Exponential Time Hypothesis (SETH)
[
ABW15
,
BK15
]
. Moreover, for the
LCS
problem in particular, it was later shown that even a high polylogarithmic speedup would be surprising
[
AHWW16
,
AB18
]
.

Even before the above hardness results, researchers started considering faster algorithms that approximate the
LCS
, but there as well the progress has been slow. The trivial
\sqrt{n}
-factor approximation was only recently improved to
\approx n^{0.497}

[
HSSS19
]
and even more recently to
n^{0.4}

[
BCD21
]
.
In parallel, a parametrized version was developed which gives a subquadratic solution with improved approximation when the longest common subsequence is promised to be above a certain threshold
[
RSSS19
]
.

In contrast, the progress for the corresponding Edit Distance problem has been more “rosy”, with a series of advances
[
BJKK04
,
BES06
,
AKO10
,
CDG+18
]
, recently culminating in a near-linear time, constant factor approximation algorithm
[
AN20
]
.

1.1
Main result

Our main result is a sub-polynomial factor approximation algorithm for
LCS
, which runs in linear time.

Theorem 1
.

\textsc{LCS}_{n}
can be approximated up to
n^{o(1)}
-factor, with high probability, and in
O(n)
time.

While we do not track the exact approximation factor in our analysis, we note it is
\exp\left(\tfrac{\log(n)}{\log^{c}\log(n)}\right)
for some absolute constant
c
. Interesting enough, we could not find a way to significantly improve the bound above, obtainable in linear time, even if one can afford
n^{1.99}
runtime. This relates to the issue of balancing of dense and sparse cases described in
[
ANSS21
]
, which is the core component of our algorithm.

To improve the bound, one would need to find a more efficient way to solve the
Block-LIS
algorithm from
[
ANSS21
]
.

One of the implications of this paper is that an affirmative resolution to the open question posed in
[
ANSS21
]
would yield surprising results, and in particular, a near-linear time,
(1+\epsilon)
approximation to
LCS
, which also provides near-linear
\epsilon n
additive approximation to the edit distance.

2
Preliminaries

2.1
Definitions

Sequences.

Fix alphabet
\Sigma
. We denote
x\in\Sigma^{n}
as a length
n
sequence.

For a sequence
x
, we denote by
\psi(x):\Sigma\rightarrow{\mathbb{N}}
, the count vector of letters in
x
, i.e.,
\psi(x)_{c}\triangleq|x^{-1}(c)|
for any
c\in\Sigma
.

We also define integer block sequence
z\in{\mathbb{N}}^{n\times k}
, and denote
|z|
as the count of all integers in all the blocks (with repetitions).

Monotone sets.

We
define monotone sets as follows:

Definition 2
.

We say that a set

P\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}\times{\mathbb{N}}
is
monotone
iff
for all
((i,k),(j,l))\in P\times P
, we have (i)
i=j\Leftrightarrow l=k
; and (ii)
i<j\Leftrightarrow k<l
.

One can observe that

\textsc{LCS}(x,y)=\max_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P\subseteq[n]\times[n]}}{{P\text{ is monotone}}}}\sum_{(i,j)\in P}{\mathbf{1}}[x_{i}=y_{j}]

The
Block-LIS
problem.

The
Block-LIS
problem was defined in
[
ANSS21
]

Block-LIS
included another extension which is not relevant for our reduction, and hence was omitted for exposition.
. In
Block-LIS
, the main input consists of
n
blocks of (at most)
k
elements each, and each block can contribute
at most one of its elements to a subsequence. Formally, for an integer block sequence

z\in{\mathbb{N}}^{n\times k}
,
\textsf{Block-LIS}(z)
is the length of a maximal sub-sequence
\textsf{OPT}\triangleq\{(w_{1},z_{w})\}_{w\in[n]\times[k]}

Other notations.

Fix vectors
a,b\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, then
\min(a,b)
(respectively
\max(a,b)
) is defined, as the vector of minimums (respectively, maximums) for each
(a_{i},b_{i})
pair.
Also, the notation
\oplus
means the direct sum.

2.2
Main technique -
Block-LIS
reduction

Our construction boils down to a formulation of the
LCS
problem as a (larger)
LIS
problem, and solve the
LIS
problem directly. Our reduction at its core is reminiscent to ideas introduced in
[
HS77
,
AG87
]
, with two important differences:

1.

We provide a simplified approach by direct “black-box” reduction to a
LIS
problem which is then solved directly. In particular, we reduce to the
Block-LIS
problem. As a result, the entire construction and proof takes roughly 1.5 pages.

2.

We show that the number of “matching pairs” is always bounded by
2n\cdot\textsc{LCS}(x,y)
using norm inequalities, implying the linear runtime from Lemma 4.3 in
[
ANSS21
]
.

In particular, we restate the following Definition and Theorem:

Definition 3
.

[
ANSS21
]

For
\alpha\geq 1
and
\beta>0
, an
(\alpha,\beta)
-approximation
\hat{q}
of a quantity
q
is an
\alpha
-multiplicative and
\beta
additive estimation of
q
, i.e.,
\hat{q}\in[q/\alpha-\beta,q]
.

Theorem 4
.

[
ANSS21
]

Fix
n,k\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
z\in{\mathbb{N}}^{n\times k}
.
For any
\lambda=o(1)
, there exists a randomized non-adaptive algorithm
{\mathcal{A}_{\text{BL}}}
that solves
Block-LIS
up to
(\alpha,\lambda n)
-approximation, where
\alpha=\left(\tfrac{|z|}{\lambda n}\right)^{o(1)}
using
\left(\tfrac{|z|}{\lambda n}\right)\cdot n^{o(1)}
time.

Block-LIS
Formulation for LCS.

To prove
Theorem

1
, we formulate the
LCS
problem as a single string
Block-LIS
.
Given
x,y\in\Sigma^{n}
, we define an integer block sequence
z
such that each
z_{i}
is a block of all
j\in[n]
indices where
y_{j}=x_{i}
. Formally:

z\triangleq\oplus_{i\in[n]}\left\{y^{-1}\left(x_{i}\right)\right\}.

(1)

We claim the following equivalence:

Claim 5
.

\textsf{Block-LIS}(z)=\textsc{LCS}(x,y)
.

Proof.

First, observe that any common subsequence of
x
and
y
has a monotone set
P\subseteq[n]\times[n]
where
x_{i}=y_{j}
for all
(i,j)\in P
. Now, since
j\in y^{-1}(x_{i})
, then the second coordinates of
P
are also an increasing subsequence of
z
.

Similarly, consider the coordinates of an increasing subsequence
W\subseteq[n]\times[k]
. Then
\{(w_{1},z_{w})\}_{w\in W}
is a monotone set where
x_{w_{1}}=y_{z_{w}}
, and hence
x
and
y
has a common subsequence of the same length.
∎

Our entire algorithm is merely an efficient implementation of the above reduction, and solving it using
Theorem

4
. In particular, we show the following:

Lemma 6
.

Suppose there exists an algorithm
{\mathcal{A}_{\text{BL}}}
which
\left(f\left(\tfrac{|z|}{\lambda n}\right),\lambda n\right)
approximates
Block-LIS
in time
t\left(\tfrac{|z|}{\lambda n},n\right)
. Then, there exists an algorithm
{\mathcal{A}}_{\textsc{LCS}}
approximating
\textsc{LCS}_{n}
up to a factor
(1+o(1))\cdot f(n\cdot\log n)
in
t\left(O(n\cdot\log(n)),n\right)+O(n)
time.

Proof of
Theorem

1
using
Theorems

4
and
6
.

The proof follow almost immediately from
Theorems

4
and
6
. However, a direct invocation of the statements only yields
n^{1+o(1)}
time algorithm. To obtain linear time, we first subsample
U\subseteq[n]
i.i.d. at rate
n^{-o(1)}
, and then apply the statements on
x_{U}
and
y_{U}
, noting
\textsc{LCS}(x_{U},y_{U})\in[n^{-o(1)},1]\cdot\textsc{LCS}(x,y)
. The resulting runtime is
|U|^{1+o(1)}=O(n)
as needed.
∎

2.3
Useful lower bound for the
LCS
using Hölder’s inequality

Hölder’s inequality.

A classical norm inequality which says that
\langle x,y\rangle\leq\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{1/p}\cdot\left\lVert y\right\rVert_{1/q}
for any
p+q=1
. In particular,
\langle x,y\rangle\leq\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{\infty}\cdot\left\lVert y\right\rVert_{1}
. Such inequality can be extended to show the following bound:

Claim 7
.

Fix
x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.
Then,
\langle x,y\rangle\leq\left\lVert\min\{x,y\}\right\rVert_{\infty}\cdot\left(\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{1}+\left\lVert y\right\rVert_{1}\right)
.

Proof.

Let
a=\min\{x,y\}
and
b=\max\{x,y\}
. Then, by Hölder’s inequality,

\langle x,y\rangle=\langle a,b\rangle\leq\left\lVert a\right\rVert_{\infty}\cdot\left\lVert b\right\rVert_{1}=\left\lVert\min\{x,y\}\right\rVert_{\infty}\cdot\left\lVert\max\{x,y\}\right\rVert_{1}\leq\left\lVert\min\{x,y\}\right\rVert_{\infty}\cdot\left(\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{1}+\left\lVert y\right\rVert_{1}\right).

∎

Simple lower bound for the
LCS
.

We next state a useful fact about the minimal
LCS
of two strings:

Fact 8
.

\textsc{LCS}(x,y)\geq\left\lVert\min\{\psi(x),\psi(y)\}\right\rVert_{\infty}
.

Proof.

That fact is immediate, since if each of
x
and
y
contain
k
copies of the same letter
c
, then
c^{k}
is a common subsequence to both
x
and
y
.
∎

We combine the above to show the following lower bound on the
LCS
:

Lemma 9
.

\textsc{LCS}(x,y)\geq\tfrac{|z|}{2n}
.

Proof.

Observe that by construction
|z|=\langle\psi(x),\psi(y)\rangle
. Now, invoking
7
and
8
, we obtain:

|z|\leq\left\lVert\min\{\psi(x),\psi(y)\}\right\rVert_{\infty}\cdot\left(\left\lVert\psi(x)\right\rVert_{1}+\left\lVert\psi(y)\right\rVert_{1}\right)\leq 2n\cdot\textsc{LCS}(x,y),

which implies the Lemma.
∎

One direct implication of the above is a simple deterministic and exact algorithm for
LCS
in time
O(n\ell\log(\ell))
, where
\ell=\textsc{LCS}(x,y)
. While we do not derive it in this paper, one can observe this can be done by simply reducing the
LCS
problem to
Block-LIS
, and solving it directly as a standard
LIS
problem, which takes
O(|z|\log(\textsc{LIS}(z)))
time.

Reduction Algorithm

In this section we prove
Lemma

6
. Namely, we are given an algorithm
{\mathcal{A}_{\text{BL}}}
for solving
{\textsf{Block-LIS}}(z)
with additive error parameter
\lambda
, and we use it to estimate
\textsc{LCS}(x,y)
.

The algorithm is the straight-forward one:

EstimateLCS
(x,y)
:

1.

Store the function
y^{-1}
over
\Sigma
(say, in the sparse matrix representation).

2.

Set
d\leftarrow|z|/2n
, where
z
is defined as in
Equation

1
.

3.

Run

{\mathcal{A}_{\text{BL}}}
with input
z
and
\lambda\leftarrow\tfrac{d}{n\log n}
.

4.

Output
\widehat{\ell}\leftarrow
the maximum between
d
and the output of
{\mathcal{A}_{\text{BL}}}
.

3.1
Analysis

We show the above algorithm provides the guarantees of
Lemma

6
.

Proof of
Lemma

6
.

First, let us argue
\widehat{\ell}\in\left[\tfrac{1-o(1)}{f(n\log n)},1\right]\cdot\textsc{LCS}(x,y)
.

Upper Bound.

The upper-bound is immediate since the output of
{\mathcal{A}_{\text{BL}}}
is promised to be at most
\textsf{Block-LIS}(z)=\textsc{LCS}(x,y)
, and since
d\leq\textsc{LCS}(x,y)
from
Lemma

9
.

Lower Bound.

Let
\widehat{\ell_{\mathcal{A}}}
be the output of
{\mathcal{A}_{\text{BL}}}
. Then, we are guaranteed

\widehat{\ell_{\mathcal{A}}}\geq\tfrac{{\textsf{Block-LIS}}(z)}{f(|z|/\lambda)}-\lambda n=\tfrac{\textsc{LCS}(x,y)}{f(n\log n)}-\tfrac{2d}{\log n}.

Therefore,
\widehat{\ell}=\max\{\widehat{\ell_{\mathcal{A}}},d\}\geq(1-o(1))\cdot\tfrac{\textsc{LCS}(x,y)}{f(n\log n)}
as needed.

Last, we show the runtime is at most
t\left(O(n\cdot\log(n)),n\right)+O(n)
.

Runtime Analysis.

The runtime for the first 2 steps is clearly
O(n)
. It remains to show the runtime on the
Block-LIS
algorithm, which is promised to be at most

t\left(\tfrac{|z|}{\lambda n},n\right)=t\left(\tfrac{2d}{\lambda},n\right)=t\left(O(n\cdot\log(n)),n\right).

This concludes the proof.

∎
