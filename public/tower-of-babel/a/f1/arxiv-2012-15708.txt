Geometry of the Wiman–Edge monodromy
Matthew Stover

Abstract
The Wiman–Edge pencil is a pencil of genus 6 6 curves for which the generic member has automorphism group the alternating group 𝔄 5 \mathfrak{A}_{5} . There is a unique smooth member, the Wiman sextic , with automorphism group the symmetric group 𝔖 5 \mathfrak{S}_{5} . Farb and Looijenga proved that the monodromy of the Wiman–Edge pencil is commensurable with the Hilbert modular group SL 2 ⁡ ( ℤ ⁡ [ 5 ] ) \SL_{2}(\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{5}]) . In this note, we give a complete description of the monodromy by congruence conditions modulo 4 4 and 5 5 . The congruence condition modulo 4 4 is new, and this answers a question of Farb–Looijenga. We also show that the smooth resolution of the Baily–Borel compactification of the locally symmetric manifold associated with the monodromy is a projective surface of general type. Lastly, we give new information about the image of the period map for the pencil.

We now consider

3
Precise determination of the monodromy

The goal of this section is to give a more refined version of the following theorem of Farb–Looijenga.

Theorem 3.1

(Thm. 1.1
[
6
]
)
.

The monodromy group of the Wiman–Edge pencil is isomorphic to a finite index subgroup of
\SL_{2}(\mathcal{O}_{o})
; in particular it is arithmetic.

Our proof will be independent of
[
6
]
beyond our used of their matrix generators in Lemma
2.5
. Farb and Looijenga also proved that the reduction of
\Gamma
modulo
\mathfrak{p}_{5}
maps onto a unipotent subgroup of
\SL_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{5})

[
6
, Cor. 4.3]
. They asked whether this congruence identity completely determines
\Gamma

[
6
, Qn. 4.4]
, and our results answer this question in the negative. In particular, we will see that there is an additional congruence condition modulo
4\mathcal{O}
.

Before stating our precise determination of the monodromy, we need a preliminary lemma that describes
\SL_{2}(\mathcal{O}/4\mathcal{O})
as a short exact sequence of algebraic groups over
\mathbb{F}_{4}
. Observe first that
\mathcal{R}_{4}=\mathcal{O}/4\mathcal{O}
can be described as
(\mathbb{Z}/4)[X]
and reduction modulo
2\mathcal{R}_{4}
is
\mathbb{F}_{2}[X]\cong\mathbb{F}_{4}
.

Lemma 3.2
.

There is a natural short exact sequence

1\to\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{4})\to\SL_{2}(\mathcal{R}_{4})\to\SL_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{4})\to 1

where
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{4})
denotes the Lie algebra of
\SL_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{4})
, considered as an additive group. The image
\SL_{2}(\mathcal{O}_{o}/4\mathcal{O})
of
\SL_{2}(\mathcal{O}_{o})
in
\SL_{2}(\mathcal{R}_{4})
is the pullback of
\SL_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{2})<\SL_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{4})
under this sequence.

Proof.

The first statement is very standard and follows from studying matrices in
\SL_{2}(\mathcal{R}_{4})
of the form
\Id+2M
and noticing that these are naturally represented by
M\in\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{4})
, where we consider
\mathbb{F}_{4}
as
(\mathbb{Z}/2)[X]
inside
\mathcal{R}_{4}\cong(\mathbb{Z}/4)[X]
. The second statement follows from the fact that
\mathcal{O}_{o}
is the pullback of
\mathbb{F}_{2}\subset\mathbb{F}_{4}
.
∎

Remark 3.3
.

Since
\mathbb{F}_{4}
has characteristic
2
, it can be identified with the additive group of matrices in
\M_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{4})
of the form
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}x_{1}&x_{2}\\
x_{3}&x_{1}\end{smallmatrix}\right)
, i.e., matrices with trace zero. As a group,
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{4})\cong(\mathbb{Z}/2)^{6}
.

We can now restate our main result.

Theorem A
.

The monodromy group
\Gamma
of the Wiman–Edge pencil can described equivalently in
\SL_{2}(\mathcal{O})
and
\SL_{2}(\mathcal{O}_{o})
by the following two properties:

1.

The reduction of
\Gamma
modulo
\mathfrak{p}_{5}
is unipotent.

2.

The reduction
\overline{\Gamma}
of
\Gamma
modulo
4\mathcal{O}
fits into an exact sequence

1\to C_{4}\to\overline{\Gamma}\to\SL_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{2})\to 1

where
C_{4}<\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{4})
denotes the subgroup of matrices
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}x_{1}&x_{2}\\
x_{3}&x_{1}\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{4})
so that
\Tr_{\mathbb{F}_{4}/\mathbb{F}_{2}}(x_{1}+x_{2}+x_{3})=0
, where
\Tr_{\mathbb{F}_{4}/\mathbb{F}_{2}}
denotes the trace.

Proof.

The proof is carried out by interpreting computations done in the computer algebra system Magma
[
4
]
. In particular, we describe how one gives arithmetic significance to standard Magma calculations and refer the reader to
[
1
]
for code that can be used to execute these calculations. Using the presentation for
\SL_{2}(\mathcal{O})
in Corollary
2.3
and the generators for
\Gamma
from Lemma
2.5
, one obtains a presentation of
\Gamma
as a finite index subgroup of
\SL_{2}(\mathcal{O})
with index
480
.

The fact that
\Gamma
has unipotent image modulo
\mathfrak{p}_{5}
is contained in
[
6
, Cor. 4.3]
, but we give an alternate argument as a warm-up for taking reductions modulo
2
and
4
. One can check with Magma that there is a unique normal subgroup of
\SL_{2}(\mathcal{O})
with quotient isomorphic to
\SL_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{5})
. Therefore this subgroup must be the congruence subgroup
\SL_{2}(\mathcal{O})[\mathfrak{p}_{5}]
consisting of those matrices congruent to the identity modulo
\mathfrak{p}_{5}
. The image of
\Gamma
in the quotient group has order
5
, which confirms our assertion, since all subgroups of
\SL_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{5})
isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}/5
are unipotent.

Similarly, one sees that there are two normal subgroups of
\Gamma
with quotient isomorphic to
\SL_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{4})
, where the second arises from its exceptional isomorphism with
\PSL_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{5})
. The subgroup that does not contain
\SL_{2}(\mathcal{O})[\mathfrak{p}_{5}]
must be the congruence subgroup
\SL_{2}(\mathcal{O})[2]
of level
2\mathcal{O}
. One checks that the image of
\Gamma
in the quotient is isomorphic to
\PSL_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{2})
. Since
\Gamma\leq\SL_{2}(\mathcal{O}_{o})
, this image must be the standard
\SL_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{2})
in
\SL_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{4})
.

We now study the reduction modulo
4\mathcal{O}
. From Lemma
3.2
, one sees that
\SL_{2}(\mathcal{R}_{4})
has order
3840
. Magma confirms that there is a unique normal subgroup of
\SL_{2}(\mathcal{O})
with that index, hence it must be the level
4
congruence subgroup
\SL_{2}(\mathcal{O})[4]
. Note that
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{4})
from Lemma
3.2
is the image of
\SL_{2}(\mathcal{O})[2]
under reduction modulo
4\mathcal{O}
.

One can then compute the intersection of
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{4})
with the image
\overline{\Gamma}
of
\Gamma
, and one obtains an index two subgroup of
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{4})
. Note that the group
C_{4}
from the statement of the theorem is also an index two subgroup, since it is defined by a linear equation over
\mathbb{Z}/2
, again considering
\mathbb{F}_{4}
as
(\mathbb{Z}/2)[X]
. One does not need Magma to see that
\mu^{3}
reduces modulo
4\mathcal{O}
to an element of
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{4})
not in
C_{4}
, hence it represents the nontrivial coset representative for
C_{4}
in
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{4})
. One then checks in Magma that the image of
\mu^{3}
in
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{4})
is also not in
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{4})\cap\overline{\Gamma}
, and it follows that
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{4})\cap\overline{\Gamma}=C_{4}
, as desired.

The above verifies that
\Gamma
satisfies the congruence conditions given in the statement of the theorem. We must check that the above conditions completely describe
\Gamma
as a subgroup of
\SL_{2}(\mathcal{O})
. This is equivalent to showing that
\Gamma
contains the congruence subgroup
\SL_{2}(\mathcal{O})[4\mathfrak{p}_{5}]
. Magma can easily check this by showing that
\Gamma
contains the intersection of
\SL_{2}(\mathcal{O})[\mathfrak{p}_{5}]
and
\SL_{2}(\mathcal{O})[4]
. This completes our description of how one verifies the theorem in Magma.
∎

4
The action on
\mathfrak{h}\times\mathfrak{h}

Let
\mathfrak{h}
be the upper-half plane. We now study the action of
\Gamma
on
\mathfrak{h}\times\mathfrak{h}
and the geometry of the quotient space
X_{\Gamma}=(\mathfrak{h}\times\mathfrak{h})/\Gamma
. Note that it is
\PSL_{2}(\mathcal{O})
that acts faithfully on
\mathfrak{h}\times\mathfrak{h}
, so it will be convenient to replace
\Gamma
with its image in
\PSL_{2}(\mathcal{O})
.

We first notice that
X_{\Gamma}
is a manifold, by which we mean as a Riemannian orbifold modeled on
\mathfrak{h}\times\mathfrak{h}
, not in the weaker sense of the analytic quasi-projective variety underlying
X_{\Gamma}
being nonsingular. This is well-known to follow immediately from the following.

Lemma 4.1
.

The monodromy group
\Gamma
is torsion-free.

Proof.

Suppose
\gamma\in\SL_{2}(\mathcal{O})
has finite order. Then
\gamma
has characteristic polynomial
t^{2}-\tr(\gamma)t+1
and some root of unity
\zeta
is a root of this polynomial. This implies that
k(\zeta)
is at most a quadratic extension of
k
. This reduces us to

n\in\{2,3,4,5,6,10\}.

An element of order
4
must have square
z_{0}
, and
z_{0}\notin\Gamma
, so we can eliminate that case.

Now note that the characteristic polynomial
p_{n}(t)
over
k
for an element of order
n
is

p_{n}(t)=\begin{cases}(t+1)^{2}&n=2\\
t^{2}+t+1&n=3\\
t^{2}+Xt+1&n=5\\
t^{2}-t+1&n=6\\
t^{2}-Xt+1&n=10\end{cases}

(For
n=5
, and
n=10
there is technically also the Galois conjugate polynomial
t^{2}\pm(1-X)t+1
.) Since the reduction of
\Gamma
modulo
1-2X
is unipotent, we must have that the reduction of
p_{n}(t)
modulo
1-2X
factors as
(t-1)^{2}
. This reduces us immediately to the case
n=5
.

Then,
p_{5}(t)
does not factor over
\mathbb{F}_{4}=\mathbb{F}_{2}(X)
. This implies that the congruence subgroup of level
2
in
\SL_{2}(\mathcal{O})
contains no
5
-torsion. Thus if
\Gamma
were to contain an element of order five, then its reduction modulo
2
would be a nontrivial element of
\SL_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{4})
of order five. However, the reduction of
\Gamma
is
\SL_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{2})
has order six, so it contains no element of order five. This proves that
\Gamma
is torsion-free.
∎

Remark 4.2

\SL_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{2})
. Since
\Pi^{\circ}
is a closed embedding, we see that the induced map
\widetilde{\Pi}^{\circ}
from
\widetilde{\mathcal{B}}^{\circ}
to
X[2]
must also be a closed embedding. It remains to show that
\widetilde{\mathcal{B}}^{\circ}
is genus one with
18
punctures.

Using the generators for
\pi_{1}(\mathcal{B}^{\circ})
in Lemma
2.5
and defining reduction of
\Gamma
modulo
2\mathcal{O}
as a homomorphism to
\SL_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{2})
, the number of lifts of each puncture of
\mathcal{B}^{\circ}
to
\widetilde{\mathcal{B}}^{\circ}
is the index in
\SL_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{2})
of the subgroup generated by the image of an element associated with a loop around that puncture. The images of
\gamma_{\alpha}
,
\gamma_{\alpha^{\prime}}
,
\gamma_{\beta}
,
\gamma_{\beta^{\prime}}
all have order
2
, so each associated puncture has three lifts. The element
\gamma_{\alpha}\gamma_{\beta}\gamma_{\alpha^{\prime}}\gamma_{\beta^{\prime}}
is the identity modulo
2\mathcal{O}
, so the associated puncture has six lifts. We then have that
\widetilde{\mathcal{B}}^{\circ}
has Euler characteristic
-18
and
18
punctures, so it has genus one. This completes the proof.
∎

6.3
The monodromy plane curve

Since
\PSL_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{2})
preserves the image of the lifted period map
\widetilde{\Pi}^{\circ}
, we conclude that
\widetilde{\Pi}^{\circ}(\widetilde{\mathcal{B}}^{\circ})
is an
S_{3}
-invariant curve of genus one on

with
18
punctures. Consider the completion

\widetilde{\Pi}:\widetilde{\mathcal{B}}\to Y[2]

and let
\mathcal{C}=(\sigma\circ\widetilde{\Pi})(\widetilde{\mathcal{B}})
be the image curve on
\mathbb{P}^{2}
. We call
\mathcal{C}
the
(Wiman–Edge) monodromy plane curve
. Given the maps and group actions described above, to completely determine the image in
X^{\circ}
of the period map for the Wiman–Edge monodromy, it suffices to describe the monodromy plane curve. Proposition
6.1
implies the following.

Corollary 6.2
.

The monodromy plane curve
\mathcal{C}
is a (possibly singular) plane curve of genus one.

6.3.1
Intersection of the monodromy plane curve with
\ell_{i,j}

We now study the intersections of
\mathcal{C}
with each line
\ell_{i,j}
. Since
\mathcal{C}
is
S_{3}
-invariant, this only depends on how
\mathcal{C}
meets a representative for each orbit. Notice that points on
\mathcal{C}\cap\ell_{i,j}
associated with a vertex of the icosahedron or dodecahedron lift to points on
\widetilde{\Pi}^{\circ}(\widetilde{\mathcal{B}}^{\circ})
, not punctures. Conversely, points on
\mathcal{C}\cap\ell_{i,j}
that are not a vertex of either the icosahedron or the dodecahedron lift to the cusp divisor for
Y[2]
, hence they are associated with the punctures of
\widetilde{\Pi}^{\circ}(\widetilde{\mathcal{B}}^{\circ})
; we call these

on
\ell_{i,j}
. The following should be compared with the description in
[
6
, §5.2]
of where the punctures of
\mathcal{B}^{\circ}
go in

.

Lemma 6.3
.

1.

There are either one or two puncture points on each
\ell_{i,j}\in\mathcal{L}_{1}
.

2.

Each line in
\mathcal{L}_{2}
contains exactly three or four puncture points.

3.

Puncture points on lines in
\mathcal{L}_{3}
or
\mathcal{L}_{4}
are fixed points for the
\mathbb{Z}/2
action on the line, where
\mathbb{Z}/2
is the stabilizer in

of the relevant triangle of lines as in Figure

. In particular, there are at most two puncture points on any such line.

The number of puncture points on any line
\ell_{i,j}
is an invariant of its
S_{3}
-orbit.

Proof.

The last statement in the lemma is obvious.

The orbit of
\mathcal{L}_{1}
is associated with the cusp of

denoted

in

. Indeed, this is the orbit consisting of exactly two triangles of lines on

. In terms of the action of

on

,

is associated with the lines
[X:1]
and

in

, which represent distinct cusps of
\SL_{2}(\mathcal{O})[2]
but the same cusp of
\SL_{2}(\mathcal{O}_{o})
. Since
\gamma_{\alpha}\gamma_{\beta}\gamma_{\alpha^{\prime}}\gamma_{\beta^{\prime}}
stabilizes

, we see that the six relevant punctures of

are given by intersections of
\mathcal{C}
with the lines in
\mathcal{L}_{1}
.

Similarly, the orbits
\mathcal{L}_{2}
,
\mathcal{L}_{3}
, and
\mathcal{L}_{4}
are associated with the cusp called

in
[
6
, §3.3]
, which is the orbit consisting of exactly three triangles of lines on

. With respect to the action of

on
\mathbb{P}^{1}(k)
, this cusp is associated with the lines

,

,
[1:1]

, which all represent distinct cusps of
\SL_{2}(\mathcal{O})[2]
but the same cusp of
\SL_{2}(\mathcal{O}_{o})
. Since
\gamma_{\alpha}

,
\mathcal{L}_{3}
, and
\mathcal{L}_{4}
.

Returning to

6.3.2
Smooth monodromy plane curve

In this section, we study the possibility that the monodromy plane curve is smooth. Mathematica code to assist the reader in verifying various assertions that follow is available from the author’s webpage
[
2
]
. Since
\mathcal{C}
is smooth of genus one, it is a cubic plane curve by the genus-degree formula. We first use
S_{3}
-invariance to restrict the possibilities for the equation of the curve.

Lemma 6.5
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{C}
is a smooth plane cubic curve that is invariant under the action of
S_{3}
described in §
6.2.3
with coordinates
[z_{1}:z_{2}:z_{3}]
on
\mathbb{P}^{2}
. Then
\mathcal{C}
is the vanishing set of the homogeneous cubic

\displaystyle F\left([z_{1}:z_{2}:z_{3}]\right)=a_{1}z_{1}^{3}

\displaystyle+a_{2}z_{2}^{3}+a_{3}z_{2}z_{3}^{2}

\displaystyle+\Big((2X-2)a_{1}-Xa_{2}-(X-1)a_{3}\Big)z_{1}^{2}z_{2}

\displaystyle+\Big((2-X)a_{1}+a_{2}+(X-1)a_{3}\Big)z_{1}z_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle+\Big((3X-5)a_{1}+(X+1)a_{2}+(2-X)a_{3}\Big)z_{1}z_{3}^{2}

for some
[a_{1}:a_{2}:a_{3}]\in\mathbb{P}^{2}
.

Proof.

Using invariance of
F
under
\sigma_{1}
, we see that either
F\circ\sigma_{1}=F
and every term of
F
of the form
z_{1}^{j_{1}}z_{2}^{j_{2}}z_{3}^{j_{3}}
has
j_{1}+j_{2}
even, or
F\circ\sigma_{1}=-F
and every term of
F
of the form
z_{1}^{j_{1}}z_{2}^{j_{2}}z_{3}^{j_{3}}
has
j_{1}+j_{2}
odd. In the first case, one can use a computer algebra program like Mathematica to see that invariance of
F
under
\sigma_{2}
implies that
F
is the singular homogeneous cubic

z_{3}\Big((X-2)z_{1}^{2}+2z_{1}z_{2}-(X+1)z_{2}^{2}+z_{3}^{2}\Big).

Under similar analysis in the second case, one sees that
F\circ\sigma_{2}=-F
(since
\sigma_{1}
and
\sigma_{2}
are conjugate in
S_{3}
) and furthermore that any such
F
invariant under
\sigma_{1}
and
\sigma_{2}
must then be of the form given in the statement of the lemma. Since
\sigma_{1}
and
\sigma_{2}
generate
S_{3}
, this proves the lemma.
∎

Note that some of the homogeneous cubics of the form given in the statement of Lemma
6.5
are singular. We will not need the finer distinction of which coefficients give a smooth cubic curve.

Bezout’s Theorem implies that
\mathcal{C}
meets every
\ell_{i,j}
in two or three points, where the case of two intersection points occurs if and only if
\ell_{i,j}
is tangent to
\mathcal{C}
. We begin by completely determining
\mathcal{C}
in the case where no
\ell_{i,j}
is tangent to
\mathcal{C}
.

Proposition 6.6
.

Suppose that the monodromy plane curve
\mathcal{C}
is smooth. Then
\mathcal{C}
is the plane cubic curve with one of the following equations:

\displaystyle z_{1}^{3}+(13-8X)z_{2}^{3}

\displaystyle+(3-X)z_{1}^{2}z_{2}+(18-11X)z_{1}z_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle+(3X-5)z_{1}z_{3}^{2}+(X-2)z_{2}z_{3}^{2}

\displaystyle z_{1}^{3}+(5-3X)z_{2}^{3}

\displaystyle+Xz_{1}^{2}z_{2}+(4-5X)z_{1}z_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle+(3X-5)z_{1}z_{3}^{2}-z_{2}z_{3}^{2}

Equivalently,
\mathcal{C}
is either

1.

the unique
S_{3}
-invariant smooth plane cubic tangent to the lines in
\mathcal{L}_{1}
at the points in
\mathcal{Q}_{2}
, or

2.

the unique
S_{3}
-invariant smooth plane cubic containing the orbit
\mathcal{Q}_{2}
(and no other
\mathcal{P}_{i}
or
\mathcal{Q}_{i}
) and the intersection points between pairs of lines in the orbit
\mathcal{L}_{1}
represented by the vertices of the triangle in Figure
14
.

Proof.

Invariance under
S_{3}
implies that
\mathcal{C}
meets one vertex of the triangle in Figure
14
if and only if it meets all the vertices. We break the proof into two cases, accordingly.

Case 1:
No vertex in the triangle from Figure
14
is on
\mathcal{C}
.

Lemma
6.3
then implies that each line in
\mathcal{L}_{1}
contains exactly one puncture point on
\mathcal{C}
. We first assume that
\mathcal{C}
intersects each line in
\mathcal{L}_{1}
transversally, hence in exactly three points by Bezout’s theorem. Then
\mathcal{C}
meets exactly two of the orbits
\mathcal{P}_{1}
,
\mathcal{P}_{2}
,
\mathcal{Q}_{1}
,
\mathcal{Q}_{2}
. However,
\mathcal{C}
cannot simultaneously meet
\mathcal{P}_{1}
and
\mathcal{Q}_{2}
or
\mathcal{P}_{2}
and
\mathcal{Q}_{2}
, since otherwise it would meet lines in
\mathcal{L}_{3}
or
\mathcal{L}_{4}
in four points, respectively. This leaves four cases.

Case 1(a):

\mathcal{P}_{1}\cup\mathcal{P}_{2}\subset\mathcal{C}

Since
\mathcal{C}
does not contain
\mathcal{Q}_{1}
or
\mathcal{Q}_{2}
, Lemma
6.4
implies that
\mathcal{Q}_{3}\subset\mathcal{C}
. One checks with a computer algebra program like Mathematica that there is no cubic curve with the form given in Lemma
6.5
containing all of
\mathcal{P}_{1}\cup\mathcal{P}_{2}\cup\mathcal{Q}_{3}
. Thus this case is impossible.

Case 1(b):

\mathcal{P}_{1}\cup\mathcal{Q}_{1}\subset\mathcal{C}

The only cubic as in Lemma
6.5
vanishing on
\mathcal{P}_{1}\cup\mathcal{Q}_{1}
is:

z_{1}^{3}-z_{2}^{3}+(X+1)z_{1}^{2}z_{2}+(X-2)z_{1}z_{2}^{2}-(X+1)z_{1}z_{3}^{2}+(2-X)z_{2}z_{3}^{2}

However, this cubic also vanishes at
\mathcal{P}_{2}
, contradicting our observation that
\mathcal{C}
meets exactly two of the orbits
\mathcal{P}_{1}
,
\mathcal{P}_{2}
,
\mathcal{Q}_{1}
,
\mathcal{Q}_{2}
. This case is therefore eliminated.

Case 1(c):

\mathcal{P}_{2}\cup\mathcal{Q}_{1}\subset\mathcal{C}

The only possibility in this case is the cubic considered in Case 1(b), which eliminates this case as well.

Case 1(d):

\mathcal{Q}_{1}\cup\mathcal{Q}_{2}\subset\mathcal{C}

Now, a computer algebra computation shows that the only possibility is the cubic:

(1+X)z_{1}^{3}+z_{2}^{2}+(1+3X)z_{1}^{2}z_{2}+(1-2X)z_{1}z_{2}^{2}+(X-2)z_{1}z_{3}^{2}-(2+3X)z_{2}z_{3}^{2}

We rule this case out using the triangle of curves in Figure
13
. The associated puncture points on the cubic curve are at the intersection of the lines in
\mathcal{L}_{3}
and
\mathcal{L}_{4}
and two points on the line in
\mathcal{L}_{2}
that do not lie on the other two lines. This implies that the triangle only determines three puncture points, but it must determine four. Indeed, the three triangles making up this
S_{3}
-orbit of must account for the
12
punctures associated with
\gamma_{\alpha}
,
\gamma_{\beta}
,
\gamma_{\alpha^{\prime}}
, and
\gamma_{\beta^{\prime}}
(cf. the proof of Lemma
6.3
). This contradiction eliminates this case.

We now consider the possibility that
\mathcal{C}
is tangent to a line in
\mathcal{L}_{1}
. Here
\mathcal{C}
meets each line in
\mathcal{L}_{1}
in exactly two points, hence
\mathcal{C}
meets exactly one of the orbits
\mathcal{P}_{1}
,
\mathcal{P}_{2}
,
\mathcal{Q}_{1}
,
\mathcal{Q}_{2}
.

Case 1(e):
The lines in
\mathcal{L}_{1}
are tangent to
\mathcal{C}
at either
\mathcal{P}_{1}
,
\mathcal{P}_{2}
, or
\mathcal{Q}_{1}
.

For each of these three possibilities, there is no
S_{3}
-invariant cubic passing through the relevant orbit that is tangent to
\mathcal{L}_{1}
at the specified points. Therefore, this case is not possible.

Case 1(f):
The lines in
\mathcal{L}_{1}
are tangent to
\mathcal{C}
at
\mathcal{Q}_{2}
.

The only possibility is the first cubic in the statement of the proposition. Then
\mathcal{C}
meets the triangle in Figure
13
in four puncture points, namely at the intersection of the lines in
\mathcal{L}_{3}
and
\mathcal{L}_{4}
along with three points on the line in
\mathcal{L}_{2}
. Therefore this case is a possibility.

Case 1(g):
The lines in
\mathcal{L}_{1}
are tangent to
\mathcal{C}
at a puncture point.

The cubic must also meet
\mathcal{L}_{1}
in exactly one of
\mathcal{P}_{i}
or
\mathcal{Q}_{i}
(
i=1,2
). For
\mathcal{P}_{1}
and
\mathcal{P}_{2}
, the cubic must also meet
\mathcal{Q}_{3}
by Lemma
6.4
. For
i=1,2
, the unique cubics through
\mathcal{P}_{i}
and
\mathcal{Q}_{3}
are:

\displaystyle z_{1}\big(z_{1}^{2}+(2X-2)z_{1}z_{2}+(2-X)z_{2}^{2}+(3X-5)z_{3}^{2}\big)

\displaystyle z_{2}\big(z_{2}^{2}+(X+1)z_{1}^{2}-2Xz_{1}z_{2}-(3X+2)z_{3}^{2}\big)

These are singular, which rules these cases out.

For
\mathcal{Q}_{1}
, there are cubics that satisfy the required properties with respect to the triangle of lines in
\mathcal{L}_{1}
. However, these cubics all would give seven puncture points on the triangle of lines in Figure
13
. This contradicts Lemma
6.3
, and hence rules out this case. There is no cubic with the required form that meets
\mathcal{Q}_{2}
and is tangent to the lines in
\mathcal{L}_{1}
in a point not in
\mathcal{Q}_{2}
. This completes the analysis of this case.

The final remaining option for Case 1 is:

Case 1(h):
The lines in
\mathcal{L}_{1}
meet
\mathcal{C}
in a triple point.

The triple point must be a puncture point of
\mathcal{C}
, so
\mathcal{C}
does not meet
\mathcal{P}_{1}
,
\mathcal{P}_{2}
,
\mathcal{Q}_{1}
, or
\mathcal{Q}_{2}
. Lemma
6.4
implies that
\mathcal{C}
meets
\mathcal{Q}_{3}
. One checks that there is no cubic of the appropriate form that meets each line in
\mathcal{L}_{1}
in a triple point, and this completes the analysis of Case 1.

Case 2:
Each vertex in the triangle from Figure
14
is on
\mathcal{C}
.

Note that a vertex cannot be an intersection point with multiplicity three. Indeed, then each line in
\mathcal{L}_{1}
would meet
\mathcal{C}
in two points, one having multiplicity three, and this contradicts Bezout’s theorem. We continue as in the first case by first assuming that no line in
\mathcal{L}_{1}
is tangent to
\mathcal{C}
. A line in
\mathcal{L}_{1}
then contains two puncture points, hence
\mathcal{C}
meets exactly one of the orbits
\mathcal{P}_{1}
,
\mathcal{P}_{2}
,
\mathcal{Q}_{1}
, or
\mathcal{Q}_{2}
.

Case 2(a):

\mathcal{P}_{i}\subset\mathcal{C}
,
i=1
or
2

Lemma
6.4
implies that
\mathcal{C}
also meets
\mathcal{Q}_{3}
. One checks that there is no such cubic with the form in Lemma
6.5
, hence this case is impossible.

Case 2(b):

\mathcal{Q}_{1}\subset\mathcal{C}

This case implies that the cubic is:

z_{1}^{3}+(1+X)z_{2}^{3}+Xz_{1}^{2}z_{2}-Xz_{1}z_{2}^{2}+(3X-1)z_{1}z_{3}^{2}-(1+4X)z_{2}z_{3}^{2}

This cubic curve meets the triangle in Figure
14
in what would be seven puncture points. In particular, it meets the triangle at the intersection of the lines in
\mathcal{L}_{3}
and
\mathcal{L}_{4}
, two additional points on each of those lines, and two points on the line in
\mathcal{L}_{2}
(neither of which is
e_{9}\in\mathcal{Q}_{3}
). This contradicts the fact that this triangle must determine exactly four puncture points, as we saw in Case 1(d). This case is therefore eliminated.

Case 2(c):

\mathcal{Q}_{2}\subset\mathcal{C}

The only possibility here is the second cubic given in the statement of the proposition. As in Case 1(h),
\mathcal{C}
then meets the triangle in Figure
13
in four puncture points, namely at the intersection of the lines in
\mathcal{L}_{3}
and
\mathcal{L}_{4}
along with three points on the line in
\mathcal{L}_{2}
. Therefore this case is a possibility.

Case 2(d):
The lines in
\mathcal{L}_{1}
are tangent to
\mathcal{C}
.

In this case, Bezout’s theorem implies that
\mathcal{C}
meets the triangle of lines in
\mathcal{L}_{1}
in only the three vertex points. Specifically, it suffices to study plane cubics passing through
\ell_{1,5}\cap\ell_{4,8}
that are tangent to either
\ell_{1,5}
or
\ell_{4,8}
. These are:

\displaystyle z_{1}^{3}+(X-1)z_{2}^{3}+Xz_{1}^{2}z_{2}+(X-2)z_{1}z_{2}^{2}+(X-1)z_{1}z_{3}^{2}+(1-2X)z_{2}z_{3}^{2}

\displaystyle z_{1}^{3}+(1-X)z_{2}^{3}+Xz_{1}^{2}z_{2}+(2-X)z_{1}z_{2}^{2}+(X-3)z_{1}z_{3}^{2}+z_{2}z_{3}^{2}

Neither cubic can vanish at any other point on the triangle, hence it cannot vanish on the orbits
\mathcal{Q}_{1}
or
\mathcal{Q}_{2}
. Lemma
6.4
then implies that it must vanish on
\mathcal{Q}_{3}=\{e_{9}\}
. Neither cubic is zero at this point, hence we have eliminated the possibility that
\mathcal{C}
is tangent to a line in
\mathcal{L}_{1}
. This completes the analysis of Case 2, and hence the proof of the proposition.
