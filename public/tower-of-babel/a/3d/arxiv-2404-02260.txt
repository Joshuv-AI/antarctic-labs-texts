On diffusion and transport acting on parameterized moving closed curves in space
Michal Beneš 1, Miroslav Kolář 1, Daniel Ševčovič 2

Abstract
. We investigate the motion of closed, smooth non-self-intersecting curves that evolve in space ℝ 3 \mathbb{R}^{3} . The geometric evolutionary equation for the evolution of the curve is accompanied by a parabolic equation for the scalar quantity evaluated over the evolving curve. We apply the direct Lagrangian approach to describe the geometric flow of 3D curves resulting in a system of degenerate parabolic equations. We prove the local existence and uniqueness of classical Hölder smooth solutions to the governing system of nonlinear parabolic equations. A numerical discretization scheme has been constructed using the method of flowing finite volumes. We present several numerical examples of the evolution of curves in 3D with a scalar quantity. In this paper, we analyze the flow of curves with no torsion evolving in rotating and parallel planes. Next, we present examples of the evolution of curves with initially knotted and unknotted curves. 2010 MSC. Primary: 35K57, 35K65, 65N40, 65M08; Secondary: 53C80. Key words and phrases. Curvature and binormal driven flow, nonlocal flow, Biot-Savart law, analytic semi-flows, Hölder smooth solutions, flowing finite volume method, knotted curves evolution.

[
36

[
20

[

[

[

[

[

[
44
]
). A closed simple curve can also represent a 2D cut through the myocardum surface, the heart muscle operated by electric signal broadcasted by the sino-atrial node
[
18
]
. This signal is represented by ions located along the cell membrane, their potential together with the ability of cell membranes to allow the motion of ions
[

Thin liquid pathways occur between grains and ice during soil freezing and mediate mass transport responsible for mechanical effects such as the frost heave (see e.g.

{\mathbf{v}}=a\kappa{\mathbf{N}}+b{\mathbf{B}}+{\mathbf{F}},\qquad\partial_{t}\varrho+R=\Delta_{\Gamma}\varrho+G,

We prove the local existence, uniqueness of classical Hölder smooth solutions to the governing system of nonlinear parabolic equations. The structure of the paper is as follows. In the following section, we provide a review of the direct Lagrangian approach used to solve curvature-driven flows of a family of curves in three dimensions. In Section 3, we establish a system of non-local partial differential equations for the parameterizations of the evolving curves. Section 4 provides a brief summary of the importance of tangential velocity in solving analytical and numerical problems. The local existence and uniqueness of classical Hölder smooth solutions are demonstrated in Section 5. Section 6 presents semi-analytical examples of curve evolution in rotating and parallel planes. In Section 7, we develop a numerical discretization scheme that utilizes the method of flowing finite volumes. Finally, Section 8 presents numerical examples of evolving curves and scalar quantities.

2.
Direct Lagrangian description for evolution of closed curves

In this paper, we consider a family
\{\Gamma_{t},t\geq 0\}
of curves that evolve in the space
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Its evolution can be described by the position vector

{\mathbf{X}}={\mathbf{X}}(t,u)
for
t\geq 0
as follows

\Gamma_{t}=\{{\mathbf{X}}(t,u),u\in I\},

where
I=\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}\simeq S^{1}
denotes the periodic interval. Therefore, we will identify
I
with the interval
[0,1]
. The unit tangent vector
{\mathbf{T}}
to
\Gamma_{t}
is defined as
{\mathbf{T}}=\partial_{s}{\mathbf{X}}
, where
s
is the unit arc-length parametrization defined by
ds=|\partial_{u}{\mathbf{X}}|du
. Here,
|\cdot|
denotes the Euclidean norm. The curvature
\kappa
of a curve
\Gamma_{t}
is defined as
\kappa=|\partial_{s}{\mathbf{X}}\times\partial^{2}_{s}{\mathbf{X}}|=|\partial_{s}^{2}{\mathbf{X}}|
. If
\kappa>0
, we can define the Frenet frame along the curve
\Gamma_{t}
with unit normal
{\mathbf{N}}={\kappa}^{-1}\partial_{s}^{2}{\mathbf{X}}
and binormal vectors
{\mathbf{B}}={\mathbf{T}}\times{\mathbf{N}}
, respectively. Recall the Frenet-Serret formulae:

\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}s}\begin{pmatrix}{\mathbf{T}}\\
{\mathbf{N}}\\
{\mathbf{B}}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0&\kappa&0\\
-\kappa&0&\tau\\
0&-\tau&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}{\mathbf{T}}\\
{\mathbf{N}}\\
{\mathbf{B}}\end{pmatrix},

where
\tau
is the torsion of
\Gamma
given by

\tau=\kappa^{-2}({\mathbf{T}}\times\partial_{s}{\mathbf{T}})\cdot\partial_{s}^{2}{\mathbf{T}}=\kappa^{-2}(\partial_{s}{\mathbf{X}}\times\partial_{s}^{2}{\mathbf{X}})\cdot\partial_{s}^{3}{\mathbf{X}}
.
We study a coupled system of evolutionary equations describing evolution of closed 3D curves evolving in normal and binormal directions, and scalar quantity
\varrho
computed over the evolving family of curves,

(2)

\begin{split}\partial_{t}{\mathbf{X}}&=a\partial^{2}_{s}{\mathbf{X}}+b(\partial_{s}{\mathbf{X}}\times\partial^{2}_{s}{\mathbf{X}})+{\mathbf{F}}({\mathbf{X}},\partial_{s}{\mathbf{X}},\varrho,\partial_{s}\varrho),\\
\partial_{t}\varrho&=c\partial^{2}_{s}\varrho+G({\mathbf{X}},\partial_{s}{\mathbf{X}},\varrho,\partial_{s}\varrho,\kappa),\end{split}

where the following scalar functions
a=a({\mathbf{X}},\partial_{s}{\mathbf{X}},\varrho,\partial_{s}\varrho)>0
,
b=b({\mathbf{X}},\partial_{s}{\mathbf{X}},\varrho,\partial_{s}\varrho)
and
c=c({\mathbf{X}},\partial_{s}{\mathbf{X}},\varrho,\partial_{s}\varrho)>0
. Source terms
{\mathbf{F}}
and
G
are assumed to be bounded and smooth functions of their arguments.
Both source terms
{\mathbf{F}},G
can depend locally on the position vector
{\mathbf{x}}
, the tangent vector
{\mathbf{t}}
, the scalar quantity
\varrho
, and nonlocally on the entire evolving curve
\Gamma
. We assume that the source term
G
may depend on the curvature
\kappa=|\partial_{s}{\mathbf{X}}\times\partial^{2}_{s}{\mathbf{X}}|=|\partial_{s}^{2}{\mathbf{X}}|
. Thus, the parabolic equation for
\varrho
may depend on the highest-order derivative
|\partial_{s}^{2}{\mathbf{X}}|
. As an example, one can consider the case where

(3)

{\mathbf{F}}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{t}})=\int_{\Gamma}f({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{t}},{\mathbf{X}},{\mathbf{\partial}_{s}{\mathbf{X}}})ds=\int_{0}^{1}f({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{t}},{\mathbf{X}}(u),\partial_{u}{\mathbf{X}}(u)/|\partial_{u}{\mathbf{X}}(u)|)|\partial_{u}{\mathbf{X}}|du.

Here
f:\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
, is a given smooth function.

Since
\partial^{2}_{s}{\mathbf{X}}=\kappa{\mathbf{N}}
and
{\mathbf{B}}={\mathbf{T}}\times{\mathbf{N}}
the governing equation of system (
2
) for the position vector
{\mathbf{X}}
and the evolution of
\Gamma_{t}
can be expressed in terms of the geometric equation

(4)

\partial_{t}{\mathbf{X}}=\beta{\mathbf{N}}+\gamma{\mathbf{B}}+\alpha{\mathbf{T}},\qquad\beta=a\kappa+{\mathbf{F}}\cdot{\mathbf{N}},\quad\gamma=b\kappa+{\mathbf{F}}\cdot{\mathbf{B}},\quad\alpha={\mathbf{F}}\cdot{\mathbf{T}},

where
\beta
,
\gamma
and
\alpha
are normal, binormal, and tangential components of the velocity, respectively. Let us denote by
L(\Gamma)
the total length of the curve
\Gamma
. It is given by

L(\Gamma)=\int_{\Gamma}ds=\int_{0}^{1}|\partial_{u}{\mathbf{X}}|du.

Furthermore, if the curve
\Gamma_{t}
evolves according to the geometric equation (
4
), we can derive an equation for the time derivative of the local length
g(t,u)|\partial_{u}{\mathbf{X}}(t,u)|
of the curve
\Gamma_{t}
:

(5)

\partial_{t}|\partial_{u}{\mathbf{X}}|=|\partial_{u}{\mathbf{X}}|(\partial_{s}\alpha-\beta\kappa),\qquad\partial_{t}L(\Gamma_{t})=-\int_{\Gamma_{t}}\beta\kappa ds,

where
\alpha
and
\beta
are tangential and normal components of velocity in (
4
), respectively.

3.
Parabolic equation for a scalar quantity on an evolving curve

Assume that
\hat{\varrho}
is a time-dependent function
\hat{\varrho}(t,\cdot):\Gamma_{t}\to\mathbb{R}
,
t\in[0,T]
, where
\Gamma_{t}
is a curve parameterized by its position vector
{\mathbf{X}}
, that is,
\Gamma_{t}=\{{\mathbf{X}}(t,u),u\in I\}
. In what follows, we shall denote by
\phi(t,u)
the scalar quantity evaluated at time
t
and the parameter
u\in[0,1]
while
\hat{\phi}(t,{\mathbf{X}})
stands for a scalar quantity evaluated at time
t
and a point
{\mathbf{X}}\in\Gamma_{t}
related to each other through equality
\phi(t,u):=\hat{\phi}(t,{\mathbf{X}}(t,u))
. Let
t\in[0,T]
be fixed. The integral of the first kind of scalar quantity
\hat{\varrho}
along the curve segment
\widehat{{\mathbf{X}_{1}}{\mathbf{X}}_{2}}\subset\Gamma_{t}
between the points
{\mathbf{X}}_{i}={\mathbf{X}}(t,u_{i})\in\Gamma_{t},i=1,2
, is given by:

(6)

m(t)=\int_{\widehat{{\mathbf{X}_{1}}{\mathbf{X}}_{2}}}\hat{\varrho}(t,{\mathbf{X}})\text{d}s=\int_{u_{1}}^{u_{2}}\varrho(t,u)|\partial_{u}{\mathbf{X}}(t,u)|\text{d}u,

where
\varrho(t,u)=\hat{\varrho}(t,{\mathbf{X}}(t,u))
is the density along the curve
\Gamma_{t}
evaluated in the terms of the dimensionless parameter
u\in[u_{1},u_{2}]\subseteq[0,1],{\mathbf{X}}_{i}={\mathbf{X}}(t,u_{i})
. The integral of the first kind
m(t)
is independent of a parameterization of the curve
\Gamma_{t}
. To derive an advection-diffusion equation for the scalar quantity
\varrho
we assume that the time derivative of
m(t)
can be expressed in terms of inflow and outflow of the flux
\hat{j}(t,{\mathbf{X}})
through the end points
{\mathbf{X}}_{1}
and
{\mathbf{X}_{2}}
of the segment
\widehat{{\mathbf{X}_{1}}{\mathbf{X}}_{2}}
, and the prescribed external source term with a density
\hat{q}(t,{\mathbf{X}})
of the segment as follows:

\displaystyle\frac{\text{d}m}{\text{d}t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\left[\hat{j}(t,{\mathbf{X}})\right]_{{\mathbf{X}}_{1}}^{{\mathbf{X}}_{2}}+\int_{\widehat{{\mathbf{X}_{1}}{\mathbf{X}}_{2}}}\hat{q}(t,{\mathbf{X}})\text{d}s=-\left[j(t,u)\right]_{u_{1}}^{u_{2}}+\int_{u_{1}}^{u_{2}}q(t,u)|\partial_{u}{\mathbf{X}}|\text{d}u

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{u_{1}}^{u_{2}}\frac{\partial}{\partial u}j(t,u)\text{d}u+\int_{u_{1}}^{u_{2}}q(t,u)|\partial_{u}{\mathbf{X}}|\text{d}u

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{u_{1}}^{u_{2}}\frac{1}{|\partial_{u}{\mathbf{X}}|}\frac{\partial}{\partial u}j(t,u)|\partial_{u}{\mathbf{X}}|\text{d}u+\int_{u_{1}}^{u_{2}}q(t,u)|\partial_{u}{\mathbf{X}}|\text{d}u

where
j(t,u)=\hat{j}(t,{\mathbf{X}}(t,u)),q(t,u)=\hat{q}(t,{\mathbf{X}}(t,u))
.

We assume that the the mass flux along the curve is expressed as a sum of the advection term and the linear Fickian term with a diffusion constant
k>0
:

\hat{j}=-k\partial_{s}\hat{\varrho}+v\hat{\varrho}=-k\frac{1}{|\partial_{u}{\mathbf{X}}|}\frac{\partial\varrho}{\partial u}+v\varrho,

where
\partial_{s}
denotes the derivative w.r.t. to the arc-length parameterization
s
, and
v(t,u)=\hat{v}(t,{\mathbf{X}}(t,u))
denotes the advection velocity along the curve. By substituting, we obtain the following equation:

(7)

\frac{\text{d}m}{\text{d}t}=\int_{u_{1}}^{u_{2}}\left[\frac{1}{|\partial_{u}{\mathbf{X}}|}\frac{\partial}{\partial u}\left(\frac{k}{|\partial_{u}{\mathbf{X}}|}\frac{\partial\varrho}{\partial u}\right)-\frac{1}{|\partial_{u}{\mathbf{X}}|}\frac{\partial(v\varrho)}{\partial u}+q\right]|\partial_{u}{\mathbf{X}}|\text{d}u.

Here, we can see that the tangential motion of
\Gamma_{t}
can also influence the distribution of the quantity
\varrho
along
\Gamma_{t}
. Using (
5
) in (
7
) we finally obtain the advection-diffusion equation:

(8)

\frac{\partial\varrho}{\partial t}+\varrho\left(\partial_{s}\alpha-\kappa\beta\right)=\partial_{s}\left(k\partial_{s}\varrho\right)-\partial_{s}(\varrho v)+q

for the scalar quantity
\varrho
depending on the evolving curve
\Gamma_{t}
.

4.
The role of the tangential velocity

Recall that tangential component
\alpha
of the velocity of evolving closed curves
\{\Gamma_{t},t\geq 0\}
has no impact on shape of evolving curves (see e.g. Epstein and Gage
[
13
]
). On the other hand, considering a numerical solution of (
4
), properly chosen tangential velocity functional
\alpha={\mathbf{F}}\cdot{\mathbf{T}}
plays an important role in the stability of a computational scheme (see e.g. Mikula and Ševčovič
[
31
,
32
,
33
]
). The tangential velocity has a significant impact on the analysis of the evolution of curves from both the analytical and numerical points of view. It was shown by Hou et al.
[
21
]
, Kimura
[
25
]
, Mikula and Ševčovič
[
31
,
32
,
33
]
, Yazaki and Ševčovič
[
42
]
. Barrett
et al.

[
6
,
7
]
, Elliott and Fritz
[
12
]
, investigated the gradient and elastic flows for closed and open curves in
\mathbb{R}^{d},d\geq 2
. They constructed a numerical approximation scheme using a suitable tangential redistribution. Beneš, Kolář and Ševčovič investigated the role of tangential velocity in the context of material science
[
26
]
and the evolution of interacting curves
[
8
]
,
[
9
]
. In
[
17
]
Garcke, Kohsaka and Ševčovič applied the uniform tangential redistribution in the theoretical proof of nonlinear stability of stationary solutions for curvature driven flow with triple junction in the plane. In
[
38
]
Remešíková
et al.
proposed and analysed the tangential redistribution for flows of closed manifolds in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Using equations (
5
) we can calculate the time derivative of the ratio of the relative local length
|\partial_{u}{\mathbf{X}}(u,t)|
to the total length
L(\Gamma_{t})=\int_{0}^{1}|\partial_{u}{\mathbf{X}}(u,t)|du
:

(9)

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\frac{|\partial_{u}{\mathbf{X}}(u,t)|}{L(\Gamma_{t})}=\frac{|\partial_{u}{\mathbf{X}}(u,t)|}{L(\Gamma_{t})}\left(\partial_{s}\alpha-\kappa\beta+\langle\kappa\beta\rangle\right),\quad\text{where}\ \langle\kappa\beta\rangle=\frac{1}{L(\Gamma)}\int_{\Gamma}\kappa\beta ds.

Therefore, the ratio
|\partial_{u}{\mathbf{X}}(u,t)|/L(\Gamma_{t})
is constant with respect to the time
t
, i.e.

(10)

\frac{|\partial_{u}{\mathbf{X}}(u,t)|}{L(\Gamma_{t})}=\frac{|\partial_{u}{\mathbf{X}}(u,0)|}{L(\Gamma_{0})},\quad\text{for any}\ t\geq 0,

provided that the tangential velocity satisfies
\partial_{s}\alpha=\kappa\beta-\langle\kappa\beta\rangle
. Another suitable choice of the tangential velocity
\alpha
is the so-called asymptotically uniform tangential velocity proposed and analyzed by Mikula and Ševčovič in
[
32
,
33
]
. If
\omega>0
and

(11)

\partial_{s}\alpha=\kappa\beta-\langle\kappa\beta\rangle+\left(\frac{L(\Gamma_{t})}{|\partial_{u}{\mathbf{X}}(u,t)|}-1\right)\omega,

then, using (
9
) we obtain

\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{|\partial_{u}{\mathbf{X}}(u,t)|}{L(\Gamma_{t})}=1
uniformly with respect to
u\in[0,1]
provided
\omega>0
is a positive constant. It means that the redistribution becomes asymptotically uniform.

5.
Existence and uniqueness of classical Hölder smooth solutions

In this section, we present results on the existence and uniqueness of the classical Hölder smooth solution to the system of equations (
2
) that governs the evolution of a curve
\Gamma
parameterized by
{\mathbf{X}}
and a scalar quantitity
\varrho
. We follow the analytical approach developed by Beneš, Kolář, Ševčovič
[
9
]
. The main idea of the proof is based on the abstract theory of analytic semiflows in Banach spaces due to DaPrato and Grisvard
[
10
]
, Angenent
[
5
,
4
]
, Lunardi
[
29
]
. The proof of the local existence and uniqueness of a classical Hölder smooth solution is based on the analysis of the position vector equation (
2
) in which we choose the uniform tangential velocity
\alpha
. It leads to a uniformly parabolic equation (
2
) provided that the diffusion coefficient
a
is uniformly bounded from below by a positive constant. In our proof method, the assumptions of strict positivity of the curvature
\kappa
and the existence of the Frenet frame are not required. The main idea is to rewrite the system (
2
) in the form of an initial value problem for the abstract parabolic equation:

(12)

\partial_{t}\Phi=\mathscr{F}(\Phi),\quad\Phi(0)=\Phi_{0},

in a scale of Banach spaces. Furthermore, we have to show that, for any
\tilde{\Phi}
, the linearization
\mathscr{A}=\mathscr{F}^{\prime}(\tilde{\Phi})
generates an analytic semigroup and belongs to the so-called maximal regularity class. Since the source term
G
may depend on the curvature
\kappa=|\partial_{s}{\mathbf{X}}\times\partial^{2}_{s}{\mathbf{X}}|=|\partial_{s}^{2}{\mathbf{X}}|
. Thus, the parabolic equation for
\varrho
may depend on the derivative of highest order
|\partial_{s}^{2}{\mathbf{X}}|
. As a consequence, linearization
\mathscr{F}^{\prime}(\tilde{\Phi})
is a skew symmetric linear operator having an off-diagonal term.

First, we define the underlying function spaces. Assume that
0<\varepsilon<1
and
k
is a non-negative integer. Let us denote by
h^{k+\varepsilon}(S^{1})
the so-called little Hölder space, that is, the Banach space, which is the closure of
C^{\infty}
smooth functions in the norm of the Banach space of
C^{k}
smooth functions defined in the periodic domain
S^{1}
, and such that the
k
-th derivative is
\varepsilon
-Hölder smooth. The norm is given as a sum of the
C^{k}
norm and the Hölder semi-norm of the
k
-th derivative. For a given
0<\varepsilon<1
we define the following scale of Banach spaces of
\varepsilon
-Hölder continuous functions defined in the periodic domain
S^{1}
:

(13)

E_{k}=h^{2k+\varepsilon}(S^{1})\times h^{2k+\varepsilon}(S^{1})\times h^{2k+\varepsilon}(S^{1}),\quad\mathscr{E}_{k}=E_{k}\times h^{2k+\varepsilon}(S^{1}),\quad k=0,1/2,1.

Recall the following continuous and compact embedding:
{\mathcal{E}}_{1}\hookrightarrow{\mathcal{E}}_{1/2}\hookrightarrow{\mathcal{E}}_{0}
. Furthermore, using the interpolation inequality at the scale of Hölder spaces we have

(14)

\|\Phi\|_{{\mathcal{E}}_{\frac{1}{2}}}\leq\|\Phi\|_{{\mathcal{E}}_{1}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\Phi\|_{{\mathcal{E}}_{0}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq\delta\|\Phi\|_{{\mathcal{E}}_{1}}+\frac{1}{4\delta}\|\Phi\|_{{\mathcal{E}}_{0}},\quad\text{for any}\ \delta>0\ \text{and}\ \Phi\in{\mathcal{E}}_{1}.

For
a,b\in h^{1+\varepsilon}(S^{1})
and
{\mathbf{t}}=(t_{1},t_{2},t_{3})^{T}\in E_{\frac{1}{2}}
we define the matrix function

A(a,b,{\mathbf{t}})=aI+b[{\mathbf{t}}]_{\times}:=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}a&-bt_{3}&bt_{2}\\
bt_{3}&a&-bt_{1}\\
-bt_{2}&bt_{1}&a\end{array}\right).

Clearly,

a\partial^{2}_{s}{\mathbf{X}}+b(\partial_{s}{\mathbf{X}}\times\partial^{2}_{s}{\mathbf{X}})=A(a,b,\partial_{s}{\mathbf{X}})\partial^{2}_{s}{\mathbf{X}}
. Then the system of equations (
2
)
can be rewritten as follows:

(15)

\begin{split}\partial_{t}{\mathbf{X}}&=A(a,b,\partial_{s}{\mathbf{X}})\partial^{2}_{s}{\mathbf{X}}+{\mathbf{F}}({\mathbf{X}},\partial_{s}{\mathbf{X}},\varrho,\partial_{s}\varrho),\\
\partial_{t}\varrho&=c\partial^{2}_{s}\varrho+G({\mathbf{X}},\partial_{s}{\mathbf{X}},\varrho,\partial_{s}\varrho,\kappa).\end{split}

Let us denote
\Phi=({\mathbf{X}},\varrho)^{T}
. In what follows, we shall assume that the mappings

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}_{1/2}\ni\Phi\mapsto F({\mathbf{X}},\partial_{s}{\mathbf{X}},\varrho,\partial_{s}\varrho)\in E_{0},

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}_{1/2}\times h^{\varepsilon}(S^{1})\ni(\Phi,\kappa)\mapsto G({\mathbf{X}},\partial_{s}{\mathbf{X}},\varrho,\partial_{s}\varrho,\kappa)\in h^{\varepsilon}(S^{1}),

are
C^{1}
continuous and globally Lipschitz continuous.

Then the system of governing equations (
15
) can be rewritten in the abstract form (
12
) where
\mathscr{F}
is defined by the right-hand side of (
15
).
Suppose that
\tilde{\Phi}=(\tilde{{\mathbf{X}}},\tilde{\varrho})^{T}\in\mathscr{E}_{1}
. Recall that
\kappa({\mathbf{X}})=|\partial_{s}{\mathbf{X}}\times\partial^{2}_{s}{\mathbf{X}}|=|A(0,1,\partial_{s}{\mathbf{X}})\partial^{2}_{s}{\mathbf{X}}|
. Therefore, the Fréchet derivative at
\tilde{{\mathbf{X}}}\in\mathscr{E}_{1}
of
\kappa
in the direction
{\mathbf{X}}\in\mathscr{E}_{1}
has the form

\kappa^{\prime}(\tilde{{\mathbf{X}}}){\mathbf{X}}=\tilde{e}^{T}\left[A(0,1,\partial_{s}\tilde{{\mathbf{X}}})\partial^{2}_{s}{\mathbf{X}}+A(0,1,\partial_{s}{\mathbf{X}})\partial^{2}_{s}\tilde{{\mathbf{X}}}\right],\quad\text{where}\quad\tilde{e}=\frac{\partial_{s}\tilde{{\mathbf{X}}}\times\partial^{2}_{s}\tilde{{\mathbf{X}}}}{|\partial_{s}\tilde{{\mathbf{X}}}\times\partial^{2}_{s}\tilde{{\mathbf{X}}}|}\in E_{0}.

Hence the principal part
\mathscr{A}_{0}
of the linearization
\mathscr{F}^{\prime}(\tilde{\Phi})
containing the second-order derivatives has the matrix form
\partial_{t}\Phi=\mathscr{A}_{0}\Phi
where
\mathscr{A}_{0}
is a skew diagonal linear operator:

\mathscr{A}_{0}\Phi=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\tilde{A}&0\\
\tilde{d}^{T}&\tilde{c}\end{array}\right)\partial_{s}^{2}\Phi,

where
\tilde{a}=a(\tilde{{\mathbf{X}}},\partial_{s}\tilde{{\mathbf{X}}},\tilde{\varrho},\partial_{s}\tilde{\varrho})>0,\ \tilde{b}=b(\tilde{{\mathbf{X}}},\partial_{s}\tilde{{\mathbf{X}}},\tilde{\varrho},\partial_{s}\tilde{\varrho}),\ \tilde{c}=c(\tilde{{\mathbf{X}}},\partial_{s}\tilde{{\mathbf{X}}},\tilde{\varrho},\partial_{s}\tilde{\varrho})>0,\tilde{d}=G^{\prime}_{\kappa}(\tilde{{\mathbf{X}}},\partial_{s}\tilde{{\mathbf{X}}},\tilde{\varrho},\partial_{s}\tilde{\varrho},\tilde{\kappa})\tilde{e}^{T}A(0,1,\partial_{s}\tilde{{\mathbf{X}}})
, and

\tilde{A}=A(\tilde{a},\tilde{b},\partial_{s}\tilde{{\mathbf{X}}})
. Clearly,
\tilde{a},\tilde{b},\tilde{c},\tilde{A}_{ij}\in h^{1+\varepsilon}(S^{1}),\tilde{d}\in E_{\frac{1}{2}}
.
The operator
\mathscr{A}_{0}
generates an analytic semigroup
\{\exp(t\,\mathscr{A}_{0}),t\geq 0\}
in
\mathscr{E}_{0}
,

\exp(t\,\mathscr{A}_{0})=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\exp(t\,\tilde{A}\partial^{2}_{s}),&0\\
D(t),&\exp(t\,\tilde{c}\partial^{2}_{s})\end{array}\right),

where
D(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\exp((t-\tau)\tilde{c}\partial^{2}_{s})(\tilde{d}^{T}\partial^{2}_{s})\exp(\tau\tilde{A}\partial^{2}_{s})d\tau
. Next, we will prove that
\mathscr{A}_{0}
belongs to the maximal regularity class
{\mathcal{M}}(\mathscr{E}_{1},\mathscr{E}_{0})
. This means that for any time interval
[0,T],T>0
, the mapping
\partial_{t}-\mathscr{A}_{0}:\Phi\mapsto(\Phi_{0},f)
from
C([0,T],\mathscr{E}_{1})\cap C^{1}([0,T],\mathscr{E}_{0})
to
\mathscr{E}_{1}\times C([0,T],\mathscr{E}_{0})
, is invertible, where
f(t)=\partial_{t}\Phi(t)-\mathscr{A}_{0}\Phi(t),\ \Phi_{0}=\Phi(0)
, i.e., the inverse operator
(\partial_{t}-\mathscr{A}_{0})^{-1}:\mathscr{E}_{1}\times C([0,T],\mathscr{E}_{0})\to C([0,T],\mathscr{E}_{1})\cap C^{1}([0,T],\mathscr{E}_{0})
exists and is a bounded linear operator. To prove this statement, assume
f=(f^{{\mathbf{X}}},f^{\varrho})\in C([0,T],\mathscr{E}_{0})=C([0,T],E_{0})\times C([0,T],h^{\varepsilon}(S^{1}))
, and
\Phi_{0}=({\mathbf{X}}_{0},\varrho_{0})\in\mathscr{E}_{1}=E_{1}\times h^{2+\varepsilon}(S^{1})
. Assume
\tilde{a},\tilde{b}\in h^{1+\varepsilon}(S^{1})
and that the function
\tilde{a}
is strictly positive,
T>0
. According to
[
9
, Proposition 3]
the operator
(\tilde{a}\pm i\tilde{b})\partial^{2}_{s}
belongs to the maximal regularity class
{\mathcal{M}}(h^{2+\varepsilon}(S^{1}),h^{\varepsilon}(S^{1}))
on the time interval
[0,T]
. Moreover, if
\tilde{{\mathbf{t}}}=\partial_{s}\tilde{{\mathbf{X}}}\in E_{\frac{1}{2}}
, then the linear operator
\tilde{A}\partial^{2}_{s}=A(\tilde{a},\tilde{b},\partial_{s}\tilde{{\mathbf{X}}})\partial^{2}_{s}
belongs to the maximal regularity class
{\mathcal{M}}(E_{1},E_{0})
in the time interval
[0,T]
. This means that the operator
\partial_{t}-\tilde{A}\partial^{2}_{s}:{\mathbf{X}}\ni C([0,T],E_{1})\cap C^{1}([0,T],E_{0})\mapsto({\mathbf{X}}_{0},f^{{\mathbf{X}}})\in E_{1}\times C([0,T],E_{0})
is invertible. Similarly, the operator
\tilde{c}\partial^{2}_{s}
belongs to the maximal regularity class
{\mathcal{M}}(h^{2+\varepsilon}(S^{1}),h^{\varepsilon}(S^{1}))
. Therefore, the inverse operator
(\partial_{t}-\mathscr{A}_{0})^{-1}
is given by
\Phi=({\mathbf{X}},\varrho)=(\partial_{t}-\mathscr{A}_{0})^{-1}(\Phi_{0},f)
is bounded. It can be expressed as
{\mathbf{X}}=(\partial_{t}-\tilde{A}\partial^{2}_{s})^{-1}({\mathbf{X}}_{0},f^{{\mathbf{X}}})
and
\varrho=(\partial_{t}-\tilde{c}\partial^{2}_{s})^{-1}(\varrho_{0},\xi)
where
\xi=f^{\varrho}+\tilde{d}^{T}\partial^{2}_{s}{\mathbf{X}}\in C([0,T],h^{\varepsilon}(S^{1}))
. As a consequence,
\mathscr{A}_{0}
belongs to the maximal regularity class
{\mathcal{M}}(\mathscr{E}_{1},\mathscr{E}_{0})
.

We decompose the linearization
\mathscr{F}^{\prime}(\tilde{\Phi})=\mathscr{A}_{0}+\mathscr{A}_{1}
where the operator
\mathscr{A}_{1}=\mathscr{F}^{\prime}(\tilde{\Phi})-\mathscr{A}_{0}
contains lower first order derivatives
\partial^{k}_{s},k=0,1
, of
\tilde{\Phi}
. Furthermore, it is a bounded linear operator
\mathscr{A}_{1}:\mathscr{E}_{\frac{1}{2}}\to\mathscr{E}_{0}
. Therefore, the operator
\mathscr{A}_{1}
considered as a mapping from
\mathscr{E}_{1}\to\mathscr{E}_{0}
has the relative zero norm with respect to
\mathscr{A}_{0}
. Therefore, the linearization
\mathscr{F}^{\prime}(\tilde{\Phi})
belongs to the maximal regularity class
{\mathcal{M}}(\mathscr{E}_{1},\mathscr{E}_{0})
because this class is closed with respect to perturbation with the relative zero norm (cf.
[

, DaPrato and Grisvard

[

]

6.
Semi-analytical examples of periodic solutions

In this section, we present two examples of the evolution of a circular curve with no torsion evolving in rotating and parallel planes. The motion of the curves is coupled by a scalar quantity
\varrho
. The method of construction of a solution in a special separated form and its reduction to solving a system of ODEs has been proposed in the recent paper by Beneš, Kolář and Ševčovič in the context of evolving 3D curves with interactions
[
9
]
.
We consider the following system of coupled equations governing the motion of curves in 3D and scalar quantity:

(16)

\begin{split}&\partial_{t}{\mathbf{X}}=\beta{\mathbf{N}}+\gamma{\mathbf{B}},\\
&\partial_{t}\varrho-\kappa\beta\varrho=\partial_{s}^{2}\varrho+q.\end{split}

The normal velocity
\beta
, the binormal velocity and
\gamma
, and the source term
q
are assumed to depend on the shape of the curve
\Gamma_{t}=\{{\mathbf{X}}(u,t),u\in I\}
and the scalar quantity
\varrho
. In this example, we shall assume a specific choice:

(17)

\beta=\kappa-\frac{2\pi}{L(\Gamma)}-\tilde{P}(L(\Gamma),\|\varrho\|_{2}),\quad q=\frac{\varrho}{\sqrt{2}\|\varrho\|_{2}},

where
\tilde{P}
is a smooth function, and
\|\varrho\|_{2}=\left(\int_{0}^{1}\varrho(u)^{2}du\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}
is the
L^{2}
-norm of
\varrho
.

If the family
\Gamma_{t},t\geq 0,
of curves evolves in parallel planes, then the area
A(\Gamma)
enclosed by the curve
\Gamma
satisfies
\frac{d}{dt}A(\Gamma)=-\int_{\Gamma}\beta ds
(see
[
9
, Prop. 2]
). Since
\int_{\Gamma}\kappa ds=2\pi
for a curve evolving in the plane, we have
\int_{\Gamma}\beta ds=0
provided that
\beta=\kappa-\frac{2\pi}{L(\Gamma)}
(see Gage
[
16
]
). In such a case, the enclosed area
A(\Gamma)
is preserved during evolution. In the next two examples, we will show that a perturbation of the normal velocity
\beta=\kappa-\frac{2\pi}{L(\Gamma)}
and consideration of the influence of the scalar quantity
\varrho
may lead to periodic motion of the curves and the scalar quantity.

6.1.
An example of coupled system of parabolic equations for evolution of a curve and scalar quantity illustrating a supercritical Hopf bifurcation

In the first example, we construct the solution
{\mathbf{X}}
in the form of circular curves with radius
r
evolving in a rotating plane. Define the vectors
\hat{{\mathbf{X}}}(u)=(\cos 2\pi u,\sin 2\pi u,0)^{T}
and
\hat{{\mathbf{X}}}^{\perp}(u)=(-\sin 2\pi u,\cos 2\pi u,0)^{T}
. Assume that a
3\times 3
orthogonal matrix
Q
and a positive scalar
r>0
are time-dependent functions. Let the family of evolving curves
\Gamma_{t}=\{{\mathbf{X}}(u,t),u\in I,t\geq 0\}
be defined as follows:

(18)

{\mathbf{X}}(u,t)=r(t)Q(t)\hat{{\mathbf{X}}}(u).

Then, for the curvature
\kappa
, arc-lenth parametrization
s
, tangent
{\mathbf{T}}
, normal
{\mathbf{N}}
and binormal
{\mathbf{B}}
vectors we have

\kappa=|\partial_{s}^{2}{\mathbf{X}}|=1/r,\quad ds=2\pi r\,du,\quad{\mathbf{T}}=Q\hat{{\mathbf{X}}}^{\perp},\quad{\mathbf{N}}=-Q\hat{{\mathbf{X}}},\quad{\mathbf{B}}=Qe_{3},\quad e_{3}=(0,0,1)^{T}.

Assume that the function
\omega(t)
satisfies
r(t)\frac{d\omega}{dt}(t)=1
. Then, it is easy to verify that if

Q(t)=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\omega(t)&0&-\sin\omega(t)\\
0&1&0\\
\sin\omega(t)&0&\cos\omega(t)\\
\end{pmatrix}\ \ \text{then}\ \ QQ^{T}=Q^{T}Q=I,\ \ r\frac{d}{dt}Q=Q\begin{pmatrix}0&0&-1\\
0&0&0\\
1&0&0\\
\end{pmatrix}.

Therefore
\partial_{t}{\mathbf{X}}=\frac{d}{dt}(rQ)\hat{{\mathbf{X}}}=\frac{dr}{dt}Q\hat{{\mathbf{X}}}+r\frac{d}{dt}Q\hat{{\mathbf{X}}}=-\frac{dr}{dt}{\mathbf{N}}+\gamma Qe_{3}=\beta{\mathbf{N}}+\gamma{\mathbf{B}}
where
\gamma=\cos 2\pi u
and
\beta=-\frac{dr}{dt}
. We search for the solution
\varrho
in separated form:

(19)

\varrho(u,t)=a(t)\cos 2\pi u,

where
a=a(t)\geq 0
is a nonnegative amplitude. As
\|\varrho(\cdot,t)\|_{2}=\left(\int_{0}^{1}a(t)^{2}(\cos 2\pi u)^{2}du\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}=\frac{a(t)}{\sqrt{2}}
we have
\gamma=q=\frac{\varrho}{\sqrt{2}\|\varrho\|_{2}}=\cos 2\pi u
. Therefore, for the normal velocity
\beta
given by (
17
) we have
\beta=1/r-2\pi/(2\pi r)-\tilde{P}(\Gamma,\varrho)=-P(r,a)
, where
P(r,a)=\tilde{P}(L(\Gamma),\|\varrho\|_{2})
.
Since
ds=2\pi rdu
we have
\partial_{s}^{2}\varrho=(2\pi r)^{2}\partial_{u}^{2}\varrho=-ar^{-2}\cos 2\pi u
. Therefore,

\partial_{t}\varrho-\kappa\beta\varrho-\partial_{s}^{2}\varrho=\left(\frac{d}{dt}a+\frac{a}{r}P(r,a)+\frac{a}{r^{2}}\right)\cos 2\pi u.

As a consequence, the pair
({\mathbf{X}},\varrho)
is a solution to the coupled evolutionary equation (
16
) if and only if the radius
r
and the amplitude
a
satisfy the planar ODE system:

(20)

\frac{d}{dt}r=P(r,a),\qquad\frac{d}{dt}a=-\frac{a}{r}P(r,a)-\frac{a}{r^{2}}+1.

System (
20
) of ODEs has a nontrivial steady state
(\tilde{r},\tilde{a})
, i.e. the time-independent solution, where

\tilde{a}=\tilde{r}^{2},\qquad P(\tilde{r},\tilde{r}^{2})=0.

The linearization
\mathcal{A}
of the right-hand side of (
20
) at the steady state
(\tilde{r},\tilde{a})
has the form of a
2\times 2
matrix
\mathcal{M}
:

\mathcal{A}=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\partial P}{\partial r}(\tilde{r},\tilde{a})&\frac{\partial P}{\partial a}(\tilde{r},\tilde{a})&\\
\\
-\frac{\tilde{a}}{\tilde{r}}\frac{\partial P}{\partial r}(\tilde{r},\tilde{a})+2\frac{\tilde{a}}{\tilde{r}^{3}}&-\frac{\tilde{a}}{\tilde{r}}\frac{\partial P}{\partial a}(\tilde{r},\tilde{a})-\frac{1}{\tilde{r}^{2}}\end{pmatrix}.

Now, as
\tilde{a}=\tilde{r}^{2}
we have

trace(\mathcal{A})=\frac{\partial P}{\partial r}(\tilde{r},\tilde{a})-\tilde{r}\frac{\partial P}{\partial a}(\tilde{r},\tilde{a})-\frac{1}{\tilde{r}^{2}},\qquad det(\mathcal{A})=-\frac{1}{\tilde{r}^{2}}\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial r}(\tilde{r},\tilde{a})+2\tilde{r}\frac{\partial P}{\partial a}(\tilde{r},\tilde{a})\right).

Assume that the source term
\tilde{P}
has the specific form

\tilde{P}(L(\Gamma),\|\varrho\|_{2})=\frac{L^{2}}{4\pi^{2}}-\lambda\sqrt{2}\|\varrho\|_{2}+1,\quad\text{that is},\quad P(a,r)=r^{2}-\lambda a+1,

where
\lambda>1
is a parameter. Then, for the steady-state solution
(\tilde{r},\tilde{a})
we have
\tilde{r}=1/\sqrt{\lambda-1},\tilde{a}=1/(\lambda-1)
.

trace(\mathcal{A})=\frac{\lambda+2}{\sqrt{\lambda-1}}-\lambda+1,\qquad det(\mathcal{A})=(\lambda-1)^{3/2}.

The nonlinear equation
trace(\mathcal{A})=0
has the unique solution for the parameter value
\lambda_{0}=4.473402
. Since
trace(\mathcal{A})<0
for
\lambda>\lambda_{0}
, and
trace(\mathcal{A})>0
for
\lambda<\lambda_{0}
. Hence, the supercritical Hopf bifurcation occurs when the parameter
\lambda
crosses the critical value
\lambda_{0}
. From a stable focus steady state
(\tilde{r},\tilde{a})
for
\lambda>\lambda_{0}
we observe a bifurcation to a stable period orbit at
\lambda_{0}
that persists to exist for
\lambda<\lambda_{0}
.

7.
Numerical discretization scheme based on the method of flowing finite volumes and the method of lines

In this section, we present a numerical discretization scheme to solve the system of equations (
2
). Our full space-time discretization scheme is based on a combination of the method of lines and the flowing finite-volume method of spatial discretization. The flowing finite-volume discretization was proposed by Mikula and Ševčovič
[
31
]
for the evolution of curves in the plane. It was further generalized and analyzed for evolving curves in 3D by Beneš, Kolář and Ševčovič in
[
8
,
9
]
.

7.1.
Method of flowing finite volumes

In this section, we describe the discretization method in more detail. We consider
M
discrete nodes
{\mathbf{X}}_{k}
approximating points
{\mathbf{X}}(u_{k})
,
k=0,1,\ldots,M
, along the curve
\Gamma=\{{\mathbf{X}}(u),u\in I\}
,
u_{k}=k/M
. Similarly, we approximate the values of the scalar quantity
\varrho_{k}=\varrho(u_{k})
. The corresponding dual nodes are defined as
{\mathbf{X}}_{k\pm\frac{1}{2}}={\mathbf{X}}(u_{k\pm\frac{1}{2}})
(see Fig.
5
). Here,
u_{k\pm\frac{1}{2}}=u_{k}\pm\frac{h}{2}
, where
h=1/M
, and
({\mathbf{X}}_{k}+{\mathbf{X}}_{k+1})/2
denote averages on segments connecting discrete nodes in the vicinity. It may differ from the position
{\mathbf{X}}_{k+\frac{1}{2}}\in\Gamma
. The
k
-th segment between
{\mathbf{X}}_{k-1}
and
{\mathbf{X}}_{k}
represents the finite volume, while the segment between
{\mathbf{X}}_{k-1/2}
and
{\mathbf{X}}_{k+1/2}
represents the dual finite volume. Integration of equations (
2
) for
({\mathbf{X}},\varrho)
in the dual volume between the dual nodes
{\mathbf{X}}_{k-\frac{1}{2}}
and
{\mathbf{X}}_{k+\frac{1}{2}}
yields the following result:

(21)

\begin{split}&\int_{u_{k-\frac{1}{2}}}^{u_{k+\frac{1}{2}}}\partial_{t}{\mathbf{X}}|\partial_{u}{\mathbf{X}}|du=\int_{u_{k-\frac{1}{2}}}^{u_{k+\frac{1}{2}}}a\frac{\partial}{\partial u}\left(\frac{\partial_{u}{\mathbf{X}}}{|\partial_{u}{\mathbf{X}}|}\right)+b(\partial_{s}{\mathbf{X}}\times\partial_{s}^{2}{\mathbf{X}})|\partial_{u}{\mathbf{X}}|+{\mathbf{F}}|\partial_{u}{\mathbf{X}}|\ du,\\
&\int_{u_{k-\frac{1}{2}}}^{u_{k+\frac{1}{2}}}\partial_{t}\varrho|\partial_{u}{\mathbf{X}}|du=\int_{u_{k-\frac{1}{2}}}^{u_{k+\frac{1}{2}}}c\frac{\partial}{\partial u}\left(\frac{\partial_{u}\varrho}{|\partial_{u}{\mathbf{X}}|}\right)+G|\partial_{u}{\mathbf{X}}|\ du.\end{split}

We denote
d_{k}=|{\mathbf{X}}_{k}-{\mathbf{X}}_{k-1}|
,
d_{M+1}=d_{1}
, and
d_{k+\frac{1}{2}}=(d_{k+1}+d_{k})/2
for
k=0,1,\ldots,M
, where
{\mathbf{X}}_{0}={\mathbf{X}}_{M}
and
{\mathbf{X}}_{1}={\mathbf{X}}_{M+1}
for a closed curve
\Gamma
. We approximate the integral terms in (
21
) using the flowing finite-volume method. We assume that the quantities
\partial_{t}{\mathbf{X}},\partial_{u}{\mathbf{X}},{\mathbf{F}},\partial_{t}\varrho,\partial_{u}\varrho
and
G
are constant in the finite volume between the nodes
{\mathbf{X}}_{k+\frac{1}{2}}
and
{\mathbf{X}}_{k-\frac{1}{2}}
, and taking the values
\partial_{t}{\mathbf{X}}_{k},\partial_{u}{\mathbf{X}}_{k},{\mathbf{F}}_{k},\partial_{t}\varrho_{k},\partial_{u}\varrho_{k}
, and
G_{k}
an respectively. Then the approximation of the terms of the first equation in (
21
) reads as follows:

(22)

\begin{split}&\int_{u_{k-\frac{1}{2}}}^{u_{k+\frac{1}{2}}}\partial_{t}{\mathbf{X}}|\partial_{u}{\mathbf{X}}|du\approx\frac{d{\mathbf{X}}_{k}}{dt}d_{k+\frac{1}{2}},\\
&\int_{u_{k-\frac{1}{2}}}^{u_{k+\frac{1}{2}}}a\partial_{u}\left(\frac{\partial_{u}{\mathbf{X}}}{|\partial_{u}{\mathbf{X}}|}\right)du\approx a_{k}\left(\frac{{\mathbf{X}}_{k+1}-{\mathbf{X}}_{k}}{d_{k+1}}-\frac{{\mathbf{X}}_{k}-{\mathbf{X}}_{k-1}}{d_{k}}\right),\\
&\int_{u_{k-\frac{1}{2}}}^{u_{k+\frac{1}{2}}}b(\partial_{s}{\mathbf{X}}\times\partial_{s}^{2}{\mathbf{X}})|\partial_{u}{\mathbf{X}}|du\approx b_{k}d_{k+\frac{1}{2}}\kappa_{k}{\mathbf{B}}_{k},\ \ \int_{u_{k-\frac{1}{2}}}^{u_{k+\frac{1}{2}}}{\mathbf{F}}|\partial_{u}{\mathbf{X}}|du\approx{\mathbf{F}}_{k}d_{k+\frac{1}{2}}.\end{split}

Discretization of a segment of a 3D curve by the method of flowing finite volumes.

Analogously, we can approximate the terms entered into the second equation in (
21
) for the scalar quantity
\varrho
. Approximations of the nonnegative curvature
\kappa=|\partial^{2}_{s}{\mathbf{X}}|
, tangent vector
{\mathbf{T}}=\partial_{s}{\mathbf{X}}
, normal vector
{\mathbf{N}}=\partial^{2}_{s}{\mathbf{X}}/|\partial^{2}_{s}{\mathbf{X}}|
and binormal vector
{\mathbf{B}}={\mathbf{T}}\times{\mathbf{N}}
read as follows:

(23)

\begin{split}&\kappa_{k}\approx\left|\frac{2}{d_{k}+d_{k+1}}\left(\frac{{\mathbf{X}}_{k+1}-{\mathbf{X}}_{k}}{d_{k+1}}-\frac{{\mathbf{X}}_{k}-{\mathbf{X}}_{k-1}}{d_{k}}\right)\right|,\ {\mathbf{T}}_{k}\approx\frac{{\mathbf{X}}_{k+1}-{\mathbf{X}}_{k-1}}{d_{k+1}+d_{k}},\\
&{\mathbf{N}}_{k}\approx\left(\frac{{\mathbf{X}}_{k+1}-{\mathbf{X}}_{k}}{d_{k+1}}-\frac{{\mathbf{X}}_{k}-{\mathbf{X}}_{k-1}}{d_{k}}\right)\big/\left|\frac{{\mathbf{X}}_{k+1}-{\mathbf{X}}_{k}}{d_{k+1}}-\frac{{\mathbf{X}}_{k}-{\mathbf{X}}_{k-1}}{d_{k}}\right|.\end{split}

In approximation
{\mathbf{F}}_{k}
of the vector-valued function
{\mathbf{F}}
, we assume that the curve
\Gamma
entering the definition of
{\mathbf{F}}
is approximated by a polygonal curve with vertices
({\mathbf{X}}_{0},{\mathbf{X}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathbf{X}}_{M})
. The semi-discrete scheme for solving (
2
) can be written as follows:

(24)

\begin{split}\frac{d{\mathbf{X}}_{k}}{dt}&=a_{k}\frac{1}{d_{k+\frac{1}{2}}}\left(\frac{{\mathbf{X}}_{k+1}-{\mathbf{X}}_{k}}{d_{k+1}}-\frac{{\mathbf{X}}_{k}-{\mathbf{X}}_{k-1}}{d_{k}}\right)+b_{k}\kappa_{k}{\mathbf{B}}_{k}+{\mathbf{F}}_{k},\\
\frac{d\varrho_{k}}{dt}&=a_{k}\frac{1}{d_{k+\frac{1}{2}}}\left(\frac{\varrho_{k+1}-\varrho_{k}}{d_{k+1}}-\frac{\varrho_{k}-\varrho_{k-1}}{d_{k}}\right)+G_{k},\\
{\mathbf{X}}_{k}(0)&={\mathbf{X}}_{ini}(u_{k}),\quad\varrho_{k}(0)=\varrho_{ini}(u_{k}),\quad\text{for}\ k=1,\ldots,M.\end{split}

Resulting system (
24
) of ODEs is solved numerically by means of the 4th-order explicit Runge-Kutta-Merson scheme with automatic time stepping control and the tolerance parameter
10^{-3}
(see
[
37
]
). We chose the initial time step as
4h^{2}
, where
h=1/M
is the spatial mesh size.

9.
Conclusions

In this paper, we investigated the motion of closed smooth curves that evolve in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
coupled with the evolution of a scalar quantity evaluated over the evolving curve. We proved the local existence and uniqueness oflassical Hölder smooth solutions. The proof is based on the analysis of the position vector equation and the parabolic equation for the evolving scalar quantity. We also proposed a numerical discretization scheme for numerical approximation of the evolving family of curves and scalar quantity. We presented several numerical examples of evolving curves and the scalar quantity.
