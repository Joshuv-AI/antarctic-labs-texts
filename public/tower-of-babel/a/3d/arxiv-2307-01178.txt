Learning Mixtures of Gaussians Using the DDPM Objective
Kulin Shah, Sitan Chen, Adam Klivans

Abstract
Recent works have shown that diffusion models can learn essentially any distribution provided one can perform score estimation. Yet it remains poorly understood under what settings score estimation is possible, let alone when practical gradient-based algorithms for this task can provably succeed. In this work, we give the first provably efficient results along these lines for one of the most fundamental distribution families, Gaussian mixture models. We prove that gradient descent on the denoising diffusion probabilistic model (DDPM) objective can efficiently recover the ground truth parameters of the mixture model in the following two settings: 1. We show gradient descent with random initialization learns mixtures of two spherical Gaussians in d d dimensions with 1 / poly ​ ( d ) 1/\text{poly}(d) -separated centers. 2. We show gradient descent with a warm start learns mixtures of K K spherical Gaussians with Ω ⁡ ( log ⁡ ( min ⁡ ( K , d ) ) ) \Omega(\sqrt{\log(\min(K,d))}) -separated centers. A key ingredient in our proofs is a new connection between score-based methods and two other approaches to distribution learning, the EM algorithm and spectral methods.

Theory for diffusion models.

Learning mixtures of Gaussians.

The literature on provable algorithms for learning Gaussian mixture models is vast, dating back to the pioneering work of Pearson
[
Pea94

[

[

A separate line of work has investigated the “textbook” algorithm for learning Gaussian mixtures, namely the EM algorithm

Lastly, we note that there are many works giving parameter recovery algorithms mixtures of Gaussians with general mixing weights and covariances, all of which are based on method of moments

1.2
Technical overview

We begin by describing in greater detail the algorithm we analyze in this work. For the sake of intuition, in this overview we will focus on the case of mixtures of two Gaussians
(K=2)
where the centers are well-separated and symmetric about the origin, that is, the data distribution is given by

q=\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{N}(\mu^{*},\mathrm{Id})+\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{N}(-\mu^{*},\mathrm{Id})\,.

(3)

At the end of the overview, we briefly discuss the key challenges for handling smaller separation and general
K
.

Loss function, architecture of the score function and student network.

The algorithmic task at the heart of score estimation is that of denoising. Formally, for some noise level
t>0
, we are given a noisy sample

X_{t}=\exp(-t)X_{0}+\sqrt{1-\exp(-2t)}Z_{t}\,,

(4)

where
X_{0}
is a clean sample drawn from the data distribution
q
, and
Z_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathrm{Id})
. Conditioning on
X_{t}
induces some posterior distribution over the noise
Z_{t}
, and our goal is to form an estimate
s
for the mean of this posterior which achieves small error on average over the randomness of
X_{0}
and
Z_{t}
. That is, we would like to minimize the
DDPM objective
, which up to rescaling is
given by
1
1

1

The real DDPM objective is slightly different, see (
13
). The latter is what we actually consider in this paper, but this distinction is unimportant for the intuition in this overview.

L_{t}(s)=\mathbb{E}_{X_{0},Z_{t}}\|s(X_{t})-Z_{t}\|^{2}\,.

(5)

As discussed in the introduction, the algorithm of choice for minimizing this objective in practice is gradient descent on some student network. To motivate our choice of architecture, note that when the data distribution is given by (
3
), the true minimizer of
L_{t}
is, up to scaling,

\tanh(\langle\mu^{*}_{t},x\rangle)\mu^{*}_{t}-x\,,\;\;\;\text{where}\ \mu^{*}_{t}\triangleq\mu^{*}\exp(-t)\,.

(6)

See Appendix
A
for the derivation. Notably, Eq. (
6
) is exactly a two-layer neural network with
\tanh
activation. As a result, we use the same architecture for our student network when running gradient descent.
That is, given weights
\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, our student network is given by
s_{\mu}(x)\triangleq\tanh(\mu^{\top}x)\mu-x
. The exact gradient updates on
\mu
are given in Lemma
C.2
.

As we discuss next, depending on whether the noise level
t
is large or small, this update closely approximates the update in one of two well-studied algorithms for learning mixtures of Gaussians: power method and EM respectively.

Learning mixtures of two Gaussians.

We first provide a brief overview of the analysis and then go into the details of the analysis. We start with mixtures of two Gaussians of the form (
3
) where
\|\mu^{*}\|
is
\Omega(1)
. In this case, we analyze the following two-stage algorithm. We first use gradient descent on the DDPM objective with large
t
starting from random initialization. We show that gradient descent in this “high noise” regime resembles a type of power iteration and gives
\mu
that has a nontrivial correlation with
\mu^{*}_{t}
. Starting from this
\mu
, we then run gradient descent with small
t
. We show that the gradient descent in this “small noise” regime corresponds to the EM algorithm and converges exponentially quickly to the ground truth.

Large noise level: connection to power iteration.

When
t
is large, we show that gradient descent on the DDPM objective is closely approximated by power iteration. More precisely, in this regime, the negative gradient of
L_{t}(s_{\mu})
is well-approximated by

-\nabla_{\mu}L_{t}(s_{\mu})\approx(2\mu_{t}^{*}\mu_{t}^{*\top}-r\mathrm{Id})\,\mu\,,

(7)

where
r
is a scalar that depends on
\mu
(See Lemma
8
). So the result of a single gradient update with step size
\eta
starting from
\mu
is given by

\mu^{\prime}\triangleq\mu-\eta\nabla_{\mu}L_{t}(s_{\mu})\approx((1-\eta r)\,\mathrm{Id}+2\eta\mu_{t}^{*}\mu_{t}^{*\top})\mu\,.

(8)

This shows us that each gradient step can be approximated by one step of power iteration (without normalization) on the matrix
(1-\eta r)\,\mathrm{Id}+2\eta\mu_{t}^{*}\mu_{t}^{*\top}
. It is know that running enough iterations of the latter from a random initialization will converge in angular distance to the top eigenvector, which in this case is given by
\mu^{*}_{t}
. This suggests that if we can keep the approximation error in (
8
) under control, then gradient descent on
\mu
will also allow us to converge to a neighborhood of the ground truth. We implement this strategy in Lemma
10
. Next, we argue that once we are in a neighborhood of the ground truth, we can run GD on the DDPM objective at
low noise level
to refine our estimate.

Low noise level: connection to the EM algorithm.

When
t
is small, we show that gradient descent on the DDPM objective is closely approximated by EM. Here, our analysis uses the fact that
\mu^{*}
is sufficiently large and requires that we initialize
\mu
to have sufficiently large correlation with the true direction
\mu^{*}_{t}
. We can achieve the latter using the large-
t
analysis in the previous section.

Provided we have this, when
t
is small it turns out that the negative gradient is well-approximated by

\displaystyle-\nabla_{\mu}L_{t}(s_{\mu})\approx\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{t}^{*},\mathrm{Id})}[\tanh(\langle\mu,X\rangle)X]-\mu\,.

(9)

Note that the expectation is precisely the “M”-step in the EM algorithm for learning mixtures of two Gaussians (see e.g. Eq. (2.2) of
[
DTZ17
]
).
We conclude that a single gradient update with step size
\eta
starting from
\mu
is given by mixing the old weights
\mu
with the result of the “M”-step in EM:

\displaystyle\mu^{\prime}\triangleq\mu-\eta\nabla_{\mu}L_{t}(s_{\mu})\approx(1-\eta)\mu+\eta\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{t}^{*},\mathrm{Id})}[\tanh(\langle\mu,X\rangle)X]}_{\text{``M'' step in the EM algorithm}}\,.

[
XHM16
]
and
[
DTZ17
]
showed that EM converges exponentially quickly to the ground truth
\mu^{*}_{t}
from a warm start, and we leverage ingredients from their analysis to prove the same guarantee for gradient descent on the DDPM objective at small noise level
t
(see Lemma
12
).

Extending to small separation.

Next, suppose we instead only assume that
\|\mu^{*}\|
is
\Omega({1}/{\mathrm{poly}(d)})
, i.e. the two components in the mixture may have small separation. The above analysis breaks down for the following reason: while it is always possible to show that gradient descent at large noise level converges in
angular distance
to the ground truth, if
\|\mu^{*}\|
is small, then we cannot translate this to convergence in Euclidean distance.

We circumvent this as follows. Extending the connection between gradient descent at large
t
and power iteration, we show that a similar analysis where we instead run
projected
gradient descent over the ball of radius
\|\mu^{*}\|
yields a solution arbitrarily close to the ground truth, even without the EM step.
2
2

2

Note that although
\mu^{*}
is unknown, we can estimate its norm from samples.
The projection step can be thought of as mimicking the normalization step in power iteration.

It might appear to the reader that this projected gradient-based approach is strictly superior to the two-stage algorithm described at the outset. However, in addition to obviating the need for a projection step when separation is large, our analysis for the two-stage algorithm has the advantage of giving much more favorable statistical rates. Indeed, we can show that the sample complexity of the two-stage algorithm has optimal dependence on the target error (
1/\varepsilon^{2}
), whereas we can only show a suboptimal dependence (
1/\varepsilon^{8}
) for the single-stage algorithm.

Extending to general
K
.

The connection between gradient descent on the DDPM objective at small
t
and the EM algorithm is sufficiently robust that for general
K
, our analysis for
K=2
can generalize once we replace the ingredients from
[
XHM16
]
and
[
DTZ17
]
with the analogous ingredients in existing analyses for EM with
K
Gaussians. For the latter, it is known that if the centers of the Gaussians have separation
\Omega(\sqrt{\log\min(K,d)})
, then EM will converge from a warm start
[
KC20
,
SN21
]
. By carefully tracking the error in approximating the negative gradient with the “M”-step in EM, we are able to show that gradient descent on the DDPM objective at small
t
achieves the same guarantee.

1.3
Preliminaries

Diffusion models.

Throughout the paper, we use either
q
or
q_{0}
to denote the data distribution and
X
or
X_{0}
to denote the corresponding random variable on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The two main components in diffusion models are the
forward process
and the
reverse process
. The forward process transforms samples from the data distribution into noise, for instance via the
Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (OU) process
:

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}X_{t}=-X_{t}\,\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2}\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}\;\;\;\text{with}\;\;\;X_{0}\sim q_{0}\,,

where
(W_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a standard Brownian motion in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
We use
q_{t}
to denote the law of the OU process at time
t
. Note that for
X_{t}\sim q_{t}
,

X_{t}=\exp(-t)X_{0}+\sqrt{1-\exp(-2t)}Z_{t}\;\;\;\text{with}\;\;X_{0}\sim q_{0},\;\;Z_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathrm{Id})\,.

(10)

The reverse process then transforms noise into samples, thus performing generative modeling. Ideally, this could be achieved by running the following stochastic differential equation for some choice of terminal time
T
:

\mathrm{d}X^{\leftarrow}_{t}=\{X^{\leftarrow}_{t}+2\nabla_{x}\ln q_{T-t}(X^{\leftarrow}_{t})\}\,\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2}\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}\;\;\;\text{with}\;\;\;X^{\leftarrow}_{0}\sim q_{T}\,,

(11)

where now
W_{t}
is the reversed Brownian motion. In this reverse process, the iterate
X^{\leftarrow}_{t}
is distributed acccording to
q_{T-t}
for every
t\in[0,T]
, so that the final iterate
X^{\leftarrow}_{T}
is distributed according to the data distribution
q_{0}
. The function
\nabla_{x}\ln q_{t}
is called the
score function
, and because it depends on
q
which is unknown, in practice one estimates it by minimizing the
score matching loss

\displaystyle\min_{s_{t}}\;\;\mathbb{E}_{X_{t}\sim q_{t}}[\|s_{t}(X_{t})-\nabla_{x}\ln q_{t}(X_{t})\|^{2}]\,.

(12)

A standard calculation (see e.g. Appendix A of
[
CCL+23b
]
) shows that this is equivalent to minimizing the
DDPM objective
in which one wants to predict the noise
Z_{t}
from the noisy observation
X_{t}
, i.e.

\displaystyle\min_{s_{t}}\;\;L_{t}(s_{t})=\mathbb{E}_{X_{0},Z_{t}}\Big[\Big\|s_{t}(X_{t})+\frac{Z_{t}}{\sqrt{1-\exp(-2t)}}\Big\|^{2}\Big]\,.

(13)

While we have provided background on diffusion models for context, in this work we focus specifically on the optimization problem (
13
).

Expectation-Maximization (EM) algorithm.

The EM algorithm is composed of two steps: the E-step and the M-step. For mixtures of Gaussians, the E-step computes the expected log-likelihood based on the current mean parameters and the M-step maximizes this expectation to find a new estimate of the parameters.

Fact 5

(See e.g.,
[
DTZ17
,
YYS17
,
KC20
]
for more details)
.

When
X
is the mixture of
K

\{\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\ldots,\mu_{K}\}
are current estimates of the means, the population EM update for all

\displaystyle\mu_{i}^{\prime}=\frac{\mathbb{E}_{X}[w_{i}(X)X]}{\mathbb{E}_{X}[w_{i}(X)]},\;\;\;\text{where}\;\;w_{i}(X)=\frac{\exp(-\|X-\mu_{i}\|^{2}/2)}{\sum_{j=1}^{K}\exp(-\|X-\mu_{j}\|^{2}/2)}.

\displaystyle\mu^{\prime}=\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu^{*},\mathrm{Id})}[\tanh(\mu^{\top}X)X].

An analogous version of the EM algorithm, called the gradient EM algorithm, takes a gradient step in the direction of the M-step instead of optimizing the objective in the M-step fully.

Fact 6

(See e.g.,

.

For all

2
Warmup: mixtures of two Gaussians with constant separation

In this section, we formally state our result for learning mixtures of two Gaussians with constant separation. This case highlights the main proof techniques, namely viewing gradient descent on the DDPM objective as power iteration and as the EM algorithm.

2.1
Result and algorithm

Theorem 7
.

There is an absolute constant
c>0
such that the following holds. Suppose a mixture of two Gaussians with the mean parameter
\mu^{*}
satisfies
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mu^{*}\right\rVert}>c
. Then, for any
\varepsilon>0
, there is a procedure that calls Algorithm
1
at two different noise scales
t
and outputs
\tilde{\mu}
such that
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\tilde{\mu}-\mu^{*}\right\rVert}\leq\varepsilon
with high probability. Moreover, the algorithm has time and sample complexity
\mathrm{poly}(d)/\varepsilon^{2}
(see Theorem
C.1
for more precise quantitative bounds).

Algorithm.

The algorithm has two stages. In the first stage we run gradient descent on the DDPM objective described in Algorithm
1
from a random Gaussian initialization and noise scale
t_{1}
for a fixed number of iterations
H
where
t_{1}=O(\log d)
(“high noise”) and
H=\mathrm{poly}(d,1/\varepsilon)
. In the second stage, the procedure uses the output of the first step as initialization and runs Algorithm
1
at a “low noise” scale of
t_{2}=O(1)
.

2.2
Proof outline of Theorem
7

We provide a proof sketch of correctness of the above algorithm and summarize the main technical lemmas here. All proofs of the following lemmas can be found in Appendix
C
.

Part I: Analysis of high noise regime and connection to power iteration.

We show that in the large noise regime, the negative gradient
-\nabla L_{t}(s_{t})
is well-approximated by
2\mu_{t}^{*}\mu_{t}^{*\top}\mu_{t}-3\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mu_{t}\right\rVert}^{2}\mu_{t}
. Recall that this result is the key to showing the resemblance between gradient descent and power iteration. Concretely, we show the following lemma:

Lemma 8

(See Lemma
C.3
for more details)
.

For
t=O(\log d)
, the gradient descent update on the DDPM objective
L_{t}(s_{t})
can be approximated with
2\mu_{t}^{*}\mu_{t}^{*\top}\mu_{t}-3\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mu_{t}\right\rVert}^{2}\mu_{t}
:

\displaystyle\Big\|\left(-\nabla L_{t}(s_{t})\right)-\left(2\mu_{t}^{*}\mu_{t}^{*\top}\mu_{t}-3\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mu_{t}\right\rVert}^{2}\mu_{t}\right)\Big\|\leq\mathrm{poly}(1/d).

From Lemma
8
, it immediately follows that
\mu^{\prime}t
, the result of taking a single gradient step starting from
\mu_{t}
, is well-approximated by the result of taking a single step of power iteration for a matrix whose leading eigenvector is
\mu^{*}_{t}
:

\displaystyle\mu^{\prime}_{t}=\mu_{t}-\eta\nabla L_{t}(s_{\mu})\approx(\mathrm{Id}(1-3\eta\|\mu_{t}\|^{2})+2\mu_{t}^{*}\mu_{t}^{*\top})\mu_{t}\,.

The second key element is to show that as a consequence of the above power iteration update, the gradient descent converges in
angular distance
to the leading eigenvector. Concretely, we show the following lemma:

Lemma 9

(Informal, see Lemma
C.5
for more details)
.

Suppose
\mu_{t}^{\prime}
is the iterate after one step of gradient descent on the DDPM objective from
\mu_{t}
. Denote the angle between
\mu_{t}
and
\mu_{t}^{*}
to be
\theta
and between
\mu^{\prime}_{t}
and
\mu_{t}^{*}
to be
\theta^{\prime}
. In this case, we show that

\displaystyle\tan\theta^{\prime}=\max\left(\kappa_{1}\tan\theta,\kappa_{2}\right),

where
\kappa_{1}<1
and
\kappa_{2}\leq 1/\mathrm{poly}(d)
.

Note
\tan\theta^{\prime}<\tan\theta
implies that
\theta^{\prime}<\theta
or equivalently
\langle\hat{\mu}_{t}^{\prime},\hat{\mu}_{t}^{*}\rangle>\langle\hat{\mu}_{t},\hat{\mu}_{t}^{*}\rangle
.
Thus, the above lemma shows that by taking a gradient step in the DDPM objective, the angle between
\mu_{t}
and
\mu_{t}^{*}
decreases. By iterating this, we obtain the following lemma:

Lemma 10

(Informal, see Lemma
C.6
for more details)
.

Running gradient descent from a random initialization on the DDPM objective
L_{t}(s_{\mu})
for
t=O(\log d)
gives
\mu_{t}
for which
\langle\hat{\mu}_{t},\hat{\mu}_{t}^{*}\rangle
is
\Omega(1)
.

Note that we cannot keep running gradient descent at this high noise scale and hope to achieve
\mu
such that
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mu-\mu^{*}\right\rVert}
is
O(\varepsilon)
. This is because Lemma
9
can only guarantee that the angle between
\mu_{t}
and
\mu_{t}^{*}
is
O(\varepsilon)
, but this does not imply
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mu-\mu^{*}\right\rVert}
is
O(\varepsilon)
. Instead, as described in Part II, we will proceed with a smaller noise scale.

Part II: Analysis of low noise regime and connection to EM.

In the low noise regime, we run Algorithm
1
using the output from Part I as our initialization. Our analysis here shows that whenever the initialization
\mu_{t}
satisfies the condition of
\langle\hat{\mu}_{t},\hat{\mu}_{t}^{*}\rangle
being
\Omega(1)
,
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mu_{t}-\mu_{t}^{*}\right\rVert}
contracts after every gradient step. To start with, we show that the result of a
population
gradient step on the DDPM objective
L_{t}(s_{\mu})
results in the following:

\displaystyle\mu^{\prime}_{t}

\displaystyle=(1-\eta)\mu_{t}+\eta\,\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{t}^{*},\mathrm{Id})}[\tanh(\mu_{t}^{\top}x)x]+\eta G(\mu_{t},\mu_{t}^{*}),

where
\mu^{\prime}_{t}
is the parameter after a gradient step,
\eta
is the learning rate, and function
G
is given by

\displaystyle G(\mu,\mu^{*})=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu^{*},\mathrm{Id})}[-\frac{1}{2}\tanh^{\prime\prime}(\mu^{\top}x)\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mu\right\rVert}^{2}x+\tanh^{\prime}(\mu^{\top}x)\mu^{\top}xx-\tanh^{\prime}(\mu^{\top}x)\mu].

Note we use the population gradient here only for simplicity; in the Appendix we show that empirical estimates of the gradient suffice.
After some calculation, we can show that

\displaystyle\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mu^{\prime}_{t}-\mu^{*}_{t}\right\rVert}\leq(1-\eta)\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mu_{t}-\mu^{*}_{t}\right\rVert}+\eta\|\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{t}^{*},\mathrm{Id})}[\tanh(\mu_{t}^{\top}x)x]-\mu_{t}^{*}\|+\eta\mathinner{\!\left\lVert G(\mu_{t},\mu^{*}_{t})\right\rVert}\,.

(21)

Using Fact
5
, we know that
\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{t}^{*},\mathrm{Id})}[\tanh(\mu_{t}^{\top}x)x]
is precisely the result of one step of EM starting from
\mu_{t}
, and it is known
[
DTZ17
]
that the EM update contracts the distance between
\mu_{t}
and
\mu_{t}^{*}
as follows:

\displaystyle\|\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{t}^{*},\mathrm{Id})}[\tanh(\mu_{t}^{\top}x)x]-\mu_{t}^{*}\|\leq\lambda_{1}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mu_{t}-\mu_{t}^{*}\right\rVert}\;\;\;\text{for some }\;\lambda_{1}<1

(22)

It remains to control the second term in Eq. (
21
), for which we prove the following:

Lemma 11

(Informal, see Lemma
C.9
for more details)
.

When
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mu^{*}\right\rVert}=\Omega(1)
and the noise scale
t=O(1)
, then for every
\mu
with
\langle\hat{\mu},\hat{\mu}^{*}\rangle
being
\Omega(1)
, the following inequality holds:

\displaystyle\|G(\mu_{t},\mu_{t}*)\|\leq\lambda_{2}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mu_{t}-\mu_{t}^{*}\right\rVert}\;\;\;\text{for some}\;\;\lambda_{2}<1\,.

Combining Eq. (
22
) and Lemma
11
with Eq. (
21
), we have

\displaystyle\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mu^{\prime}_{t}-\mu^{*}_{t}\right\rVert}\leq(1-\eta(1-\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}))\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mu_{t}-\mu^{*}_{t}\right\rVert}.

(23)

We can set parameters to ensure that
\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}<1
and therefore that
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mu_{t}-\mu^{*}_{t}\right\rVert}
contracts with each gradient step. Applying Lemma
11
and Eq. (
23
), we obtain the following lemma summarizing the behavior of gradient descent on the DDPM objective in the low noise regime.

Lemma 12

(Informal)
.

For any
\varepsilon>0
and for the noise scale
t=O(1)
, starting from an initialization
\mu_{t}
for which
\langle\hat{\mu}_{t},\hat{\mu}_{t}^{*}\rangle=\Omega(1)
, running gradient descent on the DDPM objective
L_{t}(s_{\mu})
will give us mean parameter
\tilde{\mu}
such that
\|\tilde{\mu}-\mu^{*}\|\leq O(\varepsilon)
.

Combining Lemma
10
and Lemma
12
, we obtain our first main result, Theorem
7
, for learning mixtures of two Gaussians with constant separation. For the full technical details, see Appendix
C
.

3
Extensions: small separation and more Components

3.1
Mixtures of two Gaussians with small separation

In this section, we briefly sketch how the ideas from Section
2
can be extended to give our second main result, namely on learning mixtures of two Gaussians even with
small separation
. We defer the full technical details to Appendix
D
.

Theorem 13
.

Suppose a mixture of two Gaussians has mean parameter
\mu^{*}
that satisfies
\|\mu^{*}\|=\Omega(\frac{1}{\mathrm{poly}(d)})
. Then, for any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a modification of Algorithm
1
that provides an estimate
\mu
such that
\|\mu-\mu^{*}\|\leq O(\varepsilon)
with high probability. Moreover, the algorithm has time and sample complexity
\mathrm{poly}(d)/\varepsilon^{8}
(see Theorem
D.1
for more precise quantitative bounds).

Algorithm modification.

The algorithm that we analyze runs
projected
gradient descent on the DDPM objective but only in the high noise scale regime where
t=O(\log d)
. At each step, we project the iterate
\mu
to the ball of radius
R
, where
R
is an empirical estimate for
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mu^{*}\right\rVert}
obtained by drawing samples
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
from the data distribution and forming
R\triangleq(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert x_{i}\right\rVert}^{2}-d)^{1/2}
.

Proof sketch.

Lemma
9
and Lemma
10
apply even when the components of the mixture have small separation, and they show that running gradient descent on the DDPM objective results in
\mu_{t}
and
\mu_{t}^{*}
being
O(1)
close in angular distance. Although our analysis can be extended to show that gradient descent can achieve
O(\varepsilon)
angular distance, this does not guarantee that
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mu_{t}-\mu_{t}^{*}\right\rVert}
is
O(\varepsilon)
. If in addition to being
O(\varepsilon)
close in angular distance, we also have that
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mu_{t}\right\rVert}\approx\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mu_{t}^{*}\right\rVert}
, then it is easy to see that
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mu_{t}-\mu_{t}^{*}\right\rVert}
is indeed
O(\varepsilon)
.

Observe that if
R
is approximately equal to
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mu_{t}^{*}\right\rVert}
, then the projection step in our algorithm ensures that our final estimate
\mu_{t}
satisfies this additional condition of
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mu_{t}\right\rVert}\approx\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mu^{*}_{t}\right\rVert}
. It is not hard to show that
R^{2}
is an unbiased estimate of
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mu^{*}_{t}\right\rVert}^{2}
, so standard concentration shows that taking
n=\mathrm{poly}(d,\frac{1}{\varepsilon})
suffices to ensure that
R
is sufficiently close to
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mu_{t}^{*}\right\rVert}
.

Mixtures of
K
Gaussians, from a warm start

In this section, we state our third main result, namely for learning mixtures of
K

Assumption 14

(Separation)

Theorem 16

Suppose a mixture of

Gaussians satisfies Assumption

We provide a brief overview of the proof here. The full proof can be found in Appendix

Proof sketch.

For learning mixtures of two Gaussians, we have already established the connection between gradient descent on the DDPM objective and the EM algorithm. For mixtures of

, we show an equivalence between

\displaystyle-\nabla_{\mu_{i,t}}L_{t}(s_{\theta_{t}})\approx\mathbb{E}_{X_{t}}[w_{i,t}(X_{t})(X_{t}-\mu_{i,t})].

\displaystyle\mu_{1,t}^{\prime}=\mu_{1,t}-\eta\nabla_{\mu_{1,t}}L_{t}(s_{\theta_{t}})\approx\mu_{1,t}+\eta\,\mathbb{E}_{X_{t}}[w_{1,t}(X_{t})(X_{t}-\mu_{1,t})].

(24)

Lemma B.2

Proof.

where the last inequality uses the fact that convex combinations of two values is less than the maximum of two values.
∎

Finally, we obtain the following bound on the correlation between the ground truth and the final iterate of gradient descent:

Lemma C.6
.

For any

\mu^{(h)}_{t}

\displaystyle\kappa_{1}

(C.9)

As

C.2
Low noise regime - connection to EM algorithm

In the previous section we showed how to obtain a warm start by running gradient descent on the DDPM objective at high noise. We now focus on proving the contraction of
\|\mu_{t}-\mu_{t}^{*}\|
starting from this warm start, by running gradient descent at
low
noise. We first prove the contraction for population gradient descent and then, we argue that the empirical gradient descent concentrates well around the population gradient descent.

As before, we denote
\mu_{t}
as the current iterate and
\mu^{\prime}_{t}
as the next iterate obtained by performing (population) gradient descent on the DDPM objective with step size
\eta
. We upper bound
\|\mu_{t}^{\prime}-\mu_{t}^{*}\|
as follows:

\displaystyle\|\mu_{t}^{\prime}-\mu_{t}^{*}\|

\displaystyle=\|\mu_{t}-\eta\nabla_{\mu_{t}}L_{t}(s_{\mu_{t}})-\mu_{t}^{*}\|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Big\|(1-\eta)(\mu_{t}-\mu_{t}^{*})+\eta\,\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{t}^{*},1)}\big[\big(\tanh(\mu_{t}^{\top}x)-\frac{1}{2}\tanh^{\prime\prime}(\mu_{t}^{\top}x)\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mu_{t}\right\rVert}^{2}

\displaystyle+\tanh^{\prime}(\mu_{t}^{\top}x)\mu_{t}^{\top}x\big)x\big]-\eta\,\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{t}^{*},1)}[\tanh^{\prime}(\mu_{t}^{\top}x)\mu_{t}]-\eta\mu_{t}^{*}\Big\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(1-\eta)\,\|\mu_{t}-\mu_{t}^{*}\|+\eta\big\|\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{t}^{*},1)}[\tanh(\mu_{t}^{\top}x)x]-\mu_{t}^{*}\big\|+\eta\,\|G(\mu_{t},\mu_{t}^{*})\|\,,

where

\displaystyle G(\mu_{t},\mu_{t}^{*})\triangleq\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{t}^{*},\mathrm{Id})}\Big[-\frac{1}{2}\tanh^{\prime\prime}(\mu_{t}^{\top}x)\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mu_{t}\right\rVert}^{2}x+(\tanh^{\prime}(\mu_{t}^{\top}x)\mu_{t}^{\top}x)x-\tanh^{\prime}(\mu_{t}^{\top}x)\mu_{t}\Big]\,.

Recall that
\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{t}^{*},1)}[\tanh(\mu_{t}^{\top}x)x]
is the EM update for mixtures of two Gaussians (See Fact
5
). If we can show that the
G(\mu_{t},\mu^{*}_{t})
term above is “contractive” in the sense that it is decreasing in
\|\mu_{t}-\mu^{*}_{t}\|
, then we can invoke existing results on convergence of EM to show that the distance between the current iterate and
\mu^{*}_{t}
contracts in a single gradient step
[
DTZ17
,
XHM16
]
. Our goal is thus to control
G(\mu_{t},\mu_{t}^{*})
.

For this, we start with the 1D case in Lemma
C.8
. We then extend to the multi-dimensional case in Lemma
C.9
.

Lemma C.8

(One-dimensional version)
.

Let
\mu,\mu^{*}>0
, and consider
\mu\in[c,\frac{4\mu^{*}}{3}]
for some constant
c
. In this one-dimensional case, the function
G
specializes to

\displaystyle G(\mu,\mu^{*})=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu^{*},1)}\Bigl[-\frac{1}{2}\tanh^{\prime\prime}(\mu x)\mu^{2}x+\tanh^{\prime}(\mu x)\mu x^{2}-\tanh^{\prime}(\mu x)\mu\Bigr]\,,

(C.11)

and we have

\displaystyle G(\mu,\mu^{*})\leq 0.01\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\mu-\mu^{*}\right\rvert}

The proof uses the fact that the function
G
only contains first or higher-order derivatives of the
\tanh
function and all the derivatives of
\tanh
decay exponential quickly as
\mu
increases. Therefore, when
\mu
is at least a constant, we obtain the result. The complete proof of lemma
C.8
is given in Appendix
F.2
.

Lemma C.9

(Multi-dimensional version)
.

For any noise scale
t
, when the current parameter at noise scale
t
,
\mu_{t}
, satisfies
\|\mu_{t}\|\in[c,\frac{4\langle\hat{\mu}_{t},\mu_{t}^{*}\rangle}{3}]
for some sufficiently large constant
c
, then the following inequality holds:

\displaystyle\mathinner{\!\left\lVert G(\mu_{t},\mu_{t}^{*})\right\rVert}\leq 0.01\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mu_{t}-\mu_{t}^{*}\right\rVert}

Proof.

Suppose
\{v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{d}\}
are
d
orthonormal directions such that
v_{1}=\hat{\mu}_{t}
and
v_{2}
is either of the two unit vectors
\hat{\mu}^{\perp}_{t}
which are orthogonal to
\hat{\mu}_{t}
in the plane of
\mu_{t}
and
\mu_{t}^{*}
. Recall that

\displaystyle G(\mu_{t},\mu_{t}^{*})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{t}^{*},\mathrm{Id})}\Big[-\frac{1}{2}\tanh^{\prime\prime}(\mu_{t}^{\top}x)\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mu_{t}\right\rVert}^{2}x+(\tanh^{\prime}(\mu_{t}^{\top}x)\mu_{t}^{\top}x)x-\tanh^{\prime}(\mu_{t}^{\top}x)\mu_{t}\Big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)}\Big[-\frac{1}{2}\tanh^{\prime\prime}(\mu_{t}^{\top}(x+\mu_{t}^{*}))\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mu_{t}\right\rVert}^{2}(x+\mu_{t}^{*})

\displaystyle\quad\quad{}+\tanh^{\prime}(\mu_{t}^{\top}(x+\mu_{t}^{*}))(\mu_{t}^{\top}(x+\mu_{t}^{*}))(x+\mu_{t}^{*})-\tanh^{\prime}(\mu_{t}^{\top}(x+\mu_{t}^{*}))\mu_{t}\Big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{d}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)}\Big[-\frac{1}{2}\tanh^{\prime\prime}(\;\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mu_{t}\right\rVert}(\alpha_{1}+\hat{\mu}_{t}^{\top}\mu_{t}^{*}))\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mu_{t}\right\rVert}^{2}\big(\sum\nolimits_{i}\alpha_{i}v_{i}+\mu_{t}^{*}\big)

\displaystyle\quad\quad{}+\tanh^{\prime}(\;\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mu_{t}\right\rVert}(\alpha_{1}+\hat{\mu}_{t}^{\top}\mu_{t}^{*}))\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mu_{t}\right\rVert}(\alpha_{1}+\hat{\mu}_{t}^{\top}\mu_{t}^{*})\big(\sum\nolimits_{i}\alpha_{i}v_{i}+\mu_{t}^{*}\big)

\displaystyle\quad\quad{}-\tanh^{\prime}(\;\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mu_{t}\right\rVert}(\alpha_{1}+\hat{\mu}_{t}^{\top}\mu_{t}^{*}))\mu_{t}\Big]\,,

where in the last equality we rewrote
x\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)
as
\sum_{i=1}^{d}\alpha_{i}v_{i}
for
\alpha_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
. Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\langle\hat{\mu_{t}},G(\mu_{t},\mu_{t}^{*})\rangle

\displaystyle\quad=\mathbb{E}_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{d}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)}\Big[-\frac{1}{2}\tanh^{\prime\prime}(\;\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mu_{t}\right\rVert}(\alpha_{1}+\hat{\mu}_{t}^{\top}\mu_{t}^{*}))\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mu_{t}\right\rVert}^{2}(\alpha_{1}+\hat{\mu_{t}}^{\top}\mu_{t}^{*})

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad+\tanh^{\prime}(\;\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mu_{t}\right\rVert}(\alpha_{1}+\hat{\mu_{t}}^{\top}\mu_{t}^{*}))\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mu_{t}\right\rVert}(\alpha_{1}+\hat{\mu}_{t}^{\top}\mu_{t}^{*})^{2}-\tanh^{\prime}(\;\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mu_{t}\right\rVert}(\alpha_{1}+\hat{\mu_{t}}^{\top}\mu_{t}^{*}))\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mu_{t}\right\rVert}\Big]

\displaystyle\quad=\mathbb{E}_{\alpha_{1}\sim\mathcal{N}(\hat{\mu}^{\top}_{t}\mu_{t}^{*},1)}\Big[-\frac{1}{2}\tanh^{\prime\prime}(\hskip 0.05005pt\|\mu_{t}\|\alpha_{1})\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mu_{t}\right\rVert}^{2}\alpha_{1}+\tanh^{\prime}(\;\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mu_{t}\right\rVert}\alpha_{1})\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mu_{t}\right\rVert}\alpha_{1}^{2}-\tanh^{\prime}(\;\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mu_{t}\right\rVert}\alpha_{1})\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mu_{t}\right\rVert}\Big]\,.

By taking
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mu_{t}\right\rVert}
to be
\mu
and

to be
\mu^{*}
, we observe the similarity between the right side of the above equation and the one-dimensional definition of

defined in Eq. (
C.11
). Using Lemma
C.8
and if
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mu_{t}\right\rVert}\in[c,\frac{4\langle\hat{\mu}_{t},\mu_{t}^{*}\rangle}{3}]
, we have

\displaystyle\langle\hat{\mu_{t}},G(\mu_{t},\mu_{t}^{*})\rangle\leq 0.01\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\langle\hat{\mu_{t}},\mu_{t}\rangle-\langle\hat{\mu_{t}},\mu_{t}^{*}\rangle\right\rvert}

Taking the dot product of

with

, we have

\displaystyle\langle\hat{\mu}^{\perp}_{t},G(\mu_{t},\mu_{t}^{*})\rangle=\;

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{d}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)}\Big[-\frac{1}{2}\tanh^{\prime\prime}(\;\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mu_{t}\right\rVert}(\alpha_{1}+\hat{\mu}_{t}^{\top}\mu_{t}^{*}))\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mu_{t}\right\rVert}^{2}(\alpha_{2}+\langle\hat{\mu}^{\perp}_{t},\mu_{t}^{*}\rangle)

\displaystyle\quad+\tanh^{\prime}(\;\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mu_{t}\right\rVert}(\alpha_{1}+\hat{\mu}_{t}^{\top}\mu_{t}^{*}))\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mu_{t}\right\rVert}(\alpha_{1}+\hat{\mu}_{t}^{\top}\mu_{t}^{*})(\alpha_{2}+\langle\hat{\mu}^{\perp}_{t},\mu_{t}^{*}\rangle)\Big]

\displaystyle=\;

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\alpha_{1}\sim\mathcal{N}(\hat{\mu}_{t}^{\top}\mu_{t}^{*},1)}\Big[-\frac{1}{2}\tanh^{\prime\prime}(\;\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mu_{t}\right\rVert}\alpha_{1})\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mu_{t}\right\rVert}^{2}\langle\hat{\mu}^{\perp}_{t},\mu_{t}^{*}\rangle

\displaystyle\quad+\tanh^{\prime}(\;\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mu_{t}\right\rVert}\alpha_{1})\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mu_{t}\right\rVert}\alpha_{1}\langle\hat{\mu}^{\perp}_{t},\mu_{t}^{*}\rangle\Big]

\displaystyle=\;

\displaystyle\langle\hat{\mu}^{\perp}_{t},\mu_{t}^{*}\rangle\,\mathbb{E}_{\alpha_{1}\sim\mathcal{N}(\hat{\mu}_{t}^{\top}\mu_{t}^{*},1)}\Big[-\frac{1}{2}\tanh^{\prime\prime}(\;\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mu_{t}\right\rVert}\alpha_{1})\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mu_{t}\right\rVert}^{2}+\tanh^{\prime}(\;\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mu_{t}\right\rVert}\alpha_{1})\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mu_{t}\right\rVert}\alpha_{1}\Big]\,.

In Lemma

below, we show that when
\|\mu_{t}\|\in[c,\frac{4\langle\hat{\mu}_{t},\mu_{t}^{*}\rangle}{3}]
, the expectation in the last expression is upper bounded by 0.01. Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\big|\langle\hat{\mu}^{\perp}_{t},G(\mu_{t},\mu_{t}^{*})\rangle\big|\leq 0.01|\langle\hat{\mu}^{\perp}_{t},\mu_{t}^{*}\rangle|\implies\big|\langle\hat{\mu}^{\perp}_{t},G(\mu_{t},\mu_{t}^{*})\rangle\big|\leq 0.01\big|\langle\hat{\mu}^{\perp}_{t},\mu_{t}-\mu_{t}^{*}\rangle\big|

. Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\mathinner{\!\left\lVert G(\mu_{t},\mu_{t}^{*})\right\rVert}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\langle v_{i},G(\mu_{t},\mu_{t}^{*})\rangle^{2}\leq 0.01^{2}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mu_{t}-\mu_{t}^{*}\right\rVert}^{2}\,.\qed

The next Lemma ensures that the parameter
\mu_{t}
after a few steps of gradient descent on the DDPM objective stays in the region where the function

Lemma C.10
.

Suppose the angle between initialization
\hat{\mu}_{t}^{(0)}
and optimal parameter

Proof of Theorem
C.1
.

\displaystyle\big\|\mu_{t}^{(h+1)}-\mu_{t}^{*}\big\|=\big\|\mu_{t}^{(h)}-\eta\nabla L_{t}(s_{\mu_{t}^{(h)}})-\mu_{t}^{*}\big\|+\eta\bigg\|\Big(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\nabla L_{t}(s_{\mu_{t}^{(h)}}(x_{i},z_{i}))\Big)-\nabla L_{t}(s_{\mu_{t}^{(h)}})\bigg\|

\displaystyle\leq(1-\eta)\|\mu_{t}^{(h)}-\mu_{t}^{*}\|+\eta\,\big\|\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{t}^{*},1)}[(\tanh(\mu_{t}^{(h)^{\top}}x))x]-\mu_{t}^{*}\big\|+\eta\|G(\mu_{t}^{(h)},\mu_{t}^{*})\|+\eta\varepsilon\,,

where in the last step we used Lemma

\displaystyle\|\mu_{t}^{(H)}-\mu_{t}^{*}\|\leq 0.97^{H}\|\mu_{t}^{(0)}-\mu_{t}^{*}\|+50\varepsilon.

Appendix D
Learning mixtures of two Gaussians with small separation

In this section, we extend the analysis for learning mixtures of two Gaussians with constant separation, provided in Section

Theorem D.1

(Formal version of Theorem
13
)
.

For any
\mathcal{L}>0
, let
q
be a mixture of two Gaussians (in the form of Eq. (
15
)) with mean parameter
\mu^{*}
satisfying
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mu^{*}\right\rVert}>\mathcal{L}

B
denotes an

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mu^{*}\right\rVert}

\varepsilon^{\prime}\lesssim\frac{1}{d^{2}B^{9}}

\mathrm{poly}(d,B,\frac{1}{\mathcal{L}})*\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{8}}

As described in Section

in which gradient descent is replaced by projected gradient descent. We start in Lemma
D.2
by showing that the projection step in the algorithm ensures that the norm of the current iterate
\mu_{t}
is approximately that of
\mu^{*}_{t}
. Then in Lemma
D.3

Lemma D.2
.

Let
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
be independent samples from
q

R
by
R^{2}\triangleq\frac{1}{n}\sum^{n}_{i=1}\|x_{i}\|^{2}-d

\varepsilon>0

,

|R-\|\mu^{*}\||\leq\varepsilon

Proof.

Observe that we can write the random variable corresponding to the mixture of two Gaussians
X_{0}=X=Z+p\mu^{*}
where

and

is a Rademacher random variable. Using Theorem 3.1.1 (concentration of norms) from
[
Ver
]
, we know that
\|\|Z\|-\sqrt{d}\|_{\psi_{2}}\lesssim 1
. Therefore, sub-Gaussian norm
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\|X_{0}\|\right\rVert}_{\psi_{2}}\lesssim\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\|Z\|\right\rVert}_{\psi_{2}}+\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\|p\mu^{*}\|\right\rVert}_{\psi_{2}}\lesssim B+\sqrt{d}.
Using Lemma 2.7.4 from
[
Ver
]
, we have
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\|X_{0}\|^{2}\right\rVert}_{\psi_{1}}\lesssim\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\|X_{0}\|\right\rVert}_{\psi_{2}}^{2}\lesssim B^{2}+d
. Therefore, using number of samples
n
specified in the Lemma statement, with high probability, we have

\displaystyle\Big|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert x_{i}\right\rVert}^{2}-\mathbb{E}[\|X_{0}\|^{2}]\Big|\leq\varepsilon\mathcal{L}\implies\Big|\|\mu\|^{2}-\|\mu^{*}\|^{2}\Big|\leq\varepsilon\mathcal{L}\implies\big|\|\mu\|-\|\mu^{*}\|\big|\leq\varepsilon

where the penultimate implication uses the fact that
\mathbb{E}_{X_{0}}[\|X_{0}\|^{2}]=\mathbb{E}[\|Z\|^{2}+\|\mu^{*}\|^{2}]=d+\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mu^{*}\right\rVert}^{2}
.
∎

Lemma D.3
.

Assume that
\mathcal{L}\leq\|\mu^{*}\|\leq B

Proof.

Suppose we take one projected gradient step with learning rate
\eta
starting from an iterate
\mu_{t}
. As
\mu_{t}
was the result of a projection, by Lemma

\frac{\varepsilon\mathcal{L}}{d}\lesssim\big\|\mu_{t}^{(h)}\big\|\lesssim\frac{\varepsilon B}{d}

We now bound

\displaystyle\kappa_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{500\eta\sqrt{d^{3}}\|\mu_{t}\|^{4}+20\eta d\|\mu_{t}\|^{2}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mu_{t}^{*}\right\rVert}^{2}+\eta\tilde{\varepsilon}}{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mu_{t}^{*}\right\rVert}^{2}}

where the last inequality follows by choosing population gradient estimation error parameter
\varepsilon=\frac{\varepsilon^{4}\mathcal{L}^{3}}{d^{3}}
with the number of samples

\displaystyle\kappa_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1-3\eta\|\mu_{t}\|^{2}}{(1-3\eta\|\mu_{t}\|^{2})+\eta(\|\mu_{t}^{*}\|^{2}-500\sqrt{d^{3}}\|\mu_{t}\|^{4}-20d\|\mu_{t}\|^{2}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mu_{t}^{*}\right\rVert}^{2}-\tilde{\varepsilon})}

\displaystyle=\frac{1-3\eta\|\mu_{t}\|^{2}}{(1-3\eta\|\mu_{t}\|^{2})+\eta\|\mu_{t}^{*}\|^{2}(1-\kappa_{2})}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1-3\eta\|\mu_{t}^{(h)}\|^{2}}{(1-3\eta\|\mu_{t}^{(h)}\|^{2})+\eta\|\mu_{t}^{*}\|^{2}(1-d^{2}B^{2}\varepsilon)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{1+\frac{\mathcal{L}^{2}\varepsilon^{2}}{20d^{2}}(1-d^{2}B^{2}\varepsilon)}\,.

and

where the last inequality uses

\displaystyle\lesssim 2\|\mu^{*}\|^{2}-2\|\mu^{*}\|^{2}\cos\theta^{(H)}+5B\varepsilon\lesssim 2B^{2}\Big(1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+d^{4}B^{4}\varepsilon^{2}}}\Big)+5B\varepsilon\lesssim d^{2}B^{4}\varepsilon,

(Formal version of Theorem

.

Let

E.1
EM and population gradient descent on DDPM objective

We begin by writing out the gradient update explicitly:

Lemma E.2
.

For any noise scale
t>0
, the gradient of the population DDPM objective
\mathbb{E}[L_{t}(s_{\theta_{t}}(X_{t}))]
with respect to parameter
\mu_{1,t}
is given by

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mu_{1,t}}L_{t}(s_{\theta_{t}})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big[-w_{1,t}(X_{t})(X_{t}-\mu_{1,t})+w_{1,t}(X_{t})(X_{t}-\mu_{1,t})\sum^{K}_{i=1}w_{i,t}(X_{t})\mu_{i,t}^{\top}(X_{t}-\mu_{1,t})

\displaystyle\hskip 36.98857pt+w_{1,t}(X_{t})\mu_{1,t}-w_{1,t}(X_{t})(X_{t}-\mu_{1,t})^{\top}\mu_{1,t}(X_{t}-\mu_{1,t})-w_{1,t}(X_{t})\sum^{K}_{i=1}w_{i,t}(X_{t})\mu_{i,t}

\displaystyle\hskip 36.98857pt-w_{1,t}(X_{t})\sum^{K}_{i=1}\nabla_{x}w_{i,t}(X_{t})^{\top}\mu_{i,t}(X_{t}-\mu_{1,t})\Big]

where
w_{1,t}(x)
and
\mu_{1,t}
are defined in Eq. (
17

Proof.

Recall that the score function of mixture of Gaussians is given by

\displaystyle s_{\theta_{t}}(X_{t})=\sum_{i}w_{i,t}(X_{t})\mu_{i,t}-X_{t}\hskip 14.22636pt

(E.1)

Finding the gradient
\nabla_{\mu_{1,t}}w_{i,t}(X_{t})
, we have

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mu_{1,t}}w_{i,t}(X_{t})=\begin{cases}w_{1,t}(X_{t})(1-w_{1,t}(X_{t}))(X_{t}-\mu_{1,t})&\text{if }i=1\\
-w_{1,t}(X_{t})w_{i,t}(X_{t})(X_{t}-\mu_{1,t})&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

The gradient of the score function is given by

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mu_{1,t}}s_{\theta_{t}}(X_{t})=\nabla_{\mu_{1,t}}\left(w_{1,t}(X_{t})\mu_{1,t}\right)+\sum_{i=2}^{K}\nabla_{\mu_{1,t}}\left(w_{i,t}(X_{t})\mu_{i,t}\right)

\displaystyle=w_{1,t}(X_{t})(1-w_{1,t}(X_{t}))\mu_{1,t}(X_{t}-\mu_{1,t})^{\top}+w_{1,t}(X_{t})I-w_{1,t}(X_{t})\sum_{i=2}^{K}w_{i,t}(X_{t})\mu_{i,t}(X_{t}-\mu_{1,t})^{\top}

\displaystyle=w_{1,t}(X_{t})\mu_{1,t}(X_{t}-\mu_{1,t})^{\top}+w_{1,t}(X_{t})I-w_{1,t}(X_{t})\sum_{i=1}^{K}w_{i,t}(X_{t})\mu_{i,t}(X_{t}-\mu_{1,t})^{\top}\,.

The gradient of
\frac{1}{2}\|s_{\theta_{t}}\|^{2}
is given by

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\nabla\mathinner{\!\left\lVert s_{\theta_{t}}(X_{t})\right\rVert}^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}[s_{\theta_{t}}(X_{t})]_{j}[\nabla_{\mu_{1,t}}s_{\theta_{t}}(X_{t})]_{j}=\nabla_{\mu_{1,t}}s_{\theta_{t}}(X_{t})^{\top}s_{\theta_{t}}(X_{t})

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt\text{ where }[\nabla_{\mu_{1,t}}s_{\theta_{t}}(X_{t})]_{j}\text{ is $j^{th}$ row of }\nabla_{\mu_{1,t}}s_{\theta_{t}}(X_{t})\,.

The gradient of this is given by

\displaystyle\frac{\nabla_{\mu_{1,t}}s_{\theta_{t}}(X_{t})^{\top}Z_{t}}{\beta_{t}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\beta_{t}}\Big(w_{1,t}(X_{t})(X_{t}-\mu_{1,t})\mu_{1,t}^{\top}Z_{t}+w_{1,t}(X_{t})Z_{t}

(E.2)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad-w_{1,t}(X_{t})\sum_{i=1}^{K}w_{i,t}(X_{t})(X_{t}-\mu_{1,t})\mu_{i,t}^{\top}Z_{t}\Big)

(E.3)

Applying Stein’s lemma to the expectation of the first term in Eq. (
E.3
), we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{X_{0},Z_{t}}[w_{1,t}(X_{t})(X_{t}-\mu_{1,t})\mu_{1,t}^{\top}Z_{t}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}_{X_{0},Z_{t}}[w_{1,t}(X_{t})(X_{t}-\mu_{1,t})\mu_{1,t,j}Z_{t,j}]

(E.4)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}_{X_{0},Z_{t}}[w_{1,t}(X_{t})\beta_{t}e_{j}\mu_{1,t,j}+\beta_{t}\nabla_{x}w_{1,t}(X_{t})^{\top}e_{j}(X_{t}-\mu_{1,t})\mu_{1,t,j}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{X_{0},Z_{t}}[w_{1,t}(X_{t})\beta_{t}\mu_{1,t}+\beta_{t}\nabla_{x}w_{1,t}(X_{t})^{\top}\mu_{1,t}(X_{t}-\mu_{1,t})]

The expectation of the second term in Eq. (
E.3

\beta_{t}\mathbb{E}_{X_{t}}[\nabla_{x}w_{1,t}(X_{t})]
by Stein’s Lemma. Each summand in the third term in Eq. (
E.3

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{X_{0},Z_{t}}\mathinner{\left[w_{1,t}(X_{t})w_{i,t}(X_{t})(X_{t}-\mu_{1,t})\mu_{i,t}^{\top}Z_{t}\right]}

(E.5)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}_{X_{0},Z_{t}}\mathinner{\left[w_{1,t}(X_{t})w_{i,t}(X_{t})(X_{t}-\mu_{1,t})\mu_{i,t,j}Z_{t,j}\right]}

(E.6)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\mu_{i,t,j}\mathbb{E}_{X_{0},Z_{t}}\Big[w_{1,t}(X_{t})w_{i,t}(X_{t})\beta_{t}e_{j}+\beta_{t}w_{1,t}(X_{t})\nabla_{x}w_{i,t}(X_{t})^{\top}e_{j}(X_{t}-\mu_{1,t})

(E.7)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+\beta_{t}\nabla_{x}w_{1,t}(X_{t})^{\top}e_{j}w_{i,t}(X_{t})(X_{t}-\mu_{1,t})\Big]

(E.8)

\displaystyle=\beta_{t}\,\mathbb{E}_{X_{0},Z_{t}}\Big[w_{1,t}(X_{t})w_{i,t}(X_{t})\mu_{i,t}+w_{1,t}(X_{t})\nabla_{x}w_{i,t}(X_{t})^{\top}\mu_{i,t}(X_{t}-\mu_{1,t})

(E.9)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+\nabla_{x}w_{1,t}(X_{t})^{\top}\mu_{i,t}w_{i,t}(X_{t})(X_{t}-\mu_{1,t})\Big]

(E.10)

Combining the gradients of all the terms of Eq. (
E.10
), we have

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mu_{1,t}}L_{t}(s_{\theta_{t}})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big[w_{1,t}(X_{t})(X_{t}-\mu_{1,t})\mu_{1,t}^{\top}s_{\theta_{t}}(X_{t})+w_{1,t}(X_{t})s_{\theta_{t}}(X_{t})-w_{1,t}(X_{t})(X_{t}-\mu_{1,t})\sum_{i}w_{i,t}(X_{t})\mu_{i,t}^{\top}s_{\theta_{t}}(X_{t})

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad+\nabla_{x}w_{1,t}(X_{t})+w_{1,t}(X_{t})\mu_{1,t}+\nabla_{x}w_{1,t}(X_{t})^{\top}\mu_{1,t}(X_{t}-\mu_{1,t})-w_{1,t}(X_{t})\sum_{i}w_{i,t}(X_{t})\mu_{i,t}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad-w_{1,t}(X_{t})\sum_{i}\nabla_{x}w_{i,t}(X_{t})^{\top}\mu_{i,t}(X_{t}-\mu_{1,t})-\sum_{i}\nabla_{x}w_{1,t}(X_{t})^{\top}\mu_{i,t}w_{i,t}(X_{t})(X_{t}-\mu_{1,t})\Big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big[-w_{1,t}(X_{t})(X_{t}-\mu_{1,t})+w_{1,t}(X_{t})(X_{t}-\mu_{1,t})\sum_{i}w_{i,t}(X_{t})\mu_{i,t}^{\top}(X_{t}-\mu_{1,t})

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad+w_{1,t}(X_{t})\mu_{1,t}-w_{1,t}(X_{t})(X_{t}-\mu_{1,t})^{\top}\mu_{1,t}(X_{t}-\mu_{1,t})-w_{1,t}(X_{t})\sum_{i}w_{i,t}(X_{t})\mu_{i,t}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad-w_{1,t}(X_{t})\sum_{i}\nabla_{x}w_{i,t}(X_{t})^{\top}\mu_{i,t}(X_{t}-\mu_{1,t})\Big]\,,

where the last equality uses Lemma

\displaystyle\nabla_{x}w_{1,t}(X_{t})+w_{1,t}(X_{t})s_{\theta_{t}}(X_{t})

\displaystyle=-w_{1,t}(X_{t})(X_{t}-\mu_{1,t})

\displaystyle=-w_{1,t}(X_{t})(X_{t}-\mu_{1,t})^{\top}\mu_{1,t}(X_{t}-\mu_{1,t})\,.\qed

We will also need the following intermediate calculation:

Lemma E.3
.

For any
i\in[K]

\displaystyle\nabla_{x}w_{i,t}(X_{t})

By taking the gradient of

\displaystyle\nabla_{x}w_{i,t}(X_{t})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+\frac{\exp\big(-\frac{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert X_{t}-\mu_{i,t}\right\rVert}^{2}}{2}\big)\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{K}\exp\Big(-\frac{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert X_{t}-\mu_{j,t}\right\rVert}^{2}}{2}\Big)(X_{t}-\mu_{j,t})}{\left(\sum_{j=1}^{K}\exp\Big(-\frac{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert X_{t}-\mu_{j,t}\right\rVert}^{2}}{2}\Big)\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle=-w_{i,t}(X_{t})(1-w_{i,t}(X_{t}))(X_{t}-\mu_{i,t})+w_{i,t}(X_{t})\left(\sum_{j=1,j\neq i}^{K}w_{j,t}(X_{t})(X_{t}-\mu_{j,t})\right)\,.\qed

Gaussians:

Lemma E.4

Observe that the first term in the expression for the population gradient of the DDPM objective in Lemma

K

Note that when the ground truth parameters

also satisfies Assumption

We focus on the

[

Using Lemma

\bigl\|\mathbb{E}\big[w_{1,t}(X_{t})(1-w_{1,t}(X_{t}))(X_{t}-\mu_{1,t})(X_{t}-\mu_{1,t})^{\top}\big]\mu_{1,t}\bigr\|\leq\frac{d^{2}c_{r}^{2}B}{d^{c_{r}^{2}/1000}},

for any

\displaystyle\biggl\|\sum_{i=2}^{K}\mathbb{E}[w_{1,t}(X_{t})w_{i,t}(X_{t})(X_{t}-\mu_{1,t})(X_{t}-\mu_{1,t})^{\top}\mu_{i,t}]\biggr\|

\displaystyle\biggl\|\sum_{i=1}^{K}\mathbb{E}\Big[w_{1,t}(X_{t})w_{i,t}(X_{t})(1-w_{i,t}(X_{t}))(X_{t}-\mu_{1,t})(X_{t}-\mu_{i,t})^{\top}\mu_{i,t}\Big]\biggr\|

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{KB^{2}}{d^{c_{r}^{2}/4000}}\,,

where the last inequality follows from

\mathbb{E}\big[\|X_{t}-\mu_{1,t}\|^{2}\|X_{t}-\mu_{i,t}\|^{2}\big]\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\big[\|X_{t}-\mu_{1,t}\|^{4}\big]\mathbb{E}\big[\|X_{t}-\mu_{i,t}\|^{4}\big]}\lesssim B^{2}\,.

Similarly, by simplifying the second term, we get

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{K}\sum_{j=1,j\neq i}^{K}\mathbb{E}\big[\big\|w_{1,t}(X_{t})w_{i,t}(X_{t})w_{j,t}(X_{t})(X_{t}-\mu_{1,t})(X_{t}-\mu_{j,t})^{\top}\mu_{i,t}\big\|\big]

where the last inequality uses Lemma
E.5
. Simplifying the following term using Lemma
E.5
, we have

\displaystyle\Big\|\mathbb{E}[w_{1,t}(X_{t})\mu_{1,t}-w_{1,t}(X_{t})\sum_{i=1}^{K}w_{i,t}(X_{t})\mu_{i,t}]\Big\|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=2}^{K}\mathbb{E}\big[\big\|w_{1,t}(X_{t})w_{i,t}(X_{t})\mu_{i,t}\big\|\big]+\sum_{i=2}^{K}\mathbb{E}\big[\big\|w_{1,t}(X_{t})w_{i,t}(X_{t})\mu_{1,t}\big\|\big]\leq\frac{2KB}{d^{c_{r}^{2}/200}}\,.

Combining all the results, we obtain the theorem statement.
∎

Lemma F.5
.

Suppose

\displaystyle|\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{N}(b,1)}[-0.5\tanh^{\prime\prime}(ax)a^{2}+\tanh^{\prime}(ax)ax]|\leq 0.01
