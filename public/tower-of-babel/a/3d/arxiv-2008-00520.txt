Bayesian Inference of Minimally Complex Models with Interactions of Arbitrary Order Preprint: APS/123-QED
Clélia de Mulatier, Matteo Marsili

Abstract
Finding the model that best describes a high-dimensional dataset is a daunting task, even more so if one aims to consider all possible high-order patterns of the data (i.e., correlation patterns between three or more variables), going beyond pairwise models. For binary data, we show that this task becomes feasible when restricting the search to a family of simple models, that we call Minimally Complex Models (MCMs). MCMs are maximum entropy models that have interactions of arbitrarily high order grouped into independent components of minimal complexity. They are simple in information-theoretic terms, which means they can only fit well certain types of data patterns and are therefore easy to falsify. We show that Bayesian model selection restricted to these models is computationally feasible and has many advantages. First, the model evidence, which balances goodness-of-fit against complexity, can be computed efficiently without any parameter fitting, enabling very fast explorations of the space of MCMs. Second, the family of MCMs is invariant under gauge transformations, which can be used to develop a representation-independent approach to statistical modeling. For small systems (up to 15 variables), combining these two results allows us to select the best MCM among all, even though the number of models is already extremely large ( 10 68 10^{68} models for 15 15 variables). For larger systems, we propose simple heuristics to find optimal MCMs in reasonable times. Besides, inference and sampling can be performed without any computational effort. Finally, because MCMs have interactions of any order, they can reveal the presence of important high-order dependencies in the data, providing a new approach to explore high-order dependencies in complex systems. We apply our method to synthetic data and real-world examples, illustrating how MCMs portray the structure of dependencies among variables in a simple manner, extracting falsifiable predictions on symmetries and invariance from the data.



I
Introduction

“All models are wrong, but some models are useful”
Box 1976
. This statement is particularly appropriate in the context of statistical inference of high-dimensional data. Recent spectacular advances in machine learning have shown that very complex models, such as deep neural networks
LeCun et al. 2015
, can be very “useful” in learning hidden features in high-dimensional data, making it possible to generalize from examples.
Models that encode the Laws of Nature refer to a different notion of “usefulness”.
As argued by Wigner
Wigner 1990
, they describe regularities – such as how bodies fall under the effect of gravity – in
simple
forms involving only a few variables and parameters.
These regularities occur in ways that are independent of many conditions that could affect them.
Simple models, such as Newton’s law, tell us more about independence than about dependence.
Their simplicity reflects specific principles – such as invariances, symmetries, and conservation laws – that are easy to falsify.
In this paper, we are interested in performing statistical inference for binary data with models that display such simplicity.

In information theory, the
simplicity
of a statistical model is defined unambiguously in terms of the Minimum Description Length (MDL) complexity, which is the minimal number of bits required to describe the model
Grünwald and Grunwald 2007
.
This complexity is a measure of the number of different datasets that can be described by the same parametric model
Myung et al. 2000
, and therefore, of the flexibility of the model.
A
simple
model thus fits well few types of statistical structures of data
and, as a consequence, is easy to falsify.
In Bayesian inference, the MDL complexity predicts how much a model should be penalized due to its flexibility
Myung et al. 2000
.
In the context of binary data, Beretta
et al.

Beretta et al. 2018
have studied the MDL complexity of spin models, which are maximum entropy models
with interactions of arbitrary order.
They argue that, with the same number of parameters, the simplest models are those for which statistical dependencies concentrate on the smallest subset of variables.
By contrast, spin models with pairwise interactions, such as the Ising model, turn out to be very complex. Their flexibility
Merchan and Nemenman 2016
allows them to model a broad variety of datasets, from neuronal activity
Schneidman et al. 2006
;
Savin and Tkačik 2017
to voting outcomes
Lee et al. 2015
.
Yet, statistical inference of pairwise models is known to be computationally challenging
Montanari and Pereira 2009
;
Cocco and Monasson 2012
;
Barton et al. 2016
;
Vuffray et al. 2016
;
Nguyen et al. 2017
, whereas the simplest spin models, called “sub-complete models” in
Beretta et al. 2018
,
are very easy to infer.

In this paper, we show that the evidence of sub-complete models is also straightforward to compute and that this property makes Bayesian Model Selection (BMS) possible within a remarkably broad family of simple models for binary data, for which both the maximum likelihood and the model evidence are easy to compute.
These models, which we call
Minimally Complex Models
(MCMs),
are spin models formed of independent components of minimal complexity.
In some representations, an MCM corresponds to a partition of the variables into independent parts (or “components”)
with no interactions between the parts and with all possible interactions within each part (see Fig.
1
).
The best MCM for a given dataset thus captures the global structure of dependencies and independencies in the data,
providing robust predictions on dependencies between the variables.
In particular, our approach can identify the presence of important high-order interactions (i.e, between three or more variables).
Besides being simple and easy to infer, MCMs also enjoy properties that make statistical inference invariant under changes in the representation of data. Finally, inferred MCMs can also be sampled from with minimal computational effort.

In what follows, we formally define MCMs and discuss how to compare them in the context of BMS. As the set of potential MCMs to compare is huge (see Fig.
2
), we propose different heuristics
to find the best MCM for a given dataset.
Our results are finally illustrated in several test cases, where one can appreciate how our approach uncovers global data structures of
dependencies and independencies that are consistent with the discussed phenomena.

II
Bayesian Model Selection of Spin Models

A binary dataset
\bm{\hat{s}}=(\bm{s}^{(1)},\ldots,\bm{s}^{(N)})
is a set of
N
independent observations of spin configurations
\bm{s}=(s_{1},\ldots,s_{n})
, where
s_{i}=\pm 1
. In the following, we assume that each configuration
\bm{s}^{(i)}
is independently drawn from an unknown distribution, which we aim to infer. To do so, we consider the following parametric family of probability distributions:

p(\bm{s}\,|\,\bm{g},\mathcal{M})=\frac{1}{Z_{\mathcal{M}}(\bm{g})}\,\exp\left({\;\sum_{\bm{\mu}\in\mathcal{M}}g_{\bm{\mu}}\phi^{\bm{\mu}}(\bm{s})}\right)\,,

(1)

where
\mathcal{M}=\{\bm{\mu}^{(1)},\,\ldots,\bm{\mu}^{(K)}\}
is a set of
K
interactions of arbitrary order, and where the
\bm{\mu}^{(i)}
’s are binary vectors encoding which spins are involved in each interaction.
Any choice of the set
\mathcal{M}
of interactions defines a spin model.
The operators
\phi^{\bm{\mu}}(\bm{s})=\prod_{i=1}^{n}{s_{i}}^{\mu_{i}}\,
are the product spin operators associated with the interactions
\bm{\mu}=(\mu_{1},\,\ldots,\,\mu_{n})
, while the conjugate parameters
g_{\bm{\mu}}
modulate the strength of the interactions.
For example, pairwise spin models contain operators of the form
\phi^{\bm{\mu}}(\bm{s})=s_{i}s_{j}
between pairs of spins.
In a 4-spin system, a three-body interaction between
s_{1}
,
s_{2}
, and
s_{4}
is encoded by the binary vector
\bm{\mu}=(1,1,0,1)
and is associated with the operator
\phi^{\bm{\mu}}(\bm{s})=s_{1}s_{2}s_{4}
.
Finally, the partition function
Z_{\mathcal{M}}(\bm{g})
ensures normalization (see App.
A
for more details on spin models).

Equation (
1

\mathcal{M}
of operators
Mastromatteo 2013
.
In absence of any prior knowledge about the system, one must compare the performance of all spin models to find which best describes a dataset
\bm{\hat{s}}
.
We focus on the best model
\mathcal{M}
, which is the one achieving the largest posterior probability
P(\mathcal{M}\,|\,\bm{\hat{s}})
. This is obtained with Bayes’ theorem from the
evidence
(or
marginal likelihood
) defined as follows
Kruschke 2014
:

P(\bm{\hat{s}}\,|\,\mathcal{M})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{M}}\!d\bm{g}\,\prod_{i=1}^{N}\,p\big(\bm{s}^{(i)}|\,\bm{g},\mathcal{M}\big)\;P_{0}(\bm{g}\,|\,\mathcal{M})\,,

(2)

where
P_{0}(\bm{g}\,|\,\mathcal{M})
is a prior distribution over the parameters.
We assume a uniform prior over the models
\mathcal{M}
, such that the best model for data is also the one that maximizes the evidence.
Furthermore, we assume that
P_{0}(\bm{g}\,|\,\mathcal{M})
takes the form of Jeffreys’ prior
Jeffreys 1946
. With this choice, it was shown
Myung et al. 2000
that in the limit of large datasets (
N\to\infty
) the model
\mathcal{M}
maximizing the evidence in Eq. (
2
) is also the one providing the most succinct description of the data as defined by the Minimum Description Length (MDL) principle
Rissanen 1986
;
Rissanen 1996
;
Grünwald and Grunwald 2007
.
Indeed, expanding Eq. (
2
) to order
O(1)
in
N
gives
Myung et al. 2000
:

\hskip-1.42262pt\log P(\bm{\hat{s}}\,|\,\mathcal{M})\underset{N\to\infty}{\simeq}\log P(\bm{\hat{s}}\,|\,\bm{\hat{g}},\mathcal{M})-\frac{K}{2}\log\left(\frac{N}{2\pi}\right)-c_{\mathcal{M}},

(3)

where
\bm{\hat{g}}
is the vector of maximum likelihood parameters.
Using this expansion,
the best model is the one that has the largest maximum log-likelihood penalized by two terms, which can be recognized as the MDL complexity of model
\mathcal{M}
.
The first is proportional to the number
K
of parameters in the model (the more parameters, the more complex), while the second term
c_{\mathcal{M}}

penalizes models depending on their geometry (see definition in App.
C
).

Despite the existence of clear theoretical prescriptions on how to select the best model,
it is in practice nearly impossible to find it.
Indeed, it is computationally challenging to compute the evidence in Eq. (
2
) or the complexity
c_{\mathcal{M}}
for a generic spin model
\mathcal{M}
.
In addition, the number of potential models to compare is huge: in an
n
-spin system, there exist
2^{n}-1
interactions of arbitrary order, and therefore
2^{2^{n}-1}
potential models.
An exhaustive search among all spin models is unfeasible even for small systems (see Fig.
2
).
A possible solution to this hurdle is to restrict model selection to a smaller family of models, such as pairwise models.
For this family, model selection turns into a problem of graph reconstruction
Montanari and Pereira 2009
, that aims at identifying the network of interactions among spins.
A plethora of methods and results have been derived within this framework
Cocco and Monasson 2012
;
Barton et al. 2016
;
Nguyen et al. 2017
;
Vuffray et al. 2016
;
Bulso et al. 2016
;
Tkačik et al. 2014
.
Here, we propose to focus on a different family of models, the
minimally complex models
(MCMs).

III
Minimally Complex Models (MCMs)

III.1
Definition

MCMs are a subset of the spin models defined in Eq. (
1
), for which the set
\mathcal{M}
of interactions is the union of
independent complete components
(ICCs)
\mathcal{M}_{a}
:

\mathcal{M}=\bigcup_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\mathcal{M}_{a}\,,

(4)

where
\mathcal{A}
is the set of ICCs of
\mathcal{M}
.
A
complete component

\mathcal{M}_{a}
is a set of interactions verifying that, for any two interactions
\bm{\mu},\bm{\nu}\in\mathcal{M}_{a}
, the interaction
\bm{\mu}\oplus\bm{\nu}
associated with the spin operator
\phi^{\bm{\mu}\oplus\bm{\nu}}(\bm{s})\doteq\phi^{\bm{\mu}}(\bm{s})\,\phi^{\bm{\nu}}(\bm{s})
also belongs to
\mathcal{M}_{a}

(
\oplus
denotes the element-wise sum modulo 2 of the binary vectors
\bm{\mu}
and
\bm{\nu}
– see App.
A
).
Furthermore, complete components are said to be
independent
if none of the operators of a given component
\mathcal{M}_{a}
can be obtained by combinations of operators of the other components (i.e. by products of operators of the set
\mathcal{M}\backslash\mathcal{M}_{a}
).
Figure
3
shows several examples of MCMs, including two special cases: independent models and sub-complete models.
Independent models (IMs) are MCMs for which all ICCs have only a single operator.
Sub-complete models (SCM) are MCMs with a single ICC, and they are the models with the smallest complexity in Ref.
Beretta et al. 2018
.

III.2
Preferred basis of an MCM

Ref.
Beretta et al. 2018
introduced the notion of equivalence classes of spin models.
Models of the same class can be obtained from one another by certain basis transformations of the spin variables that preserve the structure of spin operators (see examples in Fig.
3
) and have thus the same statistical properties
1
1

1

Ref.
Beretta et al. 2018
shows that models of the same class have the same Fisher information matrix up to permutations of their parameters.
. Such transformations are called
gauge transformation
in
Beretta et al. 2018
(see App.
B
).
From a different perspective, models of the same class can also be seen as the same abstract statistical model represented in different bases.
Each row in Fig.
3
gives examples of models belonging to the same class. Here, one can observe that
there are bases in which the representation of an MCM is easy to define
in terms of a partition of the basis variables (see first column in Fig.
3
).
In the following, we define this idea more formally, by introducing the notion of
preferred basis
of an MCM.

A
basis

\bm{b}
of a model
\mathcal{M}
is a minimal set of
r
independent operators,
\bm{b}=(\phi_{1},\cdots,\phi_{r})
, that can generate all the operators of
\mathcal{M}
.
Thus, any operators
\phi\in\mathcal{M}
can be written as a product of elements of
\bm{b}
in a unique way. The cardinality
r
of such basis is called the
rank
of
\mathcal{M}
(necessarily
r\leq n
).
We recall that a set
\bm{b}
of operators is said
independent
if none of its operators can be obtained as a product of other operators of
\bm{b}

Beretta et al. 2018
(such a set of operators forms an IM).
For example,
\bm{b}=\{s_{1},s_{2},s_{3}\}
and
\bm{b}^{\prime}=\{s_{1},s_{1}s_{2},s_{1}s_{2}s_{3}\}
are two sets of independent operators, and both sets are possible bases for the model
\mathcal{M}=\{s_{1},s_{2},s_{1}s_{2},s_{3}\}
.

By definition, a
complete
component
\mathcal{M}_{a}
is a special case of spin models that contains all the
2^{r_{a}}-1
operators generated by its basis
\bm{b}_{a}=(\phi_{1},\cdots,\phi_{r_{a}})
.
As a direct consequence, the number of operators of an MCM is

\displaystyle K=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}(2^{r_{a}}-1)\,.

(5)

Note that the choice of basis of an ICC is not unique.
In the example above,
\bm{b}
and
\bm{b}^{\prime}
both generate the same ICC

\mathcal{M}_{a}=\{s_{1},s_{2},s_{3},s_{1}s_{2},s_{2}s_{3},s_{1}s_{3},s_{1}s_{2}s_{3}\}
.

Finally, as a result of the
independence
between the complete components
\mathcal{M}_{a}
of an MCM
\mathcal{M}
, the union of the bases
\bm{b}_{a}
for each
\mathcal{M}_{a}
forms a basis
\bm{b}
of
\mathcal{M}
,

\displaystyle\bm{b}=\cup_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\bm{b}_{a}\,,

(6)

and the rank of an MCM is
r=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}r_{a}
.
We refer to such basis as a
preferred basis
of an MCM, because the representation of
\mathcal{M}
precisely corresponds to a partition of the basis variables into ICCs (such as in the examples of Fig.
1
).
Note that the preferred basis of an MCM is not unique (except for IMs).

III.3
Properties of MCMs

Let us now discuss the properties of MCMs.
We first observe that, as a consequence of Eq. (
6
),
the state probability distribution of an MCM
\mathcal{M}
factorizes over the probability distributions of its ICCs, when expressed in terms of the operators
\bm{b}(\bm{s})=\cup_{a}\,\bm{b}_{a}(\bm{s})
of a preferred basis of
\mathcal{M}
(see App.
D
):

p(\bm{s}\,|\,\bm{g},\mathcal{M})=\frac{1}{2^{(n-r)}}\,\prod_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\,p_{a}\big(\bm{b}_{a}(\bm{s})\,|\,\bm{g}_{a}^{\prime},\mathcal{M}_{a}^{\prime}\big).

(7)

The ICCs
\mathcal{M}_{a}^{\prime}
are the gauge transformed of the ICCs
\mathcal{M}_{a}
of
\mathcal{M}
in the basis
\bm{b}_{a}

and each
p_{a}
is a probability distribution over
r_{a}
spin variables only.
The prefactor corresponds to a
1/2
probability for each of the
(n-r)
variables not modeled by
\mathcal{M}

(such as the spins represented by empty dots in the first column of Fig.
3
).
Equation (
7
) implies that both the likelihood and the evidence of an MCM factorize over its ICCs.
Note that this doesn’t mean that these quantities are
factorizable in the variables
\bm{s}
of the original dataset.
In addition, the evidence of an ICC is remarkably simple to compute analytically (see App.
E
),
and leads to this expression for the evidence of an MCM:

P(\bm{\hat{s}}\,|\,\mathcal{M})=\frac{1}{2^{N(n-r)}}\prod_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\left[\frac{\Gamma(2^{r_{a}-1})}{\Gamma(N+2^{r_{a}-1})}\prod_{\bm{b}_{a}}\left[\frac{\Gamma(k_{\bm{b}_{a}}+\frac{1}{2})}{\Gamma(\frac{1}{2})}\right]\right]

(8)

in which
N
is the number of datapoints in
\bm{\hat{s}}
,
\Gamma
is the gamma function, and
k_{\bm{b}_{a}}
is the number of times that the basis operators of
\mathcal{M}_{a}^{\prime}
take the value
{\bm{b}_{a}}
over the dataset.
The product over
\bm{b}_{a}
denotes a product over all the
2^{r_{a}}
values that can be taken by
\bm{b}_{a}
;
states not observed in the data (
k_{\bm{b}_{a}}=0
)
contribute a factor
1
.
Finally, the value of the parameters that maximizes the likelihood function
is also straightforward to compute and doesn’t require any parameter fitting
(see App.
E
).
As a consequence, the model probability distribution at the maximum-likelihood estimate
\bm{\hat{g}}
also takes a very simple form
(see App.
E
):

p(\bm{s}\,|\,\bm{\hat{g}},\mathcal{M})=\frac{1}{2^{(n-r)}}\prod_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\frac{k_{\bm{b}_{a}(\bm{s})}}{N},

(9)

where
\bm{b}_{a}(\bm{s})
is the representation of
\bm{s}
in the basis
\bm{b}_{a}

and
k_{\bm{b}_{a}(\bm{s})}
is the number of time the state
\bm{b}_{a}(\bm{s})
is observed in the data.
This makes sampling from the model distribution computationally efficient (see App.
H
).
In summary, all the useful quantities in the context of statistical inference of MCM (maximum-likelihood estimate, value of the maximum likelihood and of the evidence, and model distribution at best fit) have simple expressions that can be readily computed from the counts
k_{\bm{b}_{a}}
of the number of times the configuration
{\bm{b}_{a}}
of the basis operators is observed in the data.

\bm{s}
by a set of
n

\bm{s}^{\prime}=\mathcal{T}(\bm{s})=\left(\phi_{1}(\bm{s}),\ldots,\phi_{n}(\bm{s})\right)
. As discussed above, GTs are changes of the reference frame with which data, operators or models are described. Indeed, GTs are
bijections from the set of all operators to itself

A transformed operator
\phi^{\prime}

is obtained from the original operator
\phi

by
\phi^{\prime}(\bm{s}^{\prime})=\phi(\mathcal{T}^{-1}(\bm{s}^{\prime}))
.
, and therefore also from the set of all spin models to itself
3

A transformed model
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}=\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{M})
is obtained by transforming each operator of
\mathcal{M}
.

(see App.

However, the mutual relation between the operators of a model

\phi_{1}(\bm{s})

Therefore the invariance of the family of MCMs under GTs makes the space of MCMs
akin to a

, as defined e.g. in Ref.

It allows one to learn the relational structure between operators – the sufficient statistics in models of the form of Eq. (

This is not true for pairwise models, because the family of pairwise models is not closed under GTs: a GT applied to a pairwise model does not necessarily return a pairwise model.
Invariance under GTs of the considered family of models is a key requirement in BMS, because it makes it possible to uncover a hidden relational structure that is independent of how the data is represented.

Finally, Ref.
Beretta et al. 2018
argues that, among all models with the same number of parameters, models with a single ICC
have the lowest MDL complexity. As MCMs are formed by the (independent) union of ICCs,
their MDL complexity is the sum of the complexity of each ICC, and is thus expected to be small.
We conjecture that MCMs are the models with
the lowest MDL complexity among all spin models with the same number of parameters and the same rank. We verified that this is true for all models with up to
4
spin variables (see App.
C
).
Although this statement has yet to be proven in the general case, our belief in its likely correctness motivated our choice of the name “minimally complex models”.

III.4
Bayesian Model Selection

All these properties are good reasons for restricting BMS to the set of MCMs.
First, the factorization in Eq. (
7
) shows that the best MCM for a given dataset provides sharp predictions on independencies between the variables modeled by different ICCs.
Second, the fact that the computation of the evidence in Eq. (
8
) is straightforward
greatly simplifies the task of BMS, because comparisons between models can be done directly on the basis of their evidence without any parameter fitting.
Finally, invariance under GTs of the family of MCMs ensures that the selected model does not depend on the basis
in which the data is represented.

Yet the number of MCMs is still astronomically large, even for moderate values of
n
(growing roughly as
2^{n^{2}}
, see Fig.
2
).
To find the MCM with maximal evidence, we propose to divide the search in two steps.
First, we search for the best model among all
Independent Models
(IM), which are MCMs with
r_{a}=1
for all
a\in\mathcal{A}
(see Fig.
3
).
The maximization of the evidence over this family can be done by finding the set
\bm{b}^{*}
of the
n
most biased independent operators in the dataset (see App.
G
).
Although the number of IMs is also huge (
\sim 2^{n^{2}}
), finding the best IM with this procedure only takes of the order of
n\,2^{n}
operations.
We developed a program performing the search for the best IM, which is available at Ref.
de Mulatier 2024
.
Once the best IM
\bm{b}^{*}
is found,
we reduce our search to the subset of MCMs that
admit
\bm{b}^{*}
as a preferred basis.
This entails finding the optimal partition of the basis
\bm{b}^{*}
into ICCs, which is the partition that maximizes the evidence.
Searching exhaustively among all possible partitions of
\bm{b}^{*}
can be done efficiently using a Gray code
Ehrlich 1973
;
Knuth 2011
; our program performing this search is available at Ref.

V
MCM selection, heuristics for large systems

When
n
is large and an exhaustive search is unfeasible, we propose the following heuristics.
In order to find the best IM
\bm{b}^{\star}
, we start from an initial basis
\bm{b}
(e.g.,
\bm{b}=\bm{s}
), and we build all interactions up to a chosen order
k
. Among these interactions, we identify the set
\bm{b}^{\prime}
of
n
independent operators that are maximally biased, and replace
\bm{b}
by
\bm{b}^{\prime}
. We repeat this procedure until we find
\bm{b}^{\prime}=\bm{b}
.
This final basis is our best choice for the IM
\bm{b}^{\star}
.
Although the exploration of the space of IM is limited by the choice of
k
, the iteration of this procedure is able, in principle, to explore the space of operators to any order. Our program implementing this algorithm is also available in Ref.
de Mulatier 2024
.

Next, we apply a hierarchical merging procedure to find the optimal MCM. We start from the IM based on the basis operators
\bm{b}^{*}
identified above, which is an MCM with
n
ICCs of rank
r_{a}=1
.
We then compare all possible merging of two ICCs to identify the pair that yields the maximal increase of the evidence (if any). We merge the corresponding ICCs, and repeat this procedure until the evidence can’t be increased by merging anymore.
This procedure finds the MCM that achieves the maximal value of the evidence along the hierarchical merging trajectory,
as the number of ICCs varies from
n
to
1
. Our program implementing this algorithm is available in Ref.
de Mulatier and Kamphof 2024
. In the following sections, we apply this algorithm to several datasets, performing the iterative search for the best basis at the
k=4^{\rm th}
order.
Detailed discussion about the performance of our heuristic algorithm is beyond the scope of the present work, which is focused on introducing MCMs.
However, some general observations to this effect are presented in
App.
L
.

VII
Discussion

Finding the model that best describes a dataset within Bayesian model selection is a daunting task.
Here, we show that this task is considerably simplified when the selection is restricted to the family of Minimally Complex Models (MCMs).
BMS within MCMs probes interactions of arbitrary order and can reveal the presence of high-order interactions or confirm that low-order interactions are prevalent in a dataset.
Yet, these models are simple in terms of their information-theoretic complexity, as defined by the Minimum Description Length principle
Rissanen 1986
.
This means that they are expected to provide robust predictions on dependencies in the data.
In particular, MCMs disentangle the structure of statistical dependencies by modeling data with independent model components:
the best MCM for a given dataset provides the factorization of the state probability distribution
that best matches the statistical structure of the data.
More precisely, in some representations, the best MCM corresponds to a partition of the variables into groups, where variables are more correlated within groups than across groups (similarly to a community structure).
This is illustrated in several examples.

Thanks to this factorization property,
future works in the modeling of very large systems could use MCMs as a pre-processing step for dividing the modeling problem into smaller sub-problems that can be analyzed in more detail.
Similarly, MCMs provide a notion of locality that could be used in the context of coarse-graining or data renormalization procedures
Meshulam et al. 2019
going beyond pairwise correlations.
Moreover, MCMs allow for modeling data with interactions of arbitrary order and thus open up new ways to infer high-order interaction structures from data.
This contributes to advancing research in the physics of high-order interactions in complex systems
Rosas et al. 2019
;
Battiston et al. 2021
;
Ruggeri et al. 2023
.
Finally, the invariance of the family of MCMs under Gauge Transformations (GTs)
ensures that the inference process is independent of how data is represented
9
9

9

Consider, for a purely illustrative purpose, an idealized problem of inference in a gene regulatory network.
Assume that whether gene
i
is expressed (
s_{i}=+1
) or not (
s_{i}=-1
) depends on whether its
t_{i}
transcription regulators are bound (
\sigma_{i,a}=+1
) or not (
\sigma_{i,a}=-1
) to the regulator binding region, i.e. that
s_{i}=f_{i}(\sigma_{i,1},\ldots,\sigma_{i,t_{i}})
is a boolean function of the
\sigma_{i,a}
’s. If the relation between
\bm{s}
and
\boldmath{\sigma}
is a GT, then our approach ensures that
inference of the gene regulatory network based on a dataset
\bm{\hat{s}}
of gene expression should give the same results as inference based on a dataset
\bm{\hat{\sigma}}
of binding on regulatory regions.
. This is not true when model selection is restricted to a family of models that is not invariant under GTs, such as the family of pairwise models. In this case, it may be hard to say whether the structure of the inferred model reflects the statistical dependencies of the data or the constraints imposed by
the considered family of models.

Our approach departs from the literature on graphical model reconstruction
Montanari and Pereira 2009
;
Ravikumar et al. 2010
;
Barton et al. 2016
;
Vuffray et al. 2016
in important ways.
Graphical model reconstruction aims at retrieving the model from which a given data could have been generated,
by identifying which interactions among a pre-assigned set (e.g. pairwise) are present.
In this work, we don’t make any assumptions on the interactions present in the models,
as our approach takes into account all possible (low- and high-order) correlation patterns in the data when searching for the best model.
Even more importantly, we are not interested in reconstructing and fitting
a specific set of interactions from which the data could have been generated.
Instead, we aim at finding the most likely
simple
model that can inform us on true structure of dependencies in the data.
Another critical aspect in graphical model reconstruction is parameter estimation, for which different approximate algorithms have been proposed
Nguyen et al. 2017
.
We show that there is no need to infer model parameters in order to perform model selection among MCMs, which is a huge computational advantage.
Moreover, MCMs can be directly compared on the basis of their evidence (marginal likelihood),
which allows to balance goodness-of-fit and model complexity.
In contrast, the complexity of graphical models is
difficult to compute,
which calls for adopting regularization methods
Ravikumar et al. 2010
;
Nguyen et al. 2017
that don’t always have a clear information-theoretic foundation.
Finally, the number of MCMs that can be compared with BMS is much larger than the number of possible pairwise models
(see Fig.
2
), covering more widely the space of all statistical structures of data.

As mentioned previously, models of the same class can be seen as different representations of the same abstract model.
In our approach to BMS of MCMs, we aim to find the best model for the data independently of its representation; in other words, we aim to find the best abstract model.
This approach to model selection that is independent of the representation of the data
may pave the way toward a better understanding of the concept of abstraction.
Put differently, our results show that the constraint on model simplicity
i)
defines
an extremely vast landscape
of possible data structures that
ii)
is endowed with a rich relational organization and
iii)
is easy to navigate by Bayesian model selection algorithms. These remarkable facts make MCMs a good candidate for the “more sophisticated forms of knowledge representation” that Tenenbaum
et al.

Tenenbaum et al. 2011
argue “must underlie the probabilistic generative models needed for Bayesian cognition”.

In summary, this paper offers a novel perspective on statistical inference of high-dimensional data by focusing on simple representations of data.
The computational efficiency of performing BMS among MCMs, their ability to uncover robust structures of dependencies in the data, and their interpretability in terms of groups of highly correlated variables make MCMs a very good tool for fast and interpretable data analysis.
MCMs open up new ways of tackling challenging statistical modeling problems, such as modeling very large systems or identifying significant high-order patterns in data.
Further extensions beyond binary variables, as well as the development of
efficient optimization algorithms
to find the MCM with maximal evidence for large systems, are
promising avenues of research.

Appendix A
Spin operators and spin models

A
spin model
is a probabilistic model that describes the state of a system of binary variables, called
spins
.
The model assumes the existence of interactions between the spins that constrain the states accessible to the system. As no spatial organization is assumed,
interactions can be of arbitrary range and arbitrary order (i.e., between two, three, or more spins).
Spin models can be seen as a generalization of the Ising model to include long-range and high-order interactions.

Spin operators.

To mathematically define a spin model, each interaction in the model is associated with a
spin operator
.
Consider a system of
n
spin variables,
\bm{s}=(s_{1},\,\cdots,s_{n})
, that take random binary values
s_{i}=\pm 1
. A
spin operator

\phi^{\bm{\mu}}(\bm{s})
associated with an interaction labeled by
\bm{\mu}
is defined as the product of the spin
s_{i}
involved in the interaction:

\phi^{\bm{\mu}}(\bm{s})=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\,s_{i}^{\,\mu_{i}}\,.

(10)

Here,
\bm{\mu}
is an
n
-dimensional binary vector encoding which spin is involved in the interaction, i.e., its
i
-th element is
\mu_{i}=1
if spin
s_{i}
is included in the interaction and
\mu_{i}=0
otherwise. Thus the spin
s_{i}
remaining in the product of Eq. (
10
) are the ones for which
\bm{\mu}_{i}=1
.
For example, in the model in the bottom left corner of Fig.
7
,
the 3-body interactions between the variables
s_{4}
,
s_{5}
and
s_{6}
is encoded by the binary vector
\bm{\mu}=(0,0,0,1,1,1)
and associated with the spin operator
\phi^{\bm{\mu}}(\bm{s})=s_{4}s_{5}s_{6}
; the pairwise interaction between
s_{2}
and
s_{3}
is encoded by
\bm{\mu}=(0,1,1,0,0,0)
and associated with
\phi^{\bm{\mu}}(\bm{s})=s_{2}s_{3}
; and the field interaction on
s_{2}
(represented by a black dot) is encoded by
\bm{\mu}=(0,1,0,0,0,0)
and associated with
\phi^{\bm{\mu}}(\bm{s})=s_{2}
.
The number of possible spin operators is equal to the number of non-null binary vector
\bm{\mu}
, which is
2^{n}-1
in an
n
-spin system.

Properties of spin operators.

With the addition of the identity operator,
\phi^{\bm{0}}(\bm{s})=1
for all
\bm{s}
, the set of all spin operators, denoted
\Omega_{n}=\{\phi^{\bm{\mu}}(\bm{s})\,|\,\bm{\mu}\in\{0,1\}^{n}\}
, with the product operation forms a commutative
group. Indeed, the square of any spin variable
s_{i}
, and therefore of any operator, is equal to the identity
\phi^{\bm{0}}(\bm{s})=1
. Besides, the product
\phi^{\bm{\mu}}(\bm{s})\phi^{\bm{\nu}}(\bm{s})=\phi^{\bm{\mu}\oplus\bm{\nu}}(\bm{s})
of any two spin operators is also a spin operator, where
\oplus
denotes the component-wise XOR Boolean operator (i.e., sum modulo 2) applied to the two binary vectors
\bm{\mu}
and
\bm{\nu}
.

The set of operators also forms a complete and orthogonal basis for all functions of
n
binary variables, as

\displaystyle\sum_{\bm{s}\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}}\phi^{\bm{\mu}}(\bm{s})\,\phi^{\bm{\nu}}(\bm{s})=2^{n}\,\delta_{\bm{\mu},\bm{\nu}}

(11)

\displaystyle{\rm and}\;\;\sum_{\bm{\mu}=0}^{2^{n}-1}\phi^{\bm{\mu}}(\bm{s})\,\phi^{\bm{\mu}}(\bm{s}^{\prime})=2^{n}\,\delta_{\bm{s},\bm{s}^{\prime}}.

(12)

Hence any function
F(\bm{s})=\sum_{\bm{\mu}\geq 0}f_{\bm{\mu}}\phi^{\bm{\mu}}(\bm{s})
defined on the spin configurations can be represented as a linear combination of spin operators, with coefficients given by
f_{\bm{\mu}}=2^{-n}\sum_{\bm{s}}\phi^{\bm{\mu}}(\bm{s})F(\bm{s})
. These completeness relations hold also on the sub-space defined by a subset of the spins.

Spin models.

A
spin model
is a maximum entropy model, whose Hamiltonian
is a linear combination of the elements of a set
\mathcal{M}
of spin operators,
\mathcal{M}\subseteq\Omega_{n}\backslash\{\phi^{\bm{0}}\}
.
The probability distribution over the spin variables
\bm{s}
under a spin model
\mathcal{M}
is therefore:

\displaystyle P(\bm{s}\,|\,\bm{g},\,\mathcal{M})=\frac{1}{Z_{\mathcal{M}}(\bm{g})}\,\exp\bigg(\sum_{\bm{\mu}\in\mathcal{M}}g_{\bm{\mu}}\,\phi^{\bm{\mu}}(\bm{s})\bigg)

\displaystyle\qquad{\rm with}\;\;Z_{\mathcal{M}}(\bm{g})=\sum_{\bm{s}}\,\exp\left({\sum_{\bm{\mu}\in\mathcal{M}}g_{\bm{\mu}}\,\phi^{\bm{\mu}}(\bm{s})}\right)\,,

(13)

where
\bm{g}=\{g_{\bm{\mu}},\,\bm{\mu}\in\mathcal{M}\}
is a vector of real parameters and where the partition function
Z_{\mathcal{M}}(\bm{g})
ensures normalization.
Each parameter
g_{\bm{\mu}}
modulates the strength of the interaction associated with the operator
\phi^{\bm{\mu}}(\bm{s})
. Each spin model thus defines a parametric family of probability distributions.
In an
n
-spin system, there are
2^{n}-1
different spin operators, hence there are
2^{2^{n}-1}
different spin models that can be constructed. Each model is identified by the set
\mathcal{M}
of spin operators that it contains.
For example, the second model of the first column in Fig.
7
has
7
interactions (three 1-body, three 2-body, and one 3-body) which are identified by the set of operators
\mathcal{M}=\{s_{4},s_{5},s_{6},s_{4}s_{5},s_{4}s_{6},s_{5}s_{6},s_{4}s_{5}s_{6}\}
and which defines the spin models whose Hamiltonian is given by:

\displaystyle g_{\cdots 100}\,s_{4}+g_{\cdots 010}\,s_{5}+g_{\cdots 010}\,s_{6}+g_{\cdots 110}\,s_{4}s_{5}+

\displaystyle g_{\cdots 101}\,s_{4}s_{6}+g_{\cdots 011}\,s_{5}s_{6}+g_{\cdots 111}\,s_{4}s_{5}s_{6}\;.

(14)

Here we only wrote the term in the exponential of Eq. (
A
)
and for clarity, we replace the three first spins (
s_{1}
to
s_{3}
) with dots in the labels of the parameters.

Appendix B
Gauge transformations

Set of independent operators.

A set of operators is said
independent
if none of the operators of the set can be obtained as a product of other operators of the set.
The sets
\{s_{1},\,s_{2},\,s_{3}\}
and
\{s_{1},\,s_{1}s_{2},\,s_{1}s_{2}s_{3}\}
are two examples, whereas
\{s_{1},\,s_{2},\,s_{1}s_{2}\}
is not an independent set (as each operator can be obtained as a product of the two others). We recall that for a spin variables
s_{i}=\pm 1

and therefore
(s_{i})^{2}=1
always.

In an
n
-spin system, the set of the
n
spin variables

\{s_{1},\ldots,s_{n}\}
is a set of independent operators.
Besides all the operators of the system can be generated as products of these
n
basis spin operators.
As a result, in an
n
-spin system, a set of independent operators can have at maximum
n
elements. The completeness relations (
11
) and (
12
) also hold on the sub-space defined by a set of independent operators.

Gauge transformations.

In an
n
-spin system
\bm{s}=(s_{1},\,\cdots,\,s_{n})
, consider the set of
n
independent operators
\bm{b}(\bm{s})=(\phi^{\bm{\mu}_{1}}(\bm{s}),\,\cdots,\,\phi^{\bm{\mu}_{n}}(\bm{s}))
. The change of variables
\bm{s}^{\prime}=\bm{b}(\bm{s})
, in which each new variable
s_{i}^{\prime}
takes the binary value
s_{i}^{\prime}=\phi^{\bm{\mu}_{i}}(\bm{s})
,
defines a bijective map from the space of states (of the system) to itself.
Ref.
Beretta et al. 2018
calls these transformations
gauge transformations
(GTs) and identifies them as being the automorphisms of the group
({\mathbb{Z}_{2}}^{n},\cdot)
(see Supplementary Materials of
Beretta et al. 2018
). Permutations of the spins are special examples of GT. The transformations
\mathcal{T}_{1}
and
\mathcal{T}_{2}
in Fig.
7
are two other examples.
As the transformation
\bm{s}^{\prime}=\bm{b}(\bm{s})
is a bijection of the space of states, one can also define the inverse transformation:
\bm{s}=\bm{b}^{-1}(\bm{s}^{\prime})
. For example, Fig.
7
gives the inverse of the transformations
\mathcal{T}_{1}
and
\mathcal{T}_{2}
.
In the following, we will denote
\mathcal{T}_{\bm{b}}
the GT defined by the set
\bm{b}(\bm{s})
of
n
independent operators (i.e.
\mathcal{T}_{\bm{b}}:\;\bm{s}\rightarrow\bm{s}^{\prime}=\bm{b}(\bm{s})
) and
\mathcal{T}_{\bm{b}}^{-1}
its inverse transformation.
Appendix.
F
gives information on how to use linear algebra to mathematically define GTs and obtain their inverse.

More generally, GTs can be thought of as a change of basis from the original spin representation to a new one. Under a GT, an operator is transformed into another operator and a spin model into another spin model.

Transformed operator.

Any spin operator
\phi^{\bm{\mu}}(\bm{s})
can be transformed under a gauge transformation
\mathcal{T}_{\bm{b}}

by simply re-expressing
\phi^{\bm{\mu}}(\bm{s})
in terms of the new basis variables
\bm{s}^{\prime}=\bm{b}(\bm{s})
. This can be done using the inverse gauge transformation
\bm{s}=\bm{b}^{-1}(\bm{s}^{\prime})
, which yields the transformed operators:

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{\bm{b}}[\,\phi^{\bm{\mu}}\,](\bm{s}^{\prime})\doteq\phi^{\bm{\mu}}(\bm{b}^{-1}(\bm{s}^{\prime}))\,.

(15)

One can easily check that the resulting expression for
\mathcal{T}_{\bm{b}}[\,\phi^{\bm{\mu}}\,](\bm{s}^{\prime})
is still a spin operator but now expressed as a product of the transformed variables
s_{i}^{\prime}
. This means that there exists a binary vector
\bm{\mu}^{\prime}
such that:

\displaystyle\phi^{\bm{\mu}^{\prime}}(\bm{s}^{\prime})=\mathcal{T}_{\bm{b}}[\,\phi^{\bm{\mu}}\,](\bm{s}^{\prime})=\phi^{\bm{\mu}}(\bm{b}^{-1}(\bm{s}^{\prime}))\,.

(16)

Appendix
F
gives the mathematical relation between
\bm{\mu}^{\prime}
,
\bm{\mu}
, and
\bm{b}(\bm{s})
(see Eq. (
37
)).

Transformed model.

The gauge transformation
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
of a model
\mathcal{M}
is obtained by transforming each operator of
\mathcal{M}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{\prime}=\mathcal{T}_{\bm{b}}[\mathcal{M}]\doteq\{\mathcal{T}_{\bm{b}}[\phi^{\bm{\mu}}]\;|\;\phi^{\bm{\mu}}\in\mathcal{M}\}\,.

(17)

Because
\mathcal{T}_{\bm{b}}
is a bijection of the space of states, the probability of finding the system in the state
\bm{s}
in model
\mathcal{M}
is the same as the probability of finding the system in the transformed state
\bm{s}^{\prime}=\bm{b}(\bm{s})
in the transformed model
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}=\mathcal{T}_{\bm{b}}[\mathcal{M}]
:

\displaystyle p(\bm{s}\,|\,\bm{g},\mathcal{M})

\displaystyle=p(\bm{s}^{\prime}\,|\,\bm{g}^{\prime},\mathcal{M}^{\prime})\,,

(18)

\displaystyle=p\left(\bm{b}(\bm{s})\,|\,\mathcal{T}_{\bm{b}}[\bm{g}],\mathcal{T}_{\bm{b}}[\mathcal{M}]\right)\,,

(19)

where the vector of parameters
\bm{g}^{\prime}=\mathcal{T}_{\bm{b}}[\bm{g}]
is a permutation of the parameters
\bm{g}
of
\mathcal{M}
that is such that
the transformed operator
\phi^{\bm{\mu}^{\prime}}=\mathcal{T}_{\bm{b}}[\phi^{\bm{\mu}}]
in
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
is parameterized by the same parameter as the operator
\phi^{\bm{\mu}}
in
\mathcal{M}
.
Fig.
7
shows examples of GTs of spin models.

Appendix C
Complexity of MCMs

Assuming Jeffreys’ prior over the model parameters, the
geometric complexity
of spin models in Eq. (
3
) is:

\displaystyle c_{\mathcal{M}}=\log\int d\bm{g}\sqrt{\det\bm{I}_{\mathcal{M}}(\bm{g})},

(20)

where
\bm{I}_{\mathcal{M}}(\bm{g})
is the Fisher information matrix (FIM):

[\bm{I}_{\mathcal{M}}(\bm{g})]_{\mu,\nu}=\partial_{g_{\mu}}\partial_{g_{\nu}}\left[\log Z_{\mathcal{M}}(\bm{g})\right]\,.

(21)

Ref.
Beretta et al. 2018
shows that gauge transformations (GTs) leave the partition function
Z_{\mathcal{M}}(\bm{g})
of a spin model invariant up to permutations of its parameters.
As a consequence, the FIM in Eq. (
21
) and the geometric complexity
c_{\mathcal{M}}
of spin models are both invariant under GTs.
This allows the classification of all spin models into equivalence classes characterized by the same complexity
c_{\mathcal{M}}
.
Fig.
8
, adapted from Ref.
Beretta et al. 2018
, shows the complexity of all the classes of spin models on
n=4
variables.

The geometric complexity of spin models is generally difficult to compute due to the high-dimensional integral in Eq. (
20
). However, one can get a closed-form expression for MCMs.
As a consequence of the factorization property of MCMs in Eq. (
7
), the FIM of an MCM
\mathcal{M}=\cup_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\mathcal{M}_{a}
is a block diagonal matrix, in which each block
\bm{I}_{\mathcal{M}_{a}}(\bm{g}_{a})

is the FIM of the ICC
\mathcal{M}_{a}
reduced to the modeled
r_{a}
spin variables. As a result, the determinant of the FIM for an MCM factorizes over the FIM of its ICCs,
and the complexity of an MCM is then the sum of the complexity of its ICCs:

\displaystyle c_{\mathcal{M}}=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\,c_{\mathcal{M}_{a}}\,,

(22)

\displaystyle{\rm where}\;\;c_{\mathcal{M}_{a}}=\log\int d\bm{g}_{a}\sqrt{\det\bm{I}_{\mathcal{M}_{a}}(\bm{g}_{a})}.

Finally, the complexity of ICCs was computed in Ref.
Beretta et al. 2018
(as the complexity of Sub-Complete Models):

\displaystyle c_{\mathcal{M}_{a}}\,=\,2^{r_{a}-1}\log\pi-\log\Gamma(2^{r_{a}-1})\,,

(23)

where
\Gamma
is the gamma function.
In Fig.
8
, we indicated the classes of MCMs with a circle symbol, and one can see that, for
n=4
or smaller, MCMs
have the lowest complexity among all models with the same rank and the same number of parameters. This remains to be proven for larger values of
n
.

For the special cases of Independent Models (IM) and Sub-Complete models (which are MCMs with a single ICC), Eq. (
22
) gives back the complexity values obtained by Ref.
Beretta et al. 2018
, i.e., respectively,
c_{\mathcal{M}}=K\log\pi
indicated by a solid line in Fig.
8
, and the complexity in Eq. (
23
) indicated by a dashed line.

Appendix D
Factorization of the probability distribution of an MCM

Consider an MCM
\mathcal{M}=\cup_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\mathcal{M}_{a}
and one of its preferred bases
\bm{b}(\bm{s})
.
By definition of a
preferred basis
,
\bm{b}(\bm{s})

can be written as
\bm{b}(\bm{s})=\cup_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\bm{b}_{a}(\bm{s})
, where each
\bm{b}_{a}(\bm{s})
is a basis of the ICC
\mathcal{M}_{a}
.
Expressing
\mathcal{M}
in the basis
\bm{b}(\bm{s})
gives the transformed MCM
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}=\mathcal{T}_{\bm{b}}[\mathcal{M}]=\cup_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\mathcal{M}_{a}^{\prime}
, in which the ICCs
\mathcal{M}_{a}^{\prime}=\mathcal{T}_{\bm{b}_{a}}[\mathcal{M}_{a}]

are now based on distinct basis variables
\bm{b}_{a}
.
Thus, the probability distribution of
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
(reduced to the sub-space defined by
\bm{b}(\bm{s})
) is factorizable over its ICCs:

\displaystyle p_{r}(\bm{b}(\bm{s})\,|\,\bm{g}^{\prime},\,\mathcal{M}^{\prime})=\prod_{a\in\mathcal{A}}p_{a}(\bm{b}_{a}(\bm{s})\,|\,\bm{g}_{a}^{\prime},\,\mathcal{M}_{a}^{\prime})\,,

(24)

where
\mathcal{M}_{a}^{\prime}=\mathcal{T}_{\bm{b}_{a}}[\mathcal{M}_{a}]

and where the parameters
\bm{g}_{a}^{\prime}
of
\mathcal{M}_{a}^{\prime}
are the permutations of the parameters
\bm{g}_{a}
of
\mathcal{M}_{a}
given by (
38
). Here, each
p_{a}
is a probability distribution over
r_{a}=|\bm{b}_{a}|
binary variables, and the model distribution
p_{r}
is over
r=\sum_{a}r_{a}=|\bm{b}|
variables.

Note that the rank
r
of an MCM can be smaller than
n
, in which case
\bm{b}(\bm{s})
is not a basis of the whole
n
-dimensional spin system,
but only of the
r
-dimensional sub-system modeled by
\mathcal{M}
.
The basis
\bm{b}(\bm{s})
thus defines a gauge transformation for this sub-system (as used above), but not for the whole
n
-spin system.
Completing
\bm{b}(\bm{s})
with
(n-r)
independent operators to form a basis
\bm{B}(\bm{s})
of the whole
n
-spin system, we can define a gauge transformation of the
n
-spin system that will transform
\mathcal{M}
into the same model
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
, i.e. such that
\mathcal{T}_{\bm{B}}[\mathcal{M}]=\mathcal{T}_{\bm{b}}[\mathcal{M}]=\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
.
Using (
18
) for the probability distribution of gauge transformed models, we can then write that:

\displaystyle p(\bm{s}\,|\,\bm{g},\,\mathcal{M})=\frac{1}{2^{(n-r)}}\,p_{r}(\bm{b}(\bm{s})\,|\,\bm{g}^{\prime},\,\mathcal{M}^{\prime})\,.

(25)

Combining this with (
24
) finally leads to the factorization in Eq. (
7
).

Appendix E
Maximum Likelihood and Evidence of MCMs

Change of parametrization.

Let us consider an ICC
\mathcal{M}_{a}
with rank
r_{a}
and a basis
\bm{b}_{a}(\bm{s})=(\phi_{1}(\bm{s}),\ldots,\phi_{r_{a}}(\bm{s}))
of
\mathcal{M}_{a}
.
The probability distribution of the states of the system under
\mathcal{M}_{a}
can be re-written in the basis of operators
\bm{b}_{a}
as:

p(\bm{s}\,|\,\bm{g},\mathcal{M}_{a})=\frac{1}{2^{(n-r_{a})}}\,q\left(\bm{b}_{a}(\bm{s})\right),

(26)

where
q(\bm{b}_{a})=p_{a}(\bm{b}_{a}\,|\,\bm{g}_{a},\,\mathcal{M}_{a})
is a normalized probability distribution over the states of
\bm{b}_{a}\in\{\pm 1\}^{r_{a}}
. For an ICC
\mathcal{M}_{a}
, there is a bijective map between the
2^{r_{a}}-1
parameters
\bm{g}_{a}
of
\mathcal{M}_{a}
and the
2^{r_{a}}-1
state probabilities
q(\bm{b}_{a})
(the last probability being fixed by the normalization).
More precisely, the value of the parameters
g_{\bm{\mu}}
can be obtained from the states probabilities
q(\bm{b}_{a})
by taking the logarithm of (
26
), multiplying by
\phi^{\bm{\mu}}(\bm{s})
and summing over all the states of
\bm{s}
. Finally using the orthogonality relation between operators (
11
),
one finds that for all interactions
\bm{\mu}\in\mathcal{M}_{a}
:

\displaystyle g_{\bm{\mu}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\,\sum_{\bm{s}}\phi^{\bm{\mu}}(\bm{s})\,\log q\left(\bm{b}_{a}(\bm{s})\right)\,.

(27)

Note that for
\bm{\mu}\not\in\mathcal{M}_{a}
this equation yields
g_{\bm{\mu}}=0
, because the function
q\left(\bm{b}_{a}(\bm{s})\right)
is expressed solely in terms of the operators
\bm{\mu}\in\mathcal{M}_{a}
.
In general, the statistical inference of ICCs (and therefore of MCMs) is more easily done in the
q
-representation, i.e. in terms of the parameters
q(\bm{b}_{a})
. The result can then be re-written in the original spin model representation using (
27
).

Maximum likelihood parameters.

Using (
26
), the likelihood function of an ICC
\mathcal{M}_{a}
for a dataset
\bm{\hat{s}}
can be written as:

P(\bm{\hat{s}}\,|\,\bm{g},\mathcal{M}_{a})=\frac{1}{2^{N(n-r_{a})}}\,\prod_{\bm{b}_{a}}\,q(\bm{b}_{a})^{k_{\bm{b}_{a}}}\,,

(28)

where the product is over all possible
2^{r_{a}}
values of
\bm{b}_{a}
, and where

k_{\bm{b}_{a}}
is the number of times the basis operators take the value
{\bm{b}_{a}}
over the entire dataset. Thanks to the bijection between the
2^{r_{a}}-1
parameters
\bm{g}
and the
2^{r_{a}}-1
parameters
q(\bm{b}_{a})
, the maximum likelihood can be obtained by maximizing (
28
) directly over the parameters
q(\bm{b}_{a})
.
This leads to the maximum likelihood estimate of
q
:

\hat{q}({\bm{b}_{a}})=\frac{k_{\bm{b}_{a}}}{N}.

(29)

Replacing these values in the likelihood function (
28
) and combining with the factorization over ICCs in (
7
) leads to the value of the maximum likelihood of an MCM:

P(\bm{\hat{s}}\,|\,\bm{\hat{g}},\,\mathcal{M})=\frac{1}{2^{N(n-r)}}\prod_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\left[\prod_{\bm{b}_{a}}\,\left(\frac{k_{\bm{b}_{a}}}{N}\right)^{\vskip-11.38109ptk_{\bm{b}_{a}}}\right]\,.

(30)

Finally, Eq. (
9
) for the probability distribution of an MCM at the maximum-likelihood estimate is obtained by replacing the values of the maximum likelihood parameters (
29
) in the factorization of (
7
) using that
p_{a}(\bm{b}_{a}\,|\,\bm{\hat{g}}_{a}^{\prime},\,\mathcal{M}_{a}^{\prime})=\hat{q}(\bm{b}_{a})
.

Model evidence.

The calculation of the evidence of an ICC is likewise straightforward. We first exploit the invariance of Jeffreys prior under reparametrization
Jeffreys 1946
, which takes the following form in the
q
-representation:

P_{0}(\bm{q}\,|\,\mathcal{M}_{a})=\frac{\Gamma(2^{r_{a}-1})}{\pi^{2^{r_{a}-1}}}\prod_{\bm{b}_{a}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{q(\bm{b}_{a})}}\;\delta\Big(\sum_{\bm{b}_{a}}q(\bm{b}_{a})-1\Big),

(31)

where we denote by
\bm{q}
the vector of
2^{r_{a}}
probabilities
q(\bm{b}_{a})
and
\Gamma
is the gamma function. One can recognize the symmetric Dirichlet distribution with hyperparameter
\alpha=1/2
.
Combined with the expression of the likelihood in (
28
), this yields the evidence of ICCs:

\displaystyle P(\bm{\hat{s}}\,|\,\mathcal{M}_{a})

\displaystyle=\int\!d\bm{g}\;P(\bm{\hat{s}}\,|\,\bm{g},\,\mathcal{M}_{a})\,P_{0}(\bm{g}\,|\,\mathcal{M}_{a})

(32)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{N(n-r_{a})}}\frac{\Gamma(2^{r_{a}-1})}{\pi^{2^{r_{a}-1}}}\times

(33)

\displaystyle\qquad\int_{0}^{1}\hskip-1.42262pt\prod_{\bm{b}_{a}}\left[q(\bm{b}_{a})^{k_{\bm{b}_{a}}-\frac{1}{2}}\right]\delta\Big(\sum_{\bm{b}_{a}}q(\bm{b}_{a})-1\Big)\,d\bm{q}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{N(n-r_{a})}}\,\frac{\Gamma(2^{r_{a}-1})}{\pi^{2^{r_{a}-1}}}\;\frac{\prod_{\bm{b}_{a}}\Gamma(k_{\bm{b}_{a}}+\frac{1}{2})}{\Gamma(N+2^{r_{a}-1})}\,,

where one can recognize the multivariate beta function in the integral of (
33
).
This calculation easily generalizes to MCMs, in which the components
\mathcal{M}_{a}
are associated with independent bases
\bm{b}_{a}
.
Indeed, using (
7
), both the likelihood and the prior distribution
P_{0}

factorize over the ICCs, which implies that the evidence in Eq. (
2
) is the product of the evidence of each ICC (reduced to their respective basis
\bm{b}_{a}
).
This finally leads to the expression of the evidence of an MCM in Eq. (
8
) (we recall that
\Gamma(\frac{1}{2})=\sqrt{\pi}
).

Appendix F
Binary linear algebra formalism for spin models and gauge transformations

The following section gives more insight into the binary linear algebra formalism used in our codes.
This framework also provides a better mathematical description of spin models and gauge transformations.
Consider an
n
-spin system
\bm{s}=(s_{1},\,\cdots,\,s_{n})
and the gauge transformation
\mathcal{T}_{\bm{b}}:\;\bm{s}\rightarrow\bm{s}^{\prime}=\bm{b}(\bm{s})
,
defined by the set of
n
independent operators
\bm{b}(\bm{s})=(\phi^{\bm{\mu}_{1}}(\bm{s}),\,\cdots,\,\phi^{\bm{\mu}_{n}}(\bm{s}))
. As it is a bijection of the space of states, one can define the inverse transformation:
\bm{s}=\bm{b}^{-1}(\bm{s}^{\prime})
.

Matrix representation of gauge transformations.

We recall that the label
\bm{\mu}
indexing an operator
\phi^{\bm{\mu}}

is a binary vector encoding which spins contribute to the operator, such that

\phi^{\bm{\mu}}(\bm{s})=\prod_{i=1}^{n}s_{i}^{\mu_{i}}
,
where
\mu_{i}\in\{0,1\}
is the
i
-th element of
\bm{\mu}
.
Similarly, we can write a gauge transformation
\mathcal{T}_{\bm{b}}
under the form of an
(n\times n)
-binary matrix:

\displaystyle\bm{T}_{\bm{b}}=(\bm{\mu}_{1},\,\cdots,\,\bm{\mu}_{n})\,,

(34)

where the
n
binary vectors
\bm{\mu}_{i}
defining the gauge transformation are now the columns of
\bm{T}_{\bm{b}}
.
The inverse gauge transformation
\bm{b}^{-1}
can then be obtained by
inverting the binary matrix
\bm{T}_{\bm{b}}
over

\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
, i.e.
\bm{T}_{\bm{b}^{-1}}=\bm{T}_{\bm{b}}^{-1}

(when taking the matrix product over
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
, the elements of the resulting matrix are taken modulo
2
).
Note that the fact that the set of operators
\{\phi^{\bm{\mu}_{1}}(\bm{s}),\,\cdots,\,\phi^{\bm{\mu}_{n}}(\bm{s})\}
is independent translates into the fact that the set of vectors
\{\bm{\mu}_{1},\,\cdots,\,\bm{\mu}_{n}\}
is linearly independent in
(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})^{n}
, and therefore that the rank of the matrix
\bm{T}_{\bm{b}}
is
n
and the matrix is invertable.

Binary representation of states and Transformed states.

Under the GT
\mathcal{T}_{\bm{b}}
, a spin configuration
\bm{s}
of the system is transformed into the new configuration
\bm{s}^{\prime}=\bm{b}(\bm{s})
. Introducing the binary variables
\alpha_{i}
such that
s_{i}=(-1)^{\alpha_{i}}
for all spins,
the state
\bm{s}
of the system is equivalently
represented by the binary vector
\bm{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\,\cdots,\,\alpha_{n})\in(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})^{n}
.
Similarly, the transformed state
\bm{s}^{\prime}=\bm{b}(\bm{s})
is represented by the binary vector
\bm{\alpha}^{\prime}
, whose elements
\alpha_{i}^{\prime}
are then given by the scalar product
\alpha_{i}^{\prime}=\bm{\alpha}\cdot\bm{\mu}_{i}
.
Thus, using the matrix representation
\bm{T}_{\bm{b}}
of gauge transformations in (
34
), the new state of the spin system
is given by the vector-matrix multiplication over
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
:

\displaystyle\bm{\alpha}^{\prime}=\bm{\alpha}\;\bm{T}_{\bm{b}}\,,

(35)

where
\bm{\alpha}
and
\bm{\alpha}^{\prime}
are row vectors.
Similarly, a state in the original basis can be recovered from the state of the system in the new basis using:

\displaystyle\bm{\alpha}=\bm{\alpha}^{\prime}\;\bm{T}_{\bm{b}}^{-1}\,.

(36)

Transformed operator.

Any spin operator
\phi^{\bm{\mu}}(\bm{s})
can be transformed under a GT
\mathcal{T}_{\bm{b}}

by re-expressing
\phi^{\bm{\mu}}(\bm{s})
in terms of the new basis variables
\bm{s}^{\prime}=\bm{b}(\bm{s})
, as in Eq. (
15
). The transformed operator is then given by:

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{\bm{b}}[\,\phi^{\bm{\mu}}\,]=\phi^{\bm{\mu}^{\prime}}\,,\;\;\;{\rm where}\;\;\;\;\bm{\mu}^{\prime}=\bm{T}_{\bm{b}}^{-1}\,\bm{\mu}

(37)

is a matrix-vector multiplication in
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
, and
\bm{\mu}
and
\bm{\mu}^{\prime}
are two column vectors.

Transformed model.

The gauge transformation
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
of a model
\mathcal{M}
is obtained by transforming each operator of
\mathcal{M}
, see Eq. (
17
). The probability of finding the system
in the transformed state
\bm{s}^{\prime}=\bm{b}(\bm{s})
in the transformed model
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}=\mathcal{T}_{\bm{b}}[\mathcal{M}]
is given by Eq.
18
, where the vector of parameters
\bm{g}^{\prime}=\mathcal{T}_{\bm{b}}[\bm{g}]
is a permutation of the parameters
\bm{g}
of
\mathcal{M}
such that the transformed operator
\phi^{\bm{\mu}^{\prime}}
in
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
is parameterized by the same parameter as the operator
\phi^{\bm{\mu}}
in
\mathcal{M}
, i.e. (based on Eq. (
37
)):

\displaystyle g_{\bm{\mu}^{\prime}}=g_{\bm{\mu}}\;\;\;\;{\rm for}\;\;\bm{\mu}^{\prime}=\bm{T}_{\bm{b}}^{-1}\bm{\mu}\,.

(38)

Example.

For
n=3
, take the gauge transformation
\bm{s}^{\prime}=\bm{b}(\bm{s})=(s_{1}s_{2},\,s_{2}s_{3},\,s_{3})
. The inverse gauge transformation is
\bm{s}=\bm{b}^{-1}(\bm{s}^{\prime})=(s_{1}^{\prime}s_{2}^{\prime}s_{3}^{\prime},\,s_{2}^{\prime}s_{3}^{\prime},\,s_{3}^{\prime})
. These two transformations can be written under the matrix forms:

\displaystyle\bm{T}_{\bm{b}}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
1&1&0\\
0&1&1\end{pmatrix}\qquad{\rm and}\qquad\bm{T}_{\bm{b}^{-1}}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
1&1&0\\
1&1&1\end{pmatrix}

(39)

One can easily check that
\bm{T}_{\bm{b}}\;\bm{T}_{\bm{b}^{-1}}=\mathbb{I}_{3}
.

Take the state
\bm{s}=(-1,\,-1,\,+1)
, which can also be defined as
\bm{\alpha}=(1,\,1,\,0)
using that
s_{i}=(-1)^{\alpha_{i}}
.
In the new basis
\bm{b}(\bm{s})
defined above, this state takes the new value
\bm{s}^{\prime}=(+1,\,-1,\,+1)
, which can also be written as
\bm{\alpha}^{\prime}=(0,\,1,\,0)
. One can check that the same value of
\bm{\alpha}^{\prime}
can be obtained using
\bm{\alpha}^{\prime}=\bm{\alpha}\;T_{\bm{b}}
, as in (
35
).

Take the operator
\phi^{\bm{\mu}}(\bm{s})=s_{1}s_{3}
, which is identified by the binary vector
\bm{\mu}=(1,\,0,\,1)
. One can write the transformed operator
\mathcal{T}_{\bm{b}}[\phi^{\bm{\mu}}]
using the inverse transformation
\bm{s}=\bm{b}^{-1}(\bm{s}^{\prime})
. In particular, using that
s_{1}=s_{1}^{\prime}s_{2}^{\prime}s_{3}^{\prime}
and that
s_{3}=s_{3}^{\prime}
, one gets:

\displaystyle\phi^{\bm{\mu}^{\prime}}(\bm{s}^{\prime})=(s_{1}^{\prime}s_{2}^{\prime}s_{3}^{\prime})\times(s_{3}^{\prime})=s_{1}^{\prime}s_{2}^{\prime}\,,

(40)

which is identified by the binary vector
\bm{\mu}^{\prime}=(1,\,1,\,0)
. One can check that the same value of
\bm{\mu}^{\prime}
is obtained by using
\bm{\mu}^{\prime}=\bm{T}_{\bm{b}}^{-1}\,\bm{\mu}
as in (
37
).

Appendix G
Search for the best IM

An independent model (IM) is an MCM for which all the components
\mathcal{M}_{a}
only contain a single operator (i.e.,
r_{a}=1
for all
a\in\mathcal{A}
).
Let us focus on the case where the number of components
|\mathcal{A}|
is equal to the number of spins
n
.
All such IM have
n
independent operators,
\mathcal{M}=\{\phi_{1}(\bm{s}),\,\cdots,\,\phi_{n}(\bm{s})\}
.
Model selection among these models only requires comparing their likelihood, because they all have the exact same complexity (as they belong to the same complexity class
Beretta et al. 2018
).
The maximum log-likelihood of an independent model takes the simple form:

\displaystyle\log P(\bm{\hat{s}}\,|\,\bm{\hat{g}},\mathcal{M})=-N\sum_{a=1}^{n}H[\phi_{a}(\bm{s})]\,,

(41)

where
H[\phi_{a}(\bm{s})]
is the entropy of the binary operator
\phi_{a}(\bm{s})
in the dataset
\bm{\hat{s}}
:

\displaystyle H[\phi_{a}]=-p_{a}\log p_{a}-(1-p_{a})\log(1-p_{a})\,,

(42)

where
p_{a}=\mathbb{P}[\phi_{a}(\bm{s})=1]
is the empirical probability that
\phi_{a}(\bm{s})=1
in the dataset. We note that the entropy of an operator can equivalently be written in terms of the bias
m_{a}=p_{a}-(1-p_{a})

of the operator in the data:

\displaystyle H[\phi_{a}]=-\frac{1+m_{a}}{2}\log\left(\frac{1+m_{a}}{2}\right)-\frac{1-m_{a}}{2}\log\left(\frac{1-m_{a}}{2}\right)

\displaystyle{\rm where}\;\;m_{a}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\phi_{a}\left(\bm{s}^{(i)}\right)=\langle\phi_{a}(\bm{s})\rangle_{D}\,.

(43)

The entropy
H[\phi_{a}]
is maximal when
p_{a}=0.5
(or equivalently when the operator is least biased,
m_{a}=0
) and is minimal
when
p=0
or
p=1
(or equivalently, when the bias of the operator is maximal,
|m_{a}|=1
).
(
41
) thus implies that the most likely independent model is given by the most biased (or least entropic) set of
n
independent operators.

The exhaustive algorithm to find the most biased set of independent operators among a pool of possible operators is the following. First, compute the bias of all the operators and rank them from the most to the least biased. This procedure is of the order
O(\bar{N}K\log K)
, where
\bar{N}
is the number of different states observed in the dataset, and
K
is the total number of operators to compare (if one considers all possible operators, then
K=2^{n}-1
). This is the most computationally expensive part of this algorithm. Next, we must extract the most biased independent set. To do so, we create an
(n\times K)
-binary matrix in which each column contains the binary representation of one of the
K
operators (we recall that the vector
\bm{\mu}_{i}
is the binary representation of the operator
\phi^{\bm{\mu}_{i}}
).
Operators are placed by decreasing order of their bias, starting with the most biased operator in the first column of the matrix.
This approach is similar to the way we defined the binary matrix representation of a gauge transformation in Eq. (
34
),
except that now the columns are not necessarily independent from each other. To identify the most biased independent operators, we then use Gaussian elimination: the columns with leading coefficients will finally identify the most biased independent operators.

In the main text, we propose a heuristic iterative algorithm to find a good basis when the dimension of the system is too large. All the steps above are then still valid, the only difference is the initial pool of operators from which we want to identify the best basis.
In the case of the exhaustive search, this pool
contains all the
2^{n}-1
operators of the
n
-spin system; while in the heuristic algorithm, it contains only the operators up to a fixed order
k
(in a given representation).

When
K
is large, there are other ways to accelerate the algorithm slightly.
Ideally, one would like to reduce the number of operators to rank (as this is computationally demanding). This can be done by excluding operators whose bias is too low, for instance
by using a threshold on the
p
-value for the bias of the selected operators.
Note that this can lead to a set of independent operators that is smaller than
n
, meaning that some dimensions would then be considered irrelevant.
Another straightforward way of accelerating the algorithm when
K
is large is to reduce the size of the matrix to contain only a fixed number of operators (for instance
100
columns). One can then perform the Gaussian elimination several times, by keeping leading columns and adding new operators as needed,
until
n
independent operators are found.

Appendix H
Sampling from an MCM

Consider the MCM
\mathcal{M}=\cup_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\mathcal{M}_{a}
of an
n
spin system
\bm{s}

and a preferred basis
\bm{b}(\bm{s})=\cup_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\bm{b}_{a}(\bm{s})
of
\mathcal{M}
.
We denote
r=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}r_{a}
the rank of
\mathcal{M}
.
For a given dataset, the probability distribution of

\mathcal{M}
at best fit reads:

P(\bm{s}\,|\,\bm{\hat{g}},\mathcal{M})=\frac{1}{2^{n-r}}\prod_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\frac{k_{\bm{b}_{a}(\bm{s})}}{N},

(44)

where
k_{\bm{b}_{a}}/N=\hat{q}({\bm{b}_{a}})
are the empirical probabilities of observing the states
\bm{b}_{a}(\bm{s})
in the data.
We recall that each distribution
\hat{q}({\bm{b}_{a}})
is normalized over the
2^{r_{a}}
states of
\bm{b}_{a}
(see (
26
)). To sample a configuration
\bm{s}
from this model, we perform the following steps:

1)

For each ICC
\mathcal{M}_{a}
, we draw a random configuration of
\bm{b}_{a}
with probability
\hat{q}(\bm{b}_{a})=k_{\bm{b}_{a}}/N
.
This can be done either by sampling directly from the empirical distribution
\hat{q}(\bm{b}_{a})
, or by drawing uniformly a configuration
\bm{s}^{(i)}
from the data and then computing the transformation
\bm{b}_{a}(\bm{s}^{(i)})
. Eq. (
44
) ensures that this procedure samples a value of
\bm{b}_{a}

with the correct distribution.

2)

If
r<n
, then one must complete
\bm{b}(\bm{s})
with
(n-r)
independent operators,

\bar{\bm{b}}(\bm{s})=(\phi_{r+1}(\bm{s}),\,\cdots,\,\phi_{n}(\bm{s}))
, to form a basis of the whole
n
-dimensional space.
The resulting basis
\bm{B}(\bm{s})=\bm{b}(\bm{s})\cup\bar{\bm{b}}(\bm{s})
defines a gauge transformation
\mathcal{T}_{B}

between the original data states
\bm{s}
and the new states
\bm{s}^{\prime}=\bm{B}(\bm{s})
.
The states of the
(n-r)
spins not modeled by
\mathcal{M}
are then randomly sampled with probability
1/2
to be
+1
or
-1
,
which produces a sampled value of
\bar{\bm{b}}
.
Note that for all the applications discussed in this paper, we searched for a best basis
\bm{b}^{*}
of the whole
n
-spin system (and then for an MCM on this basis). Therefore, for all these cases,
r=n
and we didn’t need to introduce a complementary basis
\bar{\bm{b}}
.

3)

Next, we concatenate the sampled states
\bm{b}_{a}
and
\bar{\bm{b}}
to obtain a sampled value of
\bm{s}^{\prime}=(\cup_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\bm{b}_{a})\cup\bar{\bm{b}}
.

4)

Finally, we perform an inverse GT to obtain a sampled value of

\bm{s}=\bm{B}^{-1}(\bm{s}^{\prime})
.
As described in Sec.
F
,
this last step requires the inversion of an
n\times n
binary matrix modulo 2 (see (
36
)).
If one wants to sample multiple states from the same MCM, then this inversion only needs to be performed once.

Our implementation of this inversion (using Gaussian elimination) and of the sampling functions can be found in Ref.
de Mulatier and Kamphof 2024
.
In practice it is computationally very efficient to sample from an MCM, as it only requires drawing
|\mathcal{A}|
independent configurations from the dataset
and one binary vector-matrix multiplication.
Step 1) exploits the fact each ICC
\mathcal{M}_{a}
is complete on the variables
\bm{b}_{a}
, and therefore, computing
\bm{b}_{a}(\bm{s})
on a randomly drawn datapoint
\bm{s}
from the dataset
\bm{\hat{s}}
directly generates values of
\bm{b}_{a}
that are distributed according to (
44
).
Hence, a new state of
\bm{b}
can be generated in a straightforward manner from
|\mathcal{A}|
independent draws from the dataset.

Appendix I
Enumeration

Number of Independent Models.

All the IMs with
r
interactions can be obtained by gauge transformations of the IM
\mathcal{M}_{ind}=\{s_{1},\ldots,s_{r}\}
. Therefore, their number is equal to the number of GTs that transform this model into different ones and is given by:

\mathcal{N}_{ind}(n,r)=\frac{1}{r!}\prod_{i=0}^{r-1}\left(2^{n}-2^{i}\right).

(45)

This corresponds to the number of ways of choosing
r
independent operators in the set of all possible
2^{n}-1
operators, divided by the number of possible permutations of these
r
elements. The first operator can be chosen in
2^{n}-1
ways. Then, after having chosen
i
independent operators, the
(i+1)
-th
operator can be chosen among
2^{n}-2^{i}
operators, because
2^{i}
operators are now dependent on the first
i
operators already selected.
For large values of
n
, the number of IMs of rank
r
grows as
2^{nr}/r!\,
.
Finally, the total number of IMs in an
n
-spin system is obtained by summing (
45
) over all possible values of
r
(from
r=0
to
n
) and is displayed in Fig.
2
(labeled as “IM”). We estimated that this number grows roughly as
2^{n^{2}}
.

Number of Pairwise Models.

Since there are
n(n+1)/2
possible single-spin and pairwise interactions, there are in total
2^{n(n+1)/2}
possible pairwise models (see plot labeled “PM
∗
” in Fig.
2
). This number grows slower than the number of Independent Models.

Number of Sub-Complete Models.

In an
n
-spin system, all the SCMs of a given rank
r
can be obtained by taking all possible GTs of any chosen SCM
\mathcal{M}
of rank
r
. As the GTs that involve only the operators in
\mathcal{M}
leave
\mathcal{M}
invariant,
the number of distinct SCMs is therefore:

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{\rm ICC}(n,r)=\prod_{i=0}^{r-1}\frac{2^{n}-2^{i}}{2^{r}-2^{i}}\,.

(46)

Here the numerator
\prod_{i=0}^{r-1}(2^{n}-2^{i})
counts the number of ways of choosing the
r
basis operators of the SCM.
The denominator
\prod_{i=0}^{r-1}(2^{r}-2^{i})
counts the number of GTs that transform the basis of the SCM while leaving
\mathcal{M}
invariant. Finally, the total number of SCMs (i.e of models with a single ICC) in an
n
-spin system is obtained by summing (
46
) over all possible values of
r
(from
r=0
to
n
) and is displayed in Fig.
2
(labeled as “SCM”).

Number of Minimally Complex Models.

Consider an MCM with
m
ICCs for an
n
-spin system; we denote
m_{r_{a}}
the number of ICCs of rank
r_{a}
, such that
m=\sum_{r_{a}=0}^{n}m_{r_{a}}
.
For MCMs on
n
spins with
m_{r_{a}}
ICCs of the same rank
{r_{a}}

one can combine the two previous arguments and get the number of such distinct models:

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{\rm MCM}(n,\{m_{r_{a}}\})=\frac{\displaystyle\prod_{i=0}^{r-1}\left(2^{n}-2^{i}\right)}{\displaystyle\prod_{r_{a}=1}^{n}m_{r_{a}}!\,\left[\prod_{i=0}^{r_{a}-1}(2^{r_{a}}-2^{i})\right]^{m_{r_{a}}}}

where the factor
1/m_{r_{a}}!
accounts for permutations among ICCs of the same size. To count the total number of MCMs with
n
spins (plotted in Fig.
2
), one must sum over all the classes of MCMs with different degeneracies
m_{r_{a}}
. The number of classes with different degeneracy corresponds to the number of integer partitions of the rank
r
of the MCM in the form
r=\sum_{r_{a}}{r_{a}}m_{r_{a}}
, and summing over all
r\leq n
. For instance,
r=8
admits
22
partitions which are:

\displaystyle 8,71,62,611,53,521,5111,44,431,422,4211,

\displaystyle 41111,332,3311,3221,32111,311111,2222,

\displaystyle 22211,221111,2111111,11111111\,.

Here, the partition denoted “
8
” corresponds to the class of MCMs that have only one ICC (with rank
r=8
).
The partition “
422
” is the class of MCMs formed of three ICCs,
two of rank 2 and one of rank 4.

Number of Minimally Complex Models with the same preferred basis.

For a given choice of
n
basis operators, the number of MCMs of rank
r=n
that admits that basis as a preferred basis is given by the number of possible partitions of this set of
n
basis elements, which is
the Bell number
B_{n}
. Note that Ref.
Berend and Tassa 2010
gives the upper-bound

B_{n}<\big(\frac{0.792\,n}{\ln(n+1)}\big)^{n}
.
Finally, the total number of MCMs that share the same preferred basis (indicated as “MCM
∗
” in Fig.
2
) is obtained by summing this number over all ranks
r
(from
r=0
to
n
) while accounting for the possible choices of the
r
basis operators:

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{MCM^{*}}(n)=\sum_{r=0}^{n}\binom{n}{r}B_{r}\,.

(47)

For example, for
n=9
,
there are
115\,975
MCM
∗
in total (which are the MCMs that share the same preferred basis); only
21\,147
of them have the rank
r=9
.

Appendix L
Comments on our heuristic algorithms

Greedy search for the Best MCM.

For the Big 5 dataset, the Greedy search was only able to find the best MCM (displayed in Fig.
11
and
5
, with
\log E=-35.80
bits/datapoint) from the search in the original basis, but wasn’t able to find it in the new basis. Instead, it found a sub-optimal MCM with
\log E=-36.12
bits/datapoint. Our understanding is that, because the greedy algorithm starts by making local merging steps, it is more likely to initially take a wrong path in the new basis (where basis variables are the most independent) than in the original basis (where correlations between variables are much stronger).
Yet, for most of the datasets we analyzed with the Greedy search algorithm,
we found a more optimal MCM in the best basis than in the original basis.
In the case of the Big 5, the correlations between the questions are, by design, mostly prevalent within the traits rather than between the traits,
which means that the original basis of the data is likely a preferred basis for the best MCM.
This in part explains why it was possible to find a better MCM with the greedy search in the original basis than in the new basis.

For the MNIST data, the Greedy search found a more optimal MCM in the best basis than in the original basis (see Fig.
13
.c).
In particular, the average ICC size is smaller in the best basis (
\overline{r}_{a}\simeq 7.12
) than in the original basis (
\overline{r}_{a}\simeq 8.07
),
resulting in a large reduction of the number of interactions
K_{MCM_{best}}=34\,631
in the best basis compared to
K_{MCM_{0}}=73\,251
in the original basis (see Fig.
13
.d).
This is a large number of parameters, but it is still
smaller than the number of different states observed in the dataset (which is
57\,903
). We believe that this MCM is likely not the most optimal one,
but it is still able to sample states that resemble vaguely digits (see Fig.
6
).
In the future, it would be useful to develop more efficient algorithms to solve this optimization problem.

Heuristic search for the Best Basis.

Fig.
13
.b shows the number of basis operators of order
k
obtained for different values of
k_{max}
by the heuristic “Best Basis” search described in Sec.
V
for the MNIST dataset.
We observe that the distribution doesn’t change much from
k_{max}=2
