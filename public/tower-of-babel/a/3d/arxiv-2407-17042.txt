The Hessian of elliptic curves as a Lattès map
Marzio Mula, Federico Pintore, Daniele Taufer

Abstract
We prove that the Hessian transformation of elliptic curves, both as an action on j j -invariants and on the Hesse pencil, is a rigid Lattès map fitting into a reduced diagram, hence it lifts to a degree- 3 3 endomorphism ψ \psi of a prescribed elliptic curve E E . This result provides an effective tool to investigate the dynamics of the Hessian transformation, whose symmetries are inherited from those of ψ \psi , which we characterize . In particular, over arbitrary fields of characteristic different from 2 2 and 3 3 , the functional graphs of the Hessian and, more generally, of Lattès maps fitting into analogous reduced diagrams, are completely determined by the action of ψ \psi on the twists of E E . When the underlying field is finite, we specialize these results to obtain a complete classification of Hessian functional graphs and derive an efficient method for computing iterated Hessians.

Introduction

Let
{\Bbbk}
be a field of characteristic different from
2
and
3
.
Given a projective hypersurface
V(F)\subseteq{\mathbb{P}}^{n}({\Bbbk})
, its
Hessian
is defined as the zero locus of the determinant of the Hessian matrix of
F
.
This classical construction enjoys several geometric properties, whose study began with the seminal works by Hesse
[
Hes51
,
Hes59
]
and has continued to the present day
[
CO20
,
CO26
]
.
When the considered form
F
is a projective plane cubic (i.e.,
n=2
and
\deg(F)=3
), the Hessian of
V(F)
is, in turn, defined by a (possibly zero) cubic form.
Moreover, if
V(F)
is also smooth (i.e., elliptic), the Hessian has proven to be a crucial tool for investigating its arithmetic properties
[
ATT18
,
SV21
,
ST23
]
.

It is natural to ask how a given elliptic curve relates to its Hessian and, more generally, to its iterated Hessian.
This question motivates the study of the (discrete) dynamical systems defined by the Hessian transformation, whose corresponding functional graphs will be referred to as
Hessian graphs
.
The study of these graphs has already been undertaken by several authors for
{\Bbbk}=\mathbb{C}
or
{\Bbbk}=\mathbb{R}

[
SC79
,
Hol26
,
PP09
,
AD09
,
CS24
,
Ket26
]
, and it falls within a broader field of research regarding the dynamics of rational functions
[
NS03
,
Sil07
]
.
However, despite their fundamental nature and theoretical relevance, the structure and symmetries of Hessian graphs have not been fully investigated yet.

The moduli space classically employed to study the Hessian transformation is
X(1)\simeq{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\mathbb{P}}^{1}(\overline{{\Bbbk}})}
, which parametrizes
\overline{{\Bbbk}}
-isomorphism classes of elliptic curves by means of their
j
-invariants. However, as hinted in
[
PP09
, Rmk. 5.2]
, one may also consider a more structured model, which covers
{\mathbb{P}}^{1}(\overline{{\Bbbk}})
, by viewing the Hessian transformation as a
Lattès map

[
Mil06
]
, namely a rational function
\phi:{\mathbb{P}}^{1}({\Bbbk})\to{\mathbb{P}}^{1}({\Bbbk})
, whose extension to
{\mathbb{P}}^{1}(\overline{{\Bbbk}})
fits into a commutative diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces E}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces E}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathbb{P}}^{1}(\overline{{\Bbbk}})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathbb{P}}^{1}(\overline{{\Bbbk}})}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\psi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\phi}

(1)

where
E
is an elliptic curve,
\psi
a curve morphism and
\pi
a finite projection.
The diagram of
eq.

1
is called
reduced
if
\pi
is given by the natural projection by a subgroup
\Gamma\subseteq\textnormal{Aut}(E)
. The map
\phi
is called
rigid
if
\deg(\pi)\neq 2
or
\psi
cannot be chosen as
[P]\mapsto[m]P+T
, for any
m\in{\mathbb{Z}}
and
T\in E
.

In this work, we consider two projective Hessian transformations, namely

\displaystyle\mathrm{H}:{\mathbb{P}}^{1}({\Bbbk})\to{\mathbb{P}}^{1}({\Bbbk}),\quad

\displaystyle[u:v]\mapsto[(u-6912v)^{3}:-27u^{2}v],

\displaystyle\Lambda:{\mathbb{P}}^{1}({\Bbbk})\to{\mathbb{P}}^{1}({\Bbbk}),\quad

\displaystyle[u:v]\mapsto[u^{3}+108v^{3}:-3u^{2}v].

The map
\mathrm{H}
encodes the action of the Hessian map on
j
-invariants, while
\Lambda
describes the Hessian action on the Hesse pencil
[
AD09
,
AEI23
]
, namely the curves defined by
\{x^{3}+y^{3}+z^{3}+\lambda xyz\}_{\lambda\in{\Bbbk}}\cup\{xyz\}
.
We characterize the fibers of the
j
-function on the Hesse pencil (
Proposition

2.6
), and we exhibit an explicit bijection between the order-
2

{\Bbbk}
-rational points of an elliptic curve
E
and
\mathrm{H}^{-1}(j(E))
(
Proposition

2.13
).
This shows that computing the order-
2
points of
E({\Bbbk})
is equivalent to computing its inverse Hessian.
We then turn our attention to the Lattès nature of the Hessian, proving the following:

Theorem
[
Theorem

3.19
,
Corollary

3.20
]:
For any
{\Bbbk}
, both
\mathrm{H}
and
\Lambda
are rigid Lattès maps fitting into reduced Lattès diagrams.

Such Lattès diagrams are explicitly given by projecting a degree-
3
endomorphism
\psi
of the curve

E\ :\ y^{2}=x^{3}-1728.

This curve has
j
-invariant
0
, therefore we have an embedding
{\mathbb{Z}}[(-1-\sqrt{-3})/2]\hookrightarrow\mathrm{End}(E)
.
Under this identification, the lifted Hessian
\psi
corresponds to the complex multiplication by
\sqrt{-3}
(
Section

3.3
), which implies that
E
is a CM-curve in characteristic
0
.
This is a non-generic phenomenon for elliptic curves, which leads to additional arithmetic structure and number-theoretic applications
[
Koh96
,
Cox13
,
AJT26
]
.
Such CM-curves are natural candidates to construct rigid Lattès maps (
Remark

2.10
).

To understand the global symmetries displayed by the dynamical systems of
\mathrm{H}
and
\Lambda
, we study the regular dynamics induced by group homomorphisms with prime kernel:

Theorem
[
Theorem

4.9
]:
Let
\phi
be a group endomorphism with prime kernel, and let
\tau_{{\mathcal{O}}}
be the functional graph defined by all the elements that are eventually mapped to the group identity
{\mathcal{O}}
.
The connected components in the functional graph of
\phi
consist of cycles, lines or semilines, whose vertices are roots of arborescences that are isomorphic to sub-arborescences of
\tau_{{\mathcal{O}}}
.

The above result provides a rigid classification of the connected components that may arise in the functional graph of
\psi
.
However, when the field
{\Bbbk}
is not algebraically closed, the fiber
{\mathcal{S}}_{\pi}=\pi^{-1}\big({\mathbb{P}}^{1}({\Bbbk})\big)
need not be a group, hence the regular dynamics of
\psi
on
E
is not necessarily inherited on
{\mathcal{S}}_{\pi}
.
We resolve this issue for reduced Lattès diagrams by proving the following structural result:

Theorem
[
Theorem

5.3
]:
Let
E
be an elliptic curve in (short) Weierstrass form and
\pi:E\to{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
be a suitable projection.
Then
{\mathcal{S}}_{\pi}
is covered by isomorphic copies of twists of
E
over
{\Bbbk}
.

The above covering is minimal, as different components intersect only in points with some zero coordinate (
Proposition

5.6
).
We prove that the regular action of
\psi
on these components is preserved after
\pi
, with only a few possible identifications (
Propositions

5.19
and
5.21
).
These general tools may be applied to study the dynamics of Lattès maps fitting into a reduced diagram.

We then specialize these results to the Hessian transformations, showing that the arithmetic properties of
E
can be leveraged to understand the loops (
Lemma

6.1
) and the structure of connected components (
Propositions

6.4
and
6.3
) of
\psi
.
This allows us to fully describe the identifications arising from
\pi
, and therefore provide a detailed characterization of the connected components in the dynamical systems generated by
\mathrm{H}
and
\Lambda
(
Proposition

6.8
).

In the last part of the paper, we restrict our attention to finite fields
{\Bbbk}={\mathbb{F}}_{q}
.

In this case, we give a full characterization of elements
j\in{\mathbb{P}}^{1}({\mathbb{F}}_{q})
with a prescribed size of
\mathrm{H}^{-1}(j)
:

Theorem
[
Lemma

7.4
,
Theorem

7.5
]:
Let
E_{(j)}
be an elliptic curve with
j
-invariant
j\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{*}
. Then
|\mathrm{H}^{-1}(j)|=0
if and only if the trace of
E_{(j)}
is even.
If also
j\neq 1728
, then
|\mathrm{H}^{-1}(j)|=1
if and only if
j-1728
is not a square in
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{*}
.

We exploit this characterization to provide a complete description of the structure of Hessian graphs, which decisively depends on
q\bmod 3
.
When
q\equiv 1\bmod 3
, we prove that Hessian graphs consist of unions of six known functional graphs of group endomorphisms, up to pruning certain branches (
Theorem

7.8
).
When
q\equiv 2\bmod 3
, the curve
E
is supersingular, and we exploit its known group structure to show that Hessian graphs arise from an ordered merge of two isomorphic graphs, whose depth and cycles are fully determined (
Theorem

7.13
).

Finally, we employ our results to guarantee that any iteration of the Hessian transformation over
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
can be computed in polynomial time in the size of
q
(
Proposition

8.1
).
This paves the way for potential cryptographic applications of Hessian graphs.
In this direction, we prove that supersingular
j
-invariants all lie in prescribed connected components (
Proposition

8.5
), whose vertices have all the same known trace modulo
3
(
Proposition

8.8
).

2.1
Elliptic curves

In this work, we will mostly consider irreducible cubic forms
F\in{\Bbbk}[x,y,z]
, which defines a projective curve in
{\mathbb{P}}^{2}({\Bbbk})

with a smooth
{\Bbbk}
-rational point
.
Since
\mathsf{char}({\Bbbk})\neq 2,3
, with a suitable change of coordinates over
{\Bbbk}

[
Sil09
, §III.1]
, we can always assume such an
F
to be in short Weierstrass form:

F=x^{3}+Axz^{2}+Bz^{3}-y^{2}z,\qquad A,B\in{\Bbbk}.

(2)

In particular, when its
discriminant

\Delta(F)=-16(4A^{3}+27B^{2})
is not
0
, it defines a smooth plane cubic, known as
elliptic curve
(over
{\Bbbk}
).
Under this assumption, we conventionally denote it by
E
. We will often restrict the projective plane to the affine chart
z\neq 0
, where the curve
E
is defined by

y^{2}=x^{3}+Ax+B,\qquad A,B\in{\Bbbk}.

(3)

We will denote its unique non-affine point by
{\mathcal{O}}=[0:1:0]\in{\mathbb{P}}^{2}({\Bbbk})
.

We refer to
[
Sil09
,
Was08
]
for classical definitions and results on elliptic curves, and recall only what is needed for the present paper.
The
{\Bbbk}
-rational points
of an elliptic curve
E
, denoted by
E({\Bbbk})
, are the solutions of
eq.

3
over
{\Bbbk}
, together with the point
at infinity

{\mathcal{O}}
.
Every elliptic curve
E
can be endowed with the structure of an abelian group
(E(\overline{{\Bbbk}}),+)
, whose zero element is
{\mathcal{O}}
and with
E({\Bbbk})
as a subgroup. An
isogeny
between two elliptic curves
E_{1},E_{2}
over
{\Bbbk}
is a curve morphism

\varphi\colon E_{1}\rightarrow E_{2}

that is also a group homomorphism. We say that
\varphi
is
defined over
{\Bbbk}
if the rational functions defining
\varphi
can be chosen with coefficients in
{\Bbbk}
.
We say that a separable isogeny has
degree

n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{>0}
, or it is an
n
-isogeny
, if its kernel has (finite) size
n
.
A separable isogeny of degree
1
is an
isomorphism
.
Every
\overline{{\Bbbk}}
-isomorphism class of an elliptic curve
E
defined over
{\Bbbk}
is uniquely determined by its
j
-invariant

j(E)\in{\Bbbk}
.
It can be easily retrieved from the coefficients of any elliptic curve
E
in the isomorphism class.
In particular, when
E
is defined by
eq.

3
, its
j
-invariant is

j(E)=1728\frac{4A^{3}}{4A^{3}+27B^{2}}.

An elliptic curve
E^{\prime}
that is isomorphic to
E
over
\overline{{\Bbbk}}
is called a
twist
of
E
.

In this work, we only consider isomorphisms of
elliptic curves
, namely, those respecting their group structure.

If such an isomorphism is not defined over the base field
{\Bbbk}
of
E
, then the twist
E^{\prime}
is called
proper
.
The set of twists of
E
, up to
{\Bbbk}
-isomorphism, is denoted by
\mathrm{Twist}(E,{\mathcal{O}})
, and its structure is well-known.

Lemma 2.1

(
[
Sil09
, Prop. X.5.4]
)
.

Let
E
be the elliptic curve defined over
{\Bbbk}
by
y^{2}=x^{3}+Ax+B
, and

n=\begin{cases}2\quad\text{if $j(E)\not\in\{0,1728\}$},\\
4\quad\text{if $j(E)=1728$},\\
6\quad\text{if $j(E)=0$}.\\
\end{cases}

Then
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Twist}(E,{\mathcal{O}})}\cong{\Bbbk}^{*}/({\Bbbk}^{*})^{n}
. The elements
E^{(D)}\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Twist}(E,{\mathcal{O}})}
can be listed for
D\in{\Bbbk}^{*}/({\Bbbk}^{*})^{n}
as

(i)

y^{2}=x^{3}+D^{2}Ax+D^{3}B
,
if
j(E)\not\in\{0,1728\}
,

(ii)

y^{2}=x^{3}+DAx
,
if
j(E)=1728
,

(iii)

y^{2}=x^{3}+DB
,
if
j(E)=0
.

Moreover, writing
D^{1/n}\in\overline{{\Bbbk}}
for a chosen
n
-th root of

, we denote the corresponding
\overline{{\Bbbk}}
-isomorphism by

\iota_{D}:E^{(D)}\to E,\quad(x,y)\mapsto\big(D^{-2/n}x,\;D^{-3/n}y\big).

An isogeny from an elliptic curve
E
to itself is called an
endomorphism
.
The ring of endomorphisms of
E
is denoted by
\mathrm{End}(E)
,
while the group of its automorphisms (fixing
{\mathcal{O}}
) is denoted by
\textnormal{Aut}(E)
.
This is a well-known cyclic group, which only depends on the
j
-invariant of
E

[
Sil09
, §III.10]
.
The automorphisms of
E
that are defined over
{\Bbbk}
will be denoted by
\textnormal{Aut}_{{\Bbbk}}(E)
.
For any
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
, the
multiplication-by-
m

[m]\in\mathrm{End}(E)
is defined for every
P\in E

[m]P=\underbrace{P+P+\dots+P}_{m~\text{times}}.

The above definition extends to
m\in{\mathbb{Z}}
by setting
[-m]P=-([m]P)
.
For each
m\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 1}
, the
m
-torsion
of an elliptic curve

is its subgroup
E[m]=\ker([m])\subseteq E(\overline{{\Bbbk}})
, whose points are called
m
-
torsion points
.

2.2
Hessian of a cubic

Given a homogeneous cubic
F\in{\Bbbk}[x,y,z]
, we define its
Hessian

\mathrm{H}(F)
as the determinant of its Hessian matrix
\mathbb{H}_{F}

[
CO20
, §5]
.
It is easy to see that
\mathrm{H}(F)
is either
0
or again a homogeneous cubic.
This induces an equivariant transformation of the associated projective curves: if
\phi\in\mathrm{GL}_{3}({\Bbbk})
is a linear change of variables, represented by the matrix
\Phi
in the standard basis
\{x,y,z\}
, a straightforward application of the chain rule shows that

\mathrm{H}\big(\phi(F)\big)=\det\big(\Phi^{T}\phi(\mathbb{H}_{F})\Phi\big)=(\det\Phi)^{2}\phi\big(\mathrm{H}(F)\big).

Thus, the Hessian respects
{\Bbbk}
-isomorphism classes.
As anticipated, we are mostly interested in irreducible cubics
F
, which we can assume to be in short Weierstrass form (
eq.

2
).
A straightforward computation then shows that in this case

\displaystyle\mathrm{H}(F)

\displaystyle=-8(3xy^{2}+3Ax^{2}z+9Bxz^{2}-A^{2}z^{3}).

The following result analyzes the Hessian of smooth cubics, i.e., elliptic curves.
In particular, it shows that the elliptic curves whose Hessian also defines an elliptic curve are exactly those with non-zero
j
-invariant.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
E
be an elliptic curve defined by
y^{2}z=x^{3}+Axz^{2}+Bz^{3}
.

(i)

If
A=0
, then
\mathrm{H}(E)=-24x(y^{2}+3Bz^{2})
defines three independent lines. Moreover, we have
\mathrm{H}\big(\mathrm{H}(E)\big)=(-24^{3}B)\mathrm{H}(E)
.

(ii)

If
A\neq 0
, the short Weierstrass form of
\mathrm{H}(E)
is

x^{3}-\frac{A^{3}+9B^{2}}{3A^{4}}xz^{2}-\frac{A^{3}B+6B^{3}}{3A^{6}}z^{3}-zy^{2},

(4)

and we have

\displaystyle\Delta\big(\mathrm{H}(E)\big)

\displaystyle=\frac{(64/27)A^{3}+16B^{2}}{A^{6}}=-\frac{\Delta(E)}{27A^{6}},

\displaystyle j\big(\mathrm{H}(E)\big)

\displaystyle=\frac{1728(A^{3}+9B^{2})^{3}}{A^{6}\bigl(A^{3}+(27/4)B^{2}\bigr)}=j(E)\frac{(A^{3}+9B^{2})^{3}}{A^{9}}=\frac{\big(6912-j(E)\big)^{3}}{27j(E)^{2}}.

(5)

Proof.

(i)
: When
A=0
, we straightforwardly check that

\mathrm{H}(E)=-24x(y^{2}+3Bz^{2})=-24x(y+\sqrt{-3B}z)(y-\sqrt{-3B}z).

Since
E
is smooth we have
B\neq 0
, hence the linear factors of
\mathrm{H}(E)
define independent lines.
Another direct computation shows that
\mathrm{H}\big(\mathrm{H}(E)\big)=(-24^{3}B)\mathrm{H}(E)
.

(ii)
: When
A\neq 0
, the Hessian of
E
is

\mathrm{H}(E)=-216A^{2}\left(x\left(\frac{y}{3A}\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{3A}x^{2}\left(\frac{z}{3}\right)+\frac{3B}{A^{2}}x\left(\frac{z}{3}\right)^{2}-\left(\frac{z}{3}\right)^{3}\right)

and the change of coordinates
[x:y:z]\mapsto[z:3Ay:3x+3B/A^{2}z]
yields the short Weierstrass form of
eq.

4
. The discriminant and
j
-invariant can be directly computed from the coefficients of
eq.

4
. ∎

Remark 2.3
.

In general, the Hessian fixes three independent lines: if

F=(a_{11}x+a_{12}y+a_{13}z)(a_{21}x+a_{22}y+a_{23}z)(a_{31}x+a_{32}y+a_{33}z),

then
\mathrm{H}(F)=(2\det M^{2})F
, where
M=(a_{ij})_{1\leq i,j\leq 3}
.

Remark 2.4
.

The points in
E\cap H(E)
are called
flex points
or
inflection points
of
E
.
It is known that the inflection points of an elliptic curve coincide with its
3
-torsion points, provided that the base point is chosen to be an inflection point
[
Dic14
]
.
Moreover, as observed in
[
ATT18
, §3]
, if the base point of
\mathrm{H}(E)
is also chosen among the inflection points of
E
(the natural choice being the same base point of
E
) and
\mathrm{H}(E)
is again elliptic, then
E\cap\mathrm{H}(E)
also coincides with the
3
-torsion points of
\mathrm{H}(E)
.
Indeed, the addition laws on
E
and
\mathrm{H}(E)
coincide on
E\cap\mathrm{H}(E)=E[3]
because the chord-tangent construction on
E
agrees with the same procedure on
\mathrm{H}(E)
: a line through two points (with multiplicity) of
E[3]
meets
E
in another point of
3
-torsion, hence it also belongs to
\mathrm{H}(E)
.

Proposition

2.2
shows that the Hessian defines a transformation between
\overline{{\Bbbk}}
-isomorphism classes, which we denote again by

:

\mathrm{H}(j)=\frac{(6912-j)^{3}}{27j^{2}}.

This map can be extended to a projective map of

by assigning to the union of three independent lines the value
\infty=[1:0]\in{\mathbb{P}}^{1}({\Bbbk})
, i.e.,

\mathrm{H}:{\mathbb{P}}^{1}({\Bbbk})\to{\mathbb{P}}^{1}({\Bbbk}),\quad[j:1]\mapsto[(j-6912)^{3}:-27j^{2}],\quad\quad[1:0]\mapsto[1:0].

(6)

Throughout the paper, we refer to this map as the
Hessian transformation
or
Hessian map
.

2.3
The Hesse pencil

Rather than considering cubics in short Weierstrass form, some authors use the
Hessian form

F_{\lambda}=x^{3}+y^{3}+z^{3}+\lambda xyz,\qquad\lambda\in{\Bbbk}.

(7)

The curve defined by
F_{\lambda}
is nonsingular if and only if
\lambda^{3}\neq-27

[
His10
, §2.3.3]
.
The set of forms
\{F_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\in{\Bbbk}}\cup\{xyz\}

is called the
Hesse pencil (over
{\Bbbk}
)
.
When
\lambda=0
we have
\mathrm{H}(F_{0})=xyz
, i.e., the union of three independent lines.
For every other
\lambda\neq 0
, a simple computation
[
SC79
, §V.218]
shows that
\mathrm{H}(F_{\lambda})=F_{\lambda^{\prime}}
is again in Hessian form, where

\lambda^{\prime}=-\frac{108+\lambda^{3}}{3\lambda^{2}}.

By setting
F_{\infty}=xyz
, the Hessian on the Hesse pencil determines again a projective map

\Lambda:{\mathbb{P}}^{1}({\Bbbk})\to{\mathbb{P}}^{1}({\Bbbk}),\quad[\lambda:1]\mapsto[\lambda^{3}+108:-3\lambda^{2}],\quad\quad[1:0]\mapsto[1:0].

(8)

The elliptic curves of the Hesse pencil have all the same
9
inflection points, which can be computed explicitly
[
Dic14
,
AD09
]
:

\displaystyle p_{0}

\displaystyle=[0:1:-1],

\displaystyle p_{1}

\displaystyle=[0:1:-\zeta_{3}],

\displaystyle p_{2}

\displaystyle=[0:1:-\zeta_{3}^{2}],

\displaystyle p_{3}

\displaystyle=[1:0:-1],

\displaystyle p_{4}

\displaystyle=[1:0:-\zeta_{3}^{2}],

2.4
Discrete dynamical systems and Hessian graphs

A
(discrete) dynamical system
consists of a set
S
and a map
\phi\colon S\rightarrow S

[
Sil07
]
.

Definition 2.7
.

The
functional graph of
\phi:S\to S
is the directed graph whose vertices are the elements of
S
and whose edges are
\{x\to\phi(x)\}_{x\in S}
.
We denote such a graph by
\langle S,\phi\rangle
.

Two functional graphs
\langle S,\phi\rangle
and
\langle S,\phi^{\prime}\rangle
are
isomorphic
if
\phi
and
\phi^{\prime}
are
conjugate
, namely there exists an automorphism
f
of
S
such that
\phi=f^{-1}\circ\phi^{\prime}\circ f
.

In particular, when
S={\mathbb{P}}^{1}({\Bbbk})
, a conjugation is given by a Möbius transformation
f\in\textnormal{Aut}_{{\Bbbk}}({\mathbb{P}}^{1})
.

We call
Hessian graph
the functional graph
\langle{\mathbb{P}}^{1}({\Bbbk}),\mathrm{H}\rangle
of the Hessian transformation
\mathrm{H}
defined in
eq.

6
.
We denote by
\phi^{(n)}
the
n
-th iterate of
\phi
, i.e., the composition of
\phi
with itself
n
times.
By convention,
\phi^{(0)}
is the identity map on
S
.
A point
P\in S
is called
periodic
(with respect to
\phi
) if it lies on a
cycle
of
\langle S,\phi\rangle
, i.e., if there exists
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{>0}
such that
\phi^{(n)}(P)=P
.
We denote the set of periodic points of
\phi
by
\mathrm{Per}(\phi)
.
If there exists
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
\phi^{(m)}(P)\in\mathrm{Per}(\phi)
, we say that
P
is
preperiodic
.

Definition 2.8
.

Let
\phi:S\to S
and
T\subseteq S
. For every
P\in S
, we define its
depth
(with respect to
T
) as

d_{T}(P)=\inf_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\big\{\phi^{(n)}(P)\in T\big\}.

When the set
T
is understood, we will simply denote the depth of
P
by
d(P)
.

One can readily see that if a connected component contains periodic elements, then they lie in a single cycle, whose vertices are roots of (possibly empty) directed trees.
In the following sections, we will see that these rooted directed trees, also called
arborescences
, are remarkably regular for Hessian graphs.

2.5
Lattès maps

Understanding the structure of functional graphs defined by rational functions over a field
{\Bbbk}
is, in general, a challenging task.
However,
special
families of functions are better understood and enjoy a remarkably regular structure,
namely, the
finite quotients of afﬁne maps
: Lattès, Chebyshev and power maps
[
Mil06
]
.
Those are the maps arising as finite projections of (affine) group morphisms, from which they inherit a symmetric dynamics
[
Sil07
,
Bys+20
]
.

In this paper, we focus on Lattès maps, which we define as in
[
Sil07
]
.
More results on these maps can be found in
[
Mil06
,
Sil07
,
Pak20
]
.

Definition 2.9
.

Let
\phi\colon{\mathbb{P}}^{1}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\Bbbk}})\rightarrow{\mathbb{P}}^{1}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\Bbbk}})
be a rational map of degree
d\geq 2
,
and let
\overline{\phi}:{\mathbb{P}}^{1}(\overline{{\Bbbk}})\to{\mathbb{P}}^{1}(\overline{{\Bbbk}})
denote its base change to
\overline{{\Bbbk}}
.
We say that
\phi
is a
Lattès map
if there is an elliptic curve
E
defined over
{\Bbbk}
, a curve morphism
\psi\colon E\rightarrow E
, and a finite separable covering
\pi\colon E\rightarrow{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
such that the following diagram is commutative.

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces E(\overline{{\Bbbk}})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces E(\overline{{\Bbbk}})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathbb{P}}^{1}(\overline{{\Bbbk}})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathbb{P}}^{1}(\overline{{\Bbbk}})}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\psi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\overline{\phi}}}

(10)

Moreover:

•

If there is a non-trivial subgroup
\Gamma\subseteq\textnormal{Aut}(E)
such that the covering is given by the canonical projection as

\pi:E\to E/\Gamma\xrightarrow{\simeq}{\mathbb{P}}^{1},

then
eq.

10
is called a
reduced Lattès diagram
for
\phi
.

•

If
eq.

10
may be chosen such that
\psi
is the map
P\mapsto[m]P+T
for some
m\in{\mathbb{Z}}
and
T\in E
, and
\deg(\pi)=2
, then
\phi
is called
flexible
. Otherwise, it is called
rigid
.

Remark 2.10
.

When
E
is given by a (short) Weierstrass model, any map
\phi
fitting in a reduced Lattès diagram is conjugated (over
\overline{{\Bbbk}}
) to a Lattès map whose projection is given by

\pi(x,y)=\begin{cases}x&\textnormal{if }|\Gamma|=2,\\
x^{2}&\textnormal{if }|\Gamma|=4,\\
y&\textnormal{if }|\Gamma|=3,\\
x^{3}&\textnormal{if }|\Gamma|=6.\end{cases}

as described in
[
Sil07
, Prop. 6.37]
.
In particular, for every
m\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, the projection of
[m]\in\mathrm{End}(E)
on its first coordinate always yields a reduced diagram for a flexible Lattès map
[
Sil07
, §6.5]
.
Similarly, since the degree of a flexible Lattès map is an integer square
[
Sil07
, Prop. 6.51-(a)]
, one can obtain reduced diagrams of rigid Lattès maps by projecting on the first coordinate any
\psi\in\mathrm{End}(E)
with non-square degree, which exists only if
{\mathbb{Z}}\subsetneq\mathrm{End}(E)
. The latter always occurs over any finite fields, while in
\textnormal{char}({\Bbbk})=0
it holds precisely when
E
is a CM-curve
[
Sil09
, Rmk. III.4.3]
.

We now briefly consider the case
{\Bbbk}=\mathbb{C}
.

In this case, every Lattès map fits into a reduced Lattès diagram
[
Sil07
, Thm. 6.57]
. Moreover,
there is a practical criterion to check whether a rational map
\phi:{\mathbb{P}}^{1}(\mathbb{C})\rightarrow{\mathbb{P}}^{1}(\mathbb{C})
is Lattès,
which we briefly recall here
.
The
ramification index
of
\phi
at
P\in{\mathbb{P}}^{1}(\mathbb{C})
is defined as

e_{P}(\phi)=\mathrm{ord}_{P}\big(\phi(x)-\phi(P)\big).

This quantity can be explicitly computed even when
P=\infty
or
\phi(P)=\infty
, by changing the considered affine chart.
A point
P\in{\mathbb{P}}^{1}(\mathbb{C})
is called a
critical point
for
\phi
if
e_{P}(\phi)\geq 2
. The points of the form
\{\phi^{(n)}(P)\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{>0}}
, where
P
is a critical point, are called
post-critical
.

It is known that a finite subset
T\subseteq{\mathbb{P}}^{1}(\mathbb{C})
such that
\phi^{-1}(T)=T
may contain at most two elements
[
Sil07
, Thm. 1.6]
.
Since
\phi
is surjective,
\phi^{-1}(T)=T
implies
T=\phi(T)
, hence
\phi
acts as a permutation on
T
.
The maximal such
T
is called the set of
exceptional points
of
\phi
.

Theorem 2.11
.

A rational map
\phi\colon{\mathbb{P}}^{1}(\mathbb{C})\rightarrow{\mathbb{P}}^{1}(\mathbb{C})
is a Lattès map if and only if it has no exceptional points and there exists a
ramification function

v\colon{\mathbb{P}}^{1}(\mathbb{C})\rightarrow{\mathbb{N}}_{>0}
such that
v\big(\phi(P)\big)=e_{P}(\phi)v(P)
for all
P\in{\mathbb{P}}^{1}(\mathbb{C})
.

Proof.

It follows from
[
Mil06
, Thm. 4.1]
and the classification of finite quotients of affine maps given in
[
Mil06
, §2]
.
∎

Lemma 2.12

For every

(i)

(iii)

Cases

To check for multiple roots, we consider the formal derivative

{j^{\prime}}=\frac{-j^{2}+13824j-47775744}{18j}\ \implies\ H_{j^{\prime}}(j)=\frac{1}{54}(j-6912)^{2}(j+13824).

Thus, we may have

(i.e., multiple roots of

or

are

(case

) and

(case

Proposition 2.13
.

Let

and

be any elliptic curve with that

Then the following is a well-defined

\{(\alpha,0)\in E_{(j)}({\Bbbk})\}\to\mathrm{H}^{-1}(j),\qquad(\alpha,0)\mapsto 6912\frac{A+3\alpha^{2}}{4A+3\alpha^{2}}.

Proof.

We prove that the above map is well-defined: given an order-
2

(\alpha,0)\in E_{(j)}({\Bbbk})
, we have

B=-\alpha^{3}-A\alpha\quad\implies\quad j=6912\frac{A^{3}}{(A+3\alpha^{2})^{2}(4A+3\alpha^{2})},

and a straightforward computation shows that

\mathrm{H}\left(6912\frac{A+3\alpha^{2}}{4A+3\alpha^{2}}\right)=j.

We now prove that this map is injective:
(\alpha,0),(\beta,0)\in E_{(j)}({\Bbbk})
have the same image if and only if

6912\frac{A+3\alpha^{2}}{4A+3\alpha^{2}}=6912\frac{A+3\beta^{2}}{4A+3\beta^{2}}\quad\iff\quad 3A(\alpha^{2}-\beta^{2})=0.

Since

, then
A\neq 0
so the above is equivalent to
\alpha=\pm\beta
.
However,
(\alpha,0)
and
(-\alpha,0)
cannot be both points of
E_{(j)}
, since
j\neq 1728
implies

.

If
{\Bbbk}
were algebraically closed, both domain and codomain would have precisely three elements by

2.12
, hence the considered map would be
1

.
Therefore, it is sufficient to show that
\alpha^{2}\in{\Bbbk}
implies
\alpha\in{\Bbbk}
.
This follows by

0=\alpha^{3}+A\alpha+B\implies\alpha(\alpha^{2}+A)=-B,

noticing that
B\neq 0

\alpha^{2}+A\neq 0
, therefore
\alpha=-\frac{B}{\alpha^{2}+A}
.
∎

There are always curves with

by

For every other

Let

3
The Hessian as a Lattès map

In this section, we show that the Hessian transformation
\mathrm{H}

We first show that, over

Definition 3.3

Remark 3.4

Definition 3.5
.

For any given

, we define the

Since

is a well-defined projective coordinate change of

Proposition 3.6
.

For every

\mathrm{H}_{hk}\circ\Phi_{h}=\Phi_{h}\circ\mathrm{H}_{k}\quad\textnormal{and}\quad\Lambda_{h^{3}k}\circ\Phi_{h}=\Phi_{h}\circ\Lambda_{k}.

For every

from which the proposition follows.

.

The functional graphs

An interested reader can find examples of these different dynamics in

Let
G
be
an additive group, whose identity is denoted by
{\mathcal{O}}
.
Let also
\phi\in\mathrm{End}(G)
be an endomorphism of
G
with finite prime kernel
\ell=\left|\ker\phi\right|
.
It is easy to see that the relation

P\sim Q\quad\textnormal{if}\quad\exists\,n,m\in{\mathbb{N}}:\phi^{(n)}(P)=\phi^{(m)}(Q)

is a well-defined equivalence relation on the points of
G
.

Definition 4.1
.

The equivalence classes in
G/{\sim}
will be referred to as the
connected components
of
\langle G,\phi\rangle
.

We will need the following lemma from group theory, whose proof is reported for completeness.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
G
be a group,
\ell
be a prime integer, and
\phi\in\mathrm{End}(G)
be such that
\left|\ker\phi\right|=\ell
.
Then the ascending series of groups

\{{\mathcal{O}}\}=\ker\phi^{(0)}\lneq\ker\phi\leq\ker\phi^{(2)}\leq\dots

(13)

is strictly increasing until it stabilizes.
Let
m=\inf_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}\big\{\ker\phi^{(k)}=\ker\phi^{(k+1)}\big\}
.
Then, for every
0\leq k<m
, we have

\ker\phi^{(k)}=\phi\big(\ker\phi^{(k+1)}\big).

Proof.

We first observe that the series of
eq.

13
stabilizes at
\ker\phi^{(m)}
. In fact, for every
k\geq m
, we have

x\in\ker\phi^{(k+1)}\implies\phi^{(k-m)}(x)\in\ker\phi^{(m+1)}=\ker\phi^{(m)}\implies x\in\ker\phi^{(k)}.

Since
\ker\phi^{(0)}=\{{\mathcal{O}}\}\neq\ker\phi
, then
m>0
.
For every
0\leq k<m
, we have

\phi\big(\ker\phi^{(k+1)}\big)\leq\ker\phi^{(k)}\lneq\ker\phi^{(k+1)}.

However, since
\phi
restricts to an endomorphism of the finite group
\ker\phi^{(k+1)}
, whose kernel is given by
\ker\phi\cap\ker\phi^{(k+1)}=\ker\phi
, then the fundamental theorem on homomorphisms implies

\bigl[\ker\phi^{(k+1)}:\phi\big(\ker\phi^{(k+1)}\big)\bigr]=\frac{\left|\ker\phi^{(k+1)}\right|}{\left|\phi\bigl(\ker\phi^{(k+1)}\bigr)\right|}=\left|\ker\phi\right|=\ell.

Therefore, since
\ell
is prime, we conclude that
\ker\phi^{(k)}=\phi\bigl(\ker\phi^{(k+1)}\bigr)
.
∎

, by
Lemma

4.2
we know that every element of depth
d
is the image of
\ell
elements of depth
d+1
, therefore there can be no leaves.
Thus, in both cases we conclude that
\tau_{{\mathcal{O}}}
is isomorphic to
\mathrm{T}_{\ell}^{m}
.

Every connected component either contains periodic elements (hence it contains a cycle – case
(i)
) or its points
P
define infinite semilines
\{\phi^{(i)}(P)\}_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}
.
In the latter case, either there is a semiline that can be completed to a line (case
(ii)
), or the component contains no oriented lines (case
(iii)
).
We discuss these three cases separately.

(i)
: Let us assume that the connected component contains a cycle
\mathcal{C}
.
The map
\phi|_{\mathcal{C}}
is invertible, and for every
P\in\mathcal{C}
the map

\tau_{{\mathcal{O}}}\to\tau_{P,\mathcal{C}},\quad u\mapsto\phi|_{\mathcal{C}}^{-d(u)}(P)+u

is an oriented tree isomorphism, which maps
{\mathcal{O}}
to
P
,
for the same argument of
[
dM22
, Thm. 2.8]
.
Hence, for every
P\in\mathcal{C}
this gives an isomorphism of arborescences
\tau_{{\mathcal{O}}}\simeq\tau_{P,\mathcal{C}}
.

(ii)
: Let us assume that the connected component contains an oriented line
\mathcal{L}=\{P_{i}\}_{i\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
, with
\phi(P_{i})=P_{i+1}
.
The restriction
\phi|_{\mathcal{L}}:P_{i}\mapsto P_{i+1}
is invertible, and for any
P_{i}\in\mathcal{L}
we define

\Phi:\tau_{{\mathcal{O}}}\to\tau_{P_{i},\mathcal{L}},\quad u\mapsto P_{i-d(u)}+u.

By adapting the idea of
[
dM22
, Thm. 2.8]
, we prove that
\Phi
is an isomorphism of arborescences.

\Phi
is well-defined: if
d=d(u)
, then clearly
\phi^{(d)}\big(\Phi(u)\big)=P_{i}
.
Moreover, let
0\leq f\leq d
be an integer such that
\phi^{(f)}\big(\Phi(u)\big)\in\mathcal{L}
, i.e., it is equal to
P_{m}
for some
m\in{\mathbb{Z}}
.
We have

P_{i}=\phi^{(d-f)}\circ\phi^{(f)}\big(\Phi(u)\big)=\phi^{(d-f)}\circ P_{m}=P_{m+d-f},

which implies
m=i+f-d
because
P_{i}
is not periodic. Therefore

\phi^{(f)}(P_{i-d}+u)=\phi^{(f)}\big(\Phi(u)\big)=P_{m}=P_{i+f-d}\implies\phi^{(f)}(u)={\mathcal{O}},

which proves that
f\geq d
. Hence, for every
u\in\tau_{{\mathcal{O}}}
, the minimal
f\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
\phi^{(f)}\big(\Phi(u)\big)\in\mathcal{L}
is
f=d(u)
, therefore
\Phi(u)\in\tau_{P_{i},\mathcal{L}}
.

\Phi
is surjective: let
Q\in\tau_{P_{i},\mathcal{L}}
and
r
be the unique integer such that
\phi^{(r)}(Q)=P_{i}
. We define

u=-P_{i-r}+Q.

It is easy to check that
\phi^{(r)}(u)={\mathcal{O}}
, hence
u\in\tau_{{\mathcal{O}}}
.
We claim that
d(u)=r
, which implies that
\Phi(u)=Q
.
In fact, if
\phi^{(s)}(u)={\mathcal{O}}
for some integer
0\leq s\leq r
, then

\phi^{(s)}(Q)=P_{i+s-r}\in\mathcal{L}.

Since
\phi^{(s)}(Q)\in\mathcal{L}
only for
s\geq r
, we conclude that
s=r
.

\Phi
is injective: let
u_{1},u_{2}\in\tau_{{\mathcal{O}}}
with
d_{1}=d(u_{1}),d_{2}=d(u_{2})
and such that
\Phi(u_{1})=\Phi(u_{2})
, i.e.,

u_{1}-u_{2}=-P_{i-d_{1}}+P_{i-d_{2}}.

(14)

Let
d=\max(d_{1},d_{2})
, then we have

{\mathcal{O}}=\phi^{(d)}(u_{1}-u_{2})=-P_{i+d-d_{1}}+P_{i+d-d_{2}}.

Since
P
is not periodic, this implies
d_{1}=d_{2}
, therefore also
u_{1}=u_{2}
by
eq.

14
, proving injectivity.

\Phi
respects edges: for every
u,v\in\tau_{{\mathcal{O}}}
such that
u\neq{\mathcal{O}}
and
\phi(u)=v
we have
d(u)=d(v)+1
, therefore
\phi\big(\Phi(u)\big)=\Phi(v)
.
The converse also holds: if
\phi\big(\Phi(u)\big)=\Phi(v)
, then

\phi(u)-v=-P_{i-d(u)+1}+P_{i-d(v)},

which implies as above that
d(u)=d(v)+1
, hence
\phi(u)=v
.

(iii)
: Let us assume that the connected component contains an oriented semiline
\mathcal{N}=\{P_{i}\}_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}
, with
\phi(P_{i})=P_{i+1}
.
Unless
\mathcal{N}
is part of an oriented line (case
(ii)
), we can assume
\phi^{-1}(P_{0})=\emptyset
.
For every
i\in{\mathbb{N}}
, let us consider the arborescence
\tau_{i}
obtained by restricting
\tau_{{\mathcal{O}}}
to elements of
\ker\phi^{(i)}
.
Since
\tau_{{\mathcal{O}}}\simeq\mathrm{T}_{\ell}^{m}
, using
Lemma

4.2
as above we conclude that
\tau_{i}\simeq\mathrm{T}_{\ell}^{\min(i,m)}
.
We discuss the finite and infinite cases separately.

\bullet

m=\infty
: We prove that the map

\Psi:\tau_{i}\to\tau_{P_{i},\mathcal{N}},\quad u\mapsto P_{i-d(u)}+u,

is a well-defined isomorphism of arborescences.

\Psi
is well-defined: for every
u\in\ker\phi^{(i)}
we have
d(u)\leq i
, hence
P_{i-d(u)}
is a well-defined point of
\mathcal{N}
.
Checking that
\Psi(u)\in\tau_{P_{i},\mathcal{N}}
is completely analogous to
(ii)
.

\Psi
is invertible: injectivity follows as in
(ii)
. It is easy to check that, for every
0\leq j\leq i
, we have

\ker\phi^{(j)}+P_{i-j}=\phi^{(-j)}(P_{i}).

Since
|\tau_{P_{0},\mathcal{N}}|=|\{P_{0}\}|=1
, this inductively implies that for every
i\geq 1
we have

|\tau_{P_{i},\mathcal{N}}|=|\tau_{P_{i}}|-|\tau_{P_{i-1}}|=\sum_{j=0}^{i}\ell^{j}-\sum_{j=0}^{i-1}\ell^{j}=\ell^{i}=\bigl|\ker\phi^{(i)}\bigr|=|\tau_{i}|,

from which the surjectivity of
\Psi
follows.

Finally,
\Psi
respects the edges as in
(ii)
, hence it is an isomorphism of arborescences.

\bullet

m<\infty
: We begin by refining the semiline.
Let
Q\in\tau_{P_{m}}
be a point with the longest finite path to
P_{m}
, which exists since
m
is finite and we are not in case
(ii)
.
We consider the new semiline
\mathcal{N}^{\prime}=\{Q_{i}\}_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}
, where
Q_{i}=\phi^{(i)}(Q)
.
We prove that for every
i\in{\mathbb{N}}
the map

\Psi:\tau_{i}\to\tau_{Q_{i},\mathcal{N}^{\prime}},\quad u\mapsto Q_{i-d(u)}+u,

is a well-defined isomorphism of arborescences.

\Psi
is well-defined with the same argument of the case
m=\infty
.

\Psi
is invertible: injectivity follows as in
(ii)
. For every
i<m
, the same counting argument of case
m=\infty
still holds, and implies
|\tau_{i}|=|\tau_{Q_{i},\mathcal{N}^{\prime}}|
.
For
i=m
, there cannot be elements
R\in\tau_{Q_{i}}
with
d_{\mathcal{N}^{\prime}}(R)>m
, by definition of
Q
.
For
i>m
, there are no elements
R\in\tau_{Q_{i}}
with
d(R)=m+1
, otherwise we would have

R-Q_{i-m-1}\in\ker\phi^{(m+1)}\setminus\ker\phi^{(m)},

contradicting
Lemma

4.2
.
Therefore, even for any
i\geq m
we inductively conclude that

|\tau_{Q_{i},\mathcal{N}^{\prime}}|=|\tau_{Q_{i}}|-|\tau_{Q_{i-1}}|=\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\ell^{j}+(i-m+1)\ell^{m}-\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\ell^{j}-(i-m)\ell^{m}=\ell^{m}=\bigl|\ker\phi^{(i)}\bigr|=|\tau_{i}|,

hence
\Psi
is surjective.

Again,
\Psi
respects the edges as in
(ii)
, hence it is an isomorphism of arborescences.
∎

5
Reduced Lattès diagrams

In this section, we provide general results that can be employed to study Lattès maps fitting in a reduced diagram, as in
Definition

2.9
, with a particular focus on non-algebraically closed fields.
We will further assume that the projection
\pi
is one of those given in
Remark

2.10
.
In particular,
Theorem

5.3
shows how the fibers of
\pi
can be expressed as unions of subgroups of
E
, and
Proposition

5.6
shows that these subgroups intersect each other at (at most) six points.
In
Section

5.2
, we specialize these results to the Hessian transformation. Finally, in
Section

5.3
,
Proposition

5.21
illustrates how the functional graph of
\psi
is transformed under
\pi
.

5.1
The fiber of
\pi

When
{\Bbbk}=\overline{{\Bbbk}}
, the dynamics of a Lattès map
\phi:{\mathbb{P}}^{1}({\Bbbk})\to{\mathbb{P}}^{1}({\Bbbk})
fitting in a reduced diagram
can be easily read by quotienting
\langle E({\Bbbk}),\psi\rangle
for the action of
\Gamma\subseteq\textnormal{Aut}(E)
.
The situation is different when
{\Bbbk}\neq\overline{{\Bbbk}}
, as the dynamics of
\phi
depends on the action of
\psi
restricted to

{\mathcal{S}}_{\pi}=\pi^{-1}\big({\mathbb{P}}^{1}({\Bbbk})\big)\subseteq E(\overline{{\Bbbk}}).

Hereafter, we will consider only curves
E
defined by short Weierstrass equations, and with one of the four projections
\pi:E(\overline{{\Bbbk}})\to{\mathbb{P}}^{1}(\overline{{\Bbbk}})
described in
Remark

2.10
.
This assumption is fulfilled by the Hessian (cf.
Theorem

3.19
), but does not hold for general Lattès maps since the conjugation over
\overline{{\Bbbk}}
does not imply functional graph isomorphism over
{\Bbbk}

[
Sil12
, §7]
.

With these assumptions, we have
E({\Bbbk})\subseteq{\mathcal{S}}_{\pi}\subseteq E(\overline{{\Bbbk}})
, although both inclusions may be strict.

We will employ the notation of twists introduced in
Lemma

2.1
.
In particular, let
n=|\textnormal{Aut}(E)|
and
\iota_{D}:E^{(D)}\xrightarrow{\simeq}E
be the
\overline{{\Bbbk}}
-isomorphism corresponding to a fixed choice of
D^{1/n}
.
To resolve the ambiguity in the choice of the root, we consider

\mathcal{T}_{\pi}=\{\sigma\in\textnormal{Aut}(E)\ |\ \sigma({\mathcal{S}}_{\pi})={\mathcal{S}}_{\pi}\}.

It is easy to see that
\mathcal{T}_{\pi}
is a group, which fits in the chain of subgroups

\langle\Gamma,\textnormal{Aut}_{{\Bbbk}}(E)\rangle\leq\mathcal{T}_{\pi}\leq\textnormal{Aut}(E).

In fact, by inspecting the possible choices for
\Gamma
and
j(E)
, one explicitly finds that

\mathcal{T}_{\pi}=\begin{cases}\textnormal{Aut}_{{\Bbbk}}(E)&\textnormal{if }j(E)=0\textnormal{ and }|\Gamma|=2,\\
\textnormal{Aut}(E)&\textnormal{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(15)

The next lemma further characterizes the elements of
\mathcal{T}_{\pi}
.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
\mathcal{T}_{\pi}
be defined as above.
For every
\sigma\in\textnormal{Aut}(E)
, we have
\sigma\in\mathcal{T}_{\pi}
if and only if
\exists\varphi_{\sigma}\in\textnormal{Aut}_{{\Bbbk}}({\mathbb{P}}^{1})
such that
\pi\circ\sigma=\varphi_{\sigma}\circ\pi
.

Proof.

Let
\sigma\in\mathcal{T}_{\pi}
. For every
Q\in{\mathbb{P}}^{1}(\overline{{\Bbbk}})
, we consider any
P_{Q}\in E(\overline{{\Bbbk}})
such that
\pi(P_{Q})=Q
, and set

\varphi_{\sigma}:{\mathbb{P}}^{1}(\overline{{\Bbbk}})\to{\mathbb{P}}^{1}(\overline{{\Bbbk}}),\quad Q\mapsto\pi\big(\sigma(P_{Q})\big).

This map is well-defined: since
\textnormal{Aut}(E)
is commutative and
\pi
is invariant on
\Gamma
-orbits, the map is independent of the choice of
P_{Q}
.
Since
\textnormal{Aut}(E)
acts linearly on the coordinates of
(x,y)\in E(\overline{{\Bbbk}})
, then we conclude that
\varphi_{\sigma}
is a scalar map, which satisfies
\pi\circ\sigma=\varphi_{\sigma}\circ\pi
by construction.
Moreover, a direct inspection of all the possible choices for
\Gamma
and
j(E)
shows that it is defined over
{\Bbbk}
, thus
\varphi_{\sigma}\in\textnormal{Aut}_{{\Bbbk}}({\mathbb{P}}^{1})
.

On the other hand, for each
P\in{\mathcal{S}}_{\pi}
, we have
\pi\big(\sigma(P)\big)=\varphi_{\sigma}\big(\pi(P)\big)\in{\mathbb{P}}^{1}({\Bbbk})
,
therefore
\sigma(P)\in{\mathcal{S}}_{\pi}
, so
\sigma({\mathcal{S}}_{\pi})\subseteq{\mathcal{S}}_{\pi}
.
Conversely, for every
P\in{\mathcal{S}}_{\pi}
, we have

\pi\circ\sigma^{-1}(P)=\varphi_{\sigma}^{-1}\circ\pi(P)\in{\mathbb{P}}^{1}({\Bbbk}),

hence
\sigma^{-1}(P)\in{\mathcal{S}}_{\pi}
, i.e.,
{\mathcal{S}}_{\pi}\subseteq\sigma({\mathcal{S}}_{\pi})
.
∎

Although
{\mathcal{S}}_{\pi}
is defined over a field that is potentially larger than
{\Bbbk}
, we prove that it is completely determined by isomorphic copies of
E^{(D)}({\Bbbk})
, for certain twists
E^{(D)}
of
E
.

Remark 5.2
.

Each twist intrinsically contains the information about
any
choice among the
n
-th roots of
D
, as it corresponds to a 1-cocycle in
H^{1}\big(\textnormal{Gal}(\overline{{\Bbbk}},{\Bbbk}),\textnormal{Aut}(E)\big)

[
Sil09
, §X.2-5]
.
We now need to make this choice semi-explicit. Namely, for any integer factorization
n=cm
, we define

\iota_{D^{m}}:E^{(D^{m})}\to E,\quad(x,y)\mapsto\big(D^{-2/c}x,\;D^{-3/c}y\big).

This corresponds to fixing
(D^{m})^{1/n}=D^{1/c}
, for any choice of
c
-th root of
D
.
Concretely, this means that among the
m
-th roots of
D^{m}
, we always pick
D
itself.
The choice of
D^{1/c}\in\overline{{\Bbbk}}
is arbitrary, but it is fixed once and for all for every
D\in{\Bbbk}^{*}/({\Bbbk}^{*})^{c}
, which means that we associate a unique embedding
\iota_{D^{m}}:E^{(D^{m})}\to E
to each twist
E^{(D^{m})}
.
Choosing a different root is the same as composing
\iota_{D^{m}}
by a suitable automorphism of
E
.

Theorem 5.3
.

Let
E(\overline{{\Bbbk}})\subset{\mathbb{P}}^{2}(\overline{{\Bbbk}})
be defined by
y^{2}=x^{3}+Ax+B
, with
A,B\in{\Bbbk}
.
Let
\Gamma\subseteq\textnormal{Aut}(E)
and
\pi:E\to E/\Gamma\simeq{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
be one of the four maps of
Remark

2.10
.
Let also
c=|\Gamma|
and
m=[\textnormal{Aut}(E):\Gamma]
.
Then

{\mathcal{S}}_{\pi}=\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\in\mathcal{T}_{\pi}/\textnormal{Aut}_{{\Bbbk}}(E)\\
D\in{\Bbbk}^{*}/({\Bbbk}^{*})^{c}\end{subarray}}\sigma\circ\iota_{D^{m}}\big(E^{(D^{m})}({\Bbbk})\big).

Proof.

Let
n=|\textnormal{Aut}(E)|=cm
. We prove the inclusions separately.

\supseteq)
Let
D\in{\Bbbk}^{*}
and
Q=(x,y)\in E^{(D^{m})}({\Bbbk})
.
Let
P=\iota_{D^{m}}(Q)=(\frac{x}{D^{2/c}},\frac{y}{D^{3/c}})
(
Remark

5.2
).
Since
E\simeq E^{(D)}
over
\overline{{\Bbbk}}
, we have
\textnormal{Aut}(E)=\textnormal{Aut}(E^{(D)})
, hence the projection
\pi^{(D)}:E^{(D)}\to E^{(D)}/\Gamma\simeq{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
is defined by the same rational function of
\pi
.
A straightforward computation then shows that

\pi(P)=\frac{\pi^{(D^{m})}(Q)}{D}\in{\mathbb{P}}^{1}({\Bbbk})\quad\implies\quad P\in{\mathcal{S}}_{\pi}.

For every
\sigma\in\mathcal{T}_{\pi}
, we have
\sigma(P)\in{\mathcal{S}}_{\pi}
, hence the post-composition with
\sigma
preserves the inclusion.

\subseteq)
Let
(x,y)\in{\mathcal{S}}_{\pi}
. In all possible cases, we explicitly exhibit a
D\in{\Bbbk}^{*}
such that the point

Q=(xD^{2/c},yD^{3/c})\in E^{(D^{m})}({\Bbbk})

satisfies
(x,y)=\sigma\circ\iota_{D^{m}}(Q)
, for a suitable choice of
\sigma\in\mathcal{T}_{\pi}
.

c=2
:

When the projection is unramified (i.e.,
y\neq 0
), let
D=y^{2}\in{\Bbbk}^{*}
.
Since
x=\pi(x,y)\in{\Bbbk}
, then
y^{2}\in{\Bbbk}^{*}
, and we directly verify that
Q=(xy^{2},y^{4})\in E^{(D^{m})}({\Bbbk})
.
In this case, we obtain
\iota_{D^{m}}(Q)=(x,\pm y)
, depending on the choice of the square root, hence we retrieve
(x,y)
up to the action of
[-1]\in\mathcal{T}_{\pi}
.
If
y=0
, we have
(x,0)\in E({\Bbbk})
, hence the choice
D=1
works.

c=3
:

We necessarily have
j(E)=0
, therefore
(n,m)=(6,2)
.
If
x\neq 0
, then
D=x^{3}=y^{2}-B\in{\Bbbk}^{*}
, since
y=\pi(x,y)\in{\Bbbk}
.
We immediately check that
Q=(x^{3},x^{3}y)\in E^{(D^{2})}({\Bbbk})
, and that
\iota_{D^{2}}(Q)\in\{(\zeta_{3}^{i}x,y)\}_{i\in\{0,1,2\}}
, hence there exists an element of
\textnormal{Aut}(E)=\mathcal{T}_{\pi}
(
eq.

15
) that brings it to
(x,y)
.
If
x=0
, then we choose
D=1
and
Q=(x,y)
.

c=4
:

In this case, we have
j(E)=1728
, therefore
(n,m)=(4,1)
.
Since
x^{2}=\pi(x,y)\in{\Bbbk}
, if
y\neq 0
we consider
D=y^{4}=(x^{3}+Ax)^{2}\in{\Bbbk}^{*}
.
We then directly verify that
Q=(xy^{2},y^{4})\in E^{(D)}({\Bbbk})
is the requested point, as
\iota_{D^{m}}(Q)\in\{\sigma(x,y)\}_{\sigma\in\textnormal{Aut}(E)}=\{\sigma(x,y)\}_{\sigma\in\mathcal{T}_{\pi}}
(
eq.

15
).
If
y=0
, then we have two possibilities: either
x=0
, hence we consider
D=1
and
Q=(0,0)
, or
x\neq 0
, hence we consider
D=x^{2}\in{\Bbbk}^{*}
and
Q=(x^{2},0)
.

c=6
:

This case occurs with
j(E)=0
, therefore
(n,m)=(6,1)
and
x^{3}=\pi(x,y)\in{\Bbbk}
, then also
y^{2}\in{\Bbbk}
.
When
xy\neq 0
, we consider
D=x^{6}y^{6}\in{\Bbbk}^{*}
and
Q=(x^{3}y^{2},x^{3}y^{4})\in E^{(D)}({\Bbbk})
, so that
\iota_{D}(Q)\in\{\sigma(x,y)\}_{\sigma\in\textnormal{Aut}(E)}=\{\sigma(x,y)\}_{\sigma\in\mathcal{T}_{\pi}}
(
eq.

15
).
When
x=0\neq y
, we consider
D=y^{2}\in{\Bbbk}^{*}
and
Q=(0,y^{2})
, while if
x\neq 0=y
, we consider
D=x^{6}\in{\Bbbk}^{*}
and
Q=(x^{3},0)
.

Finally, we note that
\sigma\circ\iota_{D^{m}}\big(E^{(D^{m})}({\Bbbk})\big)
does not change by varying
D
by any element in
({\Bbbk}^{*})^{c}
, and
\sigma
by any element in
\textnormal{Aut}_{{\Bbbk}}(E)
.
In fact, if
D_{u}=Du^{c}
for some
u\in{\Bbbk}^{*}
, then

\iota_{D_{u}^{m}}\big(E^{(D_{u}^{m})}({\Bbbk})\big)=\iota_{D^{m}}\circ\iota_{u^{n}}\big(E^{(D^{m}u^{n})}({\Bbbk})\big)=\iota_{D^{m}}\big(E^{(D^{m})}({\Bbbk})\big),

hence the points in
\iota_{D_{u}^{m}}\big(E^{(D_{u}^{m})}({\Bbbk})\big)
were already covered by
\iota_{D^{m}}\big(E^{(D^{m})}({\Bbbk})\big)
.
Similarly, when
\sigma
is replaced by
\tau\circ\sigma
for some
\tau\in\textnormal{Aut}_{{\Bbbk}}(E)
, we have

\tau\circ\sigma\circ\iota_{D^{m}}\big(E^{(D^{m})}({\Bbbk})\big)=\sigma\circ\iota_{D^{m}}\Big(\tau\big(E^{(D^{m})}({\Bbbk})\big)\Big)=\sigma\circ\iota_{D^{m}}\big(E^{(D^{m})}({\Bbbk})\big),

where the last equality follows from
\textnormal{Aut}_{{\Bbbk}}(E)=\textnormal{Aut}_{{\Bbbk}}(E^{(D)})
.
∎

Remark 5.4
.

By
Theorem

5.3
,
{\mathcal{S}}_{\pi}
depends on the base field
{\Bbbk}
in two, essentially independent, ways.
The first union adjusts the (arbitrary) choice of
n
-th roots in
\overline{{\Bbbk}}
, while the second selects a suitable set of twists of
E
.
Since
|\mathcal{T}_{\pi}/\textnormal{Aut}_{{\Bbbk}}(E)|\leq 3
, the first union is typically small, and it is trivial for
j(E)\neq 0,1728
.
By
Lemma

5.1
, we know that this union only contributes to a multiple covering of
{\mathbb{P}}^{1}({\Bbbk})
, hence it is not essential from a dynamical perspective (although it is needed for the set equality). The second union, instead, defines a typically irredundant covering of
{\mathbb{P}}^{1}({\Bbbk})
, and might well consist of infinitely many components (e.g., when
{\Bbbk}={\mathbb{Q}}
).

Remark 5.5
.

Theorem

5.3
establishes a general decomposition that can be used to study Lattès fitting a reduced Lattès diagram, with
E
in (short) Weierstrass form.
In particular,
Corollary

5.10
provides an algebraic interpretation of the sets
A_{n},B_{n}
defined in
[
Ugo14
, §3]
and
[
Ugo18
, §3]
.
However, we remark that in our setting, the considered curve does not need to be ordinary. In fact, when
E_{k}
is supersingular, this implies interesting dynamical contraints (see, e.g.,
Section

7.3
).

The following proposition shows that the union of
Theorem

5.3
is essentially minimal, as two distinct components intersect at most in points with a zero coordinate.

Proposition 5.6
.

Let
E
,
c
, and
m
be as in
Theorem

5.3
.
Let
D_{1},D_{2}\in{\Bbbk}^{*}/({\Bbbk}^{*})^{c}
,
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}\in\mathcal{T}_{\pi}/\textnormal{Aut}_{{\Bbbk}}(E)
, and

[x:y:z]\in\sigma_{1}\circ\iota_{D_{1}^{m}}\big(E^{(D_{1}^{m})}({\Bbbk})\big)\cap\sigma_{2}\circ\iota_{D_{2}^{m}}\big(E^{(D_{2}^{m})}({\Bbbk})\big).

Then either
xyz=0
, or
D_{1}=D_{2}
and
\sigma_{1}=\sigma_{2}
.

Proof.

For
z\neq 0
, we can restrict the proof to affine points.
Since
\sigma_{i}\in\textnormal{Aut}(E)
, then there exists
u_{i}\in\overline{{\Bbbk}}^{*}
such that
\sigma_{i}(x,y)=(u_{i}^{2}x,u_{i}^{3}y)
.
Therefore, there are
(x_{i},y_{i})\in E^{(D_{i}^{m})}({\Bbbk})
such that

(u_{1}^{2}D_{1}^{-2/c}x_{1},u_{1}^{3}D_{1}^{-3/c}y_{1})=(x,y)=(u_{2}^{2}D_{2}^{-2/c}x_{2},u_{2}^{3}D_{2}^{-3/c}y_{2}).

If
xy\neq 0
, then
x_{1}x_{2}y_{1}y_{2}\neq 0

\frac{D_{1}^{2/c}}{D_{2}^{2/c}}\left(\frac{u_{2}}{u_{1}}\right)^{2}=\frac{x_{1}}{x_{2}}\in{\Bbbk}^{*},\qquad\frac{D_{1}^{3/c}}{D_{2}^{3/c}}\left(\frac{u_{2}}{u_{1}}\right)^{3}=\frac{y_{1}}{y_{2}}\in{\Bbbk}^{*}.

Dividing the second equation by the first, we get
\frac{D_{1}^{1/c}u_{2}}{D_{2}^{1/c}u_{1}}\in{\Bbbk}^{*}
.
We straightforwardly check that, for every possible value of

, i.e.,
D_{1}^{1/c}=D_{2}^{1/c}
.
Therefore, we conclude that also

, which implies that

,
namely
\sigma_{1}=\sigma_{2}\in\mathcal{T}_{\pi}/\textnormal{Aut}_{{\Bbbk}}(E)
.
∎

5.3
Projecting the graph

In this section, we describe how, for a reduced Lattès diagram, the functional graph
\langle{\mathcal{S}}_{\pi},\psi\rangle
projects to
\langle{\mathbb{P}}^{1}({\Bbbk}),\phi\rangle
. We stress that the projected graph is not necessarily as regular as those arising from group endomorphisms, since
{\mathcal{S}}_{\pi}
need not be a group, and
\pi
can identify some of its parts.

The following lemma refines the notion of compatibility between
\psi
and
\Gamma\subseteq\textnormal{Aut}(E)
, which was introduced in
Remark

3.23
.

Lemma 5.13
.

There exists
\epsilon\in\{\pm 1\}
such that, for every
\gamma\in\Gamma
and
d\geq 0
, we have

\psi^{(d)}\circ\gamma=\gamma^{\epsilon^{d}}\circ\psi^{(d)}.

Proof.

For
d=0
the statement is trivial.
Let us now prove the case
d=1
.

We claim that, for every
\gamma\in\Gamma
, there exists a unique
\theta_{\gamma}\in\Gamma
such that

\psi\circ\gamma=\theta_{\gamma}\circ\psi.

To prove this, we observe that the reduced Lattès structure implies that every
R\in E(\overline{{\Bbbk}})

is sent to
{\mathcal{O}}
by one of the morphisms
\{\psi\circ\gamma-\gamma^{\prime}\circ\psi\}_{\gamma^{\prime}\in\Gamma}
. In particular, one of them, say
\theta_{\gamma}
, must map infinitely many points to
{\mathcal{O}}
and is therefore the zero morphism. To conclude the proof of our claim, we need to check that
\theta_{\gamma}
is unique: if
\psi\circ\gamma=\theta_{\gamma}\circ\psi=\theta^{\prime}_{\gamma}\circ\psi
, one has
(\theta_{\gamma}-\theta^{\prime}_{\gamma})\circ\psi=0
, which implies
\theta_{\gamma}=\theta^{\prime}_{\gamma}
by surjectivity of
\psi

[
Sil09
, Thm. I.2.3]
.

One can directly check that

\theta:\Gamma\to\Gamma,\quad\gamma\mapsto\theta_{\gamma},

is a group homomorphism.
A similar argument to that used above proves that
\theta
is also injective, as

\theta_{\gamma}=1\quad\implies\quad\psi\circ\gamma=\psi\quad\implies\quad\psi\circ(\gamma-1)=0\quad\implies\gamma=1.

Therefore,
\theta\in\textnormal{Aut}(\Gamma)
. Since
\Gamma\simeq\mu_{i}
for some
{i\in\{2,3,4,6\}}
, and
\textnormal{Aut}(\mu_{i})=\langle x\mapsto x^{-1}\rangle
, this implies that either

\theta(\gamma)=\gamma
or
\theta(\gamma)=\gamma^{-1}

for all
\gamma\in\Gamma
, which is the case
d=1
.
The case
d>1
is proven by induction, since

\psi^{(d+1)}\circ\gamma=\psi\circ(\psi^{(d)}\circ\gamma)=(\psi\circ\gamma^{\epsilon^{d}})\circ\psi^{(d)}=(\gamma^{\epsilon^{d}})^{\epsilon}\circ\psi\circ\psi^{(d)}=\gamma^{\epsilon^{d+1}}\circ\psi^{(d+1)},

which is the inductive step. ∎

As a consequence, the action of
\Gamma
respects periodic elements and the depth in ancestor trees.

Lemma 5.14
.

For every
\gamma\in\Gamma
and
P\in E(\overline{{\Bbbk}})
, we have

P\in\mathrm{Per}(\psi)\quad\iff\quad\gamma(P)\in\mathrm{Per}(\psi).

Proof.

If
P\in\mathrm{Per}(\psi)
, then there is
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
\psi^{(k)}(P)=P
.

Lemma

5.13
yields

\psi^{(2k)}\circ\gamma(P)=\gamma^{\epsilon^{2k}}\circ\psi^{(2k)}(P)=\gamma(P),

therefore
\gamma(P)\in\mathrm{Per}(\psi)
.
The reverse implication follows by replacing
P
by
\gamma(P)
, and
\gamma
by
\gamma^{-1}
.
∎

Lemma 5.15
.

Let
\epsilon\in\{\pm 1\}
be defined as in
Lemma

5.13
.
For every
\gamma\in\Gamma
,
P\in E(\overline{{\Bbbk}})
and
Q\in\tau_{P}
, let
d=d_{P}(Q)
.
Then we have

\gamma^{\epsilon^{d}}(Q)\in\tau_{\gamma(P)}\quad\textnormal{and}\quad d_{\gamma(P)}\big(\gamma^{\epsilon^{d}}(Q)\big)=d.

Moreover,
\gamma(P)\in\tau_{P}
if and only if
\gamma(P)=P
.

Proof.

The statement trivially holds when
d=0
, so we assume
d>0
.

Lemma

5.13
yields

\psi^{(d)}\circ\gamma^{\epsilon^{d}}(Q)=\gamma^{\epsilon^{2d}}\circ\psi^{(d)}(Q)=\gamma(P).

(16)

By
Definition

4.3
, none of
\{\psi^{(k)}(Q)\}_{0\leq k<d}
is periodic, then by
Lemma

5.14
none of
\{\psi^{(k)}\circ\gamma^{\prime}(Q)\}_{0\leq k<d}
can be periodic, for any choice of
\gamma^{\prime}\in\Gamma
.
Thus, we conclude that
\gamma^{\epsilon^{d}}(Q)\in\tau_{\gamma(P)}
.

We now show that
\gamma(P)\notin\{\psi^{(k)}\circ\gamma^{\epsilon^{d}}(Q)\}_{0\leq k<d}
, which implies the equality between distances.
Let us assume by contradiction that
\psi^{(k)}\circ\gamma^{\epsilon^{d}}(Q)=\gamma(P)
for some
0\leq k<d
. Then by
eq.

16
we have

\gamma(P)=\psi^{(d)}\circ\gamma^{\epsilon^{d}}(Q)=\psi^{(d-k)}\circ\psi^{(k)}\circ\gamma^{\epsilon^{d}}(Q)=\psi^{(d-k)}\circ\gamma(P),

therefore
\gamma(P)=\psi^{(k)}\circ\gamma^{\epsilon^{d}}(Q)=\gamma^{\epsilon^{d+k}}\circ\psi^{(k)}(Q)
is periodic.
By
Lemma

5.14
, we conclude that also
\psi^{(k)}(Q)
was periodic, which contradicts the fact that
\tau_{P}\setminus\{P\}
cannot contain periodic points.

For the last statement, let
\gamma(P)\in\tau_{P}
and

n=d_{P}(\gamma(P))
.
Let us assume by contradiction that
n>0
.
Lemma

5.13
gives

\psi^{(n)}\circ\gamma=\gamma^{\epsilon^{n}}\circ\psi^{(n)}
,
which implies

\psi^{(n)}(P)=\psi^{(n)}\big(\gamma(P)\big)=\gamma^{-\epsilon^{n}}(P).

We also have

\displaystyle\psi^{(2n)}(P)=\psi^{(n)}\bigl(\gamma^{-\epsilon^{n}}(P)\bigr)=\gamma^{-\epsilon^{2n}}\bigl(\psi^{(n)}(P)\bigr)=\gamma^{-(1+\epsilon^{n})}(P).

Since
\psi^{(2n)}
commutes with
\gamma
by
Lemma

5.13
, iterating the above identity
\mathrm{ord}(\gamma)
times yields

\psi^{(2n\cdot\mathrm{ord}(\gamma))}(P)=\gamma^{-\mathrm{ord}(\gamma)(1+\epsilon^{n})}(P)=P.

Hence
P
is periodic, and therefore so is
\gamma(P)
by
Lemma

5.14
.
This implies that
\gamma(P)\in\tau_{P}\setminus\{P\}
is a periodic point, contradicting the definition of
\tau_{P}
.
Thus we conclude that
n=0
, and consequently
\gamma(P)=P
.
The converse implication is trivial.
∎

Lemma 5.16
.

Let
P\in{\mathcal{S}}_{\pi}
. Then

•

if
Q\in\tau_{P}\cap{\mathcal{S}}_{\pi}
, then
d_{P}(Q)=d_{\pi(P)}(\pi(Q))
.

•

P\in\mathrm{Per}(\psi)\iff\pi(P)\in\mathrm{Per}(\phi).
If this is the case, let
k
be the period of
\pi(P)
and
\gamma\in\Gamma
such that
\psi^{(k)}(P)=\gamma(P)
. Then, letting
\epsilon\in\{\pm 1\}
be defined as in
Lemma

5.13
, the period of
P
is
Nk
with

N=\begin{cases}1\qquad&\text{if $\gamma(P)=P$}\\
2\qquad&\text{if $\gamma(P)\neq P$ and $\epsilon^{k}=-1$}\\
\min_{m\in{\mathbb{N}}_{>0}}\{\gamma^{m}(P)=P\}\qquad&\text{otherwise}.\\
\end{cases}

Proof.

It is clear that
d_{P}(Q)\geq d_{\pi(P)}(\pi(Q))
.
By contradiction, let us assume that the inequality is strict, i.e.
\phi^{(d)}(\pi(Q))=\pi(P)
for some
d<d_{P}(Q)
.
By commutativity of the Lattès diagram, this is equivalent to saying that
\pi(\psi^{(d)}(Q))=\pi(P)
, that is
\psi^{(d)}(Q)=\gamma(P)\in\tau_{P}
for some
\gamma\in\Gamma
such that
\gamma(P)\neq P
. This cannot happen, as it would contradict
Lemma

5.15
. Therefore,
d_{P}(Q)=d_{\pi(P)}(\pi(Q))
.

If
P\in\mathrm{Per}(\psi)
, then
\psi^{(k)}(P)=P
for some positive integer
k
. Hence
\phi^{(k)}(\pi(P))=\pi(\psi^{(k)}(P))=\pi(P)
. Vice versa, suppose that
\pi(P)
is periodic of period
k
. Then
\psi^{(k)}(P)=\gamma(P)
for some
\gamma\in\Gamma
. If
\gamma(P)=P
, the statement follows immediately. Otherwise, if
\epsilon^{k}=-1
,
Lemma

5.13
yields

\psi^{(2k)}(P)=\psi^{(k)}(\gamma(P))=\gamma^{\epsilon^{k}+1}(P)=P.

If
\epsilon^{k}=1
,
Lemma

5.13
implies that, for every
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
,

\psi^{(mk)}(P)=\gamma^{m}(P),

which completes the proof.
∎

Proposition 5.19
.

Let
P,Q\in{\mathcal{S}}_{\pi}
with
\pi(P)=\pi(Q)
.
Then we have an isomorphism of ancestor trees
\tau_{P}\simeq\tau_{Q}
in
E(\overline{{\Bbbk}})
, which restricts to an isomorphism of arborescences

\tau_{P}\cap{\mathcal{S}}_{\pi}\simeq\tau_{Q}\cap{\mathcal{S}}_{\pi}.

Moreover, they project to the same arborescence
\pi(\tau_{P}\cap{\mathcal{S}}_{\pi})=\pi(\tau_{Q}\cap{\mathcal{S}}_{\pi})
in
{\mathbb{P}}^{1}({\Bbbk})
.

Proof.

Since
\pi(P)=\pi(Q)
, there exists
\gamma\in\Gamma
such that
Q=\gamma(P)
. By
Lemma

5.13
, there exists
\epsilon\in\{\pm 1\}
such that
\psi\circ\gamma=\gamma^{\epsilon}\circ\psi.
Letting
d(H)=d_{P}(H)
for brevity, we now show that

\Phi:\tau_{P}\to\tau_{Q},\quad H\mapsto\gamma^{\epsilon^{d(H)}}(H)

is a well-defined isomorphism of ancestor trees. The map
\Phi
is well defined by
Lemma

5.15
with
Q=\gamma(P)
. For the same reason, we have a well-defined set-theoretical inverse

\Phi^{-1}:\tau_{Q}\to\tau_{P},\quad H\mapsto\gamma^{-\epsilon^{d(H)}}(H).

We now prove that
\Phi
respects the edges. Let us consider
P_{1},P_{2}\in\tau_{P}
, with
\psi(P_{1})=P_{2}
and
d(P_{2})=d(P_{1})-1
. Hence, we obtain

\Phi(P_{2})=\gamma^{\epsilon^{d(P_{2})}}(P_{2})=\gamma^{\epsilon^{d(P_{1})-1}}\circ\psi(P_{1})=\psi\circ\gamma^{\epsilon^{d(P_{1})}}(P_{1})=\psi\circ\Phi(P_{1}).

Vice versa, if
\psi\circ\Phi(P_{1})=\Phi(P_{2})
then

\gamma^{\epsilon^{d(P_{2})}}(P_{2})=\psi\circ\gamma^{\epsilon^{d(P_{1})}}(P_{1})=\gamma^{\epsilon^{d(P_{1})-1}}\circ\psi(P_{1}).

Since
\Phi
preserves the distance from the root by
Lemma

5.15
, we have
d(P_{1})=d(P_{2})+1
, from which we conclude
\psi(P_{1})=P_{2}
.

Since
\pi\circ\Phi(H)=\pi(H)
, the isomorphism
\Phi
restricts to
{\mathcal{S}}_{\pi}
, i.e.
H\in\tau_{P}\cap{\mathcal{S}}_{\pi}
if and only if
\Phi(H)\in\tau_{Q}\cap{\mathcal{S}}_{\pi}
. Finally,
\pi(\tau_{P}\cap{\mathcal{S}}_{\pi})=\pi(\tau_{Q}\cap{\mathcal{S}}_{\pi})
and it is an arborescence because
\phi
is a Lattès map and no cycles/loops are created by the projection of
\tau_{P}\cap{\mathcal{S}}_{\pi}
by
\pi
thanks to
Lemma

5.16
.
∎

Proposition 5.21
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a connected component of
\langle{\mathcal{S}}_{\pi},\psi\rangle
containing periodic points.
Then

(i)

\pi
projects
\mathcal{G}
onto a connected component of
\langle{\mathbb{P}}^{1}({\Bbbk}),\phi\rangle
.

(ii)

This projection preserves the depth of elements and maps the cycle of periodic points in

into a cycle whose length is
N
times shorter, where

is given by
Lemma

5.16
for any periodic point

of
\mathcal{G}
.

(iii)

This projection is an isomorphism of arborescences rooted in periodic points, with the following possible exception: the branches arriving at points in
\ker(\psi\circ(\gamma-1))
for

might be identified.

Proof.

(i)
: The reduced Lattès structure of

implies that
\pi
is a graph epimorphism

In particular, the image of

(ii)

.

(iii)

If the indegree is not preserved for a certain vertex

In particular, the latter equality implies that there exists

, so that the former equality implies

. The whole branches arriving at

.

6.1
Loops

The next lemma characterizes the
loops
(i.e., cycles of length
1
) in
\langle E_{k}(\overline{{\Bbbk}}),\psi_{k}\rangle
.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
k\in{\Bbbk}^{*}
and
P\in E_{k}(\overline{{\Bbbk}})
. Then

\psi_{k}(P)=P\quad\iff\quad P\in E_{k}[2]=\left\{{\mathcal{O}},\left(-\zeta_{3}^{i}\sqrt[3]{\frac{k}{4}},0\right)\right\}_{i\in\{0,1,2\}}.

The corresponding loops in the Hessian graphs are
\infty
and
-\frac{k}{4}
.

Proof.

The condition
\psi_{k}(P)=P
is equivalent to
P\in\ker(\psi_{k}-[1])
. By
Definition

3.14
, we have

\psi_{k}-[1]=[2](\rho-[1])=[2]\rho^{2}\in\mathrm{End}(E_{k}),

and
\ker([2]\rho^{2})=\ker([2])
, since
\rho\in\textnormal{Aut}(E_{k})
.
It is clear that
E_{k}[2]=\{{\mathcal{O}},(-\zeta_{3}^{i}\sqrt[3]{k/4},0)\}_{i\in\{0,1,2\}}
, and these points are mapped to
\infty
or
-\frac{k}{4}
after
\pi_{\mathrm{H}}
.
∎

Remark 6.2
.

We note that there may be loops in
\langle{\mathbb{P}}^{1}({\Bbbk}),\mathrm{H}\rangle
(
Lemma

2.14
) that do not arise from loops in
\langle E_{k}(\overline{{\Bbbk}}),\psi\rangle
(
Lemma

6.1
).
This happens when the cubing map
M_{3}
is not injective over
{\mathbb{P}}^{1}({\Bbbk})
, and
\pi_{\mathrm{H}}
sends non-trivial cycles of
\langle{\mathcal{S}}_{\mathrm{H}_{k}},\psi\rangle
into loops of
\langle{\mathbb{P}}^{1}({\Bbbk}),\mathrm{H}\rangle
.

6.2
Cycles and depth

A point
P\in E_{k}(\overline{{\Bbbk}})
is referred to as a
torsion
point if
\mathrm{ord}(P)<\infty
, i.e., it is
m
-torsion for some
m\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{>0}
.

Proposition 6.3
.

Let
k\in{\Bbbk}^{*}
. A point
P\in E_{k}(\overline{{\Bbbk}})
is periodic in
\langle E_{k}(\overline{{\Bbbk}}),\psi_{k}\rangle
if and only if it is a torsion point and

{\gcd}\big(\mathrm{ord}(P),3\big)=1.

Proof.

By
Lemma

3.15
we have
\psi_{k}^{(2)}=[-3]
, then

\mathrm{ord}\big(\psi_{k}\circ\psi_{k}(P)\big)=\begin{cases}\mathrm{ord}(P)/3&\textnormal{if }\mathrm{ord}(P)<\infty\textnormal{ and }3\ |\ \mathrm{ord}(P),\\
\mathrm{ord}(P)&\textnormal{otherwise. }\end{cases}

(17)

If
P
is periodic there exists
e\in{\mathbb{N}}_{>0}
such that
\psi^{(e)}(P)=P
, then also
[(-3)^{e}]P=P
, so
P
is a torsion point.
If
e=1
, then
\psi_{k}(P)=P
, hence
[2]P={\mathcal{O}}
by
Lemma

6.1
, so
{\gcd}\big(\mathrm{ord}(P),3\big)=1
.
If
e\geq 2
, then
P=\psi_{k}^{(e)}(P)=\psi_{k}^{(e-2)}([-3]P)
.
Since
\psi_{k}
is a group endomorphism, then
\mathrm{ord}\big(\psi_{k}(P)\big)\ |\ \mathrm{ord}(P)
, so in particular we must have
\mathrm{ord}(P)=\mathrm{ord}([-3]P)
, hence
3\nmid\mathrm{ord}(P)
by
eq.

17
.

On the other side, if
P
is
a torsion point
and
{\gcd}\big(\mathrm{ord}(P),3\big)=1
, then there exists the multiplicative order
e\in{\mathbb{N}}_{>0}
of
-3
modulo
\mathrm{ord}(P)
, namely
(-3)^{e}\equiv 1\bmod\mathrm{ord}(P)
. Therefore

\psi_{k}^{(2e)}(P)=[(-3)^{e}]P=P,

hence
P
is periodic.
∎

Proposition 6.4
.

Let
k\in{\Bbbk}^{*}
,
P\in E_{k}(\overline{{\Bbbk}})
be a preperiodic point and
d=d_{\textnormal{Per}(\psi_{k})}(P)
. Then

\mathrm{ord}(P)=3^{\lceil d/2\rceil}N,

where
N=\mathrm{ord}\big(\psi_{k}^{(d)}(P)\big)
and
{\gcd}(N,3)=1
.

Proof.

Since
\psi_{k}^{(d)}(P)
is periodic, by
Proposition

6.3
it is sufficient to prove that

\mathrm{ord}(P)=3^{\lceil d/2\rceil}\mathrm{ord}\big(\psi_{k}^{(d)}(P)\big).

(18)

We recall again that
\psi_{k}^{(2)}=[-3]
by
Lemma

3.15
.
If
d=2d^{\prime}
is even, then by the minimality of
d
and
eq.

17
, it follows that

\mathrm{ord}\big(\psi_{k}^{(d)}(P)\big)=\mathrm{ord}\big([(-3)^{d^{\prime}}]P\big)=\mathrm{ord}(P)/3^{d^{\prime}},

which proves
eq.

18
.
If
d=2d^{\prime}+1
is odd instead, the same argument shows that

\mathrm{ord}\big(\psi_{k}^{(2d^{\prime})}(P)\big)=\mathrm{ord}\big((-3)^{d^{\prime}}P\big)=\mathrm{ord}(P)/3^{d^{\prime}}.

However, by
Proposition

6.3
and the minimality of
d
, we also have that
3\nmid\mathrm{ord}\big(\psi_{k}^{(d)}(P)\big)
, while
{3\mid\mathrm{ord}\big(\psi_{k}^{(d-1)}(P)\big)}
. Therefore

\mathrm{ord}\big(\psi_{k}^{(d)}(P)\big)=\mathrm{ord}\big(\psi_{k}^{(2d^{\prime})}(P)\big)/3=\mathrm{ord}(P)/3^{d^{\prime}+1},

which is
eq.

18
for
d
odd.
∎

6.3
Projecting the Hessian

In this section we specialize
Proposition

5.21
to describe the projection of
\langle{\mathcal{S}}_{\Lambda_{k}},\psi_{k}\rangle
(resp.
\langle{\mathcal{S}}_{\mathrm{H}_{k}},\psi_{k}\rangle
) through
\pi_{\Lambda}
(resp.
\pi_{\mathrm{H}}
).
The main improvement we obtain for the Hessian map, compared to a generic Lattès map fitting into a reduced Lattès diagram, is the full characterization of the arborescences that are identified after the projection.

Lemma 6.5
.

Let
k\in{\Bbbk}^{*}
and
P,Q\in{\mathcal{S}}_{\Lambda_{k}}
such that
P\neq Q
and
P\neq\psi_{k}(P)=\psi_{k}(Q)\neq Q
. Then

\pi_{\Lambda}(P)=\pi_{\Lambda}(Q)\iff[2]\psi_{k}(P)={\mathcal{O}}.

Proof.

If
\pi_{\Lambda}(P)=\pi_{\Lambda}(Q)
then
P=-Q
, hence
\psi_{k}(P)=\psi_{k}(Q)=-\psi_{k}(P)
, so
[2]\psi_{k}(P)={\mathcal{O}}
.
Conversely, if
[2]\psi_{k}(P)={\mathcal{O}}
, then
\psi_{k}(P)=-\psi_{k}(P)=\psi_{k}(-P)
, hence
P
and
-P
have the same image under
\psi_{k}
. However, by
Lemma

6.1
we also have that
\psi_{k}\big(\psi_{k}(P)\big)=\psi_{k}(P)
and
P\neq-P
. Since
\deg\psi_{k}=3
, then

Q\in\psi_{k}^{-1}\big(\psi_{k}(P)\big)=\{P,-P,\psi_{k}(P)\}.

Thus, since
Q\neq\psi_{k}(P)
, we conclude that
Q=-P
, so
\pi_{\Lambda}(P)=\pi_{\Lambda}(Q)
.
∎

Lemma 6.6
.

Let
k\in{\Bbbk}^{*}
and
P,Q\in{\mathcal{S}}_{\mathrm{H}_{k}}
such that
P\neq Q
and
P\neq\psi_{k}(P)=\psi_{k}(Q)\neq Q
. Then

\pi_{\mathrm{H}}(P)=\pi_{\mathrm{H}}(Q)\iff[2]\psi_{k}(P)={\mathcal{O}}\quad\textnormal{or}\quad\psi_{k}^{(2)}(P)={\mathcal{O}}.

Proof.

If
\pi_{\mathrm{H}}(P)=\pi_{\mathrm{H}}(Q)
then there is
\Phi\in\textnormal{Aut}(E_{k})
such that
P=\Phi(Q)
. By
Corollary

3.22
we have

\Phi\big(\psi_{k}(P)\big)=\psi_{k}\big(\Phi(P)\big)=\psi_{k}(Q)=\psi_{k}(P).

Since
P\neq Q
, the point
\psi_{k}(P)
is then fixed by a non-trivial element of
\textnormal{Aut}(E_{k})
, namely it has a zero coordinate. Thus, it is either a
2
-torsion point or
\pm T_{k}
.
Conversely, if
[2]\psi_{k}(P)={\mathcal{O}}
, then
\pi_{\Lambda}(P)=\pi_{\Lambda}(Q)
as in
Lemma

6.5
.
If, instead,
P=(x,y)
with
x\neq 0\neq y
and
\psi_{k}^{(2)}(P)={\mathcal{O}}
, then
\{(\zeta_{3}^{i}x,\pm y)\}_{i\in\{0,1,2\}}
are six distinct points.
Since
\deg\psi_{k}=3
, we obtain

Q\in\psi_{k}^{-1}\big(\psi_{k}(P)\big)\subseteq\psi_{k}^{-1}(\{\pm T_{k}\})=\{(\zeta_{3}^{i}x,\pm y)\}_{i\in\{0,1,2\}},

which proves that
Q=\Phi(P)
for some
\Phi\in\textnormal{Aut}(E_{k})
, hence
\pi_{\mathrm{H}}(P)=\pi_{\mathrm{H}}(Q)
.
∎

The coverings of
{\mathcal{S}}_{\Lambda_{k}}
and
{\mathcal{S}}_{\mathrm{H}_{k}}
established in
Corollary

5.10
are almost disjoint,
as proved in
Proposition

5.6
.
The next lemma shows that this remains true even after projecting to
{\mathbb{P}}^{1}({\Bbbk})
.
We will prove it only for
\pi_{\mathrm{H}}
, which immediately implies an analogous result for
\pi_{\Lambda}
.

Lemma 6.7
.

Let
k,u,v\in{\Bbbk}^{*}
with
\frac{u}{v}\not\in({\Bbbk}^{*})^{6}
.
Then, for any choice of
u^{\frac{1}{6}},v^{\frac{1}{6}}\in\overline{{\Bbbk}}
, we have

\pi_{\mathrm{H}}\Big(\phi_{u^{-\frac{1}{6}}}\big(E_{uk}({\Bbbk})\big)\Big)\cap\pi_{\mathrm{H}}\Big(\phi_{v^{-\frac{1}{6}}}\big(E_{vk}({\Bbbk})\big)\Big)\subseteq\left\{0,-\frac{k}{4},\infty\right\}.

Proof.

If
P\in\phi_{u^{-\frac{1}{6}}}\big(E_{uk}({\Bbbk})\big)\cap\phi_{v^{-\frac{1}{6}}}\big(E_{vk}({\Bbbk})\big)
, then one of the coordinates of
P
is
0
by
Proposition

5.6
, hence
\pi_{\mathrm{H}}(P)\in\{0,-\frac{k}{4},\infty\}
by
Lemma

6.1
.
Let us assume by contradiction that there are points

P=(x,y)\in\phi_{u^{-\frac{1}{6}}}\big(E_{uk}({\Bbbk})\big)\quad\textnormal{and}\quad Q\in\phi_{v^{-\frac{1}{6}}}\big(E_{vk}({\Bbbk})\big),

having all non-zero coordinates and such that
\pi_{\mathrm{H}}(P)=\pi_{\mathrm{H}}(Q)
, i.e.,
Q\in\{(\zeta_{3}^{i}x,\pm y)\}_{i\in\{0,1,2\}}
.
Thus, we have

\begin{cases}\phi_{u^{\frac{1}{6}}}(P)\in E_{uk}({\Bbbk})&\implies u^{\frac{1}{3}}x,u^{\frac{1}{2}}y\in{\Bbbk}^{*},\\
\phi_{v^{\frac{1}{6}}}(Q)\in E_{vk}({\Bbbk})&\implies v^{\frac{1}{3}}\zeta_{3}^{i}x,\pm v^{\frac{1}{2}}y\in{\Bbbk}^{*}.\end{cases}

The above conditions imply
\frac{u}{v}\in({\Bbbk}^{*})^{2}\cap({\Bbbk}^{*})^{3}=({\Bbbk}^{*})^{6}
, which contradicts the hypothesis.
∎

Proposition 6.8
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a connected component of
\langle{\mathcal{S}}_{\Lambda_{k}},\psi_{k}\rangle
(resp.
\langle{\mathcal{S}}_{\mathrm{H}_{k}},\psi_{k}\rangle
) containing periodic points.
Then
\pi_{\Lambda}
(resp.
\pi_{\mathrm{H}}
) projects
\mathcal{G}
onto a connected component of
\langle{\mathbb{P}}^{1}({\Bbbk}),\Lambda_{k}\rangle
(resp.
\langle{\mathbb{P}}^{1}({\Bbbk}),\mathrm{H}_{k}\rangle
).
This projection maps the cycle of periodic points in
\mathcal{G}
into a cycle whose length is
1
or
2
(resp.
1,2,3
, or
6
) times shorter, and preserves the depth of elements.
Finally, it is an isomorphism of arborescences rooted in periodic points, with the following exceptions:

•

the branches arriving at
2
-torsion points are identified, and

•

(only for
\pi_{\mathrm{H}}
) the branches arriving at
\pm T_{k}
are identified.

Proof.

The first part of the statement follows immediately from the reduced Lattès structure of
\Lambda_{k}
and
\mathrm{H}_{k}
(
Theorem

3.19
) and parts
(i)
and
(ii)
of
Proposition

5.21
.

In particular, the integer
N
from part
(ii)
arises from
Lemma

5.16
, and always divides
|\Gamma|
by
Remark

5.17
.

Finally, the isomorphism of arborescences follows if the projection preserves the indegree of vertices.
By
Lemma

6.5
(resp.
Lemma

6.6
) this holds for all vertices of
{\mathcal{S}}_{\Lambda_{k}}
(resp.
{\mathcal{S}}_{\mathrm{H}_{k}}
) not arriving at
2
-torsion points (resp. or
\pm T_{k}
), while the ancestors of these exceptional points are identified and, consequently, their whole branches are identified by
Proposition

5.19
.
∎

are displayed in

,

, and

).

7
Hessian graphs over finite fields

In this section,
{\Bbbk}
will be a finite field
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
, for a given prime power
q=p^{r}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, with
p\neq 2,3
.
In this case, to every elliptic curve
E
over
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
we can associate its
trace
(of Frobenius)
[
Sil07
, Rmk. V.2.6]
:

\mathrm{tr}(E)=q+1-\left|E({\mathbb{F}}_{q})\right|.

Moreover, as observed in
Remark

5.9
,
both
\langle{\mathbb{P}}^{1}({\Bbbk}),\mathrm{H}_{k}\rangle
and
\langle{\mathbb{P}}^{1}({\Bbbk}),\Lambda_{k}\rangle
can be understood by means of small extensions of
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
.
Indeed, according to
Definition

5.7
, one can directly check that

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}_{\Lambda_{k}}

\displaystyle=\{(x,y)\in E_{k}({\mathbb{F}}_{q^{2}})\ |\ x\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}\}\cup\{{\mathcal{O}}\},

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}_{\mathrm{H}_{k}}

\displaystyle=\{(x,y)\in E_{k}({\mathbb{F}}_{q^{6}})\ |\ x^{3}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}\}\cup\{{\mathcal{O}}\}.

In this setting, we have effective criteria for counting the roots of cubics
[
Dic06
, §2-3]
, which we can employ to characterize the indegree (with respect to
\psi_{k}
) of points whose first entry belongs to a given field.
Such an indegree notably depends only on their second entry.

Proposition 7.1
.

Let
k\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{*}
and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P\in}{\mathcal{S}}={\mathcal{S}}_{\Lambda_{k}}\setminus\{{\mathcal{O}}\}
. Then

|(\psi_{k}|_{{\mathcal{S}}})^{-1}(P)|=1\iff[2]P\neq{\mathcal{O}}\quad\text{and}\quad\begin{cases}P\notin E_{k}({\mathbb{F}}_{q})&\quad\text{if $-3k\in({\mathbb{F}}_{q})^{2}$},\\
P\in E_{k}({\mathbb{F}}_{q})&\quad\text{if $-3k\notin({\mathbb{F}}_{q})^{2}$}.\\
\end{cases}

Proof.

Let
P=(x_{P},y_{P})
.
Since
\psi_{k}
is linear in the second entry, the fiber
(\psi_{k}|_{{\mathcal{S}}})^{-1}(P)
only depends on the number of solutions of

x^{3}+3x_{P}x^{2}+k\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}[x].

By
[
Dic06
, §2]
, such an equation has precisely one solution over
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
if and only if the discriminant of this cubic polynomial, which is

-108k\left(x_{P}^{3}+\frac{k}{4}\right)=(6y_{P})^{2}(-3k),

is not a square in
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
.
This happens if and only if

y_{P}\neq 0\quad\text{and}\quad\begin{cases}y_{P}\notin{\mathbb{F}}_{q}&\quad\text{if $-3k$ is a square in ${\mathbb{F}}_{q}$},\\
y_{P}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}&\quad\text{if $-3k$ is not a square in ${\mathbb{F}}_{q}$},\end{cases}

which proves the statement.
∎

In the remaining, we aim at detailing the structure of
\langle{\mathbb{P}}^{1}({\mathbb{F}}_{q}),\mathrm{H}\rangle
, therefore we fix
k=-6912
(
Remark

3.4
), and we drop it from our notation.
The Lattès structure is then given by the curve

E\ :\ y^{2}=x^{3}-1728,

and its endomorphism
\psi
with kernel generated by
T=(0,24\sqrt{-3})
.
Besides,
-3k=144^{2}
is a square regardless of
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
.
Thus, by
Proposition

7.1
, for every
(x,y)\in{\mathcal{S}}_{\Lambda}
, we have

(\psi|_{{\mathcal{S}}_{\Lambda}})^{-1}(x,y)=1\iff y\not\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}.

Remark 7.2
.

By
Lemma

6.1
, with our choice of
k
, the loops in
\langle E({\mathbb{F}}_{q^{2}}),\psi\rangle
are

{\mathcal{O}},\quad(12,0),\quad(-6+6\sqrt{-3},0),\quad(-6-6\sqrt{-3},0),

which correspond to the loops
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\infty},1728
in
\langle{\mathbb{P}}^{1}({\mathbb{F}}_{q}),\mathrm{H}\rangle
.

Remark 7.3
.

We investigate the dynamics of
\mathrm{H}
instead of
\Lambda
because it potentially contains more information, as observed in
Remark

3.11
.
More precisely, we have a cubic immersion

\langle{\mathbb{P}}^{1}({\mathbb{F}}_{q}),\Lambda\rangle\xrightarrow{M_{3}\circ\Phi_{-4}}\langle{\mathbb{P}}^{1}({\mathbb{F}}_{q}),\mathrm{H}\rangle,

which is an isomorphism if and only if

(see

).

\langle{\mathbb{P}}^{1}({\mathbb{F}}_{q}),\mathrm{H}\rangle

.
In

7.1
Indegrees

We prove that the leaves of Hessian graphs precisely correspond to curves with odd traces.
Moreover, concerning the covering of
{\mathcal{S}}_{\mathrm{H}}
as in
Corollary

5.10
,
these curves
always arise (via
\pi_{\mathrm{H}}
) from points belonging to (the isomorphic image of) cubic twists of
E({\mathbb{F}}_{q})
.

Lemma 7.4
.

Let
j\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{*}
and
E_{(j)}
be any elliptic curve with that
j
-invariant.
Then

2\ |\ \mathrm{tr}(E_{(j)})\quad\iff\quad\mathrm{H}^{-1}(j)\neq\emptyset.

Proof.

The equivalence follows for every
j\neq 1728
by
Proposition

2.13
.
However, any
E_{(1728)}
has at least one
2
-torsion point (hence, even order), and
\mathrm{H}^{-1}(1728)=\{1728,-13824\}
by
Lemma

2.12
, so the equivalence also holds for
j=1728
.
∎

Theorem 7.5
.

Let
j\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}\setminus\{0,1728\}
and
u
be a generator of
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{*}/({\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{*})^{6}
. Then

|\mathrm{H}^{-1}(j)|=1\quad\iff\quad j-1728\not\in({\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{*})^{2}\quad\iff\quad j\in\bigcup_{h\in\{1,3,5\}}\pi_{\mathrm{H}}\left(\phi_{u^{-\frac{h}{6}}}\big(E_{-6912u^{h}}({\mathbb{F}}_{q})\big)\right).

Proof.

Let
R=(x,y)\in{\mathcal{S}}={\mathcal{S}}_{\mathrm{H}}
be such that
\pi_{\mathrm{H}}(R)=x^{3}=j
, hence
{y=\sqrt{j-1728}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{2}}}
.
Since
j\not\in\{1728,\infty\}
, we have
[2]R\neq{\mathcal{O}}
and
\psi(R)\neq R
by
Lemma

6.1
.
From
j\neq 0
we also get
{\psi(R)\neq{\mathcal{O}}}
.
Thus, by applying
Lemma

6.6
, we obtain
|\mathrm{H}^{-1}(j)|\geq|(\psi|_{{\mathcal{S}}})^{-1}(R)|
.
We notice that
|(\psi|_{{\mathcal{S}}})^{-1}(R)|
does not depend on the choice of
R\in\pi_{\mathrm{H}}^{-1}(j)
by
Proposition

5.19
and
by the fact that
\pi
preserves periodicity (
Lemma

5.16
).
By
Proposition

5.21
, for every other
R^{\prime}\in\pi_{\mathrm{H}}^{-1}(j)
, we have
\pi_{\mathrm{H}}(\tau_{R^{\prime}}\cap\mathcal{S})=\pi_{\mathrm{H}}(\tau_{R}\cap\mathcal{S})
, hence in particular
|\mathrm{H}^{-1}(j)|\leq|(\psi|_{{\mathcal{S}}})^{-1}(R)|
.
Thus, we proved that
|\mathrm{H}^{-1}(j)|=|(\psi|_{{\mathcal{S}}})^{-1}(R)|
.

We now show that

|(\psi|_{{\mathcal{S}}})^{-1}(R)|=1\quad\iff\quad j-1728\not\in({\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{*})^{2}.

(19)

Suppose first that
j-1728\in({\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{*})^{2}
, i.e.,
y\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{*}
.
If
(\psi|_{{\mathcal{S}}})^{-1}(R)=\emptyset
, there is nothing to prove.
Given a preimage
(\alpha,\beta)\in{\mathcal{S}}
of
R
, by definition of
\psi
we have

y=-\beta\frac{\alpha^{3}+13824}{3\sqrt{-3}\alpha^{3}}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{*}\quad\implies\quad\sqrt{-3}\beta\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{*}.

From the addition formulae of
E
, one can straightforwardly check that the first entry of
(\alpha,\beta)\pm T
is

\mp\frac{48\sqrt{-3}\beta}{\alpha^{2}},

whose cube is in
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
, since
\alpha^{3}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{*}
.
Therefore,
(\alpha,\beta)\pm T\in(\psi|_{{\mathcal{S}}})^{-1}(R)
, proving that
{|(\psi|_{{\mathcal{S}}})^{-1}(R)|>1}
.
To prove the other implication in
eq.

19
,
let us assume that
y\notin{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{*}
.

By
[
Dic06
, §2]
, there is a unique root
\alpha\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{3}}
of the polynomial
g(z)=z^{3}+3xz^{2}-6912
, as in
Proposition

7.1
.
Since
\psi
is an endomorphism of
E({\mathbb{F}}_{q^{6}})
, there is a unique
\beta\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{6}}
such that
(\alpha,\beta)\in E({\mathbb{F}}_{q^{6}})
and
\psi(\alpha,\beta)=R
.
We only need to prove that
(\alpha,\beta)\in{\mathcal{S}}
, namely

\alpha^{3}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
.

Since
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}\subseteq{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{3}}
is determined by the fixed points of the
q
-Froebenius, for every

\gamma\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{3}}
we have

\gamma^{3}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}\quad\iff\quad{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}(\gamma^{q})^{3}=(\gamma^{3})^{q}=\gamma^{3}\quad\iff}\quad\exists\ i\in\{0,1,2\}\ :\ \gamma^{q}=\zeta_{3}^{i}\gamma.

(20)

Therefore, we have

g(\alpha)^{q}=\alpha^{3q}+3\zeta_{3}^{i}x\alpha^{2q}-6912=(\zeta_{3}^{2i}\alpha^{q})^{3}+3x(\zeta_{3}^{2i}\alpha^{q})^{2}-6912=g(\zeta_{3}^{2i}\alpha^{q}),

so that

and
\zeta_{3}^{2i}\alpha^{q}
are both roots of

. Two cases can occur (depending on

•

if
\zeta_{3}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
, then
\zeta_{3}^{2i}\alpha^{q}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{3}}

was the unique root of

in
{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{3}}
, then

. Therefore, by

, we conclude
\alpha^{3}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
.

•

if
\zeta_{3}\notin{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
, then
the cubing is invertible over
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}

8
A glimpse into cryptography

The regular structure of Hessian graphs over finite fields, as described in
Section

7
, might remind the reader of
(oriented)
\ell
-isogeny graphs
, which are widely studied in the context of post-quantum cryptography. We refer to
[
Koh96
,
Sut13
]
for the study of their structure and to
[
Cas+18
,
DF+23
]
for their use in cryptography.
Finding a cryptographic application for Hessian graphs and understanding their relation with isogeny graphs – if any – is beyond the scope of this paper. However, in this section, we highlight some consequences of our previous results that might motivate future research in this direction.

8.1
Efficient computation of the iterated Hessian

Isogeny-based protocols are typically performed over finite fields of cryptographic size, i.e.,
{\mathbb{F}}_{p}
or
{\mathbb{F}}_{p^{2}}
where
p
is a prime of at least
256
bits.
While evaluating many iterations of a rational map over such fields is computationally hard in general, this task has a polynomial complexity for the Hessian transformation because of its Lattès structure.

Proposition 8.1
.

Let
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
j\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
. Then
\mathrm{H}^{(N)}(j)
can be computed in polynomial time in the sizes of
q
and
N
.

Proof.

By
Proposition

3.10
and
Theorem

3.19
, one can compute
\mathrm{H}^{(N)}(j)
by considering a preimage

P=\pi_{\mathrm{H}}^{-1}(j)\in{\mathcal{S}}_{\mathrm{H}}\subseteq E({\mathbb{F}}_{q^{6}}),

computing
\psi^{(N)}(P)
, and projecting back to
{\mathbb{P}}^{1}({\mathbb{F}}_{q})
via
\pi_{\mathrm{H}}
.
All these operations can be performed in polynomial time.
Specifically,
\pi_{\mathrm{H}}^{-1}(j)
only requires finding a cubic root in
{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{3}}
and a square root in
{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{6}}
, which can be done in polynomial time
[
AMM77
]
.
Moreover, up to computing an additional iteration of
\mathrm{H}
, we can assume
N
to be even.
By
Lemma

3.15
, we have
\psi^{(N)}(P)=\left[-3^{N/2}\bmod|E({\mathbb{F}}_{q^{6}})|\right]P
, which can be computed in polynomial time, both in
N
and
q
.
In fact,
|E({\mathbb{F}}_{q^{6}})|\sim q^{6}
can be found via Schoof’s algorithm
[
Sch95
]
,
which can then be used to reduce
-3^{N/2}
to an integer of size
\sim q^{6}
.
Such a scalar multiplication of
P
can be performed via double-and-add algorithms (see e.g.
[
Sil09
, §XI.1]
).
∎

.

Corollary

5.10
and

3.21
can be leveraged to avoid computations on extensions of
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
, by replacing

with the corresponding

-rational point on a suitable twist of

.
This efficient Hessian computation is detailed in Appendix

,

.

Remark 8.3
.

The same ideas in

8.1
can be applied to efficiently compute the

-th iteration of any Lattès map

•

finding the minimal polynomial

of

, which boils down to computing its trace (see, e.g.,
[

, Thm. 81]
),

•

finding

such that

•

evaluating

(see, e.g.,
[

, Thm. 3]
) and projecting it via

In the Hessian case covered by

,
f(x)=x^{2}+3
and

or

, depending on

Similar methods may be applied to other finite quotients of affine maps (Chebyshev and power maps
[

]
).

8.2
Supersingular points in the Hessian graph

An open problem in isogeny-based cryptography is to produce a (random) supersingular
j
-invariant in such a way that its endomorphism ring remains unknown
[
Boo+24
,
MMP24
]
. This motivates the question of whether supersingular
j
-invariants
exhibit a structured distribution in the Hessian graphs
.
As we will now see, there is in fact some non-trivial necessary condition for a connected component of the Hessian graph to be
supersingular
, i.e., to contain at least one supersingular
j
-invariant.

Lemma 8.4
.

Let
j
be a supersingular
j
-invariant. Then
j\in({\mathbb{F}}_{p^{2}})^{3}
and
j-1728\in({\mathbb{F}}_{p^{2}})^{2}
.

Proof.

The fact that
j
is a cube in
{\mathbb{F}}_{p^{2}}
follows from CM-theory
[
Mor11
, Thm. 2.4]
.
By
[
AAM19
, §3]
, there is an elliptic curve
E_{(j)}:y^{2}=x^{3}+Ax+B
defined over
{\mathbb{F}}_{p^{2}}
with that
j
-invariant and such that
\mathrm{tr}(E_{(j)})=\pm 2p
.
By
[
Sch87
, Lem. 4.8]
, its affine
2
-torsion points
\{(x_{i},0)\}_{i\in\{1,2,3\}}
are defined over
{\mathbb{F}}_{p^{2}}
.
By
[
Cox13
, 206]
, the discriminant
\Delta
of
E_{(j)}
is

\Delta=16\prod_{1\leq i<k\leq 3}(x_{i}-x_{k})^{2},

which is a square in
{\mathbb{F}}_{p^{2}}
.
A straightforward computation gives

j-1728=\frac{2^{10}3^{6}B^{2}}{\Delta}\in({\mathbb{F}}_{p^{2}})^{2}
.
∎

Proposition 8.5
.

The supersingular vertices of
\langle{\mathbb{P}}^{1}(\overline{{\mathbb{F}}_{p}}),\mathrm{H}\rangle
all belong to the subgraph
\pi_{\mathrm{H}}(\langle E({\mathbb{F}}_{p^{2}}),\psi\rangle)
.

Proof.

By
Lemma

8.4
,
j
has a cubic root
x\in{\mathbb{F}}_{p^{2}}
and
j-1728
has a square root
y\in{\mathbb{F}}_{p^{2}}
.
Therefore
(x,y)\in E({\mathbb{F}}_{p^{2}})
and
j=\pi_{\mathrm{H}}([x\colon y\colon 1])
.
Since
\sqrt{-3}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{p^{2}}
, then
\psi
is closed on
E({\mathbb{F}}_{p^{2}})
, hence it projects to a subgraph of
\langle{\mathbb{P}}^{1}(\overline{{\mathbb{F}}_{p}}),\mathrm{H}\rangle
.
∎

Corollary 8.6
.

Every supersingular vertex
j\in{\mathbb{F}}_{p^{2}}\setminus\{0,1728\}
has indegree
3
in
\langle{\mathbb{P}}^{1}({{\mathbb{F}}_{p^{2}}}),\mathrm{H}\rangle
.

Proof.

By
Theorem

7.5
and
Proposition

8.5
, such a
j
cannot have indegree
1
.
Thus, by
Lemma

2.12
, it is either
0
or
3
.
Let
E_{(j)}
be any elliptic curve with
j
-invariant
j
.
Since

is even

, §3]
, then

has an order-

2.13

∎

Finally, since the Hessian preserves the

-torsion of elliptic curves, all the curves in a supersingular component share the same trace modulo

.

Lemma 8.7
.

Let
E
be an elliptic curve over
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
with

. Then

\mathrm{tr}(E)\equiv\mathrm{tr}\big(\mathrm{H}(E)\big)\bmod 3.

By Remark

Therefore, their Frobenius endomorphisms coincide over

In particular, the corresponding characteristic polynomials are equal.
∎

Proposition 8.8
.

Let
j\in{\mathbb{F}}_{p}^{*}
(resp.

) with

be a vertex in a supersingular component of

{\mathbb{F}}_{p}
(resp.

) with that

Then

(resp.

).

The above proposition follows by

.
We defer these results and the proof of

.

9
Conclusion

In this paper, we provided a novel interpretation of the Hessian transformation for plane projective cubics as a rigid Lattès map fitting into a reduced diagram.
This result allows for a description of the global symmetries of Hessian graphs, which had eluded all previous local analysis.

The tools presented in
Sections

2
,
3
,
4
,
5
, and
6
are proven over any field
{\Bbbk}
, provided that
\mathsf{char}({\Bbbk})\neq 2,3
, while the results of
Sections

7
and
8
are finer but require
{\Bbbk}
to be finite.

It is conceivable to expect Hessian graphs over infinite fields to be wilder, as they may involve connected components of types
(ii)
or
(iii)
from
Theorem

4.9
.
Nonetheless, the reduced Lattès structure still poses strong constraints on such infinite dynamical systems, which would deserve further investigation.

The tools developed in
Sections

4
and
5
are quite general and may be readily applied to study reduced Lattès diagrams coming from endomorphisms
\psi
of prime degree, e.g., arising from elliptic curves with CM by
\sqrt{-\ell}
, for some prime
\ell
.
We do not foresee major obstructions as long as
\mathsf{char}({\Bbbk})
is coprime to
\ell
.
To widen the applicability of the proposed methods, we envision two possible research directions: either factoring large degree projections
\pi
through reduced diagrams, or extending the classification of the dynamics of group endomorphisms to those with composite kernel.

The arithmetic properties of
E_{k}
could also provide
heuristics about special families of curves
.
For instance, inspecting the distribution of supersingular curves on Hessian graphs (as in
Section

8.2
) might be relevant for cryptographic applications.

Finally, since the Hesse pencil can be viewed as a model of the level-
3
modular curve
X(3)
(see e.g.
[
AD09
, §2]
), one can aim at rephrasing our approach in the language of modular curves, and consider the Hessian transformation on higher-level
genus-
1
moduli
, such as
X(6)
.

Appendix B
Hessian of twists

Passing from curves to

Appendix C
Tightness of
Theorem

4.9

In this section, we gather examples of groups
G
and endomorphisms
\phi\in\mathrm{End}(G)
with finite prime kernels, whose functional graphs realize every configuration listed by
Theorem

4.9
, regardless of the finiteness of
m
.
Since
{\mathcal{O}}
always constitutes a loop in
\langle G,\phi\rangle
, then
\tau_{{\mathcal{O}}}
is always described by case
(i)
.

We first consider the (cyclic) multiplicative group
K^{*}
of a field
K
, whose identity element is
{\mathcal{O}}=1
.
For a given prime
\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}_{>0}
, we consider the group endomorphism

\phi_{\ell}:K^{*}\to K^{*},\quad x\mapsto x^{\ell}.

•

If
K={\mathbb{F}}_{q}
is finite, every connected component is finite, hence it falls under case
(i)
. Note that
\left|\ker\phi_{\ell}\right|=\ell
if and only if
\ell\mid|{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{*}|
.
Indeed, if we denote by
u
a multiplicative generator of
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{*}
, then
\ker\phi_{\ell}
is generated by
u^{\frac{q-1}{\ell}}
.

•

If
K=\mathbb{R}
and
\ell=2
, then
\tau_{{\mathcal{O}}}=\{1,-1\}
.
Since every positive real number has precisely two square roots,
all connected components other than
\tau_{{\mathcal{O}}}
fall under case
(ii)
.

•

Let
K
be a number field.
If
K
contains an
\ell
-th primitive root of
1
, then
\left|\ker\phi_{\ell}\right|=\ell
.
Since
\textnormal{Norm}_{K|\mathbb{Q}}
commutes with
\phi_{\ell}
, every element of
K^{*}
has finitely many ancestors in
\langle K^{*},\phi_{\ell}\rangle
, so case
(ii)
never occurs.
Finally, since a number field contains only finitely many roots of unity,
then the number of periodic elements is finite.

Notice that the finiteness of preperiodic elements also follows from the
Northcott property
of number fields (see e.g.
[
Nor50
, Thm. 5]
or
[
Sil07
, Thm. 3.12]
, as in
Remark

4.10
).

•

If
K=\mathbb{C}
(or, more generally, any algebraically closed field of characteristic different from
\ell
),
\phi_{\ell}
is everywhere
\ell
-to-1, hence
\tau_{{\mathcal{O}}}
is infinite and every other non-periodic connected component falls under case
(ii)
.

•

If
K=\mathbb{Q}^{\mathrm{ab}}
, i.e., the maximal abelian extension of
\mathbb{Q}
, then
\left|\ker\phi_{\ell}\right|=\ell
and
\tau_{{\mathcal{O}}}
is infinite by the Kronecker-Weber theorem.
We now argue that case
(ii)
cannot occur:
let us denote the
Weil’s height
of
x\in\mathbb{Q}^{\mathrm{ab}}
by
h(x)
.
By
[
Zan18
, Prop. 1.2-(iii)]
, for any
m\in{\mathbb{N}}_{>0}
, we have

h\big(\phi_{\ell}^{(m)}(x)\big)=\ell^{m}h(x).

However, abelian extensions of
\mathbb{Q}
enjoy the
Bogomolov property
, i.e., for every non-zero
x\in\mathbb{Q}^{\mathrm{ab}}
that is not a root of unity, we have a uniform bound
h(x)\geq\epsilon>0

[
AD00
, Thm. 1]
.
Thus, for any given
y=\phi_{\ell}^{(m)}(x)
, we have

\frac{h(y)}{\ell^{m}}=h(x)\geq\frac{\log 5}{12}\quad\implies\quad m\leq\frac{\log\big(\frac{12\,h(y)}{\log 5}\big)}{\log\ell}.

Therefore, all the non-periodic connected components fall within case
(iii)
.

Next, to cover the remaining possible combinations, we consider the following groups:

•

Let
K
be a number field containing an
\ell
-th primitive root of
1
.
We consider
G=\mathbb{R}^{*}\times K^{*}
(with entry-wise multiplication), and

\phi:G\to G,\quad(x,y)\mapsto(x^{3},y^{\ell}).

It is easy to see that
\left|\ker\phi\right|=\ell
.
Since
(\alpha,1)\in G
has infinitely many ancestors for any
\alpha\neq\pm 1
, case
(ii)
appears in
\langle G,\phi\rangle
.
However, points
(\alpha,\beta)\in G
with
|\beta|\neq 1
have only finitely many ancestors and cannot become periodic after a finite number of iterations of
\phi
, therefore they belong to connected components as in case
(iii)
.

•

As above, but replacing
K
with
\mathbb{Q}^{\mathrm{ab}}
. The only difference is that
\tau_{{\mathcal{O}}}
is infinite.

•

Let
\mu=\{e^{\pi iq}\}_{q\in\mathbb{Q}}\subseteq\mathbb{C}
be the multiplicative group of roots of unity, and consider its endomorphism
\phi_{\ell}:x\mapsto x^{\ell}
.
We have
\left|\ker\phi_{\ell}\right|=\ell
and
|\tau_{{\mathcal{O}}}|=\infty
.
The periodic elements in
\langle\mu,\phi_{\ell}\rangle
are of the form
e^{\pi i\frac{n}{m}}
, with
\gcd(m,\ell)=1
.
Thus, every element is preperiodic, so the connected components are all of type
(i)
.
