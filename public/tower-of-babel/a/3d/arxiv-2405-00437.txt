Reduced-order modeling for second-order computational homogenization with applications to geometrically parameterized elastomeric metamaterials
T. Guo, V. G. Kouznetsova, M. G. D. Geers, K. Veroy, O. Rokoš

Abstract
The structural properties of mechanical metamaterials are typically studied with two-scale methods based on computational homogenization. Because such materials have a complex microstructure, enriched schemes such as second-order computational homogenization are required to fully capture their non-linear behavior, which arises from non-local interactions due to the buckling or patterning of the microstructure. In the two-scale formulation, the effective behavior of the microstructure is captured with a representative volume element (RVE), and a homogenized effective continuum is considered on the macroscale. Although an effective continuum formulation is introduced, solving such two-scale models concurrently is still computationally demanding due to the many repeated solutions for each RVE at the microscale level. In this work, we propose a reduced-order model for the microscopic problem arising in second-order computational homogenization, using proper orthogonal decomposition and a novel hyperreduction method that is specifically tailored for this problem and inspired by the empirical cubature method. Two numerical examples are considered, in which the performance of the reduced-order model is carefully assessed by comparing its solutions with direct numerical simulations (entirely resolving the underlying microstructure) and the full second-order computational homogenization model. The reduced-order model is able to approximate the result of the full computational homogenization well, provided that the training data is representative for the problem at hand. Any remaining errors, when compared with the direct numerical simulation, can be attributed to the inherent approximation errors in the computational homogenization scheme. Regarding run times for one thread, speed-ups on the order of 100 are achieved with the reduced-order model as compared to direct numerical simulations.

Introduction

With the recent advances in additive manufacturing, there has been a growing interest in designing and modelling metamaterials which exhibit emerging exotic properties that can be fine-tuned for specific applications. By a careful microstructural design, properties such as negative Poisson’s ratio
[
3
]
, negative compressibility
[
12
]
, or negative refractive index
[
38
]
can be achieved. They can also act as filters that absorb certain bandwidths of frequencies
[
28
]
, or act as acoustic cloaks
[
46
]
. Additionally, such materials have been applied in impact mitigation
[
44
]
or biomedical applications
[
15
,
36
]
. A broad overview on their engineering applications can be found in
[
43
]
.

Studying properties of mechanical metamaterials through direct numerical simulations (DNS) is often challenging since complex microstructural geometries need to be resolved, requiring very fine meshes. In particular, in multi-query contexts such as the design of materials, numerous simulations are required and the computational costs become infeasible. To address this issue, multiscale methods based on computational homogenization (CH)
[
11
,
14
]
are usually employed. These methods involve the separate modeling, discretization, and coupling of a microstructure defined on a representative volume element (RVE) and an effective homogenized macrostructure. The effective continuum does not resolve the complex microstructure on the macroscale but captures the underlying microscale physics. At every integration point of the macrostructure, the macroscopic kinematic quantities are used to specify the microscopic problem on the RVE which, after solution, returns effective quantities (e.g., stress and stiffness) back to the macroscopic solver. If scale separation can be assumed, i.e., the microstructural features are much smaller than the size of the macrostructure, the effective behavior of the microstructure can be adequately predicted using first-order CH
[
27
,
32
]
. However, for metamaterials the microstructure can be of comparable size with the macrostructure and non-local effects (due to, e.g., buckling; see, e.g.,
[
35
,
41
]
) may emerge at the microscale, both violating the scale separation assumptions of the first-order scheme.

Enriched CH methods, such as second-order CH
[
24
]
or micromorphic CH
[
13
]
, extend the first-order formulation by introducing additional field variables and equations. For micromorphic CH, additional fields that describe the governing behavior of the underlying microstructure are introduced at the macroscale and communicated between both scales. To determine the evolution of these quantities, additional equations need to be included and a coupled system is solved at the macroscale. As an example, the average strain of the inclusions inside a composite RVE was introduced as an additional quantity in Biswas and Poh
[
5
]
. In Jänicke et al.
[
21
]
, micro-rotations were considered as additional field variables for cellular materials. For buckling elastomeric metamaterials, prior knowledge on the buckling modes was embedded into the micromorphic framework presented in Rokoš et al.
[
41
]
. For second-order CH, a strain gradient formulation is considered at the macroscale, i.e., the gradient of the strain (or deformation gradient) is required, giving rise to a length-scale associated with the size of the underlying RVE, thus making it possible to capture size and non-local effects. To ensure a proper scale transition of the kinematical quantities, additional constraints were derived in Kouznetsova et al.
[
24
]
. However, this model leads to artificial stress concentrations at the corners of the RVE for which subsequent formulations attempted to correct
[
31
,
48
,
49
]
. In Luscher et al.
[
31
]
, additional constraints were derived from orthogonality conditions on the different components of the displacement field on the RVE, and in Wu et al.
[
48
]
and Yvonnet et al.
[
49
]
body forces were included to account for additional effects.

In spite of the simplified formulation in terms of an effective non-local continuum, the multiscale problem based on CH is still computationally expensive, as the microscopic problem needs to be solved repeatedly. Compared to the DNS, the two-scale model has much fewer degrees of freedom and elements on the macroscale. However, the evaluation of the constitutive model, i.e., solving the microscopic problem, is much more expensive. As a matter of fact, it might take longer to compute the multiscale model as compared to the DNS for small to intermediate scale ratios. To overcome this problem, a reduced-order model (ROM) for the microscopic problem that is accurate and fast to evaluate is necessary. For first-order CH, numerous methods have been proposed in the literature. These methods can be split into two main classes: (1) data-driven methods that learn a constitutive model (i.e., stress-strain relation) from large datasets obtained by solving the microscopic problem for many different inputs, and (2) projection-based methods that accelerate the microscopic problem by projecting it onto a reduced space. Notable methods for the first class include, for example, the data-driven framework introduced in Kirchdoerfer et al.
[
23
]
or constitutive artificial neural networks in Linka et al.
[
30
]
, which were applied to learn effective elastic material models and later extended for other material models (see, e.g.,
[
1
,
9
,
22
]
). Other authors generated large datasets and utilized recurrent neural networks to learn history-dependent plasticity material models in, e.g.,
[
34
,
47
]
. Even though highly accurate and efficient ROMs can be obtained with these methods, there are three concerns: (1) rather large datasets are usually needed that ideally cover all possible inputs, (2) dealing with history-dependent behaviors such as plasticity is often challenging, and (3) extrapolation is unreliable. For the second class, methods based on proper orthogonal decomposition (POD) and hyperreduction have been quite successful. Using POD, the solution space of the microscopic problem is reduced to a fraction of the original problem; with hyperreduction
[
8
,
10
]
, and more specifically, the empirical cubature method (ECM) proposed by Hernández et al.
[
18
]
and later refined in
[
19
]
, the assembly of the global stiffness matrix and internal force vector can be performed much more efficiently. Since the microscopic problem is still being solved (in a reduced form), smaller datasets are typically sufficient for good results and dealing with history-dependent behavior is not an issue. In Caicedo et al.
[
7
]
and Raschi et al.
[
39
]
, two-scale simulations involving highly non-linear RVEs were successfully accelerated. In our previous work
[
16
]
, we constructed an efficient ROM by combining POD and ECM with geometrical transformations that can be applied for two-scale shape optimization problems involving elasto-plastic RVEs under large deformations.

While there is a wide range of works on accelerating the first-order CH model, to the best of our knowledge there have been no attempts towards the reduced-order modeling of any enriched formulations. Inspired by the literature on first-order CH, in this contribution we propose a ROM for second-order CH, utilizing POD and a novel hyperreduction method that is inspired by ECM
[
18
]
. The main reasons for opting for a projection-based ROM over a data-driven one are two-fold: (1) estimation of parameter bounds for strain gradients is generally difficult a priori, and (2) the number of input parameters is large, in 2D corresponding to four components of the deformation gradient and another six of the gradient of the deformation gradient (nine and eighteen in 3D). This parameter space does not yet account for any other additional design variables, which would further increase the number of parameters. The main innovative contributions of this manuscript are:

•

design of a novel hyperreduction algorithm tailored to second-order CH,

•

development of a hyperreduced POD model for a family of geometrically parameterized microstructures,

•

derivation of effective quantities arising in reduced second-order CH, and

•

an empirical analysis of the hyperreduced model for geometrically parameterized two-scale simulations under large deformations and multiscale buckling.

After reviewing the theory on second-order CH and specifying the employed formulation used in
Section

2
, the proposed ROM, including the novel hyperreduction algorithm, is presented in
Section

3
. To validate the ROM, numerical examples are discussed in
Section

4
and obtained results are compared with reference solutions in terms of accuracy and efficiency. A summary on the findings with final remarks is provided in
Section

5
.

2.2
Parameterized Microscopic Problem

To evaluate
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\bm{P}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
and
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\bm{\mathcal{Q}}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
and their derivatives in
Eqs.

6
,
7
,
8
and
9
, the microscopic problem needs to be solved at every macroscopic integration point. Here, we follow the formulation as presented in Kouznetsova et al.
[
25
]
, where the microscopic problem is modelled as a standard Cauchy continuum. For brevity, a fixed macroscopic material point is assumed, and the dependence on the macroscopic coordinates is omitted in the definition of the microscopic problem provided below.

Consider a family of domains
\Omega^{\bm{\mu}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, parameterized by parameters
\bm{\mu}\in\mathbb{P}
with parameter space
\mathbb{P}
and spanned by position vectors
\bm{x}^{\bm{\mu}}\in\Omega^{\bm{\mu}}
, see
Fig.

2
. For all
\bm{\mu}
, the outer boundaries and topology of
\Omega^{\bm{\mu}}
are assumed to remain fixed. As a consequence, the volume
\left|\Omega^{\bm{\mu}}\right|
remains constant for all
\bm{\mu}
. Additionally, it is assumed that there exists a parent domain
\Omega^{\text{p}}\coloneqq\Omega^{\bm{\mu}^{\text{p}}}
with
\bm{\mu}^{\text{p}}\in\mathbb{P}
, which can be transformed into any
\Omega^{\bm{\mu}}
with a transformation map
\bm{\Phi}_{\bm{\mu}}:\Omega^{\text{p}}\rightarrow\Omega^{\bm{\mu}},\bm{x}^{\text{p}}\mapsto\bm{x}^{\bm{\mu}}
, transformation gradient
\bm{F}_{\bm{\mu}}\coloneqq(\bm{\nabla}_{\bm{x}^{\text{p}}}{\bm{\Phi}_{\bm{\mu}}})^{T}
and
d\bm{x}^{\bm{\mu}}=\left|\operatorname{det}\bm{F}_{\bm{\mu}}\right|d\bm{x}^{\text{p}}
. Note that the transformation map
\bm{\Phi}_{\bm{\mu}}
must be one-to-one and thus the absolute value in
\left|\operatorname{det}\bm{F}_{\bm{\mu}}\right|
is not strictly necessary, but kept for completeness. For a fixed domain, i.e.,
\Omega^{\text{p}}=\Omega^{\bm{\mu}}
, the transformations
\bm{\Phi}_{\bm{\mu}}
are identity maps, with
\bm{F}_{\bm{\mu}}=\bm{I}
and
\left|\operatorname{det}\bm{F}_{\bm{\mu}}\right|=1
. Throughout this manuscript, the superscript p will be used to denote quantities related to the parent domain.

To obtain such transformation maps
\bm{\Phi}_{\bm{\mu}}
, we solve the auxiliary problem as proposed in
[
17
]
. The key idea of the method is to pose an auxiliary linear elasticity problem on the parent domain that can be solved for the transformation displacement
\bm{d}
, which can then be utilized to compute the transformation
\bm{\Phi}_{\bm{\mu}}
with
\bm{\Phi}_{\bm{\mu}}(\bm{x}^{\text{p}})=\bm{I}+\bm{d}(\bm{x}^{\text{p}})
. The displacement
\bm{d}
is fixed on the outer boundaries with zero Dirichlet boundary conditions and prescribed on parts of the domain that are known from the parameterization (as an example, the circular interface of
\Omega^{\text{p}}
in
Fig.

2
is deformed into the elliptical interfaces in
\Omega^{\bm{\mu}_{1}}
and
\Omega^{\bm{\mu}_{2}}
). Subsequently, the auxiliary problem can be solved to find the entire field
\bm{d}(\bm{x}^{\text{p}})
. To fully specify the auxiliary problem, a Young’s modulus
E^{\text{aux}}
and Poisson’s ratio
\xi^{\text{aux}}
need to be selected, see
[
17
]
. For all RVEs considered in this work, the auxiliary problem is solved with
E^{\text{aux}}=$1\text{\,}\mathrm{M}\mathrm{P}\mathrm{a}$
and
\xi^{\text{aux}}=0.25
. More details on the auxiliary problem can be found in
[
17
]
.

Figure 2
:
Family of RVE domains
\Omega^{\bm{\mu}}
parameterized through parameters
\bm{\mu}
. A parent domain
\Omega^{\text{p}}
can be defined which can be transformed through transformations
\bm{\Phi}_{\bm{\mu}}
into any of the RVE domains
\Omega^{\bm{\mu}}
.

The microscopic displacement field
\bm{u}(\bm{x}^{\bm{\mu}})
is assumed to consist of a mean field
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\bm{u}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu(\bm{x}^{\bm{\mu}})
and a fluctuation field
\bm{w}(\bm{x}^{\bm{\mu}})
, i.e.,
\bm{u}(\bm{x}^{\bm{\mu}})=\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\bm{u}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu(\bm{x}^{\bm{\mu}})+\bm{w}(\bm{x}^{\bm{\mu}})
. The mean field
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\bm{u}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
is fully prescribed through the macroscopic quantities
(\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\bm{F}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu,\widehat{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\bm{\mathcal{G}}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu})
with

\displaystyle\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\bm{u}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu(\bm{x}^{\bm{\mu}})\coloneqq(\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\bm{F}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu-\bm{I})\cdot\bm{x}^{\bm{\mu}}+\frac{1}{2}(\bm{x}^{\bm{\mu}}\cdot\widehat{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\bm{\mathcal{G}}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu})\cdot\bm{x}^{\bm{\mu}}.

(10)

Subsequently, the microscopic deformation gradient can be defined as

\displaystyle\bm{F}\coloneqq\bm{I}+(\bm{\nabla}_{\bm{x}^{\bm{\mu}}}{\bm{u}})^{T}=\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\bm{F}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu+\bm{x}^{\bm{\mu}}\cdot\widehat{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\bm{\mathcal{G}}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}+(\bm{\nabla}_{\bm{x}^{\bm{\mu}}}{\bm{w}})^{T}.

(11)

The governing microscopic PDE is given by,

\displaystyle\bm{\nabla}_{\bm{x}^{\bm{\mu}}}\cdot\bm{P}^{T}(\bm{F})=\bm{0},

(12)

which can be written into its weak form by multiplying with a test function
\delta\bm{w}
and applying the divergence theorem,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \Pi(\bm{w})&=\displaystyle\int_{\Omega^{\bm{\mu}}}\bm{\nabla}_{\bm{x}^{\bm{\mu}}}{\delta\bm{w}}:\bm{P}\left(\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\bm{F}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu+\bm{x}^{\bm{\mu}}\cdot\widehat{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\bm{\mathcal{G}}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}+(\bm{\nabla}_{\bm{x}^{\bm{\mu}}}{\bm{w}})^{T}\right)d\bm{x}^{\bm{\mu}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle!}}{{=}}0,\end{aligned}

(13)

where
\bm{P}
is the microscopic second-order 1PK stress tensor, and the macroscopic quantities
(\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\bm{F}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu,\widehat{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\bm{\mathcal{G}}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu})
act as external forcing terms. For now, no constitutive model at the microscale level is specified, but it is assumed that
\bm{P}
is a non-linear function of the deformation gradient
\bm{F}
.
The microscopic problem can thus be stated as follows: given
(\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\bm{F}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu,\widehat{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\bm{\mathcal{G}}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu},\bm{\mu})
, find
\bm{w}
that fulfills
Eq.

13
for all
\delta\bm{w}
. To remove the dependence of the integral on parameters
\bm{\mu}
,
Eq.

13
can be transformed to the parent domain with the transformation map
\bm{\Phi}_{\bm{\mu}}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\Pi({\bm{w}^{\text{p}}})

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\int_{\Omega^{\text{p}}}\left(\bm{F}_{\bm{\mu}}^{-T}\cdot\left(\bm{\nabla}_{\bm{x}^{\text{p}}}{\delta{\bm{w}^{\text{p}}}}\right)\right):\bm{P}^{\text{p}}(\bm{F}^{\rm{p}})\left|\operatorname{det}\bm{F}_{\bm{\mu}}\right|d\bm{x}^{\text{p}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle!}}{{=}}0,

(14)

where
{\bm{w}^{\text{p}}}(\bm{x}^{\text{p}})\coloneqq(\bm{w}\circ\bm{\Phi}_{\bm{\mu}})(\bm{x}^{\text{p}})=\bm{w}(\bm{x}^{\bm{\mu}})
,
\delta{\bm{w}^{\text{p}}}(\bm{x}^{\text{p}})\coloneqq\delta\bm{w}(\bm{x}^{\bm{\mu}})
,
\bm{P}^{\text{p}}(\bm{x}^{\text{p}})\coloneqq\bm{P}(\bm{x}^{\bm{\mu}})
, and

\displaystyle\bm{F}^{\rm{p}}=\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\bm{F}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu+\bm{\Phi}_{\bm{\mu}}(\bm{x}^{\text{p}})\cdot\widehat{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\bm{\mathcal{G}}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}+\left(\bm{\nabla}_{\bm{x}^{\text{p}}}{{\bm{w}^{\text{p}}}}\right)^{T}\cdot\bm{F}_{\bm{\mu}}^{-1}.

(15)

Hereafter, we write
\bm{x}^{\bm{\mu}}(\bm{x}^{\text{p}})
instead of
\bm{\Phi}_{\bm{\mu}}(\bm{x}^{\text{p}})
for brevity. To find the
{\bm{w}^{\text{p}}}
that fulfills
Eq.

14
for all
\delta{\bm{w}^{\text{p}}}
, the linearization of
Eq.

14
around a state
{\bm{w}^{\text{p}}}
in direction
\Delta{\bm{w}^{\text{p}}}
is required,

\displaystyle\left.D\Pi\right|_{{\bm{w}^{\text{p}}}}\cdot(\Delta{\bm{w}^{\text{p}}})

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\int_{\Omega^{\text{p}}}\left(\bm{F}_{\bm{\mu}}^{-T}\cdot\left(\bm{\nabla}_{\bm{x}^{\text{p}}}{\Delta{\bm{w}^{\text{p}}}}\right)\right):\bm{\mathfrak{A}}^{\text{p}}(\bm{F}^{\rm{p}}):\left(\bm{F}_{\bm{\mu}}^{-T}\cdot\left(\bm{\nabla}_{\bm{x}^{\text{p}}}{\delta{\bm{w}^{\text{p}}}}\right)\right)\left|\operatorname{det}\bm{F}_{\bm{\mu}}\right|d\bm{x}^{\text{p}},

(16)

where
\bm{\mathfrak{A}}^{\text{p}}\coloneqq\bm{\nabla}_{\bm{F}}\bm{P}^{\text{p}}
is the fourth-order stiffness tensor on the parent domain.

To ensure a proper scale transition of the kinematic quantities, different authors derived and proposed additional constraints on the fluctuation field
{\bm{w}^{\text{p}}}
. In Kouznetsova et al.
[
24
]
, periodic boundary conditions (PBC) for
{\bm{w}^{\text{p}}}
are assumed and the following constraints are derived for a rectangular RVE

\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Omega^{\text{p}}_{\text{top}}}{\bm{w}^{\text{p}}}d\bm{x}^{\text{p}}

\displaystyle=\bm{0},

(17)

\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Omega^{\text{p}}_{\text{right}}}{\bm{w}^{\text{p}}}d\bm{x}^{\text{p}}

\displaystyle=\bm{0},

(18)

where
\partial\Omega^{\text{p}}_{\text{top}}
and
\partial\Omega^{\text{p}}_{\text{right}}
denote the top and right edge of the RVE
\Omega^{\text{p}}
, see
Fig.

2
. Due to PBC, the same conditions hold for the bottom and left edge. In addition, the fluctuation field is set to zero on all four corners of the RVE, i.e.,
{\bm{w}^{\text{p}}}=\bm{0}
.
Since it is assumed that
\Omega^{\bm{\mu}}
has fixed outer boundaries for all
\bm{\mu}
, constraints in
Eqs.

17
and
18
are independent of
\bm{\mu}
. In subsequent works, other authors developed slightly different formulations (see, e.g.,
[
6
,
31
,
40
,
48
,
49
]
). In
[
40
]
, the authors compared different formulations and pointed out that fixing the corners in the formulation in Kouznetsova et al.
[
24
]
leads to stress concentrations and artificial effects at the corners. Instead of fixing the corners, other formulations introduce an additional equation that constrains the rigid body motion with

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{\Omega^{\bm{\mu}}}\bm{w}d\bm{x}^{\bm{\mu}}=\displaystyle\int_{\Omega^{\text{p}}}{\bm{w}^{\text{p}}}\left|\operatorname{det}\bm{F}_{\bm{\mu}}\right|d\bm{x}^{\text{p}}=\bm{0}.

(19)

The complete microscopic model employed in this work consists of
Eqs.

14
,
15
,
16
,
17
,
18
and
19
together with PBC for
{\bm{w}^{\text{p}}}
. Lagrange multipliers are used to enforce the constraints in
Eqs.

17
,
18
and
19
and PBC, resulting in a saddle point problem.

To solve the microscopic problem, the fluctuation displacement is typically discretized with finite elements (FE). We thus approximate

\displaystyle{\bm{w}^{\text{p}}}(\bm{x}^{\text{p}})\approx\mathbf{N}(\bm{x}^{\text{p}})\mathbf{w},

(20)

where
\mathbf{N}(\bm{x}^{\text{p}})\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times\mathcal{N}}
denotes the FE shape functions,
\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{N}}
the coefficients of the discretized displacement fluctuation field, and
\mathcal{N}
the total number of DOFs. Note that lowercase non-italic symbols, e.g.,
\mathbf{w}
, are used for column matrices, while uppercase non-italic symbols, e.g.,
\mathbf{N}
, are used for matrices. Subsequently, the weak form in
Eq.

14
, together with the constraints, can be written as

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{w})+\mathbf{C}^{T}\mathbf{m}&&=\bm{0},\\
&\mathbf{C}\mathbf{w}&&=\bm{0},\end{aligned}

(21)

where
\mathbf{f}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{N}}
is the global internal force column matrix, the constraint matrix
\mathbf{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{c}\times\mathcal{N}}
is derived from the constraints in
Eqs.

17
,
18
and
19
and PBC, with
N_{c}
the number of constraint equations, and
\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{c}}
are the corresponding Lagrange multipliers. Note that the constraint matrix
\mathbf{C}
depends on the parameter
\bm{\mu}
because of
Eq.

19
. This dependency has been dropped for brevity. Using Newton’s method, the non-linear system of equations in
Eq.

21
can be solved for
\mathbf{w}
and
\mathbf{m}
,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{w}^{k})&\mathbf{C}^{T}\\
\mathbf{C}&\bm{0}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\Delta\mathbf{w}\\
\mathbf{m}\end{bmatrix}&=\begin{bmatrix}-\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{w}^{k})\\
\bm{0}\end{bmatrix},\\
\mathbf{w}^{k+1}&=\mathbf{w}^{k}+\Delta\mathbf{w},\end{aligned}

(22)

where
\mathbf{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{N}\times\mathcal{N}}
is the global stiffness matrix computed from
Eq.

16
,
k\geq 0
is the Newton iteration number, and
\mathbf{w}^{0}=\mathbf{0}
is the starting value.
Eq.

22
is repeated until
\left\|\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{w}^{k})+\mathbf{C}^{T}\mathbf{m}\right\|_{2}\leq\varepsilon_{\text{newton}}
with
\varepsilon_{\text{newton}}
a user-defined tolerance. For more information on the FE method and discretization of weak forms, we refer to
[
2
]
.

2.3
Effective Quantities

After the microscopic problem has been solved and a solution
\bm{w}^{*\text{p}}
obtained, the effective stress
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\bm{P}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
, higher-order stress
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\bm{\mathcal{Q}}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
and their corresponding derivatives with respect to
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\bm{F}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
and
\widehat{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\bm{\mathcal{G}}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}
must be computed. For conciseness, the following microscopic quantities are introduced:

\displaystyle\bm{F}^{*\text{p}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\bm{F}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu+\bm{x}^{\bm{\mu}}\cdot\widehat{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\bm{\mathcal{G}}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}+\left(\bm{\nabla}_{\bm{x}^{\text{p}}}{\bm{w}^{*\text{p}}}\right)^{T}\cdot\bm{F}_{\bm{\mu}}^{-1},

(23)

\displaystyle\bm{P}^{*\text{p}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\bm{P}^{\text{p}}(\bm{F}^{*\text{p}}),

(24)

\displaystyle\bm{\mathfrak{A}}^{*\text{p}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\bm{\mathfrak{A}}^{\text{p}}(\bm{F}^{*\text{p}}),

(25)

which correspond to the microstructural deformation gradient, 1PK stress, and related stiffness tensors all evaluated at the solution
\bm{w}^{*\text{p}}
on the parent domain, as indicated by the asterisk *.
Expressions for the effective stress
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\bm{P}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
and higher-order stress
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\bm{\mathcal{Q}}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
were derived in
[
25
]
which, after transformation to the parent domain, yield

\displaystyle\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\bm{P}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{1}{\left|\Omega^{\text{p}}\right|}\displaystyle\int_{\Omega^{\text{p}}}\bm{P}^{*\text{p}}\left|\operatorname{det}\bm{F}_{\bm{\mu}}\right|d\bm{x}^{\text{p}},

(26)

\displaystyle\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\bm{\mathcal{Q}}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{1}{\left|\Omega^{\text{p}}\right|}\displaystyle\int_{\Omega^{\text{p}}}\frac{1}{2}\left({\bm{P}^{*\text{p}}}^{T}\otimes\bm{x}^{\bm{\mu}}+\bm{x}^{\bm{\mu}}\otimes\bm{P}^{*\text{p}}\right)\left|\operatorname{det}\bm{F}_{\bm{\mu}}\right|d\bm{x}^{\text{p}}.

(27)

The effective stiffness components, derived by differentiating the above stress and higher-order stress quantities then yield (in index notation)

\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muP\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{ij}}{\partial\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muF\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{kl}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\left|\Omega^{\text{p}}\right|}\displaystyle\int_{\Omega^{\text{p}}}\dfrac{\partial P_{ij}^{*\text{p}}}{\partial\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muF\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{kl}}\left|\operatorname{det}\bm{F}_{\bm{\mu}}\right|d\bm{x}^{\text{p}},

(28)

\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muP\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{ij}}{\partial\widehat{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathcal{G}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}_{mno}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\left|\Omega^{\text{p}}\right|}\displaystyle\int_{\Omega^{\text{p}}}\dfrac{\partial P_{ij}^{*\text{p}}}{\partial\widehat{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathcal{G}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}_{mno}}\left|\operatorname{det}\bm{F}_{\bm{\mu}}\right|d\bm{x}^{\text{p}},

(29)

\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathcal{Q}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{ijk}}{\partial\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muF\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{mn}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\left|\Omega^{\text{p}}\right|}\displaystyle\int_{\Omega^{\text{p}}}\frac{1}{2}\left(\dfrac{\partial P_{ji}^{*\text{p}}}{\partial\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muF\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{mn}}x^{\bm{\mu}}_{k}+x^{\bm{\mu}}_{i}\dfrac{\partial P_{jk}^{*\text{p}}}{\partial\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muF\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{mn}}\right)\left|\operatorname{det}\bm{F}_{\bm{\mu}}\right|d\bm{x}^{\text{p}},

(30)

\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathcal{Q}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{ijk}}{\partial\widehat{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathcal{G}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}_{mno}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\left|\Omega^{\text{p}}\right|}\displaystyle\int_{\Omega^{\text{p}}}\frac{1}{2}\left(\dfrac{\partial P_{ji}^{*\text{p}}}{\partial\widehat{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathcal{G}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}_{mno}}x^{\bm{\mu}}_{k}+x^{\bm{\mu}}_{i}\dfrac{\partial P_{jk}^{*\text{p}}}{\partial\widehat{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathcal{G}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}_{mno}}\right)\left|\operatorname{det}\bm{F}_{\bm{\mu}}\right|d\bm{x}^{\text{p}}.

(31)

In the above,

\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial P_{ij}^{*\text{p}}}{\partial\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muF\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{kl}}

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{A}^{*\text{p}}_{ijmn}\left(\delta_{mk}\delta_{nl}+\dfrac{\partial}{\partial\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muF\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{kl}}\left(\dfrac{\partial w^{*\text{p}}_{m}}{\partial x^{\text{p}}_{r}}\right)F_{\bm{\mu},rn}^{-1}\right),

(32)

and

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \dfrac{\partial P_{ij}^{*\text{p}}}{\partial\widehat{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathcal{G}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}_{mno}}&=\mathfrak{A}^{*\text{p}}_{ijkl}\left(\dfrac{\partial\left(x^{\bm{\mu}}_{r}\widehat{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathcal{G}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}_{rkl}\right)}{\partial\widehat{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathcal{G}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}_{mno}}+\dfrac{\partial}{\partial\widehat{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathcal{G}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}_{mno}}\left(\dfrac{\partial w^{*\text{p}}_{k}}{\partial x^{\text{p}}_{s}}\right)F_{\bm{\mu},sl}^{-1}\right)\\
&=\mathfrak{A}^{*\text{p}}_{ijkl}\left(x^{\bm{\mu}}_{r}\delta_{rm}\delta_{kn}\delta_{lo}+\dfrac{\partial}{\partial\widehat{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathcal{G}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}_{mno}}\left(\dfrac{\partial w^{*\text{p}}_{k}}{\partial x^{\text{p}}_{s}}\right)F_{\bm{\mu},sl}^{-1}\right)\\
&=\mathfrak{A}^{*\text{p}}_{ijkl}\left(x^{\bm{\mu}}_{m}\delta_{kn}\delta_{lo}+\dfrac{\partial}{\partial\widehat{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathcal{G}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}_{mno}}\left(\dfrac{\partial w^{*\text{p}}_{k}}{\partial x^{\text{p}}_{s}}\right)F_{\bm{\mu},sl}^{-1}\right).\end{aligned}

(33)

To determine
\dfrac{\partial\vphantom{1}}{\partial\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muF\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{kl}}\left(\dfrac{\partial w^{*\text{p}}_{m}}{\partial x^{\text{p}}_{r}}\right)
and
\dfrac{\partial\vphantom{1}}{\partial\widehat{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathcal{G}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}_{mno}}\left(\dfrac{\partial w^{*\text{p}}_{k}}{\partial x^{\text{p}}_{s}}\right)
,
Eq.

14
is differentiated with respect to
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\bm{F}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
and
\widehat{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\bm{\mathcal{G}}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}
to derive linear tangent problems that can be solved to find the corresponding sensitivities. As an example, for one particular component
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muF\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{kl}
(where the indices
k
and
l
are assumed to be temporarily fixed), the differentiation yields

\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial\Pi(\bm{w}^{*\text{p}})}{\partial\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muF\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{kl}}=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{\Omega^{\text{p}}}\left(\bm{F}_{\bm{\mu}}^{-T}\cdot\left(\bm{\nabla}_{\bm{x}^{\text{p}}}{\delta{\bm{w}^{\text{p}}}}\right)\right):\dfrac{\partial\bm{P}^{*\text{p}}}{\partial\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muF\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{kl}}\left|\operatorname{det}\bm{F}_{\bm{\mu}}\right|d\bm{x}^{\text{p}}=0,

(34)

which can be rearranged with
Eq.

32
as

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\displaystyle\int_{\Omega^{\text{p}}}\left(\bm{F}_{\bm{\mu}}^{-T}\cdot\left(\bm{\nabla}_{\bm{x}^{\text{p}}}{\bm{q}_{kl}}\right)\right):\bm{\mathfrak{A}}^{*\text{p}}:\left(\bm{F}_{\bm{\mu}}^{-T}\cdot\left(\bm{\nabla}_{\bm{x}^{\text{p}}}{\delta{\bm{w}^{\text{p}}}}\right)\right)\left|\operatorname{det}\bm{F}_{\bm{\mu}}\right|d\bm{x}^{\text{p}}\\
&=-\bm{E}_{kl}^{T}:\left(\displaystyle\int_{\Omega^{\text{p}}}\bm{\mathfrak{A}}^{*\text{p}}:\left(\bm{F}_{\bm{\mu}}^{-T}\cdot\left(\bm{\nabla}_{\bm{x}^{\text{p}}}{\delta{\bm{w}^{\text{p}}}}\right)\right)\left|\operatorname{det}\bm{F}_{\bm{\mu}}\right|d\bm{x}^{\text{p}}\right),\end{aligned}

(35)

where a new auxiliary vector field
\bm{q}_{kl}\coloneqq\dfrac{\partial\bm{w}^{*\text{p}}}{\partial\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muF\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{kl}}
is defined, reflecting the sensitivity of the microfluctuation field with respect to the change of the macroscopic deformation gradient, and
\bm{E}_{kl}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is a second-order tensor with all entries zero except for the
kl
-th entry which is 1. The linear problem of
Eq.

35
is solved for all combinations
k,l=1,\dots,d
to obtain
\bm{q}_{kl}
for each component of
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\bm{F}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
. The same procedure is followed for
\widehat{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\bm{\mathcal{G}}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}
. With an auxiliary vector field
\bm{q}_{mno}\coloneqq\dfrac{\partial\bm{w}^{*\text{p}}}{\partial\widehat{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathcal{G}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}_{mno}}
, the differentiation of
Eq.

14
for one particular component
\widehat{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathcal{G}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}_{mno}
(where the indices
m
,
n
and
o
are assumed to be temporarily fixed) yields, together with
Eq.

33
,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\displaystyle\int_{\Omega^{\text{p}}}\left(\bm{F}_{\bm{\mu}}^{-T}\cdot\left(\bm{\nabla}_{\bm{x}^{\text{p}}}{\bm{q}_{mno}}\right)\right):\bm{\mathfrak{A}}^{*\text{p}}:\left(\bm{F}_{\bm{\mu}}^{-T}\cdot\left(\bm{\nabla}_{\bm{x}^{\text{p}}}{\delta{\bm{w}^{\text{p}}}}\right)\right)\left|\operatorname{det}\bm{F}_{\bm{\mu}}\right|d\bm{x}^{\text{p}}\\

(36)

where
\bm{\mathcal{E}}_{mno}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d\times d}
is a third-order tensor with all entries zero except for the

to obtain

.

3
Reduced-Order Modeling

Since the microscopic problem is solved at every macroscopic integration point, its solution must be efficient. Due to the often complicated RVE geometries, a fine discretization (using, e.g., finite elements) is required, resulting in a large number of DOFs and integration points, which entail a costly solution. Furthermore, computing the effective quantities for a fine RVE mesh presents another computationally expensive operation. To construct a reduced-order model (ROM) for a more efficient solution, we employ two reduction techniques: (1) we utilize proper orthogonal decomposition (POD) to reduce the number of DOFs in
Section

3.1
; and (2) to establish a more efficient integration scheme, we propose in
Section

3.2
a novel hyperreduction algorithm that uses ideas of the empirical cubature method
[
18
]
, which is specifically suited for the second-order CH formulation.

3.1
Proper Orthogonal Decomposition

To reduce the number of DOFs, the fluctuation displacement field
{\bm{w}^{\text{p}}}
is approximated with a reduced basis
[
20
,
37
]
, i.e.,

\displaystyle{\bm{w}^{\text{p}}}\approx\sum_{n=1}^{N}a_{n}\bm{v}_{n},

(37)

where
N
is typically much smaller than
\mathcal{N}
, i.e.,
N\ll\mathcal{N}
. The global basis functions,
\{\bm{v}_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
, are obtained by applying POD to a set of pre-computed snapshots of
{\bm{w}^{\text{p}}}
for different parameter values
(\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\bm{F}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu,\widehat{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\bm{\mathcal{G}}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu},\bm{\mu})
. Since each of the basis functions is computed from a linear combination of pre-computed periodic solutions that fulfill the constraints in
Eqs.

17
,
18
and
19
, every basis function is periodic and also fulfills
Eqs.

18
and
17
. This implies that any solution
{\bm{w}^{\text{p}}}
that is represented by
Eq.

37
always fulfills these conditions. However, the basis functions will only fulfill the constraint in
Eq.

19
if a fixed geometry is assumed for the RVE, i.e.,
\bm{\mu}
is constant. For varying geometries, the constraint is violated due to the influence of
\left|\operatorname{det}\bm{F}_{\bm{\mu}}\right|
. Nevertheless, in our numerical examples, tests with and without enforcing
Eq.

19
through Lagrange multipliers were run and only insignificant differences of the solutions were observed. For that reason, we do not enforce
Eq.

19
for the ROM. This has the added advantage that no constraints have to be considered for the ROM and, thus, more efficient solvers for the resulting system of linear equations can be utilized.

Remark
.

For the full problem,
Eq.

19
must be enforced to remove rigid body modes and ensure a unique solution. However, for the ROM, each basis function is a linear combination of snapshots that fulfill
Eq.

19
in their respective domains. Such basis functions should usually not span a solution space that contains a rigid body mode of the microscopic problem for any new domain. If numerical issues are encountered during the solution without the constraint, one should enforce the constraint.

By inserting
Eq.

37
into
Eqs.

14
and
16
and assuming a Galerkin projection, the components of the internal force
\mathbf{f}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
and global stiffness matrix
\mathbf{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
can be computed:

\displaystyle f_{i}(\mathbf{a})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\displaystyle\int_{\Omega^{\text{p}}}\left(\bm{F}_{\bm{\mu}}^{-T}\cdot\left(\bm{\nabla}_{\bm{x}^{\text{p}}}\bm{v}_{i}\right)\right):\bm{P}^{\text{p}}(\bm{F}^{\rm{p}})\left|\operatorname{det}\bm{F}_{\bm{\mu}}\right|d\bm{x}^{\text{p}},

(38)

\displaystyle K_{ij}(\mathbf{a})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\displaystyle\int_{\Omega^{\text{p}}}\left(\bm{F}_{\bm{\mu}}^{-T}\cdot\left(\bm{\nabla}_{\bm{x}^{\text{p}}}\bm{v}_{j}\right)\right):\bm{\mathfrak{A}}^{\text{p}}(\bm{F}^{\rm{p}}):\left(\bm{F}_{\bm{\mu}}^{-T}\cdot\left(\bm{\nabla}_{\bm{x}^{\text{p}}}\bm{v}_{i}\right)\right)\left|\operatorname{det}\bm{F}_{\bm{\mu}}\right|d\bm{x}^{\text{p}},

(39)

for all
i,j=1,\dots,N
, and

\displaystyle\bm{F}^{\rm{p}}(\mathbf{a})

\displaystyle=\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\bm{F}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu+\bm{x}^{\bm{\mu}}\cdot\widehat{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\bm{\mathcal{G}}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}+\left(\sum_{n=1}^{N}a_{n}(\bm{\nabla}_{\bm{x}^{\text{p}}}\bm{v}_{n})^{T}\right)\cdot\bm{F}_{\bm{\mu}}^{-1}.

(40)

The column matrix
\mathbf{a}=[a_{1},\dots,a_{N}]^{T}
contains the unknown coefficients to be solved for.

3.2
Hyperreduction

While the reduced system of
Eqs.

38
and
39
only has
N
DOFs, computing the integrals in
Eqs.

38
and
39
(as well as

,

,

,

,

and

To accelerate this computation, a more efficient integration scheme (i.e., fewer integration points and corresponding weights) is sought that closely approximates the numerical integration with Gaussian quadrature of the following four quantities:

•

Internal force
\mathbf{f}=[f_{1},\dots,f_{N}]^{T}
in
Eq.

38
:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} f_{i}&=\displaystyle\int_{\Omega^{\text{p}}}\left(\bm{F}_{\bm{\mu}}^{-T}\cdot\left(\bm{\nabla}_{\bm{x}^{\text{p}}}{\bm{v}_{i}}\right)\right):\bm{P}^{\text{p}}\left|\operatorname{det}\bm{F}_{\bm{\mu}}\right|d\bm{x}^{\text{p}}\\
&=\displaystyle\int_{\Omega^{\text{p}}}\bm{\nabla}_{\bm{x}^{\text{p}}}{\bm{v}_{i}}:{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{Y}^{\text{p}}}d\bm{x}^{\text{p}},\end{aligned}

(41)

for all

and where the weighted stress
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{Y}^{\text{p}}}\coloneqq\bm{P}^{\text{p}}\cdot\bm{F}_{\bm{\mu}}^{-T}\left|\operatorname{det}\bm{F}_{\bm{\mu}}\right|
is defined.

•

Effective stress
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\bm{P}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
in
Eq.

26
:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\bm{P}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu&=\frac{1}{\left|\Omega^{\text{p}}\right|}\displaystyle\int_{\Omega^{\text{p}}}\bm{P}^{\text{p}}\left|\operatorname{det}\bm{F}_{\bm{\mu}}\right|d\bm{x}^{\text{p}}\\
&=\frac{1}{\left|\Omega^{\text{p}}\right|}\displaystyle\int_{\Omega^{\text{p}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{Y}^{\text{p}}}\cdot\bm{F}_{\bm{\mu}}^{T}d\bm{x}^{\text{p}}\\
&=\frac{1}{\left|\Omega^{\text{p}}\right|}\displaystyle\int_{\Omega^{\text{p}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{Y}^{\text{p}}}d\bm{x}^{\text{p}},\end{aligned}

(42)

where, in the last line the invariance of the integral with respect to
\bm{F}_{\bm{\mu}}^{T}
was used, which was proven in
[
17
, Appendix A]
. This implies that the accurate integration of the effective stress is equivalent to the accurate integration of the weighted stress
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{Y}^{\text{p}}}
.

•

Effective higher-order stress
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\bm{\mathcal{Q}}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
in
Eq.

27
:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\bm{\mathcal{Q}}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu&=\frac{1}{\left|\Omega^{\text{p}}\right|}\displaystyle\int_{\Omega^{\text{p}}}\frac{1}{2}\left({\bm{P}^{\text{p}}}^{T}\otimes\bm{x}^{\bm{\mu}}+\bm{x}^{\bm{\mu}}\otimes\bm{P}^{\text{p}}\right)\left|\operatorname{det}\bm{F}_{\bm{\mu}}\right|d\bm{x}^{\text{p}}\\
&=\frac{1}{\left|\Omega^{\text{p}}\right|}\displaystyle\int_{\Omega^{\text{p}}}\bm{\mathcal{Y}}^{\text{p}}d\bm{x}^{\text{p}},\end{aligned}

(43)

where the weighted higher-order stress
\bm{\mathcal{Y}}^{\text{p}}\coloneqq\dfrac{1}{2}\left({\bm{P}^{\text{p}}}^{T}\otimes\bm{x}^{\bm{\mu}}+\bm{x}^{\bm{\mu}}\otimes\bm{P}^{\text{p}}\right)\left|\operatorname{det}\bm{F}_{\bm{\mu}}\right|
is defined.

•

Volume:

\displaystyle V\coloneqq\left|\Omega^{\text{p}}\right|=\displaystyle\int_{\Omega^{\text{p}}}d\bm{x}^{\text{p}}.

(44)

Even though the integration of the volume does not necessarily have to be accurate, it helps to stabilize the algorithm used to find the new integration points. In particular, it leads to fewer weights that are equal to 0 during the solution of the non-negative least squares problem introduced later in
Eq.

56
.

3.2.1
Algorithm

To find an efficient integration scheme, we use concepts from the empirical cubature method (ECM), which was previously applied to identify integration points and weights for an efficient integration of the internal force, as proposed by Hernández et al.
[
18
]
and recently extended to varying geometries in Guo et al.
[
16
]
. In the first step, similarly to the fluctuation field
{\bm{w}^{\text{p}}}
, snapshots of the weighted stress
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{Y}^{\text{p}}}
and weighted higher-order stress
\bm{\mathcal{Y}}^{\text{p}}
are collected for different parameter values
(\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\bm{F}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu,\widehat{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\bm{\mathcal{G}}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu},\bm{\mu})
. Utilizing POD, two sets of basis functions for
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{Y}^{\text{p}}}
and
\bm{\mathcal{Y}}^{\text{p}}
,
\{\bm{B}_{m}\}_{m=1}^{M}
and
\{\bm{\mathcal{H}}_{l}\}_{l=1}^{L}
, are found, with which
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{Y}^{\text{p}}}
and
\bm{\mathcal{Y}}^{\text{p}}
can be approximated, i.e.,

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{Y}^{\text{p}}}

\displaystyle\approx\sum_{m=1}^{M}\alpha_{m}\bm{B}_{m},

(45)

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{Y}}^{\text{p}}

\displaystyle\approx\sum_{l=1}^{L}\beta_{l}\bm{\mathcal{H}}_{l}.

(46)

Inserting
Eqs.

45
and
46
into
Eqs.

41
,
42
and
43
yields

\displaystyle f_{i}

\displaystyle\approx\sum_{m=1}^{M}\alpha_{m}\displaystyle\int_{\Omega^{\text{p}}}\bm{\nabla}_{\bm{x}^{\text{p}}}{\bm{v}_{i}}:\bm{B}_{m}d\bm{x}^{\text{p}},\quad\forall i=1,\dots,N,

(47)

\displaystyle\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\bm{P}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu

\displaystyle\approx\sum_{m=1}^{M}\alpha_{m}\frac{1}{\left|\Omega^{\text{p}}\right|}\displaystyle\int_{\Omega^{\text{p}}}\bm{B}_{m}d\bm{x}^{\text{p}},

(48)

\displaystyle\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\bm{\mathcal{Q}}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu

\displaystyle\approx\sum_{l=1}^{L}\beta_{l}\frac{1}{\left|\Omega^{\text{p}}\right|}\displaystyle\int_{\Omega^{\text{p}}}\bm{\mathcal{H}}_{l}d\bm{x}^{\text{p}}.

(49)

Since

47

48

49
should be accurately integrated for any values of
\alpha_{m}
and
\beta_{l}
, all the occurring integrals in the sums should be approximated accurately. Together with the volume equation in

44
, integration points and weights are sought that approximate these

and

arise due to the number of components of
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\bm{P}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
and
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\bm{\mathcal{Q}}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
.

Consider for now the full set of Gaussian integration points
\{\widehat{\bm{x}}_{q},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\widehat{h}_{q}}\}_{q=1}^{\widehat{Q}}
corresponding to the fully resolved discretization, where
\widehat{Q}
is the total number of Gauss integration points,
\widehat{\bm{x}}_{q}
their positions and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\widehat{h}_{q}}
their weights. By defining

\displaystyle\bm{1}\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}1,\dots,1\end{bmatrix}^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{\widehat{Q}},

(50)

and the flattened basis functions (in 2D, i.e.,

(51)

\displaystyle\underline{\bm{\mathcal{H}}}_{l}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{H}_{l,111},\mathcal{H}_{l,112},\mathcal{H}_{l,121},\mathcal{H}_{l,122},\mathcal{H}_{l,211},\mathcal{H}_{l,212},\mathcal{H}_{l,221},\mathcal{H}_{l,222}\end{bmatrix}^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{3}},\quad\forall l=1,\dots,L,

\displaystyle\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{A}_{1}\\

\displaystyle\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\widehat{\mathbf{A}}_{1}\\

\widehat{\mathbf{A}}_{3}\\

\displaystyle\bm{\Sigma_{1}}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{diag}(\sigma^{\bm{w}}_{1}\sigma^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{Y}}}_{1},\ldots,\sigma^{\bm{w}}_{1}\sigma^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{Y}}}_{M},\ldots,\sigma^{\bm{w}}_{N}\sigma^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{Y}}}_{1},\ldots,\sigma^{\bm{w}}_{N}\sigma^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{Y}}}_{M})

\displaystyle\bm{\Sigma_{4}}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{diag}(1)

\varepsilon_{4}

\displaystyle r_{1}\coloneqq\frac{\left|\left|\widehat{\mathbf{r}}_{1}\right|\right|_{\bm{\Sigma}_{1}}}{\operatorname{tr}\bm{\Sigma}_{1}}<\varepsilon_{1},\quad r_{2}\coloneqq\frac{\left|\left|\widehat{\mathbf{r}}_{2}\right|\right|_{\bm{\Sigma}_{2}}}{\operatorname{tr}\bm{\Sigma}_{2}}<\varepsilon_{2},\quad r_{3}\coloneqq\frac{\left|\left|\widehat{\mathbf{r}}_{3}\right|\right|_{\bm{\Sigma}_{3}}}{\operatorname{tr}\bm{\Sigma}_{3}}<\varepsilon_{3},\quad r_{4}\coloneqq\frac{\left|\left|\widehat{\mathbf{r}}_{4}\right|\right|_{\bm{\Sigma}_{4}}}{V}<\varepsilon_{4},

where

.

4
Numerical Examples

To examine and illustrate different features of the proposed ROM, two macroscopic example problems with a parameterized microstructure are studied in two dimensions and under plane strain conditions. The results are compared against the full two-scale second-order CH solution (later referred to as CH2) as well as the direct numerical simulation (DNS), where the microstructure is fully resolved at the macroscale. The ROM is discussed in detail in the first example, whereas the second example shows a possible application, in which a full DNS might not be feasible anymore (especially in three dimensions), but the ROM computes an excellent approximation in a reasonable amount of time. Note that the examples considered here could potentially also be solved with a satisfactory accuracy using first-order CH (CH1). However, as the focus of this work is on the reduced-order modeling of CH2, no comparisons with CH1 are conducted. A systematic and quantitative comparison of CH1 and CH2 applied to mechanical metamaterials can be found in
[
42
]
.

For both examples, a metamaterial-based RVE with four identical holes is selected, motivated by
Specimen 1
in Bertoldi et al.
[
4
]
. The size of the RVE is
$2\text{\,}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{m}$\times$2\text{\,}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{m}$
and the local coordinate system is chosen in the center of the domain, i.e., the domain of the RVE is given by
[$-1\text{\,}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{m}$,$1\text{\,}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{m}$]^{2}
, see
Fig.

. For the top right hole, the coordinates (in
\mathrm{m}\mathrm{m}
) of the control points are

Results

The accuracy and efficiency of the ROM depends on several factors:

•

the number of basis functions for the fluctuation displacement
N
, weighted stress
M
and weighted higher-order stress
L
,

•

the error tolerances
\varepsilon_{1}
,
\varepsilon_{2}
,
\varepsilon_{3}
and
\varepsilon_{4}
, and

•

the hyperparameters
c_{1}
,
c_{2}
and
c_{3}
that control the weighting matrix
\bm{\Sigma}
of the weighted least squares problem in
Eqs.

56
and
58

To choose an adequate amount of basis functions, the singular values of POD are often utilized, as they give an indication on the information loss due to the reduction. Given the ordered singular values
\{\sigma_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N_{\rm{S}}}
of POD, a criterion can be defined with:

\displaystyle 1-\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\rm{POD}}}\sigma_{i}^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\text{S}}}\sigma_{i}^{2}}<\mathcal{E}_{\rm{POD}},

(66)

where
N_{\text{S}}
denotes the total number of training snapshots and
\mathcal{E}_{\rm{POD}}
is a user-specified tolerance. The number of basis functions is then selected to be equal to the smallest
N_{\rm{POD}}
, for which
Eq.

66
is fulfilled. For the weighted stress and higher-order stress, good results were obtained with
\mathcal{E}_{\rm{POD}}={5}\times 10^{-3}
, for which
M=28
and
L=28
were found. For the fluctuation field, three values of
\mathcal{E}_{\rm{POD}}={1}\times 10^{-4}

and

,

and

The DNS solution took several hours (with one thread), mostly because of the detection of instabilities (i.e., checking the system matrix for negative eigenvalues and eigenvalues close to zero). If a large-scale problem (in 3D) was considered, the DNS solution might become infeasible, since (1) detecting negative eigenvalues is computationally expensive, and (2) negative eigenvalues of the system matrix may cause problems for iterative solvers, while direct solvers become too computationally expensive for such large systems. On the other hand, the ROM solution should remain relatively computationally inexpensive, since the solver can be easily parallelized by solving all RVE problems at the macroscopic integration points in parallel. An additional advantage of the ROM is that, after training, different geometrical parameters inside the macrostructure can be easily tuned, while for the DNS, the meshing can become expensive and challenging, especially for 3D problems. This makes this ROM an interesting candidate for the material design of buckling structures.

5
Conclusions

In this work, we proposed a reduced-order model (ROM) for second-order computational homogenization (CH2), based on proper orthogonal decomposition and a novel hyperreduction method that uses ideas from the empirical cubature method that is specifically suited for CH2. Several aspects on the derivation of the reduced system, including the treatment of constraints and geometrical parameterizations, expressions for the effective quantities, and the novel hyperreduction algorithm were discussed. Afterwards, the ROM was tested on two numerical examples, in which the macrostructures are compressed and multiscale buckling occurs. The ROM solutions were critically evaluated by comparison against the results obtained by direct numerical simulation (DNS) and the full CH2 model. The first example demonstrated that the proposed hyperreduction algorithm discovered integration points and weights that yield accurate results for several parameterizations of the microstructure. The second example concerned a more complex application, in which the geometry of the microstructures is varied within the macroscopic domain, and for which the DNS solution takes a substantial amount of time to compute. When employing the ROM for this problem, speed-ups ranging from 15 to 139 as compared to the DNS were achieved with one thread. These speed-ups could be further increased by employing more threads, since, in general, the multiscale problem scales much better than the DNS.

To the best of our knowledge, this work is the first attempt of accelerating an enriched computational homogenization formulation. Although we proposed a reduced-order model for CH2, we are confident that our findings and employed methods also extend to other formulations, e.g., based on micromorphic computational homogenization. It would also be interesting to test the methodology on more complicated material models, such as damage and fracture. As different parameterizations of the microstructure can be treated as well, interesting applications can be realized with this framework, such as two-scale shape optimization problems, design of materials and uncertainty quantification.
