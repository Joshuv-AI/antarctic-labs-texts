Action Principles and Conservation Laws for Chew-Goldberger-Low Anisotropic Plasmas
G. M. Webb \aff 1 \corresp, S. C. Anco \aff 2, S. V. Meleshko \aff 3, & G. P. Zank \aff 1,4

Abstract
The ideal CGL plasma equations, including the double adiabatic conservation laws for the parallel ( p ∥ p_{\parallel} ) and perpendicular pressure ( p ⟂ p_{\perp} ), are investigated using a Lagrangian variational principle. An Euler-Poincaré variational principle is developed and the non-canonical Poisson bracket is obtained, in which the non-canonical variables consist of the mass flux 𝐌 {\bf M} , the density ρ \rho , the entropy variable σ = ρ ​ S \sigma=\rho S and the magnetic induction 𝐁 {\bf B} . Conservation laws of the CGL plasma equations are derived via Noether’s theorem. The Galilean group leads to conservation of energy, momentum, center of mass, and angular momentum. Cross helicity conservation arises from a fluid relabeling symmetry, and is local or nonlocal depending on whether the gradient of S S is perpendicular to 𝐁 {\bf B} or otherwise. The point Lie symmetries of the CGL system are shown to comprise the Galilean transformations and scalings.



1
Introduction

In magnetohydrodynamics (MHD),
the ideal Chew-Goldberger-Low (CGL) equations (
Chew et al. 1956
)
describe plasmas in which
there is not enough scattering of the particles to have an isotropic pressure
and only lowest order gyro-radius effects terms are taken
into account in the particle transport
(i.e. finite Larmor-radius terms are neglected).
These equations can be viewed as
the small gyro-radius limit of the Vlasov fluid moment equations,
where the pressure tensor has the anisotropic (gyrotropic) form

{\sf p}=p_{\perp}{\sf I}+(p_{\parallel}-p_{\perp}){\bm{\tau}}{\bm{\tau}}
.
Here
{\bm{\tau}}=|{\bf B}|^{-1}{\bf B}
is the unit vector
along the magnetic field
and
{\sf I}
is the identity tensor.
The pressure component
p_{\parallel}-p_{\perp}
controls the anisotropy, i.e.
the pressure tensor is isotropic if
p_{\parallel}=p_{\perp}
.

Higher order gyro-radius effects and gyro-viscosity
lead to the extended anisotropic MHD equations
(see e.g.
Macmahon 1965
,
Ramos 2005a
;
Ramos 2005b
;

Sulem and Passot 2015
and
Hunana et al. 2019a
).
Braginskii 1965
,
Schnack 2009
;
and
Devlen and Pekunlu 2010
use an
isotropic pressure decomposition, which is not an anisotropic
CGL decomposition.
Hunana et al. 2022
considered various generalizations
of the
Braginskii 1965
model using the Landau collisional operator
for the case of multi-species plasmas.
A recent Hamiltonian version of extended gyro-viscous MHD
has been developed by
Lingam, Morrison and Wurm 2020
.

In the present work,
we develop a variational formulation of
the ideal CGL plasma equations
starting from the Lagrangian formulation of (
Newcomb 1962
).
Our main goals are to derive and discuss:

(1) an Euler-Poincare action principle;

(2) a non-canonical Poisson bracket and its Casimirs;

(3) conservation laws from application of Noether’s theorem to the Galilean group of Lie point symmetries;

(4) a cross-helicity conservation law from particle relabeling symmetries
and its nonlocal nature when the entropy gradients are non-orthogonal to the magnetic field.

Previous work on the CGL equations can be found in

Abraham-Shrauner 1967
;
Hazeltine et al. 2013
;

Holm and Kupershmidt 1986
;
Ramos 2005a
;
Ramos 2005b
;
Hunana et al. 2019a
;
Hunana et al. 2019b
;

Du et al. 2020
.

Cheviakov and Bogoyavlenskij 2004
and
Cheviakov and Anco 2008
derived exact,
anisotropic MHD equilibria solutions of the CGL equations,
with a modified equation of state
for incompressible fluid flows.
Cheviakov and Bogoyavlenskij 2004
obtained an infinite
group of Lie symmetries of the anisotropic plasma equilibium equations for steady flow configurations. An infinite dimensional family of transformations
between the isotropic MHD equilibrium equations and solutions of the
anisotropic CGL equations were obtained.
Ilgisonis 1996
studies the
stability of steady CGL plasma equilibria in a tokamak for a generalized
Grad-Shafranov
equation, taking into account the fluid relabelling symmetry,
and covers previous stability criteria for ideal CGL plasma and ideal MHD
cases.

Our methods are adapted from MHD and ideal fluid mechanics.
It is well known how to use a Lagrangian map to relate Eulerian and Lagrangian fluid quantities (
Newcomb 1962
),
and this underpins the Euler-Poincaré action principle
which is a Lagrangian counterpart of a Hamiltonian formulation
(
Holm et al. 1998
;
Webb 2018
and
Webb and Anco 2019
).
This action principle is based on the Lagrangian map
and utilizes an associated Lie algebra.

Recent work by
Dewar et al. 2020
and
Dewar and Qu 2022
developed variational
principles to describe time- dependent relaxed MHD using a
global cross helicity constraint with a phase space Lagrangian action
principle (PSL) as opposed to a configuration space Lagrangian (CSL) action.
The theory has been used to describe
multi-region, extended MHD (RxMHD) in fusion plasma devices.
In ideal MHD current sheets can develop. One
of the main ideas in this work is to provide a global fitting together
of ideal MHD sub-regions, subject to global cross helicity and magnetic
helicity constraints by using Lagrange multipliers. This is a rather
complicated theory that lies beyond the scope of the present paper.

Noether’s theorem has been used to obtain conservation laws for ideal MHD
(
Webb and Anco 2019
; see also
Padhye and Morrison 1996a
;
Padhye and Morrison 1996b
;
Padhye 1998
).
A different approach to deriving conservation laws is Lie dragging of differential
forms, vector fields and tensors, as developed by
(
Moiseev et al. 1982
;
Sagdeev et al. 1990
;

Tur and Yanovsky 1993
;
Webb et al. 2014a
;
Webb et al. 2014b
;

Gilbert and Vanneste 2020
;
Besse and Frisch 2017
;
Anco and Webb 2020
).

Section
2
summarizes the ideal CGL plasma equations.
The anisotropic pressure components
p_{\parallel}
and
p_{\perp}

satisfy double adiabatic conservation laws.
The total energy equation for the system arises from
combining the internal energy equation, the kinetic energy equation,
and the electromagnetic energy equation (i.e. Poynting’s theorem).
The cross helicity and Galilean conservation laws are obtained for
barotropic and non-barotropic gas equations of state.
The magnetic helicity transport equation and the conservation of magnetic helicity
is described.
The thermodynamics of CGL plasmas are discussed.
This leads to an internal energy density of the form
e=e(\rho,S,B)
for the plasma
(e.g.
Holm and Kupershmidt 1986
;
Hazeltine et al. 2013
),
where
B=|{\bf B}|
is the magnitude of the magnetic field.
It applies to the situation when reversible energy changes occur,
with the temperature of the plasma being given by
T=e_{S}
,
and it yields an equation of state which incorporates the double adiabatic conservation laws.
A comparison is given with a more restrictive different approach that applies the ideal gas law to the plasma (e.g.
Du et al. 2020
),
with an equation of state being derived as a consequence.

Section
3

describes the Lagrangian map between
the Eulerian fluid particle position
{\bf x}

and the Lagrangian particle position
{\bf x}_{0}
.
This map is obtained from integrating the system of ordinary differential equations

d{\bf x}/dt={\bf u}({\bf x},t)
with the fluid velocity
{\bf u}({\bf x},t)

assumed to be a known function of
{\bf x}
and
t
and
{\bf x}={\bf x}_{0}
at time
t=0
.
The Lagrangian map
{\bf x}({\bf x}_{0},t)
is used to write the fluid equations as a variational principle (
Newcomb 1962
;
Holm et al. 1998
).
In this description the canonical coordinates are
{\bf q}={\bf x}({\bf x}_{0},t)
,
and the canonical momenta are defined by the Legendre transformation

{\bf p}=\partial L_{0}/\partial\dot{\bf x}
where
L_{0}
is the Lagrangian density
in a frame moving with the fluid.

Section
4

develops the Euler-Poincaré action principle for the CGL plasma equations
by using the general method of
Holm et al. 1998
and the results of
Newcomb 1962
.

In Section
5
,
the Poisson bracket
\{F,G\}
of functionals
F
and
G
is described.
We obtain the noncanonical Poisson bracket of
Holm and Kupershmidt 1986
but give more
details.
By converting the variational derivatives for functionals from the canonical coordinates

({\bf q},{\bf p})
to physically motivated non-canonical coordinates,
the Poisson bracket is converted to its non-canonical form in the new variables.
The symplectic (i.e. Hamiltonian) form of the non-canonical Poisson bracket is determined,
which is useful in proving the Jacobi identity and for writing down the Casimir
determining equations.
The Casimirs of the Poisson bracket commute with the Hamiltonian functional
characterizing the system and are obtained using the approach
of
Hameiri 2004

(see also
Marsden and Ratiu 1994
,
Morrison 1982
,
Holm et al. 1985
,

Padhye and Morrison 1996a
;
Padhye and Morrison 1996b
and
Padhye 1998
).

Section 6
describes Noether’s theorem for the CGL variational principle
using the Lagrangian variables.
Noether’s theorem produces conservation laws in terms of these variables
from variational symmetries. The Eulerian form of the conservation laws is obtained
through the Lagrangian map by use of a result of
Padhye 1998
.
The variational symmetries include the Galilean group: in particular,
(i) time translation invariance yields energy conservation;
(ii) space translation invariance yields momentum conservation;
(iii) invariance under Galilean boosts yields center of mass conservation;
(iv) invariance of under rotations yields angular momentum conservation.

In addition, fluid relabeling symmetries are shown to be variational symmetries which yield the generalized cross helicity conservation law for CGL plasmas.
The conditions under which this conservation law is local or nonlocal are delineated.
A local conservation law is shown to arise when the parallel and perpendicular entropies,

S_{\parallel}
and
S_{\perp}
, have zero gradient along the magnetic field.
Alternatively, for an internal energy density
e=e(\rho,S,B)
,
a local conservation law occurs when
{\bf B}{\bf\cdot}\nabla S=0
.
When these conditions fail to hold in the plasma,
the cross helicity conservation law is nonlocal and depends on the integration of
the temperatures back along the Lagrangian fluid particle paths.

The present paper can be thought of as an extension of
the work of
Holm and Kupershmidt 1986
. We restrict our analysis to non-relativistic flows,
whereas
Holm and Kupershmidt 1986
study both relativistic and non-relativistic flow versions of
the CGL equations. Our analysis is more complete than
Holm and Kupershmidt 1986

in that: (i) our analysis takes into account
more recent developments in anisotropic moment equations for plasmas
with an anisotropic pressure tensor (e.g.
Hazeltine et al. 2013

and
Hunana et al. 2019a
). In particular, the formulations of the equation
of state and the first law of thermodynamics used by
Holm and Kupershmidt 1986

were not clear because of the brevity
of the exposition. (ii) We show that a slightly more
general, non-separable equation of state can be used than that of

Hazeltine et al. 2013
(see
30
et seq.);
(iii) We provide a more complete treatment of the conservation
laws for the CGL system, and identify the symmetries of the Lagrangian
which give rise to the conservation laws in Noether’s theorem. Our analysis
shows that the cross helicity conservation law is a consequence
of a fluid relabelling symmetry, which is in general
a nonlocal conservation law unless
{\bf B}
is normal to the
entropy gradients of
S_{\parallel}
,
S_{\perp}
and
S
, in which case
the conservation law is local. (iv) We describe
the CGL Poisson bracket of

Holm and Kupershmidt 1986
which uses the noncanonical
variables
\rho
,
{\bf M}=\rho{\bf u}
,
\sigma=\rho S
and
{\bf B}
.
The entropy variable used in the non-canonical Poisson bracket is

\sigma=\rho S
where
S
is the entropy. The entropy
S
can in turn be
decomposed into the form
S=S_{\parallel}+S_{\perp}
.
The non-canonical Poisson bracket
derivation in Appendix D is more rigorous than that used by
most authors as it takes into account the variations in the basis vectors
used to define vectors and tensors. However these changes
do not modify the net Poisson bracket, because they only lead at most to
changes in the boundary terms in the bracket which are discarded (see
e.g.
Holm et al. 1983
for the more rigorous general derivation
of the non-canonical Poisson bracket in MHD). (v) We provide a modern version of Noether’s theorem which only uses
the evolutionary symmetry operator. We describe
the classical form of Noether’s theorem obtained by
Bluman and Kumei 1989
,
in Appendix H. In Section 6.1 we derive CGL conservation laws
using the more modern form of Noether’s theorem.
Section 6.2 gives the classical
version of Noether’s theorem to derive CGL plasma conservation laws.

Section
7

concludes with a summary and discussion.

The various technical derivations used in the main results,
and the Lie point symmetries of the CGL system,
are summarized in Appendices A to G.

2
CGL Equations and Conservation Laws

In this section, we first summarize the basic CGL plasma model equations,
and then we discuss the thermodynamics of CGL plasmas,
followed by the key conservation laws in Eulerian form:
total energy; cross helicity and nonlocal conserved cross helicity;
magnetic helicity.
These conservation laws, in later sections, will be related to the variational symmetries of the action principle for the CGL equations and expressed in Lagrangian form.
Some general remarks about Eulerian conservation law equations,
which will be used in the discussions, are summarized in Section
6
.

2.1
CGL Model

The physical variables which describe CGL plasmas are
the fluid velocity
{\bf u}
, fluid density
\rho
, magnetic field induction
{\bf B}
,
the anisotropic pressure components
p_{\parallel}
and
p_{\perp}
,
and the entropy
S
.
The ideal CGL plasma equations are similar to the MHD equations and consist of:
the mass continuity equation

\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}+\nabla{\bf\cdot}(\rho{\bf u})=0;

(1)

the momentum equation (in semi-conservative form)

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(\rho{\bf u})+\nabla{\bf\cdot}\left(\rho{\bf u}{\bf u}+{\sf p}+{\sf M}_{B}\right)=-\rho\nabla\Phi,

(2)

in which
{\sf p}
is the gyrotropic pressure tensor
(which replaces the isotropic gas pressure
p{\sf I}
used in MHD)
given by

{\sf p}=p_{\perp}{\sf I}+(p_{\parallel}-p_{\perp})\bm{\tau}\bm{\tau},\quad\bm{\tau}=\frac{\bf B}{B},\quad B=|{\bm{B}}|,

(3)

and
{\sf M}_{B}
is the magnetic pressure tensor

{\sf M}_{B}=\frac{1}{\mu_{0}}\left(\frac{B^{2}}{2}{\sf I}-{\bf BB}\right)=\frac{B^{2}}{\mu_{0}}\left(\frac{1}{2}{\sf I}-{\bm{\tau}}{\bm{\tau}}\right);

(4)

the entropy transport equation

\frac{\partial S}{\partial t}+{\bf u}{\bf\cdot}\nabla S=0;

(5)

Faraday’s equation and Gauss’s law

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\bf B}{\partial t}-\nabla\times\left({\bf u}\times{\bf B}\right)=

\displaystyle 0,

(6)

\displaystyle\nabla{\bf\cdot B}=

\displaystyle 0;

(7)

along with the pressure equations

\displaystyle\frac{\partial p_{\parallel}}{\partial t}+\nabla{\bf\cdot}\left(p_{\parallel}{\bf u}\right)+2{\sf p}^{\parallel}{\bf:}\nabla{\bf u}=

\displaystyle 0,

(8)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial p_{\perp}}{\partial t}+\nabla{\bf\cdot}\left(p_{\perp}{\bf u}\right)+{\sf p}^{\perp}{\bf:}\nabla{\bf u}=

\displaystyle 0,

(9)

where
{\sf p}^{\parallel}
and
{\sf p}^{\perp}
are the terms comprising the gyrotropic pressure tensor

{\sf p}^{\parallel}=p_{\parallel}\bm{\tau}\bm{\tau},\quad{\sf p}^{\perp}=p_{\perp}\left({\sf I}-\bm{\tau}\bm{\tau}\right),\quad{\sf p}={\sf p}^{\parallel}+{\sf p}^{\perp}.

(10)

Through Faraday’s equation (
6
) and the mass continuity equation (
1
),
the pressure equations (
8
)-(
9
) can be expressed as the double
adiabatic equations

\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{p_{\parallel}B^{2}}{\rho^{3}}\right)=0,\quad\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{p_{\perp}}{\rho B}\right)=0,

(11)

which represent conservation of the particle magnetic moment
and the second longitudinal adiabatic moment of the particles,
where
d/dt=\partial/\partial t+{\bf u}{\bf\cdot}\nabla
is the Lagrangian time derivative following the flow.

In the momentum equation (
2
),

\Phi
is the potential of an external source of gravity
(for example, in the case of the solar wind, it could represent
the gravitational potential field of the Sun).

If one counts the number of evolution equations
in (
1
)-(
11
) there are 10 equations
for the 10 variables
{\bf u}
,
{\bf B}
,
\rho
,
S
,
p_{\parallel}
and
p_{\perp}
. If one does not need to know
S
then there are 9 equations
for 9 unknowns.

The non-canonical Poisson bracket formulation of MHD in
Morrison and Greene 1982

uses Faraday’s law (
6
) in form

\frac{\partial\bf B}{\partial t}-\nabla\times\left({\bf u}\times{\bf B}\right)+{\bf u}\nabla{\bf\cdot}{\bf B}=0

for the mathematical case in which
\nabla{\bf\cdot}{\bf B}\neq 0
(see also
Webb 2018
).
In fact, the possibility of
\nabla{\bf\cdot}{\bf B}\neq 0
arises in
numerical MHD due to numerical errors in the Gauss’ law (
7
).

To determine the relationship between the entropy
S
, the internal energy density
\varepsilon
, and the pressure components
p_{\parallel}
and
p_{\perp}

in (
1
)-(
11
) we first note that
\varepsilon

is given by:

\varepsilon=\frac{p_{\parallel}+2p_{\perp}}{2}.

(12)

A different, related approach comes from the entropy law for ideal gases.
We will consider and compare both approaches.

One link between the approaches is the observation that
integration of the double adiabatic equations (
11
) yields

p_{\parallel}=\exp\left({\bar{S}}_{\parallel}\right)\frac{\rho^{3}}{B^{2}},\quad p_{\perp}=\exp\left({\bar{S}}_{\perp}\right)\rho B,

(13)

where the quantities
{\bar{S}}_{\parallel}
and
{\bar{S}}_{\perp}

are dimensionless forms of entropy integration constants
arising from integrating the double adiabatic equations (
11
).
In principle,
{\bar{S}}_{\parallel}
and
{\bar{S}}_{\perp}

must be scalars advected with the flow. In particular these quantities
could be functions of the entropy
S
satisfying (
5
). They
could also be functions of other advected invariants of the flow,
such as
{\bf B}{\bf\cdot}\nabla S/\rho
(e.g.
Tur and Yanovsky 1993
,
Webb et al. 2014a
).
For the sake of simplicity, we assume that
S_{\parallel}
and

S_{\perp}
depend only on
S
. The overbars on
{\bar{S}}
,

{\bar{S}}_{\parallel}
and
{\bar{S}}_{\perp}

denote dimensionless versions of these quantities.

If these
S_{\parallel}
and
S_{\perp}
are functions solely of the entropy
S
,
then the internal energy density
\varepsilon
will have the
functional form
\varepsilon(\rho,S,B)
.
This form also arises from the first law of thermodynamics coming from
the transport equation for
\varepsilon
when reversible thermodynamic
processes are considered.
Strictly speaking, we should use normalized
or dimensionless variables in (
13
), i.e. we should have used
the variables:

{\bar{p}}_{\parallel}=\frac{p_{\parallel}}{p_{\parallel 0}},\quad{\bar{p}}_{\perp}=\frac{p_{\perp}}{p_{\perp 0}},\quad{\bar{\bf B}}=\frac{{\bf B}}{B_{0}},\quad\bar{\rho}=\frac{\rho}{\rho_{0}},

(14)

where the subscript zero quantities are dimensional constants. In a
convenient abuse
of notation, we have dropped the overbar superscripts in (
13
).

2.2
Thermodynamic formulation

A physical description of
p_{\parallel}
and
p_{\perp}
and their relation
to the internal energy density
\varepsilon
was developed
first by
Holm and Kupershmidt 1986
, which in part uses the work of
Volkov 1966
,
and also later by
Hazeltine et al. 2013
,
which discusses both reversible and irreversible thermodynamics.
Here we will concentrate on the ideal reversible dynamics case.

An analysis of reversible work done on the plasma
reveals that the internal energy per unit mass of the CGL plasma,

e=\varepsilon/\rho
,
obeys the Pfaffian equation

de=TdS+\frac{p_{\parallel}}{\rho^{2}}d\rho-\frac{p_{\Delta}}{\rho B}dB.

(15)

where
T
is the adiabatic temperature in the plasma.
The expression (
12
) for the internal energy density gives the relations

e=\frac{\varepsilon}{\rho}=\frac{(p_{\parallel}+2p_{\perp})}{2\rho},\quad p_{\Delta}=p_{\parallel}-p_{\perp},

(16)

(see also
Holm and Kupershmidt 1986
)
and

p\equiv\frac{(p_{\parallel}+2p_{\perp})}{3}=\frac{2\varepsilon}{3},

(17)

which is the gas pressure defined by one third of the trace of the CGL pressure tensor (
3
).

The Pfaffian equation (
15
) constitutes the first law of thermodynamics for a CGL plasma.
As outlined in
Hazeltine et al. 2013
,
it can be derived from the transport equation for
\varepsilon

under thermodynamic processes that involve reversible work done on the plasma
described by the gyrotropic part of the Vlasov distribution function.
The derivation can be generalized to include irreversible work comprising
a dissipative term and a further term due to gyro-viscosity,
which is a non-dissipative term obtained in the limit of no scattering.
Equation (
15
) is equivalent to the non-dissipative internal
energy equation (
51
) in which a source term

\rho TdS/dt
has been added to the right hand-side.

The above interpretation of equation (
15
) implies

e=e(\rho,S,B)
along with the thermodynamic relations

T=e_{S}=\varepsilon_{S}/\rho,\quad p_{\parallel}=\rho^{2}e_{\rho}=\rho\varepsilon_{\rho}-\varepsilon,\quad p_{\Delta}=-\rho Be_{B}=-B\varepsilon_{B}.

(18)

Thus, if
e(\rho,S,B)
is known, i.e. an equation of state has been specified,
then the relations (
18
) give
p_{\parallel}
and
p_{\perp}

as functions of
\rho,S,B
.
Consistency must hold with the double adiabatic equations (
11
),
which constrains the possible expressions for
e(\rho,S,B)
.

To derive the constraints, we first substitute the relations (
18
)
into the internal energy (
17
),
giving

e=\frac{1}{\rho}\left(\frac{3p_{\parallel}}{2}-p_{\Delta}\right)=\frac{3}{2}\rho e_{\rho}+Be_{B}.

(19)

This is a first order partial differential equation for
e(\rho,S,B)

which is easily solved by using the method of characteristics
(e.g.
Sneddon 1957
):

\frac{d\rho}{(3\rho/2)}=\frac{dB}{B}=\frac{de}{e}.

(20)

Integrating these differential equations yields the general solution

e=\rho^{2/3}f(\xi,S),\quad\xi=B\rho^{-2/3},

(21)

where
f(\xi,S)
is an arbitrary function of
S
and the similarity variable
\xi
.
Now, the relations (
18
) imply that

T=\rho^{2/3}f_{S},

(22)

and

p_{\parallel}=\frac{2}{3}\rho^{5/3}\left(f-\xi f_{\xi}\right),\quad p_{\perp}=\frac{1}{3}\rho^{5/3}\left(2f+\xi f_{\xi}\right).

(23)

Substitution of expressions (
23
) into the double adiabatic equations (
11
)
followed by use of the relation
B=\rho^{2/3}\xi
then yields

\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{2}{3}\xi^{2}f-\frac{2}{3}\xi^{3}f_{\xi}\right)=0,\quad\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{2}{3\xi}f+\frac{1}{3}f_{\xi}\right)=0,

(24)

with
df/dt=f_{\xi}d\xi/dt
for any process in which
S
is adiabatic
(cf the entropy equation (
5
)).
However, from Faraday’s equation (
6
) and the mass continuity equation (
1
),
we find that
\xi
obeys the transport equation

\frac{d}{dt}\xi=\left(-\frac{1}{3}\nabla{\bf\cdot}{\bf u}+{\bm{\tau}}{\bm{\tau}}{\bf:}\nabla{\bf u}\right)\xi.

(25)

Thus,
\xi
is not an advected quantity, which implies that the adiabatic equations (
24
) for
f(\xi,S)
reduce to:

\frac{2}{3}\xi^{2}f-\frac{2}{3}\xi^{3}f_{\xi}=c_{\parallel}(S),\quad\frac{2}{3\xi}f+\frac{1}{3}f_{\xi}=c_{\perp}(S).

(26)

The general solution of this pair of equations is given by

f(\xi,S)=c_{\perp}(S)\xi+c_{\parallel}(S)\frac{1}{2\xi^{2}}.

(27)

Consequently, expressions (
23
) yield the equation of state

p_{\parallel}=c_{\parallel}(S)\rho^{3}/B^{2},\quad p_{\perp}=c_{\perp}(S)\rho B,

(28)

along with the relations

{\bar{S}}_{\parallel}=\ln c_{\parallel}(S),\quad{\bar{S}}_{\perp}=\ln c_{\perp}(S)

(29)

from the double adiabatic integrals (
13
).
The corresponding internal energy (
21
) and pressure (
17
)
have the explicit form

e=c_{\perp}(S)B+c_{\parallel}(S)\frac{\rho^{2}}{2B^{2}}=\exp({\bar{S}}_{\perp})B+\exp({\bar{S}}_{\parallel})\frac{\rho^{2}}{2B^{2}},

(30)

and

p=c_{\perp}(S)\frac{2\rho B}{3}+c_{\parallel}(S)\frac{\rho^{3}}{3B^{2}}=\exp({\bar{S}}_{\perp})\frac{2\rho B}{3}+\exp({\bar{S}}_{\parallel})\frac{\rho^{3}}{3B^{2}}.

(31)

Note that the expressions for
e
and
p
in terms of the double adiabatic integrals

\bar{S}_{\parallel}
and
\bar{S}_{\perp}
hold independently of any form of equation of state.
Finally, either equation (
22
) or equation (
18
),
both of which rely on
e=e(\rho,S,B)
, yield the plasma temperature:

T=c_{\perp}^{\prime}(S)B+c_{\parallel}^{\prime}(S)\frac{\rho^{2}}{2B^{2}}=\frac{d{\bar{S}}_{\perp}}{dS}\exp({\bar{S}}_{\perp})B+\frac{d{\bar{S}}_{\parallel}}{dS}\exp({\bar{S}}_{\parallel})\frac{\rho^{2}}{2B^{2}}.

(32)

Hazeltine et al. 2013
arrives at a similar but less general result
under the assumption that
f
is separable in
S
and
\xi
.
This leads to
c_{\parallel}(S)=Q(S)d_{\parallel}
and
c_{\perp}(S)=Q(S)d_{\perp}
,
where
d_{\perp}
and
d_{\parallel}
are constants.
The assumption of separability includes, for example,
the case where the gas pressure
p
satisfies the ideal gas law:

p=\rho RT\quad\hbox{or}\quad T=\frac{p}{\rho R},

(33)

where
R
is the gas constant.
In this case, from the relation (
17
),
it follows that
f
must satisfy the equation

\frac{\partial f}{\partial S}=\frac{2}{3R}f
.
Then it further follows that
Q(S)=\exp(2S/(3R))
.

Conditions on the derivatives of the internal energy
e

for thermodynamic stability are discussed in
Hazeltine et al. 2013
,
but these considerations lie beyond the scope of the present work.

2.2.1
Ideal gas law and entropy

The double adiabatic equations (
8
) for a CGL plasma
can be combined in the form

\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{p_{\parallel}p_{\perp}^{2}}{\rho^{5}}\right)=0

(34)

which suggests the advected quantity

p_{\parallel}p_{\perp}^{2}/\rho^{5}
may be viewed as a function of the entropy
S
.
A specific functional relation can be motivated by considering
the MHD limit for a non-relativistic gas, where the gas entropy is given by the standard formula

S=C_{v}\ln\left(\frac{p}{\rho^{\gamma}}\right),\quad\gamma=\frac{C_{p}}{C_{v}}=\frac{5}{3},

(35)

with
C_{v}
and
C_{p}
being the specific heats at constant volume and pressure, respectively.
In a CGL plasma, the gas pressure
p
is given by expression (
17
)
in terms of the pressure components
p_{\parallel}
and
p_{\perp}

which are equal in the MHD limit, since the pressure tensor (
3
)
must become isotropic.
This results in the relation
p_{\parallel}=p_{\perp}=p
,
and as a consequence

p_{\parallel}p_{\perp}^{2}/\rho^{5}=p^{3}/\rho^{5}=\exp(3S/C_{v})

by assuming the formula (
35
).
Generalizing this relation away from the MHD limit then suggests the formula

S=C_{v}\ln\left(\frac{p_{\parallel}^{1/3}p_{\perp}^{2/3}}{\rho^{5/3}}\right)

(36)

for the entropy of a CGL plasma (see e.g.
Abraham-Shrauner 1967
and
Du et al. 2020
).

Furthermore, since the double adiabatic integrals (
13
)
give
p_{\parallel}p_{\perp}^{2}/\rho^{5}=\exp(\bar{S}_{\parallel}+2\bar{S}_{\perp})

where
\bar{S}_{\parallel}
and
\bar{S}_{\perp}
are each advected,
the entropy formula (
36
) can be expressed as

S=(C_{v}/3)(\bar{S}_{\parallel}+2\bar{S}_{\perp})
.
This relation now suggests that the entropy is a sum of components

S=S_{\parallel}+S_{\perp}\quad\hbox{where}\quad S_{\parallel}\equiv C_{v\parallel}{\bar{S}}_{\parallel}\quad\text{and}\quad S_{\perp}\equiv C_{v\perp}{\bar{S}}_{\perp},

(37)

with

C_{v_{\parallel}}\equiv\frac{1}{3}C_{v},\quad C_{v_{\perp}}\equiv\frac{2}{3}C_{v}.

(38)

Correspondingly, the internal energy density (
16
) of the plasma
can be split into a sum of densities

\varepsilon=\frac{p_{\parallel}+2p_{\perp}}{2}=\frac{p_{\parallel}}{(\gamma_{\parallel}-1)}+\frac{p_{\perp}}{(\gamma_{\perp}-1)}\equiv\varepsilon_{\parallel}+\varepsilon_{\perp},

(39)

with effective adiabatic indices:

\gamma_{\parallel}=3,\quad\gamma_{\perp}=2.

(40)

Further note that

\varepsilon=\frac{p}{\gamma-1},\quad p=\frac{1}{3}(p_{\parallel}+2p_{\perp}),\quad\gamma=\frac{5}{3}

(41)

in terms of the gas pressure
p
.

In addition, by applying the standard ideal gas law (
33
) to each of the pressure components
p_{\parallel}
and
p_{\perp}
,
we can define corresponding temperatures:

T_{\parallel}\equiv p_{\parallel}/(\rho R)\quad\text{and}\quad T_{\perp}\equiv p_{\perp}/(\rho R)

(42)

Then the internal energy density (
39
) can be expressed as

\varepsilon=R\rho\left(\tfrac{1}{2}T_{\parallel}+T_{\perp}\right),

(43)

which yields the relations

C_{v_{\parallel}}=e_{T_{\parallel}}=\frac{1}{2}R,\quad C_{v_{\perp}}=e_{T_{\perp}}=R,\quad C_{v}=\frac{3}{2}R

(44)

from the specific heats (
38
).
Finally, by substituting the double adiabatic integrals (
13
)
into the quantities (
42
) and using expressions (
37
) and (
44
),
we obtain

\frac{\partial T_{\parallel}}{\partial S_{\parallel}}=2T_{\parallel}/R,\quad\frac{\partial T_{\perp}}{\partial S_{\perp}}=T_{\perp}/R,

(45)

which implies the expected thermodynamic relations:

e_{S_{\parallel}}=T_{\parallel},\quad e_{S_{\perp}}=T_{\perp}.

(46)

The preceding approach to the entropy of CGL plasmas has been adopted by
Du et al. 2020
.
It is equivalent to the thermodynamic approach specialized to the case of an ideal gas
in which the gas law is assumed to hold for all three pressures
p
,
p_{\parallel}
,
p_{\perp}
.

The net upshot of the above discussion is that we take
e=e(\rho,S,B)

as the equation of state for the gas in the rest of the paper.
Note however, that
S_{\parallel}=S_{\parallel}(S)
and

S_{\perp}(S)=S-S_{\parallel}(S)
are functions of
S
.

e=e(\rho,S,B)
is the form of the equation of state used by
Holm and Kupershmidt 1986
.
The CGL Poisson bracket of
Holm and Kupershmidt 1986

uses the entropy variable
\sigma=\rho S

to describe the complicated thermodynamics of the CGL plasma.

2.3
Total Mass and Energy Conservation Laws

The mass continuity equation (
1
) can be expressed in the familiar co-moving form

\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}+{\bf u}{\bf\cdot}\nabla\rho=-\rho\nabla{\bf\cdot}{\bf u}.

(47)

On a volume
V(t)
moving with the fluid,
the corresponding mass integral is conserved:

\frac{d}{dt}\int_{V(t)}\rho\,d^{3}x=0.

(48)

A co-moving equation for the internal energy density (
12
)
is obtained by combining the parallel and perpendicular pressure equations (
8
)-(
9
), which yields

\frac{\partial\varepsilon}{\partial t}+\nabla{\bf\cdot}(\varepsilon{\bf u})+(p_{\parallel}-p_{\perp})\bm{\tau}\bm{\tau}{\bf:}\nabla{\bf u}+p_{\perp}\nabla{\bf\cdot}{\bf u}=0.

(49)

This equation can be written in terms of the gyrotropic pressure tensor
{\sf p}

by noting from expression (
10
) that

{\sf p}{\bf\cdot}\nabla{\bf u}=p_{\perp}\nabla{\bf\cdot}{\bf u}+(p_{\parallel}-p_{\perp})\bm{\tau}\bm{\tau}{\bf:}\nabla{\bf u}.

(50)

Then the co-moving equation (
49
) takes the form

\frac{\partial\varepsilon}{\partial t}+\nabla{\bf\cdot}\left(\varepsilon{\bf u}+\sf{p}{\bf\cdot u}\right)={\bf u}{\bf\cdot}(\nabla{\bf\cdot}\sf{p}),

(51)

which is analogous to the internal energy density equation in fluid dynamics and MHD.

The total kinetic energy equation for the plasma is obtained by taking
the scalar product of the momentum equation (
2
) with
{\bf u}
,
which yields:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\frac{1}{2}\rho u^{2}+\rho\Phi\right)+\nabla{\bf\cdot}\left({\bf u}\left(\frac{1}{2}\rho u^{2}+\rho\Phi\right)\right)

(52)

\displaystyle=-{\bf u}{\bf\cdot}\nabla{\bf\cdot}\sf{p}+{\bf J}{\bf\cdot}{\bf E}+{\bf u\cdot B}\frac{\nabla{\bf\cdot}{\bf B}}{\mu_{0}},

where
u=|{\bf u}|
, and

{\bf E}=-{\bf u}\times{\bf B}\quad\hbox{and}\quad{\bf J}=\frac{\nabla\times{\bf B}}{\mu_{0}},

(53)

are the electric field
{\bf E}
, and the electric current
{\bf J}
.

Using Maxwell’s equations (
6
)–(
7
), we obtain Poynting’s
theorem (the electromagnetic energy equation) in the form:

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\frac{1}{2\mu_{0}}B^{2}\right)+\nabla{\bf\cdot}\left(\frac{1}{\mu_{0}}{\bf E}\times{\bf B}\right)=-{\bf J\cdot E}-\frac{1}{\mu_{0}}(\nabla{\bf\cdot}{\bf B})({\bf u\cdot B}).

(54)

The equation for the total energy in conserved form is obtained by
adding the internal energy equation (
51
), the kinetic energy
equation (
52
) and the electromagnetic energy equation (
54
),
which gives

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\frac{1}{2}\rho u^{2}+\rho\Phi+\varepsilon+\frac{1}{2\mu_{0}}B^{2}\right)+\nabla{\bf\cdot}\left(\left(\frac{1}{2}\rho u^{2}+\rho\Phi+\varepsilon\right){\bf u}+{\sf p}{\bf\cdot}{\bf u}+\frac{1}{\mu_{0}}{\bf E}\times{\bf B}\right)=0.

(55)

It is useful here to note that the Poynting electromagnetic energy flux is

{\bf F}_{\text{em}}=\frac{1}{\mu_{0}}{\bf E}\times{\bf B}=\frac{B^{2}}{2\mu_{0}}{\bf u}+{\bf u}{\bf\cdot}{\sf M}_{B}.

(56)

The resulting energy balance equation
on a volume
V(t)
moving with the fluid is given by

\frac{d}{dt}\int_{V(t)}\left(\frac{1}{2}\rho u^{2}+\rho\Phi+\varepsilon+\frac{1}{2\mu_{0}}B^{2}\right)\,d^{3}x=-\oint_{\partial V(t)}{\bf u}{\bf\cdot}\left({\sf p}+{\sf M}_{B}\right){\bf\cdot}\hat{\bf n}\,dA

(57)

in terms of the magnetic pressure tensor (
4
).
The flux terms in (
57
) represent the rate at which the total pressure tensor

{\sf p}+{\sf M}_{B}
does work on the fluid.
Note that there is no contribution due to advection of the total energy density.
Further discussion is provided in Section
6.1.1

(see also
Padhye 1998
and
Anco and Dar 2009
;
Anco and Dar 2010
).
In addition, note that if the total pressure tensor
has no perpendicular component at the moving boundary,
i.e.
({\sf p}+{\sf M}_{B}){\bf\cdot}\hat{\bf n}=0
,
then the moving energy integral will be conserved (i.e. a constant of the motion).

2.4
Cross Helicity

The cross helicity transport equation for a CGL plasma is obtained by taking
the scalar product of
{\bf u}
with Faraday’s equation (
6
)
plus the scalar product of
{\bf B}
with the momentum equation (
2
),
in the following form.
By a standard cross-product identity,
Faraday’s equation can be written

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}{\bf B}-{\bf B}{\bf\cdot}\nabla{\bf u}+{\bf u}{\bf\cdot}\nabla{\bf B}+(\nabla{\bf\cdot}{\bf u}){\bf B}=0.

(58)

The momentum equation minus
{\bf u}
times the mass continuity equation (
1
) yields the velocity equation:

\frac{d}{dt}{\bf u}=-\frac{1}{\rho}\nabla{\bf\cdot}\left({\sf p}+{\sf M}_{B}\right)-\nabla\Phi,

(59)

where
-(1/\rho)\nabla{\bf\cdot}{\sf p}
is the acceleration
due to the anisotropic pressure.
Forming and adding the respective scalar products with
{\bf u}
and
{\bf B}

then gives the equation for cross-helicity density
{\bf u\cdot B}
as:

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}({\bf u\cdot B})+\nabla{\bf\cdot}\left[({\bf u\cdot}{\bf B}){\bf u}+\left(\Phi-\frac{1}{2}u^{2}\right){\bf B}\right]=-{\bf B}{\bf\cdot}\left(\frac{1}{\rho}\nabla{\bf\cdot}{\sf p}\right),

(60)

where
u=|{\bf u}|
.

The thermodynamic form of this transport equation (
60
)
is obtained by using the equation

-\nabla{\bf\cdot}{\sf p}={\bf B}\times(\nabla\times{\bm{\Omega}})-{\bm{\Omega}}(\nabla{\bf\cdot}{\bf B})+\rho(T\nabla S-\nabla h),

(61)

which relies on the Pfaffian equation (first law of thermodynamics) (
15
)
for a CGL plasma with an internal energy
e(\rho,B,S)
,
where, from the thermodynamic relations (
18
),

T=e_{S}
is the gas temperature,
and
h=\varepsilon_{\rho}=e+\rho e_{\rho}
is the enthalpy of the fluid:

h=\frac{3p_{\parallel}+2p_{\perp}}{2\rho}.

(62)

Here

{\bm{\Omega}}=\frac{p_{\Delta}}{B}\bm{\tau}.

(63)

Note that the case
{\bm{\Omega}}=0
corresponds to the MHD limit
in which the anisotropy vanishes,
p_{\Delta}=p_{\parallel}-p_{\perp}=0
.
A derivation of the pressure divergence equation (
61
) is given in Appendix B.
An alternative derivation is provided within the Euler-Poincaré formulation
of the CGL equations in Section 4.1 and Appendix E.
The scalar product of this equation with
\frac{1}{\rho}{\bf B}

reduces to:

-{\bf B}{\bf\cdot}\left(\frac{1}{\rho}\nabla{\bf\cdot}{\sf p}\right)={\bf B}{\bf\cdot}\left(T\nabla S\right)-\nabla{\bf\cdot}\left(h{\bf B}\right)

(64)

using Gauss’ law (
7
).
As a result, the cross helicity density transport equation in thermodynamic form is simply

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}({\bf u\cdot B})+\nabla{\bf\cdot}\left[({\bf u\cdot}{\bf B}){\bf u}+\left(\Phi+h-\frac{1}{2}u^{2}\right){\bf B}\right]={\bf B}{\bf\cdot}(T\nabla S).

(65)

The term on the righthand side of the cross helicity transport equation (
65
)
can be written in a conserved form through use of the equation

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\phi\nabla S{\bf\cdot}{\bf B}\right)+\nabla{\bf\cdot}\left[\left(\phi\nabla S{\bf\cdot}{\bf B}\right){\bf u}\right]=\left(\nabla S{\bf\cdot}{\bf B}\right)\frac{d}{dt}\phi

(66)

which holds for any scalar variable
\phi

and follows from Faraday’s equation (
6
)
and the entropy equation (
5
).
This leads to the nonlocal cross helicity density conservation law

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}({\bf w\cdot B})+\nabla{\bf\cdot}\left[({\bf w\cdot B}){\bf u}+\left(\Phi+h-\frac{1}{2}u^{2}\right){\bf B}\right]=0,

(67)

in terms of

{\bf w}={\bf u}+r\nabla S,

(68)

where
r
is a nonlocal variable obtained by integrating the temperature
T

back along the path of the Lagrangian fluid element.
Specifically,

\frac{dr}{dt}=-T,\quad r=-\int^{t}T\,dt,

(69)

where
d/dt=\partial/\partial t+{\bf u}{\bf\cdot}\nabla
is the Lagrangian time derivative.

The nonlocal conservation law (
67
) yields a moving cross-helicity balance equation (cf Appendix C):

\frac{d}{dt}\int_{V(t)}{\bf w\cdot B}\,d^{3}x=-\oint_{\partial V(t)}\left(\Phi+h-\frac{1}{2}u^{2}\right){\bf B}{\bf\cdot}\hat{\bf n}\,dA

(70)

on a volume
V(t)
moving with the fluid.
Note that if
{\bf B}
is perpendicular to the boundary, i.e.
{\bf B\cdot}\hat{\bf n}=0
,
then the cross-helicity integral
\int_{V(t)}{\bf w\cdot B}\,d^{3}{\bf x}
is conserved in the flow.

An alternative form of the nonlocal conservation law (
67
)
is obtained by directly taking the divergence of the pressure tensor
\sf p

using the gyrotropic expression (
10
),
and combining it with the gradient of the enthalpy (
62
).
As shown in Appendix B, this gives

\displaystyle{\bf B}{\bf\cdot}\left(\frac{1}{\rho}\nabla{\bf\cdot}{\sf p}-\nabla h\right)

\displaystyle=-\left(\frac{p_{\parallel}}{2\rho}{\bf B}{\bf\cdot}\nabla\ln c_{\parallel}(S)+\frac{p_{\perp}}{\rho}{\bf B}{\bf\cdot}\nabla\ln c_{\perp}(S)\right)

(71)

\displaystyle=-\left(\frac{p_{\parallel}}{2\rho}{\bf B}{\bf\cdot}\nabla{\bar{S}}_{\parallel}+\frac{p_{\perp}}{\rho}{\bf B}{\bf\cdot}\nabla{\bar{S}}_{\perp}\right)

in terms of the adiabatic integrals (
13
) and (
37
).
Expression (
71
) is equivalent to
-{\bf B}{\bf\cdot}(T\nabla S)

through the temperature expression (
32
).
Instead if we use the gas law temperatures (
49
) and the specific heats (
51
)
associated with
p_{\parallel}
and
p_{\perp}
,
then we can write

{\bf B}{\bf\cdot}\left(\frac{1}{\rho}\nabla{\bf\cdot}{\sf p}-\nabla h\right)=-\left(T_{\parallel}{\bf B}{\bf\cdot}\nabla S_{\parallel}+T_{\perp}{\bf B}{\bf\cdot}\nabla S_{\perp}\right)

(72)

in term of the gas law entropies (
37
).
Substituting expression (
72
) into the cross helicity
density

transport equation (
65
) yields

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}({\bf u\cdot B})+\nabla{\bf\cdot}\left[({\bf u\cdot}{\bf B}){\bf u}+{\bf B}\left(\Phi+h-\frac{1}{2}u^{2}\right)\right]={\bf B}{\bf\cdot}(T_{\parallel}\nabla S_{\parallel}+T_{\perp}\nabla S_{\perp}).

(73)

This result (
73
) can be also derived using the
Newcomb 1962

action principle for CGL plasmas shown in Section 4.

The corresponding nonlocal cross helicity density conservation law is given by

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(\tilde{\bf w}{\bf\cdot B})+\nabla{\bf\cdot}\left[(\tilde{\bf w}{\bf\cdot B}){\bf u}+{\bf B}\left(\Phi+h-\frac{1}{2}u^{2}\right)\right]=0,

(74)

where

\tilde{\bf w}={\bf u}+r_{\parallel}\nabla S_{\parallel}+r_{\perp}\nabla S_{\perp},

(75)

with
r_{\parallel}
and
r_{\perp}
being nonlocal variables
defined by the equations:

\frac{dr_{\parallel}}{dt}=-T_{\parallel},\quad\frac{dr_{\perp}}{dt}=-T_{\perp}.

(76)

The nonlocal cross helicity
density
conservation law for MHD analogous to equation (
67
)
was developed in
Webb et al. 2014a
;
Webb et al. 2014b
,
Webb 2018
,
Webb and Anco 2019
,
Yahalom 2017a
;
Yahalom 2017b
and
Yahalom and Qin 2021
.
A topological interpretation of the generalized cross helicity conservation law
has been found in terms of an MHD Aharonov-Bohm effect
in
Yahalom 2017a
;
Yahalom 2017b
.
Yahalom 2013
discusses a topological
interpetation of magnetic helicity as an Aharonov Bohm effect in MHD.

If
{\bf B}{\bf\cdot}\nabla S=0
, then the nonlocal conservation law (
67
)
reduces to a local cross helicity
density
conservation law given by
r=0
and
{\bf w}={\bf u}
.
This result is analogous to the local cross helicity conservation law in MHD (e.g.
Webb 2018
;
Webb and Anco 2019
).

It is interesting to note that the velocity equation (
59
)
can be written in the suggestive form:

\frac{d{\bf u}}{dt}=T\nabla S-\nabla h+\frac{\tilde{\bf J}\times{\bf B}}{\rho}-\nabla\Phi,

(77)

where

\tilde{\bf J}\equiv{\bf J}-\nabla\times{\bm{\Omega}}=\frac{\nabla\times\tilde{\bf B}}{\mu_{0}},\quad\tilde{\bf B}\equiv{\bf B}\left(1-\frac{\mu_{0}p_{\Delta}}{B^{2}}\right).

(78)

This equation (
77
) turns out to arise directly from the non-canonical Hamiltonian formulation presented in Section
5.1
.

2.5
Magnetic Helicity

In ideal MHD and in ideal CGL plasmas, Faraday’s equation (
6
)
written in terms of the electric field

\frac{\partial\bf B}{\partial t}+\nabla\times{\bf E}=0

(79)

can be uncurled to give
{\bf E}=-{\bf u}\times{\bf B}

in the form:

{\bf E}=-\nabla\phi_{E}-\frac{\partial\bf A}{\partial t}=-({\bf u}\times{\bf B})\quad\hbox{where}\quad{\bf B}=\nabla\times{\bf A}.

(80)

The uncurled form of Faraday’s equation (
80
) implies

\frac{\partial\bf A}{\partial t}+{\bf E}+\nabla\phi_{E}=0.

(81)

Combining the scalar product of Faraday’s equation (
79
) with
{\bf A}

plus the scalar product of the uncurled equation (
81
) with
{\bf B}

yields the magnetic helicity transport equation

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}({\bf A\cdot B})+\nabla{\bf\cdot}\left({\bf E}\times{\bf A}+\phi_{E}{\bf B}\right)=0,

(82)

which can also be written in the form:

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}({\bf A\cdot B})+\nabla{\bf\cdot}\left[({\bf A\cdot B}){\bf u}+(\phi_{E}-{\bf u\cdot A}){\bf B}\right]=0.

(83)

The total magnetic helicity for a volume
V(t)
moving with the fluid is given by

H^{\text{M}}=\int_{V(t)}{\bf A}{\bf\cdot}{\bf B}\,d^{3}x.

(84)

It satisfies the moving balance equation (cf Appendix C)

\frac{dH^{\text{M}}}{dt}=-\oint_{\partial V(t)}(\phi_{E}-{\bf A\cdot u}){\bf B}{\bf\cdot}\hat{\bf n}\,dA,

(85)

where
\hat{\bf n}
is the outward unit normal of the moving boundary
\partial V(t)
.
Thus, if
{\bf B}
is perpendicular to the boundary, i.e.
{\bf B\cdot}\hat{\bf n}=0
,
then
H^{\text{M}}
is conserved in the flow.
This discussion applies both to ideal MHD and also to ideal CGL plasmas.
(e.g.
Woltjer 1958
,
Kruskal and Kulsrud 1958
,
Moffatt 1969
;
Moffatt and Ricca 1992
,
Berger and Field 1984
;
Arnold and Khesin 1998
).

It is beyond the scope of the present exposition to discuss the issues of how to define
the relative magnetic helicity for volumes
V
for which
{\bf B\cdot n}\neq 0
on
\partial V

(e.g.
Berger and Field 1984
;
Finn and Antonsen 1985
;
Finn and Antonsen 1988
;
Webb et al. 2010
).
Similar considerations apply to field line magnetic helicity (e.g.
Prior and Yeates 2014
)
and absolute magnetic helicity (e.g.
Low 2006
;
Low 2011
;
Berger and Hornig 2018
).

It is of interest to note that, under gauge transformations
(i.e.
{\bf A}\to{\bf A}+\nabla\chi
and
\phi_{E}\to\phi_{E}-\frac{\partial\chi}{\partial t}
for an arbitrary function
\chi(t,{\bf x})
),
the moving balance equation is gauge invariant, but the magnetic helicity integral
changes by addition of a boundary integral:

H^{\text{M}}\to H^{\text{M}}+\oint_{\partial V(t)}\chi{\bf B}{\bf\cdot}\hat{\bf n}\,dA
.
However, if the electric field potential
\phi_{E}
satisfies the gauge

\phi_{E}={\bf A\cdot u},

(86)

then the moving flux of the magnetic helicity vanishes
and the resulting (gauge-dependent) magnetic helicity
H^{\text{M}}

is conserved in the flow.
This gauge turns out to hold precisely when
{\bf A}
is advected by the flow,
which will be shown in the next subsection.

2.6
Lie dragged (advected) quantities

A quantity,
a
, is Lie dragged by the fluid flow if its advective
Lie derivative vanishes:

\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial t}+{\cal L}_{\bf u}\right)a=0,

(87)

where
{\cal L}_{\bf u}
is the ordinary Lie derivative with respect to the vector field
{\bf u}

(e.g.
Tur and Yanovsky 1993
;
Webb et al. 2014a
;
Anco and Webb 2020
).
For a scalar quantity, its advective Lie derivative reduces to its material derivative

\frac{d}{dt}=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}+{\bf u}{\bf\cdot}\nabla.

(88)

For quantities that are vector fields or differential forms, the advective Lie derivative
also contains a rotation-shear term which involves
\nabla{\bf u}
.

In CGL plasmas,
S
is an advected scalar, as are the
double adiabatic integrals (
13
).
There are two basic advected non-scalar quantities:
the differential form

{\bm{\alpha}}=\nabla S{\bf\cdot}d{\bf x}\equiv\nabla_{i}Sdx^{i}

and the vector field
{\bf b}=(1/\rho){\bf B}\equiv b^{i}\ \nabla_{i}
(note
the vector field
{\bf b}
is a directional derivative operator).
The contraction of the vector field
{\bf b}
with the co-vector
or differential form
{\bm{\alpha}}
is a scalar (
note that
\nabla_{i}\rfloor dx^{j}=\delta^{i}_{j}
, and

{\bf b}\rfloor{\bm{\alpha}}=b^{i}\alpha_{i}\equiv b^{i}\nabla_{i}S
). Notice that

{\bf b}
is Lie dragged with the flow, (this statement
is equivalent to Faraday’s equation, when one takes into account
the mass continuity equation: i.e.,

(\partial_{t}+{\cal L}_{\bf u}){\bf b}=0
which implies

\partial_{t}{\bf b}+[{\bf u},{\bf b}]=0
).

{\bm{\alpha}}
is a one-form or co-vector, that is Lie dragged with the flow.
The inner product of the vector field
{\bf b}
with the one-form
{\bm{\alpha}}

is a scalar invariant which is advected with the
flow (e.g.
Tur and Yanovsky 1993
).
Thus, the quantities

\frac{{\bf B}{\bf\cdot}\nabla S}{\rho},\quad\frac{{\bf B}{\bf\cdot}\nabla\bar{S}_{\parallel}}{\rho},\quad\frac{{\bf B}{\bf\cdot}\nabla\bar{S}_{\perp}}{\rho}

(89)

are advected scalars in CGL plasmas.

The gauge (
86
) in which the magnetic helicity integral is conserved
is equivalent to Lie dragging the one-form
\alpha={\bf A\cdot}d{\bf x}

with the fluid flow:

\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial t}+{\cal L}_{\bf u}\right)({\bf A\cdot}d{\bf x})=\left(\frac{\partial\bf A}{\partial t}-{\bf u\times B}+\nabla({\bf A\cdot u})\right){\bf\cdot}d{\bf x}=0,

(90)

which vanishes due to the uncurled form of Faraday’s equation (
80
)
combined with equation (
86
).

Holm and Kupershmidt 1983a
;
Holm and Kupershmidt 1983b
used this advected-
{\bf A}
gauge in
the formulation of non-canonical Poisson brackets for MHD and for multi-fluid plasmas
(see also
Gordin and Petviashvili 1987
;
Gordin and Petviashvili 1989
,
Padhye and Morrison 1996a
;
Padhye and Morrison 1996b
and
Padhye 1998
).

3
The Lagrangian Map

In a Lagrangian formulation of MHD and CGL plasmas,
fluid elements are given labels that are constant in the fluid flow.
The simplest labeling consists of initial values
{\bf x}_{0}={\bf x}(0)

for integrating the flow equations of a fluid element

\frac{d{\bf x}(t)}{dt}={\bf u}({\bf x}(t),t),

(91)

in which the fluid velocity
{\bf u}
is assumed to be a known function of
{\bf x}
and
t
.
In general, fluid labels are given by functions
{\bf a}={\bf a}({\bf x}_{0})
.

A Lagrangian map is an invertible mapping from the Lagrangian fluid labels
{\bf a}
to the Eulerian position coordinates
{\bf x}(t)={\bf X}({\bf a},t)
for the motion of a fluid element.
For simplicity, we will take
{\bf x}
and
{\bf a}={\bf x}_{0}
to be expressed
in terms of Cartesian coordinates
{\bf x}=(x,y,z)
and
{\bf x}_{0}=(x_{0},y_{0},z_{0})
.
Then, the Lagrangian map takes the form

{\bf x}={\bf X}({\bf x}_{0})

(92)

with the
t
dependence being suppressed in the notation.
Invertibility implies that the Jacobian of this map is non-degenerate:

J=\det(X_{ij})\neq 0\quad\hbox{where}\quad X_{ij}\equiv\frac{\partial X^{i}({\bf x}_{0})}{\partial x_{0}^{j}}.

(93)

We will write
Y_{ij}
to denote the inverse of the matrix
X_{ij}
, whereby

Y_{ik}X_{kj}=X_{ik}Y_{kj}=\delta_{ij}

(94)

with
\delta_{ij}
denoting the components of the identity matrix.
Recall the standard formulae:

Y_{ij}=J^{-1}A_{ji},\quad X_{ji}A_{ki}=A_{ik}X_{ij}=J\delta_{jk},\quad\frac{\partial J}{\partial X_{ij}}=A_{ij},

(95)

where
A_{ij}={\rm cofac}(X_{ij})
is the cofactor matrix of
X_{ij}
.
In addition, note that

\dot{J}=\frac{\partial J}{\partial X_{ij}}\dot{X}_{ij}=A_{ij}\frac{\partial\dot{X}^{i}}{\partial x_{0}^{j}}.

(96)

3.1
Map Formulas

We now formulate the maps between the CGL plasma variables

\rho
,
S
,
{\bf B}
,
p_{\parallel}
,
p_{\perp}
,
and their Lagrangian counterparts

\rho_{0}({\bf x}_{0})
,
S_{0}({\bf x}_{0})
,
{\bf B}_{0}({\bf x}_{0})
,
p_{\parallel}({\bf x}_{0})
,
p_{\perp}({\bf x}_{0})
.

The density
\rho_{0}
of a fluid element with label
{\bf x}_{0}

is related to the Eulerian density by the mass conservation equation

\rho d^{3}x=\rho_{0}d^{3}x_{0},

(97)

where
d^{3}x=Jd^{3}x_{0}
.
This implies

\rho=\frac{\rho_{0}}{J}.

(98)

From
Newcomb 1962
the Cartesian components of
B^{i}
in the
Eulerian frame are related to the Lagrangian magnetic field component

B_{0}^{k}
by the equation:

B^{i}=\frac{X_{ik}B_{0}^{k}}{J}.

(99)

The derivation of (
99
) follows by noting:

\displaystyle B^{i}d\sigma_{i}=

\displaystyle B^{k}_{0}d\sigma_{0k},

(100)

\displaystyle d^{3}x=

\displaystyle dx^{i}d\sigma_{i}=Jd^{3}x_{0}=Jdx^{k}_{0}d\sigma_{ok}\equiv JY_{ki}dx^{i}d\sigma_{0k}.

(101)

Equation (
100
) describes the conservation of magnetic flux,
where
d\sigma_{i}
is the flux tube area normal to the
x^{i}
coordinate surface,
and
d\sigma_{0k}
is the flux tube area normal to the
x^{k}_{0}
surface
in the Lagrangian frame.
Equation (
101
) relates the volume elements

d^{3}x
to
d^{3}x_{0}
. From (
101
) one obtains

d\sigma_{i}=A_{ik}d\sigma_{0}^{k},

(102)

for the transformation between the area elements
d\sigma_{i}
and
d\sigma_{0}^{k}
.
The magnetic flux conservation equation (
100
) now gives the
transformation
B_{0}^{k}=A_{ik}B^{i}
which in turn implies the transformation
(
99
).

An alternative form of the relation (
99
) arises from the Lie dragged or frozen-in vector field

{\bf b}=\frac{B^{i}}{\rho}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{i}}=\frac{B_{0}^{k}}{\rho_{0}}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{0}^{k}}\equiv\frac{B_{0}^{k}}{\rho_{0}}X_{ik}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{i}},

(103)

which implies the transformation (
99
) between
B^{i}
and
B_{0}^{k}
.
In (
103
) we use the modern differential geometry
notion of a vector field as a directional derivative
operator (e.g.
Misner et al. 1973
).

From the relation (
99
), we have

B^{2}=\frac{\zeta^{2}B_{0}^{2}}{J^{2}}\quad\hbox{where}\quad\zeta^{2}=X_{ij}\tau_{0}^{j}X_{ik}\tau_{0}^{k}

(104)

in terms of the unit vector
\tau_{0}=B_{0}^{-1}{\bf B}_{0}
along the magnetic field
{\bf B}_{0}
(cf (
3
)),
with
B_{0}=|{\bf B}_{0}|
being the magnetic field strength at
{\bf x}_{0}
.
Hence, we obtain:

B=\frac{\zeta\,B_{0}}{J},\quad\tau^{i}=\frac{B^{i}}{B}=\frac{X_{ij}\tau_{0}^{j}}{\zeta}.

(105)

The latter relation can be inverted to obtain
\bm{\tau}_{0}

in terms of
\bm{\tau}
through the formulae (
95
):

\tau_{0}^{i}=\frac{\zeta}{J}A_{ji}\tau^{j}.

(106)

Next, from the double adiabatic conservation laws (
11
), we have
the frozen-in quantities

\frac{p_{\perp}}{\rho B}=\frac{p_{\perp 0}}{\rho_{0}B_{0}},\quad\frac{p_{\parallel}B^{2}}{\rho^{3}}=\frac{p_{\parallel 0}B_{0}^{2}}{\rho_{0}^{3}}.

(107)

By combining these equations with the previous ones (
105
) and (
98
),
we obtain

p_{\parallel}=\frac{p_{\parallel 0}}{J\zeta^{2}},\quad p_{\perp}=\frac{p_{\perp 0}\zeta}{J^{2}},

(108)

which gives the respective relations between the pressures
p_{\parallel 0}
and
p_{\perp 0}

at a fluid element with label
{\bf x}_{0}

and the Eulerian pressures
p_{\parallel}
and
p_{\perp}
.

Finally, we note that the Eulerian entropy
S
is equal to the entropy
S_{0}

at a fluid element with label
{\bf x}_{0}
,
since
S
is frozen-in by the transport equation (
5
).

3.2
Lagrangian Variational Principle and
Euler-Lagrange Equations

The Lagrangian variational principle for MHD and for CGL plasmas was obtained in
Newcomb 1962
):

{\mathcal{A}}=\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\int_{V}L\,d^{3}x\,dt=\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\int_{V_{0}}L_{0}\,d^{3}x_{0}\,dt,

(109)

where
L
is the Lagrangian in Eulerian variables and
L_{0}
is its counterpart
arising through the Lagrangian map.
Here
V
is a fixed spatial domain and
[t_{0},t_{1}]
is a fixed time interval.
In general,
L
will be given by the kinetic energy density minus the potential energy density for a fluid element.
The kinetic energy density for both MHD and CGL plasmas is simply

\frac{1}{2}\rho u^{2}
, with
u=|{\bf u}|
.
Subtracting this expression from the total energy density for a CGL plasma
(cf the moving energy balance equation (
57
))

H\equiv\frac{1}{2}\rho u^{2}+\rho\Phi+\varepsilon+\frac{1}{2\mu_{0}}B^{2}

(110)

yields the potential energy density

\rho\Phi+\varepsilon+\frac{1}{2\mu_{0}}B^{2}
,
where
\varepsilon
is the internal energy density (
12
).
Thus, a CGL plasma has the Lagrangian

L=\frac{1}{2}\rho u^{2}-\rho\Phi-\varepsilon-\frac{1}{2\mu_{0}}B^{2},

(111)

where, for the sake of completeness, we have included the energy contributed by
an external gravitational potential
\Phi({\bf x})

(e.g. which would arise from the gravitational field of the Sun in the case of solar and interplanetary physics).
The corresponding Lagrangian
L_{0}
is obtained from the relation

Ld^{3}x=L_{0}d^{3}x_{0}=LJd^{3}x_{0}
, which gives

L_{0}=JL.

(112)

Substituting the Eulerian expressions (
111
), (
108
), (
), (
98
), and (
92
) into
L_{0}
,
and then using expression (
12
) for the internal energy density
in terms of the pressures
p_{\parallel}
and
p_{\perp}
,
we obtain

L_{0}=\rho_{0}({\bf x}_{0})\left(\frac{1}{2}|\dot{\bf X}|^{2}-\Phi({\bf X})\right)-\left(\frac{p_{\parallel 0}({\bf x}_{0})}{2\zeta^{2}}+\frac{p_{\perp 0}({\bf x}_{0})\zeta}{J}\right)-\frac{\zeta^{2}B_{0}({\bf x}_{0})^{2}}{2\mu_{0}J}.

(113)

The Euler-Lagrange equations of the resulting action principle (
109
)
for a CGL plasma are obtained by variation of the Cartesian components
X^{i}
of the Lagrangian map (
92
),
modulo boundary terms:

\delta{\mathcal{A}}/\delta X^{i}=0
.
In essence, we work in a reference frame moving with the fluid flow,
where the dynamics is described in terms of how the Eulerian position
{\bf x}

of the frame varies with the fluid labels
{\bf x}_{0}
and the time
t

through the Lagrangian map (
92
).
Equivalently, the Euler-Lagrange equations are given by applying to
L_{0}

the Euler operator (variational derivative)
E_{X^{i}}
in the calculus of variations,
where
X^{i}(x_{0}^{j},t)
is a function of
x^{j}_{0}
and
t
.
Thus,

\frac{\delta{\mathcal{A}}}{\delta X^{i}}\equiv E_{X^{i}}(L_{0})=\frac{\partial L_{0}}{\partial X^{i}}-\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\frac{\partial L_{0}}{\partial\dot{X}^{i}}\right)-\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{0}^{j}}\left(\frac{\partial L_{0}}{\partial X_{ij}}\right)=0,

(114)

where
\partial/\partial_{t}
and
\partial/\partial_{x_{0}^{j}}
act as total derivatives.
(See section
6
for more details of variational calculus.)
In Appendix C,
we show that when this equation (
114
) is simplified and expressed in terms of Eulerian variables,
it reduces to the Eulerian momentum equation (
2
).

3.3
Hamilton’s Equations

We now derive the corresponding Hamilton formulation of the Euler-Lagrange equation (
114
) for a CGL plasma.
Here we will write
X^{i}=x^{i}
for simplicity.

First, the generalized momentum is defined as:

\pi_{i}=\frac{\partial L_{0}}{\partial\dot{x}^{i}}=\rho_{0}\dot{x}^{i}.

(115)

Next, the Hamiltonian density is defined by the Legendre transformation:

H_{0}=\pi_{k}\dot{x}^{k}-L_{0}.

(116)

After substitution of the CGL Lagrangian (
113
) along with
\dot{x}^{i}
in terms of the momentum (
115
),
we then obtain the expression

H_{0}=\frac{\pi_{i}\pi_{i}}{2\rho_{0}}+\rho_{0}\Phi({\bf x})+\frac{p_{\parallel 0}}{2\zeta^{2}}+\frac{p_{\perp 0}\zeta}{J}+\frac{X_{ij}X_{ik}B_{0}^{j}B_{0}^{k}}{2\mu_{0}J}.

(117)

In Eulerian variables, this expression is given by

H_{0}=JH

(118)

where
H
is the Eulerian total energy density (
110
).
The resulting Hamiltonian equations of motion consist of

\dot{x}^{i}=\frac{\delta{\mathcal{H}}}{\delta\pi^{i}},\quad\dot{\pi}^{i}=-\frac{\delta{\mathcal{H}}}{\delta x^{i}},

(119)

which yield the momentum relation (
115
)
and the Euler-Lagrange equation (
114
),
where the Hamiltonian function is defined as

{\mathcal{H}}=\int_{V}H_{0}\,d^{3}x_{0}=\int_{V}H\,d^{3}x.

(120)

As in ordinary Hamiltonian mechanics,
with the use of the canonical variables
q^{i}=x^{i}
and
p^{i}=\pi^{i}
,
Hamilton’s equations (
119
) can be written in a canonical Poisson bracket formulation:

\dot{{\mathcal{F}}}=\left\{{\mathcal{F}},{\mathcal{H}}\right\}=\int\left({\mathcal{F}}_{q^{i}}{\mathcal{H}}_{p^{i}}-{\mathcal{F}}_{q^{i}}{\mathcal{H}}_{p^{i}}\right)\,d^{3}x_{0}.

(121)

Here
{\mathcal{F}}
is a general functional of
q^{i}
and
p^{i}

(see
Morrison 1982
).
There is an equivalent, non-canonical Poisson bracket that employs only Eulerian variables,
which we will present in Section
5
.

4
Euler-Poincaré Action Principle

In this section,
we formulate the Euler-Poincaré action principle for the CGL plasma equations.
We follow the developments in
Holm et al. 1998
,
Webb et al. 2014b
,
which provide the Euler-Poincaré formulation for MHD.
The main difference for a CGL plasma compared to MHD is that
the internal energy density
\varepsilon=\rho e
(cf (
16
))
depends on
\rho
,
S
, and
B
,
whereas for MHD it depends only on
\rho
and
S
.

The Lagrangian and Hamiltonian action principles in Section
3

are based on a reference frame that is attached to fluid elements, whereby
the Eulerian position
{\bf x}
of the frame is a function of
fluid element labels
{\bf x}_{0}
and time
t
,
as given by the Lagrangian map (
92
).
In contrast, the Euler-Poincaré action principle is based on the use of a fixed Eulerian reference frame,
in which the motion of a fluid element is given by inverting the Lagrangian map
so that the fluid element label
{\bf x}_{0}
becomes a function of
{\bf x}
and
t
.
To set up this formulation,
it is convenient to introduce the notation

g{\bf x}_{0}={\bf x}

(122)

with
g
at any fixed time
t
representing an element in the group of diffeomorphisms on Euclidean space in Cartesian coordinates
{\bf x}
and
{\bf x}_{0}
.
Note that
g
has an inverse
g^{-1}
defined by
g^{-1}{\bf x}={\bf x}_{0}
,
while
g|_{t=0}
is just the identity map.
In (
122
) and the sequel,
the
t
dependence of
g
and
{\bf x}_{0}
is suppressed for simplicity of notation;
an overdot stands for
\dot{}=\partial/\partial t
.
The diffeomorphism group will be denoted
G\equiv{\rm Diff}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})
.

The coordinate components of a fluid element label,
x_{0}^{i}
,
represent advected quantities:

\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial t}+{\bf u}{\bf\cdot}\nabla\right)x_{0}^{i}=0.

(123)

From equations (
122
) and (
123
),
we see that

\dot{x}_{0}^{i}=(\dot{(g^{-1})}{\bf x})^{i}=-(g^{-1}\dot{g}g^{-1}{\bf x})^{i}=-(g^{-1}\dot{g}{\bf x}_{0})^{i}=-{\bf u}{\bf\cdot}\nabla x_{0}^{i}.

(124)

Hence, we obtain

g^{-1}\dot{g}={\bf u}{\bf\cdot}\partial_{\bf x},

(125)

which can be viewed as both a left-invariant vector field in the tangent space of
G

and the directional derivative along the fluid flow in Euclidean space.
The property of left-invariance means that, for any fixed element
h
in
G
,

g\to hg
implies
\eta\to(hg)^{-1}\dot{(hg)}=g^{-1}h^{-1}h\dot{g}=g^{-1}\dot{g}=\eta
,
with
\dot{h}\equiv 0
.

Compared to the Lagrangian formulation,
whose dynamical variable is
{\bf x}({\bf x}_{0},t)
,
the Euler-Poincaré formulation uses the dynamical variables
\rho
,
S
,
B
, and
{\bf u}
,
which are functions of
{\bf x}
and
t
.
Variations of the Euler-Poincaré variables are defined through
variation of the inverse Lagrangian map,
g
, in the following way.

We consider a general variation
\delta g
and define

\eta\equiv g^{-1}\delta g

(126)

which represents a left invariant vector field on the group
G
.
Similarly to the identification (
125
),
we can write

\eta=\bm{\eta}{\bf\cdot}\partial_{\bf x}

(127)

viewed as the directional derivative associated with
\delta g
,
where
\bm{\eta}
is an Eulerian vector field.
(In general,

{\bf\cdot}\partial_{\bf x}
identifies Eulerian vector fields in Euclidean space
with left-invariant vector fields on the diffeomorphism group
G
.)

Now, the variation
\delta g
is intended to leave the fixed Eulerian reference frame unchanged,
whereby
\delta{\bf x}\equiv 0
.
Taking the corresponding variation of the inverse Lagrangian map (
122
)
yields

\delta x_{0}^{i}=\delta(g^{-1}x)^{i}=-(g^{-1}(\delta g)g^{-1}x)^{i}=-\eta x_{0}^{i}
.
Thus, since
x_{0}^{i}
is a function of
x^{j}
and
t
, we obtain

\delta x_{0}^{i}=-{\bm{\eta}}{\bf\cdot}\nabla x_{0}^{i}=-{\mathcal{L}}_{\bm{\eta}}x_{0}^{i},

(128)

where
{\mathcal{L}}_{\bm{\eta}}
is the Lie derivative with respect to the Eulerian vector field
\bm{\eta}
.

More generally, the same Lie derivative operation is used to define
the variation of any advected quantity (a scalar, or a vector, or a differential form),
a
:

\delta a=-{\mathcal{L}}_{\bm{\eta}}a,

(129)

where
a
satisfies the advection equation (
87
).
Expressions for the induced variations of
\rho
,
S
,
{\bf B}

can then be deduced by considering advected quantities in terms of those variables
(cf Section
2.6
and Section
3.1
).
The basic advected quantities in a CGL plasma are:

S,\quad(1/\rho){\bf B},\quad\rho\,d^{3}x,\quad{\bf B}{\bf\cdot}\hat{\bf n}dA,

(130)

where
dA
is the area element on a surface moving with the fluid.
(These quantities are also referred to as a Cauchy invariant.)
Since
S
is advected, its variation is given directly by equation (
129
).
Advection of
\rho\,d^{3}x
combined with
\delta x^{i}=0
implies that

(\delta\rho)\,d^{3}x=\delta(\rho\,d^{3}x)=-{\mathcal{L}}_{\bm{\eta}}(\rho\,d^{3}x)=-({\mathcal{L}}_{\bm{\eta}}\rho+\rho\nabla{\bf\cdot}{\bm{\eta}})\,d^{3}x

due to the well known expansion/contraction formula

{\mathcal{L}}_{\bf v}(d^{3}x)=\nabla{\bf\cdot}{\bf v}d^{3}x

holding for any vector field
{\bf v}
.
Thus, we have

\displaystyle\delta S

\displaystyle=-{\bm{\eta}}{\bf\cdot}\nabla S,

(131)

\displaystyle\delta\rho

\displaystyle=-\nabla{\bf\cdot}(\rho{\bm{\eta}}).

(132)

Next, since
(1/\rho){\bf B}
is advected,
this gives

\displaystyle\delta\left(\frac{{\bf B}}{\rho}\right)

\displaystyle=-{\mathcal{L}}_{\bm{\eta}}\left(\frac{{\bf B}}{\rho}\right)=\frac{({\mathcal{L}}_{\bm{\eta}}\rho){\bf B}}{\rho^{2}}-\frac{{\mathcal{L}}_{\bm{\eta}}{\bf B}}{\rho}

(133)

\displaystyle=-\frac{(\delta\rho){\bf B}}{\rho^{2}}+\frac{\delta{\bf B}}{\rho}.

Thus, after substituting
\delta\rho
from (
132
), we obtain

\delta{\bf B}=-(\nabla{\bf\cdot}{\bm{\eta}}){\bf B}-{\mathcal{L}}_{\bm{\eta}}{\bf B}={\bf B}{\bf\cdot}\nabla\bm{\eta}-\nabla{\bf\cdot}(\bm{\eta}{\bf B})=\nabla\times({\bm{\eta}}\times{\bf B}),

(134)

where
{\mathcal{L}}_{\bm{\eta}}{\bf B}=[{\bm{\eta}},{\bf B}]
is the commutator of vector fields.

Finally, the induced variation of
{\bf u}
is given by combining the equations

(\delta{\bf u}){\bf\cdot}\partial_{\bf x}=\delta(g^{-1}\dot{g})=-g^{-1}(\delta g)g^{-1}\dot{g}+g^{-1}(\dot{\delta g})=-\bm{\eta}{\bf\cdot}\partial_{\bf x}\otimes{\bf u}{\bf\cdot}\partial_{\bf x}+g^{-1}(\dot{\delta g})

(135)

and

(\dot{\delta g})=\dot{(g\eta)}=\dot{g}\eta+g\dot{\eta}=g({\bf u}{\bf\cdot}\partial_{\bf x}\otimes\bm{\eta}{\bf\cdot}\partial_{\bf x}+\dot{\bm{\eta}}{\bf\cdot}\partial_{\bf x}).

(136)

This yields

\delta{\bf u}{\bf\cdot}\partial_{\bf x}=\dot{\bm{\eta}}{\bf\cdot}\partial_{\bf x}-[\bm{\eta}{\bf\cdot}\partial_{\bf x},{\bf u}{\bf\cdot}\partial_{\bf x}]=\left(\dot{\bm{\eta}}-{\mathcal{L}}_{\bm{\eta}}{\bf u}\right){\bf\cdot}\partial_{\bf x}

(137)

where
[\cdot,\cdot]
is the commutator of vector fields
which coincides with the Lie bracket.
Thus, we obtain the variation:

\delta{\bf u}=\dot{\bm{\eta}}-{\mathcal{L}}_{\bm{\eta}}{\bf u}=(\partial/\partial t+{\mathcal{L}}_{{\bf u}})\bm{\eta}=\frac{d}{dt}\bm{\eta}\equiv\frac{\partial\bm{\eta}}{\partial t}+\left[{\bf u},{\bm{\eta}}\right].

(138)

4.1
The Euler-Poincaré equation

For CGL plasmas, the Euler-Poincaré action principle is given by

{\cal J}=\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\int_{V}L\,d^{3}x\,dt,

(139)

where
L
is the Lagrangian density (
111
).
Here
V
is a fixed spatial domain and
[t_{0},t_{1}]
is a fixed time interval.
The stationary points of this action principle,
\delta{\cal J}=0
,
under the variations (
138
), (
131
), (
132
), (
134
)
of the respective variables
{\bf u}
,
S
,
\rho
,
{\bf B}
,
turn out to yield the Eulerian momentum equation (
2
),
as we will now show.

The derivation of the stationary points is non-trivial because of the form of the variations
in terms of
\eta
given by equations (
126
) and (
127
).
Specifically, the variation of
{\cal J}
needs to be put into the form

\delta{\cal J}=\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\left\langle\eta,F\right\rangle\,dt=\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\int_{V}{\bm{\eta}}{\bf\cdot}{\bf F}\,d^{3}x\,dt

(140)

modulo boundary terms,
where
F={\bf F}{\bf\cdot}\partial_{\bf x}
is both
a left-invariant vector field in the tangent space of
G

and the directional derivative along a vector field
{\bf F}
in Euclidean space.
Then, since
{\bm{\eta}}
is an arbitrary vector field
(corresponding to an arbitrary variation
\delta g
),
the equation yielding the stationary points of
{\cal J}
is
{\bf F}=0
.

We now proceed to find
\delta{\cal J}
and
{\bf F}
.
A general expression is available for
{\bf F}
in
Holm et al. 1998
,
which involves the diamond operator,
\diamond
, defined by property:

\left\langle\frac{\delta L}{\delta a}\diamond a,\eta\right\rangle=-\left\langle\frac{\delta L}{\delta{a}},{\cal L}_{\eta}(a)\right\rangle,

(141)

where, as earlier,
a
is an advected quantity.
It will be instructive to instead show how to obtain
{\bf F}
from the variation
(
138
), (
131
), (
132
), (
134
)
directly using the Eulerian variables.

From the Lagrangian density (
111
), we obtain:

\displaystyle\frac{\delta L}{\delta{\bf u}}=\rho{\bf u},\quad\frac{\delta L}{\delta S}=-\varepsilon_{S}=-\rho T,\quad\frac{\delta L}{\delta\rho}=\frac{1}{2}u^{2}-\varepsilon_{\rho}-\Phi=\frac{1}{2}u^{2}-h-\Phi,

(142)

\displaystyle\frac{\delta L}{\delta{\bf B}}=-\varepsilon_{B}\bm{\tau}-\frac{\bf B}{\mu_{0}}=\left(\frac{p_{\Delta}}{B^{2}}-\frac{1}{\mu_{0}}\right){\bf B}\equiv-\frac{1}{\mu_{0}}\tilde{\bf B},

using the thermodynamic relations (
18
),
where
T
is the temperature and
h
is the enthalpy (
62
),
and
p_{\Delta}=p_{\parallel}-p_{\perp}
is the pressure anisotropy.
Here we have also used the identity
\delta B=\bm{\tau}{\bf\cdot}\delta{\bf B}
,
with
{\bm{\tau}}={\bf B}/B
being the unit vector along
{\bf B}
.
Note that
\tilde{\bf B}
is related
{\bf B}
by equation (
78
).
The main difference in these variational derivative expressions (
142
)
compared to the MHD case is the addition of the anisotropy term in
\delta L/\delta{\bf B}
, i.e.
{\bf B}
is replaced by
\tilde{\bf B}
.

The total variation of
L
is given by combining the products of
the variational derivatives (
142
) and the corresponding variations
(
138
), (
131
), (
132
), (
134
).
This yields

\displaystyle\delta L

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta L}{\delta{\bf u}}{\bf\cdot}\delta{\bf u}+\frac{\delta L}{\delta S}{\delta S}+\frac{\delta L}{\delta\rho}{\delta\rho}+\frac{\delta L}{\delta{\bf B}}{\bf\cdot}\delta{\bf B}

(143)

\displaystyle=\rho{\bf u}{\bf\cdot}\left(\dot{\bm{\eta}}-{\mathcal{L}}_{\bm{\eta}}{\bf u}\right)-\rho T\left(-{\bm{\eta}}{\bf\cdot}\nabla S\right)+\left(\frac{1}{2}u^{2}-h-\Phi\right)\left(-\nabla{\bf\cdot}(\rho{\bm{\eta}})\right)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\mu_{0}}\tilde{\bf B}{\bf\cdot}\left(\nabla\times({\bm{\eta}}\times{\bf B})\right).

The next step is to bring each term into the form (
140
),
modulo a total time derivative and a total divergence,
using integration by parts.
The first term in expression (
143
) expands out to give:

\displaystyle\rho{\bf u}{\bf\cdot}\dot{\bm{\eta}}+\rho{\bf u}{\bf u}{\bf:}\nabla\bm{\eta}-\rho(\bm{\eta}{\bf\cdot}\nabla{\bf u}){\bf\cdot}{\bf u}

\displaystyle=\bm{\eta}{\bf\cdot}\left(-\frac{\partial(\rho{\bf u})}{\partial t}-\frac{1}{2}\rho\nabla(u^{2})-\nabla{\bf\cdot}(\rho{\bf u}{\bf u})\right)

(144)

\displaystyle+\frac{\partial(\rho{\bf u}{\bf\cdot}\bm{\eta})}{\partial t}+\nabla{\bf\cdot}\left(\rho({\bf u}{\bf\cdot}\bm{\eta}){\bf u}\right).

Similarly, the third term in (
143
) yields

{\bm{\eta}}{\bf\cdot}\left(\rho\nabla\left(\frac{1}{2}u^{2}-h-\Phi\right)\right)+\nabla{\bf\cdot}\left(-\left(\frac{1}{2}u^{2}-h-\Phi\right)\rho{\bm{\eta}}\right).

(145)

The second term in (

{\bm{\eta}}{\bf\cdot}\left(\rho T\nabla S\right).

(146)

The last term in (

({\bf B}\times{\bm{\eta}}){\bf\cdot}\left(\frac{1}{\mu_{0}}\nabla\times\tilde{\bf B}\right)+\nabla{\bf\cdot}\left(\frac{1}{\mu_{0}}\tilde{\bf B}\times({\bm{\eta}}\times{\bf B})\right),

({\bf B}\times{\bm{\eta}}){\bf\cdot}\left(\frac{1}{\mu_{0}}\nabla\times\tilde{\bf B}\right)={\bm{\eta}}{\bf\cdot}\left(-\frac{1}{\mu_{0}}{\bf B}\times\left(\nabla\times\tilde{\bf B}\right)\right).

(148)

Now, combining the four terms (

146
), (
145
), (
144
),
and discarding the total derivatives,
we obtain

\displaystyle\delta L={\bm{\eta}}{\bf\cdot}\bigg(

\displaystyle-\frac{\partial(\rho{\bf u})}{\partial t}-\nabla{\bf\cdot}(\rho{\bf u}{\bf u})+\rho(T\nabla S-\nabla h-\nabla\Phi)-\frac{1}{\mu_{0}}{\bf B}\times\left(\nabla\times\tilde{\bf B}\right)\bigg)

modulo total time derivatives and total divergences.
This yields the desired relation

\int_{V}\delta L\,d^{3}x=\int_{V}\bm{\eta}{\bf\cdot}{\bf F}\,d^{3}x=\left\langle\eta,F\right\rangle,

(150)

with

{\bf F}=-\frac{\partial(\rho{\bf u})}{\partial t}-\nabla{\bf\cdot}(\rho{\bf u}{\bf u})+\rho(T\nabla S-\nabla h-\nabla\Phi)-\frac{1}{\mu_{0}}{\bf B}\times\left(\nabla\times\tilde{\bf B}\right).

(151)

The equation
{\bf F}=0
resulting from the variational principle (
140

It is equivalent to the Eulerian momentum equation (
2
),
after the pressure divergence identity (
61

A direct derivation of this identity is provided in Appendix B.
Alternatively, equation (

principle (
140

Appendix E derives the identity (

Holm and Kupershmidt 1986

\displaystyle\int\frac{1}{J}\left({\mathcal{F}}_{\bf x}{\bf\cdot}\Delta{\bf x}+{\mathcal{F}}_{\bf p}{\bf\cdot}\Delta{\bf p}\right)\ d^{3}{\bf x}

\displaystyle\int\biggl(\hat{{\mathcal{F}}}_{\rho}\delta\rho+\hat{{\mathcal{F}}}_{\bf M}{\bf\cdot}\delta{\bf M}+\hat{{\mathcal{F}}}_{\sigma}\delta\sigma+\hat{{\mathcal{F}}}_{\bf B}{\bf\cdot}\delta{\bf B}\biggr)\ d^{3}{\bf x},

\displaystyle{\mathcal{F}}_{\bf x}=

\displaystyle J\biggl[(\nabla\hat{{\mathcal{F}}}_{\bf M}){\bf\cdot}{\bf M}+\sigma\nabla\hat{{\mathcal{F}}}_{\sigma}+\rho\nabla\hat{{\mathcal{F}}}_{\rho}

\displaystyle+(\nabla\hat{{\mathcal{F}}}_{\bf B}){\bf\cdot}{\bf B}-({\bf B}{\bf\cdot}\nabla)\hat{{\mathcal{F}}}_{\bf B}-(\nabla{\bf\cdot}{\bf B})\hat{{\mathcal{F}}}_{\bf B}\biggr],

\displaystyle{\mathcal{F}}_{\bf p}=

for the transformation of variational derivatives from the old variables

In general, any Poisson bracket can be expressed in a co-symplectic form
which defines a corresponding Hamiltonian (co-symplectic) operator.
Using non-canonical Eulerian variables
{\sf Z}

\left\{{\mathcal{F}},{\mathcal{G}}\right\}=\int_{V}{\mathcal{F}}_{{\sf Z}^{\sf T}}{\bm{\mathcal{D}}}{\mathcal{G}}_{{\sf Z}}\,d^{3}x,

in which

is the Hamiltonian (matrix) operator,
where
\sf T
denotes the transpose.
Note that, in the case
{\sf Z}=({\bf q},{\bf p})

this operator reduces to the skew matrix

\bm{\mathcal{D}}=\begin{pmatrix}0&{\sf I}\\
-{\sf I}&0\end{pmatrix}
.
Antisymmetry of the bracket (

\{{\mathcal{F}},{\mathcal{G}}\}+\{{\mathcal{G}},{\mathcal{F}}\}=0
,
corresponds to
\bm{\mathcal{D}}
being skew-adjoint;
the Jacobi identity,

\{\{{\mathcal{F}},{\mathcal{G}}\},{\mathcal{H}}\}+\{\{{\mathcal{H}},{\mathcal{F}}\},{\mathcal{G}}\}+\{\{{\mathcal{G}},{\mathcal{H}}\},{\mathcal{F}}\}=0
,
corresponds to
\bm{\mathcal{D}}
having a vanishing Schouten bracket with itself
Olver 1993

Taking

{\sf Z}=\big(\rho,\sigma,M^{i},B^{i}\big),

and

{\bf M}

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{D}}=-\begin{pmatrix}0&0&\nabla^{j}\circ\rho&0\\

\rho\nabla^{i}&\sigma\nabla^{i}&M^{j}\nabla^{i}+\nabla^{j}\circ M^{i}&B^{j}\nabla^{i}-\delta^{ij}B^{k}\nabla_{k}\\

We can convert (

5.1
Non-canonical Hamiltonian equations

A non-canonical Poisson bracket (
161

{\mathcal{H}}
is chosen.
The formulation involves expressing the dynamical variables
{\sf Z}({\bf x},t)

formally as functionals

{\mathcal{Z}}({\bf x}^{\prime},t)\equiv\int_{V}{\sf Z}({\bf x}^{\prime},t)\delta({\bf x}-{\bf x}^{\prime})\,d^{3}x

(164)

and writing Hamilton’s equations in the form

\frac{\partial\mathcal{Z}}{\partial t}=\{{\mathcal{Z}},{\mathcal{H}}\}.

(165)

Using the explicit expression (
161
) for the bracket yields

\frac{\partial{\sf Z}}{\partial t}=\bm{\mathcal{D}}\frac{\delta{\mathcal{H}}}{\delta{\sf Z}}

(166)

in terms of the Hamiltonian operator
\bm{\mathcal{D}}
.

The appropriate Hamiltonian for describing CGL plasmas is given by the conserved total energy (cf Section
2.3
):

{\mathcal{H}}=\int_{V}H\,d^{3}x,

(167)

where
H
is the Eulerian total energy density (
110
).
Substituting this Hamiltonian into the non-canonical Hamilton’s equations (
166
)
can be shown to yield the CGL plasma equations (
1
)–(
6
) and (
8
)–(
9
).

5.2
Casimirs

In a non-canonical Hamiltonian system,
a functional
{\mathcal{C}}=\int_{V}C\,d^{3}x
satisfying the equation

\{{\mathcal{C}},{\mathcal{F}}\}\equiv 0\quad\text{ for all functionals ${\mathcal{F}}$ }

(168)

is called a Casimir.
Existence of a non-trivial Casimir
{\mathcal{C}}\not\equiv 0
indicates that the Poisson bracket
is degenerate.
Correspondingly, the Hamiltonian operator
\bm{\mathcal{D}}

in the co-symplectic form of the bracket (
161
)
will have a non-trivial kernel:

\bm{\mathcal{D}}{\mathcal{C}}_{\sf Z}\equiv 0

(169)

where
{\mathcal{C}}_{\sf Z}\equiv\frac{\delta{\mathcal{C}}}{\delta{\sf Z}}
.
Note that a Casimir is a conserved integral, since the time evolution of any functional is given by the Hamiltonian equations (
165
):

\frac{d}{dt}{\mathcal{C}}=\frac{\partial{\mathcal{C}}}{\partial t}=\{{\mathcal{C}},{\mathcal{H}}\}=0

(170)

Casimirs are useful in stability analysis of steady flows and plasma equilibria (e.g.
Holm et al. 1985
;
Hameiri 2004
).
The conservation (
170
) holds modulo boundary integrals,
and an investigation of boundary conditions is needed
for a Casimir to be a strictly conserved integral (i.e. a constant of motion).

All Casimirs can be determined by solving the equation (
168
),
or alternatively the equation (
169
) (see e.g.

Hameiri 2004
,

Padhye and Morrison 1996a
;
Padhye and Morrison 1996b

for the MHD case).
For CGL plasmas, we obtain the Casimir determining equations:

\displaystyle\nabla^{j}(\rho{\mathcal{C}}_{M^{j}})=\nabla{\bf\cdot}(\rho{\mathcal{C}}_{\bf M})=0,\quad\nabla^{j}(\sigma{\mathcal{C}}_{M^{j}})=\nabla{\bf\cdot}(\sigma{\mathcal{C}}_{\bf M})=0,

(171)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\rho\nabla^{i}{\mathcal{C}}_{\rho}+\sigma\nabla^{i}{\mathcal{C}}_{\sigma}+M^{j}\nabla^{i}{\mathcal{C}}_{M^{j}}+\nabla^{j}(M^{i}{\mathcal{C}}_{M^{j}})+B^{j}\nabla^{i}{\mathcal{C}}_{B^{j}}-B^{j}\nabla_{j}{\mathcal{C}}_{B^{i}}\\
&\equiv\rho\nabla{\mathcal{C}}_{\rho}+\sigma\nabla{\mathcal{C}}_{\sigma}+(\nabla{\mathcal{C}}_{\bf M}){\bf\cdot}{\bf M}+\nabla{\bf\cdot}({\mathcal{C}}_{\bf M}{\bf M})+(\nabla{\mathcal{C}}_{\bf B}){\bf\cdot}{\bf B}-{\bf B}{\bf\cdot}\nabla{\mathcal{C}}_{\bf B}=0,\end{aligned}

\displaystyle\nabla^{j}(B^{i}{\mathcal{C}}_{M^{j}})-\nabla_{j}(B^{j}{\mathcal{C}}_{M^{i}})=\nabla\times({\bf B}\times{\mathcal{C}}_{\bf M})=0,

where
\nabla
(
\nabla^{k}
) acts as the total derivative with respect to
{\bf x}
(
x^{k}
).
This is an overdetermined system of linear partial differential equations for

{\mathcal{C}}=\int_{V}C(t,{\bf x},{\sf Z},\nabla{\sf Z},\ldots,\nabla^{l}{\sf Z})\,d^{3}x,

(172)

with
{\sf Z}
denoting the dynamical variables (
162
).
The system can, in principle, be integrated to find
{\mathcal{C}}
explicitly,
once a differential order
l
for the dependence of
{\mathcal{C}}

on derivatives of the variables is chosen.
Note that solutions of the divergence form
C=\nabla{\bf\cdot}{\bf F}
lead to
{\mathcal{C}}

being a boundary integral which can be assumed to be trivial
if suitable boundary conditions are imposed.
This classification problem is beyond the scope of the present work.

5.3
Mass, cross helicity, magnetic helicity Casimirs

The well-known Casimirs for ideal barotropic MHD are the mass integral,
the cross helicity integral, and the magnetic helicity integral.
CGL plasmas with an isentropic equation of state
e=e(\rho,B)

possess these same Casimirs:

{\mathcal{C}}_{1}=\int_{V}\rho\,d^{3}x,\quad{\mathcal{C}}_{2}=\int_{V}{\bf u}{\bf\cdot}{\bf B}\,d^{3}x,\quad{\mathcal{C}}_{3}=\int_{V}{\bf A}{\bf\cdot}{\bf B}\,d^{3}x,

(173)

In the more physically realistic case with an equation of state
e=e(\rho,S,B)
,

{\mathcal{C}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{C}}_{3}
still hold as Casimirs,
but
{\mathcal{C}}_{2}
turns out to be a Casimir only in the case
{\bf B}{\bf\cdot}\nabla S=0
,
as we will show shortly.

Physically,
the mass integral
{\mathcal{C}}_{1}
is the total mass of the plasma;
the cross helicity integral
{\mathcal{C}}_{2}
describes the linking of the fluid vorticity and magnetic field flux tubes;
and the magnetic helicity integral
{\mathcal{C}}_{3}

describes the knotting, linking, and twist and writhe of the magnetic flux tubes
(see e.g.
Berger and Field 1984
;
Moffatt and Ricca 1992
;
Hameiri 2004
).
(Also see e.g.
Yoshida 2016
, for interesting applications).
In a fixed volume
V
with a boundary
\partial V
,

{\mathcal{C}}_{1}
is conserved,
\frac{\partial{\mathcal{C}}_{1}}{\partial t}=0
,
if
{\bf u}
has no normal component at the boundary
(cf Section
2.3
).
Conservation of
{\mathcal{C}}_{3}
,
\frac{\partial{\mathcal{C}}_{3}}{\partial t}=0
, holds
if in addition
{\bf B}
has no normal component at the boundary,
while the cross helicity integral
{\mathcal{C}}_{2}
is conserved,
\frac{\partial{\mathcal{C}}_{2}}{\partial t}=0
,
only with the further condition
{\bf B}{\bf\cdot}\nabla S=0

(cf Sections
2.4
,
2.5
).

5.4
Advected Casimirs

In general, Casimirs can be sought be looking among advected scalars,
\theta
,
since the corresponding scalar integral
\int_{V(t)}\rho\theta\,d^{3}x

will be conserved on volumes
V(t)
moving with the flow.
This implies that, on a fixed volume
V
,
the integral
\int_{V}\rho\theta\,d^{3}x
will be conserved up to a flux integral

-\oint_{\partial V}\rho\theta{\bf u}{\bf\cdot}\hat{\bf n}\,dA

that vanishes if
{\bf u}
has no normal component at the boundary
\partial V
,
and therefore
\int_{V}\rho\theta\,d^{3}x
will satisfy the Casimir property
\frac{\partial{\mathcal{C}}}{\partial t}=0
.
Note that this property is necessary but not sufficient for
\int_{V}\rho\theta\,d^{3}x
to be a Casimir,
since there are conserved integrals such as the total energy and angular momentum
that do not belong to the kernel of the Poisson bracket.

For CGL plasmas, the basic advected scalars are
S
,
\bar{S}_{\parallel}
,
\bar{S}_{\perp}
.
Additional advected scalars are provided by Ertel’s theorem:
if
\theta
is an advected scalar, then so is

\frac{{\bf B}{\bf\cdot}\nabla\theta}{\rho}
.
This yields the advected quantities (
89
) shown in Section
2.6
.

One can show that

{\mathcal{C}}_{4}=\int_{V}\rho f(S,\theta)\,d^{3}{\bf x}\quad\text{ where }\quad\theta=\frac{{\bf B}{\bf\cdot}\nabla S}{\rho},

(174)

is an advected Casimir,
for any function
f(S,\theta)
. We omit the proof.

6
Noether’s Theorem and Conservation Laws

Conservation laws of the CGL plasma equations (
1
) to (
10
)
can be derived from Noether’s theorem applied to the Lie point symmetries of
the Lagrangian variational principle (
109
).
A brief discussion of the MHD case was outlined in
Webb and Anco 2019
.
The CGL plasma conservation laws differ in comparison with the MHD conservation laws
mainly in the form of pressure tensor:
in particular, the MHD isotropic gas pressure tensor
{\sf p}=p\,{\sf I}
is replaced by
the non-isotropic CGL plasma pressure tensor

{\sf p}=p_{\perp}{\sf I}+(p_{\parallel}-p_{\perp})\bm{\tau}\bm{\tau}
.

Appendix H gives a description of Noether’s first theorem,
for a differential equation system described by an action principle
as developed by
Bluman and Kumei 1989
. The analysis uses
canonical
Lie symmetry operators
\mathrm{X}
in which both the dependent and independent
variables change in the Lie transformation, and also the evolutionary form
of the symmetry operator denoted by
\hat{\mathrm{X}}
in which the independent
variables do not change, but the dependent variables, and the derivatives
of the dependent variables change. The prolongation
operators
\text{pr}\mathrm{X}
and
\text{pr}\hat{\mathrm{X}}
are described, and used to
derive Noether’s first theorem similar to
Bluman and Kumei 1989
.
More recent derivations of Noether’s theorem using
\text{pr}\hat{\mathrm{X}}
,
are given by
Bluman and Anco 2002
and
Olver 1993
.

In Section 6.1
we use a recent form of Noether’s theorem to derive conservation laws
using the evolutionary form of the prolonged symmetry operator

\text{pr}\hat{\mathrm{X}}
. In Section 6.2 we obtain the same results
from the classical form of Noether’s theorem given in
Appendix H. Although the recent form of Noether’s theorem,
is conceptually more appealing, it is not
any simpler than the classical form of Noether’s theorem.
The classical form of Noether’s theorem is perhaps easier
to understand, as it relates directly back to the invariance
of the action integral under Lie and divergence transformations.

6.1
Noether’s Theorem and Evolutionary Symmetries

To use Noether’s theorem,
we need to obtain the Lie point symmetries of the Lagrangian variational principle (
109
).
Since this variational principle employs the variables
x^{i}=X^{i}(x_{0}^{j},t)

given by the Cartesian components of the Lagrangian map (
92
),
a Lie point symmetry acting on the coordinate space
(t,x_{0}^{j},x^{i})

has the form

t\to t+\epsilon\xi^{t}+O(\epsilon^{2}),\quad x^{i}\to x^{i}+\epsilon\xi^{i}+O(\epsilon^{2}),\quad x_{0}^{i}\to x_{0}^{i}+\epsilon\xi_{0}^{i}+O(\epsilon^{2})

(175)

with
\epsilon
denoting the parameter in the point symmetry transformation,
where
\xi^{i}
,
\xi_{0}^{i}
,
\xi^{t}
are functions of
t,x_{0}^{j},x^{i}
.
The infinitesimal transformation corresponds to the generator

\mathrm{X}=\xi^{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{i}}+\xi_{0}^{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{0}^{i}}+\xi^{t}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}

(176)

while the finite transformation (
175
) is given by exponentiation of the generator,

(t,x^{i},x_{0}^{i})\to\exp(\epsilon\mathrm{X})(t,x^{i},x_{0}^{i})
.

To be a symmetry, a generator (
176
) must leave
the variational principle invariant modulo boundary terms.
This is equivalent to the condition that the
change in the Lagrangian must satisfy

;
Ibragimov 1985

\text{pr}\mathrm{X}L_{0}=\xi^{t}D_{t}L_{0}+\xi_{0}^{i}D_{x^{i}_{0}}L_{0}+D_{t}\Lambda_{0}^{t}+D_{x_{0}^{i}}\Lambda_{0}^{i}

(177)

where the operators

D_{t}=\partial/\partial_{t}+\dot{x}^{i}\partial/\partial_{x^{i}}+\cdots

and
D_{x_{0}^{i}}=\partial/\partial_{x_{0}^{i}}+x^{ji}\partial/\partial_{x^{j}}+\cdots

denote total derivative operators with respect to the independent variables

t
and
x_{0}^{i}
and
D_{x^{i}}
denotes the partial derivative with respect
to
x^{i}
keeping
{\bf x}_{0}
and
t
constant. Here
\Lambda^{t}_{0}
and
\Lambda^{i}_{0}

are arbitrary potentials, that arise in Noether’s theorem, because
the variational derivative of a perfect derivative term has
zero variational derivative.
The first two terms on the righthand side of (
177

represent the Lie derivative
{\cal L}_{\bf X}L_{0}

using the chain rule for differentiation.
The terms involving total derivatives of
L_{0}
on the
righthand side of (
177
)
can be understood to arise from the change in the spatial domain
V_{0}
and the time interval
[t_{0},t_{1}]
in the variational principle (
109
)
under the action of an infinitesimal point transformation (
175
).
On the lefthand side of (
177
),

pr
denotes prolongation to the extended coordinate space
(t,x_{0}^{j},x^{i},\dot{x}^{i},x^{ij})
(i.e. jet space)
in which
\dot{x}^{i}
and
x^{ij}
are coordinates that correspond to

\dot{X}^{i}(x_{0}^{j},t)
and
\partial X^{i}(x_{0}^{j},t)/\partial x_{0}^{j}

on solutions
x^{i}=X^{i}(x_{0}^{j},t)
of the Euler-Lagrange equations (
114
) of the variational principle.
An explicit formula for the components of
\text{pr}\mathrm{X}
will not be needed
if we express the invariance condition (
177
)
by using the characteristic form of the generator
in which only the dependent variables
x^{i}
undergo a transformation:

\hat{\mathrm{X}}=\hat{\xi}^{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{i}},\quad\hat{\xi}^{i}=\xi^{i}-\xi^{t}\dot{x}^{i}-\xi_{0}^{j}x^{ij}

(178)

which arises from how
\mathrm{X}
acts on solutions
x^{i}=X^{i}(x_{0}^{j},t)

The relationship between the two forms (
178
) and (
176
) is that

\text{pr}\mathrm{X}=\text{pr}\hat{\mathrm{X}}+\xi^{t}D_{t}+\xi_{0}^{i}D_{x_{0}^{i}}
.
Hence, (
177

;
Bluman and Anco 2002
)

\text{pr}\hat{\mathrm{X}}(\hat{L}_{0})=D_{t}\Lambda_{0}^{t}+D_{x_{0}^{i}}\Lambda_{0}^{i}

(179)

where

\hat{L}_{0}=L_{0}\big|_{{\bf X}={\bf x}}

(180)

is a function of
t,x_{0}^{j},x^{i},\dot{x}^{i},x^{ij}
.
The prolongation
\text{pr}\hat{\mathrm{X}}
is given by simply extending
\hat{\mathrm{X}}
to act on
\dot{x}^{i}
and
x^{ij}
through the total derivative relations

\text{pr}\hat{\mathrm{X}}(\dot{x}^{i})=D_{t}(\hat{\mathrm{X}}x^{i})=D_{t}\xi^{t}

and

\text{pr}\hat{\mathrm{X}}(x^{ij})=D_{x_{0}^{j}}(\hat{\mathrm{X}}x^{i})=D_{x_{0}^{j}}\xi^{i}

In turn, this form (
179
) of the invariance condition can be expressed succinctly as

E_{x^{i}}(\text{pr}\hat{\mathrm{X}}(\hat{L}_{0}))=0

(181)

using the Euler-Lagrange operator
E_{x^{i}}
which has the property that
it annihilates a function iff the function is given by a total divergence with respect to
t
and
x_{0}^{j}
.
The formulation (

all Lie point symmetries of the variational principle (
109

(See
Olver 1993
and
Bluman and Anco 2002
for a general discussion.)

Each Lie point symmetry (

through combining the condition (

\text{pr}\hat{\mathrm{X}}(\hat{L}_{0})=\hat{\xi}^{i}E_{x^{i}}(L_{0})+D_{t}W^{t}+D_{x_{0}^{i}}W^{i}

(182)

where

W^{t}=\hat{\xi}^{j}\frac{\partial\hat{L}_{0}}{\partial\dot{x}^{j}},\quad W^{i}=\hat{\xi}^{j}\frac{\partial\hat{L}_{0}}{\partial x^{ji}}.

(183)

Thus, we obtain the following statement of Noether’s theorem.

Proposition 6.1

If the Lagrangian variational principle (
109
) is invariant up to boundary terms
under an infinitesimal point transformation (
175
),
then the Euler-Lagrange equations (
114
) of the variational principle
possess a conservation law

D_{t}\mathcal{I}^{t}_{0}+D_{x_{0}^{i}}{\mathcal{I}}^{i}_{0}=0

(184)

in which the conserved density and the spatial flux are given by

\mathcal{I}^{t}_{0}=\left(\Lambda_{0}^{t}-W^{t}\right)\big|_{x={\bf X}},\quad\mathcal{I}^{i}_{0}=\left(\Lambda_{0}^{i}-W^{i}\right)\big|_{x={\bf X}},

(185)

using (
179
) and (
183
).

Explicit expressions for

\partial\hat{L}_{0}/\partial\dot{x}^{j}=\left(\partial L_{0}/\partial\dot{X}^{j}\right)\big|_{{\bf X}={\bf x}}

and

\partial\hat{L}_{0}/\partial x^{ji}=\left(\partial L_{0}/\partial X^{ji}\right)\big|_{{\bf X}={\bf x}}

are provided by (
1
) in appendix C.
Note that the conservation law is a local continuity equation
which holds when
x^{i}=X^{i}(x_{0}^{j},t)
satisfies the Euler-Lagrange equations (
114
).
On solutions,
D_{t}|_{x={\bf X}}=\partial/\partial t
and
D_{x_{0}^{i}}|_{x={\bf X}}=\partial/\partial x_{0}^{i}

(acting as total derivatives).

We remark that (
185

176

See
Webb and Zank 2007

Webb and Anco 2019

to conservation laws for the CGL system is also described in Appendix H.

6.1.1
Eulerian form of a Lagrangian conservation law

A Lagrangian conservation law (
185
) can be expressed equivalently
as an Eulerian conservation law (
Padhye 1998
)

\partial_{t}\Psi^{t}+\nabla_{i}\Psi^{i}=0

(186)

whose conserved density and spatial flux are given by

\Psi^{t}=\frac{1}{J}\mathcal{I}_{0}^{t}\big|_{x={\bf X}},\quad\Psi^{i}=\frac{1}{J}\left(u^{i}\mathcal{I}_{0}^{t}+X_{ik}\mathcal{I}_{0}^{k}\right)\big|_{x={\bf X}}

(187)

on solutions
x^{i}=X^{i}(x_{0}^{j},t)
of the Euler-Lagrange equations (
114
).
This form (
186
)–(
187
) of a conservation law is appropriate for considering
conserved integrals

\frac{d}{dt}\int_{V}\Psi^{t}\,d^{3}x=-\oint_{\partial V}{\bm{\Psi}}\cdot\hat{\bf n}\,dA

(188)

on a fixed spatial domain
V
in the CGL plasma.

In general,
an Eulerian conservation law is a continuity equation of the form (
186
)
holding on the solution space of the CGL plasma equations (
1
) to (
10
).
The corresponding conserved integral (
188
)
has the physical content that the rate of change of the integral quantity

\int_{V}\Phi^{t}\,d^{3}x
in
V
is balanced by the net flux leaving the boundary of
V
.
It is often physically useful to consider instead a spatial domain
V(t)
that moves with the plasma.
The form of the conservation law for moving domains is given by

\frac{d}{dt}\int_{V(t)}\Psi^{t}\,d^{3}x=-\oint_{\partial V(t)}{\bm{\Gamma}}\cdot\hat{\bf n}\,dA

(189)

in terms of the moving flux

{\bm{\Gamma}}={\bm{\Psi}}-{\bf u}\Psi^{t}=\frac{1}{J}{\bf X}\cdot{\bm{\mathcal{I}}}_{0}.

(190)

Note that the moving integral quantity
\int_{V(t)}\Psi^{t}\,d^{3}x
will be an invariant (i.e. a constant of motion) when the net moving flux vanishes on the boundary
\partial V(t)
.
(See
Anco and Webb 2020
for a discussion of moving domain conservation laws and invariants
in fluid mechanics.)
The equivalence between the conservation laws (
188
) and (
189
)
can be derived by writing the continuity equation (
186
)
in terms of the material (co-moving) derivative (
88
):

d\Phi^{t}/dt=-(\nabla{\bf\cdot}{\bf u})\Phi^{t}-\nabla{\bf\cdot}\left(\bm{\Phi}-\Phi^{t}{\bf u}\right)
,
where
(\nabla{\bf\cdot}{\bf u})
represents the expansion or contraction of
an infinitesimal volume
d^{3}x
moving with the fluid
(see e.g.
Anco and Dar 2009
;
Anco and Dar 2010
).

Now, rather than seeking to find all Lie point symmetries,
we will consider two main classes: kinematic and fluid relabelling.
Kinematic symmetries are characterized by the generator (
175
) having
\xi_{0}^{i}=0
,
with
\xi^{t}
and
\xi^{i}
being functions only of
t,x^{i}
.
Fluid relabelling symmetries have
\xi^{i}=\xi^{t}=0
in the generator (
175
),
with
\xi_{0}^{i}
being a function only of
t,x_{0}^{i}
.

A useful general remark is that any Lie point symmetry of a variational principle
corresponds to a Lie point symmetry of the Euler-Lagrange equations,
because invariance of a variational principle means that its extremals are preserved.
Thus, one way to find all Lie point symmetries of a variational principle is by
firstly obtaining the Lie point symmetries of the Euler-Lagrange equations,
and secondly checking which of those symmetries leaves invariant the variational principle.

Before deriving conservation laws using the above
analysis, it is useful to note that there are three basic steps
in the analysis.

(i) First it is necessary to determine
for a given Lie symmetry, whether the Lie invariance condition (
179
)
for the action can be satisfied by choosing the potentials

\Lambda^{t}_{0}
and
\Lambda^{i}_{0}
. Here the left handside of (
179
)
for the action
{\rm pr}\hat{X}(L_{0})
is evaluated for the symmetry
operator
X
.

(ii) Determine the surface vector components

W^{t}
and
W^{i}
that occur in the Noether identity (
182
),
where
W^{t}
and
W^{i}

are given by (
183
)
(recall
\dot{x}^{j}=\partial x^{j}({\bf x}_{0},t)/\partial t=u^{j}

and
x^{ji}=\partial x^{j}/\partial x_{0}^{i}
). Then using
Proposition
6.1
one can obtain the Lagrangian conservation
law (
184
)
with conserved density
\mathcal{I}_{0}^{t}
and flux
\mathcal{I}_{0}^{i}

given in (
185
).

(iii) Determine the Eulerian form of the conservation law
using the results of Padhye (1998) described by (
186
)
and (
187
).

6.1.2
Lie Invariance Condition

The Lie invariance condition for the action in (

\displaystyle J\biggl\{\nabla{\bf\cdot}(\rho\hat{\bm{\xi}})\left[\Phi+h-\frac{1}{2}u^{2}\right]+\rho{\bf u}{\bf\cdot}\left(\frac{d\hat{\bm{\xi}}}{dt}-\hat{\bm{\xi}}{\bf\cdot}\nabla{\bf u}\right)+\rho T\hat{\bm{\xi}}{\bf\cdot}\nabla S

6.1.3
Galilean symmetries

The Eulerian form of the CGL plasma equations (
1
) to (
10
)
clearly indicates that they possess the Galilean group of Lie point symmetries when

which are generated by (

P_{0}=\frac{\partial}{\partial t},\quad P_{i}=\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{i}},\quad K_{i}=t\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{i}}+\frac{\partial}{\partial u^{i}},\quad J_{i}=\epsilon_{ijk}\left(x^{j}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{k}}+u^{j}\frac{\partial}{\partial u^{k}}+B^{j}\frac{\partial}{\partial B^{k}}\right).

These generators respectively describe
a time translation (
P_{0}
),

,

),

,

and rotations (

,

As shown in appendix F,
the only additional Lie point symmetries admitted by equations (

) to (
10
)
consist of scalings.
Hence, Galilean symmetries and scaling symmetries comprise all kinematic Lie point symmetries of the CGL plasma equations.

The Galilean symmetries (

\mathrm{X}_{P_{0}}=\frac{\partial}{\partial t},\quad\mathrm{X}_{P_{i}}=\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{i}},\quad\mathrm{X}_{K_{i}}=t\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{i}},\quad\mathrm{X}_{J_{i}}=\epsilon_{ijk}x^{j}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{k}}.

(193)

Note that the prolongation of these Lagrangian generators to
\dot{x}^{i}=\dot{X}^{i}=u^{i}

through the Lagrangian relation (

) for the fluid velocity
yields the corresponding Eulerian generators (
192
) acting on the variables
(t,x^{i},u^{i})
.
Since
\xi_{0}^{i}=0
for all of the generators (
193

they are of kinematic type.

In the case when the gravitational potential
\Phi
is non-zero,
then the preceding generators must satisfy the condition

to be admitted as symmetries.
For example,
if
\Phi
is invariant under

then
\mathrm{X}_{P_{3}}

\mathrm{X}_{K_{3}}

if
\Phi
is invariant under

6.1.4
Galilean conservation laws

It is straightforward to show that
each Galilean generator (
193
) satisfies the condition (
181
)
for invariance of the Lagrangian variational principle (
109
) when
\Phi=0

and thereby yields a conservation law (
184
).

Specifically,
time translation (
X_{P_{0}}
) has
\hat{\xi}^{i}=-\dot{x}^{i}
,
and thus

\text{pr}\hat{\mathrm{X}}_{P_{0}}L_{0}=-\ddot{x}^{i}\partial L_{0}/\partial_{\dot{x}^{i}}-\dot{x}^{ij}\partial L_{0}/\partial_{x^{ij}}=-D_{t}L_{0}

due to
\partial L_{0}/\partial_{t}=0
.
This gives
\Lambda_{0}^{t}=-L_{0}
,
\Lambda_{0}^{i}=0
,
which yields conservation of energy

\mathcal{I}^{t}_{0}=\dot{X}^{j}\frac{\partial L_{0}}{\partial\dot{X}^{j}}-L_{0}=H_{0},\quad\mathcal{I}^{i}_{0}=\dot{X}^{j}\frac{\partial L_{0}}{\partial X^{ji}},

(194)

with
H_{0}
being the Hamiltonian (
117
).

Space translations (
X_{P_{j}}
) have
\hat{\xi}^{i}=\delta^{i}{}_{j}
,
and thus

\text{pr}\hat{\mathrm{X}}_{P_{j}}L_{0}=0
due to
\partial L_{0}/\partial_{x^{i}}=0

(where we neglect gravity)
combined with
D_{t}\hat{\xi}^{i}=0
and
D_{x_{0}^{j}}\hat{\xi}^{i}=0
.
Hence,
\Lambda_{0}^{t}=0
,
\Lambda_{0}^{i}=0
,
which yields conservation of momentum

\mathcal{I}^{t}_{0}=-\frac{\partial L_{0}}{\partial\dot{X}^{j}},\quad\mathcal{I}^{i}_{0}=-\frac{\partial L_{0}}{\partial X^{ji}}.

(195)

Galilean boosts (
X_{K_{j}}
) have
\hat{\xi}^{i}=t\delta^{i}{}_{j}
,
and thus

\text{pr}\hat{\mathrm{X}}_{K_{j}}L_{0}=\rho_{0}\dot{x}^{j}=D_{t}(\rho_{0}x^{j})
.
This gives
\Lambda_{0}^{t}=\rho_{0}x^{j}
,
\Lambda_{0}^{i}=0
,
yielding conservation of Galilean momentum (center of mass)

\mathcal{I}^{t}_{0}=\int\frac{\partial L_{0}}{\partial\dot{X}^{j}}dt-t\frac{\partial L_{0}}{\partial\dot{X}^{j}},\quad\mathcal{I}^{i}_{0}=-t\frac{\partial L_{0}}{\partial X^{ji}}.

(196)

Rotations (
X_{J_{j}}
) have
\hat{\xi}^{i}=\epsilon^{i}{}_{kj}x^{k}
.
This leads to
\text{pr}\hat{\mathrm{X}}_{J_{j}}L_{0}=0
, since
L_{0}
depends on
x^{i}

only through the scalars
|\dot{x}^{i}|
and
|X_{ij}\tau_{0}^{j}|
which are rotationally invariant.
Thus
\Lambda_{0}^{t}=0
,
\Lambda_{0}^{i}=0
,
which yields conservation of axial angular momentum

\mathcal{I}^{t}_{0}=\epsilon_{jkl}x^{k}\frac{\partial L_{0}}{\partial\dot{X}^{l}},\quad\mathcal{I}^{i}_{0}=\epsilon_{jkl}x^{k}\frac{\partial L_{0}}{\partial X^{li}}.

(197)

The set of Galilean conservation laws (
194
) to (
197
)
can be written in a unified form through use of the observation that

\Lambda_{0}^{t}=\dot{\xi}^{j}\int\frac{\partial L_{0}}{\partial\dot{X}^{j}}dt-\xi^{t}L_{0},\quad\Lambda_{0}^{i}=0,

(198)

with

\xi^{t}=a_{0},\quad\xi^{i}=a_{1}^{i}+a_{2}^{i}t+a_{3}^{k}\epsilon_{ijk}x^{j},

(199)

where
a_{0}
,
a_{1}^{i}
,
a_{2}^{i}
,

(

(parameterizing the respective Galilean symmetry generators (

Hence, we have

\mathcal{I}_{0}^{t}=\dot{\xi}^{j}\int\frac{\partial L_{0}}{\partial\dot{X}^{j}}dt-\xi^{t}L_{0}-(\xi^{j}-\xi^{t}\dot{X}^{j})\frac{\partial L_{0}}{\partial\dot{X}^{j}},\quad\mathcal{I}_{0}^{i}=-(\xi^{j}-\xi^{t}\dot{X}^{j})\frac{\partial L_{0}}{\partial X^{ji}}.

(200)

The resulting set of Eulerian conservation laws (
186
)–(
187
)
takes the form

\displaystyle\Psi^{t}

\displaystyle=H\xi^{t}-\rho u^{j}\xi^{j}+\rho x^{j}\dot{\xi}^{j},

(201)

\displaystyle\Psi^{i}

\displaystyle=u^{i}\Psi^{t}-(\xi^{j}-\xi^{t}u^{j})\left(p^{ij}+M_{B}^{ij}\right),

(202)

where we have used

\dfrac{\partial L_{0}}{\partial\dot{X}^{j}}=\rho_{0}u^{j},\quad\int\dfrac{\partial L_{0}}{\partial\dot{X}^{j}}dt=\rho_{0}x^{j},\quad\dfrac{\partial L_{0}}{\partial X^{jk}}=\left(p^{jl}+M_{B}^{jl}\right)A_{lk},

(203)

which follow from expressions (

along with the formulae (

Here

and

respectively represents the components of
the CGL non-isotropic pressure tensor (

and the magnetic pressure tensor (

Note that

is the Hamiltonian (

In the MHD case,

is replaced by the components of the isotropic MHD gas pressure tensor

To summarize the preceding derivations:

(i)
Time translation symmetry (

) yields energy conservation, for which:

\displaystyle\Psi^{t}=H=\rho\left(\frac{1}{2}|{\bf u}|^{2}+\Phi({\bf x})\right)+\varepsilon+\frac{B^{2}}{2\mu_{0}},\quad{\bm{\Psi}}=\left(\frac{1}{2}\rho|{\bf u}|^{2}+\varepsilon+\rho\Phi\right){\bf u}+{\sf p}{\bf\cdot u}+\frac{1}{\mu_{0}}{\bf E}\times{\bf B},

(204)

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\bm{\Gamma}}={\bm{\Psi}}-{\bf u}H=\left({\sf p}+{\sf M}_{B}\right)\cdot{\bf u},

where

is the electric field strength,
and

(ii)
Space translation symmetry (

(iii)

\displaystyle\Psi^{t}=\rho{\bf x}-t{\bf M}=\rho({\bf x}-t{\bf u}),\quad{\bm{\Psi}}=\rho{\bf u}{\bf x}-t{\sf T},

(207)

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\bm{\Gamma}}={\bm{\Psi}}-{\bf u}(\rho{\bf x}-t{\bf M})=-t\left({\sf p}+{\sf M}_{B}\right).

(iv)

\displaystyle\Psi^{t}={\bf x}\times{\bf M}=\rho{\bf x}\times{\bf u},\quad{\bm{\Psi}}={\bf x}\times{\sf T},

(208)

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\bm{\Gamma}}={\bm{\Psi}}-{\bf u}({\bf x}\times{\bf M})={\bf x}\times\left({\sf p}+{\sf M}_{B}\right),

6.1.5
Fluid Relabelling Symmetries

Fluid relabelling symmetries correspond to transformations (

(209)

For evaluating the condition for invariance of

\hat{\mathrm{X}}=\hat{\xi}_{0}^{i}\partial_{x^{i}},\quad\hat{\xi}_{0}^{i}=-\xi_{0}^{j}x^{ij}.

This generality is necessary to derive the cross helicity conservation law (

6.1.6
Noether’s theorem for fluid-relabelling conservation laws

The Lie invariance condition (
191

symmetry cases, may be written
in the form:

\displaystyle J\biggl\{\nabla{\bf\cdot}(\rho\hat{\bm{\xi}})\left[\Phi+h-\frac{1}{2}u^{2}\right]+\rho{\bf u}{\bf\cdot}\left(\frac{d\hat{\bm{\xi}}}{dt}-\hat{\bm{\xi}}{\bf\cdot}\nabla{\bf u}\right)

\displaystyle-\frac{\tilde{\bf B}}{\mu_{0}}{\bf\cdot}\left[\nabla\times(\hat{\bm{\xi}}\times{\bf B})-\hat{\bm{\xi}}\nabla{\bf\cdot}{\bf B}\right]\biggr\}

\displaystyle=D_{t}\left(\Lambda^{t}_{0}-\rho_{0}r{\bm{\xi}}_{0}{\bf\cdot}\nabla_{0}S\right)+D_{x_{0}^{i}}\left(\Lambda^{i}_{0}-A_{ki}G^{k}\right),

(212)

For cross helicity conservation,

6.2
Classical Noether Approach

In this subsection we use a classical version of Noether’s theorem
(see e.g.
Bluman and Kumei 1989
and Appendix H)
to derive conservation laws for the CGL plasma action (
109
)
that uses the canonical Lie symmetry operator
{\tilde{X}}={\rm pr}X

rather than the evolutionary form
{\rm pr}{\hat{X}}
.
The canonical symmetry operator form of Noether’s theorem was
used by
Webb and Zank 2007
and
Webb and Anco 2019
for the MHD fluid case.
In this approach one searches for Lie transformations and divergence
transformations that leave the
action (
175
) invariant, where

L_{0}^{\prime}=L_{0}+\epsilon D_{\alpha}{\bar{\Lambda}}^{\alpha}_{0}+O(\epsilon^{2}),

(221)

is the divergence transformation.
Here
D_{0}=\partial/\partial t
and
D_{i}=\partial/\partial x_{0}^{i}
are total
partial derivatives with respect to
t
and
x_{0}^{i}
.
Note we use
{\bar{\Lambda}}^{\alpha}_{0}
and
{\bar{\Lambda}}^{\alpha}

to denote the potentials,
in order to distinguish them from the
potentials used for the evolutionary potentials in Section 6.1.

The condition for the action to remain invariant under
(
175
) and (
221
) may be written in the form
(cf.
Bluman and Kumei 1989
):

{\rm pr}XL_{0}+L_{0}\left[D_{t}\xi^{t}+D_{x_{0}^{j}}\left(\xi^{j}_{0}\right)\right]+D_{t}{\bar{\Lambda}}^{0}_{0}+D_{x_{0}^{j}}{\bar{\Lambda}}^{j}_{0}=0,

(222)

where

{\rm pr}XL_{0}=\xi^{t}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}+\xi^{s}_{0}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{0}^{s}}+\xi^{k}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{k}}+\xi^{x^{k}_{t}}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{k}_{t}}+\xi^{x_{kj}}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{kj}}+\ldots,

(223)

is the prolonged, canonical Lie symmetry operator.

{\rm pr}X
is related to
{\rm pr}{\hat{X}}
by the
equations (
Ovsjannikov 1978
,
Ibragimov 1985
and
Bluman and Kumei 1989
):

\displaystyle{\rm pr}X=

\displaystyle{\rm pr}{\hat{X}}+\xi^{\alpha}_{0}D_{\alpha},

\displaystyle{\rm pr}{\hat{X}}=

\displaystyle{\hat{\xi}}^{k}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{k}}+D_{\alpha}\left({\hat{\xi}}^{k}\right)\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{k}_{\alpha}}+D_{\alpha}D_{\beta}\left({\hat{\xi}}^{k}\right)\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{k}_{\alpha\beta}}+\ldots,

(224)

where the evolutionary symmetry generator
{\hat{\xi}}^{i}
is
given by (
178
) and
\xi^{t}\equiv\xi^{0}_{0}
.

Noether’s theorem follows from Noether’s identity:

{\rm pr}XL_{0}+L_{0}D_{\alpha}\xi^{\alpha}_{0}+D_{\alpha}{\bar{\Lambda}}^{\alpha}_{0}=\hat{\xi}^{i}E_{x^{i}}\left(L_{0}\right)+D_{\alpha}\left(W^{\alpha}+L_{0}\xi^{\alpha}_{0}\right)+D_{\alpha}\left({\bar{\Lambda}}^{\alpha}_{0}\right),

(225)

where
E_{x^{i}}(L_{0})\equiv\delta{\mathcal{J}}/\delta x^{i}
is
the variational derivative
of the action
{\mathcal{J}}
with respect to
x^{i}
.
For the case of CGL plasmas, the surface terms
W^{\alpha}
are given by:

W^{t}\equiv W^{0}=\hat{\xi}^{j}\frac{\partial L_{0}}{\partial x^{j}_{t}},\quad W^{i}=\hat{\xi}^{j}\frac{\partial L_{0}}{\partial x^{ji}},

(226)

(see
Bluman and Kumei 1989
,
Ibragimov 1985
for more general cases).

If the Lie invariance condition (
222
) is satisfied, then
the left handside of (
225
) vanishes,
and consequently the right handside
of (
225
) must vanish. If in addition, the Euler Lagrange equations

E_{x^{i}}(L_{0})=0
are satisfied, then (
225

D_{\alpha}\left(W^{\alpha}+L_{0}\xi^{\alpha}_{0}+{\bar{\Lambda}}^{\alpha}_{0}\right)=0,

(227)

which is the conservation law of Noether’s first theorem, which applies
if the Euler -Lagrange equations
E_{x^{i}}(L_{0})=0
are independent,
which is the case for a finite Lie algebra of Lie point symmetries.

In the more general case where the symmetries depend on continuous
functions
\left\{\phi^{k}({\bf x}_{0},t):1\leq k\leq N\right\}
,
then the Lie-pseudo algebra of symmetries is infinite dimensional.
In this case,
Noether’s second theorem implies that the Euler-Lagrange equations
are not all independent, and that there exists differential relations
between the Euler-Lagrange equations (see e.g.
Olver 1993

and
Hydon and Mansfield 2011
for details). Noether’s second theorem, in some cases
results in mathematically trivial conservation laws.
Charron and Zadra 2018

discuss Ertel’s theorem and Noether’s second theorem.

For the CGL plasma model, the
W^{\alpha}
from (
226

W^{t}={\hat{\xi}}^{j}\rho_{0}u^{j},\quad W^{i}=\hat{\xi}^{j}\left(p^{js}+M_{B}^{js}\right)A_{si}.

(228)

Substitution of (
228

W^{\alpha}
into Noether’s theorem
(

\frac{\partial{\cal I}^{0}}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial{\cal I}^{i}}{\partial x_{0}^{i}}=0,

(229)

where

\displaystyle{\cal I}^{0}=

\displaystyle\rho_{0}u^{j}\hat{\xi}^{j}+\xi^{t}L_{0}+{\bar{\Lambda}}^{t}_{0},

\displaystyle{\cal I}^{i}=

\displaystyle\hat{\xi}^{j}\left(p^{js}+M_{B}^{js}\right)A_{si}+\xi^{i}_{0}L_{0}+{\bar{\Lambda}}^{i}_{0},

(230)

The Lagrangian conservation law

\displaystyle\Psi^{t}=

\displaystyle\rho u^{k}\hat{\xi}^{k}+\xi^{t}L+{\bar{\Lambda}}^{0},

\displaystyle\hat{\xi}^{k}\left(T^{jk}-L\delta^{jk}\right)+\xi^{j}L+{\bar{\Lambda}}^{j},

(231)

\displaystyle\rho u^{j}u^{k}+p^{jk}+M_{B}^{jk},

\displaystyle{\bar{\Lambda}}^{0}_{0}/J,\quad{\bar{\Lambda}}^{j}=\left(u^{j}{\bar{\Lambda}}^{0}_{0}+x^{js}{\bar{\Lambda}}^{s}_{0}\right)/J.

(232)

The Lagrangian and Eulerian conservation laws for the Galilean group

7
Summary and Concluding Remarks

In this paper an investigation was carried out of the ideal CGL plasma
equations, based in part on the Lagrangian variational formulation of

Newcomb 1962
, in which there is an anisotropic pressure tensor, with
pressure components
p_{\parallel}
and
p_{\perp}
parallel and perpendicular
to the magnetic field
{\bf B}
, which satisfy the so-called double adiabatic
equations which in turn can be described by using entropy components:

S_{\parallel}
and
S_{\perp}
parallel and perpendicular to
{\bf B}

(e.g.
Du et al. 2020
).

The total energy conservation law, and the cross helicity
and magnetic helicity conservation laws were obtained
(Section 2). The total energy equation was decomposed into the sum of
three energy equations, namely the internal energy equation or co-moving
energy equation, the total kinetic energy equation and Poynting’s theorem
(the electro-magnetic energy equation).
The cross helicity transport equation involves the effective enthalpy

h=(p_{\parallel}+\varepsilon)/\rho
of the gas associated with
pressure work terms parallel to
{\bf B}
. If
the internal energy density of the plasma per unit mass has
the form
e=e(\rho,S,B)\equiv\varepsilon/\rho

(
Hazeltine et al. 2013
;
Holm and Kupershmidt 1986
) one obtains a cross
helicity transport equation of the form:

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}({\bf u\cdot B})+\left[({\bf u\cdot B}){\bf u}+{\bf B}\left(\Phi+h-\frac{1}{2}u^{2}\right)\right]=T{\bf B}{\bf\cdot}\nabla S,

(248)

where
T=e_{S}
is the temperature of the gas.
If
{\bf B}{\bf\cdot}\nabla S=0
, then (
248
) is a local conservation
law. More generally, if the source term
Q=T{\bf B}{\bf\cdot}\nabla S\neq 0
,
(
248
) can be reduced to a nonlocal conservation law of the form:

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}({\bf w\cdot B})+\left[({\bf w\cdot B}){\bf u}+{\bf B}\left(\Phi+h-\frac{1}{2}u^{2}\right)\right]=0,

(249)

where

{\bf w}={\bf u}+r\nabla S\quad\hbox{and}\quad\frac{dr}{dt}=-T,

(250)

Here
d/dt=\partial/\partial t+{\bf u}{\bf\cdot}\nabla
is the advective
time derivative following the flow.
The CGL plasma conservation law (
249
)
generalizes the non-local, MHD cross helicity
conservation law of
Webb et al. 2014a
;
Webb et al. 2014b
and
Yahalom 2017a
;
Yahalom 2017b
).
Here
\Phi({\bf x})
is the potential for an external
gravitational field.
Similar transport equations for cross helicity to (
248
)
and (
250
) were also developed in terms of the parallel
and perpendicular temperatures
T_{\parallel}
and
T_{\perp}

defined as
T_{\parallel}=p_{\parallel}/(\rho R)
and
T_{\perp}=p_{\perp}/(\rho R)
.

There are two different, but equivalent forms of the momentum (or force) equation
for the CGL system.
The form of the momentum equation obtained
by
Newcomb 1962
Lagrangian action principle reduces to the form:

\frac{d{\bf u}}{dt}=-\frac{1}{\rho}\nabla{\bf\cdot}{\sf p}+\frac{{\bf J}\times{\bf B}}{\rho}-\nabla\Phi,

(251)

where
{\bf J}=\nabla\times{\bf B}/\mu_{0}
is the current.
This equation is essentially the same as the MHD momentum equation,
except that the isotropic gas pressure tensor
p{\sf I}
is replaced by
the anisotropic CGL pressure tensor

{\sf p}=p_{\parallel}{\bm{\tau}}{\bm{\tau}}+p_{\perp}({\sf I}-{\bm{\tau}}{\bm{\tau}})
.
However:

\nabla{\bf\cdot}{\sf p}=-\left[{\bf B}\times(\nabla\times{\bm{\Omega}})+\rho(T\nabla S-\nabla h)\right].

(252)

(e.g.
61
,
6
,
12
)
where

{\bm{\Omega}}=\frac{p_{\Delta}}{B^{2}}{\bf B},\quad p_{\Delta}=p_{\parallel}-p_{\perp},\quad h=\frac{\varepsilon+p_{\parallel}}{\rho}.

(253)

Using
\nabla{\bf\cdot}{\sf p}
from (
252
) and (
253
)
in (
251
) results in the CGL momentum equation in the form:

\frac{d{\bf u}}{dt}=T\nabla S-\nabla h+\frac{\tilde{{\bf J}}\times{\bf B}}{\rho}-\nabla\Phi,

(254)

where

\displaystyle\tilde{\bf J}=

\displaystyle{\bf J}-\nabla\times{\bm{\Omega}}=\frac{\nabla\times\tilde{\bf B}}{\mu_{0}},

(255)

\displaystyle\tilde{\bf B}=

\displaystyle{\bf B}\left[1-\frac{\mu_{0}p_{\Delta}}{B^{2}}\right].

(256)

In the form (
254
) the anisotropic pressure force term

-\nabla{\bf\cdot}{\sf p}/\rho
has been partly transformed into the
modified force
{\tilde{J}}\times{\bf B}/\rho
where

\tilde{\bf J}=(\nabla\times\tilde{\bf B})/\mu_{0}
in which
\tilde{\bf B}
is
the modified magnetic induction
\tilde{\bf B}
. The form of
\tilde{\bf J}

suggests that
-\nabla\times{\bm{\Omega}}
could be interpreted
as a magnetization current.

It is interesting to note that
\tilde{B}=B(1-\mu_{0}p_{\Delta}/B^{2})<0
if

p_{\Delta}=(p_{\parallel}-p_{\perp})>B^{2}/\mu_{0}
, which
corresponds to the firehose instability threshold (e.g.
Stix 1992
,

Hunana et al. 2016
;
Hunana and Zank 2017
).
The mirror instability threshold for the CGL plasma model does not
correspond to plasma kinetic theory.
For the CGL plasma model with zero electron pressure (i.e. cold electrons)
the mirror instability occurs if:

p_{\perp}-p_{\parallel}>(5/6)p_{\perp}+p_{\parallel}p_{B}/p_{\perp}
whereas
kinetic theory gives the threshold for the instability as
p_{\perp}-p_{\parallel}>p_{\parallel}p_{B}/p_{\perp}
where
p_{B}=B^{2}/(2\mu)
is the magnetic pressure.
Note the firehose instability occurs
if the parallel pressure dominates the perpendicular
pressure. Similarly if
p_{\perp}
dominates
p_{\parallel}
one obtains the
mirror instability.

In the
Hazeltine et al. 2013
and
Holm and Kupershmidt 1986

approaches, the internal energy density per unit mass,
e

satisfies the first law of thermodynamics, in the form:

TdS=de+p_{\parallel}d\tau+\left(\frac{p_{\Delta}}{\rho B}\right)dB.

(257)

The variational formulation of
Newcomb 1962

does not explicitly require (
257
) to apply.
The
Newcomb 1962
Lagrangian action principle leads to
the correct momentum equation for the CGL plasma,
and to a Hamiltonian formulation of the equations
in Lagrangian variables.

The Lagrangian variational principle for ideal CGL plasmas
obtained by
Newcomb 1962
was used in Section 3 to obtain
a canonical Hamiltonian formulation
of the equations based on the canonical coordinates

{\bf q}={\bf x}({\bf x}_{0},t)

and the canonical momentum
{\bm{\pi}}=\rho_{0}\dot{\bf x}({\bf x}_{0},t)
,
and also to establish that stationary variations of the action
give the CGL momentum equation in both its Lagrangian and Eulerian
forms.

Section 4 provides an Euler-Poincaré action principle derivation of the
CGL momentum equation (see also Appendix E, which uses the approach of

Holm et al. 1998
).

By transforming the canonical Poisson bracket for the CGL system
of Section 3, to non-canonical physical variables leads to
the non-canonical Poisson bracket for the CGL system of Section 5.
The detailed transformation
formulae from the canonical coordinates to the physical variables

\psi=(\rho,\sigma,{\bf M},{\bf B})^{T}

is described in Appendix D.
By writing the non-canonical Poisson bracket in co-symplectic form, leads to a system of equations for
the Casimirs
C
, as solutions of the Poisson bracket
equation:
\{C,K\}=0
where
K
is an arbitrary functional of the
physical variables. The Casimirs satisfy
{\mathcal{C}}_{t}=\{C,K\}=0
.
Hamiltonian dynamics of the system takes place on the symplectic
leaves
C=const.
of the system.

The classical Casimirs for ideal fluids and plasmas are
the mass conservation integral,
the cross helicity integral for barotropic flows, and the magnetic helicity.
For the CGL plasma case, there is effectively one entropy function
that is Lie dragged with the flow, because
S_{\parallel}
and
S_{\perp}

are assumed to be functions only of
S
. In principle, a more complicated
bracket would arise, if one used the possibility that the adiabatic
integrals for
p_{\perp}
and
p_{\parallel}
depended on other scalar invariants
that are advected with the flow. For example the integrals (
13
)
could also depend on the scalar invariant
{\bf B}{\bf\cdot}\nabla S/\rho
.
This possibility was not explored in the present paper.

The links between Noether’s theorem and conservation laws
for CGL plasmas are developed (Section 6).
The evolutionary symmetry
form of the Lie invariance condition for the action is used.
This approach differs
from the
canonical symmetry operator
form of the invariance condition (e.g.
Webb and Anco 2019
, see also Appendix H).
In the evolutionary form of the symmetry operator,
the independent variables are frozen
and all the Lie transformation changes are restricted to
changes in the dependent variables and their derivatives
(see e.g.
Olver 1993
,
Ibragimov 1985
,
Bluman and Anco 2002
).
In the canonical symmetry approach,
both the dependent and independent variables and their derivatives
change.

The CGL plasma equations admit the
Galilean Lie point symmetry group, and three extra scaling symmetries
(Appendix F).
The Galilean group leads to (i) the energy conservation law due to time translation invariance of the action;
(ii) the momentum conservation equations due
to space translation invariances; (iii) the conservation of angular momentum due to rotational invariance

Lingam, Morrison and Wurm 2020
study extended variational principles of MHD (and CGL)
type including gyro-viscous effects (i.e.
higher order finite Larmor radius effects in the collisionless limit).
A single gyro-viscous term is added to the usual action.
The gyro-viscous term alters the total
momentum density, but it does not alter the divergence of the mass flux.
The total momentum density has the form:

{\bf L}^{*}=(2m/e){\bm{\mu}}

{\bm{\mu}}=\mu{\bm{\tau}}
