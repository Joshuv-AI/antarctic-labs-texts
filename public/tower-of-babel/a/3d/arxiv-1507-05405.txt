Remarks on Contact and Jacobi Geometry
Andrew James BRUCE †, Katarzyna GRABOWSKA ‡ and Janusz GRABOWSKI §

Abstract
We present an approach to Jacobi and contact geometry that makes many facts, presented in the literature in an overcomplicated way, much more natural and clear. The key concepts are Kirillov manifolds and linear Kirillov structures , i.e., homogeneous Poisson manifolds and, respectively, homogeneous linear Poisson manifolds. The difference with the existing literature is that the homogeneity of the Poisson structure is related to a principal GL ⁡ ( 1 , ℝ ) {\rm GL}(1,{\mathbb{R}}) -bundle structure on the manifold and not just to a vector field. This allows for working with Jacobi bundle structures on nontrivial line bundles and drastically simplifies the picture of Jacobi and contact geometry. Our results easily reduce to various basic theorems of Jacobi and contact geometry when the principal bundle structure is trivial, while giving new insights into the theory.

Introduction

There is extensive literature in differential geometry devoted to Jacobi structures and derived concepts in which Jacobi structures are presented as generalising Poisson structures. The aim in this paper is to put some order into the field, and to convince the reader that the properly understood concept of a Jacobi structure is a specialisation of a Poisson structure and not a generalisation. We present an approach to Jacobi and contact geometry which results in drastic simplification of many concepts, examples and proofs, gives a completely new insight into the theory, as well giving novel discoveries and observations. The main motivation for writing this paper was our observation that many papers in the subject are unnecessarily complicated, because the authors generally ignore, to different extents, the fact that Jacobi geometry is nothing else but homogeneous Poisson geometry on principal
\GL(1,{\mathbb{R}})
-bundles (
{{\mathbb{R}}^{\times}}
-bundles, for short).

It is common practice to understand the
Jacobi bracket
as a Lie bracket on an algebra of functions on a (smooth) manifold
M
. However, a quick analysis shows its ‘module nature’, and we see that rank
1
modules (i.e., line bundles) form the natural and proper framework for such structures. In the trivial case, sections of the bundle
{\mathbb{R}}\times M\to M
are identified with the algebra
{\cal A}=C^{\infty}(M)
of functions, and the regular
{\cal A}
-module structure on
{\cal A}
looks exactly like the multiplication in
{\cal A}
, although morphisms in the category of modules are different from these in the category of rings. This we believe is the root of many misunderstandings in Jacobi geometry.

Everyone who works with Jacobi brackets knows there exists something like the
poissonisation
of a Jacobi structure. However, in most cases this is seen as the
poissonisation trick
and used only as a technical tool for proving particular results formulated in the ‘intrinsic’ terms of Jacobi geometry. In consequence, the true landscape of Jacobi geometry, which is actually a homogeneous Poisson geometry, is hidden in the fog of the ‘intrinsic Jacobi language’. In our understanding, poissonisation is not a trick, but a genuine framework for Jacobi geometry, and it necessarily comes together with an additional structure of a
{{\mathbb{R}}^{\times}}
-bundle. Moreover, the insistence of working in terms of brackets, be they Poisson or Jacobi, often leads to complicated algebraic considerations in which the geometry is completely obscured. In this paper we will work with the corresponding tensor structures and the brackets themselves, which we view as secondary notions, will play no explicit rôle beyond initial motivation.

Using Jacobi-type brackets on sections of a line bundle
L\to M
is nothing else but working with ‘local Lie algebras’ in the sense of Kirillov
[
27
]
, so we will call them
Kirillov brackets
. Moreover, the corresponding poissonisations live not on
M\times{\mathbb{R}}
but on the dual bundle
L^{*}
with the removed
0
-section,
(L^{*})^{\times}=L^{*}\setminus\{0_{M}\}
, which can be recognised as a principal
\GL(1,{\mathbb{R}})
-bundle equipped with a homogeneous Poisson tensor. Such structures we will call
Kirillov manifolds
(
Kirillov structures
). We are working with a contact structure if this tensor is actually symplectic. Note that in general by contact structure we mean
non-coorientable contact structure
, that is we do not have a globally defined contact one-form. Working with non-coorientable contact structures forces one to consider non-trivial line bundles and so one has to leave the setting of standard Jacobi geometry. We generally use the identification
\GL(1,{\mathbb{R}})\simeq{\mathbb{R}}^{\times}
, where
{\mathbb{R}}^{\times}={\mathbb{R}}\setminus\{0\}
is the group of multiplicative reals. The appearance of the non-connected group
{\mathbb{R}}^{\times}
is forced by the fact that real line bundles over
M
are classified by a
\mathbb{Z}_{2}={\mathbb{R}}^{\times}/{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
cohomology of
M
.

It is well known that the choice of one of equivalent definitions influences strongly our way of thinking and makes the formulations of some concepts and generalisations easier or harder, depending on the choice made. We will insist in this paper in understanding the ‘poissonisation’ as the genuine Jacobi structure with all consequences of this choice. In this sense, the properly understood concept of a Jacobi structure is a specialisation rather than a generalisation of a Poisson structure. However, in contrast to the standard definition, we understand the homogeneity not as associated with a vector field, but rather a principal bundle structure. Thus, our Poisson structure is ‘homogeneous’ in the sense that it is homogeneous of degree
-1
on a certain
{{\mathbb{R}}^{\times}}
-bundle. The appearance of this principal bundle structure is absolutely fundamental for the whole picture. In other words, the proper playground for Jacobi geometry will be the category of
Kirillov manifolds
, i.e.,
{{\mathbb{R}}^{\times}}
-bundles equipped with a homogeneous Poisson structure. Moreover, all derived concepts like ‘Jacobi algebroid’, ‘Jacobi bialgebroid’, ‘Jacobi/contact groupoid’, etc., should be understood as the corresponding objects in Poisson geometry, equipped additionally with a principal
{\mathbb{R}}^{\times}
-action which is compatible with the other structures. The only thing to be decided is a reasonable notion of compatibility.

Compatibility with a vector bundle structure can be described, in the spirit of viewing linear structures as defined by a certain action of the monoid
({\mathbb{R}},\cdot)
of multiplicative reals
[
18
,
19
]
, as commutation of
{\mathbb{R}}
- and
{\mathbb{R}}^{\times}
-actions. As a side remark, actions of the monoid of complex numbers were explored in
[
24
]
. Following the observations made in
[
7
]
(cf.
[
2
,
3
,
4
]
), the compatibility with a Lie algebroid/groupoid structure is described as an
{\mathbb{R}}^{\times}
-action by the Lie algebroid/groupoid morphisms, etc. We will call such Lie groupoids
\mathbb{R}^{\times}
-
groupoids
, or more generally, for any Lie group
G
, we have the notion of a
G
-
groupoid
. The infinitesimal objects are
G
-algebroids
. In particular,
{{\mathbb{R}}^{\times}}
-algebroids turn out to be the objects dual to linear Poisson manifolds equipped with a compatible principal
{{\mathbb{R}}^{\times}}
-action (
linear Kirillov structures
).

The compatibility of a symplectic or Poisson structure with the
{\mathbb{R}}^{\times}
-action is expressed in terms of homogeneity. We must again stress that this homogeneity is not defined in terms of a vector field like in
[
10
,
30
,
36
]
, but in terms of the principal
{\mathbb{R}}^{\times}
-action. The fundamental vector fields define only the action of the connected component in the group. Another important ingredient of our framework, not really exploited in the literature, is the use of tangent and phase lifts of principal
{{\mathbb{R}}^{\times}}
-bundle structures.

We stress that our definition of a contact groupoid, i.e., a
\mathbb{R}^{\times}
-groupoid equipped with a homogeneous and multiplicative symplectic form, turns out to be equivalent to the definition of Dazord
[
11
]
. The corresponding objects are called by many authors
conformal contact groupoids
, for example
[
10
]
. The first and frequently used definition, presented in
[
26
]
, is less general and involves an arbitrary multiplicative function, that is due to the fact that in this approach contact bundles are forced to be trivial. Also the definition proposed by Libermann
[
29
]
requires a contact form. There are, however, no doubts that allowing for contact structures which do not come from a global contact form is fundamental for the completeness and elegance of the theory. On the other hand, although Dazord’s definition is general and simple, it does not have any direct extension to Jacobi groupoids. Crainic and Salazar
[
9
]
also realise that there are artificial complications that arise when insisting on working with trivial line bundles, but their way of solving the problem is completely different to ours and makes use of Spencer operators.

Our framework produces, for any Lie groupoid
\mathcal{G}
, canonical examples of contact groupoids
C(\mathcal{G})\subset{{T}}^{*}\mathcal{G}
, consisting of all covectors that vanish on vectors that are tangent to the source or target fibres. Interestingly, such examples are somewhat universal and generic; every contact groupoid can be represented or realised as a contact subgroupoid of a canonical one. We will of course be more precise in due course.

Among the main results of this paper:

•

We give the description of any
G
-groupoid in terms of a ‘splitting’ into a product of the
G
-bundle of units and the reduced groupoid (Theorem
4.10
). In the simplest case, this description reduces to the well-known groupoid extension by the additive
{\mathbb{R}}
with a help of a multiplicative function.

•

We show that
G
-algebroids and
G
-groupoids are related via Lie theory (Theorem
4.7
).

•

We observe that the dual objects of linear Kirillov structures are exactly
{{\mathbb{R}}^{\times}}
-algebroids (Proposition
4.17
).

•

We present the Lie theory of Kirillov manifolds and contact groupoids (Proposition
5.6
).

•

We prove that all contact groupoids have a realisation as a contact subgroupoid of a canonical contact groupoid (Theorem
5.8
).

To sum up, our intention is to show how the setting of homogeneous Poisson geometry simplifies various concepts and problems of Jacobi and contact geometry. We would like to emphasise that, besides a number of new observations, the novelty of this paper lies mainly in the underlying approach that unifies and generalises various points of views, as well as establishes the proper language for the Jacobi and contact geometry. This results in a drastic simplification of proofs of various important facts spread over the literature and clarification of the strategies used. We hope that our work will put a new light on Jacobi and contact geometry as a whole, even if separate observations may seem to be known to the reader.

Kirillov brackets and Kirillov manifolds

3.1
Kirillov manifolds

A line bundle equipped with a local Lie bracket on its sections,
(L,[\cdot,\cdot]_{L})
, as introduced by Kirillov
[
27
]
, is known in the literature also as a
Jacobi bundle
following
[
36
]
. Locally this bracket is given by

\displaystyle[f,g]_{L}(x)=\mathchar 28675\relax^{ab}(x)\frac{\partial f}{\partial x^{a}}(x)\frac{\partial g}{\partial x^{b}}(x)+\mathchar 28675\relax^{a}(x)\left(f(x)\frac{\partial g}{\partial x^{a}}(x)-\frac{\partial f}{\partial x^{a}}(x)g(x)\right).

(3.1)

One can identify smooth sections of a line bundle
L
with smooth homogeneous functions of degree one on
L^{*}
, and further also with homogeneous functions of degree one on the principal
{\mathbb{R}}^{\times}
-bundle
L^{{\textstyle*}\times}:=(L^{*})^{\times}
, i.e., functions
f\colon L^{\times}\to{\mathbb{R}}
such that
f(\textnormal{h}_{s}(v)):=f(s.v)=sf(v)
. We denote this identification via
u\rightsquigarrow\iota_{u}
, where
u\in{Sec}(L)
.

Having a Kirillov bracket
[\cdot,\cdot]_{L}
on sections of
L
, we can try to define a Poisson bracket
\{\cdot,\cdot\}_{\mathchar 28675\relax}
, associated with a linear Poisson structure
\mathchar 28675\relax
on
L^{*}
, using the identity

\displaystyle\iota_{[u,v]_{L}}=\{\iota_{u},\iota_{v}\}_{\Lambda}.

(3.2)

However, unlike the case of a Lie algebroid, this bracket is generally singular at points of the zero-section. Instead, one has to define a Poisson tensor on
L^{*\times}
. Indeed, in dual coordinates
(x^{a},t)
on
L^{*}
,

\displaystyle\mathchar 28675\relax(x,t)=\frac{1}{2t}\mathchar 28675\relax^{ab}(x)\partial_{x^{a}}\wedge\partial_{x^{b}}+\mathchar 28675\relax^{a}(x)\partial_{t}\wedge\partial_{x^{a}}.

(3.3)

This identification allows for a very useful characterisation of Kirillov brackets (cf.
[
15
,
36
]
) in terms of
Kirillov manifolds
(
Kirillov structures
).

Definition 3.1

(
[
15
]
)
.

A
principal Poisson
{\mathbb{R}}^{\times}
-bundle
, shortly
Kirillov manifold
, is a principal
{\mathbb{R}}^{\times}
-bundle
(P,\textnormal{h})
equipped with a Poisson structure
\mathchar 28675\relax
of degree
-1
. A
morphism of Kirillov manifolds

\phi\colon P\rightarrow P^{\prime}
is a Poisson morphism that intertwines the respective
{\mathbb{R}}^{\times}
-actions.

A little more explicitly, a Kirillov manifold is a triple
(P,\textnormal{h},\mathchar 28675\relax)
, where
(P,\textnormal{h})
is a principle
{\mathbb{R}}^{\times}
-bundle, and
\mathchar 28675\relax
is a Poisson structure on
P
such that
(\textnormal{h}_{s})_{*}\mathchar 28675\relax=s^{-1}\mathchar 28675\relax
, for all
s\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\times}
. In particular,
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{\times}
with coordinates
(x^{a},t)
and trivial
{{\mathbb{R}}^{\times}}
-principal bundle structure, equipped with a Poisson tensor of the form (
3.3
), is a basic example of a Kirillov manifold.

Evidently, Kirillov manifolds form a category under the standard composition of smooth maps. We summarise all the above observations as:

Theorem 3.2
.

There is a one-to-one correspondence between Kirillov brackets
[\cdot,\cdot]_{L}
on a line bundle
L\rightarrow M
and Kirillov manifold structures on the principal
\mathbb{R}^{\times}
-bundle
P=L^{*\times}
given by (
3.2
).

Remark 3.3
.

The local representation (
3.3
) identifies (locally) Kirillov manifolds with
Jacobi manifolds
in the sense of Lichnerowicz
[
31
]
, i.e., manifolds equipped with a bivector field
\widetilde{\mathchar 28675\relax}:=\frac{1}{2}\mathchar 28675\relax^{ab}(x)\partial_{x^{a}}\wedge\partial_{x^{b}}
and a vector field
\widetilde{R}:=\mathchar 28675\relax^{a}(x)\partial_{x^{a}}
such that (
3.1
) is a Lie bracket. In terms of the Schouten bracket
[\![\cdot,\cdot]\!]
this is equivalent to the system of identities

\displaystyle[\![\widetilde{R},\widetilde{\mathchar 28675\relax}]\!]=0,\qquad[\![\widetilde{\mathchar 28675\relax},\widetilde{\mathchar 28675\relax}]\!]=2\widetilde{R}\wedge\widetilde{\mathchar 28675\relax}.

In other words, for a trivial principal
{\mathbb{R}}^{\times}
-bundle
P=M\times{\mathbb{R}}^{\times}
, the reduced tangent and cotangent bundles can be identified as

\displaystyle{{T}}(M\times{\mathbb{R}}^{\times})/{\mathbb{R}}^{\times}\simeq{{T}}M\times{\mathbb{R}},\qquad{{T}}^{*}(M\times{\mathbb{R}}^{\times})/{\mathbb{R}}^{\times}\simeq{{T}}^{*}M\times{\mathbb{R}},

and the reduced map (

\displaystyle\mathchar 28675\relax^{\#}_{0}\colon\ {{T}}^{*}M\times{\mathbb{R}}\rightarrow{{T}}M\times{\mathbb{R}},

induced by the Jacobi structure.

3.2
Coisotropic submanifolds of Kirillov manifolds

Recall that a submanifold
S
of a Poisson manifold
(P,\mathchar 28675\relax)
is called
coisotropic
if the ideal
I_{S}
of functions vanishing on
S
is closed under the Poisson bracket. In the context of a Kirillov manifold
(P,\textnormal{h},\Lambda)
, of particular interest are coisotropic submanifolds which are simultaneously
{{\mathbb{R}}^{\times}}
-subbundles; we will call them
coisotropic Kirillov submanifolds
or simply
coisotropic subbundles
. The natural inclusion
S\hookrightarrow P
implies
S_{0}\hookrightarrow P_{0}
, where
S_{0}=S/\penalty\mathbb{R}^{\times}
is the reduced manifold. Of course, in the traditional language,
S_{0}
is called a coisotropic submanifold of the corresponding Jacobi structure (cf.
[
28
]
).

Suppose now that
S
is a coisotropic subbundle of the Kirillov manifold
P
. It is clear that in local coordinates
(t,x^{\alpha},y^{i})
on
P
adapted to
S
, so
(t,x^{\alpha})
form a coordinate system on
S
, the Poisson structure on
P
encoding the Kirillov manifold structure is of the form

\displaystyle\Lambda=\frac{1}{2t}\Lambda^{\alpha\beta}(x,y)\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{\beta}}\wedge\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{\alpha}}+\frac{1}{t}\Lambda^{\alpha i}(x,y)\frac{\partial}{\partial y^{i}}\wedge\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{\alpha}}

\displaystyle\hphantom{\Lambda=}{}+\frac{1}{2t}\Lambda^{ij}(x,y)\frac{\partial}{\partial y^{j}}\wedge\frac{\partial}{\partial y^{i}}+\Lambda^{\alpha}(x,y)\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{\alpha}}\wedge\frac{\partial}{\partial t}+\Lambda^{i}(x,y)\frac{\partial}{\partial y^{i}}\wedge\frac{\partial}{\partial t},

(3.4)

where we require
\Lambda^{ij}=0
and
\Lambda^{i}=0
on
S
.

Hamiltonian vector fields
X_{f}:=\{f,\cdot\}_{\Lambda}
where
f\in I_{S}
are tangent to
S
and so form an integrable distribution. The corresponding foliation is known as the
characteristic foliation
of
S
. The space of leaves, if smooth, will inherit a Kirillov manifold structure. The reduction of the Poisson structure (
3.4
) is obvious. To see that we can canonically reduce the homogeneous structure, consider the weight vector field
\nabla_{P}
generating
h
and the Lie bracket
[\nabla_{P},X_{f}]
, where
f\in I_{S}
. We have

\displaystyle\langle[\nabla_{P},X_{f}],\operatorname{d}g\rangle={\pounds}_{\nabla_{P}}\langle{X_{f}},{\operatorname{d}g}\rangle-\langle X_{f},{\pounds}_{\nabla_{P}}\operatorname{d}g\rangle

\displaystyle\hphantom{\langle[\nabla_P,X_f],\operatorname{d}g\rangle}{}={\pounds}_{\nabla_{P}}\left(\mathchar 28675\relax(\operatorname{d}f,\operatorname{d}g)\right)-\mathchar 28675\relax(\operatorname{d}f,{\pounds}_{\nabla_{P}}\operatorname{d}g)

\displaystyle\hphantom{\langle[\nabla_P,X_f],\operatorname{d}g\rangle}{}=({\pounds}_{\nabla_{P}}\mathchar 28675\relax)(\operatorname{d}f,\operatorname{d}g)+\mathchar 28675\relax({\pounds}_{\nabla_{P}}\operatorname{d}f,\operatorname{d}g)

\displaystyle\hphantom{\langle[\nabla_P,X_f],\operatorname{d}g\rangle}{}=-\mathchar 28675\relax(\operatorname{d}f,\operatorname{d}g)+\mathchar 28675\relax({\pounds}_{\nabla_{P}}\operatorname{d}f,\operatorname{d}g)=X_{(\nabla_{P}(f)-f)}(g).

Here we used the fact that
\mathchar 28675\relax
is homogeneous. Now, as
\nabla_{P}
is tangent to
S
, we have
\nabla_{P}(f){-}f\!\in\!I_{S}
, so that
\nabla_{P}
lies in the normalizer of the Lie algebra of vector fields tangent to leaves of the characteristic distribution, hence
\nabla_{P}
(as well as
h
) preserves the foliation and thus induces a homogeneous structure on the manifold of leaves.

Studying reductions, deformations, etc., of coisotropic subbundles is an interesting but extensive task, which we postpone for future study.

3.3
Contact structures

Proceeding to contact structures, first note that a nowhere-vanishing one-form
\mathchar 28939\relax
spans a trivial one dimensional vector subbundle
[\alpha]
of
{{T}}^{*}M
. Associated with
\alpha
is a canonical embedding
I_{\alpha}\colon\mathbb{R}\times M\rightarrow{{T}}^{*}M
which induces an isomorphism of
\mathbb{R}\times M
with
[\alpha]
. In natural local coordinates the canonical embedding is given by

\displaystyle I_{\alpha}^{*}\big(x^{a},p_{b}\big)=\big(x^{a},t\alpha_{b}(x)\big),

(3.5)

where
t
is the (global) coordinate on
\mathbb{R}
and locally we have
\alpha=\alpha_{a}(x)\textnormal{d}x^{a}
.

Proposition 3.4
.

The nowhere-vanishing one-form
\alpha
is a contact form if and only if the trivial principal bundle
\mathbb{R}^{\times}\times M
is, via
I_{\alpha}
, a symplectic submanifold
[\alpha]^{\times}\subset{{T}}^{*}M
.

The above propositions is essentially a well-known rewording of the standard notion of the ‘symplectisation’ of a contact form. In particular, it is easy to see that in Darboux coordinates

\displaystyle I_{\alpha}^{*}\big(\textnormal{d}p_{a}\wedge\textnormal{d}x^{a}\big):=\omega=\textnormal{d}t\wedge\alpha,

which gives the symplectisation of
\alpha
remembering that
t\neq 0
. Moreover, note that the contact form can be recovered from

\displaystyle i_{\nabla}\omega=t\alpha,

where
\nabla
is the Euler vector field on the principal
\mathbb{R}^{\times}
-bundle
[\alpha]^{\times}
, i.e., the fundamental vector field of the
{{\mathbb{R}}^{\times}}
-action. All this implies the following.

Proposition 3.5

(
[
15
]
)
.

A line subbundle
C
of
{{T}}^{*}M
is locally generated by contact one-forms if and only if
C^{\times}
is a symplectic submanifold of
{{T}}^{*}M
.

Definition 3.6
.

A principal
\mathbb{R}^{\times}
-bundle
(P,\textnormal{h})
equipped with a 1-homogeneous symplectic form
\omega
, i.e., a symplectic form such that
(\textnormal{h}_{t})^{*}\omega=t\omega
(
t\neq 0
), will be referred to as a
contact structure
. In other words, a contact structure is a Kirillov manifold whose Poisson structure is invertible (symplectic).

Let
\nabla
be the Euler vector field on
P
,
\nabla\colon P\to{{T}}P
. It is easy to see that the composition
\mathchar 28945\relax=\mathchar 28961\relax^{\flat}\circ\nabla\colon P\to{{T}}^{*}P
is a one-form on
P
which takes values in basic covectors,
\mathchar 28945\relax(y)=\mathchar 28953\relax^{*}(\Psi(y))\in{{T}}^{*}_{y}P
,
\Psi(y)\in{{T}}^{*}_{\mathchar 28953\relax(y)}P_{0}
, so can be viewed as a map
\Psi\colon P\to{{T}}^{*}P_{0}
which locally has the form (
3.5
). Consequently, the range
C^{\times}(P)=\{\Psi(y)~|~y\in P\}
of
\Psi
is a a symplectic submanifold in
{{T}}^{*}P_{0}
. Thus we get the following.

Theorem 3.7

(
[
15
]
)
.

Any contact structure
(P,\mathchar 28961\relax,\textnormal{h})
, where
P
is an
\mathbb{R}^{\times}
-bundle over
P_{0}
, can be canonically symplectically embedded into
{{T}}^{*}P_{0}
as a symplectic principal
\mathbb{R}^{\times}
-bundle of the form
C^{\times}
for a line subbundle
C\subset{{T}}^{*}P_{0}
.

Remark 3.8
.

Commonly, a contact structure on a manifold
M
is understood as a maximally non-integrable hyperplane distribution
\mathcal{D}\subset{{T}}M
, locally given as the polar (annihilator) of a line bundle
C\subset{{T}}^{*}M
generated by contact one-forms,
\mathcal{D}=C^{0}
. We will refer to such hyperplane distributions
\mathcal{D}\subset{{T}}M
as
contact distributions
to avoid confusion. In our language, contact structures are homogeneous symplectic structures on a principal
{\mathbb{R}}^{\times}
-bundle
P
, while in the classical language they are certain hyperplane distributions on the reduced manifold
M=P/{\mathbb{R}}^{\times}
.

Example 3.9
.

The canonical symplectic structure on the cotangent bundle
{{T}}^{*}M
is linear, thus homogeneous on
({{T}}^{*}M)^{\times}
. The symplectic homogeneous manifold
P=({{T}}^{*}M)^{\times}
represents therefore a contact structure. In the traditional language it is a canonical contact structure on the reduced manifold
({{T}}^{*}M)^{\times}/{{\mathbb{R}}^{\times}}
, i.e., on the projectivisation
\mathcal{P}({{T}}^{*}M)
of the cotangent bundle.

Example 3.10
.

Consider a principal
{\mathbb{R}}^{\times}
-bundle
(P,\textnormal{h})
. It is easy to see that the canonical symplectic form on the cotangent bundle
{{T}}^{*}P
is homogeneous with respect to the lifted action
{{T}}^{*}\textnormal{h}
, so
{{T}}^{*}P
represents canonically a contact structure. If we write
P=L^{*\times}
, then in the traditional language this is exactly the canonical contact structure
C
on the reduced manifold
{{T}}^{*}P/{\mathbb{R}}^{\times}
which is the first jet bundle
J^{1}P^{+}=J^{1}L
. When
L
is the trivial bundle, i.e.,
L={\mathbb{R}}\times M
, the canonical contact structure
C
is the trivial line subbundle of
{{T}}^{*}(\mathbb{R}\times{{T}}^{*}M)
generated by the contact form
\alpha=\textnormal{d}z-p_{a}\textnormal{d}x^{a}
. Thus we have
P=C^{\times}=\mathbb{R}^{\times}\times\mathbb{R}\times{{T}}^{*}M
, which we equip with local coordinates
(t,z,x^{a},p_{b})
and thus the symplectic structure on
P
is

\displaystyle\omega=\textnormal{d}t\wedge\textnormal{d}z-p_{a}\textnormal{d}t\wedge\textnormal{d}x^{a}-t\textnormal{d}p_{a}\wedge\textnormal{d}x^{a}.

Remark 3.11
.

Contact structures on non-negatively graded manifolds further equipped with homological contact vector fields were studied by Mehta
[
37
]
using a more traditional language than put forward here. In particular, for the degree
1
case he showed that there is a one-to-one correspondence between such structures (with a global contact form) and Jacobi manifolds. The line bundle approach to the concept of a
generalised contact bundle
can be found in the work of Vitagliano and Wade
[
43
]
. Furthermore, the
{{\mathbb{R}}^{\times}}
-principal bundle approach can also be applied to the notion of a
contact structure on a Lie algebroid
following Ida and Popescu
[
20
, Remark 4.2]
.

4
Principal bundle Lie groupoids and algebroids

4.1
Morphisms of Lie groupoids and Lie algebroids

Our general reference to the theory of Lie groupoids and Lie algebroids will be Mackenzie’s book
[
34
]
.

Let
\mathcal{G}\rightrightarrows M
be an arbitrary Lie groupoid with
source map

s\colon\mathcal{G}\rightarrow M
and
target map

t\colon\mathcal{G}\rightarrow M
. There is also the inclusion map
\iota_{M}\colon M\rightarrow\mathcal{G}
,
\mathchar 28947\relax_{M}(x)=\mathbbmss{1}_{x}
, and a
partial multiplication

(g,h)\mapsto gh
which is defined on
\mathcal{G}^{(2)}=\{(g,h)\in\mathcal{G}\times\mathcal{G}\colon s(g)=t(h)\}
. Moreover, the manifold
\mathcal{G}
is foliated by
s
-fibres
\mathcal{G}_{x}=\{\left.g\in\mathcal{G}\right|s(g)=x\}
, where
x\in M
. As by definition the source and target maps are submersions, the
s
-fibres are themselves smooth manifolds. Geometric objects associated with this foliation will be given the superscript
s
. In particular, the distribution tangent to the leaves of the foliation will be denoted by
{{T}}^{s}\mathcal{G}
. To ensure no misunderstanding with the notion of a Lie groupoid morphism we recall the definition we will be using.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
\mathcal{G}_{i}\rightrightarrows M_{i}

(i=1,2)
be a pair of Lie groupoids. Then a
Lie groupoid morphisms
is a pair of maps
(\Phi,\phi)
such that the following diagram is commutative

\textstyle{\mathcal{G}_{1}}
\textstyle{\mathcal{G}_{2}}
\textstyle{M_{1}}
\textstyle{M_{2}}
\textstyle{\Phi}
\textstyle{s_{1}}
\textstyle{t_{1}}
\textstyle{s_{2}}
\textstyle{t_{2}}
\textstyle{\phi}

in the sense that
s_{2}\circ\Phi=\phi\circ s_{1}
, and
t_{2}\circ\Phi=\phi\circ t_{1}

subject to the further condition that
\Phi
respects the (partial) multiplication; if
g,h\in\mathcal{G}_{1}
are composable, then
\Phi(gh)=\Phi(g)\Phi(h)
. It then follows that for
x\in M_{1}
we have
\Phi(\mathbbmss{1}_{x})=\mathbbmss{1}_{\phi(x)}
and
\Phi(g^{-1})=\Phi(g)^{-1}
.

Consider a Lie groupoid
\mathcal{G}\rightrightarrows M
. A
Lie subgroupoid
of
\mathcal{G}
is a Lie groupoid
\mathcal{H}\rightrightarrows M^{\prime}
together with injective immersions
\mathchar 28947\relax\colon\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{G}
and
\mathchar 28947\relax_{0}\colon M^{\prime}\to M
such that
(\mathchar 28947\relax,\mathchar 28947\relax_{0})
is a morphism of Lie groupoids. In particular,
\mathchar 28947\relax(\mathcal{H})
it is closed under multiplication (when defined) and inversion. If
\mathchar 28947\relax_{0}(M^{\prime})=M
, then the Lie subgroupoid
\mathcal{H}\rightrightarrows M
is said to be a
wide subgroupoid
.

The Cartesian product
\mathcal{G}_{1}\times\mathcal{G}_{2}
of two Lie groupoids is canonically a Lie groupoid, and it follows immediately from the above definition that
\Phi
is a Lie groupoid morphism if and only if its graph is a Lie subgroupoid in
\mathcal{G}\times\mathcal{H}
.

A similar fact holds true for Lie algebroids, but as we have many alternative definitions of a Lie algebroid, there are many alternative definitions of a Lie algebroid morphism (see, e.g.,
[
14
, Theorem 14]
in a little more general setting). Dealing with homogeneous Poisson structures in this paper, we will mainly understand a Lie algebroid on a vector bundle
E
as a linear Poisson structure on
E^{*}
. Then, as is commonly known (see, e.g., Mackenzie
[
34
, p. 400]
), Lie subalgebroids of
E
correspond to coisotropic subbundles in the Poisson manifold
E^{*}
by passing to the polar in the dual bundle.

It is also well known that via a differentiation procedure one can construct the
Lie functor

\displaystyle\textnormal{{Grpd}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textnormal{Lie}}}{{\xrightarrow{\hskip 30.0pt}}}\textnormal{{Algd}},

that sends a Lie groupoid to its Lie algebroid, and sends morphisms of Lie groupoids to morphisms of the corresponding Lie algebroids. However, as is also well known, we do not have an equivalence of categories as not all Lie algebroids arise as the infinitesimal versions of Lie groupoids. There is no direct generalisation of Lie III, apart from the local case. The obstruction to the integrability of Lie algebroids, the so called
monodromy groups
, were first uncovered by Crainic and Ferandes
[
8
]
. To set some notation and nomenclature, given a Lie groupoid
\mathcal{G}
, we say that
\mathcal{G}

integrates

\textnormal{Lie}(\mathcal{G})=\textnormal{A}(\mathcal{G})
. Moreover, if
\Phi\colon\mathcal{G}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}
is a morphism of Lie groupoids, then we will write
\Phi^{\prime}=\textnormal{Lie}(\Phi)\colon\textnormal{A}(\mathcal{G})\rightarrow\textnormal{A}(\mathcal{H})
for the corresponding Lie algebroid morphism, which actually comes from the differential
{{T}}\Phi\colon{{T}}\mathcal{G}\rightarrow{{T}}\mathcal{H}
restricted to the
s
-fibres.

Let us just recall Lie II theorem as we will need it later on.

Theorem 4.2

(Lie II)
.

Let
\mathcal{G}\rightrightarrows M
and
\mathcal{H}\rightrightarrows N
be Lie groupoids. Suppose that
\mathcal{G}
is source simply-connected and that
\phi\colon\textnormal{A}(\mathcal{G})\rightarrow\textnormal{A}(\mathcal{H})
is a Lie algebroid morphism between the associated Lie algebroids. Then,
\phi
integrates to a unique Lie groupoid morphisms
\Phi\colon\mathcal{G}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}
such that
\Phi^{\prime}=\phi
.

This generalisation of Lie II to the groupoid case was first proved by Mackenzie and Xu
[
35
]
. A simplified proof was obtained shortly after by Moerdijk and Mrčun
[
38
]
. Note that the assumption that the Lie groupoid
\mathcal{G}
is source simply-connected is essential.

4.2
Compatible group actions on Lie groupoids and algebroids

In our study of Jacobi and contact groupoids we will encounter Lie groupoids that have a compatible action of
\mathbb{R}^{\times}
upon them; compatibility to be defined shortly. However, as the basic theory of compatible group actions on Lie groupoids is independent of the actual Lie group, we discuss the general setting here focusing on what we will need later in this paper.

Definition 4.3
.

An action
\textnormal{h}\colon G\times\mathcal{G}\to\mathcal{G}
of a Lie group
G
on a Lie groupoid
\mathcal{G}\rightrightarrows M
is said to be
compatible with the groupoid structure
if
\textnormal{h}_{g}\colon\mathcal{G}\to\mathcal{G}
are groupoid isomorphisms for all
g\in G
. A principal
G
-bundle
\mathchar 28953\relax\colon\mathcal{G}\to\mathcal{G}_{0}
is a
principal bundle
G
-groupoid
(
G
-groupoid in short
) if the principal action of
G
on
\mathcal{G}
is compatible with the groupoid structure. Similarly, a
G
-action on a Lie algebroid
A
is
compatible
if the group acts by Lie algebroid isomorphisms, and we get a
G
-algebroid
if a principal
G
-action is compatible with the Lie algebroid structure.

Remark 4.4
.

The notion of a group object in the category of groupoids is quite an old notion, going back at least to the mid 1960s with the unpublished works of Verdier and Duskin. The earliest published work on such objects that we are aware of is that of Brown and Spencer
[
1
]
.

Remark 4.5
.

The reader should also be reminded of Mackenzie’s notion of a

[
32
,
33
]
, which is close to our notion of a G-groupoid, although Mackenzie, being interested in extensions of principal bundles, starts with a principal

-structure on the manifold

of units extended accordingly to a Lie groupoid
\mathcal{G}
. The other difference is that what we call a Lie groupoid is a
differentiable groupoid
in the sense of Mackenzie, and his

in
[

,
33
]
(or

in
[

]
) form much smaller class and are understood as particular

. However, the following observations are independent of these details and so are probably already known to Mackenzie.

1.

The action of

on

commutes with the source and target maps, thus projects onto a

-action on the manifold

. Moreover,

as an immersed submanifold of
\mathcal{G}
is invariant with respect to the

-action, and the projected and restricted actions coincide.

2.

As the action of

on
\mathcal{G}
is principal, it is also principal on the immersed submanifold

, so

inherits a structure of a principal

-bundle. It is important to note that

is G-invariant. In particular, the quotient manifold

exists.

3.

The reduced manifold
\mathcal{G}/G=\mathcal{G}_{0}
is a Lie groupoid

5
Kirillov and contact groupoids

5.1
Kirillov and Jacobi groupoids

Definition 5.1

A
Kirillov groupoid
is a
\mathbb{R}^{\times}
-groupoid equipped with a homogeneous multiplicative Poisson structure of degree
-1

{{\mathbb{R}}^{\times}}
-groupoid which has a Poisson groupoid structure of degree

. If the Poisson structure is non-degenerate, i.e., a symplectic structure, then we will speak of a

In a slightly different words, a Kirillov groupoid is a
\mathbb{R}^{\times}
-groupoid
(\mathcal{G},\textnormal{h})
equipped with a multiplicative Poisson structure
\mathchar 28675\relax
, such that
(\mathcal{G},\textnormal{h},\mathchar 28675\relax)
is a Kirillov manifold (by forgetting the groupoid structure).

Remark 5.2
.

A contact groupoid is a
homogeneous symplectic groupoid
, i.e., a symplectic groupoid
(\mathcal{G},\mathchar 28961\relax)
equipped additionally with a compatible principal
{{\mathbb{R}}^{\times}}
-bundle structure

such that
{{\mathbb{R}}^{\times}}
acts by groupoid isomorphisms and

1
with respect to this action,
\textnormal{h}_{t}^{*}\mathchar 28961\relax=t\,\mathchar 28961\relax

[

and, under different names, independently by Karasev
[

and Zakrzewski
[

\mathchar 28961\relax
. The notion of a homogeneous symplectic groupoid can be traced back to Libermann

5.2
Contact groupoids

Let us restrict attention to a contact groupoid
(\mathcal{G},\textnormal{h},\mathchar 28961\relax)
. We know,
via
Remark

, that the homogeneous symplectic structure
\mathchar 28961\relax
on the
{{\mathbb{R}}^{\times}}
-bundle
(\mathcal{G},\textnormal{h})
is equivalent to a contact structure
C=C(\mathcal{G},\textnormal{h},\mathchar 28961\relax)\subset{{T}}^{*}\mathcal{G}_{0}
, and further to a contact distribution
\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G},\textnormal{h},\mathchar 28961\relax)=C^{0}\subset{{T}}\mathcal{G}_{0}
. Such a distribution is called a
contact groupoid
by Dazord
[
11
,
12
]
(and
conformal contact groupoid
by the authors understanding contact groupoids as groupoids equipped with a globally defined contact form) if the contact distribution is closed with respect to the operation in the tangent groupoid
{{T}}\mathcal{G}_{0}
: it is invariant with respect to inversion and
\mathcal{D}\bullet\mathcal{D}\subset\mathcal{D}
, where
\bullet
is the (partial) multiplication in
{{T}}\mathcal{G}_{0}
. In other words,
\mathcal{D}

is a Lie subgroupoid of
{{T}}\mathcal{G}_{0}\rightrightarrows{{T}}M
. We refer to such Lie groupoids as
Dazord groupoids
, keeping the term contact groupoids to refer to our notion. However, the two notions are equivalent.

Theorem 5.8
.

Any contact groupoid
(\mathcal{G},\textnormal{h},\mathchar 28961\relax)
, with
\mathcal{G}/{{\mathbb{R}}^{\times}}=\mathcal{G}_{0}
, has a canonical and equivalent realisation as each of the following:

•

a contact subgroupoid
C^{\times}(\mathcal{G},\textnormal{h},\mathchar 28961\relax)
of the canonical contact groupoid
\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{G}_{0})
;

•

a Dazord groupoid
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G},\textnormal{h},\mathchar 28961\relax)
, being simultaneously a contact distribution and a subgroupoid of
{{T}}\mathcal{G}_{0}
.

Proof.

Let
\nabla
be the Euler vector field on
\mathcal{G}
. Since
\nabla
generates groupoid isomorphisms, it is a multiplicative vector field on
\mathcal{G}
, thus
\nabla\colon\mathcal{G}\!\to\!{{T}}\mathcal{G}
is a groupoid morphism (over
\nabla_{|M}\colon M\!\to\!{{T}}M
). Since the symplectic form
\mathchar 28961\relax
is multiplicative, it defines an isomorphism of groupoids
\mathchar 28961\relax^{\flat}\colon{{T}}\mathcal{G}\to{{T}}^{*}\mathcal{G}
. The one-form
\mathchar 28945\relax=\mathchar 28961\relax^{\flat}\circ\nabla\colon\mathcal{G}\to{{T}}^{*}\mathcal{G}
is a groupoid morphism, thus multiplicative,
\mathchar 28945\relax(yy^{\prime})=\mathchar 28945\relax(y)\star\mathchar 28945\relax(y^{\prime})
, where
\star
is the groupoid multiplication in the cotangent groupoid
{{T}}^{*}\mathcal{G}\rightrightarrows\textnormal{A}^{*}(\mathcal{G})
. We know that it takes values in basic covectors,
\mathchar 28945\relax(y)=\mathchar 28953\relax^{*}(\Psi(y))\in{{T}}^{*}_{y}\mathcal{G}
,
\Psi(y)\in{{T}}^{*}_{\mathchar 28953\relax(y)}\mathcal{G}_{0}
, so can be viewed as a map
\Psi\colon\mathcal{G}\to{{T}}^{*}\mathcal{G}_{0}
. Consequently, the range
C^{\times}(\mathcal{G},\textnormal{h},\mathchar 28961\relax)=\{\Psi(y)\colon y\in\mathcal{G}\}
of
\Psi
is a subgroupoid in the cotangent groupoid
{{T}}^{*}\mathcal{G}_{0}
. According to Theorem
3.7
,
\Psi
is also an embedding of contact structures (homogeneous symplectic
{{\mathbb{R}}^{\times}}
-bundles), so
\Psi
is just a realisation of

\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{G}_{0})

Note finally that the contact distribution
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{G},\textnormal{h},\mathchar 28961\relax)

are related by the polar condition: one annihilates the other in the canonical pairing between the tangent and the cotangent bundle. Since the partial multiplication in the cotangent and tangent groupoid are related by the condition

\displaystyle\mathchar 28946\relax_{g}\star\mathchar 28946\relax^{\prime}_{h}(X_{g}\bullet X^{\prime}_{h})=\mathchar 28946\relax_{g}(X_{g})+\mathchar 28946\relax^{\prime}_{h}(X^{\prime}_{h}),
