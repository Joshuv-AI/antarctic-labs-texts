The zero-noise limit of SDEs with L ∞ L^{\infty} drift
Ulrik Skre Fjordholm, Markus Musch, Andrey Pilipenko

Abstract
. We study the zero-noise limit for autonomous, one-dimensional ordinary differential equations with discontinuous right-hand sides. Although the deterministic equation might have infinitely many solutions, we show, under rather general conditions, that the sequence of stochastically perturbed solutions converges to a unique distribution on classical solutions of the deterministic equation. We provide several tools for computing this limit distribution.



1.
Introduction

Consider a scalar, autonomous ordinary differential equation (ODE) of the form

\begin{split}\frac{dX}{dt}(t)&=a(X(t))\qquad\text{for }t>0,\\
X(0)&=0\end{split}

(1.1)

where
a\colon{\mathbb{R}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
is Borel measurable. (The initial data
X(0)=0
can be translated to an arbitrary point
x_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}
, if needed.)
If the drift
a
is non-smooth then uniqueness of solutions might fail — this is the
Peano phenomenon
. To distinguish physically reasonable solutions from non-physical ones, we add stochastic noise to the equation, with the aim of letting the noise go to zero. Thus, we consider a stochastic differential equation

\begin{split}dX_{\varepsilon}(t)&=a(X_{\varepsilon}(t))dt+\varepsilon dW(t),\\
X_{\varepsilon}(0)&=0.\end{split}

(1.2)

where
W(t)
is a one-dimensional Brownian motion on a given probability space
(\Omega,{\mathcal{F}},{\mathbb{P}})
, and
\varepsilon>0
. By the Zvonkin–Veretennikov theorem
[
28
,
29
]
, equation (
1.2
) has a unique strong solution.

In this paper we consider the following problem:

Identify the limit
\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}X_{\varepsilon}
, and show that it satisfies (
1.1
).

Somewhat informally, the challenges are:

•

determining whether the sequence
\{X_{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon}
(or a subsequence) converges, and in what sense;

•

identifying the limit(s), either by a closed form expression or some defining property;

•

proving that the limit solves (
1.1
) by passing to the limit in the (possibly discontinuous) term
a(X_{\varepsilon})
.

The problem originated in the 1981 paper by Veretennikov
[
27
]
, and was treated extensively in the 1982 paper by Bafico and Baldi
[
2
]
. Only little work has been done on this problem since then, despite its great interest. The original work of Bafico and Baldi dealt with the Peano phenomenon for an autonomous ordinary differential equation. They considered continuous drifts which are zero at some point and are non-Lipschitz continuous on at least one side of the origin. In their paper they show that the
\varepsilon\to 0
limit of the probability measure that represents the solution of the stochastic equation is concentrated on at most two trajectories. Further, they compute explicitly some limit probability measures for specific drifts. Unfortunately, since the result of Bafico and Baldi relies on the direct computation of the solution of an elliptic PDE, it only works in one dimension. In one dimension this elliptic PDE reduces to a second-order boundary value problem for which an explicit solution can be computed. Therefore, there is little hope that this approach will also work in higher dimensions.

The only other work that is known to us dating back to the previous century is the paper by Mathieu from 1994
[
18
]
. In 2001 Grandinaru, Herrmann and Roynette published a paper
[
12
]
which showed some of the results of Bafico and Baldi using a large deviations approach. Herrmann did some more work on small-noise limits later on together with Tugaut
[
13
,
14
,
15
]
.

Yet another approach to Bafico and Baldi’s original problem was presented by Delarue and Flandoli in
[
8
]
. They apply a careful argument based on exit times. Noteworthy it also works in arbitrary dimension but with a very specific right-hand side, in contrast to the original assumption of a general continuous function; see also Trevisian
[
26
]
.
We also point out the recent paper by Delarue and Maurelli
[
9
]
, where multidimensional gradient dynamics with Hölder type coefficients was perturbed by a small Wiener noise.

The 2008 paper by Buckdahn, Ouknine and Quincampoix
[
7
]
shows that the the zero noise limit is concentrated on the set of all Filippov solutions of (
1.1
). Since this set is potentially very large, this result is of limited use to us.

Even less work was done for zero noise limits with respect to partial differential equations. To our best knowledge the only paper published so far is Attanasio and Flandoli’s note on the linear transport equation
[
1
]
.

A new approach was proposed by Pilipenko and Proske when the drift in (
1.1
) has Hölder-type asymptotics in a neighborhood of
x=0
and the perturbation is a self-similar noise
[
21
]
. They used space-time scaling and reduce a solution of the small-noise problem to a study of long time behaviour of a stochastic differential equation with a
fixed
noise. This approach can be generalized to multidimensional case and multiplicative Levy-noise perturbations
[
22
,
17
,
19
,
23
]
.

1.1.
Uniqueness of classical solutions

If the drift
a=a(x)
is continuous then the question of existence and uniqueness of solutions of (
1.1
) is well established. If
a
is continuous then it’s known since Peano that there always exists at least one solution (at least for small times). Binding
[
4
]
found that the solution is unique if and only if
a
satisfies a so-called Osgood condition at all zeros
x_{0}
of
a
:

\int_{x_{0}-\delta}^{x_{0}}\frac{1}{a(z)\wedge 0}\,dz=-\infty,\qquad\int_{x_{0}}^{x_{0}+\delta}\frac{1}{a(z)\vee 0}\,dz=+\infty

(1.3)

for all
\delta\in(0,\delta_{0})
for some
\delta_{0}>0
. (Here and in the remainder we denote
\alpha\wedge\beta\coloneqq\min(\alpha,\beta)
and
\alpha\vee\beta\coloneqq\max(\alpha,\beta)
.) The unique solution starting at
x
is then given by

X(t;x)=\begin{cases}x&\text{if }a(x)=0\\
A^{-1}(t)&\text{if }a(x)\neq 0\end{cases}

(1.4)

(at least for small
t
), where
A(y)\coloneqq\int_{x}^{y}1/a(z)\,dz
and
A^{-1}
is its inverse function.

If
a
is discontinuous — say,
a\in L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}})
— then the question of existence and uniqueness is much more delicate. The paper
[
10
]
gives necessary and sufficient conditions for the uniqueness of
Filippov solutions
of (
1.1
). We remark here that the extension to Filippov solutions might lead to non-uniqueness, even when the classical solution is unique. To see this, let
E\subset{\mathbb{R}}
be measure-dense, i.e. a set for which both
U\cap E
and
U\setminus E
have positive Lebesgue measure for any nonempty, open set
U\subset{\mathbb{R}}
(see
[
25
]
for the construction of such a set), and let
a=1+\mathbbm{1}_{E}
. Then (
1.4
) is the unique classical solution for any starting point
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
, whereas any function satisfying
\frac{d}{dt}X(t)\in[1,2]
for a.e.
t>0
will be a Filippov solution. We will show that even in cases such as this one, the stochastically perturbed solutions converge to the classical solution, and not just any Filippov solution, as was shown in
[
7
]
.

1.2.
Main result

We aim to prove that the distribution of solutions
X_{\varepsilon}
of (
1.2
) converges to a distribution concentrated on either a single solution of the deterministic equation (
1.1
), or two “extremal” solutions. Based on the discussion in the previous section, we can divide the argument into cases depending on whether
a
is positive, negative or changes sign in a neighbourhood, and in each case, whether an Osgood-type condition such as (
1.3
) holds. The case of negative drift is clearly analogous to a positive drift, so we will merely state the results for negative drift, without proof.

Under the sole assumption
a\in L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}})
, the sequence
\{X_{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon}
is weakly relatively compact in
C([0,T])
, for any
T>0
. (Indeed, by (
1.2
),
X_{\varepsilon}-\varepsilon W
is uniformly Lipschitz, and
\varepsilon W\overset{P}{\to}0
as
\varepsilon\to 0
. See e.g.
[
3
]
for the full argument.) Hence, the problems are to characterize the distributional limit of any convergent subsequence, to determine whether the entire sequence converges (i.e., to determine whether the limit is unique), and to determine whether the sense of convergence can be strengthened.

Without loss of generality we will assume that the process starts at
x=0
. If
a(0)=0
but
a
does
not
satisfy the Osgood condition (
1.3
) at
x=0
, then both

\psi_{-}
and
\psi_{+}
are classical solutions
of (
1.1
) (along with infinitely many other solutions), where

\psi_{\pm}(t)\coloneqq A_{\pm}^{-1}(t),\qquad\text{where }A_{\pm}(x)\coloneqq\int_{0}^{x}\frac{1}{a(z)}\,dz\text{ for }x\in{\mathbb{R}}_{\pm}.

(1.5)

Generally, the functions
\psi_{\pm}
are defined in a neighborhood of 0. We have assumed that
a
is bounded, so

\psi_{\pm}
cannot blow up in finite time, but they can reach singular points
R_{\pm}
where
A_{\pm}
blow up. If
t_{\pm}\in(0,\infty]
are the times when
\psi_{\pm}(t_{\pm})=R_{\pm}
then we set
\psi_{\pm}(t)\equiv R_{\pm}
for all
t\geqslant t_{\pm}
. We aim to prove that the distribution of
X_{\varepsilon}
converges to a distribution concentrated on the two solutions
\psi_{-},\ \psi_{+}
, and to determine the weighting of these two solutions.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
a\in L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}})
satisfy
a\geqslant 0
a.e. in
(-\delta_{0},\delta_{0})
for some
\delta_{0}>0
, and

\int_{0}^{\delta_{0}}\frac{1}{a(z)}dz<\infty.

(1.6)

Then, for any
T>0
,
X_{\varepsilon}
converges uniformly in probability to
\psi_{+}
:

\big\|X_{\varepsilon}-\psi_{+}\big\|_{C([0,T])}\overset{P}{\to}0\qquad\text{as }\varepsilon\to 0.

(1.7)

An analogous result holds for
negative
drifts, with obvious modifications.

The proof of Theorem
1.1
for strictly positive drifts
a
is given in Section
3
, while the general case is considered in Section
5
. The final theorem applies also to signed drifts:

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
a\in L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}})
satisfy

-\int_{\alpha}^{0}\frac{1}{a(z)\wedge 0}\,dz<\infty,\qquad\int_{0}^{\beta}\frac{1}{a(z)\vee 0}\,dz<\infty

(1.8)

for some
\alpha<0<\beta
(compare with (
1.3
)). Let
\{\varepsilon_{k}\}_{k}
be some sequence satisfying
\varepsilon_{k}>0
and
\lim_{k\to\infty}\varepsilon_{k}=0
, and define

p_{k}\coloneqq\frac{s_{\varepsilon_{k}}(0)-s_{\varepsilon_{k}}(\alpha)}{s_{\varepsilon_{k}}(\beta)-s_{\varepsilon_{k}}(\alpha)}\in[0,1],\qquad s_{\varepsilon}(r)\coloneqq\int_{0}^{r}\exp\Bigl(-\frac{2}{\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{0}^{z}a(u)\,du\Bigr)\,dz.

(1.9)

Then
\{P_{\varepsilon_{k}}\}_{k}
is weakly convergent if
\{p_{k}\}_{k}
converges. Defining
p\coloneqq\lim_{k}p_{k}
and
P\coloneqq\wlim_{k}P_{\nu_{k}}
, we have

P=(1-p)\delta_{\psi_{-}}+p\delta_{\psi_{+}}.

(1.10)

The proof is given in Section
4
, where we also provide tools for computing
p
.

2.
Technical results

In this section we list a few technical results. The first two results are comparison principles. In order to prove them we use approximations by SDEs with smooth coefficients and the classical results on comparison. Since we do not suppose that the drift is smooth or even continuous, the results are not standard.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
\{a_{n}\colon{\mathbb{R}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}\}_{n\geqslant 0}
be uniformly bounded
measurable functions such that
a_{n}\to a_{0}
pointwise a.e. as
n\to\infty
. Let
X_{n}
be a solution to the SDE

X_{n}(t)=x_{n}+\int_{0}^{t}a_{n}(X_{n}(s))ds+W(t),\qquad t\in[0,T].

Then
\{X_{n}\}_{n}
converges uniformly in probability:

\bigl\|X_{n}(t)-X_{0}(t)\bigr\|_{C([0,T])}\overset{P}{\to}0\qquad\text{as }n\to\infty.

For a proof, see e.g.
[
20
, Theorem 2.1]
.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
a_{1},a_{2}\colon{\mathbb{R}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
be locally bounded measurable functions satisfying
a_{1}\leqslant a_{2}
and let
x_{1}\leqslant x_{2}
. Let
X_{1},X_{2}
be solutions to the equations

\displaystyle X_{i}(t)=x_{i}+\int_{0}^{t}a_{i}(X_{i}(s))ds+W(t),\qquad i=1,2.

Then

X_{1}(t)\leqslant X_{2}(t)\qquad\forall\ t\geqslant 0

with probability 1.

The proof is given in Appendix
A
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
\{f_{n}\}_{n\geqslant 1}\subset C([0,T])
be a uniformly convergent sequence of non-random continuous functions and let
f_{0}\in C([0,T])
be a strictly increasing function. Set
\tau^{x}_{n}\coloneqq\inf\bigl\{t\geqslant 0:f_{n}(t)=x\bigr\}
for every
n\geqslant 0
, and assume that

\tau^{x}_{n}\to\tau^{x}_{0}\qquad\text{for every }x\in\big(f_{0}(0),f_{0}(T)\bigr)\cap{\mathbb{Q}}.

Then

f_{n}\to f_{0}\qquad\text{in }C([0,T])\text{ as }n\to\infty.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{T}\coloneqq f_{0}^{-1}({\mathbb{Q}})
, and note that this is a dense subset of
[0,T]
, since
f_{0}^{-1}
is continuous. Let
t\in\mathcal{T}
be arbitrary and let
x\coloneqq f_{0}(t)\in{\mathbb{Q}}
. By assumptions of the lemma we have
t=\tau_{0}^{x}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\tau_{n}^{x}.

Moreover, since
f_{n}(\tau^{x}_{n})=x
for sufficiently large
n
, we have

f_{0}(t)=x=\lim_{n\to\infty}f_{n}(\tau^{x}_{n})=\lim_{n\to\infty}f_{n}(t),

(2.1)

the last step following from the fact that
f_{n}
converges uniformly and
\tau^{x}_{n}\to\tau^{x}_{0}=t
as
n\to\infty
. Thus,
\{f_{n}\}_{n}
converges pointwise to
f_{0}
on a dense subset of
[0,T]
. But
\{f_{n}\}_{n}
is uniformly convergent by assumption, so necessarily
f_{n}\to f_{0}
uniformly.
∎

Corollary 2.4
.

Let
\{\xi_{n}\}_{n\geqslant 1}
be a sequence of continuous stochastic processes
\xi_{n}\colon[0,\infty)\to{\mathbb{R}}

that is locally uniformly convergent with probability
1
. Let
\xi_{0}
be a strictly increasing continuous process satisfying
\xi_{0}(0)=0
and
\lim_{t\to\infty}\xi_{0}(t)=\infty
. Set
\tau_{n}^{x}\coloneqq\inf\{t\geqslant 0:\xi_{n}(t)\geqslant x\}
and assume that

\tau_{n}^{x}\overset{P}{\to}\tau_{0}^{x}\qquad\text{for every }x\in[0,\infty)\cap{\mathbb{Q}}.

Then

\xi_{n}\to\xi_{0}\qquad\text{locally uniformly with probability }1.

Proof.

Enumerate the positive rational numbers as
{\mathbb{Q}}\cap(0,\infty)=\{x_{n}\}_{n}
.
Select a sequence
\{n^{1}_{k}\}_{k}
such that

\lim_{k\to\infty}\tau^{x_{1}}_{n^{1}_{k}}=\tau^{x_{1}}_{0}\qquad\text{${\mathbb{P}}$-a.s.}

Then select a sub-subsequence
\{n^{2}_{k}\}_{k}
of
\{n^{1}_{k}\}_{k}
such that

\lim_{k\to\infty}\tau^{x_{2}}_{n^{2}_{k}}=\tau^{x_{2}}_{0}\qquad\text{${\mathbb{P}}$-a.s.,}

and so on. Then

{\mathbb{P}}\Bigl(\forall\ j\in{\mathbb{N}}\quad\lim_{k\to\infty}\tau^{x_{j}}_{n^{k}_{k}}=\tau^{x_{j}}_{0}\Bigr)=1.

From Lemma
2.3
it follows that

{\mathbb{P}}\Bigl(\lim_{k\to\infty}\xi_{n^{k}_{k}}=\xi_{0}\quad\text{uniformly in }[0,T]\Bigr)=1

for any
T>0
. This yields the result.
∎

Denote

Define the hitting time of

as
\tau^{y}\coloneqq\inf\{t\geqslant 0:\xi(t)=y\}
.
Recall that
s
and
m
are well-defined up to constants,
and
s
is a non-degenerate

Ls=0,

(2.2)

3.
Positive drifts

This section is dedicated to the proof of Theorem
1.1
. In order to prove the theorem, we first prove the following:

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
a\in L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}})
and assume that there exist positive constants
\delta_{0},c_{-}>0

such that

a(x)\geqslant c_{-}\quad\text{for a.e. }x\in(-\delta_{0},\infty).

(3.1)

Then we have the uniform convergence in probability

\|X_{\varepsilon}-\psi_{+}\|_{C([0,T])}\overset{P}{\to}0\quad\text{as }\varepsilon\to 0\text{ for all }T>0.

(3.2)

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

The proof consists of these steps:

1.

Show weak relative compactness of
\{X_{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon}
.

2.

Show that
\bar{X}_{0}
is strictly increasing, where
\bar{X}_{0}
is a limit point of
\{X_{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon}
.

3.

Reduce to proving convergence of the hitting
times
\tau^{\varepsilon}\to\tau
, see Lemma
2.3
.

Step 1:

For any
T>0
the family
\{X_{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon\in(0,1]}
is weakly relatively compact in
C([0,T])

(see e.g.
[
3
]
).
Since
\psi_{+}
is non-random, the convergence statement (
3.2
) is equivalent to the weak convergence

X_{\varepsilon}\Rightarrow\psi_{+}\qquad\text{ in }C([0,T])\text{ as }\varepsilon\to 0.

for any
T>0
. To prove the latter, it suffices to verify that if
\{X_{\varepsilon_{k}}\}_{k}
is any convergent subsequence, then
\psi_{+}
is its limit.

Step 2:

Assume that
X_{\varepsilon_{k}}\Rightarrow\bar{X}_{0}
as
k\to\infty
. Since

X_{\varepsilon_{k}}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}a(X_{\varepsilon_{k}}(s))\,ds+\varepsilon_{k}W(t)\qquad\forall\ t\in[0,T],

and
\varepsilon_{k}W\overset{P}{\to}0
, Slutsky’s theorem implies that also

\int_{0}^{\cdot}a(X_{\varepsilon_{k}}(s))\,ds\Rightarrow\bar{X}_{0}\qquad\text{in }C([0,T]).

(3.3)

By Skorokhod’s representation theorem
[
3
, Theorem 1.6.7]
, we may assume that the convergence in (
3.3
) happens almost surely. Since
c_{-}\leqslant a\leqslant c_{+}
(for some
c_{+}>0
), we conclude that

c_{-}\leqslant\frac{\bar{X}_{0}(t_{2})-\bar{X}_{0}(t_{1})}{t_{2}-t_{1}}\leqslant c_{+}\qquad\forall\ t_{1},t_{2}\in[0,T],\text{ almost surely.}

In particular,
\bar{X}_{0}
is strictly increasing.

Step 3:

Notice that assumption (
3.1
) implies that
\lim_{t\to\infty}\psi_{+}(t)=+\infty.

Define

\tau_{\varepsilon}^{x}\coloneqq\inf\{t\geqslant 0\,:\,X_{\varepsilon}(t)=x\},\qquad\tau_{0}^{x}\coloneqq\inf\{t\geqslant 0\,:\,\psi_{+}(t)=x\}=A(x)

where
A(x)\coloneqq\int_{0}^{x}a(z)^{-1}\,dz
(cf. (
1.4
)).
By Corollary
2.4
it is enough to show convergence in probability of
\tau_{\varepsilon}
:

\tau_{\varepsilon}^{x}\overset{P}{\to}A(x)\qquad\text{as }\varepsilon\to 0\text{ for every }x\in{\mathbb{Q}}\cap[0,\infty).

(3.4)

In this section we consider the repulsive case and prove Theorem
1.2
. We also provide several tools for computing the zero noise probability distribution.

4.1.
Convergence in the repulsive case

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\alpha<0<\beta
, assume that
a\in L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}})
satisfies the “repulsive Osgood condition” (
1.8
), and define
p_{\varepsilon}
by

p_{\varepsilon}\coloneqq\frac{-s_{\varepsilon}(\alpha)}{s_{\varepsilon}(\beta)-s_{\varepsilon}(\alpha)},\qquad s_{\varepsilon}(r)\coloneqq\int_{0}^{r}e^{-B(z)/\varepsilon^{2}}\,dz,\qquad B(z)\coloneqq 2\int_{0}^{z}a(u)\,du.

(4.1)

Then

\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}{\mathbb{E}}^{0}\big(\tau_{\varepsilon}^{\alpha}\wedge\tau_{\varepsilon}^{\beta}\big)\leqslant\int_{\alpha}^{\beta}\frac{1}{|a(x)|}\,dx<\infty.

If
p_{\varepsilon_{k}}\to p
as
k\to\infty
, then

{\mathbb{E}}^{0}\big(\tau_{\varepsilon_{k}}^{\alpha}\wedge\tau_{\varepsilon_{k}}^{\beta}\big)\to{(1-p)}\int_{\alpha}^{0}\frac{-1}{a(z)}\,dz+{p}\int_{0}^{\beta}\frac{1}{a(z)}\,dz\qquad\text{as }k\to\infty.

Proof.

By (
2.2
), (
2.4
), and (
2.5
)
with
f=1
we can write

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}^{0}\big(\tau_{\varepsilon}^{\alpha}\wedge\tau_{\varepsilon}^{\beta}\big)=\int_{\alpha}^{0}\frac{(s_{\varepsilon}(y)-s_{\varepsilon}(\alpha))(s_{\varepsilon}(\beta)-s_{\varepsilon}(0))}{s_{\varepsilon}(\beta)-s_{\varepsilon}(\alpha)}\frac{2e^{B(y)/\varepsilon^{2}}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\,dy

\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{0}^{\beta}\frac{(s_{\varepsilon}(0)-s_{\varepsilon}(\alpha))(s_{\varepsilon}(\beta)-s_{\varepsilon}(y))}{s_{\varepsilon}(\beta)-s_{\varepsilon}(\alpha)}\frac{2e^{B(y)/\varepsilon^{2}}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\,dy

\displaystyle\quad={(1-p_{\varepsilon})}\int_{\alpha}^{0}(s_{\varepsilon}(y)-s_{\varepsilon}(\alpha))\frac{2e^{B(y)/\varepsilon^{2}}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\,dy+{p_{\varepsilon}}\int_{0}^{\beta}(s_{\varepsilon}(\beta)-s_{\varepsilon}(y))\frac{2e^{B(y)/\varepsilon^{2}}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\,dy

\displaystyle\quad={(1-p_{\varepsilon})\int_{\alpha}^{0}\int_{\alpha}^{y}\frac{2e^{(B(y)-B(z))/\varepsilon^{2}}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\,dz\,dy+p_{\varepsilon}\int_{0}^{\beta}\int_{y}^{\beta}\frac{2e^{(B(y)-B(z))/\varepsilon^{2}}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\,dz\,dy}

\displaystyle\quad=(1-p_{\varepsilon})\int_{\alpha}^{0}\int_{\alpha}^{y}\frac{2\exp\Bigl({\textstyle-\int_{z}^{y}\frac{2a(u)}{\varepsilon^{2}}du}\Bigr)}{\varepsilon^{2}}\,dz\,dy

\displaystyle\qquad+p_{\varepsilon}\int_{0}^{\beta}\int_{y}^{\beta}\frac{2\exp\Bigl({\textstyle-\int_{z}^{y}\frac{2a(u)}{\varepsilon^{2}}du}\Bigr)}{\varepsilon^{2}}\,dz\,dy

\displaystyle\quad=(1-p_{\varepsilon})\int_{\alpha}^{0}\int_{\alpha}^{y}\exp\Bigl({\textstyle-\int_{z}^{y}\frac{2a(u)}{\varepsilon^{2}}du}\Bigr)\frac{2a(z)}{\varepsilon^{2}}\frac{1}{a(z)}\,dz\,dy

\displaystyle\qquad+p_{\varepsilon}\int_{0}^{\beta}\int_{y}^{\beta}\exp\Bigl({\textstyle-\int_{z}^{y}\frac{2a(u)}{\varepsilon^{2}}du}\Bigr)\frac{2a(z)}{\varepsilon^{2}}\frac{1}{a(z)}\,dz\,dy.

Setting
f(z)=2\mathop{\rm sign}(z)a(z)
and
g(z)=\frac{1}{a(z)}
in Lemma
2.7
, we find that the above two integrals with
\varepsilon=\varepsilon_{k}
converge to

\int_{\alpha}^{0}\frac{-1}{a(z)}\,dz\qquad\text{and}\qquad\int_{0}^{\beta}\frac{1}{a(z)}\,dz

respectively, as
k\to\infty
. This concludes the proof.
∎

We can now prove the main theorem in the repulsive case.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Let
X_{\varepsilon_{k}^{\prime}}
be any weakly convergent subsequence of
\{X_{\varepsilon_{k}}\}_{k}
, and let
\tau_{\varepsilon_{k}^{\prime}}
and
\tau
be the hitting times of
X_{\varepsilon_{k}^{\prime}}
and its limit, respectively.
By Lemma
4.1
we have for any
\alpha<0<\beta

{\mathbb{E}}^{0}(\tau^{\alpha}\wedge\tau^{\beta})\leqslant\liminf_{k\to\infty}{\mathbb{E}}^{0}\bigl(\tau^{\alpha}_{\varepsilon_{k}}\wedge\tau^{\beta}_{\varepsilon_{k}}\bigr)={(1-p)A(\alpha)+pA(\beta)}.

Consequently,
{\mathbb{P}}^{0}\bigl(\tau^{\alpha}\wedge\tau^{\beta}=\infty\bigr)=0
, so
{\mathbb{P}}^{0}(\tau^{\alpha}<\tau^{\beta})=\lim_{k\to\infty}{\mathbb{P}}^{0}(\tau^{\alpha}_{\varepsilon_{k}^{\prime}}<\tau^{\beta}_{\varepsilon_{k}^{\prime}})={1-p}
and
{\mathbb{P}}^{0}(\tau^{\alpha}>\tau^{\beta})={p}
.
Using Theorem
1.1
and the strong Markov property, the probability of convergence once the process escapes
(\alpha,\beta)
at
x=\beta
is one:

\lim_{k\to\infty}{\mathbb{P}}^{0}\Bigl(\bigl\|X_{\varepsilon_{k}^{\prime}}(\cdot-\tau_{\beta})-\psi_{+}(\cdot-A(\beta))\bigr\|_{C([0,T])}>\delta\bigm|\tau^{\alpha}>\tau^{\beta}\Bigr)=1,

for any sufficiently small
\delta>0
, and likewise for those paths escaping at
x=\alpha
. Passing
\alpha,\beta\to 0
yields

\displaystyle\lim_{\delta\to 0}\lim_{k\to\infty}{\mathbb{P}}^{0}\Bigl(\|X_{\varepsilon_{k}^{\prime}}-\psi_{-}\|_{C([0,T])}>\delta\Bigr)={1-p},

\displaystyle\lim_{\delta\to 0}\lim_{k\to\infty}{\mathbb{P}}^{0}\Bigl(\|X_{\varepsilon_{k}^{\prime}}-\psi_{+}\|_{C([0,T])}>\delta\Bigr)={p}.

Since this is true for any weakly convergent subsequence
\varepsilon_{k}^{\prime}
, and the limit is unique, the entire sequence
\varepsilon_{k}
must converge.
∎

4.2.
Probabilities in the repulsive case

Theorem
1.2
gives a concrete condition for convergence of the sequence
of perturbed solutions, as well as a characterization of the limit distribution. In this section we give
an explicit expression for the probabilities in the limit distribution, and an equivalent condition for
convergence.

Consider the integral

B(x)\coloneqq\int_{0}^{x}a(y)\,dy

and denote
B_{\pm}=B\bigr|_{{\mathbb{R}}_{\pm}}
.
Select any
\alpha>0,\beta>0
such that the function
\mu\colon[0,\beta)\to(\alpha,0]

defined by
\mu=B_{-}^{-1}\circ B_{+}
is well-defined — that is,

B_{+}(x)=B_{-}(\mu(x)),\quad\forall\ x\in[0,\beta).

Clearly,
B_{\pm}
are Lipschitz continuous. Since
a
is strictly positive (negative) for
x>0
(
x<0
), the inverses of
B_{\pm}
are absolutely continuous (see e.g.
[
6
, Exercise 5.8.52]
), so
\mu
is also absolutely continuous.
We now rewrite the probability of choosing the left/right extremal solutions
X^{\pm}
in terms of
\mu
.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
a\in L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}})
satisfy (
1.8
)
and let
\mu\colon[0,\beta)\to(\alpha,0]
be as above.
Then
\{p_{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon}
converges if either the derivative
\mu^{\prime}(0)
exists, or if
\mu^{\prime}(0)=-\infty
. In either case, we have

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}p_{\varepsilon}={\frac{-\mu^{\prime}(0)}{1-\mu^{\prime}(0)}}.

(4.2a)

Moreover, the derivative
\mu^{\prime}(0)
exists if and only if the limit
\lim_{u\downarrow 0}\frac{B_{-}^{-1}(u)}{B_{+}^{-1}(u)}
exists, and we have the equality:

\mu^{\prime}(0)=\lim_{u\downarrow 0}\frac{B_{-}^{-1}(u)}{B_{+}^{-1}(u)}.

(4.2b)

To prove the theorem we will need the following lemmas:

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
\alpha<0<\beta
. Define
p_{\varepsilon}
as in (
4.1
) and
p_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}
similarly, where

\alpha,\beta
are exchanged with any

\alpha^{\prime}<0<\beta^{\prime}.

Then
\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}p_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}/p_{\varepsilon}=1
. In particular,

p_{\varepsilon_{k}}
converges to some
p
as
k\to\infty
if and only if
p_{\varepsilon_{k}}^{\prime}
converges to
p
.

The proof follows from the following observation: Since
B
is strictly increasing,
then for any positive
r_{1}<r_{2}
or negative
r_{1}>r_{2}
,

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{\int_{r_{1}}^{r_{2}}e^{-B(z)/\varepsilon^{2}}\,dz}{\int_{0}^{r_{1}}e^{-B(z)/\varepsilon^{2}}\,dz}=0.

Next, we prove a technical lemma:

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
0<a\in L^{\infty}([0,\beta])
and
f\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}})
, and for
\varepsilon>0
and
x\in[0,\beta)
define

\displaystyle B(x)=2\int_{0}^{x}a(y)\,dy,\qquad\nu_{\varepsilon}(x)=e^{-B(x)/\varepsilon^{2}}\mathbbm{1}_{[0,\beta]}(x),

\displaystyle\bar{\nu}_{\varepsilon}=\int_{0}^{\beta}\nu_{\varepsilon}(y)\,dy,\qquad f_{\varepsilon}(x)=\frac{1}{\bar{\nu}_{\varepsilon}}\int_{0}^{\beta}f(x+y)\nu_{\varepsilon}(y)\,dy.

Then
f_{\varepsilon}(x)\to f(x)
as
\varepsilon\to 0
if and only if
x
is a Lebesgue point of
f
.

Proof.

Let
x\in[0,\beta)
. For
s\in(0,\beta-x)
, let

F(s)=\int_{0}^{s}|f(x+y)-f(x)|\,dy,\qquad C_{s}=\sup_{y\in(0,s)}\tfrac{F(y)}{y}.

Then
C_{s}\to 0
as
s\to 0
if and only if
x
is a Lebesgue point. We estimate

\displaystyle|f_{\varepsilon}(x)-f(x)|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\bar{\nu}_{\varepsilon}}\biggl|\int_{0}^{\beta}(f(x+y)-f(x))\nu_{\varepsilon}(y)\,dy\biggr|

\displaystyle\leqslant\underbrace{\frac{1}{\bar{\nu}_{\varepsilon}}\int_{0}^{s}|f(x+y)-f(x)|\nu_{\varepsilon}(y)\,dy}_{=\,I_{1}}+\underbrace{\frac{1}{\bar{\nu}_{\varepsilon}}\int_{s}^{\beta}|f(x+y)-f(x)|\nu_{\varepsilon}(y)\,dy}_{=\,I_{2}}.

For the first term we integrate by parts several times to get

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle=F(s)\frac{\nu_{\varepsilon}(s)}{\bar{\nu}_{\varepsilon}}-\frac{1}{\bar{\nu}_{\varepsilon}}\int_{0}^{s}F(y)\nu_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(y)\,dy\leqslant F(s)\frac{\nu_{\varepsilon}(s)}{\bar{\nu}_{\varepsilon}}-\frac{C_{s}}{\bar{\nu}_{\varepsilon}}\int_{0}^{s}y\nu_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(y)\,dy

\displaystyle=F(s)\frac{\nu_{\varepsilon}(s)}{\bar{\nu}_{\varepsilon}}-\frac{C_{s}}{\bar{\nu}_{\varepsilon}}s\nu_{\varepsilon}(s)+\frac{C_{s}}{\bar{\nu}_{\varepsilon}}\int_{0}^{s}\nu_{\varepsilon}(y)\,dy

\displaystyle\leqslant F(s)\frac{\nu_{\varepsilon}(s)}{\bar{\nu}_{\varepsilon}}+\frac{C_{s}}{\bar{\nu}_{\varepsilon}}\int_{0}^{\beta}\nu_{\varepsilon}(y)\,dy

\displaystyle=F(s)\frac{\nu_{\varepsilon}(s)}{\bar{\nu}_{\varepsilon}}+C_{s}.

For the second term we estimate

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\|f\|_{L^{1}}\frac{\nu_{\varepsilon}(s)}{\bar{\nu}_{\varepsilon}}.

If we can find
s=s_{\varepsilon}
such that both
s_{\varepsilon}\to 0
and
\frac{\nu_{\varepsilon}(s_{\varepsilon})}{\bar{\nu}_{\varepsilon}}\to 0

as
\varepsilon\to 0
, then both
I_{1}
and
I_{2}
vanish in the
\varepsilon\to 0
limit,
and we can conclude the result. Below we explain the existence of such a choice.

Since
B
is increasing and Lipschitz continuous, with
B(0)=0
and
\|B\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}\leqslant 2\|a\|_{L^{\infty}}<\infty
, there is some
\kappa<s
satisfying
B(\kappa)=\tfrac{1}{2}B(s)
, and
\kappa\geqslant\frac{1}{2\|B\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}}B(s)
. Moreover, since
\nu_{\varepsilon}
is decreasing we have

\bar{\nu}_{\varepsilon}=\int_{0}^{\beta}\nu_{\varepsilon}(y)\,dy\geqslant\kappa\nu_{\varepsilon}(\kappa)=\kappa e^{-B(\kappa)/\varepsilon^{2}}=\kappa e^{-B(s)/(2\varepsilon^{2})},

so

\frac{\nu_{\varepsilon}(s)}{\bar{\nu}_{\varepsilon}}\leqslant\frac{1}{\kappa}e^{-B(s)/(2\varepsilon^{2})}\leqslant 2\|B\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}\frac{e^{-B(s)/(2\varepsilon^{2})}}{B(s)}.

Now choose
s=s_{\varepsilon}
such that
B(s_{\varepsilon})=\varepsilon
. (Such a number exists for sufficiently small
\varepsilon>0
.) Then
s_{\varepsilon}\to 0
as
\varepsilon\to 0
, and

\frac{\nu_{\varepsilon}(s)}{\bar{\nu}_{\varepsilon}}\leqslant 2\|B\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}\frac{e^{-1/(2\varepsilon)}}{\varepsilon}\to 0

as
\varepsilon\to 0
. This finishes the proof.
∎

Proof of Theorem
4.2
.

We have

p_{\varepsilon}=\frac{{-s_{\varepsilon}(\alpha)}}{s_{\varepsilon}(\beta)-s_{\varepsilon}(\alpha)}={\frac{-\frac{s_{\varepsilon}(\alpha)}{s_{\varepsilon}(\beta)}}{1-\frac{s_{\varepsilon}(\alpha)}{s_{\varepsilon}(\beta)}}}.

By Lemma
4.3
we may assume
\mu(\beta)=\alpha
, so

\displaystyle s_{\varepsilon}(\alpha)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\alpha}e^{-B(\mu^{-1}(x))/\varepsilon^{2}}\,dx=\int_{0}^{\beta}e^{-B(x)/\varepsilon^{2}}\mu^{\prime}(x)\,dx.

Thus,

\frac{s_{\varepsilon}(\alpha)}{s_{\varepsilon}(\beta)}=\frac{1}{\bar{\nu}_{\varepsilon}}\int_{0}^{\beta}\nu_{\varepsilon}(x)\mu^{\prime}(x)\,dx

where

\nu_{\varepsilon}(x)=e^{-B(x)/\varepsilon^{2}},\qquad\bar{\nu}_{\varepsilon}=\int_{0}^{\beta}e^{-B(z)/\varepsilon^{2}}\,dz.

From Lemma
4.4
with
f(x)\coloneqq\mu^{\prime}(x)
it now follows that
p_{\varepsilon}
converges if either
0
is a Lebesgue point for
\mu^{\prime}
, or
\lim_{x\to 0}\mu^{\prime}(x)={-\infty}
.
In the former case, we notice that
0
is a Lebesgue point for
\mu^{\prime}
if
the following limit exists:

{\lim_{h\downarrow 0}}\frac{\int_{0}^{h}\mu^{\prime}(z)\,dz}{h}={\lim_{h\downarrow 0}}\frac{\mu(h)-\mu(0)}{h}.

The right hand side of the last equation is the usual definition of the derivative.

To prove (
4.2b
) notice that

\lim_{h\downarrow 0}\frac{\mu(h)-\mu(0)}{h}=\lim_{h\downarrow 0}\frac{\mu(h)}{h}=\lim_{h\downarrow 0}\frac{B_{-}^{-1}\circ B_{+}(h)}{h}=\lim_{u\downarrow 0}\frac{B_{-}^{-1}(u)}{B_{+}^{-1}(u)}.

∎

4.3.
Repulsive, regularly varying drifts

Although Theorem
1.2
provides an explicit expression (
4.2
) of the limit probabilities, the limit (
4.2b
) might be difficult to evaluate in practice. It is clearly easier to study existence of the limits

\lim_{x\downarrow 0}\frac{a(-x)}{a(x)}

(4.3)

or

\lim_{x\downarrow 0}\frac{B(-x)}{B(x)}

(4.4)

than that for the inverse functions in (
4.2
). We will show that the limit in (
4.2
) can easily be calculated using (
4.3
) or (
4.4
) if
a
or
B
are regularly varying at
0
.

Recall that a positive, measurable function
f\colon[0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
is
regularly varying
of index
\gamma
at
+\infty
if
\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{f(\lambda x)}{f(x)}=\lambda^{\rho}
for all
\lambda>0
. It is regularly varying of index
\rho
at
0
if the function
x\mapsto f(1/x)
is a regularly varying function of index
-\rho
at
+\infty
. The set of regularly varying functions of index
\rho

(at
+\infty
) is denoted by
R_{\rho}.
It is well known that if
f\in R_{\rho}
, then

f(x)=x^{\rho}\ell(x)
for some slowly varying function
\ell
, i.e. some
\ell\colon[0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
for which
\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{\ell(\lambda x)}{\ell(x)}=1
for all
\lambda>0
.

We first consider the case when
B
is regularly varying, and then the case when
a
is. Note that the latter implies the former, but not vice versa.

Proposition 4.5
.

Assume that the functions
x\mapsto B_{\pm}(\pm x)
are regularly varying of index

\rho>0
at 0, and that the limit
c\coloneqq\lim_{x\downarrow 0}B_{-}(-x)/B_{+}(x)
exists
(or equals
\infty
). Then
\{p_{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon>0}
converges, and

p\coloneqq\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}p_{\varepsilon}=\frac{{c^{-1/\rho}}}{1+c^{-1/\rho}}.

(4.5)

If the functions
x\mapsto B_{\pm}(\pm x)
are regularly varying of different indices
\rho_{\pm},

then

p\coloneqq\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}p_{\varepsilon}=\begin{cases}1&\rho_{+}<\rho_{-}\\
0&\rho_{+}>\rho_{-}.\end{cases}

Proof.

It follows from
[
5
, Exercise 14, p. 190]
that if
f_{1},f_{2}\colon(0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
are non-decreasing, regularly varying functions at
0
of index
\rho>0
, then

\lim_{x\to 0}\frac{f_{1}(x)}{f_{2}(x)}=1\qquad\text{if and only if}\qquad\lim_{x\to 0}\frac{f_{1}^{-1}(x)}{f_{2}^{-1}(x)}=1,

where
f_{1}^{-1},f_{2}^{-1}
are inverse functions. Write now
f_{1}(x)=B_{-}(-x)
,
f_{2}(x)=cB_{+}(x)
. Then

\lim_{x\to 0}\frac{f_{1}(x)}{f_{2}(x)}=1.

(4.6)

The inverse function for
x\mapsto cB_{+}(x)
is
x\mapsto B_{+}^{-1}(x/c),
and
B_{+}^{-1}
is
regularly varying of index
1/\rho
(see
[
5
, Theorem 1.5.12]
), so

B_{+}^{-1}(x/c)=(x/c)^{1/\rho}\ell_{1}(x/c)\sim(x/c)^{1/\rho}\ell_{1}(x)=c^{-1/\rho}B_{+}^{-1}(x)\qquad\text{as }x\to 0

(where equivalence is meant in the sense of slowly
varying functions). Hence, (
4.6
) yields

\lim_{x\to 0}\frac{B_{-}^{-1}(x)}{B_{+}^{-1}(x)}=c^{-1/\rho}.

(4.7)

The same computation can be easily performed in reverse, so
(
4.6
) and (
4.7
) are equivalent, and
the result now follows from Theorem
4.2
if
B_{\pm}
are of the same index.

If
x\mapsto B_{\pm}(\pm x)
are regularly varying of different indices
{\rho_{\pm}},
then
the inverse functions are regularly varying functions of indices
\frac{1}{\rho_{\pm}},
and the result is obvious.
∎

Proposition 4.6
.

Assume that both
x\mapsto a(\pm x)
(for
x\geqslant 0
) are regularly varying at
0
with index
\rho>0
, and that the limit
c\coloneqq\lim_{x\downarrow 0}\frac{-a(-x)}{a(x)}
exists. Then
\{p_{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon>0}
converges, and

p\coloneqq\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}p_{\varepsilon}=\frac{{c^{-1/(1+\rho)}}}{1+c^{-1/(1+\rho)}}.

If the functions
x\mapsto a(\pm x)
are regularly varying of different indices
\rho_{\pm},

then

p\coloneqq\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}p_{\varepsilon}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }\rho_{+}<\rho_{-}\\
0&\text{if }\rho_{+}>\rho_{-}.\end{cases}

Proof.

It follows from the Karamata theorem, see
[
5
, Theorem 1.6.1]
, that for
x>0
,

\displaystyle B(x)

\displaystyle=2\int_{0}^{x}a(y)\,dy=2\int_{1/x}^{\infty}a(1/z)z^{-2}\,dz=2\int_{1/x}^{\infty}\ell(z)z^{-2-\rho}\,dz

\displaystyle\sim\frac{2\ell(1/x)x^{1+\rho}}{1+\rho}\sim\frac{xa(x)}{1+\rho}

as
x\to 0
, and likewise for
x<0
. Thus,
x\mapsto B(\pm x)
are regularly varying of index
1+\rho
. Letting

c\coloneqq\lim_{x\downarrow 0}\frac{-a(-x)}{a(x)},

we can now apply Proposition
4.5
with
1+\rho
in place of
\rho
and get the desired result.

The case when
a(\pm x)
are regularly varying with different indices can be considered similarly,
cf. Proposition
4.5
.
∎

Finally, we provide a result which simplifies the computation of the limit distribution for severely oscillating drifts.

Proposition 4.7
.

Let
a\colon{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
satisfy
xa(x)\geqslant 0
for all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
, and assume that it is of the form

a(x)=b(x)+|x|^{\gamma}g(\tfrac{1}{x}),

where
\gamma>0
,
b
is regularly varying at
0
of order
\rho<\gamma+1
, and
g\in L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}})
is such that its antiderivative
G(x)=\int_{0}^{x}g(y)\,dy
also lies in
L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}})
. Assume also that the limit
c\coloneqq\lim_{x\downarrow 0}\frac{-b(-x)}{b(x)}
exists. Then
\{p_{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon>0}
converges, and

p\coloneqq\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}p_{\varepsilon}=\frac{{c^{1/(1+\rho)}}}{1+c^{1/(1+\rho)}}.

Proof.

We claim first that

\int_{0}^{x}y^{\gamma}g(\tfrac{1}{y})\,dy=O(x^{1+\gamma})=o(x^{\rho})\qquad\text{as }x\downarrow 0.

Indeed,

\displaystyle\biggl|\int_{0}^{x}y^{\gamma}g(\tfrac{1}{y})\,dy\biggr|

\displaystyle=\biggl|\int_{1/x}^{\infty}z^{-2-\gamma}g(z)\,dz\biggr|

\displaystyle=\biggl|-x^{2+\gamma}G(\tfrac{1}{x})-(\rho+2)\int_{1/x}^{\infty}z^{-3-\gamma}G(z)\,dz\biggr|

\displaystyle\leqslant x^{2+\gamma}\|G\|_{L^{\infty}}+(\rho+2)\|G\|_{L^{\infty}}\int_{1/x}^{\infty}z^{-3-\gamma}\,dz

\displaystyle=\Bigl(1+\frac{\rho+2}{\gamma+2}\Bigr)x^{2+\gamma}\|G\|_{L^{\infty}}.

It follows that the antiderivative
B(x)=\int_{0}^{x}a(y)\,dy
equals a regularly
varying function of order
1+\rho
, plus a term of order
o(x^{1+\rho})
. Following the
same procedure as in the proof of Proposition
4.6
yields the desired result.
∎

5.
Proof of Theorem
1.1

We have already proven the Theorem in Section
3
if
a>0
a.e. in a small neighborhood of 0.
We will only prove the result for
a
such that

a\geqslant 0
for negative
x
and
\int_{0}^{R}\frac{dy}{a(y)\vee 0}<\infty
for all
R>0
. The general
case, i.e.,
a(x)\geqslant 0
for a.e.
x\in(-\delta_{0},0)
and
\int_{0}^{\delta_{0}}\frac{dy}{a(y)}<0
, is considered similarly to the reasoning in Section
3
.

It follows from the comparison theorem that for any
x>0
we have the inequality

X_{\varepsilon}(t)\leqslant X_{\varepsilon}^{x}(t)
for
t\geqslant 0
with probability 1,
where
X_{\varepsilon}^{x}
is a solution of (
1.2
) that started from
x
,
X_{\varepsilon}^{x}(0)=x.

Since
a
is a.e. positive on
(0,x)
, we have already seen that
\{X_{\varepsilon}^{x}(t)\}_{\varepsilon}
converges to

\psi_{+}(\psi_{+}^{-1}(x)+t)
as
t\to\infty.
Thus, any limit point of
\{X_{\varepsilon}(t)\}_{\varepsilon}
must be less than or equal to
\psi_{+}(\psi_{+}^{-1}(x)+t)
for any
x>0
, almost surely.
Therefore, any limit point of
\{X_{\varepsilon}(t)\}_{\varepsilon}
does not exceed
\psi_{+}(t).

Define the function

a_{n}(x):=\begin{cases}a(x)&\text{if }x\geqslant 0\\
-\tfrac{1}{n}a(-\tfrac{x}{n})&\text{if }x<0,\end{cases}

and denote the corresponding solutions to stochastic differential equations by
X_{n,\varepsilon}(t).

Let us apply Theorem
4.2
to the sequence
\{X_{n,\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon}.

Calculate the limit (
4.2b
):

B_{n,+}(x)=\int_{0}^{x}a(y)dy,\qquad B_{n,-}(x)=\int_{0}^{x}a(y/n)dy/n=B_{+}(x/n).

Thus,

(B_{n,-})^{-1}(u)=n(B_{n,+})^{-1}(u),\qquad\lim_{u\downarrow 0}\frac{(B_{n,-})^{-1}(u)}{(B_{n,+})^{-1}(u)}=n,

and we get convergence

P_{X_{n,\varepsilon}}\Rightarrow\frac{1}{n+1}\delta_{-n\psi_{+}(n^{-2}t)}+\frac{n}{n+1}\delta_{\psi_{+}(t)}\qquad\text{as }\varepsilon\to 0.

By the comparison theorem we have the inequality
X_{n,\varepsilon}(t)\leqslant X_{\varepsilon}(t),t\geqslant 0
with probability 1. Therefore, any limit point of
\{X_{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon}
equals
\psi_{+}
with probability at least
\frac{n}{n+1}.

We conclude that the limit of
\{X_{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon}
exists and equals
\psi_{+}
almost surely.
The limit is non-random, so we have convergence in probability, as in (
1.7
). This finishes the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

6.
Examples

Example 6.1
.

For some fixed
\rho\in(0,1)
we consider the function

a(x)\coloneqq\mathop{\rm sign}(x)|x|^{\rho}\bigl(1+\tfrac{1}{2}\phi\bigl(\tfrac{1}{x}\bigr)\bigr)\qquad\text{where }\phi(y)\coloneqq\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\mathbbm{1}_{[2n-1,2n)}-\mathbbm{1}_{[2n,2n+1)},

defined for all
x\neq 0
. Using Proposition
4.7
with
b(x)=\mathop{\rm sign}(x)|x|^{\rho}
,
\gamma=\rho
and
g(y)=\tfrac{1}{2}\mathop{\rm sign}(y)\phi(y)
, we get
c=1
, and that
p_{\varepsilon}\to\frac{1}{2}
. We also see that
a
satisfies the repulsive condition (
1.8
) of Theorem
1.2
, so we conclude that

P_{\varepsilon}\Rightarrow\tfrac{1}{2}\delta_{\psi_{-}}+\tfrac{1}{2}\delta_{\psi_{+}}\qquad\text{as }\varepsilon\to 0

where
\psi_{\pm}
are the maximal classical solutions.

Example 6.2
.

Let
a(x)=x^{\beta}
,
x>0,
where
\beta\in(0,1).
We claim that we can continuously extend
a
to the set
(-\infty,0]
such that

(a)

-a(-x)\leqslant a(x)<0
for all
x<0
;

(b)

\int_{-1}^{0}\frac{1}{a(x)}dx=-\infty,
i.e., the Osgood condition is not satisfied to the left of zero;

(c)

P_{X_{\varepsilon}}\Rightarrow\tfrac{1}{2}\delta_{\psi_{+}}+\tfrac{1}{2}\delta_{0}
as
\varepsilon\to 0,
i.e., the limit process with probability
\tfrac{1}{2}
moves like the maximal positive solution
\psi_{+}(t)=((1-\beta)t)^{\frac{1}{1-\beta}},t\geqslant 0

and stays at 0 forever with probability
\tfrac{1}{2}
too.

This example is not covered by the theory in the previous sections, and should therefore be read as a demonstration of the complex behaviours that can occur in the zero noise limit. Note also that the zero-noise limit is
not
only concentrated on the maximal solution
\psi_{+}
, but also on the trivial solution
\psi_{-}\equiv 0
.

Before we construct the extension, let us provide some simple preliminary analysis.
If a function
a\colon{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
satisfies the linear growth condition, then the family
\{X_{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon}

is weakly relatively compact. If additionally the function
a
is continuous, then
any limit point of
\{X_{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon}
satisfies (
1.1
). Both conditions
(a) and (b) yield that any solution to (
1.1
), and hence any limit point of

\{X_{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon}
has a form

X_{0}(t)=\begin{cases}0,&t\leqslant\tau\\
((1-\beta)(t-\tau))^{\frac{1}{1-\beta}},&t>\tau,\end{cases}

(6.1)

where
\tau\in[0,\infty].
Our aim is to find an extension of
a
such that

{\mathbb{P}}(\tau=0)={\mathbb{P}}(\tau=\infty)=\tfrac{1}{2}

(6.2)

for any limit point

X_{0}
having representation
(
6.1
).

Let
A=\cup_{k\geqslant 1}[-\frac{1}{2^{k}},-\frac{1}{2^{k}}+\frac{1}{4^{k}}].
Set

\displaystyle\tilde{a}(x)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathop{\rm sign}(x)a(|x|)\mathbbm{1}_{x\notin A}=\begin{cases}x^{\beta},&x>0\\
-|x|^{\beta},&x\leqslant 0,\ x\notin A\\
0,&x\leqslant 0,\ x\in A,\end{cases}

\displaystyle\bar{a}(x)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathop{\rm sign}(x)a(|x|)=\mathop{\rm sign}(x)|x|^{\beta}=\begin{cases}x^{\beta},&x>0\\
-|x|^{\beta},&x\leqslant 0.\end{cases}

Define
a
on
(-\infty,0)
to be any negative, continuous function such that
\int_{-\delta}^{0}\frac{1}{a(x)}dx=-\infty
for any
\delta>0
, and

\bar{a}(x)\leqslant a(x)\leqslant\tilde{a}(x)\text{ for all }x\in(-\infty,0).

It is clear that there exists a function
a
satisfying these properties.
Introduce the transformed process

Y_{\varepsilon}(t)\coloneqq\varepsilon^{\frac{-2}{1+\beta}}X_{\varepsilon}\bigl(\varepsilon^{\frac{2(1-\beta)}{1+\beta}}t\bigr).

It can be seen (see
[
22
]
for a more general case) that

dY_{\varepsilon}(t)=a_{\varepsilon}(Y_{\varepsilon}(t))dt+dw_{\varepsilon}(t),

(6.3)

where
w_{\varepsilon}(t)=\varepsilon^{\frac{-(1-\beta)}{1+\beta}}w\bigl(\varepsilon^{\frac{2(1-\beta)}{1+\beta}}t\bigr)
is a Wiener process, and

a_{\varepsilon}(y)=\varepsilon^{\frac{-2\beta}{1+\beta}}a\bigl(\varepsilon^{\frac{2}{1+\beta}}y\bigr).

(6.4)

Notice that
a_{\varepsilon}(y)=a(y)
for all
y\in(0,\infty)
and for all
y<0
such that
\varepsilon^{\frac{2}{1+\beta}}y\notin A
. For all other
y<0
we have the inequality
-|y|^{\beta}\leqslant a(y)<0
, by the choice of the function
a
.
We have convergence
a_{\varepsilon}(y)\to\bar{a}(y)=\mathop{\rm sign}(y)|y|^{\beta}
in Lebesgue measure on any interval
y\in[-R,R]
.
Observe also that

\int_{0}^{x}a_{\varepsilon}(y)dy\geqslant\int_{0}^{x}\hat{a}(y)dy\qquad\forall\varepsilon>0,\ \forall x<0

where

\hat{a}(x)=\begin{cases}0,\ &x\in[-\tfrac{3}{2}\cdot 2^{n},-2^{n}]\text{ for some }n\in{\mathbb{Z}}\\
-|x|^{\beta}&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

In particular, the last estimate yields

\sup_{\varepsilon\in(0,1]}\lim_{R\to+\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{-R}\exp\biggl(-2\int_{0}^{x}a_{\varepsilon}(y)\,dy\biggr)dx=0.

Set

\sigma^{X}_{\varepsilon}(p)\coloneqq\inf\{t\geqslant 0:X_{\varepsilon}(t)=p\},

\sigma^{Y}_{\varepsilon}(p)\coloneqq\inf\{t\geqslant 0:Y_{\varepsilon}(t)=p\}.

The observations above and formulas of Theorem
2.5
yield that for any
R>0
, and for any sequences
\{R^{\pm}_{\varepsilon}\}
such that
\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}R^{\pm}_{\varepsilon}=\pm\infty
we have

\displaystyle\hskip-20.00003pt\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}{\mathbb{P}}\Bigl(\sigma^{Y}_{\varepsilon}(R)<\sigma^{Y}_{\varepsilon}(-R)\mid Y_{\varepsilon}(0)=0\Bigr)=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}{\mathbb{P}}\Big(\sigma^{Y}_{\varepsilon}(-R)<\sigma^{Y}_{\varepsilon}(R)\mid Y_{\varepsilon}(0)=0\Big)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}{\mathbb{P}}\Big(\sigma^{Y}_{\varepsilon}(R^{+}_{\varepsilon})<\sigma^{Y}_{\varepsilon}(R^{-}_{\varepsilon})\mid Y_{\varepsilon}(0)=0\Big)=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}{\mathbb{P}}\Big(\sigma^{Y}_{\varepsilon}(R^{-}_{\varepsilon})<\sigma^{Y}_{\varepsilon}(R^{+}_{\varepsilon})\mid Y_{\varepsilon}(0)=0\Big)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{2}.

Hence, for any
\delta^{\pm}>0
we have

\displaystyle

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}{\mathbb{P}}\Big(\sigma^{X}_{\varepsilon}\big(R\varepsilon^{\frac{2}{1+\beta}}\big)<\sigma^{X}_{\varepsilon}\bigl(-R\varepsilon^{\frac{2}{1+\beta}}\big)\mid X_{\varepsilon}(0)=0\Big)

(6.5)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}{\mathbb{P}}\Big(\sigma^{X}_{\varepsilon}\bigl(-R\varepsilon^{\frac{2}{1+\beta}}\big)<\sigma^{X}_{\varepsilon}\big(R\varepsilon^{\frac{2}{1+\beta}}\big)\mid X_{\varepsilon}(0)=0\Big)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}{\mathbb{P}}\Big(\sigma^{X}_{\varepsilon}(\delta^{+})<\sigma^{X}_{\varepsilon}(\delta^{-})\mid X_{\varepsilon}(0)=0\Big)=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}{\mathbb{P}}\Big(\sigma^{X}_{\varepsilon}(\delta^{-})<\sigma^{X}_{\varepsilon}(\delta^{+})\mid X_{\varepsilon}(0)=0\Big)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{2}.

Hence, if
X_{0}
is a limit point of
\{X_{\varepsilon}\}
having representation
(
6.1
), then
{\mathbb{P}}(\tau=\infty)\geqslant\tfrac{1}{2}.

It also follows from Theorem
2.5
that for any
R>0

\sup_{\varepsilon>0}{\mathbb{E}}\Big(\sigma^{Y}_{\varepsilon}(R)\wedge\sigma^{Y}_{\varepsilon}(-R)\mid Y_{\varepsilon}(0)=0\Big)<\infty.

Thus,

\sigma^{X}_{\varepsilon}\big(R\varepsilon^{\frac{2}{1+\beta}}\big)\wedge\sigma^{X}_{\varepsilon}\bigl(-R\varepsilon^{\frac{2}{1+\beta}}\big)\overset{{\mathbb{P}}}{\to}0\qquad\text{as }\varepsilon\to 0

(6.6)

if
X_{\varepsilon}(0)=0.

Let
\bar{X}_{\varepsilon}
be a solution to

d\bar{X}_{\varepsilon}(t)=\bar{a}\big(\bar{X}_{\varepsilon}(t)\big)dt+\varepsilon dw(t)

and define

\bar{Y}_{\varepsilon}(t)\coloneqq\varepsilon^{\frac{-2}{1+\beta}}\bar{X}_{\varepsilon}\bigl(\varepsilon^{\frac{2(1-\beta)}{1+\beta}}t\bigr).

Then (cf. (
6.3
), (
6.4
))

d\bar{Y}_{\varepsilon}(t)=\bar{a}(\bar{Y}_{\varepsilon}(t))dt+dw_{\varepsilon}(t).

In particular, if
\bar{X}_{\varepsilon}(0)=R\varepsilon^{\frac{2}{1+\beta}}
for all
\varepsilon>0,
where
R
is a constant, then all processes
\bar{Y}_{\varepsilon}
have the same distribution independent of
\varepsilon.

Notice that for any
R>0
,

{\mathbb{P}}\Bigl(X_{\varepsilon}(t)=\bar{X}_{\varepsilon}(t),\ t\in[0,\sigma^{X}_{\varepsilon}(0)]\mid X_{\varepsilon}(0)=\bar{X}_{\varepsilon}(0)=R\varepsilon^{\frac{2}{1+\beta}}\Bigr)=1.

(6.7)

and

\displaystyle p_{R}

\displaystyle\coloneqq{\mathbb{P}}\Bigl(\sigma^{X}_{\varepsilon}(0)=\infty\mid X_{\varepsilon}(0)=R\varepsilon^{\frac{2}{1+\beta}}\Bigr)

(6.8)

\displaystyle={\mathbb{P}}\Bigl(X_{\varepsilon}(t)>0,t\geqslant 0\mid X_{\varepsilon}(0)=R\varepsilon^{\frac{2}{1+\beta}}\Bigr)

\displaystyle={\mathbb{P}}\Bigl(\bar{X}_{\varepsilon}(t)>0,t\geqslant 0\mid\bar{X}_{\varepsilon}(0)=R\varepsilon^{\frac{2}{1+\beta}}\Bigr)

\displaystyle={\mathbb{P}}\Bigl(\bar{Y}_{\varepsilon}(t)>0\mid\bar{Y}_{\varepsilon}(0)=R\Bigr)\to 1\qquad\text{as }R\to\infty.

It follows from
[
22
]
that if
\bar{X}_{\varepsilon}(0)=R\varepsilon^{\frac{2}{1+\beta}},\varepsilon>0
,
then

\bar{X}_{\varepsilon}\Rightarrow p_{R}\delta_{\psi_{+}}+(1-p_{R})\delta_{\psi_{-}}\qquad\text{as }\varepsilon\to 0.

(6.9)

Hence, (
6.5
), (
6.6
), (
6.7
), (
6.8
),
and (
6.9
) yield that for any limit point
X_{0}

of
\{X_{\varepsilon}\}
we have
{\mathbb{P}}(\tau=0)\geqslant\tfrac{1}{2}.
This concludes the proof of the convergence
P_{X_{\varepsilon}}\Rightarrow\tfrac{1}{2}\delta_{\psi_{+}}+\tfrac{1}{2}\delta_{0}
as
\varepsilon\to 0.

Proof of Theorem
2.2
.

For a sequence of numbers
0<\varepsilon_{n}\to 0
, let
a_{i,n}=a_{i}*\omega_{\varepsilon_{n}}
, where
\omega_{\varepsilon}(z)=\varepsilon^{-1}\omega\big(z\varepsilon^{-1}\big)
and
\omega\in C_{c}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}})
is a nonnegative mollifier. Let
X_{i,n}
be the unique solution of

dX_{i,n}=a_{i,n}(X_{i,n})dt+dW,\qquad i=1,2,\ n\in{\mathbb{N}}.

(A.1)

For the smoothened drift functions it still holds
a_{1,n}\leqslant a_{2,n}
. Therefore, it follows from the classic comparison theorem that
X_{1,n}\leqslant X_{2,n}
(see e.g. the comparison theorem in
[
16
]
).

The application of Theorem
2.1
completes the proof in the case when
a_{1},a_{2}\in L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}})
. If
a_{1},a_{2}
are only locally bounded, then we
approximate
X_{1},X_{2}
by solutions to SDEs with drifts
a_{i,M}\coloneqq a_{i}\mathbbm{1}_{[-M,M]}
. It follows from
[
29
, Remark 3b, p. 145]
that

{\mathbb{P}}\bigl(X_{i}(t)=X_{i,M}(t)\;\forall\ t\leqslant\tau_{i,M}\bigr)=1,\qquad i=1,2,

where
\tau_{i,M}=\inf\{t\geqslant 0:|X_{i}(t)|\geqslant M\}.
We have already proved that
X_{1,M}(t)\leqslant X_{2,M}(t)
almost surely. This completes the proof of the theorem.
∎

Proof of Lemma
2.7
.

We assume that
f
is positive; the negative case follows similarly.
Denote
B(z)\coloneqq\int_{\alpha}^{z}f(u)\,du
. Then
B
is absolutely continuous and invertible, and since
B^{\prime}(z)=f(z)>0
for a.e.
z
, the inverse
B^{-1}
is also absolutely continuous (see e.g.
[
6
, Exercise 5.8.52]
). Hence, we can write

\displaystyle g_{\varepsilon}(y)

\displaystyle=\int_{y}^{\beta}e^{-(B(z)-B(y))/\varepsilon^{2}}\frac{B^{\prime}(z)}{\varepsilon^{2}}g(z)\,dz

\displaystyle=\int_{B(y)}^{B(\beta)}\frac{e^{-(v-B(y))/\varepsilon^{2}}}{\varepsilon^{2}}g\big(B^{-1}(v)\big)\,dv

(where we made the change of variables
v=B(z)
). The function
[0,\infty)\ni v\mapsto\frac{e^{-v/\varepsilon^{2}}}{\varepsilon^{2}}
is an approximate identity and therefore

g_{\varepsilon}(y)\to g(B^{-1}(B(y)))=g(y)\qquad\text{as }\varepsilon\to 0

in
L^{1}((\alpha,\beta))
, and pointwise whenever
v=B(y)
is a Lebesgue point for
v\mapsto g\big(B^{-1}(v)\big)
; see e.g.
[
11
, Theorems 8.14, 8.15]
. But
B
and
B^{-1}
are absolutely continuous, so these points coincide with the Lebesgue points for
g
.
∎
