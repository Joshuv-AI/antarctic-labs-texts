Power-free Complementary Binary Morphisms
Jeffrey Shallit, Arseny Shur, Stefan Zorcic

Abstract
We revisit the topic of power-free morphisms, focusing on the properties of the class of complementary morphisms. Such morphisms are defined over a 2 2 -letter alphabet, and map the letters 0 and 1 to complementary words. We prove that every prefix of the famous Thue–Morse word 𝐭 \mathbf{t} gives a complementary morphism that is 3 + 3^{+} -free and hence α \alpha -free for every real number α > 3 \alpha>3 . We also describe, using a 4-state binary finite automaton, the lengths of all prefixes of 𝐭 \mathbf{t} that give cubefree complementary morphisms. Next, we show that 3 3 -free (cubefree) complementary morphisms of length k k exist for all k ∉ { 3 , 6 } k\not\in\{3,6\} . Moreover, if k k is not of the form 3 ⋅ 2 n 3\cdot 2^{n} , then the images of letters can be chosen to be factors of 𝐭 \mathbf{t} . Finally, we observe that each cubefree complementary morphism is also α \alpha -free for some α < 3 \alpha<3 ; in contrast, no binary morphism that maps each letter to a word of length 3 (resp., a word of length 6) is α \alpha -free for any α < 3 \alpha<3 . In addition to more traditional techniques of combinatorics on words, we also rely on the Walnut theorem-prover. Its use and limitations are discussed.

Introduction

1.1
Basic definitions

This paper concerns avoidance of certain kinds of repetitions in binary words, and, more specifically, morphisms that preserve avoidance properties. We start with some definitions.

Let
w
be a (finite or infinite) word. We index words starting at
position
0
, so
w[0]
is the first letter of
w
. We indicate a contiguous sub-block within
w
(also called a
factor
) with the notation

w[i..j]:=w[i]\,w[i+1]\cdots w[j]
.
A factor
x
of
w
that is neither a prefix nor a suffix of
w
is called
internal
.
We also say that a factor
w[i..j]

occurs at position
i
.

We consider words over the alphabet
\{0,1\}
.
If
a\in\{0,1\}
, the other letter is called the
complement
of
a
and denoted by
\overline{a}
.
A word
v[0..n]
is the
complement
of
w[0..n]
, denoted by
v=\overline{w}
, if
v[i]=\overline{w[i]}
for
i=0,\ldots,n
.

The length of a finite word
w
is denoted by
|w|
.
Suppose
w
is a finite nonempty word of length
n
.
We say that an integer
p
,
1\leq p\leq n
, is a
period
of

w
if
w[i]=w[i+p]
for
0\leq i<n-p
.
A period is
proper
if
p<n
. The smallest period
of
w
is denoted by
\per(w)
. The
exponent
of a word

w
is defined to be
\mathsf{exp}(w)=n/\per(w)
, and is a measure of
the repetitivity of the word. For example,
\mathsf{exp}({\tt entente})=7/3
.

If a word has length
2n
and period
n
, such as

murmur
, it is called a
square
.
If a word has length
2n+1
and period
n
, such as

alfalfa
, it is called an
overlap
.
If a word has length
3n
and period
n
, such as

shshsh
, it is called a
cube
.

The
critical exponent
of a word
w
, denoted
\ce(w)
, is the
supremum, over all nonempty factors
x
of
w
, of
\mathsf{exp}(x)
.
For example, the critical exponent of Aristophanes’ famous coined word

brekekekex
is
7/2
.

Let
\alpha>1
be a real number. We now define the concept of

\alpha
-freeness for words:

•

If
\ce(w)<\alpha
, we say that

w
is
\alpha
-free
.

•

If
\ce(w)\leq\alpha
, we say that
w

is
\alpha^{+}
-free
.

For example, the name of the Dutch mathematician
Lekkerkerker

is
3^{+}
-free, but not
3
-free.
The terms
squarefree
,
overlap-free
, and
cubefree
are commonly used substitutes for
2
-free,
2^{+}
-free, and
3
-free, respectively.

It is convenient to extend the real numbers in such a way that every real number
\alpha

has a partner,
\alpha^{+}
that satisfies the inequality
\alpha<\alpha^{+}<\beta
for all
\alpha<\beta
. This new domain is denoted
\hat{\mathbb{R}}
.
Somewhat unintuitively, we define
\lceil\alpha^{+}\rceil=\lceil\alpha\rceil
for

\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
.

1.2
Power-free morphisms

Recall that a
morphism

h
is a map obeying the identity

h(xy)=h(x)h(y)
for all words
x,y
.
We write
h^{n}
for the composition
h\circ h\circ\cdots\circ h
(
n
times);
h^{0}
is the identity map.
An infinite word
\mathbf{u}
satisfying
h(\mathbf{u})=\mathbf{u}
is the
fixed point
of
h
.
If a morphism maps each letter to a word of the same length, say length
k
, we call it
(
k
-)uniform
; we refer to
k
as the
length
of such a morphism.
A uniform binary morphism
h
satisfying
h(1)=\overline{h(0)}
is called
complementary
.

Let
\alpha\in\hat{\mathbb{R}}
and
\alpha>1
. There is also a concept of

\alpha
-freeness for morphisms, as follows:
if for all words
w
we have

w

\alpha
-free implies that

h(w)
is
\alpha
-free,

then we call
h

\alpha
-free.
In other words, an
\alpha
-free morphism is one that preserves the property of being
\alpha
-free.
The fixed point of any
\alpha
-free morphism is
\alpha
-free; however, the converse implication is not always true.

The first result in the theory of
\alpha
-free morphisms was proven by Thue in 1912
[
26
]
:

Theorem 1
.

Define
\mu:0\mapsto 01,1\mapsto 10
. Then
\mu
is a
2^{+}
-free morphism.

In fact, even more is true. We have
the following result

[
25
,
11
]
:

Theorem 2
.

Let
\alpha\in\hat{\mathbb{R}}
and
\alpha>2
. Then
\mu
is an
\alpha
-free morphism.

The study of
\alpha
-free morphisms has been quite active.
Most of the results are related to integer values of
\alpha
and the “nearly integer” value
2^{+}
.
Thue
[
26
]
gave the first easily verifiable sufficient condition for a morphism
h
to be squarefree. The best criterion known for squarefree morphisms is due to Crochemore
[
7
]
.
A similar criterion was found for overlap-free morphisms
[
20
]
.

The case of
\alpha
-free morphisms is more subtle when
\alpha
is an integer greater than 2. There is a generalization, due to Bean et al.
[
3
]
, of Thue’s sufficient condition, but on the other hand there is evidence that no general criteria exist even for
\alpha=3

[
19
]
.
However, such criteria
do
exist under certain restrictions on morphisms—namely, for
uniform
morphisms
[
12
,
21
]
and for
binary
morphisms
[
27
,
19
]
.

The sets of
\alpha
-free morphisms for different integers
\alpha
satisfy some containment relations.
Thus, every squarefree morphism satisfying a certain additional condition is
\alpha
-free for all integers
\alpha>2

[
3
]
; an even simpler condition was presented in
[
14
]
.
Further, every
\alpha
-free
uniform
morphism is
(\alpha+1)
-free whenever
\alpha\geq 3
is an integer
[
28
,
29
]
.
On the other hand, for every integer
\alpha\geq 3
there exists an
(\alpha+1)
-free
binary
morphism that is not
\alpha
-free but has an
\alpha
-free fixed point
[
18
]
.

The case of non-integer
\alpha
is less studied. Brandenburg
[
5
]
analyzed whether a morphism over
k
letters can be
\alpha_{k}
-free, where
\alpha_{k}
is the minimum critical exponent of an infinite
k
-ary word.
He proved that
\alpha_{k}
-free morphisms do not exist if
k\geq 4
; for
k=2
and
k=3
such morphisms do exist
[
26
,
9
]
.
Note that for non-integer
\alpha
, the uniformity condition on morphisms is not restrictive, due to the following observation:

Observation 3
.

[
[
5
,
13
]
]
If a morphism
h
is
\alpha
-free for a non-integer
\alpha\in\hat{\mathbb{R}}
, then
h
is uniform, and for every pair of distinct letters
a,b
from the domain of
h
, the words
h(a)
and
h(b)
have distinct first letters and distinct last letters.

Kobayashi
[
13
]
proved the following sufficient condition for morphisms to be
\alpha
-free.

Theorem 4
.

Let
\alpha>2
be a member of
\hat{\mathbb{R}}
. Suppose that a
k
-uniform morphism
h
satisfies the following conditions:

(a)

for every letter
a
we have
\mathsf{exp}(h(a))<2
;

(b)

for every two distinct letters
a,b
,

(b1)

the words
h(a)
and
h(b)
have distinct first letters;

(b2)

the words
h(a)
and
h(b)
have distinct last letters;

(b3)

if
h(a)=uv
,
h(b)=v^{\prime}u
, and
|u|\geq k/2
, then
v=v^{\prime}
;

(c)

the word
h(x)
is
\alpha
-free for each
\alpha
-free word
x
of length
\leq\lceil\alpha\rceil+1
.

Then
h
is
\alpha
-free.

The only case in which the full description of the set of
\alpha
-free morphisms is known is presented in the following theorem. The particular case of overlap-free morphisms was explicitly stated in
[
4
]
, and the general case easily follows from Theorem
2
and known structural results on
(7/3)
-free words; see, e.g.,
[
11
,
25
]
.

Theorem 5
.

Let
\alpha\in\hat{\mathbb{R}}
and
2^{+}\leq\alpha\leq 7/3
.
A binary morphism is
\alpha
-free if and only if it equals
\mu^{n}
or
\theta\circ\mu^{n}
, where
\theta:0\mapsto 1,1\mapsto 0
,
\mu:0\mapsto 01,1\mapsto 10
, and
n\geq 0
.

In particular, Theorem
5
implies that for
2^{+}\leq\alpha\leq 7/3
a
k
-uniform
\alpha
-free binary morphism exists if and only if
k
is a power of 2.
However, for
\alpha=3
the situation is quite different. Currie and Rampersad
[
8
]

exhibit a set of morphisms proving the following result.

Theorem 6
.

For every
k\geq 1
there is a
k
-uniform cubefree binary morphism.

1.3
Questions and Results

For the present study, we took Theorem
6
as a starting point and addressed a more general question: for arbitrary
\alpha\in\hat{\mathbb{R}}
, what are the possible lengths of uniform
\alpha
-free binary morphisms?
Specifically, in this paper we answer the following particular questions:

•

Does the result of Theorem
6
extend to arbitrary values
\alpha\in\hat{\mathbb{R}}
that are greater than 3? If so, does this extended result holds if we are further restricted to the class of complementary morphisms or its subclasses?

•

Does the result of Theorem
6
hold for the class of complementary morphisms or for some its subclasses?

•

Does the result of Theorem
6
hold for any
\alpha<3
?

Note that to deal with non-integer powers
\alpha
we need morphisms satisfying Observation
3
, so the class of complementary morphisms is arguably the most natural choice.
We also note that Thue’s morphism
\mu
, as well as all other morphisms from Theorem
5
, are complementary.

Our main results are as follows. We start with the first question.

Theorem 7
.

For every
\alpha\geq 3^{+}
and each
k\geq 1
, there is an
\alpha
-free complementary morphism of length
k
.

To prove Theorem
7
, we use the infinite Thue–Morse word

\mathbf{t}=0110100110010110\cdots,

which is the fixed point of Thue’s morphism
\mu
defined previously, as a source of images of letters for our morphisms.
Namely, we prove that for each
k\geq 1
, the complementary morphism

h_{k}:0\mapsto\mathbf{t}[0..k-1],\ 1\mapsto\overline{\mathbf{t}[0..k-1]}

is
\alpha
-free for all
\alpha>3
(see Theorem
10
in Section
2.1
).
Furthermore, we observe that for some values of
k
, the morphism
h_{k}
is cubefree, and we characterize these
k
with a 4-state binary deterministic finite automaton (see Theorem
22
in Section
3.2
).

The answer to the second question is given by the following theorem.

Theorem 8
.

Cubefree complementary morphisms of length
k
exist for all values
k\geq 1
except
k=3
and
k=6
.

Proving Theorem
8
, we find factors of the Thue–Morse word that give cubefree complementary morphisms of all lengths
k
except those of the form
k=3\cdot 2^{n}
(see Theorem
15
in Section
2.2
).
The remaining cases are covered by morphisms of the form
g\circ\mu^{n}
, where
g
is a certain complementary morphism of length 12.
Covering the exceptional lengths 3 and 6 with uniform (but not complementary) morphisms
\rho
and
\rho\circ\mu
, where
\rho:0\mapsto 001,1\mapsto 011
, we get an alternative proof of Theorem
6
.

In addition, we present two more series of cubefree complementary morphisms; the images of letters in these series are certain prefixes of two infinite words densely related to the Thue–Morse word.
Using these two series and the morphisms
\mu
and
\rho
, one obtains another alternative proof of Theorem
6
(see Section 5).

Finally, we slightly extend the argument from Theorem
8
to obtain the following theorem, providing an answer to the third of studied questions.

Theorem 9
.

(a)

For all
\alpha\in\hat{\mathbb{R}}
such that
\alpha<3
,
3
-uniform and
6
-uniform
\alpha
-free binary morphisms do not exist.

(b)

For each
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
k\notin\{3,6\}
there is a complementary morphism of length
k
that is
\alpha
-free for some
\alpha<3
.

Along with more traditional methods of combinatorics on words, we use the theorem-prover

Walnut

[
16
,
23
]
designed to prove first-order sentences related to certain classes of infinite words.
Although this second approach is less general, it is conceptually quite simple, and the proof takes advantage of some existing software to replace case analysis.
It does, however, require some serious computation: sometimes
Walnut
cannot prove a first-order statement because it exceeds the limits of computational resources in the process.
Using
Walnut
, we give an alternative proof of the crucial case
\alpha=3^{+}
of Theorem
7
, though we were unable to prove Theorem
10
in this way.
The automaton in Theorem
22
is also obtained by
Walnut
.
The proof of Theorem
15
using
Walnut
currently seems to be beyond its computational limits, and thus the prover cannot help with Theorem
8
or Theorem
9
.
On the positive side,
Walnut
proved cube-freeness of the morphisms from two infinite sequences, thus providing the second alternative proof of Theorem
6
.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
we give combinatorial proofs of Theorems
7
,
8
, and
9
.
In Section
3
we introduce
Walnut
and describe all proofs obtained by its use.
We end up with a short discussion in Section
4
.

Combinatorial Proofs

2.1
Truncated Thue–Morse morphisms: proof of Theorem
7

For each integer
k\geq 1
, we define the

truncated Thue–Morse morphism

h_{k}
as the complementary morphism such that

h_{k}(0)=\mathbf{t}[0..k-1]
.
For example,
h_{3}(0)=011
and
h_{3}(1)=100
.

The following theorem is a counterpart to Theorem
2
and implies Theorem
7
.

Theorem 10
.

Suppose that
w
is a binary word and
k
is a positive integer.
Then
\ce(h_{k}(w))=\ce(w)
if

\ce(w)\geq 3
and
\ce(h_{k}(w))\leq 3
if
\ce(w)<3
.

The proof of Theorem
10
is based on some properties of prefixes of
\mathbf{t}
(we also call them Thue–Morse prefixes).
These properties are proved below in Lemmas
11
–
14
, and may be of independent interest.
We refer to the words
h_{k}(0)
and

as
k
-prefixes
and to

as

-blocks
.
The following auxiliary property was apparently known to Thue
[

]
.

Lemma 11
.

Let
a\in\{0,1\}
,
n\geq 1
.

(a)

In the word
\mu^{n}(a\,\overline{a})
, the
n
-blocks occur only at positions
0
and
2^{n}
.

(b)

In the word
\mu^{n}(aa)
, the
n
-blocks occur only at positions
0,2^{n-1}
, and

.

Proof.

Since equal factors of an overlap-free word do not overlap, we immediately get (a).
For (b), we write
\mu^{n}(aa)=\mu^{n-1}(a\,\overline{a}\,a\,\overline{a})
to see the occurrence of the
n
-block

at position

and use (a) to show that there are no other occurrences.
∎

Lemma 12
.

Let
k\geq 1
. Suppose a word
u
begins with a
k
-prefix and ends with a
k
-prefix, and
|u|>k
.
Then
|u|\geq 3k/2
. Furthermore, equality is attained if and only if

is a power of 2.

Proof.

Let
u
begin with a
k
-prefix
s
and end with a
k
-prefix

. The result is trivial for

k=1
, so assume

.

First consider the case
k=2^{n}
for some

.
We have
s=\mu^{n}(a)=\mu^{n-1}(a)\mu^{n-1}(\overline{a})
for some
a\in\{0,1\}
.
By Lemma

(a), the word

has occurrences of

-blocks only at the positions 0 and

.
Since

begins with a

-block, it cannot begin with

at a position smaller than

.
If

begins at this position, we have

; otherwise,

.

Now let

.
Then

If

.
Now suppose that

At position

.
At position

, the word

-prefix as well.
Finally, if

of

Lemma 13
.

Suppose that

Lemma 14
.

Let

with

.

(a)

If

, then any factor of

(a)

(b)

We suppose that
k

Let

In

To prove the theorem it is sufficient to show that either

2.2
Clipped Thue–Morse morphisms: proof of Theorem
8

While Theorem
10
implies that all morphisms
h_{k}
are
3^{+}
-free, many of them are not cubefree.
Indeed, if
h_{k}(a)
, where
a
is a letter, ends with a square of a letter or of an
n
-block, then either
h_{k}(a\,a)
or
h_{k}(a\,\overline{a})
contains the cube of this letter (or of this
n
-block).
However, if instead of prefixes we consider all factors of the Thue–Morse word, we can build cubefree complementary morphisms similar to
h_{k}
for almost all lengths.

For integers
\ell,k\geq 1
, define the
clipped Thue–Morse morphism

f_{\ell,k}
as the complementary morphism such that

f_{\ell,k}(0)=\mathbf{t}[\ell..\ell+k-1]
.
In particular,
h_{k}=f_{0,k}
.
When we are interested in the factor

rather than in particular numbers

and
k
, we write

instead of
f_{\ell,k}
.

Theorem 15
.

Let
k=m\cdot 2^{n}
, where
m
is odd and
n\geq 0
.
A cubefree clipped Thue–Morse morphism of length
k
exists if and only if
m\neq 3
. Moreover,

(a)

if
m\equiv 1\ \mbox{\rm(mod}\ 8\mbox{\rm)}
, then
f_{4\cdot 2^{n},k}
is cubefree;

(b)

if
m\equiv 3\ \mbox{\rm(mod}\ 8\mbox{\rm)}
, then either
f_{k-5,k}
or
f_{k-4,k}
is cubefree;

(c)

if
m\equiv 5\ \mbox{\rm(mod}\ 8\mbox{\rm)}
, then
f_{0,k}
is cubefree;

(d)

if
m\equiv 7\ \mbox{\rm(mod}\ 8\mbox{\rm)}
, then
f_{6\cdot 2^{n},k}
is cubefree.

The proof of Theorem
15
is based on two lemmas that establish sufficient conditions for cubefree and non-cubefree clipped Thue–Morse morphisms.

Lemma 16
.

Let

be a factor of
\mathbf{t}

or

As the reversal of an

\alpha

\mathbf{t}

As in the proof of Theorem

We prove the lemma by assuming

Since

Let

The markers are not factors of

\overline{v}\,v

\beta=\max\{\ce(h(x))\mid|x|\leq\lceil\alpha\rceil+1,\ce(x)<\alpha\}
.
Then
\beta<\alpha
and
h
verifies the conditions of Theorem
4
for
\beta^{+}
.
As
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
, one gets
\beta^{+}<\alpha
; the lemma now follows.
∎

By Lemma
19
, the morphism

g:0\mapsto 011001001101,1\mapsto 100110110010
from the proof of Theorem
8
is
\alpha
-free for some
\alpha<3
.
Then all morphisms of the form
g\mu^{n}
are
\alpha
-free as well, so we get statement (b) for all lengths
k
of the form
k=3\cdot 2^{n}
.

However, some clipped Thue–Morse morphisms violate condition (b3) of Theorem
4
.
For instance, the word
01101
is both a prefix of
h(0)
and a suffix of
h(1)
for the morphism
h:0\mapsto 011010010,1\mapsto 100101101
.
Hence, instead of checking whether all morphisms listed in Theorem
15
satisfy this condition, we use a different argument.
As all odd-length morphisms from Theorem
15
satisfy the conditions of Lemma
16
, we strengthen the conclusion of Lemma
16
.
Namely, we show that the morphism
f_{v}
is
(3-1/k)^{+}
-free.

Let us follow the proof of Lemma
16
.
For an arbitrary fixed period
p
, we study the longest factor
U
of
W=f_{v}(u)
satisfying
\per(U)=p
, and analyse the case where
\mathsf{exp}(U)\geq 3
.
If
p\leq k
, then the conditions
\mathsf{exp}(U)\geq 3
and
\mathsf{exp}(U)>3-1/k
are equivalent.
In this case, the proof of the lemma guarantees the existence of a factor
u
of
v
such that
\mathsf{exp}(u)=\mathsf{exp}(U)
.
Now let
p>k
.
Note that the conditions on the prefixes and suffixes of
v
imply
k\geq 5
.
We have
\lceil(1-1/k)\cdot p\rceil\geq\lceil(1-1/5)\cdot 6\rceil=5
.
Then the condition
\mathsf{exp}(U)>3-1/k
implies
|U|\geq\lceil(3-1/k)\cdot p\rceil\geq 2p+5
.
Hence
U
contains a marker; let
x=U[i..i{+}4]
be a marker.
If
i\geq p
, then
x=U[i{-}p..i{-}p{+}4]
; otherwise,
x=U[i{+}p..i{+}p{+}4]
.
In both cases, the marker
x
occurs in
U
twice at positions distinct by
p
.
Having proved this, we follow the rest of the proof of the lemma, finding the factor
u
of
v
such that
\mathsf{exp}(u)=\mathsf{exp}(U)
.
The existence of such a factor
u
whenever
\mathsf{exp}(U)>3-1/k
ensures that
f_{v}
is
(3-1/k)^{+}
-free.

Thus we have proved that for each odd integer
k
the complementary morphism of length
k
listed in Theorem
15
is
(3-1/k)^{+}
-free.
Since every even-length morphism from this theorem is the composition of an odd-length morphism and an appropriate power of
\mu

k
distinct from
3\cdot 2^{n}
, a complementary morphism that is
\alpha
-free for some
\alpha<3
.
Statement (b) of Theorem
9
is thus proved.

3.1
Automata theory and
Walnut

In this section we review the automata theory we
need to discuss our proofs using
Walnut
,
and give examples of how
Walnut
can be used to prove results.

A DFAO (deterministic finite automaton with output) is a slight generalization of the more familiar DFA (deterministic finite automaton). In it, each state has an associated output. The output associated with an input word
x
is the output of the last state reached when processing
x
.
We say that a sequence

{\bf a}=(a_{n})_{n\geq 0}
is
k
-automatic
if there exists a DFAO that, on input
n
expressed in base
k
, outputs
a_{n}
.
See
[
1
]
for more information.

A basic result that we will rely on throughout is the following
[
6
, Thm. 6.1, Cor. 6.2]
:

Theorem 20
.

There is an algorithm that, given a

k
-automatic sequence
{\bf a}=(a_{n})_{n\geq 0}
and a first-order logical formula
\varphi
in the structure

\langle\mathbb{N},+,n\rightarrow a_{n}\rangle
, computes a DFA
M_{\varphi}
that takes the unbound variables of
\varphi
as inputs, and accepts precisely those values of the variables (represented in base
k
) that make
\varphi
true. If
\varphi
has no free variables, the algorithm returns
TRUE
or
FALSE
, and accordingly constitutes a rigorous proof or disproof of the formula.

Furthermore, this algorithm has been implemented in free software called
Walnut

[
16
,
23
]
.
As a warm-up, we provide
Walnut
proofs
of Lemmas
11
,
12
, and
14
.

The letters

The symbol

denotes logical “and”,

3.2
Alternative proof of the case

We now use

The proof is based on Kobayashi’s conditions (Theorem

For every morphism

we translate each of the conditions of Theorem

for the exponent

into

Alternative proof of Theorem
7
(case
\alpha=3^{+}
).

Conditions (b1) and (b2) follow trivially from the definition of
h_{k}
.
For the other conditions, we use
Walnut
as described above.

We start with condition (c). By the criterion, we must apply

h_{k}
to all
3^{+}
-free binary words
w
of length
4
and verify
that
h_{k}(w)
is
3^{+}
-free in all cases. Of the
16
possible
binary words, only
0000
and
1111
are not
3^{+}
-free, so at first
glance it would seem that we have to check the condition (c) for
14

binary words.

However, to save effort and computation time, there are some symmetries we can exploit to avoid having to test all
14
words. Given a morphism

h
, we can define its reverse morphism
h^{R}(a)=h(a)^{R}
by
reversing the image of each letter. Then it is easy to see that

h(w)=(h^{R}(w))^{R}
. On the other hand, for the particular case of

h_{k}
, by the definition we have
h_{k}(w)=\overline{h_{k}(\overline{w})}
. Since
\ce(w)=\ce(w^{R})
and

\ce(w)=\ce(\overline{w})
, this means it suffices to test the
3^{+}
-free
property for five words:
0001
,
0010
,
0011
,
0101
, and
0110
.

We now create first-order logical formulas to test the condition. We illustrate
the idea for the specific case of
0001
. The
idea is to create a formula that asserts that
(h_{k}(0001))[n]=1
.

In order to do that, we must be able to compute
n\bmod k
and

\lfloor n/k\rfloor
with
a first-order formula, for variables
n
and
k
. In full generality, this is provably impossible
to state in the particular first-order logical theory that

Walnut
uses, so we have to use some subterfuge: since we only
care about the range

0\leq n<4k
, it actually suffices to be able to compute
n\bmod k

just in this range, and not for all
n
.
We use the following formula:

\ndmodk(k,n,r,z):=(0\leq n<k\,\wedge\,z=n\,\wedge\,r=0)\,\vee\,(k\leq n<2k\,\wedge\,z=n-k\,\wedge\,r=1)\,\vee\,\\
(2k\leq n<3k\,\wedge\,z=n-2k\,\wedge\,r=2)\,\vee\,(3k\leq n<4k\,\wedge\,z=n-3k\,\wedge\,r=3),

which evaluates to

TRUE

if and only if
0\leq n<4k
and

r=\lfloor n/k\rfloor
and
z=n\bmod k
.

This can be translated to
Walnut
as follows:

def ndmodk "(n<k & z=n & r=0) | (n>=k & n<2*k & z+k=n & r=1) |
(n>=2*k & n<3*k & z+2*k=n & r=2) | (n>=3*k & n<4*k & z+3*k=n & r=3)":

The resulting automaton has
40
states.

Next, we make a logical formula that evaluates
to
TRUE
if
h_{k}(0001)[n]=1

\displaystyle\hone(k,n)

\displaystyle:=(\exists r,z\ r\leq 2\,\wedge\,\ndmodk(k,n,r,z)\,\wedge\,{\bf t}[z]=1)\,\vee\,

\displaystyle\quad\quad(\exists r,z\ r=3\,\wedge\,\ndmodk(k,n,r,z)\,\wedge\,{\bf t}[z]=0).

This can be translated into
Walnut

def h0001 "(Er,z r<=2 & $ndmodk(k,n,r,z) & T[z]=@1) |

For the other four strings, we use analogous formulas, as follows:

def h0010 "(Er,z (r<=1|r=3) & $ndmodk(k,n,r,z) & T[z]=@1) |

Now we create a formula that asserts that

has

\testone(i,k,n):=\forall u,v\ (i\leq u\leq i+2n\,\wedge\,v=u+n)\implies(\hone(k,u)\iff\hone(k,v)).

# 27 states

4
Discussion

1. In this paper, we study
\alpha
-free uniform binary morphisms.
Our Theorems
7
–
9
, together with earlier known Theorem
6
, reveal a “phase transition” at
\alpha=3
.
Namely,
\alpha
-free
uniform
binary morphisms of all lengths exist for every
\alpha\geq 3
, but for every
\alpha<3
there are some “forbidden” lengths.
And if we consider only
complementary
morphisms instead of arbitrary uniform binary morphisms, then we get almost the same picture: the only difference is that forbidden lengths exist already for
\alpha=3
.

Given the characterization of
\alpha
-free morphisms for
\alpha\leq 7/3
(Theorem
5
), a natural open problem is to characterize the phase transitions in the remaining interval
(7/3)^{+}\leq\alpha<3
.
We conjecture that the set of admissible lengths of morphisms stays the same whenever
(8/3)^{+}\leq\alpha<3
, but there is another phase transition at
8/3
.

2. We note that there exist other properties separating cubefree and
3^{+}
-free words in the binary case.
For example, infinite cubefree words must contain squares of even period while
3^{+}
-free words can avoid them
[
2
]
.

In a recent paper, Meleshko et al.
[
15
]
studied infinite pseudoperiodic words.
We say an infinite word
\bf x
is
(p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{t})
-pseudoperiodic if for all
i
there exists
j
,
1\leq j\leq t
, such that
{\bf x}[i]={\bf x}[i+p_{j}]
.
Their Conjecture 34 asserts that for all pairs
1\leq a<b
,
b\not=2a
, there is an infinite binary
(a,b)
-pseudoperiodic word
that is
3^{+}
-free.
If the conjecture holds, it indicates another phase transition at
\alpha=3
, because for some pairs
(a,b)
the word with required properties cannot be cubefree.

Our Theorem
7
shows that, in order to prove this conjecture, it suffices to prove it in the case where
a
and
b
are relatively prime.
For if
d=\gcd(a,b)>1
, then we could find an infinite binary word
\bf w
avoiding
(a/d,b/d)
-pseudopowers that is
3^{+}
-free, and then apply
h_{g}
.
This was, in fact, the first piece of motivation for the study presented in this paper.
