On induced completely prime primitive ideals in enveloping algebras of classical Lie algebras
Simon M. Goodwin, Lewis Topley and Matthew Westaway

Abstract
. A distinguished family of completely prime primitive ideals in the universal enveloping algebra of a reductive Lie algebra 𝔤 {\mathfrak{g}} over ℂ {\mathbb{C}} are those ideals constructed from one-dimensional representations of finite W W -algebras. We refer to these ideals as Losev–Premet ideals . For 𝔤 {\mathfrak{g}} simple of classical type, we prove that for a Losev–Premet ideal I I in U ⁡ ( 𝔤 ) U({\mathfrak{g}}) , there exists a Losev–Premet ideal I 0 I_{0} for a certain Levi subalgebra 𝔤 0 {\mathfrak{g}}_{0} of 𝔤 {\mathfrak{g}} such that associated variety of I 0 I_{0} is the closure of a rigid nilpotent orbit in 𝔤 0 {\mathfrak{g}}_{0} and I I is obtained from I 0 I_{0} by parabolic induction. This is deduced from the corresponding statement about one-dimensional representations of finite W W -algebras.



1.
Introduction

Let
G
be a connected reductive algebraic group over
{\mathbb{C}}
and let
{\mathfrak{g}}=\operatorname{Lie}G
. The study of
the spectrum
\operatorname{Prim}U({\mathfrak{g}})
of primitive ideals in the universal enveloping algebra
U({\mathfrak{g}})
of
{\mathfrak{g}}
is
one of the most venerable topics in representation theory.
These primitive ideals serve as a tractable approximation to the irreducible representations of
U({\mathfrak{g}})

and have generated an enormous amount of interest from leading mathematicians. Amongst the primitive
ideals of
U({\mathfrak{g}})
, an
especially important role is played by the spectrum
\operatorname{Prim}^{1}U({\mathfrak{g}})
of completely prime primitive ideals. This importance is largely due to the close relationship between these ideals and the unitary representations of Lie groups (see
[
LMM21
]
and the references therein).

The classification of
\operatorname{Prim}U({\mathfrak{g}})
as a set was established in the early 1980s following important
contributions from Barbasch–Vogan, Duflo, Joseph and others; we refer to
[
Jo83
]
and

[
Jo84
]
for an overview with references.
Despite the classification of
\operatorname{Prim}U({\mathfrak{g}})
being established 40 years ago, the classification problem
for
\operatorname{Prim}^{1}U({\mathfrak{g}})
has proved to be very challenging and remains open. A classification is known
in the case
G
is of type
{\mathsf{A}}
, where it was established by Mœglin in
[
Mœ87
]
. There is also
work dealing with some small rank cases, for instance the work of Borho in
[
Bo76
]
for
G

of type
{\mathsf{B}}_{2}
, and there have been many further important
contributions to the general classification problem. We refer to
[
PT14
, §1.2]
for some discussion and references.

A general paradigm in classification problems in Lie theoretic representation theory is
to use a process of parabolic induction. For the case of
\operatorname{Prim}^{1}U({\mathfrak{g}})
,
parabolic induction of ideals, as recalled in §
2.6
, can be used.
Of importance for this approach is the theorem of Conze in
[
Co74
]
, which states that
the induced ideal of a completely prime primitive ideal is itself a completely prime primitive ideal.
Thus to classify completely prime primitive ideals a key problem is to determine the
so-called
rigid
completely prime primitive ideals, namely those that cannot be non-trivially obtained
by parabolic induction. The main result of this paper
solves this problem, in the case
G
is of classical type,
for an important family of completely prime primitive ideals
that arise naturally from the theory of finite
W
-algebras.
Before stating this result in Theorem
1.1
, we need to recall
some more background.

Following the classification of
\operatorname{Prim}U({\mathfrak{g}})
, an important problem was to
determine the associated variety
{\mathcal{VA}}(I)
of a primitive ideal
I
; the definition
of
{\mathcal{VA}}(I)
is recalled in §
2.6
. This problem was
made precise by Joseph in
[
Jo85
]
:
by identifying
{\mathfrak{g}}
with its dual via a nondegenerate invariant symmetric bilinear form,

{\mathcal{VA}}(I)
is viewed as a closed subvariety of
{\mathfrak{g}}
, and the main result of
op. cit.
states that
{\mathcal{VA}}(I)
is the closure
\overline{{\mathbb{O}}}_{e}
of the
G
-orbit
{\mathbb{O}}_{e}

of some nilpotent element
e\in{\mathfrak{g}}
. This leads to the decomposition
\operatorname{Prim}U({\mathfrak{g}})=\bigsqcup\operatorname{Prim}_{{\mathbb{O}}}U({\mathfrak{g}})
,
where the (finite, disjoint) union is taken over the
nilpotent
G
-orbits
{\mathbb{O}}
in
{\mathfrak{g}}
and
\operatorname{Prim}_{{\mathbb{O}}}U({\mathfrak{g}}):=\{I\in\operatorname{Prim}U({\mathfrak{g}})\mid{\mathcal{VA}}(I)=\overline{{\mathbb{O}}}\}
.
Consequently, problems regarding
\operatorname{Prim}U({\mathfrak{g}})
can broken down to problems
about
\operatorname{Prim}_{{\mathbb{O}}}U({\mathfrak{g}})
, and those about
\operatorname{Prim}^{1}U({\mathfrak{g}})
can be reduced
to
\operatorname{Prim}^{1}_{{\mathbb{O}}}U({\mathfrak{g}}):=\operatorname{Prim}^{1}U({\mathfrak{g}})\cap\operatorname{Prim}_{{\mathbb{O}}}U({\mathfrak{g}})
.

The introduction of finite
W
-algebras to the mathematical
literature by Premet in
[
Pr02
]
led to a resurgence
in interest in the primitive ideals of
U({\mathfrak{g}})
. In §
4
, we recall
some background on the finite
W
-algebra
U({\mathfrak{g}},e)
associated to a nilpotent element
e\in{\mathfrak{g}}
.
In particular, we recall the close
relationship between
\operatorname{Prim}_{{\mathbb{O}}_{e}}U({\mathfrak{g}})
and the finite dimensional irreducible representations of the finite
W
-algebra

U({\mathfrak{g}},e)
.
This gives an approach to
\operatorname{Prim}_{{\mathbb{O}}_{e}}U({\mathfrak{g}})
via the representation theory of
U({\mathfrak{g}},e)
, which
has led to spectacular progress. For instance, it leads to a resolution of the problem discussed in
[
BJ01
]

that
\operatorname{Prim}U({\mathfrak{g}})
can be described as a countable union of varieties; the work of Losev in
[
Lo15
]

made a breakthrough in the
programme proposed by Joseph in
[
Jo80
]
to determine Goldie rank polynomials; and the longstanding
problem that
\operatorname{Prim}^{1}_{{\mathbb{O}}_{e}}U({\mathfrak{g}})\neq\varnothing
has been
resolved, with the proof completed by Premet in
[
Pr14
]
.

The theory of finite
W
-algebras picks out a distinguished class of completely prime
primitive ideals, namely those corresponding to one-dimensional representations of finite
W
-algebras. We
name these ideals
Losev–Premet
ideals, reflecting that major progress on their theory has been made in the work
of both Losev and Premet.
Thanks to
[
Lo10a
, Theorem 1.2.2]
every
Losev–Premet ideal coming from a one-dimensional representation of
U({\mathfrak{g}},e)
lies in
\operatorname{Prim}^{1}_{{\mathbb{O}}_{e}}U({\mathfrak{g}})
.
It is now well-established that the Losev–Premet ideals form an important
family in
\operatorname{Prim}^{1}_{{\mathbb{O}}_{e}}U({\mathfrak{g}})
. For instance, it is proved in
[
Lo15
, Corollary 1.2]
that
for primitive ideals with integral central
character the Losev–Premet ideals give all completely prime primitive ideals (with a possible exception
for
G
of type
{\sf E}_{8}
); it is known in type
A
that this statement holds without the restriction on central character (see the remarks following
[
Pr11
, Theorem B]
); it is proved in
[
To23
, Theorem 1.2]
that, for
G
of classical type, the Losev–Premet ideals
are precisely the ideals in the image of Losev’s orbit map introduced in
[
Lo22
]
; and
it is expected that the
left and right annihilators of unitary Harish-Chandra bimodules for
U({\mathfrak{g}})
are Losev–Premet ideals,
see
[
LMM21
, Conjecture 6.3.1]
. We note that it is in general false that every
ideal in
\operatorname{Prim}^{1}U({\mathfrak{g}})
is a Losev–Premet ideal and refer to the work of Losev–Panin in
[
LP21
]

for an important recent development regarding this defect.

We now state our main theorem which, as explained after its statement,
completes the classification of rigid Losev–Premet ideals for
G
of classical type.
In the statement we refer to Lusztig–Spaltenstein induction of nilpotent orbits, which is recapped in
§
2.4
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
G
be a simple algebraic group over
{\mathbb{C}}
of classical type and let

{\mathbb{O}}
be a nilpotent orbit in
{\mathfrak{g}}=\operatorname{Lie}G
.
Let
I\subseteq U({\mathfrak{g}})
be a Losev–Premet ideal with
{\mathcal{VA}}(I)=\overline{{\mathbb{O}}}
.
Then there exists a Levi subalgebra
{\mathfrak{g}}_{0}
of
{\mathfrak{g}}
, a parabolic subalgebra
{\mathfrak{p}}
of
{\mathfrak{g}}
with Levi factor
{\mathfrak{g}}_{0}
, a rigid nilpotent
orbit
{\mathbb{O}}_{0}
in
{\mathfrak{g}}_{0}
, and a Losev–Premet ideal
I_{0}\subseteq U({\mathfrak{g}}_{0})
such that

{\mathbb{O}}
is obtained from
{\mathbb{O}}_{0}

by Lusztig–Spaltenstein induction,
{\mathcal{VA}}(I_{0})=\overline{{\mathbb{O}}}_{0}
,
and
I
is
induced from
({\mathfrak{p}},I_{0})
.

This theorem implies that the rigid Losev–Premet ideals, for
G
of classical type, are precisely those
whose associated variety is the closure of a rigid nilpotent orbit.
By
[
PT14
, Theorem 1]
it is known that, for
G
simple of classical type
and
{\mathbb{O}}
a rigid nilpotent orbit in
{\mathfrak{g}}
, there is a unique Losev–Premet ideal
I
with
{\mathcal{VA}}(I)=\overline{{\mathbb{O}}}
.
Moreover, the highest weight of a simple highest weight
U({\mathfrak{g}})
-module with annihilator
I
can be
determined using
[
LMM21
, Proposition 8.2.3]
. Thus Theorem
1.1
completes
the classification of rigid Losev–Premet ideals for
G
of classical type.

Theorem
1.1
can be viewed as an extension
of
[
PT14
, Theorem 5]
and our methods build on the methods developed there,
whilst also making use of recent results from
[
To23
]
.
The proof deduces the theorem from the corresponding statement for finite
W
-algebras.
In Theorem
4.1
we prove that, with the set up in Theorem
1.1
,
any one-dimensional
U({\mathfrak{g}},e)
-module can be obtained
from a one-dimensional
U({\mathfrak{g}}_{0},e_{0})
-module
via the parabolic induction functor introduced by Losev in
[
Lo11
]
.

We give now
a brief overview of the strategy of the proof of Theorem
4.1
. First we need to establish some notation. The variety of one-dimensional
U({\mathfrak{g}},e)
-modules is denoted
{\mathcal{E}}({\mathfrak{g}},e)
, see (
4.2
) for a formal definition. We say that

({\mathfrak{g}}_{0},{\mathbb{O}}_{0})
is a rigid induction datum for
({\mathfrak{g}},{\mathbb{O}})
if
{\mathbb{O}}
is induced from
{\mathbb{O}}_{0}

and
{\mathbb{O}}_{0}
is rigid in
{\mathfrak{g}}_{0}
.

Parabolic induction for finite
W
-algebras gives a finite morphism
{\mathcal{E}}({\mathfrak{g}}_{0},e_{0})\to{\mathcal{E}}({\mathfrak{g}},e)

for any rigid induction datum
({\mathfrak{g}}_{0},{\mathbb{O}}_{e_{0}})
for
({\mathfrak{g}},{\mathbb{O}}_{e})
. In Proposition

5.5
it is shown that the
multiset of dimensions of irreducible components of the disjoint union
\bigsqcup{\mathcal{E}}({\mathfrak{g}}_{i},{\mathbb{O}}_{e_{i}})

over the (
G
-orbits of) rigid induction data
({\mathfrak{g}}_{i},{\mathbb{O}}_{e_{i}})
for
({\mathfrak{g}},{\mathbb{O}}_{e})
is
equal to the multiset of dimensions of irreducible components
of
{\mathcal{E}}({\mathfrak{g}},e)
. Combining this with Proposition
5.4
, which shows that distinct
irreducible components of
\bigsqcup{\mathcal{E}}({\mathfrak{g}}_{i},e_{i})
are mapped by the parabolic induction morphism
to distinct irreducible components of
{\mathcal{E}}({\mathfrak{g}},e)
, we can deduce Theorem
4.1
. An important result
along the way is Theorem
3.3
, which shows that for distinct rigid induction data

({\mathfrak{g}}_{1},{\mathbb{O}}_{e_{1}})
and
({\mathfrak{g}}_{2},{\mathbb{O}}_{e_{2}})
for
({\mathfrak{g}},{\mathbb{O}}_{e})
we have
{\mathfrak{g}}_{1}\not\subseteq{\mathfrak{g}}_{2}
and
{\mathfrak{g}}_{2}\not\subseteq{\mathfrak{g}}_{1}
;
we refer also to Remark
3.4
for the extension of this to any reductive
G
.

We remark that the computer calculations reported in
[
BG18
]
show that the analogue of
Theorem
1.1
for
G
of exceptional type is not true. Those calculations show that there
are Losev–Premet ideals in
U({\mathfrak{g}})
for
G
of type
F_{4}
or
E_{6}
, with associated variety a non-rigid
nilpotent orbit, that are not parabolically induced. It is not known
whether there are such ideals for
G
of type
E_{7}
or
E_{8}
, though this seems quite plausible.

To end this introduction we draw attention to the remarkable recent work of Losev in
[
Lo22
]
and
Losev–Mason-Brown–Matvieievskyi in

[
LMM21
]
. An orbit method for Lie algebras was introduced by Losev in
[
Lo22
, §5]
,
which gives a natural embedding from the set of coadjoint orbits of
G
to
\operatorname{Prim}U({\mathfrak{g}})
.
It was conjectured that the ideals obtained in this way for classical Lie algebras are precisely the Losev–Premet ideals,
and as noted above this has now been proved by the second author in
[
To23
]
. In
[
LMM21
, Theorem 7.8.1]
, Losev’s orbit method is extended to define
an injection from isomorphism classes of
G
-equivariant covers of coadjoint orbits to isomorphism classes of

G
-equivariant filtered quantizations of affinizations of
G
-equivariant covers of nilpotent orbits.
Each of these quantizations gives rise to a primitive ideal, completing Vogan’s orbit method program, which in turn has important applications
to the theory of unipotent representations of Lie groups. Furthermore, their methods give another approach
to understanding Losev–Premet ideals via birationally rigid induction data, see for instance
[
LMM21
, Remark 8.1.2]
.

This paper is organised as follows. In §
2
we recall preliminaries on reductive groups, Levi subgroups, nilpotent orbits, Lusztig-Spaltenstein induction, sheets and primitive ideals; we also use this section to introduce notation used throughout the paper. Then, in §
3
we prove Theorem
3.3
by means of a combinatorial argument involving the Kempken-Spaltenstein algorithm. In §
4
we introduce finite
W
-algebras and establish the framework needed to state Theorem
4.1
; the paper concludes in §
5
with the proof of this theorem.

2.
Reductive groups, Levi subgroups, nilpotent orbits, Lusztig–Spaltenstein induction, sheets and primitive ideals

2.1.
Reductive groups

Throughout this paper
G
is a connected reductive algebraic group over
{\mathbb{C}}
.
We often specialize to the case where
G
is simple of classical type, by which we
mean
G
is one of
\mathrm{SL}_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
,
\mathrm{SO}_{N}({\mathbb{C}})
, or
\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}({\mathbb{C}})
,
where
n,N\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{>0}
.
The results of our paper do not depend on the isogeny class of
G
, which justifies
the restriction to these choices of
G
for types
{\mathsf{A}}
,
{\mathsf{B}}
,
{\mathsf{C}}
and
{\mathsf{D}}
.
As we work entirely over
{\mathbb{C}}
in this paper we allow
ourselves to abbreviate notation and just write
\mathrm{SL}_{n}
,
\mathrm{SO}_{N}
, or
\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}
for
these groups;
we write
{\mathfrak{sl}}_{n}
,
{\mathfrak{so}}_{N}
and
{\mathfrak{sp}}_{2n}
for their Lie algebras.
Explicitly we take
\mathrm{SO}_{N}=\{x\in\mathrm{SL}_{N}\mid x^{\mathrm{tr}}J_{N}x=J_{N}\}
, where

J_{N}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}&&1\\
&\iddots&\\
1&&\end{array}\right)

is the
N\times N
matrix with

(i,j)
entry equal to
\delta_{i,N+1-j}
; and we take
\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}=\{x\in\mathrm{SL}_{2n}\mid x^{\mathrm{tr}}\tilde{J}_{2n}x=\tilde{J}_{2n}\}

where
\tilde{J}_{2n}
has block form

\tilde{J}_{2n}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&J_{n}\\
-J_{n}&0\end{array}\right).

For a closed subgroup
H
of
G
we write
{\mathfrak{h}}=\operatorname{Lie}H
for the Lie algebra of
H
and
{\mathfrak{z}}({\mathfrak{h}})

for the centre of
{\mathfrak{h}}
.
We let
(\cdot\,,\cdot)
be a nondegenerate
G
-invariant symmetric bilinear form on
{\mathfrak{g}}
, which allows
us to identify
{\mathfrak{g}}\cong{\mathfrak{g}}^{*}
as
G
-modules. We have that
G
acts on
{\mathfrak{g}}
via the adjoint action. Let
x\in{\mathfrak{g}}
, let
X\subseteq{\mathfrak{g}}
and let
H
be a subgroup of
G
.
We write
H\cdot x
for the
H
-orbit of
x
, though often use the notation
{\mathbb{O}}_{x}
for
G\cdot x
. The centralizer of
x
in
H
is denoted
H^{x}
and
the centralizer of
x
in
{\mathfrak{h}}
is denoted
{\mathfrak{h}}^{x}
.
We write
H\cdot X
for the
H
-saturation of

X
, i.e.
H\cdot X=\bigcup_{x\in X}H\cdot x
.
The closure of
X
with respect to the Zariski topology on
{\mathfrak{g}}
is denoted
\overline{X}
.

We fix a maximal torus
T
of
G
and a Borel subgroup
B
of
G
with
T\subseteq B
, and let
U

denote the unipotent radical of
B
. We denote by
{\mathfrak{t}}
,
{\mathfrak{b}}
and
{\mathfrak{u}}
the respective Lie algebras of these subgroups.
Let
\Phi
be the root system of
G
with respect to
T
, let
\Phi^{+}
be the set
of positive roots determined by
B
and let
\Pi
be the corresponding set of simple roots.
We write
h_{\alpha}\in{\mathfrak{t}}
for the coroot corresponding to
\alpha\in\Phi
.

The Weyl group of
G
with respect to
T
is denoted
W
.
We choose

\rho\in{\mathfrak{t}}^{*}
such that
\rho(h_{\alpha})=1
for all
\alpha\in\Pi
; for instance we could
take
\rho
to be the half sum of the roots in
\Phi^{+}
.
The
dot action
of
W
on
{\mathfrak{t}}^{*}
is
defined by
w\bullet\lambda:=w(\lambda+\rho)-\rho
, and we note that this does not depend
on the choice of
\rho
.

For
\alpha\in\Phi
, we write
{\mathfrak{g}}_{\alpha}\subseteq{\mathfrak{g}}
for the corresponding root subspace.
Given a closed subgroup
H
of
G
containing
T
, we define
\Phi({\mathfrak{h}})
to be the subset of
\Phi
such that

{\mathfrak{h}}={\mathfrak{t}}\oplus\bigoplus_{\alpha\in\Phi({\mathfrak{h}})}{\mathfrak{g}}_{\alpha}
. Given a subset
\Gamma
of
\Pi
, we define

\Phi_{\Gamma}
to be the root subsystem of
\Phi
generated by
\Gamma
, more precisely

\Phi_{\Gamma}=\{\sum_{\alpha\in\Gamma}a_{\alpha}\alpha\in\Phi\mid a_{\alpha}\in{\mathbb{Z}}\}
.

2.2.
Parabolic subgroups and Levi subgroups

We recall some results about parabolic subgroups and their Levi factors that can be found in
[
CM93
, §3.8]
, for example.

First we recap that a subgroup
P

of
G
is a parabolic subgroup if it contains a conjugate of
B
.
We say that
P
is a standard parabolic subgroup
if
B\subseteq P
, so every parabolic subgroup is conjugate to a standard parabolic
subgroup. For a standard parabolic subgroup
P
with unipotent radical
U_{P}
, there is a subset
\Gamma

of
\Pi
such that
\Phi({\mathfrak{p}})=\Phi_{\Gamma}\sqcup\Phi({\mathfrak{u}}_{P})
, and we use the notation
P=P_{\Gamma}
. For
\Gamma,\Delta\subseteq\Pi
, we have that

P_{\Gamma}
is conjugate to
P_{\Delta}
if and only if
\Gamma=\Delta
. Thus
the conjugacy classes of parabolic subgroups of
G
are in bijection with subsets of
\Pi
.

A parabolic subgroup
P
of
G
has a Levi decomposition
P=G_{0}U_{P}
, where
U_{P}
is the unipotent radical of
P

and
G_{0}
is a Levi factor. By a Levi subgroup of
G
we mean a Levi factor
G_{0}
of some
parabolic subgroup, and we refer to
{\mathfrak{g}}_{0}
as a Levi subalgebra.

We recall that Levi subalgebras can equivalently be defined as centralizers of semisimple elements. More precisely,
for any semisimple
s\in{\mathfrak{g}}
, we have that
{\mathfrak{g}}^{s}
is a Levi subalgebra of
{\mathfrak{g}}
and all Levi subalgebras
are of the form
{\mathfrak{g}}^{s}
for some semisimple
s\in{\mathfrak{g}}
.
In particular, this implies that if
{\mathfrak{g}}_{1}
and
{\mathfrak{g}}_{2}
are Levi subalgebras
of
{\mathfrak{g}}
with
{\mathfrak{g}}_{1}\subseteq{\mathfrak{g}}_{2}
, then
{\mathfrak{g}}_{1}
is a Levi subalgebra of
{\mathfrak{g}}_{2}
.

We recall that for a standard parabolic subgroup
P
there is a unique Levi factor
G_{0}
containing
T
,
which we refer to as a standard Levi subgroup, and then we refer
to
{\mathfrak{g}}_{0}
as a standard Levi subalgebra.
We note that any Levi subgroup is
conjugate to a standard Levi subgroup.
For a standard Levi subgroup
G_{0}
, we have that

\Phi({\mathfrak{g}}_{0})=\Phi_{\Gamma}
for some
\Gamma\subseteq\Pi
, and we use the notation
G_{\Gamma}
for
G_{0}
.
For
\Gamma,\Delta\subseteq\Pi

we have
G_{\Gamma}
is conjugate to
G_{\Delta}
if and only if there exists
w\in W
such that
w\cdot\Gamma=\Delta
.

We make the following observation for later. Suppose that
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
are standard Levi subgroups of
G
with Lie algebras
{\mathfrak{g}}_{1}
and
{\mathfrak{g}}_{2}
respectively, and that there exists a
G
-conjugate
\tilde{\mathfrak{g}}_{1}
of
{\mathfrak{g}}_{1}
such that
\tilde{\mathfrak{g}}_{1}\subseteq{\mathfrak{g}}_{2}
. Then
\tilde{\mathfrak{g}}_{1}
is a Levi subalgebra of
{\mathfrak{g}}_{2}
, and thus is
G_{2}
-conjugate (and hence
G
-conjugate) to a standard Levi subalgebra
\widehat{{\mathfrak{g}}}_{1}
of
{\mathfrak{g}}_{2}
. It is straightforward to see that standard Levi subalgebras of
{\mathfrak{g}}_{2}
are standard Levi subalgebras of
{\mathfrak{g}}
. We therefore conclude that any inclusion of Levi subalgebras of
{\mathfrak{g}}
is
G
-conjugate to an inclusion of standard Levi subalgebras of
{\mathfrak{g}}
.

For a Levi subgroup
G_{0}
, we define

{\mathfrak{z}}({\mathfrak{g}}_{0})^{\mathrm{reg}}:=\{x\in{\mathfrak{z}}({\mathfrak{g}}_{0})\mid({\mathfrak{g}}_{0})^{x}={\mathfrak{g}}_{0}\}
. For
G_{0}=G_{\Gamma}
we have

{\mathfrak{z}}({\mathfrak{g}}_{0})=\{x\in{\mathfrak{t}}\mid\gamma(x)=0\text{ for all }\gamma\in\Gamma\}
and

{\mathfrak{z}}({\mathfrak{g}}_{0})^{\mathrm{reg}}=\{x\in{\mathfrak{t}}\mid\gamma(x)=0\text{ for all }\gamma\in\Gamma\text{ and }\alpha(x)\neq 0\text{ for all }\alpha\in\Pi\setminus\Gamma\}
.

We explain the conjugacy classes of Levi subgroups for
G
of classical type explicitly.
The
W
-orbits of subsets
\Gamma
of
\Pi
are determined by the Dynkin type of
\Phi_{\Gamma}
with root lengths taken into account and
with care needed to not identify
{\mathsf{D}}_{2}
with
{\mathsf{A}}_{1}+{\mathsf{A}}_{1}
or

{\mathsf{D}}_{3}
with
{\mathsf{A}}_{3}
; except in the case where
G
has type
{\mathsf{D}}_{n}
and
\Phi_{\Gamma}
has type
{\mathsf{A}}_{i_{1}-1}+\dots+{\mathsf{A}}_{i_{s}-1}
with all
i_{j}
even and
i_{1}+\dots+i_{s}=n
, in which case there are two
W
-orbits.

Below we give these standard Levi subgroups explicitly, up to conjugacy, when
T
is the maximal torus of diagonal matrices in
G
and
B
is the
Borel subgroup of upper triangular matrices in
G
.
To do this we define an
inc-sequence
to be a sequence
\text{\boldmath$i$}=(i_{1},\dots,i_{s})

with
i_{j}\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{>0}
,
i_{1}\leq\dots\leq i_{s}
.
For an inc-sequence
i
we define
|\text{\boldmath$i$}|=i_{1}+\dots+i_{s}
.

It is easier to explain the standard Levi subgroups
of
{\mathrm{GL}}_{n}
than those of
\mathrm{SL}_{n}
, and these are of the form

{\mathrm{GL}}_{\text{\boldmath$i$}}:={\mathrm{GL}}_{i_{1}}\times\dots\times{\mathrm{GL}}_{i_{s}},

(2.1)

for
i
an inc-sequence with
|\text{\boldmath$i$}|=n
. The standard Levi subgroups of
\mathrm{SL}_{n}
are given by

\mathrm{SL}_{\text{\boldmath$i$}}:={\mathrm{GL}}_{\text{\boldmath$i$}}\cap\mathrm{SL}_{n}

(2.2)

for
i
an inc-sequence with
|\text{\boldmath$i$}|=n
.
For
j
an inc-sequence with
|\text{\boldmath$j$}|=n
, we have that
\mathrm{SL}_{\text{\boldmath$i$}}
is conjugate to

\mathrm{SL}_{\text{\boldmath$j$}}
if and only if
\text{\boldmath$i$}=\text{\boldmath$j$}
.

The standard Levi subgroups of
\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}

are of the form

\mathrm{Sp}_{\text{\boldmath$i$},2n_{\text{\boldmath$i$}}}:={\mathrm{GL}}_{i_{1}}\times\dots\times{\mathrm{GL}}_{i_{s}}\times\mathrm{Sp}_{2n_{\text{\boldmath$i$}}},

(2.3)

for
i
an inc-sequence with
|\text{\boldmath$i$}|\leq n
, and with
n_{\text{\boldmath$i$}}:=n-|\text{\boldmath$i$}|
. For
j
an inc-sequence with
|\text{\boldmath$j$}|\leq n
,
we have that
\mathrm{Sp}_{\text{\boldmath$i$},2n_{\text{\boldmath$i$}}}
is conjugate to

\mathrm{Sp}_{\text{\boldmath$j$},2n_{\text{\boldmath$j$}}}
if and only if
\text{\boldmath$i$}=\text{\boldmath$j$}
.

The standard Levi subgroups of
\mathrm{SO}_{N}

are of the form

\mathrm{SO}_{\text{\boldmath$i$},N_{\text{\boldmath$i$}}}:={\mathrm{GL}}_{i_{1}}\times\dots\times{\mathrm{GL}}_{i_{s}}\times\mathrm{SO}_{N_{\text{\boldmath$i$}}},

(2.4)

where
i
is an inc-sequence with
2|\text{\boldmath$i$}|\leq N
, and with
N_{\text{\boldmath$i$}}:=N-2|\text{\boldmath$i$}|\neq 2
.
In the case that
N=2|\text{\boldmath$i$}|

and all
i_{j}
are even, there are two conjugacy classes of Levi subgroups of the form
\mathrm{SO}_{\text{\boldmath$i$},0}
.
In all other cases, for an inc-sequence
j
with
2|\text{\boldmath$j$}|\leq N
and
N-2|\text{\boldmath$j$}|\neq 2
,
we have that
\mathrm{SO}_{\text{\boldmath$i$},N_{\text{\boldmath$i$}}}
is conjugate to

\mathrm{SO}_{\text{\boldmath$j$},N_{\text{\boldmath$j$}}}
if and only if
\text{\boldmath$i$}=\text{\boldmath$j$}
.
We note that the case
N_{\text{\boldmath$i$}}=2
is excluded as in this case we have
\mathrm{SO}_{2}\cong{\mathrm{GL}}_{1}
, and
then
\mathrm{SO}_{\text{\boldmath$i$},2}
is conjugate to
\mathrm{SO}_{\overline{\text{\boldmath$i$}},0}
,
where
\overline{\text{\boldmath$i$}}
is obtained from
i
by appending a 1 to the start.

We write
{\mathfrak{gl}}_{\text{\boldmath$i$}}
,
{\mathfrak{sl}}_{\text{\boldmath$i$}}
,
{\mathfrak{sp}}_{\text{\boldmath$i$},2n_{\text{\boldmath$i$}}}
and
{\mathfrak{so}}_{\text{\boldmath$i$},N_{\text{\boldmath$i$}}}
for the Lie algebras of
{\mathrm{GL}}_{\text{\boldmath$i$}}
,
\mathrm{SL}_{\text{\boldmath$i$}}
,
\mathrm{Sp}_{\text{\boldmath$i$},2n_{\text{\boldmath$i$}}}
and
\mathrm{SO}_{\text{\boldmath$i$},N_{\text{\boldmath$i$}}}
respectively.

2.3.
Nilpotent orbits

We refer to
G
-orbits of nilpotent elements in
{\mathfrak{g}}
as nilpotent
G
-orbits.
We recap the parameterization of nilpotent orbits for
G
of classical type, as can be found
for instance in
[
CM93
, Chapter 5]
.
To do this we define a
partition

\lambda
to be a sequence

\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\dots)
with finitely many non-zero
\lambda_{i}\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}
,
and
\lambda_{i}\geq\lambda_{i+1}
for each
i
.
Often we write
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{r})

where
r
is maximal such that
\lambda_{r}\neq 0
.
We say that
\lambda
is a partition of
|\lambda|:=\lambda_{1}+\dots+\lambda_{r}
.

For
G=\mathrm{SL}_{n}
the nilpotent orbits are classified by
their Jordan type, which is the partition
\lambda
of
n
giving the
sizes of the Jordan blocks of an element in the orbit.

For
G=\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}
the nilpotent orbits are classified by
their Jordan type, as each nilpotent
{\mathrm{GL}}_{2n}
-orbit that
intersects
{\mathfrak{sp}}_{2n}
does so in a single
\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}
-orbit.
The nilpotent
{\mathrm{GL}}_{2n}
-orbits that have a non-empty intersection with
{\mathfrak{sp}}_{2n}
are those with Jordan type

\lambda
, where
\lambda
is a partition of
2n
such that every odd part of
\lambda
occurs with even multiplicity; we refer to such partitions
as
symplectic partitions
.

For
G=\mathrm{SO}_{N}
, there is a minor complication as the intersection of a
{\mathrm{GL}}_{N}
-orbit
with
{\mathfrak{so}}_{N}
may split into two
\mathrm{SO}_{N}
-orbits; such an intersection is
a single
\mathrm{O}_{N}
-orbit, but splits into two
\mathrm{SO}_{N}
-orbits if the centralizer (in
{\mathrm{GL}}_{N}
)
of an element of the orbit is contained in
\mathrm{SO}_{N}
. The Jordan types
\lambda
of the

{\mathrm{GL}}_{N}
-orbits having non-empty intersection with
{\mathfrak{so}}_{N}
are those
such that every positive even part of
\lambda
occurs with even multiplicity; we refer to such partitions
as
orthogonal partitions
.
This intersection is a single
\mathrm{SO}_{N}
-orbit
except when
\lambda
is a very even partition, i.e. when all parts of
\lambda
are even. For a very even partition
\lambda
, it is customary to label the two
\mathrm{SO}_{N}
-orbits by Roman numerals I and II.

For
G
of classical type and
\lambda
a partition corresponding to a nilpotent
G
-orbit in
{\mathfrak{g}}
, we
use the notation
{\mathbb{O}}_{\lambda}
for this nilpotent orbit. When this notation is used it is always clear
from the context which
G
is being considered, and in the case when
G=\mathrm{SO}_{N}
and
\lambda
is
a very even partition it refers to a fixed choice of one of the two orbits corresponding to this partition.

2.4.
Lusztig–Spaltenstein induction and rigid nilpotent orbits

We recap Lusztig–Spaltenstein induction of nilpotent orbits as introduced in
[
LS79
]
.
In fact in
[
LS79
]
the theory was developed for unipotent classes, but is also
valid for nilpotent classes, see for instance
[
CM93
, Chapter 7]
. We mention that this procedure has been extended from nilpotent orbits to equivariant covers of orbits in
[
LMM21
, §2.3]
.

Let
G_{0}
be a Levi subgroup of
G
and choose a parabolic subgroup
P
with
G_{0}
as
a Levi factor. Then we have the decomposition
{\mathfrak{p}}={\mathfrak{g}}_{0}\oplus{\mathfrak{u}}_{P}
where
{\mathfrak{u}}_{P}
is the
Lie algebra of the unipotent radical of
P
.
Given a nilpotent
G_{0}
-orbit
{\mathbb{O}}_{0}
, there is a unique nilpotent
G
-orbit which
has dense intersection with
{\mathbb{O}}_{0}+{\mathfrak{u}}_{P}
. We denote this orbit by
\operatorname{Ind}_{{\mathfrak{g}}_{0}}^{\mathfrak{g}}({\mathbb{O}}_{0})
, and say that
{\mathbb{O}}
is

Lusztig–Spaltenstein induced
from
{\mathbb{O}}_{0}
. As the notation indicates,
Remarkably, Lusztig–Spaltenstein induction depends only on the Levi subalgebra
{\mathfrak{g}}_{0}
, and not on the parabolic
subalgebra
{\mathfrak{p}}
containing
{\mathfrak{g}}_{0}
.

We note that
G
acts on the set of pairs
({\mathfrak{g}}_{0},{\mathbb{O}}_{0})
, where
{\mathfrak{g}}_{0}
is the Lie algebra of a Levi subgroup
G_{0}
of
G

and
{\mathbb{O}}_{0}
is a nilpotent
G_{0}
-orbit. We denote the
G
-orbit of
({\mathfrak{g}}_{0},{\mathbb{O}}_{0})
by
[{\mathfrak{g}}_{0},{\mathbb{O}}_{0}]
and note that
the induced orbit
\operatorname{Ind}_{{\mathfrak{g}}_{0}}^{\mathfrak{g}}({\mathbb{O}}_{0})
does not depend on the choice of representative for this orbit.
We refer to
[{\mathfrak{g}}_{0},{\mathbb{O}}_{0}]
as an
induction datum
for the induced orbit
{\mathbb{O}}=\operatorname{Ind}_{{\mathfrak{g}}_{0}}^{\mathfrak{g}}({\mathbb{O}}_{0})
. Since there
are finitely many conjugacy classes of Levi subgroups of
G
and finitely many nilpotent orbits for each
Levi subgroup, there are finitely many induction data.

We say that a nilpotent
G
-orbit
{\mathbb{O}}
is
rigid
in
{\mathfrak{g}}
if it cannot be obtained by induction
from a nilpotent orbit in a proper Levi subalgebra. We say that the induction datum
[{\mathfrak{g}}_{0},{\mathbb{O}}_{0}]
is
rigid

if
{\mathbb{O}}_{0}
is rigid in
{\mathfrak{g}}_{0}
. For a fixed nilpotent
G
-orbit
{\mathbb{O}}
, we define the
set of rigid induction data for

{\mathbb{O}}

to be

{\mathcal{R}}({\mathfrak{g}},{\mathbb{O}}):=\{[{\mathfrak{g}}_{0},{\mathbb{O}}_{0}]\mid[{\mathfrak{g}}_{0},{\mathbb{O}}_{0}]\text{ is a rigid induction datum for }{\mathbb{O}}\}.

To state what the rigid induction data are, we need to know
the rigid nilpotent orbits in Levi subalgebras of
{\mathfrak{g}}
.
For this we note that a nilpotent orbit
{\mathbb{O}}_{0}
in a Levi subalgebra
{\mathfrak{g}}_{0}
of
{\mathfrak{g}}
is rigid if and only
if the intersection of
{\mathbb{O}}_{0}
with any simple factor
{\mathfrak{h}}
of
{\mathfrak{g}}_{0}
is rigid in
{\mathfrak{h}}
. Below
we explain what the rigid induction data are for
G
simple of classical type. For this
we use the notation for Levi subgroups given in (
2.2
), (
2.3
) and (
2.4
).

For
G=\mathrm{SL}_{n}
, the only rigid nilpotent orbit is the zero orbit.
Thus the rigid induction data are
[{\mathfrak{sl}}_{\text{\boldmath$i$}},\{0\}]
for
\text{\boldmath$i$}=(i_{1},\dots,i_{s})
an inc-sequence
with
|\text{\boldmath$i$}|=n
. We add here that it is well-known that
\operatorname{Ind}_{{\mathfrak{sl}}_{\text{\boldmath$i$}}}^{{\mathfrak{sl}}_{n}}(\{0\})={\mathbb{O}}_{\text{\boldmath$i$}^{\mathrm{t}}}
, where

\text{\boldmath$i$}^{\mathrm{t}}
is the
dual
of
i
, defined by
(\text{\boldmath$i$}^{t})_{j}=|\{k\in\{1,\dots,s\}\mid i_{k}\geq j\}|
.
In particular, this implies that there is a unique rigid induction datum for each nilpotent orbit
{\mathbb{O}}
in
{\mathfrak{g}}={\mathfrak{sl}}_{n}
.

For
G=\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}
, a symplectic partition
\lambda
of
2n
is the Jordan type of a rigid nilpotent orbit if and only if

\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{i+1}\leq 1
for all
i

and even
\lambda_{i}
do not have multiplicity
2
in
\lambda
; we refer to such
\lambda
as a
rigid symplectic partition
.
The rigid induction data are
[{\mathfrak{sp}}_{\text{\boldmath$i$},2n_{\text{\boldmath$i$}}},\{0\}\times{\mathbb{O}}_{0}]
for
\text{\boldmath$i$}=(i_{1},\dots,i_{s})
an inc-sequence
with
|\text{\boldmath$i$}|\leq n
and
{\mathbb{O}}_{0}
a rigid nilpotent orbit in
{\mathfrak{sp}}_{2n_{\text{\boldmath$i$}}}
. To clarify notation we
explain that
\{0\}\times{\mathbb{O}}_{0}
denotes the nilpotent
\mathrm{Sp}_{\text{\boldmath$i$},2n_{\text{\boldmath$i$}}}
-orbit in
{\mathfrak{sp}}_{\text{\boldmath$i$},2n_{\text{\boldmath$i$}}}

that projects to
\{0\}
in each of the
{\mathfrak{gl}}_{i_{j}}
factors for
j=1,\dots,s
and projects to
{\mathbb{O}}_{0}

in the
{\mathfrak{sp}}_{2n_{\text{\boldmath$i$}}}
factor.

For
G=\mathrm{SO}_{N}
, an orthogonal partition
\lambda
of
N
is the Jordan type of a rigid nilpotent orbit if and only if

\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{i+1}\leq 1
for all
i

and odd
\lambda_{i}
do not have multiplicity
2
in
\lambda
; we refer to such
\lambda
as a
rigid orthogonal partition
.
The rigid induction data are
[{\mathfrak{so}}_{\text{\boldmath$i$},N_{\text{\boldmath$i$}}},\{0\}\times{\mathbb{O}}_{0}]
for
\text{\boldmath$i$}=(i_{1},\dots,i_{s})
an inc-sequence
with
2|\text{\boldmath$i$}|\leq N
and
2|\text{\boldmath$i$}|\neq N-2
, and
{\mathbb{O}}_{0}
a rigid nilpotent orbit in
{\mathfrak{so}}_{N_{\text{\boldmath$i$}}}
.

2.5.
Sheets and decomposition classes

We recap some aspects of the theory of sheets and decomposition classes. The material we
cover here is contained in
[
BK79
]
and
[
Bo81
]
, we also refer to
[
PT14
, §3.1]

for an overview with references.

For each induction datum
[{\mathfrak{g}}_{0},{\mathbb{O}}_{0}]

the corresponding
decomposition class
is

{\mathcal{D}}({\mathfrak{g}}_{0},{\mathbb{O}}_{0}):=G\cdot({\mathfrak{z}}({\mathfrak{g}}_{0})^{{\mathrm{reg}}}+{\mathbb{O}}_{0}).

Decomposition classes give a stratification

{\mathfrak{g}}=\bigsqcup_{[{\mathfrak{g}}_{0},{\mathbb{O}}_{0}]}{\mathcal{D}}({\mathfrak{g}}_{0},{\mathbb{O}}_{0})

where the (disjoint) union is taken over the finitely many induction data for all nilpotent orbits in
{\mathfrak{g}}
.

The
rank strata
of
{\mathfrak{g}}
are defined to be

{\mathfrak{g}}^{(j)}:=\{x\in{\mathfrak{g}}\mid\dim G\cdot x=j\}

for
j\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}
.
The
sheets
of
{\mathfrak{g}}
are the irreducible components of the rank strata.

Each decomposition class
{\mathcal{D}}
is irreducible and there exists
j_{\mathcal{D}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}

such that
{\mathcal{D}}\subseteq{\mathfrak{g}}^{(j_{\mathcal{D}})}
. Therefore, each sheet
contains a dense decomposition class.
Given a decomposition class
{\mathcal{D}}
we let
\overline{{\mathcal{D}}}^{{\mathrm{reg}}}:=\overline{{\mathcal{D}}}\cap{\mathfrak{g}}^{(j_{\mathcal{D}})}

be the union of the orbits of maximal dimension in
\overline{{\mathcal{D}}}
.
Given decomposition classes
{\mathcal{D}}_{1}:={\mathcal{D}}({\mathfrak{g}}_{1},{\mathbb{O}}_{1}),
and
{\mathcal{D}}_{2}:={\mathcal{D}}({\mathfrak{g}}_{2},{\mathbb{O}}_{2})
we have

{\mathcal{D}}_{1}\subseteq\overline{{\mathcal{D}}}_{2}^{{\mathrm{reg}}}
if and only if there are representatives
({\mathfrak{g}}_{1},{\mathbb{O}}_{1})
and
({\mathfrak{g}}_{2},{\mathbb{O}}_{2})
such that

{\mathfrak{g}}_{2}\subseteq{\mathfrak{g}}_{1}
and
{\mathbb{O}}_{2}=\operatorname{Ind}_{{\mathfrak{g}}_{1}}^{{\mathfrak{g}}_{2}}({\mathbb{O}}_{1})
. From this we can deduce that
the sheets of
{\mathfrak{g}}
are precisely the
\overline{{\mathcal{D}}}^{{\mathrm{reg}}}({\mathfrak{g}}_{0},{\mathbb{O}}_{0})
:=
\overline{{\mathcal{D}}({\mathfrak{g}}_{0},{\mathbb{O}}_{0})}^{{\mathrm{reg}}}
as we vary
over all rigid induction data
[{\mathfrak{g}}_{0},{\mathbb{O}}_{0}]
, and moreover that
\operatorname{Ind}_{{\mathfrak{g}}_{0}}^{\mathfrak{g}}{\mathbb{O}}_{0}
is the unique nilpotent
orbit lying in
\overline{{\mathcal{D}}}^{{\mathrm{reg}}}({\mathfrak{g}}_{0},{\mathbb{O}}_{0})
. Finally, we note for future reference that
\dim\overline{{\mathcal{D}}}^{{\mathrm{reg}}}({\mathfrak{g}}_{0},{\mathbb{O}}_{0})=\dim{\mathfrak{z}}({\mathfrak{g}}_{0})+\dim\operatorname{Ind}_{{\mathfrak{g}}_{0}}^{\mathfrak{g}}{\mathbb{O}}_{0}
(see
[
BK79
, Satz 4.5]
).

2.6.
Primitive ideals of universal enveloping algebras and their central characters

We recall the required background on primitive ideals and central characters of the universal
enveloping algebra
U({\mathfrak{g}})
of
{\mathfrak{g}}
. We refer to
[
BJ01
]
as a general reference
on primitive ideals and to
[
Hu08
, Chapter 1]
as a general reference for
central characters.

An ideal
I
of
U({\mathfrak{g}})
is called
primitive
if
I=\operatorname{Ann}_{U({\mathfrak{g}})}(E)
for some simple
U({\mathfrak{g}})
-module
E
,
where
\operatorname{Ann}_{U({\mathfrak{g}})}(E)
denotes the annihilator of
E
in
U({\mathfrak{g}})
.
We denote the spectrum of primitive ideals by
\operatorname{Prim}U({\mathfrak{g}})
. An ideal
I
of
U({\mathfrak{g}})

is called
completely prime
if
U({\mathfrak{g}})/I
is a domain. The spectrum of completely
prime primitive ideals is denoted
\operatorname{Prim}^{1}U({\mathfrak{g}})
.

The Poincaré–Birkhoff–Witt (PBW) filtration of
U({\mathfrak{g}})
induces a filtration on an ideal
I

of
U({\mathfrak{g}})
and we write
\operatorname{gr}I\subseteq\operatorname{gr}U({\mathfrak{g}})\cong S({\mathfrak{g}})
for the associated graded ideal.
We identify
S({\mathfrak{g}})
with
{\mathbb{C}}[{\mathfrak{g}}]
via our choice of nondegenerate invariant symmetric bilinear form

(\cdot\,,\cdot)
on
{\mathfrak{g}}
. We recall that the
associated
variety

{\mathcal{VA}}(I)
of an ideal
I
of
U({\mathfrak{g}})
is the vanishing locus in
{\mathfrak{g}}
of
\operatorname{gr}I\subseteq{\mathbb{C}}[{\mathfrak{g}}]
.
As mentioned in the introduction it is known that for
I\in\operatorname{Prim}U({\mathfrak{g}})
we have that
{\mathcal{VA}}(I)
is
the closure of a nilpotent orbit in
{\mathfrak{g}}
by Joseph’s irreducibility theorem
[
Jo85
]
.
For a nilpotent
G
-orbit
{\mathbb{O}}
we define
\operatorname{Prim}_{\mathbb{O}}U({\mathfrak{g}})=\{I\in\operatorname{Prim}(U({\mathfrak{g}}))\mid{\mathcal{VA}}(I)=\overline{{\mathbb{O}}}\}

and
\operatorname{Prim}^{1}_{\mathbb{O}}U({\mathfrak{g}})=\operatorname{Prim}^{1}U({\mathfrak{g}})\cap\operatorname{Prim}_{\mathbb{O}}U({\mathfrak{g}})
.

Let
P
be a parabolic subgroup of
G
, let
G_{0}
be a Levi factor of

P
, and let
U_{P}
be the unipotent radical of
P
. We have the decomposition
{\mathfrak{p}}={\mathfrak{g}}_{0}\oplus{\mathfrak{u}}_{P}
.
Let
I_{0}\in\operatorname{Prim}U({\mathfrak{g}}_{0})
and choose a simple
U({\mathfrak{g}}_{0})
-module
E
such that

I_{0}=\operatorname{Ann}_{U({\mathfrak{g}}_{0})}(E)\subseteq U({\mathfrak{g}}_{0})
.

We define the induced ideal

\operatorname{Ind}_{{\mathfrak{p}}}^{\mathfrak{g}}(I_{0}):=\operatorname{Ann}_{U({\mathfrak{g}})}(U({\mathfrak{g}})\otimes_{U({\mathfrak{p}})}E)

where
E
is viewed as a
U({\mathfrak{p}})
-module via the projection
U({\mathfrak{p}})\twoheadrightarrow U({\mathfrak{g}}_{0})
.
It is well-known and straightforward to show that the induced ideal
\operatorname{Ind}_{{\mathfrak{p}}}^{\mathfrak{g}}(I_{0})

can also be described as the largest
two-sided ideal contained in the left ideal
U({\mathfrak{g}})(I_{0}+{\mathfrak{u}}_{P})
.
From this alternative description it follows that
\operatorname{Ind}_{{\mathfrak{p}}}^{\mathfrak{g}}(I_{0})
does not depend
on the choice of simple
U({\mathfrak{g}}_{0})
-module
E
with
I_{0}=\operatorname{Ann}_{U({\mathfrak{g}}_{0})}(E)
.
(We remark that there appears to be no general result in the literature about the dependence of

\operatorname{Ind}_{{\mathfrak{p}}}^{\mathfrak{g}}(I_{0})
on the choice of parabolic subalgebra
{\mathfrak{p}}
with
Levi factor
{\mathfrak{g}}_{0}
.)

The induced ideal
\operatorname{Ind}_{{\mathfrak{p}}}^{\mathfrak{g}}(I_{0})
is in general not primitive. However,
if
I_{0}\in\operatorname{Prim}^{1}U({\mathfrak{g}}_{0})
, then
\operatorname{Ind}_{{\mathfrak{p}}}^{\mathfrak{g}}(I_{0})\in\operatorname{Prim}^{1}U({\mathfrak{g}})
as is proved in

[
Co74
, Theorem 3.1]
.

Although
\operatorname{Ind}_{\mathfrak{p}}^{\mathfrak{g}}(I_{0})
is not primitive in general
its associated variety is the closure of a nilpotent orbit and is given by

{\mathcal{VA}}(\operatorname{Ind}_{\mathfrak{p}}^{\mathfrak{g}}(I_{0}))=\overline{\operatorname{Ind}_{{\mathfrak{g}}_{0}}^{\mathfrak{g}}({\mathbb{O}}_{0})}

(2.5)

where
\overline{{\mathbb{O}}}_{0}={\mathcal{VA}}(I_{0})
. A proof of (
2.5
) is given in
within the proof of
[
BJ01
, Lemma 6.1]
and another proof
using results of Losev is explained within
[
BG18
, §5]
.

Let
{\mathbb{O}}_{0}
be a nilpotent
G_{0}
-orbit and let
{\mathbb{O}}=\operatorname{Ind}_{{\mathfrak{g}}_{0}}^{\mathfrak{g}}({\mathbb{O}}_{0})
.
From the discussion above it follows that parabolic induction restricts to a map

\operatorname{Ind}_{\mathfrak{p}}^{\mathfrak{g}}:\operatorname{Prim}^{1}_{{\mathbb{O}}_{0}}U({\mathfrak{g}}_{0})\to\operatorname{Prim}^{1}_{{\mathbb{O}}}U({\mathfrak{g}}).

We write
Z({\mathfrak{g}})
for the centre of
U({\mathfrak{g}})
, and refer to a character
\chi:Z({\mathfrak{g}})\to{\mathbb{C}}

as a
central character
of
U({\mathfrak{g}})
. By Quillen’s lemma we know that

Z({\mathfrak{g}})
acts on a simple
U({\mathfrak{g}})
-module
E
by a central character

\chi_{E}:Z({\mathfrak{g}})\to{\mathbb{C}}
. Thus,
\operatorname{Ann}_{U({\mathfrak{g}})}(E)\cap Z({\mathfrak{g}})
is an ideal
of
Z({\mathfrak{g}})
of codimension
1
. Setting
I=\operatorname{Ann}_{U({\mathfrak{g}})}(E)
, we write
\chi_{I}:Z({\mathfrak{g}})\to{\mathbb{C}}
for the corresponding central character.
It is then clear that
\chi_{I}=\chi_{E^{\prime}}
for any simple
U({\mathfrak{g}})
-module
E^{\prime}
with
I=\operatorname{Ann}_{U({\mathfrak{g}})}(E^{\prime})
.

We write
U({\mathfrak{g}})_{0}
for the subalgebra of
U({\mathfrak{g}})
given by
the zero weight space for the adjoint action of
{\mathfrak{t}}
on
U({\mathfrak{g}})
. Then

Z({\mathfrak{g}})\subseteq U({\mathfrak{g}})_{0}
. We have that
U({\mathfrak{g}})_{0}=S({\mathfrak{t}})\oplus(U({\mathfrak{g}}){\mathfrak{u}}\cap U({\mathfrak{g}})_{0})
with
U({\mathfrak{g}}){\mathfrak{u}}\cap U({\mathfrak{g}})_{0}
being
a 2-sided ideal of
U({\mathfrak{g}})_{0}
, where we recall that
{\mathfrak{u}}
is the Lie algebra of the unipotent radical of
B
.
Thus the projection
U({\mathfrak{g}})_{0}\to S({\mathfrak{t}})
defined from the decomposition
U({\mathfrak{g}})_{0}=S({\mathfrak{t}})\oplus(U({\mathfrak{g}}){\mathfrak{u}}\cap U({\mathfrak{g}})_{0})
is a
homomorphism. The homomorphism
Z({\mathfrak{g}})\to S({\mathfrak{t}})
given
by restriction is known to be an isomorphism onto its image and this image is

S({\mathfrak{t}})^{W,\bullet}:=\{u\in S({\mathfrak{t}})\mid w\bullet u=u\text{ for all }w\in W\}
, where to define the action of
W
on
S({\mathfrak{t}})
we identity
S({\mathfrak{t}})
with the algebra of polynomial functions on
{\mathfrak{t}}^{*}
and
recall the dot action on
{\mathfrak{t}}^{*}
as defined in §
2.1
.
This isomorphism
\psi:Z({\mathfrak{g}})\to S({\mathfrak{t}})^{W,\bullet}

is called the Harish-Chandra isomorphism. Thus as
S({\mathfrak{t}})^{W,\bullet}\cong{\mathbb{C}}[{\mathfrak{t}}^{*}/(W,\bullet)]
, where
{\mathfrak{t}}^{*}/(W,\bullet)
denotes
the quotient of
{\mathfrak{t}}^{*}
under the dot-action of
W
, we may identify central characters of primitive ideals
of
U({\mathfrak{g}})
with
(W,\bullet)
-orbits in
{\mathfrak{t}}^{*}
.
We write

\operatorname{ch}:\operatorname{Prim}U({\mathfrak{g}})\to{\mathfrak{t}}^{*}/(W,\bullet)

for this central character map.

In fact the map
\operatorname{ch}

can be extended to all ideals
I
of
U({\mathfrak{g}})
for which

I\cap Z({\mathfrak{g}})
is of codimension
1
in
Z({\mathfrak{g}})
; such ideals are said
to
admit a central character
.
Given a highest weight
U({\mathfrak{g}})
-module
M
,
we can see that the action of
Z({\mathfrak{g}})
on a highest weight vector of
M

must be given by a central character, and thus
Z({\mathfrak{g}})
acts on all of
M
by this central character.
A useful observation is that the map
\operatorname{ch}
is set up so that if
I=\operatorname{Ann}_{U({\mathfrak{g}})}(M)
where
M
is a highest
weight
U({\mathfrak{g}})
-module with highest weight
\lambda\in{\mathfrak{t}}^{*}
with respect to
{\mathfrak{b}}
,
then
\operatorname{ch}(I)=W\bullet\lambda
.

We now suppose that
P
is a standard parabolic subgroup of
G
and
G_{0}
is the standard Levi factor
of
P
.
We note that
B_{0}:=B\cap G_{0}
is a Borel subgroup of
G_{0}
.
The Weyl group of
G_{0}
with respect to
T
is denoted
W_{0}
.

We have the Harish-Chandra isomorphism
\psi_{0}:Z({\mathfrak{g}}_{0})\to S({\mathfrak{t}})^{W_{0},\bullet}
; here we note that
we can use the same
\rho
for
G_{0}
as was used for
G
to define the dot action of
W_{0}
.
Therefore, we have a central character map

\operatorname{ch}_{0}:\operatorname{Prim}U({\mathfrak{g}}_{0})\to{\mathfrak{t}}^{*}/(W_{0},\bullet).

We write

\pi:{\mathfrak{t}}^{*}/(W_{0},\bullet)\twoheadrightarrow{\mathfrak{t}}^{*}/(W,\bullet)

for the projection map.

Let
I_{0}\in\operatorname{Prim}U({\mathfrak{g}}_{0})
. By Duflo’s theorem we can assume
I_{0}=\operatorname{Ann}_{U({\mathfrak{g}}_{0})}(L_{0}(\lambda))
for some
\lambda\in{\mathfrak{t}}^{*}
,
where

denotes the simple highest weight
U({\mathfrak{g}}_{0})
-module with highest weight

with respect
to the Borel subalgebra
{\mathfrak{b}}_{0}

\operatorname{ch}_{0}(I_{0})=W_{0}\bullet\lambda
. Let

, so we have

I=\operatorname{Ann}_{U({\mathfrak{g}})}(U({\mathfrak{g}})\otimes_{U({\mathfrak{p}})}L_{0}(\lambda))
. Since
U({\mathfrak{g}})\otimes_{U({\mathfrak{p}})}L_{0}(\lambda)
is a highest weight
U({\mathfrak{g}})
-module
of highest weight
\lambda

\operatorname{ch}(I)=W\bullet\lambda
. Summarising this discussion we have that

\pi(\operatorname{ch}_{0}(I_{0}))=\operatorname{ch}(\operatorname{Ind}_{\mathfrak{p}}^{\mathfrak{g}}(I_{0})).

3.
On Lusztig–Spaltenstein induction for simple algebraic groups of classical type

For this section we restrict to the case where
G
is simple of classical type, and in fact restrict just to
G=\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}

or
G=\mathrm{SO}_{N}
until Theorem
3.3
.
Our main goal is to prove Theorem
3.3
, which gives
an important property about rigid induction data and is a key ingredient in our proof of
Theorem
4.1
.

Central to our approach is the Kempken–Spaltenstein algorithm (or KS-algorithm for short)
which was introduced in
[
PT14
, §3.1]
and determines
the rigid induction data attached to a nilpotent orbit in
{\mathfrak{g}}
.
We give an overview of this algorithm for the case
G=\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}
and then
explain the minor adaptations required for
G=\mathrm{SO}_{N}
. We refer to

[
PT14
, §3–4]
for all the claims about the KS-algorithm made below.
We begin with a symplectic partition
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{r})
of
2n
. Then, as we
define and explain next, the KS algorithm makes a sequence of reductions to
\lambda
(called type 1 reductions and type 2 reductions) corresponding to an admissible sequence

\text{\boldmath$i$}=(i_{1},\dots,i_{s})
, where
1\leq i_{j}\leq r
for each
j=1,\dots,s
.

We say that a
type 1 reduction
can be made to
\lambda
at position
i
if

\lambda_{i}\geq\lambda_{i+1}+2
. Applying this reduction gives a partition
\lambda^{i}
of
2n-2i
defined by

\lambda^{i}_{j}=\begin{cases}\lambda_{j}-2&\text{if $j\leq i$,}\\
\lambda_{j}&\text{if $j>i$}.\end{cases}

We say that a
type 2 reduction
can be made to
\lambda
at position
i
if
\lambda_{i}
is even and

\lambda_{i-1}>\lambda_{i}=\lambda_{i+1}>\lambda_{i+2}
(here we consider the inequality
\lambda_{0}>\lambda_{1}
to automatically hold). Applying this reduction gives a partition
\lambda^{i}
of
2n-2i
defined by

\lambda^{i}_{j}=\begin{cases}\lambda_{j}-2&\text{if $j<i$,}\\
\lambda_{j}-1&\text{if $i\leq j\leq i+1$,}\\
\lambda_{j}&\text{if $j>i+1$}.\end{cases}

We note that at a given position
i
, it is not possible that both a type 1 reduction and a type 2 reduction can be made to
\lambda
,
so there is no ambiguity in the notation
\lambda^{i}
. We say that
i
is an
admissible position

for
\lambda
if a type 1 or type 2 reduction can be made to
\lambda
at position
i
.

We define recursively what it means for
\text{\boldmath$i$}=(i_{1},\dots,i_{s})
to be an
admissible sequence
for
\lambda
. First we introduce the notation that for
k=1,\dots,s
we write
\text{\boldmath$i$}_{k}=(i_{1},\dots i_{k})
. We say that
\text{\boldmath$i$}_{1}
is an admissible sequence for
\lambda
if
i_{1}
is an admissible position for
\lambda
; we then set
\lambda^{\text{\boldmath$i$}_{1}}=\lambda^{i_{1}}
.
We then recursively say that
\text{\boldmath$i$}_{k}
is an admissible sequence for
\lambda
, and define
\lambda^{\text{\boldmath$i$}_{k}}:=(\lambda^{\text{\boldmath$i$}_{k-1}})^{i_{k}}
, if
i_{k}
is an admissible position for
\lambda^{\text{\boldmath$i$}_{k-1}}
. We say that
i
is a
maximal admissible sequence
if there is
no admissible position for
\lambda^{\text{\boldmath$i$}}
.

To summarise, the KS-algorithm takes as input a symplectic partition
\lambda

and an admissible sequence
i
, and outputs the symplectic partition
\lambda^{\text{\boldmath$i$}}
.
The sequence of type 1 and type 2 reductions given by
i
are used to determine
\lambda^{\text{\boldmath$i$}}
.

To explain the significance of the outcome of this algorithm we require some notation.
We fix an admissible sequence
\text{\boldmath$i$}=(i_{1},\dots,i_{s})
for
\lambda
. We write
{\mathbb{O}}_{\lambda}

for the nilpotent orbit in
{\mathfrak{g}}={\mathfrak{sp}}_{2n}
with Jordan type
\lambda
and
{\mathbb{O}}_{\lambda^{\text{\boldmath$i$}}}

for the nilpotent orbit in
{\mathfrak{sp}}_{2n_{\text{\boldmath$i$}}}
with Jordan type
\lambda^{\text{\boldmath$i$}}
.
The upshot of the KS-algorithm is that

[{\mathfrak{sp}}_{\text{\boldmath$i$},2n_{\text{\boldmath$i$}}},\{0\}\times{\mathbb{O}}_{\lambda^{\text{\boldmath$i$}}}]
is an induction datum for
{\mathbb{O}}_{\lambda}
, see
[
PT14
, Proposition 7]
.
Here we note that although in (
2.3
) we just consider
the definition of
{\mathfrak{sp}}_{\text{\boldmath$i$},2n_{\text{\boldmath$i$}}}
for
i
an inc-sequence, the definition makes sense for any
admissible sequence
i
.
Furthermore,
\{0\}\times{\mathbb{O}}_{\lambda^{\text{\boldmath$i$}}}
is a rigid nilpotent orbit in
{\mathfrak{sp}}_{\text{\boldmath$i$},2n_{\text{\boldmath$i$}}}

if and only if
i
is a maximal admissible sequence. Moreover, every rigid induction datum for
{\mathbb{O}}_{\lambda}

is of the form
[{\mathfrak{sp}}_{\text{\boldmath$i$},2n_{\text{\boldmath$i$}}},\{0\}\times{\mathbb{O}}_{\lambda^{\text{\boldmath$i$}}}]
for some
maximal admissible sequence
i
. Thus to obtain all rigid induction data for
{\mathbb{O}}_{\lambda}

we can apply the KS-algorithm for all maximal admissible sequences for
\lambda
.

Now we discuss the modification needed to the algorithm for
G=\mathrm{SO}_{N}
. This is simply that
we replace “even” with “odd” in the description of a type 2 reduction. With this version of the algorithm
all of the results given in the symplectic case above have analogues for the orthogonal case. In particular,
a maximal admissible sequence
\text{\boldmath$i$}=(i_{1},\dots,i_{s})
gives a rigid induction datum

[{\mathfrak{so}}_{\text{\boldmath$i$},N_{\text{\boldmath$i$}}},\{0\}\times{\mathbb{O}}_{\lambda^{\text{\boldmath$i$}}}]
for
{\mathbb{O}}_{\lambda}
, where
now
{\mathbb{O}}_{\lambda}
is a nilpotent orbit in
{\mathfrak{so}}_{N}
and
{\mathbb{O}}_{\lambda^{\text{\boldmath$i$}}}
is a nilpotent orbit in

{\mathfrak{so}}_{N_{\text{\boldmath$i$}}}
; and, moreover, all rigid induction data are obtained in this way.

There is a subtlety in the orthogonal case
that if there are two
\mathrm{SO}_{N}
-orbits of Jordan type
\lambda
, then either there are two
\mathrm{SO}_{N_{\text{\boldmath$i$}}}
-orbits
of Jordan type
\lambda^{\text{\boldmath$i$}}
, or there are two conjugacy classes of Levi subgroups
\mathrm{SO}_{\text{\boldmath$i$},N_{\text{\boldmath$i$}}}
. In these
cases it is implicit that we have to choose the correct
\mathrm{SO}_{\text{\boldmath$i$},N_{\text{\boldmath$i$}}}
-orbits with Jordan type
\lambda^{\text{\boldmath$i$}}
, or the
correct
\mathrm{SO}_{N}
-conjugacy class of Levi subalgebras of the form
{\mathfrak{so}}_{\text{\boldmath$i$},N_{\text{\boldmath$i$}}}
.

We recall a property of the KS-algorithm that is important in this paper
as it reduces the number of maximal admissible sequences that have to be considered. In the
statement
\lambda
is a symplectic partition or an orthogonal partition depending on which case is being
considered.

KS-property 1
.

Any reordering of the entries in a maximal admissible
sequence
i
for
\lambda
gives a maximal admissible sequence for
\lambda
, and
the partition
\lambda^{\text{\boldmath$i$}}
does not depend on the order the entries of
i
.

This property follows from
[
PT14
, Corollary 7]
and
[
PT14
, Proposition 7]
.
We remark here that for the orthogonal case the statement of
[
PT14
, Corollary 7]
excludes the
possibility that
N_{\text{\boldmath$i$}}=2
. We note, however, that in the case
N_{\text{\boldmath$i$}}=2
, we have
\lambda^{\text{\boldmath$i$}}=(1^{2})
and
we can make a final type 2 reduction at position
1
, and thus
i
is not a maximal admissible
sequence.

As a consequence of KS-property
1
we can
restrict to considering maximal admissible sequences
i
that are inc-sequences, and
we refer to such
i
as a
maximal admissible inc-sequence
.

Before embarking on the proof of Lemma
3.2
,
which provides the main step for the proof of Theorem
3.3
,
we choose to first include an example. This should be helpful for the reader
as the proof of Lemma
3.2

is combinatorial and a little technical,
and the example demonstrates some of the ideas in the proof.

We pick out a key property of the KS-algorithm, which is demonstrated in Example

.

KS-property 2
.

Let
\lambda
be a symplectic partition or an orthogonal partition (depending on which case we are considering).
Let
i
be a maximal admissible inc-sequence
for
\lambda
, and let
i\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 1}
with
i_{1}\geq i
.
Suppose that
\lambda_{i}=\lambda_{i+1}+2
. Then
i_{1}=i
or
i_{1}=i+1
, and moreover, if
i_{1}=i+1
and
k
is maximal such that
i_{k}=i+1
, then the
k
th reduction determined by
i
is of type 2.

Proof.

Since
i
is a maximal admissible sequence, we must have
\lambda^{\text{\boldmath$i$}}_{i}\leq\lambda^{\text{\boldmath$i$}}_{i+1}+1

and thus that
\lambda^{\text{\boldmath$i$}_{j}}_{i}-\lambda^{\text{\boldmath$i$}_{j}}_{i+1}<\lambda^{\text{\boldmath$i$}_{j-1}}_{i}-\lambda^{\text{\boldmath$i$}_{j-1}}_{i+1}

for some
j
. The only way we can get that
\lambda^{\text{\boldmath$i$}_{j}}_{i}-\lambda^{\text{\boldmath$i$}_{j}}_{i+1}<\lambda^{\text{\boldmath$i$}_{j-1}}_{i}-\lambda^{\text{\boldmath$i$}_{j-1}}_{i+1}
is for
i_{j}=i
, or for
i_{j}=i+1
corresponding to a type 2 reduction.
Since
i
is an admissible inc-sequence we deduce that
i_{1}=i
or
i_{1}=i+1
. Furthermore, in the latter case
there must be a type 2 reduction at position
i+1
, which implies that for the maximal
k
such that

i_{k}=i+1
, we have that the reduction corresponding to
i_{k}
is of type 2.
∎

We move on to state and prove Lemma
3.2
, which covers a key special case of Theorem
3.3
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
r\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{>0}
.

(a)

Let
G=\mathrm{Sp}_{r(r+1)}
and let
\lambda=(2r,2r-2,\dots,4,2)
. Let
[{\mathfrak{g}}_{1},{\mathbb{O}}_{1}]
and

[{\mathfrak{g}}_{2},{\mathbb{O}}_{2}]
be rigid induction data for
{\mathbb{O}}_{\lambda}
, and let
({\mathfrak{g}}_{1},{\mathbb{O}}_{1})
and
({\mathfrak{g}}_{2},{\mathbb{O}}_{2})
be representatives thereof.
Suppose that
{\mathfrak{g}}_{1}\subseteq{\mathfrak{g}}_{2}
. Then
{\mathfrak{g}}_{1}={\mathfrak{g}}_{2}
and
{\mathbb{O}}_{1}={\mathbb{O}}_{2}
.

(b)

Let
G=\mathrm{SO}_{r^{2}}
and let
\lambda=(2r-1,2r-3,\dots,3,1)
. Let
[{\mathfrak{g}}_{1},{\mathbb{O}}_{1}]
and

[{\mathfrak{g}}_{2},{\mathbb{O}}_{2}]
be rigid induction data for
{\mathbb{O}}_{\lambda}
, and let
({\mathfrak{g}}_{1},{\mathbb{O}}_{1})
and
({\mathfrak{g}}_{2},{\mathbb{O}}_{2})
be representatives thereof.
Suppose that
{\mathfrak{g}}_{1}\subseteq{\mathfrak{g}}_{2}
. Then
{\mathfrak{g}}_{1}={\mathfrak{g}}_{2}
and
{\mathbb{O}}_{1}={\mathbb{O}}_{2}
.

Proof.

(a) We assume that
r>3
as the case
r=3
is covered in Example
3.1
, and the cases
r=1
and
r=2

can be dealt with easily.

Arguing as in §
2.2
and Example
3.1
, we may assume that
{\mathfrak{g}}_{1}
and
{\mathfrak{g}}_{2}
are
standard Levi subalgebras with

{\mathfrak{g}}_{1}
contained in
{\mathfrak{g}}_{2}
.
There are maximal admissible inc-sequences
\text{\boldmath$i$}=(i_{1},\dots,i_{s})
and
\text{\boldmath$j$}=(j_{1},\dots,j_{t})
such that

{\mathfrak{g}}_{1}={\mathfrak{sp}}_{\text{\boldmath$i$},2n_{\text{\boldmath$i$}}}
and
{\mathbb{O}}_{1}={\mathbb{O}}_{\lambda^{\text{\boldmath$i$}}}
, and
{\mathfrak{g}}_{2}={\mathfrak{sp}}_{\text{\boldmath$j$},2n_{\text{\boldmath$j$}}}
and
{\mathbb{O}}_{2}={\mathbb{O}}_{\lambda^{\text{\boldmath$j$}}}
. As explained in §
2.2
,

{\mathfrak{g}}_{1}
is a standard Levi subalgebra of
{\mathfrak{g}}_{2}
. Now by considering the form of Levi subalgebras given in (
2.1
) and (
2.3
)
we deduce that there is a function
h:\{1,\dots,s\}\to\{1,\dots,t,t+1\}
such that for each

we have

\sum_{l\in h^{-1}(k)}i_{l}=j_{k}
.
The idea here is that the inclusion

{\mathfrak{g}}_{1}\subseteq{\mathfrak{g}}_{2}
is given by inclusions
\bigoplus_{l\in h^{-1}(k)}{\mathfrak{gl}}_{i_{l}}\subseteq{\mathfrak{gl}}_{j_{k}}
and an inclusion
(\bigoplus_{l\in h^{-1}(t+1)}{\mathfrak{gl}}_{i_{l}})\oplus{\mathfrak{sp}}_{2n_{\text{\boldmath$i$}}}\subseteq{\mathfrak{sp}}_{2n_{\text{\boldmath$j$}}}
.
Moreover, for such a function

we may (and shall) assume that for

with

we have

.

We prove by induction on

that
\text{\boldmath$i$}_{k}=\text{\boldmath$j$}_{k}
and that

for

. For the case

this implies that
\text{\boldmath$i$}=\text{\boldmath$j$}
and thus that
{\mathfrak{g}}_{1}={\mathfrak{g}}_{2}
and
{\mathbb{O}}_{1}={\mathbb{O}}_{2}
as required

(i_{1},i_{2},i_{3})
and the possibilities for
(j_{1},j_{2},j_{3})
.
The analysis of cases below is very similar to that in Example
3.1
.

By KS-property
2

i_{1}=1
or

Case

We strengthen Lemma

in Theorem

. For the proof
of this theorem it is helpful to pick out the following useful property of the KS-algorithm.

KS-property 3
.

Let
\lambda
be a symplectic partition or an orthogonal partition depending on which case we are considering.
Let
i
be a maximal admissible inc-sequence
for
\lambda
, and let
i\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 1}
with
i_{1}\geq i
.
Suppose that
\lambda_{i}>\lambda_{i+1}+2
. Then
i_{1}=i
.

Proof.

Suppose
i_{1}>i
. For
j\in\{1,\dots,s\}
the only way we can have that

\lambda^{\text{\boldmath$i$}_{j}}_{i}-\lambda^{\text{\boldmath$i$}_{j}}_{i+1}<\lambda^{\text{\boldmath$i$}_{j-1}}_{i}-\lambda^{\text{\boldmath$i$}_{j-1}}_{i+1}

is if
i_{j}=i+1
and this corresponds to a type 2 reduction in the KS-algorithm. Then
we have
\lambda^{\text{\boldmath$i$}_{j}}_{i}-\lambda^{\text{\boldmath$i$}_{j}}_{i+1}=\lambda^{\text{\boldmath$i$}_{j-1}}_{i}-\lambda^{\text{\boldmath$i$}_{j-1}}_{i+1}-1
.
By considering the parity of
\lambda^{\text{\boldmath$i$}_{j}}_{i+1}
there can be only
one such type 2 reduction. Thus we have that
\lambda^{\text{\boldmath$i$}}_{i}\geq\lambda^{\text{\boldmath$i$}}_{i+1}+2
, which
is contrary to
\lambda^{\text{\boldmath$i$}}
being a rigid partition. Hence, we can conclude that
i_{1}=i
.

We are now ready to state and prove Theorem
3.3
and note that
the idea of proof of the theorem is to reduce to a case given in Lemma
3.2
.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
G
be a simple algebraic group of classical type and let
{\mathbb{O}}
be a nilpotent
G
-orbit. Let
[{\mathfrak{g}}_{1},{\mathbb{O}}_{1}]

and
[{\mathfrak{g}}_{2},{\mathbb{O}}_{2}]
be rigid induction data for
{\mathbb{O}}
, and let
({\mathfrak{g}}_{1},{\mathbb{O}}_{1})
and
({\mathfrak{g}}_{2},{\mathbb{O}}_{2})
be representatives thereof.
Suppose that
{\mathfrak{g}}_{1}\subseteq{\mathfrak{g}}_{2}
. Then
{\mathfrak{g}}_{1}={\mathfrak{g}}_{2}
and
{\mathbb{O}}_{1}={\mathbb{O}}_{2}
.

Proof.

We first cover the case
G=\mathrm{SL}_{n}
. There is a unique rigid induction datum
[{\mathfrak{g}}_{0},{\mathbb{O}}_{0}]
for
{\mathbb{O}}
,
as explained in §
2.4
. Thus trivially
{\mathfrak{g}}_{1}={\mathfrak{g}}_{2}
and
{\mathbb{O}}_{1}={\mathbb{O}}_{2}
in this case.

We move on to consider the case
G=\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}
so
{\mathfrak{g}}={\mathfrak{sp}}_{2n}
.
Let
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},...,\lambda_{r})
be the symplectic partition such that
{\mathbb{O}}={\mathbb{O}}_{\lambda}
.

By
[
To23
, Lemma 8.4]
we can find
\text{\boldmath$j$}=(j_{1},\dots,j_{t})
such that the orbit

\{0\}\times{\mathbb{O}}_{\lambda}
in
{\mathfrak{sp}}_{\text{\boldmath$j$},2n}={\mathfrak{gl}}_{\text{\boldmath$j$}}\times{\mathfrak{g}}
induces to an orbit
{\mathbb{O}}_{\tilde{\lambda}}
in
{\mathfrak{sp}}_{2|\text{\boldmath$j$}|+2n}
such that the symplectic partition
\tilde{\lambda}=(\tilde{\lambda}_{1},...,\tilde{\lambda}_{\tilde{r}})
of
2|\text{\boldmath$j$}|+2n
satisfies that
\tilde{\lambda}_{i}
is even for all
i
and

\tilde{\lambda}_{i}>\tilde{\lambda}_{i+1}
for all
i=1,...,\tilde{r}
.
We have that
{\mathfrak{gl}}_{\text{\boldmath$j$}}\times{\mathfrak{g}}_{1}
and
{\mathfrak{gl}}_{\text{\boldmath$j$}}\times{\mathfrak{g}}_{2}
are Levi subalgebras of
{\mathfrak{sp}}_{\text{\boldmath$j$},2n}
and thus
Levi subalgebras of
{\mathfrak{sp}}_{2|\text{\boldmath$j$}|+2n}
. It follows that
[{\mathfrak{gl}}_{\text{\boldmath$j$}}\times{\mathfrak{g}}_{1},\{0\}\times{\mathbb{O}}_{1}]
and

[{\mathfrak{gl}}_{\text{\boldmath$j$}}\times{\mathfrak{g}}_{2},\{0\}\times{\mathbb{O}}_{2}]
are rigid induction data for
{\mathbb{O}}_{\tilde{\lambda}}
.

We have
{\mathfrak{gl}}_{\text{\boldmath$j$}}\times{\mathfrak{g}}_{1}\subseteq{\mathfrak{gl}}_{\text{\boldmath$j$}}\times{\mathfrak{g}}_{2}
.
Moreover
{\mathfrak{g}}_{1}={\mathfrak{g}}_{2}
and
{\mathbb{O}}_{1}={\mathbb{O}}_{2}

if and only if
{\mathfrak{gl}}_{\text{\boldmath$j$}}\times{\mathfrak{g}}_{1}={\mathfrak{gl}}_{\text{\boldmath$j$}}\times{\mathfrak{g}}_{2}
and
\{0\}\times{\mathbb{O}}_{1}=\{0\}\times{\mathbb{O}}_{2}
. By replacing
\lambda
by
\tilde{\lambda}
, we can
therefore assume that
\lambda
satisfies that
\lambda_{i}
is even for all
i
and

\lambda_{i}>\lambda_{i+1}
for all
i=1,...,r
.

Let
\text{\boldmath$i$}^{\prime}=(i^{\prime}_{1},...,i^{\prime}_{s^{\prime}})
be the inc-sequence in which

i\in\{1,...,r\}
occurs with multiplicity
(\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{i+1})/2-1
.
Then we have that
\text{\boldmath$i$}^{\prime}
is an admissible sequence for
\lambda
, for which reductions
of the KS-algorithm are of type 1. Further, we have that

\lambda^{\text{\boldmath$i$}^{\prime}}=(2r,2r-2,\dots,4,2)
is a partition as in Lemma
3.2
.

Let
i
be a maximal admissible sequence for
\lambda
. Using KS-property
1

we may first assume that
i
is an inc-sequence without affecting
\lambda^{\text{\boldmath$i$}}
or
{\mathfrak{sp}}_{\text{\boldmath$i$},2n_{\text{\boldmath$i$}}}
up to conjugacy.
Then we observe that

i
must start with at least
l:=(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2})/2-1
entries equal
to
1
by using KS-property
3
. We see that the maximal
k
such that

i_{k}=1
is
l
,
l+1
or
l+2
, and then the next
(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{3})/2-1
entries in
i
must be equal
to
2
by using KS-property
3
. Continuing this argument we see that
for each
i
there must be at least
(\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{i+1})/2-1
entries equal to
i

in
i
. In other words,
\text{\boldmath$i$}^{\prime}
is a subsequence of
i
.
Another application of KS-property
1
allows us reorder
i
to assume that
\text{\boldmath$i$}_{s^{\prime}}=\text{\boldmath$i$}^{\prime}

whilst not affecting
{\mathfrak{sp}}_{\text{\boldmath$i$},2n_{\text{\boldmath$i$}}}
or
\lambda^{\text{\boldmath$i$}}
.

It now follows that there is a bijection from the rigid induction data for
{\mathbb{O}}_{\lambda}\subseteq{\mathfrak{sp}}_{2n}

to the rigid induction data for
{\mathbb{O}}_{\lambda^{\text{\boldmath$i$}^{\prime}}}\subseteq{\mathfrak{sp}}_{2n_{\text{\boldmath$i$}^{\prime}}}
.
The bijection is given by
[{\mathfrak{sp}}_{\text{\boldmath$i$},2n_{\text{\boldmath$i$}}},\{0\}^{s}\times{\mathbb{O}}_{\lambda^{\text{\boldmath$i$}}}]\mapsto[{\mathfrak{sp}}_{\text{\boldmath$i$}^{\prime\prime},2(n_{\text{\boldmath$i$}^{\prime}})_{\text{\boldmath$i$}^{\prime\prime}}},\{0\}^{s^{\prime\prime}}\times{\mathbb{O}}_{(\lambda^{\text{\boldmath$i$}^{\prime}})^{\text{\boldmath$i$}^{\prime\prime}}}]

where
\text{\boldmath$i$}^{\prime\prime}=(i^{\prime\prime}_{1},...,i^{\prime\prime}_{s^{\prime\prime}})
is such that
\text{\boldmath$i$}=(i^{\prime}_{1},...,i^{\prime}_{s^{\prime}},i^{\prime\prime}_{1},...,i^{\prime\prime}_{s^{\prime\prime}})
; so that
(n_{\text{\boldmath$i$}^{\prime}})_{\text{\boldmath$i$}^{\prime\prime}}=n_{\text{\boldmath$i$}}
and
(\lambda^{\text{\boldmath$i$}^{\prime}})^{\text{\boldmath$i$}^{\prime\prime}}=\lambda^{\text{\boldmath$i$}}
.

We observe that
[{\mathfrak{g}}_{1},{\mathbb{O}}_{1}]
and
[{\mathfrak{g}}_{2},{\mathbb{O}}_{2}]
are rigid
induction data for
{\mathbb{O}}_{\lambda^{\text{\boldmath$i$}^{\prime}}}\subseteq{\mathfrak{sp}}_{\text{\boldmath$i$}^{\prime},n_{\text{\boldmath$i$}^{\prime}}}
.
Thus by Lemma
3.2
we obtain that
{\mathfrak{g}}_{1}={\mathfrak{g}}_{2}
and
{\mathbb{O}}_{1}={\mathbb{O}}_{2}
.
This completes the proof for the symplectic case.

We end the proof by considering the case
G=\mathrm{SO}_{N}
. Here we can proceed exactly as in the symplectic case. The only minor changes
required are to replace all occurrences of
{\mathfrak{sp}}
with
{\mathfrak{so}}
, to take all entries of
\tilde{\lambda}
to be odd then assume that all
\lambda_{i}
are odd and distinct, and to say that
\lambda^{\text{\boldmath$i$}^{\prime}}

is equal to
(2r-1,2r-3,\dots,3,1)
.
∎

Remark 3.4
.

We note that the statement of Theorem
3.3
holds more generally for any reductive algebraic group
G
over
{\mathbb{C}}
.
First, from the definition of Lusztig–Spaltenstein induction it is straightforward to see that the theorem
reduces to the case where
G
is simple and that it is independent of the isogeny class of
G
. So we are just
left to consider
G
simple of exceptional type.

The description of induced orbits and the Levi subalgebras from which they are induced for
G
of exceptional type can be found in

[
EdG09
, Tables 6–10]
. The majority of induced orbits have a unique rigid induction datum, and so the statement
of Theorem
3.3
holds trivially for these orbits. For all the other orbits a case by case analysis shows that
there can never be containment of Levi subalgebras for distinct rigid induction data. Thus the statement of Theorem
3.3

does hold for
G
of exceptional type.

We also comment here that it would be interesting to find a general conceptual
argument to prove this more general version of Theorem
3.3
.

4.
Finite
W
-algebras and Losev–Premet ideals

For this section
G
is any connected reductive algebraic group over
{\mathbb{C}}
. We let
e\in{\mathfrak{g}}
be
a nilpotent element and let
{\mathbb{O}}={\mathbb{O}}_{e}
. We recall the required background on
the finite
W
-algebra
U({\mathfrak{g}},e)
, move on to discuss Losev’s parabolic induction
and then provide the setup required to state Theorem
4.1
. As a general reference for this material on
finite
W
-algebras we refer for example to
[
Lo10b
]
, and note that this covers all of the fundamental results
recapped below.

To define
U({\mathfrak{g}},e)
we extend
e
to an
{\mathfrak{sl}}_{2}
-triple
(e,h,f)
in
{\mathfrak{g}}
and
consider the
\operatorname{ad}h
-grading
{\mathfrak{g}}=\bigoplus_{i\in{\mathbb{Z}}}{\mathfrak{g}}(i)

of
{\mathfrak{g}}
. We write
\chi:=(e,\cdot)\in{\mathfrak{g}}^{*}
, where we recall that

(\cdot\,,\cdot)
is a nondegenerate invariant symmetric bilinear form on
{\mathfrak{g}}
.
There is a symplectic form on
{\mathfrak{g}}(-1)
defined by
(x,y)\mapsto\chi([x,y])
, and we choose
a Lagrangian subspace
{\mathfrak{l}}\subseteq{\mathfrak{g}}(-1)
. We let
{\mathfrak{m}}:={\mathfrak{l}}\oplus\bigoplus_{i<-1}{\mathfrak{g}}(i)
and

{\mathfrak{m}}_{\chi}:=\{x-\chi(x)\mid x\in{\mathfrak{m}}\}\subseteq U({\mathfrak{g}})
. Let
Q_{\chi}:=U({\mathfrak{g}})/U({\mathfrak{g}}){\mathfrak{m}}_{\chi}
,
which is (left)
U({\mathfrak{g}})
-module called the
generalised Gelfand–Graev module
associated to
\chi
.
The
finite
W
-algebra
corresponding to
{\mathfrak{g}}
and
e
is defined as

U({\mathfrak{g}},e):=\operatorname{End}_{U({\mathfrak{g}})}(Q_{\chi})^{\mathrm{op}}.

It is known that up to isomorphism
U({\mathfrak{g}},e)
does not depend on the choice of
{\mathfrak{l}}

or on the choice of
{\mathfrak{sl}}_{2}
-triple, and in fact only depends on the

G
-orbit of
e
.

The generalised Gelfand-Graev module
Q_{\chi}
is a left
U({\mathfrak{g}})
-module and a right
U({\mathfrak{g}},e)
-module.
Let
\operatorname{Wh}_{\chi}(U({\mathfrak{g}}))
denote the full subcategory of
U({\mathfrak{g}})
-modules on which
{\mathfrak{m}}_{\chi}

acts locally nilpotently.
The functor

Q_{\chi}\otimes_{U({\mathfrak{g}},e)}(-):U({\mathfrak{g}},e)\operatorname{-mod}\overset{\sim}{\longrightarrow}\operatorname{Wh}_{\chi}(U({\mathfrak{g}}))

(4.1)

is an equivalence of categories known as
Skryabin’s equivalence
.
This leads to a close relationship between finite dimensional simple
U({\mathfrak{g}},e)
-modules and the set
\operatorname{Prim}_{\mathbb{O}}U({\mathfrak{g}})
, as
established within
[
Lo10a
, Theorem 1.2.2]
and which we now describe.

For
M
a finite dimensional simple
U({\mathfrak{g}},e)
-module,

\operatorname{Ann}_{U({\mathfrak{g}})}(Q_{\chi}\otimes_{U({\mathfrak{g}},e)}M)
lies in
\operatorname{Prim}_{\mathbb{O}}U({\mathfrak{g}})
; conversely, any
I\in\operatorname{Prim}_{\mathbb{O}}(U({\mathfrak{g}}))
is of the
form
\operatorname{Ann}_{U({\mathfrak{g}})}(Q_{\chi}\otimes_{U({\mathfrak{g}},e)}M)
for some finite dimensional simple
U({\mathfrak{g}},e)
-module
M
.
Moreover, two finite dimensional simple
U({\mathfrak{g}},e)
-modules
M
and
N
satisfy
\operatorname{Ann}_{U({\mathfrak{g}})}(Q_{\chi}\otimes_{U({\mathfrak{g}},e)}M)=\operatorname{Ann}_{U({\mathfrak{g}})}(Q_{\chi}\otimes_{U({\mathfrak{g}},e)}N)

if and only if
M

Proposition 4.2
.

Let

be a simple algebraic group over
{\mathbb{C}}
of classical type and let

be a nilpotent orbit in
{\mathfrak{g}}
with unique rigid induction datum
[{\mathfrak{g}}_{0},{\mathbb{O}}_{0}]

is a polynomial ring in
\dim{\mathfrak{z}}({\mathfrak{g}}_{0})
variables and the map
\Phi_{0}
in (

Proof.

By
[
PT14
, Theorem 1]
and
[
PT14
, Theorem 4(ii)]
,

is a polynomial algebra in

variables, where
c(e)=\dim\overline{{\mathcal{D}}}^{{\mathrm{reg}}}({\mathfrak{g}}_{0},{\mathbb{O}}_{0})-\dim{\mathbb{O}}

\dim{\mathfrak{z}}({\mathfrak{g}}_{0})
by the discussion at the end of §
2.5
(citing
[

On the other hand it follows from a definition of the finite
W

U({\mathfrak{g}},e_{0})\cong S({\mathfrak{z}}({\mathfrak{g}}_{0}))\otimes U([{\mathfrak{g}}_{0},{\mathfrak{g}}_{0}],e_{0})

U({\mathfrak{g}},e_{0})^{\mathrm{ab}}\cong S({\mathfrak{z}}({\mathfrak{g}}_{0}))\otimes U([{\mathfrak{g}}_{0},{\mathfrak{g}}_{0}],e_{0})^{\mathrm{ab}}
. Applying
[
PT14
, Theorem 1]

again, we see that

and thus that
U({\mathfrak{g}},e_{0})^{\mathrm{ab}}\cong S({\mathfrak{z}}({\mathfrak{g}}_{0}))
is a polynomial algebra in
\dim{\mathfrak{z}}({\mathfrak{g}}_{0})
variables.

We therefore conclude that
\Phi_{0}:{\mathcal{E}}({\mathfrak{g}}_{0},e_{0})\to{\mathcal{E}}({\mathfrak{g}},e)
is a finite morphism between affine spaces of the same dimension. Since finite morphisms are closed,

\Phi
is surjective.

5.1.
Preliminary observations

Before getting into the substance of the proof of Theorem
4.1
it will be useful to make a handful of observations about one-dimensional representations and central characters in the case of
{\mathfrak{g}}={\mathfrak{sp}}_{2n}
and its Levi subalgebras (analogous results hold for
{\mathfrak{so}}_{N}
). We do so here.

As discussed in §
2.2
, the standard Levi subalgebras of
{\mathfrak{sp}}_{2n}
are all of the form

{\mathfrak{sp}}_{\text{\boldmath$i$},2n_{\text{\boldmath$i$}}}={\mathfrak{gl}}_{i_{1}}\times\cdots\times{\mathfrak{gl}}_{i_{s}}\times{\mathfrak{sp}}_{2n_{\text{\boldmath$i$}}}

for
\text{\boldmath$i$}=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{s})
an inc-sequence with
|\text{\boldmath$i$}|\leq n
, and with
n_{\text{\boldmath$i$}}:=n-|\text{\boldmath$i$}|
. In particular, each
{\mathfrak{sp}}_{\text{\boldmath$i$},2n_{\text{\boldmath$i$}}}
contains
{\mathfrak{t}}=\operatorname{Lie}(T)
, where
T
is the maximal torus consisting of diagonal matrices in
\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}
. Furthermore, the rigid induction data are of the form
[{\mathfrak{sp}}_{\text{\boldmath$i$},2n_{\text{\boldmath$i$}}},\{0\}\times{\mathbb{O}}_{0}]
, where
{\mathbb{O}}_{0}
is a rigid nilpotent orbit in
{\mathfrak{sp}}_{2n_{\text{\boldmath$i$}}}
.

For such a rigid induction datum let
e_{0}\in{\mathbb{O}}_{0}
; we sometimes abuse notation to identify
e_{0}\in{\mathbb{O}}_{0}
with
(0,e_{0})\in\{0\}\times{\mathbb{O}}_{0}
, thus viewing it as a nilpotent element in
{\mathfrak{sp}}_{\text{\boldmath$i$},2n_{\text{\boldmath$i$}}}
. As algebras, we have

\displaystyle U({\mathfrak{sp}}_{\text{\boldmath$i$},2n_{\text{\boldmath$i$}}},e_{0})\cong U({\mathfrak{gl}}_{i_{1}})\otimes\cdots\otimes U({\mathfrak{gl}}_{i_{s}})\otimes U({\mathfrak{sp}}_{2n_{\text{\boldmath$i$}}},e_{0})

(5.1)

and thus

\displaystyle U({\mathfrak{sp}}_{\text{\boldmath$i$},2n_{\text{\boldmath$i$}}},e_{0})^{\mathrm{ab}}\cong S({\mathfrak{z}}({\mathfrak{gl}}_{i_{1}}))\otimes\cdots\otimes S({\mathfrak{z}}({\mathfrak{gl}}_{i_{s}}))\otimes U({\mathfrak{sp}}_{2n_{\text{\boldmath$i$}}},e_{0})^{\mathrm{ab}}.

(5.2)

Noting that
{\mathfrak{z}}({\mathfrak{sp}}_{\text{\boldmath$i$},n_{\text{\boldmath$i$}}})={\mathfrak{z}}({\mathfrak{gl}}_{i_{1}})\times\cdots\times{\mathfrak{z}}({\mathfrak{gl}}_{i_{s}})\times\{0\}
and that
[
PT14
, Theorem 1]
implies that
U({\mathfrak{sp}}_{2n_{\text{\boldmath$i$}}},e_{0})^{\mathrm{ab}}\cong{\mathbb{C}}
(since
e_{0}
is rigid in the simple Lie algebra
{\mathfrak{sp}}_{2n_{\text{\boldmath$i$}}}
), we can simplify this as

\displaystyle U({\mathfrak{sp}}_{\text{\boldmath$i$},2n_{\text{\boldmath$i$}}},e_{0})^{\mathrm{ab}}\cong S({\mathfrak{z}}({\mathfrak{sp}}_{\text{\boldmath$i$},2n_{\text{\boldmath$i$}}})).

(5.3)

In particular,
U({\mathfrak{sp}}_{\text{\boldmath$i$},2n_{\text{\boldmath$i$}}},e_{0})^{\mathrm{ab}}
is a polynomial algebra in
\dim{\mathfrak{z}}({\mathfrak{sp}}_{\text{\boldmath$i$},2n_{\text{\boldmath$i$}}})=s
variables and
{\mathcal{E}}({\mathfrak{sp}}_{\text{\boldmath$i$},2n_{\text{\boldmath$i$}}},e_{0})
is
s
-dimensional affine space.

It will be helpful for us to give a more explicit description of
{\mathcal{E}}({\mathfrak{sp}}_{\text{\boldmath$i$},2n_{\text{\boldmath$i$}}},e_{0})
; we do so by turning our attention to the central characters corresponding to one-dimensional
U({\mathfrak{sp}}_{\text{\boldmath$i$},2n_{\text{\boldmath$i$}}},e_{0})
-modules. We have seen that there is a unique
{\mathbb{C}}
-algebra homomorphism
\eta=\eta(\text{\boldmath$i$},e_{0}):U({\mathfrak{sp}}_{2n_{\text{\boldmath$i$}}},e_{0})\to{\mathbb{C}}
, and we denote the corresponding one-dimensional

U({\mathfrak{sp}}_{2n_{\text{\boldmath$i$}}},e_{0})
-module by
{\mathbb{C}}_{\eta}
.
Thanks to (
5.2
), for each
\lambda\in{\mathfrak{z}}({\mathfrak{sp}}_{\text{\boldmath$i$},2n_{\text{\boldmath$i$}}})^{*}
there is a unique one-dimensional
representation
{\mathbb{C}}_{\eta}^{\lambda}
of
U({\mathfrak{sp}}_{\text{\boldmath$i$},2n_{\text{\boldmath$i$}}},e_{0})
on which
U({\mathfrak{gl}}_{i_{1}})\otimes\cdots\otimes U({\mathfrak{gl}}_{i_{s}})

acts via
\lambda
and
U({\mathfrak{sp}}_{2n_{\text{\boldmath$i$}}},e_{0})
acts via
\eta
.
Therefore,

{\mathcal{E}}({\mathfrak{sp}}_{\text{\boldmath$i$},2n_{\text{\boldmath$i$}}},e_{0})=\{{\mathbb{C}}_{\eta}^{\lambda}\mid\lambda\in{\mathfrak{z}}({\mathfrak{sp}}_{\text{\boldmath$i$},2n_{\text{\boldmath$i$}}})^{*}\}.

(5.4)

The map
\mathscr{S}_{0}:=\mathscr{S}({\mathfrak{sp}}_{\text{\boldmath$i$},2n_{\text{\boldmath$i$}}},e_{0})
then defines for each
\lambda\in{\mathfrak{z}}({\mathfrak{sp}}_{\text{\boldmath$i$},2n_{\text{\boldmath$i$}}})^{*}

Using Duflo’s theorem, there exists
\mu=\mu(\text{\boldmath$i$},e_{0})\in{\mathfrak{t}}^{*}
such that
I_{\eta}^{0}=\operatorname{Ann}_{U({\mathfrak{sp}}_{\text{\boldmath$i$},2n_{\text{\boldmath$i$}}})}L(\mu)
.
It is
not hard to see that

\displaystyle I_{\eta}^{\lambda}=\operatorname{Ann}_{U({\mathfrak{sp}}_{\text{\boldmath$i$},2n_{\text{\boldmath$i$}}})}(L(\mu)\otimes{\mathbb{C}}_{\lambda}),

(5.5)

where
{\mathbb{C}}_{\lambda}
is the one-dimensional
U({\mathfrak{sp}}_{\text{\boldmath$i$},2n_{\text{\boldmath$i$}}})
-module upon which
U({\mathfrak{sp}}_{2n_{\text{\boldmath$i$}}})
acts trivially and

U({\mathfrak{gl}}_{i_{1}})\otimes\cdots\otimes U({\mathfrak{gl}}_{i_{s}})
acts via
\lambda
. Let
W_{\text{\boldmath$i$}}
be the Weyl group corresponding to
(\mathrm{Sp}_{\text{\boldmath$i$},2n_{\text{\boldmath$i$}}},T)

be the corresponding central character map from §

\operatorname{ch}_{\text{\boldmath$i$}}(I_{\eta}^{\lambda})=W_{\text{\boldmath$i$}}\bullet(\mu+\lambda)

Lemma 5.1

\operatorname{ch}(\operatorname{Ind}_{{\mathfrak{p}}_{\text{\boldmath$i$}}}^{{\mathfrak{sp}}_{2n}}(I_{\eta}^{\lambda}))=W\bullet(\mu+\lambda).

, Theorem A]
and there are thus two one-dimensional representations
\eta_{1},\eta_{2}:U([{\mathfrak{g}}_{0},{\mathfrak{g}}_{0}],e_{0})\to{\mathbb{C}}
. One then gets that

{\mathcal{E}}({\mathfrak{g}}_{0},e_{0})=\{{\mathbb{C}}_{\eta_{i}}^{\lambda}\mid i=1,2\mbox{ and }\lambda\in{\mathfrak{z}}({\mathfrak{g}}_{0})^{*}\}.

All the other observations work as for
{\mathfrak{sp}}_{2n}
, except that
\mu
will depend on which
\eta_{i}
is being considered.

The final result we need is the following simple lemma. Recalling that
W
acts on
{\mathfrak{t}}^{*}
via the dot-action, let us write
V/(W,\bullet)
for the image of any subset
V
of
{\mathfrak{t}}^{*}
under the surjection
{\mathfrak{t}}^{*}\twoheadrightarrow{\mathfrak{t}}^{*}/(W,\bullet)
.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
\mu_{0},\ldots,\mu_{m}\in{\mathfrak{t}}^{*}
and let
{\mathfrak{z}}_{0},\ldots,{\mathfrak{z}}_{m}\subseteq{\mathfrak{t}}^{*}
be vector subspaces. If

(\mu_{0}+{\mathfrak{z}}_{0})/(W,\bullet)
is contained in
\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}(\mu_{i}+{\mathfrak{z}}_{i})/(W,\bullet)
then
there exists
1\leq i_{0}\leq m
and
w_{0}\in W
such that
{\mathfrak{z}}_{0}\subseteq w_{0}({\mathfrak{z}}_{i_{0}})
.

Proof.

The inclusion
(\mu_{0}+{\mathfrak{z}}_{0})/(W,\bullet)\subseteq\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}(\mu_{i}+{\mathfrak{z}}_{i})/(W,\bullet)
implies that

\mu_{0}+{\mathfrak{z}}_{0}\subseteq\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}\bigcup_{w\in W}w\bullet(\mu_{i}+{\mathfrak{z}}_{i}).

The right hand side is an affine variety with irreducible components of the form

w\bullet(\mu_{i}+{\mathfrak{z}}_{i})
, where
w\in W
and
i\in\{1,...,m\}
. Since
\mu_{0}+{\mathfrak{z}}_{0}

is an irreducible subvariety there exist
1\leq i_{0}\leq m
and
w_{0}\in W
such that

\mu_{0}+{\mathfrak{z}}_{0}\subseteq w_{0}\bullet(\mu_{i_{0}}+{\mathfrak{z}}_{i_{0}}).
Now,
{\mathfrak{z}}_{0}\subseteq w_{0}\bullet\mu_{i_{0}}-\mu_{0}+w_{0}({\mathfrak{z}}_{i_{0}})

and so
0\in{\mathfrak{z}}_{0}
implies
w_{0}\bullet\mu_{i_{0}}-\mu_{0}+w_{0}({\mathfrak{z}}_{i_{0}})=w_{0}({\mathfrak{z}}_{i_{0}})
. The result follows.
∎

5.2.
Main proof

We are now in a position to proceed to the main steps in the proof of Theorem
4.1
. From now on, we recall the refined set-up of rigid induction data from §
4
and our notation is consistent with that section. In particular, we label our rigid induction data by
[{\mathfrak{g}}_{i},{\mathbb{O}}_{i}]
,
i=0,\ldots,m
, rather than by
[{\mathfrak{sp}}_{\text{\boldmath$i$},2n_{\text{\boldmath$i$}}},\{0\}\times{\mathbb{O}}_{0}]
with
i
a maximal admissible inc-sequence and
{\mathbb{O}}_{0}
a rigid nilpotent orbit in
{\mathfrak{sp}}_{2n_{\text{\boldmath$i$}}}
. The reader should nonetheless recall that our chosen representative of each
[{\mathfrak{g}}_{i},{\mathbb{O}}_{i}]
is selected to be of the form
({\mathfrak{sp}}_{\text{\boldmath$i$},2n_{\text{\boldmath$i$}}},\{0\}\times{\mathbb{O}}_{0})
so that we may use the results of §
5.1
.

Proposition 5.4
.

For
i=0,...,m
, we have
\Phi({\mathcal{E}}({\mathfrak{g}}_{i},e_{i}))\nsubseteq\bigcup_{j\neq i}\Phi({\mathcal{E}}({\mathfrak{g}}_{j},e_{j}))
.

Proof.

For each
i
, denote by
W_{i}
the Weyl group associated to
(G_{i},T)
. Proceeding as in §
5.1
, we find
\eta_{i}\in{\mathfrak{t}}^{*}
and write
{\mathcal{E}}({\mathfrak{g}}_{i},e_{i})=\{{\mathbb{C}}_{\eta_{i}}^{\lambda}\mid\lambda\in{\mathfrak{z}}({\mathfrak{g}}_{i})^{*}\}
. We pick
\mu_{i}\in{\mathfrak{t}}^{*}
as in §
5.1
so that
\operatorname{ch}_{i}(I_{\eta_{i}}^{0})=W_{i}\bullet\mu_{i}
(where
\operatorname{ch}_{i}
denotes the obvious central character map); as before,
\operatorname{ch}_{i}(I_{\eta_{i}}^{\lambda})=W_{i}\bullet(\mu_{i}+\lambda)
for
\lambda\in{\mathfrak{z}}({\mathfrak{g}}_{i})^{*}
.

Suppose now for a contradiction that
\Phi({\mathcal{E}}({\mathfrak{g}}_{i},e_{i}))\subseteq\bigcup_{j\neq i}\Phi({\mathcal{E}}({\mathfrak{g}}_{j},e_{j})
.
Since Diagram (
4
) commutes we deduce that

\operatorname{ch}\operatorname{Ind}_{{\mathfrak{p}}_{i}}^{{\mathfrak{g}}}\mathscr{S}_{i}({\mathcal{E}}({\mathfrak{g}}_{i},e_{i}))\subseteq\bigcup_{j\neq i}\operatorname{ch}\operatorname{Ind}_{{\mathfrak{p}}_{j}}^{{\mathfrak{g}}}\mathscr{S}_{j}({\mathcal{E}}({\mathfrak{g}}_{j},e_{j}))\subseteq{\mathfrak{t}}^{*}/(W,\bullet).

Making the notation
{\mathfrak{z}}_{k}:=\operatorname{Ann}_{{\mathfrak{t}}^{*}}([{\mathfrak{g}}_{k},{\mathfrak{g}}_{k}])
(which we easily identify with
{\mathfrak{z}}({\mathfrak{g}}_{k})^{*}
) and using
Lemma
5.1
we deduce that

(\mu_{i}+{\mathfrak{z}}_{i})/(W,\bullet)\subseteq\bigcup_{j\neq i}(\mu_{j}+{\mathfrak{z}}_{j})/(W,\bullet).

By Lemma
5.3
we deduce that
{\mathfrak{z}}_{i}
is
W
-conjugate
to a subspace of
{\mathfrak{z}}_{j}
for some
j\neq i
. This is equivalent to saying that
{\mathfrak{z}}({\mathfrak{g}}_{i})
is
W
-conjugate
to a subspace of
{\mathfrak{z}}({\mathfrak{g}}_{j})
for some
j\neq i
which, in turn, implies that
{\mathfrak{g}}_{j}
is
G
-conjugate to a subset
of
{\mathfrak{g}}_{i}
. This contradicts Theorem
3.3
, and the contradiction completes the proof. ∎

Recalling from §
2.5
that sheets of
{\mathfrak{g}}
containing
{\mathbb{O}}
are in bijection with the rigid induction data
[{\mathfrak{g}}_{i},{\mathbb{O}}_{i}]
,
i=0,\ldots,m
, let us denote by
{\mathcal{S}}_{i}
the sheet corresponding to
[{\mathfrak{g}}_{i},{\mathbb{O}}_{i}]
. As in §
2.5
, we can write
{\mathcal{S}}_{i}=\overline{{\mathcal{D}}}^{{\mathrm{reg}}}({\mathfrak{g}}_{i},{\mathbb{O}}_{i})
.

Now, recall the
{\mathfrak{sl}}_{2}
-triple
(e,h,f)
from the definition of the finite
W
-algebra
U({\mathfrak{g}},e)
. The Katsylo variety is defined as

e+X:=(e+{\mathfrak{g}}^{f})\cap\bigcup_{i=0}^{m}{\mathcal{S}}_{i}.

Key to the proof of Theorem
4.1
is the following result on the irreducible components of
e+X
; given a variety
Y
, we denote by
\operatorname{Comp}Y
the set of irreducible components of
Y
.

Proposition 5.5
.

There are dimension preserving bijections

\operatorname{Comp}{\mathcal{E}}({\mathfrak{g}},e)\overset{\sim}{\longrightarrow}\operatorname{Comp}(e+X)\overset{\sim}{\longrightarrow}\operatorname{Comp}\bigsqcup_{i=0}^{m}{\mathcal{E}}({\mathfrak{g}}_{i},e_{i}).

Proof.

The first bijection is given in
[
To23
, Theorem 1.1]
.

To establish the second bijection we first observe the following three facts about
e+X
which allow us to describe
\operatorname{Comp}(e+X)
:

(i)

The intersections
(e+{\mathfrak{g}}^{f})\cap{\mathcal{S}}_{i}
are all irreducible, thanks to

[
IH05
, Theorem 6.2]
.

(ii)

There are no inclusions between the varieties
\{(e+{\mathfrak{g}}^{f})\cap{\mathcal{S}}_{i}\mid i=0,...,m\}
;
this can be immediately deduced from the fact that
G\cdot((e+{\mathfrak{g}}^{f})\cap{\mathcal{S}}_{i})={\mathcal{S}}_{i}
by
[
IH05
, Lemma 2.8(i)]
and that, by definition, no sheet
can admit an inclusion into another.

(iii)

The dimension of
(e+{\mathfrak{g}}^{f})\cap{\mathcal{S}}_{i}
is
\dim{\mathfrak{z}}({\mathfrak{g}}_{i})
(see
[
IH05
, Lemma 2.8(2)]
, for example).

The result then follows from the observation in (
5.3
) that, for
G=\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}
, each
{\mathcal{E}}({\mathfrak{g}}_{i},e_{i})
is an affine space of dimension
\dim{\mathfrak{z}}({\mathfrak{g}}_{i})
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
4.1
.

When
G
is of type
{\sf A}
this is Corollary
4.3
. For
G
of type
{\sf B}
,
{\sf C}
or
{\sf D}
we easily reduce to
G=\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}
or
\mathrm{SO}_{N}
and use Proposition
5.4
and Proposition
5.5
to argue as follows.

We know that
\Phi
as in (
4
) is a finite morphism. Thus, its image

\Phi\left(\bigsqcup_{i}{\mathcal{E}}({\mathfrak{g}}_{i},e_{i})\right)\subseteq{\mathcal{E}}({\mathfrak{g}},e)
is closed and for any irreducible
component
Y
of
\bigsqcup_{i}{\mathcal{E}}({\mathfrak{g}}_{i},e_{i})
we have
\dim\Phi(Y)=\dim Y
.
Thanks to Proposition
5.4
and Proposition
5.5
we see that
the multiset of dimensions of irreducible components of

\Phi\left(\bigsqcup_{i}{\mathcal{E}}({\mathfrak{g}}_{i},e_{i})\right)
is equal to the multiset of dimensions of the irreducible components of
{\mathcal{E}}({\mathfrak{g}},e)
. This
implies that
\Phi
is surjective, completing the proof of Theorem
4.1
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

This follows directly from Theorem
4.1
using the maps
\mathscr{S}
and
\mathscr{S}_{\operatorname{rig}}
(which are surjective onto the sets of Losev-Premet ideals with the appropriate associated varieties) and the commutativity of Diagram (
4
).
∎
