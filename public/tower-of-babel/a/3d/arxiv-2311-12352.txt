Integral representations for products of two solutions of the Airy equation with shifted arguments and their applications in physics
Kirill V. Bazarov, Oleg I. Tolstikhin

Abstract
. Integral representations for a complete set of linearly independent products of two solutions of the Airy equation whose arguments differ by z 0 z_{0} are obtained using the Laplace contour integral method. This generalizes similar integral representations for the case z 0 = 0 z_{0}=0 obtained by Reid. The relation to other previous results is discussed. The results are used to obtain the outgoing-wave Green’s function for an electron in a static electric field in a closed analytic form.

1.
Introduction

Integral representations for various special functions are useful for
applications, particularly as a means to analytically calculate certain
integrals. Although the issue is thoroughly covered in classical
treatises
[
1
,
2
]
, new results in this direction continue to
appear. Let
v_{1}(z)
and
v_{2}(z)
be any solutions of
the Airy equation

\left[\frac{d^{2}}{dz^{2}}-z\right]v(z)=0.

(1.1)

Then their product

w(z)=v_{1}(z)v_{2}(z)

(1.2)

satisfies
[
2
]

\left[\frac{d^{3}}{dz^{3}}-4z\frac{d}{dz}-2\right]w(z)=0.

(1.3)

This equation can be solved using the Laplace contour integral method
[
3
]
,
which enables one to obtain integral representations for products of Airy functions.
This program was realized by Reid
[
4
]
. It is sufficient to consider
three linearly independent solutions of Eq. (
1.3
), products of any two solutions
of Eq. (
1.1
) can be expressed in terms of them. Reid obtained integral representations
for
\Ai^{2}(z)
,
\Ai(z)\Bi(z)
, and
\Bi^{2}(z)
.
A little later, a related integral representation for the product

\Ai(u)\Ai(v)
of two Airy functions
of arbitrary arguments was presented
[
5
]
.
More recently, being motivated
by a physical application and unaware of the results of Reid,
but following the same logic, one of us with colleagues obtained
integral representations for another triple

\Ai(z)\Ai(e^{\pm 2i\pi/3}z)
and
\Ai(e^{-2i\pi/3}z)\Ai(e^{+2i\pi/3}z)

of linearly independent solutions of Eq. (
1.3
)
[
6
]
.
Most recently, working on some exactly solvable models in
the theory of tunneling ionization
[
7
]
,
we have encountered the need to have integral representations for
products of two solutions of the
Airy equation with shifted arguments. In this paper we extend the
approach of Refs.
[
4
,
6
]
to such products and
show that the integral representation obtained in Ref.
[
5
]

is a special case of the present results.
We also discuss an application of the present results in physics.

2.
Basic equations

Let
v_{1}(z)
and
v_{2}(z)
be any solutions of Eq. (
1.1
), as above.
Consider the function

w(z;z_{0})=v_{1}(z+z_{0})v_{2}(z),

(2.1)

where the argument of the first factor is shifted by
z_{0}
compared to that in Eq. (
1.2
).
We treat
w(z;z_{0})
as a function of
z
which depends on
z_{0}
as a parameter.
This function satisfies

\left[\frac{d^{4}}{dz^{4}}-(4z+2z_{0})\frac{d^{2}}{dz^{2}}-6\frac{d}{dz}+z_{0}^{2}\right]w(z;z_{0})=0.

(2.2)

We are interested in three particular solutions
\Ai(z)
and
\Ai(e^{\pm 2i\pi/3}z)

of Eq. (
1.1
) which most frequently appear in applications. Using them, one
obtains nine products (
2.1
). We choose the following
products as four linearly independent solutions of Eq. (
2.2
)

U_{\pm}(z;z_{0})=\Ai(e^{\pm 2i\pi/3}(z+z_{0}))\Ai(e^{\pm 2i\pi/3}z)

(2.3)

and

W_{\pm}(z;z_{0})=\Ai(z+z_{0})\Ai(e^{\pm 2i\pi/3}z).

(2.4)

The general solution of Eq. (
2.2
) is a linear combination of these
four functions. In particular, the other five products of interest are given by

\displaystyle\Ai(z+z_{0})\Ai(z)

\displaystyle=e^{-i\pi/3}W_{+}(z;z_{0})+e^{i\pi/3}W_{-}(z;z_{0}),

(2.5a)

\displaystyle\Ai(e^{\pm 2i\pi/3}(z+z_{0}))\Ai(z)

\displaystyle=U_{\mp}(z;z_{0})+e^{\mp i\pi/3}\left[U_{\pm}(z;z_{0})-W_{\mp}(z;z_{0})\right],

(2.5b)

\displaystyle\Ai(e^{\pm 2i\pi/3}(z+z_{0}))\Ai(e^{\mp 2i\pi/3}z)

\displaystyle=e^{\mp i\pi/3}U_{\mp}(z;z_{0})+e^{\pm i\pi/3}W_{\mp}(z;z_{0}).

(2.5c)

Note that all these products are entire functions of
z
and
z_{0}
.

Similarly to Eq. (
1.3
), Eq. (
2.2
) can be solved using the Laplace
contour integral method. We thus obtain particular solutions in the form

I_{\mathcal{C}}(z;z_{0})=\int_{\mathcal{C}}\exp\left[i(z+z_{0}/2)k-\frac{iz_{0}^{2}}{4k}+\frac{ik^{3}}{12}\right]\frac{dk}{k^{1/2}}.

(2.6)

The branch of
k^{1/2}
is determined by the condition that
\arg k=0
for real
positive
k
and a branch cut specified below.
The integration contour
\mathcal{C}
should be chosen such that the integrand
multiplied by
k^{2}
turns to zero at its ends
[
3
]
.
The different solutions of Eq. (
2.2
) correspond to different contours.
Our goal is to express the solutions (
2.3
) and (
2.4
) in terms of such
contour integrals, then integral representations for the other products of interest
will follow from Eqs. (
2.5
).

The representation of a particular solution by a contour integral can be
proved by comparing their asymptotics at
|z|\to\infty
.
In doing so, one should take into account the following.
The general solution of
Eq. (
2.2
) behaves in the asymptotic region as

\displaystyle w(z;z_{0})\sim\frac{1}{z^{1/2}}\left[c_{1}\exp\left(\frac{4}{3}z^{3/2}+z_{0}z^{1/2}\right)+c_{2}\exp\left(-\frac{4}{3}z^{3/2}-z_{0}z^{1/2}\right)\right.

\displaystyle\left.+c_{3}\exp(z_{0}z^{1/2})+c_{4}\exp(-z_{0}z^{1/2})\right].

(2.7)

This shows that the leading term in the
asymptotics of a particular solution at a given
\arg z

does not generally define the solution uniquely. Indeed, consider, for example,
the ray
\arg z=0
. The most rapidly growing solution that behaves along this
ray as the first term
in Eq. (
2.7
) admits an admixture of subdominant solutions represented by the
other three terms, and hence is not defined uniquely. However, for any
\arg z
,
the most rapidly decaying solution is uniquely defined by its asymptotics.
For
\arg z=0
this is the solution that behaves as the second term
in Eq. (
2.7
).

3.
Functions
U_{\pm}(z;z_{0})

Let us make a branch cut at
\arg k=\pi/2
, as shown in Fig.
1
.
The integrand in Eq. (
2.6
) multiplied by
k^{2}

decays as
|k|\to\infty
along three asymptotic valleys
lying in sectors
-4\pi/3<\arg k<-\pi
,
-2\pi/3<\arg k<-\pi/3
, and

0<\arg k<\pi/3
. Let contours
\mathcal{L}_{\pm}
go from one of the valleys
to another, as shown in Fig.
1
. Then

U_{\pm}(z;z_{0})=\frac{e^{i\pi/4\mp i\pi/3}}{4\pi^{3/2}}\,I_{\mathcal{L}_{\pm}}(z;z_{0}).

(3.1)

To prove this equality, we compare asymptotics of its left- and right-hand
sides at
|z|\to\infty
.
The asymptotics of
U_{\pm}(z;z_{0})
can be obtained from Eq. (
2.3
).
These functions present the most rapidly decaying solutions of Eq. (
2.2
)
along rays
\arg z=\mp 2\pi/3
, respectively, and hence
are uniquely defined by their asymptotics along these rays.
The asymptotics of
I_{\mathcal{L}_{\pm}}(z;z_{0})
along these rays
can be found using the saddle-point method
[
8
]
.
The only saddle point contributing to the integral is

k=2e^{-i\pi/2\mp i\pi/3}|z|^{1/2}+O(|z|^{-1/2})
.
The evaluation of the integral confirms Eq. (
3.1
).
These integral representations hold for any complex
z
and
z_{0}
.

4.
Functions
W_{\pm}(z;z_{0})

The integral representations for
W_{\pm}(z;z_{0})
depend on
\arg z_{0}
.
We consider the whole interval
-\pi\leq\arg z_{0}\leq\pi
in
three steps.

4.1.
Ray
\arg z_{0}=0

We begin with the ray
\arg z_{0}=0
.
The integrand in Eq. (
2.6
) multiplied by
k^{2}

decays as
|k|\to 0
within the internal valley
lying in sectors
-3\pi/2<\arg k<-\pi
and

0<\arg k<\pi/2
adjacent to the branch cut.
Let contours
\mathcal{R}_{+}
and
\mathcal{R}_{-}
start at
k=0

within these sectors and go to
the corresponding asymptotic valley, as shown in Fig.
1
.
Then

W_{\pm}(z;z_{0})=\frac{e^{i\pi/4\pm i\pi/3}}{4\pi^{3/2}}I_{\mathcal{R}_{\pm}}(z;z_{0}).

(4.1)

To prove this equality for
W_{+}(z;z_{0})
and
W_{-}(z;z_{0})
, we compare
the asymptotics of its left- and right-hand sides as
|z|\to\infty
in
sectors
-2\pi/3<\arg z<0
and
0<\arg z<2\pi/3
, respectively.
The functions
W_{\pm}(z;z_{0})
behave within these sector as the
the fourth term in Eq. (
2.7
). We first compare the asymptotics
along rays
\arg z=\mp 0
.
The only saddle point contributing to
I_{\mathcal{R}_{\pm}}(z;z_{0})

is
k=(1/2)e^{-i\pi/2\mp i\pi\pm i0}z_{0}|z|^{-1/2}+O(|z|^{-3/2})
.
By evaluating the integral we confirm Eq. (
4.1
).
Note, however, that the asymptotics of

W_{\pm}(z;z_{0})
along the rays
\arg z=\mp 0
do not define these functions
uniquely, because there exists a solution
\Ai(z+z_{0})\Ai(z)

of Eq. (
2.2
) that
decays along these rays faster than
W_{\pm}(z;z_{0})
.
This solution could be added to the right-hand side of Eq. (
4.1
)
without changing its asymptotics at
\arg z=\mp 0
.
To resolve this uncertainty, we additionally compare the asymptotics
along rays
\arg z=\mp 2\pi/3\pm 0
.
The function
\Ai(z+z_{0})\Ai(z)
represents the most rapidly growing solution
along these rays, so adding it to the right-hand side of Eq. (
4.1
)
would violate the equality. This proves Eq. (
4.1
).
These integral representations hold for any
z
under the condition
\arg z_{0}=0

assumed in this subsection.

Let
\mathcal{O}
denote a contour which starts and ends at
k=0
within the internal valley
on the different sides of the branch cut,
as shown in Fig.
1
. It can be seen that

I_{\mathcal{O}}(z;z_{0})=I_{\mathcal{R}_{-}}(z;z_{0})+I_{\mathcal{L}_{-}}(z;z_{0})-I_{\mathcal{L}_{+}}(z;z_{0})-I_{\mathcal{R}_{+}}(z;z_{0}).

(4.2)

For
z_{0}=0
this contour can be contracted to the point
k=0
, so
the integral
I_{\mathcal{O}}(z;z_{0})
turns to zero.
Note that some linear combinations of the solutions (
2.3
)-(
2.5
)
also turn to zero at
z_{0}=0
. Integral representations for such linear
combinations can be conveniently expressed in terms of the contour
\mathcal{O}
.
For example, using Eqs. (
2.4
), (
2.5b
), (
3.1
), (
4.1
),
and (
4.2
) we obtain

\Ai(e^{\pm 2i\pi/3}(z+z_{0}))\Ai(z)-\Ai(z+z_{0})\Ai(e^{\pm 2i\pi/3}z)=\pm\frac{e^{i\pi/4\pm i\pi/3}}{4\pi^{3/2}}I_{\mathcal{O}}(z;z_{0}).

(4.3)

4.2.
Extension to
|\arg z_{0}|<\pi/2

The construction of the previous subsection
under an appropriate generalization can be extended to the

|\arg z_{0}|<\pi/2
.
For a nonzero value of
\arg z_{0}

2\arg z_{0}

\arg z_{0}

\arg z_{0}=0

The branch cut is made at

The contours

The way how Eq. (

4.4.
Discussion

The functions
W_{\pm}(z;z_{0})
are analytic in
z_{0}
.
Meanwhile,
the branch cut and
the integration contours
\mathcal{R}_{\pm}

defining their integral representations in
Eqs. (
4.1
) and (
4.5
)
vary continuously
within intervals
|\arg z_{0}|<\pi/2
and
\pi/2<|\arg z_{0}|\leq\pi
,
but undergo an abrupt change corresponding to the jump from
one panel in Fig.
2
to another
at
|\arg z_{0}|=\pi/2
.
To compensate this change, an additional integral over
\mathcal{O}
appears in
Eq. (
4.5
). As follows from Eqs. (
4.3
) and (
4.6
),
this additional term vanishes at
z_{0}=0
.
For
z_{0}\neq 0
, it accounts for the difference between

W_{\pm}(z;z_{0})
and
W_{\pm}(z+z_{0};-z_{0})
.
Finally, we mention that the integral representations for
W_{\pm}(z;z_{0})

at the rays
\arg z_{0}=\pm\pi/2
can be obtained by continuity in
z_{0}

in the corresponding limit
from either Eq. (
4.1
) or Eq. (
4.5
).

5.
Real
z
and
z_{0}

Consider real
z
and
z_{0}
denoted by
x
and
x_{0}
, respectively.
In this case contours
\mathcal{R}_{\pm}
can be placed on the real axis
and some of the formulas discussed above can be simplified.
For
x_{0}\geq 0
, we obtain from Eq. (
4.1
)

W_{\pm}(x,x_{0})=\frac{e^{\pm i\pi/12}}{4\pi^{3/2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp\left[\mp ik(x+x_{0}/2)\pm i\frac{x_{0}^{2}}{4k}\mp i\frac{k^{3}}{12}\right]\frac{dk}{k^{1/2}}.

(5.1)

If
x_{0}=0
, this formula reduces to the integral representation
for
\Ai(z)\Ai(e^{\pm 2i\pi/3}z)
obtained in Ref.
[
6
]
.
Note that such a simple formula cannot be written for
x_{0}<0
,
because of the term involving the contour
\mathcal{O}
in Eq. (
4.5
).
Using Eq. (
5.1
), we obtain from Eq. (
2.5a
)

\Ai(x+x_{0})\Ai(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi^{3/2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\cos\left[k(x+x_{0}/2)-\frac{x_{0}^{2}}{4k}+\frac{k^{3}}{12}+\frac{\pi}{4}\right]\frac{dk}{k^{1/2}}.

(5.2)

Remarkably, this formula holds for any real
x
and
x_{0}
, including the
case
x_{0}<0
where Eq. (
5.1
) does not hold, because the integral over

\mathcal{O}
in Eq. (
4.5
) is canceled upon substituting into Eq. (
2.5a
).
Equation (
5.2
) with
x=v
and
x_{0}=u-v

reproduces the integral representation for
\Ai(u)\Ai(v)

obtained in Ref.
[
5
]
. We conclude that this integral
representation holds only for real
u
and
v
, which was not
indicated in Ref.
[
5
]
,
and its validity
within the present analysis relies on a nontrivial cancellation
of the integral over
\mathcal{O}
in Eq. (
4.5
).

6.
An application:
Green’s function for an electron in a static electric field

Integrals of the type (
2.6
) arise in many physical applications.
In particular, such an integral defines
the outgoing-wave Green’s function for an electron in a static
electric field, which plays an important role, e.g.,
in the theory of tunneling ionization
[
7
,
9
]
.
This function is the outgoing-wave solution of
the stationary Schrödinger equation
(here we use atomic units
\hbar=m=e=1
)

\left[-\frac{1}{2}\,\Delta+\mathbf{F}\mathbf{r}-E\right]G(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})=\delta(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}),

(6.1)

where
E
is the electron energy and
\mathbf{F}

is the electric field.
The solution can be obtained by Fourier transforming
the corresponding retarded Green’s function, which can be
found analytically, and is commonly presented in the form
[
9
]

G(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})=\frac{e^{-i\pi/4}}{(2\pi)^{3/2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp\left[iEt+\frac{i(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime})^{2}}{2t}-\frac{i}{2}\,\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{r}^{\prime})t-\frac{i}{24}\,\mathbf{F}^{2}t^{3}\right]\frac{dt}{t^{3/2}}.

(6.2)

This integral can be calculated using Eq. (
5.1
).
Let us introduce new variables

\xi=\frac{\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{r}^{\prime})-2E}{(2F)^{2/3}},\quad\eta=\frac{F^{1/3}}{2^{2/3}}|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}|,

(6.3)

where
F=|\mathbf{F}|
. Note that
\eta
is real and positive,
which is important for the applicability of Eq. (
5.1
).
Comparing Eq. (
6.2
) with Eq. (
5.1
), we obtain

G(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})=\frac{-e^{i\pi/6}}{|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}|}\frac{d}{d\eta}\Ai(\xi+\eta)\Ai(e^{2i\pi/3}(\xi-\eta)).

(6.4)

As far as we know, such a closed analytic form for
this Green’s function has never been presented in
the literature. Equation (
6.4
) enables one to extend the
theory of tunneling ionization

[
7
]
to strong fields beyond the weak-field
limit, which is very important for applications in attosecond
physics
[
10
]
.
In the limit
F\to 0
Eq. (
6.4
) reduces to

G(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})=\frac{e^{ik|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}|}}{2\pi|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}|},

(6.5)

where
k=\sqrt{2E}
. This is the well-known Green’s function
for a free electron.

7.
Conclusion

We have obtained integral representations for a complete set
of four linearly independent
solutions (
2.3
) and (
2.4
) of Eq. (
2.2
) given by
Eqs. (
3.1
), (
4.1
), and (
4.5
).
Any product (
2.1
) of two solutions of
the Airy equation (
1.1
) whose arguments are shifted by
z_{0}
can be expressed
in terms of these four functions, as in Eqs. (
2.5
), and hence
our results enable one to obtain an integral representation
for any such product.
This generalizes the work of Reid
[
4
]

who realized a similar program for the case
z_{0}=0
.
Using these results, we have obtained
the outgoing-wave Green’s function for an electron in a static
electric field in a closed analytic form, Eq. (
6.4
), which is important
for many applications in physics.
