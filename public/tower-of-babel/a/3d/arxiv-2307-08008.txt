On the Hardness of PosSLP
Peter Bürgisser, Gorav Jindal

Abstract
The problem PosSLP \operatorname{PosSLP} involves determining whether an integer computed by a given straight-line program is positive. This problem has attracted considerable attention within the field of computational complexity as it provides a complete characterization of the complexity associated with numerical computation. However, non-trivial lower bounds for PosSLP \operatorname{PosSLP} remain unknown. In this paper, we demonstrate that PosSLP ∈ 𝖡𝖯𝖯 \operatorname{PosSLP}\in{\mathsf{BPP}} would imply that 𝖭𝖯 ⊆ 𝖡𝖯𝖯 {\mathsf{NP}}\subseteq{\mathsf{BPP}} , under the assumption of a conjecture concerning the complexity of the radical of a polynomial proposed by Dutta, Saxena, and Sinhababu (STOC’2018). Our proof builds upon the established 𝖭𝖯 {\mathsf{NP}} -hardness of determining if a univariate polynomial computed by an SLP has a real root, as demonstrated by Perrucci and Sabia (JDA’2005). Therefore, our lower bound for PosSLP \operatorname{PosSLP} represents a significant advancement in understanding the complexity of this problem. It constitutes the first non-trivial lower bound for PosSLP \operatorname{PosSLP} , albeit conditionally. Additionally, we show that counting the real roots of an integer univariate polynomial, given as input by a straight-line program, is # ​ 𝖯 \#{\mathsf{P}} -hard.



1
Introduction

1.1
Straight-line Programs

Given an integer
a
as input, how do we decide whether
a
is positive
or negative? This question seems very innocuous at the first glance.
Indeed, if
a
is given as a bit string, the question is trivial.
This question becomes interesting when we are given a compact expression
for
a
instead of its bit string representation. One such compact
way to represent an integer is by an arithmetic circuit
or, equivalently, a straight-line program.
These are fundamental concepts studied in algebraic complexity theory:
we refer the reader to excellent surveys
[
SY10
,
Sap21
]
.

An
arithmetic circuit

is a directed acyclic graph, whose leaves are labeled by formal variables

x_{1}\mathchar 59\penalty\hskip 0.0pt\dots\mathchar 59\penalty\hskip 0.0ptx_{n}
or scalars from the underlying field
\mathbb{F}
. The non-leaf
nodes are arithmetic gates. We assume that there is a unique output
node and use the term gate for node interchangeably. Every gate of
such a circuit computes a multivariate polynomial in the canonical
way. The polynomial computed at the output gate is said to be the
polynomial computed by the circuit. The size of the circuit is defined
as the number of gates in it.
We shall restrict our attention to constant free arithmetic circuits,
which compute univariate polynomials.
We define a
straight-line program
P
, SLP
for short, to be a sequence
of univariate integer polynomials
(a_{0},a_{1}\mathchar 59\penalty\hskip 0.0pt\dots\mathchar 59\penalty\hskip 0.0pta_{\ell})

such that
a_{0}=1,a_{1}=x

and
a_{i}=a_{j}\circ a_{k}
for all
2\leq i\leq\ell
, where
\circ\in\{+,-,*\}

and
j,k<i
. We say that
P
computes the univariate polynomial

a_{\ell}
and that
P
has length
\ell
. Note that in this definition,
we do not allow any constants different from
1
. For an integer univariate polynomial
f\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
, we define
\tau(f)
as
the length of the smallest SLP which computes
f
. It is clear that
any SLP of length
\ell
can be described using
O(\ell\log\ell)

bits. Since an integer is a special case of a univariate polynomial,
SLPs can also compute integers.

The problem
\operatorname{DegSLP}
, introduced in
[
ABKPM09
]
, is the problem
of computing the degree of a polynomial given as input
by an arithmetic circuit.

1.2
PosSLP and Related Work

Now we formally define the
problem
\operatorname{PosSLP}
,
introduced in
[
ABKPM09
]
,
which is the central object of study of this paper

Problem 1.1

(
\operatorname{PosSLP}
)
.

Given an SLP
P
computing an integer
n_{P}
, decide if
n_{P}>0
.

This problem was introduced to establish a connection between classical models of computation and
the
Blum-Shub-Smale model

[
BCSS97
]
.
The latter is an extensively studied model for
studying computations with real numbers.
A BSS machine
M
runs according to a finite program and
takes as input a finite sequence of real numbers of arbitrary length, i.e., an element of
\cup_{n}\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Moreover,
M
has an infinite tape consisting of cells containing
real numbers or blanks. In each step,
M
can copy the content of one cell into another,
perform an arithmetic operation
\circ\in\{+,-,\times,\div\}

on two cells, or branch by comparing any cell to
0
.
The class
{\mathsf{P}}_{\mathbb{R}}^{0}

denotes the set of decision problems decided by polynomial time
by
constant free
BSS machines.
To compare this with classical complexity classes, defined via Turing machines,
one considers the Boolean part

{\mathsf{BP}}({\mathsf{P}}_{\mathbb{R}}^{0}):=\{L\cap\{0,1\}^{n}\mid L\in{\mathsf{P}}_{\mathbb{R}}\}
.

In
[
ABKPM09
]
, it was shown that
the computational power of this complexity class is given by polynomial time
computations with oracle calls to
\operatorname{PosSLP}
. That is:

Proposition 1.1
.

{\mathsf{P}}^{\operatorname{PosSLP}}=

{\mathsf{BP}}({\mathsf{P}}_{\mathbb{R}}^{0})
.

[
ABKPM09
]
also explained
the relevance of
\operatorname{PosSLP}
for numerical computation
in a more direct way, without referring to the formal model of BSS machines,
as follows.
For any nonzero real number
r
, we can write
r=s2^{m}
with
\frac{1}{2}\leq\left|s\right|<1

and
m\in\mathbb{Z}
. A floating point approximation of
r
with
k
significant
bits is a floating point number
t2^{m}
such that
\left|s-t\right|\leq 2^{-(k+1)}
.

Problem 1.2

(The generic task of numerical computation)
.

Given an arithmetic circuit
C
computing a polynomial

f(x_{1}\mathchar 59\penalty\hskip 0.0pt\dots\mathchar 59\penalty\hskip 0.0ptx_{n})
, given floating point numbers
a_{1}\mathchar 59\penalty\hskip 0.0pt\dots\mathchar 59\penalty\hskip 0.0pta_{n}
, and an
integer
k
in unary, along with a promise that
f(a_{1}\mathchar 59\penalty\hskip 0.0pt\dots\mathchar 59\penalty\hskip 0.0pta_{n})
is
nonzero, compute a floating point approximation of the value of the
output
f(a_{1}\mathchar 59\penalty\hskip 0.0pt\dots\mathchar 59\penalty\hskip 0.0pta_{n})
with
k
significant bits.

[
ABKPM09
]
showed that
1.2
is polynomial
time Turing equivalent to
\operatorname{PosSLP}
. This assertion and
1.1

support the hypothesis that
\operatorname{PosSLP}
does not have efficient algorithms.
In addition, it is not hard to see that the above defined problem
\operatorname{DegSLP}
reduces to
\operatorname{PosSLP}
.
This further suggests the computational intractability of
\operatorname{PosSLP}
,
given the belief that an efficient algorithm for
\operatorname{DegSLP}
is unlikely to exist.

As for upper bounds on
\operatorname{PosSLP}
, the following result
in terms of the counting hierarchy
[
Wag86
,
Kon09
,
AB09
]

is the best known result.

Theorem 1.1

(
[
ABKPM09
]
)
.

\operatorname{PosSLP}\in{\mathsf{P}}^{{\mathsf{PP}}^{{\mathsf{PP}}^{{\mathsf{PP}}}}}
.

Jindal and Saranurak
[
JS12
]
observed that if monotone SLP complexity
\tau_{+}
and SLP complexity
\tau
of positive integers are polynomially equivalent, then
\operatorname{PosSLP}\in\Sigma_{2}^{{\mathsf{P}}}\subseteq{\mathsf{PH}}
. There are
several other important problems which reduce to
\operatorname{PosSLP}
.
One such well-studied and important problem is the following.

Problem 1.3

(Sum-of-square-roots problem,
\operatorname{SSR}
)
.

Given a list
(a_{1}\mathchar 59\penalty\hskip 0.0pt\dots\mathchar 59\penalty\hskip 0.0pta_{n})
of positive integers and a positive integer

k
, decide if
\sum_{i\in[n]}\sqrt{a_{i}}\geq k
.

This is asked as an open problem in
[
GGJ76
]
. It has connections to the Euclidean traveling salesman problem. The Euclidean
traveling salesman problem is not known to be in
{\mathsf{NP}}
, but is readily
seen to be in
{\mathsf{NP}}
relative to an
\operatorname{SSR}
oracle.
By using classical Newton iteration,
\operatorname{SSR}
reduces to
\operatorname{PosSLP}

[
ABKPM09
]
.
The sum-of-square-roots problem was conjectured
to be in
{\mathsf{P}}
in
[
Mal01
]
.

Another important problem is to decide the inequality of succinctly
represented integers
[
ESY14
]
. More precisely,
consider the following problem.

Problem 1.4

(Inequality testing of succinctly represented integers)
.

Given positive integers
a_{1}\mathchar 59\penalty\hskip 0.0pt\dots\mathchar 59\penalty\hskip 0.0pta_{n},b_{1}\mathchar 59\penalty\hskip 0.0pt\dots\mathchar 59\penalty\hskip 0.0ptb_{n},c_{1}\mathchar 59\penalty\hskip 0.0pt\dots\mathchar 59\penalty\hskip 0.0ptc_{m},d_{1}\mathchar 59\penalty\hskip 0.0pt\dots\mathchar 59\penalty\hskip 0.0ptd_{m}
,
decide if
\prod_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}^{b_{i}}\geq\prod_{i=1}^{m}c_{i}^{d_{i}}.

This problem is easily seen to be a special case of
\operatorname{PosSLP}
.
It was shown in
[
ESY14
]
that
1.4

can be solved in deterministic polynomial time if one can prove strong
lower bounds on integer linear combinations of logarithms of positive
integers, known as the
Lang-Waldschmidt conjecture
[
Lan13
,
Wal04
]
,
see
[
ESY14
, Conjecture 3.2 ]
.
However,
1.1
still provides
the best unconditional upper bound for
1.4
.

1.3
Our Results

Despite the non-trivial, but rather inefficient upper bound of
1.1
,
no hardness results are known for
\operatorname{PosSLP}
. In this
paper, we show
\operatorname{PosSLP}\in{\mathsf{P}}
would have dramatic consequences
for complexity theory,
assuming a variant of the radical conjecture proposed in
[
DSS22
]
.

We define the
radical

\operatorname{rad}(f)
of a nonzero integer polynomial

f\in\mathbb{Z}[x_{1}\mathchar 59\penalty\hskip 0.0pt\dots\mathchar 59\penalty\hskip 0.0ptx_{n}]
as the product of the irreducible integer polynomials dividing
f
.
It is also called the
square-free part
of
f
.
Note that
\operatorname{rad}(f)
is uniquely defined up to a sign.
This generalizes the radical of a nonzero integer
n
,
which is defined as the product of the distinct prime numbers dividing
n
.

We shall crucially rely on the following conjecture, which is a constructive variant
of the radical conjecture proposed in
[
DSS22
]
.
See
section

5
for a discussion of
1.1
.

Conjecture 1.1

(Constructive univariate radical conjecture)
.

For any polynomial

f\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
, we have
\tau(\operatorname{rad}(f))\leq{\mathsf{poly}}(\tau(f)
).
Moreover, there is a randomized polynomial time algorithm which,
given an SLP of size
s
computing
f
,
constructs an SLP for
\operatorname{rad}(f)
of size
{\mathsf{poly}}(s)
with success probability at least

1-\frac{1}{\Omega(s^{1+\epsilon})}
for some
\epsilon>0
.

In fact, for our purposes, it is enough to know that
1.1

applies to some nonzero integer multiple of
\operatorname{rad}(f)
. The following result is the main contribution of this paper;
see
section

3
for the proof.

Theorem 1.2
.

If
1.1
is true and
\operatorname{PosSLP}\in{\mathsf{BPP}}
then
{\mathsf{NP}}\subseteq{\mathsf{BPP}}
.

We also show that counting the real roots of univariate polynomials
computed by straight-line programs is
\#{\mathsf{P}}
-hard.
For a univariate polynomial
F
, let us denote by
Z_{\mathbb{R}}(F)

the number of its real roots, counted with multiplicity.
Consider the problem:

Problem 1.5

(
\operatorname{CountRealRoots}
)
.

Given a SLP
P
computing a univariate
polynomial
f_{P}(x)\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
, compute
Z_{\mathbb{R}}(f_{P})
.

We prove the following result in
section

4
.

Theorem 1.3
.

\operatorname{CountRealRoots}

is
\#{\mathsf{P}}
-hard.

Proof Ideas

We rely on the proof of
{\mathsf{NP}}
-hardness due to
[
PS07
]
of

The idea for this reduction goes back to

,

Problem 1.6
.

Given an SLP
P

Proof idea for
1.2
:

The reduction in
[
PS07
]
computes for a given 3SAT formula
W
in polynomial time
an SLP computing a univariate polynomial

P\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
such that
W
is satisfiable iff
P
has a real root.
All the real roots of
P
are in
the interval
[-1,1]
. Moreover,
every real root of
P
(if any) has
multiplicity two, and
P
never attains negative values. Now
we call on
1.1
to construct an SLP
computing the radical
R:=\operatorname{rad}(P)
.
By the definition of the radical, the real roots of
R

are exactly the real roots of
P
, but with multiplicity one.
If
W
is not satisfiable, then
P
has no real roots, and therefore, neither does
R
.
In this case,
R
does not change signs on
[-1,1]
: either it completely remains
below the
x
-axis or completely remains above the
x
-axis.
On the other hand, if
W
is satisfiable, then
R
does cross the
x
-axis
at the real roots of
P
, because every root of
R
has multiplicity exactly one.
Hence it attains both negative and positive
values. Therefore:

•

If
W
is not satisfiable, then
R
does not change sign on the interval
[-1,1]
.

•

If
W
is satisfiable, then
R
attains both negative and positive values on
[-1,1]
.

Now we sample a random rational point
a
from the interval
[-1,1]
.
By oracle calls to
\operatorname{PosSLP}
, we can compute the sign of
R
at
a
and
1
.
By construction, if
W
is not satisfiable, then
R(a)
has the same sign as
R(1)
.
But if
W
is satisfiable, then there
are choices of
a
for which
R(a)
and
R(1)
have different signs.
Our novel contribution is that we can adapt the
{\mathsf{NP}}
-hardness
reduction in
[
PS07
]
such that

R(a)
and
R(1)
have different signs with a constant probability,
for a random
a\in[-1,1]
, provided
W
is satisfiable.
To this end, we will assume that
W
has at most one satisfying
assignment, which can be achieved using the randomized polynomial time
reduction of 3SAT to Unique-SAT
[
VV86
]
.
Under this assumption, the set of real roots of
R
has a simpler structure,
which allows us to prove that
R(a)
and
R(1)
have different signs
with constant probability for a random
a\in[-1,1]
,
see
section

3
for details.

Proof idea for
1.3
:

We use the ideas developed in Section 4 of
[
vzGKS96
]

and the reduction of
[
PS07
]
outlined above.
Let us denote by
\#W
the number of satisfying
assignments of a 3SAT formula

.
It is well known that computing
\#W
is
\#{\mathsf{P}}
-complete.
The strategy is to prove that

\#W
can be computed in polynomial time,
if oracle calls to
\operatorname{CountRealRoots}
are allowed.

For a given 3SAT formula
W
,
we compute the polynomial
P
from above, which has a real
root iff
W
is satisfiable.
It turns out that

P
has
N(\phi)
many roots for each satisfying
assignment
\phi
of
W
, where

N(\phi):=\prod_{p_{i}\not\in\phi}(p_{i}-1)
.
Here
\phi
is seen as a subset of a set of
n
odd
primes
p_{1}\mathchar 59\penalty\hskip 0.0pt\dots\mathchar 59\penalty\hskip 0.0ptp_{n}
, see
section

2
. In the reduction of
[
PS07
]
, one
can choose any odd primes

. Here we first choose an odd prime

, and
then the prime

is chosen from the arithmetic progression

.
This can be done efficiently because primes in arithmetic
progressions have sufficiently high density

]
.
This implies that

.
We show that as a consequence,

\#W\equiv\frac{1}{2}Z_{\mathbb{R}}(P)\bmod q
.
Therefore, by using oracle calls to
\operatorname{CountRealRoots}
, we can compute

for any odd prime

.
Doing so for sufficiently many odd primes

We refer to
section

for details.

In this section we mainly recall the reduction from
[

,

]

to prove that

{\mathsf{NP}}
-hard.
For a positive integer

Let us first note the following folklore result for later use.
It follows from the observation that the graph of a real polynomial crosses the

x

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
with
a<b
and
f

is a real univariate polynomial. Assume

. Then
f

has an even number of real roots (counted with multiplicity) in
(a,b)

if and only if
f(a)f(b)>0
. ∎

We also note the following easy observation for later use.

Lemma 2.2
.

Consider the following problem: given a univariate polynomial
F(x)
as an SLP
and

, compute the sign of

.
This problem reduces to
\operatorname{PosSLP}
under polynomial time many one reductions.

First notice that we have SLPs of length
O(\log p)
and
O(\log q)
,
which compute
p
and
q
, respectively.
Suppose
F(x)
is given by an SLP
P
of length
\ell
.
By induction on the length of SLPs, we show that we can efficiently construct from

P_{1}
,

of length

, computing

.

.

2.1
Chebychev polynomials

The Chebychev polynomials
T_{k}
are univariate polynomials in one variable
x

defined by

T_{0}(x):=1
,
T_{1}(x):=x
and for an integer
k\geq 2

by the recursion

T_{k}(x):=2xT_{k-1}(x)-T_{k-2}(x).

Clearly, the
T_{k}
are integer polynomials.
They have the following well known properties; see
[
KC91
]
.

1.

\deg(T_{k})=k
and the leading coefficient of
T_{k}
is
2^{k-1}
.

2.

T_{k}(x)=\cos(k\arccos(x))
for all
x\in[-1,1]
.

3.

The roots of
T_{k}
are
\left\{\cos\left(t\frac{\pi}{2k}\right)\mid t\in\{1,3,\ldots,2k-1\}\right\}
.

4.

For every
p,q\in\mathbb{N}
, we have
T_{p}\circ T_{q}=T_{pq}
, where
\circ
denotes the composition.

The following is an easy consequence of the properties of Chebychev polynomials.

Lemma 2.3

(Lemma 1 in
[
PS07
]
)
.

The Chebychev polynomial
T_{k}
can
be computed by an SLP of length
O(k)
. Moreover, if
k=pq
for

p,q\in\mathbb{N}
, then
T_{k}=T_{pq}
can be computed
by a straight-line program of length
O(p+q)
.

2.2
Real Roots of Univariate Polynomials and Straight-line Programs

We recall here the reduction
[
PS07
,
Pla84
]

from the well-known
{\mathsf{NP}}
-complete problem 3SAT to
1.6
.
The idea is to associate with the

n
literals
x_{1}\mathchar 59\penalty\hskip 0.0pt\dots\mathchar 59\penalty\hskip 0.0ptx_{n}
of a 3SAT formula
once and for all
n
distinct odd primes
p_{1}\mathchar 59\penalty\hskip 0.0pt\dots\mathchar 59\penalty\hskip 0.0ptp_{n}
.
It will be convenient to abbreviate
p_{\max}:=\max_{i}p_{i}
and
p_{\min}:=\min_{i}p_{i}
.
We put
M:=\prod_{i\in[n]}p_{i}
and
enumerate the roots of
the
M^{\textrm{th}}
Chebychev polynomial
T_{M}

by the odd integers

t\in\operatorname{Odd}(M):=\{1,3,\ldots,2M-1\}
.
Thus we define

r_{M}(t):=\cos\left(t\frac{\pi}{2M}\right)

(2.1)

and denote by

R_{M}:=\{r_{M}(t)\mid t\in\operatorname{Odd}(M)\}

the set of zeros of
T_{M}(x)
.
This defines the bijection

\operatorname{Odd}(M)\to R_{M},t\mapsto r_{M}(t)

whose inverse we denote by

r\mapsto t_{M}(r)
.

We write
X:=\{p_{1}\mathchar 59\penalty\hskip 0.0pt\dots\mathchar 59\penalty\hskip 0.0ptp_{n}\}
and identify
subsets
\phi\subseteq X
with Boolean assignments to the literals
x_{1}\mathchar 59\penalty\hskip 0.0pt\dots\mathchar 59\penalty\hskip 0.0ptx_{n}
.
More specifically,
x_{i}
is assigned ”true” if and only if
p_{i}\in\phi
.
We now consider the map

A_{M}:R_{M}\rightarrow\{0,1\}^{X},\quad r\mapsto\{p_{j}\mid p_{j}\text{ divides }t_{M}(r)\},

which assigns to a root
r
of
T_{M}

the set of prime divisors of
t_{M}(r)
.
Note that the function
A_{M}
is surjective but not injective.
In fact, the fiber of
A_{M}
over
\phi\in\{0,1\}^{X}

is given by

S_{M}(\phi):=A_{M}^{-1}(\phi)=\{r\in R_{M}\mid\gcd(t_{M}(r),M)=\alpha(\phi)\},

(2.2)

where we have set

\alpha(\phi):=\prod_{p\in\phi}p.

Finally, we assign to a 3SAT formula
W
over the literals
x_{1}\mathchar 59\penalty\hskip 0.0pt\dots\mathchar 59\penalty\hskip 0.0ptx_{n}

the union of the sets
S_{M}(\phi)
, taken over all satisfying Boolean assignments, that is,

S_{M}(W):=\bigcup_{\phi\in\{0,1\}^{X}\text{, }\phi\text{ satisfies }W}S_{M}(\phi).

We denote by
\operatorname{PolySAT}_{M}(W)
the
monic
univariate real polynomial
with the set of roots
S_{M}(W)
:

\operatorname{PolySAT}_{M}(W):=\prod_{r\in S_{M}(W)}(x-r).

Note that
\operatorname{PolySAT}_{M}(W)
is square free and only has real roots because it is a factor
T_{M}(x)
.
As in
[
PS07
]
, we can express
\operatorname{PolySAT}_{M}(W)
in terms
of the following analogues of the cyclotomic polynomials:

C_{\ell}(x):=\prod_{t\in\operatorname{Odd}(\ell)\text{, }\gcd(t,\ell)=1}(x-r_{\ell}(t)).

(2.3)

If
\ell
is odd, then the degree of
C_{\ell}
is given by the Euler totient function
(not to be confused with assignments
\phi
)

\deg C_{\ell}=\varphi(\ell).

(2.4)

eq.

2.2
implies that for an assignment
\psi\in\{0,1\}^{X}
, we
have:

\prod_{r\in S_{M}(\psi)}(x-r)=C_{M/\alpha(\psi)}(x).

(2.5)

This immediately implies that

\operatorname{PolySAT}_{M}(W)=\prod_{\psi\in\{0,1\}^{X},\text{ }\psi\text{ satisfies }W}C_{M/\alpha(\psi)}(x).

(2.6)

Note that all the integers
M/\alpha(\psi)
are odd. Therefore,
using
eq.

2.4
, we see that
the number of real roots of
\operatorname{PolySAT}_{M}(W)
is given by

Theorem 2.1

in

Suppose

. If

This is an immediate consequence of

We slightly modify the choice of the primes

(3.1)

We think of the multiplicative group

\operatorname{PolySAT}_{M}(W)=\prod_{j=1}^{\varphi(N)}\left(x-r_{N}(t_{j})\right).

The roots of

Suppose

From now on, to simplify notation, we drop the index

The above discussion implies that if we pick any real number

is negative. An interval

is odd, otherwise it is said to be even-indexed. Now our overall strategy can be summarized as follows

1.

Show that the sum of the lengths of odd-indexed intervals

is at least

Pick a “random” rational number

With probability at least

Compute the sign of

Out next goal is to show the following result.

Proposition 3.1
.

If
W
is satisfiable, then
the sum of the lengths of odd-indexed intervals in
\mathcal{I}
is at least
\frac{1}{\pi}
.

For this, we rely on the two technical lemmas below, whose proof is
postponed to
section

3.1
. We call a subinterval
J
of
[-1,1]

a
simple interval
if it connects two subsequent real roots of the Chebychev polynomial
T_{N}
.
This means that
J=((r_{N}(s_{1}),r_{N}(s_{2}))
for
s_{1},s_{2}\in\operatorname{Odd}(N)
with
s_{1}-s_{2}=2
.
Note that the end points of a simple such interval are not required to be zeros of
\operatorname{PolySAT}_{M}(W)
.

The first lemma states that a substantial part of the interval
(-1,1)

is subsumed by simple intervals in
\mathcal{I}
.

Lemma 3.2
.

The sum of the lengths of non-simple intervals in
\mathcal{I}

is at most
\frac{1}{n}
.

We also need that two adjacent simple intervals cannot differ too much in their lengths.

Lemma 3.3
.

Suppose
I,J
are two adjacent simple intervals (not necessarily in
\mathcal{I}
).
Then we have:

\frac{1}{\pi}\leq\frac{\operatorname{length}(I)}{\operatorname{length}(J)}\leq\pi.

Proof of
3.1
.

We denote by
\ell_{se}
the sum of the lengths of simple even-indexed intervals in
\mathcal{I}
.
Analogously,
\ell_{so}
denotes the sum of the lengths of simple odd-indexed intervals in
\mathcal{I}
,
and we write
\ell_{o}
for the sum of the lengths of
all
odd-indexed intervals in
\mathcal{I}
.
By
3.2

2\leq\ell_{se}+\ell_{so}+\frac{1}{n}\leq\ell_{se}+\ell_{o}+\frac{1}{n}
,
hence
\ell_{se}\geq 2-\ell_{o}-\frac{1}{n}
.

Consider a simple even-indexed interval
I_{j}\in\mathcal{I}
.
The interval
I_{j+1}
contains a simple interval
J
.
Therefore
\left|I_{j+1}\right|/\left|I_{j}\right|\geq\left|J\right|/\left|I_{j}\right|\geq\pi^{-1}
,
where the right inequality is due to
3.3
.
Applying this argument to all simple even-indexed intervals in
\mathcal{I}
,
and adding their length, we obtain that:

\ell_{o}\geq\frac{1}{\pi}\ell_{se}\geq\frac{1}{\pi}\big(2-\ell_{o}-\frac{1}{n}\big).

A simple calculation shows that the above equation implies

\ell_{o}\geq\frac{1}{\pi+1}\left(2-\frac{1}{n}\right)\geq\frac{1}{\pi}
for

.

.

Assume

We claim that for

\operatorname{PolySAT}_{M}(W)=C_{N}
, since the root set of
C_{N}
is exactly
\{r_{N}(t)\mid t\in\mathbb{Z}_{2N}^{\times}\}
. We have
\left|D\right|=N-\varphi(N)
. Suppose
I=((r_{N}(s_{1})),r_{N}(s_{2}))\in\mathcal{I}
is a non-simple interval as in
eq.

3.2
. Since
I
is non-simple, its endpoints are not subsequent roots of
T_{N}
. Hence
I
contains some roots of
T_{N}
which are not the roots of
C_{N}
. More precisely, there are exactly
\frac{s_{1}-s_{2}}{2}-1
elements of
D
in
I
.
The length of such an interval
I
can be bounded as:

\operatorname{length}(I)=\cos\left(s_{2}\frac{\pi}{2N}\right)-\cos\left(s_{1}\frac{\pi}{2N}\right)=2\sin\left(\frac{(s_{1}+s_{2})\pi}{4N}\right)\sin\left(\frac{(s_{1}-s_{2})\pi}{4N}\right)\leq\frac{(s_{1}-s_{2})\pi}{2N}.

Since
(s_{1}-s_{2})\geq 4
implies that
(s_{1}-s_{2})\leq 2(s_{1}-s_{2})-4
, we get that
the length of such an
I
can be bounded as:

\operatorname{length}(I)\leq\frac{(s_{1}-s_{2})\pi}{2N}\leq\frac{2\pi}{N}\left(\frac{s_{1}-s_{2}}{2}-1\right).

Hence if a non-simple interval
I
contains
m

elements of
D
, then we have:

\operatorname{length}(I)\leq\frac{2\pi}{N}m.

Since
\left|D\right|=N-\varphi(N)
, we get that the total length of such non-simple
intervals is at most
\frac{2\pi(N-\varphi(N))}{N}
. This proves that the total length of non-simple
intervals is at most
\frac{2\pi(N-\varphi(N))}{N}+\frac{\pi^{2}}{4N^{2}}
. Now we use the inequality:

1-x\geq e^{-2x}\text{ for all }0\leq x\leq\frac{1}{2}

(3.3)

to first obtain that:

\displaystyle\frac{\varphi(N)}{N}

\displaystyle=\prod_{p_{i}\mid N}\left(1-\frac{1}{p_{i}}\right)\geq\left(1-\frac{1}{p_{\min}}\right)^{n}

\displaystyle\geq e^{-\frac{2n}{p_{\min}}}\geq e^{-\frac{2}{n^{2}}}\geq 1-\frac{2}{n^{2}}.

(3.4)

By using
eq.

3.4
, we obtain:

\displaystyle\frac{2\pi(N-\varphi(N))}{N}+\frac{\pi^{2}}{4N^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq 2\pi\left(1-\frac{\varphi(N)}{N}\right)+\frac{\pi^{2}}{4N^{2}}\leq 2\pi\frac{2}{n^{2}}+\frac{\pi^{2}}{4N^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4\pi}{n^{2}}+\frac{\pi^{2}}{4n^{6}}\leq\frac{1}{n}.

In proving the above upper bound, we have have used the lower bound
N\geq p_{\min}\geq n^{3}
, as established in
eq.

3.1
. We also assumed
n
to be large enough.
Hence the sum of the lengths of the non-simple intervals is at most
\frac{1}{n}
.
∎

Proof of
3.3
.

Let
I:=\left(\cos\left(t\frac{\pi}{2N}\right),\cos\left((t+2)\frac{\pi}{2N}\right)\right)
,

J:=\left(\cos\left((t+2)\frac{\pi}{2N}\right),\cos\left((t+4)\frac{\pi}{2N}\right)\right)
.
We use the trigonometric identity
\cos A-\cos B=2\sin\left(\frac{A+B}{2}\right)\sin\left(\frac{B-A}{2}\right)

to obtain:

\displaystyle\frac{\cos\left(t\frac{\pi}{2N}\right)-\cos\left((t+2)\frac{\pi}{2N}\right)}{\cos\left((t+2)\frac{\pi}{2N}\right)-\cos\left((t+4)\frac{\pi}{2N}\right)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sin\left((t+1)\frac{\pi}{2N}\right)}{\sin\left((t+3)\frac{\pi}{2N}\right)}.

We have the following cases:

Case
1.

In this case, we assume
(t+3)\frac{\pi}{2N}\leq\frac{\pi}{2}
. Now
we use the well known inequality:

\frac{2}{\pi}x\leq\sin(x)\leq x\text{ for }0<x\leq\frac{\pi}{2}.

This implies that:

\frac{2}{\pi}\frac{t+1}{t+3}\leq\frac{\sin\left((t+1)\frac{\pi}{2N}\right)}{\sin\left((t+3)\frac{\pi}{2N}\right)}\leq 1.

Since
\frac{t+1}{t+3}\geq\frac{1}{2}
. We obtain that:

\frac{1}{\pi}\leq\frac{\sin\left((t+1)\frac{\pi}{2N}\right)}{\sin\left((t+3)\frac{\pi}{2N}\right)}\leq 1.

Case
2.

Now consider the case when
(t+3)\frac{\pi}{2N}>\frac{\pi}{2}
. By
using the equality
\sin(\pi-\theta)=\sin(\theta)
, we obtain:

\frac{\sin\left((t+1)\frac{\pi}{2N}\right)}{\sin\left((t+3)\frac{\pi}{2N}\right)}=\frac{\sin\left((2N-(t+1))\frac{\pi}{2N}\right)}{\sin\left((2N-(t+3))\frac{\pi}{2N}\right)}.

In this case, we also know that
(2N-(t+1))\frac{\pi}{2N}\leq\frac{\pi}{2}
.
By using the result of the first case, we know that:

\frac{1}{\pi}\leq\frac{\sin\left((2N-(t+3))\frac{\pi}{2N}\right)}{\sin\left((2N-(t+1))\frac{\pi}{2N}\right)}\leq 1,

which proves the claim.

∎

4
Hardness of Counting Real Roots

We first recall some standard definitions of counting complexity classes from
[
AB09
]
.
A function
f:\{0,1\}^{*}\to\mathbb{N}
is in
\#{\mathsf{P}}
if there exists a
polynomial
p:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
and a polynomial-time Turing machine
M

such that for every
x\in\{0,1\}^{*}

f(x)=\left|\{y\in\{0,1\}^{p\left(\left|x\right|\right)}:M(x,y)=1\}\right|.

A function
f:\{0,1\}^{*}\to\mathbb{N}
is
\#{\mathsf{P}}
-hard if every
g\in\#{\mathsf{P}}

can be computed in polynomial time, allowing oracle calls to
f
.
We say that
f
is
\#{\mathsf{P}}
-complete if it is in
\#{\mathsf{P}}
and
\#{\mathsf{P}}
-hard.
We denote by #3SAT the problem of computing, for a given 3SAT formula
W
,
the number of satisfying assignments for
W
.
It is well known that #3SAT is
\#{\mathsf{P}}
-complete.

The main result of this section can be restated as:
See
1.3

As in
section

2.2
,
for a given 3SAT formula
W
defined over
n
literals,
we choose
n
distinct odd primes
p_{1}\mathchar 59\penalty\hskip 0.0pt\dots\mathchar 59\penalty\hskip 0.0ptp_{n}

associated with the literals, and we set
M:=\prod_{i\in[n]}p_{i}
.
According to
2.1
, for given
W
,
one can compute in polynomial time an SLP, which computes a univariate integral polynomial

P_{M}(W)
, which has the same set of real roots
as
\operatorname{PolySAT}_{M}(W)
, albeit with multiplicity two.
From
eq.

2.7
, we obtain the following equality for the number of real roots.

\frac{1}{2}Z_{\mathbb{R}}(P_{M}(W))=Z_{\mathbb{R}}(\operatorname{PolySAT}_{M}(W))=\sum_{\psi\text{ satisfies }W}\varphi(M/\alpha(\psi)).

(4.1)

Lemma 4.1
.

Suppose
q
is an odd prime. Let
p_{1}\mathchar 59\penalty\hskip 0.0pt\dots\mathchar 59\penalty\hskip 0.0ptp_{n}

be
n
distinct primes in the arithmetic progression
\{aq+2\mid a\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}\}
.
As above, define
M:=\prod_{i\in[n]}p_{i}
. Then for any 3SAT
formula
W
defined over
n
literals, we have:

\#W\bmod q\equiv\frac{1}{2}Z_{\mathbb{R}}(P_{M}(W))\bmod q.

Proof.

By assumption,
p_{i}
is an odd prime and

p_{i}-1\equiv 1\bmod q
.
Let
N=M/\alpha(\psi)
be as in
eq.

4.1
, say

N=\prod_{i\in I}p_{i}
for
I\subseteq[n]
.
Then,

\varphi(N)\bmod q\equiv\prod_{i\in I}(p_{i}-1)\equiv 1\bmod q.

Therefore, using
eq.

4.1
,

Z_{\mathbb{R}}(\operatorname{PolySAT}_{M}(W))\equiv\sum_{\psi\text{ satisfies }W}1\bmod q\equiv\#W\bmod q,

which shows the assertion.
∎

We also need the following lemma.

Lemma 4.2
.

There is a polynomial time algorithm, which on input a natural number
n
(encoded in unary) computes distinct odd primes
q_{1}\mathchar 59\penalty\hskip 0.0pt\dots\mathchar 59\penalty\hskip 0.0ptq_{n}
and, for each
i\in[n]
,
computes a collection
p_{i1},\ldots,p_{in}
of distinct primes
such that
p_{ij}\equiv 2\bmod q_{i}
for all
i\in[n],j\in[n]
.

Proof.

We can directly use the algorithm developed for Theorem 4.11 of
[
vzGKS96
]
.
Step 3 of that algorithm can be skipped.
Moreover, after replacing the randomized primality testing by
deterministic primality testing of
[
AKS04
]
, the algorithm becomes deterministic polynomial time.
∎

Proof of
1.3
.

We reduce #3SAT to
\operatorname{CountRealRoots}
. For given
n
,
we first compute in polynomial time the primes
q_{i}
and
p_{ij}
for
i,j\in[n]

as in
4.2
.
Then we set
M_{i}:=p_{i1}p_{i2}\ldots p_{in}
for
i\in[n]
.
For a given a 3SAT formula
W
over
n
literals,
we first compute in polynomial time SLPs computing the polynomials
P_{M_{i}}(W)
for
i\in[n]

according to
2.1
.
Then we compute the number of real zeros
Z_{\mathbb{R}}(P_{M_{i}}(W))
for

by oracle calls to
\operatorname{CountRealRoots}
.
We divide these even numbers by two and have thus computed

for

according to

.
Since

and

, by using efficient algorithms for Chinese

]

We have thus shown that

5
Complexity of Radicals

5.1
Complexity of Factors

For a polynomial
f\in\mathbb{F}[x_{1}\mathchar 59\penalty\hskip 0.0pt\dots\mathchar 59\penalty\hskip 0.0ptx_{n}]
, we define
L(f)
as the size
of the smallest arithmetic circuit computing
f

from the variables
x_{i}
and
any constants
in the field
\mathbb{F}
.

The
Factor Conjecture

[
Bür00
,
Bür04
]

is a central question in algebraic complexity theory.
It asks whether complexity of factors
g
of
f
can be bounded by a polynomial
in terms of
L(f)
and the degree of
g
.

Conjecture 5.1

(Factor Conjecture)
.

Over a field
\mathbb{F}
of characteristic zero,
we have
L(g)\leq{\mathsf{poly}}(L(f)+\deg(g))
for any factor
g
of
f\in\mathbb{F}[x_{1}\mathchar 59\penalty\hskip 0.0pt\dots\mathchar 59\penalty\hskip 0.0ptx_{n}]
.

Evidence for this conjecture comes from
[
Bür04
]
,
where it was shown that the conjecture is true for approximate complexity.
In addition, the factor conjecture is supported by the following result.
It tells us that the only situation in which
5.1
could fail
is when the factor
g
occurs with exponentially large multiplicity
e
.

Theorem 5.1

(
[
Kal87
,
Bür00
]
)
.

If
f,g,h\in\mathbb{F}[x_{1}\mathchar 59\penalty\hskip 0.0pt\dots\mathchar 59\penalty\hskip 0.0ptx_{n}]
are such
that
f=g^{e}h
with
g,h
being coprime, the multiplicity
e
a positive integer,
and
\operatorname{char}(\mathbb{F})=0
, then, setting
d=\deg g
, we have

L(g)=O(M(d)^{2}M(de)(L(f)+n+d\log e)).

Here
M(m)=O(m^{2})
stands for the number of arithmetic operations sufficient to multiply two degree
m
polynomials over
\mathbb{F}
.

Now consider the following related conjecture proposed in

[
DSS22
, Conjecture 1]
.

Conjecture 5.2

(Radical conjecture)
.

For a nonzero polynomial
f\in\mathbb{Z}[x_{1}\mathchar 59\penalty\hskip 0.0pt\dots\mathchar 59\penalty\hskip 0.0ptx_{n}]
we have

\min\{\deg(\operatorname{rad}(f)),L(\operatorname{rad}(f))\}\leq{\mathsf{poly}}(L(f)).

This essentially states that either the
degree of
\operatorname{rad}(f)
or the complexity of
\operatorname{rad}(f)

(or both), are polynomially bounded in the complexity of
f
.
For a univariate polynomial
f\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
,

5.2
implies that

L(\operatorname{rad}(f))\leq{\mathsf{poly}}(L(f)).

(5.1)

This follows since
L(g)=O(\deg(g))
for any
g\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
.

To arrive at the main contribution of our paper (
1.2
),
we rely on the following constructive variant of

eq.

5.1
, formulated for
\tau(f)
instead
of
L(f)
. (Recall that
\tau(f)
denotes the complexity for constant-free arithmetic circuits.)

See
1.1

Remark 5.1
.

The main difference of these radical conjectures, in comparison with the factor conjecture,
is that they make a statement about the complexity of factors
g
having huge degree.
While there is currently not much evidence for these radical conjectures, one may still view

5.1
as an indication towards them, since it shows that
small multiplicities help. When passing to the radical to
f
, all multiplicities
of the irreducible factors
g
of
f
are set to one. As for the plausibility of the constructive version

1.1
, we just note that statements on the existence of small
SLPs usually can be refined to statements concerning randomized effective constructions.

6
Conclusion and Open Questions

Assuming the constructive radical conjecture (
1.1
),
we proved that
\operatorname{PosSLP}\in{\mathsf{BPP}}
would imply
{\mathsf{NP}}\subseteq{\mathsf{BPP}}
. This was achieved by first reducing 3SAT to Unique-SAT
[
VV86
]

and then using ideas developed in
[
Pla84
,
PS07
]
. This
leads to the first non-trivial lower bound for
\operatorname{PosSLP}
, albeit
conditional. Using the ideas developed in
[
vzGKS96
]
,
we also proved that counting the real roots of univariate polynomials
computed by a given SLP is
\#{\mathsf{P}}
-hard.

There are several avenues for further research:

1.

Of course it remains intriguing to prove the unconditional
{\mathsf{NP}}
-hardness
of
\operatorname{PosSLP}
. In our approach, we first constructed an SLP for the
polynomial
P_{M}(W)
, which has same set of real roots as
\operatorname{PolySAT}_{M}(W)
,
but with multiplicity two.
One could try to directly construct a polynomial size SLP for
\operatorname{PolySAT}_{M}(W)
, which has simple
real roots. But this is unlikely because
\operatorname{PolySAT}_{M}(W)=1
iff
W

is not satisfiable. And we can check
\operatorname{PolySAT}_{M}(W)=1
by polynomial
identify testing. This would imply
{\mathsf{coNP}}\subseteq{\mathsf{coRP}}
, which is
not believed to be true.

2.

We saw that the sum-of-square-roots problem and inequality testing of
succinctly represented integers are special cases of
\operatorname{PosSLP}
.
We do not know of any non-trivial upper or lower bounds for these problems. Perhaps one can first study the complexity of these special cases.

3.

In addition, the following special case of
\operatorname{PosSLP}
are worth investigating:
A univariate polynomial
f(x)=a+bx^{\beta}+cx^{\gamma}\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
with

\beta,\gamma\in\mathbb{N}
is called a
trinomial
. Koiran
[
Koi19
]

proved that the roots of trinomials are “well-separated” and
he posed the following problem: Given a rational
p/q
and a trinomial
f(x)
as inputs, determine the sign of
f(p/q)
. This problem is easily seen to be a special case of
\operatorname{PosSLP}
.

[
BDR22
]
proved that this
problem can be solved in deterministic polynomial time, except on a
\frac{1}{\Omega(\log(dH))}

fraction of the inputs. Here
\deg(f)\leq d
and
\max\{\left|a\right|,\left|b\right|,\left|c\right|,\left|p\right|,\left|q\right|\}\leq H
.
Can we find efficient algorithms for Koiran’s problem?

4.

[
Pla84
]
proved that the following problem is
{\mathsf{NP}}
-hard:
decide for a given
k
-sparse polynomial whether it has a root on the unit circle.
Can we prove a similar lower bound for
k
-
sparse polynomials
?
