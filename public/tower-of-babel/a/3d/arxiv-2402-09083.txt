Measures for the summation of Irwin series
Jean-François Burnol

Abstract
. We consider the series of reciprocals of those positive integers with exactly k k occurrences of a given b b -ary digit d d (Irwin series), and obtain for their sums geometrically convergent representations. They are expressed in terms of the moments and Stieltjes transforms of certain measures on the unit interval and involve certain recurrences which convert straightforwardly into a numerical implementation. This framework allows a new perspective on the limit for large k k , and is the basis for obtaining, as the author has done in further works, the asymptotics for large b b .

2.
Notations and terminology

Let
b>1
be a integer, which is kept fixed throughout the paper. The set

\mathbb{N}
is defined as
\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
(i.e., it contains zero). We define
“
b
-imal” numbers as the elements of
\cup_{l\geq 0}b^{-l}\mathbb{Z}
. For
x

such a
b
-imal number, the
smallest
l\geq 0
with
x\in b^{-l}\mathbb{Z}
is called its “depth”.

Let
\mathsf{D}=\{0,\dots,b-1\}
be the set of
b
-ary digits. We pick a

d\in\mathsf{D}
which will be fixed throughout the paper. The case
d=0
has some
specifics and will require special consideration at some locations. We let

\mathsf{A}=\mathsf{D}\setminus\{d\}
. We let
N=\#\mathsf{A}=b-1
and
N_{1}=\#(\mathsf{A}\setminus\{0\})
.

The space of “strings”
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}}
is defined to be the union of all
cartesian products
\mathsf{D}^{l}
for
l\geq 0
. Notice that for
l=0
one has

\mathsf{D}^{0}=\{\emptyset\}
i.e. the set with one element. We will call this
special element the “none-string”. The length
|X|=l
of a string
X
is
the integer
l
such that
X\in\mathsf{D}^{l}
. For
l=1
a string is thus the same
thing as a digit, although for the sake of clarity it is better to imagine
those two as separate things. There is a map from strings to integers which to

X=(d_{l},\dots,d_{1})
assigns
n(X)=d_{l}b^{l}+\dots+d_{1}
(and
which sends the none-string to zero). This map is many-to-one, and each
integer
n
has a unique minimal length representation
X(n)
which is called

the
(minimal)
b
-representation of
n
.

The length of an integer
n
is
l(n)=|X(n)|
. Thus,
l(n)
is the smallest
non-negative
integer such that
n<b^{l(n)}
. Note that
l(0)=0
, not
1
.

Let us now define for
k\geq 0
:

\mathcal{X}^{(k)}=\{X\in\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}},X\textit{contains
the digit }d\textit{ exactly }k\textit{ times}\}

Note that
\mathcal{X}^{(k)}
contains the none-string if and only if
k=0
. We write

\mathcal{X}^{(k)}_{l}
for the subsets of strings of length
l
. Let
\mathcal{A}^{(k)}
be the
set of non-negative integers with minimal
b
-imal representation in
\mathcal{X}^{(k)}
, and

\mathcal{A}^{(k)}_{l}
the subset of those of length
l
. There is a
partition of
\mathbb{N}=\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
as
\mathcal{A}^{(0)}\cup\mathcal{A}^{(1)}\cup\mathcal{A}^{(2)}\cup\dots
. We thus write
k_{b,d}(n)
(soon we will simply write
k(n)
) for
the number of times the digit
d
appears in the minimal
b
-representation of
the integer
n
. With such notations, the Irwin sums considered in this paper
are:

H_{b,d}^{(k)}=\sum_{n>0,k_{b,d}(n)=k}\frac{1}{n}

As we are handling only positive terms, the ordering is indifferent and we can
use set-theoretic notations with no restrictions. These quantities are
finite. Indeed the cardinality of length
l
strings satisfying the
requirements, hence a fortiori the cardinality of the length
l
integers in

\mathcal{A}^{(k)}
is bounded above by
\binom{l}{k}N^{l-k}=O(l^{k}N^{l})
(recall
here that
N=b-1
) and each admissible term with denominator of length
l

contributes at most
(1/b)^{l-1}
. Thus grouping terms by the length of their
denominators we obtain a geometrically converging series.

Numerically, this becomes infeasible in practice, say for
b=10
and

l\geq 20
on personal computers. let us add some
specific notation for these “blocks”. For
k\geq 0
and
j\geq 1
:

S_{b,d;l}^{(k)}=\sum_{l(n)=l,k_{b,d}(n)=k}\frac{1}{n}

As the base
b
and the digit
d
are fixed throughout the paper we shall most
of the time drop them from the subscripts and we write simply:

H^{(k)}=\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}S_{l}^{(k)},\hskip 20.00003ptS_{l}^{(k)}=\sideset{}{{}^{(k)}}{\sum}_{l(n)=l}\frac{1}{n}

where the index
l
refers to the number of digits in the denominators. We
shall also use self-explanatory notation such as
S_{l}^{(\leq k)}
for

\sum_{j\leq k}S_{l}^{(j)}
. Note that
H_{2,1}^{(0)}=0
as the sum (and each
sub-block) is over the empty set.

3.
The new series representing Irwin sums

We gather here together the main results directly relevant to numerical
evaluations. The proofs are given later. The case of
b=2
,

and

Theorem 1
.

Let
b\in\mathbb{N}
,
b>1
and
d\in\{0,\dots,b-1\}
a digit in base
b
. Let

. The sub-sum of the harmonic series

which keeps

k
occurrences of the digit
d

be expressed as:

(1)

H^{(k)}=\sideset{}{{}^{(k)}}{\sum}_{0<n<b^{l-1}}\frac{1}{n}+b\cdot\sideset{}{{}^{(\leq k)}}{\sum}_{b^{l-1}\leq n<b^{l}}\frac{1}{n}+\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{m}\;\sideset{}{{}^{(\leq k)}}{\sum}_{b^{l-1}\leq n<b^{l}}\;\frac{u_{k-k(n);m}}{n^{m+1}}

Here
l\geq 1
is arbitrary.
The inverse powers
1/n^{m+1}
involved are with the integers
n

having in base
b
exactly
l
digits of which at most
k
are the digit

d
.

The coefficients
u_{j;m}
,
0\leq j\leq k
,

, arising in the above
alternating series

•

are independent of

,

•

are non-negative and vanish only if

•

,

•

•

The sequence

obeys:

(2)

m\geq 1\implies(b^{m+1}-b+1)u_{0;m}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\binom{m}{j}\gamma_{j}u_{0:m-j}

to

Remark 2
.

As the alternating series starts with
m=1
, the sum of the initial terms
gives an upper bound. With

,

In all other cases the first term

4.
Irwin sums as integrals

Let us first recall that when we have an enumerable subset
\{x_{1},x_{2},\dots\}

of the real line, and a series with
non-negative
terms
\sum_{n\geq 1}c_{n}
,
possibly diverging, one can define the set-function
\mu:\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\cup\{+\infty\}
which assigns to
any
subset
G
of the real line the
quantity
\mu(G)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}c_{n}\mathbf{1}_{G}(x_{n})\in[0,\infty]
.
This set-function is countably additive and we call it a (non-negative,
discrete, possibly infinite) measure. We define integrable functions as being
those functions with
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}c_{n}|f(x_{n})|<\infty
. Then

\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x)\,d\mu(x)
is defined as expected and obeys the usual theorems
and also is invariant under any rearrangement of the indexing of the set

\{x_{i}\}
. We will make free use of the notations of measures and integrals
in the following. We write
\mu=\sum_{i}c_{i}\delta_{x_{i}}
, where the
countable index set does not have to be
\mathbb{N}
. This abstract language is but
an alternative notation for direct re-arrangements of various summable series.
In this paper we only have measures supported in
[0,1)
and having finite
total mass, so that they are also
bona fide
distributions. The

support
is the set
\{x_{i}\}
but if continuity gets involved as in
distribution theory, then a notion of
closed support
emerges. But we
shall not engage into considerations of this type.

Definition 8
.

The measure
\mu_{b,d}^{(k)}
, or for short in the sequel
\mu_{k}
, is defined to
be the infinite combination of Dirac masses at the rational numbers
x=n(X)/b^{|X|}
, for
X\in\mathcal{X}^{(k)}
, with respective weights
1/b^{|X|}
:

(7)

\mu_{k}=\sum_{X\in\mathcal{X}^{(k)}}\frac{1}{b^{|X|}}\delta_{\frac{n(X)}{b^{|X|}}}

It is supported in
[0,1)
.

Remark 9
.

In our previous work
[

]
of which the present paper is a

Remark 10
.

Suppose

. Then the strings with

, i.e. the none-string
and those containing only

if and only if

.
For
k=0
there is
thus a total weight of

assigned to
the Dirac at the origin. But for

and

, there is no such Dirac mass.

On the other hand if

, then

. So here, there is then always a Dirac mass at the

.

In the next calculation displaying the binomial series, one has

:

\displaystyle\mu_{k}([0,1))

\displaystyle=\sum_{l\geq 0}b^{-l}\#\{X\in\mathcal{X}^{(k)}\cap\mathsf{D}^{l}\}=\sum_{l\geq k}b^{-l}\binom{l}{k}N^{l-k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{p=0}^{\infty}b^{-k-p}\binom{k+p}{k}N^{p}=b^{-k}\frac{1}{(1-N/b)^{k+1}}=\frac{b}{(b-N)^{k+1}}

And as here

this gives

. Hence the (positive)
\mu_{k}
measure is
finite and its total mass is independent of

. Dividing by
b
we could
start using the language of probability theory, and perhaps it would then be
better to use the space of strings
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}}
or some other space as
the probability space but we have chosen not to go into that direction (here).
