Higher genus relative Gromov–Witten theory and double ramification cycles
Honglu Fan, Longting Wu, Fenglong You

Abstract
. We extend the definition of relative Gromov–Witten invariants with negative contact orders to all genera. Then we show that relative Gromov–Witten theory forms a partial CohFT. Some cycle relations on the moduli space of stable maps are also proved.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Overview

Gromov–Witten theory is the first modern approach in enumerative geometry. It can be viewed as a theory that virtually counts curves in smooth projective varieties. A lot of structural properties have been developed for Gromov–Witten theory since 1990s. On the other hand, relative Gromov–Witten theory naturally appears when one considers counting curves in a smooth projective variety
X
with tangency conditions along a divisor
D
. Relative Gromov–Witten invariants are defined by Li–Ruan
[
14
]
, J. Li
[
15
]
,
[
16
]
and Ionel–Parker
[
11
]
nearly two decades ago. However, structural properties of relative Gromov–Witten theory still have a lot to develop.

The structures of genus-zero relative Gromov–Witten theory have been recently developed in
[
9
]
. The main motivation for
[
9
]
is the equality between genus-zero relative Gromov–Witten invariants of the smooth pair
(X,D)
and the orbifold Gromov–Witten invariants of the
r
th root stack
X_{D,r}
when
r
is sufficiently large (see
[
1
]
and
[
20
]
). Such equality indicates a possibility of converting known results of the orbifold theory into the relative theory. However, the results in
[
1
]
and
[
20
]
only involve orbifold Gromov–Witten invariants with small ages. In
[
9
]
, we introduced genus-zero relative invariants with negative contact orders and proved that they are equal to orbifold invariants with large ages (up to a suitable power of
r
). Several structural properties of genus-zero relative invariants can be proved using corresponding properties of orbifold invariants. These properties include relative quantum cohomology, topological recursion relation (TRR), WDVV equation, Givental’s formalism and genus-zero Virasoro constraints.

This paper is a sequel to
[
9
]
. We study the structures of higher genus relative Gromov–Witten invariants by introducing higher genus relative invariants with negative contact orders. While higher genus relative invariants and orbifold invariants are not equal in general, the result of
[
20
]
shows that orbifold invariants are polynomials in
r
for a sufficiently large
r
and relative invariants are equal to the constant terms of the orbifold invariants. The result in
[
20
]
only contains orbifold invariants with small ages. In this paper, we extend the result of
[
20
]
to orbifold invariants with large ages. The key to our results is the Theorem
4.1
relating the pushforward of DR cycles and Hurwitz–Hodge classes. It has two consequences. Firstly, it can be used to generalize the definition of genus zero relative theory in
[
9
]
to all genera relative theory which satisfies a partial cohomological field theory (partial CohFT for short, see Section
3.5
for more details). Secondly, it also generalizes
[
7
]
to DR cycles with target varieties to produce some relations (see Section
5
for more details).

1.2.
Relative Gromov–Witten theory

We first focus on relative Gromov–Witten theory with possibly negative contact orders in the sense of
[
9
]
. We quickly review the idea of
[
9
]
. We presented two definitions of relative Gromov–Witten cycles. In the first definition, we simply defined relative virtual cycles as a limit of orbifold virtual cycles. In the second definition, we first defined a type of bipartite graphs. Given a bipartite graph
{\mathfrak{G}}
, we associated each vertex with a moduli space, and glued them up using fiber products according to edges, thus forming a stack
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathfrak{G}}
. It also came with an “obstruction class”
\iota^{*}C_{\mathfrak{G}}\in A^{*}(\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathfrak{G}})
(for
\iota
, see equation (
8
)). And finally, we defined the relative Gromov–Witten cycle with negative contact as the pushforward of
\iota^{*}C_{\mathfrak{G}}\cap[\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathfrak{G}}]^{\mathrm{vir}}
divided by the number of automorphisms to the corresponding moduli of stable maps.

In
[
9
]
, we already laid down the set-up of bipartite graphs for all genera. It is natural to expect higher genus graphs lead to higher genus relative Gromov–Witten cycles. However, at that time we still made genus-zero restrictions on the bipartite graphs in the main results because no evidence was found to justify the usefulness of the higher genus generalization. In this paper, we aim to present progress in the higher genus generalization. The main point of this paper can be summarized as follows:

If we simply apply the same definition of relative Gromov–Witten cycles in
[
9
]
without genus-zero constraints on vertices and admissible bipartite graphs, the resulting all-genus relative theory with negative contact orders would satisfy a partial CohFT in the sense of
[
17
]
.

Ultimately, we look for all structures in the relative Gromov–Witten theory, and hopefully they might help us deal with long-standing questions in this field (for example, how to write down a Virasoro operator for relative theory). As a very subjective remark, the results in this paper perhaps suggest that extending
[
9
]
to higher genera might be a reasonable idea in the search of broader structures. According to
[
17
]
, a partial CohFT is a CohFT without the loop gluing axiom. Finding a replacement of this loop gluing axiom could also be an interesting question, which further relates with how to extend higher genus Givental quantization and how to write down all genus Virasoro operators.

1.3.
Double ramification cycles

Double ramification cycles on the moduli space of curves are tautological classes and can be defined using relative Gromov–Witten theory of
(\mathbb{P}^{1},0\cup\infty)
. An explicit formula for double ramification cycles is given in
[
12
]
. An introduction to
tautological classes and double ramification cycles can be found in
[
18
]
.

Recently, double ramification cycles with target varieties have been studied in
[
13
]
. Given a line bundle
L
over
D
, the double ramification cycle
\text{DR}_{\Gamma}(D,L)
with the target variety
D
is defined using the moduli space
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{\Gamma}^{\sim}(D)
of relative stable maps to rubber targets over
D
.
By
[
13
]
, the double ramification cycle
\text{DR}_{\Gamma}(D,L)
is equal to the constant term of the polynomial class
P_{\Gamma}^{g,r}(D,L)
, see Section
5
for the definition.

More generally, the polynomial class
P_{\Gamma}^{d,r}(D,L)
is a tautological class in the moduli space of stable maps to
D
. When the target variety
D
is a point, it is conjectured by A. Pixton that the constant term of
P_{\Gamma}^{d,r}(D,L)
vanishes for
d>g
. When the target variety
D
is a point, the conjecture is proved in
[
7
]
. The proof of
[
7
]
has been generalized to general targets in
[
5
]
.

The formula for double ramification cycles in
[
12
]
and
[
13
]
are obtained by relating it to certain Hurwitz–Hodge cycles which are also polynomials in
r
and the constant terms coincide with the constant terms of
P_{\Gamma}^{d,r}(D,L)
. In Section
4
, we generalize the identity between Hurwitz–Hodge cycles and double ramification cycles. As a direct consequence, Hurwitz–Hodge cycles satisfy a refined polynomiality (see Corollary
4.2
) which implies the vanishing theorem for
P_{\Gamma}^{d,r}(D,L)
in
[
7
]
and
[
5
]
.

Relative and orbifold Gromov–Witten theories

In this section, we first give a brief review of relative Gromov–Witten theory defined by Li–Ruan
[
14
]
, J. Li
[
15
]
,
[
16
]
and Ionel–Parker
[
11
]
. We then also review orbifold Gromov–Witten theory following
[
2
]
,
[
3
]
,
[
6
]
and
[
21
]
. For the orbifold theory, we only focus on root stacks and gerbes as target spaces.

2.1.
Relative theory

Let
X
be a smooth projective variety and
D
a smooth divisor. The intersection number of a curve class
\beta
with a divisor
D
is denoted by
\int_{\beta}D
.

A
topological type

\Gamma
is a tuple
(g,n,\beta,\rho,\vec{\mu})
where
g,n
are non-negative integers,
\beta\in H_{2}(X,\mathbb{Z})
is a curve class and
\vec{\mu}=(\mu_{1},\dotsc,\mu_{\rho})\in\mathbb{Z}^{\rho}
is a partition of the number
\int_{\beta}D
. Furthermore, we must have

(1)

\mu_{i}>0\text{ for }1\leq i\leq\rho.

Let
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{\Gamma}(X,D)
be the moduli of relative stable maps. There is a stabilization map
\mathfrak{s}:\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{\Gamma}(X,D)\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n+\rho}(X,\beta)
. Write
\bar{\psi}_{i}=\mathfrak{s}^{*}\psi_{i}
. There are evaluation maps

\displaystyle\operatorname{ev}_{X}=(\operatorname{ev}_{X,1},\ldots,\operatorname{ev}_{X,n}):

\displaystyle\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{\Gamma}(X,D)\rightarrow X^{n},

\displaystyle\operatorname{ev}_{D}=(\operatorname{ev}_{D,1},\ldots,\operatorname{ev}_{D,\rho}):

\displaystyle\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{\Gamma}(X,D)\rightarrow D^{\rho}.

The insertions of relative invariants are the following classes.

{\underline{\alpha}}\in(\mathbb{C}[\bar{\psi}]\otimes H^{*}(X))^{n},\quad{\underline{\varepsilon}}\in(\mathbb{C}[\bar{\psi}]\otimes H^{*}(D))^{\rho}.

For simplicity, we assume

{\underline{\alpha}}=(\bar{\psi}^{a_{1}}\alpha_{1},\ldots,\bar{\psi}^{a_{n}}\alpha_{n}),\quad{\underline{\varepsilon}}=(\bar{\psi}^{b_{1}}\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\bar{\psi}^{b_{\rho}}\varepsilon_{\rho}).

The
relative Gromov–Witten invariant with topological type
\Gamma
is defined to be

\langle{\underline{\varepsilon}}\mid{\underline{\alpha}}\rangle_{\Gamma}^{(X,D)}=\displaystyle\int_{[\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{\Gamma}(X,D)]^{\operatorname{vir}}}\operatorname{ev}_{D}^{*}{\underline{\varepsilon}}\cup\operatorname{ev}_{X}^{*}{\underline{\alpha}},

where

(2)

\operatorname{ev}_{D}^{*}{\underline{\varepsilon}}=\prod\limits_{j=1}^{\rho}\bar{\psi}^{b_{j}}_{D,j}\operatorname{ev}_{D,j}^{*}\varepsilon_{j},\quad\operatorname{ev}_{X}^{*}{\underline{\alpha}}=\prod\limits_{i=1}^{n}\bar{\psi}^{a_{i}}_{X,i}\operatorname{ev}_{X,i}^{*}\alpha_{i},

with
\bar{\psi}_{D,j},\bar{\psi}_{X,i}
the psi-classes of the corresponding markings.

We also allow disconnected domains. Let
\Gamma=\{\Gamma^{\mathcal{\pi}}\}
be a set of topological types. The relative invariant with disconnected domain curves is defined by the product rule:

\langle{\underline{\varepsilon}}\mid{\underline{\alpha}}\rangle_{\Gamma}^{\bullet(X,D)}=\prod\limits_{\mathcal{\pi}}\langle{\underline{\varepsilon}}^{\mathcal{\pi}}\mid{\underline{\alpha}}^{\mathcal{\pi}}\rangle_{\Gamma^{\mathcal{\pi}}}^{(X,D)}.

Here
\bullet
means possibly disconnected domains. We will call this
\Gamma
a
disconnected topological type
. We now recall the definition of an
admissible graph
.

Definition 2.1

(Definition 4.6,
[
15
]
)
.

An admissible graph

\Gamma
is a graph without edges plus the following data.

(a)

An ordered collection of legs.

(b)

An ordered collection of weighted roots.

(c)

A function
\mathrm{g}:V(\Gamma)\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
.

(d)

A function
b:V(\Gamma)\rightarrow H_{2}(X,\mathbb{Z})
.

Here,
V(\Gamma)
means the set of vertices of
\Gamma
. Legs and roots are regarded as half-edges of the graph
\Gamma
.
A relative stable morphism is associated to an admissible graph in the following way. Vertices in
V(\Gamma)
correspond to the connected components of the domain curve. Roots and legs correspond to relative markings and interior markings, respectively. Weights on roots correspond to contact orders at the corresponding relative markings.

The functions
\mathrm{g},b
assign a component to its genus and degree, respectively. We do not spell out the formal definitions in order to avoid heavy notation, but we refer the readers to
[
15
]
*Definition 4.7.

Remark 2.2
.

A (disconnected) topological type and an admissible graph are equivalent concepts. Different terminologies emphasize different aspects. For example, admissible graphs will be glued at half-edges into actual graphs.

2.2.
Rubber theory

Relative Gromov–Witten theory is closely related with the so-called rubber theory. Given a smooth projective variety
D
and a line bundle
L
on
D
, we denote the
moduli of relative stable maps to rubber targets
by
\overline{\mathcal{M}}^{\bullet\sim}_{\Gamma^{\prime}}(D)
. Here
\bullet
means possibly disconnected domains, and
\sim
means rubber targets. The discrete data
\Gamma^{\prime}
describing the topology of relative stable maps is defined as a slight variation of an admissible graph.

In the rest of the paper, it is very often that a pair
(X,D)
is given in the context. In this case, we always assume that

L=N_{D/X}.

Definition 2.3
.

A rubber (admissible) graph
\Gamma^{\prime}
is an admissible graph whose roots have two different types. There are

(a)

0
-roots (whose weights will be denoted by
\mu^{0}_{1},\ldots,\mu^{0}_{\rho_{0}}
), and

(b)

\infty
-roots (whose weights will be denoted by
\mu^{\infty}_{1},\ldots,\mu^{\infty}_{\rho_{\infty}}
).

Furthermore, the curve class assignment
b
maps
V(\Gamma)
to
H_{2}(D,\mathbb{Z})
.

As to the moduli space of relative stable maps to a rubber (non-rigid) target, a description can be found in, for example,
[
10
]
*Section 2.4. Briefly speaking, a relative stable map to a rubber target of
D
is a relative pre-stable map to a chain of
\mathbb{P}_{D}(L\oplus\mathcal{O})
glued along certain invariant sections. We denote the invariant divisors at two ends of the chain by
D_{0},D_{\infty}
. We make the convention that the normal bundles of
D_{0}
and
D_{\infty}
are
L
and
L^{\vee}
, respectively.

We also have evaluation maps

\operatorname{ev}_{D}:\overline{\mathcal{M}}^{\bullet\sim}_{\Gamma^{\prime}}(D)\rightarrow D^{n},\quad\operatorname{ev}_{D_{0}}:\overline{\mathcal{M}}^{\bullet\sim}_{\Gamma^{\prime}}(D)\rightarrow D^{\rho_{0}},\quad\operatorname{ev}_{D_{\infty}}:\overline{\mathcal{M}}^{\bullet\sim}_{\Gamma^{\prime}}(D)\rightarrow D^{\rho_{\infty}}.

To get a non-empty moduli space, we need the following condition.

(3)

\sum\limits_{i=1}^{\rho_{0}}\mu^{0}_{i}-\sum\limits_{j=1}^{\rho_{\infty}}\mu^{\infty}_{j}=\int_{\beta}c_{1}(L),

where
\beta
is the curve class of
\Gamma^{\prime}
. If
\Gamma^{\prime}
has more than one vertex, the above is satisfied on each of the vertex (with
\mu^{0}_{i},\mu^{\infty}_{j}
corresponding to weights of roots on a given vertex).

A relative stable map to a rubber target is associated to a rubber graph in the standard way, with relative markings at
D_{0}
and
D_{\infty}
corresponding to the
0
-roots and
\infty
-roots, respectively.

2.3.
Orbifold theory of root stacks and gerbes

We consider the
r
th root stack
X_{D,r}
of
X
along the divisor
D
. We write the coarse moduli space of the inertia stack of
X_{D,r}
as
\underline{I}(X_{D,r})
. It has
r
components:

\underline{I}(X_{D,r})\cong X\sqcup D\sqcup D\cdots\sqcup D.

The twisted sectors which are isomorphic to
D
are labeled by the ages
k_{i}/r
, where
k_{i}\in\{1,2,\ldots,r-1\}
.

Since our task is to compare orbifold theory with relative theory, we would like to match some of their notation. Let
\Gamma=(g,n,\beta,\rho,\vec{\mu})
be a topological type with
\vec{\mu}=(\mu_{1},\dotsc,\mu_{\rho})\in(\mathbb{Z}^{*})^{\rho}
being a partition of the number
\int_{\beta}D
. A topological type
\Gamma
can also be used to specify topological types of orbifold stable maps to
X_{D,r}
under the following convention.

Convention 1
.

A topological type
\Gamma=(g,n,\beta,\rho,\vec{\mu})
of orbifold stable maps contains the following data:

•

g,\beta
corresponds to the genus and curve class;

•

n
indicates a set of
n
markings without orbifold structure;

•

\rho
indicates a set of
\rho
markings with orbifold structure;

•

\vec{\mu}=(\mu_{1},\dotsc,\mu_{\rho})\in(\mathbb{Z}^{*})^{\rho}
and
\sum_{i=1}^{\rho}\mu_{i}=\int_{d}D
;

•

When
\mu_{i}>0
, the evaluation map of the corresponding marking lands on the twisted sector with age
\mu_{i}/r
;

•

when
\mu_{i}<0
, the evaluation map of the corresponding marking lands on the twisted sector with age
(r+\mu_{i})/r
.

Here, we require that
r>\max_{1\leq i\leq\rho}|\mu_{i}|
.

A priori, evaluation maps should land on
\underline{I}(X_{D,r})
. Since ages are fixed by
\vec{\mu}
, we further restrict their targets to the corresponding components. We have the restricted evaluation maps

\operatorname{ev}_{X}:\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{\Gamma}(X_{D,r})\rightarrow X^{n},\quad\operatorname{ev}_{D}:\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{\Gamma}(X_{D,r})\rightarrow D^{\rho}

corresponding to those
n
markings without orbifold structures, and those
\rho
markings with orbifold structures, respectively. We similarly denote their entries by

\operatorname{ev}_{X}=(\operatorname{ev}_{X,1},\ldots,\operatorname{ev}_{X,n}),\quad\operatorname{ev}_{D}=(\operatorname{ev}_{D,1},\ldots,\operatorname{ev}_{D,\rho}).

Consider the forgetful map

\tau:\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{\Gamma}(X_{D,r})\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n+\rho}(X,\beta)\times_{X^{\rho}}D^{\rho},

we write
\bar{\psi_{i}}=\tau^{*}\psi_{i}
.
Using the notation in the Definition
3.12
, the
orbifold Gromov–Witten invariant with topological type
\Gamma
is

(4)

\langle{\underline{\varepsilon}},{\underline{\alpha}}\rangle_{\Gamma}^{X_{D,r}}=\displaystyle\int_{[\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{\Gamma}(X_{D,r})]^{\mathrm{vir}}}\prod\limits_{j=1}^{\rho}\bar{\psi}_{D,j}^{b_{j}}\operatorname{ev}_{D,j}^{*}\varepsilon_{j}\prod\limits_{i=1}^{n}\bar{\psi}_{X,i}^{a_{i}}\operatorname{ev}_{X,i}^{*}\alpha_{i},

where
\bar{\psi}_{D,j},\bar{\psi}_{X,i}
are psi-classes corresponding to markings evaluated under
\operatorname{ev}_{D},\operatorname{ev}_{X}
.

For orbifold Gromov–Witten invariants (
4
) with topological type
\Gamma
, we define an integer
\rho_{-}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
to be

(5)

\displaystyle\rho_{-}=\sum_{\mu_{i}>0}\mu_{i}/r+\sum_{\mu_{i}<0}(r+\mu_{i})/r-\left(\int_{\beta}D\right)/r.

When
r
is sufficiently large, virtual dimension being an integer implies that
\rho_{-}
equals the number of negative parts in
\vec{\mu}
.

3.
Relative theory with negative contact orders in all genera

3.1.
Relative theory as a limit of orbifold theory

Following
[
9
]
, we define relative Gromov–Witten theory as a limit of orbifold Gromov–Witten theory. However, contrary to the genus-zero invariants, higher genus orbifold invariants of the root stack
X_{D,r}
depend on
r
. According to
[
20
]
, orbifold invariants of root stacks are polynomials in
r
and the constant terms are the corresponding relative invariants when
r
is sufficiently large. Combining the ideas in
[
20
]
and
[
9
]
, we give our first definition of relative Gromov–Witten invariants (possibly with negative contact orders) as the lowest term of orbifold invariants for a sufficiently large
r
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Fix a topological type
\Gamma=(g,n,\beta,\rho,\vec{\mu})
. For a sufficiently large
r
, the following cycle class

r^{\rho_{-}}\tau_{*}\left([\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{\Gamma}(X_{D,r})]^{\operatorname{vir}}\right)\in A_{*}(\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n+\rho}(X,\beta)\times_{X^{\rho}}D^{\rho})

is a polynomial in
r
, where
\tau
is the forgetful map

\tau:\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{\Gamma}(X_{D,r})\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n+\rho}(X,\beta)\times_{X^{\rho}}D^{\rho}.

Theorem
3.1
will be proven in Section
3.4
. For a sufficiently large
r
, denote the constant term of the above cycle by

\mathop{\text{lim}}_{r\rightarrow\infty}\left[r^{\rho_{-}}\tau_{*}([\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{\Gamma}(X_{D,r})]^{\mathrm{vir}})\right]_{r^{0}}.

Definition 3.2
.

Fix a topological type
\Gamma=(g,n,\beta,\rho,\vec{\mu})
. The
relative Gromov–Witten cycle
of the topological type
\Gamma
is defined as

\mathfrak{c}_{\Gamma}(X/D)=\mathop{\text{lim}}_{r\rightarrow\infty}\left[r^{\rho_{-}}\tau_{*}([\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{\Gamma}(X_{D,r})]^{\mathrm{vir}})\right]_{r^{0}}\in A_{*}(\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n+\rho}(X,\beta)\times_{X^{\rho}}D^{\rho}).

When
\rho_{-}=0
,
\mathfrak{c}_{\Gamma}(X/D)
coincides with the pushforward of virtual cycle from the corresponding moduli of relative stable maps.

Recall that
\rho
is used to denote the number of roots in the admissible graph
\Gamma
. Let
\rho_{+}
be the number of roots whose weights are positive. We have

Proposition 3.3
.

\mathfrak{c}_{\Gamma}(X/D)\in A_{d}(\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n+\rho}(X,\beta)\times_{X^{\rho}}D^{\rho}),

where

d=(1-g)(\mathrm{dim}_{\mathbb{C}}(X)-3)+\int_{\beta}c_{1}(T_{X}(-\mathrm{log}D))+n+\rho_{+}.

We define relative Gromov–Witten invariants (possibly with negative contact orders) by integrations against this cycle.
The insertions are

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\underline{\alpha}}=(\bar{\psi}^{a_{1}}\alpha_{1},\ldots,\bar{\psi}^{a_{n}}\alpha_{n})&\in(\mathbb{C}[\bar{\psi}]\otimes H^{*}(X))^{n},\\
{\underline{\varepsilon}}=(\bar{\psi}^{b_{1}}\epsilon_{1},\ldots,\bar{\psi}^{b_{\rho}}\epsilon_{\rho})&\in(\mathbb{C}[\bar{\psi}]\otimes H^{*}(D))^{\rho}.\end{split}

There are two types of evaluation maps from
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{\Gamma}(X_{D,r})
corresponding to interior markings and relative markings respectively:

\displaystyle\operatorname{ev}_{X}=(\operatorname{ev}_{X,1},\ldots,\operatorname{ev}_{X,n}):\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{\Gamma}(X_{D,r})

\displaystyle\rightarrow X^{n},

\displaystyle\operatorname{ev}_{D}=(\operatorname{ev}_{D,1},\ldots,\operatorname{ev}_{D,\rho}):\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{\Gamma}(X_{D,r})

\displaystyle\rightarrow D^{\rho}.

Recall that we have the stabilization map
\tau:\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{\Gamma}(X_{D,r})\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n+\rho}(X,\beta)\times_{X^{\rho}}D^{\rho}
. There are also evaluation maps

\displaystyle\overline{\operatorname{ev}}_{X}=(\overline{\operatorname{ev}}_{X,1},\ldots,\overline{\operatorname{ev}}_{X,n}):\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n+\rho}(X,\beta)\times_{X^{\rho}}D^{\rho}

\displaystyle\rightarrow X^{n},

\displaystyle\overline{\operatorname{ev}}_{D}=(\overline{\operatorname{ev}}_{D,1},\ldots,\overline{\operatorname{ev}}_{D,\rho}):\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n+\rho}(X,\beta)\times_{X^{\rho}}D^{\rho}

\displaystyle\rightarrow X^{\rho},

such that

\overline{\operatorname{ev}}_{X}\circ\tau=\operatorname{ev}_{X},\quad\overline{\operatorname{ev}}_{D}\circ\tau=\operatorname{ev}_{D}.

Definition 3.4
.

The
relative Gromov–Witten invariant of topological type
\Gamma
with insertions
{\underline{\varepsilon}},{\underline{\alpha}}
is

\langle{\underline{\varepsilon}}\mid{\underline{\alpha}}\rangle_{\Gamma}^{(X,D)}=\displaystyle\int_{\mathfrak{c}_{\Gamma}(X/D)}\prod\limits_{j=1}^{\rho}\bar{\psi}_{D,j}^{b_{j}}(\overline{\operatorname{ev}}_{D,j})^{*}\epsilon_{j}\prod\limits_{i=1}^{n}\bar{\psi}_{X,i}^{a_{i}}\overline{\operatorname{ev}}_{X,i}^{*}\alpha_{i},

where
\bar{\psi}_{D,j},\bar{\psi}_{X,i}
are pull-backs of psi-classes on
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n+\rho}(X,\beta)
corresponding to relative and interior markings.

As a direct consequence, we have the following relation between relative and orbifold invariants.

Theorem 3.5
.

Fix a topological type
\Gamma=(g,n,\beta,\rho,\vec{\mu})
. For
r\gg 1
, the following equality holds:

(6)

\left[r^{\rho_{-}}\langle{\underline{\varepsilon}},{\underline{\alpha}}\rangle_{\Gamma}^{Y_{D,r}}\right]_{r^{0}}=\langle{\underline{\varepsilon}}\mid{\underline{\alpha}}\rangle_{\Gamma}^{(Y,D)},

where the relative invariant on the right hand side of the above equation is the relative invariant with negative contact order defined in Definition
3.4
when
\rho_{-}>0
.

3.2.
Graph notations

Following
[
9
]
, the second definition of the relative Gromov–Witten cycle is given by a graph sum. In this section, we review the graph notation. In
[
9
]
*Section 4, we defined certain bipartite graphs and built moduli spaces based on those graphs. In higher genera, we in fact use the same notion of bipartite graphs except that graphs may now have loops. Readers familiar with
[
9
]
*Section 4 should keep in mind that we no longer have the genus constraint on admissible bipartite graphs and could skip to later sections. But to make a relatively self-contained treatment, here we try to make a concise (and hopefully more understandable) summary of graph notation in
[
9
]
*Section 4.

3.2.1.
Two sides of bipartite graph

The main type of bipartite graphs is called
admissible bipartite graph
. A bipartite graph divides vertices into two sides, with edges connecting back and forth from one side to the other (but not between vertices on the same side). In this paper, we label the two sides differently as
0
-side
and
\infty
-side
.

3.2.2.
Vertices on
0
-side

Vertices on the two sides have different decorations. We treat a single vertex on
0
-side along with its decorations as a graph by itself (denoted by
\Gamma^{0}_{i}
with
i
in a label set). It is called a
graph of type
0

3.5.
Relative theory forms a partial CohFT

As a consequence of Theorem
3.13
, we will show that the all genera relative theory (possibly with negative contact) forms a partial cohomological field theory (CohFT) in the sense of
[
17
]
*Definition 2.7.

We begin with a brief review of the axioms of CohFT. Let
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,m}
be the moduli space of of genus
g
,
m
-pointed stable curves. We assume that
2g-2+m>0
. There are several canonical morphisms between the
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,m}
.

•

Forgetful morphism

\pi:\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,m+1}\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,m}

obtained by forgetting the last marking.

•

Gluing the loop

\rho_{l}:\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,m+2}\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g+1,m}

obtained by identifying the last two markings of the
(m+2)
-pointed, genus
g
curves.

•

Gluing the tree

\rho_{t}:\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g_{1},m_{1}+1}\times\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g_{2},m_{2}+1}\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g_{1}+g_{2},m_{1}+m_{2}}

obtained by identifying the last markings of separate pointed curves.

Let
H
be a graded vector space with a non-degenerate pairing
\langle,\rangle
and a distinguished element
1\in H
. Given a basis
\{e_{i}\}
, let
\eta_{jk}=\langle e_{j},e_{k}\rangle
and
(\eta^{jk})=(\eta_{jk})^{-1}
.

A CohFT is a collection of homomorphisms

\Omega_{g,m}:H^{\otimes m}\rightarrow H^{*}(\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,m},\mathbb{Q})

satisfying the following axioms:

•

The element
\Omega_{g,m}
is invariant under the natural action of symmetric group
S_{m}
.

•

For all
a_{i}\in H
,
\Omega_{g,m}
satisfies

\Omega_{g,m+1}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{m},1)=\pi^{*}\Omega_{g,m}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{m}).

•

The splitting axiom:

\displaystyle\rho^{*}_{t}\Omega_{g_{1}+g_{2},m_{1}+m_{2}}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{m_{1}+m_{2}})=

\displaystyle\sum_{j,k}\eta^{jk}\Omega_{g_{1},m_{1}}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{m_{1}},e_{j})\otimes\Omega_{g_{2},m_{2}}(a_{m_{1}+1},\ldots,a_{m_{1}+m_{2}},e_{k}),

for all
a_{i}\in H
.

•

The loop axiom:

\rho_{l}^{*}\Omega_{g+1,m}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{m})=\sum_{j,k}\eta^{jk}\Omega_{g,m+2}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{m},e_{j},e_{k}),

for all
a_{i}\in H
. In addition, the equality

\Omega_{0,3}(v_{1},v_{2},1)=\langle v_{1},v_{2}\rangle

holds for all
v_{1},v_{2}\in H
.

Definition 3.14

(
[
17
]
, Definition 2.7)
.

If the collection
\{\Omega_{g,m}\}
satisfies all the axioms except for the loop axiom, we call it a partial CohFT.

Following
[
9
]
, the ring of insertions for relative Gromov–Witten theory is defined to be

Proof.

\displaystyle\rho^{*}_{t}\Omega^{(X,D)}_{g_{1}+g_{2},m_{1}+m_{2}}([\alpha_{1}],\ldots,[\alpha_{m_{1}+m_{2}}])

\displaystyle\rho^{*}_{t}\left[r^{\rho_{-}}\Omega^{X_{D,r}}_{g_{1}+g_{2},m_{1}+m_{2}}(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{m_{1}+m_{2}})\right]_{r^{0}}

Proof of Lemma

•

•

Suppose that there is a stable vertex

If

As one can check, stable vertices in the relative theory of

4.3.
Equivariant theory as a limit of non-equivariant theory

In this subsection, we briefly explain how Lemma
4.7
implies Lemma
4.6
.

According to
[
8
]
*Section 2.2, the

In our case,

\displaystyle\overline{\mathcal{M}}^{\mathrm{rel}}((P\times E)/\mathbb{C}^{*},(D_{0}\times E)/\mathbb{C}^{*})

\displaystyle\cong(\overline{\mathcal{M}}^{\mathrm{rel}}(P,D_{0})\times E)/\mathbb{C}^{*}.

\displaystyle\overline{\mathcal{M}}^{\text{orb}}((P_{D_{0},r}\times E)/\mathbb{C}^{*})

4.4.
Identifying localization residues

Lemma
4.6
implies Theorem

by comparing localization residues.

Consider the localization residue of
\overline{\mathcal{M}}^{\mathrm{rel}}(P,D_{0})
corresponding to a vertex of class
\beta
over

with
\rho_{\infty}
edges going with degrees
\nu_{1},\ldots,\nu_{\rho_{\infty}}
