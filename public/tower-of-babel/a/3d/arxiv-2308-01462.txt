Dynamical Sampling for the Recovery of Spatially Constant Source Terms in Dynamical Systems Thanks: Akram Aldroubi is supported in part by NSF Grant NSF/DMS-2208030.
A. Aldroubi, R. Diaz Martin, I, Medri

Abstract
. In this paper, we investigate the problem of source recovery in a dynamical system utilizing space-time samples. This is a specific issue within the broader field of dynamical sampling, which involves collecting samples from solutions to a differential equation across both space and time with the aim of recovering critical data, such as initial values, the sources, the driving operator, or other relevant details. Our focus in this study is the recovery of unknown, stationary sources across both space and time, leveraging space-time samples. This research may have significant applications; for instance, it could provide a model for strategically placing devices to measure the quantity of pollutants emanating from factory smokestacks and dispersing across a specific area. Space-time samples could be collected using measuring devices placed at various spatial locations and activated at different times. We present necessary and sufficient conditions for the positioning of these measuring devices to successfully resolve this dynamical sampling problem. This paper provides both a theoretical foundation for the recovery of sources in dynamical systems and potential practical applications.

Introduction

In various applications, such as environmental monitoring, it is crucial to accurately determine the location of pollution sources and assess the magnitude of their emissions. Typically, this information is obtained by deploying sensors across different locations.
Consequently, these scenarios can be framed as sampling and reconstruction problems.
For instance, in the case of atmospheric emissions, considered in
[
34
]
and the references therein, the goal is to recover the pollution intensity emitted by
K
smokestacks by using
L
sensors.

In the present work, we examine the case where source emissions are constant in time, and atmospheric pollution evolves through a linear discrete-time dynamic. To illustrate, let us consider the following dynamical system:

(1)

x({n+1})=A^{*}x(n)+\underbrace{\sum\limits_{i=1}^{K}c_{i}\,e_{j_{i}}}_{\text{source term}},\qquad x(0)=x_{0}.

Here,
x(n)
represents the state of contamination at discrete time steps indexed by
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}:=\mathbb{N}\cup{0}
. We assume that each state
x(n)
is a vector in a finite-dimensional vector space, such as
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The system initializes at an unknown vector
x_{0}
, and then the dynamic is governed by the linear operator
A^{*}
and by the source term. For each
i=1,\dots,K
, the canonical vector
e_{j_{i}}
represents the location of the
i
-th factory in an industrial zone, which releases plumes at a constant rate
c_{i}
.

Regardless of the initial state
x_{0}
, the main objective is to estimate the scalar values
c_{1},\dots,c_{K}
using space-time samples of the form:

(2)

\langle x(n),e_{k_{\ell}}\rangle

These samples are collected at specific locations
k_{\ell}
,
\ell=1,\dots,L
, where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the canonical inner product in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The new set of
L
canonical vectors

can be viewed as the sensors in this context. (See Fig.
1

The problem above is one of many problems that fall under the umbrella of dynamical sampling problems.
For example, when all

Let us return to our sampling and source reconstruction problem in order to state it in a more general manner. Instead of using the measurements (
2
) we can consider more general samples in the direction of vectors
b_{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, that is,

(3)

y_{\ell}(n):=\langle x(n),b_{\ell}\rangle,\qquad\text{ for }\ell=1,\dots,L.

Moreover, it is worth noting that we can treat the source term (
1
) as a vector in the subspace

W:=span\{e_{j_{1}},\dots,e_{j_{K}}\}.

Then, by expressing any vector in
W
as a linear combination of
e_{j_{1}},\dots,e_{j_{K}}
, that is,

(4)

\omega=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{K}c_{i}\,e_{j_{i}},

we can observe that the problem of recovering
c_{1},\dots,c_{K}
is equivalent to that of recovering the vector
\omega
in the subspace
W
.

Precisely, we state our general problem as follows.

Problem 1
.

Let
\mathbb{F}
be either
\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{C}
,
A:\mathbb{F}^{d}\to\mathbb{F}^{d}
be a linear operator with adjoint
A^{*}
, and
W
be a subspace of
\mathbb{F}^{d}
. Given vectors
b_{1},\dots,b_{L}\in\mathbb{F}^{d}
, consider the discrete-time dynamic

(5)

\begin{cases}x({n+1})=A^{*}x(n)+\omega,\qquad\text{subject to }\omega\in W\\
y_{\ell}(n)=\langle x(n),b_{\ell}\rangle,\qquad\text{ for }\ell\in\{1,\dots,L\},\end{cases}

where the initial state
x(0)=x_{0}
is unknown.
The source recovery problem consists of providing necessary and sufficient conditions on such vectors
b_{1},\dots,b_{L}
so that the constant source
\omega
can be recovered from a finite number of linear operations using a finite number of measurements
y_{\ell}(n)
.

For convenience and brevity, we adopt the following definitions.

Definition 1.1
.

(1)

The set of vectors
\{b_{1},\dots,b_{L}\}
used in Problem
1
will be called
spatial observational vectors.

(2)

If the set of spatial observational vectors
\{b_{1},\dots,b_{L}\}
allows the reconstruction of any
\omega\in W
, we say that the spatial observational vectors are
complete
for this source recovery problem.

The spatial observational vectors represent physical measuring devices used to acquire space-time samples. Specifically, if
b_{j}=e_{l_{j}}
, where
e_{l_{j}}
is a standard basis element at spatial position
l_{j}
for
\mathbb{F}^{d}
, then the set
\{b_{1},\dots,b_{L}\}
corresponds to measuring devices positioned at
l_{j}
,
1\leq j\leq l
. These devices can be activated at various time instances
n
to collect space-time samples.

We will strive to find answers to the following questions:

•

If the subspace
W
and the sampling vectors
b_{1},\dots,b_{L}
are given in advance, is it possible to recover any
\omega\in W
?

•

Given the subspace
W
, is there a minimal set
b_{1},\dots,b_{L}
such that reconstructing any
\omega\in W
is possible?

•

In case that the source reconstruction is possible, how many time iterations and spatial measurements are needed?

2.
main results

In this section, we aim to establish necessary and/or sufficient conditions for a set of spatial observational vectors

\{b_{1},\dots,b_{L}\}
for the dynamic (
5
) to be complete, as stated in Theorems
2.4
,
2.5
and their corollaries. Before we proceed, we begin by rewriting the states and measurements of the dynamic in Problem
1
.

Denoting the initial state by
x(0)=x_{0}
, the evolution of the states (
x(n)=A^{*}x(n-1)+\omega
in
1
) has the form

(6)

x(n)=(A^{*})^{n}x_{0}+\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}(A^{*})^{j}\omega,\qquad\forall n\in\mathbb{N}.

Using (
6
), the time-space measurements in (
5
) can be written in the following form:

(7)

\displaystyle y_{\ell}(n)

\displaystyle=\langle x_{0},A^{n}b_{\ell}\rangle+\langle\omega,\Lambda_{n}b_{\ell}\rangle

\displaystyle=\left[{\begin{array}[]{rrr}A^{n}b_{\ell}\\
\Lambda_{n}b_{\ell}\end{array}}\right]^{*}\left[{\begin{array}[]{rrr}x_{0}\\
\omega\end{array}}\right]\qquad

for
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
\ell\in{1,\dots,L}
, where

(12)

\Lambda_{n}:=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}A^{j}\qquad\text{ for }n\in\mathbb{N},\qquad\Lambda_{0}:={\bf 0}\text{ (the zero-matrix).}

The last equality in (
7
), is written in matrix form, where a
d\times 2d
matrix is multiplied by a vector in
\mathbb{F}^{2d}
with the first
d
components representing
x_{0}
and the last
d
components representing
\omega
.

Equation (
7
) leads us to introduce two fundamental spaces associated with
\{b_{1},\dots,b_{L}\}
.

The first fundamental space is the
observational orbits space

Z(A;b_{1},\dots,b_{L})
defined by

(13)

Z(A;b_{1},\dots,b_{L}):=span\{A^{n}b_{\ell}:\,n\in\mathbb{N}_{0},\,\,\ell\in\{1,\dots,L\}\}.

The second space we introduce is referred to as the
augmented observational orbits space
defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(b_{1},\dots,b_{\ell}):=span

\displaystyle\left\{\left[{\begin{array}[]{rrr}A^{n}b_{\ell}\\
\Lambda_{n}b_{\ell}\end{array}}\right]\in\mathbb{F}^{2d}:\,n\in\mathbb{N}_{0},\,\ell=1,\dots,L\right\},

where
\Lambda_{n}
is as in (
12
).

The space
\mathcal{O}(b_{1},\dots,b_{L})
is a subspace of
\mathbb{F}^{2d}
. Its dimension plays a crucial role in determining necessary and sufficient conditions in Theorem
2.4
for the completeness of the spatial observational vectors
{b_{1},\dots,b_{L}}
(see Definition
1.2
). For the case of
L=1
, it can be shown that
\dim\mathcal{O}(b_{1})\leq d+1
, and that the dimension

can be achieved by an appropriately chosen vectors

. Intuitively, one might expect that for any
L\geq 2
, the dimension of
\mathcal{O}(b_{1},\dots,b_{L})
could be as large as

. However, the following theorem states that
\dim\mathcal{O}(b_{1},\dots,b_{L})\leq\min\{d+L,2d\}
.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
A:\mathbb{F}^{d}\to\mathbb{F}^{d}
and vectors
b_{1},\dots,b_{L}\in\mathbb{F}^{d}\setminus\{{\bf 0}\}
.
Then, the subspace
\mathcal{O}(b_{1},\dots,b_{L})\subset\mathbb{F}^{2d}
of space-time sampling vectors defined in (
2
)
coincides with the set of vectors

\displaystyle\left\{\left[{\begin{array}[]{r}(A-I)g+\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\mu_{\ell}\,b_{\ell}\\
g\end{array}}\right]\in\mathbb{F}^{2d}:\,g\in Z(A;b_{1},\dots,b_{L}),\,\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{L}\in\mathbb{F}\right\}.

In particular,
\dim\mathcal{O}(b_{1},\dots,b_{L})\leq\min\{d+L,2d\}
.

Remark 2.2
.

When there is a single observational vector
b_{1}
(i.e.,
L=1
), the space of orbits
Z(A;b_{1})
is referred to as the Krylov subspace of the highest order generated by
A
and

. It is well-known that the dimension of

is equal to the degree

of the minimal

-annihilating polynomial

of

. In fact, if

denotes the degree of
m_{b_{1}}
, then
{b_{1},Ab_{1},\dots,A^{r-1}b_{1}}
forms a basis for

.

Corollary 2.3
.

Let
d\geq 2
and
A:\mathbb{F}^{d}\to\mathbb{F}^{d}
be a linear operator. Consider a non-zero vector
b\in\mathbb{F}^{d}
. Then, the dimension of the space
\mathcal{O}(b)
is equal to

, where

is the degree of the minimal

-annihilating polynomial of

.

2.1.
Necessary and Sufficient conditions for completeness of observational vectors

Our first result provides the necessary and sufficient conditions for the set
{b_{1},\dots,b_{L}}
to be a complete observational set for Problem
1
. However, its primary utility lies in deriving additional more useful necessary and/or sufficient conditions.

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
A:\mathbb{F}^{d}\to\mathbb{F}^{d}
be a linear operator, and let
W
be a subspace of
\mathbb{F}^{d}
with
P_{W}:\mathbb{F}^{d}\to\mathbb{F}^{d}
the orthogonal projection onto
W
. A set of spatial observational vectors
\{b_{1},\dots,b_{L}\}\subset\mathbb{F}^{d}
is complete for Problem
1
(see Definition
1.1
) if and only if there exist vectors
g_{1},\dots,g_{N}\in Z(A;b_{1},\dots,b_{L})
, for some
N\in\mathbb{N}
, such that

(1)

there exists a solution
M\in\mathbb{F}^{L\times N}
for the matrix problem

(18)

BM=(A-I)G

where
B
is the
d\times L
matrix having
b_{1},\dots,b_{L}
as columns, and
G
is the
d\times N
matrix with columns
g_{1},\dots,g_{N}
, and

(2)

\{P_{W}(g_{1}),\dots,P_{W}(g_{N})\}
spans
W
.

2.1.1.
Completeness of observational vectors when
1\notin\sigma(A)
.

We now use Theorem
2.4
to derive simple, necessary and sufficient conditions, enabling us to directly determine whether a set of observational vectors is complete.

Theorem 2.5
.

Let
A:\mathbb{F}^{d}\to\mathbb{F}^{d}
be a linear operator with spectrum
\sigma(A)
, and let
W
be a
K
-dimensional subspace of
\mathbb{F}^{d}
. Furthermore, assume that
1\notin\sigma(A)
, then, the set of spatial observational vectors
\{b_{1},\dots,b_{L}\}\subset\mathbb{F}^{d}\setminus\{{\bf 0}\}
is complete for Problem
1
if and only if

(19)

rank\left(P_{W}(A-I)^{-1}B\right)=K

where
P_{W}
denotes the orthogonal projection onto
W
, and
B
is the matrix having
b_{1},\dots,b_{L}
as column vectors.

As a corollary, we conclude that the set of observational vectors must contain at least
K
vectors when
\dim W=K
.

Corollary 2.6
.

Let
A:\mathbb{F}^{d}\to\mathbb{F}^{d}
be a linear operator with
1\notin\sigma(A)
, and let
W
be a
K
-dimensional subspace of
\mathbb{F}^{d}
. If the set
\{b_{1},\dots,b_{L}\}
forms a complete observational set for Problem
1
, then
L\geq K
.

It turns out that, when
1\notin\sigma(A)
, a complete set of observational vectors
\{b_{1},\dots,b_{K}\}
can always be found, where
K=\dim W
.

Corollary 2.7
.

Let
A:\mathbb{F}^{d}\to\mathbb{F}^{d}
be a linear operator, and let
W
be a subspace of
\mathbb{F}^{d}
. If
\dim W=K
and
1\notin\sigma(A)
, then there exists a complete set of spatial observational vectors
\{b_{1},\dots,b_{K}\}
. In particular, if
{\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{K}}
form a basis for
W
, a possible choice is given by
b_{k}=(I-A)\omega_{k}
for
1\leq k\leq K
.

Remark 2.8
.

(1)

The significance of Condition (
19
) in Theorem
2.5
is that it provides a practical method to verify whether a given set of observational vectors is complete.

(2)

When the source aligns with (
4
), the
P_{W}
in (
19
) simplifies to a diagonal matrix with diagonal entries from the set
\{0,1\}
.

(3)

The selection of the spatial observational vector in Corollary
2.7
represents merely one potential choice for a complete set of observational vectors. However, when each vector
b_{k}
is required to be a standard basis element of
\mathbb{F}^{d}
, we must choose a set of vectors
{e_{l_{1}},\dots,e_{l_{K}}}
and validate their completeness using Theorem
2.5
.

Theorem
2.5
above provides a
simple test for determining whether a set of spatial observational vectors
\{b_{1},\dots,b_{L}\}\subset\mathbb{F}^{d}\setminus\{{\bf 0}\}
is complete for Problem
1
under the assumption that
1\notin\sigma(A)
. Later, in Theorem
2.11
we will provide a general test without the assumption that
1\not\in\sigma(A)
. Allowing

to be an eigenvalue of

is more difficult to analyze. In particular, if

, then Corollary

is false as the following example shows.

Example 1
.

In dimension
2
, consider the dynamical system

(20)

x({n+1})=A^{*}x(n)+c_{1}\,\omega_{1}

where

A:=\left[{\begin{array}[]{rr}1&0\\
1&1\end{array}}\right]\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\omega_{1}:=\left[{\begin{array}[]{r}1\\
0\end{array}}\right].

The goal is to recover the unknown source intensity
c_{1}\in\mathbb{F}
.

The eigenspace of
A
corresponding to eigenvalue
1
is spanned by the vector

\left[{\begin{array}[]{r}0\\
1\end{array}}\right]

and is orthogonal to
W=span\{\omega_{1}\}
. Suppose that there exists a vector

b=\left[{\begin{array}[]{r}b^{1}\\
b^{2}\end{array}}\right]

such that, by sampling the states
x(n)
, we can recover
c_{1}
in (
20
). By denoting the initial state as

x_{0}=\left[{\begin{array}[]{r}x_{0}^{1}\\
x_{0}^{2}\end{array}}\right],

the measurements will be of the form:

\begin{cases}y(0)=x_{0}^{1}\,b^{1}+x_{0}^{2}\,b^{2}\\
y(1)=x_{0}^{1}\,b^{1}+x_{0}^{2}\,(b^{2}+b^{1})+b^{1}\,c_{1}\\
y(2)=x_{0}^{1}\,b^{1}+x_{0}^{2}\,(b^{2}+2b^{1})+2b^{1}\,c_{1}\\
\vdots\\
y(n)=x_{0}^{1}\,b^{1}+x_{0}^{2}\,(b^{2}+nb^{1})+nb^{1}\,c_{1}\\
\vdots\end{cases}

The equation above shows that to recover
c_{1}
from the observations
y(j)
, then necessarily
b^{1}\neq 0
.
However, for any
c_{1}\in\mathbb{F}
, if

x_{0}=c_{1}\left[{\begin{array}[]{r}b^{2}/b^{1}\\
-1\end{array}}\right]

then
y(n)=0
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Therefore, for any choice of
b
, there exists
x_{0}
such that
y_{n}=0
for every
c_{1}
, and hence
c_{1}
cannot be determined. Hence, if
1\in\sigma(A)
and
\mathrm{dim}(W)=1
, having only one spatial sampling vector
b_{1}:=b
might not be enough for solving Problem
1
.

2.1.2.

In order to state the general test for determining whether or not a set of spatial observational vectors is complete for Problem

Notation 2.9

Given

\widehat{p}(x):=\frac{p(x)-p(1)}{x-1}\in\mathbb{F}[x].

Given

is not an eigenvalue for

is the trivial subspace
\{{\bf 0}\}
. Thus, Condition (

) is trivially satisfied as the source location

is assumed different from the zero vector, and there is always a complete set of vectors for Problem
1
by considering only one spatial sampling vector as pointed out in Remark
3.10
.

Now, assume

. In the notation of Remark
4.2
given in the Appendix, let us assume
\lambda_{1}=1
and so

is the projector (

) onto the generalized eigenspace of

.
Then, from (

),
\omega_{1}\in\mathop{Ker}(A^{*}-I)^{d}

.
Now, we separate the rest of the proof into two cases.

(1)

Assume
\omega_{1}\in\mathop{Ker}(I-E_{1}^{*})
, that is,
E_{1}^{*}\omega_{1}=\omega_{1}
. We claim that there exists a complete

for Problem
1
if and only if
\omega_{1}\not\in(\mathop{Ker}(A-I))^{\perp}
. Indeed, since
E_{1}
is a polynomial on
A
(see Remark
4.2
), we have by Lemma
3.13
that

is complete if and only if

0\not=\langle\omega_{1},\widehat{m_{b}}(A)b\rangle=\langle E_{1}^{*}\omega_{1},\widehat{m_{b}}(A)b\rangle=\langle\omega_{1},\widehat{m_{b}}(A)E_{1}b\rangle,

where we recall that
\widehat{m_{b}}(x)\in\mathbb{F}[x]
is as in (
78
).

Thus, if
b
is a complete for Problem
1
, it is necessary that

E_{1}b\not=\textbf{0}
. This implies that
m_{b}(1)=0
(see Lemma
4.3
in the Appendix). Then, by the particular case in Corollary
3.12
, we have that the vector
\widehat{m_{b}}(A)b
is an eigenvector of
A
corresponding to the eigenvalue
1
. Moreover, notice that

\mathop{Ker}(A-I)=\{\widehat{m}_{b}(A)b:\,b\in\mathbb{F}^{d}\text{ with }m_{b}(1)=0\}

(see Lemma
4.3
.)
As a result, we can find a complete

as long as
\omega_{1}
is not orthogonal to the eigenspace
\mathop{Ker}(A-I)
. We recall that, if
\omega_{1}\not\in(\mathop{Ker}(A-I))^{\perp}
, we are in the setting of Proposition
3.11
which provides a strategy for picking an accurate sampling vector
b
.

(2)

If
\omega_{1}\not\in\mathop{Ker}(I-E_{1}^{*})
, we claim that it is always possible to find a complete observational vector
b
for Problem
1
given by

(79)

b:=(A-I)(I-E_{1})(I-E_{1}^{*})\omega_{1}.

First, we will see that
b\not=\textbf{0}
. By assumption,
(I-E_{1}^{*})\omega_{1}\not=\textbf{0}
. Then, we have

(80)

0\not=\langle(I-E_{1}^{*})\omega_{1},(I-E_{1}^{*})\omega_{1}\rangle=\langle\omega_{1},(I-E_{1})(I-E_{1}^{*})\omega_{1}\rangle,

and so
(I-E_{1})(I-E_{1}^{*})\omega_{1}\not=\textbf{0}

projects onto
\bigoplus_{\lambda\in\sigma(A),\lambda\not=1}\mathop{Ker}(A-\lambda I)^{d}
and has kernel

(see Remark

\displaystyle\widehat{m_{b}}(A)b

\displaystyle=\widehat{m_{b}}(A)(A-I)(I-E_{1})(I-E_{1}^{*})\omega_{1}

\displaystyle=\big(m_{b}(A)-m_{b}(1)\big)(I-E_{1})(I-E_{1}^{*})\omega_{1}.

\displaystyle=m_{b}(A)(I-E_{1})(I-E_{1})(I-E_{1}^{*})\omega_{1}

Appendix: Matrix Analysis

Theorem 4.1
.

[
26
, Theorem 13.2]

Let
f
be an analytic function defined on a domain containing the
s
roots of the polynomial

m_{b}(x)=\prod_{i=1}^{s}(x-\lambda_{i})^{n_{i}}
which is the minimal
A
-annihilating of
b
(each root
\lambda_{i}
has multiplicity
n_{i}
). Then

f(A)b=q(A)b

for
q
the (unique)
Hermite interpolating polynomial of degree less than or equal to
\sum_{i=1}^{s}n_{i}=deg(m_{b})
that satisfies
the interpolation conditions

(82)

\frac{d^{j}}{dx^{j}}q(\lambda_{i})=\frac{d^{j}}{dx^{j}}f(\lambda_{i})\qquad\text{ for all }j=0,\dots,n_{i}-1,\,i=1,\dots,s.

In particular, if
f
is an analytic function on the spectrum of
A
and
m_{b}
is replaced by the minimal polynomial of
A
, then
f(A)=q(A)
.

Remark 4.2
.

Let
A
be a linear operator in
\mathbb{F}^{d}
, and let

m_{A}(x):=\prod_{j=1}^{s}(x-\lambda_{j})^{n_{j}}

be its minimal polynomial (i.e., the unique monic polynomial with the least degree satisfying
m_{A}(A)={\bf 0}
). (Notice that,
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{s}\in\mathbb{C}
are the distinct eigenvalues of
A
and, when writing
A
in Jordan form,
n_{i}
determines the size of the largest Jordan block in which
\lambda_{i}
appears.)
By the Lagrange-Hermite formula, the Hermite interpolating polynomial satisfying (
82
) for the case
f(x)=1
is exactly the constant polynomial
1
and it can be written as

(83)

1=\sum_{i=1}^{s}\left[\left(\sum_{k=0}^{n_{i}-1}\frac{1}{k!}\phi_{i}^{(k)}\left(\lambda_{i}\right)\left(x-\lambda_{i}\right)^{k}\right)\prod_{j\neq i}\left(x-\lambda_{j}\right)^{n_{j}}\right]

where
\phi_{i}(x):=1/\prod_{j\neq i}\left(x-\lambda_{j}\right)^{n_{j}}
(see
[
26
]
).
Let

(84)

p_{\lambda_{i}}(x):=h_{\lambda_{i}}(x)\prod_{j\neq i}\left(x-\lambda_{j}\right)^{n_{j}},\quad\text{ where }\quad h_{\lambda_{i}}(x):=\sum_{k=0}^{n_{i}-1}\frac{1}{k!}\phi_{i}^{(k)}\left(\lambda_{i}\right)\left(x-\lambda_{i}\right)^{k}\,

and consider the operators

(85)

E_{\lambda_{i}}:=p_{\lambda_{i}}(A).

Therefore,

(86)

I=E_{\lambda_{1}}+\dots+E_{\lambda_{s}}.

It is easy to check that

E_{\lambda_{i}}E_{\lambda_{k}}=\begin{cases}{\bf 0}&\text{ if }i\not=k\\
E_{\lambda_{i}}&\text{ if }i=k\end{cases}.

Indeed, if
i\not=k
,
p_{\lambda_{i}}(x)p_{\lambda_{k}}(x)
has the same roots as
m_{A}(x)
, and so
m_{A}|p_{\lambda_{i}}p_{\lambda_{k}}
having that
E_{\lambda_{i}}E_{\lambda_{k}}=p_{\lambda_{i}}(A)p_{\lambda_{k}}(A)={\bf 0}
. Also, notice that when we multiply by
E_{\lambda_{i}}
on both sides of Identity (
86
) we get that
E_{\lambda_{i}}=(E_{\lambda_{i}})^{2}
(i.e.,
E_{\lambda_{i}}
is an idempotent operator). Moreover,
E_{\lambda_{i}}
and
I-E_{\lambda_{i}}
(where
I-E_{\lambda_{i}}=\sum_{j\not=i}E_{\lambda_{j}}
) are idempotent projectors such that
I=E_{\lambda_{i}}+(I-E_{\lambda_{i}})
and
E_{\lambda_{i}}(I-E_{\lambda_{i}})={\bf 0}
. In addition, we can write

(87)

E_{\lambda_{i}}=h_{\lambda_{i}}(A)\prod_{j\neq i}\left(A-\lambda_{j}I\right)^{n_{j}},\qquad\text{ and }\qquad I-E_{\lambda_{i}}=\widetilde{h_{\lambda_{i}}}(A)(A-\lambda_{i}I)^{n_{i}}

where the polynomial
h_{\lambda_{i}}(x)
is given in (
84
), and the explicit formulas for
\widetilde{h_{\lambda_{i}}}(x)
can be deduced from (
83
) obtaining

(88)

\widetilde{h_{\lambda_{i}}}(x):=\sum_{k=1,k\not=j}^{s}h_{\lambda_{k}}(x)\prod_{j\neq k,j\not=i}\left(x-\lambda_{j}I\right)^{n_{j}}.

Therefore, using the above characterization and the decomposition of
\mathbb{F}^{d}
as a direct sum (not necessarily orthogonal) of the generalized eigenspaces of
A

\mathbb{F}^{d}={\bigoplus_{j=1}^{s}}\mathop{Ker}(A-\lambda_{j}I)^{d},

we obtain

(89)

\mathop{Ker}(E_{\lambda_{i}})={\bigoplus_{j\not=i}}\mathop{Ker}(A-\lambda_{j}I)^{d},\qquad\mathop{Ker}(I-E_{\lambda_{i}})=\mathop{Ker}(A-\lambda_{i}I)^{d}.

In sum,
E_{\lambda_{i}}
is the projection onto the generalized eigenspace corresponding to eigenvalue
\lambda_{i}
whose kernel is the direct sum of the rest of the generalized eigenspaces of
A
.
Finally, taking the adjoint in the resolution of the identity (
86
) we get

\displaystyle I

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{s}\left[\left(\sum_{j=0}^{n_{i}-1}\frac{1}{j!}\phi_{i}^{(j)}\left(\lambda_{i}\right)\left(A-\lambda_{i}I\right)^{j}\right)\prod_{j\neq i}\left(A-\lambda_{j}I\right)^{n_{j}}\right]\right)^{*}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{s}\left[\left(\sum_{j=0}^{n_{i}-1}\frac{1}{j!}\overline{\phi_{i}^{(j)}\left(\lambda_{i}\right)}\left(A^{*}-\overline{\lambda_{i}}I\right)^{j}\right)\prod_{j\neq i}\left(A^{*}-\overline{\lambda_{j}}I\right)^{n_{j}}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{s}\underbrace{\left[\left(\sum_{j=0}^{n_{i}-1}\frac{1}{j!}(\phi_{i}^{*})^{(j)}\left(\overline{\lambda_{i}}\right)\left(A^{*}-\overline{\lambda_{i}}I\right)^{j}\right)\prod_{j\neq i}\left(A^{*}-\overline{\lambda_{j}}I\right)^{n_{j}}\right]}_{E_{\lambda_{i}}^{*}}

where
\phi_{i}^{*}(x):=1/\prod_{j\neq i}\left(x-\overline{\lambda_{j}}\right)^{n_{j}}
, which means that the operators
\{E_{\lambda_{i}}^{*}\}_{i=1}^{s}
are the corresponding idempotent projectors onto the generalized eigenspaces associated to
A^{*}
. Using the notation given in (
84
), we can write
E_{\lambda_{i}}^{*}=p_{\overline{\lambda}_{i}}(A^{*})
. Therefore, in analogy to (
89
)

(90)

\begin{array}[]{cc}\mathop{Ker}(E_{\lambda_{i}}^{*})=\displaystyle{\oplus_{j\not=i}}\mathop{Ker}(A^{*}-\overline{\lambda_{j}}I)^{d},&\qquad\mathop{Ker}(I-E_{\lambda_{i}}^{*})=\mathop{Ker}(A^{*}-\overline{\lambda_{i}}I)^{d}.\end{array}

We remark that it is not necessarily true that the projectors
E_{\lambda_{i}}
are self-adjoint operators, in fact, they are not orthogonal projections in general.
In the special case when
A
is in Jordan form (i.e., the matrix representation of the operator
A
with respect to the canonical basis gives rise to its Jordan matrix representation), we have that the generalized eigenspaces are mutually orthogonal, and so the projectors
E_{\lambda_{i}}
are orthogonal projections. In this case,
\mathop{Ker}(A-\lambda_{i}I)=\mathop{Ker}(A^{*}-\overline{\lambda_{i}}I)
.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
A
be a linear operator in
\mathbb{F}^{d}
, and
b
be a vector in
\mathbb{F}^{d}
. Let
\lambda_{i}
be an eigenvalue of
A
and
E_{\lambda_{i}}
be the projection operator onto the generalized eigenspace of
A
corresponding to
\lambda_{i}
. Then,
E_{\lambda_{i}}b\not=\textbf{0}
if and only if
m_{b}(\lambda_{i})=0
(where
m_{b}
is the unique minimal
A
-annihilating polynomial of
b
.)

Moreover, the eigenspace corresponding to
\lambda_{i}
can be characterized by

\mathop{Ker}(A-\lambda_{i}I)=\left\{p_{b,\lambda_{i}}(A)b:\,b\in\mathbb{F}^{d}\text{ with }m_{b}(\lambda_{i})=0,\text{ and }p_{b,\lambda_{i}}(x):=\frac{m_{b}(x)}{x-\lambda_{i}}\right\}.

(Notice that when
\lambda_{i}=1
, then
p_{b,1}(x)=\widehat{m_{b}}(x)
.)

Proof.

First, observe that
E_{\lambda_{i}}b\neq\textbf{0}
if and only if
p_{\lambda_{i}}(A)b\not=\textbf{0}
where
p_{\lambda_{i}}(x)
is given by (
84
). From its expression
p_{\lambda_{i}}(\lambda_{i})\neq 0
, and
p_{\lambda_{i}}(\lambda_{j})=0
for all
j\neq i
.
Thus, if
(x-\lambda_{i})
is not a factor of the minimal
A
-annihilator
m_{b}
of
b
, then,
p_{\lambda_{i}}(A)b=\textbf{0}
since for this case
m_{b}
divides
p_{\lambda_{i}}
.
Conversely, if
m_{b}(\lambda_{i})=0
then
(x-\lambda_{i})
is a factor of all
A
-annihilators of
b
. Since
p_{\lambda_{i}}(\lambda_{i})\neq 0
, then
p_{\lambda_{i}}(A)b\not=\textbf{0}
.

For the last assertion, it is easy to check that

\left\{p_{b,\lambda_{i}}(A)b:\,b\in\mathbb{F}^{d}\text{ with }m_{b}(\lambda_{i})=0,\text{ and }p_{b,\lambda_{i}}(x):=\frac{m_{b}(x)}{x-\lambda_{i}}\right\}\subseteq\mathop{Ker}(A-\lambda_{i}I).

Indeed,

(A-\lambda_{i}I)p_{b,\lambda_{i}}(A)b=m_{b}(A)b=\textbf{0}.

The other inclusion also follows easily by noticing that, in general, a vector
v
is an eigenvector of
A
corresponding to eigenvalue
\lambda_{i}
if and only if its minimal
A
-annihilating polynomial is
m_{v}(x)=(x-\lambda_{i})
. Therefore, if
v\in\mathbb{F}^{d}
is an eigenvector of
A
corresponding to eigenvalue
\lambda_{i}
, we have that

p_{b,\lambda_{i}}(x)=\frac{m_{v}(x)}{x-\lambda_{i}}=\frac{x-\lambda_{i}}{x-\lambda_{i}}\equiv 1.

So, we can trivially write
v=p_{v,\lambda_{i}}(A)v
which belongs to

\left\{p_{b,\lambda_{i}}(A)b:\,b\in\mathbb{F}^{d}\text{ with }m_{b}(\lambda_{i})=0,\text{ and }p_{b,\lambda_{i}}(x):=\frac{m_{b}(x)}{x-\lambda_{i}}\right\}.

∎
