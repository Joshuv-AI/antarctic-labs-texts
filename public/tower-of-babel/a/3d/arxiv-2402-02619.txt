Arithmetic in Transformers Explained
Philip Quirke Clement Neo Fazl Barez Apart Research Apart Research Apart Research University of Oxford University of Oxford

Abstract
While recent work has shown transformers can learn addition, previous models exhibit poor prediction accuracy and are limited to small numbers. Furthermore, the relationship between single-task and multitask arithmetic capabilities remains unexplored. In this work, we analyze 44 autoregressive transformer models trained on addition, subtraction, or both. These include 16 addition-only models, 2 subtraction-only models, 8 “mixed” models trained to perform addition and subtraction, and 14 mixed models initialized with parameters from an addition-only model. The models span 5- to 15-digit questions, 2 to 4 attention heads, and 2 to 3 layers. We show that the addition models converge on a common logical algorithm, with most models achieving >99.999% prediction accuracy. We provide a detailed mechanistic explanation of how this algorithm is implemented within the network architecture. Subtraction-only models have lower accuracy. With the initialized mixed models, through parameter transfer experiments, we explore how multitask learning dynamics evolve, revealing that some features originally specialized for addition become polysemantic, serving both operations, and boosting subtraction accuracy. We explain the mixed algorithm mechanically. Finally, we introduce a reusable library of mechanistic interpretability tools to define, locate, and visualize these algorithmic circuits across multiple models.

Introduction

Transformer-based large language models (LLMs) are powerful
(
Barak et al. 2022
)
yet largely inscrutable due to their complex, nonlinear interactions in dense layers within high-dimensional spaces. Given this complexity, their deployment in critical settings
(
Zhang et al. 2022
)
highlights the need to understand their behavior.
Hendrycks & Mazeika 2022
argue that making these models interpretable is key to their safe use.

Previous work by
(
Quirke & Barez 2024
)
demonstrated that transformers can learn addition on small numbers, but their models had poor accuracy and were limited to a single operation. Understanding how transformers can overcome these limitations to perform more accurate mathematical computations across multiple operations is crucial for several reasons. First, it provides insights into how neural networks can learn and combine multiple precise algorithms, a key capability for more complex reasoning tasks. Second, studying how models transition from single-task to multitask competence while maintaining accuracy reveals important principles about knowledge transfer and representation sharing in neural networks.

Understanding how models arrive at their predictions is critical for diagnosing errors, enhancing safety, and gaining fundamental insights into modern neural networks. Mechanistic interpretability provides a pathway toward this understanding by identifying the “circuits” responsible for particular computations within a network’s layers and attention heads.

In this work, we use multidigit arithmetic as a revealing testbed for mechanistic interpretability. We train and analyze 44 autoregressive transformer models on addition, subtraction, or both, covering question lengths of 5 to 15 digits and varying in their number of layers (2–3) and attention heads (2–4). Our experiments encompass three categories of models: addition-only, subtraction-only, and “mixed” models trained to handle both operations. Through a systematic investigation of these networks, we show that addition-only models converge on a highly accurate, shared algorithm, yielding near-perfect performance. We mechanistically explain the algorithm. Subtraction-only models, in contrast, exhibit lower accuracy.

To explore how multitask learning reshapes these internal algorithms, we then conduct parameter transfer experiments using three distinct approaches, each involving the initialization of mixed models with parameters from addition-only networks. Interestingly, one of these approaches boosts the subtraction accuracy beyond what is achieved by mixed models trained from scratch, indicating that features specialized for addition can be effectively repurposed for subtraction. We provide a detailed mechanistic explanation of the mixed algorithm, illustrating how certain addition circuits become “polysemantic” under multitask learning, driving behavior for both operations.

A further contribution of our study is the development of a reusable library of mechanistic interpretability tools. This library enables researchers to define and search for algorithmic features, visualize network structures and feature interactions, and conduct cross-model comparisons efficiently.

Overall, our results demonstrate the power of mechanistic interpretability in shedding light on how transformers learn and reuse algorithmic circuits across related tasks, paving the way for deeper understanding and more reliable deployment of large language models in diverse domains.

Related Work

Mechanistic interpretability aims to reverse engineer neural networks to find interpretable algorithms that are implemented in a model’s weights
(
Olah et al. 2020
)
. Mathematical frameworks
(
Elhage et al. 2021a
)
explain how transformer attention heads can work with each other to implement complex algorithms.

Causal Scrubbing
(
Jenner et al. 2023
)
recommends explaining a model algorithm by documenting a low-level computation graph, mapping from the graph to the model nodes that implement the computation, and performing experimentation verification. Investigative techniques such as ablation interventions, activation unembeddings
(
nostalgebraist 2020
)
, and sparse autoencoders
(
Nanda 2023
;
Cunningham et al. 2023
)
, underpinned by the more theoretical frameworks
(
Elhage et al. 2021b
;
Geva et al. 2022
)
, provide tools to help confirm a mapping.

Investigating pre-trained LMs on Arithmetic.

Even though basic arithmetic can be solved following a few simple rules, pre-trained LMs often struggle to solve simple math questions
(
Hendrycks et al. 2021
)
. Causal mediation analysis
(
Stolfo et al. 2023
)
has been used to investigate how large pre-trained LMs like Pythia and GPT-J performed addition to solve word problems. It is also possible to improve a model’s arithmetic abilities used supervised fine tuning - including enriched training data
(
Liu & Low 2023
)
.

Studying Toy Models for Arithmetic.
Applying mechanistic interpretability to toy transformers can help to better isolate clear, distinct circuits given the highly specific experimental setup for the model studied
(
Nanda et al. 2023
)
.
Quirke & Barez 2024
detailed a 1-layer, 3-head transformer model that performs 5-digit addition, showing it failed on edge cases (e.g. “77778+22222=100000” where a “carry 1” cascades through 4 digits), incorrectly predicting 1% of questions.

Many natural prediction problems decompose into a finite set of knowledge and skills that are “quantized” into discrete chunks (quanta)
(
Michaud et al. 2023
)
. Models must learn these quanta to reduce loss. Understanding a network reduces to enumerating its quanta. Other studies
(
Schaeffer et al. 2023
)
prove useful ways to measure quanta in mathematical prediction problems.

3
Methodology

Transformer models learn addition algorithms different from traditional human methods. We define an alternative, mathematically-equivalent framework for addition and demonstrate our models implements this approach.

3.1
Mathematical Framework

Consider the task of adding two
n
-digit numbers together. We define the first number (or addend) as
D=\{D_{n-1},D_{n-2},\ldots,D_{0}\}
and the second number as
D^{\prime}=\{D^{\prime}_{n-1},D^{\prime}_{n-2},\ldots,D^{\prime}_{0}\}
and the answer (or sum) as
A=\{A_{n},A_{n-1},\ldots,A_{0}\}
. Figure
2
shows an illustrative example.

Figure 2:
For 5-digit addition, our model has 12 input (question) and 6 output (answer) token positions. We name the question tokens D4, …, D0, and D’4, …, D’0 and the answer tokens A5, …, A0. For n-digits, we use the terms
D_{n}
,
D^{\prime}_{n}
and
A_{n}
.

We adopt the framework from
Quirke & Barez 2024
for our model’s addition process, where they break down the task of multi-digit addition into an addition subtask and a carry subtask. Firstly, the “Simple Addition” subtask
A_{n}.SA
naively calculates the sum of corresponding digit pairs in the addends:

A_{n}.SA=(D_{n}+D^{\prime}_{n})\mod 10

(1)

The “Simple Carry” subtask
A_{n}.SC
then determines whether the addition between the two digits creates a carry bit:

A_{n}.SC=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }(D_{n}+D^{\prime}_{n})\geq 10,\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(2)

By combining these two subtasks, the model and framework from
Quirke & Barez 2024
is capable of handling simple addition and carry bits generated directly from digit pair addition. However, it struggles with problems with ’cascading carry’ bits, where a carry bit from one digit position propagates to the next.

Consider “00144+00056=000210”. Adding 4+5 in the tens position doesn’t generate a carry bit directly, but a carry bit propagates from the ones position. A model only summing digit pairs and their direct carry bits would fail, producing an incorrect result like “00144+50006=000110”. The
Quirke & Barez 2024
1-layer model could cascade carry bits across two digits, but not three or more.

3.2
Extending the Mathematical Framework

To solve
n
-digit addition with high accuracy, we need to extend the basic framework to handle carry bits that cascade through multiple digits. Consider the problem ”44444+55556=100000”. For an autoregressive model to correctly predict the first answer digit as ”1”, it needs to track how the carry bit cascades from the rightmost digit all the way to the leftmost position in a single forward pass. This is particularly challenging for an autoregressive model that predicts answer tokens from left to right.

To solve this, we extend the mathematical framework with two key subtasks.

First, the digit-level subtask ”Sum Type”
A_{n}.ST
analyzes each pair of digits and classifies their sum as either definitely causing a carry, possibly causing a carry, or definitely not causing a carry. This is implemented by a TriCase operation that maps each digit pair (
D_{n}
and
D^{\prime}_{n}
), TriCase into one of three possible states:

\begin{split}A_{n}.ST&=\underbrace{\text{TriCase}}_{(D_{n},D^{\prime}_{n})}=\\
&\begin{cases}ST10&\text{if }(D_{n}+D^{\prime}_{n})\geq 10\text{ (definite carry)}\\
ST9&\text{if }(D_{n}+D^{\prime}_{n})=9\text{ (possible carry)}\\
ST8&\text{if }(D_{n}+D^{\prime}_{n})\leq 8\text{ (no carry)}\\
\end{cases}\end{split}

(3)

The key insight is that while some digit pairs definitely generate a carry (like 5+6), and others definitely don’t (like 2+3), pairs that sum to 9 (like 4+5) may or may not generate a carry depending on whether there’s an incoming carry bit from the next lower digit.

Then, we introduce the ”Sum Validation” subtask
A_{n}.SV
that processes these carry states sequentially to determine whether any ”possible carries” become ”definite carries”. This subtask is implemented using a TriAdd operation that examines each digit’s sum type along with the sum type of the next lower digit:

\begin{split}A_{n}.SV&=\underbrace{\text{TriAdd}}_{(A_{n}.ST,A_{n-1}.ST)}=\\
&\begin{cases}ST10&\text{if }A_{n}.ST=ST10\text{ or }\\
&\quad(A_{n}.ST=ST9\text{ and }\\
&\quad A_{n-1}.ST=ST10),\\
ST8&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}\end{split}

(4)

Revisiting the problem ”44444+55556=100000”, the model can accurately determine whether the first answer digit A5 should be 1 or 0 by examining all digit pairs in sequence. This chain of operations uses TriAdd to process the Sum Types from least to most significant digits:

\displaystyle A1.SV

\displaystyle=\text{TriAdd}(A1.ST,A0.ST)

\displaystyle A2.SV

\displaystyle=\text{TriAdd}(A2.ST,A1.SV)

\displaystyle A3.SV

\displaystyle=\text{TriAdd}(A3.ST,A2.SV)

\displaystyle A4.SV

\displaystyle=\text{TriAdd}(A4.ST,A3.SV)

\displaystyle A5

\displaystyle=(A4.SV=ST10)

Most importantly, by using this cascading calculation to calculating A5, the model also simulatenously calculates the accurate carry bits for
each
answer digit. The carry bits are simply the SV values we computed along the way—we already have A3.SV for A4’s carry bit, A2.SV for A3’s carry bit, and so on.

Within our extended framework, the model only requires the subtasks
A_{n}
.
SA,
A_{n}.ST
and
A_{n}.SV
to accurately perform
n
-digit addition accurately. Some models may also use the redundant
A_{n}
.
SC
subtask.

3.3
Techniques

To undetsand how the model implements arithmetic operations, we analyzed both attention heads and MLP layers at each token position. We define a
node
as either an attention head or MLP layer at a specific token position (e.g., the first attention head in the first layer processing the third digit). We employed four techniques to understand each node’s function:

1.

Intervention Ablation.
To determine the role of a node’s output, we replace its output with the mean vector of its outputs across a batch, then measured how this impacted downstream predictions. For each subtask, we developed automated intervention ablation tests across
n
-digits to verify whether a node implemented that subtask.

2.

Attention Patterns.
For each attention head node, we examined which input tokens received significant attention (defined as post-softmax attention score
>0.01
). This revealed which parts of the input each attention head considered relevant.

3.

Principal Component Analysis (PCA)
. We applied PCA to node outputs, especially when investigating attention heads that our framework suggests might have descrete states (e.g. tri-state or bi-state outputs).

4.

Question Complexity
. We categorized questions by computational complexity (App.
G
). For addition, categories S0-S4 reflect how many sequential digits a “carry one” operation cascades through. For subtraction, categories M0-M4 reflect a similar cascading of ”borrow one” operations. We analyzed which nodes were necessary for correct predictions in each category.

4.1
Training a Five-Digit Addition Model

Previous work by
Quirke & Barez 2024
achieved
\sim
99% accuracy on 5-digit addition using a 1-layer addition model. To improve upon this, we systematically explored different model architectures and found that a transformer with 2 layers and 3 attention heads per layer was the minimal configuration needed to achieve >99.999% accuracy (see App.
D

We trained our model to perform 5-digit addition using an architecture with 2 layers and 3 attention heads per layer. The model’s vocabulary consisted of 14 tokens: the digits 0-9 plus the mathematical operators +, -, =, *, and /. We used a batch size of 64, learning rate of 0.00008, and weight decay of 0.1.

To ensure robust performance on edge cases, we trained on an infinite dataset that was deliberately enriched with rare numerical combinations. The loss function was defined as the mean negative log likelihood across all output tokens. After 30,000 training batches, the model achieved a final training loss of approximately
2.3\times 10^{-8}
, and subsequent testing confirmed accuracy exceeding 99.999%. For comprehensive training details and results, see App.

Core Algorithm Discovery.
The model’s most challenging task is predicting A5, the first answer digit, at position P11. This digit, which can only be 0 or 1, depends on potential carry-bit cascades through multiple positions (e.g., in 55555+44445=100000). After two unsuccessful algorithm hypotheses (detailed in App.

Investigating n-digit Addition models

To test whether this algorithm generalises beyond 5-digit addition, we conducted a comprehensive study of larger addition models. We trained 15 2-layer transformer models to handle addition problems ranging from 5- to 15-digits. These models used varying random seeds and optimizers, and included a sign token in their output format (e.g. 111111+222222=+0333333).

Analysis Framework.
To systematically analyse these models, we first created a declarative method for specifying each subtask within the addition process. Each subtask definition includes (1) the input digits it processes, (2) the answer digit it affects, (3) the complexity level it influences, and (4) expected PCA patterns and ablation test results. Using this declaration, we identified the nodes performing these subtasks across all models.

Since our framework lays out the addition algorithm in terms of its required subtasks., we can also verify whether a given model implements this algorithm by checking for the presence and proper sequencing of all required subtasks. For example, our addition algorithm specifies that the model must execute
A_{n}.ST
subtasks for each question digit before the first answer token. Table
2
shows how an addition model fully implements all required subtasks in its nodes.

Using this framework, we found that all fifteen 2-layer addition models, regardless of their size, implemented the same core algorithm we identified in our 5-digit model. This consistent pattern suggests we have identified a robust and generalizable algorithm that naturally arises when transformer models are trained on addition in our setup where the digits are tokenized individually.

4.4
Training n-digit Mixed models

To explore how transformers can learn multiple arithmetic operations simultaneously, we conducted experiments with models capable of both addition and subtraction. We were particularly interested in whether knowledge from addition-specialized models could transfer to this expanded task.

We trained 14 mixed models with various architectures, including 6- and 10-digit models with 2-3 layers and 3-4 attention heads. Each model was initialized using weights from a highly accurate addition-only model, specifically copying the weights into the first two layers and first three heads of the mixed model. During training, we used a curriculum of 80% subtraction and 20% addition examples, with deliberate enrichment of rare edge cases for both operations.

This initialization approach proved effective, with most models achieving high accuracy on both operations. We explored alternative training strategies, such as periodically ”freezing” the inserted attention heads or MLPs by restoring their original addition-model weights every 100 training steps. However, these more constrained approaches resulted in lower accuracy, suggesting that some flexibility in modifying the initialized weights was necessary for optimal performance.

The initialized mixed experimental models achieve >99.999% accuracy and contain the same subtasks, implying a common algorithm.

Just as addition must handle ”cascading carry” cases (e.g., 99999+1=100000), subtraction must handle ”cascading borrow” cases. Consider 100000-1=099999, where a borrow cascades through multiple zeros. To handle such cases efficiently, we define a three-state classification
MT
that parallels addition’s
ST

\begin{split}A_{n}.MT&=\underbrace{\text{TriCase}}_{(D_{n},D^{\prime}_{n})}=\\
&\begin{cases}MTN&\text{if }D_{n}<D^{\prime}_{n}\\
MT0&\text{if }D_{n}=D^{\prime}_{n}\\
MT1&\text{if }D_{n}>D^{\prime}_{n}\\
\end{cases}\end{split}

(5)

For M questions, MTN is a

possible

not

Finally, paralleling the ”cascading carry one”

A_{n}.MV
and
A_{n}.NV
for positive and negative cases.

4.6
Mixed model question class detection

Mixed models must identify whether each input belongs to one of three classes: addition (S), positive-answer subtraction (M), or negative-answer subtraction (N). For addition questions (marked by ”+”), classification is immediate. For subtraction questions (marked by ”-”), the model must determine whether D
\geq
D’ to distinguish between M and N classes. This classification must occur by the ”=” token since it determines whether the first answer token will be ”+” or ”-”.

The model can determine D
\geq
D’ in two ways. First, it can use the
A_{n}.MT
values already computed for subtraction. Alternatively, it can use a dedicated comparison circuit based on a new subtask:

A_{n}.GT=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }D_{n}\geq D^{\prime}_{n},\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(6)

For a 4-digit number, this comparison cascades through the digits: D
\geq
D’ is true if either
A3.GT=1
, or if
A3.GT=0
and
A2.GT=1
, and so on through
A0.GT
. We verify this circuit through ablation tests that show its disruption causes answer signs to flip.

We found all mixed models incorporate the
A_{n}.MT
and
A_{n}.GT
subtasks to determine whether the answer is postiive or negative. Typically (but not always), the same node computes both subtasks (see Table
2
). Since both the
MT
and
GT
approaches can validly distinguish between positive and negative outcomes, the model sometimes chooses one approach for some digits and the other approach for others.

A single node can implement multiple arithmetic operations (e.g.,
A2.SA
,
A2.MD
,
A2.ND
) but has no intrinsic way of knowing whether the current question is addition, positive subtraction, or negative subtraction. Hence, we hypothesise that it outputs
all
three possible results into the residual stream. A different node must then select the correct result by identifying the question’s operation and required sign.

In practice, we observe that one or more attention heads attend to the question operator token (OPR) and the initial answer sign token (SGN), encoding the necessary information for the downstream MLP layers to pick which of the three outputs is correct. As Table
2
shows, each answer digit typically has one attention head that focuses on both the OPR and SGN tokens, thereby enabling the MLP to filter out the irrelevant outputs and retain the correct one.

5
Conclusion

Our analysis of 44 transformer models demonstrates their ability to learn highly accurate mathematical algorithms, achieving over 99.999% accuracy on addition tasks up to 13 digits. By investigating how models implement computations using attention heads and MLPs, we uncovered novel computational approaches distinct from traditional human calculation methods. Parameter transfer experiments revealed that features originally specialized for addition could be repurposed for subtraction, with many nodes becoming polysemantic.

Our research provides insights into how transformers learn precise algorithmic computations across related tasks, developing a reusable library of mechanistic interpretability tools that advances our understanding of neural network computational learning.

Impact Statement

Our work aims to explain the inner workings of transformer-based language models, which may have broad implications for a wide range of applications. A deeper understanding of generative AI has dual usage. While the potential for misuse exists, we discourage it. The knowledge gained can be harnessed to safeguard systems, ensuring they operate as intended. It is our sincere hope that this research will be directed towards the greater good, enriching our society and preventing detrimental effects. We encourage responsible use of AI, aligning with ethical guidelines.

Appendix B
Appendix: Model Configuration

Addition, subtraction and mixed (addition and subtraction) training experiments were done in a Colab notebook. The Colab runs on a T4 GPU. Each training run takes up to 60 mins. The key parameters (and their common configurations) are:

•

n_layers = 1, 2 or 3: Number of layers.

•

n_heads = 3 or 4: Number of attention heads.

•

n_digits = 5, 6 or 10: Number of digits in the question.

Each digit is represented as a separate token.
(
Liu & Low 2023
)
state that LLaMa’s “remarkable arithmetic ability … is mainly atributed to LLaMA’s consistent tokenization of numbers”. The model’s vocabulary contains 14 tokens ( 0, .., 9, +, -, =, *, / ) to enable this and planned future investigations.

Training uses a new batch of data each step (aka Infinite Training Data) to minimise memorisation. Depending on the configuration, each training run processes 1 to 4 million training datums. For the 5-digit addition problem there are 100,000 squared (that is 10 billion) possible questions. So the training data is much less than 1% of the possible problems.

Addition and subtraction include rare edge cases. For example, the
SS
cascades (e.g. 44445+55555=100000, 54321+45679=1000000,
44450+55550=10000, 1234+8769=10003) are exceedingly rare. The data generator
was enhanced to increase the frequency of all known edges cases. This lead to lower model loss.

Validation test data covering all edge cases was manually constructed. These test cases are not used during training.

The Colabs will be made available on publication.

Appendix C
Appendix: Model Loss

The model defaults to batch size = 64, learning rate = 0.00008 and weight decay = 0.1. The loss function is simple:

•

Per Digit Loss: For “per digit” graphs and analysis, for a given answer digit, the loss used is negative log likelihood.

•

All Digits Loss: For “all answer digits” graphs and analysis, the loss used is the mean of the “per digit” loss across all the answer digits.

In our experimental models, the number of digits in the question varies from 5 to 13, the number of layers varies from 1 to 4, the number of heads varies from 3 to 4. Each experimental model’s loss is detailed in Tabs.

Appendix D
Appendix: Addition Model Shape

While we wanted a very low loss addition model, we also wanted to keep the model compact - intuiting that a smaller model would be easier to understand than a large model. Here are the things we tried to reduce loss that
didn’t
work:

•

Increasing the frequency of hard (cascading
SS
) examples in the training data so the model has more hard examples to learn from. This improved training speed but did not reduce loss.

•

Increasing the number of attention heads from 3 to 4 or 5 (while still using 1 layer) to provide more computing power.

•

Changing the question format from “12345+22222=” to “12345+22222equals” giving the model more prediction steps after the question is revealed before it needs to state the first answer digit.

•

With n_layers = 1 increasing the number of attention heads from 3 to 4.

•

Changing the n_layers to 2 and n_heads to 2.

The smallest model shape that did reduce loss significantly was 2 layers with 3 attention heads.

Appendix M
Appendix: Mixed Model Initialization

We experimented with three approaches to re-using the trained addition model in the “mixed” (addition and subtraction) model:

•

Initialize Only: Initialize the untrained mixed model with the addition model weights before training begins.

•

Freeze Attention: As per “Initialize Only”, but also every 100 training steps recopy the attention head weights from the addition model into the partially-trained mixed model.

•

Freeze All: As per “Initialize Only”, but also every 100 training steps recopy the entire addition model (attention heads and MLP layers) into the partially-trained mixed model.

Our intuition was that “Initialize Only” would give the mixed model the most freedom to learn new algorithms, but that the “Freeze Attention” and “Freeze All” approaches would make the resulting trained mixed model easier to interpret (as we could reuse our addition model insights).

After experimentation we found that the “Initialize Only” approach was the only one that quickly trained to be able to do both addition and subtraction accurately. We concluded that the other two methods constrain the model’s ability to learn new algorithms too much.

We also experimented with “where” in the model we inserted the addition (6-digit, 2-layer, 3-head) model into the slightly larger (6-digit, 3-layer, 4-head) mixed model. That is, do we initialize the first 2 layers or the last 2 layers of the mixed model? Also do we initialize the first 3 attention heads or the last 3 attention heads of the mixed model? Our intuition was that initializing the first layers and heads would be more likely to cause the model to re-use the addition circuits adding interpretability, so we used this approach.

Some notes about the mixed models:

•

All the notes about the addition model (above) also apply to the mixed model.

•

The model contains a new subtask that stands out: The algorithm relies on calculations done at token position P0, when the model has only seen one question token! What information can the model gather from just the first token? Intuitively, if the first token is a “8” or “9” then the first answer token is more likely to be a “+” (and not a “-”). The model uses this heuristic even though this probabilistic information is sometimes incorrect and so will work against the model achieving very low loss.
