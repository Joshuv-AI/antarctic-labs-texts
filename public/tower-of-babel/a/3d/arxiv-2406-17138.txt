Lubrication-mediated rebounds off fluid baths
K. A. Phillips 1,2, P. A. Milewski 3

Abstract
We present herein the derivation of a lubrication-mediated (LM) quasi-potential model for droplet rebounds off deep liquid baths, assuming the presence of a persistent dynamic air layer which acts as a lubricating pressure transfer. We then present numerical simulations of the LM model for axisymmetric rebounds of solid spheres and compare quantitatively to current results in the literature, including experimental data in the low speed impact regime. In this regime the LM model has the advantage of being far more computationally tractable than DNS and is also able to provide detailed behaviour within the micro-metric thin lubrication region. The LM system has an interesting mathematical structure, with the lubrication layer providing a free boundary elliptic problem mediating the drop and bath free-boundary evolutionary equations.

Introduction

The role of the entrained air layer present throughout droplet rebounds has been well studied. Since the work of
Worthington (1881)
, where it was first questioned, it has been well established that rebounds are only able to occur in the presence of a lubricating layer of air preventing the two liquids contacting. The assumption that within the near-contact region, the air can behave like a lubrication layer
(
Hicks & Purvis, 2010
)
has directed a reach field of literature of impacts on other substrates such as porous media
(
Hicks & Purvis, 2017
)
. In the case of the impactor being solid, the same assumption can be made in the pre-impact time; that the layer of air helps delay contact
(
Moore, 2021
)
. Recent studies of drops that bounce exist in several configurations, for example off solid hydrophobic surfaces
(
Kolinski et al., 2014
)
, and off liquid films
(
Tang et al., 2019
)
.

There are fewer studies of drops rebounding off deep baths, particularly in the regime where the underlying fluid is inviscid (or nearly so) and capillary waves are long lived after the droplet impact. These models gained interest in part because of the work of Couder on indefinitely bouncing drops on vibrating baths
(
Couder et al., 2005
)
and its fascinating quantum mechanical analogies (
Bush (2015)
and references therein). The studies of
Galeano-Rios et al. (2017)
;
Galeano-Rios et al. (2019)
and
Alventosa et al. (2023)
imposed a so-called kinematic match between the solid sphere or drop and the bath to avoid modelling the air layer and obtained accurate results when compared to Faraday-pilot wave studies of bouncing and walking droplets. In
Galeano-Rios et al. (2021)
the kinematic match model was validated against experiments and DNS simulations of the Navier-Stokes equations of single rebounds of solid hydrophobic spheres for low Weber number impacts.

Current reduced models for solid or droplet rebounds on deep baths do not capture the dynamics of the air layer which is present throughout the drop-bath dynamics. This paper builds off the work on the impact of two-dimensional drops in
Phillips et al. (2024)
, where the lubrication air layer is considered as an additional fluid region coupling the drop deformation and the wave equations for the bath. We extend that model to three-dimensions in generality (Section 2), and compare its results to the kinematic match, direct numerical simulations and experiments for solid hydrophobic sphere impacts on water (Section 3).

2
Model

Consider an almost spherical droplet falling vertically downwards through the air, towards a deep liquid bath initially at rest. Let the subscripts
\alpha\in\{a,b,d\}
denote the air, bath, and droplet respectively, and introduce notation
\Omega_{\alpha}
for each fluid domain,
\mathbf{u}_{\alpha}
for the fluid velocity, with the density, dynamic viscosity, kinematic viscosity, and pressure of the fluids are given as
\rho_{\alpha},\mu_{\alpha},\nu_{\alpha},p_{\alpha}
respectively. Finally we use
\beta\in\{b,d\}
to denote the bath-air and droplet-air boundaries
\partial\Omega_{\beta}
, defined by a level set of
F_{\beta}
and the surface tension coefficient
\sigma_{\beta}
at the air-liquid interfaces.
In each fluid region we begin from the Navier-Stokes equations, and at the interfaces the usual kinematic condition, stress continuity and velocity continuity boundary conditions
\mathbf{u}_{a}=\mathbf{u}_{\beta}
are taken. Hence,

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=0,\qquad

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle\in\Omega_{\alpha},

(1)

\displaystyle\rho_{\alpha}(\partial_{t}\mathbf{u}_{\alpha}+(\mathbf{u}_{\alpha}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{u}_{\alpha})

\displaystyle=-\nabla p_{\alpha}+\mu_{\alpha}\nabla^{2}\mathbf{u}_{\alpha}-\rho_{\alpha}g\mathbf{e}_{z},\qquad

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle\in\Omega_{\alpha},

(2)

\displaystyle D_{t}F_{\beta}

\displaystyle=0,\qquad

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle\in\partial\Omega_{\beta},

(3)

\displaystyle p_{\beta}\mathbf{n}_{\beta}+\mu_{\beta}\tau_{\beta}\mathbf{n}_{\beta}

\displaystyle=p_{a}\mathbf{n}_{\beta}+\mu_{a}\tau_{a}\mathbf{n}_{\beta}-\sigma_{\beta}\kappa_{\beta},\qquad

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle\in\partial\Omega_{\beta}.

(4)

Where
\tau_{\alpha}=(\nabla\mathbf{u}_{\alpha}+\nabla\mathbf{u}_{\alpha}^{T})
is the stress tensor for each fluid,
\kappa_{\beta}
denotes curvature of the free surface with outward unit normal vector
\mathbf{n}_{\beta}
, gravity
g
acting downwards in the
-\mathbf{e}_{z}
direction, and
D_{t}
is the material derivative. These equations are supplemented by imposing decay of velocities in the far field or appropriate boundary conditions in the case of finite domains. We proceed with several modelling approximations which will reduce the system to a tractable state.

2.1
Linear quasi-potential model for the liquid bath

We assume viscous effects are small except in the lubrication layer, and follow methodology from
Dias et al. (2008)
which we briefly outline below. Take a Helmholtz decomposition of the liquid as small perturbation from potential flow
\mathbf{u}_{b}=\nabla\phi_{b}+\nabla\times\boldsymbol{\psi_{b}}
, and use tangential stress conditions and a boundary layer argument to eliminate the
\boldsymbol{\psi}_{b}
terms, expressing the rotational part of the flow in terms of
\phi_{b}
and
\eta_{b}
. A more detailed explanation of this argument can be found in
Phillips et al. (2024)
.
The free surface of the bath is given by
z=\eta_{b}(\mathbf{x},t)
where
z=0
is its undisturbed free surface. The governing linear system then becomes

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\Delta_{H}\phi_{b}+\partial_{z}^{2}\phi_{b},\qquad

\displaystyle z

\displaystyle\leq 0,

(5)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\phi_{b}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\rho_{b}}p_{a}-g\eta_{b}+\frac{\sigma_{b}}{\rho_{b}}\Delta_{H}\eta_{b}+2\nu_{b}\Delta_{H}\phi_{b},\qquad

\displaystyle z

\displaystyle=0,

(6)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\eta_{b}

\displaystyle=\partial_{z}\phi_{b}+2\nu_{b}\Delta_{H}\eta_{b},\qquad

\displaystyle z

\displaystyle=0,

(7)

where
\Delta_{H}
is used to denote the horizontal Laplacian, the curvature is small and of the form
\kappa_{b}=-\nabla_{H}\eta_{b}
, and velocity continuity
\mathbf{u}_{b}=\mathbf{u}_{a}
is imposed at the interface
\eta_{b}
between the bath and air layer. The terms proportional to
\nu_{b}
arise from
\boldsymbol{\psi_{b}}
, and are the corrections to the pressure and vertical velocity, respectively.

2.2
Air layer lubrication approximation

Away from the droplet-bath interaction, the air is assumed to be atmospheric pressure and have negligible effect on the system, as we must introduce the domain in which the lubrication effects may be important. The lower part of the droplet is described by
z=\eta_{d}(\mathbf{x}_{H},t)=\mathbf{X}(t)-S(\mathbf{x}_{H},t)
, composed of the shape of the lower part of the droplet
S
, and the vertical height of its centre of mass
\mathbf{X}
. The domain of
S
in
\mathbf{x}_{H}
is chosen such that
S
is single valued and with bounded gradients. The dynamics of the air layer are described through a lubrication approximation as in
Phillips et al. (2024)
. Consider a lubrication region
\Omega_{a}=\Omega_{L}\times[\eta_{b}(r,\theta,t),\eta_{d}(r,\theta,t)]
where its footprint is described in cylindrical coordinates as

\Omega_{L}=\{(r,\theta):r\in[0,r^{*}(\theta,t)],\theta\in[0,2\pi)\},

(8)

where
r^{*}
is the edge of the lubrication region. Denoting
h=\eta_{d}-\eta_{b}
, the edge of the lubrication layer is given by a criterion
h(r^{*},\theta,t)=\varepsilon
. It is assumed that the
\Omega_{L}
is a subset of the domain of S. Assuming that the layer between the droplet and the bath is thin, the leading order balance of the Navier-Stokes equations result in the lubrication balance where
\partial_{z}p_{a}=0
with

\displaystyle\nabla_{H}\cdot\mathbf{u}_{a}^{H}+\partial_{z}w_{a}

\displaystyle=0,\qquad

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle\in\Omega_{a},

(9)

\displaystyle\mu_{a}\partial_{z}^{2}\mathbf{u}_{a}^{H}

\displaystyle=\nabla_{H}p_{a},\qquad

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle\in\Omega_{a},

(10)

and velocity continuity holds at both interfaces:
\mathbf{u}_{a}=\mathbf{u}_{d}
at
z=\eta_{d}
and
\mathbf{u}_{a}=\nabla_{H}\phi
at
z=\eta_{b}
. Here
\nabla_{H}
is the horizontal gradient operator, and
\mathbf{u}_{a}^{H}=(u_{r},u_{\theta})
is the horizontal velocity of the air layer, such that
\mathbf{u}_{a}=(\mathbf{u}_{a}^{H},w_{a})
.
As expected, the pressure
p_{a}=P(r,\theta,t)
is independent of
z
throughout the air layer. Further, we can solve the equation for
\mathbf{u}_{a}^{H}
in terms of
P
:

\mathbf{u}_{a}^{H}=\frac{\nabla_{H}P}{2\mu_{a}}(z-\eta_{b})(z-\eta_{d})+\frac{\nabla_{H}\phi_{b}|_{\eta_{b}}}{\eta_{b}-\eta_{d}}(z-\eta_{d})+\frac{\mathbf{u}^{H}_{d}|_{\eta_{d}}}{\eta_{d}-\eta_{b}}(z-\eta_{b}).

(11)

To obtain the thin film equation we integrate vertically the conservation of mass (incompressibility) equation resulting in

\nabla_{H}\cdot\int_{\eta_{b}}^{\eta_{d}}\mathbf{u}_{a}^{H}\;\text{d}z+\left(\mathbf{u}_{a}^{H}\Big|_{\eta_{b}}\cdot\nabla_{H}\eta_{b}-\mathbf{u}_{a}^{H}\Big|_{\eta_{d}}\cdot\nabla_{H}\eta_{d}\right)+\left(w_{a}\Big|_{\eta_{d}}-w_{a}\Big|_{\eta_{b}}\right)=0.

(12)

All terms after the integral can expressed exactly as
\partial_{t}\eta_{d}-\partial_{t}\eta_{b}=\partial_{t}h
from the kinematic conditions at both interfaces. Finally, evaluating the integral in the first term using (
11
) to obtain the form of the flux

\mathbf{Q}=\int_{\eta_{b}}^{\eta_{d}}\mathbf{u}_{a}^{H}\;\text{d}z=-\frac{1}{12\mu_{a}}h^{3}\nabla_{H}P+\frac{1}{2}h\left(\nabla_{H}\phi_{b}+\mathbf{u}_{d}^{H}\right),

(13)

results in the thin film equation

\partial_{t}h+\nabla_{H}\cdot\mathbf{Q}=0,\qquad\mathbf{x_{H}}\in\Omega_{L}.

(14)

As we shall see below this should be considered a nonlinear free boundary elliptic equation for
P
where
-h_{t}
is considered as a forcing term, with the boundary condition
P=0
at the evolving boundary curve
\partial\Omega_{L}
.

2.3
Droplet Model

2.3.1
Droplet Deformation

Linear damped droplet oscillations are well understood
(
Lamb, 1924
)
and the results have been used in a variety of contexts e.g.
Aalilija et al. (2020)
. In this section we write equivalent equations for these oscillations using the same quasi-potential theory used above for the bath. Consider a capillary scale droplet of liquid which when unperturbed is a sphere of radius
R_{0}
. Describing the problem using spherical polar coordinates with azimuthal angle
\theta
and polar angle
\varphi
, we can take a Helmholtz decomposition for the liquid velocity
\mathbf{u}_{d}=\nabla\phi_{d}+\nabla\times\boldsymbol{\psi}_{d}
. Using the same argument argument as above
(
Phillips et al., 2024
)
we obtain similar governing equations to those of the bath (
5
)-(
7
)

\displaystyle\Delta\phi_{d}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle r<R_{0},

(15)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\phi_{d}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\rho_{d}}p_{a}-\frac{\sigma_{d}}{\rho_{d}}\kappa_{d}-2\nu_{d}\partial_{r}^{2}\phi_{d},

\displaystyle r=R_{0},

(16)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\zeta_{d}

\displaystyle=\partial_{r}\phi_{d}+2\nu_{d}\left(\frac{1}{r^{2}}\Delta_{s}\zeta_{d}-\frac{1}{r^{3}}\int\Delta_{s}\phi_{d}\;\text{d}t\right),

\displaystyle r=R_{0},

(17)

where surface of the droplet is given by
r=R_{0}+\zeta_{d}(\theta,\varphi)
, and
\kappa_{d}
is the mean curvature

\kappa_{d}=-\left(\frac{2}{R_{0}^{2}}\zeta_{d}+\frac{1}{R_{0}^{2}}\Delta_{s}\zeta_{d}\right),\qquad\Delta=\frac{1}{r^{2}}\partial_{r}(r^{2}\partial_{r})+\frac{1}{r^{2}}\Delta_{s},

(18)

with
\Delta_{s}
the Laplace-Beltrami operator (i.e. the surface Laplacian). The equations are valid in the small oscillation limit and have been truncated to contain terms that are linear in the Ohnesorge number (see appendix for a discussion of the nondimensional equations).
One may proceed with an eigenfunction decomposition of
\zeta_{d}
and
\phi_{d}
as

\phi_{d}=\sum_{l=2}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-l}^{l}a_{lm}(t)r^{l}Y_{l}^{m},\qquad\zeta_{d}=\sum_{l=2}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-l}^{l}c_{lm}(t)Y_{l}^{m},

(19)

where the
Y_{l}^{m}(\theta,\varphi)=e^{im\varphi}P_{l}^{m}(cos\theta)
are spherical harmonics satisfying

-\Delta_{s}Y_{l}^{m}=(l)(l+1)Y_{l}^{m}.

(20)

The
P_{l}^{m}
are the associated Legendre polynomials of degree
l
and order
m
. The terms
l=0,1
have been omitted as
l=0
corresponds to a dilation of the sphere and
l=1
corresponds to translational and rotational symmetries which need to be considered separately as they will be affected by the external forces due to the air layer. Substitution into the boundary conditions (
16
)-(
17
) leads to the differential equation (where terms of order
Oh^{2}
have been omitted)

\ddot{c}_{l,m}+2\lambda_{l}\dot{c}_{l,m}+\omega_{l}^{2}c_{l,m}=-\frac{l\hat{p}_{l,m}}{\rho R_{0}}\;,

(21)

where
\hat{p}_{l,m}
is the
Y_{l,m}
coefficient of of the lubrication air pressure
p_{a}
. The coefficients of the velocity potential are given to leading order by
a_{l,m}=\frac{\dot{c}_{l,m}}{lR_{0}^{l-1}}
. The damping coefficients and modal frequencies are given by

\lambda_{l}=(2l+1)(l-1)\frac{\mu}{\rho R_{0}^{2}},\qquad\omega_{l}^{2}=l(l-1)(l+2)\frac{\sigma}{\rho R_{0}^{3}},

(22)

as seen in the literature
(
Lamb, 1924
;
Aalilija et al., 2020
;
Alventosa et al., 2023
)
. A similar equation may be obtained for the coefficients
a_{lm}(t)
, however these are uneccessary for the LM model and have been omitted for brevity.

2.3.2
Equations for the centre of mass

The location of the droplet is determined through updating the position of the centre of mass, given by
\mathbf{X}(t)
. We shall disregard drag effects from the air, instead only considering contributions from the lubrication layer, then the equation of motion is

m\ddot{\mathbf{X}}=F=-\int_{\Omega_{L}}p_{a}\;\mathbf{n_{d}}\;\text{d}a-mg\;\hat{\mathbf{z}},

(23)

where
\mathbf{n_{d}}
is the outward normal of the droplet
m=4\rho\pi R_{0}^{3}/3
is its mass,
g
is the gravitational constant and
\hat{\mathbf{z}}
is the unit vector in the vertical direction. The boundary of the droplet is now given by
\mathbf{X}+(R_{0}+\zeta_{d}(\theta,\varphi))\mathbf{\hat{r}}
, where
\mathbf{\hat{r}}
is a radial unit vector relative to the position
\mathbf{X}
.

We note that we have disregarded global rotational motion of the droplet (forced by both asymmetric pressure and shear stresses in the lubrication layer) which would significantly add to the modelling complexity, and which will not be relevant for the vertical (axisymmetric) impacts we shall consider next. We also expect these effects to be small for quasi-normal impacts (e.g.
Galeano-Rios et al. (2019)
).

2.4
The lubrication-mediated droplet-bath system

We can now propose a closed system of equations for the evolution of the thin film, liquid bath and droplet. Within the bath and the droplet one may reduce the system further using the framework of Dirichelet to Neumann (DtN) maps. Consider the “trace” of the potentials on the boundary of the domains in which Laplace’s equation are solved

\Phi_{b}(\mathbf{x_{H}},t)=\phi_{b}|_{z=0},\qquad\Phi_{d}(\theta,\varphi,t)=\phi_{d}|_{r=R_{0}}.

The DtN map, denoted
D
, is the linear map that provides the normal derivative of the potential given its value on the boundary. In particular we denote

(\partial_{z}\phi_{b})|_{z=0}=D_{b}\Phi_{b},\qquad(\partial_{r}\phi_{d})|_{r=R_{0}}=D_{d}\Phi_{d}.

We shall see that these maps are easily expressed in terms of eigenfunction expansions. Making use of DtN maps we now have the evolution system

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\Phi_{b}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\rho_{b}}p_{a}-g\eta_{b}+\frac{\sigma_{b}}{\rho_{b}}\Delta_{H}\eta_{b}+2\nu_{b}\Delta_{H}\Phi_{b},

(24)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\eta_{b}

\displaystyle=D_{b}\Phi_{b}+2\nu_{b}\Delta_{H}\eta_{b}\doteq F_{b},

(25)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\Phi_{d}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\rho_{d}}p_{a}-\frac{\sigma_{d}}{\rho_{d}}\kappa_{d}-2\nu_{d}\partial_{r}^{2}\Phi_{d},

(26)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\zeta_{d}

\displaystyle=D_{d}\Phi_{d}+2\nu_{d}\left(\frac{1}{r^{2}}\Delta_{s}\zeta_{d}-\frac{1}{r^{3}}\Delta_{s}D_{d}^{-1}\zeta_{d}\right)\doteq F_{d},

(27)

\displaystyle m\ddot{\mathbf{X}}

\displaystyle=F=-\int_{\Omega_{L}}p_{a}\;\mathbf{n_{d}}\;\text{d}a-mg\;\hat{\mathbf{z}},

(28)

\displaystyle\nabla_{H}\cdot\mathbf{Q}

\displaystyle=W+F_{d}-F_{b},\qquad\mathbf{x_{H}}\in\Omega_{L}.

(29)

In the surface potential equations, the pressure
p_{a}
is nonzero only in
\Omega_{L}
and
W=\frac{d\mathbf{X}}{dt}
is the vertical velocity of the droplet’s centre of mass. In the kinematic condition for the droplet we used the leading order balance
\partial_{t}\zeta_{d}=D_{d}\Phi_{d}
to express the right hand side locally in time. While
D_{d}
was defined using the velocity potential, its inverse acts on
\zeta_{d}
here. This is admissible since it is an invertible linear operator on functions with mean zero, and we may restrict the inverse map to have mean zero also. Equation (
29
), where
\mathbf{Q}
is given by (
13
) is an inhomogeneous elliptic equation for the pressure with the boundary condition that
p_{a}=0
on the free boundary
\partial\Omega_{L}
.

3
Axisymmetric solid impacts and numerical results

We now consider the simplest case in which this framework can be used and compared to prior simulations and experiments: the rebound of solid (hydrophobic) spheres
(
Galeano-Rios et al., 2021
)
. This simplifies considerably the system, reducing the dimension and eliminating droplet oscillations. Considering the axisymmetric domain for the bath to of finite radius
r=L
, and imposing Neumann boundary conditions there for both
\eta_{b}
and
\Phi_{b}

we expand these in terms of Bessel functions of the first kind,

\eta_{b}(r,t)=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}a_{j}(t)J_{0}(k_{j}r),\qquad\Phi_{b}(r,t)=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}b_{j}(t)J_{0}(k_{j}r),

(30)

where the
k_{j}
satisfy
J_{0}^{\prime}(k_{j}L)=0
. We note that in this basis
\phi_{b}=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}b_{j}J_{0}(k_{j}r)e^{k_{j}z}
to satisfy Laplace’s equation and the DtN map is expressed simply as

D_{b}\Phi=\partial_{z}\phi_{b}|_{z=0}=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}k_{j}b_{j}J_{0}(k_{j}r).

(31)

Hence the DtN operator in this basis corresponds to multiplication by
k_{j}
. Similarly, the horizontal Laplacian
\Delta_{H}
results in a Bessel multiplier
-k_{j}^{2}
. The system can now be written for the Bessel coefficients as

\displaystyle\dot{a}_{j}

\displaystyle=k_{j}b_{j}-2\nu_{l}k_{j}^{2}a_{j},

(32)

\displaystyle\dot{b}_{j}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\rho_{b}}\hat{p}_{j}-\frac{\sigma_{b}}{\rho_{b}}k_{j}^{2}a_{j}-2\nu_{l}k^{2}b_{j},

(33)

\displaystyle m\ddot{Z}

\displaystyle={2\pi}\int_{0}^{r^{*}}p_{a}\;r\text{d}r-mg,

(34)

with the elliptic equation for pressure

-\frac{1}{r}\partial_{r}\left(\frac{r}{12\mu_{a}}h^{3}\partial_{r}p_{a}+\frac{r}{2}h\partial_{r}\Phi_{b}\right)=\partial_{t}h=W-(D_{b}\Phi_{b}+2\nu_{l}\Delta_{H}\eta_{b}),

(35)

where
W=\dot{Z}
,
h=Z-\sqrt{R_{0}^{2}-r^{2}}-\eta_{b}
for
r\leq R_{0}
,
r^{*}
is defined by
h(r^{*},t)=\varepsilon
for
\varepsilon\ll 1
, and where
\hat{p}_{j}
are the Bessel coefficients of the Pressure provided by

\hat{p}_{j}=\frac{2}{(LJ_{0}(k_{j}L))^{2}}\int_{0}^{L}p_{a}(r,t)J_{0}(k_{j}r)r\text{d}r\;.

(36)

Similar expressions apply for obtaining
a_{j}
from
\eta_{b}
and
b_{j}
from
\Phi_{b}
. The pressure can be integrated from (
35
) using
p_{a}(r^{*},t)=0
and the symmetry condition
\partial_{r}p_{a}(r^{*},t)=0
:

p_{a}=-\int_{r^{*}}^{r}\left[\frac{12\mu_{a}}{r^{\prime}h(r^{\prime},t)^{3}}\left(\int_{0}^{r^{\prime}}r^{\prime\prime}\partial_{t}h(r^{\prime\prime},t)\text{d}r^{\prime\prime}\right)+\frac{6\mu_{a}}{h(r^{\prime},t)^{2}}\partial_{r}\Phi_{b}(r^{\prime},t)\right]\text{d}r^{\prime}\;.

(37)

3.1
Numerical implementation

We shall compute a solution approximating the system (
32
)-(
37
) by truncating the Bessel expansions at a large
N
and integrating the resulting differential equations (
32
)-(
34
) with a fourth order Runge-Kutta scheme. The equation for the pressure (
37
) is calculated using a trapezium rule. Since Bessel expansions are ill-conditioned on regular grids, we use an oversampled nonuniform grid in
r
with
M
points distributed on Chebyshev collocation points on
[0,L]
using
\theta_{j}=(j-1)\pi/(M-1)
with
j=1\ldots M
and
r_{j}=\frac{L(1+\cos(\theta_{j}))}{2}
.
Hence we compute two matrices: an
M\times N
matrix which evaluates an N-term Bessel series at
r_{j}
and an
N\times M
matrix corresponding to calculating the projection formula (
36
) for the Bessel coefficients of a function given at
r_{j}
.

4
Conclusions

The lubrication-mediated model herein efficiently resolves certain features of droplet rebounds heretofore unattainable computationally in reduced models. Future work include computational studies including drop deformation and non-axisymmetric impact, coupled with the continued validation through results compared to the wider literature (e.g results from studies such as
Tang et al. (2018)
;
Alventosa et al. (2023)
etc).

This work follows several (e.g.
Galeano-Rios et al. (2019)
;
Galeano-Rios et al. (2017)
) which were motivated by Faraday bouncing droplet dynamics
(
Bush, 2015
)
. We expect as future development to use this model with Faraday pilot-wave systems, as an accurate reproduction of the vertical motion of the droplet is thought to be crucial to the observed statistical features resembling quantum mechanics
(
Durey et al., 2020
)
. The present formulation of a lubrication layer mediating the impact of two bodies of fluid also has potential interest in the mathematics community which has recently addressed rigorously questions of whether fluid surfaces can intersect
(
Fefferman et al., 2016
)
.
