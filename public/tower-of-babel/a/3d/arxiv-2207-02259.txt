A Furstenberg-type problem for circles, and a Kaufman-type restricted projection theorem in ℝ 3 \mathbb{R}^{3}
Malabika Pramanik, Tongou Yang and Joshua Zahl

Abstract
. We resolve a conjecture of Fässler and Orponen on the dimension of exceptional projections to one-dimensional subspaces indexed by a space curve in ℝ 3 \mathbb{R}^{3} . We do this by obtaining sharp L p L^{p} bounds for a variant of the Wolff circular maximal function over fractal sets for a class of C 2 C^{2} curves related to Sogge’s cinematic curvature condition. A key new tool is the use of lens cutting techniques from discrete geometry.



1.
Introduction

In 1954, Marstrand
[
20
]
proved that if
Z\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
is Borel, then
\mathrm{dim}\,(Z\cdot v)=\min(\mathrm{dim}\,Z,1)
for a.e.
v\in S^{1}
. Here and throughout, “
\mathrm{dim}\,
” will mean Hausdorff dimension. This was sharpened by Kaufman
[
16
]
in 1968, who bounded the size of the set of exceptional directions for which the above inequality fails.

Theorem 1.1

(Kaufman)
.

Let
Z\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be Borel, and let
0\leq s<\min\{\mathrm{dim}\,Z,1\}
. Then

(1.1)

\mathrm{dim}\,\{\theta\in S^{1}\colon\mathrm{dim}\,(Z\cdot\theta)<s\}\leq s.

Marstrand and Kaufman’s results have been generalized to higher dimensions and other settings, and they are the foundation of projection theory, which is an active area in geometric measure theory. See
[
22
]
for an introduction to the topic, or
[
5
]
for a recent survey of the area.

In this paper, we will be interested in analogues of the above results for Borel sets
Z\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
. As a starting point, one analogue of Marstrand’s theorem says that
\mathrm{dim}\,(Z\cdot v)=\min(\mathrm{dim}\,Z,1)
for a.e.
v\in S^{2}
. There are also analogues of Kaufman’s theorem; for example, if
0\leq s<\min\{\mathrm{dim}\,Z,1\}
then

(1.2)

\mathrm{dim}\,\{v\in S^{2}\colon\mathrm{dim}\,(Z\cdot v)<s\}\leq 1+s.

See
[
21
]
for details. In particular, (
1.2
) says that the set of
v\in S^{2}
for which
\mathrm{dim}\,(Z\cdot v)=0
has dimension at most 1, and this can be sharp—for example, if
Z
is a subset of the
z
-axis then
Z\cdot v=\{0\}
for each
v
in the great circle
S^{2}\cap\{z=0\}
. It is possible that stronger bounds hold if we restrict the set of projection directions to a subset of
S^{2}
that does not concentrate near great circles. In this direction, Fässler and Orponen
[
6
]
conjectured the following restricted Marstrand-type estimate.

Conjecture 1.2
.

Let
I\subset\mathbb{R}
be a compact interval and let
\gamma:I\to S^{2}
be a
C^{2}
curve that satisfies the “escaping great circle” condition

(1.3)

\operatorname{span}\big\{\gamma(\theta),\dot{\gamma}(\theta),\ddot{\gamma}(\theta)\big\}=\mathbb{R}^{3}\quad\textrm{for all}\ \theta\in I.

Let
Z\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
be analytic. Then for almost every
\theta\in I
,
\mathrm{dim}\,(\gamma(\theta)\cdot Z)=\min(\mathrm{dim}\,Z,1)
.

In
[
6
]
, Fässler and Orponen proved Conjecture
1.2
in the special case
\mathrm{dim}\,(Z)\leq 1/2
. In
[
12
]
, He considered the related problem of estimating the size of the set where
\mathrm{dim}\,(\gamma(\theta)\cdot Z)
is close to
(\mathrm{dim}\,Z)/3
. In
[
13
]
, Käenmäki, Orponen, and Venieri used ideas from incidence geometry, and specifically a circle tangency bound due to Wolff
[
29
]
to prove Conjecture
1.2
(for all
Z
) in the special case
\gamma(\theta)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\cos\theta,\sin\theta,1)
. Our contribution is the following Kaufman-type sharpening of Conjecture
1.2
.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
I\subset\mathbb{R}
be a compact interval and let
\gamma:I\to S^{2}
be a
C^{2}
curve that satisfies the “escaping great circle” condition (
1.3
). Let
Z\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
be analytic and let
0\leq s<\min\{\mathrm{dim}\,Z,1\}
. Then

(1.4)

\mathrm{dim}\,\{\theta\in I\colon\mathrm{dim}\,(Z\cdot\gamma(\theta))<s\}\leq s.

In particular, Conjecture
1.2
is true.

Added November 14, 2022:
There have been several exiting recent developments related to Theorem
1.3
. First, Gan, Guth and Maldague
[
9
]
have also recently and independently resolved Conjecture
1.2
, using a different method related to decoupling. Second, Gan, Guo, Guth, Harris, Maldague and Wang
[
8
]
solved a closely related conjecture of Fässler and Orponen, where projections onto one-dimensional subspaces of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
are replaced by projections onto two-dimensional subspaces. Finally, Harris
[
11
]
proved that if
\mathrm{dim}\,Z>1
, then
\gamma(\theta)\cdot Z
has positive Lebesgue measure for almost every
\theta
.

1.1.
A Furstenberg-type problem for circles

Theorem
1.3
follows from an
L^{p}
bound for a variant of Wolff’s circular maximal function, where circles have been replaced by a class of
C^{2}
curves that are the graphs of we call “cinematic functions,” and the domain of integration is restricted to a special type of fractal set. To state this result, we will introduce several definitions. First, we recall the definition of a
(\delta,\alpha;C)_{1}
-set from
[
14
]
.

Definition 1.4
.

Let
0<\alpha\leq 1
,
\delta>0
,
C\geq 1
. A set
E\subset[0,1]
is called a
(\delta,\alpha;C)_{1}
-set if

(a)

E
is a union of intervals of length
\delta
.

(b)

For every interval
I\subset[0,1]
, we have the Frostman-type non-concentration condition

(1.5)

|E\cap I|\leq C\delta^{1-\alpha}|I|^{\alpha}.

Since (
1.5
) always holds for intervals of length
\leq\delta
, every
(\delta,\alpha;C)_{1}
-set is also a
(\delta,\beta;C)_{1}
-set if
\beta\geq\alpha
. We will be concerned with certain subsets of the plane, which are fibered products of
(\delta,\alpha;C)_{1}
-sets.

Definition 1.5
.

Let
0<\alpha,\beta\leq 1
,
\delta>0
, and
C\geq 1
. A set
E\subset[0,1]^{2}
is called a
(\delta,\alpha;C)_{1}\times(\delta,\beta;C)_{1}
quasi-product if
E
is Borel and can be expressed in the form

E=\bigcup_{a\in A}\{a\}\times B_{a},

where
A
is a
(\delta,\alpha;C)_{1}
-set and
B_{a}
is a
(\delta,\beta;C)_{1}
-set for each
a\in A
.

Our main result is a maximal function estimate, where the domain of integration is a quasi-product. Our next task is to introduce the class of objects that can be analyzed using our maximal function estimate. The following definition was inspired by Sogge’s notion of cinematic curvature
[
24
]
, which was considered by Kolasa and Wolff
[
17
]
in the context of the Wolff circular maximal function.

Definition 1.6
.

Let
I\subset\mathbb{R}
be a compact interval and let
\mathcal{F}\subset C^{2}(I)
. We say
\mathcal{F}
is a
family of cinematic functions
, with
cinematic constant

K
and
doubling constant

D
, if the following conditions hold.

(1)

\mathcal{F}
has diameter at most
K
(here and in what follows, we use the usual metric on
C^{2}(I)
).

(2)

\mathcal{F}
is a doubling metric space, with doubling constant at most
D
.

(3)

For all
f,g\in\mathcal{F},
we have

(1.6)

\inf_{t}\big(|f(t)-g(t)|+|f^{\prime}(t)-g^{\prime}(t)|+|f^{\prime\prime}(t)-g^{\prime\prime}(t)|\big)\geq K^{-1}\|f-g\|_{C^{2}(I)}.

With these definitions, we can now state the following variant of Wolff’s circular maximal function theorem.

Theorem 1.7
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
,
0<\alpha\leq\zeta\leq 1,
and
D,K\geq 1
. Then the following is true for all
\delta>0
sufficiently small (depending on
D,K,\varepsilon
only). Let
\mathcal{F}
be a family of cinematic functions, with cinematic constant
K
and doubling constant
D
. Let
E
be a
(\delta,\alpha;\delta^{-\varepsilon})_{1}\times(\delta,\alpha;\delta^{-\varepsilon})_{1}
quasi-product.

Let
F\subset\mathcal{F}
be a set of functions that satisfies the Frostman-type non-concentration condition

(1.7)

\#(F\cap B)\leq\delta^{-\varepsilon}(r/\delta)^{\zeta}\quad\textrm{for all balls}\ B\subset C^{2}(I)\ \textrm{of radius}\ r\geq\delta.

Then

(1.8)

\int_{E}\Big(\sum_{f\in F}{\bf 1}_{f^{\delta}}\Big)^{3/2}\leq\delta^{2-\alpha/2-\zeta/2-C\varepsilon}(\#F),

where
C=C(D)
depends only on
D
, and
f^{\delta}
is the
\delta
-neighborhood of the graph of
f
.

Remarks.

1.

Since
[0,1]^{2}
is a
(\delta,1;1)_{1}\times(\delta,1;1)_{1}
quasi-product and
I,\mathcal{F}
are bounded, as a special case of Theorem
1.7
we obtain the estimate

(1.9)

\Big\|\sum_{f\in F}{\bf 1}_{f^{\delta}}\Big\|_{3/2}\leq\delta^{-C\varepsilon}(\delta\#F)^{2/3},

where
F
satisfies (
1.7
) for
\zeta=1
, and the order of parameters are the same as in the statement of Theorem
1.7
.

2.

As a special case of (
1.9
), Theorem
1.7
generalizes Wolff’s
L^{3/2}
circular maximal function bound from
[
27
]
. The (upper) halves of circles with centers in a small neighborhood of the origin and radii between 1 and 2 form a family of cinematic functions
\mathcal{C}
over the interval
[-1/2,1/2]
. If we paramaterize a circle by it’s center-radius pair
(x,y,r)
, then a set
C\subset\mathcal{C}
obeys the non-concentration condition (
1.7
) if the corresponding collection of centre-radius pairs
P_{C}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
obeys a corresponding Frostman-type non-concentration condition; namely,

(1.10)

\#(P_{C}\cap B)\leq\delta^{-\varepsilon}(r/\delta)\quad\textrm{for all balls}\ B\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}\ \textrm{of radius}\ r\geq\delta.

Thus if
C
is a set of circles with centers in
[0,1]^{2}
and radii between 1 and 2, whose center-radius pairs obey (
1.10
), then Theorem
1.7
says that

(1.11)

\Big\|\sum_{c\in C}{\bf 1}_{c^{\delta}}\Big\|_{3/2}\leq\delta^{-C\varepsilon}(\delta\#C)^{2/3}.

In
[
27
]
, Wolff proved (
1.7
) in the special case where the circles in
C
had
\delta
-separated radii.

3.

Theorem
1.7
generalizes the third author’s variable coefficient Wolff circular maximal function bound
[
31
]
from curves given by a
C^{\infty}
defining function to curves given by a
C^{3}
defining function (the latter is the minimal amount of regularity required to state Sogge’s cinematic curvature condition for the defining function). A precise statement is slightly technical, and is given in Appendix
A
.

4.

Theorem
1.7
has proved useful in other contexts beyond the proof of Theorem
1.3
. For example, Wang and the third author
[
26
]
used Theorem
1.7
to prove a special case of the Kakeya conjecture for sticky Kakeya sets in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, and Katz, Wu, and the third author
[
15
]
used Theorem
1.7
to prove a special case of the Kakeya conjecture for
SL_{2}
Kakeya sets. Chang, Dosidis, and Kim
[
3
]
used a corollary of Theorem
1.7
to analyze a Nikodym-type maximal function associated to spheres in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Fässler and Orponen
[
7
]
used Theorem
1.7
to study vertical projections in the first Heisenberg group. We remark that in
[
26
]
, it is crucial that Theorem
1.3
holds for
C^{2}
curves; previous maximal function bounds are insufficient.

Theorem
1.7
will be discussed further in Section
1.3
, and the complete proof will be given in Section
5
.

1.2.
From maximal functions to restricted projections

We will briefly sketch the proof of Theorem
1.3
and describe the connection between Theorems
1.3
and
1.7
. For the purpose of this proof sketch, we will ignore many technical details. A complete proof is given in Section
2
. Let
Z\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
be Borel with
\mathrm{dim}\,Z=\zeta\leq 1
, and let
0\leq s<\mathrm{dim}\,Z
. Let
\Theta=\{\theta\in I\colon\mathrm{dim}\,(Z\cdot\gamma(\theta))<s\}
, and suppose for contradiction that
\mathrm{dim}\,\Theta=\alpha>s
.

In
[
13
]
, Käenmäki, Orponen, and Venieri observed that this problem could be transformed to a question about curves in the plane. For each
z\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, define the plane curve

(1.12)

\Gamma_{z}=\{(\theta,z\cdot\gamma(\theta):\theta\in I\}.

Then for each
\theta\in\Theta
, the vertical line
L_{\theta}=\mathbb{R}\times\{\theta\}
satisfies

\mathrm{dim}\,\big(L_{\theta}\cap\bigcup\Gamma_{z}\big)<s.

Next, we discretize the arrangement at a small scale
\delta>0
. The idea (ignoring some technical details) is that we replace
Z
by a
\delta
-separated subset
Z_{\delta}
of cardinality roughly
\delta^{-\zeta}
that satisfies a Frostman-type non-concentration condition, and we replace
\Theta
by a
(\delta,\alpha;\delta^{-\varepsilon})_{1}
-set, which we denote by
\Theta_{\delta}
. For each
z\in Z_{\delta}
, let
\Gamma^{\delta}_{z}
be the
\delta
-neighborhood of the curve
\Gamma_{z}
. Then for most
\theta\in\Theta_{\delta}
,
L_{\theta}\cap\bigcup_{z\in Z_{\delta}}\Gamma^{\delta}_{z}
will have one-dimensional Lebesgue measure at most
\delta^{1-\eta-\varepsilon}
, and it will be contained in a
(\delta,s,\delta^{-\varepsilon})_{1}
-set. Thus by Hölder’s inequality we have that

(1.13)

\int_{\Theta_{\delta}\times\mathbb{R}}\Big(\sum_{z\in Z_{\delta}}{\bf 1}_{\Gamma^{\delta}_{z}}\Big)^{3/2}\geq\delta^{2+\frac{1}{2}s-\frac{3}{2}\zeta-\alpha+\varepsilon}.

Most of the integrand in (
1.13
) is supported on a
(\delta,\alpha;\delta^{-\varepsilon})_{1}\times(\delta,\alpha;\delta^{-\varepsilon})_{1}
quasi-product. We can use the “escaping great circle” condition (
1.3
) to show that the set of curves
\{\Gamma^{\delta}_{z}\colon z\in Z_{\delta}\}
are the graphs of functions from a family of cinematic functions, and the Frostman-type non-concentration condition on
Z_{\delta}
implies the analogous bound (
1.7
). Thus we can apply Theorem
1.7
to upper-bound (
1.13
). Comparing (
1.8
) and (
1.13
) (and recalling that
Z_{\delta}
has cardinality roughly
\delta^{-\zeta}
), we conclude that
\alpha\leq s
. This gives us our desired contradiction and completes the proof.

Remarks
.

1.

The main challenge when executing the proof strategy described above is that we must discretize the set
Z
, the set
\Theta
, and the sets
L_{\theta}\cap\bigcup\Gamma_{z}
at a common scale
\delta
. We refer the reader to Section
2
for details.

2.

Our bound (
1.8
) in Theorem
1.7
becomes stronger as the dimension
\alpha
of the quasi-product
E
decreases. With this sensitivity to
E
, Theorem
1.7
is related to the Furstenberg set problem for curves; see
[
18
]
for a related result on this topic. If we replace the domain of integration in (
1.8
) by
\mathbb{R}^{2}
or
[0,1]^{2}
, then it is possible for the LHS of (
1.8
) to be as large as
\delta\#F
. On the other hand, it is easy to verify that if the RHS of (
1.8
) were replaced by the weaker estimate
\delta\#F
, then this would not be sufficient to prove the Kaufman-type estimate from Theorem
1.3
(though it would still be strong enough to prove Conjecture
1.2
).

1.3.
Proof sketch of Theorem
1.7

We conclude the introduction with a discussion of the main ideas in Theorem
1.7
and a brief sketch the proof. In
[
28
]
, Wolff proved that for each
\varepsilon>0
and all
\delta>0
sufficiently small, if
C
is a set of circles with
\delta
-separated radii
1\leq r\leq 2
, then

(1.14)

\Big\|\sum_{c\in C}{\bf 1}_{c^{\delta}}\Big\|_{p}\leq\delta^{-\varepsilon}(\delta\#C)^{1/p},\quad p=3/2,

where
c^{\delta}
denotes the
\delta
-neighborhood of the circle
c
. Wolff’s proof was quite technical, but it has been simplified in several follow-up works
[
29
,
23
]
. The main ingredient in these later proofs was a bound on the number of “tangency rectangles” determined by the arrangement
C
. Informally, a tangency rectangle is a curvilinear rectangle
R
of dimensions
\delta^{1/2}\times\delta
(other dimensions are possible, but we will ignore this for now) that is contained in at least two annuli
c_{1}^{\delta},c_{2}^{\delta}
, with
c_{1},c_{2}\in C
. Heuristically, we may suppose that every pair of circles
c_{1},c_{2}\in C
either intersect transversely, in which case
|c_{1}^{\delta}\cap c_{2}^{\delta}|\sim\delta^{2}
, or they intersect tangentially, in which case
|c_{1}^{\delta}\cap c_{2}^{\delta}|\sim\delta^{3/2}
and the intersection is localized to a tangency rectangle.

The contribution to the LHS of (
1.14
) from the first type of intersection is negligible. Indeed, if every pair of circles intersected transversely, then (
1.14
) would hold for
p=2
, which is a stronger estimate. Thus the goal is to control the number of tangential intersections, or more precisely, the number of tangency rectangles where these intersections occur. Let
\mathcal{R}
be a set of tangency rectangles that cover all the tangential intersections determined by
C
. For each
R\in\mathcal{R}
, let
m(R)
be the number circles
c\in C
with
R\subset c^{\delta}
. If the rectangles in
\mathcal{R}
were disjoint and if the LHS of (
1.14
) was concentrated on these rectangles, then we would have a
\ell^{3/2}(\mathcal{R})\to L^{3/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
bound of the form

(1.15)

\Big\|\sum_{c\in C}{\bf 1}_{c^{\delta}}\Big\|_{3/2}\leq\delta\Big(\sum_{\mathcal{R}}m(R)^{3/2}\Big)^{2/3}.

We caution that reader that in general, (
1.15
) is not true as stated. However, the heuristic (
1.15
) was made precise by Schlag
[
23
]
, who used induction on scales and a bilinear
\implies
linear argument to prove that (
1.14
) would follow from a bilinear version of the weak
\ell^{3/2}
bound

(1.16)

\#\{R\in\mathcal{R}\colon m(R)\geq k\}\leq(\#C/k)^{3/2}.

Previously in
[
29
]
, Wolff had proved a suitable bilinear version of (
1.16
) using the “cuttings” method developed by researchers in computational geometry in the late 1980s (see
[
4
,
1
]
), and these two results combined to give a new proof of (
1.14
).

The cuttings method makes crucial use of the property that circles are algebraic curves. In
[
30
]
, the third author extended Wolff’s arguments and the cuttings method to obtain an analogue of (
1.14
) where circles are replaced by algebraic curves of bounded degree that satisfy Sogge’s cinematic curvature condition (see also
[
17
]
for earlier work in this direction). In
[
31
]
, the third author used the newly developed polynomial partitioning technique of Guth and Katz
[
10
]
to extend (
1.14
) to smooth curves with cinematic curvature. All of these techniques, however, require that the curves are algebraic (or in the case of smooth curves, that they can be accurately approximated by moderately low degree algebraic curves). These methods do not work, and new ideas are needed, for curves that are merely
C^{k}
.

The main difficulty in proving Theorem
1.7
is establishing an analogue of (
1.16
) for
C^{2}
curves. We do this by adapting a result due to Marcus and Tardos
[
19
]
from topological graph theory, which concerns objects called lenses. In brief, if
\mathcal{C}
is a set of Jordan curves in the plane, then a
lens
is a pair of curve-segments
\lambda_{1}\subset\gamma_{1};

\lambda_{2}\subset\gamma_{2}
;
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\in\mathcal{C},
so that
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
intersect at their endpoints, and
\lambda_{1}\cup\lambda_{2}
is homeomorphic to
S^{1}
. For example, if two circles
c_{1},c_{2}
with well-separated centers share a common tangency rectangle, then after a small perturbation we expect to find arcs of
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
of length roughly
\delta^{1/2}
that create a lens.

Lemma 10 from
[
19
]
says that an arrangement of
n
closed Jordan curves that satisfy a certain “pseudo-circle” property determines
O(n^{3/2}\log n)
pairwise non-overlapping lenses. We use this result to obtain an analogue of (
1.16
) by carefully perturbing our arrangement of curves, so that the number of tangency rectangles in
\mathcal{R}
is comparable to the number of lenses. See Proposition
4.1
for a precise statement, and Section
4
for the details of this argument.

With Proposition
4.1
in hand, it remains to prove Theorem
1.7
. We encounter several difficulties. First, our bilinear analogue of (
1.16
) is more general than the corresponding estimates from
[
29
,
30
,
31
]
because it applies to
C^{2}
curves rather than algebraic (or smooth) curves. However, our bilinear analogue of (
1.16
) requires slightly stronger assumptions, which do not work well with induction on scales. Thus we need a new bilinear
\implies
linear argument that does not use induction on scales. Closely related to this difficulty is the annoyance that intersections between curves need not be completely tangential or transverse—there are many intermediate amounts of tangency or transversality. We handle these difficulties by combining a
L^{2}
argument at fine scales with a suitable bilinear version of (
1.16
) at coarse scales. In brief, we define a parameter
\Delta
that measures the typical amount of tangency between curves; if
\Delta=\delta
then the main contribution to the LHS of (
1.14
) comes from pairs of curves that intersect completely tangentially, while if
\Delta=1
then the main contribution comes from pairs of curves that intersect completely transversely. We then divide
\mathbb{R}^{2}
into rectangles of dimensions
\Delta\times\Delta^{1/2}
. If two curves intersect, then typically this intersection is localized to one such rectangle, and the curves are tangent at scale
\Delta
. Thus we can use a bilinear analogue of (
1.16
) to control the number of tangency rectangles at scale
\Delta
. Within each rectangle, most pairs of curves intersect with a precisely prescribed amount of transversality. Thus we can use a
L^{2}
argument to control the contribution from each rectangle. This is the most technical part of the paper, and the details are in Section
5
.

1.4.
Notation

To reduce clutter, we will write
A\lesssim B
or
A=O(B)
if there is a constant
C>0
so that
A\leq CB
. The constant
C
will be allowed to depend on several quantities, which are context-dependent. Based on context,
C
may depend on any of the quantities
|I|
,
\gamma
, and
\mathrm{dim}\,Z
from the statement of Theorem
1.3
, and also the quantites
K
,
D
, and
\varepsilon
from the statement of Theorem
1.7
.

In the arguments that follow, we will frequently introduce a small parameter
\delta>0
, and we will employ dyadic pigeonholing arguments that replace a set
X
with a subset of size roughly
|\log\delta|^{-1}(\#X)
. We will write
A\lessapprox B
or
B\gtrapprox A
if
A\lesssim|\log\delta|^{C_{0}}B
for some absolute constant
C_{0}
(in practice, we will always have
C_{0}\leq 100
). When we write
A\lessapprox B
, the relevant quantity
\delta
will always be apparent from context.

2.
From Theorem
1.7
to Theorem
1.3

In this section, we will expand upon the sketch from Section
1.2
. The following version of Theorem
1.7
is a main ingredient of the proof.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let us fix
\varepsilon>0
and dimensional parameters
0<\alpha\leq\zeta\leq 1
. Let
I
be a compact interval and let
\gamma\colon I\to S^{2}
be a curve satisfying (
1.3
). Then there exists a positive constant
\delta_{0}
depending only on these quantities, such that the following is true for all
0<\delta\leq\delta_{0}
.

Let
E
be a
(\delta,\alpha;\delta^{-\varepsilon})_{1}\times(\delta,\alpha;\delta^{-\varepsilon})_{1}
quasi-product. Let
Z_{\delta}\subset B(0,1)\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a
\delta
-separated set that satisfies the Frostman-type non-concentration condition

(2.1)

\#(Z_{\delta}\cap B)\leq\delta^{-\varepsilon}(r/\delta)^{\zeta}\quad\textrm{for all balls}\ B\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}\ \textrm{of radius}\ r\geq\delta.

Then

(2.2)

\int_{E}\Big(\sum_{z\in Z_{\delta}}{\bf 1}_{\Gamma^{\delta}_{z}}\Big)^{3/2}\leq\delta^{2-\alpha/2-\zeta/2-C\varepsilon}(\#Z_{\delta}),

where
C>0
is an absolute constant, and
\Gamma^{\delta}_{z}
is the
\delta
-neighborhood of the graph
\Gamma_{z}
defined in (
1.12
).

Proof.

Proposition
2.1
follows from Theorem
1.7
, applying the latter to the family of functions

F=\bigl\{f_{z}:z\in Z_{\delta}\bigr\}\subset\mathcal{F}=\bigl\{f_{z}:z\in B(0;1)\bigr\},

where
f_{z}(\theta)=\gamma(\theta)\cdot z
and
\gamma\colon I\to S^{2}
is a curve satisfying (
1.3
). In order for Theorem
1.7
to be applicable, we need to verify that
\mathcal{F}
is cinematic (with cinematic constant that depends only on
\gamma
) and that
F
satisfies a Frostman-type non-concentration bound (
1.7
). The verification proceeds as follows. First, we have

\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\lVert f\rVert_{C^{2}(I)}=\sup_{z\in B(0;1)}\lVert f_{z}\rVert_{C^{2}(I)}\leq\sup_{\theta\in I}\bigl[|\gamma(\theta)|+|\gamma^{\prime}(\theta)|+|\gamma^{\prime\prime}(\theta)|\bigr]|z|\leq||\gamma||_{C^{2}(I)}<\infty,

and depends only on
\gamma
. Next, if
z_{1},z_{2}\in B(0,1)
then
\|f_{z_{1}}-f_{z_{2}}\|_{C^{2}(I)}\leq||\gamma||_{C^{2}(I)}|z_{1}-z_{2}|
. Furthermore, the non-degeneracy condition (
1.3
) implies that

\inf_{\theta\in I}\Big[|f_{z_{1}}(\theta)-f_{z_{2}}(\theta)|+|f^{\prime}_{z_{1}}(\theta)-f^{\prime}_{z_{2}}(\theta)|+|f^{\prime\prime}_{z_{1}}(\theta)-f^{\prime\prime}_{z_{2}}(\theta)|\Big]\gtrsim\|f_{z_{1}}-f_{z_{2}}\|_{C^{2}(I)},

where the implicit constant depends only on
\gamma
. As a consequence,

(2.3)

\|f_{z_{1}}-f_{z_{2}}\|_{C^{2}(I)}\sim|z_{1}-z_{2}|\text{ for }z_{1},z_{2}\in B(0;1).

Thus
\mathcal{F}
is a doubling metric space (with the same doubling constant as
\mathbb{R}^{3}
). Finally, identifying
F
with
Z_{\delta}
, we observe from (
2.3
) that the Frostman-type non-concentration condition (
1.7
) for
F
follows from the analogous condition (
2.1
) for the Euclidean set
Z_{\delta}
.
∎

We are now ready to prove Theorem
1.3
. Let
\gamma\colon I\to S^{2}
satisfy (
1.3
) and let
Z\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
be Borel. After a harmless re-scaling, we can suppose
Z\subset B(0,1)
and after possibly replacing
Z
by a subset, we may suppose that
\mathrm{dim}\,Z\leq 1
. Let
0\leq s<\mathrm{dim}\,Z
. Aiming for a contradiction, suppose if possible that (
1.4
) fails, i.e.
\mathrm{dim}\,\Theta_{I}>s
, where

\Theta_{I}=\bigl\{\theta\in I:\mathrm{dim}\,\bigl(Z\cdot\gamma(\theta)\bigr)<s\bigr\}.

Then
\Theta_{I}
admits a subset
\Theta
with the propery that
s<\mathrm{dim}\,\Theta\leq\mathrm{dim}\,Z
. Set
\alpha=\mathrm{dim}\,\Theta
. We will show that the existence of such a set
\Theta\subset\Theta_{I}
contradicts the validity of Proposition
2.1
.

Define

(2.4)

\varepsilon=\frac{\alpha-s}{2C+11}>0,

where
C
is the constant from (
2.2
).

2.1.
Initial discretization

Bourgain
[
2
]
proved a discretized projection theorem, which bounded the number of exceptional directions where the projection of a
\delta
-discretized set can be small. Bourgain then showed how this discretized theorem implies an analogous Hausdorff dimension bound. The next set of arguments are similar to the reduction from Theorem 4 to Theorem 3 in
[
2
]
, but with a twist. The location of the twist will be noted below.

Let
\zeta=\mathrm{dim}\,Z
and let
\mathbb{P}
be a probability measure supported on
Z
that satisfies the Frostman condition

(2.5)

\mathbb{P}(B)\lesssim r^{\zeta-\varepsilon}\qquad\textrm{for all balls}\ B\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}\ \textrm{of radius}\ r>0.

Let
\nu
be a probability measure supported on
\Theta
with

(2.6)

\nu(J)\lesssim|J|^{\alpha-\varepsilon}\ \quad\textrm{for all intervals}\ J\subset\mathbb{R}.

Let
k_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}
be chosen sufficiently large, depending on
\gamma
,
\zeta
,
s
and
\alpha
. The choice of
k_{0}
will be determined as follows: our arguments below will select a number
0<\delta\leq 2^{-\lfloor(1+\varepsilon)^{k_{0}}\rfloor}
(as in
[
14
, Section 7]
; we do not get to choose
\delta
, but our choice of
k_{0}
gives an upper bound for
\delta
). Eventually, we will arrive at the conclusion that a certain inequality involving
\delta
is impossible, provided
\delta>0
is sufficiently small (depending on
\gamma
,
\zeta
,
s
and
\alpha
). In order to arrive at a contradiction, we will select
k_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}
sufficiently large so that any positive
\delta\leq 2^{-\lfloor(1+\varepsilon)^{k_{0}}\rfloor}
obeys Proposition
2.1
.

For each
\theta\in I
, define
\rho_{\theta}\colon\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}
by
\rho_{\theta}(z)=z\cdot\gamma(\theta)
. For each
\theta\in\operatorname{supp}(\nu)\subset\Theta
, cover

\rho_{\theta}(Z)
by a union of sets
\{G_{\theta,k}\}_{k=k_{0}}^{\infty}
, where
G_{\theta,k}
is a
(2^{-\lfloor(1+\varepsilon)^{k}\rfloor},s;2^{\varepsilon\lfloor(1+\varepsilon)^{k}\rfloor})_{1}
-set (in Definition
1.4
a
(\delta,\alpha;C)_{1}
-set must be contained in
[0,1]
, but it is harmless to apply a translation); see i.e.
[
14
, Lemma 7.5]
for details on how to do this. This is the “twist” alluded to above; in
[
2
]
, Bourgain simply covers
\rho_{\theta}(A)
by unions of
2^{-k}
intervals; he does not ask that these unions satisfy the non-concentration condition (
1.5
).

For each
\theta\in\operatorname{supp}(\nu)
we have

1=\mathbb{P}(Z)=\mathbb{P}\big(Z\cap\rho_{\theta}^{-1}(\rho_{\theta}(Z)\big)=\mathbb{P}\Big(Z\cap\bigcup_{k\geq k_{0}}\rho_{\theta}^{-1}(G_{\theta,k})\Big).

Thus by integrating over
I
with respect to
\nu
we conclude that

\int_{I}\sum_{k\geq k_{0}}\mathbb{P}\big(Z\cap\rho_{\theta}^{-1}(G_{\theta,k}\big)\big)d\nu(\theta)\geq\nu(\Theta)=1.

In particular, there exists
k\geq k_{0}
so that

(2.7)

\int_{I}\mathbb{P}\big(Z\cap\rho_{\theta}^{-1}(G_{\theta,k})\big)d\nu(\theta)\gtrsim\frac{1}{k^{2}}.

We will fix this choice of
k
, and for the remiander of this proof, define
\delta=2^{-\lfloor(1+\varepsilon)^{k}\rfloor}
and
G_{\theta}=G_{\theta,k}
; thus the right hand side of (
2.7
) becomes essentially
\left(\frac{\log(1+\varepsilon)}{\log\log(1/\delta)}\right)^{2}
, which is
\geq|\log\delta|^{-2}
if
\delta
is sufficiently small relative to
\varepsilon
.

Recalling the notation introduced in Section
1.4
, we will write
A\gtrapprox B
to mean
A\gtrsim|\log\delta|^{-C}B
, where
C
is an absolute constant (in practice, we will always have
C\leq 100
).

2.2.
Replacing
\mathbb{P}
by a sum over curves

Given the choice of
\delta
above, let us consider a covering of
Z
using cubes of sidelength
\delta/2
. After dyadic pigeonholing, we can find a set
\mathcal{Q}
of such cubes and a “weight”
w
, so that
w\leq\mathbb{P}(Q)<2w
for each
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
, and

(2.8)

\int_{I}\mathbb{P}\Big(\bigcup_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}Q\cap\rho_{\theta}^{-1}(G_{\theta})\Big)d\nu(\theta)\gtrapprox 1.

Let
G_{\theta}^{\prime}=N_{\delta}(G_{\theta})
; here and henceforth,
N_{\delta}(X)
denotes the
\delta
-neighbourhood of the set
X
. Then
G_{\theta}^{\prime}
is a
(3\delta,s;3\delta^{-\varepsilon})_{1}
-set, and if
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
is a cube that intersects
\rho_{\theta}^{-1}(G_{\theta})
, then
Q\subset\rho_{\theta}^{-1}(G_{\theta}^{\prime})
. Thus (
2.8
) implies that

\int_{I}w\#\big\{Q\in\mathcal{Q}\colon Q\subset\rho_{\theta}^{-1}(G_{\theta}^{\prime})\big\}d\nu(\theta)\gtrapprox 1,

or

(2.9)

\int_{I}\#\big\{Q\in\mathcal{Q}\colon\rho_{\theta}(Q)\subset G_{\theta}^{\prime}\big\}d\nu(\theta)\gtrapprox w^{-1}.

Since
Q
is a cube of side-length
\delta/2
and
\gamma(\theta)\in S^{2}
, we have that
\rho_{\theta}(Q)
is an interval in
\mathbb{R}
of length
\sim\delta
. Thus
\int_{G_{\theta}^{\prime}}{\bf 1}_{\rho_{\theta}(Q)}(y)dy\sim\delta
for each
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
with
\rho_{\theta}(Q)\subset G_{\theta}^{\prime}
. Hence (
2.9
) implies that

(2.10)

\int_{I}\int_{G_{\theta}^{\prime}}\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}{\bf 1}_{\rho_{\theta}(Q)}(y)dyd\nu(\theta)\gtrapprox\delta w^{-1}.

Note that
\delta^{3}\lesssim w\lesssim\delta^{\zeta-\varepsilon}
, and
1\lessapprox w\#\mathcal{Q}\leq 1
. Let
A=\delta^{\zeta-\varepsilon}w^{-1}\gtrsim 1
, so that
\#\mathcal{Q}\gtrapprox A\delta^{-\zeta+\varepsilon}
and
w=\delta^{\zeta-\varepsilon}A^{-1}
. Thus the right hand side of (
2.10
) becomes
A\delta^{1-\zeta+\varepsilon}
.

Let
Z_{\delta}^{\prime}
denotes the set of centers of the cubes in
\mathcal{Q}
; then the Frostman condition (
2.5
) implies that

(2.11)

\#(Z_{\delta}^{\prime}\cap B)\lessapprox w^{-1}\mathbb{P}(B)\lesssim w^{-1}r^{\zeta-\varepsilon}=A(r/\delta)^{\zeta-\varepsilon}\qquad\textrm{for all balls}\ B\ \textrm{of radius}\ r\geq\delta.

Note that if
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
has center
z\in Z_{\delta}^{\prime}
, then
Q\subset N_{\delta}(z)
(this is why we required that the cubes in
\mathcal{Q}
have side-length
\delta/2
, rather than
\delta
). Thus for each
\theta\in I
, we have the implication

y\in\rho_{\theta}(Q)\implies|\gamma(\theta)\cdot z-y|\leq\delta.

This leads to the inequality

(2.12)

\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}{\bf 1}_{\rho_{\theta}(Q)}(y)\leq\sum_{z\in Z_{\delta}^{\prime}}{\bf 1}_{\Gamma^{\delta}_{z}}(\theta,y).

Combining (
2.10
) and (
2.12
), we obtain

(2.13)

\int_{I}\int_{G_{\theta}^{\prime}}\sum_{z\in Z_{\delta}^{\prime}}{\bf 1}_{\Gamma^{\delta}_{z}}(\theta,y)dyd\nu(\theta)\gtrapprox A\delta^{1-\zeta+\varepsilon}.

Let
Z_{\delta}
be a subset of
Z^{\prime}_{\delta}
obtained by randomly and independently selecting each point of
Z^{\prime}_{\delta}
with probability
p=A^{-1}
; we will select the set
Z_{\delta}
so that the following properties hold.

(2.14)

\#(Z_{\delta}\cap B)\leq|\log\delta|A^{-1}\#(Z_{\delta}^{\prime}\cap B)\leq\delta^{-\varepsilon}(r/\delta)^{\zeta}\qquad\textrm{for all balls}\ B\ \textrm{of radius}\ r\geq\delta,

and

(2.15)

\int_{I}\int_{G_{\theta}^{\prime}}\sum_{z\in Z_{\delta}}{\bf 1}_{\Gamma^{\delta}_{z}}(\theta,y)dyd\nu(\theta)\gtrapprox\delta^{1-\zeta+\varepsilon}.

Let us briefly discuss the selection of
Z_{\delta}
. Apriori, (
2.14
) appears to be an assertion about an uncountable set of balls
\{B\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}\colon\operatorname{radius}(B)\geq\delta\}
. However, it suffices to establish (
2.14
) (with the RHS replaced by
\frac{1}{2}\delta^{-\varepsilon}(r/\delta)^{\zeta}
) for a set of roughly
\delta^{-3}
balls: For each
j=0,\dots,|\log\delta|
, let
\mathcal{B}_{j}
be a set of about
(2^{j}\delta)^{-3}
balls of radius
2^{j+1}\delta
, so that every ball of radius
2^{j}\delta
is contained in at most
O(1)
balls from
\mathcal{B}_{j}
. Then it suffices to prove (
2.14
) for the balls in
\mathcal{B}=\bigcup_{j=0}^{|\log\delta|}\mathcal{B}_{j}
; this set has cardinality
\lesssim\delta^{-3}
. If
\delta
is small enough,
then using Chernoff’s inequality and (
2.11
) we can ensure that the probability that (
2.14
) fails for any particular ball in
\mathcal{B}
is smaller than
\delta^{4}
. Applying the union bound over
B\in\mathcal{B}
, we deduce that with probability at least
1-O(\delta)
, the estimate (
2.14
) holds for all balls
B\in\mathcal{B}
and hence in general for all balls of radius
r\geq\delta
. Choosing such an outcome with the additional requirement (also derivable from Chernoff’s inequality) that

\#Z_{\delta}>\frac{1}{100A}\#Z_{\delta}^{\prime},

the property (
2.15
) follows from (
2.13
) and an expected value calculation (expectation is linear) and Markov’s inequality.

2.3.
Replacing
\nu
by an integral over a quasi-product

Let
\mathcal{J}_{0}
be the set of intervals in
I
(recall
I
is the domain of
\gamma(\theta)
) of the form
[n\delta,(n+1)\delta)
, for
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. For each
J\in\mathcal{J}_{0}
, let
\theta(J)\in J
be a value of
\theta
that gets within a factor of 2 of maximizing the quantity

\int_{G_{\theta}^{\prime}}\sum_{z\in Z_{\delta}}{\bf 1}_{\Gamma^{\delta}_{z}}(\theta,y)dy.

For each
J\in\mathcal{J}_{0},
we have

(2.16)

\begin{split}\nu(J)\int_{G_{\theta(J)}^{\prime}}\sum_{z\in Z_{\delta}}{\bf 1}_{\Gamma^{\delta}_{z}}(\theta(J),y)dy&=\int_{J}\int_{G_{\theta(J)}^{\prime}}\sum_{z\in Z_{\delta}}{\bf 1}_{\Gamma^{\delta}_{z}}(\theta(J),y)dyd\nu(\theta)\\
&\geq\frac{1}{2}\int_{J}\int_{G_{\theta}^{\prime}}\sum_{z\in Z_{\delta}}{\bf 1}_{\Gamma^{\delta}_{z}}(\theta,y)dyd\nu(\theta).\end{split}

The first equality is just the statement that
\nu(J)=\int_{J}d\nu(\theta)
, while the second inequality follows from the definition of
\Theta(J)
.
To simplify notion, define

K(J)=\int_{G_{\theta(J)}^{\prime}}\sum_{z\in Z_{\delta}}{\bf 1}_{\Gamma^{\delta}_{z}}(\theta(J),y)dy,

so the left hand side of (
2.16
) is
\nu(J)K(J)
.
Summing (
2.16
) over
J\in\mathcal{J}_{0}
, we obtain

\begin{split}\sum_{J\in\mathcal{J}_{0}}\nu(J)K(J)&\geq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{J\in\mathcal{J}_{0}}\int_{J}\int_{G_{\theta}^{\prime}}\sum_{z\in Z_{\delta}}{\bf 1}_{\Gamma^{\delta}_{z}}(\theta,y)dyd\nu(\theta)\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\int_{I}\int_{G_{\theta}^{\prime}}\sum_{z\in Z_{\delta}}{\bf 1}_{\Gamma^{\delta}_{z}}(\theta,y)dyd\nu(\theta)\\
&\gtrapprox\delta^{1-\zeta+\varepsilon}.\end{split}

Observe that
K(J)\lesssim\delta\#Z_{\delta}\lessapprox\delta^{1-\zeta+\varepsilon}
for each
J\in\mathcal{J}_{0}
, and
\sum_{J\in\mathcal{J}_{0}}\nu(J)=1
. Thus by dyadic pigeonholing, there is a set
\mathcal{J}_{1}\subset\mathcal{J}_{0}
and a weight
\delta\lesssim w^{\prime}\leq\delta^{\alpha-\varepsilon}
so that
w^{\prime}\leq\nu(J)<2w^{\prime}
for each
J\in\mathcal{J}_{1}
, and
1\lessapprox w^{\prime}\#\mathcal{J}_{1}\leq 1
. We can write
A^{\prime}=\delta^{\alpha-\varepsilon}w^{\prime-1}\gtrsim 1
, and thus
\#\mathcal{J}_{1}\approx A^{\prime}\delta^{-\alpha+\varepsilon}
, and we have

\sum_{J\in\mathcal{J}_{1}}\delta^{\alpha-\varepsilon}(A^{\prime})^{-1}K(J)\gtrapprox\delta^{1-\zeta+\varepsilon},

i.e.

\sum_{J\in\mathcal{J}_{1}}K(J)\gtrapprox A^{\prime}\delta^{1-\zeta-\alpha+2\varepsilon}.

We will now perform a random sampling argument analogous to how we selected
Z_{\delta}\subset Z_{\delta}^{\prime}
at the end of Section
2.2
. Let
p=(A^{\prime})^{-1}
, and select
\mathcal{J}\subset\mathcal{J}_{1}
by randomly and independently choosing each
J\in\mathcal{J}_{1}
with probability
p
. By (
2.6
), with positive probability, we may choose
\mathcal{J}
so that the following two properties hold.

(2.17)

|\Lambda\cap\bigcup_{J\in\mathcal{J}}J|\leq\delta|\log\delta|(|\Lambda|/\delta)^{\alpha-\varepsilon}\leq\delta^{1-\varepsilon}(|\Lambda|/\delta)^{\alpha-\varepsilon}\qquad\textrm{for all intervals}\ \Lambda,

(i.e.
\bigcup_{J\in\mathcal{J}^{\prime\prime}}J
is a
(\delta,\alpha-\varepsilon;\delta^{-\varepsilon})_{1}
-set), and

(2.18)

\sum_{J\in\mathcal{J}}K(J)\gtrapprox\delta^{1-\zeta-\alpha+2\varepsilon}.

(
2.17
) and (
2.18
) are the analogues of (
2.14
) and (
2.15
), respectively, and the argument is the same.

Next, we claim that if
(\theta,y)\in\Gamma^{\delta}_{z}
, and if
|\theta-\theta^{\prime}|\leq\delta
, then
\theta^{\prime}\in\Gamma^{L\delta}_{z}
, where

L=\sup_{z\in Z_{\delta}}\sup_{\theta\in I}\left|\frac{d}{d\theta}\rho_{\theta}(z)\right|\lesssim 1.

Thus for each
z\in Z_{\delta}
, each
y\in\mathbb{R}
, and each
J\in\mathcal{J}
, we have

(2.19)

\int_{J}{\bf 1}_{\Gamma^{\delta}_{z}}(\theta(J),y)d\theta\leq\int_{J}{\bf 1}_{\Gamma^{L\delta}_{z}}(\theta,y)d\theta.

Integrating (
2.19
) over
G_{\theta(J)}^{\prime}
and summing over
z\in Z_{\delta}
(and interchanging the order of integration and summation), we obtain

\begin{split}\delta K(J)=&\int_{J}\int_{G_{\theta(J)}^{\prime}}\sum_{z\in Z_{\delta}}{\bf 1}_{\Gamma^{\delta}_{z}}(\theta(J),y)dyd\theta\\
&\leq\int_{J}\int_{G_{\theta(J)}^{\prime}}\sum_{z\in Z_{\delta}}{\bf 1}_{\Gamma^{L\delta}_{z}}(\theta,y)dyd\theta.\end{split}

Combining this with (
2.18
), we conclude that

(2.20)

\begin{split}\delta^{2-\zeta-\alpha+2\varepsilon}&\lessapprox\sum_{J\in\mathcal{J}}\delta K(J)\\
&\leq\sum_{J\in\mathcal{J}^{\prime\prime}}\int_{J}\int_{G_{\theta(J)}^{\prime}}\sum_{z\in Z_{\delta}}{\bf 1}_{\Gamma^{L\delta}_{z}}(\theta,y)dyd\theta.\end{split}

Finally, define

E=\bigcup_{J\in\mathcal{J}}J\times G_{\theta(J)}^{\prime}.

Then
E
is a
(\delta,\alpha-\varepsilon;\delta^{-\varepsilon})_{1}\times(3\delta,s;3\delta^{-\varepsilon})_{1}
quasi-product, and (
2.20
) can be re-written as

(2.21)

\int_{E}\sum_{z\in Z_{\delta}}{\bf 1}_{\Gamma^{L\delta}_{z}}\gtrapprox\delta^{2-\zeta-\alpha+2\varepsilon}.

Since

|E|\lesssim(\delta^{1-\alpha+\varepsilon}\delta^{-\varepsilon})\times\bigl((3\delta)^{1-s}3\delta^{-\varepsilon}\bigr)\sim\delta^{2-\alpha-s-2\varepsilon},

the relation (
2.21
) together with Hölder’s inequality implies that

(2.22)

\int_{E}\Big(\sum_{z\in Z_{\delta}}{\bf 1}_{\Gamma^{L\delta}_{z}}\Big)^{3/2}\gtrapprox\delta^{2+\frac{1}{2}s-\alpha-\frac{3}{2}\zeta+4\varepsilon}.

2.4.
Applying Proposition
2.1

Since
E
is a
(\delta,\alpha-\varepsilon;\delta^{-\varepsilon})_{1}\times(3\delta,s;3\delta^{-\varepsilon})_{1}
quasi-product and
s<\alpha
,
E
can be expressed as a union of
O(1)
many
(\delta,\alpha,\delta^{-\varepsilon})_{1}\times(\delta,\alpha,\delta^{-\varepsilon})_{1}
quasi-products.
Thus we can apply Proposition
2.1
with
\#Z_{\delta}\lessapprox\delta^{-\zeta-\varepsilon}
and use the triangle inequality to conclude that

(2.23)

\int_{E}\Big(\sum_{z\in Z_{\delta}}{\bf 1}_{\Gamma^{L\delta}_{z}}\Big)^{3/2}\lessapprox\delta^{2-\frac{1}{2}\alpha-\frac{3}{2}\zeta-C\varepsilon-\varepsilon}.

Comparing (
2.22
) and (
2.23
), we conclude that
\delta^{s-\alpha}\lessapprox\delta^{-(2C+10)\varepsilon}
. By selecting
\delta>0
sufficiently small (depending on
\varepsilon
), we conclude that
\alpha-s\leq(2C+10)\varepsilon
. But comparing with (
2.4
), we obtain a contradiction. This finishes the proof of Theorem
1.3
.

3.
The geometry of cinematic functions

In this section, we will explore some geometric properties of families of cinematic functions. Many of the results in this section are inspired by analogous results in
[
13
,
17
,
29
]
. For the definitions that follow, we will fix a family of cinematic functions
\mathcal{F}
over a compact interval
J
, with cinematic constant
K
and doubling constant
D
.

For
f\in\mathcal{F}
, we write
f^{\lambda}
to denote the closed
\lambda
-vertical neighborhood of the graph of
f
, i.e. the set

\{(\theta,y)\in J\times\mathbb{R}\colon|f(\theta)-y|\leq\lambda\}.

This definition differs slightly from the definition of
f^{\delta}
given in the statement of Theorem
1.7
. This distinction does not matter, however, since
f^{\lambda}
is comparable to the
\lambda
-neighborhood of the graph of
f
.

Definition 3.1

(Curvilinear rectangles)
.

Let
0<\delta\leq 1,

\delta\leq t\leq 1
, and
f\in\mathcal{F}
. A
(\delta,t)
-rectangle is defined to be a set of the form

f^{\delta}(I)=\{(\theta,y)\in I\times\mathbb{R}\colon|y-f(\theta)|\leq\delta\},

where
I\subset J
is an interval of length
\sqrt{\delta/t}
.

Definition 3.2

(Comparability)
.

We say two
(\delta,t)
-rectangles
R,R^{\prime}
are
\lambda
-comparable, if there is another
(\lambda\delta,t)
-rectangle
R^{\prime\prime}
that contains
R\cup R^{\prime}
. Otherwise we say
R,R^{\prime}
are
\lambda
-incomparable.

Definition 3.3

(Tangency)
.

We say a
(\delta,t)
-rectangle
R
is
\lambda
-tangent to the function
f\in\mathcal{F}
(or
f
is
\lambda
-tangent to
R
) if
R\subset f^{\lambda\delta}
. In most cases we take
\lambda=5
, and we will simply refer to this as
R
is tangent to
f
.

Definition 3.4

(Separation)
.

Let
\mathcal{W},\mathcal{B}\subset\mathcal{F}
. For
t>0
, we say
(\mathcal{W},\mathcal{B})
is
t
-separated if
\|w-b\|\geq t
for all
w\in\mathcal{W}
,
b\in\mathcal{B}
.

3.1.
Preliminary reductions

We start with the following lemma, which is similar to
[
13
, Lemma 3.1]
.

Lemma 3.5
.

If
I\subset J
is an interval of length
|I|\leq(6K)^{-1}
,
f,g\in\mathcal{F}
, and
k
is either of the functions
f-g
or
f^{\prime}-g^{\prime}
, then either of the following alternatives hold (depending on the choice of
I
and
k
):

(S)

|k(\theta)|<(3K)^{-1}\|f-g\|
for all
\theta\in I
.

(L)

|k(\theta)|\geq(6K)^{-1}\|f-g\|
for all
\theta\in I
.

Moreover, if for
I
the alternative (S) holds for both
k=f-g
and
k=f^{\prime}-g^{\prime}
, then the alternative (L) must hold for
k=f^{\prime\prime}-g^{\prime\prime}
.

Proof.

Let
\overline{B}
be the closed unit ball of
C^{2}(J)
. Then if
|\theta-\theta^{\prime}|<(6K)^{-1}
and
h\in\overline{B}
, we have
|h(\theta)-h(\theta^{\prime})|<(6K)^{-1}
and
|h^{\prime}(\theta)-h^{\prime}(\theta^{\prime})|<(6K)^{-1}
.

Next, fix
I\subset J
with
|I|<(6K)^{-1}
, and suppose that the alternative (L) fails for
k=f-g
. So there is
\theta_{0}\in I
such that
|k(\theta_{0})|<(6K)^{-1}\lVert f-g\rVert
. Then, if
\theta\in I
is arbitrary, we have
|\theta-\theta_{0}|\leq(6K)^{-1}
, and so if we define
l=k/\lVert f-g\rVert\in\overline{B}(0,1)
, then

\displaystyle\frac{|k(\theta)|}{\lVert f-g\rVert}\leq\frac{|k(\theta_{0})|}{\lVert f-g\rVert}+\left|\frac{k(\theta)}{\lVert f-g\rVert}-\frac{k(\theta_{0})}{\lVert f-g\rVert}\right|<\frac{1}{6K}+|l(\theta)-l(\theta_{0})|<\frac{1}{3K}.

Thus the alternative (S) holds for
k
. The proof of the case
k=f^{\prime}-g^{\prime}
is the same. The last assertion follows from the cinematic curvature assumption (
1.6
).
∎

To prove Theorem
1.7
, it suffices to prove (
1.8
) for each individual subinterval
I
of length
\leq(6K)^{-1}
, since the restriction of the functions in
\mathcal{F}
to each such sub-interval
I
is still family of cinematic functions with cinematic constant
K
and doubling constant
D
. By an abuse of notation, we continue to write
I=J
, and assume without loss of generality that
|J|\leq(6K)^{-1}
.

The following lemma is an analogue of
[
13
, Lemma 3.2]
.

Lemma 3.6
.

For distinct
f,g\in\mathcal{F}
, the map
\theta\mapsto f(\theta)-g(\theta)
has at most two zeros in
J
. Moreover, if
\theta\mapsto f^{\prime}(\theta)-g^{\prime}(\theta)
has two zeros in
J
, then the alternative (L) holds for
\theta\mapsto f(\theta)-g(\theta)
.

Proof.

We start with the second claim. Assume
\theta\mapsto f^{\prime}(\theta)-g^{\prime}(\theta)
has two zeros in
J
. This implies, by Rolle’s theorem, that
\theta\mapsto f^{\prime\prime}(\theta)-g^{\prime\prime}(\theta)
has a zero in
J
, so in particular the alternative (L) cannot hold for
\theta\mapsto f^{\prime\prime}(\theta)-g^{\prime\prime}(\theta)
. Now Lemma
3.5
implies that the alternative (S) holds for
J
and
\theta\mapsto f^{\prime}(\theta)-g^{\prime}(\theta)
. Thus by the last sentence of Lemma
3.5
, we must have that (L) holds for
\theta\mapsto f(\theta)-g(\theta)
.

The first claim follows from the second one: if
\theta\mapsto f(\theta)-g(\theta)
had three zeros in
J
, then
\theta\mapsto f^{\prime}(\theta)-g^{\prime}(\theta)
would have two zeros in
J
again by the Rolle’s theorem. But then, by the second claim,
\theta\mapsto f(\theta)-g(\theta)
satisfies the alternative (L) in
J
, and hence cannot have zeros in
J
.
∎

3.2.
The tangency parameter
\Delta

Next, we shall define a quantity that measures how close two functions
f,g\in\mathcal{F}
are to being tangent. Our definition is similar to the quantity (3.1) from
[
13
]
, which in turn was inspired by
[
17
,
29
]
.

Definition 3.7
.

For
f,g\in\mathcal{F}
, we define

(3.1)

\Delta(f,g)=\min_{\theta\in J/2}|f(\theta)-g(\theta)|+|f^{\prime}(\theta)-g^{\prime}(\theta)|,

where
J/2
is the interval of length
|J|/2
with the same midpoint as
J
.

In particular, we have

(3.2)

\Delta(f,g)\leq\|f-g\|.

\Delta(f,g)
measures the tangency between the graphs of functions
f,g
over the interval
J/2
. If
\Delta(f,g)=0
, then
f
and
g
are tangent at some point
\theta\in J/2
. Note that
\Delta(f,g)
is a pseudo-metric, since it is symmetric and satisfies the triangle inequality. The restriction to
J/2
but not
J
is needed for technical reasons, as is also the case of
[
17
]
and
[
13
]
.

The next proposition is a close cousin of
[
17
, Lemma 3.3]
. It describes the key geometric information about cinematic functions.

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
f,g\in\mathcal{F}
, and define
h=f-g
,
t=\|f-g\|
, and
\Delta=\Delta(f,g)
.

(1)

There exists
c_{1}=c_{1}(K)>0
so that if
\Delta\leq c_{1}t
, then the following are true.

(a)

There is a unique
\theta_{0}\in\frac{3}{5}J
such that
h^{\prime}(\theta_{0})=0
. We also have
|h(\theta_{0})|\lesssim\Delta
.

(b)

We have
|h(\theta)|\gtrsim t>0
for every
\theta\in J\backslash(\frac{4}{5}J)
. If in addition
h
changes sign in
J
, then
h
has exactly 2 zeros
\theta_{1}<\theta_{2}
, both within
\frac{4}{5}J
, such that
|\theta_{i}-\theta_{0}|\lesssim\sqrt{\Delta/t}
,
i=1,2
.

(2)

For each
0<\delta\leq t/(6K)
, the set

(3.3)

E_{\delta}=\{\theta\in J/4:|h(\theta)|\leq\delta\}

satisfies the following:

(a)

E_{\delta}
is contained in an interval of length
O(\sqrt{(\Delta+\delta)/t})
.

(b)

E_{\delta}
is either a closed interval of length
O(\delta/\sqrt{(\Delta+\delta)t})
or the union of two closed intervals of length
O(\delta/\sqrt{(\Delta+\delta)t})
.

(c)

If in addition there is some
\tilde{\theta}\in J/4
such that
|h(\tilde{\theta})|\leq\delta/2
, and
I
is the interval constituting
E_{\delta}
that contains
\tilde{\theta}
, then
|I|\gtrsim\delta/\sqrt{(\Delta+\delta)t}
.

Proof.

Let
\theta_{\Delta}\in J/2
be such that

\Delta=|h(\theta_{\Delta})|+|h^{\prime}(\theta_{\Delta})|.

Thus
|h(\theta_{\Delta})|\leq\Delta
and
|h^{\prime}(\theta_{\Delta})|\leq\Delta
.

We first prove Part (
1
). If we choose
c_{1}<(3K)^{-1}
, then using the definition of
\Delta(f,g)
, we have the alternative (S) holds for both
h,h^{\prime}
. Thus the alternative (L) must hold for
h^{\prime\prime}
. Without loss of generality we may assume
h^{\prime\prime}(\theta)\gtrsim t
.

For Part (
1a
), using
h^{\prime\prime}(\theta)\gtrsim t
, if
c_{1}
is chosen to be small enough, then there is a unique
\theta_{0}\in\big(\frac{1}{2}+O(\Delta/t)\big)J\subset\frac{3}{5}J
with
h^{\prime}(\theta_{0})=0
. Also,
|\theta_{0}-\theta_{\Delta}|\lesssim\Delta/t
, and thus

\displaystyle|h(\theta_{0})-h(\theta_{\Delta})|\lesssim t|\theta_{0}-\theta_{\Delta}|\lesssim\Delta.

Since
|h(\theta_{\Delta})|\leq\Delta
, we also have
|h(\theta_{0})|\lesssim\Delta
.

For Part (
1b
), write
J=[a,b]
. Using
|h(\theta_{0})|\lesssim\Delta
, for any
\theta\in[\theta_{0},b]
we have

\displaystyle|h(\theta)|

\displaystyle\geq\left|\int_{\theta_{0}}^{\theta}h^{\prime}(s)ds\right|-C\Delta=\int_{\theta_{0}}^{\theta}\left|h^{\prime}(s)\right|ds-C\Delta

(3.4)

\displaystyle=\int_{\theta_{0}}^{\theta}\int_{\theta_{0}}^{s}h^{\prime\prime}(u)duds-C\Delta\geq ct(\theta-\theta_{0})^{2}-C\Delta.

Here
C,c
are absolute constants. The same is true for
\theta\in[a,\theta_{0}]
. If
c_{1}
is small enough, we then have
|h(\theta)|\gtrsim t
for every
\theta\in J\backslash(\frac{4}{5}J)
, since
\theta_{0}\in\frac{3}{5}J
and
|J|\sim 1
. The statements about the zeros follow from the intermediate value theorem and a similar application of (
3.4
).

Now we come to Part (
2
). First, for Part (
2a
), if
\Delta>c_{1}t
then the result is trivial. If
\Delta\leq c_{1}t
, then the argument of Part (
1
) applies. Let
\alpha=\inf E_{\delta}
and
\beta=\sup E_{\delta}
, and hence
E_{\delta}\subset[\alpha,\beta]
. Using (
3.4
) we have
\beta-\theta_{0}\leq C\sqrt{(\Delta+\delta)/t}
and
\theta_{0}-\alpha\leq C\sqrt{(\Delta+\delta)/t}
. Thus the result follows.

For Part (
2b
), we first note that the alternative (L) cannot hold for
h
, otherwise it will contradict the assumption
\delta<t/(6K)
. Thus (L) holds for
h^{\prime}
or
h^{\prime\prime}
. In either case, we see
E_{\delta}
is a closed interval or the union of two closed intervals.

For the length estimate, we first consider the easier case
\Delta>c_{1}t
. In this case, if
\delta>c_{1}t/2
, then the result is trivial since then
\delta\sim\Delta\sim t
. If
\delta\leq c_{1}t/2
, then for
\theta\in E_{\delta}
we have

c_{1}t<\Delta\leq\min_{\theta\in E_{\delta}}\big(|h(\theta)|+|h^{\prime}(\theta)|\big)\leq\delta+\min_{\theta\in E_{\delta}}|h^{\prime}(\theta)|,

and thus
|h^{\prime}(\theta)|\gtrsim t
on
E_{\delta}
, and thus each interval constituting
E_{\delta}
has length
\lesssim\delta/t=\delta/\sqrt{(\Delta+\delta)t}
.

For the harder case
\Delta\leq c_{1}t
, the argument of Part (
1
) is applicable. We also have two subcases. First, if
\delta\geq c_{1}\Delta
, then the result follows from Part (
2a
), since then
\sqrt{(\Delta+\delta)/t}\sim\delta/\sqrt{(\Delta+\delta)t}
.

If
\delta<c_{1}\Delta
, then we may further assume
\theta_{0}\in J/2
. Otherwise, using (
3.4
) we have
h(\theta)\gtrsim t
for every
\theta\in J/4
if
c_{1}
is small enough. In particular,
E_{\delta}=\varnothing
and we have nothing to prove. Thus we have
\theta_{0}\in J/2
.

But by the definition of
\Delta
and
\theta_{0}
, we have
|h(\theta_{0})|=|h(\theta_{0})|+|h^{\prime}(\theta_{0})|\geq\Delta
. Thus for every
\theta\in E_{\delta}\cap[\theta_{0},b]
, we have

\displaystyle\delta

\displaystyle\geq|h(\theta)|\geq\Delta-\int_{\theta_{0}}^{\theta}|h^{\prime}(s)|ds

\displaystyle\geq\Delta-\int_{\theta_{0}}^{\theta}\int_{\theta_{0}}^{s}|h^{\prime\prime}(u)|duds\geq\Delta-C^{\prime}t(\theta-\theta_{0})^{2}.

Here
C^{\prime}
is an absolute constant. For
\theta\in E_{\delta}\cap[a,\theta_{0}]
the argument is the same. This implies that

(3.5)

|\theta-\theta_{0}|\gtrsim\sqrt{\Delta/t},\quad\forall\theta\in E_{\delta}.

Since
h^{\prime\prime}(\theta)\gtrsim t
in
J
, this further implies that for
\theta\in E_{\delta}

(3.6)

|h^{\prime}(\theta)|=|h^{\prime}(\theta)-h^{\prime}(\theta_{0})|\gtrsim t|\theta-\theta_{0}|\gtrsim\sqrt{t\Delta}.

Thus each interval constituting
E_{\delta}
has length
O(\delta/\sqrt{t\Delta})=O(\delta/\sqrt{(\Delta+\delta)t})
.

Now we come to Part (
2c
). Let
I
be the interval constituting
E_{\delta}
that contains
\tilde{\theta}
. If
I=J/4
then the result is trivial, so we assume
I\neq J/4
. In this case, there exists an endpoint
\theta_{I}
of
I
so that
|h(\theta_{I})|=\delta
.

If
\Delta>c_{1}t
, then we have

\delta/2\leq|h(\theta_{I})|-|h(\tilde{\theta})|\lesssim|\theta_{I}-\tilde{\theta}|t,

and so
|\theta_{I}-\tilde{\theta}|\gtrsim\delta/t\sim\delta/\sqrt{(\Delta+\delta)t}
.

If
\Delta\leq c_{1}t
, then the argument of Part (
1
) is applicable. For every
\theta\in E_{\delta}
, the argument of Part (
2a
) implies that
|\theta-\theta_{0}|\lesssim\sqrt{(\Delta+\delta)/t}
. Thus

|h^{\prime}(\theta)|=|h^{\prime}(\theta)-h^{\prime}(\theta_{0})|\lesssim t|\theta-\theta_{0}|\lesssim\sqrt{(\Delta+\delta)t},\quad\forall\theta\in E_{\delta}.

Hence

\delta/2\leq|h(\theta_{I})|-|h(\tilde{\theta})|\lesssim|\theta_{I}-\tilde{\theta}|\sqrt{(\Delta+\delta)t},

which implies that
|\theta_{I}-\tilde{\theta}|\gtrsim\delta/\sqrt{(\Delta+\delta)t}
. In particular,
|I|\gtrsim\delta/\sqrt{(\Delta+\delta)t}
.
∎

3.3.
Further geometric estimates

We conclude this section with some simple arguments that relate the quantities
\|f-g\|
,
\Delta(f,g)
, the volume of
f^{\delta}\cap g^{\delta}
, and the number of tangency rectangles associated to the intersection.

Lemma 3.9
.

Let
f,g\in\mathcal{F}
and let
t>0
. If there is a
(\delta,t)
-rectangle
R
over the interval
J/4
(i.e. the interval
I
from Definition
3.1
is contained in
J/4
) that is tangent to both
f
and
g
, then
(\Delta(f,g)+\delta)\|f-g\|\lesssim\delta t
.

Proof.

Let
R
be a
(\delta,t)
-rectangle over an interval
I\subset J/4
. By Definition
3.3
and the triangle inequality, for each
\theta\in I
we have
|f(\theta)-g(\theta)|\leq 10\lambda\delta
. Since
I\subset J/4
, by Part (
2b
) of Lemma
3.8
, we have

\sqrt{\delta/t}=|I|\lesssim\frac{\delta}{\sqrt{(\Delta(f,g)+\delta)\|f-g\|}}.\qed

Lemma 3.10
.

Let
I\subset J/4
be an interval, let
\lambda\geq 1
, let
f,g\in\mathcal{F},
and let
0<\delta\leq\|f-g\|/(6K)
. If
|f(\theta)-g(\theta)|\leq\delta
on
I
, then
|f(\theta)-g(\theta)|\lesssim\lambda^{2}\delta
on
\lambda I\cap J
.

Proof.

Let
k=f-g
, let
t=\|f-g\|
, and let
\Delta=\Delta(f,g)
. Since
I\subset J/4
, by Part (
2b
) of Lemma
3.8
we have
|I|\lesssim\delta/\sqrt{(\Delta+\delta)t}\leq\sqrt{\delta/t}
.

We will first show that
|k^{\prime}(\theta)|\leq C\lambda\delta|I|^{-1}
on
\lambda I\cap J
, for some
C\lesssim 1
. Suppose not. Then there is some
\theta_{1}\in\lambda I\cap J
with
|k^{\prime}(\theta_{1})|>C\lambda\delta|I|^{-1}
. We have
|k^{\prime\prime}(\theta)|\leq t
in
J
. Since
|I|\lesssim\sqrt{\delta/t}
, for any
\theta\in\lambda I\cap J
we have

|k^{\prime}(\theta)|\geq C\lambda\delta|I|^{-1}-\lambda t|I|\geq C\lambda\delta|I|^{-1}/2,

provided
C\sim 1
is chosen appropriately. But since
k\in C^{2}(I)
and
\lambda>1
, if
C>4
then it is impossible for
|k^{\prime}(\theta)|\geq C\lambda\delta|I|^{-1}/2
and
|k(\theta)|\leq\delta
to simultaneously hold for all
\theta\in I
. Hence
|k^{\prime}(\theta)|\lesssim\lambda\delta|I|^{-1}
on
\lambda I\cap J
. By the triangle inequality, we thus have
|k(\theta)|\lesssim\lambda^{2}\delta
on
\lambda I\cap J
.
∎

Definition 3.11
.

For
R=f^{\delta}(I)
where
I\subset J
, we define the
\lambda
-dilation of
R
to be

(3.7)

\lambda R=f^{\lambda\delta}(\lambda I\cap J).

We will record several technical lemmas related to comparability.

Lemma 3.12
.

Let
f,g\in\mathcal{F}
and
\lambda\geq 1
. Let
R=f^{\delta}(I)
and
R^{\prime}=g^{\delta}(I^{\prime})
be two
\lambda
-comparable
(\delta,t)
-rectangles over
J/4
. Let
I^{\prime\prime}
be the convex hull of
I\cup I^{\prime}
. Then
|I^{\prime\prime}|\leq\sqrt{\lambda\delta/t}
, and

(3.8)

|f(\theta)-g(\theta)|\lesssim\lambda^{3}\delta\quad\textrm{for all}\ \theta\in I^{\prime\prime}.

In particular,
R^{\prime}\subset C\lambda R
for some
C\sim 1
.

Proof.

By definition of comparability, there is some
(\lambda\delta,t)
-rectangle
R^{\prime\prime}=h^{\lambda\delta}(I_{0})
such that
R\cup R^{\prime}\subset R^{\prime\prime}
and
|I_{0}|=\sqrt{\lambda\delta/t}
. Since
I^{\prime\prime}\subset I_{0}
, we have
|I^{\prime\prime}|\leq\sqrt{\lambda\delta/t}
.

It remains to prove (
3.8
). If
\|f-g\|<12K\lambda\delta
then (
3.8
) is immediate and we are done.

Next, suppose
\|f-g\|\geq 12K\lambda\delta
. We have

|f(\theta)-h(\theta)|\leq\lambda\delta\quad\textrm{for all}\ \theta\in I.

Using Lemma
3.10
(with
2\lambda\delta
in place of
\delta
; note that
2\lambda\delta\leq\|f-g\|/(6K)
), we have

|f(\theta)-h(\theta)|\lesssim\lambda^{3}\delta\quad\textrm{for all}\ \theta\in I^{\prime\prime}.

The same is true with
f
replaced by
g
. Then the result follows from the triangle inequality.
∎

Corollary 3.13
.

Let
R_{1},R_{2},R_{3}
be
(\delta,t)
rectangles over
J/4
. Suppose that
R_{1}
is
\lambda_{1}
-comparable to
R_{2}
and
R_{2}
is
\lambda_{2}
-comparable to
R_{3}
. Then
R_{1}
is
\lambda_{3}
-comparable to
R_{3}
, for some
\lambda_{3}
depending on
K
,
\lambda_{1},
and
\lambda_{2}
.

Lemma 3.14
.

Let
0<c<1
. If
C
is large enough depending on
c
and
K
, then the following holds. Let
f,g\in\mathcal{F}
and
R=f^{\delta}(I)
and
R^{\prime}=g^{\delta}(I^{\prime})
be two
C
-incomparable
(\delta,t)
-rectangles over subintervals of
J/4
. For
0<c<1
, let
\tilde{R}=f^{\delta}(\tilde{I})
and
\tilde{R}^{\prime}=g^{\delta}(\tilde{I}^{\prime})
where
\tilde{I}=cI
,
\tilde{I}^{\prime}=cI^{\prime}
. Then
\tilde{R}
and
\tilde{R}^{\prime}
are
100
-incomparable.

Proof.

Suppose towards contradiction that
\tilde{R}
and
\tilde{R}^{\prime}
are
100
-comparable. Then

\mathrm{dist}(c_{I},c_{I^{\prime}})\leq\sqrt{100c\delta/t},

and thus

|f(\theta)-g(\theta)|\leq 100\delta

are
C

∎

Lemma 3.15
.

Let
\mathcal{R}
be a family of pairwise
100
-incomparable
(\delta,t)
-rectangles
R=f^{\delta}(I)
such that all
f\in\mathcal{F}
are contained in a ball
B\subset C^{2}(I)
of radius
3t
. Suppose on the other hand that all
R\in\mathcal{R}
are contained in a
(\lambda\delta,t)
-rectangle
\tilde{R}
where
\lambda\geq 100
. Then
\#\mathcal{R}\lesssim\lambda^{5/2}
.

Proof.

We will show that every point in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
can be contained in
\lesssim\lambda
many rectangles
R/2
where
R\in\mathcal{R}
. (Here
R/2:=f^{\delta}(I/2)
.) Then the lemma follows from the simple inequality

(\#\mathcal{R})\delta^{3/2}t^{-1/2}\lesssim\int_{\tilde{R}}\sum_{R\in\mathcal{R}}{\bf 1}_{R/2}\lesssim\lambda|\tilde{R}|\lesssim\lambda^{5/2}\delta^{3/2}t^{-1/2}.

Suppose there is some
(\theta_{0},y_{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
that is contained in
N
pairwise
100
-incomparable
(\delta,t)
-rectangles of the form
R_{i}/2
,
i=1,\ldots,N
. We want to show
N\lesssim\lambda
.

It suffices to show that for every pair
1\leq i\neq j\leq N
,

(3.9)

\displaystyle|f^{\prime}_{i}(\theta_{0})-f^{\prime}_{j}(\theta_{0})|\leq 10\lambda\sqrt{\delta t},

(3.10)

\displaystyle|f^{\prime}_{i}(\theta_{0})-f^{\prime}_{j}(\theta_{0})|>\sqrt{\delta t}.

Suppose
(\theta_{0},y_{0})\in(R_{i}/2)\cap(R_{j}/2)
. In particular,
\theta_{0}\in(I_{i}/2)\cap(I_{j}/2)
.

Denote

and

. Then
\theta_{0}\in I
,
\sqrt{\delta/t}\leq|I|\leq 2\sqrt{\delta/t}
and

.

For (

), suppose

. Then since

, we have in particular

, and thus for every

, we have

|h^{\prime}(\theta)|>10\lambda\sqrt{\delta t}-12t\sqrt{\delta/t}\geq 5\lambda\sqrt{\delta t},

since

. Since
|h(\theta_{0})|\leq\delta
, this implies that for some

contradicting the assumption that all rectangles

. This establishes (

).

For (

), we argue similarly. Suppose that
|h^{\prime}(\theta_{0})|\leq\sqrt{\delta t}
. Then using

, we have for every

Thus for every

we have

|h(\theta)|\leq|h(\theta_{0})|+26\sqrt{\delta t}\sqrt{\delta/t}\leq 100\delta,

contradicting the assumption that

are
100
-incomparable. This establishes (
3.10
).
∎

Lemma 3.16
.

Let
\mathcal{R}
be a finite family of pairwise
100
-incomparable
(\delta,t)
-rectangles
R=f^{\delta}(I)
such that all
f\in\mathcal{F}
are contained in a ball of radius
3t
. If
C\geq 100
, then there is a
C
-incomparable subcollection
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{R}
such that
\#\mathcal{R}^{\prime}\sim\#\mathcal{R}
.

Proof.

Start with
R_{1}\in\mathcal{R}
. Choose a maximally
C
-incomparable subcollection
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}

\#(F\cap B)\leq\delta^{-\varepsilon}(r/\delta)^{\zeta}\quad\textrm{for all balls}\ B\subset C^{2}(I)\ \textrm{of radius}\ r\geq\delta.

Let
J^{\prime}=\frac{1}{16}J
and let
E^{\prime}=E\cap(J^{\prime}\times\mathbb{R})
. Then

(5.1)

\int_{E^{\prime}}\Big(\sum_{f\in F}{\bf 1}_{f^{\delta}}\Big)^{3/2}\leq\delta^{2-\alpha/2-\zeta/2-C_{0}\varepsilon}(\#F),

where
C_{0}=C_{0}(D)>0
depends on
D
.

Using Lemma
5.1
we can prove Theorem
1.7
as follows. Let
J_{1},\ldots,J_{N}
,
N\sim|\log\delta|
be a sequence of sub-intervals of
J
, so that
J\backslash\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{16}J_{i}
consists of two intervals of length
\delta
(these intervals will be at the two endpoints of
J
). Note that if
J_{0}
is an interval of length
\delta
and if
E_{0}=E\cap(J_{0}\times\mathbb{R})
, then

(5.2)

\int_{E_{0}}\Big(\sum_{f\in F}{\bf 1}_{f^{\delta}}\Big)^{3/2}\lesssim\delta^{2}(\#F)^{3/2}\leq\delta^{2-\alpha/2-\zeta/2-C_{0}\varepsilon}(\#F).

For each index
i
, apply Lemma
5.1
to the restriction
\mathcal{F}|_{J_{i}}\subset C^{2}(J_{i})
; this is still a family of cinematic functions, with cinematic constant
K
and doubling constant
D
. Using the triangle inequality, we conclude that

\int_{E}\Big(\sum_{f\in F}{\bf 1}_{f^{\delta}}\Big)^{3/2}\lesssim|\log\delta|\delta^{2-\alpha/2-\zeta/2-C_{0}\varepsilon}(\#F).

Theorem
1.7
now follows, provided we select
C>C_{0}
and restrict
\delta>0
sufficiently small.

Next, we remark that when proving Lemma
5.1
, we may suppose that
F
is
\delta
-separated. This follows from the triangle inequality and observation that by (
1.7
) each
\delta
-ball contains at most
\delta^{-\varepsilon}
elements of
F
.

Finally, we claim that Lemma
5.1
follows from the following restricted weak-type estimate. For each
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, let
F(x)=\{f\in F\colon x\in f^{\delta}\}.
For each
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
\#F(x)\leq\#F\lesssim\delta^{-\varepsilon-\zeta}
. Thus by dyadic pigeonholing, there is a set
E_{0}\subset E
and an integer
\mu
so that
\mu\leq\#F(x)<2\mu
for all
x\in E_{0}
, and
\mu^{3/2}|E_{0}|\approx\textrm{LHS of}\ \eqref{eqn_L32Bd_generalEprime}
. Thus in order to prove Lemma
5.1
(and hence Theorem
1.7
), it suffices to establish the estimate

(5.3)

|E_{0}|\lessapprox\delta^{2-\frac{\alpha}{2}-\frac{\zeta}{2}-C_{1}\varepsilon}\#F\mu^{-3/2},

for some
C_{1}=C_{1}(D)
.

5.1.
Two ends reductions

In this section, we will find quantities called
t
and
\Delta
, so that for a typical point
x\in E_{0},
the functions in
F(x)
are localized to a ball in
C^{2}(J)
of radius
t
, and for a typical pair of functions
f,g\in F(x)
, we have
\Delta(f,g)\sim\Delta
. Our main tool will be the “two-ends” reduction. See
[
25
]
for an introduction to the topic.

5.1.1.
Finding the diameter of
F(x)

Since
F\subset C^{2}(J)
is bounded, so is the set
F(x)
for each
x\in E_{0}
. However, it is possible that
F(x)
(or a large fraction thereof) has diameter much smaller than
\operatorname{diam}(F)
for a typical
x\in E_{0}
. In this section, we will find the “true” diameter of
F(x)
for a typical
x\in E_{0}
.

For
t>0
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, define

(5.4)

n_{1}(t,x)=\sup_{g\in C^{2}(J)}\#F(x)\cap B(g,t),

where
B(g,t)\subset C^{2}(J)
is the ball centered at
g\in C^{2}(J)
of radius
t
. With this, we further define

(5.5)

n_{2}(x)=\sup_{t\in[\delta,K]}n_{1}(t,x)t^{-\varepsilon}.

Hence, given
x
, there is some
t(x)\in[\delta,K]
such that
n_{2}(x)\leq 2n_{1}(t(x),x)t(x)^{-\varepsilon}
. There is also some
g_{x}
such that

(5.6)

\#F(x)\cap B(g_{x},t(x))\geq n_{2}(t(x),x)/2,

so we have

(5.7)

n_{2}(x)\leq 4t(x)^{-\varepsilon}\#F(x)\cap B(g_{x},t(x)).

For simplicity we denote
B_{x}=B(g_{x},t(x))
.

Since
t(x)\in[\delta,K]
, by a dyadic pigeonholing, there is a Borel set
E_{1}\subset E_{0}
and a number
t\in[\delta,2K]
such that
t(x)\in(t/2,t]
for every
x\in E_{1}
, and
|E_{1}|\approx|E_{0}|
. It then suffices to show

(5.8)

|E_{1}|\lessapprox\delta^{2-\frac{\alpha}{2}-\frac{\zeta}{2}-C_{1}\varepsilon}\#F\mu^{-3/2}.

Since
\mathcal{F}
is doubling, we can cover it by finitely overlapping balls
B
of radius
t
, and denote

F_{B}=F\cap(3B),

so
\{F_{B}\}
covers
F
and is finitely overlapping. Also, let
E_{B}
be the set of
x\in E_{1}
such that
B_{x}
intersects
B
, and thus
\{E_{B}\}
is also finitely overlapping. Thus it suffices to show for each
B

(5.9)

|E_{B}|\lessapprox\delta^{2-\frac{\alpha}{2}-\frac{\zeta}{2}-C_{1}\varepsilon}\#F_{B}\mu^{-3/2}.

Now fix a ball
B
of radius
t
and let
x\in E_{B}
. By (
5.7
), (
5.5
) and (
5.4
), we have

\displaystyle\#F(x)\cap B_{x}\gtrsim t^{\varepsilon}n_{2}(x)\gtrsim t^{\varepsilon}n_{1}(K,x)=t^{\varepsilon}\#F(x)\sim t^{\varepsilon}\mu.

By another dyadic pigeonholing, we may find a subset
E^{\prime}_{B}\subset E_{B}
and a scale
\mu_{1}
with

(5.10)

t^{\varepsilon}\mu\lesssim\mu_{1}\leq\mu,

such that
|E^{\prime}_{B}|\approx|E_{B}|
and for every
x\in E^{\prime}_{B}
we have
\#F(x)\cap B_{x}\sim\mu_{1}
. It suffices to show (
5.9
) with
E_{B}
replaced by
E^{\prime}_{B}
.

Now let
x\in E^{\prime}_{B}
. If
B
intersects
B_{x}
then we have
B_{x}\subset 3B
. Hence

(5.11)

\#F_{B}(x)=\#F(x)\cap(3B)\cap B_{x}=\#F(x)\cap B_{x}\sim\mu_{1}.

From now on we fix such a ball
B
and aim to prove (
5.9
). We end with a useful lemma.

Lemma 5.2
.

Fix
x\in E^{\prime}_{B}
and
g\in C^{2}(J)
. If
\delta t^{-1}<\lambda<1
, then for at most
4\cdot(2\lambda)^{\varepsilon}
fraction of
f\in F_{B}(x)
we have
\|f-g\|\leq\lambda t
.

Proof.

Let
g\in C^{2}(J)
. By (
5.7
) and (
5.11
), we have

n_{2}(x)\leq 4t(x)^{-\varepsilon}\#F(x)\cap B_{x}\leq 4\cdot 2^{\varepsilon}t^{-\varepsilon}\#F_{B}(x).

But by the definition of
n_{2}
in (
5.5
) and
\lambda t\in[\delta,K]
, we also have

\displaystyle n_{2}(x)

\displaystyle\geq n_{1}(\lambda t,x)(\lambda t)^{-\varepsilon}\geq\#F(x)\cap B(g,\lambda t)\lambda^{-\varepsilon}t^{-\varepsilon}

Combining the lower and upper bounds of
n_{2}(x)
gives

\#F(x)\cap B(g,\lambda t)\leq 4\cdot(2\lambda)^{\varepsilon}\#F_{B}(x),

from which the result follows.
∎

5.1.2.
Finding the typical tangency of curve-curve intersection

As discussed in Section
1.3
, a typical pair of intersecting curves in
F
might intersect transversely, tangentially, or something in-between. In this section, we will find the typical degree of tangency when two curves intersect. We will do this by using a two-ends reduction argument similar to that in Section
5.1.1
. This two-ends reduction will use a much smaller quantity in place of
\varepsilon
, so that the resulting refinement retains the two-ends property from Section
5.1.1
.

For
x\in E_{B}
and
\Delta>0
, define

(5.12)

n_{3}(\Delta,x)=\max_{k\in F_{B}(x)}\#\{f\in F_{B}(x):\Delta(f,k)\leq\Delta\}.

Since
F_{B}
is contained in
3B
which has diameter
6t
, invoking (
5.11
) and the relation
\Delta(f,k)\leq\|f-g\|
, we have

(5.13)

n_{3}(6t,x)=\#F_{B}(x).

We further define

(5.14)

n_{4}(x)=\sup_{\Delta\in[\delta,6t]}n_{3}(\Delta,x)\Delta^{-\varepsilon^{2}}.

Given
x
, there is some
\Delta_{x}\in[\delta,6t]
such that

(5.15)

n_{4}(x)\leq 2n_{3}(\Delta_{x},x)\Delta_{x}^{-\varepsilon^{2}}.

There is also some
k_{x}\in F_{B}(x)
attaining the maximum in (
5.12
), that is,

(5.16)

\#\{f\in F_{B}(x):\Delta(f,k_{x})\leq\Delta_{x}\}=n_{3}(\Delta_{x},x),

and hence

(5.17)

n_{4}(x)\leq 2\Delta_{x}^{-\varepsilon^{2}}\#\{f\in F_{B}(x):\Delta(f,k_{x})\leq\Delta_{x}\}.

Since
\Delta_{x}\in[\delta,6t]
, by a dyadic pigeonholing, there is a Borel set
E^{\prime\prime}_{B}\subset E^{\prime}_{B}
and a number
\Delta\in[\delta,12t]
such that
\Delta_{x}\in(\Delta/2,\Delta]
for every
x\in E^{\prime\prime}_{B}
, and
|E^{\prime\prime}_{B}|\approx|E^{\prime}_{B}|
. It then suffices to show

(5.18)

|E^{\prime\prime}_{B}|\lessapprox\delta^{2-\frac{\alpha}{2}-\frac{\zeta}{2}-C_{1}\varepsilon}\#F_{B}\mu^{-3/2}.

5.1.3.
Shadings

At this point, the set
E_{0}
from (
5.3
) has been divided into (mostly) disjoint pieces
E_{B}
. For each
x\in E_{B}
, most of the functions
f\in F(x)
are contained in the ball
B
, and only a small fraction of these functions are contained in any ball substantially smaller than
B
. For a typical pair of functions
f,g\in F(x)
,
\Delta(f,g)
has size roughly
\Delta
. For technical reasons, it will be convenient to restrict the sets
f^{\delta}
to a slightly smaller “shading”
Y_{1}(f),
so that the above properties hold whenever
x\in Y_{1}(f)
. More precisely, we define

(5.19)

Y_{1}(f)=\{x\in E^{\prime\prime}_{B}\cap f^{\delta}:\Delta(f,k_{x})\leq\Delta\}.

In view of (
5.17
), (
5.13
) and
\Delta_{x}\leq\Delta
, for every
x\in E^{\prime\prime}_{B}
we have

(5.20)

\#\{f\in F_{B}(x):\Delta(f,k_{x})\leq\Delta\}\gtrsim(\Delta/t)^{\varepsilon^{2}}\#F_{B}(x)\sim(\Delta/t)^{\varepsilon^{2}}\mu_{1}.

On the other hand, we have a trivial bound

\#\{f\in F_{B}(x):\Delta(f,k_{x})\leq\Delta\}\leq\#F_{B}(x)\sim\mu_{1}.

Now we apply another dyadic pigeonholing to find a subset
E_{2}\subset E^{\prime\prime}_{B}
with
|E_{2}|\approx|E^{\prime\prime}_{B}|
and a scale
\mu_{2}
with

(5.21)

(\Delta/t)^{\varepsilon^{2}}\mu_{1}\lesssim\mu_{2}\leq\mu_{1},

such that for every
x\in E_{2}
we have

(5.22)

\#\{f\in F_{B}(x):\Delta(f,k_{x})\leq\Delta\}\sim\mu_{2}.

Equivalently, we have

(5.23)

\#\{f\in F_{B}:x\in Y_{1}(f)\}=\sum_{f\in F_{B}}{\bf 1}_{Y_{1}(f)}(x)\sim\mu_{2},\quad\forall x\in E_{2}.

It then suffices to prove

(5.24)

|E_{2}|\lessapprox\delta^{2-\frac{\alpha}{2}-\frac{\zeta}{2}-C_{1}\varepsilon}(\#F_{B})\mu^{-3/2}.

We record a coarse bound that is used in some dyadic pigeonholing argument later. Since
\#F_{B}(x)\gtrsim t^{\varepsilon}\mu
and
\mu\leq\#F\lesssim\delta^{-1-\varepsilon}
, the right hand side of (
5.24
) is trivially bounded below by
\delta^{3}
, for
\delta
small enough. If
|E_{2}|\leq\delta^{3}
then we are done, and so in the following we may assume

(5.25)

|E_{2}|>\delta^{3}.

5.2.
Fine-scale rectangles

In this section, we will show that
E_{2}
can be covered by curvilinear rectangles of dimensions roughly
\delta\times\delta/\sqrt{t\Delta}
, so that these rectangles are (mostly) disjoint, and for most pairs
f,g\in F_{B}(x)
, we have that
Y_{1}(f)\cap Y_{1}(g)
is localized to roughly one of these rectangles.

Lemma 5.3
.

There is a constant
C_{R}=C_{R}(K)
(
R
stands for “rectangle”) such that the following is true. Let

(5.26)

t^{\prime}=C_{R}t\Delta/\delta.

For each
x\in E_{2}
, there is a
(\delta,t^{\prime})
-rectangle
R_{x}
over
J/8
, such that
x\in R_{x}
, and every
f\in F_{B}
with
x\in Y_{1}(f)
is tangent to
R_{x}
.

Proof.

Denote
x=(\theta_{0},y_{0})
where
x\in J/16
. Let
I
be the interval of length
\sqrt{\delta/t^{\prime}}
centred at
\theta_{0}
. Since
t\gtrsim\Delta\geq\delta
, we have
I\subset J/8
if
C_{R}
is large enough. With this, define the curvilinear rectangle

(5.27)

R_{x}=k_{x}^{\delta}(I),

where
k_{x}
is as in (
5.16
). We now show that
R_{x}
is as required.

First we have
x\in R_{x}
since
k_{x}\in F_{B}(x)
by (
5.12
). Also, let
f
be such that
x\in Y_{1}(f)
, then in particular,
x\in f^{\delta}
. By the triangle inequality,
|f(\theta_{0})-k_{x}(\theta_{0})|\leq 2\delta
. By Part (
2c
) of Lemma
3.8
at the scale
4\delta
, we have

E_{4\delta}=\{\theta\in J/4:|f(\theta)-k_{x}(\theta)|\leq 4\delta\}

contains an interval
I^{\prime}
obeying
\theta_{0}\in I^{\prime}
and

|I^{\prime}|\gtrsim\delta d(f,k_{x})^{-1/2}(\delta+\Delta(f,k_{x}))^{-1/2}.

Since
x\in Y_{1}(f)
we have
\Delta(f,k_{x})\leq\Delta
. Also
d(f,k(x))\leq 6t
. If
C_{R}
is chosen to be large enough, then the interval
I
containing
\theta_{0}
will have length
\sqrt{\delta/t^{\prime}}\leq|I^{\prime}|
, and thus
I\subset I^{\prime}
and
R_{x}
is tangent to
f
.
∎

5.2.1.
Shadings of fine-scale rectangles

For each
(\delta,t^{\prime})
-rectangle
R
, we define its shading as

(5.28)

Y_{2}(R)=\{x\in C_{s}R\cap E_{2}:R_{x}\text{ is $C_{s}$-comparable to }R\},

where
C_{s}=C_{s}(K)\geq 100
(
s
standing for “shadings”) is a suitable constant to be determined. Intuitively,
Y_{2}(R)
is an important subset of
R
since it is where the
\delta
-neighbourhoods of the curves in
F_{B}
overlap greatly.

For technical purposes, we also define a slightly smaller shading of
R
as

Y^{\prime}_{2}(R)=\{x\in R\cap E_{2}:R_{x}\text{ is $100$-comparable to }R\}.

By Lemma
5.3
, we have

(5.29)

E_{2}=\bigcup_{x\in E_{2}}Y^{\prime}_{2}(R_{x}).

We also record an elementary lemma on the shadings of curvilinear rectangles.

Lemma 5.4
.

If
C_{s}
is suitably chosen, then for any pair
(R,R^{\prime})
of
100
-comparable
(\delta,t)
-rectangles, we have
Y^{\prime}_{2}(R^{\prime})\subset Y_{2}(R)
.

Proof.

If
R,R^{\prime}
are
100
-comparable, then by Lemma
3.12
, we have
R^{\prime}\subset C_{s}R
if
C_{s}
is large enough. Thus if
x\in Y^{\prime}_{2}(R^{\prime})
, then
x\in C_{s}R\cap E_{2}
. Also,
R_{x}
is
100
-comparable to
R^{\prime}
, and since
R^{\prime}
is
100
-comparable to
R
, Corollary
3.13
implies that
R_{x}
is
C_{s}
-comparable to
R
if
C_{s}
is large enough. Thus
Y^{\prime}_{2}(R^{\prime})\subset Y_{2}(R)
.
∎

5.2.2.
Finding an incomparable set of fine-scale rectangles

Lemma 5.5
.

There is a pairwise
100
-incomparable subcollection
\mathcal{R}
of
\{R_{x}:x\in E_{2}\}
such that the following holds:

(1)

There is some
\Lambda\in(0,1)
such that for each
R_{x}\in\mathcal{R}
we have
\Lambda\leq|Y_{2}(R_{x})|<2\Lambda
.

(2)

Y_{2}(R)\cap Y_{2}(R^{\prime})=\varnothing
for
R\neq R^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}
, and

(5.30)

|E_{2}|\lessapprox\sum_{R\in\mathcal{R}}|Y_{2}(R)|\sim\Lambda\#\mathcal{R}.

(3)

We have the coarse bounds

(5.31)

\#\mathcal{R}\leq\delta^{-C},\quad\Lambda\geq\delta^{C^{\prime}},

for
\delta
sufficiently small (depending on
K,D
), where
C,C^{\prime}
depend on
D
only.

Proof.

Before we construct
\mathcal{R}
, we first construct a larger collection
\mathcal{R}^{*}
in the following greedy way.

Denote
\Lambda_{1}=\sup_{x\in E_{2}}|Y^{\prime}_{2}(R_{x})|
. Since we have assumed
|E_{2}|>\delta^{3}
we have
\Lambda_{1}>0
by (
5.29
). So we pick
R_{1}=R_{x_{1}}
such that
|Y^{\prime}_{2}(R_{1})|>\Lambda_{1}/2
.

To choose
R_{2}
, we consider all
(\delta,t)
-rectangles
R_{x}
that are
100
-incomparable to
R_{1}
; denote this collection by
\mathcal{R}^{(1)}
. Denote
\Lambda_{2}=\sup_{R_{x}\in\mathcal{R}^{(1)}}|Y^{\prime}_{2}(R_{x})|
. If
\Lambda_{2}=0
, then we just let
\mathcal{R}^{*}=\{R_{1}\}
. With this, (
5.29
) and Lemma
5.4
, we have (here
\sim_{100}
means
100
-comparable)

\displaystyle|E_{2}|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\bigcup_{x\in E_{2},R_{x}\sim_{100}R_{1}}Y^{\prime}_{2}(R_{x})\right|+\left|\bigcup_{x\in E_{2},R_{x}\not\sim_{100}R_{1}}Y^{\prime}_{2}(R_{x})\right|

\displaystyle\leq|Y_{2}(R_{1})|+\sum_{R_{x}\in\mathcal{R}^{(1)}}\left|Y^{\prime}_{2}(R_{x})\right|

\displaystyle=|Y_{2}(R_{1})|.

If
\Lambda_{2}>0
, then we pick
R_{2}\in\mathcal{R}^{(1)}
such that
|Y^{\prime}_{2}(R_{2})|>\Lambda_{2}/2
. To choose
R_{3}
, we consider all
(\delta,t)
-rectangles that are
100
-incomparable to both
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
; denote this collection by
\mathcal{R}^{(2)}
. Denote
\Lambda_{3}=\sup_{R_{x}\in\mathcal{R}^{(2)}}|Y^{\prime}_{2}(R_{x})|
. If
\Lambda_{3}=0
, we just let
\mathcal{R}^{*}=\{R_{1},R_{2}\}
. With this, we similarly have

\displaystyle|E_{2}|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\bigcup_{x\in E_{2},R_{x}\sim_{100}R_{1}}Y^{\prime}_{2}(R_{x})\right|+\left|\bigcup_{x\in E_{2},R_{x}\sim_{100}R_{2}}Y^{\prime}_{2}(R_{x})\right|\leq|Y_{2}(R_{1})|+|Y_{2}(R_{2})|.

We can continue arguing in this fashion. Note that Lemma
3.17
implies that the process must stop at some finite time
N<\delta^{-C}
for some constant
C=C(D)
, proving the first inequality in (
5.31
). Thus
\Lambda_{N+1}=0
,
\Lambda_{N}>0
,
\mathcal{R}^{*}=\{R_{1},\dots,R_{N}\}
, and

|E_{2}|\leq\sum_{n=1}^{N}|Y_{2}(R_{n})|.

We may assume without loss of generality that
|Y_{2}(R_{n})|\geq\delta^{C+3}
for all
n
. Indeed, we may throw away all
n
such that
|Y_{2}(R_{n})|<\delta^{C+3}
, since the sum of all such
|Y_{2}(R_{n})|
is less than
\delta^{C+3}N<\delta^{3}<|E_{2}|/2
, as
|E_{2}|>\delta^{3}
by our assumption.

Now for
j\geq 0
, we denote

\mathcal{R}^{(j)}=\{R_{n}\in\mathcal{R}^{*}:2^{j-1}\Lambda_{N}\leq|Y_{2}(R_{n})|<2^{j}\Lambda_{N}\}.

There are at most
O(|\log\delta|)
many such
j
’s, since
\delta^{C+3}\leq|Y_{2}(R_{n})|\leq 1
. By pigeonholing, there is some
j
such that

\sum_{n=1}^{N}|Y_{2}(R_{n})|\lesssim|\log\delta|\sum_{n:R_{n}\in\mathcal{R}^{(j)}}|Y_{2}(R_{n})|.

We now define

\mathcal{R}=\mathcal{R}^{(j)},\quad\Lambda=2^{j-1}\Lambda_{N}>0.

Thus

|E_{2}|\leq\sum_{n=1}^{N}|Y_{2}(R_{n})|\lessapprox\Lambda\#\mathcal{R}.

Also, we have
\Lambda\geq\delta^{C+4}
, since
|E_{2}|>\delta^{3}
and
\#\mathcal{R}\leq N<\delta^{-C}
.

∎

We now observe the following. Since
Y_{2}(R)\subset C_{s}R\cap E_{2}
,
R
is over an interval of length
\sim\sqrt{\delta/t^{\prime}}
, and
E_{2}
is further contained in a
(\delta,\alpha;\delta^{-\varepsilon})_{1}\times(\delta,\alpha;\delta^{-\varepsilon})_{1}
quasi-product, we have

\Lambda\sim|Y_{2}(R)|\lesssim\delta^{2-\varepsilon}(\sqrt{\delta/t^{\prime}}/\delta)^{\alpha}\sim\delta^{2-\varepsilon}\Delta^{-\alpha/2}t^{-\alpha/2}.

With this, we write

(5.32)

\Lambda=\tau\delta^{2-\varepsilon}\Delta^{-\alpha/2}t^{-\alpha/2},

where

(5.33)

0<\tau\lesssim 1.

To establish (
5.24
), it thus suffices to show

(5.34)

\#\mathcal{R}\lessapprox\tau^{-1}\delta^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}-\frac{\zeta}{2}-C_{2}\varepsilon}\Delta^{\alpha/2}t^{\alpha/2}\#F_{B}\mu^{-3/2},

for some
C_{2}=C_{2}(D)
.

5.3.
Coarse-scale rectangles

In this section, we will show that the fine-scale rectangles from Section
5.2
are contained in coarse-scale rectangles of dimensions roughly
\Delta\times\sqrt{\Delta/t}
. If a fine-scale rectangle
R
is contained in a coarse-scale rectangle
\tilde{R}
, then
\tilde{R}
is (roughly) a dilate of
R
.

For each
R\in\mathcal{R}
, we define
\tilde{R}(R)
as follows. If

We turn to the details. By construction, each

Let

and

Denote by

the collection of all good pairs. For each

(5.38)

\mathcal{G}(f)=\{R\in\mathcal{R}:(f,R)\in\mathcal{G}\}.

For each

For each

5.4.1.
Pigeonholing the good pairs

For each
\tilde{R}\in\tilde{\mathcal{R}}

By the disjointness of

For each

By construction,

\#F(\tilde{R})\sim\frac{1}{q}\#\{(f,R)\in\mathcal{G}^{\prime}\colon R\in\mathcal{R}(\tilde{R})\}=\frac{1}{q}\sum_{R\in\mathcal{R}(\tilde{R})}\#\mathcal{G}^{\prime}(R).

More generally, for every ball

By (

.

For some applications, it may be helpful to replace the Frostman condition (
1.7
) with the weaker inequality

(5.62)

\#(F\cap B)\leq A(r/\delta)^{\zeta}\quad\textrm{for all balls}\ B\subset C^{2}(I)\ \textrm{of radius}\ r\geq\delta,

for some
A\geq 1
. By randomly selecting each
f\in F
with probability
1/A
and then applying Theorem
1.7
, we obtain the following analogue of (
1.8
).

(5.63)

\int_{E}\Big(\sum_{f\in F}{\bf 1}_{f^{\delta}}\Big)^{3/2}\leq A^{1/2}\delta^{2-\alpha/2-\zeta/2-C\varepsilon}(\#F).

Appendix A
Sogge’s cinematic curvature, and cinematic functions

In this section, we will explain the connection between cinematic functions (see Definition
1.6
) and Sogge’s cinematic curvature condition, as used by Kolasa and Wolff in
[
17
]
. First, we will show that if
\Phi\colon\mathbb{R}^{4}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfies the cinematic curvature condition (for simplicity, we will state this condition in a form that is convenient to compute with), then there are sets
Y\times R\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
and
I\times J\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
, so that for each
(y,r)\in Y\times R
, the curve
\{x\in I\times J\colon\Phi(x,y)=r\}
is the graph of a function
f_{(y,r)}\colon I\to J
, and
\{f_{(y,r)}\}
is a family of cinematic functions. The proof of this statement requires careful computation using the inverse function theorem. These computations are described below.

A.1.
Cinematic curvature for normalized defining functions

Let
U=I\times J\times Y\subset\mathbb{R}^{4},
where
I,J\subset\mathbb{R}
are open intervals containing 0, and
Y\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a neighborhood of 0. Let
\Phi(x,y)\colon U\to\mathbb{R}
, with

(A.1)

\|\Phi\|_{C^{3}(U)}=C_{0}.

We will require

(A.2)

\Phi(x,0)=x_{2}+O(x^{3}),

(A.3)

d_{y}\Big(\begin{array}[]{c}\partial_{x_{1}}\Phi(x,y)\\
\partial^{2}_{x_{1}}\Phi(x,y)/|\nabla_{x}\Phi(x,y)|\end{array}\Big)\Big|_{(x,y)=(0,0)}\quad\textrm{is invertible}.

Conditions (
A.2
) and (
A.3
) are a formulation of Sogge’s cinematic curvature condition for the defining function
\Phi
. See, for example Section 3 or the beginning of Section 5 from
[
17
]
.

Shrinking
I
and
Y
if necessary, by the inverse function theorem we can find an interval
R
containing 0 so that for each
(y,r)\in Y\times R
, the curve
\{x\in I\times J\colon\Phi(x,y)=r\}
is the graph
x_{2}=f_{y,r}(x_{1})
, where
f_{y,r}\in C^{2}(I)
. We will show that after possibly further shrinking
I
and
Y,
the set

(A.4)

\mathcal{F}=\{f_{y,r}\colon(y,r)\in Y\times R\}\subset C^{2}(I)

is a cinematic family of functions. Specifically, we will show there exists
\varepsilon>0
so that for all
(y,r),(y^{\prime},r^{\prime})\in Y\times R
and all
x_{1}\in I
, we have

(A.5)

|f_{y,r}(x_{1})-f_{y^{\prime},r^{\prime}}(x_{1})|+|\dot{f}_{y,r}(x_{1})-\dot{f}_{y^{\prime},r^{\prime}}(x_{1})|+|\ddot{f}_{y,r}(x_{1})-\ddot{f}_{y^{\prime},r^{\prime}}(x_{1})|\geq\varepsilon|(y,r)-(y^{\prime},r^{\prime})|.

Clearly the metric space
\mathcal{F}
has bounded diameter, and doubling.

Step 1

After shrinking

I,
and

if necessary (and re-defining

), we can suppose that

(A.6)

1/2\leq\partial_{x_{2}}\Phi(x,y)\leq 2\quad\textrm{for all}\ (x,y)\in U,

so that

\partial^{2}_{x_{1}}\Phi(x,y)/|\nabla_{x}\Phi(x,y)|\end{array}\Big)\Big)\Big|\geq c\quad\textrm{for all}\ (x,y)\in U.

(

) is a consequence of (
A.2
), while (
A.7
) is a consequence of (
A.3
).

\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2},\varepsilon_{3}

sufficiently small depending on

C_{0}
. We will choose
\varepsilon_{2}
sufficiently small depending on

C_{0}
and
\varepsilon_{3}
. We will choose

sufficiently small depending on

C_{0}
and
\varepsilon_{2},\varepsilon_{3}

After further shrinking
Y,

I,
and
J
if necessary, we can suppose that

Step 2

Let

. Let

, and let
