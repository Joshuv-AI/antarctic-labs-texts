A feasible method for general convex low-rank SDP problems
Tianyun Tang, Kim-Chuan Toh

Abstract
In this work, we consider the low rank decomposition (SDPR) of general convex semidefinite programming problems (SDP) that contain both a positive semidefinite matrix and a nonnegative vector as variables. We develop a rank-support-adaptive feasible method to solve (SDPR) based on Riemannian optimization. The method is able to escape from a saddle point to ensure its convergence to a global optimal solution for generic constraint vectors. We prove its global convergence and local linear convergence without assuming that the objective function is twice differentiable. Due to the special structure of the low-rank SDP problem, our algorithm can achieve better iteration complexity than existing results for more general smooth nonconvex problems. In order to overcome the degeneracy issues of SDP problems, we develop two strategies based on random perturbation and dual refinement. These techniques enable us to solve some primal degenerate SDP problems efficiently, for example, Lovász theta SDPs. Our work is a step forward in extending the application range of Riemannian optimization approaches for solving SDP problems. Numerical experiments are conducted to verify the efficiency and robustness of our method.

In this paper, our goal is to demonstrate that feasible methods can also be developed to solve a general SDP problem. However, we should emphasize that unlike other previous works

1.5
A rank-support-adaptive feasible method

In Section
3
, we will design a
rank-support-adaptive feasible method to solve (SDP) by solving (SDPR) with an adaptive parameter

Intuitively speaking, the rank-adaptive strategy is to dynamically increase the parameter

Although the rank-adaptive idea has been frequently used in the literature of

In order to fill the gap between theory and practice, we will prove that our algorithm can return an approximate KKT solution under the LICQ (linear independence constraint qualification) assumption. Surprisingly, due to the special structure of (SDPR), the iteration complexity of our algorithm is better than the state-of-the-art complexity of Riemannian trust region method in
[
15
]
by Boumal et al. Apart from the global sub-linear convergence, we also prove the local linear convergence of our algorithm under a quadratic growth condition, which will be shown to be shown to be a generic property for linear SDP problems with generic data

This additionally implies the importance of rank-adaptive technique because it not only reduces the problem dimension but also improve the algorithm’s efficiency.

1.6
Generic smoothness and random perturbation

The generic

6

smoothness of linear SDP problem was first established in
[

In

, Scholtes and Stöhr proved that for a generic

Note that the random perturbation strategy alone cannot completely overcome the ill-conditioning issue in an SDP problem with degenerate optimal solutions. We will further develop an adaptive preconditioning technique to make the problem

1.7
Refinement of dual variable

The optimality of a given point
(X,x)
in (SDP) cannot be verified without the dual variable. In Section

). However, when the Jacobian of the constraint matrix is ill-conditioned or even singular, the dual variable computed from the above mentioned linear system may not be accurate. In order to overcome this issue, we will design a method based on ALM to improve the feasibility of the dual variable in Section

In the proof of Lemma
1
, the main task is to derive the dual variable of the constraint
X_{1:k,1:k}=I_{k}
, i.e., equation (
19
). Without this constraint, conditions (i)-(iii) are exactly the KKT condition of (SDP) so there is nothing to prove. Verifying the optimality condition in Lemma
1
requires us to know
\lambda
in advance. Under the LICQ condition, the linear system (ii) of
\lambda
has at most one solution because the mapping
{\rm D}\mathcal{G}(R,y)^{*}
in (
10
) is injective. Thus, given
(R,y)
,
we can recover the dual variable
\lambda
by computing a least square solution of (ii). We call
(R,y)
a saddle point if the condition (iii) is violated.

3
Algorithm

In this section, we will design a rank-support-adaptive feasible method with convergence guarantee. Because our algorithm is based on Riemannian optimization, throughout this section, we always assume that the following LICQ assumption hold. In the next section, we will discuss how to handle the singular points in
\Omega_{r}
by
a random perturbation technique.

Assumption 1
.

There exists an positive integer
\bar{r}\geq k
such that for any
r\geq\bar{r},

\Omega_{r}\neq\emptyset
and the LICQ holds at every point in
\Omega_{r}.

Before we design our algorithm, we need some preparations in the next subsection.

3.1
Preliminaries for algorithmic design

We first study the geometric structure of (SDPR). We will focus on the case where
\Omega_{r}
is a Riemannian submanifold embedded in
\mathbb{R}^{(n-k)\times r}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}
(see Section 3 and 5 in
[
14
]
for properties of embedded manifold).
For any
(R,y)\in\Omega_{r}
, its tangent space is

{\rm T}_{(R,y)}\Omega_{r}:=\left\{(H,h)\in\mathbb{R}^{(n-k)\times r}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}:\ {\rm D}\mathcal{G}(R,y)[(H,h)]=0\right\}

(20)

where
{\rm D}\mathcal{G}(R,y)
is defined in (
9
).
The projection
{\rm Proj}_{(R,y)}:\mathbb{R}^{(n-k)\times r}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}\rightarrow{\rm T}_{(R,y)}\Omega_{r}
is the orthogonal projection onto the tangent space, which is given by:

{\rm Proj}_{(R,y)}\left((D,d)\right)=(D,d)-{\rm D}\mathcal{G}(R,y)^{*}\big({\rm D}\mathcal{G}(R,y){\rm D}\mathcal{G}(R,y)^{*}\big)^{-1}{\rm D}\mathcal{G}(R,y)[D,d].

(21)

The Riemannian gradient is the projection of the Euclidean gradient onto the tangent space:

{\rm grad}f_{r}(R,y):={\rm Proj}_{(R,y)}\left(\nabla_{R,y}f_{r}(R,y)\right)=\nabla_{R,y}f_{r}(R,y)-{\rm D}\mathcal{G}(R,y)^{*}[\lambda],

(22)

where
\lambda
is the solution of the following linear system:

{\rm D}\mathcal{G}(R,y){\rm D}\mathcal{G}(R,y)^{*}[\lambda]={\rm D}\mathcal{G}(R,y)[\nabla_{R,y}f_{r}(R,y)].

(23)

Because
(R,y)
satisfies LICQ,
{\rm D}\mathcal{G}(R,y)^{*}
is injective. Thus (
23
) has a unique solution. Recall that a retraction mapping
{\rm Rtr}_{(R,y)}:{\rm T}_{(R,y)}\Omega_{r}\rightarrow\Omega_{r}
satisfies that for any
(H,h)\in{\rm T}_{(R,y)}\Omega_{r}
and
t\in\mathbb{R},

{\rm Rtr}_{(R,y)}\left(t(H,h)\right)=(R,y)+t(H,h)+o(t).

(24)

Note that a retraction mapping is not unique, it can be any mappings that satisfies (
24
). In our algorithm, we use the Newton retraction proposed by Zhang in
[
59
]
, which has been proven to be a second-order retraction. The main cost in each iteration of
the Newton retraction is in solving several linear systems of the form (
23
).

For the purpose of convergence analysis, we need the following definition of
an approximate stationary point.

Definition 1
.

Suppose Assumption
1
holds and
r\geq\bar{r}.
For any
(R,y)\in\Omega_{r}
and
\epsilon_{g},\epsilon_{h}>0,
we call
(R,y)
an
(\epsilon_{g},\epsilon_{h})-
stationary point of (SDPR) if

\displaystyle\left\|{\rm grad}f_{r}(R,y)\right\|\leq\epsilon_{g}

(25)

\displaystyle C_{22}-\tilde{\mathcal{A}}^{*}(\lambda)\succeq-\epsilon_{h}I,\ c-B^{\top}\lambda\geq-\epsilon_{h}e,

(26)

where
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
satisfies (
23
) and
e
is the vector of all ones.

If
\epsilon_{g}=\epsilon_{h}=0,
then (
25
) and (
26
) implies the condition (ii) and (iii) in Lemma
1
. From Lemma
1
, we know that
\big(\widehat{R}\widehat{R}^{\top},y\circ y\big)
is an optimal solution of (SDP). This is the motivation of Definition
1
. In
[
15
]
, Boumal et al. use the norm of the Riemannian gradient and the positive semi-definiteness of the Riemannian Hessian to measure the second order stationarity. Our Definition
1
is as strong as their definition of stationarity for a linear SDP problem. This is because condition (
25
) also uses the norm of the Riemannian gradient. Moreover, for a linear SDP problem, condition (
26
) implies that the smallest eigenvalue of the Riemannian Hessian is lower bounded by
-2\epsilon_{h}
(see (17) of
[
17
]
). Therefore, it is reasonable for us perform complexity analysis using Definition
1
. The advantage of Definition
1
is that we don’t need the second order information of
\phi
, so we can just assume that
\phi
is continuously differentiable.

3.2
Riemannian gradient descent

In this subsection, we will apply the Riemannian gradient descent method
to decrease the function value in (SDPR). We make the following assumption.

Assumption 2
.

There exists
L_{g}\geq 0
and
\rho_{g}>0
such that for any
\bar{r}\leq r\leq n
and any
(R,y)\in\Omega_{r}

\left|f_{r}\left({\rm Rtr}_{(R,y)}\left((H,h)\right)\right)-\left[f_{r}(R,y)+\big\langle(H,h),{\rm grad}f_{r}(R,y)\big\rangle\right]\right|\\
\leq\frac{L_{g}}{2}\left(\|H\|^{2}+\|h\|^{2}\right),

(27)

for all
(H,h)\in{\rm T}_{(R,y)}\Omega_{r}
such that
\sqrt{\|H\|^{2}+\|h\|^{2}}\leq\rho_{g}.

Assumption
2
is equivalent to Assumption 2.6 in
[
15
]
, where it is used to prove the convergence of a Riemannian gradient descent method. Moreover, from Lemma 2.7 in
[
15
]
, Assumption
2
holds when
\Omega_{r}
is a compact submanifold of
\mathbb{R}^{(n-k)\times r}\times\mathbb{R}^{p},
the retraction mapping is globally defined and
f_{r}
has Lipschitz continuous gradient in the convex hull of
\Omega_{r}.
With Assumption
2
, we have the following proposition about the sufficient decrease of function value. We omit the proof because it is the same as Theorem 2.8 in
[
15
]
.

Proposition 1
.

Suppose Assumption
1
and Assumption
2
hold. Then there exists
\gamma_{g}>0
such that for any
\bar{r}\leq r\leq n
and
(R,y)\in\Omega_{r},
there exists
t>0
satisfying the following inequality:

f_{r}\left({\rm Rtr}_{(R,y)}\left(-t\cdot{\rm grad}f_{r}(R,y)\right)\right)-f_{r}(R,y)\\
\leq-\gamma_{g}\|{\rm grad}f_{r}(R,y)\|\min\{1,\|{\rm grad}f_{r}(R,y)\|\}.

(28)

Note that the parameter
t
in Proposition
1
is unknown in advance. However, from Lemma 10 in
[
15
]
, we can find such a stepsize by applying Armijo line-search.

3.3
Escaping from a saddle point

Proposition
1
shows that the function value can be decreased sufficiently when the optimality condition (
25
) is violated. In this subsection, we show that when the second optimality condition (
26
) is violated, we can also decrease the function value sufficiently by updating the rank and support of
R
and
y
respectively. We start by defining the following set:

\Omega_{r}^{\tau}:=\left\{(S,Y)\in\mathbb{R}^{(n-k)\times(r+\tau)}\times\mathbb{R}^{p\times 2}:\ \mathcal{K}\big(\widehat{S}\widehat{S}^{\top},{\rm diag}\left(YY^{\top}\right)\big)-b=0\right\}.

(29)

The above set
\Omega_{r}^{\tau}
is constructed by adding
\tau
columns to the matrix variable and 1 column to the vector variable of
\Omega_{r}
. These new columns are for us to increase the rank and support. The main idea of the support updating strategy is to consider every entry of
y
as an
1\times 1
matrix block and apply the rank adaptive strategy to them independently. This is why we add a new column to the vector variable. Note that we can maintain the vector structure by introducing
y:=\sqrt{{\rm diag}\left(YY^{\top}\right)}
after updating the support. Now we are ready to show how to decrease the function value by adding new columns.

We first study the LICQ property of
\Omega_{r}^{\tau}.
The following definitions are similar to those given in (
8
), (
9
) and (
10
). Define
\mathcal{G}_{\tau}:\mathbb{R}^{(n-k)\times(r+\tau)}\times\mathbb{R}^{p\times 2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
such that for any
(S,Y)\in\mathbb{R}^{(n-k)\times(r+\tau)}\times\mathbb{R}^{p\times 2},

\mathcal{G}_{\tau}(S,Y):=\mathcal{K}\big(\widehat{S}\widehat{S}^{\top},{\rm diag}\left(YY^{\top}\right)\big)-b.

(30)

We have that for any
\left(\lambda,U,V\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{(n-k)\times(r+\tau)}\times\mathbb{R}^{p\times 2},

{\rm D}\mathcal{G}_{\tau}(S,Y)[(U,V)]=\mathcal{K}\left(\mathcal{L}_{S}(U),\;2{\rm diag}(YV^{\top})\right),

(31)

and its adjoint mapping is given by

{\rm D}\mathcal{G}_{\tau}(S,Y)^{*}[\lambda]=2\big(J_{n-k,n}\mathcal{A}^{*}(\lambda)\widehat{S},\;{\rm diag}\left(B^{\top}\lambda\right)Y\big).

(32)

For any
(S,Y)\in\Omega_{r}^{\tau},

\big(S,\sqrt{{\rm diag}(YY^{\top})}\big)\in\Omega_{r+\tau}.
Moreover, from (
10
) and (
32
),
{\rm D}\mathcal{G}_{\tau}(S,Y)^{*}
is an injective mapping if and only if
{\rm D}\mathcal{G}\big(S,\sqrt{{\rm diag}\left(YY^{\top}\right)}\big)^{*}
is an injective mapping. Therefore, from Assumption
1
, we know that for any
r\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
such that
r\geq\bar{r},
every point
(S,Y)\in\Omega_{r}^{\tau}
satisfies the LICQ property.

With the LICQ property, we move on to study the geometric property of
\Omega_{r}^{\tau}.
The tangent space of
\Omega_{r}^{\tau}
at
(S,Y)
is given as follows:

{\rm T}_{(S,Y)}\Omega_{r}^{\tau}:=\left\{(U,V)\in\mathbb{R}^{(n-k)\times(r+\tau)}\times\mathbb{R}^{p\times 2}:\ {\rm D}\mathcal{G}_{\tau}(S,Y)[(U,V)]=0\right\}.

(33)

Because of the LICQ property,
\Omega_{r}^{\tau}
is a Riemannian submanifold of
\mathbb{R}^{(n-k)\times(r+\tau)}\times\mathbb{R}^{p\times 2}.
There exists a second order retraction
{\rm Rtr}_{(S,Y)}:{\rm T}_{(S,Y)}\Omega_{r}^{\tau}\rightarrow\Omega_{r}^{\tau}
such that for any
(U,V)\in{\rm T}_{(S,Y)}\Omega_{r}^{\tau}
and
t\in\mathbb{R},

{\rm Rtr}_{(S,Y)}\left(t(U,V)\right)=(S,Y)+t(U,V)+\frac{t^{2}}{2}(W,Z)+o(t^{2}),

(34)

for some
(W,Z)\in\big({\rm T}_{(S,Y)}\Omega_{r}^{\tau}\big)^{\perp}.

In practice, one can use the metric projection (see 5.48 of
[
14
]
), the Newton retraction in
[
59
]
or the orthographic retraction in
[
2
]
as a second order retraction. Since
(W,Z)\in\big({\rm T}_{(S,Y)}\Omega_{r}^{\tau}\big)^{\perp},
there exists
\hat{\lambda}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
such that

(W,Z)={\rm D}\mathcal{G}_{\tau}(S,Y)^{*}[\hat{\lambda}]=2\big(J_{n-k,n}\mathcal{A}^{*}(\hat{\lambda})\widehat{S},\;{\rm diag}\big(B^{\top}\hat{\lambda}\big)Y\big).

(35)

Substituting (
34
) and (
35
) into the equation
\mathcal{G}_{\tau}\left({\rm Rtr}_{(S,Y)}\left(t(U,V)\right)\right)=0
, we obtain the following equation, which says that the coefficient of
t^{2}
in the expansion of
\mathcal{G}_{\tau}\left({\rm Rtr}_{(S,Y)}\left(t(U,V)\right)\right)
is zero:

\frac{1}{2}{\rm D}\mathcal{G}_{\tau}(S,Y)\left[{\rm D}\mathcal{G}_{\tau}(S,Y)^{*}[\hat{\lambda}]\right]+\tilde{\mathcal{K}}\left(UU^{\top},{\rm diag}(VV^{\top})\right)=0.

(36)

The above equation also implies that
\hat{\lambda}
is the unique solution of the linear system (
36
). Note that we have used the formula (
7
) to derive the above equation.
Therefore,
(W,Z)
is a smooth mapping of
(U,V).
Define
f_{r,\tau}:\mathbb{R}^{(n-k)\times(r+\tau)}\times\mathbb{R}^{p\times 2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that for any
(S,Y)\in\mathbb{R}^{(n-k)\times(r+\tau)}\times\mathbb{R}^{p\times 2}

f_{r,\tau}\left((S,Y)\right):=\phi\big(\widehat{S}\widehat{S}^{\top},{\rm diag}\left(YY^{\top}\right)\big).

(37)

Now, with the geometric structure of
\Omega_{r}^{\tau},
we are able to show how the function value will change by adding new columns
(H,h)\in\mathbb{R}^{(n-k)\times\tau}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}
to
(R,y)\in\Omega_{r}.
In order to do so, consider the following variables:

(S,Y):=\left([R,0_{(n-k)\times\tau}],[y,0_{p\times 1}]\right),\ (U,V):=\left([0_{(n-k)\times r},H],[0_{p\times 1},h]\right)

(38)

such that
H\in\mathbb{R}^{(n-k)\times\tau}
and
h\in\mathbb{R}^{p}.
It is easy to see that
(S,Y)\in\Omega_{r}^{\tau}
and
(U,V)\in{\rm T}_{(S,Y)}\Omega_{r}^{\tau}.
Also, from (
35
), we know that the last
\tau
columns of
W
and the second column of
Z
are zero columns respectively i.e.,
W=[W_{1},0_{(n-k)\times\tau}]
and
Z=[z_{1},0_{p\times 1}]
for some
W_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{(n-k)\times r}
and
z_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}.

Because
\phi
is continuously differentiable,
f_{r,\tau}
is also continuously differentiable. From (
34
), we have the following result:

\displaystyle f_{r,\tau}\left({\rm Rtr}_{(S,Y)}\left(t(U,V)\right)\right)=f_{r,\tau}\Big((S,Y)+t(U,V)+\frac{t^{2}}{2}(W,Z)\Big)+o(t^{2})

\displaystyle=f_{r,\tau}\left(\Big(\big[R+\frac{t^{2}}{2}W_{1},tH\big],\big[y+\frac{t^{2}}{2}z_{1},th\big]\Big)\right)+o(t^{2})

\displaystyle=\phi\Big(\widehat{R}\widehat{R}^{\top}+\frac{t^{2}}{2}\mathcal{L}_{R}(W_{1})+t^{2}J_{n,n-k}^{\top}HH^{\top}J_{n,n-k},\ y\circ y+t^{2}y\circ z_{1}+t^{2}h\circ h\Big)+o(t^{2}).

\displaystyle=f_{r}\left(R,y\right)+\frac{t^{2}}{2}\big(\big\langle C,\mathcal{L}_{R}(W_{1})\big\rangle+\big\langle c,2y\circ z_{1}\big\rangle\big)+t^{2}\left(\big\langle C_{22},HH^{\top}\big\rangle+\big\langle c,h\circ h\big\rangle\right)+o(t^{2}),

(39)

where
C=\nabla_{X}\phi(RR^{\top},y\circ y)
and
c=\nabla_{x}\phi(RR^{\top},y\circ y)
.
By using (
6
), we have that

\displaystyle\big\langle C,\mathcal{L}_{R}(W_{1})\big\rangle+\big\langle c,2y\circ z_{1}\big\rangle=\big\langle 2J_{n-k,n}C\widehat{R},W_{1}\big\rangle+\big\langle 2c\circ y,z_{1}\big\rangle

\displaystyle=\big\langle\nabla_{R,y}f_{r}\left(R,y\right),\left(W_{1},z_{1}\right)\big\rangle=\big\langle{\rm grad}f_{r}\left(R,y\right)+{\rm D}\mathcal{G}(R,y)^{*}[\lambda],\left(W_{1},z_{1}\right)\big\rangle

\displaystyle=\big\langle{\rm grad}f_{r}\left(R,y\right),\left(W_{1},z_{1}\right)\big\rangle+\big\langle\lambda,{\rm D}\mathcal{G}(R,y)[\left(W_{1},z_{1}\right)]\big\rangle,

(40)

where the third equality comes from (
22
) and
\lambda
is the unique solution of (
23
). From (
35
) and the relation between
(W,Z)
and
(W_{1},z_{1})
, we know that
\left(W_{1},z_{1}\right)={\rm D}\mathcal{G}(R,y)^{*}[\hat{\lambda}]\in\big({\rm T}_{(R,y)}\Omega_{r}\big)^{\perp}.
Since
{\rm grad}f\left(R,y\right)\in{\rm T}_{(R,y)}\Omega_{r},
we have

\big\langle{\rm grad}f_{r}\left(R,y\right),\left(W_{1},z_{1}\right)\big\rangle=0.

(41)

Also, from (
35
) and (
36
), we know that

\displaystyle{\rm D}\mathcal{G}(R,y)[\left(W_{1},z_{1}\right)]={\rm D}\mathcal{G}(R,y)\left[{\rm D}\mathcal{G}(R,y)^{*}[\hat{\lambda}]\right]={\rm D}\mathcal{G}_{\tau}(S,Y)\left[{\rm D}\mathcal{G}_{\tau}(S,Y)^{*}[\hat{\lambda}]\right]

\displaystyle=-2\tilde{\mathcal{K}}\left(UU^{\top},{\rm diag}(VV^{\top})\right)=-2\tilde{\mathcal{K}}\left(HH^{\top},h\circ h\right),

(42)

where the first equality comes from
\left(W_{1},z_{1}\right)={\rm D}\mathcal{G}(R,y)^{*}[\hat{\lambda}]
, and the second equality comes from
\left(S,Y\right)=\left([R,0_{(n-k)\times\tau}],[y,0_{p\times 1}]\right).

Substituting (
41
) and (
42
) into (
40
), we get

\big\langle C,\mathcal{L}_{R}(W_{1})\big\rangle+\big\langle c,2y\circ z_{1}\big\rangle=-2\big\langle\lambda,\tilde{\mathcal{K}}\left(HH^{\top},h\circ h\right)\big\rangle.

(43)

Substituting (
43
) into (
39
), we get

\displaystyle f_{r,\tau}\left({\rm Rtr}_{(S,Y)}\left(t(U,V)\right)\right)

\displaystyle=f_{r}(R,y)+t^{2}\left(\big\langle C_{22},HH^{\top}\big\rangle+\big\langle c,h\circ h\big\rangle-\big\langle\lambda,\tilde{\mathcal{K}}\left(HH^{\top},h\circ h\right)\big\rangle\right)+o(t^{2})

\displaystyle=f_{r}(R,y)+t^{2}\left(\big\langle C_{22}-\tilde{\mathcal{A}}^{*}(\lambda),HH^{\top}\big\rangle+\big\langle c-B^{\top}\lambda,h\circ h\big\rangle\right)+o(t^{2}).

(44)

Note that (
44
) is a local property at
(R,y)\in\Omega_{r}.
Similar to Assumption
2
, we make the assumption below, which says that (
44
) can be extended globally on
\Omega_{r}.

Assumption 3
.

Consider
\tau\in\mathbb{N}_{+}.
There exists
\rho_{h}>0
and
L_{h}\geq 0
such that for any
integer

r
satisfying
\bar{r}\leq r\leq n,

(R,y)\in\Omega_{r},

\displaystyle\left|f_{r,\tau}\left({\rm Rtr}_{(S,Y)}\left((U,V)\right)\right)-\left(f_{r}(R,y)+\big\langle C_{22}-\tilde{\mathcal{A}}^{*}(\lambda),HH^{\top}\big\rangle+\big\langle c-B^{\top}\lambda,h\circ h\big\rangle\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{L_{h}\left(\|H\|^{2}+\|h\|^{2}\right)^{\frac{3}{2}}}{6},

for all
(H,h)\in\mathbb{R}^{(n-k)\times\tau}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}
such that
\sqrt{\|H\|^{2}+\|h\|^{2}}\leq\rho_{h},
where
(S,Y)
and
(U,V)
are defined as in (
38
) and
\lambda
is the unique solution of (
23
).

Assumption
3
is similar to Assumption 3.2 in
[
15
]
. Unlike
[
15
]
, we avoid using the Riemannian Hessian. Also, we fix our search space to be
\mathbb{R}^{(n-k)\times\tau}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}
instead of the whole tangent space
{\rm T}_{(S,Y)}\Omega_{r}^{\tau}.
We remark that Assumption
3
will hold when
\phi
has locally Lipschitz gradient and
\Omega_{r}^{\tau}
is a compact smooth submanifold with a globally defined second order retraction i.e., one can replace
o(t^{2})
with
c_{h}t^{3}
for some constant
c_{h}>0
in (
34
), when
\sqrt{\|U\|^{2}+\|V\|^{2}}=1
and
t\in[-\rho_{h},\rho_{h}].
With Assumption
3
, we can prove the following proposition on how to escape from a saddle point.

Proposition 2
.

Suppose Assumption
1
and Assumption
3
hold. Then there exists
\gamma_{h}>0
such that for any integer
r
satisfying
\bar{r}\leq r\leq n
and
(R,y)\in\Omega_{r},
there exists
t\geq 0
satisfying the following inequality:

f_{r,\tau}\left({\rm Rtr}_{(S,Y)}\left(t(U,V)\right)\right)-f_{r}(R,y)\leq\gamma_{h}\xi\min\{1,\xi^{2}\},

(45)

where
(S,Y)=\left([R,0_{(n-k)\times\tau}],[y,0_{p\times 1}]\right)
,
(U,V)=\left([0_{(n-k)\times r},H],[0_{p\times 1},h]\right)
such that
(H,h)\in\mathbb{R}^{(n-k)\times\tau}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}
is an optimal solution of the following problem:

\xi:=\min\left\{\big\langle C_{22}-\tilde{\mathcal{A}}^{*}(\lambda),HH^{\top}\big\rangle+\big\langle c-B^{\top}\lambda,h\circ h\big\rangle:\ \|H\|^{2}+\|h\|^{2}=1\right\},

(46)

where
\lambda
is the unique solution of (
23
).

Proof.

If
\xi\geq 0,
then let
t=0
and (
45
) holds. Suppose
\xi<0.
From the inequality in Assumption
3

and (
46
), we have that

f_{r,\tau}\left({\rm Rtr}_{(S,Y)}\left(t(U,V)\right)\right)-f_{r}(R,y)\leq\xi t^{2}+\frac{L_{h}}{6}t^{3},

(47)

for any
t\in[-\rho_{h},\rho_{h}].
For any constant
t_{0}>0,

\gamma_{h}^{\prime}\in(0,1)
and
\delta\in(0,1),
let
k

be the smallest number in
\mathbb{N}
such that
t_{k}:=t_{0}\delta^{k}
satisfies the following inequality

f_{r,\tau}\left({\rm Rtr}_{(S,Y)}\left(t_{k}(U,V)\right)\right)-f_{r}(R,y)\leq\gamma_{h}^{\prime}\xi t_{k}^{2}.

(48)

The existence of
k
is guaranteed by (
47
). If
k=0,
then we have that

f_{r,\tau}\left({\rm Rtr}_{(S,Y)}\left(t_{k}(U,V)\right)\right)-f_{r}(R,y)\leq\gamma_{h}^{\prime}\xi t_{0}^{2}.

(49)

If
k>0,
because
k
is the smallest integer satisfying (
48
),
t_{k}/\delta
doesn’t satisfy (
48
). This together with (
47
) implies that

t_{k}/\delta\geq\min\left\{\rho_{h},\ {6(\gamma_{h}^{\prime}-1)\xi}/{L_{h}}\right\}.

(50)

Substituting (
50
) into (
48
), we get

f_{\tau,r}\left({\rm Rtr}_{(S,Y)}\left(t_{k}(U,V)\right)\right)-f_{r}(R,y)\leq\gamma_{h}^{\prime}\delta^{2}\min\left\{\rho_{h}^{2},{36(\gamma_{h}^{\prime}-1)^{2}\xi^{2}}/{L_{h}^{2}}\right\}\xi.

(51)

Combining (
49
) and (
51
), we have that

f_{r,\tau}\left({\rm Rtr}_{(S,Y)}\left(t_{k}(U,V)\right)\right)-f_{r}(R,y)\leq\gamma_{h}\min\{1,\xi^{2}\}\xi,

(52)

where
\gamma_{h}:=\gamma_{h}^{\prime}\min\left\{t_{0}^{2},\delta^{2}\rho_{h}^{2},36(\gamma_{h}^{\prime}-1)^{2}\delta^{2}/L_{h}^{2}\right\}.

Similar to Proposition
1
, the proof of Proposition
2
suggests that we can use Armijo line-search to decrease to the function value sufficiently without knowing
L_{h}
and
\rho_{h}
in advance. Note that after we get
(S_{1},Y_{1}):={\rm Rtr}_{(S,Y)}\left(t(U,V)\right),
we define
(R_{1},y_{1}):=(S_{1},\sqrt{{\rm diag}(Y_{1}Y_{1}^{\top})}).
Then we have that
(R_{1},y_{1})\in\Omega_{r+\tau}
and
f_{r+\tau}(R_{1},y_{1})=f_{r,\tau}(S_{1},Y_{1}).

Now, we are ready to state our rank-support-adaptive feasible method in Algorithm

(R_{i+1},y_{i+1})
to be
(R^{\prime},y^{\prime})
provided that the increase of the function value is controlled by
\epsilon_{i}
. Our reduction technique is motivated by
[
26
]
. In detail, let
\sigma_{1}\geq\sigma_{2}\geq\ldots\geq\sigma_{k}>0
be the nonzero singular values of
R_{i+1}.
Let
r^{\prime}:=\min\{\arg\max\{\sigma_{i}/\sigma_{i+1}:\ i\in[k-1]\}\}.

\sigma_{r^{\prime}}/\sigma_{r^{\prime}+1}>\kappa_{1},

\kappa_{1}>1

\sigma_{r^{\prime}}

(R^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in\Omega_{r^{\prime}}.
The dropped entries of

will always be zero in the Riemannian gradient descent step (see (

one does not have to worry about that the rank and support are over-reduced because the escaping saddle point step allows us to increase the rank and support.

Although the rank reduction technique works quite well in practice and the search rank

is usually within a small constant factor of the rank of the optimal solution, we cannot theoretically exclude the possibility that
r
grows to
n

during the course of the algorithm
even if the rank of the optimal solution

. Recently, Zhang
[
60

established the global guarantee of low-rank BM approach for general unconstrained nonlinear SDP problems. In detail, he proved that in the unconstrained case, for a twice differentiable,
\mu
-strongly convex and

\phi

then a second order stationary point of (SDPR) is also a global optimal solution.
Despite the different problem settings, this result undoubtedly offers some theoretical support for our empirical observations
that we only need to increase
r
to a small constant factor of
r^{*}
in our
algorithm.

Now we state and prove our convergence result.

Theorem 1
.

Suppose Assumption
1
, Assumption
2

3

\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\epsilon_{i}<M
for some constant

Then for any
\epsilon_{g},\epsilon_{h}>0,
Algorithm
1
will return an
(\epsilon_{g},\epsilon_{h})-
stationary within
O(\epsilon_{g}^{-2}+\epsilon_{h}^{-3})
iterations.

Proof.

Suppose Algorithm
1
has not terminated in
K

{\rm fval}_{i}
be the function value at the
i
th iteration. For any
0\leq i<K,
there are two cases to consider.
If
\|{\rm grad}f_{r_{i}}(R_{i},y_{i})\|\geq\epsilon_{g},
then the gradient descent step will be conducted. From (
28
), we have that

{\rm fval}_{i+1}\leq{\rm fval}_{i}-\gamma_{g}\epsilon_{g}\min\{1,\epsilon_{g}\}+\epsilon_{i}.

(53)

If
\|{\rm grad}f_{r_{i}}(R_{i},y_{i})\|<\epsilon_{g}.
Then we know that
\xi_{i}\leq-\epsilon_{h}.
From (
45
) and the step
\left(R_{i+1},y_{i+1}\right)\leftarrow\big(S_{i+1},\sqrt{{\rm diag}\left(Y_{i+1}Y_{i+1}^{\top}\right)}\,\big)
in Algorithm
1
, we have that

{\rm fval}_{i+1}\leq{\rm fval}_{i}-\gamma_{h}\epsilon_{h}\min\{1,\epsilon_{h}^{2}\}+\epsilon_{i}.

(54)

Combining (
53
) and (
54
), we have that

\displaystyle{\rm fval}_{K}-{\rm fval}_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mbox{$\sum_{i=0}^{K-1}$}\left({\rm fval}_{i+1}-{\rm fval}_{i}\right)

(55)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle-K\min\left\{\gamma_{g}\epsilon_{g}\min\{1,\epsilon_{g}\},\gamma_{h}\epsilon_{h}\min\{1,\epsilon_{h}^{2}\}\right\}+\mbox{$\sum_{i=0}^{K-1}$}\epsilon_{i}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle-K\min\left\{\gamma_{g}\epsilon_{g}\min\{1,\epsilon_{g}\},\gamma_{h}\epsilon_{h}\min\{1,\epsilon_{h}^{2}\}\right\}+M.

From (
55
) and
{\rm fval}_{K}\geq\phi^{*},
we have that

\displaystyle K\leq\frac{M+{\rm fval}_{0}-{\rm fval}_{K}}{\min\left\{\gamma_{g}\epsilon_{g}\min\{1,\epsilon_{g}\},\gamma_{h}\epsilon_{h}\min\{1,\epsilon_{h}^{2}\}\right\}}

\displaystyle\leq\left(M+{\rm fval}_{0}-\phi^{*}\right)\left(\frac{1}{\gamma_{g}\epsilon_{g}\min\{1,\epsilon_{g}\}}+\frac{1}{\gamma_{h}\epsilon_{h}\min\{1,\epsilon_{h}^{2}\}}\right)=O(\epsilon_{g}^{-2}+\epsilon_{h}^{-3}).

(56)

Therefore,
K=O(\epsilon_{g}^{-2}+\epsilon_{h}^{-3}).

Our convergence rate
O\left(\epsilon_{g}^{-2}+\epsilon_{h}^{-3}\right)
is better than the rate
of
O\left(\epsilon_{g}^{-2}\epsilon_{h}^{-1}+\epsilon_{h}^{-3}\right)
in Theorem 3.9 of
[
15
]
. This is because our problem (SDPR) has some special structure so that the decrease of function value in Proposition
2
is a function of
\xi
, which is not related to the Riemannian gradient. Note that Theorem
1
requires that
\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\epsilon_{i}<\infty.
This can be achieved in many ways such as increasing the thresholds for rank-support reduction after every iteration and limit the number of rank-support reduction steps. In our inplementation, we set
\kappa_{1}=10
and
\kappa_{2}=10000.
The number of rank-support reductions is always smaller than 20 and so the condition is satisfied.

3.5
Local linear convergence of Algorithm
1

The previous subsection establishes the global sub-linear convergence of Algorithm
1
. In this subsection, we move on to prove the local linear convergence of this algorithm. We need the following quadratic growth condition, which is an extension of the rank
-r
quadratic growth condition in
[
6
]
by Bai et al.

Assumption 4
.

Suppose
(R^{*},y^{*})\in\Omega_{r}
is a
(0,0)-
stationary point of (SDPR),
(R^{*},y^{*})
is said to satisfy the
\left(\alpha,\epsilon\right)-
quadratic growth condition if
{\rm rank}(R^{*})=r
and

\phi\left(X,x\right)\geq\phi\left(X^{*},x^{*}\right)+\frac{\alpha}{2}\left(\|X-X^{*}\|^{2}+\|x-x^{*}\|^{2}\right),

(57)

where
\left(X^{*},x^{*}\right)=\big(\widehat{R}^{*}\widehat{R}^{*\top},y^{*}\circ y^{*}\big)
and
\left(X,x\right)=\big(\widehat{R}\widehat{R}^{\top},y\circ y\big)
for any
(R,y)\in\Omega_{r}
such that
{\rm supp}(y)={\rm supp}(y^{*})
and
\max\{\|R-R^{*}\|,\|y-y^{*}\|\}<\epsilon.

Remark 2
.

We remark that for a linear SDP problem, from Theorem 3.137 in
[
12
]
, the quadratic growth condition is satisfied if and only if the second order sufficient condition is satisfied. From Proposition 15 of
[
22
]
, the second order sufficient condition for a linear SDP
is equivalent to the dual non-degeneracy condition provided that the dual variable is unique.

With the above quadratic growth condition, we have the following lemma, which can be viewed as a manifold version of the Polyak-Lojasiewicz condition
[
32
,
36
]
.

Lemma 2
.

Suppose Assumption
1
holds,
\bar{r}\leq r\leq n
and that
(R^{*},y^{*})\in\Omega_{r}
satisfies the
(\alpha,\epsilon)-
quadratic growth condition with parameters
\alpha,\epsilon>0
. Suppose further that
{\rm grad}f_{r}
is locally Lipschitz on
\Omega_{r}.
Then there exists
\delta,\eta>0
such that for any
(R,y)\in\Omega_{r}
satisfying
\max\left\{\|R-R^{*}\|,\|y-y^{*}\|\right\}<\delta
and
{\rm supp}(y)={\rm supp}(y^{*}),
the following condition holds

\|{\rm grad}f_{r}(R,y)\|^{2}\geq\eta\left(f_{r}(R,y)-f_{r}(R^{*},y^{*})\right).

(58)

Proof.

Let
\left(X^{*},x^{*}\right)=\big(\widehat{R}^{*}\widehat{R}^{*\top},y^{*}\circ y^{*}\big)
and
\left(X,x\right)=\big(\widehat{R}\widehat{R}^{\top},y\circ y\big).
Let
\sigma_{r}(R^{*})
be the smallest singular value of
R^{*}.
Because
{\rm rank}(R^{*})=r,
we have that
\sigma_{r}(R^{*})>0.
Let
\sigma_{\min}(y^{*})
be the smallest positive value of
|y^{*}|
if
\|y^{*}\|>0
and
0
if
\|y^{*}\|=0.
Let
\delta\in(0,\epsilon)
be a constant such that for any
(R,y)\in\Omega_{r}
satisfying
{\rm supp}(y^{*})={\rm supp}(y)
and
\max\left\{\|R-R^{*}\|,\|y-y^{*}\|\right\}<\delta,
the following properties hold.

(i)

Let
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
be the multiplier of
(R,y),
which is the unique solution of (
23
). It holds that

\big\langle C_{22}-\tilde{\mathcal{A}}^{*}(\lambda),(R-R^{*})(R-R^{*})^{\top}\big\rangle\geq-\sigma_{h}\|R-R^{*}\|^{2},\\
\big\langle c-B^{\top}\lambda,\left(y-y^{*}\right)\circ\left(y-y^{*}\right)\big\rangle\geq-\sigma_{h}\|y-y^{*}\|^{2},\hskip 56.9055pt

(59)

where
\sigma_{h}
is a positive number defined as follows

\sigma_{h}:=\begin{cases}\alpha\min\{\sigma_{r}(\widehat{R}^{*})^{2},\sigma_{\min}(y^{*})^{2}\}/8&\sigma_{\min}(y^{*})>0\\
\alpha\sigma_{r}(\widehat{R}^{*})^{2}/8&\sigma_{\min}(y^{*})=0.\end{cases}

(60)

(ii)

\|X-X^{*}\|^{2}\geq 2(\sqrt{2}-1)\sigma_{r}^{2}(\widehat{R}^{*})\|R-R^{*}\|^{2}
and
\|x-x^{*}\|^{2}\geq\sigma_{\min}(y^{*})^{2}\|y-y^{*}\|^{2}.

(iii)

f_{r}(R,y)-f_{r}(R^{*},y^{*})\leq 2\kappa\left(\|R-R^{*}\|^{2}+\|y-y^{*}\|^{2}\right)
for some constant
\kappa>0.

Now we explain why the above three properties can be achieved. Let
\lambda^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
be the multiplier of
(R^{*},y^{*}).
We know that
C_{22}-\tilde{\mathcal{A}}^{*}(\lambda^{*})\in\mathbb{S}^{n-k}_{+}
and
c-B^{\top}\lambda^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}_{+}.
From the continuity of the symmetric positive definite linear system (
23
), we know that
\lambda\rightarrow\lambda^{*}
as
(R,y)\rightarrow(R^{*},y^{*}).
Thus, from the continuity of eigenvalues, property (
59
) can be guaranteed as long as
\delta>0
is small enough. For property (ii), when
\delta>0
is sufficiently small,
R^{\top}R^{*}
will be positive semidefinite. Thus, from Lemma 41 in
[
28
]
, we have that

\|X-X^{*}\|^{2}=\|\widehat{R}\widehat{R}^{\top}-\widehat{R}^{*}\widehat{R}^{*\top}\|^{2}\geq 2(\sqrt{2}-1)\sigma_{r}^{2}(\widehat{R}^{*})\|R-R^{*}\|^{2}.

(61)

Also, since
{\rm supp}(y)={\rm supp}(y^{*}),
we have that when
y
is sufficiently close to
y^{*},
every nonzero entry of
y
has the same sign as that of
y^{*},
which implies the following result

\|x-x^{*}\|^{2}=\|y\circ y-y^{*}\circ y^{*}\|^{2}=\|(y-y^{*})\circ(y+y^{*})\|^{2}\geq\sigma_{\min}(y^{*})^{2}\|y-y^{*}\|^{2}.

(62)

For property (iii), because
\Omega_{r}
is a Riemannian manifold embedded in
\mathbb{R}^{(n-k)\times r}\times\mathbb{R}^{p},
when
\delta>0
is sufficiently small, the geodesic
\gamma:[0,1]\rightarrow\Omega_{r}
from
(R^{*},y^{*})
to
(R,y)
satisfies that

\int_{0}^{1}\|\gamma^{\prime}(t)\|{\rm d}t\leq 2\sqrt{\|R-R^{*}\|^{2}+\|y-y^{*}\|^{2}}.

(63)

Moreover, from the locally Lipschitz continuity of
{\rm grad}f_{r}
and
\|{\rm grad}f_{r}(R^{*},y^{*})\|=0,
we have that for some constant
\kappa>0
,

\|{\rm grad}f_{r}(R,y)\|\leq\kappa\sqrt{\|R-R^{*}\|^{2}+\|y-y^{*}\|^{2}}.

(64)

Using (
63
), (
64
) and the Cauchy-Schwatz inequality, we have that

f_{r}(R,y)-f_{r}(R^{*},y^{*})=\int_{0}^{1}\big\langle{\rm grad}f_{r}(\gamma(t)),\gamma^{\prime}(t)\big\rangle{\rm d}t\\
\leq\int_{0}^{1}\|{\rm grad}f_{r}(\gamma(t))\|\|\gamma^{\prime}(t)\|{\rm d}t\leq 2\kappa\left(\|R-R^{*}\|^{2}+\|y-y^{*}\|^{2}\right),

(65)

which is exactly property (iii). Now, we move on to prove (
58
). Because
\delta\in(0,\epsilon)
and
{\rm supp}(y)={\rm supp}(y^{*}),
from Assumption
4
, we have the following inequality

\big\langle\nabla_{X}\phi(X,x),X-X^{*}\big\rangle+\big\langle\nabla_{x}\phi(X,x),x-x^{*}\big\rangle\geq\phi(X,x)-\phi(X^{*},x^{*})\\
\geq\alpha\left(\|X-X^{*}\|^{2}+\|x-x^{*}\|^{2}\right)/2,

(66)

where the first inequality comes from the convexity of
\phi
. Because
(X^{*},x^{*})
and
(X,x)
are feasible solutions of (SDP), we have that

\big\langle\lambda,\mathcal{A}(X-X^{*})+B(x-x^{*})\big\rangle=0,

(67)

Substituting (
67
) into the left-hand side of (
66
), we get

\big\langle\nabla_{X}\phi(X,x),X-X^{*}\big\rangle+\big\langle\nabla_{x}\phi(X,x),x-x^{*}\big\rangle\\
=\big\langle C-\mathcal{A}^{*}(\lambda),X-X^{*}\big\rangle+\big\langle c-B^{\top}\lambda,x-x^{*}\big\rangle.

(68)

From some simple algebraic manipulations, we get the following two formulas

\displaystyle\quad\widehat{R}\widehat{R}^{\top}-\widehat{R}^{*}\widehat{R}^{*\top}=\widehat{R}\big(\widehat{R}-\widehat{R}^{*}\big)^{\top}+\big(\widehat{R}-\widehat{R}^{*}\big)\widehat{R}^{\top}-\big(\widehat{R}-\widehat{R^{*}}\big)\big(\widehat{R}-\widehat{R}^{*}\big)^{\top}

(69)

\displaystyle\quad y\circ y-y^{*}\circ y^{*}=y\circ(y-y^{*})+(y-y^{*})\circ y-(y-y^{*})\circ(y-y^{*}).

(70)

Substituting (
69
) and (
70
) into (
68
) and using the fact that the first
k
rows of
\widehat{R}-\widehat{R}^{*}
are zero vectors, we get

\displaystyle\big\langle\nabla_{X}\phi(X,x),X-X^{*}\big\rangle+\big\langle\nabla_{x}\phi(X,x),x-x^{*}\big\rangle

(71)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\big\langle J_{n-k,n}\left(C-\mathcal{A}^{*}(\lambda)\right)\widehat{R},R-R^{*}\big\rangle-\big\langle C_{22}-\tilde{\mathcal{A}}^{*}(\lambda),(R-R^{*})(R-R^{*})^{\top}\big\rangle

\displaystyle+\,2\,\big\langle\left(c-B^{\top}\lambda\right)\circ y,y-y^{*}\big\rangle-\big\langle\left(c-B^{\top}\lambda\right),\left(y-y^{*}\right)\circ\left(y-y^{*}\right)\big\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\big\langle{\rm grad}f_{r}(R,y),(R,y)-(R^{*},y^{*})\big\rangle-\big\langle C_{22}-\tilde{\mathcal{A}}^{*}(\lambda),(R-R^{*})(R-R^{*})^{\top}\big\rangle

\displaystyle-\big\langle\left(c-B^{\top}\lambda\right),\left(y-y^{*}\right)\circ\left(y-y^{*}\right)\big\rangle.

From the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we have that

\big\langle{\rm grad}f_{r}(R,y),(R,y)-(R^{*},y^{*})\big\rangle\leq\|{\rm grad}f_{r}(R,y)\|\sqrt{\|R-R^{*}\|^{2}+\|y-y^{*}\|^{2}}.

(72)

Now, apply (
59
) and (
72
) in (
71
), we get

\big\langle\nabla_{X}\phi(X,x),X-X^{*}\big\rangle+\big\langle\nabla_{x}\phi(X,x),x-x^{*}\big\rangle\\
\leq\|{\rm grad}f_{r}(R,y)\|\sqrt{\|R-R^{*}\|^{2}+\|y-y^{*}\|^{2}}+\sigma_{h}\|R-R^{*}\|^{2}+\sigma_{h}\|y-y^{*}\|^{2}.

(73)

From (ii), we have the following inequality

\alpha\left(\|X-X^{*}\|^{2}+\|x-x^{*}\|^{2}\right)/2\\
\geq\alpha\sigma_{r}(\widehat{R}^{*})^{2}\|R-R^{*}\|^{2}/4+\alpha\sigma_{\min}(y^{*})^{2}\|y-y^{*}\|^{2}/4.

(74)

Combining (
66
), (

) and (

), we have that

\|{\rm grad}f_{r}(R,y)\|\sqrt{\|R-R^{*}\|^{2}+\|y-y^{*}\|^{2}}\\
\geq\big(\alpha\sigma_{r}(\widehat{R}^{*})^{2}/4-\sigma_{h}\big)\|R-R^{*}\|^{2}+\left(\sigma_{\min}(y^{*})^{2}/4-\sigma_{h}\right)\|y-y^{*}\|^{2}.

(75)

From (

in (

\|{\rm grad}f_{r}(R,y)\|\sqrt{\|R-R^{*}\|^{2}+\|y-y^{*}\|^{2}}\geq\sigma_{h}\left(\|R-R^{*}\|^{2}+\|y-y^{*}\|^{2}\right).

Note that in deriving the above inequality, when

In this case, the term

can be ignored. From (

\|{\rm grad}f_{r}(R,y)\|\geq\sigma_{h}\sqrt{\|R-R^{*}\|^{2}+\|y-y^{*}\|^{2}},

which can be derived from dividing both sides of (

Theorem 2

Suppose Assumption

holds,
\bar{r}\leq r\leq n
and
\{\left(R_{k},y_{k}\right)\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is the iteration sequence generated from Algorithm

for solving (SDP). Suppose further that
{\rm grad}f_{r}
is locally Lipschitz on
\Omega_{r}.
Moreover, assume that the following conditions hold

(i)

\left(R_{k},y_{k}\right)
converges to some
(0,0)-
stationary point
(R^{*},y^{*})\in\Omega_{r}
that satisfies Assumption
4
with parameters
\alpha,\epsilon>0
.

(ii)

There exists
N\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
such that for any
k\geq N,

\epsilon_{i}=0,

\left(R_{k},y_{k}\right)\in\Omega_{r}
and
{\rm supp}(y_{k})={\rm supp}(y).
Also, the sufficient descent condition (
28
) holds.

Then
f_{r}(R_{k},y_{k})
converges to
f_{r}(R^{*},y^{*})
R-linearly.

Proof.

Because
\left(R_{k},y_{k}\right)
converges to
(R^{*},y^{*})
and
(R_{k},y_{k})\in\Omega_{r}
when

we see that there exists
N_{1}\geq N
such that for any

\max\{\|R_{k}-R^{*}\|,\|y_{k}-y^{*}\|\}<\epsilon
and
\|{\rm grad}f(R_{k},y_{k})\|\leq 1.
From (ii), (
28
) and Lemma
2
, we have that the following inequality holds

f_{r}(R_{k+1},y_{k+1})-f_{r}(R_{k},y_{k})\leq-\gamma_{g}\eta\left(f_{r}(R_{k},y_{k})-f^{*}\right),

(78)

where

and we have used
\|{\rm grad}f_{r}(R_{k},y_{k})\|\leq 1
in the right-hand side of (

). Rearranging (

), we get the following inequality

f_{r}(R_{k+1},y_{k+1})-f^{*}\leq\left(1-\gamma_{g}\eta\right)\left(f_{r}(R_{k},y_{k})-f^{*}\right).

(79)

From (

), we see that if

then

so the algorithm terminates in finite number of

then (

Remark 3
.

Condition (ii) requires us to identify the correct rank and support of the variables

4
Generic smoothness and random perturbation strategy

The algorithmic design and theoretical analysis in the previous section are based on the LICQ property, i.e., Assumption
1
. However, when (SDP) is degenerate, the singularity of the linear system (
23
) will not only slow down the computation of the projection and retraction but also affect the feasibility of the dual variable
\lambda.
In this section, we will discuss how to deal with the degeneracy issue.

4.1
Generic smoothness of (SDPR)

In this subsection, we will show that the LICQ property of (SDPR) will hold as long as some entries of
b
are generic. Our main tool is the following Morse-Sard Theorem (see 1.3 of Chapter 3 in
[

).

Theorem 3

(Morse-Sard Theorem)
.

Let
\mathcal{M},\mathcal{N}
be manifolds of dimension
m,n
and
h:\mathcal{M}\rightarrow\mathcal{N}
a
C^{r}
mapping. If
r>\max\{0,m-n\},

then
h\left(\Sigma_{h}\right)
has measure zero in
\mathcal{N},
where
\Sigma_{h}
is the set of critical points of
h.

In the above theorem, a point
x\in\mathcal{M}
is a critical point of
h
if its differential (also known as pushforward)
{\rm T}_{x}h:{\rm T}_{x}\mathcal{M}\rightarrow{\rm T}_{h(x)}\mathcal{N}
is not surjective. Otherwise,
x
is called a regular point. Here
{\rm T}_{x}\mathcal{M}
and
{\rm T}_{h(x)}\mathcal{N}
are the tangent spaces of
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{N}
at
x
and
h(x)
respectively

8

8

For definitions in manifolds and mappings, please refer to chapter 1 of
[
29
]
.
. With Theorem
3
, we state our result.

Theorem 4
.

Consider the following set

\mathcal{N}(b,d):=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\ \mathcal{F}(x)=b\right\}\cap\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\ \mathcal{G}(x)=d\right\},

where
b\in\mathbb{R}^{p},d\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
and
\mathcal{F}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{p},

\mathcal{G}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{q}
are smooth mappings. Suppose every point in
\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\ \mathcal{F}(x)=b\right\}
satisfies the LICQ property. Then for a generic
d\in\mathbb{R}^{q},
every point in
\mathcal{N}(b,d)
satisfies the LICQ property.

Proof.

Define
\mathcal{M}:=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\ \mathcal{F}(x)=b\right\}.
Consider the mapping
\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{M}}:\mathcal{M}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{q},
which is the restriction of
\mathcal{G}
on
\mathcal{M}.
Because every point in
\mathcal{M}
satisfies LICQ and
\mathcal{F}
is a smooth mapping,
\mathcal{M}
is a smooth submanifold embedded in
\mathbb{R}^{n}.
Since
\mathcal{G}
is a smooth mapping in
\mathbb{R}^{n},
its restriction
\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{M}}
is also smooth. For any
x\in\mathcal{M},
the tangent space of
\mathcal{M}
at
x
is given by the following formula:

{\rm T}_{x}\mathcal{M}=\left\{h\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\ {\rm J}_{\mathcal{F}}(x)h=0\right\},

(80)

where
{\rm J}_{\mathcal{F}}(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times n}
is the Jacobian of
\mathcal{F}
at
x.
It has full row rank because of the LICQ property of
\mathcal{M}
. The differential of
\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{M}}
at
x\in\mathcal{M}
is a linear mapping
{\rm T}_{x}\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{M}}:{\rm T}_{x}\mathcal{M}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{q}
such that for any
h\in{\rm T}_{x}\mathcal{M},

{\rm T}_{x}\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{M}}(h)={\rm J}_{\mathcal{G}}(x)h.

Claim 1
.

For any
x\in\mathcal{M},

x
is a critical point of
\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{M}}
if and only if
x
doesn’t satisfy LICQ in
\mathcal{N}(b,\mathcal{G}(x)).

Proof of claim.
From the definition of a critical point, we know that
x\in\mathcal{M}
is a critical point if and only if following inequality holds

{\rm rank}\left({\rm J}_{\mathcal{G}}(x){\rm J}_{\mathcal{F}}(x)_{\perp}^{\top}{\rm J}_{\mathcal{F}}(x)_{\perp}\right)\leq q-1,

(81)

where
{\rm J}_{\mathcal{F}}(x)_{\perp}\in\mathbb{R}^{(n-p)\times n}
is a row-orthogonal
matrix whose row space is the orthogonal complement of the row space of
{\rm J}_{\mathcal{F}}(x).
We have that

{\rm rank}\left({\rm J}_{\mathcal{G}}(x){\rm J}_{\mathcal{F}}(x)_{\perp}^{\top}{\rm J}_{\mathcal{F}}(x)_{\perp}\right)+{\rm rank}\left({\rm J}_{\mathcal{F}}(x)\right)={\rm rank}\left(\begin{bmatrix}{\rm J}_{\mathcal{G}}(x){\rm J}_{\mathcal{F}}(x)_{\perp}^{\top}{\rm J}_{\mathcal{F}}(x)_{\perp}\\
{\rm J}_{\mathcal{F}}(x)\end{bmatrix}\right)\\
={\rm rank}\left(\begin{bmatrix}{\rm J}_{\mathcal{G}}(x){\rm J}_{\mathcal{F}}(x)_{\perp}^{\top}{\rm J}_{\mathcal{F}}(x)_{\perp}+{\rm J}_{\mathcal{G}}(x)\left(I_{n}-{\rm J}_{\mathcal{F}}(x)_{\perp}^{\top}{\rm J}_{\mathcal{F}}(x)_{\perp}\right)\\
{\rm J}_{\mathcal{F}}(x)\end{bmatrix}\right)={\rm rank}\left(\begin{bmatrix}{\rm J}_{\mathcal{G}}(x)\\
{\rm J}_{\mathcal{F}}(x)\end{bmatrix}\right),

(82)

where the second equality comes from the fact that
I_{n}-{\rm J}_{\mathcal{F}}(x)_{\perp}^{\top}{\rm J}_{\mathcal{F}}(x)_{\perp}
is the projection matrix of the row space of
{\rm J}_{\mathcal{F}}(x).
Because
{\rm J}_{\mathcal{F}}(x)
has full row rank,
{\rm rank}\left({\rm J}_{\mathcal{F}}(x)\right)=p.
Therefore, (
81

{\rm rank}\left(\begin{bmatrix}{\rm J}_{\mathcal{G}}(x)\,;\,{\rm J}_{\mathcal{F}}(x)\end{bmatrix}\right)\leq p+q-1,
which means that
x
doesn’t satisfy LICQ in
\mathcal{N}(b,\mathcal{G}(x)).

From Claim
1
, we know that the set of
d\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
such that
\mathcal{N}(b,d)
contains a singular point is exactly
\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{M}}\left(\Sigma_{\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{M}}}\right),
where
\Sigma_{\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{M}}}
is the set of critical points of
\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{M}}.
From Theorem
3
,
\mathcal{G}\left(\Sigma_{\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{M}}}\right)
has measure zero in
\mathbb{R}^{q}.
Thus, for a generic
d\in\mathbb{R}^{q},
except for a zero-measure set, every point in
\mathcal{N}(b,d)
satisfy LICQ.

Theorem
4
shows that the smoothness of (SDPR) is a generic property. It can also be used to verify that for linear SDP problems, the quadratic growth Assumption

is a generic property, as shown in the next proposition. Note that our

Consider the standard linear SDP problem (

and

the primal and dual constraint nondegeneracy hold everywhere.

Proof.

The dual problem and its factorized version of (

\displaystyle\max\left\{b^{\top}y:\ C-\mathcal{A}^{*}(y)\in\mathbb{S}^{n}_{+},\ y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\right\},

\displaystyle\max\left\{b^{\top}y:\ C-\mathcal{A}^{*}(y)=YY^{\top},\ y\in\mathbb{R}^{m},\ Y\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\right\}.

(84)

From Theorem

Because it has been shown in

4.2
Random perturbation and adaptive preconditioning

Suppose the constraints in (SDPR) can be separated into two parts
[m]=\mathcal{I}\sqcup\mathcal{J}
such that the LICQ of
\{(R,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{(n-k)\times r}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}:\ \mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{I}}(\widehat{R}\widehat{R}^{\top},y\circ y)=b_{\mathcal{I}}\}
is always satisfied, where
b_{\mathcal{I}}
denotes the components of
b
with indices in
\mathcal{I}
;
a similar definition is used for
\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{I}}
.
Then from Theorem
4
, after adding a random perturbation with normal distribution to
b_{\mathcal{J}},
the LICQ will hold everywhere with probability 1. This implies that we don’t have to perturb all the entries of
b
to achieve smoothness. After adding a random perturbation
v\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
such that
\|v\|=\epsilon
to
b
, we are solving a new problem (SDPR
ϵ
), i.e., replacing
b
by
b_{\epsilon}:=b+v
in (SDPR). Let
(R_{\epsilon},y_{\epsilon})
be an
(\epsilon_{g},\epsilon_{h})-
stationary point of (SDPR
ϵ
) and
\lambda_{\epsilon}
be the unique solution of
\eqref{projeq}.
Then
(R_{\epsilon},y_{\epsilon},\lambda_{\epsilon})
satisfies conditions (
25
) and (
26
) because they are not related to
b
explicitly. Moreover, we have that

\big\|\mathcal{K}\big(\widehat{R}_{\epsilon}\widehat{R}_{\epsilon}^{\top},y_{\epsilon}\circ y_{\epsilon}\big)-b\big\|\leq\big\|\mathcal{K}\big(\widehat{R}_{\epsilon}\widehat{R}_{\epsilon}^{\top},y_{\epsilon}\circ y_{\epsilon}\big)-(b+v)\big\|+\|v\|=\epsilon.
This means that the primal feasibility of
(R_{\epsilon},y_{\epsilon})
in (SDPR) can be directly controlled by the size of the random perturbation. Therefore, we can solve (SDPR
ϵ
) to get an approximate KKT solution of the original problem (SDPR).

Remark 4
.

Note that after adding a random perturbation to
b,
the feasible set might become empty. However, we can ensure that the perturbed feasible set is non-empty if the perturbation is sufficiently small, provided (1) the linear mapping
\mathcal{H}:(X,x)\rightarrow\left(\mathcal{K}(X,x),X_{1:k,1:k}\right)
is surjective and (2) Slater’s condition holds for (
3
). The reason is that when the Slater’s condition holds, there will exist a feasible solution

(\tilde{X},\tilde{x})
such that
\tilde{X}
is positive definite and
\tilde{x}>0.
In this case, there exists
\delta_{1}>0
such that such that
{\rm B}_{\delta_{1}}\left(\left(X,x\right)\right)\subset\mathbb{S}^{n}_{+}\cap\mathbb{R}^{p}_{+}.
Because
(X,x)\rightarrow(\mathcal{K}(X,x),X_{1:k,1:k})
is a surjective linear mapping and
\mathcal{H}(\tilde{X},\tilde{x})=\left(b,I_{k}\right)
, we have that there exists
\delta_{2}>0
such that
\left({\rm B}_{\delta_{2}}(b),I_{k}\right)\subset\mathcal{H}({\rm B}_{\delta_{1}}((\tilde{X},\tilde{x}))).
This means as long as the size of the perturbation
\epsilon
is less than
\delta_{2},
the feasible set of SDPR
ϵ

is non-empty.

In practice, the linear system (
23
) might still be ill-conditioned after a small random perturbation. To ameliorate the issue, we will
combine random perturbation with an adaptive preconditioning strategy. The idea is that we use a preconditioned conjugate gradient method (PCG) to solve the linear system (
23
) and set the maximum iteration of PCG to be some integer
{\rm T}_{\rm cg}\geq 2.
In our implementation, we choose
{\rm T}_{\rm cg}
to be 20 when
m<10000
and 50 when
m\geq 10000
. When the number of PCG iterations reaches
{\rm T}_{\rm cg}
for the first time, we add a random perturbation to
b
to ensure smoothness. Before the random perturbation, we use
{\rm Diag}(Q_{i})^{-1}
as the preconditioner of PCG. Every time when the number of PCG iterations reaches
{\rm T}_{\rm cg},
we will update the preconditioner
Q_{i}^{-1}=L^{\top-1}_{i}L^{-1}_{i},
where
Q_{i}\in\mathbb{S}^{m}
is the coefficient matrix of (
23
) in the
i
th iteration and
L_{i}L_{i}^{\top}
is its (sparse) Cholesky factoriztion. By doing so, the number of PCG iterations is upper bounded by
{\rm T}_{\rm cg}.
Moreover, the random perturbation will avoid updating the preconditioner too frequently. This is because from Theorem
4
, the feasible set of (SDPR
ϵ
) becomes a manifold with probability one. If
(R_{i},y_{i})\rightarrow(R_{\epsilon},y_{\epsilon}),
then
Q_{i}
will also converge to some positive definite matrix
Q_{\epsilon}.
Since
Q_{\epsilon}
is positive definite, we know that
Q_{i}^{-1}\rightarrow Q_{\epsilon}^{-1}.
This implies that our preconditioner would not be updated when the iterations is close enough to the optimal solution. After discussing the adaptive preconditioning strategy, we are now able to study the computational complexity of Algorithm
1
.

7
Conclusion

In this paper, we propose a rank-support adaptive feasible
method to solve general convex SDP problems with guaranteed convergence. We apply random perturbation and dual refinement strategies to overcome the numerical difficulty caused by the issue of degeneracy. Our work have significantly extended the application range of feasible method for
solving convex SDP problems.
