Dirichlet and Neumann boundary conditions in a Lattice Boltzmann Method for Elastodynamics
Erik Faust, Alexander Schlüter, Henning Müller, Ralf Müller

Abstract
Recently, Murthy et al. Murthy et al. 2017 and Escande et al. Escande et al. 2020 adopted the Lattice Boltzmann Method (LBM) to model the linear elastodynamic behaviour of isotropic solids. The LBM is attractive as an elastodynamic solver because it can be parallelised readily and lends itself to finely discretised dynamic continuum simulations, allowing transient phenomena such as wave propagation to be modelled efficiently. This work proposes simple local boundary rules which approximate the behaviour of Dirichlet and Neumann boundary conditions with an LBM for elastic solids. Both lattice-conforming and non-lattice-conforming, curved boundary geometries are considered. For validation, we compare results produced by the LBM for the sudden loading of a stationary crack with an analytical solution from Freund 1990 . Furthermore, we investigate the performance of the LBM for the transient tension loading of a plate with a circular hole, using Finite Element (FEM) simulations as a reference.

1
Introduction

As a viable simulation method, the Lattice Boltzmann Method (LBM) was first established in the context of fluid mechanics – in which the LBMs’ distribution functions are most directly subject to physical interpretation, and technologically relevant transient phenomena are commonplace
Escande et al. 2020
. However, solid mechanics also features its share of such phenomena – stress wave superposition and dynamic overshoots in the displacement under dynamic loading can result in significantly higher stresses than those predicted by quasistatic calculations – with far-reaching consequences for mining and crash-proofing, for example
Hashemi and Katsabanis 2020
. Since analytical calculations are often impossible and efficiency is a bottleneck for numerical computation in these cases, researchers have recently begun developing LBM algorithms for solid simulation: Marconi et al.
Marconi and Chopard 2003
approximated crack propagation with one such algorithm, and O’Brien et al.
O’Brien et al. 2012
based another on a wave equation for Poisson solids to model wave propagation. More recently, Murthy et al.
Murthy et al. 2017
and Escande et al.
Escande et al. 2020
extended the solid LBM to the more general class of isotropic, linear elastic solids. Given further developments, it may become feasible to perform coupled fluid-structure simulations using the LBM as both a fluid and a solid solver.

Thus, the groundwork for LBM-based transient solid simulations has been laid, but much work remains to be done if LBM algorithms are to become viable tools for applied solid mechanics, or for fluid-structure simulation. While efficient, these algorithms are not stable for all relevant combinations of material parameters and loads
Escande et al. 2020
. Additionally, only rudimentary boundary conditions – for periodic, free, and fixed boundaries, all of which must be lattice-conforming – may be modelled currently
Escande et al. 2020
.

This work takes aim at the latter obstacle. Simple local bounce-back-type boundary rules which may be used to model arbitarily valued Dirichlet and Neumann boundaries in the solid case are presented for both lattice-conforming and arbitrary geometries.

Before the formulation of these boundary rules are addressed, however, section
2
discusses the reformulation of the underlying solid mechanical equations required by the LBM. The Lamé-Navier equation is restated as a moment chain and boundary conditions are rephrased accordingly. Section
3
outlines the Lattice Boltzmann Method for solids briefly, and section
4
presents the novel boundary rules. In section
5
, we validate the LBM algorithm and the boundary rules against one analytical and one numerical benchmark problem.
While the modified boundary rules are easily generalised, for ease of visualisation and validation we limit the discussion to two-dimensional (plane strain) problems here.

2.1
A moment chain of conservation laws

Under certain smallness assumptions
2
2

2

We have not found a thorough discussion of these assumptions in the literature. An in-depth investigation of the solid-mechanical assumptions underlying the solid LBM would be interesting, but would also expand the scope of this publication considerably. Broadly speaking, terms beyond the leading order of smallness in the displacement derivatives are neglected in each equation in (
4
).
, the Lamé-Navier equation (
1
) can be rephrased as a moment chain
Murthy et al. 2017

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho+\partial_{\alpha}j_{\alpha}=0\,,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}j_{\alpha}+\partial_{\beta}P_{\alpha\beta}=F_{\alpha}+\frac{\mu-\lambda}{\rho}\partial_{\alpha}\rho\,,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}P_{\alpha\beta}+\partial_{\gamma}\Big(\frac{\mu}{\rho}(j_{\alpha}\delta_{\beta\gamma}+j_{\beta}\delta_{\alpha\gamma}+j_{\gamma}\delta_{\beta\alpha})\Big)=0\,.

(4)

Therein,
\bm{j}=\rho\partial_{t}\bm{u}
denotes the linear momentum density and
\bm{P}
is a stress tensor which is generally not equivalent to the established Cauchy stress
\bm{\sigma}
.
In (
4
), the first equation accounts for the conservation of mass, the second for the balance of linear momentum, and the third for the material law.
Note that this moment chain is somewhat inconsistent with formal small strain theory, which assumes the current and reference configurations to coincide and the current density
\rho
and the reference density
\rho_{0}
to be identical
(
Poruchikov 1993
, p.4)
.
However, the difference this introduces is of third order in the displacement derivatives and becomes negligible for infinitesimal deformations.

Mathematically, each equation in (
4
) contains the time derivative of a tensor of order
N
and the divergence of a tensor of order
N+1
on the left, as well as an
N
-th order source term on the right hand side. Farag et al.
Farag et al. 2021
demonstrated that the LBM acts as a second-order Crank-Nicholson scheme for moment chains with precisely this structure, with the tensors appearing on the left hand side of (
4
) –
\rho
,
j
, and
P
– being accounted for as moments in the LBM.
This explains why Murthy et al.
Murthy et al. 2017
and Escande et al.
Escande et al. 2020
had recourse to the alternative stress tensor
\bm{P}
and the artificial bulk force term
\frac{\mu-\lambda}{\rho}\partial_{\alpha}\rho
in their LBM algorithms:
the Cauchy stress
\bm{\sigma}
can not be made to fit the moment chain in (
4
) as elegantly as
\bm{P}
. To model
\bm{\sigma}
directly, an additional term in (
11
) or an additional source term would be required.

Via the third expression of the moment chain

-\partial_{t}P_{\alpha\beta}=\partial_{\gamma}\Big(\frac{\mu}{\rho}(j_{\alpha}\delta_{\beta\gamma}+j_{\beta}\delta_{\alpha\gamma}+j_{\gamma}\delta_{\beta\alpha})\Big)\,,

the stress tensors are related by

\displaystyle-\partial_{t}P_{\alpha\beta}

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}\Big(\mu(\partial_{\beta}u_{\alpha}+\partial_{\alpha}u_{\beta}+\partial_{\gamma}u_{\gamma}\delta_{\beta\alpha})\Big)

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}\Big(\lambda\partial_{\gamma}u_{\gamma}\delta_{\alpha\beta}+\mu(\partial_{\beta}u_{\alpha}+\partial_{\alpha}u_{\beta})-(\lambda-\mu)\partial_{\gamma}u_{\gamma}\delta_{\alpha\beta}\Big)

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}\Big(\sigma_{\alpha\beta}-(\lambda-\mu)\partial_{\gamma}u_{\gamma}\delta_{\alpha\beta}\Big)\,,

(5)

and, assuming an adequate initialisation
3
3

3

The initialisation must fulfil
P_{\alpha\beta}(x,0)=-\sigma_{\alpha\beta}(x,0)+(\lambda-\mu)\partial_{\gamma}u_{\gamma}(x,0)\delta_{\alpha\beta}
.

P_{\alpha\beta}=-\sigma_{\alpha\beta}+(\lambda-\mu)\partial_{\gamma}u_{\gamma}\delta_{\alpha\beta}\,,

(6)

i.e. the stress tensor
\bm{P}
is equivalent to a negated Cauchy stress tensor
\bm{\sigma}
, with an additional displacement divergence term with coefficient
\lambda-\mu
. The latter disappears for Poisson solids with
\mu=\lambda
(Poisson’s ratio
\nu=\nicefrac{{1}}{{4}}
), which is why it is termed the
Poisson stress tensor
in the following.

Thus, the Lamé-Navier equation - which is commonly used to model the dynamic behaviour of isotropic solids subject to small displacements - can be rephrased as a moment chain with respect to the current density
\rho
, the linear momentum density
\bm{j}
, and the Poisson stress
\bm{P}
, under the assumptions mentioned above. The LBM acts as a solver for moment chains like (
4
), and may therefore be used to tackle isotropic, small-displacement elastodynamics.

2.2
Rephrasing the boundary conditions

In accordance with the reformulation of the Lamé-Navier equation as a moment chain in
\rho
,
\bm{j}
, and
\bm{P}
, boundary conditions (BCs) must of course also be rephrased in terms of these variables.
For Dirichlet boundary conditions, this is comparatively straightforward: with the definition of the linear momentum density
\bm{j}=\rho\partial_{t}\bm{u}
4
4

4

This definition, once more, holds only under smallness assumptions which allow convective terms to be neglected.
, boundary values in the linear momentum density can be obtained from boundary values in the displacement

j(\bm{x},t)=j^{*}(\bm{x},t)=\rho(\bm{x},t)\partial_{t}u^{*}(\bm{x},t),\quad\bm{x}\in\partial\Omega\,.

(7)

With (
6
), boundary values in the Poisson stress tensor could be computed from boundary values in the Cauchy stress tensor and from values of the displacement at the boundary, via

\displaystyle P_{\alpha\beta}(\bm{x},t)=P_{\alpha\beta}^{*}(\bm{x},t)=-\sigma_{\alpha\beta}^{*}(\bm{x},t)+(\lambda-\mu)\partial_{\gamma}u_{\gamma}(\bm{x},t)\delta_{\alpha\beta}\quad\bm{x}\in\partial\Omega\,.

However, Neumann boundary conditions enforce boundary values in the Cauchy stress vector
\bm{t}^{*}
rather than the Cauchy stress tensor
\bm{\sigma}^{*}
. Furthermore, the displacement
\bm{u}
does not appear in the moment chain (
4
), and is thus not directly available in the LBM algorithm.

The latter of these issues may be circumvented by integrating the linear momentum density
\bm{j}=\rho\partial_{t}\bm{u}
to obtain the displacement
\bm{u}
. Alternatively, one may approximate the divergence of the displacement
\partial_{\alpha}u_{\alpha}
with the first-order accurate kinematic relation

\partial_{\alpha}u_{\alpha}\approx\frac{\rho_{0}-\rho}{\rho_{0}}\,,

(8)

as demonstrated in appendix
A
.

Meanwhile, Neumann boundary conditions can be transformed into a coordinate system normal to the boundary with a rotation matrix
\bm{T}

t_{\alpha}^{*n}(x,t)=T_{\beta\alpha}t_{\beta}^{*}(x,t)\quad x\in\partial\Omega\,.

The entries of the orthonormal transformation tensor
\bm{T}
are simply given by the normal tangential vectors
\bm{e}^{n}
and
\bm{e}^{t}
of the boundary, i.e.
T_{1\alpha}=e_{\alpha}^{n}
and
T_{2\alpha}=e_{\alpha}^{t}
for the two-dimensional case
(
Holzapfel 2000
, p.28)
.

For the two-dimensional case, (
3
) becomes, in this normal coordinate system

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\sigma_{nn}^{n}&\tau_{nt}^{n}\\
\tau_{nt}^{n}&\sigma_{tt}^{n}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}t_{n}^{*n}\\
t_{t}^{*n}\end{pmatrix}\,.

As is apparent, the first column of the Cauchy stress tensor
\bm{\sigma}^{n}
is fully determined by the Cauchy stress vector to be forced upon the boundary, i.e.

\displaystyle\sigma_{nn}^{n}

\displaystyle=t_{n}^{*n}\,,

\displaystyle\tau_{nt}^{n}

\displaystyle=t_{t}^{*n}\,,

meaning that, with the symmetry of the Cauchy tensor, the Neumann boundary condition determines all entries of
\bm{\sigma}^{n}
save for
\sigma_{tt}^{n}
. This remaining entry remains unaffected by the Neumann boundary condition and may instead be extrapolated from the material behaviour in the vicinity of the relevant boundary.

Thus, we obtain boundary values for the Cauchy stress
\bm{\sigma}^{*n}
in the normal coordinate system, and an inverse transformation yields boundary values for
\bm{\sigma}^{*}
in the original coordinate system,

\sigma_{\alpha\beta}^{*}(x,t)=T_{\alpha\gamma}\sigma_{\gamma\zeta}^{*n}(x,t)T_{\beta\zeta}\quad x\in\partial\Omega\,.

(9)

Using (
6
), we finally obtain the desired boundary values for the Poisson stress tensor
\bm{P}

\displaystyle P_{\alpha\beta}(\bm{x},t)=P_{\alpha\beta}^{*}(\bm{x},t)=-\sigma_{\alpha\beta}^{*}(\bm{x},t)+(\lambda-\mu)\partial_{\gamma}u_{\gamma}(\bm{x},t)\delta_{\alpha\beta}\quad\bm{x}\in\partial\Omega\,.

(10)

where
\bm{\sigma}^{*}
is determined via (
9
) and the displacement divergence may be obtained from (
8
). With (
7
) and (
10
), Dirichlet and Neumann boundary conditions for elastodynamic problems described by the Lamé-Navier equation can now be stated consistently with the moment chain form, completing the problem transformation which allows the LBM to tackle elastodynamic problems.

3
The solid LBM in a nutshell

For the sake of compactness and to make this work self-contained, we resort to a very brief outline of the LBM’s key features here.
An excellent in-depth discussion can be found in
Krüger et al. 2017
.
In keeping with
Farag et al. 2021
, we treat the Lattice Boltzmann algorithm as a (rather clever) numerical solver for PDEs described by moment chains, independently from any associations with kinetic theory
5
5

5

In this view, the distribution functions
\bar{f}_{i}
with which the LBM works are merely supplementary variables containing information about the tensors to be modelled (the current mass density
\rho
, linear momentum density
j
, and Poisson stress
P
, in this case).
.

The algorithm operates on a lattice consisting of regularly spaced lattice sites
\bm{x}\in L\subset\Omega
(the dark grey points in figure
1
) which are in turn connected by lattice links (the light grey lines in the same diagram)
(
Krüger et al. 2017
, p.94)
. Furthermore, several lattice velocity vectors
\bm{c}_{i},i\in 0,..,q-1
(indicated by the blue arrows in figure
1
) cover the distance between any lattice site
\bm{x}
and its neighbours
\bm{x}+\bm{c}_{i}\Delta t
along a lattice link
i
in one time step
\Delta t

(
Krüger et al. 2017
, p.94)
. The simplicity of this spatial discretisation makes for easy pre- and post-processing.

Generally, LBM discretisations are classed by the dimension
d
and the number of lattice velocities
q
, as DdQq lattices. For ease of visualisation and validation, we only consider D2Q9 lattices - exemplified in figure
1
- here. Note that among the
q=9
lattice velocities, there is a zero velocity
\bm{c}_{0}=\bm{0}
.

Figure 1:
(Part of) a D2Q9 lattice for the LBM.

The LBM encapsulates information about the tensors to be modelled (the current density
\rho
, linear momentum density
\bm{j}
, and Poisson stress
\bm{P}
in our case) in distribution functions.
On each site of the lattice, a distribution function vector with one entry
\bar{f}_{i}
per lattice velocity
\bm{c}_{i}
is introduced
(
Krüger et al. 2017
, p.63)
. Here and in the following, we utilise distribution functions resulting from the second-order accurate discretisation by He et al.
He et al. 1998
, which is denoted by the overbar
\bar{\cdot}
.

In each iteration, the distribution functions
\bar{f}_{i}
at each lattice site are locally relaxed toward the value of the equilibrium distribution function
f_{i}^{\text{eq}}

(
Krüger et al. 2017
, p.64)
, and a contribution due to a source term is added
(
Krüger et al. 2017
, p.239)
. The choice of equilibrium distribution function determines the PDE modelled by the LBM algorithm - as shown in
Murthy et al. 2017
and
Escande et al. 2020
, the expression

\displaystyle f_{i}^{\text{eq}}=w_{i}\Big(\rho+\frac{1}{c_{s}^{2}}c_{i\alpha}j_{\alpha}+\frac{1}{2c_{s}^{4}}(P_{\alpha\beta}-\rho c_{s}^{2}\delta_{\alpha\beta})(c_{i\alpha}c_{i\beta}-c_{s}^{2}\delta_{\alpha\beta})\Big)

(11)

may be used to recover the Lamé-Navier equation. Here,
c_{s}=\sqrt{\nicefrac{{\mu}}{{\rho}}}
denotes the speed of shear waves, and
w_{i}
is a lattice weight associated with lattice link
i

Escande et al. 2020
.
The source term

\psi_{i}=\frac{1}{c_{s}^{2}}w_{i}c_{i\alpha}S_{\alpha}\,,

(12)

meanwhile, is used to model bulk forces, where

S_{\alpha}=F_{\alpha}+\frac{\mu-\lambda}{\rho}\partial_{\alpha}\rho

(13)

accounts both for the volumetric loads
\bm{F}
and the part of the material law not contained in the Poisson stress tensor
Escande et al. 2020
- recall the moment chain (
4
).

In the solid LBM algorithm by Murthy et al.
Murthy et al. 2017
and Escande et al.
Escande et al. 2020
, a BGK collision operator with relaxation time
\bar{\tau}
is utilised. Together with the contribution due to the source term, this results in the following expression for the post-collision (i.e., post-relaxation) distribution functions
(
Krüger et al. 2017
, p.239)

\bar{f}_{i}^{\text{col}}=\bar{f}_{i}-\frac{\Delta t}{\bar{\tau}}(\bar{f}_{i}-f_{i}^{\text{eq}})+\Delta t\Big(1-\frac{\Delta t}{2\bar{\tau}}\Big)\psi_{i}\,,

(14)

where the value of
\bar{\tau}
is crucial to the stability of the method (and the accuracy of boundary condition modelling), with Escande et al.
Escande et al. 2020
using
\bar{\tau}=0.55\Delta t
. The locality of this collision step makes parallelisation relatively easy
(
Krüger et al. 2017
, p.579)
, contributing to the efficiency which makes the LBM attractive as a PDE solver.

The post-collision distribution functions are subsequently propagated to neighbouring lattice sites along the associated lattice velocities
\bm{c}_{i}
in the streaming step
(
Krüger et al. 2017
, p.66)

\bar{f}_{i}(\bm{x}+\bm{c}_{i}\Delta t,t+\Delta t)=\bar{f}_{i}^{\text{col}}\,.

(15)

Together, the collision and streaming steps define the Lattice Boltzmann equation

\displaystyle\bar{f}_{i}(\bm{x}+\bm{c}_{i}\Delta t,t+\Delta t)=\bar{f}_{i}-\frac{\Delta t}{\bar{\tau}}(\bar{f}_{i}-f_{i}^{\text{eq}})+\Delta t\Big(1-\frac{\Delta t}{2\bar{\tau}}\Big)\psi_{i}\,,

(16)

which describes the evolution of the distribution functions on the lattice
(
Krüger et al. 2017
, p.239)
. From the distribution functions, the desired tensors may finally be post-processed as moments

\displaystyle\rho

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\bar{f}_{i}\,,

\displaystyle j_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\bar{f}_{i}c_{i\alpha}+\frac{1}{2}\Delta tS_{\alpha}\,,

\displaystyle P_{\alpha\beta}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\bar{f}_{i}c_{i\alpha}c_{i\beta}\,.

(17)

Consult
Farag et al. 2021
for an enlightening discussion of why this works.

4
Local boundary rules for the solid LBM

While the LBE accounts for the evolution of the distribution functions in the interior of the lattice, the collision and streaming steps leave some distribution functions in the vicinity of the boundary
\partial\Omega\subset\Omega
undefined. As illustrated in figure
2
, these need to be ‘streamed’ from across the boundary to each boundary lattice site
\bm{x}\in\partial L
, i.e., each lattice site with lattice links crossing the boundary
(
Krüger et al. 2017
, p.165)
. For convenience, let the set of boundary lattice sites
\partial L\subset L
be divided into
\partial L_{u}
and
\partial L_{t}
depending on whether the nearby boundary is subject to Dirichlet or Neumann boundary values, respectively
6
6

6

Distinctions between boundaries can also be made on a link-by-link basis (as is done in the numerical experiments in section
5
), but this text uses the simpler site-by-site approach to simplify explanations.
.

Figure 2:
Lattice-conforming boundary (dotted line) with lattice velocities leading out of the domain (blue) and lattice velocities for which distribution functions are missing (red) for a boundary lattice site at
\bm{x}
.

Specifying the missing distribution functions in accordance with the boundary conditions of the underlying PDE is a non-trivial task: while boundary conditions are formulated in terms of the tensors appearing in the moment chain (
\rho
,
\bm{j}
,
\bm{P}
), a boundary rule specification with respect to
\bar{f}_{i}
is sought
(
Krüger et al. 2017
, p.155)
. Furthermore, the number of unknown distribution functions
\bar{f}_{i}
at some
\bm{x}\in\partial L
is frequently higher than the number of boundary condition equations available to specify them, leaving boundary rules for the LBM under-determined
(
Krüger et al. 2017
, p.155)
. Additionally, the non-trivial reconciliation of regular lattices with arbitrary bounding geometries complicates work with BCs
Jahanshaloo et al. 2016
.

4.1
Lattice-conforming boundaries

As a first step, Escande et al.
Escande et al. 2020
appropriated the popular bounce-back
(
Krüger et al. 2017
, p.175)
and anti-bounce-back
(
Krüger et al. 2017
, p.200)
rules from the fluid case, to model lattice-conforming fixed and free boundaries respectively.
In the scope of this work, these rules were extended, to approximate the behaviour in the vicinity of boundaries with arbitrary Dirichlet and Neumann boundary conditions.

The modified bounce-back rule

\bar{f}_{\bar{i}}(\bm{x},t+\Delta t)=\bar{f}_{i}^{\text{col}}(\bm{x},t)-\frac{2}{c_{s}^{2}}w_{i}c_{i\alpha}j_{\alpha}^{*}\,,

(18)

first reverses the direction of motion for the post-collision distribution functions
\bar{f}_{i}^{\text{col}}(\bm{x},t)
which would be streamed out of the bulk: the missing distribution functions
\bar{f}_{\bar{i}}(\bm{x},t+\Delta t)
for directions
\bar{i}
are set to the values of the post-collision distribution functions
\bar{f}_{i}^{\text{col}}(\bm{x},t)
associated with the opposite direction
i
. Then, a term depending on the linear momentum density boundary values
\bm{j}^{*}
is subtracted, and the distribution functions are sent back into the interior of the lattice, in direction
\bar{i}
.
The distribution function
\bar{f}_{\bar{5}}(\bm{x},t+\Delta t)=\bar{f}_{7}(\bm{x},t+\Delta t)
in figure
2
, for example, is set to the value of the post-collision distribution function
\bar{f}_{5}^{\text{col}}(\bm{x},t)
, minus the boundary value term
\frac{2}{c_{s}^{2}}w_{5}c_{5\alpha}j_{\alpha}^{*}
.

The required boundary values in the linear momentum density
\bm{j}^{*}
can be obtained from Dirichlet boundary values in the displacement
\bm{u}^{*}
via (
7
). In
(
Krüger et al. 2017
, p.208-210)
a derivation of the bounce-back boundary rule in the Chapman-Enskog framework is outlined.
Notably, the change in the distribution functions
\bar{f}_{\bar{i}}
from one iteration to the next is assumed to be negligible in this derivation, which may give rise to unexpected behaviours when highly transient phenomena in the vicinity of the boundary are considered.

The modified anti-bounce-back boundary rule - used here to model Neumann boundaries - operates similarly. The sign of the post-collision distribution function
\bar{f}_{i}^{\text{col}}(\bm{x},t)
at
\bm{x}\in\partial L_{t}
is first inverted and a contribution depending on the current density
\rho^{\text{bd}}
and the Poisson stress
\bm{P}^{*}
at the boundary is added, before the distribution functions are sent back into the direction from whence they came

\displaystyle\bar{f}_{\bar{i}}(\bm{x},t+\Delta t)=-\bar{f}_{i}^{\text{col}}(\bm{x},t)+2w_{i}\Big(\rho^{\text{bd}}+\frac{1}{2c_{s}^{4}}(P_{\alpha\beta}^{*}-\rho^{\text{bd}}c_{s}^{2}\delta_{\alpha\beta})(c_{i\alpha}c_{i\beta}-c_{s}^{2}\delta_{\alpha\beta})\Big)\,.

(19)

While in
Escande et al. 2020
, the current density and Poisson stress are extrapolated normal to the boundary, we use (
10
) to determine boundary values in the Poisson stress tensor
\bm{P}^{*}
from Neumann boundary conditions on the Cauchy traction vector
\bm{t}^{*}
. The current density at the boundary
\rho^{\text{bd}}
, meanwhile, is extrapolated along the current lattice link
i
to
\bm{x}^{\text{bd}}=\bm{x}+\frac{1}{2}\bm{c}_{i}\Delta t
,

\rho^{\text{bd}}=\frac{1}{2}\Big(3\rho(\bm{x},t)-\rho(\bm{x}-c_{i}\Delta t,t)\Big)\approx\rho(\bm{x}^{\text{bd}},t)\,.

Figure
2
indicates, using
i=1
as an example, how the location of the boundary lattice site
\bm{x}
, neighbouring lattice site
\bm{x}-\bm{c}_{i}\Delta t
, and boundary site
\bm{x}^{\text{bd}}=\bm{x}+\frac{1}{2}\bm{c}_{i}\Delta t
are defined in the lattice-conforming case.

The (admittedly hardly intuitive) link between the anti-bounce-back rule and Neumann boundary conditions may also be derived in the Chapman-Enskog framework. The assumptions and caveats entering this derivation are analogous to those relevant for the bounce-back case.

5
Numerical and analytical validation

As demonstrated by Farag et al.
Farag et al. 2021
, the Lattice Boltzmann Method should act as a second-order Crank-Nicolson scheme for the moment chain in (
4
), which suggests that the LBM can be used as a numerical solver for problems described by the Lamé-Navier equation, Dirichlet- and Neumann boundary conditions, and bulk forces.

This section considers two benchmark examples to verify whether the LBM algorithm and the boundary rules presented above can be used to approach elastodynamic problems in practice.
In the latter of these examples, we consider discontinuous behaviour in time and space to explore the limits of the resulting simulation method.

As our aim is to explore the validity of the LBM as a PDE solver for boundary value problems on the Lamé-Navier equation, we do not consider the behaviour of solids observed in experiments, but instead that of ideal (linear) elastic continua as described by analytical and numerical solutions of the underlying PDE. However, the parsimonious anticipation of real-world phenomena is the fundamental objective of solid mechanics, and this section in some sense performs a verification to this end.

6
Conclusions

In this work, simple local boundary rules for the LBM algorithm by Murthy et al.
Murthy et al. 2017
and Escande et al.
Escande et al. 2020
were proposed. These approximate the behaviour of Dirichlet and Neumann boundary conditions, both for the straightforward lattice-conforming case and for the more complicated case of arbitrary geometries.

In section
2
, the Lamé-Navier equation and the associated boundary conditions were first rephrased in moment chain form. The relationship between the Poisson stress tensor
\bm{P}
appearing in this moment chain and the established Cauchy stress tensor
\bm{\sigma}
was explored, and a convenient formula for the calculation of one from the other derived.
Section
3
then very briefly outlined the Lattice Boltzmann Method for solids, and section
4
presented the boundary rules used to model arbitrarily valued Dirichlet and Neumann boundary conditions with arbitrary geometries.
In section
5
, the LBM for elastic solids and the novel boundary rules were finally validated against one analytical and one numerical benchmark problem.

However, open issues remain. The solid LBM and the modified boundary rules are not unconditionally stable.
Furthermore, the boundary rules are not necessarily valid for all relevant time-dependent boundary values. This may explain the slightly ‘softer’ behaviour exhibited by the LBM for highly transient simulations.
Moreover, the de facto simulated location of the boundary is offset slightly from the intended location, lattice-conforming boundary rules are second-order accurate in space in the best case, and non-lattice-conforming boundary rules only allow for first-order accuracy.

Beyond this, the algorithm outlined here only facilitates the modelling of materially and geometrically linear solid behaviour. For an extension to the nonlinear regime, nonlinear terms in the material law must be accounted for. Furthermore, a formulation in the reference configuration would be desirable to simplify boundary modelling under large deformations.

On the other hand, the LBM seems promising as an elastodynamic solver: the locality of its nonlinearity and the linearity of its non-locality
(
Krüger et al. 2017
, p.55)
make the LBM exceptionally efficient and easily parallelizable.
In particular, the LBM lends itself to modelling dynamic phenomena which call for fine spatial and temporal resolutions – an area of application in which computational efficiency becomes crucial.
Furthermore, spatially discretising - ‘meshing’ - a material domain is comparatively easy. If the solid LBM can be turned into a practicable simulation method, much time might be saved in engineering design processes.

The numerical solutions to the dynamic example problems considered here are encouraging: the results produced by the LBM are in good agreement with analytical and numerical benchmark results.

However, a need for further development is apparent in several key areas. Firstly, the stability of the method must be improved. Schemes with multiple relaxation times
(
Krüger et al. 2017
, p.407)
and multi-reflection boundary rules
Ginzburg and d’Humières 2003
seem promising first places to start. Furthermore, high-frequency waves could be filtered
Escande et al. 2020
, or distribution functions ‘reset’ regularly.
Second-order accurate non-lattice-conforming boundary rules could further improve the performance of the LBM on problems with complicated bounding geometries. Finally, extensions to material nonlinearity and large deformations are desirable.

If these shortcomings are addressed, however, fascinating areas of application beckon: the LBM could be used to study stresses caused by the propagation, reflection, and superposition of waves under highly dynamic loading. Crashproofing and mining are fields that might benefit from such studies. If an extension to the nonlinear regime can be achieved, simulations of polymer components (such as seals and tires) or tissue (such as muscle) under transient loads further become feasible. Here, also, the LBM might be useful on account of its efficiency and ease of parallelization.

Appendix A
An approximation for the displacement divergence

For finite strains, the current density is coupled to the kinematic description of the continuum via the volume ratio
J
, i.e. the determinant of the deformation gradient
F
,
(
Holzapfel 2000
, p.74)

\displaystyle J

\displaystyle=\det F=\prod_{\vartheta=1}^{d}F_{(\vartheta\vartheta)}=\prod_{\vartheta=1}^{d}\frac{\partial x_{(\vartheta)}}{\partial X_{(\vartheta)}}

\displaystyle=\prod_{\vartheta=1}^{d}\Big(1+\frac{\partial u_{(\vartheta)}}{\partial X_{(\vartheta)}}\Big)=\frac{dV}{dV_{0}}=\frac{\rho_{0}}{\rho}\,,

(25)

where
d
denotes the dimension of the continuum, and
\vartheta
is the coordinate index in the principal coordinate system (summation over this index is not implied). For
d=3
, expansion yields

\displaystyle J=

\displaystyle 1+\sum_{\vartheta=1}^{3}\frac{\partial u_{(\vartheta)}}{\partial X_{(\vartheta)}}

\displaystyle+\sum_{\iota=1}^{3}\sum_{\kappa=1}^{\iota-1}\frac{\partial u_{(\iota)}}{\partial X_{(\iota)}}\frac{\partial u_{(\kappa)}}{\partial X_{(\kappa)}}+\prod_{\vartheta=1}^{3}\frac{\partial u_{(\vartheta)}}{\partial X_{(\vartheta)}}\,.

Due to the invariance of the trace, the second term may also be written in terms of the original (non-principal) coordinate system, and

\displaystyle J=

\displaystyle 1+\frac{\partial u_{\alpha}}{\partial X_{\alpha}}

\displaystyle+\sum_{\iota=1}^{3}\sum_{\kappa=1}^{\iota-1}\frac{\partial u_{(\iota)}}{\partial X_{(\iota)}}\frac{\partial u_{(\kappa)}}{\partial X_{(\kappa)}}+\prod_{\vartheta=1}^{3}\frac{\partial u_{(\vartheta)}}{\partial X_{(\vartheta)}}\,.

(26)

where we make use of Einstein summation convention again.

Meanwhile, the displacement gradient
\bm{H}=\nicefrac{{\partial\bm{u}}}{{\partial\bm{X}}}
is linked to the derivative of the displacement
\nicefrac{{\partial\bm{u}}}{{\partial\bm{x}}}
in the current configuration
\Omega
by

\displaystyle H_{\alpha\beta}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial u_{\alpha}}{\partial X_{\beta}}=\frac{\partial u_{\alpha}}{\partial x_{\gamma}}\frac{\partial x_{\gamma}}{\partial X_{\beta}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial u_{\alpha}}{\partial x_{\gamma}}\Big(\delta_{\beta\gamma}+\frac{\partial u_{\gamma}}{\partial X_{\beta}}\Big)\,.

(27)

If the displacement derivatives
\nicefrac{{\partial\bm{u}}}{{\partial\bm{x}}}
and
\nicefrac{{\partial\bm{u}}}{{\partial\bm{X}}}
are sufficiently small, lower-order terms in them dominate terms at a higher order of smallness (see, e.g.
(
Landau et al. 1986
, p.3)
). By substituting (
27
) into (
26
) and considering only terms at the first order of smallness, we obtain

J=\frac{\rho_{0}}{\rho}=1+\frac{\partial u_{\alpha}}{\partial x_{\alpha}}=1+\partial_{\alpha}u_{\alpha}\,,

and thus

\partial_{\alpha}u_{\alpha}=\frac{\rho_{0}}{\rho}-1=-\frac{\rho-\rho_{0}}{\rho}\approx-\frac{\rho-\rho_{0}}{\rho_{0}}\,,

where the density fluctuation in the denominator is neglected as
\rho=\nicefrac{{\rho_{0}}}{{1+\partial_{\alpha}u_{\alpha}}}
is dominated by the constant term.
