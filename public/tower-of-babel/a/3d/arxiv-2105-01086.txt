Racah algebras, the centralizer Z n ​ ( 𝔰 ​ 𝔩 2 ) Z_{n}({\mathfrak{sl}}_{2}) and its Hilbert–Poincaré series
Nicolas Crampé, Julien Gaboriaud, Loïc Poulain d’Andecy, Luc Vinet

Abstract
The higher rank Racah algebra R ⁡ ( n ) R(n) introduced in [ 1 ] is recalled. A quotient of this algebra by central elements, which we call the special Racah algebra s ​ R ​ ( n ) sR(n) , is then introduced. Using results from classical invariant theory, this s ​ R ​ ( n ) sR(n) algebra is shown to be isomorphic to the centralizer Z n ​ ( 𝔰 ​ 𝔩 2 ) Z_{n}(\mathfrak{sl}_{2}) of the diagonal embedding of U ⁡ ( 𝔰 ​ 𝔩 2 ) U(\mathfrak{sl}_{2}) in U ​ ( 𝔰 ​ 𝔩 2 ) ⊗ n U(\mathfrak{sl}_{2})^{\otimes n} . This leads to a first and novel presentation of the centralizer Z n ​ ( 𝔰 ​ 𝔩 2 ) Z_{n}(\mathfrak{sl}_{2}) in terms of generators and defining relations. An explicit formula of its Hilbert–Poincaré series is also obtained and studied. The extension of the results to the study of the special Askey–Wilson algebra and its higher rank generalizations is discussed. Keywords: Racah algebra, centralizer, U ⁡ ( 𝔰 ​ 𝔩 2 ) U(\mathfrak{sl}_{2}) , classical invariant theory, first and second fundamental theorems, Hilbert–Poincaré series.

This paper clarifies the connection between the higher rank Racah algebra

and the

A particular quotient of the Racah algebra

In the case

contains a number of subalgebras of

“intermediate” or “quadratic” Casimir elements

These elements realize the relations of the Racah algebra
[
3

or if more relations

A similar story repeats itself for the centralizer

, but two

As will be seen, these questions are quite close to questions that appear in

In this regard, the results that we obtain in Corollary

1.1
On the Racah algebra

At this point it would be appropriate to provide some background on the Racah
algebra, which arises in numerous areas of mathematics and physics.

A first approach to the Racah algebra is from the theory of orthogonal polynomials. The
Racah polynomials are a family of bispectral classical orthogonal polynomials which are characterized
by a difference and recurrence operator
[
4
]
. These operators obey
the quadratic algebraic relations of the Racah algebra; this is actually how the algebra
was originally introduced
[
5
]
and the reason why it inherited
its name. Thus, the representation theory of the Racah algebra involves the eponym
polynomials.

The problem of decomposing the tensor product of two irreducible representations of
{\mathfrak{sl}}_{2}
in
a direct sum of irreducible representations is known as the Clebsch–Gordan problem of
{\mathfrak{sl}}_{2}
.
When the three-fold tensor product is considered, there exist two natural decompositions. The question of
finding the overlaps between the two associated bases is called the Racah problem
of
{\mathfrak{sl}}_{2}
. The intermediate Casimir elements labelling the two mentioned
decompositions realize the Racah algebra
[
3
]
, and thus the
overlaps are found to be given in terms of Racah polynomials. There are also realizations
of the Racah algebra in terms of
U({\mathfrak{sl}}_{2})

[
6
,
7
,
8
,
9
]
.

Other instances of the Racah algebra appearing in various contexts include algebraic
combinatorics
[
10
,
11
]
,
the theory of double affine Hecke algebras
[
12
]
,
and as a symmetry algebra of physical models
[
13
,
14
,
15
,
16
,
17
]

Its finite-dimensional irreducible modules have also been classified

[
18
]
.

As is the case for various rich structures that have a lot of applications, the
generalization of the Racah algebra is something desirable.
A higher rank Racah algebra has been defined in
[
1
]
by looking
at a
3
D superintegrable system that realizes the Racah algebra as its symmetry algebra
and then generalizing this system to
n
dimensions. The algebraic relations between the
constants of motion were then computed and used to define abstractly the higher rank
Racah algebra
R(n)
. These relations of
R(n)
are also verified by the
intermediate Casimir elements that label various direct sum decompositions of the
n
-fold
tensor product of
{\mathfrak{sl}}_{2}
irreducible representations
[
19
]
.
These higher rank Racah algebras have also been observed in physical models

[
20
,
21
,
22
]
,
interpreted in the framework of Howe duality
[
23
]
,
and the study of their relation to multivariate Racah polynomials has been
initiated
[
24
,
25
,
26
]
.

(3.2b)

\displaystyle[{\textit{{P}}}_{ij},{\textit{{P}}}_{jk}]

\displaystyle=2{\textit{{F}}}_{ijk},

(3.2c)

\displaystyle[{\textit{{P}}}_{jk},{\textit{{F}}}_{ijk}]

\displaystyle={\textit{{P}}}_{ik}({\textit{{P}}}_{jk}+{\textit{{P}}}_{jj})-({\textit{{P}}}_{jk}+{\textit{{P}}}_{kk}){\textit{{P}}}_{ij},

(3.2d)

5
Isomorphism between the centralizer
Z_{n}({\mathfrak{sl}}_{2})
and the special Racah algebra

The goal of this section is to connect the (higher rank) special Racah algebra
introduced and characterized in the previous two sections with the centralizer

Z_{n}({\mathfrak{sl}}_{2})
of the diagonal action of
U({\mathfrak{sl}}_{2})
in
U({\mathfrak{sl}}_{2})^{\otimes n}
. This will
provide an a posteriori justification for the quotient that was chosen to go from the
Racah algebra
R(n)
to the special Racah algebra
sR(n)
: as will be shown, this quotient
is precisely the one that leads to an algebra isomorphic to the centralizer

Z_{n}({\mathfrak{sl}}_{2})
.

5.1
Centralizer
Z_{n}({\mathfrak{sl}}_{2})
of the diagonal action
U({\mathfrak{sl}}_{2})
into

U({\mathfrak{sl}}_{2})^{\otimes n}
and the algebra of polarized traces

We here define the centralizer associated to the Lie algebra
{\mathfrak{sl}}_{2}
.
The generators of
{\mathfrak{sl}}_{2}
are
e_{ij}
,
i,j\in\{1,2\}
obeying the defining relations

\displaystyle[e_{ij},e_{k\ell}]=\delta_{jk}e_{i\ell}-\delta_{\ell i}e_{kj},\qquad e_{11}+e_{22}=0.

(5.1)

We denote by
U({\mathfrak{sl}}_{2})
the universal enveloping algebra of
{\mathfrak{sl}}_{2}
. Its Casimir element
is given by

\displaystyle C={e_{11}}^{2}-e_{11}+e_{12}e_{21}.

(5.2)

Now consider the tensor product of
n
copies of
U({\mathfrak{sl}}_{2})
and define the following
notation for its generators

\displaystyle e_{ij}^{(a)}=1^{\otimes(a-1)}\otimes e_{ij}\otimes 1^{\otimes(n-a)}.

(5.3)

The diagonal embedding of
U({\mathfrak{sl}}_{2})
in its
n
-fold tensor product is given by

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \delta:~U({\mathfrak{sl}}_{2})&\to U({\mathfrak{sl}}_{2})^{\otimes n}\\
~e_{ij}&\mapsto\sum_{a=1}^{n}e_{ij}^{(a)}.\end{aligned}

(5.4)

There is a natural degree-preserving action of
{\mathfrak{sl}}_{2}
on
U({\mathfrak{sl}}_{2})^{\otimes n}
given
by composing the diagonal embedding
\delta
followed by the adjoint action. On the
generators, it is given by

\displaystyle e_{ij}\cdot e_{k\ell}^{(a)}=\delta_{jk}e_{i\ell}^{(a)}-\delta_{\ell i}e_{kj}^{(a)}.

(5.5)

We then define the centralizer
Z_{n}({\mathfrak{sl}}_{2})
of the diagonal embedding of
U({\mathfrak{sl}}_{2})
in

U({\mathfrak{sl}}_{2})^{\otimes n}
as the kernel of this
{\mathfrak{sl}}_{2}
action

\displaystyle Z_{n}({\mathfrak{sl}}_{2})=\left\{X\in U({\mathfrak{sl}}_{2})^{\otimes n}~|~g\cdot X=[\delta(g),X]=0\quad\forall g\in U(sl_{2})\right\}

(5.6)

or in other words, as the set of elements in
U({\mathfrak{sl}}_{2})^{\otimes n}
that commute with
the diagonal embedding of
U({\mathfrak{sl}}_{2})
.

Let us also define the polarized traces (the summation convention is assumed):

\displaystyle T^{(a_{1},\dots,a_{d})}=e_{i_{2}i_{1}}^{(a_{1})}e_{i_{3}i_{2}}^{(a_{2})}\dots e_{i_{1}i_{d}}^{(a_{d})},\qquad a_{1},\dots,a_{d}\in\{1,\dots,n\}.

(5.7)

It is seen by a direct computation that these elements are in the centralizer

Z_{n}({\mathfrak{sl}}_{2})
.

Remark 5.1
.

It is easily checked from the definition (
5.7
) that

T^{(a_{1},a_{2})}=T^{(a_{2},a_{1})}
and that
T^{(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3})}
is antisymmetric
in its indices
a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}
, i.e.

T^{(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3})}=T^{(a_{2},a_{3},a_{1})}=-T^{(a_{2},a_{1},a_{3})}
.

Remark 5.2
.

A number of papers in the literature
[
3
,
19
]

realize the Racah algebra with the so-called
“intermediate Casimir” elements

,
C_{ij}
. These elements are given by

\displaystyle C_{i}=1^{\otimes(i-1)}\otimes C\otimes 1^{\otimes(n-i)},\qquad C_{ij}=1^{\otimes(i-1)}\otimes C_{(1)}\otimes 1^{\otimes(j-i-1)}\otimes C_{(2)}\otimes 1^{\otimes(n-j)},

(5.8)

where
\Delta(e_{ij})=e_{ij}\otimes 1+1\otimes e_{ij}
and we denote

\Delta(C)=C_{(1)}\otimes C_{(2)}
in Sweedler’s notation.
Then the

and

can be expressed in terms of these intermediate
Casimir elements as follows

\displaystyle T^{(i,i)}=2C_{i},\qquad T^{(i,j)}=C_{ij}-C_{i}-C_{j}.

(5.9)

5.2
Elements of classical invariant theory

We now present results from classical invariant theory about the algebra of polynomial

\displaystyle\underbrace{{\mathfrak{sl}}_{2}\times\dots\times{\mathfrak{sl}}_{2}}_{\text{$n$ factors}}\equiv{\mathfrak{sl}}_{2}^{n},

(5.10)

For

\displaystyle G\cdot f({M_{1}},\dots,{M_{n}})=f(G^{-1}{M_{1}}G,\dots,G^{-1}{M_{n}}G),

The first fundamental theorem of classical invariant theory states that:

Theorem 5.3

(see
[
28

29
,
30
]
or
[

\displaystyle\mathfrak{T}^{(a_{1},\dots,a_{d})}\ :\ ({M_{1}},\dots,{M_{n}})\mapsto Tr({M_{a_{1}}}\dots{M_{a_{d}}})

(5.12)

for
{M_{i}}\in{\mathfrak{sl}}_{2}

Moreover, it is sufficient to take

The generating set of the invariant polynomial functions described in the preceding

Theorem 5.4

(see

The defining relations for the algebra of polynomial invariant functions are

\displaystyle\qquad\begin{aligned} &Tr([{M_{i}},{M_{j}}]{M_{k}})Tr([{M_{p}},{M_{q}}]{M_{r}})\\

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &Tr([{M_{j}},{M_{k}}]{M_{\ell}})Tr({M_{p}}{M_{i}})-Tr([{M_{i}},{M_{k}}]{M_{\ell}})Tr({M_{p}}{M_{j}})\\

With the following reasoning that is adapted from

•

\displaystyle e_{ij}\cdot{\textsf{e}}_{k\ell}^{(a)}=\delta_{jk}{\textsf{e}}_{i\ell}^{(a)}-\delta_{\ell i}{\textsf{e}}_{kj}^{(a)}.

\displaystyle\epsilon\sum_{k=1}^{n}f({M_{1}},\dots,[{M_{k}},g],\dots,{M_{n}})

5.3
The defining relations of
Z_{n}({\mathfrak{sl}}_{2})

Knowing that the generators of the invariant functions (
5.12
)
correspond to the polarized traces (
5.7
) (see Theorem
5.3
),
the image of the centralizer in
gr(U({\mathfrak{sl}}_{2})^{\otimes n})
is therefore generated by the
image of the polarized traces. Now consider an element of degree
N
in the centralizer.
Up to terms of degree
N-1
, this element can be expressed as a polynomial in

T^{(a_{1},\dots,a_{d})}
. The same can then be argued for each of the remaining lower degree
terms by induction, and thus any element in the centralizer can be expressed as a
polynomial in the polarized traces. Since all polarized traces belong in the centralizer,
the two algebras thus coincide. So we obtain the analogue of the first fundamental
theorem:

Corollary 5.5
.

It follows from Theorem
5.3
that the polarized traces
T^{(i,j)}
,
i\leq j
and

T^{(i,j,k)}
,
i<j<k
generate the centralizer
Z_{n}({\mathfrak{sl}}_{2})
of the diagonal action of

U({\mathfrak{sl}}_{2})
in
U({\mathfrak{sl}}_{2})^{\otimes n}
.

Recall that relations (
5.13
) are a set of defining relations for the
image of the centralizer in
gr(U({\mathfrak{sl}}_{2})^{\otimes n})
.
We look for analogous defining relations for the centralizer in
U({\mathfrak{sl}}_{2})^{\otimes n}
.
Once we find the deformations in
U({\mathfrak{sl}}_{2})^{\otimes n}
of relations
(
5.13
), we can prove their completeness using for the ideal of relations
the same sort of reasoning as before Corollary
5.5
. In fact the special Racah
algebra was defined such that its set of defining relations gives precisely the complete
set of relations for the centralizer.

Theorem 5.6
.

With the following identification of the generators:

\displaystyle{\textit{{P}}}_{ab}\mapsto T^{(a,b)}\qquad\text{and}\qquad{\textit{{F}}}_{ijk}\mapsto-T^{(i,j,k)}

(5.18)

for

all distinct, the centralizer
Z_{n}({\mathfrak{sl}}_{2})
is isomorphic to the special Racah
algebra
sR(n)
:

\displaystyle Z_{n}({\mathfrak{sl}}_{2})\cong sR(n).

(5.19)

In other words, a set of defining relations of
Z_{n}({\mathfrak{sl}}_{2})
is given in
(
4.4
), where
{\textit{{P}}}_{ab}
and
{\textit{{F}}}_{ijk}
are replaced by the corresponding
polarized traces.

Proof.

The defining relations are verified to hold in
U({\mathfrak{sl}}_{2})^{\otimes n}
by direct
computations. Note that, due to the symmetry under renaming the indices, we only need to
make calculations in
U({\mathfrak{sl}}_{2})^{\otimes n}
for
n\leq 5
in degrees less or equal to 6
in the generators.

Under the previous choice of degree for the generators of

U({\mathfrak{sl}}_{2})^{\otimes n}
which was
deg(e_{ij}^{(a)})=1
, it follows that

\displaystyle deg(T^{(i,j)})=2,\qquad deg(T^{(i,j,k})=3.

(5.20)

The same degrees are given to the generators of the
special Racah algebra
sR(n)
:
deg({\textit{{P}}}_{ij})=2
and
deg({\textit{{F}}}_{ijk})=3
. This makes it a filtered algebra, and it is straightforward
to observe that its associated graded algebra is isomorphic to the algebra of polynomial
invariants functions. Indeed, the first set of defining relations (
3.2
),
or equivalently (
4.4a
)–(
4.4g
),
ensures that the generators all commute in the graded algebra, and then the relations
(
4.4h
)–(
4.4i
) and (
4.5
) are mapped to
(
5.13
).

Therefore both algebras related by the morphism in (
5.18
) have the same associated graded
algebras, and so in particular have the same dimensions in each component of the
filtration (that is, in each degree). Moreover the morphism is surjective from Corollary

5.5
. Consequently, an element in the kernel of the map (in other words, a relation in

Z_{n}({\mathfrak{sl}}_{2})
not implied by the relations of the special Racah algebra), if non-zero,
would contradict the equality of dimensions for some degree.
∎

Remark 5.7
.

It is quite remarkable that we only need to quotient the Racah algebra
R(n)
by the
elements
w_{ijk}
and
x_{ijk\ell}
in order to recover the centralizer for any value of

n
. Indeed, one could have expected that in order to recover the centralizer for
increasing
n
, we would need to quotient by elements of increasing degree or spanning an
increasing number of indices. That this is not the case is quite a surprising
simplification.

6
The Hilbert–Poincaré series of
Z_{n}({\mathfrak{sl}}_{2})

For more information on Hilbert–Poincaré series of graded algebras, we refer to

[
34
]
. The Hilbert–Poincaré series contains useful information about a
graded, or filtered, algebra. We will illustrate this for the diagonal centralizer

Z_{n}({\mathfrak{sl}}_{2})
. We will provide an explicit formula for its Hilbert–Poincaré series, and
then use it in conjunction with the defining relations found in Theorem
5.6

to provide bases of
Z_{n}({\mathfrak{sl}}_{2})
for small
n
.

6.1
An explicit formula

The commutative algebra
\mathbb{C}[{\mathfrak{sl}}_{2}^{n}]^{inv}
of polynomial functions on
{\mathfrak{sl}}_{2}^{n}

that are invariant under simultaneous conjugation by
SL(2)
is a graded algebra: it is
the direct sum of the subspaces
\mathbb{C}_{k}[{\mathfrak{sl}}_{2}^{n}]^{inv}
of homogeneous invariant
polynomial functions of degree
k
. The Hilbert–Poincaré series records the dimensions
of all these subspaces:

\displaystyle F_{n}(t)=\sum_{k\geq 0}\dim\bigl(\mathbb{C}_{k}[{\mathfrak{sl}}_{2}^{n}]^{inv}\bigr)t^{k}.

(6.1)

The centralizer
Z_{n}({\mathfrak{sl}}_{2})
inherits from
U({\mathfrak{sl}}_{2})^{\otimes n}
the structure of a
filtered algebra: it is the union of the increasing sequence (in
k
) of subspaces

Z_{n}({\mathfrak{sl}}_{2})_{\leq k}
of elements of degree less or equal to
k
(the degree is in the
generators of
U({\mathfrak{sl}}_{2})^{\otimes n}
). The Hilbert–Poincaré series of
Z_{n}({\mathfrak{sl}}_{2})

records the dimensions of the homogeneous subspaces of the associated graded algebra:

\displaystyle F_{n}(t)=\sum_{k\geq 0}\dim\bigl(Z_{n}({\mathfrak{sl}}_{2})_{\leq k}/Z_{n}({\mathfrak{sl}}_{2})_{<k}\bigr)t^{k},

(6.2)

and thus, from the discussion in Section
5
, is the same as the
Hilbert–Poincaré series of the invariant polynomial functions.

Several formulas, using various approaches, have been obtained for the Hilbert–Poincaré
series
F_{n}(t)
(see references in
[
31
,
35
,
36
]
). The
formula presented below seems to be new.

Proposition 6.1
.

Let
n\geq 2
and recall that the rank
r
is defined by
r=n-2
. The Hilbert–Poincaré
series of
Z_{n}({\mathfrak{sl}}_{2})
is:

\displaystyle F_{n}(t)=\frac{P_{r}(t)}{(1-t^{2})^{3(r+1)}},

(6.3)

where the numerator is given by:

\displaystyle P_{r}(t)=(1+t)^{r}\sum_{k=0}^{2r}(-1)^{k}a_{k}t^{k},\qquad\text{where}\ \left\{\begin{array}[]{l}a_{2k}=\binom{r}{k}^{2},\\[5.0pt]
a_{2k+1}=\binom{r}{k}\binom{r}{k+1}.\end{array}\right.

Proof.

We take a detour through the graded character of
SL(2)

on the polynomial functions on
{\mathfrak{sl}}_{2}^{n}
. The character of
SL(2)
for a finite-dimensional
representation is seen as a Laurent polynomial in
x
, given by the trace of the action of
the element
\text{Diag}(x,x^{-1})
. For example, for the fundamental representation of

SL(2)
, it is
x+x^{-1}
. For the irreducible representation of dimension
d+1
, which is
the
d
-symmetrized power of the fundamental representation, the character is thus

\frac{x^{d+1}-x^{-d-1}}{x-x^{-1}}
. Now, it is easy to check that, if we have the
character
\chi(x)
of an arbitrary finite-dimensional representation of
SL(2)
, then the
formula:

\displaystyle\bigl[(1-x^{2})\chi(x)\bigr]_{0},

(6.6)

where
[\,\cdot\,]_{0}
means taking the constant term of a Laurent polynomial, gives the
multiplicity of the trivial representation.

After these classical preliminaries, note that the character of the adjoint representation
of
SL(2)
on
{\mathfrak{sl}}_{2}
is
1+x^{2}+x^{-2}
. On the polynomial function on
{\mathfrak{sl}}_{2}
, the action
of
SL(2)
preserves the grading, and we record the character of the representation on its
graded components as a formal power series in
t
(also called, the graded character). For
each degree, the representation is a symmetrized power of the adjoint representation, so
we find that the graded character is:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(1-t)(1-tx^{2})(1-tx^{-2})}.

(6.7)

Equivalently, this is the graded character for the adjoint action on
U({\mathfrak{sl}}_{2})
. On the
polynomial functions
{\mathfrak{sl}}_{2}^{n}
(or equivalently, on
U(sl_{2})^{\otimes n}
), the graded character is thus:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(1-t)^{n}(1-tx^{2})^{n}(1-tx^{-2})^{n}}.

(6.8)

Now, in each degree, we look for the dimension of the invariant subspace for the action of

SL(2)
. In other words, we look for the multiplicity of the trivial representation. By
what we have recalled above, we obtain that the Hilbert–Poincaré series of
Z_{n}({\mathfrak{sl}}_{2})

is:

\displaystyle F_{n}(t)=\left[\frac{(1-x^{2})}{(1-t)^{n}(1-tx^{2})^{n}(1-tx^{-2})^{n}}\right]_{0}.

(6.9)

Using the expansion
(1-z)^{-n}=\sum_{k\geq 0}\binom{k+n-1}{k}z^{k}
and straightforward
manipulations, we obtain:

F_{n}(t)=\frac{1}{(1-t)^{n}}\sum_{k\geq 0}(-1)^{k}\tilde{a}_{k}t^{k},\qquad\text{where}\ \left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\tilde{a}_{2k}=\binom{n+k-1}{k}^{2},\\[5.0pt]
\tilde{a}_{2k+1}=\binom{n+k-1}{k}\binom{n+k}{k+1}.\end{array}\right.

(6.10)

Thus the statement of the proposition reduces to the following equality of formal power
series:

\frac{1}{(1-t)^{r+2}}\sum_{k\geq 0}(-1)^{k}\tilde{a}_{k}t^{k}=\frac{(1+t)^{r}}{(1-t^{2})^{3(r+1)}}\sum_{k\geq 0}(-1)^{k}a_{k}t^{k}.

(6.11)

To prove this, we multiply the
\tilde{a}
-series by
(1+t)
and the
a
-series by

(1-t)
, and after an application of Pascal rule for binomials, we reach the equivalent
formula:

\displaystyle\sum_{k\geq 0}(-1)^{k}\tilde{a}^{\prime}_{k}t^{k}=\frac{1}{(1-t^{2})^{2(r+1)}}\sum_{k\geq 0}(-1)^{k}a^{\prime}_{k}t^{k},

(6.12)

where now we have

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\tilde{a}^{\prime}_{2k}=\binom{r+k+1}{k}\binom{r+k}{k},\\[5.0pt]
\tilde{a}^{\prime}_{2k+1}=\binom{r+k+1}{k}\binom{r+k+1}{k+1},\end{array}\right.

and
\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}a^{\prime}_{2k}=\binom{r}{k}\binom{r+1}{k},\\[5.0pt]
a^{\prime}_{2k+1}=\binom{r}{k}\binom{r+1}{k+1}.\end{array}\right.

This last formula is verified by writing the expansion of the right hand side and checking
the equality of the coefficients, making use of the following identity for binomial
coefficients
[
37
]
:

\displaystyle\sum_{i}\binom{i+a+b}{i}\binom{b}{k-i}\binom{a}{k^{\prime}-i}=\binom{k^{\prime}+b}{k}\binom{k+a}{k^{\prime}}.

(6.13)

This identity is valid for any
a,b,k,k^{\prime}
and we use it for
a=r+1
,
b=r
and

k^{\prime}\in\{k,k+1\}
.
∎

The exponent
3(r+1)
appearing in the denominator of
F_{n}(t)
is the Krull, or
Gelfand–Kirillov, dimension of the algebra of invariant polynomial functions (see

[
38
]
,
[
36
]
). Here it means that there is a set of
3(r+1)

algebraically independent homogeneous elements (a system of parameters)

\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{3(r+1)}
such that the algebra is a free module of finite
dimension over the polynomial subalgebra
\mathbb{C}[\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{3(r+1)}]
. The
freeness follows from the property called Cohen–Macaulay, which is ensured here by the
Hochster–Roberts theorem from general invariant theory
[
39
]
. The
form
F_{n}(t)
above with the positivity of the numerator
P_{r}(t)
(see below) suggests that it might
be possible that a system of parameters consists of
3(r+1)
elements of degrees
2
. If it
were to be the case, then
P_{r}(1)
would be the dimension of the algebra over

\mathbb{C}[\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{3(r+1)}]
. Moreover, the different monomials in

P_{r}(t)
would indicate in which degrees the elements of a basis over

\mathbb{C}[\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{3(r+1)}]
would have to be found.

Finally, the palindromic property of the numerator
P_{r}(t)
in the formula above shows
that the Hilbert–Poincaré series satisfies the functional equation:

\displaystyle F_{n}(t^{-1})=(-1)^{(n-1)}t^{3n}F_{n}(t).

(6.14)

This is well-known and related to a property, called being Gorenstein, for the algebra of
invariant polynomial functions (see
[
31
]
and references therein).

Remark 6.2
.

More generally, the Hilbert–Poincaré series of
Z_{n}({\mathfrak{sl}}_{2})
can be defined as a power
series in
t_{1},\dots,t_{n}
if we consider the gradation by the multidegree of

U({\mathfrak{sl}}_{2})^{\otimes n}
. A slight generalization of the part of the proof up to Formula
(
6.10
) gives the multigraded version of this formula:

\displaystyle F_{n}(t_{1},\dots,t_{n})=\frac{1}{(1-t_{1})\dots(1-t_{n})}\Bigl(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mu\models k\\
\nu\models k\end{subarray}}t_{1}^{\mu_{1}+\nu_{1}}\dots t_{n}^{\mu_{n}+\nu_{n}}-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mu\models k\\
\nu\models k-1\end{subarray}}t_{1}^{\mu_{1}+\nu_{1}}\dots t_{n}^{\mu_{n}+\nu_{n}}\Bigr),

(6.15)

where
\mu\models k
means that
\mu=(\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{n}
such that

\mu_{1}+\dots+\mu_{n}=k
. To recover relation (
6.10
) from this, take

t_{1}=\dots=t_{n}=t
and use that the number of
\mu\models k
is
\binom{k+n-1}{k}
.

6.2
Some related combinatorics

We have obtained an expression for the Hilbert–Poincaré series of
Z_{n}({\mathfrak{sl}}_{2})
of the
form:

\displaystyle F_{n}(t)=\frac{(1+t)^{r}Q_{r}(t)}{(1-t^{2})^{3(r+1)}},\qquad\text{where $Q_{r}(t)=\sum_{k=0}^{2r}(-1)^{k}a_{k}t^{k}$},

(6.16)

and the coefficients
a_{k}
are given in the proposition above. It is perhaps not so
surprising that the coefficients of the various polynomials involved show some connections
with well-studied combinatorial objects of “Catalan” flavor.

The polynomial
Q_{r}(t)
.

The coefficient
a_{k}
in the polynomial
Q_{r}(t)

counts the number of symmetric Dyck paths of semi-length
2r+1
with
k+1
peaks (see
A088855 in
[
40
]
). Their expression with binomial coefficients corresponds to
choosing a certain number of peaks and troughs in the first
r
steps of the paths.

In fact, the polynomial
Q_{r}(t)
is a
t
-deformation of the well-known Catalan number,
that is, the value of
Q_{r}(t)
at
t=1
is the
r
-th Catalan number:

\displaystyle Q_{r}(1)=c_{r}=\binom{2r}{r}-\binom{2r}{r+1}.

(6.17)

Indeed it is not difficult to give a combinatorial proof that the alternating sum of the

a_{k}
’s is equal to the Catalan number
c_{r}
(the number of Dyck paths of length
2r
). We
can also see it as follows. The Catalan number is equal to the constant term of a Laurent
polynomial in
x
:

\displaystyle c_{r}=\bigl[(1-x^{2})(x+x^{-1})^{2r}\bigr]_{0}=\bigl[(1-x^{2})(1+x^{2})^{r}(1+x^{-2})^{r}\bigr]_{0}.

(6.18)

Note that we keep the variable
x^{2}
to stay coherent with the notation used during the
proof above. In fact, the first equality expresses that the Catalan number
c_{r}
is the
multiplicity of the trivial representation in
V^{r}\otimes(V^{\star})^{r}
. The

t
-deformation is now immediate from this formula for
c_{r}
. Indeed, it follows from its
explicit expression that the polynomial
Q_{r}(t)
is given by:

\displaystyle Q_{r}(t)=\bigl[(1-x^{2})(1+tx^{2})^{r}(1+tx^{-2})^{r}\bigr]_{0}.

(6.19)

In this sense, the polynomial
Q_{r}(t)
is a natural
t
-deformation of the
r
-th Catalan
number.

The numerator
P_{r}(t)
.

The numerator of the Hilbert–Poincaré series of

Z_{n}({\mathfrak{sl}}_{2})
is
P_{r}(t)=(1+t)^{r}Q_{r}(t)
. We will show explicitly that its coefficients are
positive.

First, from what we have said above about
Q_{r}(t)
, it follows that
P_{r}(t)
is a

t
-deformation of the number
2^{r}c_{r}
, that is, its value at
t=1
is
P_{r}(1)=2^{r}c_{r}
.
This number counts several classes of combinatorial objects (obtained from objects counted
by the Catalan number, see A151374 in
[
40
]
). The
t
-deformation giving
P_{r}(t)

can be expressed similarly as before as:

\displaystyle P_{r}(t)=\bigl[(1-x^{2})(1+t)^{r}(1+tx^{2})^{r}(1+tx^{-2})^{r}\bigr]_{0}.

(6.20)

Now regrouping the terms with an
r
-th power, this gives the following expression:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} P_{r}(t)&=\bigl[(1-x^{2})\bigl(1+t^{3}+(t+t^{2})(1+x^{2}+x^{-2})\bigr)^{r}\bigr]_{0}\\
&=\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{r}R_{k}\binom{r}{k}(1+t^{3})^{r-k}(t+t^{2})^{k},\end{aligned}

(6.21)

where the positive integer
R_{k}
is the Riordan number, one of the closest relative of the
Catalan number, which also admits many combinatorial interpretations (see A005043 in

[
40
]
). They are given by either one of the following equalities:

\displaystyle R_{n}=\bigl[(1-x^{2})(1+x^{2}+x^{-2})^{n}\bigr]_{0}=\sum_{i=0}^{n}(-1)^{n-i}\binom{n}{i}c_{i}.

(6.22)

We actually used the first one in the above calculation, while the second one shows that
the Catalan sequence is the binomial transform of the Riordan sequence, and thus allows
to recover that
P_{r}(1)=2^{r}c_{r}
.

The formula (
6.21
) for
P_{r}(t)
has the advantage to show explicitly that it has
positive coefficients. So
P_{r}(t)
is a
t
-deformation with positive coefficients of

2^{r}c_{r}
and therefore should be given by an interesting statistics on a certain set of

2^{r}c_{r}
objects.

6.3
PBW basis of
Z_{n}({\mathfrak{sl}}_{2})
for small
n

With the help of the Poincaré–Hilbert series obtained and discussed above,
we here determine the PBW bases of
Z_{n}({\mathfrak{sl}}_{2})
for
n=2,3,4
.
Using Theorem
5.6
, we identify systematically
Z_{n}({\mathfrak{sl}}_{2})
with the special
Racah algebra, and use the generators and relations in Section
4
. Note that
from the discussion in Section
5.2
, in order to show that a subset of

Z_{n}({\mathfrak{sl}}_{2})
is a spanning set, it is enough to show that the images in the graded algebra
of invariant polynomial functions is a spanning set.

The case
n=2
.

The Hilbert–Poincaré series of
Z_{2}({\mathfrak{sl}}_{2})
is:

F_{2}(t)=\frac{1}{(1-t^{2})^{3}}.

(6.23)

This allows easily to recover that the algebra of invariant polynomial functions, and

Z_{2}({\mathfrak{sl}}_{2})
, is a commutative polynomial algebra. A basis of
Z_{2}({\mathfrak{sl}}_{2})
is

{\textit{{P}}}_{11}^{a}{\textit{{P}}}_{12}^{b}{\textit{{P}}}_{22}^{c}
with
a,b,c\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
. Indeed this set is
obviously a spanning set, and moreover spans a subspace whose dimensions in each degree
are given precisely by the series (
6.23
). So this set is also linearly independent,
and is a basis.

The case
n=3
.

The Hilbert–Poincaré series of
Z_{3}({\mathfrak{sl}}_{2})
is:

\displaystyle F_{3}(t)=\frac{1+t^{3}}{(1-t^{2})^{6}}.

(6.24)

This allows to show that the following set is a basis of
Z_{3}({\mathfrak{sl}}_{2})
:

\{{\textit{{P}}}_{11}^{a}{\textit{{P}}}_{12}^{b}{\textit{{P}}}_{13}^{c}{\textit{{P}}}_{22}^{d}{\textit{{P}}}_{23}^{e}{\textit{{P}}}_{33}^{f}{\textit{{F}}}_{123}^{\varepsilon}\},\qquad\text{where $a,b,c,d,e,f\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}$ and $\varepsilon\in\{0,1\}$.}

(6.25)

Indeed, such a set is a spanning set since
{{\textit{{F}}}_{123}}^{2}
can be expressed in terms of the

{{P}}
’s. Comparing with
F_{3}(t)
, we see directly that this set spans a subspace of the
correct dimension in each degree. So this is a basis.

The case
n=4
.

The Hilbert–Poincaré series of
Z_{4}({\mathfrak{sl}}_{2})
is:

\displaystyle F_{4}(t)=\frac{1+t^{2}+4t^{3}+t^{4}+t^{6}}{(1-t^{2})^{9}}.

(6.26)

The four sets, for
a,b,c,d,e,f,g,h,i\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq}0
,

\displaystyle\{{\textit{{F}}}_{123}{\textit{{P}}}_{11}^{a}{\textit{{P}}}_{12}^{b}{\textit{{P}}}_{13}^{c}{\textit{{P}}}_{14}^{d}{\textit{{P}}}_{22}^{e}{\textit{{P}}}_{23}^{f}{\textit{{P}}}_{24}^{g}{\textit{{P}}}_{33}^{h}{\textit{{P}}}_{34}^{i}\},

(6.27a)

\displaystyle\{{\textit{{F}}}_{124}{\textit{{P}}}_{11}^{a}{\textit{{P}}}_{12}^{b}{\textit{{P}}}_{13}^{c}{\textit{{P}}}_{14}^{d}{\textit{{P}}}_{22}^{e}{\textit{{P}}}_{23}^{f}{\textit{{P}}}_{24}^{g}{\textit{{P}}}_{34}^{h}{\textit{{P}}}_{44}^{i}\},

(6.27b)

\displaystyle\{{\textit{{F}}}_{134}{\textit{{P}}}_{11}^{a}{\textit{{P}}}_{12}^{b}{\textit{{P}}}_{13}^{c}{\textit{{P}}}_{14}^{d}{\textit{{P}}}_{23}^{e}{\textit{{P}}}_{24}^{f}{\textit{{P}}}_{33}^{g}{\textit{{P}}}_{34}^{h}{\textit{{P}}}_{44}^{i}\},

(6.27c)

\displaystyle\{{\textit{{F}}}_{234}{\textit{{P}}}_{12}^{a}{\textit{{P}}}_{13}^{b}{\textit{{P}}}_{14}^{c}{\textit{{P}}}_{22}^{d}{\textit{{P}}}_{23}^{e}{\textit{{P}}}_{24}^{f}{\textit{{P}}}_{33}^{g}{\textit{{P}}}_{34}^{h}{\textit{{P}}}_{44}^{i}\},

(6.27d)

and, for
a,b,c,d,e,f,g,h,i,j\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq}0
and
aehj=0
,

\displaystyle\{{\textit{{P}}}_{11}^{a}{\textit{{P}}}_{12}^{b}{\textit{{P}}}_{13}^{c}{\textit{{P}}}_{14}^{d}{\textit{{P}}}_{22}^{e}{\textit{{P}}}_{23}^{f}{\textit{{P}}}_{24}^{g}{\textit{{P}}}_{33}^{h}{\textit{{P}}}_{34}^{i}{\textit{{P}}}_{44}^{j}\}

(6.27e)

form a basis of
Z_{4}({\mathfrak{sl}}_{2})
.
To understand this, rewrite its Hilbert–Poincaré series as

F_{4}(t)=\frac{4t^{3}}{(1-t^{2})^{9}}+\frac{1-t^{8}}{(1-t^{2})^{10}}.

(6.28)

The first term corresponds to the first 4 sets and the second term corresponds to the fifth ones.
These sets are spanning sets since
{\textit{{F}}}_{ijk}{\textit{{F}}}_{mnp}
can be expressed in terms linear in
F

by using the relations
w_{ijk}=0
and
x_{ijk\ell}=0
and
{\textit{{F}}}_{ijk}{\textit{{P}}}_{\ell\ell}
(

pairwise distinct)

6.27
) by using (
3.9
).
The condition
aehj=0
for the fifth set comes from the following fact.
Let us define the following
2\times 2
and
4\times 4
matrices

\displaystyle{\textit{{P}}}_{ij}^{\,ab}=\begin{pmatrix}{\textit{{P}}}_{ia}&{\textit{{P}}}_{ib}\\
{\textit{{P}}}_{ja}&{\textit{{P}}}_{jb}\end{pmatrix},\qquad{\textit{{P}}}_{ijk\ell}^{\,abcd}=\begin{pmatrix}{\textit{{P}}}_{ia}&{\textit{{P}}}_{ib}&{\textit{{P}}}_{ic}&{\textit{{P}}}_{id}\\

{\textit{{P}}}_{ka}&{\textit{{P}}}_{kb}&{\textit{{P}}}_{kc}&{\textit{{P}}}_{kd}\\
{\textit{{P}}}_{\ell a}&{\textit{{P}}}_{\ell b}&{\textit{{P}}}_{\ell c}&{\textit{{P}}}_{\ell d}\end{pmatrix}.

(6.29)

Recall the definition of the symmetrized determinant (

).
In

\displaystyle\det({\textit{{P}}}_{1234}^{1234})

\displaystyle=-\tfrac{1}{3}\left(\det({\textit{{P}}}_{123}^{124})-\det({\textit{{P}}}_{123}^{134})+\det({\textit{{P}}}_{123}^{234})+\det({\textit{{P}}}_{124}^{134})-\det({\textit{{P}}}_{124}^{234})+\det({\textit{{P}}}_{134}^{234})\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\tfrac{2}{3}\left({\textit{{P}}}_{12}\det({\textit{{P}}}_{34}^{34})+{\textit{{P}}}_{13}\det({\textit{{P}}}_{24}^{24})+{\textit{{P}}}_{14}\det({\textit{{P}}}_{23}^{23})\right.

\displaystyle\hskip 30.00005pt\left.+{\textit{{P}}}_{23}\det({\textit{{P}}}_{14}^{14})+{\textit{{P}}}_{24}\det({\textit{{P}}}_{13}^{13})+{\textit{{P}}}_{34}\det({\textit{{P}}}_{12}^{12})\right).

)) and permits to express

7
Conclusion

Classical results about the invariant theory of the polynomials on
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}^{n}

has allowed to provide a description in terms of generators and relations of the diagonal
centralizer of
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
. A precise connection with the higher rank Racah algebra was given.
Various questions arise and pave the way to
different generalizations.

We would like to emphasize that the natural numbers appearing in the numerator of the
Hilbert–Poincaré form very well-known series of integers that have numerous interpretations
and appear already in the study of the representation theory of
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
. This
suggests that there should be a further understanding of these numbers.

The classification of the finite irreducible representations of the rank 1 Racah
algebra has been done in
[
12
]
. Following this, it should be possible to
study the finite-dimensional representations of the (special) higher rank Racah algebra

R(n)
(resp.
sR(n)
). These representations must be closely related to the
operators associated to the
(n-2)
-variable Racah polynomials. Indeed, the difference and
recurrence operators characterizing the univariate Racah polynomials satisfy the
relations of
R(3)
. The study of the generalization to
(n-2)
-variable polynomials has
been initiated in
[
25
,
26
]
and it would be
interesting to verify if the operators used in this case realize
R(n)
or
sR(n)
. The
Racah algebra appears also as the symmetry algebra of some superintegrable models

[
13
,
14
,
15
,
16
,
17
]
. We trust that the observations and theorems of the present paper will lead to
a deeper understanding of this symmetry.

We focused here on the diagonal centralizer of
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
in the
n
-fold tensor
product of
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
. Other cases where
{\mathfrak{sl}}_{2}
is replaced by other algebras are
also known. For example the diagonal centralizer of the oscillator algebra has been
studied in
[
41
]
, the diagonal centralizer of the super Lie
algebra
\mathfrak{osp}(1|2)
is known to be related to the Bannai–Ito algebra
[
42
]

and the centralizer of
\mathfrak{sl}_{3}
in its twofold tensor product has been introduced
in
[
33
]
. An important generalization concerns the quantum
group
U_{q}(\mathfrak{sl}_{2})
. Its diagonal centralizer in the
3
-fold tensor product has
been examined
[
43
]
and is associated to the Askey–Wilson algebra (see e.g.

[
27
]
for a review). Many attempts

[
44
,
45
,
46
]
to generalize this result to

n
-fold tensor products have yielded relations of this centralizer but certainly did not
give all the defining relations. Looking ahead, we are planning to provide a complete set of
defining relations, by using some deformation of the defining relations of the special
Racah algebra given in this paper.

We have studied the centralizer
Z_{n}({\mathfrak{sl}}_{2})
at an algebraic level. It is however equally important to study this centralizer
when each factor in the
n
-fold tensor product is in a finite-dimensional irreducible representation of

\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
. In the case

n=3
, a conjecture stating that the centralizer is a quotient of the Racah algebra

R(3)
was given in
[
47
]
(see
[
48
]
for the
