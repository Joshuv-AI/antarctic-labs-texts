Can we observe non-perturbative vacuum shifts in cavity QED?
Rocío Sáez-Blázquez, Daniele de Bernardis, Johannes Feist, Peter Rabl

Abstract
We address the fundamental question whether or not it is possible to achieve conditions under which the coupling of a single dipole to a strongly confined electromagnetic vacuum can result in non-perturbative corrections to the dipole’s ground state. To do so we consider two simplified, but otherwise rather generic cavity QED setups, which allow us to derive analytic expressions for the total ground state energy and to distinguish explicitly between purely electrostatic and genuine vacuum-induced contributions. Importantly, this derivation takes the full electromagnetic spectrum into account while avoiding any ambiguities arising from an ad-hoc mode truncation. Our findings show that while the effect of confinement per se is not enough to result in substantial vacuum-induced corrections, the presence of high-impedance modes, such as plasmons or engineered L ​ C LC resonances, can drastically increase these effects. Therefore, we conclude that with appropriately designed experiments it is at least in principle possible to access a regime where light-matter interactions become non-perturbative.



The first theoretical prediction of the Lamb shift by Bethe in 1947
Bethe1947
was an important milestone of modern physics. Not only did he show that the quantized electromagnetic vacuum leads to an observable energy shift in the Hydrogen spectrum, but also how a finite value for this shift can be obtained from a divergent perturbation theory through renormalization. In recent years, vacuum-induced modifications of molecular properties have regained considerable attention in the context of cavity QED

;

;

;

;

, where the coupling of matter to individual electromagnetic modes is strongly enhanced by a tight confinement of the field. It has been speculated that under such
ultrastrong
coupling conditions

;
FriskKockum2019
, the electromagnetic vacuum could change the rate of chemical reactions

;
Thomas2016
;
Thomas2019
;

and (super-) conductivity

;

;
FornDiaz2019
;
FriskKockum2019
;
GarciaVidal2021
;
Schlawin2021
;
Ruggenthaler2022

per se
are incapable of making reliable predictions about the magnitude or even the sign of vacuum-induced energy shifts.

In this Letter we investigate the ground state energy shift of a single dipole due to its coupling to the electromagnetic vacuum in a confined geometry. Specifically, we focus on the two basic settings of a lumped-element
LC
-resonator and a nanoplasmonic cavity, which are representative for a large variety of ultrastrong coupling experiments
FornDiaz2019
;
FriskKockum2019
and allow us to perform an analytic and cutoff-independent derivation of the vacuum-induced shifts of the ground state. These shifts can further be interpreted in terms of purely electrostatic effects and genuine vacuum corrections and studied as a function of the relevant system parameters. This analysis explicitly shows that when relying on confinement only, the resulting energy shifts are dominated by electrostatic corrections, while contributions from dynamical modes remain perturbative at most. However, the effect of vacuum fluctuations can be strongly enhanced for electromagnetic modes with a high impedance, where the ratio between the electric and the magnetic field strength is modified compared to free space. This condition can be reached, for example, with appropriately designed lumped-element resonators or with plasmonic resonances, where the matter component contributes a large kinetic inductance. Indeed, we find that in both of the considered settings, non-perturbative shifts of the ground state, which are comparable to the bare transition frequency of the dipole, are feasible in principle. These predictions thus serve as an important guideline for designing experiments that can reach this regime as well as a benchmark for more refined models and numerical simulations of ultrastrong coupling physics.

Ground state energy shift.
—A central quantity of interest in ultrastrong-coupling cavity QED is the change in the ground state energy,

However, due to an infinite number of modes

Rokaj2022

Supp

\Delta E_{\rm GS}=\Delta E_{\rm im}+\Delta E_{{{\boldsymbol{A}}}}+\Delta E_{\rm cav}.

((4))

Summary and conclusions.
—In summary, we have presented an ab-initio and cutoff-independent derivation of the total ground state energy shift in two prototypical cavity QED settings. By focusing on simple geometries, we have obtained analytic predictions for the dependence of this shift on the most relevant experimental parameters and provided a clear distinction between purely electrostatic corrections and genuine vacuum effects. Our results demonstrate that the coupling to strongly confined transverse modes, as often considered in cavity QED, is not enough to induce significant perturbations of the ground state. The main effect of the confinement are electrostatic modifications, which in turn are often neglected in this context. These findings are consistent with the previous literature on Casimir-Polder interactions and establish a direct link between those closely related, but so far largely disconnected fields of research.

However, going beyond such conventional settings, our analysis predicts a significant enhancement of vacuum corrections in the presence of high-impedance modes. Under such conditions, the regime of non-perturbative cavity QED
DeBernardis2018PRA97
, with its many intriguing phenomena
Ridolfo2013
;
Galego2015
;
Flick2017
;
MartinezMartinez2018
;
Schlawin2019
;
Rivera2019
;
Schuler2020
;
Pilar2020
;
Li2020
;
Ashida2021
;
Couto2022
, comes within experimental reach. The current very general analysis can help to guide further experimental and theoretical progress in this direction.

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\nabla^{2}\phi=-\frac{\rho}{\epsilon_{0}},\qquad&\left(\frac{1}{c^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t^{2}}-\nabla^{2}\right){{\boldsymbol{A}}}=\frac{{{\boldsymbol{j}}^{\perp}}}{\epsilon_{0}c^{2}},\end{aligned}

((20))

where
c
is the speed of light,
\epsilon_{0}
is the vacuum permittivity, and

{{\boldsymbol{j}}}^{\perp}={{\boldsymbol{j}}}-\epsilon_{0}\nabla\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\phi

((21))

is the transverse current density.

As usual, the potential
\phi
is not an independent dynamical degree of freedom and represents the instantaneous potential,

\phi({{\boldsymbol{x}}},t)=\frac{1}{\epsilon_{0}}\int\mbox{d}^{3}x^{\prime}\,G({{\boldsymbol{x}}},{{\boldsymbol{x}}}^{\prime})\rho({{\boldsymbol{x}}}^{\prime},t),

((22))

associated with the charge distribution
\rho({{\boldsymbol{x}}}^{\prime},t)
. Here,
G({{\boldsymbol{x}}},{{\boldsymbol{x}}}^{\prime})
is the electrostatic Green’s function, which satisfies
\nabla^{2}G({{\boldsymbol{x}}},{{\boldsymbol{x}}}^{\prime})=-\delta({{\boldsymbol{x}}}-{{\boldsymbol{x}}}^{\prime})
and
G(z=0,{{\boldsymbol{x}}}^{\prime})=G(z=d,{{\boldsymbol{x}}}^{\prime})=0
. In the limit
d\ll l
, this Green’s function can be written as

G({{\boldsymbol{x}}},{{\boldsymbol{x}}}^{\prime})\simeq\frac{1}{4\pi|{{\boldsymbol{x}}}-{{\boldsymbol{x}}}^{\prime}|}+G_{\rm im}({{\boldsymbol{x}}},{{\boldsymbol{x}}}^{\prime}),

((23))

where
G_{\rm im}({{\boldsymbol{x}}},{{\boldsymbol{x}}}^{\prime})
accounts for the contribution from all the image charges in an infinite parallel plate configuration. Similarly, we can write

\phi({{\boldsymbol{x}}})\simeq\phi_{\rm free}({{\boldsymbol{x}}})+\phi_{\rm im}({{\boldsymbol{x}}}),

((24))

where
\phi_{\rm free}({{\boldsymbol{x}}})
is the unperturbed Coulomb potential in free space and
\phi_{\rm im}({{\boldsymbol{x}}})
is the additional potential arising from the image charges. Explicit expressions for
\phi_{\rm im}({{\boldsymbol{x}}})
will be derived below (see
Section II
).

For the vector potential we use the mode expansion

{{\boldsymbol{A}}}({{\boldsymbol{x}}},t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\epsilon_{0}}}\sum_{\lambda}\left[{\boldsymbol{u}}_{\lambda}({{\boldsymbol{x}}})\alpha_{\lambda}(t)+{\boldsymbol{u}}_{\lambda}^{*}({{\boldsymbol{x}}})\alpha^{*}_{\lambda}(t)\right],

((25))

where the amplitudes
\alpha_{\lambda}
are the dynamical degrees of freedom and the mode functions
{\boldsymbol{u}}_{\lambda}
satisfy the Helmholtz equation

\left(\frac{\omega_{\lambda}^{2}}{c^{2}}+\nabla^{2}\right){\boldsymbol{u}}_{\lambda}=0.

((26))

They are normalized to

\int_{V}\mbox{d}^{3}x\ {\boldsymbol{u}}_{\lambda}({{\boldsymbol{x}}})\cdot{\boldsymbol{u}}_{\lambda^{\prime}}^{*}({{\boldsymbol{x}}})=\delta_{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}.

((27))

The index
\lambda\equiv({{\boldsymbol{k}}},\sigma)
runs over two possible polarizations (
\sigma=1,2
) and all wavevectors
{{\boldsymbol{k}}}=(k_{x},k_{y},k_{z})
compatible with the boundary conditions (namely, perfect metallic boundary conditions in the
z
-direction and, for simplicity, periodic boundary conditions along
x
and
y
). Therefore,

k_{x}=\frac{2\pi}{l_{x}}n_{x}\ ,\hskip 8.53581ptk_{y}=\frac{2\pi}{l_{y}}n_{y}\ ,\hskip 8.53581ptk_{z}=\frac{\pi}{d}n\ ,\vskip 2.84526pt

((28))

where
n_{x},n_{y}\in\mathbb{Z}
and
n=0,1,2\dots
The frequency of the mode is given by
\omega_{\lambda}=ck
, where
k=|{\boldsymbol{k}}|
. Explicitly, the mode functions are given by

{\boldsymbol{u}}_{\lambda}({{\boldsymbol{x}}})=\sqrt{\frac{2}{V(1+\delta_{n0})}}\ {\rm{e}}^{\mbox{\scriptsize{i}}(k_{x}x+k_{y}y)}\begin{pmatrix}\mbox{i}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\lambda x}\sin(k_{z}z)\\
\mbox{i}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\lambda y}\sin(k_{z}z)\\
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\lambda z}\cos(k_{z}z)\end{pmatrix},

((29))

where
\delta_{n0}
is the Kronecker delta accounting for the extra factor when
n=0
. The (unit) polarization vectors

\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\lambda}(=\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}}\sigma})=(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\lambda x},\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\lambda y},\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\lambda z})

obey

{{\boldsymbol{k}}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}},\sigma}=0,\hskip 14.22636pt\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}},\sigma}^{*}\cdot\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}},\sigma^{\prime}}=\delta_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}.

((30))

I.1.2
LC
resonance

By allowing charges to flow on and off the metallic plates, we obtain the potential difference
U
as an additional dynamical degree of freedom, which is independent of the vector and scalar potentials introduced above. In the GHz and THz regime this degree of freedom can be generically modelled as a lumped-element
LC
resonator, with a capacitance
C=\epsilon_{0}A/d
formed by the two metallic plates and a system specific inductance
L
. By denoting by
Q
the total charge on the upper capacitor plate and by
\Phi
the magnetic flux in the inductor, the relevant circuit equations of motion are

\dot{\Phi}=U=\frac{Q-Q_{\rm ind}}{C},\qquad\dot{Q}=-\frac{\Phi}{L}.

((31))

Here,
Q_{\rm ind}
is the charge on the upper plate that is induced by the dipole and thus does not contribute to the potential difference
U
. For a large aspect ratio
l\gg d
and sufficiently far away from the edges of the metallic plates, this induced charge is well approximated by
Takae2013_SM

Q_{\rm ind}=\frac{z_{d}q}{d}.

((32))

The resulting equation of motion for the magnetic flux is thus given by
DeBernardis2018_SM

C\ddot{\Phi}+\frac{1}{L}\Phi\simeq-\frac{q\dot{z}_{d}}{d}.

((33))

I.2
Quantization

Starting from the equations of motion for the dipole and all electromagnetic degrees of freedom we obtain the Hamiltonian for this setup following the usual canonical quantization approach. First, the Lagrangian of the system reads

\begin{split}L=\frac{1}{2}C\dot{\Phi}^{2}-\frac{1}{2L}\Phi^{2}+\dot{\Phi}Q_{\rm ind}+\frac{1}{2}m\dot{\boldsymbol{r}}_{d}^{2}+\int\mbox{d}^{3}x\,\mathcal{L}_{\rm em}({\boldsymbol{x}})\end{split}

((34))

with the Lagrange density

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}_{\rm em}({\boldsymbol{x}})=&\frac{\epsilon_{0}}{2}\dot{\boldsymbol{A}}^{2}({\boldsymbol{x}},t)-\frac{1}{2\mu_{0}}\boldsymbol{B}^{2}({\boldsymbol{x}},t)\\
&+{{\boldsymbol{j}}^{\perp}}({\boldsymbol{x}},t)\cdot{\boldsymbol{A}}({\boldsymbol{x}},t)-\frac{1}{2}\rho({\boldsymbol{x}},t)\phi({\boldsymbol{x}},t).\end{split}

((35))

This yields the following canonical momenta for the system variables

\displaystyle\pi_{\Phi}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{\Phi}}=C\dot{\Phi}+Q_{\rm ind}=Q,

((36))

\displaystyle\pi_{{\boldsymbol{r}}_{d}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{{\boldsymbol{r}}}_{d}}=m\dot{\boldsymbol{r}}_{d}-q{\boldsymbol{A}}({\boldsymbol{r}}_{0}+{\boldsymbol{r}}_{d})={\boldsymbol{p}},

((37))

\displaystyle\pi_{\boldsymbol{A}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{\rm em}}{\partial\dot{\boldsymbol{A}}}=\epsilon_{0}\dot{\boldsymbol{A}}=-\epsilon_{0}{\boldsymbol{E}}_{\perp}.

((38))

By promoting these classical variables and their conjugate momenta to operators satisfying
[\Phi,Q]=\mbox{i}\hbar
and
[{\boldsymbol{r}}_{d},{\boldsymbol{p}}]=\mbox{i}\hbar
, we obtain the Hamiltonian operator for this system,

\begin{split}H&=\frac{({\boldsymbol{p}}+q{\boldsymbol{A}})^{2}}{2m}+V+\frac{(Q-Q_{\rm ind})^{2}}{2C}+\frac{\Phi^{2}}{2L}\\
&+\int\mbox{d}^{3}x\ \left\{\frac{\epsilon_{0}}{2}\boldsymbol{E}_{\perp}^{2}({\boldsymbol{x}},t)+\frac{1}{2\mu_{0}}\boldsymbol{B}^{2}({\boldsymbol{x}},t)\right\},\end{split}

((39))

where

V=\frac{1}{2}\int\mbox{d}^{3}x\,\rho({\boldsymbol{x}})\phi({\boldsymbol{x}})=V_{\rm free}+V_{\rm im}.

((40))

is the total Coulomb energy.

We proceed by expressing the quantized radiation field in terms of the usual annihilation and creation operators
a_{\lambda}
and
a^{\dagger}_{\lambda}
. For the
LC
resonator we introduce the corresponding operators
a_{c}
and
a_{c}^{\dagger}
via

Q=-\sqrt{\frac{\hbar\omega_{\rm c}C}{2}}(a_{c}^{\dagger}+a_{c})\ ,\qquad\Phi=\mbox{i}\sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{2C\omega_{\rm c}}}(a_{c}^{\dagger}-a_{c})\ ,

((41))

finally yielding

\begin{split}H&=\sum_{\lambda}\hbar\omega_{\lambda}a_{\lambda}^{\dagger}a_{\lambda}+\hbar\omega_{c}a_{c}^{\dagger}a_{c}+H_{\rm dip}^{0}+V_{\rm im}\\
&+\frac{q}{m}{\boldsymbol{A}}({\boldsymbol{r}}_{0})\cdot{\boldsymbol{p}}+\frac{q^{2}}{2m}{\boldsymbol{A}}^{2}({\boldsymbol{r}}_{0})\\
&+\frac{q}{d}\sqrt{\frac{\hbar\omega_{c}}{2C}}z_{d}(a_{c}+a_{c}^{\dagger})+\frac{q^{2}}{2d^{2}C}z_{d}^{2},\end{split}

((42))

where we have already made the dipole approximation
{\boldsymbol{A}}({\boldsymbol{r}}_{0}+{\boldsymbol{r}}_{d})\approx{\boldsymbol{A}}({\boldsymbol{r}}_{0})
and defined the bare dipole Hamiltonian as
H_{\rm dip}^{0}=\frac{{\boldsymbol{p}}^{2}}{2m}+V_{\rm free}
.

In a final step we introduce the dimensionless dipole transition operator
\mu=z_{d}/a_{0}
, where
a_{0}=|\langle 0|z_{d}|1\rangle|
, and define the coupling constant

\hbar g=\frac{qa_{0}}{d}\sqrt{\frac{\hbar\omega_{c}}{2C}}\ .

((43))

By considering the impedance of the circuit,
Z=\sqrt{L/C}
, the relevant dimensionless coupling parameter is given by
DeBernardis2018PRA97

\eta=\frac{g}{\omega_{\rm c}}=\frac{qa_{0}}{ed}\sqrt{2\pi\alpha\frac{Z}{Z_{\rm vac}}}\ ,

((44))

where
\alpha=e^{2}Z_{\rm vac}/(4\pi\hbar)
is the fine-structure constant and
Z_{\rm vac}=1/(\epsilon_{0}c)
is the impedance of free space.

II
Electrostatic potential for the parallel plate configuration

For the setup examined in
Section I
, here we compute the modification of the potential seen by the dipole due to the charges induced on the metallic plates.
For the particular case of a point charge
q
at position
{\boldsymbol{r}}^{\prime}
,

\rho({\boldsymbol{r}})=q\delta({\boldsymbol{r}}-{\boldsymbol{r}}^{\prime})
the electrostatic potential between the plates (
Eq. (22)
) reduces to

\phi^{(q)}({\boldsymbol{r}};{\boldsymbol{r}}^{\prime})=\frac{q}{\epsilon_{0}}G({{\boldsymbol{r}}},{{\boldsymbol{r}}^{\prime}})\ ,

((45))

where
G({\boldsymbol{r}},{\boldsymbol{r}}^{\prime})
is the Green’s function satisfying

\nabla^{2}G({{\boldsymbol{r}}},{{\boldsymbol{r}}^{\prime}})=-\delta({{\boldsymbol{r}}}-{{\boldsymbol{r}}^{\prime}})

((46))

and with the boundary condition
G(z=0,{\boldsymbol{r}}^{\prime})=G(z=d,{\boldsymbol{r}}^{\prime})=0
at the plates. In the other two directions we assume periodic boundary conditions,
G(x+l_{x},y+l_{y},z)=G(x,y,z)
, which can be enforced by changing to Fourier space, that is,

G({{\boldsymbol{r}}},{{\boldsymbol{r}}^{\prime}})=\frac{1}{l_{x}l_{y}}\sum_{k_{x},k_{y}}\ \mathcal{G}_{k}({z},{z^{\prime}})\mbox{e}^{-\mbox{\scriptsize{i}}k_{x}(x-x^{\prime})}\mbox{e}^{-\mbox{\scriptsize{i}}k_{y}(y-y^{\prime})},

((47))

with
\mathcal{G}_{k}(z=0,z^{\prime})=\mathcal{G}_{k}(z=d,z^{\prime})=0
and
k_{x}
and
k_{y}
as given in
Eq. (28)
. The substitution of this expression into
Eq. (46)
yields

\left(k_{\parallel}^{2}-\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial z^{2}}\right)\mathcal{G}_{k}(z,z^{\prime})=\delta(z-z^{\prime})\ ,

where
k_{\parallel}^{2}\equiv k_{x}^{2}+k_{y}^{2}
. The solution to this equation can be written as

\begin{split}\mathcal{G}_{k}&(z,z^{\prime})=\frac{1}{2k_{\parallel}}{\rm{e}}^{-k_{\parallel}|z-z^{\prime}|}-\frac{1}{2k_{\parallel}\sinh(k_{\parallel}d)}\\
&\left[\sinh(k_{\parallel}z)\ {\rm{e}}^{-k_{\parallel}(d-z^{\prime})}+\sinh[k_{\parallel}(d-z)]\ {\rm{e}}^{-k_{\parallel}z^{\prime}}\right],\end{split}

((48))

that is, the sum of the homogeneous solution (which represents the regular Coulomb interaction) and an additional term that follows from the boundary conditions (and is thus associated with the charges induced on the metallic plates). By considering the expansion
{1}/{\sinh(x)}=2\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\mbox{e}^{-x(2n+1)}
, the Green’s function reduces to
Takae2013_SM

\mathcal{G}_{k}(z,z^{\prime})=\frac{1}{2k_{\parallel}}\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\left(\mbox{e}^{-k_{\parallel}|z-z^{\prime}-2dn|}-\mbox{e}^{-k_{\parallel}|z+z^{\prime}-2dn|}\right).

((49))

Substituting this expression into
Eq. (47)
we obtain

\begin{split}G({{\boldsymbol{r}}},{{\boldsymbol{r}}^{\prime}})=\frac{1}{l_{x}l_{y}}\sum_{k_{x},k_{y}}\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\ \frac{1}{2k_{\parallel}}\left(\mbox{e}^{-k_{\parallel}|z-z^{\prime}-2dn|}\right.\\
\left.-\mbox{e}^{-k_{\parallel}|z+z^{\prime}-2dn|}\right)\mbox{e}^{-\mbox{\scriptsize{i}}k_{x}(x-x^{\prime})}\mbox{e}^{-\mbox{\scriptsize{i}}k_{y}(y-y^{\prime})},\end{split}

((50))

Finally, for
l_{x}=l_{y}=l\gg d
, we replace the sums over the
k
-vectors by integrals and end up with

\begin{split}G({{\boldsymbol{r}}},{{\boldsymbol{r}}^{\prime}})&=\frac{1}{4\pi}\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{|{\boldsymbol{r}}-{\boldsymbol{r}}^{\prime}+2nd\boldsymbol{e}_{z}|}\right.\\
&\left.-\frac{1}{|{\boldsymbol{r}}-{\boldsymbol{r}}^{\prime}+(2nd+2z^{\prime})\boldsymbol{e}_{z}|}\right).\end{split}

((51))

If we substitute this expression into
Eq. (45)
, we obtain the electrostatic potential associated with this configuration,

\begin{split}\phi^{(q)}({\boldsymbol{r}};{\boldsymbol{r}}^{\prime})=\frac{q}{4\pi\epsilon_{0}}\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}&\left(\frac{1}{|{\boldsymbol{r}}-{\boldsymbol{r}}^{\prime}+2nd\boldsymbol{e}_{z}|}\right.\\
&\left.-\frac{1}{|{\boldsymbol{r}}-{\boldsymbol{r}}^{\prime}+(2nd+2z^{\prime})\boldsymbol{e}_{z}|}\right).\end{split}

((52))

This corresponds to the potential created by an infinite collection of charges
+q
located at
z=z^{\prime}-2nd
and an infinite collection of charges
-q
at
z=-z^{\prime}-2nd
, with
n
running over all integers. Notice that the electrostatic potential in free space would reduce to the Coulomb expression

\phi_{\rm free}^{(q)}({\boldsymbol{r}};{\boldsymbol{r}}^{\prime})=\frac{q}{4\pi\epsilon_{0}}\frac{1}{|{\boldsymbol{r}}-{\boldsymbol{r}}^{\prime}|}\ .

((53))

In the setup considered here, a positive charge
+q
is fixed at position
{\boldsymbol{r}}_{0}=(0,0,z_{0})
while the opposite charge
-q
is displaced by
{\boldsymbol{r}}_{d}=(x_{d},y_{d},z_{d})
, resulting in the total potential

\phi({\boldsymbol{r}})=\phi^{(q)}({\boldsymbol{r}};{\boldsymbol{r}}_{0})+\phi^{(-q)}({\boldsymbol{r}};{\boldsymbol{r}}_{0}+{\boldsymbol{r}}_{d}),

((54))

where each of these terms are of the form given in
Eq. (52)
.
We are interested in the modification of the electrostatic energy due to the presence of the metallic plates, which is nothing but its difference with respect to free space,

V_{\rm im}=\frac{1}{2}\int\mbox{d}^{3}r\rho({\boldsymbol{r}})\left[\phi({\boldsymbol{r}})-\phi_{\rm free}({\boldsymbol{r}})\right].

((55))

Therefore, taking into account the charge density in
Eq. (16)
and the electrostatic potential in
Eq. (54)
, the electrostatic energy associated with the image charges becomes

\begin{split}V_{\rm im}=\frac{q}{2}&\left[\phi^{(q)}({\boldsymbol{r}}_{0},{\boldsymbol{r}}_{0})-\phi^{(q)}({\boldsymbol{r}}_{0}+{\boldsymbol{r}}_{d},{\boldsymbol{r}}_{0})\right.\\
&\left.+\phi^{(-q)}({\boldsymbol{r}}_{0},{\boldsymbol{r}}_{0}+{\boldsymbol{r}}_{d})-\phi^{(-q)}({\boldsymbol{r}}_{0}+{\boldsymbol{r}}_{d},{\boldsymbol{r}}_{0}+{\boldsymbol{r}}_{d})\right]\\
-\frac{q}{2}&\left[\phi^{(q)}_{\rm free}({\boldsymbol{r}}_{0},{\boldsymbol{r}}_{0})-\phi^{(q)}_{\rm free}({\boldsymbol{r}}_{0}+{\boldsymbol{r}}_{d},{\boldsymbol{r}}_{0})\right.\\
&\left.+\phi^{(-q)}_{\rm free}({\boldsymbol{r}}_{0},{\boldsymbol{r}}_{0}+{\boldsymbol{r}}_{d})-\phi^{(-q)}_{\rm free}({\boldsymbol{r}}_{0}+{\boldsymbol{r}}_{d},{\boldsymbol{r}}_{0}+{\boldsymbol{r}}_{d})\right].\end{split}

((56))

The explicit expression of these terms can be taken from
Eq. (52)
and
Eq. (53)
, yielding

\begin{split}V_{\rm im}=\frac{q^{2}}{4\pi\epsilon_{0}}&\left[\sum_{n\neq 0}\frac{1}{|2nd|}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n}\frac{1}{|2z_{0}+2nd|}\right.\\
&-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n}\frac{1}{|2z_{0}+2z_{d}+2nd|}\\
&-\sum_{n\neq 0}\frac{1}{\sqrt{x_{d}^{2}+y_{d}^{2}+(z_{d}+2nd)^{2}}}\\
&\left.+\sum_{n}\frac{1}{\sqrt{x_{d}^{2}+y_{d}^{2}+(2z_{0}+z_{d}+2nd)^{2}}}\right].\end{split}

((57))

This expression can be decomposed into the parts associated with the positive and negative charges,
V_{\rm im}=V_{\rm im}^{+}+V_{\rm im}^{-}
, where

\begin{split}V_{\rm im}^{+}=-\frac{q^{2}}{4\pi\epsilon_{0}}&\left[\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{x_{d}^{2}+y_{d}^{2}+(z_{d}+2nd)^{2}}}\right.\\
&+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{x_{d}^{2}+y_{d}^{2}+(z_{d}-2nd)^{2}}}\\
&\left.-\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{x_{d}^{2}+y_{d}^{2}+(2z_{0}+z_{d}+2nd)^{2}}}\right]\end{split}

((58))

and

\begin{split}V_{\rm im}^{-}=-\frac{q^{2}}{4\pi\epsilon_{0}d}&\left[\frac{d}{4z_{0}}-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{n}{n^{2}-(z_{0}/d)^{2}}\right.\\
&\left.+\frac{1}{4}\frac{d}{z_{0}+z_{d}}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{n}{n^{2}-[(z_{0}+z_{d})/d]^{2}}\right].\end{split}

((59))

).

III
Ground state energy for the
LC
-resonator setup

In this section we present the derivation of the energy shift experienced by the ground state of the matter system in the
LC
-resonator setup described in
Section I
. This shift can be decomposed into three different contributions,

\Delta E_{\rm GS}=\Delta E_{\rm im}+\Delta E_{{\boldsymbol{A}}}+\Delta E_{\rm cav}\ ,

((62))

which come from electrostatic corrections, the coupling to the transverse modes within the cavity, and the coupling to the
LC
resonance, respectively. With the purpose of determining the precise value of these corrections, we will consider the particular case of an isotropic harmonic dipole of mass
m
and frequency
\omega_{0}
. The wavefunction describing the ground state for the three-dimensional harmonic oscillator reads

\psi_{\rm GS}({\boldsymbol{r}}_{d})=\left(\frac{1}{2\pi a_{0}^{2}}\right)^{3/4}\mbox{e}^{-{\boldsymbol{r}}_{d}^{2}/(4a_{0}^{2})}\ ,

((63))

where
a_{0}\equiv\sqrt{\hbar/(2m\omega_{0})}
is the characteristic length of the dipole. Nonetheless, up to numerical prefactors, our predictions are valid for other cavity QED systems with a similar transition frequency.

III.1
Electrostatic effects

Due to the presence of induced charges on the cavity mirrors, the electrostatic energy presents a correction
V_{\rm im}
to that corresponding to free space, namely,
V=V_{\rm free}+V_{\rm im}
. To lowest order in perturbation theory this induces a shift of the dipole’s ground state energy of

\Delta E_{\rm im}=\langle\psi_{\rm GS}|V_{\rm im}|\psi_{\rm GS}\rangle.

((64))

In general, this expression can be evaluated numerically by averaging the full expression for
V_{\rm im}
in
Eq. (57)
over the ground state wavefunction. However, for most situations of interest, a good estimate of this shift can already be obtained by taking only the second-order expansion,
V_{\rm im}^{(2)}
in
Eq. (60)
into account, in which case

\Delta E_{\rm im}\simeq\langle\psi_{\rm GS}|V_{\rm im}^{(2)}|\psi_{\rm GS}\rangle=-V_{C}\frac{a_{0}^{3}}{d^{3}}\mathcal{F}_{\rm im}.

((65))

Here,
V_{C}=q^{2}/(4\pi\epsilon_{0}a_{0})
and we have defined the numerical prefactor

\begin{split}\mathcal{F}_{\rm im}=\frac{1}{8}\left[\frac{2}{(z_{0}/d)^{3}}-\Psi^{(2)}\left(1-\frac{z_{0}}{d}\right)-\Psi^{(2)}\left(1+\frac{z_{0}}{d}\right)\right].\end{split}

((66))

In the limit
z_{0}/d\rightarrow 0
, this factor can be approximated by

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\rm im}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4(z_{0}/d)^{3}}-\frac{1}{4}\Psi^{(2)}\left(1\right)+o(z_{0}/d)

((67))

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{4(z_{0}/d)^{3}}\ ,

((68))

thus reproducing the van der Waals interaction for a dipole close to a single plate,

\Delta E_{\rm im}^{(\rm vdW)}=-\frac{q^{2}}{4\pi\epsilon_{0}}\frac{a_{0}^{2}}{4z_{0}^{3}}\ .

((69))

In
Fig. 5
we plot the factor
\mathcal{F}_{\rm im}
as a function of the position of the positive charge,
z_{0}/d
, as well as its approximation in the single-plate limit.

III.2
Transverse modes

The ground state energy also becomes modified due to the coupling to the transverse electromagnetic field confined within the plates, where the interaction Hamiltonian is given by

H_{\perp}=\frac{q}{m}{\boldsymbol{A}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{p}}+\frac{q^{2}}{2m}{\boldsymbol{A}}^{2}.

((70))

For the potential operator
{\boldsymbol{A}}
we use the mode expansion in
Eq. (2)
.
Given the symmetry of our setup, the mode functions
{\boldsymbol{u}}_{\lambda}({{\boldsymbol{x}}})
in
Eq. (29)
can be rewritten in terms of the two-dimensional vectors
\boldsymbol{\rho}
,
{\boldsymbol{k}}_{\parallel}
, and
{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\lambda}}_{\parallel}
giving the components parallel to the
xy
-plane of the corresponding three-dimensional vectors, namely,
{\boldsymbol{r}}:(\boldsymbol{\rho},z)
,
{\boldsymbol{k}}:({\boldsymbol{k}}_{\parallel},k_{z})
, and
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\lambda}:({\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\lambda}}_{\parallel},{\varepsilon_{\lambda}}_{z})
, yielding

\boldsymbol{u}_{\lambda}({\boldsymbol{x}})=\sqrt{\frac{2}{V(1+\delta_{n0})}}\ {\rm{e}}^{\mbox{\scriptsize{i}}{\boldsymbol{k}}_{\parallel}\cdot\boldsymbol{\rho}}\begin{pmatrix}\mbox{i}{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\lambda}}_{\parallel}\sin(k_{z}z)\\
{\varepsilon_{\lambda}}_{z}\cos(k_{z}z)\end{pmatrix}.

((71))

From
Eq. (28)
, we can express
k_{\parallel}=|{\boldsymbol{k}}_{\parallel}|
and
k=|{\boldsymbol{k}}|
in terms of the indices
\kappa=\sqrt{n_{x}^{2}+n_{y}^{2}}
and
\nu=\sqrt{({4}/{x^{2}})\kappa^{2}+n^{2}}
,

\displaystyle k_{\parallel}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{k_{x}^{2}+k_{y}^{2}}=\frac{2\pi}{l}\kappa=\frac{\pi}{d}\frac{2\kappa}{x}\ ,

((72))

\displaystyle k

\displaystyle=\sqrt{k_{\parallel}^{2}+k_{z}^{2}}=\frac{\pi}{d}\nu\ ,

((73))

where the length
l
is given in terms of the distance
d
between the plates through the ratio parameter
x
, that is,
l=xd
. Likewise, we can define the wavevector
k_{0}
and the dimensionless number
\nu_{0}
associated with the transition within the molecular dipole,

\omega_{0}=ck_{0}=\frac{\pi c}{d}\nu_{0}\ .

((74))

III.2.1
Perturbation theory and renormalization

Using perturbation theory up to second order in
q
, the correction to the ground state energy presents two contributions,

\Delta\tilde{E}_{{\boldsymbol{A}}}=\Delta E_{{\boldsymbol{A}}^{2}}+\Delta E_{{\boldsymbol{A}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{p}}}\ ,

((75))

coming from each of the terms in the interaction Hamiltonian (
Eq. (70)
), with

\displaystyle\Delta E_{{\boldsymbol{A}}^{2}}=\frac{q^{2}\hbar}{2mc\epsilon_{0}V}{\sum_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}}^{\prime}\frac{1}{k}\left[1-\frac{k_{z}^{2}}{k^{2}}\cos(2k_{z}z_{0})\right],

((76))

\displaystyle\Delta E_{{\boldsymbol{A}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{p}}}=-\frac{q^{2}}{m^{2}c^{2}\epsilon_{0}V}\sum_{j>0}{\sum_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}}^{\prime}\frac{1}{k(k+k_{j})}

\displaystyle\left[\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\frac{k_{z}^{2}}{k^{2}}\right)\sin^{2}(k_{z}z_{0})p_{j\parallel}^{2}+\left(1-\frac{k_{z}^{2}}{k^{2}}\right)\cos^{2}(k_{z}z_{0})p_{jz}^{2}\right].

((77))

The prime in the summation over
{\boldsymbol{k}}
recalls the extra factor 1/2 associated with
n=0
(coming from the renormalization of the mode functions in
Eq. (71)
) and the summation over polarizations has been already performed using that

\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma}|\varepsilon_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}}\sigma z}|^{2}=1-\frac{k_{z}^{2}}{k^{2}}\ ,\hskip 14.22636pt\sum_{\sigma}|\varepsilon_{{{\boldsymbol{k}}}\sigma\parallel}|^{2}=1+\frac{k_{z}^{2}}{k^{2}}\ .

((78))

Besides, we have defined the matrix elements
p_{j\parallel}^{2}\equiv|\langle j|p_{x}|0\rangle|^{2}+|\langle 1|p_{y}|0\rangle|^{2}
and
p_{jz}^{2}\equiv|\langle j|p_{z}|0\rangle|^{2}
, where the index
j
labels all possible excited states of the matter system (characterized by the transition frequency
\omega_{j}=ck_{j}
to the ground state). In the case of the harmonic oscillator, the first excited state is the only one that contributes, and
p_{z}^{2}=p_{\parallel}^{2}/2=\hbar m\omega_{m}/2
. Therefore,
Eq. (77)
simplifies to

\begin{split}\Delta E_{{\boldsymbol{A}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{p}}}=-\frac{q^{2}\hbar}{2mc\epsilon_{0}V}{\sum_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}}^{\prime}\frac{k_{m}}{k(k+k_{m})}\left[1-\frac{k_{z}^{2}}{k^{2}}\cos(2k_{z}z_{0})\right].\end{split}

((79))

When the plates have infinite extension (
l\rightarrow\infty
), we can transform the sum over the parallel component of the wavevector into an integral, yielding

\begin{split}\Delta E_{{\boldsymbol{A}}^{2}}&=\frac{q^{2}\hbar}{mc\epsilon_{0}l^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mbox{d}{\kappa}\ \kappa{\sum_{n}}^{\prime}\frac{1}{\nu}\left[1-\frac{n^{2}}{\nu^{2}}\cos\left(\frac{2\pi nz_{0}}{d}\right)\right]\\
&=\frac{q^{2}\hbar}{4mc\epsilon_{0}d^{2}}{\sum_{n}}^{\prime}\int_{n}^{\infty}\mbox{d}\nu\,\left[1-\frac{n^{2}}{\nu^{2}}\cos\left(\frac{2\pi nz_{0}}{d}\right)\right],\end{split}

((80))

where in the second line we changed the integration variable from
\kappa
to
\nu
. Similarly,

\begin{split}\Delta E_{{\boldsymbol{A}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{p}}}=-\frac{q^{2}\hbar}{4mc\epsilon_{0}d^{2}}{\sum_{n}}^{\prime}\int_{n}^{\infty}\mbox{d}\nu&\frac{\nu_{0}}{\nu+\nu_{0}}\\
&\left[1-\frac{n^{2}}{\nu^{2}}\cos\left(\frac{2\pi nz_{0}}{d}\right)\right].\end{split}

((81))

The sums in
Eq. (80)
and
Eq. (81)
are performed over an infinite number of modes, which would lead to an infinite value of the energy shift. To obtain a finite expression for the energy shift, we must keep in mind that
\Delta E_{{\boldsymbol{A}}}
is defined relative to the ground state energy in free space,

\Delta E_{{\boldsymbol{A}}}=\Delta\tilde{E}_{{\boldsymbol{A}}}-\left.\Delta\tilde{E}_{{\boldsymbol{A}}}\right|_{\rm free}.

((82))

We can obtain the free-space contribution from
Eq. (80)
and
Eq. (81)
above by replacing the discrete sum over
n
by an integral and ignoring the terms that depend explicitly on
z_{0}
. As a result, the final expression for the energy shift
\Delta E_{{\boldsymbol{A}}}
can be written as

\begin{split}\Delta E_{{\boldsymbol{A}}}=\alpha\hbar\omega_{m}\left(\frac{qa_{0}}{ed}\right)^{2}\mathcal{F}_{{\boldsymbol{A}}},\end{split}

((83))

where

\mathcal{F}_{{\boldsymbol{A}}}=2\pi\left[g_{\boldsymbol{A}^{2}}+f_{\boldsymbol{A}^{2}}(z_{0}/d)-g_{\boldsymbol{A}\cdot\boldsymbol{p}}(\nu_{0})-f_{\boldsymbol{A}\cdot\boldsymbol{p}}(\nu_{0},z_{0}/d)\right]

((84))

is a dimensionless function. Here we have defined the two quantities

\displaystyle{g_{\boldsymbol{A}^{2}}}

\displaystyle={\sum_{n}}^{\prime}\int_{n}^{\infty}\mbox{d}\nu-\int_{0}^{\infty}\mbox{d}n\ \int_{n}^{\infty}\mbox{d}\nu\ ,

((85))

\displaystyle{f_{\boldsymbol{A}^{2}}}(z_{0}/d)

\displaystyle=-{\sum_{n}}^{\prime}n^{2}\cos\left(\frac{2\pi nz_{0}}{d}\right)\int_{n}^{\infty}\frac{\mbox{d}{\nu}}{\nu^{2}}\ ,

((86))

which arise from the evaluation of the
{\boldsymbol{A}}^{2}
contribution. The other two functions

\displaystyle g_{\boldsymbol{A}\cdot\boldsymbol{p}}(\nu_{0})

\displaystyle={\sum_{n}}^{\prime}\int_{n}^{\infty}\frac{\nu_{0}}{\nu+\nu_{0}}\mbox{d}\nu

((87))

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{\infty}\mbox{d}n\int_{n}^{\infty}\frac{\nu_{0}}{\nu+\nu_{0}}\mbox{d}\nu

((88))

and

\displaystyle f_{\boldsymbol{A}\cdot\boldsymbol{p}}(\nu_{0},z_{0}/d)

\displaystyle=-{\sum_{n}}^{\prime}\cos\left(\frac{2\pi nz_{0}}{d}\right)\int_{n}^{\infty}\frac{n^{2}\nu_{0}}{\nu^{2}(\nu+\nu_{0})}\mbox{d}\nu

((89))

arise from the evaluation of the
{\boldsymbol{A}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{p}}
term and depend on the dipole frequency
\nu_{0}
.

III.2.2
Evaluation of the individual sums and integrals

The expressions in
Eq. (85)
-
Eq. (89)
have previously been derived in Ref.
Barton1970_SM
, where the author then introduced a sharp frequency cutoff
\Lambda
to evaluate the otherwise diverging sums and integrals. However, working with a sharp cutoff is somewhat ambiguous since some of the expressions do not converge for
\Lambda\rightarrow\infty
and an additional ad-hoc averaging procedure must be performed in order to get meaningful results. In the following we use a combined analytic and numerical approach to evaluate
Eq. (85)
-
Eq. (89)
also for a smooth and therefore more physical cutoff function. Importantly, this analysis shows that all results are independent of the details of the cutoff function and also that convergence is reached for rather modest values of the cutoff. We further provide additional analytic results for the limit of strong confinement,
\nu_{0}\rightarrow 0
, which is the regime most relevant for the current study.

We start with the evaluation of the function
g_{\boldsymbol{A}^{2}}
in
Eq. (85)
, which is the difference between a diverging sum and a diverging integral. To obtain a finite result, we need to introduce a cutoff scale
\Lambda
at high frequency for both the sum and the integral
Casimir1984_SM
. We can use either a sharp cutoff at
\nu=\Lambda
or multiply the integrand by a smooth cutoff function
h_{c}(\nu/\Lambda)
. In particular, we consider a cutoff function that satisfies
h_{c}(0)=1
,
h_{c}^{\prime}(0)\simeq 0
, and
h_{c}(\nu/\Lambda\gg 1)\rightarrow 0
sufficiently fast.
In that case,
Eq. (85)
transforms to

g_{\boldsymbol{A}^{2}}=-\frac{1}{2}F(0,\nu/\Lambda)+{\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}}F(n,\nu/\Lambda)-\int_{0}^{\infty}\mbox{d}n\ F(n,\nu/\Lambda)\ ,

((90))

with
F(n,\nu/\Lambda)=\int_{n}^{\infty}h_{c}(\nu/\Lambda)\ \mbox{d}\nu
and where we have already taken into account the extra factor associated with the summand
n=0
.
The difference between the sum and the integral can be evaluated using the Euler–Maclaurin formula and we obtain

\begin{split}g_{\boldsymbol{A}^{2}}=-\frac{B_{2}}{2}F^{\prime}(0,\nu/\Lambda)+o[F^{\prime\prime\prime}(0,\nu/\Lambda)]\ ,\end{split}

((91))

where
B_{2}=1/6
is the second Bernoulli number and the apostrophe indicates derivative with respect to
n
. We have assumed that
F(n,\nu/\Lambda)
and all its derivatives go to zero for
n\rightarrow\infty
. Further, the higher-order derivatives of
F
decrease with increasing
\Lambda
and can therefore be neglected. Since
F^{\prime}(0,\nu/\Lambda)=-h_{c}(0)=-1
, we finally have that

g_{\boldsymbol{A}^{2}}=\frac{1}{12},

((92))

regardless of the precise details of the cutoff function.

Note that the same result has been obtained in Ref.
Barton1970_SM
by introducing a sharp cutoff, i.e.,
h_{c}(\nu/\Lambda)=\Theta(1-\nu/\Lambda)
, where
\Theta
is the unit step function. However, as shown in
Fig. 6
(a) this creates an ambiguity, since in this case the value of
g_{\boldsymbol{A}^{2}}
varies periodically with
\Lambda
and
g_{\boldsymbol{A}^{2}}=1/12
is only obtained on average. In contrast, for the smooth cutoff assumed in
Fig. 6
(b), the value of
g_{\boldsymbol{A}^{2}}
converges toward the analytic prediction. This plot also shows that this convergence happens already for very small values of
\Lambda\approx 5
.

Now we turn to the computation of the term that depends on the particle position,
f_{\boldsymbol{A}^{2}}
(
Eq. (86)
). By solving the integral over

, we obtain

numerically from

. As above, a high-frequency limit can be introduced via a smooth cutoff function

. The latter yields the following expression for

where

f_{\boldsymbol{A}^{2}}
,
g_{\boldsymbol{A}\cdot\boldsymbol{p}}
and
f_{\boldsymbol{A}\cdot\boldsymbol{p}}
, which appear in the expression for
\Delta E_{{\boldsymbol{A}}}
in
Eq. (83)
. In the limit of strong confinement,
\nu_{0}\ll 1
, we approximately obtain

\begin{split}\mathcal{F}_{{\boldsymbol{A}}}\simeq 2\pi&\left[\frac{1}{12}+\frac{1}{4\sin^{2}(\pi z_{0}/d)}\right.\\
&\left.-\nu_{0}\left(\nu_{0}+\frac{1}{2}\right)\ln\left(\frac{\nu_{0}+1}{\nu_{0}}\right)+\frac{2\nu_{0}}{3}\right].\end{split}

((113))

The contributions in the second line, which arise from the
{\boldsymbol{p}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{A}}
term, vanish as
\nu_{0}\rightarrow 0
and therefore we obtain
\mathcal{F}_{{\boldsymbol{A}}}(\nu_{0}\rightarrow 0,z_{0}/d=1/2)\approx 2\pi/3
. In the opposite limit,
\nu_{0}\gg 1
, we find instead

\begin{split}\mathcal{F}_{{\boldsymbol{A}}}\simeq\frac{\mathcal{F}_{\rm im}(z_{0}/d)}{\pi\nu_{0}}+o[\nu_{0}^{-2}].\end{split}

((114))

Here the first term cancels exactly the electrostatic contribution,
\Delta E_{\rm im}
, such that the total energy shift will be determined by higher-order terms in the expansions in
Eq. (99)
and
Eq. (109)
. This corresponds to the regime of retarded Casimir-Polder interactions, which, in view of the small absolute value of vacuum shifts, is less relevant for the current study. Finally, we can use numerics to evaluate
\mathcal{F}_{{\boldsymbol{A}}}
also for all intermediate values of
\nu_{0}
and the results are summarized in
Fig. 10
(a). In
Fig. 10
(b), we also show the ratio
\Delta E_{{\boldsymbol{A}}}/|\Delta E_{\rm im}|
, which verifies the bound given in
Eq. (8)
in the main text.

IV
Plasmonic cavities

In this section we provide a detailed derivation of all the results concerning the plasmonic nanosphere setup discussed in the second part of the main text. Here a single dipole is coupled to the quantized plasmon modes of a metallic nanosphere of radius
R
and electron density
n_{0}
. To describe plasmonic excitations, we follow Ref.
Prodan2004_SM
and model the electron gas as an incompressible, irrotational fluid. Then, for small displacements of this electron fluid, the plasmonic excitations are fully described in terms of the surface charge density
\sigma(t)
. Further, we can assume that for small enough dimensions, interactions between the electrons and between the electrons and the dipole are only due to Coulomb interactions. Therefore, in the following analysis we ignore all interactions with the transverse electromagnetic field, which would contribute additional corrections
\sim O(\alpha)
.

IV.1
Quantized plasmon modes

For an irrotational fluid with
\nabla\times{{\boldsymbol{v}}}=0
, the velocity field
{{\boldsymbol{v}}}
can be written as the gradient of a scalar field
\eta
, which obeys the Laplace equation
\nabla^{2}\eta=0
and satisfies the boundary condition
Prodan2004_SM

\dot{\sigma}=n_{0}e\partial_{r}\eta.

((115))

In the following we expand the surface density in terms of the spherical harmonics
Y_{\ell,m}
,

\sigma(t,\widehat{{\boldsymbol{R}}})=n_{0}e\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-\ell}^{\ell}\sqrt{\frac{4\pi\ell}{3}}\xi_{\ell,m}(t)Y_{\ell,m}(\widehat{{\boldsymbol{R}}}).

((116))

Here we introduced the convention
\widehat{{\boldsymbol{x}}}\equiv(\theta,\varphi)
, where
\theta
and
\varphi
are the polar and the azimuthal angles of
{{\boldsymbol{x}}}
, respectively. The amplitudes
\xi_{\ell,m}(t)
are the dynamical degrees of freedom and have units of length. Correspondingly, for the scalar field we obtain the expansion

\eta({{\boldsymbol{r}}},t)=R\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-\ell}^{\ell}\sqrt{\frac{4\pi}{3\ell}}\left(\frac{r}{R}\right)^{\ell}Y_{\ell,m}(\widehat{{\boldsymbol{r}}})\dot{\xi}_{\ell,m}(t),

((117))

where we made use of Eq. (
(115)
).

The Lagrangian of the plasmonic sphere is
\mathcal{L}_{P}=T_{P}-V_{P}
. The kinetic energy is given by
Prodan2004

\begin{split}T_{P}=&\frac{n_{0}m_{e}}{2}\int\eta\partial_{r}\eta\,dS=\frac{M}{2}\sum_{\ell,m}\dot{\xi}^{2}_{\ell,m}(t),\end{split}

((118))

where
M=4\pi R^{3}n_{0}m_{e}/3
is the total mass of all electrons. The potential term,
V_{P}
, accounts for the Coulomb interaction between the surface charges,

V_{P}=\frac{1}{8\pi\epsilon_{0}}\int dS\int dS^{\prime}\frac{\sigma(\widehat{{\boldsymbol{r}}})\sigma(\widehat{{\boldsymbol{r}}}^{\prime})}{|{{\boldsymbol{r}}}-{{\boldsymbol{r}}}^{\prime}|},

((119))

where the integration runs over the surface
S
of the sphere. To evaluate this integral, we make use of the Laplace expansion,

\frac{1}{|{{\boldsymbol{r}}}-{{\boldsymbol{r}}}^{\prime}|}=\frac{4\pi}{R}\sum_{\ell,m}\frac{1}{2\ell+1}Y^{*}_{\ell,m}(\widehat{{\boldsymbol{r}}})Y_{\ell,m}(\widehat{{\boldsymbol{r}}}^{\prime}),

((120))

and obtain

\mathcal{L}_{P}=\frac{M}{2}\sum_{\ell,m}\left[\dot{\xi}^{2}_{\ell,m}(t)-\omega_{\ell}^{2}\xi^{2}_{\ell,m}(t)\right].

((121))

Here,

\omega_{\ell}=\sqrt{\frac{\ell}{2\ell+1}}\omega_{P},\qquad\omega_{P}=\sqrt{\frac{n_{0}e^{2}}{\epsilon_{0}m_{e}}},

((122))

are the frequencies of the plamonic eigenmodes of the sphere. Since the plamonic excitations are harmonic, we immediately obtain the
Hamiltonian of the quantized nanosphere,

H_{P}=\sum_{\ell,m}\frac{p_{\ell,m}^{2}}{2M}+\frac{1}{2}M\omega_{\ell}^{2}\xi^{2}_{\ell,m}=\sum_{\ell,m}\hbar\omega_{\ell}a^{\dagger}_{\ell,m}a_{\ell,m},

((123))

where the
p_{\ell,m}
are the canonical momentum operators and
a_{\ell,m}
and
a^{\dagger}_{\ell,m}
are bosonic annihilation and creation operators defined via

\displaystyle\xi_{\ell,m}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{2M\omega_{\ell}}}\left(a^{\dagger}_{\ell,m}+a_{\ell,m}\right),

((124))

\displaystyle p_{\ell,m}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle i\sqrt{\frac{\hbar M\omega_{\ell}}{2}}\left(a^{\dagger}_{\ell,m}-a_{\ell,m}\right).

((125))

The quantized surface charge density is given by

\sigma(t,\widehat{{\boldsymbol{R}}})=n_{0}e\sum_{\ell,m}\sqrt{\frac{2\pi\hbar\ell}{3M\omega_{\ell}}}Y_{\ell,m}(\widehat{{\boldsymbol{R}}})\left(a_{\ell,m}+a^{\dagger}_{\ell,m}\right).

((126))

IV.2
Dipole-plasmon interactions

We now consider a point-like dipole with a positive charge
+q
placed at a fixed distance
D=R+z_{0}
away from the center of the sphere. We model the motion of the negative charge as an effective particle moving in a potential
V_{d}(z_{d})
along the z-direction, but ignore the motion along the
x
and
y
directions for simplicity. Then, the total Lagrangian for this system is

\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{P}+\frac{m\dot{z}_{d}^{2}}{2}-V_{d}(z_{d})-V_{\rm int}.

((127))

Here,
V_{\rm int}
accounts for the electrostatic interaction between the dipole and the surface charges on the sphere, which under the dipole approximation is given by

V_{\rm int}=-\frac{qz_{d}}{4\pi\epsilon_{0}}\nabla_{D}\int dS\frac{\sigma({{\boldsymbol{r}}})}{|D{\bf e}_{z}-{{\boldsymbol{r}}}|}.

((128))

We can use again the Laplace expansion,

\begin{split}\frac{1}{|D{\bf e}_{z}-{{\boldsymbol{r}}}|}&=\sum_{\ell,m}\frac{4\pi}{2\ell+1}\frac{r^{\ell}}{D^{\ell+1}}Y^{*}_{\ell,m}(-\widehat{{\boldsymbol{r}}})Y_{\ell,m}(0,0)\\
&=\sum_{\ell}\sqrt{\frac{4\pi}{2\ell+1}}\frac{r^{\ell}}{D^{\ell+1}}Y_{\ell,0}(\widehat{{\boldsymbol{r}}}),\end{split}

((129))

and write the interaction as

V_{\rm int}=\frac{n_{0}eqz_{d}}{4\pi\epsilon_{0}}\sum_{\ell}\sqrt{\frac{4\pi}{2\ell+1}}\sqrt{\frac{4\pi\ell}{3}}(\ell+1)\left(\frac{R}{D}\right)^{\ell+2}\xi_{\ell,0}.

((130))

The total Hamiltonian is then given by

H=H_{\rm dip}^{0}+\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\frac{p_{\ell}^{2}}{2M}+\frac{1}{2}M\omega_{\ell}^{2}\xi^{2}_{\ell}+\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}K_{\ell}\xi_{\ell}z_{d}.

((131))

where
H_{\rm dip}^{0}
is the Hamiltonian of the bare dipole and

K_{\ell}=\frac{n_{0}eq}{\epsilon_{0}}\sqrt{\frac{\ell(\ell+1)^{2}}{6\ell+3}}\left(\frac{R}{D}\right)^{\ell+2}.

((132))

Note that in Eq. (
(131)
) we have already omitted the sum over the uncoupled modes with
m\neq 0
and redefined
\xi_{\ell}\equiv\xi_{\ell,0}
for brevity.

IV.3
Electrostatics

From the Hamiltonian in Eq. (
(131)
) we obtain the equation of motion for the dipole,

\ddot{z}_{d}=-\partial_{z_{d}}V_{d}(z_{d})-\sum_{\ell}K_{\ell}\xi_{\ell}.

((133))

If we assume that the dipole moves slowly compared to the plasmon frequencies, the plasmon modes follow the dipole adiabatically and assume a value

\xi_{\ell}(t)\simeq-\frac{K_{\ell}}{M\omega_{\ell}^{2}}z_{d}(t).

((134))

When we plug this result back into
Eq. (133)
, we can derive an effective potential,

\begin{split}V_{\rm im}(z_{d})&=-\frac{z_{d}^{2}}{2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\frac{K^{2}_{\ell}}{M\omega_{\ell}^{2}}\\
&=-\frac{q^{2}z_{d}^{2}}{8\pi\epsilon_{0}R^{3}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}(\ell+1)^{2}\left(\frac{R}{R+z_{0}}\right)^{2\ell+4}\\
&=-\frac{q^{2}z_{d}^{2}}{4\pi\epsilon_{0}z_{0}^{3}}\mathcal{F}_{\rm im}^{{\rm sp}}(z_{0}/R),\end{split}

((135))

which just corresponds to the electrostatic potential of a static dipole in front of the sphere. Here we introduced the function

\mathcal{F}_{\rm im}^{{\rm sp}}(x)=\frac{x^{3}}{2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}(\ell+1)^{2}\left(\frac{1}{1+x}\right)^{2\ell+4},

((136))

with an asymptotic value
\mathcal{F}_{\rm im}^{{\rm sp}}(x\ll 1)\approx 1/8
. Therefore, for
z_{0}\ll R
, i.e., when the dipole sees an almost flat surface, we recover the usual van der Waals potential,

V_{\rm im}(z_{d})\simeq-\frac{q^{2}z_{d}^{2}}{4\pi\epsilon_{0}(2z_{0})^{3}}.

((137))

Note that here only the dipole moment along the
z
-direction is taken into account.

IV.4
Cavity QED Hamiltonian

To derive an appropriate multi-mode cavity QED Hamiltonian for this system we set
z_{d}=a_{0}\mu
and write the plasmon amplitudes in terms of
a_{\ell}
and
a_{\ell}^{\dagger}
. We obtain

H=H_{\rm dip}^{0}+\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\hbar\omega_{\ell}a_{\ell}^{\dagger}a_{\ell}+\hbar\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}g_{\ell}(a_{\ell}+a_{\ell}^{\dagger})\mu.

((138))

Here, we have introduced the coupling constants

g_{\ell}=g_{P}\frac{\ell+1}{2}\sqrt[4]{\frac{\ell}{2\ell+1}}\left(\frac{R}{R+z_{0}}\right)^{\ell+2},

((139))

where

\frac{g_{P}}{\omega_{P}}=\frac{qa_{0}}{eR}\sqrt{2\pi\alpha\frac{Z_{P}}{Z_{\rm vac}}}

((140))

and
Z_{P}=1/(\pi\epsilon_{0}R\omega_{P})
is the characteristic impedance of the fundamental plasmon mode.

Note that Hamiltonian (
(138)
) includes only the coupling to dynamical modes, while electrostatic effects arise through virtual excitations of those modes, as illustrated by the adiabatic elimination procedure in
Subsection IV.3
. In the following we use this adiabatic elimination for all modes with index
\ell>\ell_{\rm max}
, but we keep the full dynamics of all other modes up to
\ell=\ell_{\rm max}
. We then make use of the general relation
DeBernardis2018PRA97

-\sum_{\ell=\ell_{\rm max}+1}^{\infty}\frac{\hbar g_{\ell}^{2}}{\omega_{\ell}}\mu^{2}=V_{\rm im}(z_{d})+\sum_{\ell=1}^{\ell_{\rm max}}\frac{\hbar g_{\ell}^{2}}{\omega_{\ell}}\mu^{2}

((141))

to rewrite the remaining multi-mode cavity QED Hamiltonian in its canonical form

\begin{split}H=&H_{\rm dip}^{0}+V_{\rm im}(z_{d})+\sum_{\ell=1}^{\ell_{\rm max}}\hbar\omega_{\ell}a_{\ell}^{\dagger}a_{\ell}\\
&+\hbar\sum_{\ell=1}^{\ell_{\rm max}}g_{\ell}(a_{\ell}+a_{\ell}^{\dagger})\mu+\sum_{\ell=1}^{\ell_{\rm max}}\frac{\hbar g_{\ell}^{2}}{\omega_{\ell}}\mu^{2}.\end{split}

((142))

This representation has the advantage that the electrostatic contribution appears explicitly, while the added
P^{2}
-terms for each dynamical mode avoid an unphysical double counting of static interactions. For a weak and near-resonant coupling to the lowest mode, we can set
\ell_{\rm max}=1
to obtain the correct single-mode cavity QED Hamiltonian. However, for the current analysis we do not make any restrictions on
\ell_{\rm max}
yet.

IV.5
Ground state energy

Since we ignore the corrections from the transverse modes in this calculation, the shift of the ground state,

\Delta E_{\rm GS}=\Delta E_{\rm im}+\Delta E_{P},

((143))

contains a first-order contribution from the image potential,
V_{\rm im}
, and a second-order contribution from the coupling to all plasmon modes up to
\ell_{\rm max}
. The electrostatic contribution can be obtained by averaging the expression in Eq. (
(135)
) over the ground-state wavefunction and it reduces to the van der Waals energy for small
z_{0}
. From the second-order coupling to the plasmon modes we obtain

\Delta E_{P}=-\sum_{\ell=1}^{\ell_{\rm max}}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{\hbar g_{\ell}^{2}|\langle 0|\mu|j\rangle|^{2}}{\omega_{\ell}+\omega_{j}}+\sum_{\ell=1}^{\ell_{\rm max}}\frac{\hbar g_{\ell}^{2}}{\omega_{\ell}}\langle 0|\mu^{2}|0\rangle,

((144))

where the states
|j\rangle
are the eigenstates of
H_{\rm dip}^{0}
with eigenfrequencies
\omega_{j}
. To simplify the following analysis we consider a harmonic oscillator with frequency
\omega_{0}
. Then,

\begin{split}\Delta E_{P}=&\sum_{\ell=1}^{\ell_{\rm max}}\hbar g_{\ell}^{2}\left[\frac{1}{\omega_{\ell}}-\frac{1}{\omega_{\ell}+\omega_{0}}\right]=\hbar\omega_{0}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\ell_{\rm max}}\frac{g_{\ell}^{2}}{\omega_{\ell}(\omega_{\ell}+\omega_{0})}\\
=&\alpha\hbar\omega_{0}\frac{Z_{P}}{Z_{\rm vac}}\left(\frac{qa_{0}}{eR}\right)^{2}\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{P}\left(x=\frac{\omega_{0}}{\omega_{P}},y=\frac{z_{0}}{R}\right),\end{split}

((145))

where

\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{P}(x,y)=\frac{\pi}{2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\ell_{\rm max}}\frac{(\ell+1)^{2}\sqrt{(2\ell+1)/\ell}}{1+x\sqrt{(2\ell+1)/\ell}}\left(\frac{1}{1+y}\right)^{2\ell+4}.

((146))

Importantly, this sum converges as we take the limit
\ell_{\rm max}\rightarrow\infty
, which allows us to obtain a cutoff-free value for the ground state energy shift
\Delta E_{\rm GS}
. Further,
in the low-frequency regime,
\omega_{0}/\omega_{P}\ll 1
and by approximating
\sqrt{(2\ell+1)/\ell}\approx\sqrt{2}
, this sum can be evaluated analytically and scales as

\lim_{\ell_{\rm max}\rightarrow\infty}\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{P}(x=0,y\ll 1)\approx\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{32}y^{3}}.

((147))

Therefore, we define
\mathcal{F}_{P}(x,y)=y^{3}\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{P}(x,y)
and rewrite the energy shift as

\Delta E_{P}\simeq\alpha\hbar\omega_{0}\left(\frac{qa_{0}}{ed}\right)^{2}\frac{Z_{\rm eff}}{Z_{\rm vac}}\mathcal{F}_{P}\left(\frac{\omega_{0}}{\omega_{P}},\frac{z_{0}}{R}\right),

((148))

where we introduced the effective impedance

Z_{\rm eff}=\frac{1}{\pi\epsilon_{0}z_{0}\omega_{P}}.

((149))

This result shows that in the full multi-mode calculation, the radius
R
of the sphere is replaced by the distance
z_{0}
as the relevant length scale.

IV.6
Sign of the total energy shift

By combining the full results for
\Delta E_{\rm im}
and for
\Delta E_{P}
in
Eq. (148)
, we can write the ratio between dynamical and static corrections as

\frac{\Delta E_{P}}{|\Delta E_{\rm im}|}=\frac{\omega_{0}}{\pi\omega_{P}}\frac{\mathcal{F}_{P}(\omega_{0}/\omega_{P},z_{0}/R)}{\mathcal{F}_{\rm im}^{P}(z_{0}/R)}<1,

((150))

where the upper bound is reached for
\omega_{0}/\omega_{P}\gg 1
. This shows that the total energy shift is negative for all parameter regimes.
