1.
introduction

The Riemann hypothesis, formulated by Bernhard Riemann in his seminal paper
[
26
]
in 1859, stands as one of the most renowned yet unsolved problems in the realm of mathematics. Until now, no counterexample has been discovered, and the hypothesis receives substantial numerical support. Nevertheless, despite the persistent endeavours of mathematicians over time, the Riemann hypothesis continues to elude proof. Over the course of time, mathematicians have put forth numerous equivalent criterion for the Riemann hypothesis in their attempts to establish its proof. A noteworthy equivalent criterion, attributed to Riesz
[
25
]
in 1916 has shown that the Riemann hypothesis is equivalent to the following bound for an infinite series associated with
\mu(n)
,

P_{2}(x):=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\mu(n)}{n^{2}}\exp\left(-\frac{x}{n^{2}}\right)=O_{\epsilon}\left(x^{-\frac{3}{4}+\epsilon}\right),\quad{\rm as}\,\,x\rightarrow\infty,

(1.1)

for any positive
\epsilon
. Driven by this motivation, Hardy and Littlewood
[
16
, p. 161]
established another equivalent criterion for the Riemann hypothesis while rectifying an identity of Ramanujan. Mainly, they showed that

\displaystyle P_{1}(x):=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\mu(n)}{n}\exp\left({-\frac{x}{n^{2}}}\right)=O_{\epsilon}\left(x^{-\frac{1}{4}+\epsilon}\right),\quad\mathrm{as}\,\,x\rightarrow\infty.

(1.2)

Their intuition stemmed from the following identity found in Ramanujan’s notebooks
[
24
, p. 312]
,
[
5
, Equation (37.3), p. 470]
, where he mentioned that for any
x>0
,

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\mu(n)}{n}\exp\left({-\frac{x}{n^{2}}}\right)=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{x}}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\mu(n)}{n}\exp\left(-\frac{\pi^{2}}{n^{2}x}\right).

(1.3)

Berndt
[
5
, p. 468-469]
presented a compelling numerical explanation that sheds light on the discrepancy of the identity (
1.3
).
Hardy and Littlewood
[
16
, p. 156, Section 2.5]
established a corrected version of (
1.3
). They proved that, for
x>0
,

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\mu(n)}{n}\exp\left({-\frac{x}{n^{2}}}\right)=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{x}}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\mu(n)}{n}\exp\left(-\frac{\pi^{2}}{n^{2}x}\right)-\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi}}\sum_{\rho}\left(\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{x}}\right)^{\rho}\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{1-\rho}{2}\right)}{\zeta^{\prime}(\rho)}.

(1.4)

A symmetric form of the above identity is the following. For two positive real numbers
\alpha
,
\beta
with
\alpha\beta=\pi
, we have

\displaystyle\sqrt{\alpha}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\mu(n)}{n}\exp\left({-\left(\frac{\alpha}{n}\right)^{2}}\right)-\sqrt{\beta}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\mu(n)}{n}\exp\left({-\left(\frac{\beta}{n}\right)^{2}}\right)=-\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\beta}}\sum_{\rho}\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{1-\rho}{2}\right)\beta^{\rho}}{\zeta^{\prime}(\rho)},

(1.5)

where the right-side sum over
\rho
runs through the non-trivial zeros of the Riemann zeta function
\zeta(s)
. The identity (
1.4
) holds under the assumption that, all the non-trivial zeros of
\zeta(s)
are simple. The convergence of the sum over
\rho
on the right-hand side of (
1.4
) is not immediately evident. While there is a belief that the series converges rapidly, to date, its convergence is only established under the assumption of bracketing the terms. That is, if for some positive constant
A_{0}
, the terms corresponding to the non-trivial zeros
\rho_{1}
and
\rho_{2}
satisfy

\displaystyle|\operatorname{Im}(\rho_{1})-\operatorname{Im}(\rho_{2})|<\exp\left(-\frac{A_{0}\operatorname{Im}(\rho_{1})}{\log(\operatorname{Im}(\rho_{1}))}\right)+\exp\left(-\frac{A_{0}\operatorname{Im}(\rho_{2})}{\log(\operatorname{Im}(\rho_{2}))}\right),

(1.6)

then they will be considered inside the same bracket. A more detailed explanation of the convergence intricacies concerning series containing
\zeta^{\prime}(\rho)
in the denominator has been provided in
[
19
, p. 783]
.

The identity (
1.4
) has captured the attention of numerous mathematicians over the years. For further insights into this identity, interested readers are encouraged to refer to Berndt
[
5
, p. 470]
, Paris and Kaminski
[
23
, p. 143]
, and Titchmarch
[
27
, p. 219]
. Remarkably, Bhaskaran
[
6
]
discovered a connection between Fourier reciprocity and Wiener’s Tauberian theory. Ramanujan himself indicated a fascinating generalization of (
1.4
) related to a pair of reciprocal functions, which was later explored by Hardy and Littlewood
[
16
, p. 160, Section 2.5]
.

In 2012, Dixit
[
9
]
established an elegant character analogue of (
1.4
). Subsequently, Dixit, Roy, and Zaharescu
[
12
]
investigated a one-variable generalization of (
1.4
), deriving equivalent criteria for both the Riemann hypothesis and the generalized Riemann hypothesis. They
[
11
]
also found an identity analogous to (
1.4
) for
L
-functions associated with Hecke eigenforms. In
2022
, Dixit, Gupta, and Vatwani
[
10
]
obtained a generalization for the Dedekind zeta function and established an equivalent criterion for the extended Riemann hypothesis. In 2023, Banerjee and Kumar
[
4
]
derived an analogous identity for
L
-functions associated to Maass cusp forms and discovered Riesz-Hardy-Littlewood type equivalent criterion for the corresponding
L
-function. Very recently, Gupta and Vatwani
[
15
]
further examined this identity for
L
-functions in the Selberg class and obtained Riesz type equivalent criteria. Interested people can also explore the work of Báez-Duarte
[
3
]
, where a sequential Riesz-type criterion for the Riemann hypothesis was presented. This phenomenon was further studied by Cislo and Wolf
[
8
]
.

In a recent development, Agarwal and the authors
[
1
]
gave an interesting one-variable generalization of the identity of Hardy-Littlewood (
1.4
). The following identity has been established.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
k\geq 1
be a real number. Under the hypothesis that the non-trivial zeros of
\zeta(s)
are simple, for
x>0
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\mu(n)}{n^{k}}\exp\left({-\frac{x}{n^{2}}}\right)=\frac{\Gamma(\frac{k}{2})}{x^{\frac{k}{2}}}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\mu(n)}{n}{}_{1}F_{1}\left(\frac{k}{2};\frac{1}{2};-\frac{\pi^{2}}{n^{2}x}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\rho}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{k-\rho}{2})}{\zeta^{\prime}(\rho)}x^{-\frac{(k-\rho)}{2}},

(1.7)

where the sum over
\rho
runs through all the non-trivial zeros of
\zeta(s)
and satisfies the bracketing condition (
1.6
) and the entire function
{}_{1}F_{1}(a;b;z)
is defined by

\displaystyle{}_{1}F_{1}(a;b;z):=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(a)_{n}}{(b)_{n}}\frac{z^{n}}{n!},\quad{where}~~(a)_{n}:=\frac{\Gamma(a+n)}{\Gamma(a)}.

Building upon Dixit’s
[
9
]
work, the authors
[
14
]
provided a character analogue of (
1.7
) and established the identity as follows:

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
k\geq 1
and
\chi
be a primitive character modulo
q
. Assume that all the non-trivial zeros of
L(s,\chi)
are simple. For
x>0
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\chi(n)\mu(n)}{n^{k}}\exp\left({-\frac{\pi x^{2}}{qn^{2}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{i^{a}\sqrt{q}}{G(\chi)}\left(\frac{q}{\pi x^{2}}\right)^{\frac{k+a}{2}}\left(\frac{\pi}{q}\right)^{a+\frac{1}{2}}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{k+a}{2})}{\Gamma(a+\frac{1}{2})}

\displaystyle\times\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\overline{\chi(n)}\mu(n)}{n^{1+a}}{}_{1}F_{1}\left(\frac{k+a}{2};a+\frac{1}{2};-\frac{\pi}{qn^{2}x^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\rho}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{k-\rho}{2})}{L^{\prime}(\rho,\chi)}\left(\frac{q}{\pi x^{2}}\right)^{\frac{k-\rho}{2}},

(1.8)

where the sum over
\rho
runs through the non-trivial zeros of
L(s,\chi)
and satisfy the bracketing conditions (
1.6
).

Inspired by the works of Riesz, Hardy and Littlewood, the authors
[
14
]
have established the following equivalent criteria for the generalized Riemann hypothesis for
L(s,\chi)
. For
k\geq 1
and
\ell>0
, we have

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\chi(n)\mu(n)}{n^{k}}\exp\left(-\frac{x}{n^{\ell}}\right)=O_{\epsilon,k,\ell}\left(x^{-\frac{k}{\ell}+\frac{1}{2\ell}+\epsilon}\right),\quad\mathrm{as}\,\,x\rightarrow\infty,

(1.9)

for any
\epsilon>0
. This bound extends the previously established bounds by Riesz (
1.1
), Hardy-Littlewood (
1.2
), as well as the earlier bound given by Agarwal and the authors
[
1
, Theorem 1.2]
. This bound, in turn, has motivated us to propose a broader conjecture
[
14
, Conjecture 5.1]
, which we restate below for the clarity of the readers. Let us consider

\displaystyle L(f,s)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{A_{f}(n)}{n^{s}},~{\rm{for}}~\operatorname{Re}(s)>1,

(1.10)

be a “nice”
L
-function. Mainly, we are assuming that
L(f,s)
obeys the grand Riemann hypothesis
[
18
, p. 113]
, which says that all the non-trivial zeros of
L(f,s)
in the critical strip
0<\operatorname{Re}(s)<1
lie on the critical line
\operatorname{Re}(s)=\frac{1}{2}
. Let us suppose that

\displaystyle 1/L(f,s)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\mu_{f}(n)}{n^{s}},~\text{for}~\operatorname{Re}(s)>1.

(1.11)

Moreover, we assume that
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mu_{f}(n)n^{-1}
is convergent. Littlewood’s prediction says that the grand Riemann hypothesis
[
18
, Proposition 5.14]
is equivalent to the following bound:

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leq x}\mu_{f}(n)\ll_{\epsilon,f}x^{\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon},

(1.12)

for any
\epsilon>0
.
The above bound played a significant role to prove (
1.9
).
Inspired from this observation, the authors gave the following conjecture.

Conjecture 1.3
.

Let
k\geq 1
and
\ell>0
be two real numbers. Let
L(f,s)
be a “nice”
L
-function satisfying
(
1.12
)
. Then the grand Riemann hypothesis for
L(f,s)
is equivalent to the bound

\displaystyle P_{k,f,\ell}:=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\mu_{f}(n)}{n^{k}}\exp\left(-\frac{x}{n^{\ell}}\right)=O_{\epsilon,k,\ell}\left(x^{-\frac{k}{\ell}+\frac{1}{2\ell}+\epsilon}\right),\quad\mathrm{as}\,\,x\rightarrow\infty.

(1.13)

In this manuscript, one of our main goals is to prove the above conjecture. Moreover, we also establish a generalization of the Ramanujan-Hardy-Littlewood identity (
1.4
) within the framework of Chandrasekharan-Narasimhan class of
L
-functions
[
7
]
, defined in the next section. Furthermore, we obtain an entirely novel form of equivalent criteria for the Riemann hypothesis of
\zeta(s)
, see Theorem
3.13
.

2.
Chandrasekharan-Narasimhan class of
L
-functions

We now introduce the class of
L
-functions established by Chandrasekharan and Narasimhan
[
7
]
.
Consider two arithmetical functions, denoted as
a_{1}(n)
and
b_{1}(n)
, which are not identically zero. Additionally, let
{\lambda_{n}}
and
{\mu_{n}}
be two monotonically increasing sequences of positive real numbers tending to infinity. Then the associated Dirichlet series are defined as follows:

\displaystyle\phi(s)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{a_{1}(n)}{\lambda_{n}^{s}}\quad{\rm{for}}\quad\operatorname{Re}(s)=\sigma>\sigma_{a},

(2.1)

\displaystyle\psi(s)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{b_{1}(n)}{\mu_{n}^{s}}\quad{\rm{for}}\quad\operatorname{Re}(s)=\sigma>\sigma_{b}.

(2.2)

Here,
\sigma_{a}
and
\sigma_{b}
indicate the finite abscissae of absolute convergence for
\phi(s)
and
\psi(s)
, respectively. It is assumed that both
\phi(s)
and
\psi(s)
can be analytically continued to the entire
\mathbb{C}
except for a finite number of poles. For a given
\delta>0
, we assume that
\phi(s)
and
\psi(s)
are connected by the subsequent functional equation:

\displaystyle(2\pi)^{-s}\Gamma(s)\phi(s)=(2\pi)^{-(\delta-s)}\Gamma(\delta-s)\psi(\delta-s).

(2.3)

In this paper, we adopt their framework in a slightly general form stated below. Essentially, we consider a definition that involves a certain twist. We define

\displaystyle a(n)=\begin{cases}a_{1}(k),&\textit{if}\quad n=\lambda_{k},\\
0,&otherwise,\end{cases}\quad\text{and}\quad b(n)=\begin{cases}b_{1}(k),&\textit{if}\quad n=\mu_{k},\\
0,&otherwise.\end{cases}

Thus, the Dirichlet series in (
2.1
) and (
2.2
) can be rewritten as follows:

\displaystyle\phi(s)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{a(n)}{n^{s}},\quad\text{for}\quad\operatorname{Re}(s)=\sigma>\sigma_{a},

\displaystyle\psi(s)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{b(n)}{n^{s}},\quad\text{for}\quad\operatorname{Re}(s)=\sigma>\sigma_{b}.

Now, for a specified
\delta>0
, we assume that
\phi(s)
and
\psi(s)
satisfy the following general functional equation:

\displaystyle c^{s}\Gamma(As+B)\phi(s)=\nu c^{\delta-s}\Gamma(A(\delta-s)+B)\psi(\delta-s),

(2.4)

where
|\nu|=1
,
A>0
,
B\geq 0
and
c\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
.
One can easily see that (
2.3
) is a particular case of (
2.4
) by letting
c=\frac{1}{2\pi},A=1,B=0
and
\nu=1.

We can observe that
\zeta(s)
and
L(s,\chi)
trivially fall under the category of the above class of
L
-functions.
A few more examples that belong to the Chandrasekharan-Narasimhan class of
L
-functions are given below.

Example 1
.

Let
r\in\mathbb{Z}
and
\sigma_{r}(n)
denote the sum of
r^{th}
powers of positive divisors of
n
. Then the
L
-function associated to
\sigma_{r}(n)
is given by

\displaystyle\ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\sigma_{r}(n)}{n^{s}}=\zeta(s)\zeta(s-r),\quad{\rm{for}}\quad\operatorname{Re}(s)=\sigma>\max\{1,r+1\},

and if
r
is an odd positive integer, it satisfies the following functional equation:

\displaystyle(2\pi)^{-s}\Gamma(s)\zeta(s)\zeta(s-r)=(-1)^{\frac{r+1}{2}}(2\pi)^{-(r+1-s)}\Gamma(r+1-s)\zeta(r+1-s)\zeta(1-s).

(2.5)

This corresponds to (
2.4
) for
a(n)=b(n)=\sigma_{r}(n),c=\frac{1}{2\pi},A=1,B=0,\delta=r+1
and
\nu=(-1)^{\frac{r+1}{2}}.
One can refer
[
7
, Example 2]
for further insight into the functional equation (
2.5
).

An intriguing observation about Example
1
is that, despite the function
\zeta(s)\zeta(s-r)
having non-trivial zeros on the lines
\operatorname{Re}(s)=\frac{1}{2}
and
\operatorname{Re}(s)=r+\frac{1}{2}
, and thus not adhering its own Riemann hypothesis but it offers surprising insights into the Riemann hypothesis for
\zeta(s)
by presenting an entirely novel form of equivalent criteria, as demonstrated in Theorem
3.13
. The next example corresponds to the cusp form of weight
12
.

Example 2
.

The Ramanujan tau function
\tau(n)
is defined by

\displaystyle\Delta(q)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\tau(n)q^{n}=q\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}(1-q^{n})^{24},\quad|q|<1.

Note that
\Delta(q)
is a cusp form of level
1
and weight
12
. The
L
-function associated to normalized coefficients
\tau_{0}(n)=\tau(n)n^{-\frac{11}{2}}
is given by

\displaystyle L(\Delta,s)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\tau_{0}(n)}{n^{s}}\quad{\rm{for}}\quad\operatorname{Re}(s)=\sigma>1,

and can be analytically continued to the whole complex plane as an entire function. Moreover,
L(\Delta,s)
satisfies the following nice-looking functional equation:

\displaystyle(2\pi)^{-s}\Gamma\left(s+\frac{11}{2}\right)L(\Delta,s)=(2\pi)^{-(1-s)}\Gamma\left(\frac{13}{2}-s\right)L(\Delta,1-s),

which aligns with (
2.4
) for
a(n)=b(n)=\tau_{0}(n),c=\frac{1}{2\pi},A=1,B=\frac{11}{2}
,
\delta=1
and
\nu=1
. It has been conjectured that all the non-trivial zeros of
L(\Delta,s)
lie on the critical line
\operatorname{Re}(s)=\frac{1}{2}
.

Example 3
.

Let

be a holomorphic Hecke eigenform of weight

\displaystyle f(z)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{f}(n)n^{\frac{\omega-1}{2}}e^{2\pi inz},

\displaystyle L(f,s)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\lambda_{f}(n)}{n^{s}}=\prod_{p:~\rm{prime}}\left(1-\frac{\lambda_{f}(p)}{p^{s}}+\frac{1}{p^{2s}}\right)^{-1}\quad{\rm{for}}\quad\operatorname{Re}(s)>1.

Hecke proved that

\displaystyle\Lambda(f,s)=\Lambda(f,1-s),

Example 4
.

Let
r_{j}(n)
counts the number of ways a positive integer
n
can be written as sum of
j

\displaystyle\zeta_{id}(j,s):=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{r_{j}(n)}{n^{s}}\quad{\rm{for}}\quad\operatorname{Re}(s)>\frac{j}{2},

is the Epstein zeta function and satisfies the functional equation

\displaystyle\pi^{-s}\Gamma(s)\zeta_{id}(j,s)=\pi^{s-\frac{j}{2}}\Gamma\left(\frac{j}{2}-s\right)\zeta_{id}\left(j,\frac{j}{2}-s\right),

. It can be analytically continued to the whole complex plane except for a simple pole at

.
This corresponds to (
2.4
) for
a(n)=b(n)=r_{j}(n),c=\frac{1}{\pi},A=1,B=0,\delta=\frac{j}{2}
and
\nu=1.

When

\zeta_{id}(2,s)
lies on the critical line
\operatorname{Re}(s)=\frac{1}{2}.

Exploring whether a given subset

Typically, Epstein zeta functions are investigated for positive definite

\displaystyle\zeta_{M}(j,s)=\sum_{\kappa\in\mathbb{Z}^{j}/\{0\}}(\kappa^{T}M\kappa)^{-s}\quad{\rm{for}}\quad\operatorname{Re}(s)>\frac{j}{2}.

Example
4
deals with

, the Epstein zeta function can be expressed in terms of Riemann zeta function and Dirichlet

\displaystyle\zeta_{id}(2,s)=4\zeta(s)\beta(s),

(2.8)

,
which is noting but
L(s,\chi_{4,2})

3.
Main Results

In this section, we state the main results of this paper.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
a(n)
and
b(n)
be two arithmetical functions whose corresponding
L
-functions are
\phi(s)

Lemma 4.4
.

Let

-function and its inverse defined in (

We start with the definition of

\displaystyle P_{k,f,\ell}(x)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\mu_{f}(n)}{n^{k}}\exp\left(-\frac{x}{n^{\ell}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\mu_{f}(n)}{n^{k}}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{m}x^{m}}{n^{\ell m}m!}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{m}x^{m}}{m!}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\mu_{f}(n)}{n^{k+\ell m}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{m}x^{m}}{m!L(f,\ell m+k)}

\displaystyle L(f,k+\ell s)\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{-s-1}P_{k,f,\ell}(x)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{A_{f}(n)}{n^{k}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{x^{-s-1}}{n^{\ell s}}P_{k,f,\ell}(x)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{A_{f}(n)}{n^{k}}\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{-s-1}P_{k,f,\ell}\left(\frac{x}{n^{\ell}}\right)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{-s-1}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{A_{f}(n)}{n^{k}}P_{k,f,\ell}\left(\frac{x}{n^{\ell}}\right)\mathrm{d}x.

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{A_{f}(n)}{n^{k}}P_{k,f,\ell}\left(\frac{x}{n^{\ell}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{A_{f}(n)}{n^{k}}\left(\frac{1}{L(f,k)}+\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{m}x^{m}}{m!L(f,k+\ell m)n^{\ell m}}\right)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}e^{-x}-1,&\text{if}~k=1~\text{and}~L(f,s)~\text{has a pole at}~s=1,\\
e^{-x},&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(4.3)

\displaystyle=\frac{-1}{s}\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{(1-s)-1}e^{-x}\mathrm{d}x\

\operatorname{Re}(s)=\frac{1}{2}+r+\epsilon

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\bigg[\int_{\frac{1}{2}+r+\epsilon+iT}^{1+r+\epsilon+iT}+\int_{\frac{1}{2}+r+\epsilon-iT}^{\frac{1}{2}+r+\epsilon+iT}+\int_{1+r+\epsilon-iT}^{\frac{1}{2}+r+\epsilon-iT}\bigg]\frac{1}{\zeta(w)\zeta(w-r)}\frac{x^{w}}{w}\mathrm{d}w

\displaystyle+O\left(\frac{x^{1+r+\epsilon}}{T}\right)+O(x^{r+\epsilon}).

(4.4)

Under the assumption of RH, we know
\frac{1}{\zeta(\sigma+it)}=O(|t|^{\epsilon})

. Using this bound and under simplification, one can check that

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\frac{1}{2}+r+\epsilon+iT}^{1+r+\epsilon+iT}\frac{1}{\zeta(w)\zeta(w-r)}\frac{x^{w}}{w}\mathrm{d}w\right|

\displaystyle\ll\int_{\frac{1}{2}+r+\epsilon}^{1+r+\epsilon}x^{\sigma}T^{2\epsilon-1}d\sigma\ll T^{2\epsilon-1}x^{2+r+\epsilon},

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\frac{1}{2}+r+\epsilon-iT}^{\frac{1}{2}+r+\epsilon+iT}\frac{1}{\zeta(w)\zeta(w-r)}\frac{x^{w}}{w}\mathrm{d}w\right|

\displaystyle\ll\int_{-T}^{T}|t|^{2\epsilon}x^{\frac{1}{2}+r+\epsilon}\frac{dt}{|t|}\ll T^{2\epsilon}x^{\frac{1}{2}+r+\epsilon},

\displaystyle\left|\int_{1+r+\epsilon-iT}^{\frac{1}{2}+r+\epsilon-iT}\frac{1}{\zeta(w)\zeta(w-r)}\frac{x^{w}}{w}\mathrm{d}w\right|

\displaystyle\ll\int_{1+r+\epsilon}^{\frac{1}{2}+r+\epsilon}x^{\sigma}T^{2\epsilon-1}d\sigma\ll T^{2\epsilon-1}x^{2+r+\epsilon}.

Utilizing these bounds in (

\displaystyle=O\left(T^{2\epsilon-1}x^{2+r+\epsilon}\right)+O\left(T^{2\epsilon}x^{\frac{1}{2}+r+\epsilon}\right)+O\left(\frac{x^{1+r+\epsilon}}{T}\right)+O\big(x^{r+\epsilon}\big)

\displaystyle=O\left(T^{2\epsilon-1}x^{2+r+\epsilon}\right)+O\left(T^{2\epsilon}x^{\frac{1}{2}+r+\epsilon}\right).

Taking

, the result follows.

Now we define an important special function, namely, the Meijer

-function

Let

\displaystyle\quad=\sum_{k=1}^{m}A_{p,q,k}^{m,n}(z){}_{p}F_{q-1}\left(\begin{matrix}1+b_{k}-a_{1},\cdots,1+b_{k}-a_{p}\\

L(f,s)
be a nice
L
-function defined as in (
1.10
).
First, we begin by presupposing that the grand Riemann hypothesis (GRH) for
L(f,s)
holds true. Then from (
1.12
), for any
\epsilon>0
, we have

\displaystyle G(x):=\sum_{1\leq n\leq x}\mu_{f}(n)\ll_{\epsilon,f}x^{\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon}.

(5.20)

Now, we employ Euler’s partial summation formula, i.e., Lemma
4.2
with
a(n)=\mu_{f}(n)
and
f(t)=t^{-k}
to see that

\displaystyle H(m,n):=\sum_{i=m}^{n}\frac{\mu_{f}(i)}{i^{k}}

\displaystyle=G(n)f(n)-G(m-1)f(m-1)-\int_{m-1}^{n}G(t)f^{\prime}(t){\rm d}t.

(5.21)

Utilizing (
5.20
) in (
5.21
), one can obtain

\displaystyle H(m,n)=O_{\epsilon,k}\left(m^{\frac{1}{2}-k+\epsilon}\right).

(5.22)

The above bound is uniform in
n
. Our main objective is to determine the bound for the following infinite series, under the assumption of the GRH for
L(f,s)
,

\displaystyle P_{k,f,\ell}(x):=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\mu_{f}(n)}{n^{k}}\exp\left(-\frac{x}{n^{\ell}}\right).

For simplicity, we replace
x
by
x^{\ell}
and separate the sum into finite and infinite part as

\displaystyle P_{k,f,\ell}(x^{\ell})

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{m-1}\frac{\mu_{f}(n)}{n^{k}}\exp\left(-\frac{x^{\ell}}{n^{\ell}}\right)+\sum_{n=m}^{\infty}\frac{\mu_{f}(n)}{n^{k}}\exp\left(-\frac{x^{\ell}}{n^{\ell}}\right)

\displaystyle:=Q_{1}(x^{\ell})+Q_{2}(x^{\ell}).

(5.23)

where
m=[x^{1-\epsilon}]+1
. To get an estimate for
Q_{1}(x^{\ell})
, we write

\displaystyle|Q_{1}(x^{\ell})|=\bigg|\sum_{n=1}^{m-1}\frac{\mu_{f}(n)}{n^{k}}\exp\left(-\frac{x^{\ell}}{n^{\ell}}\right)\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=1}^{m-1}|H(n,n)|\exp\left(-\frac{x^{\ell}}{m^{\ell}}\right)

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{n=1}^{m-1}n^{\frac{1}{2}-k+\epsilon}\exp\left(-\frac{x^{\ell}}{m^{\ell}}\right)

\displaystyle\ll_{k,\epsilon}m^{\frac{3}{2}-k+\epsilon}\exp\left(-\frac{x^{\ell}}{m^{\ell}}\right).

This gives

\displaystyle Q_{1}(x^{\ell})=O\left(\exp(-x^{\ell\epsilon})x^{(1-\epsilon)(\frac{3}{2}-k+\epsilon)}\right),

(5.24)

as
m-1=[x^{1-\epsilon}]
.
We shall now try to estimate
Q_{2}(x^{\ell})
.
For sufficiently large integer
N>m
, we write

\displaystyle\sum_{n=m}^{N}\frac{\mu_{f}(n)}{n^{k}}\exp\left(-\frac{x^{\ell}}{n^{\ell}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=m}^{N-1}H(m,n)\left(\exp\left(-\frac{x^{\ell}}{n^{\ell}}\right)-\exp\left(-\frac{x^{\ell}}{(n+1)^{\ell}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle+H(m,n)\exp\left(-\frac{x^{\ell}}{N^{\ell}}\right).

Allowing
N\rightarrow\infty
and invoking the bound (
5.22
) for
H(m,n)
, it can be inferred that

\displaystyle Q_{2}(x^{\ell})=Q_{3}(x^{\ell})+O_{\epsilon,k}\left(m^{\frac{1}{2}-k+\epsilon}\right),

(5.25)

where

\displaystyle Q_{3}(x^{\ell})=\sum_{n=m}^{N-1}H(m,n)\left(\exp\left(-\frac{x^{\ell}}{n^{\ell}}\right)-\exp\left(-\frac{x^{\ell}}{(n+1)^{\ell}}\right)\right).

Now to simplify further, we use Cauchy’s mean value theorem with the function
T(z)=\exp\left(-\frac{x^{\ell}}{z^{\ell}}\right).
One can find
z_{n}\in(n,n+1)
such that

T(n)-T(n+1)=-T^{{}^{\prime}}(z_{n})=-\frac{\ell x^{\ell}}{z_{n}^{\ell+1}}\exp\left(-\frac{x^{\ell}}{z_{n}^{\ell}}\right).

Employing the above fact in conjunction with (
5.22
), we obtain

\displaystyle|Q_{3}(x^{\ell})|

\displaystyle\ll_{\epsilon,\ell}m^{\frac{1}{2}-k+\epsilon}\sum_{n=m}^{\infty}\frac{x^{\ell}}{z_{n}^{\ell+1}}\exp\left(-\frac{x^{\ell}}{z_{n}^{\ell}}\right)

\displaystyle\ll_{\epsilon,\ell}m^{\frac{1}{2}-k+\epsilon}\sum_{n=m}^{\infty}\frac{x^{\ell}}{n^{\ell+1}}

\displaystyle\ll_{\epsilon,\ell}x^{\frac{1}{2}-k+\epsilon}\frac{x^{\ell}}{m^{\ell}}\ll_{\epsilon,\ell}x^{\frac{1}{2}-k+\epsilon^{\prime}}

(5.26)

as
m\sim x^{1-\epsilon}
. Now utilizing (
5.26
) in (
5.25
), we see that

\displaystyle|Q_{2}(x^{\ell})|=O_{\epsilon,k,\ell}\left(m^{\frac{1}{2}-k+\epsilon}\right).

(5.27)

Finally, substituting the bounds (
5.24
) and (
5.27
) in (
5.23
), one can see that due to exponential decay, the bound for
Q_{1}(x^{\ell})
goes to zero much faster than the bound for
Q_{2}(x^{\ell})
as
x\rightarrow\infty
, and therefore

\displaystyle P_{k,f,\ell}(x^{\ell})=O_{\epsilon,k,\ell}\left(x^{\frac{1}{2}-k+\epsilon}\right).

Replace
x
by
x^{1/\ell}
, we get the desired bound (
1.13
).

Now, turning to the converse aspect, that is, assuming the validity of the bound (
1.13
), we aim to show that the GRH for
L(f,s)
follows.
Utilizing Lemma
4.4
, we deduced that

\displaystyle L(f,\ell s+k)\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{-s-1}P_{k,f,\ell}(x)\mathrm{d}x=\Gamma(-s),

(5.28)

valid for
\operatorname{Re}(s)>\frac{1-k}{\ell}
except for non-negative integers. We aim to extend the validity of (
5.28
) towards the left half plane. To accomplish this, we choose a sufficiently large positive real number
R
and rewrite the expression (
5.28
) as

\displaystyle L(f,\ell s+k)\left(\int_{0}^{R}+\int_{R}^{\infty}\right)x^{-s-1}P_{k,f,\ell}(x)\mathrm{d}x=\Gamma(-s).

Utilizing the bound (
1.13
) for
P_{k,f,\ell}(x)
, it is evident that unbounded part possesses analyticity for
\operatorname{Re}(s)>\frac{1}{2\ell}-\frac{k}{\ell}
.
Consequently, identity (
5.28
) is analytic in the strip
\frac{1}{2\ell}-\frac{k}{\ell}<\operatorname{Re}(s)<\frac{1-k}{\ell}
.
Considering the fact that
\Gamma(s)
never attains zero, it implies that
L(f,\ell s+k)
is non-vanishing in the strip
\frac{1}{2\ell}-\frac{k}{\ell}<\operatorname{Re}(s)<\frac{1-k}{\ell}
, which in turn implies that
L(f,s)
does not possess any zero in the strip
\frac{1}{2}<\operatorname{Re}(s)<1
.
From the symmetry of the functional equation, one can conclude that
L(f,s)
is non-vanishing in
0<\operatorname{Re}(s)<\frac{1}{2}
. This proves that all the non-trivial zeros of
L(f,s)
lies on the critical line
\operatorname{Re}(s)=\frac{1}{2}
.
Thus, the GRH follows. This completes the proof of Conjecture
1.3
.
∎

For the direct part, we assume RH for
\zeta(s)
to be true and we wish to obtain the bound (
3.5
) for
P_{k,\sigma,\ell}(x)
. The proof goes along the similar line of reasoning as in the proof of Theorem
3.10
except for that fact that we start with

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leq x}\sigma_{r}^{-1}(n)=O(x^{\frac{1}{2}+r+\epsilon}),

as established in Lemma
4.7
under the assumption of RH. Thus, we omit the proof here for the direct part.

For the converse part, assuming the validity of the bound (
3.5
) for
P_{k,\sigma,\ell}(x)
, that is,

\displaystyle P_{k,\sigma,\ell}(x):=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\sigma_{r}^{-1}(n)}{n^{k}}\exp\left(-\frac{x}{n^{\ell}}\right)=O_{\epsilon,k,r,\ell}\left(x^{-\frac{k}{\ell}+\frac{1+2r}{2\ell}+\epsilon}\right),

for any
\epsilon>0
,
we will try to show that all the non-trivial zeros of
\zeta(s)
are located on the critical line
\operatorname{Re}(s)=\frac{1}{2}
. Here, we utilize Lemma
4.5
to deduce that

\displaystyle\zeta(k+\ell s)\zeta(k+\ell s-r)\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{-s-1}P_{k,\sigma,\ell}(x)\mathrm{d}x=\Gamma(-s),

(5.29)

holds within the region
\operatorname{Re}(s)>\frac{1-k+r}{\ell}
except for
s=0,1,2\cdots
. Continuing along the path drawn in the proof of Theorem
3.10
, one can extend the region of analyticity of (
5.29
) in
\frac{1}{2\ell}-\frac{k}{\ell}+\frac{r}{\ell}<\operatorname{Re}(s)<\frac{1-k+r}{\ell}.
Considering the fact that
\Gamma(s)
never attains zero, it implies that
\zeta(k+\ell s)
and
\zeta(k+\ell s-r)
is non-vanishing in the strip
\frac{1}{2\ell}-\frac{k}{\ell}+\frac{r}{\ell}<\operatorname{Re}(s)<\frac{1-k+r}{\ell}.
Since

\operatorname{Re}(k+\ell s)>1

and thus the functional equation for

implies that it is non-vanishing in the strip
