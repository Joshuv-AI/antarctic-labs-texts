Sticky diffusions on graphs
Adam Gregosiewicz

Abstract
. We consider diffusion processes on metric graphs with semipermeable sticky membranes in each vertex. We prove that the process is governed by a Feller semigroup and find its asymptotic behavior as diffusion’s speed increases to infinity with the same rate as permeability coefficients decreases to zero.



1.
Introduction

Since around 1980 numerous papers have been published on the topic of evolution
operators acting on metric graphs – see for example
[
6
,
14
]
,
or recent survey
[
12
]
and references given there.
In this context operators related to the diffusion process are one of the most
extensively examined.
More specifically, let
\mathcal{G}
be a finite graph without loops, and assume
that there is a Markov process on
\mathcal{G}
, which on each edge behaves like a
Brownian motion with given variance.
Moreover, suppose that each vertex of the graph is a semipermeable membrane with
given permeability coefficients, that is for each vertex there are nonnegative
numbers, describing the possibility of a particle passing through the membrane
from an edge
\mathsf{e}
to
\mathsf{f}
.
In 2012, A. Bobrowski and K. Morawska
[
5
]
considered such
diffusion processes on a simple graph to model synaptic depression dynamic.
The results were generalized by Bobrowski in
[
3
]
to the case
of arbitrary graphs.
He proved that these processes are governed by strongly continuous semigroups of
operators (see, for example,
[
7
]
for an introduction to the theory
of semigroup of linear operators), and, moreover, that if the diffusion’s speed
increases to infinity with the same rate as permeability coefficients decreases
to zero (this is an example of a
small parameter
, or
singular
perturbation
, problem), then there is a limit process, in the sense of
Theorem 3.26 in
[
13
]
, which behaves like a Markov chain on the
vertices of the line graph of
\mathcal{G}
, see Figure
1
.

Figure 1.
Diffusion on a graph
\mathcal{G}
becomes a Markov chain on the vertices
of the line graph of
\mathcal{G}
.

Because in Bobrowski’s papers the analysis takes place in the space of
continuous functions on
\mathcal{G}
, the related semigroups describe dynamics of
(weighted) conditional expected values of these processes.
It is also worth noting that the communication between edges is based on the
Fick law, or, in other words, that boundary conditions at vertices are
Robin-type.

In 2014, in order to obtain the dynamics of densities of Bobrowski’s processes
distributions, we considered in
[
9
]
a “dual” description of
the processes with the underlying space being the
L^{1}
-type space of
Lebesgue integrable functions on
\mathcal{G}
.
One may wish to mimic the argument of the continuous case but this is not fully
possible (the reason is that a pointwise evaluation is not a bounded functional
in an
L^{1}
-type space), thus a different method is needed.

These results, in the space of continuous functions and in the
L^{1}
-type
space, were improved by J. Banasiak
et
al.

[
1
,
2
]
.
Moreover, if we are interested in generation theorems only, then Banasiak’s
results follow from a recent and very general scheme developed by T. Binz and
K.J. Engel (in the space of continuous functions), and M. Kramar Fijavž and
K.J. Engel (in the
L^{1}
-type space).

In this paper we generalize results of
[
3
]
to the case of

sticky boundary conditions
.
We consider the process that on each edge of the underlying graph behaves as a
Brownian motion, and in each vertex we put a semipermeable
sticky

membrane; see Example 3.59 in
[
13
]
or Appendix for detailed
description of this type of boundary condition.
We prove, see Theorem
2.1
, that the process is governed by a
Feller semigroup and, see Theorem
2.4
, find its
asymptotic behavior as diffusion’s speed increases to infinity with the same
rate as permeability coefficients decreases to zero.

2.
Sticky diffusion on a graph

Let
\mathsf{G}=(\mathsf{V},\mathsf{E})
be a finite graph without loops, where

\mathsf{V}=\{\mathsf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathsf{v}_{n}\}
is the set of vertices and and

\mathsf{E}=\{\mathsf{e}_{1},\ldots,\mathsf{e}_{m}\}
is the set of edges of
\mathsf{G}
.
We arbitrarily fix an orientation of
\mathsf{G}
and introduce the
incidence
matrix

\mathcal{I}=(\iota_{\mathsf{v},\mathsf{e}})_{\mathsf{v}\in\mathsf{V},\mathsf{e}\in\mathsf{E}}
defined by

\iota_{\mathsf{v},\mathsf{e}}=\begin{cases}-1,&\text{$\mathsf{v}$ is the initial endpoint $\mathsf{e}_{\mathrm{init}}$ of $\mathsf{e}$},\\
+1,&\text{$\mathsf{v}$ is the terminal endpoint $\mathsf{e}_{\mathrm{term}}$ of $\mathsf{e}$},\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

To define a metric analogue of the discrete object
\mathsf{G}
, we assign to each
edge
\mathsf{e}\in\mathsf{E}
a closed interval, which we normalize to
[0,1]
for
simplicity.
We let
\mathcal{G}
to be the disjoint union of these intervals, that is

\mathcal{G}\coloneqq\bigsqcup_{\mathsf{e}\in\mathsf{E}}[0,1],

and denote elements of
\mathcal{G}
by
\mathsf{e}(s)
for all
\mathsf{e}\in\mathsf{E}
and

s\in[0,1]
.
Notice that there can be many ‘copies’ of a single vertex
\mathsf{v}\in\mathsf{V}
in

\mathcal{G}
, and that
\mathcal{G}
does not itself posses a graph structure – there
is no information about connections between vertices.
From this point of view, if we want to treat
\mathcal{G}
as a metric graph, we
need to refer to adjacency matrix
\mathcal{I}
, for example.
If we endow each interval of
\mathcal{G}
with the standard metric on
[0,1]
,
then
\mathcal{G}
becomes a disconnected compact metric space.
With slight abuse of notation, we view
\mathsf{e}
as an element of
\mathsf{E}
as
well as a connected component of
\mathcal{G}
.
We parametrize each component according to the orientation of the related edge

\mathsf{e}
, and call
\mathsf{e}^{-}\coloneqq\mathsf{e}(0)
and

\mathsf{e}^{+}\coloneqq\mathsf{e}(1)
the
left
and
right endpoints
of (the
interval related to)
\mathsf{e}
.
By
C(\mathcal{G})
we denote the space of continuous function on
\mathcal{G}
with the
standard supremum norm.
This space is isometrically isomorphic to the Cartesian product

\bigtimes_{\mathsf{e}\in\mathsf{E}}C[0,1]
, where
C[0,1]
is the space of continuous
functions on
[0,1]
.
Therefore, we may identify
u\in C(\mathcal{G})
with
(u_{\mathsf{e}})_{\mathsf{e}\in\mathsf{E}}
, where

u_{\mathsf{e}}
is a member of
C[0,1]
.
Nevertheless, whenever possible, we consider
u\in C(\mathcal{G})
as a real-valued
function on a disconnected space
\mathcal{G}
, and use the edgewise identification

u=(u_{\mathsf{e}})_{\mathsf{e}\in\mathsf{E}}
when needed.
Such approach is convenient, since when we apply the positive maximum principle
for Feller semigroups, we have to work in the space of real-valued continuous
functions.
Note also that it makes sense to speak about differentiable functions on

\mathcal{G}
, and in particular, by
C^{k}(\mathcal{G})
we denote the space of

k
-times continuously differentiable functions on
\mathcal{G}
.

Fix
\epsilon\in(0,1]
, and for every
\mathsf{e}\in\mathsf{E}
let
\sigma_{\mathsf{e}}
be
a positive constant.
We define
\sigma\in C(\mathcal{G})
to be the continuous function on
\mathcal{G}
,
which on each edge
\mathsf{e}\in\mathsf{E}
equals
\sigma_{\mathsf{e}}
, and consider the
diffusion equation

\partial_{t}u(t,x)=\epsilon\sigma(x)\partial_{xx}u(t,x),\qquad t>0,\ x\in\Int\mathcal{G},

where
\Int\mathcal{G}
is the interior of
\mathcal{G}
, which is the disjoint union of
the intervals
(0,1)
, that is

\Int\mathcal{G}=\bigsqcup_{\mathsf{e}\in\mathsf{E}}(0,1).

This means that on the edge
\mathsf{e}
the related process behaves like a
Brownian motion with variance
\epsilon\sigma_{\mathsf{e}}
.

To describe the communication between the edges, we
follow
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
10
]
, and
for each
\mathsf{e}\in\mathsf{E}
we let
l_{\mathsf{e}}
and
r_{\mathsf{e}}
be nonnegative real
numbers giving the rates at which Brownian particles pass through the membrane
from the edge
\mathsf{e}
to the edges incident in the left and right endpoints,
respectively.
Also, for
\mathsf{e},\mathsf{f}\in\mathsf{E}
such that
\mathsf{e}\neq\mathsf{f}
let
l_{\mathsf{e},\mathsf{f}}
and

r_{\mathsf{e},\mathsf{f}}
be nonnegative real numbers satisfying

\sum_{\mathsf{f}\neq\mathsf{e}}l_{\mathsf{e},\mathsf{f}}\leqslant l_{\mathsf{e}}
and

\sum_{\mathsf{f}\neq\mathsf{e}}r_{\mathsf{e},\mathsf{f}}\leqslant r_{\mathsf{e}}
; the summation here is taken over all

\mathsf{f}\in\mathsf{E}
such that
\mathsf{f}\neq\mathsf{e}
.
These numbers determine the probability that
after
filtering through the
membrane of the edge
\mathsf{e}
a particle will enter the edge
\mathsf{f}
.
More specifically, the probability that a particle after filtering through the
membrane at the left endpoint
\mathsf{e}^{-}
will enter the edge
\mathsf{f}
equals

l_{\mathsf{e},\mathsf{f}}/l_{\mathsf{e}}
, and, by default, if
\mathsf{f}
is not incident with the initial
endpoint of
\mathsf{e}
in
\mathsf{G}
, we put
l_{\mathsf{e},\mathsf{f}}=0
.
Analogously,
r_{\mathsf{e},\mathsf{f}}/r_{\mathsf{e}}
is the probability that after filtering through
the membrane at
\mathsf{e}^{+}
the particle will enter the edge
\mathsf{f}
, and

r_{\mathsf{e},\mathsf{f}}=0
, provided that
\mathsf{f}
is not incident with the terminal
endpoint of
\mathsf{e}
in
\mathsf{G}
.
For
\mathsf{e}\neq\mathsf{f}
we denote by
\mathsf{f}_{\mathsf{e}}^{-}
and
\mathsf{f}_{\mathsf{e}}^{+}
the left and
right, respectively, endpoint of
\mathsf{e}
seen as an endpoint of
\mathsf{f}
.
In other words,

\mathsf{f}_{\mathsf{e}}^{-}=\begin{cases}\mathsf{f}^{-},&\mathsf{f}_{\mathrm{init}}=\mathsf{e}_{\mathrm{init}},\\
\mathsf{f}^{+},&\mathsf{f}_{\mathrm{term}}=\mathsf{e}_{\mathrm{init}},\\
\text{undefined},&\text{$\mathsf{f}$ is not incident to $\mathsf{e}_{\mathrm{init}}$},\end{cases}

and similarly for
\mathsf{f}_{\mathsf{e}}^{+}
.

With these notations we impose for each
\mathsf{e}\in\mathsf{E}
the following sticky
transmission conditions (see Appendix for a detailed description) between the
edges:

p_{\mathsf{e}}\partial_{xx}u(t,\mathsf{e}^{-})-p_{\mathsf{e}}^{*}\partial_{x}u(t,\mathsf{e}^{-})=\sum_{\mathsf{f}\in\mathsf{E}}\epsilon l_{\mathsf{e},\mathsf{f}}u(t,\mathsf{f}_{\mathsf{e}}^{-})-\epsilon l_{\mathsf{e}}u(t,\mathsf{e}^{-}),

and

q_{\mathsf{e}}\partial_{xx}u(t,\mathsf{e}^{+})+q_{\mathsf{e}}^{*}\partial_{x}u(t,\mathsf{e}^{+})=\sum_{\mathsf{f}\in\mathsf{E}}\epsilon r_{\mathsf{e},\mathsf{f}}u(t,\mathsf{f}_{\mathsf{e}}^{+})-\epsilon r_{\mathsf{e}}u(t,\mathsf{e}^{+}),

where
p_{\mathsf{e}},q_{\mathsf{e}}\in[0,1]
,
p_{\mathsf{e}}^{*}\coloneqq 1-p_{\mathsf{e}}
,

q_{\mathsf{e}}\coloneqq 1-q_{\mathsf{e}}
.
By convention, if
\mathsf{f}_{\mathsf{e}}^{-}
or
\mathsf{f}_{\mathsf{e}}^{+}
is not defined, or, equivalently,

l_{\mathsf{e},\mathsf{f}}=0
or
r_{\mathsf{e},\mathsf{f}}=0
, we let
l_{\mathsf{e},\mathsf{f}}u(t,\mathsf{f}_{\mathsf{e}}^{-})=0
or

r_{\mathsf{e},\mathsf{f}}u(t,\mathsf{f}_{\mathsf{e}}^{+})=0
, respectively.

2.1.
Generation theorem

We begin by considering the abstract operator related to the diffusion process.
To this end, let
\epsilon\in(0,1]
,
\sigma=(\sigma_{\mathsf{e}})_{\mathsf{e}\in\mathsf{E}}

be a positive continuous function on
\mathcal{G}
that is constant on each edge,
and
p_{\mathsf{e}},q_{\mathsf{e}}\in[0,1]
.
We let
A_{\epsilon}
to be the operator in
C(\mathcal{G})
given by

(2.1)

A_{\epsilon}u\coloneqq\epsilon^{-1}\sigma u^{\prime\prime},\qquad u\in\dom(A_{\epsilon}).

Recall that we consider
C(\mathcal{G})
as the space of real-valued continuous
functions on the disconnected space
\mathcal{G}
, hence,

A_{\epsilon}u(x)=\epsilon^{-1}\sigma(x)u^{\prime\prime}(x)
for all

x\in\mathcal{G}
.
We define the domain of
A_{\epsilon}
as

(2.2)

\dom(A_{\epsilon})\coloneqq\{u\in C^{2}(\mathcal{G})\colon Lu=\epsilon\Phi u\},

where
L\colon C^{2}(\mathcal{G})\to\mathbb{R}^{2\lvert\mathsf{E}\rvert}
(
\lvert E\rvert
is the
cardinality of
E
) is given by

Lu\coloneqq\big\lparen p_{\mathsf{e}}u^{\prime\prime}(\mathsf{e}^{-})-(1-p_{\mathsf{e}})u^{\prime}(\mathsf{e}^{-}),q_{\mathsf{e}}u^{\prime\prime}(\mathsf{e}^{+})+(1-q_{\mathsf{e}})u^{\prime}(\mathsf{e}^{+})\big\rparen_{\mathsf{e}\in\mathsf{E}},\qquad u\in C^{2}(\mathcal{G}),

and
\Phi\colon C(\mathcal{G})\to\mathbb{R}^{2\lvert\mathsf{E}\rvert}
is the bounded linear
operator

\Phi u=(\Phi_{\mathsf{e}}^{-},\Phi_{\mathsf{e}}^{+})_{\mathsf{e}\in\mathsf{E}}\coloneqq\bigg\lparen\sum_{\mathsf{f}\in\mathsf{E}}l_{\mathsf{e},\mathsf{f}}u(\mathsf{f}_{\mathsf{e}}^{-})-l_{\mathsf{e}}u(\mathsf{e}^{-}),\sum_{\mathsf{f}\in\mathsf{E}}r_{\mathsf{e},\mathsf{f}}u(\mathsf{f}_{\mathsf{e}}^{+})-r_{\mathsf{e}}u(\mathsf{e}^{+})\bigg\rparen_{\mathsf{e}\in\mathsf{E}}

for all
u\in C(\mathcal{G})
.
We also denote

\Phi_{\epsilon}\coloneqq\epsilon\Phi.

Theorem 2.1
.

The operator
A_{\epsilon}
given by (
2.1
)–(
2.2
)
generates a Feller semigroup in
C(\mathcal{G})
.
The semigroup is conservative if and only if

(2.3)

\sum_{f\in\mathsf{E}}l_{\mathsf{e},\mathsf{f}}=l_{\mathsf{e}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\sum_{f\in\mathsf{E}}r_{\mathsf{e},\mathsf{f}}=r_{\mathsf{e}}

for all
\mathsf{e}\in\mathsf{E}
.

Before we prove Theorem
2.1
we introduce the operator

A_{0}
as
\epsilon A_{\epsilon}
with
\Phi=0
, that is

A_{0}u=\sigma u^{\prime\prime}
for all
u\in\dom(A_{0})
, where

\dom(A_{0})\coloneqq\{u\in C^{2}(\mathcal{G})\colon Lu=0\}.

In other words, on each edge
\mathsf{e}
,
A_{0}
is a (rescaled by

\sigma_{\mathsf{e}}
) copy of the generator
G_{p_{\mathsf{e}},q_{\mathsf{e}}}
of a
one-dimensional sticky diffusion on
[0,1]
– see Appendix.

Proof.

By a well-known characterisation of Feller semigroups, see Theorem 2.2
in
[
8
]
, the operator
A_{\epsilon}
is a Feller generator if and
only if it is densely defined, satisfies the positive maximum principle, and the
range of
\lambda-A_{\epsilon}
is
C(\mathcal{G})
for some

\lambda>0
.
The fact that the domain
\dom(A_{\epsilon})
is dense in
C(\mathcal{G})

follows by Lemma
3.6
, and arguing as
in
[
4
]
p. 17, it is easy to check that
A_{\epsilon}

satisfies the positive maximum principle.
Hence, we are left with proving the range condition.
To this end we use Greiner’s idea of perturbing the boundary conditions,
see
[
11
, Lemma 1.4]
.

Let
\Lambda_{\epsilon}
be the operator in
C(\mathcal{G})
defined as

A_{\epsilon}
with domain
C^{2}(\mathcal{G})
, that is

\Lambda_{\epsilon}u\coloneqq\epsilon^{-1}\sigma u^{\prime\prime}
for all

u\in C^{2}(\mathcal{G})
, and define

L_{\lambda,\epsilon}\colon\mathbb{R}^{2\lvert\mathsf{E}\rvert}\to\ker(\lambda-\Lambda_{\epsilon}),\qquad L_{\lambda,\epsilon}\coloneqq(L_{|\ker(\lambda-\Lambda_{\epsilon})})^{-1}.

That is,
L_{\lambda,\epsilon}
is the inverse of
L
as restricted to

\ker(\lambda-\Lambda_{\epsilon})
.
By Lemma
3.2
in Appendix such inverse exists for all
\lambda>0
and

\big\lparen L_{\lambda,\epsilon}(a_{\mathsf{f}},b_{\mathsf{f}})_{\mathsf{f}\in\mathsf{E}}\big\rparen(\mathsf{e}(s))=u_{\mathsf{e}}(\mathsf{e}(s))\coloneqq c_{\mathsf{e}}\mathrm{e}^{\gamma_{\mathsf{e}}s}+d_{\mathsf{e}}\mathrm{e}^{-\gamma_{\mathsf{e}}s}

for all
(a_{\mathsf{f}},b_{\mathsf{f}})_{\mathsf{f}\in\mathsf{E}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\lvert\mathsf{E}\rvert}
,

\mathsf{e}\in\mathsf{E}
, and
s\in[0,1]
, where

\gamma_{\mathsf{e}}\coloneqq\sqrt{\lambda\epsilon/\sigma_{\mathsf{e}}}
, and

c_{\mathsf{e}}
,
d_{\mathsf{e}}
is the unique pair of real numbers
satisfying (
3.3
) for

\mu\coloneqq\gamma_{\mathsf{e}},\qquad a(\mu)\coloneqq a_{\mathsf{e}}\gamma_{\mathsf{e}}^{-1},\qquad b(\mu)\coloneqq b_{\mathsf{e}}\gamma_{\mathsf{e}}^{-1}.

Moreover, by (
3.6
) there exist
\lambda_{0}>0
and

M>0
(that depend merely on
\epsilon
and
\sigma
) such that

\lVert u_{\mathsf{e}}(\mathsf{e}(\cdot))\rVert_{C[0,1]}\leq\frac{M}{\sqrt{\lambda}}(\lvert a_{\mathsf{e}}\rvert+\lvert b_{\mathsf{e}}\rvert),\qquad\mathsf{e}\in\mathsf{E},

provided that
\lambda>\lambda_{0}
.
This proves that
L_{\lambda,\epsilon}
is a bounded operator

\mathbb{R}^{2\lvert\mathsf{E}\rvert}\to C(\mathcal{G})
and its norm satisfies

(2.4)

\lVert L_{\lambda,\epsilon}\rVert\leq\frac{M^{\prime}}{\sqrt{\lambda}},\qquad\lambda>\lambda_{0}

for some
M^{\prime}>0
depending on the norm introduced in

\mathbb{R}^{2\lvert\mathsf{E}\rvert}
.
Therefore, since
\Phi_{\epsilon}
is bounded in
C(\mathcal{G})
, there exists

\lambda_{1}>0
such that the norm of the bounded operator

L_{\lambda,\epsilon}\Phi_{\epsilon}
in
C(\mathcal{G})
is less then
1

for
\lambda>\lambda_{1}
, and consequently the operator

I_{C(\mathcal{G})}-L_{\lambda,\epsilon}\Phi_{\epsilon}
, where
I_{C(\mathcal{G})}

is the identity operator in
C(\mathcal{G})
, is invertible.

Let
v\in C(\mathcal{G})
, and choose
\lambda>\lambda_{1}
.
Set
w\coloneqq(\lambda-A_{0})^{-1}v
, which exists by
Theorem
3.1
, and define

u\coloneqq(I_{C(\mathcal{G})}-L_{\lambda,\epsilon}\Phi_{\epsilon})^{-1}w.

We show that
u
belongs to
\dom(A_{\epsilon})
and that

(\lambda-A_{\epsilon})u=v
.
We have
w\in\dom(A_{0})
, and hence

u=w+L_{\lambda,\epsilon}\Phi_{\epsilon}u
belongs to
C^{2}(\mathcal{G})
.
To check that
u\in D(A_{\epsilon})
, note that

Lu=Lw+LL_{\lambda,\epsilon}\Phi_{\epsilon}u=0+\Phi_{\epsilon}u=\Phi_{\epsilon}u.

Finally,

\displaystyle A_{\epsilon}u

\displaystyle=\Lambda_{\epsilon}w+\lambda L_{\lambda,\epsilon}\Phi_{\epsilon}u-(\lambda-\Lambda_{\epsilon})L_{\lambda,\epsilon}\Phi_{\epsilon}u

\displaystyle=-(\lambda-\Lambda_{\epsilon})w+\lambda w+\lambda L_{\lambda,\epsilon}\Phi_{\epsilon}u=-v+\lambda u,

which completes the proof of the generation part.

The semigroup generated by
A_{\epsilon}
is conservative if and only if the
function
1_{\mathcal{G}}
that equals
1
for every
x\in\mathcal{G}
belongs to
the domain of
A_{\epsilon}
.
Since
L1_{\mathcal{G}}=0
,
1_{\mathcal{G}}\in\dom(A_{\epsilon})
is equivalent to

\Phi 1_{\mathcal{G}}=0
, which is exactly (
2.3
).
∎

As a by-product of the proof we obtain the formula for the resolvent

\res(\lambda,A_{\epsilon})
of
A_{\epsilon}
in terms of the resolvent

\res(\lambda,A_{0})
of
A_{0}
.

Corollary 2.2
.

For sufficiently large
\lambda>0
we have

\res(\lambda,A_{\epsilon})u=(I_{C(\mathcal{G})}-L_{\lambda,\epsilon}\Phi_{\epsilon})^{-1}\res(\lambda,A_{0})u,\qquad u\in C(\mathcal{G}),

where

L_{\lambda,\epsilon}\coloneqq(L_{|\ker(\lambda-\Lambda_{\epsilon})})^{-1}

for

\Lambda_{\epsilon}u\coloneqq\epsilon^{-1}\sigma u^{\prime\prime},\qquad u\in C^{2}(\mathcal{G}).

2.2.
Convergence

Here we examine what happens with the semigroup generated by
A_{\epsilon}
,
when
\epsilon
converges to
0^{+}
.
To this end we recall (a special case of) the singular convergence theorem due to
T. Kurtz (see
[
8
, Corollary 7.7, p. 40]
or
[
4
, Theorem 42.2 and
Theorem 7.1]
).

Theorem 2.3
.

Assume that the following conditions hold.

(i)

For every
\epsilon\in[0,1]
each operator

\mathcal{A}_{\epsilon}
is the generator of a strongly continuous semigroup in a
Banach space
\mathbb{X}
, and there exists
M>0
such that

\lVert\mathrm{e}^{t\mathcal{A}_{\epsilon}}\rVert_{\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{X})}\leq M
for all

\epsilon\in(0,1]
and
t\geq 0
.

(ii)

For some
(
hence all
)

\lambda>0
the resolvent
R(\lambda,\epsilon\mathcal{A}_{\epsilon})
converges
strongly to the resolvent
R(\lambda,\mathcal{A}_{0})
as
\epsilon\to 0^{+}
.

(iii)

For every
x\in\mathbb{X}
the limit

\mathcal{P}x\coloneqq\lim_{t\to+\infty}\mathrm{e}^{t\mathcal{A}_{0}}x

exists.

(iv)

An operator
\mathcal{Q}
generates a strongly continuous semigroup in the space

\mathbb{X}_{0}\coloneqq\range\mathcal{P},

and for some
(
hence all sufficiently large
)

\lambda>0
we have

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}R(\lambda,\mathcal{A}_{\epsilon})x=R(\lambda,\mathcal{Q})\mathcal{P}x,\qquad x\in\mathbb{X}.

Then

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\mathrm{e}^{t\mathcal{A}_{\epsilon}}x=\mathrm{e}^{t\mathcal{Q}}\mathcal{P}x

for all
t>0
and
x\in\mathbb{X}
.
The convergence is uniform in
t
on compact subsets of
(0,+\infty)
,
and if
x\in\mathbb{X}_{0}
, then the formula holds also for
t=0
, and the
convergence is uniform in
t
on compact subsets of
[0,+\infty)
.

We apply Kurtz’s theorem in the following setup.
Let
\mathbb{X}\coloneqq C(\mathcal{G})
and
\mathcal{A}_{\epsilon}\coloneqq A_{\epsilon}
for
every
\epsilon\in[0,1]
.
Then condition
(i)
holds by Theorem
2.1
.
Moreover, by Theorem
3.1
, we obtain

(2.5)

\lim_{t\to+\infty}\mathrm{e}^{tA_{0}}u=\lim_{\lambda\to 0^{+}}\lambda\res(\lambda,A_{0})u=Pu,\qquad u\in C(\mathcal{G}),

for

(2.6)

P\coloneqq(P_{p_{\mathsf{e}},q_{\mathsf{e}}})_{\mathsf{e}\in\mathsf{E}},

establishing
(iii)
.
Therefore, we are left with choosing appropriate
\mathcal{Q}
and
proving
(ii)
,
(iv)
.

To this end for each
\mathsf{e}\in\mathsf{E}
we define the operator

Q_{\mathrm{e}}\colon C(\mathcal{G})\to C[0,1]
in the following way.
If
p_{\mathsf{e}}=1
and
q_{\mathsf{e}}=1
, then

Q_{\mathsf{e}}u(x)\coloneqq(1-x)\Phi_{\mathsf{e}}^{-}u+x\Phi_{\mathsf{e}}^{+}u,\qquad u\in C(\mathcal{G}),\ x\in[0,1].

On the other, if
p_{\mathsf{e}}\neq 1
or
q_{\mathsf{e}}\neq 1
, then

Q_{\mathsf{e}}u\coloneqq\frac{1}{1-p_{\mathsf{e}}q_{\mathsf{e}}}(q_{\mathsf{e}}^{*}\Phi_{\mathsf{e}}^{-}u+p_{\mathsf{e}}^{*}\Phi_{\mathsf{e}}^{+}u),\qquad u\in C(\mathcal{G}),

where the right-hand side is identified with the constant function on
\mathsf{e}
.
Now we define the operator
Q\colon C(\mathcal{G})\to C(\mathcal{G})
by the formula

(2.7)

Q\coloneqq\sigma(Q_{\mathsf{e}})_{\mathsf{e}\in\mathsf{E}}=(\sigma_{\mathsf{e}}Q_{\mathsf{e}})_{\mathsf{e}\in\mathsf{E}}.

Finally, in order to characterize
\mathbb{X}_{0}=\range P
, note that

\range P_{p_{\mathsf{e}},q_{\mathsf{e}}}=\mathbb{R}
, provided that

p_{\mathsf{e}}q_{\mathsf{e}}\neq 1
, and
\range P_{p_{\mathsf{e}},q_{\mathsf{e}}}=\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
provided that
p_{\mathsf{e}}q_{\mathsf{e}}=1
.
Hence

\range P\eqqcolon C_{0}(\mathcal{G}),

where
C_{0}(\mathcal{G})
is isometrically isomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{N+N_{1}}
,
N_{1}

being the number of
\mathsf{e}\in\mathsf{E}
such that
p_{\mathsf{e}}=q_{\mathsf{e}}=1
.
Note that the operator
Q
is bounded on
C_{0}(\mathcal{G})
.
Now we are ready to state the main result.

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
A_{\epsilon}
,
\epsilon\in(0,1]
be defined by (
2.1
) with domain (
2.2
). For operators
P
and
Q
given by (
2.6
) and (
2.7
), respectively, it follows that

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\mathrm{e}^{tA_{\epsilon}}u=\mathrm{e}^{tQ}Pu,\qquad t>0,\ u\in C(\mathcal{G})

uniformly in
t
on compact subsets of
(0,+\infty)
.
Moreover, if
u\in C_{0}(\mathcal{G})
, then the formula holds also for
t=0
,
and the convergence is uniform on compact subsets of
[0,+\infty)
.

To verify conditions
(ii)
and
(iv)
and consequently
prove Theorem
2.4
, we need the following result.

Lemma 2.5
.

For sufficiently large
\lambda>0
and all
u\in C(\mathcal{G})
we have

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\res(\lambda,\epsilon A_{\epsilon})u=\res(\lambda,A_{0})u,

and

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\res(\lambda,A_{\epsilon})u=\res(\lambda,Q)Pu.

Proof.

We first prove the second equality.
Combining Theorem
3.1
and
Corollary
2.2
, we are left with investigating the (strong) limit
of
L_{\lambda,\epsilon}\Phi_{\epsilon}
.
Let
u=(u_{\mathsf{e}})_{\mathsf{e}\in\mathsf{E}}\in C(\mathcal{G})
.
Then, calculating as in the proof of Theorem
2.1
, we have

L_{\lambda,\epsilon}\Phi_{\epsilon}u(x)=(v_{\mathsf{e}})_{\mathsf{e}\in\mathsf{E}},

where

v_{\mathsf{e}}(x)\coloneqq c_{\mathsf{e}}\mathrm{e}^{\gamma_{\mathsf{e}}x}+d_{\mathsf{e}}\mathrm{e}^{-\gamma_{\mathsf{e}}x},\qquad x\in[0,1]

for
\gamma_{\mathsf{e}}\coloneqq\sqrt{\lambda\epsilon/\sigma_{\mathsf{e}}}
, and

c_{\mathsf{e}}
,
d_{\mathsf{e}}
being the unique pair of real numbers satisfying
the system of linear equations (
3.3
) for

z\coloneqq\gamma_{\mathsf{e}}
, and

a\coloneqq\epsilon\gamma_{\mathsf{e}}^{-1}\Phi_{\mathsf{e}}^{-}u
,

b\coloneqq\epsilon\gamma_{\mathsf{e}}^{-1}\Phi_{\mathsf{e}}^{+}u
.
Now, for each
\mathsf{e}\in\mathsf{E}
we consider two cases, depending on whether

p_{\mathsf{e}}=q_{\mathsf{e}}=1
or not.

Case
1: Assume that
p_{\mathsf{e}}\neq 1
or
q_{\mathsf{e}}\neq 1
.
Then, solving the system explicitly using Lemma
3.2
and taking the
limit as
\epsilon\to 0^{+}
, we obtain that both
c_{\mathsf{e}}
and

d_{\mathsf{e}}
converges to
\delta_{\mathsf{e}}/2
, where

\delta_{\mathsf{e}}\coloneqq\lambda^{-1}\sigma_{\mathsf{e}}\frac{q_{\mathsf{e}}^{*}\Phi_{\mathsf{e}}^{-}u+p_{\mathsf{e}}^{*}\Phi_{\mathsf{e}}^{+}u}{1-p_{\mathsf{e}}q_{\mathsf{e}}}.

Consequently
v_{\mathsf{e}}
converges in
C[0,1]
to the constant function
that equals
\delta_{\mathsf{e}}
on
[0,1]
.

Case
2: Assume that
p_{\mathsf{e}}=q_{\mathsf{e}}=1
.
Then, using Lemma
3.2
, we have

v_{\mathsf{e}}(x)=\lambda^{-1}\sigma_{\mathsf{e}}\Phi_{\mathsf{e}}^{-}u\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\gamma_{\mathsf{e}}(1-x)}-\mathrm{e}^{-\gamma_{\mathsf{e}}(1-x)}}{\mathrm{e}^{\gamma_{\mathsf{e}}}-\mathrm{e}^{-\gamma_{\mathsf{e}}}}+\lambda^{-1}\sigma_{\mathsf{e}}\Phi_{\mathsf{e}}^{+}u\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\gamma_{\mathsf{e}}x}-\mathrm{e}^{-\gamma_{\mathsf{e}}x}}{\mathrm{e}^{\gamma_{\mathsf{e}}}-\mathrm{e}^{-\gamma_{\mathsf{e}}}},\qquad x\in[0,1],

and taking
\epsilon\to 0^{+}
, we see that
v_{\mathsf{e}}
converges in

C[0,1]
to the function

x\mapsto(1-x)\lambda^{-1}\sigma_{\mathsf{e}}\Phi_{\mathsf{e}}^{-}u+x\lambda^{-1}\sigma_{\mathsf{e}}\Phi_{\mathsf{e}}^{+}u.

Combining Case 1 and Case 2 we see that
L_{\lambda,\epsilon}\Phi_{\epsilon}

converges in
C(\mathcal{G})
to
\lambda^{-1}Q
as
\epsilon\to 0^{+}
.
Hence (recall that the norm of
L_{\lambda,\epsilon}
is uniformly bounded
in
\epsilon
, see (
2.4
)), by (
2.5
) we obtain

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\res(\lambda,A_{\epsilon})u

\displaystyle=\lambda^{-1}\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}(I_{C(\mathcal{G})}-L_{\lambda,\epsilon}\Phi_{\epsilon})^{-1}Pu

\displaystyle=\lambda^{-1}(I_{C(\mathcal{G})}-\lambda^{-1}Q)^{-1}Pu

\displaystyle=(\lambda-Q)^{-1}Pu

for all
u\in C(\mathcal{G})
, which completes the proof of the second part of the
lemma.

The first part follows similarly from the identity (obtained in the same way as
Corollary
2.2
)

\res(\lambda,\epsilon A_{\epsilon})=\res(1,L_{\lambda,1}\Phi_{\epsilon})\res(\lambda,A_{0}),

which holds for sufficiently large
\lambda>0
.
∎

3.
Appendix: One-dimensional sticky diffusion

Fix
p,q\in[0,1]
and consider the linear operator
G_{p,q}
in

C[0,1]
given by

G_{p,q}f\coloneqq f^{\prime\prime},\qquad f\in D(G_{p,q})

with domain
D(G_{p,q})
consisting of functions
f\in C^{2}[0,1]

satisfying boundary conditions

(3.1)

pf^{\prime\prime}(0)-p^{*}f^{\prime}(0)=qf^{\prime\prime}(1)+q^{*}f^{\prime}(1)=0,

where

p^{*}\coloneqq 1-p,\qquad q^{*}\coloneqq 1-q.

We prove that
G_{p,q}
is the generator of a Feller semigroup in

C[0,1]
.
The stochastic process related to
G_{p,q}
, in the sense of Theorem 3.15
in
[
13
]
, may be described as follows.
Inside
(0,1)
the process behaves like a Brownian motion with variance

1
.
When a Brownian particle hits
0
, then the behaviour depends on
p
:

•

If
p=0
, then the barrier at
0
is reflecting, and the
particle bounces back to
(0,1)
.
The time that this particle spends at
0
is of measure zero with respect to
the Lebesgue measure, however, it has a positive measure with respect to the
Lévy local time
t^{+}
, see
[
5
, Section 4]
and the
references given there.

•

If
p=1
, then the barrier at
0
is absorbing, and the
particle stays at
0
forever.

•

If
p\in(0,1)
, then the barrier at
0
is sticky, and the
particle stays at
0
for some time depending on
p
.
In contradistinction to the case
p=0
, the time has positive Lebesgue
measure and increases with
p
, see the first displayed formula after (3.42)
in
[
13
, p. 128]
.

We call
p
a stickiness coefficient at
0
.
Similar description is valid for the barrier at
1
and the stickiness
coefficient
q
.

It is also possible to describe the behaviour of the process governed by

G_{p,q}
as the time increases.
To this end we introduce
P_{p,q}
as the bounded linear operator in

C[0,1]
defined for
p\neq 1
and
q\neq 1
by

P_{p,q}f\coloneqq\frac{pq^{*}f(0)+p^{*}qf(1)+p^{*}q^{*}\int_{0}^{1}f}{pq^{*}+p^{*}q+p^{*}q^{*}},\qquad f\in C[0,1];

here the right-hand side is identified with the constant function.
For
p=q=1
we additionally set

P_{1,1}f(x)\coloneqq(1-x)f(0)+xf(1),\qquad f\in C[0,1],\ x\in[0,1].

Note that for all
p,q\in[0,1]
the operator
P_{p,q}
is a
projection, that is
P_{p,q}^{2}=P_{p,q}
.

The main result concerning one-dimensional diffusion with sticky barriers is as
follows.

Theorem 3.1
.

The operator
G_{p,q}
generates a conservative, bounded analytic Feller
semigroup in
C[0,1]
of angle
\pi/2
, and

(3.2)

\lim_{t\to+\infty}\mathrm{e}^{tG_{p,q}}f=P_{p,q}f,\qquad f\in C[0,1].

Before we prove Theorem
3.1
we first state some
auxiliary results.
By
\mathbb{C}_{+}
we denote the right-half of the complex plane, that is

\mathbb{C}_{+}\coloneqq\{\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\colon\re\lambda>0\}.

Lemma 3.2
.

For every
\mu\in\mathbb{C}_{+}
,
p,q\in[0,1]
and functions

a,b\colon\mathbb{C}_{+}\to\mathbb{C}
, the linear system

(3.3)

\begin{bmatrix}p\mu-p^{*}&p\mu+p^{*}\\
\mathrm{e}^{\mu}\lparen q\mu+q^{*}\rparen&\mathrm{e}^{-\mu}\lparen q\mu-q^{*}\rparen.\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}c_{\mu}\\
d_{\mu}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}a(\mu)\\
b(\mu)\end{bmatrix}

has a unique complex solution
(c_{\mu},d_{\mu})
given by

(3.4)

c_{\mu}=\frac{(p\mu+p^{*})b(\mu)-\mathrm{e}^{-\mu}(q\mu-q^{*})a(\mu)}{\mathrm{e}^{\mu}(p\mu+p^{*})(q\mu+q^{*})-\mathrm{e}^{-\mu}(p\mu-p^{*})(q\mu-q^{*})}

and

(3.5)

d_{\mu}=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\mu}(q\mu+q^{*})a(\mu)-(p\mu-p^{*})b(\mu)}{\mathrm{e}^{\mu}(p\mu+p^{*})(q\mu+q^{*})-\mathrm{e}^{-\mu}(p\mu-p^{*})(q\mu-q^{*})}

Moreover,

(3.6)

c_{\mu}=O(\lvert a(\mu)\rvert\mathrm{e}^{-2\re\mu}+\lvert b(\mu)\rvert\mathrm{e}^{-\re\mu}),\qquad d_{\mu}=O(\lvert a(\mu)\rvert+\lvert b(\mu)\rvert\mathrm{e}^{-\re\mu})

as
\re\mu\to+\infty
.

Proof.

A little bit of algebra shows that the determinant
D_{\mu}
of the system
equals

(3.7)

\mathrm{e}^{-\mu}(p\mu+p^{*})(q\mu+q^{*})\big[H_{p}(\mu)H_{q}(\mu)-\mathrm{e}^{2\mu}\big],

where

H_{r}(z)\coloneqq\frac{rz-1+r}{rz+1-r},\qquad r\in[0,1],\ z\in\mathbb{C}_{+}.

We have

\lvert H_{r}(z)\rvert\leq 1,\qquad r\in[0,1],\ z\in\mathbb{C}_{+}.

Indeed, this is trivial for
r=0
, and for fixed
r\in(0,1]
the
function
H_{r}
is (a restriction of) the Möbius transformation that maps

\mathbb{C}_{+}
onto the open unit ball in
\mathbb{C}
.
Therefore, by (
3.7
) it follows that

\lvert D_{\mu}\rvert\geq\mathrm{e}^{-\re\mu}\lvert p\mu+p^{*}\rvert\lvert q\mu+q^{*}\rvert(\mathrm{e}^{2\re\mu}-1)>0,

where in the last inequality we used the fact that
\re\mu>0
.
Hence the system has a unique solution, and the remaining part of the lemma
follows by applying Cramer’s rule.
∎

We are ready to prove that the resolvent of
G_{p,q}
exists.
To this end, for every
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
we define the function
\mathrm{e}_{\lambda}
in

C[0,1]
by

\mathrm{e}_{\lambda}(x)\coloneqq\mathrm{e}^{\lambda x},\qquad x\in[0,1].

Moreover, for
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
and
f\in C[0,1]
we
introduce
h_{\lambda}=h_{\lambda,f}\in C[0,1]
by the formula

h_{\lambda}(x)\coloneqq\frac{1}{2\lambda}\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda\lvert x-y\rvert}f(y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y,\qquad x\in[0,1].

Lemma 3.3
.

The resolvent set of
G_{p,q}
contains
\mathbb{C}\setminus(-\infty,0]
and

(3.8)

R(\lambda^{2},G_{p,q})f=c_{\lambda}\mathrm{e}_{\lambda}+d_{\lambda}\mathrm{e}_{-\lambda}+h_{\lambda},\qquad\lambda\in\mathbb{C}_{+},\ f\in C[0,1],\qquad

where
c_{\lambda}
and
d_{\lambda}
are given
by (
3.4
)-(
3.5
) with

(3.9)

a(\lambda)\coloneqq pf(0)\lambda^{-1}-h_{\lambda}(0)(p\lambda-p^{*}),\qquad b(\lambda)\coloneqq qf(1)\lambda^{-1}-h_{\lambda}(1)(q\lambda-q^{*}).

Proof.

Since each complex number in
\mathbb{C}\setminus(-\infty,0]
has a unique square
root with positive real part, to prove that
\rho(G_{p,q})
contains

\mathbb{C}\setminus(-\infty,0]
it is enough to show that the resolvent

R(\lambda^{2},G_{p,q})
exists for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}_{+}
.
Hence, fix
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}_{+}
and let
f\in C[0,1]
.
Observe that the function
h_{\lambda}
belongs to
C^{2}[0,1]
and

\lambda^{2}h_{\lambda}-h_{\lambda}^{\prime\prime}=f
.
Therefore, each
g\in C^{2}[0,1]
satisfying
\lambda^{2}g-g^{\prime\prime}=f
may
be written in the form

g=c_{\lambda}\mathrm{e}_{\lambda}+d_{\lambda}\mathrm{e}_{-\lambda}+h_{\lambda}

for some
c_{\lambda},d_{\lambda}\in\mathbb{C}
.
It follows that
g
belongs to
D(G_{p,q})
,
see (
3.1
), if and only if (
3.3
) holds with

a
and
b
as in (
3.9
).
Consequently, Lemma
3.2
implies that
g\in D(G_{p,q})
for the
unique pair
(c_{\lambda},d_{\lambda})
given
by (
3.4
)-(
3.5
) with
a
and
b
as
in (
3.9
).
Moreover, there is an
M=M(\lambda)>0
such that

\lVert g\rVert_{C[0,1]}\leq M\lVert f\rVert_{C[0,1]}
, which proves that

\lambda^{2}
belongs to the resolvent set of
G_{p,q}
and

R(\lambda^{2},G_{p,q})=g
.
∎

Using the explicit formula obtained in Lemma
3.3
, we calculate
the strong limit of the resolvent
R(\lambda,G_{p,q})
as
\lambda

converges to
0
.

Proposition 3.4
.

We have

\lim_{\lambda\to 0}\lambda R(\lambda,G_{p,q})f=P_{p,q}f,\qquad f\in C[0,1],

in
C[0,1]
, where the limit is taken over

\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\setminus(-\infty,0]
.

Proof.

By the same reason as in the beginning of the proof of
Lemma
3.3
, it suffices to find the limit of

\lambda^{2}R(\lambda^{2},G_{p,q})f
as
\lambda\to 0
in
\mathbb{C}_{+}
.
Furthermore, since
\lambda^{2}h_{\lambda}
converges to zero as

\lambda\to 0
, by (
3.8
) we are left with calculating the
limit of
\lambda^{2}(c_{\lambda}\mathrm{e}_{\lambda}+d_{\lambda}\mathrm{e}_{-\lambda})
.

We split the proof into two cases depending on whether both barriers are
absorbing, that is
p=q=1
, or not.

Case
1: Suppose
p\neq 1
or
q\neq 1
.
We have

h_{\lambda}(0)=\frac{1}{2\lambda}\int_{0}^{1}(1+O(\lambda))f(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=\frac{1}{2\lambda}\int_{0}^{1}f+O(1),

and similarly

h_{\lambda}(1)=\frac{1}{2\lambda}\int_{0}^{1}f+O(1);

here, and in what follows in the proof, when we use the big-O notation we always
mean “as
\lambda\to 0
in
\mathbb{C}_{+}
.”
Hence, we may rewrite
a(\lambda)
and
b(\lambda)
given
by (
3.9
) in the asymptotic form

a(\lambda)=\frac{pf(0)}{\lambda}+\frac{p^{*}}{2\lambda}\int_{0}^{1}f+O(1),\qquad b(\lambda)=\frac{qf(1)}{\lambda}+\frac{q^{*}}{2\lambda}\int_{0}^{1}f+O(1).

Denoting by
D_{\lambda}
the determinant of the coefficient matrix
in (
3.3
), we have

\displaystyle\lambda c_{\lambda}

\displaystyle=\frac{-\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda}pq^{*}f(0)-\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda}\frac{p^{*}q^{*}}{2}\int_{0}^{1}f-p^{*}qf(1)-\frac{p^{*}q^{*}}{2}\int_{0}^{1}f+O(\lambda)}{D_{\lambda}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{pq^{*}f(0)+p^{*}qf(1)+p^{*}q^{*}\int_{0}^{1}f+O(\lambda)}{D_{\lambda}}.

Since

D_{\lambda}=(\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda}-\mathrm{e}^{\lambda})p^{*}q^{*}-2\lambda pq^{*}-2\lambda p^{*}q+O(\lambda^{2})=-2\lambda(p^{*}q^{*}+pq^{*}+p^{*}q)+O(\lambda^{2}),

this leads to

\lambda c_{\lambda}=\frac{1}{2\lambda}P_{p,q}f+O(1).

Similarly, the same relation holds with
c_{\lambda}
replaced by

d_{\lambda}
, and consequently we easily check that

\lVert\lambda^{2}(c_{\lambda}\mathrm{e}_{\lambda}+d_{\lambda}\mathrm{e}_{-\lambda})-P_{p,q}f\rVert_{C[0,1]}=O(\lambda).

Case
2: Suppose
p=q=1
.
Then by Lemma
3.3
it follows that

\lambda^{2}c_{\lambda}=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda}f(0)-f(1)-\lambda^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda}h_{\lambda}(0)+\lambda^{2}h_{\lambda}(1)}{\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda}-\mathrm{e}^{\lambda}},

and

\lambda^{2}d_{\lambda}=\frac{-\mathrm{e}^{\lambda}f(0)+f(1)+\lambda^{2}\mathrm{e}^{\lambda}h_{\lambda}(0)-\lambda^{2}h_{\lambda}(1)}{\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda}-\mathrm{e}^{\lambda}}.

Denoting

E_{\lambda}(x)\coloneqq\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda x}-\mathrm{e}^{\lambda x}}{\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda}-\mathrm{e}^{\lambda}},\qquad x\in[0,1],

for all
x\in[0,1]
we have

\displaystyle\lambda^{2}[c_{\lambda}\mathrm{e}_{\lambda}(x)+d_{\lambda}\mathrm{e}_{-\lambda}(x)]=[f(0)-\lambda^{2}h_{\lambda}(0)]E_{\lambda}(1-x)+[f(1)-\lambda^{2}h_{\lambda}(1)]E_{\lambda}(x).

Since
\sup_{x\in[0,1]}\lvert E_{\lambda}(x)-x\rvert=O(\lambda)
, this leads
to

\lVert\lambda^{2}(c_{\lambda}\mathrm{e}_{\lambda}(x)+d_{\lambda}\mathrm{e}_{-\lambda})-P_{1,1}f\rVert_{C[0,1]}=O(\lambda),

which completes the proof.
∎

Proposition 3.5
.

The operator
G_{p,q}
is sectorial with angle
\pi/2
.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.3
we are left with proving that for each

\delta\in(0,\pi/2)
there exists
M>0
such that

(3.10)

\lVert R(\lambda,G_{p,q})\rVert_{\mathcal{L}(C[0,1])}\leq\frac{M}{\lvert\lambda\rvert},\qquad\lambda\in\Sigma_{\pi/2+\delta}.

Since the resolvent is an analytic function on the resolvent set,
see
[
7
, Proposition IV.1.3]
, by Proposition
3.4

it follows that the function

\lambda\mapsto\lambda\lVert R(\lambda,G_{p,q})\rVert_{\mathcal{L}(C[0,1])}
is
bounded in every bounded subset of
\Sigma_{\pi/2+\delta}
.

We estimate the resolvent “at infinity”.
Note that it suffices to prove (
3.10
) in the sector

\Sigma_{\pi/4+\delta/2}
with
\lambda
replaced by
\lambda^{2}
.
Let
f\in C[0,1]
be such that
\lVert f\rVert_{C[0,1]}\leq 1
.
Lemma
3.3
implies that

(3.11)

\lVert R(\lambda^{2},G_{p,q})f\rVert_{C[0,1]}\leq\lvert c_{\lambda}\rvert\mathrm{e}^{\re\lambda}+\lvert d_{\lambda}\rvert+\lVert h_{\lambda}\rVert_{C[0,1]},\qquad\lambda\in\mathbb{C}_{+}.

For every
\lambda\in\Sigma_{\pi/4+\delta/2}
we have

\re\lambda\geq\cos(\pi/4+\delta/2)\lvert\lambda\rvert
, hence

\lVert h_{\lambda}\rVert_{C[0,1]}\leq\frac{1}{2\lvert\lambda\rvert}\sup_{x\in[0,1]}\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{e}^{-\re\lambda\lvert x-y\rvert}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y=O(\lambda^{-2})

uniformly in
f
; here, and in what follows in the proof, when we use the
big-O notation we always mean “as
\lvert\lambda\rvert\to+\infty
in the
sector
\Sigma_{\pi/4+\delta/2}
.”
This leads to

c_{\lambda}=\frac{O(\lambda^{-2})}{\mathrm{e}^{\lambda}+O(1)}=O(\lambda^{-2}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda}),\qquad d_{\lambda}=\frac{O(\lambda^{-2}\mathrm{e}^{\lambda})}{\mathrm{e}^{\lambda}+O(1)}=O(\lambda^{-2})

uniformly in
f
.
Using (
3.11
) we obtain

\lVert R(\lambda^{2},G_{p,q})\rVert_{\mathcal{L}(C[0,1])}=O(\lambda^{-2})
as desired.
∎

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
a_{0}
,
a_{1}
,
b_{0}
and
b_{1}
be real numbers.
For every
\epsilon>0
there exists a function
f\in C^{2}[0,1]

satisfying

(3.12)

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(0)

\displaystyle=a_{0},

\displaystyle f^{\prime\prime}(0)

\displaystyle=b_{0},

(3.13)

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(1)

\displaystyle=a_{1},

\displaystyle f^{\prime\prime}(1)

\displaystyle=b_{1},

and such that

\lVert f\rVert_{C[0,1]}\leq\epsilon.

Proof.

Let
f_{\gamma}\in C[0,1]
be given by

(3.14)

f_{\gamma}(x)\coloneqq\alpha_{0}\mathrm{e}^{-\gamma x}+\beta_{0}\mathrm{e}^{-\gamma^{2}x}+\alpha_{1}\mathrm{e}^{-\gamma(1-x)}+\beta_{1}\mathrm{e}^{-\gamma^{2}(1-x)},\qquad x\in[0,1],

where
\alpha_{0}
,
\alpha_{1}
,
\beta_{0}
and
\beta_{1}
are real
numbers.
Such
f_{\gamma}
satisfies (
3.12
)–(
3.13
) if and only if

(3.15)

\begin{bmatrix}-\gamma&-\gamma^{2}&\gamma\mathrm{e}^{-\gamma}&\gamma^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-\gamma^{2}}\\
\gamma^{2}&\gamma^{4}&\gamma^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-\gamma}&\gamma^{4}\mathrm{e}^{-\gamma^{2}}\\
-\gamma\mathrm{e}^{-\gamma}&-\gamma^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-\gamma^{2}}&\gamma&\gamma^{2}\\
\gamma^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-\gamma}&\gamma^{4}\mathrm{e}^{-\gamma^{2}}&\gamma^{2}&\gamma^{4}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\alpha_{0}\\
\beta_{0}\\
\alpha_{1}\\
\beta_{1}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}a_{0}\\
b_{0}\\
a_{1}\\
b_{1}\end{bmatrix}.

For the determinant
D_{\gamma}
of the coefficients matrix we have

D_{\gamma}=-\gamma^{10}+O(\gamma^{8})\qquad\text{as
$\gamma\to+\infty$}.

Hence, for sufficiently large
\gamma_{0}>0
, it follows that

D_{\gamma}\neq 0
for
\gamma>\gamma_{0}
.
Consequently, for all
\gamma>\gamma_{0}
we choose
\alpha_{0}
,

\alpha_{1}
,
\beta_{0}
and
\beta_{1}
in such a way that

f_{\gamma}
given by (
3.14
)
satisfies (
3.12
)–(
3.13
).
Moreover, by (
3.15
) it follows that

\alpha_{0}=\alpha_{1}=O(\gamma^{-1}),\qquad\beta_{0}=\beta_{1}=O(\gamma^{-3})\qquad\text{as $\gamma\to+\infty$.}

Therefore,
\lim_{\gamma\to+\infty}\lVert f_{\gamma}\rVert_{C[0,1]}=0
,
which completes the proof.
∎

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

By Lemma
3.6
it follows that the operator
G_{p,q}

is densely defined.
Hence, by Proposition
3.5
, it generates an analytic
semigroup
\{\mathrm{e}^{tG_{p,q}}\}_{t\geq 0}
in
C[0,1]
.
We prove that
G_{p,q}
is a Feller generator.
By a well-known characterization of Feller semigroups, see
[
8
, Theorem 2.2,
p. 165]
, it suffices to check that
G_{p,q}
satisfies the positive
maximum principle, that is: if
f\in D(G_{p,q})
attains the maximum at

x\in[0,1]
, then
f(x)\geq 0
implies
G_{p,q}f(x)\leq 0
.
This is clear for
x\in(0,1)
, and if
f
attains the nonnegative
maximum at
x=0
or
x=1
, then
f^{\prime}(0)\leq 0
or

f^{\prime}(1)\geq 0
, respectively.
Thus the claim follows, since
f
satisfies boundary
conditions (
3.1
).
Moreover, the function
1_{[0,1]}
belongs to the domain of
G_{p,q}

and
G_{p,q}1_{[0,1]}=0
, hence the semigroup is conservative.

To prove (
3.2
) note first that the limit on the left-hand side
exists, which follows by the sectoriality of the semigroup and
Proposition
3.4
,
see
[
4
, Corollary 32.1]
.
Let
f\in C[0,1]
, and write

f=(f-P_{p,q}f)+P_{p,q}f.

By Proposition
3.4
we have

\lambda R(\lambda,G_{p,q})(f-P_{p,q}f)\to P_{p,q}(f-P_{p,q}f)=0

as
\lambda
converges to zero in
\mathbb{C}\setminus(-\infty,0]
.
However, the resolvent
R(\lambda,G_{p,q})
is the Laplace transform of the
semigroup generated by
G_{p,q}
, hence

\lambda R(\lambda,G_{p,q})=\int_{0}^{+\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-t}\mathrm{e}^{\frac{t}{\lambda}G_{p,q}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t,

which implies that
\mathrm{e}^{tG_{p,q}}(f-P_{p,q}f)\to 0
as

t\to+\infty
.
Finally, because
G_{p,q}P_{p,q}f=0
for all
p,q\in[0,1]
, we
have
R(\lambda,G_{p,q})P_{p,q}f=\lambda^{-1}P_{p,q}f
.
Therefore, by the Yosida approximation it follows that

\mathrm{e}^{tG_{p,q}}P_{p,q}f=P_{p,q}f
, which completes the proof.
∎
