A Congruence Family For 2-Elongated Plane Partitions: An Application of the Localization Method
Nicolas Allen Smoot

Abstract
. George Andrews and Peter Paule have recently conjectured an infinite family of congruences modulo powers of 3 for the 2-elongated plane partition function d 2 ​ ( n ) d_{2}(n) . This congruence family appears difficult to prove by classical methods. We prove a refined form of this conjecture by expressing the associated generating functions as elements of a ring of modular functions isomorphic to a localization of ℤ ⁡ [ X ] \mathbb{Z}[X] .

1.
Introduction

1.1.
k
-Elongated Plane Partitions

In a recent paper
[
2
]
, George Andrews and Peter Paule study the properties of various plane partition functions. In particular, they provide a large number of results for the
k
-elongated partition function
d_{k}(n)
, for
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
. This function is enumerated by

(1.1)

\displaystyle D_{k}(q):=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}d_{k}(n)q^{n}=\frac{(q^{2};q^{2})^{k}_{\infty}}{(q;q)^{3k+1}_{\infty}}.

For
n\geq 0
,
d_{k}(n)
counts the number of unique directed graphs of
k
-elongated diamonds, shown in the diagrams below, in which each node is assigned a nonnegative integer label
a_{j}
, a directed edge
a_{b}\rightarrow a_{c}
indicates that
a_{b}\geq a_{c}
, and the sum of the central linking nodes
a_{1}+a_{2k+2}+a_{4k+3}+...+a_{(2k+1)m+1}
yields
n
.

Figure 1.
A schematic of a
k
-elongated partition diamond of length 1.

Figure 2.
A schematic of a
k
-elongated partition diamond of length
m
.

Notice that
d_{k}(n)
generalizes the unrestricted partition function, since
d_{0}(n)=p(n)
. Moreover,
k
-elongated plane partitions are also a generalization of partition diamonds, which were developed in
[
1
]
as an application of the algorithmic procedures of Partition Analysis begun with MacMahon
[
11
, Vol. II, Section VIII]
, and fully developed by Andrews, Paule, and Riese in their series of papers on the subject.

One peculiar characteristic of
d_{k}(n)
is a richness of arithmetic properties which is surprising even by the standards of partition-type functions. Andrews and Paule discover many such properties in
[
2
, Section 7]
. In particular, they propose a conjecture
[
2
, Section 7, Conjecture 3]
regarding an infinite family of congruences for
d_{2}(n)
:

Conjecture 1.1

(G.E. Andrews, P. Paule)
.

Let
n,\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
such that
8n\equiv 1\pmod{3^{\alpha}}
. Then
d_{2}(n)\equiv 0\pmod{3^{\alpha}}
.

Andrews and Paule state that this conjecture “seem[s] to be particularly challenging”
[
2
, Section 8]
. In this paper we refine and prove Conjecture
1.1
.

1.2.
A Brief History of Partition Congruence Families

The conjecture above resembles the form of a very large number of similar partition congruence families that have been studied over the last century. The first of such congruence families came from Ramanujan’s groundbreaking work in 1918
[
16
]
. He conjectured that if
\ell\in\{5,7,11\}
and
n,\alpha\geq 1
are positive integers such that
24n\equiv 1\pmod{\ell^{\alpha}}
, then
p(n)\equiv 0\pmod{\ell^{\alpha}}.
This conjecture has since been proved, with a slight modification in the case of
\ell=7
:

(1.2)

\displaystyle\text{If }24n\equiv 1\pmod{5^{\alpha}},

\displaystyle\text{ then }p(n)\equiv 0\pmod{5^{\alpha}},

(1.3)

\displaystyle\text{If }24n\equiv 1\pmod{7^{\alpha}},

\displaystyle\text{ then }p(n)\equiv 0\pmod{7^{\left\lfloor\alpha/2\right\rfloor+1}},

(1.4)

\displaystyle\text{If }24n\equiv 1\pmod{11^{\alpha}},

\displaystyle\text{ then }p(n)\equiv 0\pmod{11^{\alpha}}.

Many similar congruence families have since been found, in a very large variety of more restrictive partition functions. Such congruence families reveal an astonishing depth of arithmetic properties within integer partitions, which do not appear at first sight to have any substantial arithmetic structure.

More surprising still is the enormous variety in the difficulty of the proofs of such congruence families. Many are proved routinely using classical methods. For example, Watson proved (
1.2
) and (
1.3
) in 1938
[
19
]
, and Ramanujan himself appears to have understood the proof of (
1.2
) years earlier
[
5
]
. Many congruence families can be proved with similar techniques used by Ramanujan and Watson.

Other examples are much more arduous. The case of (
1.4
) was not proved until Atkin’s work in 1967
[
3
]
. Even today some contemporary families can take years to resolve. The Andrews–Sellers congruence family for generalized 2-colored Frobenius partitions was proposed in 1994, but it was not proved until 2012
[
13
]
. Conjecture
1.1
appears at first sight to be of the more difficult variety, as Andrews and Paule observe
[
2
, Sections 7, 8]
.

Nevertheless, the opening steps of a proof of a given infinite family of partition congruences are almost always the same: we identify an associated modular curve (usually
\mathrm{X}_{0}(N)
for some
N\geq 2
), and construct a sequence of modular functions
\mathcal{L}:=\left(L_{\alpha}\right)_{\alpha\geq 1}
which enumerate the cases of the congruence family for each power
\alpha
. In the case of Conjecture
1.1
, we have the associated modular curve
\mathrm{X}_{0}(6)
, and the functions
L_{\alpha}
have the form

\displaystyle L_{\alpha}=\Phi_{\alpha}(q)\cdot\sum_{8n\equiv 1\bmod{3^{\alpha}}}d_{2}(n)q^{\left\lfloor n/3^{\alpha}\right\rfloor+1},

with
q=e^{2\pi i\tau}
,
\tau\in\mathbb{H}
and
\Phi_{\alpha}(q)-1\in q\mathbb{Z}[[q]]
(see Section 2).

One then defines a sequence of linear operators (generally an alternating sequence)
U^{(\alpha)}
, which are usually modified forms of the classical operator
U_{\ell}
for the relevant prime
\ell
(in our case,
U_{3}
), and in which
U^{(\alpha)}(L_{\alpha})=L_{\alpha+1}
.

We want to know how to properly represent
L_{\alpha}
in terms of some useful reference functions, and how such functions behave on the application of
U^{(\alpha)}
. Because the reference functions most useful to us are themselves modular functions, they are generally subject to a modular equation. From this we can construct a recurrence relation for the application of
U^{(\alpha)}
on the reference functions, and ultimately an induction proof for the overall congruence family.

This approach is common to all known proofs of infinite congruence families. Indeed, it was understood at least roughly by Ramanujan and Watson. However, in the cases (
1.2
) and (
1.3
) that Ramanujan and Watson proved, the topology of the associated modular curves is very simple. In particular, the genus
\mathfrak{g}
of each curve is 0, and the cusp count
\epsilon_{\infty}
of each curve is 2. This reduces the associated function spaces to single-variable polynomial rings. Thus, a detailed understanding of the underlying modular curve is not necessary in these cases.

Difficulties quickly mount once
\mathfrak{g}
or
\epsilon_{\infty}
grow even slightly. Lehner
[
9
]
,
[
10
]
, and later Atkin
[
3
]
understood that the primary difficulty in the proof of (
1.4
) is that the genus of the associated modular curve is 1. The Weierstrass gap theorem
[
14
]
thus requires a more intricate algebraic structure to represent the analogues to the functions
L_{\alpha}
.

The cusp count further complicates matters. If
\epsilon_{\infty}\left(\mathrm{X}_{0}(N)\right)=2
—as in the case for
\mathrm{X}_{0}(\ell)
with
\ell
a prime—then the functions
L_{\alpha}
would necessarily have a zero at one cusp and a pole at the other
[
8
, Chapter V, Theorem 4F]
. This would ensure that
L_{\alpha}
will have a polynomial representation in terms of a given set of reference functions. It is this representation which would likely hold the arithmetic information of interest to us. This kind of representation is indeed common to (
1.2
), (
1.3
), and even (
1.4
), since the associated modular curve for each of these families has a prime level.

In the case of Conjecture
1.1
, we have
\mathfrak{g}\left(\mathrm{X}_{0}(6)\right)=0
and
\epsilon_{\infty}\left(\mathrm{X}_{0}(6)\right)=4.
Thus the genus simplifies the problem, while the cusp count complicates it. The zero genus means that our space of modular functions meromorphic at a single cusp of the corresponding modular curve will be as simple as in the cases of (
1.2
) and (
1.3
). In particular, the space is isomorphic to
\mathbb{C}[x]
for a Hauptmodul
x
.

However, due to the larger number of cusps,
L_{\alpha}
may have poles at multiple points, and a polynomial expression in terms of a Hauptmodul is far from assured. We therefore need to express each
L_{\alpha}
as a member of a certain space of functions meromorphic at multiple cusps. Importantly, this space must have stability under repeated application of
U^{(\alpha)}
. Atkin appears to have been the first to recognize the importance of organizing modular functions by this property
[
3
]
. However, Atkin worked over
\mathrm{X}_{0}(11)
, with only two cusps, and thus function spaces reducible to polynomial representations for the relevant functions. We need to generalize to a larger number of cusps.

This is a new approach to the problem of congruence families, which to our knowledge has yet to be carefully examined. The conceptual linchpin is that we can push each
L_{\alpha}
into the space of modular functions with a pole at a single cusp of
\mathrm{X}_{0}(6)
. This can be done by multiplying each
L_{\alpha}
by a suitable nonnegative power
n_{\alpha}
of a specific modular function
z
which has a pole at a single cusp, and positive order at all of the other cusps for which
L_{\alpha}
has poles, as well as an integer power series expansion in the Fourier variable
q
, and leading coefficient 1. So long as we are working over classical modular curves, such a modular function is guaranteed to exist
[
15
, Lemma 20]
,
[
12
]
.

Because
z^{n_{\alpha}}
and
z^{n_{\alpha}}L_{\alpha}
are both within the space of modular functions with a pole at a single cusp, they may both be expressed in polynomial form in terms of a given Hauptmodul
x
. Upon division by
z^{n_{\alpha}}
, we have a representation of
L_{\alpha}
not as a polynomial, but rather as a
rational polynomial in

x
. If
x,z
are chosen well, then the coefficients of the numerator may contain the arithmetic information of interest to us. In particular, because
z
has leading coefficient 1, the presence of
z
in the denominator will not impede our efforts to determine divisibility properties which might be glimpsed from the numerator.

As we will see, this allows us to study a large class of modular functions (localized polynomials in a Hauptmodul), with poles at multiple cusps.

1.3.
Main Theorem

Using the approach discussed above, we have proved the following:

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
n,\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
such that
8n\equiv 1\pmod{3^{\alpha}}
. Then
d_{2}(n)\equiv 0\pmod{3^{2\left\lfloor\alpha/2\right\rfloor+1}}
.

Notice that this theorem extends Conjecture
1.1
by accounting for an additional power of 3 when
\alpha
is even.

As an example, we take the first case of Theorem
1.2
. The first function of
\mathcal{L}
has the form

(1.5)

\displaystyle L_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{(q^{3};q^{3})^{7}_{\infty}}{(q^{6};q^{6})^{2}_{\infty}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}d_{2}\left(3n+2\right)q^{n+1}.

With the Hauptmodul

(1.6)

\displaystyle x=x(\tau):=

\displaystyle q\frac{(q^{2};q^{2})_{\infty}(q^{6};q^{6})_{\infty}^{5}}{(q;q)_{\infty}^{5}(q^{3};q^{3})_{\infty}},

we have the following identity:

(1.7)

\displaystyle L_{1}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1+9x}\cdot\left(33x+1392x^{2}+21120x^{3}+138240x^{4}+331776x^{5}\right).

Given that
1+9x\equiv 1\pmod{3}
, the divisibility of the numerator coefficients by 3 yields the information we need.

Such a representation exists for every
L_{\alpha}\in\mathcal{L}
. Indeed, Theorem
1.2
and Conjecture
1.1
immediately arise from the following theorem—the central result of this paper:

Theorem 1.3
.

Let

\displaystyle\psi:=\left\lfloor\frac{3^{\alpha+1}}{8}\right\rfloor,\ \ \ \ \ \beta:=2\left\lfloor\alpha/2\right\rfloor+1.

For all
\alpha\geq 1
,

(1.8)

\displaystyle\frac{(1+9x)^{\psi}}{3^{\beta}}\cdot L_{\alpha}\in\mathbb{Z}[x].

The methods just described were used to prove Theorem
1.3
, and therefore Theorem
1.2
and Conjecture
1.1
, with astonishing rapidity. The author successfully constructed the proof given in this article less than a week after first glimpsing Conjecture
1.1
. We stress this point to emphasize the utility of our localization method for resolving congruence conjectures. We also point out that this technique has already been applied
[
17
]
to prove a congruence family associated with a family of weakly modular forms. Indeed, many of the key steps in the proof given here are similar to the steps specified in the proof of the congruence family in
[
17
]
(though in our current case, the question of proving the associated initial relations is easier than the analogous relations in
[
17
]
). We are compelled to believe that these techniques will prove useful elsewhere in the theory of modular forms and its applications. We hope that other researchers will benefit from our work.

The remainder of our paper is organized as follows: In Section
2
we will briefly review the necessary theory of modular functions. In Section
2.2
we will more precisely define the modular functions
L_{\alpha}
, together with an associated alternating linear operating sequence
U^{(i)}
, with
i\equiv\alpha\pmod{2}
. In Section
2.3
we also define our functions
x,z
, together with their modular equations.

In Section
3
we will define the localized polynomial ring that is most important to us, together with some important subgroups
\mathcal{V}_{n}^{(i)}
. We will also use the modular equations for
x,z
to develop recurrences for
U^{(i)}\left(x^{m}/(1+9x)^{n}\right)
.

In Section
4
we will use these recurrences to study how the operator
U^{(i)}
takes elements from
\mathcal{V}_{n}^{(i)}
and sends them to
\mathcal{V}_{n^{\prime}}^{(i+1)}
for some
n^{\prime}>n
. In particular, we will show that elements from
\mathcal{V}_{n}^{(1)}
will gain two powers of 3 upon being sent to
\mathcal{V}_{3n}^{(0)}
. We then demonstrate that
L_{1}\in\mathcal{V}_{1}^{(1)}
, and complete the proof of Theorem
1.3
.

Section
5
will show how to prove the 18 initial relations necessary for the recurrences of Section
3
. We show that these relations are algebraically dependent on 6 fundamental relations: namely,
U^{(i)}\left(x^{m}\right)
for
i=0,1
, and
0\leq m\leq 2
. These relations can easily be proved, along with some additional properties involving the functions
x
and
z
, using the finiteness principles of the modular cusp analysis.

Appendix I will cover some additional computational details needed in Section
4
. Appendix II will list the 6 fundamental relations from Section
5
. We also provide a Mathematica supplement which will detail many of the other calculations too cumbersome to include here. All of the key computations in this paper can be justified by the algorithms developed by Silviu Radu in
[

2.
Basic Theory

We preface our results with an overview of the theory of modular functions. Given the importance of the topology of the associated Riemann surface to constructing a proof of Theorem
1.3
, we will (briefly!) define the modular curve
\mathrm{X}_{0}(N)
and highlight some of the key topological numbers mentioned in the Introduction. We will also define a modular function for our purposes, some key finiteness conditions imposed by the theory, and some useful tools for computing the order of certain modular functions. We will also define the
U_{3}
operator and describe some of its properties.

Our treatment is necessarily limited for want of space. For a classic review of the theory, see
[
7
]
. For a more modern treatment, see
[
6
]
.

2.1.
Preliminaries

Define
\mathbb{H}
to be the upper half complex plane, and
\hat{\mathbb{H}}:=\mathbb{H}\cup\{\infty\}\cup\mathbb{Q}
. We define
\hat{\mathbb{Q}}:=\mathbb{Q}\cup\{\infty\}
, and for any
a\neq 0
we let
a/0=\infty
. We also define
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
as the set of all
2\times 2
integer matrices with determinant 1.

For any given
N\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
, let

\displaystyle\Gamma_{0}(N)=\Bigg\{\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\in\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z}):N|c\Bigg\}.

We define a group action

\displaystyle\Gamma_{0}(N)\times\hat{\mathbb{H}}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\hat{\mathbb{H}},

\displaystyle\left(\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix},\tau\right)

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\frac{a\tau+b}{c\tau+d}.

If
\gamma=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}
and
\tau\in\hat{\mathbb{H}}
, then we write

\displaystyle\gamma\tau:=\frac{a\tau+b}{c\tau+d}.

The orbits of this action are defined as

\displaystyle[\tau]_{N}:=\left\{\gamma\tau:\gamma\in\Gamma_{0}(N)\right\}.

Definition 2.1
.

For any
N\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
, we define the classical modular curve of level
N
as the set of all orbits of
\Gamma_{0}(N)
applied to
\hat{\mathbb{H}}
:

\displaystyle\mathrm{X}_{0}(N):=\left\{[\tau]_{N}:\tau\in\hat{\mathbb{H}}\right\}

We can restrict the group action to
\hat{\mathbb{Q}}
: for each
\tau\in\hat{\mathbb{Q}}
,
[\tau]_{N}\subseteq\hat{\mathbb{Q}}
. There are only a finite number of such orbits
[
6
, Section 3.8]
.

Definition 2.2
.

For any
N\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
, the cusps of
\mathrm{X}_{0}(N)
are the orbits of
\Gamma_{0}(N)
applied to
\hat{\mathbb{Q}}
.

Two rational elements
a_{1}/c_{1},a_{2}/c_{2}
are in the same cusp if and only if
[
6
, Proposition 3.8.3]
there exist some
j,y\in\mathbb{Z}
with
\mathrm{gcd}(y,N)=1
and

(2.1)

\displaystyle y\cdot a_{2}

\displaystyle\equiv a_{1}+j\cdot c_{1}\pmod{N},

(2.2)

\displaystyle c_{2}

\displaystyle\equiv y\cdot c_{1}\pmod{N}.

A detailed description of the Riemann surface structure of
\mathrm{X}_{0}(N)
is given in
[
6
, Chapters 2,3]
. We will additionally state that
\mathrm{X}_{0}(N)
possesses two unique nonnegative integers of interest to us: the genus, denoted
\mathfrak{g}\left(\mathrm{X}_{0}(N)\right)
, and the number of cusps, denoted
\epsilon_{\infty}\left(\mathrm{X}_{0}(N)\right)
. These are both referenced in the Introduction, and can be computed according to formulae in
[
6
, Chapter 3, Sections 3.1, 3.8]
.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
f:\mathbb{H}\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}
be holomorphic on
\mathbb{H}
. Then
f
is a modular function over
\Gamma_{0}(N)
if the following properties are satisfied for every
\gamma=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
:

(1)

If
\gamma\in\Gamma_{0}(N)
, we have
\displaystyle{f\left(\gamma\tau\right)=f(\tau)}.

(2)

We have

\displaystyle{f\left(\gamma\tau\right)=\sum_{n=n_{\gamma}}^{\infty}\alpha_{\gamma}(n)q^{n\gcd(c^{2},N)/N}},

with
n_{\gamma}\in\mathbb{Z}
, and
\alpha_{\gamma}(n_{\gamma})\neq 0
. If
n_{\gamma}\geq 0
, then
f
is holomorphic at the cusp
[a/c]_{N}
. Otherwise,
f
has a pole of order
n_{\gamma}
, and principal part

(2.3)

\displaystyle\sum_{n=n_{\gamma}}^{-1}\alpha_{\gamma}(n)q^{n\gcd(c^{2},N)/N},

at the cusp
[a/c]_{N}
.

We refer to
\mathrm{ord}_{a/c}^{(N)}(f):=n_{\gamma}(f)
as the order of
f
at the cusp
[a/c]_{N}
.

We now define the relevant sets of all modular functions:

Definition 2.4
.

Let
\mathcal{M}\left(\Gamma_{0}(N)\right)
be the set of all modular functions over
\Gamma_{0}(N)
, and
\mathcal{M}^{a/c}\left(\Gamma_{0}(N)\right)\subset\mathcal{M}\left(\Gamma_{0}(N)\right)
to be those modular functions over
\Gamma_{0}(N)
with a pole only at the cusp
[a/c]_{N}
. These are commutative algebras with 1, and standard addition and multiplication
[
15
, Section 2.1]
.

Due to its precise symmetry over
\Gamma_{0}(N)
, any modular function
f\in\mathcal{M}\left(\Gamma_{0}(N)\right)
induces a well-defined function

\displaystyle\hat{f}

\displaystyle:\mathrm{X}_{0}(N)\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}\cup\{\infty\}

\displaystyle:[\tau]_{N}\longrightarrow f(\tau).

The notions of pole order and cusps of
f
used in Definition
2.3
are constructed so as to coincide with these notions applied to
\hat{f}
on
\mathrm{X}_{0}(N)
. Indeed, the set of meromorphic functions on
\mathrm{X}_{0}(N)
with poles only at the cusps has a one-to-one correspondence with
\mathcal{M}\left(\Gamma_{0}(N)\right)

[
8
, Chapter VI, Theorem 4A]
. In particular, (
2.3
) represents the principal part of
\hat{f}
in local coordinates near the cusp
[a/c]_{N}
. Notice that as
\tau\rightarrow i\infty
, we must have
\gamma\tau\rightarrow a/c
, and
q\rightarrow 0
.

Because
f
is holomorphic on
\mathbb{H}
by definition, all possible poles for
\hat{f}
must be found for
[\tau]_{N}\subseteq\hat{\mathbb{Q}}
.

Theorem 2.5
.

Let
\mathrm{X}
be a compact Riemann surface, and let
\hat{f}:\mathrm{X}\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}
be analytic on all of
\mathrm{X}
. Then
\hat{f}
must be a constant function.

This theorem is central to our work, as can be shown in the following corollary:

Corollary 2.6
.

For a given
N\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
, if
f\in\mathcal{M}\left(\Gamma_{0}(N)\right)
has positive order at every cusp of
\Gamma_{0}(N)
, then
f
must be a constant.

To illustrate the importance of these results, we consider
f,g\in\mathcal{M}^{\infty}\left(\Gamma_{0}(N)\right)
.

Suppose that the principal parts of each function match. In that case,
f-g\in\mathcal{M}\left(\Gamma_{0}(N)\right)
will have no poles whatsoever, and will thus be analytic on the whole of
\mathrm{X}_{0}(N)
. Theorem
2.5
forces
\hat{f}-\hat{g}
, and therefore
f-g
, to be a constant. Of course, we can quickly compare the constant terms of both functions.

We now want to show how to construct modular functions. Most useful to us in this case is Dedekind’s eta function
[
7
, Chapter 3]
:

\displaystyle\eta(\tau):=e^{\pi i\tau/12}\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(1-e^{2\pi in\tau}\right).

The following theorem is due to Newman
[
12
, Theorem 1]
:

Theorem 2.7
.

Let
f=\prod_{\delta|N}\eta(\delta\tau)^{r_{\delta}}
, with
\hat{r}=(r_{\delta})_{\delta|N}
an integer-valued vector, for some
N\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
. Then
f\in\mathcal{M}\left(\Gamma_{0}(N)\right)
if and only if the following apply:

(1)

\sum_{\delta|N}r_{\delta}=0;

(2)

\sum_{\delta|N}\delta r_{\delta}\equiv 0\pmod{24};

(3)

\sum_{\delta|N}\frac{N}{\delta}r_{\delta}\equiv 0\pmod{24};

(4)

\prod_{\delta|N}\delta^{|r_{\delta}|}
is a perfect square.

We now want to be able to examine the order of a given eta quotient at some cusp of the corresponding modular curve. The following theorem can be found in
[
15
, Theorem 23]
, though it is attributed to Ligozat:

Theorem 2.8
.

If
f=\prod_{\delta|N}\eta(\delta\tau)^{r_{\delta}}\in\mathcal{M}\left(\Gamma_{0}(N)\right)
, then the order of
f
at the cusp
[a/c]_{N}
is given by the following:

\displaystyle\mathrm{ord}_{a/c}^{(N)}(f)=\frac{N}{24\gcd{(c^{2},N)}}\sum_{\delta|N}r_{\delta}\frac{\gcd{(c,\delta)}^{2}}{\delta}.

Finally, we give a theorem from
[
15
, Theorem 39]
which we will use to identify our function
z
.

Theorem 2.9
.

Suppose that for some
M\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
and an index-valued vector
(r_{\delta})_{\delta|M}
we define the function
a(n)
by

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a(n)q^{n}:=\prod_{\delta|M}(q^{\delta};q^{\delta})_{\infty}^{r_{\delta}}

Moreover, suppose that
m,t,N\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
such that
0\leq t<m
, and that

\displaystyle f=\prod_{\lambda|N}\eta(\lambda\tau)^{s_{\delta}}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a(mn+t)q^{n}\in\mathcal{M}\left(\Gamma_{0}(N)\right).

\displaystyle\geq\left\lfloor\frac{3r+10}{4}\right\rfloor-\left\lfloor\frac{3r+1}{4}\right\rfloor

(3.35)

\displaystyle\geq 2.

For
1\leq m\leq 3
,
r\geq 2
and
r-l\leq 1
, we have
l\geq r-1
, and therefore

(3.36)

\displaystyle\pi_{1}(m,r-l)+

\displaystyle\phi(l)-\pi_{1}(m,r)

(3.37)

\displaystyle\geq 0+\left\lfloor\frac{3l+12}{4}\right\rfloor-\left\lfloor\frac{3r+1}{4}\right\rfloor

(3.38)

\displaystyle\geq\left\lfloor\frac{3r+9}{4}\right\rfloor-\left\lfloor\frac{3r+1}{4}\right\rfloor

(3.39)

\displaystyle\geq 2

For
1\leq m\leq 3
,
r\leq 2
, we have

(3.40)

\displaystyle\pi_{1}(m,r-l)+

\displaystyle\phi(l)-\pi_{1}(m,r)=\phi(l)\geq 2.

We now state the key theorem on the behavior of
U^{(i)}
on elements of
\mathcal{V}^{(0)}_{n}
.

Theorem 3.4
.

There exist discrete arrays
h_{1},h_{0}:\mathbb{Z}^{3}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}
such that

\displaystyle U^{(1)}

\displaystyle\left(\frac{x^{m}}{(1+9x)^{n}}\right)=\frac{1}{(1+9x)^{3n}}\sum_{r\geq\left\lceil m/3\right\rceil}h_{1}(m,n,r)\cdot 3^{\pi_{1}(m,r)}\cdot x^{r},

\displaystyle U^{(0)}

\displaystyle\left(\frac{x^{m}}{(1+9x)^{n}}\right)=\frac{1}{(1+9x)^{3n+1}}\sum_{r\geq\left\lceil(m+1)/3\right\rceil}h_{0}(m,n,r)\cdot 3^{\pi_{0}(m,r)}\cdot x^{r}.

We prove this theorem by first demonstrating the following two lemmas. Because the proofs work identically whether we apply
U^{(1)}
or
U^{(0)}
, we denote the differing denominator powers as
3n+\kappa
, lower bounds for
r
by
\left\lceil(m+\delta)/3\right\rceil
, and powers of 3 by
\left\lfloor\frac{3r-m+\mu}{4}\right\rfloor
. We define
\kappa=1,0
,
\delta=1,0
,
\mu=-4,1
depending on whether
i=0,1
.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
\kappa,\delta,\mu\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
be fixed, and fix
i
to either 0 or 1. If there exists a discrete array
h_{i}
such that

(3.41)

\displaystyle U^{(i)}\left(\frac{x^{m}}{(1+9x)^{n}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(1+9x)^{3n+\kappa}}\sum_{r\geq\left\lceil(m+\delta)/3\right\rceil}h_{i}(m,n,r)\cdot 3^{\left\lfloor\frac{3r-m+\mu}{4}\right\rfloor}\cdot x^{r}

for
1\leq m\leq 3
,
1\leq n\leq 3
, then such a relation can be made to hold for all
m\geq 1
,
n\geq 1
.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
\kappa,\delta\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
and
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
be fixed, and fix
i
to either 0 or 1. If there exists a discrete array
h_{i}
such that

(3.42)

\displaystyle U^{(i)}\left(\frac{x^{m}}{(1+9x)^{n}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(1+9x)^{3n+\kappa}}\sum_{r\geq\left\lceil(m+\delta)/3\right\rceil}h_{i}(m,n,r)\cdot 3^{\pi_{i}(m,r)}\cdot x^{r}

for
1\leq n\leq 3
, then such a relation can be made to hold for all
n\geq 1
.

The latter lemma has the advantage of allowing us to more closely study the arithmetic properties of the coefficients
h_{i}(m,n,r)
.

Proof of Lemma
3.5
.

We use induction. Suppose that our relation holds for all positive integers strictly less than some
m_{0},n_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 6}
. We show that the relation will hold for
m_{0}
and
n_{0}
. We have

\displaystyle U^{(i)}\left(\frac{x^{m_{0}}}{(1+9x)^{n_{0}}}\right)

(3.43)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{(1+9x)^{3}}

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{3}a_{j}(\tau)b_{k}(\tau)\cdot U^{(i)}\left(\frac{x^{m_{0}+j-3}}{(1+9x)^{n_{0}-k}}\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{(1+9x)^{3}}\sum_{j=0}^{2}

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{3}\frac{a_{j}(\tau)b_{k}(\tau)}{(1+9x)^{3(n_{0}-k)+\kappa}}

(3.44)

\displaystyle\times\sum_{r\geq\left\lceil(m_{0}+j-3+\delta)/3\right\rceil}

\displaystyle h_{i}(m_{0}+j-3,n_{0}-k,r)\cdot 3^{\left\lfloor\frac{3r-(m_{0}+j-3)+\mu}{4}\right\rfloor}\cdot x^{r}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(1+9x)^{3n_{0}+\kappa}}

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{3}w(j,k)

(3.45)

\displaystyle\times\sum_{r\geq\left\lceil(m_{0}+j-3+\delta)/3\right\rceil}

\displaystyle h_{i}(m_{0}+j-3,n_{0}-k,r)\cdot 3^{\left\lfloor\frac{3r-(m_{0}+j-3)+\mu}{4}\right\rfloor}\cdot x^{r},

with

\displaystyle w(j,k):=

\displaystyle-a_{j}(\tau)b_{k}(\tau)(1+9x)^{3(k-1)}

(3.46)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{l=1}^{12}v(j,k,l)\cdot 3^{\left\lfloor\frac{3l+j}{4}\right\rfloor}\cdot x^{l}.

This can be demonstrated by an expansion of
a_{j}(\tau)b_{k}(\tau)(1+9x)^{3(k-1)}
which is detailed in the Mathematica supplement. We have

\displaystyle U^{(i)}\left(\frac{x^{m_{0}}}{(1+9x)^{n_{0}}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(1+9x)^{3n_{0}+\kappa}}\sum_{j=0}^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{3}\sum_{l=1}^{12}\sum_{r\geq\left\lceil(m_{0}+j-3+\delta)/3\right\rceil}

(3.47)

\displaystyle v(j,k)\cdot h_{i}(m_{0}+j-3,n_{0}-k,r)\cdot 3^{\left\lfloor\frac{3r-(m_{0}+j-3)+\mu}{4}\right\rfloor+\left\lfloor\frac{3l+j}{4}\right\rfloor}\cdot x^{r+l}.

Notice that for any
M,N\in\mathbb{Z}
,

\displaystyle\left\lfloor\frac{M}{4}\right\rfloor+\left\lfloor\frac{N}{4}\right\rfloor\geq\left\lfloor\frac{M+N-3}{4}\right\rfloor.

Because of this,

(3.48)

\displaystyle\left\lfloor\frac{3r-(m_{0}+j-3)+\mu}{4}\right\rfloor+\left\lfloor\frac{3l+j}{4}\right\rfloor\geq\left\lfloor\frac{3(r+l)-m_{0}+\mu}{4}\right\rfloor.

And because

(3.49)

\displaystyle r+l

\displaystyle\geq\left\lceil\frac{m_{0}+j-3+\delta}{3}\right\rceil+l

(3.50)

\displaystyle\geq\left\lceil\frac{m_{0}+\delta}{3}-\frac{3-j}{3}\right\rceil+l

(3.51)

\displaystyle\geq\left\lceil\frac{m_{0}+\delta}{3}\right\rceil-1+l

(3.52)

\displaystyle\geq\left\lceil\frac{m_{0}+\delta}{3}\right\rceil,

we can relabel our powers of
x
so that

\displaystyle U^{(i)}\left(\frac{x^{m_{0}}}{(1+9x)^{n_{0}}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(1+9x)^{3n_{0}+\kappa}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq j\leq 2,\\
1\leq k\leq 3,\\
1\leq l\leq 12\end{subarray}}\sum_{r\geq\left\lceil\frac{m_{0}+\delta}{3}\right\rceil}

(3.53)

\displaystyle v(j,k)\cdot h_{i}(m_{0}+j-3,n_{0}-k,r-l)\cdot 3^{\left\lfloor\frac{3r-(m_{0}+j-3)+\mu}{4}\right\rfloor+\left\lfloor\frac{3l+j}{4}\right\rfloor}\cdot x^{r}.

If we define the discrete array
H_{i}
by

(3.54)

\displaystyle H_{i}(m,n,r):=\begin{cases}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq j\leq 2,\\
1\leq k\leq 3,\\
1\leq l\leq 12\end{subarray}}&\sum_{r\geq\left\lceil\frac{m+\delta}{3}\right\rceil-1+l}\hat{H}(i,j,k,l,r),\ r\geq l\\
0,\ r<l\end{cases}

with

\displaystyle\hat{H}(i,j,k,l,r):=v(j,k)\cdot h_{i}(m+j-3,n-k,r-l)\cdot 3^{\epsilon(i,j,l,m,r)},

\displaystyle\epsilon(i,j,l,m,r):=\left\lfloor\frac{3(r-l)-(m+j-3)+\mu}{4}\right\rfloor+\left\lfloor\frac{3l+j}{4}\right\rfloor-\left\lfloor\frac{3r-m_{0}+\mu}{4}\right\rfloor,

then

\displaystyle U^{(i)}\left(\frac{x^{m_{0}}}{(1+9x)^{n_{0}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(1+9x)^{3n_{0}+\kappa}}

(3.55)

\displaystyle\times\sum_{r\geq\left\lceil\frac{m_{0}+\delta}{3}\right\rceil}H_{i}(m_{0},n_{0},r)\cdot 3^{\left\lfloor\frac{3r-m_{0}+\mu}{4}\right\rfloor}\cdot x^{r}.

∎

Proof of Lemma
3.6
.

We begin in a manner analogous to that of the previous lemma. However, we only want a recursion for the denominator of each term; we fix
m
.

\displaystyle U^{(i)}

\displaystyle\left(\frac{x^{m}}{(1+9x)^{n}}\right)

(3.56)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{(1+9x)^{3}}\sum_{k=1}^{3}b_{k}(\tau)\cdot U^{(i)}\left(\frac{x^{m}}{(1+9x)^{n-k}}\right)

(3.57)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{(1+9x)^{3}}\sum_{k=1}^{3}\frac{b_{k}(\tau)}{(1+9x)^{3(n-k)-\kappa}}\sum_{r\geq 1}h_{i}(m,n-k,r)\cdot 3^{\pi_{i}(m,r)}\cdot x^{r}

(3.58)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(1+9x)^{3n+\kappa}}\sum_{k=1}^{3}\hat{w}(k)\sum_{r\geq 1}h_{i}(m,n-k,r)\cdot 3^{\pi_{i}(m,r)}\cdot x^{r},

with

\displaystyle\hat{w}(k):

\displaystyle=-b_{k}(\tau)(1+9x)^{3(k-1)}

(3.59)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}&\displaystyle{\sum_{l=0}^{6}\hat{v}(k,l)\cdot 3^{\phi(l)}\cdot x^{l}},\ k<3\\
&\displaystyle{1+\sum_{l=1}^{6}\hat{v}(3,l)\cdot 3^{\phi(l)}\cdot x^{l}},\ k=3.\end{cases}

Similar to
w(i,j)
above, this is a straightforward expansion of
\hat{w}(k)
that we detail in our Mathematica supplement. Expanding, we have

\displaystyle U^{(i)}

\displaystyle\left(\frac{x^{m}}{(1+9x)^{n}}\right)=\frac{1}{(1+9x)^{3n+\kappa}}

(3.60)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\Bigg(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq k\leq 2,\\
0\leq l\leq 6,\\
r\geq\left\lceil\frac{m+\delta}{3}\right\rceil\end{subarray}}\hat{v}(k,l)\cdot h_{i}(m,n-k,r)\cdot 3^{\pi_{i}(m,r)+\phi(l)}\cdot x^{r+l}

(3.61)

\displaystyle+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq l\leq 6,\\

(3.62)

\displaystyle+\sum_{r\geq\left\lceil\frac{m+\delta}{3}\right\rceil}h_{i}(m,n-3,r)\cdot 3^{\pi_{i}(m,r)}\cdot x^{r}\Bigg).

With a change of index, we have

(3.63)

\displaystyle U^{(i)}\left(\frac{x^{m}}{(1+9x)^{n}}\right)=\frac{1}{(1+9x)^{3n+\kappa}}\times

\displaystyle\Bigg(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq k\leq 2,\\
0\leq l\leq 6,\\
r\geq l+\left\lceil\frac{m+\delta}{3}\right\rceil\end{subarray}}\hat{v}(k,l)\cdot h_{i}(m,n-k,r-l)\cdot 3^{\pi_{i}(m,r-l)+\phi(l)}\cdot x^{r}

(3.64)

\displaystyle+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq l\leq 6,\\
r\geq l+\left\lceil\frac{m+\delta}{3}\right\rceil\end{subarray}}\hat{v}(3,l)\cdot h_{i}(m,n-3,r-l)\cdot 3^{\pi_{i}(m,r-l)+\phi(l)}\cdot x^{r}

(3.65)

\displaystyle+\sum_{r\geq\left\lceil\frac{m+\delta}{3}\right\rceil}h_{i}(m,n-3,r)\cdot 3^{\pi_{i}(m,r)}\cdot x^{r}\Bigg).

To finish the proof, we note from Lemma

(3.66)

\displaystyle=U^{(i)}\left(\frac{x^{m}}{(1+9x)^{n-3}}\right).

We rearrange the sum and define a discrete array in the same manner that we defined (
3.54

∎

\displaystyle\equiv h_{i}(m,n-3,r)\pmod{3}.

In particular, for all

\displaystyle h_{1}(m,1,2)

\displaystyle\equiv 0\pmod{3},

(3.69)

\displaystyle h_{0}(m,3,4)

\displaystyle\equiv 0\pmod{3},.

Finally,

\displaystyle h_{1}(m,1,4)

\displaystyle\equiv 0\pmod{3},

Proof.

We reexamine (

3.64

3.65

Corollary 3.8
.

For all
m,r\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
1\leq m\leq 3r
,
1\leq r\leq 5
, and
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
we have:

(3.71)

\displaystyle h_{1}(m,3n+1,r)

\displaystyle\equiv h_{1}(m,1,r)\pmod{9}.

Moreover, for all
r,w\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
1\leq r\leq 3w-1
,
1\leq w\leq 3
,

(3.72)

\displaystyle h_{0}(m,3n,r)

\displaystyle\equiv h_{0}(m,3,r)\pmod{9}.

In particular,

(3.73)

\displaystyle h_{1}(m,3n+1,1)\equiv 0\pmod{9}.

Proof.

Notice that
\pi_{i}(m,r-l)+\phi(l)-\pi_{i}(m,r)\geq 2
except when
l=0
. On the other hand,
\hat{v}(1,0)=1
and
\hat{v}(2,0)=-1
. We can therefore reduce (
3.63
), (
3.64
), (
3.65
) modulo 9 and examine the coefficient of

(3.74)

\displaystyle h_{i}(m,n,r)\equiv

\displaystyle 3\left(h_{i}(m,n-1,r)-h_{i}(m,n-2,r)\right)+h_{i}(m,n-3,r)\pmod{9}.

For
1\leq m\leq 3r
,

\displaystyle h_{1}(m,1,r)\equiv h_{1}(m,2,r)\equiv h_{1}(m,3,r)\pmod{3}.

Similarly, for

,

\displaystyle h_{0}(m,1,r)\equiv h_{0}(m,2,r)\equiv h_{0}(m,3,r)\pmod{3}.

This ensures that

. Combining this with (

(3.75)

\displaystyle h_{i}(m,n,r)\equiv

\displaystyle h_{i}(m,n-3,r)\pmod{9}.

This of course permits us to reduce

to

, and

4.
Main Theorem

We can now work towards the proof of Theorem
1.3
. We start with the simpler question of how to elements of
\mathcal{V}_{n}^{(0)}
respond under
U^{(0)}
:

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{V}_{n}^{(0)}
. Then

(4.1)

\displaystyle U^{(0)}\left(f\right)\in\mathcal{V}_{3n+1}^{(0)}.

Proof.

Let
f\in\mathcal{V}_{n}^{(0)}
. Then we can express
f
as

\displaystyle f=\frac{1}{(1+9x)^{n}}\sum_{m\geq 1}s(m)\cdot 3^{\theta(m)}\cdot x^{m}.

We write

(4.2)

\displaystyle U^{(0)}\left(f\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m\geq 1}s(m)\cdot 3^{\theta(m)}\cdot U^{(0)}\left(\frac{x^{m}}{(1+9x)^{n}}\right)

(4.3)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(1+9x)^{3n+1}}\sum_{m\geq 1}\sum_{r\geq\left\lceil(m+1)/3\right\rceil}s(m)\cdot h_{0}(m,n,r)3^{\theta(m)+\pi_{0}(m,r)}\cdot x^{r}

(4.4)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(1+9x)^{3n+1}}\sum_{r\geq 1}\sum_{m\geq 1}s(m)\cdot h_{0}(m,n,r)3^{\theta(m)+\pi_{0}(m,r)}\cdot x^{r}.

We want to show that

(4.5)

\displaystyle\theta(m)+\pi_{0}(m,r)-\theta(r)\geq 0.

For
1\leq m\leq 6
and
1\leq r\leq 6
, it is a simple calculation. Some exceptions to (
4.5
) occur, but they can be handled with relative ease. See Appendix I.

We consider the remaining cases below:

4.1.
m=1,2,3
,
r\geq 7

\displaystyle\theta(m)+\pi_{0}(m,r)-\theta(r)

\displaystyle=\left\lfloor\frac{3r-m}{4}\right\rfloor-1-\left(\left\lfloor\frac{3r-3}{4}\right\rfloor-1\right)

\displaystyle=\left\lfloor\frac{3r-m}{4}\right\rfloor-\left\lfloor\frac{3r-3}{4}\right\rfloor

\displaystyle\geq 0.

4.2.
m=4,5,6
,
r\geq 7

\displaystyle\theta(m)+\pi_{0}(m,r)-\theta(r)

\displaystyle=\left\lfloor\frac{3r-m}{4}\right\rfloor-1+2-\left(\left\lfloor\frac{3r-3}{4}\right\rfloor-1\right)

\displaystyle\geq\left\lfloor\frac{3r-6}{4}\right\rfloor-\left\lfloor\frac{3r-3}{4}\right\rfloor+2

\displaystyle=\left\lfloor\frac{3r-2}{4}\right\rfloor-\left\lfloor\frac{3r-3}{4}\right\rfloor+1

\displaystyle\geq 0.

4.3.
m\geq 7
,
r\geq 7

For
r\geq 7
, we have:

\displaystyle\theta(m)+\pi_{0}(m,r)-\theta(r)

\displaystyle=\left\lfloor\frac{3r-m-4}{4}\right\rfloor+\left\lfloor\frac{3m-3}{4}\right\rfloor-1-\left(\left\lfloor\frac{3r-3}{4}\right\rfloor-1\right)

\displaystyle\geq\left\lfloor\frac{3r+2m-10}{4}\right\rfloor-\left\lfloor\frac{3r-3}{4}\right\rfloor

\displaystyle=\left\lfloor\frac{3r-3}{4}\right\rfloor+\left\lfloor\frac{2m-7}{4}\right\rfloor-\left\lfloor\frac{3r-3}{4}\right\rfloor

\displaystyle\geq\left\lfloor\frac{7}{4}\right\rfloor\geq 0.

4.4.
m\geq 7
,
r\leq 6

We note that
r\geq\left\lfloor(m+1)/3\right\rfloor\geq 3
. We have

\displaystyle\theta(m)+\pi_{0}(m,r)-\theta(r)

\displaystyle=\left\lfloor\frac{3r-m-4}{4}\right\rfloor+\left\lfloor\frac{3m-3}{4}\right\rfloor-1-\theta(r)

\displaystyle\geq\left\lfloor\frac{3r+2m-10}{4}\right\rfloor-1-\theta(r)

\displaystyle\geq\left\lfloor\frac{-1+2m}{4}\right\rfloor-3

\displaystyle\geq\left\lfloor\frac{-1+14}{4}\right\rfloor-3

\displaystyle\geq 0.

∎

We now study the analogous question of how elements of
\mathcal{V}_{n}^{(1)}
respond under
U^{(1)}
:

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{V}_{n}^{(1)}
with

(4.6)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{9}U^{(1)}\left(f\right)

\displaystyle f

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(1+9x)^{n}}\sum_{m\geq 1}s(m)\cdot 3^{\theta(m)}\cdot x^{m}.

\displaystyle U^{(1)}\left(f\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(1+9x)^{3n}}\sum_{m\geq 1}\sum_{r\geq\left\lceil m/3\right\rceil}s(m)\cdot h_{1}(m,n,r)\cdot 3^{\theta(m)+\pi_{1}(m,r)}\cdot x^{r}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(1+9x)^{3n}}\sum_{r\geq 1}\sum_{m\geq 1}s(m)\cdot h_{1}(m,n,r)\cdot 3^{\theta(m)+\pi_{1}(m,r)}\cdot x^{r}

We want to show that

(4.7)

\displaystyle\theta(m)+\pi_{1}(m,r)-\theta(r)-2\geq 0.

For
1\leq m\leq 6
and
2\leq r\leq 6
, it is a simple calculation. In fact, exceptions to (

We handle the remaining cases as follows:

4.5.
m=1,2,3
,
r\geq 7

\displaystyle=\left\lfloor\frac{3r+1}{4}\right\rfloor-\left(\left\lfloor\frac{3r-3}{4}\right\rfloor-1\right)-2

\displaystyle\geq 0.

4.6.
m=4,5,6
,

\displaystyle=\left\lfloor\frac{3r-1}{4}\right\rfloor-\left\lfloor\frac{3r-3}{4}\right\rfloor

\displaystyle\geq 0.

4.7.
m\geq 7
,
3\leq r\leq 6

For

\displaystyle\theta(m)+\pi_{1}(m,r)-\theta(r)-2
