Stability of normal quantum-fluid mixtures


Abstract
Mixtures of quantum fluids, that is gases or liquids, are considered with the emphasis on the conditions characterizing the stability of the mixtures. The mixtures, that can be formed by cold atoms or molecules, are assumed to be quantum requiring the description using quantum techniques, but not so cold that to exhibit superfluidity or superconductivity. Reviewing the stability conditions of such normal quantum systems is important for the comparison of these conditions with the stability conditions of, e.g., Bose-condensed mixtures. The behavior of observable quantities under the stratification of quantum mixtures is discussed.

due to the developed techniques of cooling and trapping

.
The major interest has been paid to the study of Bose systems in the condensed
state, which several books
[

and review articles

,

]
are devoted to. Of particular
interest are the mixtures of quantum superfluids, Bose-Bose mixtures
[

]
,
Bose-Fermi mixtures
[
18
]
, and Fermi-Fermi superfluid mixtures

[
19
]
.

One of the main points in considering quantum mixtures is the problem of their
stability with respect to their stratification into separate components occupying
different spatial locations. This type of stability, for brevity, is called

mixture stability
. The mixture stability of superfluid Bose-Bose mixtures
is usually analyzed in the coherent approximation
[
20
,
21
]

or Bogolubov approximation
[
22
]
. The Hugenholtz-Pines relation

[
23
]
for superfluid systems can be generalized for superfluid
mixtures
[
24
,
25
]
. The stability of superfluid mixture
in the Hartree-Fock-Bogolubov approximation has been recently analyzed

[
26
]
.

The mixture stability for normal (not superfluid) quantum fluids has been considered
to a much lesser extent. The aim of the present paper is to compensate this
deficiency and to survey what is known on the stability conditions of normal quantum
mixtures.

The principal difference in the study of mixture stability for superfluid and normal
fluids is in the following. The single-particle spectrum of a superfluid, where the
gauge symmetry is broken, coincides with the spectrum of collective excitations

[
27
,
28
]
. Therefore, to investigate the dynamic stability of a
superfluid spectrum it is sufficient to consider the poles of the single-particle
Green function. Contrary to this, for a normal quantum system, where the gauge symmetry
is preserved, it is necessary to study the poles of the two-particle Green function or
of the response function (dynamic susceptibility). For a normal system, the
single-particle Green function does not define the spectrum of collective excitations.

It is interesting and important to compare the conditions of mixture stability for
superfluid and normal quantum systems. Also, it is useful to compare dynamic and
thermodynamic stability
[
29
,
30
]
. Dynamic stability requires that
the spectrum of collective excitations be positive for finite momenta. And
thermodynamic stability studies conditions where the thermodynamic potential of a
mixture is minimal.

We consider the systems composed of neutral atoms or molecules, whose density
fluctuations enjoy sound modes. The particles can be bosons or fermions, provided
that sound modes are well defined for them. The instability of a mixture with respect
to the spatial separation of the components can be caused by the interspecies
interactions. The instability can arise when the repulsive interspecies interactions
become sufficiently strong. The derivation of conditions characterizing the mixture
stability of normal quantum-fluid mixtures is the main topic of the present survey.

Throughout the paper, the system of units is employed where the Planck and Boltzmann
constants are set to one.

2
Quantum-fluid mixture

Let us start with the analysis of dynamic stability of a quantum-fluid mixture.
We consider particles without internal degrees of freedom, such as spin. In the case
of particles with spin, this could imply that their spins are frozen in one direction
and thus are switched off the particle dynamics.

The grand Hamiltonian of the mixture of several components, enumerated by the
index
i
, has the form

H=\sum_{i}\int\psi_{i}^{\dagger}({\bf r})\;\left(-\;\frac{\nabla^{2}}{2m}+U_{i}-\mu_{i}\right)\;\psi_{i}({\bf r})\;d{\bf r}\;+

+\;\frac{1}{2}\sum_{ij}\psi_{i}^{\dagger}({\bf r})\;\psi_{j}^{\dagger}({\bf r}^{\prime})\;\Phi_{ij}({\bf r}-{\bf r}^{\prime})\;\psi_{j}({\bf r}^{\prime})\;\psi_{i}({\bf r})\;d{\bf r}d{\bf r}^{\prime}\;,

(1)

where
\psi_{i}({\bf r})=\psi_{i}({\bf r},t)
are field operators (either Bose
or Fermi),
U_{i}=U_{i}({\bf r},t)
is an external potential,
\mu_{i}
are chemical
potentials, and
\Phi_{ij}({\bf r})
is an interaction potential.

The response functions are the variational derivatives

\chi_{ij}({\bf r},t,{\bf r}^{\prime},t^{\prime})=\frac{\delta\rho_{i}({\bf r},t)}{\delta U_{j}({\bf r}^{\prime},t^{\prime})}

(2)

of the density

\rho_{i}({\bf r},t)=\langle\;\psi_{i}^{\dagger}({\bf r},t)\;\psi_{i}({\bf r},t)\;\rangle=\pm iG_{i}({\bf r},t,{\bf r},t)\;,

(3)

where we assume the causal Green functions

G_{i}({\bf r},t,{\bf r}^{\prime},t^{\prime})=-i\;\langle\;\hat{T}\psi_{i}({\bf r},t)\;\psi_{i}^{\dagger}({\bf r}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\;\rangle\;.

(4)

The Green functions at coinciding times are defined as

G_{i}({\bf r},t,{\bf r}^{\prime},t)=\lim_{t^{\prime}\rightarrow t+0}\;G_{i}({\bf r},t,{\bf r}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\;.

(5)

Some interaction potentials are known to be non-integrable and not allowing
for their Fourier transformation. This obstacle can be overpassed by accepting
an effective interaction potential smoothed by the pair correlation function

[
31
]
. It has been shown
[
7
,
32
,
33
]

that, starting from such a correlated approximation, it is possible to formulate
a self-consistent iterative theory of all orders. Keeping this in mind, we
assume that the interaction potential is integrable and allows for the Fourier
transformation

\Phi_{ij}({\bf r})=\int\overline{\Phi}_{ij}({\bf k})\;e^{i{\bf k}\cdot{\bf r}}\;\frac{d{\bf k}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\;,\qquad\overline{\Phi}_{ij}({\bf k})=\int\Phi_{ij}({\bf r})\;e^{-i{\bf k}\cdot{\bf r}}\;d{\bf r}\;,

(6)

with the Fourier transform (
6
) existing for all
k
, including
k=0
.

Considering a uniform system in volume
V
, we have the constant densities of
the components

\rho_{j}\equiv\frac{N_{j}}{V}\qquad\left(N=\sum_{j}N_{j}\right)\;,

(7)

hence the constant total density

\rho\equiv\frac{N}{V}=\sum_{j}\rho_{j}\;.

(8)

Assuming that the single-particle spectrum is real, for the Fourier transform
of the Green function (
4
) we have

G_{i}({\bf k},\omega)=\frac{1\pm n_{i}({\bf k})}{\omega-\omega_{i}({\bf k})+i0}\;\mp\;\frac{n_{i}({\bf k})}{\omega-\omega_{i}({\bf k})-i0}\;,

(9)

with the single-particle spectrum given by the equation

\omega_{i}({\bf k})=\frac{k^{2}}{2m}+\Sigma_{i}({\bf k},\omega_{i})-\mu_{i}\;,

(10)

where
\Sigma_{i}({\bf k},\omega)
is a self-energy and the momentum distribution
is

n_{i}({\bf k})=\frac{1}{\exp\{\beta\omega_{i}({\bf k})\}\mp 1}\qquad(\beta T=1)\;,

(11)

with
T
being temperature. Here the upper sign is for Bose-Einstein statistics,
while the lower sign, for Fermi-Dirac statistics. The chemical potentials are
defined by the normalization to the density

\rho_{i}=\int n_{i}({\bf k})\;\frac{d{\bf k}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\;.

(12)

3
Random-phase approximation

The response functions are usually calculated in the random-phase approximation

[
34
]
with the self-energy taken in the Hartree form

\Sigma_{i}({\bf k},\omega)=\sum_{j}\Phi_{ij}\;\rho_{j}\;,

(13)

where

\Phi_{ij}\equiv\int\Phi_{ij}({\bf r})\;d{\bf r}=\overline{\Phi}_{ij}(0)\;.

(14)

Then the Fourier transforms of the mixture response functions satisfy the
equations

\chi_{ij}({\bf k},\omega)=\Pi_{ij}({\bf k},\omega)+\Pi_{ii}({\bf k},\omega)\sum_{n}\overline{\Phi}_{in}({\bf k})\;\chi_{nj}({\bf k},\omega)\;,

(15)

with the polarization functions

\Pi_{ij}({\bf k},\omega)=\pm i\delta_{ij}\int G_{i}({\bf k}+{\bf k}^{\prime},\omega+\omega^{\prime})\;G_{i}({\bf k}^{\prime},\omega^{\prime})\;\frac{d{\bf k}^{\prime}d\omega^{\prime}}{(2\pi)^{4}}\;.

(16)

For the Green function (
9
), we get

\Pi_{ii}({\bf k},\omega)=\frac{k^{2}}{m_{i}}\int\frac{n_{i}({\bf k}^{\prime})}{(\omega-{\bf k}\cdot{\bf k}^{\prime}/m_{i})^{2}-(k^{2}/2m_{i})^{2}}\;\frac{d{\bf k}^{\prime}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\;.

(17)

In the case of two components, the solutions to these equations are

\chi_{11}=\frac{\Pi_{11}(1-\Pi_{22}\overline{\Phi}_{22})}{(1-\Pi_{11}\overline{\Phi}_{11})(1-\Pi_{22}\overline{\Phi}_{22})-\Pi_{11}\;\Pi_{22}\overline{\Phi}_{12}^{2}}\;,

\chi_{12}=\frac{\Pi_{11}\Pi_{22}\overline{\Phi}_{12}}{(1-\Pi_{11}\overline{\Phi}_{11})(1-\Pi_{22}\overline{\Phi}_{22})-\Pi_{11}\;\Pi_{22}\overline{\Phi}_{12}^{2}}\;.

(18)

Here, for compactness, the variables
{\bf k}
and
\omega
are omitted. The
response functions
\chi_{21}
and
\chi_{22}
can be obtained by exchanging
the indices
1
and
2
in the above expressions.

We consider here an arbitrary potential
\Phi({\bf r})
. In particular, for
dilute systems of trapped atoms, one has

\Phi_{ii}=4\pi\;\frac{a_{i}}{m_{i}}\;,\qquad\Phi_{ij}=2\pi\;\frac{a_{ij}}{m_{ij}}\;\qquad\left(m_{ij}\equiv\frac{m_{i}m_{j}}{m_{i}+m_{j}}\right)\;,

where
a_{i}
and
a_{ij}
are scattering lengths.

4
Excitation spectrum

The spectrum of collective excitations is given by the poles of the response
functions, that is by the equation

\chi_{ij}^{-1}({\bf k},\omega)=0\;.

(19)

It is convenient to introduce the function

f_{i}({\bf k},\omega)\equiv\frac{1}{\rho_{i}}\int\frac{[\omega^{2}-(k^{2}/2m_{i})^{2}]n_{i}({\bf k}^{\prime})}{(\omega-{\bf k}\cdot{\bf k}^{\prime}/m_{i})^{2}-(k^{2}/2m_{i})^{2}}\;\frac{d{\bf k}^{\prime}}{(2\pi)^{3}}

(20)

satisfying the limits

\lim_{k\rightarrow 0}f_{i}({\bf k},\omega)=\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}f_{i}({\bf k},\omega)=1\;.

(21)

For the mixture of two components, the spectrum of collective excitations can be
represented
[
35
]
as

\varepsilon^{2}_{\pm}=\frac{1}{2}\;\left[\;\varepsilon_{1}^{2}+\varepsilon_{2}^{2}\;\pm\;\sqrt{(\varepsilon_{1}^{2}-\varepsilon_{2}^{2})^{2}+4\varepsilon_{12}^{4}}\;\right]\;,

(22)

where

\varepsilon_{i}^{2}({\bf k})=s_{i}^{2}({\bf k})\;k^{2}+\left(\frac{k^{2}}{2m_{i}}\right)^{2}\;,

(23)

with the effective sound velocity

s_{i}^{2}({\bf k})=\frac{\rho_{i}}{m_{i}}\;\overline{\Phi}_{ii}({\bf k})f_{i}({\bf k},\varepsilon_{\pm})\;,

(24)

and where

\varepsilon_{12}^{2}({\bf k})=s_{12}^{2}({\bf k})\;k^{2}\;,

(25)

with

s_{12}^{2}({\bf k})=\sqrt{\frac{\rho_{1}\rho_{2}}{m_{1}m_{2}}\;f_{1}({\bf k},\varepsilon_{\pm})\;f_{2}({\bf k},\varepsilon_{\pm})}\;\overline{\Phi}_{12}({\bf k})\;.

(26)

In the long-wave limit, the spectra of a binary mixture reduce to the sound-type
form

\varepsilon_{\pm}({\bf k})\simeq s_{\pm}\;k\qquad(k\rightarrow 0)\;,

(27)

with the sound velocities given by the equation

s_{\pm}^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\;\left[\;s_{1}^{2}+s_{2}^{2}\;\pm\;\sqrt{(s_{1}^{2}-s_{2}^{2})^{2}+4s_{12}^{4}}\;\right]\;,

(28)

where

s_{i}^{2}\equiv s_{i}^{2}(0)=\frac{\rho_{i}}{m_{i}}\;\Phi_{ii}\;,\qquad s_{12}^{2}\equiv s_{12}^{2}(0)=\sqrt{\frac{\rho_{1}\rho_{2}}{m_{1}m_{2}}}\;\Phi_{12}\;.

(29)

From here, it is seen that for the separate stability of each of the components,
it should be

\Phi_{ii}>0\;.

(30)

However, the interaction of different components,
\Phi_{12}
can be of both
signs.

In the short-wave limit, one has

\varepsilon_{\pm}({\bf k})\simeq\frac{k^{2}}{2m_{i}}\qquad(k\rightarrow\infty)\;.

(31)

As is clear, the spectrum branch
\varepsilon_{+}({\bf k})
, describing the
total density oscillations, is non-negative, and the branch
\varepsilon_{-}({\bf k})
,
characterizing the oscillations of the components with respect to each other,
is non-negative under the condition

\varepsilon_{12}^{2}({\bf k})<\varepsilon_{1}({\bf k})\;\varepsilon_{2}({\bf k})\;,

(32)

which is the condition of mixture stability. In the long-wave limit, we have

s_{12}^{2}<s_{1}\;s_{2}\;,

(33)

which gives the stability condition

\Phi_{12}^{2}<\Phi_{11}\;\Phi_{22}\;.

(34)

It is interesting that this is the same condition as for a coherent, completely
Bose-condensed mixture
[
20
]
. In general, depending on the
interaction potentials, there can appear a finite momentum where the stability
condition (
32
) becomes broken, as under the roton instability in dipolar
systems
[
36
]
.

5
Moving fluids

The stability conditions also depend on the motion of fluids

[
20
,
30
,
35
]
. Let us consider a binary mixture, such
that the components move with velocities
{\bf v}_{i}
. Then, acting similarly to
the above analysis, we come to the equation for the spectrum of collective
excitations

\left[\;(\omega-\alpha_{1})^{2}-\varepsilon_{1}^{2}\;\right]\left[\;(\omega-\alpha_{2})^{2}-\varepsilon_{2}^{2}\;\right]=\varepsilon_{12}^{4}\;,

(35)

in which

\alpha_{i}({\bf k})\equiv{\bf v}_{i}\cdot{\bf k}=v_{i}\;k\;\cos(\vartheta_{i})\;.

(36)

By the Descartes polynomial theorem
[
37
]
this equation enjoys two
real roots provided that

\varepsilon_{12}^{4}({\bf k})<\left[\;\varepsilon_{1}^{2}({\bf k})-\alpha_{1}^{2}({\bf k})\;\right]\left[\;\varepsilon_{2}^{2}({\bf k})-\alpha_{2}^{2}({\bf k})\;\right]

(37)

for all angles
\vartheta_{i}
. Minimizing the right-hand side of the above inequality
yields

\varepsilon_{12}^{4}({\bf k})<\left[\;\varepsilon_{1}^{2}({\bf k})-v_{1}^{2}\;k^{2}\;\right]\left[\;\varepsilon_{2}^{2}({\bf k})-v_{2}^{2}\;k^{2}\;\right]

(38)

for the angles
\vartheta_{i}=0,\pi
. For the broken condition (
38
), under the
angles
\vartheta_{1}=0
and
\vartheta_{2}=\pi
or
\vartheta_{1}=\pi
and

\vartheta_{2}=0
, this is called the counter-flow instability, and when

\vartheta_{1}=\vartheta_{2}
, this is the instability of motion.

In the long-wave limit, inequality (
38
) reduces to the form

s_{12}^{4}<(s_{1}^{2}-v_{1}^{2})(s_{2}^{2}-v_{2}^{2})\;.

(39)

In terms of the interaction potentials, this is equivalent to the condition

\Phi_{12}^{2}<\left(\Phi_{11}\;-\;\frac{m_{1}v_{1}^{2}}{\rho_{1}}\right)\left(\Phi_{22}\;-\;\frac{m_{2}v_{2}^{2}}{\rho_{2}}\right)\;.

(40)

6
Dynamic form-factor

The measured quantity under neutron or x-ray scattering is the double-differential
cross section (see
[
5
,
38
]
)

\frac{1}{N}\;\frac{d^{2}\sigma}{d\Omega_{k}\;d\omega}=b_{sc}^{2}\;\frac{k_{sc}}{k_{in}}\;S_{tot}({\bf k},\omega)\;,

(41)

in which the transferred momentum and energy are

{\bf k}\equiv{\bf k}_{in}-{\bf k}_{sc}\;,\qquad\omega\equiv E_{in}-E_{sc}\;,

\Omega_{k}
is a solid angle around
{\bf k}
, and
b_{sc}
is the scattering
length of a neutron on an atom. The dynamic form-factor at equilibrium is the
Fourier transform

S_{tot}({\bf k},\omega)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{ij}\int D_{ij}({\bf r},t,{\bf r}^{\prime},0)\;e^{-i{\bf k}\cdot({\bf r}-{\bf r}^{\prime})+i\omega t}\;d{\bf r}d{\bf r}^{\prime}dt

(42)

of the density-density correlation function

D_{ij}({\bf r},t,{\bf r}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\equiv\langle\;\hat{n}_{i}({\bf r},t)\;\hat{n}_{j}({\bf r}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\;\rangle\;,

(43)

where the density operator is

\hat{n}_{i}({\bf r},t)\equiv\psi_{i}^{\dagger}({\bf r},t)\;\psi_{i}({\bf r},t)\;.

(44)

The total dynamic form-factor is the sum of two terms,

S_{tot}({\bf k},\omega)=S_{el}({\bf k},\omega)+S({\bf k},\omega)\;.

(45)

The first term

S_{el}({\bf k},\omega)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{ij}\int\rho_{i}({\bf r},t)\;\rho_{j}({\bf r}^{\prime},0)\;e^{-i{\bf k}\cdot({\bf r}-{\bf r}^{\prime})+i\omega t}\;d{\bf r}d{\bf r}^{\prime}dt\;,

(46)

where

\rho_{i}({\bf r},t)\equiv\langle\;\hat{n}_{i}({\bf r},t)\;\rangle\;,

(47)

describes elastic scattering. The second term

S({\bf k},\omega)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{ij}\int\left[\;D_{ij}({\bf r},t,{\bf r}^{\prime},0)-\rho_{i}({\bf r},t)\;\rho_{j}({\bf r}^{\prime},0)\;\right]\;e^{-i{\bf k}\cdot({\bf r}-{\bf r}^{\prime})+i\omega t}\;d{\bf r}d{\bf r}^{\prime}dt\;,

(48)

also called the van Hove function, describes inelastic scattering.

For a uniform system, where

\rho_{i}({\bf r},t)=\rho_{i}=\frac{N_{i}}{V}\;,

the elastic scattering term is

S_{el}({\bf k},\omega)=(2\pi)^{4}\sum_{ij}\frac{\rho_{i}\rho_{j}}{\rho}\;\delta({\bf k})\;\delta(\omega)\;.

(49)

The inelastic term takes the form

S({\bf k},\omega)=\sum_{ij}S_{ij}({\bf k},\omega)\;,

(50)

where

S_{ij}({\bf k},\omega)=\frac{1}{N}\int\left[\;D_{ij}({\bf r},t,{\bf r}^{\prime},0)-\rho_{i}({\bf r},t)\;\rho_{j}({\bf r}^{\prime},0)\;\right]\;e^{-i{\bf k}\cdot({\bf r}-{\bf r}^{\prime})+i\omega t}\;d{\bf r}d{\bf r}^{\prime}dt\;.

(51)

Defining the Fourier transform of the density-density correlation function

D_{ij}({\bf k},\omega)=\int D_{ij}({\bf r},t,0,0)\;e^{-i{\bf k}\cdot{\bf r}+i\omega t}\;d{\bf r}dt

(52)

gives for a uniform system

S_{ij}({\bf k},\omega)=\frac{1}{\rho}\;\left[\;D_{ij}({\bf k},\omega)-(2\pi)^{4}\;\rho_{i}\;\rho_{j}\;\delta({\bf k})\;\delta(\omega)\;\right]\;.

(53)

The density-density correlation function (
43
) is connected with the
density-density Green function

-i\;\langle\;\hat{T}\;\hat{n}({\bf r},t)\;\hat{n}({\bf r},t^{\prime})\;\rangle\;.

Using the properties of this Green function
[
39
]
results in the
Fourier transform

D_{ij}({\bf k},\omega)=-\;\frac{2{\rm Im}\chi_{ij}({\bf k},\omega)}{1+e^{-\beta\omega}}+(2\pi)^{4}\;\rho_{i}\;\rho_{j}\;\delta({\bf k})\;\delta(\omega)\;,

(54)

where
\chi_{ij}({\bf k},\omega)
is the Fourier transform of the response
function (
2
). Respectively, for the dynamic form-factor (
51
), we get

S_{ij}({\bf k},\omega)=-\;\frac{2{\rm Im}\chi_{ij}({\bf k},\omega)}{\rho(1+e^{-\beta\omega})}\;.

(55)

The response function enjoys the spectral representation

\chi_{ij}({\bf k},\omega)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\gamma_{ij}({\bf k},\omega^{\prime})}{\omega-\omega^{\prime}}\;\frac{d\omega^{\prime}}{2\pi}\;,

(56)

with the spectral function

\gamma_{ij}({\bf k},\omega)=i\;[\;\chi_{ij}({\bf k},\omega+i0)-\chi_{ij}({\bf k},\omega-i0)\;]\;,

(57)

and the dispersion relation

{\rm Im}\chi_{ij}({\bf k},\omega)=-\;\frac{1}{2}\;\gamma_{ij}({\bf k},\omega)\;\coth\left(\frac{\beta\omega}{2}\right)\;.

(58)

This gives

S_{ij}({\bf k},\omega)=\frac{\gamma_{ij}({\bf k},\omega)}{\rho(1-e^{-\beta\omega})}\;.

(59)

The dynamic form-factor possesses the properties

S_{ij}(-{\bf k},\omega)=S_{ij}({\bf k},\omega)\;,\qquad S_{ij}({\bf k},-\omega)=S_{ij}({\bf k},\omega)\;e^{-\beta\omega}\;.

(60)

It can be written as the sum

S_{ij}({\bf k},\omega)=S_{ij}^{+}({\bf k},\omega)+S_{ij}^{-}({\bf k},-\omega)

(61)

of the symmetric term

S_{ij}^{+}({\bf k},\omega)\equiv\frac{1}{2}\;[\;S_{ij}({\bf k},\omega)+S_{ij}({\bf k},-\omega)\;]=\frac{1}{2\rho}\;\gamma_{ij}({\bf k},\omega)\;\coth\left(\frac{\beta\omega}{2}\right)=S_{ij}^{+}({\bf k},-\omega)

(62)

and the antisymmetric term

S_{ij}^{-}({\bf k},\omega)\equiv\frac{1}{2}\;[\;S_{ij}({\bf k},\omega)-S_{ij}({\bf k},-\omega)\;]=\frac{1}{2\rho}\;\gamma_{ij}({\bf k},\omega)=-S_{ij}^{-}({\bf k},-\omega)

(63)

with respect to the change of the sign of
\omega
.

7
Response functions

The response functions (
18
) can be written in the form

\chi_{11}({\bf k},\omega)=\left(\frac{\rho_{1}}{m_{1}}\right)\;\frac{[\omega^{2}-\varepsilon_{2}^{2}({\bf k})]k^{2}}{[\omega^{2}-\varepsilon_{+}^{2}({\bf k})][\omega^{2}-\varepsilon_{-}^{2}({\bf k})]}\;,

\chi_{12}({\bf k},\omega)=\sqrt{\frac{\rho_{1}\rho_{2}}{m_{1}m_{2}}}\;\frac{\varepsilon_{12}^{2}({\bf k})k^{2}}{[\omega^{2}-\varepsilon_{+}^{2}({\bf k})][\omega^{2}-\varepsilon_{-}^{2}({\bf k})]}\;.

(64)

Then the spectral functions (
57
) become

\gamma_{11}({\bf k},\omega)=\frac{\rho_{1}}{m_{1}}\;[\;\omega^{2}-\varepsilon_{2}^{2}({\bf k})\;]\;k^{2}\;\gamma({\bf k},\omega)\;,

\gamma_{12}({\bf k},\omega)=\sqrt{\frac{\rho_{1}\rho_{2}}{m_{1}m_{2}}}\;\varepsilon_{12}^{2}({\bf k})\;k^{2}\;\gamma({\bf k},\omega)\;,

(65)

where

\gamma({\bf k},\omega)=2\pi\delta\left(\;[\;\omega^{2}-\varepsilon_{+}^{2}({\bf k})\;]\;[\;\omega^{2}-\varepsilon_{-}^{2}({\bf k})\;]\;\right)=

=\frac{\pi}{\varepsilon_{+}^{2}({\bf k})-\varepsilon_{-}^{2}({\bf k})}\;\left\{\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{+}({\bf k})}\;[\;\delta(\omega-\varepsilon_{+}({\bf k}))-\delta(\omega+\varepsilon_{+}({\bf k}))\;]\;-\right.

-\;\left.\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{-}({\bf k})}\;[\;\delta(\omega-\varepsilon_{-}({\bf k}))-\delta(\omega+\varepsilon_{-}({\bf k}))\;]\;\right\}\;.

(66)

8
Structure factor

The system structure factor

S({\bf k})=\sum_{ij}S_{ij}({\bf k})

(67)

is the sum of the partial structure factors

S_{ij}({\bf k})=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}S_{ij}({\bf k},\omega)\;\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\;.

(68)

For the latter, we have

S_{ij}({\bf k})=\frac{1}{\rho}\int_{0}^{\infty}\gamma_{ij}({\bf k},\omega)\coth\left(\frac{\beta\omega}{2}\right)\;d\omega\;.

(69)

Explicitly, for a binary mixture, this reads as

S_{11}({\bf k})=\left(\frac{\rho_{1}}{m_{1}}\right)\;\frac{k^{2}}{2\rho[\;\varepsilon_{+}^{2}({\bf k})-\varepsilon_{-}^{2}({\bf k})\;]}\;\times

\times\left[\;\frac{\varepsilon_{+}^{2}({\bf k})-\varepsilon_{2}^{2}({\bf k})}{\varepsilon_{+}({\bf k})}\;\coth\left(\frac{\beta\varepsilon_{+}({\bf k})}{2}\right)\;-\;\frac{\varepsilon_{-}^{2}({\bf k})-\varepsilon_{2}^{2}({\bf k})}{\varepsilon_{-}({\bf k})}\;\coth\left(\frac{\beta\varepsilon_{-}({\bf k})}{2}\right)\;\right]

(70)

and

S_{12}({\bf k})=\sqrt{\frac{\rho_{1}\rho_{2}}{m_{1}m_{2}}}\;\frac{\varepsilon_{12}^{2}({\bf k})k^{2}}{2\rho[\varepsilon_{+}^{2}({\bf k})-\varepsilon_{-}^{2}({\bf k})]}\;\times

\times\left[\;\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{+}({\bf k})}\;\coth\left(\frac{\beta\varepsilon_{+}({\bf k})}{2}\right)\;-\;\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{-}({\bf k})}\;\coth\left(\frac{\beta\varepsilon_{-}({\bf k})}{2}\right)\;\right]\;.

(71)

It is possible to check that in the short-wave limit

\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\;\sum_{ij}S_{ij}({\bf k})=1\;.

(72)

The limiting behavior of the partial structure factors depends on the
relation between the sound modes and temperature. If the sound-mode energy
is much smaller than temperature, then the structure factors tend to the
limits

S_{11}(0)=\left(\frac{\rho_{1}}{m_{1}}\right)\;\frac{s_{2}^{2}(0)T}{\rho s_{+}^{2}(0)s_{-}^{2}(0)}\;,

S_{12}(0)=\sqrt{\frac{\rho_{1}\rho_{2}}{m_{1}m_{2}}}\;\frac{s_{12}^{2}(0)T}{\rho s_{+}^{2}(0)s_{-}^{2}(0)}\qquad\left(\frac{s_{\pm}k}{T}\ll 1\right)\;.

(73)

In the opposite limit of temperature much smaller than the sound-mode energy,
one has

S_{11}(k)\simeq\left(\frac{\rho_{1}}{m_{1}}\right)\;\frac{[\varepsilon_{+}({\bf k})\varepsilon_{-}({\bf k})+\varepsilon_{2}^{2}({\bf k})]k^{2}}{2\rho\varepsilon_{+}({\bf k})\varepsilon_{-}({\bf k})[\varepsilon_{+}({\bf k})+\varepsilon_{-}({\bf k})]}\;,

S_{12}(k)\simeq-\;\sqrt{\frac{\rho_{1}\rho_{2}}{m_{1}m_{2}}}\;\frac{\varepsilon_{12}^{2}({\bf k})k^{2}}{2\rho\varepsilon_{+}({\bf k})\varepsilon_{-}({\bf k})[\varepsilon_{+}({\bf k})-\varepsilon_{-}({\bf k})]}\;,

(74)

provided that

\frac{T}{s_{\pm}k}\ll 1\;.

If both temperature and the sound-mode energy tend to zero so that
T/s_{\pm}k

tends to zero, then

S_{11}({\bf k})\simeq\left(\frac{\rho_{1}}{m_{1}}\right)\;\frac{[s_{+}(0)s_{-}(0)+s_{2}^{2}(0)]k}{2\rho s_{+}(0)s_{-}(0)[s_{+}(0)+s_{-}(0)]}\;,

S_{12}({\bf k})\simeq-\;\sqrt{\frac{\rho_{1}\rho_{2}}{m_{1}m_{2}}}\;\frac{s_{12}^{2}(0)k}{2\rho s_{+}(0)s_{-}(0)[s_{+}(0)+s_{-}(0)]}\;,

(75)

under the condition

T\rightarrow 0\;,\qquad k\rightarrow 0\;,\qquad\frac{T}{s_{\pm}k}\rightarrow 0\;.

In any case, when the mixture looses stability and starts separating, with

s_{-}(0)
tending to zero, the structure factors diverge.

9
Sum rules

One considers the integral

K_{ij}({\bf k})=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\omega S_{ij}({\bf k},\omega)\;\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}

(76)

that, using the properties of the dynamic form-factor, can be written as

K_{ij}({\bf k})=\frac{1}{\rho}\int_{0}^{\infty}\omega\gamma_{ij}({\bf k},\omega)\;\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\;.

(77)

With the spectral functions (
65
), this yields

K_{ij}({\bf k})=\delta_{ij}\;\frac{\rho_{i}}{\rho}\;\left(\frac{k^{2}}{2m_{i}}\right)\;.

(78)

The other important integral is

Q_{ij}({\bf k})=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\omega}\;S_{ij}({\bf k},\omega)\;\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\;,

(79)

which can be rewritten as

Q_{ij}({\bf k})=\frac{1}{\rho}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\omega}\;\gamma_{ij}({\bf k},\omega)\;\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}.

(80)

Taking into account the spectral representation (
56
) gives

Q_{ij}({\bf k})=-\;\frac{1}{\rho}\;{\rm Re}\;\chi_{ij}({\bf k},0)\;.

(81)

Since the response function has the spectral representation

\chi_{ij}({\bf k},\omega)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\gamma_{ij}({\bf k},\omega^{\prime})\;\left[\;\frac{1+n(\omega^{\prime})}{\omega-\omega^{\prime}+i0}\;-\;\frac{n(\omega^{\prime})}{\omega-\omega^{\prime}-i0}\;\right]\;\frac{d\omega^{\prime}}{2\pi}\;,

(82)

where
n(\omega)\equiv(e^{\beta\omega}-1)^{-1}
, then using the property

\frac{1}{\omega\pm i0}=P\;\frac{1}{\omega}\;\mp\;i\pi\delta(\omega)\;,

where
P
implies the principal value, we obtain the real part

{\rm Re}\;\chi_{ij}({\bf k},\omega)=P\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\gamma_{ij}({\bf k},\omega^{\prime})}{\omega-\omega^{\prime}}\;\frac{d\omega^{\prime}}{2\pi}

(83)

and the imaginary part

{\rm Im}\;\chi_{ij}({\bf k},\omega)=-\;\frac{1}{2}\;\gamma_{ij}{\bf k},\omega)\;[\;1+2n(\omega)\;]\;.

(84)

Here

1+2n(\omega)=\frac{e^{\beta\omega}+1}{e^{\beta\omega}-1}=\coth\left(\frac{\beta\omega}{2}\right)\;.

10
Isothermal compressibility

The response functions define the compressibility functions

\varkappa_{i}({\bf r},t,{\bf r}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\equiv-\;\frac{\chi_{ii}({\bf r},t,{\bf r}^{\prime},t^{\prime})}{\rho_{i}({\bf r},t)\rho_{i}({\bf r}^{\prime},t^{\prime})}\;,

(85)

whose Fourier transforms are

\varkappa_{i}({\bf k},\omega)=\int\chi_{i}({\bf r},t,0,0)\;e^{-i{\bf k}\cdot{\bf r}+i\omega t}\;d{\bf r}dt\;.

(86)

For an equilibrium uniform system, we have

\varkappa_{i}({\bf k},\omega)=-\;\frac{\chi_{ii}({\bf k},\omega)}{\rho_{i}^{2}}\;.

(87)

The coefficient of isothermal compressibility is defined by the expression

\varkappa_{i}\equiv{\rm Re}\;\varkappa_{i}(0,0)=-\;\frac{{\rm Re}\chi_{ii}(0,0)}{\rho_{i}^{2}}\;.

(88)

Hence it is given by the relation

\varkappa_{i}=\frac{\rho}{\rho_{i}^{2}}\;Q_{ii}(0)\;.

(89)

For a binary mixture, we get

{\rm Re}\;\chi_{11}(0,0)=-\;\frac{\rho_{1}s_{2}^{2}(0)}{m_{1}s_{+}^{2}(0)s_{-}^{2}(0)}\;,

{\rm Re}\;\chi_{12}(0,0)=\sqrt{\frac{\rho_{1}\rho_{2}}{m_{1}m_{2}}}\;\frac{s_{12}^{2}(0)}{s_{+}^{2}(0)s_{-}^{2}(0)}\;.

(90)

Therefore

\varkappa_{1}=\frac{s_{2}^{2}(0)}{\rho_{1}m_{1}s_{+}^{2}(0)s_{-}^{2}(0)}\;,\qquad\varkappa_{2}=\frac{s_{1}^{2}(0)}{\rho_{2}m_{2}s_{+}^{2}(0)s_{-}^{2}(0)}\;.

(91)

Thus we find another relation for the isothermal compressibilities

\varkappa_{i}=\frac{\rho S_{ii}(0)}{\rho_{i}^{2}T}\;.

(92)

When the mixture looses its stability and
s_{-}(0)
tends to zero, the
compressibility coefficients diverge.

11
Thermodynamic stability

Thermodynamic stability conditions are based on the comparison of the free energies
of the mixed,
F_{mix}
, and separated,
F_{sep}
, systems. The mixture is stable
when

F_{mix}<F_{sep}\;.

(93)

In what follows, we consider a uniform system with the component densities

\rho_{i}({\bf r})=N_{i}/V
and use the Hartree approximation.

The free energy of a mixed system writes as

F_{mix}=E_{mix}-TS_{mix}\;.

(94)

The energy

E_{mix}=\langle\;H\;\rangle+\sum_{i}\mu_{i}N_{i}=K_{mix}+E_{mix}^{ext}+E_{mix}^{int}

(95)

includes the kinetic energy term
K_{mix}
, the energy due to external
potentials,

E_{mix}^{ext}=\sum_{i}\int_{V}U_{i}({\bf r})\;\rho_{i}({\bf r})\;d{\bf r}=\sum_{i}U_{i}\;N_{i}\;,

(96)

where

U_{i}\equiv\frac{1}{V}\int_{V}U_{i}({\bf r})\;d{\bf r}\;,

and the interaction energy

E_{mix}^{int}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{ij}\int_{V}\rho_{i}({\bf r})\;\Phi_{ij}({\bf r}-{\bf r}^{\prime})\;\rho_{j}({\bf r}^{\prime})\;d{\bf r}d{\bf r}^{\prime}\;.

(97)

For a uniform system, the latter is

E_{mix}^{int}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{ij}\Phi_{ij}\;\frac{N_{i}N_{j}}{V}\;,

(98)

where

\Phi_{ij}\equiv\int_{V}\Phi_{ij}({\bf r})\;d{\bf r}\;.

(99)

The Hamiltonian of a separated system is

H_{sep}=\sum_{i}\int\psi_{i}^{\dagger}({\bf r})\;\left(-\;\frac{\nabla^{2}}{2m}+U_{i}-\mu_{i}\right)\psi_{i}({\bf r})\;d{\bf r}\;+

+\;\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}\int\psi_{i}^{\dagger}({\bf r})\;\psi_{i}^{\dagger}({\bf r}^{\prime})\;\Phi_{ii}({\bf r}-{\bf r}^{\prime})\;\psi_{i}({\bf r}^{\prime})\;\psi_{i}({\bf r})\;d{\bf r}d{\bf r}^{\prime}\;.

(100)

The free energy writes as

F_{sep}=E_{sep}-TS_{sep}\;.

(101)

The energy

E_{sep}=\langle\;H_{sep}\;\rangle+\sum_{i}\mu_{i}N_{i}=K_{sep}+E_{sep}^{ext}+E_{sep}^{int}

(102)

contains the kinetic energy
K_{sep}
, the energy caused by external potentials

E_{sep}^{ext}
, and the interaction energy
E_{sep}^{int}
.

The assumption of the system uniformity presupposes that the system volumes are
sufficiently large, so that the equalities are valid:

\frac{1}{V}\int_{V}U_{i}({\bf r})\;d{\bf r}=\frac{1}{V_{i}}\int_{V_{i}}U_{i}({\bf r})\;d{\bf r}\;,\qquad\int_{V}\Phi_{ii}({\bf r})\;d{\bf r}=\int_{V_{i}}\Phi_{ii}({\bf r})\;d{\bf r}\;.

Also, we assume that the difference in the kinetic energy, under the same given
conditions, can be neglected as compared to the difference in the potential
energy, thus setting
K_{mix}-K_{sep}\approx 0
. The component densities of
the uniform separated system are

\rho_{i}^{loc}({\bf r})=\langle\;\psi_{i}^{\dagger}({\bf r})\;\psi_{i}({\bf r})\;\rangle=\frac{N_{i}}{V_{i}}\;.

(103)

Then the energy due to external potentials is

E_{sep}^{ext}=\sum_{i}\int_{V_{i}}U_{i}({\bf r})\;\rho_{i}^{loc}({\bf r})\;d{\bf r}=\sum_{i}U_{i}\;N_{i}\;,

(104)

while the interaction energy of the separated system becomes

E_{sep}^{int}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}\int_{V_{i}}\rho_{i}^{loc}({\bf r})\;\Phi_{ii}({\bf r}-{\bf r}^{\prime})\;\rho_{i}^{loc}({\bf r}^{\prime})\;d{\bf r}d{\bf r}^{\prime}\;,

(105)

which gives

E_{sep}^{int}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}\Phi_{ii}\;\frac{N_{i}^{2}}{V_{i}}\;.

(106)

The condition of thermodynamic stability (
93
) takes the form

E_{mix}^{int}-E_{sep}^{int}<N\;T\;\Delta S_{mix}\;,

(107)

in which

\Delta S_{mix}=\frac{1}{N}\;(S_{mix}-S_{sep})

(108)

is the entropy of mixing per particle.

Mechanical equilibrium implies the equality of the pressures

P_{i}=-\;\frac{\partial F_{sep}}{\partial V_{i}}=P_{j}=-\;\frac{\partial F_{sep}}{\partial V_{j}}.

(109)

From the latter condition, we have

\frac{V_{j}}{V_{i}}=\frac{N_{j}}{N_{i}}\;\sqrt{\frac{\Phi_{jj}}{\Phi_{ii}}}\;.

This allows us to rewrite the stability condition (
107
) as the inequality

\sum_{ij}\frac{N_{i}N_{j}}{2V}\;\left(\Phi_{ij}\;-\;\sqrt{\Phi_{ii}\Phi_{jj}}\;-\;\frac{2T}{\rho}\;\Delta S_{mix}\right)<0\;.

(110)

Assuming that this inequality is valid for any
N_{i}
and
N_{j}
, we come to the
stability condition
[
30
]

\Phi_{ij}\;-\;\sqrt{\Phi_{ii}\;\Phi_{jj}}\;<\;\frac{2T}{\rho}\;\Delta S_{mix}\;.

(111)

The entropy of mixing per particle can be represented as

\Delta S_{mix}=-\sum_{i}n_{i}\;\ln n_{i}\qquad\left(n_{i}\equiv\frac{N_{i}}{N}\right)\;.

(112)

The mixture stability condition (
111
) shows that finite temperatures facilitate
the component mixing. The components could be immiscible at zero temperature but become
miscible at finite temperature. This is also in agreement with the miscibility criterion
for Bose-condensed mixtures, taking into account strong interactions and temperature

[
26
]
. The miscibility condition of type (
34
) can be invalid, but
the components, anyway, can be miscible at finite temperatures and interactions.

12
Conclusion

Mixtures of quantum fluids, liquids or gases, are considered. The studied systems
are treated as normal quantum systems, that is requiring quantum techniques for their
description, but exhibiting no superfluidity or superconductivity. The main point of
interest in the present review is the conditions of quantum mixture stability with
respect to the spatial separation into different components. The mixture stability
can be studied from the point of view of dynamic or thermodynamic stability.

The study of dynamic stability is based on the investigation of spectra of collective
excitations. The principal difference between Bose-condensed and normal fluids is in
the following. In a Bose-condensed system, the global gauge symmetry is broken. This
symmetry breaking is conveniently characterized by the Bogolubov shift

[
40
,
41
,
42
]
. Then the vacuum state is described by
a coherent state formed by completely condensed particles, while the uncondensed
particles play the role of excitations above the vacuum
[
43
]
. Because
of this, the single-particle Green functions of uncondensed particles, actually,
describe excitations above the coherent-state vacuum. This is why to study the
collective spectrum of a Bose-condensed system it is sufficient to consider solely
the single-particle Green functions of uncondensed particles, whose poles give the
required collective spectrum.

On the other hand, the vacuum for a normal quantum system is an empty state. The poles
of single-particle Green functions describe the single-particle spectra, but the
spectrum of collective excitations is characterized by the poles of two-particle Green
functions or by the poles of response functions.

For the description of response functions, one usually involves the random-phase
approximation. This approximation describes well the low-temperature states, but the
influence of temperature on the spectrum of collective excitations is not as important.

The role of temperature can be investigated by resorting to the study of thermodynamic
stability, when one compares the free energies of a mixed and separated systems. The
found stability criterion demonstrates that temperature facilitates the mixing of
different quantum components. At low temperature, the system could be immiscible,
while becomes miscible when rising temperature.

This effect also is in agreement with the study of stability of Bose-condensed systems
with strong interactions and finite temperature. For the correct description of such
systems, it is necessary to take into account anomalous averages and the existence of
two chemical potentials for each of the components, which is caused by the global gauge
symmetry breaking
[
44
,
45
,
46
]
. Including into the description
the corrections above the coherent approximation implies the inclusion into consideration
of quantum and thermal fluctuations. These fluctuations make the mixing easier. The
Bose-condensed mixture stability, accurately taking account of fluctuations, has been
recently analyzed
[
26
]
, confirming that fluctuations facilitate the mixing
of the components.
