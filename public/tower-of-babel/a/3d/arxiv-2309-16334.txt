Abstract
Prediction via deterministic continuous-time models will always be subject to model error, for example due to unexplainable phenomena, uncertainties in any data driving the model, or discretisation/resolution issues. In this paper, we build upon previous small-noise studies to provide an explicit bound for the error between a general class of stochastic differential equations and corresponding computable linearisations written in terms of a deterministic system. Our framework accounts for non-autonomous coefficients, multiplicative noise, and uncertain initial conditions. We demonstrate the predictive power of our bound on diverse numerical case studies. We confirm that our bound is sharp , in that it accurately predicts the error scaling in the moments of the linearised approximation as both the uncertainty in the initial condition and the magnitude of the noise in the differential equation are altered. This paper also provides an extension of stochastic sensitivity, a recently introduced tool for quantifying uncertainty in dynamical systems, to arbitrary dimensions and establishes the link to our characterisation of stochastic differential equation linearisations.

Introduction

Stochastic differential equations (SDEs) are a natural framework for introducing different sources of uncertainty into the continuous time evolution of a variable
[
28
,
37
,
20
]
.
Generally, in modelling situations the dynamics are highly nonlinear and one expects the noise to be multiplicative (i.e. vary with state), e.g. in atmospheric
[
39
]
and oceanic
[
21
]
systems and from experimental and observational considerations.
Such SDEs are usually intractable to solve analytically and computationally expensive to approximate accurately.
Data-based models—that is, models possessing terms in the equations which are driven by data rather than by explicitly specified functions—and uncertainty in the initial state render additional problems in obtaining a theoretical understanding of the stochastic system.

A common approach to characterising and approximating the solution of nonlinear SDEs with small noise is via a “linearisation” through time about a single deterministic trajectory.
A linearised stochastic differential equation, obtained by truncating Taylor expansions of each coefficient
[
18
,
9
, e.g.]
, can be solved analytically and is accordingly used across a diversity of literature and applications
[
18
,
37
,
22
,
2
,
36
,
23
,
33
,
13
]
.

Much is already known about these linearisations; classical results in the context of small-noise series expansions
[
9
]
and large deviations theory
[
14
]
show that the strong error between SDE solution with a fixed initial condition and that of an appropriate linearisation is bounded.

Sanz-Alonso and Stuart 2017
establish a strong result, bounding the Kullback-Leibler divergence between the solutions of autonomous SDEs with additive stationary noise and a linearised equivalent. Their result considers both an uncertain initial condition, and the evolving error due to the discrepancy between the models.

In
Section

2
, we directly bound the expectation of all moments of the distance between the linearisation and the true solution of the stochastic differential equation.
Our result extends the former convergence result of
[
36
]
in three ways: we predict the scaling of all moments of the error, in the presence of multiplicative noise terms, and including time-dependent coefficients in the stochastic differential equations. We directly compare our results with those of
[
36
]
in
Section

2.1
.
In
Section

4
, we show numerically that the scaling of the strong error predicted by
Theorem

2.1
matches our predictions on three example SDEs in
1
- and
2
-dimensions.

The second contribution of the paper is to the notion of “stochastic sensitivity”
[
4
]
, which seeks to explicitly quantify the impact of vector field uncertainty on the solution trajectories of dynamical systems
[
10
,
22
,
11
,
6
]
.
This methodology was originally developed by Balasuriya
[
4

as a tool for determining Lagrangian coherent structures (LCS)
[

in fluid flows, in that clusters of trajectories which have small uncertainty may be thought of as more “coherent” than others.
In

Section

Our expressions extend

Convergence of a SDE to a linearisation

Suppose we are interested in the evolution of a
\mathbb{R}^{n}
-valued state variable
y_{t}
over a finite time interval
[0,T]
.
Our model, accounting for uncertainties arising from a range of sources, for the evolution of this variable is the Itô stochastic differential equation

\dif y_{t}^{(\varepsilon)}=u\!\left(y_{t}^{(\varepsilon)},t\right)\dif t+\varepsilon\,\sigma\!\left(y_{t}^{(\varepsilon)},t\right)\dif W_{t},\quad y_{0}^{(\varepsilon)}=x

(2.1)

where
u\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\times[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the governing reference vector field.
The canonical
m
-dimensional Wiener process
W_{t}
is a continuous white-noise stochastic process with independent Gaussian increments.
The scale of the ongoing noise is assumed to be small and is parameterised as
0<\varepsilon\ll 1
.
The noise in
Equation

2.1
is multiplicative, in that the diffusion matrix
\sigma\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\times[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
can vary with state
x
, as well as with time
t
.
We assume that
\sigma
is specified
a priori
, or if no such information is known, then
\sigma\equiv I
, the
n\times m
identity matrix, is a default modelling choice.
We consider
Equation

2.1
subject to the
general
uncertain initial condition
y_{0}^{(\varepsilon)}=x
, where
x
is an
n
-dimensional random vector with some given distribution. The two sources of randomness,
x
and
W_{t}
, are assumed independent from each other.

In the absence of any uncertainty (i.e.
\varepsilon=0
and the initial condition is a known deterministic quantity),
Equation

2.1
reduces to the ordinary differential equation

\mathinner{\dfrac{\dif{}y_{t}^{(0)}}{\dif{t}}}=u\!\left(y_{t}^{(0)},t\right),\quad y_{0}^{(0)}=x_{0}.

(2.2)

where the initial condition
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is fixed.
The formal convergence of the stochastic solution
y_{t}^{(\varepsilon)}
(under certain conditions on the initial condition) to the deterministic
y_{t}^{(0)}
in the limit as
\varepsilon\to 0
is well-established using the large deviations principle
[
14
, e.g]
.
We refer to
Equation

2.2
as the
reference
deterministic model associated with
Equation

2.1
.
Solutions to the reference deterministic model are more readily available, e.g. in terms of computational efficiency when solving numerically, than those of the stochastic model, but do not account for inevitable uncertainty.

Let the flow map
F_{0}^{t}\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
be the function which evolves an initial condition from time
0
to time
t
according to the flow of
Equation

2.2
, i.e.
F_{0}^{t}\!\left(x_{0}\right)=y_{t}^{(0)}
.

We assume certain smoothness and boundedness conditions on the various terms outlined, which are stated explicitly in
H
.
Throughout this article, we use the norm symbol
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}
to denote (i) for a vector, the standard Euclidean vector norm, (ii) for a matrix, the spectral norm induced by the Euclidean norm, and (iii) for a 3rd-order tensor, the spectral norm induced by the matrix norm.
The gradient symbol
\nabla
generically refers to derivatives with respect to the state variable.

Hypothesis H

Let the deterministic functions
u\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\times[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\sigma\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\times[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
, and the random initial condition
x
be such that:

(H.1)

For all
t\in[0,T]
flow map
F_{0}^{t}\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is well-defined, and continuously differentiable (with respect to the initial condition) with invertible derivative.

(H.2)

For each
t\in[0,T]
, the function
u(\cdot,t)\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
given by
u(x,t)
is twice continuously differentiable on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and each component of the function
\sigma(\cdot,t)\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
given by
\sigma(x,t)
is differentiable on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

(H.3)

There exists a constant
K_{\nabla u}\geq 0
such that for any
t\in[0,T]
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\nabla u(x,t)\right\rVert}\leq K_{\nabla u}.

Equivalently, for all
t\in[0,T]
, the function
u\!\left(\cdot,t\right)
is Lipschitz continuous with Lipschitz constant
K_{\nabla u}
.

(H.4)

For each
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the function
u(x,\cdot)\colon[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
and each component of the function
\sigma(x,\cdot)\colon[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
are Borel-measurable on
[0,T]
.

(H.5)

There exists a constant
K_{L}
such that for any
t\in[0,T]
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert u\left(x,t\right)\right\rVert}+\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\sigma\left(x,t\right)\right\rVert}\leq K_{L}\left(1+\mathinner{\!\left\lVert x\right\rVert}\right).

(H.6)

There exists a constant
K_{\nabla\sigma}\geq 0
such that for any
t\in[0,T]
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\nabla\sigma(x,t)\right\rVert}\leq K_{\nabla\sigma},

and we take
K_{\nabla\sigma}=0
if there is no spatial dependence in
\sigma
.
Equivalently, for all
t\in[0,T]
, the function
\sigma\!\left(x,\cdot\right)
is Lipschitz continuous with Lipschitz constant
K_{\nabla\sigma}
.

(H.7)

The initial condition
x
is defined on the same probability space as
W_{t}
, and is independent of
W_{t}
for all
t\in[0,T]
.

(H.8)

There exists a constant
K_{\nabla\nabla u}\geq 0
such that for any
t\in[0,T]
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\nabla\nabla u(x,t)\right\rVert}\leq K_{\nabla\nabla u},

and we take
K_{\nabla\nabla}=0
if the second spatial derivatives of
u
are all zero.

(H.9)

There exists a constant
K_{\sigma}\geq 0
such that for any
t\in[0,T]
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\sigma(x,t)\right\rVert}\leq K_{\sigma}.

The conditions
H.2
to
H.7
guarantee that
Equation

2.1
with the initial condition
y_{0}=x
has a unique strong solution
[
20
]
.
The bound
K_{\nabla\nabla u}
placed on the second derivatives of
u
in
H.8
quantifies exactly when the deterministic dynamics (that is,
u
) of
Equation

2.1
are linear.
Similarly, the bound
K_{\nabla\sigma}
on the spatial derivatives of
\sigma
in
H.6
allows us to distinguish when the noise in
Equation

2.1
is multiplicative.

Our aim is to construct and formally justify a computable linearisation of
Equation

2.1
about a trajectory solving the deterministic system
Equation

2.2
.
To that end, we take a
fixed
initial condition
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
to the reference deterministic model
Equation

2.2
and consider linearising the SDE
Equation

2.1
about the corresponding trajectory
F_{0}^{t}\!\left(x_{0}\right)
.
We consider the following linearisation of
Equation

2.1
:

\dif l_{t}^{(\varepsilon)}=\left[u\!\left(F_{0}^{t}\!\left(x_{0}\right),t\right)+\nabla u\!\left(F_{0}^{t}\!\left(x_{0}\right),t\right)\left(l_{t}^{(\varepsilon)}-F_{0}^{t}\!\left(x_{0}\right)\right)\right]\!\dif t+\varepsilon\sigma\!\left(F_{0}^{t}\!\left(x_{0}\right),t\right)\!\dif W_{t},\quad l_{0}^{(\varepsilon)}=x,

(2.3)

where the initial condition
x
is still permitted to be random.
Informally, we can arrive at
Equation

2.3
by performing a Taylor expansion of the coefficient
u
up to first-order and
\sigma
to zeroth-order about the time-varying trajectory
F_{0}^{t}\!\left(x_{0}\right)
.
Such a linearisation is advantageous over the nonlinear SDE
Equation

2.1
, since
Equation

2.3
can be solved analytically.
We will later (see
Corollary

2.8
) provide explicit expressions for computing the distribution of the solution
l_{t}^{(\varepsilon)}
solely in terms of the solution dynamics of the deterministic system
Equation

2.2
, the specified diffusion matrix
\sigma
, and the distribution of
x
.

In order to quantify the error arising from the choice of reference point
x_{0}
, we define

\delta_{r}\coloneqq\mathds{E}\!\left[\mathinner{\!\left\lVert x-x_{0}\right\rVert}^{r}\right]^{1/r},

i.e.
\delta_{r}
is the
L_{r}
distance between
x
and the deterministic point
x_{0}
.
We can think of
\delta_{r}
as a scalar measure of the uncertainty in the initial condition, relative to the choice of reference point
x_{0}
.
Alternatively, the limit as
\delta_{r}
approaches zero is equivalent to convergence in
r
th mean of
x
to the fixed point
x_{0}
.
We can therefore distinguish two sources of uncertainty in our model; that arising from the initial condition, quantified by
\delta_{r}
, and the ongoing uncertainty driven by the Wiener process
W_{t}
as measured by
\varepsilon
.

Our first and primary result,
Theorem

2.1
, provides an explicit bound on the
r
th moment of the error between the SDE solution
y_{t}^{(\varepsilon)}
and the linearised solution
l_{t}^{(\varepsilon)}
.

Theorem 2.1

(Linearisation error is bounded)
.

Let
y_{t}^{(\varepsilon)}
be the strong solution to the SDE
Equation

2.1
and
l_{t}^{(\varepsilon)}
be the strong solution to the corresponding linearisation
Equation

2.3
, both driven by the same Wiener process
W_{t}
and subject to the same random initial condition
y_{0}^{(\varepsilon)}=l_{0}^{(\varepsilon)}=x
.
Then, for any
r\geq 1
such that
\delta_{2r}<\infty
and

, there exist constants
D_{1}\!\left(r,t,K_{\nabla u},K_{\sigma}\right),\,D_{2}\!\left(r,t,K_{\nabla u}\right),\,D_{3}\!\left(r,t,K_{\nabla u}\right)\in[0,\infty)

\mathds{E}\!\left[\mathinner{\!\left\lVert y_{t}^{(\varepsilon)}-l_{t}^{(\varepsilon)}\right\rVert}^{r}\right]\leq\begin{multlined}\left(K_{\nabla\nabla u}^{r}+K_{\nabla\sigma}^{r}\right)D_{1}\!\left(r,t,K_{\nabla u},K_{\sigma}\right)\,\varepsilon^{2r}\\
+K_{\nabla\nabla u}^{r}D_{2}\!\left(r,t,K_{\nabla u}\right)\delta_{2r}^{2r}+K_{\nabla\sigma}^{r}D_{3}\!\left(r,t,K_{\nabla u}\right)\delta_{r}^{r}\varepsilon^{r}.\end{multlined}

(2.4)

Proof 2.2

See

Our proof employs the Burkholder-Davis-Gundy inequality, Grönwall’s inequality, and Taylor’s theorem to explicitly construct the bounding coefficients in terms of the conditions on the SDE coefficients set out in

The bounding coefficients

In

The three terms can be informally considered as: a contribution purely from the ongoing linearisation error, a contribution purely from the initial uncertainty, and a term resulting from the interaction between the initial and ongoing uncertainties.
By explicitly identifying the dependence of the bound on

Remark 2.3

(Linear drift)
.

When the deterministic dynamics are linear, we set
K_{\nabla\nabla u}=0
and

2.4

\mathds{E}\!\left[\mathinner{\!\left\lVert y_{t}^{(\varepsilon)}-l_{t}^{(\varepsilon)}\right\rVert}^{r}\right]\leq K_{\nabla\sigma}^{r}D_{1}\!\left(r,t,K_{\nabla u},K_{\sigma}\right)\,\varepsilon^{2r}+K_{\nabla\sigma}^{r}D_{3}\!\left(r,t,K_{\nabla u}\right)\delta_{r}^{r}\varepsilon^{r}.

The linearisation of the drift term

Remark 2.4

(Additive noise)
.

When the noise in

is additive (non-multiplicative), we set
K_{\nabla\sigma}=0

\mathds{E}\!\left[\mathinner{\!\left\lVert y_{t}^{(\varepsilon)}-l_{t}^{(\varepsilon)}\right\rVert}^{r}\right]\leq K_{\nabla\nabla u}^{r}D_{1}\!\left(r,t,K_{\nabla u},K_{\sigma}\right)\,\varepsilon^{2r}+K_{\nabla\nabla u}^{r}D_{2}\!\left(r,t,K_{\nabla u}\right)\delta_{2r}^{2r}.

The error is then purely due to the linearisation of the drift term

, and as expected is of second order in both the initial condition uncertainty
\delta_{2r}
and the ongoing uncertainty

Remark 2.5

(Exact linearisation)
.

When the deterministic dynamics are linear and the noise in

is additive (non-multiplicative), the linearisation

2.3
should be exact.
Accordingly, we set

\mathds{E}\!\left[\mathinner{\!\left\lVert y_{t}^{(\varepsilon)}-l_{t}^{(\varepsilon)}\right\rVert}^{r}\right]=0.

In turn, this implies that

almost surely, for any choice of reference point

\displaystyle\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mathds{E}\!\left[y_{t}^{(\varepsilon)}-l_{t}^{(\varepsilon)}\right]\right\rVert}\leq cD_{\mathrm{H}}\!\left(\xi_{t}^{(\varepsilon)},\,\nu_{t}^{(\varepsilon)}\right)\leq c\sqrt{D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(\xi_{t}^{(\varepsilon)}\,\middle|\middle|\,\nu_{t}^{(\varepsilon)}\right)}\leq c\,\varepsilon\;,

(2.12)

while our result
Equation

2.4
and Jensen’s inequality imply

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mathds{E}\!\left[y_{t}^{(\varepsilon)}-l_{t}^{(\varepsilon)}\right]\right\rVert}\leq\mathds{E}\!\left[\mathinner{\!\left\lVert y_{t}^{(\varepsilon)}-l_{t}^{(\varepsilon)}\right\rVert}\right]\leq c\,\varepsilon^{2}\;.

Thus, our bound on the moments is quadratic in
\varepsilon
rather than linear.
If our conversion in
Equation

2.12
was optimal, then our approach in this article
provides a sharper bound on
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mathds{E}\!\left[y_{t}^{(\varepsilon)}-l_{t}^{(\varepsilon)}\right]\right\rVert}
that the
results of
[
36
]
imply, and do so for a
more general
\sigma
.
The results in
[
36
]
on the KL divergence would be more natural in information-theoretic contexts, and our hope is that our explicit bound on the moments would be similarly preferred in other contexts.

2.2
Gaussian initial condition

We now briefly consider the case when the initial condition follows a Gaussian distribution, i.e.
x\sim\mathcal{N}\!\left(\mu_{0},\Sigma_{0}\right)
, where
\mu_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\Sigma_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
are fixed and specified.
The linearisation then follows a Gaussian distribution itself, which is entirely characterised by the mean and covariance matrix described in
Corollary

2.8
.
Alternatively, these moments can be conveniently computed by simultaneously solving
Equation

2.2
for the state variable and
Equation

2.9
for the linearised covariance.

A natural choice of reference point
x_{0}
is the mean of the initial Gaussian density, i.e.
x_{0}=\mu_{0}
.
The
L_{r}
distance between
x
and the mean
\mu_{0}
can be bounded by the trace of
\Sigma_{0}
; for example, one such bound is

\delta_{r}^{r}\leq n^{3r/2-1}M_{r}\mathrm{tr}\left(\Sigma_{0}\right)^{r/2},\quad M_{r}\coloneqq\frac{2^{r/2}\Gamma\!\left(\frac{r+1}{2}\right)}{\sqrt{\pi}},

(2.13)

where
\Gamma
denotes the Gamma function, with equality when
n=1
.
The initial covariance
\Sigma_{0}
directly measures the uncertainty in the initial condition, and we see through
Equation

2.13
that as the components of
\Sigma_{0}
approach zero, the contribution of the initial uncertainty to the linearisation error in
Equation

2.4
approaches zero also.
The linearised solution is then

l_{t}^{(\varepsilon)}\sim\mathcal{N}\!\left(F_{0}^{t}\!\left(x_{0}\right),\,\nabla F_{0}^{t}\!\left(x_{0}\right)\Sigma_{0}\left[\nabla F_{0}^{t}\!\left(x_{0}\right)\right]^{{}^{\!\!\intercal}}+\varepsilon^{2}\Sigma_{0}^{t}\!\left(x_{0}\right)\right),

where
\Sigma_{0}^{t}\!\left(x_{0}\right)
is given explicitly by

\Sigma_{0}^{t}\!\left(x_{0}\right)=\nabla F_{0}^{t}\!\left(x_{0}\right)\left(\int_{0}^{t}{L\!\left(x_{0},\tau\right)L\!\left(x_{0},\tau\right)^{{}^{\!\!\intercal}}\dif\tau}\right)\left[\nabla F_{0}^{t}\!\left(x_{0}\right)\right]^{{}^{\!\!\intercal}},

(2.14)

and is the solution to the matrix differential equation
Equation

2.9
in
Remark

2.10
, subject to
\Sigma_{0}^{0}\!\left(x_{0}\right)=O
, the
n\times n
zero matrix.
The covariance matrix
\Sigma_{0}^{t}\!\left(x_{0}\right)
characterises the contribution of the ongoing uncertainty in the stochastic system.
The full covariance matrix
\mathds{V}\!\left[l_{t}^{(\varepsilon)}\right]
is also the solution to
Equation

2.9
subject to the initial condition
\Pi(0)=\mathds{V}\!\left[x\right]
.
By jointly solving
Equation

2.2
for the deterministic trajectory (the mean of
l_{t}^{(\varepsilon)}
) and
Equation

2.9
for the covariance matrix, one can easily compute the linearised solution, describing exactly the assumed Gaussian approximation presented in
Särkkä and Solin 2019
, and the dynamics linearisation used in the extended Kalman filter
[
18
]
.

2.3
Fixed initial condition

Consider when the initial condition
x
is itself a fixed and known deterministic value, in which case we take
x=x_{0}
and
\delta_{r}=0
for all
r
.
In this situation, the bound
Equation

2.4
on the linearisation error reduces to

\mathds{E}\!\left[\mathinner{\!\left\lVert y_{t}^{(\varepsilon)}-l_{t}^{(\varepsilon)}\right\rVert}^{r}\right]\leq\left(K_{\nabla\nabla u}^{r}+K_{\nabla\sigma}^{r}\right)D_{1}\!\left(r,t,K_{\nabla u},K_{\sigma}\right)\varepsilon^{2r}.

(2.15)

We can consider the linearisation as equivalently arising from a first-order power series expansion of
y_{t}^{(\varepsilon)}
in the noise-scale parameter
\varepsilon
, i.e.

y_{t}^{(\varepsilon)}=F_{0}^{t}\!\left(x_{0}\right)+\varepsilon z_{t}^{(\varepsilon)}+R_{2}\left(x,t,\varepsilon\right).

where
z_{\varepsilon}\coloneqq\left(l_{t}^{(\varepsilon)}-F_{0}^{t}\!\left(x_{0}\right)\right)/\varepsilon
is the first order term and
R_{2}
is a random quantity capturing the remaining deviation between
y_{t}^{(\varepsilon)}
and the linearisation.
By rearranging and taking
r=1
in
Equation

2.15
, we therefore have the explicit Taylor-like bound

\frac{\mathds{E}\!\left[\mathinner{\!\left\lVert R_{2}\left(x,t,\varepsilon\right)\right\rVert}\right]}{\varepsilon^{2}}\leq\left(K_{\nabla\nabla u}+K_{\nabla\sigma}\right)D_{1}\!\left(1,t,K_{\nabla u},K_{\sigma}\right),

This result is consistent with the formulation of the linearisation error bounds by
Blagoveshchenskii 1962
and
Freidlin and Wentzell 1998
, for instance.
Moreover, the distribution of the linearisation solution
Equation

2.5
is Gaussian, which through
Corollary

2.8
we can again explicitly characterise in terms of the deterministic system, namely

l_{t}^{(\varepsilon)}\sim\mathcal{N}\!\left(F_{0}^{t}\!\left(x_{0}\right),\varepsilon^{2}\Sigma_{0}^{t}\!\left(x_{0}\right)\right),

(2.16)

where
\Sigma_{0}^{t}\!\left(x_{0}\right)
is defined in
Equation

2.14
.
The distribution can be computed
entirely
from the solution behaviour of the deterministic equation
Equation

2.2
and prior specification of
\sigma
.
In
Section

3
, we demonstrate an application of these results to extend stochastic sensitivity
[
4
]
to arbitrary dimension.

3
Extending stochastic sensitivity

The results of
Section

2.3
for a fixed initial condition provide a direct extension of the stochastic sensitivity tools first introduced by
Balasuriya 2020
for the fluid flow context.
Here, the deterministic model
Equation

2.2
is seen as a “best-available” model for the evolution of Lagrangian trajectories, and the driving vector field
u
is the Eulerian velocity of the fluid.
Stochastic sensitivity ascribes a scalar value to each deterministic trajectory by computing a maximum variance of projected deviation
[
4
]
.
The aim is to provide a
single
computable number for each deterministic trajectory quantifying the impact of uncertainty in the velocity, independent of the scale (
\varepsilon
) of the noise.
The natural restating of this original definition of stochastic sensitivity
[
4
]
in the
n
-dimensional setting is as follows:

Definition 3.1

(Stochastic sensitivity in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
)
.

The
stochastic sensitivity
is a scalar field
S^{2}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathbb{R}^{n}\times[0,T]\to\left[0,\infty\right)
given by

S^{2}\!\left(x_{0},t\right)\coloneqq\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\sup\mathinner{\left\{\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\mathds{V}\!\left[p^{{}^{\!\!\intercal}}\left(y_{t}^{(\varepsilon)}-F_{0}^{t}\!\left(x_{0}\right)\right)\right]\,\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\,p\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\,\mathinner{\!\left\lVert p\right\rVert}=1\right\}}.

Definition

3.1
is in the spirit of principal components analysis
[
19
]
, performing a dimension reduction by projecting onto the direction in which the variance is maximised, thus capturing the most uncertainty in the data with a scalar value.
The anisotropic uncertainty in two-dimensions
[
4
]
is the direction-dependent projection (prior to optimising over all directions in
Definition

3.1
).
Explicit theoretical expressions for both the stochastic sensitivity and the anisotropic sensitivity in two dimensions were obtained by
Balasuriya 2020
; these allowed for quantifying certainty in eventual trajectory locations without having to perform stochastic simulations.
We show here that our results in
n
-dimensions are a generalisation of the two-dimensional ones in
[
4
]
, which moreover establish Gaussianity as well as an explicit expression for the uncertainty measure.
A theoretically pleasing and computable expression for the stochastic sensitivity is obtainable;

Theorem 3.2

(Computation of
S^{2}
)
.

For any
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
t\in[0,T]
,

S^{2}\!\left(x_{0},t\right)=\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\Sigma_{0}^{t}\!\left(x_{0}\right)\right\rVert},

(3.17)

where the covariance matrix
\Sigma_{0}^{t}
is defined in
Equation

2.14
.
Equivalently,
S^{2}\!\left(x_{0},t\right)
is given by the maximum eigenvalue of
\Sigma_{0}^{t}\!\left(x_{0}\right)
.

Proof 3.3
.

See
Appendix

E
.
This result uses
Theorem

2.1
to establish the convergence of the covariance matrices, and then the properties of the spectral norm to establish
Equation

3.17
.

Independent of the fluid mechanics context,
Theorem

3.2
indicates that even for general systems, the matrix norm of
\Sigma_{0}^{t}(x)
, i.e., the stochastic sensitivity
S^{2}(x,t)
, can be used as
one
number which encapsulates the uncertainty of an initial state
x
after
t
time units.
The significance of this result is that the stochastic sensitivity has here been recovered as the maximal eigenvalue of a covariance matrix that is ubiquitous in the literature.
Stochastic sensitivity was formerly defined in
[
4
]
as the maximal value of the anisotropic uncertainty of a particular stochastic flow, and the connection to a linearisation was not apparent.

The stochastic sensitivity field can be calculated given any velocity data
u
, and through the explicit expression
Equation

2.14
for
\Sigma_{0}^{t}
can even be computed from only flow map data.
Computation does not require knowledge of the noise scale
\varepsilon
, so the
S^{2}
field is intrinsic in capturing the impact of the model dynamics on uncertainty, and any specified non-uniform diffusivity.
It has already been shown that, in the fluid flow context, stochastic sensitivity can identify coherent regions in two-dimensions
[
3
,
4
]
.
A simple approach is to define robust sets, which are those initial conditions for which the corresponding
S^{2}
value, i.e., the uncertainty in eventual location, are below some specified threshold.
This threshold can be defined precisely in terms of a spatial lengthscale of interest and the advective and diffusive characteristics of the flow, as Definition 2.9 of
[
4
]
.
Such a definition extends to the
n
-dimensional case as presented here, moreover establishing an easily computable method for determining coherent sets from the covariance matrix
\Sigma_{0}^{t}
.

Fixed initial condition

In this example, we consider a two-dimensional model and a fixed initial condition, to validate the results presented in

Following the example in Chapter 5 of

The velocity field for

u\!\left(y,t\right)=\begin{bmatrix}c-A\sin\!\left(Ky_{1}\right)\cos\!\left(y_{2}\right)+\epsilon_{\mathrm{mj}}l_{1}\sin\!\left(k_{1}\left(y_{1}-c_{1}t\right)\right)\cos\!\left(l_{1}y_{2}\right)\\
AK\cos\!\left(Ky_{1}\right)\sin\!\left(y_{2}\right)+\epsilon_{\mathrm{mj}}k_{1}\cos\!\left(k_{1}\left(y_{1}-c_{1}t\right)\right)\sin\!\left(l_{1}y_{2}\right)\end{bmatrix}.

(4.26)

The velocity field describes a kinematic travelling wave with deterministic oscillatory perturbations in a co-moving frame.
Here,
A
is the amplitude and
c
is the phase speed of the primary wave, and
K
is the wavenumber in the
y_{1}
-direction.
The oscillatory perturbation has amplitude
\epsilon_{\mathrm{mj}}
, phase speed
c_{1}
(in the co-moving frame), and wavenumbers

and

in the
y_{1}

-directions respectively.
Throughout, we take the parameter values

,

,
K=4
,

,
k_{1}=1
,
c_{1}=\pi
, and
\epsilon_{\mathrm{mj}}=0.3
.
For these values, the flow consists of a meandering jet with vortex structures within the meanders, and a chaotic zone which influences the fluid transfer between the jet and the vortices.

We introduce multiplicative noise by considering stochastic perturbations to the phase speed

and the primary amplitude

W_{t}=\left(W_{t}^{(1)},W_{t}^{(2)}\right)^{{}^{\!\!\intercal}}

Then, we specify the diffusion term as

\sigma\!\left(y,t\right)=\begin{bmatrix}1&\sin\!\left(Ky_{1}\right)\cos\!\left(y_{2}\right)\\
0&K\cos\!\left(Ky_{1}\right)\sin\!\left(y_{2}\right)\end{bmatrix}.

(4.27)

We consider the fixed initial condition
x_{0}=\left(0,1\right)
and the prediction of the model at time

.
We then consider a linearisation of the SDE about the deterministic trajectory
F_{0}^{t}\!\left(x_{0}\right)
, where
F_{0}^{t}
is the deterministic flow map corresponding to the vector field
Equation

4.26
.
To compute the Gaussian distribution
Equation

2.16

Equation

5
Conclusions

This paper has provided an explicit bound on the error between the solution of a nonlinear stochastic differential equation and an easily computable linearisation approximation that has been used across many different applications
[
18
,
36
,
22
,
2
, e.g.]
, building upon previous studies
[
9
,
14
,
36
]
.
The theory applies to fully non-autonomous SDEs with multiplicative noise and a random initial condition.
Our bound is written in terms of the scale of the ongoing noise (that is, a scaling of the diffusivity coefficient) and a measure of the uncertainty in the initial condition using the
L_{r}
-norm.
A comparison in
Section

2.1
suggests that our bound on the moments is tighter than implied by the gold standard
[
36
]
in the literature.

We also provided an explicit characteristation of the distribution of the solution to the linearised SDE, enabling efficient approximation of the original nonlinear SDE using solutions to the corresponding deterministic equation, whereas previously special cases of these computations were dispersed across other literature
[
18
,
36
,
37
]
.
Finally, we validated the form of the bound numerically on three example stochastic differential equations, and in particular find that the strong error scales with the initial uncertainty and ongoing uncertainty exactly as predicted.

Our error bound may be useful to investigate the validity of SDE linearisations in application domains such as Stochastic Parameterisation
[
7
,
29
,
24
]
or Data Assimilation
[
13
,
33
,
23
,
16
]
, or to improve such linearisations by monitoring the skew or kurtosis.

This paper also supplied theoretical and computational extension to the “stochastic sensitivity” tools introduced by
Balasuriya 2020
.
Stochastic sensitivity was hitherto derived as the variance of an unknown limiting distribution and could only be computed in two spatial dimensions; we established that stochastic sensitivity, in any number of dimensions, is computable as the operator norm of the covariance matrix of the linearised SDE.
We have also established that the limiting distribution in question is Gaussian, which may provide insight into properties of stochastic sensitivity as a means of uncertainty quantification in any model (not just in the fluids context) where an
n
-dimensional state variable evolves according to a “best available” model.

Our work on stochastic sensitivity computes the exact distribution of a Lagrangian coherent structure measure for the first time, and may be used to employ coherent structures as ‘data’ in a Data Assimilation scheme. This idea is not new, and is explored in
[
25
,
27
,
38
]
, albeit that the likelihood of a coherent structure was not computable. Our work fills that gap.
Moreover, most traditional LCS measures are completely deterministic measures, not accounting for any uncertainty in the driving velocity field, and the sensitivity of these methods to such uncertainty has not been investigated in detail.
The robustness of several LCS methods to stochastic noise has recently been explored in
[
3
]
, but via stochastic simulation and summary statistics.
In this paper we have presented a theoretical result for characterising Lagrangian trajectory uncertainty, which can be used to perform a purely theoretical analysis of such sensitivity in LCS computations.

Appendix A
Preliminaries for proofs

There are several generic results and inequalities that we use several times throughout our proofs, which we state here for completeness.
We write
W_{t}=\left(W_{t}^{(1)},\ldots,W_{t}^{(m)}\right)^{{}^{\!\!\intercal}}
as the components of the canonical
m
-dimensional Wiener process, where each
W_{t}^{(i)}
are mutually independent 1-dimensional Wiener processes.
The flow map
F_{0}^{t}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
summarises solutions of the deterministic model
Equation

2.2
, given by

F_{0}^{t}(x)=x+\int_{0}^{t}{u\left(F_{0}^{\tau}(x),\tau\right)\dif\tau},

(1.28)

for an initial condition
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
The spatial gradient (with respect to the initial condition) of the flow map solves the equation of variations associated with
Equation

2.2
, i.e.

\mathinner{\dfrac{\partial{}}{\partial{t}}}\nabla F_{0}^{t}(x)=\nabla u\left(F_{0}^{t}(x),t\right)\nabla F_{0}^{t}(x).

(1.29)

For any real numbers
x_{1},\ldots,x_{p}\geq 0
and
r\geq 1
,

\left(\sum_{i=1}^{p}{x_{i}}\right)^{r}\leq p^{r-1}\sum_{i=1}^{p}{x_{i}^{r}}.

(1.30)

This results from an application of the finite form of Jensen’s inequality.
An implication of the equivalence of the
L_{1}
and Euclidean norms and
Equation

1.30
is that for any
z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
r\geq 1
,

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert z\right\rVert}^{r}\leq\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\mathinner{\!\left\lvert z_{i}\right\rvert}}\right)^{r}\leq n^{r-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\mathinner{\!\left\lvert z_{i}\right\rvert}^{r}},

(1.31)

where
z_{i}
denotes the
i
th component of
z
.
If each component
z_{i}
of a vector
z
is bounded by a constant
K
, then

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert z\right\rVert}\leq\sqrt{n}K.

(1.32)

Similarly, if
f\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a vector-valued function such that each component of
f
is integrable over an interval
[0,t]
, then for all
r\geq 1
,

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\int_{0}^{t}{f\left(\tau\right)\dif\tau}\right\rVert}^{r}\leq t^{r-1}\int_{0}^{t}{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert f\left(\tau\right)\right\rVert}^{r}\dif\tau}.

(1.33)

This inequality results from an application of Hölder’s inequality.

Proof B.2
.

Next, we establish a bound on the Itô integral term in

For
