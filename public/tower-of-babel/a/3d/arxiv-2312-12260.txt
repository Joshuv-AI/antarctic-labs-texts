Connectedness in weighted consensus division of graphical cakes between two agents
Josef Hanke, Ana Rita Pires

Abstract
Austin’s moving knife procedure was originally introduced to find a consensus division of a cake between two agents: each agent believes that they receive exactly half of the cake. Here, we adapt it to the case when the two agents have arbitrary entitlements of the cake and seek a weighted consensus division – one where each agent believes that they received exactly the share that they are entitled to – which also minimizes the number of connected components that each agent receives. First, we find a weighted consensus division of a circular cake, which gives exactly one connected piece to each agent: this gives an explicit protocol for the Stromquist-Woodall theorem for two agents. Next, by judiciously mapping a circle to a graph, we produce a weighted consensus division of a star graph cake that gives at most two connected pieces to each agent – and show that this bound on the number of connected pieces is tight. For trees, each agent receives at most h + 1 h+1 connected pieces, where h h is the height of the tree, and for general (connected) graphical cakes each agent receives r + 2 r+2 connected pieces, where r r is the radius of the graph.



Keywords —
Fair division, consensus division, exact division, graphical cakes, weighted division.

1
Introduction

Fair division refers to the problem of allocating resources among individuals with different preferences, according to some notion of fairness. The simplest model is divide-and-choose: to divide a resource (metaphorically, a cake) evenly between two agents, have the first agent cut it into two and the second choose which piece they prefer. The first agent will receive a piece they view as half the (value of) cake, but the second agent will most likely receive a piece they value as more than half.

Austin’s moving knife procedure solves this inequity: the first agent places two knives, one at the left edge of the cake and another parallel to it such that (in their view) half of the cake lies between the knives. She then moves the two knives continuously to the right, always keeping what she views as half of the cake between the knives until the second agent identifies a point where he agrees that the piece between the knives (and thus the piece(s) outside the knives) is worth half of the cake
[
Au
,
BT
,
RW
]
.
Each agent assigns the same value to each of the pieces (in this case with a value of
\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
); this is called an
exact division
or
consensus division
.
Note that this is stronger than the well-studied
proportional division

[
St
]
, where each agent values their piece at least
\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
(more generally,
\nicefrac{{1}}{{n}}
in the case of
n
agents)
[
GL
]
.
In general, a consensus division splits a resource into
k
pieces, such that
n
different agents agree on the valuation of each piece: the valuations of the
k
pieces need not be all equal to
\nicefrac{{1}}{{k}}
, they just need to add up to 1. We focus here on the case of
n=k=2
, and call it
weighted consensus
to stress that the valuation of the two pieces need not be
\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
and
\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
. Indeed, Austin’s moving knife procedure can be modified to produce a consensus division when the two agents have different (rational) entitlements
[
RW
]
and further modified for other situations. For example, Brams, Taylor, and Zwicker give a procedure that produces an envy-free division (not consensus) among four agents
[
BTZ
]
, and Shishido and Zeng give one that produces a proportional division of a circular cake among an arbitrary number of agents
[
SZ
]
.

In this paper, we modify Austin’s moving knife procedure to produce a consensus division of a cake between two agents with arbitrary entitlements. We consider cakes with increasingly complex shapes – first a circle, then star graphs, then general graphs – and put a bound on the number of connected components that each agent may receive. These various aspects reflect natural constraints in possible real-world applications. Graphical cakes model road or power cable networks, and it makes sense to try to give each agent a piece that is as connected as possible. The division of graphical cakes has been studied by various authors. For different types of graphs and numbers of agents, Bei and Suksompong
[
BS
]
investigated what the most proportional allocation possible with the fewest connected components given to each agent can be, whereas Yuen and Suksompong
[
MS
]
looked instead at connected allocations with low envy. Consensus divisions may feel fairer to the agents than other, usual definitions of fairness (e.g., envy-free, proportional, equitable), and have been studied in various contexts. Simmons and Su
[
SS
]
studied consensus-halving (i.e.
k=2
with a 50-50 split, arbitrary
n
) of interval cakes with pieces as connected as possible, Goldberg and Li looked at the weighted version of consensus-halving
[
GL
]
, and Alon and West
[
A
,
AW
]
investigated the related problem of necklace splitting (where each agent assigns value only to the beads of a different colour on a section of a necklace).

This paper gives algorithms which produce weighted consensus divisions between two agents, with a bound on the number of connected components each agent receives, in the following cases:

•

For circular cakes, with each agent receiving one connected piece. The existence is a direct result of Stromquist-Woodall theorem
[
SW
, Theorem 1]
for two agents; see Section
3
for an alternative proof.

•

For star cakes, with each agent receiving at most two connected pieces (Section
4
). This bound is sharp.

•

For tree cakes, with each agent receiving at most
h+1
connected pieces, where
h
is the height of the tree (Section
5
).

•

For graph cakes, with each agent receiving at most
r+2
connected pieces, where
r
is the radius of the graph (Section
6
).

A repeated application of the Stromquist-Woodall theorem guarantees the existence of a weighted consensus division of a circle cake among
n
agents that requires at most
(n-1)(2n-2)
cuts. The study of weighted consensus division of non-circular cakes between
n\geq 3
agents remains an open question.

2
Setup

Consider a cake
C
represented by a connected finite graph, and two agents
A
and
B
represented by the valuation functions
V_{A}
and
V_{B}
and a vector
(t,1-t)
of their respective entitlements, where
0\leq t\leq 1
is arbitrary.
The valuation functions
V_{A}
and
V_{B}
are given by non-atomic probability measures, which ensures that single isolated points can be allocated to both agents without affecting their valuation of their share. Thus, it makes sense to define a division of the cake
C
between the two agents as a pair
(\alpha,\beta)
such that
\alpha\cup\beta=C
and
\alpha\cap\beta
consists of finitely many points.

Definition 2.1
.

A division
(\alpha,\beta)
is
a
weighted consensus division
if both agents believe that they (and therefore the other agent) have been allocated exactly their entitlement:

V_{A}(\alpha)=V_{B}(\alpha)=t\ \text{ and }\ V_{A}(\beta)=V_{B}(\beta)=1-t.

Remark 2.2
.

Note that the valuation functions can assign a value of zero to open subsets of the cake. This requires a more subtle use of the Intermediate Value Theorem in the argument of Austin’s moving knife procedure than otherwise, and we detail that here because we did not find it elsewhere in the literature. Let
C=[0,1]
be an interval cake and the entitlement vector be
(t,1-t)=(\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}},\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}})
; we follow Austin’s moving knife procedure described in
[
Au
, p.214-215]
and summarized above in Section
1
. We let
c
denote the position of the first knife and
\kappa(c)
the position of the second knife, positioned by
A
such that
V_{A}\bigl([c,\kappa(c)]\bigr)=\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}.

Implicit in the definition of the
V_{P}
valuation function as a probability measure are the probability density and cumulative distribution functions
f_{P}
and
F_{P}
respectively. If we do not allow a value of zero to be assigned to open subsets of the cake,
F_{P}
is strictly monotone increasing and thus has a well-defined continuous inverse. In this case, the position of the second knife can indeed be viewed as a continuous function of the position of the first:

\kappa(c)=F_{A}^{-1}\bigl(\frac{1}{2}+F_{A}(c)\bigr).

(2.3)

The Intermediate Value Theorem is then applied to the following continuous function:

c\mapsto V_{B}\bigl([c,\kappa(c)]\bigr)=F_{B}\bigl(\kappa(c)\bigr)-F_{B}\bigl(c\bigr).

(2.4)

Figure 2.5
:
If the probability density function of
A
has a zero-valued interval as in the example on the graph on the left-hand side, then the cumulative distribution function will be constant on the same interval as on the graph on the right-hand side. In that case, the function
\kappa
in (
2.3
) is not well defined. Indeed, at the point in the procedure where the knives are at the locations indicated in red on the graph on the right, keeping
c
constant and increasing slightly the value denoted in the figure by
\kappa(c)
would still yield
V_{A}\bigl([c,\kappa(c)]\bigr)=\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
.

Figure
2.5
illustrates the problem with allowing
f_{P}
to vanish on an interval. In practical terms, in many situations it may be perfectly fine to edit the agents’ probability density functions slightly, assigning a small positive value to any vanishing intervals and rescaling the rest of the function accordingly. Alternatively, it is possible to modify the moving knife procedure in order to ensure that a continuous function
\kappa(c)
can be defined and the Intermediate Value Theorem can be applied to the function (
2.4
). The modification is as follows:

Move the knives as in Austin’s moving knife procedure until the rightmost knife reaches a point
\kappa(c)
where

f_{A}\bigl(\kappa(c)\bigr)=0.

Stop moving the left knife, and continue moving the right knife until
f_{A}\bigl(\kappa(c)\bigr)>0
. Start moving both knives again and repeat this procedure until the whole cake is covered.

3
Circular cakes

A circle can be modelled by a graph consisting of one vertex and edge connecting that vertex to itself: the edge is
[0,1]
and the vertex is
0=1
.

The Stromquist-Woodall theorem
[
SW
, Theorem 1]
in the case of
n=2
agents guarantees the existence of a subset of a circle cake that both agents value as
t
, for any given
0\leq t\leq 1
, requiring only
2n-2=2
cuts. This implies that the agents also agree on the value of the remainder of the cake, and therefore guarantees the existence of a weighted consensus division with entitlements
t
and
1-t
, in which both agents receive a connected piece. The existence of such a division suffices for the proofs of the results in Sections
4
–
6
, but we present here a more explicit proof for our particular case of two agents.

, as noted by an anonymous referee.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let two agents be owed arbitrary entitlements of a circular cake. There exists a weighted consensus division of the cake such that each agent’s piece is connected.

Proof.

Let the entitlement vector be
(t,1-t)
and denote the circular cake by
\Theta
. When writing an interval
[a,b]\in\Theta
, we mean as usual the interval
[a,b]\subset[0,1]
if
a\leq b
and
[a,1]\cup[0,b]
if
a>b
. This implies

V_{P}\left([a,b]\right)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\int_{a}^{b}f_{P}(\theta)\ d\theta&\mbox{if }a\leq b\\
1-\int_{b}^{a}f_{P}(\theta)\ d\theta&\mbox{if }a>b\end{array}\right.

for each agent
P=A,B
.

Agent
A
places two knives on the cake; the first at
c=0
and the second at
\kappa(0)
such that they value the piece of cake between the knives as their entitlement
t
. Then the first knife is moved around the cake anticlockwise, and
A
moves the second knife such that they consistently value the cake between the two knives as equal to
t
. This defines the position of the second knife as a function of the position of the first knife:
\kappa:\Theta\rightarrow\Theta
such that
V_{A}\bigl([c,\kappa(c)]\bigr)=t
. As noted in Remark
2.2
, if
f_{A}
is strictly positive then
\kappa
is well-defined, otherwise we can modify how to move the second knife; this is explained in the last paragraph of Remark
2.2
.

We claim that at some point agent
B
also values the cake between the knives as
t
. By contradiction, assume otherwise. By continuity of
c\mapsto V_{B}\bigl([c,\kappa(c)]\bigr)
, we can assume that for all
c\in\Theta
we have

V_{B}\bigl([c,\kappa(c)]\bigr)>t
. By compactness of
\Theta
, this means that there exists an
\varepsilon>0
such that
V_{B}\bigl([c,\kappa(c)]\bigr)\geq t+\varepsilon.

We lift the probability density functions
f_{P}:\Theta\to\mathbb{R}
to
\widetilde{f_{P}}:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
by defining
\widetilde{f_{P}}(x+1)=\widetilde{f_{P}}(x)
and obtain corresponding cummulative distribution functions
\widetilde{F_{P}}:\mathbb{R}\to R
which satisfy

\widetilde{F_{P}}(x+1)=\int_{0}^{x+1}\widetilde{f_{P}}(\theta)\ d\theta=\int_{0}^{x}\widetilde{f_{P}}(\theta)\ d\theta+\int_{x}^{x+1}\widetilde{f_{P}}(\theta)\ d\theta=\widetilde{F_{P}}(x)+1.

As a consequence, the difference
\bigl(\widetilde{F_{A}}-\widetilde{F_{B}}\bigr):\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is periodic with period 1
and thus descends to a function
\bigl(F_{A}-F_{B}\bigr):\Theta\rightarrow\mathbb{R}.
By compactness of
\Theta
there exists an upper bound
M
such that
|\bigl(F_{A}-F_{B}\bigr)|\leq M
on
\Theta
.

The function
\kappa:\Theta\to\Theta
also induces a function
\widetilde{\kappa}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
in the obvious way. We use it to inductively define a sequence
(\widetilde{c_{i}})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathbb{R}
via
\widetilde{c_{0}}=0
and
\widetilde{c_{i+1}}=\widetilde{\kappa}(\widetilde{c_{i}})
, which then induces a corresponding sequence
(c_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\Theta
.

Note that by construction
\widetilde{F_{A}}(\widetilde{c_{i+1}})-\widetilde{F_{A}}(\widetilde{c_{i}})=t
and
\widetilde{F_{B}}(\widetilde{c_{i+1}})-\widetilde{F_{B}}(\widetilde{c_{i}})=V_{B}\bigl([c_{i},c_{i+1}]\bigr)\geq t+\varepsilon
. Now

\bigl(F_{A}-F_{B}\bigr)(c_{i+1})=\widetilde{F_{A}}(\widetilde{c_{i+1}})-\widetilde{F_{B}}(\widetilde{c_{i+1}})\leq\widetilde{F_{A}}(\widetilde{c_{i}})-\widetilde{F_{B}}(\widetilde{c_{i}})-\varepsilon=\bigl(F_{A}-F_{B}\bigr)(c_{i})-\varepsilon

which implies

\bigl(F_{A}-F_{B}\bigr)(c_{i})\leq\bigl(F_{A}-F_{B}\bigr)(0)-i\varepsilon
, contradicting the bound
|\bigl(F_{A}-F_{B}\bigr)|\leq M
.

∎

4
Star cakes

We now turn to the case where the cake can be modelled by a star graph. An (
l
-)
star graph
is a tree on
l+1
nodes with one node having vertex degree
l
and the other
l
vertices having degree one. We assume that
l
is at least three since, for cake-cutting purposes,
1
-stars and
2
-stars can be modelled as interval cakes, in which case one agent receives one connected piece and the other gets at most two (by applying Theorem
3.1
without identifying the endpoints of
[0,1]
).

Theorem 4.1
.

Let two agents be owed arbitrary entitlements of a star graph. There exists a weighted consensus division of the cake such that each agent receives at most two connected pieces.

Figure 4.2
:
The 4-star graph rearranged as a circle.

Proof.

The star graph
\Gamma
can be represented as a disjoint union of
l
edges
e_{k}=[v_{k},u_{k}]
,
k=0,1,\ldots,l-1
, under the identification of every vertex
u_{k}
into a single centre vertex
R
:

\Gamma\simeq\bigcup_{k=0}^{l-1}e_{k}/\sim\qquad\mbox{where}\qquad u_{0}\sim u_{1}\sim\ldots\sim u_{l-1}=:R.

We rearrange the edges
e_{k}
to form a circle, see Figure
4.2
:

\Theta\simeq\bigcup_{k=0}^{l-1}e_{k}/\sim^{\prime}\qquad\mbox{where}\qquad u_{k}\sim^{\prime}v_{k+1}.

The agents’ valuation functions on
\Gamma
induce valuation functions on
\Theta
.
Theorem
3.1
gives a weighted consensus division of
\Theta
where each agent’s piece is connected, which induces a weighted consensus division of
\Gamma
, possibly with the centre vertex
R
being assigned to both agents.

Figure 4.3
:
Case (i) with
c^{e}
and
\kappa(c^{e})
on the same branch. Case (ii) with
c^{e}
and
\kappa(c^{e})
on different branches.

It remains to show that this division gives each agent at most two connected pieces. The counterclockwise movement of the knives along the circle
\Theta
translates to moving the knives along each of the edges
e_{0},e_{1},\ldots,e_{l-1}
, outwards from the centre vertex
R
. There are two distinct cases to consider: either (i) the two cuts,
c^{e}
and
\kappa(c^{e})
, are on the same edge of
\Gamma
, or (ii) they are on different edges; see Figure
4.3
.

Case (i):

In this case, one agent receives the single connected piece
[c^{e},\kappa(c^{e})]\subset e_{i}
, while the other receives two pieces: the outer remainder of the edge
e_{i}
, which is
[\kappa(c^{e}),v_{i}]
, plus the rest of the graph (including
[u_{i},c^{e}]
, the inner remainder of the edge
e_{i}
) that is connected through
R
.

Case (ii):
In this case, the cuts
c^{e}
and
\kappa(c^{e})
are on two distinct edges,
e_{i}
and
e_{j}
respectively,
i<j
. Agent
A
receives the piece
[c^{e},v_{i}]
as well as the following piece, connected through
R
:

[u_{j},\kappa(c^{e})]\cup\bigcup_{k=i+1}^{j-1}e_{k}.

Agent
B
receives the connected piece
[\kappa(c^{e}),v_{j}]
as well as the following piece, connected through
R
:

[u_{i},c^{e}]\cup\bigcup_{k=j+1}^{i-1}e_{k}.

∎

To conclude this section, we show with Example
4.5
that Theorem
4.1
is sharp in the sense that it is not generally possible to guarantee a consensus division such that one of the agents gets at most one piece while the other one gets at most two pieces. Whether this bound is sharp for a weighted proportional division of a star graph (recall that a weighted consensus division is also a weighted proportional division) remains an open question.

Example 4.5
.

Let
l=3
and
t=\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
, and consider the value functions represented in Figure
4.4
. The corresponding distribution functions must vanish at various points, but in practice, we can modify the functions slightly to avoid the type of complications described in Remark
2.2
.
Assume, towards a contradiction, that there exists a consensus division
(\alpha,\beta)
of
\Gamma
such that one of the agents receives one connected piece and the other receives two exists.
Agent
A
must receive a piece touching at least two of
\{a_{0},a_{1},a_{2}\}
, otherwise the value of her piece is at most
\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}
. Agent
B
must also receive a piece touching at least two of
\{a_{0},a_{1},a_{2}\}
, otherwise
A
’s value is at least
\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}
.
Thus, as we assumed one of
A
or
B
receives a connected piece, one of them must receive at least two of
\{b_{0},b_{1},b_{2}\}
entirely. If it is
B
, then
B
values his piece at least
\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}
; if it is
A
, then
B
’s value is at most
\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}
. In both cases, the allocation is not a consensus allocation.

Note that we can find a proportional division of the same star graph cake by letting
\alpha=a_{0}\cup a_{1}
(two connected pieces) and
\beta=\Gamma\setminus\alpha
(one connected piece). In this case,
V_{A}(\alpha)=\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}\geq\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
and
V_{B}(\beta)=1\geq\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
.

5
Tree cakes

Next, we look at the case when the cake can be modelled by a tree – a connected graph that has no cycles. The method of mapping between a circle and a star graph used in the proof of Theorem
4.1
can be extended to a tree graph: we define a
tree tracing trajectory
that sweeps along each edge of the tree graph exactly once, and can then be used to rearrange the edges of the tree into a circle that is traced counterclockwise from 0.

Let
\Gamma
be a tree graph of height
h
, with root vertex
R
. Label the subtrees below
R
as
\gamma_{0},...\gamma_{l-1}
, each with a leading edge
E_{k}
connecting
\gamma_{k}
to
R
; see Figure
5.2
. Note that each of these subtrees has height at most
h-1
. For trees of height
h=1
, each subtree
\gamma_{k}
is just a vertex
v_{k}
, and the tree tracing trajectory covers each edge
E_{k}
outwards, as in Theorem
4.1
: first from the root
R
towards
v_{0}
, then from
R
to
v_{1}
, and so on. For trees of height
h>1
, we define the tree tracing trajectory recursively: trace the trajectory for the subtree
\gamma_{0}
starting at its root
R_{0}
, then trace the edge
E_{0}
inwards from
R_{0}
to
R
. Next, trace the trajectory for the subtree
\gamma_{1}
, then the edge
E_{1}
inwards, and so on until all subtrees and edges
E_{k}
have been covered.

Lemma 5.1
.

Any point in the tree tracing trajectory divides the tree into two sections: the section already traced has at most
h
connected pieces, and the other one has at most 2 connected pieces (at most one of which is not connected to the root).

Proof.

We proceed by complete induction on the height
h
of the tree
\Gamma
.

For trees of height
h=1
, the already traced section is connected through
R
. The other section has at most one connected piece which is not connected to
R
, thus at most two connected components.

Now let
\Gamma
be a rooted tree of height
h
. We consider separately the cases of (i) the point in the tree tracing trajectory being on an edge
E_{i}
emanating from the root
R
or (ii) it being on a subtree
\gamma_{i}
of
\Gamma
; see Figure
5.2
.

Figure 5.2
:
The two possible cases of a point in the tree tracing trajectory, as indicated by the red line. The traced section is in pink and the untraced section in black.

Case (i):
In this case, the traced section of the tree consists of two connected pieces. One is
\bigcup_{k=0}^{i-1}\gamma_{k}
which is connected through
R
. The other is all of
\gamma_{i}
together with the traced part of the edge
E_{i}
. The untraced section consists of
\bigcup_{k=i+1}^{l-1}\gamma_{k}
together with the untraced part of the edge
E_{i}
, which are connected through
R
.

Case (ii):
In this case, the traced part of the tree forms at most
h
connected pieces. Specifically,
\bigcup_{k=0}^{i-1}\gamma_{k}

is connected through
R
and forms the first connected piece. The traced part of
\gamma_{i}
has at most
h-1
connected pieces by induction. The untraced part of
\Gamma
contains at most two connected pieces. This is because the untraced part of the subtree
\gamma_{i}
has at most two connected pieces, again by induction. One of these pieces always contains
E_{i}
(and thus the root
R
) and this is connected to the other subtrees
\bigcup_{k=i+1}^{l-1}\gamma_{k}
forming the rest of the untraced section of
\Gamma
.
∎

Theorem 5.3
.

Let two agents be owed arbitrary entitlements of a tree graph of height
h
. There exists a weighted consensus division of the cake such that each agent receives at most
h+1
connected pieces.

Proof.

The tree tracing trajectory on a tree
\Gamma
translates the agents’ valuations on
\Gamma
to valuations on a circle. Theorem
3.1
cuts the circle at two points to produce a weighted consensus division of the circle. Via the tree tracing trajectory, we obtain two cuts on
\Gamma
and corresponding weighted consensus division. We claim that this division results in at most
h+1
connected components for each agent.

We proceed by complete induction on
h
. The base case of
h=1
is Theorem
4.1
.

Now let
\Gamma
be a rooted tree of height
h
. There are two cases of where the cuts could lie on
\Gamma
: either
(i) the two cuts are on the same subtree,
\gamma_{i}
or
(ii) each cut is on a different subtree,
\gamma_{i}
and
\gamma_{j}
.

Case (i):

By the induction hypothesis,
\gamma_{i}
can be assigned to the agents such that each agent receives at most
h
connected components. Each of the other subtrees is wholy assigned to one agent. As all the subtrees are connected through
R
, the agent assigned the part of
E_{i}
connected to
R
receives at most
h
connected pieces, and the other agent receives at most
h+1
pieces.

Case (ii):

In this case, the end of the leading branches
E_{i}
and
E_{j}
of
\gamma_{i}
and
\gamma_{j}
connected to
R
are assigned to different agents, let us say
A
and
B
respectively. By Lemma
5.1
, the subtree
\gamma_{i}
is divided such that
A
gets at most two pieces, and
B
gets at most
h-1
pieces. For
\gamma_{j}
, this is the opposite. As all the
\gamma_{k}
’s are connected through
R
, and each of the other subtrees is assigned wholy to one agent, overall each agent gets at most
h+1
pieces.
∎

6
Graph cakes

Finally, we tackle the case of a general (connected) graph
G
. Recall that a
spanning tree
of a connected graph
G
is a subgraph of
G
that is a tree (a connected graph without cycles) and includes all of the vertices of
G
. It can be obtained by inductively deleting edges of
G
that form cycles in
G
. We define a related notion:

Definition 6.1
.

Given a connected graph
G
, an
edge-spanning tree
of
G
is any tree obtained by inductively detaching edges that form cycles in
G
from one of their endpoints and adding a vertex to serve as a new endpoint.

The result is a tree that contains as many edges as
G
had. Equivalently, an edge-spanning tree of
G
can be constructed (not uniquely) from a given spanning tree of
G
by augmenting it: for each edge
(u,v)
