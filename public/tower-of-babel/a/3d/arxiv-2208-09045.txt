Monte Carlo is a good sampling strategy for polynomial approximation in high dimensions Thanks: Submitted to the editors DATE.
Ben Adcock, Simone Brugiapaglia

Abstract
This paper concerns the approximation of smooth, high-dimensional functions from limited samples using polynomials. This task lies at the heart of many applications in computational science and engineering – notably, some of those arising from parametric modelling and computational uncertainty quantification. It is common to use Monte Carlo sampling in such applications, so as not to succumb to the curse of dimensionality. However, it is well known that such a strategy is theoretically suboptimal. Specifically, there are many polynomial spaces of dimension n n for which the sample complexity scales log-quadratically, i.e., like c ⋅ n 2 ⋅ log ⁡ ( n ) c\cdot n^{2}\cdot\log(n) as n → ∞ n\rightarrow\infty . This well-documented phenomenon has led to a concerted effort over the last decade to design improved, and moreover, near-optimal strategies, whose sample complexities scale log-linearly, or even linearly in n n . In this work we demonstrate that Monte Carlo is actually a perfectly good strategy in high dimensions, despite its apparent suboptimality. We first document this phenomenon empirically via a systematic set of numerical experiments. Next, we present a theoretical analysis that rigorously justifies this fact in the case of holomorphic functions of infinitely-many variables. We show that there is a least-squares approximation based on m m Monte Carlo samples whose error decays algebraically fast in m / log ⁡ ( m ) m/\log(m) , with a rate that is the same as that of the best n n -term polynomial approximation. This result is non-constructive, since it assumes knowledge of a suitable polynomial subspace in which to perform the approximation. We next present a compressed sensing-based scheme that achieves the same rate, except for a larger polylogarithmic factor. This scheme is practical, and numerically it performs as well as or better than well-known adaptive least-squares schemes. Overall, our findings in this paper demonstrate that Monte Carlo sampling is a good choice for polynomial approximation in high dimensions and shed light on why this is the case. Therefore, the benefits of improved sampling strategies are generically limited to lower-dimensional settings.

Introduction

Approximating a smooth function
f:\mathcal{U}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
from (noisy) sample values

f(\bm{y}_{i})+e_{i},\quad i=1,\ldots,m,\qquad\text{where }\bm{y}_{1},\ldots\bm{y}_{m}\in\mathcal{U},

(1)

is a task of fundamental importance in computational science and engineering. This task is well understood in low dimensions. But many modern applications
[
47
,
49
]
call for the approximation of functions depending on many (and potentially infinitely many) variables. Such high-dimensional approximation problems occur in many fields, for example in parametric modelling and computational Uncertainty Quantification (UQ). Here, smooth, high-dimensional functions commonly arise as solution maps of parametric Differential Equations (DEs).

Methods based on polynomials have proved to be effective tools for approximating such functions. Both classical techniques such as Least Squares (LS) and more recent tools such as Compressed Sensing (CS) have been intensively investigated over the last several decades. See §
1.5
for a historical discussion. Although by no means the only choice, these tools have been quite widely adopted in the aforementioned applications.

1.1
Monte Carlo sampling and sample complexity

This paper is about the choice of sampling strategy for polynomial approximation in high
dimensions. In any approximation scheme, the choice of sample points
\bm{y}_{1},\ldots,\bm{y}_{m}
is of singular importance. Obtaining samples is often a key bottleneck in applications, since they may require time- or resource-consuming numerical simulations or physical experiments. Thus, it is vital to choose sample points in a judicious manner, so as to facilitate accurate and stable approximations from as few samples as possible.

Care is needed when selecting a sampling strategy for a high-dimensional problem so as not to succumb to the
curse of dimensionality
. Taking inspiration from high-dimensional quadrature, it is common to use
Monte Carlo (MC)
sampling in polynomial approximation schemes, i.e., the points
\bm{y}_{1},\ldots,\bm{y}_{m}
are drawn randomly and independently from some underlying probability measure. Such sampling strategies also occur naturally in UQ settings, where the variables are stochastic.

MC sampling is a remarkably effective technique for high-dimensional integration. Yet, in the context of high-dimensional polynomial approximation, MC sampling suffers from a critical limitation. The
sample complexity
– the number of samples
m
required for a stable and accurate approximation – usually scales poorly with the problem dimension
d
or approximation space dimension
n
. For example, if
\mathcal{U}=[-1,1]^{d}
and the underlying measure is the uniform measure, then there are polynomial subspaces of dimension
n
for which the sample complexity scales
log-quadratically
, i.e.,

m=c\cdot n^{2}\cdot\log(n)

(2)

for some
c>0
. See, e.g.,
[
7
,
39
,
12
]
. Note that
n
also scales exponentially with
d
for many classical approximation spaces, such as those corresponding to
tensor-product
,
total degree
, or
hyperbolic cross
index sets (see §
2.3
). As a result, (
2
) implies that
m
may need to grow twice as exponentially fast in
d
as
n
for these choices.

1.3
Aims of this paper

The previous discussion suggests that MC is a bad sampling strategy for polynomial approximation. And indeed, almost all of the above strategies yield significant benefits over MC sampling in low dimensions, where the dimension
d
may be on the order of five or less. However, such benefits have been consistently observed to lessen as the dimension increases. When
d
is on the order of 10 or more, the performance gap between MC sampling and any improved sampling strategy is often strikingly less. This is even the case for the near-optimal sampling strategies, in spite of their theoretical optimality.

1.4
Nontechnical summary of main theoretical results

Our theoretical analysis centres on polynomial approximation of holomorphic functions of infinitely-many variables. Specifically, we combine the theory of
best
n
-term polynomial approximation
of such functions with the theories of LS and CS.

1.4.1
Polynomial approximation theory in infinite dimensions

The topic of polynomial approximation theory for infinite-dimensional functions has developed over the last decade or so, motivated by applications in parametric DEs and UQ.
See
[
13
]
and
[
2
, Chpts. 3 & 4]
for reviews. A key result in this area is the assertion of certain
algebraic
rates of convergence for best
n
-term polynomial approximations to certain classes of holomorphic, infinite-dimensional functions.

Consider the infinite hypercube
\mathcal{U}=[-1,1]^{\mathbb{N}}
equipped with the uniform measure
\varrho
and let
L^{2}_{\varrho}(\mathcal{U})
be the Lebesgue space of (complex-valued) square-integrable functions
[-1,1]^{\mathbb{N}}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
. (For the reader who is unfamiliar with concepts of infinite-dimensional measures and function spaces, we refer to §
5
). Any function
f\in L^{2}_{\varrho}(\mathcal{U})
has an infinite expansion with respect to multivariate orthogonal polynomials, which in this case are tensor products of the univariate Legendre polynomials. The
best
n
-term approximation
to
f
is the polynomial formed simply by picking the largest
n
terms of this expansion in absolute value. In this paper, we denote this approximation as
f_{n}
.

Now let
0<p<1
,
\varepsilon>0
and
\bm{b}\in\ell^{p}(\mathbb{N})
. Then a key result in this area is that there is a class
\mathcal{H}(\bm{b},\varepsilon)\subset L^{2}_{\varrho}(\mathcal{U})

of holomorphic functions within which the best
n
-term approximation converges algebraically with rate depending on
1/p
. Specifically,

{\|f-f_{n}\|}_{L^{2}_{\varrho}(\mathcal{U})}\leq C(\bm{b},\varepsilon,p)\cdot n^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p}},\quad\forall f\in\mathcal{H}(\bm{b},\varepsilon),\ n\in\mathbb{N}.

(4)

In other words, there are classes of functions whose approximation is free from the curse of dimensionality: despite depending on infinitely many variables, they can be approximated with algebraic rate by a finite (
n
-term) polynomial approximation.

The class
\mathcal{H}(\bm{b},\varepsilon)
, which is known as the class of (unit-norm)
(\bm{b},\varepsilon)
-holomorphic
functions
[
9
,
45
]
, is both theoretically interesting, since it defines a class where algebraic rates are obtained,
and
practically relevant. It was first introduced to describe the parametric regularity of the solutions of certain parametric DEs. It is now known that several important families
of parametric DEs have parametric solution maps that belong to such classes (for suitable
\bm{b}
,
\varepsilon
depending on the DE). These include parametric diffusion equations, parametric heat equations, PDEs over parametrized domains, and parametric initial value problems with affine parametric dependence. See §
for some further discussion.

Note that
(\bm{b},\varepsilon)
-holomorphic functions are
anisotropic
. As we see in §
5
, the
j
th component
b_{j}
of the sequence
\bm{b}
effectively controls the degree of smoothness of functions in
\mathcal{H}(\bm{b},\varepsilon)
with respect to the
j
th variable
y_{j}
.

1.4.2
First contribution: sharpness of the rate (
4
)

In our first contribution, Theorem
11
, we show that the rate (
4
) is effectively sharp. In particular, there exist sequences
\bm{b}\in\ell^{p}(\mathbb{N})
and infinitely many functions
f\in\mathcal{H}(\bm{b},\varepsilon)
for which
n^{1/2-1/p}
is the best possible algebraic rate of convergence.

1.4.3
Second contribution: near-optimal approximation in the case of known anisotropy

Our other two theoretical results concern constructing polynomial approximations to functions in
\mathcal{H}(\bm{b},\varepsilon)
from MC samples, i.e.,
\bm{y}_{1},\ldots,\bm{y}_{m}\sim_{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}\varrho
, that attain this algebraic rate.
In view of (
2
), one would generally expect to be able to recover an
n
-term polynomial approximation from
m
samples via LS for
n
at most
\sqrt{2m/(c\log m)}
. Combining this upper bound on
n
with (
4
), it is therefore tempting to believe that the best one could hope to achieve is

{\|f-\hat{f}\|}_{L^{2}_{\varrho}(\mathcal{U})}\leq C\cdot\left(m/\log(m)\right)^{\frac{1}{2}(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p})},

when
\bm{b}\in\ell^{p}(\mathbb{N})
.
In other words, the algebraic rate is halved, due to the log-quadratic sample complexity (
2
). However, this is not the case. In Theorem
12
we show that there is a LS approximation for which the error behaves like

{\|f-\hat{f}\|}_{L^{2}_{\varrho}(\mathcal{U})}\leq C\cdot\left(m/\log(m)\right)^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p}}.

(5)

Hence the approximation converges with the same rate as that of the best
n
-term approximation, up to constants and the log term. We conclude that MC sampling is not only a good sampling strategy in infinitely many dimensions, but in view of the result in §
1.4.2
, it is in fact near optimal for the class
\mathcal{H}(\bm{b},\varepsilon)
.

1.4.4
Second contribution: near-optimal approximation in the case of unknown anisotropy

A limitation of any LS scheme is that it requires knowledge of a space in which the function is well approximated. The above result is no different, since it involves a judicious choice of polynomial subspace of dimension
\leq n
(depending on the parameters
\bm{b}
and
\varepsilon
) in which one simultaneously obtains the same algebraic rate of convergence (
4
) as the best
n
-term approximation, while also not suffering from poor sample worst-case complexity bound (
3
) for MC sampling. At the very least, constructing such a subspace requires
a priori
knowledge of the parameters
\bm{b}
and
\varepsilon
. Unfortunately, these are generally unknown in practice.

Because of this limitation, LS is often used as part of an adaptive approximation scheme
[
36
]
. Here, rather than a single approximation, one computes a sequence of approximations
\hat{f}^{(1)},\hat{f}^{(2)},\ldots
and (nested) polynomial subspaces
\mathcal{P}^{(1)}\subseteq\mathcal{P}^{(2)}\subseteq\cdots
, in which the approximation
\hat{f}^{(l)}
is used to construct the next subspace
\mathcal{P}^{(l+1)}
, typically in a greedy manner. As we see later in this paper, so-called
Adaptive Least-Squares (ALS)
approximation is often quite effective. However, it lacks theoretical guarantees. It is currently unknown whether such an approximation achieves the algebraic rates of convergence (
4
) of the best
n
-term approximation (up to constants and log factors).

With this in mind, in the final part of this paper we demonstrate that this issue can be avoided by changing the approximation procedure. By using ideas from CS, we show it is possible to compute a polynomial approximation from
m
MC samples for which the error behaves like

{\|f-\hat{f}\|}_{L^{2}_{\varrho}(\mathcal{U})}\leq C\cdot\left(m/\log^{4}(m)\right)^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p}},

(6)

subject to the slightly stricter requirement
\bm{b}\in\ell^{p}_{\mathsf{M}}(\mathbb{N})
, where
\ell^{p}_{\mathsf{M}}(\mathbb{N})
is the
monotone

\ell^{p}
-space. See Theorem
16
. Thus, for a slightly restricted class of functions, it is possible to achieve the same algebraic rates of convergence as the best
n
-approximation in terms of the number of samples
m
, up to a polylogarithmic factor.

This scheme is also practical. We present a series of numerical experiments comparing it to ALS. These show that the CS scheme offers consistently as good as, or sometimes better, performance, while also having theoretical guarantees.

1.4.5
The gap between theory and practice

Our theoretical analysis asserts that MC sampling is near-optimal for polynomial approximation in infinite dimensions. Throughout this article we present numerical experiments for functions of finitely many variables only. Our analysis does apply in finite dimensions – since a function of
d
variables can be viewed as a function of infinitely many variables that is constant with respect to all but the first
d
variables – but, as we discuss later, it may not accurately predict the convergence rate when the dimension is small. There is consequently a gap between the theoretical analysis and practice, which leads one naturally to wonder ‘how high is high-dimensional’? In our experiments, we consistently witness this effect in finite dimensions. Most often, it occurs when
d
is still relatively small (e.g.,
d\leq 8
), and certainly by

d=32
, which is the largest dimension considered in this paper. However, we currently have no theoretical understanding of how large
d
should be for such behaviour to kick in. This is an interesting problem that we leave for future work.

1.4.6
Why bother?

As noted, the development of improved sampling strategies for high-dimensional approximation has been an active area of interest over the last few years. The purpose of this work is to show that MC sampling is actually eminently suitable (in fact, near-optimal) for certain high-dimensional approximation tasks – in particular, those arising from parametric DE problems, which served as the original motivation for much of this line of research.

However, the reader may justifiably be wondering why we bother. Why use MC sampling when we know how to design sampling strategies that are near-optimal regardless of the dimension? For this, we offer several arguments.
First, it is both academically interesting and practically relevant to understand the limits of what one stands to gain by changing the sampling strategy. Our results suggest that these gains are limited to lower-dimensional problems, at least for smooth function approximation on bounded domains.
Second, methods based on MC sampling have the distinct advantage of allowing one to decouple the function evaluation and function approximation phases. Thus, in the parametric DE context, the major computational burden of evaluating the target function at the sample points can be trivially parallelized. On the other hand, when adaptive approximation schemes (e.g., the aforementioned ALS method) are combined with near-optimal sampling, the sampling strategy also becomes adaptive.
Hence, the tasks of sampling the function (i.e., numerically solving a DE) and constructing the polynomial approximation cannot be decoupled. In practice, this might require the design of
ad hoc
software interfaces or data transfer strategies.
Third and finally, MC samples are exchangeable and usually easy to generate. They are found ubiquitously in UQ and machine learning applications; in particular, applications involving legacy data, where one is not afforded the luxury to adapt the samples to the target function and/or approximation scheme.

In summary, there are ample reasons why MC samples may be preferred in practice. Hence investigations into their theoretical and practical performance in high dimensions are particularly relevant.

1.5
Historical context and discussion

Our primary interest is the polynomial approximation of smooth high- and infinite-dimensional functions, with specific focus on
Least Squares (LS)
and
Compressed Sensing (CS)

techniques. LS approximation is a classical topic, with a genesis that
can be traced back to very early work by Gauss and Legendre at the turn of the 18th and 19th centuries
[
48
]
. LS is utilized in countless areas of applied mathematics and statistics, with eminent examples such as function approximation (of direct interest in this paper) and statistical regression.

Our work stems from a vigorous research stream started in the early 2010s, characterized by renewed interest towards LS
[
12
,
7
,
39
]
and the application of the CS paradigm
[
20
,
33
,
43
]

in the context of high-dimensional function approximation. The fast development of this research area is in tandem with progress in the fields of stochastic and parametric DEs. For stochastic DEs, this is strongly related to
polynomial chaos
technique. This dates back to the 1930s
[
53
]
, and has found tremendous success in computational science and engineering since its inception in the 1990s
[
22
]
and thanks to a series of influential works in the 2000s, such as
[
54
,
4
]
, which contributed to the development of the UQ field (see also
[
47
,
49
]
). As noted, a signature result for parametric DEs was the discovery that solution maps of a large class of parametric models admit best
n
-term polynomial approximation rates that are independent of the number of parameters, and therefore free from the curse of dimensionality. For more details about parametric DEs and historical remarks we refer to
[
13
]
and
[
2
, Chpt. 4]
.

Although considerably younger than LS, MC sampling is also a classical technique. The happenings that led to its widespread use in computational science are intertwined with the construction of the first electronic programmable digital computer (ENIAC), pioneering simulations of complex physical systems, and – regrettably
– the creation of the first nuclear weapons in the 1940s. The history of the MC method features scientists of the calibre of Fermi, von Neumann, Ulam and Metropolis
[
35
,
34
]
.

One of the most popular applications of MC sampling is numerical quadrature. It is well known that the MC quadrature error scales proportionally to
m^{-1/2}
, where
m
is the number of MC samples of the function to be integrated (see, e.g.,
[
42
, Section 2.1]
). Remarkably, this decay rate is independent of the number of function’s variables, which – at least, intuitively – motivates the use of MC in high dimensions. We recall, though, that in this work we are concerned with high-dimensional function approximation, as opposed to quadrature. In particular, it is worth noting that best
n
-term approximation error rates of the smooth high-dimensional functions of interest in this paper decay much faster than the MC quadrature error (see §
1.4.1
).

Naturally, any discussion of LS and MC sampling would be incomplete without a word on regression. We now warn the reader that this is
not
a paper on statistical regression. Statistical regression refers to the problem of fitting a model to noisy data under some probabilistic assumptions on the noise (e.g., homoscedasticity). This is not the perspective adopted in this paper. In fact, our goal is the accurate and stable approximation of a ‘ground truth’ function from pointwise samples, where the noise corrupting the samples need not be modelled as a random variable. For a more in-depth discussion of the differences between the function approximation and statistical regression settings, we refer to
[
24
, §1.1]
, of which we share the same viewpoint.

2
Polynomials and least-squares polynomial approximation

In this section, we describe multivariate orthogonal polynomials and polynomial approximation via LS. In order to keep the technical level reasonable, we consider the finite-dimensional case only. The infinite-dimensional case is introduced in §
5
.

2.1
Univariate notation

Let
\varrho
be a probability measure on
[-1,1]
and write
L^{2}_{\varrho}([-1,1])
for the corresponding Lebesgue space of square-integrable functions
f:[-1,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
. We assume that
\varrho
generates a unique sequence of orthonormal polynomials
\{\psi_{\nu}\}_{\nu\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\subset L^{2}_{\varrho}([-1,1])
. In other words,

\langle\psi_{\nu},\psi_{\nu^{\prime}}\rangle=\delta_{\nu,\nu^{\prime}},\quad\forall\nu,\nu^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}_{0},\quad\text{and}\quad\mathrm{span}\{\psi_{0},\ldots,\psi_{n}\}=\mathbb{P}_{n},\quad\forall n\in\mathbb{N}_{0},

where
\mathbb{P}_{n}
is the space of polynomials of degree at most
n
.
Note that this is a mild assumption (see, e.g.,
[
40
, §2.1]
). Two particular cases we focus on in this paper are the uniform and Chebyshev (arcsine) measures

\,\mathrm{d}\varrho(y)=2^{-1}\,\mathrm{d}y,\qquad\,\mathrm{d}\varrho(y)=(\pi\sqrt{1-y^{2}})^{-1}\,\mathrm{d}y,

which generate the Legendre and (first kind) Chebyshev polynomials, respectively.

2.2
Multivariate polynomial approximation

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
and consider the symmetric hypercube
\mathcal{U}=[-1,1]^{d}
. We define a probability measure over
\mathcal{U}
via tensor products. Abusing notation, we write
\varrho=\varrho\times\cdots\times\varrho
for this measure,
where on the right-hand side
\varrho
denotes the probability measure on
[-1,1]
.

Let
L^{2}_{\varrho}(\mathcal{U})
be the Lebesgue space of square-integrable functions
f:\mathcal{U}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
.
We construct an orthonormal polynomial basis for this space via tensor products. Writing
\bm{\nu}=(\nu_{1},\ldots,\nu_{d})\in\mathbb{N}^{d}_{0}

for an arbitrary multi-index, we define

\Psi_{\bm{\nu}}(\bm{y})=\psi_{\nu_{1}}(y_{1})\cdots\psi_{\nu_{d}}(y_{d}),\qquad\bm{y}=(y_{i})^{d}_{i=1}\in\mathcal{U}.

The set
\{\Psi_{\bm{\nu}}\}_{\bm{\nu}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}_{0}}\subset L^{2}_{\varrho}(\mathcal{U})
forms an orthonormal basis. Hence any function
f\in L^{2}_{\varrho}(\mathcal{U})
has the convergent expansion

f=\sum_{\bm{\nu}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}_{0}}c_{\bm{\nu}}\Psi_{\bm{\nu}},\qquad\text{where }c_{\bm{\nu}}=\langle f,\Psi_{\bm{\nu}}\rangle_{L^{2}_{\varrho}(\mathcal{U})}=\int_{\mathcal{U}}f(\bm{y})\overline{\Psi_{\bm{\nu}}(\bm{y})}\,\mathrm{d}\varrho(\bm{y}).

(7)

We are interested in
n
-term approximations to such functions. An
n
-term approximation
to
f
based on an multi-index set
S\subset\mathbb{N}^{d}_{0}
,
|S|=n
, has the form

f_{S}=\sum_{\bm{\nu}\in S}c_{\bm{\nu}}\Psi_{\bm{\nu}}.

(8)

Due to Parseval’s identity, the error of such an approximation is determined by the size of the coefficients
c_{\bm{\nu}}
not included in
S
. Specifically,

{\|f-f_{S}\|}^{2}_{L^{2}_{\varrho}(\mathcal{U})}=\sum_{\bm{\nu}\notin S}|c_{\bm{\nu}}|^{2}.

(9)

2.3
Choosing
S

In standard multivariate polynomial approximation it is common to make an
a priori
choice of
S
with some prescribed structure (see, e.g.,
[
24
]
). Given
\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, classical examples are the
tensor-product
index set of order
\ell
,

S^{\mathsf{TP}}_{\ell}=\left\{\bm{\nu}=(\nu_{k})_{k=1}^{d}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}:\max\{\nu_{1},\ldots,\nu_{d}\}\leq\ell\right\},

or the
total degree
index set of order
\ell
,

S^{\mathsf{TD}}_{\ell}=\left\{\bm{\nu}=(\nu_{k})_{k=1}^{d}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}:\nu_{1}+\cdots+\nu_{d}\leq\ell\right\}.

However, the cardinality of these index sets scales exponentially with the dimension
d
, making them poorly suited in all but low-dimensional problems. Specifically,
|S^{\mathsf{TP}}_{\ell}|=(\ell+1)^{d}
and
|S^{\mathsf{TD}}_{\ell}|={\ell+d\choose d}
for any
\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
d\in\mathbb{N}
. Applying Stirling’s formula, one deduces that
|S^{\mathsf{TD}}_{\ell}|\sim\ell^{d}/d!
as
\ell\to\infty
for fixed
d
. This effect can be partially mitigated by working with
hyperbolic cross
index sets (see, e.g.,
[
24
]
). Yet, the cardinalities of these sets also grow exponentially with dimension.

Alternatively, one can look to replace such
isotropic
sets with anisotropic variants. High-dimensional functions may be highly
anisotropic
, i.e., they may depend more strongly on some variables than others. The aforementioned index sets fail to capture this behaviour, thus potentially leading to poor approximations. However, selecting a good anisotropic set
a priori
requires detailed knowledge about the function
f
, which is typically unavailable in practice.

A theoretical alternative is provided by the concept of best
n
-term approximation. Here, one does away with the classical notion of polynomial degree (as described by the parameter
\ell
in
S^{\mathsf{TP}}_{\ell}
and
S^{\mathsf{TD}}_{\ell}
) and instead seeks an optimal index set in a function-dependent manner by minimizing the error (
9
) over all possible sets. A
best
n
-term approximation

f_{n}
of
f
is thus defined as

f_{n}=f_{S^{*}},\qquad\text{where }S^{*}\in{\underset{}{\operatorname{argmin}}}\{{\|f-f_{S}\|}_{L^{2}_{\varrho}(\mathcal{U})}:S\subset\mathbb{N}^{d}_{0},\ |S|=n\}.

(10)

It follows straightforwardly from (
9
) that
S^{*}
consists of those multi-indices corresponding to the
n
largest coefficients
c_{\bm{\nu}}
of
f
in absolute value; that is to say,
S^{*}=\{\bm{\nu}_{1},\bm{\nu}_{2},\ldots,\bm{\nu}_{n}\}
, where
\bm{\nu}_{1},\bm{\nu}_{2},\ldots
are such that
|c_{\bm{\nu}_{1}}|\geq|c_{\bm{\nu}_{2}}|\geq\cdots
.

Unfortunately, the best
n
-term approximation is a theoretical benchmark. It is generally impossible to construct, since doing so would generically involve computing and sorting infinitely-many coefficients – something that clearly cannot be done from the finite data (
1
). A more practical approach is therefore to construct a set
S
– or more precisely, a nested sequence of set
S^{(1)}\subseteq S^{(2)}\subseteq\cdots
– in a function dependent, adaptive manner. This is typically done via a greedy procedure, with multi-indices being added according to some importance criterion. The aforementioned ALS method is a procedure of this type. We discuss it further in §
4.1
and §
.

Regardless of how the index set (or sets) is constructed, however, it is generally useful to restrict one’s attention sets with certain structure. A
lower
set (also known as
monotone
or
downward closed
– see, e.g.,
[
13
]
) is a set
S\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{d}_{0}
for which

(\bm{\nu}\in S\text{ and }\bm{\mu}\leq\bm{\nu})\ \Rightarrow\ \bm{\mu}\in S,

(here the inequality
\bm{\mu}\leq\bm{\nu}
is understood componentwise). Lower sets are ubiquitous in multivariate polynomial approximation, with (isotropic or anisotropic) tensor-product, total degree and hyperbolic cross index sets all being examples. Lower sets are also commonly employed in adaptive strategies such as ALS (see §
), so as to make the greedy selection procedure tractable. Indeed, given a lower set
S^{(l)}
there are only finitely many multi-indices
\bm{\nu}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}_{0}\backslash S^{(l)}
for which
S^{(l)}\cup\{\bm{\nu}\}
is also lower.

2.4
Weighted least-squares polynomial approximation

Fix a set multi-indices
S\subset\mathbb{N}^{d}_{0}
,
|S|=n
, and consider
m\geq n
noisy samples (
1
)
of a function
f\in L^{2}_{\varrho}(\mathcal{U})
at
sample points

\bm{y}_{1},\ldots,\bm{y}_{m}\in\mathcal{U}
. We wish to compute an approximation to
f
from the polynomial space defined by
S
: namely, the subspace

\mathcal{P}_{S}=\mathrm{span}\{\Psi_{\bm{\nu}}:\bm{\nu}\in S\}\subset L^{2}_{\varrho}(\mathcal{U}).

Given a positive and almost everywhere finite weight function
w:\mathcal{U}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, we define a
weighted least-squares approximation
to
f
as

\hat{f}\in{\underset{p\in\mathcal{P}_{S}}{\operatorname{argmin}}}{\frac{1}{m}}\sum^{m}_{i=1}w(\bm{y}_{i})|f(\bm{y}_{i})+e_{i}-p(\bm{y}_{i})|^{2}.

(11)

This is readily computed by solving an algebraic LS problem for the coefficients of
\hat{f}
. Indeed, let
\bm{\nu}_{1},\ldots,\bm{\nu}_{n}
be an enumeration of the indices in
S
. Then

\hat{f}=\sum^{n}_{i=1}\hat{c}_{i}\Psi_{\bm{\nu}_{i}},\quad\text{where }\hat{\bm{c}}=(\hat{c}_{i})^{n}_{i=1}\in{\underset{\bm{z}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}}{\operatorname{argmin}}}{\left\|\bm{A}\bm{z}-\bm{f}\right\|}_{2}^{2},

and the LS matrix
\bm{A}\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times n}
and vector
\bm{f}\in\mathbb{C}^{m}
are given by

\bm{A}=\left(\sqrt{w(\bm{y}_{i})/m}\Psi_{\bm{\nu}_{j}}(\bm{y}_{i})\right)^{m,n}_{i,j=1},\qquad\bm{f}=\left(\sqrt{w(\bm{y}_{i})/m}(f(\bm{y}_{i})+e_{i})\right)^{m}_{i=1}.

(12)

3
Theory of (weighted) least-squares approximation

In this section, we present some elementary theory for weighted LS approximation. For the sake of generality, in the majority of this section we consider an arbitrary
n
-dimensional subspace
\mathcal{P}\subset L^{2}_{\varrho}(\mathcal{U})
(i.e., not necessarily a polynomial subspace of the form
\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{P}_{S}
). We write
\{\Psi_{i}\}^{n}_{i=1}
for an orthonormal basis for
\mathcal{P}
.
However, for convenience, we make the mild assumption that the constant function
p(\bm{y})=1
,
\forall\bm{y}\in\mathcal{U}
, is an element of
\mathcal{P}
. Note that this always holds when
\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{P}_{S}
and
S
is a lower set.

3.1
Accuracy and stability

Given sample points
\bm{y}_{1},\ldots,\bm{y}_{m}\in\mathcal{U}
and a weight function
w:\mathcal{U}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, define the discrete semi-norm

{\|f\|}^{2}_{\mathsf{disc},w}=\frac{1}{m}\sum^{m}_{i=1}w(\bm{y}_{i})|f(\bm{y}_{i})|^{2},\quad\forall f\in L^{2}_{\varrho}(\mathcal{U})\cap C(\mathcal{U}),

(here
C(\mathcal{U})
is the set of continuous functions on
\mathcal{U}
)
and the
discrete stability
constants

\displaystyle\alpha_{w}

\displaystyle=\inf\left\{{\|p\|}_{\mathsf{disc},w}:p\in\mathcal{P},\ {\left\|p\right\|}_{L^{2}_{\varrho}(\mathcal{U})}=1\right\},

\displaystyle\beta_{w}

\displaystyle=\sup\left\{{\|p\|}_{\mathsf{disc},w}:p\in\mathcal{P},\ {\left\|p\right\|}_{L^{2}_{\varrho}(\mathcal{U})}=1\right\},

Notice that
\alpha_{w}=\sigma_{\min}(\bm{A})
and
\beta_{w}=\sigma_{\max}(\bm{A})
, where is the LS matrix (
12
).

Lemma 1
(Accuracy and stability of weighted LS).

Let
\mathcal{P}\subset L^{2}_{\varrho}(\mathcal{U})\cap C(\mathcal{U})
with
1\in\mathcal{P}
,
f\in L^{2}_{\varrho}(\mathcal{U})\cap C(\mathcal{U})
,
\bm{e}\in\mathbb{C}^{m}
,
\bm{y}_{1},\ldots,\bm{y}_{m}\in\mathcal{U}
and
w:\mathcal{U}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be such that
w(\bm{y}_{i})>0
,
\forall i\in[m]:=\{1,\ldots,m\}
. If
\alpha_{w}>0
then the problem

\min_{p\in\mathcal{P}}{\frac{1}{m}}\sum^{m}_{i=1}w(\bm{y}_{i})|f(\bm{y}_{i})+e_{i}-p(\bm{y}_{i})|^{2}

(13)

has a unique solution
\hat{f}
. This solution satisfies

{\|f-\hat{f}\|}_{L^{2}_{\varrho}(\mathcal{U})}\leq\inf_{p\in\mathcal{P}}\left\{{\|f-p\|}_{L^{2}_{\varrho}(\mathcal{U})}+\frac{1}{\alpha_{w}}{\|f-p\|}_{\mathsf{disc},w}\right\}+\frac{\beta_{w}}{\alpha_{w}}{\left\|\bm{e}\right\|}_{\infty}.

(14)

Also, the (
2
-norm) condition number of the LS matrix satisfies
\mathrm{cond}(\bm{A})=\beta_{w}/\alpha_{w}
.

This result is standard (see, e.g.,
[
39
, Prop. 1]
). We include a short proof in §
for completeness. Note that the last statement is immediate, since
\mathrm{cond}(\bm{A})=\sigma_{\max}(\bm{A})/\sigma_{\min}(\bm{A})
by definition.
The main takeaway from this lemma is that the accuracy and stability of
\hat{f}
are determined by the size of the constants
\alpha_{w}
and
\beta_{w}
. In the next subsection, we discuss how to control these constants in the case of random sampling. In doing so, we also obtain an estimate for the sample complexity.

3.2
Sample complexity

We now consider the sample complexity of weighted LS approximation in the case of random sampling. Specifically, we now assume that
\bm{y}_{1},\ldots,\bm{y}_{m}\sim_{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}\mu
, where
\mu
is some probability measure with support in
\mathcal{U}
.

The analysis of this type of sampling strategy involves the (reciprocal)
Christoffel function
of the subspace
\mathcal{P}

[
12
]
:

\mathcal{K}(\mathcal{P})(\bm{y})=\sup\left\{|p(\bm{y})|^{2}:p\in\mathcal{P},\ {\left\|p\right\|}_{L^{2}_{\varrho}(\mathcal{U})}=1\right\},\quad\forall\bm{y}\in\mathcal{U}.

Observe that
\mathcal{K}(\mathcal{P})(\bm{y})\geq 1
,
\forall\bm{y}\in\mathcal{U}
, since, by assumption, the function
1\in\mathcal{P}
.
It is also a short argument to show that
\mathcal{K}(\mathcal{P})
has the equivalent expression

\mathcal{K}(\mathcal{P})(\bm{y})=\sum^{n}_{i=1}|\Psi_{i}(\bm{y})|^{2},\quad\forall\bm{y}\in\mathcal{U},

(15)

where
\{\Psi_{i}\}^{n}_{i=1}
is any orthonormal basis for
\mathcal{P}
.
Given
\mathcal{K}
and a weight function
w:\mathcal{U}\rightarrow(0,\infty)
, we now also define

\kappa(\mathcal{P};w)=\sup_{\bm{y}\in\mathcal{U}}w(\bm{y})\mathcal{K}(\mathcal{P})(\bm{y}).

(16)

Theorem 2
(Sample complexity of weighted LS with random sampling).

Let
\mathcal{P}\subset L^{2}_{\varrho}(\mathcal{U})\cap C(\mathcal{U})
with
\dim(\mathcal{P})=n
and
1\in\mathcal{P}
,
0<\epsilon<1
and
\mu
be a probability measure on
\mathcal{U}
such that

\,\mathrm{d}\mu(\bm{y})=(w(\bm{y}))^{-1}\,\mathrm{d}\varrho(\bm{y})

(17)

for some strictly positive and finite almost everywhere weight function
w:\mathcal{U}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. Let
\bm{y}_{1},\ldots,\bm{y}_{m}\sim_{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}\mu
, where
m
satisfies

m\geq 7\cdot\kappa(\mathcal{P};w)\cdot\log(2n/\epsilon).

(18)

Then the following holds with probability at least
1-\epsilon
. For any
f\in L^{2}_{\varrho}(\mathcal{U})\cap C(\mathcal{U})
, the solution
\hat{f}
of the weighted LS problem (
13
) is unique and satisfies

{\|f-\hat{f}\|}_{L^{2}_{\varrho}(\mathcal{U})}\leq\inf_{p\in\mathcal{P}}\left\{{\|f-p\|}_{L^{2}_{\varrho}(\mathcal{U})}+2{\|f-p\|}_{\mathsf{disc},w}\right\}+2{\|\bm{e}\|}_{\infty}.

(19)

Moreover, the condition number satisfies
\mathrm{cond}(\bm{A})\leq 2
.

This result is based on standard ideas, in particular, the use of the matrix Chernoff inequality (see, e.g.,
[
14
, Thm. 2.1]
). We include a short proof in §
for completeness.
Note that the number
7
in (
18
) is somewhat arbitrary. As can be seen from the proof, one can replace it with a smaller constant at the expense of larger constants in the error and condition number bounds.

The most important aspect of this result is the sample complexity bound (
18
). Since
\mu
is a probability measure, (
17
) implies that

\int_{\mathcal{U}}(w(\bm{y}))^{-1}\,\mathrm{d}\varrho(\bm{y})=1,

(20)

and orthonormality and the alternative expression (
15
) for
\mathcal{K}(\mathcal{P})
imply that

\int_{\mathcal{U}}\mathcal{K}(\mathcal{P})(\bm{y})\,\mathrm{d}\varrho(\bm{y})=n

(21)

and

\kappa(\mathcal{P};w)=\int_{\mathcal{U}}\kappa(\mathcal{P};w)/w(\bm{y})\,\mathrm{d}\varrho(\bm{y})\geq\int_{\mathcal{U}}\mathcal{K}(\mathcal{P})(\bm{y})\,\mathrm{d}\varrho(\bm{y})=n.

Hence
\kappa(\mathcal{P};w)\geq n
for any
\mathcal{P}
and
w
. As a result, the sample complexity bound (
18
) is always at least log-linear in
n
. In the next subsection, we show that it is generally superlinear in
n
. However, in §
3.4
we show that it is always possible to choose the sampling measure
\mu
so as to achieve the optimal value of the right-hand side of (
18
).

3.3
The case of Monte Carlo sampling

MC sampling corresponds to the choice
\mu=\varrho
, in which case (
17
) holds with
w=1
. The approximation (
13
) is correspondingly an
unweighted
LS approximation.
In this case, the sample complexity estimate (
18
) takes the form

m\geq c\cdot\kappa(\mathcal{P})\cdot\log(n/\epsilon),\quad\text{where }\kappa(\mathcal{P})=\kappa(\mathcal{P};1)={\left\|\mathcal{K}(\mathcal{P})\right\|}_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{U})}.

(22)

Recall that
\kappa(\mathcal{P})\geq n=\dim(\mathcal{P})
. Unfortunately,
\kappa(\mathcal{P})
can be arbitrarily large, even in cases such as Chebyshev and Legendre polynomial approximation.
We now state two standard results, which are based on
[
7
,
8
,
10
]
(see also
[
2
, Props. 5.13 & 5.17]
).

Proposition 3
.

Let
d,n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\varrho
be the Chebyshev (arcsine) measure over
\mathcal{U}=[-1,1]^{d}
and
\{\Psi_{\bm{\nu}}\}_{\bm{\nu}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}_{0}}\subset L^{2}_{\varrho}(\mathcal{U})
be the orthonormal Chebyshev polynomial basis. Then

\max\left\{\kappa(\mathcal{P}_{S}):S\subset\mathbb{N}^{d}_{0},|S|\leq n\right\}=2^{d}n.

(23)

However, for lower sets, if
1\leq n\leq 2^{d+1}
then

n^{\log(3)/\log(2)}/3\leq\max\left\{\kappa(\mathcal{P}_{S}):S\subset\mathbb{N}^{d}_{0},|S|\leq n,\ \textnormal{$S$ lower}\right\}\leq n^{\log(3)/\log(2)}.

(24)

Moreover, the upper bound holds for any
n\geq 1
.

Proposition 4
.

Let
d,n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\varrho
be the uniform measure over
\mathcal{U}=[-1,1]^{d}
and
\{\Psi_{\bm{\nu}}\}\subset L^{2}_{\varrho}(\mathcal{U})
be the orthonormal Legendre polynomial basis. Then

\max\left\{\kappa(\mathcal{P}_{S}):S\subset\mathbb{N}^{d}_{0},\ |S|\leq n,\ \textnormal{$S$ lower}\right\}=n^{2}.

(25)

However,
\kappa(\mathcal{P}_{S})
is unbounded for arbitrary sets
S\subset\mathbb{N}^{d}_{0}
of size
|S|\leq n
. That is, for every
c>0
there exists a set
S
of size
|S|\leq n
for which
\kappa(\mathcal{P}_{S})\geq c
.

These two results suggest that MC sampling generally suffers from a poor sample complexity in the case of Chebyshev or Legendre polynomials. For non-lower sets the situation can be arbitrarily bad in the case of Legendre polynomial approximation, and in the case of Chebyshev polynomial approximation, highly susceptible to the curse of dimensionality. Even for lower sets, these results in combination with Theorem
2
suggest the superlinear sample complexity bound
m\geq 7\cdot n^{\gamma}\cdot\log(2n/\epsilon)
,
where
\gamma=\log(3)/\log(2)
(Chebyshev) or
\gamma=2
(Legendre).

3.4
Near-optimal sampling

We now describe a random sampling scheme that obtains provably log-linear sample complexity. This was introduced in
[
14
]
; see also
[
27
]
for earlier work in this direction.

The idea is to choose the
weight function
w
, and therefore, via (
17
), the sampling measure
\mu
, so as to minimize the constant
\kappa(\mathcal{P};w)
appearing in the sample complexity bound (
18
).
It is immediate from (
16
) that
\kappa(\mathcal{P};w)
is minimized whenever the weight function
w\propto(\mathcal{K}(\mathcal{P}))^{-1}
. Recall that
w
and
\mathcal{K}(\mathcal{P})
satisfy (
20
) and (
21
), respectively. Hence, the choice of
w
that minimizes
\kappa(\mathcal{P};w)
is precisely

w(\bm{y})=\left(n^{-1}\mathcal{K}(\mathcal{P})(\bm{y})\right)^{-1},

(26)

and the corresponding sampling measure is

\,\mathrm{d}\mu(\bm{y})=n^{-1}\mathcal{K}(\mathcal{P})(\bm{y})\,\mathrm{d}\varrho(\bm{y})=n^{-1}\sum^{n}_{i=1}|\Psi_{i}(\bm{y})|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\varrho(\bm{y}).

(27)

Since
\kappa(\mathcal{P};w)=n
in this case, the sample complexity bound (
18
) reads as

m\geq 7\cdot n\cdot\log(2n/\epsilon).

(28)

In other words, it is optimal up to the constant
7
and the logarithmic factor
\log(2n/\epsilon)
.

Notice that this property holds for
any
subspace
\mathcal{P}
, regardless of whether it is a polynomial space or not. However, when applied to Chebyshev or Legendre polynomial approximation, it reduces the sample complexity from the superlinear rates asserted in Propositions
3
and
4
to log-linear in
n
.

Remark 5
(Optimal sampling).

Recently, a series of works have strived to remove the logarithmic dependence in the sample complexity (
28
) by carefully modifying the sampling strategy and weight function (see
[
5
,
31
,
19
,
51
]
and references therein). These works use nontrivial constructions based on
[
6
,
32
]
. We do not consider such approaches in this paper. As discussed in
[
19
, §5]
, of the existing approaches, those which are computationally feasible (i.e., implementable in polynomial time) are accompanied by error bounds that involve the
L^{\infty}
-norm error
\inf_{p\in\mathcal{P}}{\|f-p\|}_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{U})}
, this being an upper bound for the term
{\|f-p\|}_{\mathsf{disc},w}
in (
19
). If applied to the approximation of
(\bm{b},\varepsilon)
-holomorphic functions, this would lead to suboptimal algebraic rates of the form
(m/c)^{1-\frac{1}{p}}
(recall §
1.4
). Later, when we prove our main result, we use Bernstein’s inequality to estimate the term
{\|f-p\|}_{\mathsf{disc},w}
in a more careful way to obtain near-optimal rates for MC sampling. See Theorem
12
and §
.

4
Monte Carlo sampling is good in high dimensions

The previous discussion suggests that MC sampling is a poor strategy, especially in the case of Legendre polynomials, and that significant improvements may be realized with the near-optimal sampling scheme of §
3.4
. However, as we noted in §
1.3
it has often been observed that MC sampling performs relatively well in high dimensions. The purpose of this section is to demonstrate this phenomenon via numerical experiments.

This phenomenon was briefly investigated in
[
14
]
. Here, phase transition plots were produced showing the empirical probability
\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{cond}(\bm{A})\leq 3)
for randomly generated sequences of lower sets. As observed therein, in low dimensions MC sampling is significantly worse than the near-optimal scheme, but in higher dimensions the difference in performance is greatly reduced.

4.1
Adaptive (weighted) least-squares approximation

Since the focus of this paper is on function approximation, in our experiments we compare MC and near-optimal sampling on several different smooth function approximation tasks.

As mentioned previously, we shall use an
Adaptive LS (ALS)
approximation scheme
[
36
]
. The procedure is described in full detail in §
. However, in short, it proceeds as follows. Starting from an initial index set
S^{(1)}=\{\bm{0}\}
, at step
l
this scheme first computes a (weighted) LS approximation
\hat{f}^{(l)}\in\mathcal{P}_{S^{(l)}}
using the index set
S^{(l)}
, and then uses the set
S^{(l)}
and the approximation
\hat{f}^{(l)}
to construct the next index set
S^{(l+1)}\supseteq S^{(l)}
in a greedy manner. It does this by using
\hat{f}^{(l)}
to estimate the coefficients with indices belonging to the so-called
reduced margin
of
S^{(l)}
, and then chooses those coefficients which are largest in magnitude.
The result of this procedure is a sequence of approximations
\hat{f}_{1},\hat{f}_{2},\ldots
and nested multi-index sets
S^{(1)}\subseteq S^{(2)}\subseteq\cdots
of sizes
1=n^{(1)}\leq n^{(2)}\leq\cdots
, where
n^{(l)}=|S^{(l)}|
. We remark in passing that the generated multi-index sets are always lower sets, due to the use of the reduced margin.

4.2
Experimental setup

We now describe the salient aspects of the experiments. See §
for further details.
In these experiments, we choose the number of samples at step
l
to be log-linear in
n^{(l)}
, i.e.,

m=\max\{n^{(l)}+1,\lceil n^{(l)}\cdot\log(n^{(l)})\rceil\}

(29)

(the use of the maximum simply ensures that
m>n^{(l)}
for all
l
, so the LS problem is overdetermined). Thus, in view of Theorem
2
and the discussion in §
3.4
we expect the near-optimal sampling scheme to be stable.

In this and other experiments, we consider the (relative) error of the LS approximation
\hat{f}^{(l)}
and the condition number
\mathrm{cond}(\bm{A}^{(l)})
, where
\bm{A}^{(l)}
is the
l
th LS matrix. For the former, we compute the discrete
L^{2}_{\varrho}
-norm error over a grid of
100,000
MC points. To ensure a valid comparison, we also use this grid to generate the sample points. See §
for further discussion on this point.

Since all experiments involve random sampling, we perform
T=50
trials. In these experiments, each trial corresponds to a sequence of approximations
\hat{f}^{(1)},\hat{f}^{(2)},\ldots
and LS matrices
\bm{A}^{(1)},\bm{A}^{(2)},\ldots
. Thus, each figure shows the statistics relating to the computed quantities. As discussed in §
, we plot a main curve showing the geometric mean over the trials, and a shaded region showing one (geometric) standard deviation. These shaded regions play a role similar to
standard errors
in the context of MC quadrature
[
42
]
and they are sometimes too small to be visible. When this occurs, it is due to an extreme concentration of the visualized data around its mean.

4.3
Test functions

In addition to the parametric DE considered in Fig.

, we also consider various other test functions. First, we consider

f_{1}(\bm{y})=\exp\left(\sum^{d}_{i=1}\frac{y_{i}}{2i}\right),\quad\forall\bm{y}\in[-1,1]^{d}.

(30)

This function is entire and anisotropic with respect to its variables. For larger
d
it is sharply peaked near its maximal and minimal values at
\bm{y}=\pm\bm{1}
, with
f_{1}(+\bm{1})\sim 1.34\sqrt{d}
,
f_{1}(-\bm{1})\sim(1.34\sqrt{d})^{-1}
as

, while
f_{1}(\bm{0})=1
. Next, we consider

f_{2}(\bm{y})=\left(1+\frac{1}{2d}\sum^{d}_{i=1}q_{i}y_{i}\right)^{-1},\quad\forall\bm{y}\in[-1,1]^{d},\qquad\text{where }q_{i}=10^{-\frac{3(i-1)}{d-1}}

(31)

(see, e.g.,
[
36
]
).
This function is anisotropic and holomorphic, but not entire. Unlike
f_{1}
it varies between finite maximum and minimum values, with
0.93+\mathcal{O}\left(1/d\right)\leq f_{2}(\bm{y})\leq 1.08+\mathcal{O}\left(1/d\right)

.

Next, we consider several examples from the family of test functions

f_{3}(\bm{y})=\prod^{d}_{i=1}\frac{\sqrt{2\delta_{i}+\delta^{2}_{i}}}{y_{i}+1+\delta_{i}},\quad\forall\bm{y}\in[-1,1]^{d},

(32)

with positive parameters
\delta_{i}>0
(the factor in the numerator ensures this function has unit norm, and therefore avoids scale effects for large

due to the
d

with singularities at any
\bm{y}
for which
y_{i}=-1-\delta_{i}
for some

. They can be either isotropic or anisotropic depending on the choice of
\delta_{i}
: a larger
\delta_{i}
implies a smoother dependence on the variable
y_{i}
, and smaller
\delta_{i}
implies a less smooth dependence. As we discuss later in Remark
10
, our later theoretical analysis is readily applied to such functions.

Finally, we consider a test function
f_{\mathsf{bor}}
from the
Virtual Library of Simulation Experiments

[

]
, which serves as a physical model for water flow through a borehole:

f_{4}(\bm{y})=f_{\mathsf{bor}}(\bm{y}),\quad\forall\bm{y}\in[-1,1]^{d}.

(33)

The original function in
[

]

d_{*}=8

[-1,1]
and then, for

4.4
Numerical experiments

Figs.
2
–
5
show numerical results for the test functions
f_{i}
,
i=1,2,3,4
.
In all cases, there is a substantial difference between the two sampling strategies when
d=1
. MC sampling leads to a highly ill-conditioned LS problem, with condition number increasing exponentially fast in
m
. Concurrently, the approximation error, while initially decreasing rapidly, eventually begins to increase exponentially, due to the effect of round-off error when solving the ill-conditioned LS problem. The standard deviation (which is represented by the shaded region) is also high – an effect that is symptomatic of an ill-conditioned problem.

This behaviour is well known (see, e.g.,
[
39
]
). The reason stems from the choice of log-linear scaling in (
29
). This scaling is asymptotically much smaller than the log-quadratic scaling which suffices for accuracy and stability with MC sampling. The result is ill-conditioning of the LS problem, and poor stability and accuracy of the corresponding approximation.

On the other hand, the near-optimal sampling strategy is perfectly well conditioned. The error decreases rapidly to roughly machine epsilon, and remains at this level even as
m
increases. The standard deviation of both the approximation error and condition number are also much lower than in the case of MC sampling.

Note that functions
f_{1}
and
f_{3}
are products of univariate functions, which means that their best
n
-term approximation (
10
) can be computed. We include this as a benchmark in Figs.
2
and
4
. It is notable that the error from ALS with near-optimal sampling closely tracks that of the best
n
-term approximation in low dimensions. Yet as
d
increases there is a widening gap in performance – in other words, ALS may struggle to identify good index sets in high dimensions.

This aside, the situation in
d=1
dimensions insofar as sampling goes is unambiguous: near-optimal sampling leads to a significant improvement over MC sampling. However, the picture begins to change as the dimension increases. For
d=2
and
d=4
dimensions, the latter still has a growing condition number. However, the rate of growth is much slower than in the one-dimensional case, and as a result, there is a lower standard deviation and far less drift in the error as
m\rightarrow\infty
. Moreover, when the dimension is increased further, such effect dramatically lessens. In
d=16
or
d=32
dimensions, the condition number of MC sampling is virtually the same as that of the near-optimal sampling strategy, as is the error.
In other words, while MC sampling (with log-linear scaling) is a poor strategy in low dimensions, in higher dimensions, its performance in these examples is very close to that of the near-optimal strategy.

To gain some further insight, in Fig.
6
we plot the function
\kappa(\mathcal{P}_{S})
for the index sets

produced by the ALS scheme with MC sampling. In one dimension, this function behaves like

, where

On the other hand, as the dimension increases, we see that

scales more slowly with

, it again highlights the fact that MC sampling becomes a progressively less bad a sampling strategy as the dimension increases.

4.5
Illustrative case studies

Recall that the index sets
S^{(1)},S^{(2)},\ldots
generated by the adaptive LS approximation scheme are function dependent. As these experiments make clear, the worst-case quadratic scaling of
\kappa(\mathcal{P}_{S})
identified in Proposition
4
is not realized for any of the three functions when
d\geq 2
. To examine this situation more concretely, it is informative to consider some specific cases.

4.5.1
Additively separable functions

First, consider a function of the form
f(\bm{y})=g(y_{1})+\cdots+g(y_{d})
,
where
g:[-1,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
.
Write
g=\sum^{\infty}_{\nu=0}d_{\nu}\psi_{\nu}
and suppose without loss of generality that the coefficients are in nonincreasing order, i.e.,
|d_{0}|\geq|d_{1}|\geq\cdots
.
Let
\bm{c}=(c_{\bm{\nu}})_{\bm{\nu}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}_{0}}
be the coefficients of
f
. Then
c_{\bm{0}}=d\cdot d_{0}
,
c_{\bm{\nu}}=d_{\nu}
if
\bm{\nu}=\nu\bm{e}_{j}
for some
j\in[d]
and
\nu\in\mathbb{N}
, and
c_{\bm{\nu}}=\bm{0}
otherwise.
Here
\bm{e}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the multi-index with
1
in its
j
th component and zero otherwise.
Now consider the best
n
-term approximation
f_{n}
to
f
. Suppose that
n=dp+1
for some

. Then it is clear that the set
S^{*}
defined in (
10
) is precisely

S^{*}=\left\{k\bm{e}_{j}:k=0,\ldots,p,\ j=1,\ldots,d\right\}.

It is then a short argument based on (
15
) to show that

\kappa(\mathcal{P}_{S^{*}})=d\cdot\kappa(\mathcal{Q}_{p}){+1-d},\qquad\mathcal{Q}_{p}:=\mathrm{span}\{\psi_{0},\ldots,{\psi_{p}}\}\subset L^{2}_{\varrho}([-1,1]).

In particular, in the case of Legendre polynomials, Proposition
4
implies that

\kappa(\mathcal{P}_{S^{*}})=d\cdot{\kappa(\mathbb{P}_{p})+1-d=(n-1)^{2}/d+2n-1.}

Hence,
\kappa(\mathcal{P}_{S^{*}})
is quadratic in
n
, but with a constant of
1/d
. In higher dimensions, one therefore expects less oversampling to be needed to ensure stability. In Fig.
7
we examine the performance of ALS for such a function. As expected, MC sampling gives nearly as good performance in higher dimensions as the near-optimal scheme.

5
Polynomial approximation theory in infinite dimensions

We now turn our attention to theoretical analysis of the phenomenon seen in the previous section. As mentioned in §
1.4
, we shall do this by considering polynomial approximation of infinite-dimensional holomorphic functions. In this section, we describe the standard setup for this approach, before presenting our theoretical result in §
6
. For further information on the material presented in this section, see, e.g.,
[
2
, Chpt. 3]
and
[
13
,
9
]
.

5.1
Infinite-dimensional setup

We now consider scalar-valued functions of the form
f:\mathcal{U}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
, where
\mathcal{U}=[-1,1]^{\mathbb{N}}
. As in §
2.1
, we let
\varrho
be a probability measure on
[-1,1]
. The Kolmogorov extension theorem (see §
) guarantees the existence of a probability measure on
\mathcal{U}
formed as the infinite tensor-product of this measure. Abusing notation, we denote this measure as
\varrho=\varrho\times\varrho\times\cdots
and let
L^{2}_{\varrho}(\mathcal{U})
be the corresponding Lebesgue space of square-integrable functions.

We construct an orthonormal polynomial basis of
L^{2}_{\varrho}(\mathcal{U})
by tensor products. Let
\bm{\nu}=(\nu_{1},\nu_{2},\ldots)\in\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
be an infinite multi-index and define the set

\mathcal{F}=\{\bm{\nu}\in\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}_{0}:{\left\|\bm{\nu}\right\|}_{0}<\infty\}\subset\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}_{0}.

(35)

Here
{\left\|\bm{\nu}\right\|}_{0}=|\{i:\nu_{i}\neq 0\}|
is the
\ell^{0}
-‘norm’. Thus,
\mathcal{F}
is the set of infinite multi-indices with at most finitely-many nonzero terms.
For any
\bm{\nu}=(\nu_{i})^{\infty}_{i=1}\in\mathcal{F}
, we define the corresponding tensor-product orthonormal polynomial
\Psi_{\bm{\nu}}
as

\Psi_{\bm{\nu}}(\bm{y})=\prod_{i\in\mathrm{supp}(\bm{\nu})}\psi_{\nu_{i}}(y_{i}),\quad\bm{y}=(y_{i})^{\infty}_{i=1}\in\mathcal{U},

where
\mathrm{supp}(\bm{\nu})=\{i:\nu_{i}\neq 0\}
is the
support
of
\bm{\nu}
.
It can be shown that the set of such functions
\{\Psi_{\bm{\nu}}:\bm{\nu}\in\mathcal{F}\}\subset L^{2}_{\varrho}(\mathcal{U})

forms an orthonormal basis for
L^{2}_{\varrho}(\mathcal{U})
. Hence, much as in the finite-dimensional case (see §
2.2
), any function
f\in L^{2}_{\varrho}(\mathcal{U})
has an expansion

f=\sum_{\bm{\nu}\in\mathcal{F}}c_{\bm{\nu}}\Psi_{\bm{\nu}},\qquad\text{where }c_{\bm{\nu}}=\langle f,\Psi_{\bm{\nu}}\rangle_{L^{2}_{\varrho}(\mathcal{U})}=\int_{\mathcal{U}}f(\bm{y})\overline{\Psi_{\bm{\nu}}(\bm{y})}\,\mathrm{d}\varrho(\bm{y}).

(36)

5.2
Smoothness and the class of
(b,\varepsilon)
-holomorphic functions

We now introduce the class of holomorphic functions considered. In the univariate setting, it is well known that the convergence rate of a polynomial approximation to
f
is dictated by the largest
Bernstein ellipse
within which
f
is holomorphic (see, e.g.,
[
52
, Chpt. 8]
). Recall that the Bernstein ellipse of parameter
\rho>1
is defined by

\mathcal{E}_{\rho}=\{(z+z^{-1})/2:z\in\mathbb{C},\ 1\leq|z|\leq\rho\}\subset\mathbb{C}.

For convenience, we set
\mathcal{E}_{1}=[-1,1]
. In the infinite-dimensional setting, we consider Cartesian products of Bernstein ellipses. Given a parameter
\bm{\rho}=(\rho_{1},\rho_{2},\ldots)\in[1,\infty)^{\mathbb{N}}
we define the
Bernstein polyellipse
of parameter
\bm{\rho}
as

\mathcal{E}_{\bm{\rho}}=\mathcal{E}_{\rho_{1}}\times\mathcal{E}_{\rho_{2}}\times\cdots\subset\mathbb{C}^{\mathbb{N}}.

Definition 6
(
(\bm{b},\varepsilon)
-holomorphy).

Let
\bm{b}\in[0,\infty)^{\mathbb{N}}
and
\varepsilon>0
. A function
f:\mathcal{U}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
is
(\bm{b},\varepsilon)
-holomorphic if it is holomorphic in every Bernstein polyellipse
\mathcal{E}_{\bm{\rho}}
with parameter
\bm{\rho}=(\rho_{i})^{\infty}_{i=1}\in[1,\infty)^{\mathbb{N}}
satisfying

\sum^{\infty}_{i=1}\left(\frac{\rho_{i}+\rho^{-1}_{i}}{2}-1\right)b_{i}\leq\varepsilon.

(37)

See, e.g.,
[
9
,
45
]
.
For convenience we denote the corresponding region as

\mathcal{R}_{\bm{b},\varepsilon}=\bigcup\{\mathcal{E}_{\bm{\rho}}:\bm{\rho}\in[1,\infty)^{\mathbb{N}},\ \text{$\bm{\rho}$ satisfies \eqref{b-eps-holo}}\}\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{\mathbb{N}}.

We also write
\mathcal{H}(\bm{b},\varepsilon)=\{\text{$f:\mathcal{U}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}$ $(\bm{b},\varepsilon)$-holomorphic},\ {\left\|f\right\|}_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{R}_{\bm{b},\epsilon})}\leq 1\}

for the set of functions that are holomorphic in
\mathcal{R}_{\bm{b},\varepsilon}
with uniform norm at most one.

Note that the sequence
\bm{b}
determines the type of anisotropic behaviour of functions in
\mathcal{H}(\bm{b},\varepsilon)
. Indeed, if
b_{j}
is large, then (
37
) holds only for small values of
\rho_{j}
, meaning that
f
is less smooth with respect to the variable
y_{j}
. Conversely, if
b_{j}
is small (or even
b_{j}=0
), then
f
is more smooth (entire) in the variable
y_{j}
.

The additional parameter
\varepsilon
in Definition
6
is technically redundant. However, it is customary to include it because of the parametric DE context. As observed previously, parametric DEs were the original motivations for the study of this class of functions. As noted (see also §
), the parametric solution maps of many different classes of parametric DEs are
(\bm{b},\varepsilon)
-holomorphic functions.

Remark 7
(Finite-dimensional functions).

Definition
6
is somewhat complicated, in that it requires the function to have a holomorphic extension to a union of Bernstein polyellipses. This is needed in infinite dimensions to obtain algebraic rates of convergence of the best
n
-term approximation. In finite dimensions, it is enough for the function to be holomorphic in a single Bernstein polyellipse. However, any such function can be considered within this definition. Indeed, let
f:[-1,1]^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
be a function of finitely-many variables that is holomorphic in the finite-dimensional Bernstein polyellipse
\mathcal{E}_{\bar{\rho}_{1}}\times\cdots\times\mathcal{E}_{\bar{\rho}_{d}}\subset\mathbb{C}^{d}
. Now let

\displaystyle b_{i}=\varepsilon\left((\bar{\rho}_{i}+\bar{\rho}^{-1}_{i})/2-1\right)^{-1},\ i\in[d],\qquad b_{i}=0,\ i\in\mathbb{N}\backslash[d].

Then the extension of
f
to a function of infinitely-many variables is
(\bm{b},\varepsilon)
-holomorphic. Hence, the various results that follow also apply to finite-dimensional functions.

5.3
Best
n
-term polynomial approximation in
\mathcal{H}(b,\varepsilon)

Let
f\in L^{2}_{\varrho}(\mathcal{U})
. As in §
2.3
, we consider
n
-term approximations to
f
, i.e., those taking the form

f\approx f_{S}:=\sum_{\bm{\nu}\in S}c_{\bm{\nu}}\Psi_{\bm{\nu}},

where, in this case,
S\subset\mathcal{F}
,
|S|=n
. Also as before, we write

f_{n}=f_{S^{*}},\qquad\text{where }S^{*}\in{\underset{}{\operatorname{argmin}}}\{{\|f-f_{S}\|}_{L^{2}_{\varrho}(\mathcal{U})}:S\subset{\mathcal{F}},\ |S|=n\}

for the best
n
-term approximation to
f
.
The following result is well known (see, e.g.,
[
2
, Thm. 3.28]
or
[
13
, §3.2]
, as well as §
). It demonstrates that the best
n
-term approximation of any function in
\mathcal{H}(\bm{b},\varepsilon)
converges with algebraic rate.

Theorem 8
(Algebraic convergence of the best
n
-term approximation).

Let
\varepsilon>0
and
\bm{b}\in[0,\infty)^{\mathbb{N}}
be such that
\bm{b}\in\ell^{p}(\mathbb{N})
for some
0<p<1
. Then

{\|f-f_{n}\|}_{L^{2}_{\varrho}(\mathcal{U})}\leq C=C(\bm{b},\varepsilon,p)\cdot n^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p}},\qquad\forall f\in\mathcal{H}(\bm{b},\varepsilon),\ n\in\mathbb{N}.

Remark 9
(Differences between finite and infinite dimensions).

The appearance of algebraic rates is one way in which the infinite-dimensional setting differs from the
d
-dimensional setting. When
d
is fixed and
n\rightarrow\infty
, the best
n
-term polynomial approximation of any
d
-dimensional function that is holomorphic in a Bernstein polyellipse
\mathcal{E}_{\rho_{1}}\times\cdots\mathcal{E}_{\rho_{d}}
converges exponentially fast in
n^{\frac{1}{d}}
(and therefore faster than any algebraic power of
n
) with the precise rate depending on the parameters
\rho_{1},\ldots,\rho_{d}
(see
[
13
, §3.9]
or
[
2
, §3.5-3.6]
).
However, the number
n^{\frac{1}{d}}
grows exceedingly slowly with
n
for moderate to large
d
. Hence, this asymptotic regime of exponential convergence is almost never encountered in practice, besides low-dimensional cases. In this case, the practical convergence behaviour in finite, but large dimensions is often better described by the above algebraic rates – see next for an illustration.

Remark 10
(The function (
32
)).

The function (
32
) has a singularity at
y_{i}=-1-\delta_{i}
. Hence it is holomorphic in the Bernstein polyellipse
\mathcal{E}_{\rho_{1}}\times\cdots\times\mathcal{E}_{\rho_{d}}
for any
\rho_{i}\geq 1
satisfying
(\rho_{i}+\rho_{i}^{-1})/2<1+\delta_{i}
(recall the left-hand side is major semi-axis length of the Bernstein ellipse
\mathcal{E}_{\rho_{i}}
). Due to Remark
7
, it is therefore
(\bm{b},\varepsilon)
-holomorphic for any
\bm{b}=(b_{i})^{\infty}_{i=1}
with
b_{i}>\varepsilon/\delta_{i}
,
i\in[d]
, and
b_{i}=0
otherwise.
Theorem
8
now implies the dimension-independent algebraic convergence rate of
n^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p}}
whenever the sequence
(1/\delta_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\in\ell^{p}(\mathbb{N})
. In particular, when
\delta_{i}=i^{2}
as in Fig.
4
, this guarantees algebraic convergence with rate arbitrarily close to
n^{-\frac{3}{2}}
in any dimensions. Fig.
9
compares this theoretical rate to the practical performance of ALS. In low dimensions, as noted in the previous remark, the algebraic rate is a poor predictor of the method’s behaviour. However, in higher (
d=32
) dimensions, we see a very close agreement between the theoretical rate and empirical performance.

In the next result, we show that the rate
n^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p}}

This result involves the so-called
weak-
\ell^{p}
space. Let
\bm{c}=(c_{i})^{\infty}_{i=1}
be a sequence and write
\bm{c}^{*}=(c^{*}_{i})^{\infty}_{i=1}
for a nonincreasing rearrangement of the absolute value sequence
(|c_{i}|)^{\infty}_{i=1}
. Then, for
p>0
the
weak
\ell^{p}
-quasinorm
is defined as
{\left\|\bm{c}\right\|}_{p,\infty}=\sup_{i=1,\ldots,\infty}i^{1/p}c^{*}_{i}
.
The weak
\ell^{p}
-space
w\ell^{p}(\mathbb{N})
consists of all sequences for which
{\left\|\bm{c}\right\|}_{p,\infty}<\infty

[
17
]
.

Theorem 11
(Sharpness of the rate
n^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p}}
).

Let
\bm{b}\in[0,\infty)^{\mathbb{N}}
and
\varepsilon>0
. Suppose that
\bm{b}\in\ell^{p}(\mathbb{N})
for some
0<p\leq 1
but
\bm{b}\notin w\ell^{r}(\mathbb{N})
for some
0<r<p
. Then there are infinitely many non-linearly dependent functions
f\in\mathcal{H}(\bm{b},\varepsilon)
such that

\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{{\|f-f_{n}\|}_{L^{2}_{\varrho}(\mathcal{U})}}{n^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{r}}}=+\infty.

Note that there exist sequences with
\bm{b}\in\ell^{p}(\mathbb{N})
but
\bm{b}\notin w\ell^{r}(\mathbb{N})
for
any

0<r<p
. For example, the sequence
b_{i}=(i\log^{2}(i))^{-1/p}
has this property.

6
Near-best least-squares polynomial approximation in infinite dimensions with Monte Carlo sampling

We now present our first main theoretical result on polynomial approximation from MC samples. This shows that, in infinite dimensions, MC sampling is
near best
, in the sense that there is a LS procedure that achieves the same algebraic rates as the best
n
-term approximation, up to a log factor.

Theorem 12
(MC sampling is near-best for known
\bm{b}
and
\varepsilon
).

Let
0<\epsilon<1
,
\varrho
be either the uniform or Chebyshev measure on
\mathcal{U}=[-1,1]^{\mathbb{N}}
,
\bm{b}\in[0,\infty)^{\mathbb{N}}
with
\bm{b}\in\ell^{p}(\mathbb{N})
for some
0<p<1
,
\varepsilon>0
,
m\geq 3
and
\bm{y}_{1},\ldots,\bm{y}_{m}\sim_{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}\varrho
. Then there exists a set
S\subset\mathcal{F}
(depending on
\bm{b}
and
\varepsilon
only) of cardinality
|S|\leq\left\lceil m/\log(m/\epsilon)\right\rceil

such that the following holds with probability at least
1-\epsilon
for each fixed
f\in\mathcal{H}(\bm{b},\varepsilon)
. For any
\bm{e}\in\mathbb{C}^{m}
, the LS approximation

\hat{f}={\underset{p\in\mathcal{P}_{S}}{\operatorname{argmin}}}{\frac{1}{m}}\sum^{m}_{i=1}|f(\bm{y}_{i})+e_{i}-p(\bm{y}_{i})|^{2}

is unique and satisfies

{\|f-\hat{f}\|}_{L^{2}_{\varrho}(\mathcal{U})}\leq C(\bm{b},\varepsilon,p)\cdot\left(m/\log(m/\epsilon)\right)^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p}}+2\cdot{\|\bm{e}\|}_{\infty}.

Moreover, the condition number of the LS matrix
\bm{A}
satisfies
\mathrm{cond}(\bm{A})\leq 2
.

We give the proof of this result in §
. Its main ingredient is showing the existence of a set
S
of size
|S|\leq k:=\frac{m}{\log(m/\epsilon)}
for which: (i) the error
{\|f-f_{S}\|}_{L^{2}_{\varrho}(\mathcal{U})}
is
\mathcal{O}(k^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p}})
, and (ii) the constant
\kappa(\mathcal{P}_{S})
in (
22
) is at most
\mathcal{O}\left(k\right)
.
Having done this, the result follows from Theorem
2
with
p=f_{S}
in (
19
), albeit after taking some additional care to bound discrete norm
{\|f-p\|}_{\mathsf{disc},1}
.

Note that the error bound of Theorem
12
(also Theorem
16
later) depends on
\|\bm{e}\|_{\infty}
, which allows for bounded, adversarial noise corrupting the samples. The noise vector
\bm{e}
might be either deterministic or random. In the random case, it could be, e.g., of the form
e_{i}=g(\bm{y}_{i})
for some function
g
for which
\|g\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{U})}
is finite. Note that it is possible to deal with unbounded random noise models using tools from
[
38
]
.

Remark 13
(The function (
32
)).

Recall Remark
10
and the function
f_{3}
with
\delta_{i}=i^{2}
. Theorem
12
implies that there exists a least-squares approximation based on MC sampling that achieves the rate
m^{-\frac{3}{2}+\gamma}
for arbitrarily small
\gamma>0
. It does not show that the ALS scheme achieves this rate: as discussed, there is no theoretical guarantee that ALS selects ‘good’ index sets in practice. However, Fig.

suggests that ALS does appear to achieve the theoretical rate, at least in the case of this function.

Remark 14
.

In §
4.5.2
we considered a function of one variable
f(\bm{y})=g(y_{1})
, wherein
\kappa(\mathcal{P}_{S^{*}})=n^{2}
, where
S^{*}
,
|S^{*}|=n
, is the index set corresponding to the best
n
-term approximation. See (
34
). Such pathological examples do not contradict Theorem
12
, even though stable polynomial approximation in
\mathcal{P}_{S^{*}}
necessitates a quadratic scaling of
m
with
n
in such cases. The reason is that the best
n
-term approximation error decays exponentially fast in
n
for such functions (Remark
9
), and therefore root exponentially fast in
m
. But this is still faster than the algebraic convergence rate asserted in Theorem
12
. A similar argument applies for functions of
d
-variables, since in such cases, the best
n
-approximation error decays exponentially-fast in
n^{1/d}
.
In general, it is the presence of large multi-indices in
S
that cause
\kappa(\mathcal{P}_{S})
to scale quadratically in
n=|S|
. Theorem
12
essentially says that algebraic rates of convergence can be obtained without using multi-indices that are too large.

6.1
Weighted
k
-term approximation

As a way to motivate the approach considered in the next section, we now elaborate on the construction employed in Theorem
12
. This is based on
weighted
(k,\bm{u})
-term approximation

[
44
]
. Let

\bm{u}=(u_{\bm{\nu}})_{\bm{\nu}\in\mathcal{F}},\qquad\text{where }u_{\bm{\nu}}={\|\Psi_{\bm{\nu}}\|}_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{U})},\ \forall\bm{\nu}\in\mathcal{F},

(38)

and observe that
u_{\bm{\nu}}\geq{\|\Psi_{\bm{\nu}}\|}_{L^{2}_{\varrho}(\mathcal{U})}=1
since
\varrho
is a probability measure and the
\Psi_{\bm{\nu}}
are orthonormal. Next, we define the weighted cardinality of a set
S\subset\mathcal{F}
as
|S|_{\bm{u}}=\sum_{\bm{\nu}\in S}u^{2}_{\bm{\nu}}
.
Then, for
k\geq 0
(note that
k
need not be an integer in this case) we define the
weighted best
(k,\bm{u})
-term approximation
to
f
as

f_{k,\bm{u}}=f_{S^{*}},\qquad\text{where }S^{*}\in{\underset{}{\operatorname{argmin}}}\{{\|f-f_{S}\|}_{L^{2}_{\varrho}(\mathcal{U})}:S\subset\mathcal{F},\ |S|_{\bm{u}}\leq k\}.

In the proof of Theorem
12
, we exploit the key fact that

{\|f-f_{k,\bm{u}}\|}_{L^{2}_{\varrho}(\mathcal{U})}\leq C(\bm{b},\varepsilon,p)\cdot k^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p}},\quad\forall f\in\mathcal{H}(\bm{b},\varepsilon).

(39)

Thus, the set
S^{*}=S
provides a suitable choice in terms of (i) above. However, it transpires that it is also suitable in the terms of (ii). Indeed, consider any set
S\subseteq\mathcal{F}
with weighted cardinality
|S|_{\bm{u}}\leq k
. Then (
22
) states that the sample complexity of LS in the subspace
\mathcal{P}_{S}
with MC sampling is determined by the constant

\kappa(\mathcal{P}_{S})={\|\mathcal{K}(\mathcal{P}_{S})\|}_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{U})}=\sup_{\bm{y}\in\mathcal{U}}\sum_{\bm{\nu}\in S}|\Psi_{\bm{\nu}}(\bm{y})|^{2}.

However, Chebyshev and Legendre polynomials attain the maximum absolute value at the point
\bm{y}=\bm{1}
. Hence

\kappa(\mathcal{P}_{S})=\sum_{\bm{\nu}\in S}|\Psi_{\bm{\nu}}(\bm{1})|^{2}=\sum_{\bm{\nu}\in S}{\|\Psi_{\bm{\nu}}\|}_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{U})}=|S|_{\bm{u}}\leq k.

(40)

This is the essence of the proof. Sets of fixed weighted cardinality a ‘good’ candidates for MC sampling due to (
40
), but also good for approximating functions, due to (
39
).

7
Near-best polynomial approximation in infinite dimensions via compressed sensing

Theorem
12
asserts the existence of a set
S
for which the corresponding LS approximation from MC samples converges with the desired algebraic rate. It says nothing about how to construct such a set in practice. At the very least, this set depends on the anisotropy parameters
\bm{b}
and
\varepsilon
, which are generally unknown.

We now show how it is possible to compute polynomial approximations which attain the same rates of convergence without any knowledge of these parameters, subject to a slightly stronger assumption on
\bm{b}
.
Furthermore, we also demonstrate that these lead to practical methods, with performance comparable or sometimes better than the ALS approximation studied previously.

7.1
Polynomial approximation via compressed sensing

Originating in the works
[
20
,
33
,
43
]
, polynomial approximation via
Compressed Sensing (CS)
is now well established.
In this section we primarily follow the layout of
[
2
, Chpt. 7]
, which is based on ideas originating in
[
44
]
.

In §
6.1
, we observed that any function in
\mathcal{H}(\bm{b},\varepsilon)
can be approximated with algebraically-decay error using its weighted best
(k,\bm{u})
-term approximation. However, the set
S^{*}
that yields this approximation is unknown. The idea now is to use CS techniques to promote such approximate
weighted sparsity
by using a weighted
\ell^{1}
-norm penalty term based on the weights (
38
).

Before doing this, however, we first need to truncate the infinite expansion (
7
). Let
\Lambda\subset\mathcal{F}
be a finite multi-index set and write
f_{\Lambda}=\sum_{\bm{\nu}\in\Lambda}c_{\bm{\nu}}\Psi_{\bm{\nu}}
for the truncated expansion of
f
. Then, given samples points
\bm{y}_{1},\ldots,\bm{y}_{m}
, we have

f(\bm{y}_{i})+e_{i}=f_{\Lambda}(\bm{y}_{i})+(f-f_{\Lambda})(\bm{y}_{i})+e_{i}=\sum_{\bm{\nu}\in\Lambda}c_{\bm{\nu}}\Psi_{\bm{\nu}}(\bm{y}_{i})+(f-f_{\Lambda})(\bm{y}_{i})+e_{i}.

Now let
\bm{\nu}_{1},\ldots,\bm{\nu}_{N}
be an enumeration of
\Lambda
and
\bm{c}_{\Lambda}=(c_{\bm{\nu}_{i}})^{N}_{i=1}
. Then we have

\bm{f}=\left((f(\bm{y}_{i})+e_{i})/\sqrt{m}\right)^{m}_{i=1}=\bm{A}\bm{c}_{\Lambda}+\bm{n},

(41)

where

\bm{A}=\left(\Psi_{\bm{\nu}_{j}}(\bm{y}_{i})/\sqrt{m}\right)^{m,N}_{i,j=1}\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times N},\quad\bm{n}=\left(((f-f_{\Lambda})(\bm{y}_{i})+e_{i})/\sqrt{m}\right)^{m}_{i=1}\in\mathbb{C}^{m}.

(42)

The idea now is to search for (approximate) solutions of (
41
) that have small
\ell^{1}_{\bm{u}}
-norm. We do this via the following
weighted square-root LASSO
program:

\hat{f}=\sum_{\bm{\nu}\in\Lambda}\hat{c}_{\bm{\nu}}\Psi_{\bm{\nu}},\quad\text{where }\hat{\bm{c}}=(\hat{c}_{\bm{\nu}})_{\bm{\nu}\in\Lambda}\in{\underset{\bm{z}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}}{\operatorname{argmin}}}\lambda{\|\bm{z}\|}_{1,\bm{u}}+{\|\bm{A}\bm{z}-\bm{f}\|}_{2}.

Here
{\|\bm{z}\|}_{1,\bm{u}}=\sum_{\bm{\nu}\in\Lambda}u_{\bm{\nu}}|z_{\bm{\nu}}|
is the
\ell^{1}_{\bm{u}}
-norm and
\lambda>0
is a parameter.

7.2
Truncation error and anchored sets

It remains to choose the set
\Lambda
. This set must be sufficiently large so that the truncation error
f-f_{\Lambda}
is no larger than the approximation error (
39
). However,
\Lambda
must also be a finite set.
To ensure this property, we now introduce the concept of anchored sets. A multi-index set
S\subset\mathcal{F}
is
anchored
if it is lower and if, for every
j\in\mathbb{N}
,

\bm{e}_{j}\in S\ \Rightarrow\ \{\bm{e}_{1},\ldots,\bm{e}_{j}\}\subseteq S.

Here
\bm{e}_{j}
denotes the sequence with one in its
j
th entry and zero elsewhere. We now need the following result (see, e.g.,
[
2
, Thm. 3.33]
or
[
13
, §3.8]
), which states that near-best
n
-term
can be attained in anchored sets, for certain
\bm{b}
.

Theorem 15
(Algebraic convergence in anchored sets).

Let
\varepsilon>0
and
\bm{b}\in[0,\infty)^{\mathbb{N}}
be monotonically nonincreasing and such that
\bm{b}\in\ell^{p}(\mathbb{N})
for some
0<p<1
. Then, for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists an anchored set
S\subset\mathcal{F}
,
|S|=n
, such that

{\|f-f_{S}\|}_{L^{2}_{\varrho}(\mathcal{U})}\leq C(\bm{b},\varepsilon,p)\cdot n^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p}},\qquad\forall f\in\mathcal{H}(\bm{b},\varepsilon),\ n\in\mathbb{N}.

With this in hand, we now construct
\Lambda
so that it contains all anchored sets of a given size
n
. This turns out to be a finite set (see, e.g.,
[
15
]
or
[
2
, Prop. 2.18]
):

\bigcup\{S\subset\mathcal{F}:|S|\leq n,\text{ $S$ anchored}\}\subset\Lambda^{\mathsf{HCI}}_{n},

where

\Lambda^{\mathsf{HCI}}_{n}=\left\{\bm{\nu}=(\nu_{k})^{\infty}_{k=1}\in\mathcal{F}:\prod^{n-1}_{k=1}(\nu_{k}+1)\leq n,\ \nu_{k}=0,\ \forall k\geq n\right\},

(43)

is isomorphic to the
(n-1)
-dimensional hyperbolic cross index set of order
n-1
.

7.3
Near-best polynomial approximation for unknown
b
and
\varepsilon

We now present the main result of this section. For this, we need one additional concept. Let
\bm{b}=(b_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence. We define its
minimal monotone majorant
as the sequence
\tilde{\bm{b}}=(\tilde{b}_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
, where
\tilde{b}_{i}=\sup_{j\geq i}{|b_{j}|}
,
\forall i\in\mathbb{N}
.
Then, given
0<p<\infty
, we define the
monotone
\ell^{p}
space
\ell^{p}_{\mathsf{M}}(\mathbb{N})
as

\ell^{p}_{\mathsf{M}}(\mathbb{N})=\{\bm{b}\in\ell^{\infty}(\mathbb{N}):{\|\bm{b}\|}_{p,\mathsf{M}}:={\|\tilde{\bm{b}}\|}_{p}<\infty\}.

Theorem 16
(MC sampling is near-best for unknown
\bm{b}
and
\varepsilon
).

Let
0<\epsilon<1
,
\varrho
be either the uniform or Chebyshev measure on
\mathcal{U}=[-1,1]^{\mathbb{N}}
,
m\geq 3
and
\bm{y}_{1},\ldots,\bm{y}_{m}\sim_{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}\varrho
. Let
\Lambda=\Lambda^{\mathsf{HCI}}_{n}
, where

n=\left\lceil m/L(m,\epsilon)\right\rceil,\qquad L(m,\epsilon):=\log(m)\cdot(\log^{3}(m)+\log(\epsilon^{-1})),

\varepsilon>0
and
\bm{b}\in[0,\infty)^{\mathbb{N}}
with
\bm{b}\in\ell^{p}_{\mathsf{M}}(\mathbb{N})
for some
0<p<1
. Then the following holds with probability at least
1-\epsilon
for each fixed
f\in\mathcal{H}(\bm{b},\varepsilon)
. For any
\bm{e}\in\mathbb{C}^{m}
, every

\hat{\bm{c}}=(\hat{c}_{\bm{\nu}})_{\bm{\nu}\in\Lambda}\in{\underset{\bm{z}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}}{\operatorname{argmin}}}\lambda{\|\bm{z}\|}_{1,\bm{u}}+{\|\bm{A}\bm{z}-\bm{f}\|}_{2},

(44)

where
\bm{A}
and
\bm{f}

are as in (
41
)–(
42
) and
\lambda=(4\sqrt{m/L(m,\epsilon)})^{-1}
, yields an approximation
\hat{f}=\sum_{\bm{\nu}\in\Lambda}\hat{c}_{\bm{\nu}}\Psi_{\bm{\nu}}
satisfying

{\|f-\hat{f}\|}_{L^{2}_{\varrho}(\mathcal{U})}\leq C=C(\bm{b},\varepsilon,p)\cdot\left(m/L(m,\epsilon)\right)^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p}}+c\cdot{\left\|\bm{e}\right\|}_{\infty}.

Here
c\geq 1
is a universal constant. Furthermore, given
\hat{\bm{c}}
it is possible to compute a set
S\subset\Lambda
of cardinality
|S|\leq\left\lceil m/L(m,\epsilon)\right\rceil

for which
\hat{f}_{S}=\sum_{\bm{\nu}\in S}\hat{c}_{\bm{\nu}}\Psi_{\bm{\nu}}
satisfies

{\|f-\hat{f}_{S}\|}_{L^{2}_{\varrho}(\mathcal{U})}\leq 10\cdot C(\bm{b},\varepsilon,p)\cdot\left(m/L(m,\epsilon)\right)^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p}}+3\cdot c\cdot{\left\|\bm{e}\right\|}_{\infty}.

This result is an extension of
[
1
, Thm. 3.7]
– see §
for further details. Comparing it with Theorem
12
, we conclude the following. In the absence of knowledge about the parameters
\bm{b}
and
\varepsilon
, it is still possible to obtain the same algebraic rates of convergence using MC sampling, up to a larger polylogarithmic factor
L(m,\epsilon)
, subject to the slightly stronger assumption
\bm{b}\in\ell^{p}_{\mathsf{M}}(\mathbb{N})
. In particular, MC sampling remains near-optimal in infinite dimensions, even when the anisotropy parameters are unknown. Note that (
44
) yields a polynomial approximation
\hat{f}
that generally has many more nonzero terms than the approximation in Theorem
12
, since it is a polynomial defined over the large index set
\Lambda=\Lambda^{\mathsf{HCI}}_{n}
. The second part of Theorem
16
states that one can compute a polynomial approximation with a comparable number of terms to that of Theorem
12
that also achieves the desired rate.

Note that
\ell^{p}_{\mathsf{M}}(\mathbb{N})\subset\ell^{p}(\mathbb{N})
and that
{\left\|\bm{b}\right\|}_{p,\mathsf{M}}={\left\|\bm{b}\right\|}_{p}
whenever
\bm{b}
is monotonically nonincreasing. Monotonicity of
\bm{b}
means that the variables are ordered in terms of importance. Thus, the assumption
\ell^{p}_{\mathsf{M}}(\mathbb{N})
in effect says that important variables, while not necessarily being ordered, cannot occur at arbitrarily high indices.

We remark in passing that this theorem does not give an algorithm for computing a minimizer (
44
). However, it has been shown in
[
1
]
that this can be done via efficient iterative algorithms. We use such algorithms in the numerical experiments in the next subsection. See §
and §
8
for further details and discussion.

The worse performance of CS with MC sampling in low dimensions can be largely attributed to the sampling strategy. To see this, we also compute a CS approximation where the samples are drawn randomly from the measure

]
.

\eta

\log(\eta^{-1})
. See §
for further information.

8
Conclusions

The purpose of this paper has been to show that MC sampling is a not only a good sampling strategy for polynomial approximation in high dimensions, but, in fact, a near-optimal one. Hence, efforts to design optimal sampling strategies are, for this problem at least, only effective in low dimensions.

8.1
Caveats

There are several caveats we now mention. First, our study is limited to polynomial approximation. There are many other popular tools for high-dimensional approximation which may outperform such schemes in practice. We mention in passing Gaussian process regression (kriging), kernel methods, reduced-order models and, recently, deep neural networks. These approaches are outside the scope of this work. Second, our study is also limited to bounded hypercubes, which we take to be
[-1,1]^{\mathbb{N}}
without loss of generality. The situation in either the half space
[0,\infty)^{\mathbb{N}}
or whole space
\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}
is quite different. Here MC sampling performs substantially worse even in moderately large dimensions
[
24
,
41
]
. Whether it is optimal in infinite dimensions is currently unknown. Third, our study is limited to the Chebyshev and uniform measures on
[-1,1]^{\mathbb{N}}
. However, we expect identical conclusions to hold for more general Jacobi measures. See §
for some further discussion on this point. Fourth, in this paper we have only considered errors measured in the
L^{2}_{\varrho}
-norm. In many applications, one also desires pointwise control of the functions being approximated, i.e.,
L^{\infty}
-norm errors. Empirically, one observes the same effect if the error is measured in this norm instead (see §
for several such experiments). Whether our theoretical analysis extends to the
L^{\infty}
-norm is currently unknown.

Fifth, our analysis is also limited to classes of holomorphic functions. Clearly, other classes – in particular, classes of piecewise smooth functions or functions with local features – stand to benefit from changing the sampling strategy, e.g., in an adaptive, function-dependent manner.
However, we note that MC sampling has also shown to be optimal for approximation in Sobolev spaces
[
30
]
.

Finally, as mentioned in §
1.4.5
, there is a gap between our theoretical analysis and our numerical examples.
Fig.
9
and Remark
10
suggest that the infinite-dimensional analysis does indeed govern the empirical performance in finite (and moderate) dimensions, at least for some functions. Further investigation is needed in this direction.

8.2
Future work

Our main results are
nonuniform
guarantees, in the sense that a single draw of the sample points ensures the error bound for each fixed
f\in\mathcal{H}(\bm{b},\varepsilon)
with high probability. Optimal and near-optimal sampling measures that yield
uniform
guarantees for weighted LS approximation, i.e., those holding simultaneously for all functions in a certain class, can be constructed. See, e.g.,
[
31
]
. We believe these techniques could be modified to prove uniform guarantees for LS with MC sampling in the class
\mathcal{H}(\bm{b},\varepsilon)
. See also
[
2
, Thm. 7.13]
in the case of CS.

The numerical comparison in §
7.4
warrants some further discussion. In parametric modelling and UQ, the substantial computational burden is usually in generating the samples. Nonetheless, it is worth noting that the CS scheme is more expensive than the ALS scheme, both in terms of time and memory. For both schemes, the cost-per-iteration of an iterative solver – e.g., conjugate gradients in the case of LS or the scheme described in §
for CS – is proportional to
mp
(the cost of matrix-vector multiplications) and the number of iterations needed for an accuracy of
\eta
is proportional to
\log(\eta^{-1})
. Here
p
is the number of columns of the matrix
\bm{A}
. For LS, we have
p=|S|\leq m
. However, in the CS it is much larger: namely,
p=|\Lambda|
, where
\Lambda
is the truncated index set. In
[
1
]
it was shown that the computational cost per iteration for achieving the rates of Theorem
16
is
subexponential
in
m
, specifically,
\mathcal{O}\left(m^{1+(1+\alpha)\log_{2}(4m)}\right)
, where
\alpha=\log(3)/\log(4)\approx 0.79
. This situation arises because
\Lambda=\Lambda^{\mathsf{HCI}}_{n}
is an isotropic index set, even though the underlying function is anisotropic.

Whether it is possible to achieve the same rates as Theorem
16
with a polynomial-time algorithm is currently unknown. Recent work of
[
11
]
may provide an answer. Interestingly, the algorithms in
[
11
]
use structured sampling strategies to achieve polynomial-time complexity. Hence, this could lead to the interesting conclusion that MC sampling is acceptable for approximation purposes, but not for fast computations. Conversely, if one is willing to forgo theoretical guarantees with MC sampling, then one can develop significantly more efficient CS-based schemes using
basis adaptivity

[
29
,
28
]
, in which
\Lambda
is adapted at each step to the anisotropy of the function.
