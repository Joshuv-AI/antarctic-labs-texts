Minimal Semisimple Hessenberg Schemes
Rebecca Goldin, Martha Precup

Abstract
. We study a collection of Hessenberg varieties in the type A flag variety associated to a nonzero semisimple matrix whose conjugacy class has minimal dimension. We prove each such minimal semisimple Hessenberg variety is a union Richardson varieties and compute this set of Richardson varieties explicitly. Our methods leverage the notion of matrix Hessenberg schemes to answer questions about the geometry of minimal semisimple Hessenberg varieties using commutative algebra and known results on Schubert determinantal ideals. In particular, we show that all type A minimal semisimple matrix Hessenberg schemes are reduced.

Introduction

Let

In this paper, we consider Hessenberg varieties in the flag variety

a

is a minimal element, then we say that

.

Consider the minimal nilpotent element

, where

is the elementary matrix with unique nonzero entry equal to

in row

and column

. Conjugation by

The minimal nilpotent Hessenberg varieties

]

[

]
, where they identify

[
GP
]
we prove that these Hessenberg schemes lie in flat families over lines in the union of all minimal orbits, and determine the (generally non-reduced) scheme structure on minimal nilpotent Hessenberg varieties as well.

Throughout this paper, we identify

. Denote by

on

denote the permutation matrix with entry equal to

. Our convention is that the

\displaystyle Y_{\mathsf{x},h}:=\{V_{\bullet}\mid\mathsf{x}(V_{i})\subseteq V_{h(i)}\}

If

We employ the term

In this article we characterize minimal semisimple Hessenberg varieties

.
Following Insko–Tymoczko–Woo

We show in Sections

that, for all

, it suffices to consider the matrix Hessenberg scheme defined using the minimal semisimple element

In Theorem

, we
identify all components of

.
As a consequence,

Theorem 1
.

For all Hessenberg functions

is equal to a reduced union of Richardson varieties,

The top-dimensional components are detected by its cohomology class in

In a companion paper, we show that the minimal semisimple and nilpotent matrix Hessenberg schemes lie in a flat family

Let

Let

For any

Subvarieties of the flag variety equal to the intersection of a Schubert and opposite Schubert variety are called
Richardson varieties

Lemma 2.2

For all

Observe that
\mathcal{L}_{i}=\left<z_{11},z_{12},\ldots,z_{1i}\right>

Next, we argue that the product of ideals
\mathcal{L}_{i}\cdot\mathcal{K}_{j}
is radical.

Lemma 2.4
.

For all
i,j\in[n-1]
, the product
\mathcal{L}_{i}\cdot\mathcal{K}_{j}

Proof.

Let
\preceq

[

]

Let
\preceq
be any diagonal term order on
\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{z}]
. It is well known (see e.g.
[

, Thm. B]
) that

\{p_{B}\mid B\subseteq\{2,\ldots,n\},|B|=j\}

is a Gröbner basis for
\mathcal{K}_{j}
with respect to this ordering and it’s obvious that

\mathcal{L}_{i}

. The leading term of every polynomial in this Gröbner basis is square free, so
\mathcal{L}_{i}\cdot\mathcal{K}_{j}
is radical. Thus

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{i}\cdot\mathcal{K}_{j}=\mathrm{rad}(\mathcal{L}_{i}\cdot\mathcal{K}_{j})=\mathrm{rad}(\mathcal{L}_{i}\cap\mathcal{K}_{j})=\mathrm{rad}(\mathcal{L}_{i})\cap\mathrm{rad}(\mathcal{K}_{j})=\mathcal{L}_{i}\cap\mathcal{K}_{j}

3.
Matrix Hessenberg schemes

Recall from the Introduction that a function
h:[n]\to[n]
is a
Hessenberg function
if
h(i-1)\leq h(i)
and
h(i)\geq i
for all
i
.
We denote a Hessenberg function
h:[n]\to[n]
by listing its values, e.g.
h=(h(1),\ldots,h(n))
.
Although
0
is not technically in the domain of
h
, it will be convenient for us to assume
h(0)=0
.

There is a bijection from the set of all Hessenberg functions
h:[n]\to[n]
to the set of Dyck paths of length
2n
, as follows. Each Hessenberg function
h
may be uniquely identified with a lattice path from the upper left corner of an
n\times n
grid to the lower right corner obtained by requiring that the horizontal step in column
i
occurs
h(i)
rows from the top. We call the resulting image
diagram of
h
. The diagrams of the functions
h_{1}=(2,3,3,4)
and
h_{2}=(2,2,4,4)

are displayed below.

The diagrams help visualize examples presented in later sections.

For
j\in[n]
, let
v_{j}
denote the

th column vector of the matrix

, i.e.

(3.1)

v_{j}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}z_{ij}e_{i}.

Let
\mathsf{x}\in\mathfrak{gl}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
. Define the ideal
\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{x},h,i}\subseteq\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{z}]
as follows. If

, then

.
For each

such that

generated by the rank condition:

\displaystyle\mathrm{rk}\begin{bmatrix}|&|&&|&|&|&&|\\
\mathsf{x}v_{1}&\mathsf{x}v_{2}&\cdots&\mathsf{x}v_{i}&v_{1}&v_{2}&\cdots&v_{h(i)}\\
|&|&&|&|&|&&|\end{bmatrix}\leq h(i).

That is,
\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{x},h,i}
is the ideal generated by all

Remark 3.1

When

\displaystyle\mathrm{rk}\begin{bmatrix}z_{11}&z_{12}&\cdots&z_{1i}&z_{11}&z_{12}&\cdots&z_{1h(i)}\\

\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&0&z_{n1}&z_{n2}&\cdots&z_{nh(i)}\end{bmatrix}\leq h(i).

In particular,
\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{s},h,i}=\mathcal{L}_{i}\cdot\mathcal{K}_{h(i)}

We define

(3.3)

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{x},h}:=\sum_{i\in[n]}\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{x},h,i}\subseteq\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{z}].

Consider the action of
B
on

by right multiplication. This induces an action of

on
\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{z}]
defined on each variable

by

z_{ij}\cdot b=\sum_{k}z_{ik}b_{kj}=\sum_{k\leq j}b_{kj}z_{ik}

by a nonzero value, and adding any multiple of ‘earlier’ columns

.
Note that the ideal
\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{x},h}
is obviously invariant under this action of

since the rank condition (

Recall that
G=GL_{n}(\mathbb{C})
is a principal open subset of the affine scheme

of

matrices. As is typical, we identify

as an affine subscheme of
\mathrm{Spec}(\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{z},y])
with coordinate ring
\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{z},d^{-1}]:=\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{z},y]/\left<yd-1\right>
obtained via localization, where
d\in\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{z}]
denotes the determinant function
\det(Z)
. Let

(3.4)

\displaystyle\iota:\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{z}]\hookrightarrow\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{z},d^{-1}]

denote the canonical injection. Given an ideal
\mathcal{I}\subseteq\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{z}]
, we write
\widetilde{\mathcal{I}}
to denote the preimage of the ideal generated by
\iota(\mathcal{I})
.

Definition 3.2
.

Given a matrix
\mathsf{x}\in\mathfrak{gl}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
and Hessenberg function
h:[n]\to[n]
, the associated
matrix Hessenberg scheme
is the affine scheme

\mathcal{Y}_{\mathsf{x},h}:=\mathrm{Spec}(\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{z}]/\widetilde{\mathcal{I}}_{\mathsf{x},h}).

Recall that
\pi:G\to G/B
defines a correspondence between (right)
B
-invariant subschemes of
G
and subschemes of
G/B
.
Thus, given a Hessenberg scheme
Y_{\mathsf{x},h}

in the flag variety, we may instead consider the (right)
B
-invariant subscheme
\pi^{-1}(Y_{\mathsf{x},h})
of
GL_{n}(\mathbb{C})
. By construction, the matrix Hessenberg scheme
\mathcal{Y}_{\mathsf{x},h}
is precisely the Zariski closure of
\pi^{-1}(Y_{\mathsf{x},h})
in
M_{n}(\mathbb{C})=\mathrm{Spec}(\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{z}])
. Furthermore, though the inclusion

\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{x},h}\subset\widetilde{\mathcal{I}}_{\mathsf{x},h}

may be strict,
\mathcal{Y}_{\mathsf{x},h}
is precisely the closure of the open set of prime ideals in the scheme
\mathrm{Spec}(\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{z}]/\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{x},h})
that do not contain the determinant function
d
.
We summarize these observations in the following remark.

Remark 3.3
.

Given the matrix
\mathsf{x}\in\mathfrak{gl}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
and Hessenberg function
h:[n]\to[n]
, let
Y_{\mathsf{x},h}
denote corresponding the Hessenberg scheme in the flag variety
GL_{n}(\mathbb{C})/B
(also refered to as the Hessenberg variety). Then

(1)

\pi^{-1}(Y_{\mathsf{x},h})=GL_{n}(\mathbb{C})\cap\mathrm{Spec}(\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{z}]/\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{x},h})
, and

(2)

\mathcal{Y}_{\mathsf{x},h}=\overline{\pi^{-1}(Y_{\mathsf{x},h})}
.

A natural question is to identify suitable generators of
\widetilde{\mathcal{I}}_{\mathsf{x},h}
. Here “suitable” means explicit generators that can be used for computation using commutative algebra, such as a Gröbner basis. In general, the ideals
\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{x},h}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{I}}_{\mathsf{x},h}
may be distinct, demonstrated by Example
3.4
.
However, when the ideals
are
equal, we can leverage tools from commutative algebra on
\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{z}]
to study matrix Hessenberg schemes via the generators of
\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{x},h}
. This is exactly the approach of our arguments below for
\mathsf{x}
a minimal semisimple element.

While the equations defining
\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{x},h}
are given by rank conditions, it isn’t obvious how to write down equations defining
\widetilde{\mathcal{I}}_{\mathsf{x},h}
.
For example, Berget and Fink
[
BF18
]
study ideals defining certain torus orbits in the set of
r\times n
matrices over
\mathbb{C}
, and find that even in this case
the answer is not straightforward.

Let

Remark 3.5
.

Let
h
be a fixed Hessenberg function. Matrix Hessenberg schemes for
h
corresponding to conjugate matrices are isomorphic. Indeed, suppose
\mathsf{x},\mathsf{x}^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{gl}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
such that

\mathsf{x}=g\mathsf{x}^{\prime}g^{-1}
for some
g=(g_{ij})_{1\leq i,j\leq n}\in G
. The action of
g
on the matrix
Z\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{z}])
by left multiplication induces an isomorphism
\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{z}]\to\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{z}]
defined by
z_{ij}\mapsto g_{i1}z_{1j}+g_{i2}z_{2j}+\cdots+g_{in}z_{nj}
for each
1\leq i,j\leq n
. This isomorphism maps
\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{x},h}
onto
\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{x}^{\prime},h}
and thus results in an isomorphism of schemes.

The definition of the ideal
\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{x},h}
given in (
3.3
) is overdetermined by the sum. It is sufficient to restrict to ‘corners’ of the Hessenberg function
h
.

Definition 3.6
.

Let
h:[n]\to[n]
be a Hessenberg function. We say
i\in[n]
is a
corner of
h
whenever
h(i)>h(i-1)
. Denote the set of all corners by
\mathcal{C}(h)
.

Our convention that
h(0)=0
implies
1
is always a corner of
h
. The corners of
h
are an indexing set for the columns in the diagram of
h
containing corners. More specifically,
i\in\mathcal{C}(h)
implies that
(h(i),i)
is a corner box in the diagram of
h
.
For each
i\in[n]
, let

Notice that if
i
is a corner of
h
, then
i^{*}+1\in\mathcal{C}(h)
. Furthermore, there is no corner
k
with
i<k<i^{*}+1
in
\mathcal{C}(h)
.

Lemma 3.8
.

For all Hessenberg functions
h
and matrices
\mathsf{x}\in\mathfrak{gl}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
,

\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{x},h}=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{C}(h)}\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{x},h,i^{*}}.

Recall that
\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{x},h,n}=0
, so the corner
i\in\mathcal{C}(h)
such that
h(i)=n
does not contribute to the sum.

Proof.

The desired formula follows from that fact that if
i,j\in[n]
such that
i<j
and
h(i)=h(j)
, then
\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{x},h,i}\subseteq\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{x},h,j}
. Indeed, our assumptions imply that the matrix

(3.5)

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}|&|&&|&|&|&&|\\
\mathsf{x}v_{1}&\mathsf{x}v_{2}&\cdots&\mathsf{x}v_{i}&v_{1}&v_{2}&\cdots&v_{h(i)}\\
|&|&&|&|&|&&|\end{bmatrix}

can be obtained from

(3.6)

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}|&|&&|&|&|&&|\\
\mathsf{x}v_{1}&\mathsf{x}v_{2}&\cdots&\mathsf{x}v_{j}&v_{1}&v_{2}&\cdots&v_{h(j)}\\
|&|&&|&|&|&&|\end{bmatrix}

by deleting the columns
\mathsf{x}v_{i+1},\ldots,\mathsf{x}v_{j}
. Furthermore, since
h(i)=h(j)
, the collection of all
(h(i)+1)\times(h(i)+1)
-minors of (
3.5
) is a subset of all
(h(j)+1)\times(h(j)+1)
-minors of (
3.6
). Thus
\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{x},h,i}\subseteq\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{x},h,j}
as desired.
∎

4.
The main theorem

Suppose
\mathsf{x}\in\mathfrak{gl}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
is a minimal semisimple element. It is straightforward to show that any such matrix is conjugate to
\mathsf{s}^{\prime}=\operatorname{diag}(c_{1},c_{2},\ldots,c_{2})
for some
c_{1},c_{2}\in\mathbb{C}
such that
c_{1}\neq c_{2}
. The rank inequalities of (
3.2
) are satisfied for
\mathsf{s}^{\prime}
if and only if they are satisfied for
\mathsf{s}^{\prime\prime}=\operatorname{diag}(c_{1}-c_{2},0,\dots,0)
which are themselves satisfied if and only if they are satisfied for
\mathsf{s}=\operatorname{diag}(1,0,\dots,0)
. Thus
\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{s}^{\prime},h}=\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{s},h}
for any fixed choice of Hessenberg function
h
. Using Remark
3.5
, we conclude that any minimal semisimple Hessenberg scheme
\mathcal{Y}_{\mathsf{x},h}
is isomorphic to
\mathcal{Y}_{\mathsf{s},h}
.
For this reason, it is sufficient to restrict our attention to the
geometry
of
\mathcal{Y}_{\mathsf{s},h}
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\mathsf{s}=\mathrm{\operatorname{diag}}(1,0,\ldots,0)
and let
h:[n]\to[n]
be a Hessenberg function. The matrix Hessenberg scheme
\mathcal{Y}_{\mathsf{s},h}
is a reduced union of matrix Richardson schemes:

\mathcal{Y}_{\mathsf{s},h}=\bigcup_{i\in\mathcal{C}(h)}\left(\mathcal{X}_{u[i]}^{\mathrm{op}}\cap\mathcal{X}_{w_{0}v[h(i)]}\right).

Moreover, the ideal defining
\mathcal{Y}_{\mathsf{s},h}
satisfies

(4.1)

\widetilde{\mathcal{I}}_{\mathsf{s},h}=\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{s},h}=\bigcap_{i\in\mathcal{C}(h)}\left(\mathcal{L}_{i-1}+\mathcal{K}_{h(i)}\right).

Proof.

The statement about the structure of the scheme follows from the claim about the ideals by Lemma
2.2
. Recall the definition of
\mathcal{L}_{i}
and
\mathcal{K}_{j}
in Equation
2.3
.
By Remark
3.1
,
\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{s},h,i}=\mathcal{L}_{i}\cdot\mathcal{K}_{h(i)}
. Then

(4.2)

\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{s},h}=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{C}(h)}\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{s},h,i}=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{C}(h)}\mathcal{L}_{i^{*}}\cdot\mathcal{K}_{h(i)}=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{C}(h)}\mathcal{L}_{i^{*}}\cap\mathcal{K}_{h(i)}

where the first equality follows from Lemma
3.8
and the last equality follows from Lemma
2.4
. We aim to prove the second equality of (
4.1
) using induction on the size of
\mathcal{C}(h)
.

If
h
has one corner, then
h=(n,n,\dots,n)
. It follows that
1^{*}=h(1)=n
, and (
4.2
) becomes

\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{s},h}=\mathcal{L}_{1^{*}}\cap\mathcal{K}_{h(1)}=\mathcal{L}_{n}\cap\mathcal{K}_{n}=\langle 0\rangle

since
\mathcal{K}_{n}=\langle 0\rangle
. On the other hand,
\bigcap_{i\in\mathcal{C}(h)}\left(\mathcal{L}_{i-1}+\mathcal{K}_{h(i)}\right)=\mathcal{L}_{0}+\mathcal{K}_{n}=0
, since
\mathcal{K}_{n}=\mathcal{L}_{0}=\langle 0\rangle
. This proves the second equality of (
4.1
) in this case.

Now suppose the equality holds for any Hessenberg function whose corner set has size
k
, and suppose
|\mathcal{C}(h)|=k+1
. Observe that
\{i\in\mathcal{C}(h)\mid h(i)<n\}
is nonempty, since
\mathcal{C}(h)
has at least two elements. Let
m=\max\{i\in\mathcal{C}(h)\mid h(i)<n\}
. Then
m^{*}+1
is the maximum element of
\mathcal{C}(h)
, and
h(m^{*}+1)=n
.

Define
h^{\prime}:[n]\to[n]
to be the Hessenberg function such that
h^{\prime}(i)=h(i)
for all
i<m
and
h^{\prime}(i)=n
for all
i\geq m
. By construction,
\mathcal{C}(h^{\prime})=\mathcal{C}(h)\setminus\{m^{*}+1\}
,
m
is the largest corner of
h^{\prime}
, and
\mathcal{K}_{h(i)}=\mathcal{K}_{h^{\prime}(i)}
for all
i<m
.
Applying (
4.2
) and the inductive assumption to
h^{\prime}
and the fact that
\mathcal{K}_{n}=\langle 0\rangle
, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\mathcal{C}(h),\,i<m}\mathcal{L}_{i^{*}}\cap\mathcal{K}_{h(i)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{C}(h^{\prime})}\mathcal{L}_{i^{*}}\cap\mathcal{K}_{h^{\prime}(i)}=\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{s},h^{\prime}}=\bigcap_{i\in\mathcal{C}(h^{\prime})}\left(\mathcal{L}_{i-1}+\mathcal{K}_{h^{\prime}(i)}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\bigcap_{i\in\mathcal{C}(h),i<m}\left(\mathcal{L}_{i-1}+\mathcal{K}_{h(i)}\right)\right)\cap\mathcal{L}_{m-1}.

Let
\mathcal{J}=\bigcap\limits_{i\in\mathcal{C}(h),\,i<m}\left(\mathcal{L}_{i-1}+\mathcal{K}_{h(i)}\right)
for notational simplicity.
By the previous calculation,

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{s},h}=\left(\sum_{i\in\mathcal{C}(h),i<m}\mathcal{L}_{i^{*}}\cap\mathcal{K}_{h(i)}\right)+\mathcal{L}_{m^{*}}\cap\mathcal{K}_{h(m)}=\mathcal{J}\cap\mathcal{L}_{m-1}+\mathcal{L}_{m^{*}}\cap\mathcal{K}_{h(m)}

where the first equality follows from (
4.2
) and
\mathcal{K}_{h(m^{*}+1)}=\mathcal{K}_{n}=\langle 0\rangle
. Note that
h(i)\leq h(m)
for all
i<m
, so that
\mathcal{K}_{h(m)}\subseteq\mathcal{L}_{i-1}+\mathcal{K}_{h(i)}
and thus
\mathcal{K}_{h(m)}\subseteq\mathcal{J}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{s},h}

\displaystyle=\left(\mathcal{J}\cap\mathcal{L}_{m-1}\right)+(\mathcal{L}_{m^{*}}\cap\mathcal{K}_{h(m)})

\displaystyle=\left(\mathcal{L}_{m^{*}}\cap\mathcal{J}\cap\mathcal{L}_{m-1}\right)+(\mathcal{L}_{m^{*}}\cap\mathcal{K}_{h(m)})

since
\mathcal{L}_{m-1}\subseteq\mathcal{L}_{m^{*}},
as
m-1<m\leq m^{*}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}_{m^{*}}\cap\left[\left(\mathcal{J}\cap\mathcal{L}_{m-1}\right)+\mathcal{K}_{h(m)}\right]

since
\mathcal{J}\cap\mathcal{L}_{m-1}\subseteq\mathcal{L}_{m^{*}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}_{m^{*}}\cap\left[\left(\mathcal{J}\cap\mathcal{L}_{m-1}\right)+(\mathcal{J}\cap\mathcal{K}_{h(m)})\right]

since
\mathcal{K}_{h(m)}\subseteq\mathcal{J}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{J}\cap\left(\mathcal{L}_{m-1}+\mathcal{K}_{h(m)}\right)\cap\mathcal{L}_{m^{*}}

\displaystyle=\bigcap_{i\in\mathcal{C}(h)}\left(\mathcal{L}_{i-1}+\mathcal{K}_{h(i)}\right)

where the last equality follows from the definition of
\mathcal{J}
and the fact that
\mathcal{L}_{m^{*}}+\mathcal{K}_{h(m^{*}+1)}=\mathcal{L}_{m^{*}}+\mathcal{K}_{n}=\mathcal{L}_{m^{*}}
. This proves the second equality of (
4.1
).

Each ideal
\mathcal{L}_{i-1}+\mathcal{K}_{h(i)}
in (
4.1
) is prime by Lemma
2.2
, so
\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{s},h}
is an intersection of prime ideals and therefore radical. By Lemma
2.3
, we have
\mathcal{L}_{i-1}+\mathcal{K}_{h(i)}\subseteq\mathcal{L}_{j-1}+\mathcal{K}_{h(j)}
if and only if
i\leq j
and
h(i)\geq h(j)
. However, for distinct corners
i,j\in\mathcal{C}(h)
, the inequality
i\leq j
implies
h(i)<h(j)
. It follows that the primes in the set
\left\{\mathcal{L}_{i-1}+\mathcal{K}_{h(i)}\mid i\in\mathcal{C}(h)\right\}
are distinct and none is contained in any of the others. Consequently,
\left\{\mathcal{L}_{i-1}+\mathcal{K}_{h(i)}\mid i\in\mathcal{C}(h)\right\}
is the set of associated primes of
\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{s},h}
.

Recall that
d\in\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{z}]
denotes the determinant function of the generic
n\times n
matrix
Z=(z_{ij})_{1\leq i,j\leq n}
. If
d\in\mathcal{L}_{i-1}+\mathcal{K}_{h(i)}
for some
i
then
\operatorname{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{C})\cap\mathrm{Spec}(\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{z}]/(\mathcal{I}_{i-1}+\mathcal{K}_{h(i)}))=\emptyset
, contradicting Lemma
2.2
. This proves
d\notin\mathcal{L}_{i-1}+\mathcal{K}_{h(i)}
for all
i
, so

\widetilde{\mathcal{L}_{i-1}+\mathcal{K}_{h(i)}}=\mathcal{L}_{i-1}+\mathcal{K}_{h(i)}.

Since the localization map is injective, it preserves intersections of ideals, and the previous observation implies
\widetilde{\mathcal{I}}_{\mathsf{s},h}=\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{s},h}
, proving the first equality of (
4.1
).
∎

Corollary 4.2
.

For any Hessenberg function
h:[n]\to[n]
, the minimal semisimple Hessenberg variety

is isomorphic to a reduced union of Richardson varieties,

Y_{\mathsf{s},h}=\bigcup_{i\in\mathcal{C}(h)}\left(X_{u[i]}^{\mathrm{op}}\cap X_{w_{0}v[h(i)]}\right).

4.1
shows that the structure of the semisimple Hessenberg variety

Corollary 4.4
.

Let

be a Hessenberg function. The dimension of the minimal semisimple matrix Hessenberg scheme

\dim\mathcal{Y}_{\mathsf{s},h}=\frac{n(n+1)}{2}+\frac{(n-1)(n-2)}{2}+\max_{i\in\mathcal{C}(h)}\{h(i)-i\}.

\displaystyle\left(\frac{n(n-1)}{2}-(n-h(i))\right)-(i-1)

(4.3)

\displaystyle\dim\mathcal{X}_{u[i]}^{\mathrm{op}}\cap\mathcal{X}_{w_{0}v[h(i)]}

It is now straightforward to determine when the minimal semisimple Hessenberg scheme is equidimensional.

Corollary 4.5
.

Let
h:[n]\to[n]
be a Hessenberg function. The minimal semisimple matrix Hessenberg scheme
\mathcal{Y}_{\mathsf{s},h}
is equidimensional if and only if all of the corners of the associated diagram of
h
lie on the same subdiagonal. In other words,
\mathcal{Y}_{\mathsf{s},h}
is equidimensional if and only if
h(i)-i=h(j)-j
for all distinct corners
i
and
j
of
h
.

Proof.

Given distinct corners
i
and
j
of the Hessenberg function
h
, the formula (
4.3
) from the proof of Corollary
4.4
implies that the corresponding irreducible components of
\mathcal{Y}_{\mathsf{s},h}
have the same dimension if and only if
h(i)-i=h(j)-j
. Since the subdiagonal containing the corner box
(h(i),i)
is the set of all
(a,b)
such that
a-b=h(i)-i
, the result is proved.
∎

Notice that minimal semisimple Hessenberg schemes appearing in Example
4.3
are equidimensional, which is obvious from Corollary
4.5
by looking at the diagrams for the corresponding Hessenberg functions. Similarly, Example
3.10
considers a case that is not equidimensional, which is again obvious in light of Corollary
4.5
since the two corners appear in different subdiagonals of the diagram for
h
.

Next, we compute the cohomology class of the minimal semisimple Hessenberg variety
Y_{\mathsf{s},h}
. Recall Borel’s description of the integral cohomology ring
H^{*}(GL_{n}(\mathbb{C})/B,\mathbb{Z})
as the ring of coinvariants, that is,

H^{*}(GL_{n}/B,\mathbb{Z})\simeq\mathbb{Z}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]/I,

where
I\subset\mathbb{Z}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
is the ideal generated by the symmetric polynomials without a constant term. The Schubert polynomial
\mathfrak{S}_{w}\in\mathbb{Z}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
is a polynomial representative for the class
[X_{w}^{\mathrm{op}}]
of the opposite Schubert variety
X_{w}^{\mathrm{op}}
. It is well-known that the product of Schubert polynomials
\mathfrak{S}_{u}\cdot\mathfrak{S}_{v}
is a polynomial representative for the cohomology class
[X_{u}^{\mathrm{op}}\cap X_{w_{0}v}]
of the Richardson variety
X_{u}^{\mathrm{op}}\cap X_{w_{0}v}
. For a more detailed definition of Schubert polynomials see
[
Man01
]
.

With this notation in place, Corollary
4.2
allows us to compute a representative for
[Y_{\mathsf{s},h}]
as a sum of Schubert polynomials.

Corollary 4.6
.

Suppose
n\geq 3
, and let
h
be a Hessenberg function and
d_{h}=\max\{h(i)-i\mid i\in[n]\}
. The cohomology class of the Hessenberg variety
Y_{\mathsf{s},h}
is

[Y_{\mathsf{s},h}]=\begin{cases}\displaystyle{\ \sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\ 2\ \mathfrak{S}_{w[i+1,i]}}&\mbox{if $h=(1,2,\dots,n)$}\\
\\
\displaystyle{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in\mathcal{C}(h)\\
d_{h}=h(i)-i\end{subarray}}\mathfrak{S}_{w[i,h(i)]}}&\mbox{if $h\neq(1,2,\dots,n)$.}\end{cases}

where
w[i,j]
is the shortest permutation
w
in
S_{n}
with
w(i)=1
and
w(j)=n
,

Proof.

By Corollary
4.2
,

Y_{\mathsf{s},h}=\bigcup_{i\in\mathcal{C}(h)}\left(X^{\mathrm{op}}_{u[i]}\cap X_{w_{0}v[h(i)]}\right).

The largest dimensional terms occur when
h(i)-i=d_{h}
. The cohomology class is therefore given by

\displaystyle[Y_{\mathsf{s},h}]

\displaystyle=\displaystyle{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in\mathcal{C}(h)\\
d_{h}=h(i)-i\end{subarray}}\mathfrak{S}_{u[i]}\cdot\mathfrak{S}_{v[h(i)]}}.

Consider first the case that
h\neq(1,2,\dots,n)
. Then
d_{h}>0
and thus
i<h(i)
for each
i
in the sum. Since
i<h(i)
,
u[i]
and
v[h(i)]
are in commuting subgroups of the permutation group, implying
\mathfrak{S}_{u[i]}\mathfrak{S}_{v[h(i)]}=\mathfrak{S}_{u[i]v[h(i)]}=\mathfrak{S}_{w[i,h(i)]}.
The second equality follows.

When
h=(1,2,\dots,n)
, observe that
\mathcal{C}(h)=[n]
and
d_{h}=0
is achieved for all
i\in[n]
. Since
u[1]=v[n]=e
, the expression simplifies to

\displaystyle[Y_{\mathsf{s},h}]

\displaystyle=\displaystyle{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathfrak{S}_{u[i]}\cdot\mathfrak{S}_{v[i]}}=\mathfrak{S}_{v[1]}+\left(\displaystyle{\sum_{i=2}^{n-1}\mathfrak{S}_{u[i]}\cdot\mathfrak{S}_{v[i]}}\right)+\mathfrak{S}_{u[n]}.

The terms in the middle sum can be evaluated using the fact that
u[i]=s_{1}s_{2}\cdots s_{i-1}
(here
s_{i}
denotes the simple transposition swaping
i
and
i+1
) and the Pieri rule, which dictates that

\mathfrak{S}_{u[i]}\cdot\mathfrak{S}_{v[i]}=\mathfrak{S}_{w[i,i-1]}+\mathfrak{S}_{w[i+1,i]},\quad\mbox{for }1<i<n.

Since
v[1]=w[2,1]
and
u[n]=w[n,n-1]
, we obtain

\displaystyle[Y_{\mathsf{s},h}]

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{S}_{w[2,1]}+\left(\displaystyle{\sum_{i=2}^{n-1}\left(\mathfrak{S}_{w[i,i-1]}+\mathfrak{S}_{w[i+1,i]}\right)}\right)+\mathfrak{S}_{w[n,n-1]}=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\ 2\ \mathfrak{S}_{w[i+1,i]}.

This concludes the proof.
∎

We show in
[
GP
]
that
\mathcal{Y}_{\mathsf{s},h}
admits a flat degeneration to the minimal nilpotent matrix Hessenberg scheme
\mathcal{Y}_{\mathsf{n},h}
. Since the underlying variety of
\mathcal{Y}_{\mathsf{n},h}
is a union of matrix Schubert varieties, the factor of 2 occurring when
h=(1,2,\dots,n)
implies that
\mathcal{Y}_{\mathsf{n},h}
is not a reduced scheme.
