Approximating Maximally Localized Wannier Functions with Position Scaling-Eigenfunctions Preprint: APS/123-QED
Yuji Hamai, Katsunori Wakabayashi

Abstract
Position scaling-eigenfunctions are generated by transforming compactly supported orthonormal scaling functions and utilized for faster alternatives to maximally localized Wannier functions (MLWFs). The position scaling-eigenfunctions are first applied to numerical procedures solving Schrödinger and Maxwell’s equations, and the solutions well agree with preceding results. Subsequently, by projecting the position scaling-eigenfunctions onto the space spanned by the Bloch functions, approximated MLWFs are obtained. They show good agreements with preceding results using MLWFs. In addition, analytical explanations of the agreements and an estimate of the error associated with the approximation are provided.

II
Composing Position Scaling-Eigenfunction and Computing Matrix Elements of Operators

In this section, position eigenfunctions in a discrete system are generated from
known SFs by taking advantage of the two-scale relation, and their properties are
studied. Subsequently, the matrix elements of the kinetic energy operator are
calculated with the generated position eigenfunctions, since the matrix is used in
Sec.
V
to solve Schrödinger and Maxwell’s equations.

II.1
Overview of Scaling Function

The lower half of
Fig.
1
shows
N
compactly supported SFs
deployed in one unit cell to compose BFs and WFs. The SFs in the series have the
identical shape, and they are translationally orthonormal, i.e.,

\begin{array}[]{c}\int\phi(x-n_{1})\phi(x-n_{2})dx=\delta_{n_{1},n_{2}}\quad(n_{1},n_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}).\\
\end{array}

(1)

The particular SFs shown in the Fig.
1
are
Daubechies-4s (Db4s). Each of them has nonzero value only inside the continuous
region whose width is
7
. Since the continuous region, the support, limits the
range of calculations such as integration, compactly supported SFs are
particularly useful basis functions when composing and decomposing a function.

While many of the orthonormal sets used in solid state physics are
eigenfunctions of Hermitian operators, an SF does not have its origin in
physics. They are the solutions of the following recursive algebraic equation
called two-scale relation
Daubechies 1992c
:

\begin{split}\phi(x)=\sqrt{2}\sum_{l=0}^{S}h_{l}\phi(2x-l),\\
\{\ h_{l}:0\leq l\leq S,l\in\mathbb{Z},h_{l}\in\mathbb{R}\},\end{split}

(2)

with

\begin{split}\int\phi(x-n)dx=1\ (n\in\mathbb{Z}).\\
\end{split}

(3)

Once the series
\{h_{l}\}
is given, Eq. (
2
), the two-scale
equation, is recursively solved point-by-point (see Ref.
Daubechies 1992b
for
the specific values of
\{h_{l}\}
and more sophisticated and detailed
treatment of the two-scale relation). Thus, the series
\{h_{l}\}
determines all
characteristics of the corresponding SF.

Figure
2
shows examples of orthonormal compactly
supported scaling functions
Daubechies 1992b
, Daubechies-2 to -5 (Db2 to Db5),
Symlet-4(Sy4) and Coiflet-1(Cf1), which are created from different sets of
\{h_{l}\}
.

. Each compactly supported scaling function has its own support, outside
which the value of the scaling function is completely zero. In general, the
longer the support is, the smoother the SF becomes.

II.2
Composing Position Eigenfunction

Position eigenvectors in a discrete system not only have to be translationally
orthonormal, but also they are desired to be strongly localized and at least
twice differentiable. If the space is spanned by a limited number of broad and
smooth basis vectors, such as plane waves, strongly localized orbits/position
eigenvectors with an exponential decay may not be composed.

The compactly supported SFs, such as Db4s, readily posse some of the rare
characters
mentioned above. In fact, a compactly supported SF is a good first
approximation of the constructed position eigenvector as shown later in Table

1
in Sec.
II.2.3
. Furthermore, most of
them are found to be
practically twice differentiable
in a sense shown
in Appendix
A.3
and as demonstrated by the numerical results.
Thus, the compactly supported SFs are chosen to be the building blocks of
position eigenfunctions.

II.2.1
Strategy

To switch to
the Dirac notation, we would like to have the state space representation of an
SF such as Db2-Db5, Sy4 and Cf1. Let
|n\rangle
be the state
vector pertaining to an SF,
\phi(x-n)
. Then
|n\rangle
is formally
composed by the position eigenvector
|x\rangle
:

|n\rangle=\int dx\phi(x-n)|x\rangle\quad(\;\mathrm{or}\;\;\phi(x-n)=\langle x|n\rangle\;),

(4)

where
n
corresponds to the position of a sub-cell seen in Fig. (
1
).
Therefore, the scaling-kets span the following space:

\mathcal{H}_{S}={\mathrm{Span}}\{|n\rangle:n\in\mathbb{Z}\}.

(5)

By defining the position operator
\tilde{x}
in
\mathcal{H}_{S}
:

\begin{array}[]{c}\tilde{x}=\hat{P}_{S}\hat{x}\hat{P}_{S},\\
\hat{P}_{S}=\sum_{n}|n\rangle\langle n|,\end{array}

(6)

the eigenvectors of the position operator,
\{|x_{p}\rangle\}
, are given as solutions of the following
eigenvalue equation:

\tilde{x}|x_{p}\rangle=x_{p}|x_{p}\rangle,\ (|x_{p}\rangle\in\mathcal{H}_{S},p\in\mathbb{Z}).

(7)

|x_{p}\rangle
is expanded by the compactly supported scaling kets:

\begin{split}|x_{p}\rangle&=\sum_{n}|n\rangle\langle n|x_{p}\rangle\\
&=\sum_{n}\xi_{n,p}|n\rangle,\end{split}

(8)

where

\begin{split}\xi_{n,p}=\langle n|x_{p}\rangle.\end{split}

(9)

Thus, the unknown to solve is now shifted from the position eigenvectors to the
matrix
\{\xi_{n,p}\}
.
By multiplying
\langle m|
from the left to the both sides of Eq. (
7
):

\begin{split}\langle m|\tilde{x}|x_{p}\rangle&=\sum_{n}X_{m,n}\xi_{n,p},\\
\langle m|x_{p}|x_{p}\rangle&=x_{p}\xi_{m,p},\\
\end{split}

(10)

where

X_{m,n}=\langle m|\tilde{x}|n\rangle.

(11)

And hence the equation for
\xi_{m,n}
is obtained:

\sum_{n}X_{m,n}\xi_{n,p}=\xi_{m,p}x_{p}.

(12)

II.2.2
Position Operators and Matrix Elements

\{X_{m,n}\}
can be obtained from the following integral:

\begin{split}X_{m,n}=\int\phi(x-m)x\phi(x-n)dx.\\
\end{split}

(13)

Owing to the two-scale relation, Eq. (
2
), however,
the above is recursively expressed by
\{X_{m,n}\}
without actual integration:

\begin{split}\int&\phi(x-m)x\phi(x-n)dx\\
&=\sum_{l,k}2\int h_{l}h_{k}\phi(2x-2m-l)x\phi(2x-2n-k)dx\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{l,k}h_{l}h_{k}X_{2m+l,2n+k}.\end{split}

(14)

By combining Eqs. (
13
) and (
14
), we have
solvable algebraic equations for
\{X_{m,n}\}
with given coefficients

\{h_{l}\}
(see Appendix
A.1
for the general strategy to calculate matrix elements).
Since the support is compact, the integration becomes
0
for

|m-n|\geq S
. This makes matrix
\{X_{m,n}\}
sparse
and easy to handle.
From the translational symmetry, the diagonal elements are
simplified to be:

\begin{split}X_{m,m}&=\int\phi(x-m)x\phi(x-m)dx\\
&=\int\phi(y)(y+m)\phi(y)dy\quad(y=x-m)\\
&=X_{0,0}+m.\end{split}

(15)

By defining the following series
\{X_{r}\}
:

X_{r}=\begin{cases}X_{m+r,m}&(\mathrm{for\ }r\neq 0)\\
X_{0,0}&(\mathrm{for\ }r=0),\end{cases}

(16)

X_{m,n}
is described by
X_{r}
as follows:

X_{m,n}=X_{r}+m\delta_{m,n}\quad(r=m-n).

(17)

By substituting this into Eqs. (
13
) and (
14
), the following equations
for
\{X_{r}\}
are obtained:

\begin{array}[]{l}\bm{X}=\mathbf{H}\bm{X}+\bm{h},\\
\left[\mathbf{H}\right]_{r,q}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k}h_{k}h_{k+2r-q},\\
\left[\bm{X}\right]_{q}=X_{q},\\
\left[\bm{h}\right]_{r}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k}kh_{k-2r}h_{k}.\end{array}

(18)

Since the above equations are linear algebraic and
\{h_{l}\}
is given, it does
not take any actual integration to obtain the matrix elements of the position
operator,
\{X_{r}\}
.

II.2.4
Shifting Eigenfunction and Eigenvalue

The
0
-th eigenvector has
X_{0}

A.2

It is however more convenient to have a direct association of the eigenvector number and the eigenvalue.
By shifting the eigenfunctions by
X_{0}

\xi_{p}(x)\rightarrow\xi_{p}(x+X_{0}),

(23)

the transformation from an SF to the position eigenfunction changes as follows:

\xi_{p}(x)=\sum_{n}\xi_{n-p}\phi((x+X_{0})-n).

(24)

\tilde{x}|x_{p}\rangle=p|x_{p}\rangle.

(25)

II.2.5
Position Eigenvector as Scaling Function

As detailed in Appendix
B.2.2
, the translational invariance of

\{\xi_{n-m}\}
leads to the conclusion that the position eigenvectors are
also categorized to be SFs. Namely, they themselves satisfy the following
two-scale relation:

\xi(x)=\sqrt{2}\sum_{l}\eta_{l}\xi(2x-l),\\
\quad\eta_{l}=\sum_{p,q,l}\xi_{p}\xi_{q}h_{q+l-2p}.

(26)

The position eigenvectors therefore have their own wavelets, which are
shown in Appendix
B.3
. They may be utilized
to solve or analyze quantum mechanical systems where
multi-resolution capability are important.

II.4
Adapting Eigenvectors to Crystal System

To apply the position eigenfunctions to 1D electronic and photonic crystals with continuous real coordinates, the following assumptions are made: (1) The periodic boundary condition is imposed at the both ends of the crystal.
(2) The unit cell length is set at 1. (3) In electronic systems, the following
units are used:
m=1,\;\hbar=1
. (4) In optical systems, the speed of light

c
is set at 1. (5) The unit cells are divided in
N
and
\Delta x=1/N
.
These assumptions result in the following adjustment to the functions,
operators and variables as described in Appendix
B.4
:

\begin{array}[]{c}p\rightarrow x_{p}=l+m\Delta x,\\
Q_{r}\rightarrow N^{2}Q_{r}\quad(\rho=4),\\
\end{array}

(29)

with

\begin{array}[]{c}\Delta x=1/N,\\
l=p\!\!\!\!\mod N,\\
m=p-lN,\\
\end{array}

(30)

and

\begin{split}\langle x|x_{p}\rangle&=\sqrt{N}\xi(N(x-x_{p})),\\
\end{split}

(31)

\begin{split}\int dx\langle x|x_{p}\rangle&=\sqrt{N}\int dx\xi(N(x-x_{p}))\\
&=\sqrt{N}\int\xi(y-p)\frac{dy}{N}\\
&=1/\sqrt{N}.\\
\end{split}

(32)

II.5
Single-Band Bloch Functions

A BF pertaining to the
n
-th band with its wave number
k
has the next form:

\begin{split}|\psi_{k}^{n}\rangle&=\frac{1}{\sqrt{L}}e^{ik\tilde{x}}|u_{k}^{n}\rangle.\\
\end{split}

(33)

The cell-periodic part
|u_{k}^{n}\rangle
is expanded by
\{|x_{p}\rangle\}
:

\begin{split}|u_{k}^{n}\rangle&=\left\{\sum_{p=0}^{NL-1}|x_{p}\rangle\langle x_{p}|\right\}|u_{k}^{n}\rangle\\
&=\sum_{p=0}^{NL-1}c_{p,k}^{n}|x_{p}\rangle,\\
\end{split}

(34)

where
\{c^{n}_{p,k}\}
is the
N
-periodic (cell-periodic) coefficients of expansion:

c^{n}_{p,k}=c_{p+N,k}^{n}=\langle x_{p}|u_{k}^{n}\rangle,

(35)

and hence by utilizing Eq. (
30
):

\begin{split}|u_{k}^{n}\rangle&=\sum_{l=0}^{L-1}\sum_{m=0}^{N-1}c_{m,k}^{n}|x_{Nl+m}\rangle\\
&=\sum_{l=0}^{L-1}\sum_{m=0}^{N-1}c_{m,k}^{n}|x_{p}\rangle.\\
\end{split}

(36)

The BF is thus decomposed with
\{|x_{p}\rangle\}
:

\begin{split}|\psi^{n}_{k}\rangle&=\frac{1}{\sqrt{L}}\sum_{l=0}^{L-1}\sum_{m=0}^{N-1}e^{ik\tilde{x}}c_{m,k}^{n}|x_{p}\rangle\\
&=\frac{1}{\sqrt{L}}\sum_{l=0}^{L-1}\sum_{m=0}^{N-1}e^{ikl}e^{ikm\Delta x}c_{m,k}^{n}|x_{p}\rangle,\end{split}

(37)

where Eq. (
166
) in Appendix
B.5.1
is applied.

II.6
State Vectors and Spatial Resolution

In many cases, to solve the Schrödinger equation, it takes discretization of
the entire state space
\mathcal{H}
by a set of a finite number of basis
vectors,
\{|\eta_{m}\rangle\}
, which may be plain waves, Gaussian
functions, atomic orbits and so on. The position eigenvectors of the discretized
space:

\mathcal{H}_{\eta}=\mathrm{Span}\{|\eta_{m}\rangle\>,0\leq m<N\},

(38)

are obtained by solving the following equation:

\begin{split}\tilde{x}|x^{\eta}_{p}\rangle=x^{\eta}_{p}|x^{\eta}_{p}\rangle,\ \left(|x^{\eta}_{p}\rangle=\sum_{m}u^{p}_{m}|\eta_{m}\rangle\right).\end{split}

(39)

Then
\xi^{\eta}_{p}(x)=\sqrt{N}\langle x|x^{\eta}_{p}\rangle
is the
discretized version of
\delta
-function in
\mathcal{H}_{\eta}
, and it provides
the information on the spatial resolution constrained by
\{|\eta_{m}\rangle\}
.

In this paper, the basis vectors themselves are position eigenvectors and the spatial
resolution of the solutions such as BFs and WFs are clear from the time of
formulation, as described in Appendix
B.5
.

III
MLWF and Position Scaling-Eigenvector in State Space

The section focuses on the properties of the position eigenvectors
projected onto a composite energy band space,

\mathcal{H}_{C}
, consisting of
n_{c}
energy bands defined as follows:

\begin{split}&\mathcal{H}_{C}=\sum_{n}\mathcal{H}_{B_{n}},\\
&\mathcal{H}_{B_{n}}=\mathrm{Span}\{|\psi_{lk_{0}}^{n}\rangle\>\ 0\leq l<L,k_{0}=2\pi/L\},\end{split}

(40)

where
L
is the number of the unit cells in the system and energy bands
included in
\mathcal{H}_{C}
are arbitrary and
n
does not have to begin from

0
and the band indexes do not have to be successive numbers.

Subsequently, the relationship between the projected position eigenvectors and MLWFs in
\mathcal{H}_{C}
leading to approximations of MLWFs by the projected eigenvectors is discussed.

III.1
MLWF

In the section, important properties of MLWFs utilized in the succeeding
sections are reviewed in preparation for discussing approximation utilizing the
position scaling-eigenvectors,

III.1.1
Eigenvalue Equations for MLWFs

MLWFs satisfy the following equation
Marzari and Vanderbilt 1997
;
Kivelson 1982
:

\begin{split}\tilde{x}^{C}|W\rangle&=x|W\rangle,\\
\end{split}

(41)

where
\tilde{x}^{C}
is the position operator in
\mathcal{H}_{C}
:

\begin{split}\tilde{x}^{C}&=\hat{P}_{C}\hat{x}\hat{P}_{C},\\
\hat{P}_{C}&=\sum_{k,n}|\psi_{k}^{n}\rangle\langle\psi_{k}^{n}|.\end{split}

(42)

Because of the number of basis vectors and the translational invariance of the
system, the solution has to have the following form (see
Appendix
C.1
):

\begin{split}\tilde{x}^{C}|W^{s}_{l}\rangle&=(x_{0s}+l)|W^{s}_{l}\rangle,\\
\end{split}

(43)

with

\tilde{x}^{C}|W^{s}_{0}\rangle=x_{0s}|W^{s}_{0}\rangle,

(44)

where
s
and
l
denote the series and the cell number, respectively.
The number of series is
n_{c}
, for the number of energy bands involved is
n_{c}
.

The MLWF as a function of position is given by the following projection
(see Appendix
C.2
):

\begin{split}\langle x_{p}|W^{s}_{l}\rangle&=N^{-1/2}W^{s}_{0}(x_{p}-l)\\
&=N^{-1/2}W^{s}_{l}(x_{p}).\end{split}

(45)

III.1.2
Orthonormality of MLWFs

Because MLWFs have to be orthonormal:

\begin{split}\langle W^{s_{1}}_{l_{1}}|W^{s_{2}}_{l_{2}}\rangle=\delta_{s_{1},s_{2}}\delta_{l_{1},l_{2}}.\\
\end{split}

(46)

The numbers of MLWFs and BFs are the same, and they span the same space,
\mathcal{H}_{C}
and hence:

\hat{P}_{C}=\sum_{l,s}|W_{l}^{s}\rangle\langle W_{l}^{s}|=\sum_{k,n}|\psi_{k}^{n}\rangle\langle\psi_{k}^{n}|.

(47)

Since MLWFs are expanded as described in Appendix
C.1
:

|W_{l}^{s}\rangle=\sum_{k}e^{-ikl}w_{k}^{s,n}|\psi_{k}^{n}\rangle,

(48)

the following holds:

\langle W_{l_{1}}^{s_{1}}|W_{l_{2}}^{s_{2}}\rangle=\sum_{k}e^{-ik(l_{2}-l_{1})}\sum_{n}\left(\overline{w}_{k}^{s_{1},n}w_{k}^{s_{2},n}\right).

(49)

Throughout the paper
overline
on a variable, such as
\overline{\square}
, denotes the complex conjugate of the variable underneath.

By the requirement given by Eq. (
46
) for any
l_{1},l_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}
, we have:

\sum_{n}\overline{w}_{k}^{s_{1},n}w_{k}^{s_{2},n}=\frac{1}{L}\delta_{s_{1},s_{2}}.

(50)

III.2
Position Eigenvectors Projected onto Composite Band Space

The section focuses on the properties of the position eigenvectors when they are projected
onto composite bands.

III.2.1
Definition

MLWFs are the eigenvectors of the operator that is the position operator projected onto
\mathcal{H}_{C}
,
as described in Eq. (
42
). In contrast to this, the projected position
eigenvectors (PPE)
are defined as the position eigenvectors,
|x_{p}\rangle\in\mathcal{H}_{X}
, projected onto
\mathcal{H}_{C}
:

\begin{split}|x^{C}_{p}\rangle&=\hat{P}_{C}|x_{p}\rangle\\
&=\sum_{k,n}\!|\psi^{n}_{k}\rangle\langle\psi^{n}_{k}|x_{p}\rangle\\
&=\frac{1}{\sqrt{L}}\sum_{k,n}e^{-ikl}e^{-ikm\Delta x}\bar{c}^{n}_{m,k}|\psi^{n}_{k}\rangle,\end{split}

(51)

where the superscript
C
denotes that the vector is not a position eigenvector in
\mathcal{H}_{X}
,
but in
\mathcal{H}_{C}
.

III.2.2
Properties of PPE

Since any vector in
\mathcal{H}_{C}
is expanded by
|\psi^{n}_{k}\rangle\in\mathcal{H}_{C}
:

|f\rangle=\sum_{n,k}f^{n}_{k}|\psi^{n}_{k}\rangle.

(52)

The projection of
|f\rangle
onto a PPE becomes:

\begin{split}\langle x^{C}_{p}|f\rangle&=\left\{\langle x_{p}|\sum_{n_{1},k_{1}}|\psi^{n_{1}}_{k_{1}}\rangle\langle\psi^{n_{1}}_{k_{1}}|\right\}\sum_{n,k}f^{n}_{k}|\psi^{n}_{k}\rangle\\
&=\sum_{n,k}\langle x_{p}|\psi^{n}_{k}\rangle f^{n}_{k}\\
&=\langle x_{p}|f\rangle,\end{split}

(53)

and hence PPEs behave as if they were the position eigenvectors in

\mathcal{H}_{C}
.

It is also noted that the inner product is a measure of
closeness of the two vectors and by definition:

\cos\theta_{x_{p},f}=\langle x^{C}_{p}|f\rangle/\sqrt{\langle x^{C}_{p}|x^{C}_{p}\rangle\langle f|f\rangle}.

(54)

From Eq. (
167
) in Appendix
B.5.2
,

\langle x_{p}|f\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}(f(x_{p})+O(\sigma_{0}^{2}/N^{2})),

(55)

the value of a function at
x=x_{p}
is a measure of the closeness between the vector and
the corresponding PPE.

III.2.3
Approximation of PPE

By utilizing Eq. (
55
),

\langle\psi^{n}_{k}|x_{p}\rangle\simeq\frac{\overline{\psi}^{n}_{k}(x_{p})}{\sqrt{N}}.

(56)

PPE defined by Eq. (
51
) is therefore approximated with the values of
the BFs pointwise:

|\tilde{x}^{C}_{p}\rangle=\sum_{k,n}\frac{\overline{\psi}^{n}_{k}(x_{p})}{\sqrt{N}}|\psi_{k}^{n}\rangle,

(57)

which is calculated from the values of the BFs rather than
\{c^{n}_{pk}\}
.
From Eq. (
167
), the error is given as follows:

\langle x|x^{C}_{p}\rangle=\langle x_{p}|\tilde{x}^{C}_{p}\rangle+O(\sigma_{0}^{2}/N^{2}).

(58)

To distinguish
|\tilde{x}^{C}_{p}\rangle
from PPE, it is termed pointwise projected position eigenvector (PWE).

III.3
Approximation of MLWF with PPE

The section describes the following properties of PPEs and PWEs in relation to
MLWFs to examine the possibility of PPEs and PWEs being used as alternatives or
supplements to MLWFs: (1) Closeness in terms of shape and position. (2)
Closeness in terms of their characters in the relevant space, i.e., intra- and
inter-series orthonormality.

The results of the analysis justify the intuitive closeness of PPEs and PWEs to
MLWFs. The similarities and differences are summarized in Appendix

C.5
. Furthermore, it is shown that a relatively light calculation,
searching the maximum point of a function,
F(m)=\sum_{k}\left|c_{m,k}\right|^{2}
, gives the maximum point of the MLWF.

The specific estimate of error associated with the approximation of an MLWF by
the PPE will be presented later in Sec.
IV.2.2
, albeit, for single
band cases.

III.3.1
Maximum Point of PPE and MLWF

When
|x_{p}^{C}\rangle
is closest to
|W^{s}_{l}\rangle

among:

\{|x^{C}_{q}\rangle:0\leq q<NL\},

(59)

x_{p}
is the maximum point of
\left|W_{l}(x)\right|
from Eqs. (
45
) and (
54
).
Combining the above and the following equation
obtained in Appendix
C.4.1
:

\begin{split}\xi_{p}^{C}(x)=\langle x|x_{p}^{C}\rangle&\simeq N^{-1/2}W^{s}_{l}(x)\overline{W}^{s}_{l}(x_{p}),\end{split}

(60)

we notice the position
x_{p}
is also the maximum point of
\xi^{C}_{p}(x)
. Thus,
finding the maximum point of an MLWF is achieved by searching the maximum point
of the tallest PPE. It should be however noted, in cases PPEs do not have a
clear maximum, as will be shown later in Sec.
V.3

(e.g.,Fig.
11
(d)), the actual
\sigma^{2}_{x}
(spread) has to be
calculated to identify the maximally localized PPE.

To signify the difference between PPE and PWE, and to show the procedure of
finding the approximate position of an MLWF to a
programmable
degree,
the section focuses on the single band case. By restricting the band index to

n
, a PPE projected onto itself is calculated as follows:

\begin{split}\xi_{p}^{n}(x_{p})&=\langle x^{n}_{p}|x_{p}^{n}\rangle\\
&=\sum_{k}\langle x_{p}|\psi^{n}_{k}\rangle\langle\psi^{n}_{k}|x_{p}\rangle\\
&=\sum_{k,p_{1},p_{2}}\langle x_{p}|\left\{\frac{1}{\sqrt{L}}e^{ikx_{p_{1}}}c^{n}_{p_{1},k}|x_{p_{1}}\rangle\right\}\\
&\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\left\{\frac{1}{\sqrt{L}}\langle x_{p_{2}}|e^{-ikx_{p_{2}}}\overline{c}^{n}_{p_{2},k}\right\}|x_{p}\rangle\\
&=\sum_{k}\left|c^{n}_{p,k}\right|^{2},\end{split}

(61)

with

\begin{split}m=p\ \mathrm{mod}\ N,x_{p}=p\Delta x.\end{split}

(62)

On the other hand, a PWE projected onto itself is calculated as follows:

\begin{split}&\ \langle\tilde{x}_{p}^{n}|\tilde{x}_{p}^{n}\rangle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k_{1},k}\left\{\langle\psi_{k_{1}}^{n}|\psi^{n}_{k_{1}}(x_{p})\right\}\left\{\overline{\psi}_{k}^{n}(x_{p})|\psi^{n}_{k}\rangle\right\}\\
&\quad\quad\quad=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k}\psi_{k}^{n}(x_{p})\overline{\psi}_{k}^{n}(x_{p})\\
&\ \quad\quad\left(=\sum_{k}\left|c_{p,k}^{n}\right|^{2}+O\left(\sigma^{2}/N^{2}\right)\right),\end{split}

(63)

where Eq. (
167
) in Appendix
B.5.1
is
utilized.

From the above, the following points are easily understood: (1) Finding the
approximate position of the MLWF in a unit cell is reduced to searching the
maximum point of
F(m)=\sum_{k}\left|c_{m,k}\right|^{2}
. This is
expected to be a relatively light work on computer. (2) PWEs are potentially
useful, for it can be computed from known values of the BFs calculated in some
other ways.

However, it has to be noted: (a) A PPE and the corresponding PWE are not
identical. (b) The spatial resolution is
limited by the number of the independent bases vectors used in the calculation of BFs.
(c) The distinctiveness of the peak of the PWE depends on the nature of the
basis as is mentioned in the opening of Sec.
II.2
.

III.3.2
Maximum Values of PPE and MLWF

By normalizing the PPE closest to an MLWF, we have the following approximation:

|W_{l}^{s}\rangle\simeq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\left|\langle x^{C}_{p}|x^{C}_{p}\rangle\right|}}|x^{C}_{p}\rangle.

(64)

By combining the above and Eq. (
45
), we have:

|{W}^{s}_{l}(x_{p})|=\sqrt{N\left|\langle x^{C}_{p}|x^{C}_{p}\rangle\right|}.

(65)

Thus, the maximum value of an MLWF is obtained from that of the tallest PPE.

III.3.3
Intra-Series Orthonormalization of PPEs

Since the PPEs defined and described so far are not translationally orthonormal within a series, i.e.:

\langle x^{C}|(x+l)^{C}\rangle=N^{-1}\sum_{k,n}\psi^{n}_{k}(x)\overline{\psi}^{n}_{k}(x+l).

(66)

To use PPEs as an alternative to MLWFs, they have to be orthonormalized (Appendix
C.3
),
and hence PPEs is redefined as follows:

\begin{split}|x^{\perp C}_{p}\rangle&=\sum_{k,n}\frac{1}{\Phi_{k}}\left\{|\psi^{n}_{k}\rangle\langle\psi^{n}_{k}|x_{p}\rangle\right\},\\
\Phi_{k}&=\sqrt{\sum_{n}|\langle\psi^{n}_{k}|x_{p}\rangle|^{2}}.\end{split}

(67)

The above definition differs from that of Eq. (
51
), for
\Phi_{k}
is
at the denominator.
However, when
|x^{C}_{p}\rangle
is closest to an MLWF,
from Eq. (
198
) in Appendix
C.4.2
, the following holds:

\begin{split}\Phi_{k}\simeq\sqrt{\left|\langle x^{C}_{p}|x^{C}_{p}\rangle\right|}.\end{split}

(68)

And hence:

\begin{split}|x^{\perp C}_{p}\rangle&\simeq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\left|\langle x^{C}_{p}|x^{C}_{p}\rangle\right|}}|x^{C}_{p}\rangle.\end{split}

(69)

Therefore, the orthonormalized PPEs are equal to the approximated MLWFS seen in Eq. (
64
).

Similarly, orthonormalized PWEs are defined as follows:

\begin{split}|\tilde{x}^{\perp C}_{p}\rangle&=\sum_{k,n}\frac{\psi^{n}_{k}(x_{p})}{\tilde{\Phi}_{k}}|\psi^{n}_{k}\rangle,\\
\tilde{\Phi}_{k}&=\sqrt{\sum_{n}|\psi^{n}_{k}(x_{p})|^{2}}.\end{split}

(70)

III.3.4
Inter-Series Orthonormalization of PPEs

When there are two or more series of MLWFs, the same number of PPE series have to exist, and
one series has to be orthogonal to others. Let
|x^{C}_{p_{1}}\rangle
be the closest PPE to an MLWF in
series
s_{1}
,
and

x_{p_{0}}
be the zero of
\xi^{C}_{p_{1}}(x)=N^{-1/2}\langle x|x^{C}_{p_{1}}\rangle

then

\begin{split}|\xi_{p_{1}}(x_{p_{0}})|&=N^{-1/2}|\langle x^{C}_{p_{0}}|x^{C}_{p_{1}}\rangle|\\
&=N^{-1/2}|\langle x^{C}_{p_{1}}|x^{C}_{p_{0}}\rangle|=|\xi_{p_{0}}(x_{p_{1}})|=0.\end{split}

(71)

We find
|x^{C}_{p_{1}}\rangle
and
|x^{C}_{p_{0}}\rangle
are
orthogonal. Thus, an approximation of another series is obtained
(see Fig.
14
for example). One unresolved
problem is it does not guarantee that
|x^{C}_{p_{0}}\rangle
is the most
localized PPE in the vicinity of
x_{p_{0}}
. Likewise, if
x_{p_{0}}
is chosen so
that
|x^{C}_{p_{0}}\rangle
is most localized in the vicinity, the
inter-series orthogonality is not guaranteed. Nonetheless, the numerical results
shown in Sec.
VI
well agree with MLWFs at least on the figures.

III.4
Density Matrix and Initial-Guess Orbit

Position representation of
\hat{P}_{C}
, the density matrix
Vitale et al. 2020
,

\begin{split}&\rho_{x_{2}}(x_{1})=\langle x_{1}|\hat{P}_{C}|x_{2}\rangle\\
&\left(|x_{n}\rangle\in\mathcal{H},\ \langle x|x_{n}\rangle=\delta(x-x_{n})\ ,n=1,\ 2\right),\end{split}

(72)

is proved to fall off exponentially as a function of

x_{1}

Cloizeaux 1963
;
Cloizeaux 1964a
;
Cloizeaux 1964b
;
Prodan and Kohn 2005
;
Benzi et al. 2013
, and it is used as an initial-guess of the MV method either as it is

Pizzi et al. 2020
;
Vitale et al. 2020
;
Damle et al. 2015
;
Damle et al. 2016
or by replacing the

|x_{2}\rangle
with a localized wave function such as an atomic orbit
expected to be close to the MLWF
Freimuth et al. 2008
;
Cancès et al. 2017
;
Stubbs et al. 2021
;
Takeda et al. 2006
;
Vitale et al. 2020
;
Mustafa et al. 2015
;
Pizzi et al. 2020
.
The position representation of PPE defined by Eq. (
51
) and

\langle x^{C}_{p}|f\rangle
in Eq. (
55
) resemble the
density matrix. In the continuous limit, they are identical, however
|x_{p}\rangle
and
|f\rangle
in the paper are vectors in a discretized space

\mathcal{H}_{X}
.

When the state space is spanned by a finite number of orthonormal vectors

\{|\eta_{m}\rangle\}
, as in the case of numerical simulations or
mathematical modeling, the state space is discretized as discussed in
Sec.
II.6
:

\begin{split}\mathcal{H}&\rightarrow\mathcal{H}_{\eta},\\
\int dx|x\rangle\langle x|&\rightarrow\sum_{m}|\eta_{m}\rangle\langle\eta_{m}|\\
&=\sum_{p}|x^{\eta}_{p}\rangle\langle x^{\eta}_{p}|,\\
\end{split}

(73)

where
\{|x^{\eta}_{p}\rangle\}
is the set of position eigenvectors in
\mathcal{H}_{\eta}

and spans the same space:

\mathrm{Span}\{|x^{\eta}_{p}\rangle\}=\mathrm{Span}\{|\eta_{m}\rangle\}=\mathcal{H}_{\eta}.

(74)

And hence the density matrix becomes:

\begin{split}\langle x|\sum_{p}|x^{\eta}_{p}\rangle\langle x^{\eta}_{p}|x_{2}\rangle=&N^{-1/2}\langle x|\sum_{p}|x^{\eta}_{p}\rangle\bar{\xi}^{\eta}_{p}(x_{2})\\
=&N^{-1}\sum_{p}\xi^{\eta}_{p}(x)\bar{\xi}^{\eta}_{p}(x_{2}).\end{split}

(75)

The right-hand side of Eq. (
75
) involves more than one
position eigenvectors, thereby making the density matrix potentially broader
than the inherent spread of
|x^{\eta}_{p}\rangle
(one example is a delta
function overlapping all molecular orbits).

On the other hand, the PPE for
|x_{p_{2}}\rangle\in\mathcal{H}_{x}
is:

\begin{split}\langle x|\sum_{p}|x_{p}\rangle\langle x_{p}|x_{p_{2}}\rangle=&\langle x|\sum_{p}|x_{p}\rangle\delta_{p,p_{p_{2}}}\\
=&N^{-1/2}\xi_{p_{2}}(x).\end{split}

(76)

The right-hand side of the above equation consists of only one position
eigenfunction.

In the case of the position scaling-eigenfunction, Pdb3, Pdb4, Pdb5 and Psy4, the spreads
increase by 10 to 50 % by replacing
|x_{p}\rangle\in\mathcal{H}_{X}
with

|x_{2}\rangle\in\mathcal{H}
.

IV
MLWF and PPE in k-Space

Partial differential equations for MLWFs in k-space give insight into properties of MLWFs and their relatives.
The focus of the section is on a description of the degree of the closeness of MLWFs and PPEs, namely
Eq. (
64
).

IV.1
Partial Differential Equations for MLWFs

By expanding MLWFs with the BFs, another solution procedure for MLWFs is obtained:

\begin{split}|W\rangle=\sum_{k}\sum_{n}w_{k}^{n}|\psi_{k}^{n}\rangle,\end{split}

(77)

where the series number
s
is dropped and the home unit cell index
l
is assumed to be
0
.
After projecting the Eq. (
41
) onto the bra of a BF,
we have a set of partial differential equations for
w_{k}
(see Appendix
C.6
for the detail):

\frac{\partial w_{k}^{n_{1}}}{\partial k}=\sum_{m,n_{2}}\frac{\partial\bar{c}_{m,k}^{n_{1}}}{\partial k}c_{m,k}^{n_{2}}w_{k}^{n_{2}}-ix_{0}w_{k}^{n_{1}},\\

(78)

where
x_{0}
has to be chosen to have maximum localization.

When all energy bands are included in the calculation of MLWFs, the following holds:

\mathcal{H}_{C}=\mathcal{H}_{B}=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\mathcal{H}_{B_{n}}\\
=\mathcal{H}_{X}.

(79)

Therefore, position eigenvectors
\{|x_{p}\rangle\}
, MLWFs and PPEs are identical.
In fact, the position eigenvectors can conversely be expanded by the BFs:

\begin{split}|x_{p}\rangle&=\sum_{k}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}|\psi_{k}^{n}\rangle\langle\psi_{k}^{n}|x_{p}\rangle\\
&=\frac{1}{\sqrt{L}}\sum_{k}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}e^{-ikp\Delta x}\overline{c}_{m}^{n,k}|\psi_{k}^{n}\rangle,\\
\end{split}

(80)

and it is easily checked the following satisfies Eq. (
78
):

\begin{split}&w_{k,p}=e^{-ikx_{p}}\overline{c}_{m}^{n,k},\\
&x_{0}=p\Delta x=x_{p}.\end{split}

(81)

IV.2
MLWF and PPE in Single-Band System

IV.2.1
Phase of MLWF

When an MLWF’s home unit cell is
[0:1)
,
Eq. (
78
) for single-band system is simplified:

\begin{split}\frac{\partial w_{k}}{\partial k}&=\sum_{m}\frac{\partial\bar{c}_{m,k}}{\partial k}c_{m,k}w_{k}-ix_{0}w_{k}.\\
\end{split}

(82)

By defining
r_{m,k}
and
\theta_{m,k}
in the following way:

c_{m,k}=r_{m,k}e^{i\theta_{m,k}},\;r_{m,k},\in\mathbb{R}^{+},\;\theta_{m,k}\in\mathbb{R},

(83)

the first term on the right-hand side of Eq. (
82
)
turns out to be the cell expectation value of the change in
\theta_{m,k}
:

\begin{split}\sum_{m=0}^{N-1}\frac{\partial\bar{c}_{m,k}}{\partial k}c_{m,k}&=\sum_{m=0}^{N-1}\frac{\partial r_{m,k}e^{-i\theta_{m,k}}}{\partial k}r_{m,k}e^{i\theta_{m,k}}\\
&=-i\sum_{m=0}^{N-1}\frac{\partial\theta_{m,k}}{\partial k}r_{m,k}^{2}.\\
\end{split}

(84)

By defining:

\bar{\theta}_{k}=\int^{k}dk_{1}\langle\frac{\partial\theta_{k_{1}}}{\partial k_{1}}\rangle_{cell}=\int^{k}dk_{1}\sum_{m=0}^{N-1}\frac{\partial\theta_{m,k_{1}}}{\partial k_{1}}r_{m,k_{1}}^{2},

(85)

and choosing
x_{0}
to make the WF most localized, the MLWF is solved as follows:

\begin{split}|W_{l}\rangle&=\sum_{k}e^{-i\bar{\theta}_{k}}e^{-ikx_{0}}e^{-ikl}|\psi_{k}\rangle.\\
\end{split}

(86)

In the above equation, the term
e^{-i\bar{\theta}_{k}}
works to reshape the resulting WF sharper,
by making the expectation value of the phase zero at each wave number.

IV.2.2
The Most Localized PPE and Its Error

A PPE in
\mathcal{H}_{B_{1}}
is:

\begin{split}\sum_{k}\!\!|\psi_{k}\rangle\langle\psi_{k}|x_{p}\rangle&\!=\!\frac{1}{\sqrt{L}}\sum e^{-ikl}e^{-ikm\Delta x}\bar{c}_{m,k}|\psi_{k}\rangle.\\
\end{split}

(87)

By applying the procedure described in Sec.
III.3.3
, we have orthonormalized PPE:

\begin{split}|x^{\perp}_{p}\rangle&=\frac{1}{\sqrt{L}}\sum_{k}e^{-ikl}e^{-ikm\Delta x}\frac{\bar{c}_{m,k}}{|c_{m,k}|}|\psi_{k}\rangle\\
&=\frac{1}{\sqrt{L}}\sum_{k}e^{-ikl}e^{-ikm\Delta x}e^{-i\theta_{m,k}}|\psi_{k}\rangle\\
&=\sum_{k}b_{p,k}|\psi_{k}\rangle.\end{split}

(88)

Thus, the series
\{b_{p,k}\}
satisfies the following equation:

\frac{\partial b_{p,k}}{\partial k}=-i\frac{\partial\theta_{m,k}}{\partial k}b_{p,k}-i(l+m\Delta x)b_{p,k}.

(89)

\bar{c}_{m,k}
in Eq. (
88
) offsets the phase of each BF
k
-by-
k
at
x=p\Delta x=l+m\Delta x
,
so that all BFs are aligned at this position.

One of the possible first approximation of Eq. (
85
),
is to replace the expectation values of the phases with the phases at maximum point of the
following
probability density
:

\sum_{k}\left|c^{n}_{m,k}\right|^{2}=\sum_{k}\left|r_{m,k}\right|^{2}.

(90)

And hence,

\begin{split}\int^{k}dk_{1}\sum_{m=0}^{N-1}\frac{\partial\theta_{m,k_{1}}}{\partial k_{1}}r_{m,k_{1}}^{2}&\simeq\sum_{m=0}^{N-1}\theta_{m,k}r_{m,k}^{2}\\
&\sim\theta_{m_{0},k},\\
\end{split}

(91)

where
m_{0}
is the maximum point of Eq. (
90
).

For the reason, PPEs and MLWFs are close to each other when they are highly localized.
And the measure of the error
\delta_{k}
is:

\delta_{k}=|\overline{\theta}_{k}-\theta_{m_{0},k}|.

(92)

The above analysis does not directly apply in the case of composite band systems, and still the analysis needs more development. In the paper,
the numerical results supporting the validity of the PPE
will be shown later in Sec.
VI
.

V
Numerical Scheme

The section describes solution procedures of the 1D Schrödinger and Maxwell’s equations. Especially, the capability of the position scaling-eigenfunctions
to diagonalize the potential energy and the electric susceptibility
is explained and utilized.

V.1
Diagonalization of Potential Energy and Electric Susceptibility

Since the position scaling-eigenfunctions are chosen as the basis vectors, the
position operator
\tilde{x}\in\mathcal{H}_{X}
is diagonal by definition.
And if
V(x)
is piecewise quadratic over a few calculation cells,
as described in detail in Appendix
B.5.3
,
the following holds:

\begin{split}\langle x_{p_{1}}|V(\hat{x})|x_{p_{2}}\rangle=V(x_{p_{2}})\delta_{p_{1},{p_{2}}}+O(\sigma^{2}_{0}/N^{2}),\end{split}

(93)

where

\begin{array}[]{c}\sigma^{2}_{0}=\langle x_{0}|\hat{x}^{2}|x_{0}\rangle,\\
p_{1},p_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}.\end{array}

(94)

Since
\sigma^{2}_{0}
is of the order of
0.1
, the error above is estimated to be

\sim 0.1/N
and practically negligible. Thus, the potential energy and the
electric susceptibility become diagonal with respect to the position
scaling-eigenfunctions.

When
V(x)
is given as a
\delta
-function such as:

\begin{split}V(x,a)&=V_{0}\delta(x-a),\end{split}

(95)

the corresponding potential energy operator is discretized to be
(see Appendix
B.5.3
for detail):

\begin{split}\hat{V}(a)=NV_{0}|x_{p}\rangle\langle x_{p}|\quad(x_{p}=a,p=a/\Delta x).\end{split}

(96)

Thus, the numerical expression of the matrix is diagonal:

\begin{split}\langle x_{p_{1}}|\hat{V}(a)|x_{p_{2}}\rangle=V_{0}N\delta_{p_{1},p_{2}}.\end{split}

(97)

V.2
Electronic System

Owing to Eq. (
171
), the operators of the discretized
Hamiltonian in
\mathcal{H}_{X}
becomes:

\begin{split}\hat{H}(\hat{p}^{2},\hat{x})\rightarrow\tilde{H}(\tilde{p}^{2},\tilde{x}).\end{split}

(98)

By this, the potential energy matrix becomes diagonal if the operator is composed of
\hat{x}
alone.
Thus, the Schrödinger equation becomes:

\tilde{H}|\psi^{n}_{k}\rangle=E_{k}^{n}|\psi^{n}_{k}\rangle.

(99)

By multiplying the ket of a position eigenvector and substituting Eq. (
37
) into Eq. (
99
), we have:

\begin{split}\sum_{m_{2}=0}^{N-1}\langle x_{m_{1}}|H|x_{m_{2}}\rangle e^{ik(m_{2}-m_{1})\Delta x}c^{n,k}_{m_{2}}=E_{k}^{n}c^{n,k}_{m_{1}},\\
\langle x_{m_{1}}|H|x_{m_{2}}\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\langle x_{m_{1}}|\hat{p}^{2}|x_{m_{2}}\rangle+v(x_{m_{1}})\delta_{m_{1,}m_{2}}.\end{split}

(100)

With the kinetic energy matrix elements,
\{Q_{r}\}
, listed on Table
2
,
the final form of the eigenvalue equation to determine the series
\{c_{m}^{n,k}\}
is obtained as follows:

\begin{split}\sum_{m_{2}=0}^{N-1}\left[Q_{m_{1}-m_{2}}+\delta_{m_{1},m_{2}}V(m_{2}\Delta x)\right]&e^{ik(m_{2}-m_{1})\Delta x}c_{m_{2}}^{n,k}\\
=&E_{k}^{n}c_{m_{1}}^{n,k}.\end{split}

(101)

V.3
Optical System

The equations for the electric field
f(x)
and magnetic field
h(x)
satisfy the following equations
Gupta and Bradlyn 2022
:

\begin{split}-\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x^{2}}f(x)&=\left(\frac{\omega}{c}\right)^{2}\epsilon(x)f(x),\\
h(x)&=-\frac{c}{\omega}\frac{\partial}{\partial x}f(x),\end{split}

(102)

where
\epsilon(x)\geq 0
is the dielectric susceptibility function.

By using the same strategy described in Sec.
V.2
, the discretized equation for
the electric field becomes:

\begin{split}\sum_{m_{2}=0}^{N-1}Q_{m_{1}-m_{2}}&e^{ik(m_{2}-m_{1})\Delta x}c_{m_{2}}^{n,k}\\
&=\omega^{2}_{k}\epsilon(m_{1}\Delta x)c_{m_{1}}^{n,k}.\end{split}

(103)

The set of eigenvalue equations are solved with a math library such as the GSL

Galassi et al.
.

V.4
Basis Vectors and BFs

The solutions of Eqs. (
101
) and
(
103
),
\{c_{k,m}^{n}\}
are elements of a matrix
\bm{c}_{k}
transforming the position scaling-eigenvectors
\{|x_{m}\rangle\}
to
the BFs
\{|\psi^{k}_{n}\rangle\}
and the matrix is inherently square with
respect to
m
(position) and
n
(energy band). It is noted even if we replace
the position scaling-eigenvectors with other basis set, the number of the
independent basis vectors in a unit cell and the number of the computable bands
are identical.

VII
Summary

VII.1
Position Eigenfunctions

Position scaling-eigenfunctions with practically compact supports and relatively
good interpolation capability are generated from compactly supported
orthonormal SFs.

The matrix elements of the operators, especially those of the kinetic energy
operator, are obtained without performing differentiation. This relaxes the
condition on differentiability of the original compact support SFs.

VII.2
MLWF, PPE and PWE

When the MLWFs are composed of all BFs of all energy bands, the MLWFs and the
position scaling-eigenfunctions
are identical.

In single band cases, the difference between PPEs and MLWFs is the choice of

anti-phases
to cancel the phases of the BFs. In case the error
estimated by Eq. (
92
) is small enough, it is sufficient to
use PPEs as an alternative to MLWFs.

The relationship between the zeros and maxima of MLWFs and the corresponding
PPEs are explained in relation to the inter- and intra-series orthogonality of
MLWFs.

One of the advantages of using PPE is that it does not take additional
localized orbits/bases other than the position scaling-eigenfunctions.
Especially, in cases the centers of the MLWFs do not coincide with the centers
of the atoms
Freimuth et al. 2008
and photonic crystal cases, in which
there is no inherently localized orbits
Takeda et al. 2006
, the PPEs seem
to be particularly useful.

The other advantage followed from the investigation of the PPE and PWE is the
variable to be maximized has a simple form:

\begin{split}&F(m)=\sum_{k}\left|c_{m,k}\right|^{2}\ \left(m=p\ \mathrm{mod}\ N\right).\\
\end{split}

(104)

And hence it is expected to be computationally inexpensive.

VII.3
Numerical Scheme

The results well agree with the preceding results, in some cases, with less
calculation cells in a unit cell.

The number of the bases, i.e., the position scaling-eigenfunctions in one unit
cell and the spatial resolution are equivalent, and hence all spatial
information on the system are readily stored in
\{c^{n}_{k,m}\}

position-by-position.

The particular advantage of the numerical scheme developed is to diagonalize the
potential energy, when it is composed only of the position operator. Because of
this, once the discretization of the Hamiltonian is done, no actual integration
is needed. In addition, the kinetic energy part is band diagonal with constant
matrix elements and hence the eigenvalue and vectors are easy to obtain.

VII.4
Future Direction

On the mathematical side, the mathematical
mechanism of giving compactly supported SFs a higher degree of
differentiability than the Hölder exponent through Eq. (
110
) will
have to be rigorously investigated. A method to estimate the error of PPEs
with respect to the MLWFs in composite band cases has also to be developed to the
degree performed in single band cases.

One of the most important steps to take is to extend the entire scheme to 2 and
3D. For the purpose, the multidimensional scaling functions may have to be
constructed in the way described in Ref.
Cohen and Schlenker 1993
, so that the symmetry
of the system is inherent to the basis set. If the proposed scheme is not
seriously restricted by the extension, it allows actually to calculate the
measurable physical properties of materials, such as electric polarization,
chemical bonding known to be closely related to the configuration of MLWFs

Vanderbilt and King-Smith 1993
;
Resta 1994
;
Wu et al. 2006
;
King-Smith and Vanderbilt 1993
;
Vanderbilt and King-Smith 1993
;
Mustafa et al. 2015
;
Marzari et al. 2012
;
Cancès et al. 2017
;
Alexandradinata et al. 2014
;
King-Smith and Vanderbilt 1993
;
Vanderbilt and King-Smith 1993
;
Cloizeaux 1963
;
Alexandradinata et al. 2014
;
Mustafa et al. 2015
;
Cancès et al. 2017
;
Marzari et al. 2012
;
Koumpouras and Larsson 2020
;
Smirnov and Usvyat 2001
. Concrete estimates of the
efficacy of the method, such as the accuracy, speed and portability, in relation
to the existing codes can be performed at this future stage.

Since the obtained position eigenfunctions are also scaling functions and
satisfy the two-scale relation, the computational speed of the numerical
procedures such as the maximum point searching, solving the eigenvalue problems
associated with Schrödinger equations, can be improved by combining the
multiscale nature, i.e., iterative methods and mesh refinement, in the future
step.

A.1
General
Strategy

If an operator
\hat{A}
satisfies the following
dilation relation:

(x\rightarrow 2x)\longmapsto(\hat{A}\rightarrow 2^{n}\hat{A}).

(105)

The two-scale relation becomes linear equations for the matrix elements
of the operator:

\begin{split}A_{m,n}&=\langle m|\hat{A}|n\rangle\\
&=\int\phi(x-m)A(x,\frac{1}{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x})\phi(x-n)dx\\
&=2\sum_{l,k}h_{l}h_{k}\int\phi(2x-2m-l)\\
&\quad\quad A(x,\frac{1}{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x})\phi(2x-2n-k))dx\\
&=2\sum_{l,k}h_{l}h_{k}\int\phi(y-2m-l)\\
&\quad\quad A(2^{-1}y,\frac{1}{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial\left(2^{-1}y\right)})\phi(y-2n-k)d\left(\frac{y}{2}\right)\\
&=2\sum_{l,k}h_{l}h_{k}\int\phi(y-2m-l)\\
&\quad\quad 2^{-n}A(y,\frac{1}{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial y})\phi(y-2n-k)d\left(\frac{y}{2}\right)\\
&=2^{-n}\sum_{l,k}h_{l}h_{k}A_{2m+l,2n+k}.\end{split}

(106)

It only takes linear algebraic calculations to obtain the
matrix elements, since the final form of the equations involves neither integration
nor differentiation.

A.3.3
Commutation Relation

Since the operators
\tilde{x}
and
\tilde{p}
are defined in a space spanned by a finite number of basis
vectors:

\mathrm{Tr}\{\tilde{x}\tilde{p}\}=\mathrm{Tr}\{\tilde{p}\tilde{x}\},

(117)

and hence, they cannot satisfy the usual commutation relations such as:

[\tilde{p},\tilde{x}]=-i.

(118)

In stead, they satisfy the following commutation relation:

\begin{split}\sum_{l}&P_{m,l}X_{l,n}-X_{m,l}P_{l,n}\\
=&\sum_{l}P_{m-l}(X_{l-n}+n\delta_{l,n})-(X_{m-l}+m\delta_{m,l})P_{l-n}\\
=&(m-n)P_{m-n}.\end{split}

(119)

B.1
Position Eigenvalue

B.1.1
From translational symmetry

A position eigenvector satisfies the following:

\tilde{x}|x_{n}\rangle=x_{n}|x_{n}\rangle.

(120)

By introducing the translation operator:

T(\beta)=e^{i\beta\hat{p}}\ (\beta\in\mathbb{R}),

(121)

it is obvious:

\hat{T}(1)\tilde{x}|x_{n}\rangle=\hat{T}(1)\{x_{n}|x_{n}\rangle\}=x_{n}\{\hat{T}(1)|x_{n}\rangle\}.

(122)

On the other hand, if
\hat{T}(1)
is applied first to
\tilde{x}
:

\begin{split}\hat{T}(1)\tilde{x}|x_{n}\rangle&=\hat{T}(1)\tilde{x}\hat{T}^{\dagger}(1)\hat{T}(1)|x_{n}\rangle\\
&=(\tilde{x}+1)\{\hat{T}(1)|x_{n}\rangle\}.\end{split}

(123)

By comparing the two equations, we get:

\begin{split}x_{n}\{\hat{T}(1)|x_{n}\rangle\}&=(\tilde{x}+1)\{\hat{T}(1)|x_{n}\rangle\}\\
\tilde{x}\{\hat{T}(1)|x_{n}\rangle\}&=(x_{n}-1)\{\hat{T}(1)|x_{n}\rangle\}.\\
\end{split}

(124)

And hence

\begin{array}[]{c}\hat{T}(1)|x_{n}\rangle=|x_{n-1}\rangle,\\
x_{n}=x_{n-1}+1.\end{array}

(125)

From the above,
x_{n}
has to have the following form with an unknown
C_{0}
:

x_{n}=C_{0}+n.

(126)

B.2.1
Equation

The position eigenvectors are expressed by a unitary transformation and the SFs:

|x_{n}\rangle=\sum_{n}\xi_{n,m}|m\rangle.

(129)

By translating an eigenvector by 1:

\begin{split}|x_{n-1}\rangle&=\hat{T}(1)|x_{n}\rangle\\
&=\sum_{m}\xi_{n,m}\hat{T}(1)|m\rangle\\
&=\sum_{m}\xi_{n,m}|m-1\rangle\\
&=\sum_{m}\xi_{n,m+1}|m\rangle.\end{split}

(130)

On the other hand, by definition:

|x_{n-1}\rangle=\sum_{m}\xi_{n-1,m}|m\rangle.

(131)

From this, we find:

\xi_{n-1,m}=\xi_{n,m+1},

and the matrix
\{\xi_{n,m}\}
is simplified to a series:

\xi_{n,m}\rightarrow\xi_{n-m}.

By combining the above with Eq. (
12
)
and Eq. (
128
)
the equation for
\{\xi_{n}\}
becomes:

\sum_{n}\{X_{m-n}\xi_{n-p}+m\delta_{m,n}\xi_{n-p}\}=x_{p}\xi_{m-p}.

(132)

Since the solution has the translational symmetry, it is enough to solve for

p=0
. From Eq. (
126
) regarding the eigenvalue and
Eq. (
128
) regarding the diagonal elements,
Eq. (
132
) has
X_{0}
on the both side of it. By subtracting

X_{0}
from the both side of Eq. (
132
), or equivalently
setting
X_{0}=0
and
x_{p=0}=0
, we get:

\sum_{n}\{X_{m-n}\xi_{n}+m\delta_{m,n}\xi_{n}\}=0.\\

(133)

B.2.2
Analytical Solution Procedure

Although Eq. (
133
) for
\{\xi_{n}\}
is numerically solvable
with a math library such as GSL
Galassi et al.
, the analytical solution gives more
insight into the position eigenvectors.

Since Eq. (
133
) has a form of convolution, Fourier
transformation makes it easier to solve:

\displaystyle\sum_{m,n}X_{m-n}e^{-im\omega}e^{in\omega}\xi_{n}e^{-in\omega}+\sum_{m,n}m\delta_{m,n}\xi_{n}e^{-im\omega}

\displaystyle=0.

(134)

By defining the following two functions:

\displaystyle X(\omega)=\sum_{m}X_{m}e^{-im\omega},

(135)

\displaystyle\xi(\omega)=\sum_{m}\xi_{m}e^{-im\omega},

we get:

\displaystyle X(\omega)\xi(\omega)+i\frac{{\partial}}{\partial\omega}\xi(\omega)

\displaystyle=0.

(136)

With
C
as the normalization factor, the solution is thus:

\begin{split}\xi(\omega)&=C\exp\left(i\sum_{m}\int^{\omega}d\Omega X_{m}e^{im\Omega}\right)\\
&=C\exp\left(-\sum_{m\neq 0}\frac{X_{m}}{m}e^{im\omega}\right).\end{split}

(137)

By using the Bessel function of the first kind
J_{n}(z)
, the following holds
Cuyt et al. 2008
:

\begin{split}e^{-(\frac{X_{m}}{m}e^{im\omega}-\frac{X_{m}}{m}e^{-im\omega})}&=e^{-2iX_{m}\sin(m\omega)}\\
&=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{+\infty}J_{n}\left(-\frac{2X_{m}}{m}\right)e^{inm\theta}.\\
\end{split}

(138)

By defining:

Y_{m}=-\frac{X_{m}}{m},

(139)

\xi(\omega)
is thus factorized as follows:

\begin{split}\xi(\omega)=C&\left\{\sum_{n_{1}=-\infty}^{+\infty}J_{n_{1}}(Y_{1})e^{in_{1}\omega}\right\}\left\{\sum_{n_{2}=-\infty}^{+\infty}J_{n_{2}}(Y_{2})e^{i2n_{2}\omega}\right\}\\
&...\left\{\sum_{n_{T}=-\infty}^{+\infty}J_{n_{M}}(Y_{M})e^{iMn_{M}\omega}\right\}.\\
\end{split}

(140)

By segregating the terms in Eq. (
140
) exponent-by-exponent, we have:

\begin{split}\xi_{n}&=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\xi(\omega)e^{in\omega}d\omega\\
&=C\sum_{n_{1}+2n_{2}+...+Mn_{M}=-n}J_{n_{1}}(Y_{1})J_{n_{2}}(Y_{2})...J_{n_{M}}(Y_{M}).\end{split}

(141)

The following is an example hand calculation of Pdb2:

\begin{split}\xi(\omega)\simeq&(J_{2}(Y_{1})e^{-2i\omega}-J_{1}(Y_{1})e^{-i\omega}\\
&\quad+J_{0}(Y_{1})+J_{1}(Y_{1})e^{i\omega}+J_{2}(Y_{2})e^{2i\omega})\\

&\quad+0.9950+0.0708e^{i\omega}+0.0025e^{2i\omega})\\

\simeq&0.0015e^{-2i\omega}-0.0709e^{-i\omega}\\
&+0.9950+0.0708e^{i\omega}+0.0035e^{2i\omega},\end{split}

(142)

where the values of
\{Y_{m}\}
are obtained from Table

through Eq. (

B.2.3
Rate of Decay of
\xi_{n}

If

F^{m}(\omega)=\left\{\sum_{n=-\infty}^{+\infty}J_{n}(Y_{m})e^{inm\omega}\right\}.

(143)

is analytic,
\xi(\omega)
is also analytic
Ahlfers 1979
and hence

\xi_{n}
decays faster than any polynomial
Pinsky 2008
.

The decay of the following Fourier series is, therefore, first estimated to show

is analytic:

f^{m}_{nm}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int F^{m}(\omega)e^{-inm\omega}d\omega.

(144)

When
|n|
is large enough to meet:

0<|Y_{m}|\ll\sqrt{n+1},\\

(145)

the following holds
Abramowitz and Stegun 1964
:

J_{n}(Y_{m})\sim\sign(n)^{|n|}\frac{1}{|n|!}\left(\frac{Y_{m}}{2}\right)^{|n|}.

(146)

And hence

\begin{split}|f^{m}_{nm}|&=\frac{1}{|n|!}\left|\frac{Y_{m}}{2}\right|^{|n|}\\
&=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi|n|}}\left(\frac{e}{|n|}\right)^{|n|}\left|\frac{Y_{m}}{2}\right|^{|n|}\\
&=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi|n|}}\left(\frac{e\left|Y_{m}\right|}{2|n|}\right)^{|n|}.\end{split}

(147)

From Table

\left(\frac{e\left|Y_{m}\right|}{2|n|}\right)^{|n|}<1,

(148)

and hence positive constants
A
and
c_{0}

satisfying the following system of inequality exist:

|f^{m}_{nm}|<Ae^{-c_{0}|n|}\ \quad(n\in\mathbb{Z}).

(149)

By defining
r=nm

c_{1}=c_{0}/m
, we have

|f^{m}_{r}|<Ae^{-c_{1}|r|}\ \quad(r\in\mathbb{Z}).

(150)

Thus,
F^{m}(\omega)
is analytic
Pinsky 2008

\xi(\omega)
is analytic and
\xi_{n}
decays faster than any polynomial of
r

\begin{split}\delta_{mn}=&\langle m|\hat{1}|n\rangle\\

\begin{split}\tilde{f}(x)&=\sum f_{p}\phi(x-p),\\

(160)

Certain SFs called interpolation SFs, such as the Shannon SF
Daubechies 1992f
, satisfy:

f_{p}=f(p).

(161)

This implies the values of an incoming signal or a function on
the ticks are by themselves expansion coefficients, thereby substantially
reducing the cost of decomposition (albeit, the Shannon SF is hard to employ in
practical cases for the slow decay). This section therefore focuses on the
interpolation capability of the position scaling-eigenfunctions and the eventual
reduction in the potential energy matrix elements.

B.5.1
Interpolation Capability of Position Scaling-Eigenfunctions

In general case, by using a set of general orthonormal scaling functions

\{\phi_{p}(x)\}
,
f_{p}
is expressed as follows:

\begin{split}f_{p}&=\int dxf(x)\phi_{p}(x)\\
&=\int dx\langle x|f(\hat{x})|\phi_{p}\rangle.\end{split}

(162)

If
f(x)
is piece-wise quadratic polynomial over a few calculation cells:

f(\hat{x})=a_{0}+a_{1}\hat{x}+a_{2}\hat{x}^{2}.

(163)

And if the sampling rate or the resolution is high enough to reconstruct the
signal or the function, the operation can be done in
\mathcal{H_{S}}
.
Thus, following approximation is valid in the vicinity where Eq. (
163
)
holds:

\begin{split}f(\hat{x})|\phi_{p}\rangle&=a_{0}|\phi_{p}\rangle+a_{1}\hat{x}|\phi_{p}\rangle+a_{2}\hat{x}^{2}|\phi_{p}\rangle\\
&=a_{0}|\phi_{p}\rangle+a_{1}(\sum_{r}X_{r}|\phi_{p+r}\rangle+p|\phi_{p}\rangle)\\
&\ +\!\!a_{2}(\sum_{r}\sigma_{r}^{2}|\phi_{p+r}\rangle\!+\!2p\sum_{r}X_{r}|\phi_{p+r}\rangle\!+\!p^{2}|\phi_{p}\rangle)\\
&=\{f(p)+(a_{1}X_{0}+2a_{2}pX_{0}+a_{2}\sigma^{2}_{0})\}|\phi_{p}\rangle\\
&\ +\sum_{r\neq 0}\{(a_{1}+2a_{2}p)X_{r}+a_{2}\sigma_{r}^{2}\}|\phi_{p+r}\rangle\\
&=f_{p}|\phi_{p}\rangle+\sum_{r\neq 0}f_{p}^{r}|\phi_{p+r}\rangle,\end{split}

(164)

where

\begin{array}[]{l}f_{p}=f(p)+(a_{1}X_{0}+2a_{2}pX_{0}+a_{2}\sigma^{2}_{0}),\\
f_{p}^{r}=(a_{1}+2a_{2}p)X_{r}+a_{2}\sigma_{r}^{2},\end{array}

(165)

and the contribution from the higher resolution wavelets are ignored when the
operator acts on the kets.

By choosing one of the position scaling-eigenvectors as
\phi(x)
, all elements
of
\{X_{r}\}
become zero. Furthermore,
\sigma_{r}^{2}(r\neq 0)
shown in Table
13

becomes substantially smaller than those of the original SFs listed in Table

9
. And hence:

\begin{array}[]{l}f_{p}=f(p)+O(\sigma^{2}_{0}),\\
f_{p}^{r}=0+O(\sigma^{2}_{r}).\end{array}

(166)

In the physical domain:

\begin{array}[]{l}f_{p}=N^{-1/2}f(x_{p})+O(\sigma^{2}_{0}/N^{2}),\\
f_{p}^{r}=0+O(\sigma^{2}_{r}/N^{2}),\\
x_{p}=p/N\ (L_{0},V_{0}\in\mathbb{R}).\end{array}

(167)

B.5.2
Application to BFs

From Eqs. (
37
) and (
166
),

\begin{split}e^{ikl}e^{ikm\Delta x}c_{m,k}^{n}&=\psi_{k,p}^{n}\\
&\simeq N^{-1/2}\psi_{k}^{n}(x_{p}).\\
\end{split}

(168)

And hence,

\begin{split}|\psi^{n}_{k}\rangle=N^{-1/2}\sum_{n,k}\psi^{n}_{k}(x_{p})|x_{p}\rangle+O(\sigma^{2}_{0}/N^{2}).\\
\end{split}

(169)

It also applies to Wannier functions and in the same way:

|W_{l}^{s}\rangle=N^{-1/2}\sum_{p}W_{l}^{s}(x_{p})|x_{p}\rangle+O(\sigma^{2}_{0}/N^{2}).

(170)

B.5.3
Application to Potential Energy Matrix Elements

If the potential energy as a function of
x
is piece-wise quadratic polynomial
over a few calculation cells, the matrix elements of operator
V(\hat{x})
is
calculated by utilizing Eq. (
164
):

\begin{split}\langle x_{p_{1}}|V(\hat{x})|x_{p_{2}}\rangle&=\langle x_{p_{1}}|\{V(\hat{x})|x_{p_{2}}\rangle\}\\
&=\langle x_{p_{1}}|\{V(x_{p_{2}})|x_{p_{2}}\rangle\}+O(\sigma^{2}_{0}/N^{2})\\
&=V(x_{p_{2}})\delta_{p_{1},{p_{2}}}+O(\sigma^{2}_{0}/N^{2}).\end{split}

(171)

The approximation above is, in effect, equivalent of:

V(\hat{x})\rightarrow V(\tilde{x}).

(172)

B.5.4
\delta
-function Potential

When
V(x)
is given as a
\delta
-function such as:

\begin{split}V(x,a)&=V_{0}\delta(x-a),\\
\end{split}

(173)

preserving the character of the
\delta
-function as an
impulse
,
rather than the direct projection of the delta function onto the composite band
space, is prioritized.

The formal potential operator is calculated as follows:

\begin{split}\hat{V}(a)&=\int\!\!\!dx|x\rangle V_{0}\delta(x\!\!-\!\!a)\langle x|\\
&=V_{0}|a\rangle\langle a|,\end{split}

(174)

and hence,

\begin{array}[]{r}\langle f|\hat{V}(a)|g\rangle=V_{0}f(a)g(a)\quad\left(\forall|f\rangle,|g\rangle\in\mathcal{H}\right).\end{array}

(175)

In
\mathcal{H}_{X}
, on the other hand,

\begin{array}[]{r}\begin{aligned} \langle f|x_{p}\rangle\langle x_{p}|g\rangle&=f_{p}g_{p}\\
&=\frac{1}{N}f(x_{p})f(x_{p})\quad\left(\forall|f\rangle,|g\rangle\in\mathcal{H}_{X}\right).\end{aligned}\end{array}

(176)

Thus, the potential energy operator is discretized to be:

\begin{array}[]{c}V_{0}|a\rangle\langle a|\rightarrow NV_{0}|x_{p}\rangle\langle x_{p}|,\\
x_{p}=a,\\
p=a/\Delta x.\end{array}

(177)

Eventually, the numerical expression of the
\delta
-function potential becomes as follows:

\begin{split}\langle x_{p}|\hat{V}(a)|x_{p_{1}}\rangle=V_{0}N\delta_{p,p_{1}}.\end{split}

(178)

Note that if the direct projection of
\hat{V}(a)
on to
\mathcal{H}_{X}
is
carried out, it will result in a possibly broader potential energy operator as
described in Sec.
III.4
:

\begin{split}\sum_{p,q}|x_{p}\rangle\langle x_{p}|\hat{V}(a)|x_{q}\rangle\langle x_{q}|=\sum_{p,q}|x_{p}\rangle\xi_{p}(a)\xi_{q}(a)\langle x_{q}|.\end{split}

(179)

C.1
Eigenvalue Equations for MLWFs

An MLWF satisfies the following equations
Marzari and Vanderbilt 1997
Kivelson 1982
:

\begin{split}\tilde{x}|W\rangle&=x|W\rangle.\\
\end{split}

(182)

Since the number of energy bands involved is
n_{c}
and the total unit cell
number is
L
, Eq. (
182
) has
n_{c}\times L
eigenvalues and
eigenvectors, if there is no degeneracy:

\begin{split}\tilde{x}|W^{s}_{l}\rangle&=x^{s}_{l}|W^{s}_{l}\rangle.\\
\end{split}

(183)

An MLWF is expanded by the primal WFs pertaining to cell
l
and energy band
n
,
\{|\tilde{W}^{n}_{l}\rangle\}
:

\begin{split}|W^{s}_{l}\rangle&=\sum_{l_{1},n}U_{l,l_{1}}^{s,n}|\tilde{W}^{n}_{l_{1}}\rangle,\\
\end{split}

(184)

with

\begin{split}\quad|\tilde{W}^{n}_{l}\rangle=L^{-1/2}\sum_{k}e^{-ikl}|\psi_{k}^{n}\rangle.\end{split}

(185)

Substituting the above into Eq. (
183
), the eigenvalue equations are obtained:

\begin{array}[]{l}\sum_{l_{2},n_{2}}X^{n_{1},n_{2}}_{l_{1},l_{2}}U_{l,l_{2}}^{s,n_{2}}=x^{s}_{l}U_{l,l_{1}}^{s,n_{1}},\\
X^{n_{1},n_{2}}_{l_{1},l_{2}}=\langle\tilde{W}^{n_{1}}_{l_{1}}|\tilde{x}|\tilde{W}^{n_{2}}_{l_{2}}\rangle.\end{array}

(186)

From the translational invariance of system, we have:

\begin{split}x^{s}_{l+1}&=x^{s}_{l}+1.\\
\end{split}

(187)

Since each series has corresponding eigenvalue for each cell number
l
, one
unit cell hosts
n_{c}
different
maximally
localized Wannier functions
and they are the candidates for the truly maximally localized Wannier
function.

In the same manner, an eigenvalue equation for MLWFs can also be obtained using the BFs:

\begin{array}[]{l}\sum_{k_{2},n_{2}}X^{n_{1},n_{2}}_{k_{1},k_{2}}U_{l,k_{2}}^{s,n_{2}}=x^{s}_{l}U_{l,k_{1}}^{s,n_{1}},\\
X^{n_{1},n_{2}}_{k_{1},k_{2}}=\langle\psi^{n_{1}}_{k_{1}}|\tilde{x}|\psi^{n_{2}}_{k_{2}}\rangle.\end{array}

(188)

The above equation is equivalent of Eq. (
78
) (see Eq. (
201
))
and useful, when the gauge of the BFs is
dealt with. However, it is not suited to solving
Eq. (
183
), since the position operator matrix
is not diagonal dominant. In the present paper,
therefore, MLWFs are obtained by solving Eq. (
186
) with the GSL
library
Galassi et al.
