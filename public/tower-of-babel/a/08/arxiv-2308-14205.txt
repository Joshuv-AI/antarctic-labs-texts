Schur-positive Sets

Abstract

The notion of descent set for permutations and standard Young tableaux (abbreviated to SYT) is classical. A significant problem in contemporary algebraic combinatorics is determining whether the associated quasisymmetric function is symmetric and Schur-positive.
Gessel proved that Knuth classes of permutations and inverse descent classes are Schur-positive.
Other examples of Schur-positive permutation sets follow from seminal works by Foata-Schützenberger and Gessel-Reutenauer: permutations of fixed Coxeter length, conjugacy classes, and
d
-roots of unity.

Sometimes it is possible to define descents on maximal chains of certain posets using an
EL
-labeling
of the edges in the Hasse diagram. A classic example of a Schur-positive set of this type is the set of all maximal chains in the non-crossing partition lattice of type
A
, which was given by Stanley. In this case, the maximal chains can be realized by edge-labeled trees. A long-standing problem is to find other Schur-positive sets of maximal chains or trees.

In Chapter 3, we present an appealing set of maximal chains in the non-crossing
partition lattice (equivalently: a set of edge-labeled trees) that is Schur-positive:
we show that the subset of maximal chains in the non-crossing partition lattice, which may be realized
by convex caterpillars, is Schur positive. We prove the following.

Theorem

(
3.1.4
)
.

The set of linearly ordered factorizations of the
n
-cycle
(1,2,\dots,n)

satisfies

\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{L}_{n})=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}(k+1)s_{(n-k,1^{k})},

where the descent set of any
u\in\mathcal{L}_{n}
is defined by the edge labeling of
[
9
]
.
In particular,
\mathcal{L}_{n}
is Schur-positive.

The proof uses the Goulden-Yong partial order on the edges together with
a variant of an
EL
-labeling due to Björner.

The rest of our work studies
cyclic descent extensions
(CDE) of Schur-positive sets of permutations. The study of cyclic descent extensions has been an active research area in recent years.
Klyachko and Cellini introduced a natural notion of cyclic descent set for permutations. This notion was further studied by Dilks, Petersen, Stembridge, and many others.
An analogous notion for standard Young tableaux, using an axiomatic approach, was presented by Rhoades, Adin-Reiner-Roichman, and others.

A significant problem in this field is whether a given set of permutations admits a cyclic descent extension.
The existence of cyclic extensions is related to significant problems in various fields, in particular, to the non-negativity of the coefficients of the Postnikov toric Schur functions and a long-standing open problem of Thrall on higher Lie characters. Adin, Hegedüs and Roichman, in
[
3
]
, gave an algebraic characterization for Schur-positive sets that admit a CDE.

In Chapter 4, we study an important Schur-positive subset of the symmetric group, the set of all permutations of fixed Coxeter length.
We obtain sufficient conditions for the positive integer
m
, under which there is a CDE for permutations of length
m
in the symmetric group
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
, for almost all
n
.
The result involves surprising connections to generalized pentagonal numbers.
We prove the following.

Theorem

(
4.3.11
)
.

For
k<n
, the set of permutations in
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
with
k
inversions has a cyclic descent extension if and only if
k
is not a generalized pentagonal number.

In Chapter 5, we state a conjectured necessary and sufficient condition for
d
-roots of unity in
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
to carry a cyclic descent extension.

Conjecture
.

The set of roots of unity of order
d
in
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
has a cyclic descent extension if and only if
d
and
n
are not coprime.

We prove the following results, towards this conjecture.

Theorem

(
5.1.6
)
.

The set of roots of unity of order
d
in
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
:

1.

Does not have a cyclic descent extension if
n
and
d
are coprime.

2.

Has a cyclic descent extension when
d
is a prime power and
d
and
n

are not coprime.

Chapter 1
Introduction

Given a set
A
of combinatorial objects equipped with a descent function
Des:A\to 2^{[n-1]}
, define the quasi-symmetric function

\mathcal{Q}(A)\coloneqq\sum_{a\in A}\mathcal{F}_{n,\Des(a)}

where
\mathcal{F}_{n,D}
are Gessel’s
fundamental quasi-symmetric functions
; see Subsection
2.5.2
for more details. The following long-standing problem was posed by Gessel and Reutenauer in
[
22
]
.

Problem
.

For which sets
A
is
\mathcal{Q}(A)
symmetric?

A symmetric function is called
Schur-positive
if all the coefficients in its expansion in the basis of Schur functions are non-negative; see Subsection
2.5.1
for definitions.
Determining whether a given symmetric function is Schur-positive is a significant problem in contemporary algebraic combinatorics; see Stanley’s article
[
45
]
.
We say that the set
A
of combinatorial objects is Schur-positive if
\mathcal{Q}(A)
is Schur-positive; for details and examples, see Sections
2.5.1
and
2.5.3
.

Another approach to Schur-positivity is via a significant problem in combinatorial representation theory: Provide explicit formulas that express the character values of interesting representations as a weighted enumeration of combinatorial objects. The reader is referred to the survey article by Barcelo and Ram
[
11
]
. Many such formulas have been found; examples include the Murnaghan-Nakayama rule
[
44
, Section 7.17]
, characters of Specht modules of ribbon shapes
[
21
]
, Lie characters
[
22
]
, Gelfand models
[
5
]
, characters of homogeneous components of the coinvariant algebra
[
4
]
, characters induced from a lower ranked exterior algebra
[
17
]
and k-root enumerators
[
37
]
, among others. It turns out that the sets that appear in these formulas are Schur-positive sets.
An abstract framework for this phenomenon was proposed in
[
8
]
. In particular, it was shown in
[
8
]
that the equality of the characters of two Schur-positive sets is equivalent to the equidistribution of their descent sets, implying the equivalence of classical theorems in representation theory, for example, the equivalence of theorems of Lusztig-Stanley in invariant theory
[
42
]
and Foata-Schützenberger in permutation statistics
[
19
]
. Their work is a development of the ideas of Gessel and Reutenauer in
[
22
]
.

Note that equivalent - but different terminology is used in the literature in this field. In particular,
Schur-positive sets are also called
fine sets
. In this work, we use only the term Schur-positive.

Schur-positive sets are often subsets of the symmetric group
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
. However, there are important examples that are not of this form. In particular, a part of our work deals with Schur-positive sets of maximal chains in posets. Maximal chains in a labeled poset
P
are equipped with a natural descent map. A well-known conjecture of Stanley
[
40
, III, Ch. 21]

implies that all examples of Schur-positive labeled posets, in this sense
correspond to intervals in the Young lattice.

Another way to equip the set of maximal chains with a descent map is by labeling the edges in the Hasse diagram.
A classic example of a Schur-positive set
of this type,
the set of all maximal chains in the non-crossing partition lattice of type
A
,
was given by Stanley
[
41
]
.
An
EL
edge-labeling of this poset was presented in an earlier work of Björner
[
12
]
; see also
[
14
,
31
,
9
]
.

Adin and Roichman presented in
[
9
]
a variant of the
EL
-labeling that was defined by Björner, using the fact that every maximal chain bijectively corresponds to a factorization of the cycle
(1\ \dots\ n)
in
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
into a product of
n-1
transpositions. This variant will be used in our work.

Goulden and Yong in
[
23
]
showed that every maximal chain in the non-crossing partition lattice can be interpreted as a geometric non-crossing tree with an ordering of the edges (see Chapter
3
for more details). In particular, their construction defines a poset structure on the edges of a non-crossing geometric tree.
It is natural to ask when this order is a
linear order
.
In Chapter
3
, we provide a detailed description of these trees, named
convex caterpillars
. We show that the set
\mathcal{L}_{n}
of maximal chains that correspond to these trees is Schur-positive and calculate the corresponding quasisymmetric function.
We prove the following result:

Theorem

(
3.1.4
)
.

The set of linearly ordered factorizations of the
n
-cycle
(1,2,\dots,n)

satisfies

\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{L}_{n})=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}(k+1)s_{(n-k,1^{k})},

where the descent set of any
u\in\mathcal{L}_{n}
is defined by the edge labeling of
[
9
]
.
In particular,
\mathcal{L}_{n}
is Schur-positive.

Here and elsewhere,
s_{\lambda}
denotes the Schur function indexed by a partition
\lambda
. See Subsection
2.5.1
.

The rest of our work is dedicated to studying
cyclic descent extensions
for several classes of Schur-positive sets in
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
. The study of cyclic descent extensions has been an active research area in recent years.

One of the first works studying cyclic descents was a paper by Cellini
[
15
]
, who introduced a natural notion of cyclic descent on permutations as follows:

{\rm CDES}(\pi)\coloneqq\left\{1\leq i\leq n:\pi_{i}>\pi_{i+1}\right\}\subseteq\left[n\right]\ \text{for all}\ \pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}.

with the convention
\pi_{n+1}\coloneqq\pi_{1}
.
A more restricted notion of a cyclic descent number was used by Klyachko in
[
28
]
. Cyclic descent sets were further studied by Dilks, Petersen and Stembridge in
[
16
]
.

A well-established notion of descent also exists for standard Young tableaux (SYT); however, there is no apparent cyclic analog. A significant milestone on the way to defining a cyclic descent set on SYT was the work of Rhoades
[
35
]
, who described a notion of cyclic descent set for SYT of rectangular shape. Some properties common to Cellini’s definition for permutations and Rhoades’ construction for SYT appeared in other combinatorial settings
[
33
,
32
,
18
,
1
]
. These properties led to an axiomatic definition of a cyclic descent extension in
[
6
]
:

Definition

(
4.1.3
)
.

Let
\mathcal{T}
be a finite set, equipped with a descent map
\Des:\mathcal{T}\longrightarrow 2^{[n-1]}
.
A cyclic extension of
\Des
is
a pair
(\cDes,p)
, where

\cDes:\mathcal{T}\longrightarrow 2^{[n]}
is a map
and
p:\mathcal{T}\longrightarrow\mathcal{T}
is a bijection,
satisfying the following axioms: for all
T
in
\mathcal{T}
,

\begin{array}[]{rl}\text{(extension)}&\cDes(T)\cap[n-1]=\Des(T),\\
\text{(equivariance)}&\cDes(p(T))={\rm sh}(\cDes(T)),\\
\text{(non-Escher)}&\varnothing\subsetneq\cDes(T)\subsetneq[n].\\
\end{array}

Here
\mathrm{sh}
denotes the cyclic shift of a subset of
[n]
, as in Definition
4.1.1
.

Cellini’s and Rhoades’ constructions are special cases of Definition
4.1.3
. A naturally arising question is the existence of such an extension for specific subsets of the symmetric group
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
(see Chapter
4
and the discussion at the beginning of
[
3
]
).
It is also not apparent whether a cyclic descent set similar to Rhoades’ can be defined for the set of SYT of every shape.
The question for SYT was solved by Adin, Reiner and Roichman in
[
6
]
. They proved that a cyclic descent extension exists for the set of SYT of skew shape
\lambda/\mu
if and only if
\lambda/\mu
is not a connected ribbon. They used Postnikov’s toric symmetric functions. A constructive proof was found later by Huang in
[
25
]
.

Adin, Hegedüs and Roichman provided in
[
3
]
an algebraic necessary and sufficient condition for the existence of a cyclic descent extension on Schur-positive sets. In addition, they characterized the conjugacy classes in
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
that possess a cyclic descent extension. Their proof was not constructive - providing a combinatorial construction of a cyclic descent extension for conjugacy classes is a significant work in progress. An explicit construction for involutions with fixed points was described in
[
7
]
.
Explicit constructions of a cyclic descent extension for Dyck paths appear in
[
33
]
. This construction was extended to Motzkin paths by B. Han in
[
24
]
.
Another recent development is a cyclic analog of quasi-symmetric functions by Adin, Gessel, Reiner and Roichman in
[
2
]
.

In Chapter
4
, we study the existence of cyclic descent extensions on inverse descent classes and their generalizations.
In particular, we obtain the following result:

Theorem

(Theorem
4.2.6
)
.

The following sets in
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
have cyclic descent extensions:

1.

D_{2^{J},n}^{-1}
if and only if
J\neq\emptyset
.

2.

D_{[I,J],n}^{-1}
for an interval

[I,J]=\left\{K:I\subseteq K\ and\ K\subseteq J\right\}

(in the poset
2^{[n-1]}
ordered by inclusion),
if and only if
I\subsetneq J
.

3.

For every maximal chain
C
in the poset
[I,J]
,
D_{C,n}^{-1}
has a cyclic descent extension if and only if
I\subsetneq J
and
2\not|(|J|-|I|)
.
In particular,
D_{C,n}^{-1}
for a maximal chain
C
in
2^{[n-1]}
has a cyclic descent extension if and only if
n-1
is odd, or equivalently
n
is even.

Here
D_{S,n}^{-1}
denotes, for a set
S
of subsets of
[n]
, a
generalized inverse descent class
, as in Definition
4.2.2
.

In the last part of Chapter
4
, we use this result to describe conditions for the existence of cyclic descent extensions for permutations with a given number of inversions. We determine necessary and sufficient conditions for permutations with a given inversion number in
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
for sufficiently large
n
. Our result has a surprising connection to Euler’s pentagonal number theorem.
We prove the following.

Theorem

(
4.3.11
)
.

For
k<n
, the set of permutations in
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
with
k
inversions has a cyclic descent extension if, and only if,
k
is not a generalized pentagonal number.

In the last chapter, we study roots of unity in
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
and conditions for the existence of a cyclic descent extension, which can be seen as a generalization of the criterion given in
[
3
]
.

In particular, we obtain the following result:

Theorem

(
5.1.6
)
.

The set of roots of unity of order
d
in
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
:

1.

Does not have a cyclic descent extension if
n
and
d
are co-prime.

2.

Has a cyclic descent extension when
d
is a prime power and
d
and
n
are not co-prime.

Finally, we present the following conjecture, a subject for future work.

Conjecture
.

The set of roots of unity of order
d
in
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
has a cyclic descent extension if and only if
d
and
n
are not co-prime.

Chapter 2
Background

2.1
Basic notation and definitions

This section quickly reviews some notation and basic facts about permutations, partitions, and related structures.

We use
[n]
to denote the set
\left\{1,\dots,n\right\}
. Unless explicitly stated, the arithmetic on
[n]
is performed modulo
n
.
For a set
X
, the
power set of
X
, consisting of all subsets of
X
, will be denoted
2^{X}
. For sets
X
and
Y
, the set of all functions from
X
to
Y
is denoted
Y^{X}
.

2.1.1
Compositions and partitions

Definition 2.1.1
.

A
weak composition
of
n
is a sequence
\alpha=\left(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\dots\right)
of non-negative integers such that
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{k}=n
.

Definition 2.1.2
.

A
partition
of
n
is a weakly decreasing sequence of non-negative integers
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\dots)
such that
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{k}=n
. We denote
\lambda\vdash n
.

Definition 2.1.3
.

The
length
of a partition
\lambda=\left(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\dots\right)
, denoted
l(\lambda)
, is the number of non-zero parts
\lambda_{i}
.
The non-zero
\lambda_{i}
are called the
parts
of
\lambda
.

Remark 2.1.4
.

Sometimes we use the notation
\lambda=(a_{1}^{i_{1}},\dots,a_{k}^{i_{k}})

where
i_{k}
is the number of parts of
\lambda
equal to
a_{k}
.

2.1.2
Permutations

Definition 2.1.5
.

The
symmetric group
or the group of
permutations
is the group of bijections from
[n]
to itself. We denote the symmetric group
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
.

For a permutation
\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
we often write
\pi_{i}
instead of
\pi(i)
.
In some cases, we will use cycle decomposition representation, which we now describe.

Definition 2.1.6
.

A cycle
\pi=\left(i_{1}\ \dots\ i_{l}\right)
is a permutation in
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
such that
\pi(i_{k})=i_{k+1}
for all
1\leq k\leq l-1
,
\pi(i_{l})=i_{1}
and
\pi(m)=m
if
m\notin\left\{i_{1},\dots,i_{l}\right\}
. The number
l
is called the length of the cycle. We say that cycles
(i_{1},\dots,i_{l})
and
(i^{\prime}_{1},\dots,i^{\prime}_{l^{\prime}})
are
disjoint if
i_{k}\neq{j_{k^{\prime}}}
for all
1\leq k\leq l
and
1\leq k^{\prime}\leq l^{\prime}
.

Recall the basic fact from group theory.

Theorem 2.1.7
.

Every
\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
can be represented as a product of disjoint cycles

\pi=\left(a_{1}\ \dots\ a_{l_{1}}\right)\left(a_{l_{1}+1}\dots a_{l_{1}+l_{2}}\right)\dots\left(a_{l_{1}+\dots+l_{k-1}+1}\ \dots\ a_{n}\right).

Furthermore:

1.

The representation is unique, up to the order of the cycles.

2.

Two permutations
\pi,\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
with

\pi=\left(a_{1}\dots\right)\dots\left(\dots a_{n}\right)

are conjugate in
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
if and only if there is
\tau\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
such that

\sigma=\left(\tau(a_{1})\dots\right)\dots\left(\dots\tau(a_{n})\right).

In such case we have
\sigma=\tau\pi\tau^{-1}
.

Definition 2.1.8
.

The
cycle type
of a permutation

\pi=\left(a_{1}\ \dots\ a_{l_{1}}\right)\left(a_{l_{1}+1}\dots a_{l_{1}+l_{2}}\right)\dots\left(a_{l_{1}+\dots+l_{k-1}+1}\ \dots\ a_{n}\right)

is the partition of
n
obtained by writing the lengths of cycles in the cycle decomposition of
\pi
in weakly decreasing order. The cycle type of
\pi
is denoted by
\mathbf{type}\,\pi
.

Part 2 of theorem
2.1.7
is equivalent to the statement that two permutations in
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
are conjugate if and only if they have the same cycle type. The conjugacy class of all permutations
\pi
with
\mathbf{type}\,\pi=\lambda
will be denoted by
\mathcal{C}_{\lambda}
.

2.2
Representations

Representations and their characters play an essential role in understanding objects’ symmetries and studying the structures of groups. We recall the basic definitions and theorems we need from representation theory.

Definition 2.2.1
.

An
action
of a group
G
on a set
X
is a function from
G\times X
to
X
, written as
g\cdot x
or simply
gx
for all
g\in G
and
x\in X
with the following properties:

1.

g_{1}\cdot(g_{2}\cdot x)=(g_{1}g_{2})\cdot x
for all
g_{1},g_{2}\in G
and
x\in X
.

2.

For the identity element
e
of
G
we have
e\cdot x=x
for all
x\in X
.

For a vector space
V
, we often identify a linear map
T:V\to V
with its representation matrix relative to the standard basis. Let
GL(V)
denote the general linear group of invertible linear transformations from
V
to itself. For a field
\mathbb{F}
, let
GL_{n}(\mathbb{F})
denote group of invertible matrices of size
n\times n
with elements in
\mathbb{F}
. If
V
is an
n
-dimensional vector space over
\mathbb{F}
, then
GL(V)
and
GL_{n}(\mathbb{F})
are isomorphic; therefore, unless stated explicitly, no distinction will be made between them.

Definition 2.2.2
.

Let
G
be a finite group,
\mathbb{F}
a field, and
V
a vector space over
\mathbb{F}
.
A
linear representation
of
G
is a group homomorphism

\rho:G\to GL(V).

Observation 2.2.3
.

Every linear group representation
\rho:G\to GL(V)
defines an action of
G
on
V

by
g\cdot v=\rho(g)v
.

Definition 2.2.4
.

For a set
X
and field
\mathbb{F}
, the
free vector space
\mathbb{F}^{(X)}
generated by
X
over
F
is the direct sum of copies of
F
indexed by
X

\mathbb{F}^{(X)}=\bigoplus_{x\in X}\mathbb{F}

which is the subspace of the cartesian product

\prod\limits_{x\in X}F
of elements with finitely many nonzero components. It can be equivalently viewed as the vector space of formal linear combinations
\sum\limits_{x\in X}a_{x}x
with coefficients in
F
with finitely many
x\in X
with non-zero
a_{x}
. If we replace field
\mathbb{F}
in the definition with a commutative ring
R
, then
R^{(X)}

is the a
R
-module over
X
.

If the set
X
in the previous definition is the group
G
itself, then
R^{(G)}
can be given a ring structure as follows.

Definition 2.2.5
.

Let
G
be a finite group and
R
a commutative ring. Then
R\left[G\right]
, the
group ring
or the
group algebra
of
G
over
R
is the free
R
-module over
G
, together with multiplication between two elements defined as follows:

\sum_{g\in G}a_{g}g\sum_{h\in G}b_{h}h=\sum_{k\in G}\left(\sum_{gh=k}a_{g}b_{h}\right)k.

Note that for every finite group
G
and finite set
X
, if
G
acts on
X
, then this action can be extended to an invertible linear transformation from
\mathbb{F}^{(X)}
to itself by linear extension of the action, namely:

g\cdot\left(\sum_{x\in X}a_{x}x\right)=\sum_{x\in X}a_{x}(g\cdot x).

In turn, if
V
is a vector space over the field
\mathbb{F}
, then every group representation
\rho:G\to GL(V)
turns the vector space
V
into ab
\mathbb{F}[G]
module with elements of
\mathbb{F}\left[G\right]
acting on an element of
V
by

\left(\sum_{g\in G}a_{g}g\right)v=\sum_{g\in G}a_{g}\rho(g)v.

On the other hand, every module
V
over
\mathbb{F}[G]
is a vector space, and the action of
\mathbb{F}[G]
is a linear transformation. Hence, when a module over
\mathbb{F}[G]
is a finite-dimensional vector space over
\mathbb{F}
, it defines a matrix representation of
G
.

2.3
Shapes and Standard Young Tableaux

Standard Young tableaux are a classical object widely studied in many branches of mathematics. Often, they are used to describe the irreducible representations of
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
. This section provides the basic definitions that will be used later. Our exposition is mainly based on
[
38
]
and
[
44
]
, which one can refer to for a more detailed treatment of the subject.

Definition 2.3.1
.

Let
\lambda\vdash n
. The
Ferrer’s diagram
or
shape
of
\lambda
is an array
n
of cells, having
l(\lambda)
left justified rows, such that row
i
contains
\lambda_{i}
cells for
1\leq i\leq l(\lambda)
.

Remark 2.3.2
.

In this text, we use the
English notation
, where row
i
appears above the row
i+1
. Sometimes Ferrer’s diagrams are called
Young diagrams
.

The cell in row
i
and column
j
has coordinates
(i,j)
.

If
m\leq n
and
\mu\vdash m
and
\lambda\vdash n
, we denote
\mu\subseteq\lambda
if
l(\mu)\leq l(\lambda)
and
\mu_{i}\leq\lambda_{i}
for every
1\leq i\leq l(\mu)
.

Definition 2.3.3
.

Let
m\leq n
,
\mu\vdash m
,
\lambda\vdash n
. The
skew shape
of
\lambda/\mu
is an array of
n-m
cells obtained by removing the first
\mu_{i}
cells from row
i
in the shape of
\lambda
. If
m=0
, the skew shape
\lambda/\mu
is called normal or just a shape, and we write
\lambda
instead of
\lambda/\mu
.

Definition 2.3.4
.

A
Young tableau
of shape
\lambda
is an array
T
obtained by filling the cells of the Young diagram of
\lambda
by the numbers
1,\dots,n
bijectively.
A young tableau T of shape
\lambda
is also called a
\lambda
-tableau
and is denoted
T^{\lambda}
. We also write
\mathbf{sh}\,T=\lambda
.

Definition 2.3.5
.

The tableau
T
is a
standard Young tableau
if it is a Young tableau and every row read left to right and column read from top to bottom of
T
is an increasing sequence. The set of all standard Young tableaux of shape
\lambda
will be denoted
{\rm SYT}(\lambda)
. The size of
{\rm SYT}(\lambda)
will be denoted
f^{\lambda}
.

Definition 2.3.6
.

A semistandard Young tableau of shape
\lambda
is an array
T
obtained by replacing the cells of shape
\lambda
with positive integers, such that
the rows of
T
are non-decreasing sequences and the columns of
T
are increasing sequences. The set of all semistandard Young tableaux of shape
\lambda
will be denoted
{\rm SSYT}(\lambda)
.

Definition 2.3.7
.

For a standard or semistandard Young tableaux
T
, the
content
or
type
of
T
is the sequence
\mathbf{type}\,T=\mu=(\mu_{1},\dots,)
where
\mu_{i}
is equal to the number of times that
i
appears in
T
. The set of semistandard Young tableaux of shape
\lambda
and content

is denoted with
\mathcal{T}_{\lambda\mu}^{0}
. The size of
\mathcal{T}_{\lambda\mu}^{0}
is called the Kostka number
K_{\lambda\mu}
.

Remark 2.3.8
.

All the definitions involving shapes can be generalized to skew shapes by replacing
\lambda
with
\lambda/\mu
in the definition to obtain
skew Young tableaux, standard skew Young tableaux and semistandard skew Young tableaux,
f^{\lambda/\mu}
, etc.

2.4
Statistics on permutations and Young tableaux

Definition 2.4.1
.

Let
X
be a set. A
statistic
on
X
is a function
f:X\to A
where
A\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}\cup\left\{0\right\}
or
A\subseteq 2^{{\mathbb{N}}\cup\left\{0\right\}}
.

The study of statistics on finite objects has a crucial role in combinatorics. The notion of equidistribution is crucial as it often allows us to unify questions that deal with seemingly different statistics.

Definition 2.4.2
.

Let
f:X\to A
be a statistic on the set
X
and
g:Y\to A
a statistic on the set
Y
. We say that
f
and
g
are
equidistributed
if for every
a\in A
we have

\left|\{x\in X:f(x)=a\}\right|=\left|\{y\in Y:g(x)=a\}\right|.

Remark 2.4.3
.

The notions of a statistic and a distribution of a statistic are naturally extended to multisets.

In the rest of this section, we describe some of the classical statistics on
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
and their generalizations on
{\rm SYT}(n)
that will be needed later.

Definition 2.4.4
.

Let
\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
. We say that
i
is a descent of
\pi
if
\pi_{i}>\pi_{i+1}
. We denote the descent set of
\pi
by

\Des(\pi)\coloneqq\left\{i:\pi_{i}>\pi_{i+1}\right\}.

The notion of descent can be extended to other objects. For example, it is easily extended to finite sequences of integers. In Chapter
3
of this work, we study descents defined on non-crossing partitions of
[n]
.

Extension of descents on the standard Young tableaux plays a significant role in algebraic combinatorics and is defined as follows.

Definition 2.4.5
.

The descent set of a standard (skew) Young tableau
T
is

\Des(T)\coloneqq\left\{i:\text{$i+1$ appears in a lower row of $T$ than $i$}\right\}.

The Robinson-Schensted-Knuth correspondence, or RSK in short, is a bijection between permutations on
n
and ordered pairs of standard Young tableaux of the same shape. It also connects the descents of permutations and standard Young tableaux.

Theorem 2.4.6
.

[
38
, Chapter 3]

There exists a bijection

RSK:\mathfrak{S}_{n}\to\biguplus_{\lambda\vdash n}{\rm SYT}(n)\times{\rm SYT}(n)

such that if
\pi\xrightarrow{RSK}\left({P_{\pi}},{Q_{\pi}}\right)
then:

1.

\mathbf{sh}\,P_{\pi}=\mathbf{sh}\,Q_{\pi}.

2.

\pi^{-1}\xrightarrow{RSK}\left({Q_{\pi}},{P_{\pi}}\right).

3.

\Des(\pi)=\Des(Q_{\pi})
and
\Des(\pi^{-1})=\Des(P_{\pi})
.

Definition 2.4.7
.

Let
X
be a set with descent statistic
Des:X\to\left[n\right]
. For
x\in X
the
descent number
of
x
is the size of its descent set:

\des(x)\coloneqq|Des(x)|.

Inversions are similar to descents and can be seen as their generalization.

Definition 2.4.8
.

Let
\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
. We say that
(i,j)
is an inversion of
\pi
if
i<j
and
\pi_{i}>\pi_{j}
. We denote the inversion set of
\pi
with

\Inv(\pi)\coloneqq\left\{(i,j):\text{$i<j$ and $\pi_{i}>\pi_{j}$}\right\}.

The number of inversions of
\pi
is called the
inversion number
of
\pi
and is denoted with
\inv(\pi)
.

A statistic closely related to descents and inversions is the major index.

Definition 2.4.9
.

Let
\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
. The
major index
of
\pi
is defined as the sum of its descents:

\maj(\pi)\coloneqq\sum_{i\in\Des{\pi}}i.

The
inverse major index
of
\pi
is defined as the major index of its inverse.

\imaj(\pi)=\maj(\pi^{-1}).

Definition 2.4.10
.

The standard
Coxeter generators
of
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
are the transpositions
s_{i}=(i\ i+1)

for
1\leq i\leq n-1
. The minimal number of Coxeter generators required to express the permutation
\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
as a product of Coxeter generators is called the
Coxeter length
of
\pi
.

The connection between the length of a permutation and its inversion number is stated below.

Theorem 2.4.11
.

[
13
, Chapter 1]

For every
\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
,
\inv(\pi)
is equal to its Coxeter length.

2.5
Symmetric and quasi-symmetric functions

2.5.1
Symmetric functions

Let
{\mathbf{x}}=\left\{x_{n}\right\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a set of commuting indeterminates.

Symmetric and quasi-symmetric functions in
{\bf x}
can be defined over various (commutative) rings of coefficients, including the ring of integers
\mathbb{Z}
; for simplicity we define it over the field
\mathbb{Q}
of rational numbers.
For a sequence
\mu=\left\{\mu_{n}\right\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, let

{\mathbf{x}}^{\mu}=\prod_{n\in\mathbb{N}}x_{n}^{\mu_{n}}.

For formal power series
f({\mathbf{x}})=\sum\limits_{a\in\mathcal{A}}c_{a}x^{a}
,
[x^{a}]f
will denote
c_{a}
, the coefficient of
x^{\alpha}
in
f
.

Definition 2.5.1
.

A
symmetric function
in the variables
x_{1},x_{2},\ldots
is a formal power series
f({\bf x})\in\mathbb{Q}[[{\bf x}]]
, of bounded degree,
such that for any three sequences (of the same length
k
) of positive integers,
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k})
,
(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})
and
(j_{1},\dots,j_{k})
, the coefficients of
x_{i_{1}}^{a_{1}}\cdots x_{i_{k}}^{a_{k}}
and of
x_{j_{1}}^{a_{1}}\cdots x_{j_{k}}^{a_{k}}
in
f
are the same:

[x_{i_{1}}^{a_{1}}\cdots x_{i_{k}}^{a_{k}}]f=[x_{j_{1}}^{a_{1}}\cdots x_{j_{k}}^{a_{k}}]f.

A symmetric function
f
is called
homogeneous
of degree
n
if every for every
\alpha
such that
\left[x^{\alpha}\right]\neq 0
, we have
\sum\limits_{i=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{i}=n
. The vector space of homogeneous functions of degree
n
is denoted
\Lambda_{n}
.

It is easy to see, that if
f\in\Lambda_{n}
and
\left[{\mathbf{x}}^{\mu}\right]f\neq 0
,
then by definition of symmetric function,
[{\mathbf{x}}^{\lambda}]f=[{\mathbf{x}}^{\mu}]f
where
\lambda
is the partition obtained by writing the parts of
\mu
in non-increasing order.
Hence, every
f\in\Lambda_{n}
can be written as a linear combination of

m_{\lambda}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\left(x_{i_{1}},\dots,x_{l}\right)\\
\text{$i_{j}\neq i_{k}$ for $j\neq k$}\end{subarray}}x_{i_{1}}^{\lambda_{1}}\dots x_{i_{l}}^{\lambda_{l}}

where
\lambda=\left(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{l}\right)
ranges over all partitions of
n
.
It is also clear that
m_{\lambda}
are independent, therefore

{m_{\lambda}:\lambda\vdash n}

is a basis o
\Lambda_{n}
. The function
m_{\lambda}
is called
monomial symmetric function corresponding to
\lambda
.

We will work with another basis for
\Lambda_{n}
.

Definition 2.5.2
.

For a semistandard Young tableau
T
, define
x^{T}
to be
x^{\mu}
where
\mu=\mathbf{type}\,T
as in Definition
2.3.7
.

Definition 2.5.3
.

Schur functions
, indexed by partitions, are defined as follows:

s_{\lambda}=\sum_{T\in{\rm SSYT}(\lambda)}x^{T},

where
{\rm SSYT}(\lambda)
is the set of semistandard Young tableaux of shape
\lambda
, defined in Section
2.3
.

Remark 2.5.4
.

Skew Schur functions
are defined analogously by replacing partitions with skew shapes in the definition.

There is a dictionary relating symmetric functions to characters of the symmetric group
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
. The irreducible characters of
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
are indexed by partitions
\lambda\vdash n
and denoted
\chi^{\lambda}
.
The
Frobenius characteristic map

{\operatorname{ch}}
from class functions on
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
to symmetric functions is defined by
{\operatorname{ch}}(\chi^{\lambda})=s_{\lambda}
, and extended by linearity.
The Frobenius characteristic map has two properties that allow reducing the study of characters to the study of symmetric function.
First, it is an isometry between the space of class functions on
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
and
\Lambda_{n}
, where the inner product on characters is defined by
\left<\chi^{\mu},\chi^{\nu}\right>=\delta_{\mu\nu}
and the inner product on
\Lambda_{n}
is defined by
\left<s_{\mu},s_{\nu}\right>=\delta_{\mu\nu}.

The other property is that if
\chi
and
\mu
are irreducible characters for
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
and
\mathfrak{S}_{m}
respectively, then
{\operatorname{ch}}(\chi\cdot\mu)=s_{\mu}s_{\nu}

where
\chi\cdot\mu
denotes the induced character
(\chi\otimes\mu)\uparrow^{\mathfrak{S}_{n+m}}
.
A symmetric function
f
is
Schur-positive
if in the expansion of
f
in the basis of symmetric functions

f=\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}a_{\lambda}s_{\lambda}

we have
a_{\lambda}\geq 0
and
a_{\lambda}
is an integer for every
\lambda\vdash n
.

2.5.2
Quasisymmetric functions

Quasisymmetric functions allow us to reduce questions about representations and symmetric functions to the study of descents.
The following definition of a quasi-symmetric function can be found in
[
44
, 7.19]
.

Definition 2.5.5
.

A
quasi-symmetric function
in the variables
x_{1},x_{2},\ldots
is a formal power series
f({\bf x})\in\mathbb{Q}[[{\bf x}]]
, of bounded degree,
such that for any three sequences (of the same length
k
) of positive integers,
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k})
,
(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})
and
(j_{1},\dots,j_{k})
, where the last two are
increasing
, the coefficients of
x_{i_{1}}^{a_{1}}\cdots x_{i_{k}}^{a_{k}}
and of
x_{j_{1}}^{a_{1}}\cdots x_{j_{k}}^{a_{k}}
in
f
are the same:

[x_{i_{1}}^{a_{1}}\cdots x_{i_{k}}^{a_{k}}]f=[x_{j_{1}}^{a_{1}}\cdots x_{j_{k}}^{a_{k}}]f

whenever
i_{1}<\ldots<i_{k}
and
j_{1}<\ldots<j_{k}
.
The space of quasisymmetric functions is denoted
\mathcal{Q}
. A quasisymmetric function
f
is homogeneous of degree
n
if
[x^{\alpha}]f
implies that
\sum\limits_{i=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{i}=n
. The space of homogeneous quasisymmetric functions of degree
n
is denoted
\mathcal{Q}_{n}
.

Every symmetric function is quasi-symmetric but not conversely. For example, the function
\sum_{i<j}{x_{i}^{2}x_{j}}
is quasi-symmetric but not symmetric.

The
monomial quasisymmetric functions

M_{\alpha}
are defined by

M_{\alpha}=\sum_{i_{1}<\dots<i_{k}}x_{i_{1}}^{\alpha_{1}}\dots x_{i_{k}}^{\alpha_{k}}

where
\alpha
runs over compositions of
n
that do not have zero parts, viewed as finite sequences.

It is easy to see that
M_{\alpha}
is a basis of
\mathcal{Q}_{n}
. However, we will use another basis that provides a connection from descents to characters and symmetric functions.

Definition 2.5.6
.

For each subset
D\subseteq[n-1]
define the
fundamental quasi-symmetric function

\mathcal{F}_{n,D}(x):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i_{1}\leq i_{2}\leq\ldots\leq i_{n}\\
{i_{j}<i_{j+1}\text{ if }j\in D}\end{subarray}}x_{i_{1}}x_{i_{2}}\cdots x_{i_{n}}.

Definition 2.5.7
.

Let
{\mathcal{B}}
be a set of combinatorial objects, equipped with a
descent map

\Des:{\mathcal{B}}\to 2^{[n-1]}
which associates to each
element
b\in{\mathcal{B}}
a subset
\Des(b)\subseteq[n-1]
. Define the

quasi-symmetric function associated with
{\mathcal{B}}
by

\mathcal{Q}({\mathcal{B}}):=\sum\limits_{b\in{\mathcal{B}}}\mathcal{F}_{n,\Des(b)}.

Definition 2.5.8
.

We say that
{\mathcal{B}}
is a
fine set
or
Schur-positive
if
\mathcal{Q}({\mathcal{B}})
is symmetric and the coefficients
a_{\lambda}
are non-negative integers in the expansion

\mathcal{Q}({\mathcal{B}})=\sum\limits_{\lambda\vdash n}a_{\lambda}s_{\lambda}.

where
s_{\lambda}
are Schur functions.

The following fundamental theorem is due to Gessel.

Theorem 2.5.9
.

[
44
, Theorem 7.19.7]

For every shape
\lambda\vdash n
,

\mathcal{Q}({{\rm SYT}(\lambda)})=s_{\lambda}.

Remark 2.5.10
.

Skew Schur functions are defined similarly to Schur functions by replacing shape with skew shape. The result of Theorem
2.5.9
also holds for skew shapes. Namely, we have:

\mathcal{Q}({\rm SYT}(\lambda/\mu))=s_{\lambda/\mu}.

Every subset of
J={\mathbb{N}}
can be associated with the sequence
\left\{j_{n}\right\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
where

j_{n}=\begin{cases}1&n\in J\\
0&n\notin J\end{cases}.

Definition 2.5.11
.

For a subset
J\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}
, define
X^{J}=x^{\{j_{n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}}
where
\{j_{n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is the sequence
associated with
J
.

Theorem 2.5.12
.

[
3
]

For every set
\mathcal{A}
, equipped with a descent map
\Des:\mathcal{A}\to 2^{[n-1]}
, the following statements are equivalent:

\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{A})=\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}c_{\lambda}s_{\lambda}

and

\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}x^{\Des(a)}=\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}c_{\lambda}\sum_{T\in SYT(\lambda)}x^{\Des(T)}.

In particular, if
\mathcal{A}
is Schur-positive, then
c_{\lambda}\geq 0
.

Remark 2.5.13
.

The theorem in the paper is stated for subsets of
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
. However, the proof remains correct if we replace
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathfrak{S}_{n}
with
“
\mathcal{A}
equipped with a descent map”
.

The Frobenius characteristic map can be redefined in terms of quasisymmetric functions as follows:

{\operatorname{ch}}(\chi^{\lambda})=\sum_{T\in SYT(\lambda)}\mathcal{F}_{n,\Des(T)}.

We often rely on the following observation. Hence, even though it is evident, we state it explicitly.

Lemma 2.5.14
.

Let
A
and
B
disjoint sets equipped with a descent map

\Des:A\uplus B\to 2^{n-1}

for some
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Then

\mathcal{Q}(A\uplus B)=\mathcal{Q}(A)+\mathcal{Q}(B).

2.5.3
Examples of Schur-positive sets

Recall from the Subsection
2.5.1
that a symmetric function is
Schur-positive
if all the coefficients in its expansion in the basis of Schur functions are non-negative. Determining whether a given symmetric function is Schur-positive is a significant problem in contemporary algebraic combinatorics
[
45
]
.

The following problem is also long-standing; see, for example,
[
22
]
.

Problem 2.5.15
.

Given a set
A
, equipped with a descent map, is
\mathcal{Q}(A)
symmetric?
In case of an affirmative answer, is it Schur-positive?

In the remainder of the section, we describe a few classic examples of Schur-positive sets in
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
.
These sets and their generalizations are studied in more depth in Chapters
4
and
5
in perspective of cyclic descent extensions, which are defined in Section
4.1
.

Before proceeding to the mentioned above examples, we note that
Schur-positivity questions are also crucial for sets that are not subsets of
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
, as it implies the existence of a representation of
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
. We have already seen an example in the previous subsection, the set of (skew) Young tableaux. Another set - of maximal chains in the non-crossing partition lattice and its subset of convex caterpillars will be studied in Chapter
3
.

Definition 2.5.16
.

The permutations
\pi,\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
are said to be
Knuth equivalent
if
P_{\sigma}=P_{\sigma}
where
P_{\sigma}
is the first SYT in the RSK bijection.
A
Knuth class
of
T
is the set

K_{T}=\left\{\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}:P_{\pi}=T\right\}.

Schur-positivity of a Knuth class is an immediate result of theorems
2.5.9
and
2.4.6
. To see that, observe that if
\pi\xrightarrow{RSK}\left({P_{\pi}},{Q_{\pi}}\right)
then
\Des(\pi)=\Des(Q_{\pi})
and that by theorem
2.4.6
we have bijection from
K_{T}
to
SYT_{\lambda}
where
\lambda=\mathbf{sh}\,T
and therefore the descents of
K_{T}
and
SYT(\lambda)
are equally distributed. Combining with theorem
2.5.9
we obtain

\mathcal{Q}(K_{T})=\mathcal{Q}(SYT(\lambda)=s_{\lambda}.

Inverse descent classes defined below are another classic example.

Definition 2.5.17
.

Let
J\subseteq[n-1]
. The
inverse descent class
of
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
corresponding to
J
is defined by

D_{J,n}^{-1}=\left\{\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}:\Des(\pi^{-1})=J\right\}.

Schur-positivity of inverse descent classes follows from theorem
2.4.6
and the following equality:

\displaystyle D_{J,n}^{-1}

\displaystyle=\left\{\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}:\Des(\pi^{-1})=J\right\}

\displaystyle=\biguplus_{\Des{T}=J}\left\{\pi:\pi\xrightarrow{RSK}\left({P_{\pi}},{T}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle=\biguplus_{\Des{T}=J}K_{T}.

Combining with lemma
2.5.14
we obtain

\mathcal{Q}(D_{J,n}^{-1})=\mathcal{Q}(\biguplus_{\Des{T}=J}K_{T})=\sum_{\Des{T}=J}s_{\mathbf{sh}\,T}.

A classic result by Foata and Schützenberger proved in
[
19
]
(also see Section 1.4 in
[
43
]
for more accessible proof) states that there exists a bijection
\phi:\mathfrak{S}_{n}\to\mathfrak{S}_{n}
such that

1.

\maj(\pi)=\inv(\phi(\pi))
.

2.

\Des(\pi^{-1})=\Des(\phi(\pi)^{-1})
.

It immediately followsfrom Defiition
2.4.10
that Coxeter length of
\pi
and
\pi^{-1}
are equal. In addition, by Theorem
2.4.11
, the Coxeter length of a permutation
\pi
in
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
is equal to
\inv(\pi)
. Therefore, we
\inv(\pi)=\inv(\pi^{-1})
. Hence, the mapping
\phi
satisfies the following equations:

1.

\imaj(\pi)=\maj(\pi^{-1})=\inv(\phi(\pi^{-1}))=\inv(\phi(\pi^{-1})^{-1})
.

2.

\Des(\pi)=\Des(\phi(\pi^{-1})^{-1})

If we denote
\psi(\pi)=(\phi^{-1}(\pi^{-1}))^{-1}
we obtain a bijective function from
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
to
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
such that

1.

\inv(\pi)=\imaj(\psi(\pi))
.

2.

\Des(\pi)=\Des(\psi(\pi))
.

Hence the distributions of
\Des
on the sets

A=\left\{\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}:\inv(\pi)=m\right\}

and

B=\left\{\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}:\imaj(\pi)=m\right\}

are equal. By Theorem
2.5.12
, the Schur-positivity of a set
X
depends only on the distribution of
\Des
on
X
. Therefore,
A
is Schur-positive only and only if
B
is. It is obvious that
B
, the set of permutations
\pi
in
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
with
\\
imaj(\pi)=m
, can be expressed as a disjoint union of inverse descent classes and is Schur-positive. Therefore, the set of permutations with fixed length
m
is also Schur-positive.

The Schur-positive sets mentioned above will be explored in more depth in Chapter
4
.

Another important class is the set of permutations with a fixed cycle type. In
[
22
]
, it was shown that they are Schur-positive. Their generalization - the roots of unity are studied in Chapter
5
.

Chapter 3
Schur-positivity of convex caterpillars

3.1
Introduction

A symmetric function is called
Schur-positive
if all the
coefficients in its expansion in terms of Schur functions are non-negative.

With a set
A
of combinatorial objects, equipped with a
descent map

\Des:A\to 2^{[n-1]}
, one associates the
quasi-symmetric function

\mathcal{Q}(A):=\sum\limits_{\pi\in A}\mathcal{F}_{n,\Des(\pi)}

where
\mathcal{F}_{n,D}
(for
D\subseteq[n-1]
) are Gessel’s
fundamental quasi-symmetric functions;
see Subsection
2.5
for more details.

This chapter focuses on Schur-positive sets of maximal chains in partially ordered sets. Maximal chains in a labeled poset
P
are equipped with a natural descent map.
A well-known conjecture of Stanley
[
40
, III, Ch. 21]

implies that all examples of Schur-positive labeled posets, in this sense,
correspond to intervals in the Young lattice.

Another way to equip the set of maximal chains with a descent map is by the edges in the Hasse diagram.
A classic example of a Schur-positive set
of this type, the set of all maximal chains in the non-crossing partition lattice of type
A
,
was given by Stanley
[
41
]
.
An
EL
edge-labeling of this poset was presented in an earlier work of Björner
[
12
]
; see also
[
14
,
31
,
9
]
.

The goal of this chapter is to present an interesting set of maximal chains in the non-crossing partition lattice
NC_{n}

(equivalently: a set of edge-labeled trees)
which is Schur-positive.
We will use a variant of Björner’s
EL
-labeling, presented in
[
9
]
.

It is well known that
maximal chains in the non-crossing partition lattice may be interpreted as factorizations of the
n
-cycle
(1,2,\dots,n)

into a product of
n-1
transpositions.

Definition 3.1.1
.

A factorization
t_{1}\cdots t_{n-1}
of the
n
-cycle
(1,2,\dots,n)
as a product of transpositions is called
linearly ordered
if,
for every
1\leq i\leq n-2
,
t_{i}
and
t_{i+1}
have a common letter.

Example 3.1.2
.

The factorization
(1\ 4)(1\ 3)(1\ 2)
of
(1\ 2\ 3\ 4)
is linear, while
(1\ 3)(3\ 4)(1\ 2)
is not.

This definition is motivated by Theorem
3.3.1
below.
Denote the set of linearly ordered factorizations of
(1,2,\dots,n)
by
\mathcal{L}_{n}
.

Proposition 3.1.3
.

For every
n\geq 1
, the number of linearly ordered factorizations of the
n
-cycle
(1,2,\dots,n)
is

|\mathcal{L}_{n}|=n2^{n-3}.

Our main result is

Theorem 3.1.4
.

The set of linearly ordered factorizations of the

It should be noted that Theorem

We prove Theorem

Definition 3.1.5
.

.

Denote by

3.2
Background

This section provides the necessary definitions and historical background to explain the main results. More information can be found in the references.

3.2.1
Maximal chains in the non-crossing partition lattice

The systematic study of non-crossing partitions began with Kreweras
[
29
]
and Poupard
[
34
]
.
Surveys of results and connections with various areas of mathematics can be found in
[
39
]
and
[
10
]
.

A
non-crossing partition
of the set
[n]
is a partition
\pi
of
[n]
into nonempty blocks with the following property:
for every
a<b<c<d
in
[n]
, if some block
B
of
\pi
contains
a
and
c
and some block
B^{\prime}
of
\pi
contains
b
and
d
, then
B=B^{\prime}
.
Let
NC_{n}
be the set of all non-crossing partitions of
[n]
.
Define a partial order on
NC_{n}
, by refinement:

\pi\leq\sigma
if every block of
\pi
is contained in a block of
\sigma
.
This partial order turns
NC_{n}
into a graded lattice.

An
edge labeling
of a poset
P
is a function from the set of edges of the Hasse diagram of
P
to the set of integers. Several different edge labelings of
NC_{n}
were defined and studied
by Björner
[
12
]
, Stanley
[
41
]
, and Adin and Roichman
[
9
]
.
Let
\Lambda
be an edge labeling of
NC_{n+1}
,
and let
F_{n+1}
be the set of maximal chains in
NC_{n+1}
.
For each maximal chain
\mathfrak{m}:\pi_{0}<\pi_{1}<\dots<\pi_{n}
define

\Lambda^{*}(\mathfrak{m}):=\left(\Lambda(\pi_{0},\pi_{1}),\ldots,\Lambda(\pi_{n-1},\pi_{n})\right)\in{\mathbb{N}}^{n},

with a corresponding
descent set

\Des(\mathfrak{m}):=\left\{i\in[n-1]\,:\,\Lambda(\pi_{i-1},\pi_{i})>\Lambda(\pi_{i},\pi_{i+1})\right\}.

The non-crossing partition lattice is intimately related to cycle factorizations.
The
n
-cycle
(1,2,\ldots,n)
can be written as a product of
n-1
transpositions. There is a well-known bijection between such factorizations and the maximal chains in
NC_{n+1}
; see, for example,
[
30
, Lemma 4.3]
.
A classical result of Hurwitz states that the number of such factorizations is
n^{n-2}

[
26
,
46
]
,
thus equal to the number of labeled trees of order
n
.
In the next section, we will describe a connection between maximal chains and geometric trees.

is the line segment connecting vertices

and

.

Let

n

into a product of

Write each element of

The following theorem of Goulden and Yong gives the necessary and sufficient conditions for a sequence of

Theorem 3.2.1
.

[

A sequence of transpositions

if and only if the following three conditions hold:

1.

is a tree.

2.

3.

Cyclically decreasing neighbors: For every

, if

. Here,

Note that a sequence

carries more information than its Goulden-Yong tree

: It defines a

on the edges, with the edge corresponding to

preceding the edge corresponding to
t_{j}
whenever

.
How much of this information can be recovered from the tree?

Definition 3.2.2
.

Let

be a non-crossing geometric tree
(namely, satisfying conditions 1 and 2 of Theorem

)
on the set of vertices of a regular
n
-gon, labeled clockwise

Define relations

and

on the set of edges of

as follows:

1.

For edges

of

, we have

, if
s=(x,z)
and

have a common vertex

as in condition 3 of Theorem

2.

We have
(a,b)\leq_{T}(c,d)
if there exists a sequence of edges
(a,b)=t_{0},\ldots,t_{k}=(c,d)

(k\geq 0)
such that for every
0\leq i\leq k-1

and

Lemma 3.2.3
.

Let

be a non-crossing geometric tree. Then

is a partial order on the set of edges of

.

Proof.

One needs to show that
\leq_{T}
is reflexive, transitive and antisymmetric relation on the edges of

. Observe that
\leq_{T}
is reflexive, since for every edge
t
of
T
, the sequence
)
trivially satisfies the conditions of
\leq_{T}
as in Definition

, hence

holds. Next, observe that if
s\leq_{T}t
and
t\leq_{T}u
, then by definition of
\leq_{T}
there exist sequences
t=t_{0},t_{1},\dots,t_{k}=s
,
s=s_{0}<\dots s_{l}=u
such that
t_{i}\prec_{T}t_{i+1}
for every
0\leq i\leq k-1
and
s_{j}\prec_{T}s_{j+1}
for every
0\leq j\leq l-1
.
Then the sequence
t=t_{0},\dots,t_{k}=s=s_{0},s_{1},\dots,s_{l}=u

is the sequence that satisfies the definition of
\leq_{T}
for
t
and
u
.

Before showing that
\leq_{T}
is antisymmetric, note that
\prec_{T}
is a strict order on any subset of edges of
T
that share a common vertex. In addition,
\prec_{T}
is an amtisymmetric relation as well. Let
e
and
f
edges in
T
such that
e\leq_{T}f
.
By definition of
\leq_{T}
there exists a sequence
e=t_{0},\dots,t_{k}=f
such that
t_{i}\prec_{T}t_{i+1}
for every
0\leq i\leq k-1
such that
t_{i}\prec_{T}t_{i+1}
. Therefore, if we replace every subsequence
t_{1},\dots,t_{k}
of form
(v,u_{m}),\dots,(v,u_{m+l})
with
(v,u_{m}),(v,u_{m+l})
, we obtain a subsequence
s_{1},\dots,s_{k^{\prime}}
of
t_{0},\dots,t_{k}
with the same property
s_{i}\prec_{T}s_{i+1}
for every
0\leq i\leq k^{\prime}-1
, every two consecutive edges share a common vertex, and for every vertex there are at most two edges in the sequence
s_{0},\dots,s_{k^{\prime}}
that have it as an endpoint. If we denote
e=(a,u_{1})
,
s_{i}=(u_{i},u_{i+1})
for
1\leq i\leq k^{\prime}-1
,
f=(u_{k^{\prime}-1},b)
the sequence of vertices

is a path from

to
b
in the tree

. One the other hand, if
f\leq_{T}e

f=s^{\prime}_{1},\dots,s^{\prime}_{k^{\prime\prime}}
with the same properties as

. Since there is exactly one path between two vertices in a tree, we have

and that
(s_{0},\dots,s_{k^{\prime}})=(s^{\prime}_{k^{\prime}\prime},\dots,s^{\prime}_{9}).
Hence, we have both

for every

, which is a contradiction, unless

\leq_{T}
prime is antisymmetric.
We have shown that
\leq_{T}
is reflexive, transitive and antisymmetric. Therefore, we are done.
∎

We call

3.3
Convex caterpillars

In this section, we prove Theorem
3.1.4
using the properties of convex caterpillars.

3.3.1
Basic properties of convex caterpillars

Let us use the following conventions.
All arithmetical operations on the elements of
[n]
will be performed modulo
n
. We write
[a,b]
to denote the cyclic interval
\{a,a+1,\dots,b\}
. Using this notation, for the edges
(a\ b),(a\ c)
of a geometric non-crossing tree
T
, we have
(a\ c)<_{T}(a\ b)
if and only if
b\in[a,c]
.

The following result was proved in
[
27
]
. Later in this section, we provide a somewhat different proof, details of which will be used later.

Theorem 3.3.1
.

[
27
, Theorem 3.2]

The Goulden-Yong order on the edge set of a non-crossing geometric tree
T
is linear (total) if and only if
T
is a convex caterpillar.

The following observation follows from the fact that a linear extension of a Goulden-Yong order
<_{T}
on the edges of
the geometric non-crossing tree
T
corresponds to a factorization of the cycle

(1\dots n)
into
n-1
transpositions.

Observation 3.3.2
.

If
T
is a geometric non-crossing tree and
<_{T}
is linear, then any two consecutive edges in
<_{T}
, viewed as transpositions in
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
, do not commute and therefore have a common vertex.

The following lemma gives sufficient conditions for
<_{T}
to be non-linear.

Lemma 3.3.3
.

Let
T
be a non-crossing geometric tree. The order
<_{T}
is not linear in the following cases.

1.

There are edges
(a\ b),(c\ d),(e\ f)
of
T
such that
(a\ b)<_{T}(c\ d),(e\ f)
and

c,d\in[a,b-1]
and
e,f\in[b,a-1]
.

2.

T
has edges
(a\ b),(c\ d),(e\ f)
such that

(c\ d),(e\ f)<(a\ b)
and
c,d\in[b+1,a]
and
e,f\in[a+1,b]
.

Proof.

We prove the first case, the second one being similar by reversing directions.
Assume that
<_{T}
is linear and that the first case holds. For every
v\in[a+1,b-1]
, the edge
(v\ b)
is smaller than
(a\ b)
in
<_{T}
. The same is true for any edge
(a\ v)
with
v\in[b+1,a-1]
.
Combining this fact with the non-crossing property of
T
, we deduce that the endpoints of any edge that is larger than
(a\ b)
in
<_{T}
are either both in
[a,b-1]
or both in
[b,a-1]
. On the other hand, by Observation
3.3.2
, every pair of consecutive edges shares a common vertex. Therefore, the endpoints of any two consecutive edges greater than
(a\ b)
in
<_{T}
must all be in either
[a,b-1]
or
[b,a-1]
. Using induction, we conclude that the endpoints of the edges greater than
(a\ b)
in
<_{T}
are all in
[a,b-1]
or all in
[b,a-1]
, contradicting the assumption.
∎

We are ready to prove Theorem
3.3.1
.

Proof of Theorem
3.3.1
.

Assume that
T
is a convex caterpillar. If the spine of
T
does not contain any edges, then
T
is a star; hence, every pair of edges is comparable because they have a common vertex, and the Goulden-Yong order is linear.

Otherwise, let
(a\ a+1),(a+1\ a+2)\dots,(b-2\ b-1),(b-1\ b)
be the spine of
T
. For every pair of edges
(k\ l)
and
(k\ m)
that share a common vertex
k
,
(k\ l)<_{T}(k\ m)
holds if and only if
m\in[k+1,l-1]
where
[k+1,l-1]
denotes the cyclic interval
\{k+1,\dots,l-1\}
. Note that it is simply a restatement of the fact that the neighbors of
k
are ordered counterclockwise.
Therefore, all the edges of the spine are comparable and
(a\ a+1)<_{T}(a+1\ a+2)\dots<_{T}(b-1\ b)
. It also implies that if
(k\ k+1)
is an edge in the spine, then for every edge
(k\ l)
that has
k
as an endpoint,
(k\ l)\leq_{T}(k\ k+1)
and for every edge
(k+1\ m)
that has
k+1
as an endpoint, we have
(k\ k+1)\leq_{T}(k+1\ m)
. It follows that if the edges
(k\ k+1),(k+1\ k+2)\dots,(m-1\ m)
are in the spine of
T
, then for every edge
(k\ j)
connected to
k
and every edge
(m\ l)
connected to
m
we have
(k\ j)\leq_{T}(k\ k+1)<_{T}\dots<_{T}(m-1\ m)\dots\leq_{T}(m\ l)
. Any edge in the caterpillar is either in the spine or has a common vertex with an edge in the spine. Therefore, any two edges are comparable.

To prove the converse statement, assume that
<_{T}
is linear. Enumerate the edges such that
(a_{1}\ b_{1})<_{T}\dots<_{T}(a_{n-1}\ b_{n-1})
. Let
t_{l}
denote the
l
-th edge in
<_{T}
, namely
(a_{l}\ b_{l})
.

By Observation
3.3.2
, since
<_{T}
is linear, every pair of adjacent edges shares a common vertex.
Now we claim that the first edge must be of the form
(i\ i+1)
for some
i
. To see that, denote
t_{1}=(i\ j)
. By the proof of Lemma
3.3.3
the endpoints of every other edge of
T
, which is necessarily larger that
t_{1}
in the linear order, must be either both in
[i,j-1]
or both in
[j,i-1]
. By Lemma
3.3.3
, either there are no edges with endpoints in
[i,j-1]
or no edges with endpoints in
[j,i-1]
, which implies that either
[i,j]
or
[j,i]
consists of precisely two points, which means that either
i=j+1
or
j=i+1
holds.

Next, observe that
i
must be a leaf since the existence of an edge
(i\ j)<_{T}(i\ i+1)
, contradicts the fact that
(i\ i+1)
is the first edge in
<_{T}
.
Next, we use induction to show that for every
1\leq m\leq n-1
, the following statements hold:

1.

The endpoints of the first

<_{T}
form a cyclic interval
[j,k]
in
[n]
.

2.

Vertices

are leaves in
T
.

3.

Edges
(i\ i+1),(i+1\ i+2),\dots(k-1\ k)
are edges in
T
and occur among the first
m
edges.

4.

Every edge that has at least one of

as an endpoint occurs among the first
m
edges.

5.

Either
t_{m}
=
(k-1\ k)
or
t_{m}=(k\ j)
is true.

As we have shown,
t_{1}=(i\ i+1)
for some
1\leq i\leq n
and
i
is a leaf. Therefore, the statements hold for
m=1
. Assume that the statement holds for
m
. According to the induction hypothesis, we have either
t_{m}=(k\ j)
or
t_{m}=(k-1\ k)
. From the linearity of

,
t_{m+1}
and

have a common vertex. Again, by the induction hypothesis

and

cannot be endpoints of

, therefore,

must have

as an endpoint.
Next, we show that

is either
(k\ k+1)
or
(k\ j-1)
. Assume
t_{m+1}=(k\ l)
for
l\neq j-1,k+1
. There must be an edge (necessarily not among the first

, and similarly an edge with endpoint

. Therefore we must have edges

with

and

.
Now if

, we are done, since statements

and

hold by the induction hypothesis for

and

hold for

, and

If

, then for every

then

according to the induction assumption.
On the other hand, for every

Again, it is easy to check that assumptions

Now, for

, we see that

Corollary 3.3.4
.

A non-crossing geometric tree

such that

.

We can view each edge of

Chapter 4
Cyclic descent extensions

4.1
Introduction

This chapter and the next study
cyclic descent extensions
.
We will build upon recent work by Adin, Hegedüs and Roichman
[
3
]
.

Recall that the
descent set
of a permutation
\pi=\left[\pi_{1},\dots,\pi_{n}\right]\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
is

\Des(\pi)\coloneqq\left\{1\leq i\leq n-1:\pi_{i}>\pi_{i+1}\right\}\subseteq\left[n-1\right].

Its
cyclic descent set
was defined by Cellini in
[
15

as

\CDes(\pi)\coloneqq\left\{1\leq i\leq n:\pi_{i}>\pi_{i+1}\right\}\subseteq\left[n\right],

with the convention that
\pi_{n+1}\coloneqq\pi_{1}
.

Definition 4.1.1
.

The
cyclic shift
of a set is the bijection
{\rm sh}:2^{[n]}\longrightarrow 2^{[n]}
defined by

\mathrm{sh}(P)\coloneqq\left\{i+1\pmod{n}:i\in P\right\}.

Define also the
cyclic shift
of a permutation
\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
by

\rho(\pi)=\left[\pi_{n},\pi_{1},\dots,\pi_{n-1}\right].

Observation 4.1.2
.

\CDes
together with
\rho
satisfy the following 3 conditions.

1.

\CDes(\pi)\cap\left[n\right]=\Des(\pi
).

2.

\CDes\left(\rho\left(\pi\right)\right)=\rm{sh}\left(\CDes\left(\pi\right)\right)
.

3.

For all
\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
,
\emptyset\subsetneq\CDes(\pi)\subsetneq\left[n\right]
.

We are interested in generalizing this situation as follows.

Definition 4.1.3
.

[
6
]

Let
\mathcal{T}
be a finite set, equipped with a
descent map

\Des:\mathcal{T}\longrightarrow 2^{[n-1]}
.
A
cyclic extension
of
\Des
is
a pair
(\cDes,p)
, where

\cDes:\mathcal{T}\longrightarrow 2^{[n]}
is a map
and
p:\mathcal{T}\longrightarrow\mathcal{T}
is a bijection,
satisfying the following axioms: for all
T
in
\mathcal{T}
,

\begin{array}[]{rl}\text{(extension)}&\cDes(T)\cap[n-1]=\Des(T),\\
\text{(equivariance)}&\cDes(p(T))={\rm sh}(\cDes(T)),\\
\text{(non-Escher)}&\varnothing\subsetneq\cDes(T)\subsetneq[n].\end{array}

The term non-Escher refers to M. C. Escher’s drawing “Ascending and Descending”
[
48
]
, which illustrates the impossibility
of the cases
\cDes(\cdot)=\varnothing
and
\cDes(\cdot)=[n]
for permutations in
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
.

As stated earlier, Cellini’s
\CDes
(with
p=\rho
) is a cyclic extension of
\Des
on
\mathcal{T}=\mathfrak{S}_{n}
.

Before we proceed, let us stress several properties of cyclic descent extensions.

Example 4.1.4

Consider the following subset of

4.2
Inverse descent classes and their cyclic descent extensions

We restate Definition

Definition 4.2.1

Let
J\subseteq[n-1]
. Define the
inverse descent class
of
J
in
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
by

D_{J,n}^{-1}=\left\{\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}|Des(\pi^{-1})=J\right\}.

We are also interested in a generalization of inverse descent classes.

Definition 4.2.2
.

Let
\mathcal{J}\subseteq 2^{[n]}
.
Define
generalized inverse descent class

D_{\mathcal{J},n}^{-1}=\left\{\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}|\Des(\pi^{-1})\in\mathcal{J}\right\}.

or equivalently

D_{\mathcal{J},n}^{-1}=\biguplus_{J\in\mathcal{J}}D_{J,n}^{-1}.

It is easy to see that generalized inverse decent classes are Schur-positive since they are disjoint unions of Schur-positive sets.
We are interested in determining the conditions for generalized inverse descent classes to have cyclic descent extensions.

To answer this question, we first calculate

H_{D^{-1}_{J,n}}(x)
for
J\subseteq[n]
.
By discussion after Definition
2.5.17
,
the inverse descent class
D_{J,n}^{-1}
is the disjoint union of the Knuth classes for standard Young tableaux
T
that have
\Des{T}=J
. Namely, we have

D_{J,n}^{-1}=\biguplus_{\Des{T}=J}K_{T}.

Now, observe that
Des
is a bijection between the hooks with leg length
k
and the subsets of
[n-1]
of size
k
.
Hence, there is exactly one hook
T
with
\Des(T)=J
, and it has of shape
(n-|J|,1^{|J|})
.
Therefore we have
h_{|J|}=1
and
h_{k}=0
for
k\neq|J|
.
This result is summarized in the following lemma.

Lemma 4.2.3
.

Let
J\subseteq[n]
.

h_{k}(D^{-1}_{J,n})=\delta_{k,|J|}.

where
\delta
is the Kronecker delta.
Equivalently,

H_{D_{J,n}^{-1}}(x)=x^{|J|}
.

Since
1+x
does not divide
x^{k}
, we immediately obtain the following result by lemma
4.1.14
.

Theorem 4.2.4
.

For every
J\subseteq[n-1]
, no cyclic descent extension exists for
D_{J,n}^{-1}
.

For the generalized inverse descent class, we obtain the following result:

Lemma 4.2.5
.

Let
\mathcal{J}\in 2^{n-1}
. Let
j_{k}=\big|\left\{J\in\mathcal{J}\mid|J|=k\right\}\big|
. Then a cyclic descent extension for
D^{-1}_{\mathcal{J},n}
exists if and only if

\frac{\sum\limits_{k=0}^{n-1}j_{k}x^{k}}{1+x}

is a polynomial with non-negative integer coefficients.

Proof.

Recall that
D_{\mathcal{J},n}^{-1}=\biguplus_{J\in\mathcal{J}}D_{J,n}^{-1}
.
By lemma
4.1.15
we have

\begin{split}H_{D_{\mathcal{J},n}^{-1}}(x)=H_{\bigcup\limits_{J\in\mathcal{J}}D_{J,n}^{-1}}(x)=\sum\limits_{J\in\mathcal{J}}H_{J}(x)=\sum\limits_{J\in\mathcal{J}}x^{|J|}\\
=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}J\in\mathcal{J}\\
|J|=k\end{subarray}}x^{k}=\sum\limits_{k=0}^{n-1}j_{k}x^{k}\end{split}.

and by lemma
4.1.14
has a cyclic descent extension only if
\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}j_{k}x^{k}
is divisible by
1+x
and the quotient polynomial has non-negative integer coefficients, as desired.
∎

Applying the theorem, we obtain the following result.

Theorem 4.2.6
.

The following sets in
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
have a cyclic descent extension under the following conditions

1.

D_{2^{J},n}^{-1}
if and only if
J\neq\emptyset
.

2.

D_{[I,J],n}^{-1}
for an interval

[I,J]=\left\{K:I\subseteq K\ and\ K\subseteq J\right\}

(in the poset
2^{[n-1]}
ordered by inclusion),
if and only if
I\subsetneq J
.

3.

For a maximal chain
C
in the poset
[I,J]
,
D_{C,n}^{-1}
has a cyclic descent extension if and only
2\not|(|J|-|I|)
.
In particular,
D_{C,n}^{-1}
for a maximal chain
C
in
2^{[n-1]}
has a cyclic descent extension if and only if
n-1
is odd, or equivalently
n
is even.

Proof.

1.

The number of subsets of
J
of size
k
is
j_{k}=\binom{|J|}{k}
. Hence, we have

\sum_{k=0}^{|J|}j_{k}x^{k}=\sum_{k=0}^{|J|}\binom{|J|}{k}x^{k}=(1+x)^{|J|}

which is divisible by
1+x
unless
|J|=0
. The quotient

\frac{(1+x)^{|J|}}{1+x}=(1+x)^{|J-1|}

is clearly a polynomial with non-negative integer coefficients. Hence
D_{2^{J},n}^{-1}

has a cyclic descent extension unless
J=\emptyset
, as desired.

2.

The number of subsets
D^{-1}_{[I,J],n}
of size
k
is
\binom{|J|-|I|}{k-|I|}.
Thus

\sum_{k}j_{k}x^{k}=x^{|I|}(1+x)^{|J|-|I|}

.

3.

For a maximal chain
C
in
[I,J]
, we have

\sum_{k}j_{k}x^{k}=\sum_{k=|I|}^{k=|J|}x^{k}

which is divisible by
1+x
with quotient having non-negative integer coefficients when
|J|-|I|
is odd, and is not divisible by
1+x
otherwise.

∎

4.3
Permutations with given inversion number

It is known that the set of permutations in
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
with a given inversion number is Schur-positive. See for example
[

Hence, it is natural to ask the following question.

Question 4.3.1

Is there a cyclic descent extension for the set of permutations in
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
with a given inversion number

In this section, we give an answer to this question for case

Theorem

(
4.3.11
)
.

For
k<n
, the set of permutations in
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
with
k
inversions has a cyclic descent extension if and only if
k
is not a generalized pentagonal number.

Recall the definition of generalized pentagonal numbers.

Definition 4.3.2

A number

is a

if there is
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
n=\frac{(3m\pm 1)m}{2}
.

The rest of this section is devoted to proving this theorem. First, we introduce some notation and definitions. Let
\inv(\pi)
denote the number of inversions
\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
.Recall that by Definition
2.4.9
the
major index
of a permutation
\pi
denoted
\maj(\pi)
is the sum of descents, namely

{\maj(\pi)=\sum\limits_{i\in Des(\pi)}i}

and the
inverse major index
of
\pi
, denoted
\imaj(\pi)
is the major index of
\pi^{-1}
, specifically,
{\imaj(\pi)=\sum\limits_{i\in Des(\pi^{-1})}i}
. We define the following sets.

Definition 4.3.3
.

Let
k\in{\mathbb{N}}\cup\{0\}.
Denote
\inv_{k}
the set of permutations in
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
with inversion number
k
, that is

\inv_{k}(\mathfrak{S}_{n})=\{\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}:inv(\pi)-k\}.

The set of permutations in
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
with inverse major index
k
is denoted
\imaj_{k}(\mathfrak{S}_{n})
, namely

\imaj_{k}(\mathfrak{S}_{n})=\{\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}:\imaj(\pi)=k\}.

Before going into detail, let us describe the structure of the proof.
First, using a classical result by Foata and Schützenberger from
[
19
]
, we show that the descents of
\inv_{k}(\mathfrak{S}_{n})
and
\imaj_{k}(\mathfrak{S}_{n})
are equidistributed, which implies that that
\inv_{k}(\mathfrak{S}_{n})
admits a cyclic descent extension if and only if
\imaj_{k}(\mathfrak{S}_{n})
does.

However,
\imaj_{k}(\mathfrak{S}_{n})
can be expressed as a disjoint union of inverse descent classes, which allows us easily to calculate
H_{\inv_{k}(\mathfrak{S}_{n}}(x)
, the hook polynomial of
\inv_{k}(\mathfrak{S}_{n})
.

Then we check when the conditions of Lemma
4.1.14
hold for
H_{\imaj_{k}(\mathfrak{S}_{n})}(x)
. We show then, that divisibility by
(1+x)
for
n>k
, is equivalent to coefficients of
x^{k}
being
0
in the expansion

\prod_{0<n}(1-x^{n})=\sum_{0\leq k}a_{k}x^{k},

which, by the famous Euler’s pentagonal number theorem, implies that
k
is not a generalized pentagonal number.

Using the same tools used in the proof of Euler’s pentagonal number theorem, we show the second condition of Lemma
4.1.14
for
H_{inv_{k}(\mathfrak{S}_{n})}

(1+x)
divides
H_{\imaj_{k}(\mathfrak{S}_{n})}
still holds. That is, the coefficients
\frac{H_{\inv_{k}(\mathfrak{S}_{n})(x)}}{1+x}
non-negative which finishes our proof of Theorem
4.3.11
.

We proceed to carry out the details of the proof.

We begin citing the classical result of Foata and Schützenberger from
[
19
]
.

Theorem 4.3.4

(
[
19
]
, Theorem 1)
.

There exists a bijection
\phi:\mathfrak{S}_{n}\to\mathfrak{S}_{n}
such that

\Des(\phi(\pi)^{-1})=\Des(\pi^{-1})

and

\inv(\phi(\pi))=\maj(\pi).

Recall that by Theorem
2.4.11
, the inversion number of
\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
is equal to its length in Coxeter generators
s_{1},\dots,s_{n-1}
and therefore
\inv(\pi)=\inv(\pi^{-1})
. Using this fact, we show that a bijection exists with similar relations for
\imaj
and
\inv
.

Corollary 4.3.5
.

There exists a bijection
\psi:\mathfrak{S}_{n}\to\mathfrak{S}_{n}
such that

\Des(\psi(\tau))=\Des(\tau)

and

\inv(\psi(\tau))=\imaj(\tau).

Proof.

Let us define
\psi:\mathfrak{S}_{n}\to\mathfrak{S}_{n}
by

\psi(\tau)=\phi(\tau^{-1})^{-1}.

By the preceding theorem, we have

\Des\left(\psi(\tau)\right)=\Des\left(\phi(\tau^{-1})^{-1}\right)=\Des(\left(\tau^{-1}\right)^{-1})=\Des(\tau)

and

\inv(\psi(\tau))=\inv\left(\phi(\tau^{-1})^{-1}\right)=\inv\left(\phi(\tau^{-1})\right)=\maj\left(\tau^{-1}\right)=\imaj(\tau),

which proves our claim.
∎

For our purposes, it is more useful to restate it in the following form.

The sets

It is easy to see that

\begin{split}H_{\inv_{k}(\mathfrak{S}_{n})}(x)=H_{\imaj_{k}(\mathfrak{S}_{n})}(x)=\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}J\subseteq[n-1]\\

Lemma 4.3.8

Let

such that

in the expansion

Proof.

Let us examine the series

\begin{split}\sum_{0\leq k}Pd_{k}(t)x^{k}&=\sum_{0\leq k}x^{k}\sum_{m=0}^{k}pd(k,m)t^{m}=\sum_{0\leq m}\sum_{m\leq k}pd(k,m)t^{m}x^{k}\\
&=\sum_{m\leq 0}t^{m}\sum_{m\leq k}\left(\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq i_{1}<\dots<i_{m}\\
i_{1}+\dots+i_{m}=k\end{subarray}}\prod_{j=1}^{m}x^{i_{j}}\right)\\
&=\sum_{0\leq m}\left(\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<\dots<i_{m}}\prod_{j=1}^{m}tx^{i_{j}}\right)=\prod_{1\leq n}(1+tx^{n}).\end{split}

Substituting

Since

,

By Lemma

then

.
But

The following result, known as the pentagonal number theorem, is due to Euler.

Theorem 4.3.9

(Pentagonal number theorem)
.

We have

\prod\limits_{1\leq n}(1-x^{n})=1+\sum\limits_{1\leq m}(-1)^{m}\left(x^{\frac{(3m-1)m}{2}}+x^{\frac{(3m+1)m}{2}}\right)

Some notation and arguments from the proof of this theorem, as in
[
43
, Proposition 1.8.7]
, are used later. Therefore, we provide a slightly modified version here to ease comprehension.

Proof.

Let
Q(k)
be the set of partitions of

into distinct parts. Let

be the number of partitions of
k
into an even number of distinct parts, and let
q_{o}(k)
be the number of partitions of
k
into an odd number of distinct parts. From the proof of Lemma
4.3.8
, we have
a_{k}=Pd_{k}(-1)
in the expansion

\prod_{1\leq n}(1-x^{n})=\sum_{0\leq k}a_{k}x^{k}.

On the other hand,

Pd_{k}(-1)=\sum_{m=1}^{k}pd(k,m)(-1)^{m}==q_{e}(k)-q_{o}(k)=\sum_{\lambda\in Q(k)}(-1)^{l(\lambda)}.

Thus, to prove the theorem, it is enough to show that

a_{k}=q_{e}(k)-q_{o}(k)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}(-1)^{m}&\text{if}\ k=\frac{(3m\pm 1)m}{2}\text{\ }{forsome}\ m\in{\mathbb{N}}\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

It is easy to see that

. Therefore the following arguments assume that

.
To prove the statement

by defining an involution

For a partition

denote the length of the last row of the Ferrers diagram of

the number of rows

.
For example for

.

If

\lambda_{i}&\text{if}\ D_{\lambda}+1\leq i\leq l(\lambda)\\
D_{\lambda}&\text{if}\ i=l(\lambda)+1.\end{array}\right.

If
D_{\lambda}\geq L_{\lambda}
. Define

\phi(\lambda)_{i}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\lambda_{i}+1&\text{if}\ 1\leq i\leq L_{\lambda}\\
\lambda_{i}&\text{if}\ L_{\lambda}+1\leq i\leq l(\lambda)-1.\end{array}\right.

When

then

unless

. In this case, we have
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{1}-1,\dots,\lambda_{1}-l(\lambda)+1=l(\lambda)+1)
and
\phi(\lambda)=(\lambda_{1}-1,\dots,l(\lambda),l(\lambda))
, and since two smallest parts of

for

.

When

then
\phi(\lambda)\in Q(k)
, unless
{L_{\lambda}=D_{\lambda}=l_{\lambda}}
. In this case, we have
{\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{1}-1,\dots,\lambda_{1}-l(\lambda)+1=l(\lambda))}

By the definition of

{l(\phi(\lambda))=l(\lambda)\pm 1}

some

Therefore, for

∎

Now we proceed to show that if

such that

Using the involution

Theorem 4.3.11
.

For
k<n
, the set of permutations in
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
with
k

is not a generalized pentagonal number.

Proof.

First, observe that if

is a generalized pentagonal number, then

by Euler’s Pentagonal Number Theorem, which implies that

does not divide

.

For the opposite direction, we show that if

is not a generalized pentagonal number, then

is a polynomial with integer non-negative coefficients.
Let

be the degree of

, which is equal to

\max\{l(\lambda)|\lambda\in Q(k)\}
.
Denote

and

b_{m}=\big|\{\lambda\in Q(k)|l(\phi(\lambda))=l(\lambda)+1\}\big|.

Observe that
a_{1}=b_{1}
, as there is exactly one partition
\lambda=(k)
of

.

Also, we must have
b_{d}=0
as

contradicts the fact that
d
is the maximal possible length of an element
\lambda
of
Q(k)
.

Since we have
l(\phi(\lambda))=l(\lambda)\pm 1
, and
\phi(\phi(\lambda))=\lambda
, the number of elements
\lambda
of
Q(k)
with

for which

with length

which is exactly

with length

Combining everything we have

Multiplying

It is easy to see that the coefficients

are not negative. By Corollary

.

The set
\mathcal{R}_{2}{\left(\mathfrak{S}_{n}\right)}
has a cyclic descent extension if and only if
n
is even.

Proof.

Recall that
RSK
is a bijective function between permutations in
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
and pairs of Standard Young Tableaux
(P,Q)
of the same shape, such that if
RSK(\pi)=(P,Q)
then
RSK(\pi^{-1})=(Q,P)
and
\Des(\pi)=\Des(Q)
. Since,
\pi
is an involution if and only if
\pi^{-1}=\pi
, we have from the properties of
RSK
that
\pi
is an involution if and only if
RSK(\pi)=(P,P)
for some
P
. Hence, we have
Des(\mathcal{R}_{2}{\left(\mathfrak{S}_{n}\right)})=Des(SYT(n))
as multisets. Therefore,

\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{R}_{2}{\left(\mathfrak{S}_{n}\right)})=\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}s_{\lambda},

which obviously implies that
s_{(n-k,1^{k})}
appears exactly once in
\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{R}_{2}{\left(\mathfrak{S}_{d}\right)})
. Hence we have,

H_{\mathcal{R}_{2}{\left(\mathfrak{S}_{n}\right)}}(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}x^{i}

which is divisible by
(1+x)
if and only if
n
is even, as claimed.
∎

5.3
Reduction to unimodal permutations

In this section, we show that for a Schur-positive subset
A
of
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
, the study of hook multiplicities in
\mathcal{Q}(A)
can be reduced to the study of multiplicities of unimodal permutations in
A
.
First, we prove a slightly different form of lemma
4.1.12
.

Lemma 5.3.1
.

Let
A
be a Schur-positive set with
\mathcal{Q}(A)={\operatorname{ch}}(\phi)
and
h_{k}
defined as in lemma
4.1.12
. Then

h_{k}=\big|\bigl\{a\in A:\Des(a)=\left\{n-k,\dots,n-1\right\}\bigr\}\big|.

Proof.

Let
T\in{\rm SYT}(n-k,1^{k})
be the hook with

in the first column.

\Des(T)=\{n-k,\dots,n-1\}.
.
Hence,

h_{k}\leq\big|\bigl\{a\in A:\Des(a)=\left\{n-k,\dots,n-1\right\}\bigr\}\big|.

Now, if

\Des(S)=\left\{n-k,\dots,n-1\right\}
for some standard Young tableau
S
, then the numbers
1,\dots,n-k
must appear in the first row, since
i+1
appearing south of
i
would imply that
i\in\Des(S)
.
Now,
n-k\in\Des(S)
implies that
n-k+1
appears south of
n-k
and can not appear in the first row. On the other hand, it must appear in position
(2,1)
; otherwise, there must be a number larger than
n-k+1
, which appears west or north of
n-k
, contradicting
S
being a standard Young tableau. By induction,
n-k+j
appears in position
(j+1,1)
for every
1\leq j\leq k
. Hence, we must have
S=T
. Hence, there exists a unique standard Young tableau
T
with

Des(T)=\left\{n-k,\dots,n-1\right\}
.
Since
A
is Schur-positive we have by Theorem
2.5.12

\sum_{a\in A}x^{\Des(A)}=\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}c_{\lambda}\sum_{T\in{\rm SYT}(\lambda)}x^{\Des(T)},

where
c_{\lambda}
is the multiplicity of
s_{\lambda}
in
\mathcal{Q}(A)
.
The desired result follows by comparing coefficients of
x^{\left\{n-k,\dots,n-1\right\}}
on both sides of the equation.
∎

For a permutation
\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
, the condition

\Des(\pi)=\left\{n-k,\dots,n-1\right\}

implies that
\pi_{i}<\pi_{i+1}
for
1<i<n-k
and
\pi_{i}>\pi_{i+1}
for
i\geq n-k
. Namely, if we view
\pi
as a finite sequence, it is an ascending sequence up to the position
n-k
and a descending sequence after. Recall that such sequences are called
unimodal.
In particular, the last lemma can be restated for permutations in the following form.

Lemma 5.3.2
.

Let
A
be a Schur-positive subset of
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
. Then,
h_{k}
equals the number of unimodal permutations in
A
that have the maximum at
n-k
.

Therefore, the calculation of
h_{k}(\mathcal{R}_{d}{\left(\mathfrak{S}_{n}\right)})
reduces to counting the number of unimodal permutations that have the maximum at
n-k
.

Definition 5.3.3
.

For a subset
A
of
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
, let
u_{k}(A)
denote the number of unimodal permutations in
A
that have the maximum at
k
. Define the
unimodal maxima polynomial
of
A
by

U_{A}(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}u_{k}(A)x^{k}.

The following lemma allows us to work with
U_{A}(x)
instead of
H_{A}(x)
to check an existence of a cyclic descent extension for a Schur-positive set
A
, as in Lemma
4.1.14

Lemma 5.3.4
.

Let
A
be a Schur-positive subset of
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
with unimodal maxima and hook polynomials
U_{A}(x)
and
H_{A}(x)
respectively. Then
U_{A}(x)
is divisible by
1+x
with the quotient having non-negative coefficients if and only if
H_{A}(x)
is.

Proof.

By Lemma
5.3.2
, we have
h_{k}(A)=u_{n-k}(A)
,
which implies the equality
U_{A}(x)=x^{n}H_{A}(\frac{1}{x})
. If

H_{A}(x)=(1+x)(a_{0}+\dots a_{n-1}x^{n-1}),

then

\begin{split}U_{A}(x)&=x^{n}(1+\frac{1}{x})(a_{0}+\dots a_{n-1}\frac{1}{x^{n-1}})\\
&=(1+x)(a_{0}x^{n-1}+\dots a_{n-1})\end{split}

Hence, divisibility by
1+x
of
H_{A}(x)
with quotient coefficients being non-negative implies the same for
U_{A}(x)
. The converse follows similarly.
∎

Corollary 5.3.5
.

A Schur-positive subset
A
of
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
has a cyclic descent extension if and only if
U_{A}(x)
is divisible by
1+x
with coefficients of the quotient being non-negative integers.

5.4
The case of prime
n

In this section, we prove the existence of a cyclic descent extension for the set
\mathcal{R}_{p}{\left(\mathfrak{S}_{p}\right)}
when
p
is a prime number.

Before we proceed to calculations, we cite a result by Gannon from
[
20
]
. Let
\Delta_{n}^{m}
denote the set of all unimodal
n
-cycles with the maximum at
m
.

Lemma 5.4.1
.

The number of unimodal cycles of length
n
, with maximum at
m
is

\displaystyle|\Delta_{n}^{m}|

\displaystyle=\delta_{n,1}+\frac{(-1)^{n-m}}{n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d|n\\
d\leq n-m\end{subarray}}\mu(d)\sum_{j=1}^{(n-m)/d}(-1)^{j}\binom{n/d}{j}

\displaystyle=\delta_{n,1}+(-1)^{m+1}\delta_{n,2}+\frac{(-1)^{n-m+1}}{n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d|n\\
d<m\end{subarray}}\mu(d)\sum_{j=1}^{\left\lceil m/d\right\rceil-1}(-1)^{j+n/d}\binom{n/d}{j}.

where
\mu
is the Möbius function on
{\mathbb{N}}
.

Observation 5.4.2
.

Binomial coefficients satisfy the following identity

\sum_{i=0}^{k}(-1)^{i}\binom{n}{i}=(-1)^{k}\binom{n-1}{k}.

or equivalently

\sum_{i=1}^{k}\binom{n}{i}(-1)^{i}=(-1)^{k}\binom{n-1}{k}-1.

Lemma 5.4.3
.

When
p
is prime and
n=p
, the polynomial
U_{\mathcal{R}_{n}{\left(\mathfrak{S}_{n}\right)}}\left(x\right)
is divisible by
1+x
and the quotient has non-negative coefficients.

Proof.

Observe that roots of unity of order
p
in
\mathfrak{S}_{p}
are either cycles of length
p
or the identity permutation.
Replace

\sum\limits_{j=1}^{(n-m)/d}(-1)^{j}\binom{n/d}{j}

by

(-1)^{(n-m)/d}\binom{n/d-1}{(n-m)/d}-1

in

|\Delta_{n}^{m}|=\delta_{n,1}+\frac{(-1)^{n-m}}{n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d|n\\
d\leq n-m\end{subarray}}\mu(d)\sum_{j=1}^{(n-m)/d}(-1)^{j}\binom{n/d}{j}

to obtain

|\Delta_{n}^{m}|=\delta_{n,1}+\frac{(-1)^{n-m}}{n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d|n\\
d\leq n-m\end{subarray}}\mu(d)\left((-1)^{(n-m)/d}\binom{n/d-1}{(n-m)/d}-1\right).

Note that no unimodal cycles of length
p
have their maximum at
p
; the only unimodal permutation with the maximum at
p
is the identity permutation. The case
p=2
has already been handled in Theorem
5.2.5
. Therefore, we assume that
p\geq 3
is an odd prime. Since
p
is prime, the only divisor
d
of
p
with
d<p-m
for
m\geq 1
is
d=1
with
\mu(d)=1
and obviously
\delta_{p,1}=0
.
Thus

\begin{split}\Delta_{p}^{m}&=\frac{1}{p}\left(\binom{p-1}{p-m}-(-1)^{p-m}\right)\\
&=\frac{1}{p}\left(\binom{p-1}{m-1}+(-1)^{m}\right).\end{split}

Substituting
u_{m}(\mathcal{R}_{p}{\left(\mathfrak{S}_{p}\right)})=\Delta_{p}^{m}
for
1\leq m\leq p-1
and
u_{p}(\mathcal{R}_{p}{\left(\mathfrak{S}_{p}\right)})=1
we obtain

\begin{split}U_{\mathcal{R}_{p}{\left(\mathfrak{S}_{p}\right)}}(x)&=x^{p}+\frac{1}{p}\sum_{m=1}^{p-1}\left(\binom{p-1}{m-1}+(-1)^{m}\right)x^{m}\\
&=x^{p}+\frac{1}{p}\left(\sum_{m=1}^{p}\binom{p-1}{m-1}x^{m}+\sum_{m=1}^{p}(-1)^{m}x^{m}\right)\\
&=\frac{1}{p}\left(\sum_{m=1}^{p}\binom{p-1}{m-1}x^{m}+px^{p}+\sum_{m=1}^{p}(-1)^{m}x^{m}\right)\\
&=\frac{1}{p}\left(\sum_{m=1}^{p}\binom{p-1}{m-1}x^{m}+\sum_{m=1}^{p-1}\left(x^{p}-(-1)^{m}x^{m}\right)\right)\\
&=\frac{1}{p}\left(\left(1+x\right)^{p-1}+\left(1+x\right)\sum_{m=1}^{p-1}\sum_{j=m}^{p-1}(-1)^{j}x^{j}\right)\\
&=\frac{1+x}{p}\left(\sum_{m=1}^{p-1}\binom{p-2}{m-1}x^{m}+\sum_{m=1}^{p-1}(-1)^{m}mx^{m}\right)\\
&=(1+x)\sum_{m=1}^{p-1}\frac{1}{p}\left(\binom{p-2}{m-1}+(-1)^{m}m\right)x^{m}.\end{split}

We have to show that the coefficients in the summation are non-negative integers.
For
2\leq m\leq p-2
,
\binom{p-2}{m-1}\geq p-2
and also
\binom{p-2}{0}-1=0
(for
m=1
) and
\binom{p-2}{p-2}+(p-1)=p
(for
m=p-1
). Therefore the coefficient is non-negative, and it remains to prove that they are integers. It is sufficient to show that

\binom{p-2}{m-1}+(-1)^{m}{m}\equiv 0\ (\mathrm{mod}\ p)

for every
1\leq m\leq p-1
or equivalently,

\binom{p-2}{m}+(-1)^{m+1}(m+1)\equiv 0\ (\mathrm{mod}\ p)

for every
0\leq m\leq p-2
.

We proceed by reverse induction. For
m=p-2

\binom{p-2}{p-2}+(-1)^{p-1}(p-1)=p\equiv 0\ (\mathrm{mod}\ p).

Assume that for some
m<p-2
,

\binom{p-2}{m+1}\equiv(-1)^{m+1}(m+2)\ (\mathrm{mod}\ p)

holds.
Recall that

\binom{p-2}{m}=\binom{p-2}{m+1}\frac{m+1}{p-2-m}.

But

\binom{p-2}{m+1}\equiv(-1)^{m+1}(m+2)\ (\mathrm{mod}\ p),

therefore

\begin{split}\binom{p-2}{m}&\equiv(-1)^{m+1}(m+2)\frac{m+1}{p-2-m}\ (\mathrm{mod}\ p)\\
&\equiv(-1)^{m+1}(m+2)\frac{m+1}{-(m+2)}\ (\mathrm{mod}\ p)\\
&\equiv(-1)^{m}(m+1)\ (\mathrm{mod}\ p)\end{split}

as desired.
We have proved that the coefficients of
\frac{U_{\mathcal{R}_{p}{\left(\mathfrak{S}_{p}\right)}}(x)}{1+x}
are non-negative integers.
∎

Applying Corollary
5.3.5
, we immediately obtain the following.

Corollary 5.4.4
.

For every prime
p
, the set
\mathcal{R}_{p}{\left(\mathfrak{S}_{p}\right)}
has a cyclic descent extension.

Corollary 5.4.5
.

There exists an involution

\phi:\mathcal{R}_{p}{\left(\mathfrak{S}_{p}\right)}\cap\Delta(p)\to\mathcal{R}_{p}{\left(\mathfrak{S}_{p}\right)}\cap\Delta(p)

such that
m_{\phi(\pi)}=m_{\pi}\pm 1
where
m_{\phi(\pi)}=m_{\pi}\pm 1
and
m_{\phi(\pi)}
denote the positions of maxima of
\phi(\pi)
and
\pi
respectively.

5.5
Gluing unimodal permutations

In this section, we study the cycle structure of unimodal permutations. Intuitively, we show the existence of a total ordering
<_{\Delta}
on
unimodal full cycles
and define a partial action
\oplus
on unimodal full cycles, such that all unimodal permutations can be described as
\sigma_{1}\oplus\dots\oplus\sigma_{k}
where
\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{k}
are unimodal full cycles. We then proceed to show that
\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{k}
is defined if any only in
\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{k}
is a unimodal sequence relative to
<_{\Delta}
.

Most of the definitions, results and basic notation in this section are based on the work of Gannon
[
20
]
on the structure of unimodal permutations. Details and further references can be found there. There are
other approaches to the enumeration of unimodal permutations with given cycle structure and maximum, for example, in the work of Thibon
[
47
]
, who employed methods similar to those of Gessel and Reutenauer
[
22
]
.

We use the following notation.
\Delta(n)
denotes the set of unimodal permutations in
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
. The set of unimodal
full cycles
in
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
will be denoted by
\Delta_{n}
. Denote by
\Delta(*)=\biguplus\limits_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\Delta(n)
the set of all unimodal permutations, and
\Delta_{*}=\biguplus\limits_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\Delta_{n}
the set of all unimodal full cycles. The
maximum
of a permutation
\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
is
m_{\pi}=\pi^{-1}(n)
.

For a linearly ordered set
J=\left\{j_{1}<\dots<j_{n}\right\}
,
let
\Omega_{J}:[n]\to J
be the unique order preserving bijection, namely
\Omega_{J}(i)=j_{i}
.
For a linearly ordered set
X
and a function
f:X\to X
, when
f
preserves a finite
J\subseteq X
and
f|_{J}
is a bijection, the
permutation type
or
shape
of
f|_{J}
is the permutation in
\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
obtained by relabeling
j_{i}
with
i
or equivalently
\pi=\Omega_{J}^{-1}\circ f|_{J}\circ\Omega_{J}
.

We proceed to describe the key results from
[
20
]
that we use in our work.

Lemma 5.5.1
.

[
20
]

Let
\pi\in\Delta(n)
and
J\subseteq[n]
such that
\pi
preserves
J
. Then
\pi|_{J}
is unimodal.
In particular, if
\pi=\sigma_{1}\dots\sigma_{k}
is the cycle decomposition of
\pi
, then
\sigma_{i}
is unimodal for every
1\leq i\leq k
.

Definition 5.5.2
.

Let
\pi_{i}\in\Delta(n_{i})
for
i=1,\dots,k
,
n=n_{1}+\dots+n_{k}
and disjoint
J_{i}\subseteq[n]
with
|J|_{i}=n_{i}
, and
\Omega_{J_{i}}:[n_{i}]\to J_{i}
be the unique order preserving bijections. Define the permutation
\pi\in S_{n}
by

\pi(x)=\left(\pi_{1}\oplus_{J_{1}}\dots\oplus_{J_{k-1}}\pi_{k}\right)(x)=\left(\Omega_{J_{i}}\circ\pi_{i}\circ\Omega_{J_{i}}^{-1}\right)(x)\ \text{for}\ x\in J_{i}.

Note, that
\pi
is always well defined, because
\left\{J_{1},\dots J_{k},\right\}
is a set partition of
[n]
.

Remark 5.5.3
.

An alternative notation will be used.
When
k=2
, we sometimes write
J
and
\overline{J}
instead of
J_{1}
and
J_{2}
respectively. When we do that, we also write
\Omega
and
\overline{\Omega}
instead of
\Omega_{J_{1}}
and
\Omega_{J_{2}}
.
Often, we write
\Omega_{i}
instead
\Omega_{J_{i}}
and
\oplus_{i}
instead of

\oplus_{J_{i}}
. When the context is clear, the indices may be omitted.

Using the
\oplus
notation, we make the following observation.
Let
\pi\in\Delta(*)
and let
{\pi=\sigma_{1}\circ\dots\circ\sigma_{k}}
be the cycle decomposition of
\pi
. Let
J_{i}
be the subset of
[n]
preserved by
\sigma_{i}
,
n_{i}
be the maximum of
J_{i}
and
m_{i}=\sigma_{i}^{-1}(n_{i})
. Let
\pi_{i}
be the shape of
\sigma_{i}
.

Assume that
m_{i}<m_{i+1}
, since it is always possible to enumerate the cycles in this way.
Then,
\pi=\pi_{1}\oplus_{J_{1}}\dots\oplus_{J_{k-1}}\pi_{k}
and the sequence
n_{1},\dots,n_{k}
has to be unimodal.
We obtain the following observation.

Observation 5.5.4
.

Every unimodal permutation
\pi\in\Delta(*)
can be uniquely represented as

\pi_{1}\oplus_{J_{1}}\dots\oplus_{J_{k-1}}\pi_{k}
with
\Omega_{i}(m_{i})<\Omega_{i+1}(m_{i+1})
and the sequence
\Omega_{i}(n_{i})
is unimodal.

From now on, unless stated otherwise, when

\pi=\pi_{1}\oplus_{1}\dots\oplus_{k-1}\pi_{k}
is unimodal and
m_{i}
denote the maxima of
\pi_{i}
, then
i<j
implies that
\Omega_{i}(m_{i})<\Omega_{j}(m_{j})
.
In particular, when indices are omitted and the sum
\pi_{1}\oplus\dots\oplus\pi_{k}
is unimodal, we always mean that
\Omega_{i}(m_{i})<\Omega_{j}(m_{j})
for
i<j
.

The lemma below is essential for building unimodal permutations from smaller ones. A simple observation is that if
\pi=\pi_{1}\oplus_{J}\pi_{2}
holds, then the maximum
m
of
\pi
is equal to either
\Omega(m_{1})
or
\overline{\Omega}(m_{2})
where
m_{i}
denotes the maximum of
\pi_{i}
.

Lemma 5.5.5
.

[
20
, Lemma 1]

Let
\pi_{1}\in\Delta(n_{1}),\pi_{2}\in\Delta(n_{2})
,

and

with

such that
\Omega(m_{1})<\overline{\Omega}(m_{2})
. Let

. Use

to denote maxima of
\pi_{1},\pi_{2},\pi
respectively. Assume that
\pi_{1}\oplus\pi_{2}\in\Delta(n)
. Then for every

and

we have:

1.

If
i_{1}>m_{1}
and

, then
\Omega(i_{1})>m
and
\overline{\Omega}(i_{2})\geq m
.

2.

If

and

If

.

We have the following relation on the maxima unimodal sums, which we use extensively in our calculations.

Corollary 5.5.6
.

Let

be as in Lemma
5.5.5
. Then

1.

m=m_{1}+m_{2}-1

j=1
then

i\sigma=\sigma_{1}\oplus\pi\oplus j\sigma=(a_{1}-1)\sigma_{1}\oplus\pi\oplus\sigma_{1}=\pi\boxplus a_{1}\sigma_{1}.

Similarily, when
\sigma
is grave, we have either
i=1
or
i=0
,

\sigma=\pi\oplus a_{1}\sigma_{1}=\pi\boxplus a_{1}\sigma_{1}

when
i=0
and

\sigma=\sigma_{1}\oplus\pi\oplus(a_{1}-1)\sigma_{1}

when
i=1
.
∎

In the following proofs, we often rely on the observation stated below.

Observation 5.6.6
.

Let
A\subseteq\mathfrak{S}_{n}
be a set of unimodal permutations in
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
.
Then unimodal maxima polynomial
U_{A}(x))
, just counts the number of elements in
A
with given maximum and can be expressed as

\sum_{\pi\in A}x^{m_{\pi}},

where
m_{\pi}
is the maximum of
\pi
.

Lemma 5.6.7
.

Let
a_{1},\dots,a_{l}>0
and
\sigma_{1}<_{\Delta}\dots<_{\Delta}\sigma_{l}
different full cycles, and let
m_{i}
denote the maximum of
a_{i}\sigma_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq l
and let
m=m_{1}+\dots+m_{l}
. Let
U_{{\left\langle a_{1}\sigma_{1},\dots,a_{l}\sigma_{l}\right\rangle_{\boxplus}}}(x)
be the unimodal maxima polynomial of
{\left\langle a_{1}\sigma_{1},\dots,a_{l}\sigma_{l}\right\rangle_{\boxplus}}
.
Then,

U_{{\left\langle a_{1}\sigma_{1},\dots,a_{l}\sigma_{l}\right\rangle_{\boxplus}}}(x)=x^{m-l+1}(1+x)^{l-1}.

In particular, for every
2<l

1+x|U_{{\left\langle a_{1}\sigma_{1},\dots,a_{l}\sigma_{l}\right\rangle_{\boxplus}}}(x)

and the quotient has non-negative coefficients.

Proof.

Let
m_{1}
,…
m_{l}
be the maxima of
a_{1}\sigma_{1},\dots,a_{l}\sigma_{l}
respectively. We proceed by induction. When
l=1
,
U_{\left\langle a_{1}\sigma_{1}\right\rangle_{\boxplus}}(x)=x^{m}_{1}
and result is trivial. When
l=2
, by Corollary
5.6.5
, the elements of
{\left\langle a_{1}\sigma_{1},a_{2}\sigma_{2}\right\rangle_{\boxplus}}
are
a_{1}\pi_{1}\boxplus a_{2}\pi_{2}
and
a_{2}\pi_{2}\boxplus a_{1}\pi_{1}
. By Observation
5.5.6
, and by definition of
<_{\Delta}
, the maximum of
a_{1}\pi_{1}\boxplus a_{2}\pi_{2}
is
m_{1}+m_{2}
and the maximum of
a_{2}\pi_{2}\boxplus a_{1}\pi_{1}
is
m_{1}+m_{2}-1
, and therefore

U_{{\left\langle a_{1}\sigma_{1},a_{2}\sigma_{2}\right\rangle_{\boxplus}}}(x)=x^{m_{1}+m_{2}-1}(1+x)
and the statement is true for
l=2
.

Now, assume that the statement is true for
l-1
. By Corollary
5.6.5
, for every element of
\sigma\in{\left\langle a_{1}\sigma_{1},\dots,a_{l}\sigma_{l}\right\rangle_{\boxplus}}
exists a unique
\pi\in{\left\langle a_{2}\sigma_{2},\dots,a_{l}\sigma_{l}\right\rangle_{\boxplus}}
such that either

\sigma=a_{1}\sigma_{1}\boxplus\pi
or
\sigma=\pi\boxplus a_{1}\sigma_{1}
.

Denote
m_{\pi},m_{\sigma}
denote the maxima of
\pi,\sigma
respectively. By applying Observation
5.5.6
and the definition of
<_{\Delta}
again,
we have
m_{\sigma}=m_{1}+m_{\pi}
for
\sigma=a_{1}\sigma_{1}\boxplus\pi
and

m_{\sigma}=m_{1}+m_{\pi}-1
for
\sigma=\pi\boxplus a_{1}\sigma_{1}
is
m_{1}+m_{\pi}-1
.
By Observation
5.6.6
,

\begin{split}U_{{\left\langle a_{1}\sigma_{1},\dots,a_{l}\sigma_{l}\right\rangle_{\boxplus}}}(x)&=\sum_{\sigma\in{\left\langle a_{1}\sigma_{1},\dots,a_{l}\sigma_{l}\right\rangle_{\boxplus}}}x^{m_{\sigma}}\\
&=\sum_{\pi\in{\left\langle a_{2}\sigma_{2},\dots,a_{l}\sigma_{l}\right\rangle_{\boxplus}}}x^{m_{1}+m_{\pi}-1}+x^{m_{1}+m_{\pi}}\\
&=x^{m_{1}-1}(1+x)\sum_{\pi\in{\left\langle a_{2}\sigma_{2},\dots,a_{l}\sigma_{l}\right\rangle_{\boxplus}}}x^{m_{\sigma}}\\
&=x^{m_{1}-1}(1+x)U_{{\left\langle a_{2}\sigma_{2},\dots,a_{l}\sigma_{l}\right\rangle_{\boxplus}}}(x)\end{split}

By the induction hypothesis,

U_{{\left\langle a_{2}\sigma_{2},\dots,a_{l}\sigma_{l}\right\rangle_{\boxplus}}}(x)=x^{m^{\prime}-l+2}(1+x)^{l-2}

where
m^{\prime}=m_{2}+\dots+m_{l}
.
Therefore,

\begin{split}U_{{\left\langle a_{1}\sigma_{1},\dots,a_{l}\sigma_{l}\right\rangle_{\boxplus}}}(x)&=x^{m_{1}-1}(1+x)x^{m^{\prime}-l+2}(1+x)^{l-2}\\
&=x^{m_{1}+m^{\prime}-l-1}(1+x)^{l-1}=x^{m-l+1}(1+x)^{l-1}\end{split}

as desired.
∎

Observation 5.6.8
.

When
\pi\in\Delta(n)
is a pure permutation, with maximum
m
, the maximum of
\pi\oplus\pi
behaves precisely in the same way as the unique shape
\sigma
of its subcycles.
Namely, if
\sigma
is acute, the maximum of
\pi+\pi
is
2m
when
\sigma
is acute, and
2m-1
when
\sigma
is grave, parity of
n-m
. Hence, in the following discussion, we will refer to the pure permutations as
acute
or
grave
, even when they are not cycles.

The involution
\phi
from Corollary
5.4.5
plays a crucial role in proving our theorem.
For a permutation
\pi
, let
m_{\pi}
denote the minimum of
\pi
.
Recall that
m_{\phi}(\pi)=m_{\pi}\pm 1
.

In the proof of Theorem
5.1.6
, we construct unimodal permutations from
\pi
and
\phi(\pi)
. The following lemma is crucial for understanding the behavior of maxima of unimodal permutations built this way.

Lemma 5.6.9
.

Let
\pi
and
\sigma
be two pure permutations, such that
m_{\sigma}=m_{\pi}+1
holds.
Then for every
k
and
l
the set of maxima of

k\pi\boxplus l\sigma
and
l\pi\boxplus k\sigma
is

1.

\left\{(k+l)m_{\pi}+1,(k+l)m_{\pi}\right\}
when
\pi
is acute.

2.

\left\{(k+l)m_{\pi}+l-k+1,(k+l)m_{\pi}+l-k\right\}
when
\pi
is grave.

Proof.

By Observation
5.5.6
the minima of
k_{\pi}\boxplus l\sigma
and
l\sigma\boxplus k\pi
are
m_{k\pi}+m_{l\sigma}
and
m_{k\pi}+m_{l\sigma}-1
. Also, both numbers appear as the minimum of one of the sums, regardless of the order between
\sigma
and
\pi
.

If
\pi
is acute, then
\sigma
is grave, the maximum of
k\pi
is
km_{\pi}
, and the maximum of
l\sigma
is
l(m_{\sigma}-1)+1
. By substituting
(m_{\sigma}-1)
with
m_{\pi}
and applying Corollary
5.5.6
, shows conclude the set of the maxima of
k\pi\boxplus l\sigma
and
l\sigma\boxplus k\pi
is
\left\{(k+l)m_{\pi}+1(k+l)m_{\pi}\right\}
.

Similarily, if
\pi
is grave then
\sigma
is acute, the the maximum of
k\pi
is
k(m_{\pi}-1)+1
and the maximum of
l\sigma
is
lm_{\sigma}
. By substituing
m_{\sigma}

with
m_{\pi}+1
and applying
Corollary
5.5.6
, we show that the set of maxima of
k\pi\boxplus l\sigma
and
l\sigma\boxplus k\pi
is
\left\{m_{k\pi}+m_{l\sigma},m_{k\pi}+m_{l\sigma}-1\right\}
, as desired.
∎

We are ready to complete the proof of Theorem
5.1.6
.

Theorem 5.6.10
.

For a prime
p
, a cyclic descent extension for
\mathcal{R}_{p}{\left(\mathfrak{S}_{n}\right)}
exists if and only if
p\mid n
.

Proof.

Let
p
be a prime number. We prove that if
p|n
, then a cyclic descent extension exists for
\mathcal{R}_{p}{\left(\mathfrak{S}_{n}\right)}
. The proof of the converse, namely that if a cyclic descent extension exists for
\mathcal{R}_{p}{\left(\mathfrak{S}_{n}\right)}
, then
p|n
was given in Lemma
5.2.1
.

By the given assumption,
p
divides
n
, and hence
q=\frac{n}{p}
is an integer. For any unimodal element
\pi\in\mathcal{R}_{p}{\left(\mathfrak{S}_{n}\right)}
, there exists a unique set of unimodal cycles
\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{l}
such that
\sigma_{i}\in\Delta_{p}\cup\Delta_{1}
for
1\leq i\leq l
, and positive numbers
a_{1},\dots a_{l}
, such that
\pi
has exactly
a_{i}
subcycles of shape
\sigma_{i}
and no other subcycle shapes. In other words,
\pi\in{\left\langle a_{1}\sigma_{1},\dots,a_{l}\sigma_{l}\right\rangle_{\boxplus}}
.

Let
S
be the set of all possible combinations of
{{a_{1}\sigma_{1},\dots,a_{l}\sigma_{l}}}
such that

{{\left\langle a_{1}\sigma_{1},\dots,a_{l}\sigma_{l}\right\rangle_{\boxplus}}\cap\mathcal{R}_{n}{\left(\mathfrak{S}_{p}\right)}\neq\emptyset}.

Then

\mathcal{R}_{p}{\left(\mathfrak{S}_{n}\right)}\cap\Delta(n)=\biguplus_{{a_{1}\sigma_{1},\dots,a_{l}\sigma_{l}}\in S}{\left\langle a_{1}\sigma_{1},\dots,a_{l}\sigma_{l}\right\rangle_{\boxplus}}.

Obviously,

U_{\mathcal{R}_{p}{\left(\mathfrak{S}_{n}\right)}}(x)=U_{\mathcal{R}_{p}{\left(\mathfrak{S}_{n}\right)}\cap\Delta(n)}(x).

Therefore,

U_{\mathcal{R}_{p}{\left(\mathfrak{S}_{n}\right)}}(x)=\sum_{\{a_{1}\sigma_{1},\dots,a_{l}\sigma_{l}\}\in S}U_{{\left\langle a_{1}\sigma_{1},\dots,a_{l}\sigma_{l}\right\rangle_{\boxplus}}}(x).

By Lemma
5.6.7
, when
l\geq 2
.
U_{{\left\langle a_{1}\sigma_{1},\dots,a_{l}\sigma_{l}\right\rangle_{\boxplus}}}(x)
is divisible by
1+x
, with the coefficients of the quotient being non-negative.

o finish the proof, we need to handle the case when
l=1
, as
U_{{\left\langle q\sigma\right\rangle_{\boxplus}}}=x^{m_{q\sigma}}
is not divisible by
1+x
. To address this issue, we use the involution
\phi
from Corollary
5.4.5
.

By using
\phi
, we can partition
\mathcal{R}_{p}{\left(\mathfrak{S}_{p}\right)}\cap\Delta(p)
into pairs
{\pi,\phi(\pi)}
. Without loss of generality, we assume that
m_{\phi(\pi)}=m_{\pi}+1
. If this is not the case, we can replace
\pi
with
\phi(\pi)
.

Let
S_{\pi}
denote all possible pairs
{a_{\pi}\pi,a_{\phi(\pi)}\phi(\pi)}
with
a_{\pi}+a_{\phi(\pi)}=q
, where
q=\frac{n}{p}
. If
\pi=e
or
\phi(\pi)=e
, we replace
e
with
p(1)
when definitions require it. Observe that
e=p(1)
can be treated as a regular acute full cycle for calculation purposes by Observation
5.6.8
.

Note that the parities of
m_{\pi}
and
m_{\phi(\pi)}
are different, meaning that
\pi
is acute if and only if
\phi(\pi)
is grave.

We proceed to calculate

\sum_{\{a_{\pi}\pi,a_{\phi(\pi)}\phi(\pi)\}\in S_{\pi}}U_{{\left\langle a_{\pi}\pi,a_{\phi(\pi)}\phi(\pi)\right\rangle_{\boxplus}}}(x).

If
\pi
is acute and
\phi(\pi)
is grave, then the maxima of
{\left\langle a_{\pi}\pi,a_{\phi(\pi)}\phi(\pi)\right\rangle_{\boxplus}}
for every
1\leq a_{\pi}\leq q-1
are
qm_{\pi}+1
and
qm_{\pi}
, with maximum of
q\pi
being
qm_{\pi}
and the maximum of
q\phi(\pi)
being
qm_{\pi}+1
, by Lemma
5.6.9
.
Hence,

\sum_{\{a_{\pi}\pi,a_{\phi(\pi)}\phi(\pi)\}\in S_{\pi}}U_{{\left\langle a_{\pi}\pi,a_{\phi(\pi)}\phi(\pi)\right\rangle_{\boxplus}}}(x)=q(x^{qm_{\pi}}+x^{qm_{\pi}+1})

which is divisible by
1+x
with quotient
qx^{qm_{\pi}}
.
If
\pi
is grave then the maxima of
q\pi
is
q(m_{\pi}-1)+1
and the maximum of
q\phi(\pi)
is
q(m_{\pi}+1)
.
For every
1<a_{\pi}<q-1
, by Lemma
5.6.9
the maxima of

{\left\langle a_{\pi}\pi,a_{\phi(\pi)}\phi(\pi)\right\rangle_{\boxplus}}
are
(a_{\pi}+a_{\phi(\pi)})m_{\pi}+q-2a_{\pi}+1
and
(a_{\pi}+a_{\phi(\pi)})m_{\pi}+q-2a_{\pi}
.
Thus, we have

1.

U_{{\left\langle q\pi\right\rangle_{\boxplus}}}=x^{qm_{\pi}-q+1}
,

2.

U_{{\left\langle q\phi(\pi)\right\rangle_{\boxplus}}}(x)=x^{q(m_{\pi}+1)}

3.

U_{{\left\langle a_{\pi}\pi,a_{\phi(\pi)}\phi(\pi)\right\rangle_{\boxplus}}}=x^{qm_{\pi}+q-2a_{\pi}+1}+x^{qm_{\pi}+q-2a_{\pi}}
for
1\leq a_{\pi}\leq q-1
.

Therefore,

\begin{split}&\sum_{{\left\langle a_{\pi}\pi,a_{\phi(\pi)}\phi(\pi)\right\rangle_{\boxplus}}\in S_{\pi}}U_{{\left\langle a_{\pi}\pi,a_{\phi(\pi)}\phi(\pi)\right\rangle_{\boxplus}}}(x)=\\
&x^{qm_{\pi}-q+1}+\sum_{a_{\pi}=1}^{q-1}(x^{qm_{\pi}+q-2a_{\pi}+1}+x^{qm_{\pi}+q-2a_{\pi}})+x^{q(m_{\pi}+1)}=\\
&x^{qm_{\pi}-q+1}\sum_{i=1}^{2q-1}x^{i}\end{split}

which is also divisible by
1+x
, with the quotient having negative coefficients.
Hence, the terms of

U_{\mathcal{R}_{p}{\left(\mathfrak{S}_{n}\right)}}(x)

coming from
S_{\pi}
sum up to polynomial
divisible by
1+x
with non-negative coefficients in the quotient, and other terms
are form
U_{{\left\langle a_{1}\sigma_{1},\dots,a_{l}\sigma_{l}\right\rangle_{\boxplus}}}(x)
with
l\geq 2
and are also divisible by
1+x
with non-negative coefficients in the quotient.
Thus
U_{\mathcal{R}_{p}{\left(\mathfrak{S}_{n}\right)}}(x)
is divisible by
1+x
, and the quotient has non-negative coefficients. The result follows.
∎
