On seeded subgraph-to-subgraph matching: The ssSGM Algorithm and matchability information theory
Lingyao Meng, Mengqi Lou, Jianyu Lin, Vince Lyzinski, Donniell E. Fishkind

Abstract
The subgraph-subgraph matching problem is, given a pair of graphs and a positive integer K K , to find K K vertices in the first graph, K K vertices in the second graph, and a bijection between them, so as to minimize the number of adjacency disagreements across the bijection; it is “seeded” if some of this bijection is fixed. The problem is intractable, and we present the ssSGM algorithm, which uses Frank-Wolfe methodology to efficiently find an approximate solution. Then, in the context of a generalized correlated random Bernoulli graph model, in which the pair of graphs naturally have a core of K K matched pairs of vertices, we provide and prove mild conditions for the subgraph-subgraph matching problem solution to almost always be the correct K K matched pairs of vertices. Background and overview

Given a pair of graphs, the graph matching problem seeks to discover a correspondence between the vertex sets of the graphs that minimizes a measure of graph dissimilarity; see, for example,

,

This problem of optimally aligning a pair of graphs is practically useful, theoretically challenging, and computationally difficult.
Practically, graph matching methods have been successfully used for aligning protein interaction networks

,

]
, uncovering bilateral brain structure

, to name a few applications.
Theoretically, there is a growing literature devoted to uncovering sharp information-theoretic thresholds for graph matchability (i.e., when can graph matching algorithms uncover a latent correspondence across the vertex sets of the graph pair in the presence of noise); see, for example,

[

,

,

,

,

,

,

Computationally, when the graphs are allowed to be weighted, directed and loopy, the graph matching problem is equivalent to the NP-hard quadratic assignment problem.
Even the simpler graph isomorphism problem—determining if two graphs are isomorphic—is NP-intermediate (see
[
1
]
for the remarkable proof of the quasipolynomial time complexity of the problem).
This combination of utility and complexity has led to a huge number of approximate graph matching algorithms in the literature; see, for example,
[

,
26
,
51
,
22
,
24
,

,

,

,

One particular branch of algorithms (see, for example,
[

,
15
,
52
,
41
]
)
proceeds by relaxing the combinatorial graph matching problem
to a continuous optimization problem via relaxing the permutation matrix constraint to the set of doubly-stochastic matrices (e.g. see Section

This constrained, continuous optimization problem can then be approximately solved via the Frank-Wolfe method
[

]
or a fast approximation thereof (see for example
[

,

,

]
), and the relaxed solution is projected to the nearest feasible point in the original combinatorial problem.
These Frank-Wolfe-based algorithms empirically provide excellent performance, especially in the presence of seeded vertices (ie. when some of a natural correspondence is known a-priori).

In the present work, we consider the problem of finding the best-matched pair of subgraphs (of a fixed order) in a pair of graphs, simultaneously optimally aligning these subgraphs, also incorporating any seeds; this seeded subgraph-subgraph matching problem is described in Section

Our contributions are two-fold:

First
, in Section

Our second contribution

Graph Matching and subgraph-subgraph matching

All graphs in this paper are simple (i.e. no orientation to edges, no loops, no multi-edges).

Suppose

For any bijection

Suppose that

\delta^{(2)}
, and
\delta^{(3)}

\delta^{(1)}\neq\delta^{(2)}
.
We can equivalently express the graph matching problem as follows. Say the vertex sets are

,v_{n}\}

\delta^{(1)}
or
\delta^{(2)}

according as
v_{i}\sim v_{j}
or
v_{i}\not\sim v_{j}
, and
B_{ij}
is defined as
\delta^{(1)}
or
\delta^{(2)}

according as
w_{i}\sim w_{j}
or
w_{i}\not\sim w_{j}
, and define
A_{ii}=B_{ii}=\delta^{(3)}
. Let

denote the set of

\min\|A-XBX^{T}\|_{F}^{2}
s.t.
X\in\varPi_{n}
.
The usual choice of
\delta^{(1)}
,

\delta^{(2)}
, and

, and

,
-1
, and
-1
.)

The implicit story is that there is a natural correspondence between the two vertex sets that we wish to discover. For example, if the two graphs are modeling two
different social media platforms with the same users in each platform (perhaps the users identify themselves by their real names in one platform and a different identifier in the other platform) and, for any two users, adjacency in a platform
means the respective users communicated in that platform, then the graph matching problem would attempt to cross-identify the users between platforms.

The

graph matching problem refers to the setting where the natural correspondence between the vertex sets is partially known a priori, so we restrict the
optimization to bijections that agree with the known correspondences

[

]
.
In the social media story above, this would happen if some users identify themselves by their real name in both platforms. (See
[

]
for a variant, known as

The practical utility of even a few seeds on matching performance (see
[

]
) has led to seeded graph matching becoming a popular variant of the classical framework; see, for example,
[

,
2
,
42
,
3

38
,

The focus of our work here is a further generalization of this problem setting,

2.1
The seeded subgraph-subgraph matching problem

In this section, we formally present the seeded subgraph-subgraph matching problem,
which is the main focus of our work here.

Suppose
G
and
H
are graphs with respective vertex sets
V
and
W
,
say
m:=|V|
,
n:=|W|
.
Let
K
be a positive integer such that
K\leq\min\{m,n\}
. For any two
subsets
\Omega\subseteq V
and
\Lambda\subseteq W
such that
|\Omega|=|\Lambda|=K
,
and any bijection
\phi:\Omega\rightarrow\Lambda
, a
disagreeing pair
is any
pair of vertices
v,v^{\prime}\in\Omega
such that
v\sim v^{\prime}
and
\phi(v)\not\sim\phi(v^{\prime})
,
or
v\not\sim v^{\prime}
and
\phi(v)\sim\phi(v^{\prime})
. The
subgraph-subgraph
matching problem
is to find subsets
\Omega\subseteq V
and
\Lambda\subseteq W
with
|\Omega|=|\Lambda|=K
,
and to find a bijection
\phi:\Omega\rightarrow\Lambda
, minimizing the number of disagreeing pairs (optimized over all possible
choices of pairs of vertex subsets
and also
all bijections between the corresponding vertex subsets).

Analogously to the previous section, the implicit story is that there is a natural correspondence between a
core
of
K
vertices in each graph,
and we want to estimate the core and the correspondence. For example, if the two graphs are modeling two
different social media platforms, where adjacency of two vertices in a platform
means the respective users communicated in that platform, and if we know that the number of users who are common to
both platforms is
K
(albeit, each user with different identifiers in the two platforms), then the subgraph-subgraph matching problem would attempt to
find these
K
common users and to cross-identify them between platforms.

We can equivalently express the problem as follows. Say the vertices are
V=\{v_{1},v_{2},\ldots

,v_{m}\}
and

W=\{w_{1},w_{2},\ldots,w_{n}\}
, and then let
A\in\{1,-1\}^{m\times m}
and

B\in\{1,-1\}^{n\times n}
be the respective
\pm 1
-adjacency matrices of
G
and
H
.
That is, for all indices
i,j
, we have that
A_{ij}
is
1
or
-1
according as
v_{i}\sim v_{j}
or

v_{i}\not\sim v_{j}
, and
B_{ij}
is
1
or
-1
according as
w_{i}\sim w_{j}
or

w_{i}\not\sim w_{j}
. For any positive integers
c,d,e
such that
e\leq\min\{c,d\}
, let
\varPi_{c,d,e}
denote the set of
c\times d
binary matrices with at most one
1
per row
and at most one
1
per column, with the total number of
1
’s being
e
; that is,

\varPi_{c,d,e}:=\{X\in\{0,1\}^{c\times d}:X\vec{\mathbf{1}}_{d}\leq\vec{\mathbf{1}}_{c},\vec{\mathbf{1}}_{c}^{T}X\leq\vec{\mathbf{1}}_{d}^{T},\vec{\mathbf{1}}_{c}^{T}X\vec{\mathbf{1}}_{d}=e\}
, where

\vec{\mathbf{1}}
denotes a vector of ones with length indexed by its subscript. The
subgraph-subgraph matching problem can be expressed as

\min\|A-XBX^{T}\|_{F}^{2}
s.t.
X\in\varPi_{m,n,K}
; indeed, for all
i=1,2,\ldots,m
and
j=1,2,\ldots,n
such that

X_{ij}=1
, if we understand this to say that
v_{i}\in\Omega
and
w_{j}\in\Lambda
and
\phi(v_{i})=w_{j}
, then the objective function
is eight times the number of disagreeing pairs plus a constant (this constant is
m^{2}-K^{2}
), so we are
basically minimizing the number of disagreeing pairs.

Next, we expand the objective function
\|A-XBX^{T}\|_{F}^{2}=\|A\|^{2}_{F}-2\textup{trace}A^{T}XBX^{T}+\|XBX^{T}\|^{2}_{F}
; note that
\|A\|_{F}^{2}
and

\|XBX^{T}\|_{F}^{2}
are constants, respectively equal to
m^{2}
and
K^{2}
, and note that
A
is symmetric, from which we get the
following equivalent formulation of the subgraph-subgraph matching problem:

\displaystyle\max_{X\in\varPi_{m,n,K}}\textup{trace}AXBX^{T}\ \ .

(1)

Note that if we had instead used standard
\{0,1\}
-adjacency matrices, rather than
\pm 1
-adjacency
matrices, then this expression would
not
be equivalent to the subgraph-subgraph matching problem.

In the social media story above, sometimes we will know a-priori a few members of the core and their correspondence
across the media platforms. With this in mind, the

seeded subgraph-subgraph matching problem
is where the subgraph-subgraph matching optimization is
done when fixing
s
vertices of
\Omega
,
s
vertices of
\Lambda
, and
\phi
is fixed in mapping the former fixed vertices to the latter fixed vertices in some particular manner.
Without loss of generality, the fixed vertices (“seeds”) are
v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{s}
and
w_{1},w_{2},\ldots,w_{s}
and, without loss of generality,
for each
i=1,2,\ldots,s
, we fix
\phi(v_{i})=w_{i}
. Thus we can equivalently express:

\displaystyle(\textup{seeded subgraph-subgraph matching problem})\ \ \max_{X\in\varPi_{m-s,n-s,K-s}}\textup{trace}A(I_{s}\oplus X)B(I_{s}\oplus X)^{T}\ \ .

(2)

This problem is computationally intractable;
indeed, the special cases seeded graph matching problem and graph matching problem
are computationally intractable, as elaborated in Section
1
.

Thus we seek approximate solutions that are efficiently
computable. In the next section, we adapt the Frank-Wolfe methodology—that was used for
efficient approximate solution of the seeded graph matching problem in
[
15
]
—to our setting of seeded subgraph-subgraph graph matching.

Much of the literature on subgraph matching is focused on matching
G
to a subgraph of
H
(i.e.,
K=m<n
) where
H
is often of much larger order than
G
; see, for example,
[
44
,
48
,
34
,
35
,
45
]
.
Our problem is more akin to the
K
-core alignment problem
[
9
]
or partial alignment problem
[
21
,
47
]
, wherein we seek to correctly partially align (often all but a vanishing fraction of) a pair of large networks.
However, in our present framework we do not assume the subgraphs we seek to align have any structural properties a priori (i.e., density, average degree, etc.), and we do not posit a latent (or structural) alignment of the remainder of the graphs after aligning the subgraphs.

3
The ssSGM algorithm

In this section we introduce the ssSGM algorithm, which provides an efficiently computable,
approximate solution to the seeded subgraph-subgraph matching problem, by
generalizing the techniques of
[
46
,
15
,
45
]
. The algorithm is described as follows.
We begin by relaxing the seeded subgraph-subgraph matching problem, and then we apply Frank-Wolfe methodology to approximately
solve the relaxed problem. Finally, we project the relaxed solution to the nearest feasible point
in the original problem, which provides an approximate solution to the seeded subgraph-subgraph
matching problem. We begin by stating two key technical lemmas.

3.1
Key technical lemmas

For any positive integers
c,d,e
such that
e\leq\min\{c,d\}
, let
{\mathcal{D}}_{c,d,e}

denote the set of
c\times d
nonnegative, real matrices with row sums at most
1
, column sums at most
1
,
and all entries summing to
e
; that is,
{\mathcal{D}}_{c,d,e}:=\{X\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{c\times d}:X\vec{\mathbf{1}}_{d}\leq\vec{\mathbf{1}}_{c},\vec{\mathbf{1}}_{c}^{T}X\leq\vec{\mathbf{1}}_{d}^{T},\vec{\mathbf{1}}_{c}^{T}X\vec{\mathbf{1}}_{d}=e\}
. The following
result is quite useful; it is essentially due to Mendelsohn and Dulmage
[
33
]
(as extended in
[
20
]
), and it is a generalization
of the classical Birkhoff-Von Neumann Theorem.
Let
\mathcal{H}(\cdot)
denote the convex hull of its argument.

Lemma 1
.

For any positive integers
c,d,e
such that
e\leq\min\{c,d\}
, it holds
\mathcal{H}(\varPi_{c,d,e})={\mathcal{D}}_{c,d,e}
.

Next, let
M
be any given matrix in
\mathbb{R}^{c\times d}
. The
generalized linear assignment problem
is defined as

\displaystyle\max_{X\in\varPi_{c,d,e}}\textup{trace}M^{T}X

(3)

The special case where
c=d=e
is the well-known linear assignment problem, and is solvable in time
O(e^{3})
by the
Hungarian Algorithm
[
27
,
23
]
.
Note that (
3
) is equivalent to
\max_{X\in{\mathcal{D}}_{c,d,e}}\textup{trace}M^{T}X
; this is an immediate consequence of
Lemma
1
and since, for any linear function, the function value of a convex combination
of vectors is the convex combination of the vectors’ function values.

Let
\underline{\xi}
be any real number less than all of the entries of
M
, and let
\overline{\xi}
be any real number greater than
all of the entries of
M
. Define the matrix
\mathcal{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{(c+d-e)\times(c+d-e)}
such that

\mathcal{M}=\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}M&\overline{\xi}E\\
\overline{\xi}E&\underline{\xi}E\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]
, where
E
denotes a
matrix of all
1
s, appropriately sized. In Appendix
A
we prove the following lemma.

Lemma 2
.

For any positive integers
c,d,e
such that
e\leq\min\{c,d\}
, and for any
M\in\mathbb{R}^{c\times d}
, suppose

\mathcal{X}^{*}\in\varPi_{c+d-e,c+d-e,c+d-e}
is a solution of the linear assignment problem

\max_{{\mathcal{X}}\in\varPi_{c+d-e,c+d-e,c+d-e}}\textup{trace}\mathcal{M}^{T}{\mathcal{X}}.

Let
X^{*}
be the
c\times d
upper-left corner submatrix of
\mathcal{X}^{*}
; ie induced by the first
c
rows and first
d

columns of
\mathcal{X}^{*}
. Then
X^{*}
is a solution to the generalized linear assignment problem

\max_{X\in\varPi_{c,d,e}}\textup{trace}M^{T}X
.

3.2
Solving the relaxed problem with Frank-Wolfe methodology

In order to efficiently obtain an approximate solution to the
seeded subgraph-subgraph matching problem as expressed in (
2
), we begin by expressing its relaxation:

\displaystyle(\textup{relaxed seeded subgraph-subgraph matching problem})\ \ \max_{X\in{\mathcal{D}}_{m-s,n-s,K-s}}\textup{trace}A(I_{s}\oplus X)B(I_{s}\oplus X)^{T}.

(4)

We will apply Frank-Wolfe
[
18
]
methodology to approximately solve this relaxed problem; afterwards, we will project the
relaxed solution back to the original feasible region.

In general, when maximizing any objective function over any polytope, Frank-Wolfe methodology consists of
generating a sequence of iterates, which are points in the polytope, terminating when an iterate does not change much
from the previous iterate. The first iterate is an arbitrary point in the polytope and, given any iterate, the next iterate
is calculated from the current iterate in two steps.
The first step is to linearize the objective function about the current iterate, and to maximize this
linear function over the polytope; this is simply a linear programming problem.
The second step is to maximize the (original) objective function along (only) the line segment
between the current iterate and the linear program solution from the first step;
the solution to this one-dimensional optimization problem is the next iterate.

The objective function for the seeded subgraph-subgraph matching problem is the
quadratic function
f(X):=\textup{trace}A(I_{s}\oplus X)B(I_{s}\oplus X)^{T}
, and we want to maximize it over the set

{\mathcal{D}}_{m-s,n-s,K-s}
, which is clearly a polytope. A natural choice for the first Frank-Wolfe iterate is the

(m-s)\times(n-s)
matrix with all entries
\frac{K-s}{(m-s)(n-s)}
, which is clearly in
{\mathcal{D}}_{m-s,n-s,K-s}
.

Given any current Frank-Wolfe iterate, say
Z\in{\mathcal{D}}_{m-s,n-s,K-s}
, the linearization of
f
about
Z
is given by

\textup{trace}[\nabla f(Z)]^{T}X
plus a fixed constant that can be ignored when maximizing. If we partition

A=\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}A^{(11)}&A^{(21)^{T}}\\
A^{(21)}&A^{(22)}\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]
and

B=\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}B^{(11)}&B^{(12)}\\
B^{(12)^{T}}&B^{(22)}\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]
, where

A^{(21)}\in\{-1,1\}^{(m-s)\times s}
,
B^{(12)}\in\{-1,1\}^{s\times(n-s)}
,

A^{(22)}\in\{-1,1\}^{(m-s)\times(m-s)}
, and
B^{(22)}\in\{-1,1\}^{(n-s)\times(n-s)}
then

\nabla f(X)=2A^{(21)}B^{(12)}+2A^{(22)}XB^{(22)}
, hence the first step to computing the next Frank-Wolfe
iterate is to solve the generalized linear assignment problem

\displaystyle\max_{X\in{\mathcal{D}}_{m-s,n-s,K-s}}\textup{trace}\left(A^{(21)}B^{(12)}+A^{(22)}ZB^{(22)}\right)^{T}X,

(5)

which can be done efficiently (in cubic time) via the Hungarian Algorithm, thanks to Lemma
2
.
Say the solution
X
to this linear program is
X^{*}\in{\mathcal{D}}_{m-s,n-s,K-s}
. The second step to computing the
next Frank-Wolfe iterate is maximizing
f
along the line
segment
\{\lambda X^{*}+(1-\lambda)Z:\lambda\in[0,1]\}
; this is trivial to do since
f
is a
quadratic function in
\lambda
. Say the optimal
\lambda
is
\lambda^{*}
. The the next Frank-Wolfe
iterate is defined to be
\lambda^{*}X^{*}+(1-\lambda^{*})Z\in{\mathcal{D}}_{m-s,n-s,K-s}
.

When the Frank-Wolfe algorithm terminates, say that
Z\in{\mathcal{D}}_{m-s,n-s,K-s}
is the output. That is,
Z
is
the approximate solution to the relaxed problem (
4
). Since
Z
may not be binary valued,
our last task is to find a nearest matrix that is feasible in the original problem, ie. we
solve
\min_{X\in\varPi_{m-s,n-s,K-s}}\|X-Z\|_{F}^{2}
.
Noting that
\|X-Z\|_{F}^{2}=\|X\|_{F}^{2}-2\textup{trace}Z^{T}X+\|Z\|_{F}^{2}
, we see that this is equivalent to
solving
\max_{X\in\varPi_{m-s,n-s,K-s}}\textup{trace}Z^{T}X
, which is again a generalized linear assignment problem,
solvable efficiently (in cubic time) via the Hungarian Algorithm, thanks to Lemma
2
.
The optimal solution will be an approximate solution to the seeded subgraph-subgraph matching
problem as expressed in (
2
) and is the output of this algorithm which we call ssSGM. We summarize the
algorithm as follows:

Input :
Adjacency matrices
A\in\{-1,1\}^{m\times m}
,
B\in\{-1,1\}^{n\times n}
of
G
,
H

1

Calculate

3

4

5

Solve

; this is done exactly since the objective is
quadratic in the one variable
\lambda
. Say
\lambda^{*}
is the solution ;

6

7

Set
Z\leftarrow\lambda^{*}X^{*}+(1-\lambda^{*})Z
;

8

end while

9

10

Compute

11

12

Output :
sets

. function

\phi(v_{i})=w_{j}

.

6
Consistency

Until this point in this paper, our work has been focused on developing the ssSGM algorithm and highlighting it as an efficient and effective tool for the seeded subgraph-subgraph matching problem. This section, Section

, is concerned with matchability from the information theory direction and is in the spirit of
[

31
]

Our main result for this work is Theorem

which, under the model described in Section

, guarantees that this almost always happens when there are mild assumptions.

7
Discussion, future work

The graph matching problem (as well as the many variants and generalizations) is important because of its wide-ranging application as well as its challenging theory and computational complexity.
We provide here an efficient and effective algorithm (ssSGM) to approximately solve the seeded subgraph-subgraph matching problem, and we state and prove an information-theoretic matchability result (Theorem
3
)
in this context.

There are a number of promising directions for further work that build on our work here.

The seeds that are incorporated into
ssSGM are vertices in the core together with their a priori known alignments across the pair of graphs; such known alignments are the usual
definition of “seeds” in graph
matching. However, in our setting
where there are core vertices and non
core vertices, we might know
a priori that certain vertices are
in the core, but not know their alignments.
It would be interesting to
incorporate such “pre-seeds” into our
subgraph-subgraph matching problem, and compare their effectiveness to seeds in identifying a latent matching.

Also, Theorem
3
provides
(mild) sufficient conditions for matchability in our setting, but it does not provide necessary conditions.
It would be worth extending our results to obtain necessary and sufficient conditions
(up to order).
In particular, the “total correlation” (a measurement of correlation between a pair of graphs) introduced in
[
16
]
and
[
14
]
combines
the edge correlation together with
the Bernoulli coefficients, and
was demonstrated to be an
extremely effective
tool for determining matchability.
Their random pair-of-graphs model
was less general than ours here,
but sets forward an promising tool
to achieve necessary and sufficent
conditions.
