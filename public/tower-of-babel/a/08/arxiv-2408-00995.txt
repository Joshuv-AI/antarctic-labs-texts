Introduction

Random geometric graphs have emerged as a fruitful way to model dependence between edges in random networks in both theory and practice
[
Pen03
,
SKB08
,
BBDD+21
,
DdC22
]
.
Their defining property is that edges are included based on similarity of feature vectors associated to the nodes.
Geometric graphs
accurately capture certain properties of social networks
[
MB73
,
MSLC01
,
HRH02
,
PJ09
,
ES16
]
, biological networks
[
HRP08
,
RV23
,
SGN+21
]
, and wireless communication networks
[
Gil61
,
Hae12
]
.

A simple example is given by the
Spherical Random Geometric Graph
, popular in the mathematical community due to its simplicity and elegance.

Definition 1

(Spherical Random Geometric Graph)
.

A sample from the distribution
{\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{S}^{d-1},p)}
over graphs on
n
vertices
[n]=\{1,\ldots,n\}
of
dimension
d
with expected density
p
is generated as follows. First,
n
vectors
V_{1},\ldots,V_{n}
are drawn independently from the uniform distribution on the sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}.
Then, an edge between
i
and
j
is formed if and only if
\langle V_{i},V_{j}\rangle\geq\tau^{p}_{d}.
Here,
\tau^{p}_{d}
is chosen so that
p=\,{\rm I}\kern-1.66492pt{\rm P}[\langle V_{i},V_{j}\rangle\geq\tau^{p}_{d}].

Much is known about the theoretical properties of random geometric graphs in the low-dimensional regime when
d
is constant or
on the order of
O(\mathsf{polylog}(n))

[
Pen03
]
. A recent breakthrough work in this setting
[
LMSY23
]
shows that in certain dimensions
d=\Theta(\log n),
random geometric graphs are sparse two-dimensional expanders, an object for which no randomized construction was previously known.

The interest in the high-dimensional regime when
d\ggg\mathsf{polylog}(n)
is much more recent.

A strikingly simple, but fundamental, question remains open after more than a decade of continuous work and improvements:
For what sequences of
n,d,p
are the edges of
{\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{S}^{d-1},p)}
actually dependent?
When
d
is large enough (as a function of
n
and
p
), the
{\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{S}^{d-1},p)}
distribution is only
o(1)
total variation distance away from
\mathsf{G}(n,p).
In other words, for large enough
d,
the edges are asymptotically independent
[
DGLU11
]
. The majority of the work on high-dimensional random geometric graphs has focused on determining the precise dimension for which this occurs
[
DGLU11
,
BDER14
,
BBN20
,
LMSY22
,
BB24b
]
. When
d\lll(np)^{3},
a simple triangle-based statistic witnesses edge dependence. Conjecturally, when
d\ggg(np)^{3},

{\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{S}^{d-1},p)}
converges to
\mathsf{G}(n,p)
in total variation distance.

Insofar as the purpose of the
{\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{S}^{d-1},p)}
distribution is to model edge dependence, the interesting regime is
d\lll(np)^{3}
, where the distribution is far from Erdős-Rényi and the edges are meaningfully dependent. Yet, very little is known in the “bulk”
\mathsf{polylog}(n)\lll d\lll(np)^{3}
of this regime. This leads to the main motivation of the current paper:

What can be said about
{\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{S}^{d-1},p)}
in the regime
\mathsf{polylog}(n)\lll d\lll(np)^{3}
?

The higher the dimension, the weaker the dependence is between edges and, thus, one can expect behaviour more similar to Erdős-Rényi.
One well-studied property in this regime is the spectrum of the adjacency matrix
[
LR23
,
LS23
,
BB24b
]
. When
d\lll np,
the centered adjacency matrix of a sample from
{\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{S}^{d-1},p)}
has an operator norm of
\tilde{\Theta}(np/\sqrt{d})
which is significantly larger than that of
\mathsf{G}(n,p)
at
\tilde{\Theta}(\sqrt{np}).
Conversely, when
d\ggg np,
the operator norm is
\tilde{\Theta}(\sqrt{np}),
which is on the same order of
\mathsf{G}(n,p).
A different result is that when
d\ggg\mathsf{polylog}(n),
the clique number of
{\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{S}^{d-1},p)}
is essentially the same as that of
\mathsf{G}(n,p)

[
DGLU11
]
. Aside from existence of a clique of a given size, the thresholds in
p
for other natural properties such as connectivity and Hamiltonicity are completely open when
\mathsf{polylog}(n)\lll d\lll(np)^{3}.
Is the behaviour of
{\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{S}^{d-1},p)}
with respect to those properties similar to the behaviour of the much better-understood
\mathsf{G}(n,p)
distribution?

1.1
Our Angle

In order to understand which properties might be shared between the random geometric graph and Erdős-Rényi in the regime
\log(n)\lll d\lll(np)^{3}
, we aim to show a new style of comparison result between the two graph models.
Of course, such a comparison should
not be in total variation
, because in this regime
the two distributions have total variation distance
1-o(1)
. Large total variation is a consequence of differing signed triangle counts, but what about other properties? It seems plausible that the distributions share many properties in addition to their clique number (which was studied in
[
DGLU11
]
).

Rather than studying properties individually, we aim to simultaneously address all
edge-monotone properties
. These include existence of a clique of a given size, but also Hamiltonicity, connectivity, existence of a perfect matching and many more. One of the most fundamental facts about
\mathsf{G}(n,p)
is that
monotone properties
subject to very general criteria exhibit a sharp-threshold in
p

[
FabJB99
]
. Yet, extending this result to non-product measures has proven to be a challenging task. Important progress has been made in the case of measures satisfying the FKG lattice condition
[
Gri06
]
. However, the RGG does not satisfy this condition (see
Proposition

7.1
).
[
GRK05
]
prove sharp thresholds for monotone properties for random geometric graphs but in the low-dimensional regime
o(\log n).
As addressing monotone properties is one of our main goals, we aim for a comparison with Erdős-Rényi under which similar distributions assign similar probabilities to monotone events.

Besides exploring the similarities between
\mathsf{G}(n,p)
and
{\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{S}^{d-1},p)},
we will also aim for a comparison that evinces
meaningful
differences between the two distributions.
Real-world networks typically
do not exactly match any natural generative model such as the one in
Definition

1
, and model misspecification can be captured by allowing for a small proportion of the edges to be adversarially corrupted.
While the signed triangle count distinguishes the two graph models when
d\lll(np)^{3},
this test turns out to be rather brittle.

When are there tests between
{\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{S}^{d-1},p)}
and
\mathsf{G}(n,p)
that are robust to edge corruptions?

The combination of
monotonicity
and
edge corruptions
leads us to the following notion of comparison with Erdős-Rényi: approximate stochastic dominance.
Our goal will be to

Couple
H_{-}\sim\mathsf{G}(n,p(1-\epsilon)),H_{+}\sim\mathsf{G}(n,p(1+\epsilon)),
and
G\sim{\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{S}^{d-1},p)}
for some vanishingly small
\epsilon
in such a way that

H_{-}\subseteq G\subseteq H_{+}
with high probability (where inclusion is with respect to the edge sets).

Our main result
is that such a coupling exists if and only if
d\ggg np.
(
[
LMSY22
]
also exhibit such a coupling but in the weaker regime
d\ggg n^{2}p^{2}
. Furthermore, their result is not algorithmic while ours is. A more detailed comparison is made in
Section

1.7
.)
This has several applications:

1.

Sharp Thresholds:
We use the
edge-monotonicity
of the coupling to compare monotone properties of
\mathsf{G}(n,p)
and
{\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{S}^{d-1},p)}.
We give the first proof of sharp thresholds for monotone properties of
{\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{S}^{d-1},p)}
for a polynomially large range of dimensions where
{\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{S}^{d-1},p)}
is TV-distance
1-o(1)
away from
\mathsf{G}(n,p)
. Furthermore, we show that the critical probabilities are nearly identical with those of
\mathsf{G}(n,p)
in this range.

2.

Robust Testing:
One interpretation of the approximate stochastic dominance is that a sample of
\mathsf{G}(n,p)
is a sample of
{\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{S}^{d-1},p)}
with
O(\epsilon n^{2}p)
adversarially corrupted edges. This leads us to revisit the well-studied question of testing between
\mathsf{G}(n,p)
and
{\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{S}^{d-1},p)}

in the previously unexplored setting with adversarial edge-corruptions. Our comparison result shows impossibility of robust testing for
d\ggg np
and in the complementary regime we provide an efficient sum-of-squares algorithm.

3.

Enumeration:
The approximate stochastic dominance implies a lower bound on the support size of the
{\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{S}^{d-1},p)}
distribution. This allows us to use
{\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{S}^{d-1},p)}
as a randomized construction for
counting the number of geometric graphs in dimension
d.

We next state our main coupling result and then describe these applications in more detail.

1.2
Main Coupling Theorem

Theorem 1.1

(Main Coupling Result)
.

Consider some
n,d,p
such that
d=\Omega(\max(np,\log n))
and
p=\Omega(1/n),p\leq 1/2.
There exists a polynomial-time algorithm which on input
H\sim\mathsf{G}(n,p),
outputs
n
vectors
V_{1}=V_{1}(H),V_{2}=V_{2}(H),\ldots,V_{n}=V_{n}(H)
in
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
with the following two properties:

1.

The marginal distribution of the vectors is independent uniform:
V_{1},V_{2},\ldots,V_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathsf{Unif}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}).

2.

With high probability (over the input and internal randomness),
\mathds{1}[\langle V_{i},V_{j}\rangle\geq\tau^{p}_{d}]=H_{ij}
for all pairs
i,j
such that
|\langle V_{i},V_{j}\rangle-\tau_{p}^{d}|\geq\frac{c\max(\sqrt{np},\sqrt{\log n})(\log n)^{3/2}}{d}
for some absolute constant
c.

In other words, the algorithm described above couples
{\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{S}^{d-1},p)}
and
\mathsf{G}(n,p)
in such a way that they only differ at pairs
(i,j)
for which
\langle V_{i},V_{j}\rangle
is very close to the threshold
\tau^{p}_{d}
. Elementary calculations shows that with high probability,
G,H
differ at only
\tilde{O}\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$($}}}\binom{n}{2}p\times\sqrt{\frac{{np}}{d}}\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$)$}}}=o\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$($}}}\binom{n}{2}p\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$)$}}}
edges.

Remark 1
.

Theorem

1.1
shows that one can represent
{\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{S}^{d-1},p)}
as an Erdős-Rényi graph with few “defect edges.” By performing multiple rounds of our coupling algorithm, we can sequentially “fix” these defect edges.
We obtain a curious novel representation of
{\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{S}^{d-1},p)}
by recursively planting Erdős-Rényi graphs (of exponentially shrinking size) in
\mathsf{G}(n,p).
When
d=n^{1+\epsilon}p,
the recursion only takes constantly many rounds. See

Section

3.3
for the representation result.

An immediate implication of
Theorem

1.1
is the following approximate stochastic dominance.

Corollary 1.1

(Approximate Stochastic Dominance)
.

Consider some
n,d,p
such that
d=\Omega(np\log^{4}n,\log^{5}n)
and
p=\Omega(1/n).
Then, there exists a coupling of
G\sim\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{S}^{d-1},p),

H_{-}\sim\mathsf{G}(n,p(1-\frac{c\max(\sqrt{np},\sqrt{\log n})(\log n)^{2}}{\sqrt{d}}))
and
H_{+}\sim\mathsf{G}(n,p(1+\frac{c\max(\sqrt{np},\sqrt{\log n})(\log n)^{2}}{\sqrt{d}})),

such that

H_{-}\subseteq G\subseteq H_{+}

with probability
1-o(1)
, where inclusion is defined as inclusion over the edge set.

In
[
LMSY22
]
, the authors prove
Corollary

1.1
under the weaker condition
d\ggg n^{2}p^{2}
with the purpose of describing the local structure of random geometric graphs. Our result, which holds for
d\ggg np
is based on an entirely different proof technique. The difference between
np
and
(np)^{2}
is not merely quantitative. Qualitatively,
d=\tilde{\Theta}(np)
is of fundamental importance to spherical random geometric graphs as it is simultaneously a spectral and an entropic threshold, see
Section

1.6
. This dual interpretation makes it key to our applications and using
d\ggg n^{2}p^{2}
would give highly suboptimal results for robust testing and enumeration.

Remark 2
.

We note that in general a relaxed stochastic dominance of the form in
Corollary

1.1
does not guarantee closeness in total variation even if one couples

H_{-}\sim\mathsf{G}(n,p),H_{+}\sim\mathsf{G}(n,p),
and
G\sim\mathcal{D}
such that
H_{-}\subseteq G\subseteq H_{+}
with high probability for some
\mathcal{D}.
Specifically, consider a distribution
\mathcal{D}
defined by the following coupling.
H_{-}
is drawn from
\mathsf{G}(n,p).
Then,
H_{+}
is a copy of
H_{-}
except that a uniformly random non-edge is changed to an edge. One can check that whenever
p=\Omega(1/n),
the distribution of
H_{+}
is only
o(1)
TV distance away from
\mathsf{G}(n,p).
Finally,
G
is the graph among
H_{-},H_{+}
with an even number of edges. Clearly,
H_{-}\subseteq G\subseteq H_{+}
w.h.p. but
{d_{\mathsf{TV}}}(\mathcal{D},\mathsf{G}(n,p))\geq 1/2-o(1)
as the distribution
\mathcal{D}
is supported on graphs with an even number of edges.

1.3
Sharp Thresholds

The question of whether monotone properties exhibit sharp thresholds is by far best understood for product distributions.
2
2

2

See
Section

2
for the formal definitions of sharp thresholds, critical probability, and critical window.
The seminal paper of
[
FabJB99
]
building on decades of work on the subject
[
Mar74
,
Rus81
,
Rus82
,
BKK+92
,
Tal94
,
FK96
]
establishes very broad conditions on vertex-transitive properties for which the Erdős-Rényi distribution exhibits sharp-thresholds. In non-product settings, much less is understood. Important progress has been made in the case of positive measures satisfying the FKG lattice property
[
Gri06
,
GG06
,
GG11
]
. However,
this property does not hold for random geometric graphs, which we check in
Proposition

7.1
.

In the case of low-dimensional random geometric graphs (over the solid cube
[0,1]^{d}
),
[
GRK05
]
show that monotone properties have
additive
sharp-thresholds by proving that if
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
are independent samples with densities
p(1-o(1))
and
p
, then
G_{1}\subseteq G_{2}
with high probability after an appropriate vertex relabelling.
Also in low dimension, but in a somewhat different setting where the vertices come from a Poisson process and hence the random geometric graph is of variable size,
[
BP13
]
gives necessary conditions for properties monotone in
both
edges and vertices that do not exhibit
multiplicative
sharp thresholds. Under certain compatibility of the vertex and edge thresholds, they can transfer the results to the fixed-size edge-monotone setting. However, a generic result for the fixed-size edge-monotone setting is only conjectured in
[
BP13
]
.
The methods of
[
GRK05
,
BP13
]
do not seem applicable to the high-dimensional setting because both rely on the fact that a very fine (grid) subdivision of the space is well-covered by the latent vectors. In
d
dimensions, the subdivision has
\exp(\Omega(d))
cells, which is much larger than
n
when
d=\omega(\log n).

This suggests a natural question, posed by Perkins
[
Per24
]
:
Do random geometric graphs in higher dimensions exhibit sharp thresholds?
Of course, when the dimension is sufficiently large, the
{\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{S}^{d-1},p)}
distribution is asymptotically the same as
\mathsf{G}(n,p)
and the answer is “yes”. But what about the case when
d\lll n^{3}p^{3}
and the two distributions are provably far in total variation?

Our coupling
Corollary

1.1
in the regime
d\ggg np
between random geometric graph
G
and Erdős-Rényi
H_{-}
and
H_{+}
is monotone in the sense that

H_{-}\subseteq G\subseteq H_{+}.

This implies that
whenever
H_{-}\sim\mathsf{G}(n,p(1-\frac{c\max(\sqrt{np},\sqrt{\log n})(\log n)^{2}}{\sqrt{d}}))
satisfies an increasing property with high probability, so does
G\sim{\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{S}^{d-1},p)},
and whenever

G\sim{\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{S}^{d-1},p)}
satisfies an increasing property, so does
H_{+}\sim\mathsf{G}(n,p(1+\frac{c\max(\sqrt{np},\sqrt{\log n})(\log n)^{2}}{\sqrt{d}})).

This allows us to translate sharp-threshold phenomena from Erdős-Rényi to RGG. We formalize this argument in
Section

7.1
and record it as the following theorem.

Theorem 1.2
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{P}_{n}
is a monotone property with critical probability
p^{c}_{n}
in
\mathsf{G}(n,p)
, which satisfies
p^{c}_{n}=\Omega(1/n).

Suppose further that
\mathcal{P}_{n}
exhibits a sharp threshold with respect to
\mathsf{G}(n,p).

If
d_{n}=\omega(\max(np^{c}_{n}\log^{4}n,\log^{5}n)),
the property
\mathcal{P}_{n}
also exhibits a sharp threshold for
\mathsf{RGG}(n,d_{n},p)
and has critical probability
p^{c}_{n}(1+o_{n}(1)).

1.4
Robust Testing

The problem of testing between Erdős-Rényi and a spherical random geometric graph has attracted significant attention in recent years
[
DGLU11
,
BDER14
,
BBN20
,
LMSY22
,
BB24b
]
. Formally, one observes a graph
G
and needs to decide
H_{0}:G\sim\mathsf{G}(n,p)
or
H_{1}:G\sim{\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{S}^{d-1},p)}.
By comparing the number of signed triangles
\sum_{i<j<k}(G_{ij}-p)(G_{jk}-p)(G_{ki}-p)
to a threshold, one can succeed with high probability whenever
d=\omega(n^{3}p^{3}\log^{3}(1/p))

[
BDER14
,
SW22
]
. The current state-of-the-art information-theoretic lower bounds are that when
d\geq(\log n)^{C}n^{3}p^{2},
the problem is information theoretically impossible as well as when

p=\Theta(1/n)
and
d\geq(\log n)^{C}
for some constant
C

[
LMSY22
]
. Even ignoring
\mathsf{polylog}
factors, there is still a
1/p
gap between the testing algorithm and the information-theoretic lower bound when
1/n\lll p\lll 1/2.
This gap was recently closed with respect to low-degree polynomial tests by
[
BB24b
]
, i.e., counting signed triangles is optimal among all degree
\log^{2-\epsilon}n
polynomial tests.

The existing methods in the literature for both the upper and lower-bounds for feasibility of testing are extremely brittle – they
fail even under a very small fraction of adversarially corrupted edges. On the algorithmic side,
consider the well-studied test mentioned above based on comparing the number of signed triangles to a threshold. The expected number of signed triangles in
{\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{S}^{d-1},p)}
is
\tilde{\Theta}(n^{3}p^{3}/\sqrt{d})

[
LMSY22
]
while in
\mathsf{G}(n,p)
it is 0.
Thus, planting a clique of size
\tilde{O}(np/d^{1/6})
in
\mathsf{G}(n,p)
(which requires
\tilde{O}(n^{2}p^{2}/d^{1/3})=o(n^{2}p)
adversarial corruptions) makes the expected counts match and hence the test fails. One can argue similarly for other tests based on counting subgraphs. Regarding lower-bounds, all of the prior works in the setting without adversarial corruptions
[
BDER14
,
BBN20
,
LMSY22
]
are based on bounding total variation distance and strongly exploit distributional assumptions.
We do not expect that the techniques in any of those papers apply to the robust testing setting.

The methods of the current work not only apply to, but also give tight upper and lower bounds, in a setting of a ”maximum possible” fraction of corruptions. We work in the strong contamination model with edge corruptions, which has been extensively studied for the stochastic block model, e.g.
[
BMR21
]
for testing and
[
MS16
,
DdNS22
,
MRW24
]
for recovery.

Problem 1

(Robust Testing of Geometry With Edge Corruptions)
.

Let
n,d\in\mathbb{N},

p\in[0,1]
and
\epsilon>0
be a fixed constant.
One observes a graph
H
and needs to decide between the two hypotheses
H_{0}:H=\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{G}),\mathbf{G}\sim\mathsf{G}(n,p)
and

H_{1}:H=\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{G}),\mathbf{G}\sim\mathsf{RGG}(n,d,p).
Here,
\mathcal{A}:\{0,1\}^{\binom{n}{2}}\longrightarrow\{0,1\}^{\binom{n}{2}}
is any unknown mapping such that

\mathcal{A}(G)
and
G
differ in at most
\epsilon\binom{n}{2}p
entries for any adjacency matrix
G\in\{0,1\}^{\binom{n}{2}}.

We restrict the adversary to changing at most
\epsilon\binom{n}{2}p
edges for a small constant
\epsilon
as otherwise
Problem

1
is trivially impossible. To see this, note that both
\mathsf{RGG}(n,d,p)
and

\mathsf{G}(n,p)
have with high probability at most
1.5\binom{n}{2}p
edges. Hence, an adversary
\mathcal{A}
who simply deletes all edges of a sample from
\mathsf{RGG}
and then inserts Bernoulli
p
edges (in total
3\binom{n}{2}p
w.h.p.) can transform
{\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{S}^{d-1},p)}
into a sample from
\mathsf{G}(n,p).
Thus,
Problem

1
captures the setting of “maximal adversaries.”

Our first result is an information-theoretic lower bound which immediately follows from
Theorem

1.1
.
Whenever
d\geq K(\epsilon)\max(np(\log n)^{4},(\log n)^{5})
(where
K(\epsilon)
depends solely on
\epsilon
), one can with high probability (adversarially) corrupt
\epsilon\binom{n}{2}p
edges of a sample from
{\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{S}^{d-1},p)}
and produce a sample from
\mathsf{G}(n,p).

1.5
Enumeration

A classic result in algebraic geometry due to Warren
[
War68
]
nearly immediately implies that the number of geometric graphs in
d

for some explicit constant

when
d=o(n)

[
MM11
,
Sau21
]
. The main idea is that the edges of a geometric graph correspond to the sign patterns of
\binom{n}{2}
polynomials over the
dn

The variables are the coordinates of the latent vectors and the polynomials are the squared pairwise
\ell_{2}
distance minus the radius squared.

Proving lower bounds, however, turns out to be a much more challenging task. In
[
MM11
]
, it is shown that in 2 dimensions the number of geometric graphs (known also as unit disk graphs in this case) is
n^{(2+o(1))n}.
Using more sophisticated tools from algebraic geometry and topology,
[
Sau21
]
shows that the number of
geometric graphs in
d
dimensions is
n^{(d+o(1))n}
when
d=o(n).

We recover this result up to a polylogarithmic factor in the exponent. Namely, using
Corollary

1.1
, we demonstrate that
{\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{S}^{d-1},p)}
is a distribution over at least
\exp(\tilde{\Omega}(n^{2}p))
graphs when
d=\max(np\log^{4}n,\log^{5}n)
which yields the following statement.

Theorem 1.4
.

The number of graphs which can be realized as intersection graphs of unit spheres in dimension
d
is at least
\exp(nd\log^{-7}n).

Admittedly, our proof loses a non-trivial polylogarithmic factor. Yet, one advantage of our proof is its simplicity – it is based on a natural probabilistic construction, spherical random geometric graphs. By comparison, the techniques from algebraic geometry and algebraic topology in
[

]
are significantly more involved, although certainly more systematic and sharper.

The proof idea in
Theorem

1.4
is rather simple: if
\mathcal{M}
is the support of
{\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{S}^{d-1},p)},
a counting argument bounds the number of graphs that are edge-subgraphs of some graph
H
in
\mathcal{M}
and differ from
H
in
o\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$($}}}\binom{n}{2}p\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$)$}}}
edges. Combined with

, this immediately gives an upper bound on the entropy of

in terms of

As the latter is
\exp(\tilde{\Omega}(n^{2}p)),
we get a lower bound on

1.6
Entropic and Spectral Interpretations of
d=\tilde{\Theta}(np)

The reader might have observed the omnipresence of the dimension

in the current work. This is a polynomially smaller dimension than the one required for

For the latter to hold,

1.

Entropic Threshold:
The maximum entropy of a graph distribution with expected density

\mathsf{G}(n,p).
On the other hand, a simple heuristic

2.

Somewhat miraculously, as soon as

We leave the open-ended question of explaining why the entropic and spectral thresholds coincide as an exciting direction for future work.
While we are not aware of any reasons for why this is the case, it is fundamentally the reason why problems like latent vector embedding and robust testing for random geometric graphs do
not
have statistical-computational gaps. Entropy roughly controls the size of the support and, hence, the success of (inefficient) exhaustive search algorithms. The spectrum of a matrix, on the other hand, is one of the most fundamental linear-algebraic primitives and is used throughout theoretical computer science for the design of computationally efficient algorithms.

The fact that simple spectral and SDP approaches match brute-force algorithms for random geometric graphs is not a given,
and indeed this is not the case for many other latent space graph distributions.
Most famously, for the planted clique distribution

For a random geometric graphs example, the planted dense cycle model also exhibits an information-computation gap

[

the authors give a general condition under which a gap does not exist, but their model is qualitatively different and does not capture our setting. In it, one only observes a predefined subset of the edges. The absence of a gap depends on the structure of this subset.

3.1
The Coupling Algorithm

Motivating Our Construction.

Suppose that we are given the adjacency matrix
(H_{ji})_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathsf{Bern}(p)
of
H\sim\mathsf{G}(n,p).
Our goal is to couple
H
with
n
vectors
V_{1},V_{2},\ldots,V_{n}
on the sphere such that:

1.

V_{1},V_{2},\ldots,V_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathsf{Unif}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}).

2.

Conditioned on
(H_{ji})_{1\leq i<j\leq n},
for most pairs
(j,i),
it is the case that
\mathds{1}[\langle V_{i},V_{j}\rangle\geq\tau^{p}_{d}]=H_{ji}.

While property 1 is easy to achieve on its own, property 2 is much less trivial. One needs to approximately embed the adjacency matrix of
H
as a geometric graph on the sphere. Even if the adjacency matrix already corresponds to a sample from
{\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{S}^{d-1},p)},
this step is non-trivial and requires some embedding algorithm, for example the spectral approach of
[
LMSY22
]
. And even if one succeeds in this embedding step, it is unclear how one could enforce the distributional assumption in property 1.

Our approach is to build up a coupling incrementally, starting with vectors satisfying property 1 and maintaining this as an invariant. That is, the first step is to sample
n
vectors
V_{1}^{(0)},V_{2}^{(0)},\ldots,V_{n}^{(0)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathsf{Unif}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}).
We then incrementally transform them so that 1) they remain uniform on the sphere and 2) they produce a geometric graph that is close to the input
H
.

We start with the edge
(2,1).
If
H_{21}=\mathds{1}[\langle V^{(0)}_{1},V^{(0)}_{2}\rangle\geq\tau^{p}_{d}],
we do not need to do anything since property 2 is already satisfied for this edge. Otherwise, we transform
V^{(0)}_{2}
into
V^{(1)}_{2}
such that
\mathds{1}[\langle V^{(0)}_{1},V^{(1)}_{2}\rangle\geq\tau^{p}_{d}]=1-\mathds{1}[\langle V^{(0)}_{1},V^{(0)}_{2}\rangle\geq\tau^{p}_{d}]
, i.e., presence of edge
(2,1)
in our RGG is flipped. One way to do this transformation is as follows: we keep unchanged
the direction of
V^{(0)}_{2}
in the space orthogonal to
V^{(0)}_{1}

and flip
V^{(0)}_{2}
in the direction of
V^{(0)}_{1}
via a measure-preserving (with respect to the
\mathsf{Unif}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
distribution) map so that the inner product is on the correct side of
\tau^{p}_{d}.
We call this procedure a
flip orientation
of
V^{(0)}_{2}
along
V^{(0)}_{1}
so that the edge between them is set to
H_{21}
and we write
V^{(1)}_{2}=\rho^{H_{21}}_{V^{(0)}_{1}}(V^{(0)}_{2}).
In particular, in this notation
\rho^{b}_{a}(z)=z
whenever
b=\mathds{1}[\langle a,z\rangle\geq\tau^{p}_{d}].

Having coupled
H_{21}
with
V^{(0)}_{1},V^{(1)}_{2},
we proceed to flip
V^{(0)}_{3}
first around
V^{(0)}_{1}
and then around
V^{(1)}_{2}
and so on. In short, the algorithm we use to prove
Theorem

1.1
is as follows.

Algorithm 1
.

First, sample
V_{1}^{(0)},V_{2}^{(0)},\ldots,V_{n}^{(0)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathsf{Unif}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}).
Then, flip sequentially:

\begin{split}&V_{1}^{(0)},\hskip 9.24994pt\widetilde{V}_{1}\coloneqq V_{1}^{(0)}\\
&V_{2}^{(0)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rho^{H_{21}}_{\widetilde{V}_{1}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}V_{2}^{(1)},\hskip 9.24994pt\widetilde{V}_{2}\coloneqq V_{2}^{(1)}\\
&V_{3}^{(0)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rho^{H_{31}}_{\widetilde{V}_{1}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}V_{3}^{(1)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rho^{H_{32}}_{\widetilde{V}_{2}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}V_{3}^{(2)},\hskip 9.24994pt\widetilde{V}_{3}\coloneqq V_{3}^{(2)}\\
&\,\,\,\vdots\\
&V_{n}^{(0)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rho^{H_{n1}}_{\widetilde{V}_{1}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}V_{n}^{(1)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rho^{H_{n2}}_{\widetilde{V}_{2}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}V_{n}^{(2)}\cdots\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rho^{H_{n,n-1}}_{\widetilde{V}_{n-1}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}V_{n}^{(n-1)},\hskip 9.24994pt\widetilde{V}_{n}\coloneqq V_{n}^{(n-1)}.\end{split}

The final output vectors are denoted by
(\widetilde{V}_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
where
\widetilde{V}_{i}\coloneqq V^{(i-1)}_{i}.
Importantly, observe that
flips occur with respect to the final vectors.
In general, the flip orientation transformation is succinctly expressed as

V^{(i)}_{j}=\rho^{H_{ji}}_{\widetilde{V}_{i}}(V^{(i-1)}_{j}),

meaning that we flip
V^{(i-1)}_{j}
in the direction of
\widetilde{V}_{i}
so that

\mathds{1}[\langle V^{(i)}_{j},\widetilde{V}_{i}\rangle\geq\tau^{p}_{d}]=H_{ji}.

Analysis Sketch.

We need to show that the two properties claimed in
Theorem

1.1
hold.

Independence follows directly from the “measure-reserving” property of
\rho^{b}_{a}(\cdot),
which we formalize in
Section

4.1
. For technical reasons, the following slightly stronger independence statement will be useful.

Lemma 3.1
.

For any choice of
s\in\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
and
i_{s}\in\{0,1,2\ldots,s-1\},

\widetilde{V}_{1},\widetilde{V}_{2},\ldots,\widetilde{V}_{s-1},V_{s}^{(i_{s})}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathsf{Unif}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}).

The main idea behind the proof is that before any operation is performed on
V_{s}^{(0)},
it is uniform on the sphere and independent from
\widetilde{V}_{1},\widetilde{V}_{2},\ldots,\widetilde{V}_{s-1}.
Since the transformation
V_{s}^{(0)}\longrightarrow V_{s}^{(1)}
is measure-preserving for any choice of
\widetilde{V}_{1},\widetilde{V}_{2},\ldots,\widetilde{V}_{s-1},
the distribution of
V_{s}^{(1)}
remains uniform on the sphere and independent from
\widetilde{V}_{1},\widetilde{V}_{2},\ldots,\widetilde{V}_{s-1}.
We proceed by induction.

We also need to prove that with high probability, all pairs
i,j
such that
|\langle\widetilde{V}_{i},\widetilde{V}_{j}\rangle-\tau_{p}^{d}|\geq\frac{c\max(\sqrt{np},\sqrt{\log n})(\log n)^{3/2}}{d}
satisfy

\mathds{1}[\langle\widetilde{V}_{i},\widetilde{V}_{j}\rangle\geq\tau^{p}_{d}]=H_{ji}.
By construction of the flip orientation map
\rho
,
\mathds{1}[\langle\widetilde{V}_{i},V^{(i)}_{j}\rangle\geq\tau^{p}_{d}]=H_{ji}
, because
V^{(i)}_{j}
has just been oriented relative to
\widetilde{V}_{i}
to achieve this.
We will show that the final
\widetilde{V}_{j}=V_{j}^{(j-1)}
has not changed too significantly from
V^{(i)}_{j}
in the
\widetilde{V}_{i}
direction:

Lemma 3.2
.

For any
1\leq i<j\leq n,

\,{\rm I}\kern-1.66492pt{\rm P}\bigg[\Big|\langle\widetilde{V}_{i},\widetilde{V}_{j}\rangle-\langle\widetilde{V}_{i},V^{(i)}_{j}\rangle\Big|\geq\frac{c\max(\sqrt{np},\sqrt{\log n})(\log n)^{3/2}}{d}\bigg]\leq\frac{1}{n^{100}}.

(1)

To give intuition about the quantity
\widetilde{\Theta}({\sqrt{np}}/{d})
, we write
\langle\widetilde{V}_{i},\widetilde{V}_{j}\rangle-\langle\widetilde{V}_{i},V^{(i)}_{j}\rangle
as a telescoping sum, recalling that
\widetilde{V}_{j}=V^{(j-1)}_{j}:

\begin{split}&\langle\widetilde{V}_{i},\widetilde{V}_{j}\rangle-\langle\widetilde{V}_{i},V^{(i)}_{j}\rangle=\sum_{u=i+1}^{j-1}\langle\widetilde{V}_{i},V^{(u)}_{j}-V^{(u-1)}_{j}\rangle=\sum_{u=i+1}^{j-1}\langle\widetilde{V}_{i},\rho^{H_{ju}}_{\widetilde{V}_{u}}(V^{(u-1)}_{j})-V^{(u-1)}_{j}\rangle.\end{split}

(2)

Each of the terms is equal to
0
with probability
1-p.
That is because
\rho^{H_{ju}}_{\widetilde{V}_{u}}(V^{(u-1)}_{j})-V^{(u-1)}_{j}=0
whenever
\mathds{1}[\langle\widetilde{V}_{u},V^{(u-1)}_{j}\rangle\geq\tau^{p}_{d}]=H_{ji}
and the two sides are independent
\mathsf{Bern}(p)
random variables (the left-hand side because
\widetilde{V}_{u},V^{(u-1)}_{j}
are independent by
Lemma

3.1
). Hence, we expect only
\widetilde{O}(np)
non-zero terms.

Whenever
\rho^{H_{ju}}_{\widetilde{V}_{u}}(V^{(u-1)}_{j})-V^{(u-1)}_{j}
is non-zero, its norm is on the order of
\widetilde{O}(1/\sqrt{d})
with high probability. This is the case because
\widetilde{V}_{i},V^{(u)}_{j}
and
\widetilde{V}_{i},V^{(u-1)}_{j}
are pairwise independent vectors on the unit sphere by
Lemma

3.1
and
V_{j}
changes in direction
\widetilde{V}_{u}
when
\rho^{H_{ju}}_{\widetilde{V}_{u}}(\cdot)
is applied. Moreover,
\rho^{H_{ju}}_{\widetilde{V}_{u}}(V^{(u-1)}_{j})-V^{(u-1)}_{j}
is in the span of
\widetilde{V}_{u},V^{(u-1)}_{j}.
Both directions are independent of
\widetilde{V}_{i}
and, hence, only have a
\widetilde{O}(1/\sqrt{d})
inner product with
\widetilde{V}_{i}.
Altogether, this means that each
\langle\widetilde{V}_{i},\rho^{H_{ju}}_{\widetilde{V}_{u}}(V^{(u-1)}_{j})-V^{(u-1)}_{j}\rangle
is of order
\widetilde{O}(1/d).
As there are
\widetilde{O}(np)
non-zero terms, the sum is of order
\widetilde{O}(np/d)
with high probability.

However, we need to prove that the sum is of order
\widetilde{O}(\sqrt{np}/d).
The key insight is that a CLT-type square-root cancellation occurs in the sum
Eq.

2
. We show this via the classic martingale inequality
Theorem

2.2
. The technical challenge is that
Eq.

2
is
not actually a sum of martingale differences
for the following “martingale violation” reasons:

(V1)

Whenever
\rho^{H_{ju}}_{\widetilde{V}_{u}}(V^{(u-1)}_{j})-V^{(u-1)}_{j}\neq 0,
it has a non-trivial component in the direction of
V^{(u-1)}_{j}.
The reason is that while the map
V\mapsto\rho^{H_{ju}}_{\widetilde{V}_{u}}(V)
preserves the direction of
V
on the space orthogonal to
\widetilde{V}_{u},
it does not preserve the norm of
V
in this orthogonal space. This is because
\rho^{H_{ju}}_{\widetilde{V}_{u}}(V)
must also have unit norm and, thus, a rescaling occurs. As the difference
\rho^{H_{ju}}_{\widetilde{V}_{u}}(V^{(u-1)}_{j})-V^{(u-1)}_{j}
is not independent of
V^{(u-1)}_{j},
one can easily show that the martingale property is violated.

(V2)

Whether
\rho^{H_{ju}}_{\widetilde{V}_{u}}(V^{(u-1)}_{j})-V^{(u-1)}_{j}=0
holds depends on the projection of
V_{j}^{(u-1)}
on
\widetilde{V}_{u}.
Conditioned on
H_{ji},H_{j,i+1},\ldots,H_{j,u-1}
(which need to appear in the underlying filtration),
V_{j}^{(u-1)}
and
\widetilde{V}_{i}
are not independent. Hence, the projections of
\widetilde{V}_{u}
on
V_{j}^{(u-1)},\widetilde{V}_{i}
are not independent. So,
\langle\widetilde{V}_{i},\rho^{H_{ju}}_{\widetilde{V}_{u}}(V^{(u-1)}_{j})-V^{(u-1)}_{j}\rangle
does not have a zero conditional expectation.

We deal with these issues by manually subtracting off the component of
\rho^{H_{ju}}_{\widetilde{V}_{u}}(V^{(u-1)}_{j})-V^{(u-1)}_{j}
in the direction of
V^{(u-1)}_{j}
and the component of
V_{j}^{(u-1)}
in the
\widetilde{V}_{i}
direction. We show that these lead to lower-order contributions and what remains is a true sum of martingale differences.

Remark 4
.

On a high-level “flipping
V^{(i-1)}_{j}
in the direction of
\widetilde{V}_{i}
” is analogous to the Gram-Schmidt procedure of
[
BB24b
]
which aims to isolate the component of
V_{j}
in the direction of
V_{i}.
In
[
BB24b
]
, this is achieved by forming an orthonormal basis
e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{n}
such that
V_{i}\approx e_{i}.
In fact, one can modify their argument and derive the equivalent statement of
Theorem

1.1
when
d\gg n
(by flipping measure-preservingly the
Z_{ji}
values around
\phi^{p}_{d}
in their notation). Nevertheless, when
d<n,
one cannot form an orthonormal basis. A “nearly orthonormal” set is not sufficient for the arguments in
[
BB24b
]
as they heavily rely on the fact that the components of a Gaussian vector in orthogonal directions are independent. Hence, we need a different approach for the regime
np\ll d\ll n
that is central to our applications.

3.2
The Robust Testing Algorithm

Motivation.

The motivation behind our testing algorithm comes from the interpretation of
d=\widetilde{\Theta}(np)
as a spectral threshold. Specifically, when
d\ll np,
the centered adjacency matrix of
{\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{S}^{d-1},p)}
typically has
d
large eigenvalues of order
\widetilde{\Theta}({np}/{\sqrt{d}}).
This is larger than the typical second eigenvalue of a sample from
\mathsf{G}(n,p)
which is only
\sqrt{np}.
Something more turns out to be true: the eigenvectors corresponding to these
d
eigenvalues are very close to the latent vectors
V_{1},V_{2},\ldots,V_{n},
a key insight in
[
LS23
]
. Formally, the
d
rows of the matrix
V=(V_{1},V_{2},\ldots,V_{n})
are close to the
d
eigenvectors with large eigenvalues. In
[
LS23
]
, the authors use this fact to estimate the true Gram matrix
VV^{T}
from the adjacency matrix. Our end goal is different – to construct a spectral refutation algorithm. Formally, we give a certificate robust to
\epsilon\binom{n}{2}p
edge corruptions that the
p
-centered adjacency matrix of
{\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{S}^{d-1},p)}
has a large operator norm.

The case of no corruptions.

Suppose for now that we wanted to use the spectrum to test between
\mathsf{G}(n,p)
and
{\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{S}^{d-1},p)}
in the case of no corruptions when
d\ll np.

6
6

6

Of course, this regime is suboptimal in the case of no adversarial corruptions as the signed triangle count succeeds all the way up to
d=\widetilde{\Theta}(n^{3}p^{3})

[
BDER14
,
LMSY22
]
.

For the uncorrupted centered adjacency matrix
A
of a sample from
{\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{S}^{d-1},p)}
, we know that there exists some matrix
Y\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}
such that
\|Y_{:,i}\|_{2}=1
for each
i
and

\langle Y^{T}Y,A\rangle
is large: One such matrix is
Y=V,
the matrix of latent vectors. As discussed above,
V
is close to the top
d
eigenvectors of
A
, each corresponding to eigenvalue
\lambda=\widetilde{\Theta}({np}/\sqrt{d}).
Furthermore,
\|V\|^{2}_{F}=n.
Hence, the objective value
\langle V^{T}V,A\rangle
is on the order of
\lambda\times\frac{\|V\|^{2}_{F}}{{d}}\times d=\widetilde{\Theta}({n^{2}p}/\sqrt{d}).
To illustrate the main ideas, assume that it is exactly
n^{2}p/\sqrt{d}.

Consider now the centered adjacency matrix
A
of a
\mathsf{G}(n,p)
sample.
Its largest eigenvalue is
\widetilde{O}(\sqrt{np}),
so

\langle Y^{T}Y,A\rangle\leq\langle Y^{T}Y,\widetilde{O}(\sqrt{np})\times I\rangle\leq\widetilde{O}(\sqrt{np})\times\mathsf{tr}(Y^{T}Y)=\widetilde{O}(n\sqrt{np}).

Again, for simplicity, assume that it is
n\sqrt{np}.
Note that
n\sqrt{np}\ll n^{2}p/\sqrt{d}
when
d\ll np.

We will therefore search (via a suitable SoS program) for a matrix
Y\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}
such that
\langle YY^{T},A\rangle
is larger than some threshold. If we succeed, then our graph ought to be a sample from
{\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{S}^{d-1},p)}.

Handling corruptions.

Observe that the objective value
\langle Y^{T}Y,A\rangle
for
Y=V
changes by very little when an adversary corrupts an edge
A_{ij}.
The reason is that
(V^{T}V)_{ij}=\langle V_{i},V_{j}\rangle,
which with high probability is only on the order of
\widetilde{O}(1/\sqrt{d}).
Hence, after
\epsilon\binom{n}{2}p
corruptions, the objective value can change by at most
\widetilde{\Theta}(\epsilon n^{2}p/\sqrt{d}).
Again, for simplicity, assume that the change is at most
\epsilon n^{2}p/\sqrt{d}.
This, however, is less than the difference between
n^{2}p/\sqrt{d}
(the uncorrupted
\mathsf{RGG}
value) and
n\sqrt{np}
(the uncorrupted
\mathsf{G}(n,p)
value)! Hence, imposing that
|\langle Y_{i},Y_{j}\rangle|=\widetilde{O}(1/\sqrt{d}),
the SoS program will distinguish between the two graph models.

To get the full statement of
Theorem

1.3
, we need to be more careful due to the log factors that we suppressed everywhere. Otherwise, we may end up with an algorithm that only works for
\epsilon=\log n^{-C}
for some absolute constant
C>0.
We deal with this by imposing a more fine-grained bound on the empirical distribution of
\{|\langle Y_{i},Y_{j}\rangle|\}_{1\leq i<j\leq n}
instead of
|\langle Y_{i},Y_{j}\rangle|=\widetilde{O}(1/\sqrt{d}).

Remark 5
.

The idea of maximizing a quadratic form against the centered adjacency has been used for robust statistics in the stochastic block model, e.g.
[
GV16
]
. What is new in our work is the realization that the latent vectors witness a large operator norm in the case of random geometric graphs. To the best of our knowledge, novel is also the use of a constraint on the tails of the empirical distribution of the entries of a given matrix (
\{|\langle Y_{i},Y_{j}\rangle|\}_{1\leq i<j\leq n}
).

3.3
A Curious Representation: RGG as Recursively Planted Erdős-Rényi

We now describe a curious representation result, which furthers the view of RGG as Erdős-Rényi with few planted edges arising in
Corollary

1.1
and
[
BB24b
]
. Most strikingly, when
d=n^{1+\epsilon}p
for some absolute constant
\epsilon,
we show that one can represent
{\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{S}^{d-1},p)}
by starting with a sample from
\mathsf{G}(n,p)
and performing constantly many rounds of planting independent density
1/2
Erdős-Rényi graphs. The sizes of these planted Erdős-Rényi graphs decrease exponentially in
n.
The entire “geometry” of the
{\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{S}^{d-1},p)}
distribution is, thus, hidden in the locations of the planted Erdős-Rényi graphs.

Concretely, in the current
Theorem

1.1
, we allow for some amount of “defect edges”, i.e. the ones for which
|\langle V_{i},V_{j}\rangle-\tau^{p}_{d}|\leq\frac{c\max(\sqrt{np},\sqrt{\log n})(\log n)^{3/2}}{d}.
One may try to correct these edges after the termination of
Algorithm

1
. Indeed, recall from
Section

3.1
that one starts with arbitrary vectors
V_{1}^{(0)},V_{2}^{(0)},\ldots,V^{(0)}_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathsf{Unif}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
and transforms them into
\widetilde{V}_{1},\widetilde{V}_{2},\ldots,\widetilde{V}_{n}.
However,

\widetilde{V}_{1},\widetilde{V}_{2},\ldots,\widetilde{V}_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathsf{Unif}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}).
Thus, one may try to apply the algorithm again and correct the defect edges. That is,
sequentially apply

V^{(i)}_{j}=\rho^{H_{ji}}_{\widetilde{V}_{i}}(\widetilde{V}_{j})
for all
i,j
such that

|\langle\widetilde{V}_{i},\widetilde{V}_{j}\rangle-\tau^{p}_{d}|\leq\frac{c\max(\sqrt{np},\sqrt{\log n})(\log n)^{3/2}}{d}.

Intuitively, this should fix most of the disagreements between
H
and
G_{ji}=\mathds{1}[\langle V_{i},V_{j}\rangle\geq\tau^{p}_{d}].
The reason is that each
\widetilde{V}_{i}

will only be changed
\widetilde{O}(p\sqrt{np/d})
times as (with high probability there are at most
\widetilde{O}(p\sqrt{np/d})
values of
j
for which
|\langle\widetilde{V}_{i},\widetilde{V}_{j}\rangle-\tau^{p}_{d}|\leq\frac{c\max(\sqrt{np},\sqrt{\log n})(\log n)^{3/2}}{d}
). Furthermore, one can easily show that the changes will be of smaller order than the ones
Section

3.1
(see
Appendix

C
). Altogether, running through the same martingale-based analysis, one should expect to fix all edges except the ones for which
\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$|$}}}\langle\widetilde{V}_{i},\widetilde{V}_{j}\rangle-\tau^{p}_{d}\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$|$}}}\lll\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$($}}}\max(\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\times(np/d)^{5/4}),\frac{1}{d}(np/d)^{1/2})\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$)$}}}.
This will vastly reduce the number of defect edges. After several more iterations, one can hope to completely remove all defect edges.

Of course, this should not be possible since
\mathsf{G}(n,p)
and
{\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{S}^{d-1},p)}
are
far in total variation
in the regime
np\lll d\lll n^{3}p^{3}.

The issue with the argument above is that the independence is lost since we have already used
H_{ji}
once to
flip
V^{(i)}_{j}=\rho^{H_{ji}}_{\widetilde{V}_{i}}(V^{(i-1)}_{j})
in
Algorithm

1
. Hence,
the distributional
Lemma

3.1
does not hold any more and
the resulting sample will not be a sample from
{\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{S}^{d-1},p)}.

A simple way to resolve this issue is to draw fresh values of
H^{1}_{ji}
for the second round of flippings. Then, draw fresh values for a third round and so on until no defect edges are left. This algorithm yields the following representation of
{\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{S}^{d-1},p)}.
We give more detail in
Appendix

C
.

Theorem 3.1

(Description of Random Geometric Graphs as Recursively Planted Erdős-Rényi)
.

Suppose that
p=\Omega(1/n)
and
d=\kappa\times np\log^{6}n
for some
\kappa>1.
Then, there exist some
T=O(\frac{\log d}{\log\kappa}+\frac{\log n}{\log d})
and a set of
random functions
F_{1},F_{2},\ldots,F_{T},
where each
F_{i}
takes an
n
-vertex graph as an input and outputs a subset of
[n]\times[n]

with the following property. Consider the algorithm:

1.

Draw
H^{0}\sim\mathsf{G}(n,p)
and set
G^{0}=H^{0}.

2.

For
t=1,2,\ldots,T:

(a)

Draw a fresh
H^{t}\sim\mathsf{G}(n,1/2).

(b)

Compute the set of pairs of vertices
F_{t}(G^{t-1})\in[n]\times[n].

(c)

Define
G^{t}
by
G^{t}|_{F_{t}(G^{t-1})}=H^{t}
and

G^{t}|_{F_{t}(G^{t-1})^{c}}=G^{t-1}.

Then,
{d_{\mathsf{TV}}}(\mathcal{L}(G^{T}),{\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{S}^{d-1},p)})=o(1).
Furthermore, with high probability,
F_{t}(G^{t-1})\subseteq F_{t+1}(G^{t})
for each
t
and there exists some
T_{1}=\Theta(\frac{\log d}{\log\kappa})
such that

\displaystyle|F_{t}(G^{t-1})|\leq\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} &C_{D}n^{2}p\sqrt{\log 1/p}\times\Big(\frac{np\log^{6}n}{d}\Big)^{{\frac{3^{t-1}}{2^{t}}}}\text{ for }t\leq T_{1}\\[5.0pt]
&C_{D}n^{3/2}p^{1/2}\sqrt{\log 1/p}\times\Big(\frac{\log^{2}n}{\sqrt{d}}\Big)^{t-T_{1}}\text{ for }t>T_{1}.\end{aligned}\end{cases}

One should think of
F_{t}(G^{t-1})
as the set of defect edges after
t
rounds of the coupling algorithm. Note that we always resolve the defect edges by planting an Erdős-Rényi graph on them. Hence, the above observation produces a description of
{\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{S}^{d-1},p)}
as a recursively planted Erdős-Rényi. Each next planted Erdős-Rényi is (doubly-)exponentially smaller.

When
\kappa=n^{\epsilon}
for some constant
\epsilon>0,
in the above statement
T=O(1/\epsilon).
Hence, to construct a random geometric graph in dimension
n^{1+\epsilon}p,
one only needs to recursively plant constantly many times an Erdős-Rényi graph in an Erdős-Rényi graph according to the functions
F_{i}.

4.1
The Flip-Orientation Map

Define the unique monotone map
\phi:[-1,1]\longrightarrow[-1,1]
such that

\phi(\tau^{p}_{d})=\tau^{p}_{d},\phi(1)=-1,\phi(-1)=1
and
\phi:[-1,\tau^{p}_{d}]\longrightarrow[\tau^{p}_{d},1],

\phi:[\tau^{p}_{d},1]\longrightarrow[-1,\tau^{p}_{d}]
is measure-preserving with respect to
\mathcal{D}_{d}|_{(-1,\tau^{p}_{d}]}
and

\mathcal{D}_{d}|_{[\tau^{p}_{d},1)}.
In particular,
\phi\circ\phi=\mathsf{identity}.

For
b\in\{0,1\}
, define the map
\rho^{b}_{a}(z)
which (possibly) flips
z\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
around
a\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
so that
b=\mathds{1}[\langle a,z\rangle\geq\tau^{p}_{d}].
The vector
z
is transformed
in the
a
-direction so that the projection on
a
changes to
\phi(\langle a,z\rangle)
instead of
\langle a,z\rangle
if
\mathds{1}[\langle\rho_{a}^{b}(z),a\rangle\geq\tau^{p}_{d}]\neq b.
Formally,

\begin{split}&\rho^{b}_{a}(z)\coloneqq\begin{cases}z\hskip 9.24994pt\text{ if }\mathds{1}[\langle a,z\rangle\geq\tau^{p}_{d}]=b,\\
(z-\langle a,z\rangle a)\sqrt{\frac{1-\phi^{2}(\langle a,z\rangle)}{1-\langle a,z\rangle^{2}}}+\phi(\langle a,z\rangle)a\hskip 9.24994pt\text{ if }\mathds{1}[\langle a,z\rangle\geq\tau^{p}_{d}]\neq b.\end{cases}\end{split}

(3)

To simplify notation, we will denote

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{1-\phi^{2}(\langle a,z\rangle)}{1-\langle a,z\rangle^{2}}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq 1+\psi(\langle a,z\rangle),

\displaystyle s^{b}(\langle a,z\rangle)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathds{1}\Big[\mathds{1}[\langle a,z\rangle\geq\tau^{p}_{d}]\neq b\Big],\text{ and }

\displaystyle\kappa(\langle a,z\rangle)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$($}}}-\langle a,z\rangle-\psi(\langle a,z\rangle)\langle a,z\rangle+\phi(\langle a,z\rangle)\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$)$}}}.

Observation 4.1
.

Let

.
If

, and

, and

\rho^{b}_{a}(Z)\sim\mathsf{Unif}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}).

Proof.

First, we will show that the projection

of

\mathcal{D}_{d}.
By the independence of
a
and
z,
the distribution of the projection of

on
a
is
\mathcal{D}_{d},
which is the same as the mixture
(1-p)\mathcal{D}_{d}|_{[-1,\tau^{p}_{d}]}+p\mathcal{D}_{d}|_{[\tau^{p}_{d},1]}.
As
\phi
measure-preservingly maps
\mathcal{D}_{d}|_{[-1,\tau^{p}_{d}]}
and
\mathcal{D}_{d}|_{[\tau^{p}_{d},1]}
to each other,
\langle a,\rho_{a}^{1}(z)\rangle\sim\mathcal{D}_{d}|_{[\tau^{p}_{d},1]}

\langle a,\rho_{a}^{1}(z)\rangle\sim\mathcal{D}_{d}|_{[-1,\tau^{p}_{d}]}.
As

b

a,z,

\langle a,\rho_{a}^{b}(z)\rangle
has distribution

. The distribution of
\rho_{a}^{b}(z)

in the
a

The distribution in the orthogonal space to

has the same direction as
z
(which is uniform in this orthogonal space). The norm is uniquely determined by

as
\rho_{a}^{b}(z)
is unit norm. By the previous paragraph,
\langle\rho_{a}^{b}(z),a\rangle
has the correct distribution, hence so does the norm and so too the distribution in the orthogonal space.
∎

Proposition 4.1
.

Suppose that

For item 1, if

\,{\rm I}\kern-1.66492pt{\rm P}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}_{d}|_{[-1,\tau^{p}_{d}]}}\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$[$}}}x\leq-\tfrac{100C_{A}\sqrt{\log n}}{\sqrt{d}}\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$]$}}}=\,{\rm I}\kern-1.66492pt{\rm P}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}_{d}}\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$[$}}}x\leq-\tfrac{100C_{A}\sqrt{\log n}}{\sqrt{d}}\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$]$}}}/(1-p)\geq n^{-K},

For item 2,

we have that

Item 3 Follows from the definition of

We end by applying

. Recall that

5
Robust Testing

5.1
The SoS Program and Refutation

We now give the details for the SoS program. Consider the following axiom set over
n

d
-dimensional variables
(Y_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
stacked together in a
n\times d
matrix
Y
and indicator variables
w=(w_{ij})_{1\leq i<j\leq n}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(Y,w)\coloneqq\begin{cases}\langle Y_{i},Y_{i}\rangle=1\;\forall i,\\
w_{i,j}^{2}=w_{i,j}\;\forall i\neq j,\\
(1-w_{i,j})\langle Y_{i},Y_{j}\rangle^{2}\leq(\tau_{d}^{p})^{2}\;\forall i\neq j,\\
\sum_{i,j}w_{i,j}\langle Y_{i},Y_{j}\rangle^{2}\leq\frac{2C_{2}n^{2}p\log(1/p)}{d},\end{cases}

(7)

where
C_{2}
is the constant from
Lemma

2.1
.
One should interpret the condition imposed by variables
\omega_{ij}
as a tail bound on the empirical distribution of

\{\langle Y_{i},Y_{j}\rangle^{2}\}_{1\leq i<j\leq n}.
The constraint essentially reads

\sum_{i,j\;:\;\langle Y_{i},Y_{j}\rangle^{2}\geq(\tau^{p}_{d})^{2}}\langle Y_{i},Y_{j}\rangle^{2}\leq\frac{2C_{2}n^{2}p\log(1/p)}{\sqrt{d}},
which holds w.h.p. when one takes
Y_{i}=V_{i}.

Spectral Refutation Algorithm.

On input a
p
-centered adjacency matrix
A=A_{G},
try to prove in degree
12
SoS that

\Big(\sum_{i,j}A_{i,j}\langle Y_{i},Y_{j}\rangle\Big)^{2}\leq\frac{n^{4}p^{2}\log(1/p)}{8C_{B}^{2}d},
where
C_{B}
is the constant from
Lemma

2.1
. If the algorithm fails, report that
G
is a corrupted
\mathsf{RGG}(n,d,p).
If it succeeds, report that it is a corruption of a graph with small operator norm of its
p
-centered adjacency matrix (e.g.
\mathsf{G}(n,p)
for concreteness).

5.2
Analysis of Corrupted Erdős-Rényi

Let
A
be the centered adjacency matrix of a corrupted
\mathsf{G}(n,p).
Let
A=U+S,
where
U
is the uncorrupted adjacency matrix and
S
is defined by
S=A-U.
The entries of
S
are in
\{-1,0,1\}
and at most
\epsilon p\binom{n}{2}
of them are non-zero (
S
is supported on corrupted entries). Hence,

\begin{split}&\Big(\sum_{i,j}A_{i,j}\langle Y_{i},Y_{j}\rangle\Big)^{2}=\Big(\sum_{i,j}U_{i,j}\langle Y_{i},Y_{j}\rangle+\sum_{i,j}S_{i,j}\langle Y_{i},Y_{j}\rangle\Big)^{2}\\
&=\Big(\sum_{i,j}U_{i,j}\langle Y_{i},Y_{j}\rangle+\sum_{i,j}S_{i,j}w_{i,j}\langle Y_{i},Y_{j}\rangle+\sum_{i,j}S_{i,j}(1-w_{i,j})\langle Y_{i},Y_{j}\rangle\Big)^{2}\\
&\leq 4\Big(\sum_{i,j}U_{i,j}\langle Y_{i},Y_{j}\rangle\Big)^{2}+4\Big(\sum_{i,j}S_{i,j}w_{i,j}\langle Y_{i},Y_{j}\rangle\Big)^{2}+4\Big(\sum_{i,j}S_{i,j}(1-w_{i,j})\langle Y_{i},Y_{j}\rangle\Big)^{2},\end{split}

(8)

where so far all inequalities are in SoS.
We bound each of the three terms separately.

Case 1) Uncorrupted value:

Note that
\sum_{i,j}U_{i,j}\langle Y_{i},Y_{j}\rangle=\langle U,Y^{T}Y\rangle.
Since
U
is the signed adjacency matrix of
\mathsf{G}(n,p),
with high probability
\lambda_{\max}(U)\leq C\max(\sqrt{np},\log n)
for some absolute constant
C
by
Theorem

2.1
. Hence,

C\max(\sqrt{np},\log n)I-U\succeq 0
and, thus, in SoS,

\displaystyle\langle U,YY^{T}\rangle\leq\langle C\max(\sqrt{np},\log n)I,YY^{T}\rangle=C\max(\sqrt{np},\log n)\times\mathsf{tr}(YY^{T})=C\max(\sqrt{np},\log n)\times n.

Similarly,
\langle U,YY^{T}\rangle\geq-C\max(\sqrt{np},\log n)\times n.
Thus, in SoS,

\Big(\sum_{i,j}U_{i,j}\langle Y_{i},Y_{j}\rangle\Big)^{2}\leq C^{2}\max({n^{3}p},n^{2}\log^{2}n).

Case 2)

Corrupted value with large entries. By Cauchy-Schwartz inequality,

\displaystyle\Big(\sum_{i,j}S_{i,j}w_{i,j}\langle Y_{i},Y_{j}\rangle\Big)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\sum_{i,j}S_{i,j}^{2}\Big)\Big(\sum_{i,j}w_{i,j}^{2}\langle Y_{i},Y_{j}\rangle^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\sum_{i,j\;:\;(ij)\text{ corrupted}}1\Big)\Big(\sum_{i,j}w_{i,j}\langle Y_{i},Y_{j}\rangle^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon pn^{2}\times\frac{4C_{2}^{2}n^{2}p\log(1/p)}{d}.

Case 3)

Corrupted value with small entries. Again, by Cauchy-Schwartz,

\displaystyle\Big(\sum_{i,j}S_{i,j}(1-w_{i,j})\langle Y_{i},Y_{j}\rangle\Big)^{2}

\displaystyle=\Big(\sum_{i,j}S^{3}_{i,j}(1-w_{i,j})\langle Y_{i},Y_{j}\rangle\Big)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\sum_{i,j}S_{i,j}^{2}\Big)\Big(S_{i,j}^{4}\sum_{i,j}(1-w_{i,j})^{2}\langle Y_{i},Y_{j}\rangle^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon pn^{2}\times\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}S_{i,j}^{4}(\tau^{p}_{d})^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon pn^{2}\times\epsilon pn^{2}\times(\tau^{p}_{d})^{2}\leq\frac{C_{A}^{2}\epsilon^{2}n^{4}p^{2}\log(1/p)}{d},

using
Lemma

2.1
.
Altogether, for some absolute constant
C^{\prime},
when
A
is an
\epsilon
-corruption of
\mathsf{G}(n,p),
with high probability there is a degree 12 proof in SoS that

\displaystyle\Big(\sum_{i,j}A_{i,j}\langle Y_{i},Y_{j}\rangle\Big)^{2}\leq C^{\prime}\Big(\max(n^{3}p,n^{2}\log^{2}n)+\frac{\epsilon n^{4}p^{2}\log(1/p)}{d}\Big).

(9)

5.3
Analysis of Corrupted RGG

We will show that with high probability over
\mathsf{RGG}(n,d,p),
for no
\epsilon
-corruption
A
can one prove
\Big(\sum_{i,j}A_{i,j}\langle Y_{i},Y_{j}\rangle\Big)^{2}\leq\frac{n^{4}p^{2}\log(1/p)}{d}.
We simply need to exhibit a pair
(Y,w)
that satisfies the axioms
\mathcal{A}(Y,w)
and
\Big(\sum_{i,j}A_{i,j}\langle Y_{i},Y_{j}\rangle\Big)^{2}\geq\frac{n^{4}p^{2}\log(1/p)}{8C_{B}^{2}d}.

Let
Y_{i}=V_{i},1\leq i\leq n,
where this is the true embedding that produced
A.
Let
w_{i,j}=\mathds{1}[\langle V_{i},V_{j}\rangle^{2}\geq(\tau^{p}_{d})^{2}].

Feasibility.

Clearly, the first three axioms are satisfied. For the last one,

\displaystyle\E_{V_{1},V_{2},\ldots,V_{n}\sim_{iid}\mathsf{Unif}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})}\Big[\sum_{i\neq j}\mathds{1}[\langle V_{i},V_{j}\rangle^{2}\geq(\tau^{p}_{d})^{2}]\times\langle V_{i},V_{j}\rangle^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle\leq n^{2}\E_{V_{1},V_{2}\sim_{iid}\mathsf{Unif}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})}\Big[\mathds{1}[\langle V_{1},V_{2}\rangle^{2}\geq(\tau^{p}_{d})^{2}]\times\langle V_{1},V_{2}\rangle^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle=n^{2}\E_{V_{1},V_{2}\sim_{iid}\mathsf{Unif}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})}\Big[\langle V_{1},V_{2}\rangle^{2}|\langle V_{1},V_{2}\rangle^{2}\geq(\tau^{p}_{d})^{2}\Big]\times\,{\rm I}\kern-1.66492pt{\rm P}\Big[\langle V_{1},V_{2}\rangle^{2}\geq(\tau^{p}_{d})^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle\leq n^{2}p\times\frac{C_{2}^{2}\log(1/p)}{d}

by
Lemma

2.1
. Furthermore, the value concentrates extremely well as the collection of random variables
\{\mathds{1}[\langle V_{i},V_{j}\rangle^{2}\geq(\tau^{p}_{d})^{2}]\times\langle V_{i},V_{j}\rangle^{2}\}_{1\leq i<j\leq n}
is pairwise independent! (Note that
\langle V_{1},V_{2}\rangle
and
\langle V_{2},V_{3}\rangle
are independent.) Hence,

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}_{V_{1},V_{2},\ldots,V_{n}\sim_{iid}\mathsf{Unif}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})}\Big[\sum_{i\neq j}\mathds{1}[\langle V_{i},V_{j}\rangle^{2}\geq(\tau^{p}_{d})^{2}]\times\langle V_{i},V_{j}\rangle^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle\leq n^{2}\mathbb{V}_{V_{1},V_{2}\sim_{iid}\mathsf{Unif}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})}\Big[\mathds{1}[\langle V_{1},V_{2}\rangle^{2}\geq(\tau^{p}_{d})^{2}]\times\langle V_{1},V_{2}\rangle^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle\leq n^{2}\E_{V_{1},V_{2}\sim_{iid}\mathsf{Unif}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})}\Big[\langle V_{1},V_{2}\rangle^{4}|\langle V_{1},V_{2}\rangle^{2}\geq(\tau^{p}_{d})^{2}\Big]\times\,{\rm I}\kern-1.66492pt{\rm P}\Big[\langle V_{1},V_{2}\rangle^{2}\geq(\tau^{p}_{d})^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle\leq n^{2}p\times\frac{C_{4}\log(1/p)^{2}}{d^{2}}=o\bigg(\Big(n^{2}p\times\frac{C_{2}^{2}\log(1/p)}{d}\Big)^{2}\bigg).

Hence,

\sum_{i\neq j}\mathds{1}[\langle V_{i},V_{j}\rangle^{2}\geq(\tau^{p}_{d})^{2}]\times\langle V_{i},V_{j}\rangle^{2}\leq\frac{2C^{2}_{2}n^{2}p\log(1/p)}{d}
w.h.p. by Chebyshev’s inequality.

Objective Value.

Finally, we need to bound
\sum_{i,j}A_{i,j}\langle V_{i},V_{j}\rangle.
As in the Erdős-Rényi case,

\displaystyle\Big|\sum_{i,j}S_{i,j}\langle V_{i},V_{j}\rangle\Big|\leq 2\sqrt{\Big(\sum_{i,j}S_{i,j}w_{i,j}\langle V_{i},V_{j}\rangle\Big)^{2}+\Big(\sum_{i,j}S_{i,j}(1-w_{i,j})\langle Y_{i},Y_{j}\rangle\Big)^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq 2\sqrt{\epsilon pn^{2}\times\frac{4C_{2}^{2}n^{2}p\log(1/p)}{d}+\frac{C_{A}^{2}\epsilon^{2}n^{4}p^{2}\log(1/p)}{d}}\leq\frac{C^{\prime\prime}\sqrt{\epsilon}n^{2}p\sqrt{\log(1/p)}}{\sqrt{d}}.

Hence, we only need to show that
\sum_{i,j}U_{i,j}\langle V_{i},V_{j}\rangle
is large. Again, as the different summands are pairwise independent and this value concentrates well, we only consider the expectation:

\displaystyle\E\Big[\sum_{i,j}U_{i,j}\langle V_{i},V_{j}\rangle\Big]

\displaystyle=n(n-1)\E\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$[$}}}\mathds{1}[\langle V_{i},V_{j}\rangle\geq\tau^{p}_{d}]\langle V_{i},V_{j}\rangle\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$]$}}}

\displaystyle=n(n-1)\E\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$[$}}}\langle V_{i},V_{j}\rangle|\langle V_{i},V_{j}\rangle\geq\tau^{p}_{d}\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$]$}}}\times\,{\rm I}\kern-1.66492pt{\rm P}\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$[$}}}\langle V_{i},V_{j}\rangle\geq\tau^{p}_{d}\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$]$}}}

\displaystyle\geq n(n-1)p\E\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$[$}}}\langle V_{i},V_{j}\rangle|\langle V_{i},V_{j}\rangle\geq\tau^{p}_{d}\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$]$}}}\geq\frac{n^{2}p\sqrt{\log(1/p)}}{2C_{B}\sqrt{d}},

the last inequality by
Lemma

2.1
.
Hence,
\sum_{i,j}A_{i,j}\langle V_{i},V_{j}\rangle\geq\frac{n^{2}p\sqrt{\log(1/p)}}{2C_{B}\sqrt{d}}-\frac{C^{\prime\prime}\sqrt{\epsilon}n^{2}p\sqrt{\log(1/p)}}{\sqrt{d}}
and so

\Big(\sum_{i,j}A_{i,j}\langle V_{i},V_{j}\rangle\Big)^{2}\geq\frac{n^{4}p^{2}\log(1/p)}{8C^{2}_{B}d}
for all
\epsilon\leq K_{r}^{\prime}
for some absolute constant
K_{r}^{\prime}.

Putting It All Together

By the previous two sections, as long as
\frac{n^{4}p^{2}\log(1/p)}{8C^{2}_{B}d}\geq C^{\prime}\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$($}}}\max(n^{3}p,n^{2}\log^{2}n)+\frac{\epsilon n^{4}p^{2}\log(1/p)}{d}\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$)$}}},

C.

\exp(2n^{2}p\epsilon\log(1/\epsilon)).

With probability more than
1/2,
it is the case that

H
is an edge-subgraph of
G
by
Corollary

6.1
and
G
has at most
2\binom{n}{2}p\leq n^{2}p
edges (again, edges of random geometric graphs are pairwise-independent). Let
B
be the support of
\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{S}^{d-1},p(1+\delta)
restricted to graphs on at most
n^{2}p
edges. Hence, the number of edge-subgraphs of graphs in

which differ in at most
\epsilon n^{2}p
edges is at most

by

6.3
. By

6.2
,

|B|\times\exp(o(n^{2}p\log(1/p)))\geq\exp(\frac{1}{6}n^{2}p\log(1/p)).

∎

RGG and the FKG Lattice Condition

A measure
\mu
over
\{0,1\}^{N}
is strictly positive if
\mu(\omega)>0
for each
\omega\in\{0,1\}^{n}.
A strictly positive measure satisfies the FKG lattice condition if for any two
\omega_{1},\omega_{2}\in\{0,1\}^{N},
it holds that
\mu(\omega_{1}\vee\omega_{2})\mu(\omega_{1}\wedge\omega_{2})\geq\mu(\omega_{1})\mu(\omega_{2})

[
GG06
]
. We denote pointwise maximum with
\vee
and pointwise minimum with
\wedge.

Proposition 7.1
.

Suppose that
d\geq 3.
Then, the measure corresponding to
\mathsf{RGG}(3,\mathbb{S}^{d-1},1/2)
is strictly positive but does not satisfy the FKG lattice property.

Proof.

Denote by
\mu
the measure corresponding to
\mathsf{RGG}(3,\mathbb{S}^{d-1},1/2).
Due to symmetry, note that
\mu(\omega)
only depends on
|\omega|.
For simplicity of notation, we will also write
\mu(|\omega|)
for
\mu(\omega).

We will prove that for some
a\in(0,1/8),
it is the case that

\displaystyle\mu(3)=1/8+a,\mu(2)=1/8-a,\mu(1)=1/8+a,\mu(0)=1/8-a.

(11)

This is enough to show that the FKG lattice condition does not hold. Indeed, take
\omega_{1}=\{(1,2)\},\omega_{2}=\{(2,3)\}.
Then,

\omega_{1}\vee\omega_{2}=\{(1,2),(2,3)\},\omega_{1}\wedge\omega_{2}=\emptyset.
For the FKG lattice property to hold, it must be the case that
\mu(2)\mu(0)\geq\mu(1)^{2}.
This, however, is false as the left hand-side is
(1/8-a)^{2}
while the right hand-side is
(1/8+a)^{2}.

Going back to
Eq.

11
. Let
\mu(3)=1/8+a,
where
a
is for now some number that we will later prove to be strictly positive.
First, note that

\mu(3)+\mu(2)=\,{\rm I}\kern-1.66492pt{\rm P}[\mathbf{G}_{12}=1,\mathbf{G}_{13}=1,\mathbf{G}_{23}=1]+\,{\rm I}\kern-1.66492pt{\rm P}[\mathbf{G}_{12}=1,\mathbf{G}_{13}=1,\mathbf{G}_{23}=0]=\,{\rm I}\kern-1.66492pt{\rm P}[\mathbf{G}_{12}=1,\mathbf{G}_{13}=1].

The last expression equals
1/4
as every two edges in
{\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{S}^{d-1},p)}
are pairwise independent. Hence,
\mu(2)=1/4-\mu(3)=1/8-a.
Similarly,
\mu(1)=1/4-\mu(2)=1/8+a
as

\mu(2)+\mu(1)=\,{\rm I}\kern-1.66492pt{\rm P}[\mathbf{G}_{12}=1,\mathbf{G}_{13}=1,\mathbf{G}_{23}=0]+\,{\rm I}\kern-1.66492pt{\rm P}[\mathbf{G}_{12}=1,\mathbf{G}_{13}=0,\mathbf{G}_{23}=0]=\,{\rm I}\kern-1.66492pt{\rm P}[\mathbf{G}_{12}=1,\mathbf{G}_{23}=0],

and
\mu(0)=1/4-\mu(1)=1/8-a
as

\mu(1)+\mu(0)=\,{\rm I}\kern-1.66492pt{\rm P}[\mathbf{G}_{12}=1,\mathbf{G}_{13}=0,\mathbf{G}_{23}=0]+\,{\rm I}\kern-1.66492pt{\rm P}[\mathbf{G}_{12}=0,\mathbf{G}_{13}=0,\mathbf{G}_{23}=0]=\,{\rm I}\kern-1.66492pt{\rm P}[\mathbf{G}_{13}=0,\mathbf{G}_{23}=0].

Thus, all that is left to show is that
a\geq 0.

We accomplish this by bounding
\mu(3).

\displaystyle\mu(3)=\,{\rm I}\kern-1.66492pt{\rm P}[\mathbf{G}_{12}=1,\mathbf{G}_{13}=1,\mathbf{G}_{23}=1]

\displaystyle=\,{\rm I}\kern-1.66492pt{\rm P}[\mathbf{G}_{23}=1|\mathbf{G}_{12}=1,\mathbf{G}_{13}=1]\times\,{\rm I}\kern-1.66492pt{\rm P}[\mathbf{G}_{12}=1,\mathbf{G}_{13}=1]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\,{\rm I}\kern-1.66492pt{\rm P}[\mathbf{G}_{23}=1|\mathbf{G}_{12}=1,\mathbf{G}_{13}=1]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\,{\rm I}\kern-1.66492pt{\rm P}[\langle V_{2},V_{3}\rangle\geq 0|\langle V_{1},V_{2}\rangle\geq 0,\langle V_{1},V_{3}\rangle\geq 0].

First, note that this probability is the same if
V_{1},V_{2},V_{3}
were isotropic Gaussian vectors instead of spherical - the sign of inner products depends only on the direction but not on the magnitude of vectors.
Due to rotational invariance, we can assume that
V_{1}\in\mathsf{span}\{e_{1}\},
so
V_{1}=z_{1,1}e_{1},
where
z_{1,1}>0,
and
V_{2}\in\mathsf{span}\{e_{1},e_{2}\}.
So,
V_{2}=z_{2,1}e_{1}+z_{2,2}e_{2}.
Finally, let
V_{3}=z_{3,1}e_{1}+z_{3,2}e_{2}+z_{3,3}e_{3}.
Since the components of an isotropic Gaussian are are independent in different directions, all of the numbers
z_{1,1},z_{2,1},z_{2,2},z_{3,1},z_{3,2},z_{3,3}
are independent, Furthermore,
z_{2,1},z_{3,1},z_{3,2}
are standard Gaussians and

z_{1,1},z_{2,2},z_{3,3}>0
as these are norms of certain projections of the Gaussian vectors.
We want to show that

\,{\rm I}\kern-1.66492pt{\rm P}[z_{3,1}z_{2,1}+z_{3,2}z_{2,2}\geq 0|z_{3,1}\geq 0,z_{2,1}\geq 0]\in(1/2,1).

We compute

\displaystyle\,{\rm I}\kern-1.66492pt{\rm P}[z_{3,1}z_{2,1}+z_{3,2}z_{2,2}\geq 0|z_{3,1}\geq 0,z_{2,1}\geq 0]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{+\infty}\,{\rm I}\kern-1.66492pt{\rm P}[z_{3,1}z_{2,1}+z_{3,2}z_{2,2}\geq 0|z_{3,1}\geq 0,z_{2,1}\geq 0,z_{3,1}z_{2,1}=x]\times\,{\rm I}\kern-1.66492pt{\rm P}[z_{3,1}z_{2,1}=x|z_{3,1}\geq 0,z_{2,1}\geq 0]dx

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{+\infty}\,{\rm I}\kern-1.66492pt{\rm P}[z_{3,2}\geq-x/z_{2,2}]\times\,{\rm I}\kern-1.66492pt{\rm P}[z_{3,1}z_{2,1}=x|z_{3,1}\geq 0,z_{2,1}\geq 0]dx.

Note that for each
x,z_{2,2}\geq 0,
it holds that

\,{\rm I}\kern-1.66492pt{\rm P}[z_{3,2}\geq-x/z_{2,2}]\in(1/2,1)
as
z_{3,2}
is a standard Gaussian and
-x/z_{2,2}<0.
The conclusion follows.
∎

8
Discussion and Open Problems

8.1
Robust Testing With Fewer Corruptions

One of the main results of the current paper is settling (up to log factors) the regime in which testing between
\mathsf{G}(n,p)
and
{\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{S}^{d-1},p)}
with
\epsilon\binom{n}{2}p
corruptions is possible when
\epsilon>0
is a small constant. We show via
Theorems

1.1
and
1.3
that the threshold is
d=\widetilde{\Theta}(np)
both computationally and information-theoretically. The analogous question without corruptions, i.e.
\epsilon=0,
is still open as discussed in the introduction. It is natural to interpolate between
\epsilon=0
and
\epsilon=\Theta(1).

Problem 2
.

Given are
n,d\in\mathbb{N},

p\in[0,1]
and a function
\epsilon(n,p)\geq 0
such that
\epsilon(n,p)\leq K_{r}
for some universal constant
K_{r}.

Consider the following hypothesis testing problem. One observes a graph
H
and needs to decide between
H_{0}:H=\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{G}),\mathbf{G}\sim\mathsf{G}(n,p)
and

H_{1}:H=\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{G}),\mathbf{G}\sim\mathsf{RGG}(n,d,p).
Here,
\mathcal{A}:\{0,1\}^{\binom{n}{2}}\longrightarrow\{0,1\}^{\binom{n}{2}}
is any unknown mapping such that

\mathcal{A}(G)
and
G
differ in at most
\epsilon(n,p)\times\binom{n}{2}p
entries for any adjacency matrix
G\in\{0,1\}^{\binom{n}{2}}.

We expect that this question will require both novel algorithmic and lower-bound ideas. Neither the spectral refutation algorithm (when
\epsilon=\Theta(1)
) nor the signed triangle count algorithm (when
\epsilon=0
) seems to generalize trivially. Regarding lower-bounds, even the case of no corruptions is still open. Particularly interesting is whether there is a statistical-computational gap for any
\epsilon(n,p).
There is no evidence for such a gap when
\epsilon(n,p)=0
and
\epsilon(n,p)=\Theta(1).

Theorems

1.1
and
1.1
show that if
\epsilon(n,p)\geq C\frac{\max(\sqrt{np},\sqrt{\log n})(\log n)^{2}}{\sqrt{d}}
for some absolute constant
C,
the distinguishing problem is impossible information theoretically against the (efficient)
adversary from
Theorem

1.1
. We do not expect this to be tight for all values of
\epsilon
(in particular, when
\epsilon
approaches 0). This naturally also raises a question about the optimality of
Theorems

1.1
and
1.1
. We only phrase the question for the latter.

Problem 3
.

What is the smallest value of
\delta(d,n,p)
for which there exists a coupling of
G\sim\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{S}^{d-1},p),

H_{-}\sim\mathsf{G}(n,p(1-\delta(d,n,p)))
and
H_{+}\sim\mathsf{G}(n,p(1+\delta(d,n,p))),

such that

H_{-}\subseteq G\subseteq H_{+}

with high-probability.

New Algorithms For Embedding.

Theorem

1.1
can also be viewed as an approximate embedding of a sample
H
from
\mathsf{G}(n,p)
as a geometric graph. This naturally raises the question whether one can use

n

Robust Embedding.

A different algorithmic question is whether there exist embedding algorithms in the setting of adversarial corruptions. One way to phrase this question (using a Frobenius norm error) is:

Problem 4
.

Let
V=(V_{1},V_{2},\ldots,V_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}

\lim_{\epsilon\longrightarrow 0}f(\epsilon)=0.

The case of no corruptions (i.e.
\epsilon=0
) is treated in
[
LS23
]
via a spectral algorithm which succeeds whenever
d=o(np).
