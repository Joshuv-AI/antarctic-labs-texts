Inferring physical properties of symmetric states from the fewest copies
Da-Jian Zhang, D. M. Tong

Abstract
Learning physical properties of high-dimensional states is crucial for developing quantum technologies but usually consumes an exceedingly large number of samples which are difficult to afford in practice. In this Letter, we use the methodology of quantum metrology to tackle this difficulty, proposing a strategy built upon entangled measurements for dramatically reducing sample complexity. The strategy, whose characteristic feature is symmetrization of observables, is powered by the exploration of symmetric structures of states which are ubiquitous in physics. It is provably optimal under some natural assumption, efficiently implementable in a variety of contexts, and capable of being incorporated into existing methods as a basic building block. We apply the strategy to different scenarios motivated by experiments, demonstrating exponential reductions in sample complexity.



Learning properties of states like expectation values of observables is of increasing importance in quantum physics, underpinning vast applications in emerging fields, such as quantum machine learning
Biamonte et al. 2017
, quantum computational chemistry
McArdle et al. 2020
, and variational quantum computing
Cerezo et al. 2021
;
Tilly et al. 2022
. A fundamental difficulty in this task is that
any full reconstruction of a generic unknown state inevitably
consumes exponentially many samples due to the growth of the state space dimension with the system size. This makes traditional learning methods like full state tomography hopelessly inefficient and poses a serious challenge in the Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum (NISQ) era
Preskill 2018
.

Fortunately, most of the states that are relevant in quantum physics admit some nontrivial structures, such as those of low rank
Gross et al. 2010
;
Liu et al. 2012
, matrix product states
Cramer et al. 2010
;
Baumgratz et al. 2013
, and those with quasi-local structures
Anshu et al. 2021
;
Rouzé and França 2024
.
Furthermore, for many purposes, one is only interested in some specific properties of states, e.g., mean energies of many-body systems, which makes it unnecessary to fully reconstruct states for obtaining all their information. These well-motivated physical considerations have led to a series of proposals for more efficient alternatives to full state tomography, with excellent examples including compressed sensing
Gross et al. 2010
;
Liu et al. 2012
, adaptive tomography
Mahler et al. 2013
;
Qi et al. 2017
, self-guided tomography
Ferrie 2014
;
Rambach et al. 2021
, and classical shadows
Aaronson 2018
;
Huang et al. 2020
.

While existing methods generally rely on local measurements, it has been recognized that entangled measurements are typically far more efficient than local measurements for extracting information from unknown states
Peres and Wootters 1991
;
Gisin and Popescu 1999
;
et al. 2022a
;
Aharonov et al. 2022
;
Miguel-Ramiro et al. 2022
;
Larocca et al. 2022
. Moreover, the ongoing development of large-scale quantum computers opens up exciting possibilities of leveraging quantum computational resources to realize entangled measurements
et al. 2022a
;
Aharonov et al. 2022
. In particular, motivated by the availability of NISQ computers
et al. 2022b
, a number of experiments
Tang et al. 2020
;
Huang et al. 2022
;
Conlon et al. 2023
have been carried out for realizing entangled measurements as well as demonstrating their superiority over local measurements. An important issue following is therefore to explore the usefulness of entangled measurements in learning properties of states
et al. 2022a
;
Aharonov et al. 2022
.

Here we propose a strategy built upon entangled measurements, for dramatically reducing sample complexity beyond what can be achieved with local measurements. The basic idea is to explore symmetric structures of states which are ubiquitous in quantum physics and known
a priori
in various situations. Using information-theoretic tools from quantum metrology
Helstrom 1976
;
Holevo 2011
, we figure out the measurement that can make optimal use of these symmetric structures for learning expectation values of observables. This enables our strategy to operate at the optimal sample efficiency in a variety of physical contexts, thereby achieving a sought-after goal in quantum metrology
Braunstein and Caves 1994
;
Zhang and Gong 2020
;
Zhang and Tong 2022
, which is unlikely, if not impossible, to reach with local measurements.
Further, we apply our strategy to two physical contexts involving translational and permutational symmetries, showing that it allows for saving exponentially many samples while merely consuming polynomial amounts of quantum computational resources. The findings of this Letter uncover an intriguing route to reducing sample complexity via taking advantage of symmetric structures of states, which opens opportunities for leveraging recent breakthroughs on large-scale quantum computers to facilitate a plethora of learning tasks.

We first recall some basic facts. A common task in quantum physics is to measure the expectation value
\overline{X}=\tr(X\rho)
of an observable
X
in a state
\rho
.
Traditionally,
\overline{X}
is obtained from the projective measurement of
X
, provided that this measurement is not difficult to implement in experiments. As the measurement outcomes are the eigenvalues of
X
, there is some uncertainty in obtaining
\overline{X}
, which can be characterized by the variance
(\Delta X)^{2}=\overline{(X-\overline{X})^{2}}
. More precisely, the uncertainty is
(\Delta X)^{2}/M
if the measurement is repeated
M
times. So, to measure
\overline{X}
up to a certain prescribed tolerance
\epsilon
, the number of measurements needed and hence samples consumed is

\displaystyle M_{X}=\left\lceil\frac{(\Delta X)^{2}}{\epsilon}\right\rceil,

(1)

where
\lceil\cdot\rceil
denotes the ceiling function.

We now consider the setting that we know
a priori
the symmetric structures of
\rho
, which can be described by a finite or compact Lie group
G
, that is,
U_{g}\rho U_{g}^{\dagger}=\rho
for
g\in G
, where
U_{g}
denotes a unitary representation of
{G}
. It is worth noting that the above consideration covers the vast majority of symmetries of interest in quantum physics.
Then a question of relevance in a startling variety of contexts is: How can we exploit the symmetric structures of
\rho
to reduce
to the greatest extent
the number of samples required in measuring the expectation value
\overline{X}
of
any given observable

X
in
\rho
up to the prescribed tolerance
\epsilon
?

In this Letter, we will answer the above question by showing that
the projective measurement of another observable

\displaystyle Y=\mathcal{T}(X)

(2)

can not only be alternatively employed to measure
\overline{X}
but also maximally reduce the number of samples required by taking advantage of the symmetric structures of
\rho
.
Here,
\mathcal{T}
is the so-called
G
-twirling operation defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}(X)={\absolutevalue{G}}^{-1}\sum_{g\in G}U_{g}XU_{g}^{\dagger},

(3)

for a finite group
G
, with
\absolutevalue{G}
being the number of elements in
G
. When
G
is a compact Lie group,

\mathcal{T}(X)=\int_{G}\mathrm{d}\nu(g)U_{g}XU_{g}^{\dagger}
, where
\nu(g)
denotes the normalized Haar measure
Halmos 1950
.
It is interesting to note that
Y
satisfies
[Y,U_{g}]=0
for
g\in G
and is therefore the symmetrized counterpart of
X
.
We emphasize that
Y
is always different from
X
except when
X
commutes with all
U_{g}
.

We first show that
X
and
Y
have the following subtle relations.

Theorem 1
.

The observable
Y
shares the same expected value with
X
but with a generally smaller variance, i.e.,
\overline{Y}=\overline{X}
but
(\Delta Y)^{2}\leq(\Delta X)^{2}
.

Physically, the equality
\overline{Y}=\overline{X}
means that the projective measurement of
Y
can be an alternative to the projective measurement of
X
for obtaining
\overline{X}
. The inequality
(\Delta Y)^{2}\leq(\Delta X)^{2}
implies that the former generally consumes fewer samples than the latter for reaching the same measurement precision, that is,
M_{Y}\leq M_{X}
, where

\displaystyle M_{Y}=\left\lceil\frac{(\Delta Y)^{2}}{\epsilon}\right\rceil

(4)

is the number of samples required in the projective measurement of
Y
.

We now prove Theorem
1
. Let us consider the case that
G
is a finite group. Using the cyclic property of the trace and noting that
[\rho,U_{g}]=0
for
g\in G
, we have
\overline{U_{g}XU_{g}^{\dagger}}=\overline{X}
. In conjunction with Eq. (
3
), this further leads to
\overline{Y}=\sum_{g}\overline{U_{g}XU_{g}^{\dagger}}/\absolutevalue{G}=\overline{X}
. To prove
(\Delta Y)^{2}\leq(\Delta X)^{2}
, we make use of the known result that, for two observables
A
and
B
, there is
\Delta(A+B)\leq\Delta A+\Delta B

Pati and Sahu 2007
;
Maccone and Pati 2014
. Using this result and noting that
\Delta(U_{g}XU_{g}^{\dagger})=\Delta X
, we have that
\Delta Y=\Delta(\absolutevalue{G}^{-1}\sum_{g}U_{g}XU_{g}^{\dagger})\leq\absolutevalue{G}^{-1}\sum_{g}\Delta(U_{g}XU_{g}^{\dagger})=\Delta X
. Then the proof of Theorem
1
is completed by further noting that the above reasoning can be carried over straightforwardly to the case of
G
being a compact Lie group.

We then address the optimality of the projective measurement of
Y
. Note that there are many other measurement strategies that can be used to measure
\overline{X}
, e.g., classical shadows based on randomized measurements
Aaronson 2018
;
Huang et al. 2020
.
Without loss of generality, any strategy for measuring
\overline{X}
can be described as first performing a measurement on
\rho^{\otimes M}
and then classically post-processing the measurement outcome to yield an estimate of
\overline{X}

Helstrom 1976
;
Holevo 2011
. Here
M
denotes the number of samples consumed in the strategy in question. Any measurement can be described by a positive operator-valued measure (POVM)
\{\Pi_{\bm{x}}\}
satisfying
\sum_{\bm{x}}\Pi_{\bm{x}}=\openone
, where
\bm{x}
labels the measurement outcome and could be multivariate in general. Any classical post-processing of
\bm{x}
amounts to a map
\overline{X}_{\textrm{est}}(\bm{x})
, which takes
\bm{x}
as the input and outputs an estimate of
\overline{X}
. The uncertainty in measuring
\overline{X}
can be quantified by the variance
\sum_{\bm{x}}\tr(\Pi_{\bm{x}}\rho^{\otimes M})\left[\overline{X}_{\textrm{est}}(\bm{x})-\overline{X}\right]^{2}

Helstrom 1976
;
Holevo 2011
. The requirement of measuring
\overline{X}
up to
\epsilon
amounts to demanding

\displaystyle\epsilon\geq\sum_{\bm{x}}\tr(\Pi_{\bm{x}}\rho^{\otimes M})\left[\overline{X}_{\textrm{est}}(\bm{x})-\overline{X}\right]^{2},

(5)

which imposes constraints on
M
so that
M
cannot be arbitrarily small. We uncover a fundamental bound on
M
as follows.

Theorem 2
.

The number
M
of samples consumed in any strategy for measuring
\overline{X}
up to
\epsilon
is bounded as
M\geq M_{Y}
if nothing about
\rho
is known except its symmetric structures.

Theorem
2
means that the projective measurement of
Y
consumes the fewest copies of
\rho
for measuring
\overline{X}
up to
\epsilon
, under the assumption that our knowledge about
\rho
is only its symmetric structures. That is, the projective measurement of
Y
has the optimal sample efficiency allowed by quantum mechanics whenever the assumption holds.

Our proof of Theorem
2
is based on the quantum Cramér-Rao bound
Helstrom 1976
;
Holevo 2011
(see Supplemental Material
SM
for details). To calculate this bound, the key step in our proof is to write out a general expression of
\rho
based on the known symmetric structures. According to the representation theory of groups
Curtis and Reiner 1962
,
U_{g}
can be expressed as

\displaystyle U_{g}\cong\bigoplus_{\alpha=1}^{s}\openone_{n_{\alpha}}\otimes U_{\alpha}(g),

(6)

where
U_{\alpha}(g)
is the
\alpha
-th irreducible representation of
G
with dimension
d_{\alpha}
and multiplicity
n_{\alpha}
, and
\openone_{n_{\alpha}}
is the
n_{\alpha}\times n_{\alpha}
identity matrix. As
[\rho,U_{g}]=0
for
g\in G
,
\rho
must be of the form

\displaystyle\rho\cong\bigoplus_{\alpha=1}^{s}q_{\alpha}\rho_{\alpha}\otimes\frac{\openone_{d_{\alpha}}}{d_{\alpha}},

(7)

where
q_{\alpha}\geq 0
satisfies
\sum_{\alpha=1}^{s}q_{\alpha}=1
and
\rho_{\alpha}
denotes a density matrix. Using the generalized Bloch representation
Kimura 2003
, we can further express
\rho_{\alpha}
as (see Sec. II of Ref.
SM
)

\displaystyle\rho_{\alpha}=\frac{\openone_{n_{\alpha}}}{n_{\alpha}}+\frac{1}{2}\bm{r}_{\alpha}\cdot\bm{\lambda}_{\alpha},

(8)

where
\bm{r}_{\alpha}
and
\bm{\lambda_{\alpha}}
are used to denote the generalized Bloch vector and the generators of Lie algebra
\mathfrak{su}(n_{\alpha})
, respectively
SM
.
It follows from Eqs. (
7
) and (
8
) that
\rho
is explicitly expressed in terms of the parameters
\{q_{\alpha},\bm{r}_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha=1}^{s}
.
This allows us to figure out the quantum Cramér-Rao bound,

\displaystyle\sum_{\bm{x}}\tr(\Pi_{\bm{x}}\rho^{\otimes M})\left[\overline{X}_{\textrm{est}}(\bm{x})-\overline{X}\right]^{2}\geq\frac{1}{MJ[\overline{X};\rho]},

(9)

where
J[\overline{X};\rho]
denotes the quantum Fisher information about
\overline{X}
given
\rho
(see Sec. V of Ref.
SM
for its explicit expression). The meaning of Eq. (
9
) is that the uncertainty in any strategy for measuring
\overline{X}
is fundamentally constrained by
\frac{1}{MJ[\overline{X};\rho]}
, irrespective of the choices of
\{\Pi_{\bm{x}}\}
and
\overline{X}_{\textrm{est}}(\bm{x})

Helstrom 1976
;
Holevo 2011
.
Using Eqs. (
5
) and (
9
), we obtain

\displaystyle M\geq\frac{1}{\epsilon J[\overline{X};\rho]}.

(10)

Besides, we derive, after some algebra (see Sec. VI of Ref.
SM
),

\displaystyle(\Delta Y)^{2}=\frac{1}{J[\overline{X};\rho]}.

(11)

The proof of Theorem
2
is completed by inserting Eq. (
11
) into Eq. (
10
).

With the above analysis, we are ready to specify our strategy.
To measure
\overline{X}
, our strategy is to repeatedly perform the projective measurement of
Y
on
M_{Y}
samples.
This allows for yielding
\overline{X}
up to
\epsilon
after averaging the
M_{Y}
outcomes, i.e.,
\overline{X}\overset{\epsilon}{\approx}\sum_{i=1}^{M_{Y}}y_{i}/M_{Y}
, where
y_{i}
denotes the
i
-th measurement outcome and is an eigenvalue of
Y
. Interestingly, as demonstrated below, the projective measurement of
Y
is often an entangled measurement
Tang et al. 2020
;
Huang et al. 2022
;
Conlon et al. 2023
, since the eigenbasis of
Y
may contain entangled states. To implement this measurement, we can first apply a quantum circuit
V
to transform the eigenbasis of
Y
into the computational basis and then perform the standard measurement in the computational basis (see Fig.
1
). So, the working principle of our strategy is to leverage quantum computational resources to reduce sample complexity.
Notably, our strategy operates at the optimal sample efficiency allowed by quantum mechanics and outperforms other measurement strategies in terms of sample complexity whenever the assumption in Theorem
2
holds.

Figure 1:
Schematic of our strategy. To measure the expectation value
\overline{X}
of an observable
X
in
\rho
which is possibly a many-body state, we perform the projective measurement of the observable
Y
on each sample. This measurement can be implemented by utilizing the quantum circuit
V
to transform the eigenbasis of
Y
into the computational basis and then performing the standard measurement in the computational basis. Repeating this procedure
M_{Y}=\lceil(\Delta Y)^{2}/\epsilon\rceil
times, we can obtain
\overline{X}
up to a prescribed tolerance
\epsilon
by averaging the outcomes
y_{i}
.

Application 1: translational symmetries.
Let us apply our strategy to the scenario that
\rho
is a state of
n
qubits with the symmetric structures described by the translation group
G=\{T^{i},i=0,\cdots,2^{n}-1\}
. Here,
T
is defined as
T\ket{j}=\ket{j+1}
with the periodic boundary condition
\ket{2^n}=\ket{0}
, where
\ket{j}
,
j=0,\cdots,2^{n}-1
, denote the computational basis. Note that translational symmetries are ubiquitous in condensed-matter physics. The above scenario could arise, e.g., in quantum simulations of electrons in crystalline solids
Bloch et al. 2012
;
Georgescu et al. 2014
, for which Bloch’s theorem states that the solutions to the Schrödinger equations are Bloch states and hence respect translational symmetries.

To show the usefulness of our strategy, we calculate
(\Delta X)^{2}/(\Delta Y)^{2}
, which characterizes the ratio between
M_{X}
and
M_{Y}
. Using
[\rho,T]=0
and noting that the eigenbasis of
T
is the Fourier basis
\ket{f_j}=\sum_{k}e^{2\pi\mathrm{i}jk/2^{n}}\ket{k}/{\sqrt{2^{n}}}
, we can write
\rho
as
\rho=\sum_{j}p_{j}\ket{f_j}\bra{f_j}
, where
p_{j}\geq 0
satisfies
\sum_{j}p_{j}=1
. Then, expressing
X
as

X=\sum_{jk}X_{jk}\ket{f_j}\bra{f_k}

with
X_{jk}=\bra{f_j}X\ket{f_k}
, we have

\displaystyle(\Delta X)^{2}=(\sum_{jk}p_{j}\absolutevalue{X_{jk}}^{2})-(\sum_{j}p_{j}X_{jj})^{2}.

(12)

Besides, using Eq. (
3
) and
T\ket{f_j}=e^{-2\pi\mathrm{i}j/2^{n}}\ket{f_j}
, we have

Y=\sum_{j}X_{jj}\ket{f_j}\bra{f_j}
,
which further leads to

\displaystyle(\Delta Y)^{2}=(\sum_{j}p_{j}{X}_{jj}^{2})-(\sum_{j}p_{j}X_{jj})^{2}.

(13)

Hence,

(\Delta X)^{2}-(\Delta Y)^{2}=\sum_{j}\sum_{k\neq j}p_{j}\absolutevalue{X_{jk}}^{2}

contains exponentially many nonnegative terms. Roughly speaking, this indicates that
(\Delta X)^{2}/(\Delta Y)^{2}
is very large for countless choices of
X
.
To clearly see this point, we take

\displaystyle X=\bigotimes_{l=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\sigma_{x}^{l}+\sigma_{z}^{l})

(14)

as an example,
where
\sigma_{\alpha}^{l}
,
\alpha=x,y,z
, are the Pauli matrices acting on the
l
-th qubit. Direct calculations show
SM
that

\displaystyle(\Delta X)^{2}/(\Delta Y)^{2}\geq 2^{n}-1,

(15)

implying that the reduction allowed by our strategy can be exponential in
n
.

We point out that our strategy is efficiently implementable on a quantum computer in the scenario under consideration. Indeed, the eigenbasis of
Y
is the Fourier basis, which implies that the quantum circuit
V
is just the inverse quantum Fourier transform. That our strategy is efficiently implementable follows from the known result that the inverse quantum Fourier transform can be realized as a quantum circuit consisting of only
\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
Hadamard gates and controlled phase shift gates
Nielsen and Chuang 2010
(see also Ref.
Griffiths and Niu 1996
for a semiclassical realization without using two-bit gates).

Application 2: permutational symmetries.
Let us consider again
n
qubits but in the state whose symmetric structures are described by the permutation group
G=\{P_{s},s\in S_{n}\}
. Here,
s
labels a permutation in the symmetric group
S_{n}
and
P_{s}
is defined by
P_{s}\ket{\psi_1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\ket{\psi_n}=\ket{\psi_{s(1)}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\ket{\psi_{s(n)}}
. This scenario arises frequently in multipartite experiments
Kiesel et al. 2007
;
Wieczorek et al. 2009
;
Prevedel et al. 2009
, in which the states involved are typically invariant under permutations
Tóth et al. 2010
. For example, three well-known states of this type are Werner states
Werner 1989
, Dicke states
Dicke 1954
, and Greenberger–Horne–Zeilinger (GHZ) states
Greenberger et al. 1989
, which are key resources in quantum information processing
Horodecki et al. 2009
;
Gühne and Tóth 2009
;
Streltsov et al. 2017
;
Hu et al. 2018
. Below, motivated by the fact that Pauli measurements are widely used in multipartite experiments, we explore our strategy to reduce sample complexity in Pauli measurements.

We can express a generic Pauli observable as

\displaystyle X_{\bm{kl}}=\sigma_{x}^{k_{1}}\sigma_{z}^{l_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\sigma_{x}^{k_{n}}\sigma_{z}^{l_{n}}(\mathrm{i})^{\bm{k}\cdot\bm{l}},

(16)

where
\bm{k}=(k_{1},\cdots,k_{n})
and
\bm{l}=(l_{1},\cdots,l_{n})
are two vectors of binary numbers and
\bm{k}\cdot\bm{l}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}k_{i}l_{i}
denotes the usual dot product.
Note that, associated to each
X_{\bm{kl}}
, there is a symmetrized counterpart
Y_{\bm{kl}}=\mathcal{T}(X_{\bm{kl}})
. To illustrate the superiority of the projective measurement of
Y_{\bm{kl}}
over the Pauli measurement of
X_{\bm{kl}}
, we evaluate
(\Delta X_{\bm{kl}})^{2}
and
(\Delta Y_{\bm{kl}})^{2}
on the GHZ state of
n
qubits. Hereafter we assume for simplicity that
n
is odd. It can be shown that

\displaystyle(\Delta X_{\bm{kl}})^{2}=1

(17)

for any Pauli observable with
\absolutevalue{\bm{k}}\neq 0
and
n

SM
. Here
\absolutevalue{\bm{k}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}k_{i}
. By contrast,

\displaystyle(\Delta Y_{\bm{kl}})^{2}=1\Big/\binom{n}{\absolutevalue{\bm{k}}}

(18)

for the same Pauli observable
SM
. To clearly see the difference between
(\Delta X_{\bm{kl}})^{2}
and
(\Delta Y_{\bm{kl}})^{2}
, we consider the Pauli measurements with

\displaystyle(1-\delta)\frac{n}{2}<\absolutevalue{\bm{k}}<(1+\delta)\frac{n}{2},

(19)

referred to as the typical Pauli measurements. Here,
0<\delta<1
is fixed. We show that

\displaystyle\frac{(\Delta X)^{2}}{(\Delta Y)^{2}}\geq\sqrt{\frac{2}{n\pi(1-\delta^{2})}}\left[\frac{4}{(1-\delta)^{1-\delta}(1+\delta)^{1+\delta}}\right]^{\frac{n}{2}}

(20)

for any typical Pauli measurement
SM
. Noting that
(1-\delta)^{1-\delta}(1+\delta)^{1+\delta}<4
for
0<\delta<1
, we deduce that
(\Delta X)^{2}/(\Delta Y)^{2}
is exponential in
n
. Besides, the number of typical Pauli measurements is
\geq 4^{n}(1-\frac{1}{n\delta^{2}})

SM
, which means most of Pauli measurements are typical for a large
n
. So, our strategy allows for exponentially reducing sample complexity for most of Pauli measurements when
n
is large.

Notably, our strategy can be efficiently implemented on a quantum computer in the considered scenario, too. Indeed, as detailed in Ref.
SM
, the eigenbasis of
Y
can be mapped into the computational basis via the quantum Schur transform followed by at most
\lceil\frac{n}{2}\rceil
controlled gates. That our strategy is efficiently implementable follows from the known result that the quantum Schur transform can be realized as a quantum circuit of polynomial size
Bacon et al. 2006
.

Before concluding, we remark that while the assumption in Theorem
2
holds in numerous scenarios, there are also many cases in which we know other structures of
\rho
besides its symmetric structures. For example, apart from translational symmetries, a many-body state usually admits quasi-local structures, based on which some learning methods have been proposed
Anshu et al. 2021
;
Rouzé and França 2024
. As such, our strategy could be incorporated into existing methods as a basic building block for further reducing sample complexity by exploring symmetric structures of states.
Besides, it should be noted that there are difficulties in implementing the projective measurement of
X
in many situations. For example, it was recognized that the mean energy of a many-body system is considerably difficult to measure, due to which only a few architecture-specific experiments have been carried out so far
Šafránek and Rosa 2023
. Interestingly, our strategy allows for inferring
\overline{X}
from the projective measurement of
Y
which, as demonstrated above, may be efficiently implementable even when this is not the case for
X
.

In conclusion, targeting at measuring expectation values of observables, we have proposed an entangled-measurement-based strategy that can leverage quantum computational resources to dramatically reduce sample complexity. Our strategy, which is powered by the exploration of symmetric structures of states, is to infer the expectation value
\overline{X}
of an observable
X
from the projective measurement of its symmetrized counterpart
Y
in Eq. (
2
) rather than
X
itself. This allows our strategy to be an intriguing alternative with the optimal sample efficiency in a variety of contexts, as stated in Theorems
1
and
2
.

To illustrate the significance of our strategy, we have applied it to two scenarios involving different kinds of symmetric structures of states, i.e., those described respectively by the translation and permutation groups, which are ubiquitous in condensed-matter physics and quantum many-body physics. These applications clearly illustrate that our strategy allows for yielding exponential reductions in sample complexity while merely consuming polynomial amounts of quantum computational resources.

The present Letter opens many interesting topics for future work, e.g., how to optimally take advantage of symmetric structures of states to reduce the sample complexity in simultaneously measuring expectation values of multiple observables
Zhang et al. 2018
;
Huggins et al. 2022
and further to extend the scope of discussions from expectation values of observables to other properties of states like various resource measures
Horodecki et al. 2009
;
Gühne and Tóth 2009
;
Streltsov et al. 2017
;
Hu et al. 2018
.

Supplemental Material

I
I. Fundamentals of quantum metrology

Here we recall some fundamentals of quantum metrology, whose mathematical foundation is the quantum parameter estimation theory
Helstrom 1976
;
Holevo 2011
. The theory deals with the quantum parameter estimation problems, i.e., the problems in which both the state in question and the quantity to be estimated are characterized by some unknown parameters.
Specifically, the state in question can be a density matrix
\rho(\bm{\theta})
characterized by
\mathfrak{p}
unknown parameters

\bm{\theta}=(\theta_{1},\cdots,\theta_{\mathfrak{p}})
,
and the quantity to be estimated could be a function of
\bm{\theta}
, denoted as
\beta(\bm{\theta})
. Given
M
copies of
\rho(\bm{\theta})
, the parameter estimation problem is to infer the value of
\beta
by performing a measurement on
\rho(\bm{\theta})^{\otimes M}
.

Any measurement can be described by a positive operator-valued measure (POVM)
\Pi_{\bm{x}}
satisfying
\sum_{x}\Pi_{\bm{x}}=\openone
, where
\bm{x}
labels the outcome of the measurement and could be multivariate in general.
Any inference rule amounts to an estimator

{\beta}_{\textrm{est}}({\bm{x}})
, which takes
{\bm{x}}
as the input and outputs an estimate of
\beta
. It is a natural requirement for an estimator
\beta_{\textrm{est}}({\bm{x}})
to be unbiased, that is,

\displaystyle\sum_{\bm{x}}p_{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{x}})\beta_{\textrm{est}}(x)=\beta,

(I.0S1)

where

\displaystyle p_{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{x}})=\tr[\Pi_{\bm{x}}\rho(\bm{\theta})^{\otimes M}]

(I.0S2)

denotes the probability of getting outcome
{\bm{x}}
. The estimation error can be quantified by the variance of the estimator
\beta_{\textrm{est}}

Helstrom 1976
;
Holevo 2011
,

\displaystyle\textrm{Var}[\beta_{\textrm{est}}]=\sum_{\bm{x}}p_{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{x}})[\beta_{\textrm{est}}({\bm{x}})-\beta]^{2}.

(I.0S3)

One celebrated result in quantum metrology is the quantum Cramér-Rao theorem
Helstrom 1976
;
Holevo 2011
;
Braunstein and Caves 1994
. It states that
\textrm{Var}[\beta_{\textrm{est}}]
is bounded as

\displaystyle\textrm{Var}[\beta_{\textrm{est}}]\geq\frac{1}{MJ[\beta;\rho(\bm{\theta})]},

(I.0S4)

known as the quantum Cramér-Rao bound (QCRB).
Here,
J[\beta;\rho(\bm{\theta})]
is the quantum Fisher information (QFI) about
\beta
when the state in question is
\rho(\bm{\theta})
.
J[\beta;\rho(\bm{\theta})]
is defined as

\displaystyle J[\beta;\rho(\bm{\theta})]=\frac{1}{\partial\beta[H(\bm{\theta})]^{-1}\partial\beta^{T}},

(I.0S5)

where

\displaystyle\partial\beta=\left(\frac{\partial\beta}{\partial\theta_{1}},\cdots,\frac{\partial\beta}{\partial\theta_{\mathfrak{p}}}\right)

(I.0S6)

is a
\mathfrak{p}
-dimensional row vector
and
H(\bm{\theta})
is a
\mathfrak{p}\times\mathfrak{p}

symmetric matrix known as the QFI matrix. The
kl
element of
H(\bm{\theta})
is

\displaystyle H_{kl}=\tr[\rho(\bm{\theta})(L_{k}\circ L_{l})],

(I.0S7)

where

\displaystyle L_{k}\circ L_{l}=(L_{k}L_{l}+L_{l}L_{k})/2

(I.0S8)

denotes the Jordan product, and
L_{k}
is the symmetric logarithmic derivative defined as the Hermitian operator satisfying

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{k}}\rho(\bm{\theta})=L_{k}\circ\rho(\bm{\theta}).

(I.0S9)

The physical meaning of the QCRB is that quantum mechanics does not allow for estimating
\beta
with arbitrarily high precision. Instead, the estimation error produced in any strategy for estimating
\beta
with the
M
copies of
\rho(\bm{\theta})
is fundamentally constrained by the QCRB, which represents the ultimate precision allowed by quantum mechanics
Braunstein and Caves 1994
.

We remark that the QCRB also holds when
\beta_{\textrm{est}}(x)
is biased, provided that the estimation error in Eq. (
I.0S3
) is redefined to remove the local difference
in the “units” of the estimate
\beta_{\textrm{est}}(x)
and the value
\beta
(see Ref.
Braunstein and Caves 1994
for details).

II
II. Explicit expression of
\rho

Here we write out an explicit expression of the
\rho
that satisfies
[\rho,U_{g}]=0
for all
g\in G
.
According to the representation theory of groups
Curtis and Reiner 1962
,
U_{g}
can always be expressed as

\displaystyle U_{g}\cong\bigoplus_{\alpha=1}^{s}I_{n_{\alpha}}\otimes U_{\alpha}(g),

(II.0S10)

where
U_{\alpha}(g)
denotes the
\alpha
-th irreducible representation of
G
with dimension
d_{\alpha}
and multiplicity
n_{\alpha}
. Here,
\cong
means that these equalities are up to a unitary transformation. We deduce from
[\rho,U_{g}]=0
that
\rho
can be expressed as

\displaystyle\rho\cong\bigoplus_{\alpha=1}^{s}A_{\alpha}\otimes\openone_{d_{\alpha}},

(II.0S11)

where
A_{\alpha}
is an
n_{\alpha}\times n_{\alpha}
semipositive matrix. Noting that
A_{\alpha}/\tr A_{\alpha}
is a density matrix and
\tr\rho=1
, we can rewrite Eq. (
II.0S11
) as

\displaystyle\rho\cong\bigoplus_{\alpha=1}^{s}q_{\alpha}\rho_{\alpha}\otimes\frac{\openone_{d_{\alpha}}}{d_{\alpha}},

(II.0S12)

where
q_{\alpha}\geq 0
satisfies
\sum_{\alpha=1}^{s}q_{\alpha}=1
and
\rho_{\alpha}
denotes a density matrix.

Note that any traceless
n_{\alpha}\times n_{\alpha}
matrix can be expressed in terms of the generators of Lie algebra
\mathfrak{su}(n_{\alpha})
. We collectively denote the generators of Lie algebra
\mathfrak{su}(n_{\alpha})
as

\displaystyle\bm{\lambda}_{\alpha}=(\lambda_{\alpha,1},\lambda_{\alpha,2},\cdots,\lambda_{\alpha,n_{\alpha}^{2}-1}),

(II.0S13)

where
\lambda_{\alpha,i}
,
i=1,2,\cdots,n_{\alpha}^{2}-1
, are
n_{\alpha}\times n_{\alpha}
matrices.
It has been shown
Kimura 2003
that
\lambda_{\alpha,i}
can be chosen to satisfy

\displaystyle\lambda_{\alpha,i}^{\dagger}=\lambda_{\alpha,i},~~\tr\lambda_{\alpha,i}=0,~~\tr(\lambda_{\alpha,i}\lambda_{\alpha,j})=2\delta_{ij},

(II.0S14)

and be characterized by structure constants
f_{\alpha,ijk}
and
g_{\alpha,ijk}
as

\displaystyle[\lambda_{\alpha,i},\lambda_{\alpha,j}]=2i\sum_{k}f_{\alpha,ijk}\lambda_{\alpha,k},

(II.0S15)

and

\displaystyle\{\lambda_{\alpha,i},\lambda_{\alpha,j}\}=4\delta_{ij}\frac{\openone_{n_{\alpha}}}{n_{\alpha}}+2\sum_{k}g_{\alpha,ijk}\lambda_{\alpha,k},

(II.0S16)

where
\{\cdot,\cdot\}
denotes the anticommutator. Note that
f_{\alpha,ijk}
is completely antisymmetric and
g_{\alpha,ijk}
is completely symmetric. The generators
\bm{\lambda}_{\alpha}
satisfying Eqs. (
II.0S14
), (
II.0S15
), and (
II.0S16
) can be regarded as generalizations of the Pauli matrices.
We can then expand
\rho_{\alpha}
in terms of the generators as

\displaystyle\rho_{\alpha}=\frac{\openone_{n_{\alpha}}}{n_{\alpha}}+\frac{1}{2}\bm{r}_{\alpha}\cdot\bm{\lambda}_{\alpha}.

(II.0S17)

Here

\displaystyle\bm{r}_{\alpha}=(r_{\alpha,1},r_{\alpha,2},\cdots,r_{\alpha,n_{\alpha}^{2}-1})

(II.0S18)

is a
(n_{\alpha}^{2}-1)
-dimensional real vector known as the generalized Bloch vector
Kimura 2003
.

From Eqs. (
II.0S12
) and (
II.0S17
), it follows that
\rho
is explicitly expressed in terms of the parameters

\displaystyle\bm{\theta}=({\bm{q}},\bm{r}_{1},\cdots,\bm{r}_{s}),

(II.0S19)

with

\displaystyle{\bm{q}}=(q_{1},\cdots,q_{s-1}),

(II.0S20)

where in view of the constraint that
\sum_{\alpha=1}^{s}q_{\alpha}=1
, we have chosen
q_{1},\cdots,q_{s-1}
as independent parameters without loss of generality. That is,
\rho=\rho(\bm{\theta})
is characterized by
\bm{\theta}
in Eq. (
II.0S19
) via Eqs. (
II.0S12
) and (
II.0S17
).
It is worth noting that

\bm{\theta}
are unknown parameters,

since they depend on
\rho
and the only knowledge about
\rho
is assumed to be the symmetric structures described by
G
in Theorem 2.

IV
IV. Proof of Theorem 2

Here we prove Theorem 2 by following the methodology of quantum metrology. We divide the proof into three steps.

First, we set the stage of our analysis.
We treat
\overline{X}
as the quantity to be estimated.
Resorting to the language of quantum metrology, we can describe a strategy for measuring
\overline{X}
as first performing a measurement on
\rho^{\otimes M}
and then inferring the value of
\overline{X}
from the resultant measurement outcome.
Here,
M
denotes the number of samples consumed in the measurement strategy in question.
As mentioned in Sec. I, any measurement can be described by a POVM
\{\Pi_{\bm{x}}\}_{\bm{x}}
, and any inference rule amounts to an estimator
\overline{X}_{\textrm{est}}(\bm{x})
, which takes the possible measurement outcome
\bm{x}
of the POVM as its input and outputs an estimate of
\overline{X}
.
Associated with the POVM
\{\Pi_{\bm{x}}\}_{\bm{x}}
and the (unbiased) estimator
\overline{X}_{\textrm{est}}(\bm{x})
, the error in measuring
\overline{X}
can be quantified by the variance
\textrm{Var}[\overline{X}_{\textrm{est}}]
,

\displaystyle\textrm{Var}[\overline{X}_{\textrm{est}}]=\sum_{\bm{x}}\tr(\Pi_{\bm{x}}\rho^{\otimes M})\left[\overline{X}_{\textrm{est}}(\bm{x})-\overline{X}\right]^{2}.

(IV.0S25)

The requirement of measuring
\overline{X}
up to
\epsilon
amounts to demanding

\displaystyle\epsilon\geq\textrm{Var}[\overline{X}_{\textrm{est}}],

(IV.0S26)

where
\epsilon
is the prescribed error as mentioned in the main text.

Second, we introduce a fundamental bound on
M
.
According to the quantum Cramér-Rao theorem
Helstrom 1976
;
Holevo 2011
;
Braunstein and Caves 1994
(see Sec. I), there is

\displaystyle\textrm{Var}[\overline{X}_{\textrm{est}}]\geq\frac{1}{MJ[\overline{X};\rho]},

(IV.0S27)

where
J[\overline{X};\rho]
denotes the QFI about
\overline{X}
given
\rho
.
The meaning of Eq. (
IV.0S27
) is that the error produced in any strategy for measuring
\overline{X}
is fundamentally constrained by
\frac{1}{MJ[\overline{X};\rho]}
, irrespective of the choices of a POVM
\{\Pi_{\bm{x}}\}_{\bm{x}}
and an estimator
\overline{X}_{\textrm{est}}(\bm{x})
.
Inserting Eq. (
IV.0S26
) into Eq. (
IV.0S27
), we obtain the following fundamental bound on
M
,

\displaystyle M\geq\frac{1}{\epsilon J[\overline{X};\rho]}.

(IV.0S28)

The explicit expression of
J[\overline{X};\rho]
is figured out in Sec. V and is given by

\displaystyle{J[\overline{X};\rho]}=1\Bigl/\left[\left(\sum_{\alpha=1}^{s}q_{\alpha}l_{\alpha}^{2}\right)-\left(\sum_{\alpha=1}^{s}q_{\alpha}l_{\alpha}\right)^{2}\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\sum_{\alpha=1}^{s}q_{\alpha}\bm{b}_{\alpha}\left(R_{\alpha}-\bm{r}_{\alpha}^{T}\bm{r}_{\alpha}+\frac{2}{n_{\alpha}}\openone_{n_{\alpha}^{2}-1}\right)\bm{b}_{\alpha}^{T}\right],

(IV.0S29)

where

\displaystyle l_{\alpha}=a_{\alpha}+\bm{b}_{\alpha}\bm{r}_{\alpha}^{T},

(IV.0S30)

and
R_{\alpha}
is a
(n_{\alpha}^{2}-1)\times(n_{\alpha}^{2}-1)
symmetric matrix with its
jk
element defined as

\displaystyle R_{\alpha,jk}=\sum_{i}r_{\alpha,i}g_{\alpha,ijk}.

(IV.0S31)

Third, we connect the bound (
IV.0S28
) to
(\Delta Y)^{2}
.
Using Eqs. (
II.0S12
), (
II.0S17
), (
III.0S22
), and (
III.0S23
), we can figure out the explicit expression of
(\Delta Y)^{2}
(see Sec. VI for details), given by

\displaystyle(\Delta Y)^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha=1}^{s}q_{\alpha}(a_{\alpha}^{2}+2a_{\alpha}\bm{b}_{\alpha}\bm{r}_{\alpha}^{T}+\frac{2}{n_{\alpha}}\bm{b}_{\alpha}\bm{b}_{\alpha}^{T}+

(IV.0S32)

\displaystyle\bm{b}_{\alpha}R_{\alpha}\bm{b}_{\alpha}^{T})-\left(\sum_{\alpha=1}^{s}q_{\alpha}l_{\alpha}\right)^{2}.

Rewriting Eq. (
IV.0S32
) by taking into account the equality

\displaystyle a_{\alpha}^{2}+2a_{\alpha}\bm{b}_{\alpha}\bm{r}_{\alpha}^{T}=l_{\alpha}^{2}-\bm{b}_{\alpha}\bm{r}_{\alpha}^{T}\bm{r}_{\alpha}\bm{b}_{\alpha}^{T},

(IV.0S33)

we obtain

\displaystyle(\Delta Y)^{2}=\frac{1}{J[\overline{X};\rho]}.

(IV.0S34)

Inserting Eq. (
IV.0S34
) into Eq. (
IV.0S28
) and noting that
M_{Y}=\left\lceil\frac{(\Delta Y)^{2}}{\epsilon}\right\rceil
, we have

\displaystyle M\geq M_{Y}.

(IV.0S35)

This completes the proof of Theorem 2.

V
V. Explicit Expression of

Here we figure out the explicit expression of
J[\overline{X};\rho]
. To this end, we work out the explicit expressions of
H(\bm{\theta})
and
\partial\overline{X}
and substituting them into the definition of
J[\overline{X};\rho]
,

\displaystyle J[\overline{X};\rho]=\frac{1}{{\partial\overline{X}\left[H(\bm{\theta})\right]^{-1}\partial\overline{X}^{T}}}.

(V.0S36)

To derive
H(\bm{\theta})
, we figure out the symmetric logarithmic derivatives (SLDs) associated with the unknown parameters
\bm{\theta}=({\bm{q}},\bm{r}_{1},\cdots,\bm{r}_{s})
.
The SLD associated with
q_{\alpha}
, denoted by
L_{q_{\alpha}}
, is the Hermitian operator that satisfies

\displaystyle\partialderivative{\rho}{q_\alpha}=\rho\circ L_{q_{\alpha}},

(V.0S37)

where
\alpha\in\{1,\cdots,s-1\}
. Noting that the LHS of Eq. (
V.0S37
) reads

\displaystyle\partialderivative{\rho}{q_\alpha}\cong\rho_{\alpha}\otimes\frac{\openone_{d_{\alpha}}}{d_{\alpha}}-\rho_{s}\otimes\frac{\openone_{d_{s}}}{d_{s}},

(V.0S38)

we have

\displaystyle L_{q_{\alpha}}\cong\frac{1}{q_{\alpha}}\openone_{n_{\alpha}}\otimes\openone_{d_{\alpha}}-\frac{1}{q_{s}}\openone_{n_{s}}\otimes\openone_{d_{s}}.

(V.0S39)

The SLD associated with
r_{\alpha,i}
, denoted by
L_{r_{\alpha,i}}
, is the Hermitian operator satisfying

\displaystyle\partialderivative{\rho}{r_{\alpha,i}}=\rho\circ L_{r_{\alpha,i}}.

(V.0S40)

To solve Eq. (
V.0S40
), we assume the following ansatz for
L_{r_{\alpha,i}}
,

\displaystyle L_{r_{\alpha,i}}\cong\left(w_{\alpha,i}\openone_{n_{\alpha}}+\bm{v}_{\alpha,i}\cdot\bm{\lambda}_{\alpha}\right)\otimes\openone_{d_{\alpha}},

(V.0S41)

where
w_{\alpha,i}\in\mathbb{R}
and
\bm{v}_{\alpha,i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\alpha}^{2}-1}
are to be determined.
Besides, it is easy to see that the LHS of Eq. (
V.0S40
) reads

\displaystyle\partialderivative{\rho}{r_{\alpha,i}}\cong\frac{1}{2}q_{\alpha}\lambda_{\alpha,i}\otimes\frac{\openone_{d_{\alpha}}}{d_{\alpha}}.

(V.0S42)

Substituting Eqs. (
V.0S41
) and (
V.0S42
) into Eq. (
V.0S40
), we have

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\openone_{n_{\alpha}}}{n_{\alpha}}+\frac{1}{2}\bm{r}_{\alpha}\cdot\bm{\lambda}_{\alpha}\right)\circ\left(w_{\alpha,i}\openone_{n_{\alpha}}+\bm{v}_{\alpha,i}\cdot\bm{\lambda}_{\alpha}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\lambda_{\alpha,i},

which may be viewed as an equation in terms of
w_{\alpha,i}
and
\bm{v}_{\alpha,i}
. Solving Eq. (
V
) by resorting to the defining properties of
\bm{\lambda}_{\alpha}
[specified in Eqs. (
II.0S14
), (
II.0S15
), and (
II.0S16
)], we have

\displaystyle w_{\alpha,i}=-\bm{v}_{\alpha,i}\bm{r}_{\alpha}^{T},

(V.0S44)

and

\displaystyle\bm{v}_{\alpha,i}=\bm{h}_{\alpha,i}\left[R_{\alpha}-\bm{r}_{\alpha}^{T}\bm{r}_{\alpha}+\frac{2}{n_{\alpha}}\openone_{n_{\alpha}^{2}-1}\right]^{-1}.

(V.0S45)

Here,
R_{\alpha}
is a
(n_{\alpha}^{2}-1)\times(n_{\alpha}^{2}-1)
symmetric matrix with its
jk
element defined as

\displaystyle R_{\alpha,jk}=\sum_{i}r_{\alpha,i}g_{\alpha,ijk},

(V.0S46)

and
\bm{h}_{\alpha,i}
is a
(n_{\alpha}^{2}-1)
-dimensional vector with its
i
-th component identical to one and all others being zero. By the way, we point out that in the case that
[R_{\alpha}-\bm{r}_{\alpha}^{T}\bm{r}_{\alpha}+\frac{2}{n_{\alpha}}\openone_{n_{\alpha}^{2}-1}]
is singular,
[R_{\alpha}-\bm{r}_{\alpha}^{T}\bm{r}_{\alpha}+\frac{2}{n_{\alpha}}\openone_{n_{\alpha}^{2}-1}]^{-1}
is understood as the Moore–Penrose inverse
of
[R_{\alpha}-\bm{r}_{\alpha}^{T}\bm{r}_{\alpha}+\frac{2}{n_{\alpha}}\openone_{n_{\alpha}^{2}-1}]
.

It is easy to see that for three matrices
A
,
B
, and
C
, there are

\displaystyle A\circ B=B\circ A,~~~~\tr[A(B\circ C)]=\tr[(A\circ B)C].

(V.0S47)

Using these two equalities and Eqs. (
V.0S39
), (
V.0S40
), and (
V.0S42
), we have

\displaystyle\tr\left[\rho\left(L_{q_{\alpha}}\circ L_{r_{\beta,i}}\right)\right]

(V.0S48)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tr\left[\left(\rho\circ L_{r_{\beta,i}}\right)L_{q_{\alpha}}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tr(\pdv{\rho}{r_{\beta,i}}L_{q_\alpha})

\displaystyle\cong

\displaystyle\tr\left[\frac{1}{2}q_{\beta}\lambda_{\beta,i}\otimes\frac{\openone_{d_{\beta}}}{d_{\beta}}\left(\frac{1}{q_{\alpha}}\openone_{n_{\alpha}}\otimes\openone_{d_{\alpha}}-\frac{1}{q_{s}}\openone_{n_{s}}\otimes\openone_{d_{s}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0.

Here,
\beta
is a subscript and should be distinguished from the notation used in Sec. I. Using Eq. (
V.0S41
), we easily have

\displaystyle\tr\left[\rho\left(L_{r_{\alpha,i}}\circ L_{r_{\beta,j}}\right)\right]=0,

(V.0S49)

for
\alpha\neq\beta
.

From Eqs. (
V.0S48
) and (
V.0S49
), we deduce that
H(\bm{\theta})
can be expressed in a block-diagonal form

\displaystyle H(\bm{\theta})=H({\bm{q}})\bigoplus\left[\bigoplus_{\alpha=1}^{s}H(\bm{r}_{\alpha})\right],

(V.0S50)

where
H({\bm{q}})
is a
(s-1)\times(s-1)
symmetric matrix with its
\alpha\beta
element defined as

\displaystyle\left[H({\bm{q}})\right]_{\alpha\beta}=\tr[\rho(L_{q_{\alpha}}\circ L_{q_{\beta}})],

(V.0S51)

and
H(\bm{r}_{\alpha})
is a
(n_{\alpha}^{2}-1)\times(n_{\alpha}^{2}-1)
symmetric matrix with its
ij
element defined as

\displaystyle\left[H(\bm{r}_{\alpha})\right]_{ij}=\tr[\rho\left(L_{r_{\alpha,i}}\circ L_{r_{\alpha,j}}\right)].

(V.0S52)

Inserting Eq. (
V.0S39
) into Eq. (
V.0S51
), we have, after simple algebra,

\displaystyle\left[H({\bm{q}})\right]_{\alpha\beta}=\delta_{\alpha\beta}\frac{1}{q_{\alpha}}+\frac{1}{q_{s}},

(V.0S53)

that is,

\displaystyle H({\bm{q}})=\textrm{diag}(\frac{1}{q_{1}},\cdots,\frac{1}{q_{s-1}})+\frac{1}{q_{s}}\bm{e}^{T}\bm{e},

(V.0S54)

where
\bm{e}
is a
(s-1)
-dimensional vector with all components identical to one.
Substituting Eqs. (
V.0S41
), (
V.0S42
), (
V.0S44
), and (
V.0S45
) into Eq. (
V.0S52
) gives

\displaystyle\left[H(\bm{r}_{\alpha})\right]_{ij}

(V.0S55)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tr[\rho\left(L_{r_{\alpha,i}}\circ L_{r_{\alpha,j}}\right)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tr[\left(\rho\circ L_{r_{\alpha,i}}\right)L_{r_{\alpha,j}}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tr[\partialderivative{\rho}{r_{\alpha,i}}L_{r_{\alpha,j}}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tr[\left(\frac{1}{2}q_{\alpha}\lambda_{\alpha,i}\otimes\frac{\openone_{d_{\alpha}}}{d_{\alpha}}\right)\left(w_{\alpha,j}\openone_{n_{\alpha}}+\bm{v}_{\alpha,j}\cdot\bm{\lambda}_{\alpha}\right)\otimes\openone_{d_{\alpha}}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle q_{\alpha}\bm{h}_{\alpha,i}\bm{v}_{\alpha,j}^{T}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle q_{\alpha}\bm{h}_{\alpha,i}\left[R_{\alpha}-\bm{r}_{\alpha}^{T}\bm{r}_{\alpha}+\frac{2}{n_{\alpha}}\openone_{n_{\alpha}^{2}-1}\right]^{-1}\bm{h}_{\alpha,j}^{T}.

\displaystyle H(\bm{r}_{\alpha})=q_{\alpha}\left[R_{\alpha}-\bm{r}_{\alpha}^{T}\bm{r}_{\alpha}+\frac{2}{n_{\alpha}}\openone_{n_{\alpha}^{2}-1}\right]^{-1}.

(V.0S56)

Now,
H(\bm{\theta})
is given by Eqs. (
V.0S50
) with Eqs. (
V.0S54
) and (
V.0S56
).

On the other hand, we have shown that
\overline{X}
can be expressed as

\displaystyle\overline{X}=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{s}q_{\alpha}l_{\alpha},

(V.0S57)

with

\displaystyle l_{\alpha}=a_{\alpha}+\bm{b}_{\alpha}\bm{r}_{\alpha}^{T}.

(V.0S58)

Differentiating
\overline{X}
with respect to
q_{\alpha}
where

, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\overline{X}}{\partial q_{\alpha}}=l_{\alpha}-l_{s},

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\overline{X}}{\partial r_{\alpha,i}}=q_{\alpha}b_{\alpha,i}.

(V.0S61)

Therefore,

\displaystyle\partial\overline{X}=\left(\bm{l}-l_{s}\bm{e},q_{1}\bm{b}_{1},\cdots,q_{s}\bm{b}_{s}\right),

(V.0S62)

where

\displaystyle\bm{l}=(l_{1},\cdots,l_{s-1}).

(V.0S63)

Using Eqs. (
V.0S50
) and (
V.0S62
), we obtain

\displaystyle{J[\overline{X};\rho]}=1\Bigl/\left[\left(\sum_{\alpha=1}^{s}q_{\alpha}l_{\alpha}^{2}\right)-\left(\sum_{\alpha=1}^{s}q_{\alpha}l_{\alpha}\right)^{2}\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\sum_{\alpha=1}^{s}q_{\alpha}\bm{b}_{\alpha}\left(R_{\alpha}-\bm{r}_{\alpha}^{T}\bm{r}_{\alpha}+\frac{2}{n_{\alpha}}\openone_{n_{\alpha}^{2}-1}\right)\bm{b}_{\alpha}^{T}\right],

(V.0S64)

where we have used the equality

\displaystyle[H({\bm{q}})]^{-1}=\textrm{diag}(q_{1},\cdots,q_{s-1})-{\bm{q}}^{T}{\bm{q}}.

(V.0S65)

VI
VI. Explicit expression of
(\Delta Y)^{2}

Here we figure out the explicit expression of
(\Delta Y)^{2}
. We deduce from Eqs. (
II.0S12
) and (
III.0S22
) that

\displaystyle\overline{Y^{2}}=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{s}q_{\alpha}\tr(\rho_\alpha Y_\alpha^2).

(VI.0S66)

Then, using Eqs. (
II.0S17
) and (
III.0S23
) and noting that

\displaystyle\tr(\bm{b}_\alpha\cdot\bm{\lambda}_\alpha\bm{r}_\alpha\cdot\bm{\lambda}_\alpha)=2\bm{b}_{\alpha}\bm{r}_{\alpha}^{T},

(VI.0S67)

\displaystyle\tr(\bm{b}_\alpha\cdot\bm{\lambda}_\alpha\bm{b}_\alpha\cdot\bm{\lambda}_\alpha)=2\bm{b}_{\alpha}\bm{b}_{\alpha}^{T},

(VI.0S68)

\displaystyle\tr(\bm{b}_\alpha\cdot\bm{\lambda}_\alpha\bm{r}_\alpha\cdot\bm{\lambda}_\alpha\bm{b}_\alpha\cdot\bm{\lambda}_\alpha)=2\bm{b}_{\alpha}R_{\alpha}\bm{b}_{\alpha}^{T},

(VI.0S69)

we have

\displaystyle\overline{Y^{2}}=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{s}q_{\alpha}\left(a_{\alpha}^{2}+2a_{\alpha}\bm{b}_{\alpha}\bm{r}_{\alpha}^{T}+\frac{2}{n_{\alpha}}\bm{b}_{\alpha}\bm{b}_{\alpha}^{T}+\bm{b}_{\alpha}R_{\alpha}\bm{b}_{\alpha}^{T}\right).

Using this equality and noting that
\overline{Y}=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{s}q_{\alpha}l_{\alpha}
, we obtain

\displaystyle(\Delta Y)^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha=1}^{s}q_{\alpha}(a_{\alpha}^{2}+2a_{\alpha}\bm{b}_{\alpha}\bm{r}_{\alpha}^{T}+\frac{2}{n_{\alpha}}\bm{b}_{\alpha}\bm{b}_{\alpha}^{T}+

(VI.0S70)

\displaystyle\bm{b}_{\alpha}R_{\alpha}\bm{b}_{\alpha}^{T})-\left(\sum_{\alpha=1}^{s}q_{\alpha}l_{\alpha}\right)^{2}.

As shown in Sec. IV, Eq. (
VI.0S70
) can be rewritten by taking into account the equality

\displaystyle a_{\alpha}^{2}+2a_{\alpha}\bm{b}_{\alpha}\bm{r}_{\alpha}^{T}=l_{\alpha}^{2}-\bm{b}_{\alpha}\bm{r}_{\alpha}^{T}\bm{r}_{\alpha}\bm{b}_{\alpha}^{T},

(VI.0S71)

which gives

\displaystyle(\Delta Y)^{2}=\frac{1}{J[\overline{X};\rho]}.

(VI.0S72)

VII
VII. Details on application 1

Here we prove the two inequality used in the main text,

\displaystyle\frac{(\Delta X)^{2}}{(\Delta Y)^{2}}\geq 2^{n}-1.

(VII.0S73)

We resort to the product representation of the Fourier basis
Nielsen and Chuang 2010
,

\displaystyle\ket{f_j}=\frac{\left(\ket{0}+e^{2\pi i0.j_{n}}\ket{1}\right)\cdots\left(\ket{0}+e^{2\pi i0.j_{1}\cdots j_{n}}\ket{1}\right)}{2^{n/2}},

where
0.j_{l}j_{l+1}\cdots j_{m}
denotes the binary fraction
j_{l}/2+j_{l+1}/4+\cdots+j_{m}/2^{m-l+1}
. Using Eq. (
VII
) to calculate
X_{jj}=\bra{f_j}X\ket{f_j}
gives

\displaystyle X_{jj}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^{n}}}\cos(2\pi 0.j_n)\cdots\cos(2\pi 0.j_1j_2\cdots j_n).

(VII.0S75)

Noting that
\absolutevalue{X_{jj}}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^{n}}}
and
\rho=\sum_{j}p_{j}\ket{f_j}\bra{f_j}
, we have

\displaystyle\absolutevalue{\overline{X}}=\absolutevalue{\sum_jp_jX_{jj}}\leq\sum_{j}p_{j}\absolutevalue{X_{jj}}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^{n}}}\sum_{j}p_{j}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^{n}}}.

Besides, note that
X^{2}=\openone\otimes\cdots\otimes\openone
and therefore

\displaystyle\overline{X^{2}}=1,

(VII.0S76)

which further leads to

\displaystyle 1-\frac{1}{2^{n}}\leq(\Delta X)^{2}\leq 1.

(VII.0S77)

On the other hand, noting that
Y=\sum_{j}X_{jj}\ket{f_j}\bra{f_j}
, we have

\displaystyle\absolutevalue{\overline{Y}}=\absolutevalue{\overline{X}}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^{n}}},

(VII.0S78)

and

\displaystyle\overline{Y^{2}}=\sum_{j}p_{j}X_{jj}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{2^{n}}.

(VII.0S79)

Therefore,

\displaystyle 0\leq(\Delta Y)^{2}\leq\frac{1}{2^{n}}.

(VII.0S80)

Then Eq. (
VII.0S73
) follows from Eqs. (
VII.0S77
) and (
VII.0S80
).

\displaystyle\sigma_{x}^{k_{1}}\sigma_{z}^{l_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\sigma_{x}^{k_{n}}\sigma_{z}^{l_{n}}\ket{GHZ}=

n

\ket{GHZ}=\left(\ket{0\cdots 0}+\ket{1\cdots 1}\right)/\sqrt{2}

\absolutevalue{\bm{l}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}l_{i}

Using Eq. (
VIII.1
) and

\displaystyle X_{\bm{kl}}=\sigma_{x}^{k_{1}}\sigma_{z}^{l_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\sigma_{x}^{k_{n}}\sigma_{z}^{l_{n}}(i)^{\bm{k}\cdot\bm{l}},

(VIII.1S82)

we have

\displaystyle\bra{GHZ}X_{\bm{kl}}\ket{GHZ}=(i)^{\bm{k}\cdot\bm{l}}\frac{1+(-1)^{\absolutevalue{\bm{l}}}}{2}(\delta_{\bm{k},\bm{0}}+\delta_{\bm{k},\bm{1}}),

where
\delta_{\bm{k},\bm{0}}:=\delta_{k_{1},0}\cdots\delta_{k_{n},0}
and
\delta_{\bm{k},\bm{1}}:=\delta_{k_{1},1}\cdots\delta_{k_{n},1}
. Hence,

\displaystyle\bra{GHZ}X_{\bm{kl}}\ket{GHZ}=

\displaystyle\begin{cases}1&\bm{k}=\bm{0}~~\textrm{and}~~\absolutevalue{\bm{l}}\in\textrm{even numbers},\\
(i)^{\absolutevalue{\bm{l}}}&\bm{k}=\bm{1}~~\textrm{and}~~\absolutevalue{\bm{l}}\in\textrm{even numbers},\\
0&\textrm{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(VIII.1S84)

It follows from Eq. (
VIII.1
) and
X_{\bm{kl}}^{2}=\openone_{2^{n}}

1&\textrm{otherwise}.\end{cases}

To calculate

, we resort to the defining properties of
P_{s}

\displaystyle P_{s}\ket{\psi_1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\ket{\psi_n}=\ket{\psi_{s(1)}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\ket{\psi_{s(n)}}

(VIII.1S86)

\displaystyle P_{s}A_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes A_{n}P_{s}^{\dagger}=A_{s(1)}\otimes\cdots\otimes A_{s(n)},

(VIII.1S87)

which, in conjunction with the rearrangement theorem, lead to

\displaystyle P_{s}\ket{GHZ}=\ket{GHZ},~~P_{s}Y_{\bm{kl}}=Y_{\bm{kl}}P_{s}.

(VIII.1S88)

Using Eq. (
VIII.1S88

\displaystyle Y_{\bm{kl}}=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{s\in S_{n}}\sigma_{x}^{k_{s(1)}}\sigma_{z}^{l_{s(1)}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\sigma_{x}^{k_{s(n)}}\sigma_{z}^{l_{s(n)}}(i)^{\bm{k}\cdot\bm{l}},

\displaystyle\bra{GHZ}Y_{\bm{kl}}^{2}\ket{GHZ}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{s\in S_{n}}\bra{GHZ}\sigma_{x}^{k_{s(1)}}\sigma_{z}^{l_{s(1)}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\sigma_{x}^{k_{s(n)}}\sigma_{z}^{l_{s(n)}}(i)^{\bm{k}\cdot\bm{l}}Y_{\bm{kl}}\ket{GHZ}

(VIII.1S90)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{s\in S_{n}}\bra{GHZ}P_{s}^{\dagger}\sigma_{x}^{k_{s(1)}}\sigma_{z}^{l_{s(1)}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\sigma_{x}^{k_{s(n)}}\sigma_{z}^{l_{s(n)}}(i)^{\bm{k}\cdot\bm{l}}Y_{\bm{kl}}P_{s}\ket{GHZ}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{s\in S_{n}}\bra{GHZ}P_{s}^{\dagger}\sigma_{x}^{k_{s(1)}}\sigma_{z}^{l_{s(1)}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\sigma_{x}^{k_{s(n)}}\sigma_{z}^{l_{s(n)}}(i)^{\bm{k}\cdot\bm{l}}P_{s}Y_{\bm{kl}}\ket{GHZ}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{s\in S_{n}}\bra{GHZ}\sigma_{x}^{k_{1}}\sigma_{z}^{l_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\sigma_{x}^{k_{n}}\sigma_{z}^{l_{n}}(i)^{\bm{k}\cdot\bm{l}}Y_{\bm{kl}}\ket{GHZ}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bra{GHZ}\sigma_{x}^{k_{1}}\sigma_{z}^{l_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\sigma_{x}^{k_{n}}\sigma_{z}^{l_{n}}(i)^{\bm{k}\cdot\bm{l}}Y_{\bm{kl}}\ket{GHZ}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{s\in S_{n}}\bra{GHZ}\sigma_{x}^{k_{1}}\sigma_{z}^{l_{1}}\sigma_{x}^{k_{s(1)}}\sigma_{z}^{l_{s(1)}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\sigma_{x}^{k_{n}}\sigma_{z}^{l_{n}}\sigma_{x}^{k_{s(n)}}\sigma_{z}^{l_{s(n)}}(-1)^{\bm{k}\cdot\bm{l}}\ket{GHZ}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{s\in S_{n}}\bra{GHZ}\sigma_{x}^{k_{1}+k_{s(1)}}\sigma_{z}^{l_{1}+l_{s(1)}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\sigma_{x}^{k_{n}+k_{s(n)}}\sigma_{z}^{l_{n}+l_{s(n)}}(-1)^{(\bm{k}+\bm{k}_{s})\cdot\bm{l}}\ket{GHZ}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{s\in S_{n}}(-1)^{(\bm{k}+\bm{k}_{s})\cdot\bm{l}}\frac{1+(-1)^{\absolutevalue{\bm{l}+\bm{l}_s}}}{2}(\delta_{\bm{k}\oplus\bm{k}_{s},\bm{0}}+\delta_{\bm{k}\oplus\bm{k}_{s},\bm{1}}),

where
\bm{k}_{s}=(k_{s(1)},\cdots,k_{s(n)})

\bm{k}\oplus\bm{k}_{s}=(k_{1}+k_{s(1)},\cdots,k_{n}+k_{s(n)})

\absolutevalue{\bm{l}+\bm{l}_s}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}l_{i}+l_{s(i)}=2\absolutevalue{\bm{l}}

[1+(-1)^{\absolutevalue{\bm{l}+\bm{l}_s}}]/{2}=1

\delta_{\bm{k}\oplus\bm{k}_{s},\bm{1}}=0

VIII.2
Lower bound on
(\Delta X)^{2}/(\Delta Y)^{2}
for typical Pauli measurements

Here we figure out the lower bound on
(\Delta X)^{2}/(\Delta Y)^{2}
for typical Pauli measurements. Using the Stirling’s approximation

\displaystyle n!\approx\sqrt{2\pi n}\left(\frac{n}{e}\right)^{n}

(VIII.2S93)

and noting that

\displaystyle(1-\delta)\frac{n}{2}<\absolutevalue{\bm{k}}<(1+\delta)\frac{n}{2},

(VIII.2S94)

we have

\displaystyle{(\Delta X)^{2}}/{(\Delta Y)^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\binom{n}{\absolutevalue{\bm{k}}}

(VIII.2S95)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\binom{n}{(1-\delta)\frac{n}{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{n!}{\left[(1-\delta)\frac{n}{2}\right]!\left[(1+\delta)\frac{n}{2}\right]!}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{2\pi n}\left(\frac{n}{e}\right)^{n}}{\sqrt{2\pi(1-\delta)\frac{n}{2}}\left(\frac{(1-\delta)\frac{n}{2}}{e}\right)^{(1-\delta)\frac{n}{2}}\sqrt{2\pi(1+\delta)\frac{n}{2}}\left(\frac{(1+\delta)\frac{n}{2}}{e}\right)^{(1+\delta)\frac{n}{2}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{2}{n\pi(1-\delta^{2})}}\left[\frac{4}{(1-\delta)^{1-\delta}(1+\delta)^{1+\delta}}\right]^{\frac{n}{2}}.

Here we have assumed for simplicity that
(1-\delta)\frac{n}{2}
is an integer. The above proof can be made more rigorous by replacing the Stirling’s approximation with the inequalities
\sqrt{2\pi n}\left(\frac{n}{e}\right)^{n}e^{\frac{1}{12n+1}}\leq n!\leq\sqrt{2\pi n}\left(\frac{n}{e}\right)^{n}e^{\frac{1}{12n}}
which hold for
n\geq 1
.

VIII.3
Lower bound on the number of typical Pauli measurements

Here we find a lower bound on the number of typical Pauli measurements. To do this, we interpret all
k_{i}
’s as independent and identically distributed binary random variables taking on two values
0
and
1
with equal probabilities
1/2
. Then, all the bit strings
\bm{k}=(k_{1},\cdots,k_{n})
are with equal probabilities
1/2^{n}
. Using the Chebyshev’s inequality
Mitzenmacher and Upfal 2005
and noting that
\textrm{Var}[\absolutevalue{\bm{k}}]=\textrm{Var}[k_{1}+\cdots+k_{n}]=n/4
, we have that the number of the bit strings with
(1-\delta)\frac{n}{2}<\absolutevalue{\bm{k}}<(1+\delta)\frac{n}{2}
, denoted as
N
, is

\displaystyle N

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2^{n}\textrm{Pr}\left((1-\delta)\frac{n}{2}<\absolutevalue{\bm{k}}<(1+\delta)\frac{n}{2}\right)

(VIII.3S96)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2^{n}\left[1-\textrm{Pr}\left(\absolutevalue{\abs{\bm{k}}-\frac{n}{2}}\geq\frac{n\delta}{2}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle 2^{n}\left[1-\frac{\textrm{Var}[\absolutevalue{\bm{k}}]}{\left(\frac{n\delta}{2}\right)^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2^{n}\left(1-\frac{1}{n\delta^{2}}\right),

where
\textrm{Pr}\left((1-\delta)\frac{n}{2}<\absolutevalue{\bm{k}}<(1+\delta)\frac{n}{2}\right)
stands for the probability that a bit string
\bm{k}
is with
(1-\delta)\frac{n}{2}<\absolutevalue{\bm{k}}<(1+\delta)\frac{n}{2}
, and similarly for the notation
\textrm{Pr}\left(\absolutevalue{\abs{\bm{k}}-\frac{n}{2}}\geq\frac{n\delta}{2}\right)
. The number of typical Pauli measurements is

\displaystyle 2^{n}N\geq 4^{n}\left(1-\frac{1}{n\delta^{2}}\right),

(VIII.3S97)

which follows from the fact that there are
2^{n}
different
\bm{l}
for each
\bm{k}
.

VIII.4
Discussion on the implementation of
V

Here we explain why the quantum circuit
V
can be efficiently realized as a quantum circuit. According to the Schur–Weyl duality
Weyl 1950
, the Hilbert space
(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes n}
of
n
qubits can be decomposed into a direct sum of tensor products,

\displaystyle(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes n}\cong\bigoplus_{j=j_{\textrm{min}}}^{j_{\textrm{max}}}\mathbb{C}^{2j+1}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{d_{n,j}},

(VIII.4S98)

where
\mathbb{C}^{2j+1}
and
\mathbb{C}^{d_{n,j}}
are irreducible representations of
U(2)
and
S_{n}
, respectively.
j
labels the eigen-subspace of the total squared angular
momentum with eigenvalue
j(j+1)
, where
j=j_{\textrm{min}}
,
j_{\textrm{min}}+1
,
\cdots
,
j_{\textrm{max}}
with
j_{\textrm{max}}=n/2
and
j_{\textrm{min}}=0
,
1/2
for even and odd
n
, respectively.
The decomposition (
VIII.4S98
) corresponds to a basis for
(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes n}
,
\ket{\Psi_{j,q,p}}
, known as the Schur basis
Bacon et al. 2006
, where
j
labels the subspaces
\mathbb{C}^{2j+1}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{d_{n,j}}
, and
q
and
p
label bases for
\mathbb{C}^{2j+1}
and
\mathbb{C}^{d_{n,j}}
, respectively. The Schur transform, denoted as
U_{\textrm{Sch}}
hereafter, is defined as the unitary operator mapping the Schur basis into the computational basis,

\displaystyle U_{\textrm{Sch}}\ket{\Psi_{j,q,p}}=\ket{j,q,p}.

(VIII.4S99)

Here,
\ket{j,q,p}
stands for the computational basis, with
j,q,p
expressed as bit strings.
A central result of Ref.
Bacon et al. 2006
is that
U_{\textrm{Sch}}
can be realized as a quantum circuit of polynomial size.

Without loss of generality, we can express
X
in the form

\displaystyle X=\sum_{j,j^{\prime}}\sum_{q,q^{\prime}}\sum_{p,p^{\prime}}X_{jqp;j^{\prime}q^{\prime}p^{\prime}}\ket{\Psi_{j,q,p}}\bra{\Psi_{j^\prime,q^\prime,p^\prime}},

(VIII.4S100)

where

\displaystyle X_{jqp;j^{\prime}q^{\prime}p^{\prime}}=\bra{\Psi_{j,q,p}}X\ket{\Psi_{j^\prime,q^\prime,p^\prime}}.

(VIII.4S101)

Moreover, noting that
\rho
and
Y
respect the permutation symmetries, we can express
\rho
and
Y
in the form

\displaystyle\rho=\sum_{j}\sum_{q,q^{\prime}}\sum_{p}p_{j}(\rho_{j})_{qq^{\prime}}\ket{\Psi_{j,q,p}}\bra{\Psi_{j,q^\prime,p}}

(VIII.4S102)

and

\displaystyle Y=\sum_{j}\sum_{q,q^{\prime}}\sum_{p}(Y_{j})_{qq^{\prime}}\ket{\Psi_{j,q,p}}\bra{\Psi_{j,q^\prime,p}},

(VIII.4S103)

where
p_{j}\geq 0
satisfies
\sum_{j}p_{j}=1
,
\rho_{j}
is a density matrix, and
Y_{j}
is a Hermitian matrix. To find the connection between
X
and
Y
, we make use of the equality
\overline{X}=\overline{Y}
, which leads to

\displaystyle({Y}_{j})_{qq^{\prime}}=\frac{1}{d_{n,j}}\sum_{p}X_{jqp;jq^{\prime}p}.

(VIII.4S104)

It follows from Eqs. (
VIII.4S99
) and (
VIII.4S103
) that

\displaystyle U_{\textrm{Sch}}YU_{\textrm{Sch}}^{\dagger}=\sum_{j}\sum_{q,q^{\prime}}\sum_{p}(Y_{j})_{qq^{\prime}}\ket{{j,q,p}}\bra{{j,q^\prime,p}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\ket{j}\bra{j}\otimes\left(\sum_{q,q^{\prime}}(Y_{j})_{qq^{\prime}}\ket{q}\bra{q^\prime}\right)\otimes\openone_{d_{n,j}}.

Let the eigendecomposition of
Y_{j}
be

\displaystyle Y_{j}=U_{j}D_{j}U_{j}^{\dagger},

(VIII.4S106)

where
U_{j}
denotes a unitary matrix and
D_{j}
is a diagonal matrix with real entries. We then define
V
to be of the form

\displaystyle V=V_{j_{\textrm{min}}}V_{j_{\textrm{min}}+1}\cdots V_{j_{\textrm{max}}}U_{\textrm{Sch}},

(VIII.4S107)

where

\displaystyle V_{j}=\ket{j}\bra{j}\otimes U_{j}^{\dagger}+\sum_{j^{\prime}\neq j}\ket{j^\prime}\bra{j^\prime}\otimes\openone_{2j+1}

(VIII.4S108)

is a controlled gate. It is easy to see that the
V
thus defined transforms the eigenbasis of
Y
into the computational basis
\ket{j,q,p}
. As each
V_{j}
is a unitary matrix of polynomial size,
V
can be realized as a quantum circuit of polynomial size.
