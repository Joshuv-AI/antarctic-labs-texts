Generalised Rank-Constrained Approximations of Hilbert–Schmidt Operators on Separable Hilbert Spaces and Applications
Giuseppe Carere, Han Cheng Lie

Abstract
In this work we solve, for given bounded operators B , C B,C and Hilbert–Schmidt operator M M acting on potentially infinite-dimensional separable Hilbert spaces, the reduced rank approximation problem, min ⁡ { ∥ M − B ​ X ​ C ∥ L 2 : dim ran ⁡ X ≤ r } . \displaystyle\min\{\lVert M-BXC\rVert_{L_{2}}:\ \dim\operatorname{ran}{X}\leq r\}. This extends the result of Sondermann ( Statistische Hefte , 1986) and Friedland and Torokhti ( SIAM J. Matrix Analysis and Applications , 2007), which studies this problem in the case of matrices M M , B B , C C , X X , and the analysis involves the Moore–Penrose inverse. In classical approximation problems that can be solved by the singular value decomposition or Moore–Penrose inverse, the solution satisfies a minimal norm property. Friedland and Torokhti state such a minimal norm property of the solution. We show that this minimal norm property does not hold in general and give a modified minimality property that does hold. We show that the solution may be discontinuous in infinite-dimensional settings. We give conditions for continuity of the solutions and construct continuous approximations when such conditions are not met. Finally, we study problems from signal processing, reduced rank regression and linear operator learning under a rank constraint. Our theoretical results enable us to explicitly find solutions to these problems and to characterise their existence, uniqueness and minimality property.



Keywords
: rank-constrained approximation, Hilbert-Schmidt operators, infinite-dimensional analysis, unbounded operators, signal processing, reduced rank regression, linear operator learning

Introduction

We study the following reduced rank approximation in Hilbert–Schmidt norm
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{L_{2}}
,

\displaystyle\min\{\lVert M-BXC\rVert_{L_{2}}:\ \dim\operatorname{ran}{X}\leq r\},

(1.1)

where

and

in Hilbert–Schmidt norm. Such approximations have also been called ‘generalised approximations’, see e.g.
[

In the finite-dimensional matrix case, the generalised approximation problem is well studied, cf.
[
38
,
19
,
46
]
. In
[
19

and
[

[

]

Next, we state the main contributions of this work, review some of the literature on applications of generalised rank-constrained approximations—with or without the minimal norm property—introduce notation and outline the rest of the paper.

1.3
Notation

For a matrix
Y\in\mathbb{C}^{s\times t}
, we denote by
(Y)_{r}
a rank-
r
truncated singular value decomposition (SVD) of
Y
. Thus if
Y=U\Sigma V^{*}
is an SVD of
Y
, then
(Y)_{r}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\sigma_{i}u_{i}v_{i}^{*}
where
\sigma_{i}=\Sigma_{ii}
are nonincreasing and
u_{i}
and
v_{i}
are the
i
-th column of
U
and
V
respectively. The Frobenius norm of
Y
is denoted as
\lVert Y\rVert_{F}
. By
Y^{\dagger}\in\mathbb{C}^{t\times s}
we denote the Moore–Penrose inverse, also known as the pseudo-inverse or generalised inverse.
We define
P_{Y,L}\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{\operatorname{rank}\left({Y}\right)}u_{i}u_{i}^{*}
and
P_{Y,R}\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{\operatorname{rank}\left({Y}\right)}v_{i}v_{i}^{*}
. Thus,
P_{Y,L}
and
P_{Y,R}
are the matrix representations in the standard bases of the projectors onto
\operatorname{ran}{Y}
and
\ker{Y}^{\perp}
respectively. It holds that
P_{Y,L}=YY^{\dagger}
and
P_{Y,R}=Y^{\dagger}Y
. We note that the equality
P_{Y,L}=YY^{\dagger}
is no longer true in general in a infinite-dimensional setting.

We let
\mathcal{H}
and
\mathcal{K}
be possibly infinite-dimensional separable Hilbert spaces over
\mathbb{C}
or
\mathbb{R}
. By
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}
,
\mathcal{K})
,
\mathcal{B}_{0}(\mathcal{H}
,
\mathcal{K})
,
\mathcal{B}_{00}(\mathcal{H}
,
\mathcal{K})
and
L_{2}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{K})
, we denote the linear spaces of bounded, compact, finite-rank and Hilbert–Schmidt operators between Hilbert spaces
\mathcal{H}
and
\mathcal{K}
. The Hilbert–Schmidt norm of a linear operator between finite-dimensional spaces is equal to the Frobenius norm of any matrix representation of this operator in an orthonormal basis (ONB). By
\mathcal{B}_{00,r}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{K})
we denote the set of finite-rank operators, i.e. operators that are bounded, have finite-dimensional range, and have rank at most
r\in\mathbb{N}
. Further details of these classes of operators is given in
Appendix

A
.

2
Optimal approximation for matrices

In this section we recall the statement of
[
19
, Theorem 2.1]
and discuss the minimal norm property stated therein.

The statement of
[
19
, Theorem 2.1]
, which provides an expression (
2.1
) for a solution of a rank-constrained approximation problem (
2.2
) and asserts that any such solution has a certain minimal norm property (
2.3
), is:

Claim
of
[
19
, Theorem 2.1]
: Let
M\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times n}
,
B\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times p}
and
C\in\mathbb{C}^{q\times n}
be given.
Then,

\displaystyle\hat{X}=B^{\dagger}(P_{B,L}MP_{C,R})_{r}C^{\dagger}

(2.1)

solves the low-rank approximation problem

\displaystyle\min{\left\{\lVert M-BXC\rVert_{F}:\ X\in\mathbb{C}^{p\times q},\ \operatorname{rank}\left({X}\right)\leq r\right\}},

(2.2)

and satisfies the minimal norm property

\displaystyle\lVert X\rVert_{F}=\min\left\{\lVert\tilde{X}\rVert_{F}:\ \tilde{X}\text{ solves }\eqref{eqn:matrix_problem}\right\}.

(2.3)

A few remarks are in order. Firstly, in general not all solutions of (
2.2
) are of the form (
2.1
). Secondly, as
P_{C,R}=C^{\dagger}C
and as in finite dimensions it holds that
P_{B,L}=BB^{\dagger}
, one may write (
2.1
) as
B^{\dagger}(BB^{\dagger}XC^{\dagger}C)_{r}C^{\dagger}
.
Thirdly, also a necessary and sufficient condition for uniqueness of
\hat{X}
is given in
[
19
, Theorem 2.1]
.
Finally, if
(P_{B,L}MP_{C,R})_{r}
is not uniquely specified, then the matrix
\hat{X}
in (
2.1
) is not uniquely determined. Hence
eq.

2.1
should be interpreted as a set of solutions. Furthermore, this lack of uniqueness extends also to
\lVert\hat{X}\rVert_{F}
: if there exists more than one solution of the form (
2.1
), then these solutions might not all have the same norm. In other words, whether
\hat{X}
in (
2.1
) is of minimal norm or not in general depends on the choice of truncated SVD
(P_{B,L}MP_{C,R})_{r}
. This fact indicates that the claim above is not sufficiently precise.

The following example demonstrates that if there is more than one solution to (
2.2
) of the form (
2.1
), then these solutions may have different Frobenius norm.

Example 2.1
.

Let us take
n=m=2,\ p=q=3,\ r=1
, and

\displaystyle M=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},\quad B=C^{\top}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&\frac{1}{2}&0\\
\end{pmatrix}.

(2.4)

Then,

\displaystyle C^{\dagger}=(B^{\dagger})^{\top}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&2&0\\
\end{pmatrix},\quad P_{B,L}=P_{C,R}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}.

Hence
M=P_{B,L}MP_{C,R}
and its rank-1 truncated SVDs are

\displaystyle M_{1,a}\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},\quad M_{1,b}\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&1\\
\end{pmatrix}.

The two minimizing matrices of the form (
2.1
) then are

\displaystyle\hat{X}_{a}\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&0&0\\
0&0&0\\
\end{pmatrix},\quad\hat{X}_{b}\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0\\
0&4&0\\
0&0&0\\
\end{pmatrix}.

However, the Frobenius norms of
\hat{X}_{a},\hat{X}_{b}
differ, and thus cannot both be minimal. In particular, (
2.3
) does not hold.

The preceding example suggests that
[
19
, Theorem 2.1]
can be made more precise, either by removing the minimum norm condition (
2.3
), or by replacing this condition by a weaker condition which all solutions
\hat{X}
given by (
2.1
) satisfy. Such a weaker condition must thus be independent of the choice of truncated SVD
(P_{B,L}MP_{C,R})_{r}
. Furthermore, if
C
is a linear operator mapping between infinite-dimensional spaces, then
{C}^{\dagger}
will in general not be bounded (cf.
Proposition

A.5
), and thus cannot be Hilbert–Schmidt. It can then happen that also solutions
\hat{X}
of the form (
2.1
) are not Hilbert–Schmidt, in which case
\lVert\hat{X}\rVert_{L_{2}}
may be ill-defined, and we need a minimality condition that does not involve the Frobenius norm in order to generalise this condition to the infinite-dimensional setting. For these two reasons we motivate an alternative condition, by continuing our Example
2.1
.

Example 2.1

(Continued)
.

Let us put

\displaystyle\hat{X}_{a}(\alpha)\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}\lx@intercol\hfil\hbox{\multirowsetup$X_{a,11}$}\hfil\lx@intercol&\alpha_{1}\\
&&\alpha_{2}\\
\alpha_{3}&\alpha_{4}&\alpha_{5}\end{pmatrix},\quad\hat{X}_{b}(\alpha)\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}\lx@intercol\hfil\hbox{\multirowsetup$X_{b,11}$}\hfil\lx@intercol&\alpha_{1}\\
&&\alpha_{2}\\
\alpha_{3}&\alpha_{4}&\alpha_{5}\end{pmatrix},

with

0&0\end{smallmatrix}\big)

,

and
\hat{X}_{b}(0)=\hat{X}_{b}
. For

4
Unbounded solution

We consider an example that shows that for a given approximation problem, all solutions
\hat{X}
of (
3.2
) of the form (
3.3
)—and in fact all solutions of (
3.2
)—are unbounded. This example also shows it is possible that some solutions of the form (
3.3
) are bounded, while others are unbounded.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a separable Hilbert space and take
\mathcal{H}_{i}\coloneqq\mathcal{H}
for
1\leq i\leq 4
.
Let
(e_{n})_{n}
be an orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{H}
. Let
(\gamma_{n})_{n},(\mu_{n})_{n}\in\mathbb{C}\backslash\{0\}
converge to zero. We assume
(\mu_{n})_{n}
is nonincreasing. Let
(\alpha_{n})_{n}\in\mathbb{C}
be such that
\lvert\alpha_{n}\rvert\rightarrow\infty
and
(\alpha_{n}\gamma_{n})_{n}
is square summable. For example,
\gamma_{n}=n^{-2}
and
\alpha_{n}=n
satisfy these conditions.

Define
w\coloneqq\sum_{n\geq 2}\alpha_{n}\gamma_{n}e_{n}\in\mathcal{H}
. Put
f_{1}\coloneqq w\lVert w\rVert^{-1}
and
f_{2}\coloneqq e_{1}
and extend
\{f_{1},f_{2}\}
to an ONB
(f_{n})_{n}
of
\mathcal{H}
. We then set, for
h\in\mathcal{H}
,

\displaystyle Mh=\sum_{n}\mu_{n}\langle h,f_{n}\rangle f_{n},

\displaystyle Ch=\sum_{n}\gamma_{n}\langle h,e_{n}\rangle e_{n}.

Then
C
is injective and
\operatorname{ran}{C}=\{h\in\mathcal{H}:\ \sum_{n}\gamma_{n}^{2}\langle h,e_{n}\rangle^{2}<\infty\}
. As
\operatorname{ran}{C}
contains
\{e_{n}\}_{n}
, it is dense in
\mathcal{H}
, thus
\operatorname{ran}{C}^{\perp}=\{0\}
. Therefore,
\operatorname{dom}{C^{\dagger}}=\operatorname{ran}{C}
and the Moore–Penrose inverse of
C
is given, as in
Example

A.6
, by

\displaystyle C^{\dagger}h=\sum_{n}\gamma_{n}^{-1}\langle h,e_{n}\rangle e_{n},\quad h\in\operatorname{dom}{C^{\dagger}}.

Let us take
B=I
for simplicity, so
P_{\overline{\operatorname{ran}{B}}}=I
. As
C
is injective,
P_{\overline{\operatorname{ran}{B}}}MP_{\ker{C}^{\perp}}=M
. Since
(\mu_{n})_{n}
is nonincreasing, for any
r\geq 1
, one of the rank-
r
truncated SVDs of
P_{\overline{\operatorname{ran}{B}}}MP_{\ker{C}^{\perp}}
is given by

\displaystyle(P_{\overline{\operatorname{ran}{B}}}MP_{\ker{C}^{\perp}})_{r}h=\sum_{n=1}^{r}\mu_{n}\langle h,f_{n}\rangle f_{n}.

(4.1)

With this SVD for given
r\geq 1
, we now compute for
h\in\operatorname{dom}{C}^{\dagger}
,

\displaystyle B^{\dagger}(P_{\overline{\operatorname{ran}{B}}}MP_{\ker{C}^{\perp}})_{r}C^{\dagger}h=\sum_{n=1}^{r}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\mu_{n}\gamma_{m}^{-1}\langle h,e_{m}\rangle\langle e_{m},f_{n}\rangle f_{n}.

As
f_{1}=w\lVert w\rVert^{-1}
, it follows for
h=e_{m}\in\operatorname{dom}{C^{\dagger}}
,
m\geq 2
,

\displaystyle\lVert B^{\dagger}(P_{\overline{\operatorname{ran}{B}}}MP_{\ker{C}^{\perp}})_{r}C^{\dagger}e_{m}\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{r}\big\lvert\mu_{n}\gamma^{-1}_{m}\langle e_{m},f_{n}\rangle\big\rvert^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\big\lvert\mu_{1}\gamma^{-1}_{m}\langle e_{m},f_{1}\rangle\big\rvert^{2}=\lvert\mu_{1}\rvert^{2}\lvert\alpha_{m}\rvert^{2}\lVert w\rVert^{-2}\rightarrow\infty

as

, showing that
B^{\dagger}(P_{\overline{\operatorname{ran}{B}}}MP_{\ker{C}^{\perp}})_{r}C^{\dagger}
is unbounded. This shows that (
3.3
) can indeed give unbounded solutions. If
\mu_{1}>\mu_{2}
and
r=1
, then (
3.3
) gives a unique solution, which shows that (
3.3
) can also give only unbounded solutions. In this case,
\mathcal{S}_{r,*}
only contains unbounded solutions, so that in fact all solutions of (
3.2
) are unbounded by
Corollary

3.10
.

Now let
r=1
and
\mu_{1}=\mu_{2}
, so that
P_{\overline{\operatorname{ran}{B}}}MP_{\ker{C}^{\perp}}
has at least two distinct rank-
1
truncated SVDs.
The rank-
1
truncated SVD (
4.1
), i.e.
(P_{\overline{\operatorname{ran}{B}}}MP_{\ker{C}^{\perp}})_{1}=\mu_{1}\langle\cdot,f_{1}\rangle f_{1}
, yields the following solution, which is of the form (
3.3
):

\displaystyle\hat{X}_{a}h\coloneqq\mu_{1}\langle C^{\dagger}h,f_{1}\rangle f_{1}=\mu_{1}\sum_{n}\gamma_{n}^{-1}\langle h,e_{n}\rangle\langle e_{n},f_{1}\rangle f_{1}=\mu_{1}\lVert w\rVert^{-1}\sum_{n\geq 2}\alpha_{n}\langle h,e_{n}\rangle f_{1},

for

. By again taking

,
\hat{X}_{a}
is seen to be unbounded.
As
f_{2}=e_{1}
, the rank-

\mu_{2}\langle\cdot,e_{1}\rangle e_{1}
yields the solution

\displaystyle\hat{X}_{b}h\coloneqq\mu_{2}\langle C^{\dagger}h,e_{1}\rangle e_{1}=\mu_{2}\gamma_{1}^{-1}\langle h,e_{1}\rangle e_{1},\quad h\in\operatorname{dom}{C^{\dagger}},

which is bounded.

We have thus constructed two solutions of the form (

): one of these is bounded, in particular it has finite rank and has finite Hilbert–Schmidt norm; the other is unbounded.

We give a final sufficient condition, namely a lower bound condition, that is weaker than

is closed. We recall that the quotient space

then is a Banach space with norm

Corollary 5.3
.

Suppose that there exists

such that
\lVert Ch\rVert\geq c\lVert h+\ker{C}\rVert
for all

\hat{X}
is bounded.

Proof.

Let us denote
W\coloneqq\ker{Z}\cap\ker{C}^{\perp}
. We show that
C(W)
is closed in
\mathcal{H}_{2}
. Then
C(W)
is also closed in
\operatorname{ran}{C}
and we may apply
Proposition

5.2
. Now, we can decompose

for some
\tilde{C}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{1}/\ker{C},\mathcal{H}_{2})
. Let
(w_{n})_{n}
be a sequence in
W
such that
(Cw_{n})_{n}=(\tilde{C}(w_{n}+\ker{C}))_{n}
converges to

. We need to show

for some
w\in W
. By assumption,
\lVert w_{n}-w_{m}+\ker{C}\rVert\leq c^{-1}\lVert C(w_{n}-w_{m})\rVert
, so
(w_{n}+\ker{C})_{n}
is a Cauchy sequence in
\mathcal{H}_{1}/\ker{C}
.
But
\mathcal{H}_{1}/\ker{C}
is complete, so
w_{n}+\ker{C}
converges to some

. As

and

are orthogonal, their direct sum

is closed, c.f.
[
4
, Section 9.2.(A)]
. Hence

, i.e.
h+\ker{C}=w+\ker{C}
for some

, by continuity of
\tilde{C}
.
∎

In case

has closed range, the operator

in the proof of

also has closed range. But

is also injective, so it has a bounded inverse on its range, by the inverse mapping theorem, c.f.
[

, Theorem III.12.5]
. Hence

\displaystyle\lVert h+\ker{C}\rVert=\lVert\tilde{C}^{-1}\tilde{C}h\rVert\leq\lVert\tilde{C}^{-1}\rVert\lVert Ch\rVert,\quad\text{for all }h\in\mathcal{H}_{1}.

(5.1)

In particular, we conclude that the lower bound condition of
Corollary

5.3
is satisfied with

in the case where
C
has closed range.
The lower bound condition in
Corollary

5.3
need not hold everywhere on
\mathcal{H}_{1}
, so it is weaker than (
5.1
). Hence, the condition in
Corollary

5.3
is strictly weaker than the condition that
\operatorname{ran}{C}
is closed.

5.2
Bounded approximations

Even when the equivalent conditions of
Proposition

5.2
do not hold, we can enforce bounded solutions by instead considering the problem

\displaystyle\inf\{\lVert M-BXC\rVert_{L_{2}}:\ X\in\mathcal{B}_{00,r}(\mathcal{H}_{2},\mathcal{H}_{3})\},

(5.2)

with the minimality property

\displaystyle P_{\ker{B}^{\perp}}XP_{\overline{\operatorname{ran}{C}}}=X.

(5.3)

As
\mathcal{B}_{00,r}(\mathcal{H}_{2},\mathcal{H}_{3})\subset\mathcal{X}_{r}
, this infimum is bounded from below by the minimum in (
3.2
). In the case that

is closed, the problems (

)-(
3.4
) and (
5.2
)-(
5.3
) coincide by
Remark

3.5
(i)
. Let us therefore assume that
\operatorname{ran}{C}
is not closed, so

is infinite-dimensional by

5.1
. We now show that in this case the infimum (

3.2
). To do so, we construct a minimizing sequence
\hat{X}_{n}\in\mathcal{B}_{00,r}(\mathcal{H}_{2},\mathcal{H}_{3})
to (
5.2
)-(
5.3

\hat{X}_{n}\rightarrow\hat{X}
pointwise on
\operatorname{dom}{\hat{X}}
. We do this under the assumption that
\mathcal{Y}_{r}\not=\emptyset
and hence that a solution
\hat{X}
of the form (
3.3

Given
\hat{X}
of the form (
3.3
), we construct the approximating sequence
\hat{X}_{n}\in\mathcal{B}_{00,r}(\mathcal{H}_{2},\mathcal{H}_{3})
based on the approximation of the unbounded Moore–Penrose inverse
C^{\dagger}
by outer inverses as proposed by
[
25
]
. We briefly repeat the construction and its properties here, but refer to
[
25
]
for elaboration, see also
[

Let
(Y_{n})_{n}
be an increasing sequence of finite-dimensional subspaces of

such that

, so

has finite rank. It can be shown, c.f.
[
25
, p114]
, that
(X_{n})_{n}
is an increasing sequence of finite-dimensional subspaces, that

and that

,
\operatorname{ran}{C_{n}}=Y_{n}
so that
{\left.\kern-1.2ptC_{n}\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{X_{n}}}:X_{n}\rightarrow Y_{n}
is a boundedly invertible operator. Hence, we can define the finite-rank operator

as

\displaystyle C_{n}^{\#}(y)\coloneqq\begin{cases}({\left.\kern-1.2ptC_{n}\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{X_{n}}})^{-1}(y),\quad y\in Y_{n},\\

The following result states the approximation property of

by

, see
[

, Theorem 2.1]
for a proof.

Theorem 5.4
.

The sequence
(C_{n})_{n}\subset\mathcal{B}_{00}(\mathcal{H}_{2},\mathcal{H}_{1})
satisfies the following:

(i)

\operatorname{dom}{C^{\dagger}}=\{h\in\mathcal{H}_{2}:\lim_{n}C_{n}^{\#}h\text{ exists}\},

(ii)

C^{\dagger}h=\lim_{n}C_{n}^{\#}h
for

,

(iii)

is an outer inverse of
C.

Under the condition
\mathcal{Y}_{r}\not=\emptyset
, let us now set

\displaystyle\hat{X}_{n}\coloneqq B^{\dagger}(P_{\overline{\operatorname{ran}{B}}}MP_{\ker{C}^{\perp}})_{r}C_{n}^{\#}.

(5.4)

6
Application in Signal Processing, Reduced Rank Regression and Linear Operator Learning

In this section we describe how
Theorem

3.2
can be leveraged to obtain results in infinite-dimensional settings. As example applications, we consider the fields of signal processing, reduced rank regression, and linear operator learning.
We first recall some examples of approximation problems that arise in these applications, before describing a problem that includes these examples as special cases.

In the field of signal processing, one common problem is to reconstruct a random input signal
x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
from a random observation signal
y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
that is related to
x
. One can assume that each observation satisfies
y=Gx+\varepsilon
for some known linear map
G
and additive noise
\varepsilon
, or weaker assumptions can be made on the relationship between
x
and
y
. In
[
51
]
, one considers the problem of reconstructing the signal
x
as
Ay
, assuming additional structure only in the form of a known covariance matrix of
(x,y)
. To do so, the reconstruction quality is assessed in the expected squared reconstruction error, and thus the objective is to minimise
\mathbb{E}\lVert x-Ay\rVert^{2}
over
A
. As the observation signal can be noisy, the rank of
A
is restricted in order to filter out this noise. For more generality and motivated by applications, one can also allow
x
,
y
, and the reconstruction
Ay
to be weighted by matrices
W_{x},W_{y}
and
W_{A}
. The problem then becomes to find the rank-constrained minimiser
A
of
\mathbb{E}\lVert W_{x}x-W_{A}AW_{y}y\rVert^{2}
.
In
[
53
]
, one considers an extension of this problem in which not just one signal but sets of signals
\{x(\alpha)\in\mathbb{R}^{m}:\ \alpha\in K\}
and
\{y(\alpha)\in\mathbb{R}^{m}:\ \alpha\in K\}
are considered, where
K
is a suitable index set that may be countably or uncountably infinite, e.g.
K=\mathbb{N}
or
K=[0,1]
, and
x(\alpha)
and
y(\alpha)
are finite-dimensional random vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
for every
\alpha
.
Below, we consider the settings in
[
51
,
53
]
in the case of infinite-dimensional signals, that is, in case the signals
x(\alpha)
and
y(\alpha)
are function-valued for every
\alpha\in K
.

In the setting of reduced rank regression of
[
27
]
, the task is to find a mean vector
\mu
and rank-restricted matrix
A
that minimize the mean square error
\mathbb{E}\lVert\Gamma(x-\mu-Ay)\rVert^{2}
for random vectors
x
and
y
and fixed weighting matrix
\Gamma
. Assuming
x
and
y
have zero mean so that
\mu=0
and writing
W_{x}=\Gamma=W_{A}
and
W_{y}=I
, we recover the same formulation as in the signal processing setting. This also relates to the regression setting of
[
62
]
, where
x
and
y
are allowed to be infinite-dimensional and are transformed through kernel functions first. Furthermore, in
[
28
]
, one studies the reduced rank regression problem of minimizing
\lVert BX-M\rVert
where
M
and
B
are given matrices,
\lVert\cdot\rVert
denotes the operator norm, and
X
is restricted in rank.
Below, we study the case that
x
and
y
in
[
27
]
may be infinite-dimensional and that the operator norm in
[
28
]
is replaced with the Hilbert–Schmidt norm.

A closely related problem that arises in the setting of linear operator learning of
[
36
]
is to minimise
\mathbb{E}\lVert x-Ay\rVert^{2}
, where
x
and
y
are allowed to take values in a separable Hilbert space, and where the search space for

is taken to be either the space of bounded operators or Hilbert–Schmidt operators between these Hilbert spaces. This has applications in various kinds of regression problems, such as functional regression with functional response; see
[
36
]
. In this section, we study the operator learning problem in the case that

is bounded and the rank of

is restricted and finite.

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
be a countably generated probability space, so that

is separable, c.f.
[

[

Remark 6.6

(Relation between minimality condition and maximal kernel)
.

The minimality condition
\hat{A}=\hat{A}P_{\ker{C_{y}}^{\perp}}
of
Corollary

6.4
is equivalent to
\hat{A}(\ker{C}_{y})=0
. In
[
36
, Proposition 3.11 and Remark 3.12(b)]
it is shown that the solution of the unconstrained problem also satisfies this property, and in fact has maximal kernel among all solutions. The solution of the rank-constrained problem (
6.9
) can be seen to have maximal kernel among all solutions as well. Indeed, by applying
Corollary

3.9
to (
6.8
) and taking the adjoint, and using
\ker{C_{y}^{1/2}}=\ker{C_{y}}
as explained in the proof of
Corollary

6.4
, we see that an arbitrary solution of (
6.9
) is of the form
\tilde{A}\coloneqq\hat{A}+T^{*}P_{\ker{C}_{y}}
for some bounded
T:F\rightarrow G
. Now
\ker{(T^{*}P_{\ker{C}_{y}})}^{\perp}\subset\ker{C}_{y}
. Furthermore,
\ker{\hat{A}}^{\perp}\subset\ker{C_{y}}^{\perp}
because
\hat{A}(\ker{\mathcal{C}_{y}})=0
. Hence
\ker{\tilde{A}}
is maximal if and only if
T^{*}P_{\ker{C_{y}}}=0
, which holds if and only if
\tilde{A}=\hat{A}
. Furthermore,
\tilde{A}=\hat{A}
holds if and only if
\tilde{A}
satisfies the minimality property
\tilde{A}P_{\ker{C_{y}}^{\perp}}=\tilde{A}
. Thus, the minimality property originating from (
3.4
) is exactly the maximal kernel property mentioned in
[
36
, Proposition 3.11 and Remark 3.12(b)]
.

7
Conclusion

Generalised rank-constrained matrix approximation problems in Frobenius norm have had a myriad of applications, for example in signal processing, statistical inverse problems and data transmission systems.
These finite-dimensional approximation problems have been analysed in
[
38
,
19
,
46
]
, for example. In this work we have extended the analysis in the following ways.

In
[
19
]
, a solution to the generalised rank-constrained approximation problem was proposed, which also satisfies a minimal norm property. We have shown that this property does not hold in general. We have proposed the modified minimality property (
3.7
) which is satisfied in general. The modified result in the finite-dimensional setting is given in
Corollary

3.8
.

Secondly, we have studied the infinite-dimensional version of the generalised approximation problem, studying existence, uniqueness and continuity of a solution. In particular, we have given necessary and sufficient conditions for existence and uniqueness of a solution. This is done in
Theorem

3.2
.
Theorem

3.2
also gives explicit solutions that satisfy the minimality property. We have also shown that the proposed solutions—and in fact all solutions—may be discontinuous in
Section

4
, and have given necessary and sufficient conditions for the proposed solutions to be continuous in
Section

5.1
. In addition, we have constructed approximate solutions when no solution exists in
Section

3.2
, and continuous approximations to discontinuous solutions in
Section

5.2
.

Thirdly and finally, we have studied problems from signal processing, reduced rank regression and linear operator learning in infinite-dimensional settings. In particular, we have generalised the signal processing problem of
[
51
,
53
]
in the case where both the unknown signal and the observation are finite-dimensional random vectors, to the case where both can be random functions. We have also studied the reduced rank regression setting of
[
28
]
to infinite dimensions but with Hilbert–Schmidt norm error instead of operator norm error. Finally, we have discussed the linear operator learning setting of
[
36
]
, where in our case the operator to be learned is not only bounded or Hilbert–Schmidt but has finite rank. In these settings, we used
Theorem

3.2
to find explicit solutions, and to give necessary and sufficient conditions for existence and uniqueness of solutions.

In summary, since finite-dimensional approximation problems often originate from a discretised version of an infinite-dimensional problem, our infinite-dimensional result paves the way for the analysis of various reduced rank approximation problems such as reduced rank linear operator learning in their native infinite-dimensional formulation.
