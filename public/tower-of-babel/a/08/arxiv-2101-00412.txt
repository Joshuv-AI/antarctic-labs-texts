Mean-Field Linear-Quadratic Stochastic Differential Games
Jingrui Sun Hanxiao Wang Zhen Wu

Abstract




Abstract.

The paper is concerned with two-person zero-sum mean-field linear-quadratic stochastic differential games
over finite horizons.
By a Hilbert space method, a necessary condition and a sufficient condition are derived for the existence
of an open-loop saddle point.
It is shown that under the sufficient condition, the associated two Riccati equations admit unique strongly
regular solutions, in terms of which the open-loop saddle point can be represented as a linear feedback
of the current state.
When the game only satisfies the necessary condition,
an approximate sequence is constructed by solving a family of Riccati equations and closed-loop systems.
The convergence of the approximate sequence turns out to be equivalent to the open-loop solvability of the game,
and the limit is exactly an open-loop saddle point, provided that the game is open-loop solvable.

Keywords.

linear-quadratic differential game, mean-field stochastic differential equation,
two-person, zero-sum, open-loop saddle point, Riccati equation, closed-loop representation,
perturbation approach.

AMS subject classifications.
91A15, 93E20, 49N10, 49N70.

Introduction

Let
(\Omega,{\cal F},\mathbb{P})
be a complete probability space on which a standard one-dimensional
Brownian motion
W=\{W(t);0\leqslant t<\infty\}

The augmented natural filtration of

is denoted by
\mathbb{F}=\{{\cal F}_{t}\}_{t\geqslant 0}

Consider the following controlled linear mean-field stochastic differential equation
(MF-SDE, for short) on a finite horizon
[0,T]

\left\{\begin{aligned} dX(s)&=\big\{A(s)X(s)+\bar{A}(s)\mathbb{E}[X(s)]+B_{1}(s)u_{1}(s)+\bar{B}_{1}(s)\mathbb{E}[u_{1}(s)]\\
&~\hphantom{=}+B_{2}(s)u_{2}(s)+\bar{B}_{2}(s)\mathbb{E}[u_{2}(s)]\big\}ds+\big\{C(s)X(s)+\bar{C}(s)\mathbb{E}[X(s)]\\
&~\hphantom{=}+D_{1}(s)u_{1}(s)+\bar{D}_{1}(s)\mathbb{E}[u_{1}(s)]+D_{2}(s)u_{2}(s)+\bar{D}_{2}(s)\mathbb{E}[u_{2}(s)]\big\}dW(s),\\
X(0)&=x,\end{aligned}\right.

(1.1)

where
A,\bar{A},\,C,\bar{C}:[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
,

(

(

The solution

,

(

is called the
control process
of Player
i
.
To measure the performance of the controls
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
, we introduce the following functional:

\displaystyle J(x;u_{1},u_{2})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big\{\langle GX(T),X(T)\rangle+\langle\bar{G}\mathbb{E}[X(T)],\mathbb{E}[X(T)]\rangle

\displaystyle~\hphantom{=}+\int_{0}^{T}\left\langle\!\begin{pmatrix}Q&S_{1}^{\top}&S_{2}^{\top}\\
S_{1}&R_{11}&R_{12}\\
S_{2}&R_{21}&R_{22}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}X\\
u_{1}\\
u_{2}\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}X\\

u_{2}\end{pmatrix}\!\right\rangle ds

\bar{S}_{2}&\bar{R}_{21}&\bar{R}_{22}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{E}[X]\\

\mathbb{E}[u_{1}]\\
\mathbb{E}[u_{2}]\end{pmatrix}\!\right\rangle ds\Bigg\},

where

\bar{G}

Q,\bar{Q}:[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
,

R_{ij},\bar{R}_{ij}:[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}^{m_{i}\times m_{j}}

,

In the Lebesgue integral on the right-hand side of (

The functional
J(x;u_{1},u_{2})
represents the cost of Player
1
and the payoff of Player

for

u_{1}
and
u_{2}
to control the state process that starts from

.
Naturally, in this two-person zero-sum mean-field linear-quadratic stochastic differential game
(Problem (MF-SG), for short), Player 1 wishes to minimize (

) by selecting his/her control

The control pair

of Problem (MF-SG), which is mathematically defined by the following inequalities:

J(x;u^{*}_{1},u_{2})\leqslant J(x;u^{*}_{1},u^{*}_{2})\leqslant J(x;u_{1},u^{*}_{2}),\quad\forall(u_{1},u_{2})\in{\cal U}_{1}\times{\cal U}_{2}.

In this sense, an open-loop saddle point (if exists) will be the best choice for both players.

Let us briefly recall the motivation for studying mean-field LQ stochastic optimal control problems proposed by Yong

In some cases, one hopes that the

To achieve this, one needs to keep the variances var

Therefore, it is natural to take var

\displaystyle J(x;u)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big\{\langle GX(T),X(T)\rangle+g\hbox{var$\,$}[X(T)]+\int_{0}^{T}\big[\langle Q(s)X(s),X(s)\rangle

\displaystyle~\hphantom{=}+q(s)\hbox{var$\,$}[X(s)]+\langle R(s)u(s),u(s)\rangle+r(s)\hbox{var$\,$}[u(s)]\big]ds\Big\}.

(1.4)

In particular, in the mean-variance model (see
[

\mu\hbox{var$\,$}[X(T)]-\mathbb{E}[X(T)]

Note that

\hbox{var$\,$}[X(s)]=\mathbb{E}[|X(s)|^{2}]-(\mathbb{E}[X(s)])^{2},\quad\hbox{var$\,$}[u(s)]=\mathbb{E}[|u(s)|^{2}]-(\mathbb{E}[u(s)])^{2}.

due to the presence of

For mean-field LQ control problems, two Riccati equations are derived by Yong
[
33
]

to construct an open-loop optimal control.
The solvability of these two Riccati equations is established in
[
33
]
under
certain positivity conditions and is further shown to be equivalent to the uniform convexity
of the cost functional by Sun
[

.
However, to our best knowledge, there are few significant results on the general solvability
of the Riccati equations associated with Problem (MF-SG) so far.
One of the main contributions of this paper is to fill up this gap.
Compared with Sun
[
24
]
, the additional difficulty mainly comes from the solvability of
the second Riccati equation associated with Problem (MF-SG). To overcome this difficulty,
besides establishing a technical
2.3
,
we also show that the solution of the first Riccati equation satisfies a
comparison property

(i.e., inequality (
4.7
) in
Theorem 4.2
).
This property follows from the following observations:
One control in the saddle point of Problem (SG)
is optimal for a backward stochastic LQ control problem
and the value function of this backward problem is given exactly in terms of the solution to the first Riccati equation.
This observation is interesting in its own right and to our best knowledge, it is completely new in the literature.

In the literature, forward-backward stochastic differential equations (FBSDEs, for short) are usually used to
characterize the open-loop solvability of Problem (MF-SG) (see, for example,
[
28
,
29
]
).
This method is suitable for deciding whether a control pair is an open-loop saddle point or not,
but not very effective in constructing open-loop saddle points,
because the associated Riccati equations might be not solvable and then
the optimality system cannot be decoupled.
This paper provides an alternative characterization (see
Theorem 5.1
)
for the open-loop solvability of Problem (MF-SG) by a perturbation approach, which can be regarded
as another important contribution.
It is worthy to point out that the characterization is new even for Problem (SG) (in which there are
no mean-field terms present).

The idea is to add two terms,
\varepsilon\|u_{1}\|^{2}
and
-\varepsilon\|u_{2}\|^{2}
, to the original functional so that
the Problem (MF-SG) with the new functional
J_{\varepsilon}(x;u_{1},u_{2})\triangleq J(x;u_{1},u_{2})+\varepsilon\|u_{1}\|^{2}-\varepsilon\|u_{2}\|^{2}

admits a unique open-loop point
(u_{1}^{\varepsilon},u_{2}^{\varepsilon})
that can be represented explicitly in terms of
the solutions to the associated Riccati equations.
Then using the boundedness/convergence of the family
\{(u_{1}^{\varepsilon},u_{2}^{\varepsilon})\}_{\varepsilon>0}
to justify the open-loop solvability
of the original game.
The main difficulty here is that the value function
V_{\varepsilon}(x)
of the perturbed game is not monotone in
\varepsilon
,
due to which the technique used in the LQ control problem (see
[
25
]
) cannot be applied directly.
The significant difference between the perturbation methods of controls and games is illustrated
by presenting an elaborate example (see
5.3
).
To overcome the difficulty, we restudy the perturbation approach by a Hilbert space method,
which helps us to change the boundedness problem of
\{(u_{1}^{\varepsilon},u_{2}^{\varepsilon})\}_{\varepsilon>0}
into an equivalent one:
the norm estimate of some perturbed operators with special structures
(see
2.4
).
Furthermore, it is found that the explicit upper bound estimate (
2.5
) in
2.4

also plays a crucial role in proving the strong convergence of
\{(u_{1}^{\varepsilon},u_{2}^{\varepsilon})\}_{\varepsilon>0}
,
because in
Theorem 5.1
we hope to show that
\{(u_{1}^{\varepsilon},u_{2}^{\varepsilon})\}_{\varepsilon>0}
itself is strongly convergent
when Problem (MF-SG) is open-loop solvable.

To summarize, we list the main contributions of the paper as follows.

(1) The open-loop solvability of Problem (MF-SG) is studied by a Hilbert space method.
A necessary and sufficient condition for the existence of an open-loop saddle point
is derived (see
3.1
).

(2) Under the uniform convexity-concavity condition, the strongly regular solvability
of the Riccati equations associated with Problem (MF-SG) is established (see
Theorem 4.2

and
Theorem 4.4
).
Further, in terms of the solutions to the Riccati equations, a closed-loop representation of the unique
open-loop saddle point is obtained (see
Theorem 4.6
).

(3) Under a necessary condition for the existence of an open-loop saddle point,
an equivalent characterization of the open-loop solvability is established
by a perturbation approach.
This approach also provides an explicit procedure for finding open-loop saddle
points (see
Theorem 5.1
).

In other words, under the uniform convexity-concavity condition, we first extend the results obtained in Sun
[
24
]

to the mean-field system.
As explained before, to prove the solvability of the associated Riccati equations,
we need to make some new observations and to overcome some new difficulties.
Then under the weaker convexity-concavity condition,
we develop a perturbation approach to characterize the open-loop solvability of Problem (MF-SG).
This approach is first established for the game problem and can be regarded as the most technical part in the paper.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows.
Section
2
collects some preliminary results.
Section
3
is devoted to the study of the performance functional
from a Hilbert space point of view.
Section
4
establishes the solvability of the associated Riccati
equations and provides a closed-loop representation of the open-loop saddle point.
Section
5
investigates the open-loop solvability of Problem (MF-SG)
by a perturbation method.
An example is presented in Section
6
to illustrate the results obtained
in previous sections.

Proposition 2.4
.

Suppose that
{\cal M}_{11}
and
-{\cal M}_{22}
are positive operators; that is
{\cal M}_{11}\geqslant 0
and
-{\cal M}_{22}\geqslant 0

Then for any

{\cal M}_{\varepsilon}\triangleq\begin{pmatrix}{\cal M}_{11}+\varepsilon I&{\cal M}_{12}\\

\displaystyle\|{\cal M}_{\varepsilon}^{-1}{\cal M}\|\leqslant 1,\quad\forall\varepsilon>0.

(2.5)

Proof.

Since
{\cal M}_{11}+\varepsilon I\geqslant\varepsilon I
,
{\cal M}_{11,\varepsilon}\triangleq{\cal M}_{11}+\varepsilon I

\Phi_{\varepsilon}\triangleq{\cal M}_{22,\varepsilon}-{\cal M}_{21}{\cal M}_{11,\varepsilon}^{-1}{\cal M}_{12}\equiv{\cal M}_{22,\varepsilon}-{\cal M}_{12}^{*}{\cal M}_{11,\varepsilon}^{-1}{\cal M}_{12}

{\cal M}_{\varepsilon}
is invertible with inverse

{\cal M}_{\varepsilon}^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}{\cal M}_{11,\varepsilon}^{-1}+({\cal M}_{11,\varepsilon}^{-1}{\cal M}_{12})\Phi_{\varepsilon}^{-1}({\cal M}_{11,\varepsilon}^{-1}{\cal M}_{12})^{*}&-({\cal M}_{11,\varepsilon}^{-1}{\cal M}_{12})\Phi_{\varepsilon}^{-1}\\[2.84526pt]
-\Phi_{\varepsilon}^{-1}({\cal M}_{11,\varepsilon}^{-1}{\cal M}_{12})^{*}&\Phi_{\varepsilon}^{-1}\end{pmatrix}.

To prove (
2.5
), we write

{\cal M}_{\varepsilon}^{-1}{\cal M}=\begin{pmatrix}I&0\\
0&I\end{pmatrix}-\varepsilon{\cal M}_{\varepsilon}^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}[r]I&0\\
0&-I\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}I&0\\
0&I\end{pmatrix}-\varepsilon\begin{pmatrix}{\cal M}_{11,\varepsilon}&{\cal M}_{12}\\
-{\cal M}_{12}^{*}&-{\cal M}_{22,\varepsilon}\end{pmatrix}^{-1}.

Denote

\hat{\cal M}_{\varepsilon}\triangleq\begin{pmatrix}{\cal M}_{11,\varepsilon}&{\cal M}_{12}\\
-{\cal M}_{12}^{*}&-{\cal M}_{22,\varepsilon}\end{pmatrix}.

(2.6)

Then

\displaystyle{\cal M}_{\varepsilon}^{-1}{\cal M}=I-\varepsilon\hat{\cal M}_{\varepsilon}^{-1},

(2.7)

and thus

\displaystyle({\cal M}_{\varepsilon}^{-1}{\cal M})^{*}{\cal M}_{\varepsilon}^{-1}{\cal M}

\displaystyle=(I-\varepsilon\hat{\cal M}_{\varepsilon}^{-1})^{*}(I-\varepsilon\hat{\cal M}_{\varepsilon}^{-1})

\displaystyle=I-\varepsilon(\hat{\cal M}_{\varepsilon}^{-1})^{*}-\varepsilon\hat{\cal M}_{\varepsilon}^{-1}+\varepsilon^{2}(\hat{\cal M}_{\varepsilon}^{-1})^{*}\hat{\cal M}_{\varepsilon}^{-1}

\displaystyle=I-\varepsilon(\hat{\cal M}_{\varepsilon}^{-1})^{*}\big[\hat{\cal M}_{\varepsilon}+\hat{\cal M}_{\varepsilon}^{*}-\varepsilon I\big]\hat{\cal M}_{\varepsilon}^{-1}.

(2.8)

Note that

\displaystyle\hat{\cal M}_{\varepsilon}+\hat{\cal M}_{\varepsilon}^{*}-\varepsilon I

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}{\cal M}_{11,\varepsilon}&{\cal M}_{12}\\
-{\cal M}_{12}^{*}&-{\cal M}_{22,\varepsilon}\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}{\cal M}_{11,\varepsilon}&-{\cal M}_{12}\\
{\cal M}_{12}^{*}&-{\cal M}_{22,\varepsilon}\end{pmatrix}-\varepsilon\begin{pmatrix}I&0\\
0&I\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}2{\cal M}_{11}+\varepsilon I&0\\
0&-2{\cal M}_{22}+\varepsilon I\end{pmatrix}.

Using the fact that
{\cal M}_{11}
and
-{\cal M}_{22}
are positive operators, we have

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}2{\cal M}_{11}+\varepsilon I&0\\
0&-2{\cal M}_{22}+\varepsilon I\end{pmatrix}\geqslant 0;

that is

\hat{\cal M}_{\varepsilon}+\hat{\cal M}_{\varepsilon}^{*}-\varepsilon I\geqslant 0,

which implies that

\varepsilon(\hat{\cal M}_{\varepsilon}^{-1})^{*}\big[\hat{\cal M}_{\varepsilon}+\hat{\cal M}_{\varepsilon}^{*}-\varepsilon I\big]\hat{\cal M}_{\varepsilon}^{-1}\geqslant 0.

Combining the above with (
2.8
) yields

\displaystyle 0\leqslant({\cal M}_{\varepsilon}^{-1}{\cal M})^{*}{\cal M}_{\varepsilon}^{-1}{\cal M}=I-\varepsilon(\hat{\cal M}_{\varepsilon}^{-1})^{*}\big[\hat{\cal M}_{\varepsilon}+\hat{\cal M}_{\varepsilon}^{*}-\varepsilon I\big]\hat{\cal M}_{\varepsilon}^{-1}\leqslant I.

(2.9)

Thus, we get

\|{\cal M}_{\varepsilon}^{-1}{\cal M}\|^{2}=\|({\cal M}_{\varepsilon}^{-1}{\cal M})^{*}{\cal M}_{\varepsilon}^{-1}{\cal M}\|\leqslant 1.

The proof is complete.
∎

3
Representation of the functional

In this section, we shall study the functional (
1
) from a Hilbert space point of view and
represent it as a quadratic functional of the controls
(u_{1},u_{2})
,
by which a necessary condition and a sufficient condition will be derived for the open-loop solvability.
For any
u_{i}\in{\cal U}_{i}
(
i=1,2
), consider the following MF-SDE:

\left\{\begin{aligned} dX^{i,0}(s)&=\big\{A(s)X^{i,0}(s)+\bar{A}(s)\mathbb{E}[X^{i,0}(s)]+B_{i}(s)u_{i}(s)+\bar{B}_{i}(s)\mathbb{E}[u_{i}(s)]\big\}ds\\
&\hphantom{=}~+\big\{C(s)X^{i,0}(s)+\bar{C}(s)\mathbb{E}[X^{i,0}(s)]+D_{i}(s)u_{i}(s)\\
&\hphantom{=}~+\bar{D}_{i}(s)\mathbb{E}[u_{i}(s)]\big\}dW(s),\quad s\in[0,T],\\
X^{i,0}(0)&=0.\end{aligned}\right.

(3.1)

Under
(H1)
, the above MF-SDE admits a unique solution
X^{i,0}\in L_{\mathbb{F}}^{2}(\Omega;C([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{n}))

satisfying

\mathbb{E}\Big[\sup_{s\in[0,T]}|X^{i,0}(s)|^{2}\Big]\leqslant K\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{T}|u_{i}(s)|^{2}ds,

(3.2)

where the constant
K>0
is independent of
u_{i}
.
Thus we can define two bounded linear operators
{\cal L}_{i}:{\cal U}_{i}\to L_{\mathbb{F}}^{2}(\Omega;C([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{n}))

and
\hat{\cal L}_{i}:{\cal U}_{i}\to L_{{\cal F}_{T}}^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
as follows:

{\cal L}_{i}u_{i}=X^{i,0},\quad\hat{\cal L}_{i}u_{i}=X^{i,0}(T),\quad\forall u_{i}\in{\cal U}_{i};\quad i=1,2.

(3.3)

Also we can define the linear operators
{\cal N}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to L_{\mathbb{F}}^{2}(\Omega;C([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{n}))

and
\hat{\cal N}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to L_{{\cal F}_{T}}^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
as follows:

{\cal N}x=X^{0,x},\quad\hat{\cal N}x=X^{0,x}(T),\quad\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

(3.4)

with
X^{0,x}
being the unique solution to the following MF-SDE:

\left\{\begin{aligned} dX^{0,x}(s)&=\big\{A(s)X^{0,x}(s)+\bar{A}(s)\mathbb{E}[X^{0,x}(s)]\big\}ds\\
&\hphantom{=}~+\big\{C(s)X^{0,x}(s)+\bar{C}(s)\mathbb{E}[X^{0,x}(s)]\big\}dW(s),\quad s\in[0,T],\\
X^{0,x}(0)&=x.\end{aligned}\right.

(3.5)

For any given
(x,u_{1},u_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times{\cal U}_{1}\times{\cal U}_{2}
,
it is easily checked that
X^{0,x}+X^{1,0}+X^{2,0}
satisfies state equation (
1.1
).
Thus, by the uniqueness of the solution to MF-SDE (
1.1
), we have

X=X^{0,x}+X^{1,0}+X^{2,0}={\cal N}x+{\cal L}_{1}u_{1}+{\cal L}_{2}u_{2},\quad\forall(x,u_{1},u_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times{\cal U}_{1}\times{\cal U}_{2}.

(3.6)

In particular, the terminal value of
X
can be represented by

\displaystyle X(T)

\displaystyle=X^{0,x}(T)+X^{1,0}(T)+X^{2,0}(T)

\displaystyle=\hat{\cal N}x+\hat{\cal L}_{1}u_{1}+\hat{\cal L}_{2}u_{2},\quad\forall(x,u_{1},u_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times{\cal U}_{1}\times{\cal U}_{2}.

(3.7)

Then using (
3.6
)–(
3.7
), by the completion
of squares technique it is straightforward to obtain the following representation of the functional (
1
):

\displaystyle J(x;u_{1},u_{2})=\langle{\cal M}u,u\rangle+2\langle{\cal K}x,u\rangle+\langle{\cal O}x,x\rangle,

\displaystyle~\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},~u=(u_{1}^{\top},u^{\top}_{2})^{\top}\in{\cal U}_{1}\times{\cal U}_{2},

(3.8)

where

{\cal M}\triangleq\begin{pmatrix}{\cal M}_{11}&{\cal M}_{12}\\
{\cal M}_{21}&{\cal M}_{22}\end{pmatrix},~{\cal K}\triangleq\begin{pmatrix}{\cal K}_{1}\\
{\cal K}_{2}\end{pmatrix},~{\cal O}\triangleq\hat{\cal N}^{*}(G+\mathbb{E}^{*}\bar{G}\mathbb{E})\hat{\cal N}+{\cal N}^{*}(Q+\mathbb{E}^{*}\bar{Q}\mathbb{E}){\cal N},

(3.9)

with

\displaystyle{\cal M}_{ij}\triangleq\hat{\cal L}_{j}^{*}(G+\mathbb{E}^{*}\bar{G}\mathbb{E})\hat{\cal L}_{i}+{\cal L}_{j}^{*}(Q+\mathbb{E}^{*}\bar{Q}\mathbb{E}){\cal L}_{i}+R_{ji}+\mathbb{E}^{*}\bar{R}_{ji}\mathbb{E}

\displaystyle\qquad\quad+(S_{j}+\mathbb{E}^{*}\bar{S}_{j}\mathbb{E}){\cal L}_{i}+{\cal L}_{j}^{*}(S_{i}^{\top}+\mathbb{E}^{*}\bar{S}_{i}^{\top}\mathbb{E}),\quad i,j=1,2;

(3.10)

\displaystyle{\cal K}_{i}\triangleq\hat{\cal L}_{i}^{*}(G+\mathbb{E}^{*}\bar{G}\mathbb{E})\hat{\cal N}+{\cal L}_{i}^{*}(Q+\mathbb{E}^{*}\bar{Q}\mathbb{E}){\cal N}+(S_{i}+\mathbb{E}^{*}\bar{S}_{i}\mathbb{E}){\cal N},\quad i=1,2.

(3.11)

By the above expression of
{\cal M}_{ij};i,j=1,2
, we have
{\cal M}_{ji}={\cal M}_{ij}^{*};\,i,j=1,2
,
which implies that
{\cal M}
is a self-adjoint operator. With the representation (
3.8
),
we provide the following characterization for the open-loop saddle points of Problem (MF-MG).

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
(H1)
–
(H2)
hold. Let
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be any given initial state
and
u^{*}=(u_{1}^{*\top},u_{2}^{*\top})^{\top}\in{\cal U}_{1}\times{\cal U}_{2}
.
Then
u^{*}
is an open-loop saddle point of
Problem (MF-MG)
for
x
if and only if

(-1)^{i+1}{\cal M}_{ii}\geqslant 0;~i=1,2\quad\hbox{and}\quad{\cal M}u^{*}+{\cal K}x=0.

(3.12)

Proof.

By
2.1
,
u^{*}=(u_{1}^{*\top},u_{2}^{*\top})^{\top}
is an open-loop saddle point of Problem (MF-MG)
if and only if

\displaystyle J(x;u_{1}^{*}+\lambda u_{1},u_{2}^{*})-J(x;u_{1}^{*},u_{2}^{*})\geqslant 0,\quad\forall u_{1}\in{\cal U}_{1},~\lambda\in\mathbb{R};

(3.13)

\displaystyle J(x;u_{1}^{*},u_{2}^{*}+\lambda u_{2})-J(x;u_{1}^{*},u_{2}^{*})\leqslant 0,\quad\forall u_{2}\in{\cal U}_{2},~\lambda\in\mathbb{R}.

(3.14)

For any
u_{1}\in{\cal U}_{1}
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, by (
3.8
) we have

\displaystyle J(x;u_{1}^{*}+\lambda u_{1},u_{2}^{*})-J(x;u_{1}^{*},u_{2}^{*})

\displaystyle\quad=\lambda^{2}\big\langle{\cal M}_{11}u_{1},u_{1}\big\rangle+2\lambda\big[\big\langle{\cal M}_{11}u^{*}_{1},u_{1}\big\rangle+\big\langle{\cal M}_{12}u^{*}_{2},u_{1}\big\rangle+\big\langle{\cal K}_{1}x,u_{1}\big\rangle\big].

(3.15)

Thus (
3.13
) holds if and only if

{\cal M}_{11}\geqslant 0\quad\hbox{and}\quad{\cal M}_{11}u^{*}_{1}+{\cal M}_{12}u^{*}_{2}+{\cal K}_{1}x=0.

(3.16)

By the same argument as the above, we can show that (
3.14
) holds if and only if

{\cal M}_{22}\leqslant 0\quad\hbox{and}\quad{\cal M}_{22}u^{*}_{2}+{\cal M}_{21}u^{*}_{1}+{\cal K}_{2}x=0.

(3.17)

Note that (
3.12
) is equivalent to (
3.16
) and (
3.17
).
The proof is thus complete.
∎

From
3.1
, we see that the following
convexity-concavity condition
,

\displaystyle\langle{\cal M}_{11}u_{1},u_{1}\rangle

\displaystyle=J(0;u_{1},0)\geqslant 0,\quad\forall u_{1}\in{\cal U}_{1};

(3.18)

\displaystyle\langle{\cal M}_{22}u_{2},u_{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=J(0;0,u_{2})\leqslant 0,\quad\forall u_{2}\in{\cal U}_{2},

is necessary for the existence of an open-loop saddle point.
Next we introduce a condition slightly stronger than (
3.18
):

(H3)

There exists a constant
\alpha>0
such that

\displaystyle\langle{\cal M}_{11}u_{1},u_{1}\rangle

\displaystyle=J(0;u_{1},0)\geqslant\alpha\|u_{1}\|^{2},\quad~~\forall u_{1}\in{\cal U}_{1};

(3.19)

\displaystyle\langle{\cal M}_{22}u_{2},u_{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=J(0;0,u_{2})\leqslant-\alpha\|u_{2}\|^{2},\quad\forall u_{2}\in{\cal U}_{2}.

If
(H3)
holds, for convenience we usually write (
3.19
) as follows:

\displaystyle\langle{\cal M}_{11}u_{1},u_{1}\rangle

\displaystyle=J(0;u_{1},0)\gg 0,\quad\forall u_{1}\in{\cal U}_{1};

\displaystyle\langle{\cal M}_{22}u_{2},u_{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=J(0;0,u_{2})\ll 0,\quad\forall u_{2}\in{\cal U}_{2}.

The following result shows that the
uniform convexity-concavity condition

(H3)

is sufficient for the open-loop solvability of Problem (MF-MG).

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
(H1)
–
(H3)
hold, and the operators
{\cal M}
and
{\cal K}
be defined by (
3.9
).
Then
{\cal M}
is invertible and for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

Problem (MF-MG)
admits a unique open-loop saddle point
u^{*}=(u_{1}^{*\top},u_{2}^{*\top})^{\top}
given by

u^{*}=-{\cal M}^{-1}{\cal K}x.

(3.20)

Proof.

By (
3.19
), we obtain that the operators
{\cal M}_{11}
,
{\cal M}_{22}
and

\Phi\triangleq{\cal M}_{22}-{\cal M}_{21}{\cal M}_{11}^{-1}{\cal M}_{12}
are invertible.
Then it is straightforward to verify that
{\cal M}
is invertible with inverse

{\cal M}^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}{\cal M}_{11}^{-1}+({\cal M}_{11}^{-1}{\cal M}_{12})\Phi^{-1}({\cal M}_{11}^{-1}{\cal M}_{12})^{*}&-({\cal M}_{11}^{-1}{\cal M}_{12})\Phi^{-1}\\[2.84526pt]
-\Phi^{-1}({\cal M}_{11}^{-1}{\cal M}_{12})^{*}&\Phi^{-1}\end{pmatrix}.

(3.21)

The remaining results follow from
3.1
directly.

4
Open-loop saddle points and Riccati equations

According to
3.2
, under the uniform convexity-concavity condition
(H3)
,
the open-loop saddle point for the given
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
can be uniquely determined by (
3.20
).
However, since
{\cal M}^{-1}{\cal K}
is an abstract operator and very complicated,
it is usually difficult to find the open-loop saddle point by computing (
3.20
) directly.
Thus in this section, we shall give a more explicit form of the open-loop saddle point
by introducing two associated Riccati equations. Furthermore,
it will be shown that the unique open-loop saddle point admits a closed-loop representation.

Recall from
[
24
]
that the Riccati equation associated with Problem (SG) reads

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\dot{P}+PA+A^{\top}P+C^{\top}PC+Q\\
&\hphantom{\dot{P}}-(PB+C^{\top}PD+S^{\top})(R+D^{\top}PD)^{-1}(B^{\top}P+D^{\top}PC+S)=0,\\
&P(T)=G,\end{aligned}\right.

(4.1)

where

\displaystyle B=(B_{1},B_{2}),\quad D=(D_{1},D_{2}),\quad S=\begin{pmatrix}S_{1}\\
S_{2}\end{pmatrix},\quad R=\begin{pmatrix}R_{11}&R_{12}\\
R_{21}&R_{22}\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle R+D^{\top}PD=\begin{pmatrix}R_{11}+D_{1}^{\top}PD_{1}&R_{12}+D_{1}^{\top}PD_{2}\\
R_{21}+D_{2}^{\top}PD_{1}&R_{22}+D_{2}^{\top}PD_{2}\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle B^{\top}P+D^{\top}PC+S=\begin{pmatrix}B_{1}^{\top}P+D_{1}^{\top}PC+S_{1}\\
B_{2}^{\top}P+D_{2}^{\top}PC+S_{2}\end{pmatrix}.

(4.2)

Definition 4.1
.

An absolutely continuous function
P:[0,T]\to\mathbb{S}^{n}
is called a
strongly regular solution
of Riccati equation (
4.1
) if

(i)

For
i=1,2
,
(-1)^{i+1}[R_{ii}+D_{i}^{\top}PD_{i}]\gg 0
, and

(ii)

P
satisfies (
4.1
) almost everywhere on
[0,T]
.

To establish the solvability of Riccati equation (
4.1
), we introduce the following two optimal control problems:
For
i=1,2
, consider the state equation

\left\{\begin{aligned} dX(s)&=\big\{A(s)X(s)+\bar{A}(s)\mathbb{E}[X(s)]+B_{i}(s)u_{i}(s)+\bar{B}_{i}(s)\mathbb{E}[u_{i}(s)]\big\}ds\\
&\hphantom{=~}+\big\{C(s)X(s)+\bar{C}(s)\mathbb{E}[X(s)]+D_{i}(s)u_{i}(s)+\bar{D}_{i}(s)\mathbb{E}[u_{i}(s)]\big\}dW(s),\\
X(0)&=x,\end{aligned}\right.

(4.3)

and the cost functional

\displaystyle J_{i}(x;u_{i})=(-1)^{i+1}\mathbb{E}\big\{\langle GX(T),X(T)\rangle+\langle\bar{G}\mathbb{E}[X(T)],\mathbb{E}[X(T)]\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{0}^{T}\Bigg[\left\langle\!\begin{pmatrix}Q&S_{i}^{\top}\\
S_{i}&R_{ii}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}X\\
u_{i}\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}X\\
u_{i}\end{pmatrix}\!\right\rangle+\left\langle\!\begin{pmatrix}\bar{Q}&\bar{S}_{i}^{\top}\\
\bar{S}_{i}&\bar{R}_{ii}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{E}[X]\\
\mathbb{E}[u_{i}]\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{E}[X]\\
\mathbb{E}[u_{i}]\end{pmatrix}\!\right\rangle\Bigg]ds\Bigg\}.

(4.4)

If the mean-field terms in the above vanish, then (
4.3
) and (
4
) reduce to

\left\{\begin{aligned} dX(s)&=\big\{A(s)X(s)+B_{i}(s)u_{i}(s)\big\}ds+\big\{C(s)X(s)+D_{i}(s)u_{i}(s)\big\}dW(s),\\
X(0)&=x,\end{aligned}\right.

(4.5)

and

\displaystyle{\cal J}_{i}(x;u_{i})

\displaystyle=(-1)^{i+1}\mathbb{E}\Bigg\{\langle GX(T),X(T)\rangle+\int_{0}^{T}\left\langle\!\begin{pmatrix}Q&S_{i}^{\top}\\
S_{i}&R_{ii}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}X\\
u_{i}\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}X\\
u_{i}\end{pmatrix}\!\right\rangle ds\Bigg\}.

The Riccati equations associated with the above LQ control problems read

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\dot{P}_{i}+P_{i}A+A^{\top}P_{i}+C^{\top}P_{i}C+Q-(P_{i}B_{i}+C^{\top}P_{i}D_{i}+S_{i}^{\top})\\
&\hphantom{\dot{P}}\quad\times(R_{ii}+D_{i}^{\top}P_{i}D_{i})^{-1}(B_{i}^{\top}P_{i}+D_{i}^{\top}P_{i}C+S_{i})=0,\quad i=1,2,\\
&P_{i}(T)=G,\quad i=1,2.\end{aligned}\right.

(4.6)

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
(H1)
–
(H3)
hold.
Then Riccati equation (
4.1
) admits a unique strongly regular solution
P\in C([0,T];\mathbb{S}^{n})
.
Moreover, the strongly regular solution
P
satisfies

P_{1}(t)\leqslant P(t)\leqslant P_{2}(t),\quad\forall t\in[0,T],

(4.7)

and

(-1)^{i+1}[R_{ii}+\bar{R}_{ii}+(D_{i}+\bar{D}_{i})^{\top}P(D_{i}+\bar{D}_{i})]\gg 0;\quad i=1,2,

(4.8)

where
P_{i}\in C([0,T];\mathbb{S}^{n})
(
i=1,2
) is the unique solution of (
4.6
).

Proof.

Note that the assumption
(H3)
implies that for any
(u_{1},u_{2})\in{\cal U}_{1}\times{\cal U}_{2}
,

J_{1}(0;u_{1})=J(0;u_{1},0)\gg 0\quad\hbox{and}\quad J_{2}(0;u_{2})=-J(0;0,u_{2})\gg 0.

Thus for
i=1,2
, we obtain from
[
23
, Theorems 4.2 and 4.4]
that Riccati equation (
4.6
)
admits a unique solution
P_{i}\in C([0,T];\mathbb{S}^{n})
satisfying

(-1)^{i+1}[R_{ii}+D_{i}^{\top}P_{i}D_{i}]\gg 0,

(4.9)

and

(-1)^{i+1}[R_{ii}+\bar{R}_{ii}+(D_{i}+\bar{D}_{i})^{\top}P_{i}(D_{i}+\bar{D}_{i})]\gg 0.

(4.10)

According to
[
23
, Theorem 5.2]
, (
4.9
) implies that
the mapping
u_{i}\mapsto{\cal J}_{i}(0;u_{i})
is uniformly convex.
Thus,

\displaystyle{\cal J}(0;u_{1},0)={\cal J}_{1}(0;u_{1})\gg 0,\quad~~\forall u_{1}\in{\cal U}_{1};

\displaystyle{\cal J}(0;0,u_{2})=-{\cal J}_{2}(0;u_{2})\ll 0,\quad\forall u_{2}\in{\cal U}_{2},

(4.11)

where
{\cal J}
denotes the functional of Problem (SG);
that is
{\cal J}(x;u_{1},u_{2})\triangleq J(x;u_{1},u_{2})

with
\bar{A}
,
\bar{B}_{i}
,
\bar{C}
,
\bar{D}_{i}
,
\bar{G}
,
\bar{Q}
,
\bar{S}_{i}
and
\bar{R}_{ij}
(
i,j=1,2
)
all vanishing.
Then from
[
24
, Theorem 4.3]
, we obtain that Riccati equation (
4.1
) has a strongly regular solution

P\in C([0,T];\mathbb{S}^{n})
.

To prove the uniqueness, we suppose that
\bar{P},\tilde{P}\in C([0,T];\mathbb{S}^{n})

are two strongly regular solutions of (
4.1
).
Then
\bar{P}
and
\tilde{P}
satisfy:

(-1)^{i+1}[R_{ii}+D_{i}^{\top}\tilde{P}D_{i}]\gg 0\quad\hbox{and}\quad(-1)^{i+1}[R_{ii}+D_{i}^{\top}\bar{P}D_{i}]\gg 0,\quad i=1,2.

Similar to (
3.21
),
R+D^{\top}\bar{P}D
and
R+D^{\top}\tilde{P}D
are invertible
with their inverses being bounded. Denote
\Delta P=\bar{P}-\tilde{P}
.
Then
\Delta P
satisfies the following linear ordinary differential equation:

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\Delta\dot{P}+\Delta PA+A^{\top}\Delta P+C^{\top}\Delta PC-(\Delta PB+C^{\top}\Delta PD)(R+D^{\top}\bar{P}D)^{-1}\\
&\hphantom{\dot{P}}\times(B^{\top}\bar{P}+D^{\top}\bar{P}C+S)-(\tilde{P}B^{\top}+C^{\top}\tilde{P}D+S^{\top})(R+D^{\top}\bar{P}D)^{-1}D^{\top}\Delta PD\\
&\hphantom{\dot{P}}\times(R+D^{\top}\tilde{P}D)^{-1}(B^{\top}\bar{P}+D^{\top}\bar{P}C+S)-(\tilde{P}B^{\top}+C^{\top}\tilde{P}D+S^{\top})\\
&\hphantom{\dot{P}}\times(R+D^{\top}\tilde{P}D)^{-1}(B^{\top}\Delta P+D^{\top}\Delta PC)=0,\\
&\Delta P(T)=0.\end{aligned}\right.

(4.12)

Note that
\bar{P}
,
\tilde{P}
,
(R+D^{\top}\bar{P}D)^{-1}
and
(R+D^{\top}\tilde{P}D)^{-1}
are bounded.
Then by a standard argument using the Grönwall’s inequality, we get
\Delta P\equiv 0
,
which yields the uniqueness of the strongly regular solution to (
4.1
).

Next let us prove the unique strongly regular solution
P\in C([0,T];\mathbb{S}^{n})
of (
4.1
) satisfies (
4.7
).
To this end, for any fixed
u_{2}\in{\cal U}_{2}
, we introduce the following LQ control problem: Consider the
state equation

\left\{\begin{aligned} dX(s)&=\big\{A(s)X(s)+B_{1}(s)u_{1}(s)+B_{2}(s)u_{2}(s)\big\}ds\\
&~\hphantom{=}+\big\{C(s)X(s)+D_{1}(s)u_{1}(s)+D_{2}(s)u_{2}(s)\big\}dW(s),\\
X(0)&=x,\end{aligned}\right.

(4.13)

and the cost functional

\displaystyle{\cal J}_{u_{2}}(x;u_{1})

\displaystyle\triangleq{\cal J}(x;u_{1},u_{2})=\mathbb{E}\bigg\{\langle GX(T),X(T)\rangle+\int_{0}^{T}\Big[\langle QX,X\rangle+2\langle S_{1}X,u_{1}\rangle

u^{*}
defined by (
4.39
) is an open-loop saddle point.
The uniqueness of open-loop saddle points follows from
3.2
.

By integration by parts and (

), we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\langle GX^{*}(T),X^{*}(T)\rangle+\langle\bar{G}\mathbb{E}[X^{*}(T)],\mathbb{E}[X^{*}(T)]\rangle=\mathbb{E}\langle Y^{*}(T),X^{*}(T)\rangle

\displaystyle\quad=\mathbb{E}\langle Y^{*}(0),X^{*}(0)\rangle+\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{T}\Big[\big\langle B^{\top}Y^{*}+\bar{B}^{\top}\mathbb{E}[Y^{*}]+D^{\top}Z^{*}+\bar{D}^{\top}\mathbb{E}[Z^{*}]-SX^{*}

\displaystyle\qquad-\bar{S}\mathbb{E}[X^{*}],u^{*}\big\rangle-\langle QX^{*},X^{*}\rangle-\langle\bar{Q}\mathbb{E}[X^{*}],X^{*}\rangle\Big]ds

\displaystyle\quad=\mathbb{E}\langle Y^{*}(0),X^{*}(0)\rangle-\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{T}\Big[\langle Ru^{*},u^{*}\rangle+\langle\bar{R}\mathbb{E}[u^{*}],\mathbb{E}[u^{*}]\rangle+2\langle SX^{*},u^{*}\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad+2\langle\bar{S}\mathbb{E}[X^{*}],\mathbb{E}[u^{*}]\rangle+\langle QX^{*},X^{*}\rangle+\langle\bar{Q}\mathbb{E}[X^{*}],\mathbb{E}[X^{*}]\rangle\Big]ds.

Noting that

\displaystyle V(x)

\displaystyle=J(x;u^{*})=\mathbb{E}\langle GX^{*}(T),X^{*}(T)\rangle+\langle\bar{G}\mathbb{E}[X^{*}(T)],\mathbb{E}[X^{*}(T)]\rangle

\displaystyle\quad+\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{T}\Big[\langle Ru^{*},u^{*}\rangle+\langle\bar{R}\mathbb{E}[u^{*}],\mathbb{E}[u^{*}]\rangle+2\langle SX^{*},u^{*}\rangle+2\langle\bar{S}\mathbb{E}[X^{*}],\mathbb{E}[u^{*}]\rangle

\displaystyle\quad+\langle QX^{*},X^{*}\rangle+\langle\bar{Q}\mathbb{E}[X^{*}],\mathbb{E}[X^{*}]\rangle\Big]ds

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\langle Y^{*}(0),X^{*}(0)\rangle=\langle\Pi(0)x,x\rangle,\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\hfill\qed

5
Open-loop solvability: a perturbation approach

In
Theorem 4.6
, it is shown that under the uniform convexity-concavity condition
(H3)
,
Problem (MF-SG) is uniquely open-loop solvable and the unique open-loop saddle point
admits the closed-loop representation (
4.39
).
In this section, we shall establish a characterization for the open-loop solvability of Problem (MF-SG)
without assumption
(H3)
.
Recall from
3.1
that the following convexity-concavity condition
is necessary for the open-loop solvability of Problem (MF-SG):

\displaystyle\langle{\cal M}_{11}u_{1},u_{1}\rangle

\displaystyle=J(0;u_{1},0)\geqslant 0,\quad\forall u_{1}\in{\cal U}_{1};

(5.1)

\displaystyle\langle{\cal M}_{22}u_{2},u_{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=J(0;0,u_{2})\leqslant 0,\quad\forall u_{2}\in{\cal U}_{2}.

Thus in this section, we always assume that (
5.1
) holds.

5.1
The perturbation approach

For each
\varepsilon>0
, we introduce the following perturbed functional:

\displaystyle J_{\varepsilon}(x;u_{1},u_{2})\triangleq J(x;u_{1},u_{2})+\varepsilon\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{T}|u_{1}(s)|^{2}ds-\varepsilon\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{T}|u_{2}(s)|^{2}ds

\displaystyle\quad=\mathbb{E}\big\{\langle GX(T),X(T)\rangle+\langle\bar{G}\mathbb{E}[X(T)],\mathbb{E}[X(T)]\rangle

\displaystyle\hphantom{=\mathbb{E}\big\lx@text@lbrace ~~}+\int_{0}^{T}\left\langle\!\begin{pmatrix}Q&S_{1}^{\top}&S_{2}^{\top}\\
S_{1}&R_{11}+\varepsilon I_{m_{1}}&R_{12}\\
S_{2}&R_{21}&R_{22}-\varepsilon I_{m_{2}}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}X\\

u_{2}\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}X\\

u_{2}\end{pmatrix}\!\right\rangle ds

\displaystyle\hphantom{=\mathbb{E}\big\lx@text@lbrace ~~}+\int_{0}^{T}\left\langle\!\begin{pmatrix}\bar{Q}&\bar{S}_{1}^{\top}&\bar{S}_{2}^{\top}\\
\bar{S}_{1}&\bar{R}_{11}&\bar{R}_{12}\\
\bar{S}_{2}&\bar{R}_{21}&\bar{R}_{22}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{E}[X]\\
\mathbb{E}[u_{1}]\\
\mathbb{E}[u_{2}]\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{E}[X]\\
\mathbb{E}[u_{1}]\\
\mathbb{E}[u_{2}]\end{pmatrix}\!\right\rangle ds\Bigg\}.

(5.2)

We denote the two-person zero-sum LQ stochastic differential game associated with (

ε

.
Notice that

\displaystyle J_{\varepsilon}(0;u_{1},0)

\displaystyle=J(0;u_{1},0)+\varepsilon\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{T}|u_{1}(s)|^{2}ds\geqslant\varepsilon\|u_{1}\|^{2},\quad~~\forall u_{1}\in{\cal U}_{1};

(5.3)

\displaystyle J_{\varepsilon}(0;0,u_{2})

\displaystyle=J(0;0,u_{2})-\varepsilon\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{T}|u_{2}(s)|^{2}ds\leqslant-\varepsilon\|u_{2}\|^{2},\quad\forall u_{2}\in{\cal U}_{2}.

Then with

R_{\varepsilon}\triangleq\begin{pmatrix}R_{11}+\varepsilon I_{m_{1}}&R_{12}\\
R_{21}&R_{22}-\varepsilon I_{m_{2}}\end{pmatrix},

(5.4)

it follows from

and

that the Riccati equations

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\dot{P}_{\varepsilon}+P_{\varepsilon}A+A^{\top}P_{\varepsilon}+C^{\top}P_{\varepsilon}C+Q\\
&\hphantom{\dot{P}}-(P_{\varepsilon}B+C^{\top}P_{\varepsilon}D+S^{\top})(R_{\varepsilon}+D^{\top}P_{\varepsilon}D)^{-1}(B^{\top}P_{\varepsilon}+D^{\top}P_{\varepsilon}C+S)=0,\\

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\dot{\Pi}_{\varepsilon}+\Pi_{\varepsilon}(A+\bar{A})+(A+\bar{A})^{\top}\Pi_{\varepsilon}+Q+\bar{Q}+(C+\bar{C})^{\top}P_{\varepsilon}(C+\bar{C})\\

&\hphantom{\dot{\Pi}}\times\big[R_{\varepsilon}+\bar{R}+(D+\bar{D})^{\top}P_{\varepsilon}(D+\bar{D})\big]^{-1}\\

(5.6)

admit unique solutions

and

satisfying

\displaystyle(-1)^{i+1}[R_{ii}+(-1)^{i+1}\varepsilon I_{m_{i}}+D_{i}^{\top}P_{\varepsilon}D_{i}]\gg 0,\quad i=1,2,

(5.7)

\displaystyle(-1)^{i+1}[R_{ii}+\bar{R}_{ii}+(-1)^{i+1}\varepsilon I_{m_{i}}+(D_{i}+\bar{D}_{i})^{\top}P_{\varepsilon}(D_{i}+\bar{D}_{i})]\gg 0,\quad i=1,2.

(5.8)

Denote

\displaystyle\Sigma_{\varepsilon}=R_{\varepsilon}+D^{\top}P_{\varepsilon}D,\quad\bar{\Sigma}_{\varepsilon}=R_{\varepsilon}+\bar{R}+(D+\bar{D})^{\top}P_{\varepsilon}(D+\bar{D}),

(5.9)

\displaystyle\Theta_{\varepsilon}=-\Sigma_{\varepsilon}^{-1}\big(B^{\top}P_{\varepsilon}+D^{\top}P_{\varepsilon}C+S\big),

(5.10)

\displaystyle\bar{\Theta}_{\varepsilon}=-\bar{\Sigma}^{-1}_{\varepsilon}[(B+\bar{B})^{\top}\Pi_{\varepsilon}+(D+\bar{D})^{\top}P_{\varepsilon}(C+\bar{C})+S+\bar{S}].

By

,
the unique open-loop saddle point

ε

with

solving the closed-loop system:

\left\{\begin{aligned} dX_{\varepsilon}(s)&=\big\{(A+B\Theta_{\varepsilon})(X_{\varepsilon}-\mathbb{E}[X_{\varepsilon}])+[(A+\bar{A})+(B+\bar{B})\bar{\Theta}_{\varepsilon}]\mathbb{E}[X_{\varepsilon}]\big\}ds\\
&\hphantom{=\ }+\big\{(C+D\Theta_{\varepsilon})(X_{\varepsilon}-\mathbb{E}[X_{\varepsilon}])+[(C+\bar{C})+(D+\bar{D})\bar{\Theta}_{\varepsilon}]\mathbb{E}[X_{\varepsilon}]\big\}dW(s),\\

For any
\varepsilon>0
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the value of Problem (MF-SG)
ε
at
x
is given by

V_{\varepsilon}(x)=J_{\varepsilon}(x;u_{1}^{\varepsilon},u_{2}^{\varepsilon}),

where
u_{\varepsilon}=(u_{1}^{\varepsilon},u_{2}^{\varepsilon})
is defined by (

).
If the value
V(x)
of Problem (MF-SG) exists for some

,
then by the same arguments as in the proof of
[
24
, Proposition 3.5]
we have

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}V_{\varepsilon}(x)=V(x).

This means that the family
\{u_{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon>0}
defined by (

) is an
approximate sequence
of Problem (MF-SG).
But as pointed out in
[
24
]
, the existence of a value function does not imply that Problem (MF-SG) has an open-loop saddle point.
Thus in terms of the family
\{u_{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon>0}
, we present the following characterization for the open-loop solvability of Problem (MF-SG),
which is the main result of this section.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
(H1)
–
(H2)
and (
5.1
) hold.
Let
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be any given initial state and
\{u_{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon>0}
be the sequence defined by (
5.12
).
Then the following statements are equivalent:

(a)

Problem

(MF-SG)
has an open-loop saddle point at
x
;

(b)

the family
\{u_{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon>0}
is bounded in the Hilbert space
L^{2}_{\mathbb{F}}(0,T;\mathbb{R}^{m_{1}+m_{2}})\equiv{\cal U}_{1}\times{\cal U}_{2}
, i.e.,

\sup_{\varepsilon>0}\,\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{T}|u_{\varepsilon}(s)|^{2}ds<\infty;

(c)

the family
\{u_{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon>0}
is strongly convergent in
L^{2}_{\mathbb{F}}(0,T;\mathbb{R}^{m_{1}+m_{2}})
as
\varepsilon\to 0
.

Whenever
(a)
,
(b)
, or
(c)
is satisfied, the strong limit of
\{u_{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon>0}

is an open-loop saddle point of
Problem (MF-SG)
for the initial state
x
.

Remark 5.2
.

Since there is no coupled system in the above perturbation approach and
all the equations involved can be solved by iteration method,
it will be much more convenient for computational purposes.

The perturbation approach in stochastic LQ control problems was initially introduced by Sun–Li–Yong
[
25
]
,
and further sharpened by Wang–Sun–Yong
[
31
]
for finding the so-called weak closed-loop optimal strategies.
However, compared with the control problems
[
25
,
31
]
,
in the game problem there are some new difficulties, especially in proving the boundedness of
\{u_{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon>0}
.
Before proving
Theorem 5.1
, we present the following tailormade example,
from which we can perceive some essential differences between the perturbation approaches of LQ game and control problems.

Example 5.3
.

For any
x\in\mathbb{R}
, consider the one-dimensional state equation

\left\{\begin{aligned} \dot{X}(s)&=su_{1}(s)+u_{2}(s),\quad s\in[0,1],\\
X(0)&=x,\end{aligned}\right.

(5.13)

and the quadratic functional

J(x;u_{1},u_{2})=-|X(1)|^{2}+\int_{0}^{1}s^{2}|u_{1}(s)|^{2}ds.

(5.14)

In the example, we let
{\cal U}_{1}={\cal U}_{2}
be the space of
\mathbb{R}
-valued square-integrable
functions on
[0,1]
. Note that

\displaystyle J(1;0,u_{2})

\displaystyle=-|X(1)|^{2}=-\Big|1+\int_{0}^{1}u_{2}(s)ds\Big|^{2}\leqslant 0=J(1;0,-1),\qquad\forall u_{2}\in{\cal U}_{2};

\displaystyle J(1;u_{1},-1)

\displaystyle=-\Big|\int_{0}^{1}su_{1}(s)ds\Big|^{2}+\int_{0}^{1}|su_{1}(s)|^{2}ds\geqslant 0=J(1;0,-1),\quad\forall u_{1}\in{\cal U}_{1}.

Thus the control pair
(0,-1)
is an open-loop saddle point for
x=1

and the convexity-concavity condition (
5.1
) holds.

For any
\varepsilon>0
and
(u_{1},u_{2})\in{\cal U}_{1}\times{\cal U}_{2}
, denote

J_{\varepsilon}(x;u_{1},u_{2})=J(x;u_{1},u_{2})-\varepsilon\int_{0}^{1}|u_{2}(s)|^{2}ds.

(5.15)

Then

\displaystyle J_{\varepsilon}(0;0,u_{2})=J(0;0,u_{2})-\varepsilon\int_{0}^{1}|u_{2}(s)|^{2}ds\leqslant-\varepsilon\int_{0}^{1}|u_{2}(s)|^{2}ds,\quad\forall u_{2}\in{\cal U}_{2},

(5.16)

\displaystyle J_{\varepsilon}(0;u_{1},0)=J(0;u_{1},0)\geqslant 0,\quad\forall u_{1}\in{\cal U}_{1}.

(5.17)

Roughly speaking, the above implies that the convexity of the mapping
u_{1}\mapsto J_{\varepsilon}(x;u_{1},u_{2})
equals that of
u_{1}\mapsto J(x;u_{1},u_{2})

and the concavity of
u_{2}\mapsto J_{\varepsilon}(x;u_{1},u_{2})
is stronger than that of
u_{2}\mapsto J(x;u_{1},u_{2})
.
However, we will show that the LQ game problem associated with (
5.13
)–(
5.15
)
has no open-loop saddle point for
x=1
.

We shall prove the above claim by contradiction.
Suppose that the LQ game problem associated with (
5.13
)–(
5.15
) has an
open-loop saddle point
(u_{1}^{*},u_{2}^{*})
. Then we must have

\int_{0}^{1}u_{2}^{*}(s)ds=-1.

(5.18)

Otherwise,
u^{*}_{1}
is optimal for the LQ control problem associated with the state equation

\dot{X}(s)=su_{1}(s),\quad s\in[0,T];\quad X(0)=h\triangleq 1+\int_{0}^{1}u_{2}^{*}(s)ds\neq 0,

(5.19)

and the cost functional
J(u_{1})=J_{\varepsilon}(1;u_{1},u_{2}^{*})
. The corresponding optimal state is denoted by
X^{*}
.
Then by
[
25
, Corollary 3.3]
,
u_{1}^{*}
satisfies the following stationary condition:

sY^{*}(s)+s^{2}u_{1}^{*}(s)=0,\quad s\in[0,1],

(5.20)

where
Y^{*}
is the solution to the following adjoint equation:

\dot{Y}^{*}(s)=0,\quad s\in[0,T];\quad Y^{*}(1)=-X^{*}(1).

(5.21)

By solving (
5.21
), the stationary condition (
5.20
) can be rewritten as

-sX^{*}(1)+s^{2}u_{1}^{*}(s)=0,\quad s\in[0,1].

(5.22)

Recalling that
u_{1}^{*}\in{\cal U}_{1}
is a square-integrable function, we get

X^{*}(1)=0,

(5.23)

which implies that

u^{*}_{1}(s)=0,\quad s\in[0,1].

(5.24)

Thus,

0=X^{*}(1)=h+\int_{0}^{1}su_{1}^{*}(s)ds=h.

(5.25)

This contradicts (
5.19
) and thus (
5.18
) holds.
Further, by (
5.18
), note that

\displaystyle J_{\varepsilon}(1;u_{1},u^{*}_{2})

\displaystyle=-\Big|\int_{0}^{1}su_{1}(s)ds\Big|^{2}+\int_{0}^{1}|su_{1}(s)|^{2}ds-\varepsilon\int_{0}^{1}|u^{*}_{2}(s)|^{2}ds,

\displaystyle J_{\varepsilon}(1;u^{*}_{1},u^{*}_{2})

\displaystyle=\inf_{u_{1}\in{\cal U}_{1}[0,1]}J_{\varepsilon}(1;u_{1},u^{*}_{2}),

(5.26)

then we have

u_{1}^{*}(s)=0,\quad s\in[0,1].

(5.27)

It follows that
u^{*}_{2}
is optimal for the control problem associated with the state equation

\dot{X}(s)=u_{2}(s),\quad s\in[0,1];\quad X(0)=1,

(5.28)

and the cost functional
J(u_{2})=-J_{\varepsilon}(1;0,u_{2})
. Then,
u_{2}^{*}
satisfies the stationary condition:

Y^{*}(s)+\varepsilon u_{2}^{*}(s)=0,\quad s\in[0,1],

(5.29)

with
Y^{*}
solving the adjoint equation:

\dot{Y}^{*}(s)=0,\quad s\in[0,1];\quad Y^{*}(1)=X^{*}(1)=0.

(5.30)

It follows that

u_{2}^{*}(s)=-{Y^{*}(s)\over\varepsilon}=0,\quad s\in[0,1],

(5.31)

which contradicts (
5.18
). Therefore, the claim is proved; that is the LQ game problem associated
with (
5.13
)–(
5.15
) has no open-loop saddle points for
x=1
.

In the LQ control problems
[
25
,
31
]
,
the perturbed cost functional
J_{\varepsilon}(x;u)
is defined by adding
\varepsilon\|u\|^{2}
to the
original one
J(x;u)
, where
u
is the control process.
By the monotonicity of the mapping
\varepsilon\mapsto V_{\varepsilon}(x)
,

[
25
]
showed that
\|u_{\varepsilon}\|^{2}
is bounded by
\|u^{*}\|^{2}
,
provided the original problem has an optimal control
u^{*}
.

From
5.3
, we see that the new game associated with

J(x;u_{1},u_{2})-\varepsilon\|u_{2}\|^{2}
possibly has no saddle point even if the original is open-loop solvable.
Thus in the perturbation approach of games,
adding both
\varepsilon\|u_{1}\|^{2}
and
-\varepsilon\|u_{2}\|^{2}
to the original functional turns out to be necessary,
due to which the value function
V_{\varepsilon}(x)
is not monotone in
\varepsilon
.
Noticing this key point,
a seemingly feasible approach is to introduce two parameters
\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2}>0

and consider the game problem with the functional:

J_{\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2}}(x;u_{1},u_{2})=J(x;u_{1}.u_{2})+\varepsilon_{1}\|u_{1}\|^{2}-\varepsilon_{2}\|u_{2}\|^{2}.

Since
J_{\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2}}
is monotone in each
\varepsilon_{i}
,
it seems that the convergence of
\{u_{\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2}}\}_{\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2}>0}

can be obtained by letting
\varepsilon_{1}\to 0
and
\varepsilon_{2}\to 0
separately.
But in fact, after letting
\varepsilon_{1}\to 0
, one cannot take the limit by letting
\varepsilon_{2}\to 0
,
because the game with the functional
J_{0,\varepsilon_{2}}
is possibly unsolvable (see
5.3
).
In conclusion, the perturbation approaches of LQ games (i.e.,
Theorem 5.1
) and controls
(i.e.,
[
25
,
31
]
) are essentially different.

5.2
Proof of
Theorem 5.1

With the preparations in Sections
2
,
3
and Subsection
5.1
,
now we are ready to prove
Theorem 5.1
by a Hilbert space method,
in which Proposition
2.4
and the Mazur’s theorem (see Yosida
[

, p.120, Theorem 2]
) play important roles.

Proof of
Theorem 5.1
.

(i) We begin by proving the implication (a)
\Rightarrow
(b).
Let
v^{*}
be an open-loop saddle point of Problem (MF-SG) for the initial state
x
.
Then by
3.1
,
v^{*}
must satisfy

{\cal M}v^{*}+{\cal K}x=0,

(5.32)

where the operators
{\cal M}
and
{\cal K}
are defined by (
3.9
).
On the other hand,
3.2
shows that for any
\varepsilon>0
,
the unique open-loop saddle point
u_{\varepsilon}
of Problem (MF-SG)
ε
for
x
can be also given by

u_{\varepsilon}=-{\cal M}^{-1}_{\varepsilon}{\cal K}x,

(5.33)

where

\displaystyle{\cal M}_{\varepsilon}\triangleq{\cal M}+\begin{pmatrix}\varepsilon I_{m_{1}}&0\\
0&-\varepsilon I_{m_{2}}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}{\cal M}_{11}+\varepsilon I_{m_{1}}&{\cal M}_{12}\\
{\cal M}_{21}&{\cal M}_{22}-\varepsilon I_{m_{2}}\end{pmatrix}.

(5.34)

Combining (
5.32
) with (
5.33
) yields that

u_{\varepsilon}=-{\cal M}^{-1}_{\varepsilon}{\cal K}x={\cal M}^{-1}_{\varepsilon}{\cal M}v^{*}.

(5.35)

Then by
2.4
, noting (
5.1
), we have

\|u_{\varepsilon}\|^{2}=\|{\cal M}^{-1}_{\varepsilon}{\cal M}v^{*}\|^{2}\leqslant\|{\cal M}^{-1}_{\varepsilon}{\cal M}\|^{2}\|v^{*}\|^{2}\leqslant\|v^{*}\|^{2}.

(5.36)

Thus,
\{u_{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon>0}
is bounded in the Hilbert space
L^{2}_{\mathbb{F}}(0,T;\mathbb{R}^{m_{1}+m_{2}})
.

(ii) We show that (b)
\Rightarrow
(a). For convenience, we let

\sup_{\varepsilon>0}\|u_{\varepsilon}\|^{2}\leqslant\|\bar{u}\|^{2},\quad\hbox{for some}~\bar{u}\in{\cal U}_{1}\times{\cal U}_{2}.

(5.37)

Since
\{u_{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon>0}
is bounded in the Hilbert space
L^{2}_{\mathbb{F}}(0,T;\mathbb{R}^{m_{1}+m_{2}})
,
it admits a weakly convergent subsequence.
We denote this subsequence by
\{u_{\varepsilon_{j}}\}_{j\geqslant 1}=\{(u_{1}^{\varepsilon_{j}},u_{2}^{\varepsilon_{j}})\}_{j\geqslant 1}
and its weak limit by
u^{*}=(u_{1}^{*},u_{2}^{*})
.
Then by Mazur’s theorem there exist
\lambda_{kj}\in[0,1]
,
j=1,2,...,N_{k}
such that

\sum_{j=1}^{N_{k}}\lambda_{kj}=1,\quad k=1,2,...,

(5.38)

and

\Big\|\sum_{j=1}^{N_{k}}\lambda_{kj}u_{\varepsilon_{k+j}}-u^{*}\Big\|\to 0,\quad\hbox{as}\quad k\to\infty.

(5.39)

By the convexity of the mapping
u_{1}\mapsto J(x;u_{1},u_{2})
,
we have

\displaystyle J\big(x;\sum_{j=1}^{N_{k}}\lambda_{kj}u_{1}^{\varepsilon_{k+j}},\sum_{j=1}^{N_{k}}\lambda_{kj}u_{2}^{\varepsilon_{k+j}}\big)\leqslant\sum_{j=1}^{N_{k}}\lambda_{kj}J\big(x;u_{1}^{\varepsilon_{k+j}},\sum_{j=1}^{N_{k}}\lambda_{kj}u_{2}^{\varepsilon_{k+j}}\big)

\displaystyle\quad=\sum_{j=1}^{N_{k}}\lambda_{kj}J_{\varepsilon_{k+j}}\big(x;u_{1}^{\varepsilon_{k+j}},\sum_{j=1}^{N_{k}}\lambda_{kj}u_{2}^{\varepsilon_{k+j}}\big)-\sum_{j=1}^{N_{k}}\lambda_{kj}\varepsilon_{k+j}\|u_{1}^{\varepsilon_{k+j}}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{j=1}^{N_{k}}\lambda_{kj}\varepsilon_{k+j}\Big\|\sum_{j=1}^{N_{k}}\lambda_{kj}u_{2}^{\varepsilon_{k+j}}\Big\|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad\leqslant\sum_{j=1}^{N_{k}}\lambda_{kj}J_{\varepsilon_{k+j}}\big(x;u_{1}^{\varepsilon_{k+j}},\sum_{j=1}^{N_{k}}\lambda_{kj}u_{2}^{\varepsilon_{k+j}}\big)+\sum_{j=1}^{N_{k}}\lambda_{kj}\varepsilon_{k+j}\Big\|\sum_{j=1}^{N_{k}}\lambda_{kj}u_{2}^{\varepsilon_{k+j}}\Big\|^{2}.

By (
5.37
), we get

\Big\|\sum_{j=1}^{N_{k}}\lambda_{kj}u_{2}^{\varepsilon_{k+j}}\Big\|^{2}\leqslant\sum_{j=1}^{N_{k}}\lambda_{kj}\|u_{2}^{\varepsilon_{k+j}}\|^{2}\leqslant\|\bar{u}\|^{2},

and thus

\displaystyle J\big(x;\sum_{j=1}^{N_{k}}\lambda_{kj}u_{1}^{\varepsilon_{k+j}},\sum_{j=1}^{N_{k}}\lambda_{kj}u_{2}^{\varepsilon_{k+j}}\big)

\displaystyle\quad\leqslant\sum_{j=1}^{N_{k}}\lambda_{kj}J_{\varepsilon_{k+j}}\big(x;u_{1}^{\varepsilon_{k+j}},\sum_{j=1}^{N_{k}}\lambda_{kj}u_{2}^{\varepsilon_{k+j}}\big)+\sum_{j=1}^{N_{k}}\lambda_{kj}\varepsilon_{k+j}\|\bar{u}\|^{2}.

(5.40)

Moreover, note that
(u_{1}^{\varepsilon_{k+j}},u_{2}^{\varepsilon_{k+j}})
is an open-loop saddle of Problem (MF-SG)
{}_{\varepsilon_{k+j}}
.
Thus for any
u_{1}\in{\cal U}_{1}
,

J_{\varepsilon_{k+j}}\big(x;u_{1}^{\varepsilon_{k+j}},\sum_{j=1}^{N_{k}}\lambda_{kj}u_{2}^{\varepsilon_{k+j}}\big)\leqslant J_{\varepsilon_{k+j}}\big(x;u_{1}^{\varepsilon_{k+j}},u_{2}^{\varepsilon_{k+j}}\big)\leqslant J_{\varepsilon_{k+j}}\big(x;u_{1},u_{2}^{\varepsilon_{k+j}}\big).

(5.41)

Substituting the above into (
5.40
) and then by the concavity of the mapping
u_{2}\mapsto J(x;u_{1},u_{2})
,
we have

\displaystyle J\big(x;\sum_{j=1}^{N_{k}}\lambda_{kj}u_{1}^{\varepsilon_{k+j}},\sum_{j=1}^{N_{k}}\lambda_{kj}u_{2}^{\varepsilon_{k+j}}\big)\leqslant\sum_{j=1}^{N_{k}}\lambda_{kj}J_{\varepsilon_{k+j}}\big(x;u_{1},u_{2}^{\varepsilon_{k+j}}\big)+\sum_{j=1}^{N_{k}}\lambda_{kj}\varepsilon_{k+j}\|\bar{u}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad\leqslant\sum_{j=1}^{N_{k}}\lambda_{kj}J\big(x;u_{1},u_{2}^{\varepsilon_{k+j}}\big)+\sum_{j=1}^{N_{k}}\lambda_{kj}\varepsilon_{k+j}\|u_{1}\|^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{N_{k}}\lambda_{kj}\varepsilon_{k+j}\|\bar{u}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad\leqslant J\big(x;u_{1},\sum_{j=1}^{N_{k}}\lambda_{kj}u_{2}^{\varepsilon_{k+j}}\big)+\sum_{j=1}^{N_{k}}\lambda_{kj}\varepsilon_{k+j}\|u_{1}\|^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{N_{k}}\lambda_{kj}\varepsilon_{k+j}\|\bar{u}\|^{2}.

(5.42)

Thus by (
5.38
)–(
5.39
) and the (strong) continuity of the mapping
(u_{1},u_{2})\mapsto J(x;u_{1},u_{2})
,
letting
k\to\infty
in (
5.42
) yields that

J(x;u_{1}^{*},u_{2}^{*})\leqslant J(x;u_{1},u_{2}^{*}),\quad\forall u_{1}\in{\cal U}_{1}.

(5.43)

By the same argument as the above, we also have

J(x;u_{1}^{*},u_{2}^{*})\geqslant J(x;u^{*}_{1},u_{2}),\quad\forall u_{2}\in{\cal U}_{2}.

(5.44)

The result then concludes from (
5.43
)–(
5.44
).

(iii) The implication (c)
\Rightarrow
(b) is trivially true.
We next prove (b)
\Rightarrow
(c).
We first claim:
The family
\{u_{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon>0}
is weakly convergent as
\varepsilon\to 0

and the weak limit is an open-loop saddle point of
Problem (MF-SG)
for
x
.

If the above claim holds, then the family
\{u_{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon>0}
converges weakly to an open-loop saddle point
u^{*}
of
Problem (MF-SG) as
\varepsilon\to 0
. Thus by the weakly lower semicontinuity of the mapping
u\mapsto\|u\|^{2}
,
we have

\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{T}|u^{*}(s)|^{2}ds\leqslant\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{T}|u_{\varepsilon}(s)|^{2}ds.

(5.45)

On the other hand, by (
5.36
),
with
v^{*}
replaced by
u^{*}
, we obtain

\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{T}|u_{\varepsilon}(s)|^{2}ds\leqslant\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{T}|u^{*}(s)|^{2}ds,\quad\forall\varepsilon>0.

(5.46)

Combining (
5.45
) with (
5.46
) yields that

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{T}|u_{\varepsilon}(s)|^{2}ds=\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{T}|u^{*}(s)|^{2}ds.

Recall that
u^{*}
is the weak limit of
\{u_{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon>0}
. Then the above implies that

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{T}|u_{\varepsilon}(s)-u^{*}(s)|^{2}ds=0.

It shows that
\{u_{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon>0}
converges strongly to
u^{*}
as
\varepsilon\to 0
.
Thus to show (b)
\Rightarrow
(c), we only need to prove the claim.

Noting that
L_{\mathbb{F}}^{2}(0,T;\mathbb{R}^{m_{1}+m_{2}})
is a Hilbert space, to verify the claim,
it suffices to show that every weakly convergent subsequence of
\{u_{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon>0}

has the same weak limit, which is an open-loop saddle point of Problem (MF-SG) for
x
.
Let
u^{*}
and
\hat{u}
be the weak limits of two different weakly convergent subsequences

\{u_{\varepsilon^{i}_{k}}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}

(i=1,2)
of
\{u_{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon>0}
.
Then by the same argument as in the proof of (b)
\Rightarrow
(a),
we can show that both
u^{*}
and
\hat{u}
are open-loop saddle points.
By the convexity of the mapping
u_{1}\mapsto J(x;u_{1},u_{2})
,
we have

\displaystyle J\left(x;{u_{1}^{*}+\hat{u}_{1}\over 2},{u_{2}^{*}+\hat{u}_{2}\over 2}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant{1\over 2}J\left(x;u_{1}^{*},{u_{2}^{*}+\hat{u}_{2}\over 2}\right)+{1\over 2}J\left(x;\hat{u}_{1},{u_{2}^{*}+\hat{u}_{2}\over 2}\right).

(5.47)

Noting that both
u^{*}
and
\hat{u}
are open-loop saddle points,
and the mapping
u_{2}\mapsto J(x;u_{1},u_{2})
is concave, we have

\displaystyle{1\over 2}J\left(x;u_{1}^{*},{u_{2}^{*}+\hat{u}_{2}\over 2}\right)+{1\over 2}J\left(x;\hat{u}_{1},{u_{2}^{*}+\hat{u}_{2}\over 2}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\leqslant{1\over 2}J\left(x;u_{1}^{*},u_{2}^{*}\right)+{1\over 2}J\left(x;\hat{u}_{1},\hat{u}_{2}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\leqslant{1\over 2}J\left(x;u_{1},u_{2}^{*}\right)+{1\over 2}J\left(x;u_{1},\hat{u}_{2}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\leqslant J\left(x;u_{1},{u_{2}^{*}+\hat{u}_{2}\over 2}\right),\quad\forall u_{1}\in{\cal U}_{1}.

(5.48)

Thus,

J\left(x;{u_{1}^{*}+\hat{u}_{1}\over 2},{u_{2}^{*}+\hat{u}_{2}\over 2}\right)\leqslant J\left(x;u_{1},{u_{2}^{*}+\hat{u}_{2}\over 2}\right),\quad\forall u_{1}\in{\cal U}_{1}.

(5.49)

Similarly, we can prove

J\left(x;{u_{1}^{*}+\hat{u}_{1}\over 2},{u_{2}^{*}+\hat{u}_{2}\over 2}\right)\geqslant J\left(x;{u_{1}^{*}+\hat{u}_{1}\over 2},u_{2}\right),\quad\forall u_{2}\in{\cal U}_{2}.

(5.50)

Combining (
5.49
) with (
5.50
), we get that
{u^{*}+\hat{u}\over 2}

is also an open-loop saddle point of Problem (MF-SG) with respect to
x
.
Thus by (
5.36
),
with
v^{*}
replaced by
{u^{*}+\hat{u}\over 2}
, we obtain

\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{T}|u_{\varepsilon^{i}_{k}}(s)|^{2}ds\leqslant\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{T}\left|{u^{*}(s)+\hat{u}(s)\over 2}\right|^{2}ds,\quad i=1,2.

(5.51)

By the weakly lower semicontinuity of the mapping
u\mapsto\|u\|^{2}
again, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{T}|u^{*}(s)|^{2}ds\leqslant\liminf_{k\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{T}|u_{\varepsilon^{1}_{k}}(s)|^{2}ds,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{T}|\hat{u}(s)|^{2}ds\leqslant\liminf_{k\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{T}|u_{\varepsilon^{2}_{k}}(s)|^{2}ds.

Thus by taking inferior limits on the both sides of (
5.51
), we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{T}|u^{*}(s)|^{2}ds\leqslant\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{T}\left|{u^{*}(s)+\hat{u}(s)\over 2}\right|^{2}ds,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{T}|\hat{u}(s)|^{2}ds\leqslant\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{T}\left|{u^{*}(s)+\hat{u}(s)\over 2}\right|^{2}ds,

which implies that

\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{T}|u^{*}(s)-\hat{u}(s)|^{2}ds\leqslant 0.

The claim is established.
\hfill\qed

Remark 5.4
.

The boundedness of
\|{\cal M}_{\varepsilon}^{-1}{\cal M}\|^{2}
is sufficient for that of
\|u_{\varepsilon}\|^{2}
,
which implies that
\{u_{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon>0}
admits a weakly convergent subsequence.
With the help of Mazur’s theorem, the (strong) convergence of
\{u_{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon>0}

follows from the explicit upper bound (which is exactly
1
) of
\|{\cal M}_{\varepsilon}^{-1}{\cal M}\|^{2}
.

Remark 5.5
.

If Problem (MF-SG) reduces to a (mean-field) LQ optimal control problem,
then
{\cal M}={\cal M}_{1}
and (
5.35
) becomes

u_{\varepsilon}={\cal M}_{1\varepsilon}^{-1}{\cal M}_{1}v^{*}=({\cal M}_{1}+\varepsilon I)^{-1}{\cal M}_{1}v^{*}.

Note that
{\cal M}_{1}
is a positive operator, while
{\cal M}
is indefinite in general.
Then the Hilbert space method brings the following new viewpoint: The perturbation approaches of LQ controls and games
are the outcomes of the explicit norm estimates for perturbed positive operators and indefinite operators, respectively.

6
Example

In this section, we present a simple example to illustrate the procedure for finding the open-loop saddle points
by
Theorem 4.6
under the sufficient condition (
3.19
);
and identifying the open-loop solvability by
Theorem 5.1
under the necessary condition (
3.18
).

Example 6.1
.

Consider the one-dimensional state equation

\left\{\begin{aligned} dX(s)&=u_{1}(s)ds+u_{2}(s)dW(s),\quad s\in[0,1],\\
X(0)&=x,\end{aligned}\right.

(6.1)

and the quadratic functional

J(x;u_{1},u_{2})=\mathbb{E}\Big\{-|X(1)|^{2}+\int_{0}^{1}\Big(|u_{1}(s)|^{2}-|\mathbb{E}[u_{2}(s)]|^{2}\Big)ds\Big\}.

(6.2)

It is straightforward to see that

J(0;u_{1},0)\geqslant 0\quad\hbox{and}\quad J(0;0,u_{2})\leqslant 0,\quad\forall(u_{1},u_{2})\in{\cal U}_{1}\times{\cal U}_{2}.

(6.3)

Suppose that
(u_{1}^{*},u_{2}^{*})
is an open-loop saddle point, then by
4.8
,

(u_{1}^{*},u_{2}^{*})
must satisfy

u_{1}^{*}=-Y,\quad\mathbb{E}[u_{2}^{*}]=-Z,

with

\left\{\begin{aligned} &dX(s)=-Y(s)ds-Z(s)dW(s),\\
&dY(s)=Z(s)dW(s),\\
&X(0)=x,\quad Y(T)=-X(T).\end{aligned}\right.

(6.4)

Note that the solution of the Riccati equation associated with (
6.4
) is
-{1\over s}
,
which is not integrable over
[0,1]
, thus the decoupling technique is not applicable.
Moreover, FBSDE (
6.4
) does not satisfy the so-called
monotone condition
in Hu–Peng
[
12
]
.
Thus we also cannot determine the solvability of (
6.4
) by
[
12
]
directly.

In the following, let us apply
Theorem 5.1
to determine the open-loop solvability of the game.
For any
\varepsilon>0
, we denote

J_{\varepsilon}(x;u_{1},u_{2})=J(x;u_{1},u_{2})+\varepsilon\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{1}|u_{1}(s)|^{2}ds-\varepsilon\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{1}|u_{2}(s)|^{2}ds.

(6.5)

By (
6.3
), we have

\displaystyle J_{\varepsilon}(0;u_{1},0)\geqslant\varepsilon\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{1}|u_{1}(s)|^{2}ds,\quad~~\forall u_{1}\in{\cal U}_{1},

(6.6)

\displaystyle J_{\varepsilon}(0;0,u_{2})\leqslant-\varepsilon\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{1}|u_{2}(s)|^{2}ds,\quad\forall u_{2}\in{\cal U}_{2}.

Then we can apply
Theorem 4.6
to find the unique open-loop saddle point
of Problem (MF-SG)
ε
with state equation (
6.1
) and functional (
6.5
).
The corresponding Riccati equations (
5.5
)–(
5.6
) in the example read:

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\dot{P_{\varepsilon}}(s)-{P_{\varepsilon}(s)^{2}\over 1+\varepsilon}=0,\quad s\in[0,1],\\
&\dot{\Pi_{\varepsilon}}(s)-{\Pi_{\varepsilon}(s)^{2}\over 1+\varepsilon}=0,\quad s\in[0,1],\\
&P_{\varepsilon}(1)=-1,\quad\Pi_{\varepsilon}(1)=-1.\end{aligned}\right.

(6.7)

Solving (
6.7
) by separating variables, we get

P_{\varepsilon}(s)=-{1+\varepsilon\over s+\varepsilon},\quad\Pi_{\varepsilon}(s)=-{1+\varepsilon\over s+\varepsilon},\quad s\in[0,1].

(6.8)

Define the corresponding feedback operators
\Theta_{\varepsilon}
(
5.10
) and
\bar{\Theta}_{\varepsilon}
(
5.11
) by

\Theta_{\varepsilon}(s)=\begin{pmatrix}{1\over s+\varepsilon}\\
0\end{pmatrix},\quad\bar{\Theta}_{\varepsilon}(s)=\begin{pmatrix}{1\over s+\varepsilon}\\
0\end{pmatrix},\quad s\in[0,1].

(6.9)

Then the unique open-loop saddle point of Problem (MF-SG)
ε
is given by

u_{\varepsilon}=\begin{pmatrix}u_{1}^{\varepsilon}\\
u^{\varepsilon}_{2}\end{pmatrix}=\Theta_{\varepsilon}\big\{X_{\varepsilon}-\mathbb{E}[X_{\varepsilon}]\big\}+\bar{\Theta}_{\varepsilon}\mathbb{E}[X_{\varepsilon}]=\begin{pmatrix}{X_{\varepsilon}\over s+\varepsilon}\\
0\end{pmatrix},\quad s\in[0,1],

(6.10)

with
X_{\varepsilon}
being the unique solution to the following closed-loop system:

\left\{\begin{aligned} dX_{\varepsilon}(s)&={X_{\varepsilon}(s)\over s+\varepsilon}ds,\quad s\in[0,1],\\
X_{\varepsilon}(0)&=x.\end{aligned}\right.

(6.11)

By the variation of constants formula for ordinary differential equations, we get

X_{\varepsilon}(s)={s+\varepsilon\over\varepsilon}x,\quad s\in[0,1].

(6.12)

Combining the above with (
6.10
),
we obtain the following explicit representation of
u_{\varepsilon}
:

u_{\varepsilon}=\begin{pmatrix}u^{\varepsilon}_{1}\\
u^{\varepsilon}_{2}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}{x\over\varepsilon}\\
0\end{pmatrix},\quad s\in[0,1].

(6.13)

Moreover, note that

\sup_{\varepsilon>0}\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{1}|u_{\varepsilon}(s)|^{2}=\infty,\quad\hbox{if}\quad x\neq 0,

(6.14)

and

\sup_{\varepsilon>0}\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{1}|u_{\varepsilon}(s)|^{2}=0,\quad\hbox{if}\quad x=0.

(6.15)

Thus according to
Theorem 5.1
, the problem
has no open-loop saddle point for
x\neq 0
and has an open-loop saddle point
(0,0)
for
x=0
.
