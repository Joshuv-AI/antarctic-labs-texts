Generalised solutions to linear and non-linear Schrödinger-type equations with point defect: Colombeau and non-Colombeau regimes Thanks: Partially supported by the Ministry of Science, Technological Development and Innovation of the Republic of Serbia (Grant No. 451-03-47/2023-01/200125)(N.D. and I.V.), the Italian National Institute for Higher Mathematics INdAM (A.M.) and the Alexander von Humboldt Foundation, Bonn (A.M.).
Nevena Dugandžija, Alessandro Michelangeli, Ivana Vojnović

Abstract
. For a semi-linear Schrödinger equation of Hartree type in three spatial dimensions, various approximations of singular, point-like perturbations are considered, in the form of potentials of very small range and very large magnitude, obeying different scaling limits. The corresponding nets of approximate solutions represent actual generalised solutions for the singular-perturbed Schrödinger equation. The behaviour of such nets is investigated, comparing the distinct scaling regimes that yield, respectively, the Hartree equation with point interaction Hamiltonian vs the ordinary Hartree equation with the free Laplacian. In the second case, the distinguished regime admitting a generalised solution in the Colombeau algebra is studied, and for such a solution compatibility with the classical Hartree equation is established, in the sense of the Colombeau generalised solution theory.

1.
Introduction and background

Popular and ubiquitous partial differential equations such as the linear Schrödinger equation

(1.1)

\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}u\;=\;-\Delta u+Vu

and the non-linear Schrödinger equation

(1.2)

\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}u\;=\;-\Delta u+Vu+g(u)

(in the complex-valued unknown
u\equiv u(t,x)
with
t\in\mathbb{R}
,
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\in\mathbb{N}
, and for given measurable function
V:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
and given non-linearity
g(u)
, i.e., a semi-linearity) are meaningful, among many other contexts, in applications where a point-like perturbation of the
d
-dimensional Laplacian
\Delta
is added, modelling some type of defect or impurity extremely localised at one point or at a discrete set of points, and/or also when the initial data of the associated Cauchy problem display themselves a point-like profile of infinite magnitude.

This class of problems appear, with different motivations and tools, in different branches of the literature.

One mainstream concerns
generalised solutions
to PDEs, beyond the ordinary solution theory in customary Sobolev spaces
H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
s\geqslant 0

The

theory of
generalised functions
is the distributional one initiated by Schwartz in the late 1940’s (‘generalised functions’ refers to the embedding of

into
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\Omega)

\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
), whose triumph for differential equations, among other fields of analysis, is best exemplified by the Ehrenpreis-Malgrange existence result
(see, e.g.,
[
64
, Theorem IX.23]
,
[
36
, Theorem 7.3.10]
) for which every linear PDE with constant coefficients has a solution in
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\Omega)
), and whose limitations are represented by the existence of linear PDEs with variable (even polynomial) coefficients without solutions in
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\Omega)
, as shown first by Lewy
[
42
]
. In this respect it is worth recalling that a non-
L^{2}
solution theory for (
1.1
) generically features
non-uniqueness
, since the Cauchy problem for (
1.1
) is a characteristic initial value problem in the sense that the initial plane
t=0
is characteristic for the equation (we refer, e.g., to Hörmander’s discussion
[
35
, Chapter VIII]
and
[
37
, Section 13.6]
; one may also find therein an example of non-zero solutions to the free Schrödinger equation with zero initial value – see in particular
[
35
, Theorem 8.9.2 and Section 8.9]
and
[
37
, Example 13.6.5]
– however, none of such solutions belongs to
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for any positive time).

For
non-linear
PDEs a counterpart distributional solution theory is problematic in many instances – one instructive representative is the Kenig-Ponce-Vega ill-posedness in
L^{\infty}([0,+\infty),\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}))
of (
1.2
) with
V\equiv 0
, and

[
40

[

that for an associative algebra
\mathcal{A}
containing
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})

\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})

cannot agree with the ordinary product on all continuous functions.

In order to accommodate a point-like, or measure-type defect in the Cauchy problem (in the equation and/or in the initial data), and hence to manipulate, at least in the non-linear case, possibly ill-defined products of distributions, an industry has developed over the years to give meaning to generalised solutions to partial differential equations of Schrödinger, heat, KdV, semi-linear wave, and Burgers type, among others, within suitable algebraic structures that extend the distribution space
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and are identified by representatives in the form of nets of smooth solutions to regularised problems, i.e., where the original singular coefficients (viewed as generalised functions) or singular initial data are suitably smoothed out. To exemplify, for concreteness with the singular version of (
1.1
) of the form
\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}u=Au
with “
A=-\Delta+\delta(x)
”, a generalised solution involves the existence of limits of the form

(1.3)

\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\varepsilon^{-a}\big\|\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}u_{\varepsilon}-A_{\varepsilon}u_{\varepsilon}\big\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}([0,T),L^{2}_{x})}\;=\;0\,,\qquad a>0\,,

where
(A_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon>0}
and
(u_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon\in(0,1]}
are suitable nets, respectively, of regularised operators representing

and of smooth functions representing the generalised solution.

2.
Set-up and main results

As emergent PDEs after a process of limit we consider two time-dependent Schrödinger-type equations in three spatial dimensions, that is,

(2.1)

\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}u\;=\;-\Delta u+(w*|u|^{2})u

and

(2.2)

\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}v\;=\;-\Delta_{\alpha}v+(w*|v|^{2})v\,,

for given
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
and given real-valued measurable potential
w
, in the unknowns
u\equiv u(t,x)
and
v\equiv v(t,x)
, with
t\in\mathbb{R}
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, where the convolution in the cubic Hartree non-linearity is with respect to the
x
-variable. The choice
w\equiv\mathbf{0}
vs
w\equiv\!\!\!\!\!/\;\>\mathbf{0}
discriminates between the linear and the non-linear problem.

The linear action
u\mapsto\Delta_{\alpha}u
in (
2.2
) refers to the singular perturbation of the Laplacian with a point interaction supported at the origin. By this one means the standard construction, concisely recalled in Appendix
B
, of the self-adjoint extension, with respect to
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
, of the densely defined symmetric operator
(-\Delta)\upharpoonright C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\{0\})
, characterised by having inverse
s
-wave scattering length equal to
-(4\pi\alpha)^{-1}
. This is the relevant Hamiltonian of point interaction in quantum mechanics for a Schrödinger particle coupled to the origin by means of a non-trivial interaction of zero range.

Next, as approximants to (
2.1
) and (
2.2
), we consider, for each
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
, the time-dependent Schrödinger equation

(2.3)

\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}u_{\varepsilon}\;=\;-\Delta u_{\varepsilon}+V_{\varepsilon}u_{\varepsilon}+(w*|u_{\varepsilon}|^{2})u_{\varepsilon}\,,

in the unknown
u_{\varepsilon}\equiv u_{\varepsilon}(t,x)
, with
(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}
, where
V_{\varepsilon}
is a real-valued potential effectively supported around the origin at a spatial scale
\varepsilon
, and is meant to represent a singular, delta-like profile of some magnitude and centred at
x=0
. The general law by which
V_{\varepsilon}
is assumed to shrink around the origin is

(2.4)

V_{\varepsilon}(x)\;:=\;\frac{\,\eta(\varepsilon)\,}{\>\varepsilon^{\sigma}}V\Big(\frac{x}{\varepsilon}\Big)\,,

for a given measurable function
V:\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}
, a given smooth function
\eta:[0,1]\to[0,+\infty)
such that
\eta(0)=\eta(1)=1
, and a given
\sigma\geqslant 0
.
In (
2.4
) the parameter
\sigma
governs the magnitude of the scaling, whereas the function
\eta
only provides a ‘distortion’ factor that is relevant in certain modelling (otherwise it can be just assumed to be
\eta\equiv 1
). Convenient regularity, integrability, and decay assumptions on
V
are needed in practice, as is going to be declared in due time.

The scaling law (
2.4
) covers distinguished and fundamentally different regimes. A meaningful one is with
\sigma=3
, in which case
V_{\varepsilon}\to(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}V)\delta(x)
in
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
as
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
, provided that
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}V
is (non-zero, and) finite. In particular, as discussed in Appendix
A
, the case where
V\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
or
V\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
,
V\geqslant 0
(and not identically zero),
\eta\equiv 1
, and
\sigma=3
is the standard ‘
Colombeau regime
’, the net
(u_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon\in(0,1]}
then representing a generalised solution to the formal equation

(2.5)

\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}u\;=\;-\Delta u+a_{V}\delta u+(w*|u|^{2})u\,,

where
a_{V}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}V(x)\,\mathrm{d}x
.

The regimes
\sigma\in[0,3)
and
\sigma>3
in (
2.4
) represent a point-like defect that, as compared to (
2.5
), is, respectively,
much weaker
or
much stronger
than the Dirac delta. Somewhat counter-intuitively, as we shall see, the net effect may be a trivial or non-trivial “impurity” localised at
x=0
. In fact, we shall prove that as long as
\sigma\in[0,3]
the only non-trivial interaction that may emerge in the limit

is in the already well known distinguished regime

, under the spectral condition of presence of a zero-energy resonance for
-\Delta+V
, and absence of zero-energy eigenvalue, on which we shall comment in a moment.

It is important to underline for the present purposes the following well-posedness result under standard working assumptions on the potentials

Theorem 2.1
.

When

w\,\in\,L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3},\mathbb{R})\cap W^{1,3}(\mathbb{R}^{3},\mathbb{R})\,,\qquad\textrm{$w$ is an even function,}

V\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{3},\mathbb{R})+L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3},\mathbb{R})\,,\qquad p>\frac{3}{2}\,,

\textrm{$V$ is real-valued}\,,\qquad V\in\mathcal{R}\,,

In fact the above assumptions, including the restriction to the Rollnik class, may be further relaxed while keeping the following discussion essentially unaltered; yet, the main focus here is the magnitude of the scaling, and not the generality of the un-scaled potential.

All

As for the

We should like to conclude this presentation of the main results by mentioning another potential virtue of an analysis of this kind, or at least of its spirit. Precisely because the limiting behaviour of solutions to non-linear (Schrödinger) equations with approximate point-like perturbations appears to be a relevant information in several contexts, also quite independent in their motivations, it is of interest to investigate the general issue of how the

Proposition 3.2

(H_{\varepsilon}+\lambda\mathbbm{1})^{-1}-(-\Delta+\lambda\mathbbm{1})^{-1}\;=\;-A_{\varepsilon}^{(\lambda)}F_{\varepsilon}^{(\lambda)}C_{\varepsilon}^{(\lambda)}

Set
v_{\varepsilon}:=\sqrt{|V_{\varepsilon}|}

and

for any
\lambda>0
with

\begin{split}&(-\Delta+\lambda\mathbbm{1})^{-1}v_{\varepsilon}(U_{\varepsilon}U_{\varepsilon}^{*})\big(\mathbbm{1}+u_{\varepsilon}(-\Delta+\lambda\mathbbm{1})^{-1}v_{\varepsilon}\big)^{-1}u_{\varepsilon}(-\Delta+\lambda\mathbbm{1})^{-1}\\

&\qquad\times\;\varepsilon^{3-\sigma}\eta(\varepsilon)\big(\mathbbm{1}+\varepsilon^{2-\sigma}\eta(\varepsilon)u(-\Delta+\lambda\mathbbm{1})^{-1}v\big)^{-1}\times\\
&\qquad\times\;U_{\varepsilon}^{*}u_{\varepsilon}(-\Delta+\lambda\mathbbm{1})^{-1}(\varepsilon^{3-\sigma}\eta(\varepsilon))^{-\frac{1}{2}}\end{split}

(whenever the division by non-zero

\begin{split}\big\|F_{\varepsilon}^{(\lambda)}f\big\|_{L^{2}}\;&=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}g\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\\

\|g\|_{L^{2}}=1\end{subarray}}\Big\|\big(F_{\varepsilon}^{(\lambda)}\big)^{\frac{1}{2}}g\Big\|_{L^{2}}\;\Big\|\big(F_{\varepsilon}^{(\lambda)}\big)^{\frac{1}{2}}f\Big\|_{L^{2}}\\
&\leqslant\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}g\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\\
\|g\|_{L^{2}}=1\end{subarray}}\Big\|\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}^{\frac{1}{2}}g\Big\|_{L^{2}}\;\Big\|\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}^{\frac{1}{2}}f\Big\|_{L^{2}}\;=\;\big\|\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}^{\frac{1}{2}}\big\|_{\mathrm{op}}\big\|\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}^{\frac{1}{2}}f\big\|_{L^{2}}\,,\end{split}

having used the inequality
\mathbb{O}\leqslant F_{\varepsilon}^{(\lambda)}\leqslant\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}
in the third step.

Concerning the norm
\big\|\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}^{\frac{1}{2}}f\big\|_{L^{2}}
,

\begin{split}\big\|\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}^{\frac{1}{2}}f\big\|^{2}_{L^{2}}\;&=\;\int_{[0,+\infty)}\Big|\frac{\varepsilon\,\eta(\varepsilon)}{\,\varepsilon^{\sigma-2}+\eta(\varepsilon)t}\Big|\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{f}^{(B)}(t)\\
&=\;\varepsilon^{3-\sigma}\mu_{f}^{(B)}(\{0\})+\int_{(0,+\infty)}\Big|\frac{\varepsilon\,\eta(\varepsilon)}{\,\varepsilon^{\sigma-2}+\eta(\varepsilon)t}\Big|\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{f}^{(B)}(t)\,,\end{split}

having set
B:=v\big(2(-\Delta)+\mathbbm{1}\big)^{-1}v
, a
positive
self-adjoint Hilbert-Schmidt operator on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
, and having denoted with
\mu_{f}^{(B)}
the scalar spectral measure associated with
B
and
f
. Actually,

\mu_{f}^{(B)}(\{0\})\;=\;\big\langle f,E^{(B)}(\{0\}))f\big\rangle_{L^{2}}\;=\;0

(where
E^{(B)}(\cdot))
is the projection-valued measure associated to
B
) as
E^{(B)}(\{0\}))=\mathbb{O}
, because the spectral value zero is
not
an eigenvalue for
B
. The latter fact is a consequence of the positivity of
V

almost everywhere
, which allows to deduce from
0=Bg=v\big(2(-\Delta)+\mathbbm{1}\big)^{-1}vg
precisely
g=0
. Therefore,

\big\|\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}^{\frac{1}{2}}f\big\|^{2}_{L^{2}}\;=\;\int_{(0,+\infty)}\Big|\frac{\varepsilon\,\eta(\varepsilon)}{\,\varepsilon^{\sigma-2}+\eta(\varepsilon)t}\Big|\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{f}^{(B)}(t)

and since
t>0
dominated convergence is applicable (with integrable majorant given by the constant function
\|\eta\|_{L^{\infty}}\mathbf{1}
inside the integral), and

\big\|\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}^{\frac{1}{2}}f\big\|_{L^{2}}\;\;\xrightarrow{\;\varepsilon\downarrow 0\;}\;0\qquad\forall f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\,.

This shows that
\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}^{\frac{1}{2}}\xrightarrow{\,\varepsilon\downarrow 0\,}\mathbb{O}
in the strong operator topology, whence also

\big\|\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}^{\frac{1}{2}}\big\|_{\mathrm{op}}\;\leqslant\;C\;<\;+\infty\qquad\textrm{uniformly in $\varepsilon$}\,,

owing to the uniform boundedness principle.

The latter findings yield

\big\|F_{\varepsilon}^{(\lambda)}f\big\|_{L^{2}}\;\leqslant\;\big\|\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}^{\frac{1}{2}}\big\|_{\mathrm{op}}\big\|\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}^{\frac{1}{2}}f\big\|_{L^{2}}\;\;\xrightarrow{\;\varepsilon\downarrow 0\;}\;0\qquad\forall f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\,,

which is precisely the limit (
3.11
).
∎

Remark 3.6

The factorised structure (

Remark 4.1
.

Whereas we used the known property (Trotter’s theorem) for which strong resolvent convergence between self-adjoint operators is equivalent to the strong convergence of the unitary groups (propagators) generated by them,
the convergence of such propagators
in operator norm
cannot hold (as emerges, e.g., from the proof of

Next, let us discuss the non-linear case.

Proof of Theorem
2.3
– non-linear case
w\equiv\!\!\!\!\!/\;\>\mathbf{0}
.

Let us consider non-restrictively the forward in time evolution (
t>0
) and re-write the differential problems under considerations, namely

\begin{cases}\;\;\;\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}u_{\varepsilon}\,=\,H_{\varepsilon}u_{\varepsilon}+(w*|u_{\varepsilon}|^{2})u_{\varepsilon}\,,\\
u_{\varepsilon}(0,\cdot)\,=\,a\quad(\forall\varepsilon>0)\end{cases}

and

\begin{cases}\;\;\;\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}u\,=\,H^{\circ}u+(w*|u|^{2})u\,,\\
u(0,\cdot)\,=\,a\,,\end{cases}

in integral form, that is, respectively,

u_{\varepsilon}(t)\;=\;e^{-\mathrm{i}tH_{\varepsilon}}a-\mathrm{i}\int_{0}^{t}\big(e^{-\mathrm{i}(t-s)H_{\varepsilon}}(w*|u_{\varepsilon}|^{2})u_{\varepsilon}\big)(s)\,\mathrm{d}s

and

u(t)\;=\;e^{-\mathrm{i}tH^{\circ}}a-\mathrm{i}\int_{0}^{t}\big(e^{-\mathrm{i}(t-s)H^{\circ}}(w*|u|^{2})u\big)(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\,.

To unify the treatment, in the above expressions
H^{\circ}
stays for either the self-adjoint negative Laplacian
-\Delta
or for the self-adjoint point interaction Hamiltonian
-\Delta_{\alpha}
, depending on case (i) or (ii) of the theorem. Beside, we make the customary abuse of notation writing
u(t)
for
u(t,\cdot)
, and the like. The above expressions are all meaningful, owing to the well-posedness provided by Theorem
2.1
.

We have

(a)

u_{\varepsilon}(t)-u(t)\;=\;P_{\varepsilon}(t)+Q_{\varepsilon}(t)+R_{\varepsilon}(t)\,,

where

\begin{split}P_{\varepsilon}(t)\;&:=\;e^{-\mathrm{i}tH_{\varepsilon}}a-e^{-\mathrm{i}tH^{\circ}}a\,,\\
Q_{\varepsilon}(t)\;&:=\;-\mathrm{i}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\mathrm{i}(t-s)H_{\varepsilon}}\big((w*|u_{\varepsilon}|^{2})u_{\varepsilon}-(w*|u|^{2})u\big)(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\,,\\
R_{\varepsilon}(t)\;&:=\;-\mathrm{i}\int_{0}^{t}\Big(e^{-\mathrm{i}(t-s)H_{\varepsilon}}-e^{-\mathrm{i}(t-s)H^{\circ}}\Big)(w*|u|^{2})u(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\,.\end{split}

Concerning
P_{\varepsilon}
,

\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\big\|P_{\varepsilon}\big\|_{L^{\infty}([0,\textsf{T}],L^{2}_{x})}=\,0\,,

as obtained already in the proof for the linear problem.

Concerning
R_{\varepsilon}
, the Hölder and Young inequalities and the preservation of the
L^{2}
-norm of the solution (Theorem
2.1
) yield (at any
t
)

\begin{split}\big\|(w*|u|^{2})u\big\|_{L^{2}_{x}}\;&\leqslant\;\big\|w*|u|^{2}\big\|_{L^{\infty}_{x}}\|u\|_{L^{2}_{x}}\\
&\lesssim\;\|w\|_{L^{\infty}_{x}}\|u\|^{3}_{L^{2}_{x}}\\
&=\;\|w\|_{L^{\infty}_{x}}\|a\|^{3}_{L^{2}_{x}}\,,\end{split}

meaning that
(w*|u|^{2})u\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
at any time, and therefore, again on account of the proof for the linear problem,

\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\big\|R_{\varepsilon}\big\|_{L^{\infty}([0,\textsf{T}],L^{2}_{x})}=\,0\,.

Last, concerning
Q_{\varepsilon}
, and for
T\in(0,\textsf{T}]
to be specified in a moment,

\begin{split}\big\|Q_{\varepsilon}\big\|_{L^{\infty}([0,T],L^{2}_{x})}&\leqslant\;T\,\big\|(w*|u_{\varepsilon}|^{2})u_{\varepsilon}-(w*|u|^{2})u\big\|_{L^{\infty}([0,T],L^{2}_{x})}\,,\end{split}

owing to
L^{2}
-unitarity. In the above r.h.s.,

\begin{split}\big|(w*|u_{\varepsilon}|^{2})u_{\varepsilon}-(w*|u|^{2})u\big|\;&\leqslant\;(|w|*|u_{\varepsilon}|^{2})|u_{\varepsilon}-u|\\
&\quad+\big(|w|*\big(|u_{\varepsilon}-u|(|u_{\varepsilon}|+|u|)\big)\big)|u|\,.\end{split}

The Young inequality and the conservation of the
L^{2}
-norm of the solutions allow to deduce

(d)

\big\|Q_{\varepsilon}\big\|_{L^{\infty}([0,T],L^{2}_{x})}\;\leqslant\;3\,T\,\|w\|_{L^{\infty}_{x}}\|a\|_{L^{2}_{x}}^{2},\|u_{\varepsilon}-u\|_{L^{\infty}([0,T],L^{2}_{x})}\,.

Selecting
T
so as
T<(3\|w\|_{L^{\infty}_{x}}\|a\|_{L^{2}_{x}}^{2})^{-1}
, (
a
) and (
d
) together yield

\|u_{\varepsilon}-u\|_{L^{\infty}([0,T],L^{2}_{x})}\;\leqslant\;\frac{\;\big\|P_{\varepsilon}\big\|_{L^{\infty}([0,T],L^{2}_{x})}+\big\|R_{\varepsilon}\big\|_{L^{\infty}([0,T],L^{2}_{x})}}{\big(1-3\,T\,\|w\|_{L^{\infty}_{x}}\|a\|_{L^{2}_{x}}^{2}\big)}

and (
4
)-(
4
) then imply

\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\|u_{\varepsilon}-u\|_{L^{\infty}([0,T],L^{2}_{x})}=\;0\,.

We then repeat the above reasoning finitely many times by partitioning the interval
[0,\mathsf{T}]
into intervals
[kT,(k+1)T]\cap[0,\mathsf{T}]
,
k=0,1,2,\dots
.
For each such step, the difference

\|u_{\varepsilon}-u\|_{L^{\infty}([kT,(k+1)T],L^{2}_{x})}

is investigated with a splitting that is completely analogous to the above terms
P_{\varepsilon}
,
Q_{\varepsilon}
,
R_{\varepsilon}
, where clearly the initial time is now
t=kT
instead of
t=0
as in the first step. Obviously, an additional term arises, of the form

e^{-\mathrm{i}(t-kT)H^{\circ}}\big(u_{\varepsilon}(kT)-u(kT)\big)\,,

due to the fact that the initial data for the
k
-th interval are different for
u_{\varepsilon}
and
u
: its
L^{2}_{x}
-vanishing follows from the uniform control on the
(k-1)
-th time interval.

The final conclusion is

\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\|u_{\varepsilon}-u\|_{L^{\infty}([0,\mathsf{T}],L^{2}_{x})}=\;0\,.

The limits (
2.15
) and (
2.17
) are thus proved.

Appendix A
The Colombeau algebra and the notions of generalised solutions and compatibility for Schrödinger equations.

We recall in this Appendix the main features of the Colombeau algebra of generalised functions, we referred to in Section
1
and in Theorem
2.4
.

The Colombeau algebra is an associative differential algebra, and as already mentioned it displays properties that overcome the problem of multiplication of distributions, thus making it well suited as a solution framework for non-linear partial differential equations. It was introduced by Colombeau
[
14
,
15
]
in the early 1980’s, and it is discussed in the literature through an ample spectrum of precursors, refinements, and more recent reviews, as in
[
65
,
66
,
67
,
10
,
61
,
32
,
33
,
34
]
(observe, for instance, that in the precursor represented by Rosinger’s notion of generalised functions
[
65
,
66
]
, the “product of deltas”
\delta^{2}
was still ill-defined, whereas it is allowed within the Colombeau algebra).

For concreteness, and for its relevance in the discussion of this work, we present here in particular the
H^{2}
-based Colombeau algebra, following the approach of
[
55
,
57
]
. Additional details in this respect may be found in
[
22
]
. A completely analogous construction can be made for
E
-based Colombeau algebras for functional spaces
E
that are locally convex spaces and multiplicative algebras.

Given
T>0
, one denotes by

{\mathcal{E}_{C^{1},H^{2}}([0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{3})}\,,\qquad\textrm{respectively,}\qquad{\mathcal{N}_{C^{1},H^{2}}([0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{3})}

the vector
space of nets
(u_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon\in(0,1]}
(with natural component-wise linear structure), of functions

u_{\varepsilon}\;\in\;C([0,T),H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))\cap C^{1}([0,T),L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))

satisfying the following growth properties as
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
: for
a
\mathcal{E}_{C^{1},H^{2}}
-net there is
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that

(A.1)

\max\bigg\{\sup_{t\in[0,T)}\|u_{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{H^{2}}\,,\sup_{t\in[0,T)}\|\partial_{t}u_{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{2}\bigg\}\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\varepsilon\downarrow 0}}{{=}}\;O(\varepsilon^{-N})\,,

and for
a
\mathcal{N}_{C^{1},H^{2}}
-net the following holds true
\forall M\in\mathbb{N}
:

(A.2)

\max\bigg\{\sup_{t\in[0,T)}\|u_{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{H^{2}}\,,\sup_{t\in[0,T)}\|\partial_{t}u_{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{2}\bigg\}\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\varepsilon\downarrow 0}}{{=}}\;O(\varepsilon^{M})\,.

Elements of
\mathcal{E}_{C^{1},H^{2}}([0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{3})
and of
\mathcal{N}_{C^{1},H^{2}}([0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{3})
are called, respectively,
moderate nets
and
negligible nets
.

\mathcal{N}_{C^{1},H^{2}}([0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{3})
is a (multiplicative) bilateral ideal in
\mathcal{E}_{C^{1},H^{2}}([0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{3})
, and this allows to introduce the quotient space

(A.3)

\mathcal{G}_{C^{1},H^{2}}([0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{3})\;:=\;\mathcal{E}_{C^{1},H^{2}}([0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{3})\,/\,\mathcal{N}_{C^{1},H^{2}}([0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{3})

which is a Colombeau-type vector space. In a completely analogous manner one defines

(A.4)

\mathcal{E}_{H^{2}}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\;:=\;\left\{(u_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon\in(0,1]}\left|\begin{array}[]{c}u_{\varepsilon}\in H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\,,\;\textrm{and for each such net}\\
\exists\,N\in\mathbb{N}\;\textrm{such that}\,\;\|u_{\varepsilon}\|_{H^{2}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\varepsilon\downarrow 0}}{{=}}O(\varepsilon^{-N})\end{array}\!\!\right.\right\},

(A.5)

\mathcal{N}_{H^{2}}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\;:=\;\left\{(u_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon\in(0,1]}\left|\begin{array}[]{c}u_{\varepsilon}\in H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\,,\;\textrm{and for each such net}\\
\|u_{\varepsilon}\|_{H^{2}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\varepsilon\downarrow 0}}{{=}}O(\varepsilon^{M})\;\;\forall M\in\mathbb{N}\end{array}\!\!\right.\right\},

and

(A.6)

\mathcal{G}_{H^{2}}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\;:=\;\mathcal{E}_{H^{2}}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\,/\,\mathcal{N}_{H^{2}}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\,,

with the natural vector space structure.

In fact, precisely because each
H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
d\in\{1,2,3\}
is a multiplicative algebra, so too are
\mathcal{G}_{C^{1},H^{2}}([0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{3})
and
\mathcal{G}_{H^{2}}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
with respect to the product inherited by the quotients.

Furthermore,
\mathcal{E}_{C^{1},H^{2}}([0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{3})
and
\mathcal{E}_{H^{2}}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
can be suitably topologised by semi-norms making them locally convex spaces, and in turn a sharp topology can be defined in
\mathcal{G}_{C^{1},H^{2}}([0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{3})
and
\mathcal{G}_{H^{2}}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
making them topological vector spaces – see, e.g.,
[
27
,
26
]
for details.

The Sobolev spaces
H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
, for
s\in\mathbb{R}
, as well as the space
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
of distributions with compact support, are identified (one also customarily says ‘embedded’) as subspaces of
\mathcal{G}_{H^{2}}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
by associating to each
F\in H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
, or
F\in\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
, the equivalence class of the net
(F*\rho_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon\in(0,1]}
up to
\mathcal{N}_{H^{2}}
-nets, where

(A.7)

\begin{split}\rho_{\varepsilon}(x)\,&:=\,\frac{1}{\,\varepsilon^{3}}\,\rho\Big(\frac{x}{\varepsilon}\Big)\quad\textrm{for some }\rho\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\textrm{ such that }\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\rho(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\,=\,1\,.\\
\end{split}

Indeed, all such
F*\rho_{\varepsilon}
’s are well defined, belong to
H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
, and satisfy the growth condition (
A.4
). (Recall, in particular, that for
F\in\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
the object
F*\rho_{\varepsilon}
is the distribution acting on test functions
\varphi
as
(F*\rho_{\varepsilon})(\varphi)=F(\rho_{\varepsilon}*\varphi)
.)

Next, let us review the notion of
generalised Colombeau solution
to the Cauchy problem for semi-linear Schrödinger equations with possible distributional coefficients, explicitly the problem

(A.8)

\begin{cases}\;\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}u\;=\;-\Delta_{x}u+c\,\delta\,u+g(u)\,,\\
u(0,\cdot)\;=\;a\in H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\end{cases}

for given
c\in\mathbb{R}
, given non-linearity
g(\cdot)
of the usual form
g(u)=(w*|u|^{2})u
or
g(u)=\gamma\,|u|^{p-1}u
with
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
,
p\geqslant 1
, and given measurable, real-valued
w
. Observe that (
A.8
) encompasses both the classical problem, when
c=0
, and the one with distributional (Dirac delta) coefficient in the linear part, when
c\neq 0
.

One says that (
A.8
) admits a (local in time) solution
u\in\mathcal{G}_{C^{1},H^{2}}([0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{3})
, for some
T>0
, if there are three nets

•

(u_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon\in(0,1]}\in\mathcal{E}_{C^{1},H^{2}}([0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{3})
,

•

(n_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon\in(0,1]}\in\mathcal{N}_{H^{2}}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
,

•

and
(M_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon\in(0,1]}\in C([0,T),L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))
with
\|M_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{\infty}([0,T),L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\varepsilon\downarrow 0}}{{=}}O(\varepsilon^{M})

\forall M\in\mathbb{N}

satisfying

(A.9)

\begin{cases}\;\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}u_{\varepsilon}-\big(-\Delta_{x}u_{\varepsilon}+c\,\delta_{\varepsilon}\,u_{\varepsilon}+g(u_{\varepsilon})\big)\,=\,M_{\varepsilon}\,,\\
u(0,\cdot)\;=\;a_{\varepsilon}+n_{\varepsilon}\,,\end{cases}

where

(A.10)

a_{\varepsilon}\,:=\,a*\rho_{\varepsilon}\,,\qquad\delta_{\varepsilon}\,:=\,\delta*\rho_{\varepsilon}\,,

and
\rho_{\varepsilon}
is given by (
A.7
) (i.e., the equivalence classes
[(a_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon\in(0,1]}]
and
[(\delta_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon\in(0,1]}]
are the identifications (‘embeddings’) in
\mathcal{G}_{H^{2}}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
, respectively, of
a\in H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
and of
\delta\in\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
). This definition characterises the generalised solution
u
independently of its representative
(u_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon\in(0,1]}\in\mathcal{E}_{C^{1},H^{2}}([0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{3})
with respect to the quotient (
A.3
).

In addition, the problem (
A.8
) is said to admit a
unique
generalised Colombeau solution if for any two
u,v\in\mathcal{G}_{C^{1},H^{2}}([0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{3})
satisfying (
A.8
) in the above generalised Colombeau sense one has

(A.11)

\|u_{\varepsilon}-v_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{\infty}([0,T),L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))}\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\varepsilon\downarrow 0}}{{=}}\;O(\varepsilon^{M})\qquad\forall M\in\mathbb{N}

for a pair (hence for any pair) of nets
(u_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon\in(0,1]}
and
(v_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon\in(0,1]}
in
\mathcal{E}_{C^{1},H^{2}}([0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{3})
representing, respectively,
u
and
v
.

Existence and uniqueness of the generalised Colombeau
\mathcal{G}_{C^{1},H^{2}}
-solution
u_{\mathrm{Col}}
to the Cauchy problem for the Hartree equation with
\delta
-coefficient, namely

(A.12)

\begin{cases}\;\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}u\;=\;-\Delta_{x}u+\delta\,u+(w*|u|^{2})u\,,\\
u(0,\cdot)\;=\;a\in H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\,,\end{cases}

was established in
[
21
]
with even-symmetric
w\in W^{2,p}(\mathbb{R}^{3},\mathbb{R})
,
p\in(2,\infty]
: in this case, according to the general definition stated above, a representative net
(u_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon\in(0,1]}
of such
u_{\mathrm{Col}}
satisfies

(A.13)

\begin{cases}\;\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}u_{\varepsilon}\;=\;-\Delta_{x}u_{\varepsilon}+V_{\varepsilon}u_{\varepsilon}+(w*|u_{\varepsilon}|^{2})u_{\varepsilon}\\
u(0,\cdot)\;=\;a*\rho_{\varepsilon}\end{cases}\qquad\forall\varepsilon\in(0,1]\,,

where
V_{\varepsilon}
is given by (
2.4
) with
\eta\equiv 1
,
\sigma=3
, and
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}V=1
. On the other hand, the classical Cauchy problem

(A.14)

\begin{cases}\;\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}u\;=\;-\Delta_{x}u+(w*|u|^{2})u\,,\\
u(0,\cdot)\;=\;a\in H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\end{cases}

is globally (hence also locally) well posed in
H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
in the classical (Sobolev) sense
[
13
, Section 5.3]
, say, with solution
u_{\mathrm{cl}}\in C(\mathbb{R},H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))
. Observe that in general

u_{\mathrm{Col}}\;=\;\big[(u_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon\in(0,1]}\big]\;\neq\;[(u_{\mathrm{cl}}*\rho_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon\in(0,1]}]

that is, such two equivalence classes in
\mathcal{G}_{C^{1},H^{2}}([0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{3})
do not necessarily coincide.

A weaker identification that replaces the above coincidence in
\mathcal{G}_{C^{1},H^{2}}([0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{3})
, or lack thereof, is the notion of
association
. One says that
u\in\mathcal{G}_{C^{1},H^{2}}([0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{3})
is
associated
with a distribution-valued map
v:[0,T)\to\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
, and then writes
u\approx v
, if there is a representative
(u_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon\in(0,1]}
of
u
such that
u_{\varepsilon}(t,\cdot)\xrightarrow{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}v(t)
in
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
for all
t\in[0,T)
.

A meaningful association one may establish is of the type

u_{\mathrm{Col}}\;=\;\big[(u_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon\in(0,1]}\big]\;\approx\;u_{\mathrm{cl}}\,.

In fact, the association above is typically implied by the following notion of

compatibility
, formulated as follows for concreteness for the Hartree equation: one says that the Colombeau generalised solution
u_{\mathrm{Col}}=[(u_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon\in(0,1]}]\in\mathcal{G}_{C^{1},H^{2}}([0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{3})
to the Cauchy problem (
A.12
), thus with the
u_{\varepsilon}
’s satisfying (
A.13
), is
compatible
with the classical solution
u_{\mathrm{cl}}\in C([0,T),H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))
to the Cauchy problem (
A.14
), precisely when

(A.15)

\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\|u_{\mathrm{cl}}-u_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{\infty}([0,T),L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))}\,=\;0\,.

Such a definition does not depend on the representative net for
u_{\mathrm{Col}}
.
Clearly, re-interpreting each
u_{\mathrm{cl}}(t,\cdot)
as a
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
-element, the occurrence of (
A.15
) does imply the association
u_{\mathrm{Col}}\approx u_{\mathrm{cl}}
.

It is worth mentioning that association, compatibility, and variants thereof, between generalised and classical solutions to non-linear PDEs are the object of intensive investigation. This includes, among others, the proof of the analogous of (
A.15
) in
L^{\infty}([0,T),H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))
for the semi-linear wave equation
[
55
]
, and in
L^{\infty}([0,T),\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))
for hyperbolic conservation laws
[
62
]
, as well as modified notions such as a type of
L^{\infty}
-association (thus, stronger than the association introduced above)
[
56
]
.

Appendix B
The operator
\Delta_{\alpha}

We recall here the standard construction of the singular point-like perturbation
\Delta_{\alpha}
of
\Delta
in dimension
d=3
– see, e.g.,
[
4
, Chapter I.1]
and
[
49
, Section 3]
.

The operator
S:=(-\Delta)\upharpoonright C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\{0\})
is densely defined, symmetric, and positive with respect to the Hilbert space
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
. Its self-adjoint extensions form the one-parameter family
(-\Delta_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}}
, where the element corresponding to
\alpha=\infty
is the Friedrichs extension of
S
and consists of the self-adjoint negative Laplacian in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
with domain
H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
, whereas, for fixed
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
and
\lambda>0
,

(B.1)

\begin{split}\mathrm{dom}(-\Delta_{\alpha})\;&=\;\left\{\psi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\left|\begin{array}[]{c}\exists\,f_{\psi}^{(\lambda)}\!\in H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\textrm{ such that}\\
\psi=f_{\psi}^{(\lambda)}+\frac{\,f_{\psi}^{(\lambda)}(0)\,}{\alpha+\frac{\sqrt{\lambda}}{4\pi}}\,G_{\lambda}\end{array}\!\!\right.\right\},\\
(-\Delta_{\alpha}+\lambda\mathbbm{1})\psi\;&=\;(-\Delta+\lambda\mathbbm{1})f_{\psi}^{(\lambda)}\,,\end{split}

having denoted by
G_{\lambda}
the fundamental solution (Green function) to
(-\Delta+\lambda 1)G_{\alpha}=\delta(x)
, explicitly,

(B.2)

G_{\lambda}(x)\;=\;\frac{\,e^{-|x|\sqrt{\lambda}}\,}{4\pi|x|}\,.

In (
B.1
) the space
\mathrm{dom}(-\Delta_{\alpha})
is independent of
\lambda
, whereas the decomposition of
\psi
into a regular
H^{2}
-part and a singular part proportional to
G_{\lambda}
does depends on the chosen
\lambda>0
; each
\psi\in\mathrm{dom}(-\Delta_{\alpha})
and
\lambda>0
uniquely identifies
f_{\psi}^{(\lambda)}
.

Each self-adjoint extension
-\Delta_{\alpha}
has spectrum with the following features:

(B.3)

\begin{split}\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}(-\Delta_{\alpha})\;=\;\sigma_{\mathrm{ac}}(-\Delta_{\alpha})\;&=\;[0,+\infty)\,,\\
\sigma_{\mathrm{sc}}(-\Delta_{\alpha})\;&=\;\emptyset\,,\\
\sigma_{\mathrm{p}}(-\Delta_{\alpha})\;&=\;\begin{cases}\quad\;\;\emptyset\,,&\textrm{ if }\alpha\geqslant 0\,,\\
\{-(4\pi\alpha)^{2}\}\,,&\textrm{ if }\alpha<0\,.\end{cases}\end{split}

The negative eigenvalue, when existing, is non-degenerate, and has normalised eigenfunction

(B.4)

\Psi_{\alpha}(x)\;:=\;\sqrt{-2|\alpha|\,}\,\frac{\,e^{-4\pi|\alpha||x|}\,}{|x|}\,.

For each
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\setminus\sigma(-\Delta_{\alpha})
the resolvent of
-\Delta_{\alpha}
is given by

(B.5)

(-\Delta_{\alpha}+\lambda\mathbbm{1})^{-1}\;=\;(-\Delta+\lambda\mathbbm{1})^{-1}+\frac{1}{\alpha+\frac{\sqrt{\lambda}}{4\pi}}\,|G_{\lambda}\rangle\langle G_{\lambda}|\,.

It is therefore a rank-one perturbation of the free resolvent.

The operator
\Delta_{\alpha}
is a perturbation of
\Delta
in the sole
L^{2}
-sector of spherically symmetric functions, and coincides with the self-adjoint Laplacian on all the
H^{2}
-functions orthogonal to the spherically symmetric ones.
The
s
-wave scattering length of
-\Delta_{\alpha}
amounts to
-(4\pi\alpha)^{-1}
.
Beside,
\Delta_{\alpha}
is a local operator, in the sense that if
\psi\in\mathrm{dom}(-\Delta_{\alpha})
vanishes on an open
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
, then
(\Delta_{\alpha}\psi)|_{U}\equiv 0
.

The integral kernel
K_{\alpha}(x,y;t)
of the unitary propagator
e^{\mathrm{i}t\Delta_{\alpha}}
,
t>0
, generated by
-\Delta_{\alpha}
is also explicit
[
2
]
, and given by

(B.6)

\begin{split}K_{\alpha}(x,y;t)\;=\;\begin{cases}\begin{array}[]{c}K(x,y;t)+\displaystyle\frac{1}{\,|x|\,|y|\,}\int_{0}^{+\infty}e^{-4\pi\alpha s}(s+|x|+|y|)\,\times\\
\times\,K(s+|x|+|y|,0;t)\,\mathrm{d}s\,,\end{array}&\textrm{if }\alpha>0\,,\\
\\
K(x,y;t)+\displaystyle\frac{2\,\mathrm{i}\,t}{\,|x|\,|y|\,}\,K(|x|+|y|,0;t)\,,&\textrm{if }\alpha=0\,,\\
\\
\begin{array}[]{l}K(x,y;t)+e^{\mathrm{i}t(4\pi\alpha)^{2}}\Psi_{\alpha}(x)\Psi_{\alpha}(y)\\
+\displaystyle\frac{1}{\,|x|\,|y|\,}\int_{0}^{+\infty}e^{-4\pi|\alpha|s}(s-|x|-|y|)\,\times\\
\qquad\qquad\qquad\times\,K(s-|x|-|y|,0,t)\,\mathrm{d}s\,,\end{array}&\textrm{if }\alpha<0\,,\end{cases}\end{split}

where

(B.7)

K(x,y;t)\;:=\;\frac{\,e^{-\frac{\>|x-y|^{2}}{4\mathrm{i}t}}}{\,(4\pi\mathrm{i}t)^{\frac{3}{2}}}\,,\qquad t>0

is the kernel of the free Schrödinger propagator.
