Walls for 𝑮 \boldsymbol{G} -Hilb via Reid’s Recipe
Ben WORMLEIGHTON

Abstract
The three-dimensional McKay correspondence seeks to relate the geometry of crepant resolutions of Gorenstein 3 3 -fold quotient singularities 𝔸 3 / G \mathbb{A}^{3}/G with the representation theory of the group G G . The first crepant resolution studied in depth was the G G -Hilbert scheme G ​ -Hilb ​ 𝔸 3 G\text{-Hilb}\,\mathbb{A}^{3} , which is also a moduli space of θ \theta -stable representations of the McKay quiver associated to G G . As the stability parameter θ \theta varies, we obtain many other crepant resolutions. In this paper we focus on the case where G G is abelian, and compute explicit inequalities for the chamber of the stability space defining G ​ -Hilb ​ 𝔸 3 G\text{-Hilb}\,\mathbb{A}^{3} in terms of a marking of exceptional subvarieties of G ​ -Hilb ​ 𝔸 3 G\text{-Hilb}\,\mathbb{A}^{3} called Reid’s recipe. We further show which of these inequalities define walls. This procedure depends only on the combinatorics of the exceptional fibre and has applications to the birational geometry of other crepant resolutions.

Introduction

Let
G\subset\operatorname{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
be a finite subgroup. When
n=2
there is a famous ADE classification of such subgroups that matches the classification of Du Val or modality zero singularities by taking a subgroup
G
to the quotient singularity
0\in\mathbb{A}^{2}/G
. This observation and the surrounding deep interactions of the geometry of
\mathbb{A}^{2}/G
and its resolutions, and the representation theory of
G
are known as the two-dimensional McKay correspondence
[
1
,
2
,
13
,
16
,
17
,
21
,
23
]
. In this case, the unique minimal or crepant resolution has a modular interpretation as the
G
-Hilbert scheme
G\text{{-Hilb}}\,\mathbb{A}^{2}
. The moduli space
G\text{{-Hilb}}\,M
for
M
a variety and
G\subset\operatorname{Aut}(M)
a finite subgroup parameterises
G
-clusters in
M
: zero-dimensional
G
-invariant subschemes
M
of
\mathbb{A}^{2}
with
H^{0}(\mathcal{O}_{Z})\cong\mathbb{C}[G]
as
G
-modules. This was generalised to three dimensions for finite abelian subgroups of
\operatorname{SL}_{3}(\mathbb{C})
by Nakamura
[
22
]
who showed that
G\text{{-Hilb}}\,\mathbb{A}^{3}
is a crepant resolution of
\mathbb{A}^{3}/G
and then to all subgroups
G
by the celebrated work of Bridgeland–King–Reid
[
5
]
. They moreover established an equivalence of categories

\displaystyle D^{b}\big(G\text{{-Hilb}}\,\mathbb{A}^{3}\big)\simeq D^{b}_{G}\big(\mathbb{A}^{3}\big),

(1.1)

which also holds if
G\text{{-Hilb}}\,\mathbb{A}^{3}
is replaced by any projective crepant resolution of
\mathbb{A}^{3}/G
. Compare also the results of Bridgeland
[
4
]
.

Using the GIT approach of King
[
19
]
to constructing moduli of quiver representations the
G
-Hilbert
scheme can also be realised as a moduli space of
\theta
-stable quiver representations
\mathcal{M}_{\theta}(Q,\underline{d})
, where
Q
is the McKay quiver of
G
and
\underline{d}=(d_{i})
is a given dimension vector. In this situation the stability parameter
\theta
lives in the stability space

\displaystyle\Theta:=\Bigg\{\theta\in\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb{Z}}\big(\mathbb{Z}^{Q_{0}},\mathbb{Q}\big)\colon\sum_{i\in Q_{0}}d_{i}\theta(i)=0\Bigg\},

where
Q_{0}
is the set of vertices of
Q
. By definition, the vertices of the McKay quiver biject with the irreducible representations
\operatorname{Irr}(G)
of
G
and so one can view
\mathbb{Z}^{Q_{0}}
as the abelian group underlying the representation ring of
G
. As
\theta
varies, it is possible that one obtains many different crepant resolutions of
\mathbb{A}^{3}/G
; in the case that
G
is abelian, Craw–Ishii
[
10
]
show that all projective crepant resolutions arise in this way. The stability space
\Theta
has a wall-and-chamber structure such that the moduli space
\mathcal{M}_{\theta}(Q,\underline{d})
is constant so long as
\theta
remains inside a given chamber. We denote the moduli space
\mathcal{M}_{\mathfrak{C}}:=\mathcal{M}_{\theta}(Q,\underline{d})
for any generic
\theta
in a chamber
\mathfrak{C}
. Denote the chamber corresponding to
G\text{{-Hilb}}\,\mathbb{A}^{3}
by
\mathfrak{C}_{0}
. The positive orthant

\displaystyle\Theta^{+}:=\big\{\theta\in\Theta\colon\theta(\rho)>0\text{ for all nontrivial $\rho\in\operatorname{Irr}(G)$}\big\}

lies inside
\mathfrak{C}_{0}
however it is not usually equal to it. The primary purpose of this paper is to provide explicit combinatorial inequalities defining
\mathfrak{C}_{0}
and identify precisely which of these define walls of
\mathfrak{C}_{0}
. We remark that such equations were computed for a group of order
11
in
[
10
, Example 9.6]
.

Assume that
G
is abelian. In this context
[
10
, Theorem 9.5]
gives an abstract description of such inequalities, however it is difficult to perform explicit calculations or deduce general statements from their presentation. One can view some of the results herein as a combinatorialisation of
[
10
, Theorem 9.5]
, which turn out to be very amenable to applications.

We briefly outline the context and notation of
[
10
]
that we will also use. For a chamber
\mathfrak{C}\subset\Theta
the equivalence from (
1.1
) induces an isomorphism
\varphi_{\mathfrak{C}}\colon K_{0}(\mathcal{M}_{\mathfrak{C}})\to K_{G}\big(\mathbb{A}^{3}\big)=\operatorname{Rep}(G)
. Here
K_{0}(\mathcal{M}_{\mathfrak{C}})
denotes the
K
-group of sheaves supported on the preimage of the
G
-orbit for the origin under the resolution
\mathcal{M}_{\mathfrak{C}}\to\mathbb{A}^{3}/G
. Walls in
\Theta
are cut out by hyperplanes
\big(\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}\cdot\theta(\chi_{i})=0\big)
for some characters
\chi_{i}\in\operatorname{Irr}(G)
and integers
\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
, though in general not all
\theta
on such a hyperplane will be non-generic. The inequalities in
[
10
]
have three different forms, each coming from exceptional subvarieties. Firstly, each exceptional curve
C\subset G\text{{-Hilb}}\,\mathbb{A}^{3}
gives an inequality of the form

\displaystyle\theta\big(\varphi_{\mathfrak{C}_{0}}(\mathcal{O}_{C})\big)>0.

The characters appearing in these inequalities are packaged in collections of monomials associated to exceptional curves that were named by Nakamura
[
22

in a different context as
G
-igsaw pieces
. Our first result is to pin down which characters lie in
G
-igsaw pieces. In general there are several

-igsaw pieces corresponding to a single curve

C
. We call this union the

G
-igsaw piece

As
G
is abelian the singularity
\mathbb{A}^{3}/G
and its crepant resolutions are toric. There is a method of marking the exceptional subvarieties of
G
-Hilb – the edges and vertices in the triangulation – by characters of

indexed by characters by Craw
[
9
]
. It was later categorified by Logvinenko
[
20
]
, Cautis–Logvinenko
[
7
]
, and Cautis–Craw–Logvinenko
[
6
]
, who expressed the locus labelled by a character
\chi
in terms of the support of an object associated to
\chi
in the derived category of
G
-Hilb. We will discuss this in more detail in Sections

and
2.4
.

Proposition 1.2

(Propositions
4.1
and
4.2
)
.

Suppose

is an exceptional curve marked with character
\chi
by Reid’s recipe. The inequality corresponding to

is given by

\displaystyle\theta(\varphi_{\mathfrak{C}_{0}}(\mathcal{O}_{C}))=\sum_{\chi\in\text{$G$-{ig}}(C)}\deg(\mathcal{R}_{\chi}|_{C})\theta(\chi)>0.

If
C
is a
(-1,-1)
-curve then the necessary inequality corresponding to
C
that defines a Type

is given by

\displaystyle\theta\big(\varphi_{\mathfrak{C}_{0}}(\mathcal{O}_{C})\big)=\sum_{\chi\in\text{$G$-{ig}}(C)}\theta(\chi)>0.

C
is a
(1,-3)
-curve then the inequality corresponding to

is given by

\displaystyle\theta\big(\varphi_{\mathfrak{C}_{0}}(\mathcal{O}_{C})\big)=2\cdot\theta\big(\chi^{\otimes 2}\big)+\sum_{\chi\in\text{$G$-{ig}}(C)\setminus\{\chi^{\otimes 2}\}}\theta(\chi)=0.

In all three cases
\text{$G$-{ig}}(C)
is computed by the unlocking procedure.

Resolutions of

2.1
Setup

Let
G\subset\operatorname{SL}_{3}(\mathbb{C})
be a finite abelian subgroup. We will assume that
G
is cyclic for notational simplicity during this preliminary section however we will shed this assumption from Section
3
onwards. We will denote by
\frac{1}{r}(a,b,c)
the cyclic subgroup of
\operatorname{SL}_{3}(\mathbb{C})
generated by the matrix

\displaystyle g=\begin{pmatrix}\varepsilon^{a}\\
&\varepsilon^{b}\\
&&\varepsilon^{c}\end{pmatrix},

where
\varepsilon
is a primitive
r
th root of unity. The first resolution of the quotient singularity
\mathbb{A}^{3}/G
to be studied was the
G
-Hilbert scheme
G\text{{-Hilb}}\,\mathbb{A}^{3}
, the fine moduli space of
G
-clusters: zero-dimensional
G
-invariant subschemes
Z\subset\mathbb{A}^{3}
with
H^{0}(\mathcal{O}_{Z})\cong\mathbb{C}[G]
as
G
-modules.
G
-Hilb was shown to be smooth for abelian
G
by Nakamura
[
22
]
, and subsequently shown to be smooth – and hence a resolution – for all finite
G\subset\operatorname{SL}_{3}(\mathbb{C})
by Bridgeland–King–Reid
[
5
]
.

From the work of King
[
19
]
, Ito–Nakajima
[
16
]
, and Craw
[
8
]
one can reinterpret
G
-Hilb as a moduli space of quiver representations. The quiver in question is the
McKay quiver
with vertices indexed by irreducible representations of
G
and the number of arrows between
\rho
and
\rho^{\prime}
defined to be

\displaystyle\operatorname{dim}\operatorname{Hom}_{G}(\rho^{\prime}\otimes\rho_{\text{std}},\rho),

where
\rho_{\text{std}}
is the standard representation of
G
acting on
\mathbb{C}^{3}
. We choose the dimension vector
\underline{d}=(\operatorname{dim}{\rho})_{\rho}
and a stability parameter
\theta\in\Theta
as defined above. We define
\mathcal{M}_{\theta}:=\mathcal{M}_{\theta}(Q,\underline{d})
to be the fine moduli space of
\theta
-stable representations of the McKay quiver with dimension vector
\underline{d}
subject to certain relations coming from the associated preprojective algebra. When
\theta(\rho)>0
for all nontrivial
\rho
one has that
\mathcal{M}_{\theta}(Q,\underline{d})=G\text{{-Hilb}}\,\mathbb{A}^{3}
.

It was apparent from
[
5
]
that their smoothness result and equivalence of categories (
1.1
) holds for any generic
\theta
and so one obtains potentially many different resolutions of the form
\mathcal{M}_{\theta}(Q,\underline{d})
and also the corresponding equivalences of categories

\displaystyle\Phi_{\theta}\colon\ D^{b}(\mathcal{M}_{\theta})\to D^{b}_{G}\big(\mathbb{A}^{3}\big).

As discussed above, the stability space
\Theta
has a wall-and-chamber structure such that any
\theta
,
\theta^{\prime}
from the same open chamber
\mathfrak{C}\subset\Theta
produce isomorphic moduli spaces:
\mathcal{M}_{\theta}\cong\mathcal{M}_{\theta^{\prime}}
. For simplicity we denote by
\mathcal{M}_{\mathfrak{C}}
and
\Phi_{\mathfrak{C}}
the moduli space and equivalence of categories for any generic
\theta\in\mathfrak{C}
.

When
G
is abelian, each resolution
\mathcal{M}_{\mathfrak{C}}
is toric. Fix the lattice
N=\mathbb{Z}^{3}+\mathbb{Z}\cdot\big(\frac{a}{r},\frac{b}{r},\frac{c}{r}\big)
. The singularity
\mathbb{A}^{3}/G
is described by the cone
\sigma=\operatorname{Cone}(e_{1},e_{2},e_{3})
inside
N_{\mathbb{R}}=N\otimes_{Z}\mathbb{R}=\mathbb{R}^{3}_{\langle x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}\rangle}
and crepant resolutions correspond to triangulations of the slice
\sigma\cap(x_{1}+x_{2}+x_{3}=1)
– usually referred to as the “junior simplex” – such that the vertices of each triangle lies in
N
, and each triangle is smooth (its vertices form a
\mathbb{Z}
-basis of
N
). In pictures we will always draw only the slice to produce two-dimensional pictures.

2.2
Reid’s recipe

Let us focus on the case
\mathcal{M}_{\mathfrak{C}}=G\text{{-Hilb}}\,\mathbb{A}^{3}

G
-cluster by
\mathcal{Z}
and the chamber of
\Theta
corresponding to

-Hilb by

We call the triangulation for

-Hilb the

. It divides the junior simplex into so-called “regular triangles” of equal side length that fall into one of two cases:

,

•

-Hilb. We call line segments on the boundary of a regular triangle

In early versions of the McKay correspondence

An exceptional curve

\displaystyle x^{\alpha}y^{\beta}z^{\gamma}=m_{1}/m_{2},

where

,

,

\mathbb{C}^{3}
for

. Mark the curve

with the character by which
G
acts on

(or
m_{2}
). We define the
\chi
-chain
to be the collection of all exceptional curves (or edges in the Craw–Reid triangulation) marked by the character
\chi
. We say that a triangle in the Craw–Reid triangulation is a
\chi
-triangle
if one of its edges is marked with the character
\chi
.

After marking all curves, there is a procedure for labelling the compact exceptional divisors, or interior vertices of the triangulation. Let
D
be such a divisor corresponding to a vertex

•

is trivalent:
D\cong\mathbb{P}^{2}
and the three exceptional curves in

\chi
.
Mark
D
with
\chi^{\otimes 2}
.

•

4

5
-valent, or
6

\chi^{\prime}
.
Mark

with
\chi\otimes\chi^{\prime}
.

•

v
is
6
-valent and lies in the interior of a regular triangle:
D
is a del Pezzo surface of degree

\chi
,
\chi^{\prime}
,
\chi^{\prime\prime}
.
D

\mathbb{P}^{2}
,
mark

by the two characters arising from the monomials constituting these two maps
. These two characters

2.4
Tautological bundles

The sheaf
\mathcal{R}=\pi_{*}\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{Z}}
is locally free with fibre
H^{0}(\mathcal{O}_{Z})
above
Z\in G\text{{-Hilb}}\,\mathbb{A}^{3}
. It splits into eigensheaves

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}=\bigoplus_{\chi\in\operatorname{Irr}{G}}\mathcal{R}_{\chi}

and these summands are called
tautological bundles
. Since
G
is abelian, the
\mathcal{R}_{\chi}
are line bundles.
[
9
]
gives relations between these line bundles in the Picard group, which translate to divisibility relations between eigenmonomials. For a triangle
\tau
in the Craw–Reid triangulation, denote the generator of
\mathcal{R}_{\chi}
on the affine piece corresponding to
\tau
by
r_{\chi,\tau}
. We usually omit reference to
\tau
so long as the context is clear

Theorem 2.4

(
[
9
, Theorem 6.1]
)
.

The relations between
(
generators of
)
tautological line bundles are described by Reid’s recipe in the following way.

•

If three lines marked with the same character
\chi
meet at a vertex marked with
\psi=\chi^{\otimes 2}
then

\displaystyle r_{\chi}^{2}=r_{\psi}.

•

If four or five or six lines consisting of two pairs marked by characters
\chi
,
\chi^{\prime}
and zero or one or two extra lines marked with further characters meet at a vertex marked with
\psi=\chi\otimes\chi^{\prime}
then

\displaystyle r_{\chi}\cdot r_{\chi^{\prime}}=r_{\psi}.

•

If six lines consisting of three pairs marked by characters
\chi
,
\chi^{\prime}
,
\chi^{\prime\prime}
meet at a vertex marked with
\phi
,
\phi^{\prime}
then

\displaystyle r_{\chi}\cdot r_{\chi^{\prime}}\cdot r_{\chi^{\prime\prime}}=r_{\phi}\cdot r_{\phi^{\prime}}.

The claim is that these relations hold and generate all relations between tautological bundles. We will make heavy use of these divisibility relations between eigenmonomials to study
G
-igsaw pieces for exceptional curves.

As alluded to, the work of Logvinenko
[

]
, Cautis–Logvinenko
[

]
, and Craw–Cautis–Logvinenko
[

]
categorifies Reid’s recipe via the tautological bundles. Many of the constructions in

, Sections 3–4]
resemble constructions made in Section

below, however the computations they make are for the inverse equivalence of (

) to that utilised in
[

]
and here.

Evident from
[

,

]
and below, characters marking a divisor or a single curve are special. They are termed “essential characters” and have been further examined in
[

,

]
.

2.5
Abstract inequalities for
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{C}_{0}}

In
[
10
, Section 9]
Craw–Ishii provide an abstract description of sufficiently many inequalities to carve out the chamber
\mathfrak{C}_{0}
. These inequalities arise from the linearisation map

\displaystyle L_{\mathfrak{C}_{0}}\colon\ \Theta\to\operatorname{Pic}(\mathcal{M}_{\mathfrak{C}_{0}}),

which takes
\theta
to the ample
\mathbb{Q}
-divisor on
\mathcal{M}_{\theta}
arising from GIT; note that we have identified the Picard groups of different resolutions. By construction
L_{\mathfrak{C}_{0}}(\mathfrak{C}_{0})\subset\operatorname{Amp}(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}_{0}})
and so one obtains inequalities

\displaystyle\theta\big(\varphi_{\mathfrak{C}_{0}}(\mathcal{O}_{C})\big)>0

for all exceptional curves
C\subset G
-Hilb. If such an inequality is necessary to define
\mathfrak{C}_{0}
, the geometry of
C
determines the type of the corresponding wall as follows:

•

If
C
is a
(-1,-1)
-curve – that is, it corresponds to an interior edge inside a regular triangle – then
(\theta(\varphi_{\mathfrak{C}_{0}}(\mathcal{O}_{C}))=0)\cap\overline{\mathfrak{C}}_{0}
is a Type
I
wall.

•

If
C
is a
(1,-3)
-curve – that is, it corresponds to one of the edges incident to a trivalent vertex – then
(\theta(\varphi_{\mathfrak{C}_{0}}(\mathcal{O}_{C}))=0)\cap\overline{\mathfrak{C}}_{0}
is a Type
II
wall.

•

If
C
is contained in a Hirzebruch divisor but it is not in either of the previous cases, then
(\theta(\varphi_{\mathfrak{C}_{0}}(\mathcal{O}_{C}))=0)\cap\overline{\mathfrak{C}}_{0}
is a Type
III
wall.

One can express the inequality
\theta(\varphi_{\mathfrak{C}}(\mathcal{O}_{C}))>0
abstractly via
[
10
, Corollary 5.2]
, a consequence of which is

\displaystyle\theta\big(\varphi_{\mathfrak{C}}(\mathcal{O}_{C})\big)=\sum_{\rho}\deg(\mathcal{R}_{\rho}|_{C})\theta(\rho).

Any character
\rho
not in
\text{$G$-{ig}}(C)
has
\mathcal{R}_{\rho}|_{C}=\mathcal{O}_{C}
and so it doesn’t appear in the sum above. It follows that

\displaystyle\theta\big(\varphi_{\mathfrak{C}}(\mathcal{O}_{C})\big)=\sum_{\rho\in G\text{-ig}(C)}\deg(\mathcal{R}_{\rho}|_{C})\theta(\rho).

To complete the description by Craw–Ishii, their remaining inequalities – which if they are necessary inequalities will define walls of Type
0
– are obtained from divisors in two ways outlined in Lemma
2.5
and Proposition
2.6
.

Lemma 2.5

(
[
10
, Corollary 5.6 and Theorem 9.3]
)
.

Suppose that
D\subset G\text{{-Hilb}}\,\mathbb{A}^{3}
is an irreducible exceptional divisor marked with a character
\psi
. Then all
\theta\in\mathfrak{C}_{0}
satisfy the inequality

\displaystyle\theta\big(\varphi_{\mathfrak{C}_{0}}\big(\mathcal{R}_{\psi}^{-1}|_{D}\big)\big)=\theta(\psi)>0.

Moreover, the inequalities of this form are necessary and hence define walls of
\mathfrak{C}_{0}
.

We call the inequalities coming from Lemma
2.5

subsheaf inequalities
. The reason for this is the following. Suppose that
\mathcal{O}_{Z}
is a
G
-cluster parameterised by a point in an irreducible exceptional divisor
D\subset G
-Hilb. Let
\psi
be a character marking
D
. It follows from
[
10
, Corollary 4.6 and Lemma 9.1]
that
r_{\psi}
is in the socle of
H^{0}(\mathcal{O}_{Z})
and is constant on
D
, hence defines a subsheaf
S=\mathcal{O}_{0}\otimes\psi
of
\mathcal{O}_{Z}
for all
Z\in D
. By definition we must have
\theta(S)=\theta(\psi)>0
for all
\theta\in\mathfrak{C}_{0}
and
[
10
, Proposition 9.3]
shows that this is indeed a wall of
\mathfrak{C}_{0}
.

There is a dual version of this using quotients instead of subsheaves.
[
10
, Lemma 5.7 and Theorem 9.5]
shows that

\displaystyle\theta\big(\varphi_{\mathfrak{C}_{0}}(\omega_{D^{\prime}})\big)<0

for all
\theta\in\mathfrak{C}_{0}
and for every possibly reducible but connected exceptional divisor
D^{\prime}
. From
[
10
, Theorem 9.5]
this inequality corresponds to evaluating
\theta
on the minimal rigid quotient
Q
of
\mathcal{O}_{Z}
for all
Z\in D^{\prime}
.

Proposition 2.6
.

Suppose
D^{\prime}\subset G\text{{-Hilb}}\,\mathbb{A}^{3}
is a possibly reducible but connected exceptional divisor. Then all
\theta\in\mathfrak{C}_{0}
satisfy the inequality

\displaystyle\sum_{C\subset D^{\prime}}\sum_{\chi\in\text{$G$-{ig}}(C)}\theta(\chi)>0.

We call the inequalities from Proposition
2.6

quotient inequalities
.

Proof.

For the sheaf
Q
to be trivial on
D^{\prime}
it means that
\mathcal{R}_{\rho}|_{D^{\prime}}
is trivial for all
\rho\subset Q
. Equivalently, all torus-invariant
G
-clusters in
D^{\prime}
share the same eigenmonomial
r_{\rho}
for each
\rho\subset Q
or, also equivalently,
\rho\notin\text{$G$-{ig}}(C)
for any
C\subset D^{\prime}
. Reversing the inequality
\theta(Q)<0
gives
\theta(\mathbb{C}[G]/Q)>0
and
\mathbb{C}[G]/Q
contains exactly the characters in the statement of the result since

is abelian and so all irreducible representations have multiplicity

in
\mathbb{C}[G]
.
∎

The value of Proposition
2.6
is that it reduces computing inequalities from divisors to computing various total
G
-igsaw pieces, which is the topic of the next section.

3.2
Unlocking procedure

In this subsection we outline the algorithm that we use to compute
\text{$G$-{ig}}(C)
. We will spend the remainder of this section proving its validity.

Algorithm 3.3

(unlocking procedure)
.

Input:
An exceptional curve
C\subset G\text{{-Hilb}}\,\mathbb{A}^{3}
marked with a character
\chi
by Reid’s recipe.

Ch

Let
S=\{\chi\}
.

dP

For each del Pezzo divisor
D
along the
\chi
-chain, add
\chi_{\text{dP}}(C,D)
to
S
.

H1

For each Hirzebruch divisor along the
\chi
-chain, add the character marking it to
S
.

Re

For each Hirzebruch divisor
D
downstream of
C
and for each
E\in\mathcal{C}_{C}(D)
, compute
\text{$G$-{ig}}(E)
by running the unlocking procedure with
E
as input.

H2

For each Hirzebruch divisor
D
downstream of
C
, add the characters in
\bigcup_{E\in\mathcal{C}_{C}(D)}\text{$G$-{ig}}(E)
to
S
.

Output:

\text{$G$-{ig}}(C)=S
.

We call this the
unlocking procedure
as passing through a Hirzebruch divisor “unlocks” simpler
G
-igsaw puzzles for the curves
E
downstream of
C
that one recursively solves in the step
Re
and then feeds into the total
G
-igsaw piece for
C
. It can be visualised as a flow through the triangulation emanating from the curve
C
with preferred paths defining its tributaries. We note that the convoluted definition of
\chi_{\text{dP}}(C,D)
is only important for explicit calculations and not for qualitative discussion; the step
dP
states that
\text{$G$-{ig}}(C)
contains exactly one of the characters marking each del Pezzo divisor along the
\chi
-chain. We will often refer to curves downstream of
C
as being “unlocked” by
C
.

As an example use case, if
G
-Hilb has a meeting of champions of side length
0
with the three champions marked with a character
\chi
then for any curve
C
along the
\chi
-chain the characters in the
G
-igsaw piece are given by the unlocking procedure applied to the branch of the
\chi
-chain that
C
lies on, combined with all the characters from (Hirzebruch) divisors along the other two branches of the
\chi
-chain. We will see an example of this in Section
3.8
.

4
Walls of
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{C}_{0}}

In this section we will compute explicit inequalities carving out
\mathfrak{C}_{0}
, and will determine which of these inequalities are necessary and hence define walls of
\mathfrak{C}_{0}
.

4.1
Type
I
walls

We know from
[
10
, Theorem 9.12]
that all flops in a single
(-1,-1)
-curve
C
are achieved by a wall-crossing from
\mathfrak{C}_{0}
. Moreover, we have
\deg(\mathcal{R}_{\rho}|_{C})=1
for all
\rho\in\text{$G$-{ig}}(C)
from
[
10
, Corollary 6.3]
. The unlocking procedure hence gives a combinatorial way of writing down the equations of these walls.

Proposition 4.1
.

Suppose
C\subset G\text{{-Hilb}}\,\mathbb{A}^{3}
is an exceptional
(-1,-1)
-curve marked with character
\chi
by Reid’s recipe. Then, the Type
I
wall corresponding to
C
is given by

\theta(\varphi_{\mathfrak{C}_{0}}(\mathcal{O}_{C}))=\sum_{\chi\in\text{$G$-{ig}}(C)}\theta(\chi)>0,

where
\text{$G$-{ig}}(C)
is computed by the unlocking procedure.

4.2
No Type
II
walls

Proposition 4.2
.

Suppose
C\subset G\text{{-Hilb}}\,\mathbb{A}^{3}
is an exceptional
(1,-3)
-curve marked with character
\chi
by Reid’s recipe. Then, the inequality corresponding to
C
is given by

\theta\big(\varphi_{\mathfrak{C}_{0}}(\mathcal{O}_{C})\big)=2\cdot\theta\big(\chi^{\otimes 2}\big)+\sum_{\chi\in\text{$G$-{ig}}(C)\setminus\{\chi^{\otimes 2}\}}\theta(\chi)>0,

where
\text{$G$-{ig}}(C)
is computed by the unlocking procedure.

Proof.

Notice that such a curve
C
lies inside the exceptional
\mathbb{P}^{2}
in the meeting of champions case when the meeting of champions triangle has side length
0
. Thus the
\mathbb{P}^{2}
is marked with
\chi^{\otimes 2}
and lies in the socle of any torus-invariant
G
-cluster. From Theorem
2.4

r_{\chi^{\otimes 2}}=r_{\chi}^{2}
and so
r_{\chi}^{2}
is the furthest character from
r_{\chi}
in the
G
-igsaw piece in some direction. Note that

\deg(\mathcal{R}_{\rho}|_{C})=\min\big\{k\colon r_{\chi}^{k}\,|\,r_{\rho}\big\}

and so all the characters in
\text{$G$-{ig}}(C)
appear with multiplicity
1
except for
r_{\chi}^{2}
, which appears with multiplicity
2
. This gives the required formula.
∎

As a result we can immediately deduce the conclusion of
[
10
, Proposition 3.8]
for
\mathfrak{C}_{0}
.

Corollary 4.3
.

\mathfrak{C}_{0}
has no Type
II
walls.

Proof.

Suppose
C
is an exceptional
(1,-3)
-curve marked with
\chi
. From the unlocking procedure a total
G
-igsaw piece for
C
consists of
\chi
,
\chi^{2}
, and the characters marking the (Hirzebruch) divisors along the
\chi
-chain. Let
D^{\prime}
be the exceptional
\mathbb{P}^{2}
containing
C
. Consider the inequality for rigid quotients parameterised by
D^{\prime}
: from Proposition
2.6
the characters appearing in this inequality are exactly the characters in the
G
-igsaw pieces of all three
\chi
-curves converging at
D^{\prime}
. These are

\big\{\chi,\chi^{\otimes 2}\big\}\cup\text{{Hirz}}(\chi),

which are exactly the characters appearing in the inequality for
C
. However, the inequality for rigid quotients parameterised by
D^{\prime}
has multiplicities all equal to
1
. When combined with the inequality

4.3
All flops in
\boldsymbol{(-1,-1)}
-curves

Using Proposition
4.1
and the unlocking procedure one can show directly that every
(-1,-1)
-curve produces a necessary inequality, recovering
[

, Theorem 9.12]
by purely combinatorial means.

In order to test redundancy of inequalities we say that an inequality
\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}\theta(\chi_{i})>0
with nonnegative coefficients is a
summand
of another inequality
\sum_{j}\beta_{j}\theta(\rho_{j})>0
with nonnegative coefficients if the difference
\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}\theta(\chi_{i})-\sum_{j}\beta_{j}\theta(\rho_{j})
also has nonnegative coefficients in the basis
\operatorname{Irr}{G}
. If an inequality coming from curves or divisors decomposes into other inequalities as summands, then it is redundant and does not define a wall of
\mathfrak{C}_{0}
.

Proposition 4.4
.

Suppose
C
is an exceptional
(-1,-1)
curve inside
G\text{{-Hilb}}\,\mathbb{A}^{3}
. Then the inequality

is necessary and so defines a wall of
\mathfrak{C}_{0}
.

Proof.

Suppose
C
is marked with
\chi
. From the unlocking procedure we can write the inequality corresponding to

\displaystyle\theta(\chi)+\sum_{i}\theta(\psi_{i})+\theta(\rho_{1})+\sum_{i}\theta\big(\psi_{i}^{1}\big)+\dots+\theta(\rho_{m})+\sum_{i}\theta\big(\psi^{m}_{i}\big)>0,

where
\rho_{j}

unlocked by

and

are the characters in the

-igsaw piece for

C
cannot continue on both sides of the
\chi
-chain, since they meet the
\chi

Lemma 4.15
.

Suppose
\chi

\chi
-chain is a generalised long side. Then, the inequality for each non-final curve

\chi

which is a necessary inequality defining a Type
III

\mathfrak{C}_{0}
.

Proof.

First, the inequality for a final
\chi
-curve
C
features only the Hirzebruch divisors along the
\chi
-chain by the unlocking procedure. It has all nonzero coefficients equal to
1
for the following reason.
\chi^{2}
cannot mark a Hirzebruch divisor along the
\chi
-chain because to do so one would require another chain, say with character
\rho
, to cross the
\chi
-chain and have
\chi\otimes\rho=\chi^{2}
. Of course, this would mean that
\rho=\chi
, but chains do not self-intersect. Hence,
\chi^{2}
does not appear in the
G
-igsaw piece for
C
and so all multiplicities must be equal to
1
by Lemma
4.9
. This is clearly a necessary inequality, as
\chi
is the only character in the inequality coming from a curve and there is no divisor that contains only
\chi
-curves – in contrast to the case of a trivalent vertex.

C

\theta\big(\varphi_{\mathfrak{C}_{0}}(\mathcal{O}_{C})\big)=\theta(\chi)+\sum_{\psi\in\text{{Hirz}}(C)}\alpha_{\psi}\theta(\psi)+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{\rho\in\text{$G$-{ig}}(C_{i})}\beta^{i}_{\rho}\theta(\rho),

where
C_{1},\dots,C_{n}
are the curves unlocked by
C

4.11
, one can express the final term as a sum of inequalities from curves and divisors. The first two terms are equal to

\theta(\chi)+\sum_{\psi\in\text{{Hirz}}(C)}\alpha_{\psi}\theta(\psi)=\theta\big(\varphi_{\mathfrak{C}_{0}}(\mathcal{O}_{C^{\prime}})\big)+\sum_{\psi\in\text{{Hirz}}(\chi)}(\alpha_{\psi}-1)\theta\big(\varphi_{\mathfrak{C}_{0}}\big(\mathcal{R}_{\psi}^{-1}|_{D_{\psi}}\big)\big),

\chi

\psi

and so we have shown that the inequality from

Theorem 4.17
.

Suppose
G\subset\operatorname{SL}_{3}(\mathbb{C})
is a finite abelian subgroup. The walls of the chamber
\mathfrak{C}_{0}
for
G\text{{-Hilb}}\,\mathbb{A}^{3}
and their types are as follows:

•

a Type
I
wall for each exceptional
(-1,-1)
-curve,

•

a Type
III
wall for each generalised long side,

•

a Type
0
wall for each irreducible exceptional divisor,

•

each remaining wall is of Type
0
and comes from a divisor parameterising a rigid quotient.

Moreover, for every contraction of Type
I
or
III
for
G\text{{-Hilb}}\,\mathbb{A}^{3}
there is a wall of the corresponding type that induces the contraction by VGIT.

Proposition
2.6
describes how to recover the unstable locus or the corresponding reducible divisor
D^{\prime}
for each wall of Type
0
from a rigid quotient. Let
\mathfrak{w}
be a wall of
\mathfrak{C}_{0}
. Denote by
E(\mathfrak{w})
the set of edges in the Craw–Reid triangulation corresponding to curves
C
for which all characters in
\text{$G$-{ig}}(C)
appear in the equation of the wall. The desired divisor
D^{\prime}
inducing
\mathfrak{w}
is then the union of the divisors corresponding to vertices for which all incident edges are in
E(\mathfrak{w})
. We observe that the unlocking procedure allows the check of which walls from rigid quotients are necessary to be performed combinatorially.
