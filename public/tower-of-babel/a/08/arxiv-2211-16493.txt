The backward problem for time fractional evolution equations
S. E. Chorfi, L. Maniar, M. Yamamoto

Abstract
. In this paper, we consider the backward problem for fractional in time evolution equations ∂ t α u ⁡ ( t ) = A ​ u ​ ( t ) \partial_{t}^{\alpha}u(t)=Au(t) with the Caputo derivative of order 0 < α ≤ 1 0<\alpha\leq 1 , where A A is a self-adjoint and bounded above operator on a Hilbert space H H . First, we extend the logarithmic convexity technique to the fractional framework by analyzing the properties of the Mittag-Leffler functions. Then we prove conditional stability estimates of Hölder type for initial conditions under a weaker norm of the final data. Finally, we give several applications to show the applicability of our abstract results.

1.
Introduction and main results

Backward problems for evolution equations have many applications in science and engineering. It mainly aims at reconstructing a past profile from the present measured data of a given dynamical system. Such problems are known to be ill-posed in general, and have been extensively investigated for classical evolution equations with derivatives of integer order, see for instance
[
2
,
12
,
20
]
and the references therein.

Recently, backward and inverse problems for time fractional evolution equations of order
0<\alpha<1
have attracted more attention. This class of equations has proved powerful in modeling, e.g., slow and anomalous diffusion phenomena in heterogeneous or porous media
[
1
,
19
]
.

It turned out that fractional backward problems exhibit an essential difference from the classical case
\alpha=1
in terms of their well-posedness and also the techniques used.
Needless to say, the well-posedness or the ill-posedness of the backward problems are concerned with the essence of the diffusion processes under consideration. For example, in the case where the backward problem is well-posed, it is suggested that diffusion profiles are well preserved. On the other hand, the backward problem in the case of
\alpha=1
is severely ill-posed and the equation possesses strong smoothing property in time, and so the recovery of initial value is
extremely difficult by the state at positive time even if time is small.

In spite of the importance, regarding the literature, general results
on the uniqueness and stability for fractional backward problems are
quite missing, although researches grow up rapidly. This is why we consider here an abstract framework that covers and generalizes many examples of applications.

Let
(H,\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)
be a separable Hilbert space and
\|\cdot\|
its associated norm. Let
0<\alpha\leq 1
and
T>0
be a fixed positive time. We are concerned with the following abstract backward problem

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}^{\alpha}u(t)=Au(t),&&\qquad t\in(0,T),\\
&u(T)=u_{T},\end{aligned}

(1)

where
A:D(A)\subset H\rightarrow H
is a densely defined linear operator such that:

Assumption I
:

(i)

A
is self-adjoint,

(ii)

A
is bounded above: there exists
\kappa\geq 0
such that

\langle Au,u\rangle\leq\kappa\|u\|^{2}
for all
u\in D(A)
,

(iii)

A
has compact resolvent: there exists
\lambda>-\kappa

such that the resolvent
R(\lambda,A)=(\lambda I-A)^{-1}
is compact.

The Caputo derivative
\partial_{t}^{\alpha}g
is defined by

\partial_{t}^{\alpha}g(t)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\int^{t}_{0}(t-s)^{-\alpha}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}g(s)\,\mathrm{d}s,&0<\alpha<1,\\
\dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}g(t),&\alpha=1,\end{cases}

(2)

as long as the right-hand side is defined. Here

\Gamma
is the standard Gamma function.

First, we state a main ingredient to prove some stability results for the backward problem (
1
).

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
0<\alpha\leq 1
.
Let
u_{T}\in H
and
u
be the solution to (
1
). Then there exists a constant
K\geq 1
such that

\|u(t)\|\leq K\|u(0)\|^{1-\frac{t}{T}}\|u(T)\|^{\frac{t}{T}},\qquad 0\leq t\leq T.

(3)

Moreover, if
\kappa=0
, then we can choose
K=1
.

We emphasize that the stability estimate is uniform for all

0<\alpha\leq 1
in terms of the logarithmic convexity,
although the behavior of
u
for
0<\alpha<1
near
t=0
is expected to
be very different from the
parabolic case
\alpha=1
.

We note that in the proof, the constant
K
is given in terms of the first eigenvalue of
-A
. The estimate (
3
) is commonly referred to as the logarithmic convexity of the solution to (
1
). The logarithmic convexity method has been extensively investigated for backward parabolic problems, see for instance

[
2
,
3
,
4
,
13
,
20
,
30
]
and the references therein. The method in
[
13
]
relies on the complex variables to prove (
3
). Owing to the rapidly increasing references, we do not create a list of works and we refer to
[
27
]
and
[
28
]
for example.
However, to the best of the authors’ knowledge, Theorem
1.1
is the first logarithmic convexity result for time fractional evolution equations.

Moreover, we consider

Inverse initial data problem.

Let
R>0
be a fixed constant. We denote the set of admissible initial data by

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{R}:=\{u_{0}\in D(A)\colon\|Au_{0}\|,\|u_{0}\|\leq R\}.

(4)

We are concerned with the stable determination of an initial datum
u_{0}\in\mathcal{I}_{R}
, where
u
is the solution to (
1
), from the final datum

u(T)
.

More precisely, we prove conditional stability estimates of Hölder type. Conditional stability in this context means that a priori bound of the initial data is known. We emphasize that such a stability estimate is useful when dealing with numerical reconstruction of initial data, see for instance
[
14
,
30
]
. In practice, the original problem is replaced by an approximated one called the regularized problem. The conditional stability can be used to provide a suitable choice for the regularization parameter and to determine the rate of convergence to an exact solution of the original problem
[
8
]
.

Now we state two kinds of Hölder stability as Theorems
1.2
and
1.3

concerning the backward problem of determining an initial value.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
0<\alpha\leq 1
.
Let
u_{0}\in\mathcal{I}_{R}
and let
u(t)
be the corresponding solution to (
1
). Then there exists a constant
\theta\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
depending on

u_{0}
such that the following estimate holds

\|u_{0}\|\leq\|u(T)\|^{\theta}\sqrt{\|u(T)\|^{2-2\theta}+KR^{2-2\theta}\frac{2T^{\alpha}}{\alpha\Gamma(\alpha)}}.

(5)

Remark 1
.

Surprisingly, Theorem
1.2
is new even for the integer case
\alpha=1
. Usually in this case, only a logarithmic stability is known, see e.g.,
[
12
, p. 51]
and
[
4
, Theorem 2]
.

In Theorem
1.2
, the exponent
\theta=\theta(u_{0})
depends on
u_{0}\in\mathcal{I}_{R}
and we may be able to choose
a sequence
u_{0}^{k}\in\mathcal{I}_{R}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\lim_{k\to\infty}\theta(u_{0}^{k})=0
, which means that
the estimate (
5
) does not make sense in the whole

\mathcal{I}_{R}
.

We can guarantee a uniform lower bound
\theta
if we assume
a lower bound of
\|u_{0}\|
.

Proposition 1
.

Let
0<\alpha\leq 1
.
We assume that
\lambda_{n}>0
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and that
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\lambda_{n}^{2}}<\infty
.
For arbitrarily fixed constants
R>0
and
\delta>0
, we assume that
a set
M\subset\mathcal{I}_{R}
satisfies

M\subset\{u_{0}\in\mathcal{I}_{R}:\,\|u_{0}\|\geq\delta\}

and that the embedding
M\subset H
is compact.
Then there exists a constant
\theta_{0}(\delta)\in(0,\,\frac{1}{2})

depending on
R,\delta
, such that

\|u_{0}\|\leq KR\sqrt{1+\frac{2T^{\alpha}}{\alpha\Gamma(\alpha)}}\|u(T)\|^{\theta_{0}(\delta)}

for all
u_{0}\in M
.

The condition
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\lambda_{n}^{2}}<\infty
is satisfied for
a regular elliptic operator
A
in a bounded domain

\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
,
N=1,2,3
, for example with the zero Dirichlet
boundary condition (see Subsection
4.1
).
In general,
\lim_{\delta\to 0}\theta_{0}=0
.

We can rewrite the proposition as

\|u_{0}\|\leq KR\sqrt{1+\frac{2T^{\alpha}}{\alpha\Gamma(\alpha)}}\|u(T)\|^{\theta_{0}(\delta)}+\delta

for all
u_{0}\in\mathcal{I}_{R}
and all
\delta>0
.
If we know the decay rate of
\theta_{0}(\delta)
as

\delta\to 0
, then by minimizing the right-hand side of the above
inequality with respect to
\delta
, we can reach concrete
conditional stability estimate. However it is difficult to discuss uniformly
in
0<\alpha\leq 1
, as we can understand that the case
\alpha=1

makes solution very smooth for any small
t>0
, but
0<\alpha<1
does not
have such strong smoothing property.

Restricted to
0<\alpha<1
, we can prove conditional
stability of Hölder type with explicit exponent. For the statement, we introduce more general admissible sets of unknown
initial values.
Since the operator
\kappa-A
is non-negative, the fractional powers

(\kappa-A)^{\epsilon}
are well-defined for any
\epsilon>0
. Moreover, since
H
is a Hilbert space, it is known that

D((\kappa-A)^{\epsilon})=[H,D(A)]_{\epsilon},\qquad\text{if }0<\epsilon\leq 1,

where
[\cdot,\cdot]_{\epsilon}
denotes the complex interpolation bracket. We refer to
[
17
]
for more details. Let
\epsilon>0
and
R>0
be two positive constants. We consider the admissible set

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\epsilon,R}:=\{u_{0}\in D((\kappa-A)^{\epsilon})\colon\|u_{0}\|_{D((\kappa-A)^{\epsilon})}\leq R\}.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
0<\alpha<1
, and
let
\beta=\frac{\epsilon}{\epsilon+1}
,
u_{0}\in\mathcal{I}_{\epsilon,R}
and
u(t)
being the corresponding solution to (
1
). Then there exists a constant
C_{2}>0
depending on
\alpha
and
\lambda_{m+1}
such that

\|u(0)\|\leq\left(1+C_{2}^{\beta}R^{1-\beta}\right)\|u(T)\|^{\beta}.

(6)

Comparing to the existing literature, Sakamoto and Yamamoto
[
24
]
were the first to prove the well-posedness of a backward problem like (
1
) with smooth data
u_{T}
. More precisely, they considered the case when
H=L^{2}(\Omega)
, with
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
is a smooth bounded domain, and
A
is a symmetric uniformly elliptic operator with zero Dirichlet boundary condition. Namely, they proved that there exists a constant
C>0
depending on
T
such that for all
u_{T}\in H^{2}(\Omega)\cap H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
the corresponding solution exists and satisfies

\|u(0,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C\|u(T,\cdot)\|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}.

(7)

This is an unconditional Lipschitz stability, that is,
a strong stability result, but under a stronger norm of the data
\|u(T,\cdot)\|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}
. In this particular case, although the Hölder stability
(
6
)
is a slightly weaker
estimate, (
6
) establishes an estimate in terms of the same norm

\|u(0,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
and
\|u(T,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
.
This is crucial from a practical perspective, since only a measurement
u_{T}^{\delta}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
of
u_{T}
is accessible, where
\delta>0
indicates the noise level. Note that
u_{T}^{\delta}
is usually non-smooth due to the random noise. It should be pointed out that (
7
) has been recently extended to the non-symmetric operator case in
[
10
]
.

We further note that by the interpolation inequality of the Sobolev norms,
for
\epsilon>1
, we can derive the conclusion of Theorem
1.3

directly from (
7
).

The rest of the article is organized as follows: in Section
2
, we start by recalling some preliminaries. Then we prove some key properties of the Mittag-Leffler function that will be used later. In Section
3
, we prove the logarithmic convexity estimate stated in Theorem
1.1
. Next, we apply the logarithmic convexity to establish Theorem
1.2
and then we prove Theorem
1.3
on the Hölder stability for the inverse initial data problem. At this level, some further results are developed and the reconstruction of the solution at past times from noisy data is briefly discussed. Finally, in Section
4
, we illustrate the abstract results by presenting several examples of time fractional systems.

2.
Preliminary results

Let us start by recalling some definitions and properties needed later.

A function
f:[0,\infty)\rightarrow(0,\infty)
is log-convex if
\log f(t)
is convex, i.e., for all
t,s\geq 0\text{ and }0<\theta<1
,

f((1-\theta)t+\theta s)\leq f(t)^{1-\theta}f(s)^{\theta}.

(8)

If
f(t)
is
C^{2}[0,\infty)
, then it is log-convex on
[0,\infty)
if and only if the differential inequality

f(t)f^{\prime\prime}(t)-(f^{\prime}(t))^{2}\geq 0

(9)

holds for all
t\geq 0
. It is known that the sum of two log-convex functions is also log-convex, see e.g.,
[
18
, p. 22]
.

A function
f:[0,\infty)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is called completely monotone if
f
has derivatives of all orders and satisfies

(-1)^{k}f^{(k)}(t)\geq 0\qquad\text{ for all }t>0,\;k=0,1,2,\ldots

The function
f(t)
is completely monotone if and only if

f(t)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-st}\,\mathrm{d}\rho(s),\qquad t>0,

(10)

where
\rho(s)
is non-decreasing and the integral converges. We refer to
[
29
, Theorem 12b, p. 161]
. Note that the complete monotonicity is closed under addition, multiplication and pointwise convergence, see
[
25
, Corollary 1.6]
.

We will show the following key lemma (e.g., Theorem 1.3.4 (p. 21) in

[
18
]
).

Lemma 2.1
.

Any completely monotone function
f:[0,\infty)\rightarrow(0,\infty)
is log-convex.

Proof.

By (
10
), we see that
\displaystyle f^{\prime}(t)=-\int_{0}^{\infty}s\mathrm{e}^{-st}\,\mathrm{d}\rho(s)
and
\displaystyle f^{\prime\prime}(t)=\int_{0}^{\infty}s^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-st}\,\mathrm{d}\rho(s)
. Then the quadratic polynomial
\displaystyle f(t)x^{2}+2f^{\prime}(t)x+f^{\prime\prime}(t)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-st}(x-s)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\rho(s)
is non-negative for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
. Therefore, it has a non-positive discriminant. Thus, (
9
) is satisfied. This completes the proof.
∎

Let
\alpha,\beta>0
. We denote by
E_{\alpha,\beta}(z)
the Mittag-Leffler function with two parameters

E_{\alpha,\beta}(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{z^{k}}{\Gamma(\alpha k+\beta)},\qquad z\in\mathbb{C}.

The function
E_{\alpha,\beta}(z)
is an entire function in
\mathbb{C}
. Next, we collect some useful properties of
E_{\alpha,1}
. We refer to
[
22
]
for more details. Let
\alpha>0
and
\lambda\geq 0
. Then

•

\dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}E_{\alpha,1}(t)=\frac{1}{\alpha}E_{\alpha,\alpha}(t)
;

•

\dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}E_{\alpha,1}(-\lambda t^{\alpha})=-\lambda t^{\alpha-1}E_{\alpha,\alpha}(-\lambda t^{\alpha})
;

•

\partial_{t}^{\alpha}E_{\alpha,1}(-\lambda t^{\alpha})=-\lambda E_{\alpha,1}(-\lambda t^{\alpha})
;

•

\lim\limits_{t\to-\infty}E_{\alpha,1}(t)=0
.

The next lemma will be a key to prove the main result.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
0<\alpha\leq\beta\leq 1
and
\lambda\geq 0
. Then the functions
t\mapsto E_{\alpha,1}(-\lambda t^{\alpha})
and
t\mapsto t^{\beta-1}E_{\alpha,\beta}(-\lambda t^{\alpha})
are log-convex on
[0,\infty)
.

Proof.

It is known that
t\mapsto E_{\alpha,1}(-\lambda t^{\alpha})
and
t\mapsto t^{\beta-1}E_{\alpha,\beta}(-\lambda t^{\alpha})
are completely monotone on
[0,\infty)
, see, e.g.,
[
11
, p. 268-269]
. Since they are not identically zero, they are positive on
[0,\infty)
. By Lemma
2.1
, these functions are log-convex on
[0,\infty)
.
∎

Remark 2
.

Let
0<\alpha\leq 1
. The function
t\mapsto E_{\alpha,1}(-\lambda t^{\alpha})
is not completely monotone for
\lambda<0
.

Remark 3
.

Let
0<\alpha\leq 1
and
\beta\geq\alpha
. The function
t\mapsto E_{\alpha,\beta}(-t)
is completely monotone on
[0,\infty)
, see
[
23
]
and
[
26
]
. By the same argument as above we deduce that
t\mapsto E_{\alpha,\beta}(-t)
is log-convex on
[0,\infty)
.

3.
Logarithmic convexity and its consequences

In this section, we discuss the ill-posedness of the backward problem (
1
). Then we prove Theorem
1.1
and Theorem
1.2
. We start by recalling some useful facts. From Assumption I, it follows that the operator
A
is closed and
(D(A),\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{D(A)})
is a Hilbert space where

\langle x,y\rangle_{D(A)}=\langle x,y\rangle+\langle Ax,Ay\rangle,\quad\forall x,y\in D(A).

Note that the operator
A
generates a
C_{0}
-semigroup
(\mathrm{e}^{tA})_{t\geq 0}
which is analytic of angle
\frac{\pi}{2}
on
H
, see
[
9
, p. 106]
. Moreover, there exists an orthonormal basis
\left\{\varphi_{n}\right\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
H
of eigenvectors of
-A
such that
\varphi_{n}\in D(A),\left\|\varphi_{n}\right\|=1
and
-A\varphi_{n}=\lambda_{n}\varphi_{n},\forall n\in\mathbb{N}
, where
\left\{\lambda_{n}\right\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathbb{R}
denote the corresponding eigenvalues which can be ordered so that

-\kappa\leq\lambda_{1}\leq\cdots\leq\lambda_{m}\leq 0<\lambda_{m+1}\leq\cdots\,\rightarrow\infty.

3.1.
Ill-posedness of the backward problem

Let us consider the forward problem

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}^{\alpha}u(t)=Au(t),&&\qquad t\in(0,T),\\
&u(0)=u_{0}.\end{aligned}

(11)

Following
[
24
]
, we can obtain the following well-posedness result by the Fourier method.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
0<\alpha\leq 1
and
u_{0}\in D(A)
. Then there exists a unique solution
u\in C\left([0,T];D(A)\right)
to (
11
) such that
\partial_{t}^{\alpha}u\in C\left([0,T];H\right)
. Moreover, the solution is given by

u(t)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\langle u_{0},\varphi_{n}\rangle E_{\alpha,1}\left(-\lambda_{n}t^{\alpha}\right)\varphi_{n}.

(12)

From (
12
), we see that

u(T)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\langle u_{0},\varphi_{n}\rangle E_{\alpha,1}\left(-\lambda_{n}T^{\alpha}\right)\varphi_{n}.

Hence, for all
n\geq 1
, we have

\langle u_{0},\varphi_{n}\rangle E_{\alpha,1}\left(-\lambda_{n}T^{\alpha}\right)=\langle u(T),\varphi_{n}\rangle.

This shows that a solution
u
to (
1
) for
u_{T}\in H
does not exist in general, and when it exists, it is unstable for non smooth data. For instance, if we choose
u_{0}=\frac{1}{E_{\alpha,1}\left(-\lambda_{n}T^{\alpha}\right)}\varphi_{n}
, then
\|u_{0}\|=\frac{1}{E_{\alpha,1}\left(-\lambda_{n}T^{\alpha}\right)}\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
, whereas
\|u_{T}\|=1
. Thus, an estimate of the form
\|u_{0}\|\leq C_{1}\|u_{T}\|
does not hold in general. Note that the well-posedness of the fractional backward problem (
0<\alpha<1
) holds for diffusion equations if we smooth the data
u_{T}
as in (
7
). This is considerably different from the classical parabolic case
\alpha=1
where the problem is still unstable, even if
we adopt any Sobolev norms
\|u_{T}\|_{H^{m}(\Omega)}
with
arbitrary
m>0
.

3.2.
Proof of logarithmic convexity

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

We shall use the results of Section
2
. By the representation formula (
12
), we obtain that

\displaystyle u(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{m}\langle u_{0},\varphi_{n}\rangle E_{\alpha,1}\left(-\lambda_{n}t^{\alpha}\right)\varphi_{n}+\sum_{n=m+1}^{\infty}\langle u_{0},\varphi_{n}\rangle E_{\alpha,1}\left(-\lambda_{n}t^{\alpha}\right)\varphi_{n}

\displaystyle=:u_{1}(t)+u_{2}(t).

(13)

Observe that
\|u(t)\|^{2}=\|u_{1}(t)\|^{2}+\|u_{2}(t)\|^{2}
. First, we have
\lambda_{n}>0
for
n\geq m+1
and

\|u_{2}(t)\|^{2}=\sum_{n=m+1}^{\infty}\langle u_{0},\varphi_{n}\rangle^{2}\left(E_{\alpha,1}(-\lambda_{n}t^{\alpha})\right)^{2}.

In Lemma
2.2
, we have seen that the functions
t\mapsto E_{\alpha,1}(-\lambda_{n}t^{\alpha})
are completely on
[0,T]
for
\lambda_{n}\geq 0
. By
[
25
, Corollary 1.6]
, it follows that the functions
t\mapsto\left(E_{\alpha,1}(-\lambda_{n}t^{\alpha})\right)^{2}
and then
t\mapsto\|u_{2}(t)\|^{2}
are completely monotone on
[0,T]
. Thus, Lemma
2.1
implies that the function
t\mapsto\|u_{2}(t)\|^{2}
is log-convex on
[0,T]
. Therefore

\displaystyle\|u_{2}(t)\|

\displaystyle\leq\|u_{2}(0)\|^{1-\frac{t}{T}}\|u_{2}(T)\|^{\frac{t}{T}}

\displaystyle\leq\|u(0)\|^{1-\frac{t}{T}}\|u(T)\|^{\frac{t}{T}}.

(14)

On the other hand,
\displaystyle\|u_{1}(t)\|^{2}=\sum_{n=1}^{m}\langle u_{0},\varphi_{n}\rangle^{2}\left(E_{\alpha,1}(-\lambda_{n}t^{\alpha})\right)^{2}
.
It holds that the function
\lambda\mapsto\left(E_{\alpha,1}(-\lambda T^{\alpha})\right)^{2}
is non-increasing for
\lambda\leq 0
. By
\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{n}
for
n\leq m
, we have

\displaystyle\|u_{1}(T)\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\max_{1\leq n\leq m}|E_{\alpha,1}(-\lambda_{n}T^{\alpha})|^{2}\sum_{n=1}^{m}\langle u_{0},\varphi_{n}\rangle^{2}

\displaystyle=|E_{\alpha,1}(-\lambda_{1}T^{\alpha})|^{2}\|u_{1}(0)\|^{2}

\displaystyle=:K_{1}^{2}\|u_{1}(0)\|^{2}.

Since
\lambda_{n}\leq 0
for
n\leq m
, the function
t\mapsto\left(E_{\alpha,1}(-\lambda_{n}t^{\alpha})\right)^{2}
is non-decreasing for
n\leq m
. Hence, for
0\leq t\leq T
,

\displaystyle\|u_{1}(t)\|

\displaystyle\leq\|u_{1}(T)\|\leq K_{1}\|u(0)\|,

where the constant satisfies
K_{1}\geq 1
. Thus

\displaystyle\|u_{1}(t)\|

\displaystyle\leq K_{1}\|u(0)\|^{1-\frac{t}{T}}\|u_{1}(T)\|^{\frac{t}{T}}

\displaystyle\leq K_{1}\|u(0)\|^{1-\frac{t}{T}}\|u(T)\|^{\frac{t}{T}}.

(15)

From (
14
) and (
15
), setting
K:=K_{1}+1
, we obtain

\|u(t)\|\leq K\|u(0)\|^{1-\frac{t}{T}}\|u(T)\|^{\frac{t}{T}},\qquad 0\leq t\leq T.

∎

As a direct corollary of Theorem
1.1
, we obtain the following backward uniqueness result.

Corollary 1
.

Let
u_{T}\in H
. If the solution of (
1
) satisfies
u(T)=0
, then
u(0)=0
and hence
u(t)=0
for all
t\in[0,T]
.

Consequently, the fractional backward system (
1
) has at most one solution.

3.3.
Proof of Hölder stability

In order to prove Theorem
1.2
on the Hölder stability,
we need the following lemma.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
0<\alpha\leq 1
. Let
u_{0}\in D(A)
and
u
be the corresponding solution to (
1
). Then for all
t\in(0,T)
, we have

-\partial_{t}^{\alpha}\,\|u(t)\|^{2}\leq 2\|u(t)\|\|Au_{0}\|.

(16)

Proof.

We borrow the notations from the proof of Theorem
1.1
. By the formula (
13
), we see that

-\partial_{t}^{\alpha}\,\|u(t)\|^{2}=-\partial_{t}^{\alpha}\,\|u_{1}(t)\|^{2}-\partial_{t}^{\alpha}\,\|u_{2}(t)\|^{2}.

(17)

First, we have

-\partial_{t}^{\alpha}\,\|u_{1}(t)\|^{2}=-\sum_{n=1}^{m}\langle u_{0},\varphi_{n}\rangle^{2}\,\partial_{t}^{\alpha}\left[\left(E_{\alpha,1}(-\lambda_{n}t^{\alpha})\right)^{2}\right].

\displaystyle\|u(0)\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{m}\langle u_{0},\varphi_{n}\rangle^{2}+\sum_{n=m+1}^{\infty}\langle u_{0},\varphi_{n}\rangle^{2}

\displaystyle=:\|u_{1}(0)\|^{2}+\|u_{2}(0)\|^{2}.

\lambda_{n}\leq 0
for

, the function

. Then

3.4.
Backward problems from noisy data

The purpose of this part is to reconstruct the solution
u(t)
for
0<t<T
to the following problem

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}^{\alpha}u(t)=Au(t)+f(t),&&\qquad t\in(0,T),\\
&u(T)=u_{T},\end{aligned}

(23)

from a noisy measurement
u_{T}^{\delta}
of the exact final datum
u(T)
, i.e.,

\|u(T)-u_{T}^{\delta}\|\leq\delta,

where
\delta>0
is some known measurement error. Here the source term
f
is assumed to be known. Then an approximate solution to the original problem is given by

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}^{\alpha}u^{\delta}(t)=Au^{\delta}(t)+f(t),&&t\in(0,T),\\
&u^{\delta}(T)=u_{T}^{\delta}.\end{aligned}

(24)

Let
R>0
be a known a priori bound. Let us set the following class of solutions

\mathcal{M}=\left\{u\in C([0,T];H)\colon\|u(0)\|\leq R\right\}.

(25)

Restricted to the class
\mathcal{M}
, the logarithmic convexity estimate (
3
) yields a Hölder continuous dependence of the solution
u(t)
,
0<t<T
, with respect to the data. More precisely, we have the following result.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
u
and
u^{\delta}
be the solutions
to (
23
) and (
24
) respectively such that
u,u^{\delta}\in\mathcal{M}
. Then

\|u(t)-u^{\delta}(t)\|\leq 2KR^{1-\frac{t}{T}}\delta^{\frac{t}{T}},\qquad 0\leq t\leq T.

(26)

Proof.

If
v(t)=u(t)-u^{\delta}(t)
, then
v
is a solution to (
1
) corresponding to the final datum
v(T)=u(T)-u_{T}^{\delta}
and the initial datum
v(0)=u_{0}-u_{0}^{\delta}
. Since
\|v(0)\|\leq 2R
, the estimate (
3
) applied to
v
yields the desired result.
∎

As a widely used numerical method for reconstructing
an initial value
u_{0}
from noisy data at
t=T
, we can refer to
the Tikhonov regularization, where one important issue is choices of
the regularizing parameters according to given noise level

\delta>0
. The conditional stability Theorem
1.3
provides
a choice principle for quasi-optimal convergence of the scheme, and
as for the details, see Cheng and Yamamoto
[
8
]
.

4.1.
Fractional uniformly elliptic equations

Henceforth,
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
is a bounded domain with boundary
\partial\Omega
of class
C^{2}
. We consider the backward problem with Dirichlet or Neumann boundary conditions:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}^{\alpha}u(t,x)=Au(t,x),&&\qquad\text{ in }(0,T)\times\Omega,\\
&\begin{cases}u\rvert_{\partial\Omega}=0,&\quad\text{ on }(0,T)\times\partial\Omega\;\text{ (Dirichlet case) or}\\
\partial_{\nu}^{A}u=0,&\quad\text{ on }(0,T)\times\partial\Omega\;\,\text{ (Neumann case)},\end{cases}\\
&u(T,x)=u_{T}(x)&&\qquad\text{ in }\Omega,\end{aligned}

(27)

where the state space is
H=L^{2}(\Omega)
, and
A
is a symmetric uniformly elliptic operator, i.e.,

Au\,(x):=\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\partial_{i}(a_{ij}(x)\partial_{j}u)(x)+p(x)u,

(28)

such that

a_{ij}=a_{ji}\in C^{1}(\overline{\Omega}),\quad 1\leq i,j\leq N,\qquad p\in L^{\infty}(\Omega),

and there exists a constant
\kappa>0
such that

\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}a_{ij}(x)\xi_{i}\xi_{j}\geq\kappa\sum_{j=1}^{N}\xi_{j}^{2},\quad x\in\overline{\Omega},\;(\xi_{1},...,\xi_{N})\in\mathbb{R}^{N}.

The unit outwards normal vector to
\partial\Omega
is denoted by
\nu
and the conormal derivative with respect to
A
by

\partial_{\nu}^{A}u=\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}a_{ij}\nu_{j}(\partial_{i}u)_{|\partial\Omega}.

The domain of the operator
A
is given by

D(A)=\begin{cases}H^{2}(\Omega)\cap H^{1}_{0}(\Omega),&(\text{Dirichlet case) or}\\
\left\{u\in H^{2}(\Omega)\colon\partial_{\nu}^{A}u=0\right\}&(\text{Neumann case}).\end{cases}

(29)

It is known that the operator
A
satisfies Assumption I. Indeed, it is self-adjoint and bounded above thanks to Green’s formula. Since the embedding
D(A)\hookrightarrow L^{2}(\Omega)
is compact,
A
has compact resolvent, see
[
9
, Proposition 4.25]
.
Then Theorem
1.2
yields

Corollary 2
.

Let
u_{0}\in\mathcal{I}_{R}
and let
u(t,x)
be the corresponding solution to (
27
). Then there exists a constant
\theta\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
such that

\|u(0,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq KR^{1-\theta}\sqrt{1+\frac{2\,T^{\alpha}}{\alpha\Gamma(\alpha)}}\|u(T,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{\theta}.

We can apply Theorem
1.3
, but we omit the statements of stability estimates
which Theorem
1.3
brings in this and the succeeding cases.

4.2.
Fractional advection-diffusion equation

Let
l>0
,
b\in\mathbb{R}
and
d>0
. We consider the following one-dimensional problem

\begin{cases}\partial^{\alpha}_{t}u(t,x)=du_{xx}(t,x)-bu_{x}(t,x),&(t,x)\in(0,T)\times(0,l),\\
u(t,0)=u(t,l)=0,&t\in(0,T),\\
u(T,x)=u_{T}(x),&x\in(0,l).\end{cases}

(30)

Although the equation (
30
) is not symmetric, it can be reduced to the form (
27
). Let us make the change of variables
\xi=\frac{x}{\sqrt{d}}
and
u(t,x)=v(t,\xi)\,\mathrm{e}^{\frac{b\xi}{2\sqrt{d}}}
. By a simple calculation, (
30
) can be transformed to the following equation

\begin{cases}\partial^{\alpha}_{t}v(t,\xi)=v_{\xi\xi}(t,\xi)+pv(t,\xi),&(t,\xi)\in(0,T)\times(0,\ell),\\
v(t,0)=v(t,\ell)=0,&t\in(0,T),\\
v(T,\xi)=u_{T}(\xi\sqrt{d})\,\mathrm{e}^{\frac{-b\xi}{2\sqrt{d}}},&x\in(0,\ell),\end{cases}

(31)

where
p=-\frac{b^{2}}{4d}
and
\ell=\frac{l}{\sqrt{d}}
.
Here we note
v(0,\xi)=u(0,\xi\sqrt{d})\,\mathrm{e}^{\frac{-b\xi}{2\sqrt{d}}}
.
From Corollary
2
applied to (
31
), we obtain

Corollary 3
.

Let
u_{0}\in\mathcal{I}_{R}
and let
u(t,x)
be the corresponding solution to (
30
). Then there exist
\theta\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
and a positive constant
C=C(b,l,d,\theta)
such that

\|u(0,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(0,l)}\leq CKR^{1-\theta}\sqrt{1+\frac{2\,T^{\alpha}}{\alpha\Gamma(\alpha)}}\|u(T,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(0,l)}^{\theta}.

4.3.
Space-time fractional diffusion equations

Let
0<s<1
. We consider the backward problem

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}^{\alpha}u(t,x)=-(-A_{0})^{s}u(t,x),&&\qquad\text{ in }(0,T)\times\Omega,\\
&u\rvert_{\partial\Omega}=0,&&\qquad\text{ on }(0,T)\times\partial\Omega,\\
&u(T,x)=u_{T}(x)&&\qquad\text{ in }\Omega,\end{aligned}

(32)

where (as in (
28
))
A_{0}
is a symmetric uniformly elliptic operator given by

A_{0}u\,(x):=\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\partial_{i}(a_{ij}(x)\partial_{j}u)(x),\qquad D(A_{0})=H^{2}(\Omega)\cap H^{1}_{0}(\Omega).

The spectrum
\sigma(-A_{0})
consists of eigenvalues with finite multiplicities so that we can write

0<\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}\leq\lambda_{3}<\cdots\rightarrow\infty.

Let
\left\{\varphi_{n}\right\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be an orthonormal basis of associated eigenfunctions in
L^{2}(\Omega)
. Then the spectral fractional power
(-A_{0})^{s}
is defined by

(-A_{0})^{s}u=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{n}^{s}\langle u,\varphi_{n}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\varphi_{n},

with domain

D((-A_{0})^{s})=\left\{u\in L^{2}(\Omega)\colon\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{n}^{2s}\langle u,\varphi_{n}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}<\infty\right\},

endowed with the norm
\|u\|_{D((-A_{0})^{s})}=\|(-A_{0})^{s}u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
for all
u\in D((-A_{0})^{s})
. From the definition, it can be proved that the operator
-(-A_{0})^{s}
satisfies Assumption I. Hence, we have the following estimate.

Corollary 4
.

Let
u_{0}\in\mathcal{I}_{R}
and let
u(t,x)
be the corresponding solution to (
32
). Then there exists a constant
\theta\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
such that

\|u(0,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq KR^{1-\theta}\sqrt{1+\frac{2\,T^{\alpha}}{\alpha\Gamma(\alpha)}}\|u(T,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{\theta}.

4.4.
Fractional coupled diffusion systems

We consider the coupled system of diffusion equations with Dirichlet or Neumann boundary conditions:

\displaystyle{\small\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}^{\alpha}u_{1}(t,x)=A_{0}^{1}u_{1}(t,x)+\sum_{j=1}^{N}c_{1j}(x)u_{j}(t,x),&&\text{in }(0,T)\times\Omega,\\
&\partial_{t}^{\alpha}u_{2}(t,x)=A_{0}^{2}u_{2}(t,x)+\sum_{j=1}^{N}c_{2j}(x)u_{j}(t,x),&&\text{in }(0,T)\times\Omega,\\
&\cdots\\
&\partial_{t}^{\alpha}u_{N}(t,x)=A_{0}^{N}u_{N}(t,x)+\sum_{j=1}^{N}c_{Nj}(x)u_{j}(t,x),&&\text{in }(0,T)\times\Omega,\\
&\begin{cases}u_{i}\rvert_{\partial\Omega}=0,&\text{on }(0,T)\times\partial\Omega\;\text{ (Dirichlet case)},\hskip 2.84544pt1\leq i\leq N,\text{ or}\\
\partial_{\nu}^{A_{0}^{i}}u_{i}=0,&\text{on }(0,T)\times\partial\Omega\;\,\text{ (Neumann case)},\;\,1\leq i\leq N,\end{cases}\\
&u_{i}(T,x)=u_{T}^{i}(x),\hskip 45.52458pt1\leq i\leq N,&&\text{in }\Omega,\end{aligned}}

(33)

where, for every
1\leq k\leq N
,
A_{0}^{k}
is a symmetric uniformly elliptic operator,

A_{0}^{k}u:=\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\partial_{i}(a_{ij}^{k}(x)\partial_{j}u),

such that

a_{ij}^{k}=a_{ji}^{k}\in C^{1}(\overline{\Omega}),\quad 1\leq i,j\leq N,\qquad c_{ij}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega),\;c_{ij}(\cdot)=c_{ji}(\cdot),

and there exists a constant constant
\kappa_{k}>0
such that

\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}a_{ij}^{k}(x)\xi_{i}\xi_{j}\geq\kappa_{k}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\xi_{j}^{2},\quad x\in\overline{\Omega},\;(\xi_{1},...,\xi_{N})\in\mathbb{R}^{N}.

Moreover, the domain of each operator
A_{0}^{k}
is given by (
29
). The system (
33
) can be written as

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}^{\alpha}\mathbf{u}(t)=\mathcal{A}_{0}\mathbf{u}(t)+\mathbf{C}\mathbf{u}(t),&&\qquad t\in(0,T),\\
&\mathbf{u}(T)=\mathbf{u}_{T},\end{aligned}

where the state space is
H=L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{N})
,

\mathbf{u}_{T}=(u_{T}^{i})_{1\leq i\leq N}
,
\mathbf{u}=(u_{i})_{1\leq i\leq N}
,
\mathbf{C}(\cdot)=(c_{ij}(\cdot))_{1\leq i,j\leq N}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{R}^{N}))
and
\mathcal{A}_{0}=\mathrm{diag}(A_{0}^{1},\cdots,A_{0}^{N})
.

We can directly prove that the operator
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}_{0}+\mathbf{C}
satisfies Assumption I. Consequently, we have

Corollary 5
.

Let
\mathbf{u}_{0}\in\mathcal{I}_{R}
and let
\mathbf{u}(t)
be the corresponding solution to (
33
). Then there exists a constant
\theta\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
such that

\|\mathbf{u}(0)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{N})}\leq KR^{1-\theta}\sqrt{1+\frac{2\,T^{\alpha}}{\alpha\Gamma(\alpha)}}\|\mathbf{u}(T)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{N})}^{\theta}.

4.5.
Fractional degenerate equations

Let
0\leq\beta<2
. We consider the fractional degenerate problem

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}^{\alpha}u=\left(x^{\beta}u_{x}\right)_{x},&(t,x)\in(0,T)\times(0,1),\\
u(t,1)=0,\qquad\begin{cases}u(t,0)=0,&\text{ if }\beta\in[0,1),\\
\left(x^{\beta}u_{x}\right)(t,0)=0,&\text{ if }\beta\in[1,2),\end{cases}\\
u(T,x)=u_{T}(x)&x\in(0,1).\end{cases}

(34)

The state space is
H=L^{2}(0,1)
and the governing operator is given by

Au=\left(x^{\beta}u_{x}\right)_{x},

with domain:

If
\beta\in[0,1)
(weakly degenerate), then

D(A)=\left\{u\in H_{\beta,0}^{1}(0,1):x^{\beta}u_{x}\in H^{1}(0,1)\right\},

where

H_{\beta,0}^{1}(0,1)=\left\{u\in L^{2}(0,1):u\in AC([0,1]),x^{\frac{\beta}{2}}u_{x}\in L^{2}(0,1),u(0)=0,u(1)=0\right\}.

If
\beta\in[1,2)
(strongly degenerate), then

D(A)=\left\{u\in H_{\beta,0}^{1}(0,1):x^{\beta}u_{x}\in H^{1}(0,1)\right\},

where we changed the definition to

H_{\beta,0}^{1}(0,1)=\left\{u\in L^{2}(0,1):u\in AC((0,1]),x^{\frac{\beta}{2}}u_{x}\in L^{2}(0,1),u(1)=0\text{ and }x^{\beta}u_{x}\in H^{1}(0,1)\right\}.

AC(I)
denotes the space of absolutely continuous functions on the interval
I
.

In both cases, the operator
A
satisfies Assumption I, see, e.g.,
[
6
,
7
]
for (i)-(ii) and
[
5
, Appendix]
for (iii). Consequently, we infer the following corollary.

Corollary 6
.

Let
u_{0}\in\mathcal{I}_{R}
and let
u(t,x)
be the corresponding solution to (
34
). Then there exists a constant
\theta\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
such that

\|u(0,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(0,1)}\leq KR^{1-\theta}\sqrt{1+\frac{2\,T^{\alpha}}{\alpha\Gamma(\alpha)}}\|u(T,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(0,1)}^{\theta}.

4.6.
Fractional dynamic boundary conditions

Let us set
\Gamma:=\partial\Omega
. We consider the backward parabolic system with dynamic boundary conditions

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}^{\alpha}u(t,x)=\Delta u(t,x),&&\qquad\text{ in }(0,T)\times\Omega,\\
&\partial_{t}^{\alpha}u_{\Gamma}(t,x)=\Delta_{\Gamma}u_{\Gamma}(t,x)-\partial_{\nu}u(t,x),&&\qquad\text{ on }(0,T)\times\Gamma,\\
&u_{\Gamma}(t,x)=u_{|\Gamma}(t,x),&&\qquad\text{ on }(0,T)\times\Gamma,\\
&\left(u(T,\cdot),u_{\Gamma}(T,\cdot)\right)=\left(u_{T},u_{T,\Gamma}\right),&&\qquad\text{ on }\Omega\times\Gamma,\end{aligned}

(35)

where
\Delta_{\Gamma}
is the the Laplace-Beltrami operator on
\Gamma
. The system (
35
) can be written in the state space

\mathbb{L}^{2}:=L^{2}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Gamma)
as:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\hskip-5.69046pt\partial_{t}^{\alpha}\mathbf{U}(t)=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{U}(t),\qquad t\in(0,T),\\
\hskip-5.69046pt\mathbf{U}(T)=\mathbf{U}_{T},\end{array}\right.

where
\mathbf{U}:=\left(u,u_{\Gamma}\right)
,
\mathbf{U}_{T}:=\left(u_{T},u_{T,\Gamma}\right)
, and the linear operator
\mathbf{A}:D(\mathbf{A})\subset\mathbb{L}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{L}^{2}
is given by

\mathbf{A}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\Delta&0\\
-\partial_{\nu}&\Delta_{\Gamma}\end{array}\right),\qquad D(\mathbf{A})=\mathbb{H}^{2}:=\{(u,u_{\Gamma})\in H^{2}(\Omega)\times H^{2}(\Gamma):u_{|\Gamma}=u_{\Gamma}\}.

In
[
16
]
, it has been proven that
\mathbf{A}
is self-adjoint and dissipative. Since
\mathbb{H}^{2}\hookrightarrow H^{2}(\Omega)\times H^{2}(\Gamma)
is continuous, then
\mathbb{H}^{2}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{L}^{2}
is compact. By
[
9
, Proposition 4.25]
, we deduce that
\mathbf{A}
has compact resolvent. Therefore, the operator
\mathbf{A}
satisfies Assumption I.

Corollary 7
.

Let
\mathbf{U}_{0}\in\mathcal{I}_{R}
and let
\mathbf{U}(t)
be the corresponding solution to (
35
). Then there exists a constant
\theta\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
such that

\|\mathbf{U}(0)\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}\leq KR^{1-\theta}\sqrt{1+\frac{2\,T^{\alpha}}{\alpha\Gamma(\alpha)}}\|\mathbf{U}(T)\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{\theta}.
