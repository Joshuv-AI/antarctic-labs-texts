Some definite integrals involving Jacobi polynomials.
Enno Diekema

Abstract
Szmytkowski derived a certain integral with Gegenbauer polynomials. A natural generalization is to derive lookalike integrals with Jacobi polynomials. Six methods are treated to derive the first integral. The first method should be enough to prove the first integral, but by the other methods there arises remarkable formula such as par example a zero-balanced F 3 F_{3} Appell function which can be converted into a F 1 2 {}_{2}F_{1} hypergeometric function. Another three integrals complete the paper.

Theorem 2

\int_{x}^{1}\dfrac{(1-t)^{\alpha}(1+t)^{\beta}}{(t-x)^{\lambda}}P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(t)dt=2^{\beta}\dfrac{\Gamma(n+\alpha+1)\Gamma(1-\lambda)}{\Gamma(n+1)\Gamma(\alpha-\lambda+2)}(1-x)^{\alpha+1-\lambda}\,\mbox{}_{2}F_{1}\!\left(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{\alpha+n+1,-\beta-n}{\alpha-\lambda+2};\dfrac{1-x}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{2^{\alpha+\beta+n+1}}{(1-x)^{n+\lambda}}\binom{-\lambda}{n}B(\alpha+n+1,\beta+n+1)\,\mbox{}_{2}F_{1}\!\left(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{\alpha+n+1,n+\lambda}{\alpha+\beta+2+2n};\dfrac{2}{1-x}\right)-

3
Proof of the theorems

Proof of Theorem 1.

We treat several methods to compute the integral.
For the first method we use the formula of Rodrigues for the Jacobi polynomials. This is a very direct method. The next methods are given because there arises many remarkable formulas. For the second method we use an integral given in
[
8
, 2.22.4.11]
. This integral can be converted into the desired one. The third method uses also a formula of a known integral
[
8
, (2.22.4.9)]
. Because there are no proofs of these formula in the reference the first method can be used as a proof of these formula. The fourth method uses again the formula of Rodrigues for the Jacobi polynomials. The fifth method is a variation of the fourth one. The sixth method is a so-called brute force method. The Jacobi polynomials are written as a summation. Interchanging the integral and the summation gives after a lot of manipulations with the Gamma functions and the Pochhammer symbols the desired result.

First method.

This first method starts with the Rodrigues formula for the Jacobi polynomials

P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(t)=\dfrac{(-1)^{n}}{2^{n}\,n!}\dfrac{1}{(1-t)^{\alpha}(1+t)^{\beta}}\dfrac{d^{n}}{dt^{n}}\big((1-t)^{\alpha}(1+t)^{\beta}(1-t^{2})^{n}\big).

Application to the integral (
2.1
) gives

I=\int_{-1}^{1}\dfrac{(1-t)^{\alpha}(1+t)^{\beta}}{(z-t)^{\lambda}}P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(t)dt=\dfrac{(-1)^{n}}{2^{n}\,n!}\int_{-1}^{1}\dfrac{1}{(z-t)^{\lambda}}\dfrac{d^{n}}{dt^{n}}\big((1-t)^{\alpha+n}(1+t)^{\beta+n}\big)dt.

n
-times partial integration gives

I=\dfrac{2^{\alpha+\beta+n+1}(\lambda)_{n}}{(z+1){\lambda+n}n!}B(\alpha+n+1,\beta+n+1)\,\mbox{}_{2}F_{1}\!\left(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{\beta+1+n,\lambda+n}{\alpha+\beta+2n+2};\dfrac{2}{z+1}\right)

Using a standard Gaussian transformation gives

\int_{-1}^{1}\dfrac{(1-t)^{\alpha}(1+t)^{\beta}}{(z-t)^{\lambda}}P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(t)dt=\dfrac{2^{\alpha+\beta+n+1}(\lambda)_{n}}{(z-1){\lambda+n}n!}B(\alpha+n+1,\beta+n+1)\,\mbox{}_{2}F_{1}\!\left(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{\alpha+1+n,\lambda+n}{\alpha+\beta+2n+2};\dfrac{2}{1-z}\right)

This proves the theorem.
\square

Third method.

Our starting formula is the integral
[
8
, (2.22.4.9)]

\displaystyle\int_{-b}^{b}t^{m}(b-t)^{\alpha}(b+t)^{\beta}P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}\left(\dfrac{t}{b}\right)dt=I_{m}

\displaystyle I_{m}=0,\qquad m=0,1,2,\dots,n-1

\displaystyle I_{n}=B(\alpha+n+1,\beta+n+1)(2b)^{\alpha+\beta+n+1}

\displaystyle I_{m}=\binom{m}{n}B(\alpha+n+1,\beta+n+1)2^{\alpha+\beta+n+1}b^{\alpha+\beta+m+1}\,\mbox{}_{2}F_{1}\!\left(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n-m,\alpha+n+1}{\alpha+\beta+2n+2};2\right)\qquad m>n.

We use the next formula

\dfrac{1}{(z-t)^{\lambda}}=\dfrac{1}{z^{\lambda}}\dfrac{1}{\left(1-\dfrac{t}{z}\right)^{\lambda}}=\dfrac{1}{z^{\lambda}}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\dfrac{(\lambda)_{k}}{k!}\left(\dfrac{t}{z}\right)^{k}

\displaystyle=\int_{-1}^{1}\dfrac{(1-t)^{\alpha}(1+t)^{\beta}}{(z-t)^{\lambda}}P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(t)dt

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{z^{\lambda}}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\dfrac{(\lambda)_{k}}{k!z^{k}}\int_{-1}^{1}t^{k}(1-t)^{\alpha}(1+t)^{\beta}P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(t)dt=

\displaystyle=\dfrac{2^{\alpha+\beta+n+1}B(\alpha+n+1,\beta+n+1)}{z^{\lambda}n!}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\dfrac{(\lambda)_{k}}{z^{k}\Gamma(k-n+1)}\,\mbox{}_{2}F_{1}\!\left(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n-k,\alpha+n+1}{\alpha+\beta+2n+2};2\right).

Setting

I=\dfrac{2^{\alpha+\beta+n+1}B(\alpha+n+1,\beta+n+1)}{z^{n+\lambda}\Gamma(n+1)\Gamma(\lambda)}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\dfrac{\Gamma(m+n+\lambda)}{\Gamma(m+1)}\dfrac{1}{z^{m}}\,\mbox{}_{2}F_{1}\!\left(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{-m,\alpha+n +1}{\alpha+\beta+2n+2};2\right).

The hypergeometric function can be written as a summation. We get

\,\mbox{}_{2}F_{1}\!\left(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{-m,\alpha+\beta+1}{\alpha+\beta+2n+2};2\right)=\dfrac{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta+2n+2)}{\Gamma(-m)\Gamma(\alpha+n+1)}\sum_{k=0}^{m}\dfrac{\Gamma(k-m)\Gamma(k+\alpha+n+1)}{\Gamma(k+\alpha+\beta+2n+2)}\dfrac{2^{k}}{k!}.

I=\dfrac{2^{\alpha+\beta+n+1}\Gamma(\beta+n+1)}{z^{n+\lambda}\Gamma(n+1)\Gamma(\lambda)}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\dfrac{\Gamma(m+n+\lambda)}{\Gamma(m+1)\Gamma(-m)}\dfrac{1}{z^{m}}\sum_{k=0}^{m}\dfrac{\Gamma(k-m)\Gamma(k+\alpha+n+1)}{\Gamma(k+\alpha+\beta+2n+2)}\dfrac{2^{k}}{k!}.

Rewriting the double summation gives

I=\dfrac{2^{\alpha+\beta+n+1}\Gamma(\beta+n+1)}{z^{n+\lambda}\Gamma(n+1)\Gamma(\lambda)}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\dfrac{\Gamma(k+\alpha+n+1)}{\Gamma(k+\alpha+\beta+2n+2)}\dfrac{2^{k}}{k!}\sum_{m=k}^{\infty}\dfrac{\Gamma(k-m)\Gamma(m+n+\lambda)}{\Gamma(m+1)\Gamma(-m)}\dfrac{1}{z^{m}}.

For the Gamma functions there is the following property

\dfrac{\Gamma(k-m)}{\Gamma(-m)}=(-1)^{k}\dfrac{\Gamma(1+m)}{\Gamma(1+m-k)}

(3.7)

Application yields

Substitution gives

(3.8)

Writing the summation as a hypergeometric function proves the theorem.

Fourth method.

This method starts with the Rodrigues formula for the Jacobi polynomials

P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(t)=\dfrac{(-1)^{n}}{2^{n}\,n!}\dfrac{1}{(1-t)^{\alpha}(1+t)^{\beta}}\dfrac{d^{n}}{dt^{n}}\big((1-t)^{\alpha}(1+t)^{\beta}(1-t^{2})^{n}\big).

Application to the integral (
2.1
) gives

I=\int_{-1}^{1}\dfrac{(1-t)^{\alpha}(1+t)^{\beta}}{(z-t)^{\lambda}}P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(t)dt=\dfrac{(-1)^{n}}{2^{n}\,n!}\int_{-1}^{1}\dfrac{1}{(z-t)^{\lambda}}\dfrac{d^{n}}{dt^{n}}\big((1-t)^{\alpha+n}(1+t)^{\beta+n}\big)dt.

(3.9)

One possibility is to apply partial integration. But we prefer to apply the standard formula for the
n

\dfrac{d^{n}}{dt^{n}}\big(f(t)g(t)\big)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}\dfrac{d^{n-k}}{dt^{n-k}}f(t)\dfrac{d^{k}}{dt^{k}}g(t).

Then we get

\displaystyle\dfrac{d^{k}}{dt^{k}}(1+t)^{\beta+n}=\dfrac{\Gamma(\beta+1+n)}{\Gamma(\beta+1+n-k)}(1+t)^{\beta+n-k}

\displaystyle\dfrac{d^{n-k}}{dt^{n-k}}(1-t)^{\alpha+n}=(-1)^{n-k}\dfrac{\Gamma(\alpha+1+n)}{\Gamma(\alpha+1+k)}(1-t)^{\alpha+k}.

Application to (
3.9
) and interchanging the summation and the integral, which is allowed because the summation is convergent, gives

I=\dfrac{(-1)^{n}}{2^{n}\,n!}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}(-1)^{n-k}\dfrac{\Gamma(\alpha+1+n)}{\Gamma(\alpha+1+k)}\dfrac{\Gamma(\beta+1+n)}{\Gamma(\beta+1+n-k)}\int_{-1}^{1}\dfrac{1}{(z-t)^{\lambda}}(1-t)^{\alpha+k}(1+t)^{\beta+n-k}dt.

Using

changes the bounds from
(-1,1)
to
(0,1)
. We use also
\binom{n}{k}=(-1)^{k}(-n)_{k}/k!
. We get

I=\dfrac{2^{\alpha+\beta+1}}{n!}\dfrac{1}{(z+1)^{\lambda}}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\dfrac{(-n)_{k}}{k!}\dfrac{\Gamma(\alpha+1+n)}{\Gamma(\alpha+1+k)}\dfrac{\Gamma(\beta+1+n)}{\Gamma(\beta+1+n-k)}\int_{0}^{1}x^{\beta+n-k}(1-x)^{\alpha+k}\left(1-\dfrac{2}{z+1}x\right)^{-\lambda}dx.

The integral gives a hypergeometric function

I=\dfrac{2^{\alpha+\beta+1}}{n!}\dfrac{1}{(z+1)^{\lambda}}\dfrac{\Gamma(\alpha+1+n)\Gamma(\beta+1+n)}{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta+n+2)}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\dfrac{(-n)_{k}}{k!}\,\mbox{}_{2}F_{1}\!\left(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{\lambda,1+\beta+n-k}{\alpha+\beta+n+2};\dfrac{2}{z+1}\right).

(3.10)

with
\operatorname{Re}(z)\geq 1
. Using a Gauss transformation results in

I=\dfrac{2^{\alpha+\beta+1}}{n!}\dfrac{1}{(z-1)^{\lambda}}\dfrac{\Gamma(\alpha+1+n)\Gamma(\beta+1+n)}{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta+n+2)}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\dfrac{(-n)_{k}}{k!}\,\mbox{}_{2}F_{1}\!\left(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{\lambda,1+\alpha+k}{\alpha+\beta+n+2};\dfrac{2}{1-z}\right).

Writing the hypergeometric function as a summation, using

(3.11)

and interchanging the summations, which is allowed because the summations are convergent, gives

I=\dfrac{2^{\alpha+\beta+1}}{n!}\dfrac{1}{(z-1)^{\lambda}}\dfrac{\Gamma(\alpha+1+n)\Gamma(\beta+1+n)}{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta+n+2)}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\dfrac{(\lambda)_{j}(\alpha+1)_{j}}{(\alpha+\beta+n+2)_{j}}\dfrac{1}{j!}\left(\dfrac{2}{1-z}\right)^{j}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\dfrac{(-n)_{k}(\alpha+1+j)_{k}}{(\alpha+1)_{k}}\dfrac{1}{k!}.

The last summation is standard. There rests

I=\dfrac{2^{\alpha+\beta+1}}{n!}\dfrac{1}{(z-1)^{\lambda}}\dfrac{\Gamma(\alpha+1)\Gamma(\beta+1+n)}{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta+n+2)}\sum_{j=n}^{\infty}\dfrac{(\lambda)_{j}(\alpha+1)_{j}}{(\alpha+\beta+n+2)_{j}}\dfrac{\Gamma(n-j)}{\Gamma(-j)}\dfrac{1}{j!}\left(\dfrac{2}{1-z}\right)^{j}.

Using the transformation

This is the same equation as (

Sixth method.

We call this method a brute force method. The Jacobi polynomial is written as a hypergeometric function which is written as a summation. After interchanging the summation and the integration which is allowed because of the convergence of the Jacobi polynomial and integration gives a double summation which can be simplified until we reach the final result.

\displaystyle I

\displaystyle=\int_{-1}^{1}\dfrac{(1-t)^{\alpha}(1+t)^{\beta}}{(z-t)^{\lambda}}P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(t)dt

\displaystyle=\int_{-1}^{1}\dfrac{(1-t)^{\alpha}(1+t)^{\beta}}{(z-t)^{\lambda}}\binom{n+\alpha}{n}\,\mbox{}_{2}F_{1}\!\left(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{-n,n+\alpha+\beta+1}{\alpha+1};\dfrac{1-t}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\Gamma(n+\alpha+1)}{\Gamma(n+1)\Gamma(\alpha_{n}+1)}\sum_{i=0}^{n}\dfrac{(-n)_{i}(n+\alpha+\beta+1)_{i}}{(\alpha+1)_{i}}\dfrac{1}{i!}\left(\dfrac{1}{2}\right)^{i}\int_{-1}^{1}\dfrac{(1-t)^{\alpha+i}(1+t)^{\beta}}{(z-t)^{\lambda}}dt

Integration gives a hypergeometric function which can be written as a summation

I=2^{\alpha+\beta+1}\dfrac{\Gamma(n+\alpha+1)\Gamma(\beta+1)}{\Gamma(n+1)\Gamma(\alpha+\beta+2)}\left(\dfrac{1}{z-1}\right)^{\lambda}\sum_{i=0}^{n}\dfrac{(-n)_{i}(n+\alpha+\beta+1)_{i}}{(\alpha+\beta+2)_{i}}\dfrac{1}{i!}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\dfrac{(\lambda)_{j}(\alpha+1+i)_{j}}{(\alpha+\beta+2+i)_{j}}\dfrac{1}{j!}\left(\dfrac{2}{1-z}\right)^{j}

Making use of (
3.11
) gives

I=2^{\alpha+\beta+1}\dfrac{\Gamma(n+\alpha+1)\Gamma(\beta+1)}{\Gamma(n+1)\Gamma(\alpha+\beta+2)}\left(\dfrac{1}{z-1}\right)^{\lambda}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\dfrac{(\alpha+1)_{j}(\lambda)_{j}}{(\alpha+\beta+2)_{j}}\ \dfrac{1}{j!}\left(\dfrac{2}{1-z}\right)^{j}\\
\sum_{i=0}^{n}\dfrac{(-n)_{i}(n+\alpha+\beta+1)_{i}(\alpha+1+j)_{i}}{(\alpha+1)_{i}(\alpha+\beta+2+j)_{i}}\dfrac{1}{i!}

For the last summation we can use Saalschütz’s theorem. After much manipulations with the Gamma functions we get

I=2^{\alpha+\beta+1}\dfrac{\Gamma(\alpha+1)\Gamma(\beta+1+n)}{\Gamma(n+1)\Gamma(\alpha+\beta+2+n)}\left(\dfrac{1}{z-1}\right)^{\lambda}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\dfrac{(\alpha+1)_{j}(\lambda)_{j}}{(\alpha+\beta+2+n)_{j}}\dfrac{1}{j!}\left(\dfrac{2}{1-z}\right)^{j}\dfrac{\Gamma(n-j)}{\Gamma(-j)}
