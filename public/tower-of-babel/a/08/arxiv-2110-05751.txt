Sensitivity to habitable planets in the Roman microlensing survey Sensitivity to habitable planets in the Roman microlensing survey –
Sedighe Sajadian

Abstract
We study the Roman sensitivity to exoplanets in the Habitable Zone (HZ). The Roman efficiency for detecting habitable planets is maximized for three classes of planetary microlensing events with close caustic topologies. (a) The events with the lens distances of D l ≳ 7 D_{\rm l}\gtrsim 7 kpc, the host lens masses of M h ≳ 0.6 ​ M ⊙ M_{\rm h}\gtrsim 0.6M_{\odot} . By assuming Jupiter-mass planets in the HZs, these events have q ≲ 0.001 q\lesssim 0.001 and d ≳ 0.17 d\gtrsim 0.17 ( q q is their mass ratio and d d is the projected planet-host distance on the sky plane normalized to the Einstein radius). The events with primary lenses, M h ≲ 0.1 ​ M ⊙ M_{\rm h}\lesssim 0.1M_{\odot} , while their lens systems are either (b) close to the observer with D l ≲ 1 D_{\rm l}\lesssim 1 kpc or (c) close to the Galactic bulge, D l ≳ 7 D_{\rm l}\gtrsim 7 kpc. For Jupiter-mass planets in the HZs of the primary lenses, the events in these two classes have q ≳ 0.01 q\gtrsim 0.01 , d ≲ 0.04 d\lesssim 0.04 . The events in the class (a) make larger caustics. By simulating planetary microlensing events detectable by Roman , we conclude that the Roman efficiencies for detecting Earth- and Jupiter-mass planets in the Optimistic HZs (OHZs, which is the region between [ 0.5 , 2 ] [0.5,\penalty\ 2] AU around a Sun-like star) are 0.01 % 0.01\% and 5 % 5\% , respectively. If we assume that one exoplanet orbits each microlens in microlensing events detectable by Roman ( i.e., ∼ 27000 \sim 27000 ), this telescope has the potential to detects 35 35 exoplanets with the projected planet-host distances in the OHZs with only one having a mass ≲ 10 ​ M ⊕ \lesssim 10M_{\oplus} . According to the simulation, 27 27 of these exoplanets are actually in the OHZs.

Introduction

One of the primary motivations for searching for extrasolar planets is to look for extraterrestrial life outside of our solar system and to resolve the Fermi paradox
(
Landis 1998
;
Cirkovic 2018
;
Webb 2015
;
Prantzos 2020
, see, e.g., )
. According to this paradox, the likelihood of extraterrestrial civilizations elsewhere in our galaxy is high, despite the fact that no evidence of them has been found. Indeed, there are many planetary systems in our galaxy similar to our solar system. Some of them must be suitable for developing intelligent beings and we should have received signals from them up to now
(
Huang 1959
;
Kopparapu 2013
, e.g., see,)
.

The existence of a liquid bio-solvent is one of the necessary factors for life. Liquid water is the best candidate that exits in a wide temperature range, i.e.,
[273,\penalty\ 373]
K
(
Huang 1959
;
Kasting et al. 1993
;
Kopparapu
et al. 2019
;
Forget 2013
)
. The range of orbits around each star that can support liquid water over the surface of its orbiting planets is the so-called ’Habitable Zone’ (HZ). However, the existence of liquid water additionally needs sufficient atmospheric pressure over the planet’s surface.

The equilibrium temperature over the planet’s surface,
T_{\rm p}
, is determined by the Bond albedo,
A_{\rm B}
, which is the fraction of the incident radiation to the planet’s surface that is scattered back into space, and the re-radiation factor,
f
, and is given by
(

;

Kasting 2017

:

\displaystyle T_{\rm p}=T_{\ast}\sqrt{\frac{R_{\ast}}{s}}\Big[f(1-A_{\rm B})\Big]^{1/4}

(1)

where,
T_{\ast}
and
R_{\ast}
are the effective temperature and the radius of the host star, respectively,
s
is the planet-host distance. Here, the planet is considered as a black body being heated only by its host star. For rocky planets, the Bond albedo and re-radiation factor are
A_{\rm B}\simeq 0.2
and
f\simeq 0.35
which result the Earth temperature equals to
T_{\oplus}\simeq 289

Two kinds of HZs are defined (narrower and wider zones): Conservative (CHZ) and Optimistic (OHZ). In our solar system, the CHZ and OHZ represent the circumstellar zones with boundaries
[0.95,\penalty\ 1.67]
AU and
[0.5,\penalty\ 2]
AU (including Venus and Mars orbits), respectively
(
Kopparapu
et al. 2013
;
Kopparapu
et al. 2014
;
Chandler
et al. 2016
)
. In the CHZ and OHZ of the Sun, the surface temperature of orbiting planets can be between
[228,\penalty\ 294]
K and
[203,\penalty\ 405]
K, respectively. In order to determine the outer and inner boundaries of the OHZ, a thick atmosphere with a strong greenhouse effect and a thin atmosphere with a weak greenhouse effect are assumed, respectively. Therefore, the first step to probing extraterrestrial life is searching for extrasolar planets whose orbits stay in the HZs, such as the Earth in our solar system.

The highest sensitivity to exoplanet detection through microlensing observations occurs on planets beyond the snow line. These planetary systems frequently make resonant or close-to-resonant caustic topologies

(

. Their results suggest a lower mass for the host star.

Habitable planetary systems most often form binary-lens systems with close caustic topologies characterized by three small caustic curves. The probability of passing through these small caustics or covering such short caustic-crossing features is low. Nonetheless, the new generation of telescopes and microlensing surveys, such as
The Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope
(
Roman
) survey with the improved cadence (i.e.,
\simeq 15
min) and better resolutions potentially may discover more habitable planets through microlensing observations. However, one of the main goals of the
Roman
Galactic Exoplanet Survey is to detect a large sample of free-floating or bound, cool and low-mass exoplanets and measure the mass functions of these planets, which differs somewhat from searching exclusively for exoplanets in the HZs. We emphasize that the microlensing method can detect planetary systems at farther distances with respect to the observer (even in the Galactic bulge) than those detectable through transit or radial velocity measurements.

In this paper, we first revisit the subject of probing habitable planets through microlensing observations. Then, we will do a Monte-Carlo simulation of planetary microlensing events to evaluate the
Roman
efficiency for detecting habitable planets and probe the special microlensing configurations with the maximum
Roman
sensitivity to habitable planets. Finally, we calculate how many habitable planets could be discovered during the mission.

The paper is organized as follows. In section

3

Di
Stefano 1999

In this reference, the author has studied the advantages and disadvantages of the blending effect of these bright host stars. 3\%

This choice caused an overestimation for detectable habitable planets. In this section, I theoretically revisit the mentioned subject.

During a microlensing event, the angular Einstein radius,
\theta_{\rm E}
, is much smaller than the angular size of the FWHM (Full width at Half Maximum) of the target PSF (Point Spread Function),
\theta_{\rm PSF}
. In the
Roman
microlensing survey,
\theta_{\rm PSF}\simeq 0.33
arcs, i.e., around three times of the
Roman
pixel size which is
0.11
arcs
(
Penny et al. 2019
)
. Hence, the lens flux is always blended with the source flux and we consider this effect in the simulation. In order to evaluate the blending effect owing to the lens flux, we define the lens blending factor as:

\displaystyle f_{\rm l}=\frac{F_{\rm l}}{F_{\ast}+\sum_{i}F_{i}},

(8)

which is the ratio of the lens flux
F_{\rm l}
to the total flux due to the source star itself
F_{\ast}
and other blending stars (which their angular distances from the source star are smaller than the radius of the source PSF) at the baseline. The greater the
f_{\rm l}
, the greater the contamination caused by the lens brightness.

Using the specified parameters, e.g.,
\rho_{\ast},\penalty\ t_{\rm E},\penalty\ u_{0},t_{0},...
, we make the microlensing lightcurves. For each simulated lightcurve, we generate synthetic data points taken during the
Roman
observing program, as described in
Bagheri
et al. 2019
;
Sajadian &
Salehi 2020
. The cadence of data is fixed at
15.16
min. The photometric error bars of the synthetic data points,
\sigma_{\rm m}
, are specified based on the magnified apparent magnitudes of the targets, as shown in Figure (4) of
Penny et al. 2019
.

We first extract the detectable microlensing events from simulated events using three criteria which have been explained in
Sajadian &
Poleski 2019
. Then, we evaluate the detectability of the planetary signatures in extracted events. In order to realize the planetary signatures, we exert two extra criteria. Because the planet-host mass ratio,
q=M_{\rm p}/M_{\rm h}
, is small, the resulting planetary signatures from caustic-crossing features (or passing close to caustic curves) are small and short. For these events, the best-fitted single models are the real event by considering only primary lenses (ignoring their planets), which are located at
(-d\penalty\ q/(1+q),\penalty\ 0)
. Here,
d=s_{\mathcal{P}}/R_{\rm E}
is the planet-host distance projected on the sky plane and normalized to the Einstein radius. Our criteria are as follows. (i) There are at least three consecutive data points that are
4\sigma_{\rm m}
above (or below) the best-fitted single models. (ii)
\Delta\chi^{2}_{\rm p}>300,\penalty\ 800
, corresponding to low and high sensitivities to planetary signatures (LS and HS), where
\Delta\chi^{2}_{\rm p}=\chi^{2}_{\rm{real}}-\chi^{2}_{\rm{single}}
, the difference between
\chi^{2}
values obtained by fitting the real models and the best-fitted single-lens models. Here, we assume that the best-fitted planetary models are real models. We note that the threshold of
500
for
\Delta\chi^{2}_{\rm p}
is often adjudged as a robust criterion for detecting planetary signatures in microlensing events
(
Gould
et al. 2010
;
Udalski
et al. 2018
;
Ryu et al. 2018
;
Hirao
et al. 2020
, see, e.g., )
. The first criterion ejects symmetric events that may be misinterpreted as single microlensing events, unless the deviated data points exactly occur at the time of the closest approach, which is rare. Our aim is to evaluate the detectability of planetary signatures and we do not pay attention to any other possible degenerate solutions.

We aim to respond two main questions from this simulation. (1) If there is an exoplanet with a given mass in the HZ, what is the
Roman
efficiency for detecting this planet? Furthermore, for what lensing configurations is this efficiency maximized? (2) During the
Roman
mission, how many habitable planets are detectable? On average what properties do these detectable planets within the HZs have? These two issues are referred to in the following subsections.

3.1
Detection efficiency

Here, we study the
Roman
efficiency for detecting HZ planets during its mission by doing a Monte-Carlo simulation. For each simulated microlensing event, we assume that one exoplanet is located in the OHZ of the lens object and choose the semi-major axis of its orbit uniformly from the range of
s\in[s_{\rm{OHZ},\penalty\ i},\penalty\ s_{\rm{OHZ},\penalty\ o}]
. We assume the planet’s orbit around its host star is circular and disregard the orbital motion effect of microlens systems
(
Sajadian 2014
, see, e.g., )
. Hence, for projecting the planetary orbit onto the sky plane, we need one inclination angle,
i
, as
(
Gould &
Loeb 1992
;
Batista
et al. 2015
, see, e.g.,)
:

\displaystyle s_{\mathcal{P}}=s\penalty\ \sin(i).

(9)

In fact, if we assume the host star at the center of a sphere, its orbiting planet can be at any random position over the surface of that sphere. Its location is specified with two angles in the spherical coordinate system which are the azimuthal and polar angles. The second one is the inclination angle of the planet orbit with respect to the line of sight toward the observer (polar axis). To uniformly choose initial position of the planet over the sphere surface,
\cos(i)
is chosen uniformly from a range of
[0,\penalty\ 1]
in the simulation. Therefore, on average the ratio of the projected planet-host distance to their real distance,
f_{\mathcal{P}}=\big<s_{\mathcal{P}}/s\big>
, is

\displaystyle f_{\mathcal{P}}=\left<\sin(i)\right>=\int_{0}^{1}\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\penalty\ dx=\frac{\pi}{4},

(10)

where
x=\cos(i)
.

In the left and right panels, the planetary systems make close and wide caustic topologies, respectively. The source trajectories projected on the lens plane in these events (cyan lines) pass close to and over the planetary caustic curves. In the second lightcurve, although the blending effect due to the host lens is high, the planetary signature is detectable during its caustic-crossing features.

3.2
Statistical Calculations

In order to estimate the number of habitable planets that could potentially be detected during the
Roman
mission, we will do a more realistic simulation of all possible planetary systems detectable by this telescope and extract the events with planets in the HZs.

For doing this realistic simulation, we choose semi-major axis of the planetary orbit,
s
, from the Öpik’s law
(
Öpik 1924
)
, i.e.,

\displaystyle dN/ds\propto 1/s,

(11)

and from the range
s\in[0.01,\penalty\ 100]
AU. To extract the lens mass ratio, we use the mass ratio function which has been resulted from microlensing observations. One of the advantages of microlensing observations is studying the abundance of bound planets in planetary systems. For instance,
Cassan
et al. 2012
have found that
17\%
of stars have Jupiter-mass planets and low-mass planets are more common. From the MOA microlensing observations,
Suzuki
et al. 2016
have discovered a turnover in the mass ratio function at
q\simeq 1.7\times 10^{-4}
. Their results have been confirmed by
Udalski
et al. 2018
for a larger sample and they have offered a twofold mass ratio function which is given by:

\displaystyle\frac{dN}{d\log_{10}q}\propto\penalty\ \begin{cases}q^{0.73}&q\lesssim 2\times 10^{-4},\\
q^{-0.93}&q>2\times 10^{-4}.\\
\end{cases}

(12)

In the simulation, we choose the mass ratio using this function and from the range
q\in[10^{-5},\penalty\ 0.06]
. However, we disregard the events with
M_{\rm p}(=qM_{\rm h})>15M_{\rm J}
which are brown dwarfs. In the simulation, we extract planetary microlensing events which (i) have detectable planetary signatures by applying LS and HS criteria, and (ii) their planets are within the HZs. We perform the simulation toward all mentioned sub-fields and the average parameters of these events are mentioned in Table
2
.

We conclude that the fraction of simulated microlensing events with detectable planetary signatures (by exerting LS criteria) during the
Roman
mission is
\epsilon_{\rm p}=3.0\%
. The fractions of these planetary events that their planets locate in the OHZs and CHZs (i.e.,
s\in[s_{\rm{HZ},\penalty\ i},\penalty\ s_{\rm{HZ},\penalty\ o}]
) are
\epsilon_{\rm{HZ}}\simeq 3.35,\penalty\ 1.79\%
, respectively.

In order to estimate the number of habitable planets that can be detected by
Roman
, we use predictions of detectable microlensing events by this telescope, done by
Penny et al. 2019
. Accordingly,
Roman
will detect
N_{\rm e}\sim 27000
microlensing events. If we assume that each of these microlenses has one planet orbiting it at a distance of
[0.01,\penalty\ 100]
AU (specified by the Öpik’s law), the number of habitable planets which are detectable by
Roman
is evaluated as
N_{\rm HZ}\simeq N_{\rm e}\penalty\ \epsilon_{\rm p}\penalty\ \epsilon_{\rm{HZ}}
. Hence, by considering LS criteria
Roman
will detect
27
exoplanets in the OHZs, where
15
of them are in the CHZs. If there is more than one exoplanet for each microlens, the total number of habitable exoplanets will increase. In fact, the simulations in this work are based on the Besançon model, similar to ones done by
Penny et al. 2019
. We note that the
Roman
efficiencies (given by our simulations) are the average values due to all
Roman
observing directions. If we had had
N_{\rm e}(l,\penalty\ b)
for each line of sight, by calculating the efficiencies in different line of sights we could have determined
N_{\rm HZ}
more accurately. However,
N_{\rm HZ}
given in this paper is an acceptable estimation, because the
Roman
observing directions are close to each other and the variations of related parameters are small. For instance, the average extinction in W149 towards the mentioned sub-fields are
\left<A_{\rm{W149}}\right>=1.58,\penalty\ 1.97,\penalty\ 0.79,\penalty\ 0.69,\penalty\ 0.79,\penalty\ 0.86,\penalty\ 0.94
, and the average optical depth for the observations toward these sub-fields are
\left<\tau(\times 10^{6})\right>=10.4,\penalty\ 10.5,\penalty\ 8.5,\penalty\ 9.7,\penalty\ 10.6,\penalty\ 10.2,\penalty\ 9.9
.

By applying HS criteria for detectability of planetary signatures, the number of detectable planets in the OHZs and CHZs is
24
and
12
, respectively.

These planets are most often rotating around
\rm{K}
-type stars which are on average at the distance farther than
6.7
kpc from us. Targets in these events are on average as bright as
19.5
mag in the W149 filter at the baseline. Some part of this brightness is due to the primary lens. Because primary lenses have a high brightness (around
0.24
of the source brightness), follow-up observations can resolve primary lens objects. Detecting lens objects directly helps to resolve microlensing degeneracy and inferring parameters of planets.

Owing to the brightness of primary lens objects, around
10.5\%
of source stars in our sample (of detectable planetary events by
Roman

\sim 18.4\%

(
Wyrzykowski
et al. 2015

Sajadian 2015b

.

From our simulation, we found that
20.7\%

5\rho_{\ast}
, where
\rho_{\ast}
is the projected source radius normalized to the Einstein radius). In other events, source stars pass close to the caustic instead of crossing it, e.g., the left event in Figure

Han
et al. 2021

Kondo
et al. 2021

4
Discussion

In planetary microlensing events, one can measure the planet-host mass ratio and planet-host distance projected on the sky plane. The mass of primary components
M_{\rm h}
is carefully evaluated in caustic-crossing microlensing events, if parallax and finite-size effects are measured
(
Yoo et al. 2004
;
Shvartzvald
et al. 2019
;
Han
et al. 2018
, see, e.g.,)
. But, microlensing observations give the projected planet-host distance onto the sky plane,
s_{\mathcal{P}}
, which is given by Equation
9
. The projected planet-host distance is always less than the actual distance, i.e.,
s_{\mathcal{P}}<s
.

For a given
s
, we choose
\cos(i)
randomly. The distribution of resulting projected distances normalized to real distance,
s_{\mathcal{P}}/s
, is shown in Figure
6
. On average,
\left<s_{\mathcal{P}}\right>=0.79\penalty\ s
, as given by Equation
10
. Hence, if microlensing observations find a planetary system with the projected distance in the habitable zone, the real distance between the planet and its host star is most likely larger than the measured amount. Since microlensing is more sensitive to normalized planet-host distances larger than those owing to habitable planetary systems, so microlensing observations overestimate the number of HZ planets. In the simulation of section
3
, we found that fraction of detectable planetary microlensing events (by considering LS criteria) for which
s_{\mathcal{P}}
is located in the OHZs and CHZs are
\epsilon_{\rm{HZ},\penalty\ \mathcal{P}}=4.3,\penalty\ 2.8\%
, respectively. These efficiencies are greater than
\epsilon_{\rm{HZ}}
. Accordingly,
Roman
will detect
N_{\rm{HZ},\penalty\ \mathcal{P}}=35
exoplanets with projected distances in the OHZs, which
22
of them have projected distances in CHZs, by applying LS criteria. These numbers are given in the two last columns of Table
2
as well.

If we consider only Earth-mass and super-Earth planets, fractions of total detectable planetary microlensing events by
Roman
that (i) the planet mass is
M_{\rm p}<10M_{\oplus}
(Earth and Super Earth-mass planets) and (ii) their projected positions are within the OHZs and CHZs are
\epsilon_{\rm{HZ},\penalty\ \mathcal{P},\penalty\ \oplus}\simeq 0.11,\penalty\ 0.05\%
, respectively. Hence, only
\sim 1
of total detectable exoplanets in the OHZs (i.e.,
35
) has
M_{\rm p}<10M_{\oplus}
.

The orbital motion effect should be very small in detectable planetary microlensing events with planets in the HZs. There are two reasons. (i) In these events, the caustic-crossing features are rare and in most cases, caustic sizes are very small and source stars are passing close to caustic curves. (ii) Most detectable events are from the second class of microlensing events introduced in subsection
3.1
, i.e., the events with
D_{\rm l}\gtrsim 7
kpc and
M_{\rm h}\gtrsim 0.6M_{\odot}
with the orbital period
P\gtrsim 100
days. These long orbital periods likely have a small effect on microlensing lightcurves.

5
conclusions

Detecting planets in the HZs is an important stage for probing extraterrestrial life, which is one of the active research areas in astrophysics and astronomy. One method for detecting exoplanets is microlensing observations. Although the sensitivity of microlensing observations to habitable planets is low, by this method one can probe planetary systems farther from the Earth position than those detectable through transit or radial velocity measurements. Hence, microlensing is a complementary method for detecting habitable planets in terms of their distances from us.

In this work, we evaluated the
Roman
efficiency to detect planets within the HZs by doing a Monte-Carlo simulation. We concluded that the LS
Roman
efficiencies for detecting planets in the HZs were
0.01,\penalty\ 5,\penalty\ 25\%
for Earth, Jupiter, and Super Jupiter-mass planets, respectively. This efficiency increases linearly with the mass, for
M_{\rm p}\lesssim M_{\rm J}
. But, it tends toward a constant value for the heavier planets. Earth-mass planets within the HZs are detectable around massive host stars with
\left<M_{\rm h}\right>\simeq 0.8M_{\odot}
at the distances farther than
7
kpc from us.

The
Roman
efficiency for detecting planets in the HZs is maximized for three classes of planetary microlensing events with close caustic topologies.

(a) The events with
D_{\rm l}\gtrsim 7
kpc,
M_{\rm h}\gtrsim 0.6M_{\odot}
(massive primary lenses). By assuming Jupiter-mass planets located within the HZs of these lenses, these events have
q\lesssim 0.001
and
d\gtrsim 0.17
with negligible effects due to parallax and orbital motion of lenses (Their orbital periods are longer than
100
days).

The events with low-mass primary lenses,
M_{\rm h}\lesssim 0.1M_{\odot}
, while their lens systems are either (b) close to the observer with
D_{\rm l}\lesssim 1
kpc (which have considerable parallax effects with the amplitude
\pi_{\rm{rel}}>0.87
mas) or (c) close to the Galactic bulge,
D_{\rm l}\gtrsim 7
