Geometry and distribution of roots of μ 2 ≡ D mod m \mu^{2}\equiv D\bmod m with D ≡ 1 mod 4 D\equiv 1\bmod 4
Zonglin Li, Matthew Welsh

Abstract
We extend the geometric interpretation of the roots of the quadratic congruence μ 2 ≡ D ( mod m ) \mu^{2}\equiv D\pmod{m} as the tops of certain geodesics in the hyperbolic plane to the case when D D is square-free and D ≡ 1 ( mod 4 ) D\equiv 1\pmod{4} . This allows us to establish limit laws for the fine-scale statistics of the roots using results by Marklof and the second author.



1
Introduction

The recent paper
[
11
]
by Marklof and the second author established limit laws for the fine-scale distribution of the roots of the quadratic congruence
\mu^{2}\equiv D\pmod{m}
, ordered by the modulus
m
, where
D
is a square-free positive integer and
D\not\equiv 1\pmod{4}
.
This is achieved by relating the roots (when
D>0
) to the tops of certain geodesics in the Poincaré upper half-plane
\mathbb{H}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\operatorname{Im}z>0\}
and (when
D<0
) to orbits of points, under the action of the modular group. Previous studies of quadratic congruences by means of the spectral theory of automorphic forms include the works of Bykovskii
[
3
]
, Duke, Friedlander and Iwaniec
[
4
]
, Hejhal
[
5
]
and many others (see
[
11
]
for further context and references).

Geodesics in
\mathbb{H}
are either vertical lines or semi-circles centred on the real line, and in the latter case we say that a geodesic is positively oriented if it is traced from left to right.
Letting
z_{\bm{c}}
denote the top of a positively oriented geodesic
\bm{c}
, i.e. the point on
\bm{c}
with the largest imaginary part, the correspondence established in
[
11
]
is that for
D
positive, square-free and
D\not\equiv 1\pmod{4}
, there is a finite set of geodesics
\{\bm{c}_{1},\dots,\bm{c}_{h}\}
such that

z_{\gamma\bm{c}_{l}}\equiv\frac{\mu}{m}+\mathrm{i}\frac{\sqrt{D}}{m}\pmod{\Gamma_{\infty}}

(1.1)

parametrizes all the roots
\mu\bmod m
as
l
varies over
1\leq l\leq h
and
\gamma
varies over the double cosets
\Gamma_{\infty}\backslash\operatorname{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})/\Gamma_{\bm{c}_{l}}
such that
\gamma\bm{c}_{l}
is positively oriented.
Here
\Gamma_{\bm{c}_{l}}
is the stabilizer in
\operatorname{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
of the geodesic
\bm{c}_{l}
(acting on
\mathbb{H}
by fractional linear transformations) and

\Gamma_{\infty}=\left\{\pm\begin{pmatrix}1&k\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}:k\in\mathbb{Z}\right\}

(1.2)

is the stabilizer in
\operatorname{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
of the boundary point
\infty\in\partial\mathbb{H}
.
Moreover, it is established that, for a fixed root
\nu\bmod n
, the subset of the roots
\mu\bmod m
satisfying
m\equiv 0\pmod{n}
and
\mu\equiv\nu\pmod{n}
is parametrized in the same way by (
1.1
) with
\operatorname{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
replaced by the congruence subgroups

\Gamma_{0}(n)=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\in\operatorname{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z}):c\equiv 0\pmod{n}\right\}

(1.3)

and a possibly different finite set of geodesics
\{\bm{c}_{1},\dots,\bm{c}_{h}\}
.

In this paper, we extend this geometric realization of the roots to remove the restriction
D\not\equiv 1\pmod{4}
, obtaining the following theorem.

Theorem 1
.

Let
D>0
be a square-free integer satisfying
D\equiv 1\pmod{4}
. Given
n>0
and
\nu\bmod n
with
\nu^{2}\equiv D\pmod{n}
, there exists a finite set of geodesics
\{\bm{c}_{1},\cdots,\bm{c}_{h}\}
, having the following properties:

(i)

For any positive integer
m
and
\mu\bmod m
satisfying
\mu^{2}\equiv D\pmod{m}
,
m\equiv 0\pmod{n}
, and
\mu\equiv\nu\pmod{n}
, there exists a unique
k
and a double coset
\Gamma_{\infty}\gamma\Gamma_{\bm{c}_{k}}\in\Gamma_{\infty}\backslash\Gamma_{0}(n)/\penalty\Gamma_{\bm{c}_{k}}
with
\gamma\bm{c}_{k}
positively oriented such that

z_{\gamma\bm{c}_{k}}\equiv\frac{\mu}{m}+\mathrm{i}\frac{\sqrt{D}}{m}\pmod{\Gamma_{\infty}}.

(1.4)

(ii)

Conversely, given
k
and a double coset
\Gamma_{\infty}\gamma\Gamma_{\bm{c}_{k}}\in\Gamma_{\infty}\backslash\Gamma_{0}(n)/\penalty\Gamma_{\bm{c}_{k}}
with positively oriented
\gamma\bm{c}_{k}
, there exists a unique positive integer
m
and
\mu\bmod m
satisfying
\mu^{2}\equiv D\pmod{m}
,
m\equiv 0\pmod{n}
, and
\mu\equiv\nu\pmod{n}
such that (
1.4
) holds.

The main difficulty in the proof of Theorem
1
is that when
D\equiv 1\pmod{4}
, the quadratic order
\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{D}]
is not maximal; the ring of integers of
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{D})
with
D\equiv 1\pmod{4}
is the larger order
\mathbb{Z}[\frac{1+\sqrt{D}}{2}]
.
This complicates the situation because not all ideals in
\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{D}]
are invertible, and so do not correspond naturally with geodesics in
\mathbb{H}
.
We resolve this by simultaneously considering ideals (and their corresponding geodesics) from the two orders
\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{D}]
and
\mathbb{Z}[\frac{1+\sqrt{D}}{2}]
.
In fact, ideals in the two orders correspond to two complementary subsets of roots, see Theorems
22
and
23
below.

Having Theorem
1
, we may apply the results of
[
11
]
to study the fine-scale distribution of these roots.
Specifically, we let
\{x_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
denote the sequence of normalized roots
\frac{\mu}{m}\in\mathbb{T}=\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
satisfying
m\equiv 0\pmod{n}
and
\mu\equiv\nu\pmod{n}
for a fixed root
\nu^{2}\equiv D\pmod{n}
.
Here we order by the modulus, with the ordering of the roots with the same modulus being inconsequential.
For example, with
D=5
,
n=1
and
\nu=0
, our sequence would begin

\left\{\frac{0}{1},\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{4},\frac{3}{4},\frac{0}{5},\frac{5}{10},\frac{4}{11},\frac{7}{11},\dots\right\}.

(1.5)

We remark that it has been proved by Hooley
[
6
]
that these sequences (without the congruence restrictions, i.e. for
n=1
,
\nu=0
) are uniformly distributed mod
1
if
D
is not a perfect square.
We also remark that the uniform distribution of the subsequence with
m\equiv 0\pmod{n}
was proved by Iwaniec
[
7
]
, where it was a crucial input to proving that the polynomial
x^{2}+1
, for example, is infinitely often a product of at most two primes.

Having understood the uniform distribution of the sequence
\frac{\mu}{m}
, it is natural to investigate its pseudo-randomness properties, in particular the fine-scale statistics.
An attractive example of such a statistic is the pair correlation function
R_{N,\nu,n}^{2}(I)
, which for a finite interval
I\subset\mathbb{R}
is defined by

R_{N,\nu,n}^{2}(I)=\frac{1}{N}\#\!\left\{1\leq i\neq j\leq N:x_{i}-x_{j}\in N^{-1}I+\mathbb{Z}\right\}.

(1.6)

Theorem
1
together with theorem
4.10
in
[
11
]
shows the existence of a limiting density function
\omega_{D,\nu,n}
for the pair correlation function.

Theorem 2
.

Let
D>0
be a square-free integer with
D\equiv 1\pmod{4}
and
\nu\bmod n
satisfy
\nu^{2}\equiv D\pmod{n}
. Then there exists an even and continuous function
\omega_{D,\nu,n}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
such that for any bounded interval
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
,

\lim_{N\to\infty}R_{N,\nu,n}^{2}(I)=\int_{I}\omega_{D,\nu,n}(t)\,dt.

(1.7)

[

]
, the methods in fact give an explicit expression for the density functions
\omega_{D,\nu,n}
.
Moreover, we provide a somewhat simplified expression than that in
[

]
, see section

.

For comparison, we remark that the pair correlation density for a Poisson point process would be the constant function
\omega(t)=1
, while the normalized eigenvalues of a large random matrix and (conjecturally) the zeros of the Riemann zeta function have pair correlation density
\omega(t)=1-(\frac{\sin\pi t}{\pi t})^{2}

In Figures

,

and
3
, the pair correlation total density
\omega_{D}=\omega_{D,0,1}
considers the sequence of all roots
\frac{\mu}{m}
of
\mu^{2}\equiv D\pmod{m}
, while the pair correlation partial density
\omega_{D,\mathcal{O}_{1}}
is for the subsequence of the roots
\frac{\mu}{m}
corresponding to ideals in the ring
\mathcal{O}_{1}=\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{D}]
, while the partial density
\omega_{D,\mathcal{O}_{2}}
is for the subsequence of the roots
\frac{\mu}{m}
corresponding to ideals in the ring
\mathcal{O}_{2}=\mathbb{Z}[\tfrac{1+\sqrt{D}}{2}]
.
An explicit condition on the roots determining which subset they belong to is given in Lemma
4
, and in the case that
D\equiv 5\pmod{8}
, can be reduced to a congruence condition on
m
, see Lemma
7
.

We can in fact prove limit distributions for a wide variety of fine-scale statistics in addition to the pair correlation.
We let
\lambda
be a Borel probability measure on
\mathbb{T}
and
X
be a random variable distributed according to
\lambda
.
We then define the following random counting measure, or random point process,

\Xi_{N,\lambda,\nu,n}=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\delta_{N(x_{j}-X+k)},

(1.8)

where
\delta_{y}
is a Dirac measure at
y\in\mathbb{R}
.
Given a non-negative integer
k
and
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
, we further denote the counting functions

\mathcal{N}_{I,\nu,n}(x,N)=\#\!\left\{1\leq j\leq N:x_{j}-x\in N^{-1}I+\mathbb{Z}\right\},

(1.9)

so that

\mathbb{P}(\Xi_{N,\lambda,\nu,n}(I)=k)=\lambda(\{x\in\mathbb{T}:\mathcal{N}_{I,\nu,n}(x,N)=k\}).

(1.10)

The next theorem can be proved by applying theorem
2.1
in
[
11
]
.

Theorem 3
.

Let
D>0
be a square-free integer with
D\equiv 1\pmod{4}
and
\nu\bmod n
satisfy
\nu^{2}\equiv D\pmod{n}
. Then there exists a random point process
\Xi_{\nu,n}
(depending only on
\nu,n
and
D
) such that for any Borel probability measure
\lambda
absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure,

\lim_{N\to\infty}\Xi_{N,\lambda,\nu,n}=\Xi_{\nu,n}.

(1.11)

In particular, given
k_{j}\in\mathbb{N}
and bounded interval
I_{j}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
, then

\lim_{N\to\infty}\lambda(\{x\in\mathbb{T}:N_{I_{j},\nu,n}(x,N)=k_{j},\forall\,1\leq j\leq r\})=\mathbb{P}(\Xi_{\nu,n}(I_{j})=k_{j},\forall\,1\leq j\leq r),

(1.12)

which is a continuous function of the endpoints of
I_{j}
.

.
Our results still hold by considering the fine-scale distribution of real parts of hyperbolic lattice points instead of tops of geodesics, see

n=1

.

2
Geodesics and roots of quadratic congruences

A convenient model for the hyperbolic plane is the Poincaré upper half-plane

\mathbb{H}=\{x+\mathrm{i}y\in\mathbb{C}:y>0\}

(2.1)

equipped with the metric

\differential s^{2}=\frac{1}{y^{2}}(\differential x^{2}+\differential y^{2}).

(2.2)

The special linear group
G=\operatorname{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})
, consisting of
2\times 2
matrices with real entries and determinant one, acts on
\mathbb{H}

by Möbius transformations

g=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}:z\mapsto\frac{az+b}{cz+d}.

(2.3)

We can in fact identify the projective special linear group
\operatorname{PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})=G/\{\pm I\}
with the unit tangent bundle
\mathrm{T}_{1}(\mathbb{H})
of the hyperbolic plane via

\pm g=\pm\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\mapsto\left(\frac{a\mathrm{i}+b}{c\mathrm{i}+d},\frac{\mathrm{i}}{(c\mathrm{i}+d)^{2}}\right),

(2.4)

where we have expressed
\mathrm{T}_{1}(\mathbb{H})
as the set of pairs of complex numbers
(z,w)
with
z\in\mathbb{H}
and
|w|=\operatorname{Im}z
(so
w
has length
1
with respect to the metric (
2.2
)).
We will however work with
G
directly and only emphasize the difference between
G
and
\mathrm{T}_{1}(\mathbb{H})
when there is a chance of confusion.
We note that the correspondence (
2.4
) is conveniently expressed in terms of the Iwasawa coordinates:

\begin{pmatrix}1&x\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}y^{\frac{1}{2}}&0\\
0&y^{-\frac{1}{2}}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\cos\frac{\theta}{2}&-\sin\frac{\theta}{2}\\
\sin\frac{\theta}{2}&\cos\frac{\theta}{2}\end{pmatrix}\mapsto\left(x+\mathrm{i}y,\mathrm{i}y\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\theta}\right),

(2.5)

so here
\theta
measures the angle clockwise from vertical.

Geodesics in
\mathbb{H}
are either vertical lines or semi-circles centred on the real line.
An oriented geodesic
\bm{c}\subset\mathbb{H}
is parametrized by

\bm{c}=\left\{g\begin{pmatrix}s&0\\
0&s^{-1}\end{pmatrix}\mathrm{i}:s>0\right\}

(2.6)

for some
g\in G
.
We let
\bm{c}_{0}
denote the vertical geodesic through
\mathrm{i}
, which corresponds to
g=I
in (
2.6
).
Any geodesic can be written as
\bm{c}=g\bm{c}_{0}
for some
g\in G
.

We say that a geodesic
\bm{c}
as in (
2.6
) is positively oriented if the backwards endpoint
\bm{c}^{-}
corresponding to
s\to 0
is less than the forward endpoint
\bm{c}^{+}
corresponding to
s\to\infty
, i.e. if the geodesic is traced from left to right.
In this case, we define the point
z_{\bm{c}}\in\mathbb{H}
to be the point on
\bm{c}
with the largest imaginary part, and we accordingly refer to it as the top of the geodesic.
We leave
z_{\bm{c}}
undefined for vertical or negatively oriented geodesics.

We let
\Gamma=\operatorname{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
, and we denote by
\Gamma_{\infty}
the stabilizer in
\Gamma
of the boundary point
\infty\in\partial\mathbb{H}
.
Explicitly,

\Gamma_{\infty}=\Gamma\cap\left\{\pm\begin{pmatrix}1&x\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}:x\in\mathbb{R}\right\}=\left\{\pm\begin{pmatrix}1&n\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}:n\in\mathbb{Z}\right\}.

(2.7)

For a geodesic
\bm{c}=g\bm{c}_{0}
, we let
\Gamma_{\bm{c}}
be the stabilizer of
\bm{c}
in
\Gamma
, so

\Gamma_{\bm{c}}=\Gamma\cap\left\{\pm g\begin{pmatrix}s&0\\
0&s^{-1}\end{pmatrix}g^{-1}:s>0\right\}.

(2.8)

If
\bm{c}
projects to a closed geodesic on the surface
\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H}
, then
\Gamma_{\bm{c}}
is infinite cyclic, up to sign:

\Gamma_{\bm{c}}=\left\{\pm g\begin{pmatrix}\varepsilon^{k}&0\\
0&\varepsilon^{-k}\end{pmatrix}g^{-1}:k\in\mathbb{Z}\right\},

(2.9)

for some
\varepsilon>1
, and the length of
\bm{c}
in
\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H}
is then
2\log\varepsilon
.

2.1
Roots and ideals

We recall that since
D\equiv 1\pmod{4}
, the quadratic order
\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{D}]
is not maximal, i.e. it is not the ring of integers of
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{D})
.
As such, not all ideals in
\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{D}]
are invertible.
The following lemma gives a criterion to determine if an ideal is invertible.
Here
(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{k})
denotes the ideal of a ring (which will be made clear by the context) generated by the ring elements
\alpha_{j}
.

Lemma 4
.

Let
D>0
be a square-free integer satisfying
D\equiv 1\pmod{4}
and
\mu\bmod m
satisfy
\mu^{2}\equiv D\pmod{m}
.
Then the ideal
I=(m,\sqrt{D}+\mu)\subseteq\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{D}]
is invertible if and only if either
m
or
\frac{D-\mu^{2}}{m}
is odd.
Moreover, in this case the inverse ideal of
I
is given by
I^{-1}=\frac{1}{m}(m,\sqrt{D}-\mu)
.

Before the proof we make the following remarks:
First, the parity of
\frac{D-\mu^{2}}{m}
is well-defined only if
m
is even, so the phrase “either
m
or
\frac{D-\mu^{2}}{m}
is odd” would be more correctly stated as “either
m
is odd or
m
is even and
\frac{D-\mu^{2}}{m}
is odd.”
Second, the proof of Proposition
5
below shows that any ideal
I\subseteq\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{D}]
can be expressed as a rational integer multiple of an ideal
(m,\sqrt{D}+\mu)
as in the lemma.

Proof.

Let
\overline{I}
denote the ideal
(m,\sqrt{D}-\mu)\subseteq\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{D}]
.
We have

\displaystyle I\overline{I}

\displaystyle=(m^{2},m(\sqrt{D}+\mu),m(\sqrt{D}-\mu),D-\mu^{2})

(2.10)

\displaystyle=m(m,\sqrt{D}+\mu,\sqrt{D}-\mu,(D-\mu^{2})/m)

\displaystyle=m(m,2\mu,\sqrt{D}-\mu,(D-\mu^{2})/m),

so
m(m,2\mu,\frac{D-\mu^{2}}{m})\subseteq I\overline{I}\subseteq m\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{D}]
.

Now suppose that
p\neq 2
divides all of
m,2\mu
and
\frac{D-\mu^{2}}{m}
.
Then
p^{2}
divides
D=m\frac{D-\mu^{2}}{m}+\mu^{2}
, which contradicts
D
being square-free.
It follows that if either
m
or
\frac{D-\mu^{2}}{m}
is odd, then
\gcd(m,2\mu,\frac{D-\mu^{2}}{m})=1
, and so
(m,2\mu,\frac{D-\mu^{2}}{m})=\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{D}]
.
Therefore
I\overline{I}=m\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{D}]
, i.e.
I^{-1}=\frac{1}{m}\overline{I}
.

Conversely, we assume
m
and
\frac{D-\mu^{2}}{m}
are even.
Dividing (
2.10
) by
m
, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m}I\overline{I}

\displaystyle=(m,2\mu,\mu+\sqrt{D},(D-\mu^{2})/m)

(2.11)

\displaystyle=(2,\mu+\sqrt{D})=(2,1+\sqrt{D})

as
\mu
is odd.
Letting
I_{0}=(2,1+\sqrt{D})
,
we have

\displaystyle I_{0}^{2}

\displaystyle=(2,1+\sqrt{D})^{2}=(4,2+2\sqrt{D},1+D+2\sqrt{D})

(2.12)

\displaystyle=2(2,1+\sqrt{D},(D-1)/2)=2I_{0}

since
\frac{D-1}{2}
is even.
As
I_{0}\neq(2)
, it is therefore not invertible, so either
I
or
\overline{I}
is not invertible, and it follows that neither are as they are conjugate.
∎

We now turn to the correspondence between roots and ideals.

Proposition 5
.

Let
D\equiv 1\pmod{4}
and let
I\subseteq\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{D}]
be an ideal with no rational integer divisors greater than
1
.
Then
I
has a
\mathbb{Z}
-basis
\{\beta_{1},\beta_{2}\}
, unique modulo the action of
\Gamma_{\infty}
, with the form

\begin{pmatrix}\beta_{1}\\
\beta_{2}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1&\mu\\
0&m\\
\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{D}\\
1\end{pmatrix},

(2.13)

where
\mu^{2}\equiv D\pmod{m}
.
Moreover,
I
is invertible if and only if either
m
or
\frac{D-\mu^{2}}{m}
is odd.

Conversely, given
\mu\bmod m
such that
\mu^{2}\equiv D\pmod{m}
,
the sublattice of
\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{D}]
with
\mathbb{Z}
-basis
\{\beta_{1},\beta_{2}\}
given above is an ideal in
\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{D}]
, and this ideal is invertible if and only if either
m
or
\frac{D-\mu^{2}}{m}
is odd.

Proof.

Any lattice
I\subseteq\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{D}]
has a unique basis
\{\beta_{1},\beta_{2}\}
in the Hermite normal form

\begin{pmatrix}\beta_{1}\\
\beta_{2}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}b_{1}&b_{2}\\
0&b_{3}\\
\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{D}\\
1\end{pmatrix},

(2.14)

where
b_{1}>0
and
b_{2}>b_{3}\geq 0
.
Left multiplying a matrix
\gamma\in\Gamma_{\infty}
on the basis is equivalent to adding multiples of
b_{3}
to
b_{2}
, so considering the basis modulo
\Gamma_{\infty}
is the same as considering
b_{2}\bmod{b_{3}}
.

Now, the sublattice
I
is an ideal if and only if
\sqrt{D}I\subseteq I
, as the quadratic order
\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{D}]
is generated by
\sqrt{D}
. The basis of
\sqrt{D}I
can be expressed by

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{D}\beta_{1}\\
\sqrt{D}\beta_{2}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}b_{1}&b_{2}\\
0&b_{3}\\
\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0&D\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}b_{1}&b_{2}\\
0&b_{3}\end{pmatrix}^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}\beta_{1}\\
\beta_{2}\end{pmatrix}

(2.15)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{b_{1}b_{3}}\begin{pmatrix}b_{2}b_{3}&-b_{2}^{2}+Db_{1}^{2}\\
b_{3}^{2}&-b_{2}b_{3}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\beta_{1}\\
\beta_{2}\end{pmatrix}.

Hence
I
is an ideal if and only if the entries of the matrix in the final step are all integers.
It is clear that we need
b_{1}\mid b_{2}
and
b_{1}\mid b_{3}
, so let
b_{2}=\mu b_{1}
and
b_{3}=mb_{1}
.
Moreover,
I
not having rational integer divisors is equivalent to
b_{1}
, which we now assume.
From the top right entry, we obtain the constraint
\mu^{2}\equiv D\pmod{m}
.
Finally, with the assumption that
I=(m,\sqrt{D}+\mu)
is invertible in
\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{D}]
, we have that either
m
or
\frac{D-\mu^{2}}{m}
is odd by Lemma
4
.

Conversely, suppose that
\mu\bmod m
satisfies
\mu^{2}\equiv D\pmod{m}
.
Then (
2.13
) demonstrates that given the basis in the form above, the sublattice
I
satisfies
\sqrt{D}I\subseteq I
as required.
Moreover, if either
m
or
\frac{D-\mu^{2}}{m}
is odd, Lemma
4
implies that
I
is invertible.
∎

We now turn to the roots that do not satisfy the invertibility condition of Lemma
4
.
As it happens, these roots naturally correspond to ideals in the maximal order
\mathbb{Z}[\tfrac{1+\sqrt{D}}{2}]
, where all ideals are invertible.

Proposition 6
.

Let
D\equiv 1\pmod{4}
and let
J\subseteq\mathbb{Z}[\frac{1+\sqrt{D}}{2}]
be an ideal with no rational integer divisors greater than
1
.
Then
J
has a
\mathbb{Z}
-basis
\{\delta_{1},\delta_{2}\}
, unique modulo the action of
\Gamma_{\infty}
, with the form

\begin{pmatrix}\delta_{1}\\
\delta_{2}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1&\frac{\mu-1}{2}\\
0&\frac{m}{2}\\
\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1+\sqrt{D}}{2}\\
1\end{pmatrix},

(2.16)

where
\mu^{2}\equiv D\pmod{m}
and both
m
and
\frac{D-\mu^{2}}{m}
are even.

Conversely, given
\mu\bmod m
such that
\mu^{2}\equiv D\pmod{m}
and both
m
and
\frac{D-\mu^{2}}{m}
are even, the sublattice of
\mathbb{Z}[\frac{1+\sqrt{D}}{2}]
with
\mathbb{Z}
-basis
\{\delta_{1},\delta_{2}\}
given above is an ideal in
\mathbb{Z}[\frac{1+\sqrt{D}}{2}]
.

Proof.

As above, any lattice
J\subseteq\mathbb{Z}[\frac{1+\sqrt{D}}{2}]
has a unique basis in Hermite normal form:

\begin{pmatrix}\delta_{1}\\
\delta_{2}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}b_{1}&b_{2}\\
0&b_{3}\\
\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1+\sqrt{D}}{2}\\
1\end{pmatrix},

(2.17)

where
b_{1}>0
and
b_{2}>b_{3}\geq 0
.
The sublattice
J
is an ideal if and only if
\frac{1+\sqrt{D}}{2}J\subseteq J
.
The basis of
\frac{1+\sqrt{D}}{2}J
can be expressed by

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1+\sqrt{D}}{2}\delta_{1}\\
\frac{1+\sqrt{D}}{2}\delta_{2}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}b_{1}&b_{2}\\
0&b_{3}\\
\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&\frac{D-1}{4}\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}b_{1}&b_{2}\\
0&b_{3}\end{pmatrix}^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}\delta_{1}\\
\delta_{2}\end{pmatrix}

(2.18)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{b_{1}b_{3}}\begin{pmatrix}(b_{1}+b_{2})b_{3}&-(b_{1}+b_{2})b_{2}+\frac{D-1}{4}b_{1}^{2}\\
b_{3}^{2}&-b_{2}b_{3}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\delta_{1}\\
\delta_{2}\end{pmatrix}.

As in the proof of Proposition
5
, we need
b_{1}
to divide both
b_{2}
and
b_{3}
for the matrix to have integer entries, so we set
b_{2}=\nu b_{1}
and
b_{3}=nb_{1}
, and as before we assume
b_{1}=1
.

From the top right entry, we obtain the quadratic congruence
\nu^{2}+\nu\equiv\frac{D-1}{4}\pmod{n}
, which is equivalent to
(2\nu+1)^{2}\equiv D\pmod{4}n
.
Let
\mu=2\nu+1
and
m=2n
, and note that
\frac{D-\mu^{2}}{m}
is even and
\mu^{2}\equiv D\pmod{m}
.

For the converse part, suppose that
\mu\bmod m
satisfies
\mu^{2}\equiv D\pmod{m}
and both
m=2n
and
\frac{D-\mu^{2}}{m}=2k
are even.
Further, we set
\mu=2\nu+1
as
m
even implies
\mu
is odd.
We have
4\nu^{2}+4\nu+1\equiv D\pmod{4n}
, so
\nu^{2}+\nu\equiv\frac{D-1}{4}\pmod{n}
.
From (
2.16
) it then follows that the sublattice
J
with basis
\{\delta_{1},\delta_{2}\}
as above satisfies
\frac{1+\sqrt{D}}{2}J\subseteq J
as required.
∎

We record the following lemma, whose proof is obvious, that gives a simple characterization of invertible ideals in the special case when
D\equiv 5\pmod{8}
.

Lemma 7
.

Let
D\equiv 5\pmod{8}
and
\mu\bmod m
satisfy
\mu^{2}\equiv D\pmod{m}
. Then
m
is even and
\frac{D-\mu^{2}}{m}
is odd if and only if
m\equiv 0\pmod{4}
.
In particular, the ideal
I=(m,\mu+\sqrt{D})\subseteq\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{D}]
is invertible if and only if
m\not\equiv 2\pmod{4}
.

As
D\equiv 1\pmod{4}
and
\mathbb{Z}[\frac{1+\sqrt{D}}{2}]
is the ring of integers of the number field
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{D})
, we may define the narrow class group of
\mathbb{Z}[\frac{1+\sqrt{D}}{2}]
in the usual way: the group of fractional ideals of
\mathbb{Z}[\tfrac{1+\sqrt{D}}{2}]
modulo ideals of the form
\zeta\mathbb{Z}[\tfrac{1+\sqrt{D}}{2}]
with
\zeta\in\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{D})
totally positive.
We recall that
\zeta=a+b\sqrt{D}\in\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{D})
is said to be totally positive if both
a+b\sqrt{D}>0
and
a-b\sqrt{D}>0
.

We define the narrow class group of
\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{D}]
in the same way, with the added restriction that the ideals are invertible.
That is, the narrow class group of
\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{D}]
is the group of invertible, fractional ideals of
\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{D}]
modulo ideals of the form
\xi\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{D}]
for some
\xi\in\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{D})
totally positive.
We let the narrow class numbers
h_{1}^{+}(D)
, respectively
h_{2}^{+}(D)
, denote the order of the narrow class group in
\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{D}]
, respectively
\mathbb{Z}[\tfrac{1+\sqrt{D}}{2}]
.

We now use representatives of the narrow class groups to find new bases for invertible ideals
I\subseteq\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{D}]
and ideals
J\subseteq\mathbb{Z}[\frac{1+\sqrt{D}}{2}]
.
We fix representatives
I_{k}
,
1\leq k\leq h_{1}^{+}(D)
of the narrow class group of
\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{D}]
, so that given any invertible ideal
I\subseteq\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{D}]
, there exists a totally positive
\xi\in I_{k}^{-1}
such that
I=\xi I_{k}
.

To conveniently write a basis for
I
using this equality, we embed
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{D})
inside
\mathbb{R}^{2}
via

a+b\sqrt{D}\mapsto(a+b\sqrt{D},a-b\sqrt{D}),

so that
\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{D}]
and
\mathbb{Z}[\tfrac{1+\sqrt{D}}{2}]
are lattices in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
For
\xi=a+b\sqrt{D}\in\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{D})
, we use the notation
\overline{\xi}=a-b\sqrt{D}
to represent its conjugate.

According to this embedding, given a
\mathbb{Z}
-basis
\{\beta_{k1},\beta_{k2}\}
of
I_{k}
, we substitute

\begin{pmatrix}\beta_{k1}\\
\beta_{k2}\end{pmatrix}\rightarrow\mathfrak{B}_{k}=\begin{pmatrix}\beta_{k1}&\overline{\beta}_{k1}\\
\beta_{k2}&\overline{\beta}_{k2}\end{pmatrix},

(2.19)

and we may assume that
\det\mathfrak{B}_{k}>0
.
Similarly, we write our basis for
\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{D}]
as

\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{D}\\
1\end{pmatrix}\rightarrow\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{D}&-\sqrt{D}\\
1&1\end{pmatrix}.

(2.20)

In this notation, we have that the basis
\{\xi\beta_{k1},\xi\beta_{k2}\}
of
\xi I_{k}
corresponds to the matrix

\begin{pmatrix}\xi\beta_{k1}&\overline{\xi}\overline{\beta}_{k1}\\
\xi\beta_{k2}&\overline{\xi}\overline{\beta}_{k2}\end{pmatrix}=\mathfrak{B}_{k}\begin{pmatrix}\xi&0\\
0&\overline{\xi}\end{pmatrix}.

(2.21)

Let
\varepsilon_{1}>1
be the generator of the group of totally positive units of
\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{D}]
, and we define

\displaystyle\Gamma_{1;k}

\displaystyle=\left\{\pm\mathfrak{B}_{k}\begin{pmatrix}\varepsilon_{1}^{s}&0\\
0&\varepsilon_{1}^{-s}\end{pmatrix}\mathfrak{B}_{k}^{-1}:s\in\mathbb{Z}\right\}

(2.22)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})\cap\left\{\pm\mathfrak{B}_{k}\begin{pmatrix}t&0\\
0&t^{-1}\end{pmatrix}\mathfrak{B}_{k}^{-1}:t>0\right\},

since
I_{k}=\varepsilon I_{k}
if and only if
\varepsilon
is a unit of
\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{D}]
.

Proposition 8
.

Let
D\equiv 1\pmod{4}
and
\mu\bmod m
satisfy
\mu^{2}\equiv D\pmod{m}
.
If either
m
or
\frac{D-\mu^{2}}{m}
is odd, then there exists a unique
1\leq k\leq h_{1}^{+}(D)
and a unique double coset
\Gamma_{\infty}\gamma\Gamma_{1;k}\in\Gamma_{\infty}\backslash\operatorname{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})/\penalty\Gamma_{1;k}
such that

\begin{pmatrix}1&\mu\\
0&m\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{D}&-\sqrt{D}\\
1&1\end{pmatrix}=\gamma\mathfrak{B}_{k}\begin{pmatrix}\xi&0\\
0&\overline{\xi}\end{pmatrix}

(2.23)

for some totally positive
\xi
.

Conversely, if for
\Gamma_{\infty}\gamma\Gamma_{1;k}\in\Gamma_{\infty}\backslash\operatorname{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})/\penalty\Gamma_{1;k}
there exists positive real numbers
\xi_{1}
,
\xi_{2}
such that

\gamma\mathfrak{B}_{k}\begin{pmatrix}\xi_{1}&0\\
0&\xi_{2}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1&*\\
0&*\\
\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{D}&-\sqrt{D}\\
1&1\end{pmatrix},

(2.24)

then in fact
\xi_{1}=\xi
and
\xi_{2}=\overline{\xi}
for some totally positive
\xi\in I_{k}^{-1}
and we have

\gamma\mathfrak{B}_{k}\begin{pmatrix}\xi&0\\
0&\overline{\xi}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1&\mu\\
0&m\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{D}&-\sqrt{D}\\
1&1\end{pmatrix},

(2.25)

where
\mu\bmod m
satisfies
\mu^{2}\equiv D\pmod{m}
and either
m
or
\frac{D-\mu^{2}}{m}
is odd.

Proof.

By Proposition
5
, the left side of (
2.23
) gives a basis for an invertible ideal
I\subseteq\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{D}]
.
Writing
I=\xi I_{k}
for some
k
and totally positive
\xi\in\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{D})
, we obtain another basis for
I
as in (
2.21
).
These bases must be related by some
\gamma\in\operatorname{GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
, giving (
2.23
). Furthermore, by comparing the sign of determinants of both sides, we in fact have
\gamma\in\Gamma=\operatorname{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
.

We note that considering
\mu
as a residue class modulo
m
is the same as considering the coset
\gamma
in
\Gamma_{\infty}\backslash\Gamma
.
Moreover, replacing
\xi
up to multiplication by the group of totally positive units in
\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{D}]
amounts to considering the coset
\gamma\Gamma_{1;k}
.
It follows that the double coset
\Gamma_{\infty}\gamma\Gamma_{1;k}
is uniquely determined.

Conversely, suppose that there exist positive real numbers
\xi_{1}
,
\xi_{2}
so that (
2.24
) holds.
We let
\{\beta^{\prime}_{k1},\beta^{\prime}_{k2}\}
be a basis of
I_{k}^{-1}
and observe that via the embedding
\mathbb{Q}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
, we may express
(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})=c_{1}\beta^{\prime}_{k1}+c_{2}\beta^{\prime}_{k2}
for some real numbers
c_{1},c_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
.
The claim that
\xi_{1}=\xi
and
\xi_{2}=\overline{\xi}
for some totally positive
\xi\in I_{k}^{-1}
is equivalent to
c_{1},c_{2}
being integers.

As
I_{k}^{-1}I_{k}=\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{D}]
, we define integers
a_{kij}
and
b_{kij}
by

\beta^{\prime}_{ki}\beta_{kj}=a_{kij}+b_{kij}\sqrt{D}.

(2.26)

This then yields

\mathfrak{B}_{k}\begin{pmatrix}\xi_{1}&0\\
0&\xi_{2}\end{pmatrix}=(c_{1}A_{k1}+c_{2}A_{k2})\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{D}&-\sqrt{D}\\
1&1\end{pmatrix},

(2.27)

where

A_{ki}=\begin{pmatrix}b_{ki1}&a_{ki1}\\
b_{ki2}&a_{ki2}\end{pmatrix}.

(2.28)

Hence,

B_{k}\begin{pmatrix}c_{1}\\
c_{2}\end{pmatrix}=\gamma^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix},

(2.29)

where

B_{k}=\begin{pmatrix}b_{k11}&b_{k21}\\
b_{k12}&b_{k22}\end{pmatrix}.

(2.30)

That
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
are in fact integers now follows immediately from Lemma
10
, which we prove later.
∎

Lemma 9
.

Let
M\in\mathrm{M}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
. If the entries of
M\begin{pmatrix}a\\
b\end{pmatrix}
are coprime for any coprime integers
a
and
b
, then
M\in\operatorname{GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
.

Proof.

As
M
is a
2\times 2
matrix with integer entries, it can be written in the Smith normal form

M=S_{1}\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_{1}&0\\
0&\lambda_{2}\end{pmatrix}S_{2},

(2.31)

where
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}
are non-negative integers and
S_{1},S_{2}\in\operatorname{GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
.
By considering the first column of the diagonal matrix, we have

MS_{2}^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix}=M\begin{pmatrix}a\\
b\end{pmatrix}=\lambda_{1}S_{1}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix},

(2.32)

for some coprime integers
a
and
b
. With the assumption on
M
, we must have
\lambda_{1}=1
.
By considering the second column, we similarly obtain
\lambda_{2}=1
, and therefore,
M\in\operatorname{GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
.
∎

Lemma 10
.

For any
k
, we have
B_{k}=\begin{pmatrix}b_{k11}&b_{k21}\\
b_{k12}&b_{k22}\end{pmatrix}\in\operatorname{GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
.

Proof.

Let
a,b
be any coprime integers. Then
\xi^{\prime}=a\beta_{k1}^{\prime}+b\beta_{k2}^{\prime}
is a primitive vector in
I_{k}^{-1}
. Thus, the invertible ideal
\xi^{\prime}I_{k}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{D}]
has no rational integer divisors greater than
1
. By Proposition
5
,
\xi^{\prime}I_{k}
has a basis of the form

\begin{pmatrix}1&\mu^{\prime}\\
0&m^{\prime}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{D}&-\sqrt{D}\\
1&1\end{pmatrix}=\gamma\mathfrak{B}_{k}\begin{pmatrix}\xi^{\prime}&0\\
0&\overline{\xi^{\prime}}\end{pmatrix},

(2.33)

arguing as we did in the proof of the first part of Proposition
8
.
By considering the first column of (
2.33
), we have

B_{k}\begin{pmatrix}a\\
b\end{pmatrix}=\gamma^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix},

(2.34)

for some
\gamma\in\operatorname{GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
.
As
a
and
b
are arbitrary coprime integers, (
2.34
) shows that
B_{k}
satisfies the condition in Lemma
9
.
∎

We now turn to ideals in
\mathbb{Z}[\tfrac{1+\sqrt{D}}{2}]
.
For
1\leq l\leq h_{2}^{+}(D)
, we fix representatives
J_{l}
of the narrow class group of
\mathbb{Z}[\frac{1+\sqrt{D}}{2}]
.
As before, for any ideal
J\subseteq\mathbb{Z}[\frac{1+\sqrt{D}}{2}]
there exists a totally positive
\zeta\in J_{l}^{-1}
such that
J=\zeta J_{l}
.
Using the embedding
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{D})\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
we associate a
\mathbb{Z}
-basis
\{\delta_{l1},\delta_{l2}\}
of
J_{l}
with the matrix

\begin{pmatrix}\delta_{l1}\\
\delta_{l2}\end{pmatrix}\rightarrow\mathfrak{D}_{l}=\begin{pmatrix}\delta_{l1}&\overline{\delta}_{l1}\\
\delta_{l2}&\overline{\delta}_{l2}\end{pmatrix},

(2.35)

and as before we may assume
\det(\mathfrak{D}_l)>0
.
Thus, the basis
\{\zeta\delta_{l1},\zeta\delta_{l2}\}
of
\zeta J_{l}
has basis matrix

\begin{pmatrix}\zeta\delta_{l1}&\overline{\zeta}\overline{\delta}_{l1}\\
\zeta\delta_{l2}&\overline{\zeta}\overline{\delta}_{l2}\end{pmatrix}=\mathfrak{D}_{l}\begin{pmatrix}\zeta&0\\
0&\overline{\zeta}\end{pmatrix}.

(2.36)

Letting
\varepsilon_{2}>1
be the generator of the group of totally positive units of
\mathbb{Z}[\frac{1+\sqrt{D}}{2}]
. We define

\displaystyle\Gamma_{2;l}

\displaystyle=\left\{\pm\mathfrak{D}_{l}\begin{pmatrix}\varepsilon_{2}^{s}&0\\
0&\varepsilon_{2}^{-s}\end{pmatrix}\mathfrak{D}_{l}^{-1}:s\in\mathbb{Z}\right\}

(2.37)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})\cap\left\{\pm\mathfrak{D}_{l}\begin{pmatrix}t&0\\
0&t^{-1}\end{pmatrix}\mathfrak{D}_{l}^{-1}:t>0\right\},

since
J_{l}=\varepsilon J_{l}
if and only if
\varepsilon
is a unit of
\mathbb{Z}[\frac{1+\sqrt{D}}{2}]
.

Using Proposition
6
in place of Proposition
5
, we obtain the following analogue of Proposition
8
.
We omit its proof as it is so similar to the proof above.

Proposition 11
.

Let
D\equiv 1\pmod{4}
and suppose
\mu\bmod m
satisfies
\mu^{2}\equiv D\pmod{m}
.
If both
m
and
\frac{D-\mu^{2}}{m}
are even, then there exists a unique

and a unique double coset
\Gamma_{\infty}\gamma\Gamma_{2;l}\in\Gamma_{\infty}\backslash\operatorname{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})/\penalty\Gamma_{2;l}
such that

\begin{pmatrix}1&\frac{\mu-1}{m}\\

(2.38)

for some totally positive

.

Conversely, if for

then in fact
\zeta_{1}=\zeta
and

for some totally positive
\zeta\in J_{l}^{-1}
and we have

\gamma\mathfrak{D}_{l}\begin{pmatrix}\zeta&0\\
0&\overline{\zeta}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1&\frac{\mu-1}{m}\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&\frac{m}{2}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1+\sqrt{D}}{2}&\frac{1-\sqrt{D}}{2}\\
1&1\end{pmatrix},

(2.40)

where

satisfies
\mu^{2}\equiv D\pmod{m}
and both

2.2
Roots and geodesics

We define oriented geodesics
\bm{c}_{I_{k}}
for
1\leq k\leq h_{1}^{+}(D)
, and
\bm{c}_{J_{l}}
for
1\leq l\leq h_{2}^{+}(D)
in
\mathbb{H}
by

\bm{c}_{I_{k}}=\left\{(\det\mathfrak{B}_{k})^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mathfrak{B}_{k}\begin{pmatrix}t&0\\
0&t^{-1}\end{pmatrix}\mathrm{i}:t>0\right\}

(2.41)

and

\bm{c}_{J_{l}}=\left\{(\det\mathfrak{D}_{l})^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mathfrak{D}_{l}\begin{pmatrix}t&0\\
0&t^{-1}\end{pmatrix}\mathrm{i}:t>0\right\}.

(2.42)

Recalling that the top
z_{\bm{c}}\in\mathbb{H}
of a positively oriented geodesic
\bm{c}
is the point on
\bm{c}
which has the largest imaginary part on
\bm{c}
, we now interpret Propositions
8
and
11
geometrically as follows.

Proposition 12
.

Let
D\equiv 1\pmod{4}
and let
\mu\bmod m
satisfy
\mu^{2}\equiv D\pmod{m}
. If either
m
or
\frac{D-\mu^{2}}{m}
is odd, then there exists a unique
1\leq k\leq h_{1}^{+}(D)
and a unique double coset
\Gamma_{\infty}\gamma\Gamma_{1;k}\in\Gamma_{\infty}\backslash\Gamma/\penalty\Gamma_{1;k}
such that

z_{\gamma\bm{c}_{I_{k}}}\equiv\frac{\mu}{m}+\mathrm{i}\frac{\sqrt{D}}{m}\pmod{\Gamma_{\infty}}.

(2.43)

Conversely, given
1\leq k\leq h_{1}^{+}(D)
and a double coset
\Gamma_{\infty}\gamma\Gamma_{1;k}
such that
\gamma\bm{c}_{I_{k}}
is positively oriented, there exists a unique positive integer
m
and a residue class
\mu\bmod m
with either
m
or
\frac{D-\mu^{2}}{m}
odd satisfying (
2.43
).

Proof.

In the identification of
G/\{\pm I\}
with the unit tangent bundle
\mathrm{T}_{1}(\mathbb{H})
, the matrices

\begin{pmatrix}*&*\\
0&*\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\cos\frac{\pi}{4}&-\sin\frac{\pi}{4}\\
\sin\frac{\pi}{4}&\cos\frac{\pi}{4}\end{pmatrix}

(2.44)

are identified with points in
\mathbb{H}
whose tangent vectors point horizontally to the right.
The intersection, if it exists, of these matrices with those of the form

(\det\mathfrak{B}_{k})^{-\frac{1}{2}}\gamma\mathfrak{B}_{k}\begin{pmatrix}*&0\\
0&*\end{pmatrix}

(2.45)

is the point on
\gamma\bm{c}_{I_{k}}
which has a right horizontal tangent vector. Such a point exists exactly when
\gamma\bm{c}_{I_{k}}
is positively oriented, and its imaginary part is the largest on the geodesic
\gamma\bm{c}_{I_{k}}
. Scaling and rewriting (
2.23
), we have

(\det\mathfrak{B}_{k})^{-\frac{1}{2}}\gamma\mathfrak{B}_{k}\begin{pmatrix}t&0\\
0&t^{-1}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1&\frac{\mu}{m}\\
0&1\\
\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\left(\frac{\sqrt{D}}{m}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}&0\\
0&\left(\frac{\sqrt{D}}{m}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\\
\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}&-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\\
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}&\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\\
\end{pmatrix},

finishing the proof via Proposition
8
.

Proposition 19
.

Let
\varepsilon_{1}
and
\varepsilon_{2}
be totally positive fundamental units of
\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{D}]
and
\mathbb{Z}[\frac{1+\sqrt{D}}{2}]
respectively.

(i)

If
D\equiv 1\pmod{8}
, then
\varepsilon_{1}=\varepsilon_{2}
.

(ii)

If
D\equiv 5\pmod{8}

\varepsilon_{1}=\varepsilon_{2}
or
\varepsilon_{1}=\varepsilon_{2}^{3}

Proof.

Set
\varepsilon_{2}=a+b\frac{1+\sqrt{D}}{2}
with
a,b\in\mathbb{Z}

(i) We have
\operatorname{N}(\varepsilon_{2})=a^{2}+ab-b^{2}\frac{D-1}{4}=1
, and since
D\equiv 1\pmod{8}
, we know
\frac{D-1}{4}
is even.
If
b
is odd, then
a(a+b)
is even and so is
\operatorname{N}(\varepsilon_{2})
, a contradiction. Hence,
b
is even, so
\varepsilon_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{D}]
as required.

(ii) Now, in the case that
D\equiv 5\pmod{8}
, if
b
is even, then
\varepsilon_{1}=\varepsilon_{2}
as before.
Suppose that
b
is odd, so
\varepsilon_{2}\not\in\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{D}]
, that is,
\varepsilon_{1}\neq\varepsilon_{2}
. As
a^{2}+ab-b^{2}\frac{D-1}{4}=1
,

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=a^{2}+ab(1+\sqrt{D})+b^{2}\tfrac{D-1}{4}+b^{2}\tfrac{1+\sqrt{D}}{2}

(3.45)

\displaystyle=1-ab+b^{2}\tfrac{D-1}{2}+b(b+2a)\tfrac{1+\sqrt{D}}{2}.

Since
b(b+2a)
is odd,
\varepsilon_{2}^{2}\not\in\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{D}]
so
\varepsilon_{1}\neq\varepsilon_{2}^{2}
. Now,

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{2}^{3}

\displaystyle=a-a^{2}b+ab^{2}(D-1)+b^{3}\tfrac{D-1}{4}+b\left(1+2a^{2}+2ab+b^{2}\tfrac{D+1}{2}\right)\tfrac{1+\sqrt{D}}{2}

(3.46)

\displaystyle=(a+ab^{2}D-b)+b(3+b^{2}D)\tfrac{1+\sqrt{D}}{2}.

As
b
is odd,
b(3+b^{2}D)
is even. This implies
\varepsilon_{2}^{3}\in\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{D}]
and so
\varepsilon_{1}=\varepsilon_{2}^{3}
.
∎

q
.

5
Negative
D

In this final section, we briefly sketch analogous results in the case that
D
is negative.
The negative setting is simpler in the sense that we consider
\Gamma
-orbits of points in
\mathbb{H}
instead of the tops of geodesics, and their statistical distribution has been thoroughly studied.
However, algebraically the
D<0
setting is almost identical with the following modification: instead of embedding
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{D})
into a two-dimensional real subspace of
\mathbb{C}^{2}
via
(\xi,\overline{\xi})
as in the real case, we embed
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{D})
inside
\mathbb{R}^{2}
by

a+b\sqrt{D}\mapsto(a,b\sqrt{|D|}).

The first embedding is transformed into the second by the matrix
P=\begin{pmatrix}-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}&\frac{1}{2}\\
\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}&\frac{1}{2}\end{pmatrix}
.
Since

\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{D}&-\sqrt{D}\\
1&1\end{pmatrix}P=\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{|D|}&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}

(5.1)

and

P^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}\xi&0\\
0&\overline{\xi}\end{pmatrix}P=\begin{pmatrix}\operatorname{Re}\xi&-\operatorname{Im}\xi\\
\operatorname{Im}\xi&\operatorname{Re}\xi\end{pmatrix},

(5.2)

then rewriting (
2.23
), we have

\begin{pmatrix}1&\mu\\
0&m\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{|D|}&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}=\gamma\widetilde{\mathfrak{B}}_{k}\begin{pmatrix}\operatorname{Re}\xi&-\operatorname{Im}\xi\\
\operatorname{Im}\xi&\operatorname{Re}\xi\end{pmatrix},

(5.3)

where

\widetilde{\mathfrak{B}}_{k}=\mathfrak{B}_{k}P=\begin{pmatrix}\operatorname{Im}\beta_{k1}&\operatorname{Re}\beta_{k1}\\
\operatorname{Im}\beta_{k2}&\operatorname{Re}\beta_{k2}\end{pmatrix}.

(5.4)

We note that the matrix on the right of (
5.3
) stabilizes the point
\mathrm{i}\in\mathbb{H}
, as opposed to stabilizing the geodesic
\{\mathrm{i}y:y>0\}\subset\mathbb{H}
in the real setting.

The left side of (
5.3
) yields

\begin{pmatrix}1&\mu\\
0&m\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{|D|}&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\mathrm{i}=\frac{\mu}{m}+\mathrm{i}\frac{\sqrt{|D|}}{m},

(5.5)

and the right side of (
5.3
) yields

\gamma\begin{pmatrix}\operatorname{Im}\beta_{k1}&\operatorname{Re}\beta_{k1}\\
\operatorname{Im}\beta_{k2}&\operatorname{Re}\beta_{k2}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\operatorname{Re}\xi&-\operatorname{Im}\xi\\
\operatorname{Im}\xi&\operatorname{Re}\xi\end{pmatrix}\mathrm{i}=\gamma\frac{\xi\beta_{k1}}{\xi\beta_{k2}}=\gamma z_{I_{k}},\;\text{where}\;z_{I_{k}}=\frac{\beta_{k1}}{\beta_{k2}}.

(5.6)

Therefore,
\gamma z_{I_{k}}=\frac{\mu}{m}+\mathrm{i}\frac{\sqrt{|D|}}{m}
. With a similar construction as in the positive
D
case, we have the following analogue of Theorem
22
.

Theorem 24
.

Let
D<0
be a square-free integer satisfying
D\equiv 1\pmod{4}
and
h_{1}
be the class number of
\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{D}]
. Given
n>0
and
\nu\bmod n
with
\nu^{2}\equiv D\pmod{n}
, there exists a finite set of points
\{z_{I_{1}},\cdots,z_{I_{h_{1}}}\}
in
\mathbb{H}
with the following properties:

(i)

For
m\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\mu\bmod m
satisfy
\mu^{2}\equiv D\pmod{m}
such that
m\equiv 0\pmod{n}
and
\mu\equiv\nu\pmod{n}
. If either
m
or
\frac{D-\mu^{2}}{m}
is odd, then there exists a unique
k
and a double coset
\Gamma_{\infty}\gamma\Gamma_{1;k}\in\Gamma_{\infty}\backslash\Gamma_{0}(n)/\penalty\Gamma_{1;k}
such that

\gamma z_{I_{k}}\equiv\frac{\mu}{m}+\mathrm{i}\frac{\sqrt{|D|}}{m}\pmod{\Gamma_{\infty}}.

(5.7)

(ii)

Conversely, given
k
and a double coset
\Gamma_{\infty}\gamma\Gamma_{1;k}\in\Gamma_{\infty}\backslash\Gamma_{0}(n)/\penalty\Gamma_{1;k}
, there exists a unique positive integer
m
and a residue class
\mu\bmod m
satisfying (
5.7
) and either
m
or
\frac{D-\mu^{2}}{m}
being odd.

In the same way, we obtain the analogue of Theorem
23
.

Theorem 25
.

Let
D<0
be a square-free integer satisfying
D\equiv 1\pmod{4}
and
h_{2}
be the class number of
\mathbb{Z}[\frac{1+\sqrt{D}}{2}]
. Given
n>0
and
\nu\bmod n
with
\nu^{2}\equiv D\pmod{n}
, there exists a finite set of points
\{z_{J_{1}},\cdots,z_{J_{h_{2}}}\}
in
\mathbb{H}
with the following properties:

(i)

For even
m\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\mu\bmod m
satisfy
\mu^{2}\equiv D\pmod{m}
and
\frac{D-\mu^{2}}{m}
even such that
m\equiv 0\pmod{n}
and
\mu\equiv\nu\pmod{n}
. If
n
is odd (resp.
n
is even), then there exists a unique
l
and a double coset
\Gamma_{\infty}\gamma\Gamma_{2;l}\in\Gamma_{\infty}\backslash\Gamma_{0}(n)/\penalty\Gamma_{2;l}

\gamma z_{J_{l}}\equiv\frac{\mu}{m}+\mathrm{i}\frac{\sqrt{D}}{m}\pmod{\Gamma_{\infty}}.

(5.8)

(ii)

Conversely, given
l
and a double coset
\Gamma_{\infty}\gamma\Gamma_{J;l}\in\Gamma_{\infty}\backslash\Gamma_{0}(n)/\penalty\Gamma_{2;l}
(resp.
{\Gamma_{\infty}\backslash\Gamma_{0}(\frac{n}{2})/\penalty\Gamma_{2;l}}

m

\mu\bmod m

5.8
) and
\frac{D-\mu^{2}}{m}

Combining these two theorems, we obtain the analogue of Theorem
