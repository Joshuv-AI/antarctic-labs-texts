From Turing patterns to chimera states in the 2D Brusselator model Preprint: AIP/123-QED
A. Provata

Abstract
The Brusselator has been used as a prototype model for autocatalytic reactions, and in particular for the Belouzov-Zhabotinsky reaction. When coupled at the diffusive limit, the Brusselator undergoes a Turing bifurcation resulting in the formation of classical Turing patterns, such as spots, stripes and spirals in 2 spatial dimensions. In the present study we use generic nonlocally coupled Brusselators and show that in the limit of the coupling range R → 1 R\to 1 (diffusive limit), the classical Turing patterns are recovered, while for intermediate coupling ranges and appropriate parameter values chimera states are produced. This study demonstrates how the parameters of a typical nonlinear oscillator can be tuned so that the coupled system passes from spatially stable Turing structures to dynamical spatiotemporal chimera states.



Oscillating reactions, also known as chemical clocks, have been studied intensively in the
past, with notable examples
the Bray-Liebhafsky reaction (1921), the Belousov-Zhabotinsky reaction (1958),
the Briggs-Rauscher reaction (1972) and the Chlorite-Iodide-Malonic Acid (CIMA) reaction (1990).
They are characterized by periodical color changing
or periodic gas emissions and this periodic activity
was earlier considered as a contradiction to common sense.
The explanation of the mechanisms behind the unexpected
concentration oscillations was proposed in the 1970’s,
and relies on the nonlinear nature of the interactions between the species,
which give rise to a limit cycle following a Hopf bifurcation scenario
Epstein and Pojman 1998
.
The same nonlinear mechanisms are responsible for the emergence of spectacular Turing
patterns when spatial diffusion is taken into account, as demonstrated by Alan Turing in his 1952
seminal paper
Turing 1952
. In the present study we quest the existence of a
different unexpected spatiotemporal effect, the chimera states, which are mainly observed
in nonlinear reactive dynamics at intermediate coupling ranges, whereas the Turing patterns
emerge at the limit of local diffusive coupling. To that purpose, we study the generic Brusselator
model
Nicolis and Prigogine 1977
;
Lefever and Nicolis 1971
, which
was proposed as a reduction of the complex chemical mechanism representing
the Belouzov-Zhabotinsky reaction.
For this model we show, in a unifying description, that by appropriate choice of the
parameter values and coupling ranges we can pass from the spatial Turing patterns to the
oscillating spatiotemporal chimera states. In the simulations coupled
Brusselators linked in 2D torus
geometry are employed, where both Turing patterns and chimera states demonstrate
spectacular formations.

I
Introduction

The motivation of the present study is to describe, in a unifying way, the emergence of
spatial structures and spatiotemporal patterns in different regions of the parameter space
in the same dynamical system. For this reason we use a typical nonlinear dynamical system
with minimal number of variables (two), the Brusselator, constructed specifically to model the
complex spatiotemporal organization of the Belouzov-Zhabotinsky reaction (see
Nicolis 1995

and references therein). For this model, we numerically demonstrate
that it is possible to drive the system from spatial Turing
pattern to spatiotemporal oscillating chimera states
by controlling the range of the diffusion
from classical (local) diffusion to intermediate range (nonlocal) diffusion with appropriate
choice of the reaction rates (parameters).

Turing patterns were first predicted in 1952 by Alan Turing in a set of
nonlinear PDEs
describing a hypothetical chemical reaction, which involved autocatalytic steps
and species diffusion
Turing 1952
.
Experimentally, these states were observed in several reactive systems beyond
the Belouzov-Zhabotinsky, such as the Bray–Liebhafsky,
the Briggs–Rauscher and the Chlorite-Iodide-Malonic Acid (CIMA) reactions

Epstein and Showalter 1996
;
Mikhailov and Showalter 2006
;
Winfree 1978
;
Kepper et al. 1991
;
Castets et al. 1990

Regarding the numerical evidence of Turing patterns, until the
late 1980’s it was generally admitted in the literature that

far from equilibrium conditions, … have not yet been observed in a clear-cut way”

As earlier stated, in the present study
we consider a well known reaction diffusion scheme, the Brusselator,
proposed as a minimal prototype scheme for demonstrating the effects of nonlinear
interactions in chemistry. Its minimal dynamics involves two interacting variables with
3rd order nonlinearities and an autocatalytic step.
Numerous studies in the past have shown that this model
gives rise to interesting Turing patterns in 1D, 2D and 3D
spatial geometries, when normal diffusion in the form of
nearest neighbor interactions is included in the dynamics
and the parameter values are chosen in the Turing bifurcation regime

;

In the sequel, we extend gradually the range of interactions,
going from nearest neighbors (normal diffusion) to nonlocal, intermediate ranges
where the chimera states prevail. In parallel, we determine
the reaction rates and parameter ranges which trigger the appearance of
complex synchronization phenomena and chimera states in two spatial dimensions.

II
The Model

In this section we first present the ODEs of the uncoupled Brusselator
model and discuss, in brief, the Hopf bifurcation scenario giving rise to
a limit cycle. Next, we present the nonlocally coupled Brusselator network and
discuss the emergence of a Turing bifurcation at the limit of local coupling.
In the last part of this section, we introduce some quantitative indices
for quantifying the spatial and temporal properties of the system.

II.1
The uncoupled Brusselator Model

As discussed in the Introduction, the Brusselator model was introduced in late 1960’s
by R. Lefever, G. Nicolis and I. Prigogine as a minimal, two-variable, autocatalytic model producing all
spatiotemporal complexity of the Belouzov-Zhabotinsky oscillatory
reaction
Nicolis and Prigogine 1977
;
Lefever and Nicolis 1971
.
It consists of two ODEs of 3rd order, with two variables,
\displaystyle X
and
\displaystyle Y
, and two
control parameters,
\displaystyle A
and
\displaystyle B
, as follows:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{dX}{dt}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=A-(B+1)X+X^{2}Y

(1a)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{dY}{dt}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=BX-X^{2}Y.

(1b)

In Eqs. (
1
), the variables
\displaystyle X
and
\displaystyle Y

take positive values since they were first constructed to represent chemical species concentrations,
while the parameters
\displaystyle A
and
\displaystyle B
represent the initial product concentrations and remain constant
(considered as sources).

The Brusselator model, Eqs. (
1
), has a single fixed point,
\displaystyle X_{s}
,

\displaystyle\displaystyle(X_{s},Y_{s})=(A,B/A).

(2)

Stability analysis around
\displaystyle(X_{s},Y_{s})
reveals that this fixed point changes its asymptotic stability
when the parameter
\displaystyle B
attains its critical value
\displaystyle B_{c}
:

\displaystyle\displaystyle B_{c}=A^{2}+1.

(3)

Namely, for

\displaystyle B<B_{c}=A^{2}+1
,
\displaystyle(X_{s},Y_{s})
is a stable node or
stable focus and the phase space trajectories are attracted to the
fixed point. At
\displaystyle B=B_{c}
the dynamics around the fixed point changes
from asymptotic stability to instability
and beyond this point a limit cycle is created
Nicolis 1995
. Because limit cycles are stable
oscillators under reasonable perturbations, they are prerequisites for the emergence of the
complex synchronized states, chimera states, when many limit cycles are coupled in networks.
For this reason, in section
IV
the parameter values
\displaystyle A=1.0
and
\displaystyle B=2.5-3.5
will be used
(such that
\displaystyle B>A^{2}+1
),
when the network composed by Brusselator units will be tuned to produce chimera states.

On the other hand, in the Brusselator model
the Turing structures are produced away from the regime of limit cycles
and under the Turing bifurcation conditions, where the coupling terms need to be taken into
account. For this reason, the discussion on the precise Brusselator parameter values
which lead to the emergence of Turing structures is postponed till section
III
,
after the coupling terms have been included.

II.2
The coupled Brusselator Model

In this section, spatial coupling is introduced between units in 2D, square lattice geometry.
Earlier studies have discovered spectacular Turing patterns in 2D, such as dots and
stripes, and the aim of the present work is to clarify the different parameter domains in
the 2D Brusselator network
where the Turing patterns and the chimera states reside.
On the square
lattice network containing
\displaystyle N\times N
units,
the Brusselator located at position coordinates
\displaystyle(i,j)
is
described by the time
dependent variables
\displaystyle X_{ij}
and
\displaystyle Y_{ij}
. The communication between the Brusselators
is achieved by the coupling
matrix
\displaystyle C
whose elements,
\displaystyle c_{ij:kl}
,
represent the strength of the link between Brusselators at
positions
\displaystyle(i,j)
and
\displaystyle(k,l)
on the network, with
\displaystyle i,j,k,l\leq N
.
Recent investigations
of synchronization phenomena on complex networks have shown that coupling matrices
with strong cross-coupling terms
\displaystyle(w_{XY},\>w_{YX})
and both excitatory (positive)
and inhibitory (negative) terms
are necessary (but not sufficient) for partial synchronization in the form of chimera states

Omelchenko et al. 2013
;
Omel’Chenko 2018
.
Under these conditions, the
nonlocally coupled Brusselator dynamics on the 2D square lattice is governed by the following equations:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{dX_{ij}}{dt}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=A-(B+1)X_{ij}+X_{ij}^{2}Y_{ij}+\sum_{(k,l)}c_{kl:ij}\left[w_{XX}(X_{kl}-X_{ij})+w_{XY}(Y_{kl}-Y_{ij})\right]

(4a)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{dY_{ij}}{dt}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=BX_{ij}-X_{ij}^{2}Y_{ij}+\sum_{(k,l)}c_{kl:ij}\left[w_{YX}(X_{kl}-X_{ij})+w_{YY}(Y_{kl}-Y_{ij})\right]

(4b)

In Eqs.
4
, we implicitly assume that all Brusselators are identical since they
have the same parameters
\displaystyle A
and
\displaystyle B
.

The coupling matrix
\displaystyle C
is limited by
the range
\displaystyle R
and strength
\displaystyle\sigma

of the nonlocal interactions. The elements
\displaystyle c_{ij:kl}
representing the interactions
between Brusselators at positions
\displaystyle(i,j)
and
\displaystyle(k,l)
have the form:

\displaystyle\displaystyle c_{ij:kl}=\begin{cases}\dfrac{\sigma}{\displaystyle{(2R+1)^{2}-1}},&\text{when}\>\>i-R\leq k\leq i+R,\>\>\>j-R\leq l\leq j+R\\
\>\>\>\>\>\>\>\>\>\>\>\>\>\>0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(5)

Namely, the Brusselator at position
\displaystyle(i,j)
is coupled with all other elements located
only within a rectangle
of size

\displaystyle(2R+1)\>{\rm X}\>(2R+1)
, whose center is at position
\displaystyle(i,j)
. Each nonzero
link carries constant weight (coupling strength)
\displaystyle\sigma
,
while the overall contribution is normalized
by the total number of nonzero links, which is
\displaystyle(2R+1)^{2}-1
. In Eqs. (
4
) and (
5
),
periodic boundary conditions on the torus are considered and, therefore, all indices
\displaystyle i,j,k,l,

are taken

\displaystyle\mod N
.

Apart for the coupling matrix
\displaystyle C
between the nodes of the network,
a rotational matrix,
\displaystyle W
, is introduced to account for the cross-coupling terms.
\displaystyle W
contains
both diagonal coupling, in the form
\displaystyle(w_{XX},w_{YY})
and
cross coupling
\displaystyle(w_{XY},w_{YX})
and is parameterized by a single parameter
\displaystyle\varphi
,
which is close to
\displaystyle\pi/2
if cross-coupling is dominant
Omelchenko et al. 2013
.
This coupling phase
\displaystyle\varphi
is similar to the phase-lag parameter
\displaystyle\alpha
of the
paradigmatic Kuramoto phase oscillator model, which is widely used to generically
describe coupled oscillator networks. The coupling phase is necessary for the
development of nontrivial partial synchronization patterns, as has been shown for the Kuramoto model

Omel’Chenko et al. 2010
and
for the FHN model
Omelchenko et al. 2013
.
This rotational matrix
\displaystyle W
is also used in
Refs.
Omelchenko et al. 2015
;
Schmidt et al. 2017
;
Ramlow et al. 2019
;
Mikhaylenko et al. 2019

and has the form:

W=\begin{pmatrix}w_{XX}&w_{XY}\\
w_{YX}&w_{YY}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\cos{\varphi}&\sin{\varphi}\\
-\sin{\varphi}&\cos{\varphi}\end{pmatrix}.

(6)

In the present study different values of the coupling range
\displaystyle R

and the coupling phase

\displaystyle 0\leq\varphi\leq 2\pi
are examined to determine the domains in the
\displaystyle(\varphi,R)
parameter
intervals where the Turing patterns or the chimera states dominate.

II.3
Quantitative measures

The synchrony/asynchrony in the coupled Brusselator system
can be verified using the mean phase velocity profile,
as in most studies where many oscillators are interlinked.
The mean phase velocity
\displaystyle\omega_{ij}

accounts for the average number of cycles that oscillator at position
\displaystyle(i,j)

has performed in the time unit
Omelchenko et al. 2015
;
Tsigkri-DeSmedt et al. 2018
. Let us call

\displaystyle Q_{ij}
the number of complete cycles
that oscillator
\displaystyle(i,j)
has performed during
a certain time interval
\displaystyle\Delta T
. Then
\displaystyle\omega_{ij}
is defined as:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\omega_{ij}=2\pi\frac{Q_{ij}}{\Delta T}=2\pi f_{ij}\>\>,

(7)

where
\displaystyle f_{ij}
is the frequency of the oscillator at position
\displaystyle(i,j)
.
Because the definitions of frequency and mean phase
velocity differ by just a factor of
\displaystyle 2\pi
, in the following the two expressions
(“frequency” and “mean phase velocity”) are used interchangeably.

Chimera states are characterized by
a distribution of frequencies, while the coherent states are characterized by a single, common
frequency. Full incoherence may also be characterized by a single, common frequency, in some cases.
Let us denote by
\displaystyle\omega_{\rm min}=\min\{\omega_{ij}\}
,
\displaystyle 1\leq i,j\leq N
,
the minimum mean phase velocity recorded in the
Brusselator network. Similarly,
\displaystyle\omega_{\rm max}=\max\{\omega_{ij}\}
,
\displaystyle 1\leq i,j\leq N
, is the maximum mean phase velocity observed.
The difference

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Delta\omega=\omega_{\rm max}-\omega_{\rm min},

(8)

is a measure of the extent of
the mean phase velocity distribution in the system. We may assume
that if
\displaystyle\Delta\omega>2*\pi/\Delta T
,
then the difference of the mean phase velocity between nodes can be considered significant
and may be an indication of the establishment of a chimera state in the network.
If
\displaystyle\Delta\omega<2*\pi/\Delta T
, this might be considered as a one-cycle over- or under-estimation
and can be regarded as the error threshold.

The Kuramoto order parameter is another measure often used to quantify coherence/incoherence
in the system
Kuramoto and Battogtokh 2002
;
Bick et al. 2016
.
Let us denote
\displaystyle\theta_{ij}
the instantaneous phase of Brusselator at position
\displaystyle(i,j)
,
which is defined as
\displaystyle\theta_{ij}=\arctan{\left[Y_{ij}/X_{ij}\right]}
. The Kuramoto order parameter
\displaystyle Z

is defined globally, over the entire system as:

Z=\frac{1}{N^{2}}\displaystyle{\left|\sum_{i,j}^{N}\mathrm{e}^{+i\theta_{ij}}\right|}.

(9)

In Eq. (
9
), if the system is synchronous and all phases are identical, then
\displaystyle Z=1
.
If all phases are random as in the asynchronous state, the various phases in the sum
of Eq. (
9
) cancel out and
\displaystyle Z=0
. For intermediate cases, where synchronous and asynchronous
regions are present,
\displaystyle 0<Z<1
, and this is the domain where chimera states can be observed.
On the other hand, even in cases that the oscillators in the network have identical mean phase
velocities but they operate under constant phase lags, they can be mistakenly considered as
chimera states, because
\displaystyle Z<1
.
Therefore, both measures
\displaystyle\Delta\omega>0
and
\displaystyle 0<Z<1
are necessary to quantify synchronization
in networks of coupled elements.

Other measures such as the spatial and/or temporal Fourier spectra can be used
to further verify the spatial and/or temporal order of the network nodes, respectively.

III
Turing Structures in 2D

Classical Turing structures are obtained when a nonlinear reactive system (the Brusselator
in the present case) is subjected to interactions in the form of diffusion. The diffusion terms which
are added to the original Brusselator
model, Eqs. (
1
), involve the Laplacian operator and
have the form
\displaystyle D_{X}\nabla^{2}X
for Eq. (
1a
) and

\displaystyle D_{Y}\nabla^{2}Y
for Eq. (
1b
). The parameters
\displaystyle D_{X}
and
\displaystyle D_{Y}
represent the diffusion coefficients
and Eqs. (
1
) then become:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{dX}{dt}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=A-(B+1)X+X^{2}Y+D_{X}\nabla^{2}X

(10a)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{dY}{dt}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=BX-X^{2}Y+D_{Y}\nabla^{2}Y.

(10b)

The study of these equations
demonstrates that the fixed point
\displaystyle(X_{s},Y_{s})
undergoes a Turing bifurcation at a
critical value
\displaystyle B^{T}_{c}
of the parameter B, such that
Nicolis 1995
;
Verdasca et al. 1992
;
Pena and Perez-Garcia 2001
:

\displaystyle\displaystyle B^{T}_{c}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle(1+\eta A)^{2},

\displaystyle\displaystyle\eta

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left(\frac{D_{X}}{D_{Y}}\right)^{1/2}<(1+A^{2})^{1/2}-1.

(11)

In addition, the discretization in space of the Laplacian operator retains only interactions with

A

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{dX_{ij}}{dt}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=A-(B+1)X_{ij}+X_{ij}^{2}Y_{ij}+\frac{D_{X}}{h^{2}}\sum_{\{k,l\}}\left[X_{kl}-X_{ij}\right]

(12a)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{dY_{ij}}{dt}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=BX_{ij}-X_{ij}^{2}Y_{ij}+\frac{D_{Y}}{h^{2}}\sum_{\{k,l\}}\left[Y_{kl}-2Y_{ij}\right].

(12b)

Here the indices vary as
\displaystyle i-1\leq k\leq i+1
and
\displaystyle j-1\leq l\leq j+1
, (i.e., the coupling range

\displaystyle R=1
) and
\displaystyle h
denotes the lattice constant. Equations (

)
are of the same form as Eqs. (

\displaystyle c_{ij;kl}\>w_{XX}=D_{X}/h^{2}
,

\displaystyle c_{ij;kl}\>w_{YY}=D_{Y}/h^{2}

\displaystyle w_{XY}=w_{YX}=0

\displaystyle R=1

VI
Conclusions

We have investigated the conditions under which Turing patterns and chimera states
can be produced in the same nonlinear dynamical system. Using as exemplary case the Brusselator model,
we have demonstrated Turing patterns for parameter regions where the single node dynamics presents
fixed points.
The same dynamical system exhibits chimera states if the parameters of the
single element are chosen in the oscillatory (limit cycle) regime. Connectivity-wise,
for the emergence of the Turing patterns small connectivity neighborhoods are required, mimicking
the nearest neighbor, Laplacian diffusion, while for the production of chimera states the
connectivity regions need to be of intermediate range.

Based on the above results and discussions,
we may add that it is not possible to obtain chimera states
and Turing patterns for the same values of parameters
\displaystyle A
and
\displaystyle B
in the Brusselator model,
evenif the other (connectivity) parameters are different.
This is because for the emergence of Turing patterns (stable steady states)
the single element dynamics need to lead to
fixed points, while for the emergence of chimera states (oscillatory steady states)
a limit cycle is necessary in the
single element dynamics. The above conclusions
hold provided that the coupling terms do not drastically alter
the nodal dynamics. Additional numerical and analytical studies using
different coupled nonlinear
models are necessary to further elucidate the necessary and sufficient conditions for the
presence of chimera states in these systems.

Appendix A
Discrete form of Brusselator with diffusion

In this appendix we discretize the Brusselator model with diffusion and bring
it to the general
form, Eq. (
4
), in order to use a generic system of differential equations,
both for the production
of Turing patterns and chimera states (at different regions of the parameter space).

Starting from Eq. (
10
), we specify the location
\displaystyle(i,j)
of a Brusselator
node and analyze the
Laplacian operator around this location. Then, Eq. (
10
) reduces to:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{dX_{ij}}{dt}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=A-(B+1)X_{ij}+X_{ij}^{2}Y_{ij}+D_{X}\nabla^{2}X_{ij}

(13a)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{dY_{ij}}{dt}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=BX_{ij}-X_{ij}^{2}Y_{ij}+D_{Y}\nabla^{2}Y_{ij}.

(13b)

where
\displaystyle\nabla^{2}
is the Laplacian operator in two dimensions and
\displaystyle D_{X}
,
\displaystyle D_{Y}

are the diffusion constants of the
\displaystyle X
and
\displaystyle Y
variables, respectively. By expanding the Laplacian
operator in space around position
\displaystyle(i,j)
we obtain:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{dX_{ij}}{dt}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=A-(B+1)X_{ij}+X_{ij}^{2}Y_{ij}+\frac{D_{X}}{(\Delta x)^{2}}\left[X_{i+1,j}-2X_{ij}+X_{i-1,j}\right]+\frac{D_{X}}{(\Delta y)^{2}}\left[X_{i,j+1}-2X_{ij}+X_{i,j-1}\right]

(14a)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{dY_{ij}}{dt}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=BX_{ij}-X_{ij}^{2}Y_{ij}+\frac{D_{Y}}{(\Delta x)^{2}}\left[Y_{i+1,j}-2Y_{ij}+Y_{i-1,j}\right]+\frac{D_{Y}}{(\Delta y)^{2}}\left[Y_{i,j+1}-2Y_{ij}+Y_{i,j-1}\right].

(14b)

In Eqs. (
14
),
\displaystyle\Delta x
and
\displaystyle\Delta y
represent the infinitesimal displacement
on the x- and y-axis, respectively, as per the derivative definition. In the case of discretization,
the smallest possible displacement on the lattice is one lattice site,
and therefore
\displaystyle\Delta x=\Delta y=h=1

in the network discretization approximation. Seeing from a different point of view, the displacement values

\displaystyle\Delta x
and
\displaystyle\Delta y
can be absorbed in the factor
\displaystyle D_{X}
and
\displaystyle D_{Y}
. Gathering together the terms
which are linked to the neighboring nodes, Eq. (
14
) reduce further to:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{dX_{ij}}{dt}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=A-(B+1)X_{ij}+X_{ij}^{2}Y_{ij}+\frac{D_{X}}{(\Delta x)^{2}}\sum_{k=i-1}^{i+1}\left[X_{kj}-X_{ij}\right]+\frac{D_{X}}{(\Delta y)^{2}}\sum_{l=j-1}^{j+1}\left[X_{il}-X_{ij}\right]

(15a)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{dY_{ij}}{dt}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=BX_{ij}-X_{ij}^{2}Y_{ij}+\frac{D_{Y}}{(\Delta x)^{2}}\sum_{k=i-1}^{i+1}\left[Y_{kj}-Y_{ij}\right]+\frac{D_{Y}}{(\Delta y)^{2}}\sum_{l=j-1}^{j+1}\left[Y_{il}-Y_{ij}\right].

(15b)

Finally, gathering the terms on the right-hand-sides and because
\displaystyle\Delta x=\Delta y=h=1
,
Eqs. (
15
) result in:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{dX_{ij}}{dt}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=A-(B+1)X_{ij}+X_{ij}^{2}Y_{ij}+\frac{D_{X}}{h^{2}}\sum_{\{k,l\}}\left[X_{kl}-X_{ij}\right]

(16a)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{dY_{ij}}{dt}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=BX_{ij}-X_{ij}^{2}Y_{ij}+\frac{D_{Y}}{h^{2}}\sum_{\{k,l\}}\left[Y_{kl}-Y_{ij}\right].

(16b)

Equations (
16
) have the same form with the more general Eqs. (
4
),
with the following restrictions:

\displaystyle\displaystyle c_{ij;kl}\>w_{XX}=D_{X}/h^{2},

(17a)

\displaystyle\displaystyle c_{ij;kl}\>w_{YY}=D_{Y}/h^{2},

(17b)

\displaystyle\displaystyle w_{XY}=w_{YX}=0,

(17c)

\displaystyle\displaystyle R=1.

(17d)

I.e., the more generic form of the Brusselator network, Eqs. (
4
), can give rise to
Turing patterns when cross-coupling terms are absent from the dynamics and in the limit of
local, nearest-neighbor exchanges, i.e., under the conditions Eqs. (
17
).
