Equilibrium in Functional Stochastic Games with Mean-Field Interaction
Eduardo Abi Jaber, Eyal Neuman, Moritz Voß

Abstract
We consider a general class of finite-player stochastic games with mean-field interaction, in which the linear-quadratic cost functional includes linear operators acting on controls in L 2 L^{2} . We propose a novel approach for deriving the Nash equilibrium of the game semi- explicitly in terms of operator resolvents, by reducing the associated first order conditions to a system of stochastic Fredholm equations of the second kind and deriving their solution in semi-explicit form . Furthermore, by proving stability results for the system of stochastic Fredholm equations, we derive the convergence of the equilibrium of the N N -player game to the corresponding mean-field equilibrium. As a by-product, we also derive an ε \varepsilon -Nash equilibrium for the mean-field game, which is valuable in this setting as we show that the conditions for existence of an equilibrium in the mean-field limit are less restrictive than in the finite-player game. Finally, we apply our general framework to solve various examples, such as stochastic Volterra linear-quadratic games, models of systemic risk and advertising with delay, and optimal liquidation games with transient price impact.

Introduction

Large population stochastic games and their mean-field limits have attracted a considerable attention in the past decades since the pioneering work of
Lasry and Lions 2007
and
Huang et al. 2006
, which was further developed by
Carmona and Delarue 2018
,
Carmona and Delarue 2018
,
Cardaliaguet et al. 2019
,
Fischer 2017
, among others.
The tractability and convergence properties of this versatile class of
models
has applications in various research areas such as mathematical finance, economics, population modeling, and marketing (see, e.g., Chapter 1 of
[
7
]
).
One of the main challenges in the area of finite population stochastic games is to derive explicitly the Nash equilibrium of the system. Motivating examples from mathematical finance include price impact games with competition for liquidity between agents
[
37
,
13
,
15
,
36
,
33
]
, systemic risk games introduced by
Carmona et al. 2013
,
Carmona et al. 2018
,
Fouque and Zhang 2018
, as well as optimal investment problems studied in
Lacker and Zariphopoulou 2019
,
Lacker and Soret 2020
. In various extensions of the aforementioned models it turns out that the state variables of the players and their objective functionals naturally depend on the entire trajectory of the controls. These generalizations give rise to infinite-dimensional dynamic stochastic games. In such a setting, deriving a Nash equilibrium of the system is in general considered to be intractable, and solutions to such games only appear in very particular examples; see Section
3
for a survey of such problems.

In this work we develop a novel approach for solving a general class of infinite-dimensional stochastic games with mean-field interaction between the players. Specifically, we consider
N
-player stochastic games in which each agent
i
has an objective functional of the form

\displaystyle J^{i}(u^{i}):=

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\mathbb{E}}\left[-\langle\bar{u},\bm{A}_{1}\bar{u}\rangle_{L^{2}}-\langle u^{i},\bm{A}_{2}u^{i}\rangle_{L^{2}}-\langle u^{i},(\bm{A}_{3}+\bm{A}^{*}_{3})\bar{u}\rangle_{L^{2}}\right.

(1.1)

\displaystyle\left.+\langle b^{i},u^{i}\rangle_{L^{2}}+\langle b^{0},\bar{u}\rangle_{L^{2}}+c^{i}\right],

where
u^{i}
represents the agent’s control and
\bar{u}=N^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}u^{i}
captures a mean-field interaction between all agents. Here, the symbols
\bm{A}_{i}
,
i=1,2,3
denote non-anticipative linear operators on
L^{2}([0,T],{\mathbb{R}})
;
(b^{i}_{s})_{s\in[0,T]},(b^{0}_{s})_{s\in[0,T]}
are progressively measurable stochastic processes; and
c^{i}
is a random variable. The inner product is defined in the usual sense as
\langle f,g\rangle_{L^{2}}:=\int_{0}^{T}f(s)g(s)ds
for
f,g\in L^{2}([0,T],{\mathbb{R}})
.

The main goal of this work is to derive the Nash equilibrium
in semi-explicit form
of finite-player games with reward functionals (
1.1
). In order to achieve this, we
propose
a new technique to solve simultaneously for all agents
i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
their individual path-dependent stochastic control problem

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\underset{u^{i}}{\text{maximize}}\;J^{i}(u^{i})}

(1.2)

over progressively measurable and square-integrable
u^{i}
’s. Moreover, we also apply our method to solve the corresponding simpler
limit
mean-field game when the number of agents
N
tends to infinity and provide a convergence result of the finite-player equilibrium strategies toward their mean-field limits.

Our approach uses variational calculus in order to establish sufficient first order conditions for a Nash equilibrium which take the form of stochastic Fredholm equations with both forward and backward components. The derivation of the equilibrium then relies on solving this system of equations for which we develop a new method of solution. The approach is versatile enough to allow for non-Markovian and non-semimartingale settings with no additional effort, in the sense that the controls are allowed to depend on the entire trajectories of the inputs
(b_{i})_{i=0}^{N}
which are only assumed to be progressively measurable; that is, neither necessarily semimartingales nor independent. Our framework unifies and extends
several
examples of stochastic dynamical games that have appeared in the literature beyond the Markovian and semimartingale case and were often considered as intractable (see Section
3
). In the following, we give a concrete summary of the contribution of the methods developed in this paper.

Our contributions.
In Theorems
2.7
and
2.8

is characterized via the stochastic Fredholm equation

\displaystyle\bar{u}_{t}=f_{t}-\int_{0}^{t}K(t,r)\bar{u}_{r}dr-\int_{t}^{T}L(r,t)\mathbb{E}_{t}\bar{u}_{r}dr,\quad t\in[0,T],

(1.3)

In Section

Convergence results of the finite-player Nash equilibrium to the corresponding mean-field equilibrium in Markovian settings have attracted considerable attention recently

In our specific setup
, we provide in Theorems

) also includes games in which the cost functional explicitly contains state variables. We refer to Section

Motivating example.
Let us consider a simple
stochastic differential
Markovian
N
-player game with controlled state variables given by

\displaystyle dX^{i}_{t}=\left(aX^{i}_{t}+u^{i}_{t}\right)dt+dW^{i}_{t},\quad X^{i}_{0}=0,\quad i=1,\ldots,N,

(1.4)

where
u^{i}
is the control of the
i
-th player,
a\in{\mathbb{R}}
and
(W^{1},\ldots,W^{N})
is an
N
-dimensional Brownian motion. Each player
i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
wishes to maximize following objective functional with mean-field interaction in the controls

J^{i}(u^{i})=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\left(-(u^{i}_{t})^{2}+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}u^{i}_{t}}\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}u^{j}_{t}\right)\right)dt+(X^{i}_{T})^{2}\right].

(1.5)

We will show how this game can be transformed into a game of type (
1.1
). Indeed, note that we can rewrite
(X^{i}_{T})^{2}
by first using the variation of constants formula to solve for
X^{i}
in (
1.4
), i.e.,

\displaystyle X^{i}_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}e^{a(t-s)}u_{s}^{i}ds+\int_{0}^{t}e^{a(t-s)}dW_{s}^{i},

and then apply Fubini’s theorem to obtain

\displaystyle(X^{i}_{T})^{2}

\displaystyle=2\int_{0}^{T}u^{i}_{s}\left(\int_{0}^{s}e^{a(2T-s-r)}u^{i}_{r}dr\right)ds

(1.6)

\displaystyle\quad\quad+2\int_{0}^{T}\left(\int_{0}^{T}e^{a(2T-s-r)}dW^{i}_{r}\right)u^{i}_{s}ds+\left(\int_{0}^{T}e^{a(T-s)}dW_{s}^{i}\right)^{2}.

(1.7)

This shows that the objective functional (
1.5
) can be written in the form

\displaystyle J^{i}(u^{i})=\mathbb{{\mathbb{E}}}\left[-\langle u^{i},\bm{A}_{2}u^{i}\rangle_{L^{2}}+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\langle u^{i},(\bm{A}_{3}+\bm{A}_{3}^{*})\bar{u}\rangle_{L^{2}}}+\langle b^{i},u^{i}\rangle_{L^{2}}+c^{i}\right],

(1.8)

where
\bm{A}_{3}+\bm{A}_{3}^{*}={\rm id}
, with
{\rm id}
being the identity operator,

(\bm{A}_{2}u)(s)=u_{s}-{2}\int_{0}^{s}e^{a(2T-s-r)}u_{r}dr,\quad s\leq T,

and

b^{i}_{s}=2\int_{0}^{T}e^{a(2T-s-r)}dW^{i}_{r},\quad b^{0}_{s}=0,\quad c^{i}=\left(\int_{0}^{T}e^{a(T-s)}dW_{s}^{i}\right)^{2},

and
\bar{u}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}u^{j}
. Hence, we observe that the objective functional in (
1.8
) corresponds to a stochastic differential game without state variables. Moreover, this representation very naturally lends itself to directly employing first order variational calculus. This motivates us to study more general objective criteria in the spirit of (
1.8
).

2
The Finite-Player Game

In this section we derive the Nash equilibrium of (
1.1
)
in semi-explicit form
. Before stating this result, we introduce some essential definitions of function spaces and integral operators.

2.1
Function spaces, integral operators

We denote by
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{L^{2}}
the inner product on
L^{2}([0,T],{\mathbb{R}})
, that is

\langle f,g\rangle_{L^{2}}=\int_{0}^{T}f(s)g(s)ds,\quad f,g\in L^{2}\left([0,T],\mathbb{R}\right),

(2.1)

where
\|\cdot\|_{L^{2}}
the induced norm.
We define
L^{2}\left([0,T]^{2},\mathbb{R}\right)
to be the space of measurable kernels
G:[0,T]^{2}\to{\mathbb{R}}
such that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{T}|G(t,s)|^{2}dtds<\infty.

For any kernel
G\in L^{2}\left([0,T]^{2},\mathbb{R}\right)
, we denote by
\bm{G}
the integral operator induced by the kernel
G
that is

\displaystyle({\bm{G}}f)(s)=\int_{0}^{T}G(s,u)f(u)du,\quad f\in L^{2}\left([0,T],\mathbb{R}\right).

\bm{G}
is a linear bounded operator from
L^{2}\left([0,T],\mathbb{R}\right)
into itself.
We denote by
G^{*}
the adjoint kernel of
G
for
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{L^{2}}
, that is

\displaystyle G^{*}(s,u)

\displaystyle=\;G(u,s),\quad(s,u)\in[0,T]^{2},

and by
\bm{G}^{*}
the corresponding adjoint integral operator.

2.2
Definition of the
N
-player game

We present the class of functional stochastic games which are studied in this paper. Let
T>0
denote a finite deterministic time horizon and let
N\geq 2
be an integer. We fix a filtered probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{F}:=(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{0\leq t\leq T},\mathbb{P})
satisfying the usual conditions of right continuity and completeness and use the notation
\mathbb{E}_{t}=\mathbb{E}[\cdot|\mathcal{F}_{t}]
to represent the conditional expectation with respect to
\mathcal{F}_{t}
.

For
i=0,\ldots,N
, let
c^{i}
be a random variable
and
b^{i}=(b^{i}_{t})_{0\leq t\leq T}
be progressively measurable processes
satisfying

\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[(b_{s}^{i})^{2}\right]ds<\infty,\quad\mathbb{E}[c^{i}]<\infty,\quad i=0,\ldots,N.

(2.2)

We say that a measurable Volterra kernel
G:[0,T]^{2}\to{\mathbb{R}}
, i.e., such that
G(t,s)=0
whenever
s\geq t
, is nonnegative definite if for every
f\in L^{2}\left([0,T],\mathbb{R}\right)
we have

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{T}\big(G(t,s)+G(s,t)\big)f(s)f(t)dsdt\geq 0.

(2.3)

Next we define the class of Volterra kernels which will be used in our setting.

Definition 2.1

(Class of admissible kernels
\mathcal{G}
)
.

We say that a nonnegative definite Volterra kernel
G:[0,T]^{2}\mapsto{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
is in the class of kernels
\mathcal{G}
if it satisfies the following conditions:

\displaystyle\sup_{t\leq T}\int_{0}^{T}|G(t,s)|^{2}ds+\sup_{s\leq T}\int_{0}^{T}|G(t,s)|^{2}dt<\infty.

(2.4)

Definition 2.2

(Admissible Volterra operator)
.

We say that an integral operator
\bm{G}
is an admissible Volterra operator if
the kernel
G
by which it is induced is in
\mathcal{G}
.

Remark 2.3
.

Observe that if
G\in\mathcal{G}
, property (
2.3
) implies for any
f\in L^{2}\left([0,T],\mathbb{R}\right)
,

\langle f,\bm{G}f\rangle_{L^{2}}=\frac{1}{2}\langle f,(\bm{G}+\bm{G}^{*})f\rangle_{L^{2}}\geq 0.

(2.5)

We consider
N
agents
i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
who select their controls
u^{i,N}
from the admissible set

\mathcal{U}:=\left\{u\,:\,\mathbb{F}\textrm{-progressively measurable s.t. }\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[u_{s}^{2}\right]ds<\infty\right\}

(2.6)

and let

u^{-i,N}:=(u^{1,N},\ldots,u^{i-1,N},u^{i+1,N},\ldots,u^{N,N}),\quad\bar{u}^{N}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}u^{j,N}.

(2.7)

In the following, we consider operators
(\bm{{A}}_{1},\bm{{A}}_{2},\bm{{A}}_{3})
which satisfy the following assumptions.

Assumption 2.4
.

We assume that
\bm{A}_{1}
and
\bm{A}_{3}
are admissible Volterra operators, and
\bm{A}_{2}
is given by

\bm{A}_{2}:=\lambda{\rm id}+\bm{\hat{A}}_{2}

(2.8)

where
\bm{\hat{A}}_{2}
is an admissible Volterra operator,
\lambda>0
is a constant and
{\rm id}
is the identity operator, i.e.

({\rm id}f)(t)=f(t),\quad\textrm{for all }t\in[0,T],\,f\in L^{2}\left([0,T],\mathbb{R}\right).

Each agent
i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
has the following individual performance functional

\displaystyle J^{i,N}(u^{i,N};u^{-i,N}):=

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\mathbb{E}}\left[-\langle\bar{u}^{N},\bm{A}_{1}\bar{u}^{N}\rangle_{L^{2}}-\langle u^{i,N},\bm{A}_{2}u^{i,N}\rangle_{L^{2}}-\langle u^{i,N},(\bm{A}_{3}+\bm{A}^{*}_{3})\bar{u}^{N}\rangle_{L^{2}}\right.

(2.9)

\displaystyle\left.+\langle b^{i},u^{i,N}\rangle_{L^{2}}+\langle b^{0},\bar{u}^{N}\rangle_{L^{2}}+c^{i}\right],

where
\bar{u}^{N}\in\mathcal{U}
describes the mean-field interaction between the agents.

Remark 2.5
.

Note that in the objective functional in (
2.9
) the processes
(b^{i}_{t})_{0\leq t\leq T}
,
i=0,\ldots,N
, are fairly general. They can be thought of as incorporating simultaneously different sources of noise: i.e., a common noise affecting all
N
players as well as player
i
’s independent individual source of noise; and also other random or deterministic factors idiosyncratic to player
i
. We refer to Section
3
below for specific examples.

Our main goal in this section is to solve simultaneously for each agent
i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
their individual optimal stochastic control problem

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\underset{u^{i}\in\mathcal{U}}{\text{maximize}}\;J^{i,N}(u^{i,N};u^{-i,N}).}

(2.10)

This solution will establish a Nash equilibrium in the following usual sense.

Definition 2.6
.

A set of strategies
\hat{u}^{N}=(\hat{u}^{1,N},\ldots,\hat{u}^{N,N})\in\mathcal{U}^{N}
is called a Nash equilibrium if for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
and for all admissible strategies
v\in\mathcal{U}
it holds that

J^{i,N}(\hat{u}^{i,N};\hat{u}^{-i,N})\geq J^{i,N}(v;\hat{u}^{-i,N}).

Remark 3.2

In the following we will show that the dynamics (

itself and in
\bar{X}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}X^{j}

We recall the definition of the product of kernels. For any
G,H\in L^{2}\left([0,T]^{2},\mathbb{R}\right)
we define the
\star
-product as follows

\displaystyle(G\star H)(s,u)=\int_{0}^{T}G(s,z)H(z,u)dz,\quad(s,u)\in[0,T]^{2},

which is a well-defined kernel in
L^{2}\left([0,T]^{2},\mathbb{R}\right)
due to Cauchy-Schwarz inequality. Denoting by

\bm{H}

G
and

\bm{G}\bm{H}
is an integral operator induced by the kernel
G\star H

For a kernel
K\in L^{2}([0,T]^{2},{\mathbb{R}})

by the unique solution to

\displaystyle R_{T}=K+K\star R_{T},\quad\quad K\star R_{T}=R_{T}\star K.

(3.7)

In terms of integral operators, this translates into

\displaystyle\bm{R}_{T}=\bm{K}+\bm{K}\bm{R}_{T},\quad\bm{K}\bm{R}_{T}=\bm{R}_{T}\bm{K}.

In particular, if
K
admits a resolvent,
({{\rm id}}-\bm{K})
is invertible and

\displaystyle({{\rm id}}-\bm{K})^{-1}={\rm id}+\bm{R}_{T}.

(3.8)

Lemma 3.3
.

For

be a progressively measurable process with sample paths in

\displaystyle X^{i}_{t}=M^{i}_{t}+\int_{0}^{t}K(t,s)X^{i}_{s}ds+\int_{0}^{t}H(t,s)\bar{X}_{s}ds,\quad i=1,\ldots,N,

\displaystyle\widetilde{M}^{i}_{t}

\displaystyle=M^{i}_{t}+\int_{0}^{t}H(t,s)\bar{M}_{s}ds+\int_{0}^{t}\left(H\star R^{K+H}\right)(t,s)\bar{M}_{s}ds,\quad i=1,\ldots,N,

Plugging this expression back in (

3.2
Propagator models: optimal liquidation with transient impact and signals

The framework for optimal liquidation with transient impact and price predicting signals was first introduced in
[
32
]
. The stochastic game, where several financial agents aim to liquidate in an optimal way their positions in a risky asset was presented in
[
36
]
. In the following we briefly describe the model.

The model.
We consider
N
traders with an initial position of
x^{i}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
shares in a risky asset for the
i
-th trader,
i=1,\ldots,N
. The number of shares the trader holds at time
t
is prescribed as

\displaystyle\widetilde{X}^{i}_{t}=x^{i}_{0}-\int_{0}^{t}u^{i}_{s}ds,

(3.15)

where
(u^{i}_{s})_{s\in[0,T]}
denotes the selling speed which is chosen by the trader. We assume that the traders’ individual and aggregated trading activity causes price impact on the risky asset’s execution price. The actual price at which the orders are executed for the
i
-th trader is given by

S^{i}_{t}:=N^{i}_{t}-\lambda u^{i}_{t}-\widetilde{Y}^{\bar{u}}_{t},\qquad 0\leq t\leq T,

(3.16)

where
N^{i}
denotes some unaffected progressively measurable price process incorporating an individual (or common) trading signal. The process

\displaystyle\widetilde{Y}_{t}^{\bar{u}}=\int_{0}^{t}G(t,s)\bar{u}_{s}ds,\qquad 0\leq t\leq T,

(3.17)

captures an aggregated linear decaying price impact with a general propagator kernel
G
and
\lambda>0
captures instantaneous slippage costs which are incurred by the
i
-th trader’s execution strategy.

Remark 3.5
.

In
[
35
,
36
]
an exponentially decaying kernel of the form
G(t,s)=1_{\{s<t\}}H(t-s)
with
H(t)=e^{-\rho t}
for some
\rho>0
is specified, so the control problem can be regarded as Markovian. However, in practice, the decay of the price impact has been shown to be slower than exponential, and more realistically modeled by a power-law kernel, which makes the problem non-Markovian and
difficult
to solve. For the single player case, the problem with general propagator
G\in\mathcal{G}
and a progressively measurable
P
was solved recently in
[
2
]
. We refer to
[
2
, Example 2.5]
for some examples of realistic kernels and to the references therein for the empirical motivation.

The objective criterion.
The aim of each trader is to maximize the profit and loss from implementing the trading strategy
u^{i}
given by

-\int_{0}^{T}S^{i}_{t}d\widetilde{X}^{i}_{t}+N^{i}_{T}\widetilde{X}^{i}_{T}=\int_{0}^{T}S^{i}_{t}u^{i}_{t}dt+N^{i}_{T}\widetilde{X}^{i}_{T}

in the presence of running and terminal penalizations on the trader’s inventory of the form
\phi(x^{i}_{t})^{2}
and
\varrho(x^{i}_{T})^{2}
with
\phi,\varrho>0
, which encourage the trader to liquidate her position. This leads to the following performance functional

J^{i}_{liq}(u^{i})=\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\int_{0}^{T}f^{liq}_{i}(t,\widetilde{X}^{i}_{t},\widetilde{Y}^{\bar{u}}_{t},u^{i}_{t})dt+g^{liq}_{i}(\widetilde{X}^{i}_{T})\Bigg],

(3.18)

with

\displaystyle f^{liq}_{i}(t,x,y,u)

\displaystyle=-\lambda u^{2}-\phi x^{2}-uy+P^{i}_{t}u,\quad g^{liq}_{i}(x)=-\varrho x^{2}+P^{i}_{T}x.

(3.19)

The correspondence with our Volterra game framework.

The optimal liquidation problem with aggregated transient price impact and signals corresponds to a Volterra game in the sense of Section
3.1
with controlled variables
X^{i}
in (
3.1
) and
Y^{i}
in (
3.2
) given by
(X^{i},Y^{i})=(\widetilde{X}^{i},P^{i}-\widetilde{Y}^{\bar{u}})
with

G_{2}(t,s)=-1_{s\leq t},\quad G_{3}=0\quad G_{1}=-G\quad P^{i}=x_{0}^{i},\quad R^{i}=N^{i}.

The objective criterion in (
3.18
) can be written as in (
3.4
) in terms of
f^{Vol}
in (
3.5
) and
g^{Vol}
in (
3.6
) where

\displaystyle p

\displaystyle=\lambda,\quad Q={\begin{pmatrix}\phi&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}},\quad q={(0,1)^{\top}},\quad S={\begin{pmatrix}\varrho&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}},\quad s^{i}=(P^{i}_{T},0)^{\top}.

Remark 3.6

(Improvements with respect to the related literature)
.

In the single player case, the above non-Markovian problem was solved only very recently in
[
2
]
by making an ansatz on the value function combined with a martingale verification argument. Our approach not only extends such results of
[
2
]
to a multi-player price impact game but also provides a new and direct argument for deriving the solution for the single player case.

3.3
Delay in the control: an inter-bank lending and borrowing model

In this subsection we show how our results extend
models on systemic risk with delay studied in
[
10
,
17
]

The model.
The log-monetary reserves

of
N

\displaystyle dx^{i}_{t}=\left(h_{i}(t)+\int_{[0,t]}\nu(ds)u^{i}_{t-s}\right)dt+\sigma_{i}dW^{i}_{t},\quad x^{i}_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}},

which corresponds to the case
\nu=\delta_{0}-\delta_{\tau}

u^{i}_{(t+\tau)-\tau}dt=u^{i}_{t}dt
, which explains the form of the drift

Remark 3.7

We note that in

]

\displaystyle dx^{i}_{t}=\left(\int_{[0,\tau]}\theta(ds)u^{i}_{t-s}\right)dt+\sigma_{i}dW^{i}_{t},\quad x^{i}_{0}\in\mathbb{R},

\quad u^{i}_{s}=\phi^{i}(s),\quad\mbox{for }s\in[-\tau,0],

The objective criterion.
The
i
-th bank chooses the strategy

to minimize the following objective criterion that involves the aggregated average

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}^{i}_{sys}(u^{i},u^{-i})=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}f^{sys}(x^{i}_{t},\bar{x}_{t},u^{i}_{t})dt+g^{sys}(x^{i}_{T},\bar{x}_{T})\right],

\displaystyle f^{sys}(x,\bar{x},u)

\displaystyle=\frac{u^{2}}{2}-\beta u(\bar{x}-x)+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}(\bar{x}-x)^{2},

\displaystyle g^{sys}(x,\bar{x})

\displaystyle=\frac{c}{2}(\bar{x}-x)^{2}.

\displaystyle x_{t}^{i}=P^{i}_{t}+\int_{0}^{t}G(t-s)u^{i}_{s}ds

Clearly, this corresponds to a Volterra game in the sense of Section

in (

and where the objective criterion to be maximized in (

as in (

as in (

\displaystyle p

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2},\quad Q=\frac{\epsilon}{2}\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\
-1&1\end{pmatrix},\quad q=\beta(-1,1)^{\top},\quad S=\frac{c}{\epsilon}Q,\quad s^{i}=(0,0)^{\top}.

Remark 3.9

(Improvements with respect to the related literature)

In

Lemma 3.10

Let

Proof.

This is an application of Fubini’s theorem and the change of variables formula:

\displaystyle\int_{[0,t]}G(t-s)u_{s}ds

\displaystyle=\int_{[0,t]}\left(\int_{[0,t-s]}\nu(dr)\right)u_{s}ds

)

\displaystyle=\int_{[0,t]}\left(\int_{[0,t]}1_{\{r+s\leq t\}}\nu(dr)\right)u_{s}ds

\displaystyle=\int_{[0,t]}\left(\int_{[0,t]}1_{\{r+s\leq t\}}u_{s}ds\right)\nu(dr)

(Fubini)

\displaystyle=\int_{[0,t]}\left(\int_{[r,t+r]}1_{\{l\leq t\}}u_{l-r}dl\right)\nu(dr)

\displaystyle=\int_{[0,t]}\left(\int_{[0,t]}1_{\{r\leq l\leq t\}}u_{l-r}dl\right)\nu(dr)

\displaystyle=\int_{[0,t]}\left(\int_{[0,t]}1_{\{r\leq l\leq t\}}u_{l-r}\nu(dr)\right)dl

(Fubini)

\displaystyle=\int_{[0,t]}\left(\int_{[0,l]}u_{l-r}\nu(dr)\right)dl,

where we have done a change of variable
l=r+s
in the inner integral in the fourth equation.
∎

3.4
Delay in the state: an advertising model with mean-field effect

Our next application is motivated by problems of stochastic optimal advertising
[

with time-delay and persistence of advertising expenditures, in the presence of competition between products or firms.

The model.
We consider a company launching
N

x^{i}
of the

\displaystyle dx^{i}_{t}=\left(\beta u^{i}_{t}+h_{i}(t)+\int_{[0,t]}\nu(ds)x^{i}_{t-s}+\int_{[0,t]}\mu(ds)\bar{x}_{t-s}\right)dt+\sigma_{i}dW^{i}_{t},\quad x^{i}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}.\qquad

Remark 3.11

We note that the dynamics of

\displaystyle dx^{i}_{t}=\left(\beta u^{i}_{t}+\int_{[0,\tau]}\theta_{1}(ds)x^{i}_{t-s}+\int_{[0,\tau]}\theta_{2}(ds)\bar{x}_{t-s}\right)dt+\sigma_{i}dW^{i}_{t},

for some fixed

and

Setting

\displaystyle=1_{t\leq\tau}\int_{[t,\tau]}\left(\theta_{1}(ds)x^{i}_{t-s}+\theta_{2}(ds)\bar{x}_{t-s}\right)

\displaystyle=1_{t\leq\tau}\int_{[t,\tau]}\left(\theta(ds)\phi^{i}(t-s)+\theta_{2}(ds)\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\phi^{j}(t-s)\right),

The objective criterion.

The following objective functional to be maximized was presented in

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}^{i}_{adv}(u^{i},u^{-i})=\mathbb{E}\left[-\int_{0}^{T}\lambda(u^{i}_{t})^{2}dt+\beta x^{i}_{T}\right].

with

Then, an application of Lemma

shows clearly that

for the case

locally integrable,

Thanks to Remark

applied to (

P^{i}=\sigma_{i}W^{i}+\int_{0}^{\cdot}R^{H}(\cdot,s)\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sigma_{j}W^{j}_{s}ds,\quad R^{i}=0,

where

and where the objective criterion in (

Remark 3.12

(Improvements with respect to the related literature)
.

To the best of our knowledge, the advertising multi-player game above has not been solved yet. A similar advertising game has been briefly presented in
[

in the context of certain Linear-Quadratic Stackelberg games. In

[

[

.

4
The Mean-Field Game

In this section we formulate and solve the corresponding asymptotic mean-field game version of the finite-player game from Section
2
when the number of players
N
tends to infinity. We prove that the finite-player Nash equilibrium strategies converge in the infinite population limit to the mean-field game equilibrium and show that the latter yields an approximate equilibrium for the finite-player setup from Section
2
. We first consider a generic player in the mean-field game limit and then provide an equivalent formulation in terms of infinitely many players. The main advantage of the analysis in this section is that the mean-field limit provides both simpler expressions to the equilibrium strategies and more relaxed conditions for existence of an equilibrium, compared to the finite-player game.

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F}_{T},\mathbb{F}=(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{0\leq t\leq T},\mathbb{P})
denote the filtered probability space from Section
2
. Recall that we use the notation
\mathbb{E}_{t}=\mathbb{E}[\cdot|\mathcal{F}_{t}]
to represent the conditional expectation with respect to
\mathcal{F}_{t}
. Moreover, let
c
denote an
\mathcal{F}_{T}
-measurable random variable and let
\beta,\beta^{0}
denote
\mathbb{F}
-progressively measurable processes such that
\beta^{0}
is independent of
\beta
. We set

b:=\beta+\beta^{0}

(4.1)

and denote by
\mathbb{F}^{0}:=(\mathcal{F}^{0}_{t})_{0\leq t\leq T}
the filtration generated by
\beta^{0}
satisfying the usual conditions.

Assumption 4.1
.

We assume that for all
t\in[0,T]
,
\mathcal{F}_{T}^{0}
and
\mathcal{F}_{t}
are conditionally independent given
\mathcal{F}^{0}_{t}
.

Remark 4.2
.

For instance, if we denote by
(\mathcal{F}^{\beta}_{t})_{t\leq T}
the filtration generated by the process
\beta
, then the filtration
\mathbb{F}
defined by
\mathcal{F}_{t}:=\mathcal{F}^{\beta}_{t}\vee\mathcal{F}^{0}_{t}
for all
t\leq T
, satisfies Assumption
4.1
.

In addition to the set
\mathcal{U}
introduced in (
2.6
) we also define the subset of processes

\mathcal{U}^{0}:=\left\{v:\,\mathbb{F}^{0}\mbox{-progressively measurable s.t. }\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}[v_{s}^{2}]ds<\infty\right\}\subset\mathcal{U}.

(4.2)

The paradigm of the mean-field game limit of the finite-player game from Section
2
is the following: First, we consider a generic player who seeks to implement a strategy
v\in\mathcal{U}
in order to maximize for a fixed
\mu\in\mathcal{U}^{0}
the objective function

\displaystyle J(v;\mu):=

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\mathbb{E}}\left[-\langle\mu,\bm{A}_{1}\mu\rangle_{L^{2}}-\langle v,\bm{A}_{2}v\rangle_{L^{2}}-\langle v,(\bm{A}_{3}+\bm{A}^{*}_{3})\mu\rangle_{L^{2}}\right.

(4.3)

\displaystyle\left.+\langle b,v\rangle_{L^{2}}+\langle b^{0},\mu\rangle_{L^{2}}+c\right],

which is akin to (
2.9
) with same operators
\bm{A}_{1},\bm{A}_{2},\bm{A}_{3}
as well as
\mathbb{F}
-progressively measurable process
b^{0}
as introduced above in Section
2
. Then, we determine the
\mathbb{F}^{0}
-measurable process
\mu
such that a mean-field game equilibrium with common noise
\beta^{0}
is obtained in the following sense.

Definition 4.3
.

A pair
(\hat{v},\hat{\mu})\in\mathcal{U}\times\mathcal{U}^{0}
is called a mean-field game equilibrium if the control
\hat{v}
solves the optimization problem

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\underset{v\in\mathcal{U}}{\text{maximize}}\;J(v;\hat{\mu})}

(4.4)

under the consistency condition

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[\hat{v}_{t}|\mathcal{F}^{0}_{T}]=\hat{\mu}_{t},\quad\Omega\times[0,T]\text{ almost everywhere.}

(4.5)

Remark 4.4
.

Note that due to Assumption
4.1
, it holds in (
4.5
) that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[\hat{v}_{t}|\mathcal{F}^{0}_{T}]={\mathbb{E}}[\hat{v}_{t}|\mathcal{F}^{0}_{t}]

for all
t\in[0,T]
. Indeed, for all
\xi\in\mathcal{F}^{0}_{T}
it follows that

\mathbb{E}[\xi\,{\mathbb{E}}[\hat{v}_{t}|\mathcal{F}^{0}_{t}]]=\mathbb{E}[{\mathbb{E}}[\xi|\mathcal{F}^{0}_{t}]\,{\mathbb{E}}[\hat{v}_{t}|\mathcal{F}^{0}_{t}]]=\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[\xi\hat{v}_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{t}^{0}]]=\mathbb{E}[\xi\hat{v}_{t}],

where the second equality is obtained from the conditional independence given by Assumption
4.1
.

To state the mean-field game equilibrium, it is convenient to introduce the following two solution maps
F
and
G
of two associated Fredholm equations:

\begin{aligned} F(t,x):=&\left(({\rm id}-\bm{\tilde{B}})^{-1}\tilde{a}(x)\right)(t)\\
G(t,x):=&\left(({\rm id}-\bm{\hat{B}})^{-1}\hat{a}(x)\right)(t)\end{aligned}\qquad\qquad(0\leq t\leq T),

(4.6)

where

\displaystyle\tilde{a}_{t}(x)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2\lambda}\left(x_{t}-\langle 1_{\{t\leq\cdot\}}\hat{A}_{2}(\cdot,t),\bm{\tilde{D}}_{t}^{-1}1_{\{t\leq\cdot\}}{\mathbb{E}}_{t}[x_{\cdot}]\rangle_{L^{2}}\right),

(4.7)

\displaystyle\tilde{B}(t,s)

\displaystyle:=1_{\{s\leq t\}}\frac{1}{2\lambda}\left(\langle 1_{\{t\leq\cdot\}}\hat{A}_{2}(\cdot,t),\bm{\tilde{D}}_{t}^{-1}1_{\{t\leq\cdot\}}\hat{A}_{2}(\cdot,s)\rangle_{L^{2}}-\hat{A}_{2}(t,s)\right),

\displaystyle\bm{\tilde{D}}_{t}

\displaystyle:=2\lambda{\rm id}+(\bm{\hat{A}}_{2})_{t}+(\bm{\hat{A}}^{*}_{2})_{t};

and

\displaystyle\hat{a}_{t}(x)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2\lambda}\left(x_{t}-\langle 1_{\{t\leq\cdot\}}(\hat{A}_{2}(\cdot,t)+{A}_{3}(\cdot,t)),\bm{\hat{D}}_{t}^{-1}1_{\{t\leq\cdot\}}{\mathbb{E}}_{t}[x_{\cdot}]\rangle_{L^{2}}\right),

(4.8)

\displaystyle\hat{B}(t,s):=

\displaystyle 1_{\{s\leq t\}}\frac{1}{2\lambda}\Big(\langle 1_{\{t\leq\cdot\}}(\hat{A}_{2}(\cdot,t)+{A}_{3}(\cdot,t)),\bm{\hat{D}}_{t}^{-1}1_{\{t\leq\cdot\}}(\hat{A}_{2}(\cdot,s)+{A}_{3}(\cdot,s))\rangle_{L^{2}}\Big.

\displaystyle\Big.-(\hat{A}_{2}(t,s)+{A}_{3}(t,s))\Big),

\displaystyle\bm{\hat{D}}_{t}

\displaystyle:=2\lambda{\rm id}+(\bm{\hat{A}}_{2})_{t}+(\bm{\hat{A}}^{*}_{2})_{t}+(\bm{A}_{3})_{t}+(\bm{A}^{*}_{3})_{t}.

We are now ready to provide the solution to the mean-field game:

Theorem 4.5
.

Assume that (
2.2
) as well as Assumptions
2.4
and
4.1
are satisfied.
Then, the unique mean-field game equilibrium
(\hat{v},\hat{\mu})\in\mathcal{U}\times\mathcal{U}^{0}
in the sense of Definition
4.3
is given by

\displaystyle\hat{v}_{t}

\displaystyle=F(t,b-\bm{A}_{3}(\hat{\mu})-\bm{A}^{*}_{3}({\mathbb{E}}_{\cdot}\hat{\mu})),

(4.9)

\displaystyle\hat{\mu}_{t}

\displaystyle=G(t,\mathbb{E}[\beta]+\beta^{0}),

(4.10)

for all
t\in[0,T]
, where
F
and
G
are defined in (
4.6
).

Remark 4.6
.

To be more explicit, note that the process which appears on the right-hand side of (
4.9
) is

b_{s}-\bm{A}_{3}(\hat{\mu})(s)-\bm{A}^{*}_{3}({\mathbb{E}}_{s}\hat{\mu})(s)=b_{s}-\int_{0}^{s}A_{3}(s,r)\hat{\mu}_{r}dr-\int_{s}^{T}A_{3}(r,s){\mathbb{E}}_{s}[\hat{\mu}_{r}]dr,\quad s\leq T.

The proof of Theorem
4.5
is given in Section
7
. We note that Remarks
2.12
and
3.2
remain valid for the expressions (
4.9
) and (
4.10
).

4.1
The Infinite-Player Game Formulation

It is also very natural to formulate the mean-field game
limit
of the finite-player game introduced in Section
2
as an infinite-player game when the number of players
N
is sent to infinity. We show below that the resulting infinite-player mean-field game Nash equilibrium is equivalent to a generic player’s mean-field game equilibrium derived above.

While there is some overlap between the result of Theorem
4.5
and some of the results in this section, we have decided to include the infinite-player game formulation, as it is a more natural framework to study convergence of
N
-player games as
N\rightarrow\infty
, which is done in Section
4.2
. Moreover, this approach is often used in applications, particularly in price impact games, where the agents are not necessarily identical (see, e.g.,
[
11
,
13
,
18
,
19
,
36
]
).

Specifically, consider a countable collection of controls
(v^{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathcal{U}
associated with infinitely many agents indexed by
i\in\mathbb{N}
. Moreover, we let
\nu\in\mathcal{U}
denote a stochastic process which represents the limit of the control processes’ averages. Then, the infinite-player game version of each player
i
’s objective from (
2.9
) is given by

\displaystyle J^{i,\infty}(v^{i};\nu)=

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\mathbb{E}}\left[-\langle\nu,\bm{A}_{1}\nu\rangle_{L^{2}}-\langle v^{i},\bm{A}_{2}v^{i}\rangle_{L^{2}}-\langle v^{i},(\bm{A}_{3}+\bm{A}^{*}_{3})\nu\rangle_{L^{2}}\right.

(4.11)

\displaystyle\left.+\langle b^{i},v^{i}\rangle_{L^{2}}+\langle b^{0},\nu\rangle_{L^{2}}+c^{i}\right],

with same operators
\bm{A}_{1},\bm{A}_{2},\bm{A}_{3}
, as well as
\mathbb{F}
-progressively measurable processes
b^{0},(b^{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
and
\mathcal{F}_{T}
-measurable
(c^{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
as introduced above in Section
2
.

The notion of a Nash equilibrium in this infinite-player mean-field game is to first solve for a fixed
\nu\in\mathcal{U}
simultaneously for each player
i\in\mathbb{N}
the optimal stochastic control problem

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\underset{v^{i}\in\mathcal{U}}{\text{maximize}}\;J^{i,\infty}(v^{i};\nu)}\qquad(i\in\mathbb{N}),

(4.12)

and then to determine the process
\nu
in equilibrium in the following sense.

Definition 4.7
.

A collection of processes
(\hat{\nu},(\hat{v}^{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}})\subset\mathcal{U}
is called a mean-field game Nash equilibrium with mean-field strategy
\hat{\nu}
if for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
the control
\hat{v}^{i}
solves the optimization problem

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\underset{v^{i}\in\mathcal{U}}{\text{maximize}}\;J^{i,\infty}(v^{i};\hat{\nu})}\qquad(i\in\mathbb{N})

(4.13)

and
\hat{\nu}
satisfies the consistency condition

\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat{v}^{i}_{t}-\hat{\nu}_{t}\right)^{2}\right]=0.

(4.14)

We make the following assumption on the players’ individual processes
(b^{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
in (
4.11
):

Assumption 4.8
.

We assume that there exists a unique
\mathbb{F}
-progressively measurable process
b^{\infty}\in L^{2}(\Omega\times[0,T])
and a bounded function
h:\mathds{R}_{+}\mapsto\mathds{R}_{+}
with
h(x)\rightarrow 0
as
x\rightarrow\infty
such that

\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}b^{i}_{t}-b^{\infty}_{t}\right)^{2}\right]\leq h(N),\quad\textrm{for all }N\geq 1.

(4.15)

Remark 4.9
.

The general Assumption
4.8
can be made precise in specific examples. For instance, consider the portfolio liquidation price impact game from Section
3.2
above. Suppose that for each agent
i\in\mathbb{N}
the initial inventory is given by
x^{i}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
and their trading signal is given by
N^{i}=B^{i}+B
with idiosyncratic independent Brownian motions
(B^{i})_{i\geq 1}
which are also independent of a common noise Brownian motion
B
. With this configuration, we have

b^{i}_{t}=2(\varrho+\phi(T-t))x^{i}_{0}+\mathbb{E}_{t}\left[B^{i}_{t}-B^{i}_{T}\right]+\mathbb{E}_{t}\left[B_{t}-B_{T}\right]=2(\varrho+\phi(T-t))x^{i}_{0},\qquad t\leq T.

(4.16)

Therefore, the condition in (
4.15
) boils down to requiring that the limit
x^{\infty}\in\mathbb{R}
of the averages of all initial positions
(x^{i}_{0})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
exists, i.e.,

x^{\infty}=\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}x^{i}_{0}.

(4.17)

Moreover, the process
b^{\infty}
in (
4.15
) is then given by

b^{\infty}_{t}=2(\varrho+\phi(T-t))x^{\infty},\qquad t\leq T.

(4.18)

Akin to Theorem
4.5
, the solution to the infinite-player mean-field game is characterized as follows:

Theorem 4.10
.

Assume that (
2.2
) as well as Assumptions
2.4
and
4.8
are satisfied.
Then, the unique solution
(\hat{\nu},(\hat{v}^{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}})\subset\mathcal{U}
of the infinite-player mean-field game in the sense of Definition

is given by

\displaystyle\hat{v}^{i}_{t}

\displaystyle=F(t,b^{i}-\bm{A}_{3}(\hat{\nu})+\bm{A}^{*}_{3}({\mathbb{E}}_{\cdot}\hat{\nu})),

(4.19)

\displaystyle\hat{\nu}_{t}

\displaystyle=G(t,b^{\infty}),

(4.20)

for all
t\in[0,T]
, where
F
and
G
are defined in (
4.6
).

The proof of Theorem
4.10
is deferred to Section
7
.

In light of Theorems

and

Definition 4.14
.

For any

introduce the following norm

\|u\|_{2,T}=\left(\int_{0}^{T}{\mathbb{E}}[u_{t}^{2}]dt\right)^{1/2}.

We have following approximation result.

Theorem 4.15
.

Suppose that (

) as well as Assumptions

Stochastic Fredholm equations

In this section we derive general results on linear stochastic Fredholm equations of the second kind, which appear in the first order condition of the
N

Recall that for any

we define

G_{t}(s,r)=G(s,r)1_{\{r\geq t\}},

and we denote by
{\bm{G}}_{t}

The following proposition derives the solution to stochastic Fredholm equations of the second kind.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let

be two Volterra kernels in
\mathcal{G}
such that the operator

(\bm{K}+\bm{L}^{*})
is self-adjoint and let
(f_{t})_{0\leq t\leq T}
be a progressively measurable process such that
\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}[f_{t}^{2}]dt<\infty

v_{t}=f_{t}-\int_{0}^{t}K(t,r)v_{r}dr-\int_{t}^{T}L(r,t)\mathbb{E}_{t}v_{r}dr,\quad t\in[0,T],

admits a unique progressively measurable solution
(v_{t})_{0\leq t\leq T}
in
\mathcal{U}
given by

\displaystyle v_{t}=\left(({\rm id}-\bm{B})^{-1}a\right)(t),\quad 0\leq t\leq T,

(5.2)

where

\displaystyle a_{t}

\displaystyle=f_{t}-\langle 1_{\{t<\cdot\}}L(\cdot,t),{\bm{D}}_{t}^{-1}1_{\{t<\cdot\}}{\mathbb{E}}_{t}f_{\cdot}\rangle_{L^{2}}

(5.3)

\displaystyle B(t,s)

\displaystyle=1_{\{s<t\}}\left(\left\langle 1_{\{t<\cdot\}}L(\cdot,t),{\bm{D}}_{t}^{-1}1_{\{t\leq\cdot\}}K(\cdot,s)\right\rangle_{L^{2}}-K(t,s)\right),

{\bm{D}}_{t}={\rm id}+\bm{K}_{t}+\bm{L^{*}}_{t},

(5.4)

and
\bm{B}
is an integral operator induced by the kernel
B
.

The proof of Proposition
5.1
is postponed to the end of this section.

Remark 5.2
.

Note that the assumption that

is self-adjoint in Proposition

are invertible for all

We define operator norm as follows,

\|\bm{G}\|_{\rm{op}}=\sup_{f\in L^{2}([0,T],{\mathbb{R}})}\frac{\|\bm{G}f\|_{L^{2}}}{\|f\|_{L^{2}}},

for an operator

from

to itself.

The following proposition derives a stability result for stochastic Fredholm equations of the second kind.

Proposition 5.3

where

Proof.

\displaystyle d^{N}_{t}

Lemma 5.4

For any

u\in\mathcal{U}
and
i\in\mathbb{N}
we have

\left|J^{i,N}(u,\hat{v}^{-i})-{J}^{i,\infty}(u,\hat{\nu})\right|\leq C\|u\|_{2,T}(1+\|u\|_{2,T})\left(h(N)\vee N^{-1}\right).

Proof.

Let
u\in\mathcal{U}
. From (
2.9
) and (
4.11
) we get

\displaystyle\left|J^{i,N}(u;\hat{v}^{-i})-J^{i,\infty}(u;\hat{\nu})\right|

(7.25)

\displaystyle\leq\left|{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left\langle\frac{1}{N}\left(\sum_{j\not=i}\hat{v}^{j}+u\right),\bm{A}_{1}\left(\frac{1}{N}\big(\sum_{j\not=i}\hat{v}^{j}+u\big)\right)\right\rangle_{L^{2}}-\langle\hat{\nu},\bm{A}_{1}\hat{\nu}\rangle_{L^{2}}\right]\right|

\displaystyle+\left|{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left\langle u,(\bm{A}_{3}+\bm{A}_{3}^{*})\left(\frac{1}{N}\left(\sum_{j\not=i}\hat{v}^{j}+u\right)-\hat{\nu}\right)\right\rangle_{L^{2}}\right]\right|

\displaystyle+\left|{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left\langle b^{0},\frac{1}{N}\left(\sum_{j\not=i}\hat{v}^{j}+u\right)-\hat{\nu}\right\rangle_{L^{2}}\right]\right|

\displaystyle=:I_{1}+I_{2}+I_{3}.

Using Fubini’s Theorem, Jensen and Hölder inequalities we get for the second term
I_{2}
in (
7.25
):

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle=\left|{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left\langle u,(\bm{A}_{3}+\bm{A}_{3}^{*})\left(\frac{1}{N}\left(\sum_{j\not=i}\hat{v}^{j}+u\right)-\hat{\nu}\right)\right\rangle_{L^{2}}\right]\right|

(7.26)

\displaystyle\leq C\|u\|_{2,T}\|\bm{A}_{3}+\bm{A}_{3}^{*}\|_{op}\left\|\frac{1}{N}\left(\sum_{j\not=i}\hat{v}^{j}+u\right)-\hat{\nu}\right\|_{2,T}

\displaystyle\leq C\|u\|_{2,T}\|\bm{A}_{3}+\bm{A}_{3}^{*}\|_{op}\left(\left\|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\hat{v}^{j}-\hat{\nu}\right\|_{2,T}+\frac{1}{N}\left\|\hat{v}^{i}-u\right\|_{2,T}\right)

\displaystyle\leq C(h(N)^{1/2}\vee N^{-1})\|u\|_{2,T}\|\bm{A}_{3}+\bm{A}_{3}^{*}\|_{op}\left(1+\|\hat{v}^{i}\|_{2,T}+\|u\|_{2,T}\right)

\displaystyle\leq C(h(N)^{1/2}\vee N^{-1})(1+\|u\|_{2,T})\|u\|_{2,T},

where we used Lemma
7.3
and the fact that
\hat{v}^{i}\in\mathcal{U}
in the last two inequalities. Similarly, using once more Fubini’s Theorem, Jensen and Hölder inequalities we get for the third
I_{3}
in (
7.25
):

\displaystyle I_{3}

\displaystyle=\left|{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left\langle b^{0},\frac{1}{N}\left(\sum_{j\not=i}\hat{v}^{j}+u\right)-\hat{\nu}\right\rangle_{L^{2}}\right]\right|

(7.27)

\displaystyle\leq\|b^{0}\|_{2,T}\left\|\frac{1}{N}\left(\sum_{j\not=i}\hat{v}^{j}+u\right)-\hat{\nu}\right\|_{2,T}

\displaystyle\leq\|b^{0}\|_{2,T}\left(\left\|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\hat{v}^{j}-\hat{\nu}\right\|_{2,T}+\frac{1}{N}\left\|\hat{v}^{i}-u\right\|_{2,T}\right)

\displaystyle\leq C(h(N)^{1/2}\vee N^{-1})\|b^{0}\|_{2,T}\left(1+\|\hat{v}^{i}\|_{2,T}+\|u\|_{2,T}\right)\|u\|_{2,T}

\displaystyle\leq C(h(N)^{1/2}\vee N^{-1})\|b^{0}\|_{2,T}\left(1+\|u\|_{2,T}\right)\|u\|_{2,T},

where we used again Lemma
7.3
and the fact that
\hat{v}^{i}\in\mathcal{U}
in the last two inequalities.

Lastly, for the first term
I_{1}
in (
7.25
) we obtain

\displaystyle I_{1}=

\displaystyle\left|{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left\langle\frac{1}{N}\left(\sum_{j\not=i}\hat{v}^{j}+u\right),\bm{A}_{1}\left(\frac{1}{N}\big(\sum_{j\not=i}\hat{v}^{j}+u\big)\right)\right\rangle_{L^{2}}-\langle\hat{\nu},\bm{A}_{1}\hat{\nu}\rangle_{L^{2}}\right]\right|

(7.28)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left|{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left\langle\frac{1}{N}\left(\sum_{j\not=i}\hat{v}^{j}+u\right),\bm{A}_{1}\left(\frac{1}{N}\big(\sum_{j\not=i}\hat{v}^{j}+u\big)-\hat{\nu}\right)\right\rangle_{L^{2}}\right]\right|

\displaystyle+\left|{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left\langle\frac{1}{N}\left(\sum_{j\not=i}\hat{v}^{j}+u\right)-\hat{\nu},\bm{A}_{1}\hat{\nu}\right\rangle_{L^{2}}\right]\right|.

Once can repeat similar steps as in (
7.26
) and (

) by using Lemma
7.3
in order to get

\displaystyle I_{3}

\displaystyle\leq C(h(N)^{1/2}\vee N^{-1})\left(1+\|u\|_{2,T}\right)\|u\|_{2,T}.

(7.29)

Then, from (

), (

).
∎

We are now ready to prove Theorem
4.15
.

Proof of Theorem
4.15
.

First, note that the inequality

J^{i,N}(\hat{v}^{i};\hat{v}^{-i})\leq\sup_{u\in\mathcal{U}}J^{i,N}(u;\hat{v}^{-i})

holds trivially by the definition of the supremum. Next, by virtue of Lemma
7.4
we get for any
u\in\mathcal{U}
the upper bound

\displaystyle J^{i,N}(u;\hat{v}^{-i})

\displaystyle\leq J^{i,\infty}(u,\hat{\nu})+C(h(N)^{1/2}\vee N^{-1})\|u\|_{2,T}(1+\|u\|_{2,T})

(7.30)

\displaystyle\leq J^{i,\infty}(\hat{v}^{i},\hat{\nu})+C(h(N)^{1/2}\vee N^{-1})\|u\|_{2,T}(1+\|u\|_{2,T}),

where
C>0
is a constant not depending on
u
or
N
and we used
J^{i,\infty}(\hat{u}^{i},\hat{\nu})=\sup_{u\in\mathcal{U}}J^{i,\infty}(u,\hat{\nu})
in the second inequality. Moreover, using Lemma
7.4
again we get for some constant
\tilde{C}>0
the upper bound

\displaystyle J^{i,\infty}(\hat{v}^{i},\hat{\nu})

\displaystyle\leq J^{i,N}(\hat{v}^{i},\hat{v}^{-i})+C(h(N)^{1/2}\vee N^{-1})\|\hat{v}^{i}\|_{2,T}(1+\|\hat{v}^{i}\|_{2,T})

(7.31)

\displaystyle\leq J^{i,N}(\hat{v}^{i},\hat{v}^{-i})+\tilde{C}(h(N)^{1/2}\vee N^{-1}),

where we used Lemma
7.3
and the fact that
\hat{v}^{i}\in\mathcal{U}
in the second inequality. Finally, using (
7.31
) to bound the right hand side of (
7.30
), we get

J^{i,N}(u;\hat{v}^{-i})\leq J^{i,N}(\hat{v}^{i},\hat{v}^{-i})+C(h(N)^{1/2}\vee N^{-1})\|u\|^{2}_{2,T}(1\vee\|u\|^{2}_{2,T})

for all
u\in\mathcal{U}
which completes the proof.
∎
