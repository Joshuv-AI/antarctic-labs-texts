Tight bound for independent domination of cubic graphs without 4 4 -cycles
Eun-Kyung Cho, Ilkyoo Choi, Hyemin Kwon, Boram Park

Abstract
Given a graph G G , a dominating set of G G is a set S S of vertices such that each vertex not in S S has a neighbor in S S . The domination number of G G , denoted γ ⁡ ( G ) \gamma(G) , is the minimum size of a dominating set of G G . The independent domination number of G G , denoted i ⁡ ( G ) i(G) , is the minimum size of a dominating set of G G that is also independent. Recently, Abrishami and Henning proved that if G G is a cubic graph with girth at least 6 6 , then i ⁡ ( G ) ≤ 4 11 ​ | V ⁡ ( G ) | i(G)\leq\frac{4}{11}|V(G)| . We show a result that not only improves upon the upper bound of the aforementioned result, but also applies to a larger class of graphs, and is also tight. Namely, we prove that if G G is a cubic graph without 4 4 -cycles, then i ⁡ ( G ) ≤ 5 14 ​ | V ⁡ ( G ) | i(G)\leq\frac{5}{14}|V(G)| , which is tight. Our result also implies that every cubic graph G G without 4 4 -cycles satisfies i ⁡ ( G ) γ ⁡ ( G ) ≤ 5 4 \frac{i(G)}{\gamma(G)}\leq\frac{5}{4} , which partially answers a question by O and West in the affirmative.

The

This concept appeared in the literature as early as 1962 by Berge
[

]

[

For a connected

The next natural step is to try to improve the upper bound on the independent domination number when the balanced complete bipartite graph is excluded.
Extending a result by Lam, Shiu, and Sun
[

[

Theorem 1.1

(

For

Another popular way to exclude the complete bipartite graph is to impose restrictions on cycle lengths.

Ratio of independent domination and number of vertices

Given a vertex
v
of a subcubic graph
G
, its
weight

w_{G}(v)
is defined as follows:

\displaystyle w_{G}(v)=\begin{cases}14&\text{ if }d(v)=0\\
9&\text{ if }d(v)=1\\
6&\text{ if }d(v)=2\\
5&\text{ if }d(v)=3\\
\end{cases}

The
weight
of a non-empty set
X\subseteq V(G)
, denoted
w_{G}(X)
, is defined by
w_{G}(X)=\displaystyle\sum_{v\in X}w_{G}(v)
, and
the
weight
of the entire graph
G
, denoted
w(G)
, is the weight of
V(G)
.
That is,

w(G)=w(V(G))=\displaystyle\sum_{v\in V(G)}w_{G}(v)=14n_{0}(G)+9n_{1}(G)+6n_{2}(G)+5n_{3}(G).

For a non-empty subset
X
of
V(G)
,
we denote the sum of weight changes of the vertices in
G-X
by
c_{G}(X)
.
That is,

\displaystyle c_{G}(X)=\sum_{v\in V(G-X)}(w_{G-X}(v)-w_{G}(v))=w(G-X)-(w(G)-w_{G}(X)).

To show Theorem
1.6
, suppose to the contrary that there exists a counterexample, and let
G
be a counterexample with the minimum number of vertices and edges.
This implies that

G
is a subcubic graph without
4
-cycles satisfying
14i(G)>w(G)
, and every proper subgraph
H
of
G
satisfies
14i(H)\leq w(H)
.

If
G
is the disjoint union of two graphs
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
, then by the minimality of
G
,

\displaystyle 14i(G)=14i(G_{1})+14i(G_{2})\leq w(G_{1})+w(G_{2})=w(G),

which is a contradiction.
Thus,
G
is connected.
One can check that the theorem holds for all graphs with at most four vertices, and all paths and cycles on at least five vertices.
Thus,
G
is a graph on at least five vertices and has a
3
-vertex.

We will often use the following observation.
For a vertex
v
, a vertex of
G-N[v]
cannot be adjacent to two vertices in
N[v]
since
G
has no
4
-cycles.
By the same reason, two vertices cannot have two common neighbors.
We now prove a key lemma that will be crucial in our future arguments.

Key Lemma
.

If
S
is an independent set of
G
, then
14|S|>w_{G}(N[S])-c_{G}(N[S])
.

Proof.

Let
U=N[S]
, and let
S^{\prime}
be a minimum independent dominating set of
G-U
. By the minimality of
G
, we have
w(G-U)\geq 14i(G-U)=14|S^{\prime}|
.
Since
S
is an independent dominating set of
G[U]
and
S\cup S^{\prime}
is an independent set,
S\cup S^{\prime}
is an independent dominating set of
G
.
Since

\displaystyle 14|S|+14|S^{\prime}|\geq 14i(G)>w(G)=w(G-U)+w_{G}(U)-c_{G}(U)\geq 14|S^{\prime}|+w_{G}(U)-c_{G}(U),

we obtain the desired conclusion.
∎

\partial(U)
is incident with a vertex in
X^{*}\cup Y^{*}
.

[Rule]
Let
uv\in\partial(U)
where
u\in U
.
If
u\in X^{*}
, then
u
sends
-3
to
v
. If
u\in Y^{*}
, then
u
sends
-1
to
v
.

Let
\mu^{*}(u)
be the final charge of each vertex
u
after applying the above rule.
We will obtain a contradiction by proving that the final charge of each vertex in
X^{*}\cup Y^{*}\cup(V(G)\setminus U)
is non-positive.

Claim 2.7
.

\mu^{*}(v)\leq 0
for a vertex
v\in X^{*}\cup Y^{*}\cup(V(G)\setminus U)
.

Proof.

Since an
X^{*}
-vertex is incident with at most one edge of
\partial(U)
and a
Y^{*}
-vertex is incident with at most two edges of
\partial(U)
, it is clear from the definitions that

\mu^{*}(v)\leq 0
for a vertex
v\in X^{*}\cup Y^{*}
.
Suppose to the contrary that there is a vertex
v\in V(G)\setminus U
such that
\mu^{*}(v)>0
.
Clearly,
v
is incident with a vertex in
X^{*}\cup Y^{*}
.
By [
Rule
], we have

w_{G-U}(v)-w_{G}(v)>3|X^{*}\cap N(v)|+|Y^{*}\cap N(v)|.

(2.2)

We divide the proof into two cases.

(Case 1)

Suppose that
v
has no
Y^{*}
-neighbor, so
v
must have an
X^{*}
-neighbor.
If
|X^{*}\cap N(v)|=1
, then
w_{G-U}(v)-w_{G}(v)\leq 3=3|X^{*}\cap N(v)|
, which is a contradiction to (
2.2
).
If
|X^{*}\cap N(v)|=3
, then
v
is a
3
-vertex in
G
, and
w_{G-U}(v)-w_{G}(v)=9=3|X^{*}\cap N(v)|
, which is a contradiction to (
2.2
).
Thus
|X^{*}\cap N(v)|=2
.
If
v
is a
3
-vertex, then
w_{G-U}(v)-w_{G}(v)=4<6=3|X^{*}\cap N(v)|
, which is a contradiction to (
2.2
).
Thus,
v
is a
2
-vertex with only
X^{*}
-neighbors, which is a contradiction to Claim
2.5
.

(Case 2)

Suppose that
v
has a
Y^{*}
-neighbor.
By the definition of
Y^{*}
, we see that
v
is a
3
-vertex.
By Claim
2.6
,
v
has exactly one
Y^{*}
-neighbor.
If
|X^{*}\cap N(v)|=0
,
then
w_{G-U}(v)-w_{G}(v)=1=3|X^{*}\cap N(v)|+|Y^{*}\cap N(v)|
, which is a contradiction to (
2.2
).
If
|X^{*}\cap N(v)|=1
,
then
w_{G-U}(v)-w_{G}(v)=4=3|X^{*}\cap N(v)|+|Y^{*}\cap N(v)|
, which is a contradiction to (
2.2
).
Thus
|X^{*}\cap N(v)|=2
, which implies that
v
is an
A_{0}
-vertex, since
X^{*}\subseteq A_{2}
and
Y^{*}\subseteq A_{1}
.
This is a contradiction since an
A_{0}
-vertex is not adjacent to an
A_{2}
-vertex.
∎

By Claim
2.7
, the final charge of each vertex is non-positive, and we reach a contradiction to (
2.1
).
Hence, the counterexample
G
cannot exist.

3
Ratio of independent domination and domination

In this section, we prove Theorem
1.8
.
Given a graph
G
and
v\in X\subseteq V(G)
, the
X
-external private neighborhood
of a vertex
v
is defined by
\epn(v,X)=\{u\in V(G)\setminus X\mid N_{G}[u]\cap X=\{v\}\}
.
A dominating set
X
of
G
inducing a graph with maximum degree at most
1
is a
near independent dominating set.

Lemma 3.1
.

For a near independent dominating set
X
of a cubic graph
G
,
i(G)\leq|X|+\frac{1}{2}n_{1}(G[X])
.

Proof.

We use induction on
n_{1}(G[X])
.
If
n_{1}(G[X])=0
, then
X
is an independent dominating set of
G
, so the statement holds since
i(G)\leq|X|
.

Now, assume
n_{1}(G[X])>0
.
Take a vertex
v\in X
with a neighbor in
X
.
Since
G
is cubic,
|\epn(v,X)|\leq 2
.
Take a maximal independent set
I
of
G
such that
I\subseteq\epn(v,X)
, and let
X^{\prime}=(X\cup I)\setminus\{v\}
. (Note that
I=\emptyset
when
\epn(v,X)=\emptyset
.)
Then
X^{\prime}
is a near independent dominating set of
G
such that
|X^{\prime}|\leq|X|+1
and
n_{1}(G[X^{\prime}])\leq n_{1}(G[X])-2
, so by the inductive hypothesis, we have
i(G)\leq|X^{\prime}|+\frac{1}{2}n_{1}(G[X^{\prime}])\leq(|X|+1)+\frac{1}{2}(n_{1}(G[X])-2)=|X|+\frac{1}{2}n_{1}(G[X])
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.8
.

Let
G
be a cubic graph without 4-cycles, and let

D
be a minimum dominating set of
G
with the minimum number of edges in
G[D]
.
Suppose that a vertex
v\in D
has two neighbors in
D
.
By the minimality of
|D|
, we have
|\epn(v,D)|=1
.
Then
D^{\prime}=(D\cup\epn(v,D))\setminus\{v\}
is a dominating set of
G
.
Note that
|D^{\prime}|=|D|
and
|E(G[D^{\prime}])|<|E(G[D])|
, which is a contradiction to the choice of
D
.
Thus, every vertex in
D
has at most one neighbor in
D
, so
D
is a near independent dominating set of
G
.

Let
m_{i}=n_{i}(G[D])
. Then

\displaystyle\gamma(G)=|D|=m_{0}+m_{1}.

(3.1)

If
m_{0}\geq m_{1}
,
then (
3.1
) implies
\gamma(G)\geq 2m_{1}
, so
together with Lemma
3.1
, we have the desired inequality since

i(G)\leq|D|+\frac{1}{2}m_{1}\leq\gamma(G)+\frac{1}{4}\gamma(G)=\frac{5}{4}\gamma(G).

If
m_{0}\leq m_{1}
, then (
3.1
) implies
\gamma(G)\geq 2m_{0}
, so by Theorem
1.5
, it holds that

i(G)\leq\frac{5}{14}|V(G)|\leq\frac{5}{14}\left(4m_{0}+3m_{1}\right)=\frac{5}{14}\left(3\gamma(G)+m_{0}\right)\leq\frac{5}{14}\left(3\gamma(G)+\frac{1}{2}\gamma(G)\right)=\frac{5}{4}\gamma(G),

where the second inequality is from the fact that
D
is a dominating set of a cubic graph, and the third equality is from (
3.1
).
∎
