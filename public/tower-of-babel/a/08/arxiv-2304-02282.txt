About optimal loss function for training physics-informed neural networks under respecting causality
Vasiliy A. Es’kin, Danil V. Davydov, Ekaterina D. Egorova, Alexey O. Malkhanov, Mikhail A. Akhukov, Mikhail E. Smorkalov

Abstract
A method is presented that allows to reduce a problem described by differential equations with initial and boundary conditions to the problem described only by differential equations. The advantage of using the modified problem for physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) methodology is that it becomes possible to represent the loss function in the form of a single term associated with differential equations, thus eliminating the need to tune the scaling coefficients for the terms related to boundary and initial conditions. The weighted loss functions respecting causality were modified and new weighted loss functions based on generalized functions are derived. Numerical experiments have been carried out for a number of problems, demonstrating the accuracy of the proposed methods.

Formulation of the problem

2.1
Physical-informed neural networks (PINNs)

Consider nonlinear partial differential equations (PDEs), which depend on time
t
and spatial coordinates
{\bf x}
, and in general take the following form

{\bf u}_{t}+\mathcal{N}[{\bf u}]=0,\quad t\in[0,T],\quad{\bf x}\in\Omega,

(1)

under the initial and boundary conditions

\displaystyle{\bf u}(0,{\bf x})={\bf g}({\bf x}),\quad{\bf x}\in\Omega,

(2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}[{\bf u}]=0,\quad t\in[0,T],\quad{\bf x}\in\partial\Omega,

(3)

where
{\bf u}(t,{\bf x})
denotes the latent solution that is governed by the PDE system of equation (
1
),
{\bf u}_{t}
is temporal derivative,
\mathcal{N}
is a nonlinear differential operator,
\mathcal{B}
is a boundary operator corresponding to boundary conditions (Dirichlet, Neumann, Robin, periodic conditions),
{\bf{g}}({\bf x})
is initial distribution of
{\bf u}
,
\Omega
and
\partial\Omega
are spatial domain and its boundary, respectively.

According to PINN approach
[
18
]
, which stands on the shoulders of the universal approximation theorem
[
45
]
, the aim of PINN is to approximate the unknown solution
{\bf u}(t,{\bf x})
with a deep neural network
{\bf u}_{{\bm{\theta}}}(t,{\bf x})
, where
{\bm{\theta}}
denote all trainable parameters (e.g., weight and biases) of the network. Finding optimal parameters is an optimization problem, which requires definition of a loss function such that its minimum gives the solution of the PDE. The physical-informed model is trained by minimizing the composite loss function which consists of the local residuals of the differential equation over the problem domain and its boundary as shown below:

\mathcal{L}({\bm{\theta}})=\lambda_{ic}\mathcal{L}_{ic}({\bm{\theta}})+\lambda_{bc}\mathcal{L}_{bc}({\bm{\theta}})+\lambda_{r}\mathcal{L}_{r}({\bm{\theta}}),

(4)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{ic}\left({\bm{\theta}}\right)=\frac{1}{N_{ic}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{ic}}\left|{\bf u}_{{\bm{\theta}}}\left(0,{\bf x}_{i}^{(ic)}\right)-{\bf g}\left({\bf x}_{i}^{(ic)}\right)\right|^{2},

(5)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{bc}\left(\bm{\theta}\right)=\frac{1}{N_{bc}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{bc}}\left|\mathcal{B}\left[{\bf u}_{{\bm{\theta}}}\right]\left(t_{i}^{(bc)},{\bf x}_{i}^{(bc)}\right)\right|^{2},

(6)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{r}\left(\bm{\theta}\right)=\frac{1}{N_{r}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{r}}\left|\mathcal{R}\left[{\bf u}_{{\bm{\theta}}}\right]\left(t_{i}^{(r)},{\bf x}_{i}^{(r)}\right)\right|^{2},

(7)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}\left[{\bf u}\right]:={\bf u}_{t}+\mathcal{N}\left[{\bf u}\left(t,{\bf x}\right)\right].

(8)

Here
\left\{{\bf x}_{i}^{(ic)}\right\}^{N_{ic}}_{i=1}
,
\left\{{t}_{i}^{(bc)},{\bf x}_{i}^{(bc)}\right\}^{N_{bc}}_{i=1}
, and
\left\{{t}_{i}^{(r)},{\bf x}_{i}^{(r)}\right\}^{N_{r}}_{i=1}
are sets of points corresponding to initial condition domain, boundary condition domain and PDE domain, respectively. These points can be the vertices of a fixed mesh or can be randomly sampled at each iteration of a gradient descent algorithm. All required gradients w.r.t. input variables (
t
and
\bf x
) and network parameters
\theta
can be efficiently computed via automatic differentiation
[
46
]
with algorithmic accuracy, which is defined by the accuracy of computation system. Note, the hyperparameters
\lambda_{ic}
,
\lambda_{bc}
and
\lambda_{r}
allow for separate tuning of learning rate for each of the loss terms in order to improve convergence of the model
[
47
,
48
]
.

The optimization problem can be defined as follow

{\bm{\theta}}^{*}=\underset{{\bm{\theta}}}{\arg}\min\mathcal{L}({\bm{\theta}}),

(9)

where
{\bm{\theta}}^{*}
are optimal parameters of the neural network which minimize the discrepancy between the exact unknown solution
{\bf u}
and the approximate one
{\bf u}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}}
. Schematic diagram of the general PINN method, which is used throughout this paper, is shown on the Figure
1
.

Let’s rewrite the loss components (
5
)–(
7
) in the continuous form for the further analysis. These representations are

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{ic}\left({\bm{\theta}}\right)=\frac{1}{V_{\Omega}}\int_{\Omega}\left|{\bf u}_{{\bm{\theta}}}\left(0,{\bf x}\right)-{\bf g}\left({\bf x}\right)\right|^{2}d{\bf x},

(10)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{bc}\left(\bm{\theta}\right)=\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\left|\mathcal{B}\left[{\bf u}_{{\bm{\theta}}}\right]\left(t,{\bf x}\right)\right|^{2}dt,\quad{\bf x}\in{\partial\Omega},

(11)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{r}\left(\bm{\theta}\right)=\frac{1}{T}\frac{1}{V_{\Omega}}\int^{T}_{0}dt\int_{\Omega}d{\bf x}\left|\mathcal{R}\left[{\bf u}_{{\bm{\theta}}}\right]\left(t,{\bf x}\right)\right|^{2},

(12)

where
V_{\Omega}
is the “volume” of domain
\Omega
.

2.2
Reformulation of the problem

2.2.1
Problem in time domain

Let’s modify the problem (
1
)–(
3
) to the one which is free from the initial conditions. For this we expand the time interval from infinitely distant moment in the past to the time
T
. On time interval
(-\infty,0]
the value of given process (
\bf U
) is assumed to be constant in time and has a spatial distribution described by the initial distribution (
2
). The value
\bf U
has the following form:

{\bf U}={\bf g}({\bf x})\chi(-t)+{\bf u}(t,{\bf x})\chi(t),\quad t\in(-\infty,T],

(13)

where
{\bf u}(t,{\bf x})
is unknown function that supposed to be a solution of the system of equations (
1
)–(
3
),
\chi(t)
is Heaviside step function
[
49
]
defined as

\chi(t)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0,&t<0,\\
1/2,&t=0,\\
1,&t>0.\end{array}\right.

(14)

In order the new function
\bf U
to be the solution at time interval
(-\infty,0]
the initial equation (
1
) should be supplied with additional term. Such term corresponds to “force” which disappears instantly at the moment
t=0
.

Without the loss of generality and for the sake of simplicity, we will consider the problem in an unbounded domain, i.e. without boundary conditions. As result the problem (
1
)–(
3
) is reduced to the following differential equations

{\bf U}_{t}+\mathcal{N}[{\bf U}]=\mathcal{N}[{\bf g}({\bf x})]\chi(-t),\quad t\in(-\infty,T],\quad{\bf x}\in\Omega,

(15)

where unknown function
{\bf u}(t,{\bf x})
is encapsulated into
\bf U
. As previously, we approximate the unknown solution
{\bf U}(t,{\bf x})
with a deep neural network
{\bf U}_{{\bm{\theta}}}(t,{\bf x})={\bf g}({\bf x})\chi(-t)+{\bf u}_{{\bm{\theta}}}(t,{\bf x})\chi(t)
.

In this problem statement, the loss function for problem (
15
) contains only the term corresponding to residuals of differential equation:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}({\bm{\theta}})=\mathcal{L}_{r}({\bm{\theta}}),

(16)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{r}\left(\bm{\theta}\right)=\frac{1}{T}\frac{1}{V_{\Omega}}\int^{T}_{-\infty}dt\int_{\Omega}d{\bf x}\left|\mathcal{R}^{*}\left[{\bf U}_{{\bm{\theta}}},{\bf g}\right]\left(t,{\bf x}\right)\right|^{2},

(17)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{*}\left[{\bf u},{\bf g}\right]:={\bf u}_{t}+\mathcal{N}\left[{\bf u}\left(t,{\bf x}\right)\right]-\mathcal{N}[{\bf g}({\bf x})]\chi(-t).

(18)

Expanding the integrand in (
17
) we have

\hskip-14.22636pt\mathcal{R}^{*}\left[{\bf U}_{{\bm{\theta}}},{\bf g}\right]\left(t,{\bf x}\right){=}\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathcal{R}\left[{\bf u}_{{\bm{\theta}}}\right]\left(t,{\bf x}\right){+}\left[{\bf u}_{{\bm{\theta}}}\left(t,{\bf x}\right){-}{\bf g}\left({\bf x}\right)\right]\delta(t),&t\geq 0,\\
0&t<0.\end{array}\right.

(19)

Here
\delta(t)
is Dirac delta function
[
50
]
. We use the following properties of delta function and Heaviside step function
[
50
]

\displaystyle\delta(x)=\frac{\partial\chi(x)}{\partial x},\quad\chi(-x)=1-\chi(x).

(20)

Substitution of (
19
) to (
17
) gives expression for loss in the following representation

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{r}\left(\bm{\theta}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T}\frac{1}{V_{\Omega}}\lim_{t\to 0}\delta(t)\int_{\Omega}d{\bf x}\left|{\bf u}_{{\bm{\theta}}}\left(0,{\bf x}\right){-}{\bf g}\left({\bf x}\right)\right|^{2}

\displaystyle+2\frac{1}{T}\frac{1}{V_{\Omega}}\int_{\Omega}d{\bf x}\left|\mathcal{R}\left[{\bf u}_{{\bm{\theta}}}\right]\left(0,{\bf x}\right)\left[{\bf u}_{{\bm{\theta}}}\left(0,{\bf x}\right){-}{\bf g}\left({\bf x}\right)\right]\right|

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{T}\frac{1}{V_{\Omega}}\int^{T}_{0}dt\int_{\Omega}d{\bf x}\left|\mathcal{R}\left[{\bf u}_{{\bm{\theta}}}\right]\left(t,{\bf x}\right)\right|^{2}.

(21)

Here we have taken into account the property of delta function which states its dominance in the finite sum, i.e.
\left|a\delta(x)+b\right|^{2}\simeq\left|a\right|^{2}\delta^{2}(x)+2\left|ab\right|\delta(x)+\left|b\right|^{2}
.

2.3
Optimization problem reformulation

Then we apply the well known representation of the Dirac delta function
[
50
]

\displaystyle\delta(x)=\lim_{\sigma\to 0}\frac{\sigma}{x^{2}+\sigma^{2}}.

(22)

We obtain the following formulation of the loss function for (
9
)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\left(\bm{\theta}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T}\frac{1}{V_{\Omega}}\int^{T}_{0}dt\int_{\Omega}d{\bf x}\left|\mathcal{R}\left[{\bf u}_{{\bm{\theta}}}\right]\left(t,{\bf x}\right)\right|^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{T}\frac{1}{V_{\Omega}}\left\{2\int_{\Omega}d{\bf x}\left|\mathcal{R}\left[{\bf u}_{{\bm{\theta}}}\right]\left(0,{\bf x}\right)\left[{\bf u}_{{\bm{\theta}}}\left(0,{\bf x}\right){-}{\bf g}\left({\bf x}\right)\right]\right|\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\to 0\\
t\to 0\end{subarray}}\frac{\sigma}{t^{2}+\sigma^{2}}\int_{\Omega}d{\bf x}\left|{\bf u}_{{\bm{\theta}}}\left(0,{\bf x}\right){-}{\bf g}\left({\bf x}\right)\right|^{2}\right\}.

(23)

To proceed from continuous time to the discrete time representation, we divide the time interval
[0,T]
into
N_{t}
intervals and use the rectangular rule of integration for this mesh with step size
T/N_{t}
. We take
\sigma=T/N_{t}
for the approximation of the delta function in the time domain. This representation of delta function is valid as it preserves its fundamental property
[
50
]
[
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x)\delta(x-a)dx=f(a)
] for the numerical integration. Finally, we get the following loss function

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\left(\bm{\theta}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{t}}\frac{1}{V_{\Omega}}\sum\limits_{i=0}^{N_{t}}\int_{\Omega}d{\bf x}\left|\mathcal{R}\left[{\bf u}_{{\bm{\theta}}}\right]\left(t_{i},{\bf x}\right)\right|^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{T}\frac{1}{V_{\Omega}}\left\{2\int_{\Omega}d{\bf x}\left|\mathcal{R}\left[{\bf u}_{{\bm{\theta}}}\right]\left(t_{0},{\bf x}\right)\left[{\bf u}_{{\bm{\theta}}}\left(t_{0},{\bf x}\right){-}{\bf g}\left({\bf x}\right)\right]\right|\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\frac{N_{t}}{T}\int_{\Omega}d{\bf x}\left|{\bf u}_{{\bm{\theta}}}\left(t_{0},{\bf x}\right){-}{\bf g}\left({\bf x}\right)\right|^{2}\right\}.

(24)

Here we take
t_{0}=0
. Note, the multiplier
{N_{t}}
of third term of (
24
) coincides with coefficient
\lambda_{ic}
empirically found in
[
19
,
20
,
51
]
in the case of
N_{t}=100
.

With this representation, the approximation of delta function is associated with a numerical integration scheme. For a number of tasks though, an approximation of the delta function is possible that is not tied to integration rules. To achieve that the value
\sigma
in (
22
) can be taken as an estimated relaxation time of the initial conditions to zero, or of doubling or halving values of initial conditions. Note that obtained approximation of the delta function can be used as a weight
\lambda_{ic}
in the original training scheme described by loss terms (
5
)–(
7
). Example of deriving such approximation for specific problem is provided in section
4.3.1
.

2.3.1
MAE loss

Interestingly, if instead of MSE loss we use MAE loss for optimization problem (
9
), then no additional representation of the Dirac delta function is required and instead of loss (
23
) we have the following function

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\left(\bm{\theta}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T}\frac{1}{V_{\Omega}}\left(\int^{T}_{0}dt\int_{\Omega}d{\bf x}\left|\mathcal{R}\left[{\bf u}_{{\bm{\theta}}}\right]\left(t,{\bf x}\right)\right|+\int_{\Omega}d{\bf x}\left|{\bf u}_{{\bm{\theta}}}\left(0,{\bf x}\right){-}{\bf g}\left({\bf x}\right)\right|\right).

(25)

Based on our experiments, training a neural network with such a loss function converges extremely weak to the required solution, despite the simplicity of the equation (
25
). For this reason, in all of the experiments presented in section
4
, MSE loss has been used.

2.4
Problem in spatial-temporal domain

We can reformulate the problem in spatial domain in the same manner. For greater clarity and brevity, and without violating generality, we will consider a problem with dependence on only one spatial coordinate
x
.

Let spatial domain be determined by a coordinate
x
(
x\in[X_{1},X_{2}]
), and problem (
1
)–(
3
) is subject to the following boundary conditions

\displaystyle{\bf u}(t,X_{1})={\bf f_{1}}(t),\quad{\bf u}(t,X_{2})={\bf f_{2}}(t).

(26)

Then, artificially extended solution of (
1
)–(
3
) takes the following form

\displaystyle{\bf U}

\displaystyle={\bf g}({x})\chi(-t)+{\bf u}(t,{x})\chi(t)\left[\chi(x-X_{1})-\chi(x-X_{2})\right]

\displaystyle+\left[{\bf f_{1}}(t)\chi(X_{1}-x)+{\bf f_{2}}(t)\chi(x-X_{2})\right]\chi(t),

(27)

\displaystyle{\text{for the domain }}t\in(-\infty,T],\quad x\in(-\infty,\infty).

In this case, the problem (
1
)–(
3
) is reduced to the following differential equations

\displaystyle{\bf U}_{t}+\mathcal{N}[{\bf U}]

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}[{\bf g}({x})]\chi(-t)

\displaystyle+\left[\frac{\partial{\bf f_{1}}(t)}{\partial t}\chi(X_{1}-x)+\frac{\partial{\bf f_{2}}(t)}{\partial t}\chi(x-X_{2})\right]\chi(t)

\displaystyle+\left\{\mathcal{N}\left[{\bf f_{1}}(t)\right]\chi(x-X_{1})+\mathcal{N}\left[{\bf f_{2}}(t)\right]\chi(X_{2}-x)\right\}\chi(t),\quad t\in(-\infty,T],\quad x\in(-\infty,\infty).

(28)

The value
\mathcal{R}^{*}\left[{\bf U}_{{\bm{\theta}}},{\bf g}\right]\left(t,{x}\right)
has form similar to (
19
)

\hskip-14.22636pt\mathcal{R}^{*}\left[{\bf U}_{{\bm{\theta}}},{\bf g}\right]\left(t,{x}\right){=}\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\mathcal{R}\left[{\bf U}^{*}_{{\bm{\theta}}}\right]\left(t,{x}\right)&\hskip-8.53581pt{+}\left[{\bf u}_{{\bm{\theta}}}\left(t,{x}\right){-}{\bf g}\left({x}\right)\right]\delta(t)&\\
&\hskip-36.98857pt-\left[\frac{\partial{\bf f_{1}}(t)}{\partial t}\chi(X_{1}-x)+\frac{\partial{\bf f_{2}}(t)}{\partial t}\chi(x-X_{2})\right]&\\
&\hskip-36.98857pt-\left\{\mathcal{N}\left[{\bf f_{1}}(t)\right]\chi(x-X_{1})+\mathcal{N}\left[{\bf f_{2}}(t)\right]\chi(X_{2}-x)\right\},&t\geq 0,\quad x\in[X_{1},X_{2}],\\
0,&&t<0,\quad\text{or }x\notin[X_{1},X_{2}],\end{array}\right.

(29)

where

\displaystyle{\bf U}^{*}_{{\bm{\theta}}}

\displaystyle={\bf u}_{{\bm{\theta}}}(t,{x})\left[\chi(x-X_{1})-\chi(x-X_{2})\right]+{\bf f_{1}}(t)\chi(X_{1}-x)+{\bf f_{2}}(t)\chi(x-X_{2}).

(30)

The representation of
\mathcal{R}\left[{\bf U}^{*}_{{\bm{\theta}}}\right]
in terms of
\mathcal{R}\left[{\bf u}_{{\bm{\theta}}}\right]
similarly to equation (
19
) depends on the specific type of differential equation. Example of such representation can be found in section
4.2.1
.

3
Causal training for physics-informed neural network

It was suggested in
[
19
]
to reformulate the loss function so that it would respect the physical causality when solving PDEs with the help of PINNs:

\mathcal{L}_{r}\left(\bm{\theta}\right)=\frac{1}{N_{t}}\sum_{i=0}^{N_{t}}{w}_{i}\mathcal{L}_{r}\left(t_{i},{\bm{\theta}}\right),

(31)

where weights
w_{i}
are

w_{i}=\exp\left[-\varepsilon\sum_{k=0}^{i-1}\mathcal{L}_{r}\left(t_{k},{\bm{\theta}}\right)\right],\quad\text{for }i=1,2,3,\ldots,N_{t},\text{and }w_{0}=1,

(32)

and where
\varepsilon
is causality parameter that controls the steepness of the weights
w_{i}
. The temporal residual loss [
\mathcal{L}_{r}\left(t_{i},{\bm{\theta}}\right)
] for the total loss (
23
) is

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{r}\left(t_{i},{\bm{\theta}}\right)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{V_{\Omega}}\int_{\Omega}d{\bf x}\left\{\left|\mathcal{R}\left[{\bf u}_{{\bm{\theta}}}\right]\left(t_{i},{\bf x}\right)\right|^{2}\right.

\displaystyle+2\frac{N_{t}}{T}\delta_{0i}\left|\mathcal{R}\left[{\bf u}_{{\bm{\theta}}}\right]\left(t_{0},{\bf x}\right)\left[{\bf u}_{{\bm{\theta}}}\left(t_{0},{\bf x}\right){-}{\bf g}\left({\bf x}\right)\right]\right|

\displaystyle\left.+\frac{N_{t}^{2}}{T^{2}}\delta_{0i}\left|{\bf u}_{{\bm{\theta}}}\left(t_{0},{\bf x}\right){-}{\bf g}\left({\bf x}\right)\right|^{2}\right\},

(33)

\delta_{0i}
is the Kronecker symbol.

In the case when domain
\Omega
consists of single spatial dimension
x
(
x\in[X_{1},X_{2}]
,
V_{\Omega}=X_{2}-X_{1}
) the temporal residual loss has discrete form under regular mesh with
N_{x}
th nodes

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{r}\left(t_{i},{\bm{\theta}}\right)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N_{x}}\sum_{n=0}^{N_{x}}\left\{\left|\mathcal{R}\left[{\bf u}_{{\bm{\theta}}}\right]\left(t_{i},{x_{n}}\right)\right|^{2}\right.

\displaystyle+2\frac{N_{t}}{T}\delta_{0i}\left|\mathcal{R}\left[{\bf u}_{{\bm{\theta}}}\right]\left(t_{0},{x_{n}}\right)\left[{\bf u}_{{\bm{\theta}}}\left(t_{0},{x_{n}}\right){-}{\bf g}\left({x_{n}}\right)\right]\right|

\displaystyle\left.+\frac{N_{t}^{2}}{T^{2}}\delta_{0i}\left|{\bf u}_{{\bm{\theta}}}\left(t_{0},{x_{n}}\right){-}{\bf g}\left({x_{n}}\right)\right|^{2}\right\}.

(34)

The resulting weighted residual loss is

\mathcal{L}_{r}\left(\bm{\theta}\right)=\frac{1}{N_{t}}\sum_{i=0}^{N_{t}}\exp\left[-\varepsilon\sum_{k=0}^{i-1}\mathcal{L}_{r}\left(t_{k},{\bm{\theta}}\right)\right]\mathcal{L}_{r}\left(t_{i},{\bm{\theta}}\right).

(35)

Authors of
[
19
]
recognized that the weights
w_{i}
should be large — and therefore allow the minimization of
\mathcal{L}_{r}\left(t_{i},{\bm{\theta}}\right)
— only if all residuals
\left\{\mathcal{L}_{r}\left(t_{k},{\bm{\theta}}\right)\right\}_{k=0}^{i}
before
t_{i}
are minimized properly, and vice versa. As a consequence,
\mathcal{L}_{r}\left(t_{i},{\bm{\theta}}\right)
is not minimized unless all previous residuals
\left\{\mathcal{L}_{r}\left(t_{k},{\bm{\theta}}\right)\right\}_{k=0}^{i-1}
decrease to some small value such that
w_{i}
is large enough.

3.1
Modification of the causal training for physics-informed neural network

Despite the causal training method significantly improves the convergence of neural network learning this approach does not scale for time grids with the growth of number of nodes. This can be explained by the fact that the exponent of the weighting function (
32
) contains the cumulative sum. This sum is increasing with the increase of the
N_{t}
and such behavior hinders learning of neural network for large
t
. Note that this feature in row of cases can be lead to the best convergence of training in compared to other methods of weighting.

This drawback becomes obvious when trying to write the residual loss (
35
) in continuous form

\mathcal{L}_{r}\left(\bm{\theta}\right)=\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\lim_{N_{t}\to\infty}\left[\exp\left(-\varepsilon\frac{N_{t}}{t}\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{L}_{r}\left(t^{\prime},{\bm{\theta}}\right)dt^{\prime}\right)\right]\mathcal{L}_{r}\left(t,{\bm{\theta}}\right)dt.

(36)

This integral tends to zero for any
\varepsilon
and time due to unlimited increase of
N_{t}
. Such disadvantage prevents mathematical analysis of the loss function using standard methods based on integral and differential calculus.

In order to mitigate the above drawback, the residual loss (
35
) can be rewritten in the following form

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{r}\left(\bm{\theta}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{t}}\sum_{i=0}^{N_{t}}w_{i}\mathcal{L}_{r}\left(t_{i},{\bm{\theta}}\right),

(37)

\displaystyle w_{i}

\displaystyle=\exp\left[-\varepsilon s_{i}\right],

(38)

\displaystyle s_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{i}\sum_{k=0}^{i-1}\mathcal{L}_{r}\left(t_{k},{\bm{\theta}}\right).

(39)

The continuous form of residual loss (
37
) is non zero and has the following representation

\mathcal{L}_{r}\left(\bm{\theta}\right)=\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}dt\exp\left[-\frac{\varepsilon}{t}\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\mathcal{L}_{r}\left(t^{\prime},{\bm{\theta}}\right)\right]\mathcal{L}_{r}\left(t,{\bm{\theta}}\right).

(40)

3.2
Alternatives of the causal training for physics-informed neural network

3.3
Heaviside–weighted residual loss

Let’s consider residual loss weighted using Heaviside step function

\mathcal{L}_{r}\left(\bm{\theta}\right)=\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\left\{1-\chi\left[S(t)\right]\right\}\mathcal{L}_{r}\left(t,{\bm{\theta}}\right)dt,

(41)

where

S(t)=\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{L}_{r}\left(t^{\prime},{\bm{\theta}}\right)dt^{\prime}.

(42)

In this case term
1-\chi(x)
in the integrand of equation (
41
) does not tend to zero under condition
x\leq 0
. It means that causality condition is satisfied. To calculate the loss we use the following well known representations of the Heaviside step function
[
50
]

\chi(x)=\lim_{\varepsilon\to\infty}\frac{1}{1+\exp\left(-2\varepsilon x\right)}.

(43)

The equation (
43
) leads to the following representation of residual loss

\mathcal{L}_{r}\left(\bm{\theta}\right)=\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}dt\frac{1}{1+\exp\left[2\varepsilon S(t)\right]}\mathcal{L}_{r}\left(t,{\bm{\theta}}\right),

(44)

which has the discrete form (
31
) with weights

\displaystyle w_{i}=\left[1+\exp\left({2\varepsilon}s_{i}\right)\right]^{-1}.

(45)

3.3.1
Dirac–weighted residual loss

Let’s consider the extreme form of the weighted residual loss

\mathcal{L}_{r}\left(\bm{\theta}\right)=\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\delta\left[S(t)\right]\mathcal{L}_{r}\left(t,{\bm{\theta}}\right)dt.

(46)

Such loss function has well-pronounced property that it prohibits training for large time stamps without have been previously trained for small ones.

To proceed with the discrete form of this loss function we will use the following well known representation of the Dirac delta function
[
50
]

\displaystyle\delta(x)=\lim_{\sigma^{2}\to 0}\frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\left(-\frac{x^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right).

(47)

The integral form of residual loss in this case is

\mathcal{L}_{r}\left(\bm{\theta}\right)=\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}dt\exp\left\{-\varepsilon\left[\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}t^{\prime}\mathcal{L}_{r}\left(t^{\prime},{\bm{\theta}}\right)\right]^{2}\right\}\mathcal{L}_{r}\left(t,{\bm{\theta}}\right).

(48)

Note, here the causality properties of weights are further enhanced with multiplying by
t
inside the inner integral. The discrete form of (
48
) in case of equidistant time slices is

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{r}\left(\bm{\theta}\right)=\frac{1}{N_{t}}\sum_{i=0}^{N_{t}}w_{i}\mathcal{L}_{r}\left(t_{i},{\bm{\theta}}\right),

(49)

\displaystyle w_{i}=\exp\left(-\varepsilon\tilde{s}_{i}^{2}\right),

(50)

\displaystyle\tilde{s}_{i}=\frac{1}{i}\sum_{k=0}^{i-1}k\mathcal{L}_{r}\left(t_{k},{\bm{\theta}}\right).

(51)

When deriving the equation (
51
) we have taken into account that
t_{k}=kT/N_{t}
and multiplier
T/N_{t}
of
\tilde{s}_{i}
was “hidden” in causality parameter
\varepsilon
in (
50
).

3.4
Expanding of the causal training approach to the spatial-temporal domain

For problem (
2.4
) the loss function has the following representation

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\left(\bm{\theta}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T}\frac{1}{V_{\Omega}}\int^{T}_{0}dt\int_{\Omega}d{x}\left|\mathcal{R}^{*}\left[{\bf U}^{*}_{{\bm{\theta}}}\right]\left(t,{x}\right)\right|^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{T}\frac{1}{V_{\Omega}}\left\{2\int_{\Omega}d{x}\left|\mathcal{R}^{*}\left[{\bf U}^{*}_{{\bm{\theta}}}\right]\left(0,{x}\right)\left[{\bf u}_{{\bm{\theta}}}\left(0,{x}\right){-}{\bf g}\left({x}\right)\right]\right|\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\to 0\\
t\to 0\end{subarray}}\frac{\sigma}{t^{2}+\sigma^{2}}\int_{\Omega}d{x}\left|{\bf u}_{{\bm{\theta}}}\left(0,{x}\right){-}{\bf g}\left({x}\right)\right|^{2}\right\}.

(52)

The weighted residual loss in the spatial-temporal domain can be defined as follows

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{*}_{r}\left(\bm{\theta}\right)=\frac{1}{N_{t}}\sum_{i=0}^{N_{t}}w_{i}\mathcal{L}^{*}_{r}\left(t_{i},{\bm{\theta}}\right),

(53)

\displaystyle w_{i}=\exp\left[-\varepsilon s_{i}\right],\quad s_{i}=\frac{1}{i}\sum_{k=0}^{i-1}\mathcal{L}^{*}_{r}\left(t_{k},{\bm{\theta}}\right),

(54)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{*}_{r}\left(t_{i},{\bm{\theta}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{x}}\sum_{n=0}^{N_{x}}\left(w_{n}^{(\to)}+w_{n}^{(\leftarrow)}\right)\left\{\left|\mathcal{R}\left[{\bf U}^{*}_{{\bm{\theta}}}\right]\left(t_{i},{x_{n}}\right)\right|^{2}\right.

\displaystyle+2\frac{N_{t}}{T}\delta_{0i}\left|\mathcal{R}\left[{\bf U}^{*}_{{\bm{\theta}}}\right]\left(t_{0},{x_{n}}\right)\left[{\bf u}_{{\bm{\theta}}}\left(t_{0},{x_{n}}\right){-}{\bf g}\left({x_{n}}\right)\right]\right|

\displaystyle\left.+\frac{N_{t}^{2}}{T^{2}}\delta_{0i}\left|{\bf u}_{{\bm{\theta}}}\left(t_{0},{x_{n}}\right){-}{\bf g}\left({x_{n}}\right)\right|^{2}\right\},

(55)

and

\displaystyle w_{n}^{(\to)}

\displaystyle=\exp\left[-\varepsilon s^{(\to)}_{n}\right],\quad w_{n}^{(\leftarrow)}=\exp\left[-\varepsilon s^{(\leftarrow)}_{n}\right],

\displaystyle s^{(\to)}_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{r}\left(x_{k},{\bm{\theta}}\right),\quad s^{(\leftarrow)}_{n}=\frac{1}{N_{x}-n}\sum_{k=0}^{N_{x}-n-1}\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{r}\left(x_{N_{x}-k},{\bm{\theta}}\right).

(56)

The spatial residual loss is

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{r}\left(x_{k},{\bm{\theta}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{t}}\sum_{i=0}^{N_{t}}\left|\mathcal{R}\left[{\bf u}_{{\bm{\theta}}}\right]\left(t_{i},{x_{k}}\right)\right|^{2}.

(57)

Here superscripts
(\to)
and
(\leftarrow)
mean weighting in the forward and backward directions along the
x
axis, respectively (i.e. from
X_{1}
to
X_{2}
and from
X_{2}
to
X_{1}
).

5
Conclusion

In this article, we presented several approaches to neural network training within the framework of the PINN concept. These approaches simplify the construction and analysis of the loss function and improve the training convergence. We have also proposed the neural network architectures which are more relevant for the problems under consideration.

We have reformulated the original problem described by the differential equation and the initial and boundary conditions into the problem described only by the differential equation. The major advantage of such a trick is that it becomes possible to reduce the loss function to the single term associated with the differential equations, thus eliminating the need to tune the scaling coefficients for the terms associated with the boundary and initial conditions. Based on this approach we have derived closed-form expressions for the MAE and MSE losses. Approximations of the Dirac delta function, which is part of the losses in the MSE formulation, have been proposed. These approximations are determined by the discretization method of the domain or by the behavior of the expected solution at the initial time and at the boundaries of the domain.

Inspired by
[
19
]
we have proposed loss functions based on causality and spatial-temporal locality principles within the framework of the formalism of generalized functions such as Heaviside step and Dirac delta functions. It has also been shown that the causality training can be extended to the spatial domains and can be transformed into the spatial-temporal training.

Finally, a modification of the MLP model for the Korteweg–De Vries problem has been proposed based on
[
59
]
. This modification is based on the fundamental solution of the Korteweg–De Vries equation and the nonlinear superposition principle for the solitons. It has been shown that such a neural network with heterogeneous activation functions can be trained much faster and the obtained results have significantly better accuracy compared to MLP with homogeneous activation functions. In addition, such neural network is much better able to extrapolate to new domains.

Numerical experiments have been performed for a number of problems and the accuracies of the proposed methods are given. The causal trainings with weights based on the Dirac delta function were achieved the best results in all carried out the numerical experiments.
