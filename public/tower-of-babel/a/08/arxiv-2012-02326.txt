Introduction

1.1.
The reduced Hartree-Fock equation

The success of the Hartree-Fock and density functional theories in revealing the electronic structure of matter warrants their use as a starting point in the derivation of emergent macroscopic properties of quantum matter.

Here, one of the central problems is the derivation of macroscopic Maxwell’s equations in dielectrics.
The first attack on such a derivation was made in the pioneering works of E. Cancès, M. Lewin and G. Stoltz and W. E and J. Lu and their collaborators (

The REHF obtained from the Hartree-Fock equation (HFE) by omitting the exchange term, see below.
and the Kohn-Sham equation (KSE) of the density functional theory (DFT) at zero temperature.
The first treatment of the positive temperature REHF was given by Levitt (

In this paper, we consider the REHF at positive temperature, which is also a simplified DFT equation, and derive from it
the linearized effective Poisson-Boltzmann equation of electrostatics, widely used in molecular and structural biology (see e.g.

For a positive temperature
T

of the electron (or generally any Fermi) gas, as

(1.1)

\displaystyle\rho=\operatorname{den}[f_{T}(h_{\rho}-\mu)],

where
\operatorname{den}:A\rightarrow\rho_{A}
is the map from operators,
A
, to functions
\rho_{A}(x):=A(x,x)
(here

stands for the integral kernel of an operator

),
f_{T}(\lambda)
is the Fermi-Dirac distribution,

(1.2)

\displaystyle f_{T}(\lambda):=f_{FD}(\lambda/T),\ \quad f_{FD}(\lambda)=\frac{1}{e^{\lambda}+1}\,

(due to the Fermi-Dirac statistic),

\mu
is the chemical potential and

is a self-adjoint one-particle Hamiltonian depending on the density
\rho
(self-consistency). Since

is self-adjoint the r.h.s. of (

is given by

\displaystyle h_{\rho}:=-\Delta-v*(\kappa-\rho)\,,

where

is an inter-particle pair potential. It is taken to be the electrostatic potential, as specified below.

Let
L^{2}_{\rm loc}\equiv L^{2}_{\rm loc}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
denote the space of locally square integrable functions. We fix a lattice
\mathcal{L}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and let

be the space of

be the orthogonal complement of the constant functions in

. In what follows, we assume

and

is the electrostatic potential,

in
3

v*f=(-\Delta)^{-1}f,

for

, so that

is well-defined.

The decomposition

The

For

1.2.
Electrostatic potential

Due to the choice
v*f=(-\Delta)^{-1}f
, the electrostatic potential
\phi=v*(\kappa-\rho)
satisfies the Poisson equation

(1.4)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\Delta\phi=\kappa-\rho.

Plugging
\rho
from (
1.1
) into this equation and taking
v*(\kappa-\rho)=\phi
in (
1.3
), we find the equation for
\phi

(1.5)

\displaystyle-\Delta\phi=\kappa-\operatorname{den}[f_{T}(h^{\phi}-\mu)],

where

(1.6)

\displaystyle h^{\phi}=-\Delta-\phi.

We can recover
\rho
from
\phi
via equation (
1.4
) or the equation

(1.7)

\displaystyle\rho=\operatorname{den}[f_{T}(h^{\phi}-\mu)].

Let
H^{s}
and
H^{s}_{\rm per}
be the Sobolev spaces corresponding to
L^{2}
and
L^{2}_{\rm per}
. If
\kappa,\rho\in L^{2}+L^{2}_{\rm per}
,
\phi\in H^{2}+H^{2}_{\rm per}
and

and
\rho-\kappa
satisfy (

), then

(1.8)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\rho_{\rm per}=\int_{\Omega}\kappa_{\rm per},

where
\Omega
is an arbitrary fundamental cell of
\mathcal{L}
and the subindex ‘per’ denotes the periodic part of the corresponding function (
\in L^{2}+L^{2}_{\rm per}
). Indeed, let
\Lambda_{n}:=\cup_{\lambda\in\mathcal{L}_{n}}(\Omega+\lambda)
, where
\mathcal{L}_{n}:=\mathcal{L}\cap[-n,n]^{d}
. Integrating (
1.4
) over the domain
\Lambda_{n}
and using the Stokes’ theorem, we find

\int_{\partial\Lambda_{n}}\nabla\phi=\int_{\Lambda_{n}}(\kappa-\rho).

Since
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{|\Lambda_{n}|}\int_{\partial\Lambda_{n}}\nabla\phi=0
and
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{|\Lambda_{n}|}\int_{\Lambda_{n}}(\kappa-\rho)=\int_{\Omega}(\rho_{\gamma}-\kappa)_{\rm per}
, the last relation gives (
1.8
).

Eq (
1.8
) shows that

, i.e. it satisfies the conditions mentioned in the paragraph after (

Eq (
1.8

\mu
and expresses the conservation of the charge per fundamental cell of

. It is considered as the solvability condition and should be added to (
1.1
) in the periodic case.

In what follows we associate with a solution

of (

(1.9)

\displaystyle\phi_{\rho}=(-\Delta)^{-1}(\kappa-\rho)\,,

and with a solution

of Eq. (

according to (

1.3.
Relation to the TEHF and KSE

The key positive temperature HFE is given by

(1.10)

\displaystyle\gamma=f_{T}(h_{\gamma}-\mu),

where
f_{T}(\lambda)
is as above and, for an external charge distribution
\kappa
,

(1.11)

\displaystyle h_{\gamma}:=-\Delta-v*(\kappa-\rho_{\gamma})+ex(\gamma)\,.

Here, recall,
\rho_{\gamma}(x):=\gamma(x,x)
and
v*f=(-\Delta)^{-1}f
, and
ex(\gamma)
(the exchange term) is the operator with the integral kernel

ex(\gamma)(x,y):=-v(x-y)\gamma(x,y)
, where
\gamma(x,y)
is the integral kernel of
\gamma
. Observing that
h_{\gamma}\big|_{ex(\gamma)=0}=h_{\rho_{\gamma}}
, where
h_{\rho}
is given in (
1.3
), one sees that (
1.10
) with
ex(\gamma)=0
implies the equation

(1.12)

\displaystyle\gamma=f_{T}(h_{\rho_{\gamma}}-\mu).

Eq. (
1.12
) is equivalent to Eq. (
1.1
). Indeed, applying the map
\operatorname{den}
to equation (
1.12
) gives (
1.1
).
In the opposite direction,
if
\rho
solves (
1.1
), then the density operator

(1.13)

\displaystyle\gamma=f_{T}(h_{\rho}-\mu),

acting on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, solves (
1.12
). Thus, (
1.12
) is the TREHF in terms of the density operator
\gamma
.

By replacing
ex(\gamma)
in (
1.11
) by a local exchange-correlation term
\text{xc}(\rho)
and then applying, as above, the map
\operatorname{den}
to the resulting equation, one obtains the natural extension of the original Kohn-Sham equation to positive temperatures:

(1.14)

\displaystyle\rho=\operatorname{den}[f_{T}(h_{\rho}^{\rm KS}-\mu)],

(1.15)

\displaystyle h_{\rho}^{\rm KS}:=-\Delta-v*(\kappa-\rho)+\text{xc}(\rho)\,.

1.4.
The origin of the TEHF/TREHF equations

As the TEHF and TREHF arise in the same way, in order to avoid repetitions, we consider here only the later.

Equation (
1.12
) originates from the static version

(1.16)

\displaystyle[h_{\rho_{\gamma}},\gamma]=0

of the time dependent RHF equation (see e.g.
[
12
]
for a review)

(1.17)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\gamma=i[h_{\rho_{\gamma}},\gamma]\,.

Indeed, ignoring symmetries and accidental divergence,
\gamma
solves (
1.16
) if and only if
\gamma
solves
\gamma=f((h_{\rho_{\gamma}}-\mu)/T)
for some reasonable function
f
. (The parameters
T
and
\mu
are of no significance at this stage; they are introduced for future reference.)

The selection of
f
is done on physics grounds, either bringing the system in question in contact with a thermal reservoir at temperature
T
and the chemical potential
\mu
, or passing to the thermodynamic limit. This leads to Eq. (
1.12
).

As we discuss below, Eq. (
1.12
) is the Euler-Lagrange equation for the natural free energy.

Remark.

If the particles in question were bosons, then
f_{FD}
would be replaced by the Bose-Einstein distribution

(1.18)

\displaystyle f_{BE}(\lambda)=\frac{1}{e^{\lambda}-1}\,.

Approach.

As in
[
23
]
, our starting point is Eq. (
1.5
) for the electrostatic potential
\phi
. We also use some important ideas from
[
8
]
. However, our approach to proving Theorem

is fairly novel. Rather that employing variations-based techniques, we use the Lyapunov-Schmidt reduction, which also allows us to estimate the remainders.

The starting equation of our analysis can be formulated as follows. Let
(\phi_{\rm per}(x),\mu_{\rm per})
be the solution of (
1.5
), with
\kappa(x)=\kappa_{\rm per}(x)
, and let
\kappa_{\delta}
be given in (
1.23
).
Define
\psi
by the equality

\phi=\phi_{\rm per}+\psi.

Plugging this decomposition into (
1.5
), with
\kappa=\kappa_{\delta}
and
\mu=\mu_{\rm per}
, and using that

, we arrive at the equation for
\psi

(1.44)

\displaystyle-\Delta\psi={\kappa^{\prime}}_{\delta}-\operatorname{den}[g_{\phi_{\rm per}^{\prime}}(\psi)],

where
\phi_{\rm per}^{\prime}:=\phi_{\rm per}+\mu_{\rm per}
,
g_{\phi_{\rm per}^{\prime}}(\psi):=f_{T}(h^{\phi_{\rm per}^{\prime}}-\psi)-f_{T}(h^{\phi_{\rm per}^{\prime}})
and
{\kappa^{\prime}}_{\delta}(y):=\delta^{3}\kappa^{\prime}(\delta y)

This is a nonlinear and nonlocal Poisson equation for

\phi_{\rm per}^{\prime}:=\phi_{\rm per}+\mu_{\rm per}
, but not
\kappa_{\rm per}
, enters Eq. (

Though we deal with the simplest microscopic model - the reduced HF equation - our techniques are fairly robust and would work for the full-fledged DFT. Also, we favoured rough estimates to more precise but lengthier ones which produce better bounds on

2.1.
Locally trace class operators

Let
C_{c}\equiv C_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
denote the space of compactly supported continuous functions on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. An operator
A
on
L^{2}
is said to be locally trace class if
fA
and
Af
are trace class for all
f\in C_{c}
. (For the proofs below, it suffices to require that
fA
is trace class.)

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a Bravais lattice on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
and
\Omega
a fundamental domain of
\mathcal{L}
as in Section
1.5
. Denote
|S|
to be the volume of a measurable set
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
and note that
|\Omega|
is independent of the choice of the fundamental cell
\Omega
. Let
T_{s}
be the translation operator

(2.1)

\displaystyle T_{s}:f(x)\mapsto f(x-s).

We say that a function
f:\mathbb{R}^{3}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
is
\mathcal{L}
-periodic if and only if it is invariant under the translations action of
T_{s}
for all lattice elements
s\in\mathcal{L}
. We define the space

(2.2)

\displaystyle L^{p}_{\rm per}\equiv L^{p}_{\rm per}(\mathbb{R}^{3})=\{f\in L^{p}_{\rm loc}(\mathbb{R}^{3}):f\text{ is $\mathcal{L}$-periodic}\},

with the norm of
L^{p}(\Omega)
for some
\Omega
. The norms for
L^{p}_{\rm per}
and
L^{p}\equiv L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
are distinguished by the subindices
L^{p}_{\rm per}
and
L^{p}
.

We say that a bounded operator
A
on
L^{2}
is
\mathcal{L}
-periodic if and only if
[A,T_{s}]=0
for all
s\in\mathcal{L}
where
T_{s}
is the translation operator defined in (
2.1
).

Let
S^{p}
be the standard
p
-Schatten space of bounded operators on
L^{2}
with the
p
-Schatten norm

(2.3)

\displaystyle\|A\|_{S^{p}}^{p}:=

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}_{L^{2}}((A^{*}A)^{p/2}).

Next, let
\chi_{Q}
denote the characteristic function of a set
Q\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
and let
S^{p}_{\rm per}
be the space of bounded,
\mathcal{L}
-periodic operators
A
on
L^{2}
with
\|A\|_{S^{p}_{\rm per}}<\infty
where

(2.4)

\displaystyle\|A\|_{S^{p}_{\rm per}}^{p}:=

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}_{\Omega}((A^{*}A)^{p/2}):=\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\mathrm{Tr}_{L^{2}}(\chi_{\Omega}(A^{*}A)^{p/2}\chi_{\Omega}).

We remark that the
S_{\rm per}^{2}
norm does not depend on the choice of
\Omega
since
A
is
\mathcal{L}
-periodic. We have the following estimates for the densities in terms of Schatten norms.

2.2.
Densities

For a locally trace class operator
A
, we define its density
\operatorname{den}[A]
to be a regular countably additive complex Borel measure satisfying

(2.5)

\displaystyle\int\operatorname{den}(A)f=\mathrm{Tr}(fA),

for every
f\in C_{c}
. If
\mathrm{Tr}(fA)
is continuous in
f
in the
C_{c}
-topology, then the Riesz representation theorem shows that (
2.5
), for every
f\in C_{c}
, define
\operatorname{den}[A]
uniquely. In our case, we will frequently stipulate stronger regularity assumptions on
A
, implying that
\operatorname{den}[A]
is actually in a reasonable function space. (e.g. Lemma
2.1
below).

If an operator
A
has an (distributional) integral kernel,
A(x,y)
, with the diagonal,
A(x,x)
, being a regular countably additive complex Borel measure, then

(2.6)

\displaystyle\operatorname{den}(A)(x)=A(x,x).

Finally, den is a linear map on locally trace class operators with the property that for any
f\in C_{c}
,

(2.7)

\displaystyle\operatorname{den}(fA)=f\operatorname{den}(A).

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
A
be a locally trace class operator on
L^{2}
and
\epsilon>0
. We have the following statements.

(1)

If
(1-\Delta)^{3/4+\epsilon}A\in S^{2}
, resp.

, then
\operatorname{den}[A]\in L^{2}
, resp.
L^{2}_{\rm per}
. Moreover, respectively,

(2.8)

\displaystyle\|\operatorname{den}[A]\|_{L^{2}}\lesssim\|(1-\Delta)^{3/4+\epsilon}A\|_{S^{2}},

(2.9)

\displaystyle\|\operatorname{den}[A]\|_{L^{2}_{\rm per}}\lesssim|\Omega|^{1/2}\|(1-\Delta)^{3/4+\epsilon}A\|_{S^{2}_{\rm per}}

(2)

If
(1-\Delta)^{1/4+\epsilon}A\in S^{6/5}
, then
\operatorname{den}[A]\in\dot{H}^{-1}
(where
\dot{H}^{s}
is defined in (
1.24

(2.10)

\displaystyle\|\operatorname{den}[A]\|_{\dot{H}^{-1}}\lesssim\|(1-\Delta)^{1/4+\epsilon}A\|_{S^{6/5}}

Proof.

We prove (
2.9
) and (
2.10
) only; (

-periodic, its density, if it exists, is also

-periodic. By the

-

duality, relation (

and (

(2.11)

\displaystyle|\mathrm{Tr}_{\Omega}(fA)|\lesssim|\Omega|^{1/2}\|f\|_{L^{2}_{\rm per}}\|(1-\Delta)^{3/4+\epsilon}A\|_{S^{\rm per}_{2}}

for all

with support in
\Omega

. Since the support of

is in

(2.12)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\Omega|}|\mathrm{Tr}_{\Omega}

\displaystyle(fA)|=\frac{1}{|\Omega|}|\mathrm{Tr}(\chi_{\Omega}fA\chi_{\Omega})|

\displaystyle\lesssim\|A(1-\Delta)^{3/4+\epsilon}\|_{S^{2}_{\rm per}}\|(1-\Delta)^{-3/4-\epsilon}f\|_{S^{2}_{\rm per}}.

By the Kato-Seiler-Simon inequality

(2.14)

\displaystyle\|f(x)g(-i\nabla)\|_{S^{p}}\lesssim\|f\|_{L^{p}}\|g\|_{L^{p}}

for

(see
[
36

; one can also replace

Now we prove (

duality, it suffices to show that

(2.15)

\displaystyle|\mathrm{Tr}(fA)|\lesssim\|f\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}\|(1-\Delta)^{1/4+\epsilon}A\|_{S^{6/5}}

for all

and for

. So, we estimate

(2.16)

\displaystyle|\mathrm{Tr}(fA)|\lesssim\|f(1-\Delta)^{-1/4-\epsilon}\|_{S^{6}}\|(1-\Delta)^{1/4+\epsilon}A\|_{S^{6/5}}.

The Kato-Seiler-Simon inequality (

\displaystyle|\mathrm{Tr}(fA)|\lesssim\|f\|_{L^{6}}\|(1-\Delta)^{1/4+\epsilon}A\|_{S^{6/5}}.

Now, applying the Gagliardo-Nirenberg-Sobolev inequality (for

(2.18)

\displaystyle\|f\|_{L^{6}}\lesssim\|\nabla f\|_{L^{2}}

2.3.

Let
\mathcal{L}^{*}
denote the lattice reciprocal to
\mathcal{L}
, with the reciprocity relation between bases for
\mathcal{L}
and
\mathcal{L}^{*}
given by

Define the (fiber integral) space

(2.19)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{L}}^{\oplus}=\{f\in L^{2}_{\rm loc}

\displaystyle(\mathbb{R}^{3}_{k}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}_{x}):T_{s}^{x}f=f

(2.20)

\displaystyle\text{ and }T_{r}^{k}f=e^{-ir\cdot x}f,\ \,\forall s\in\mathcal{L},\ \forall r\in\mathcal{L}^{*}\},

where

is the translation in the

We write

\displaystyle f=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}/\mathcal{L}^{*}}^{\oplus}\,f_{k}\,d\hat{k}=\int_{\Omega^{*}}^{\oplus}\,f_{k}\,d\hat{k},

for some choice of a fundamental cell
\Omega^{*}
of the reciprocal lattice

and

We use the Bloch-Floquet decomposition

into

as

(2.22)

\displaystyle U_{\rm BF}f:=\int^{\oplus}_{\Omega^{*}}d\hat{k}f_{k}\,,

(2.23)

\displaystyle f_{k}(x):=\sum_{t\in\mathcal{L}}e^{-ik(x+t)}f(x+t)

and the inverse Bloch-Floquet transform

(2.24)

\displaystyle U_{\rm BF}^{-1}\big(\int^{\oplus}_{\Omega^{*}}d\hat{k}f_{k}\big)(x):=\int_{\Omega^{*}}d\hat{k}\,e^{ikx}f_{k}(x),\,\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}.

Lemma 2.2
.

We have, for any

,

(2.25)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}f_{k}(x)dx=\hat{f}(k)

Proof.

By (
2.23
) and a change of variable, we see that

(2.26)

\displaystyle\int f_{k}(x)dx:=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{L}}e^{-ik(x+t)}f(x+t)d{x}

(2.27)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{t\in\mathcal{L}}\int_{t+\Omega}e^{-ikx}f(x)d{x}

(2.28)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int e^{-ikx}f(x)dx.

Equation (
2.25
) follows from the definition of the Fourier transform.
∎

Let
\langle f\rangle_{S}=|S|^{-1}\int_{S}f(x)dx
, the average of

on a set

, and

be the indicator (characteristic) function of

.
