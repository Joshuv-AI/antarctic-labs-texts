Monotonicity of the quantum 2-Wasserstein distance
Rafał Bistroń, Michał Eckstein, Karol Życzkowski

Abstract
We study a quantum analogue of the 2-Wasserstein distance as a measure of proximity on the set Ω N \Omega_{N} of density matrices of dimension N N . We show that such (semi-)distances do not induce Riemannian metrics on the tangent bundle of Ω N \Omega_{N} and are typically not unitary invariant. Nevertheless, we prove that for N = 2 N=2 dimensional Hilbert space the quantum 2-Wasserstein distance (unique up to rescaling) is monotonous with respect to any single-qubit quantum operation and the solution of the quantum transport problem is essentially unique. Furthermore, for any N ≥ 3 N\geq 3 and the quantum cost matrix proportional to a projector we demonstrate the monotonicity under arbitrary mixed unitary channels. Finally, we provide numerical evidence which allows us to conjecture that the unitary invariant quantum 2-Wasserstein semi-distance is monotonous with respect to all CPTP maps in any dimension N N .

Introduction

The distances on the space of probability vectors are a primary tool to compare and analyse various statistical distributions. On the mathematical side, they lie at the heart of information geometry
Amari1
;
Amari2
, which unveils a powerful interplay between Riemannian geometry and statistics. In particular, with some distances on the space of probability vectors one can associate a Riemannian metric on the suitable tangent bundle. In the set of all such distances the one generated by the Fischer–Rao metric is distinguished as the unique continuous distance monotone under classical stochastic maps (Cencov theorem
cencov
).

In the modern field of quantum information
NielsenChuang
;
BZ17
classical probability vectors are replaced by density matrices. The quantum analogue of a stochastic map is a completely positive trace preserving (CPTP) linear map,
\Phi:\Omega_{N}\to\Omega_{M}
, where
\Omega_{N}\vcentcolon=\{\rho\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}\>|\>\rho=\rho^{*},\ \rho\geq 0,\ \Tr\rho=1\}
is the set of density matrices of order
N
. A semi-distance
D_{\text{mon}}
on the space of quantum states is called
monotone
if the inequality

\displaystyle D_{\text{mon}}(\Phi(\rho^{A}),\Phi(\rho^{B}))\leq D_{\text{mon}}(\rho^{A},\rho^{B})

(1.1)

holds for all states
\rho^{A},\rho^{B}\in\Omega_{N}
, for any dimension
N
, and all CPTP maps
\Phi
.

The set of all monotone Riemannian metrics on the tangent bundle of
\Omega_{N}
, can be characterised, via the Morozova–Cencov–Petz theorem
Morozova
;
Petz1
, through a single-parameter operator-monotone function. On the other hand, there exist important monotone distances, which do not induce a Riemannian metric — most notably, the trace distance
D_{\text{Tr}}(\rho^{A},\rho^{B})=\Tr|\rho^{A}-\rho^{B}|
, with
|X|=\sqrt{XX^{\dagger}}
.

The property of monotonicity is crucial for applications in quantum information processing. It implies that the distinguishability, quantified by a given distance function, cannot increase under an application of any quantum map.

First attempts to generalise the classical Monge transport distance to the quantum setting with the use of Husimi function were done in
ZS98
;
ZS01
. More recently, a more general approach of Kantorovich

and Wasserstein

was extended to the quantum case an analysed from the mathematical

;
CGGT17
;
BGJ19
;
Fri20
;

, physical

;

;
CGP20
;
CM20
;

and information-theoretical
CGNT17
;
PMTL20
;
PT21
;
KdPMLL21
perspective.
Among these, a recent proposal
transport1
;
transport2
is based on the optimization over the set of bipartite quantum states with fixed marginals of the expectation value of a certain ‘cost observable’ (see also
qWGAN
;
YZYY18
;
Reira18
). Concretely, for any classical distance
d
on the set of
N
points one can define

an associated

\displaystyle C^{Q}_{E}=\sum_{j>i=1}^{N}E_{ij}|\psi^{-}_{ij}\rangle\langle\psi^{-}_{ij}|,

(1.2)

where

|\psi^{-}_{ij}\rangle=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|i\rangle|j\rangle-|j\rangle|i\rangle)

one introduces the set

optimal quantum transport cost
,

\displaystyle T^{Q}_{E}(\rho^{A},\rho^{B})\vcentcolon=\min_{\rho^{AB}\in\Gamma^{Q}(\rho^{A},\rho^{B})}\Tr\ \big(C^{Q}_{E}\rho^{AB}\big).

(1.3)

It was shown in

that
T^{Q}_{E}
is a weak metric (i.e. a semi-metric bounded from below by a genuine metric) on
\Omega_{N}
for any
N
and any quantum cost matrix
C^{Q}_{E}
. Following the classical analogy, one can thus define

the corresponding
quantum 2-Wasserstein semi-distance

\displaystyle W_{E}(\rho^{A},\rho^{B})\vcentcolon=\sqrt{\min_{\rho^{AB}\in\Gamma^{Q}(\rho^{A},\rho^{B})}\Tr\ \Big((C^{Q}_{E})^{2}\rho^{AB}\Big)}.

(1.4)

On the 2-point set all classical distance matrices
E
are equivalent up to rescaling. In higher dimensions a distinguished role is played by the simplex geometry, for which
E_{ij}=1-\delta_{ij}
. In this case, the corresponding quantum cost matrix (

. For sake of brevity we shall denote such a quantum cost matrix by

\displaystyle C^{Q}\vcentcolon=\tfrac{1}{2}(\mathbbm{1}_{N}-S),\quad\text{ where }\quad S(|x\rangle|y\rangle)=|y\rangle|x\rangle

(1.5)

and, since

\displaystyle W(\rho^{A},\rho^{B})\vcentcolon=\sqrt{T^{Q}(\rho^{A},\rho^{B})},\;\text{ with }\;T^{Q}(\rho^{A},\rho^{B})\vcentcolon=\min_{\rho^{AB}}\ \Tr\big( C^Q \rho^{AB} \big).

(1.6)

For single-qubit states,
N=2
, it was shown

;

enjoys the triangle inequality and hence is a genuine distance on the Bloch ball
\Omega_{2}
. Furthermore, the numerical simulations strongly suggest that the triangle inequality actually holds for the quantum 2-Wasserstein semi-distance (
1.6

1.5

The quantum optimal transport problem (

\displaystyle T^{Q}_{E}(\rho^{A},\rho^{B})=\sup_{(\sigma^{A},\sigma^{B})\in\Sigma_{N}}\;\Tr(\sigma^A\rho^A + \sigma^B\rho^B).

(1.8)

If neither of the states

is pure, then the supremum is achieved

The quantum optimal transport cost (

. Moreover it seems to be a valuable tool for quantum

In particular, the quantum 2-Wasserstein semi-distance (

The primary purpose of this work is to study the monotonicity with respect to the quantum channels of the optimal quantum transport cost

defined above. We start in Section

)

2
No unitary invariance for general quantum 2-Wasserstein semi-distances

In the single-qubit case,
N=2
, the only (up to a trivial multiplicative factor) quantum cost matrix (
1.2
) is a projector in the antisymmetric subspace (
1.5
). But already in the qutrit case,
N=3
, there exists different cost matrices
C^{Q}_{E}
, for instance the one induced by the classical Euclidean distance on the line,
E_{12}=E_{23}=1
,
E_{13}=2
(see the Supplemental Material in
transport1
).

For the projection matrix
C^{Q}
the optimal quantum transport cost (
1.3
) is invariant under unitary channels:

\displaystyle T^{Q}(U\rho^{A}U^{\dagger},U\rho^{B}U^{\dagger})=T^{Q}(\rho^{A},\rho^{B}),\quad\text{ for any }\quad U\in\mathrm{U}(N).

This stems from the fact that
(U\otimes U)C^{Q}(U^{\dagger}\otimes U^{\dagger})=C^{Q}
, for any unitary matrix
U\in\mathrm{U}(N)
.

The latter property, however, does not hold for a general quantum cost matrix (
1.2
). This implies that one cannot expect the monotonicity to hold in full generality for
T^{Q}_{E}
, even under unitary channels. Indeed, let us take two qutrit states

\displaystyle\rho^{A}=\tfrac{1}{2}\big(|1\rangle+|2\rangle\big)\big(\langle 1|+\langle 2|\big),\qquad\rho^{B}=\tfrac{1}{5}\big(|1\rangle+2|2\rangle\big)\big(\langle 1|+2\langle 2|\big)

and the quantum cost matrix induced by the line geometry,

\displaystyle C^{Q}_{E}=|\psi^{-}_{12}\rangle\langle\psi^{-}_{12}|+|\psi^{-}_{23}\rangle\langle\psi^{-}_{23}|+2|\psi^{-}_{13}\rangle\langle\psi^{-}_{13}|.

Consider now a unitary channel, which interchanges the states
|2\rangle
and
|3\rangle
,

U=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&0&-1\\
0&1&0\\
\end{pmatrix}

and yields

\displaystyle\eta^{A}

\displaystyle=U\rho^{A}U^{\dagger}=\tfrac{1}{2}\big(|1\rangle+|3\rangle\big)\big(\langle 1|+\langle 3|\big),

\displaystyle\eta^{B}

\displaystyle=U\rho^{B}U^{\dagger}=\tfrac{1}{5}\big(|1\rangle+2|3\rangle\big)\big(\langle 1|+2\langle 3|\big).

Since all of the involved states are pure, there is only one coupling matrix for both pairs
(
transport2
, Lemma A.3)
,
\rho^{AB}=\rho^{A}\otimes\rho^{B}
and
\eta^{AB}=\eta^{A}\otimes\eta^{B}
. We thus have

\displaystyle T^{Q}_{E}(U\rho^{A}U^{\dagger},U\rho^{B}U^{\dagger})=\Tr C^{Q}_{E}\eta^{AB}=\tfrac{1}{10}>T^{Q}_{E}(\rho^{A},\rho^{B})=\Tr C^{Q}_{E}\rho^{AB}=\tfrac{1}{20}.

Let us note that the lack of unitary invariance of a distance on the space of quantum states can be a desirable property in certain applications. In particular, the quantum Wasserstein distance of order 1 proposed in
PMTL20
, which is not unitarily invariant, offers improved efficiency in quantum learning algorithms
KdPMLL21
.

3
The lack of Riemannian structure

Let us now focus on the case of the projective cost matrix
C^{Q}
. The corresponding transport cost (
1.6
) can be associated with
SWAP-fidelity

transport1
,

F_{S}(\rho^{A},\rho^{B})\vcentcolon=\max_{\rho^{AB}\in\Gamma^{Q}(\rho^{A},\rho^{B})}\big(\Tr\,S\rho^{AB}\big)=1-2T^{Q}(\rho^{A},\rho^{B}).

(3.1)

Clearly, the monotonicity of
T^{Q}
under CPTP maps is equivalent to the reverse monotonicity of the SWAP-fidelity,
F_{S}(\Phi(\rho^{A}),\Phi(\rho^{B}))\geq F_{S}(\rho^{A},\rho^{B})
. The SWAP-fidelity shares many properties with the standard Uhlmann–Jozsa quantum fidelity
Uh76
;
Jo94
and equals to the latter if either of the states
\rho^{A},\rho^{B}
is pure
transport1
.

The quantum fidelity
F
is indeed reverse monotone under all CPTP maps
Jo94
. Consequently, any distance on the space of quantum state determined by a strictly decreasing function
h:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
,
D_{h,F}(\rho^{A},\rho^{B})=h(F(\rho^{A},\rho^{B}))
, will automatically be monotone. The prominent examples include the root infidelity,
I\vcentcolon=\sqrt{1-F}
, the Bures distance,
B\vcentcolon=\sqrt{2(1-\sqrt{F})}
and the Bures angle,
A\vcentcolon=\tfrac{2}{\pi}\arccos\sqrt{F}
. As the SWAP-fidelity (
3.1
) shares many important properties with the standard Uhlmann–Jozsa fidelity, one might expect it to be reverse monotone as well.

All of the above-mentioned fidelity-based distances generate Riemannian metrics on the tangent bundle of
\Omega_{N}
, for any
N
. More precisely (see e.g.
SIOR17
and
Amari1
), a semi-distance

generates a Riemannian metric

on

if the expansion

\displaystyle d(\rho,\rho+tv)^{2}=g_{\rho}(v,v)t^{2}+o(t^{2}),\text{ as }t\to 0^{+},

(3.2)

holds for any
\rho\in\Omega_{N}
and any
v\in T_{\rho}\Omega_{N}=\{v\in H_{N},\text{ with }\Tr v=0\}
.

All monotone Riemannian metrics on the set of quantum states, are characterised via the Morozova–Cencov–Petz theorem
Morozova
;
Petz1
. The latter gives an explicit formula for the metric
g
in terms of a single operator monotone function.
However, there are important distances, which do
not
generate a Riemannian metric, but are nevertheless monotone. A classical example is the
l_{1}
-distance (the ‘taxicab distance’), which corresponds to the trace distance in the quantum case. It turns out that the quantum 2-Wasserstein distance (
1.6
) shares this feature with the trace distance.

Let us focus on the case

and show the failure of (

) for the quantum 2-Wasserstein distance (

\displaystyle\rho(r,\theta)=\tfrac{1}{2}\big[\mathbbm{1}+(2r-1)(\sigma_{1}\sin\theta+\sigma_{3}\cos\theta)\big],\quad\text{ with }r\in[0,1],\theta\in[0,\pi),

For any

4
Monotonicity under general mixed unitary channels

Recall that the CPTP maps can be conveniently characterised with the help of Kraus operators. For any channel
\Phi:\Omega_{N}\to\Omega_{M}
there exists a set of
N\times M
matrices
\{K_{i}\}_{i=1}^{R}
, with
R\leq NM
, called Kraus operators, such that

\sum_{i}K_{i}^{\dagger}K_{i}=\mathbbm{1}_{N}

(4.1)

and for any
\rho\in\Omega_{N}

\Phi(\rho)=\sum_{i}K_{i}\rho K_{i}^{\dagger}.

(4.2)

Conversely, quantum channels can be constructed through Kraus operators, since every set of
N\times M
matrices satisfying (
4.1
) defines a CPTP map via (
4.2
).

A particular class of quantum channels arises from the statistical mixtures of unitary maps. In such a case, we have
K_{i}=\sqrt{p_{i}}U_{i}
, where
U_{i}\in U(N)
and
p_{i}\geq 0
,
\sum_{i}p_{i}=1
. The general properties of the projective quantum transport cost imply the following result:

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
\Psi
be a mixed unitary channel on
\Omega_{N}
, then for any
\rho^{A},\rho^{B}\in\Omega_{N}
we have

\displaystyle T^{Q}\big(\Psi(\rho^{A}),\Psi(\rho^{B})\big)\leq T^{Q}(\rho^{A},\rho^{B}).

Proof.

This follows directly from the joint convexity of the transport cost
(
transport2
, Proposition 2.2)
and the unitary invariance of
T^{Q}
.
∎

The convexity of the optimal quantum transport cost
T^{Q}_{E}
implies a more general result, which holds for any quantum cost matrix (
1.2
).

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
\Phi_{1},\ldots,\Phi_{R}
be quantum channels under which the optimal quantum transport cost is monotonous:

T_{E}^{Q}\big(\Phi_{i}(\rho^{A}),\Phi_{i}(\rho^{B})\big)\leq T_{E}^{Q}(\rho^{A},\rho^{B})\quad\text{ for all }\;\rho^{A},\rho^{B}\in\Omega_{N}\text{ and }i\in\{1,\ldots,R\}.

Then,
T^{Q}_{E}
is monotonous under any convex combination of the channels
\Phi_{1},\ldots,\Phi_{R}
,

T_{E}^{Q}\big(\Psi(\rho^{A}),\Psi(\rho^{B})\big)\leq T_{E}^{Q}(\rho^{A},\rho^{B})\quad\text{ for all }\;\Psi=\sum_{i}p_{i}\Phi_{i},\text{ with }p_{i}\geq 0,\sum_{i}p_{i}=1.

The last result implies that it is sufficient to study the monotonicity of the optimal quantum transport under the
extremal channels
, i.e. the ones which cannot be decomposed as a convex combination of CPTP maps.

We can also invoke the dual formulation (
1.8
) to derive the following useful result:

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
\Phi:\Omega_{N}\to\Omega_{M}
be a CPTP map characterised by Kraus operators
\{K_{i}\}
and denote by
\Phi^{*}:H_{M}\to H_{N}
the map dual to
\Phi
, ie.
\Phi^{*}(\sigma)=\sum_{i}K_{i}^{\dagger}\sigma K_{i}
. If for any pair of matrices
\big(\sigma^{A},\sigma^{B}\big)\in\Sigma_{M}
, with
\Sigma_{M}
defined in (
1.7
), we have
\big(\Phi^{*}(\sigma^{A}),\Phi^{*}(\sigma^{B})\big)\in\Sigma_{N}
, then
T^{Q}_{E}
is monotone with respect to the map
\Phi
,
T^{Q}_{E}\big(\Phi(\rho^{A}),\Phi(\rho^{B})\big)\leq T^{Q}_{E}(\rho^{A},\rho^{B})
.

Proof.

Clearly, if
\sigma
is a Hermitian matrix, then so is
\Phi^{*}(\sigma)
. From the dual formulation of the optimal quantum transport problem (
1.8
), we deduce

\displaystyle T^{Q}_{E}\big(\Phi(\rho^{A}),\Phi(\rho^{B})\big)

\displaystyle=\sup_{(\sigma^{A},\sigma^{B})\in\Sigma_{M}}\Tr( \sigma^A \Phi(\rho^A) + \sigma^B \Phi(\rho^B) )

\displaystyle=\sup_{(\sigma^{A},\sigma^{B})\in\Sigma_{M}}\Tr( \Phi^*(\sigma^A) \rho^A + \Phi^*(\sigma^B) \rho^B )

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{(\eta^{A},\eta^{B})\in\Sigma_{N}}\Tr\big( \eta^A \rho^A + \eta^B \rho^B \big)=T^{Q}_{E}(\rho^{A},\rho^{B}).

∎

5
Monotonicity under arbitrary single-qubit channels

Let us now focus on the single qubit case,
N=2
. If we write a state

\rho(\vec{r})=\frac{1}{2}\big(\mathbbm{1}+\vec{r}\cdot\vec{\sigma}\big)

\displaystyle\Phi(\rho)=U\big[\Phi_{d}\big(V\rho V^{\dagger}\big)\big]U^{\dagger},

\displaystyle K_{2}

Lemma 5.1

Let
\Phi:\Omega_{2}\to\Omega_{2}

\sigma^{A},\sigma^{B}\in H_{2}
are diagonal. If

then also

It turns out that the matrices

yielding the maximum in (

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
\rho^{A}
,
\rho^{B}\in\Omega_{2}
be mixed quantum states

commute.

This fact is shown in the course of the proof of Theorem 5.1 in

∎

We are now in a position to present the main result of this paper.

Theorem 5.3
.

The optimal quantum transport cost is monotonous under all CPTP maps
\Phi:\Omega_{2}\to\Omega_{2}
.

Proof.

Thanks to the unitary invariance of the transport cost
T^{Q}
we can restrict ourselves to channels
\Phi_{d}
defined by Eq. (
5.1
). Furthermore, by Proposition
4.2
, it is sufficient to check the monotonicity for extremal single-qubit channels. Such channels are completely characterised by two Kraus operators (
5.2
), with

,
v\in[0,\pi)
such that either

or
|\cos u\cdot\cos v|<1
(see Theorem 1.4 in
RSW01
). We can thus use the characterisation (
5.2
) and apply Lemma
5.2
.

Now, assume that the states
\rho^{A},\rho^{B}\in\Omega_{2}
are non-isospectral and neither of them is pure. By Lemma
5.2
we know that the Hermitian matrices saturating the dual problem,
\underline{\sigma}^{A},\underline{\sigma}^{B}
, commute. Let then
V\in U(2)
be the matrix diagonalizing simultaneously
\underline{\sigma}^{A}
and
\underline{\sigma}^{B}
. Using the unitary invariance of

we obtain

\displaystyle T^{Q}\big(\Phi(\rho^{A}),\Phi(\rho^{B})\big)

\displaystyle=T^{Q}\Big(V^{\dagger}\Phi(\rho^{A})V,V^{\dagger}\Phi(\rho^{B})V\Big)

\displaystyle=\max_{(\sigma^{A},\sigma^{B})\in\Sigma_{2}}\Tr( \sigma^A V^\dagger\Phi(\rho^A) V + \sigma^B V^\dagger\Phi(\rho^B) V)

\displaystyle=\Tr( \us^A V^\dagger\Phi(\rho^A) V + \us^B V^\dagger\Phi(\rho^B) V)

\displaystyle=\Tr( \Phi^*(V \us^A V^\dagger) \rho^A + \Phi^*(V \us^B V^\dagger) \rho^B ).

Now, Lemma
5.1
implies that

\displaystyle T^{Q}\big(\Phi(\rho^{A}),\Phi(\rho^{B})\big)

\displaystyle=\Tr( \Phi^*(V \us^A V^\dagger) \rho^A + \Phi^*(V \us^B V^\dagger) \rho^B )

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{(\eta^{A},\eta^{B})\in\Sigma_{2}}\Tr\big( \sigma^A \rho^A + \sigma^B \rho^B \big)=T^{Q}(\rho^{A},\rho^{B}).

This inequality extends by the continuity of

and

\rho^{A},\rho^{B}
.
∎

a\in\mathbb{R}
, does not affect neither the operator
F
, nor the value of the optimal quantum transport cost (
1.8
). Consequently, we can eliminate one of these four parameters. It is convenient to use the following parametrisation:

\displaystyle c\vcentcolon=\Tr\big( \sigma^A \sigma_3 \big)\,,

\displaystyle d\vcentcolon=\Tr\big( \sigma^B \sigma_3 \big)\,,

\displaystyle x\vcentcolon=\Tr\big( \sigma^A + \sigma^B \big),

where
\sigma_{3}
denotes the third Pauli matrix. Then, both hermitian matrices
F
and
F^{\Phi}
take the following form

\displaystyle F=\frac{1}{2}\,\begin{pmatrix}F_{11}&0&0&0\\
0&F_{22}&F_{23}&0\\
0&F_{23}&F_{33}&0\\
0&0&0&F_{44}\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle F^{\Phi}=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}F^{\Phi}_{11}&0&0&0\\
0&F^{\Phi}_{22}&F^{\Phi}_{23}&0\\
0&F^{\Phi}_{23}&F^{\Phi}_{33}&0\\
0&0&0&F^{\Phi}_{44}\end{pmatrix},

(7.8)

with entries:

\displaystyle F_{11}

\displaystyle=-\left(c+d+x\right),

\displaystyle F_{22}

\displaystyle=1-c+d-x,

\displaystyle F_{23}=F_{32}=-1,

(7.9)

\displaystyle F_{33}

\displaystyle=1+c-d-x,

\displaystyle F_{44}

\displaystyle=c+d-x,

\displaystyle F_{11}^{\Phi}

\displaystyle=-\left(c+d\right)\cos(u-v)-x,

(7.10)

\displaystyle F_{22}^{\Phi}

\displaystyle=1-c\cos(u-v)+d\cos(u+v)-x,

\displaystyle F_{23}^{\Phi}=F_{32}^{\Phi}=-1,

\displaystyle F_{33}^{\Phi}

\displaystyle=1+c\cos(u+v)-d\cos(u-v)-x,

\displaystyle F_{44}^{\Phi}

\displaystyle=\left(c+d\right)\cos(u+v)-x.

The demand
F\geq 0
implies, in particular, the non-negativity of the diagonal elements of
F
, which yields the following constraints on the parameters,

\displaystyle x\leq 0,

\displaystyle x\leq c+d\leq-x,

\displaystyle x-1\leq c-d\leq 1-x.

(7.11)

The non-negativity of the central 2
\times
2 minor of
F
, which is the only non-trivial one, gives an additional constraint

\displaystyle(c-d)^{2}\leq x(x-2).

(7.12)

To simplify notation let us set

\displaystyle\alpha\vcentcolon=\cos(u-v)\quad\text{ and }\quad\beta\vcentcolon=\cos(u+v).

We start with showing that the diagonal elements of
F^{\Phi}
are non-negative. If
\alpha\in[0,1]
then (
7.11
) yields

F^{\Phi}_{11}=-\left(c+d\right)\alpha-x\geq x\big(\alpha-1\big)\geq 0,

while if
\alpha\in[-1,0]
then we obtain

F^{\Phi}_{11}\geq-x(\alpha+1)\geq 0.

Analogously, we deduce that
F^{\Phi}_{44}\geq 0
.

Let us now rewrite

\displaystyle F_{22}^{\Phi}

\displaystyle=1-x-\tfrac{1}{2}(c+d)(\alpha-\beta)+\tfrac{1}{2}(d-c)(\alpha+\beta)

and assume that
(\alpha-\beta)\geq 0
and
(\alpha+\beta)\geq 0
. Then, inequalities (
7.11
) yield,

\displaystyle F_{22}^{\Phi}

\displaystyle\geq 1-x+\tfrac{1}{2}x(\alpha-\beta)+\tfrac{1}{2}(x-1)(\alpha+\beta)=\big[1-\tfrac{1}{2}(\alpha+\beta)\big]+\tfrac{1}{2}x(\alpha-1)\geq 0,

because
\alpha\in[0,1]
and
(\alpha+\beta)\in[0,2]
. Analogously, one shows that
F_{22}^{\Phi}\geq 0
under three other possible assumptions about the signs of
\alpha-\beta
and
\alpha+\beta
. Along the same lines, one can prove that
F_{33}^{\Phi}\geq 0
.

Finally, let us consider the central 2
\times
2 minor of
F^{\Phi}
. With
\pm
denoting the sign of
\alpha+\beta
and using the previous estimates on
F_{22}^{\Phi}
and
F_{33}^{\Phi}
, along with constraint (
7.12
), we can write the determinant of this minor as

\displaystyle F_{22}^{\Phi}F_{33}^{\Phi}-1

\displaystyle=\Big[1-x-\tfrac{1}{2}(c+d)(\alpha-\beta)-\tfrac{1}{2}(c-d)(\alpha+\beta)\Big]\times

\displaystyle\qquad\times\Big[1-x-\tfrac{1}{2}(c+d)(\alpha-\beta)+\tfrac{1}{2}(c-d)(\alpha+\beta)\Big]-1

\displaystyle\geq\Big[1-x\pm\tfrac{1}{2}x(\alpha-\beta)-\tfrac{1}{2}(c-d)(\alpha+\beta)\Big]\times

\displaystyle\qquad\times\Big[1-x\pm\tfrac{1}{2}x(\alpha-\beta)+\tfrac{1}{2}(c-d)(\alpha+\beta)\Big]-1

\displaystyle=\Big[1-x\Big(1\mp\tfrac{1}{2}(\alpha-\beta)\Big)\Big]^{2}-\tfrac{1}{4}(c-d)^{2}(\alpha+\beta)^{2}-1

\displaystyle\geq-2x\Big(1\mp\tfrac{1}{2}(\alpha-\beta)\Big)+x^{2}\Big(1\mp\tfrac{1}{2}(\alpha-\beta)\Big)^{2}-\tfrac{1}{4}x(x-2)(\alpha+\beta)^{2}

\displaystyle=x^{2}(1\mp\alpha)(1\pm\beta)-x\Big[\tfrac{1}{2}(1\mp\alpha)^{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}(1\pm\beta)^{2}+1+\alpha\beta\Big]\geq 0.

The last inequality holds because
\alpha,\beta\in[-1,1]
, while
x\leq 0
from constraints (
7.11
).

Since the central minor of
F^{\Phi}
is the only non-trivial one we conclude that, indeed, if
F
is positive semidefinite, then
F^{\Phi}
is so, for any quantum channel determined by Kraus operators (
5.2
).

7.3
Conditions for positive semi-definiteness in Fano form

In this Appendix, we derive a useful form of the constraint for the coupling matrix between two qubits to be positive semidefinite, using its Fano form
Fano
. The obtained results are based on general formulas from
byrd
.

The density matrix representing any quantum state must be Hermitian, positive semidefinite and of trace
1
.
The first and the last property can be expressed easily using parametrisation via generators of the
su(n)
Lie algebras, i.e., Hermitian traceless matrices. This leads to the Fano representation of a bipartite quantum state
\rho^{AB}
, which for two qubits takes the following form:

\rho^{AB}=\frac{1}{4}\left(\mathbbm{1}_{4}+\sqrt{6}\vec{n}\cdot\vec{\lambda}\right)~,

where we temporarily adapt the normalization form
byrd
, and use the basis of Hermitian operators on
\mathbb{C}^{2\times 2}
defined by the tensor product of Pauli operators:

\displaystyle\lambda_{i},~i=1,2,3

\displaystyle\leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sigma_{j}\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{2},

\displaystyle\lambda_{i},~~i=4,5,6

\displaystyle\leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\mathbbm{1}_{2}\otimes\sigma_{j},

\displaystyle\lambda_{i},~~i=7,8,9

\displaystyle\leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sigma_{1}\otimes\sigma_{j},

\displaystyle\lambda_{i},~~i=10,11,12

\displaystyle\leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sigma_{2}\otimes\sigma_{j},

\displaystyle\lambda_{i},~~i=13,14,15

\displaystyle\leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sigma_{3}\otimes\sigma_{j},

with
j=1,2,3
and the vector
\vec{n}
has the form:

\vec{n}=(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3},b_{1},b_{2},b_{3},R_{11},R_{12},R_{13},R_{21},R_{22},R_{23},R_{31},R_{32},R_{33})~.

Here
\vec{a}
,
\vec{b}
are proportional to the Bloch vectors for consecutive subsystems and the matrix
R
is defined in Eq. (
6.2
).
The density matrix
\rho^{AB}
has non-negative eigenvalues if and only if all coefficients in its characteristic polynomial are non-negative
byrd
,

\det[\rho^{AB}-\lambda\mathbb{I}]=\lambda^{4}-S_{1}\lambda^{3}+S_{2}\lambda^{2}-S_{3}\lambda+S_{4}=0~.

In
byrd
it was shown tha these coefficients can be written as:

\displaystyle S_{1}=1~,

(7.13)

\displaystyle S_{2}=\frac{3}{8}(1-\vec{n}\cdot\vec{n})~,

\displaystyle S_{3}=\frac{1}{16}\left(1-3\vec{n}\cdot\vec{n}+2\vec{n}\cdot(\vec{n}\star\vec{n})\right)~,

\displaystyle S_{4}=\frac{1}{64}\left(1-6\vec{n}\cdot\vec{n}+8\vec{n}\cdot(\vec{n}\star\vec{n})+9(\vec{n}\cdot\vec{n})^{2}-12(\vec{n}\star\vec{n})\cdot(\vec{n}\star\vec{n})\right)~,

where
\star
denotes the product defined
byrd
via symmetric structure constants
d_{ijk}
of the Lie algebra
su(4)
,

(\vec{a}\star\vec{b})_{k}=\frac{\sqrt{6}}{2}d_{ijk}a_{i}b_{j}~.

The expression
\vec{n}\cdot(\vec{n}\star\vec{n})
has a compact form

\vec{n}\cdot(\vec{n}\star\vec{n})=3\sqrt{3}\left(\vec{a}^{T}R\vec{b}-\text{det}(R)\right)~,

while the expression
(\vec{n}\star\vec{n})\cdot(\vec{n}\star\vec{n})
can be simplified to

(\vec{n}\star\vec{n})\cdot(\vec{n}\star\vec{n})=3\bigg[\vec{b}^{T}R^{T}R\vec{b}+\vec{a}^{T}RR^{T}\vec{a}+\\
+(\text{det}R)^{2}\sum_{i,j}\big((R^{-1})_{ij}\big)^{2}-2(\text{det}R)~\vec{b}^{T}R^{-1}\vec{a}+\vec{a}^{2}\vec{b}^{2}\bigg]~,

under the assumption that the matrix
R
is invertible.
Hence the coefficients
S_{1},S_{2},S_{3},S_{4}
entering (
7.13
) can be rewritten as:

\displaystyle S_{1}=1~,

(7.14)

\displaystyle S_{2}=\frac{3}{8}\bigg(1-\sum_{i,j}(R_{ij})^{2}-\vec{a}^{2}-\vec{b}^{2}\bigg)~,

\displaystyle S_{3}=\frac{1}{16}\left[1-3\bigg(\sum_{i,j}(R_{ij})^{2}+\vec{a}^{2}+\vec{b}^{2}\bigg)+6\sqrt{3}\bigg(\vec{a}^{T}R\vec{b}-(\text{det}R)\bigg)\right]~,

\displaystyle S_{4}=\frac{1}{64}\Bigg[1-6\bigg(\sum_{i,j}(R_{ij})^{2}+\vec{a}^{2}+\vec{b}^{2}\bigg)+24\sqrt{3}\bigg(\vec{a}^{T}R\vec{b}-(\text{det}R)\bigg)+

\displaystyle+9\bigg(\sum_{i,j}(R_{ij})^{2}+\vec{a}^{2}+\vec{b}^{2}\bigg)^{2}-36\bigg(\vec{b}^{T}R^{T}R\vec{b}+\vec{a}^{T}RR^{T}\vec{a}+

\displaystyle+(\text{det}R)^{2}\sum_{i,j}\big((R^{-1})_{ij}\big)^{2}-2(\text{det}R)~\vec{b}^{T}R^{-1}\vec{a}+\vec{a}^{2}\vec{b}^{2}\bigg)\Bigg]~.

The necessary and sufficient conditions for
\rho^{AB}
to be a density matrix come down to

0\leq S_{l},\quad\text{ for }\quad l=1,2,3,4.

We end this section by discussing the special case of
\rho^{A}=\rho^{B}
, i.e.
\vec{a}=\vec{b}=(0,0,a_{3})
, and a diagonal correlation matrix
R=\diag(R_{11},R_{22},R_{33})
. Adopting the normalization from (
6.2
), the expression form (
7.14
) for
S_{3}
and
S_{4}
simplifies to the product of two planes and a hyperbola:

\displaystyle S_{3}=(R_{33}-1)\left(2a_{3}^{2}-R_{33}+R_{-}^{2}-1\right)-(R_{33}+1)R_{+}^{2}\geq 0~,

(7.15)

\displaystyle S_{4}=(1-R_{33}+\sqrt{2}R_{+})(1-R_{33}-\sqrt{2}R_{+})\left(4a_{3}^{2}+2R_{-}^{2}-(1+R_{33})^{2}\right)\geq 0~,

where
R_{\pm}=(R_{11}\pm R_{22})/\sqrt{2}
.

7.4
Solutions of dual quantum transport problem for isospectral qubit states

In this Appendix we present the construction of a one parametric family of solutions to dual quantum optimal transport problem for isospectral qubit states. Afterwards, we use those solutions to show that the solution of the original transport problem (
6.10
) is unique.

For isospectral states
\rho^{A}
,
\rho^{B}
the optimal coupling
\underline{\rho}^{AB}
, given by (
6.10
), has only one nonzero eigenvalue, which demonstrates that Theorem
6.2
cannot hold and we can expect multiple solutions both to the original and the dual problem.

The construction of the solutions to the dual problem for the isospectral states is based on the fact that any optimal coupling
\underline{\rho}^{AB}
has disjoint support with any optimal
\underline{F}
, as follows from Eq. (
6.5
).
Hence, by the eigendecomposition of
\underline{\rho}^{AB}
we know that the optimal
\underline{F}
must have the form

\underline{F}=\sum_{i,j}\vec{v_{i}}\vec{v_{j}}^{\dagger}s_{i,j}~,

(7.16)

where
\vec{v_{i}}
are eigenvectors to zero eigenvalues of
\underline{\rho}^{AB}
and
s_{ij}=s_{ji}^{*}
are some a priori unknown coefficients.
On the other hand, the general form of
F
is given by

F=\left(\frac{1}{4}-x_{0}\right)\mathbbm{1}_{4}-\sum_{i=1}^{3}c_{i}\sigma_{i}\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{3}d_{i}\mathbbm{1}_{2}\otimes\sigma_{i}-\frac{1}{4}\sum_{i=1}^{3}\sigma_{i}\otimes\sigma_{i}~,

(7.17)

where
c_{i}=\frac{1}{2}\Tr[\sigma^{A}\sigma_{i}]
,
d_{i}=\frac{1}{2}\Tr[\sigma^{B}\sigma_{i}]
,
x_{0}=\Tr[\sigma^{A}]+\Tr[\sigma^{B}]
, and
\sigma_{i}
are Pauli matrices.

Now, we have to compare (
7.16
) with (
7.17
) to eliminate as many coefficients as possible and then check when the resulting matrix
\underline{F}
is positive semidefinite. Elementwise comparison gives the following form of optimal couplings
\underline{\sigma}^{A}
,
\underline{\sigma}^{B}

\displaystyle(\underline{\sigma}^{A})_{11}={\frac{a_{3}\left(2w+2a_{3}-1\right)-2w\left(4a_{3}d_{3}+1-w\right)\sec\phi-2(a_{3}-1)\left(w-1\right)\cos\phi}{8a_{3}w}}~,

\displaystyle(\underline{\sigma}^{A})_{12}=\frac{\sqrt{1-a_{3}}\left(4a_{3}d_{3}+1-w\right)\tan\phi}{4a_{3}\sqrt{1+a_{3}}}+

\displaystyle-\frac{(\cos\phi+1)\tan(\phi/2)\left(4wa_{3}d_{3}-(a_{3}+1)\left(w-1\right)\cos\phi+w-w^{2}\right)}{4a_{3}(1+a_{3})\cos\phi}~,

\displaystyle(\underline{\sigma}^{A})_{21}=(\underline{\sigma}^{A})_{12}~,

\displaystyle(\underline{\sigma}^{A})_{22}=-(\underline{\sigma}^{A})_{11}+\frac{(w-1)(1-\cos\phi)}{2w}~,

\displaystyle\underline{\sigma}^{B}=\frac{1}{4a_{3}}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}4a_{3}d_{3}&(4a_{3}d_{3}-w+1)\tan\phi\\
(4a_{3}d_{3}-w+1)\tan\phi&-4a_{3}d_{3}\\
\end{array}\right)~,

(7.18)

where
w=\sqrt{1-a_{3}^{2}}
and
d_{3}
is the only parameter that has not been eliminated.
For generic values of
d_{3}
parameter, those solutions correspond to optimal
\underline{F}
which has a rank equal to three.

To ensure the positivity of
\underline{F}
, we check the values of coefficients
S_{1},S_{2},S_{3},S_{4}
of the characteristic polynomial of
\underline{F}
, similarly as in Appendix
7.3
,

\det[\underline{F}-\lambda\mathbb{I}]=\lambda^{4}-S_{1}\lambda^{3}+S_{2}\lambda^{2}-S_{3}\lambda+S_{4}~.

Firstly, let us note that
S_{1}=\Tr[F]=(1-4x_{0})\geq 0
if and only if
(1-w)\cos(\phi)\leq 1
, which is true for any values of
w=\sqrt{1-a_{3}^{2}}
and
\phi
. Since
\underline{F}
has at most
3
nonzero eigenvalues, the coefficient
S_{4}=\det\underline{F}
must be equal to 0. The
S_{2}
coefficient imposes a quadratic condition in
d_{3}
:

-32d_{3}^{2}a_{3}^{2}w^{3}\sec^{2}(\phi)-8d_{3}a_{3}w^{2}(\sec\phi-1)\left(2w(1-w)\sec\phi-(1-w)^{2}\right)+\\
+\sin^{2}\left(\phi/2\right)\sec^{2}(\phi)w(1-w)^{2}\Big(2\left(1+(2-w)w\right)-a_{3}^{2}\cos 3\phi+\\
+2(1+w)^{2}\cos 2\phi+(w(4+3w)-3)\cos\phi\Big)\geq 0~,

(7.19)

and
S_{3}
imposes a quadratic condition in
d_{3}
as well

8d_{3}^{2}a_{3}^{2}w^{3}\sin^{2}\left(\phi/2\right)\sec^{2}(\phi)+\sin^{4}(\phi/2)\sec^{2}(\phi)w(1-w)^{2}(w-\cos(\phi))+\\
+4d_{3}a_{3}\sin^{4}(\phi/2)\sec(\phi)w(1-w)\big(w\left(\sec(\phi)+1\right)-1\big)\leq 0~.

(7.20)

For both these quadratic inequalities, the discriminants are greater than
0
. Therefore, there exist intervals of
d_{3}
satisfying each of them separately. Moreover, the roots of (
7.19
) lie between the roots of (
7.20
). Hence, introducing the auxiliary variables

\displaystyle d_{3,1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{16a_{3}w}\Bigg[(w-1)(1+3w)-(1-w)^{2}\cos 2\phi+

\displaystyle\quad+2a_{3}\cos\phi\bigg(a_{3}-\frac{\sqrt{2}}{|a_{3}|}\left|\sin\left(\phi/2\right)\right|\sqrt{(1-w)^{3}(1+3w)-(1-w)^{4}\cos\phi}\bigg)\Bigg]

\displaystyle d_{3,2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{16a_{3}w}\Bigg[(w-1)(1+3w)-(1-w)^{2}\cos 2\phi+

\displaystyle\quad+2a_{3}\cos\phi\bigg(a_{3}+\frac{\sqrt{2}}{|a_{3}|}\left|\sin\left(\phi/2\right)\right|\sqrt{(1-w)^{3}(1+3w)-(1-w)^{4}\cos\phi}\bigg)\Bigg]

we obtain the following bounds for the value of
d_{3}
,

\displaystyle\text{if }\phi\leq\pi/2

\displaystyle\text{ then}\quad d_{3,1}\leq d_{3}\leq d_{3,2}~,

(7.21)

\displaystyle\text{if }\phi\geq\pi/2

\displaystyle\text{ then}\quad d_{3,1}\geq d_{3}\geq d_{3,2}~.

In the singular case,
\phi=\pi/2
, the entire interval of allowed values of
d_{3}
shrinks to a single point:

d_{3}=d_{3,1}=d_{3,2}=\frac{w-1}{4a_{3}}

resulting in only a single solution of the dual problem, with rank
(\underline{F})=3
for any value of
a_{3}\in(0,1)
.

We constructed a family of the dual problem solutions in which for any values of
\phi
and
a_{3}
there exists an optimal
\underline{F}
with rank
3
. Therefore, the corresponding optimal coupling
\underline{\rho}^{AB}
is uniquely defined by the eigenvector to zero eigenvalue of the abovementioned
\underline{F}
, hence the original problem has only one solution (
6.10
).

8
Monotonicity: numerical results

In this Appendix the results of numerical calculations are presented. We performed Monte Carlo simulations in which we drew pairs of quantum states and a quantum channel and checked how the optimal quantum transport cost between the selected states changes after the application the channel.

We examined the monotonicity of the optimal quantum transport for qutrits (
N=3
) and ququarts (
N=4
) using two different types of tests. In the first one we picked random quantum channels corresponding to dynamical Choi matrices of a fixed rank. In the second one we employed random extremal quantum channels, defined up to unitary pre- and post-processing of the quantum states. Because of the unitary invariance of
T^{Q}
and its convexity (recall Prop.
4.2
), the monotonicity of
T^{Q}
under such channels implies the monotonicity with respect to all CPTP maps.

To generate a random quantum state of order
N
we first drew a rectangular complex random matrix
X
of size
N\times k
, with
k\leq N
.
Setting
\rho=XX^{\dagger}/\Tr[XX^{\dagger}]
we ensure that a random matrix
\rho
of size
N
and rank
k
is non-negative and
\Tr\rho=1
. Our calculations show that the rank of states does not affect significantly the results. Therefore we focus on full rank states to maximize the explored space.

Random quantum channels
\Phi:\Omega_{N}\to\Omega_{N}
of a given rank
k
were generate via the Choi–Jamiołkowski isomorphism. We generated a random
N^{2}\times k
complex matrix
X
and created an auxiliary
N^{2}\times N^{2}
positive matrix
\widetilde{Y}=XX^{\dagger}\geq 0
. By the Choi–Jamiołkowski isomorphism a quantum state corresponds to a quantum channel if the second of its partial traces is a totally mixed state. To assure this property we defined
Y=\Tr_{1}\widetilde{Y}
, where
\Tr_{1}
is the partial trace on the first subsystem. Then, we set
\rho_{\Phi}\propto\left(\mathbbm{1}_{N}\otimes Y^{-1/2}\right)\widetilde{Y}\left(\mathbbm{1}_{N}\otimes Y^{-1/2}\right)
with the proportionality coefficient adjusted by the condition
\Tr[\rho_{\Phi}]=1
. For further details on random quantum channel generation we encourage the Reader to consult
rand_quant
;
ZPNC11
.

Having a normalized density matrix
\rho_{\Phi}
, to calculate the action of channel
\Phi
on any state
\rho
we used the definition of Choi–Jamiołkowski isomorphism:

\displaystyle N\Tr_{2}[\rho_{\Phi}~(\mathbbm{1}_{N}\otimes\rho^{T})]=N\Tr_{2}\left[(\Phi\otimes\mathbbm{1})(|\psi^{+}\rangle\langle\psi^{+}|)~(\mathbbm{1}_{N}\otimes\rho^{T})\right]=

(8.1)

\displaystyle=\Tr_{2}\left[(\Phi\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{N\times N})\left(\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}|ii\rangle\right)\left(\sum_{j=1}^{N}\langle jj|\right)\right)~(\mathbbm{1}_{N}\otimes\rho^{T})\right]=

\displaystyle=\sum_{ij}\Phi(|i\rangle\langle j|)\Tr[|i\rangle\langle j|\rho^{T}]=\Phi(\rho),

where
|\psi^{+}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}|ii\rangle
is a maximally entangled state.

In the test of the second type we focused on extremal quantum channels. Using the methods presented in
rand_extr_chann
we described all such channels by their Kraus operators,
K_{i}=U_{i}D_{i}
with
i=1,\ldots,N
, where
U_{i}
and
D_{i}
denote, respectively, the unitary matrices and diagonal matrices of order
N
. An explicit form of
U_{i}
and
D_{i}

for a qutrit reads,

\displaystyle D_{1}=\diag(a,b,c)~,~D_{2}=\diag(d,e,f)~,~D_{3}=\sqrt{1-D_{1}^{2}-D_{2}^{2}}~,

\displaystyle U_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&0&1\\
0&1&0\end{pmatrix},~~U_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&1\\
0&1&0\\
1&0&0\end{pmatrix},~~U_{3}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\\
1&0&0\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix}.

An analogous parametrisation works for
N=4
(see
rand_extr_chann
).
All of the parameters in the above formulae are non-negative and chosen so that the matrices
D_{3}
(for the qutrit case) and
D_{4}
(for the ququart case) are positive semi-definite.

In each Monte Carlo simulation we generated a random pair of initial states
\rho^{A},\rho^{B}
of order
N
and a quantum channel
\Phi
using the method presented above. Then, we calculated the quantum transport cost before and after the application of the channel,
T^{Q}(\rho^{A},\rho^{B})
and
T^{Q}\big(\Phi(\rho^{A}),\Phi(\rho^{B})\big)
, respectively and compared the results.

The simulations were programmed in the Python language, using Numpy library for algebraic calculations, Cvxpy library for the transport costs calculations and a solver supplied by the MOSEK Optimizer for solving semidefinite programming problems. The plots were generated with help of the Matplotlib library. The numerical precision of calculations was set to
10^{-12}
. We checked the accuracy of the optimisation algorithm by generating random unitary channels, which correspond to
k=1
in the procedure described above. The deviation between the obtained values of
T^{Q}
before and after the application of a unitary channel was not greater than
10^{-9}
, hence we can take this value as the numerical accuracy of the algorithm.

The results of the simulations are presented in Table
1
and Figure
2
. In all of the considered cases the difference
T^{Q}(\rho^{A},\rho^{B})-T^{Q}(\Phi(\rho^{A}),\Phi(\rho^{B}))
was positive and a few orders of magnitude larger than the numerical accuracy.
These results allow us to conjecture that optimal quantum transport cost
T^{Q}
for dimensions
3
and
4
is monotonous under all CPTP maps.
