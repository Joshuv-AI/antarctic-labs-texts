Abstract
Conditional graph entropy is known to be the minimal rate for a natural functional compression problem with side information at the receiver. In this paper we show that it can be formulated as an alternating minimization problem, which gives rise to a simple iterative algorithm for numerically computing (conditional) graph entropy. This also leads to a new formula which shows that conditional graph entropy is part of a more general framework: the solution of an optimization problem over a convex corner. In the special case of graph entropy (i.e., unconditioned version) this was known due to Csiszár, Körner, Lovász, Marton, and Simonyi. In that case the role of the convex corner was played by the so-called vertex packing polytope. In the conditional version it is a more intricate convex body but the function to minimize is the same. Furthermore, we describe a dual problem that leads to an optimality check and an error bound for the iterative algorithm.

Background and related work

Conditional graph entropy

Suppose that the random variable
X
takes values in a finite alphabet
\mathcal{X}
, where not every pair of letters can be distinguished. Let
G
be a graph with vertex set
V(G)=\mathcal{X}
describing which pairs are distinguishable:
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
can be distinguished if and only if
xx^{\prime}
is an edge of
G
. Furthermore, we say that the sequences
x_{1},\ldots,x_{\ell}
and
x^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,x^{\prime}_{\ell}
are distinguishable if
x_{i}
and
x^{\prime}_{i}
are distinguishable for at least one index
i
. We wish to encode an i.i.d. sequence
X_{1},\ldots,X_{\ell}
with high probability in a way that distinguishable sequences are mapped to different codewords. An
independent set
of
G
contains no edges, and hence any two letters in the set are indistinguishable. Therefore one possible strategy is to replace each
X_{i}
with an independent set
J_{i}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
containing
X_{i}
, and encode the sequence
J_{1},\ldots,J_{\ell}
instead. If we do this randomly in a way that
(X_{i},J_{i})
are i.i.d. samples of some
(X,J)
where
J
is a random independent set containing
X
, then we can encode the
J_{i}
sequence with rate
H(J)
(asymptotically as
\ell\to\infty
). Note that the number of times any given typical
X_{i}
sequence is covered has exponential rate
H(J|X)
. Based on this, one can design an encoding with rate
H(J)-H(J|X)=I(X;J)
. Then, for a given
X
, one needs to choose
(X,J)
in a way that the mutual information
I(X;J)
is as small as possible. Körner showed that this is the best achievable code rate and introduced the corresponding notion of
graph entropy

[
12
]
:

H_{G}(X)=\min_{J}I(X;J),\quad\mbox{where $J$ is a random independent set of $G$ such that $X\in J$.}

(1)

The analogous problem with side information
Y_{i}
at the receiver leads to the notion of
conditional graph entropy

H_{G}(X|Y)
. Let
(X,Y)
be discrete random variables of some given joint distribution and let
(X_{i},Y_{i})
be i.i.d. samples. We assume that the decoder knows the sequence
Y_{1},Y_{2},\ldots
. If we want to use the same approach (i.e., choosing a random
J
), then
J
and
Y
should be independent conditioned on
X
(because the sender does not know
Y_{i}
when choosing
J_{i}
). This can be made rigorous, leading to the following formula:

H_{G}(X|Y)=\min_{J}I(X;J\,|\,Y)=\min_{J}\bigg(H(J|Y)-\underbrace{H(J|X,Y)}_{H(J|X)}\bigg),

(2)

where
J
is a random independent set of
G
such that
X\in J
, and
J
and
Y
are conditionally independent conditioned on
X
(which is equivalent to saying that
Y-X-J
is a Markov chain).

Much less is known about conditional graph entropy, however. Let us first describe its connection to functional compression.

Compression with side information

Suppose now that the receiver wishes to recover the values
f(X_{i},Y_{i})
of some given function
f\colon\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathcal{Z}
(with high probability, over long blocks) as depicted in Figure

[
14

showed that the minimal rate of information that needs to be transmitted is precisely the conditional graph entropy of the so-called

as follows: vertices

are connected with an edge if and only if

\exists y\in\mathcal{Y}\mbox{ s.t.\ }\big(f(x_{1},y)\neq f(x_{2},y)\,\,\&\,\,\mathbb{P}(X=x_{1},Y=y)>0\,\,\&\,\,\mathbb{P}(X=x_{2},Y=y)>0\big).

(This definition goes back to Witsenhausen
[
20
]
.) We mention that in the special case
f(x,y)=x
, which was already studied in Shannon’s classical work
[
15
]
, the optimal rate is given by the conditional entropy
H(X|Y)=H(X,Y)-H(Y)

Also note that these problems are naturally connected to graph coloring. Doshi et al.
[
7
]
extended the notion of

[
1
]
and defined

Alternating optimization

As we have mentioned, it turns out that conditional graph entropy can be obtained by alternating optimization. This family includes a large number of problems from a variety of fields. A usual feature is that although the optimum has no closed-form expression, there is an efficient way to optimize in each variable. Thus, optimizing in the different variables in turns can lead to a good numerical approximation of the optimum. A well-known example is the expectation–-maximization (EM) algorithm. Another prominent example is the Blahut–Arimoto (BA) algorithm
[
2
,
3
]
, which deals with the capacity of discrete memoryless channels. To find the capacity achieving input, the algorithm turns the objective into a double supremum and alternately optimizes over the distribution parameters from random initialization. Also,
[
8
]
provides a numerical method for the Gel’fand-Pinsker problem
[
9
]
, where noncausal state information is known at the encoder. Generalization of the BA algorithm for finite-state channels, proposed in
[
18
]
, also takes advantage of the iterative nature of alternating optimization. To the best of our knowledge, no similar procedure was proposed for the Orlitsky–Roche problem
[
14
]
beforehand.

It is important to point out that Csiszár and Tusnády
[
6
]
initiated the systematic study of such problems in the 1980s already. The cornerstone of their theory is a collection of inequalities called 3-point, 4-point, and 5-point properties. They will play a key role in our problem as well.

Contributions

Alternating minimization

Let us consider the following optimization problem.

Problem
.

Suppose that we have two finite families of probability measures on a given finite set
\mathcal{J}
:
\mu_{x}
,
x\in\mathcal{X}
and
\nu_{y}
,
y\in\mathcal{Y}
. In the first family for each
x\in\mathcal{X}
we have a constraint: the support
\supp\mu_{x}
must be contained in a given subset
\mathcal{J}_{x}
of
\mathcal{J}
. Find the measures
\mu_{x}
,
\nu_{y}
that minimize the weighted sum of the Kullback–Leibler divergences:

\sum_{x,y}p_{x,y}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mu_{x}\;\|\;\nu_{y})\mbox{ for some given weights }p_{x,y}\geq 0.

That is, given
\mathcal{J}_{x}\subseteq\mathcal{J}
,
x\in\mathcal{X}
and
p_{x,y}\geq 0
,
x\in\mathcal{X},y\in\mathcal{Y}
, find the minimum of the above sum under the constraint
\supp\mu_{x}\subseteq\mathcal{J}_{x}
.

In our setting we have random variables
X
and
Y
taking values in the finite sets
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
, respectively, and
G
is a graph on the vertex set
\mathcal{X}
. Then by
j
we denote an independent set of
G
, hence each
j
is a subset of
\mathcal{X}
. We choose
\mathcal{J}
to be the set of all
j
, while

\mathcal{J}_{x}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\{j\,:\,x\in j\}

consists of the independent sets containing a fixed
x
. With this setup and with
p_{x,y}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\mathbb{P}(X=x,Y=y)
, the minimum of the problem above turns out to be precisely

.

To get concrete formulas, let us represent the distributions
\mu_{x}
and
\nu_{y}
by the following vectors:

\displaystyle\bm{q}

\displaystyle=\big(q_{j|x}\big)_{(j,x)\in\mathcal{J}\times\mathcal{X}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{J}\times\mathcal{X}};

\displaystyle\bm{r}

\displaystyle=\big(r_{j|y}\big)_{(j,y)\in\mathcal{J}\times\mathcal{Y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{J}\times\mathcal{Y}},

where
q_{j|x}
and
r_{j|y}
stand for
\mu_{x}(\{j\})
and
\nu_{y}(\{j\})
, respectively.

1

We index the coordinates/variables by

and

and

The constraints for

Definition 1
.

We define the convex polytopes
K_{\textrm{q}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{J}\times\mathcal{X}}
and
K_{\textrm{r}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{J}\times\mathcal{Y}}
as

K_{\textrm{q}}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\bigg\{\bm{q}=\big(q_{j|x}\big)\,:\,q_{j|x}\geq 0;\,\sum_{j\ni x}q_{j|x}=1\,(\forall x\in\mathcal{X});\,q_{j|x}=0\mbox{ if }x\notin j\bigg\}

K_{\textrm{r}}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\bigg\{\bm{r}=\big(r_{j|y}\big)\,:\,r_{j|y}\geq 0;\,\sum_{j}r_{j|y}=1\,(\forall y\in\mathcal{Y})\bigg\}.

In the sequel we will always assume that

\bm{r}\in K_{\textrm{r}}

D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mu_{x}\;\|\;\nu_{y})=\sum_{j}q_{j|x}\log\frac{q_{j|x}}{r_{j|y}}.

Therefore we need to minimize the function

\varphi(\bm{q},\bm{r})\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\sum_{x,y,j}p_{x,y}\,q_{j|x}\log\frac{q_{j|x}}{r_{j|y}}

(3)

\bm{q}\in K_{\textrm{q}}

As we will see, this is an alternating minimization problem. The point is that if we fix one of the two variables

\displaystyle\varphi_{\textrm{q}}(\bm{q})

\displaystyle\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\varphi\big(\bm{q},R(\bm{q})\big)=\min_{\bm{r}}\varphi(\bm{q},\bm{r})\mbox{ and}

\displaystyle\varphi_{\textrm{r}}(\bm{r})

\displaystyle\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\varphi\big(Q(\bm{r}),\bm{r}\big)=\min_{\bm{q}}\varphi(\bm{q},\bm{r}),

Convex corners

As we have mentioned, the q-problem
\min\varphi_{\textrm{q}}
gives back the original formula (
2
). On the other hand, the r-problem
\min\varphi_{\textrm{r}}
gives us a new formula. We make this new formula explicit in the next theorem because it shows how conditional graph entropy is related to convex corners (a known phenomenon in the unconditioned case).

Theorem 4
.

For any (maximal) independent set
j
of
G
and any possible value
y
of
Y
we have a variable
r_{j|y}
. Then conditional graph entropy can be expressed as the solution of the following optimization problem:

H_{G}(X|Y)=\min-\sum_{x}p_{x}\log\bigg(\sum_{j\,:\,x\in j}\,\prod_{y}\big(r_{j|y}\big)^{p^{y|x}}\bigg),

(5)

where the minimum is taken over all choices of
r_{j|y}\geq 0
satisfying
\sum_{j}r_{j|y}=1
for each fixed
y
.

We can easily turn this new r-problem into another one (that we will call the a-problem) which attests that conditional graph entropy is a special case of a more general entropy notion defined for convex corners.
2
2

2

A convex corner of
\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{X}}
is a convex compact set in the positive orthant
[0,\infty)^{\mathcal{X}}
that is
downward closed
, i.e., if we take any point in the set and decrease some of its coordinates, then the new point still lies in the set (see Definition
15
).

To see this connection, note that (
5
) is in the form

H_{G}(X|Y)=\min_{K_{\textrm{r}}}-\sum_{x}p_{x}\log A_{x},

where
A_{x}
is a function of the variables
r_{j|y}
. The key property is that
A_{x}\colon K_{\textrm{r}}\to[0,1]
is a concave function for each
x
. It means that the set of image points
\bm{a}=(a_{x})_{x\in\mathcal{X}}
with
a_{x}=A_{x}(\bm{r})
, as
\bm{r}
ranges over
K_{\textrm{r}}
, (essentially) defines a convex corner
K_{\textrm{a}}
in
\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{X}}
. Then we have

H_{G}(X|Y)=\min_{K_{\textrm{a}}}\varphi_{\textrm{a}}\mbox{, where }\varphi_{\textrm{a}}(\bm{a})=-\sum_{x}p_{x}\log a_{x}.

A nice feature of this a-problem is that the minimum is attained at a single point
\bm{a}\in K_{\textrm{a}}
because
\varphi_{\textrm{a}}
is strictly convex (provided that
p_{x}=\mathbb{P}(X=x)>0
for each
x
). Also note that
\varphi_{\textrm{a}}
depends only on the distribution of
X
, while the convex corner
K_{\textrm{a}}
depends only on the graph
G
and the conditional distributions
Y\,|\,X=x
for any given
x
. Thus, the parameters of the problem are, so to say, split between
\varphi_{\textrm{a}}
and
K_{\textrm{a}}
.

Moreover, we will define another convex corner, denoted by
L
, that can be regarded as the
dual problem
. To keep the introduction concise, we will postpone the actual definition of
L
and the precise statements until Section
IV-C
. In short, we will show that

H_{G}(X|Y)=\min_{K_{\textrm{a}}}\varphi_{\textrm{a}}=-\min_{L}\varphi_{\textrm{a}},

and a vector
\bm{a}=\big(a_{x}\big)_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\in K_{\textrm{a}}
is optimal (i.e., the minimum point of
\varphi_{\textrm{a}}
) if and only if
\bm{a}^{-1}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\big(a_{x}^{-1}\big)_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\in L
. This provides a fairly simple way to check optimality, and even leading to an error bound for our iterative algorithm as we will explain in Section
V
.

In the special case when
Y
is trivial, i.e.,
\mathcal{Y}
is a one-element set, we get back Körner’s original setting of graph entropy, and things simplify considerably. For example,
K_{\textrm{a}}
is simply a polytope: the aforementioned
vertex packing polytope
(or
independent/stable set polytope
)
\VP(G)
.
We did not find any mention in the literature of the fact that graph entropy can be considered as an alternating minimization problem. In particular, to the best of our knowledge, the corresponding iterative algorithm has not been used or proposed before even in this unconditioned setting.

A\colon K_{\textrm{r}}\to\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{X}}

Q\colon K_{\textrm{r}}\to K_{\textrm{q}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{J}\times\mathcal{X}}
;

R\colon K_{\textrm{q}}\to K_{\textrm{r}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{J}\times\mathcal{Y}}

by the following coordinate-wise functions
Q_{j|x}
,
R_{j|y}
,
A_{x}
:

\displaystyle R_{j|y}(\bm{q})

\displaystyle\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\sum_{x\in j}p_{x|y}\,q_{j|x};

\displaystyle A_{x}(\bm{r})

\displaystyle\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\sum_{j\ni x}\,\prod_{y}\big(r_{j|y}\big)^{p^{y|x}};

\displaystyle Q_{j|x}(\bm{r})

\displaystyle\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\begin{cases}0&\mbox{ if }x\notin j;\\
\prod_{y}\big(r_{j|y}\big)^{p^{y|x}}\bigg/A_{x}(\bm{r})&\mbox{ if }x\in j.\end{cases}

Since the formula for
Q_{j|x}
involves a division by
A_{x}
, it only defines
Q
over the subset

K^{\ast}_{\textrm{r}}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=K_{\textrm{r}}\setminus\bigcup_{x}A^{-1}_{x}(0).

(6)

For
\bm{r}\in K_{\textrm{r}}\setminus K^{\ast}_{\textrm{r}}
let
Q(\bm{r})
be an arbitrary point in
\ints(K_{\textrm{q}})
.

In the formulas above we define
t^{0}=1
even for
t=0
. This ensures that
A
is continuous over the entire
K_{\textrm{r}}
even when
p^{y|x}=0
for some pairs
x,y
. It is also consistent with the convention
0\cdot\log 0=0
which is implicit in the definition of Shannon entropy.

It is straightforward to check that
Q(\bm{r})\in K_{\textrm{q}}
and
R(\bm{q})\in K_{\textrm{r}}
always hold. For example, in the definition of
Q_{j|x}(\bm{r})
, dividing by
A_{x}(\bm{r})
ensures that their sum is
1
for any fixed
x
.

Remark 6
.

Note that
R
is a linear map and it actually describes how the conditional distributions
J\,|\,Y=y
can be expressed in terms of
J\,|\,X=x
in a Markov chain
Y-X-J
; see the proof of Proposition

for details.

II-C

The functions

Now we can turn our attention to the functions to be minimized. We already gave an explicit formula (
3
) for
\varphi(\bm{q},\bm{r})
in the introduction. However, we did not mention a few subtleties there. In particular, we need to specify the function values when some of the variables
q_{j|x}
or
r_{j|y}
are
0
.

Definition 7
.

For
u,v\in[0,1]
let

f(u,v)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=u\log u-u\log v

with the usual conventions
\log 0=-\infty
and
0\cdot\infty=0
so that

f(0,v)=0\mbox{ if }v\in[0,1]\quad\mbox{ and }\quad f(u,0)=\infty\mbox{ if }u\in(0,1].

Then

\varphi(\bm{q},\bm{r})\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\sum_{x,y,j}p_{x,y}\,f\big(q_{j|x},r_{j|y}\big)

(7)

is well-defined for any
\bm{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{J}\times\mathcal{X}}
and
\bm{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{J}\times\mathcal{Y}}
. Note that we may restrict the sum for
x\in j
because otherwise
q_{j|x}=0
, and hence the summand is
0
anyway.

Let us also define the following auxiliary function that we will need for establishing the so-called 3-point and 4-point properties.

\delta(\bm{q},\bm{q^{\prime}})\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\sum_{x}p_{x}\,\sum_{j\ni x}f\big(q_{j|x},q^{\prime}_{j|x}\big)=\sum_{x}p_{x}\,\sum_{j\ni x}q_{j|x}\log q_{j|x}-q_{j|x}\log q^{\prime}_{j|x}.

(8)

One may think of
\varphi(\bm{q},\bm{r})
and
\delta(\bm{q},\bm{q^{\prime}})
as (non-symmetric) squared distances between these points. We mention that both functions are convex combinations of certain Kullback–Leibler divergences.
In particular, they are nonnegative and they may be
\infty
. For example,
\varphi(\bm{q},\bm{r})=\infty
if and only if there exist
x,y,j
such that
r_{j|y}=0
while
p_{x,y}>0
and
q_{j|x}>0
. It is easy to see that if
\bm{r}\in K_{\textrm{r}}\setminus K^{\ast}_{\textrm{r}}
, then
\varphi(\bm{q},\bm{r})=\infty
for any choice of
\bm{q}\in K_{\textrm{q}}
. (The two-line proof of this fact is included in the proof of Proposition
13
.)

We include here two useful equivalent formulas for
\varphi
. On the one hand, summing
q_{j|x}\log q_{j|x}
and
q_{j|x}\log r_{j|y}
separately gives

\varphi(\bm{q},\bm{r})=\sum_{x}p_{x}\sum_{j\ni x}q_{j|x}\log q_{j|x}-\sum_{y}p^{y}\sum_{j}R_{j|y}(\bm{q})\log r_{j|y}.

(9)

On the other hand, for
\bm{r}\in K^{\ast}_{\textrm{r}}
we can write

\varphi(\bm{q},\bm{r})=\sum_{x}p_{x}\sum_{j\ni x}q_{j|x}\bigg(\log q_{j|x}-\sum_{y}p^{y|x}\log r_{j|y}\bigg)\\
=\sum_{x}p_{x}\sum_{j\ni x}q_{j|x}\log\frac{q_{j|x}}{\prod_{y}\big(r_{j|y}\big)^{p^{y|x}}}=\sum_{x}p_{x}\sum_{j\ni x}q_{j|x}\log\frac{q_{j|x}}{Q_{j|x}(\bm{r})A_{x}(\bm{r})}

(10)

with the remark that if
q_{j|x}
and
Q_{j|x}(\bm{r})
are both
0
, then the fraction in the
\log
should simply be
1/A_{x}(\bm{r})
.

Next we define
\varphi_{\textrm{q}}
and
\varphi_{\textrm{r}}
. At this point we simply express them using
Q
,
R
, and
\varphi
, but we will shortly see that they are indeed the minimum of
\varphi
with one of the variables fixed. Using (
9
) and (
10
) we get the following specific formulas: for
\bm{q}\in K_{\textrm{q}}
and
\bm{r}\in K_{\textrm{r}}
let

\varphi_{\textrm{q}}(\bm{q})\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\varphi\big(\bm{q},R(\bm{q})\big)=\sum_{x}p_{x}\sum_{j\ni x}q_{j|x}\log q_{j|x}-\sum_{y}p^{y}\sum_{j}R_{j|y}(\bm{q})\log R_{j|y}(\bm{q});

(11)

\varphi_{\textrm{r}}(\bm{r})\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\varphi\big(Q(\bm{r}),\bm{r}\big)=-\sum_{x}p_{x}\log A_{x}(\bm{r})=-\sum_{x}p_{x}\log\sum_{j\ni x}\,\prod_{y}\big(r_{j|y}\big)^{p^{y|x}}.

(12)

Note that (
12
) works even for
\bm{r}\notin K^{\ast}_{\textrm{r}}
as all the expressions are
\infty
in that case.

Proposition 8
.

If
\mathcal{J}
is the set of independent sets of some graph
G
on the vertex set
\mathcal{X}
, then

H_{G}(X|Y)=\min_{K_{\textrm{q}}}\varphi_{\textrm{q}}.

Proof.

Recall that the original formula (
2
) for
H_{G}(X|Y)
involves minimization over random
J
containing
X
and independent from
Y
when conditioned on
X
(in other words,
Y-X-J
is a Markov chain). To define such a

one needs to specify the conditional probabilities
\mathbb{P}(J=j|X=x)
whenever
x\in j
. These conditional probabilities can be represented by a vector
\bm{q}\in K_{\textrm{q}}
. Due to the conditional independence, we have the expression

\mathbb{P}(J=j|Y=y)=\sum_{x\in j}p_{x|y}\,\mathbb{P}(J=j|X=x).

(13)

Note that we defined
R
using the same linear combinations, see Definition
5
. Consequently, if the
\mathbb{P}(J=j|X=x)

\bm{q}
, then the
\mathbb{P}(J=j|Y=y)

. Therefore

\displaystyle H(J|X)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{x}p_{x}\sum_{j\ni x}q_{j|x}\log q_{j|x};

\displaystyle H(J|Y)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{y}p^{y}\sum_{j}R_{j|y}(\bm{q})\log R_{j|y}(\bm{q}),

and hence the conditional mutual information
I(X;J\,|\,Y)=H(J|Y)-H(J|X)
is precisely
\varphi_{\textrm{q}}(\bm{q})
according to (

H_{G}(X|Y)=\min_{K_{\textrm{q}}}\varphi_{\textrm{q}}

∎

III
Convergence

In this section we derive various properties of the the minimization problems introduced in Section
II
. They will culminate in the proof that alternating optimization converges to the true minimum (Theorem
3
). We will also prove Theorems
2
and
4
along the way.

Proposition 9
.

The functions
\varphi
and
\delta
are nonnegative, lower semicontinuous, and convex. Moreover,
\delta(\bm{q},\bm{q^{\prime}})=0
if and only if
\bm{q}=\bm{q^{\prime}}
.

Proof.

Recall that
\varphi
and
\delta
were defined using the function
f\colon[0,1]^{2}\to(-\infty,\infty]
in Definition
7
. It is well known and easy to show that
f
is convex and lower semicontinuous, and hence so are
\varphi
and
\delta
.

Using the convexity of
f
we get that for any fixed
x,y
:

\mbox{both }\sum_{j}f\big(q_{j|x},r_{j|y}\big)\mbox{ and }\sum_{j}f\big(q_{j|x},q^{\prime}_{j|x}\big)\geq|\mathcal{J}|\,f\big(1/|\mathcal{J}|,1/|\mathcal{J}|\big)=0

showing that
\varphi,\delta\geq 0
. (This, of course, also follows from their representations as the sum of Kullback–Leibler divergences.)
∎

Note that lower semicontinuity implies that
\varphi
attains its minimum over any compact set. In particular, it has a minimum over
K_{\textrm{q}}\times K_{\textrm{r}}
.

Proposition 10

(
\bm{r}=R(\bm{q})
is optimal for fixed
\bm{q}
)
.

We have

\varphi(\bm{q},\bm{r})\geq\varphi\big(\bm{q},R(\bm{q})\big)=\varphi_{\textrm{q}}(\bm{q})\mbox{ for any }\bm{q}\in K_{\textrm{q}};\bm{r}\in K_{\textrm{r}}.

Equality holds if and only if
\bm{r}=R(\bm{q})
.

Proof.

Using formula (
9
) for a fixed
\bm{q}
, it immediately follows from Gibbs’ inequality (applied for each
y
in the second sum) that the unique optimal choice for
\bm{r}
is
R(\bm{q})
.
∎

Proposition 11

(
\bm{q}=Q(\bm{r})
is optimal for fixed
\bm{r}
)
.

We have

\varphi(\bm{q},\bm{r})\geq\varphi\big(Q(\bm{r}),\bm{r}\big)=\varphi_{\textrm{r}}(\bm{r})\mbox{ for any }\bm{q}\in K_{\textrm{q}};\bm{r}\in K_{\textrm{r}}.

If
\bm{r}\notin K^{\ast}_{\textrm{r}}
, then both sides are
\infty
. Furthermore, for
\bm{r}\in K^{\ast}_{\textrm{r}}
equality holds if and only if
\bm{q}=Q(\bm{r})
.

Proof.

This is an immediate consequence of the 3-point property (that we will shortly state in Proposition
13
) and the fact that
\delta\geq 0
.
∎

Corollary 12
.

Propositions
10
and
11
clearly show that

\varphi_{\textrm{q}}(\bm{q})=\min_{\bm{r}\in K_{\textrm{r}}}\varphi(\bm{q},\bm{r})\mbox{ and }\varphi_{\textrm{r}}(\bm{r})=\min_{\bm{q}\in K_{\textrm{q}}}\varphi(\bm{q},\bm{r}).

In particular,
\varphi
,
\varphi_{\textrm{q}}
,
\varphi_{\textrm{r}}
have the same minimum over their respective convex domains:

\min_{K_{\textrm{q}}\times K_{\textrm{r}}}\varphi=\min_{K_{\textrm{q}}}\varphi_{\textrm{q}}=\min_{K_{\textrm{r}}}\varphi_{\textrm{r}}.

It also follows that both
\varphi_{\textrm{q}}
and
\varphi_{\textrm{r}}
are convex as they can be obtained as minimizing the convex
\varphi(\bm{q},\bm{r})
in one of the variables.

Note that, combined with Proposition
8
, this completes the proof of Theorem
2
. Moreover, Theorem
4
also follows as we simply need to substitute (
12
), which expresses
\varphi_{\textrm{r}}
, into
H_{G}(X|Y)=\min_{K_{\textrm{r}}}\varphi_{\textrm{r}}
.

From this point on we follow the footsteps of the general theory
[
6
]
of alternating minimization problems by proving the so-called
3-point and 4-point properties
, and show how they imply convergence to the minimum through the 5-point property.

The following identity can be thought of as a Pythagorean theorem for the “squared distances”
\varphi
and
\delta
. Csiszár and Tusnády refer to it as the 3-point property. (In their general setting it may hold only as an inequality
\geq
but in our case we always have equality.)

Proposition 13

(3-point property)
.

For any
\bm{q}\in K_{\textrm{q}}
and
\bm{r}\in K_{\textrm{r}}
we have

\varphi(\bm{q},\bm{r})=\delta\big(\bm{q},Q(\bm{r})\big)+\underbrace{\varphi\big(Q(\bm{r}),\bm{r}\big)}_{=\varphi_{\textrm{r}}(\bm{r})}.

Proof.

First let us consider the cases when one of the terms on the right-hand side is
\infty
. In both cases we need to show that one can find
x,y,j
with
p_{x,y}>0
,
q_{j|x}>0
,
r_{j|y}=0
so that we can conclude that the left-hand side
\varphi(\bm{q},\bm{r})
is also
\infty
.

•

We have
\varphi_{\textrm{r}}(\bm{r})=\infty
if and only
A_{x}(\bm{r})=0
for some
x
. Fix such an
x
and take a
j\ni x
with
q_{j|x}>0
, which must exist as their sum is
1
. Since
A_{x}(\bm{r})=0
, there must exist
y
such that
r_{j|y}=0
and
p_{x,y}>0
.

•

We have
\delta\big(\bm{q},Q(\bm{r})\big)=\infty
if and only if there exist
j,x
such that
q_{j|x}>0
but
Q_{j|x}(\bm{r})=0
, which means, by the definition of
Q_{j|x}
, that there exists
y
such that
p_{x,y}>0
and
r_{j|y}=0
.

Otherwise we can simply combine formula (
10
) for
\varphi(\bm{q},\bm{r})
, formula (
12
) for
\varphi_{\textrm{r}}(\bm{r})
, and formula (
8
) for
\delta(\bm{q},\bm{q^{\prime}})
with
\bm{q^{\prime}}=Q(\bm{r})
to get the claim.
∎

Proposition 14

(4-point property)
.

For any
\bm{q},\bm{q^{\prime}}\in K_{\textrm{q}}
and
\bm{r}\in K_{\textrm{r}}
we have

\varphi\big(\bm{q},R(\bm{q^{\prime}})\big)\leq\varphi(\bm{q},\bm{r})+\delta(\bm{q},\bm{q^{\prime}}).

Proof.

We may assume that the right-hand side is finite, otherwise the inequality is trivial. It follows that for any triple
x,y,j
with
p_{x,y}>0
,
q_{j|x}>0
we must have both
r_{j|y}>0
and
q^{\prime}_{j|x}>0
. Let
\bm{r^{\prime}}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=R(\bm{q^{\prime}})
. Then
r^{\prime}_{j|y}\geq p_{x|y}\,q^{\prime}_{j|x}>0
. So for any such triple all the variables are positive and we may write:

\varphi(\bm{q},\bm{r})+\delta(\bm{q},\bm{q^{\prime}})-\varphi(\bm{q},\bm{r^{\prime}})=\sum_{x,y,j}p_{x,y}\,q_{j|x}\log\frac{q_{j|x}r^{\prime}_{j|y}}{q^{\prime}_{j|x}r_{j|y}},

which, using that
\log t\geq 1-1/t
, can be bounded from below as follows:

\sum_{x,y,j}p_{x,y}\,q_{j|x}\left(1-\frac{q^{\prime}_{j|x}r_{j|y}}{q_{j|x}r^{\prime}_{j|y}}\right)=1-\sum_{y}p^{y}\sum_{j}\frac{r_{j|y}}{r^{\prime}_{j|y}}\underbrace{\sum_{x}p_{x|y}q^{\prime}_{j|x}}_{=R_{j|y}(\bm{q^{\prime}})=r^{\prime}_{j|y}}=1-\sum_{y}p^{y}\sum_{j}r_{j|y}=0,

and the proof is complete.
∎

Now we are ready to prove that the alternating optimization process converges to the minimum.

Proof of Theorem
3
.

Consider the sequences
\bm{q}^{(n)}
and
\bm{r}^{(n)}
of alternating optimization started from some
\bm{q}^{(0)}\in\ints(K_{\textrm{q}})
. Fix any pair
\bm{q}\in K_{\textrm{q}}
,
\bm{r}\in K_{\textrm{r}}
, and let
n
be a positive integer. Using Proposition
13
for the triple
\bm{q},\bm{r}^{(n-1)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle Q}}{{\longmapsto}}\bm{q}^{(n)}
and Proposition
14
for the quadruple
\bm{q},\bm{r},\bm{q}^{(n)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle R}}{{\longmapsto}}\bm{r}^{(n)}
we get that

\displaystyle\delta\big(\bm{q},\bm{q}^{(n)}\big)+\varphi\big(\bm{q}^{(n)},\bm{r}^{(n-1)}\big)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\tiny{3-pt}}}{{=}}\varphi\big(\bm{q},\bm{r}^{(n-1)}\big);

\displaystyle\varphi\big(\bm{q},\bm{r}^{(n)}\big)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\tiny{4-pt}}}{{\leq}}\varphi(\bm{q},\bm{r})+\delta\big(\bm{q},\bm{q}^{(n)}\big).

Since
\bm{q}^{(n)}\in\ints(K_{\textrm{q}})
holds for all
n
, we have
\delta\big(\bm{q},\bm{q}^{(n)}\big)<\infty
. Therefore adding the two inequalities above results in

\varphi\big(\bm{q}^{(n)},\bm{r}^{(n-1)}\big)+\varphi\big(\bm{q},\bm{r}^{(n)}\big)\leq\varphi(\bm{q},\bm{r})+\varphi\big(\bm{q},\bm{r}^{(n-1)}\big).

Since

\varphi\big(\bm{q}^{(n)},\bm{r}^{(n)}\big)\leq\varphi\big(\bm{q}^{(n)},\bm{r}^{(n-1)}\big)

by Proposition
10
, it follows that

\varphi\big(\bm{q}^{(n)},\bm{r}^{(n)}\big)+\varphi\big(\bm{q},\bm{r}^{(n)}\big)\leq\varphi\big(\bm{q},\bm{r}\big)+\varphi\big(\bm{q},\bm{r}^{(n-1)}\big).

(14)

This is what Csiszár and Tusnády refer to as the
5-point property
for the points
\bm{q},\bm{r},\bm{r}^{(n-1)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle Q}}{{\longmapsto}}\bm{q}^{(n)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle R}}{{\longmapsto}}\bm{r}^{(n)}
.

Note that the second term on either side is an element of the sequence
\varphi\big(\bm{q},\bm{r}^{(n)}\big)\geq 0
. First we assume that these elements are all finite. Then for any
\varepsilon>0
there must be infinitely many
n
such that

\varphi\big(\bm{q},\bm{r}^{(n)}\big)\geq\varphi\big(\bm{q},\bm{r}^{(n-1)}\big)-\varepsilon,

otherwise the sequence would converge to
-\infty
, contradicting that each element is nonnegative. For any such
n
we get from (
14
) that

\varphi\big(\bm{q}^{(n)},\bm{r}^{(n)}\big)\leq\varphi\big(\bm{q},\bm{r}\big)+\varepsilon.

Since
\varphi\big(\bm{q}^{(n)},\bm{r}^{(n)}\big)
is monotone decreasing, it has a limit that must satisfy

\lim_{n\to\infty}\varphi\big(\bm{q}^{(n)},\bm{r}^{(n)}\big)\leq\varphi\big(\bm{q},\bm{r}\big)+\varepsilon

for any positive
\varepsilon
, and hence for
\varepsilon=0
as well.

If, on the other hand,
\varphi\big(\bm{q},\bm{r}^{(n)}\big)=\infty
for some
n
, then
\varphi(\bm{q},\bm{r})=\infty
follows from the 4-point property as
\delta\big(\bm{q},\bm{q}^{(n)}\big)<\infty
, and we have the same conclusion: the limit is at most
\varphi(\bm{q},\bm{r})
.

Since this holds for any
\bm{q}
and
\bm{r}
, it follows that the limit must be the minimum of
\varphi
.
∎

IV
Convex corners

Convex corners are downward closed, convex subsets of
[0,\infty)^{n}
. It is possible to define entropy functions for convex corners, and this general theory was known to include the notion of graph entropy (via the vertex packing polytope, a convex corner associated to a graph). In this section we will show that conditional graph entropy can also be expressed as the entropy of an associated convex corner. Moreover, we will even define a dual problem in the form of another convex corner.

Besides revealing a nice theoretical connection to a general theory, this also has significant practical implications: the dual problem provides a way to check optimality in the primal problem, even yielding an error bound. The error bound comes in particularly handy when combined with alternating optimization: we can stop at any time through the iterations
\bm{r}^{(n)}
and compute this error bound
\delta
, which then ensures that we are at most
\delta
away from the optimum:

\varphi_{\textrm{r}}\big(\bm{r}^{(n)}\big)-\delta\leq H_{G}(X|Y)\leq\varphi_{\textrm{r}}\big(\bm{r}^{(n)}\big).

We start by recalling the basic concepts regarding convex corners.

IV-A

Entropy of convex corners

Definition 15
.

A set
K\subset[0,\infty)^{\mathcal{X}}
is said to be
downward closed
if the following property
4
4

4

Here
0\leq\bm{a^{\prime}}\leq\bm{a}
means that
0\leq a^{\prime}_{x}\leq a_{x}
for each

\bm{a}=\big(a_{x}\big)_{x\in\mathcal{X}}
for points in
\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{X}}
.
holds:

\mbox{if }\bm{a}\in K\mbox{, then }\bm{a^{\prime}}\in K\mbox{ for all points }0\leq\bm{a^{\prime}}\leq\bm{a}.

Similarly,

if
\bm{a^{\prime}}\in K
whenever
\bm{a}\in K
and
\bm{a^{\prime}}\geq\bm{a}

We say that

Given a random variable

denote the following

Note that
\varphi_{\textrm{a}}
depends on the distribution of

\varphi_{\textrm{a}}^{X}
when we want to emphasize this dependence. The entropy is defined as the minimum of

H_{K}(X)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\min_{\bm{a}\in K}\varphi_{\textrm{a}}^{X}(\bm{a}).

The function

corresponding to the convex corner

A related useful concept is the

K^{\ast}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\big\{\bm{b}\geq 0\,:\,\sum_{x}a_{x}b_{x}\leq 1\mbox{ for all }\bm{a}\in K\big\}.

One can show that

is also a convex corner,
\big(K^{\ast}\big)^{\ast}=K

H_{K}(X)+H_{K^{\ast}}(X)=H(X)
.
For these and further properties of

, see
[

and
[

.

We will also use the following notations: for
\bm{a},\bm{b}\in[0,\infty)^{\mathcal{X}}
let
\bm{a}\bm{b}
denote the vector
\big(a_{x}b_{x}\big)_{x\in\mathcal{X}}
(coordinate-wise multiplication). Similarly,
\bm{a}^{-1}
denotes the vector with coordinates

(provided that each

is positive). Furthermore,

\bm{a}K\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\big\{\bm{a}\bm{b}\,:\,\bm{b}\in K\big\}\mbox{ and }K^{-1}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\big\{\bm{b}^{-1}\,:\,\bm{b}\in K\big\}.

Note that
\varphi_{\textrm{a}}(\bm{a}\bm{b})=\varphi_{\textrm{a}}(\bm{a})+\varphi_{\textrm{a}}(\bm{b})
and
\varphi_{\textrm{a}}(\bm{a}^{-1})=-\varphi_{\textrm{a}}(\bm{a})
. Finally, we denote the vector
\big(p_{x}\big)_{x\in\mathcal{X}}
by
\bm{p}
. Then
\varphi_{\textrm{a}}(\bm{p})=-\sum_{x}p_{x}\log p_{x}
is the entropy of
X
.

IV-B

Primal problem

Now we introduce the
a-problem

\min_{K_{\textrm{a}}}\varphi_{\textrm{a}}
, which is, in fact, an equivalent formulation of the r-problem. We have already defined the function to minimize:
\varphi_{\textrm{a}}
. Next we define the convex corner
K_{\textrm{a}}
(associated to
X,Y,\mathcal{J}
) simply as the smallest downward closed set containing
A(K_{\textrm{r}})
.

Definition 16
.

Let

K_{\textrm{a}}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\big\{\bm{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{X}}\,:\,0\leq\bm{a}\leq A(\bm{r})\mbox{ for some }\bm{r}\in K_{\textrm{r}}\big\}.

Proposition 17
.

The set
K_{\textrm{a}}
is a convex corner and
\displaystyle H_{K_{\textrm{a}}}(X)=\min_{K_{\textrm{a}}}\varphi_{\textrm{a}}=H_{G}(X|Y)
.

Proof.

The key observation is that
A_{x}
is a concave function for each
x
, which follows immediately from the following claim:
let
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k}\geq 0
with
\alpha_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{k}\leq 1
; then

f(t_{1},\ldots,t_{k})\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=t_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots t_{k}^{\alpha_{k}}

is a concave function in the positive orthant

\big\{(t_{1},\ldots,t_{k})\,:\,t_{1},\ldots,t_{k}\geq 0\big\}
.
Indeed, it is easy to see that the Hessian of
f
is given by

H_{i,j}=\begin{cases}\frac{\alpha_{i}\alpha_{j}}{t_{i}t_{j}}f(\bm{t})&\mbox{ if }i\neq j;\\
\frac{\alpha_{i}(\alpha_{i}-1)}{t_{i}^{2}}f(\bm{t})&\mbox{ if }i=j.\end{cases}

Then for a vector
\bm{u}=(u_{i})
we have

\bm{u}H\bm{u}^{\top}\big/f(\bm{t})=\bigg(\sum_{i}\frac{\alpha_{i}u_{i}}{t_{i}}\bigg)^{2}-\sum_{i}\frac{\alpha_{i}u_{i}^{2}}{t_{i}^{2}}\leq 0

by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, proving that the Hessian is negative semidefinite.

Since each
A_{x}
is the sum of such functions, it is concave as well.

Now suppose that
\bm{a},\bm{a^{\prime}}\in K_{\textrm{a}}
. By definition, there exist
\bm{r},\bm{r^{\prime}}\in K_{\textrm{r}}
such that
\bm{a}\leq A(\bm{r})
and
\bm{a^{\prime}}\leq A(\bm{r^{\prime}})
. Then for any
t\in(0,1)
and for any
x
we have

ta_{x}+(1-t)a^{\prime}_{x}\leq tA_{x}(\bm{r})+(1-t)A_{x}(\bm{r^{\prime}})\leq A_{x}\big(t\bm{r}+(1-t)\bm{r^{\prime}}\big),

where the second inequality is due to the concavity of
A_{x}
. It follows that the convex combination

t\bm{a}+(1-t)\bm{a^{\prime}}\leq A\big(\underbrace{t\bm{r}+(1-t)\bm{r^{\prime}}}_{\in K_{\textrm{r}}}\big)

also lies in
K_{\textrm{a}}
, proving the convexity of
K_{\textrm{a}}
.

Since
K_{\textrm{r}}
is compact and
A
is continuous, the image
A(K_{\textrm{r}})
is also compact, and hence so is
K_{\textrm{a}}
. Furthermore, if
\bm{r}\in K^{\ast}_{\textrm{r}}\supseteq\ints(K_{\textrm{r}})\neq\emptyset
, then
A_{x}(\bm{r})>0
for each
x
, so
K_{\textrm{a}}
has a nonempty interior.

Finally, to see that
H_{K_{\textrm{a}}}(X)=H_{G}(X|Y)
, it suffices to show that

\min_{K_{\textrm{a}}}\varphi_{\textrm{a}}=\min_{K_{\textrm{r}}}\varphi_{\textrm{r}},

which follows immediately from
\varphi_{\textrm{r}}=\varphi_{\textrm{a}}\circ A
and the monotonicity of
\varphi_{\textrm{a}}
: if
\bm{a}\leq A(\bm{r})
, then

\varphi_{\textrm{a}}(\bm{a})\geq\varphi_{\textrm{a}}\big(A(\bm{r})\big)=\varphi_{\textrm{r}}(\bm{r})

with equality when
\bm{a}=A(\bm{r})\in K_{\textrm{a}}
.
∎

Remark 18
.

We make some comments regarding the a-problem.

•

Note that
K_{\textrm{a}}
depends only on
\mathcal{J}
(or the graph) and the conditional distributions of
Y\,|\,X=x
(but not on the distribution of
X
). In the unconditioned case
K_{\textrm{a}}
is the vertex packing polytope
\VP(G)
of the graph: the convex hull of the indicator functions of the independent sets. In general,
K_{\textrm{a}}
is not necessarily a polytope, it may be a more complicated convex set with “curvy” boundary. For an example, see Figure
2
in Section
IV-D
.

•

It is easy to see that
\varphi_{\textrm{a}}
is a strictly convex function over
(0,1]^{\mathcal{X}}
. Consequently, the a-problem always has a unique minimum point.

•

Note that the dimension of the a-problem is usually much smaller than that of the q-problem or the r-problem. However, the domain is not a polytope in this case and the complexity of the a-problem is, in some sense, hidden in the definition of the domain.

IV-C

Dual problem

Now we introduce another convex corner that will lead to a dual problem. To this end, for each
j
we define a function
\tau_{j}\colon[0,\infty)^{\mathcal{X}}\times[0,\infty)^{\mathcal{Y}}\to\mathbb{R}
: for
\bm{b}\in[0,\infty)^{\mathcal{X}}
and
\bm{t}\in[0,\infty)^{\mathcal{Y}}
we set

\tau_{j}(\bm{b},\bm{t})\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\sum_{x\in j}p_{x}b_{x}\prod_{y}\big(t_{y}\big)^{p^{y|x}}.

Note that
\sum_{y}p^{y|x}=1
, hence
\tau_{j}(\bm{b},\bm{t})
is homogeneous in
\bm{t}
: for any scalar
\lambda>0
we have
\tau_{j}(\bm{b},\lambda\bm{t})=\lambda\tau_{j}(\bm{b},\bm{t})
.

Definition 19
.

Let

L_{j}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\left\{\bm{b}\in[0,\infty)^{\mathcal{X}}\,:\,\forall\bm{t}\,\tau_{j}(\bm{b},\bm{t})\leq\sum_{y}p^{y}t_{y}\right\}=\left\{\bm{b}\in[0,\infty)^{\mathcal{X}}\,:\,\tau_{j}(\bm{b},\bm{t})\leq 1\mbox{ for all }\bm{t}\mbox{ with }\sum_{y}p^{y}t_{y}=1\right\}.

(15)

Finally, we define
L
as the intersection of all
L_{j}
:

L\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\bigcap_{j}L_{j}.

To see that
L
is a convex corner, notice that for any given
j
and
\bm{t}
, the points
\bm{b}
for which
\tau_{j}(\bm{b},\bm{t})\leq 1
form a downward closed polyhedron, and
L
is the intersection of such sets.

Remark 20
.

For graph entropy (i.e., the unconditioned case
|\mathcal{Y}|=1
) it can be seen easily that

L=\big\{\bm{b}\,:\,\forall j\,\sum_{x\in j}p_{x}b_{x}\leq 1\big\}=(\bm{p}K_{\textrm{a}})^{\ast}.

As the following lemma shows, the containment
L\subseteq(\bm{p}K_{\textrm{a}})^{\ast}
is true in general.

Lemma 21
.

For any
\bm{a}\in K_{\textrm{a}}
and
\bm{b}\in L
we have
\displaystyle\sum_{x}p_{x}a_{x}b_{x}\leq 1
. In other words,
L\subseteq(\bm{p}K_{\textrm{a}})^{\ast}
.

Proof.

We have
\bm{a}\leq A(\bm{r})
for some
\bm{r}\in K_{\textrm{r}}
, thus

\sum_{x}p_{x}a_{x}b_{x}\leq\sum_{j}\sum_{x\in j}p_{x}b_{x}\,\prod_{y}\big(r_{j|y}\big)^{p^{y|x}}\leq\sum_{j}\sum_{y}p^{y}r_{j|y}=\sum_{y}p^{y}\underbrace{\sum_{j}r_{j|y}}_{=1}=\sum_{y}p^{y}=1,

where we used that
\bm{b}\in L_{j}
for any given
j
, and hence
\tau_{j}(\bm{b},\bm{t})=\sum_{x\in j}p_{x}b_{x}\prod_{y}\big(t_{y}\big)^{p^{y|x}}\leq\sum_{y}p^{y}t_{y}
holds for
t_{y}=r_{j|y}
.
∎

Corollary 22

For any
\bm{a}\in K_{\textrm{a}}
and
\bm{b}\in L

\varphi_{\textrm{a}}(\bm{a})+\varphi_{\textrm{a}}(\bm{b})\geq 0.

In other words,

\min_{K_{\textrm{a}}}\varphi_{\textrm{a}}+\min_{L}\varphi_{\textrm{a}}\geq 0.

(16)

Proof.

Since

The Orlitsky–Roche example

Orlitsky and Roche considered the following simple example, see

. Let

IV-E

Fractional chromatic number

Given a convex corner
K
, it is natural to ask what the maximum of its entropy function is. That is, by varying the distribution of
X
, what is the maximal possible
H_{K}(X)
we can get for a fixed
K
? In general one can say the following about this maximum entropy.

Lemma 25

(see Corollary 1.2.21 in
[
4
]
)
.

Let
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{X}}
be an arbitrary convex corner. Then

\max_{X}H_{K}(X)=\log\tau(K),

where
\tau(K)
denotes the smallest
t\geq 1
such that the constant
1/t
vector lies in
K
. (Note that here
X
can be any random variable on
\mathcal{X}
: its support may be a proper subset of
\mathcal{X}
.)

The question arises: is there a special meaning of
\tau(K)
in our setting? In the unconditioned case, that is, for the vertex packing polytope
K=\VP(G)
,
\tau(K)
is known to be equal to the fractional chromatic number of the graph
[
17
, Lemma 4]
. Is there a generalization of this result: does
\tau(K_{\textrm{a}})
have a nice graph theoretic meaning in the conditional setting?

Problem 26
.

Fix a graph
G
equipped with a distribution on
\mathcal{Y}
at each vertex
x
(described by
p_{y|x}
). Note that this determines the convex corner
K_{\textrm{a}}
. Is it possible to give a (graph theoretic) description of
\tau(K_{\textrm{a}})
? This could lead to a notion generalizing the fractional chromatic number to
measure-labelled graphs
.

V
Discussion of the algorithm

As we have seen in the introduction, one may start at any point
\bm{q}^{(0)}\in\ints(K_{\textrm{q}})
and alternate in applying the mappings
R
and
Q
to get a sequence (
4
) with decreasing
\varphi
-values. In fact, Theorem
3
tells us that the values always converge to
\min\varphi(\bm{q},\bm{r})=H_{G}(X|Y)
.

In this section, we provide an error bound for the algorithm, then propose a tweak for improving the running time, and finally analyze the rate of convergence in the unconditioned case of graph entropy.

V-A

Error bound

How long should we run the iterations? A natural stopping rule is to terminate the algorithm at a step where the drop in the
\varphi
-value gets below some threshold. Is there a way to know how far we are from the actual minimum? Using the dual problem defined in Section
IV-C
, we can easily get an error bound for any given
\bm{r}^{(n)}
we stop at.

Theorem 27
.

Let
\bm{r}\in K_{\textrm{r}}
arbitrary and set
\bm{a}=A(\bm{r})
. For each
j
consider the following maximization problem:

1+\delta_{j}(\bm{a})\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\max_{\bm{t}}\tau_{j}\big(\bm{a}^{-1},\bm{t}\big)=\max_{\bm{t}}\sum_{x\in j}\frac{p_{x}}{a_{x}}\prod_{y}\big(t_{y}\big)^{p^{y|x}}\mbox{ under the constraints }t_{y}\geq 0;\,\sum_{y}p^{y}t_{y}=1.

(20)

Then
\varphi_{\textrm{a}}(\bm{a})=\varphi_{\textrm{r}}(\bm{r})
is at most
\delta(\bm{a})\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\max_{j}\delta_{j}(\bm{a})
away from the minimum. More precisely,

\varphi_{\textrm{r}}(\bm{r})-H_{G}(X|Y)\leq\log\big(1+\delta(\bm{a})\big)\leq\delta(\bm{a}).

In particular,
\bm{a}
(and hence
\bm{r}
) is optimal if and only if
\delta(\bm{a})=0
.

Remark 28
.

Note that each maximization is a convex optimization problem, whose dimension (
|\mathcal{Y}|
) is small compared to that of the r-problem (
|\mathcal{Y}|\cdot|\mathcal{J}|
) so we can solve them with high precision relatively fast.

Proof.

By definition,
\bm{b}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\big(1+\delta(\bm{a})\big)^{-1}\bm{a}^{-1}
lies in
L
. Therefore

H_{G}(X|Y)=-\min_{L}\varphi_{\textrm{a}}\leq-\varphi_{\textrm{a}}(\bm{b})=\log\big(1+\delta(\bm{a})\big)+\varphi_{\textrm{a}}(\bm{a}).

∎

The table at the end of Section
IV-D
compares this error bound to the true error for the Orlitsky–Roche example.

V-B

A tweak: deleting redundant sets

The running time of the algorithm depends on two things: the time required to perform a single step and the number of steps required to get within the desired distance of the minimum. With one small tweak we can achieve significant gains for both at the same time.

First of all, note that at each step the algorithm performs
\mathcal{O}\big(|\mathcal{X}|\cdot|\mathcal{Y}|\cdot|\mathcal{J}|\big)
operations when computing
\bm{r}^{(n)}=R\big(\bm{q}^{(n)}\big)
and
\bm{q}^{(n+1)}=Q\big(\bm{r}^{(n)}\big)
.

In examples there are often a large number of (independent sets)
j
that are actually not “used” at the optimal
\bm{q}
and
\bm{r}
in the sense that
q_{j|x}=0
and
r_{j|y}=0
for all
x,y
. For any such
j
, these variables will converge to
0
through the iterations. To speed things up, we may want to detect such
redundant
sets
j
early and set the corresponding variables to
0
. Note that these variables remain to be
0
from this point on, so we may remove such a
j
from
\mathcal{J}
and proceed with the iterations using a smaller set
\mathcal{J}
. This immediately reduces the computational complexity for each subsequent step. Moreover, it typically results in a better rate of convergence as well: without redundant sets, the error usually decays at a faster rate. Consequently, this version of the algorithm often requires considerably fewer steps to reach the desired precision. (This phenomenon will be illustrated for graph entropy both by an example and an analysis.)

However, when the algorithm terminates and outputs an (approximate) minimum point for some subsystem
\mathcal{J}
of the original
\mathcal{J}_{\textrm{or}}
, we should justify that all deletions we made along the way were indeed necessary. So we take the corresponding point
\bm{a}
and perform our optimality check/error bound calculations: we compute
\delta_{j}(\bm{a})
as in (
20
). For each
j\in\mathcal{J}
we should get a negative number or a very small positive number, confirming that we are indeed close to the minimum point of the problem corresponding to the subsytsem
\mathcal{J}
. If
\delta_{j}(\bm{a})\leq 0
for all deleted sets
j\in\mathcal{J}_{\textrm{or}}\setminus\mathcal{J}
, it means that we cannot do better even if we used the deleted sets. If, on the other hand,
\delta_{j}(\bm{a})>0
for some of the deleted sets
j
, then we should “re-activate” them (i.e., add them back to
\mathcal{J}
).

So we propose the following
tweaked version of the iterative process
.

•

Set
r^{(0)}_{j|y}=1/|\mathcal{J}|
for each
j
and
y
. Note that
\bm{r}^{(0)}\in K_{\textrm{r}}
.

•

Set
\varepsilon_{\mbox{\scriptsize{act}}}=10^{-3}|\mathcal{Y}|/|\mathcal{J}|
.

•

At step
n
:

–

compute
\bm{r}^{(n-1)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle Q}}{{\longmapsto}}\bm{q}^{(n)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle R}}{{\longmapsto}}\bm{r}^{(n)}
;

–

for any
j
with
\sum_{y}r^{(n)}_{j|y}<\varepsilon_{\mbox{\scriptsize{act}}}
, remove
j
from
\mathcal{J}
and delete the corresponding variables
r^{(n)}_{j|y}
for all
y
;

–

for each
y
, re-normalize the remaining variables
r^{(n)}_{j|y}
,
j\in\mathcal{J}
such that

\mbox{the constraint }\sum_{j}r^{(n)}_{j|y}=1\mbox{ is satisfied again.}

•

Compute the value
\varphi_{\textrm{r}}(\bm{r}^{(n)})
after every
10
steps, and terminate the iterations when this value, compared to the previous one, decreases by less than some small
\varepsilon_{\mbox{\scriptsize{prec}}}
(say,
10^{-15}
).

•

Set
\bm{a}=A\big(\bm{r}^{(n)}\big)
.

•

Compute
\delta_{j}(\bm{a})
as in (
20
) for all
j\in\mathcal{J}
as well as for all previously deleted sets
j
.

•

If
\delta_{j}(\bm{a})\leq 0
for each deleted
j
, then return
\varphi_{\textrm{a}}(\bm{a})
with the error bound
\displaystyle\max_{j\in\mathcal{J}}\delta_{j}(\bm{a})
.

•

Otherwise, for each deleted
j\in\mathcal{J}_{\textrm{or}}\setminus\mathcal{J}
with
\delta_{j}(\bm{a})>0
, add
j
back to
\mathcal{J}
and create the corresponding variables
r^{(n)}_{j|y}
for each
y
, setting them to some small positive values.
6
6

6

Any values work but the following choice should guarantee that we get a smaller
\varphi_{\textrm{r}}
-value right after restart: set
r^{(n)}_{j|y}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\varepsilon t_{y}
for a sufficiently small
\varepsilon
, where
\bm{t}
denotes the vector at which (
20
) takes its maximum.
Then re-normalize as before so that
\bm{r}^{(n)}\in K_{\textrm{r}}
holds again. Finally, restart the iterations, this time with no set-deletions.

Normally, we set
\varepsilon_{\mbox{\scriptsize{act}}}
to be fairly small so that it is extremely unlikely that we unjustifiably delete a set
j
, and the check at the end should (essentially always) confirm this.

Our implementation in Python is available on GitHub
[
11
]
.

An example

Let

V-D

Rate of convergence for graph entropy

As we have seen in the example of the previous section, the precision of the iterative algorithm appears to grow at some linear rate (for steps
n\geq n_{0}
). The following analysis confirms this observation and explains how one can determine this (“eventual”) rate in the unconditioned setting (i.e., graph entropy). Rigorous proofs would make the analysis undesirably long and technical so in this section we settle for only sketching the arguments.

Formulas for graph entropy

In the special case of graph entropy (i.e., when
|\mathcal{Y}|=1
so there is only one
y
) the formulas simplify considerably. First of all, we have
p^{y}=p^{y|x}=1
and
p_{x,y}=p_{x|y}=p_{x}
, and we may omit
y
in the indices. So
\bm{r}
now denotes a point
(r_{j})_{j\in\mathcal{J}}
in the set

K_{\textrm{r}}=\bigg\{\bm{r}=\big(r_{j}\big)\,:\,r_{j}\geq 0;\,\sum_{j}r_{j}=1\bigg\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{J}}.

Furthermore, we have the following simple formulas:

\displaystyle R_{j}(\bm{q})

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in j}p_{x}\,q_{j|x};

\displaystyle A_{x}(\bm{r})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\ni x}r_{j};

\displaystyle Q_{j|x}(\bm{r})

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}0&\mbox{ if }x\notin j;\\
r_{j}\big/A_{x}(\bm{r})&\mbox{ if }x\in j;\end{cases}

\displaystyle\varphi_{\textrm{r}}(\bm{r})

\displaystyle=-\sum_{x}p_{x}\log A_{x}(\bm{r})=-\sum_{x}p_{x}\log\sum_{j\ni x}r_{j}.

So
A
is simply a linear
\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{J}}\to\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{X}}
map corresponding to the following matrix
M\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{J}\times\mathcal{X}}
:

M_{x,j}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\begin{cases}1&\mbox{if }x\in j;\\
0&\mbox{if }x\notin j.\end{cases}

That is, the columns of
M
are the indicators functions of the sets
j
, and we have
A(\bm{r})=M\bm{r}
.

As for the stepping map
F_{\textrm{r}}\colon\bm{r}\mapsto\bm{r^{\prime}}
for the r-problem, we have

r^{\prime}_{j}=\bigg(\underbrace{\sum_{x\in j}\frac{p_{x}}{A_{x}(\bm{r})}}_{\Delta_{j}(\bm{r})\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=}\bigg)r_{j}.

(21)

It follows that if
\bm{r}
is a fixed point of
F_{\textrm{r}}
(i.e.,
r^{\prime}_{j}=r_{j}
for each
j
), if and only if
\Delta_{j}(\bm{r})=1
for any
j
with
r_{j}>0
.

It is worth mentioning that if
\partial_{j}
denotes the partial derivative w.r.t. the variable
r_{j}
, then we have

\partial_{j}\varphi_{\textrm{r}}(\bm{r})=-\sum_{x}p_{x}\frac{\partial_{j}A_{x}(\bm{r})}{A_{x}(\bm{r})}=-\sum_{x\in j}\frac{p_{x}}{A_{x}(\bm{r})}=-\Delta_{j}(\bm{r}).

So what the stepping map
F_{\textrm{r}}(\bm{r})
does in this unconditioned setting is simply multiply
\bm{r}
(coordinate-wise) by the negative of the gradient
\nabla\varphi_{\textrm{r}}(\bm{r})
.

Case of no redundant sets

We start our analysis with the case when each
j
is “used” (
r_{j}>0
) at the minimum point
\bm{r}
of
\varphi_{\textrm{r}}
. This is always the case in the tweaked version of the algorithm which ensures that all redundant sets are eventually deleted. Note that
\Delta_{j}(\bm{r})=1
for all
j
in this case, and hence the gradient
\nabla\varphi_{\textrm{r}}(\bm{r})=-\bm{e}
for the all-ones vector
\bm{e}
.

For a vector
\bm{v}
we will use the notation
D(\bm{v})
for the corresponding diagonal matrix. In particular,
D(\bm{r})\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{J}\times\mathcal{J}}
is the diagonal matrix with entries
r_{j}
, while
D(\bm{p}\bm{a}^{-2})\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}}
is the diagonal matrix with entries
p_{x}/a_{x}^{2}
.

Lemma 30
.

Assume that
\bm{r}
is a minimum point of
\varphi_{\textrm{r}}
and that each
r_{j}>0
. Set
\bm{a}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=A(\bm{r})=M\bm{r}
so that
\bm{a}
is the minimum point of
\varphi_{\textrm{a}}
. Let

N\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=D(\bm{r})M^{\top}D(\bm{p}\bm{a}^{-2})M.

Then
N\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{J}\times\mathcal{J}}
is a square matrix with nonnegative entries and with the following properties:

•

in each column the sum of the entries is
1
(and hence
1
is an eigenvalue);

•

N
is diagonalizable with eigenvalues in
[0,1]
;

•

\ker N=\ker M
.

The proof of the lemma can be found at the end of the section.

Claim
.

The rate of convergence is governed by the smallest nonzero eigenvalue
\lambda_{\min}
of
N
:

\varphi_{\textrm{r}}\big(\bm{r}^{(n)}\big)=\min_{K_{\textrm{r}}}\varphi_{\textrm{r}}+O\big((1-\lambda_{\min})^{2n}\big).

(22)

So the rate of growth for the precision is
-2\lg(1-\lambda_{\min})
.

Example
.

The dodecahedral graph of Example
29
has five independent sets of size
8
. Note that their pairwise intersections are of size
2
. Independent sets of smaller size are all redundant so let
\mathcal{J}
be the set of these five sets. Then we have
r_{j}=1/5
for all
j
and
a_{x}=2/5
for all
x
. It follows that each diagonal entry of
N
is equal to
1/2
, while all other entries are equal to
1/8
. Therefore, the eigenvalues (with multiplicity) are
1;\ 3/8;\ 3/8;\ 3/8;\ 3/8
. So
\lambda_{\min}=3/8
and we get that the rate of growth for the precision is
-2\lg(5/8)+o(1)
, which is consistent with our numerical findings presented earlier.

Now we will sketch the proof of the claim. For the sake of simplicity we assume that
\ker M=\{0\}
.
In this case
\bm{r}
is the unique minimum point of
\varphi_{\textrm{r}}
and hence
\bm{r}^{(n)}\to\bm{r}
as
n\to\infty
. So difference vector
\bm{\varrho}^{(n)}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\bm{r}^{(n)}-\bm{r}
converges to
0
. Set
\bm{\alpha}^{(n)}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=M\bm{\varrho}^{(n)}
. Then
\|\bm{\alpha}^{(n)}\|=O\big(\|\bm{\varrho}^{(n)}\|\big)
converges to
0
as well.

With these notations, we compute the coordinates of the next point
\bm{r}^{(n+1)}=F_{\textrm{r}}\big(\bm{r}^{(n)}\big)
of our sequence:

r^{(n+1)}_{j}=\bigg(\sum_{x\in j}\frac{p_{x}}{a_{x}+\alpha^{(n)}_{x}}\bigg)(r_{j}+\varrho^{(n)}_{j})=\bigg(\underbrace{\sum_{x\in j}\frac{p_{x}}{a_{x}}}_{=1}-\sum_{x\in j}\frac{p_{x}\alpha^{(n)}_{x}}{a^{2}_{x}}+\sum_{x\in j}\frac{p_{x}\big(\alpha^{(n)}_{x}\big)^{2}}{a^{2}_{x}(a_{x}+\alpha^{(n)}_{x})}\bigg)(r_{j}+\varrho^{(n)}_{j}).

It follows that

\varrho^{(n+1)}_{j}=\varrho^{(n)}_{j}-r_{j}\sum_{x\in j}\frac{p_{x}}{a_{x}^{2}}\alpha^{(n)}_{x}+O\big(\|\bm{\varrho}^{(n)}\|^{2}\big).

Since
\bm{\alpha}^{(n)}=M\bm{\varrho}^{(n)}
, we conclude that

\bm{\varrho}^{(n+1)}=(I-N)\bm{\varrho}^{(n)}+O\big(\|\bm{\varrho}^{(n)}\|^{2}\big).

(23)

Recall that
\lambda_{\min}
is the smallest nonzero eigenvalue of
N
. Under our assumption
\ker N=\ker M=\{0\}
, so
0
is not an eigenvalue now, meaning that the largest eigenvalue of the diagonalizable matrix
I-N
is
1-\lambda_{\min}
. Then it is not hard to deduce from (
23
) that

\|\bm{\varrho}^{(n)}\|=O\big((1-\lambda_{\min})^{n}\big).

As for the
\varphi_{\textrm{r}}
-value,

\varphi_{\textrm{r}}\big(\bm{r}^{(n)}\big)=\varphi_{\textrm{r}}\big(\bm{r}+\bm{\varrho}^{(n)}\big)=\varphi_{\textrm{r}}(\bm{r})+\underbrace{\nabla\varphi_{\textrm{r}}(\bm{r})}_{=-\bm{e}}\cdot\bm{\varrho}^{(n)}+O\big(\|\bm{\varrho}^{(n)}\|^{2}\big),

where the dot product
\bm{e}\cdot\bm{\varrho}^{(n)}
, which is simply the sum of the coordinates of
\bm{\varrho}^{(n)}
, is equal to
0
because this sum is
1
both for
\bm{r}\in K_{\textrm{r}}
and for
\bm{r}^{(n)}\in K_{\textrm{r}}
. Then (
22
) clearly follows.

In fact, heuristically,

\bm{\varrho}^{(n+1)}\approx(I-N)\bm{\varrho}^{(n)}
means that if we write
\bm{\varrho}^{(n)}
in an eigenbasis, then the parts corresponding to smaller eigenvalues will become negligible and
\bm{\varrho}^{(n)}
will be close to an eigenvector with the maximal eigenvalue
1-\lambda_{\min}
, and hence
\bm{\varrho}^{(n+1)}\approx(1-\lambda_{\min})\bm{\varrho}^{(n)}
for large
n
(at least for typical starting points).

When
\ker M=\ker N
has positive dimension,
1
is an eigenvalue of
I-N
and it seems that we do not necessarily have exponential decay. Note, however, that vectors from
\ker M
do not make a difference from the point of view of
\varphi
-value because for any
\bm{v}\in\ker M
we have
A(\bm{r^{\prime}}+\bm{v})=A(\bm{r^{\prime}})
, and hence
\varphi_{\textrm{r}}(\bm{r^{\prime}}+\bm{v})=\varphi_{\textrm{r}}(\bm{r^{\prime}})
.

We close this section by proving the required properties of
N
.

Proof of Lemma
30
.

Since
\bm{r}
is a minimum point of
\varphi_{\textrm{r}}
, by Proposition
24

\bm{r}
is a fixed point of
F_{\textrm{r}}
, and hence
\Delta_{j}(\bm{r})=1
for each
j
. This means that
M^{\top}(\bm{p}\bm{a}^{-1})
is the all-ones vector
\bm{e}
.

We also have
M\bm{r}=A(\bm{r})=\bm{a}
. Then

N^{\top}\bm{e}=M^{\top}D(\bm{p}\bm{a}^{-2})M\underbrace{D(\bm{r})\bm{e}}_{=\bm{r}}=M^{\top}D(\bm{p}\bm{a}^{-2})\underbrace{M\bm{r}}_{=\bm{a}}=M^{\top}\underbrace{D(\bm{p}\bm{a}^{-2})\bm{a}}_{=\bm{p}\bm{a}^{-1}}=M^{\top}(\bm{p}\bm{a}^{-1})=\bm{e},

confirming that
1
is an eigenvalue and that each column sum of
N
is
1
. Since all entries are nonnegative, it follows that
N
is a (left) stochastic matrix, and hence
|\lambda|\leq 1
for each eigenvalue
\lambda
.

Furthermore,
N
is similar to a positive semidefinite matrix:

D(\bm{r}^{-1/2})ND(\bm{r}^{1/2})=D(\bm{r}^{1/2})M^{\top}D(\bm{p}\bm{a}^{-2})MD(\bm{r}^{1/2}),

so
N
is diagonalizable with nonnegative eigenvalues.

Finally, let
B=D(\bm{p}^{1/2}\bm{a}^{-1})M
. Then

VI
Conclusion

The optimal rate of lossless functional compression with side information at the receiver can be characterized by conditional graph entropy. However, little can be found in the literature about this entropy notion. So we set out to study conditional graph entropy in more detail. Our starting point was the original formula (
2
) which can also be formulated as the q-problem. Our first step was the discovery of the r-problem and the stepping maps
Q,R
between the two problems. This interaction was reminiscent of the alternating optimization in the EM algorithm, which made us realize that there might be an underlying alternating minimization problem. This, in turn, helped us to analyze the iterative algorithm because we could turn to the general theory of Csiszár and Tusnády: we verified that the 3-point, 4-point, and 5-point properties hold in our setting, and showed that the iterations always converge to the minimum. Our theoretical results lead to a practical algorithm for computing conditional graph entropy that also comes with an error bound based on a dual problem.

Alternating optimization has a vast and growing literature. The fact that (conditional) graph entropy is part of this family of problems will hopefully inspire future research in the area.
