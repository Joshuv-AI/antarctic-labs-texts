On the distribution of the mean energy in the unitary orbit of quantum states
Raffaele Salvia, Vittorio Giovannetti

Abstract
Given a closed quantum system, the states that can be reached with a cyclic process are those with the same spectrum as the initial state. Here we prove that, under a very general assumption on the Hamiltonian, the distribution of the mean extractable work is very close to a gaussian with respect to the Haar measure. We derive bounds for both the moments of the distribution of the mean energy of the state and for its characteristic function, showing that the discrepancy with the normal distribution is increasingly suppressed for large dimensions of the system Hilbert space.



1
Introduction

Haar-uniform random unitary matrices are a resource required for various quantum algorithm
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
.
As an example, the
randomised benchmark
protocol is a method to test the error rate of a quantum circuit, requiring it to perform a sequence of random operations
[
4
]
. Versions of the randomised benchmark are used by the companies IBM
[
5
]
and Microsoft
[
6
]
to test the functionality of their experimental quantum computing hardware.
Other applications of random unitaries include quantum cryptography
[
7
]
and the simulation of many body physics
[
8
,
9
]
.

In this paper we charachterise the the distribution of the energetic cost of implementing a random unitary transformation. To be more specific, we study the distribution of the expected value of the energy gained from the cycle which sends the quantum state
\rho
to
U\rho U^{\dagger}
, where
U
is a matrix extracted randomly with respect to the Haar measure of the unitrary group.
Our main finding is that, when the quantum system has many degrees of freedom (that is when the Hilbert space has dimension
d\gg 1
), then -provided a weak condition on the system Hamiltonian- the distribution of the energy cost of a random unitary matrix is approximately Gaussian.

Implementing a random unitary is not an easy task. Actually, only a small subset of quantum operations (called the
Clifford gates

[
10
]
) are easy to implement in an actual circuit - the complexity of a quantum circuit is often measured with the number of non-Clifford gates it requires
[
11
]
.
To overcome these difficulties, it has been theorised the possibility of circuits that simulate a random unitary up to a certain moment of the distribution
[
12
]
. A circuit which is able to emulate a uniform distribution up to the
t
-th moment is called an
unitary t-design
. If we want to realise a
t
-design with
t>3
, it is still necessary to use non-Clifford gates
[
13
,
14
]
, but only a small amount of them
[
15
]
.

The results of this paper are useful for characterising an ideal source of uniformly distributed unitary operations. Furthermore, they are valid also for its approximations which are used in actual quantum computing, the
t
-designs which match the uniform unitary distribution up to the
t
-th moment.
Indeed we will show that, if the dimension of the Hilbert space is big enough, and if it holds a very general condition, the first moments of the distribution are very well approximated by the moments of a gaussian distribution with the same variance.
Exploiting this result, we will then be able to estimate the maximum error that we make in replacing the charachteristic function of distribution of the energy with the characteristic function of a normal distribution.

We emphasize that our paper is concerned with the distribution of the mean estracted work
between different processes
, which is different from the distribution of extracted work
in a given process
(a much more studied subject in in classical and quantum theormodynamics
[
16
,
17
]
).
The latter distribution is in many cases Gaussian for classical systems in the quasistatic limit
[
18
,
19
,
20
]
, but not quantum system
[
21
]
: in particular, as shown in Ref.
[
22
]
, for slowly driven quantum-thermodynamical processes the work distribution becomes non-gaussian whenever quantum coherences are generated during the protocol.
However, we may think to a “process” which consists in selecting a random unitary matrix according to the Haar measure and then applying it to the system: in this case, under the conditions mentioned above the work distribution would be Gaussian: we think it worth to notice that, when the initial state of the system is a state of thermal equilibrium (so that Jarzynski’s equality
[
23
]
holds), this implies the validity of the classical fluctuation-dissipation relation
[
16
]
.

2
Introduction to the problem

Let
A
be a
d
-dimensional quantum system initialized in the state
\hat{\rho}
and forced to evolve in
time by external modulations of its Hamiltonian
\hat{H}
.
Following
[
24
,
25
]
, the average amount of work one can extract from the process
can be computed as

W_{U}(\hat{\rho};\hat{H}):=E(\hat{\rho};\hat{H})-E(\hat{U}\hat{\rho}\hat{U}^{\dagger};\hat{H})\;,

(1)

where

\displaystyle E(\hat{\rho};\hat{H}):=\mbox{Tr}[\hat{\rho}\hat{H}]\;,

(2)

is the mean energy of
\hat{\rho}
, and
\hat{U}
is the element of the unitary group
\mathbf{U}(d)

associated to the applied driving.
The allowed values of
W_{U}(\hat{\rho};\hat{H})

are limited by the inequalities

\displaystyle{\cal A}(\hat{\rho};\hat{H})\leq W_{U}(\hat{\rho};\hat{H})\leq{\cal E}(\hat{\rho};\hat{H})\;,

(3)

where
{\cal E}(\hat{\rho};\hat{H})
and
{\cal A}(\hat{\rho};\hat{H})

are respectively the
ergotropy
and
anti-ergotropy
functionals of the model.
The first one was introduced in Ref.
[
26
]
and corresponds to maximum work one can
extract from the system by optimizing
W_{U}(\hat{\rho};\hat{H})
with respect to the choices of the control unitary
\hat{U}
for fixed

\hat{\rho}
and
\hat{H}
.
A closed formula for the ergotropy is provided by the expression

\displaystyle{\cal E}(\hat{\rho};\hat{H})

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\max_{U}W_{U}(\hat{\rho};\hat{H})=W_{\hat{U}^{(\downarrow)}}(\hat{\rho};\hat{H})

(4)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E(\hat{\rho};\hat{H})-E(\hat{\rho}^{(\downarrow)};\hat{H})\;,

obtained by setting
\hat{U}
equal to the optimal unitary
\hat{U}^{(\downarrow)}
for which
the final state of the cycle
\hat{U}\hat{\rho}\hat{U}^{\dagger}
corresponds to the passive counterpart
\hat{\rho}^{(\downarrow)}
of
\hat{\rho}

[
24
,
25
]
, i.e.

\hat{U}^{(\downarrow)}:=\sum_{j=1}^{d}|\epsilon^{(\uparrow)}_{j}\rangle\langle\lambda_{j}^{(\downarrow)}|\;,\quad\hat{\rho}^{(\downarrow)}:=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\lambda_{j}^{(\downarrow)}|\epsilon^{(\uparrow)}_{j}\rangle\langle\epsilon^{(\uparrow)}_{j}|\;,

(5)

with
|\lambda_{j}^{(\downarrow)}\rangle
the eigenvector of
\hat{\rho}
associated with the eigenvalue
\lambda_{j}^{(\downarrow)}

listed in non-increasing order (i.e.

\lambda_{j}^{(\downarrow)}\geq\lambda_{j+1}^{(\downarrow)}
), and
|\epsilon^{(\uparrow)}_{j}\rangle

the eigenvector of
\hat{H}
with eigenvalue
\epsilon^{(\uparrow)}_{j}
listed instead in non-decreasing order
(i.e.

\epsilon_{j}^{(\uparrow)}\leq\epsilon_{j+1}^{(\uparrow)}
).
Similarly for the anti-ergotropy we can write

\displaystyle{\cal A}(\hat{\rho};\hat{H})

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\min_{U}W_{U}(\hat{\rho};\hat{H})=W_{\hat{U}^{(\uparrow)}}(\hat{\rho};\hat{H})

(6)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E(\hat{\rho};\hat{H})-E(\hat{\rho}^{(\uparrow)};\hat{H})\;,

where now
\hat{\rho}^{(\uparrow)}
is the anti-passive state of
\hat{\rho}
,
obtained by the unitary
\hat{U}^{(\uparrow)}
that reverses the order in which the populations of
\hat{\rho}^{(\downarrow)}
are listed, i.e.

\hat{U}^{(\uparrow)}:=\sum_{j=1}^{d}|\epsilon^{(\uparrow)}_{j}\rangle\langle\lambda_{j}^{(\uparrow)}|\;,\quad\hat{\rho}^{(\uparrow)}:=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\lambda_{j}^{(\uparrow)}|\epsilon^{(\uparrow)}_{j}\rangle\langle\epsilon^{(\uparrow)}_{j}|\;,

(7)

with
\lambda_{j}^{(\uparrow)}:=\lambda_{d-j+1}^{(\downarrow)}
.

Saturating the upper bound (
3
) is an important optimization task which can be practically difficult to implement, as it implicitly requires an exact knowledge of the full spectral decomposition of the system Hamiltonian.
In this perspective, it is interesting to understand how close one can get from the boundary values (
3
) by randomly selecting

\hat{U}
for a model in which both
\hat{\rho}
and
\hat{H}
are assigned. Clearly,
as the dimensionality of the system increases, we do not expect such a naive approach to be particularly effective.
Still, providing an exact characterization of the associated efficiency is a well-posed statistical question which can be of some help in identifying which physical systems
are best suited as successful candidates for implementing quantum battery models
[
27
,
28
,
29
,
30
,
31
]
.
In order to tackle this issue, in the present paper we
study
the probability distribution
P(E|\hat{\rho};\hat{H})

of the mean output energy

\displaystyle E:=E(\hat{U}\hat{\rho}\hat{U}^{\dagger};\hat{H})\;,

(8)

which originates by random sampling
\hat{U}
on the unitary group
\mathbf{U}(d)
via its natural measure (the Haar measure
d\mu(\hat{U})
)
[
32
]
. A numerical example of this distribution, with
d=7
is showed in Fig.
1
.
From Eq. (
1
) it is clear that knowing
P(E|\hat{\rho};\hat{H})
we can then reconstruct the
probability distribution

P_{\text{work}}(W|\hat{\rho};\hat{H})

of the average extracted work
W:=W_{U}(\hat{\rho};\hat{H})
via a simple shift of the argument, i.e.

P_{\text{work}}(W|\hat{\rho};\hat{H}):=P\Big(E=E(\hat{\rho};\hat{H})-W\Big|\hat{\rho};\hat{H}\Big)\;.

(9)

Figure 1:
Numerical estimation of the distribution
P(E|\hat{\rho};\hat{H})
in a seven-levels (
d=7
) system in which
\hat{H}
has eigenvalues
\{-1.6,-1.2,-0.6,0,0.4,1.3,1.7\}
, and
\hat{\rho}
has eigenvalues

\{0.395,0.224,0.151,0.115,0.079,0.0020,0.013\}
.
The histogram plots the empirical distribution of
E(\hat{U}\hat{\rho}\hat{U}^{\dagger};\hat{H})
for a sample of
10^{5}
unitary matrices
U\in{\bf U}(7)
, which are distributed uniformly according to the Haar measure. The blue line is the probability density function of the Gaussian distribution
P_{G}^{(\mu,\Sigma^{(2)})}(E)
with the variance
\Sigma^{(2)}
given by (
32
). In this example
\Sigma^{(2)}\simeq 0.02024
and
\eta_{\hat{H}}\simeq 0.2317
.

Our main finding is that, under mild assumptions on the system Hamiltonian, when the dimension of the Hilbert space
d
is sufficiently large, the central moments of the probability
P(E|\hat{\rho};\hat{H})

can be approximated by those of a gaussian distribution
P_{G}^{(\mu,\Sigma^{(2)})}(E)
having mean value
\mu
and variance
\Sigma^{(2)}
equal to those of
P(E|\hat{\rho};\hat{H})
, i.e.

\Sigma^{(p)}:=\left\langle\left(E-\mu\right)^{p}\right\rangle\simeq\Sigma_{G}^{(p)}\;,

(10)

where
\langle f(E)\rangle
denotes the mean value of the function
f(E)
with respect to

P(E|\hat{\rho};\hat{H})

[
32
]
and

\displaystyle\Sigma_{G}^{(p)}:={\mathbf{G}}_{p}\times\begin{cases}1&\mbox{($p$ even)}\;,\\
\\
0&\mbox{($p$ odd)\;,}\end{cases}

(11)

with
{\mathbf{G}}_{p}
the scaling factor

\displaystyle{\mathbf{G}}_{p}:=(p-1)!!\;\left(\Sigma^{(2)}\right)^{p/2}\;.

(12)

In order to settle the approximation (
10
) into firm quantitative ground
we prove that for
d
sufficiently larger than
p

the discrepancies between the l.h.s and the r.h.s of such equation can be bounded as

\displaystyle{\left\lvert\Sigma^{(p)}-\Sigma_{G}^{(p)}\right\rvert}\leq{\mathbf{G}}_{p}\;f_{\hat{H}}(d,p)\;,

(13)

where
f_{\hat{H}}(d,p)
is a positive function which
in the large
d
limit scales as
\mathcal{O}(1/d+\eta_{\Delta\hat{H}})
for
p
even, and
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\eta_{\Delta\hat{H}}})
for
p
odd,
with
\eta_{\Delta\hat{H}}
being a functional (see Eq. (
84
) below)
that for typical choices of the system Hamiltonian is very much depressed.
Using the theory of generating functions, we also show a stronger result which, aside from bounding the error on each individual moment of the distribution

P(E|\hat{\rho};\hat{H})
, directly links its characteristic function with the one of the gaussian function
P_{G}^{(\mu,\Sigma^{(2)})}(E)
.

The rest of the manuscript is organised as follows.
Section
3
introduces the concepts and some known results we will use in the proof.
In particular, Sec.
3.2
sets up the problem of calculating the moments of the distribution, which can be expressed as the Haar integral (
20
). The evaluation of this integral can be set up using the theory of Weingarten calculus, that we introduce in section
3.4
. The practical computation of the integrals involved in the proof will require other combinatorical concepts, which will be introduced at the points where they are needed.
In Sec.
4
we derive some useful inequalities and discuss the assumptions on the system that are needed to enforce the Gaussian approximation.
Section
5
presents a proof of Eq. (
13
) that applies for the special case in which
the input state of the system
\hat{\rho}
is pure, and in
Sec.
6
we address instead the case of arbitrary input states. Finally, in Sec.
7
we bound instead the distance between the characteristic function
\langle e^{-itE}\rangle
of the distribution
P(E|\hat{\rho};\hat{H})
, and the characteristic function of the Gaussian distribution
P_{G}^{(\mu,\Sigma^{(2)})}(E)
.
Conclusion and outlook are finally given in Sec.
8
.

3
Preliminary considerations

This section is dedicated to clarify some useful mathematical properties of the model.

3.1
Basic properties of
P(E|\hat{\rho};\hat{H})

From Eqs. (
2
) and (
3
) it follows that for fixed
\hat{\rho}
and
\hat{H}
the range of the random variable
E
is limited by the inequalities

\mbox{Tr}[\hat{\rho}^{(\downarrow)}\hat{H}]=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\lambda_{j}^{(\downarrow)}\epsilon^{(\uparrow)}_{j}\leq E\leq\sum_{j=1}^{d}\lambda_{j}^{(\uparrow)}\epsilon^{(\uparrow)}_{j}=\mbox{Tr}[\hat{\rho}^{(\uparrow)}\hat{H}]\;.

(14)

In the special cases where either the input state is completely mixed (i.e.
\hat{\rho}={\hat{\mathbbm{1}}}/d
), or the Hamiltonian is proportional to the identity
(i.e.
\hat{H}=E_{0}{\hat{\mathbbm{1}}}
),
the upper and lower bounds of (
14
) coincide
forcing
E
to assume the constant value
\mbox{Tr}[\hat{H}]/d
, i.e. imposing
the distribution
P(E|{\hat{\rho}};\hat{H})
to become a Dirac delta

\displaystyle P(E|{\hat{\mathbbm{1}}}/d;\hat{H})=P(E|\hat{\rho};E_{0}{\hat{\mathbbm{1}}})=\delta(E-\tfrac{\mbox{\small Tr}[\hat{H}]}{d})\;.

(15)

From Eq. (
8
) it is also clear that for arbitrary choices of
\hat{\rho}
any rigid shift of the Hamiltonian spectrum results in a translation of the distribution

P(E|\hat{\rho};\hat{H})
, i.e.

\displaystyle P(E|\hat{\rho};\hat{H})=P\left(E-E_{0}\Big|\hat{\rho};\hat{H}-{E_{0}}{\hat{\mathbbm{1}}}\right)\;.

(16)

More generally noticing that for all choices of
\Delta_{0},E_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
one has

\displaystyle E(\hat{U}\hat{\rho}\hat{U}^{\dagger};\hat{H})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mbox{Tr}\left[\hat{U}\left(\hat{\rho}-\Delta_{0}\tfrac{{\hat{\mathbbm{1}}}}{d}\right)\hat{U}^{\dagger}\left(\hat{H}-E_{0}{\hat{\mathbbm{1}}}\right)\right]

(17)

\displaystyle+\Delta_{0}\left(\tfrac{\mbox{Tr}[\hat{H}]}{d}-E_{0}\right)+E_{0}\;,

we can draw the following formal identity

\displaystyle P(E|\hat{\rho};\hat{H})

(18)

\displaystyle=P\left(E-\Delta_{0}\left(\tfrac{\mbox{Tr}[\hat{H}]}{d}-E_{0}\right)-E_{0}\Big|\hat{\rho}-\Delta_{0}\frac{{\hat{\mathbbm{1}}}}{d};\hat{H}-E_{0}{\hat{\mathbbm{1}}}\right)\;,

where, generalizing the definition of
P(E|\hat{\rho};\hat{H})
, given
\hat{A}
and
\hat{B}
generic operators,
we use the symbol
P(E^{\prime}|\hat{A};\hat{B})
to represent the distribution of the variable
E^{\prime}:=\mbox{Tr}[\hat{U}\hat{A}\hat{U}^{\dagger}\hat{B}]
induced by the Haar measure
d\mu(\hat{U})
.

We observe next that
any two input states

\hat{\rho}^{\prime}
and
\hat{\rho}
which have the same spectrum will have the same energy probability distribution, i.e.

P(E|\hat{\rho}^{\prime};\hat{H})=P(E|\hat{\rho};\hat{H})
.
Indeed
under this condition we can always express
\hat{\rho}^{\prime}
as
\hat{V}\hat{\rho}\hat{V}^{\dagger}
with
\hat{V}\in\mathbf{U}(d)
, so that
for all functions
f(E)
one has

\displaystyle\int dEf(E)P(E|\hat{\rho}^{\prime};\hat{H})=\int d\mu(\hat{U})f(E(U\hat{V}\hat{\rho}\hat{V}^{\dagger}U^{\dagger};\hat{H}))

\displaystyle=\int d\mu(\hat{U}^{\prime})f(E(\hat{U}^{\prime}\hat{\rho}\hat{U}^{\prime\dagger};\hat{H}))=\int dEf(E)P(E|\hat{\rho};\hat{H})\;,

where we used the fact that
\hat{U}^{\prime}=\hat{U}\hat{V}\in\mathbf{U}(d)
, and the invariance property
d\mu(\hat{U})=d\mu(\hat{U}\hat{V})
of the Haar measure. Similarly due to
the cyclicity of the trace appearing in Eq. (
2
), we can conclude that

P(E|\hat{\rho};\hat{H})
is also invariant under unitary rotations of the system Hamiltonian, leading to the identity

P(E|V\rho V^{\dagger};WHW^{\dagger})=P(E|\hat{\rho};\hat{H})\;,\quad\forall V,W\in\mathbf{U}(d)\;,

(19)

which ultimately implies that
P(E|\hat{\rho};\hat{H})
can only depend upon the spectra
\{\lambda_{j}^{(\downarrow)}\}_{i}
and

\{\epsilon_{j}^{(\uparrow)}\}_{i}

of
\hat{\rho}
and
\hat{H}
but not on the specific choices of their associated
eigenstates
\{|\lambda_{j}^{(\downarrow)}\rangle\}_{i}
and
\{|\epsilon_{j}^{(\uparrow)}\rangle\}_{i}
nor on the relative overlap between them.

3.2
Central moments

In evaluating the moments of the distribution
P(E;\hat{\rho},\hat{H})
we have to consider the quantities

\displaystyle\langle E^{p}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int d\mu(\hat{U})\left(\Tr[\hat{U}\hat{\rho}\hat{U}^{\dagger}\hat{H}]\right)^{p}

(20)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1},\cdots j_{p}\\
k_{1},\cdots,k_{p}\end{subarray}}\rho_{j_{1}k_{1}}\cdots\rho_{j_{p}k_{p}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell_{1},\cdots\ell_{p}\\
i_{1},\cdots,i_{p}\end{subarray}}H_{\ell_{1}i_{1}}\cdots H_{\ell_{p}i_{p}}

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\int d\mu(\hat{U})\;U_{i_{1}j_{1}}U_{i_{2}j_{2}}\cdots U_{i_{p}j_{p}}U^{\dagger}_{k_{1}\ell_{1}}U^{\dagger}_{k_{2}\ell_{2}}\cdots U^{\dagger}_{k_{p}\ell_{p}}\;,

where
\rho_{jk}
,
H_{\ell i}
,
U_{ij}
are matrix elements of
\hat{\rho}
,
\hat{H}
,
\hat{U}
with respect to fixed basis
of the Hilbert space of the system
[
33
]
.
The integral appearing on the last term of (
20
) is widely known and admit solution
[
34
,
35
,
36
]

in terms of the
Weingarten functions

C_{[\sigma]}
for the unitary group
[
34
,
37
]
, i.e.

\displaystyle\int d\mu(\hat{U})\;U_{i_{1}j_{1}}U_{i_{2}j_{2}}\cdots U_{i_{p}j_{p}}U^{\dagger}_{k_{1}\ell_{1}}U^{\dagger}_{k_{2}\ell_{2}}\cdots U^{\dagger}_{k_{p}\ell_{p}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\tau,\sigma\in S_{p}}C_{[\sigma]}\prod_{a=1}^{p}\delta_{i_{a}\ell_{\tau(a)}}\delta_{j_{a}k_{\tau\sigma(a)}}\;,

(21)

with
S_{p}
representing the permutation group of
p
elements.
A precise definition of the
C_{[\sigma]}
s and their properties will be given in Sec.
3.4
. Here we simply notice that
replacing (
21
) into (
20
) leads to the expression

\langle E^{p}\rangle=\sum_{\tau,\sigma\in S_{p}}C_{[\sigma]}\;\rho{[\sigma\tau]}\;H{[\tau]}\;,

(22)

where for
\hat{\Theta}
generic operator and
\sigma\in S_{p}
, we introduced the functional

\displaystyle\Theta{[\sigma]}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\sum_{k_{1},\cdots,k_{p}}\Theta_{k_{1}k_{\sigma(1)}}\Theta_{k_{2}k_{\sigma(2)}}\cdots\Theta_{k_{p}k_{\sigma(p)}}\;.

(23)

To get a more intuitive understanding of what is this product, we need to introduce an important property of a permutation
\sigma\in S_{p}
: the structure of its cycles, which is sufficient to specify its
conjugacy class

[\sigma]
.
If the permutation
\sigma
has a fixed point, i.e. if there exists an
i\in\{1,2,\cdots,p\}
such that
\sigma(i)=i
, then we say that the permutation
\sigma
has a cycle of length 1. A cycle of length 2 (i.e.
a transposition) means that there exist two indices
i,j\in\{1,2,\cdots,p\}
such that
\sigma(i)=j
and
\sigma(j)=i
. In general given

\sigma\in S_{p}
its conjugacy class
[\sigma]
is uniquely identified via
the correspondence

\displaystyle[\sigma]\longleftrightarrow\{\alpha^{(1)}_{[\sigma]},\alpha^{(2)}_{[\sigma]},\dots,\alpha^{(c[\sigma])}_{[\sigma]}\}\;,

(24)

with

c[\sigma]
being the number of independent cycles admitted by
\sigma
, and with the positive integers

\alpha^{(j)}_{[\sigma]}
’s representing instead the lengths of such cycles organized in decreasing order, i.e.

\alpha^{(j)}_{[\sigma]}\geq\alpha^{(j+1)}_{[\sigma]}
.
Because each element in
\{1,2,\cdots,p\}
belongs to one and only one of the cycles of
\sigma
, we must have that

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{c[\sigma]}\alpha^{(j)}_{[\sigma]}=p\;,

(25)

implying that the
\alpha^{(j)}_{[\sigma]}
s provide a proper partition of the integer
p
(represented graphically with a
Young diagram

[
38
]
).
The set of indices
\{1,2,\cdots,p\}
can thus be partitioned in
c[\sigma]
subsets
\mathfrak{c}^{(i)}
, which each
\mathfrak{c}^{(i)}
having
\alpha^{(j)}_{[\sigma]}
elements.
We finally observe that two permutations
\sigma
and
\sigma^{\prime}
will belong to the
same conjugacy class if and only if we can
identify a third permutation that allows us to relate them via conjugation, i.e.

\displaystyle[\sigma]=[\sigma^{\prime}]\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad\exists\tau\in S_{p}|\quad\sigma^{\prime}=\tau\sigma\tau^{-1}\;.

(26)

The fact that each element
i\in\{1,2,\cdots,p\}
belongs to exactly one cycle of
\sigma
allows us to rewrite Eq. (
23
) as

\displaystyle\Theta{[\sigma]}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k_{1},\cdots,k_{p}}\prod_{r=1}^{c[\sigma]}\prod_{i\in\mathfrak{c}^{(r)}}\Theta_{k_{i}k_{\sigma(i)}}

(27)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{r=1}^{c[\sigma]}\sum_{\{k_{i}\}_{i\in\mathfrak{c}^{(r)}}}\prod_{i\in\mathfrak{c}^{(r)}}\Theta_{k_{i}k_{\sigma(i)}}=\prod_{r=1}^{c[\sigma]}\Tr[\hat{\Theta}^{\alpha^{(1)}_{[\sigma]}}]\;,

where in the last identity we used the fact that the the
r
-th summation runs over a set of
\alpha^{(r)}_{[\sigma]}
cyclical indices.
Equation (
27
) makes it clear that, as explicitly indicated by the notation, the terms
\Theta{[\sigma]}
(as well as
the coefficients
C_{[\sigma]}
, see Eq. (
44
) below)
depend upon
\sigma
only via its conjugacy class
[\sigma]
.
Replaced into Eq. (
22
), the identity (
27
) also
implies that
\langle E^{p}\rangle
can be expressed as linear combination of products of
traces of powers of
\hat{\rho}
and
\hat{H}
, i.e. explicitly

\langle E^{p}\rangle=\sum_{\tau,\sigma\in S_{p}}C_{[\sigma]}\;\left(\prod_{i=1}^{c[\sigma\tau]}\mbox{Tr}[\hat{\rho}^{\alpha^{(i)}_{[\sigma\tau]}}]\right)\left(\prod_{j=1}^{c[\tau]}\mbox{Tr}[\hat{H}^{\alpha^{(j)}_{[\tau]}}]\right)\;.

(28)

Equation (
28
) is the starting point of our analysis: we notice incidentally that it confirms
by virtue of Specht’s theorem
[
39
]
, that
P(E|\hat{\rho};\hat{H})
(and hence its moments) depends upon

\hat{\rho}
and
\hat{H}
only through their spectra.
In particular
for the cases
p=1,2
we get

\displaystyle\langle E\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle C_{[1]}\mbox{Tr}[\hat{H}]\;,

(29)

\displaystyle\langle E^{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle C_{[1,1]}(\mbox{Tr}[\hat{H}])^{2}+C_{[2]}(\Tr[\hat{\rho}^{2}])(\mbox{Tr}[\hat{H}])^{2}

(30)

\displaystyle+C_{[1,1]}(\mbox{Tr}[\hat{H}^{2}])(\Tr[\hat{\rho}^{2}])+C_{[2]}(\mbox{Tr}[\hat{H}^{2}])\;,

which using the explicit values of the functions
C_{[\sigma]}
reported in Tab.
1
leads to

\displaystyle\mu

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\langle E\rangle=\mbox{Tr}[\hat{H}]/d\;,

(31)

3.4
Combinatorial coefficients

The Weingarten functions

introduced in Eq. (

These terms are in general difficult to compute but a formal expression for them is provided by the formula
[

42

where the sum runs over all the irreducible representations

of the permutation group

which are generated by partitions of

which have at most

For

small
a list of the values of the

is reported in Tab.

As the number of irreducible representations of

increases with

For the purposes of the present work, however we do not need to compute the coefficients, indeed we just need to use the following known properties.

First of all, since
C_{[\sigma]}
is a functional of the conjugacy class, exploiting Eq. (
26
)
we can write

\displaystyle C_{[\tau\sigma\tau^{-1}]}=C_{[\sigma]}\;,

(44)

for all
\tau,\sigma\in S_{p}

Second, the overall sign of

is the sign of the permutation
\sigma

\mbox{Sign}[C_{[\sigma]}]=(-1)^{\lvert\sigma\rvert}=(-1)^{p-c[\sigma]}\;,

where
\lvert\sigma\rvert
is the minimal number of transpositions

is a product of, see Eq. (

).
Finally we shall use the fact that for large enough

the following asymptotic behaviour holds true

|C_{[\sigma]}|=\frac{\mathcal{C}_{[\sigma]}}{d^{p+|\sigma|}}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{d^{p+|\sigma|+2}}\right)\;,

(46)

where the integer number
\mathcal{C}_{[\sigma]}
– sometimes called the
Möbius function
of the permutation
\sigma
– is equal to the product

\mathcal{C}_{[\sigma]}:=\prod_{i=1}^{c[\sigma]}Cat_{{\alpha^{(i)}_{[\sigma]}}}\;,

(47)

with the
\alpha^{(j)}_{[\sigma]}

24
), and with

\displaystyle Cat_{n}:=\frac{1}{n+1}{2n\choose n}\;,

(48)

being the

-th Catalan number.
In particular
for the identical permutation this implies

|C_{[1^{p}]}|=\frac{1}{d^{p}}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{d^{p+2}}\right)\;.

(49)

The scaling (

) can be derived e.g. from Ref.
[
44
]
where
Collins and Matsumoto proved that when

\displaystyle p\leq\left\lfloor(d/\sqrt{6})^{4/7}\right\rfloor\;,

(50)

one has

\frac{\mathcal{C}_{[\sigma]}}{d^{p+\lvert\sigma\rvert}}\left(1-\frac{p-1}{d^{2}}\right)^{-1}\leq|C_{[\sigma]}|\leq\frac{\mathcal{C}_{[\sigma]}}{d^{p+\lvert\sigma\rvert}}\left(1-\frac{6p^{7/2}}{d^{2}}\right)^{-1}\;,

(51)

which via some simple algebraic manipulation can be casted in a weaker, but sometimes more useful form:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{C}_{[\sigma]}}{(d^{2}-1)^{p/2}d^{\rvert\sigma\lvert}}\left(1+\frac{p/2-1}{d^{2}}\right)\leq|C_{[\sigma]}|\leq\frac{\mathcal{C}_{[\sigma]}}{(d^{2}-1)^{p/2}d^{\rvert\sigma\lvert}}\left(1-\frac{6p^{7/2}}{d^{2}}\right)^{-1}\;.

(52)

4
Bounding the trace terms

Here we establish some useful relations that allow us to bound
the terms
\Delta H[\tau]
and
\Delta\rho[\sigma]
entering in Eqs. (
42
) and (
42
) and which
will allow us to identify the necessary conditions on

that are needed to prove the Gaussian approximation (
10

For this purpose we shall relay on the properties of the derangement set
S_{p}^{D}
and on the inequality
[
45
]

|\mbox{Tr}[\hat{\Theta}^{q_{2}}]|^{1/q_{2}}\leq\|\hat{\Theta}\|_{q_{2}}\leq\|\hat{\Theta}\|_{q_{1}}\;,\quad\forall q_{2}\geq q_{1}>0\;,

(53)

where for

\displaystyle\|\hat{\Theta}\|_{q}:=\mbox{Tr}[|\hat{\Theta}|^{q}]^{1/q}\;,

(54)

is the
q
-th Shatten norm of the operator
\hat{\Theta}
.

4.1
A useful inequality

To begin with let consider the special subset
S_{p}^{D*}
of
S_{p}^{D}
formed by those derangements
\tau

which can be decomposed into
p/2
cycles of length 2.
If
p
is even there are
(p-1)!!
of such elements, i.e.

\displaystyle|S_{p}^{D*}|=(p-1)!!\;,\qquad\mbox{($p$ even)}\;.

(55)

On the contrary
if
p
is odd there are no such permutations (at least one cycle must be of odd length): in this case
we identify
S_{p}^{D*}
with the empty set

\displaystyle S_{p}^{D*}=\varnothing\;,\qquad\mbox{($p$ odd)}\;.

(56)

By definition the elements of
S_{p}^{D*}
verify the condition

\alpha^{(j)}_{[\tau]}=2
for all
j
and thus,
by explicit computation, they fulfil the identity

\displaystyle\Theta[\tau]=(\mbox{Tr}[\hat{\Theta}^{2}])^{p/2}=\|\hat{\Theta}\|_{2}^{p}\;,\qquad\forall\tau\in S_{p}^{D*}\;,

(57)

for all operators
\hat{\Theta}
.
Now let
\tau\in S_{p}^{D}/S_{p}^{D*}
a derangement which cannot be decomposed into
p/2
cycles of length 2:
for such permutations we can prove that
\|\hat{\Theta}\|_{2}^{p}
provides an upper bound for the associated value
|\Theta[\tau]|
, i.e.

\displaystyle{|\Theta[\tau]|}\leq\|\hat{\Theta}\|_{2}^{p}\times\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\eta_{\hat{\Theta}}\;,&\quad\mbox{\rm($p$ even)}\;,\\
\\
\sqrt{\eta_{\hat{\Theta}}}\;,&\quad\mbox{\rm{($p$ odd)}}\;,\end{array}\right.

where

\displaystyle\eta_{\hat{\Theta}}:=\left(\frac{\|\hat{\Theta}\|_{3}}{\|\hat{\Theta}\|_{2}}\right)^{6}=\frac{(\mbox{Tr}[|\hat{\Theta}|^{3}])^{2}}{(\mbox{Tr}[\hat{\Theta}^{2}])^{3}}\;,

(62)

is a functional of
\hat{\Theta}
which due to (
53
)
fulfils the inequality
[
46
]

\displaystyle\frac{1}{d^{6}}\leq\eta_{\hat{\Theta}}\leq 1\;.

(63)

Equation (
4.1
) is a direct consequence of the following more general observation:

Proposition 1
.

Given
\tau
an element of the derangement set
S_{p}^{D}
we have

\displaystyle|\Theta[\tau]|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\eta_{\hat{\Theta}}^{|\tau|-p/2}(\mbox{\rm{Tr}}[\hat{\Theta}^{2}])^{p/2}=\eta_{\hat{\Theta}}^{|\tau|-p/2}\|\hat{\Theta}\|_{2}^{p}\;,

(64)

with
\eta_{\hat{\Theta}}
the parameter defined in Eq. (
62
).

Proof.

The bound (
64
) is clearly fulfilled by
\tau\in S_{p}^{D*}
due to Eq. (
57
) and the fact that for such permutations
c[\tau]=p/2=|\tau|
, e.g. see Eq. (
39
).

Now let
\tau\in S_{p}^{D}/S_{p}^{D*}
a derangement which cannot be decomposed into
p/2
cycles of length 2.
Since
\tau
is an element of
S_{p}^{D}

all its coefficients

\{\alpha^{(1)}_{[\tau]},\alpha^{(2)}_{[\tau]},\dots,\alpha^{(c[\tau])}_{[\tau]}\}
are guaranteed to be greater or equal to 2, i.e.

\displaystyle\tau\in S_{p}^{D}\;\Longrightarrow\;\alpha^{(j)}_{[\tau]}\geq 2\qquad\forall j\;.

(65)

However since
\tau\notin S_{p}^{D*}
then some those terms
(say the first
K\geq 1
) must be strictly larger than
2
, i.e.

\displaystyle\alpha^{(j)}_{[\tau]}\geq 3,\qquad\forall 1\leq j\leq K\;.

(66)

Invoking (
53
) we can hence claim

\left|\mbox{Tr}[\hat{\Theta}^{\alpha^{(j)}_{[\tau]}}]\right|\leq(\mbox{Tr}[|\hat{\Theta}|^{3}])^{\alpha^{(j)}_{[\tau]}/3}=\|\hat{\Theta}\|_{3}^{\alpha^{(j)}_{[\tau]}}\;,\quad\forall 1<j\leq K\;,

(67)

and

\left|\mbox{Tr}[\hat{\Theta}^{\alpha^{(j)}_{[\tau]}}]\right|\leq\mbox{Tr}[\hat{\Theta}^{2}]^{{\alpha^{(j)}_{[\tau]}}/{2}}=\|\hat{\Theta}\|_{2}^{\alpha^{(j)}_{[\tau]}}\;,\quad\forall K<j\leq c[\tau]\;.

(68)

Observe also that
since
K
can be expressed as the number of cycles
c[\tau]
minus the number
c_{2}[\tau]
of cycles of length
2
, and since the latter is always smaller than or equal to the minimal number
|\tau|
of transpositions

\tau
can be decomposed of, we can bound this quantity as follows

\displaystyle K=c[\tau]-c_{2}[\tau]\geq c[\tau]-|\tau|=p-2|\tau|\;,

(69)

where in the last identity we used (
39
).
From the above expressions we can hence establish that for all
\tau\in S_{p}^{D}/S_{p}^{D*}
we have

\displaystyle|\Theta[\tau]|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|\prod_{j=1}^{c[\tau]}\mbox{Tr}[\hat{\Theta}^{\alpha^{(j)}_{[\tau]}}]\right|\leq\|\hat{H}\|_{3}^{\sum_{j=1}^{K}\alpha^{(j)}_{[\tau]}}\;\|\hat{\Theta}\|_{2}^{p-\sum_{j=1}^{K}\alpha^{(j)}_{[\tau]}}

(70)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\eta_{\hat{\Theta}}^{{\sum_{j=1}^{K}\alpha^{(j)}_{[\tau]}}/{6}}\|\hat{\Theta}\|_{2}^{p}\;,

where we employ the definition (
62
) and use the normalization condition Eq. (
25
) to write

\displaystyle\sum_{j=K+1}^{c[\tau]}\alpha^{(j)}_{[\tau]}=p-\sum_{j=1}^{K}\alpha^{(j)}_{[\tau]}\;.

(71)

From Eqs. (
66
) and (
69
)
we have also

\displaystyle{\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{K}\alpha^{(j)}_{[\tau]}}{6}}\geq\frac{K}{2}\geq\frac{p}{2}-|\tau|\;.

(72)

Therefore we can claim

\displaystyle\eta_{\hat{\Theta}}^{\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{K}\alpha^{(j)}_{[\tau]}}{6}}\leq\eta_{\hat{\Theta}}^{\frac{p}{2}-|\tau|}\;,

(73)

which replaced into Eq. (
70
) finally yields Eq. (
64
).
∎

The derivation of (
4.1
) from (
64
) finally proceed by observing that for
\tau\in S_{p}^{D}/S_{p}^{D*}
the exponent of
\eta_{\hat{\Theta}}
appearing in (

is always greater or equal to
1
when
p
even, and greater or equal to

for
p
odd, i.e.

\displaystyle|\tau|-p/2=p/2-c[\tau]\geq\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1\;,&\quad\mbox{\rm($p$ even)}\;,\\
\\
1/2\;,&\quad\mbox{\rm{($p$ odd)}}\;.\end{array}\right.

5
Central moments asymptotic expression for pure input states

In this section we present a proof of our main result that applies in the special scenario where the input state of the system is

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}=\ket{\psi}\bra{\psi}\;.

(88)

This constraint allows for some simplifications that help in clarifying the derivation.

Therefore invoking Eq. (

\displaystyle\Sigma^{(p)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{\sigma\in S_{p}}C_{[\sigma]}\right)\;\left(\sum_{\tau\in S^{D}_{p}}\Delta H[\tau]\right)\;.

This quantity should be compared with the corresponding Gaussian expression (

with the scaling factor

\displaystyle{\mathbf{G}}_{p}=\frac{(p-1)!!}{(d(d+1))^{p/2}}\|\Delta\hat{H}\|_{2}^{p}\;,

\displaystyle\Sigma^{(2)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\|\Delta\hat{H}\|_{2}^{2}}{d(d+1)}\;.

Under the above premise in the next section we show that Eq. (

\displaystyle f_{\hat{H}}(d,p)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{lr}\tfrac{p(p-2)}{2d}+\eta_{\Delta\hat{H}}\left(\tfrac{\left\lceil{p!}/{e}\right\rceil}{(p-1)!!}-1\right),&\mbox{($p$ even),}\\

\eta_{\Delta\hat{H}}

It is worth stressing that in this case, the validity of Eq. (

5.1
The geometric coefficient Eq (

A closed expression for the first factor on the r.h.s. of Eq. (

\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma\in S_{p}}C_{[\sigma]}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{(d-1)!}{(p+d-1)!}=\frac{D(d,p)}{(d(d+1))^{p/2}}\;,

\displaystyle D(d,p)

\displaystyle\frac{(d-1)!d^{p/2}(d+1)^{p/2}}{(p+d-1)!}\;,

fulfils the inequalities

\displaystyle 1\geq D(d,p)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1-\tfrac{p(p-2)}{2d}&\mbox{($p$ even),}\\
\\
1-\tfrac{(p+1)(p-1)}{2d}&\mbox{($p$ odd).}\end{array}\right.

An explicit derivation of Eq. (
97
) can be obtained by focusing on the trivial case where
the system Hamiltonian is proportional to the identity operator, i.e.

\displaystyle\hat{H}=E_{0}\hat{\mathbbm{1}}\;,\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad\Delta\hat{H}=0\;.

(103)

In this case in agreement with Eq. (

\displaystyle 0=\Sigma^{(p)}=\langle E^{p}\rangle-E_{0}^{p}\;,

(104)

Invoking hence Eqs. (

\displaystyle E_{0}^{p}=\langle E^{p}\rangle

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{\sigma\in S_{p}}C_{[\sigma]}\right)\;\left(\sum_{\tau\in S_{p}}H[\tau]\right)\;.

Our next step is to compute
\sum_{\tau\in S_{p}}H[\tau]

\tau

that is

\displaystyle\sum_{\tau\in S_{p}}H[\tau]=\sum_{k=0}^{p}\sum_{\tau\in S_{p}(k)}H[\tau]\;.

Then we recall that the number of elements of

corresponds to the number of permutations having exaclty

\left[{p\atop p-k}\right]

[

Observe next that from Eq. (

\displaystyle H[\tau]=E_{0}^{p}\;\mathbbm{1}[\tau]=E_{0}^{p}\;d^{c[\tau]}=E_{0}^{p}\;d^{p-|\tau|}\;,

\left(\tfrac{d}{d+2}\tfrac{d+1}{d+3}\right)\cdots\left(\tfrac{d}{d+p-3}\tfrac{d+1}{d+p-2}\right)\tfrac{\sqrt{d(d+1)}}{d+p-1}&\mbox{($p$ odd),}\\

The lower bound instead follows by using the inequalities

and noticing that in case of

\displaystyle\left(\tfrac{d}{d+2}\tfrac{d}{d+2}\right)\cdots\left(\tfrac{d}{d+p-2}\tfrac{d}{d+p-2}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{i=0}^{p/2-1}\left(1+\tfrac{2i}{d}\right)^{-2}\geq\prod_{i=0}^{p/2-1}\left(1-\tfrac{4i}{d}\right)

\displaystyle 1-\sum_{i=0}^{p/2-1}\tfrac{4i}{d}=1-\tfrac{p(p-2)}{2d}\;.

\displaystyle=\left(\tfrac{d}{d+2}\tfrac{d+1}{d+3}\right)\cdots\left(\tfrac{d}{d+p-3}\tfrac{d+1}{d+p-2}\right)\left(\tfrac{d}{d+p-1}\tfrac{d+1}{d+p}\right)\tfrac{d+p}{\sqrt{d(d+1)}}

\displaystyle=\Gamma(d,p+1)\tfrac{d+p}{\sqrt{d(d+1)}}

\displaystyle\geq\Gamma(d,p+1)\geq 1-\tfrac{(p+1)(p-1)}{2d}\;,

(115)

where the first inequality is obtained by invoking (

we can now estimate the distance of
\Sigma^{(p)}

7
Moment generating function

In this section we shall estimate the distance between the generating function of the
moments of the distribution
P(E;\hat{\rho},\hat{H})
, i.e.

\mathcal{G}(t):=\left\langle e^{t\left(E-\mu\right)}\right\rangle=\sum_{p=0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{p}\Sigma^{(p)}}{p!}\;,

(166)

and the one associated with the Gaussian distribution
P_{G}^{(\mu,\Sigma^{(2)})}(E)
, i.e.

\sum_{p=0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{p}\Sigma_{G}^{(p)}}{p!}=e^{\frac{1}{2}t^{2}\Sigma^{(2)}}\;,

(167)

where
\Sigma_{G}^{(p)}
are the Gaussian moments defined in Eq. (
10
).

In particular we shall show that, for values of
\lvert t\rvert
such that

\displaystyle\lvert t\rvert\leq\min\left\{\frac{\sqrt{N^{\star}}}{\sqrt{\Sigma^{(2)}}},\frac{{N^{\star}}}{{\Delta E}_{\max}}\right\}\;,

(168)

where

{\Delta E}_{\max}:=\max\left\{\left\rvert\mbox{Tr}[\hat{\rho}^{(\downarrow)}\hat{H}]-\mu\right\rvert,\left\rvert\mbox{Tr}[\hat{\rho}^{(\uparrow)}\hat{H}]-\mu\right\rvert\right\}\;.

(169)

and

\displaystyle N^{\star}:=\min\left\{\left\lfloor\sqrt{d}\right\rfloor,\left\lfloor(d/2\sqrt{3})^{4/7}\right\rfloor\right\}\;,

(170)

then the following inequality holds:

\displaystyle\left\lvert\mathcal{G}(t)-e^{\frac{1}{2}t^{2}\Sigma^{(2)}}\right\rvert\leq e^{2t^{2}\Sigma^{(2)}}-2t^{2}\Sigma^{(2)}-1

\displaystyle+\tfrac{32t^{2}\Sigma^{(2)}}{\sqrt{\pi}d}\left(e^{16t^{2}\Sigma^{(2)}}-1\right)\;

\displaystyle+\tfrac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi}}\left[\tfrac{e^{\left[-4\lvert t\rvert\sqrt{\Sigma^{(2)}/\eta_{\hat{H}}}\right]}}{\left(1-4\lvert t\rvert\sqrt{\eta_{\hat{H}}\Sigma^{(2)}}\right)^{\tfrac{1}{\eta_{\hat{H}}}}}-e^{16t^{2}\Sigma^{(2)}}\right]

\displaystyle+\tfrac{1}{\left\lceil N^{\star}/2\right\rceil!}\tfrac{t^{2}\Sigma^{(2)}}{1-\left(t^{2}\Sigma^{(2)}/N^{\star}\right)}+\tfrac{1}{N^{\star}!}\tfrac{\lvert t\rvert{\Delta E}_{\max}}{1-\left(\lvert t\rvert{\Delta E}_{\max}/N^{\star}\right)}

\displaystyle=\frac{32}{3}\sqrt{\eta_{\hat{H}}/\pi}\cdot\left\lvert t\sqrt{\Sigma^{(2)}}\right\rvert^{3}+\mathcal{O}\left(\eta_{\hat{H}}t^{4}\left(\sqrt{\Sigma^{(2)}}\right)^{2}\right)\;.

(171)

The error (
171
) is also valid for the characteristic function of the distribution, namely
\left\langle e^{-itE}\right\rangle

[
51
]
.

7.1
Initial considerations

We shall estimate the maximum distance between the moment generating functions with the series

\left\lvert\mathcal{G}(t)-e^{\frac{1}{2}t^{2}\Sigma^{(2)}}\right\rvert\leq\sum_{p=0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{p}}{p!}\left\lvert\Sigma^{(p)}-\Sigma_{G}^{(p)}\right\rvert.

(172)

All the estimations we made in Sec.
6
are valid only under the assumption
(
125
).
At some point, the momenta
\Sigma^{(p)}
will become much more different from
\Sigma^{(p)}_{G}
, because the distribution
P(E|\hat{\rho};\hat{H})
has a compact support, while the gaussian distribution
P_{G}^{(\mu,\Sigma^{(2)})}(E)
has infinite tails which become more and more important for the momenta of higher order.
For this reason, it is convenient to consider separately the terms of the sum on the r.h.s. of Eq. (
172
) with

p
not fulfilling (
125
) from the rest
writing

\left\lvert\mathcal{G}(t)-e^{\frac{1}{2}t^{2}\Sigma^{(2)}}\right\rvert\leq\Gamma^{*}_{<}+\Delta\Gamma_{<}+\Gamma_{>}\;,

(173)

where, given the cutoff (
170
),
we set

\displaystyle\Gamma_{>}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\sum_{p=N^{\star}}^{\infty}\frac{t^{p}}{p!}\left\lvert\Sigma^{(p)}-\Sigma_{G}^{(p)}\right\rvert\;,

(174)

and, invoking the decomposition (
130
),

\displaystyle\Gamma^{*}_{<}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\sum_{p=1}^{N^{\star}}\frac{t^{p}}{p!}\left\lvert\Sigma^{(p)*}-\Sigma_{G}^{(p)}\right\rvert=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p=4\\
p\text{ even}\end{subarray}}^{N^{\star}}\frac{t^{p}}{p!}\left\lvert\Sigma^{(p)*}-\Sigma_{G}^{(p)}\right\rvert\;,

\displaystyle\Delta\Gamma_{<}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\sum_{p=1}^{N^{\star}}\frac{t^{p}}{p!}\left\lvert\Delta\Sigma^{(p)}\right\rvert\;,

(176)

with
\Sigma^{(p)*}
and
\Delta\Sigma^{(p)})
defined as in
Eqs. (
131
) and (
132
) – notice that Eq. (
) was simplified by using the fact that
for
p
odd both
\Sigma^{(p)*}
and
\Sigma_{G}^{(p)}
are equal to zero, while by definition
\Sigma^{(2)*}=\Sigma_{G}^{(2)}
.

7.2
Bound on
\Gamma_{>}

When
p
is large enough the condition (
125
) brakes and
we can not use anymore the bounds of Sec.
6
. However since the spectrum of the random
variable
E
is limited as in Eq. (
14
), for all
p
we can write

\displaystyle\Sigma^{(p)}\leq{\Delta E}_{\max}^{p}\;,

(177)

with
{\Delta E}_{\max}^{p}
as defined in (
169
).

Therefore we can write

\left\lvert\Sigma^{(p)}-\Sigma^{(p)}_{G}\right\rvert=\left\lvert\Sigma^{(p)}\right\rvert\leq{\Delta E}_{\max}^{p}\;,

(178)

for
p
odd, and

\left\lvert\Sigma^{(p)}-\Sigma^{(p)}_{G}\right\rvert\leq\Sigma^{(p)}+\Sigma^{(p)}_{G}\leq{\Delta E}_{\max}^{p}+\Sigma^{(p)}_{G}\;,

(179)

for
p
even.
Exploiting these inequalities, when (
168
) holds we can estimate the quantity (
174
) as

\Gamma_{>}\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p=N^{\star}\\
p\text{ even}\end{subarray}}^{\infty}\frac{t^{p}(p-1)!!\left(\Sigma^{(2)}\right)^{p/2}}{p!}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p=N^{\star}\end{subarray}}^{\infty}\frac{t^{p}{\Delta E}_{\max}^{p}}{p!}\\
\leq\sum_{n=\left\lceil N^{\star}/2\right\rceil}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!}\left(\frac{t^{2}\Sigma^{(2)}}{2}\right)^{n}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p=N^{\star}\end{subarray}}^{\infty}\frac{t^{p}{\Delta E}_{\max}^{p}}{p!}\\
\leq\tfrac{\left(N^{\star}\right)^{\left\lceil N^{\star}/2\right\rceil}}{\left\lceil N^{\star}/2\right\rceil!}\sum_{n=\left\lceil N^{\star}/2\right\rceil}^{\infty}\left(\frac{t^{2}\Sigma^{(2)}}{N^{\star}}\right)^{n}\\
+\frac{\left(N^{\star}\right)^{N^{\star}}}{N^{\star}!}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p=N^{\star}\end{subarray}}^{\infty}\left(\frac{t{\Delta E}_{\max}}{N^{\star}}\right)^{p}\\
\leq\frac{1}{\left\lceil N^{\star}/2\right\rceil!}\frac{t^{2}\Sigma^{(2)}}{1-\left(t^{2}\Sigma^{(2)}/N^{\star}\right)}\\
+\frac{1}{N^{\star}!}\frac{t{\Delta E}_{\max}}{1-\left(t{\Delta E}_{\max}/N^{\star}\right)}\\
=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\left\lceil N^{\star}/2\right\rceil!}\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\left\lceil\sqrt{d}/2\right\rceil!}\right)\;.

(180)

7.3
Bound on
\Gamma^{*}_{<}

From Eq. (
138
) we can write

\displaystyle\Gamma^{*}_{<}\leq\overline{\Gamma}^{*}_{<}+\underline{\Gamma}^{*}_{<}\;,

(181)

where

\displaystyle\overline{\Gamma}^{*}_{<}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p=4\\
p\text{ even}\end{subarray}}^{N^{\star}}\frac{t^{p}}{p!}\left|\overline{\Sigma}^{(p)*}-\Sigma_{G}^{(p)}\right|\;,

(182)

\displaystyle\underline{\Gamma}_{<}^{*}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p=4\\
p\text{ even}\end{subarray}}^{N^{\star}}\frac{t^{p}}{p!}|\underline{\Sigma}^{(p)*}|\;,

(183)

and with
\overline{\Sigma}^{(p)*}
and
\underline{\Sigma}^{(p)*}
as defined in (
136
) and (
).

First we provide a bound for the term
\overline{\Gamma}^{*}_{<}
. Retrieving from (
143
) the bound on
\left|\overline{\Sigma}^{(p)*}-\Sigma_{G}^{(p)}\right|
, and using the expression (
124
) for the variance
\Sigma^{(2)}
, we have

\displaystyle\overline{\Gamma}^{*}_{<}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p=4\\
p\text{ even}\end{subarray}}^{\infty}\tfrac{(p-1)!!}{p!}\left(1-\tfrac{6p^{7/2}}{d^{2}}\right)^{-1}\tfrac{6p^{7/2}t^{p}}{d^{2}}\left(\Sigma^{(2)}\right)^{p/2}

(184)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p=4\\
p\text{ even}\end{subarray}}^{\infty}\frac{(p-1)!!}{p!}t^{p}\left(\Sigma^{(2)}\right)^{p/2}\leq\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\frac{2^{n}t^{2n}}{n!}\left(\Sigma^{(2)}\right)^{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp\left[2t^{2}\Sigma^{(2)}\right]-2t^{2}\Sigma^{(2)}-1

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}\left(t^{4}\left(\Sigma^{(2)}\right)^{2}\right)\;,

in the second inequality we used the fact that
the condition
p\leq N^{\star}\leq\left(d/2\sqrt{3}\right)^{4/7}
implies

\tfrac{6p^{7/2}}{d^{2}}\left(1-\tfrac{6p^{7/2}}{d^{2}}\right)^{-1}\leq 1\;.

(185)

Regarding
\underline{\Gamma}^{*}_{<}
, recalling equation (
155
), and expliciting the definitions (
156
) and (
123
) of the quantities
\underline{f}^{*}(d,p)
and
{\mathbf{G}}_{p}
we have that

\displaystyle\left|\underline{\Sigma}^{(p)*}\right|\leq\tfrac{(p-1)!!}{(d^{2}-1)^{p/2}}\|\Delta\hat{H}\|_{2}^{p}\;\|\Delta\hat{\rho}\|_{2}^{p}\left(1-\tfrac{6p^{7/2}}{d^{2}}\right)^{-1}\;\tfrac{p^{2}Cat_{p}}{d}\;,

\displaystyle=(p-1)!!\left(1-\tfrac{6p^{7/2}}{d^{2}}\right)^{-1}\left(\Sigma^{(2)}\right)^{p/2}

from which, using the bound (
128
) on Catalan numbers we can write

\displaystyle\underline{\Gamma}_{<}^{*}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p=4\\
p\text{ even}\end{subarray}}^{N^{\star}}\tfrac{(p-1)!!}{p!}\left(1-\tfrac{6p^{7/2}}{d^{2}}\right)^{-1}\;\tfrac{(4t)^{p}\sqrt{p}}{\sqrt{\pi}d}\left(\Sigma^{(2)}\right)^{p/2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\sum_{n=2}^{d}\frac{\left(2n-1\right)!!}{(2n)!}\left(16t^{2}\Sigma^{(2)}\right)^{n}\frac{\sqrt{2n}}{\sqrt{\pi}d}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\frac{\left(2n-1\right)!!}{(2n)!}\left(16t^{2}\Sigma^{(2)}\right)^{n}\frac{{n}}{\sqrt{\pi}d}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}d}\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\frac{2n}{n!}\left(16t^{2}\Sigma^{(2)}\right)^{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}d}\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\frac{t}{n!}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(16t^{2}\Sigma^{(2)}\right)^{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{t}{\sqrt{\pi}d}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!}\left(16t^{2}\Sigma^{(2)}\right)^{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{t}{\sqrt{\pi}d}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left[\exp\left(16t^{2}\Sigma^{(2)}\right)-16t^{2}\Sigma^{(2)}-1\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{32t^{2}\Sigma^{(2)}}{\sqrt{\pi}d}\left[\exp\left(16t^{2}\Sigma^{(2)}\right)-1\right]\;,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}\left(t^{4}\left(\Sigma^{(2)}\right)^{2}d^{-1}\right)\;,

where in the second inequality we let
n=p/2
and used the fact that, since
p\leq N^{\star}\leq\left(d/2\sqrt{3}\right)^{4/7}
,

\left(1-\tfrac{6p^{7/2}}{d^{2}}\right)^{-1}\leq 2\;,

(188)

and in the third inequality we used
\sqrt{2n}\leq n
.

7.4
Bound on
\Delta\Gamma_{<}

From the definitions (
176
) and (
132
), we can write the term
\Delta\Gamma_{<}
as

\displaystyle\Delta\Gamma_{<}=\sum_{p=1}^{\infty}\frac{t^{p}}{p!}\sum_{\sigma\in S_{p}^{D}}\sum_{\tau\in S^{D}_{p}/S^{D*}_{p}}C_{[\sigma\tau^{-1}]}\;\;\Delta\rho{[\sigma]}\;\Delta H{[\tau]}\;.

First we use (
148
), as well as
\Delta\rho{[\sigma\tau]}\leq\|\Delta\hat{\rho}\|^{p}_{2}
, to write

\displaystyle\left\lvert\Delta\Gamma_{<}\right\rvert\leq\sum_{p=3}^{\infty}\frac{t^{p}}{p!}\|\Delta\rho\|^{p}_{2}\left(\sum_{\sigma\in S_{p}^{D}}\left\lvert C_{[\sigma]}\right\rvert\right)\left(\sum_{\tau\in S^{D}_{p}/S^{D*}_{p}}\;\Delta H{[\tau]}\right)

\displaystyle<\sum_{p=3}^{\infty}\frac{t^{p}\|\Delta\rho\|^{p}_{2}}{p!}\left(1-\tfrac{6p^{7/2}}{d^{2}}\right)^{-1}\tfrac{Cat_{p}(d+p-1)!}{d^{2p}(d-1)!}\sum_{\tau\in S^{D}_{p}/S^{D*}_{p}}H{[\tau]}

\displaystyle=\sum_{p=3}^{\infty}\frac{t^{p}}{p!}\left(1-\tfrac{6p^{7/2}}{d^{2}}\right)^{-1}\tfrac{Cat_{p}(d+p-1)!}{d^{2p}(d-1)!}\sum_{k=\lfloor p/2\rfloor+1}^{p}\sum_{\tau\in S^{D}_{p}(k)}H{[\tau]}\;,

(190)

where in thethird line we introduced the sets

\displaystyle S^{D}_{p}(k):=S^{D}_{p}\cap S_{p}(k)\;,

(191)

and noticed that
S^{D}_{p}(k)=\varnothing
when
k<\lfloor p/2\rfloor+1
and that, when
p
is even,
S^{D}_{p}(p/2)=S^{D*}_{p}
(recall also that
S^{D*}_{p}=\varnothing
in the case of odd
p
).

Replacing the bounds (
128
) and (
154
) in (
190
), we have

\displaystyle\left\lvert\Delta\Gamma_{<}\right\rvert\leq\sum_{p=3}^{\infty}\frac{4^{p}t^{p}\|\Delta\hat{\rho}\|^{p}_{2}}{\sqrt{\pi}d^{p}p^{3/2}p!}\left(1-\tfrac{6p^{7/2}}{d^{2}}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle\times\left(1+\frac{p^{2}}{d}\right)\sum_{k=\lfloor p/2\rfloor+1}^{p}\sum_{\tau\in S^{D}_{p}(k)}\eta_{\hat{H}}^{k-p/2}\|\Delta\hat{H}\|^{p}_{2}\;.

(192)

Now, in order to simplify the calculations, we will use the inequality (
188
) to infer that, since
4\leq p\leq\sqrt{d}
,

p^{-3/2}\left(1-\frac{6p^{7/2}}{d^{2}}\right)^{-1}\left(1+\frac{p^{2}}{d}\right)<4p^{-3/2}\leq\frac{1}{2}\;.

(193)

With the help of (
193
), and noticing from (
124
) that
\|\Delta\hat{H}\|_{2}\|\Delta\hat{\rho}\|_{2}/d<\sqrt{\Sigma^{(2)}}
, we can turn (
192
) into

\displaystyle\left\lvert\Delta\Gamma_{<}\right\rvert\leq\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi}}\sum_{p=3}^{\infty}\frac{1}{p!}\left(\tfrac{4t\|\Delta\hat{H}\|_{2}\|\Delta\hat{\rho}\|_{2}}{d}\right)^{p}

\displaystyle\times\sum_{k=\lfloor p/2\rfloor+1}^{p}\sum_{\tau\in S^{D}_{p}(k)}\eta_{\hat{H}}^{k-p/2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi}}\sum_{p=3}^{\infty}\frac{1}{p!}\left(4t\sqrt{\Sigma^{(2)}}\right)^{p}\sum_{k=\lfloor p/2\rfloor+1}^{p}\sum_{\tau\in S^{D}_{p}(k)}\eta_{\hat{H}}^{k-p/2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi}}\sum_{p=3}^{\infty}\frac{1}{p!}\left(4t\sqrt{\eta_{\hat{H}}\Sigma^{(2)}}\right)^{p}\sum_{k=\lfloor p/2\rfloor+1}^{p}\mathrm{d}_{p,k}\eta_{\hat{H}}^{k-p}\;,

(194)

where we called
\mathrm{d}_{p,n}
the number of elements in the set
S^{D}_{p}(k)
.

We do not need the explicit values of
\mathrm{d}_{p,k}
. They are implicitly given by the coefficients in the expansion of the exponential generating function
[
52
]

\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\sum_{p=i}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}_{p,p-i}c^{i}\frac{x^{p}}{p!}=\frac{e^{-cx}}{(1-x)^{c}}\;.

(195)

In wiev of (
194
), we want to keep in the sum only the derangements with less than
p/2
cycles (
\tau\not\in[2^{p/2}]
), whose generating functions is

\sum_{p=2}^{\infty}\sum_{k=\lfloor p/2\rfloor-1}^{p}\mathrm{d}_{p,k}c^{p-k}\frac{x^{p}}{p!}=\sum_{p=2}^{\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{\lceil p/2\rceil-1}\mathrm{d}_{p,p-i}c^{i}\tfrac{x^{p}}{p!}\\
=\sum_{p=0}^{\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor p/2\rfloor}\mathrm{d}_{p,p-i}c^{i}\frac{x^{p}}{p!}-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\text{ even}\end{subarray}}^{\infty}\tfrac{(p-1)!!}{p!}c^{p/2}x^{p}\\
=\frac{e^{-cx}}{(1-x)^{c}}-e^{\frac{cx^{2}}{2}}=\frac{cx^{3}}{3}+\mathcal{O}\left(cx^{4}\right)\;.

(196)

Using (

\displaystyle\left\lvert\Delta\Gamma_{<}\right\rvert\leq\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi}}\left[\frac{e^{\left[-4\lvert t\rvert\sqrt{\Sigma^{(2)}/\eta_{\hat{H}}}\right]}}{\left(1-4\lvert t\rvert\sqrt{\eta_{\hat{H}}\Sigma^{(2)}}\right)^{\tfrac{1}{\eta_{\hat{H}}}}}-e^{16t^{2}\Sigma^{(2)}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{32}{3}\sqrt{\eta_{\hat{H}}/\pi}\cdot\left\lvert t\sqrt{\Sigma^{(2)}}\right\rvert^{3}+\mathcal{O}\left(\eta_{\hat{H}}t^{4}\left(\sqrt{\Sigma^{(2)}}\right)^{2}\right)\;.

(197)

8
Conclusions

We derived a bound for the distance between the energy distribution in the unitary orbit of a quantum state and the normal distribution with the same variance, showing that for large dimensions of the Hilbert space the difference in the charachteristic function is suppressed as
d^{-3}
.
We have also characterised the individual moments of the distribution: these result apply also to unitary
t
-design, which mimic the uniform distribution of unitary transformations up to the
t
-th moments.
Our findings, therefore, are suitable to be be employed for certifying that an unitary
t
-design behaves as expected.
Since the results do not depend on the specific form of the Hamiltonian (but only on a very general hypotesis on its eigenvalues), they can be applied to every observable that can be measured on the system.

The validity of our results is limited to the specific (but relevant in the field of quantum computing) case in which the evolution of the system is described by a random unitary matrices drawn from the Haar-uniform ensemble (also known as
Circular Unitary Ensemble

[
53
,
54
]
).
In our opinion, it would be interesting to perform a similar analysis on the work distribution in the case in which the time evolution of the system is given by an Hamiltonian drawn from the Wigner-Dyson Gaussian Unitary Ensemble
[
55
,
56
]
, which describes quantum chaotic systems lacking time-reversal symmetry
[
57
,
58
]
, or from the Gaussian Orthogonal or the Gaussian Symplectic Ensembles, which model the evolution quantum chaotic systems in presence time-reversal symmetry
[
58
]
. The combinatorical tools necessary for this analogous task have been in part already developed
[
59
]
, but significant more work may be required.

Another possible future developement of this work is generalizing it to Hilbert space of infinite dimension. An immediate difficulty is that the Haar measure on the unitary group of infinite dimension is not well-defined
[
60
]
. However, a class of states of particular interest are the gaussian states on systems charachterised by a
N
-modes quadratic bosonic Hamiltonian
[
61
]
: in this setting, it could be of some interest to study the distribution of their energy over the group of symplectic transformations
[
62
]
.
