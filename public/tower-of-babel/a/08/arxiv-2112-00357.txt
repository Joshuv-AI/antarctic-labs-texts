Negation-Free Definitions of Paraconsistency
Sankha S. Basu Sayantan Roy

Abstract
Paraconsistency is commonly defined and/or characterized as the failure of a principle of explosion. The various standard forms of explosion involve one or more logical operators or connectives, among which the negation operator is the most frequent. In this article, we ask whether a negation operator is essential for describing paraconsistency. In other words, is it possible to describe a notion of paraconsistency that is independent of connectives? We present two such notions of negation-free paraconsistency, one that is completely independent of connectives and another that uses a conjunction-like binary connective that we call ‘fusion’. We also derive a notion of ‘quasi-negation’ from the former, and investigate its properties.



Keywords:

Paraconsistency, Negation, Negation-free paraconsistency, Quasi-negation

1
Introduction

Paraconsistency is characterized, and sometimes even defined, as the failure of the so-called principle of
‘explosion’
. The latter, also known as
ex contradictione sequitur quodlibet
or ECQ, is most often expressed as follows. For any formula
\alpha
,

\{\alpha,\neg\alpha\}\vdash\beta,\,\hbox{ or equivalently }\,C_{\vdash}(\{\alpha,\neg\alpha\})=\mathcal{L},

where
\neg
is a negation operator,
\beta
is an arbitrary element of the set of all formulas
\mathcal{L}
,
\vdash
is the logical consequence relation
\subseteq\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{L})\times\mathcal{L}
, and
C_{\vdash}
is its corresponding consequence operator from
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{L})
to
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{L})
. A logic
\langle\mathcal{L},\vdash\rangle
is thus said to be paraconsistent when the above rule fails in it.

There are, however, alternatives to the above standard understanding of explosion and the paraconsistency defined as the denial of that. A variety of these alternative notions are discussed in
[
10
]
. The following are some of the other incarnations of the principle of explosion.

1.

For each formula
\alpha
,
\alpha\land\neg\alpha\vdash\beta
, where
\land
denotes conjunction and
\beta
is any formula. This rule has been called rECQ in
[
10
]
and
\land
-ECQ in
[
2
]
. We will use the latter name. Paraconsistency can also be described as the failure of this rule. This kind of paraconsistency will be referred to as
\land
-paraconsistency in this article. As remarked in both these articles, in any logic
\langle\mathcal{L},\vdash\rangle
with the usual introduction and elimination rules for
\land
, viz.,

\begin{array}[]{ll}\land I.&\{\alpha,\beta\}\vdash\alpha\land\beta,\\
\land E.&\alpha\land\beta\vdash\alpha,\,\alpha\land\beta\vdash\beta,\end{array}

failure of ECQ is equivalent to the failure of
\land
-ECQ. Hence a logic possessing
\land I
and
\land E
is paraconsistent iff it is
\land
-paraconsistent. However, there are logics, such as the non-adjunctive logics, where one or both of the conjunction rules are not available and in these cases, we need to clearly indicate the sense in which such logics are paraconsistent.

2.

\vdash(\alpha\land\neg\alpha)\longrightarrow\beta
, where
\alpha,\beta
are any two formulas. This rule is called aECQ in
[
10
]
.

3.

\vdash\neg\alpha\longrightarrow(\alpha\longrightarrow\beta)
and
\vdash\alpha\longrightarrow(\neg\alpha\longrightarrow\beta)
, where
\alpha,\beta
are any two formulas. These are called aEFQ1 and aEFQ2 in
[
10
]
. Here EFQ stands for
ex falso quodlibet
, another name for ECQ.

It may be noted that in logics with modus ponens and the Deduction theorem, aECQ is equivalent to
\land
-ECQ and aEFQ1, aEFQ2 are equivalent to ECQ.

We observe that in each of the above principles of explosion and hence the paraconsistency arising out of the failure of it, the role of the negation operator is of prime importance. This is not surprising at all considering that paraconsistent logics aim to enable one to draw reasonable conclusions from a contradiction. A contradiction is after all an unfeasible combination of two pieces of information, hence the simplest way to describe a contradiction is through the presence of a statement and its negation. There are, however, other pairs of opposing statements possible, viz.,
\alpha\&\beta
and
\neg\alpha
, where
\alpha,\beta
are formulas and
\&
is a logical operator that has some conjunction-like properties (but may not have all the properties of the classical conjunction). In a situation like this, we may be able to say that the set
\{\alpha\&\beta,\neg\alpha\}
is contradictory or inconsistent but that is only if we can derive from this set
\gamma,\neg\gamma
for some formula
\gamma
. Even after that to prove that this set is explosive, we will need to use the Tarskian transitivity rule. What if one or more of these intermediary expedients is not available? Should the two statements be not regarded as opposing?

One might also note from the various explosion inducing principles that there are multiple ways of bringing about a contradiction or fusing together opposing statements. While in ECQ, the job is done by a meta-linguistic comma, the other principles use one or more object-language connectives. Hence apart from the notion of opposing statements, the method of fusion also plays a vital role in paraconsistency.

There is, however, one notion of paraconsistency that is slightly different. This depends on the presence of a falsity constant in the language and its properties. A falsity constant is usually denoted by
\bot
or
F
. We will use the former. The concepts of
F
-consistency and
F
-paraconsistency were defined in
[
10
]
. We call these
\bot
-consistency and
\bot
-paraconsistency, respectively, and adapt the definitions with suitable changes of notation, below.

Definition 1.1
.

Suppose
\langle\mathcal{L},\vdash\rangle
is a logic with a
falsity constant

\bot\in\mathcal{L}
. A set
\Gamma\subseteq\mathcal{L}
is called
\bot
-inconsistent
if
\bot\in\Gamma
, it is called
\bot
-consistent
otherwise.

A logic
\mathcal{S}=\langle\mathcal{L},\vdash\rangle
is called
\bot
-paraconsistent
if
\bot\vdash\alpha
for all
\alpha\in\mathcal{L}
is not a rule of
\mathcal{S}
, i.e. there is an
\alpha\in\mathcal{L}
such that
\bot\nvdash\alpha
.

As discussed in
[
10
]
, in general
\bot
(referred to as “Das Absurde” there) is assumed to be equivalent to some contradiction or to all of them, or to the negation of any theorem. In these cases,
\bot
-paraconsistency is essentially the same as paraconsistency. There are, however, other understandings of
\bot
, for example, it could be equivalent to the conjunction of the negation of each theorem as in minimal intuitionistic logic, but not to any contradiction. In this case,
\bot
-paraconsistency is different from paraconsistency. However, these understandings of
\bot
make the notion of
\bot
-paraconsistency inherently dependent on negation. There are still other cases where
\bot
is not definitionally eliminable (see
[
11
]
) and in those cases although this notion of paraconsistency, also different from that of paraconsistency, is independent of negation, it requires the presence of a logical operator (
\bot
can be seen as a nullary operator) in the language.

Our question here is the following. Is it possible to describe paraconsistency independently of the language or connectives? The motivation for this question came about from a different line of thought, viz., that of universal logic
[
3
,
4
]
. The fundamental notion in universal logic is that of a logical structure, which is a pair of the form
\langle\mathcal{L},\vdash\rangle
, where
\mathcal{L}
is a set and
\vdash\,\subseteq\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{L})\times\mathcal{L}
. A logical structure is thus defined without regard to connectives and the set
\mathcal{L}
need not be a formula algebra as in a logic. The above question can thus be rephrased as follows. Can we define paraconsistency in the context of universal logic, i.e. is it possible to define something like a
paraconsistent logical structure
? In this paper we have not focused on the distinction between a logic and a logical structure and have used the former term throughout. However, many of the assertions apply equally well to logical structures.

2
Negation-free paraconsistency: first attempt

We will assume that any logic, unless otherwise stated, satisfies the Tarskian rules of reflexivity, transitivity, and monotonicity. However, it will be pointed out at places if a result can be lifted to certain non-Tarskian logics as well.

The principle of explosion is first generalized as follows. For each
\alpha\in\mathcal{L}
in a logic
\langle\mathcal{L},\vdash\rangle
, there exists
\beta\in\mathcal{L}
such that

\{\alpha,\beta\}\vdash\gamma,\hbox{ where }\gamma\in\mathcal{L}\hbox{ is arbitrary}.

We call this rule gECQ. In other words, gECQ says that for each
\alpha\in\mathcal{L}
, there exists
\beta\in\mathcal{L}
such that
C_{\vdash}(\{\alpha,\beta\})=\mathcal{L}
. Then our first attempt at defining paraconsistency without referring to a negation operator can be described as follows.

Definition 2.1
.

A logic
\langle\mathcal{L},\vdash\rangle
is
NF
-paraconsistent
(
NF
stands for negation-free) if gECQ fails in it, i.e. there exists an
\alpha\in\mathcal{L}
such that for every
\beta\in\mathcal{L}
,
C_{\vdash}(\{\alpha,\beta\})\neq\mathcal{L}
.

Theorem 2.2
.

Suppose
\langle\mathcal{L},\vdash\rangle
is an
NF
-paraconsistent logic with a negation operator
\neg
. Then it is also paraconsistent with respect to
\neg
.

Proof.

Suppose
\langle\mathcal{L},\vdash\rangle
is
NF
-paraconsistent. Then there exists
\alpha\in\mathcal{L}
such that for all
\beta\in\mathcal{L}
,
C_{\vdash}(\{\alpha,\beta\})\neq\mathcal{L}
. Then, in particular,
C_{\vdash}(\{\alpha,\neg\alpha\})\neq\mathcal{L}
. Hence
\langle\mathcal{L},\vdash\rangle
is paraconsistent.
∎

It is, however, possible for a logic to be paraconsistent but not
NF
-paraconsistent (see Examples
2.5
and
2.6
below).

Theorem 2.3
.

Suppose
\langle\mathcal{L},\vdash\rangle
is a logic with a falsity constant
\bot
. If
\langle\mathcal{L},\vdash\rangle
is
NF
-paraconsistent then it is also
\bot
-paraconsistent.

Proof.

Suppose
\langle\mathcal{L},\vdash\rangle
is not
\bot
-paraconsistent. So,
C_{\vdash}(\{\bot\})=\mathcal{L}
. Now, let
\alpha\in\mathcal{L}
. Then
C_{\vdash}(\{\alpha,\bot\})=\mathcal{L}
by monotonicity. Thus for every
\alpha\in\mathcal{L}
, there exists a
\beta\in\mathcal{L}
, viz.,
\bot
such that
C_{\vdash}(\{\alpha,\beta\})=\mathcal{L}
. Hence
\langle\mathcal{L},\vdash\rangle
is not
NF
-paraconsistent.
∎

Remark 2.4
.

To prove the above theorem, we used only the Tarskian monotonicity condition. Thus the result carries through for certain non-Tarskian logics as well, as long as they are monotone.

Example 2.5
.

Paraconsistent weak Kleene logic (PWK), axiomatized in
[
5
]
, and intuitionistic paraconsistent weak Kleene logic, axiomatized in
[
2
]
, have
0\longrightarrow\alpha
as an axiom, where 0 is the falsity constant,
\longrightarrow
denotes implication and
\alpha
is any formula. Both PWK and IPWK have a restricted version of modus ponens as a valid rule of inference. That is enough to guarantee explosion from 0. Hence they are not
\bot
-paraconsistent. Thus by Theorem
2.3
, PWK and IPWK are not
NF
-paraconsistent, although they are paraconsistent.

Example 2.6
.

Another example of a paraconsistent logic that is not
NF
-paraconsistent is minimal logic. Minimal logic is not
\bot
-paraconsistent, as noted in
[
10
]
, and hence not
NF
-paraconsistent.

We now turn to some examples of
NF
-paraconsistent logics.

Example 2.7
.

Suppose
\mathcal{S}=\langle\mathcal{L},\vdash\rangle
is a logic where
\vdash
is defined as follows. For any
\Gamma\cup\{\alpha\}\subseteq\mathcal{L}
,

\Gamma\vdash\alpha
iff
\alpha\in\Gamma
. Such a logic, which may be called a
purely reflexive
logic, is clearly
NF
-paraconsistent as long as
\mathcal{L}
has at least three distinct elements.

It may be noted that such a purely reflexive logic is also monotone and transitive, and hence Tarskian.

Example 2.8
.

Suppose
(\mathcal{L},\leq)
is a partially ordered set with three distinct elements,
0,1,2
, where
0\leq 1
and 2 is incomparable to both 0 and 1. This can be visualized as follows.
0
2
1

We now define
\langle\mathcal{L},\vdash\rangle
as follows. For any
\Gamma\cup\{\alpha\}\subseteq\mathcal{L}
,
\Gamma\vdash\alpha
iff for all
\gamma\in\Gamma
,
\gamma\leq\alpha
. It is then easy to check that
\{1,0\}\not\vdash 0,2
and
\{1,2\}\not\vdash 0,1,2
. Thus for all
\beta\in\mathcal{L}
,
C_{\vdash}(\{1,\beta\})\neq\mathcal{L}
. Hence
\langle\mathcal{L},\vdash\rangle
is
NF
-paraconsistent.

A dual of
\langle\mathcal{L},\vdash\rangle
,
\langle\mathcal{L},\vdash^{\prime}\rangle
, where for any
\Gamma\cup\{\alpha\}\subseteq\mathcal{L}
,
\Gamma\vdash^{\prime}\alpha
iff for all
\gamma\in\Gamma
,
\alpha\leq\gamma
is also
NF
-paraconsistent. This is because
\{0,1\}\not\vdash^{\prime}1,2
and
\{0,2\}\not\vdash^{\prime}0,1,2
and hence for all
\beta\in\mathcal{L}
,
C_{\vdash^{\prime}}(\{0,\beta\})\neq\mathcal{L}
.

It may be noted that both systems are non-reflexive and non-monotonic, and hence non-Tarskian.

Example 2.9
.

This example is also based on a poset
(\mathcal{L},\leq)
with three elements
0,1,2
as in the previous example but in this case we take
0,1\leq 2
while 0 and 1 are incomparable.
This can be visualized as follows.
0
1
2

We take the same definition for
\vdash
as in Example
2.8
. Then
\{0,2\}\not\vdash 0,1
and
\{1,2\}\not\vdash 0,1
. Thus for all
\beta\in\mathcal{L}
,
C_{\vdash}(\{2,\beta\})\neq\mathcal{L}
.

Working with the dual poset
(\mathcal{L},\leq)
, where
2\leq 0,1
while

are incomparable, and taking
\vdash^{\prime}
as defined in Example

The fact that
\langle\mathcal{L},\vdash^{\prime}\rangle
is

\{0,2\}\not\vdash^{\prime}0,1
and
\{1,2\}\not\vdash^{\prime}0,1
.

These systems are also non-reflexive and non-monotonic, and hence non-Tarskian.

Remark 2.10
.

The above examples can be generalized in the following way. Let
(\mathcal{L},\leq)
be a poset with two incomparable elements
\alpha,\beta
(i.e.
\alpha\not\leq\beta
and
\beta\not\leq\alpha
). Then defining
\langle\mathcal{L},\vdash\rangle
as in the above examples, we see that for all
\gamma\in\mathcal{L}
,
\{\alpha,\gamma\}\not\vdash\beta
and
\{\beta,\gamma\}\not\vdash\alpha
. Thus for all
\gamma\in\mathcal{L}
,
C_{\vdash}(\{\alpha,\gamma\})\neq\mathcal{L}
and
C_{\vdash}(\{\beta,\gamma\})\neq\mathcal{L}
. Hence
\langle\mathcal{L},\vdash\rangle
is
NF
-paraconsistent.

It can similarly be argued that
\langle\mathcal{L},\vdash^{\prime}\rangle
, where
\vdash^{\prime}
is defined as in the above examples, is also
NF
-paraconsistent.

Moreover, these systems are all non-reflexive and non-monotonic, and hence non-Tarskian.

Continuing along the same lines, let
\mathcal{L}
be any set,
(P\leq)
be any poset, and
\emptyset\subsetneq\mathcal{V}\subseteq P^{\mathcal{L}}
be a set of functions (these may be thought of as
valuation
functions). We can now define a (semantic) consequence relation
\models_{\mathcal{V}}\subseteq\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{L})\times\mathcal{L}
as follows. For all
\Gamma\cup\{\alpha\}\subseteq\mathcal{L}
,

\Gamma\models_{\mathcal{V}}\alpha\quad\hbox{ iff }\quad\hbox{ for all }v\in\mathcal{V},\,v(\beta)\leq v(\alpha)\hbox{ for every }\beta\in\Gamma.

We then have the following theorem.

Theorem 2.11
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a non-empty set,
(P,\leq)
be a poset with at least two incomparable elements, and
\mathcal{V},\models_{\mathcal{V}}
be as above. If there exists a
\widehat{v}\in\mathcal{V}
such that
\widehat{v}(\gamma)
and
\widehat{v}(\delta)
are incomparable for some
\gamma,\delta\in\mathcal{L}
, then
\langle\mathcal{L},\models_{\mathcal{V}}\rangle
is
NF
-paraconsistent.

Proof.

Suppose
\widehat{v}\in\mathcal{V}
and
\gamma,\delta\in\mathcal{L}
such that
\widehat{v}(\gamma)
and
\widehat{v}(\delta)
are incomparable. Let
\beta\in\mathcal{L}
. Then we note that
\{\gamma,\beta\}\not\models_{\mathcal{V}}\delta
since
\widehat{v}(\gamma)\not\leq\widehat{v}(\delta)
. Thus
C_{\models_{\mathcal{V}}}(\{\gamma,\beta\})\neq\mathcal{L}
for all
\beta\in\mathcal{L}
. Hence
\langle\mathcal{L},\models_{\mathcal{V}}\rangle
is
NF
-paraconsistent.
∎

Remark 2.12
.

At the cost of a slight digression, it may be pointed out here that the style of defining a semantic consequence relation using the order relation if and when present in the semantic structures, as opposed to using a set of designated elements or logical matrices, is not novel. While for certain logics, such as classical propositional logic, the two approaches lead to the same consequence relation, such is not the case for other logics. A comparison between the two approaches has been discussed in
[
12
]
. The consequence relation above is, however, slightly different from the one defined using the order relation in the semantic structure in
[
12
]
. There
\Gamma\models_{\mathcal{V}}\alpha
iff for all
v\in\mathcal{V}
,
v(\Gamma)\leq v(\alpha)
, where
\Gamma\cup\{\alpha\}\subseteq\mathcal{L}
with
\mathcal{L},\mathcal{V}
as in Theorem
2.11
, and
v(\Gamma)
denotes some kind of a composition of the values in
\{v(\beta)\mid\,\beta\in\Gamma\}
, the composition being an operation in the semantic structure. Due to this, while in our case, every
\alpha\in\mathcal{L}
is a
theorem
, i.e.
\emptyset\models_{\mathcal{V}}\alpha
, the logics in
[
12
]
equipped with such consequence relations are without any theorems.

Our next example is taken, with some change of notation, from
[
6
]
where this is used in a different context.

Example 2.13
.

Let
\mathcal{L}=\mathbb{R}
, the set of real numbers.
\vdash\,\subseteq\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{L})\times\mathcal{L}
is then defined as follows. For any
\Gamma\cup\{\alpha\}\subseteq\mathcal{L}
,

\Gamma\vdash\alpha\quad\hbox{iff}\quad\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\alpha\in\Gamma,\,\hbox{or}\\
\alpha=\frac{1}{n}\,\hbox{ for some natural number }\,n\in\mathbb{N},\,n\geq 1,\,\hbox{or}\\
\hbox{there is a sequence }\,(\alpha_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\,\hbox{ in }\,\Gamma\,\hbox{ that converges to }\,\alpha.\end{array}\right.

Now, it is easy to see that for any
\alpha,\beta,\gamma\in\mathcal{L}
with
\gamma\neq\alpha,\beta
and
\gamma\neq\frac{1}{n}
for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\{\alpha,\beta\}\not\vdash\gamma
. Thus
\langle\mathcal{L},\vdash\rangle
is
NF
-paraconsistent.

The system
\langle\mathcal{L},\vdash\rangle
is reflexive and monotone but not transitive. To see the latter, one may note that for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\vdash\frac{1}{n}
and
\left\{\frac{1}{n}\mid\,n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}\vdash 0
but
\not\vdash 0
. This phenomenon is also noted in
[
6
]
.

Our next set of examples of
NF
-paraconsistent logics use the notion of
q
-consequence (
quasi
-consequence) introduced in
[
8
]
to produce a reasoning scheme in which “rejection” and “acceptance” of a proposition are not necessarily reciprocal of each other. Before proceeding to show that logics equipped with certain
q
-consequence operators are
NF
-paraconsistent, we describe the concept of a
q
-consequence operator below.

Definition 2.14
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a set and
W:\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{L})\to\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{L})
.
W
is called a
q
-consequence operator
on
\mathcal{L}
if the following conditions hold.

(i)

For all
\Gamma\cup\Sigma\subseteq\mathcal{L}
,
\Gamma\subseteq\Sigma
implies that
W(\Gamma)\subseteq W(\Sigma)
.

(ii)

For all
\Gamma\subseteq\mathcal{L}
,
W(\Gamma\cup W(\Gamma))=W(\Gamma)
.

Given such a
q
-consequence operator
W
on
\mathcal{L}
, the pair
\langle\mathcal{L},\vdash^{W}\rangle
is called the logic induced by
W
, where
\vdash^{W}\,\subseteq\,\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{L})\times\mathcal{L}
is defined as follows. For any
\Gamma\cup\{\alpha\}\subseteq\mathcal{L}
,
\Gamma\vdash^{W}\alpha
iff
\alpha\in W(\Gamma)
.

Theorem 2.15
.

Let
\langle\mathcal{L},\vdash^{W}\rangle
be the logic induced by a
q
-consequence operator
W
on the set
\mathcal{L}
. If
W(\mathcal{L})\subsetneq\mathcal{L}
, then
\langle\mathcal{L},\vdash^{W}\rangle
is
NF
-paraconsistent.

Proof.

We note that since
W(\mathcal{L})\subsetneq\mathcal{L}
, there exists an
\alpha\in\mathcal{L}
such that
\alpha\notin W(\mathcal{L})
, i.e.
\mathcal{L}\not\vdash^{W}\alpha
. Then, by condition (i) of Definition
2.14
, every
\Gamma\subseteq\mathcal{L}
is non-trivial. Thus in particular, for any
\beta,\gamma\in\mathcal{L}
,
C_{\vdash^{W}}(\{\beta,\gamma\})\neq\mathcal{L}
. Hence
\langle\mathcal{L},\vdash^{W}\rangle
is
NF
-paraconsistent.
∎

2.1
A quasi-negation

Suppose
\mathcal{S}=\langle\mathcal{L},\vdash\rangle
is a logic. For any
\alpha,\beta\in\mathcal{L}
, if
C_{\vdash}(\{\alpha,\beta\})=\mathcal{L}
, then
\beta
acts like a negation of
\alpha
. This can be justified by noting that in case
\mathcal{S}
has a negation operator,
\neg
, and is not paraconsistent, i.e. ECQ holds, then
\neg\alpha
is indeed such a
\beta
. However, in general, such a
\beta
need not be unique. For example, in case of classical propositional logic (CPC) or intuitionistic propositional logic (IPC), we have
C_{\vdash}(\{\alpha,\alpha\longrightarrow\neg\alpha\})=\mathcal{L}
. Thus
\alpha\longrightarrow\neg\alpha
is also an instance of such a
\beta
. Motivated by this, we now have the following definition.

Definition 2.16
.

Suppose
\mathcal{S}=\langle\mathcal{L},\vdash\rangle
is a logic and
\alpha\in\mathcal{L}
. Then a
quasi-negation
of
\alpha
is any
\beta\in\mathcal{L}
such that
C_{\vdash}(\{\alpha,\beta\})=\mathcal{L}
. We denote a quasi-negation of
\alpha
by
\sim\!\alpha
and the set of all quasi-negations of
\alpha
by
QN(\alpha)
.

We now explore some properties of the quasi-negation in the following theorem.

Theorem 2.17
.

Suppose
\mathcal{S}=\langle\mathcal{L},\vdash\rangle
is a logic and
\alpha,\beta\in\mathcal{L}
.

(1)

\alpha
is a
\sim\!\sim\!\alpha
, i.e.
\alpha\in QN(\sim\!\alpha)
. (Quasi double negation)

(2)

\alpha
is a
\sim\!\beta
iff
\beta
is a
\sim\!\alpha
, i.e.
\alpha\in QN(\beta)
iff
\beta\in QN(\alpha)
. (Quasi contraposition).

(3)

If
\langle\mathcal{L},\vdash\rangle
is Tarskian and
\alpha\vdash\beta
then any
\sim\!\beta
is a
\sim\!\alpha
, i.e.
QN(\beta)\subseteq QN(\alpha)
.

Proof.

(1)

By the definition of quasi-negation,
C_{\vdash}(\{\alpha,\sim\!\alpha\})=\mathcal{L}
. Hence
\alpha\in QN(\sim\!\alpha)
, i.e.
\alpha
is a quasi-negation of
\sim\!\alpha
. In other words
\alpha
is a
\sim\!\sim\!\alpha
.

(2)

Suppose
\alpha
is a
\sim\!\beta
, i.e.
\alpha\in QN(\beta)
. Then
C_{\vdash}(\{\beta,\alpha\})=\mathcal{L}
. So,
\beta
is a
\sim\!\alpha
, i.e.
\beta\in QN(\alpha)
. The converse follows by symmetry.

(3)

Suppose
\langle\mathcal{L},\vdash\rangle
is Tarskian and
\alpha\vdash\beta
. Then
\{\alpha,\sim\!\beta\}\vdash\sim\!\beta
and
\{\alpha,\sim\!\beta\}\vdash\beta
, by reflexivity and monotonicity, respectively. Now, since
C_{\vdash}(\{\beta,\sim\!\beta\})=\mathcal{L}
, by transitivity, we have
C_{\vdash}(\{\alpha,\sim\!\beta\})=\mathcal{L}
. Hence
\sim\!\beta
is a
\sim\!\alpha
. Since
\sim\!\beta
was an arbitrary quasi-negation of
\beta
, this implies that
QN(\beta)\subseteq QN(\alpha)
.

∎

The following result establishes some connections between the above defined quasi-negation and the concepts of paraconsistency and
NF
-paraconsistency.

Theorem 2.18
.

Suppose
\mathcal{S}=\langle\mathcal{L},\vdash\rangle
is a logic.

(1)

Suppose
\mathcal{S}
has a negation operator,
\neg
. Then
\mathcal{S}
is paraconsistent iff there exists an
\alpha\in\mathcal{L}
such that
\neg\alpha\notin QN(\alpha)
.

(2)

\mathcal{S}
is
NF
-paraconsistent iff there exists an
\alpha\in\mathcal{L}
such that
QN(\alpha)=\emptyset
.

(3)

If
\mathcal{S}
is monotonic and
QN(\bot)\neq\mathcal{L}
then
\mathcal{S}
is
\bot
-paraconsistent.

Proof.

The statements (1) and (2) follow directly from the definition of quasi-negation. To prove statement (3), suppose
\mathcal{S}
is a monotonic logic. Suppose
\mathcal{S}
is not
\bot
-paraconsistent. Then
C_{\vdash}(\{\bot\})=\mathcal{L}
. So, by monotonicity,
C_{\vdash}(\{\bot,\beta\})=\mathcal{L}
for all
\beta\in\mathcal{L}
. This implies that
\beta\in QN(\bot)
for all
\beta\in\mathcal{L}
, i.e.
QN(\bot)=\mathcal{L}
. Hence
QN(\bot)\neq\mathcal{L}
implies that
\mathcal{S}
is
\bot
-paraconsistent.
∎

Remark 2.19
.

It may be noted that a logic is
NF
-paraconsistent iff it is paraconsistent with respect to the quasi-negation
\sim\!
.

We end this subsection with a few properties of quasi-negation in case of CPC
=\langle\mathcal{L},\vdash_{\mathrm{CPC}}\rangle
, where
\vdash_{\mathrm{CPC}}
is the usual classical consequence relation, using the kite of negations in
[
7
]
. We recall that CPC is sound and complete with respect to the 2-element Boolean algebra
\langle\{0,1\},\land,\lor,\neg\rangle
. Now, for any
\alpha\in\mathcal{L}
,
QN(\alpha)\neq\emptyset
, i.e. there exists a
\sim\!\alpha
and
C_{\vdash_{\mathrm{CPC}}}(\{\alpha,\sim\!\alpha\})=\mathcal{L}
. This implies that for any valuation
v:\mathcal{L}\to\{0,1\}
, if
v(\alpha)=1
then
v(\sim\!\alpha)=0
. Here, by
v(\sim\!\alpha)=0
, we mean
v(\beta)=0
for every
\beta\in QN(\alpha)
. Conversely, suppose
v:\mathcal{L}\to\{0,1\}
is a valuation such that
v(\sim\!\alpha)=0
. Then, in particular,
v(\neg\alpha)=0
since
\neg\alpha\in QN(\alpha)
. So
v(\alpha)=1
. Thus we can say that for any
\alpha\in\mathcal{L}
and any valuation
v:\mathcal{L}\to\{0,1\}
,
v(\alpha)=1
iff
v(\sim\!\alpha)=0
. Now, for any
\alpha,\beta\in\mathcal{L}
, the following properties are easy to check.

(1)

If
\alpha\vdash\beta
then
\sim\!\beta\vdash\sim\!\alpha
(Contraposition)

(2)

\alpha\vdash\neg\sim\!\alpha
and
\alpha\vdash\sim\!\neg\alpha
(Galois double negation)

(3)

\alpha\vdash\sim\!\,\sim\!\alpha
(Constructive double negation)

(4)

\sim\!\,\sim\!\alpha\vdash\alpha
(Classical double negation)

(5)

\alpha\land\sim\!\alpha\vdash\beta
(Absurdity)

It is also easy to check that in case of IPC, all the above properties, except (4), are satisfied by the quasi-negation. Thus, at least in case of CPC and IPC, the quasi-negations of a formula behave in much the same way as the regular classical and intuitionistic negation operators, respectively.

3
Negation-free paraconsistency: second attempt

A second attempt at defining paraconsistency, without reference to a negation operator, involves a
fusion
operator in the object language instead of the meta-linguistic comma. This fusion operator, which we denote by
\curlywedge
, has some conjunction-like properties and in special cases, may be the standard conjunction operator
\land
. Given a logic
\langle\mathcal{L},\vdash\rangle
with
\curlywedge
as a logical operator, we demand the following commutativity property.

\alpha\curlywedge\beta\vdash\beta\curlywedge\alpha,\;\hbox{for all }\alpha,\beta\in\mathcal{L}.

We now give another definition of paraconsistency which can be seen as a generalization of the notion of
\land
-paraconsistency. The following principle of explosion, which we call
\curlywedge
-ECQ may be compared with
\land
-ECQ. For each
\alpha\in\mathcal{L}
, there exists a
\beta\in\mathcal{L}
such that

\alpha\curlywedge\beta\vdash\gamma,\hbox{ where }\gamma\in\mathcal{L}\hbox{ is arbitrary}.

Definition 3.1
.

A logic
\langle\mathcal{L},\vdash\rangle
with a fusion operator
\curlywedge
is called
NFF
-paraconsistent
(
NFF
stands for negation-free with a fusion operator) if
\curlywedge
-ECQ fails in it, i.e. there exists an
\alpha\in\mathcal{L}
such that for all
\beta\in\mathcal{L}
,
C_{\vdash}(\{\alpha\curlywedge\beta\})\neq\mathcal{L}
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Suppose
\mathcal{S}=\langle\mathcal{L},\vdash\rangle
is a logic with
\curlywedge
. If
\mathcal{S}
validates the following
\curlywedge
-introduction rule

\curlywedge I.\quad\{\alpha,\beta\}\vdash\alpha\curlywedge\beta,\;\hbox{for all }\alpha,\beta\in\mathcal{L},

then
\mathcal{S}
is
NF
-paraconsistent implies that it is also
NFF
-paraconsistent. On the other hand, if
\mathcal{S}
validates the following
\curlywedge
-elimination rules

\curlywedge E.\quad\alpha\curlywedge\beta\vdash\alpha,\;\alpha\curlywedge\beta\vdash\beta,\;\hbox{for all }\alpha,\beta\in\mathcal{L},

then
\mathcal{S}
is
NFF
-paraconsistent implies that it is also
NF
-paraconsistent.

Proof.

Suppose
\mathcal{S}=\langle\mathcal{L},\vdash\rangle
validates
\curlywedge I
and is
NF
-paraconsistent. Then there is an
\alpha\in\mathcal{L}
such that for all
\beta\in\mathcal{L}
,
C_{\vdash}(\{\alpha,\beta\})\neq\mathcal{L}
. Now, since
\mathcal{S}
validates
\curlywedge I
,
C_{\vdash}(\{\alpha\curlywedge\beta\})\subseteq C_{\vdash}(\{\alpha,\beta\})
. Thus
C_{\vdash}(\{\alpha\curlywedge\beta\})\neq\mathcal{L}
. So, there exists an
\alpha\in\mathcal{L}
such that for all
\beta\in\mathcal{L}
,
C_{\vdash}(\{\alpha\curlywedge\beta\})\neq\mathcal{L}
. Hence
\mathcal{S}
is
NFF
-paraconsistent.

On the other hand, suppose
\mathcal{S}=\langle\mathcal{L},\vdash\rangle
validates
\curlywedge E
and is
NFF
-paraconsistent. Then there is an
\alpha\in\mathcal{L}
such that for all
\beta\in\mathcal{L}
,
C_{\vdash}(\{\alpha\curlywedge\beta\})\neq\mathcal{L}
. Now, since
\mathcal{S}
validates
\curlywedge E
,
C_{\vdash}(\{\alpha,\beta\})\subseteq C_{\vdash}(\{\alpha\curlywedge\beta\})
. So, there exists an
\alpha\in\mathcal{L}
such that for all
\beta\in\mathcal{L}
,
C_{\vdash}(\{\alpha,\beta\})\neq\mathcal{L}
. Hence
\mathcal{S}
is
NF
-paraconsistent.
∎

Remark 3.3
.

It may be noted here that the above proof rests only on the transitivity of the logic
\mathcal{S}
. Thus the result can be lifted to certain non-Tarskian logics as well, as long as they are transitive.

Theorem 3.4
.

A logic
\mathcal{S}=\langle\mathcal{L},\vdash\rangle
, with a fusion operator
\curlywedge
, is
NF
-paraconsistent iff it is
NFF
-paraconsistent provided it validates
\curlywedge I
and
\curlywedge E
.

Moreover, if
\mathcal{S}
validates
\curlywedge E
and is
NFF
-paraconsistent then by Theorem
2.2
, it is paraconsistent (if it has
\neg
), and by Theorem
2.3
, it is
\bot
-paraconsistent (if it has
\bot
).

Proof.

Straightforward using Lemma
3.2
.
∎

Remark 3.5
.

Suppose
\mathcal{S}=\langle\mathcal{L},\vdash\rangle
is a logic with
\curlywedge
and
\neg
. Then we might say that
\mathcal{S}
is
\curlywedge
-paraconsistent
iff there is an
\alpha\in\mathcal{L}
such that
C_{\vdash}(\{\alpha\curlywedge\neg\alpha\})\neq\mathcal{L}
. This can be seen as a generalization of
\land
-paraconsistency. Moreover, if a logic with
\curlywedge
and
\neg
is
NFF
-paraconsistent then it is also
\curlywedge
-paraconsistent.

We next turn to some concrete examples of
NFF
-paraconsistent logics. Certain
infectious logics
constitute a class of examples of such systems. As mentioned in
[
9
]
, an infectious logic can be intuitively described as a many-valued logic that has an infectious or contaminating truth-value, i.e. a truth-value that if assigned to any formula, is also necessarily assigned to any formula containing the former as a subformula. PWK and IPWK are examples of infectious logics. In the following example, we describe the case of PWK in detail.

Example 3.6
.

Let
\mathbf{WK}
denote the algebra with two binary operations
\land,\lor
and a unary operation
\neg
, given by the following tables.

\begin{array}[]{lll}\begin{array}[]{c|ccc}\land&0&\omega&1\\
\hline\cr 0&0&\omega&0\\
\omega&\omega&\omega&\omega\\
1&0&\omega&1\end{array}&\begin{array}[]{c|ccc}\lor&0&\omega&1\\
\hline\cr 0&0&\omega&1\\
\omega&\omega&\omega&\omega\\
1&1&\omega&1\end{array}&\begin{array}[]{c|c}\neg&\\
\hline\cr 0&1\\
\omega&\omega\\
1&0\end{array}\end{array}

Clearly,
\omega
is an infectious element in this algebra in the sense that any operation of
\mathbf{WK}
yield
\omega
whenever one of the operands is
\omega
. Now, taking
\land,\lor,\neg
as the connectives and and a countable set of propositional variables, we construct the formula algebra
\mathcal{L}
in the usual way. Then PWK can be described as the logic
\langle\mathcal{L},\vdash\rangle
induced by the matrix
\langle\mathbf{WK},\{1,\omega\}\rangle
, where
\{1,\omega\}
is the set of designated elements. This is in the following sense. For any
\Gamma\cup\{\alpha\}\subseteq\mathcal{L}
,

\Gamma\vdash\alpha\hbox{ iff for any valuation }v:\mathcal{L}\to\mathbf{WK},v(\Gamma)\subseteq\{1,\omega\}\hbox{ implies }v(\alpha)\in\{1,\omega\}.

Now, taking
\land
as the fusion operator, we can prove that PWK is
NFF
-paraconsistent as follows. Suppose
\alpha,\gamma\in\mathcal{L}
and
v:\mathcal{L}\to\mathbf{WK}
be a valuation such that
v(\alpha)=\omega
and
v(\gamma)=0
. Then for any
\beta\in\mathcal{L}
,
v(\alpha\land\beta)=\omega
. Hence
\alpha\land\beta\not\vdash\gamma
and thus for all
\beta\in\mathcal{L}
,
C_{\vdash}(\{\alpha\land\beta\})\neq\mathcal{L}
. It is noteworthy here that PWK is not
NF
-paraconsistent, as pointed out earlier in Example
2.5
.

Remark 3.7
.

The above example can be generalized to any infectious logic with a fusion operator, provided the infectious truth-value is designated.

4
Summary and future directions

In this article, we have explored the possibility of describing notions of paraconsistency without depending on negation operators in the language. The first such notion, which we call
NF
-paraconsistency, uses the meta-linguistic comma to juxtapose opposing or contradictory statements while the second one, which we call
NFF
-paraconsistency uses a conjunction-like fusion operator in the object language to do the same. The two notions are, however, equivalent in the presence of a set of introduction and elimination rules for the fusion operator (see Theorem
3.4
). We have proved that
NF
-paraconsistency is a strictly stronger notion than paraconsistency and
\bot
-paraconsistency. Various examples of
NF
-paraconsistent and
NFF
-paraconsistent logics have been provided, including an important class of paraconsistent logics that are
NFF
-paraconsistent but not
NF
-paraconsistent.

These negation free notions of explosion or paraconsistency could allow one to see contradictions more generally, not just as pairs of formulas where one is the direct negation of the other. This could then lead to an exploration of paraconsistency that is independent of the logical operators and also in weaker or substructural logics.

We have used the notion of
NF
-paraconsistency to define a quasi-negation and have explored some properties of it in general, as well as in some particular logics. The quasi-negation of a formula is not unique, but quite surprisingly, it has many negation-operator-like properties.

The following are some future directions in which the current work may be extended.

•

While the usual notion of paraconsistency is satisfied with the non-explosion of a set of the form
\{\alpha,\neg\alpha\}
for some
\alpha
, the concept of
NF
-paraconsistency demands that there exist an
\alpha
such that
\{\alpha,\beta\}
does not explode for every
\beta
. There can also be intermediary notions - one can consider a set
K\subseteq\mathcal{L}
such that there exists an
\alpha
with the property that
\{\alpha,\beta\}
is non-explosive for every
\beta\in K
. Such ‘relativized’ notions of paraconsistency can be called
K
-paraconsistency. One can then perhaps achieve different ‘degrees of paraconsistency’ by varying the set
K
.

•

The quasi-negation can be studied further in general and also for specific logics. In the latter case, this can lead to characterizations of the sets of quasi-negations of a formula in these logics. The quasi-negation can also be studied algebraically or topologically whenever the logic has an algebraic or topological semantics.

•

A similar notion of quasi-negation can be defined and studied using the notion of
NFF
-paraconsistency.
