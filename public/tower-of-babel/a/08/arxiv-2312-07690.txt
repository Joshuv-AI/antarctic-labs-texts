The discrete adiabatic quantum linear system solver has lower constant factors than the randomised adiabatic solver
Pedro C. S. Costa, Dong An, Ryan Babbush, Dominic W. Berry

Abstract
The solution of linear systems of equations is the basis of many other quantum algorithms, and recent results provided an algorithm with optimal scaling in both the condition number κ \kappa and the allowable error ϵ \epsilon [PRX Quantum 3 , 040303 (2022)]. That work was based on the discrete adiabatic theorem, and worked out an explicit constant factor for an upper bound on the complexity. Here we show via numerical testing on random matrices that the constant factor is in practice about 1,500 times smaller than the upper bound found numerically in the previous results. That means that this approach is far more efficient than might naively be expected from the upper bound. In particular, it is over an order of magnitude more efficient than using a randomised approach from [arXiv:2305.11352] that claimed to be more efficient.



I
Introduction

Finding solutions to a system of linear equations is of great importance in many areas of science and technology, and is the foundation for many quantum algorithms.
When we consider classical approaches for solving linear system problems, we have in the best cases a total number of steps proportional to the number of equations, which can make the solution prohibitively costly when we have a large linear systems problem.
On the other hand, quantum computers allow us to deal with the linear systems problems in these prohibitive regions by providing the output as a quantum state. The first proposal for the quantum linear system problem (QLSP) was given by Harrow, Hassidim, and Lloyd (HHL)
Harrow et al. 2009
, which was subsequently refined in later works
An and Lin 2022
;
Ambainis 2010
;
Lin and Tong 2020a
;
Childs et al. 2017
with the optimal approach provided by Costa et al. in
Costa et al. 2022
.

The goal of the QLSP is to efficiently produce a quantum state
\ket{x}
proportional to the solution of
Ax=b
, where
A\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
. For simplicity we assume
N=2^{n}
and
\|A\|=1
, where
\|\cdot\|
denotes the spectral norm. The ground-breaking algorithm from HHL produces the state
\ket{x}
with cost
\order{\text{poly}(n)\kappa^2/\epsilon}
, where
\kappa=\|A\|\|A^{-1}\|
is the condition number of
A
, and
\epsilon
is the maximum allowable error.
Despite the exponential improvement given by HHL over the classical results on the dimension of the problem, e.g.,
\order{N \sqrt{\kappa}\log(1/\epsilon)}
achieved by the conjugate gradient descent method, there was a plenty of scope to improve on the other parameters in the complexity.

Quantum algorithms aim to provide complexity polynomial in
n
, an exponential speedup over the classical methods.
It is known
Harrow and Kothari 2021
that the lower bound for scaling of the complexity of quantum algorithms in
\kappa
and
\epsilon
is
\Omega(\text{poly}(n)\kappa\log(1/\epsilon))
(see
Appendix

A
for details).
That shows that the optimal scaling is linear in
\kappa
when aiming for the exponential quantum speedup in
n
, as opposed to the
\sqrt{\kappa}
classical complexity.
This lower bound on the quantum complexity was achieved in
Costa et al. 2022
, thereby providing the asymptotically optimal quantum algorithm.

The most significant improvements for the QLSP after
Harrow et al. 2009
are based on adiabatic quantum computing (AQC)
An and Lin 2022
;
Lin and Tong 2020b
;
Subaşı et al. 2019
, as opposed to the quantum phase estimation and amplitude amplification approach of HHL.
Reference
Costa et al. 2022
is based upon AQC, but the main new feature is a discrete formulation of the adiabatic theorem, where the time evolution is based on the quantum walk operator
W_{T}(s)
; that enables the optimal complexity for the QLSP.
We refer to that as the quantum walk (QW) method.
In addition to the asymptotically scaling results for the QLSP in
Costa et al. 2022
, the authors also provided strict upper bounds, which can be used to estimate the total number of quantum walk steps for a given solution error
\epsilon
.

The upper bound derived for the discrete adiabatic theorem (DAT) given in
Costa et al. 2022
results from a combination of several approximations from different operators. The approximations allow them to show that the total number of walk steps grows linearly in
\kappa
, i.e.,
T=\alpha\kappa/\Delta
when the parameters of the QLSP are plugged in the DAT. However, due to the loose upper bound from DAT, the value for
\alpha
is very large.
The constant factor
\alpha=2305
, given on page 16 of
Costa et al. 2022
, can be used only for a rough estimate of the total number of quantum walk steps needed to yield a desired error.

An earlier proposal for solving the QLSP is from Ref.
Subaşı et al. 2019
, which uses time evolution for randomised times in order to emulate measurements for the quantum Zeno effect.
Here, we refer to that as the randomisation method (RM).
The complexity of that approach scales as
\order{\kappa\log(\kappa/\epsilon)}
, so has an extra factor of
\log\kappa
in the complexity over the QW method.
A later analysis in Ref.
Jennings et al. 2023
showed a relatively tight upper bound for the RM, and argued that it would be faster in practice than the QW method due to the smaller constant factor.

Here, we test the actual performance of the QW method and RM
by performing numerical simulation of the solution with many samples of randomly generated matrices.
Our numerical tests show that in practice, the constant factor for the QW method is
\alpha=1.56
, which is about 1,500 times smaller than what is given in
Costa et al. 2022
.
Moreover, our tests demonstrated that the quantum walk is on average about 20 times more efficient the RM, even without accounting for the additional complexity of simulating the time evolution.

Details about the numerical tests for the quantum walk and randomisation method are given in
Section

II
.
In
Section

III
, we discuss the cost of implementation of the filtering method, which is applied after the walk steps, to estimate the appropriate target precision for the adiabatic evolution.
We then conclude in
Section

IV
.
In appendices we provide technical details about the quantum walk and randomisation method for solving quantum linear system problems, including the circuit construction of the walk operators and further numerical tests.

Randomisation method

In the randomisation method, we measure the cost by the total time, defined as
\sum_{j=1}^{q}|t_{j}|

Hamiltonian evolution may be simulated using calls to block encodings of the Hamiltonian via quantum signal processing.

\|H\|=1

Berry et al. 2024

It is therefore reasonable to compare the total time here to the number of walk steps;

III
Cost with filter

Typically we would aim to perform the discrete adiabatic algorithm followed by a filter to ensure the correct eigenvalue has been obtained.
First assume that the adiabatic algorithm gives an infidelity error
\delta
, in the sense that the probability of measuring the correct eigenstate is
1-\delta
.
The relation between
\Delta
(2-norm error) and infidelity error
\delta
is
\delta=\Delta^{2}-\Delta^{4}/4
.
Then if we were to perform filtering as described in
Costa et al. 2022
(see Eq. (113) of that work), the complexity of the filter would be
\kappa\ln(2/\epsilon_f)
, with
\epsilon_{f}
the parameter for the filter.
In that work it is shown that the 2-norm error at the end is upper bounded by
\epsilon_{f}
(for
\delta>1/2
), and
\epsilon_{f}^{2}
is also the maximum probability for failure (accepting an incorrect state as correct).

If the state fails the filtering step, then the adiabatic algorithm and filter are repeated.
It should also be taken into account that the error probability will be increased if there are multiple failures and filters.
The set of possible outcomes is as follows.

1.

With probability
1-\delta
the correct state is obtained the first time, which then passes the filtering test.

2.

With probability
\delta
there is an incorrect state. With probability no greater than
\delta\epsilon_{f}^{2}
this state will be accepted regardless, and otherwise it will be flagged as a failure.
The probability of this occurring is at least
\delta(1-\epsilon_{f}^{2})
.

3.

To upper bound the probability of error we should take the probability of performing the procedure a second time as
\delta(1-\epsilon_{f}^{2})
.
The probability of incorrectly accepting the wrong state is then upper bounded as
\delta^{2}\epsilon_{f}^{2}(1-\epsilon_{f}^{2})
.

In general, the probability of flagging a failure and repeating the measurement
n
times is
[\delta(1-\epsilon_{f}^{2})]^{n}
.
For the average number of repetitions, it will be, in this worst-case scenario for error,

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(n+1)[\delta(1-\epsilon_{f}^{2})]^{n}=\frac{1}{[1-\delta(1-\epsilon_{f}^{2})]^{2}}.

(1)

For the costing, we need to bear in mind that for the cases where the filter fails the LCU approach will on average flag a failure after half the maximum number of operations.
This means that the cost of the filter can be given as

\displaystyle\kappa\ln(2/\epsilon_f)+\frac{1}{2}\kappa\ln(2/\epsilon_f)\left(\frac{1}{[1-\delta(1-\epsilon_{f}^{2})]^{2}}-1\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\kappa\ln(2/\epsilon_f)\left(\frac{1}{[1-\delta(1-\epsilon_{f}^{2})]^{2}}+1\right)

(2)

If the number of steps in the adiabatic algorithm to obtain error
\Delta
is
\alpha\kappa/\Delta
, then the cost of the adiabatic repetitions is

\frac{\alpha\kappa}{\Delta[1-\delta(1-\epsilon_{f}^{2})]^{2}}\,,

(3)

for a total complexity

\frac{1}{2}\kappa\ln(2/\epsilon_f)\left(\frac{1}{[1-\delta(1-\epsilon_{f}^{2})]^{2}}+1\right)+\frac{\alpha\kappa}{\Delta[1-\delta(1-\epsilon_{f}^{2})]^{2}}\,.

(4)

Here we can use
\delta=\Delta^{2}-\Delta^{4}/4
or
\Delta=\sqrt{2-2\sqrt{1-\delta}}
to give the expression in terms of a single variable (
\delta
or
\Delta
).
The value of
\kappa
factors out, and given
\alpha
one can optimise
\delta
or
\Delta
for a given
\epsilon_{f}
to minimise the cost.

A further adjustment to the complexity can be made by noting that
\epsilon_{f}
here is just an upper bound on the 2-norm error
\epsilon
.
As per Eq. (J3) of
Costa et al. 2022
, the fidelity in the filtered state can be lower bounded as

f\geq\frac{1-\delta}{1-\delta+\delta\epsilon_{f}^{2}}.

(5)

Then the 2-norm error is
\epsilon=\sqrt{2-2\sqrt{f}}
, so solving for
\epsilon_{f}
gives

\epsilon_{f}=\epsilon\frac{\sqrt{(1-\delta)(1-\epsilon^{2}/4)}}{(1-\epsilon^{2}/2)\sqrt{\delta}}.

(6)

We are using the notation
\epsilon_{f}
for the parameter used in the filter, and
\epsilon
for the final allowable error.
We can use this expression for
\epsilon_{f}
in Eq. (
4
) to give a slightly tighter bound for the complexity.

The results for numerically solving for the optimal
\Delta
are shown in Fig.

for the case
\alpha=1
.
There it is found that optimal values of
\Delta
are around
0.3

When
\alpha

0.2
(as for the PD case for the quantum walk), the optimal
\Delta
is around
0.15
.
A larger
\alpha
of
20
(comparable to the results for the randomisation method) would give an optimal
\delta
around
0.45
.
In the preceding section, we have used the same
\Delta
for the different methods despite the different
\alpha
, to give a fair comparison of the approaches.

IV
Conclusion

The optimal quantum linear systems algorithm of
Costa et al. 2022
works by first applying a quantum walk to provide a result with low accuracy, then filtering the resulting state to improve the precision to the desired level.
The initial quantum walk has an upper bound on its error with a large constant factor in
Costa et al. 2022
which would suggest it is a costly algorithm despite having optimal asymptotic complexity.

Here we have numerically tested this initial quantum walk with random matrices and found that the constant factor is about 1,500 times smaller.
This means that the algorithm will be highly efficient in practice, and the dominant contribution to the complexity will be the filtering step, as would be expected since that is the step that has the factor of
\log(1/\epsilon)
in the complexity.

Reference
Jennings et al. 2023
provided a tighter upper bound on the complexity for an approach with randomised evolution times for initial adiabatic evolution, followed by a filtering step that is similar to
Costa et al. 2022
.
In our numerical testing of that approach, we find that it is more than an order of magnitude more costly in practice, despite having a tighter analytic bound on the complexity.
It is somewhat ambiguous to compare the complexity because it is in terms of an evolution time rather than the number of steps of a quantum walk.
The evolution time is about 20 times the number of steps of the quantum walk needed for the discrete adiabatic approach, and in practice the cost to simulate that Hamiltonian evolution would be even larger.

Appendix A
Lower bound

The
\Omega(\kappa\log(1/\epsilon))
part of the lower bound from
Harrow and Kothari 2021
can be obtained via a minor modification of the proof from HHL
Harrow et al. 2009
.
Adopting the notation of
Harrow et al. 2009
, in Eq. (4) of that work a unitary
U
is constructed such that for
T+1\leq t\leq 2T
, applying
U^{t}
to
\ket{1}\ket{\psi}
yields
\ket{t+1}\otimes U_{T}\cdots U_{1}\ket{\psi}
for some sequence of unitary operators
U_{j}
.
Now define

A=I-U\epsilon^{1/T}

so the condition number
\kappa=\mathcal{O}(T/\log(1/\epsilon))
and we can expand

A^{-1}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}U^{k}\epsilon^{k/T}.

(7)

After applying
A^{-1}
one would obtain
t
in the range
T+1
to
2T
with
\Theta(\epsilon)
probability.
Therefore, solving the linear system with precision
\epsilon
provides an unbounded-error algorithm for implementing
T
steps
U_{T}\ldots U_{1}
.

It is straightforward to choose the operations
U_{j}
so that implementing them in succession gives the parity of
T
bits, and each corresponds to a single query to these bits.
An unbounded-error algorithm for determining the parity of
T
bits requires
\Omega(T)
queries
Farhi et al. 1998
;
Beals et al. 2001
.
That means there must be a lower bound
\Omega(T)
for the number of queries to the operators
U_{j}
.
Now
A
can be block encoded with a single query to
U_{j}
, there is similarly a lower bound
\Omega(T)
to the number of calls to the block encoding of
A
.
Since
\kappa=\mathcal{O}(T/\log(1/\epsilon))
, we have
T=\Omega(\kappa\log(1/\epsilon))
, so the complexity of the linear systems algorithm is
\Omega(\kappa\log(1/\epsilon))
.

Appendix B
Quantum walk method

We start this section by introducing the basic theory of adiabatic quantum computation which is independent of the application.
In AQC, we have a time-dependent system constructed from a linear interpolation between two time-independent Hamiltonians as follows

H(s)=(1-f(s))H_{0}+f(s)H_{1},

(8)

where the function
f(s):[0,1]\rightarrow[0,1]
is called the schedule function. Normally we have an eigenpath between these Hamiltonians where the starting eigenstate of
H_{0}
is easy to prepare, and the final eigenstate given by
H_{1}
is the one that encodes the solution to the problem that we are trying to determine.

For linear systems solvers, the final eigenstate given by
H(1)
should encode the normalised solution for a linear system. In other words, for
A\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
an invertible matrix with
\|A\|=1
, and a normalised vector
\ket{b}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
the goal is to prepare a normalised state
\ket{\tilde{x}}
, which is an approximation of
\ket{x}=A^{-1}\ket{b}/\|A^{-1}\ket{b}\|
.
For precision
\epsilon
of the approximation, we require
\|\ket{\tilde{x}}-\ket{x}\|\leq\epsilon
.

In the following subsections, we show how we should encode both
H_{0}
and
H_{1}
when our goal is to use them for solving QLSP when
A
is a Hermitian and positive-definite matrix, and also the case where
A
does not have to be a Hermitian matrix.
In both cases, we also look at a family of scheduling functions for the problem that slows down the evolution according to the gap from the interpolated Hamiltonian given by
Eq.

8
.

After we set up the time-dependent Hamiltonian in both scenarios of
A
, we apply the method from
Costa et al. 2022
to transform the Hamiltonian into a discrete quantum walk, which is used as the evolution operator for the discrete adiabatic evolution.

B.1
Positive definite and Hermitian matrix A

As explained in
Costa et al. 2022
we have
\ket{\tilde{x}}
as the state achieved from the steps of the walk, and
\ket{x}
would be obtained from the ideal adiabatic evolution. Starting with the simplest case, where
A
is Hermitian and positive definite, one takes the Hamiltonians
An and Lin 2022

H_{0}\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}0&Q_{b}\\
Q_{b}&0\end{pmatrix},

(9)

and

H_{1}\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}0&AQ_{b}\\
Q_{b}A&0\end{pmatrix},

(10)

where
Q_{b}=I_{N}-\ket{b}\bra{b}
. The state
\ket{0,b}
is an eigenstate of
H_{0}
with eigenvalue 0, and one would aim for this to evolve adiabatically to eigenstate
\ket{0,A^{-1}b}
of
H_{1}
.

Denoting the condition number of the matrix as
\kappa
, a lower bound for the gap of
H(s)
is
An and Lin 2022

\Delta_{0}(s)=1-f(s)+f(s)/\kappa.

(11)

As explained in
An and Lin 2022
;
Costa et al. 2022
the goal is to provide a schedule function that slows down the evolution as the gap becomes small, according to the condition given in
Jansen et al. 2007

\dot{f}(s)=d_{p}\Delta_{0}^{p}(s),

(12)

where
f(0)=0
,
p>0
and
d_{p}=\int_{0}^{1}\Delta_{0}^{-p}(u)\,du
is a normalisation constant chosen so that
f(1)=1
. According to
An and Lin 2022
the following schedule function

f(s)=\frac{\kappa}{\kappa-1}\left[1-\left(1+s\left(\kappa^{p-1}-1\right)\right)^{\frac{1}{1-p}}\right],

(13)

satisfies
Eq.

12
.

Distinct from the continuous version of the adiabatic theorem, in the discrete version of the theorem, we have to take into account the smallest gap between the different groups of eigenvalues of
W(s)
for
s,s+1/T
, and
s+2/T
, which are given by

\Delta_{k}(s)=1-f(s+k/T)+f(s+k/T)/\kappa,\quad k=0,1,2.

(14)

Block encoding

Now we show how to block encoding
H(s)
from
Eq.

8
, when we have
H_{0}
and
H_{1}
given by
Eq.

9
and
Eq.

10
respectively.
The general principle for the block encoding of our Hamiltonian is to block encode both
H_{0}
and
H_{1}
, and apply the following controlled operation

\textsc{sel}=\ket{0}\bra{0}\otimes U_{H_{0}}+\ket{1}\bra{1}\otimes U_{H_{1}},

(15)

to select between
H_{0}
and
H_{1}
.
To give the linear combination of
H_{0}
and
H_{1}
we apply the one-qubit rotation on the ancilla qubit

R(s)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\left(1-f(s)\right)^{2}+f(s)^{2}}}\begin{pmatrix}1-f(s)&f(s)\\
f(s)&-(1-f(s))\end{pmatrix}.

(16)

In the following we describe the details of this implementation.

We denote the unitary for the block encoding of
A
as
U_{A}
, which acts on an ancilla denoted with subscript
a
and the system such that

{}_{a}\!\bra{0}U_{A}\ket{0}_{a}=A.

(17)

We also denote the unitary oracle for preparing
\ket{b}
as
U_{b}
such that

U_{b}\ket{0}=\ket{b}.

(18)

As well as the ancilla system used for the block encoding of
A
, we use three ancilla qubits. These ancilla qubits are used as follows.

1.

The first selects between the blocks in
H_{0}
and
H_{1}
.

2.

The next is used for preparing the combination of
H_{0}
and
H_{1}
.

3.

The third is used in implementing
Q_{b}
.

We use the subscripts
a_{h}
,
a_{1}
and
a_{2}
for these three qubits.
Note that although we have called
a_{h}
an ancilla qubit, it part of the systam that the Hamiltonian acts upon, not an ancilla used in the block encoding.
This means that we do not apply the reflection operator on that ancilla to construct the walk operator.

To make the block encoding
H(s)
simpler let us rewrite
Eq.

10
as

H_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}I&0\\
0&Q_{b}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0&I\\
I&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}A&0\\
0&A\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}I&0\\
0&Q_{b}\end{pmatrix},

(19)

and
Eq.

9
as

H_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}I&0\\
0&Q_{b}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0&I\\
I&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}I&0\\
0&I\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}I&0\\
0&Q_{b}\end{pmatrix}.

(20)

This new way to express the Hamiltonians will then facilitate the construction of the select operator since the total Hamiltonian can be expressed as

\displaystyle H(s)

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}I&0\\
0&Q_{b}\end{pmatrix}\left[(1-f(s))\begin{pmatrix}0&I\\
I&0\end{pmatrix}\right.

\displaystyle\quad+\left.f(s)\begin{pmatrix}0&I\\
I&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}A&0\\
0&A\end{pmatrix}\right]\begin{pmatrix}I&0\\
0&Q_{b}\end{pmatrix}.

(21)

For the first block matrix inside the bracket in the equation above, we have

\begin{pmatrix}0&I\\
I&0\end{pmatrix}=\sigma^{x}_{a_{h}}\otimes I\,,

(22)

where
\sigma^{x}_{a_{h}}
indicates that we have the Pauli
X
operator acting on the register
a_{h}
. Now, for the other block matrix which comes from
H_{1}
, we have

\begin{pmatrix}A&0\\
0&A\end{pmatrix}=I_{a_{h}}\otimes A\,,

(23)

where we have included
I_{a_{h}}
to indicate that the operation acts as the identity on the first qubit.
Moreover, we have
U_{A}
as the unitary for the block encoding of
A
, with ancilla qubit
a
.
Thus when we combine
Eq.

22
and
Eq.

23
, the two block matrices for
H_{1}
, we have

\displaystyle\left(\sigma^{x}_{a_{h}}\otimes I_{N}\otimes I_{a}\right)\left(I_{a_{h}}\otimes U_{A}\right)

\displaystyle=\ket{0}\bra{1}_{a_{h}}\otimes U_{A}+\ket{1}\bra{0}_{a_{h}}\otimes U_{A}\,.

(24)

However, the encoding above does not guarantee the hermiticity of
H_{1}
, which can be fixed by the following expression

\ket{0}\bra{1}_{a_{h}}\otimes U_{A}+\ket{1}\bra{0}_{a_{h}}\otimes U_{A}^{\dagger}\,.

(25)

Therefore, we have
Eq.

25
as the block encoding of the two middle operators of
H_{1}
and
\sigma^{x}_{a_{h}}\otimes I_{N}\otimes I_{a}
as the block encoding of the two middle operators of
H_{0}
.
We can therefore select between
H_{0}
and
H_{1}
using the operator

\displaystyle U_{A(f)}

\displaystyle=\ket{0}\bra{0}_{a_{1}}\otimes\sigma^{x}_{a_{h}}\otimes I_{N}\otimes I_{a}

\displaystyle\quad+\ket{1}\bra{1}_{a_{1}}\otimes\left(\ket{0}\bra{1}_{a_{h}}\otimes U_{A}+\ket{1}\bra{0}_{a_{h}}\otimes U_{A}^{\dagger}\right),

(26)

together with the operators involving
Q_{b}
in
Section

B.1
and rotation on the ancilla qubit
a_{1}
.
The operator involving
Q_{b}
in
Section

B.1
can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}I&0\\
0&Q_{b}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\ket{0}\bra{0}_{a_{h}}\otimes I_{N}+\ket{1}\bra{1}_{a_{h}}\otimes Q_{b}

\displaystyle=\ket{0}\bra{0}_{a_{h}}\otimes I_{N}+\ket{1}\bra{1}_{a_{h}}\otimes\left(I_{N}-\ket{b}\bra{b}\right)

\displaystyle=\ket{0}\bra{0}_{a_{h}}\otimes I_{N}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2}\ket{1}\bra{1}_{a_{h}}\otimes\left(I_{N}+\left(I_{N}-2\ket{b}\bra{b}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\ket{0}\bra{0}_{a_{h}}\otimes I_{N}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2}\ket{1}\bra{1}_{a_{h}}\otimes\left[I_{N}+\left(I_{N}-2U_{b}\ket{0}\bra{0}U_{b}^{\dagger}\right)\right].

(27)

In the second equality, we have written the projection in terms of a sum of the identity and a projector, and in the last equality, we used the oracle for preparing
\ket{b}
as given in
Eq.

18
. We can block encode this projector using the ancilla
a_{2}
. We simply need to create this ancilla in an equal superposition and use the following unitary operation

\displaystyle CU_{Q_{b}}

\displaystyle=\left(I_{a_{h}}\otimes I_{a_{2}}\otimes U_{b}^{\dagger}\right)\bigg\{\ket{0}\bra{0}_{a_{h}}\otimes I_{a_{2}}\otimes I_{N}

\displaystyle\quad+\ket{0}\bra{0}_{a_{h}}\otimes\left[\ket{0}\bra{0}_{a_{2}}\otimes I_{N}+\ket{1}\bra{1}_{a_{2}}\right.

\displaystyle\quad\otimes\left.\left(I_{N}-2\ket{0}\bra{0}\right)\right]\bigg\}\left(I_{a_{h}}\otimes I_{a_{2}}\otimes U_{b}\right).

(28)

To block encode
H(s)
as in
Section

B.1
, the block encoding above is applied before and after the select operator
U_{A(f)}
.

We also need to perform the rotation
R(s)
in order to interpolate between the Hamiltonians. The interpolation operator can then act on the ancilla qubit used in the select operator. However, instead of applying the inverse of
R(s)
, we simply perform a Hadamard and to produce the right weights for the Hamiltonians we need to apply the rotation operator controlled by ancilla qubit
a_{h}
as

CR(s)=\ket{0}\bra{0}_{a_{h}}\otimes R(s)_{a_{1}}+\ket{1}\bra{1}_{a_{h}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{a_{1}}

(29)

where
\mathcal{H}
denotes the Hadamard operation. This means that our block encoding gives an extra factor of
1/\sqrt{2[(1-f(s))^{2}+f(s)^{2}]}
.
This prefactor is between
1/\sqrt{2}
and 1, and will reduce the gap.

Then the complete circuit diagram can be given as in
Fig.

. The walk operator
W_{T}(s)
can be completed by a reflection about zero on all ancilla registers except
a_{h}
which is part of the target system for the Hamiltonian.

B.2
Non-Hermitian matrix A

To address the case where
A
is not positive definite or Hermitian, we pay an extra space cost by doubling its dimension to transform it into a Hermitian matrix before solving it in the quantum algorithm, i.e.,

\mathbf{A}\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}0&A\\
A^{\dagger}&0\end{pmatrix}.

(30)

As described in Ref.
An and Lin 2022
the Hamiltonian is then constructed as

H(s)=\begin{pmatrix}0&A(f(s))Q_{\mathbf{b}}\\
Q_{\mathbf{b}}A(f(s))&0\end{pmatrix},

(31)

where

A(f)\coloneqq(1-f)\sigma_{z}\otimes I_{N}+f\mathbf{A}=\begin{pmatrix}(1-f)I&fA\\
fA^{\dagger}&-(1-f)I\end{pmatrix},

(32)

and
Q_{\mathbf{b}}=I_{2N}-\ket{0,b}\bra{0,b}
as the projection for this Hamiltonian formulation. As discussed in
Costa et al. 2022
it is equivalent to taking
H(s)=(1-f(s))H_{0}+f(s)H_{1}

with

\displaystyle H_{0}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{+}\otimes\left[(\sigma_{z}\otimes I_{N})Q_{\mathbf{b}}\right]+\sigma_{-}\otimes\left[Q_{\mathbf{b}}(\sigma_{z}\otimes I_{N})\right]

\displaystyle H_{1}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{+}\otimes\left[\mathbf{A}Q_{\mathbf{b}}\right]+\sigma_{-}\otimes\left[Q_{\mathbf{b}}\mathbf{A}\right],

(33)

where
\sigma_{+}=\ket{1}\bra{0}
,
\sigma_{-}=\ket{0}\bra{1}
.

As in the positive-definite case, the schedule function can be determined from gap via the differential equation (
12
).
In this case the gap is lower bounded as
\sqrt{(1-f(s))^{2}+(f(s)/\kappa)^{2}}
so there is not a closed-form solution of
Eq.

12
.
Instead, one can use the relation that for
0\leq f(s)\leq 1
,

\sqrt{(1-f(s))^{2}+(f(s)/\kappa)^{2}}\geq(1-f(s)+f(s)/\kappa)/\sqrt{2}.

(34)

As a result of using this formula, and the way we use the rotation operator
R(s)
, we can use the same schedule function when
A
is not positive definite.

Appendix C
Randomisation method

A randomised QLSP algorithm inspired by AQC was first proposed in Ref.
Subaşı et al. 2019
and further improved in recent work
Jennings et al. 2023
.
The method uses the same
H_{0}
,
H_{1}
, and the same input state as the adiabatic quantum walk method, in order to approximate the desired eigenstate of
H_{1}
with zero eigenvalue encoding the solution.

Let
H(v)=(1-f(v))H_{0}+f(v)H_{1}
be the interpolating time-dependent Hamiltonian.
Here we change the notation of the dimensionless time from
s
to
v
to avoid possible confusion, as in the randomisation method
v
does not range from
0
to
1
.
Specifically, let

f(v)=\frac{-\kappa^{2}\exp\left(-v\frac{\sqrt{\kappa^{2}+1}}{\sqrt{2}\kappa}\right)+\exp\left(v\frac{\sqrt{\kappa^{2}+1}}{\sqrt{2}\kappa}\right)+2\kappa^{2}}{2(\kappa^{2}+1)},

(35)

and

\displaystyle v_{a}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{2}\kappa}{\sqrt{\kappa^{2}+1}}\log(\kappa\sqrt{1+\kappa^2} - \kappa^2),

(36)

\displaystyle v_{b}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{2}\kappa}{\sqrt{\kappa^{2}+1}}\log(\sqrt{1+\kappa^2} + 1).

(37)

Such a choice ensures that
f(v_{a})=0
and
f(v_{b})=1
.
The randomisation method then implements the operation

e^{-it_{q}H(v_{q})}\cdots e^{-it_{2}H(v_{2})}e^{-it_{1}H(v_{1})}

(38)

on the input state.
Here
v_{j}=v_{a}+j(v_{b}-v_{a})/q
, and
t_{j}
are independent random variables.

In the original work
Subaşı et al. 2019
,
t_{j}
was chosen to be uniformly distributed in
[0,2\pi/\Delta(v_{j})]
.
Recent work
Jennings et al. 2023
suggests to choose
t_{j}
according to the probability density function

p_{j}(t)\propto\left(\frac{J_{p}(\Delta(v_{j})|t|/2)}{\Delta^{p-1}(v_{j})|t|^{p}}\right)^{2},

(39)

where
J_{p}
is a Bessel function of first kind and
p=1.165
.
It has the average time
\left<|t_{j}|\right>=2.32132/\Delta(v_{j})
.
We remark that the way of constructing the optimal probability distribution has been proposed in
Sanders et al. 2020
.
It uses

p_{j}(t)=\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\sum_{l\geq 0}a_{l}\int_{-1}^{1}\!\!\cos(xt\Delta(v_j)/2)(1-x^{2})x^{2l}dx\right)^{2}\!\!,

(40)

where the coefficients
a_{l}
can be numerically computed by minimising
\left<|t_{j}|\right>
.
This optimal choice gives
\left<|t_{j}|\right>=2.3160/\Delta(v_{j})
, which is slightly better than
Eq.

39
.
The corresponding probability distributions (
Eq.

39
and
Eq.

40
) are almost the same (see
Fig.

6
for a comparison), so in this work we still use
Eq.

39
.

Figure 6:
A comparison between two probability distribution functions used in the randomisation method. Here the legend “JLPSS” refers to the one in
Jennings et al. 2023
and “Optimal” to the one in
Sanders et al. 2020
. The gap
\Delta(v_{j})
is chosen to be
0.5
in this test.

Applying
Eq.

38
to the input state produces the final state.
It is shown in
Jennings et al. 2023
that, in order to achieve
1-\delta
fidelity, it suffices to choose
q
such that

\left(1-\frac{(v_{b}-v_{a})^{2}}{q^{2}}\right)^{q}\geq 1-\delta.

(41)

Asymptotically
q\sim(v_{b}-v_{a})^{2}/\delta\sim(\log\kappa)^{2}/\delta
.
In practice,
\delta
can be chosen to be
\mathcal{O}(1)
, and the state produced by the randomisation method will be passed into the filter to improve its fidelity.

To implement the randomised evolution operator
Eq.

38
, Ref.
Jennings et al. 2023
uses quantum signal processing (QSP) together with the Jacobi-Anger expansion.
The cost of implementing an exponential
e^{-itH(v)}
is given as

3\left(e\alpha_{H}|t|/2+\log(2c/\gamma)\right)

(42)

queries to the block encoding of
H(v)
, with
c=1.47762
and
\gamma
the allowed error in each exponential.
Therefore the overall number of queries to the block encoding of
H(v)
becomes

3\left(\frac{e\alpha_{H}}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{q}|t_{j}|+q\log(2c/\gamma)\right).

(43)

That complexity analysis gives a non-asymptotic expression suitable for small times.
In contrast, Ref.
Babbush et al. 2019
gives an asymptotic analysis of the cost of implementing
e^{-itH(v)}
(with the same algorithm) to give the leading-order terms

2\alpha_{H}|t|+3^{2/3}(\alpha_{H}|t|)^{1/3}(\log(1/\gamma))^{2/3}

(44)

for the number of queries to the block encoding of
H(v)
.
The constant factor on
\alpha_{H}|t|
is only
2
here, which is better than the factor
3e/2
in the bound of
Eq.

42
.
Recent work
Berry et al. 2024
removes a factor of
2
in the cost of quantum signal processing for Hamiltonian simulation.
That would remove a factor of 2 from both complexity estimates.
The leading-order term for the analysis of Ref.
Babbush et al. 2019
would then be
\alpha_{H}|t|
.
Therefore, with
\alpha_{H}=1
the number of calls to the block encoding of
H(v)
should be approximately (and no less than)
|t|
.
It is therefore reasonable to compare the total time in the randomisation method with the number of steps in the quantum walk approach.
