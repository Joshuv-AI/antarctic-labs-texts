Li Cai, Bin Xu

Abstract
. Isolating cuspidal automorphic representations from the whole automorphic spectrum is a basic problem in the trace formula approach. For example, matrix coefficients of supercupidal representations can be used as test functions for this, which kills the continuous spectrum, but also a large class of cuspidal automorphic representations. For the case of number fields, multipliers of the Schwartz algebra is used in the recent work [ 3 ] to isolate all cuspidal spectrum which provide enough test functions and suitable for the comparison of orbital integrals. These multipliers are then applied to the proof of the Gan-Gross-Prasad conjecture for unitary groups [ 3 , 2 ] . In this article, we prove similar result on isolating the cuspidal spectrum in [ 3 ] for the function field case.

1.
Isolation of the cuspidal spectrum

The spectral expansion in (relative) trace formulae is a central but difficult problem.
Simple trace formulae can largely simplify this problem.
The traditional simple (relative) trace formulae use matrix coefficients
of supercuspidal representations as (local components of) test functions, which exclude many important cases.
In
[
8
]
, Lindenstrauss and Venkatesh introduce a new type of simple trace
formula to prove the Weyl’s law for spherical cusp forms on locally symmetric spaces associated to a split adjoint semisimple group
G
over
{\mathbb{Q}}
.
Their approach is based on the observation that there are strong relations between the spectrum of the Eisenstein series at different places.

Recently, in
[
3
]
, the authors develop a new technique for isolating components on the spectral side of the trace formula.
Precisely, they introduce an analogue of the Bernstein center at Archimedean places, and construct enough multipliers preserving matching of test functions by considering Schwartz functions, instead of smooth functions with compact supports.
Using these multipliers, one can isolate the cuspidal spectrum without the full spectral decomposition, and establish the refined Gan-Gross-Prasad conjecture for a large class of representations (see also
[
2
]
), which is also important to the work
[
9
]
on the Beilinson-Bloch-Kato conjecture for certain Rankin-Selberg motives.

The goal of this article is to give a proof of the result on isolating the cuspidal spectrum (see
[
3
, Theorem 1.1]
) for the function field case.
Similar to the number field case, the result here is expected to be applied to general situation of trace formula approach over function fields.
This work came to be through an effort to understand the paper
[
3
]
, and will be the starting point of our project on the trace formula approach for the arithmetic problems over function fields.

Comparing with the number field case, the proof of the above result is much more simpler.
In the number field case, there are two main ingredients:

(1)

(Killing the continuous spectrum)
Let
M
be the Levi part of a proper parabolic subgroup
P
of
G
, and
\sigma
be a cupsidal automorphic representation on

To kill the continuous spectrum coming from

I_{P}^{G}(\sigma_{\lambda})
, where
\lambda
runs over (a subspace of) the complex space
{\mathfrak{a}}_{M}^{*}={\mathrm{Rat}}(M)\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}{\mathbb{C}}
(see the notation in Section
3

Let

be a non-Archimedean place such that
\sigma_{v}
is unramified. For each spherical Hecke function

, the function

{\mathfrak{a}}_{M}^{*}\ni\lambda\mapsto{\mathrm{tr}}(I_{P}^{G}(\sigma_{\lambda,v})(f))

is of

To construct the desired global multiplier, one has to show that there are enough multipliers at Archimedean places to match with the above functions of exponential type from non-Archiemdean places.
This is the hardest part (see
[
3

).

(2)

(Isolating cuspidal representations) By a theorem of Donnelly (
[
5
]
), the Casimir eigenvalues of all cuspidal automorphic representations of

with given central character distributes discretely, so that one can isolate the spectrum using certain Weierstrass product from complex analysis (see
[
3
, §2.1]

While in the function field case, one has:

(1’)

(Killing the continuous spectrum)
One can just work with Hecke algebras in the function field case.
For a global function field

4.1

Using this observation, we can construct the desired global Hecke algebra element which annihilates the continuous spectrum coming from

(2’)

[
6

T=\prod_{k=1}^{4}T_{k}\in{\mathbb{C}}\left[q_{\mathbf{v}_{f}}^{\pm\underline{\lambda}_{f}},q_{\mathbf{v}_{\infty}}^{\pm\underline{\lambda}_{\infty}}\right]^{\underline{W}}.

In the above notation,
\underline{W}=\prod_{v\in\mathbf{v}_{\infty}\cup\mathbf{v}_{f}}W
acts on each variable respectivly.

Then it is clear that such
T
annihilates the the continuous spectrum
I_{P}^{G}(\sigma_{\lambda})
for all
\lambda
’s but preserves
\pi
.
As there are finitely many
[\sigma]
’s, a finite product of such Hecke elements
T
kills the space orthogonal to
L^{2}_{0}(G(F)\backslash G({\mathbb{A}})/K)
in
L^{2}(G(F)\backslash G({\mathbb{A}})/K)
, but does not kill
\pi
.

Step 2: Isolating
\pi

By Harder’s theorem, there are only finitely many cuspidal representations in the cuspidal spectrum
L^{2}_{0}(G(F)\backslash G({\mathbb{A}})/K)
.
Denote by
\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{n}
the cuspidal representations which are not nearly equivalent to
\pi
.
In particular, for
\pi_{1}
, there is a place
v_{1}
of
F
outside the union of
S
and
\cup_{[\sigma]}S_{\sigma}
, such that
\pi_{1,v_{1}}\not\cong\pi_{v_{1}}
. Here
S_{\sigma}
is a finite set of places such that the Hecke algebra element used to kill the Eisenstein part
L^{2}_{[\sigma]}
above lies in
{\mathcal{H}}_{S_{\sigma}}
, and
[\sigma]
runs over all such equivalence classes.
Equivalently, we have
{\mathrm{tr}}(\pi_{1,v_{1}})\not={\mathrm{tr}}(\pi_{v_{1}})
, and hence the Hecke algebra element
\left[T_{v_{1}}-{\mathrm{tr}}(\pi_{1,v_{1}}(T_{v_{1}}))\right]
kills
\pi_{1}
, but does not kill
\pi
.
Here
T_{v_{1}}
is a generator in the Hecke algebra
{\mathcal{H}}_{v_{1}}
.
Continue this procedure for
\pi_{2},\ldots,\pi_{n}
, we can construct a Hecke algebra element which kills all the cuspidal representations not nearly equivalent to
\pi
in the spectrum, but does not kill
\pi
.
This finishes the proof.

Remark 2.2
.

In
[
11
]
, the authors kill the continuous part in the case that
G={\mathrm{PGL}}_{2}
and
K
is maximal, by employing the so-called
Eisenstein ideal
in Hecke algebra.
Take
S=\emptyset
, and let
{\mathcal{H}}=\otimes_{v}{\mathcal{H}}_{v}
be the spherical Hecke algebra of
G
with respect to
K
.
Consider the ring homomorphisms (See
[
11
, (4.1)]
)

a_{{\mathrm{Eis}}}:{\mathcal{H}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\mathcal{S}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}{\mathbb{C}}[A({\mathbb{A}})/A({\mathbb{O}})]\longrightarrow{\mathbb{C}}[A(F)\backslash A({\mathbb{A}})/A({\mathbb{O}})],

where
A
is the diagonal subgroup of
G
, and
{\mathbb{O}}=\prod_{v}{\mathcal{O}}_{v}
.
The image of
a_{\mathrm{Eis}}
can be described clearly, which is the subspace of
{\mathbb{C}}[A(F)\backslash A({\mathbb{A}})/A({\mathbb{O}})]
invariant under the involution from the Weyl group
W
of
G
(see
[
11
, Lemma 4.2 (2)]
).
The Eisenstein ideal
I_{\mathrm{Eis}}
of
{\mathcal{H}}
is then defined to be the kernel of
a_{\mathrm{Eis}}
.
By the spectral decomposition of
L^{2}(G(F)\backslash G({\mathbb{A}})/K)
, and the fact that the characters of Eisenstein series factor through
a_{\mathrm{Eis}}
(that is,

{\mathrm{tr}}(I_{P}^{G}\chi)=\chi\circ a_{\mathrm{Eis}}
for any unramified Hecke character
\chi
on
A({\mathbb{A}})
), any element in
I_{\mathrm{Eis}}
kills the continuous spectrum, and vice versa.
On the other hand, the ideal
I_{\mathrm{Eis}}
is large enough in the sense that for any cuspidal automorphic representation
\pi
of
G({\mathbb{A}})
which is unramified everywhere, there exists an element
f\in I_{\mathrm{Eis}}
such that
{\mathrm{tr}}(\pi(f))\not=0
.
In fact, if not,
I_{\mathrm{Eis}}
will be contained in the kernel of
{\mathrm{tr}}\pi
so that
{\mathrm{tr}}\pi
factors though
a_{\mathrm{Eis}}
.
Since the image of
a_{{\mathrm{Eis}}}
is the
W
-invariant subspace of

{\mathbb{C}}[A(F)\backslash A({\mathbb{A}})/A({\mathcal{O}})]
,
{\mathrm{tr}}\pi
is given by a (
W
-orbit of) Hecke character
\chi
on
A({\mathbb{A}})/A({\mathcal{O}})
.
This is impossible since
\pi
is not
(G,\emptyset)
-CAP.
Moreover, based on the above, one can obtain an element
\mu\in I_{\mathrm{Eis}}
satisfying the conditions in Theorem
1.1
, by applying Harder’s theorem.

Comparing with the strategy above, for a given cuspidal automorphic representation
\pi
, in this section we construct an explicit element
\mu_{\pi}\in I_{\mathrm{Eis}}
isolating
\pi
from
L^{2}(G(F)\backslash G({\mathbb{A}})/K)
.
In particular,
\mu_{\pi}
depends on
\pi
, while
I_{\mathrm{Eis}}
does not.

Remark 2.3
.

We discuss a possible generalization of the above Eisenstein ideal for a general reductive group
G
and any level
K\subset G({\mathbb{A}})
. Let
\omega
be a unitary Hecke character on the center
Z
of
G
and
assume
\omega
is invariant under
Z({\mathbb{A}})\cap K
. Let

S
be a finite set of places such that

is maximal. For each standard Levi subgroup

of

(after fixing a Borel subgroup of

the spherical Hecke algebra of

Then for each irreducible unramified representation

Denote by

, and consider the following map

Here,

on

such that

Denote by

the kernel
of

. Consider the following ideal of

Then by the spectral decomposition of

,
any element in

kills the continuous spectrum, and vice versa.
On the other hand, one needs to know that the ideal

on

with

(G,S)
-CAP, there exists an element

One may prove this by studying the image of

However, the property that

3.
Spectral decomposition along the cuspidal data

In this section, we recall the spectral decomposition of

along the cuspidal supports in the case of function field, following

]
.

For convenience, we list some notation first, which will be used in the remaining parts of this note.

•

Let
P_{0}
be a fixed minimal parabolic subgroup of
G
defined over
F
, with Levi subgroup
M_{0}
.
A subgroup

of

containing

Let

, let

be the maximal split torus in the center of

.

•

Fix a maximal compact subgroup

such that

–

;

–

for every standard parabolic subgroup

•

Denote by

. Then denote

•

For

|\chi|(m)=\prod_{v}|\chi_{v}(m_{v})|_{v},\quad(m=(m_{v})_{v}\in M({\mathbb{A}}))

•

Denote by

, which can be realized
as a quotient of

.
In fact, let

j:M^{1}({\mathbb{A}})\backslash M({\mathbb{A}})\longrightarrow\left(q^{\mathbb{Z}}\right)^{r},\quad m\mapsto\left(|\chi_{1}|(m),\ldots,|\chi_{r}|(m)\right)

defines a topological group isomorphism onto its image, which is a subgroup of

Then

\kappa:{\mathfrak{a}}_{M}^{*}\longrightarrow X_{M},\quad\chi_{i}\mapsto|\chi_{i}|

is a surjective morphism of groups, and the kernel of

is of the form

(\frac{2\pi i}{\log q})L
, where

is a lattice of
{\mathrm{Rat}}(M)\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}{\mathbb{Q}}
. We also denote
{\mathrm{Re}}(X_{M})=\kappa({\mathrm{Re}}({\mathfrak{a}}_{M}^{*}))

induces an isomorphism

.

•

Denote by
X_{M}^{G}
the subgroup of

with characters trivial on

(recall that

, the center of

). In particular,
there is a perfect pairing

(3.1)

X_{M}^{G}\times M({\mathbb{A}})^{1}Z({\mathbb{A}})\backslash M({\mathbb{A}})\longrightarrow{\mathbb{C}}^{\times}.

•

For standard Levi subgroups
M\subset M^{\prime}
of
G
, denote by
{\mathrm{Re}}(({\mathfrak{a}}_{M}^{M^{\prime}})^{*})
the real vector subspace of
{\mathrm{Re}}({\mathfrak{a}}_{M}^{*})
generated by

, the set of roots (see
[
7
, §I.1.6]

relative to

.
Identifying
{\mathrm{Re}}({\mathfrak{a}}_{M^{\prime}}^{*})
with a real vector subspace of

{\mathrm{Re}}({\mathfrak{a}}_{M}^{*})={\mathrm{Re}}({\mathfrak{a}}_{M^{\prime}}^{*})\oplus{\mathrm{Re}}(({\mathfrak{a}}_{M}^{M^{\prime}})^{*}).

Moreover, the elements of

can be identified with the elements of

which are trivial on the center of

.
After tensor product by

, one also has the decomposition

(3.2)

{\mathfrak{a}}_{M}^{*}={\mathfrak{a}}_{M^{\prime}}^{*}\oplus({\mathfrak{a}}_{M}^{M^{\prime}})^{*},

where
({\mathfrak{a}}_{M}^{M^{\prime}})^{*}={\mathrm{Re}}(({\mathfrak{a}}_{M}^{M^{\prime}})^{*})\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}{\mathbb{C}}
.

•

For a compact open subgroup
K\subset G({\mathbb{A}})
such that
G({\mathbb{A}})=P({\mathbb{A}})K
, one defines a map

m_{P}:G({\mathbb{A}})\longrightarrow M^{1}({\mathbb{A}})\backslash M({\mathbb{A}})

by
m_{P}(g)=M^{1}m
if

with
u\in U({\mathbb{A}})
,
m\in M({\mathbb{A}})
and
k\in K
.

We recall some notions on automorphic forms and automorphic representations.
Let
P=MU
be a standard parabolic subgroup.
We call a smooth (locally constant) function

(3.3)

\varphi:U({\mathbb{A}})M(F)\backslash G({\mathbb{A}})\longrightarrow{\mathbb{C}}

an
automorphic form
if

(i)

\varphi
is of moderate growth;

(ii)

\varphi
is
K_{0}
-finite;

(iii)

\varphi
is
{\mathfrak{Z}}(G(F_{v}))
-finite for any place

of
F
. Here,
{\mathfrak{Z}}(G(F_{v}))
is the Bernstein
center (see
[
1
]
) of

.

We denote the space of all such automorphic forms by
{\mathcal{A}}(U({\mathbb{A}})M(F)\backslash G({\mathbb{A}}))
.
For a unitary automorphic character
\omega:Z(F)\backslash Z({\mathbb{A}})\longrightarrow{\mathbb{C}}^{\times}
, we also denote by
{\mathcal{A}}(U({\mathbb{A}})M(F)\backslash G({\mathbb{A}}))_{\omega}
the automorphic forms
\varphi
with central character
\omega
, i.e.
\varphi(zg)=\omega(z)\varphi(g)
for all
z\in Z({\mathbb{A}})
.
We say
\varphi
is

if for all parabolic subgroups
P^{\prime}
with

, its constant term along

is zero.
The space of cuspidal automorphic forms on
U({\mathbb{A}})M(F)\backslash G({\mathbb{A}})
is denoted by
{\mathcal{A}}_{0}(U({\mathbb{A}})M(F)\backslash G({\mathbb{A}}))
.

Moreover, for any

, we define
\varphi_{k}:M(F)\backslash M({\mathbb{A}})\longrightarrow{\mathbb{C}}
by

\varphi_{k}(m)=m^{-\rho_{P}}\varphi(mk),

where
\rho_{P}
is the half-sum of roots of
M
in the Lie algebra of
U
.
Then a smooth function (
3.3
) is an automorphic form if it is
K_{0}
-finite and for all

,
\varphi_{k}
is an automorphic form on
M(F)\backslash M({\mathbb{A}})
(
[
7
, §I.2.17]
).

The spectral decomposition is given by Eisenstein series associated to different cuspidal data.
We set some more notation:

•

Denote by
\Pi_{0}(M({\mathbb{A}}))
the set of cuspidal automorphic representations
\sigma
of
M({\mathbb{A}})
, i.e., the set of equivalence classes of irreducible subquotients of the space of cusp forms
{\mathcal{A}}_{0}(M(F)\backslash M({\mathbb{A}}))

•

For any unitary automorphic character
\omega:Z(F)\backslash Z({\mathbb{A}})\longrightarrow{\mathbb{C}}^{\times}

\Omega_{M}(\omega)
be the set of unitary automorphic characters
\omega_{M}:Z_{M}(F)\backslash Z_{M}({\mathbb{A}})\longrightarrow{\mathbb{C}}^{\times}
such that

.

•

Denote by
\Pi_{0}(M({\mathbb{A}}))_{\omega}
the subspace of
\Pi_{0}(M({\mathbb{A}}))
consisting of cuspidal automorphic representations with central character
\omega_{M}\in\Omega_{M}(\omega)
.

•

For
\sigma\in\Pi_{0}(M({\mathbb{A}}))_{\omega}
, denote by
{\mathcal{A}}(M,\sigma)
the subspace consisting of

\varphi\in{\mathcal{A}}(U({\mathbb{A}})M(F)\backslash G({\mathbb{A}}))_{\omega}

such that
\varphi_{k}\in{\mathcal{A}}(M(F)\backslash M({\mathbb{A}}))_{\sigma}
for all
k\in K_{0}
, here
{\mathcal{A}}(M(F)\backslash M({\mathbb{A}}))_{\sigma}
is the isotypic submodule of
\sigma
in
{\mathcal{A}}(M(F)\backslash M({\mathbb{A}}))
.

The group
X_{M}^{G}
acts on the space
\Pi_{0}(M({\mathbb{A}}))_{\omega}
via

\sigma\longmapsto\sigma_{\lambda}=\sigma\otimes\lambda

with
\lambda\in X_{M}^{G}
and
\sigma\in\Pi_{0}(M({\mathbb{A}}))_{\omega}
.
We say
\sigma
is equivalent to
\sigma^{\prime}
if there exisits
\lambda\in X_{M}^{G}
such that
\sigma_{\lambda}\simeq\sigma^{\prime}
, and denote such an equivalent class by
{\mathfrak{P}}
.
A cuspidal datum (of central character
\omega
) is a pair
(M,{\mathfrak{P}})
where
M
is a standard Levi of
G
, and
{\mathfrak{P}}
is an equivalence class of
\sigma\in\Pi_{0}(M({\mathbb{A}}))_{\omega}
as above.
Two cuspidal data

and
(M^{\prime},{\mathfrak{P}}^{\prime})
are called equivalent if there exists some

such that

w\cdot M=M^{\prime}
and

. By the Bruhat decomposition, if such

.

The group
X_{M}^{G}
also acts on the space
{\mathcal{A}}(U({\mathbb{A}})M(F)\backslash G({\mathbb{A}}))_{\omega}
via

\varphi\longmapsto\varphi_{\lambda}:=\varphi\cdot(\lambda\circ m_{P}).

Then any

induces an isomorphism

\lambda:{\mathcal{A}}(M,\sigma)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\longrightarrow}}{\mathcal{A}}(M,\sigma_{\lambda}).

For any
\varphi\in{\mathcal{A}}(M,\sigma)
, the Eisenstein series on
G(F)\backslash G({\mathbb{A}})
associated to

is defined by

E(\varphi,\sigma)(g)=\sum_{\gamma\in P(F)\backslash G(F)}\varphi(\gamma g),

whenever the sum converges.

Proposition 3.1

(
[
7
]
§II.1.5, Proposition)
.

There exists an open cone
C_{M}^{G}
in
X_{M}^{G}

such that for any
\varphi\in\sigma
, if
\lambda\in C_{M}^{G}
,
then the summation defining
E(\varphi_{\lambda},\sigma_{\lambda})(g)
converges absolutely and uniformly when

varies in a compact set. Moreover, one also has

if it is convergent.

Let
P(X_{M}^{G})
be the set of Paley-Wiener functions on

, i.e. the image of the Fourier transform (recall (
3.1
))

f\mapsto\widehat{f}(\lambda)=\int_{M({\mathbb{A}})^{1}Z({\mathbb{A}})\backslash M({\mathbb{A}})}f(m)\lambda(m)\,\mathrm{d}m

on the space
C_{c}^{\infty}(M({\mathbb{A}})^{1}Z({\mathbb{A}})\backslash M({\mathbb{A}}))
.
A section
\Phi:X_{M}^{G}\rightarrow{\mathcal{A}}(M,\sigma)
is called a Paley-Wiener section if
\Phi
is a sum of sections
of the form

X_{M}^{G}\ni\lambda\longmapsto\widehat{f}(\lambda)\cdot\varphi

for some
\widehat{f}\in P(X_{M}^{G})
and
\varphi\in{\mathcal{A}}(M,\sigma)
.
Denote by
P(M,\sigma)
the space of all the Paley-Wiener sections on
{\mathcal{A}}(M,\sigma)
.
For any
\Phi\in P(M,\sigma)
, consider the pseudo-Eisenstein series (see
[
7

)

\theta_{\Phi}(g)=\int_{\lambda\in X_{M}^{G},{\mathrm{Re}}(\lambda)=\lambda_{0}}E(\Phi(\lambda)_{\lambda},\sigma_{\lambda})\,\mathrm{d}\lambda

where
\lambda_{0}
is an arbitrary element in
{\mathrm{Re}}(X_{M}^{G})
which is positive enough.

Let
L^{2}(G(F)\backslash G({\mathbb{A}}))_{\omega}
be the space of functions on
G(F)\backslash G({\mathbb{A}})
with central character
\omega
and square-integrable modulo the center

.
By computing the inner product of two psuedo-Eisenstein series, one obtains the following spectral decomposition result:

Theorem 3.2

(Spectral decomposition along cuspidal data,
[
7
]
§II.2.4, Proposition)
.

Let
{\mathfrak{X}}
be an equivalence class of cuspidal data. Denote by

the closed subspace of
L^{2}(G(F)\backslash G({\mathbb{A}}))_{\omega}
spanned by

\theta_{\Phi}
with
\Phi\in P(M,\sigma)
where
(M,\sigma)
is an arbitrary representative of

.
Then

L^{2}(G(F)\backslash G({\mathbb{A}}))_{\omega}=\bigoplus_{{\mathfrak{X}}}L^{2}(G(F)\backslash G({\mathbb{A}}))_{\mathfrak{X}}.

We also need some finiteness property for the spectral decomposition in our proof later.
The following theorem is due to Harder:

Theorem 3.3

(
[
6
]
, Corollary 1.2.3)
.

Let
G
be a reductive group over
F
and
\omega
be a
unitary character of

. Then for any open compact subgroup
K

of
G({\mathbb{A}})
, the vector space
L_{0}^{2}(G(F)\backslash G({\mathbb{A}})/K)_{\omega}
is of finite dimension.

Corollary 3.4
.

Let
G
be a reductive group over
F
and
\omega
be a
unitary character of
Z(F)\backslash Z({\mathbb{A}})
. Let
K
be an open compact subgroup of
G({\mathbb{A}})
.
Then there are only finitely many cuspidal data occurring in the spectral decomposition of

L^{2}(G(F)\backslash G({\mathbb{A}})/K)_{\omega}
.

Proof.

Let
{\mathfrak{X}}
be an equivalence class of cuspidal data.
Assume that
L^{2}(G(F)\backslash G({\mathbb{A}})/K)_{\mathfrak{X}}\not=0
.
Then for any
(M,\sigma)\in{\mathfrak{X}}
, we have
(I_{P}^{G}\sigma)^{K}\not=0
.
Using
[
10
, §III.2.2, Lemme]
, one sees that there exists an open compact subgroup
K_{M}
of
M({\mathbb{A}})
depending on
K
such that
\sigma^{K_{M}}\not=0
for any
\sigma
with
(I_{P}^{G}\sigma)^{K}\not=0
.
We claim that by modifying
\sigma
to
\sigma_{\lambda}
with
\lambda\in X_{M}^{G}
,
the central character of
\sigma
belongs to a finite set.
Hence, by Harder’s theorem (Theorem
3.3
), there are only finitely many such
\sigma
’s.

To prove the claim, consider the
set
\Sigma
of characters
\omega_{M}:Z_{M}(F)\backslash Z_{M}({\mathbb{A}})/K_{Z_{M}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{C}}^{\times}

with
\omega_{M}|_{Z({\mathbb{A}})}=\omega
, here
K_{Z_{M}}
is a fixed open compact subgroup
of
Z_{M}({\mathbb{A}})
.
The group of characters of the quotient
Z_{G}({\mathbb{A}})Z_{M}({\mathbb{A}})^{1}\backslash Z_{M}({\mathbb{A}})
acts on
\Sigma
.
The claim is then equivalent to saying that the number of the orbits of
\Sigma
under this action is finite.
To see the finiteness, let
T
be the torus
Z_{M}/Z_{G}
over
F
and
K_{T}
the image of

K_{Z_{M}}
in
T({\mathbb{A}})
.
Consider the exact sequence

1\longrightarrow Z_{G}(F)\backslash Z_{G}({\mathbb{A}})/(K_{Z_{M}}\cap Z_{G}({\mathbb{A}}))\longrightarrow Z_{M}(F)\backslash Z_{M}({\mathbb{A}})/K_{Z_{M}}\longrightarrow T(F)\backslash T({\mathbb{A}})/K_{T}\longrightarrow 1.

We may write

being finite, where

is a split torus and
T_{0}
an anisotropic torus.
For the anisotropic part, the quotient

And for the split part, if we denote its rank by

T_{s}(F)\backslash T_{s}({\mathbb{A}})\cong(F^{\times}\backslash{\mathbb{A}}^{1})^{d}\times{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}

Note that we may modify

Therefore, there must be finite number of such orbits by the exact sequence above.

∎
