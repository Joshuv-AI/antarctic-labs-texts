Geometric planted matchings beyond the Gaussian model
Lucas R. Schwengber, Roberto I. Oliveira

Abstract




Abstract

We consider the problem of recovering an unknown matching between a set of
n
randomly placed points in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and random perturbations of these points. This can be seen as a model for particle tracking and more generally, entity resolution. We use matchings in random geometric graphs to derive minimax lower bounds for this problem that hold under great generality. Using these results we show that for a broad class of distributions, the order of the number of mistakes made by an estimator that minimizes the sum of squared Euclidean distances is minimax optimal when
d
is fixed and is optimal up to
n^{o(1)}
factors when
d=o(\log n)
. In the high-dimensional regime we consider a setup where both initial positions and perturbations have independent sub-Gaussian coordinates. In this setup we give sufficient conditions under which the same estimator makes no mistakes with high probability. We prove an analogous result for an adapted version of this estimator that incorporates information on the covariance matrix of the perturbations.

1
Introduction

We study the geometric planted matching model introduced by D. Kunisky and J. Niles-Weed in
[
11
]
. Given
n,d\in\mathbb{N}
, we generate a sequence
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
of independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) random positions in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and independently a sequence of i.i.d. random perturbations
Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n}
, from which we derive,

Y_{i}=X_{\pi^{\star}(i)}+Z_{i},\penalty\ i\in[n],

(1.1)

a sequence of perturbed versions of the original points with the label information hidden by an unknown random permutation
\pi^{\star}\sim\text{Unif}(S_{n})
.
The goal is to recover
\pi^{\star}
given direct observation of
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
and
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}
.

We can interpret
\{X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\}
and
\{Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}\}
as being snapshots at different times of the positions of
n
particles performing some form of random motion in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Based on these snapshots we would like to track each particle’s trajectory. This is a natural problem that arises in the life sciences
[
18
,
3
,
9
]
in the context of analyzing single-cell dynamics, and in the physical sciences
[
12
]
in problems involving fluid dynamics. This model also makes sense for growing
d
. For instance, if we imagine that the
X_{i}
’s are high-dimensional feature vectors, and the
Y_{i}
’s are noisy versions of the
X_{i}
’s, then estimating
\pi^{*}
is a toy model for
entity resolution
or
deduplication

[
5
]
.

Under the assumption that
X_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
and
Z_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}I_{d})
,
[
11
]
established sufficient and necessary conditions on
\sigma^{2}
for the number of mistakes made by the maximum likelihood estimator (MLE) to be
0
or
o(n)
with high-probability for different regimes of
d
. Under the same Gaussian model,
[
24
]
showed that some of these conditions are information theoretical optimal. In
[
6
]
and
[
2
]
information-theoretical limits were also established under different models for database matching with Gaussian assumptions. Another line of research, tracing back to
[
4
]
, considers the problem of recovering planted matchings in weighted bipartite graphs without a latent geometric structure. In that case, more general results are available
[
13
,
17
,
8
]
.

In this work we consider the geometric planted matching problem following (
1.1
) for broader classes of distributions for
X_{i}
and
Z_{i}
. In this more general setting, we obtain a general lower bound on the expected number of mistakes made by any estimator. Our proof is based on a simple argument involving matchings in random geometric graphs. The same strategy can also be used to establish negative results similar to the ones in
[
11
]
and
[
24
]
. We showcase some consequences for the regime when
d=\Theta(1)
and
d=o(\log n)
providing natural scenarios where it gives optimal results up to constant factors in the first regime, and up to
n^{o(1)}
factors in the latter one.

We also obtain results in high-dimensional settings when both the initial positions and noise vectors have anisotropic sub-Gaussian distributions. This is motivated by the connection with data deduplication, where non-isotropy seems natural. Our main finding is that there seems to be natural “effective dimension” and “effective SNR” parameters that govern the matching problem in this setting, much like the true dimension determines the error rate for isotropic Gaussians
[
11
]
.

1.1
Main results

In what follows, an
estimator

\hat{\pi}
is a map that takes as input two sequences of
n
points in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
and
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}
, and outputs a permutation
\hat{\pi}\in S_{n}
. We measure the performance of such estimator
\hat{\pi}
using the Hamming distance with respect to the true planted matching
\pi^{\star}
:

d_{H}\left({\hat{\pi}},{\pi^{\star}}\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}_{[\hat{\pi}(j)\neq\pi^{\star}(j)]}.

(1.2)

As in previous works
[
4
,
13
,
17
,
8
,
11
,
24
]
we want to understand the behaviour of the above quantity and its expectation for large
n
. More concretely, we would like to establish upper bounds on the expected number of mistakes made by a certain estimator (positive results), and lower bounds on the same quantity that hold for some, or for any, estimator (negative results).

For our positive results, we focus on an estimator that solves:

\displaystyle\hat{\pi}_{\mathrm{LSS}}

\displaystyle=\underset{\pi\in S_{n}}{\text{arg min}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left\|{X_{\pi(j)}-Y_{j}}\right\|_{2}^{2}

(1.3)

\displaystyle=\underset{\pi\in S_{n}}{\text{arg max}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\langle X_{\pi(j)},Y_{j}\rangle.

Under the Gaussian model from
[
11
]
this coincides with the MLE, but it can also be used in more general settings. Following
[
6
]
we will refer to this estimator as the
Least Sum of Squares Estimator
(LSS).

This estimator is distribution agnostic and can also be evaluated efficiently using the Hungarian method
[
10
]
since it is an instance of a linear assignment problem. It appeared in previous theoretical analysis
[
11
,
24
,
2
,
6
]
under Gaussian assumptions and in practical applications
[
9
]
. Not directly related to this work there exists an extensive literature on understanding the asymptotic behaviour of so-called Euclidean Bipartite Matching Problems
[
19
,
20
]
, which have the same structure as the random optimization problem in (
1.3
), but the
Y_{i}
’s are assumed to be an independent copy of the
X_{i}
’s.

1.1.1
Low-dimension

Our first two results are specially informative when
d=o(\log n)
. This includes the case where
d
is fixed, which is the natural setting for the problem of particle tracking when the number of particles being tracked is very large
[
4
]
.

Model LD
.

We generate a model following (
1.1
) with the assumption that:

1.

X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{iid}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{P}
where
\mathcal{P}
is a probability distribution on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that:

(a)

\mathcal{P}(\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d};\penalty\ \left\|{x}\right\|<R_{d}\})\geq\gamma
for some norm
\|\,\cdot\,\|
and
R_{d},\gamma>0
.

(b)

\mathcal{P}
has a density
f_{\mathcal{P}}
, with respect to Lebesgue measure in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, such that:

\frac{\esssup\limits_{\left\|{x}\right\|<R_{d}}f_{\mathcal{P}}(x)}{\essinf\limits_{\left\|{x}\right\|<R_{d}}f_{\mathcal{P}}(x)}\leq e^{\beta d},

(1.4)

for some constant
\beta\geq\log(2)
.

2.

Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n}=\sigma\tilde{Z}_{1},\dots,\sigma\tilde{Z}_{n}
, where
\sigma>0
is the noise level and
\tilde{Z}_{1},\dots,\tilde{Z}_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{iid}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{Q}
are random directions of motion following
\mathcal{Q}
a distribution in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that:

\sup_{\left\|{v}\right\|<R_{d}}\left\|{\mathcal{Q}(v)\otimes\mathcal{Q}(-v)-\mathcal{Q}\otimes\mathcal{Q}}\right\|_{TV}\leq 1-e^{-\beta d},

(1.5)

where
\mathcal{Q}(v)
is the law of
\tilde{Z}_{1}+v
, and
\|\,\cdot\,\|,R_{d},\gamma
and
\beta
are the same as in assumption
1
.

Loosely speaking, assumption (a) says that there is a ball carrying a positive part of the mass of
\mathcal{P}
where the density
f_{\mathcal{P}}
does not vary too much. Assumption (b) is expected to be satisfied when
\mathcal{Q}
has a density with a significant overlap with bounded translations of itself. For instance, these assumptions naturally hold for
\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{Q}=\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
with
\|\,\cdot\,\|=\left\|{\,\cdot\,}\right\|_{2}
,
R_{d}=\sqrt{2d}
and
\beta,\gamma>0
which are independent of
d
and
n
. But they also hold if we only assume that
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{Q}
have bounded continuous densities (see §
2.2
below).

Under the above assumptions, we have following explicit minimax lower bound:

Theorem 1.1

(Minimax lower bound (proof in §
3.3
))
.

Under
LD
, for all
n\geq 3
,

\inf_{\hat{\pi}}\mathbb{E}\left[d_{H}\left({\hat{\pi}},{\pi^{\star}}\right)\right]\geq\frac{\gamma^{2}}{32}e^{-7\beta d}\left((n^{2}\sigma^{d})\wedge n\right),

(1.6)

where the infimum above is taken over all estimators
\hat{\pi}
.

The proof of this result is based on a natural argument that uses matchings in the random geometric graph
G(n,r,d,\|\,\cdot\,\|)=G(\{X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\},r,d,\|\,\cdot\,\|)
constructed by joining pairs of initial positions which are within a distance
r
of each other with respect to norm
\|\,\cdot\,\|
. As a main step in this proof we also show the following result of independent interest:

Lemma 1.2

(Largest matching size lower bound (proof in §
3.2
))
.

Let
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{iid}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{P}
and
M_{r}
be the largest matching in
G(n,r,d,\|\,\cdot\,\|)
and suppose
\mathcal{P}
,
\|\,\cdot\,\|,R_{d},\gamma
and
\beta
follow assumption
1
from
LD
. Then for all
n\geq 3
and
r>0
,

\mathbb{E}\left[|M_{r}|\right]\geq\frac{\gamma^{2}}{16}e^{-6\beta d}\left(\left(n^{2}\left(\frac{r}{R_{d}}\right)^{d}\right)\wedge n\right).

(1.7)

Given
Lemma

1.2
, the idea of the proof of Theorem
1.1
is that a large matching in
G(n,r,d,\|\,\cdot\,\|)
implies that a large number of pairs of points
X_{i}
and
X_{j}
are very close to one another. Since points which are close to each other are hard to distinguish once noise is added, they are likely to lead to mistakes. This phenomenon is illustrated in
Figure

1
for
\hat{\pi}_{\mathrm{LSS}}
. Following this proof strategy, when
d
is fixed, the order
(n^{2}\sigma^{d})\wedge n
appearing in
Theorem

1.1
has a very natural geometrical interpretation: it is the order of the size of the largest matching of initial positions within distance
\Theta(\sigma)
.

Figure 1
:
A small window of a simulation of the performance of
\hat{\pi}_{\mathrm{LSS}}
on a sample following (
1.1
) of total size
n=3000
with
X_{1}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
,
Z_{1}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}I_{d})
for two different values of
\sigma^{2}
. Arrows indicate to which initial position
X_{i}
a given
Y_{j}
was associated by
\hat{\pi}_{\mathrm{LSS}}
. Red arrows indicate that the estimated match for the given point was incorrect, while grey arrows indicate that the estimated match was correct. Blue lines between pairs of initial positions indicate that they are within a distance of
r=\sqrt{2}\sigma
of each other. Note that most incorrect matches involve initial positions that share an edge on
G(n,r,d,\left\|{\,\cdot\,}\right\|_{2})
. Our strategy to prove our lower bounds takes inspiration from this observation. The code to generate this image can be found at
https://github.com/Lucas-Schwengber/particle_tracking
.

Although to the best of our knowledge understanding matchings in random geometric graphs has not received direct attention in previous works,
Lemma

1.2
is not significantly far from what is already well-known in the literature. For instance, if
d
,
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{Q}
are fixed,
r\to 0
and
n^{2}r^{d}\to+\infty
, one can use general results of the classical monograph by Penrose
[
15
, Propositions 3.2 and 3.3]
to count isolated edges in a random geometric graph, giving a lower bound on the size of its largest matching which is sharp up to constant factors. For our purposes, the main advantage of Lemma
1.2
is that it is general, nonasymptotic and can be made to work in growing dimensions. Moreover, its proof is simple and constructive.

Remark 1.3
.

Our approach to establish lower bounds is different from what was done in
[
11
]
where the authors look at matchings in a different random graph, which is built by adding edges between the endpoints of augmenting
2
-cycles (see
Section

B.1
below for a definition). There is a direct connection between the two approaches, which we outline in
Appendix

B
. In particular, when
d=o(\log n)
or
d
is fixed, our techniques are able to recover the lower bounds from
[
11
]
up to factors of order
n^{o(1)}
, while also working under greater generality (see
Theorem

B.1
).

Remark 1.4
.

The proof strategy of our negative results also differs from the one in
[
24
]
where the authors either count augmenting
2
-cycles as in
[
11
]
or use classical tools from information theory. But one can also prove a version of
Theorem

1.1
that gives a lower bound in probability for the performance of any estimator, thus allowing us to derive negative results that complement and extend the negative results from
[
24
]
(see
Corollary

B.6
in
Appendix

B
) when
d
is fixed or
d=o(\log n)
.

Remark 1.5
.

We note that, similar to this work,
[
2
]
and
[
6
]
also established information-theoretical limits by analyzing the distances between uncorrupted feature vectors. However their conditions are stated in terms of the minimum distance between uncorrupted feature vectors.

Remark 1.6
.

Although the negative results established in this paper concern the case when
d=o(\log n)
, we believe our strategy can also lead to non-trivial results when
d=\Omega(\log n)
. In particular, in
Appendix

C
we showcase how they can be used to partially recover the lower bounds from
[
11
]
in the regime where
d=\Theta(\log n)
.

Our next result shows that the LSS estimator achieves minimax optimal rates in a slightly more restrictive setting than
LD
. Specifically, let
LD
+S
be
LD
with the additional assumptions that:

3.

\left\|{f_{\mathcal{P}}}\right\|_{\infty}\leq e^{\beta d}
, for the same
\beta
as in assumption
1
.

4.

The distribution of the
\tilde{Z}_{i}
’s satisfy:

\sup\limits_{\left\|{v}\right\|=1}\left\|{\left\langle{\tilde{Z}_{i}},{v}\right\rangle}\right\|_{\psi_{2}}\leq K_{Q}\text{ and }\mathbb{E}[\tilde{Z}_{i}]=0.

(1.8)

Under these additional assumptions, we have the following upper bound on
\mathbb{E}\left[d_{H}\left({\hat{\pi}_{\mathrm{LSS}}},{\pi^{\star}}\right)\right]
:

Theorem 1.7

(LSS error upper bound (proof in §
4.1
))
.

Under
LD
+S , for all
n\geq 2
,

\mathbb{E}\left[d_{H}\left({\hat{\pi}_{\mathrm{LSS}}},{\pi^{\star}}\right)\right]\leq(3K^{d}n^{2}\sigma^{d})\wedge n,

(1.9)

where
K=b_{\mathrm{LD}}\left\|{f_{\mathcal{P}}}\right\|_{\infty}^{\frac{2}{d}}K_{\mathcal{Q}}^{2}e^{2\beta}
for some absolute constant
b_{\mathrm{LD}}>0
.

In
Section

2.2
below we show that if
d
is fixed we can improve our constant
3K^{d}
to a constant
\tau_{d,\mathcal{P},\mathcal{Q}}
which we conjecture to be sharp when
n^{2}\sigma^{d}\ll n
(see
Figure

2
). This constant depends very mildly on the geometrical properties of
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{Q}
thus suggesting that when
n^{2}\sigma^{d}=o(n)
,
\mathbb{E}\left[d_{H}\left({\hat{\pi}_{\mathrm{LSS}}},{\pi^{\star}}\right)\right]
behaves as in the Gaussian model for a broad class of distributions.

The proof of
Theorem

1.7
follows a similar strategy as in
[
11
]
. Given a
t
-cycle
C_{t}=(i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{t})
of distinct elements from
[n]
we say that it is an
augmenting cycle
(for
\hat{\pi}_{\mathrm{LSS}}
), or just
augmenting
, if:

\sum_{k=1}^{t}\langle X_{\pi^{*}(i_{k})},Y_{i_{k+1}}\rangle\geq\sum_{k=1}^{t}\langle X_{\pi^{*}(i_{k})},Y_{i_{k}}\rangle,

(1.10)

where
i_{t+1}:=i_{1}
. If we assume that
\pi^{\star}=\text{Id}
, all cycles of size
2
or greater on the cycle decomposition of
\hat{\pi}_{\mathrm{LSS}}
will be mistakes. Moreover, these cycles must be
augmenting cycles
, otherwise they would not appear in the optimal assignment
\hat{\pi}_{\mathrm{LSS}}
. It follows that we have the following deterministic bound:

d_{H}\left({\hat{\pi}_{\mathrm{LSS}}},{\pi^{\star}}\right)\leq\sum_{t=2}^{n}\sum_{C_{t}}t\mathbf{1}_{[C_{t}\text{ is augmenting}]},

(1.11)

where the inner sum is over all
t
-cycles
C_{t}
.

To bound
\mathbb{E}\left[d_{H}\left({\hat{\pi}_{\mathrm{LSS}}},{\pi^{\star}}\right)\right]
, we bound the probability that
t
-cycles are augmenting. Similarly to
[
11
]
our upper bounds on these probabilities involve bounding the expectation of exponentials of certain quadratic forms on the initial positions. Unlike in the Gaussian setting of
[
11
]
, we cannot evaluate these expectations explicitly. Fortunately, the assumptions in
LD
+S are good enough for calculations to go through.

In
Section

2
we show that Theorems
1.1
and
1.7
can be combined to obtain optimal rates for some natural choices of
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{Q}
. One such rate is when both
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{Q}
have continuous and bounded densities with
d
fixed. Another case is the Gaussian model studied in
[
11
]
in the regime where
d=o(\log n)
, where our bounds are optimal up to
n^{o(1)}
factors. We additionally consider the cases of
\mathcal{P}
uniform or from the Laplace family.

1.1.2
High-dimension

Our next results concern the high-dimensional setting. As mentioned above, this is a natural toy model for
deduplication
and
entity resolution
between high-dimensional datasets
[
5
]
.

Model HD
.

In this setup we let
n\to+\infty
while making the following assumptions in (
1.1
):

1.

d=\omega(\log n)
.

2.

X_{1},\dots,X_{n}=(\Sigma_{X})^{\frac{1}{2}}\tilde{X}_{1},\dots,(\Sigma_{X})^{\frac{1}{2}}\tilde{X}_{n}
and
Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n}=(\Sigma_{Z})^{\frac{1}{2}}\tilde{Z}_{1},\dots,(\Sigma_{Z})^{\frac{1}{2}}\tilde{Z}_{n}
where:

(a)

\tilde{X}_{1},\dots,\tilde{X}_{n}
are i.i.d. and independent from
\tilde{Z}_{1},\dots,\tilde{Z}_{n}
, which are also i.i.d.

(b)

Both
\tilde{X}_{1}
and
\tilde{Z}_{1}
have independent coordinates with:

\mathbb{E}[\tilde{X}_{1}[j]]=0,\ \mathbb{E}[\tilde{Z}_{1}[j]]=0,\,\forall j\in[d],

(1.12)

and,

\max_{j\in[d]}\left\|{\tilde{X}_{1}[j]}\right\|_{\psi_{2}}\leq K_{X}\text{, }\ \max_{j\in[d]}\left\|{\tilde{Z}_{1}[j]}\right\|_{\psi_{2}}\leq K_{Z},

(1.13)

for constants
K_{X},K_{Z}>0
that do not depend on
n
.

(c)

\Sigma_{X}=\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diag}\left(\sigma_{X}[1],\dots,\sigma_{X}[d]\right)
and
\Sigma_{Z}=\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diag}\left(\sigma_{Z}[1],\dots,\sigma_{Z}[d]\right)
are diagonal matrices with strictly positive diagonal entries.

We leave implicit the dependence on
n
of the distribution of the
X_{i}
’s,
Z_{i}
’s and
Y_{i}
’s as well as
\Sigma_{X}
and
\Sigma_{Z}
.
Under the above model we have the following result:

Theorem 1.8

(Perfect recovery for LSS in high-dimensions (proof in §
4.2
))
.

Under
HD
, suppose
\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{tr}\left(\Sigma_{Z}\Sigma_{X}\right)}{\left\|{\Sigma_{Z}\Sigma_{X}}\right\|_{op}}=\omega(\log n)
,
\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{tr}\left(\Sigma_{X}\right)}{\left\|{\Sigma_{X}}\right\|_{op}}=\omega(\log n)
and let,

\tilde{\sigma}^{2}_{X}[j]=\frac{\sigma^{2}_{X}[j]}{\sum\limits_{k=1}^{d}\sigma^{2}_{X}[k]},\penalty\ j\in[d].

(1.14)

There exists an absolute constant
b_{\mathrm{HD}}>0
such that if

b_{\mathrm{HD}}K^{2}_{Z}\leq\log n^{-1}\frac{\sum\limits_{j=1}^{d}\sigma^{2}_{X}[j]}{\left(\sum\limits_{j=1}^{d}\tilde{\sigma}^{2}_{X}[j]\sigma^{2}_{Z}[j]\right)}

(1.15)

then
d_{H}\left({\hat{\pi}_{\mathrm{LSS}}},{\pi^{\star}}\right)=0
with high probability.

This theorem establishes a sufficient condition for perfect recovery for an estimator which is agnostic to any information on the distribution of the noise and its covariance structure. It extends the results on the high-dimensional setting from
[
11
]
. This condition can be slightly improved if we assume knowledge of
\Sigma_{Z}
. We consider a modified version of LSS, LSS-C, which incorporates the information from
\Sigma_{Z}
. It is defined as:

\displaystyle\hat{\pi}_{\mathrm{LSS-C}}

\displaystyle:=\underset{\pi\in S_{n}}{\text{arg min}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left\|{(\Sigma_{Z})^{-\frac{1}{2}}(X_{\pi(j)}-Y_{j})}\right\|_{2}^{2}

(1.16)

\displaystyle=\underset{\pi\in S_{n}}{\text{arg max}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left\langle(\Sigma_{Z})^{-1}X_{\pi(j)},Y_{j}\right\rangle.

Regarding this estimator we have the following sufficient condition for perfect recovery:

Theorem 1.9

(Perfect recovery for LSS-C in high-dimensions (proof in §
4.2
))
.

Under
HD
, suppose
\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{tr}\left((\Sigma_{Z})^{-1}\Sigma_{X}\right)}{\left\|{(\Sigma_{Z})^{-1}\Sigma_{X}}\right\|_{op}}=\omega(\log n)
and let
(\tilde{\sigma}^{2}_{X}[j])_{j=1}^{d}
and
b_{\mathrm{HD}}
be as in
Theorem

1.8
. If

\begin{split}b_{\text{HD}}K^{2}_{Z}&\leq\log n^{-1}\frac{\sum\limits_{j=1}^{d}\sigma^{2}_{X}[j]}{\left(\sum\limits_{j=1}^{d}\tilde{\sigma}^{2}_{X}[j]\sigma^{-2}_{Z}[j]\right)^{-1}},\end{split}

(1.17)

then
d_{H}\left({\hat{\pi}_{\mathrm{LSS-C}}},{\pi^{\star}}\right)=0
with high probability.

The terms on the right hand side of
Equation

1.15
and
Equation

1.17
play the role of signal-to-noise ratios. When
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{iid}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
and
Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{iid}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}I_{d})
they simplify to

\frac{d}{\log n\sigma^{2}}
recovering the signal-to-noise ratio from the high-dimensional setting in
[
11
]
. In
[
11
]
the authors conjecture that for the Gaussian case there exists a phase transition as
n\to+\infty
from
partial recovery
(
i.e.
\mathbb{E}\left[d_{H}\left({\hat{\pi}_{\mathrm{LSS}}},{\pi^{\star}}\right)\right]=\Omega(n)
) when
\frac{d}{\log n\sigma^{2}}<4
to
perfect recovery
(
i.e.
\mathbb{E}\left[d_{H}\left({\hat{\pi}_{\mathrm{LSS}}},{\pi^{\star}}\right)\right]=o(1)
) when

\frac{d}{\log n\sigma^{2}}>4
. Similar to our results, they prove the sufficient condition for perfect recovery. However showing sharp necessary conditions for perfect recovery by
\hat{\pi}_{\mathrm{LSS}}
when
d=\omega(\log n)
is still an open problem. In
[
24
]
Appendix E.1 the authors show that when
d=\omega(\log n)
, any estimator that achieves
strong recovery
(
i.e.
\mathbb{E}\left[d_{H}\left({\hat{\pi}_{\mathrm{LSS}}},{\pi^{\star}}\right)\right]=o(n)
) must have
\frac{d}{\log n\sigma^{2}}>2
. This seems to settle the information theoretic threshold for
strong recovery
in this regime as this condition is conjectured to be sufficient for the greedy inner product algorithm studied in
[
11
]
Appendix A.2 to achieve
strong recovery
.

The difference between
Theorem

1.8
and
Theorem

1.9
lies in how the variance of the coordinates of the initial positions and noise interact. In
Theorem

1.8
the noise term includes a weighted arithmetic mean of the variances of the noise vector with weights given by the proportion of each coordinate on the signal term. The noise term in
Theorem

1.9
has a similar interpretation but with a weighted harmonic mean. Since the harmonic mean is always smaller than the arithmetic mean, the signal-to-noise term in
Theorem

1.9
dominates the one from
Theorem

1.8
, as expected.
The conditions,

\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{tr}\left(\Sigma_{Z}\Sigma_{X}\right)}{\left\|{\Sigma_{Z}\Sigma_{X}}\right\|_{op}}=\omega(\log n),\,\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{tr}\left(\Sigma_{X}\right)}{\left\|{\Sigma_{X}}\right\|_{op}}=\omega(\log n)\text{ and }\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{tr}\left((\Sigma_{Z})^{-1}\Sigma_{X}\right)}{\left\|{(\Sigma_{Z})^{-1}\Sigma_{X}}\right\|_{op}}=\omega(\log n)

(1.18)

are technical conditions on the stable rank of
\Sigma_{Z}\Sigma_{X}
,
\Sigma_{X}
and
(\Sigma_{Z})^{-1}\Sigma_{X}
respectively. These conditions roughly state that the sum of the entries of these diagonal matrices cannot be too concentrated along a small subset of coordinates. All three conditions necessarily require that
d=\omega(\log n)
. For
\Sigma_{X}
this condition can be interpreted as saying that we must
effectively
be in a high-dimensional setting by ruling out scenarios where we might have
d=\omega(\log n)
but our signal is concentrated in a low-dimensional subspace. If
\Sigma_{X}
is isotropic, the first condition in (
1.18
) rules out the possibility of the strength of noise being concentrated along a low-dimensional subset of coordinates and the last condition rules out the possibility of there being a small subset of coordinates with a level of noise much smaller than the other ones.

Before proceeding, we note that the proofs of our high-dimensional results also rely on the concept of augmenting cycles that was outlined in the discussion of
Theorem

1.7
. The main difference is that we must use the Hanson-Wright inequality to show that the quadratic forms on the initial positions concentrate around their expected value. This allows us to obtain sharp estimates as the dimension grows.

2.1
Gaussian initial positions and noise

We first consider the Gaussian model from
[
11
]
and show that the rate obtained for the MLE is informational theoretical optimal up to factors of order
n^{o(1)}
. More precisely we have:

Proposition 2.1

(Low-dimension, Gaussian initial positions, Gaussian noise)
.

In (
1.1
), let
d=o(\log n)
,
X_{1}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
and
Z_{1}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}I_{d})
. Then as

\inf_{\hat{\pi}}\mathbb{E}\left[d_{H}\left({\hat{\pi}},{\pi^{\star}}\right)\right]=(n^{(2+o(1))}\sigma^{d})\wedge n^{(1+o(1))}.

(2.1)

Proof.

If we take
\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
and
R_{d}=\sqrt{2d}
, we have that for all sufficiently large
d
, assumption
1
from
LD
holds with
\|\,\cdot\,\|=\|\,\cdot\,\|_{2}
,
\gamma=\frac{1}{2}
and
\beta=1
. If we let
\mathcal{Q}=\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
, using Bretagnolle–Huber inequality
[
1
]
and the formula for the Kullback-Leibler divergence between multivariate Gaussian distributions we have:

\begin{split}\left\|{\mathcal{Q}(v)\otimes\mathcal{Q}(-v)-\mathcal{Q}\otimes\mathcal{Q}}\right\|_{TV}&\leq 1-\frac{1}{2}\exp\left(-\left\|{v}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right),\end{split}

(2.2)

and so assumption
2
holds with
\beta=2+\log(2)
. From
Theorem

1.1
it follows that:

\inf_{\hat{\pi}}\mathbb{E}\left[d_{H}\left({\hat{\pi}},{\pi^{\star}}\right)\right]=\Omega\left((n^{(2-o(1))}\sigma^{d}))\wedge n^{(1-o(1))}\right).

(2.3)

Since
\left\|{f_{\mathcal{P}}}\right\|_{\infty}\leq(2\pi)^{-\frac{d}{2}}
and
\mathcal{Q}
is sub-Gaussian and zero-mean, assumptions
3
and
4
are also satisfied. It follows from
Theorem

1.7
that,

\inf_{\hat{\pi}}\mathbb{E}\left[d_{H}\left({\hat{\pi}},{\pi^{\star}}\right)\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[d_{H}\left({\hat{\pi}_{\mathrm{LSS}}},{\pi^{\star}}\right)\right]=O\left((n^{(2+o(1))}\sigma^{d}))\wedge n^{(1+o(1))}\right),

(2.4)

from which the result follows.
∎

Note that our upper bound for the Gaussian model is slightly worse than the one in
[
11
]
because of an extra

factor. This is natural since our results make use of less properties of the distribution of the noise. On other other hand, if

is fixed we can ignore the

terms in the exponent and thus obtain that the optimal error rate is of order
\Theta\left((n^{2}\sigma^{d})\wedge n\right)
. This in particular confirms that the upper bound for the expected number of mistakes made by the greedy distance algorithm analyzed in Appendix A.1 of
[
11

is sharp.

Uniformly distributed initial positions

Proposition 2.4

(Low-dimension, uniform initial positions, noise with i.i.d. sub-Gaussian coordinates)

In (
1.1

X_{1}\sim\mathrm{Unif}([-1,1]^{d})

\inf_{\hat{\pi}}\mathbb{E}\left[d_{H}\left({\hat{\pi}},{\pi^{\star}}\right)\right]=(n^{(2+o(1))}\sigma^{d})\wedge n^{(1+o(1))}.

\mathcal{P}=\text{Unif}([-1,1]^{d})

2.4
Noise with a log-Lipschitz density

We consider a lower bound in the regime where
d=o(\log n)
with initial positions following a Laplace distribution and a distribution on the noise which is compatible with this assumption.

Proposition 2.5

(Low-dimension, Laplace initial positions, noise with log-Lipschitz density)
.

In (
1.1
), let
d=o(\log n)
and assume that
X_{1}
has a standard multivariate Laplace distribution, i.e.,

X_{1}[1],\dots,X_{1}[d]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{iid}}}{{\sim}}\mathrm{Lap}(0,1),

(2.17)

where
\mathrm{Lap}(0,1)
is the standard Laplace distribution on
\mathbb{R}
. Assume also that
\mathcal{Q}
has a continuous density
f_{\mathcal{Q}}
such that
\log(f_{\mathcal{Q}})
is

-Lipschitz on the
\left\|{\,\cdot\,}\right\|_{1}
norm. Then as
n\to+\infty
,

\inf_{\hat{\pi}}\mathbb{E}\left[d_{H}\left({\hat{\pi}},{\pi^{\star}}\right)\right]=\Omega((n^{(2-o(1))}\sigma^{d})\wedge n^{(1-o(1))}).

(2.18)

Proof.

Note that
|X_{1}[j]|\sim\text{Exp}(1)
and so
\left\|{X_{1}}\right\|_{1}
is sub-Exponential with
\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|{X_{1}}\right\|_{1}\right]=d
. It follows that condition
1
from
LD
hold for sufficiently large
d
taking
R_{d}=2d,\gamma=\frac{1}{2}
and
\beta=2
. Since
\mathcal{Q}
has a continuous density
f_{\mathcal{Q}}
such that

is

\left\|{\,\cdot\,}\right\|_{1}

\begin{split}D_{KL}(\mathcal{Q}\otimes\mathcal{Q}||\mathcal{Q}(v)\otimes\mathcal{Q}(-v))&=D_{KL}(\mathcal{Q}||\mathcal{Q}(v))+D_{KL}(\mathcal{Q}||\mathcal{Q}(-v))\\
&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(\log(f_{\mathcal{Q}}(z))-\log(f_{\mathcal{Q}}(z+v)))f_{\mathcal{Q}}(z)dz\\
&+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(\log(f_{\mathcal{Q}}(z))-\log(f_{\mathcal{Q}}(z-v)))f_{\mathcal{Q}}(z)dz\\
&\leq 2L\left\|{v}\right\|_{1}.\end{split}

(2.19)

It follows by the Bretagnolle–Huber inequality that,

\left\|{\mathcal{Q}(v)\otimes\mathcal{Q}(-v)-\mathcal{Q}\otimes\mathcal{Q}}\right\|_{TV}\leq 1-\frac{1}{2}\exp\left(-2L\left\|{v}\right\|_{1}\right),

(2.20)

and so condition
2
holds taking
\beta=4L+\log(2)
. So

holds and we have a lower bound of order
\Omega\left(n^{(2-o(1))}\sigma^{d})\wedge n^{(1-o(1))}\right)

∎

\mathcal{Q}

\hat{\pi}_{\mathrm{LSS}}
is also nearly sharp, as
\mathcal{Q}
fails to be sub-Gaussian but it still is sub-Exponential. Although our techniques to prove the upper bounds cannot be directly extended to this case we believe the rate should have the same behaviour, but leave this question for future work.

In this section we prove
Theorem

1.1
and
Lemma

1.2
. We first introduce some notation for this section. Given a finite collection of points
\mathcal{X}_{n}=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d},r>0
and a norm
\|\,\cdot\,\|
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we define a geometric graph as a graph
G(n,r,d,\|\,\cdot\,\|)=G(\mathcal{X}_{n},r,d,\|\,\cdot\,\|)
with,

V(G(n,r,d,\|\,\cdot\,\|))=[n]\text{ and }E(G(n,r,d,\|\,\cdot\,\|))=\{\{i,j\}\subseteq[n];\penalty\ i\neq j\text{ and }\left\|{x_{i}-x_{j}}\right\|<r\}.

(3.1)

Following the convention of the literature, we leave the dependence of
r
and
d
on

implicit.
If we take:

\mathcal{X}_{n}=\{X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\}\text{ with }X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{iid}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{P},

(3.2)

where
\mathcal{P}
is a distribution in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we call the corresponding random graph
G(n,r,d,\|\,\cdot\,\|)
a random geometric graph (RGG). As noted previously, asymptotic properties of random geometric graphs have been previously explored in several works, see for instance the book
[
15
]
for results in fixed dimension and
[
7
]
for the high-dimensional regime (
i.e.
d=\omega(\log n)
).

Recall that a matching in a graph
G
is a subset of edges
M\subset E(G)
with no vertices in common,
i.e.
such that
e\cap e^{\prime}=\emptyset
for any pair of distinct
e,e^{\prime}
from
M
. We let
M_{r}=M_{r}(\mathcal{X}_{n})
denote the largest matching on
G(\mathcal{X}_{n},r,d,\|\,\cdot\,\|)
.

3.1
A general lower bound

The next result gives us a general method to move from large matchings in
G(n,r,d,\|\,\cdot\,\|)
to lower bounds on
\inf_{\hat{\pi}}\mathbb{E}\left[d_{H}\left({\hat{\pi}},{\pi^{\star}}\right)\right]
.
For a distribution
\mathcal{Q}
over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and a vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, let
\mathcal{Q}(v)
denote the law of
\tilde{Z}+v
where
\tilde{Z}\sim\mathcal{Q}
and set:

t_{s}(\mathcal{Q},\|\,\cdot\,\|)=t_{s}(\mathcal{Q}):=\left(1-\sup_{\|v\|<s}\left\|{\mathcal{Q}(v)\otimes\mathcal{Q}(-v)-\mathcal{Q}\otimes\mathcal{Q}}\right\|_{TV}\right).

(3.3)

Our lower bound is as follows:

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be distinct. Given
\sigma>0
set,

Y_{i}=x_{\pi^{\star}(i)}+\sigma\tilde{Z}_{\pi^{\star}(i)},\penalty\ i\in[n],

(3.4)

where
\pi^{\star}\sim{\rm Unif}(S_{n})
is generated uniformly at random and (independently)
\tilde{Z}_{1},\dots,\tilde{Z}_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{iid}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{Q}
, for
\mathcal{Q}
a distribution over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then, for any estimator,

\hat{\pi}=\hat{\pi}(x_{1},\dots x_{n},Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n})\in S_{n},

(3.5)

the following holds:

\mathbb{E}\left[d_{H}\left({\hat{\pi}},{\pi^{\star}}\right)\right]\geq\frac{|M_{r}|t_{\frac{r}{\sigma}}(\mathcal{Q})}{2},

(3.6)

for
t_{s}(\mathcal{Q})
as in (
3.3
) above and
M_{r}=M_{r}(\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\})
the largest matching in
G(\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\},r,d,\|\,\cdot\,\|)
.

The Lemma above gives a lower bound which takes into account two effects:
|M_{r}|
accounts for pairs of close initial positions, while
t_{\frac{r}{\sigma}}(\mathcal{Q})
accounts for the difficulty of distinguishing their associated noisy versions. The free parameter
r
controls the trade-off between these two quantities: as
r
increases we have more matched initial positions but their noisy versions become more distinguishable.

Proof of
Lemma

3.1
.

Given
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\sigma>0
we let
\mathcal{Q}(v,\sigma)
denote the law of
v+\sigma\tilde{Z}
for
\tilde{Z}\sim\mathcal{Q}
. In particular,
\mathcal{Q}(v)=\mathcal{Q}(v,1)
.
We assume for ease of notation that
M_{r}=\{\{2j-1,2j\}\}_{j=1}^{m}
, but the general case follows analogously. Letting
\Omega=\{0,1\}^{m}
, we associate to each element of
\Omega
a permutation
\pi_{\omega}\in S_{n}
given by:

\pi_{\omega}=\prod_{j=1}^{m}((2j-1)2j)^{\omega_{j}}.

(3.7)

In other words,
\pi_{\omega}
swaps
(2j-1)
and
2j
if
\omega_{j}=1
and leave all other indices unchanged. Denote by
S_{\Omega}\subseteq S_{n}
the subset of permutations of this form. We associate to each
\omega\in\Omega
a probability measure
P_{\omega}
on
(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{n}
given by:

P_{\omega}=\left(\bigotimes_{j=1}^{m}\left(\mathcal{Q}\left(x_{\pi_{\omega}(2j-1)},\sigma\right)\otimes\mathcal{Q}\left(x_{\pi_{\omega}(2j)},\sigma\right)\right)\right)\otimes\bigotimes_{k=2m+1}^{n}\mathcal{Q}\left(x_{k},\sigma\right).

(3.8)

That is, letting
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}
be random vectors with joint distribution
P_{\omega}
, we have that for
j\in[m]
, if
\omega_{j}=0
then,

Y_{2j-1}=x_{2j-1}+\sigma\tilde{Z}_{2j-1},\ Y_{2j}=x_{2j}+\sigma\tilde{Z}_{2j},

(3.9)

if
\omega_{j}=1
then,

Y_{2j-1}=x_{2j}+\sigma\tilde{Z}_{2j-1},\ Y_{2j}=x_{2j-1}+\sigma\tilde{Z}_{2j},

(3.10)

and for
k=2m+1,\dots,n
,

Y_{k}=x_{k}+\sigma\tilde{Z}_{k},

(3.11)

where
\tilde{Z}_{1},\dots,\tilde{Z}_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{iid}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{Q}
. Note that if
\omega
and
\omega^{\prime}
differ by a single coordinate
j
then:

\begin{split}\left\|{P_{\omega}-P_{\omega^{\prime}}}\right\|_{TV}&=\left\|{\mathcal{Q}\left(x_{\pi_{\omega}(2j-1)},\sigma\right)\otimes\mathcal{Q}\left(x_{\pi_{\omega}(2j)},\sigma\right)-\mathcal{Q}\left(x_{\pi_{\omega^{\prime}}(2j-1)},\sigma\right)\otimes\mathcal{Q}\left(x_{\pi_{\omega^{\prime}}(2j)},\sigma\right)}\right\|_{TV}\\
&=\left\|{\mathcal{Q}\left(\frac{x_{\pi_{\omega}(2j-1)}}{\sigma}\right)\otimes\mathcal{Q}\left(\frac{x_{\pi_{\omega}(2j)}}{\sigma}\right)-\mathcal{Q}\left(\frac{x_{\pi_{\omega^{\prime}}(2j-1)}}{\sigma}\right)\otimes\mathcal{Q}\left(\frac{x_{\pi_{\omega^{\prime}}(2j)}}{\sigma}\right)}\right\|_{TV}\\
&\leq\sup_{\left\|{v}\right\|<\frac{r}{\sigma}}\left\|{\mathcal{Q}(v)\otimes\mathcal{Q}(-v)-\mathcal{Q}\otimes\mathcal{Q}}\right\|_{TV}=1-t_{\frac{r}{\sigma}}(\mathcal{Q})\end{split}

(3.12)

where the second equality and third inequality follow since the total variation distance is invariant under translations and dilations respectively and in the last inequality we also used the fact that
\|x_{2j}-x_{2j-1}\|<r
.
Suppose we observe
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}\sim P_{\omega}
from an unknown
\omega
. Given
\hat{\omega}
an estimator for
\omega
based on
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
and
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}
, it follows by Assouad’s Lemma
[
21
]
and (
3.12
) that:

\inf_{\hat{\omega}}\max_{\omega\in\Omega}\mathbb{E}_{\omega}\left[d_{H}\left({\hat{\omega}},{\omega}\right)\right]\geq\frac{mt_{\frac{r}{\sigma}}(\mathcal{Q})}{2},

(3.13)

where
\mathbb{E}_{\omega}\left[\,\cdot\,\right]
denotes expectations under
P_{\omega}
and the infimum is taken over all (possibly non-deterministic) estimators
\hat{\omega}
.

We can convert any estimator
\hat{\pi}
for a hidden permutation
\pi^{\star}
based on
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
and
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}
into an estimator
\hat{\omega}^{(\hat{\pi})}
for
\omega
by setting:

\hat{\omega}^{(\hat{\pi})}_{j}=\begin{cases}1,\penalty\ \text{if $\hat{\pi}(2j-1)=2j$ and $\hat{\pi}(2j)=2j-1$}\\
0,\penalty\ \text{if $\hat{\pi}(2j-1)=2j-1$ and $\hat{\pi}(2j)=2j$}\\
\epsilon,\penalty\ \text{otherwise}\end{cases},

(3.14)

where
\epsilon
indicates an error regardless of the true
\omega_{j}
. For any
\omega\in\Omega
,
\hat{\omega}^{(\hat{\pi})}_{j}\neq\omega_{j}
implies that
\hat{\pi}(2j)\neq\pi_{\omega}(2j)
or
\hat{\pi}(2j-1)\neq\pi_{\omega}(2j-1)
. It follows that:

\begin{split}d_{H}\left({\hat{\pi}},{\pi_{\omega}}\right)&=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}_{[\hat{\pi}(i)\neq\pi_{\omega}(i)]}\\
&\geq\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathbf{1}_{[\hat{\pi}(2j)\neq\pi_{\omega}(2j)]}+\mathbf{1}_{[\hat{\pi}_{\omega}(2j-1)\neq\pi_{\omega}(2j-1)]}\\
&\geq d_{H}\left({\hat{\omega}^{(\hat{\pi})}},{\omega}\right),\end{split}

(3.15)

and so,

\inf_{\hat{\pi}}\max_{\pi_{\omega}\in S_{\Omega}}\mathbb{E}\left[d_{H}\left({\hat{\pi}},{\pi_{\omega}}\right)|\pi^{\star}=\pi_{\omega}\right]\geq\frac{mt_{\frac{r}{\sigma}}(\mathcal{Q})}{2},

(3.16)

where expectations are taken with respect to (
3.4
).
Since
S_{\Omega}\subseteq S_{n}
,

\inf_{\hat{\pi}}\max_{\pi\in S_{n}}\mathbb{E}\left[d_{H}\left({\hat{\pi}},{\pi}\right)|\pi^{\star}=\pi\right]\geq\frac{mt_{\frac{r}{\sigma}}(\mathcal{Q})}{2}.

(3.17)

This proves the minimax lower bound. Note that we could take
\hat{\pi}
to be a non-deterministic estimator and the argument still follows. Also, due to the structure of the problem, we always have that:

\inf_{\hat{\pi}}\max_{\pi\in S_{n}}\mathbb{E}\left[d_{H}\left({\hat{\pi}},{\pi}\right)|\pi^{\star}=\pi\right]=\inf_{\hat{\pi}}\mathbb{E}\left[d_{H}\left({\hat{\pi}},{\pi^{*}}\right)\right],

(3.18)

where the infimum is taken over all (possibly non-deterministic) estimators
\hat{\pi}
. We provide a proof of (
3.18
) on
Appendix

A
in a more general setting. From (
3.18
) the result follows.
∎

3.2
Matchings in random geometric graphs

Our next step to use
Lemma

3.1
to prove
Theorem

1.1
is proving
Lemma

1.2
. As in the Introduction, we consider a random geometric graph
G(n,r,d,\|\,\cdot\,\|)=G(\mathcal{X}_{n},r,d,\|\,\cdot\,\|)
induced by
\mathcal{X}_{n}=\{X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\}
, where
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{iid}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{P}
a distribution with density
f_{\mathcal{P}}
satisfying assumption
1
from
LD
.

Proof of
Lemma

1.2
.

Let

l=\frac{2R_{d}e^{-\beta}}{(n-1)^{\frac{1}{d}}}.

(3.19)

Assume first that
r\leq l
, which implies in particular that
r<R_{d}
since
\beta\geq\log(2)
. Let
B_{d}=B_{\|\,\cdot\,\|}\left(0,R_{d}\right)
be such that
\mathcal{P}\left(B_{d}\right)\geq\gamma>0
. Let
k_{n}
be the maximum number of non-overlapping (open) balls of radius
\frac{r}{2}
(on norm
\|\,\cdot\,\|
) that are entirely contained in
B_{d}
. Since
r<R_{d}
,
k_{n}
is at least the size of the largerst
r
-packing in
B_{\|\,\cdot\,\|}\left(0,\frac{R_{d}}{2}\right)
. Following the volumetric lower bound for the size of the
r
-packing number in
[
25
, Theorem 14.2]
, we obtain

\left(\frac{R_{d}}{2r}\right)^{d}\leq k_{n}.

(3.20)

Let
\mathcal{K}_{n}=\{B_{i,d}\}_{i=1}^{k_{n}}
be a collection of
k_{n}
non-overlapping balls of radius
\frac{r}{2}
fully contained in
B_{d}
. Denote by
N=|\mathcal{X}_{n}\cap B_{d}|
the number of samples from
\mathcal{X}_{n}=\{X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\}
that fall inside
B_{d}
, and define

U_{i,d}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}_{[X_{j}\in B_{i,d}]}=\left|\mathcal{X}_{n}\cap B_{i,d}\right|,\penalty\ i\in[k_{n}],

(3.21)

the number of points from
\mathcal{X}_{n}
that fall inside
B_{i,d}
. It is clear that, conditionally on
N
,
U_{i,d}\sim\textnormal{Bin}(N,p_{i,d})
, where
p_{i,d}=\mathbb{P}\left(X_{1}\in B_{i,d}|X_{1}\in B_{d}\right)
for each
i\in[k_{n}]
. Moreover,

e^{-\beta d}\left(\frac{r}{2R_{d}}\right)^{d}\leq p_{i,d}\leq e^{\beta d}\left(\frac{r}{2R_{d}}\right)^{d},

(3.22)

since
\frac{\text{ess sup}_{x\in B_{d}}f_{\mathcal{P}}(x)}{\text{ess inf}_{x\in B_{d}}f_{\mathcal{P}}(x)}\leq e^{\beta d}
by assumption. Let

I_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{k_{n}}\mathbf{1}_{[U_{i,d}=2]},

(3.23)

be the number of balls
B_{i,d}\in\mathcal{K}_{n}
that have exactly
2
elements from
\mathcal{X}_{n}
. Note that
|M_{r}|\geq I_{n}
since we can build a matching in
G(n,r,d,\|\,\cdot\,\|)
by considering the edges between each pair of points on the same
B_{i,d}
such that
U_{i,d}=2
. Moreover, for
k\in\{0\}\cup[n]
and
n\geq 3
,

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}\left(U_{i,d}=2|N=k\right)&=\binom{k}{2}p^{2}_{i,d}(1-p_{i,d})^{k-2}\\
&\geq\binom{k}{2}p^{2}_{i,d}(1-p_{i,d})^{n-2}\\
&\geq\binom{k}{2}\frac{e^{-2\beta d}}{4}\left(\frac{r}{2R_{d}}\right)^{2d},\end{split}

(3.24)

where we used (
3.22
) to bound
p_{i,d}
above and below and also
e^{\beta d}\left(\frac{r}{2R_{d}}\right)^{d}\leq\frac{1}{n-1}
by our assumption that
r\leq l
, and the fact that
(1-\frac{1}{(n-1)})^{n-1}\geq\frac{1}{4}
if
n\geq 3
. It follows that,

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}\left(U_{i,d}=2\right)&=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{P}\left(U_{i,d}=2|N\right)\right]\\
&\geq\mathbb{E}\left[\binom{N}{2}\right]\frac{e^{-2\beta d}}{4}\left(\frac{r}{2R_{d}}\right)^{2d}\\
&\geq\gamma^{2}\binom{n}{2}\frac{e^{-2\beta d}}{4}\left(\frac{r}{2R_{d}}\right)^{2d},\end{split}

(3.25)

where in the last inequality we used the fact that
N\sim\textnormal{Bin}(n,\mathbb{P}\left(X_{1}\in B_{d}\right))
implies
\mathbb{E}\left[\binom{N}{2}\right]=(\mathbb{P}\left(X_{1}\in B_{d}\right))^{2}\binom{n}{2}
and also
\mathbb{P}\left(X_{1}\in B_{d}\right)\geq\gamma
by assumption.
So,

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\left[|M_{r}|\right]&\geq\mathbb{E}\left[I_{n}\right]\\
&\geq k_{n}\gamma^{2}\binom{n}{2}\frac{e^{-2\beta d}}{4}\left(\frac{r}{2R_{d}}\right)^{2d}\\
&\geq\gamma^{2}\binom{n}{2}\frac{e^{-2\beta d}}{4}\left(\frac{r}{8R_{d}}\right)^{d},\end{split}

(3.26)

where the last inequality follows from (
3.20
). If
r>l
, then
|M_{r}|\geq|M_{l}|
, and so,

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\left[|M_{r}|\right]&\geq\mathbb{E}\left[|M_{l}|\right]\\
&\geq\gamma^{2}\binom{n}{2}\frac{e^{-2\beta d}}{4}\left(\frac{l}{8R_{d}}\right)^{d}\\
&=\gamma^{2}\frac{n}{2}\frac{e^{-2\beta d}}{4}e^{-\beta d}4^{-d}\\
&=\gamma^{2}4^{-d}n\frac{e^{-3\beta d}}{8}.\end{split}

(3.27)

Since
\binom{n}{2}\geq\frac{n^{2}}{4}
for all
n\geq 2
, it follows that for a general
r>0
,

\mathbb{E}\left[|M_{r}|\right]\geq\frac{\gamma^{2}}{16}e^{-d(3\beta+\log(8))}\left(n^{2}\left(\frac{r}{R_{d}}\right)^{d}\wedge n\right),

(3.28)

and so the result follows.
∎

The size of the largest matching
M_{r}
in
G(n,r,d,\|\,\cdot\,\|)
cannot exceed
\lfloor n/2\rfloor
or the total number of edges of
G(n,r,d,\|\,\cdot\,\|)
,
|E(G(n,r,d,\|\,\cdot\,\|))|
. This implies that:

\mathbb{E}\left[|M_{r}|\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[|E(G(n,r,d,\|\,\cdot\,\|))|\right]\wedge\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor.

(3.29)

A simple calculation shows that this implies:

\mathbb{E}\left[|M_{r}|\right]\leq\left(\left\|{f_{\mathcal{P}}}\right\|_{\infty}\text{Vol}(B_{\|\,\cdot\,\|}(0,1))\,r^{d}\binom{n}{2}\right)\wedge\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor.

(3.30)

It follows that for fixed
d
, the order from
Lemma

1.2
is sharp.

We now prove
Theorem

1.1
.

Proof of
Theorem

1.1
.

Under
LD
, using our assumptions on
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{Q}
and applying
Lemma

1.2
to
Lemma

3.1
with

,

(3.31)

4
Upper bounds

In this section we provide the technical results necessary to prove the upper bounds in
Theorem

1.7
,
Proposition

2.3
,
Theorem

1.8
and
Theorem

1.9
.

We first note that conditioned on
\pi^{\star}=\pi
, for some fixed
\pi\in S_{n}
, the law of
d_{H}\left({\hat{\pi}_{\mathrm{LSS}}},{\pi^{\star}}\right)
is the same regardless of the choice of
\pi

[
11
]
. So in this section, unless stated otherwise, we assume without loss of generality that
\pi^{\star}=\text{Id}
. Under this condition we will consider a
t
-cycle
C_{t}=(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})
to be augmenting for
\hat{\pi}_{\mathrm{LSS}}
if and only if:

\sum_{k=1}^{t}\langle X_{i_{k}},Y_{i_{k+1}}\rangle\geq\sum_{k=1}^{t}\langle X_{i_{k}},Y_{i_{k}}\rangle.

(4.1)

The main content of the Lemmata we use to prove our upper bounds are bounds on the probability that
t
-cycles are augmenting. Taking expectations on (
1.11
) we can use these bounds to bound
\mathbb{E}\left[d_{H}\left({\hat{\pi}_{\mathrm{LSS}}},{\pi^{\star}}\right)\right]
.

4.1
Upper bounds for low-dimension

Recall that a random variable
U
is sub-Gaussian
[
22
]
if
\left\|{U}\right\|_{\psi_{2}}<+\infty
, where:

\left\|{U}\right\|_{\psi_{2}}:=\inf\left\{L>0;\penalty\ \mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\frac{U^{2}}{L^{2}}\right)\right]\leq 2\right\}.

(4.2)

Moreover, a random vector
V
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is sub-Gaussian if:

\left\|{V}\right\|_{\psi_{2}}:=\sup_{\|v\|=1}\left\|{\langle V,v\rangle}\right\|_{\psi_{2}}<+\infty.

(4.3)

We have the following bound on the probability that a given
t
-cycle is augmenting:

Lemma 4.1
.

Under
LD
+S , for any
t
-cycle
C_{t}
,

\mathbb{P}\left(C_{t}\text{ is augmenting}\right)\leq 2\left(K\sigma\right)^{d(t-1)},

(4.4)

for
K=b_{\mathrm{LD}}\left\|{f_{\mathcal{P}}}\right\|_{\infty}^{\frac{2}{d}}K_{\mathcal{Q}}^{2}e^{2\beta}
where
b_{\mathrm{LD}}>0
is an absolute constant.

Proof.

Let
\Delta_{1}:=X_{t}-X_{1}
,
\Delta_{i}:=X_{i-1}-X_{i},\penalty\ i=2,\dots,t
,
\underaccent{\bar}{Z}\in\mathbb{R}^{t\times d}
be a matrix with entries
\underaccent{\bar}{Z}[i,j]=Z_{i}[j]
,
i.e.
the matrix whose rows are given by
Z_{i}
, and define the matrices
\underaccent{\bar}{\nnoise},\penalty\ \underaccent{\bar}{\Delta},\penalty\ \underaccent{\bar}{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{t\times d}
analogously. We first assume that
C_{t}=(1,2,\ldots,t)
. It is easy to see that
C_{t}
will be augmenting if and only if,

\sum_{i=1}^{t}\langle\Delta_{i},Z_{i}\rangle\geq\frac{1}{2}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{t}\lVert\Delta_{i}\rVert_{2}^{2}\right).

(4.5)

This is equivalent to,

\langle\underaccent{\bar}{Z},\underaccent{\bar}{\Delta}\rangle\geq\frac{1}{2}\|\underaccent{\bar}{\Delta}\|_{2}^{2},

(4.6)

and,

\left\langle\underaccent{\bar}{\nnoise},\frac{\underaccent{\bar}{\Delta}}{\left\|{\underaccent{\bar}{\Delta}}\right\|_{2}}\right\rangle\geq\frac{1}{2}\frac{\left\|{\underaccent{\bar}{\Delta}}\right\|_{2}}{\sigma}.

(4.7)

This last step is valid since
\mathbb{P}\left(\left\|{\underaccent{\bar}{\Delta}}\right\|_{2}=0\right)=0
, as
\mathcal{P}
has no atoms, so we can also assume that
\left\|{\underaccent{\bar}{\Delta}}\right\|_{2}\neq 0
.
Let
v_{1},\dots,v_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\underaccent{\bar}{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{t\times d}
with
\underaccent{\bar}{v}[i,j]=v_{i}[j]
and
\left\|{\underaccent{\bar}{v}}\right\|_{2}=1
. We have that for some absolute constant
\kappa>0
and
K_{\mathcal{Q}}
,

\begin{split}\left\|{\left\langle\underaccent{\bar}{\nnoise},\underaccent{\bar}{v}\right\rangle}\right\|_{\psi_{2}}^{2}&=\left\|{\sum_{i=1}^{t}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\underaccent{\bar}{\nnoise}[i,j]\underaccent{\bar}{v}[i,j]}\right\|_{\psi_{2}}^{2}\\
&\leq\kappa\sum_{i=1}^{t}\left\|{\sum_{j=1}^{d}\tilde{Z}_{i}[j]v_{i}[j]}\right\|_{\psi_{2}}^{2}\\
&=\kappa\sum_{i=1}^{t}\mathbf{1}_{[\left\|{v_{i}}\right\|_{2}\neq 0]}\left\|{v_{i}}\right\|_{2}^{2}\left\|{\left\langle\tilde{Z}_{i},\frac{v_{i}}{\left\|{v_{i}}\right\|_{2}}\right\rangle}\right\|_{\psi_{2}}^{2}\\
&\leq\kappa K_{\mathcal{Q}}^{2}.\end{split}

(4.8)

For any sub-Gaussian random variable
U
we have that,

\mathbb{P}\left(|U|\geq t\right)\leq 2e^{-\frac{t^{2}}{\left\|{U}\right\|_{\psi_{2}}^{2}}}.

(4.9)

It follows that since
\underaccent{\bar}{\nnoise}
and
\underaccent{\bar}{\dpos}
are independent,

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}\left(\left\langle{\underaccent{\bar}{\nnoise}},{\frac{\underaccent{\bar}{\dpos}}{\left\|{\underaccent{\bar}{\Delta}}\right\|_{2}}}\right\rangle\geq\frac{1}{2}\frac{\left\|{\underaccent{\bar}{\Delta}}\right\|_{2}}{\sigma}\Big\rvert\underaccent{\bar}{\Delta}\right)&\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{1}{4\kappa K^{2}_{\mathcal{Q}}}\frac{\left\|{\underaccent{\bar}{\Delta}}\right\|_{2}^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\right)\\
&=2\exp\left(-\frac{1}{4\kappa K^{2}_{\mathcal{Q}}}\frac{\sum\limits_{i=1}^{t}\left\|{\Delta_{i}}\right\|_{2}^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\right)\\
&\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{1}{4\kappa K^{2}_{\mathcal{Q}}}\frac{\sum\limits_{i=1}^{t-1}\left\|{\Delta_{i}}\right\|_{2}^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\right),\end{split}

(4.10)

where in the last inequality we used the fact that
\left\|{\Delta_{t}}\right\|_{2}^{2}\geq 0
. Although this last step might seem arbitrary we will see that it allows us to bound the expectation of the expressions above by a Gaussian integral. Remember that
f_{\mathcal{P}}
denotes the density of
X_{1}
with respect to Lebesgue measure in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Clearly
\underaccent{\bar}{X}
also has a density with respect to Lebesgue measure in
\mathbb{R}^{t\times d}
, but
\underaccent{\bar}{\Delta}
does not, since

(4.11)

In particular,

\left\|{f_{\underaccent{\bar}{\Delta}_{(t-1)}}}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\|{f_{\mathcal{P}}}\right\|_{\infty}^{(t-1)}f_{\mathcal{P}}(x)dx=\left\|{f_{\mathcal{P}}}\right\|_{\infty}^{(t-1)}.

It follows that,

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}\left(C_{t}\text{ is augmenting}\right)&=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{P}\left(\left\langle{\underaccent{\bar}{\nnoise}},{\frac{\underaccent{\bar}{\dpos}}{\left\|{\underaccent{\bar}{\Delta}}\right\|_{2}}}\right\rangle\geq\frac{1}{2}\frac{\left\|{\underaccent{\bar}{\Delta}}\right\|_{2}}{\sigma}\Big\rvert\underaccent{\bar}{\Delta}\right)\right]\\
&\leq 2\int_{\mathbb{R}^{(t-1)\times d}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{4\kappa K^{2}_{\mathcal{Q}}}\frac{\sum\limits_{i=1}^{t-1}\left\|{b[i,:]}\right\|_{2}^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\right)f_{\underaccent{\bar}{\Delta}_{(t-1)}}\left(b\right)db\\
&\leq 2\|f_{\underaccent{\bar}{\Delta}_{(t-1)}}\|_{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{(t-1)\times d}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{4\kappa K^{2}_{\mathcal{Q}}}\frac{\sum\limits_{i=1}^{t-1}\left\|{b[i,:]}\right\|_{2}^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\right)db\\
&\leq 2\left\|{f_{\mathcal{P}}}\right\|_{\infty}^{(t-1)}\left(4\pi\kappa K^{2}_{\mathcal{Q}}\sigma^{2}\right)^{\frac{d(t-1)}{2}},\end{split}

(4.13)

and so the result follows for
C_{t}=(1,2,\ldots,t)
taking
b_{\mathrm{LD}}=4\pi\kappa
. Since both
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
and
Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n}
have exchangeable distributions, the same argument follows if we take
C_{t}
to be any other
t
-cycle.
∎

Future work

Besides the open problems already posed in

Direction 5.1
.

Although the minimax lower bounds established in this work hold under mild assumptions on the tail of the noise distribution, we were only able to show that they are optimal making the assumption that the noise is sub-Gaussian. A natural follow-up question is what happens with noise distributions that do not have sub-Gaussian tails. What are the minimal assumptions on the noise we need to obtain rates of order

Appendix A
A technical result on estimation in groups

We prove the equality in (
3.18
), under a more general setting.

Lemma A.1
.

Let
\{P_{g}\}_{g\in G}
be a family of probability measures on some measure space
\left(\mathcal{Z},\mathcal{A}\right)
, indexed by
G
a finite group. Assume that there exists a family of (measurable) mappings
\{T_{g}:\mathcal{Z}\to\mathcal{Z};\penalty\ g\in G\}
such that for any
g,h\in G
,

Z\sim P_{g}\mbox{ implies }T_{h}(Z)\sim P_{gh}.

(A.1)

Let
d_{G}:G\times G\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
be a distance metric which is invariant under right multiplication by a group element, i.e., for any
g,g^{\prime},h\in G
,

d_{G}(g,g^{\prime})=d_{G}(gh,g^{\prime}h).

(A.2)

Let
g^{*}\sim\mathrm{Unif}(G)
be a random group element and
Z\sim P_{g^{*}}
. Then,

\inf_{\hat{g}}\max_{g\in G}\mathbb{E}\left[d_{G}(\hat{g}(Z),g^{*})|g^{*}=g\right]=\inf_{\hat{g}}\mathbb{E}\left[d_{G}(\hat{g}(Z),g^{*})\right],

(A.3)

where the infimum is taken over all (possibly non-deterministic) estimators
\hat{g}:\mathcal{Z}\to G
of
g^{*}
.

Proof.

It is always the case that,

\inf_{\hat{g}}\max_{g\in G}\mathbb{E}\left[d_{G}(\hat{g}(Z),g^{*})|g^{*}=g\right]\geq\inf_{\hat{g}}\mathbb{E}\left[d_{G}(\hat{g}(Z),g^{*})\right].

(A.4)

As for the other direction, let
\hat{g}
be some (possibly non-deterministic) estimator of
g^{*}
. Letting
\tilde{h}\sim\text{Unif}(G)
be sampled independently from
Z
and
g^{*}
, set
\tilde{Z}=T_{\tilde{h}}(Z)
. Note that
\tilde{Z}\sim P_{g^{*}\tilde{h}}
by (
A.1
). Define a new non-deterministic estimator:

\check{g}(Z):=\hat{g}(\tilde{Z})\tilde{h}^{-1}.

(A.5)

Due to the invariance of
d_{G}
,

d_{G}(\check{g}(Z),g^{*})=d_{G}(\hat{g}(\tilde{Z}),g^{*}\tilde{h}).

(A.6)

In particular, conditioned on
g^{*}=g
,
d_{G}(\check{g}(Z),g^{*})
has the same law as
d_{G}(\hat{g}(\tilde{Z}),g^{*}\tilde{h})
and
g^{*}\tilde{h}\sim\text{Unif}(G)
. Thus
d_{G}(\hat{g}(\tilde{Z}),g^{*}\tilde{h})
conditioned on
g^{*}=g
has the same law as
d_{G}(\hat{g}(Z),g^{*})
with
g^{*}\sim\text{Unif}(G)
. It follows that for any
g\in G
,

\mathbb{E}\left[d_{G}(\check{g}(Z),g^{*})|g^{*}=g\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[d_{G}(\hat{g}(\tilde{Z}),g^{*}\tilde{h})|g^{*}=g\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[d_{G}(\hat{g}(Z),g^{*})\right],

(A.7)

and so,

\max_{g\in G}\mathbb{E}\left[d_{G}(\check{g}(Z),g^{*})|g^{*}=g\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[d_{G}(\hat{g}(Z),g^{*})\right].

(A.8)

From this it follows that,

\inf_{\hat{g}}\max_{g\in G}\mathbb{E}\left[d_{G}(\hat{g}(Z),g^{*})|g^{*}=g\right]\leq\inf_{\hat{g}}\mathbb{E}\left[d_{G}(\hat{g}(Z),g^{*})\right].

(A.9)

∎

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}\left(U_{i,d}=2\right)\mathbb{P}\left(U_{j,d}=2\right)&=\mathbb{E}\left[\binom{N}{2}p^{2}_{i,d}(1-p_{i,d})^{N-2}\right]\mathbb{E}\left[\binom{N}{2}p^{2}_{j,d}(1-p_{j,d})^{N-2}\right]\\
&\geq\mathbb{P}\left(|N-qn|<\delta qn\right)^{2}\binom{(1-\delta)qn}{2}^{2}p^{2}_{i,d}p^{2}_{j,d}(1-p_{i,d})^{(1+\delta)qn}(1-p_{j,d})^{(1+\delta)qn},\end{split}

(B.20)

and,

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}\left(U_{i,d}=2,U_{j,d}=2\right)&=\mathbb{E}\left[\binom{N}{4}\binom{4}{2}p^{2}_{i,d}p^{2}_{j,d}(1-p_{i,d}-p_{j,d})^{N-4}\right]\\
&\leq\mathbb{P}\left(|N-qn|<\delta qn\right)\frac{(1+\delta)^{4}q^{4}n^{4}}{4}p^{2}_{i,d}p^{2}_{j,d}(1-p_{i,d}-p_{j,d})^{(1-\delta)qn-4}\\
&+\mathbb{P}\left(|N-qn|\geq\delta qn\right).\end{split}

(B.21)

Note that,

\begin{split}\log\left(\frac{(1-p_{i,d}-p_{j,d})^{(1-\delta)qn-4}}{(1-p_{i,d})^{(1+\delta)qn-2}(1-p_{j,d})^{(1+\delta)qn-2}}\right)&=-qn\log\left(1+\frac{p_{i,d}p_{j,d}}{(1-p_{i,d}-p_{j,d})}\right)\\
&-(\delta qn+4)\log\left(1-p_{i,d}-p_{j,d}\right)\\
&-\delta qn\log\left(1-p_{i,d}\right)\\
&-\delta qn\log\left(1-p_{j,d}\right).\end{split}

(B.22)

For sufficiently large
n
, using our assumption that
r\leq l
,

\begin{split}\left|qn\log\left(1+\frac{p_{i,d}p_{j,d}}{(1-p_{i,d}-p_{j,d})}\right)\right|&\leq 2qnp_{i,d}p_{j,d}\\
&\leq 2qne^{2\beta d}\left(\frac{r}{2R_{d}}\right)^{2d}\\
&\leq 2qn\frac{1}{(n-1)^{2}}\underset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}0.\end{split}

(B.23)

Also, for sufficiently large
n
,

|(\delta qn+4)\log\left(1-p_{i,d}-p_{j,d}\right)|\leq 2\delta qn\left|\log\left(1-\frac{2}{(n-1)}\right)\right|\underset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}4\delta q.

(B.24)

Since,

(\delta qn+4)|\log\left(1-p_{i,d}-p_{j,d}\right)|\geq\delta qn|\log\left(1-p_{i,d}\right)|\vee\delta qn|\log\left(1-p_{j,d}\right)|,

(B.25)

it follows that,

\limsup_{n\to+\infty}\left|\log\left(\frac{(1-p_{i,d}-p_{j,d})^{(1-\delta)qn-4}}{(1-p_{i,d})^{(1+\delta)qn-2}(1-p_{j,d})^{(1+\delta)qn-2}}\right)\right|\leq 12\delta q,

(B.26)

thus taking
\delta>0
sufficiently small, for all sufficiently large
n
,

\frac{(1-p_{i,d})^{(1+\delta)qn}(1-p_{j,d})^{(1+\delta)qn}}{(1-p_{i,d}-p_{j,d})^{(1-\delta)qn-4}}\geq\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\right).

(B.27)

From this (
B.19
) follows taking
n
large and
\delta
smaller if necessary to make the other factors of the ratio become closer to
1
. It follows that,

\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[I^{2}_{n}\right]}{\mathbb{E}\left[I_{n}\right]^{2}}\underset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}1,

(B.28)

from which it follows that
I_{n}\geq\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[I_{n}\right]}{2}
with high probability. Repeating the last part of the proof of
Lemma

1.2
for the case
r>l
, it follows that,

|M_{r}|\geq\frac{\gamma^{2}}{32}e^{-d(3\beta+\log(8))}\left(n^{2}\left(\frac{r}{R_{d}}\right)^{d}\wedge n\right)

(B.29)

with high probability and thus the result follows.

∎

B.3
and

.

B.2
Information theoretic results

In this subsection we give the following positive probability analogue of
Theorem

1.1
:

Theorem B.5

(Positive probability lower bound)
.

Assume that for each
n
,
LD
in (
1.1
) holds for some
R_{d},\|\,\cdot\,\|,\gamma
and
\beta
with
\gamma
and
\beta
that do not depend on
n
. If
e^{-10\beta d}\sigma^{d}n^{2}\to+\infty
, then as
n\to+\infty
,

\inf_{\hat{\pi}}\mathbb{P}\left(d_{H}\left({\hat{\pi}},{\pi^{*}}\right)\geq\frac{\gamma^{2}}{128}e^{-7\beta d}\left((n^{2}\sigma^{d})\wedge n\right)\right)\geq\frac{(1-o(1))}{8},

(B.30)

where the infimum above is taken over all estimators
\hat{\pi}
.

This result in particular completes the picture given by Theorem 3 in
[
24
]
for
d=o(\log n)
and generalizes their conclusions when
d
is fixed for a wider class of distributions:

Corollary B.6
.

Given the assumptions of
Theorem

B.5
and
d=o(\log n)
, for any estimator
\hat{\pi}
and
\alpha\in[0,1)
, if

d_{H}\left({\hat{\pi}},{\pi^{\star}}\right)=o(n^{\alpha})\text{ with high probability},

(B.31)

then,

\sigma=o\left(n^{\frac{\alpha-2}{d}}\right).

(B.32)

If we assume additionally that
d
is fixed, the result also holds for
\alpha=1
.

Proof.

d_{H}\left({\hat{\pi}},{\pi^{\star}}\right)=o(n^{\alpha})
with high probability implies that,

\mathbb{P}\left(d_{H}\left({\hat{\pi}},{\pi^{\star}}\right)\leq n^{\alpha}\right)\underset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}1.

(B.33)

But then from our lower bound in
Theorem

B.5
,

n^{\alpha}\geq\frac{\gamma^{2}}{128}e^{-7\beta d}\left((n^{2}\sigma^{d})\wedge n\right),

(B.34)

for sufficiently large
n
. Since
e^{-7\beta d}n=n^{1-o(1)}>n^{\alpha}
for sufficiently large
n
, this implies that,

n^{\alpha}\geq\frac{\gamma^{2}}{128}e^{-7\beta d}n^{2}\sigma^{d},

(B.35)

and thus,

\sigma=o\left(n^{\frac{\alpha-2}{d}}\right),

(B.36)

as claimed. If we assume that
d
is fixed and
d_{H}\left({\hat{\pi}},{\pi^{\star}}\right)=o(n)
with high probability, then for any
\varepsilon>0
,

\mathbb{P}\left(d_{H}\left({\hat{\pi}},{\pi^{\star}}\right)<\varepsilon\frac{\gamma^{2}}{128}e^{-7\beta d}n\right)\underset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}1

(B.37)

and in particular we must have:

\varepsilon n\geq n^{2}\sigma^{d},

(B.38)

which implies
\sigma=o(n^{-\frac{1}{d}})
.
∎

In order to prove
Theorem

B.5
we prove the following analogue of
Lemma

3.1
:

Lemma B.7

(Positive probability lower bound on
d_{H}\left({\hat{\pi}},{\pi^{\star}}\right)
)
.

Given the same setting as
Lemma

3.1
, if
|M_{r}|t_{\frac{r}{\sigma}}(\mathcal{Q})\geq 8
, then for any estimator
\hat{\pi}=\hat{\pi}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n},Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n})\in S_{n}
,

\mathbb{P}\left(d_{H}\left({\hat{\pi}},{\pi^{\star}}\right)\geq\frac{|M_{r}|t_{\frac{r}{\sigma}}(\mathcal{Q})}{4}\right)\geq\frac{1}{8},

(B.39)

for
t_{s}(\mathcal{Q})
as in (
3.3
) and
M_{r}=M_{r}(\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\})
the largest matching in
G(\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\},r,d,\|\,\cdot\,\|)
.

Proof.

Assume once again for ease of notation that
M_{r}=\{\{2j-1,2j\}\}_{j=1}^{m}
and let
\mathcal{Q}(v,\sigma)
denote the law of
v+\sigma\tilde{Z}
,
\tilde{Z}\sim\mathcal{Q}
. Note that given
P
and
Q
two measures on
(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{2}
,

\left\|{P-Q}\right\|_{TV}=\inf_{(W^{(0)},W^{(1)})}\mathbb{P}\left(W^{(0)}\neq W^{(1)}\right),

(B.40)

where the infimum is taken over all pairs
(W^{(0)},W^{(1)})
such that
W^{(0)}\sim P
and
W^{(1)}\sim Q
. Following
[
23
]
Theorem 4.1 we have that for any pair
x_{2j-1},x_{2j}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
there exists an optimal coupling
(W^{(0)},W^{(1)})
in
(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{2}\times(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{2}
with,

W^{(0)}\sim Q(x_{2j-1},\sigma)\otimes Q(x_{2j},\sigma)\text{ and }W^{(1)}\sim Q(x_{2j},\sigma)\otimes Q(x_{2j-1},\sigma)

(B.41)

such that,

\mathbb{P}\left(W^{(0)}=W^{(1)}\right)=1-\left\|{Q(x_{2j-1},\sigma)\otimes Q(x_{2j},\sigma)-Q(x_{2j},\sigma)\otimes Q(x_{2j-1},\sigma)}\right\|_{TV}=:p_{j}.

(B.42)

Note that from (
3.12
) we know that for all
j\in[m]
,

p_{j}\geq t_{\frac{r}{\sigma}}(\mathcal{Q}).

(B.43)

Let
\{(W^{(0)}_{j},W^{(1)}_{j})\}_{j=1}^{m}
be independent optimal couplings of the respective collection of pairs of laws,

\{\left(Q(x_{2j-1},\sigma)\otimes Q(x_{2j},\sigma),Q(x_{2j},\sigma)\otimes Q(x_{2j-1},\sigma)\right)\}_{j=1}^{m}.

(B.44)

Let
C_{j}
be the indicator of the event
W^{(0)}_{j}=W^{(1)}_{j}
. We define
b
and
b^{\prime}
two random elements of
\{0,1\}^{m}
by doing the following procedure independently for each
j\in[m]
:

1.

b_{j},b^{\prime}_{j}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{iid}}}{{\sim}}\text{Unif}(\{0,1\})
if
C_{j}=1
,

2.

b_{j}\sim\text{Unif}(\{0,1\})
and
b^{\prime}_{j}=b_{j}
if
C_{j}=0
.

The key aspect of this construction is that although the joint pair
(b_{j},b_{j}^{\prime})
depends on
C_{j}
, taken individually, both
b_{j}
and
b^{\prime}_{j}
have the same marginal distribution and are each independent of
C_{j}
, and thus from
W_{j}^{(0)},W_{j}^{(1)}
. Let
\tilde{Z}_{1},\dots,\tilde{Z}_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{iid}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{Q}
be generated independently. Given
\omega\in\{0,1\}^{m}
we define a sequence of final positions
Y^{(\omega)}_{1},\dots,Y^{(\omega)}_{n}
by:

(Y^{(\omega)}_{2j-1},Y^{(\omega)}_{2j})=W^{(\omega_{j})}_{j},\penalty\ j\in[m],

(B.45)

and,

Y^{(\omega)}_{i}=x_{i}+\sigma\tilde{Z}_{i},\penalty\ i=2m+1,\dots,n.

(B.46)

Note that
Y^{(b)}_{1},\dots,Y^{(b)}_{n}=Y^{(b^{\prime})}_{1},\dots,Y^{(b^{\prime})}_{n}
by construction. Moreover, conditioned on
b=\omega
, since
b
is independent of the
W_{j}^{(0)}
’s and
W_{j}^{(1)}
’s,

Y^{(b)}_{1},\dots,Y^{(b)}_{n}\sim\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{Q}\left(x_{\pi_{\omega}(i)},\sigma\right),

(B.47)

where
\pi_{\omega}=\prod_{j=1}((2j-1)2j)^{\omega_{j}}
. By symmetry the same conclusion holds for
b^{\prime}
. Since
b,b^{\prime}\sim\mathrm{Unif}\left(\{0,1\}^{m}\right)
it follows that,

(b,Y^{(b)}_{1},\dots,Y^{(b)}_{n})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(d)}}{{=}}(b^{\prime},Y^{(b^{\prime})}_{1},\dots,Y^{(b^{\prime})}_{n}).

(B.48)

Let
\pi^{*}\sim\mathrm{Unif}(S_{n})
be sampled independently. Note that conditioned on
b=\omega
and
\pi^{*}=\pi
,

Y^{(b)}_{\pi^{*}(1)},\dots,Y^{(b)}_{\pi^{*}(n)}\sim\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{Q}\left(x_{(\pi_{\omega}\circ\pi)(i)},\sigma\right).

(B.49)

Since
\pi_{b}\circ\pi^{*},\pi_{b^{\prime}}\circ\pi^{*}\sim\mathrm{Unif}(S_{n})
, it follows that,

\begin{split}(\pi_{b}\circ\pi^{*},Y^{(b)}_{\pi^{*}(1)},\dots,Y^{(b)}_{\pi^{*}(n)})&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(d)}}{{=}}(\pi_{b^{\prime}}\circ\pi^{*},Y^{(b^{\prime})}_{\pi^{*}(1)},\dots,Y^{(b^{\prime})}_{\pi^{*}(n)})\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(d)}}{{=}}(\pi^{*},Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}),\end{split}

(B.50)

where
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}
is defined by:

Y_{i}=x_{\pi^{*}(i)}+\sigma\tilde{Z}_{i},\penalty\ i\in[n].

(B.51)

In particular, since
\hat{\pi}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n},Y^{(b)}_{\pi^{*}(1)},\dots,Y^{(b)}_{\pi^{*}(n)})=\hat{\pi}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n},Y^{(b^{\prime})}_{\pi^{*}(1)},\dots,Y^{(b^{\prime})}_{\pi^{*}(n)})
, setting,

\hat{\pi}_{b}=\hat{\pi}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n},Y^{(b)}_{\pi^{*}(1)},\dots,Y^{(b)}_{\pi^{*}(n)}),

(B.52)

for ease of notation, we have by the triangular inequality,

\begin{split}d_{H}\left({\hat{\pi}_{b}},{\pi_{b}\circ\pi^{*}}\right)+d_{H}\left({\hat{\pi}_{b}},{\pi_{b^{\prime}}\circ\pi^{*}}\right)&\geq d_{H}\left({\pi_{b}\circ\pi^{*}},{\pi_{b^{\prime}}\circ\pi^{*}}\right)\\
&=2d_{H}\left({b^{\prime}},{b}\right).\end{split}

(B.53)

Now, by a coupling argument using (
B.43
) and the independence of
\{(b_{j},b^{\prime}_{j})\}_{j=1}^{m}
,

d_{H}\left({b^{\prime}},{b}\right)\geq H,

(B.54)

where
H\sim\mathrm{Binom}(m,\frac{1}{2}t_{\frac{r}{\sigma}}(\mathcal{Q}))
. Since
|M_{r}|t_{\frac{r}{\sigma}}(\mathcal{Q})\geq 8
using Chernoff’s inequality,

\mathbb{P}\left(H\geq\frac{t_{\frac{r}{\sigma}}(\mathcal{Q})|M_{r}|}{4}\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(H\geq\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[H\right]}{2}\right)\geq 1-e^{-\frac{1}{2}}\geq\frac{1}{4}.

(B.55)

We thus have that:

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}\left(d_{H}\left({\hat{\pi}_{b}},{\pi_{b}\circ\pi^{*}}\right)\geq\frac{t_{\frac{r}{\sigma}}(\mathcal{Q})|M_{r}|}{4}\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(d_{H}\left({\hat{\pi}_{b}},{\pi_{b^{\prime}}\circ\pi^{*}}\right)\geq\frac{t_{\frac{r}{\sigma}}(\mathcal{Q})|M_{r}|}{4}\right)&\geq\mathbb{P}\left(d_{H}\left({b^{\prime}},{b}\right)\geq\frac{t_{\frac{r}{\sigma}}(\mathcal{Q})|M_{r}|}{4}\right)\\
&\geq\mathbb{P}\left(H\geq\frac{t_{\frac{r}{\sigma}}(\mathcal{Q})|M_{r}|}{4}\right)\\
&\geq\frac{1}{4}.\end{split}

(B.56)

Note that,

\begin{split}d_{H}\left({\hat{\pi}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n},Y^{(b)}_{1},\dots,Y^{(b)}_{n})},{\pi_{b}\circ\pi^{*}}\right)&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(d)}}{{=}}d_{H}\left({\hat{\pi}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n},Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n})},{\pi^{*}}\right)\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(d)}}{{=}}d_{H}\left({\hat{\pi}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n},Y^{(b^{\prime})}_{1},\dots,Y^{(b^{\prime})}_{n})},{\pi_{b^{\prime}}\circ\pi^{*}}\right)\end{split}

(B.57)

and so,

\mathbb{P}\left(d_{H}\left({\hat{\pi}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n},Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n})},{\pi^{*}}\right)\geq\frac{t_{\frac{r}{\sigma}}(\mathcal{Q})|M_{r}|}{4}\right)\geq\frac{1}{8},

(B.58)

as desired.

∎

Theorem

B.5
:

Proof of
Theorem

B.5
.

Just combine
Lemma

B.7
and
Lemma

B.4
taking
r=R_{d}\sigma
.

Appendix C
Recovering lower bounds for the logarithmic regime in the Gaussian model

In this section we show that if
5.3
holds, then we can use
Lemma

B.3
to recover the lower bound from
[
11
]
on the logarithmic regime for some choices of
a>0
and
\sigma^{2}>0
.

We assume the Gaussian model from
[
11
]
,
i.e.
\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
and
\mathcal{Q}=\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}I_{d})
in (
1.1
), and the logarithmic regime
i.e.
d=a\log n
. For the Gaussian model we have:

q_{\frac{r}{\sigma}}(\mathcal{Q})=\left(1-\Phi\left(\frac{r}{\sqrt{2}\sigma}\right)\right),

(C.1)

where
\Phi
is the cumulative distribution function of a standard Gaussian random variable.
Let,

I(t)=\frac{1}{2}\left(t-1-\log\left(t\right)\right)\text{ for }t>0

(C.2)

be the large deviation rate function of a chi-squared random variable with
1
degree of freedom. For a fixed
\sigma^{2}>0
consider
r^{2}=\gamma d
for some
\gamma\in(0,2)
. Letting
G(n,r,d)=G(n,r,d,\left\|{\,\cdot\,}\right\|_{2})
be the random geometric graph induced by joining initial positions within Euclidean distance
r
, we have that:

\displaystyle\log\left(\mathbb{E}\left[E(G(n,r,d))\right]\left(1-\Phi\left(\frac{r}{\sqrt{2}\sigma}\right)\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\log\left(\binom{n}{2}\right)+\log\left(\mathbb{P}\left(\|X_{1}-X_{2}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\gamma d\right)\right)+\log\left(1-\Phi\left(\frac{\sqrt{\gamma d}}{\sqrt{2}\sigma}\right)\right).

Dividing all terms by
\log n
, taking the limit as
n\to\infty
, and using that
d=a\log n
,

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to+\infty}\frac{\log\left(\mathbb{E}\left[E(G(n,r,d))\right]\left(1-\Phi\left(\frac{r}{\sqrt{2}\sigma}\right)\right)\right)}{\log n}

\displaystyle=2-aI\left(\frac{\gamma}{2}\right)-\frac{a\gamma}{4\sigma^{2}}

(C.3)

\displaystyle=2-\frac{a}{2}\left(2I\left(\frac{\gamma}{2}\right)+\frac{\gamma}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)

(C.4)

\displaystyle=2-\frac{a}{2}\left(\frac{\gamma}{2}\left(1+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\right)-1-\log\left(\frac{\gamma}{2}\right)\right).

(C.5)

The last expression is maximized by taking
\gamma^{*}=2\left(1+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\right)^{-1}
which gives a value of
2-\frac{a}{2}\log\left(1+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\right)
, precisely the rate established in
[
11
]
. Let
r^{*}=\sqrt{\gamma^{*}d}
. If we assume
5.3
then
|M_{r^{*}}|=\Theta\left(\mathbb{E}\left[E(G(n,r^{*},d))\right]\wedge n\right)
with high probability, from which it follows that:

\frac{\log\left(|M_{r^{*}}|\right)}{\log\left(\mathbb{E}\left[E(G(n,r^{*},d))\right]\wedge n\right)}\overset{(\mathbb{P})}{\underset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}}1,

(C.6)

since
\mathbb{E}\left[E(G(n,r^{*},d))\right]\to+\infty
.
In particular, if
1\ll\mathbb{E}\left[E(G(n,r^{*},d))\right]\ll n
, then by
Lemma

B.3
, we have with high probability that,

\begin{split}\frac{\log\left(d_{H}\left({\hat{\pi}_{\mathrm{LSS}}},{\pi^{\star}}\right)\vee 1\right)}{\log\left(n\right)}\geq\frac{\log\left(|M_{r^{*}}|q_{\frac{r^{*}}{\sigma}}\right)-\log(2)}{\log n}.\end{split}

(C.7)

Since the last expression converges to
2-\frac{a}{2}\log\left(1+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\right)
in probability we have that,

d_{H}\left({\hat{\pi}_{\mathrm{LSS}}},{\pi^{\star}}\right)\vee 1\geq n^{\alpha+o_{p}(1)},

(C.8)

where
\alpha=2-\frac{a}{2}\log\left(1+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\right)
.

Unfortunately the additional constraint that
1\ll\mathbb{E}\left[E(G(n,r^{*},d))\right]\ll n
implies that
2-aI\left(\frac{\gamma^{*}}{2}\right)\in(0,1)
and so in (
C.8
) we have an additional constraint on
a>0
and
\sigma^{2}>0
. Moreover, similar to
[
11
]
we are unable to establish a lower bound of order
\Omega(n)
with this strategy, as it also only deals with augmenting cycles of size
2
.
