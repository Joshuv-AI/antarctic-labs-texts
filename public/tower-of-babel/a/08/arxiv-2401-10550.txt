Large recurrence of the polynomial Van der Waerden’s theorem and its application in Ramsey theory Ramakrishna Mission Vivekananda Educational and Research Institute, Belur, Howrah, 711202, India
Sayan Goswami

Abstract
Recently M. Di Nasso and M. Ragosta proved an exponential version of Hindman’s finite sum theorem: for every finite partition of ℕ \mathbb{N} , there exists a monochromatic Hindman tower of a single sequence. They used the stronger version of Furstenberg’s Central Sets Theorem and the theory of ultrafilters to prove their results. In this article, we prove these patterns are abundant by considering two-fold extensions of the Di Nasso-Ragosta theorem. We prove that each term of this pattern can be chosen from a finite set of any given length, consisting of a multiplicative I ​ P r IP_{r} (that we will define later) set and a set consisting of geometric progression (sets containing both arithmetic and geoarithmetic progression) of finite length. To prove our result, first, we improve the size of the recurrence set of V. Bergelson and A. Leibmann’s polynomial Van der Waerden’s Theorem, and then we study its two-fold applications. First, we study monochromatic patterns involving polynomials over sum sets in additive piecewise syndetic sets. Then in the second, applying methods of induction, we construct infinitary patterns involving a two-fold improvement of the Di Nasso-Ragosta theorem. Then we study the properties of some special classes of ultrafilters witnessing variants of these patterns. Throughout our article, we use the methods from the Algebra of the Stone-Čech-Compactification of discrete semigroups (for details see [ 31 ] ).



Keywords:
Polynomial van der Waerden theorem, Exponential Hindman theorem, Piecewise syndetic set, Central set, ultrafilters, Algebra of the Stone-Čech compactification of discrete semigroup

1  Introduction

Arithmetic Ramsey theory deals with the monochromatic patterns found in any given finite coloring of the
integers or of the natural numbers
\mathbb{N}
. Here, “coloring” means disjoint partition, and a set is called “monochromatic” if it is included in one piece of the partition. Let
\mathcal{F}
be a family of finite subsets of
\mathbb{N}.
If for every finite coloring of
\mathbb{N}
, there exists a monochromatic member of
\mathcal{F}
, then such a family
\mathcal{F}
is called a partition regular family. So basically, Ramsey theory is the study of the classifications of partitioned regular families. Arguably the first substantial development in this area of research was due to I. Schur
[
38
]
in
1916,
when he proved that the family
\{\{x,y,x+y\}:x\neq y\}
is a partitioned regular family over
\mathbb{N}.

The second cornerstone development was due to Van der Waerden in
1927
when he proved the following theorem.

Theorem 1.1

(
Van der Waerden theorem
,
[
41
]
)
.

For any finite coloring of the natural numbers one always finds arbitrarily long monochromatic arithmetic progressions. In other words, the set of all arithmetic progressions of finite length is partitioned regular.

Nonlinear versions of classical Ramsey theoretic are difficult to prove. One of the first progress in this direction was due to V. Bergelson and A. Liebman in
[
12
]
, where they proved the nonlinear version of van der Waerden’s theorem, known as Polynomial van der Waerden’s Theorem. The authors proved a generalized version of the much stronger Szémeredi’s Theorem, but we are not going to discuss it in this paper. They used the methods from the topological dynamics and the PET induction, introduced in
[
3
]
. Throughout this article, we assume
\mathbb{P}
to be the set of all polynomials from
\mathbb{Z}
to
\mathbb{Z}
with no constant term.

Theorem 1.2

(
Polynomial van der Waerden Theorem
,
[
12
]
)
.

Let
r\in\mathbb{N},
and
\mathbb{N}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{r}C_{i}
be a
r
-coloring of
\mathbb{N}
. Then for any finite collection of polynomials
F
in
\mathbb{P}
, there exist
a,d\in\mathbb{N}
and
1\leq j\leq r
such that

\{a+p(d):p\in F\}\subset C_{j}.

We will come back to our discussion after a short while.

Ultrafilters and its connections with Ramsey theory

Ultrafilters are set-theoretic objects that are intimately related to Ramsey theory. In this section, we give a brief introduction to this theory. For details, we refer to the book
[
31
]
to the readers. For a quick review authors can see the article
[
5
]
. For some recent developments we refer
[
32
]
.
A filter
\mathcal{F}
over any nonempty set
X
is a collection of subsets of
X
such that

1.

\emptyset\notin\mathcal{F}
, and
X\in\mathcal{F}
,

2.

A\in\mathcal{F}
, and
A\subseteq B
implies
B\in\mathcal{F},
and

3.

A,B\in\mathcal{F}
implies
A\cap B\in\mathcal{F}.

Using Zorn’s lemma we can guarantee the existence of maximal filters which are called ultrafilters. Any ultrafilter
p
has the following property:

•

if
X=\bigcup_{i=1}^{r}A_{i}
is any finite partition of
X
, then there exists
i\in\{1,2,\ldots,r\}
such that
A_{i}\in p.

Therefore to prove a certain structure is monochromatic, it is sufficient to study ultrafilters each of whose members contain that structure.

Space of ultrafilters

Let
(S,\cdot)
be a discrete semigroup. Denote by
\beta S
the collection of all ultrafilters over
S.
For any
(\emptyset\neq)A\subseteq S,
let
\overline{A}=\{p\in\beta S:A\in p\}.
The collection of sets
\{\overline{A}:(\emptyset\neq)A\subseteq S\}
forms a basis for a topology. One can show with this topology
\beta S
is the Stone-Čech compactification
of
S.
For any
A\subseteq S,
and
x\in S
denote
x^{-1}A=\{y:x\cdot y\in A\}.
Now one can naturally extend the operation ‘
\cdot
’ over the set
\beta S:
for any
p,q\in\beta S
, let us define
p\cdot q
by

A\in p\cdot q\iff\{x:x^{-1}A\in q\}\in p.

One can show with this definition
(\beta S,\cdot)
is a semigroup. In fact one can prove that

(\beta S,\cdot)
is a compact right topological semigroup. Here right topological means right action is continuous: for each
q\in\beta S,
the map
\rho_{q}:\beta S\rightarrow\beta S
defined by
\rho_{q}(p)=p\cdot q
is continuous. However, the left action is not always continuous but: for
x\in S,
the map
\lambda_{x}:\beta S\rightarrow\beta S
defined by
\lambda_{x}(p)=x\cdot p
is continuous.
Now from Ellis
[
19
]
Theorem, we can say that any closed subsemigroup of
(\beta S,\cdot)
contains idempotents. However, with some little modification, we can define the notions of
IP
sets over any arbitrary semigroup, but we are not going for that. One can see that

•

If
(S,\cdot)
is a semigroup, then a set
A\subseteq S
contains an
IP
set if and only if there exists an element
p\in E(\beta S,\cdot)
such that
A\in p.

Note that if
f:(S,\cdot)\rightarrow(T,\cdot)
be any map between discrete semigroups. Then
\tilde{f}:\beta S\rightarrow\beta T
be the continuous extension of
f
defined by
\tilde{f}(p)=\tilde{f}(\lim_{x\rightarrow p}x).

Notions of large sets

In the study of ultrafilter theory, one can show some members of
\beta S
are combinatorial rich. In fact one can characterize combinatorially the members of such ultrafilters. During our study we will apply both combinatorial and algebraic characterizations of those ultrafilters. Here we summarize some of the notions of large sets intimately related to our study. For detailed study on large sets, we refer to the articles

Definition 1.6
.

Let
(S,\cdot)
be a semigroup, let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and let
A\subseteq S
. We say that

1.

for any subset
T\subseteq(\beta S,\cdot)
, we let
E(T)
be the collection of all idempotents of

2.

A
is a

if for any finite subset
F\subset S

such that
Fx=\{fx:f\in F\}\subset A
;

3.

A
is a
syndetic set
if there exists a finite set

such that
S=\bigcup_{x\in F}x^{-1}A
, where
x^{-1}A=\{y:xy\in A\}
;

4.

A
is

if there exists a finite set
F\subset S
such that
\bigcup_{x\in F}x^{-1}A
is a thick set. It is well known that
A
is piecewise syndetic if and only if there exists
p\in K(\beta S,\cdot)
such that
A\in p.

A set

is said to be a left ideal if
\beta S\cdot L=\{q\cdot p:p\in L,q\in\beta S\}\subseteq L.
Similarly
R\subseteq\beta S
is said to a left ideal if
R\cdot\beta S=\{p\cdot q:p\in R,q\in\beta S\}\subseteq R.
Again using Zorn’s lemma one can show that minimal left/ right ideals (w.r.t. inclusion) exist. It can be proved that minimal left ideals are closed.
One can show that if
A\subseteq S
is a piecewise syndetic set, then there exists a minimal left ideal
L
of
(\beta S,\cdot)
, and
p\in L
such that
A\in p.
Denote by
K(\beta S,\cdot)
, the union of all minimal left ideals of
(\beta S,\cdot)
. It can be proved that
K(\beta S,\cdot)
is also the union of all minimal right ideals of
(\beta S,\cdot)
. That is

\displaystyle K(\beta S)

\displaystyle=\bigcup\{L:L\text{ is a minimal left ideal of }\beta S\}

\displaystyle=\bigcup\{R:R\text{ is a minimal right ideal of }\beta S\}.

As each minimal left ideal is the image of
\beta S
right action map, it is closed, and hence it contains idempotents. Hence,
E\left(K(\beta S,\cdot)\right)\neq\emptyset
.

2  Large recurrence of the polynomial Van der Waerden’s theorem

Hales-Jewett theorem
[
23
]
is one of the fundamental pillars of Ramsey theory. Many classical results in Ramsey theory directly followed from this theorem. The strength of this theorem is that the statement of this theorem is set-theoretical, and different algebraic operations induce different Ramsey theoretic statements. For example, van der Waerden’s theorem is an immediate consequence of this theorem. The original proof of the Hales-Jewett theorem uses simple combinatorial arguments. Later people found dynamical and algebraic proofs. One may see
[
4
,
33
]
for reference. Soon after V. Bergelson and A. Leibman proved the polynomial van der Waerden theorem (Theorem
1.2
), an immediate question appears if there exists any polynomial extension of the Hales-Jewett theorem.
Later in
[
13
]
, V. Bergelson and A. Leibman established the Polynomial extension of the Hales-Jewett theorem. The proof uses methods from Topological dynamics and PET induction. Then in
[
42
]
, M. Walter found a combinatorial proof. In
[
29
,
30
]
, N. Hindman and R. McCutcheon extensively studied this theorem using the algebraic approach. Using the Polynomial Hales-Jewett theorem, in this section, first we refine Theorem
1.9
. Then we use it to deduce some new results.

Revisiting Polynomial Hales-Jewett Theorem

Now we pause to recall the Hales-Jewett Theorem and it’s polynomial extension.
Let
\omega=\mathbb{N}\cup\left\{0\right\}
, where
\mathbb{N}

is the set of positive integers. Given a nonempty set
\mathbb{A}

called alphabet, a finite word is an expression of the form
w=a_{1}a_{2}\ldots a_{n}

with
n\geq 1
and
a_{i}\in\mathbb{A}
. The quantity
n
is called
the length of
w
and denoted
\left|w\right|
. Let
v
(a variable)
be a letter not belonging to
\mathbb{A}
. By a variable word over

\mathbb{A}
we mean a word
w
over
\mathbb{A}\cup\left\{v\right\}

that has at least one occurrence of
v
. For any variable word
w
,

w\left(a\right)
is the result of replacing each occurrence of
v

by
a
.

The following theorem is known as Hales-Jewett theorem, is due to
A. W. Hales and R. I. Jewett.

Theorem 2.1
.

[
23
, Hales-Jewett Theorem (1963)]

For all values
t,r\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists a number
\text{HJ}\left(r,t\right)

such that, if
N\geq\text{HJ}\left(r,t\right)
and
\left[t\right]^{N}

is
r
colored then there exists a variable word
w
such that
\left\{w\left(a\right):a\in\left[t\right]\right\}

is monochromatic.

The word space
\left[t\right]^{N}
is called Hales-Jewett space
or H-J space. The number
\text{HJ}\left(r,t\right)
is called Hales-Jewett
number.

Before we recall the polynomial Hales-Jewett theorem we need some new notions.
For
q,N\in\mathbb{N}
, let
Q=[q]^{N}
. For
a\in Q
,
\emptyset\neq\gamma\subseteq[N]

and
1\leq x\leq q
,
a\oplus x\gamma
is defined to be the vector

b
in
Q
obtained by setting
b_{i}=x
if
i\in\gamma
and
b_{i}=a_{i}

otherwise.

In the statement of Theorem
[
42
, Polynomial Hales-Jewett Theorem]
, we have
a\in Q
so that

a=\langle\vec{a}_{1},\vec{a}_{2},\ldots,\vec{a}_{d}\rangle
where
for
j\in\{1,2,\ldots d\}
,
\vec{a}_{j}\in[q]^{N^{j}}
and we have

\gamma\subseteq[N]=\{1,2,\ldots,N\}
. Given

,
let
\vec{a}_{j}=\langle a_{j,\vec{i}}\rangle_{\vec{i}\in N^{j}}
.
Then
a\oplus x_{1}\gamma\oplus x_{2}(\gamma\times\gamma)\oplus\ldots\oplus x_{d}\gamma^{d}=b

where

and for

,
\vec{b}_{j}=\langle b_{j,\vec{i}}\rangle_{\vec{i}\in N^{i}}

The following theorem is the Polynomial Hales-Jewett Theorem.

[

k\in\mathbb{N}
,
z_{k}\in FS(G_{k})
. But there is a drawback when we compare it with Theorem
3.12
. Because in the later case, we were able to show
z_{k}\in R_{k}
. This immediately requires a refinement of Theorem
3.3
, asking whether
z_{k}\in FS(G_{k})\cup FP(G_{k})?
So we leave the following question open.

Question 4.3
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}_{>1},

\langle N_{i}\rangle_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence in
\mathbb{N}
,
F_{1}\in\mathcal{P}_{f}(\mathbb{P})
. Let
\varPhi=\left(f_{n}:n\geq 2\right)
be a sequence
of functions
f_{n}:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}.

For every
n(>1),G\in\mathcal{P}_{f}(\mathbb{N})
, let
F_{n,G}=\{p\in\mathbb{P}:deg(p)\leq f_{n}(x)\text{ and }coef(p)\leq f_{n}(x)\text{ for }x=\max G\}.

Now for every finite coloring of
\mathbb{N}
, does there exists a sequence of finite sets
\langle G_{i}\rangle_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
such that

1.

for each
i\in\mathbb{N},

|G_{i}|=N_{i}
, and

2.

\left\{y_{k}\cdot n^{\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}p_{i}\left(y_{i}\right)}:k\in\mathbb{N},p_{1}\in F_{1},y_{j}\in FS(G_{j})\cup FP(G_{j})\text{ for }1\leq j\leq k,\right.

\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\left.\text{ and }\,p_{i}\in F_{i,f_{i}\left(G_{i-1}\right)}\text{ for }2\leq i\leq k-1\right\}

is monochromatic?

While proving Corollary
3.18
, we used the property: for any
q\in K(\beta\mathbb{N},\cdot)
, and some suitably chosen
p\in\beta\mathbb{N}
,
p\star_{n}q
witness the conclusion of Theorem
3.12
. But we could not prove Theorem
3.3
for any
q\in K(\beta\mathbb{N},\cdot)
. That’s why the technique of the proof of Corollary
3.18
can’t be adapted for the case of
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{PF}}
. So we leave the following question open.

Question 4.4
.

Does every multiplicative piecewise syndetic set satisfies the conclusion of
3.3
? In other words does
\overline{K\left(\beta\mathbb{N},\cdot\right)}\subseteq\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{PF}}?
