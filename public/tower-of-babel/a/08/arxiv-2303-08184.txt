Highly resolved spectral functions of two-dimensional systems with neural quantum states
Tiago Mendes-Santos, Markus Schmitt, Markus Heyl

Abstract
Spectral functions are central to link experimental probes to theoretical models in condensed matter physics. However, performing exact numerical calculations for interacting quantum matter has remained a key challenge especially beyond one spatial dimension. In this work, we develop a versatile approach using neural quantum states to obtain spectral properties based on simulations of the dynamics of excitations initially localized in real or momentum space. We apply this approach to compute the dynamical structure factor in the vicinity of quantum critical points (QCPs) of different two-dimensional quantum Ising models, including one that describes the complex density wave orders of Rydberg atom arrays. When combined with deep network architectures we find that our method reliably describes dynamical structure factors of arrays with up to 24 × 24 24\times 24 spins, including the diverging time scales at critical points. Our approach is broadly applicable to interacting quantum lattice models in two dimensions and consequently opens up a route to compute spectral properties of correlated quantum matter in yet inaccessible regimes.



Introduction.

Spectral functions are key tools to characterize and probe quantum many-body phases and their transitions.
In addition, they serve as a common framework to connect theoretical descriptions with experimental probes such as photoemission or inelastic neutron scattering.
In this context, a regime of particular interest is two-dimensional (2D) interacting quantum matter, where experimental probes can indicate the occurrence of prominent properties, such as exotic fractionalized quasiparticles in
candidate materials realizing 2D spin-liquid phases
Balents 2010
or universal features associated to quantum critical points
Sachdev 2011
.

At the theoretical level, accessing and describing spectral functions is, thus, of great interest in strongly interacting solid-state materials. But addressing dynamical properties of correlated matter in a controlled manner poses, at the same time, substantial challenges. Quantum Monte Carlo is poised by a sign problem

and the applicability of dynamical mean field theory
Aoki et al. 2014
is limited in low dimensions. Tensor network approaches, which render the treatment of weakly entangled states feasible, can be used to obtain numerically exact results for one-dimensional systems
Hallberg 1995
;
Kühner and White 1999
;
Jeckelmann 2002
;
Benthien et al. 2004
;
White and Feiguin 2004
;
Paeckel et al. 2019

Zaletel et al. 2015
;
Gohlke et al. 2017
;
Verresen et al. 2018
;
Verresen et al. 2019
;
Van Damme and Vanderstraeten 2022
, the growth of entanglement in time together with the two-dimensional lattice structure that increases the complexity of tensor contractions remains as a challenge for tensor network methods.
In addition, variational methods for
capturing excitations based on Gutzwiller-projected mean-field states are restricted to specific cases due to their built-in bias
Ferrari et al. 2018
;
Ferrari and Becca 2019
.
Finally, programmable quantum simulation could emerge as a new route
Knap et al. 2013
;
Baez et al. 2020
;
Sun et al. 2023
, but it is still in its infancy at this point.

Recently, the idea to combine the variational Monte Carlo (VMC) framework with neural quantum states (NQSs)

For spectral functions, first attempts proposed NQS-based algorithms built directly in the frequency domain

;
Hendry and Feiguin 2019
;
Hendry et al. 2021
or a method simulating the response to an initial time-dependent perturbation of the system
Fabiani and Mentink 2019
. However, it remains desirable to enhance the resolution and reachable system sizes over what has been achieved so far in order to address open physical questions.

In this work, we introduce an alternative versatile scheme for the simulation of spectral functions based on the direct encoding of local excitations in the neural network architecture – the specNQS, see Fig.
1
(a).
The spectral information is then extracted from dynamical correlation functions that are obtained by real-time evolution.
When combined with convolutional neural networks, we demonstrate that our scheme allows us to access dynamical properties beyond what has been feasible with other state-of-the-art approaches.
As a benchmark, we simulate the dynamical structure factor (DSF) of the 2D quantum Ising model (QIM), and we showcase that the specNQS reliably describes spectral features associated with a diverging correlation length for system sizes up to
24\times 24
sites.
Furthermore, we contribute to the characterization of quantum phase transitions in experimentally realized long-range interacting Rydberg atom arrays
Ebadi et al. 2021
;
Scholl et al. 2021
by revealing spectral properties close to phase boundaries, the nature of which is under ongoing debate
Felser et al. 2021
;
Kalinowski et al. 2022

Method.

In the following, we will be interested in computing the DSF

\displaystyle S^{zz}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{s}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dte^{i\omega t}\big\langle\hat{\sigma}^{z}_{-\boldsymbol{q}}(t)\hat{\sigma}^{z}_{\boldsymbol{q}}\big\rangle.

(1)

Here,
\langle\hat{A}(t)\hat{B}\rangle=\braket{\psi_{0}|e^{i\hat{H}t}\hat{A}e^{-i\hat{H}t}\hat{B}|\psi_{0}}
denotes the dynamical correlation function in the ground state
\ket{\psi_{0}}
of a given Hamiltonian
\hat{H}
,
\hat{H}\ket{\psi_{0}}=E_{0}\ket{\psi_{0}}
with
E_{0}
being the ground-state energy.

\hat{\sigma}^{z}_{\boldsymbol{q}}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{r}}e^{-i\boldsymbol{q}\cdot\boldsymbol{r}}\hat{\sigma}^{z}_{\boldsymbol{r}}
is the spin operator in momentum space, where
\hat{\sigma}^{z}_{\mathbf{r}}
denotes the Pauli-
z
operator at lattice site
\boldsymbol{r}
and
N_{s}
is the number of lattice sites.

The central idea of our approach is to obtain the DSF from a variational representation of suitably time-evolved wave functions. NQSs constitute a versatile family of variational wave functions relying on the proven representational power of artificial neural networks (ANNs).
In particular, any function can be accurately approximated by an ANN in the limit of large network sizes
Cybenko 1989
;
Hornik 1991
;
Kim and Adalı 2003
;
Roux and Bengio 2008
.
This means that the accuracy of the proposed approach can be asserted self-consistently by checking the convergence with increasing network size.

As a first step to access the DSF, we compute the NQS representation of the ground state

\ket{\psi_{0}}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{s}}\psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(0)}(\boldsymbol{s})\ket{\boldsymbol{s}},

(2)

where
\boldsymbol{s}=(s_{1},s_{2},...,s_{N_{s}})
labels the Pauli-Z basis of spin configurations.
The variational ansatz
\psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(0)}(\boldsymbol{s})
is parameterized by
\boldsymbol{\theta}=(\theta_{1},\theta_{2},...,\theta_{M})
and it takes the form of an ANN.
The ground state is then obtained by optimizing
\boldsymbol{\theta}
to minimize the energy expectation value
\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\braket{\psi(\boldsymbol{\theta})|\hat{H}|\psi(\boldsymbol{\theta})}/\braket{\psi(\boldsymbol{\theta})|\psi(\boldsymbol{\theta})}
.
For the results presented throughout this manuscript, we employed the Stochastic Reconfiguration algorithm to find the ground states
Sorella 1998
;
Sorella 2001
.

Our approach to access the dynamics relies on computing the time-evolved wave functions following an excitation,

\displaystyle\ket{\phi_{\boldsymbol{r}/\boldsymbol{q}}^{\alpha}(t)}=e^{-i\hat{H}t}\hat{\sigma}_{\boldsymbol{r}/\boldsymbol{q}}^{\alpha}\ket{\psi_{0}}\equiv e^{-i\hat{H}t}\ket{\phi_{\boldsymbol{r}/\boldsymbol{q}}^{\alpha}(0)}\ .

(3)

Here,
\hat{\sigma}_{\boldsymbol{r}/\boldsymbol{q}}^{\alpha}
is an operator in either position (
\boldsymbol{r}
) or momentum space (
\boldsymbol{q}
).
In our numerical approach we rely in either case on an exact representation of
\ket{\phi_{\boldsymbol{r}/\boldsymbol{q}}^{\alpha}(0)}
, which will be discussed in the following paragraphs. Let us first discuss the time-evolution algorithm, assuming that the representation of the initial state is given. We employ a time-dependent variational principle (TDVP) that is based on the minimization of the Fubini-Study distance between the variational time-evolved state
\ket{\psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}(t+\delta t)}}
and the exact one
e^{-i\hat{H}\delta t}\ket{\psi(t)}
, where
\delta t
is an infinitesimal time interval. It yields an ordinary non-linear differential equation prescribing the optimal evolution of the variational parameters
Carleo and Troyer 2017
;
Schmitt and Heyl 2020
,

\displaystyle S_{k,k^{\prime}}\dot{\theta}_{k^{\prime}}=-iF_{k}\ ,

(4)

where
\dot{\theta}_{k^{\prime}}
is the time derivative,
S_{k,k^{\prime}}=\braket{\partial_{\theta_{k}}\psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}|\partial_{\theta_{k^{\prime}}}\psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}-\braket{\partial_{\theta_{k}}\psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}|\psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\braket{\psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}|\partial_{\theta_{k^{\prime}}}\psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}
is the quantum metric tensor and
F_{k}=\braket{\partial_{\theta_{k}}\psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}|\hat{H}|\psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}-\braket{\partial_{\theta_{k}}\psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}|\psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\braket{\psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}|\hat{H}|\psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}
. Both
S_{k,k^{\prime}}
and
F_{k}
can be estimated efficiently via Monte-Carlo sampling of the Born distribution
|\psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(s)|^{2}
.

Upon integration, the TDVP in Eq. (
4
) in general yields the time-evolved state up to a global phase and normalization
Hackl et al. 2020
.
The phase is irrelevant for equal-time correlation functions. For computing the dynamical correlation functions, however, it becomes important, because we are interested in evaluating the overlap of two time-evolved states.
To keep track of relative changes in phase between such time-evolved states we consider the (logarithmic) prefactor
\theta_{0}
as an additional variational parameter:
\ket{\psi_{\theta_{0},\boldsymbol{\theta}}}=e^{\theta_{0}}\ket{\psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}
.
By using the TDVP, one can establish the following equation of motion for
\theta_{0}

Carleo 2011

\dot{\theta}_{0}=-i\braket{\hat{H}}-\dot{\theta}_{k}\braket{\psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}|\partial_{\theta_{k}}\psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}.

(5)

Equation (
4
) for the other parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}
remains unchanged.
Thus, for each time step, we first obtain
\dot{\theta}_{k}
and then use the result to solve Eq. (
5
) for the evolution of
\theta_{0}
.

Momentum-space scheme.

First, we discuss how to access the dynamical spin structure factor directly in momentum space,

S^{zz}(\boldsymbol{q},t)=e^{iE_{0}t}\bra{\psi_{0}}\hat{\sigma}_{\boldsymbol{q}}^{z}e^{-i\hat{H}t/2}e^{-i\hat{H}t/2}\hat{\sigma}_{\boldsymbol{q}}^{z}\ket{\psi_{0}}\ .

(6)

To simplify the discussion we focus on the
z
component, but our approach can be straightforwardly generalized for
\alpha=x,y
by choosing the computational basis accordingly.
The central idea is that the action of the operator
\hat{\sigma}_{\boldsymbol{q}}^{z}
on the initial state
\ket{\psi_{0}}
can be captured explicitly and efficiently by modifying the individual wave function coefficients with corresponding prefactors.
Concretely, the excitation
\sigma_{\boldsymbol{q}}^{z}\ket{\psi_{0}}
is encoded on the NQS ansatz
by adding a configuration-dependent factor
f(\boldsymbol{q},\boldsymbol{s})=\sum_{j}e^{-i\boldsymbol{r}_{j}\boldsymbol{q}}e^{i\pi/2(s_{j}-1)}
on the initial-time quantum state

\ket{\phi_{\boldsymbol{q}}^{z}(0)}=\hat{\sigma}_{\boldsymbol{q}}^{z}\ket{\psi_{0}}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{s}}f(\boldsymbol{q},\boldsymbol{s})\psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{s})\ket{\boldsymbol{s}}\ .

(7)

We then obtain
S^{zz}(\boldsymbol{q},t)
by performing a two-sided time evolution with the TDVP approach,
followed by evaluating the overlap of the two time evolved states,

S^{zz}(\boldsymbol{q},t)=e^{iE_{0}t}\braket{\phi^{z}_{\boldsymbol{q}}(-t/2)|\phi^{z}_{\boldsymbol{q}}(t/2)}.

(8)

This is a the central object to calculate the dynamical structure factor by means of our NQS approach. Notice that this implies that for the dynamical correlation function up to time
t
numerical integration is only required up to time
t/2
.
Here, it is important to account for the global factor associated to each state of the overlap as discussed above, see Eq. (
5
). Refs
SM
;
Wu et al. 2020
presents further details about the calculation of the overlap.

Real-space scheme.

Second, we discuss a strategy to obtain dynamical correlations in real space.
Our scheme is based on a many-body Ramsey protocol
Knap et al. 2013
, that is used to simulate the retarded Green’s function (GF)

G^{\alpha\alpha}_{\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}}(t)=-\frac{i}{2}\bra{\psi_{0}}[\hat{\sigma}_{\boldsymbol{r}}^{\alpha}(t),\hat{\sigma}_{\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}}^{\alpha}(0)]\ket{\psi_{0}},

(9)

where
\boldsymbol{r}
,
\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}
are the sites of a lattice, and
[\hat{A},\hat{B}]
denotes the commutator.
In particular, to access the longitudinal
G^{zz}_{\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}}(t)
component, we start the protocol with the following quantum state

\ket{\phi_{\boldsymbol{r}}^{z}(0)}=e^{i\frac{\pi}{4}\hat{\sigma}^{z}_{\boldsymbol{r}}}\ket{\psi_{0}}
,
where the local pertubation is represented by the
\frac{\pi}{4}
-rotation of a spin at site
\boldsymbol{r}
.
Further, we obtain the time-evolved state
\ket{\phi_{\boldsymbol{r}}^{z}(t)}=e^{-i\hat{H}t}\ket{\phi_{\boldsymbol{r}}^{z}(0)}
.
Following Ref.
Knap et al. 2013
, the result of a local measurement of
\hat{\sigma}_{\boldsymbol{r}}^{z}
at a time
t
depends on the GF, i.e.,

\displaystyle\bra{\phi_{\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}}^{z}(t)}\hat{\sigma}_{\boldsymbol{r}}^{z}\ket{\phi_{\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}}^{z}(t)}=

\displaystyle\bra{\psi_{0}}\hat{\sigma}_{\boldsymbol{r}}^{z}(t)\ket{\psi_{0}}+G^{zz}_{\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}}(t)+\bra{\psi_{0}}\hat{\sigma}_{\boldsymbol{r}}^{z}\hat{\sigma}_{\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}}^{z}(t)\hat{\sigma}_{\boldsymbol{r}}^{z}\ket{\psi_{0}}.

(10)

The
G^{zz}_{\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}}(t)
is obtained by reconstructing the terms of the Eq. (
10
).
The first term on the right hand side is accessed from the ground state. For the remaining contributions we time-evolve the initial states
e^{i\frac{\pi}{4}\hat{\sigma}^{z}_{\boldsymbol{r}}}\ket{\psi_{0}}
and
\hat{\sigma}_{\boldsymbol{r}}^{z}\ket{\psi_{0}}
after incorporating the operator action into the variational ansatz in analogy to Eq. (
7
).

One central difference between the momentum- and the real-space approach is that the latter does not require the calculation of state overlaps. Moreover, they differ in the way translational symmetry can be exploited for the efficiency of simulations. In the real space approach all correlation functions in Eq. (
10
) depend only on relative positions
\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{r}
, which means that all the momentum points of
S^{zz}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)
can be obtained from the two time-evolved states
\ket{\phi_{\boldsymbol{r}=0}^{z}(t)}
and
e^{-i\hat{H}t}\hat{\sigma}_{\boldsymbol{r}=0}^{z}\ket{\psi_{0}}
. However, with this approach, translational symmetry cannot be built into the variational ansatz to enhance efficiency. By contrast, the time-evolved states in Eq. (
8
) for the momentum-space approach preserve translational symmetry, which can be exploited to introduce beneficial bias through built-in invariance of the NQS. This comes, however, at the cost of individual simulations required for each point in momentum space.

Finally, it is worth mentioning that we compute
S^{zz}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)
by performing a Fourier transform with a Gaussian envelope to avoid the finite-time effects of our simulation (our simulations are performed up to a time
t_{max}
)
SM
.

Neural quantum states architectures.

In conjunction with the momentum-space scheme, we employ convolutional neural networks (CNNs)
Sharir et al. 2020

Schmitt and Heyl 2020

The hyperparameters of the CNNs are the total number of layers

k

\alpha_{k}

F
; in the following, we characterize the CNN architecture with the tuples
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},...,\alpha_{l};F)

Meanwhile, to implement the real-space scheme, we use Restricted Boltzmann Machines (RBMs)
Carleo and Troyer 2017
composed of a single fully-connected hidden layer with
M
nodes, where

is a hyperparameter; further details about the NQSs are discussed in the SM

Results I: Two-dimensional Quantum Ising model.

To benchmark our approach, we consider the paradigmatic 2D QIM on a square lattice:

\hat{H}=-\sum_{\left<i,j\right>}\hat{\sigma}^{z}_{i}\hat{\sigma}^{z}_{j}-g\sum_{i}\hat{\sigma}^{x}_{i}.

(11)

This model describes a second-order QCP at
g_{c}\approx 3.044
, separating a ferromagnetic from a paramagnetic phase.
In the vicinity of
g_{c}
, the major contributions to
S^{zz}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)

come from low-energy quasiparticle excitations whose frequency and spectral weight are expected to scale as
\omega\sim L^{-z}
and

\tilde{S}\sim L^{(1-\eta)}
, respectively
Sachdev 2011
;
here,
z=1
and
\eta\approx 0.04
are critical exponents of the 3D-Ising universality class
Pelissetto and Vicari 2002
.

We start discussing the DSF obtained with the real-space scheme shown in Fig.
1
(c1). The plots show the DSF along a path through the first Brillouin zone for a lattice of size
N_{s}=10\times 10
at transverse field
g=3.1
.
The main feature of
S^{zz}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)
is a minimum at the
\Gamma
point, corresponding to the low-energy gap.
We compare our results to series-expansion results up to fourth order in
g^{-1}

Hamer et al. 2006a
;
Hamer et al. 2006b
and find very good agreement almost everywhere in the Brillouin zone.
The most notable deviation appears at the gap closing point when approaching the critical value

, which we will discuss in more detail below.
Figure

(c2) shows similar results obtained with the momentum scheme in the vicinity of the

The fact that we can in this case use more efficient NQS architectures with built-in translational invariance allows us to simulate substantially larger system sizes up to

lattice sites and we again find good agreement with the series expansion.

Let us now focus on the behavior of the DSF in the vicinity of the QCP.
As a demanding benchmark for the accuracy of the NQS approach, we investigate the finite-size scaling of the spectral gap extracted from the DSF,
\Delta(\boldsymbol{q}=\Gamma)=\text{argmax}_{\omega}S^{zz}(\boldsymbol{q}=\Gamma,\omega)
. The expected universal scaling behavior

the linear system size, can be compellingly confirmed by our finite-size simulations up to

. We emphasize that our results show that with our NQS approach such large system sizes required to extract this universal behavior have now become within reach, while simultaneously also the diverging time scale associated with the closing of the spectral gap can be captured.

In Fig.
2

Discussion and conclusions.

In summary, we have proposed a cutting-edge method to simulate spectral properties of 2D quantum many-body systems, which relies on the representational power of NQSs to access spectral functions during the real-time dynamics of local excitations. We demonstrated that this scheme allows us to reliably perform finite-size scaling of dynamical properties near 2D quantum critical points for unprecedented system sizes.
A promising future direction is to characterize spectral features of 2D models stabilizing quantum spin liquid phases.
Of particular current interest are spin liquids with Ising-like interactions, such as the 2D RyM in a ruby lattice, which has recently been proposed as a way to realize
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
topological order states in programmable quantum simulators
Semeghini et al. 2021
;
Verresen et al. 2021
, and also Kitaev-type spin models
Kitaev 2006
, where highly-accurate simulations of DSF are essential to characterize exotic fractionalized excitations
Becker and Wessel 2018
;
Sun et al. 2018
.

I
Further details on the simulation of dynamical correlations with tVMC

In this section, we discuss further important details to simulate the dynamical correlations
S^{zz}(\boldsymbol{q},t)
and
G^{zz}_{\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}}(t)
.

Momentum-space scheme.
As described in the main text, we obtain
S^{zz}(\boldsymbol{q},t)
by (i) performing a two-sided time evolution followed by (ii) the evaluation of the overlap of two time-evolved states; see Eq. (
8
).

First, to time evolve the states
\bra{\phi^{z}_{\boldsymbol{q}}(-\delta t)}
and
\ket{\phi^{z}_{\boldsymbol{q}}(\delta t)}
, we solve the TDVP equation [Eq. (
4
)] with a second-order integration method. Specifically, we implement the adaptative-time-step scheme to describe the time evolution of the ket-state
\ket{\phi^{z}_{\boldsymbol{q}}(\delta t)}
.
Such an approach allows us to estimate errors based on varying time step sizes
and adjust
\delta t
during the simulation. The adaptative-time-step scheme is implemented with the Heun method; we refer the reader to ref.
Schmitt and Heyl 2020
for further details.
The time evolution of the bra-state
\bra{\phi^{z}_{\boldsymbol{q}}(-\delta t)}
is then performed by using the Heun method with the time step
\delta t
defined by the ket-state time evolution.

Second, to compute the overlap between the two unnormalized states
\bra{\phi^{z}_{\boldsymbol{q}}(-t/2)}
and
\ket{\phi^{z}_{\boldsymbol{q}}(t/2)}
we employ Monte Carlo sampling to obtain the normalized overlap (to simplify the notation, let us define
\bra{\phi^{z}_{\boldsymbol{q}}(-t/2)}=\bra{\phi_{1}}
and
\ket{\phi^{z}_{\boldsymbol{q}}(t/2)}=\ket{\phi_{2}}
)

O=\frac{\braket{\phi_{1}|\phi_{2}}}{\sqrt{\braket{\phi_{1}|\phi_{1}}\braket{\phi_{2}|\phi_{2}}}}.

(13)

The idea is that the quantity
p_{1}=\braket{\phi_{1}|\phi_{2}}/\braket{\phi_{1}|\phi_{1}}
, can be estimated via Monte Carlo sampling, i.e.,

p_{1}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{s}}\frac{|\phi_{1}(\boldsymbol{s})|^{2}}{\braket{\phi_{1}|\phi_{1}}}\frac{\phi_{2}(\boldsymbol{s})}{\phi_{1}(\boldsymbol{s})}\approx\frac{1}{N_{MC}}\sum_{i}\frac{\phi_{2}(\boldsymbol{s}_{i})}{\phi_{1}(\boldsymbol{s}_{i})},

(14)

where
\boldsymbol{s}_{1},...,\boldsymbol{s}_{N_{MC}}
represent a set of
N_{MC}
MC samples generated by the Born distribution
|\phi_{1}(\boldsymbol{s})|^{2}
.
Analogously,
p_{2}=\braket{\phi_{2}|\phi_{1}}/\braket{\phi_{2}|\phi_{2}}
is estimated via MC sampling of
|\phi_{2}(\boldsymbol{s})|^{2}
.
By combining the two results, we obtain
O=\sqrt{p_{1}p_{2}^{*}}

Wu et al. 2020
.

Finally, by computing
O_{\boldsymbol{q}}=\braket{\phi^{z}_{\boldsymbol{q}}(-t/2)|\phi^{z}_{\boldsymbol{q}}(t/2)}/N_{\boldsymbol{q}}
,
where the normalization constant

N_{\boldsymbol{q}}=\sqrt{\braket{\phi^{z}_{\boldsymbol{q}}(-t/2)|\phi^{z}_{\boldsymbol{q}}(-t/2)}\braket{\phi^{z}_{\boldsymbol{q}}(t/2)|\phi^{z}_{\boldsymbol{q}}(t/2)}}

(15)

is simply related to the ground-state static structure fractor,
N_{\boldsymbol{q}}=N_{s}\sum_{\boldsymbol{r}}e^{i\boldsymbol{r}\boldsymbol{q}}\left<S^{z}_{\boldsymbol{r}}S^{z}_{\boldsymbol{0}}\right>
, we obtain the dynamical correlation in momentum space

S^{zz}(\boldsymbol{q},t)=e^{iE_{0}t}N_{\boldsymbol{q}}O_{\boldsymbol{q}}.

(16)

Real-space scheme.
We use Eq. (
10
) to compute the longitudinal component of the Green function
G^{zz}_{\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}}(t)
,
for a deduction of such expression see refs.
Knap et al. 2013
;
Baez et al. 2020
.
The expectation value
\bra{\phi_{\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}}^{z}(t)}\hat{S}_{\boldsymbol{r}}^{z}\ket{\phi_{\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}}^{z}(t)}
is obtained by simulating the
time evolution of the local excitation
\ket{\phi_{\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}}^{z}(t)}=e^{-i\hat{H}t}e^{i\frac{\pi}{4}\hat{S}_{\boldsymbol{r^{\prime}}}^{z}}\ket{\psi_{0}}
with the tVMC, where
the local excitation is encoded on the NQS ansatz by adding the configuration-dependent factor
f(\boldsymbol{r^{\prime}},\boldsymbol{s})=1/\sqrt{2}\left(1-is_{\boldsymbol{r^{\prime}}}\right)
on the initial-time quantum state

\ket{\phi_{\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}}^{z}(0)}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{s}}f(\boldsymbol{r^{\prime}},\boldsymbol{s})\psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{s})\ket{\boldsymbol{s}}.

(17)

Similarly, the term
\bra{\psi_{0}}\hat{S}_{\boldsymbol{r}}^{z}\hat{S}_{\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}}^{z}(t)\hat{S}_{\boldsymbol{r}}^{z}\ket{\psi_{0}}
is obtained by simulating the time evolution of
the local excitation
\hat{S}_{\boldsymbol{r}}^{z}\ket{\psi_{0}}
, which is encoded on the NQS ansatz by adding the factor
f^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{s})=s_{\boldsymbol{r}}
.

Fourier transform

Let us now discuss the details of the Fourier transform (FT) used to obtain the dynamical structure factor in the frequency-momentum domain:
S^{zz}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)
.

In the momentum-space scheme, we perform a FT of
S^{zz}(\boldsymbol{q},t)
to the frequency domain

S^{zz}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dte^{i\omega t}e^{-\gamma^{2}t^{2}}S^{zz}(\boldsymbol{q},t),

(18)

where we apply a Gaussian envelope to avoid the finite-time effects of our simulation;
the parameter
\gamma=2/t_{max}
controls the broadening factor of the Gaussian peaks, with
t_{max}
being the maximum times reliably obtained during our simulations.
For the results shown in the main text, we typically performed simulations with
t_{max}\approx 20J
and
t_{max}\approx 40\Omega
for the 2D QIM and RyM, respectively. Furthermore, we employ the trapezoidal rule to perform the numerical integration of Eq. (
18
).

In the real-space scheme, we calculate the Green function,
G^{z}_{\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}}(t)
, for all pairs of spins (
\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}
), and then perform a FT in real space and time to obtain
G^{z}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)
(the FT to the frequency domain is performed as described above).
Finally, we relate the retarded Green function to the DSF via the fluctuation-dissipation theorem

S^{zz}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)=-\frac{1}{\pi}Im[G^{z}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)].

(19)

II
Network architectures and hyperparameters

As mentioned in the main text, the real-space-scheme is implemented with restricted Boltzmann machine (RBM) NQSs
Carleo and Troyer 2017
,
which are architectures composed by a single fully-connected (dense) hidden layer.
Concretely, RBMs are defined as

\psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{s})=\prod_{i=1}^{M}\cosh\left(\sum_{j=1}^{N}W_{i,j}s_{j}+b_{i}\right),

(20)

where the set of variational parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}=(W,\boldsymbol{b})
are the complex weight matrix,
W
, and the hidden bias,
\boldsymbol{b}
.
The hyperparameter
M
represents the number of nodes in the single hidden layer, which sets the
total number of variational parameters, i.e.,

N_{p}=2(NM+M)
.

For the results shown in Fig.
1
(b1) and (c1) we consider
M=40
.

The momentum-space scheme is implemented with the convolutional neural network ansatz
Schmitt and Heyl 2020
.
Specifically, we consider architectures composed of a set of
L_{l}
stacked convolutional layers.
Each output neuron of a convolutional layer is described by

a^{(l)}_{c,k}(s)=f_{l}\left(\sum_{c^{\prime}=1}^{\alpha_{(l-1)}}\sum_{t=1}^{F^{(l)}}\tilde{W}^{(c,l)}_{k,c^{\prime},t}a^{(l-1)}_{c^{\prime},T_{k}(t)}+b_{c}^{(l)}\right),

(21)

where
a^{(l)}_{c,k}
represent a value at a channel
c
of an output layer
l
; the index
c=1,...,\alpha_{l}
runs over the total number of output channels.
The value of a neuron,
a^{(l)}_{c,k}
, is defined by an affine map that resembles a convolution of the previous layer with
a filter along an orbit generated by translations.
Specifically, the individual neurons
a^{(l)}_{c,k}
are coupled with identical weights
\tilde{W}
to the neurons of the previous layer,
which are transformed by translations,
T_{k}(t)
, by some fixed
stride
.
In particular, we consider a
2D
filter with size
F\times F
.
Such map can also include a bias
b_{m}^{(l)}
for each channel.
Finally,
f_{l}(...)
represents an activation function.

Starting with the initial layer, which is the spin configuration
a^{l=0}_{1,j}(s)=s_{j}
, and considering the recursive relation for the
L_{l}
convolutional layers,
we can define the logarithm of the wave function in terms of the last convolutional layer

\ln\psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\sum_{c,k}a^{(L_{l})}_{c,k}(s).

(22)

The total number of variational parameters a CNN NQS is given by

N_{p}^{CNN}=2\left(F^{2}\sum_{l=1}^{L_{l}}\alpha_{l-1}\alpha_{l}+N_{b}\right),

(23)

where
\alpha_{0}=1
and
N_{b}
is the number of biases.
In this work, we consider CNN NQSs with the following structure
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},...,\alpha_{L_{l}};F)
, where

\alpha_{k}
is the number of channels in each layer
k
.
Furthermore, we use a stride
\boldsymbol{s}=[1,1]
.

IV
Ground state results for the 2D RyM

As a complement to the results of the 2D RyM,
we discuss the onset of the CbDW and StDW phases by
considering the ground-state (GS) correlations

C^{z}(\boldsymbol{r})=\left<\sigma^{z}_{\boldsymbol{0}}\sigma^{z}_{\boldsymbol{r}}\right>-\left<\sigma^{z}_{\boldsymbol{0}}\right>\left<\sigma^{z}_{\boldsymbol{r}}\right>.

(24)

Particularly, we show in Figs.
7
(a) and (b) the behavior of
the static structure factor,
S_{\boldsymbol{q}}=1/N_{s}\sum_{\boldsymbol{r}}e^{i\boldsymbol{r}\boldsymbol{q}}C^{z}(\boldsymbol{r})
, along the lines
R_{b}=1.2
and
R_{b}=1.45
, respectively.
For
R_{b}=1.2
, we note an increase of the
S_{\boldsymbol{q=M}}
for
\delta\approx 1.05
, indicating the emergence of a phase with CbDW pattern of correlations, while for
R_{b}=1.45
we note an increase of both

S_{\boldsymbol{q=M}}
as
S_{\boldsymbol{q=X}}
for
\delta\approx 2.1
, indicating the emergence of the StDW phase. Those results are in agreement with previous simulations of GS properties the 2D RyM
Samajdar et al. 2020
;
Kalinowski et al. 2022
.
