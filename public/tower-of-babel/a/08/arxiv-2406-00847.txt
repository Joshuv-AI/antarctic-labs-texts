Criteria for extension of commutativity to fractional iterates of holomorphic self-maps in the unit disc Thanks: This research was supported in part by Ministerio de Innovación y Ciencia, Spain, project PID2022-136320NB-I00.
Manuel D. Contreras †, Santiago Díaz-Madrigal †, Pavel Gumenyuk ‡

Abstract
. Let φ \varphi be a univalent non-elliptic self-map of the unit disc 𝔻 \mathbb{D} and let ( ψ t ) (\psi_{t}) be a continuous one-parameter semigroup of holomorphic functions in 𝔻 \mathbb{D} such that ψ 1 ≠ 𝗂𝖽 𝔻 \psi_{1}\neq{\sf id}_{\mathbb{D}} commutes with φ \varphi . This assumption does not imply that all elements of the semigroup ( ψ t ) (\psi_{t}) commute with φ \varphi . In this paper, we provide a number of sufficient conditions that guarantee that ψ t ∘ φ = φ ∘ ψ t {\psi_{t}\circ\varphi=\varphi\circ\psi_{t}} for all t > 0 {t>0} : this holds, for example, if φ \varphi and ψ 1 \psi_{1} have a common boundary (regular or irregular) fixed point different from their common Denjoy – Wolff point τ \tau , or when ψ 1 \psi_{1} has a boundary regular fixed point σ ≠ τ {\sigma\neq\tau} at which φ \varphi is isogonal, or when ( φ − 𝗂𝖽 𝔻 ) / ( ψ 1 − 𝗂𝖽 𝔻 ) (\varphi-{\sf id}_{\mathbb{D}})/(\psi_{1}-{\sf id}_{\mathbb{D}}) has an unrestricted limit at τ \tau . In addition, we analyze how φ \varphi behaves in the petals of the semigroup ( ψ t ) (\psi_{t}) .

1.
Introduction and main results

This paper is motivated by the following natural and quite old problem in discrete holomorphic iteration: given a holomorphic self-map of the unit disc, i.e.
\varphi\in{\sf Hol}(\mathbb{D})
, determine or at least analyse those
\psi\in{\sf Hol}(\mathbb{D})
that commute with
\varphi
, see
[
1
, Section 4.10]
. This kind of questions has also been treated in the framework of fractional iteration of holomorphic self-maps, with the aim to study all continuous one-parameter semigroups in the unit disc
(\psi_{t})
that commute with a given continuous one-parameter semigroup
(\varphi_{t})
in the sense that
\psi_{t}\circ\varphi_{s}=\varphi_{s}\circ\psi_{t}
for all
t,s\geqslant 0
, see for instance
[
21
]
and
[
22
]
.

In this paper, we tackle an intermediate situation, which has interesting implications in the discrete as well as in the fractional framework. Given
\varphi\in\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{D})
, a univalent self-map of the unit disc, the
centralizer

\mathcal{Z}(\varphi)
of
\varphi
is defined as

\mathcal{Z}(\varphi):=\{\psi\in\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{D}):\varphi\circ\psi=\psi\circ\varphi\},\quad\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{D}):=\{\psi:\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{D}\penalty\ \text{\penalty\ holomorphic injective}\}.

The problem we are interested in is the following:

Problem 1.1
.

Fix
\varphi\in\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{D})
and a continuous one-parameter semigroup in the unit disc
(\psi_{t})
. Suppose that
{\psi_{1}\in\mathcal{Z}(\varphi)}
, with
\psi_{1}\neq{\sf id}_{\mathbb{D}}
. Does it necessarily follow that
\psi_{t}\in\mathcal{Z}(\varphi)

for all

{t>0}
? If not, provide conditions on
\varphi
and
\psi_{1}
— and maybe on some finite number of
\psi_{t}
’s — under which the relation
{\psi_{1}\in\mathcal{Z}(\varphi)}
implies that the whole semigroup
(\psi_{t})
is contained in
\mathcal{Z}(\varphi)
.

First of all,
it is worth mentioning that the assumption of
\varphi
being univalent is completely natural and not restrictive. As we will see (Propositions
3.3
and
11.1
), univalence is a necessary condition for
\varphi
to commute with a non-trivial continuous one-parameter semigroup.
We would also like to underline that the context of this problem is really different from the other two mentioned above because of its lack of symmetry.
In particular, for two continuous one-parameter semigroups in the unit disc,
(\varphi_{t})
and
(\psi_{t})
, we can have
{\psi_{1}\in\mathcal{Z}(\varphi_{t})}
for all
{t>0}
and at the same time,
{\varphi_{1}\not\in\mathcal{Z}(\psi_{t})}
for some
{t>0}
; see Example
3.1
. The same example shows that, in general, the answer to the question in Problem
1.1
is negative.

When
\varphi
is elliptic, the above Problem
1.1
was implicitly solved by Cowen
[
18
]
. For the sake of completeness, at the end of the paper we include a brief appendix with a very simple, and independent from Cowen’s work, solution for the elliptic case; see Proposition
11.1
.
In the rest of the paper, we restrict ourselves to the non-elliptic case.

In that non-elliptic case, it is not difficult to give a complete answer to Problem
1.1
in terms of the Koenigs function
H
of the semigroup
(\psi_{t})
. Indeed,
{(\psi_{t})\subset\mathcal{Z}(\varphi)}
if and only there exists
{c\in\mathbb{C}}
such that
{H\circ\varphi}={H+c}
, see Proposition
3.3
. The latter condition means exactly that
\varphi
is affine with respect to
\psi_{1}
(see Definition
2.6
and, in general, Section
2.4
for further details). Moreover, as a quite direct consequence of our results in
[
13
]
, we can answer the question in Problem
1.1
positively if the semigroup
(\psi_{t})
is hyperbolic or parabolic of zero hyperbolic step. However, when
(\psi_{t})
is parabolic of positive hyperbolic step, giving any significant answer to Problem
1.1
that does not involve
H
or the infinitesimal generator
{G=1/H^{\prime}}
is really not that easy.

In the following theorem, we summarize our positive answers to Problem
1.1
including the two ones described in the former paragraph (as items
(a)
and
(b)
). For the definition of isogonality at a boundary point involved in
(e)
, see Section
8
. Other useful definitions can be found in the preliminaries, see Section
2
. Note that in our terminology every non-elliptic self-map is different from the identity map
{\sf id}_{\mathbb{D}}
. Furthermore, by a repelling fixed point we mean a boundary regular fixed point different from the Denjoy – Wolff point.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\varphi\in\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{D})
be non-elliptic and
(\psi_{t})
be a continuous one-parameter semigroup in
\mathbb{D}
such that
\psi_{1}\neq{\sf id}_{\mathbb{D}}
and
\varphi\circ\psi_{1}=\psi_{1}\circ\varphi
. Assume that one of the following conditions holds:

(a)

\varphi
is affine with respect to
\psi_{1}
,

(b)

\psi_{1}
is either hyperbolic or parabolic of zero hyperbolic step,

(c)

there exist
r\in(0,+\infty)\setminus\mathbb{Q}
such that
\psi_{r}\in\mathcal{Z}(\varphi)
,

(d)

the limit

\lim_{z\to\tau}\,\frac{\varphi(z)-z}{\,\psi_{1}(z)-z\,},

where
\tau
is the Denjoy – Wolff point of
\psi_{1}
(and hence also of
\varphi
),
exists unrestrictedly in
\mathbb{D}
,

(e)

\psi_{1}
has a repelling fixed point at which
\varphi
is isogonal,

or

(f)

\varphi
and
\psi_{1}
have a common boundary fixed point different from the Denjoy – Wolff point.

Then,
\psi_{t}\in\mathcal{Z}(\varphi)
for all
t>0
.

In general, conditions
(b)
and
(d)
–
(f)
in Theorem
1.2
are only sufficient ones.
At the same time, it is worth remarking that these conditions involve
\varphi
and
\psi_{1}
, but do not depend on the knowledge of other elements of the semigroup
(\psi_{t})
, its Koenigs map, or infinitesimal generator. A priori, this is not the case for the
necessary and sufficient
condition
(a)
, see Definition
2.6
. However, in many cases our next result allows to bypass this difficulty.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
\varphi\in\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{D})
be parabolic of positive hyperbolic step, and let
\psi\in\mathcal{Z}(\varphi)\setminus\{{\sf id}_{\mathbb{D}}\}
. Denote by
\tau
the Denjoy – Wolff point of
\varphi
and let

R_{\varphi,\psi}(z):=\frac{\psi(z)-z}{\varphi(z)-z},\quad z\in\mathbb{D}.

Then, the following statements hold.

(A)

The sequence
(R_{\varphi,\psi}\circ\varphi^{\circ n})
converges locally uniformly in
\mathbb{D}
to some function
f_{\varphi,\psi}\in{\sf Hol}(\mathbb{D},\mathbb{C})
.

(B)

\psi
is affine with respect to
\varphi
if and only if   the function
f_{\varphi,\psi}
is constant in
\mathbb{D}
. In fact, in such a case,
f_{\varphi,\psi}(\zeta)=\angle\lim_{z\to\tau}\big(\psi(z)-z\big)/\big(\varphi(z)-z\big)
for all
{\zeta\in\mathbb{D}}
.

(C)

Let
{(S,h_{\varphi},z\mapsto z+1)}
, where
S=\mathbb{H}:={\{z\colon\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}z>0\}}
or
{S=-\mathbb{H}}
, stand for the canonical holomorphic model of
\varphi
. Then
f_{\varphi,\psi}\circ h_{\varphi}^{-1}
extends holomorphically to a map

G:S\to S\cup\mathbb{R}
with
{G(w+1)=G(w)}
for all
{w\in S}
.

(D)

The self-map
g(w):=w+G(w)
is univalent in
S
,
{g(h_{\varphi}(\mathbb{D}))\subset h_{\varphi}(\mathbb{D})}
, and

\psi\,=\,h_{\varphi}^{-1}\circ g\circ h_{\varphi}\,=\,h_{\varphi}^{-1}\circ\big(h_{\varphi}+f_{\varphi,\psi}\big).

Coming back to Theorem
1.2
, condition
(f)
is probably the most interesting and deepest result of the paper. In fact, according to the following theorem, this condition implies the stronger conclusion that
\varphi
is an element of
(\psi_{t})
. We exclude from the statement hyperbolic automorphisms,
because in this case the result is essentially known: if
\varphi
is a hyperbolic automorphism, then
(\psi_{t})
is a hyperbolic one-parameter group and all the three conditions in Theorem
1.4
below hold, except that in condition
(a)
the words “for some
{t_{0}>0}
” have to be replaced by “for some
{t_{0}\in\mathbb{R}}
”; see e.g.
[
1
, Section 4.10]
(see also
[
13
]
).

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
\varphi\in\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{D})
be a non-elliptic self-map different from a hyperbolic automorphism, and suppose that it has a boundary fixed point
\sigma
different from its Denjoy – Wolff point. Let
(\psi_{t})
be a continuous one-parameter semigroup such that
{\psi_{1}\in\mathcal{Z}(\varphi)}\setminus\{{\sf id}_{\mathbb{D}}\}
. Then the following conditions are equivalent:

(a)

{\varphi=\psi_{t_{0}}}
for some
{t_{0}>0}
;

(b)

(\psi_{t})\subset\mathcal{Z}(\varphi)
;

(c)

\sigma
is a boundary fixed point also for
(\psi_{t})
.

Remark 1.5
.

If at least one of the self-maps
\varphi
or
\psi_{1}
--- and hence both of them
1
1

1

See
[
18
, Corollary 4.1]
.
— are hyperbolic, then all the equivalent conditions
(a)
,
(b)
, and
(c)
in the above theorem are automatically satisfied. This follows from the fact that by
[
13
, Propositions 6.6 and 6.9]
, in the hyperbolic case, we have
{\mathcal{Z}(\varphi)=\mathcal{Z}(\psi_{1})=\{\psi_{t}:t\geqslant 0\}}
.

Remark 1.6
.

Let
\varphi
and
\psi
be two commuting holomorphic self-maps of
\mathbb{D}
and suppose that
\varphi
has a boundary fixed point
\sigma
. It is known (see, e.g.,
[
8
]
; see also Remark
3.2
) that in general,
\sigma
does not have to be a boundary fixed point for
\psi
, even if we additionally assume that
\sigma
is regular,
\varphi
is univalent, and
\psi
is embeddable in a continuous one-parameter semigroup. Therefore, the implications
(b)

\Rightarrow

(c)
and
(b)

\Rightarrow

(a)
in the theorem stated above can be regarded as an illustration of a neat difference between commutativity with a self-map (and hence with all its natural iterates) and commutativity with all the fractional iterates.

If a continuous one-parameter semigroup
(\psi_{t})
is contained in the centralizer of a non-elliptic self-map
\varphi
, then by Theorem
1.4
every boundary fixed point of
\varphi
has to be among boundary fixed points of
(\psi_{t})
. The converse is not true: we may have a continuous one-parameter semigroup
(\psi_{t})\subset\mathcal{Z}(\varphi)
with many boundary fixed points, while
\varphi
has no boundary fixed point other than its Denjoy – Wolff point; see, e.g., Example
3.1
with
(\varphi_{t})
and
(\psi_{t})
interchanged. This leads us to the following natural question (in which, in fact, we impose some weaker assumptions).

Problem 1.7
.

Let
\varphi\in\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{D})
be a non-elliptic self-map different from a hyperbolic automorphism and let
(\psi_{t})
be a continuous one-parameter semigroup such that
{\psi_{1}\in\mathcal{Z}(\varphi)\setminus\{{\sf id}_{\mathbb{D}}\}}
. Further, suppose that
{\varphi\not\in\{\psi_{t}:t\geqslant 0\}}
. Is there any relationship between
\varphi
and the boundary fixed points of
(\psi_{t})
in this case?

A natural way to attack this problem is through the concept of petal of a continuous one-parameter semigroup, concept intimately linked to the notion of boundary regular fixed point of such a semigroup. We refer the reader to Section
5
for more details, or to the monograph
[
10
, Section 13]
for a detailed exposition, which includes a number of related results. It is worth mentioning that this theory of petals plays also an important role in the proof of Theorem
1.4
.

We begin by showing that, as a general fact,
\varphi
always maps petals into petals and in a rather specific way. The degenerate case of
(\psi_{t})
being a one-parameter group is excluded from the statement of the theorem below; it is covered separately, see Remark
7.1
in Section
7
.

Theorem 1.8
.

Let
\varphi\in\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{D})
be non-elliptic and let
(\psi_{t})\not\subset{\sf Aut}(\mathbb{D})
be a continuous one-parameter semigroup in
\mathbb{D}
with Denjoy – Wolff point
{\tau\in\partial\mathbb{D}}
such that
\psi_{1}\in\mathcal{Z}(\varphi)\setminus\{{\sf id}_{\mathbb{D}}\}
.

(A)

Assume that
\Delta
is a hyperbolic petal of
(\psi_{t})
with associated boundary repelling fixed point
\sigma\in\partial\mathbb{D}
. Then, one and only one of the following three statements holds:

(i)

\varphi(\Delta)=\Delta
. In this case, there exists
t_{0}>0
such that
\varphi=\psi_{t_{0}}
and, in particular,
\sigma
is a repelling fixed point of
\varphi
.

(ii)

\Delta\cap\varphi(\Delta)=\varnothing
and there exists a hyperbolic petal
\Delta^{\prime}
of
(\psi_{t})
with associated repelling fixed point
\sigma^{\prime}
such that
\varphi(\Delta)\subset\Delta^{\prime}
and
\angle\lim_{z\to\sigma}\varphi(z)=\sigma^{\prime}
.

(iii)

\Delta\cap\varphi(\Delta)=\varnothing
and there exists a parabolic petal
\Delta^{\prime}
of
(\psi_{t})
such that
\varphi(\Delta)\subset\Delta^{\prime}
and
\angle\lim_{z\to\sigma}\varphi(z)=\tau
. In this case,
\sigma
is an irregular contact point for
\varphi
.

(B)

Assume that
\Delta
is a parabolic petal of
(\psi_{t})
. Then the following two statements hold:

(a)

\varphi(\Delta)\subset\Delta
;

(b)

\varphi(\Delta)=\Delta
if and only if
\varphi=\psi_{t_{0}}
for some
t_{0}>0
.

In the hyperbolic case, by the reason mentioned in Remark
1.5
, only alternative
(A)(i)
may occur, and moreover,
(B)
becomes trivial because a hyperbolic semigroup cannot have parabolic petals.

In the parabolic case, based on Theorem
1.8
, we are then able to give a quite complete answer to Problem
1.7
as follows.
We again exclude from consideration the case
(\psi_{t})\subset{\sf Aut}(\mathbb{D})
, in which the result, with some obvious modifications, holds trivially.

Theorem 1.9
.

Let
\varphi\in\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{D})
be a non-elliptic self-map with Denjoy – Wolff point
{\tau\in\partial\mathbb{D}}
and let
{(\psi_{t})\not\subset{\sf Aut}(\mathbb{D})}
be a continuous one-parameter semigroup in
\mathbb{D}
such that
{\psi_{1}\in\mathcal{Z}(\varphi)\setminus\{{\sf id}_{\mathbb{D}}\}}
. Suppose that
\varphi
is not an element of
(\psi_{t})
. Then the following statements hold.

(I)

Both
\varphi
and
(\psi_{t})
are parabolic. Moreover,
(\psi_{t})
has at most one parabolic petal.

(II)

Suppose
(\psi_{t})
has a parabolic petal
\Delta_{*}.
Then for each hyperbolic petal
\Delta
of
(\psi_{t})
, there exist a finite collection
\Delta_{1},\,\Delta_{2},...,\,\Delta_{n}\,
of pairwise disjoint hyperbolic petals of
(\psi_{t})
such that

\Delta_{1}=\Delta,\quad\varphi(\Delta_{k})\subset\Delta_{k+1},\ k=1,...,n-1,\quad\varphi(\Delta_{n})\subset\Delta_{*}.

Moreover,

\varphi(\sigma_{k})=\sigma_{k+1},\ k=1,...,n-1,\quad\varphi(\sigma_{n})=\tau,

where
\sigma_{k}

(k=1,...,n)
stands for the repelling fixed point associated with
\Delta_{k}
.

(III)

Suppose that
(\psi_{t})
has no parabolic petal. Then for each hyperbolic petal
\Delta
of
(\psi_{t})
, there exists a sequence
(\Delta_{n})
of pairwise disjoint hyperbolic petals of
(\psi_{t})
such that

\Delta_{1}=\Delta,\quad\varphi(\Delta_{n})\subset\Delta_{n+1},\quad n\in\mathbb{N}.\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad\mbox{\penalty\ }

Moreover,

\varphi(\sigma_{n})=\sigma_{n+1},\ n\in\mathbb{N},\quad\text{\penalty\ and}\quad\lim_{n\to\infty}\sigma_{n}=\tau,

where
\sigma_{n}

(n\in\mathbb{N})
stands for the repelling fixed point associated with
\Delta_{n}
.

Remark 1.10
.

The above two theorems imply that every repelling fixed point
\sigma
of
{\psi:=\psi_{1}}
is a contact point for
\varphi
and that
\varphi(\sigma)
is also a boundary regular fixed point of
\psi
. Moreover, if
{\varphi(\sigma)\neq\sigma}
, then the (forward) orbit of
\sigma
w.r.t.
\varphi
(extended to a.e. point of
\partial\mathbb{D}
by angular limits) either hits the Denjoy – Wolff point within finite time, or it consists of pairwise distinct repelling fixed points of
\psi
, but tends to the Denjoy – Wolff point in the limit. In part, this resembles the situation with two general commuting holomorphic self-maps
\varphi,\psi\in{\sf Hol}(\mathbb{D})
studied by Bracci
[
8
]
. In this general case,
\varphi
can have a repelling cycle consisting of repelling fixed points of
\psi
, but this possibility is ruled out if
\varphi
is univalent and non-elliptic, see
[
8
, Proposition 5.2]
. At the same time, the results established in
[
8
]
do not seem to exclude some other situations not occurring in our case, such as a possibility that the orbit of
\sigma
hits within finite time a common fixed point of
\varphi
and
\psi
different from their Denjoy – Wolff point. Moreover, in contrast to the context of Theorem
1.9
, it is not clear in general whether any two orbits starting from different repelling fixed points of
\psi
necessarily fall into the same category.

2.1.
Notation

As usual, we denote the unit disc by

{\mathbb{D}:=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|<1\}}
,
and we write
\mathbb{H}:={\{w\in\mathbb{C}:\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}w>0\}}
for the upper half-plane and
\mathbb{H}_{\mathrm{Re}}:={\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}w>0\}}
for the right half-plane.

Furthermore, denote by
{\sf Hol}(D,E)
the class of all holomorphic mappings of a domain
D\subset\mathbb{C}
into a set
E\subset\mathbb{C}
,
and let
\mathcal{U}(D,E)
stand for the class of all
univalent
(i.e. injective holomorphic) mappings from
D
to
E
. As usual, we endow
{\sf Hol}(D,E)
and
\mathcal{U}(D,E)
with the topology of locally uniform convergence. In case
E=D
, we will write
{\sf Hol}(D)
and
\mathcal{U}(D)
instead of
{\sf Hol}(D,D)
and
\mathcal{U}(D,D)
, respectively.

For a self-map
\varphi:D\to D
of a domain
D\subset\mathbb{C}
and
{n\in\mathbb{N}}
we denote by
\varphi^{\circ n}
the
n
-th iterate of
\varphi
, and let
\varphi^{\circ 0}:={\sf id}_{D}
. Moreover, if
\varphi
is an automorphism of
D
, then for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we denote by
\varphi^{\circ-n}
the
n
-th iterate of
\varphi^{-1}
.

2.2.
Holomorphic self-maps of the unit disc

The study of the dynamics of an arbitrary holomorphic self-map
\varphi
of the unit disc
\mathbb{D}
is a classical and well-established branch of Complex Analysis. An important role is played by the fixed points, all of which — except for at most one — lie on the boundary and hence should be understood in the sense of angular limits:
{\sigma\in\partial\mathbb{D}}
is a
boundary fixed point
of
\varphi\in{\sf Hol}(\mathbb{D})
if
\varphi(\sigma):={\angle\lim_{z\to\sigma}\varphi(z)}
exists and coincides with
\sigma
. It is known that the angular derivative
\varphi^{\prime}(\sigma)
exists at every boundary fixed point
\sigma
, but it can be infinite. In this latter case,
\sigma
is referred to as a
super-repelling
fixed point; otherwise, i.e. when
\varphi^{\prime}(\sigma)
is finite, it is in fact a positive real number and the boundary fixed point
\sigma
is said to be
regular
(
BRFP
for short).

The central result in the area is the Denjoy – Wolff Theorem, which states that if
\varphi
is different from an elliptic automorphism (i.e. not an automorphism of
\mathbb{D}
possessing a fixed point in
\mathbb{D}
), then the sequence of the iterates
(\varphi^{\circ n})
converges locally uniformly in
\mathbb{D}
to a certain point
{\tau\in\overline{\mathbb{D}}}
. This point is called the
Denjoy – Wolff point
of
\varphi
. Moreover, if
\tau\in\partial\mathbb{D}
, it is the unique boundary fixed point at which the angular derivative
\varphi^{\prime}(\tau)
is finite and belongs to
(0,1]
. In particular, for every BRFP
\sigma
different from the Denjoy – Wolff point
\tau
, we have
{\varphi^{\prime}(\sigma)}\in{(1,+\infty)}
. By this reason such points are referred to as
repelling fixed points
of
\varphi
.

According to the position of the Denjoy – Wolff point
\tau
and to the value of the
multiplier

\varphi^{\prime}(\tau)
, holomorphic self-maps
\varphi\in{\sf Hol}(\mathbb{D})
different from elliptic automorphisms are divided into three categories. Namely,
\varphi
is called:

(a)

elliptic
if
\tau\in\mathbb{D}
,

(b)

hyperbolic
if
\tau\in\partial\mathbb{D}
and
\varphi^{\prime}(\tau)<1
, and

(c)

parabolic
if
\tau\in\partial\mathbb{D}
such that
\varphi^{\prime}(\tau)=1
.

The identity mapping
{\sf id}_{\mathbb{D}}
and all elliptic automorphisms of
\mathbb{D}
are conventionally included in the category (a) of elliptic self-maps.
Similarly, for an elliptic automorphism different from
{\sf id}_{\mathbb{D}}
, its Denjoy – Wolff point is defined to be its unique fixed point in
\mathbb{D}
.

Parabolic self-maps can have very different properties depending on the so-called
hyperbolic step
.
Denote by
\rho_{\mathbb{D}}
the hyperbolic distance in
\mathbb{D}
, and let
\varphi\in{\sf Hol}(\mathbb{D})
be non-elliptic. Thanks to the Schwarz – Pick Lemma, for the orbit
\big(z_{n}\big):=\big(\varphi^{\circ n}(z_{0})\big)
of any point
{z_{0}\in\mathbb{D}}
, there exists a finite limit
q(z_{0}):=\lim_{n\to+\infty}\rho_{\mathbb{D}}(z_{n},z_{n+1})
. It is known, see e.g.
[
1
, Corollary 4.6.9]
, that either
q(z_{0})>0
for all
{z_{0}\in\mathbb{D}}\penalty\
, or
q\equiv 0
in
\mathbb{D}
. The self-map
\varphi
is said to be of
positive
or of
zero hyperbolic step
depending on whether the former or the latter alternative occurs.
If
\varphi
is
hyperbolic, then it is always of positive hyperbolic step. However, there exist parabolic self-maps of zero as well as of positive hyperbolic step.

2.3.
Holomorphic models for univalent self-maps

An indispensable role in our study is played by the concept of a holomorphic model, which goes back to Pommerenke
[
29
]
, Baker and Pommerenke
[
4
]
, and Cowen
[
17
]
and which is discussed below for the special case of a univalent self-map. The terminology we use is mainly borrowed from
[
2
]
.

Definition 2.1
.

A
holomorphic model
of
\varphi\in\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{D})
is any triple
\mathcal{M}:=(S,h,\alpha)
, where
S
is a Riemann surface,
\alpha
is an automorphism of
S
, and
h
is a univalent map from
\mathbb{D}
into
S
satisfying the following two conditions:

(HM1)

h\circ\varphi=\alpha\circ h
, and

(HM2)

S\,=\,\bigcup_{n\geqslant 0}\alpha^{\circ\,-n}(h(\mathbb{D}))
.

The Riemann surface
S
is called the
base space
, and the map
h
is called the
intertwining map
of the holomorphic model
\mathcal{M}
.

Every
\varphi\in\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{D})\setminus\{{\sf id}_{\mathbb{D}}\}
admits an essentially unique holomorphic model. More precisely, the following fundamental theorem holds.

Theorem 2.2

(
[
2
, Theorem 1.1]
)
.

Every
\varphi\in\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{D})
admits a holomorphic model. Moreover such a model is unique up to a model isomorphism; i.e., if
(S_{1},h_{1},\alpha_{1})
and
(S_{2},h_{2},\alpha_{2})
are holomorphic models for
\varphi
, then there exists a biholomorphic map
\eta
of
S_{1}
onto
S_{2}
such that

h_{2}=\eta\circ h_{1},\quad\alpha_{2}=\eta\circ\alpha_{1}\circ\eta^{-1}.

The type of a univalent self-map (elliptic, hyperbolic, or parabolic) is reflected in, and actually can be fully determined from the kind of holomorphic model
\varphi
admits. For an open interval
I\subset\mathbb{R}
, we define

S_{I}:=\mathbb{R}\times I=\{x+iy:x\in\mathbb{R},\,y\in I\}.

Theorem 2.3

(
[
17
]
, see also
[
2
]
)
.

Let
\varphi\in\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{D})\setminus\{{\sf id}_{\mathbb{D}}\}
. The following statements hold.

(1)

\varphi
is an elliptic automorphism with multiplier
\lambda\in\partial\mathbb{D}\setminus\{1\}
if and only if
\varphi
admits a holomorphic model of the form
{\mathcal{M}_{\varphi}:=(\mathbb{D},h,z\mapsto\lambda z)}
, where
{h\in{\sf Aut}(\mathbb{D})}
.

(2)

\varphi
is an elliptic self-map with multiplier
\lambda\in\mathbb{D}^{*}
(and hence it is not an automorphism) if and only if
\varphi
admits a holomorphic model of the form
{\mathcal{M}_{\varphi}:=(\mathbb{C},h,z\mapsto\lambda z)}
.

(3)

\varphi
is a hyperbolic self-map with multiplier
\lambda\in(0,1)
if and only if
\varphi
admits a holomorphic model of the form
\mathcal{M}_{\varphi}:=(S_{I},h,z\mapsto z+1)
, where
I=(a,b)
is a bounded open interval of length
{b-a=\pi/|\log\lambda|}
.

(4)

\varphi
is a parabolic self-map of positive hyperbolic step if and only if
\varphi
admits a holomorphic model of the form
{\mathcal{M}_{\varphi}:=(S_{I},h,z\mapsto z+1)}
, where
I
is an open unbounded interval different from the whole
\mathbb{R}
.

(5)

\varphi
is a parabolic self-map of zero hyperbolic step if and only if
\varphi
admits a holomorphic model of the form
{\mathcal{M}_{\varphi}:=(\mathbb{C},h,z\mapsto z+1)}
.

Remark 2.4
.

In the above theorem, we may assume that:

-

in case
(1)
,
h^{\prime}(\tau)>0
, where
\tau
is the Denjoy – Wolff point of
\varphi
;

-

in case
(2)
,
h^{\prime}(\tau)=1
, where
\tau
is the Denjoy – Wolff point of
\varphi
;

-

in cases
(3)
and
(5)
,
h(0)=0
;

-

in case
(4)
,
\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}h(0)=0
and
{S_{I}=S_{(0,+\infty)}=\mathbb{H}}
or
{S_{I}=S_{(-\infty,0)}=-\mathbb{H}}
.

Using the uniqueness part of Theorem
2.2
, one can show (see e.g.
[
1
, Corollary 4.6.12]
for details) that the above assumptions play the role of a normalization under which the holomorphic model
\mathcal{M}_{\varphi}
for a given
\varphi\in\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{D})\setminus\{{\sf id}_{\mathbb{D}}\}
is unique. Note that the normalization for cases
(3)
and
(5)
would also work in case
(4)
, but we prefer to use another normalization, so that for parabolic self-maps of positive hyperbolic step, the base space
S_{I}
of
\mathcal{M}_{\varphi}
coincides with
\mathbb{H}
or
-\mathbb{H}
. Moreover, replacing, if necessary,
\varphi
with
z\mapsto\overline{\varphi(\bar{z})}
we may assume that
{S_{I}=\mathbb{H}}
.

Definition 2.5
.

The unique holomorphic model
\mathcal{M}_{\varphi}
of a self-map
\varphi\in\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{D})\setminus\{{\sf id}_{\mathbb{D}}\}
defined in Theorem
2.3
and normalized as in Remark
2.4
is called
canonical (holomorphic) model
for
\varphi
. The intertwining map
h
of the canonical model
\mathcal{M}_{\varphi}
is called the
Koenigs function
, and
{\Omega:=h(\mathbb{D})}
is called the
Koenigs domain
of
\varphi
.

2.4.
Commuting holomorphic self-maps

It is clear that if two holomorphic self-maps
\varphi,\psi\in{\sf Hol}(\mathbb{D})\setminus\{{\sf id}_{\mathbb{D}}\}
commute, i.e.
{\varphi\circ\psi}={\psi\circ\varphi}
, and if one of them is elliptic, then the other is also elliptic and they share the Denjoy – Wolff point. The situation is not so evident when we consider non-elliptic self-maps. In 1973, Behan
[
5
]
, see also
[
1
, Section 4.10]
, proved that if
\varphi,\psi
are non-elliptic self-maps of
\mathbb{D}
with Denjoy – Wolff points
\tau_{\varphi}
and
\tau_{\psi}
, respectively, then:

(i)

if
\varphi
is not a hyperbolic automorphism, then
\varphi
and
\psi
share the Denjoy – Wolff point, i.e.
\tau_{\varphi}=\tau_{\psi}
;

(ii)

if
\varphi
is a hyperbolic automorphism, then
\psi
is a hyperbolic automorphism as well and it has the same fixed points as
\varphi
.

Later, Cowen proved that if
\varphi
and
\psi
are two non-elliptic commuting holomorphic self-maps of
\mathbb{D}
and if
\varphi
is hyperbolic, then
\psi
is also hyperbolic (and thus if
\varphi
is parabolic, then
\psi
is parabolic)
[
18
, Corollary 4.1]
, see also
[
3
, Theorem 1.3]
.

In contrast to the Denjoy – Wolff point, a repelling fixed point
\sigma
of a holomorphic self-map
\psi
commuting with
\varphi
does not have to be a boundary fixed point of
\varphi
: it is only guaranteed that
\sigma
is a
contact point
of
\varphi
, i.e. the angular limit
\varphi(\sigma):={\angle\lim_{z\to\sigma}\varphi(z)}
exists and belongs to
\partial\mathbb{D}
, and that
\varphi(\sigma)
is also a BRFP of
\psi
; see e.g.
[
8
]
or
[
1
, Sect. 4.10]
and references therein. Clearly, the study of contact points reduces to that of boundary fixed points by composing with suitable rotations, and as a result one can classify contact points of a holomorphic self-map into
regular
and
irregular
depending on whether the angular derivative at a given contact point is finite or not.

Restricted to
univalent
self-mappings of
\mathbb{D}
, in
[
13
]
we studied the so-called
centralizers
of non-elliptic self-maps
\varphi\in\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{D})
defined by

\mathcal{Z}(\varphi):=\big\{\psi\in\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{D}):\varphi\circ\psi=\psi\circ\varphi\big\}.

Let
{(S,h,z\mapsto z+1)}
be the canonical holomorphic model for the self-map
\varphi
. Then, by
[
13
, Theorem 3.3]
, a self-map
\psi\in\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{D})
commutes with
\varphi
if and only if
g_{\psi}:={h\circ\psi\circ h^{-1}\,:\,\Omega\to\Omega}:=h(\mathbb{D})
extends holomorphically to a univalent self-map of
S
commuting with
{w\mapsto w+1}
.

Moreover, if
\varphi
is hyperbolic or parabolic of zero hyperbolic step, then the function
g_{\psi}
corresponding to any
{\psi\in\mathcal{Z}(\varphi)}
is necessarily an affine map of the form
{w\mapsto w+c}
, where
c
is a (complex) constant; see
[
13
, Theorems 5.2 and 6.4]
. However, for the case of a parabolic self-map
\varphi
of positive hyperbolic step, the situation is quite different: there can be many
\psi
’s in the centralizer
\mathcal{Z}(\varphi)
, for which
g_{\psi}
is not an affine map.

Definition 2.6
.

In the above setting, we say that
\psi\in\mathcal{Z}(\varphi)
is
affine w.r.t.
\varphi
if
g_{\psi}
is an affine map.

Remark 2.7
.

It is easy to see that if
\psi\in\mathcal{Z}(\varphi)
is affine w.r.t.
\varphi
, then
g_{\psi}(w)=w+c
. Moreover, see
[
13
, Remark 4.3]
, the constant
c=c_{\varphi,\psi}
is given by

(2.1)

c_{\varphi,\psi}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\angle\lim_{z\to\tau}\big(\psi(z)-z\big)/\big(\varphi(z)-z\big),&\text{if $\varphi$ is parabolic,}\\[6.45831pt]
\hphantom{\angle}\frac{\log{\psi^{\prime}(\tau)}}{\log{\varphi^{\prime}(\tau)}},&\text{if $\varphi$ is hyperbolic,}\end{cases}

where
\tau
stands for the Denjoy – Wolff point of
\varphi
.

2.5.
One-parameter semigroups in the unit disc

The study of one-parameter semigroups of holomorphic self-maps goes back to the early 1900s. However, one of the first pivot works, which brought this topic to the forefront, was published much later, namely in 1978, by Berkson and Porta
[
6
]
, who studied continuous one-parameter semigroups of holomorphic self-maps of the unit disc
\mathbb{D}
in connection with composition operators. The current state of the art in this rich topic is presented in the monograph
[
10
]
; see also
[
20
]
.
We recall the definition straight away.

Definition 2.8
.

We say that a family
(\psi_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
(or to simplify,
(\psi_{t})
)
of holomorphic functions
{\psi_{t}:\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{D}}
is a
one-parameter semigroup
if it verifies the following two algebraic properties:

(i)

\psi_{0}={\sf id}_{\mathbb{D}}
;

(ii)

\psi_{t}\circ\psi_{s}=\psi_{t+s}\penalty\
for every
t,s\geqslant 0
.

If, in addition,
\psi_{t}\to\psi_{0}
uniformly on compact subsets of
\mathbb{D}
, as
t\to 0^{+}
, we say that the one-parameter semigroup
(\psi_{t})
is
continuous
.

Convention.

From now on, unless explicitly indicated otherwise, by a continuous one-parameter semigroup we will mean a
non-trivial
one, i.e. containing at least one element different from the identity map.

It is worth recalling that all elements of any continuous one-parameter semigroup are univalent functions (see, e.g.,
[
10
, Theorem 8.1.17]
). Moreover, all of them, except for the identity map, have the same Denjoy – Wolff point, so one can talk about elliptic and non-elliptic continuous one-parameter semigroups (see, e.g.,
[
10
, Theorem 8.3.1]
). A similar remark concerns the repelling and super-repelling fixed points (see
[
14
]
and
[
12
]
).

Given a continuous one-parameter semigroup
(\psi_{t})
, it is possible to show that all the functions of the semigroup
essentially
share their canonical model.
Indeed, for non-elliptic semigroups, if
(S,h,z\mapsto z+1)
is the canonical holomorphic model for
\psi_{1}
given in Theorem
2.3
, then the triple
\big(S,h,(z\mapsto z+t)_{t\geqslant 0}\big)
is the canonical holomorphic model for
(\psi_{t})
(see
[
10
, Theorem 9.3.5]
for the precise definition and further details). In particular, we have that

(2.2)

h\circ\psi_{t}=h+t\qquad\text{for all $\penalty\ t\geqslant 0$.}

It follows that
\psi_{t}={h^{-1}\circ(h+t)}
for all
{t\geqslant 0}
, we can differentiate this equality w.r.t.
t
and get
{\mathop{\mathrm{d}}\nolimits\hskip-0.74997pt\psi_{t}/\mathop{\mathrm{d}}\nolimits t=G\circ\psi_{t}}
, where
{G:=1/h^{\prime}}
is called the
infinitesimal generator
of
(\psi_{t})
.
(In a similar way, one can introduce the infinitesimal generator for an elliptic continuous one-parameter semigroup; see, e.g.,
[
10
, Theorem 10.1.4 (2)]
. A detailed description of the properties of infinitesimal generators can be found in
[
10
, Chapter 10]
.)

Moreover, in combination with Theorem
2.3
, the fact concerning holomorphic models of
\psi_{t}
’s mentioned above implies that the non-elliptic semigroup
(\psi_{t})
, with the exclusion of
{\psi_{0}={\sf id}_{\mathbb{D}}}
, is entirely contained in one of the categories: hyperbolic holomorphic self-maps, parabolic holomorphic self-maps of zero hyperbolic step, or those of positive hyperbolic step — and hence the whole semigroup can be classified accordingly.

Definition 2.9
.

The function
h
and the domain
\Omega:=h(\mathbb{D})
defined above are called the
Koenigs function
and
Koenigs domain
of the non-elliptic semigroup
(\psi_{t})
.

Remark 2.10
.

It is worth mentioning that if a continuous one-parameter semigroup
(\psi_{t})
contains an automorphism of
\mathbb{D}
different from
{\sf id}_{\mathbb{D}}
, then
(\psi_{t})\subset{\sf Aut}(\mathbb{D})
and in such case we say that
(\psi_{t})
is a
group
, as it can be extended to a one-parameter group by setting
{\psi_{t}:=(\psi_{-t})^{-1}}
for all
{t<0}
; see e.g.
[
10
, §8.2]
for more details.

3.
The starting example and a characterization of self-maps commuting with a semigroup

As it was mentioned in the introduction, there are functions
\varphi\in\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{D})
and continuous one-parameter semigroups
(\psi_{t})
in the unit disc such that
\varphi\circ\psi_{1}=\psi_{1}\circ\varphi
and, at the same time,
{\varphi\circ\psi_{t}}\neq{\psi_{t}\circ\varphi}
for some
{t>0}
. An example of this can be found in
[
13
, Example 8.5]
. Below we present a similar but more direct example.

Example 3.1
.

Consider the complex domain

\Omega:=\mathbb{C}\setminus\Big(\bigcup_{p\in\mathbb{Z}}\Gamma_{p}\Big),\quad\Gamma_{p}:=\{x+ip:\,x\leqslant 0\}.

Denote by
h
a Riemann map from
\mathbb{D}
onto
\Omega
and consider the continuous one-parameter semigroup
(\varphi_{t})
formed by the functions
\varphi_{t}:={h^{-1}\circ(z\mapsto z+t)\circ h}
.

Note that
\Psi(w):=w+i
is an automorphism of
\Omega
. Hence there exists a continuous one-parameter semigroup
(\Psi_{t})\subset\mathsf{Aut}(\Omega)
such that
{\Psi_{1}=\Psi}
.
Now consider the continuous one-parameter semigroup
(\psi_{t})
in
\mathbb{D}
defined by
\psi_{t}:={h^{-1}\circ\Psi_{t}\circ h}
for all
{t\geqslant 0}
.

Suppose that
{\psi_{t}\circ\varphi_{s}}={\varphi_{s}\circ\psi_{t}}
for some
{s,t>0}
. Then for any
{k\in\mathbb{N}}
and all
{z\in\Omega}
, we have

(3.1)

\Psi_{t}(z)=\Psi_{t}(z+ks)-ks.

By this relation,
\Psi_{t}
can be extended to a univalent function in
{\Omega-ks}
. Since
{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is arbitrary, this in fact means that
\Psi_{t}
extends as a univalent function to
{\bigcup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}(\Omega-ks)=\mathbb{C}}
. Therefore,
\Psi_{t}(w)=aw+b
for all
{w\in\Omega}
and some constants
{a\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}}
and
{b\in\mathbb{C}}
. Taking into account that
\Psi_{t}
is an automorphism of
\Omega
, it further follows that
{a=1}
and
{b=in}
for some
{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
. Hence
\Psi_{t}=\Psi^{\circ n}
. Since
{t>0}
and since the semigroup
(\Psi_{t})
is non-elliptic, the latter is possible only if
{t=n\in\mathbb{N}}
.

The above argument shows that although
\psi_{1}
commutes with the whole semigroup
(\varphi_{t})
, the functions
\psi_{t}
and
\varphi_{s}
do not commute whenever
{s>0}
and
{t>0}
is not integer. In particular, this shows that the answer to the question in Problem
1.1
is negative: in general, the fact
\psi_{1}
that commutes with
\varphi(:=\varphi_{1})
does not allow to conclude — if no additional condition is imposed — that the whole semigroup
(\psi_{t})
commutes with
\varphi
.

Remark 3.2
.

Note that in the above example, the self-map
\varphi:=\varphi_{1}
has infinitely many BRFPs
\sigma_{k}:=\lim_{x\to-\infty}h^{-1}\big(x+i(k+\tfrac{1}{2})\big)
,
{k\in\mathbb{Z}}
(see
[
12
]
). Although
\varphi
commutes with
\psi_{1}
, none of these points is fixed by
\psi_{1}
. In fact,
\psi_{1}(\sigma_{k})=\sigma_{k+1}
for all
{k\in\mathbb{Z}}
.

The example presented above gives a hint on how to characterize holomorphic self-maps of
\mathbb{D}
that commute with all elements of a given (non-elliptic) continuous one-parameter semigroup.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
(\psi_{t})
be a non-elliptic continuous one-parameter semigroup with Koenigs function
H
. For a holomorphic self-map
\varphi\in{\sf Hol}(\mathbb{D})
the following two condition are equivalent:

(a)

{\varphi\circ\psi_{t}}={\psi_{t}\circ\varphi}
for all
t>0
;

(b)

there exists a constant
{c\in\mathbb{C}}
such that

H\circ\varphi=H+c.

In particular, any
\varphi\in{\sf Hol}(\mathbb{D})
satisfying
(a)
is univalent.

Proof.

Clearly,
(b)
implies
(a)
. To prove the converse implication,
consider the holomorphic map
{g:\Omega\to\Omega:=H(\mathbb{D})}
defined by
g:={H\circ\varphi\circ H^{-1}}
.
Condition
(a)
is equivalent to

g(w+t)=g(w)+t\quad\text{for all\penalty\ $\penalty\ w\in\Omega\penalty\ $ and all\penalty\ $\penalty\ t\geqslant 0$.}

Fix some
w_{0}\in\Omega
. The above identity means that for all
w\in\{w_{0}+t:t\geqslant 0\}
, we have
{g(w)=w+c}
, where
{c:=g(w_{0})-w_{0}}
. By the identity principle for holomorphic functions,
{g(w)=w+c}
for all
{w\in\Omega}
, which is equivalent to
(b)
.

Finally, if
(b)
holds, then
\varphi=H^{-1}\circ(H+c)
and it immediately follows that
\varphi
is univalent.
∎

Remark 3.4
.

Differentiating both sides of the functional equation
H\circ\varphi=H+c
, one can write a further condition equivalent to conditions
(a)
and
(b)
in the above proposition:

(c)

G\circ\varphi=\varphi^{\prime}G
, where
G
stands for the infinitesimal generator of
(\psi_{t})
.

Note that in our main results established in this paper, it is not supposed that the
whole
semigroup
{(\psi_{t})}
is known. In most of the cases, we only assume that
\psi_{1}
is given. Therefore, in our situation, the above characterizations cannot be applied directly. Nevertheless, Proposition
3.3
turns out to be very helpful in some of our arguments.

4.
Proof of Theorem
1.3

We start by recalling a key result on holomorphic models for parabolic self-maps of positive hyperbolic step that we need and use strongly in the proof (restricting to the special case of a univalent self-map).

Theorem 4.1

(
[
29
]
; see also
[
27
, Lemma 2.2]
)
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{H}_{\mathrm{Re}})
be parabolic of positive hyperbolic step with Denjoy – Wolff point
\infty
, and let
w_{0}\in\mathbb{H}_{\mathrm{Re}}
. Then the sequence
{(h_{n})\subset\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{H}_{\mathrm{Re}})}
defined as

h_{n}(w):=\frac{f^{\circ n}(w)-i\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}f^{\circ n}(w_{0})}{\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}f^{\circ n}(w_{0})},\quad n\in\mathbb{N},\ w\in\mathbb{H}_{\mathrm{Re}},

converges uniformly on compact subsets of
\mathbb{H}_{\mathrm{Re}}
to a certain
h\in\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{H}_{\mathrm{Re}})
such that
h(w_{0})=1
. Moreover:

(i)

there exists
b=b(w_{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{*}:=\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
such that
h\circ f=h+ib
;

(ii)

(\mathbb{H}_{\mathrm{Re}},h,w\mapsto w+ib)
is a holomorphic model for
f
.

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

Let
\tau\in\partial\mathbb{D}
be the Denjoy – Wolff point of
\varphi
and consider the standard Cayley map
C(z):=\frac{\tau+z}{\tau-z},\ z\in\mathbb{D}
. Moreover, define

f:=C\circ\varphi\circ C^{-1}\in\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{H}_{\mathrm{Re}}),\quad\Psi:=C\circ\psi\circ C^{-1}\in\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{H}_{\mathrm{Re}}).

By the hypothesis,
\psi\neq{\sf id}_{\mathbb{D}}
and it commutes with the parabolic self-map
\varphi
. Therefore, by Behan’s Theorem
[
5
]
and by a result of Cowen
[
18
, Corollary 4.1]
,
\psi
is parabolic and has the same Denjoy – Wolff point
\tau
as
\varphi
. As a consequence, both
f
and
\Psi
are parabolic self-maps of
\mathbb{H}_{\mathrm{Re}}
with Denjoy – Wolff point at
\infty
.

Proof of
(A)
.

Fix
z\in\mathbb{D}
. A direct computation shows that

\frac{\psi(z)-z}{\varphi(z)-z}=\frac{1+f(C(z))}{1+\Psi(C(z))}\,\frac{\Psi(C(z))-C(z)}{f(C(z))-C(z)}.

Therefore, denoting
w:=C(z)\in\mathbb{H}_{\mathrm{Re}}
and, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

(4.1)

{\big(S_{b},\frac{1}{ib}(h\circ C),z\mapsto z+1\big)}

5.
Petals and commutativity I. Auxiliary results

Definition 5.1
.

Let
(\psi_{t})
be a continuous one-parameter semigroup of the unit disc. A connected component of
\mathcal{W}^{\circ}
, the interior of the backward invariant set
\mathcal{W}:={\bigcap_{t\geqslant 0}\psi_{t}(\mathbb{D})}={\bigcap_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\psi_{1}^{\circ n}(\mathbb{D})}

of
(\psi_{t})
.

It is clear that if
(\psi_{t})
is a group, then the backward invariant set
\mathcal{W}
is the unit disc and thus, it has a unique petal. If
(\psi_{t})
is not a group with Denjoy – Wolff point
\tau
, it was proved in
[
10
, Propositions 13.4.2 and 13.4.12]
that:

(1)

Every petal
\Delta
is a simply connected domain,

(2)

\psi_{t}(\Delta)=\Delta
for all
t\geqslant 0
and
(\psi_{t}|_{\Delta})
is a continuous group of automorphisms of
\Delta
,

(3)

\tau\in\partial\Delta
, and

(4)

there exists
\sigma\in\partial\mathbb{D}\cap\partial\Delta
(possibly
\sigma=\tau
) such that the backward orbits
\psi_{-t}(z_{0}):=(\psi_{t}|_{\Delta})^{-1}(z_{0})\to\sigma
as

for every
{z_{0}\in\Delta}
. Moreover,
\sigma
is a BRFP of the semigroup, and
\partial\Delta
contains no boundary fixed points of

other than
\sigma
and
\tau
. We say that the petal
\Delta
is
parabolic
if
\sigma=\tau
. Otherwise, we say that the petal is

.

(5)

If

has a parabolic petal, then
(\psi_{t})
is parabolic.

(6)

Every backward orbit

starting from a point
{z_{0}\in\mathcal{W}^{\circ}}
is
regular
, meaning that
{\sup_{t\geqslant 0}\rho_{\mathbb{D}}(z_{t},z_{t+1})<+\infty}
.

(7)

Suppose
\sigma\in\partial\mathbb{D}
is a boundary regular fixed point of
(\psi_{t})
different from
\tau
. Then there exists a unique hyperbolic petal
\Delta
such that
\sigma\in\partial\Delta
.

Following the conventional terminology in dynamical systems, we call the above BRFP

the
\alpha
-point
of the corresponding hyperbolic petal
\Delta
. In case of a parabolic petal, by its

-point we mean the Denjoy – Wolff point
\tau
.

In the rest of this section we suppose that
\varphi\in\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{D})
is a non-elliptic self-map with Denjoy – Wolff point
\tau\in\partial\mathbb{D}
, that
\varphi
is different from a hyperbolic automorphism, and that
(\psi_{t})
is a continuous one-parameter semigroup of

satisfying

.

Lemma 5.2
.

In the above assumptions,
\varphi(\mathcal{W})\subset\mathcal{W}
, where
\mathcal{W}
stands for the backward invariant set of
(\psi_{t})
. In particular, the image
\varphi(\Delta)
of any petal

of
(\psi_{t})
is contained again in some petal of
(\psi_{t})
(which may coincide with
\Delta
).

Proof.

Denote by

the Koenigs function of
(\psi_{t})
.
Using Abel’s equation (
2.2
), and taking into account that

is univalent, it is easy to see that

(5.1)

h(\mathcal{W})\penalty\ =\penalty\ \bigcap_{t\geqslant 0}\,h(\mathbb{D})+t\penalty\ =\penalty\ \bigcap_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\,h(\mathbb{D})+n.

The second equality holds because, again by (

{h(\mathbb{D})+t_{2}\subset h(\mathbb{D})+t_{1}}

{t_{2}\geqslant t_{1}\geqslant 0}

By the hypothesis

∎

Note that the petals of

are pairwise disjoint. Therefore, in Lemma

Lemma 5.3

Let

be the petal of

defined as above, i.e.

{\psi_{t}(z_{t})=z_{0}}

Since

Thus, we see that

∎

Lemma 5.4
.

If
\Delta
is a parabolic petal of
(\psi_{t})
, then
\varphi(\Delta)\subset\Delta
.

Proof.

By the definition of a parabolic petal, the
\alpha
-point
\sigma
of the petal
\Delta
coincides with the Denjoy – Wolff point
\tau
of the semigroup
(\psi_{t})
, which in turn coincides with the Denjoy – Wolff point of
\varphi
. Therefore,
{\varphi(\sigma)=\sigma}
. According to Lemma

, it follows that the petal
\Delta^{\prime}
containing

must be also parabolic.

By
[
10
, Theorem 13.5.7]
,
(\psi_{t})
has at most two parabolic petals. If it has exactly one parabolic petal, then we are done. If there are two distinct parabolic petals, then (again according
[
10
, Theorem 13.5.7]
) the images

,

, of the two parabolic petals w.r.t. the Koenigs map

are of the form
{P_{1}=\{w:\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}w<a\}}
and
{P_{2}=\{w:\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}w>b\}}
for some

. As a consequence, the semigroup

, and hence the self-map

, are of zero hyperbolic step. Since

, by
[
13
, Proposition 4.3]
,

is affine w.r.t.

, i.e.
{h\circ\varphi}={h+c}

and

were two different parabolic petals, then the translation by

{\varphi(\Delta)\subset\Delta^{\prime}=\Delta}
.
∎

Corollary 5.5

In the notation of Lemma

\varphi(\Delta)\subset\Delta
if and only if
{\varphi(\sigma)=\sigma}
.

Proof.

If
\Delta
is a hyperbolic petal, then the corollary follows from Lemma

So suppose
\Delta
is a parabolic petal. Then

. Moreover, as we have seen in the proof of Lemma

,
{\varphi(\sigma)=\sigma}
because in this case,

is the Denjoy – Wolff point for

and hence also for

. These two observations complete the proof.
∎

Lemma 5.6
.

Suppose that
\varphi
is not an element of
(\psi_{t})
. Then
(\psi_{t})
can have at most one parabolic petal.

Proof.

Suppose that
(\psi_{t})
has two distinct parabolic petals. Then, as in the proof of Lemma

, we see that
{h\circ\varphi}={h+c}
, where the constant

must be real because each of the half-planes

and

are mapped by the translation

into itself. Moreover,
{h(\mathbb{D})+c}={h\big(\varphi(\mathbb{D})\big)}\subset h(\mathbb{D})
and hence, by

, which contradicts the hypothesis.
∎

We conclude this section with a lemma, which is definitely known for specialists and holds also for non-univalent self-maps, but lacking a reference we prefer to include a short proof making use of commutativity.

Lemma 5.7
.

Let
\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2}\in\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{D})
be such that
\varphi_{1}^{\circ k}=\varphi_{2}^{\circ k}

Proof.

Since
\varphi_{1}
is univalent and non-elliptic, so is

stand for the canonical holomorphic model of

. Clearly, both

. Therefore, see e.g.
[

8.

Given

\Delta^{\prime}

\varphi(\Delta)\subset\Delta^{\prime}
and
\Delta^{\prime}
is a parabolic petal.

In the following two results, we analyse when

maps
\Delta

onto

\Delta^{\prime}
in both situations.

Proposition 8.1
.

Assume we are in
Situation A

\sigma

\sigma^{\prime}
be the
\alpha

\Delta

\Delta^{\prime}

(a)

\varphi(\Delta)=\Delta^{\prime}

(b)

\varphi

\sigma

(c)

the spectral values of
(\psi_{t})
at
\sigma

Moreover, if the above equivalent conditions hold, then
\varphi
is affine with respect to
\psi_{1}
and hence,
{(\psi_{t})\subset\mathcal{Z}(\varphi)}

Proposition 8.2
.

In

\varphi(\Delta)\neq\Delta^{\prime}
.

The proof of these two propositions require some backgrounds which we expose thereafter. We begin by recall the definition of isogonality, which we extend to the case of infinite angular limit.

Definition 8.3
.

A univalent function

is said to be

at a point
{\zeta\in\partial\mathbb{D}}
if the following two angular limits exist:

f(\zeta):=\angle\lim_{z\to\zeta}f(z)\in\mathbb{C}\cup\{\infty\}\quad\text{and}\quad\angle\lim_{z\to\zeta}\arg\frac{f_{0}(z)}{z-\zeta},

where
\arg
is to be understood as a continuous map from
\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
to
{\mathbb{R}/(2\pi\mathbb{Z})}
and

f_{0}(z):=\begin{cases}f(z)-f(\zeta)&\text{if\penalty\ $f(\zeta)\neq\infty$},\\
1/f(z)&\text{if\penalty\ $f(\zeta)=\infty$.}\end{cases}

Further, a univalent function
\Psi:\mathbb{H}_{\mathrm{Re}}\to\mathbb{C}
is said to be isogonal at
\omega\in\partial\mathbb{H}_{\mathrm{Re}}
if the composition
f:=\Psi\circ H_{\omega}
, where
H_{\omega}
is the Cayley map of
\mathbb{D}
onto
\mathbb{H}_{\mathrm{Re}}
with
{H_{\omega}(0)=0}
,
{H_{\omega}(1)=\omega}
, is isogonal at
{\zeta=1}
.

Remark 8.4
.

If
f\in\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{D})
and
\zeta
is a contact point of

, then an elementary argument shows that the isogonality condition requires that the image of the radial segment
[0,\zeta]
under

approaches the point
{f(\zeta)\in\partial\mathbb{D}}
orthogonally to
\partial\mathbb{D}
. Therefore, in this case, the isogonality at

is equivalent to

(8.1)

\angle\lim_{z\to\zeta}\mathop{\mathrm{Arg}}\frac{1-\overline{f(\zeta)}f(z)}{1-\overline{\zeta}z}=0,

where
\mathop{\mathrm{Arg}}w
stands for the unique value of the argument of

contained in
{(-\pi,\pi]}
.
Passing this remark to the right half-plane with the help of the Cayley map, we can say that a univalent self-map
{T\in\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{H}_{\mathrm{Re}})}
with a boundary fixed point at
0
is isogonal at
0
if and only if

(8.2)

\angle\lim_{w\to 0}\mathop{\mathrm{Arg}}\frac{T(w)}{w}=0.

Definition 8.5
.

be a repelling fixed point of a continuous one-parameter semigroup

(ii)

the map

is holomorphic and injective,
\angle\lim_{w\to 0}\Psi(w)=\sigma
, and
\psi
is isogonal at

, i.e.

(8.3)

\angle\lim_{w\to 0}\mathop{\mathrm{Arg}}\frac{1-\overline{\sigma}\Psi(w)}{w}=0;

for all

Remark 8.6
.

It is known
[

\lim_{z\to\tau}\frac{\varphi_{1}(z)-z}{\psi_{1}(z)-z}.

Then the semigroups
(\varphi_{t})
and
(\psi_{t})
commute.

This corollary is a generalization of
[
21
, Theorem 5(ii)]
, where the commutativity of
(\varphi_{t})
and
(\psi_{t})
is assured under the following three hypothesis: (i)
\varphi_{1}
and
\psi_{1}
commute; (ii) for all
{t\geqslant 0}
,
\varphi_{t}
and
\psi_{t}
have unrestricted limits at the common Denjoy – Wolff point
\tau
; and also (iii) the unrestricted limits
\lim_{z\to\tau}F^{\prime\prime}(z)
and
\lim_{z\to\tau}G^{\prime\prime}(z)
, where
F
and
G
stand for the infinitesimal generators of
(\varphi_{t})
and
(\psi_{t})
, respectively, exist and are both different from zero. But, denoting
A:=\lim_{z\to\tau}F^{\prime\prime}(z)
and
B:=\lim_{z\to\tau}G^{\prime\prime}(z)
, by
[
21
, Remark 3]
, we have that

\frac{A}{2}=\lim_{z\to\tau}\frac{\varphi_{1}(z)-\tau}{(z-\tau)^{2}},\quad\frac{B}{2}=\lim_{z\to\tau}\frac{\psi_{1}(z)-\tau}{(z-\tau)^{2}}.

Therefore, under those three hypothesis, the unrestricted limit in (
10.1
) does exist and, in fact, it is equal to
{A/B}
.

11.
Appendix: the elliptic case

In
[
18
, Corollary 4.9]
, Cowen proved that if two holomorphic self-maps
\varphi,\psi\in{\sf Hol}(\mathbb{D})\setminus{\sf Aut}(\mathbb{D})
commute and
\psi
is univalent and either elliptic or hyperbolic, then
\varphi
is also univalent. Furthermore, under the same assumptions, if another self-map
\widetilde{\psi}\in{\sf Hol}(\mathbb{D})\setminus{\sf Aut}(\mathbb{D})
commutes with
\psi
, then
\widetilde{\psi}
commutes with
\varphi
as well. It follows that, in the elliptic case, Problem
1.1
has a positive answer, at least if automorphisms of
\mathbb{D}
are excluded from consideration. Below we provide an elementary proof of this fact thus providing a positive answer to Problem
1.1
when
\varphi
is elliptic (except for the case of an automorphism satisfying
{\varphi^{\circ n}={\sf id}_{\mathbb{D}}}
for some
{n\in\mathbb{N}}
).

Proposition 11.1
.

Let
\varphi\in{\sf Hol}(\mathbb{D})
and let
(\psi_{t})
be an elliptic continuous one-parameter semigroup in
\mathbb{D}
. Suppose that
\psi_{1}
is not an automorphism and that
{\varphi\circ\psi_{1}}={\psi_{1}\circ\varphi}
. Then
\varphi
is univalent and
{\psi_{t}\in\mathcal{Z}(\varphi)}
for all
{t>0}
.

Proof.

According to the hypothesis of the proposition and Subsections
2.3
and
2.5
,
(\psi_{t})
admits a holomorphic model of the form
{\big(\mathbb{C},H,(z\mapsto e^{\lambda t}z)_{t\geqslant 0}\big)}
with
{\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}\lambda<0}
.
Write
\Omega=H(\mathbb{D})
. Consider
g:=H\circ\varphi\circ H^{-1}:\Omega\to\Omega
. Since
\psi^{\circ n}
commutes with
\varphi
for all
{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, we have

g(e^{-\lambda n}w)=e^{-\lambda n}g(w),\quad\textrm{ for all }\penalty\ w\in\Omega\penalty\ \text{ and all }\penalty\ n\in\mathbb{N}.

In particular,
{g(0)=0}
and it follows that the function
f(w):={g(w)/w}
,
{w\in\Omega\setminus\{0\}}
, has a holomorphic extension to
{w=0}
. Moreover,

f(e^{-\lambda n}w)=\frac{e^{-\lambda n}g(w)}{e^{-\lambda n}w}=f(w)\quad\textrm{ for all }\penalty\ w\in\Omega\penalty\ \text{ and all }\penalty\ n\in\mathbb{N}.

Passing to the limit as
{n\to+\infty}
, we have that
f(w)=f(0)=:c
for all
{w\in\Omega}
, i.e.
{g(w)=cw}
in
\Omega
. Therefore,
\varphi
is univalent and commutes with
\psi_{t}
for all
{t>0}
.
∎

The situation is different for automorphisms. More precisely, suppose that
\psi_{1}
is an elliptic automorphism with the fixed point
{\tau\in\mathbb{D}}
, and hence so are all
\psi_{t}
’s. Write
\mu:=\psi_{1}^{\prime}(\tau)
. It is not difficult to show, see e.g.
[
1
, Proposition 2.6.10]
, that:

(i)

if the multiplier
\mu
is not a root of the unity and if
{\varphi\in{\sf Hol}(\mathbb{D})}
commutes with
\psi_{1}
, then
\varphi
is a linear-fractional map with the same fixed points as
\psi_{1}
; hence, in this case,
\varphi
commutes with
\psi_{t}
for all
{t>0}
.

(ii)

However, if
\mu^{n}=1
for some
{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, then
{\varphi\in{\sf Hol}(\mathbb{D})}
commutes with
\psi_{1}
if and only if
\varphi
is of the form
\varphi={\gamma^{-1}\circ\big(z\mapsto z\phi(z^{n})\big)\circ\gamma}
, where
\phi\in{\sf Hol}(\mathbb{D})
and
\gamma(z):={(z-\tau)/(1-\overline{\tau}z)}
.

An argument similar to that given in the proof of Proposition
11.1
shows that in case
(ii)
,
\varphi
commutes with all
\psi_{t}
’s if and only if the function
\phi
is constant, which means that there are many
\varphi
’s that commute with
\psi_{1}
but not with the whole semigroup
(\psi_{t})
.
