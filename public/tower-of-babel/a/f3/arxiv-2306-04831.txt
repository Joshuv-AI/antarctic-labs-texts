Jordan–Kronecker invariants of Lie algebra representations: examples and computations
Ivan Kozlov

Abstract
In these paper we compute Jordan-Kronecker invariants of Lie algebra representations, introduced earlier by A. V. Bolsinov, A. M. Izosimov and I. K. Kozlov, for a number of representations. In particular, we compute them for • the sums of standard representations of gl ⁡ ( n ) \operatorname{gl}(n) , sl ⁡ ( n ) \operatorname{sl}(n) , so ⁡ ( n ) \operatorname{so}(n) , sp ⁡ ( n ) \operatorname{sp}(n) , and the Lie algebra of upper triangular matrices b ⁡ ( n ) \operatorname{b}(n) ; • the standard representation of Lie algebra of strictly upper triangular matrices n ⁡ ( n ) \operatorname{n}(n) ; • and for the differential of the congruence action of GL ⁡ ( n ) \operatorname{GL}(n) and SL ⁡ ( n ) \operatorname{SL}(n) on symmetric forms and skew-symmetric forms.

Introduction

The Jordan–Kronecker invariants for Lie algebra representation were introduced in
[
1
]
. They are defined as follows. Let
\rho:\mathfrak{g}\to\mathrm{gl}\,(V)
be a linear representation of a complex finite-dimensional Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
on a finite-dimensional vector space
V
. To this representation and an arbitrary element
x\in V
, one can naturally associate an operator

\displaystyle R_{x}:\mathfrak{g}\to V,

\displaystyle R_{x}(\xi)=\rho(\xi)x.

Consider a pair of such operators
R_{a},R_{b}
and the pencil
R_{a}+\lambda R_{b}=R_{a+\lambda b}
generated by them. It is well known that such a pencil can be completely characterized by a collection of simple numerical invariants (see Section
2
for details). The Jordan–Kronecker invariants of
\rho
are such invariants for a pencil
R_{a+\lambda b}
generated by a generic pair
(a,b)\in V\times V
.

2
Jordan-Kronecker invariants of representations

In this section we recall the definition of the JK invariants and their basic properties. This is mostly a retelling of
[
1
]
. We leave it for the sake of completeness.

2.1
Canonical form of a pair of linear maps

In this section, we recall the normal form theorem for a pair of linear maps. We first state the theorem in the matrix form, and then discuss the corresponding invariants.

Theorem 2.1
(on the Jordan-Kronecker normal form
[
3
]
).

Consider two complex vector spaces

U
and
V
. Then for every two linear maps
A,B:U\to V
there are bases in
U
and
V
in which the matrices of the pencil
\mathcal{P}=\{A+\lambda B\}
have the following block-diagonal form:

{\footnotesize A+\lambda B=\begin{pmatrix}0_{m,n}&&&\\
&\!\!\!A_{1}+\lambda B_{1}&&\\
&&\!\!\!\!\ddots&\\
&&&\!\!\!A_{k}+\lambda B_{k}\\
\end{pmatrix},}

(1)

where
0_{m,n}
is the zero
m\times n
-matrix, and each pair of the corresponding blocks
A_{i}
and
B_{i}
takes one of the following forms:

1. Jordan block with eigenvalue
\lambda_{0}\in\mathbb{C}

A_{i}=\left(\begin{matrix}\lambda_{0}&1&&\\
&\lambda_{0}&\ddots&\\
&&\ddots&1\\
&&&\lambda_{0}\\
\end{matrix}\right),\quad B_{i}=\left(\begin{matrix}1&&&\\
&1&&\\
&&\ddots&\\
&&&1\\
\end{matrix}\right).

2. Jordan block with eigenvalue
\infty

A_{i}=\left(\begin{matrix}1&&&\\
&1&&\\
&&\ddots&\\
&&&1\\
\end{matrix}\right),\quad B_{i}=\left(\begin{matrix}0&1&&\\
&0&\ddots&\\
&&\ddots&1\\
&&&0\\
\end{matrix}\right).

3. Horizontal Kronecker block

A_{i}=\left(\begin{matrix}0&1&&\\
&\ddots&\ddots&\\
&&0&1\\
\end{matrix}\right),\quad B_{i}=\left(\begin{matrix}1&0&&\\
&\ddots&\ddots&\\
&&1&0\\
\end{matrix}\right)

4. Vertical Kronecker block

A_{i}=\left(\begin{matrix}0&&\\
1&\ddots&\\
&\ddots&0\\
&&1\\
\end{matrix}\right),\quad B_{i}=\left(\begin{matrix}1&&\\
0&\ddots&\\
&\ddots&1\\
&&0\\
\end{matrix}\right).

The number and types of blocks in decomposition (
1
) are uniquely defined up to permutation.

Remark 2.2
.

Unlike
[
1
]
(and similar to
[
3
]
) there are no negative signs in the matrices
B_{i}
. It doesn’t affect much: there is a change of signs in some formulas (e.g. one should consider
\operatorname{Ker}(A-\lambda B)
instead of
\operatorname{Ker}(A+\lambda B)
) but the JK invariants do not change.

It is convenient to regard the zero block
0_{m,n}
in (
1
) as a block-diagonal matrix that is composed of
m
vertical Kronecker blocks of size
1\times 0
and
n
horizontal Kronecker blocks of size
0\times 1
.

Definition 2.3
.

The horizontal indices
\mathrm{h}_{1},\dots,\mathrm{h}_{p}
of the pencil
\mathcal{P}=\{A+\lambda B\}
are defined to be the horizontal dimensions (widths) of horizontal Kronecker blocks:

A_{i}+\lambda B_{i}=\underbrace{\left(\begin{matrix}\lambda&1&&\\
&\ddots&\ddots&\\
&&\lambda&1\\
\end{matrix}\right)}_{\mathrm{h}_{i}}.

Similarly, the vertical indices
\mathrm{v}_{1},\dots,\mathrm{v}_{q}
are the vertical dimensions (heights) of vertical blocks:

A_{i}+\lambda B_{i}=\left.\left(\begin{matrix}\lambda&&\\
1&\ddots&\\
&\ddots&\lambda\\
&&1\\
\end{matrix}\right)\right\}{\scriptstyle\mathrm{v}_{i}}.

In particular, in view of the above interpretation of the
0_{m,n}
block, the first
m
horizontal indices and first
n
vertical indices are equal to
1
.
We will denote the total number
p
of horizontal indices by
n_{{h}}
, and the total number
q
of vertical indices by
n_{{v}}
.

Remark 2.4
.

There also exist closely related notions of minimal row and column indices (see
[
3
]
). Namely, minimal column indices are equal to the numbers
h_{i}-1
, while minimal row indices are equal to
v_{i}-1
. However, we find the terms horizontal and vertical indices more intuitive and more suitable for our purposes.

2.2
Jordan–Kronecker invariants of Lie algebra representations

Consider a finite-dimensional linear representation
\rho:\mathfrak{g}\to\mathrm{gl}\,(V)
of a finite-dimensional Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
. To each point
x\in V
, the representation
\rho
assigns a linear operator

\displaystyle R_{x}:

\displaystyle\mathfrak{g}\to V,

\displaystyle R_{x}(\xi)=

\displaystyle\rho(\xi)x\in V.

Definition 2.5
.

The
algebraic type
of a pencil
R_{a}+\lambda R_{b}
,
a,b\in V
is the following collection of discrete invariants:

•

the number of distinct eigenvalues of Jordan blocks,

•

the number and sizes of Jordan blocks associated with each eigenvalue,

•

horizontal and vertical Kronecker indices.

Note that for Jordan blocks we can consider tuples of sizes for each eigenvalue.

Remark 2.6
.

The algebraic type of a pencil
R_{a}+\lambda R_{b}
does not change under replacing
a
and
b
with any linearly independent combinations of them
a^{\prime}=\alpha a+\beta b
and
b^{\prime}=\gamma a+\delta b
.

Definition 2.7
.

The Jordan–Kronecker invariant
of
\rho
is the algebraic type of a pencil
R_{a+\lambda b}
for a generic pair
a,b\in V
.

In particular, horizontal and vertical Kronecker indices of a generic pencil will be denoted by

\mathrm{h}_{1}(\rho),\dots,\mathrm{h}_{p}(\rho)
and
\mathrm{v}_{1}(\rho),\dots,\mathrm{v}_{q}(\rho)
and will be called
horizontal and vertical indices
of the representation
\rho
.

2.2.1
Invariance under group action

The next statement that we will need below deals with the invariace of JK invariants under the corresponding group action.

Proposition 2.8
.

Assume that a representation of Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
is the differential
d\phi
of the Lie group representation
\phi:G\to\operatorname{GL}(V)
. Then for any
g\in G
the JK invariants for pencils
R_{x+\lambda a}
and
R_{\phi(g)(x+\lambda a)}
coincide.

Proof.

Note that

R_{\phi(g)x}(\xi)=\phi(g)R_{x}\left(\operatorname{Ad}_{g^{-1}}\xi\right).

Indeed, any element of Lie algebra
\xi\in\mathfrak{g}
has the from
\xi=\frac{d}{dt}h_{t}
, where
h_{t}\in G
. Thus

R_{\phi(g)x}(\xi)=\frac{d}{dt}\phi\left(h_{t}\right)\phi(g)x=\phi(g)\frac{d}{dt}\phi\left(g^{-1}h_{t}g\right)x=\phi(g)R_{x}\left(\operatorname{Ad}_{g^{-1}}\xi\right).

So, the following diagram commutes

\begin{CD}\mathfrak{g}@>{R_{\phi(g)(x+\lambda a)}}>{}>V\\
@V{\operatorname{Ad}_{g^{-1}}}V{}V@A{}A{\phi(g)}A\\
\mathfrak{g}@>{R_{x+\lambda a}}>{}>V\end{CD}

The JK decomposition for any pair of linear maps doesn’t depend on a choice of bases in the spaces (i.e. they don’t change under linear automorphisms of these spaces). Therefore the JK invariants of
R_{x+\lambda a}
and
R_{\rho(g)(x+\lambda a)}
coincide. Proposition
2.8
is proved. ∎

2.3
Interpretation of Jordan-Kronecker invariants

In this section we give an interpretation for some of the Jordan-Kronecker invariants. All properties we discuss here are quite elementary but will be useful in the sequel. We begin with the Jordan part, then discuss horizontal and vertical indices.

2.3.1
Jordan blocks

The Jordan blocks correspond to
elementary divisors
(see
[
3
]
), which are defined using minors of a pencil
R_{x+\lambda a}
. For Lie algebra representations the Jordan blocks are connected with the singular set
\mathsf{Sing}
. In this section we connect the gcd of the highest order non-trivial minors (i.e. the characterictic polynomial) with the singular set
\mathsf{Sing}
of the representation.

Definition 2.9
.

The
rank of a pencil

\mathcal{P}=\{A+\lambda B\}
is the number

\mathrm{rk}\,\mathcal{P}=\max_{\lambda\in\mathbb{C}}\mathrm{rk}\,(A+\lambda B).

Definition 2.10
.

The
characteristic polynomial

\chi(\alpha,\beta)
of the pencil
\mathcal{P}
is defined as the greatest common divisor of all the
r\times r
minors of the matrix
\alpha A+\beta B
, where
r=\mathrm{rk}\,\mathcal{P}
.

One can show that the polynomial
\chi(\alpha,\beta)
does not depend on the choice of bases and therefore is an invariant of the pencil (up to multiplication on a constant). It is easy to calculate
\chi(\alpha,\beta)
for a single block from the JK normal form:

•

\chi(\alpha,\beta)=1
for a horizontal or vertical Kronecker block;

•

\chi(\alpha,\beta)=(\alpha\lambda_{0}+\beta)^{n}
for a
n\times n
Jordan block with eigenvalue
\lambda_{0}
(if
\lambda_{0}=\infty
, then
\chi(\alpha,\beta)=\alpha^{n}
.)

In general,
\chi(\alpha,\beta)
is the product of characteristic polynomials for Jordan and Kronecker blocks (see
[
3
]
). Thus
\chi(\alpha,\beta)
is a homogeneous polynomial of degree equal to the sum of the sizes of all Jordan blocks. The characteristic polynomials of Jordan blocks
(\alpha\lambda_{0}+\beta)^{n}
are called
elementary divisors
of the pencil. For
\lambda_{0}<\infty
they can also be written as
(\lambda+\lambda_{0})^{n}
if we put
\lambda=\frac{\beta}{\alpha}
. The elementary divisors admit a natural invariant interpretation, see
[
3
]
for details.

Proposition 2.11
.

The eigenvalues of Jordan blocks can be characterized as those
\lambda\in\mathbb{\mathbb{C}}
for which the rank of
A-\lambda B
drops, i.e.
\mathrm{rk}\,(A-\lambda B)<r=\mathrm{rk}\,\mathcal{P}
. The infinite eigenvalue appears in the case when
\mathrm{rk}\,B<r
. In other words, the eigenvalues of Jordan blocks, written as
-\lambda=(\beta:\alpha)\in\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}^{1}
, are solutions of the characteristic equation
\chi(\alpha,\beta)=0
. Moreover, multiplicity of each eigenvalue coincides with the multiplicity of the corresponding root of the characteristic equation. Jordan blocks are absent if and only if the rank of all non-trivial linear combinations
\alpha A+\beta B
is the same.

Definition 2.12
.

A point
a\in V
is called
regular
, if

\dim\mathrm{St}_{a}\leq\dim\mathrm{St}_{x}\quad\text{for all }x\in V.

Those points which are not regular are called
singular
.

Note that a
stabilizer
of
x\in V
can be defined in terms of
R_{x}
:

\mathrm{St}_{x}=\mathrm{Ker}\,R_{x}=\{\xi\in\mathfrak{g}~|~R_{x}(\xi)=\rho(\xi)x=0\}\subset\mathfrak{g}.

The set of singular points will be denoted by
\mathsf{Sing}\subset V
. In terms of
R_{x}
we have

\mathsf{Sing}=\{y\in V~|~\mathrm{rk}\,R_{y}<r=\max_{x\in V}\mathrm{rk}\,R_{x}\}.

Proposition 2.13
(
[
1
]
).

1.

The eigenvalues of Jordan blocks of a pencil
R_{a+\lambda b}
are those values of
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
for which the line
a-\lambda b
intersects the singular set
\mathsf{Sing}
. In particular,
R_{a+\lambda b}
has a Jordan block with eigenvalue
\infty
if and only if
b
is singular.

2.

A generic pencil
R_{a}+\lambda R_{b}
has no Jordan blocks if and only if the codimension of the singular set
\mathsf{Sing}
is greater or equal than
2
.

Let us discuss the case
\mathrm{codim}\,\mathsf{Sing}=1
in more detail. Consider the matrix of the operator
R_{x}
and take all of its minors of size
r\times r
, where
r=\dim\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{reg}}
is the dimension of a regular orbit, that do not vanish identically (such minors certainly exist). We consider them as polynomials
p_{1}(x),\dots,p_{N}(x)
on
V
. The singular set
\mathsf{Sing}\subset V
is then given by the system of polynomial equations

p_{i}(x)=0,\quad i=1,\dots,N.

This set is of codimension one if and only if these polynomials possess a non-trivial greatest common divisor which we denote by
\mathsf{p}_{\rho}
.

Thus, we have
p_{i}(x)=\mathsf{p}_{\rho}(x)h_{i}(x)
, which implies that the singular set
\mathsf{Sing}
can be represented as the union of two subsets:

\mathsf{Sing}_{0}=\{\mathsf{p}_{\rho}(x)=0\}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\mathsf{Sing}_{1}=\{h_{i}(x)=0,\ i=1,\dots,N\}.

(2)

It is easy to see that
\mathsf{p}_{\rho}(x)
is a semi-invariant of the representation
\rho
. This follows from the fact that the action of
G
leaves the singular set
\mathsf{Sing}_{0}
invariant and therefore may only multiply
\mathsf{p}_{\rho}
by a character of
G
. We will refer to this polynomial
\mathsf{p}_{\rho}
as the
fundamental semi-invariant
of
\rho
. The fundamental semi-invariant is closely related to the characteristic polynomial
\chi_{a,b}
of the pencil
R_{a+\lambda b}
:

Proposition 2.14
(
[
1
]
).

Let
a,b\in V
be such that the projective line
\alpha a+\beta b
does not intersect
\mathsf{Sing}_{1}
and is not completely contained in
\mathsf{Sing}
(i.e. contains at least one regular element). Then

\chi_{a,b}(\alpha,\beta)=\mathsf{p}_{\rho}(\alpha a+\beta b).

Corollary 2.15
(
[
1
]
).

The degree of the fundamental semi-invariant
\mathsf{p}_{\rho}
is equal to the sum of the sizes of all Jordan blocks for a generic pencil
R_{a+\lambda b}
.

Definition 2.16
.

The total number of columns in horizontal blocks

\mathrm{h}_{tot}=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{{h}}}\mathrm{h}_{i}
is said to be the
total Kronecker
h
-index
of the pencil
\mathcal{P}
. Similarly, the total number of rows in the vertical Kronecker blocks
\mathrm{v}_{tot}=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{{v}}}\mathrm{v}_{j}
is said to be the
total Kronecker
v
-index
of
\mathcal{P}
.

There is a relation between the total indices and the degree of the characteristic polynomial:

Proposition 2.17
(
[
1
]
).

We have

\mathrm{v}_{tot}+\mathrm{h}_{tot}=\dim V+\dim U-\mathrm{rk}\,\mathcal{P}-\deg\chi.

(3)

Below we will also need the following simple statement about the number of Jordan blocks for each eigenvalue.

Proposition 2.18
.

Consider an arbitrary representation of a Lie algebra
\rho:\mathfrak{g}\to\operatorname{gl}(V)
and an arbitrary pencil
R_{x+\lambda a}
. Then the number of Jordan blocks with eigenvalue
\lambda_{0}
is

\dim\mathrm{Ker}\,R_{x-\lambda_{0}a}-\min_{\lambda}\dim\mathrm{Ker}\,R_{x+\lambda a}=\dim\mathrm{St}_{x-\lambda_{0}a}-\dim\mathrm{St}_{\mathrm{reg}}.

2.3.2
Kronecker blocks

In general, Kronecker blocks for a pencil
R_{x}+\lambda R_{a}
correspond to the polynomial solutions of the following equations (
4
) and (
5
).

Proposition 2.19
(
[
1
]
).

Let
A
be regular in a pencil
\mathcal{P}=\{A+\lambda B\}
, i.e.
\mathrm{rk}\,A=\mathrm{rk}\,\mathcal{P}
. Then for every
u_{0}\in\mathrm{Ker}\,A
there exists a sequence of vectors
\{u_{0},\dots,u_{l}\in U\}
such that the expression
u(\lambda)=\sum_{j=0}^{l}u_{j}\lambda^{j}
is a solution of the equation

(A+\lambda B)u(\lambda)=0.

(4)

Similarly, for any
u_{0}\in\mathrm{Ker}\,A^{*}
there exists a sequence of vectors
\{u_{0},\dots,u_{l}\in V^{*}\}
such that the expression
u(\lambda)=\sum_{j=0}^{l}u_{j}\lambda^{j}
is a solution of the equation

(A+\lambda B)^{*}u(\lambda)=0.

(5)

As it was shown in
[
1
]
, if we consider polynomial solutions of
u_{i}(\lambda)
of (
4
) then

\deg u_{i}\geq\mathrm{h}_{i}-1,

(6)

where
\deg u_{1}(\lambda)\leq\dots\leq\deg u_{l}(\lambda)
and
\mathrm{h}_{1}\leq\mathrm{h}_{2}\leq\dots
is the ordered sequence of horizontal indices. A similar statement holds for vertical indices and solutions of (
5
). It is not hard to find polynomial solutions for which the inequality (
6
) becomes an equality.

Proposition 2.20
(
[
1
]
).

Horizontal Kronecker indices
\mathrm{h}_{1},\dots,\mathrm{h}_{p}
are given by
\mathrm{h}_{i}=r_{i}+1
, where
r_{1},\dots,r_{p}
are the minimal degrees of independent solutions of (
4
). Similarly, vertical Kronecker indices
\mathrm{v}_{1},\dots,\mathrm{v}_{q}
are given by
\mathrm{v}_{i}=r^{\prime}_{i}+1
, where
r^{\prime}_{1},\dots,r^{\prime}_{q}
are the minimal degrees of independent solutions of the dual problem (
5
).

It is not hard to describe all solutions of (
4
). For Jordan and vertical Kronecker blocks there are no solutions of (
4
). Let
A,B:U\to V
and let
f_{0},\dots,f_{h-1}
be a basis in
U
from the JK normal form for a horizontal Kronecker block, i.e.

Af_{0}=0,\qquad Af_{1}=Bf_{0},\qquad\dots,\qquad Af_{h-1}=Bf_{h-2},\qquad Bf_{h-1}=0.

Then we have the following solution of (
4
):

m(\lambda)=f_{0}-\lambda f_{1}+\dots+(-\lambda)^{h-1}f_{h-1}.

We call
m(\lambda)
a
minimal polynomial
corresponding to a horizontal Kronecker block. Obviously, these minimal polynomials are generators of polynomial solutions of (
4
)
(as a
\mathbb{C}[\lambda]
-module).

Proposition 2.21
.

Let
m_{i}(\lambda)
be minimal polynomials of horizontal Kronecker blocks for any JK normal form of
A,B
. Then any polynomial solutions of (
4
) has the form

u(\lambda)=\sum_{i}P_{i}(\lambda)m_{i}(\lambda),

where
P_{i}(\lambda)\in\mathbb{C}[\lambda]
are arbitrary polynomials.

Below we use the following statement that allows us to calculate Kronecker indices.

Proposition 2.22
.

Let
u_{i}(\lambda)=\sum_{j=0}^{r_{i}}u_{ij}\lambda^{j}
, for
i=1,\dots,p
, be polynomial solutions of (
4
). If the following two conditions are satisfied:

1.

the number of solutions
p
is equal to the number of horizontal Kronecker blocks,

2.

the vectors
u_{ij}
are linearly independant,

then the horizontal indices
\mathrm{h}_{i}=r_{i}+1
. A similar statement holds for vertical Kronecker indices and solutions of (
5
).

Proof of Proposition
2.22
.

By Proposition
2.21
all vectors
u_{ij}
belong to the sum of horizontal Kronecker blocks. Since
u_{ij}
are linearly independant,
\sum h_{i}\geq\deg u_{i}+1
. On the other hand, we have (
6
). Thus,
h_{i}=\deg u_{i}+1=r_{i}+1
. Proposition
2.22
is proved. ∎

For a Lie algebra representation
\rho:\mathfrak{g}\to\mathrm{gl}\,(V)
the vertical indices are related with invariant polynomials of representations and horizontal indices are connected with stabilizers.

The dimension of the stabilizer of a regular point is a natural characteristic of
\rho
and is denoted by
\dim\mathrm{St}_{\mathrm{reg}}
. It is convenient to keep in mind the action of the Lie group
G
associated with the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
and its orbits. In particular, for the dimension of a regular orbit we will use the notation
\dim\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{reg}}
. Notice that

T_{x}\mathcal{O}_{x}=\mathrm{Im}\,R_{x}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\dim\mathcal{O}_{x}=\mathrm{rk}\,R_{x}.

Proposition 2.23
(
[
1
]
).

For any Lie algebra representation
\rho
:

1.

the number
n_{{h}}(\rho)
of horizontal indices of
\rho
is equal to
\dim\mathrm{St}_{\mathrm{reg}}
.

2.

the number
n_{{v}}(\rho)
of vertical indices of
\rho
is equal to
\mathrm{codim}\,\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{reg}}
.

It is not hard to prove the following statement about the number of indices equal to
1
.

Proposition 2.24
.

For any pencil
R_{x}+\lambda R_{a}
of a Lie algebra representation

1.

the number of horizontal indices equal to
1
(i.e. the number of horizontal
0\times 1
Kronecker blocks) is

\dim\left(\operatorname{St}_{x}\cap\operatorname{St}_{a}\right),

2.

the number of vertical indices equal to
1
(i.e. the number of vertical
1\times 0
Kronecker blocks) is

\mathrm{codim}\,\left(\operatorname{Im}R_{x}+\operatorname{Im}R_{a}\right)=\dim\left(\mathrm{Ker}\,R_{x}^{*}\cap\mathrm{Ker}\,R_{a}^{*}\right).

Since
\dim\left(\operatorname{Im}R_{x}+\operatorname{Im}R_{a}\right)\leq 2\dim\mathfrak{g}
there will always be trivial vertial blocks for high-dimensional representations. Namely, the following holds.

Corollary 2.25
.

Consider an arbitrary representation of a Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}\to\mathrm{gl}\,(V).
If
\dim V>2\dim\mathfrak{g}
, then in the JK decomposition there is no less than
(\dim V-2\dim\mathfrak{g})
vertical indices
\mathrm{v}_{i}=1
.

2.3.3
Degrees of invariant polynomials and vertical indices

Let
\rho\colon\mathfrak{g}\to\mathrm{gl}\,(V)
be a representation of a finite-dimensional Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
on a finite-dimensional vector space
V
. Let also
\mathrm{v}_{1}(\rho),\dots,\mathrm{v}_{q}(\rho)
be the vertical indices of
\rho
. In this section we state three results from
[
1
]
about vertical indices and invariant polynomials. In sections below we use Theorem
2.29
. Theorem
2.26
can also be useful in practice. Theorem
2.28
complements Theorem
2.29
.

The first result gives a bound for degrees of invariant polynomials in terms of vertical indices. In the case of the coadjoint representation it was obtained by A. Vorontsov
[
9
]
.

Theorem 2.26
(Lower bounds for degrees of polynomial invariants,
[
1
]
).

Assume that
f_{1},\dots,f_{m}
are algebraically independent invariant polynomials of
\rho
,
and
\deg f_{1}\leq\dots\leq\deg f_{m}
. Then

\deg f_{i}\geq\mathrm{v}_{i}(\rho)

(7)

for
i=1,\dots,m
.

Corollary 2.27
(
[
1
]
).

Suppose that there exist algebraically independent invariant polynomials
f_{1},f_{2},\dots,f_{q}
,
q=\mathrm{codim}\,\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{reg}}
, of a representation
\rho
satisfying the condition

\sum_{i=1}^{q}\deg f_{i}=\mathrm{v}_{tot}(\rho).

(8)

Then

\mathrm{v}_{i}(\rho)=\deg f_{i}.

(9)

There are also two theorems about the case
\operatorname{deg}f_{i}=v_{i}(\rho)
.

Theorem 2.28
(On the set where the invariants become dependent,
[
1
]
).

Assume that
f_{1},f_{2},\dots,f_{q}
is a complete set of algebraically independent invariant polynomials of
\rho
(in other words,
q=\mathrm{codim}\,\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{reg}}
). The following conditions are equivalent:

1.

The degrees of
f_{i}
’s are equal to the vertical indices of
\rho
:
\deg f_{i}=\mathrm{v}_{i}(\rho)
.

2.

The sum of the degrees of
f_{i}
’s is equal to the total vertical index of
\rho
:
\sum\deg f_{i}=\sum\mathrm{v}_{i}(\rho)
.

3.

The set where the differentials
df_{1},\dots,df_{q}
are linearly dependent has codimension
\geq 2
in
V
.

4.

The set where the differentials
df_{1},\dots,df_{q}
are linearly dependent is contained in the set
\mathsf{Sing}_{1}
, i.e. in the codimension
\geq 2
stratum of the set of singular points of
\rho
in
V
.

In practice, the following theorem can be even more useful.

Theorem 2.29
(On polynomiality of the algebra of invariants,
[
1
]
).

Assume that
f_{1},f_{2},\dots,f_{q}
is a complete set of algebraically independent invariant polynomials of
\rho
(in other words,
q=\mathrm{codim}\,\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{reg}}
). Then we have the following:

1.

If the degrees of
f_{i}
’s are equal to the vertical indices:
\deg f_{i}=v_{i}(\rho)
(equivalently,
\sum\deg f_{i}=\sum v_{i}(\rho)
), then the algebra
\mathbb{C}[V]^{\mathfrak{g}}
of polynomial invariants of
\rho
is freely generated by
f_{1},f_{2},\dots,f_{q}
(i.e. it is a polynomial algebra).

2.

Conversely, if the algebra
\mathbb{C}[V]^{\mathfrak{g}}
of polynomial invariants of
\rho
is freely generated by
f_{1},f_{2},\dots,f_{q}
, and, in addition,
\rho
has no proper semi-invariants (i.e. any semi-invariant is an invariant), then the degrees of
f_{i}
’s are equal to the vertical indices.

Remark 2.30
.

A similar result is obtained in
[
6
]
(see Corollary
5.5
): if the algebra of
polynomial invariants is freely generated by
f_{1},f_{2},\dots,f_{q}
, and, in addition, a certain
semi-invariant is an invariant, then
\sum\deg g_{i}
is equal to the degree of a certain form.

3
JK invariants for standard representations of matrix
Lie algebras

3.1
Standard representations of
\operatorname{gl}(n)

Consider the sum of
m
standard representations of
\operatorname{gl}(n)

\rho^{\oplus_{m}}:\operatorname{gl}(V)\to\operatorname{gl}(V^{\oplus_{m}})

Fix a basis of
V
, so that we could identify all the linear maps with matrices. Then an element of
V^{\oplus_{m}}
is given by a matrix
X\in\operatorname{Mat}_{n\times m}
and the corresponding linear mapping is the right multiplication:

\begin{gathered}R_{X}:\operatorname{Mat}_{n\times n}\to\operatorname{Mat}_{n\times m}\\
Y\to YX\end{gathered}

(10)

In fact, in this case of
\operatorname{gl}(n)
we can immediately describe the JK invaraints for any pair
R_{X},R_{A}
. Moreover, we can easily do it for an arbitrary composition of matrices, not necessarily square ones.

Theorem 3.1
.

Consider a pencil of right multiplications

\displaystyle R_{X}+\lambda R_{A}:

\displaystyle\operatorname{Mat}_{k\times n}\to\operatorname{Mat}_{k\times m}

(11)

\displaystyle Y\to Y(X+\lambda A)

(12)

for arbitrary
X,A\in\operatorname{Mat}_{n\times m}
. Then the JK decomposition of the pencil
R_{x}+\lambda A
is obtained from the JK decomposition of the pencil
X^{T}+\lambda A^{T}
if we take each block
k
times.

First, let us prove that the natural left-right
\operatorname{GL}(n)\times\operatorname{GL}(m)
action doesn’t change JK decompositions.

Proposition 3.2
.

Consider the pencil of right multiplications (
11
). Then for any
C\in\operatorname{GL}(n),D\in\operatorname{GL}(m)
the JK decompositions of the pencil
R_{X}+\lambda R_{A}
and for the pencil
R_{CXD}+\lambda R_{CAD}
coincide.

Proof of Proposition
3.2
.

JK decomposition for any pair of linear maps doesn’t depend on a choice of bases in the spaces. Thus the JK decomposition of
R_{X}+\lambda R_{A}
won’t change if we take their composition with any linear automorphisms
\varphi,\psi
of
\operatorname{Mat}_{k\times n}
and
\operatorname{Mat}_{k\times m}
. It remains to notice that for the automophisms

\varphi(Y)=YC,\qquad\psi(Z)=ZD

the following diagram commutes

\begin{CD}\operatorname{Mat}_{k\times n}@>{R_{CXD}+\lambda R_{CAD}}>{}>\operatorname{Mat}_{n\times m}\\
@V{}V{\varphi}V@A{}A{\psi}A\\
\operatorname{Mat}_{k\times n}@>{R_{X}+\lambda R_{A}}>{}>\operatorname{Mat}_{n\times m}\end{CD}

Proposition
3.2
is proved. ∎

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

X
and
A
multiply each row of
Y\in\operatorname{Mat}_{k\times n}
independantly. Thus if we regard
{k\times n}
matrices as vector-columns consisting of its
1\times n
rows, then the operators
R_{X}
and
R_{A}
are given by block-diagonal matrices

R_{X}=\left(\begin{matrix}X^{T}&&\\
&\ddots&\\
&&X^{T}\end{matrix}\right),\qquad R_{A}=\left(\begin{matrix}A^{T}&&\\
&\ddots&\\
&&A^{T}\end{matrix}\right)

(13)

with
k
blocks. Here the matrices are transposed because we act right on
k\times n
matrices and left on vectors. Using Proposition
3.2
we can assume that
X^{T}
and
A^{T}
are as in the JK theorem
2.1
. Then
R_{X}
and
R_{A}
are also as in the JK theorem
2.1
. Theorem
3.1
is proved. ∎

We have found the JK decomposition for any pair of matrices
X,A
, but what is the generic one? In the following Lemma, answering this question, we denote by

the

Lemma 3.3
.

Consider the sum of
m
standard representations for the Lie algebra
\operatorname{gl}(n)
. Define
n\times m
matrices

and

as follows:

•

If

X=\left(\begin{matrix}I_{m}\\

•

If

X=\left(\begin{matrix}\lambda_{1}&&\\
&\ddots&\\
&&\lambda_{n}\end{matrix}\right),\qquad A=\left(\begin{matrix}1&&\\
&\ddots&\\
&&1\end{matrix}\right),

(15)

where

are distinct numbers.

•

If

X=\left(\begin{matrix}I_{n}&0\end{matrix}\right),\qquad A=\left(\begin{matrix}0&I_{n}\end{matrix}\right).

(16)

Then the pencil

is generic.

Proof of Lemma
3.3
.

By Proposition
3.2
, it suffices to show that there exists an open dense subset

such that for any pair
\left(\tilde{X},\tilde{A}\right)\in U
its orbit under the natural left-right action of

on

contains a pair

of the above form. This is a simple and well-known fact, see e.g.
[
7
]
[p. 119]. Lemma

is proved. ∎

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
\rho
be the sum of m standard representations of

.

1.

Assume that

. Divide

by
n-m
with the remainder:

n

n

3.

Assume that

. Divide
n
by
m-n
with the remainder:

Then the JK invariants of
\rho
are
n(m-n)

\underbrace{q+1,\dots,q+1}_{n(m-n-r)},\qquad\underbrace{q+2,\dots,q+2}_{nr}.

By Theorem

the JK invariants of

are the JK invariants of a generic pair of

-matrices taken
n

Corollary 3.5
.

A generic pair
A,B\in\operatorname{Mat}_{m\times n}
has the following JK invariants:

1.

If
m<n
and
m=q(n-m)+r
, then the JK invariants are
(n-m-r)

h_{i}=q+1
and
r
horizontal indices

.

2.

If
m=n

n

blocks with distinct eigenvalues.

3.

If
m>n
and
m=q(m-n)+r
, then the JK invariants are
(m-n-r)
vertical indices
h_{i}=q+1
and
r
vertical indices
h_{i}=q+2
.

Remark 3.6
.

For any JK decomposition we can always check that the sum of sizes of blocks is equal to the size of matrices. The sizes of blocks are:

•

(\mathrm{h}_{i}-1)\times\mathrm{h}_{i}
for horizontal indices;

•

\mathrm{v}_{i}\times(\mathrm{v}_{i}-1)
for vertical indices;

•

n_{i}\times n_{i}
for Jordan blocks.

For a Lie algebra representation
\rho:\mathfrak{g}\to V
the corresponding matrices have the size
\operatorname{dim}V\times\operatorname{dim}\mathfrak{g}
. Hence, for the sum of m standard representations of
\operatorname{gl}(n)
the total size is
nm\times n^{2}
. For
n<m
we can informally write the equality for the sum of sizes as

n(n-m-r)\left[q\times(q+1)\right]+nr\left[(q+1)\times(q+2)\right]=nm\times n^{2}.

For
n=m
we get an obivous “equality”

n^{2}(1\times 1)=n^{2}\times n^{2}.

And for
n>m
we get a similar informal equality:

n(m-n-r)\left[(q+1)\times q\right]+nr\left[(q+2)\times(q+1)\right]=nm\times n^{2}.

In these equalities
x\times y
is just a notation for a vector
(x,y)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
.

Remark 3.7
.

Note that for
m\not=n
in Theorem
3.4
the Kronecker indices are “as equal as possible”: they don’t differ more then by
1
.

Proof of Theorem
3.4
.

The proof is rather straightforward: we take a generic pair
(X,A)
and then explicitly describe bases for all the blocks in the JK decomposition. Of course, we try to find a generic pair
(X,A)
with a “simple form”, to simplify the calculations. Luckily, we already know a “rather simple” generic pair
(X,A)
from Lemma
3.3
. Now we describe the bases of the blocks for this pair
(X,A)
. Denote by
E_{k,l}
the matrix with
1
at position
(k,l)
and zeros everywhere else.

•

First, consider the case
m<n
. For the pair (
14
) the following identity holds

E_{i,j}X=E_{i,j+(n-m)}A

(18)

Here if the second index goes beyond the interval
[1,n]
, then we formally put that side to zero. Thus for any
i,j
such that

1\leq i\leq n,\quad 1\leq j\leq n-m

we get a horizontal Kronecker block with the bases

E_{i,j+l_{1}(m-n)},\quad\text{ in }\operatorname{gl}(n),\qquad\text{ and }\qquad E_{i,j+l_{2}(n-m)}\quad\text{ in }\quad V^{\oplus_{m}}.

(19)

We take all
l_{1}
and
l_{2}
such that the matrices lie in the corresponding spaces.
We get the horizontal indices
\mathrm{h}_{i}=q+2
for
1\leq j\leq r
and
\mathrm{h}_{i}=q+1
for
r+1\leq j\leq n-m
.

•

Then, in the case
m=n
we have obvious Jordan blocks defined by the following idenities for the pairs (
15

E_{ij}X=\lambda_{j}E_{ij}A

(20)

•

Finally, we have a natural duality between the cases
m>n
and

because the pair (

) is the transpose of the pair (

Theorem
3.4
is proved. ∎

3.2.3
Algebra of invariant polynomials

Now, let us demonstrate how Theorem
2.29
works in this case. The following proposition and its proof are taken from
[
1
]
for the sake of completeness.

Proposition 3.18
.

Let
\rho:\operatorname{sl}(n)\to\operatorname{Mat}_{n\times m}
be the sum of
m
standard representations of
\operatorname{sl}(n)
, and let
X\in\operatorname{Mat}_{n\times m}
. Then:

1.

If
m<n
, the algebra of invariants of
\rho
is trivial.

2.

If
m=n
, the algebra of invariants is freely generated by the polynomial
\operatorname{det}X
.

3.

If
m=n+1
, the algebra of invariants is freely generated by
n\times n
minors of
X
.

4.

If
m\geq 2n
, the algebra of invariants is not freely generated.

Remark 3.19
.

Of course, these results are well-known. For
m>n
, the algebra of invariants
of
\rho
can be identified with the homogeneous coordinate ring of the Grassmannian

\operatorname{Gr}(n,m)
.

•

If
m=n+1
, then the Grassmannian
\operatorname{Gr}(n,m)
is the projective space
\mathbb{P}^{n}
, whose homogeneous coordinate ring is the ring of polynomials in
n+1
variables, hence freely
generated.

•

For
m>n+1
, the homogeneous coordinate ring of the Grassmannian
\operatorname{Gr}(n,m)

is generated by Plücker coordinates, subject to Plücker relations.

Proof of Proposition
4.22
.

•

There is a clearly no nonconstant invariants in the
m<n
case, because in
this case the action of the group
\operatorname{SL}(n)
on
m
-tuples of vectors in
\operatorname{R}^{n}
has an open orbit. (One could also say that in this case there is no vertical indices and hence no invariants by
Theorem
2.26
.)

•

As for the case
m=n
, in this case we have one vertical index equal to
n
, and
a polynomial invariant
\operatorname{det}X
of degree
n
. So, by Theorem
2.26
, the algebra of invariants is freely generated by
\operatorname{det}X
(which is obvious in this case, because the transcendence degree of the algebra of invariants is equal to 1, so it must be freely generated).

•

Further, when
m=n+1
, the vertical indices are

\underbrace{n,\dots,n}_{n+1\text{ times}}

which again coincides with the degrees of the
n\times n
minors of
X
. So, these minors generate the algebra of invariants by Theorem
2.26
(they are clearly independent, as one can find a matrix X with any prescribed values of these minors).

•

Finally, consider the case
m>n+1
. Observe that in this case there may also be no invariants of degree
k<n
. Indeed, assume that
f
is such an invariant. Then there exist
k
indices
1\leq i_{1}<\dots<i_{k}\leq n
such that
f
has non-trivial restriction to the subspace of matrices whose all columns, except for the ones with indices
i_{1},\dots,i_{k}
, vanish. But this restriction must be an invariant of the representation
\operatorname{sl}(n)\to\operatorname{gl}\left(\operatorname{Mat}_{n\times k}\right)
, and hence trivial, which is a contradiction. So, any non-trivial invariant of
\rho
has degree at least n. On the other hand, it is easy to see from Proposition
3.9
that at least one of the vertical indices is less than n. So, by the second part of Theorem
2.26
, the algebra of invariants is not freely generated, as stated (Theorem
2.26
applies in our case, because
\operatorname{sl}(n)
is simple and thus none of its representations admit proper semi-invariants).

Proposition
4.22
is proved. ∎

3.3.1

Let us now consider the Lie algebras

we always assume that

and we denote by

what would usually be denoted by

X^{T}\Omega+\Omega X=0,\qquad\Omega={\begin{pmatrix}0&I_{\frac{n}{2}}\\
-I_{\frac{n}{2}}&0\\

(37)

Note that for both

Remark 3.20
.

Since

and

correspond to skew-symmetric and symmetric matrices of the same size, the obtained in the sequal results for them would usually differ by some choices of signs. For this reason it is convenient to write the formulas in terms of the number

\varepsilon=\begin{cases}+1,\qquad\text{ for }\operatorname{sp}(n),\\
-1,\qquad\text{ for }\operatorname{so}(n).\end{cases}

Theorem 3.21
.

Let

be the sum of m standard representations of

or
\operatorname{sp}(n)
and let
\varepsilon=\pm 1
be given by (
39
).

1.

Assume that
m<n
. Divide

by
n-m
with the remainder:

Then the JK invariants of

are

•

\displaystyle\frac{(n-m)(n-m+\varepsilon)}{2}
horizontal indices:

\underbrace{2q+1,\dots,2q+1}_{\frac{(n-m-r)(n-m-r+\varepsilon)}{2}},\qquad\underbrace{2q+2,\dots,2q+2}_{(n-m-r)r},\qquad\underbrace{2q+3,\dots,2q+3}_{\frac{r(r+\varepsilon)}{2}},

.

2.

Assume that

3.

Assume that

are

Remark 3.22
.

Similar to Remark
3.6
we can check that the sum of sizes of blocks is

\operatorname{dim}V\times\operatorname{dim}\mathfrak{g}=nm\times\frac{n(n+\varepsilon)}{2}.

For
n<m
the sum of “widths” of horizontal blocks is

\frac{(n-m-r)(n-m-r+\varepsilon)}{2}\cdot 2q+(n-m-r)r\cdot(2q+1)+\frac{r(r+\varepsilon)}{2}\cdot(2q+2)=nm-m(m-\varepsilon).

Hence the sum of “heights” of horizontal blocks is

nm-m(m-\varepsilon)+\frac{(n-m)(n-m+\varepsilon)}{2}=\frac{n(n+\varepsilon)}{2}-\frac{m(m-\varepsilon)}{2}

Adding the total size of vertical blocks we get the desired equality:

\left(nm-m(m-\varepsilon)\right)\times\left(\frac{n(n+\varepsilon)}{2}-\frac{m(m-\varepsilon)}{2}\right)+\frac{m(m-\varepsilon)}{2}\left[2\times 1\right]=nm\times\frac{n(n+\varepsilon)}{2}.

For
n=m
and Lie algebra
\operatorname{so}(n)
we have

n\left[1\times 0\right]+\frac{n(n-1)}{2}\left[2\times 1\right]=n^{2}\times\frac{n(n-1)}{2}.

For
n=m
and Lie algebra
\operatorname{sp}(n)
we have

n\left[1\times 1\right]+\frac{n(n-1)}{2}\left[2\times 1\right]=n^{2}\times\frac{n(n+1)}{2}.

For
n>m
, we have

n(m-n-\varepsilon)\left[1\times 0\right]+\frac{n(n+\varepsilon)}{2}\left[2\times 1\right]=nm\times\frac{n(n+\varepsilon)}{2}.

All the necessary equalities hold.

Remark 3.28
.

For

, the algebra of invariants of

is known to be generated by

g_{ij}=\left(X_{i},X_{j}\right)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}X_{ik}X_{jk},

Remark 3.30

In contrast to the

Remark 3.32
.

Similar to Remark
3.6
we can check that the sum of sizes of blocks is

\operatorname{dim}V\times\operatorname{dim}\mathfrak{g}=nm\times\frac{n(n+1)}{2}.

•

For
n\leq m
the sum of “widths” of horizontal blocks is

\sum_{j=1}^{n-m}(q_{j}+1)(j-r_{j})+(q_{j}+2)r_{j}=\sum_{j=1}^{n-m}m+j=\frac{(n-m)(n+m+1)}{2}.

Since there are
\frac{(n-m)(n-m+1)}{2}
such blocks their total sum of sizes is

(n-m)m\times\frac{(n-m)(n+m+1)}{2}.

The sum of “heights” of vertical blocks is

\sum_{j=1}^{m-1}q_{j}(j-r_{j})+(q_{j}+1)r_{j}=\sum_{j=1}^{m-1}m=m(m-1).

Hence the total size of vertical blocks is

m(m-1)\times\frac{m(m-1)}{2}.

In total, since the Jordan blocks add

to the total size we get

(n-m)m\times\frac{(n-m)(n+m+1)}{2}+m\times m+m(m-1)\times\frac{m(m-1)}{2}=nm\times\frac{n(n+1)}{2}.

•

For
m>n
, the sum of “heights” of blocks is

\sum_{j=1}^{n}(m-j-r_{j})q_{j}+r_{j}(q_{j}+1)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}m=nm.

Thus the total sum of sizes is

nm\times\left(nm-nm+\frac{n(n+1)}{2}\right)=nm\times\frac{n(n+1)}{2}.

All the necessary equalities hold.

Case

.
We can assume that

. Note that we can solve (

We start with the following trivial statement.

Proposition 3.40
.

Let
U\subset V
be a linear subspace. Then for a generic linear map

\dim\left(U+A(U)+\dots+A^{q}(U)\right)=\min(q\cdot\dim U,\dim V).

.

If (

A=\left(\begin{matrix}0&C\\
I_{n-k}&D\end{matrix}\right),\qquad C\in\operatorname{GL}(k).

The vectors

from Corollary

satisfy the following conditions:

e_{i}\in U,\qquad e_{i+qk}-Ae_{i+(q-1)k}=0,

for

and
i+qk\leq n
. Denote by

the maximal

e_{i}\in U,\qquad e_{i+qk}-Ae_{i+(q-1)k}\in U,\qquad Ae_{i+q_{i}k}\in U.

It is not hard to add to vectors

so that they would satisfy (

The first column (

Remark 3.43
.

Similar to Remark

The required “identity” is satisfied.

For a vector

The rest easily follows. Proposition

Since a generic line

For the standard representation of

with

Proposition 3.46
.

Consider the standard representation for the Lie algebra of strictly upper triangular matrices

. Then the pencil

, where

and

Proof of Proposition
3.46
.

The Lie algebra
\operatorname{n}(n)
can be invariantly defined through the standard flag

0\subset\operatorname{Span}(e_{1})\subset\dots\subset\operatorname{Span}(e_{1},\ldots,e_{n})=V

as operators
A
such that

,

. Therefore, the JK invariants for

with any basis

Since

are generic, we can assume that

is a regular element, i.e.

Thus we can take

as another element of the basis

.

Consider a generic pencil

.

from Proposition

It follows from Propositions

Remark 3.48

It is not hard to describe an explicit bases of all blocks for

4
JK invariants for actions by congruence on symmetric and skew-symmetric forms

In this section we consider the action of the group
\operatorname{GL}(V)
by congruence on the space of bilinear forms on
V
:

P,Q\to PQP^{T}.

The corresponding Lie algebra representation

\rho:\mathrm{gl}\,(V)\to\mathrm{gl}\,(V\otimes V)

is given by

\rho(X)Q=XQ+QX^{T}.

In this section, we study the JK invariants for the corresponding representations of the Lie algebras
\mathfrak{g}=\mathrm{gl}\,(V)
or
\operatorname{sl}(V)
on the spaces of symmetric forms
S^{2}(V)
and skew-symmetric forms
\Lambda^{2}(V)
.

4.1
Action of
\operatorname{GL}(n)
on symmetric forms

4.1.1
JK invariants

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\rho
be the representation of
\mathrm{gl}\,(V)
on the space of symmetric forms
S^{2}(V)
corresponding to the congruence action. Then the JK invariants of
\rho
are

•

n
Jordan
1\times 1
blocks with different eigenvalues,

•

\frac{n(n-1)}{2}
horizontal indices
h_{i}=2
.

First, let us find the number of blocks. It is well-known that any complex symmetric form
Q
with
\mathrm{rk}\,Q=k
is congruent to

Q=\operatorname{diag}(\underbrace{1,\dots,1}_{k},0\dots 0).

The stabilizer is given by the condition

XQ=-\left(XQ\right)^{T},

hence

\dim\mathrm{St}_{Q}=\frac{k(k-1)}{2}+n(n-k).

We get the following proposition.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
\rho
be the representation of
\mathrm{gl}\,(V)
on the space of symmetric forms
S^{2}(V)
corresponding to the congruence action.

1.

The singuar set is

\mathsf{Sing}=\left\{\left.Q\right|\quad\det Q=0\right\}.

2.

Hence, the JK invariants contain Jordan blocks with
n
different eigenvalues.

3.

The numbers of horizontal and vertical indices are

\dim\mathrm{St}_{\mathrm{reg}}=\frac{n(n-1)}{2},\qquad\mathrm{codim}\,\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{reg}}=0

respectively.

4.1.2
Generic pairs of forms

Since we calculate the JK invariants for generic pencils, we want to know what pairs of symmetric forms are generic. Luckily there is a canonical form for pairs of symmetric forms, described in
[
3
]
(see also
[
8
]
). We reformulate this result similar to the Jordan-Kronecker theorem below.

Theorem 4.3
(Jordan-Kronecker theorem for symmetric forms,
[
3
]
).

Let
A
and
B
be symmetric bilinear forms on a
finite-dimension vector space
V
over a field
\mathbb{K}
with
\operatorname{char}\mathbb{K}=0
. If the field
\mathbb{K}

is algebraically closed, then there exists a basis of the space
V

such that the matrices of both forms
A
and
B
are block-diagonal
matrices:

A=\begin{pmatrix}A_{1}&&&\\
&A_{2}&&\\
&&\ddots&\\
&&&A_{k}\\
\end{pmatrix},\qquad B=\begin{pmatrix}B_{1}&&&\\
&B_{2}&&\\
&&\ddots&\\
&&&B_{k}\\
\end{pmatrix}

where each pair of corresponding blocks
A_{i}
and
B_{i}
is one of
the following:

•

(symmetric) Jordan block with eigenvalue
\lambda_{i}\in\mathbb{K}
:

A_{i}=\left(\begin{matrix}&&&\lambda_{i}\\
&&\lambda_{i}&1\\
&\udots&\udots&\\
\lambda_{i}&1&&\\
\end{matrix}\right),\qquad B_{i}=\left(\begin{matrix}&&&1\\
&&\udots&\\
&\udots&&\\
1&&&\\
\end{matrix}\right)

•

(symmetric) Jordan block with eigenvalue
\infty
:

A_{i}=\left(\begin{matrix}&&&1\\
&&\udots&\\
&\udots&&\\
1&&&\\
\end{matrix}\right),\qquad B_{i}=\left(\begin{matrix}&&&0\\
&&0&1\\
&\udots&\udots&\\
0&1&&\\
\end{matrix}\right)

•

(symmetric) Kronecker block:

A_{i}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c}0&\begin{matrix}1&0&&\\
&\ddots&\ddots&\\
&&1&0\\
\end{matrix}\\
\hline\cr\begin{matrix}1&&\\
0&\ddots&\\
&\ddots&1\\
&&0\\
\end{matrix}&0\end{array}\right),\qquad B_{i}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c}0&\begin{matrix}0&1&&\\
&\ddots&\ddots&\\
&&0&1\\
\end{matrix}\\
\hline\cr\begin{matrix}0&&\\
1&\ddots&\\
&\ddots&0\\
&&1\\
\end{matrix}&0\end{array}\right).

Now, in theory, one could calculate the JK invariants for any pair of forms
Q,B
. Yet it is much simpler to do it for generic pairs.

Corollary 4.4
.

Any generic pair of symmetric forms
Q
and
B
is congruent to one of the following pairs:

Q=\left(\begin{matrix}\lambda_{1}&&\\
&\ddots&\\
&&\lambda_{n}\end{matrix}\right),\qquad B=\left(\begin{matrix}1&&\\
&\ddots&\\
&&1\end{matrix}\right),

(56)

where
\lambda_{i}
are distinct.

Proof of Corollary
4.4
.

Since the pair
Q,B
is generic, we can assume that both forms are non-degenerate. Thus there are no (symmetric) Kronecker blocks, since the number of such blocks is
\operatorname{Ker}(Q+\lambda B)
for generic
\lambda
. Next, for generic
Q,B
the operator
P=B^{-1}Q
is semisimple with distinct eigenvalues. Therefore all (symmetric) Jordan blocks are
1\times 1
and all eigenvalues are distinct. Corollary
4.4
is proved. ∎

Calculating stabilizers and images for the pairs
(Q,B)
from Corollary
4.4
we get the following.

Proposition 4.5
.

Consider a generic pencil
R_{Q}+\lambda R_{B}
for the representation of
\mathrm{gl}\,(V)
on the space of symmetric forms
S^{2}(V)
corresponding to the congruence action. Then the number of horizontal and vertical indices equal to
1
is

\dim\left(\operatorname{St}_{Q}\cap\operatorname{St}_{B}\right)=0,\qquad\mathrm{codim}\,\left(\operatorname{Im}R_{Q}+\operatorname{Im}R_{B}\right)=0

respectively.

Proof of Theorem
4.1

Proof of Theorem
4.1
.

Theorem
4.1
follows from Propositions
4.2
and
4.5
and considerations of dimensions.∎

Remark 4.6
.

For pairs

given by (
56
) we can explicitely describe bases of all blocks.

•

The Jordan
1\times 1
blocks correpsond to the matrices
E_{ii}\in\mathrm{gl}\,(n)
and

.

Let
\rho

\operatorname{sl}(V)

\operatorname{GL}(n)

Proposition 4.8
.

For any complex symmetric form
Q

PQP^{T}=\operatorname{diag}(\underbrace{\mu,\dots,\mu}_{k},0\dots 0).

from Theorem

\dim\mathrm{St}_{Q}=\begin{cases}\frac{k(k-1)}{2}+n(n-k)-1,\qquad&\text{ if }k<n,\\
\frac{n(n-1)}{2},\qquad&\text{ if }k=n.\end{cases}

(57)

Using Proposition
4.8

.

Let
\rho
be the representation of
\operatorname{sl}(V)
on the space of symmetric forms

corresponding to the congruence action.

\mathsf{Sing}=\left\{\left.Q\right|\quad\mathrm{rk}\,Q<n-1\right\}.

2.

.

The dimension

with

It is easy to see that for lesser

is (

5
Problems

All irreducible representations of
\operatorname{sl}(2)
are well-known. A simple problem for an under-graduate student is

Problem 5.1
.

Describe the JK invariants for all the irreducible representations of
\operatorname{sl}(2)
.

One may wonder: what JK invariants for Lie algebra representations are possible? For low-dimensional algebras it should be easy to answer this question completely.

Problem 5.2
.

Describe all possible JK invariants for representations of low-dimensional Lie algebras
\rho:\mathfrak{g}\to\operatorname{gl}(V)
, e.g. for
\dim\mathfrak{g}=2
or
3
.

Corollary
2.25
can be useful, when solving Problem
5.2
. One can ask a more general question.

Problem 5.3
.

What JK invariants of linear pencils can be realized by Lie algebra representations?

For Lie algebras the JK invariants of a sum
\mathfrak{g}_{1}\oplus\mathfrak{g}_{2}
is the union of JK invariants of Lie algebras
\mathfrak{g}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{g}_{2}
. For Lie algebra representations we have the following similar statement.

Proposition 5.4
.

Let
\rho_{i}:\mathfrak{g}_{i}\to\operatorname{gl}(V_{i})
,
i=1,2
, be Lie algebra representations. Then the JK invariants of the representation

\displaystyle\rho:\mathfrak{g}_{1}\oplus\mathfrak{g}_{2}\to\operatorname{gl}(V_{1})\oplus\operatorname{gl}(V_{2}),

\displaystyle\rho(x_{1},x_{2})=\left(\rho_{1}(x_{1}),\rho_{2}(x_{2})\right),

is the union of the JK invariants of
\rho_{1}
and
\rho_{2}
.

Proposition
5.4
may help to construct new JK invariants, when solving Problem
5.3
. In general, standard operations with representations do not interact well with the JK invariants.

Problem 5.5
.

Let
\mathfrak{h}\subset\mathfrak{g}
be a subalgebra with
\mathrm{codim}\,\mathfrak{h}=1
. Consider a restriction
\rho\bigr|_{\mathfrak{h}}
of a Lie algebra representation
\rho:\mathfrak{g}\to\operatorname{gl}(V)
on
\mathfrak{h}
. What combinations of JK invariants for
\rho
and
\rho\bigr|_{\mathfrak{h}}
are possible? What is the “generic case”?

A slightly different problem would be to calculate the JK invariants for all pencils
R_{X}+\lambda R_{A}
, not only generic one. Here the comparison of
\operatorname{gl}(n)
(where the answer is known, see Theorem
3.1
), and
\operatorname{sl}(n)
can be interesting.

Problem 5.6
.

What are the JK invariants of any pencil
R_{X}+\lambda R_{A}
for the sum of standard representations of
\operatorname{sl}(n)
?

Also note, that we know the canonical form for any pair

for the congruence actions of
\operatorname{GL}(n)
and
\operatorname{SL}(n)
on symmetric forms and skew-symmetric forms (see Section
4
). But the calculations are probably cumbersome and not worth it.

Problem 5.7
.

There are a lot of representations for which one can try to calculate the Jordan–Kronecker invariants. Just to name a few:

1.

sum of standard representations of the Lie algebra of strictly upper triangular matrices
\operatorname{n}(n)
.

2.

sum of standard representations of Borel subalgebras of semisimple Lie algebras.

3.

differential of the congruence action for the Lie algeras
\operatorname{so}(n)
and
\operatorname{sp}(n)
on symmetric forms and skew-symmetric forms.

4.

adjoint and coadjoint representations of classical matrix Lie algebras.

Invariants for adjoint representation of a simple Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
are well-known and they are related with exponents of
\mathfrak{g}
. For example, invariants of the algebra of invariants for the adjoint representation of
\operatorname{sl}(n)
is generated by
\operatorname{tr}\left(M^{k}\right)
,
k=2,\dots,n
. A generic matrix is semisimple, hence it is similar to a diagonal matrix. Thus, there are
n-1
horizontal and
n-1
vertical indices:

\dim\mathrm{St}_{\mathrm{reg}}=n-1,\qquad\mathrm{codim}\,\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{reg}}=n-1.

By Theorem
2.29
the vertical indices are equal to the exponents
