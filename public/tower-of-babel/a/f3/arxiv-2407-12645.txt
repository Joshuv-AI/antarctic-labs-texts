Effect of strong confinement on the order parameter dynamics in fermionic superfluids Preprint: APS/123-QED
C.R. Cabrera, R. Henke, L. Broers, J. Skulte, H.P. Ojeda Collado, H. Biss, L. Mathey, H. Moritz

Abstract
Fermionic pairing and the superfluid order parameter change dramatically in low-dimensional systems such as high-T c superconductors. Here we show how the order parameter dynamics, which defines essential collective properties, is modified by strong confinement. Using a model system for strongly correlated superfluidity, an ultracold fermionic gas, we study the response to a weak modulation of the confinement. Surprisingly, we observe a well-defined collective mode throughout the entire crossover from the Bardeen-Cooper-Schrieffer (BCS) state to Bose-Einstein condensation (BEC) of molecules. Starting in the BCS regime, the excitation energy follows twice the pairing gap, then drops below it in the strongly correlated regime, and finally approaches twice the harmonic level spacing imposed by the confinement in the BEC regime. Its spectral weight vanishes when approaching the superfluid critical temperature. The experimental results are in excellent agreement with an effective field theory, providing strong evidence that amplitude oscillations of the order parameter hybridize with and eventually transform into spatial excitations along the confined direction. The strong modification of the excitation spectrum highlights the relevance of confinement to fermionic superfluids and superconductors, and raises questions about its influence on other fundamental quantities.



Studying the dynamics of the order parameter in fermionic systems provides a route to understanding the collective behavior of superconductors and superfluids.
The order parameter
\Psi=|\Psi|e^{-i\theta}
becomes non-zero at the phase transition when the U(1) symmetry is spontaneously broken, giving rise to two fundamental excitations:
the Goldstone and amplitude mode, which correspond to fluctuations in
\theta
and
|\Psi|
, respectively
Pekker and Varma 2015
;
Shimano and Tsuji 2020
.
The Goldstone mode leads to gapless collective excitations and manifests as a linear branch of phononic excitations in neutral superfluids
Bogolubov 1947
.
In contrast, the amplitude mode consists of a gapped excitation occurring at a characteristic energy of twice the pairing gap
\Delta
.
Its experimental observation and interpretation has been the subject of strong interest during the last decades
Littlewood and Varma 1981
;
Shimano and Tsuji 2020
, complicated by the fact that the amplitude mode resides at the onset of the pair-breaking continuum, leading to coupling and decay
Cea et al. 2015
;
Cea et al. 2016
;
Kurkjian et al. 2019
;
Kurkjian et al. 2020
.
Gapped excitations located at
2\Delta
have been observed in various fermionic systems, including superconductors
Sooryakumar and Klein 1980
;
Matsunaga et al. 2014
;
Méasson et al. 2014
;
Sherman et al. 2015
and neutral superfluids
Behrle et al. 2018
;
Bayha et al. 2020
;
Dyke et al. 2024
;
Kell et al. 2024
.
Here, we refer to such damped excitations as amplitude oscillations of the order parameter.
Similar observations have also been made with ultracold bosonic atoms in the supersolid phase
Léonard et al. 2017
and near the superfluid-to-insulating transition
Bissbort et al. 2011
;
Endres et al. 2012
.

One central aspect in low-dimensional systems is the impact of confinement on the order parameter dynamics.
In this context, coupling to higher-lying spatial modes becomes relevant
Korolyuk et al. 2011
;
Korolyuk et al. 2014
;
Hertkorn et al. 2019
;
Bjerlin et al. 2016
;
Bayha et al. 2020
, leading to a hybridization with amplitude oscillations when their excitation energies become comparable.
Here, we experimentally study the order parameter dynamics in the presence of strong confinement using ultracold fermionic atoms in the BEC-BCS crossover.
When modulating the confinement, we observe a well-defined resonance located at
\omega_{R}
whose energy follows
2\Delta
in the BCS regime (Fig.
1
a, left panel).
Towards the strongly interacting regime, where
2\Delta
increases, spatial excitations are energetically favoured (central panel) and the resonance energy drops below the onset of the pair-breaking continuum.
Finally, in the BEC regime, it asymptotically approaches twice the trap level spacing (right panel).
The measurements are in good agreement with an effective field theory, continuously connecting the Gross-Pitaevski and Klein-Gordon functionals to describe the dynamics along the crossover
Pekker and Varma 2015
;
Skulte et al. 2021
.
In essence, we observe a hybridization between an amplitude oscillation and the lowest parity-allowed spatial excitation: a breathing mode of
\Psi
containing an admixture of the second excited state (
n_{z}=2
).

The experiment begins with a homogeneous fermionic superfluid of
6
Li atoms in the lowest two hyperfine states
Hueck et al. 2018
.
The superfluid is trapped in a box potential with strong vertical confinement of
\omega_{z}=2\pi\cdot 8.7\,\mathrm{kHz}
(Fig.
1
b).
This ensures that for typical temperatures
T_{\mathrm{BEC}}<0.02\,T_{\mathrm{F}}
, where
T_{\mathrm{F}}
is the Fermi temperature, thermal excitations along the
z
direction do not play a relevant role.
Here,
T_{\mathrm{BEC}}
is measured after an adiabatic ramp to the BEC regime
sup
.
Due to the strong confinement, we use two-dimensional (2D) quantities where the Fermi energy is given by
E_{\mathrm{F}}=\hbar^{2}4\pi n_{\mathrm{2D}}/(2m)
, with the atom mass
m
and Planck’s constant
\hbar
.
The ratio
E_{\mathrm{F}}/\hbar\omega_{z}\sim 1.5-2
is controlled via the fermionic density per spin state
n_{\mathrm{2D}}
.
The BEC-BCS crossover is explored using a broad Feshbach resonance

which allows tuning the dimensionless interaction parameter
\eta=\ln(k_{\mathrm{F}}a_{\mathrm{2D}})
.
Here
k_{\mathrm{F}}=\sqrt{4\pi n_{\mathrm{2D}}}
is the Fermi momentum and
a_{2D}
is the 2D scattering length
Petrov and Shlyapnikov 2001
;
Levinsen and Parish 2015
;
Turlapov and Yu Kagan 2017
.

The results can be understood as follows:
In the weakly interacting BCS regime, the well-defined resonance is located at nearly twice the pairing gap
\Delta
.
It agrees with the mean-field prediction of
\Delta=\sqrt{2E_{\mathrm{b}}E_{\mathrm{F}}}
(red dotted line), where the binding energy
E_{\mathrm{b}}
is calculated in the 2D limit
sup
.
This agreement breaks down towards the strongly interacting regime for
\eta<1.9
, where the observed mode deviates from the onset of the pair breaking continuum and becomes an in-gap excitation.
This is confirmed independently by measuring this onset (red points in Fig.
2
a) using Bragg spectroscopy
sup
;
Veeravalli et al. 2008
;
Hoinka et al. 2017
;
Sobirey et al. 2021
;
Biss et al. 2022
.
We note that trap modulation spectroscopy does not show any response for
\omega_{m}\approx 2\Delta/\hbar
for
\eta<1.9
, suggesting that this probing scheme does not couple to pair breaking excitations.
Finally, in the BEC regime, the order parameter corresponds to the total density and hence only supports spatial oscillations
Pekker and Varma 2015
.
Here, the resonance corresponds to a breathing excitation of the condensate shifted downwards from
2\hbar\omega_{z}
due to repulsive dimer-dimer interactions.

To understand the continuous evolution and character of the resonance, we develop a theoretical model in which the dynamics of the order parameter
\Psi(z,t)
are described by the equation of motion

iK_{1}\frac{\partial\Psi(z,t)}{\partial t}-K_{2}\frac{\partial^{2}\Psi(z,t)}{\partial t^{2}}\\
=\left(-\frac{\hbar^{2}\partial^{2}_{z}}{2M}+V(z,t)-r_{0}+u_{0}|\Psi(z,t)|^{2}\right)\Psi(z,t),

(1)

derived from a zero-temperature effective field theory
sup
.
We consider a homogeneous system along the x-y plane and decompose
\Psi(z,t)
into the
n_{z}=0
and
n_{z}=2
eigenstates of the harmonic potential
V(z,t)

sup
.
In this model, the coefficients
K_{1}
,
K_{2}
,
r_{0}
, and
u_{0}
parameterize the BEC-BCS crossover.
These are determined from theoretical predictions for the energy gap, condensate density
Salasnich 2007
, and speed of sound
Shi et al. 2015
in the crossover
sup
.
In the limit where
K_{1}=0
, Eq.
1
reduces to a nonlinear Klein-Gordon equation, describing the order parameter dynamics in the weakly interacting BCS regime.
The resulting equation is Lorentz invariant which is a manifestation of particle-hole symmetry.
In the BEC limit, where
K_{2}=0
, Eq.
1
reduces to the usual Gross-Pitaevskii equation.

The black dashed line in Fig.
2
a represents the eigenfrequency difference between the ground and second excited state as analytically obtained from Eq.
1
, taking into account interactions to leading order
sup
.
Towards the BEC regime, this analytical result is in excellent agreement with the experimental observation.
The full numerical simulation in the presence of trap modulation (black solid line) extends the agreement throughout the entire BEC-BCS crossover without any fitting parameters.

In addition, this full simulation gives insight into the change of the collective mode composition due to hybridization, i.e. the amplitude or breathing character of the excitation.
The theoretically expected amplitude contribution is shown in Fig.
2
b, representing the fraction of the total response, which does not conserve the integrated field density
sup
.
The maximum is reached in the BCS regime (
\eta\gtrsim 1.9
) where the total superfluid density is not conserved (see red curves in Fig.
1
a).
Going towards the BEC regime, the amplitude contribution decreases.
In this limit, the dominant excitation is a breathing mode which conserves the total superfluid density.
This corresponds to a spatial oscillations of the order parameter, characterized by the transition between
n_{z}=0
and
n_{z}=2
.
Notably, in the presence of interactions its energy deviates from hydrodynamic theory, similar to the radial breathing mode observed in strongly confined fermionic superfluids in cigar-shaped potentials
Bartenstein et al. 2004
.
These findings suggest that hydrodynamic predictions

and local density approximation break down when the pairing gap and chemical potential are comparable to the energy scale imposed by the trap
Grasso et al. 2005
.

In conclusion, our study focuses on a fundamental feature of strongly confined fermionic superfluids: the hybridization of collective modes.
The main result is the observation of a well defined resonance in the entire BEC-BCS crossover, which we interpret as an excitation of the order parameter.
This is in line with the observation that the resonance is strongly suppressed above the critical temperature.
We explain our results with an effective field theory, which reproduces the experimental findings in the entire crossover and identifies the mode as a novel hybridized excitation with amplitude and breathing character.
Our study opens the possibility to investigate more complex dynamics of the order parameter under confinement
Hannibal et al. 2015
;
Hannibal et al. 2018
, such as frequency beating
Collado et al. 2023
, drum modes
Endres et al. 2012
, and exotic Higgs bound and antibound states
Nakayama et al. 2015
;
Nakayama and Tsuchiya 2019
.
An exciting prospect is the possibility to engineer Leggett modes
Leggett 1966
;
Krull et al. 2016
;
Giorgianni et al. 2019
and to control the properties of many-body systems by admixing higher harmonic oscillator states.
Finally, the experiments have established trap modulation spectroscopy as a powerful tool to determine the superfluid-to-normal transition and measure the pairing gap energy, with great potential for the study of spin-imbalanced superfluids and other strongly correlated 2D systems.

III
Effective field theory

To gain further insights, we model the system using a low-energy effective field theory
Pekker and Varma 2015
;
Skulte et al. 2021
describing the dynamics of a complex-valued bosonic scalar field
\Psi
which represents the condensed pairs.
To be able to capture both, the BCS- and BEC-dynamics, we include a dynamical term in the form of
K_{2}|\partial_{t}\Psi(\mathbf{r},t)|^{2}
to model particle-hole symmetric dynamics and a term of the form of
iK_{1}\Psi^{*}(\mathbf{r},t)\partial_{t}\Psi(\mathbf{r},t)
to include non-particle-hole symmetric dynamics. The parameters
K_{1}
and
K_{2}
control the influence of both terms and are determined by the underlying system as discussed later.
The action can be written as

\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{dyn}}+\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{stat}}\equiv\int dt\mathcal{L}.

(7)

We use the following action and Lagrangian in the form of

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{dyn}}=

(8)

\displaystyle\int\mathrm{d}^{3}\mathrm{r}\mathrm{d}\mathrm{t}~\bigg(iK_{1}\left(\Psi(\mathbf{r},t)\partial_{t}\Psi^{*}(\mathbf{r},t)-\mathrm{c.c.}\right)+K_{2}|\partial_{t}\Psi(\mathbf{r},t)|^{2}\bigg)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{stat}}=

(9)

\displaystyle\int\mathrm{d}^{3}\mathrm{r}\mathrm{d}\mathrm{t}\bigg(-r_{0}|\Psi(\mathbf{r},t)|^{2}+\frac{u_{0}}{2}|\Psi(\mathbf{r},t)|^{4}+\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2M}|\nabla\Psi(\mathbf{r},t)|^{2}\bigg)

where the parameters
K_{1}
and
K_{2}
interpolate between particle-hole symmetric and non particle-hole symmetric dynamics,
r_{0}
is a bias with the character of a chemical potential and
u_{0}
is the density-density interaction strength. The precise values of these parameters have to be determined for different values of
\eta
throughout the crossover. We note that the static part only contains standard terms included in Ginzburg-Landau theory
Cyrot 1973
.

Due to the second time derivative, the total particle number is not conserved as we can see from the Noether charge, which is given by

J=i\int\mathrm{d}^{3}r\left[\Pi(r,t)\Psi(r,t)-\Pi(r,t)^{*}\Psi(r,t)^{*}\right]

(10)

with the canonical conjugate defined as

\Pi(r,t)=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial(\partial_{t}\Psi(r,t))}=K_{2}\partial_{t}\Psi^{*}(r,t)-iK_{1}\Psi^{*}(r,t).

(11)

III.1
Determination of the free parameters

In order to extract predictions from the effective field theory, the values of our free parameters
K_{1/2}
,
u_{0}
,
M
and
r_{0}
have to be determined throughout the entire BEC-BCS crossover.
We use the following constraints: As the complex scalar field describes the bosonic Cooper pair wavefunction, we set
M=2m
. We will further consider that during the crossover the energy scale has to be fixed. This enforces a constraint on the sum of
K_{1}
and
K_{2}
.
To recover the GPE limit for
K_{2}=0
and the BCS limit for
K_{1}=0
we choose

K_{1}=\hbar-2K_{2}E_{\mathrm{F}}/\hbar.

(12)

Next, we identify the amplitude and Goldstone mode in our effective model in the absence of confinement. For this we invoke the Euler-Lagrange equations to obtain

\partial_{t}\left(-K_{2}\partial_{t}+iK_{1}\right)\Psi(r,t)=\\
(-r_{0}-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2M}\nabla^{2}+u_{0}|\Psi(r,t)|^{2})\Psi(r,t).

(13)

We obtain the steady state solution by setting all time derivatives to zero and find
|\Psi_{0}|^{2}=\frac{r_{0}}{u_{0}}
. Next, we expand our complex field around the fixed point to lowest order

\Psi=\Psi_{0}+\delta_{a}+i\Psi_{0}\phi\equiv\Psi_{0}+\delta_{a}+i\delta_{\phi},

(14)

where we used the underlying
U(1)
symmetry to choose the phase of the fixed point to be zero. We expand the complex field in Eq.
14
, keeping only the lowest order. By taking the Fourier transform and separating the real and imaginary part we obtain

\displaystyle K_{2}\omega^{2}\delta_{a}-i\omega K_{1}\delta_{\phi}

\displaystyle=2r_{0}\delta_{a}+\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2M}q^{2}\delta_{a}

(15)

\displaystyle K_{2}\omega^{2}\delta_{\phi}+i\omega K_{1}\delta_{a}

\displaystyle=\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2M}q^{2}\delta_{\phi},

(16)

where
\omega
is the frequency and
q
the momentum.
Solving for
\omega
, we obtain

\displaystyle\omega_{\phi}=\sqrt{\frac{K_{1}^{2}+2K_{2}\left(\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2M}q^{2}+r_{0}\right)-\sqrt{K_{1}^{4}+4K_{2}^{2}r_{0}^{2}+4K_{1}^{2}K_{2}\left(\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2M}q^{2}+r_{0}\right)}}{2K_{2}^{2}}}

(17)

\displaystyle\omega_{a}=\sqrt{\frac{K_{1}^{2}+2K_{2}\left(\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2M}q^{2}+r_{0}\right)+\sqrt{K_{1}^{4}+4K_{2}^{2}r_{0}^{2}+4K_{1}^{2}K_{2}\left(\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2M}q^{2}+r_{0}\right)}}{2K_{2}^{2}}},

(18)

where
\omega_{a}
denotes the amplitude mode and
\omega_{\phi}
the Goldstone mode.
For the special case
K_{1}=0
, corresponding to particle-hole symmetry, we recover the well-known result, that the modes decouple
Pekker and Varma 2015
and are given by

\displaystyle\omega_{\phi,K_{1}=0}=\sqrt{\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2MK_{2}}}|q|

(19)

\displaystyle\omega_{a,K_{1}=0}=\sqrt{\frac{2r_{0}+\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2M}q^{2}}{K_{2}}}.

(20)

Taking the limit of
K_{2}\rightarrow 0
in Eq.
17
recovers the Boguliubov dispersion relation with
K_{1}=\hbar
and
r_{0}=\mu

\hbar\omega=\sqrt{\left(\frac{\hbar^{2}q^{2}}{2M}\right)^{2}+2\mu\frac{\hbar^{2}q^{2}}{2M}}.

(21)

To determine the remaining free parameters, we use the condensate density
n_{0}
in the 2D BEC-BCS crossover, which we take from
Salasnich 2007
.
For the amplitude mode energy
2\Delta
, we take the 2D mean-field result (Eq.
5

Additionally, we consider the speed of sound
v_{s}
as reported from previous work using 2D Quantum Monte Carlo
Shi et al. 2015

In our model these quantities relate to each other as

\displaystyle n_{0}

\displaystyle=r_{0}/u_{0}

(22)

\displaystyle v_{s}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\omega_{a}}{\partial q}\left.\right|_{q=0}=\hbar^{2}\sqrt{\frac{r_{0}}{M}}\sqrt{\frac{1}{\left(\hbar^{2}-2E_{\mathrm{F}}K_{2}\right)^{2}+2\hbar^{2}K_{2}r_{0}}}

(23)

\displaystyle 2\Delta

\displaystyle=\hbar\omega_{a}\left.\right|_{q=0}=\sqrt{\frac{2(\hbar-\frac{2E_{\mathrm{F}}K_{2}}{\hbar})^{2}+4K_{2}r_{0}}{2K_{2}^{2}}},

(24)

thereby fixing the remaining free parameters in our effective low-energy model,
r_{0}
,
u_{0}
,
K_{1}
and
K_{2}
(using Eq.
12

Fig.

depicts these parameters between
\eta=-8

during the crossover. We find that for

III.2
Effective two-mode model

After fixing the parameters by considering a 2-dimensional condensate, we introduce a strong spatial confinement along the
z
direction analogous to the experiment and expand the wavefunction to find a simplified two-mode model that we use for our numerical results in the main text. We also carry out numerics including the full
z
-dynamics and quantitatively find the same results. For simplicity, and as it allows a direct interpretation of the emerging mode, we have chosen to present the two-mode model here and in the main text.

We start by rewriting the complex scalar field as

\Psi(x,y,z)=\Phi(x,y)\Phi(z).

(25)

Since the experiment uses a box potential, we assume that there are no spatial modulations along the
x-y
direction and expand the field as uniform in this plane. Along the
z
direction, we have a strong harmonic confinement, meaning the atoms only occupy the harmonic oscillator ground state. Assuming that our effective
u_{0}
is small and only acts as a perturbation to the harmonic oscillator ground state and assuming we only access the lowest eigenstates, we expand the complex scalar field as

\Psi(x,y,z)=\frac{1}{L_{x}L_{y}}\left[f_{0}(z)\alpha_{0}+f_{1}(z)\alpha_{1}+f_{2}(z)\alpha_{2}\right]

(26)

with the harmonic oscillator (HO) eigenfunctions

f_{n}(z)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^{n}n!}}\left(\frac{1}{l_{z,M}\sqrt{\pi}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\hskip-6.45831pte^{-\frac{M\omega_{z}}{2\hbar}z^{2}}H\left(n,\frac{z}{l_{z,M}}\right)

(27)

with the harmonic oscillator length
l_{z,M}=\sqrt{\hbar/(M\omega_{z})}
and n-th Hermite polynomial
H(n,z)
. Due to the driving in the experiment, the system can couple to higher states. However, due to parity symmetry, our drive does not couple to the first, but to the second excited state.
By construction, integration over the
x-y
direction leads to unity.
We checked numerically that the first level of the HO is always empty during the dynamics and we neglect this contribution in the following, reducing our system to the ground and second excited state.
Next, we use this expansion to compute the integrals in Eq.
9
and Eq.
8
, from which we obtain

\displaystyle\int d^{3}r\Psi^{*}(x,y,z)\biggl[-r_{0}+\frac{u_{0}}{2}\Psi^{*}(x,y,z)\Psi(x,y,z)\biggr]\Psi(x,y,z)=-r_{0}(\alpha^{*}_{0}\alpha_{0}+\alpha^{*}_{2}\alpha_{2})

(28)

\displaystyle+\frac{u_{0}}{2}\frac{1}{16}\sqrt{\frac{M\omega_{z}}{\pi\hbar}}\left[12\sqrt{2}\alpha^{*}_{2}\alpha^{*}_{0}\alpha_{2}\alpha_{0}+8\sqrt{2}\alpha^{*}_{0}\alpha^{*}_{0}\alpha_{0}\alpha_{0}+\frac{41}{8}\sqrt{2}\alpha^{*}_{2}\alpha^{*}_{2}\alpha_{2}\alpha_{2}\right]

\displaystyle+\frac{u_{0}}{2}\frac{1}{16}\sqrt{\frac{M\omega_{z}}{\pi\hbar}}\left[\left(-8\alpha^{*}_{0}\alpha^{*}_{0}\alpha_{2}\alpha_{0}+\alpha^{*}_{2}\alpha^{*}_{2}\alpha_{2}\alpha_{0}+3\sqrt{2}\alpha^{*}_{2}\alpha^{*}_{2}\alpha_{0}\alpha_{0}\right)+\mathrm{h.c.}\right].

From the driven HO contribution, we further assume
\omega_{z}(t)^{2}=\omega_{z}^{2}+a(t)
and
a(t)=b\omega_{z}^{2}\sin(\omega_\mathrm{m}t)
. We compute

\displaystyle\int d^{3}r\Psi^{*}(x,y,z)\biggl[-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2M}\partial^{2}_{z}+\frac{1}{2}M(\omega_{z}^{2}+a(t))z^{2}\biggr]\Psi(x,y,z)

(29)

\displaystyle=\frac{\hbar\omega_{z}}{2}\left(\alpha^{*}_{0}\alpha_{0}+5\alpha^{*}_{2}\alpha_{2}+\frac{b(t)}{2}\left[\alpha^{*}_{0}\alpha_{0}+5\alpha^{*}_{2}\alpha_{2}+\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\left(\alpha^{*}_{2}\alpha_{0}+\alpha^{*}_{0}\alpha_{2}\right)\right]\right),

with the short hand notation
a(t)=b\omega_{z}^{2}\sin(\omega_\mathrm{m}t)=b(t)\omega_{z}^{2}
.
Finally, for the dynamical part of the Lagrangian we obtain

\displaystyle\int d^{3}r\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{dyn}}=-K_{2}\left(\partial_{t}\alpha_{0}\partial_{t}\alpha^{*}_{0}+\partial_{t}\alpha_{2}\partial_{t}\alpha^{*}_{2}\right)+iK_{1}\left\{\left(\alpha^{*}_{0}\partial_{t}\alpha_{0}-\alpha_{0}\partial_{t}\alpha^{*}_{0}\right)+\left(\alpha^{*}_{2}\partial_{t}\alpha_{2}-\alpha_{2}\partial_{t}\alpha^{*}_{2}\right)\right\}.

(30)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\left(-K_{2}\partial_{t}+iK_{1}\right)\alpha_{0}=\left(-\tilde{r}_{0}+\frac{\hbar\omega_{z}}{2}\left[1+b(t)\right]\right)\alpha_{0}+b(t)\frac{\sqrt{2}\hbar\omega_{z}}{4}\alpha_{2}

(31)

\displaystyle+\frac{u_{0}}{2}\frac{1}{16}\sqrt{\frac{M\omega_{z}}{\pi\hbar}}\left[12\sqrt{2}\alpha^{*}_{2}\alpha_{2}\alpha_{0}+16\sqrt{2}\alpha^{*}_{0}\alpha_{0}\alpha_{0}-16\alpha^{*}_{0}\alpha_{2}\alpha_{0}-8\alpha^{*}_{2}\alpha_{0}\alpha_{0}+\alpha^{*}_{2}\alpha_{2}\alpha_{2}+6\sqrt{2}\alpha^{*}_{0}\alpha_{2}\alpha_{2}\right]

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\left(-K_{2}\partial_{t}+iK_{1}\right)\alpha_{2}=\left(-\tilde{r}_{0}+5\frac{\hbar\omega_{z}}{2}\left[1+b(t)\right]\right)\alpha_{2}+b(t)\frac{\sqrt{2}\hbar\omega_{z}}{4}\alpha_{0}

We study the linear response of the system to the trap modulation

in the left-hand side of Eq.

\Psi(t)

\Delta n(t)=n(t)-n(0)

\omega_{\textrm{m}}

(33)

where
n(z,t)=\sum_{i=0,2}|\alpha_{i}|^{2}
is the field density.
After the system reaches a steady state at time

(

Finally we compute the response function
\Delta n(\omega_{\textrm{m}})=\int|\Delta n(z,\omega_{\textrm{m}})|dz

for different modulation frequencies
\omega_{\textrm{m}}
and extract the frequency

\omega_{c}
give us the corresponding collective mode. Doing this for different interaction parameters
\eta=\ln(k_{\mathrm{F}}a_{\mathrm{2D}})

which is depicted by a black solid line in Fig. 2 a of the main text.

To quantify the amplitude contribution to the collective mode, we define

\kappa_{\mathrm{A}}=\frac{|\int\Delta n(z,\omega_{c})dz|}{\int|\Delta n(z,\omega_{c})|dz},

(34)

representing the fraction of the total response, which does not conserve the integrated field density.
Following this definition, far in the BEC regime the integral in the numerator vanishes, so
\kappa_{\mathrm{A}}=0
and the collective mode has a pure breathing contribution.
This is because in the BEC regime, the order parameter response is simply redistributed along the

Conversely, in the BCS regime, the order parameter dynamics is dominated by amplitude oscillations i.e. the integrated field density changes and the numerator does not vanish.
In this case, the collective mode shows an amplitude contribution (

We compute the amplitude contribution

III.4
Analytical results

Here we derive the analytical solution for the transition frequency
\Delta\omega=\omega_{2}-\omega_{0}
between the ground and second excited state shown as a dashed black line in Fig. 2 a of the main text.

For this we compute the collective modes associated to the bosonic system described by Eqs.
31
,
32
by keeping only the leading order interacting terms, i.e. considering
\alpha_{2}\sim 0
and
\alpha_{0}^{*}\alpha_{0}=(r_{0}-\omega_{z}K_{1}/2)/u_{0}
in the terms proportional to
u_{0}
.
By collecting terms proportional to
\alpha_{0}
in Eq.
31
and proportional to
\alpha_{2}
in Eq.
32
the equations of motion simplify to:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\left(-K_{2}\partial_{t}+iK_{1}\right)\alpha_{0}=\left(-\tilde{r}_{0}+\frac{\hbar\omega_{z}}{2}+\delta_{0}\right)\alpha_{0},

(35)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\left(-K_{2}\partial_{t}+iK_{1}\right)\alpha_{2}=\left(-\tilde{r}_{0}+5\frac{\hbar\omega_{z}}{2}+\delta_{2}\right)\alpha_{2},

(36)

with the last terms on the right hand side of both equations representing a correction to the energy (mean-field shift) due to interactions. They are given by:

\displaystyle\delta_{0}=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\sqrt{\frac{M\omega_{z}}{\pi\hbar}}(r_{0}-\omega_{z}K_{1}/2)

(37)

\displaystyle\delta_{2}=\frac{12\sqrt{2}}{32}\sqrt{\frac{M\omega_{z}}{\pi\hbar}}(r_{0}-\omega_{z}K_{1}/2).

(38)

The collective modes associated to the resulting equations of motion (34) and (35) can be obtained by introducing an ansatz of the type
\alpha_{n}\sim\exp(i\omega_n t)
. This yields

\omega_{n}^{\pm}=\frac{-K_{1}}{2K_{2}}\pm\sqrt{\frac{K_{1}^{2}}{4K_{2}^{2}}+\frac{E_{n}}{K_{2}}}

(39)

with
E_{n}=\hbar\omega_{z}(n+\frac{1}{2})-\tilde{r}_{0}+\delta_{n}
and
n=0,2
.
Finally, we define
\Delta\omega=\omega_{2}^{+}-\omega_{0}^{+}=-(\omega_{2}^{-}-\omega_{0}^{-})
.
