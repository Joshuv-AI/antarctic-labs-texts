Fourier orthogonal series on a paraboloid
Yuan Xu

Abstract
. We study orthogonal structures and Fourier orthogonal series on the surface of a paraboloid 𝕍 0 d + 1 = { ( x , t ) : ∥ x ∥ = t , x ∈ ℝ d , 0 ≤ t < 1 } {\mathbb{V}}_{0}^{d+1}=\{(x,t):\|x\|=\sqrt{t},\,x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\,0\leq t<1\} . The reproducing kernels of the orthogonal polynomials with respect to t β ​ ( 1 − t ) γ t^{\beta}(1-t)^{\gamma} on 𝕍 0 d + 1 {\mathbb{V}}_{0}^{d+1} are related to the reproducing kernels of the Jacobi polynomials on the parabolic domain { ( x 1 , x 2 ) : x 1 2 ≤ x 2 ≤ 1 } \{(x_{1},x_{2}):x_{1}^{2}\leq x_{2}\leq 1\} in ℝ 2 {\mathbb{R}}^{2} . This connection serves as an essential tool for our study of the Fourier orthogonal series on the surface of the paraboloid, which allow us, in particular, to study the convergence of the Cesàro means on the surface. Analogous results are also established for the solid paraboloid bounded by 𝕍 0 d + 1 {\mathbb{V}}_{0}^{d+1} and the hyperplane t = 1 t=1 .

1.
Introduction

The Laplace series, so named the generalized Fourier series in spherical harmonics on the unit sphere, has
been extensively studied. One essential ingredient for understanding these series is the addition formula for
spherical harmonics, which states that the reproducing kernel of the orthogonal projection operator from
L^{2}({\mathbb{S}^{d-1}})

onto the space of spherical harmonics of degree
n
can be written as
Z_{n}({\langle}\xi,\eta{\rangle})
, where
Z_{n}

is a Gegenbauer polynomial of degree
n
in one variable and
{\langle}\xi,\eta{\rangle}
is the Euclidean inner product
of
\xi,\eta\in{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}
. Because of this closed formula of the reproducing kernels, much of the study of the
Laplace series can be reduced to the study of the Fourier-Gegenbauer series of one variable
[
1
,
5
,
14
]
.

The above narrative turns out to be the prototype for Fourier orthogonal series domains in
higher dimensions. In the past two decades, starting from the addition formula for classical orthogonal
polynomials on the unit ball
[
21
]
, closed form formulas for reproducing kernels of orthogonal
polynomials have been discovered for several regular domains, including the unit ball, regular simplex,
cylinder, as well as the unit sphere with inner product defined by weighted integrals, which makes study
of the Fourier orthogonal series on these domain feasible; see, for example,
[
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
,
9
,
10
,
11
,
13
,
18
,
19
,
20
,
21
,
22
]
and their references. For unbounded classical domains,
we refer to
[
16
]
as well as to
[
2
,
17
]
for references on more recent works, which however
require techniques beyond our narrative.

To step beyond the regular domains, we recently started to analyze orthogonal structure on quadratic
surfaces of revolutions other than the unit sphere as well as on domains bounded by such quadratic
surfaces. Let
{\mathbb{V}}_{0}^{d+1}
be a quadratic surface in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}
, parametrized in
(x,t)
,
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}

and
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
, that is a surface of revolution around the
t
axis. We consider orthogonal structure defined by
the inner product

{\langle}f,g{\rangle}=\int_{{\mathbb{V}}_{0}^{d+1}}f(x,t)g(x,t)\varpi(t){\mathrm{d}}\sigma(x,t),

where
\varpi(t)
is a weight function and
{\mathrm{d}}\sigma
is the Lebesgue measure of
{\mathbb{V}}_{0}^{d+1}
. Taking the cue
of spherical harmonics, we look for families of orthogonal polynomials that share two characteristic properties
of spherical harmonics, one is an addition formula and the other is the existence of a second order partial
differential operator that has orthogonal polynomials as eigenfunctions with eigenvalues depending only
on the degree of the polynomials, which is an analogue of the Laplace-Beltrami operator on the unit sphere.

In
[
23
]
we studied orthogonal polynomials on the surface of the cone

{\mathbb{V}}_{0}^{d+1}=\left\{(x,t):\|x\|=t,\,0\leq t\leq b,\,x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\right\},

where
b=1
or
b=\infty
, and identified two families of orthogonal polynomials that are eigenfunctions of
a differential operator, the Laguerre polynomials on the cone with
b=+\infty
and the Jacobi polynomials on
the cone with
b=1
, and also established that the Jacobi family possesses an addition formula, which allows
us to carry out a preliminary study of the Fourier orthogonal series on the surface of the cone. Moreover,
analogous results were also established on the domain bounded by the surface of the cone, together with the
hyperplane
t=1
when
b=1
. In
[
24
]
, we considered the orthogonal structure on the surface of the
hyperboloid

{\mathbb{V}}_{0}^{d+1}=\left\{(x,t):\|x\|^{2}=c^{2}(t^{2}-\varrho^{2}),\,x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\,\varrho\leq|t|\leq b\right\},

where
\varrho\geq 0
and
b=1+\varrho
or
\infty
, and it degenerates to the double cone when
\varrho=0
.
In this case the weight function
\varpi
is an even function. We again identified two families
of orthogonal polynomials, the Hermite polynomials on the hyperboloid with
b=\infty
and the Gegenbauer
polynomials on the hyperboloid with
b=1+\rho
. However, for these two families, only those polynomials
that are even in
t
are eigenfunctions of a differential operator. Furthermore, the addition formula holds
for the Gegenbauer polynomials on the hyperboloid that are even in
t
. These results allowes us to carry out
a study of the Fourier orthogonal series for functions that are even in
t
over the hyperboloid. Moreover,
analogous results were also established on the domain bounded by the surface of the hyperboloid, together
with the hyperplane
t=1
when
b=1+\varrho
.

In the present paper, we study orthogonal structure on a paraboloid of revolution, which turns out to be
very different from those on the cone and on the hyperboloid. We shall consider the surface of paraboloid
defined by

{\mathbb{V}}_{0}^{d+1}=\left\{(x,t):\|x\|=\sqrt{t},\,x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\,0\leq t\leq b\right\}

as well as the solid paraboloid
{\mathbb{V}}^{d+1}
bounded by
{\mathbb{V}}_{0}^{d+1}
and the hyperplane
t=b
. For
our study we shall consider only
b=1
, or the compact case, since there is not as much that makes
the case
b=\infty
standing out for paraboloids.

On the surface of the paraboloid, we consider a family of orthogonal polynomials with respect to the
weight function
\varpi(t)=t^{\beta}(1-t)^{\gamma}
, which shall be called the Jacobi polynomials on the paraboloid.
These polynomials will be shown to be eigenfunctions of a second order differential operator but with the
eigenvalues depending on two indices, the degree of the polynomials as well as another index that
depends on the particular choice of the orthogonal basis, in contrast to the cone and the hyperboloid for
which the corresponding eigenvalue property is independent of the choice of orthogonal bases. The
Jacobi polynomials on the paraboloid also do not possess an explicit addition formula. What they do
have is a connection to an orthogonal structure on the parabolic domain

{\mathbb{U}}=\{(x_{1},x_{2}):x_{1}^{2}\leq x_{2}\leq 1\}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
, bounded by the parabola
x_{2}=x_{1}^{2}

and the line
x_{2}=1
. A family of orthogonal polynomials on
{\mathbb{U}}
, called the Jacobi polynomials on
the parabolic domain, was first considered in
[
7
]
and they satisfy a product formula as shown
in
[
8
]
. The latter was used to study the Fourier orthogonal series in
[
4
]
, where the
essential ingredient is an addition formula that holds when one argument of the reproducing kernel is
at the corner
(1,1)
of the domain. Our essential realization is that the reproducing kernel of the
orthogonal polynomials on the paraboloid can be expressed in terms of the reproducing kernel on the
parabola domain, which provides us with the tool for studying the Fourier orthogonal series on the
paraboloid. In particular, it allows us to study the convergence of the Cesàro means of the series.
We shall also show that the connection to the structure on
{\mathbb{U}}
also extends to the solid paraboloid,
which allows us to carry out our study of the Fourier orthogonal series on the solid paraboloid.

The paper is organized as follows. In the next section, we review orthogonal structure on the parabolic
domain, where enough detail will be provided to prepare for their usage in latter sections. The orthogonal
structure and the Fourier series on the surface of the paraboloid will be discussed in Section 3, and
analogous results on the solid paraboloid will be discussed in Section 4.

2.
Orthogonal polynomials on a parabolic domain

As mentioned in the introduction, our development on
{\mathbb{V}}^{d+1}
depends heavily on what is
known on the parabolic domain

{\mathbb{U}}=\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}:x_{1}^{2}\leq x_{2}\leq 1\},

bounded by the parabola
x_{2}=x_{1}^{2}
and the line
x_{2}=1
, which we review in this section.
For
a>-1
and
b>-\frac{1}{2}
, we define the weight function

U_{a,b}(x)=(1-x_{2})^{a}(x_{2}-x_{1}^{2})^{b-\frac{1}{2}}

on
{\mathbb{U}}
and consider orthogonal polynomials with respect to the inner product

(2.1)

{\langle}f,g{\rangle}_{\mathbb{U}}:={\mathbf{d}}_{a,b}\int_{\mathbb{U}}f(x)g(x)U_{a,b}(x){\mathrm{d}}x,

where the normalization constant
{\mathbf{d}}_{a,b}

is chosen so that
{\langle}1,1{\rangle}_{a,b}=1
and its value can be verified by writing the integral over
{\mathbb{U}}
as

(2.2)

\int_{\mathbb{U}}f(x_{1},x_{2}){\mathrm{d}}x_{1}{\mathrm{d}}x_{2}=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{-1}^{1}f\big(u\sqrt{x_{2}},x_{2}\big)\sqrt{x_{2}}{\mathrm{d}}u{\mathrm{d}}x_{2}.

An orthogonal basis for this inner product was defined in
[
7
]
in terms of the Jacobi polynomials.
For
{\alpha},{\beta}>-1
, the Jacobi polynomials
P_{n}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}
are defined by

P_{n}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}(t)=\frac{({\alpha}+1)_{n}}{n!}{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\begin{matrix}-n,n+{\alpha}+{\beta}+1\\
{\alpha}+1\end{matrix};\frac{1-t}{2}\right),

and they are orthogonal with respect to the weight function
w_{{\alpha},{\beta}}(x):=(1-x)^{\alpha}(1+x)^{\beta}
,

c_{{\alpha},{\beta}}^{\prime}\int_{-1}^{1}P_{n}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}(t)P_{m}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}(t)w_{{\alpha},{\beta}}(t){\mathrm{d}}t=h_{n}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}\delta_{n,m},

where
c^{\prime}_{{\alpha},{\beta}}=2^{-{\alpha}-{\beta}-1}c_{{\alpha},{\beta}}
with

(2.3)

\displaystyle c_{{\alpha},{\beta}}:=\frac{\Gamma({\alpha}+{\beta}+2)}{\Gamma({\alpha}+1)\Gamma({\beta}+1)}\quad\hbox{and}\quad h_{n}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}:=\frac{({\alpha}+1)_{n}({\beta}+1)_{n}({\alpha}+{\beta}+n+1)}{n!({\alpha}+{\beta}+2)_{n}({\alpha}+{\beta}+2n+1)}.

In terms of
c_{{\alpha},{\beta}}
, the constant
{\mathbf{d}}_{a,b}
in (
2.1
) is given by
{\mathbf{d}}_{a,b}=c_{b-\frac{1}{2},b-\frac{1}{2}}c_{b,a}
.

Let
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\mathbb{U}},U_{a,b})
,
n=0,1,\ldots
, be the space of orthogonal polynomials of degree
n

with respect to the inner product
{\langle}\cdot,\cdot{\rangle}_{{\mathbb{U}}}
on the parabolic domain. Then

\dim{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\mathbb{U}},U_{a,b})=n+1.

The orthogonal basis for
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\mathbb{U}},U_{a,b})
given in
[
7
]
consists of polynomials

(2.4)

P_{k,n}^{a,b}(x_{1},x_{2})=P_{n-k}^{(b+k,a)}(1-2x_{2})x_{2}^{\frac{k}{2}}P_{k}^{(b-\frac{1}{2},b-\frac{1}{2})}\left(\frac{x_{1}}{\sqrt{x_{2}}}\right),\quad 0\leq k\leq n.

Their orthogonality can be verified by using (
2.2
) and so are their
L^{2}
norm. In particular, in
terms of the quantities in (
2.3
),

(2.5)

h_{k,n}^{a,b}={\mathbf{d}}_{a,b}\int_{\Omega}|P_{k,n}^{a,b}(x)|^{2}U_{a,b}(x){\mathrm{d}}x=\frac{c_{b,a}}{c_{b+k,a}}h_{n-k}^{(b+k,a)}h_{k}^{(b-\frac{1}{2},b-\frac{1}{2})}.

The polynomials
P_{k,n}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}
satisfy a product formula due to Koornwinder and Schwartz
[
8
]
.
The formula is rather complicated and takes the following form:

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
a\geq b\geq 0
. For
x=(x_{1},x_{2}),y=(y_{1},y_{2})\in{\mathbb{U}}
,

(2.6)

\displaystyle P_{n,k}^{a,b}(x_{1},x_{2})\,P_{n,k}^{a,b}(y_{1},y_{2})

\displaystyle\quad\qquad=P_{n,k}^{a,b}(1,1)\,\int_{[0,1]\times[0,\pi]^{3}}P_{n,k}^{a,b}\big(\xi_{1}(x,y;r,\psi_{1}),\xi_{2}(x,y;r,\psi)\big){\mathrm{d}}m_{a,b}(r,\psi),

where
\psi=(\psi_{1},\psi_{2},\psi_{3})
and
{\mathrm{d}}m_{a,b}(r,\psi)
is a probability measure given by

{\mathrm{d}}m_{a,b}(r,\psi)=c_{a,b}(1-r^{2})^{a-b-1}r^{2b+1}(\sin\psi_{3})^{2b-1}(\sin\psi_{2})^{2b-1}(\sin\psi_{1})^{2b}{\mathrm{d}}r{\mathrm{d}}\psi_{1}\,{\mathrm{d}}\psi_{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}\psi_{3}.

The complication of the product formula lies in the functions
\xi_{1}(x,y;r,\psi_{1})
and

\xi_{2}(x,y;r,\psi)
, which are explicitly given by fairly involved formulas, and they satisfy

\xi=(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})\in{\mathbb{U}}
. Since we do not need their explicit formulas, we will not state them
here but refer to
[
8
]
.

For
f\in L^{2}({\mathbb{U}};U_{a,b})
, its Fourier orthogonal series is defined by

f=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\operatorname{proj}_{n}(U_{a,b};f)\quad\hbox{with}\quad\operatorname{proj}_{n}(U_{a,b};f)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{{\langle}f,P_{k,n}^{a,b}{\rangle}_{\mathbb{U}}}{h_{k,n}^{a,b}}P_{k,n}^{a,b}.

The operator
\operatorname{proj}_{n}(U_{a,b}):L^{2}({\mathbb{U}};U_{a,b})\mapsto{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\mathbb{U}},U_{a,b})
is the orthogonal
projection operator, which can be written as an integral

\operatorname{proj}_{n}(U_{a,b};f,x)={\mathbf{d}}_{a,b}\int_{{\mathbb{U}}}f(y){\mathbf{P}}_{n}(U_{a,b};x,y)U_{a,b}(y){\mathrm{d}}y

in terms of the reproducing kernel
{\mathbf{P}}_{n}(U_{a,b})
of
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\mathbb{U}},U_{a,b})
. The kernel is uniquely
determined and it satisfies, in terms of the orthogonal basis (
2.4
),

{\mathbf{P}}_{n}(U_{a,b};x,y)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{P_{k,n}^{a,b}(x)P_{k,n}^{a,b}(y)}{h_{k,n}^{a,b}}.

The product formula (
2.6
) leads to a convolution structure, which can be defined as follows.
Let
\xi_{1}(x,y;r,\psi_{1})
and
\xi_{2}(x,y;r,\psi)
be given in (
2.6
).
For
g\in C({\mathbb{U}})
, define

({\mathcal{T}}_{x}g)(y):=\int_{[0,1]\times[0,\pi]^{3}}g\big(\xi_{1}(x,y;r,\psi_{1}),\xi_{2}(x,y;r,\psi)\big){\mathrm{d}}m_{a,b}(r,\psi).

The operator
{\mathcal{T}}_{x}
is an analogue of the translation operator, since the product formula can be written
as

(2.7)

P_{k,n}^{a,b}(x)P_{k,n}^{a,b}(y)=P_{k,n}^{a,b}({\mathbf{1}})\big({\mathcal{T}}_{x}P_{k,n}^{a,b}\big)(y),\qquad{\mathbf{1}}=(1,1).

This generalized translation operator is bounded in the space
L^{p}({\mathbb{U}};U_{{\alpha},b})
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
g\in L^{p}({\mathbb{U}};U_{a,b})
,
1\leq p<\infty
, or
g\in C({\mathbb{U}})
,
p=\infty
. Then, for
x\in{\mathbb{U}}
,

(2.8)

\|{\mathcal{T}}_{x}g\|_{L^{p}({\mathbb{U}};U_{a,b})}\leq\|g\|_{L^{p}({\mathbb{U}};U_{a,b})},\qquad 1\leq p\leq\infty,

where the norm is taken as the uniform norm on
{\mathbb{U}}
when
p=\infty
.

This is stated and used in
[
4
]
. Its proof follows from the product formula. Indeed, for
p=1
, we
use
|{\mathcal{T}}_{x}g(y)|\leq{\mathcal{T}}_{x}(|g|)(y)
and expand
|g|
in its Fourier orthogonal series over
{\mathbb{U}}
, then
we use the product formula on the Fourier series of
{\mathcal{T}}_{x}(|g|)
and integrate it to obtain, by orthogonality,
the identity
\|{\mathcal{T}}_{x}|g|\|_{L^{1}({\mathbb{U}};U_{a,b})}=\|g\|_{L^{1}({\mathbb{U}};U_{a,b})}
. Thus, (
2.8
)
holds for
p=1
. The inequality is also trivial for
p=\infty
. The case
1<p<\infty
then follows from
the Riesz-Thorin theorem.

The boundedness of
{\mathcal{T}}_{x}g
can be used to study the convergence of the Fourier orthogonal
expansion on
{\mathbb{U}}
. For
p\neq 2
, we need to consider a summability method. We choose the
Cesàro
(C,\delta)
means, which can be given in terms of
\operatorname{proj}_{n}(U_{a,b};f)
or the partial sum
operator
S_{n}(U_{a,b};f)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\operatorname{proj}_{k}(U_{a,b};f)
. For the reason that will become clear later,
we choose the latter one. The operator
S_{n}(U_{a,b};f)
has the kernel

{\mathbf{K}}_{n}(U_{a,b};x,y)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}{\mathbf{P}}_{k}(U_{a,b};x,y).

For
\delta>0
, let
{\mathbf{K}}_{n}^{\delta}\big(U_{a,b}\big)
denote the kernel for the Cesàro
(C,\delta)

means, which can be written in terms of
{\mathbf{P}}_{k}(U_{a,b})
or
{\mathbf{K}}_{m}\big(U_{a,b}\big)
. In particular,

(2.9)

{\mathbf{K}}_{n}^{\delta}(U_{a,b};x,y)=\frac{1}{\binom{n+\delta}{n}}\sum_{m=0}^{n}\binom{n-m+\delta-1}{n-m}{\mathbf{K}}_{m}(U_{a,b};x,y).

Then the
(C,\delta)
means
S_{n}^{\delta}(U_{a,b};f)
of the Fourier orthogonal series satisfy

S_{n}^{\delta}(U_{a,b};f)={\mathbf{d}}_{a,b}\int_{{\mathbb{U}}}f(y){\mathbf{K}}_{n}^{\delta}(U_{a,b};x,y)U_{a,b}(y){\mathrm{d}}y.

Since this is a linear integration operator, a standard argument shows that
S_{n}^{\delta}f
converges
to
f
in
L^{1}({\mathbb{U}},U_{a,b})
norm or in the uniform norm if and only if

(2.10)

\max_{x\in{\mathbb{U}}}\big\|{\mathbf{K}}_{n}^{\delta}\big(U_{a,b};x,\cdot\big)\big\|_{L^{1}({\mathbb{U}};U_{a,b})}=\max_{x\in{\mathbb{U}}}{\mathbf{d}}_{a,b}\int_{{\mathbb{U}}}\big|{\mathbf{K}}_{n}^{\delta}\big(U_{a,b};x,y\big)\big|U_{a,b}(y){\mathrm{d}}y<\infty

uniformly in
n
. Now, by the definition of
{\mathcal{T}}_{x}
, we have

{\mathbf{K}}_{n}^{\delta}(U_{a,b};x,y)={\mathcal{T}}_{x}{\mathbf{K}}_{n}^{\delta}(U_{a,b};{\mathbf{1}},\cdot)(y),

so that it follows from the inequality (
2.8
) that

(2.11)

\max_{x\in{\mathbb{U}}}\big\|{\mathbf{K}}_{n}^{\delta}\big(U_{a,b};x,\cdot\big)\big\|_{L^{1}({\mathbb{U}};U_{a,b})}\leq\big\|{\mathbf{K}}_{n}^{\delta}\big(U_{a,b};{\mathbf{1}},\cdot\big)\big\|_{L^{1}({\mathbb{U}};U_{a,b})}.

In particular, this shows that the convergence of
S_{n}^{\delta}(U_{a,b};f)
follows from the convergence
at the point
x={\mathbf{1}}=(1,1)
.

More generally, we could define a convolution structure for
f,g\in L^{1}({\mathbb{U}};U_{a,b})
by

f\ast_{U}g(x):=\int_{\mathbb{U}}f(y)({\mathcal{T}}_{x}g)(y)U_{a,b}(y){\mathrm{d}}y,\qquad x\in{\mathbb{U}}.

Since
{\mathcal{T}}_{x}g(y)
is symmetric in
x
and
y
by (
2.7
), it is not difficult to see that this
convolution is associative and commutative, as can be seen by by considering, for example, polynomials first.
Furthermore, using (
2.8
),
it follows readily that, for
f\in L^{p}({\mathbb{U}};U_{a,b})
and
g\in L^{1}({\mathbb{U}};U_{a,b})
,

(2.12)

\|f\ast_{U}g\|_{L^{p}({\mathbb{U}};U_{a,b})}\leq\|f\|_{L^{p}({\mathbb{U}};U{a,b})}\,\|g\|_{L^{1}({\mathbb{U}};U_{a,b})},\quad 1\leq p\leq\infty.

The projection operators
\operatorname{proj}_{n}(U_{a,b};f)
and the Cesàro means can be written as

(2.13)

\operatorname{proj}_{n}(U_{a,b};f)=f\ast_{U}{\mathbf{P}}_{n}\big(U_{a,b};{\mathbf{1}},\cdot\big),\qquad S_{n}^{\delta}(U_{a,b};f)=f\ast_{U}{\mathbf{K}}_{n}^{\delta}\big(U_{a,b};{\mathbf{1}},\cdot\big),

so that it again follows, by (
2.12
), that the convergence of
S_{n}^{\delta}(U_{a,b};f)
reduces
to the boundedness of
\big\|{\mathbf{K}}_{n}^{\delta}\big(U_{a,b};{\mathbf{1}},\cdot\big)\big\|_{L^{1}({\mathbb{U}};U_{a,b})}
of a
single point.

It turns out that the kernel
{\mathbf{K}}_{n}(U_{a,b};{\mathbf{1}},\cdot)
satisfies a closed formula
[
4
]
.

Theorem 2.3
.

For
a>-1
,
b>-\frac{1}{2}
and
x\in{\mathbb{U}}
,

(2.14)

\displaystyle{\mathbf{K}}_{n}(U_{a,b};{\mathbf{1}},x)=

\displaystyle\,\frac{P_{n}^{(a+b+1,b)}(1)}{h_{n}^{(a+b+1,b)}}c_{{\alpha}+b+1,b}\int_{-1}^{1}P_{n}^{(a+b+1,b)}(z(x,t))w_{a+b+1,b}(t)\ {\mathrm{d}}t,

where

z(x,t)=1-(1-t^{2})(1-x_{1})-\frac{1}{2}(1-t)^{2}(1-x_{2}).

In particular, let us denote by
k_{n}^{\delta}(w_{{\alpha},{\beta}};\cdot,\cdot)
the kernels of the Cesàro means of the Jacobi
polynomials that are given by

k_{n}^{\delta}(w_{{\alpha},{\beta}};s,t)=\frac{1}{\binom{n+\delta}{n}}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n-k+\delta}{n-k}\frac{P_{k}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}(s)P_{k}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}(t)}{h_{k}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}},

which are the kernels of the Cesàro
(C,\delta)
means of the Fourier-Jacobi series. Then (
2.14
)
leads to

(2.15)

{\mathbf{K}}_{n}^{\delta}(U_{a,b};{\mathbf{1}},x)=\frac{\delta}{n+\delta}c_{{\alpha}+b+1,b}\int_{-1}^{1}k_{n}^{\delta-1}\big(w_{a+b+1,b};1,z(x,t)\big)w_{a+b+1,b}(t)\ {\mathrm{d}}t.

The identity (
2.15
) allows us to bound the
L^{1}
norm of
{\mathbf{K}}_{n}^{\delta}(U_{a,b};{\mathbf{1}},x)
and,
as a result, obtain the convergence of the
(C,\delta)
means
S_{n}^{\delta}(U_{a,b};f)
. The result is the
following theorem established in
[
4
]
.

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
a\geq b\geq 0
. Then the Cesàro means of the Fourier orthogonal series with
respect to
U_{a,b}
satisfy

1.

if
\delta\geq a+2b+4
, then
S_{n}^{\delta}(U_{a,b};f)
is nonnegative if
f
is nonnegative;

2.

if
\delta>a+b+\frac{3}{2}
, then
S_{n}^{\delta}(U_{a,b};f)
converge to
f
in
L^{p}({\mathbb{U}};U_{a,b})
,
1\leq p<\infty
,
and in
C({\mathbb{U}})
.

Remark 2.1
.

The proof in
[
4
]
shows the boundedness of the
L^{1}
norm of
{\mathbf{K}}_{n}^{\delta}(U_{a,b};{\mathbf{1}},x)

for
a>-1
and
b>-\frac{1}{2}
, so that the convergence of
S_{n}^{\delta}(U_{a,b};f)
at
x={\mathbf{1}}
holds
for
\delta>a+b+\frac{3}{2}
without the restriction
a\geq b\geq 0
. The latter condition is imposed because
of the product formula.

The method that we outlined above also applies to other summability methods of the Fourier orthogonal series
on
{\mathbb{U}}
. In particular, it shows that the convergence can often be reduced to that at the point
x={\mathbf{1}}
.

3.
Orthogonality and Fourier orthogonal series on the surface of paraboloid

We consider orthogonal structure on the surface of the paraboloid of revolution

{\mathbb{V}}_{0}^{d+1}:=\left\{(x,t):\|x\|^{2}=t,\,\,0\leq t\leq 1,\,\,x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\right\},

which is compact since its
t
direction is bounded by
0\leq t\leq 1
. For
x=\sqrt{t}\xi
,
\xi\in{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}
, we
define the measure
{\mathrm{d}}\sigma(x,t)
on the surface
{\mathbb{V}}_{0}^{d+1}
by
{\mathrm{d}}\sigma(x,t)=t^{\frac{d-1}{2}}{\mathrm{d}}\sigma_{\mathbb{S}}(\xi){\mathrm{d}}t
,
where
{\mathrm{d}}\sigma_{\mathbb{S}}
denote the surface measure of the unit sphere
{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}
. For
d\geq 2
,
{\beta}>-\frac{d+1}{2}

and
{\gamma}>-1
, we define an inner product on the surface
{\mathbb{V}}_{0}^{d+1}

{\langle}f,g{\rangle}_{{\beta},{\gamma}}={\mathsf{b}}_{{\beta},{\gamma}}\int_{{\mathbb{V}}_{0}^{d+1}}f(x,t)g(x,t)\varpi_{{\beta},{\gamma}}(t){\mathrm{d}}\sigma(x,t),

where the weight function is defined by

\varpi_{{\beta},{\gamma}}(t):=t^{\beta}(1-t)^{\gamma},\qquad 0\leq t\leq 1,

and
{\mathsf{b}}_{{\beta},{\gamma}}
is the normalization constant given by

{\mathsf{b}}_{{\beta},{\gamma}}=\frac{1}{{\omega}_{d}}\frac{1}{\int_{0}^{1}t^{\frac{d-1}{2}}\varpi_{{\beta},{\gamma}}(t)dt}=\frac{1}{{\omega}_{d}}c_{{\beta}+\frac{d-1}{2},{\gamma}},

where the constant
c_{a,b}
is defined in (
2.3
) and
{\omega}_{d}=2\pi^{\frac{d}{2}}/\Gamma(\frac{d}{2})

is the surface area of
{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}
. The value of
{\mathsf{b}}_{{\beta},{\gamma}}
can be verified by the decomposition of the integral on
the surface of the paraboloid

\int_{{\mathbb{V}}_{0}^{d+1}}f(x,t){\mathrm{d}}\sigma(x,t)=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\|x\|^{2}=t}f(x,t){\mathrm{d}}\sigma(x,t)=\int_{0}^{1}t^{\frac{d-1}{2}}\int_{{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}}f(\sqrt{t}\xi,t){\mathrm{d}}\sigma_{{\mathbb{S}}}(\xi){\mathrm{d}}t.

3.1.
Spherical harmonics

In order to understand the orthogonal structure on the surface of the paraboloid, we first review orthogonal
polynomials on the unit sphere which are spherical harmonics. A harmonic polynomial of degree
n
is a
homogeneous polynomial of degree
n
that satisfies
\Delta Y=0
, where
\Delta
is the Laplace operator.
Its restriction on the unit sphere is called a spherical harmonic. Let
{\mathcal{H}}_{n}^{d}
denote the space of spherical
harmonics of degree
n
in
d
variables. It is known that

\dim{\mathcal{H}}_{n}^{d}=\binom{n+d-1}{n}-\binom{n+d-3}{n-2}.

Spherical harmonics of different degrees are orthogonal on the sphere. For
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
let

\{Y_{\ell}^{n}:1\leq\ell\leq\dim{\mathcal{H}}_{n}^{d}\}
be an orthonormal basis of
{\mathcal{H}}_{n}^{d}
in this subsection; then

\frac{1}{{\omega}_{d}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}Y_{\ell}^{n}(\xi)Y_{\ell^{\prime}}^{m}(\xi){\mathrm{d}}\sigma_{\mathbb{S}}(\xi)=\delta_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}}\delta_{m,n}.

A fundamental property of the spherical harmonics is that they are eigenfunctions of the Laplace-Beltrami
operator
\Delta_{0}

[
5
, (1.4.9)]
,

(3.1)

\Delta_{0}Y=-n(n+d-2)Y,\qquad Y\in{\mathcal{H}}_{n}^{d},

where
\Delta_{0}
is the restriction of
\Delta
on the unit sphere. Another important property is that
they satisfy an addition formula
[
5
, (1.2.3) and (1.2.7)]
: for
\xi\in{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}
and
\eta\in{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}
,

(3.2)

\sum_{\ell=1}^{\dim{\mathcal{H}}_{n}^{d}}Y_{\ell}^{n}(\xi)Y_{\ell}^{n}(\eta)=Z_{n}^{\frac{d-2}{2}}({\langle}\xi,\eta{\rangle}),\qquad Z_{n}^{\lambda}(t)=\frac{n+{\lambda}}{{\lambda}}C_{n}^{\lambda}(t),

where
C_{n}^{\lambda}
is the Gegenbauer polynomial of degree
n
that satisfies

c_{\lambda}\int_{-1}^{1}C_{n}^{\lambda}(t)C_{m}^{\lambda}(t)(1-t^{2})^{{\lambda}-\frac{1}{2}}{\mathrm{d}}t=\frac{{\lambda}}{n+{\lambda}}C_{n}^{\lambda}(1)\delta_{m,n},

where
C_{n}^{\lambda}(1)=(2{\lambda})_{n}/n!
and
c_{\lambda}
is the constant determined by

(3.3)

c_{\lambda}=\Big(\int_{-1}^{1}(1-t^{2})^{{\lambda}-\frac{1}{2}}{\mathrm{d}}t\Big)^{-1}=\frac{\Gamma({\lambda}+1)}{\Gamma(\frac{1}{2})\Gamma({\lambda}+\frac{1}{2})}.

The left-hand side of (
3.2
) is the reproducing kernel of
{\mathcal{H}}_{n}^{d}
and the kernel of
the projection operator
\operatorname{proj}_{n}:L^{2}({\mathbb{S}^{d-1}})\to{\mathcal{H}}_{n}^{d}
:

\operatorname{proj}_{n}f(\xi)=\frac{1}{{\omega}_{d}}\int_{{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}}f(y){\mathsf{P}}_{n}(\xi,\eta){\mathrm{d}}\sigma_{\mathbb{S}}(\eta),\qquad{\mathsf{P}}_{n}(\xi,\eta)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\dim{\mathcal{H}}_{n}^{d}}Y_{\ell}^{n}(\xi)Y_{\ell}^{n}(\eta).

Thus, the product formula shows that
\operatorname{proj}_{n}f
, hence the Fourier series on the sphere defined by

L^{2}({\mathbb{S}^{d-1}})=\bigoplus_{n=0}^{\infty}{\mathcal{H}}_{n}^{d}\,:\qquad f=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\operatorname{proj}_{n}f,

has a one-dimensional structure that can be used to reduce a large portion of the Fourier analysis on
the sphere to that of the Fourier Gegenbauer series (e.g.
[
5
]
).

3.2.
Orthogonal structure on the surface of the paraboloid

For
n=0,1,2,\ldots,
let
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\mathbb{V}}_{0}^{d+1},\varpi_{{\beta},{\gamma}})
be the space of orthogonal polynomials of
degree
n
with respect to the inner product
{\langle}\cdot,\cdot{\rangle}_{{\beta},{\gamma}}
on the surface
{\mathbb{V}}_{0}^{d+1}
.
If
Q\in{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\mathbb{V}}_{0}^{d+1},\varpi_{{\beta},{\gamma}})
, then
Q(x,t)
is a polynomial of total degree
n
in
(x,t)

variables restricted on the surface of paraboloid. Since
{\mathbb{V}}_{0}^{d+1}
is a quadratic surface,
the dimension of
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\mathbb{V}}_{0}^{d+1},\varpi_{{\beta},{\gamma}})
is equal to that of
{\mathcal{H}}_{n}^{d+1}
as
established in
[
12
, Cor. 4.2]
; that is,

\dim{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\mathbb{V}}_{0}^{d+1},\varpi_{{\beta},{\gamma}})=\binom{n+d}{n}-\binom{n+d-2}{n-2}.

An orthogonal basis of
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\mathbb{V}}_{0}^{d+1},\varpi_{{\beta},{\gamma}})
can be given in terms of the Jacobi polynomials
and spherical harmonics
[
12
]
. We also need norms of the elements in this basis.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
{\beta}>-\frac{d+1}{2}
and
{\gamma}>-1
. Let
\{Y_{\ell}^{m}:1\leq\ell\leq\dim{\mathcal{H}}_{m}^{d}\}
be an orthonormal
basis of
{\mathcal{H}}_{m}^{d}
. For
0\leq m\leq n
, define

(3.4)

{\mathsf{Q}}_{m,\ell}^{n}(x,t)=P_{n-m}^{({\beta}+m+\frac{d-1}{2},{\gamma})}(1-2t)t^{\frac{m}{2}}Y_{\ell}^{m}\left(\frac{x}{\sqrt{t}}\right).

Then
\{{\mathsf{Q}}_{m,\ell}^{n}:0\leq m\leq n,\,1\leq\ell\leq\dim{\mathcal{H}}_{m}^{d}\}
is an orthogonal basis of

{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\mathbb{V}}_{0}^{d+1},\varpi_{{\beta},{\gamma}})
. Moreover, the norm square of
{\mathsf{Q}}_{m,\ell}^{n}
is given by

(3.5)

{\mathsf{h}}_{m,n}^{{\beta},{\gamma}}={\langle}{\mathsf{Q}}_{m,\ell}^{n},{\mathsf{Q}}_{m,\ell}^{n}{\rangle}_{{\beta},{\gamma}}=\frac{c_{{\beta}+\frac{d-1}{2},{\gamma}}}{c_{m+{\beta}+\frac{d-1}{2},{\gamma}}}h_{n-m}^{(m+{\beta}+\frac{d-1}{2},{\gamma})}.

Proof.

A simple combinatorial identity shows that the cardinality of
\{(m,\ell):0\leq m\leq n,\,1\leq\ell\leq\dim{\mathcal{H}}_{m}^{d}\}

is equal to
\dim{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\mathbb{V}}_{0}^{d+1},\varpi_{{\beta},{\gamma}})
, so we only need to verify the orthogonality.
Since
Y_{\ell}^{m}
is homogeneous of degree
m
, the polynomial
{\mathsf{Q}}_{m,\ell}^{n}
is of
total degree
n
in
(x,t)
variables.
Let
{\alpha}={\beta}+\frac{d-1}{2}
. Setting
\xi=x/\sqrt{t}
, we obtain

\displaystyle{\mathsf{b}}_{{\beta},{\gamma}}

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{V}}_{0}^{d+1}}{\mathsf{Q}}_{m,\ell}^{n}(x,t){\mathsf{Q}}_{m^{\prime},\ell^{\prime}}^{n^{\prime}}(x,t)\varpi_{{\beta},{\gamma}}(t){\mathrm{d}}\sigma(x,t)=\frac{1}{{\omega}_{d}}\int_{{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}}Y_{\ell}^{m}(\xi)Y_{\ell^{\prime}}^{m^{\prime}}(\xi){\mathrm{d}}\sigma_{\mathbb{S}}(\xi)

\displaystyle\times c_{{\alpha},{\gamma}}\int_{0}^{1}P_{n-m}^{(m+{\alpha},{\gamma})}(1-2t)P_{n^{\prime}-m^{\prime}}^{(m^{\prime}+{\alpha},{\gamma})}(1-2t)t^{\frac{m+m^{\prime}}{2}+{\alpha}}(1-t)^{\gamma}{\mathrm{d}}t.

Since
Y_{\ell}^{m}
are orthonormal, the orthogonality of
{\mathsf{Q}}_{m,\ell}^{n}
follows from that of the Jacobi polynomials
in the identity, and so is the formula for
{\mathsf{h}}_{m,n}^{{\beta},{\gamma}}
.
∎

In analogue of spherical harmonics, we have shown in
[
23
,
24
]
that on the surface of the cone and the
hyperboloid, orthogonal polynomials of degree
n
or those that are even in
t
variable of degree
n
are
eigenfunctions of a second order differential operator with eigenvalues depending only on
n
. This important
property, however, does not hold for the paraboloid. The best we can do is the following proposition.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
{\beta}=-\frac{1}{2}
and
{\gamma}>-1
. Then
{\mathsf{Q}}_{m,\ell}^{n}
in (
3.4
) satisfies the differential equation

(3.6)

\displaystyle\left[t(1-t)\partial_{t}^{2}+\left(\tfrac{d}{2}-({\gamma}+\tfrac{d}{2}+1)t\right)\partial_{t}+\frac{1-t}{4t}\Delta_{0}^{(\xi)}\right]y

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad=-\left(n\big(n+{\gamma}+\tfrac{d}{2}\big)-m\big(n+\tfrac{{\gamma}+d-1}{2}\big)\right)y,

where
\Delta_{0}^{(\xi)}
is the Laplace-Beltrami operator acting on
\xi=x/\sqrt{t}\in{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}
.

Proof.

Let
f_{n,m}(t)=P_{n-m}^{(m+{\alpha},{\gamma})}(1-2t)t^{\frac{m}{2}}
and
{\alpha}={\beta}+\frac{d-1}{2}
, so that

{\mathsf{Q}}_{m,\ell}^{n}(x,t)=f_{n,m}(t)Y_{\ell}^{m}(\xi)
, where
\xi=x/\sqrt{t}
. Since the Jacobi polynomial

P_{n}^{({\alpha},{\gamma})}(1-2t)
satisfies the differential equation

(3.7)

t(1-t)y^{\prime\prime}+(1+{\alpha}-({\alpha}+{\gamma}+2)t)y^{\prime}+n(n+{\alpha}+{\gamma}+1)y=0,

a straightforward computation shows that
f_{n,m}
satisfies

\displaystyle t(1-t)f_{n,m}^{\prime\prime}(t)+

\displaystyle\big(1+{\alpha}-(2+{\alpha}+{\gamma})t\big)f_{n,m}^{\prime}(t)-m(m+2{\alpha})\frac{1-t}{4t}f_{n,m}(t)

\displaystyle=-\big(n(n+{\alpha}+{\gamma}+1)-\tfrac{1}{2}m(2n+2{\alpha}+{\gamma})\big)f_{n,m}(t).

For
{\beta}=-\frac{1}{2}
, we have
2{\alpha}=d-2
, so that
-m(m+2{\alpha})
is the eigenvalue of the Laplace-Beltrami
operator
\Delta_{0}^{(\xi)}
. Hence, multiplying the above identity by
Y_{\ell}^{m}(\xi)
and replacing

-m(m+2{\alpha})Y_{\ell}^{m}
by
\Delta_{0}^{(\xi)}Y_{\ell}^{m}
, we have proved (
3.6
).
∎

Remark 3.1
.

The right-hand side of (
3.6
) depends on
m
, so that
{\mathsf{Q}}_{m,\ell}^{n}
is the eigenfunction of
the operator in the left-hand side of (
3.6
) with the eigenvalue depends on both
m
and
n
.
This is in sharp contrast with the orthogonal structure on the cone and on the hyperboloid, for which the
eigenvalue depends only on the degree of the orthogonal polynomials.

The reproducing kernel of
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\mathbb{V}}_{0}^{d+1},\varpi_{{\beta},{\gamma}})
is defined by

\displaystyle{\mathsf{P}}_{n}\left(\varpi_{{\beta},{\gamma}};(x,t),(y,s)\right)=\sum_{m=0}^{n}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\dim{\mathcal{H}}_{m}^{d}}\frac{{\mathsf{Q}}_{m,\ell}^{n}(x,t){\mathsf{Q}}_{m,\ell}^{n}(y,s)}{{\mathsf{h}}_{m,n}^{{\beta},{\gamma}}},

which is the kernel of the orthogonal projection operator
\operatorname{proj}_{n}(\varpi_{{\beta},{\gamma}})
from the space

L^{2}({\mathbb{V}}_{0}^{d+1};\varpi_{{\beta},{\gamma}})
onto
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\mathbb{V}}_{0}^{d+1};\varpi_{{\beta},{\gamma}})
. In contrast to the cone and to
the hyperboloid, this kernel does not satisfy an addition formula that is of a closed formula of one-dimensional in
essence. Instead, however, we can express this kernel in terms of the reproducing kernel

{\mathbf{P}}_{n}(U_{a,b};\cdot,\cdot)
on the parabola domain
{\mathbb{U}}
.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
d\geq 2
and
{\gamma}>-1
. Let
(x,t)=(\sqrt{t}\xi,t)\in{\mathbb{V}}_{0}^{d+1}
and
(y,s)=(\sqrt{s}\eta,s)\in{\mathbb{V}}_{0}^{d+1}

with
\xi,\eta\in{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}
. Then for
{\beta}>-\frac{1}{2}
,

(3.8)

\displaystyle{\mathsf{P}}_{n}

\displaystyle\big(\varpi_{{\beta},{\gamma}};(x,t),(y,s)\big)=c_{\frac{d-2}{2},{\beta}-\frac{1}{2}}c_{{\beta}+\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad\times\int_{-1}^{1}\int_{-1}^{1}{\mathbf{P}}_{n}\left(U_{{\gamma},{\beta}+\frac{d-1}{2}};\big(\sqrt{t},t\big),\big(\sqrt{s}\big(\tfrac{1-z_{1}}{2}{\langle}\xi,\eta{\rangle}+\tfrac{1+z_{1}}{2}z_{2}\big),s\big)\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\times(1-z_{1})^{\frac{d-2}{2}}(1+z_{1})^{{\beta}-\frac{1}{2}}(1-z_{2}^{2})^{{\beta}}{\mathrm{d}}z_{1}{\mathrm{d}}z_{2},

and for
{\beta}=-\frac{1}{2}
,

(3.9)

\displaystyle{\mathsf{P}}_{n}(\varpi_{-\frac{1}{2},{\gamma}};(x,t),(y,s))={\mathbf{P}}_{n}\left(U_{{\gamma},\frac{d-2}{2}};\big(\sqrt{t},t\big),\big({\langle}\xi,y{\rangle},s\big)\right).

Proof.

First we note that the right-hand side of (
3.8
) is well defined. Indeed, since
|{\langle}\xi,\eta{\rangle}|\leq 1

and
-1\leq z_{1},z_{2}\leq 1
, we see
|\sqrt{s}\big(\tfrac{1-z_{1}}{2}{\langle}\xi,\eta{\rangle}+\tfrac{1+z_{1}}{2}z_{2}\big)|\leq\sqrt{s}
, so that both variables in
{\mathbf{P}}_{n}(U_{{\gamma},{\beta}+\frac{d-1}{2}})
are elements of
{\mathbb{U}}
.

We first need to specify the reproducing kernel
{\mathbf{P}}_{n}(U_{a,b})
on
{\mathbb{U}}
when one of its variables is
on the curved boundary of
{\mathbb{U}}
. Using the well-known identity
[
15
, (4.7.1)]

\frac{P_{m}^{(b-\frac{1}{2},b-\frac{1}{2})}(1)P_{m}^{(b-\frac{1}{2},b-\frac{1}{2})}(\rho)}{h_{m}^{(b-\frac{1}{2},b-\frac{1}{2})}}=\frac{C_{m}^{b}(1)C_{m}^{b}(\rho)}{h_{m}^{b}}=Z_{m}^{b}(\rho),

it follows from (
2.4
) and (
2.5
) that

(3.10)

\displaystyle{\mathbf{P}}_{n}\left(U_{a,b};(\sqrt{x_{2}},x_{2}),(y_{1},y_{2})\right)=\sum_{m=0}^{n}\frac{P_{m,n}^{a,b}(\sqrt{x_{2}},x_{2})P_{m,n}^{a,b}(y_{1},y_{2})}{h_{m,n}^{a,b}}

\displaystyle\qquad=\sum_{m=0}^{n}\frac{c_{b+m,a}}{c_{b,a}}\frac{P_{n-m}^{(b+m,a)}(1-2x_{2})P_{n-m}^{(b+m,a)}(1-2y_{2})}{h_{n-m}^{(b+m,a)}}x_{2}^{\frac{m}{2}}y_{2}^{\frac{m}{2}}Z_{m}^{b}\left(\frac{y_{1}}{\sqrt{y_{2}}}\right).

In terms of the orthogonal basis (
3.4
) and using the addition formula (
3.2
) for the
spherical harmonics, we obtain, with
{\alpha}={\beta}+\frac{d-1}{2}
,

\displaystyle{\mathsf{P}}_{n}(\varpi_{{\beta},{\gamma}};(x,t),(y,s))=\sum_{m=0}^{n}\frac{P_{n-m}^{({\alpha}+m,{\gamma})}(1-2t)P_{n-m}^{({\alpha}+m,{\gamma})}(1-2s)}{{\mathsf{h}}_{m,n}^{{\beta},{\gamma}}}t^{\frac{m}{2}}s^{\frac{m}{2}}Z_{m}^{\frac{d-2}{2}}({\langle}\xi,\eta{\rangle}),

where
\xi=\frac{x}{\sqrt{t}}\in{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}
and
\eta=\frac{y}{\sqrt{s}}\in{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}
. For
{\beta}=-\frac{1}{2}
, the sum in
the right-hand side can be identified, using (
3.5
), with
{\mathbf{P}}_{n}(U_{{\gamma},\frac{d-2}{2}})
in
(
3.10
) by setting
x_{2}=t
,
y_{2}=s
and
y_{1}=\sqrt{s}{\langle}\xi,\eta{\rangle}
, which proves (
3.9
).

For
{\beta}>-\frac{1}{2}
, we need to increase the value of the index in the zonal harmonic by using the following identity,
proved recently in
[
22
]
,

(3.11)

\displaystyle Z_{m}^{\lambda}(t)=c_{{\lambda},\sigma-1}c_{\sigma}\int_{-1}^{1}\int_{-1}^{1}

\displaystyle Z_{m}^{{\lambda}+\sigma}\left(\tfrac{1-z_{1}}{2}t+\tfrac{1+z_{1}}{2}z_{2}\right)

\displaystyle\times(1-z_{1})^{\lambda}(1+z_{1})^{\sigma-1}(1-z_{2}^{2})^{\sigma-\frac{1}{2}}{\mathrm{d}}z_{1}{\mathrm{d}}z_{2}

with
{\lambda}=\frac{d-2}{2}
and
\sigma={\beta}+\frac{1}{2}
. This shows that

\displaystyle{\mathsf{P}}_{n}\big(\varpi_{{\beta},{\gamma}};

\displaystyle(x,t),(y,s)\big)=c_{\frac{d-2}{2},{\beta}-\frac{1}{2}}c_{{\beta}+\frac{1}{2}}\int_{-1}^{1}\int_{-1}^{1}\sum_{m=0}^{n}\frac{P_{n-m}^{({\alpha}+m,{\gamma})}(1-2t)P_{n-m}^{({\alpha}+m,{\gamma})}(1-2s)}{{\mathsf{h}}_{m,n}^{{\beta},{\gamma}}}

\displaystyle\times t^{\frac{m}{2}}s^{\frac{m}{2}}Z_{m}^{{\alpha}}\big(\tfrac{1-z_{1}}{2}{\langle}\xi,\eta{\rangle}+\tfrac{1+z_{1}}{2}z_{2}\big)(1-z_{1})^{\frac{d-2}{2}}(1+z_{1})^{{\beta}-\frac{1}{2}}(1-z_{2}^{2})^{{\beta}}{\mathrm{d}}z_{1}{\mathrm{d}}z_{2}.

The sum in the right-hand side can be identified, using (
3.5
), with the reproducing kernel

{\mathbf{P}}_{n}(U_{{\gamma},{\beta}+\frac{d-1}{2}})
in (
3.10
), which gives (
3.8
).
∎

The identity (
3.9
) for
{\beta}=-\frac{1}{2}
can be regarded as the limit of (
3.8
)
as
{\beta}\to-\frac{1}{2}
by using the limit relation (
4.3
).

The kernel
{\mathsf{P}}_{n}(\varpi_{{\beta},{\gamma}})
, however, does not satisfy a closed formula in general. In the case that

(x,t)
is on the boundary
t=1
of the paraboloid
{\mathbb{V}}_{0}^{d+1}
, however, we could derive a closed
form formula for the kernel of the partial sum operator

{\mathsf{K}}_{n}\big(\varpi_{{\beta},{\gamma}},(x,t),(y,s)\big)=\sum_{m=0}^{n}{\mathsf{P}}_{m}\big(\varpi_{{\beta},{\gamma}};(x,t),(y,s)\big)

on the paraboloid by using the closed formula of
{\mathbf{K}}_{n}(U_{a,b};{\mathbf{1}},\cdot)
in Theorem
2.3
.
We state the result for
{\beta}=-\frac{1}{2}
as an example.

Corollary 3.4
.

Let
d\geq 2
and
{\gamma}>-1
. Then, for
\xi\in{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}
,

\displaystyle{\mathsf{K}}_{n}\big(\varpi_{-\frac{1}{2},{\gamma}};(\xi,1),(y,s)\big)

\displaystyle=\frac{P_{n}^{({\gamma}+\frac{d}{2},\frac{d-2}{2})}(1)}{h_{n}^{({\gamma}+\frac{d}{2},\frac{d-2}{2})}}

\displaystyle\times c_{{\gamma}+\frac{d}{2},\frac{d-2}{2}}\int_{-1}^{1}P_{n}^{({\gamma}+\frac{d}{2},\frac{d-2}{2})}\big(z^{\prime}(\xi,y,v)\big)w_{{\gamma}+\frac{d}{2},\frac{d-2}{2}}(v)\ {\mathrm{d}}v,

where, with
y=\sqrt{s}\eta
,

z^{\prime}(\xi,y,v)=z(({\langle}\xi,\eta{\rangle},s),v)=1-(1-v^{2})(1-{\langle}\xi,y{\rangle})-\frac{1}{2}(1-v)^{2}(1-s).

This is a corollary of (
3.9
) and (
2.14
). Similarly, using (
3.8
)
and (
2.14
), we can derive an explicit formula for
{\beta}>-\frac{1}{2}
, which is however more involved.

3.3.
Summability of Fourier orthogonal series

Let
\operatorname{proj}_{n}(\varpi_{{\beta},{\gamma}})
be the orthogonal projection operator

\operatorname{proj}_{n}(\varpi_{{\beta},{\gamma}}):L^{2}({\mathbb{V}}_{0}^{d+1};\varpi_{{\beta},{\gamma}})\mapsto{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\mathbb{V}}_{0}^{d+1},\varpi_{{\beta},{\gamma}}).

In terms of the reproducing kernel
{\mathsf{P}}_{n}(\varpi_{{\beta},{\gamma}})
of
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\mathbb{V}}_{0}^{d+1},\varpi_{{\beta},{\gamma}})
, we have

\operatorname{proj}_{n}(\varpi_{{\beta},{\gamma}};f)={\mathsf{b}}_{{\beta},{\gamma}}\int_{{\mathbb{V}}_{0}^{d+1}}f(y,s){\mathsf{P}}_{n}\big(\varpi_{{\beta},{\gamma}};(x,t),(y,s)\big)\varpi_{{\beta},{\gamma}}(s){\mathrm{d}}\sigma(y,s).

For
f\in L^{2}({\mathbb{V}}_{0}^{d+1};\varpi_{{\beta},{\gamma}})
, the Fourier orthogonal series of
f
on
{\mathbb{V}}_{0}^{d+1}
is defined by

f=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\operatorname{proj}_{n}(\varpi_{{\beta},{\gamma}};f).

Below we study the summability of this Fourier orthogonal series. We start with a definition.
Recall that
{\mathbb{U}}
denotes the domain bounded by the parabolic
x_{2}=x_{1}^{2}
and
x_{2}=1
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
.
We further denote by
{\mathbb{U}}_{0}
the curved portion of the boundary of
{\mathbb{U}}
,

{\mathbb{U}}_{0}:=\{(x_{1},x_{2})\in{\mathbb{U}}:x_{2}=x_{1}^{2}\}.

Definition 3.5
.

Let
{\beta}\geq-\frac{1}{2}
and
{\gamma}>-1
. Let
g:{\mathbb{U}}_{0}\times{\mathbb{U}}\mapsto{\mathbb{R}}
such that, for each
t\in[0,1]
,
the function
x\mapsto g((\sqrt{t},t);x)
is in
L^{1}\big({\mathbb{U}};U_{{\gamma},{\alpha}}\big)
. For
(x,t)=(\sqrt{t}\xi,t)\in{\mathbb{V}}_{0}^{d+1}
and
(y,s)=(\sqrt{s}\eta,s)\in{\mathbb{V}}_{0}^{d+1}
, define for
{\beta}>-\frac{1}{2}

\displaystyle{\mathsf{T}}_{{\beta},{\gamma}}g\big((x,t),(y,s)\big):=c_{\frac{d-2}{2},{\beta}-\frac{1}{2}}c_{{\beta}+\frac{1}{2}}\int_{-1}^{1}\int_{-1}^{1}

\displaystyle g\left(\big(\sqrt{t},t\big),\big(\sqrt{s}\big(\tfrac{1-z_{1}}{2}{\langle}\xi,\eta{\rangle}+\tfrac{1+z_{1}}{2}z_{2}\big),s\big)\right)

\displaystyle\times(1-z_{1})^{\frac{d-2}{2}}(1+z_{1})^{{\beta}-\frac{1}{2}}(1-z_{2}^{2})^{{\beta}}{\mathrm{d}}z

and, furthermore, define for
{\beta}=-\frac{1}{2}

{\mathsf{T}}_{-\frac{1}{2},{\gamma}}g\big((x,t),(y,s)\big):=g\left(\big(\sqrt{t},t\big),\big({\langle}\xi,y{\rangle},s\big)\right).

The definition of

) and (

{\mathsf{P}}_{n}(\varpi_{{\beta},{\gamma}};(x,t),(y,s))={\mathsf{T}}_{{\beta},{\gamma}}{\mathbf{P}}_{n}(U_{{\gamma},{\beta}+\frac{d-1}{2}})\big((x,t),(y,s)\big).

For each fixed

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
{\beta}\geq-\frac{1}{2}
and
{\gamma}>-1

,

(3.13)

\int_{{\mathbb{V}}_{0}^{d+1}}\left|{\mathsf{T}}_{{\beta},{\gamma}}g\big((x,t),(y,s)\big)\right|\varpi_{{\beta},{\gamma}}(s){\mathrm{d}}\sigma(y,s)\leq c\int_{{\mathbb{U}}}\left|g\left(\big(\sqrt{t},t\big),z\right)\right|U_{{\gamma},{\alpha}}(z){\mathrm{d}}z.

Let

Using the well-known integral relation

\displaystyle\qquad={\mathsf{b}}_{-\frac{1}{2},{\gamma}}c_{\frac{d-2}{2}}{\omega}_{d}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{-1}^{1}\left|G\big(\sqrt{s}\,u,s\big)\right|(1-u^{2})^{\frac{d-3}{2}}{\mathrm{d}}u\,s^{\frac{d-2}{2}}(1-s)^{\gamma}{\mathrm{d}}s

\displaystyle\qquad={\mathbf{d}}_{{\gamma},\frac{d-2}{2}}\int_{\mathbb{U}}|G(z)|(z_{2}-z_{1}^{2})^{\frac{d-3}{2}}(1-z_{2})^{\gamma}{\mathrm{d}}z,

where we have used (
2.2
) in the last step, and the constant can be verified simply
by the fact that if
g=1
, then
{\mathsf{T}}_{{\beta},{\gamma}}g=1
. In particular, this shows that the inequality (
3.13
)
is in fact an identity for
{\beta}=-\frac{1}{2}
.

We now consider the case
{\beta}>-\frac{1}{2}
. Using (
3.14
), we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{V}}_{0}^{d+1}}

\displaystyle\left|{\mathsf{T}}_{{\beta},{\gamma}}g\big((x,t),(y,s)\big)\right|\varpi_{{\beta},{\gamma}}(s){\mathrm{d}}\sigma(y,s)

\displaystyle\leq c\int_{0}^{1}\int_{-1}^{1}\int_{-1}^{1}\int_{-1}^{1}\left|G\big(\sqrt{s}\big(\tfrac{1-z_{1}}{2}u+\tfrac{1+z_{1}}{2}z_{2}\big),s\big)\right|

\displaystyle\times(1-z_{1})^{\frac{d-2}{2}}(1+z_{1})^{{\beta}-\frac{1}{2}}(1-z_{2}^{2})^{{\beta}}(1-u^{2})^{\frac{d-3}{2}}s^{{\beta}+\frac{d-1}{2}}(1-s)^{\gamma}{\mathrm{d}}z{\mathrm{d}}u{\mathrm{d}}s,

where
c={\omega}_{d}c_{\frac{d-2}{2}}c_{\frac{d-2}{2},{\beta}-\frac{1}{2}}c_{{\beta}+\frac{1}{2}}
. Making a change of variables
z_{1}\mapsto y

with

y=\tfrac{1-z_{1}}{2}u+\tfrac{1+z_{1}}{2}z_{2}

and dividing the integral over
{\mathrm{d}}u{\mathrm{d}}z_{2}
on

into two integrals over the triangles

\{(u,z_{2})\in[-1,1]^{2}:u\geq z_{2}\}
and

\displaystyle 2^{{\beta}+\frac{d-1}{2}}\int_{-1}^{1}\int_{u}^{1}\int_{u}^{z_{2}}\left|G(\sqrt{s}y,s)\right|(z_{2}-y)^{\frac{d-2}{2}}(y-u)^{{\beta}-\frac{1}{2}}dy\frac{(1-z_{2}^{2})^{\beta}(1-u^{2})^{\frac{d-3}{2}}}{(z_{2}-u)^{{\beta}+\frac{d-1}{2}}}{\mathrm{d}}z_{2}{\mathrm{d}}u

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle 2^{{\beta}+\frac{d-1}{2}}\int_{-1}^{1}\int_{-1}^{u}\int_{z_{2}}^{u}\left|G(\sqrt{s}y,s)\right|(y-z_{2})^{\frac{d-2}{2}}(u-y)^{{\beta}-\frac{1}{2}}dy\frac{(1-z_{2}^{2})^{\beta}(1-u^{2})^{\frac{d-3}{2}}}{(u-z_{2})^{{\beta}+\frac{d-1}{2}}}{\mathrm{d}}z_{2}{\mathrm{d}}u.

\displaystyle 2^{{\beta}+\frac{d-1}{2}}\int_{-1}^{1}\left|G(\sqrt{s}y,s)\right|

\displaystyle\left[\int_{-1}^{y}\int_{y}^{1}(z_{2}-y)^{\frac{d-2}{2}}(y-u)^{{\beta}-\frac{1}{2}}\frac{(1-z_{2}^{2})^{\beta}(1-u^{2})^{\frac{d-3}{2}}}{(z_{2}-u)^{{\beta}+\frac{d-1}{2}}}{\mathrm{d}}z_{2}{\mathrm{d}}u\right.

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\left.\int_{y}^{1}\int_{-1}^{y}(y-z_{2})^{\frac{d-2}{2}}(u-y)^{{\beta}-\frac{1}{2}}\frac{(1-z_{2}^{2})^{\beta}(1-u^{2})^{\frac{d-3}{2}}}{(u-z_{2})^{{\beta}+\frac{d-1}{2}}}{\mathrm{d}}z_{2}{\mathrm{d}}u\right]{\mathrm{d}}y.

(u,z_{2})\mapsto(v_{1},v_{2})

v_{2}=(y-u)/(1+y)

\displaystyle(1-y^{2})^{{\beta}+\frac{d-2}{2}}

\displaystyle\bigg[(1-y)\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{(1+y+(1-y)v_{1})^{\beta}(1-y+(1+y)v_{2})^{\frac{d-3}{2}}}{((1-y)v_{1}+(1+y)v_{2})^{{\beta}+\frac{d-1}{2}}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\times v_{1}^{\frac{d-2}{2}}v_{2}^{{\beta}-\frac{1}{2}}(1-v_{1})^{\beta}(1-v_{2})^{\frac{d-3}{2}}{\mathrm{d}}v_{1}{\mathrm{d}}v_{2}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\,(1+y)\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{(1-y+(1+y)v_{1})^{\beta}(1+y+(1-y)v_{2})^{\frac{d-3}{2}}}{((1+y)v_{1}+(1-y)v_{2})^{{\beta}+\frac{d-1}{2}}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\times v_{1}^{\frac{d-2}{2}}v_{2}^{{\beta}-\frac{1}{2}}(1-v_{1})^{\beta}(1-v_{2})^{\frac{d-3}{2}}{\mathrm{d}}v_{1}{\mathrm{d}}v_{2}\bigg]{\mathrm{d}}y.

\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}v_{1}^{-\frac{1}{2}}v_{2}^{-\frac{1}{2}}(1-v_{1})^{\beta}(1-v_{2})^{\frac{d-3}{2}}{\mathrm{d}}v_{1}{\mathrm{d}}v_{2}=\frac{\pi\Gamma({\beta}+1)\Gamma(\frac{d-1}{2})}{\Gamma({\beta}+\frac{3}{2})\Gamma(\frac{d}{2})}.

Similarly, it is easy to see that the same bound holds for the second term in the square bracket.
Putting all these estimates together, we conclude that

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{V}}_{0}^{d+1}}

\displaystyle\left|{\mathsf{T}}_{{\beta},{\gamma}}g\big((x,t),(y,s)\big)\right|\varpi_{{\beta},{\gamma}}(s){\mathrm{d}}\sigma(y,s)

\displaystyle\leq c\int_{0}^{1}\int_{-1}^{1}|G(\sqrt{s}y,s)|(1-y^{2})^{{\beta}+\frac{d-2}{2}}s^{{\beta}+\frac{d-1}{2}}(1-s)^{\gamma}{\mathrm{d}}y{\mathrm{d}}s

\displaystyle=c\int_{{\mathbb{U}}}|G(z_{1},z_{2})|U_{{\gamma},{\beta}+\frac{d-1}{2}}(z_{1},z_{2}){\mathrm{d}}z,

where the last step follows from (
2.2
). This completes the proof.
∎

Proposition 3.8

4.
Orthogonality and Fourier orthogonal series on the solid paraboloid

We consider orthogonal structure on the solid paraboloid of revolution

{\mathbb{V}}^{d+1}:=\left\{(x,t):\|x\|^{2}\leq t,\,\,0\leq t\leq 1,\,\,x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\right\},

which is bounded by the surface
{\mathbb{V}}_{0}^{d+1}
and the hyperplane
t=1
of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}
.
The
t
-section of the domain,
\{x:\|x\|\leq\sqrt{t}\}
, is the ball of radius
\sqrt{t}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. W
review the orthogonal structure on the unit ball first.

4.1.
Classical orthogonal polynomials on the unit ball

For
\mu>-\frac{1}{2}
, let
\varpi_{\mu}
be the weight function

\varpi_{\mu}(x):=(1-\|x\|^{2})^{\mu-\frac{1}{2}},\qquad\|x\|<1.

The classical orthogonal polynomials on the unit ball are orthogonal with respect to the inner product

{\langle}f,g{\rangle}_{\mu}=b_{\mu}\int_{{\mathbb{B}}^{d}}f(x)g(x)\varpi_{\mu}(x){\mathrm{d}}x\quad\hbox{with}\quad b_{\mu}=\frac{\Gamma(\mu+\frac{d+1}{2})}{\pi^{\frac{d}{2}}\Gamma(\mu+\frac{1}{2})},

where
b_{\mu}
is the normalization constant of
\varpi_{\mu}
so that
{\langle}1,1{\rangle}=1
.

Let
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\mathbb{B}}^{d},\varpi_{\mu})
be the space of orthogonal polynomials of degree
n
with respect to

\varpi_{\mu}
. Then
\dim{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\mathbb{B}}^{d},\varpi_{\mu})=\binom{n+d-1}{n}
. An orthogonal
basis of
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\mathbb{B}}^{d},\varpi)
can be given in terms of the Jacobi polynomials or spherical harmonics,
see
[
6
, Chapter 5]
, which we shall call the
basis with parity
, since its elements are polynomials
that are even in each of its variables if
n
is even and odd in each of its variables if
n
is odd. The
orthogonal polynomials of degree
n
are eigenfunctions of a second order differential operator:
for
u\in{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\mathbb{B}}^{d},\varpi_{\mu})
,

(4.1)

\left(\Delta-{\langle}x,\nabla{\rangle}^{2}-(2\mu+d-1){\langle}x,\nabla{\rangle}\right)u=-n(n+2\mu+d-1)u.

Furthermore, these polynomials also satisfy an addition formula. Let
{\mathbf{P}}_{n}(\varpi_{\mu};\cdot,\cdot)
be
the reproducing kernel of the space
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\mathbb{B}}^{d},\varpi_{\mu})
. In terms of an orthonormal basis

\{P_{{\mathbf{k}}}^{n}:|{\mathbf{k}}|=n\}
of
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\mathbb{B}}^{d},\varpi_{\mu})
, the kernel can be written as

{\mathbf{P}}_{n}(\varpi_{\mu};x,y)=\sum_{|{\mathbf{k}}|=n}P_{{\mathbf{k}}}^{n}(x)P_{{\mathbf{k}}}^{n}(y).

The addition formula on the unit ball states
[
21
]
, for
\mu\geq 0
,

(4.2)

\displaystyle{\mathbf{P}}_{n}(\varpi_{\mu};x,y)=c_{\mu-\frac{1}{2}}\int_{-1}^{1}Z_{n}^{\mu+\frac{d-1}{2}}

\displaystyle\left({\langle}x,y{\rangle}+t\sqrt{1-\|x\|^{2}}\sqrt{1-\|y\|^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\times(1-t^{2})^{\mu-1}{\mathrm{d}}t,

where the identity holds for
\mu=0
under the limit

(4.3)

\lim_{\mu\to 0}c_{\mu-\frac{1}{2}}\int_{-1}^{1}f(t)(1-t^{2})^{\mu-1}{\mathrm{d}}t=\frac{f(1)+f(-1)}{2}.

4.2.
Orthogonal structure of the solid paraboloid

For
{\beta}>-\frac{d+1}{2}
,
{\gamma}>-1
and
\mu>-\frac{1}{2}
, we define a weight function
W_{{\beta},{\gamma},\mu}

on
{\mathbb{V}}^{d+1}
,

W_{{\beta},{\gamma},\mu}(x,t):=t^{\beta}(1-t)^{\gamma}(t-\|x\|^{2})^{\mu-\frac{1}{2}},\qquad(x,t)\in{\mathbb{V}}^{d+1}.

With respect to this weight function, we define an inner product

{\langle}f,g{\rangle}_{{\beta},{\gamma},\mu}={\mathbf{b}}_{{\beta},{\gamma},\mu}\int_{{\mathbb{V}}^{d+1}}f(x,t)g(x,t)W_{{\beta},{\gamma},\mu}(x,t){\mathrm{d}}x{\mathrm{d}}t,

where
{\mathbf{b}}_{{\beta},{\gamma},\mu}=b_{\mu}c_{{\beta}+\mu+\frac{d-1}{2},{\gamma}}
with
b_{\mu}
is the normalization constant
of
\varpi_{\mu}
on the unit ball and
c_{{\alpha},{\gamma}}
is defined in (
2.3
). The weight function

W_{{\beta},{\gamma},\mu}
can be written as

(4.4)

W_{{\beta},{\gamma},\mu}(x,t)=t^{{\beta}+\mu-\frac{1}{2}}(1-t)^{\gamma}(1-\|x^{\prime}\|^{2})^{\mu-\frac{1}{2}},\quad\hbox{with}\quad x^{\prime}=\frac{x}{\sqrt{t}}\in{\mathbb{B}}^{d}.

Hence, the value of the constant
{\mathbf{b}}_{{\beta},{\gamma},\mu}
can be verified by using the identity

\int_{{\mathbb{V}}^{d+1}}f(x,t){\mathrm{d}}x{\mathrm{d}}t=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\|x\|^{2}\leq t}f(x,t){\mathrm{d}}x{\mathrm{d}}t=\int_{0}^{1}t^{\frac{d}{2}}\int_{{\mathbb{B}}^{d}}f\big(\sqrt{t}y,t\big){\mathrm{d}}y{\mathrm{d}}t.

For
n=0,1,2,\ldots,
let
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\mathbb{V}}^{d+1},W_{{\beta},{\gamma},\mu})
denote the space of orthogonal polynomials
of degree
n
in
(x,t)
variables with respect to the inner
product

{\langle}\cdot,\cdot{\rangle}_{{\beta},{\gamma},\mu}
on the paraboloid. Then
\dim{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\mathbb{V}}^{d+1},W_{{\beta},{\gamma},\mu})=\binom{n+d}{n}
. An orthogonal basis of this space can be given in terms of the Jacobi polynomials
and the classical orthogonal polynomials on the unit ball
[
12
]
. We will also need the norms of
these orthogonal polynomials.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
{\beta}>-\frac{d+1}{2}
and
{\gamma}>-1
. Let
\{P_{{\mathbf{k}}}^{m}:|{\mathbf{k}}|=m,\,{\mathbf{k}}\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}^{d}\}

{\mathcal{V}}_{m}^{d}({\mathbb{B}}^{d},\varpi_{\mu})

(4.5)

Then
\{{\mathsf{Q}}_{m,{\mathbf{k}}}^{n}:|{\mathbf{k}}|=m,\,0\leq m\leq n,\,{\mathbf{k}}\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}^{d}\}

{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\mathbb{V}}^{d+1},W_{{\beta},{\gamma},\mu})

{\mathsf{Q}}_{m,\ell}^{n}
is given by

(4.6)

{\mathbf{h}}_{m,n}^{{\beta},{\gamma},\mu}={\langle}{\mathbf{Q}}_{m,{\mathbf{k}}}^{n},{\mathbf{Q}}_{m,{\mathbf{k}}}^{n}{\rangle}_{{\beta},{\gamma},\mu}=\frac{c_{{\beta}+\mu+\frac{d-1}{2},{\gamma}}}{c_{m+{\beta}+\mu+\frac{d-1}{2},{\gamma}}}h_{n-m}^{(m+{\beta}+\mu+\frac{d-1}{2},{\gamma})}.

Proof.

Using the parity of
P_{{\mathbf{k}}}^{m}
, it is not difficult to see that
{\mathbf{Q}}_{m,{\mathbf{k}}}^{n}
is a polynomial
of degree
n
in
(x,t)
variables.
Let
{\alpha}={\beta}+\mu+\frac{d-1}{2}
. Setting
