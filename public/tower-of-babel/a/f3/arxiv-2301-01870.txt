A class of nonlinear elasticity problems with no local but many global minimizers
Yury Grabovsky, Lev Truskinovsky

Abstract
We present a class of models of elastic phase transitions with incompatible energy wells in any space dimension, where an abundance of Lipschitz global minimizers in a hard device coexists with a complete lack of strong local minimizers. The analysis hinges on the proof that every strong local minimizer in a hard device is also a global minimizer which is applicable much beyond the chosen class of models. Along the way we show that a new proof of sufficiency for a subclass of affine boundary conditions can be built around a novel nonlinear generalization of the classical Clapeyron theorem, whose subtle relation to dynamics was studied extensively by R. Fosdick.

t\in(0,1)

satisfies the jump set equations (
2.17
) with
\bm{H}_{-}
as the
corresponding pair.

Then all of the matrices
\bm{H}_{t}
,
t\in(0,1)
must fail rank-one convexity
in the sense that they must fail to satisfy the inequality

W(\bm{H})\geq W(\bm{H}_{t})+\langle\bm{P}_{t},\bm{H}-\bm{H}_{t}\rangle\quad\forall\bm{H}\colon\;\mathrm{rank}(\bm{H}-\bm{H}_{t})=1,

(2.18)

where
\bm{P}_{t}=W_{\bm{H}}(\bm{H}_{t})
.

Proof.

Suppose, with the goal of getting a contradiction, that (
2.18
) holds. Then, since matrices

\bm{H}_{-}
,
\bm{H}_{t}
and
\bm{H}_{+}
all lie on the same rank-one line, we obtain

W(\bm{H}_{-})+t\langle\bm{P}_{-},[\![\bm{H}]\!]\rangle\leq W(\bm{H}_{t})\leq W(\bm{H}_{+})-(1-t)\langle\bm{P}_{t},[\![\bm{H}]\!]\rangle.

Now, using the jump set equations (
2.17
), we obtain

(1-t)(\langle\bm{P}_{-},[\![\bm{H}]\!]\rangle-\langle\bm{P}_{t},[\![\bm{H}]\!]\rangle)\geq 0.

Using the fact that
\bm{H}_{t}-\bm{H}_{-}=t[\![\bm{H}]\!]
we obtain

\langle\bm{P}_{t}-\bm{P}_{-},\bm{H}_{t}-\bm{H}_{-}\rangle\leq 0.

(2.19)

The variational significance of inequality (
2.19
), called the phase
interchange stability inequality, was understood in
[
20
]
, where,
according to
[
20
, Lemma 4.1]
, the pair of rank-one related matrices

\bm{H}_{-}
and
\bm{H}_{t}
would have to satisfy the jump set equations
(
2.17
), in contradiction with the assumption (ii) of the lemma. Thus, the
assumption that (
2.18
) holds cannot be true, and all matrices

\bm{H}_{t}
,
t\in(0,1)
, must fail to be rank-one convex.
∎

Remark 2.4
.

The energy density
QW_{0}(\bm{H})=RW_{0}(\bm{H})
is non-convex as it has a
double-well shape along the multiples of
\bm{I}
, specifically,

QW_{0}(\varepsilon\bm{I})=\Phi^{**}(d\varepsilon)-d(d-1)\mu\varepsilon^{2}
. This is clear from the fact
that its bulk modulus is negative in the interval

\theta\in(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})
, where
\theta_{1}
and
\theta_{2}
are given by
(
2.13
) for the bi-quadratic material model (
1.4
). Moreover,
the value of this modulus is constant and equal to
-2(d-1)\mu/d
,
cf.
[
9
]
, which corresponds exactly to the threshold for the loss
of strong ellipticity of the equilibrium equations (saturation of the
Legendre-Hadamard conditions). This is associated with the generic
degeneration of the acoustic tensor along rank one envelopes as we explain
in our Appendix
A
. The vanishing of the velocity of the
longitudinal waves in our example turns the phase-transforming material into
an elastic æther
[
14
]
.

We are now ready to show that for a class of energies
W(\bm{H})
to which our example
belongs all strong local minimizers in a hard device (Dirichlet boundary conditions) must be global. The first observation, leading to this result is
that any two energies
W
and
W^{\prime}
that have one and the same quasiconvex
envelope are equivalent as far as existence of metastable states are
concerned. This is because for any metastable state
\bm{u}

\int_{\Omega}W(\nabla\bm{u})d\bm{x}=\int_{\Omega}QW(\nabla\bm{u})d\bm{x}.

Thus, any metastable configuration for
W
is also metastable for
QW
. Theorem
2.1
shows that our energy has the property

QW(\bm{H})=N(\bm{H})+C(\bm{H}),

(2.20)

where
N(\bm{H})
is a null-Lagrangian and
C(\bm{H})
is convex and
C^{1}
smooth.

Theorem
2.5
.

Let
W(\bm{H})
be the energy density satisfying (
2.20
). Then any Lipschitz strong local minimizer of
\int_{\Omega}W(\nabla\bm{u})d\bm{x}
with prescribed Dirichlet boundary conditions is a global minimizer.

Proof.

Let
\bm{v}
be a Lipschitz competitor
that agrees with
\bm{u}
on
\partial\Omega
. Then

\int_{\Omega}N(\nabla\bm{v})d\bm{x}=\int_{\Omega}N(\nabla\bm{u})d\bm{x},\qquad C(\nabla\bm{v})\geq C(\nabla\bm{u})+\langle C_{\bm{F}}(\nabla\bm{u}),\nabla(\bm{v}-\bm{u})\rangle.

(2.21)

Since
\bm{u}
is an equilibrium we have

0=\nabla\cdot W_{\bm{F}}(\nabla\bm{u})=\nabla\cdot QW_{\bm{F}}(\nabla\bm{u})=\nabla\cdot N_{\bm{F}}(\nabla\bm{u})+\nabla\cdot C_{\bm{F}}(\nabla\bm{u})=\nabla\cdot C_{\bm{F}}(\nabla\bm{u}).

The equality of
W_{\bm{F}}(\bm{H})=QW_{\bm{F}}(\bm{H})
for any
\bm{H}\in\bm{{\mathcal{A}}}
follows from the fact that the function
W(\bm{H})-QW(\bm{H})
is nonnegative,
C^{1}
smooth and attains its minimum value of 0 at all
\bm{F}\in\bm{{\mathcal{A}}}
.
Now, the integration by parts in the second term on the right-hand side in the inequality in (
2.21
) implies

\int_{\Omega}C(\nabla\bm{v})d\bm{x}\geq\int_{\Omega}C(\nabla\bm{u})d\bm{x}
. It follows that

\int_{\Omega}QW(\nabla\bm{v})d\bm{x}\geq\int_{\Omega}QW(\nabla\bm{u})d\bm{x}.

∎

Remark 2.6
.

An important observation was made by Sivaloganathan and Spector in
[
46
]
that
C(\bm{H})

does not need to be convex for inequality in (
2.21
) to hold. In fact, as the authors show, it is only
required that
C^{**}(\nabla\bm{u}(\bm{x}))=C(\nabla\bm{u}(\bm{x}))
for all
\bm{x}\in\Omega
,
i.e.
C(\bm{F})
agrees with its convex hull at all values of
\nabla\bm{u}
.

3
Global minimizers

In this section we temporarily switch to a more general
setting. For instance, we assume that the “deformation”
\bm{y}
is a vector field

\bm{y}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
. In fact, the analysis presented below does not depend on either
dimension of
\bm{x}
space or the dimension of
\bm{y}
space. It also does not depend on the specific form of
W(\bm{F})
.

Consider the problem of attainment in the definition of the quasiconvex
envelope

QW(\bm{F})=\inf_{\bm{y}\in\bm{F}\bm{x}+W_{0}^{1,\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{\Omega}W(\nabla\bm{y})d\bm{x},

(3.1)

where
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a star-shaped Lipschitz domain. Below we show that Lipschitz equilibrium configurations for (
3.1
) must be global minimizers, provided they satisfy the necessary conditions for metastability and are sufficiently regular near
\partial\Omega
.

Recall first the well-known necessary conditions for Lipschitz strong
local minima of variational functionals.

The first classical necessary condition is the
Euler-Lagrange equation

\nabla\cdot\bm{P}(\nabla\bm{y})=\bm{0},\qquad\bm{P}(\bm{F})=W_{\bm{F}}(\bm{F}),

(3.2)

which even weak local minimizers have to satisfy. The second, is
the Noether equation

\nabla\cdot\bm{P}^{*}(\nabla\bm{y})=\bm{0},\qquad\bm{P}^{*}(\bm{F})=W(\bm{F})\bm{I}_{d}-\bm{F}^{t}\bm{P}(\bm{F}).

(3.3)

We recall that if

\bm{y}(\bm{x})
is of class
C^{2}
then (
3.3
) is a consequence
of (
3.2
), while there are Lipschitz
configurations satisfying (
3.2
), but not (
3.3
). For instance, the Maxwell relation, which is the last equation in (
2.17
), is a consequence of (
3.3
), but not of (
3.2
).

Definition 3.1
.

A configuration
\bm{y}\in W^{1,\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})
is called stationary
if it satisfies both (
3.2
) and (
3.3
).

The second classical necessary condition is quasiconvexity
\nabla\bm{y}\in\bm{{\mathcal{A}}}
for a.e.
\bm{x}\in\Omega
, where
\bm{{\mathcal{A}}}
is defined in (
2.3
). It was shown in
[
20
]
that for configurations, whose only singularities are smooth phase boundaries the combination of (
3.2
) and quasiconvexity implies stationarity. In general, however, an example in
[
34
]
shows that there could be weak
local minimizers for strictly quasiconvex energies that are not strong local minimizers; most probably, the configuration in
[
34
]
fails stationarity.

It turns out that in the case affine boundary conditions in a star-shaped domain, the
two necessary conditions formulated above are sufficient for
\bm{y}(\bm{x})
to be a global
minimizer, provided we assume its regularity near the boundary of the domain.

Theorem
3.2
.

Let
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
be a star-shaped domain with a Lipschitz boundary. Assume that

(i)

\bm{y}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
is Lipschitz continuous,

(ii)

\bm{y}
solves (
3.2
) and (
3.3
) in the sense of distributions,

(iii)

\nabla\bm{y}(\bm{x})
is locally stable for a.e.
\bm{x}\in\Omega
,

(iv)

\bm{y}(\bm{x})=\bm{F}\bm{x}
for all
\bm{x}\in\partial\Omega
,

(v)

There exists
\epsilon>0
, such that
\bm{y}\in C^{1}(\overline{\Omega_{\epsilon}};\mathbb{R}^{m})
,
where
\Omega_{\epsilon}=\{\bm{x}\in\Omega:{\rm dist}(\bm{x},\partial\Omega)<\epsilon\}
.

Then
\bm{y}(\bm{x})
is the global minimizer in (
3.1
), i.e.

\int_{\Omega}W(\nabla\bm{y})d\bm{x}=|\Omega|QW(\bm{F}).

(3.4)

Proof.

While each ingredient of the proof presented below
was in one way or another already present in the uniqueness proof of Knops and Stuart

[
30
]
, our theorem is new, since it does not assume that the energy
density
W(\bm{F})
is quasiconvex.
The idea is to express
the energy of an equilibrium configuration as a boundary integral using
a far reaching generalization of a formula in
[
30
]
which,
by itself, can be viewed as a nontrivial nonlinear generalization of the
classical Clapeyron theorem
5
5

5

The classical Clapeyron theorem in linear
elasticity states that the elastic energy

E[\bm{u}]=(1/2)\int_{\Omega}\langle\mathsf{C}(\bm{x})e(\bm{u}),e(\bm{u})\rangle d\bm{x}
of an equilibrium configuration
can be computed by the formula

E[\bm{u}]=(1/2)\int_{\partial\Omega}\bm{\sigma}\bm{n}\cdot\bm{u}dS
, where
\bm{\sigma}=\mathsf{C}(\bm{x})e(\bm{u})

is the stress tensor,
[
16
]
.

Lemma
3.3
.

Suppose
\bm{y}(\bm{x})
is a Lipschitz stationary equilibrium in a Lipschitz domain

\Omega
. Then

E[\bm{y}]=\int_{\Omega}W(\nabla\bm{y})=\displaystyle\frac{1}{d}\int_{\partial\Omega}\{\bm{P}\bm{n}\cdot\bm{y}+\bm{P}^{*}\bm{n}\cdot\bm{x}\}d\bm{x},

(3.5)

where
\bm{P}\bm{n}
and
\bm{P}^{*}\bm{n}
can be regarded as trace
functionals, since they act on Lipschitz functions
\bm{y}(\bm{x})
and
\bm{x}
, respectively.

Proof.

\int_{\partial\Omega}\bm{P}^{*}\bm{n}\cdot\bm{x}dS=\int_{\Omega}\langle\bm{P}^{*},\nabla\bm{x}\rangle d\bm{x}=\int_{\Omega}\mathrm{Tr}\,(\bm{P}^{*})d\bm{x}=d\int_{\Omega}W(\nabla\bm{y})d\bm{x}-\int_{\Omega}\langle\bm{P},\nabla\bm{y}\rangle d\bm{x}.

Equation (
3.5
) follows from

\int_{\Omega}\langle\bm{P},\nabla\bm{y}\rangle d\bm{x}=\int_{\partial\Omega}\bm{P}\bm{n}\cdot\bm{y}dS.

∎

We now apply formula (
3.5
) for the energy of
\bm{y}(\bm{x})
. Using
the boundary condition (iv) and the assumption (v) we have at all
\bm{x}\in\partial\Omega

\nabla\bm{y}=\bm{F}+\bm{a}\otimes\bm{n},\qquad\bm{a}=\displaystyle\frac{\partial\bm{y}}{\partial\bm{n}}-\bm{F}\bm{n}.

We therefore compute

dE[\bm{y}]=\int_{\partial\Omega}(W(\bm{F}+\bm{a}\otimes\bm{n})-\bm{P}(\bm{F}+\bm{a}\otimes\bm{n})\bm{n}\cdot\bm{a})(\bm{n}\cdot\bm{x})dS

next, we use the local stability condition (iii) and appeal to
[
20
, Lemma 4.2]
.
We quote the part of the lemma we need for the sake of completeness.

Lemma
3.4
.

Let
V(\bm{F})
be a rank-one convex function such that
V(\bm{F})\leq W(\bm{F})
. Let

{\mathcal{A}}_{V}=\{\bm{F}\in{\mathcal{O}}:W(\bm{F})=V(\bm{F})\},

where
{\mathcal{O}}
is an open subset of
\mathbb{M}
on which
W(\bm{F})
is of class

C^{1}
. Then for every
\bm{F}\in{\mathcal{A}}_{V}
,
\bm{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, and
\bm{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

V(\bm{F}+\bm{u}\otimes\bm{v})\geq W(\bm{F})+\bm{P}(\bm{F})\bm{v}\cdot\bm{u}.

(3.6)

We now apply Lemma
3.4
by choosing
V(\bm{F})
to be
QW(\bm{F})
. By
assumption (iii) for each
\bm{x}\in\partial\Omega
the field

\nabla\bm{y}(\bm{x})=\bm{F}+\bm{a}\otimes\bm{n}
is in
{\mathcal{A}}_{V}
. Choosing

\bm{u}\otimes\bm{v}=-\bm{a}\otimes\bm{n}
, inequality (
3.6
) becomes

QW(\bm{F})\geq W(\bm{F}+\bm{a}\otimes\bm{n})-\bm{P}(\bm{F}+\bm{a}\otimes\bm{n})\bm{n}\cdot\bm{a}.

(3.7)

Finally, we use the assumption of
\Omega
being star-shaped. If we choose the
origin at the star point, then the function
\bm{n}(\bm{x})\cdot\bm{x}
is always
non-negative at all points on
\partial\Omega
. Therefore, inequality (
3.7
)
implies

dE[\bm{y}]\leq QW(\bm{F})\int_{\partial\Omega}(\bm{n}\cdot\bm{x})dS=d|\Omega|QW(\bm{F}).

Since
|\Omega|QW(\bm{F})
is the minimal value of
E[\bm{y}]
we conclude that

E[\bm{y}]=QW(\bm{F})
, and
\bm{y}(\bm{x})
is the global minimizer.
∎

4
Multiplicity of global minimizers

The absence of strong local minimizers in hard device with
affine boundary conditions demonstrated in the previous section leaves
the question of the characterization of all global minimizers in

QW_{0}(\bm{H}_{0})=\inf_{\bm{u}|_{\partial\Omega}=\bm{H}_{0}\bm{x}}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{\Omega}W_{0}(\nabla\bm{u})d\bm{x},

(4.1)

where
W_{0}(\bm{H})
is defined in (
1.3
).
We remark that the construction of the periodic laminate in
Section
2
provides us with a minimizing sequence in (
4.1
) in

arbitrary domains
. In this section we raise the question of
attainability of the minimum in (
4.1
). In this regard, laminates can no
longer be used as the boundary conditions

\bm{u}(\bm{x})=\bm{H}_{0}\bm{x},\quad\bm{x}\in\partial\Omega.

(4.2)

are inconsistent with
\nabla\bm{u}
taking exactly two specific values almost everywhere
in
\Omega
. We will therefore attempt to find energy minimizers using the
sufficiency conditions from Theorem
3.2
. Accordingly, we only need
to find solutions
\bm{u}(\bm{x})
of (
3.2
) that satisfies (
4.2
) while
respecting the stability condition

\nabla\cdot\bm{u}(\bm{x})\not\in(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})\mbox{ for a.e. }\bm{x}\in\Omega.

(4.3)

Let
\bm{\phi}(\bm{x})=\bm{u}(\bm{x})-\bm{H}_{0}\bm{x}
. Then (
3.2
) can be written in
terms of
\bm{\phi}
as follows

\mu\Delta\bm{\phi}+\nabla\Phi^{\prime}(\nabla\cdot\bm{u})=\mu\nabla(\nabla\cdot\bm{\phi}),\quad\bm{x}\in\Omega.

(4.4)

Integrating the dot product of
\bm{\phi}(\bm{x})
and (
4.4
) by parts
we obtain

\int_{\Omega}\left\{\mu(|\nabla\cdot\bm{\phi}|^{2}-|\nabla\bm{\phi}|^{2})-\Phi^{\prime}(\nabla\cdot\bm{u})\nabla\cdot\bm{\phi}\right\}d\bm{x}=0.

Now we use the algebraic identity

|\nabla\cdot\bm{\phi}|^{2}-|\nabla\bm{\phi}|^{2}=2J_{2}(\nabla\bm{\phi})-\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}|\nabla\bm{\phi}-(\nabla\bm{\phi})^{t}|^{2}

and the fact that
J_{2}(\nabla\bm{\phi})
given by (
2.6
) is a null-Lagrangian to
obtain

\int_{\Omega}\left\{\frac{\mu}{2}|\nabla\bm{\phi}-(\nabla\bm{\phi})^{t}|^{2}+\Phi^{\prime}(\nabla\cdot\bm{u})\nabla\cdot\bm{\phi}\right\}d\bm{x}=0.

(4.5)

Now let us show that (
4.3
) implies that

\int_{\Omega}\Phi^{\prime}(\nabla\cdot\bm{u})\nabla\cdot\bm{\phi}(\bm{x})d\bm{x}\geq 0.

(4.6)

Indeed, from the geometric interpretation of
\theta_{1}
and
\theta_{2}

(as the points of common tangency

) we can write

\Phi(\nabla\cdot\bm{u}(\bm{x}))=\Phi^{**}(\nabla\cdot\bm{u}(\bm{x}))

(4.7)

for a.e.
\bm{x}\in\Omega
.
For a convex function
\Phi^{**}(\theta)
the tangent line to the graph
of that function lies always below the graph which means that

\Phi^{**}(\eta)\geq\Phi^{\prime}(\theta)(\eta-\theta)+\Phi(\theta)

for any
\eta\in\mathbb{R}
and any
\theta\not\in(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})

Substituting
\eta=\mathrm{Tr}\,\bm{H}_{0}

\Phi^{\prime}(\nabla\cdot\bm{u}(\bm{x}))\nabla\cdot\bm{\phi}(\bm{x})\geq\Phi(\nabla\cdot\bm{u}(\bm{x}))-\Phi^{**}(\mathrm{Tr}\,\bm{H}_{0})=\Phi^{**}(\nabla\cdot\bm{u}(\bm{x}))-\Phi^{**}(\mathrm{Tr}\,\bm{H}_{0})

\bm{x}\in\Omega
. Now (
4.6
) follows from Jensen’s
inequality for a convex function
\Phi^{**}(\theta)
and the fact that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\int_{\Omega}\nabla\cdot\bm{u}(\bm{x})d\bm{x}=\mathrm{Tr}\,\bm{H}_{0}.

(4.8)

Now, inequality (
4.6
) together with (

) imply
that
\nabla\bm{\phi}=(\nabla\bm{\phi})^{t}

0=\nabla\cdot(\nabla\bm{\phi}-(\nabla\bm{\phi})^{t})=\Delta\bm{\phi}-\nabla(\nabla\cdot\bm{\phi}).

(4.9)

Thus, (
4.4
) implies that
\nabla\Phi^{\prime}(\nabla\cdot\bm{u})=0

\Phi^{\prime}(\nabla\cdot\bm{u})=P_{0}.

) then implies that either

\Omega

\nabla\cdot\bm{u}=\mathrm{Tr}\,\bm{H}_{0}

\nabla\cdot\bm{\phi}=0

according to (

),
\bm{\phi}
solves

If
\mathrm{Tr}\,\bm{H}_{0}\in(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})

\omega=\frac{|A|}{|\Omega|}=\frac{\mathrm{Tr}\,\bm{H}_{0}-\theta_{2}}{\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}}

If, in addition,
\Omega

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\Delta h=(\theta_{1}-\theta_{2})(\chi_{A}(\bm{x})-\omega),\quad\bm{x}\in\Omega\\[4.30554pt]

5
Conclusions

In this paper we considered a classical problem of nonlinear elasticity for a material undergoing phase transition. Mathematically this physical problem reduces to a non-convex vectorial problem of the calculus of variations.

In this framework we presented
a simple, yet non-trivial example of an energy density (of a material) for
which
the absence of metastability in a hard device coexists with a wild nonuniqueness of global minimizers.

Metastability in elastostatics, understood as the existence of strong local
minimizers that are not global, is ubiquitous as hysteresis is a typical phenomenon accompanying martensitic phase transitions. While for the Neumann boundary conditions the existence of such local minimizers is indeed common, as its existence can be linked to the generic incompatibility of the energy wells, here we showed that for the Dirichlet boundary conditions the incompatibility of the wells
does not necessarily cause
“metastability”.
More specifically, we have presented an example of
the energy density of a hyperelastic material with non-rank-one convex,
double-well energy and non-rank one connected energy wells, for which we could prove the lack of strong local
minimizers which are not global on any domain and for any Dirichlet boundary conditions. The analytical transparency of our arguments was due to the utmost simplicity of the chosen energy density which is geometrically linear and isotropic.

For the same material model we could fully characterize the necessary and
sufficient conditions defining global energy minima in hard device loading. The obtained conditions allowed us to reveal the multiplicity of global minimizers with the associated nonuniqueness unrelated to either objectivity or crystallographic symmetry. As an important element of this analysis we used a novel way of expressing the energy of equilibrium configurations as boundary integrals which can be viewed as a nontrivial nonlinear generalization of the classical Clapeyron theorem.

While we showed that the relaxation (quasi-convexification) of the energy in our model can be achieved by simple lamination or coated sphere construction, these optimal microstructures are hardly physical as the associated surface area is infinite. In real physical situations finite surface energy plays the role of the selection mechanism among otherwise energetically equivalent configurations

[
29
,
33
,
11
]
, ruling out constructions with infinite surface area. Therefore, of particular interest in physical applications are global minimizers with finite surface area, and we explicitly computed a one-parameter
family of non-affine energy minimizing configurations for the case of a
finite domain with smooth boundary. An interesting aspect of the obtained solution is that, despite the absence of metastability and the associated constitutive hysteresis, it shows that the direct and the reverse transformation may follow different morphological paths while traversing energetically equivalent configurations. The possibility of such a morphological hysteresis may be found advantageous in applications if the ways of manipulating the microstructure, beyond the scale and range of classical continuum elasticity, are employed.
