The Isoperimetric Problem In Randers Planes
Arti Sahu, Ranadip Gangopadhyay, Hemangi Madhusudan Shah, Bankteshwar Tiwari

Abstract
In this paper, the isoperimetric problem in Randers planes, ( ℝ 2 , F = α + β ) (\mathbb{R}^{2},F=\alpha+\beta) , which are slight deformation of the Euclidean plane ( ℝ 2 , α ) (\mathbb{R}^{2},\alpha) by suitable one forms β \beta , have been studied. We prove that the circles centred at the origin achieves the local maximum area of the isoperimetric problem with respect to well known volume forms in Finsler geometry.



keywords:
Isoperimetric problem, Randers Planes, Minkowski Planes, Calculus of variations.

1
Introduction

The isoperimetric problem has a history of more than two thousand years. The original isoperimetric problem can be posed as: to find a simple closed curve of given perimeter which encloses the maximum area. Mathematically, the isoperimetric problem is to find a simple closed curve for which the area achieves the equality in the isoperimetric inequality
L^{2}\geq 4\pi A
.

The ancient greeks knew that a circle has greater area than any polygon with the same perimeter
[
2
]
. The famous Roman poet Virgil mentioned about this problem in his epic ‘Aeneid’. Although the statement is really very simple and the possible answer was known to the mankind, but the first step to obtain the mathematical proof was due to Swiss geometer J. Steiner in
1839
. He proved that circle is the only possible solution in plane geometry. The first complete proof was given by Weierstrass using the techniques of calculus of variations. After that many different proofs were published by several mathematicians including Hurwitz, Schimdt
[
9
,
13
,
15
]
. For constant curvature spaces the isoperimetric inequality is given by
L^{2}\geq 4\pi A-kA^{2},

where
k
is the curvature of the space. The study of the isoperimetric inequality has been done extensively in e.g.
[
1
,
7
,
11
,
17
]
. For the Finsler case Zhou studied the isoperimetric problem in 2-dimensional spherically symmetric spaces with zero curvature
[
18
]
with respect to the Busemann-Hausdorff volume form. Zhan and Zhou studied this problem with respect to the Holmes-Thompson volume form
[
19
]
. More precisely, they studied the isoperimetric problem for the following Berwald metric,

F(x,y)=\frac{(\sqrt{|y|^{2}-(|x|^{2}|y|^{2})-\langle x,y\rangle^{2}}+\langle x,y\rangle)^{2}}{(1-|x|^{2})(\sqrt{|y|^{2}-(|x|^{2}|y|^{2})-\langle x,y\rangle^{2})}}

and showed that the circles
\gamma_{0}(t)=(a\cos t,a\sin t)
are the local solutions of the isoperimetric problem in
2
-dimensional spherically symmetric Finsler metrics with zero flag curvature. Li and Mo
[
10
]
studied the isoperimetric problem for the two dimensional Funk space, which is a spherically symmetric metric with constant flag curvature
-1
and is given by,

F(x,y)=\frac{(\sqrt{|y|^{2}-(|x|^{2}|y|^{2})-\langle x,y\rangle^{2}}+\langle x,y\rangle)}{2(1-|x|^{2})}.

It should be noted that the Funk metric,
F
defined above, is an example of Randers metric. In this paper, we consider the Randers metric, which is a class of simplest non-Riemannian Finsler metric introduced by G. Randers, and is defined by
F=\alpha+\beta
, where
\alpha
is a Riemannian metric and
\beta
is a one-form with
b:=\|\beta\|_{\alpha}<1
,
[
12
]
.

In order to simplify the calculations we choose
\beta=b(\cos\theta(x^{1},x^{2})dx^{1}+\sin\theta(x^{1},x^{2})dx^{2})
, where
\theta
is a function of
x=(x^{1},x^{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
0<b<1
. We show that under certain conditions on
\theta
, circles centred at the origin are solutions of the isoperimetric problem locally with respect to different known volume forms on the Randers manifolds. More precisely, we prove the following:

Theorem 1.1

Let
(\mathbb{R}^{2},F)
, be a Randers plane, where

F=\sqrt{(dx^{1})^{2}+(dx^{2})^{2}}+b(\cos\theta(x^{1},x^{2})dx^{1}+\sin\theta(x^{1},x^{2})dx^{2}).

If
\theta=c
, or,
t+c
, for some constant
c
on the circles of the form
\gamma_{0}=(a\cos t,a\sin t)
, then
\gamma_{0}
is a solution of the isoperimetric problem, with respect to each of the volume forms: the Busemann-Hausdorff, the Holmes-Thompson, the Maximum, and the Minimum.

If we choose
\theta=0
, then
F=\sqrt{(dx^{1})^{2}+(dx^{2})^{2}}+bdx^{1}
with
0<b<1
. In this case the Finsler metric
F
defined on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
gives a Minkowski space as
F
is independent of the point on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and therefore,
(\mathbb{R}^{2},F)
has zero flag curvature. For this Randers-Minkowski metric, we obtain:

Corollary 1.1

Let
(\mathbb{R}^{2},F)
be a Randers plane, where
F=\sqrt{(dx^{1})^{2}+(dx^{2})^{2}}+bdx^{1}
,
0<b<1
. Then
\gamma_{0}(t)=(a\cos t,a\sin t)
is a solution of the Minkowski isoperimetric problem.

Considering
\theta\equiv constant
in Theorem
1.1
, we obtain:

Corollary 1.2

Consider the Finsler metric
F=\sqrt{(dx^{1})^{2}+(dx^{2})^{2}}+d\tau
, where
\tau
is a smooth real valued function on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with
\|d\tau\|
constant and
\|d\tau\|<1
(
\|.\|
denotes the Euclidean norm on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
). Then
\gamma_{0}(t)=(a\cos t,a\sin t)
is a solution of the isoperimetric problem.

Remark 1.1

As per Busemann
[
4
]
, circle may not be a solution of the isoperimetric problem in Minkowski plane. However, for the Minkowski plane discussed above, indeed the circle
\gamma_{0}
turns out to be a solution of the isoperimetric problem.

Preliminaries

Let
M
be an
n
-dimensional smooth manifold.
T_{x}M
denotes the tangent space of
M

at
x
. The tangent bundle of
M
is the disjoint union of tangent spaces,
TM:=\sqcup_{x\in M}T_{x}M
. We denote the elements of
TM
by
(x,y)
where
y\in T_{x}M
and
TM_{0}:=TM\setminus\left\{0\right\}
, the slit tangent bundle of
M
.

Definition 2.1

[
6
]
A Finsler metric on
M
is a function
F:TM\to[0,\infty)
satisfying the following conditions:

(i)

F
is smooth on
TM_{0}
,

(ii)

F
is positively 1-homogeneous on the fibers of the tangent bundle
TM
,

(iii)

The Hessian of
\frac{F^{2}}{2}
with element
g_{ij}=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}F^{2}}{\partial y^{i}\partial y^{j}}
is positive definite on
TM_{0}
.

The pair
(M,F)
is called a Finsler space and
g_{ij}
is called the fundamental tensor.

It is known that there is a canonical volume form in a Riemannian manifold
(M^{n},\alpha)
,
\alpha=\sqrt{a_{ij}dx^{i}dx^{j}}
, given by
dV=\sqrt{\det(a_{ij})}dx
, whereas in Finsler manifold there are several volume forms some of them are defined as follows:

Definition 2.2

For an
n
-dimensional Finsler manifold
(M^{n},F)
, the Busemann-Hausdorff volume form is defined as
dV_{BH}=\sigma_{BH}(x)dx
, where

\sigma_{BH}(x)=\frac{vol(B^{n}(1))}{vol\left\{(y^{i})\in T_{x}M:F(x,y)<1\right\}}.

(1)

The Holmes-Thompson volume form is defined as
dV_{HT}=\sigma_{HT}(x)\penalty\ dx
, where

\sigma_{HT}(x)=\frac{1}{vol(B^{n}(1))}\int_{F(x,y)<1}\det(g_{ij}(x,y))dy.

(2)

Here,
B^{n}(1)
is the Euclidean unit ball in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
vol
is the Euclidean volume.

Definition 2.3

The maximum volume form of a Finsler metric
F
with fundamental metric tensor
g_{ij}
is defined as

dV_{\max}=\sigma_{\max}(x)dx,

(3)

where
\sigma_{\max}(x)=\max\limits_{y\in I_{x}}\sqrt{\det(g_{ij}(x,y))}
and,
I_{x}=\left\{y\in T_{x}M|F(x,y)=1\right\}
, is the indicatrix at the point
x
of the Finsler manifold.

The minimum volume form of a Finsler metric
F
with the fundamental metric tensor
g_{ij}
is defined as

dV_{\min}=\sigma_{\min}(x)dx,

(4)

where
\sigma_{\min}(x)=\min\limits_{y\in I_{x}}\sqrt{\det(g_{ij}(x,y))}
.

Let

\frac{\int\limits_{0}^{\pi}\sin^{n-2}(t)T(b\cos(t))dt}{\int\limits_{0}^{\pi}\sin^{n-2}(t)dt},&\mbox{if }dV=dV_{HT},\end{cases}

(5)

and

[

(7)

Definition 2.4

A continuous function
\gamma:\left[t_{0},t_{1}\right]\to M^{2}
is called an admissible curve on
M
, if there exists a partition
P:=\left\{t_{0}=a_{0}<a_{1}<...<a_{k}=t_{1}\right\}
of the interval
[t_{0},t_{1}]
such that the curve
\alpha
has continuous derivative in each subinterval
\left[a_{i},a_{i+1}\right]
, for all
i=0,1,...,k-1

Consider the set of all admissible curves joining two fixed points for which the definite integral
L=\int\limits_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}g(x^{1},x^{2},\dot{x}^{1},\dot{x}^{2})dt
takes a constant value
l
. Then the isoperimetric problem is about finding an admissible curve
\gamma_{0}(t)=(x^{1}(t),x^{2}(t))
for which the integral
A=\int\limits_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}f(x^{1},x^{2},\dot{x}^{1},\dot{x}^{2})dt
has its
extremum
.

To solve the isoperimetric problem we first consider the Lagrange functional:

J(x)=A+\lambda L=\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}h(x^{1},x^{2},\dot{x}^{1},\dot{x}^{2},\lambda)dt,

(10)

where,

h=f+\lambda g.

(11)

Definition 2.5

An admissible curve
\gamma_{0}
is said be an isoperimetric extremal, if it satisfies the following Euler-Lagrange equations:

\frac{\partial h}{\partial x^{i}}-\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{\partial h}{\partial\dot{x}^{i}}\right)=0,\penalty\ i=1,2.

(12)

And an admissible curve
\gamma_{0}
satisfying the Euler-Lagrange equations is said to be normal if
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
are non-zero functions, where
P_{i}=g_{x^{i}}-\frac{d}{dt}g_{\dot{x}^{i}}
, for
i=1,2
.

The
increment
of the functional
J
is defined by

\bigtriangleup J=\int\limits_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}E(x^{1},x^{2},\dot{x}^{1},\dot{x}^{2},u^{1},u^{2})dt,

(13)

where

E(x^{1},x^{2},\dot{x}^{1},\dot{x}^{2},u^{1},u^{2})=h(x^{1},x^{2},u^{1},u^{2})-h(x^{1},x^{2},\dot{x}^{1},\dot{x}^{2})-\sum\limits_{j=1}^{2}(u^{j}-\dot{x}^{j})\frac{\partial h(x^{1},x^{2},\dot{x}^{1},\dot{x}^{2})}{\partial\dot{x}^{j}}.

(14)

The function

is called the

of
J
.

The

of

\gamma_{0}
is defined as

J^{\prime\prime}(\gamma_{0},y)=\int\limits_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}2\omega(t,y(t),\dot{y}(t))dt,

(15)

where,
\omega=\sum\limits_{i,j=1}^{2}h_{x^{i}x^{j}}y^{i}y^{j}+2h_{x^{i}\dot{x}^{j}}y^{i}\dot{y}^{j}+h_{\dot{x}^{i}\dot{x}^{j}}\dot{y}^{i}\dot{y}^{j}
.

Definition 2.6

A point

on
t_{0}<t\leq t_{1}
is said to be a conjugate point to

on
\gamma_{0}
if, there exist a solution

of the Jacobi equations

\omega_{y^{i}}-\frac{d\omega_{\dot{y}^{i}}}{dt}=0,\quad i=1,2,

such that
\int\limits_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\left((g)_{x^{i}}y^{i}+(g)_{\dot{x}^{i}}\dot{y}^{i}\right)dt=0

A sufficiency theorem for a strong maximum of the isoperimetric problem proved by Hestenes in
