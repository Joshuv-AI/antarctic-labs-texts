Constrained multibody kinematics and dynamics in absolute coordinates: a discussion of three approaches to representing rigid body rotation
Alexandra Kissel, Jay Taves, Dan Negrut

Abstract
We compare three approaches to posing the index 3 set of differential algebraic equations (DAEs) associated with the constrained multibody dynamics problem formulated in absolute coordinates. The first approach works directly with the orientation matrix and therefore eschews the need for generalized coordinates used to produce the orientation matrix 𝐀 {\bf{A}} . The approach is informed by the fact that rotation matrices belong to the SO(3) Lie matrix group. The second approach employs Euler parameters, while the third uses Euler angles. In all cases, the index 3 DAE problem is solved via a first order implicit numerical integrator. We note a roughly twofold speedup of rA over r ϵ \bm{\epsilon} , and a 1.2 – 1.3 times speedup of r ϵ \bm{\epsilon} over rp . The tests were carried out in conjunction with four 3D mechanisms. The improvements in simulation speed of the rA approach are traced back to a simpler form of the equations of motion and more concise Jacobians that enter the numerical solution. The contributions made herein are twofold. First, we provide first order variations of all the quantities that enter the rA formulation when used in the context of implicit integration; i.e., sensitivity of the kinematic constraints for all lower pair joints, as well as the sensitivity of the constraint reaction forces. Second, to the best of our knowledge, there is no other contribution that compares head to head the solution efficiency of rA , rp , and r ϵ \bm{\epsilon} in the context of the multibody dynamics problem posed in absolute coordinates.



1
Introduction

In rigid multibody dynamics simulation using absolute coordinates, several approaches can be employed to track the attitude of a body in 3D motion. This paper is concerned with comparing the performance (accuracy and efficiency) of three approaches. The first, called herein
rA
, solves the kinematics and dynamics problems by computing the orientation matrix
{{\bf{A}}}_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3\times 3}
of each body
i
in the mechanical system directly. The other two approaches use Euler parameters (
rp
) and Euler angles (
r
\bm{\epsilon}
) to express the orientation matrix
{{\bf{A}}}_{i}
. To the best of our knowledge, the results of such a study has not been reported in the literature.

While
rA
,
rp
, and
r
\bm{\epsilon}
use different paths to produce the orientation matrix
{\bf{A}}
, all three formulations share the same high-level process for generating a numerical solution. Specifically, for dynamics analysis, the numerical solution is produced using a direct index 3 approach that uses a first order implicit integrator to discretize the Newton-Euler constrained equations of motion
[
1
,
2
]
. Herein, we compare the simulation times and the number of Newton-Raphson iterations taken until convergence of
rA
,
rp
, and
r
\bm{\epsilon}
in both kinematics and dynamics. We also confirm the first order accuracy of the three dynamics solvers.

2.1
The
rA
Formulation

In the
rA
formulation, the multibody system kinematics and dynamics problems are solved at each time step by directly computing the Cartesian position
{{\bf{r}}}_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
and orientation matrix
{{\bf{A}}}_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3\times 3}
of each body
i
in the mechanical system. Both
{{\bf{r}}}_{i}
and
{{\bf{A}}}_{i}
are relative to a global, fixed reference frame. The salient point is that the approach does not rely on Euler angles, Euler parameters, or similar generalized coordinates used to express the orientation matrix
{{\bf{A}}}_{i}
in terms thereof. Instead, the orientation matrix is generated directly by the solution process and computed such that
{{\bf{A}}}_{i}^{T}{{\bf{A}}}_{i}={{\bf{I}}}_{3}
; i.e.,
{{\bf{A}}}_{i}\in SO(3)
. The fact that the orientation matrix
{\bf{A}}
belongs to the special orthogonal Lie matrix group
SO(3)

[
4
,
5
]
has rich implications. However, in this contribution the connection to the Lie group and its algebra will be almost entirely bypassed. For how this connection is exploited in constrained multibody dynamics, the interested reader is referred to
[
6
,
7
,
8
,
9
,
10
]
.

Assume a local reference frame (L-RF, also called body reference frame) is attached to a rigid body that changes its orientation in time. As spelled out in
[
11
]
, if at time
t_{0}
the rotation matrix for the L-RF is
{\bf{A}}(t_{0})
, then for any time
t>t_{0}
this matrix, and implicitly the attitude of the rigid body, can be obtained by rotating the L-RF from its pose at
t_{0}
by a certain angle
\chi(t)
about a unit axis
{\bar{{\bf{u}}}}(t)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
. Throughout, an “over-bar” is used to indicate that a vector is expressed in the L-RF. The subscript
i
was dropped for brevity.

The expression of the rotation matrix associated with the
({\bar{{\bf{u}}}},\chi)
rotation is given as
[
3
]

{{\bf{R}}}({\bar{{\bf{u}}}},\chi)=\cos\chi{{\bf{I}}}_{3}+(1-\cos\chi){\bar{{\bf{u}}}}{\bar{{\bf{u}}}}^{T}+\sin\chi({\tilde{{\bar{{\bf{u}}}}}})\;.

The tilde operator acts on a vector to produce the cross-product matrix; i.e.,
{\bf{a}}\times{\bf{b}}=\tilde{{\bf{a}}}{\bf{b}}
, see
[
3
]
. After simple manipulations,

{{\bf{R}}}({\bar{{\bf{u}}}},\chi)={{\bf{I}}}_{3}+\sin\chi{\tilde{{\bar{{\bf{u}}}}}}+(1-\cos\chi){\tilde{{\bar{{\bf{u}}}}}}{\tilde{{\bar{{\bf{u}}}}}}\;,

(5)

and therefore, based on Rodrigues’s formula
[
12
]
,

{{\bf{R}}}({\bar{{\bf{u}}}},\chi)=\exp({\tilde{{\bar{\bm{\chi}}}}})\;,

(6)

where the Euler rotation vector
{{\bar{\bm{\chi}}}}(t)\equiv\chi{\bar{{\bf{u}}}}
, and for a matrix
{\bf{C}}\in\mathbbm{R}^{n\times n}
, the matrix exponential is defined as

\exp({\bf{C}})\equiv{{\bf{I}}}_{n}+\frac{1}{1!}{{\bf{C}}}+\frac{1}{2!}{{\bf{C}}}^{2}+\frac{1}{3!}{{\bf{C}}}^{3}+\ldots\quad.

(7)

Although the series in Eq. (
7
) might be divergent, it always converges for skew symmetric matrices
{\bf{C}}
. Moreover, if the matrix is skew-symmetric, then
\exp({{\bf{C}}})\in SO(3)

[
4
]
. As such, by composing the two rotations – from the global reference to the orientation at
t_{0}
, and then from
t_{0}
to
t
, one has that

{\bf{A}}(t)={\bf{A}}(t_{0})\exp({\tilde{{\bar{\bm{\chi}}}}})\;.

(8)

The new orientation matrix
{\bf{A}}(t)
is orthonormal since it is the product of two orthonormal matrices. Equation (
8
) provides the means to evolve, incrementally, the orientation matrix in the kinematics analysis: the new matrix at time
t
is obtained from the rotation matrix
{\bf{A}}(t_{0})
at a previous time
t_{0}
multiplied by a second orientation matrix associated with the attitude change from
t_{0}
to
t
.

2.1.1
Carrying out the Kinematics Analysis

Assume the multibody system is subject to
nc=6nb
kinematic constraints: some scleronomic, some rheonomic. The collection of these kinematic constraints is denoted as

{{\Phi}}^{\bf{A}}({\bf{r}}_{1},\ldots,{\bf{r}}_{nb},{{\bf{A}}}_{1},\ldots,{{\bf{A}}}_{nb},t)={\bf 0}_{6nb}\;.

(9)

At time
t_{n}
, one in a sequence of time steps
0<t_{1}<t_{2}<\ldots<t_{end}
, the location and orientation of the bodies are computed by solving the nonlinear system of equations

{{\Phi}}^{\bf{A}}({{\bf{r}}}_{1,n},\ldots,{{\bf{r}}}_{nb,n},{{\bf{A}}}_{1,n},\ldots,{{\bf{A}}}_{nb,n},t_{n})={\bf 0}_{6nb}\;.

(10)

For convenience, the subscript
i
in
{{\bf{r}}}_{i,n}
and
{{\bf{A}}}_{i,n}
is dropped. With the position
{{\bf{r}}}_{n-1}
and pose
{{\bf{A}}}_{n-1}
known at time
t_{n-1}
, the immediate goal is to compute the new position
{{\bf{r}}}_{n}
and pose
{{\bf{A}}}_{n}
at
t_{n}=t_{n-1}+h
. To that end, take the initial guess of the pose to be

{{\bf{A}}}^{(0)}_{n}={{\bf{A}}}_{n-1}\exp(h\>{{\tilde{\bar{\bm{\omega}}}}}_{n-1})\;.

(11)

Subsequently, over a series of iterations

{{\bf{A}}}^{(k+1)}_{n}={{\bf{A}}}^{(k)}_{n}\>\exp({{\tilde{\bar{\bm{\theta}}}}}^{(k)})\;,\qquad k=1,2,\ldots\;,

(12)

where
{{{\bar{\bm{\theta}}}}}^{(k)}
is an Euler rotation vector that leads to a small rotation applied at each iteration in order to improve an imperfect pose
{{\bf{A}}}_{n}^{(k)}
. The process concludes at an iteration
K
for which
{\left|{\left|{{{\bar{\bm{\theta}}}}}^{(K)}\right|}\right|}\leq\epsilon_{R}
, where the positive threshold value
\epsilon_{R}
is chosen to be small enough.

Note that the body’s orientation matrix
{\bf{A}}
does not appear in any kinematic quantity of interest by itself. Rather, it always multiplies a vector
{\bar{{\bf{s}}}}
expressed in the local reference frame associated with the body, as in
{\bf{A}}\bar{{\bf{s}}}\in{\mathbbm{R}}^{3}
. Since a Newton step is employed to find

{\bf{s}}\equiv{\bf{A}}\bar{{\bf{s}}}
changes when
{\bf{A}}
changes slightly; i.e., from

to
{\bf{A}}\exp({{\tilde{\bar{\bm{\theta}}}}})
, where
{{{\bar{\bm{\theta}}}}}
is an Euler rotation vector associated with a small rotation.

Thus, using Eqs. (
6
) and (
7
) and limiting to linear terms, it turns out that a small rotation characterized by
{{{\bar{\bm{\theta}}}}}
leads to a first order variation in

of the form

\delta_{{{{\bar{\bm{\theta}}}}}}{\bf{s}}=\delta_{{{{\bar{\bm{\theta}}}}}}({\bf{A}}{\bar{{\bf{s}}}})\equiv{\bf{A}}\exp({{\tilde{\bar{\bm{\theta}}}}})\bar{{\bf{s}}}-{\bf{A}}\bar{{\bf{s}}}\approx-{\bf{A}}{\tilde{\bar{{\bf{s}}}}}\>{{\bar{\bm{\theta}}}}\equiv{\bar{\Pi}}({\bf{A}}{\bar{{\bf{s}}}})\>{{\bar{\bm{\theta}}}}\;.

In other words, the first order variation of
{\bf{A}}{\bar{{\bf{s}}}}
is obtained via the operator
{\bar{\Pi}}
applied to this quantity to yield

\delta_{{{{\bar{\bm{\theta}}}}}}{\bf{s}}={\bar{\Pi}}({\bf{A}}{\bar{{\bf{s}}}})\>{{\bar{\bm{\theta}}}}={\bar{\Pi}}({{{\bf{s}}}})\>{{\bar{\bm{\theta}}}}=-{\bf{A}}{\tilde{\bar{{\bf{s}}}}}\>{{\bar{\bm{\theta}}}}\;.

(13a)

Equation (

changes when the matrix
{\bf A}
is slightly perturbed, as done via the Euler rotation vector
{{{\bar{\bm{\theta}}}}}
. Note the similarity in the expression of the time derivative of

and its first order variation when expressed via the

\frac{\operatorname{d}\!{}}{\operatorname{d}\!{t}}({\bf{A}}{\bar{{\bf{s}}}})={\bar{\Pi}}({\bf{A}}{\bar{{\bf{s}}}})\>{\bar{\bm{\omega}}}\quad\leftrightarrow\quad\delta_{{{{\bar{\bm{\theta}}}}}}({\bf{A}}{\bar{{\bf{s}}}})={\bar{\Pi}}({\bf{A}}{\bar{{\bf{s}}}})\>{{\bar{\bm{\theta}}}}\;.

(13b)

It is also relevant to understand how the representation of a vector
{\bf s}
that is
fixed
in the global reference frame changes in the local reference frame associated with a rigid body when the attitude of the body is slightly perturbed, as done via the Euler rotation vector
{{{\bar{\bm{\theta}}}}}

\delta_{{{{\bar{\bm{\theta}}}}}}{\bar{{\bf{s}}}}=\delta_{{{{\bar{\bm{\theta}}}}}}({{\bf{A}}}^{T}{\bf{s}})\equiv[{{\bf{A}}}\exp({{\tilde{\bar{\bm{\theta}}}}})]^{T}{{\bf{s}}}-{{\bf{A}}}^{T}{{\bf{s}}}\approx{\widetilde{{{\bf{A}}}^{T}{{\bf{s}}}}}\>{{\bar{\bm{\theta}}}}\equiv{\bar{\Pi}}({{\bf{A}}}^{T}{\bf{s}})\>{{\bar{\bm{\theta}}}}={\tilde{\bar{{\bf{s}}}}}\>{{\bar{\bm{\theta}}}}\;.

In other words, the variation of

relative to
{{{\bar{\bm{\theta}}}}}
, is obtained as

\delta_{{{{\bar{\bm{\theta}}}}}}({{\bf{A}}}^{T}{\bf{s}})=\delta_{{{{\bar{\bm{\theta}}}}}}{\bar{{\bf{s}}}}={\bar{\Pi}}({{\bf{A}}}^{T}{\bf{s}})\>{{\bar{\bm{\theta}}}}={\bar{\Pi}}(\bar{{\bf{s}}})\>{{\bar{\bm{\theta}}}}={\widetilde{{{\bf{A}}}^{T}{{\bf{s}}}}}\>{{\bar{\bm{\theta}}}}\;.

(13c)

Note the similarity in the expression of the time derivative of
{\bf{A}}^{T}{{{\bf{s}}}}
and its first order variation:

\frac{\operatorname{d}\!{}}{\operatorname{d}\!{t}}\left({\bf{A}}^{T}{{{\bf{s}}}}\right)={\bar{\Pi}}({\bf{A}}^{T}{{{\bf{s}}}})\>{\bar{\bm{\omega}}}\;\leftrightarrow\;\delta_{{{{\bar{\bm{\theta}}}}}}({\bf{A}}^{T}{{{\bf{s}}}})={\bar{\Pi}}({\bf{A}}^{T}{{{\bf{s}}}})\>{{\bar{\bm{\theta}}}}\;.

One can conclude that the action of the operator

on
{\bf{A}}{\bar{{\bf{s}}}}
or

In the light of this discussion, assume that corrections in the kinematics analysis are carried out like

{{\bf r}}^{(k+1)}_{i,n}={{\bf r}}^{(k)}_{i,n}+{\bm{\delta}}^{(k)}_{i,r}\quad,\qquad{\bar{\bm{\theta}}}^{(k+1)}_{i,n}={\bar{\bm{\theta}}}^{(k)}_{i,n}+{\bm{\delta}}^{(k)}_{i,A}\;.

(14a)

Then, this perturbation in the position and pose of the bodies will lead to a change (variation) in the value of the kinematic constraints that is approximated as

{\bm{\delta}}^{(k)}_{A}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbbm{R}^{6nb}\;.

(14c)

The correction/perturbation

(14d)

The notation

is used since the first order variation of
{{\Phi}}^{\bf{A}}

{\bm{\delta}}^{(k)}_{r}
and
{\bm{\delta}}^{(k)}_{A}

\displaystyle\frac{\mbox{d}{{{\Phi}}^{\bf{A}}}({\bf r},{{\bf{A}}},t)}{\mbox{d}t}

\displaystyle={{\Phi}}^{\bf{A}}_{,{{\bf{r}}}}{\dot{\bf r}}+{\bar{\Pi}}({\Phi}^{\bf{A}}){\bar{\bm{\omega}}}+{{\Phi}}^{\bf{A}}_{t}

\displaystyle=\left[{{{\Phi}}^{\bf{A}}_{,{{\bf{r}}}}\;\;{\bar{\Pi}}({\Phi}^{\bf{A}})}\right]\left[{\begin{array}[]{c}{\dot{\bf r}}\\
{\bar{\bm{\omega}}}\end{array}}\right]+{{\Phi}}^{\bf{A}}_{t}={\bf 0}_{6nb}\;.

{{\bf G}^{\bf{A}}}\left[{\begin{array}[]{c}{\dot{\bf r}}\\

{{\bf G}^{\bf{A}}}\left[{\begin{array}[]{c}{\ddot{\bf r}}\\
\dot{\bar{\bm{\omega}}}\end{array}}\right]={\bf\gamma}^{\bf{A}}_{6nb}\;.

In the dynamics analysis, the evolution of the orientation matrix is governed by a differential equation, which ties the rate of change of the orientation matrix to the angular velocity of the body expressed in the body reference frame

as in
[
3

{\dot{\bf{A}}}={\bf{A}}{\tilde{\bar{\bm{\omega}}}}\;.

(30a)

To find the evolution of

from

to

30a

{{\bf{A}}}(t)={{\bf{A}}}_{n+1}\exp({t{\tilde{\bar{\bm{\omega}}}}}_{n+1})

[
14

. Setting
t=-h
, where
h
is the step size, leads to
{{\bf{A}}}_{n}={{\bf{A}}}_{n+1}\exp({-h{\tilde{\bar{\bm{\omega}}}}}_{n+1})
. Since
\exp({{\bf{C}}}_{1}+{{\bf{C}}}_{2})=\exp({{\bf{C}}}_{1})\exp({{\bf{C}}}_{2})
as soon as
{{\bf{C}}}_{1}{{\bf{C}}}_{2}-{{\bf{C}}}_{2}{{\bf{C}}}_{1}={{\bf{0}}}

[
5
]
, a right multiplication by
\exp({h{\tilde{\bar{\bm{\omega}}}}}_{n+1})
leads to

(30b)

with the matrix exponential evaluated based on Eqs. (
6
) and (
7

{h{\tilde{\bar{\bm{\omega}}}}}_{n+1}
is skew-symmetric, its exponential is an orthonormal matrix. As such
{{\bf{A}}}_{n+1}
is a proper orthonormal matrix. Note that the Lie integration formula in Eq. (
30b
) is implicit.

As described in
[
3
]
, by applying D’Alembert’s principle one gets the so called Newton-Euler constrained equations of motion for body
i
in the system. By changing the notation in
[
3
]
to follow the conventions adopted herein, these equations assume the form

\left\{{\begin{array}[]{l}m_{i}{\ddot{\bf r}}_{i}+[{{\Phi}^{\bf{A}}_{,\bf r_{i}}]}^{T}{\bm{\lambda}}={\bf f}_{i}\\
{\bar{\bf J}}_{i}{\dot{\bar{\bm{\omega}}}}_{i}+{\bar{\Pi}}^{T}_{i}({{\Phi}^{\bf{A}}}){\bm{\lambda}}={\bar{\bm{\tau}}}_{i}\end{array}}\right.\;,\qquad i=1,\ldots,nb\;.

(31a)

Above,
m_{i}
is body
i
’s mass; the mass moment of inertia
{\bar{\bf J}}_{i}
is constant and diagonal; the applied force depends on level zero (position/orientation) and level one (velocity) information as in
{\bf f}_{i}={\bf f}_{i}({\bf r},{\bf A},{\dot{\bf r}},{\dot{\bar{\bm{\omega}}}})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
; and,

, which is considered known and provided as a function of level zero and one information. Specifically,
{\bar{{\bf{n}}}}_{i}
is the sum of three components: resultant of the distributed torques
{\bar{{\bf{n}}}}_{i}^{m}
, which are distributed over the volume of body
i
;
{\bar{{\bf{n}}}}_{i}^{a}
, resultant torque which is obtained from applied, pointwise torques, e.g., produced by an electric motor; and, torques induced by forces
{\bf{f}}_{i}^{P}
applied to body
i
at a point P of location
{\bar{{\bf{s}}}}_{i}^{P}
:
{\bar{{\bf{n}}}}_{i}^{f}={\tilde{\bar{{\bf{s}}}}}_{i}^{P}{{\bf{A}}}_{i}^{T}{{\bf{f}}}_{i}^{P}
.

The motion is subject to a set of algebraic constraints

{{\Phi}^{\bf{A}}}({\bf{r}}_{1},\ldots,{\bf{r}}_{nb},{{\bf{A}}}_{1},\ldots,{{\bf{A}}}_{nb},t)={\bf 0}_{nc}\;,

(31b)

where

.

For brevity, we assume that all kinematic constraints are holonomic, yet non-holonomic constraints are handled equally well in this solution approach as long as they are linear in velocity (Pfaffian).

In the light of Eq. (
30b
), the first order, implicit Euler integration scheme is posed as follows (

\displaystyle{\dot{\bf r}}_{i,n+1}

\displaystyle={\dot{\bf r}}_{i,n}+h{\ddot{\bf r}}_{i,n+1}

(32a)

\displaystyle{\bar{\bm{\omega}}}_{i,n+1}

\displaystyle={\bar{\bm{\omega}}}_{i,n}+h{\dot{\bar{\bm{\omega}}}}_{i,n+1}

(32b)

\displaystyle{{\bf r}}_{i,n+1}

\displaystyle={{\bf r}}_{i,n}+h{\dot{\bf r}}_{i,n+1}

(32c)

\displaystyle{{\bf A}}_{i,n+1}

\displaystyle={{\bf A}}_{i,n}\exp(h{\tilde{\bar{\bm{\omega}}}}_{i,n+1})

(32d)

The formula in Eq. (
32

32a
) through (

) are classical Backward Euler formulas; Eq. (
32d

Given the state of the system at time
t_{n}
, the immediate goal is to find the values
{\ddot{\bf r}}_{i,n+1}
,
{\dot{\bar{\bm{\omega}}}}_{i,n+1}
, and
{\bm{\lambda}}_{n+1}
at
t_{n+1}
. To that end, for any set of translation and angular accelerations
{\ddot{\bf r}}_{i,n+1}
and
{\dot{\bar{\bm{\omega}}}}_{i,n+1}

) and then Eq. (

) to get the new velocity and location of the bodies, respectively; and Eq. (
32b
) and then Eq. (
32d
) to get the new angular velocity and orientation of the bodies. Note that the matrix exponential is computed using Rodrigues’s formula in Eq. (

The values
{\ddot{\bf r}}_{i,n+1}
,
{\dot{\bar{\bm{\omega}}}}_{i,n+1}
, and
{\bm{\lambda}}_{n+1}
at

are obtained by solving (via a Newton algorithm) the discretized form of the constrained equations of motion

\displaystyle{\bf M}{\ddot{\bf r}}_{n+1}+\left[[{\Phi}^{\bf{A}}_{,{\bf r}}]^{T}{\bm{\lambda}}\right]_{n+1}

\displaystyle={\bf f}_{n+1}\vskip 5.69046pt

(33a)

\displaystyle{\bar{\bf J}}{\dot{\bar{\bm{\omega}}}}_{n+1}+\left[{\bar{\Pi}}^{T}({{\Phi}^{\bf{A}}}){\bm{\lambda}}\right]_{n+1}

\displaystyle={\bar{\bm{\tau}}}_{n+1}\quad,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{h^{2}}{{\Phi}^{\bf{A}}}({\bf r}_{n+1},{\bf A}_{n+1},t_{n+1})

\displaystyle={\bf 0}

where the scaling by
1/h^{2}
is done to improve the condition number of the Newton-step Jacobian
[
15
]
and

\displaystyle{\bf M}

\displaystyle\equiv\mbox{diag}\{m_{1}{\bf I}_{3},\ldots,m_{nb}{\bf I}_{3}\}

(33b)

\displaystyle{\bar{\bf J}}

\displaystyle\equiv\mbox{diag}\{{\bar{\bf J}}_{1},\ldots,{\bar{\bf J}}_{nb}\}

\displaystyle{\bf f}

\displaystyle\equiv[{\bf f}_{1}^{T},\ldots,{\bf f}_{nb}^{T}]^{T}

\displaystyle{\bar{\bm{\tau}}}

\displaystyle\equiv[{\bar{\bm{\tau}}}_{1}^{T},\ldots,{\bar{\bm{\tau}}}_{nb}^{T}]^{T}

\displaystyle{{\Phi}^{\bf{A}}}({\bf r},{\bf A},t)

\displaystyle\equiv{{\Phi}^{\bf{A}}}({\bf{r}}_{1},\ldots,{\bf{r}}_{nb},{{\bf{A}}}_{1},\ldots,{{\bf{A}}}_{nb},t)\;.

For 3D dynamics,
\bf M
and
\bar{\bf J}
are diagonal and constant throughout the simulation. Also, although in Eq. (
33a
) the arguments of
{{\Phi}^{\bf{A}}}
are the position and orientation at time

, note that these two quantities are functions of
{\ddot{\bf r}}_{n+1}
and
{\dot{\bar{\bm{\omega}}}}_{n+1}
, respectively, through the integration formulas in Eq. (

33a

{\bf g^{\bf{A}}}({\ddot{\bf r}}_{n+1},{\dot{\bar{\bm{\omega}}}}_{n+1},{\bm{\lambda}}_{n+1})={\bf 0}_{6nb+nc}\;,

(34a)

where

{\bf g^{\bf{A}}}({\ddot{\bf r}},{\dot{\bar{\bm{\omega}}}},{\bm{\lambda}})\equiv\left[{\begin{array}[]{l}{\bf M}{\ddot{\bf r}}-{\bf f}^{c}-{\bf f}\\
{\bar{\bf J}}{\dot{\bar{\bm{\omega}}}}+{\tilde{\bar{\bm{\omega}}}}{\bar{\bf J}}{{\bar{\bm{\omega}}}}-{\bar{\bf n}}^{c}-{\bar{\bf n}}\\
\frac{1}{h^{2}}{{\Phi}^{\bf{A}}}({\bf r},{\bf A},t)\end{array}}\right]\;.

(34b)

{\bf f}^{c}\equiv-[{\Phi}^{\bf{A}}_{,{\bf r}}]^{T}{\bm{\lambda}}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad{\bar{\bf n}}^{c}\equiv-{\bar{\Pi}}^{T}({{\Phi}^{\bf{A}}}){\bm{\lambda}}\;.

(34c)

Note that
{\bf f}^{c}={\bf f}^{c}({\bf r},{\bf A},{\bm{\lambda}})
and
{\bar{\bf n}}^{c}={\bar{\bf n}}^{c}({\bf r},{\bf A},{\bm{\lambda}})
, and both
{\bf f}^{c}
and
{\bar{\bf n}}^{c}
are linear in
{\bm{\lambda}}
.
In terms of notation used, any quantity without a subscript is understood to be evaluated in the configuration associated with the time
t_{n+1}
. If quantities that depend on the previous time steps come into play, a subscript will explicitly show when such quantities are evaluated.

An iterative approach is used to solve
{\bf g^{\bf{A}}}({\ddot{\bf r}},{\dot{\bar{{\bm{\omega}}}}},{\bm{\lambda}})={\bf 0}
. The
(k+1)
approximation of the solution is computed by applying corrections to the iteration
(k)
values:

\displaystyle{\ddot{\bf r}}^{(k+1)}

\displaystyle={\ddot{\bf r}}^{(k)}+{\bm{\delta}}^{(k)}_{r}

(35)

\displaystyle{\dot{\bar{\bm{\omega}}}}^{(k+1)}

\displaystyle={\dot{\bar{\bm{\omega}}}}^{(k)}+{\bm{\delta}}^{(k)}_{\omega}\quad.

\displaystyle{{\bm{\lambda}}}^{(k+1)}

\displaystyle={{\bm{\lambda}}}^{(k)}+{\bm{\delta}}^{(k)}_{\lambda}

For notation brevity, the subscripts associated with the time step (
n+1
) and body index (

) have been dropped above. Based on Eq. (
32
), a change
{\bm{\delta}}^{(k)}_{r}
in accelerations will lead to a change
h{\bm{\delta}}^{(k)}_{r}
in velocities:
{\dot{\bf r}}^{(k+1)}={\dot{\bf r}}^{(k)}+h{\bm{\delta}}^{(k)}_{r}

in positions:
{{\bf r}}^{(k+1)}={{\bf r}}^{(k)}+h^{2}{\bm{\delta}}^{(k)}_{r}
. Likewise, a change
{\bm{\delta}}^{(k)}_{\omega}
in angular accelerations will lead to a change

in velocities:
{\bar{\bm{\omega}}}^{(k+1)}={\bar{\bm{\omega}}}^{(k)}+h{\bm{\delta}}^{(k)}_{\omega}

{{\bf{A}}}
but rather its action on other quantities. The relevant question is as follows: in light of Eq. (

{{\bf{A}}}^{(k)}{\bar{{\bf{s}}}}

\displaystyle{{\bf{A}}}

(36a)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\approx

(36b)

6
Conclusions and Future Work

Classical multibody dynamics in absolute coordinates commonly relies on Euler parameters or Euler angles to produce the orientation matrix of each body in the system at each time step. The methodology of the
rA
formulation proposed herein eschews this step by explicitly computing the orientation matrix
{\bf{A}}
via numerical integration. The highlight of this contribution is that the methodology discussed herein generalizes this approach to arbitrarily complex multibody systems by systematically formulating lower-pair joints in terms of four basic kinematic geometric constraints:
DP1
,
DP2
,
D
, and
CD
. Understanding the first order variation of these GCONs relative to changes in the orientation of a body draws on the ability to produce the first order variation of two simple quantities:
{\bf{A}}\bar{{\bf{s}}}
and
{{\bf{A}}}^{T}{\bf{s}}
, which are used time and again in the kinematic constraint equations. The solution of the Newton-Euler equations is found via a first order implicit Euler integration scheme that numerically integrates the index 3 DAEs of multibody dynamics. The SO(3) structure of the rotation matrices is accounted for by applying an exponential map numerical integration method that builds off Euler’s theorem and the Rodrigues formula.

A performance comparison is made between the proposed
rA
formulation and two commonly used formulations
rp
and
r
\bm{\epsilon}
that use Euler parameters and Euler angles as generalized coordinates, respectively. Using
rp
as a baseline, it is found that
rA
is approximately two times faster than
r
\bm{\epsilon}
, which, surprisingly, turned out to be faster than
rp
.

The behavior of the
rA
solution approach and the results of the performance comparisons are confirmed by two independently developed publicly available Python codes. The speed gains associated with
rA
are traced back to a simpler form of the equations of motion and a terser Jacobian matrix. The speed gains associated with
r
\bm{\epsilon}
indicate that the larger linear system in
rp
is more of a computational bottleneck than the expensive trigonometric functions involved in
r
\bm{\epsilon}
.

In future work, we will investigate how the reported speed gains of
rA
over the alternative
rp
or
r
\bm{\epsilon}
solutions can be leveraged in other contexts. First, we plan to investigate its performance in conjunction with a second order Lie group implicit integrator. It also remains to formulate an
rA
solution approach using a state space method, which reduces the DAE to an ODE problem
[
20
,
21
]
. Another direction of investigation is tied to handling friction and contact in a differential-variational framework
[
22
]
. The handling of higher-pair kinematic constraints will require additional insights since the four GCONs introduced will not be sufficient to capture, for instance, unilateral kinematic constraints. Finally, it remains to investigate how the simpler form of the equations of motion impacts the controls problem in multibody dynamics as well as the task of Machine Learning, where fast simulation is key for effective training.
