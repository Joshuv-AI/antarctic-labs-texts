On the time consistent solution to optimal stopping problems with expectation constraint
S. Christensen, M. Klein, B. Schultz

Abstract
We study the (weak) equilibrium problem arising from the problem of optimally stopping a one-dimensional diffusion subject to an expectation constraint on the time until stopping. The weak equilibrium problem is realized with a set of randomized but purely state dependent stopping times as admissible strategies. We derive a verification theorem and necessary conditions for equilibria, which together basically characterize all equilibria. Furthermore, additional structural properties of equilibria are obtained to feed a possible guess-and-verify approach, which is then illustrated by an example.

1
Introduction

In this paper we consider the stopping problem of maximizing the reward functional
\mathbb{E}_{x}[e^{-r\tau}g(X_{\tau})]
subject to
\mathbb{E}_{x}[\tau]\leq T
, where
X=(X_{t})_{t\in[0,\infty)}
is a regular Itô diffusion on an interval
I\subset\mathbb{R}
,
x
is the starting value of
X
,
g
is a payoff function,
r\geq 0
is a discount factor and
T>0
is a time constraint.

This problem generalizes optimal stopping with fixed time horizon to include situations where, e.g. due to uncertainty in planning, such a time constraint seems unreasonable although a tractable notion of a time limitation should be imposed. Particularly if such problems occur
repeatedly
and
independently
it appears to be a natural choice to bound the average time spend. Optimal stopping problems with expectation constraints were first studied by Kennedy
[
24
]
in 1982 and later on were analyzed in e.g.
[
26
,
2
,
1
,
5
,
4
]
.

Most recently, the question of time-(in)consistency of optimal stopping and more general stochastic control problems has increasingly become the focus of mathematical discussion, cf.
[
7
]
. The main idea is that in practical optimal stopping an agent observes a single path
t\mapsto X_{t}(\omega)
of the state process and makes a stopping decision at each point in time based on her past observations. This gives the agent the ability to reconsider her initial strategy (consisting of a stopping time) for maximizing her utility, if her preferences change in the future. This idea traces back to the concept of
consistent planning
pioneered by Strotz
[
34
]
.

In the following, we elaborate the (rather far-reaching) implications of this comparatively simple observation for the problem with expectation constraint, but first we take a closer look at how the expectation constraint turns the classical, time-consistent problem of maximizing
\mathbb{E}_{x}[e^{-r\tau}g(X_{\tau})]
(without the constraint and infinite time horizon) into a time-inconsistent one.

An agent facing this problem by observing the state process and acting upon it would, during her time of observing, i.e. for all
t
up to the time she stops, try to solve the problem of maximizing
e^{-rt}\mathbb{E}_{X_{t}(\omega)}[e^{-r\tau_{t}}g(X_{\tau_{t}})]
over all stopping times
\tau_{t}\geq 0
. By the Markovian nature of the state process
X
, these problems are all structurally the same. This leads to the well known fact that for the unconstrained problem it is optimal to make the stopping decision solely based on the current state of the process, i.e.
the optimal stopping time is a first exit time
.

The situation changes if we introduce the expectation constraint. Even though the optimization target at time
t
is still given by
e^{-rt}\mathbb{E}_{X_{t}(\omega)}[e^{-r\tau_{t}}g(X_{\tau_{t}})]
we can now only choose stopping times
\tau_{t}
such that the expectation constraint is met. Thus, to implement an optimal stopping strategy in practice, an agent who makes her stopping decision at each point in time based on her single observation of the state process and the
stopping strategy
up to that point faces the problem that she must still satisfy her initial constraint of
\mathbb{E}_{x}[\tau]\leq T
even though some time has passed.

It is common to assume that the agent displays exactly this type of precommitment behavior in the mathematical literature (see discussion below), while in practice this seems to be the exception rather than the rule. To highlight that this problem setting is particularly prone to such irrationalities note that the expectation constraint allows for
sacrificial strategies
, such as to stop early in some instances, or in other words
sacrifice
potential future gain, in order to save up time to wait for a maximal gain on other occasions and still meet the expectation constraint. Moreover, the stopping decision must not even be solely based on the state process but could also rely on some external randomization device. Even if such a strategy is deemed optimal at first, in practice it seems very likely that at the time such a strategy calls for stopping an agent will fail to commit to it. In the agent’s mind, it now seems preferable to keep the process alive for some potential future benefit and sacrifice another
future run
rather than satisfy the constraint.

Due to the Markovian nature of the state process, especially when the time horizon
T
is rather large compared to the elapsed time, it seems even more likely that from the agents’ point of view the problems at time 0 and at time
t
are largely the same, up to the current state of
X
, and should be treated as such. This means at time
t
the constraint is perceived as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{X_{t}(\omega)}[\tau_{t}]\leq T

and not as
\mathbb{E}_{x}[\mathds{1}_{\{{\tau>t}\}}(\tau_{t}+t)]\leq T-\mathbb{E}_{x}[\mathds{1}_{\{{\tau\leq t}\}}\tau]
. Embedding that into the space time frame and accounting for all possible states of
X_{t}
we end up with the constraint

\mathbb{E}_{y}[\tau_{t}]\leq T

for all
y\in I
and all
t\in[0,\infty)
. To address the issues of partially rational behavior, such as procrastination the problem is now treated as a game-theoretic (weak) equilibrium problem. We realize the (weak) equilibrium problem with a set of purely state dependent stopping times, see Section
2
. This allows us to reformulate the problem as the (weak) equilibrium problem corresponding to the maximization of
\mathbb{E}_{x}[e^{-r\tau}g(X_{\tau})]
subject to
\mathbb{E}_{y}[\tau]\leq T
for all
y\in I
.

Literature

Different formulations of equilibrium problems to various problems in optimal stopping/control have been treated by now. In
[
7

Especially for continuous time problems with non discrete state space

, there are multiple equilibrium concepts to model the previously mentioned aspect of consistent planing in optimal stopping. A notable aspect of such equilibrium problems is the choice of the set of admissible stopping times. Usually, to achieve compatibility with the common equilibrium concepts, some
Markovian
structure must be imposed for stopping times to be admissible strategies. For Itô diffusions the monograph
[
7
]
introduces a version of the most common notion of an equilibrium in continuous time optimal stopping that is, like the one we consider, based on a first order condition, but with first exit times of the space time process as admissible stopping times. Other works that feature closely related equilibrium concepts based on a first order condition are
[
9
,
12
,
17
,
35
]
. The first two even allow for mixed/randomized
Markovian
stopping strategies. Mixed stopping strategies in discrete time frameworks are treated in
[

,

]
.
[

]
introduces and compares the concepts of mild, weak, strong and optimal mild equilibria in a diffusion setting with only pure first entry times being admissible, while the weak equilibrium corresponds to the concept in
[

.

The introduction of randomized stopping times follows the well-known principle from game theory: In general, there are no equilibria in pure strategies. This is also the case for our problem class, see Example
5.2
. This changes if the players are allowed to randomize. Since the randomization here must be of Markov type by the problem formulation (see (
2.3
) below), this leads to the class formally introduced in Definition
2.1
below. For a detailed discussion and motivation we refer to
[
9
,
12
]
.

For the formulation of the equilibrium problem with expectation constraint we drew inspiration from corresponding (precommitment) optimal stopping problems. Such optimal stopping problems were first examined by Kennedy
[
24
]
for discrete time processes. Using Lagrangian techniques he reduces the constrained problem to a classical unconstrained one and obtains optimal stopping times in the constrained problem.
The Lagrangian approach has often been successfully employed to fully solve optimal stopping problems even in continuous time with expectation constraints, e.g. in
[
25
,
30
,
28
]
.

In
[
26
]
and
[
2
]
the optimal stopping problem with expectation constraint is turned into an unconstrained optimal control problem with extended state space.
The first article
[
26
]
exploits the optimal stopping problem with expectation constraint for solving a time-inconsistent, but unconstrained stopping problem. The article
[
2
]
formulates a dynamic programming principle (DPP), characterizes the value function in terms of a Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman equation and proves a verification theorem. Bayraktar and Yao
[
5
]
provide a proof of the DPP in a general non-Markovian framework with a series of inequality-type and equality-type expectation constraints. Moreover,
[
4
]
extends
[
5
]
to the case where in the constrained optimal stopping problem also the diffusion is controlled.

In
[
10
]
a DPP for an optimal control problem with intermediate expectation constraints at each point in time in a general non-Markovian framework is derived.

For optimal stopping problems of one-dimensional processes with stopping times satisfying an expectation constraint
[
1
]
shows that the set of stopping times can be restricted to those stopping times such that the law of the process at the stopping time is a weighted sum of three Dirac measures.

2
Model and problem formulation

In this paper we consider a 1-dimensional Itô diffusion
X=(X_{t})_{t\in[0,\infty)}
taking values in an interval
(\alpha,\beta)
with
-\infty\leq\alpha<\beta\leq\infty
and
defined on a filtered probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{F},\mathbb{P})
with filtration
\mathbb{F}:=(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\in[0,\infty)}
satisfying the usual hypotheses.
Generally we assume that
X
is the strong solution to the stochastic differential equation

\displaystyle dX_{t}=\mu(X_{t})dt+\sigma(X_{t})dW_{t},\quad X_{0}=x

with an
\mathbb{F}
-adapted, real valued, standard Brownian motion
W=(W_{t})_{t\in[0,\infty)}
and Lipschitz continuous coefficients
\mu\colon(\alpha,\beta)\to\mathbb{R}
,
\sigma\colon(\alpha,\beta)\to(0,\infty)
. The infinitesimal generator
\mathcal{A}
of
X
is given by
\mathcal{A}f(x)=\mu(x)\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(x)+\frac{\sigma^{2}(x)}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial x^{2}}(x)
with
f:(\alpha,\beta)\to\mathbb{R}
twice continuously differentiable.

Other general notations used in the following are
\overline{D}
for the closure of a set
D\subset(\alpha,\beta)
,
D^{\circ}
for its interior,
\partial D
for its boundary and
D^{c}
for its complement
(\alpha,\beta)\setminus D
. Given
D_{1}\subset D_{2}\subset(\alpha,\beta)
and a function
f\colon D_{2}\to\mathbb{R}
we denote the restriction of
f
to
D_{1}
by
f|_{D_{1}}
. For
x\in(\alpha,\beta)
and
f\colon(\alpha,\beta)\to\mathbb{R}
we set
f(x-):=\lim_{y\nearrow x}f(y)
and
f(x+):=\lim_{y\searrow x}f(y)
whenever the corresponding limit exists.

Furthermore, let
\mathbb{F}^{X}:=(\mathcal{F}^{X}_{t})_{t\in[0,\infty)}
be the canonical filtration of
X
, i.e.
\mathcal{F}_{t}^{X}=\sigma(X_{s}:s\leq t)
. We also set
\mathcal{F}^{X}_{\infty}:=\sigma(\bigcup_{t\geq 0}\mathcal{F}_{t}^{X})
.
For every Borel measurable
D\subset(\alpha,\beta)
and
h>0
we define
\mathbb{F}^{X}
-stopping times

\displaystyle\tau^{D}

\displaystyle:=\inf\{t\geq 0\colon X_{t}\not\in D\}\quad\text{ and}

\displaystyle\tau_{h}

\displaystyle:=\inf\{t\geq 0\colon|X_{t}-X_{0}|\geq h\}.

Moreover, we assume that there is an
\mathbb{F}
-adapted Poisson process
N=(N_{t})_{t\in[0,\infty)}
on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{F},\mathbb{P})
with intensity 1, that is independent of
X
and
W
. The process
N
acts as an
external
source of randomness in the model that is going to be introduced in the following.

For fixed
D\subset(\alpha,\beta)
the set of càdlàg functions
\psi\colon(\alpha,\beta)\to[0,\infty)
with finitely many discontinuities that vanish on
(\alpha,\beta)\setminus\overline{D}
shall be denoted by
\mathfrak{V}_{D}
. For
\psi\in\mathfrak{V}_{D}
we set

\displaystyle\Psi=(\Psi_{t})_{t\in[0,\infty)},\quad\Psi_{t}:=\int_{0}^{t}\psi(X_{s})ds

(2.1)

and denote the set of such processes
\Psi
by
\mathfrak{W}_{D}
. For each
D\subset(\alpha,\beta)
and
\Psi\in\mathfrak{W}_{D}
let
\mathbb{F}^{X,N_{\Psi}}:=(\mathcal{F}^{X,N_{\Psi}}_{t})_{t\in[0,\infty)}
given by
\mathcal{F}^{X,N_{\Psi}}_{t}:=\sigma((X_{s},N_{\Psi_{s}}):s\leq t)
for all
t\in[0,\infty)
denote the canonical filtration of the 2-dimensional Markov process
((X_{t},N_{\Psi_{t}}))_{t\in[0,\infty)}
. Moreover, let
\mathcal{F}^{X,N_{\Psi}}_{\infty}:=\sigma(\bigcup_{t\geq 0}\mathcal{F}^{X,N_{\Psi}}_{t})
. With this we define
\tau^{\Psi}
as the first jump time of the process
N_{\Psi}
, i.e.

\displaystyle\tau^{\Psi}:=\inf\{t\geq 0\colon N_{\Psi_{t}}\geq 1\}.

Moreover, we set

\displaystyle\tau^{D,\Psi}:=\tau^{D}\wedge\tau^{\Psi}

(2.2)

where
\wedge
denotes the minimum.

As usual we denote the conditional distribution of
\mathbb{P}
given
(X_{0},N_{\Psi_{0}})=(x,n)
by
\mathbb{P}_{x,n}
and the expectation with respect to
\mathbb{P}_{x,n}
by
\mathbb{E}_{x,n}
. For short we write
\mathbb{P}_{x}
and
\mathbb{E}_{x}
instead of
\mathbb{P}_{x,0}
and
\mathbb{E}_{x,0}
, respectively.

Definition 2.1
.

(randomized Markovian time)

If
D
is open, we call stopping times of type (
2.2
)
randomized Markovian time
.
The set of randomized Markovian times is denoted by

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}

\displaystyle:=\{\tau^{D,\Psi}\colon D\subset(\alpha,\beta)\text{ open},\Psi\in\mathfrak{W}_{D}\}

and define the set of randomized Markovian times with expectation up to
T\in[0,\infty)
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}(T):=\{\tau\in\mathcal{S}\colon\mathbb{E}_{x}[\tau]\leq T\,\,\,\,\forall\,x\in(\alpha,\beta)\}.

Remark 2.2
.

(i)

The requirement of right continuity and existence of real left limits in the definition of

(ii)

.

(iii)

The function
\psi
can be regarded as the
rate of randomized stopping
of the stopping times
\tau^{\Psi}
and
\tau^{D,\Psi}
respectively.

(iv)

For each randomized Markovian time

there is an associated partition of

•

the

(v)

If we define

as the first jump time of

, we have
\tau^{\Psi}=\inf\{t\geq 0\colon\Psi_{t}\geq U\}
. Moreover,

is independent of
X
.

(vi)

Keep in mind that all
\tau\in\mathcal{S}
share the same
external source of randomization

that also defines

.

(vii)

The structure of the stopping times
\tau^{\Psi}
enables a simple calculation of the corresponding expectation functionals: Let
x\in(\alpha,\beta)
. Given any random variable
Y
on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P}_{x})
with values in a measure space
(E,\mathcal{E})
we denote the image measure of
Y
on
(E,\mathcal{E})
by
\mathbb{P}_{x}^{Y}
. Let
\pi_{t}\colon(\alpha,\beta)^{[0,\infty)}\to(\alpha,\beta)

x\mapsto x(t)
denote the projection on the
t
-coordinate. We regard
X
as a random variable with values in
((\alpha,\beta)^{[0,\infty)},

\bigotimes_{t\in[0,\infty)}\mathcal{B}((\alpha,\beta)^{[0,\infty)}))
, where
\bigotimes_{t\in[0,\infty)}
denotes the product of sigma algebras of the indexed set and
\mathcal{B}(E)
the Borel sigma algebra on a topological space
E
.

Let
A:=\{\pi_{t_{1}}^{-1}(\Gamma_{1})\cap\ldots\cap\pi_{t_{n}}^{-1}(\Gamma_{n})\}
,
t,t_{1},...,t_{n}\in[0,\infty)
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
\Gamma_{1},...,\Gamma_{n}\subset(\alpha,\beta)
be Borel measurable. By the independence of
X,U
using
U\sim\Exp(1)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x}[\mathds{1}_{\{{X\in A}\}}\mathds{1}_{\{{\tau^{\Psi}\leq t}\}}]=\mathbb{E}_{x}[\mathds{1}_{\{{X\in A}\}}\mathds{1}_{\{{\int_{0}^{t}\psi(X_{s})ds\geq U}\}}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{(\alpha,\beta)^{[0,\infty)}}\mathds{1}_{\{{y\in A}\}}\int_{[0,\infty)}\mathds{1}_{\{{\int_{0}^{t}\psi(\pi_{s}(y))ds\geq u}\}}\mathbb{P}^{U}(du)\mathbb{P}_{x}^{X}(dy)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{(\alpha,\beta)^{[0,\infty)}}\mathds{1}_{\{{y\in A}\}}\left(1-e^{-\int_{0}^{t}\psi(\pi_{s}(y))ds}\right)\mathbb{P}_{x}^{X}(dy)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\mathds{1}_{\{{X\in A}\}}\left(1-e^{-\int_{0}^{t}\psi(\pi_{s}(X))ds}\right)\right]=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\int_{0}^{t}\mathds{1}_{\{{X\in A}\}}\psi(X_{s})e^{-\int_{0}^{s}\psi(X_{r})dr}ds\right].

Since
\big(\bigotimes_{t\in[0,\infty)}\mathcal{B}((\alpha,\beta)^{[0,\infty)})\big)\otimes\mathcal{B}([0,\infty))=\sigma\big(\{\pi_{t_{1}}^{-1}(\Gamma_{1})\cap\ldots\cap\pi_{t_{n}}^{-1}(\Gamma_{n})\cap[0,t]:t,t_{1},...,t_{n}\in[0,\infty)

,

with

\mathbb{E}_{x}[f(X,\tau^{\Psi})]=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\int_{[0,\infty)}f(X,s)\psi(X_{s})e^{-\int_{0}^{s}\psi(X_{r})dr}ds\right]

for all integrable
f\colon(\alpha,\beta)^{[0,\infty)}\times[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
. In particular

\mathbb{E}_{x}[f(X,\tau^{\Psi})|\mathcal{F}^{X}_{\infty}]=\int_{[0,\infty)}f(X,s)\psi(X_{s})e^{-\int_{0}^{s}\psi(X_{r})dr}ds.

is the first entry time of the right continuous process

\displaystyle\mathds{1}_{\{{\tau\geq\sigma}\}}\tau=\mathds{1}_{\{{\tau\geq\sigma}\}}(\theta_{\sigma}\circ\tau+\sigma)

The problem is that it is not clear how to reasonably regard

For fixed open

Definition 2.3
.

(local perturbation operator)

For

Remark 2.4

\Psi\in\mathfrak{W}_{D}
and all
h\geq 0
by (
2.3
) we infer

\displaystyle\tau^{D,\Psi}=\tau^{D,\Psi}\diamond\tau^{D,\Psi}(h).

Thus in particular
\mathcal{S}\subset\mathcal{S}^{\diamond}
.

(ii)

Generally
\tau^{D,\Psi}\diamond\tau^{\tilde{D},\tilde{\Psi}}(h)
is neither an
\mathbb{F}^{X,N_{\Psi}}
- nor an
\mathbb{F}^{X,N_{\tilde{\Psi}}}
-stopping time, since it draws information from
N_{\Psi}
and
N_{\tilde{\Psi}}
. Due to the path dependence induced by the indicators
\mathds{1}_{\{{\tau^{\tilde{D},\tilde{\Psi}}\leq\tau_{h}}\}}
,
\mathds{1}_{\{{\tau^{\tilde{D},\tilde{\Psi}}>\tau_{h}}\}}
the local perturbation
\tau^{D,\Psi}\diamond\tau^{\tilde{D},\tilde{\Psi}}(h)
is not in
\mathcal{S}
and thus
\mathcal{S}\subsetneq\mathcal{S}^{\diamond}
.

Definition 2.5
.

(reward functional)

For all almost surely finite
\tau\in\mathcal{S}^{\diamond}
we define the reward functional

\displaystyle J_{\tau}(x):=\mathbb{E}_{x}[e^{-r\tau}g(X_{\tau})],\,\,x\in(\alpha,\beta)

with some fixed discount factor
r\geq 0
and a measurable payoff function
g\colon(\alpha,\beta)\to[0,\infty)
.

Definition 2.6
.

(equilibrium)

Let
T\in[0,\infty)
be a fixed constant. We call a stopping time
\tau^{*}\in\mathcal{S}(T)
equilibrium randomized Markovian time for the optimal stopping problem associated to the functional
J
with expectation constraint
T
, or just equilibrium for brevity, if we have

\displaystyle\liminf_{h\searrow 0}\frac{J_{\tau^{*}}(x)-J_{\tau^{*}\diamond\tau(h)}(x)}{\mathbb{E}_{x}[\tau_{h}]}\geq 0

(2.4)

for all
x\in(\alpha,\beta)
and any
\tau\in\mathcal{S}
such that there exists some
h>0
and a neighborhood
U
of
x
with
\mathbb{E}_{y}[\tau^{*}\diamond\tau(h^{\prime})]\leq T
for all
h^{\prime}\in[0,h)
and all
y\in U
.

Note that the additional condition on the expectation(s) of
\tau^{*}\diamond\tau(h^{\prime})
excludes deviating strategies from consideration that locally violate the constraint.

In order to investigate the fulfillment of the constraint for a given randomized Markovian time and shorten the notation the following definition will be useful.

Definition 2.7
.

(expected time function)

Let
\tau^{D,\Psi}\in\mathcal{S}
be a randomized Markovian time. We call the function

\displaystyle e_{D,\Psi}(x):=\mathbb{E}_{x}[\tau^{D,\Psi}]

expected time function.

3
Equilibrium randomized Markovian times

The aim of this section is to derive some necessary conditions for equilibrium strategies in the sense of Definition

natural
parts of the Verification Theorem

in Section

3.1
Properties of equilibria

We now provide necessary conditions for an equilibrium.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
\tau^{D,\Psi}\in\mathcal{S}(T)

\psi\in\mathfrak{V}_{D}
the function defining
\Psi
via (

). Assume that

be an equilibrium in the sense of Definition

(\mathcal{A}-r)g(x)\leq 0

(ii)

(iii)

Remark 3.2
.

The parts (

(

(

in an equilibrium randomization can only be used if we are indifferent between stopping and continuing or to fully exploit the expected time constraint before stopping

In Lemma (

3.2
Verification Theorem

In this subsection we derive a Verification Theorem that provides sufficient conditions for a candidate randomized Markovian time
\tau^{D,\Psi}\in\mathcal{S}
to be an equilibrium.
We start by introducing a notions of regularity for the continuation region
D
of the candidate
\tau^{D,\Psi}
and the payoff function

Definition 3.3
.

An open
D\subset(\alpha,\beta)
is called regular if for every
x\in\partial D
one of the following conditions holds true:

•

x
is an isolated point in
D^{c}
.

•

There exists
\varepsilon>0
such that either
(x-\varepsilon,x)\subset D,[x,x+\varepsilon)\subset D^{c}
or
(x-\varepsilon,x]\subset D^{c},(x,x+\varepsilon)\subset D

Moreover, we call the payoff function

regular with respect to

if

is
\ccc^{2}
on
(D^{c})^{\circ}
and
g(x+),g(x-)
exist in
\mathbb{R}
for all

.

This notion of regularity of the stopping region can also be found in
[
3
]
and excludes the case from consideration where
D
consists of infinitely many disjoint intervals that cluster towards some points as this would pose technical difficulties.

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\liminf_{h\searrow 0}\frac{J_{\tau^{D,\Psi}}(x)-J_{\theta_{\tau_{h}}\circ\tau^{D,\Psi}+\tau_{h}}(x)}{\mathbb{E}_{x}[\tau_{h}]}+\frac{J_{\theta_{\tau_{h}}\circ\tau^{D,\Psi}+\tau_{h}}(x)-J_{\tau^{D,\Psi}\diamond\tau^{\tilde{D},\tilde{\Psi}}(h)}(x)}{\mathbb{E}_{x}[\tau_{h}]}

\displaystyle\,\frac{1}{2}((\tilde{\psi}(x-)-\psi(x-))(J_{\tau^{D,\Psi}}(x-)-g(x-))+(\tilde{\psi}(x)-\psi(x))(J_{\tau^{D,\Psi}}(x+)-g(x+)))

\displaystyle\,0.

4
Finding equilibria

We derive further properties of (equilibrium) randomized Markovian times and provide a guess-and-verify approach for finding equilibria.

the function defining

(i)

By the Markov property, Remark

(

\displaystyle e_{D,\Psi}(x)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x}[\tau^{D,\Psi}]=\mathbb{E}_{x}[\tau^{U}\wedge\tau^{D,\Psi}]+\mathbb{E}_{x}[\theta_{\tau^{U}\wedge\tau^{D,\Psi}}\circ\tau^{D,\Psi}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\int^{\tau^{U}}_{0}\mathds{1}_{\{{t<\tau^{\Psi}}\}}dt\bigg|\mathcal{F}_{\infty}^{X}\right]\right]+\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\mathbb{E}_{x,0}\left[\theta_{\tau^{U}\wedge\tau^{D,\Psi}}\circ\tau^{D,\Psi}|\mathcal{F}_{\tau^{U}\wedge\tau^{D,\Psi}}\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\int^{\tau^{U}}_{0}\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\mathds{1}_{\{{t<\tau^{\Psi}}\}}\big|\mathcal{F}_{\infty}^{X}\right]dt\right]+\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\mathbb{E}_{X_{\tau^{U}\wedge\tau^{D,\Psi}},N_{\Psi_{\tau^{U}\wedge\tau^{D,\Psi}}}}[\tau^{D,\Psi}]\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau^{U}}e^{-\Psi_{t}}dt\right]+\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\mathds{1}_{\{{\tau^{U}<\tau^{\Psi}}\}}\mathbb{E}_{X_{\tau^{U}}}[\tau^{D,\Psi}]\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau^{U}}e^{-\int_{0}^{t}\psi(X_{s})ds}dt\right]+\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[e^{-\int_{0}^{\tau^{U}}\psi(X_{s})ds}e_{D,\Psi}(X_{\tau^{U}})\right]=f_{e_{D,\Psi}}(x).

Together with the fact that

\displaystyle 0=\overline{\mathcal{A}}f_{e_{D,\Psi}}=\psi f_{e_{D,\Psi}}-1=\psi T-1\qquad\text{on $U$.}

(4.1)

). Right continuity of

are either

or
\frac{1}{T}

, which shows (

For the proof of (

) and the right continuity of

,

\displaystyle e_{D,\Psi}(y)

\displaystyle e_{D,\Psi}(y)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{y}[\tau^{(L,a+\delta)}\wedge\tau^{\Psi}]+\mathbb{E}_{y}[\mathds{1}_{\{{\tau^{\Psi}>\tau^{(L,a+\delta)}}\}}\mathds{1}_{\{{X_{\tau^{(L,a+\delta)}}=a+\delta}\}}]\mathbb{E}_{a+\delta}[\tau^{D,\Psi}]

Remark 4.2
.

Proposition
4.1
gives us a method to construct an equilibrium
\tau^{D,\Psi}
with connected
D
. By (
iib
) a candidate for
\psi\not\equiv 0
that satisfies (
ii
) from the Verification Theorem has the form
\frac{1}{T}\mathds{1}_{{[a,b)}}
,
\frac{1}{T}\mathds{1}_{{(\alpha,b)}}
,
\frac{1}{T}\mathds{1}_{{[a,\beta)}}
or
\frac{1}{T}\mathds{1}_{{(\alpha,\beta)}}
for some
\alpha<a<b<\beta
. By (
i
), (
iib
) and (
iic
) we find that
a
and
b
depend on the boundaries of
D
via

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}e_{D,\Psi}=-1\quad\text{on }\,(\inf D,a)

\displaystyle e_{D,\Psi}(\inf D)=0,\quad e_{D,\Psi}(a)=T,\quad e_{D,\Psi}^{\prime}(a)=0

(4.3)

if
\inf D\in(\alpha,\beta)
and

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}e_{D,\Psi}=-1\quad\text{on }\,(b,\sup D)

\displaystyle e_{D,\Psi}(\sup D)=0,\quad e_{D,\Psi}(b)=T,\quad e_{D,\Psi}^{\prime}(b)=0

(4.4)

if
\sup D\in(\alpha,\beta)
respectively, while
a=\alpha
if
\inf D=\alpha
and
b=\beta
if
\sup D=\beta
. Given
D
basic ODE theory does not provide existence nor uniqueness of solutions to the boundary value problems (
4.3
) and (
4.4
). Nevertheless, we have three equations and one free variable, namely
a
or
b
, it seems reasonable to believe that the systems (
4.3
) and (
4.4
) have exactly one solution. In that case, similar to classical optimal stopping we rely on the smooth fit principle from Theorem
3.5
(
iii
) in order to determine the boundary of
D
of the equilibrium randomized Markovian time.

Remark 4.3
.

The scheme described in Remark
4.2
can even be employed in situations where
D
is not an interval by applying it to the connected components of

, see Example

.

5
Examples

The aim of this section is to give some examples for the scheme proposed in Remark
4.2
. We start with the most simple case.

Remark 5.1
.

If
g
continuous with at most polynomial growth and
(e^{-rt}g(X_{t}))_{t\in[0,\infty)}
is a submartingale, then
\tau^{*}=\tau^{D,\Psi}
with
D=(\alpha,\beta)
and
\psi\equiv\frac{1}{T}
defining
\Psi
via (
2.1
) is an equilibrium in the sense of Definition
2.6
.

Here in view of Remark
2.2
(
v
) condition (
ii
) from the Verification Theorem is easily seen to hold via

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x}[\tau^{*}]=\mathbb{E}_{x}[\tau^{D,\Psi}]=\mathbb{E}_{x}[\tau^{\Psi}]=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\inf\left\{t\geq 0\colon\frac{1}{T}t\geq U\right\}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{x}[T\cdot U]=T.

Since
(\alpha,\beta)=\spt(\psi)
it only remains to show
g(x)\leq J_{\tau^{*}}(x)
from condition (
iv
) in Theorem
3.5
. As
(e^{-rt}g(X_{t}))_{t\in[0,\infty)}
is a submartingale we apply the optional sampling theorem to obtain

\displaystyle J_{\tau^{*}}(x)=\mathbb{E}_{x}[e^{-r\tau^{*}}g(X_{\tau^{*}})]\geq g(x).

Therefore the Verification Theorem
3.5
yields the claim.

The next example deals with a special case of Remark
5.1
and shows that considering randomized Markovian times instead of only first exit times of sets that are regular in the sense of Definition
3.3
as admissible strategies leads to a truly more general concept of equilibrium.

Example 5.2
.

Let
\alpha:=-\infty
,
\beta:=\infty
,
X=W=(W_{t})_{t\in[0,\infty)}
a standard Brownian motion,
g(x):=x^{2}
and
r=0
. In the corresponding equilibrium problem there are no equilibria
\tau^{D}=\tau^{D,\Psi}\in\mathcal{S}(T)
with
\Psi\equiv 0
and regular
D
. Indeed, suppose
\tau^{D}
were such an equilibrium. Here
(\mathcal{A}-r)g=2>0
, so by Proposition
3.1
(
i
) it holds that
(D^{c})^{\circ}=\varnothing
, in particular
D\neq\varnothing
. Now let
x\in D
. Since
\mathbb{E}_{y}[\tau^{D}]\leq T
for all
y\in\mathbb{R}
the sets
D^{c}\cap(-\infty,x)
and
D^{c}\cap(x,\infty)
are non empty. Based on that we set
x_{l}:=\sup(D^{c}\cap(-\infty,x))
and
x_{r}:=\inf(D^{c}\cap(x,\infty))
. As

is open we infer
x_{l}<x<x_{r}
. Now
J_{\tau^{D}}(y)=x_{r}^{2}\frac{y-x_{l}}{x_{r}-x_{l}}+x_{l}^{2}\frac{x_{r}-y}{x_{r}-x_{l}}
for
y\in[x_{l},x_{r}]
. Using that
J_{\tau^{D}}\geq g
by Proposition
3.1
(
ii
) and
J_{\tau^{D}}(x_{r})=g(x_{r})
we infer

\displaystyle\partial_{x}J_{\tau^{D}}(x_{r}-)

\displaystyle=x_{l}+x_{r}<2x_{r}=\partial_{x}g(x_{r}+)=\lim_{h\searrow 0}\frac{g(x_{r}+h)-g(x_{r})}{h}

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{h\searrow 0}\frac{J_{\tau^{D}}(x_{r}+h)-J_{\tau^{D}}(x_{r})}{h}=\partial_{x}J_{\tau^{D}}(x_{r}+),

which contradicts Proposition
3.1
(
iii
).

Example 5.3
.

We now consider a Brownian motion
(W_{t})_{t\in[0,\infty)}
and the payoff function
g(x)=|x|
. Using the guess-and-verify approach from Remark
4.2
we derive an equilibrium whose structure depends on the discount factor
r
and if
r>0
also on the size of
r\,T
.

First we focus on
\boxed{r=0}
. In this case the process
g(W_{t})=|W_{t}|
,
t\in[0,\infty)
, is a strict submartingale under every
\mathbb{P}_{x}
.
By Remark
5.1
an equilibrium is given by
\tau^{*}=\tau^{D,\Psi}
, where
D=\mathbb{R}
and
\psi\equiv\frac{1}{T}
defining
\Psi
via (
2.1
).
The corresponding
J_{\tau^{*}}
is given by

\displaystyle J_{\tau^{*}}(x)=\mathbb{E}_{x}[|W_{\tau^{*}}|]=|x|+\sqrt{\frac{T}{2}}\exp\left(-\sqrt{\frac{2}{T}}\,|x|\right)\!.

For a positive discount factor
\boxed{r>0}
we first consider the unconstrained optimal stopping problem

\displaystyle\sup_{\tau\in\mathcal{T}}\mathbb{E}_{x}[e^{-r\tau}|W_{\tau}|],

where
\mathcal{T}
denotes the set of all almost surely finite stopping times. With the usual methods (for more details we refer to
[
31
]
) one can show that the optimal stopping time is given by
\tau^{\tilde{D}}
, where
\tilde{D}=(-\tilde{x},\tilde{x}
) with
\tilde{x}=\tilde{x}(r)=\frac{\tilde{z}}{\sqrt{2r}}
and
\tilde{z}
is the unique positive solution of

\displaystyle z=\frac{\cosh(z)}{\sinh(z)}.

(5.1)

Note that
\tilde{z}\approx 1.19967864
.
Moreover, we have

\displaystyle\sup_{\tau\in\mathcal{T}}\mathbb{E}_{x}[e^{-r\tau}|W_{\tau}|]=\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} &\frac{\tilde{x}\cosh(\sqrt{2r}\,x)}{\cosh(\sqrt{2r}\,\tilde{x})},\qquad&&|x|<\tilde{x},\\[5.69046pt]
&|x|,&&|x|\geq\tilde{x}.\end{aligned}\end{cases}

(5.2)

In particular, it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x}\big[\tau^{\tilde{D}}\big]=\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} &\frac{\tilde{z}^{2}}{2r}-x^{2},\qquad&&x\in\tilde{D},\\[5.69046pt]
&0,&&x\notin\tilde{D}.\end{aligned}\end{cases}

Hence,
\mathbb{E}_{x}\big[\tau^{\tilde{D}}\big]\leq T
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
if and only if
r\,T\geq\frac{\tilde{z}^{2}}{2}\approx 0.7196144195
.

Now we come back to the optimal stopping problem with expectation constraint. If
\boxed{r\,T\geq\frac{\tilde{z}^{2}}{2}}
then the optimal stopping time
\tau^{\tilde{D}}
of the unconstrained problem is admissible, i.e.
\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\tau^{\tilde{D}}\right]\leq T
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
. Let

\displaystyle D^{*}=\tilde{D}=(-\tilde{x},\tilde{x}),\qquad\psi\equiv 0

and
\tau^{*}=\tau^{D^{*}}
. The corresponding
J_{\tau^{*}}
is given by (
5.2
). Then all conditions of Theorem
3.5
are satisfied and
\tau^{*}
is an equilibrium strategy.

If
\boxed{r\,T<\frac{\tilde{z}^{2}}{2}}
then the optimal strategy of the unconstrained optimal stopping problem does not fulfill the expectation constraint for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
and, thus, is not admissible.

To find an equilibrium we follow Remark
4.2
and assume that the strategy
\tau
stops immediately under
\mathbb{P}_{x}
if
|x|
is greater or equal to
b
, continues if
|x|
is sufficiently large but not greater than
b
and stops with rate
\psi(x)=\frac{1}{T}
for small
|x|
. More precisely, we consider
\tau^{D^{b},\Psi^{a}}
with
D^{b}=(-b,b)
and
\psi^{a}=\frac{1}{T}\mathds{1}_{[-a,a)}
defining
\Psi^{a}
via (
2.1
) for
0<a<b
. Here
a
and
b
have to be chosen in such a way that the expectation constraint
\mathbb{E}_{x}[\tau^{D^{b},\Psi^{a}}]\leq T
holds true for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
.
Observe that (
4.3
) and (
4.4
) imply that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}e_{D^{b},\Psi^{a}}^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle=-1\qquad\qquad\text{on }(-b,-a)\cup(a,b),

\displaystyle e_{D^{b},\Psi^{a}}(-b)

\displaystyle=e_{D^{b},\Psi^{a}}(b)=0,

\displaystyle e_{D^{b},\Psi^{a}}(-a)

\displaystyle=e_{D^{b},\Psi^{a}}(a)=T,

\displaystyle e_{D^{b},\Psi^{a}}^{\prime}(-a)

\displaystyle=e_{D,\Psi^{a}}^{\prime}(a)=0,

which yields
a=b-\sqrt{T}
. In particular,
b>\sqrt{T}
. To simplify notation we set
\tau^{b}=\tau^{D^{b},\Psi^{b-\sqrt{T}}}
and
a=b-\sqrt{T}
.

Now we derive
J_{\tau^{b}}
. For
|x|\geq b
we have
J_{\tau^{b}}(x)=|x|
. Moreover, the symmetry of the Brownian motion
W
, of the payoff function
g
and of the randomized Markovian time
\tau^{b}
yield
J_{\tau^{b}}(x)=J_{\tau^{b}}(-x)
.
By
[
15
, Theorem 5.9]
it holds that

•

J_{\tau^{b}}|_{(-a,a)}\in\ccc^{2}((-a,a))
,

•

J_{\tau^{b}}|_{(-b,-a)}\in\ccc^{2}((-b,-a))
,

•

J_{\tau^{b}}|_{(a,b)}\in\ccc^{2}((a,b))
.

Lemma
3.4
implies that
J_{\tau^{b}}
satisfies

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{1}{2}J_{\tau^{b}}^{\prime\prime}(x)-\left(r+\frac{1}{T}\right)J_{\tau^{b}}(x)&=-\frac{1}{T}|x|,\quad\ \ x\in(-a,a),\\[5.69046pt]
\frac{1}{2}J_{\tau^{b}}^{\prime\prime}(x)-rJ_{\tau^{b}}(x)&=0,\qquad\qquad x\in(-b,-a)\cup(a,b).\end{split}

(5.3)

The differential equations (
5.3
) together with
J_{\tau^{b}}(x)=J_{\tau^{b}}(-x)
result in

\displaystyle J_{\tau^{b}}(x)=\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} &\xi\cosh\left(\gamma|x|\right)+\frac{|x|}{rT+1}+\frac{1}{\gamma(rT+1)}e^{-\gamma|x|},&&|x|<a=b-\sqrt{T},\\[5.69046pt]
&\lambda e^{\sqrt{2r}|x|}+\nu e^{-\sqrt{2r}|x|},\qquad&&|x|\in(a,b)=(b-\sqrt{T},b)\end{aligned}\end{cases}

for some
\xi=\xi(T,r,b),

\lambda=\lambda(T,r,b)
,
\nu=\nu(T,r,b)
and with
\gamma=\sqrt{2\left(r+\frac{1}{T}\right)}
.
Since the function
J_{\tau^{b}}
is continuous on
[-b,b]
by Lemma
A.3
and even
J_{\tau^{b}}|_{(-b,b)}\in\ccc^{1}((-b,b))
by direct computations we obtain boundary conditions to determine
\xi
,
\lambda
and
\nu
which depend on
b
, but are unique for fixed
r,T
and
b
.

We now derive
b
such that
\partial_{x}J_{\tau^{b}}(b-)=\partial_{x}J_{\tau^{b}}(b+)=1
which guarantees that the second part of Condition (iii) in Theorem
3.5
is satisfied.
A straight forward calculation shows that
\partial_{x}J_{\tau^{b}}(b-)=1
holds if and only if
b
is a zero of the function
\ell\colon[\sqrt{T},\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
with

\displaystyle\ell(b)

\displaystyle=\gamma\sinh(\gamma(b-\sqrt{T}))\left[b\cosh(\sqrt{2rT})-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2r}}\sinh(\sqrt{2rT})-\frac{b-\sqrt{T}}{rT+1}-\frac{e^{-\gamma(b-\sqrt{T})}}{\gamma(rT+1)}\right]

\displaystyle\quad+\cosh(\gamma(b-\sqrt{T}))\left[\sqrt{2r}b\sinh(\sqrt{2rT})-\cosh(\sqrt{2rT})+\frac{1-e^{-\gamma(b-\sqrt{T})}}{rT+1}\right].

One can show that
\ell
is strictly increasing with
\lim_{b\to\infty}\ell(b)=\infty
and

\displaystyle\ell(\sqrt{T})=\sqrt{2rT}\sinh(\sqrt{2rT})-\cosh(\sqrt{2rT})<0,

where we use for the inequality that
\sqrt{2rT}<\tilde{z}
and
y\sinh(y)-\cosh(y)<0
for all
y\in[0,\tilde{z})
.
Hence, there exists a unique
b^{*}=b^{*}(T,r)\in(\sqrt{T},\infty)
with
\ell(b^{*})=0
which implies that
\partial_{x}J_{\tau^{b^{*}}}(b^{*}-)=1
.

In addition, we have

\displaystyle J_{\tau^{b^{*}}}(x)=\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} &\xi(T,r,b^{*})\,\cosh\left(\gamma|x|\right)+\frac{|x|}{rT+1}+\frac{1}{\gamma(rT+1)}e^{-\gamma|x|},\\
&\hskip 256.0748pt|x|\leq b^{*}-\sqrt{T},\\[14.22636pt]
&\frac{1}{2}\left(b^{*}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2r}}\right)\,e^{-\sqrt{2r}\,(b^{*}-|x|)}+\frac{1}{2}\left(b^{*}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2r}}\right)\,e^{\sqrt{2r}\,(b^{*}-|x|)},\\
&\hskip 256.0748pt|x|\in(b^{*}-\sqrt{T},b^{*}),\\[14.22636pt]
&|x|,\hskip 237.58067pt|x|\geq b^{*},\end{aligned}\end{cases}

where

\displaystyle\xi(T,r,b^{*})=\frac{1}{\cosh(\gamma(b^{*}-\sqrt{T}))}

\displaystyle\left(b^{*}\cosh(\sqrt{2rT})-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2r}}\sinh(\sqrt{2rT})\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\left.-\frac{1}{rT+1}(b^{*}-\sqrt{T}+\frac{1}{\gamma}e^{-\gamma(b^{*}-\sqrt{T})}\right).

Now one can check that
\tau^{b^{*}}
is indeed an equilibrium using Theorem
3.5
.

Remark 5.4
.

The most common formulation of the constrained optimal stopping problem for the Brownian motion
W
and the payoff function
g(x)=|x|
is

\displaystyle\text{maximize }\mathbb{E}_{x}[|W_{\tau}|]\qquad\text{subject to $\tau\in\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}(x,T)$},

(5.4)

for given
x\in\mathbb{R}
, where
\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}(x,T)
denotes the set of all stopping times with respect to the augmented natural filtration of the Brownian motion satisfying
\mathbb{E}_{x}[\tau]\leq T
. A major difference to our equilibrium approach is that the constraint only applies for the fixed starting value
x
but not uniformly on the entire state space.

An optimal stopping time in (
5.4
) for fixed
x
and
T
is given by
\tau^{D(x,T)}
with
D(x,T)=(-\sqrt{T+x^{2}},\sqrt{T+x^{2}})
and the value of (
5.4
) is
\sqrt{T+x^{2}}
, see
[
2
, Example 4.5]
. In particular, for any
y
with
|y|<|x|
the stopping time
\tau^{D(x,T)}
does not satisfy the expectation constraint
\mathbb{E}_{y}\big[\tau^{D(x,T)}\big]\leq T
that would be required in the equilibrium problem.

Observe that in (
5.4
) we do not allow for an external source of randomization.

The precommitment approach presented in
[
2
,
26
]
does not cover the discounted optimal stopping problem

\displaystyle\text{maximize }\mathbb{E}_{x}[e^{-r\tau}|W_{\tau}|]\qquad\text{subject to $\tau\in\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}(x,T)$}

(5.5)

for
r>0
, which we treat in our equilibrium framework. Although
[
4
]
comprises Problem (
5.5
), to the best of our knowledge there are no solution methods at hand to derive an optimal stopping time or the value of (
5.5
).

Finally, we present an example where in an equilibrium
\tau^{D,\Psi}
the set
D
is not an open interval but an open set.

1,&\text{else,}\end{cases}

.

and

, where
\widetilde{z}

), an equilibrium is given by
\tau^{D,\Psi}
with

Lemma A.1
.

(i)

For every randomized Markovian time
\tau^{D,\Psi}\in\mathcal{S}(T)
the expected time function
e_{D,\Psi}
is continuous.

(ii)

For every
x\in(\alpha,\beta)
there is some neighborhood
[x-\eta,x+\eta]
with
\eta>0
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{y}[\tau_{h}]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle h\searrow 0}}{{\to}}0

uniformly in
y\in[x-\eta,x+\eta]
.

Proof.

Since
e_{D,\Psi}=0
outside
D
it is sufficient to consider
x\in\overline{D}
. Let
a<b\in\overline{D}
such that
x\in[a,b]
.
Let
X^{\tau^{\Psi}}=(X^{\tau^{\Psi}}_{t})_{t\in[0,\infty)}
be given by

\displaystyle X^{\tau^{\Psi}}_{t}:=\begin{cases}X_{t},&t<\tau^{\Psi},\\
\partial,&t\geq\tau^{\Psi}\end{cases}

with a coffin state
\partial
. Additionally we set
\mathfrak{m}_{y}:=\inf\{t\geq 0:X^{\tau^{\Psi}}_{t}=y\}
,
y\in(\alpha,\beta)
and
\mathfrak{m}_{\infty}:=\inf\{t\geq 0:X^{\tau^{\Psi}}_{t}=\partial\}
. By
[
23
, p. 119, p. 121]
the functions
x\mapsto\mathbb{P}_{x}(\mathfrak{m}_{a}<\mathfrak{m}_{b})=\mathbb{P}_{x}(X_{\tau^{(a,b)}\wedge\tau^{\Psi}}=a)
,
x\mapsto\mathbb{P}_{x}(\mathfrak{m}_{a}>\mathfrak{m}_{b})=\mathbb{P}_{x}(X_{\tau^{(a,b)}\wedge\tau^{\Psi}}=b)
and
x\mapsto\mathbb{E}_{x}[\mathfrak{m}_{a}\wedge\mathfrak{m}_{b}\wedge\mathfrak{m}_{\infty}]=\mathbb{E}_{x}[\tau^{(a,b)}\wedge\tau^{\Psi}]
are continuous.
The Markov property yields

\displaystyle e_{D,\Psi}(x)=\mathbb{E}_{x}[\tau^{(a,b)}\wedge\tau^{\Psi}]+e_{D,\Psi}(a)\mathbb{P}_{x}(X_{\tau^{(a,b)}\wedge\tau^{\Psi}}=a)+e_{D,\Psi}(b)\mathbb{P}_{x}(X_{\tau^{(a,b)}\wedge\tau^{\Psi}}=b),

which proves the first claim.

For the second claim let
f:(\alpha,\beta)\to\mathbb{R}
solve
\mathcal{A}f=1
. By Dynkin’s formula

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{y}[\tau_{h}]=\mathbb{E}_{y}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau_{h}}\mathcal{A}f(X_{s})ds\right]=\mathbb{E}_{y}[f(X_{\tau_{h}})]-f(y)\leq\sup_{z_{1},z_{2}\in[y-h,y+h]}|f(z_{1})-f(y_{2})|.

Uniform continuity of
f
on any
[x-\eta,x+\eta]\subset(\alpha,\beta)
provides the claim.
∎

Lemma A.2
.

Let
\tau^{D,\Psi},\tau^{\tilde{D},\tilde{\Psi}}\in\mathcal{S}
be randomized Markovian times and
\psi\in\mathfrak{V}_{D}
,
\tilde{\psi}\in\mathfrak{V}_{\tilde{D}}
the functions defining
\Psi
and
\tilde{\Psi}
via (
2.1
), respectively. Suppose that
g
has real left and right limits for all
x\in D
. For all
x\in D\cap\tilde{D}
such that
J_{\tau^{D,\Psi}}
is continuous in
x
we have

\displaystyle\lim_{h\searrow 0}\frac{J_{\theta_{\tau_{h}}\circ\tau^{D,\Psi}+\tau_{h}}(x)-J_{\tau^{D,\Psi}\diamond\tau^{\tilde{D},\tilde{\Psi}}(h)}(x)}{\mathbb{E}_{x}[\tau_{h}]}=

\displaystyle\,\frac{1}{2}\tilde{\psi}(x-)(J_{\tau^{D,\Psi}}(x)-g(x-))

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\tilde{\psi}(x)(J_{\tau^{D,\Psi}}(x)-g(x+)).

Proof.

Note that by the Markov property

\displaystyle J_{\theta_{\tau_{h}}\circ\tau^{D,\Psi}+\tau_{h}}(x)=\mathbb{E}_{x}[e^{-r\tau_{h}}J_{\tau^{D,\Psi}}(X_{\tau_{h}})]

(A.1)

and

\displaystyle J_{\tau^{D,\Psi}\diamond\tau^{\tilde{D},\tilde{\Psi}}(h)}(x)=\mathbb{E}_{x}\big[\mathds{1}_{\{{\tau^{\tilde{D},\tilde{\Psi}}\leq\tau_{h}}\}}e^{-r\tau^{\tilde{D},\tilde{\Psi}}}g(X_{\tau^{\tilde{D},\tilde{\Psi}}})+\mathds{1}_{\{{\tau^{\tilde{D},\tilde{\Psi}}>\tau_{h}}\}}e^{-r\tau_{h}}J_{\tau^{D,\Psi}}(X_{\tau_{h}})\big].

(A.2)

Let
h>0
such that
[x-h,x+h]\subset\tilde{D}
. This leads to
\tau^{\tilde{D},\tilde{\Psi}}=\tau^{\tilde{\Psi}}
on
\{\tau^{\tilde{D},\tilde{\Psi}}\leq\tau_{h}\}
. Now using (
A.1
), (
A.2
) and Remark
2.2
(
vii
) we obtain

\displaystyle J_{\theta_{\tau_{h}}\circ\tau^{D,\Psi}+\tau_{h}}(x)-J_{\tau^{D,\Psi}\diamond\tau^{\tilde{D},\tilde{\Psi}}(h)}(x)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x}\big[\mathds{1}_{\{{\tau^{\tilde{D},\tilde{\Psi}}\leq\tau_{h}}\}}\big(e^{-r\tau_{h}}J_{\tau^{D,\Psi}}(X_{\tau_{h}})-e^{-r\tau^{\tilde{D},\tilde{\Psi}}}g(X_{\tau^{\tilde{D},\tilde{\Psi}}})\big)\big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\mathds{1}_{\{{\tau^{\tilde{\Psi}}\leq\tau_{h}}\}}\big(e^{-r\tau_{h}}J_{\tau^{D,\Psi}}(X_{\tau_{h}})-e^{-r\tau^{\tilde{\Psi}}}g(X_{\tau^{\tilde{\Psi}}})\big)|\mathcal{F}^{X}_{\infty}\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x}\bigg[\int_{0}^{\tau_{h}}\big(e^{-r\tau_{h}}J_{\tau^{D,\Psi}}(X_{\tau_{h}})-e^{-rt}g(X_{t})\big)\tilde{\psi}(X_{t})e^{-\int_{0}^{t}\tilde{\psi}(X_{s})ds}dt\bigg]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau_{h}}J_{\tau^{D,\Psi}}(X_{\tau_{h}})\tilde{\psi}(X_{t})\left(e^{-\int_{0}^{t}\tilde{\psi}(X_{s})ds-r\tau_{h}}-1\right)dt\right]+\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau_{h}}J_{\tau^{D,\Psi}}(X_{\tau_{h}})\tilde{\psi}(X_{t})dt\right]

\displaystyle\quad-\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau_{h}}g(X_{t})\tilde{\psi}(X_{t})\left(e^{-\int_{0}^{t}\tilde{\psi}(X_{s})ds-rt}-1\right)dt\right]-\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau_{h}}g(X_{t})\tilde{\psi}(X_{t})dt\right].

(A.3)

We now divide (
A.3
) by
\mathbb{E}_{x}[\tau_{h}]
and evaluate the limits of all the summands on the new right-hand side in order to prove the claim. The first and third summand can be estimated as follows using
e^{-z}-1\geq-z
for all
z\geq 0
.

\displaystyle\Bigg|\frac{\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau_{h}}J_{\tau^{D,\Psi}}(X_{\tau_{h}})\tilde{\psi}(X_{t})\left(e^{-\int_{0}^{t}\tilde{\psi}(X_{s})ds-r\tau_{h}}-1\right)dt\right]}{\mathbb{E}_{x}[\tau_{h}]}

\displaystyle-\frac{\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau_{h}}g(X_{t})\tilde{\psi}(X_{t})\left(e^{-\int_{0}^{t}\tilde{\psi}(X_{s})ds-rt}-1\right)dt\right]}{\mathbb{E}_{x}[\tau_{h}]}\Bigg|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\,\frac{\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau_{h}}(g(X_{t})+J_{\tau^{D,\Psi}}(X_{\tau_{h}}))\tilde{\psi}(X_{t})(\int_{0}^{\tau_{h}}\tilde{\psi}(X_{s})ds+r\tau_{h})dt\right]}{\mathbb{E}_{x}[\tau_{h}]}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\,\sup_{y_{1},y_{2},y_{3},y_{4}\in[x-h,x+h]}(g(y_{1})+J_{\tau^{D,\Psi}}(y_{2}))\tilde{\psi}(y_{3})(\tilde{\psi}(y_{4})+r)\frac{\mathbb{E}_{x}[\tau_{h}^{2}]}{\mathbb{E}_{x}[\tau_{h}]}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle h\searrow 0}}{{\to}}0.

For the proof of
\frac{\mathbb{E}_{x}[\tau_{h}^{2}]}{\mathbb{E}_{x}[\tau_{h}]}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle h\searrow 0}}{{\to}}0
we refer to
[
9
, Lemma 25]
. With Lemma
A.4
from the appendix we can determine the limit of the fourth summand of the right-hand side of (
A.3
) divided by
\mathbb{E}_{x}[\tau_{h}]
:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau_{h}}g(X_{t})\tilde{\psi}(X_{t})dt\right]}{\mathbb{E}_{x}[\tau_{h}]}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle h\searrow 0}}{{\to}}\frac{1}{2}(\tilde{\psi}(x-)g(x-)+\tilde{\psi}(x+)g(x+)).

For the second summand of (
A.3
) over
\mathbb{E}_{x}[\tau_{h}]
we have

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\mathbb{E}_{x}[\int_{0}^{\tau_{h}}J_{\tau^{D,\Psi}}(X_{\tau_{h}})\tilde{\psi}(X_{t})dt]}{\mathbb{E}_{x}[\tau_{h}]}-\frac{1}{2}J_{\tau^{D,\Psi}}(x)(\tilde{\psi}(x-)+\tilde{\psi}(x+))\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\mathbb{E}_{x}[\int_{0}^{\tau_{h}}J_{\tau^{D,\Psi}}(X_{\tau_{h}})\tilde{\psi}(X_{t})dt]}{\mathbb{E}_{x}[\tau_{h}]}-\frac{\mathbb{E}_{x}[\int_{0}^{\tau_{h}}J_{\tau^{D,\Psi}}(x)\tilde{\psi}(X_{t})dt]}{\mathbb{E}_{x}[\tau_{h}]}\right|

\displaystyle+\left|J_{\tau^{D,\Psi}}(x)\frac{\mathbb{E}_{x}[\int_{0}^{\tau_{h}}\tilde{\psi}(X_{t})dt]}{\mathbb{E}_{x}[\tau_{h}]}-\frac{1}{2}J_{\tau^{D,\Psi}}(x)(\tilde{\psi}(x-)+\tilde{\psi}(x+))\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sup_{y\in[x-h,x+h]}|J_{\tau^{D,\Psi}}(y)-J_{\tau^{D,\Psi}}(x)|\frac{\mathbb{E}_{x}[\int_{0}^{\tau_{h}}\tilde{\psi}(X_{t})dt]}{\mathbb{E}_{x}[\tau_{h}]}

\displaystyle+|J_{\tau^{D,\Psi}}(x)|\left|\frac{\mathbb{E}_{x}[\int_{0}^{\tau_{h}}\tilde{\psi}(X_{t})dt]}{\mathbb{E}_{x}[\tau_{h}]}-\frac{1}{2}(\tilde{\psi}(x-)+\tilde{\psi}(x+))\right|.

By Lemma
A.4
and the continuity of
J_{\tau^{D,\Psi}}
in
x
the right-hand side of this inequality goes to 0 for
h\searrow 0
. Invoking the left-hand side of (
A.3
) as well as the right continuity of
\psi
this finishes the proof.
∎

Lemma A.3
.

Let
\tau^{D,\Psi}\in\mathcal{S}(T)
be a randomized Markovian time and
\psi\in\mathfrak{V}_{D}
the function defining
\Psi
via (
2.1
). Let
D^{\prime}
be some connected component of
D
. If
g|_{\overline{D^{\prime}}}
is bounded, then
J_{\tau^{D,\Psi}}|_{\overline{D^{\prime}}}
is continuous.

Proof.

Let
E\sim\Exp(r)
be a random variable on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
that is independent of
X
and
N
and let
\hat{X}=(\hat{X}_{t})_{t\in[0,\infty)}
denote the process
X
killed with
E
, i.e.

\displaystyle\hat{X}_{t}(\omega):=\begin{cases}X_{t}(\omega)&\text{if }t<E(\omega),\\
\infty&\text{if }t\geq E(\omega).\end{cases}

We extend the function
g
to
\infty
by setting
g(\infty):=0
. Now for
\inf D^{\prime}<a<x<b<\sup D^{\prime}
the Markov property gives

\displaystyle J_{\tau^{D,\Psi}}(x)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x}[g(\hat{X}_{\tau^{D,\Psi}})]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{x}(\hat{X}_{\tau^{(a,b)}\wedge\tau^{\Psi}}=a)\mathbb{E}_{a}[g(\hat{X}_{\tau^{D,\Psi}})]+\mathbb{E}_{x}[\mathds{1}_{\{{\hat{X}_{\tau^{(a,b)}\wedge\tau^{\Psi}}\neq a}\}}g(\hat{X}_{\tau^{D,\Psi}})]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle x\searrow a}}{{\to}}\mathbb{E}_{a}[g(\hat{X}_{\tau^{D,\Psi}})]=J_{\tau^{D,\Psi}}(a)

since
\mathbb{P}_{x}(\hat{X}_{\tau^{(a,b)}\wedge\tau^{\Psi}}=a)\to 1
for
x\to a
by

23
, p. 119]
as in the proof of Lemma

and thus also

\displaystyle\big|\mathbb{E}_{x}[\mathds{1}_{\{{\hat{X}_{\tau^{(a,b)}\wedge\tau^{\Psi}}\neq a}\}}g(\hat{X}_{\tau^{D,\Psi}})]\big|\leq(1-\mathbb{P}_{x}(\hat{X}_{\tau^{(a,b)}\wedge\tau^{\Psi}}=a))\sup_{y\in\overline{D^{\prime}}}g(y)\to 0.

The case

∎

Lemma A.4
.

For every
x\in(\alpha,\beta)
and each function
f\colon(\alpha,\beta)\to\mathbb{R}
such that
f(x+)
and
f(x-)
exist (in
\mathbb{R}
), we have

\displaystyle\lim_{h\searrow 0}\frac{\mathbb{E}_{x}[\int_{0}^{\tau_{h}}f(X_{s})ds]}{\mathbb{E}_{x}[\tau_{h}]}=\frac{1}{2}(f(x-)+f(x+)).

(A.4)

Proof.

Without loss of generality we may assume that
\mu,\sigma
are bounded and
\sigma
is also bounded away from
0
as well as
\alpha=-\infty
and
\beta=\infty
, since (
A.4
) only depends on
X
in a neighborhood of
x
.
First we show that in order to prove (
A.4
) it suffices to verify

\displaystyle\lim_{h\searrow 0}

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{E}_{x}[\int_{0}^{\tau_{h}}\mathds{1}_{\{{X_{s}>x}\}}ds]}{\mathbb{E}_{x}[\tau_{h}]}=\lim_{h\searrow 0}\frac{\mathbb{E}_{x}[\int_{0}^{\tau_{h}}\mathds{1}_{\{{X_{s}<x}\}}ds]}{\mathbb{E}_{x}[\tau_{h}]}=\frac{1}{2},

(A.5)

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau_{h}}\mathds{1}_{\{{X_{s}=x}\}}ds\right]=0\,\text{ for all }h>0.

(A.6)

For this purpose observe

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau_{h}}f(X_{s})ds\right]=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau_{h}}\mathds{1}_{\{{X_{s}>x}\}}f(X_{s})ds\right]+\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau_{h}}\mathds{1}_{\{{X_{s}<x}\}}f(X_{s})ds\right]

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau_{h}}\mathds{1}_{\{{X_{s}=x}\}}f(X_{s})ds\right].

For
\varepsilon>0
we choose
h>0
such that
|f(y)-f(x-)|<\varepsilon
for all
y\in[x-h,x)
and
|f(y)-f(x+)|<\varepsilon
for all
y\in(x,x+h]
. With this we get

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau_{h}}\mathds{1}_{\{{X_{s}>x}\}}f(X_{s})ds\right]}{\mathbb{E}_{x}[\tau_{h}]}-\frac{1}{2}f(x+)\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau_{h}}\mathds{1}_{\{{X_{s}>x}\}}f(X_{s})ds\right]}{\mathbb{E}_{x}[\tau_{h}]}-\frac{\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau_{h}}\mathds{1}_{\{{X_{s}>x}\}}f(x+)ds\right]}{\mathbb{E}_{x}[\tau_{h}]}\right|

\displaystyle+\left|\frac{\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau_{h}}\mathds{1}_{\{{X_{s}>x}\}}f(x+)ds\right]}{\mathbb{E}_{x}[\tau_{h}]}-\frac{1}{2}f(x+)\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\frac{\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau_{h}}\mathds{1}_{\{{X_{s}>x}\}}|f(X_{s})-f(x+)|ds\right]}{\mathbb{E}_{x}[\tau_{h}]}+|f(x+)|\cdot\left|\frac{\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau_{h}}\mathds{1}_{\{{X_{s}>x}\}}ds\right]}{\mathbb{E}_{x}[\tau_{h}]}-\frac{1}{2}\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\,\varepsilon+|f(x+)|\cdot\left|\frac{\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau_{h}}\mathds{1}_{\{{X_{s}>x}\}}ds\right]}{\mathbb{E}_{x}[\tau_{h}]}-\frac{1}{2}\right|

An analogous estimate with
\mathds{1}_{\{{X_{s}>x}\}}
and
f(x+)
replaced by
\mathds{1}_{\{{X_{s}<x}\}}
and
f(x-)
, respectively, together with
\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau_{h}}\mathds{1}_{\{{X_{s}=x}\}}f(X_{s})ds\right]=f(x)\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau_{h}}\mathds{1}_{\{{X_{s}=x}\}}ds\right]
shows that (
A.5
) and (
A.6
) are in fact sufficient for (
A.4
).

The transition densities
p_{t}(x,y):=\mathbb{P}_{x}(X_{t}\in dy)
can be estimated as follows:

\displaystyle p_{t}(x,y)\leq\frac{K_{1}}{\sqrt{t}}\exp\left(-K_{2}\frac{(y-x)^{2}}{t}\right),

(A.7)

cf.
[
33
, Equation (1.2)]
.
With this we immediately obtain (
A.6
) via

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau_{h}}\mathds{1}_{\{{X_{s}=x}\}}ds\right]\leq\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathds{1}_{\{{X_{s}=x}\}}ds\right]=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\mathds{1}_{\{{X_{s}=x}\}}\right]ds

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathds{1}_{\{{y=x}\}}\frac{K_{1}}{\sqrt{s}}\exp\left(-K_{2}\frac{(y-x)^{2}}{s}\right)dyds=0.

Concerning (
A.5
) we only show
\lim_{h\searrow 0}\frac{\mathbb{E}_{x}[\int_{0}^{\tau_{h}}\mathds{1}_{\{{X_{s}>x}\}}ds]}{\mathbb{E}_{x}[\tau_{h}]}=\frac{1}{2}
as for the other term the argument is exactly the same. By
[
15
, Theorem 5.5]
and Fubini’s theorem

\displaystyle\lim_{h\searrow 0}\frac{\mathbb{E}_{x}[\int_{0}^{\tau_{h}}\mathds{1}_{\{{X_{s}>x}\}}ds]}{\mathbb{E}_{x}[\tau_{h}]}=\lim_{t\searrow 0}\frac{\mathbb{E}_{x}[\int_{0}^{t}\mathds{1}_{\{{X_{s}>x}\}}ds]}{t}=\lim_{t\searrow 0}\frac{\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{P}_{x}(X_{s}>x)ds}{t}.

By
[
3
, Lemma 5.5]

\lim_{s\searrow 0}\mathbb{P}_{x}(X_{s}>x)=\frac{1}{2}
and thus

\displaystyle\lim_{t\searrow 0}\frac{\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{P}_{x}(X_{s}>x)ds}{t}=\frac{1}{2}

which proves the claim.
∎
