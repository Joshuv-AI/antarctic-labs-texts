Automated Fact-Checking of Climate Change Claims with Large Language Models
Markus Leippold, Saeid Ashraf Vaghefi, Dominik Stammbach, Veruska Muccione, Julia Bingler, Jingwei Ni, Chiara Colesanti-Senni, Tobias Wekhof, Tobias Schimanski, Glen Gostlow, Tingyu Yu, Juerg Luterbacher, Christian Huggel

Abstract
This paper presents Climinator , a novel AI-based tool designed to automate the fact-checking of climate change claims. Utilizing an array of Large Language Models (LLMs) informed by authoritative sources like the IPCC reports and peer-reviewed scientific literature, Climinator employs an innovative Mediator-Advocate framework. This design allows Climinator to effectively synthesize varying scientific perspectives, leading to robust, evidence-based evaluations. Our model demonstrates remarkable accuracy when testing claims collected from Climate Feedback and Skeptical Science. Notably, when integrating an advocate with a climate science denial perspective in our framework, Climinator ’s iterative debate process reliably converges towards scientific consensus, underscoring its adeptness at reconciling diverse viewpoints into science-based, factual conclusions. While our research is subject to certain limitations and necessitates careful interpretation, our approach holds significant potential. We hope to stimulate further research and encourage exploring its applicability in other contexts, including political fact-checking and legal domains.



Introduction

In the ongoing debate on climate change, the truthfulness of public statements is regularly called into question, emphasizing the critical need for swift and reliable fact-checking. A case in point is the recent claim made by Sultan Al Jaber, the president of COP28 and chief executive of the United Arab Emirates’ state oil company Adnoc. On November 21, 2023, Al Jaber controversially asserted that
“There is no science out there, or no scenario out there, that says that the phase-out of fossil fuel is what’s going to achieve 1.5C.”
This statement, which climate scientists and policymakers immediately challenged, highlights the complexities of separating factual information from opinion in shaping effective, evidence-based climate policy. Recognizing this challenge, our paper introduces
Climinator
, a novel framework designed to assess climate-related claims, leveraging advancements in LLMs.

In an era where information proliferates at an unprecedented pace, the task of manually reviewing claims for accuracy becomes increasingly resource-intensive and challenging. Over a decade ago, scholars warned that the exponential growth of online content would eventually overwhelm journalistic fact-checkers, diminishing news quality and contributing to societal harms like diminished government accountability
(
Cohen et al. 2011
)
. This concern has given rise to a new strand of research in Natural Language Processing (NLP), namely automated fact-checking
(

. With misinformation spreading faster and deeper than factual news
(

)
, there is a pressing need for sophisticated tools capable of effective and real-time fact-checking.

While early automated fact-checking tools, such as those based on the FEVER dataset
(
Thorne et al. 2018
)
and climate-focused datasets like climateFEVER
(
Diggelmann et al. 2020
)
, have made significant progress, they often fall short in providing the nuanced reasoning necessary for a comprehensive understanding of complex claims. This is where generative AI models, specifically LLMs, come into play. LLMs can offer holistic evaluations rooted in an extensive scientific knowledge base. They can provide the necessary context, reasoning, and argumentation essential for reaching well-informed verdicts on complex climate-related claims. Building on this premise,
Climinator
leverages the capabilities of LLMs to evaluate climate-related claims based on empirical evidence and scientific consensus. The aim is to provide a comprehensive, transparent, and objective assessment of claims that is not limited to countering polarized views but rather enables a more nuanced understanding of climate issues.

The Pipeline

Figure
1
depicts the operational flow of the
Climinator
framework, where the initial claim is parsed by an LLM into subclaims, enhancing the specificity and efficiency of the evaluation process. Specialized LLMs, henceforth referred to as advocates, are pivotal in the next phase. Each advocate examines the claim against a curated corpus of texts. The general GPT-4 model
(
OpenAI 2023
)
serves as one Advocate, while other advocates consist of retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) systems. These systems, which ensure LLM responses are grounded in credible sources, draw from diverse scientific and trusted repositories: the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) AR6 reports, World Meteorological Organization (WMO) sources, and two different collections of climate scientists’ articles, AbsCC and S1000. We describe the data in Section
A
. Each of the advocates is grounded on one particular text corpus and delivers a verdict informed by its respective data sources, prompted to provide evidence-backed rationales.

Figure 1:
Climinator
: An LLM-based framework within a Mediator-Advocate system to assess the veracity of climate-related claims.

The verdicts, reflecting the different perspectives inherent to each Advocate’s text corpus, are consolidated by the Mediator LLM. When advocates concur, the mediator synthesizes these findings into a final judgment. Conversely, disagreements between the advocates trigger a dynamic dialog that is moderated by the mediator, who encourages a reassessment and clarification of the positions by asking questions. This iterative exchange persists until a unified verdict is achieved.
In the design of
Climinator
, we have crafted prompts to evaluate a wide array of climate-related claims, enhancing the tool’s versatility and reliability in AI-driven fact-checking. These prompts are thoroughly documented in the appendix, ensuring transparency and maintaining the integrity of our approach.

To rigorously test the effectiveness of our
Climinator
framework, we also run experiments in which we introduce an adversarial advocate within the system. This advocate operates under a climate denial stance, processing and challenging information from a contrarian viewpoint. This inclusion serves not to endorse denialism, but to critically examine the robustness of our tool against diverse arguments, including those lacking scientific backing. It aims to identify any potential weaknesses in both the moderated consensus and the scientific literature underpinning it.

Results

We apply our approach to different sources of climate-change claims. In particular, we analyze 170 claims fact-checked by
Climate Feedback
, a website on which a network of climate scientists manually evaluates the credibility of media climate narratives. In the second step, we analyze 163 claims, which are typically made by climate denialists, from the webpage of
Skeptical Science
.
Finally, we analyze 81 claims from the
Heartland Institute
, which we obtain by extracting the claims from the executive summaries from publications of the so-called NIPCC (Non-governmental International Panel on Climate Change).

Climate Feedback

Climate Feedback categorizes the verdicts into twelve different categories, ranging from incorrect to correct, with different shades of correctness and accuracy in between. A typical example from Climate Feedback is, e.g., the following claim:

More than 75 percent of the rainforest is losing resilience. More than half of the rainforest could be converted into savanna in a matter of decades.

source
: Washington Post, March 15, 2022

Climate Feedback Verdict
:
mostly accurate

This claim obtained the verdict “mostly accurate” on
Climate Feedback
. A climate scientist usually provides the verdict and the reasoning, writing a few paragraphs with references to the relevant literature. In this case, the scientist argues that the claim is accurate in the sense that recent scientific findings indicate that a substantial portion, over 75 percent, of the Amazon rainforest has indeed experienced a decrease in resilience since the early 2000s. However, they note that the term "savanna-like" is employed to describe the potential transition of the Amazon rainforest rather than a complete transformation into a savanna ecosystem. The key distinction lies in the reduced diversity of tree species and a diminished capacity to store carbon in the altered Amazon ecosystem as compared to a typical savanna ecosystem. This verdict exemplifies the subtleties involved in providing a verdict to a given claim.

In summary, the analysis reveals that
Climinator
and
Climinator
+ are strong contenders, excelling in nuanced categorization tasks while maintaining high accuracy. IPCC and WMO demonstrate competitive performance, particularly in multi-class scenarios, albeit with some trade-offs. AbsCC and S1000 are effective in less granular tasks. Clearly, the choice of class configuration impacts model performance, highlighting the trade-offs between granularity and accuracy in climate claim categorization. Higher granularity in categorizing claims can lead to more detailed and specific insights. However, this inevitably reduces overall accuracy due to the complexity of making fine distinctions between multiple categories.

A high granularity may be crucial in contexts where nuanced understanding is essential, such as in academic research, policy formulation, or advanced educational settings. In these scenarios, detailed categorization can shed light on the specific nature of inaccuracies or misconceptions, aiding in targeted corrective measures or in-depth analysis. However, for broader public communication or preliminary screenings, where the primary goal is to quickly identify and address blatantly false claims, such high levels of detail may not be necessary.

Analyzing diverging verdicts.

Fact-checking the 170 claims on Climate Feedback, all the models reached a final verdict after the first round. Hence, there was no debating taking place. We argue that the absence of debating is due to the fact that the claims are clearly defined and, given that all the advocates draw their information from text sources that are grounded on climate science, have inherently low disagreement. Therefore, the mediator does not ask for an additional debating round.

Although there is no debating, it may still be instructive to analyze the few cases in which
Climinator
and the verdict from the scientists from Climate Feedback diverge when we analyze the claims using the binary “correct” versus “incorrect” classification. As an illustrative example, we take the following claim:

Amazon near tipping point of switching from rainforest to savannah.

source
: Fiona Harvey, The Guardian, 5 Oct. 2020

Climate Feedback Verdict
:
imprecise

Climinator
:
mostly accurate

To reach a verdict,
Climinator
first notes that while the IPCC, AbsCC, and S1000 LLMs offer substantial backing, the WMO concurs only on the drying trend, citing a lack of information on the tipping point. The primary caveat in the final assessment is the uncertainty regarding the precise timing of this ecological shift, although the overall direction towards a tipping point is clear. Hence,
Climinator
reaches the verdict that the claim is mostly accurate, with the noted uncertainty about the timing. This verdict, according to the aggregation in Figure
3
, merges into the category “correct”.

In contrast, Climate Feedback marks the claim as “imprecise” due to a lack of context and potential misinterpretation of the findings in
Staal et al. 2020
, to which the quoted claim refers. The cited study does not support the immediacy suggested by the claim but rather discusses forest resilience and the existing climatic potential for savanna conditions. The complexity of forest-rainfall feedback mechanisms and the gradual nature of ecological transitions are not adequately conveyed, leading to an imprecise portrayal of the Amazon’s current state. Thus, the verdict is “imprecise,” which then collapses to “incorrect” in our setting (see Figure
3
).

The evaluation of the claim regarding the Amazon’s potential tipping point from rainforest to savannah reveals a divergence in focus between Climate Feedback and
Climinator
. While
Climinator
addresses the claim as quoted above, which can be found on the overview page of Climate Feedback, Climate Feedback examines a more expanded version of the claim in its discussion, including details about the percentage of the rainforest likely to turn into savannah and the processes involved in the shift.

The verdict ‘imprecise’ primarily addresses the percentage of the rainforest turning into savannah and hence suggests an oversimplification. The verdict thus emphasizes a lack of necessary context, particularly as it implies an immediacy not fully supported by the source study. Conversely, the "mostly accurate" verdict, applied to the broader assertion of the Amazon nearing a tipping point, acknowledges substantial scientific support for this phenomenon. In this context,
Climinator
could provide insights into the likelihood and occurrence of a tipping point, which is a high relevant question in the development of nature-realted scenarios. In particular, by assessing the accuraccy of statements related to this topic, the algorithm could help assess the likelihood of a tipping points under different beliefs about the future. This would be of practical relevance in that users could be able to pick a scenario (with a given likelihood of occurrence) depending on their beliefs about fure developments (e.g. more or less optimistic).

From the above discussion, it becomes clear that decomposing the claim into its constituent parts — the likelihood of a tipping point and the specific percentage of the forest affected — allows for a more detailed and accurate evaluation of its various elements. Hence, we feed in the whole claim as eventually analyzed by Climate Feedback into
Climinator
and ask the LLM model to first decompose the claim into its part. The aggregated verdict is still “mostly accurate” but with the nuanced comment:

The summary of the verdict is that the claim is mostly accurate, with the caveat that the specific figure of 40% is not fully supported by the evidence provided. The sensitivity of rainforests to changes in rainfall and moisture, the impact of fires and droughts, and the gradual nature of the transition from rainforest to savannah are well-supported by the evidence. The difficulty in reversing the process once it has begun is also supported, although specific details on the timescale and the exact percentage of the Amazon at risk are less certain. Therefore, the overall claim is ([[mostly_accurate]]), with a high level of evidence and agreement for the sensitivity to rainfall and moisture, and the impact of fires and droughts, but a lower level of confidence in the specific figure of 40% and the timescale of the transition.

Interestingly, the lead author of the study referenced in the claim noted on Climate Feedback that the statement “in itself is not a false representation of our work,” suggesting that while specific aspects of the claim may lack precision, the overall concept is not inaccurately portrayed. Climinator
would arguably be even higher than reported in Table

Skeptical Science

Skeptical Science is one of the first and arguably the most popular platforms for verifying climate change claims. The typical claims on this platform are often of a more colloquial nature than on Climate Feedback. For example, Skeptical Science analyzes claims such as:

We are heading into an ice age.

source
: Skeptical Science

Such vague claims leave a lot of ambiguity and room for interpretation and may, therefore, pose problems for automated claim verification models to reach a final verdict.

Obtaining claim verdicts.

Compared to Climate Feedback, Skeptical Science does not provide an annotated label for the verdict. To obtain a label, we therefore proceed as follows. We feed the claims with the scientific explanations from Skeptical Science and ask GPT-4 to evaluate the claim in relation to the explanations given, without using any further information. To simplify compatibility, we use the same set of possible verdicts as for Climate Feedback (see figure
3
), and we plot their distribution in Figure
5
. Based on this classification, we then simplify the dataset to a binary classification task, following the procedure laid out in Figure
3
.

Explaining the fraction of NEIs.

The vagueness of the claims on Skeptical Science may cause some problems for automated fact-checking systems. Figure
6
shows the relative fraction of cases in which a given model cannot come to a verdict due to a lack of sufficient information about a given claim. We observe that, for Skeptical Science claims, the WMO advocate struggles even more than for Climate Feedback claims to reach a conclusion, with the highest
NEI
fraction at 46.23%. Surprisingly, even GPT-4 has a very high rate of
NEI
’s with 20.1%, compared to
Climinator
and
Climinator
+ with only 1.5% and 0.5%, respectively. Such a more assertive stance in categorization could potentially lead to an increased misclassification risk, highlighting a fundamental trade-off between the models’ decisiveness and risk aversion in classification tasks.

A straightforward question to ask next is: Why do we observe such differences in the reported amount of
NEI
’s in the two datasets, the claims from Climate Feedback and Skeptical Science. In Appendix
B
, we analyze the semantic and syntactic differences of the claims from the different sources available. We find that the higher incidence of
NEI
-classifications for Skeptical Science is due to its broad but shallow approach. The lower syntactic complexity might strip away the contextual or semantic depth needed for a model to make a confident, nuanced assessment. Interestingly, this is particularly the case for GPT-4. To reduce the impact of NEI’s, we therefore include the statement “This claim is made in a climate-change context: [claim]," so that the model can better capture the context of the claim.

Climinator

AbsCC and S1000 perform reasonably well, with accuracies above 93% when excluding NEI, but their performance again decreases when including NEI claims. Lastly, GPT-4 achieves an accuracy of 95.14% (excl. NEI) and 84.05% (incl. NEI), showcasing a noticeable decrease when NEI categorizations are considered. These results emphasize the importance of handling NEI instances effectively in such tasks.

Our analysis suggests that
Climinator
diverges only in one of the Skeptical Science claims. The claim is the following:

Hansen’s 1988 prediction was wrong.

source
: Skeptical Science

Skeptical Science categorizes this claim as “incorrect.” They explain that although Hansen’s prediction of a global temperature increase was higher than the actual warming observed, this discrepancy stems from his use of a high climate sensitivity parameter. Had Hansen employed the currently accepted value of approximately 3°C warming for a doubling of atmospheric CO2, his model would have more accurately projected the observed global warming.

In contrast,
Climinator
’s assessment posits:

[…] The final verdict, synthesizing the information from the Advocates, is that the claim is [[mostly_accurate]] but lacks context regarding the reasons for the overestimation and the general correctness of the warming trend predicted by Hansen once actual forcings are considered.

While
Climinator
concludes with a “mostly accurate” verdict, it acknowledges the absence of context concerning Hansen’s overestimation and the broader accuracy of his warming trend prediction when actual forcings are taken into account. This perspective, despite differing in classification from Skeptical Science’s “incorrect” label, is not fundamentally at odds with Skeptical Science’s assessment. Both analyses underscore the significance of understanding the underlying assumptions in Hansen’s model, thereby highlighting that
Climinator
’s evaluation, though different in its final verdict, aligns in principle with the critical assessment provided by Skeptical Science.

Absence of follow-up questions.

(e.g.,
Myers et al. 2021
, for a recent survey)
. Conclusion

Marvin Minsky’s "The Society of Mind"
(
Minsky 1986
)
provides a compelling analogy for understanding the collaborative and iterative nature of modern AI systems in tackling complex challenges, such as those faced by LLMs. Through their collective intelligence, LLMs effectively address the issue of ’hallucination’ in AI responses
(
Du et al. 2023
;
Zhuge et al. 2023
)
. The ‘agents’ conceptualized by Minsky find a parallel in the Advocate components of our
Climinator
framework, each specializing in distinct aspects of climate science. This allows for a nuanced and multifaceted analysis of climate-related claims, significantly reducing biases and errors.

Our application of
Climinator
to the controversial statement by Sultan Al Jaber (see Appendix
D
) exemplifies the framework’s potential for providing transparent and objective evaluations.
However, while
Climinator
marks a significant step forward, it is not without limitations. Continuous updates to its database are essential to keep abreast of the latest scientific developments. Additionally, ongoing efforts to mitigate inherent biases and improve the model’s accuracy are crucial for maintaining trust in AI-driven fact-checking tools. In conclusion, the
Climinator
framework, not only offers a promising approach to fact-checking but also serves as a compelling argument about the effectiveness of AI in enhancing our understanding of complex global challenges.

Limitations and Future Research

While
Climinator
represents a significant advancement in AI-supported climate discourse, it is subject to certain limitations that open avenues for future research and development.

Recency of information.

One of the key challenges lies in the tool’s reliance on potentially outdated information. The rapidly evolving nature of climate science means that newer studies and findings might not be immediately integrated into the system’s knowledge base. To address this issue, future iterations of
Climinator
could include a mechanism to prioritize and incorporate the most recent scientific publications and news, ensuring that the tool’s assessments are always grounded in the latest information.

Source quality and comprehensiveness.

Another limitation is the scope and quality of sources from which
Climinator
retrieves information. While the tool currently utilizes reputable sources, it may not capture the entire spectrum of relevant and emerging scientific data, especially if such information is behind paywalls. Hence, enhancing the information retrieval process and broadening the base of sources, including more diverse scientific journals, reports, and datasets, will be crucial. This will allow the tool to provide more comprehensive and robust answers to a wider array of climate-related queries.

Technical infrastructure.

Currently,
Climinator
is built on GPT-4, a closed-source model, which might pose challenges in terms of transparency and consistency over time as the model evolves. Therefore, transitioning to open-source language model checkpoints that are specifically trained for climate science fact-checking tasks can offer more transparency and control. This shift would also facilitate continuous updates and customizations tailored to the evolving needs of climate science communication.

Multi-modal debating system.

Looking ahead, there is potential for expanding
Climinator
into a multi-modal debating system. This would involve integrating various forms of data input, such as images, graphs, and videos, along with textual information. Such a system could provide a more holistic and interactive platform for climate science communication, engaging users in a richer and more informative experience. This multi-modal approach could significantly enhance the tool’s ability to debunk myths and clarify misconceptions by presenting evidence in various formats, making the information more accessible and comprehensible to a broader audience.

Output Evaluation.

Lastly, extending our
Climinator
framework to incorporate the evaluation approach presented in
Bulian et al. 2023
could significantly enhance our understanding of the system’s capabilities in climate communication. By adopting their comprehensive evaluation criteria focused on presentational and epistemological adequacy, we can conduct a more nuanced analysis of
Climinator
’s outputs, ensuring both accuracy and clarity in climate-related information dissemination. This extension would allow us to benchmark our system against other LLMs, utilizing a scalable oversight protocol with AI assistance and educated raters. Such a rigorous analysis could highlight potential areas for improvement, contributing to the development of more reliable and effective AI tools in the realm of climate change communication and fact-checking.

Prompting

We first note that in developing the LLM’s prompt, we have incorporated the IPCC’s Guidance Note on Uncertainty, which offers a crucial framework for assessing scientific knowledge about climate change. This framework emphasizes two key dimensions: "evidence" and "agreement." Evidence pertains to the type, amount, quality, and consistency of data and findings, ranging from limited to robust. For instance, a conclusion supported by numerous high-quality, consistent studies is considered to have robust evidence. Agreement, on the other hand, refers to the level of consensus among experts and scientific studies on a particular issue, which can vary from low to high.

prompt_arbitrator

In the
Climinator
framework, the Mediator, designated as the "Arbitrator" prompt in Listing
, plays a pivotal role in synthesizing and consolidating assessments from the various Advocates to determine the final verdict on climate-related claims. Each Advocate is equipped with expertise in specific domains of climate science and operates based on different authoritative documents, ensuring a well-rounded and comprehensive analysis. The Arbitrator, an authoritative climate scientist system, is tasked with reviewing the individual verdicts and explanations provided by these Advocates.

Key responsibilities of the Arbitrator include reviewing and synthesizing the verdicts, clarifying discrepancies, and determining the final assessment. In cases where Advocates provide conflicting assessments or lack specific information, the Arbitrator prioritizes verdicts based on concrete evidence and clarity. It employs a holistic judgment approach, considering the importance and disparities of each evaluation rather than solely relying on majority verdicts. The Arbitrator is guided by a set of criteria that emphasize the consideration of evidence and agreement levels, and it is instructed to treat inputs from Advocates.

The Arbitrator’s process involves asking follow-up questions to resolve inconsistencies and further debate, with the capability to initiate additional rounds of discussion if needed. This approach ensures that the final verdict is not based on mere majority voting but on a consensus reached after thorough examination and debate. The final output includes a restatement of the original user query, a summary of the verdict, and a clear explanation of the reasoning behind it, all articulated in accessible language. It ensures that the tool’s assessments are not only grounded in the latest and most reliable scientific information but also presented in a manner that is understandable and actionable for users, thereby enhancing the tool’s effectiveness in promoting informed decision-making in climate science.
