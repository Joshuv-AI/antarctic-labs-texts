Modularity of Erdős-Rényi random graphs
Colin McDiarmid, Fiona Skerman

Abstract
For a given graph G G , each partition of the vertices has a modularity score, with higher values indicating that the partition better captures community structure in G G . The modularity q ∗ ​ ( G ) q^{*}(G) of the graph G G is defined to be the maximum over all vertex partitions of the modularity score, and satisfies ≤ q ∗ ​ ( G ) < 1 0\!\leq\!q^{*}(G)\!<\!1 . Modularity is at the heart of the most popular algorithms for community detection. We investigate the behaviour of the modularity of the Erdős-Rényi random graph G n , p G_{n,p} with n n vertices and edge-probability p p . Two key findings are that the modularity is 1 + o ⁡ ( 1 ) 1+o(1) with high probability (whp) for n ​ p np up to 1 + o ⁡ ( 1 ) 1+o(1) and no further; and when n ​ p ≥ 1 np\geq 1 and p p is bounded below 1, it has order ( n p ) − 1 / 2 (np)^{-1/2} whp, in accord with a conjecture by Reichardt and Bornholdt in 2006. We also show that the modularity of a graph is robust to changes in a few edges, in contrast to the sensitivity of optimal vertex partitions.

Introduction

We start this section with some background and definitions, and then present our results on the modularity value
q^{*}
of the random graph
G_{n,p}
, followed by corresponding results for the random graph
G_{n,m}
with

1.1
Definitions

The large and ever-increasing quantities of network data available in many fields has led to great interest in techniques to discover network structure. We want to be able to identify if a network can be decomposed into dense clusters or ‘communities’.

Modularity was introduced by Newman and Girvan in 2004
[
41
]
. It gives a measure of how well a graph can be divided into communities, and now forms the backbone of the most popular algorithms used to cluster real data
[
26
]
. Here a ‘community’ is a collection of nodes which are more densely interconnected than one would expect – see the discussion following the definition of modularity below. There are many applications, including for example protein discovery, and identifying connections between websites: see
[
16
]
and
[
43
]
for surveys on the use of modularity for community detection in networks. Its widespread use and empirical success in finding communities in networks makes modularity an important function to understand mathematically.

Given a graph
G
, we give a modularity score to each vertex partition (or ‘clustering’) : the modularity
q^{*}(G)
(sometimes called the ‘maximum modularity’) of
G
is defined to be the maximum of these scores over all vertex partitions. For a set
A
of vertices, let
e(A)
be the number of edges within
A
, and let the
volume

{\rm vol}(A)
be the sum over the vertices
v
in
A
of the degree
d_{v}
.

Definition 1.1

(Newman & Girvan
[
41
]
, see also Newman
[
40
]
)
.

Let
G
be a graph with
m\geq 1
edges. For a partition
{\mathcal{A}}
of the vertices of
G
,
the modularity score of
{\mathcal{A}}
on
G
is

q_{\mathcal{A}}(G)=\frac{1}{2m}\sum_{A\in{\mathcal{A}}}\sum_{u,v\in A}\left({\mathbf{1}}_{uv\in E}-\frac{d_{u}d_{v}}{2m}\right)=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{A\in{\mathcal{A}}}e(A)-\frac{1}{4m^{2}}\sum_{A\in{\mathcal{A}}}{\rm vol}(A)^{2};

and the modularity of
G
is
q^{*}(G)=\max_{\mathcal{A}}q_{{\mathcal{A}}}(G)
, where the maximum is over all vertex partitions
{\mathcal{A}}
of
G
.

Isolated vertices are irrelevant. We need to give empty graphs (graphs with no edges) some modularity value. Conventionally we set
q^{*}(G)=0
for each such graph
G
(though the value will not be important). The second equation for
q_{{\mathcal{A}}}(G)
expresses modularity as the difference of two terms, the
edge contribution
or
coverage

q^{E}_{\mathcal{A}}(G)=\tfrac{1}{m}\sum_{A}e(A)
, and the
degree tax

q^{D}_{\mathcal{A}}(G)=\tfrac{1}{4m^{2}}\sum_{A}{\rm vol}(A)^{2}
.
Since
q^{E}_{{\mathcal{A}}}(G)\leq 1
and
q^{D}_{{\mathcal{A}}}(G)>0
, we have
q_{{\mathcal{A}}}(G)<1
for any non-empty graph
G
. Also, the trivial partition
{\mathcal{A}}_{0}
with all vertices in one part has
q^{E}_{{\mathcal{A}}_{0}}(G)=q^{D}_{{\mathcal{A}}_{0}}(G)=1
, so
q_{{\mathcal{A}}_{0}}(G)=0
. Thus we have

0\leq q^{*}(G)<1.

Suppose that we pick uniformly at random a multigraph with degree sequence
(d_{1},\ldots,d_{n})
where
\sum_{v}d_{v}=2m
. Then the expected number of edges between distinct vertices

and
v
is
d_{u}d_{v}/(2m-1)
. This is the original rationale for the definition: whilst rewarding the partition for capturing edges within the parts, we should penalise by (approximately) the expected number of such edges. The corresponding modularity scores for bipartite or directed graphs
[
42
,
27
,
20
]
and for hypergraphs
[
23
]
have also been defined and require an amended degree tax.

A differentiation between graphs which are truly modular and those which are not can … only be made if we gain an understanding of the intrinsic modularity of random graphs.
– Reichardt and Bornholdt
[
45
]
. In this paper we investigate the likely value of the modularity of an Erdős-Rényi random graph. Let
n
be a positive integer. Given

, the random graph

has vertex set

and the

possible edges appear independently with probability

. Given an integer

with
0\leq m\leq\binom{n}{2}
, the random graph
G_{n,m}
is sampled uniformly from the

. These two random graphs are closely related when

: we shall focus on

, but see results on

in Section

For a sequence of events

we say that

{\mathbb{P}}(A_{n})\to 1
as

For a sequence of random variables

and a real number

(that is, if for each

1.2
Results on the modularity of the random graph
G_{n,p}

Our first theorem, the Three Phases Theorem, gives the big picture. The three phases correspond to when (a) the expected vertex degree (essentially
np
) is at most about 1, (b) bigger than 1 but bounded, or (c) tending to infinity.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
p=p(n)
satisfy
0<p\leq 1
.

(a)

If
n^{2}p\rightarrow\infty
and
np\leq 1+o(1)
then
\;q^{*}(G_{n,p})\overset{p}{\rightarrow}1
.

(b)

Given constants
1<c_{0}\leq c_{1}
, there exists
\delta=\delta(c_{0},c_{1})>0
such that if
c_{0}\leq np\leq c_{1}
for
n
sufficiently large, then whp
\;\delta<q^{*}(G_{n,p})<1-\delta
.

(c)

If
np\rightarrow\infty
then
\;q^{*}(G_{n,p})\overset{p}{\rightarrow}0
.

Following the above general theorem we now give more detailed results. In each of the above parts (a), (b), (c) of Theorem
1.1
we can be more precise. Let us start with the sparse case, for
p
ranging from
0
up to a little above
1/n
, corresponding to part (a) and a little into part (b).
In this sparse case, the modularity
q^{*}
is near 1 whp, so we are interested in the
modularity deficit

1-q^{*}
. Given a graph
G
, let
\mathcal{C}
denote the connected components partition, in which the parts are the vertex sets of the connected components of
G
.

Theorem 1.2
.

(i) If
n^{3/2}p=o(1)
then whp exactly one of the following two statements holds: either there are no edges (that is
e(G_{n,p})=0
), or
\,q^{*}(G_{n,p})=q_{\mathcal{C}}(G_{n,p})=1-1/e(G_{n,p})
.

(ii)
If
n^{2}p\rightarrow\infty
and

np\leq 1-(\log n)^{1/2}n^{-1/4}
,
then

q^{*}(G_{n,p})=q_{\mathcal{C}}(G_{n,p})=1-\Theta(\tfrac{1}{n^{2}p(1-np)})\;\;\;\mbox{ whp}.

(iii)
If
0<\varepsilon\leq 1/4
and
1\leq np\leq 1+\varepsilon
then
q^{*}(G_{n,p})\geq q_{\mathcal{C}}(G_{n,p})>1-(4\varepsilon)^{2}
whp.

Part (i) above shows that we need the condition

in Theorem
1.1
part (a). For, if

is bounded, say

, then
e(G_{n,p})\leq\alpha
whp ; and so by part (i), whp
q^{*}(G_{n,p})\leq 1-1/\alpha
(or there are no edges). (In fact, for each

, the maximum value of

over all
m
-edge graphs

is
1-1/m
, see
[
34
]
.)

In parts (i) and (ii), the connected components partition

is the unique optimal partition, ignoring isolated vertices – see Proposition
2.5
. The upper bound condition on

in part (ii) is nearly best possible: it is shown in
[

]
that, if

and

, then whp there is a partition for

with strictly higher modularity than the connected components partition. In part (iii), corresponding to the lower bound on

there is a matching upper bound on

The next theorem confirms the

Theorem 1.3
.

There exists

q^{*}(G_{n,p})<\frac{b}{\sqrt{np}}

Also, given

such that, if

The lower bound here comes from analysing the algorithm

: for further information see Section

.
Theorem

implies part (c) of Theorem

, and implies part (b) of Theorem

except for the upper bound

As an immediate corollary of Theorem

we have:

Corollary 1.4
.

There exists
0<a<b
such that, if

\frac{a}{\sqrt{np}}<q^{*}(G_{n,p})<\frac{b}{\sqrt{np}}\;\;\;\mbox{ whp}.

A higher modularity score is taken to indicate a better community division. Thus to determine whether a clustering
{\mathcal{A}}
in a graph

shows significant community structure we should compare

to the likely (maximum) modularity for an appropriate null model, that is, to the likely value of

for null model

. It is an interesting question which null model may be most appropriate in a given situation. For example, real networks have been shown to exhibit power law degree behaviour, and so null models which can mimic this have been suggested, for example the Chung-Lu model
[
1
]
or random hyperbolic graphs
[

. However, a natural minimum requirement is not to consider a community division of a real network as statistically significant unless its modularity score is higher than the typical modularity value of an Erdős-Rényi random graph

of the same edge density.

1.4
Previous work on modularity

The vast majority of papers referencing modularity are papers in which real data, clustered using modularity-based algorithms, are analysed. Alongside its use in community detection, many interesting properties of modularity have been documented.

Properties of modularity

A basic observation is that, given a graph
G
without isolated vertices, in each optimal partition, for each part the corresponding induced subgraph of
G
must be connected. The idea of a
resolution limit
was introduced by Fortunato and Barthélemy
[
17
]
in 2007: in particular, if a connected component
C
in an
m
-edge graph has strictly fewer than
\sqrt{2m}
edges, then every optimal partition will cluster the vertices of
C
together.
This is so even if the connected component
C
consists of two large cliques joined by a single edge. This property highlights the sensitivity of optimal partitions to noise in the network: if that edge between the cliques, perhaps a mistake in the data, had not been there then the cliques would be in separate parts in every optimal partition. In contrast, although the structure of optimal partitions is not robust to small changes in the edge set, the modularity
value
of the graph is robust in this sense; see Section
5
.

Concerning computational complexity, Brandes et al. showed that finding the (maximum) modularity of a graph is NP-hard
[
5
]
. Furthermore it is NP-hard to approximate modularity to within any constant multiplicative factor
[
11
]
. Modularity maximisation is also
W[1]
-hard, a measure of hardness in parameterised complexity, when parameterised by pathwidth; but approximating modularity to within multiplicative error
1\pm\varepsilon
is fixed parameter tractable when parameterised by treewidth
[
38
]
. The reduction in
[
5
]
required some properties of optimal partitions; for example it was shown that a vertex of degree 1 will be placed in the same part as its neighbour in every optimal partition. Indeed, every part in every optimal partition has size at least 2 or is an isolated vertex, see Lemma 1.6.5 in
[
46
]
. The paper
[
5
]
also began the rigorous study of the modularity of classes of graphs, in particular of cycles and complete graphs. Later Bagrow
[
2
]
and Montgolfier et al.
[
10
]
proved that some classes of trees have high modularity, and this was extended in
[
33
]
to all trees with maximum degree
o(n)
, and indeed to all graphs where the product of treewidth and maximum degree grows more slowly than the number of edges.
There is a growing literature concerning the modularity behaviour of graphs in different classes, which we summarise in the appendix.

Ehrhardt and Wolfe in
[
13
]
look at the distribution of the modularity score of a random partition of a graph or random graph. They consider different random weighted models, including the Erdős-Rényi random graph
G_{n,p}
for
np\rightarrow\infty
, where they show that the modularity score of a random partition is asymptotically normally distributed. They do not investigate
q^{*}(G_{n,p})
.

Statistical Physics predictions

In 2004 Guimera et al.
[
19
]
observed through simulations that the modularity of random graphs can be surprisingly high. They conjectured that, for each (large) constant
c>1
,
whp
q^{*}({G_{n,c/n}})\approx c^{-2/3}
. In 2006 Reichardt and Bornholdt
[
45
]
made a different conjecture for the modularity in this range. They assumed that an optimal partition will have parts of equal size, then approximated the number of edges between parts, using spin glass predictions from
[
24
]
for the minimum number of cross-edges in a balanced partition of a random graph, and predicted
q^{*}(G_{n,c/n})\approx 0.97\,c^{-1/2}
whp. We confirm this growth rate. Indeed they predicted
q^{*}(G_{n,p})\approx 0.97\sqrt{(1-p)/{np}}
, which is
\Theta((np)^{-1/2})
, for
1/n\leq p\leq 0.99
. Thus Corollary
1.4
shows that, for a wide range of probabilities
p
, the prediction of Reichardt and Bornholdt
[
45
]
is correct up to constant factors (and refutes that of Guimera et al.).

Finally let us note that the conference version
[
32
]
contained some of the same material as the current full paper, but deferred several proofs and results to this paper.

2.1

It is convenient to record first one standard preliminary result on degree tax.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let the graph
G
have
m\geq 1
edges, and let
{\mathcal{A}}
be a
k
-part vertex partition for some
k\geq 2
. Then
q^{D}_{\mathcal{A}}(G)\geq 1/k
; and if
x,y
are respectively the largest, second largest volume of a part, then
(x/2m)^{2}\leq q^{D}_{\mathcal{A}}(G)\leq x/2m
and
q^{D}_{\mathcal{A}}(G)\leq(x/2m)^{2}+y/2m
.

Proof.

All the bounds follow from the convexity of
f(t)=t^{2}
.
Let
x_{i}
be the volume of the
i
th part in
{\mathcal{A}}
.
For the
1/k
lower bound, observe that
x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}\geq 0
and
\sum_{i=1}^{k}x_{i}=2m
together imply that
\sum_{i=1}^{k}x_{i}^{2}\geq k\,(2m/k)^{2}=(2m)^{2}/k\,
; and
thus
q_{{\mathcal{A}}}^{D}(G)=\sum_{i}x_{i}^{2}/(2m)^{2}\geq 1/k
.

For the upper bounds, observe that
0\leq x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}=x
and
\sum_{i=1}^{k}x_{i}=2m
together imply that
x^{2}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}x_{i}^{2}\leq(2m/x)\,x^{2}=2mx
; and so
x^{2}/(2m)^{2}\leq q^{D}_{\mathcal{A}}(G)\leq x/2m
.
Similarly, supposing that
x_{k}=x
and
x_{i}\leq y
for
i=1,\ldots,k-1
, we have

\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}x_{i}^{2}\leq(2m-x)y\leq 2my
; and so

q^{D}_{\mathcal{A}}(G)\leq(x^{2}+2my)/(2m)^{2}=(x/2m)^{2}+y/2m
.
∎

Next we consider the connected components partition
\mathcal{C}
, which has edge contribution 1.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
0<\varepsilon<1
, let
c=1+\varepsilon
, and let
p=(c+o(1))/n
.
Then whp

1-\frac{16\varepsilon^{2}}{(1+\varepsilon)^{4}}\;<\;q_{\mathcal{C}}(G_{n,p})\;<\;1-\frac{16\varepsilon^{2}}{(1+\varepsilon)^{4}}\,(1-\sqrt{\varepsilon}).

Proof.

Let
f(x)=xe^{-x}
for
x>0
, and note that
f
is strictly increasing on
(0,1)
and strictly decreasing on
(1,\infty)
. Let
x=x(c)
be the unique root in
(0,1)
to
f(x)=f(c)
.
Let
m=e(G_{n,p})
, and let
X
be the maximum number of edges in a connected component of
G_{n,p}
, so
q_{\mathcal{C}}^{D}(G_{n,p})\geq(X/m)^{2}
. Now whp

(that is,
m=(1+o(1))cn/2
),

, and each component other than the giant has

[

]

,

q_{\mathcal{C}}(G_{n,p})=1-(X/m)^{2}+O((\log n)/n)\;\;\mbox{ whp};

and so

q_{\mathcal{C}}(G_{n,p})=1-(1-x^{2}/c^{2})^{2}+o(1)\;\;\mbox{ whp}.

(2.1)

Lower bound

We claim that

We claim that

Hence,

∎

3.1
Proof of lower bound

There is a simple reason why the modularity

Lemma 3.1

Assume that

q_{{\mathcal{A}}}(R_{n,\alpha,\beta})=\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}-\frac{1}{2}+o(\sqrt{\omega/n})=\frac{\alpha-\beta}{2(\alpha+\beta)}+o(\sqrt{\omega/n})\;\;\mbox{ whp};

(4.11)

and hence, by our choice of
\alpha
and
\beta
, whp
q_{{\mathcal{A}}}(R_{n,\alpha,\beta})=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{c}}+o(\sqrt{\omega/n})
.

Further, for any set
U
of vertices, let
{\rm isol}(U)
be the number of isolated vertices in
U
. Then for
i=\pm
,
{\rm isol}(V_{i})=\frac{1}{2}ne^{-c}+o(\sqrt{\omega n})
whp.
We may shuffle isolated vertices in a partition without changing the modularity, so whp we may modify
{\mathcal{A}}
to a balanced partition as required.

Balanced
k
-part partitions for
k\geq 3

Define the random graph
R_{n,\alpha,\beta,k}
by letting
\sigma_{v}
for
v\in V
be iid random variables, uniformly distributed on
[k]

model, and we then forget the labels. By Theorem 1 of
[

, for

Let

G_{n,c/n}
and
R_{n,\alpha,\beta,k}

are contiguous. Thus it is sufficient to show that whp we have the claimed partition in
R_{n,\alpha,\beta,k}
.

Let
V_{i}=\{v:\sigma_{v}=i\}
for each
i\in[k]
, and let
{\mathcal{A}}
be the partition into the sets
V_{i}
. We again use Chebyshev’s inequality repeatedly. For each
i\in[k]
, we have
|V_{i}|=\frac{1}{k}n+o(\sqrt{\omega n})
whp. Hence

i

q_{{\mathcal{A}}}(R_{n,\alpha,\beta,k})\geq\frac{\alpha/2k}{c/2}-\frac{1}{k}+o(\sqrt{\omega/n})=\frac{x}{k\sqrt{c}}+o(\sqrt{\omega/n});

and as before we may shuffle isolated vertices to obtain a balanced partition as required.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph, let
E_{0}
be a non-empty subset of
E
, let
E^{\prime}=E\setminus E_{0}
and let
G^{\prime}=(V,E^{\prime})
.
Then

|q^{*}(G)-q^{*}(G^{\prime})|<2\,|E_{0}|/|E|.

(5.1)

Proof.

Clearly we may assume that
G^{\prime}
has at least one edge. We shall prove a pair of inequalities which together are slightly stronger than (
5.1
). Let
\mathcal{A}
be any partition of
V
. Let
E_{1}
be the set of edges in
E_{0}
that lie within parts of
\mathcal{A}
; and let
E_{2}=E_{0}\setminus E_{1}
, the set of edges in
E_{0}
that lie between parts of
\mathcal{A}
. Let
\alpha=\alpha(\mathcal{A})=|E_{1}|/|E|
and
\beta=\beta(\mathcal{A})=|E_{2}|/|E|
. Then we make two claims:
we claim that

q_{\mathcal{A}}(G^{\prime})-q_{\mathcal{A}}(G)<2\alpha+2\beta,

(5.2)

and that

q^{*}(G)-q^{*}(G^{\prime})<2\alpha+\beta.

(5.3)

Let us first show that the claims (
5.2
) and (
5.3
) yield inequality (
5.1
).
Suppose first that
q^{*}(G^{\prime})\geq q^{*}(G)
. Then, by claim (
5.2
), if
\mathcal{A}
is an optimal partition for
G^{\prime}
,

{|q^{*}(G)-q^{*}(G^{\prime})|}=q_{\mathcal{A}}(G^{\prime})-q^{*}(G)\leq q_{\mathcal{A}}(G^{\prime})-q_{\mathcal{A}}(G)<2(\alpha+\beta)=2|E_{0}|/|E|,

so (
5.1
) holds.
Conversely, if
q^{*}(G)\geq q^{*}(G^{\prime})
, then (
5.1
) follows directly from claim (
5.3
).

It remains to prove the claims (
5.2
) and (
5.3
).
For the vertex partition
\mathcal{A}
we can calculate the difference in edge contribution between
G
and
G^{\prime}
precisely: we have

q^{E}_{\mathcal{A}}(G)=\frac{1}{|E|}\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}}e_{G}(A)=\frac{1}{|E|}\Big(\alpha|E|+\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}}e_{G^{\prime}}(A)\Big)=\alpha+(1-\alpha-\beta)q^{E}_{\mathcal{A}}(G^{\prime}).

Hence

q^{E}_{\mathcal{A}}(G)-q^{E}_{\mathcal{A}}(G^{\prime})=\alpha-(\alpha+\beta)q^{E}_{\mathcal{A}}(G^{\prime})

(5.4)

and in particular

q^{E}_{\mathcal{A}}(G^{\prime})-q^{E}_{\mathcal{A}}(G)\leq\beta.

(5.5)

Next we bound the possible decrease in degree tax as we move from
G
to
G^{\prime}
.
For each part
A_{i}\in\mathcal{A}
, let
\alpha_{i}=|E_{1}\cap E(A_{i})|/|E|
and
\beta_{i}=|E_{2}\cap E(A_{i},V\backslash A_{i})|/|E|
; and note that
\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}=\alpha
,
\sum_{i}\beta_{i}=2\beta
and for each
i
,
\beta_{i}\leq\beta
. We may now relate the volumes of any part
A_{i}
in
G
and
G^{\prime}
: we have

{\rm vol}_{G^{\prime}}(A_{i})={\rm vol}_{G}(A_{i})-(2\alpha_{i}+\beta_{i})|E|.

Thus

\displaystyle\sum_{i}{\rm vol}_{G}(A_{i})^{2}-\sum_{i}{\rm vol}_{G^{\prime}}(A_{i})^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i}({\rm vol}_{G}(A_{i})+{\rm vol}_{G^{\prime}}(A_{i}))({\rm vol}_{G}(A_{i})-{\rm vol}_{G^{\prime}}(A_{i}))

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle 2|E|\sum_{i}{\rm vol}_{G}(A_{i})(2\alpha_{i}+\beta_{i}).

Relabel parts in order of decreasing volume and let
\eta_{i}={\rm vol}_{G}(A_{i})/(2|E|)
, so
1\geq\eta_{1}\geq\eta_{2}\geq\ldots
and
\sum_{i}\eta_{i}=1
. Observe that
\sum_{i}\eta_{i}\alpha_{i}\leq\eta_{1}\alpha\leq\alpha
and
\sum_{i}\eta_{i}\beta_{i}\leq(\eta_{1}+\eta_{2})\beta\leq\beta
; so

\sum_{i}{\rm vol}_{G}(A_{i})^{2}-\sum_{i}{\rm vol}_{G^{\prime}}(A_{i})^{2}<4|E|^{2}\sum_{i}\eta_{i}(2\alpha_{i}+\beta_{i})\leq 4|E|^{2}(2\alpha+\beta).

Since
|E^{\prime}|\leq|E|
, this gives

q^{D}_{\mathcal{A}}(G)-q^{D}_{\mathcal{A}}(G^{\prime})<2\alpha+\beta;

and using also (
5.5
) we obtain claim (
5.2
).

Now we shall prove claim (
5.3
). To bound the possible
increase
in degree tax when we move from
G
to
G^{\prime}
, first observe that

\displaystyle q^{D}_{\mathcal{A}}(G)>\frac{1}{4|E|^{2}}\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}}{\rm vol}_{G^{\prime}}(A)^{2}=(1-\alpha-\beta)^{2}q^{D}_{\mathcal{A}}(G^{\prime})\,>(1-2(\alpha+\beta))\,q^{D}_{\mathcal{A}}(G^{\prime}),

and so

q^{D}_{\mathcal{A}}(G^{\prime})-q^{D}_{\mathcal{A}}(G)<2(\alpha+\beta)q^{D}_{\mathcal{A}}(G^{\prime}).

Together with (
5.4
) this gives

\displaystyle q_{\mathcal{A}}(G)-q_{\mathcal{A}}(G^{\prime})

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle\alpha-(\alpha+\beta)q^{E}_{\mathcal{A}}(G^{\prime})+2(\alpha+\beta)q^{D}_{\mathcal{A}}(G^{\prime}).

(5.6)

We shall use (
5.6
) to prove claim (
5.3
), namely that
q^{*}(G)-q^{*}(G^{\prime})<2\alpha+\beta
. Clearly we may assume that
q^{*}(G)>0
. Fix
\mathcal{A}
to be an optimal partition for
G
. There are two cases:
q_{\mathcal{A}}(G^{\prime})\geq 0
and
q_{\mathcal{A}}(G^{\prime})\leq 0
.

Case 1:
q_{\mathcal{A}}(G^{\prime})\geq 0
.

First note that
q^{*}(G)-q^{*}(G^{\prime})=q_{\mathcal{A}}(G)-q^{*}(G^{\prime})\leq q_{\mathcal{A}}(G)-q_{\mathcal{A}}(G^{\prime})
.
But by (
5.6
), and because in this case
q^{E}_{\mathcal{A}}(G^{\prime})\geq q^{D}_{\mathcal{A}}(G^{\prime})
,

q_{\mathcal{A}}(G)-q_{\mathcal{A}}(G^{\prime})<\alpha+(\alpha+\beta)\,q^{D}_{\mathcal{A}}(G^{\prime})\leq 2\alpha+\beta,

and claim (
5.3
) follows.

Case 2:
q_{\mathcal{A}}(G^{\prime})\leq 0
.

Since
q^{*}(G^{\prime})\geq 0
,

q^{*}(G)-q^{*}(G^{\prime})\leq q^{*}(G)=q_{\mathcal{A}}(G)=q_{\mathcal{A}}(G)-q_{\mathcal{A}}(G^{\prime})+q^{E}_{\mathcal{A}}(G^{\prime})-q^{D}_{\mathcal{A}}(G^{\prime}).

Hence by (
5.6
)

q^{*}(G)-q^{*}(G^{\prime})<\alpha+(1-\alpha-\beta)q^{E}_{\mathcal{A}}(G^{\prime})+(2\alpha+2\beta-1)q^{D}_{\mathcal{A}}(G^{\prime}),

and so by re-arranging,

q^{*}(G)-q^{*}(G^{\prime})<\alpha+(\alpha+\beta)q^{D}_{\mathcal{A}}(G^{\prime})-(1-\alpha-\beta)\big(q^{D}_{\mathcal{A}}(G^{\prime})-q^{E}_{\mathcal{A}}(G^{\prime})\big).

But
\alpha+\beta\leq 1
and (in this case)
q^{D}_{\mathcal{A}}(G^{\prime})\geq q^{E}_{\mathcal{A}}(G^{\prime})
, so

q^{*}(G)-q^{*}(G^{\prime})<\alpha+(\alpha+\beta)q^{D}_{\mathcal{A}}(G^{\prime})\leq 2\alpha+\beta.

This completes the proof of claim (
5.3
), and hence of the lemma.
∎

There is a similar bound when our two graphs have the same number of edges. The following result will be used in the proof of Theorem
7.1
, which concerns concentration of modularity in
G_{n,p}
and
G_{n,m}
.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
G=(V,E)
and
G^{\prime}=(V,E^{\prime})
be distinct graphs on the same vertex set
V
, each with
m\geq 1
edges. Then

|q^{*}(G)-q^{*}(G^{\prime})|\,<\,\frac{|E\triangle E^{\prime}|}{m}.

Proof.

It suffices to consider the case when
|E\triangle E^{\prime}|=2
. Write
E\triangle E^{\prime}=\{e,e^{\prime}\}
where
e\in E\backslash E^{\prime}
and
e^{\prime}\in E^{\prime}\backslash E
.
Let
{\mathcal{A}}
be a partition of
V
, and suppose wlog that
q_{\mathcal{A}}(G)\leq q_{\mathcal{A}}(G^{\prime})
. It suffices to show that

q_{\mathcal{A}}(G^{\prime})<q_{\mathcal{A}}(G)+\frac{2}{m}.

(5.7)

We consider two cases, depending on whether the edge
e
is internal or external in
{\mathcal{A}}
.

Suppose first that
e
lies within some part
A
in
{\mathcal{A}}
. Then
q^{E}_{\mathcal{A}}(G^{\prime})\leq q^{E}_{\mathcal{A}}(G)
. Also, if
x={\rm vol}(A)
then
x\leq 2m
and so

q^{D}_{\mathcal{A}}(G)-q^{D}_{\mathcal{A}}(G^{\prime})<\frac{x^{2}-(x-2)^{2}}{4m^{2}}=\frac{4x-4}{4m^{2}}<\frac{x}{m^{2}}\leq\frac{2}{m}.

Thus (
5.7
) holds in this case.

Now suppose that
e
lies between parts
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
of
{\mathcal{A}}
.
Then
q^{E}_{\mathcal{A}}(G^{\prime})-q^{E}_{\mathcal{A}}(G)\leq\frac{1}{m}
.
Let
{\rm vol}(A_{1})=x_{1}
and
{\rm vol}(A_{2})=x_{2}
. Then
x_{1}+x_{2}\leq 2m
, so much as before

q^{D}_{\mathcal{A}}(G)-q^{D}_{\mathcal{A}}(G^{\prime})<\frac{x_{1}^{2}-(x_{1}-1)^{2}+x_{2}^{2}-(x_{2}-1)^{2}}{4m^{2}}=\frac{2(x_{1}+x_{2})-2}{4m^{2}}<\frac{1}{m}.

Hence again (
5.7
) holds, and we are done.
∎

The following lemma extends Lemmas
5.1
and
5.2
(note that if
|E|=|E^{\prime}|
then
2|E\backslash E^{\prime}|=|E\Delta E^{\prime}|
).

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
G=(V,E)
and
G^{\prime}=(V,E^{\prime})
be distinct graphs on the same vertex set
V
with
|E|\geq|E^{\prime}|
. Then

|q^{*}(G)-q^{*}(G^{\prime})|\,<\,\frac{2|E\backslash E^{\prime}|}{|E|}.

Proof.

Let
F
be a set of
|E|-|E^{\prime}|
elements of
E\setminus E^{\prime\prime}
, where
E^{\prime\prime}=E\cap E^{\prime}
. Let
H
be the graph on
V
with edge set
E^{\prime}\cup F
, with
|E|
edges.
By Lemma
5.1
,
|q^{*}(H)-q^{*}(G^{\prime})|\leq 2|F|/|E|
; and by Lemma
5.2
,
|q^{*}(G)-q^{*}(H)|\leq|E\triangle(E^{\prime}\cup F)|/|E|=2(|E^{\prime}|-|E^{\prime\prime}|)/|E|
. Further, either
H\neq G^{\prime}
or
H\neq G
(or both), so at least one of these inequalities is strict.
Hence

|q^{*}(G)\!-\!q^{*}(G^{\prime})|<\frac{2|F|\!+\!2(|E^{\prime}|\!-\!|E^{\prime\prime}|)}{|E|}=\frac{2(|E|\!\!-\!|E^{\prime\prime}|)}{|E|}=\frac{2|E\backslash E^{\prime}|}{|E|},

as required.
∎

It is not clear how tight these bounds are. Let
\alpha^{*}
be the infimum of
\alpha
such that for all distinct graphs
G=(V,E)
and
G^{\prime}=(V,E^{\prime})
with
|E|\geq|E^{\prime}|
we have
|q^{*}(G)-q^{*}(G^{\prime})|<\alpha|E\backslash E^{\prime}|/|E|
.
By Lemma
5.3

we have
\alpha^{*}\leq 2
. The following example shows that
\alpha^{*}\geq\frac{3}{2}
.
Let
G
consist of 3 disjoint edges, with
q^{*}(G)=\frac{2}{3}
. By moving one edge we can form
G^{\prime}
consisting of a 3-edge path and 2 isolated vertices, with
q^{*}(G^{\prime})=\frac{2}{3}-\frac{1}{2}=\frac{1}{6}
. Thus

|q^{*}(G)-q^{*}(G^{\prime})|=\frac{1}{2}=\frac{3}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{3}
.

6.1

The main task of this subsection is prove that the modularity of a graph is bounded above by the spectral gap of the normalised Laplacian. We begin with a definition. Following Chung

7
]
, for a graph
G
on vertex set
[n]
, with adjacency matrix
A_{G}
and vertex degrees
d_{1},\ldots,d_{n}>0
, define the
degrees matrix

D
to be the diagonal matrix
\mbox{diag}(d_{1},\ldots,d_{n})
and the
normalised Laplacian
to be
\mathcal{L}=I-D^{-1/2}A_{G}D^{-1/2}
. Here
D^{-1/2}
is
\mbox{diag}(d_{1}^{-1/2},\ldots,d_{n}^{-1/2})
.
Denote the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{L}
by
0=\lambda_{0}\leq\ldots\leq\lambda_{n-1}\,(\leq 2)
, see
[
7
]
. We call

\max_{i\neq 0}|1-\lambda_{i}|=\max\{|1-\lambda_{1}|,|\lambda_{n-1}-1|\}

the
spectral gap
of
G
,
and denote it by
\bar{\lambda}(G)
.
(In terms of the eigenvalues
\tilde{\lambda}_{0}\geq\cdots\geq\tilde{\lambda}_{n-1}
of
D^{-1/2}A_{G}D^{-1/2}
, we have
\tilde{\lambda}_{i}=1-\lambda_{i}
and so
\bar{\lambda}(G)=\max_{i\neq 0}|\tilde{\lambda}_{i}|=\max\{|\tilde{\lambda}_{1}|,|\tilde{\lambda}_{n-1}|\}
.)

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
G
be a graph with at least one edge and no isolated vertices.
Then

q_{{\mathcal{A}}}(G)\leq\bar{\lambda}(G)\,(1-1/k)\leq\bar{\lambda}(G)

for each
k
-part vertex partition
{\mathcal{A}}
, and so
q^{*}(G)\leq\bar{\lambda}(G)
.

In the special case of
r
-regular graphs, Lemma
6.1
may be written in terms of the spectrum of the adjacency matrix
A_{G}
,
r=\lambda_{0}(A_{G})\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{n-1}(A_{G})
, since
\bar{\lambda}(G)=\frac{1}{r}\max_{i\neq 0}|\lambda_{i}(A_{G})|
. This special case of Lemma
6.1
is already known, see
[
47
,
15
]
, and was used to prove upper bounds on the modularity of random regular graphs in
[
33
]
and
[
44
]
.

The proof of Lemma
6.1
relies on a corollary of the Discrepancy Inequality, Theorem 5.4 of
[
7
]
, which is an extension of the Expander-Mixing Lemma to non-regular graphs. Write
\bar{S}=V\backslash S
where
V=V(G)
.

Lemma 6.2

(Corollary 5.5 of
[
7
]
)
.

Let
G
be a graph with at least one edge and no isolated vertices. Then for each
S\subseteq V

e(S,\bar{S})\geq(1-\bar{\lambda}(G))\,{\rm vol}(S){\rm vol}(\bar{S})/{\rm vol}(G).

Proof of Lemma
6.1
.

Let
G
have
m\geq 1
edges.
Let
{\mathcal{A}}=\{A_{1},\ldots,A_{k}\}
be a vertex partition of
G
. Lemma
6.2
guarantees many edges between the parts of
{\mathcal{A}}
. The edge contribution satisfies

1-q_{\mathcal{A}}^{E}(G)=\frac{1}{2m}\sum_{i}e(A_{i},\bar{A_{i}})\geq(1-\bar{\lambda})\frac{1}{4m^{2}}\sum_{i}{\rm vol}(A_{i}){\rm vol}(\bar{A_{i}});

and

\frac{1}{4m^{2}}\sum_{i}{\rm vol}(A_{i}){\rm vol}(\bar{A_{i}})=\frac{1}{4m^{2}}\sum_{i}{\rm vol}(A_{i})(2m-{\rm vol}(A_{i}))=1-q^{D}_{\mathcal{A}}(G).

Hence

1-q^{E}_{\mathcal{A}}(G)\geq(1-\bar{\lambda})(1-q^{D}_{\mathcal{A}}(G)),

and so

q_{\mathcal{A}}(G)=q^{E}_{\mathcal{A}}(G)-q^{D}_{\mathcal{A}}(G)\leq\bar{\lambda}(1-q^{D}_{\mathcal{A}}(G))\leq\bar{\lambda}(1-\tfrac{1}{k})

(since
q_{{\mathcal{A}}}^{D}(G)\geq 1/k
by Lemma
2.1
). This completes the proof.
∎

6.2
The
b(np)^{-1/2}
upper bound on the modularity
q^{*}(G_{n,p})
.

We are now ready to prove the spectral upper bound for
q^{*}(G_{n,p})
. Let us restate the upper bound in Theorem
1.3
as a lemma. (Observe that Lemma
6.3
implies part (c) of Theorem
1.1
.)

Lemma 6.3
.

There is a constant
b
such that for
\>0<p=p(n)\leq 1

q^{*}(G_{n,p})\leq\frac{b}{\sqrt{np}}\;\;\;\mbox{ whp}.

Proof.

Notice first that it suffices to show that there exist
c_{0}
and
b
such that for
np\geq c_{0}
whp
q^{*}(G_{n,p})\leq b/\sqrt{np}
, and then replace
b
by
\max\{\sqrt{c_{0}},b\}
.

For
p\gg\log^{2}n/n
,
the result follows directly from Lemma
6.1
, and Theorem 3.6 of Chung, Vu and Lu
[
8
]
(see also (1.2) in
[
9
]
), which shows that

\bar{\lambda}(G_{n,p})\leq 4(np)^{-1/2}(1+o(1))\;\;\mbox{whp}.

For the remainder of the proof we assume that
c_{0}/n\leq p\leq 0.99
for some large constant
c_{0}\geq 1
. We will use the spectral bound in Lemma
6.1
on a subgraph
H
which is obtained from the random graph
G=G_{n,p}
by deleting a small subset of the vertices (and the incident edges).

Following the construction in
[
9
]
, let
H
be the induced subgraph of
G
obtained as follows.

•

Initially set
H=G\setminus\{v\in V(G)\;:\;d_{v}<(n-1)p/2\}
.

•

While there is a vertex
v\in V(H)
with at least 100 neighbours in
V(G)\setminus V(H)
, remove
v
from
H
.

Let
V^{\prime}
be the set of deleted vertices, and let
E^{\prime}
be the set of deleted edges (the edges incident with vertices in
V^{\prime}
). Then by Theorem 1.2 of Coja-Oghlan
[
9
]
, assuming that
c_{0}
is sufficiently large, there are positive constants
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
such that whp
|V^{\prime}|\leq ne^{-np/c_{2}}
and
\bar{\lambda}(H)\leq c_{1}(np)^{-1/2}
.

We want a bound on
|E^{\prime}|
, not
|V^{\prime}|
.
By the proof of Corollary 2.3 in
[
9
]
, whp in
G_{n,p}
we have

{\rm vol}(S)\leq 2np|S|+ne^{-np/1500}
simultaneously for each set
S
of vertices. (The result is stated with
{\rm vol}(S)
replaced by
|N_{G}(S)|
, the number of neighbours of
S
outside
S
, but the proof actually shows the result for
{\rm vol}(S)
.)
Hence, noting also that
np\geq 1
and setting
c_{3}=\max\{c_{2},1500\}
, whp

|E^{\prime}|\leq{\rm vol}(V^{\prime})\leq 2n^{2}p\,e^{-np/c_{2}}+ne^{-np/1500}\leq 3n^{2}p\,e^{-np/c_{3}}\leq e(G)\cdot 9e^{-np/c_{3}},

where the last inequality follows since whp
e(G)\geq n^{2}p/3
. By making
c_{0}
larger if necessary we can ensure that
9e^{-np/c_{3}}\leq\frac{1}{3}\,(np)^{-1/2}
. Now, by Lemma
6.1
, whp

q^{*}(G\setminus E^{\prime})=q^{*}(H)\leq\bar{\lambda}(H)\leq c_{1}(np)^{-1/2}.

Hence, by Lemma
5.1
, whp

q^{*}(G)\leq q^{*}(G\setminus E^{\prime})+2|E^{\prime}|/e(G)\leq(c_{1}+1)\,(np)^{-1/2},

and the proof is complete.
∎

Remark 6.4
.

The upper bound on
q^{*}(G_{n,p})
just proven implies that modularity values will whp distinguish the stochastic block model from the Erdős-Rényi model, when the probabilities are only a constant factor past the detectability threshold, as we now explain. Consider the stochastic block model in which there is a hidden partition of the vertex set into two parts
V_{-}
and
V_{+}
, and the edges are placed with probability
p
inside these parts and with probability
p^{\prime}<p
between the parts – see Section
4.2
for the definition. It is a challenge to distinguish this model from the Erdős-Rényi random graph with the same expected edge density, and there are theoretical limits on how close the edge probabilities
p
and
p^{\prime}
can be for this to be possible
[
39
]
.

Suppose that the planted partition has edge probabilities
p=\alpha/n
(inside parts) and
p^{\prime}=\beta/n
(between parts) for constants
\alpha>\beta
; and denote the random graph by
R_{n,\alpha,\beta}
. As shown earlier, see (
4.11
), the modularity score of the planted partition itself is whp
(\alpha\!-\!\beta)/2(\alpha\!+\!\beta)+o(1)
.
By Theorem
1.3
there is a constant
b
such that, for the Erdős-Rényi random graph with edge probability
(\alpha\!+\!\beta)/2n
, the maximum modularity is whp less than
b\,\sqrt{2/(\alpha\!+\!\beta)}
. Thus for
(\alpha\!-\!\beta)^{2}>8b^{2}(\alpha\!+\!\beta)
, whp the modularity score of the planted partition in
R_{n,\alpha,\beta}
is higher than that of any partition of the Erdős-Rényi random graph
G_{n,(\alpha+\beta)/2n}
. In particular, for
(\alpha\!-\!\beta)^{2}>8b^{2}(\alpha\!+\!\beta)
, if the procedure is to flip a coin and sample the stochastic block model
R_{n,\alpha,\beta}
if heads and Erdős-Rényi random graph
G_{n,(\alpha\!+\!\beta)/2n}
if tails, then whp the modularity of the random graph would tell us the outcome of the coin flip, i.e. it distinguishes the stochastic block model from the Erdős-Rényi model.

The theoretical lower bound for detectability is
(\alpha\!-\!\beta)^{2}\geq 2(\alpha\!+\!\beta)

[
39
]
. Thus at a constant factor, namely
4b^{2}
, past the detectability threshold, modularity whp distinguishes the stochastic block model from the Erdős-Rényi model.

7
Concentration and expectation of
q^{*}(G_{n,p})

We shall see in Theorem
7.1
that the modularity of our random graphs
is highly concentrated about the expected value. We use the result for
q^{*}(G_{n,m})
to deduce that for
q^{*}(G_{n,p})
.

Theorem 7.1
.

(a) Given
n\geq 1
and
0\leq m\leq\binom{n}{2}
, for each
t>0

{\mathbb{P}}\Big(\big|q^{*}(G_{n,m})-{\mathbb{E}}[q^{*}(G_{n,m})]\big|\geq t\Big)<2e^{-t^{2}m/2}.

(b) There is a constant
\eta>0
such that for each
n\geq 1
and each
0<p<1
the following holds, with
\mu=\mu(n,p)=\binom{n}{2}p
. For each
t\geq 0

{\mathbb{P}}\Big(\big|\,q^{*}(G_{n,p})-{\mathbb{E}}[q^{*}(G_{n,p})]\,\big|\geq t\Big)<2\,e^{-\eta\mu t^{2}}.

For example, we may use part (b) to consider separately small and large deviations for
q^{*}(G_{n,p})
.

Corollary 7.2
.

Let
c>0
be a constant, let
p=p(n)
satisfy
np\geq c
.
Then the variance of
q^{*}(G_{n,p})
is
O(1/n)
, so for any function
\omega(n)\rightarrow\infty

\left|q^{*}(G_{n,p})-{\mathbb{E}}[q^{*}(G_{n,p})]\right|\leq\frac{\omega(n)}{\sqrt{n}}\;\;\mbox{whp};

and, for any fixed
\varepsilon>0
,

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\left|q^{*}(G_{n,p})-{\mathbb{E}}[q^{*}(G_{n,p})]\right|\geq\varepsilon\right)=e^{-\Omega(n)}.

The only part of Corollary
7.2
that is not immediate is to check that the variance is as claimed. Let
n\geq 3
, and let
X=q^{*}(G_{n,p})-{\mathbb{E}}[q^{*}(G_{n,p})]
. Then, for each
t>0
, by Theorem
7.1
(b) (and noting that

\mu/n\geq\tfrac{1}{2}c(n\!-\!1)/n\geq\tfrac{1}{3}c
),

{\mathbb{P}}(nX^{2}\geq t)={\mathbb{P}}(|X|\geq\sqrt{t/n})\leq 2\,e^{-\eta\mu t/n}\leq 2e^{-\tfrac{1}{3}\eta c\,t}.

It follows that
{\mathbb{E}}[nX^{2}]
is at most some constant
\alpha
, and so
{\rm var}(q^{*}(G_{n,p}))\leq\alpha/n
, as required.

To prove Theorem
7.1
we make use of Lemmas
5.1
and
5.2
which bound the sensitivity of modularity to changes in the edge set. We also use the following concentration result from
[
30
]
Theorem 7.4 (see also Example 7.3) or Theorem 3.3 of
[
37
]
.

Lemma 7.3
.

Let
A
be a finite set, let
a
be an integer such that
0\leq a\leq|A|
, and consider the set
\binom{A}{a}
of all
a
-element subsets of
A
.
Suppose that the function
f:\binom{A}{a}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
satisfies
|f(S)-f(T)|\leq c
whenever
|S\triangle T|=2
(i.e. the
a
-element subsets
S
and
T
are minimally different). If the random variable
X
is uniformly distributed over
\binom{A}{a}
, then

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\bigl|f(X)-{\mathbb{E}}[f(X)]\bigr|\geq t\right)\leq 2e^{-2t^{2}/ac^{2}}.

Recall from Lemma
5.2
that if
E(G)
and
E(G^{\prime})
are both of size
m
and are minimally different then
|q^{*}(G)-q^{*}(G^{\prime})|<2/m
. Hence, Lemma
7.3
with
a=m
and
c=2/m
immediately yields
part (a) of Theorem
7.1
.

Proof of Theorem
7.1
part (b).

Let
G\sim G_{n,p}
. Let
M=e(G)
and let
\mu=\mu(n,p)={\mathbb{E}}[M]=\binom{n}{2}p
.
We will first show the more detailed statement that,
for each
t\geq 42/\sqrt{\mu}
, we have

{\mathbb{P}}\big(\big|q^{*}(G)-{\mathbb{E}}[q^{*}(G)]\big|\geq t\big)\leq 5e^{-t^{2}\mu/103},

(7.1)

from which we will deduce part (b) of the theorem easily. Clearly we may assume that
0\leq t\leq 1
. Define the event
\mathcal{E}=\{M>2\mu/3\}
. Now, letting
{\mathcal{E}}^{c}
denote the complement of
{\mathcal{E}}
,

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!{\mathbb{P}}\big(\big|q^{*}(G)\!-\!{\mathbb{E}}[q^{*}(G)]\big|\geq t\big)

(7.2)

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbb{P}}\big(\big(\big|q^{*}(G)\!-\!{\mathbb{E}}[q^{*}(G)|M]\big|\geq\tfrac{t}{2}\big)\land{\mathcal{E}}\big)+{\mathbb{P}}\big(\big|{\mathbb{E}}[q^{*}(G)|M]\!-\!{\mathbb{E}}[q^{*}(G)]\big|\geq\tfrac{t}{2}\big)\land{\mathcal{E}}\big)+{\mathbb{P}}({\mathcal{E}}^{c}).

The proof proceeds by bounding separately the terms on the right in (
7.2
).

Firstly, by using part (a) of the theorem and conditioning on
M=m
where
m>2\mu/3
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\big(\big(\,\big|\,q^{*}(G)-{\mathbb{E}}[q^{*}(G)|M]\,\big|\geq\tfrac{t}{2}\big)\land{\mathcal{E}}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\exp(-\tfrac{1}{2}(\tfrac{t}{2})^{2}(\tfrac{2\mu}{3}))=2\exp(-\tfrac{t^{2}\mu}{12}).

(7.3)

We now work towards a bound of the second term of (
7.2
). Let
G^{\prime}\sim G_{n,p}
independently of
G
, and let
M^{\prime}=e(G^{\prime})
. By Lemma
5.1
and a simple coupling argument, for
0<m\leq\binom{n}{2}

\Big|{\mathbb{E}}\big[q^{*}(G)|M=m]-{\mathbb{E}}[q^{*}(G^{\prime})|M^{\prime}=m^{\prime}]\Big|\leq\frac{2|m-m^{\prime}|}{\max\{m,m^{\prime}\}}\leq\frac{2|m-m^{\prime}|}{m}.

Also, for any
x
and any random variable
Y
(with finite mean) we have

\big|x-{\mathbb{E}}[Y]\big|\leq{\mathbb{E}}_{Y}[|x-Y|]
.
Thus, for
0<m\leq\binom{n}{2}
,

\displaystyle\big|{\mathbb{E}}[q^{*}(G)|M=m]-{\mathbb{E}}(q^{*}(G^{\prime}))\big|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{M^{\prime}}\big[\big|{\mathbb{E}}[q^{*}(G)|M=m]-{\mathbb{E}}[q^{*}(G^{\prime})|M^{\prime}]\big|\big]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(2/m)\,{\mathbb{E}}_{M^{\prime}}[|m-M^{\prime}|]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(2/m)\,\big(|m-\mu|+{\mathbb{E}}[|M^{\prime}-\mu|]\big)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(2/m)\,\big(|m-\mu|+\sqrt{\mu}\big),

since

{\mathbb{E}}[|M^{\prime}-\mu|]\leq\sqrt{{\mathbb{E}}[(M^{\prime}-\mu)^{2}]}\leq\sqrt{\mu}
. Hence

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\Big(\big(\big|{\mathbb{E}}[q^{*}(G)|M]-{\mathbb{E}}[q^{*}(G)]\big|\geq\tfrac{t}{2}\big)\land{\mathcal{E}}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\Big(\big(|M-\mu|+\sqrt{\mu}\geq\tfrac{tM}{4}\big)\land{\mathcal{E}}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\Big(|M-\mu|\geq\tfrac{t\mu}{6}-\sqrt{\mu}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\Big(|M-\mu|\geq\tfrac{t\mu}{7}\Big)

since we assumed that
t\geq 42\mu^{-1/2}
and so
t\mu/6-\sqrt{\mu}\geq t\mu/7
.
But by a Chernoff inequality (see for example Theorem 2.1 of
[
22
]
)

{\mathbb{P}}\Big(|M-\mu|\geq\frac{t\mu}{7}\Big)\leq 2\exp\Big(-\frac{(t/7)^{2}\mu}{2(1+\tfrac{1}{3}(t/7))}\Big)\;\;\leq\;\;2\exp\Big(-\frac{t^{2}\mu}{103}\Big),

so

{\mathbb{P}}\Big(\big(\big|{\mathbb{E}}[q^{*}(G)|M]-{\mathbb{E}}[q^{*}(G)]\big|\geq\tfrac{t}{2}\big)\land{\mathcal{E}}\Big)\leq 2\exp\Big(-\frac{t^{2}\mu}{103}\Big).

(7.4)

Finally, by a Chernoff inequality (again see Theorem 2.1 of
[
22
]
),

{\mathbb{P}}({\mathcal{E}}^{c})\leq\exp(-\tfrac{1}{2}(\tfrac{1}{3})^{2}\mu)=\exp(-\mu/18)\leq\exp(-t^{2}\mu/18).

This inequality, together with (
7.2
), (
7.3
) and (
7.4
), yields (
7.1
).
It remains to use (
7.1
) to deduce the statement in part (b) of the theorem.
Let
a=e^{42^{2}/103}(\geq 5)
. Then
ae^{-t^{2}\mu/103}\geq 1
for
0\leq t\leq 42/\sqrt{\mu}
; and so by (
7.1
)

{\mathbb{P}}\big(\big|q^{*}(G)-{\mathbb{E}}[q^{*}(G)]\big|\geq t\big)\leq ae^{-t^{2}\mu/103}\;\mbox{ for all }t\geq 0.

Now let
\beta=\log_{2}a
, so
\beta>1
and
2(1/a)^{1/\beta}=1
. Thus
2y^{1/\beta}\geq 1
for each
y\geq 1/a
. Also, letting
f(y)=2y^{1/\beta}-ay
for
y\geq 0
, we have
f(y)\geq 0
for each

, since
f(0)=f(1/a)=0
and

is increasing then decreasing. Therefore

\min\{1,ay\}\leq 2y^{1/\beta}\;\;\mbox{ for all }y\geq 0.

Hence, letting
\eta=1/(103\beta)
, we have

{\mathbb{P}}\big(\big|q^{*}(G)-{\mathbb{E}}[q^{*}(G)]\big|\geq t\big)\leq 2e^{-\eta\mu t^{2}}\;\mbox{ for all }t\geq 0,

as required.

We may use the first robustness lemma, Lemma
5.1
, to show that the expected modularity of a random graph with edge probability

is similar to that of a random graph with edge probability

when

is near

(and

is large). At the moment it is an open question whether the expected modularity

tends to a limit

as

. However, if such a limit

did exist then Lemma

would show that

is uniformly continuous in

.

Lemma 7.4
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
. If
n^{2}p\rightarrow\infty
and
p\leq p^{\prime}\leq(1+\tfrac{\varepsilon}{3})\,p
then for
n
sufficiently large,

\Big|{\mathbb{E}}[q^{*}(G_{n,p})]-{\mathbb{E}}[q^{*}(G_{n,p^{\prime}})]\Big|<\varepsilon.

Proof.

First let us consider very small
p
and large
p
. If
p\leq 1/n
then whp
q^{*}(G_{n,p})>1-\varepsilon/2
by Theorem
1.1
(a), and so for large enough
n
we have
{\mathbb{E}}[q^{*}(G_{n,p})]>1-\varepsilon
.
On the other hand, for large
p
, by Theorem
1.3
there exists a constant
K=K(\varepsilon)
so that for
p\geq K/n
whp
q^{*}(G_{n,p})<\varepsilon/2
and so for large enough
n
,
{\mathbb{E}}[q^{*}(G_{n,p})]<\varepsilon
. Hence we may assume that
p,p^{\prime}=\Theta(1/n)
.

To sample
G_{n,p^{\prime}}
we may first sample edges with probability

and then independently resample with probability
p^{\prime\prime}=(p^{\prime}-p)/(1-p)
. Write
\mathcal{G}_{n}
for the set of all graphs on vertex set
[n]
. For
H,H^{\prime}\in\mathcal{G}_{n}
write
H\cup H^{\prime}
to denote the (simple) graph with vertex set
[n]
and edge set

. Then

{\mathbb{E}}\big(q^{*}(G_{n,p})-q^{*}(G_{n,p^{\prime}})\big)=\!\!\sum_{H\in\mathcal{G}_{n}}\!\!{\mathbb{P}}(G_{n,p}=H)\!\!\sum_{H^{\prime}\in\mathcal{G}_{n}}\!\!{\mathbb{P}}(G_{n,p^{\prime\prime}}=H^{\prime})(q^{*}(H)-q^{*}(H\cup H^{\prime})).

(7.5)

Let
p^{+}=\tfrac{\varepsilon}{3}\tfrac{p}{1-p}
, and note that
p^{\prime\prime}\leq p^{+}
.
Let
J
be the event that
e(G_{n,p})\geq\tfrac{19}{20}\cdot\binom{n}{2}p
and
e(G_{n,p^{\prime\prime}})\leq\tfrac{21}{20}\cdot\binom{n}{2}p^{+}
; and notice that, for large enough

, event

occurs with probability at least

.
If
J
holds, and
n
is sufficiently large that

, then the number of edges added in the second exposure is a small proportion of those already there:

e(G_{n,p^{\prime\prime}})<\tfrac{7}{18}\varepsilon\,e(G_{n,p})

and we can apply Lemma

. Hence by (

)

|{\mathbb{E}}\big[q^{*}(G_{n,p})-q^{*}(G_{n,p^{\prime}})\big]|<\tfrac{7}{9}\varepsilon+{\mathbb{P}}(J^{c})<\varepsilon,

which completes the proof.
∎

8
Concluding remarks

In this section, we briefly describe what we have done in this paper; mention some other current work; and present two questions, one concerning modularity just above the threshold, and one question inspired by the statistical physics literature concerning partitions with few parts.

The definition of modularity is most well-fitted to graphs that are reasonably sparse.
We have given quite a full picture of the behavior of the modularity of the
random graphs
G_{n,p}
and
G_{n,m}
, for a wide range of densities. We have not looked in detail here inside the critical window, when the giant component is forming. Also, we have not looked in detail here at the very dense case: that is done in the companion paper
[
36
]
, which in particular investigates the threshold when the modularity drops to exactly 0, and finds that this happens when the complementary graph has average degree 1. Another companion paper
[
34
]
investigates the maximum and minimum modularity of graphs with given numbers of edges or given density. These results help to set in context the results given here on the modularity of random graphs.
A further related paper ‘Modularity and edge-sampling’
[
35
]
considers the situation where there is an unknown underlying graph
G
on a large vertex set, and we can test only a proportion
p
of the possible edges to check whether they are present in
G
. It investigates how large
p
should be so that
the modularity of the observed graph
G^{\prime}
is likely to give good upper or lower bounds on
q^{*}(G)
.

We refer the reader also to
[
33
]
for open questions on regular graphs. For any 3-regular graph the modularity is at least
2/3\!-\!o(1)
and the modularity of a random 3-regular graph
q^{*}(G_{n,3})
is whp in the range
(0.66,0.81)
see
[
33
,
44
]
. Is it possible to bound
q^{*}(G_{n,3})
strictly above
2/3+\varepsilon
whp? Is it the case that of 3-regular graphs the random graph has asymptotically the lowest modularity?

Modularity just above the threshold

In Theorem
1.2
part (iii) and comments at the end of Section
3
, we learned about the behaviour of
q^{*}(G_{n,p})
when
np
is just above the threshold value 1. We saw that if
np=1+t
with
t>0
and small, then whp the modularity deficit
1-q^{*}(G_{n,p})
has order between
t^{2}
and about
t^{3}
. It would be interesting to learn more about this threshold behaviour.

Conjecture 8.1
.

If
np=1+t
with
t>0
sufficiently small, then whp

1-q^{*}(G_{n,p})=\Theta(t^{2}).

In full detail, the conjecture is that there exist
t_{0}>0
and
0<a<b
such that for each fixed
0<t\leq t_{0}
, whp
at^{2}\leq 1-q^{*}(G_{n,(1+t)/n})\leq bt^{2}
.

For the connected components partition
\cal C
, by Lemma
2.2
there exists
t_{0}>0
such that for each
0<t\leq t_{0}
, whp

15t^{2}<1-q_{\mathcal{C}}(G_{n,p})<16t^{2}.

Thus Conjecture
8.1
would imply that whp
\cal C
has modularity deficit (that is, 1 minus its modularity score) of optimal order in terms of
t
, although by
[
46
]
we know that whp
\cal C
is not the optimal partition in this range.

Do few parts suffice?

Corollary
1.4
confirmed the
c^{-1/2}
growth rate conjectured for the modularity of
G_{n,c/n}
by Reichardt and Bornholdt
[
45
]
. In that paper, it was also conjectured that the optimal partition would have five parts. This is not exactly true, since every optimal partition for a graph must have at least as many parts as there are connected components of size at least 2, and whp there are linearly many isolated edges in
G_{n,c/n}
. However, an approximate version of the prediction may be correct: perhaps whp there is a partition with only five parts which has modularity score close to the optimum. Let us explore further.

Given a graph
G
and a positive integer
k
, let
q_{\leq k}(G)
be the maximum modularity score of a vertex partition with at most
k
parts; that is,
q_{\leq k}(G)=\max_{|{\mathcal{A}}|\leq k}q_{\mathcal{A}}(G)
. By Lemma 1 of
[
12
]
, for every graph
G
and positive integer
k
,

q_{\leq k}(G)\geq q^{*}(G)\,(1-1/k).

(8.1)

On the other hand, we shall see that for every
c>0
there is a constant
\delta=\delta(c)>0
, such that for each positive integer
k

q_{\leq k}(G_{n,c/n})\leq q^{*}(G_{n,c/n})\,(1-\delta/k)\;\;\mbox{ whp}.

(8.2)

To prove this, let
G
be any graph with at least one edge, and let
{\mathcal{A}}=(A_{1},\ldots,A_{k^{\prime}})
(where
k^{\prime}\leq k
) be a partition achieving the optimal modularity score over all partitions with at most
k
parts. Suppose that
G
has components
C_{1},C_{2},\ldots
ordered by decreasing volume. We focus on
C_{1}
.
Let
a_{j}={\rm vol}(A_{j}\cap V(C_{1}))
and
b_{j}={\rm vol}(A_{j}\backslash V(C_{1}))
for
j=1,\ldots,k^{\prime}
. Then

\sum_{j}((a_{j}+b_{j})^{2}-a_{j}^{2})=\sum_{j}(2a_{j}b_{j}+b_{j}^{2})>\sum_{j}b_{j}^{2}\geq(\sum_{j}b_{j})^{2}/k\,=\tfrac{1}{k}{\rm vol}^{2}(G\,\backslash\,C_{1}).

Hence

{\rm vol}^{2}(G)\cdot q^{D}_{{\mathcal{A}}}(G)=\sum_{j}(a_{j}+b_{j})^{2}\geq\sum_{j}a_{j}^{2}+\tfrac{1}{k}{\rm vol}^{2}(G\,\backslash\,C_{1}).

Let
\mathcal{B}
be the vertex partition with parts the non-empty sets
A_{i}\cap V(C_{1})
together with the parts
V(C_{2})
,
V(C_{3})
,
\ldots
and
observe that
q_{\mathcal{B}}^{E}(G)\geq q_{{\mathcal{A}}}^{E}(G)
. Thus,

{\rm vol}^{2}(G)\cdot q^{D}_{\mathcal{B}}(G)=\sum_{j}a_{j}^{2}+\sum_{i\geq 2}{\rm vol}^{2}(C_{i}).

Hence

q^{*}(G)-q_{\leq k}(G)\geq q_{\mathcal{B}}(G)-q_{{\mathcal{A}}}(G)\geq q_{{\mathcal{A}}}^{D}(G)-q_{\mathcal{B}}^{D}(G)\geq\frac{{\rm vol}^{2}(G\,\backslash\,C_{1})}{k\,{\rm vol}^{2}(G)}-\frac{\sum_{i\geq 2}{\rm vol}^{2}(C_{i})}{{\rm vol}^{2}(G)}.

But when
G\sim G_{n,c/n}
, the second term tends to 0 in probability; and
{\rm vol}(G\,\backslash\,C_{1})/{\rm vol}(G)
tends in probability to a constant
y=y(c)>0
(where
y=1-x^{2}/c^{2}
in the notation in Lemma
2.2
) so the first term above tends in probability to
y^{2}/k
.
Thus, if we let
\delta=\frac{1}{2}y^{2}>0
, then

q_{\leq k}(G_{n,c/n})\leq q^{*}(G_{n,c/n})-\delta/k\leq q^{*}(G_{n,c/n})\,(1-\delta/k)\;\;\mbox{ whp},

and we have proved (
8.2
).

In the spirit of (
8.1
), and despite (
8.2
), we propose the following amended version of the ‘five parts’ conjecture of Reichardt and Bornholdt
[
45
]
.

Conjecture 8.2
.

There exist a positive integer
k
with the property that, for each
\varepsilon>0
there exists
c_{0}
such that, if
c\geq c_{0}
then

q_{\leq k}(G_{n,c/n})\geq q^{*}(G_{n,c/n})\,(1-\varepsilon)\;\;\mbox{ whp}.

Observe that, by (
8.1
), this inequality must hold if
\varepsilon\geq 1/k
. We are conjecturing that there is some finite number
k
of parts (perhaps
k=5
?) such that whp an optimal partition over the restricted class with at most
k
parts achieves modularity score at least
(1-\varepsilon(c))
times the (unrestricted) optimal value, where
\varepsilon(c)\to 0
as
c\to\infty
.

Appendix A

In this appendix we use the robustness lemma, Lemma

{\rm Bin}(n,p)
.

Lemma A.1
.

If
0<\varepsilon\leq 1
,
X\sim{\rm Bin}(n,p)
,
\sigma^{2}=np(1-p)
and
\varepsilon\sigma\geq 1
, then
{\mathbb{P}}(|X-np|\leq\varepsilon\sigma)\geq\varepsilon/8
.

Proof.

If
k\geq np
then
{\mathbb{P}}(X=k)\geq{\mathbb{P}}(X=k+1)
, and if
k\leq np
then
{\mathbb{P}}(X=k)\geq{\mathbb{P}}(X=k-1)
.
Also, for any
x>0
,
\big|{\mathbb{Z}}\cap(np,np+x]\big|
is either
\lfloor x\rfloor
or
\lceil x\rceil
. Hence

\frac{{\mathbb{P}}(np<X\leq np+\varepsilon\sigma)}{{\mathbb{P}}(np<X\leq np+2\sigma)}\geq\frac{\big|{\mathbb{Z}}\cap(np,np+\varepsilon\sigma]\big|}{\big|{\mathbb{Z}}\cap(np,np+2\sigma]\big|}\geq\frac{\lfloor\varepsilon\sigma\rfloor}{\lceil 2\sigma\rceil}.

But
\lfloor\varepsilon\sigma\rfloor>\varepsilon\sigma/2
since
\varepsilon\sigma\geq 1
, and

\lceil 2\sigma\rceil<2\sigma+1\leq 3\sigma
since
\sigma\geq 1
. Hence

{\mathbb{P}}(np<X\leq np+\varepsilon\sigma)\geq(\varepsilon/6)\,{\mathbb{P}}(np<X\leq np+2\sigma).

Similarly

{\mathbb{P}}(np-\varepsilon\sigma\leq X<np)\geq(\varepsilon/6)\,{\mathbb{P}}(np-2\sigma\leq X<np).

Adding the last two inequalities we find

{\mathbb{P}}(0<|X-np|\leq\varepsilon\sigma)\geq(\varepsilon/6)\,{\mathbb{P}}(0<|X-np|\leq 2\sigma)

and since
\varepsilon/6\leq 1
it follows that

{\mathbb{P}}(|X-np|\leq\varepsilon\sigma)\geq(\varepsilon/6)\,{\mathbb{P}}(|X-np|\leq 2\sigma).

But
{\mathbb{P}}(|X-np|\leq 2\sigma)\geq 1-(1/4)
by Chebyshev’s inequality, so

{\mathbb{P}}(|X-np|\leq\varepsilon\sigma)\geq(\varepsilon/6)(3/4)=\varepsilon/8,

as required.
∎
