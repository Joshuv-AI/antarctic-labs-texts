Sharpness and non-sharpness of occupation measure bounds for integral variational problems Thanks: August 8, 2026.
Giovanni Fantuzzi, Ian Tobasco

Abstract
We analyze two recently proposed methods to establish a priori lower bounds on the minimum of general integral variational problems. The methods, which involve either ‘occupation measures’ or a ‘pointwise dual relaxation’ procedure, are shown to produce the same lower bound under a coercivity hypothesis ensuring their strong duality. We then show by a minimax argument that the methods actually evaluate the minimum for classes of one-dimensional, scalar-valued, or convex multidimensional problems. For generic problems, however, these methods should fail to capture the minimum and produce non-sharp lower bounds. We demonstrate this using two examples, the first of which is one-dimensional and scalar-valued with a non-convex constraint, and the second of which is multidimensional and non-convex in a different way. The latter example emphasizes the existence in multiple dimensions of nonlinear constraints on gradient fields that are ignored by occupation measures, but are built into the finer theory of gradient Young measures.



keywords

Occupation measures, gradient Young measures, relaxation, convex duality, calculus of variations

Introduction

Integral variational problems arise throughout mathematics and the applied sciences. A typical problem takes the form

\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in W^{1,p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})\\
+\text{constraints}\end{subarray}}\int_{\Omega}f(x,u,\nabla u)\,\,{\rm d}x,

(1)

where the minimization is over the space of Sobolev maps
W^{1,p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})
consisting of all
u:\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
with
p
-integrable dervatives
(\nabla u)_{ij}=\smash{\frac{\partial u_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}}
for
i=1,\dots,m
,
j=1,\dots,n
.
It is generally difficult to find the minimum value
\mathcal{F}^{*}
of
eq.

1
. Traditional optimization algorithms based on gradient or Newton methods compute stationary points, which need not be global minimizers when the problem is non-convex. Put another way, knowledge of a stationary point provides at best an upper bound
U\geq\mathcal{F}^{*}
in the general case. To complete the picture, one desires methods for proving
a priori
lower bounds
L\leq\mathcal{F}^{*}
, whose values can be compared with upper bounds to estimate the minimum from above and below. Finding a lower bound is non-trivial, since it requires addressing general properties of admissible maps. Standard approaches include the ‘translation method’ from the theory of composite materials
[
16
,
27
]
, or the ‘calibration method’, which has been used in geometry
[
10
,
13
]
.

This paper discusses a recent method for finding lower bounds on integral variational problems based on the use of ‘occupation measures’
[
20
,
21
]
(see also
[
5
]
).
The basic idea is to replace the original problem
eq.

1
with a convex minimization problem posed over measures obtained via a push-forward operation. The resulting ‘occupation measure relaxation’ bound
\mathcal{F}_{\rm omr}
is never larger than the original minimum
\mathcal{F}^{*}
.
A natural question is whether
\mathcal{F}_{\rm omr}=\mathcal{F}^{*}
; the answer, as we shall show, depends on the nature of the problem.
As we were preparing this article for submission, we became aware of related progress in
[
21
,
17
]
. Our results are the same in some cases, but differ in general. Specific comparisons to help the reader sort through what is known about the sharpness/non-sharpness of the occupation measures method appear later in this introduction and throughout the article.

The idea of relaxing a variational problem posed over maps into one involving other quantities is much older than the notion of occupation measures, and dates back at least to Young’s generalized curves
[
40
]
. In the modern calculus of variations, the notions of Young measures and gradient Young measures
[
32
,
33
,
34
]
are nowadays used to find measure-theoretic ‘relaxed problems’ that are well-posed and whose optimal values are guaranteed to capture the original minimum,
\mathcal{F}^{*}
.
Related to this is the notion of quasiconvex functions (in the sense of Morrey
[
28
]
), which are the natural class of integrands for which minimizers are gauranteed to exist
[
12
,
28
,
34
]
. It is well known that to find a sharp lower bound on a general integral functional one can replace the integrand with its quasiconvex hull. Measure-theoretically, this is the same as extending the minimization from Sobolev maps to couples of maps and their compatible gradient Young measures.
Although these measures have been characterized through Jensen-type inequalities involving quasiconvex (in addition to convex) integrands
[
19
]
, the lack of a ‘local’ test for quasiconvexity
[
22
]
complicates applications of this result. Notable exceptions include one-dimensional (
n=1
) and scalar-valued (
m=1
) problems, for which quasiconvexity reduces to convexity. In these cases, gradient Young measures have been explored numerically as a way to approximate global minima
[
4
,
6
,
14
,
18
,
23
,
24
,
25
,
26
]
.

Occupation measures are instead defined via linear constraints regardless of the dimensions
n
and
m
of the problem. This has been motivated
[
20
]
by the possibility of using semidefinite programming to compute the optimal lower bound
\mathcal{F}_{\rm omr}\leq\mathcal{F}^{*}
provable with occupation measures, for polynomial integrands and constraints. However, this reliance on linear constraints should mean that
\mathcal{F}_{\rm omr}\neq\mathcal{F}^{*}
for generic non-convex problems, and especially in the vectorial case where
n,m\geq 2
. Our key point is that, without extra hypotheses, the set of occupation measures for a given problem (‘relaxed occupation measures’ in the language of
[
21
]
) should be strictly larger than the set of gradient Young measures. Since the latter give sharp lower bounds, the former should not, except in particular cases. We illustrate this with a well-known example from the theory of relaxed variational problems, involving a non-convex ‘double-well’ potential
f(\nabla u)
built with pairs of incompatible matrices (see
section

7
). A separate example of non-sharpness appeared in
[
21
]
, which, in contrast to ours, uses an integrand
f(x,u,\nabla u)
that is convex in
\nabla u
and non-convex in
u
.

Due to the examples, structural hypotheses are needed for the occupation measure relaxation of a variational problem to be sharp. Given known results on the sharpness of gradient Young measure relaxations
[
32
,
33
,
34
]
, we expect occupation measures to produce sharp lower bounds (
\mathcal{F}_{\rm omr}=\mathcal{F}^{*}
) if the integrand
f(x,u,\nabla u)
is convex in
\nabla u
and if
n
or
m
is equal to one. Surprisingly, this remains an open problem.
Here, using the Legendre transform, we prove that
\mathcal{F}_{\rm omr}=\mathcal{F}^{*}
for integrands
f(x,u,\nabla u)=f_{0}(x,\nabla u)+f_{1}(x,u)
where
f_{0}
and
f_{1}
are convex in
\nabla u
and
u
, and
n
and
m
are arbitrary. After this paper was submitted, such sharpness was proved for
f(x,u,\nabla u)
that are jointly convex in
u
and
\nabla u
, and for arbitrary
n,m

[
17
]
. (Convexity is not necessary for sharpness, however, as occupation measures produce sharp lower bounds for some non-convex problems
[
11
]
.)

Finally, we address a general drawback of the occupation measures approach: it requires the solution of an infinite-dimensional linear program to produce even a single bound (sharp or not). The ‘pointwise dual’ approach from
[
11
]
, instead, leads to a linear program over continuous functions where admissible functions give lower bounds. In
section

4
, we show that these approaches are dual, i.e., they are the min-max and max-min versions of a saddle-point problem; in particular, the occupation measures bound
\mathcal{F}_{\rm omr}
is greater than or equal to the best pointwise dual lower bound
\mathcal{L}_{\rm pdr}
. Under a suitable coercivity hypothesis for the original minimization problem guaranteeing its optimal value is finite, we prove the strong duality
\mathcal{L}_{\rm pdr}=\mathcal{F}_{\rm omr}
.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows.
Section

2
states the general class of integral minimization problems we study.
Section

3
reviews the occupation measures approach to proving lower bounds, following
[
20
]
.
Section

4
derives the alternative pointwise dual relaxation from
[
11
]
via a minimax argument, and proves the weak and strong duality results
\mathcal{F}_{\rm omr}\geq\mathcal{L}_{\rm pdr}
and
\mathcal{F}_{\rm omr}=\mathcal{L}_{\rm pdr}
(the former always holds, the latter holds under a coercivity hypothesis).
Section

5
proves the sharpness result
\mathcal{F}^{*}=\mathcal{F}_{\rm omr}=\mathcal{L}_{\rm pdr}
for various convex or convexifiable problems by exhibiting an optimal pointwise dual bound.
In general, when sharpness holds, one can find optimality conditions differing from the usual Euler–Lagrange ones; we explain this briefly in
section

6
.

Section

7
presents our two counterexamples in which
\mathcal{F}^{*}>\mathcal{F}_{\rm omr}
, and includes a discussion of the relationship between occupation measures and gradient Young measures.
Section

8
concludes with some final remarks.

2
Setup

This paper studies general integral variational problems of the form

\mathcal{F}^{*}:=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in W^{1,p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})\\
\text{\lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{e:constraints-general-1}--\lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{e:constraints-general-3}}\end{subarray}}\;\int_{\Omega}f(x,u,\nabla u)\,\,{\rm d}x+\int_{\partial\Omega}g(x,u)\,{\rm d}S.

(2)

Here,
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is an open bounded Lipschitz domain with boundary
\partial\Omega
, and
\,{\rm d}x
and
\,{\rm d}S
are the usual volume and
(n-1)
-dimensional surface measures. The minimization is over weakly differentiable functions
u:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
in the Sobolev space
W^{1,p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})
with some fixed
p\in(1,\infty)
, subject to the constraints

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}a(x,u,\nabla u)\,{\rm d}x+\int_{\partial\Omega}b(x,u)\,{\rm d}S=0,

(3a)

\displaystyle c(x,u,\nabla u)=0\quad\text{ a.e. on }\Omega,

(3b)

\displaystyle d(x,u)=0\quad\text{ a.e. on }\partial\Omega.

(3c)

The functions
f,a,c:\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}\to\mathbb{R}
and
g,b,d:\partial\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}
are assumed to be continuous, but not necessarily bounded.
Moreover,
f
,
g
,
a
and
b
are assumed to grow no faster than a degree-
p
polynomial in the second and third arguments, e.g.

\left|f(x,u,F)\right|\lesssim 1+|u|^{p}+|F|^{p}\quad\text{a.e. }x\in\Omega.

(4)

This last restriction ensures that all integrals above are well-defined.

3
Occupation and boundary measures

As explained in
[
20
]
, lower bounds on the optimal value of problem
eq.

2
can be derived by first posing the minimization over so-called
occupation
and
boundary measures
generated by admissible functions
u
, and then by convexifying this measure-theoretic problem into a linear program over a larger set of measures.
This section reviews the derivation of this linear program to set the stage for the remainder of the article.

3.1
Measures generated by Sobolev functions

The
occupation measure

\mu
generated by
u\in W^{1,p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})
is the pushforward of the Lebesgue measure on
\Omega
by the map
x\mapsto(x,u(x),\nabla u(x))
from
\Omega
into
\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
. Likewise, the
boundary measure

\nu
generated by
u
is the pushforward of the surface measure on
\partial\Omega
by the map
x\mapsto(x,u(x))
from
\partial\Omega
into
\partial\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{m}
. Precisely,

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}h(x,u(x),\nabla u(x))\,\,{\rm d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}}h(x,y,z)\,{\rm d}\mu(x,y,z)

(5a)

\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Omega}\ell(x,u(x))\,\,{\rm d}S

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{m}}\ell(x,y)\,{\rm d}\nu(x,y)

(5b)

for all continuous functions
h\in C(\overline{\Omega}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{m\times n})
and
\ell\in C(\partial\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{m})
satisfying
p
-growth bounds analogous to
eq.

4
. To lighten the notation we denote integration against measures using angled brackets:

\displaystyle\langle h,\mu\rangle

\displaystyle:=\int_{\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}}h(x,y,z)\,{\rm d}\mu(x,y,z)

(6a)

\displaystyle\langle\ell,\nu\rangle

\displaystyle:=\int_{\partial\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{m}}\ell(x,y)\,{\rm d}\nu(x,y)

(6b)

Occupation and boundary measures generated by Sobolev functions subject to the constraints
eq.

3
of the variational problem
eq.

2
satisfy a number of conditions, such as moment bounds. We summarize these conditions now. Define the sets

\displaystyle\Gamma

\displaystyle:=\{(x,y,z)\in\overline{\Omega}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}:\,c(x,y,z)=0\},

(7a)

\displaystyle\Lambda

\displaystyle:=\{(x,y)\in\partial\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{m}:\,d(x,y)=0\}.

(7b)

We use standard multi-index notation, such as
x^{\alpha}=x_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots x_{n}^{\alpha_{n}}
for a multivariate monomial and
\left|\alpha\right|=\alpha_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{n}
for the order of the multi-index
\alpha
.

Lemma 1
.

Let
\mu
and
\nu
be the occupation and boundary measures generated by a function
u\in W^{1,p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})
that satisfies the constraints
eq.

3
. Then,
\mu
and
\nu
have bounded moments of order
p
or less, i.e.,

\displaystyle\langle x^{\alpha}y^{\beta}z^{\gamma},\,\mu\rangle<\infty\quad\forall(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)\in\mathbb{N}^{n}\times\mathbb{N}^{m}\times\mathbb{N}^{m\times n}\text{ s.t. }|\beta|+|\gamma|\leq p,

(8a)

\displaystyle\langle x^{\alpha}y^{\beta},\,\nu\rangle<\infty\quad\forall(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathbb{N}^{n}\times\mathbb{N}^{m}\text{ s.t. }|\beta|\leq p.

(8b)

Moreover,

\displaystyle\langle a,\mu\rangle+\langle b,\nu\rangle=0,

(9a)

\displaystyle\textstyle\langle h,\mu\rangle=\int_{\Omega}h(x)\,{\rm d}x\quad\forall h\in C(\overline{\Omega}),

(9b)

\displaystyle\textstyle\langle\ell,\nu\rangle=\int_{\partial\Omega}\ell(x)\,{\rm d}S\quad\forall\ell\in C(\partial\Omega),

(9c)

\displaystyle\supp(\mu)\subseteq\Gamma,

(9d)

\displaystyle\supp(\nu)\subseteq\Lambda.

(9e)

Proof.

The boundedness of all moments of order
p
or less follows from the definition of
\mu
and
\nu
as pushforward measures of functions in
W^{1,p}
.
Conditions
eqs.

9b
and
9c
follow from
eqs.

5a
and
5b
when
h
and
\ell
depend only on
x
.
The same two identities applied to the integral constraint
eq.

3a
yield
eq.

9a
. To see that
\supp(\mu)\subseteq\Gamma
, assume by contradiction that there exists a set

K\subset\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}

such that
\mu(K)>0
and
c(x,y,z)\neq 0
on
K
. Then, writing
\chi_{K}
for the indicator function of
K
, we obtain the contradiction

0<\langle\chi_{K},\,\mu\rangle=\int_{\Omega}\chi_{K}(x,u(x),\nabla u(x))\,{\rm d}x=0.

(10)

The last equality holds because
u
satisfies
eq.

3b
and, therefore,
\chi_{K}(x,u(x),\nabla u(x))=0
a.e. on
\Omega
. A similar argument proves that
\supp(\nu)\subseteq\Lambda
.

A second group of constraints is derived from the divergence theorem and, loosely speaking, encodes the fact that occupation and boundary measures are constructed using
u
as well as its gradient
\nabla u
. Define the vector space

\displaystyle\Phi^{p}:=\bigg\{\varphi\in C^{1}(\overline{\Omega}\times\mathbb{R}^{m};\mathbb{R}^{n}):\;

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left|\tfrac{\partial}{\partial y_{j}}\varphi_{i}(x,y)\right|\lesssim 1+\left|y\right|^{p-1},

(11)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|\varphi_{i}(x,y)\right|+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|\tfrac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}\varphi_{i}(x,y)\right|\lesssim 1+\left|y\right|^{p}\bigg\},

where
f\lesssim g
means
f\leq Cg
for some constant
C>0
. This space is clearly not empty; for example, it contains all degree-
p
polynomials of
y
whose coefficients depend on
x
and are uniformly bounded on
\overline{\Omega}
.
For any
\varphi\in\Phi^{p}
, define the
total divergence

\mathcal{D}\varphi:\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}\to\mathbb{R}
as

\mathcal{D}\varphi(x,y,z):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}\varphi_{i}(x,y)+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{j}}\varphi_{i}(x,y)\,z_{ji}.

(12)

This definition is such that
\mathcal{D}\varphi(x,u(x),\nabla u(x))=\nabla\cdot\varphi(x,u(x))
when the right-hand side is calculated using the chain rule. The divergence theorem then gives

\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{D}\varphi(x,u,\nabla u,s)\,\,{\rm d}x=\int_{\partial\Omega}\varphi(x,u,s)\cdot\hat{n}(x)\,\,{\rm d}S,

(13)

where
\hat{n}(x)
is the outward unit vector normal to the boundary of
\Omega
at
x
. Applying
eqs.

5a
and
5b
to this identity we find the second group of constraints on occupation and boundary measures generated by Sobolev functions.

Lemma 2
.

Let
\mu
and
\nu
be occupation and boundary measures generated by
u\in W^{1,p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})
, and let
\Phi^{p}
be the vector space of functions in
eq.

11
. Then,

\langle\mathcal{D}\varphi,\mu\rangle-\langle\varphi\cdot\hat{n},\nu\rangle=0\qquad\forall\varphi\in\Phi^{p}.

(14)

3.2
Lower bounds via occupation measures

We now apply the definitions above to bound the minimum of problem
eq.

2
from below. First, observe that

\mathcal{F}^{*}=\inf_{(\mu,\,\nu)}\left\{\langle f,\mu\rangle+\langle g,\nu\rangle\right\},

(15)

where the minimization is over all pairs
(\mu,\nu)
of occupation and boundary measures generated by functions
u
admissible for
eq.

2
.
To bound
\mathcal{F}^{*}
from below, we simply extend the minimization to all pairs
(\mu,\nu)
in the convex set defined by the conditions in
lemmas

1
and
2
, which we call occupation and boundary measures. (Note our terminology departs slightly from
[
20
,
21
]
, where such measures are called
relaxed
occupation and boundary measures.)

Precisely, let
\mathcal{M}^{p}(\Gamma)
and
\mathcal{M}^{p}(\Lambda)
be the convex cones of Radon measures that are supported on the sets
\Gamma
and
\Lambda
defined in
eqs.

7a
and
7b
, and whose moments of order
p
or less are bounded as in
eq.

8
. Let

\mathbb{M}^{p}:=\mathcal{M}^{p}(\Gamma)\times\mathcal{M}^{p}(\Lambda).

(16)

The set of
occupation and boundary measures
is defined here as

\mathbb{O}^{p}:=\left\{(\mu,\nu)\in\mathbb{M}^{p}:\;\text{\lx@cref{creftypeplural\lx@tilde refnum}{e:mu-nu-equalities}, \lx@cref{refnum}{e:marginal-constraints-mu}, and~\lx@cref{refnum}{e:marginal-constraints-nu} and \lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{e:divergence-constraints-measures}}\right\}.

(17)

It is clearly convex, as it is defined using linear and one-sided constraints, and it contains all occupation and boundary measures generated by Sobolev functions
u
admissible in
eq.

2
.

Extending the minimization in
eq.

15
to
\mathbb{O}^{p}
yields an infinite-dimensional linear program whose minimum bounds
\mathcal{F}^{*}
from below:

\mathcal{F}^{*}\geq\inf_{(\mu,\nu)\in\mathbb{O}^{p}}\;\left\{\langle f,\mu\rangle+\langle g,\nu\rangle\right\}=:\mathcal{F}_{\rm omr}.

(18)

The rest of this article analyzes the
occupation measure relaxation

eq.

18
with an eye towards understanding whether it is or is not sharp, i.e., if
\mathcal{F}_{\rm omr}=\mathcal{F}^{*}
or not.

4
A dual scheme for proving lower bounds

Calculating the occupation measure relaxation bound
\mathcal{F}_{\rm omr}
amounts to solving an infinite-dimensional linear program posed over measures. In principle, this admits a dual problem posed over continuous functions. The advantage of this dual problem is that any admissible choice of functions proves a lower bound on
\mathcal{F}_{\rm omr}
.
Section

4.1
derives the dual problem via a minimax argument, leading to a ‘weak’ duality result.
Section

4.2
improves this to a ‘strong’ duality under suitable conditions. When strong duality holds,
\mathcal{F}_{\rm omr}
is computed by the dual maximization.

4.1
Weak duality

Consider the vector space of continuous functions

\mathbb{V}:=\Phi^{p}\times\mathbb{R}\times C(\overline{\Omega})\times C(\partial\Omega),

(19)

where
\Phi^{p}
is defined in
eq.

11
. Consider also the convex subset

\displaystyle\mathbb{B}:=\{(\varphi,\eta,h,\ell)\in\mathbb{V}:\;F^{\varphi,\eta,h}(x,y,z)

\displaystyle\geq 0\;\text{on }\Gamma,\;G^{\varphi,\eta,\ell}(x,y)

\displaystyle\geq 0\;\text{on }\Lambda\},

(20)

where

\displaystyle F^{\varphi,\eta,h}(x,y,z)

\displaystyle:=f(x,y,z)+\mathcal{D}\varphi(x,y,z)-\eta\cdot a(x,y,z)-h(x),

(21a)

\displaystyle G^{\varphi,\eta,\ell}(x,y)

\displaystyle:=g(x,y)-\varphi(x,y)\cdot\hat{n}(x)-\eta\cdot b(x,y)-\ell(x).

(21b)

A straightforward minimax argument yields the following weak duality result. (See
[
11
]
for a derivation of the dual problem that avoids the use of measures.)

Theorem 3
.

The optimal value
\mathcal{F}_{\rm omr}
of the linear program
eq.

18
satisfies

\mathcal{F}_{\rm omr}\geq\sup_{(\varphi,\eta,h,\ell)\in\mathbb{B}}\left\{\int_{\Omega}h(x)\,{\rm d}x+\int_{\partial\Omega}\ell(x)\,{\rm d}S\right\}=:\mathcal{L}_{\rm pdr}.

(22)

Proof of
theorem

3
.

Define a Lagrangian function
\mathscr{L}:\mathbb{M}^{p}\times\mathbb{V}\to\mathbb{R}
as

\mathscr{L}[(\mu,\nu),(\varphi,\eta,h,\ell)]:=\langle F^{\varphi,\eta,h},\mu\rangle+\langle G^{\varphi,\eta,\ell},\nu\rangle+\int_{\Omega}h(x)\,\,{\rm d}x+\int_{\partial\Omega}\ell(x)\,\,{\rm d}S.

(23)

Recalling the definition of
\mathcal{F}_{\rm omr}
from
eq.

18
, it suffices to establish that

\displaystyle\inf_{(\mu,\nu)\in\mathbb{O}^{p}}\,\left\{\langle f,\mu\rangle+\langle g,\nu\rangle\right\}

\displaystyle=\;\;\inf_{(\mu,\nu)\in\mathbb{M}^{p}}\;\sup_{(\varphi,\eta,h,\ell)\in\mathbb{V}}\;\mathscr{L}[(\mu,\nu),(\varphi,\eta,h,\ell)]

(24a)

\displaystyle\geq\sup_{(\varphi,\eta,h,\ell)\in\mathbb{V}}\;\inf_{(\mu,\nu)\in\mathbb{M}^{p}}\;\mathscr{L}[(\mu,\nu),(\varphi,\eta,h,\ell)]\phantom{\int_{\Omega}}

(24b)

\displaystyle=\sup_{(\varphi,\eta,h,\ell)\in\mathbb{B}}\,\left\{\int_{\Omega}h(x)\,\,{\rm d}x+\int_{\partial\Omega}\ell(x)\,\,{\rm d}S\right\}.

(24c)

The equality in
eq.

24a
holds because the supremum on the right-hand side is equal to
\langle f,\mu\rangle+\langle g,\nu\rangle
if
(\mu,\nu)\in\mathbb{O}^{p}
, whereas it is infinite otherwise.
The former statement is an immediate consequence of the definition of
\mathscr{L}
and of the constraints defining
\mathbb{O}^{p}
.
For the latter, observe that at least one condition from
eq.

9a
,
eq.

9b
,
eq.

9c
and
eq.

14
fails when
(\mu,\nu)\notin\mathbb{O}^{p}
.
Assume for definiteness that the condition being violated is
eq.

14
; similar arguments apply to the other cases.
Then, there exists
\varphi_{0}\in\Phi^{p}
such that
\langle\mathcal{D}\varphi_{0},\mu\rangle-\langle\varphi_{0}\cdot\hat{n},\nu\rangle=C\neq 0
, and we may take
C>0
by replacing
\varphi_{0}
with
-\varphi_{0}
if necessary. Since
(k\varphi_{0},0,0,0)
is in
\mathbb{V}
for all
k\in\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\sup_{(\varphi,\eta,h,\ell)\in\mathbb{V}}\;\mathscr{L}[(\mu,\nu),(\varphi,\eta,\xi,h,\ell)]

\displaystyle\geq\mathscr{L}[(\mu,\nu),(k\,\varphi_{0},0,0,0)]

\displaystyle=\langle f+k\,\mathcal{D}\varphi_{0},\mu\rangle+\langle g-k\,\varphi_{0}\cdot n,\nu\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle f,\mu\rangle+\langle g,\nu\rangle+kC.

Letting
k\to+\infty
shows that the supremum on the left-hand side is unbounded, as claimed. Identity
eq.

4.2
Strong duality

The weak duality achieved above provides lower bounds on
\mathcal{F}_{\rm omr}
through the choice of admissible functions for

\mathcal{F}_{\rm omr}=\mathcal{L}_{\rm pdr}

So far, such sharpness has been reported for particular problems where in fact

Theorem 4
.

The equality

\displaystyle\quad\text{on }\Gamma,

(25a)

\displaystyle g(x,y)-\varphi_{0}(x,y)\cdot\hat{n}(x)\geq\beta\left(\left|y\right|^{q}-1\right)

\displaystyle\quad\text{on }\Lambda,

(25b)

\displaystyle\left|a(x,y,z)\right|\leq\beta\left(\left|y\right|^{r}+\left|z\right|^{r}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\text{on }\Gamma,

(25c)

\displaystyle\left|b(x,y)\right|\leq\beta\left|y\right|^{r}

\displaystyle\quad\text{on }\Lambda.

(25d)

Here, we include

5
Sharpness of occupation measure bounds

We now ask whether
\mathcal{F}_{\rm omr}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\rm pdr}
are sharp lower bounds on the optimal value
\mathcal{F}^{*}
of the original minimization problem

\mathcal{F}^{*}=\mathcal{F}_{\rm omr}=\mathcal{L}_{\rm pdr}
. These identities are proved in
[

for particular classes of quadratic problems. Here, we establish a different sharpness result covering problems in the form

\mathcal{F}^{*}=\inf_{u\in W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})}\int_{\Omega}f_{0}(x,\nabla u)+f_{1}(x,u)\,\,{\rm d}x

(37)

where
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a bounded Lipschitz domain,

is subject only to the homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions

, and the functions
f_{0}
,

are continuous in both arguments and convex in their second arguments.

Theorem 5
.

Consider the variational problem
eq.

37

\Omega
is a bounded Lipschitz domain,

f_{0}
and

are continuous. Suppose that:

1.

The functions

and

are convex for every

2.

There exist constants

such that

\displaystyle c_{1}\left|z\right|^{p}-c_{2}\leq f_{0}(x,z)

\displaystyle\leq c_{3}(\left|z\right|^{p}+1)\quad\forall x\in\Omega,

\displaystyle c_{1}\left|y\right|^{p}-c_{2}\leq f_{1}(x,y)

\displaystyle\leq c_{3}(\left|y\right|^{p}+1)\quad\forall x\in\Omega.

Then,
\mathcal{F}^{*}=\mathcal{F}_{\rm omr}=\mathcal{L}_{\rm pdr}
. Furthermore, the maximization in
eq.

22

of the form

Remark 6

The choice

.

Remark 7
.

We use the homogeneous boundary condition
u=0
for simplicity; one can handle non-zero boundary conditions
u=u_{0}
by writing the minimization in terms of
v=u-u_{0}
, for suitably regular
u_{0}
. We leave this to the reader.

Remark 8
.

The coercivity and growth conditions on
f_{1}
in
2
can be replaced with the assumption that
f_{1}(x,y)=F(x)\cdot y
for some smooth function
F
with straightforward changes to the proof.

Remark 9
.

A more general version of
theorem

5
appeared in
[
17
]
after this paper was submitted. It allows for integrands
f(x,u,\nabla u)
that are jointly convex in
u
and
\nabla u
, as well as for certain convex constraints.

The proof of
theorem

5
is given in
section

5.2
and uses three technical lemmas established in
section

5.1
. There, we make extensive use of the Legendre transforms

\displaystyle f_{0}^{*}(x,z^{*})=\sup_{z\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}}\{z\cdot z^{*}-f_{0}(x,z)\},

\displaystyle f_{1}^{*}(x,y^{*})=\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\{y\cdot y^{*}-f_{1}(x,y)\}.

First, however, we show that the convexity assumption on
f_{0}
can be removed for one-dimensional or scalar variational problems simply by replacing
f_{0}
with its convexification (double Legendre transform)
f_{0}^{**}
. Thus, when
n=1
or
m=1
the occupation measure and pointwise dual relaxations are sharp even when the integrand in
eq.

37
is not convex in the gradient term.

Corollary 10
.

Consider the variational problem
eq.

37
where
\Omega
is a bounded Lipschitz domain,
p>1
, and
n=1
or
m=1
. If the growth conditions
2
hold and the function
y\mapsto f_{1}(x,y)
is convex for every
x\in\Omega
, then
\mathcal{F}^{*}=\mathcal{F}_{\rm omr}=\mathcal{L}_{\rm pdr}
.
Moreover, the maximization in
eq.

22
can be restricted as explained in
theorem

5
.

Remark 11
.

The result extends to functions
f_{1}
that do not satisfy condition
2
, but are linear in
y
as explained in
remark

8
. This is the case for the one-dimensional example in §5.3 of
[
11
]
, where numerical lower bounds on
\mathcal{L}_{\rm pdr}
computed using semidefinite programming agree with
\mathcal{F}^{*}
to high accuracy.

Remark 12
.

The convexification argument used to prove
corollary

10
cannot be applied to remove the convexity assumption on
f_{1}
without changing the minimum in general. A simple counterexample is the one-dimensional scalar problem

\mathcal{F}^{*}:=\inf_{u(\pm 1)=0}\int_{-1}^{1}\left|\frac{{\rm d}u}{{\rm d}x}\right|^{2}+\left|u-1\right|\left|u+1\right|\,\,{\rm d}x,

(38)

where
f_{0}(x,z)=z^{2}
is convex but
f_{1}(x,y)=\left|y-1\right|\left|y+1\right|
is not. It is clear that
\mathcal{F}^{*}
is strictly positive.
On the other hand, since

f_{1}^{**}(x,y)=\begin{cases}\left|y-1\right|\left|y+1\right|&\text{if }\left|y\right|\geq 1\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(39)

the minimum of the convexified functional
\int_{-1}^{1}\left|\frac{{\rm d}u}{{\rm d}x}\right|^{2}+f_{1}^{**}(x,u)\,\,{\rm d}x
is zero.

Proof of
corollary

10
.

It is known (see, e.g.,
[
12
, Theorem 9.8]
) that when
n=1
or
m=1
we can replace
f_{0}
with its convexification
f_{0}^{**}
without changing
\mathcal{F}^{*}
, i.e.

\mathcal{F}^{*}=\inf_{u\in W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})}\int_{\Omega}f_{0}^{**}(x,\nabla u)+f_{1}(x,u)\,\,{\rm d}x.

To prove the corollary we apply
theorem

5
and the standard identity
f_{0}^{***}=f_{0}^{*}
. For this, we must check that
f_{0}^{**}
satisfies the same growth and coercivity conditions as
f_{0}
, possibly with different constants
c_{1}
,
c_{2}
and
c_{3}
. This follows from the definition of the Legendre transform and the growth and coercivity conditions on
f_{0}
. Indeed, by the coercivity of
f_{0}
,

f_{0}^{*}(x,z^{*}):=\sup_{z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\{z^{*}\cdot z-f_{0}(x,z)\}\leq\sup_{z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\{z^{*}\cdot z-c_{1}\left|z\right|^{p}\}+c_{2}\\
=\frac{1}{q}\left(\frac{1}{pc_{1}}\right)^{\frac{1}{p-1}}\left|z^{*}\right|^{q}+c_{2}\leq c_{4}\left(\left|z^{*}\right|^{q}+1\right),

(40)

where
q=p/(p-1)
and
c_{4}
is the largest of the two constants appearing in the penultimate line. Similarly, the growth condition on
f_{0}
gives

f_{0}^{*}(x,z^{*}):=\sup_{z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\{z^{*}\cdot z-f_{0}(x,z)\}\\
\geq\sup_{z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\{z^{*}\cdot z-c_{3}\left|z\right|^{p}\}-c_{3}=\frac{1}{q}\left(\frac{1}{pc_{3}}\right)^{\frac{1}{p-1}}\left|z^{*}\right|^{q}+c_{3}.

(41)

Thus,
f_{0}^{*}
satisfies coercivity and growth conditions similar to those of
f_{0}
, but with different constants and the conjugate exponent
q
. By the same arguments one concludes that the double Legendre transform
f_{0}^{**}=(f_{0}^{*})^{*}
satisfies growth and coercivity conditions with exponent
p
, as desired.

The rest of this section proves
theorem

5
.
The key is to recognize that pointwise dual relaxations solve a dual variational problem that can be obtained directly using the Legendre transform (cf.
lemma

14
).

5.1
Three technical lemmas

First, we prove that certain integral functionals on
L^{q}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m\times n})
and
L^{q}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})
defined using
f_{0}^{*}
and
f_{1}^{*}
are continuous. Here,
q
is the Hölder conjugate of the exponent
p
from
theorem

5
, meaning that
1/p+1/q=1
.

Lemma 13
.

Under the growth and coercivity conditions
2
, the functions
\sigma^{*}\mapsto\int_{\Omega}f_{0}^{*}(x,\sigma^{*}(x))\,{\rm d}x
and
\rho^{*}\mapsto\int_{\Omega}f_{1}^{*}(x,\rho^{*}(x))\,{\rm d}x
are finite and strongly continuous from
L^{q}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m\times n})
and
L^{q}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})
into
\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

We prove the results only for

\sigma^{*}\mapsto\int_{\Omega}f_{0}^{*}(x,\sigma^{*}(x))\,{\rm d}x

since the arguments for
\rho^{*}\mapsto\int_{\Omega}f_{1}^{*}(x,\rho^{*}(x))\,{\rm d}x
are identical. To see that
\int_{\Omega}f_{0}^{*}(x,\sigma^{*}(x))\,{\rm d}x<+\infty
for all
\sigma\in L^{q}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m\times n})
, observe that straightforward estimates using assumption
2
and Young’s inequality yield

\left|f_{0}^{*}(x,z^{*})\right|\lesssim 1+\left|z^{*}\right|^{q}
for all
x\in\Omega
. To establish strong continuity, instead, it suffices to check that
z^{*}\mapsto f_{0}^{*}(x,z^{*})
is continuous.
This can be done with the help of
[
35
, Theorem 7.A]
, which guarantees continuity if, for every
z^{*}
and any
\alpha<f^{*}(x,z^{*})
, the set
\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}:f_{0}(x,z)+\alpha\leq z\cdot z^{*}\}
is bounded. This is an easy consequence of the lower bound on
f_{0}
in assumption
2
, combined with the Hölder inequality
z\cdot z^{*}\leq|z|^{p}/p+|z^{*}|^{q}/q
with
p>1
and
1/p+1/q=1
.

Next, we use classical duality theory
[
15
]
to express
\mathcal{F}^{*}
as the optimal value of a constrained maximization problem involving the Legendre transforms
f_{0}^{*}
and
f_{1}^{*}
.

Lemma 14
.

Let
q=\frac{p}{p-1}
. Under the assumptions of
theorem

5
,

\mathcal{F}^{*}=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\in L^{q}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m\times n})\\
\rho\in L^{q}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})\\
\nabla\cdot\sigma=\rho\end{subarray}}\int_{\Omega}-f_{0}^{*}(x,\sigma)-f_{1}^{*}(x,\rho)\,\,{\rm d}x.

(42)

Proof.

Define the convex functions

\displaystyle\Phi(\sigma,\rho)

\displaystyle:=\int_{\Omega}f_{0}^{*}(x,\sigma)+f_{1}^{*}(x,\rho)\,{\rm d}x,

and

\displaystyle\Psi(\sigma,\rho)

\displaystyle:=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }\nabla\cdot\sigma=\rho,\\
+\infty&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

which are convex from
L^{q}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m\times n})\times L^{q}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})
into
(-\infty,+\infty]
. Let
D(\Phi)
and
D(\Psi)
be the sets in
L^{q}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m\times n})\times L^{q}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})
where they take on finite values.
The right-hand side of
eq.

42
is equivalent to maximizing
-\Phi(\sigma)-\Psi(\sigma)
, and the identity

\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\in L^{q}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m\times n})\\
\rho\in L^{q}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})\end{subarray}}\{-\Phi(\sigma,\rho)-\Psi(\sigma,\rho)\}=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma^{*}\in L^{p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m\times n})\\
\rho^{*}\in L^{p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})\end{subarray}}\{\Phi^{*}(-\sigma^{*},-\rho^{*})+\Psi^{*}(\sigma^{*},\rho^{*})\}

(43)

holds if there exists
(\sigma_{0},\rho_{0})\in D(\Phi)\cap D(\Psi)
at which
\Phi
is continuous in the strong topology of
L^{q}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m\times n})\times L^{q}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})

[
8
, Theorem 1.12]
.
This is true because
\Phi
is continuous on the entire space
L^{q}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m\times n})\times L^{q}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})
by
lemma

13
, while
D(\Psi)
is nonempty because the equation
\nabla\cdot\sigma=\rho
admits at least one solution

\sigma\in L^{q}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m\times n})
for every
\rho\in L^{q}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})

[
36
]
.
The lemma is therefore proven if we can show that the right-hand side of
eq.

43
is equal to
\mathcal{F}^{*}
, i.e.

\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma^{*}\in L^{p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m\times n})\\
\rho^{*}\in L^{p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})\end{subarray}}\{\Phi^{*}(-\sigma^{*},-\rho^{*})+\Psi^{*}(\sigma^{*},\rho^{*})\}=\inf_{u\in W_{0}^{1,p}}\int_{\Omega}f_{0}(x,\nabla u)+f_{1}(x,u)\,{\rm d}x.

(44)

To establish this identity, observe that

\displaystyle\Phi^{*}(-\sigma^{*},-\rho^{*})

\displaystyle=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\in L^{q}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m\times n})\\
\rho\in L^{q}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})\end{subarray}}\int_{\Omega}-\sigma^{*}\cdot\sigma-\rho^{*}\cdot\rho-f_{0}^{*}(x,\sigma)-f_{1}^{*}(x,\rho)\,{\rm d}x

(45)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}f_{0}^{**}(x,-\sigma^{*})+f_{1}^{**}(x,-\rho^{*})\,{\rm d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}f_{0}(x,-\sigma^{*})+f_{1}(x,-\rho^{*})\,{\rm d}x.

The second equality follows from
[
15
, Proposition IX.2.1]
and the last one is a consequence of assumption
1
, since the double Legendre transform of a convex function is the function itself.
To calculate the Legendre transform of
\Psi
, instead, observe via the Helmholtz decomposition that any
\sigma
satisfying
\nabla\cdot\sigma=\rho
can be written as
\sigma=\nabla v^{\rho}+\xi
, where
\xi
is divergence-free and the components of
v^{\rho}=(v^{\rho}_{1},\ldots,v^{\rho}_{m})\in\smash{W^{1,p}_{0}}
satisfy the Poisson equation
\Delta v^{\rho}_{i}=\rho_{i}
. Thus,

\displaystyle\Psi^{*}(\sigma^{*},\rho^{*})

\displaystyle=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\in L^{q}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m\times n})\\
\rho\in L^{q}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})\\
\nabla\cdot\sigma=\rho\end{subarray}}\int_{\Omega}\sigma^{*}\cdot\sigma+\rho^{*}\cdot\rho\,{\rm d}x

\displaystyle=\sup_{\rho\in L^{q}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\xi\in L^{q}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m\times n})\\
\nabla\cdot\xi=0\end{subarray}}\int_{\Omega}\sigma^{*}\cdot\nabla v^{\rho}+\sigma^{*}\cdot\xi+\rho^{*}\cdot\rho\,{\rm d}x.

The inner supremum is unbounded unless
\int_{\Omega}\sigma^{*}\cdot\xi\,{\rm d}x=0
, which requires
\sigma^{*}=-\nabla u
for some
u\in\smash{W^{1,p}_{0}}
. In this case, integration by parts and the identity
\Delta v^{\rho}_{i}=\rho_{i}
yield

\Psi^{*}(-\nabla u,\rho^{*})=\sup_{\rho\in L^{q}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})}\int_{\Omega}u\cdot\Delta v^{\rho}+\rho^{*}\cdot\rho\;\,{\rm d}x\\
=\sup_{\rho\in L^{q}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})}\int_{\Omega}(u+\rho^{*})\cdot\rho\;\,{\rm d}x=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }\rho^{*}=-u,\\
+\infty&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Thus,

\Psi^{*}(\sigma^{*},\rho^{*})=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }\exists u\in W_{0}^{1,p}:\;\sigma^{*}=-\nabla u,\,\rho^{*}=-u,\\
+\infty&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(46)

Combining
eq.

45
and
eq.

46
yields
eq.

44
, as required.

Finally, we show that feasible functions for the maximization problem
eq.

42
can be approximated by feasible functions that are smooth on
\overline{\Omega}
. We believe the result is standard, but give a proof for completeness.

Lemma 15
.

Suppose
\sigma\in L^{q}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m\times n})
and
\rho\in L^{q}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})
satisfy

\nabla\cdot\sigma=\rho
.
There exists sequences

\{\sigma_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subset C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega};\mathbb{R}^{m\times n})

and

\{\rho_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subset C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega};\mathbb{R}^{m})

with

\nabla\cdot\sigma_{k}=\rho_{k}

such that

\|\sigma_{k}-\sigma\|_{q}\to 0

and

\|\rho_{k}-\rho\|_{q}\to 0
as
k\to\infty
.

Proof.

Let
B
be a ball with
\Omega\subset B
and
{\rm dist}(\Omega,\partial B)\geq 1
, so
B\setminus\Omega
is a bounded Lipschitz domain with boundary
\partial\Omega\cup\partial B
.
Let
p=q/(q-1)
and let
u
solve

\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in W^{1,p}(B\setminus\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})\\
u=0\text{ on }\partial B\end{subarray}}\int_{B\setminus\Omega}\frac{\left|\nabla u\right|^{p}}{p}\,{\rm d}x-\int_{\partial\Omega}(\sigma\cdot\hat{n})\cdot u\,{\rm d}S.

This is a convex problem, so
u
satisfies the Euler–Lagrange equation

\begin{cases}\nabla\cdot(\left|\nabla u\right|^{p-2}\nabla u)=0&\text{on }B\setminus\Omega,\\
\left|\nabla u\right|^{p-2}\nabla u\cdot\hat{n}=\sigma\cdot\hat{n}&\text{on }\partial\Omega,\\
u=0&\text{on }\partial B.\end{cases}

We claim that

\overline{\sigma}:=\begin{cases}\sigma&\text{if }x\in\Omega\\
\left|\nabla u\right|^{p-2}\nabla u&\text{if }x\in B\setminus\Omega\end{cases}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\overline{\rho}:=\begin{cases}\rho&\text{if }x\in\Omega\\
0&\text{if }x\in B\setminus\Omega\end{cases}

extend
\sigma
and
\rho
to functions in
L^{q}(B;\mathbb{R}^{m\times n})
and
L^{q}(B;\mathbb{R}^{m})
, respectively, and satisfy
\nabla\cdot\overline{\sigma}=\overline{\rho}
in the sense of distribution.
Indeed, it is clear that
\overline{\rho}\in L^{q}(B;\mathbb{R}^{m})
, while
\overline{\sigma}\in L^{q}(B;\mathbb{R}^{m\times n})
because
q=p/(p-1)
and, consequently,

\int_{B}\left|\overline{\sigma}\right|^{q}\,{\rm d}x=\int_{\Omega}\left|\sigma\right|^{q}\,{\rm d}x+\int_{B\setminus\Omega}\left|\nabla u\right|^{(p-1)q}\,{\rm d}x=\int_{\Omega}\left|\sigma\right|^{q}\,{\rm d}x+\int_{B\setminus\Omega}\left|\nabla u\right|^{p}\,{\rm d}x<\infty.

The identity
\nabla\cdot\overline{\sigma}=\overline{\rho}
is verified by a direct calculation: given any smooth and compactly supported test function
\varphi:B\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
,

\int_{B}(\nabla\cdot\overline{\sigma})\cdot\varphi\,{\rm d}x=-\int_{B}\overline{\sigma}\cdot\nabla\varphi\,{\rm d}x=-\int_{\Omega}\sigma\cdot\nabla\varphi\,{\rm d}x-\int_{B\setminus\Omega}\left|\nabla u\right|^{p-2}\nabla u\cdot\nabla\varphi\,{\rm d}x\\
=\int_{\Omega}\underbrace{(\nabla\cdot\sigma)}_{=\rho}\cdot\varphi\,{\rm d}x+\int_{B\setminus\Omega}\underbrace{[\nabla\cdot(\left|\nabla u\right|^{p-2}\nabla u)]}_{=0}\cdot\varphi\,{\rm d}x=\int_{B}\overline{\rho}\cdot\varphi\,{\rm d}x,

where in the second line we used the boundary conditions on
u
.
Standard mollification arguments then yield a sequence
\{{\sigma}_{k},{\rho}_{k}\}
of functions that are smooth on
B
(hence, on
\overline{\Omega}
), converge to
\overline{\sigma}
and
\overline{\rho}
in
L^{q}
, and satisfy
\nabla\cdot{\sigma}_{k}={\rho}_{k}
.

5.2
Proof of
theorem

5

Since
\mathcal{F}^{*}\geq\mathcal{F}_{\rm omr}\geq\mathcal{L}_{\rm pdr}
by
theorem

3
, it suffices to show that
\mathcal{L}_{\rm pdr}\geq\mathcal{F}^{*}-\varepsilon
for every
\varepsilon>0
.
To do so, we use an almost-optimal solution of the dual problem
eq.

42
obtained in
lemma

14
to construct
(\varphi_{\varepsilon},\eta_{\varepsilon},h_{\varepsilon},\ell_{\varepsilon})\in\mathbb{B}
feasible for the maximization problem defining
\mathcal{L}_{\rm pdr}
in
eq.

22
and satisfying

\int_{\Omega}h_{\varepsilon}(x)\,\,{\rm d}x+\int_{\partial\Omega}\ell_{\varepsilon}(x)\,\,{\rm d}S\geq\mathcal{F}^{*}-\varepsilon.

(47)

Let
\sigma_{\varepsilon}\in L^{q}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m\times n})
and
\rho_{\varepsilon}\in L^{q}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})
satisfy
\nabla\cdot\sigma_{\varepsilon}=\rho_{\varepsilon}
, as well as

\int_{\Omega}-f_{0}^{*}(x,\sigma_{\varepsilon})-f_{1}^{*}(x,\rho_{\varepsilon})\,\,{\rm d}x\geq\mathcal{F}^{*}-\varepsilon.

The existence of such functions is guaranteed by
lemma

14
.
Using
lemmas

13
and
15
, we may assume
\sigma_{\varepsilon}\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega};\mathbb{R}^{m\times n})
and
\rho_{\varepsilon}\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega};\mathbb{R}^{m})
.
Set

\displaystyle\varphi_{\varepsilon}(x,y)

\displaystyle=-\sigma_{\varepsilon}(x)y,

\displaystyle\eta_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle h_{\varepsilon}(x)

\displaystyle=-f_{0}^{*}(x,\sigma_{\varepsilon}(x))-f_{1}^{*}(x,\rho_{\varepsilon}(x)),

\displaystyle\ell_{\varepsilon}=0.

Note that
\varphi_{\varepsilon}\in\Phi^{p}
since
\sigma_{\varepsilon}
is smooth up to the boundary of
\Omega
, and that
h_{\varepsilon}\in C(\overline{\Omega})
because
f_{0}^{*}
and
f_{1}^{*}

).
These choices satisfy
eq.

, so to prove
theorem

5
we only need to check the inequalities defining the set
\mathbb{B}
in
eq.

20
.

Given the constraints of
eq.

37
, these inequalities become

\displaystyle F^{\varphi_{\varepsilon},\eta_{\varepsilon},h_{\varepsilon}}

\displaystyle\geq 0\quad\text{on }\Gamma=\overline{\Omega}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{m\times n},

\displaystyle G^{\varphi_{\varepsilon},\eta_{\varepsilon},\ell_{\varepsilon}}

\displaystyle\geq 0\quad\text{on }\Lambda=\partial\Omega\times\{0\}.

\mathcal{D}\varphi_{\varepsilon}=-(\nabla\cdot\sigma_{\varepsilon})\cdot y-\sigma_{\varepsilon}\cdot z=-\rho_{\varepsilon}\cdot y-\sigma_{\varepsilon}\cdot z
and, therefore,

\displaystyle F^{\varphi_{\varepsilon},\eta_{\varepsilon},h_{\varepsilon}}(x,y,z)=

6
Characterizing minimizers and near-minimizers

If
\mathcal{F}^{*}=\mathcal{L}_{\rm pdr}
, so that the occupation measure and pointwise dual relaxation bounds are sharp, solutions of the pointwise dual relaxation
eq.

22
can be used to derive necessary optimality conditions for global minimizers of the original problem
eq.

2
. We explain why in this brief section, before turning to our examples of non-sharpness.

Proposition 16
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{F}^{*}=\mathcal{L}_{\rm pdr}
. If
u
solves the minimization problem
eq.

2
and
(\varphi,\eta,h,\ell)\in\mathbb{B}
solves its pointwise dual relaxation
eq.

22
,

\displaystyle F^{\varphi,\eta,h}(x,u(x),\nabla u(x))

\displaystyle=0\quad\text{ a.e. on }\Omega,

\displaystyle G^{\varphi,\eta,\ell}(x,u(x))

\displaystyle=0\quad\text{ a.e. on }\partial\Omega.

Remark 17
.

These optimality conditions distinguish local minimizers from global ones, unlike Euler–Lagrange equations (with the exception of convex problems).

Remark 18
.

Recall from
corollary

10
that if
n=1
or
m=1
, then any nonconvex dependence on
\nabla u
can be convexified while preserving the minimum. In this case, optimality conditions alternative to those in
proposition

16
take the form of a differential inclusion; see
[
29
]
for an example. Elucidating the precise relation between the various optimality conditions in the literature is beyond the scope of this work.

Proof of
proposition

16
.

Let

\Psi(u)=\int_{\Omega}f(x,u,\nabla u)\,{\rm d}x+\int_{\partial\Omega}g(x,u)\,{\rm d}S

(48)

be the integral functional being minimized in
eq.

2
, and suppose that
u
is optimal. Since
(\varphi,\eta,h,\ell)
attains equality in
eq.

22
and
\mathcal{L}_{\rm pdr}=\mathcal{F}^{*}=\Psi(u)
, we have

\Psi(u)=\int_{\Omega}h(x)\,{\rm d}x+\int_{\partial\Omega}\ell(x)\,{\rm d}S.

(49)

Next, recall the definitions of the sets
\Gamma
and
\Lambda
from
eqs.

7a
and
7b
. Since
(\varphi,\eta,h,\ell)
is feasible for the dual relaxation
eq.

22
, the functions
F^{\varphi,\eta,h}
and
G^{\varphi,\eta,\ell}
are nonnegative on
\Gamma
and
\Lambda
, respectively. Moreover,
(x,u(x),\nabla u(x))\in\Gamma
for almost every
x\in\Omega
and

(x,u(x))\in\Lambda

for almost every
x\in\partial\Omega
since
u
satisfies the constraints in
eq.

3
. Thus,

\displaystyle\Psi(u)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\Psi(u)+\int_{\Omega}F^{\varphi,\eta,h}(x,u,\nabla u)\,{\rm d}x+\int_{\partial\Omega}G^{\varphi,\eta,\ell}(x,u)\,{\rm d}S

\displaystyle\overset{\text{\lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{e:exact-bound}}}{=}

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\![F^{\varphi,\eta,h}(x,u,\nabla u)+h(x)]\,{\rm d}x+\int_{\partial\Omega}\![G^{\varphi,\eta,\ell}(x,u)+\ell(x)]\,{\rm d}S

\displaystyle\overset{\text{\lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{e:constraints-general-1}\&\lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{e:div-theorem}}}{=}

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}f(x,u,\nabla u)\,{\rm d}x+\int_{\partial\Omega}g(x,u)\,{\rm d}S

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Psi(u).

The first inequality is therefore an equality, so the functions
F^{\varphi,\eta,h}(x,u(x),\nabla u(x))
and
G^{\varphi,\eta,\ell}(x,u(x))
vanish almost everywhere on
\Omega
and
\partial\Omega
, respectively.

The previous result assumed that the pointwise dual relaxation can be solved, i.e., that it admits an optimizer. In lieu of this, a partial characterization of ‘near optimizers’ can still be obtained provided that
\mathcal{F}^{*}=\mathcal{L}_{\rm pdr}
.
Let
\Psi
be as in
eq.

48
.

Proposition 19
.

Let
\mathcal{F}^{*}=\mathcal{L}_{\rm pdr}
.
Given
\varepsilon>0
, let
u
satisfy
\Psi(u)\leq\mathcal{F}^{*}+\varepsilon
and let
(\varphi,\eta,h,\ell)\in\mathbb{B}
be admissible for the pointwise dual relaxation
eq.

22
with
\mathcal{F}^{*}-\varepsilon\leq\int_{\Omega}h(x)\,{\rm d}x+\int_{\partial\Omega}\ell(x)
.
Then, for every
\delta>0
, the volume and surface measures

\displaystyle\lambda_{\delta}

\displaystyle:=\left|\{x\in\Omega:\;F^{\varphi\eta h}(x,u(x),\nabla u(x))\geq\delta\}\right|

\displaystyle\sigma_{\delta}

\displaystyle:=\left|\{x\in\partial\Omega:\;G^{\varphi\xi\ell}(x,u(x))\geq\delta\}\right|

satisfy

\lambda_{\delta}+\sigma_{\delta}\leq\frac{2\varepsilon}{\delta}.

Proof.

Our assumptions on
u
and
(\varphi,\eta,h,\ell)
imply that

\displaystyle 2\varepsilon

\displaystyle\geq\int_{\Omega}\left[f(x,u,\nabla u)+h(x)\right]\,{\rm d}x+\int_{\partial\Omega}\left[g(x,u)+\ell(x)\right]\,{\rm d}S

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}F^{\varphi,\eta,h}(x,u,\nabla u)\,{\rm d}x+\int_{\partial\Omega}G^{\varphi,\eta,\ell}(x,u)\,{\rm d}S,

where the second line follows because the terms added to the integrands give a zero net contribution by
eqs.

3a
and
13
.
Since the tuple
(\varphi,\eta,h,\ell)
is feasible for
eq.

22
, the functions
x\mapsto F^{\varphi\eta h}(x,u(x),\nabla u(x))
and
x\mapsto G^{\varphi\xi\ell}(x,u(x))
are nonnegative. The result follows from Chebyshev’s inequality.

7
Non-sharpness of occupation measure bounds

We end with two examples where the relaxation by occupation and boundary measures presented in
section

3
is not sharp, meaning that
\mathcal{F}^{*}>\mathcal{F}_{\rm omr}
. In both cases, we exhibit a pair
(\mu,\nu)
of measures satisfying the constraints of the occupation measure relaxation
eq.

18
and for which the objective value is strictly less than
\mathcal{F}^{*}
.
Section

7.1
treats a vectorial example which is nonconvex in
\nabla u
, coming from mathematical material science. A different example with convex dependence on
\nabla u
and nonconvex dependence on
u
appears in
[
21
]
.

Section

7.2
gives an example showing how occupation measures fail to capture optimal Poincaré constants for mean-zero functions in one-dimension. Other examples of non-sharpness based on non-convex constraints also appear in
[
21
]
.

7.1
A double-well problem

Fix any
n,m\geq 2
, let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a bounded Lipschitz domain, and consider the non-convex minimization problem

\mathcal{F}^{*}:=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}u:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{m}\\[1.50694pt]
u=0\text{ on }\partial\Omega\end{subarray}}\int_{\Omega}\left|\nabla u(x)-A\right|^{2}\,\left|\nabla u(x)-B\right|^{2}\,\,{\rm d}x,

(50)

where
\left|\cdot\right|
is the Frobenius matrix norm. We take
A,B\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
to satisfy

\rank(A-B)\geq 2

(51)

which is possible given our choices for
n
and
m
.
It is a well-known but non-trivial fact that
eq.

50
does not have a minimizer, even though its infimum
\mathcal{F}^{*}
is finite. To evaluate it, then, one must find a way to pass to the limit along minimizing sequences that drive the integral to its infimal value. Here, we explain this using the theory of gradient Young measures, which we briefly recall; see
[
30
,
32
,
34
]
for details.

A
W^{1,p}
-
gradient Young measure
generated by a weakly converging sequence
\{u_{k}\}

\subset W^{1,p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})
is a family
\{\lambda_{x}\}_{x\in\Omega}
of probability measures on
\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
such that
[
34
]
:

1.

\int_{\Omega}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}}\left|z\right|^{p}{\rm d}\lambda_{x}(z)\,{\rm d}x<\infty
;

2.

Given any
\varphi:\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}\to\mathbb{R}
that is measurable in
x
and continuous in
z
,
2
2

2

Such functions are called
Carathéodory functions
.
the function
x\mapsto\int\varphi(x,z){\rm d}\lambda_{x}(z)
is measurable;

3.

Given any
\varphi
as in
2
such that
\{\varphi(x,\nabla u_{k})\}
is uniformly
L^{1}
-bounded and equiintegrable,
\varphi(x,\nabla u_{k})
converges to
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}}\varphi(x,z){\rm d}\lambda_{x}(z)
weakly in
L^{1}(\Omega)
.

The space of all
W^{1,p}
-gradient Young measures is denoted by
{\rm GY}^{p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m\times n})
.

Applying the theory of gradient Young measures to problem
eq.

50
, one finds that

\mathcal{F}^{*}=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in W^{1,p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m\times n})\\
\{\lambda_{x}\}\in{\rm GY}^{p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m\times n})\\
\int z{\rm d}\lambda_{x}(z)=\nabla u(x)\\
u=0\text{ on }\partial\Omega\end{subarray}}\int_{\Omega}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}}\left|z-A\right|^{2}\left|z-B\right|^{2}\,{\rm d}\lambda_{x}(z)\,{\rm d}x

(52)

(see, e.g.,
[
30
, Theorem 4.9]
). In contrast, the occupation measure relaxation is

\mathcal{F}_{\rm omr}=\min_{(\mu,\nu)\in\mathbb{O}^{p}}\langle\left|z-A\right|^{2}\left|z-B\right|^{2},\mu\rangle,

(53)

where for our example the set
\mathbb{O}^{p}
from
eq.

17
is

\mathbb{O}^{p}=\left\{(\mu,\nu)\in\mathbb{M}^{p}:\;\text{\lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{e:marginal-constraints-mu}, \lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{e:marginal-constraints-nu}
and \lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{e:divergence-constraints-measures}}\right\}.

Whether or not the minima in
eq.

52
and
eq.

53
are the same comes down to the relation between gradient Young and occupation measures.

The pushforward operation provides a natural embedding of
W^{1,p}\times{\rm GY}^{p}
into
\mathbb{O}^{p}
. Precisely, to each Sobolev function–gradient Young measure pair
(u,\{\lambda_{x}\})
we can associate the occupation and boundary measures

\displaystyle\mu({\rm d}x,{\rm d}y,{\rm d}z)

\displaystyle:=\,{\rm d}x\otimes\delta_{u(x)}({\rm d}y)\otimes\lambda_{x}({\rm d}z),

\displaystyle\nu({\rm d}x,{\rm d}y)

\displaystyle:=\,{\rm d}S\otimes\delta_{u(x)}({\rm d}y).

However, this embedding is far from a one-to-one correspondence since when
n,m\geq 2
the definition of
\mathbb{O}^{p}
misses crucial nonlinear constraints satisfied by gradient Young measures. Indeed, the latter satisfy the nonlinear Jensen-type inequalities
[
19
]

h\!\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}}z{\rm d}\lambda_{x}(z)\right)\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}}h(z){\rm d}\lambda_{x}(z)

(54)

for every function
h:\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}\to\mathbb{R}
that grows no faster than
|z|^{p}
and is
quasiconvex
, i.e.,
h(z)\leq\fint_{D}h(z+\nabla\varphi)\,{\rm d}x
for all
z\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
and all compactly supported smooth functions
\varphi:D\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
(
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
can be any bounded Lipschitz domain
[
34
, Ch. 5]
). The inability of occupation measure relaxations to capture these nonlinear constraints results in a relaxation gap.

Proposition 20
.

For the minimization problem in
eq.

50
,
\mathcal{F}^{*}>0
but
\mathcal{F}_{\rm omr}=0
.

Proof.

It suffices to consider the case
A=I
,
B=-I
by a change of variables. We also fix
n=m=2
, since the general case is analogous. The exponent is
p=4
.

First, we prove that
\mathcal{F}^{*}>0
. By contradiction, if the minimimum of the gradient Young measure problem
eq.

52
is zero, it is solved by a pair
(u,\{\lambda_{x}\})
with

\lambda_{x}(z)=\theta(x)\delta_{I}(z)+\left(1-\theta(x)\right)\delta_{-I}(z)

(55)

for
\theta:\Omega\to[0,1]
. However, this is incompatible with the constraint
\int z{\rm d}\lambda_{x}=\nabla u(x)
and the inequalities
eq.

54
. Specifically, since
\det(z)
and
-\det(z)
are both quasiconvex (see, e.g.,
[
34
, Corollary 5.9]
), any gradient Young measure in
eq.

52
must obey

\det\left(\nabla u(x)\right)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}}\det(z){\rm d}\lambda_{x}(z)\qquad\text{a.e. }x\in\Omega.

This identity fails for the measure in
eq.

55
because

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}}\det(z){\rm d}\lambda_{x}(z)=\theta(x)\det(I)+\left(1-\theta(x)\right)\det(-I)=1,

while

\int_{\Omega}\det(\nabla u)\,{\rm d}x=\int_{\Omega}\nabla\cdot\left(u_{1}\frac{\partial u_{2}}{\partial x_{2}},-u_{1}\frac{\partial u_{2}}{\partial x_{1}}\right)\,{\rm d}x=\int_{\partial\Omega}\left(u_{1}\frac{\partial u_{2}}{\partial x_{2}},-u_{1}\frac{\partial u_{2}}{\partial x_{1}}\right)\cdot\hat{n}\,{\rm d}S=0

by the boundary condition
u=0
at
\partial\Omega
.

Next, we show that
\mathcal{F}_{\rm omr}=0
. It is clear that
\mathcal{F}_{\rm omr}\geq 0
, so we only need to find measures
\mu
and
\nu
that are feasible for
eq.

53
and such that
\langle\left|z-I\right|^{2}\left|z+I\right|^{2},\mu\rangle=0
. We claim that suitable choices are

\displaystyle\mu:=\,{\rm d}x\otimes\delta_{0}({\rm d}y)\otimes\left(\frac{1}{2}\delta_{I}({\rm d}z)+\frac{1}{2}\delta_{-I}({\rm d}z)\right)

\displaystyle\nu:=\,{\rm d}S\otimes\delta_{0}({\rm d}y).

It is clear that
\mu
and
\nu
have finite moments of order
p
or less. It is also clear that the
x
-marginals of
\mu
and
\nu
coincide with the volume and surface measures on
\Omega
, as required by
eqs.

9b
and
9c
.
To check
eq.

14
, fix any
\varphi\in\Phi^{4}
and, summing over repeated indices to simplify the notation, observe that

\langle\mathcal{D}\varphi,\mu\rangle=\int_{\Omega}\left[\frac{\partial\varphi_{i}}{\partial x_{i}}+\frac{\partial\varphi_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}\left(\frac{1}{2}I_{ji}-\frac{1}{2}I_{ji}\right)\right]_{(x,0)}\,{\rm d}x\\
=\int_{\Omega}\nabla\cdot\varphi(x,0)\,{\rm d}x=\int_{\partial\Omega}\varphi(x,0)\cdot\hat{n}(x)\,{\rm d}S=\langle\varphi\cdot\hat{n},\nu\rangle.

An analogous calculation shows that
\langle\left|z-I\right|^{2}\left|z+I\right|^{2},\mu\rangle=0
, concluding the proof of
proposition

20
.

7.2
Optimal Poincaré constant for univariate mean-zero functions

For our next example, we consider a one-dimensional minimization problem giving the optimal Poincaré constant for univariate mean-zero functions on
\Omega=(-1,1)
,

\mathcal{F}^{*}:=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}\int_{-1}^{1}u^{2}\,{\rm d}x=1\\
\int_{-1}^{1}u\,{\rm d}x=0\end{subarray}}\;\int_{-1}^{1}\left|\frac{{\rm d}u}{{\rm d}x}\right|^{2}\,{\rm d}x.

(56)

The occupation measure relaxation for this minimization problem reads

\mathcal{F}_{\rm omr}=\min_{(\mu,\nu)\in\mathbb{O}^{2}}\langle z^{2},\mu\rangle,

(57)

where the constraints set
\mathbb{O}^{2}
in
eq.

17
now includes the constraints
\langle y,\mu\rangle=0
and
\langle y^{2},\mu\rangle=1
as well as
eq.

9b
,
eq.

9c
and
eq.

14
.

The next result proves that
\mathcal{F}^{*}>\mathcal{F}_{\rm omr}
. The relaxation gap arises because the
average
of the occupation and boundary measures generated by the constant functions
u(x)=\pm\smash{1/\sqrt{2}}
is feasible for
eq.

57
, even though their generating functions are clearly not admissible in
eq.

56
(they violate the mean-zero constraint).

Proposition 21
.

For the minimization problem in
eq.

56
,
\mathcal{F}^{*}=\smash{\frac{\pi^{2}}{4}}
but
\mathcal{F}_{\rm omr}=0
.

Remark 22
.

The reader may want to contrast this with
[
11
]
, which proves sharpness for some optimal Poincaré constants in the Dirichlet boundary condition case.

Proof.

The optimal value
\mathcal{F}^{*}=\smash{\frac{1}{4}}\pi^{2}
is easily computed upon solving the Euler–Lagrange equation for problem
eq.

56
, and it is attained by
u^{*}(x)=\sin(\pi x/2)
.

To prove that
\mathcal{F}_{\rm omr}=0
we observe that
\mathcal{F}_{\rm omr}
is clearly nonnegative, so it suffices to exhibit a pair
(\mu,\nu)
that is feasible for the relaxed problem
eq.

57
and achieves
\langle z^{2},\mu\rangle=0
. For this, we consider

\displaystyle\mu:=\tfrac{1}{2}\,{\rm d}x\otimes\left[\delta_{\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}}({\rm d}y)+\delta_{-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}}({\rm d}y)\right]\otimes\delta_{0}({\rm d}z),

\displaystyle\nu:=\tfrac{1}{2}\Big[\delta_{-1}({\rm d}x)+\delta_{1}({\rm d}x)\Big]\otimes\left[\delta_{\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}}({\rm d}y)+\delta_{-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}}({\rm d}y)\right]

These measures clearly have bounded moments of order up to
2
, and their
x
-marginals are the volume and surface measures by
eqs.

9b
and
9c
. Further, for any
\varphi\in\Phi^{2}

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{D}\varphi,\mu\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial x}\!\left(x,\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right)+\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial x}\!\left(x,-\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\;\,{\rm d}x

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left[\varphi\!\left(1,\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right)+\varphi\!\left(1,-\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right)-\varphi\!\left(-1,\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right)-\varphi\!\left(-1,-\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\langle\varphi\cdot n,\nu\rangle.

In the last step, we used our choice of boundary measure
\nu
and the fact that the unit normal vector to the boundary of
\Omega=(-1,1)
is
\hat{n}(x)=x
.
Similar calculations show that
\langle y,\mu\rangle=0
,
\langle y^{2},\mu\rangle=0
, and
\langle z^{2},\mu\rangle=0
.
Proposition

21
is therefore proved.

8
Conclusions

We have investigated the problem of finding
a priori
lower bounds on integral minimization problems. We have shown that lower bounds obtained using the occupation measure framework of
[
5
,
20
]
can be estimated from below by a dual problem, which coincides with the pointwise dual relaxation developed in
[
11
]
using ideas introduced in the context of Lyapunov methods for partial differential equations
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
37
,
38
,
39
]
.
We have also identified general sufficient conditions ensuring that this duality is strong (
theorem

4
), in which case the occupation measure and pointwise dual relaxations are the same. Checking our sufficient conditions for strong duality requires one to construct a function
\varphi_{0}
satisfying the two coercivity inequalities
eqs.

25a
and
25b
.
In applications, these inequalities may simply hold with
\varphi_{0}=0
. Our choice to include non-zero
\varphi_{0}
allows for more general problems where coercivity is not evident, but can be proved after a translation.
If the constraints imposed on the original problems imply uniform bounds on admissible functions and their gradients, as assumed in
[
20
,
21
]
, coercivity is immediate and our strong duality theorem applies.

In the second part of this work, we proved that relaxations via occupation measures are sharp for variational problems with a particular additive convex structure. Our proof uses convex duality, which is an established route to study such variational problems (see, e.g.,
[
15
]
); here, we recognized its relation to the occupation measure framework.
After this paper was submitted, different measure-theoretic arguments appeared in
[
17
]
extending our sharpness result to other convex problems including ones whose integrands do not necessarily have an additive structure.

On the other hand, as the results of this paper show, occupation measure relaxations are not always sharp: the counterexamples in
section

7
show that sharpness can fail even in the one-dimensional scalar case. The lack of sharpness for vectorial problems such as the double-well example of
section

7.1
does not come as a surprise, because the linear constraints imposed by the occupation measure framework do not fully characterize the gradient Young measures needed for sharp relaxations
[
30
,
32
,
34
]
. This fundamental limitation of the method prevents occupation measures from producing sharp lower bounds in general. Moreover, the lack of a simple characterization of quasiconvexity makes it hard to incorporate the Jensen-type inequalities satisfied by gradient Young measures to improve occupation measure relaxations.

Nevertheless, the computational tractability of occupation measure relaxations
[
11
,
20
]
means that they should be added to the list of available techniques for proving nontrivial
a priori
bounds on integral minimization problems, such as translation and calibration methods as well as polyconvexity (see, e.g.,
[
10
,
12
,
16
,
27
]
).
Future work should clarify the relationship between occupation measures and these well-known techniques.
