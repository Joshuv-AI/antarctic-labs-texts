An effective subgradient algorithm via Mifflin’s line search for nonsmooth nonconvex multiobjective optimization
Morteza Maleknia, Majid Soleimani-damaneh

Abstract




Abstract

We propose a descent subgradient algorithm for unconstrained nonsmooth nonconvex multiobjective optimization problems. To find a descent direction, we present an iterative process that efficiently approximates the (Goldstein)
\varepsilon
-subdifferential of each objective function. To this end, we develop a new variant of Mifflin’s line search in which the subgradients are arbitrary and its finite convergence is proved under a semismooth assumption. In order to reduce the number of subgradient evaluations, we employ a backtracking line search that identifies the objectives requiring an improvement in the current approximation of the
\varepsilon
-subdifferential. Meanwhile, for the remaining objectives, new subgradients are not computed. Unlike bundle-type methods, the proposed approach can handle nonconvexity without the need for algorithmic or parametric adjustments. Moreover, the quadratic subproblems have a simple structure, and hence the method is easy to implement. We analyze the global convergence of the proposed method and prove that any accumulation point of the generated sequence satisfies a necessary Pareto optimality condition. Furthermore, our convergence analysis addresses a theoretical challenge in a recently developed subgradient method. By conducting numerical experiments, we observe the practical capability of the proposed method and evaluate its efficiency when applied to a diverse range of nonsmooth test problems.

1
Introduction

In many practical situations, one needs to make a decision under multiple conflicting criteria. For instance, consider a refinery that intends to maximize petrol production while minimizing toxic emissions at the same time. This problem is a decision-making problem with two conflicting objectives. Such a problem is called a
M
ultiobjective
O
ptimization
P
roblem
(MOP). In contrast with single objective problems, due to the conflicts among objectives, there is no solution to an MOP optimizing all objectives simultaneously. Therefore, the optimality notion is replaced with

Pareto optimality
, which represents an optimal compromise among all objectives. In practical terms, it is of crucial importance to approximate the set of all Pareto optimal points, namely the
Pareto front
, which is a challenging issue. This problem becomes even more complicated when objectives are not smooth and convex.

In this paper, we consider the following unconstrained multiobjective minimization problem:

\min\,\,\left(f_{1}(\mathbf{x}),f_{2}(\mathbf{x}),\ldots,f_{p}(\mathbf{x})\right)\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

(1)

where the objective functions
f_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
,
i=1,\ldots,p
, are locally Lipschitz, but not necessarily smooth or convex. One of the main approaches to deal with MOP (
1
) is to employ the scalarization methods
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
. This approach turns an MOP to a nonsmooth single-objective optimization problem. Then, a global optimal solution of the resulted single-objective problem provides a (weak) Pareto optimal point for the MOP. Regarding a nonsmooth single-objective optimization problem, various types of methods are developing rapidly. For instance, subgradient methods
[
4
,
5
]
, bundle methods
[
6
,
7
,
8
,
9
,
10
]
, and gradient sampling methods
[
11
,
12
,
13
,
14
]
are among the most common numerical approaches to minimize a locally Lipschitz function. The weighted sum method
[
15
,
16
]
is widely recognized as one of the most popular scalarization methods. By adjusting the weighting vectors, it is possible to obtain the entire Pareto front in the convex case, at least in theory. However, in situations where the image space is not
\mathbb{R}^{p}_{\geq}
-convex, which occurs in nonconvex cases, a significant portion of the Pareto front cannot be achieved using this method (for a bi-objective example, see Subsection
5.2
). In addition, for some MOPs, even in the convex case, a broad range of weighting vectors lead to an unbounded single-objective problem (for a bi-objective instance, see
[
17
]
). Similar limitations can also be observed with other aggregation techniques.

As another class of methods for solving MOP (
1
), one can point to the iterative descent methods. These approaches are extensions of the existing descent methods in single-objective optimization to the multiobjective case, which can be directly applied to the non-scalarized MOP (
1
). These methods are descent in the sense that, during each iteration, a descent direction that simultaneously reduces the value of all the objectives is found and utilized to update the current point. Due to the theoretical and practical challenges in developing descent methods for MOPs, the literature along this line of research is scarce, especially for problems with nonsmooth and nonconvex data. Some extensions of the subgradient methods that can be applied to MOPs with convex objectives can be found in
[
18
,
19
,
20
]
. As a more recent subgradient method, Gebken and Peitz
[
21
]
developed a subgradient method that can efficiently solve nonsmooth nonconvex MOP (
1
). In
[
22
]
, Mäkelä et al. presented an effective generalization of the proximal bundle methods that can deal with MOP (
1
). A quasi-Newton method can be found in
[
23
]
. Furthermore, some variants of the proximal bundle methods for solving nonsmooth nonconvex MOPs which are able to handle inexact information have been proposed in
[
24
,
25
]
.

In this paper, we develop a descent implementable subgradient algorithm to find Pareto optimal points of MOP (
1
). The main idea is to develop an iterative approach to efficiently approximate the (Goldstein)
\varepsilon
-subdifferential
[
26
]
of each objective function. The least-norm element in the convex hull of the approximated
\varepsilon
-subdifferentials provides a trial search direction. Such an element is computed by solving a convex quadratic subproblem. Next, a limited backtracking line search determines whether we can take a serious step or we need to take a null step to improve our approximations of
\varepsilon
-subdifferentials. In this way, for the given positive sequences
\varepsilon_{\nu}\downarrow 0
and
\delta_{\nu}\downarrow 0
, we generate a sequence of
\big(\delta_{\nu},\mathcal{S}_{\varepsilon_{\nu}}(\mathbf{x}_{\nu+1})\big)
-substationary points (see Definition
4.1
), such that any accumulation point of this sequence is Clarke substationary for MOP (
1
).

Regarding the details of the presented algorithm, the following features are highlighted. The
improvement function
which is used in Mäkelä et al’s proximal bundle method
[
22
,
1
]
poses an underlying issue. Indeed, once a null step occurs, the improvement function is not able to identify those objectives whose bundles of subgradient have not been enriched adequately. Therefore, after a null step, it is inevitable to compute a new subgradient for all the objectives. The same happens in recently developed proximal bundle methods
[
24
,
25
]
. To resolve this difficulty, we propose a simple backtracking line search that excludes the objectives for which, after a null step, we do not need to enrich the bundle of information. For the remaining objectives, we develop a new variant of Mifflin’s line search
[
6
]
that finds a new effective subgradient to improve the bundle of information. In this manner, one can significantly reduce the number of subgradient evaluations. Moreover, in Gebken and Peitz
[
21
]
, the proposed line search procedure to find effective subgradients lacks the finite convergence property. More precisely, they merely argue that the asymptotic behavior of the proposed line search provides a subdifferential set within which the desired subgradient element exists (see Lemma 3.4 in
[
21
]
). In contrast, our proposed line search exactly finds the desired subgradient element in finite number of iterations.

We study the global convergence of the proposed method and show that any accumulation point of the generated sequence is Clarke substationary for MOP (
1
). Furthermore, in Gebken and Peitz
[
21
]
, convergence of the method to a Clarke substationary point through a sequence of
\big(\delta,\mathcal{S}_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})\big)
-substationary points remains as a theoretical challenge (see Remark 4.1 in
[
21
]
). This problem is comprehensively solved in our convergence analysis.

By means of numerical tests, we show the efficiency and ability of the proposed method in practice. For this purpose, we first analyze the typical behavior of the method on a bi-objective test problem. Next, by using the multi-start technique, we assess the efficiency of our method in approximating the Pareto front. In our third experiment, we seek a sparse solution to an underdetermined linear system by minimizing a nonsmooth bi-objective model. Eventually, a set of nonsmooth convex
and nonconvex test problems are considered to provide some comparative results with the multiobjective proximal bundle method proposed by Mäkelä et al.
[
27
]
.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Some required basic concepts are provided in Section
1
. Our main strategy for finding a descent direction along with finite convergence property of the proposed line search is given in Section
3
. Global convergence is studied in Section
4
. Numerical results are reported in Section
5
, and Section
6
concludes the paper.

Mathematical preliminaries

Throughout this paper, we use the following notations. The usual inner product in the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is denoted by
\mathbf{x}^{T}\mathbf{y}
, which induces the Euclidean norm
\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert=(\mathbf{x}^{T}\mathbf{x})^{1/2}
. An open ball with center
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and radius
\varepsilon>0
is denoted by
\mathcal{B}(\mathbf{x},\varepsilon)
, that is,

\mathcal{B}(\mathbf{x},\varepsilon):=\{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,\,:\,\,\lVert\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\rVert<\varepsilon\}.

Moreover,
\mathbb{N}
is the set of natural numbers,
\mathbb{N}_{0}:=\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
, and
\mathbb{R}_{+}:=(0,\infty).

Suppose
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is a locally Lipschitz function. Then, by Rademacher’s theorem
[
28
]
,
f
is differentiable almost everywhere on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Let

\Omega_{f}:=\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,\,:\,\,f\,\,\text{is not differentiable at}\,\,\mathbf{x}\}.

Then, the Clarke subdifferential of
f
at a point
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is defined as
[
29
]

\partial f(\mathbf{x}):=\texttt{conv}\{\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,\,:\,\,\exists\,\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega_{f}\,\,\,\,\text{s.t.}\,\,\,\,\mathbf{x}_{i}\to\mathbf{x}\,\,\text{and}\,\,\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{i})\to\boldsymbol{\xi}\},

where
conv
denotes the convex hull of a set. Furthermore, for any
\varepsilon>0,
the (Goldstein)
\varepsilon
-subdifferential of
f
at a point
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the set
[
26
]

\partial_{\varepsilon}f(\mathbf{x}):=\texttt{cl\,conv}\{\partial f(\mathbf{y})\,\,:\,\,\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbf{x},\varepsilon)\},

in which
cl conv
is the closure of the convex hull. For any
\varepsilon>0
and
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the set
\partial_{\varepsilon}f(\mathbf{x})
is a nonempty, convex and compact subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. If
f
is differentiable at
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then
\nabla f(\mathbf{x})\in\partial f(\mathbf{x})
. Furthermore, if
f
is smooth at
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we have
\partial f(\mathbf{x})=\{\nabla f(\mathbf{x})\}
. Also, the set-valued map
\partial_{\varepsilon}f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{n}
is locally bounded and upper semicontinuous
[
29
]
. The generalized directional derivative of function
f
at point
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and direction
\mathbf{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is given by
[
29
]

f^{\circ}(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{d}):=\limsup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{y}\to\mathbf{x}\\
t\downarrow 0\end{subarray}}\frac{f(\mathbf{y}+t\mathbf{d})-f(\mathbf{y})}{t}.

For the class of locally Lipschitz functions,
f^{\circ}(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{d})
always exists for any
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and it is shown that
f^{\circ}(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{d})=\max\{\boldsymbol{\xi}^{T}\mathbf{d}\,\,:\,\,\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\partial f(\mathbf{x})\}

[
29
]
.

The notion of
Pareto optimality
is of crucial importance in multiobjective optimization. A point
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is called Pareto optimal for problem (
1
) if there is not any
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
f_{i}(\mathbf{y})\leq f_{i}(\mathbf{x})
, for all
i=1,\ldots,p
, and
f_{j}(\mathbf{y})<f_{j}(\mathbf{x})
, for some
j\in\{1,\ldots,p\}

[
2
]
. It is recalled that for a point
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
to be a Pareto optimal, it is necessary that
[
1
]

\mathbf{0}\in\texttt{conv}\left\{\cup_{i=1}^{p}\partial f_{i}(\mathbf{x})\right\}.

A point
\mathbf{x}
which satisfies the above condition is called a
Clarke substationary
point.

In the current paper, the notion of weakly upper semismoothness is important in designing an effective line search procedure.

Definition 2.1

(
[
30
]
)
.

The locally Lipschitz function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is called weakly upper semismooth if for any
\mathbf{z},\mathbf{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and sequences
\{\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i}\}_{i}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\{h_{i}\}_{i}\subset\mathbb{R}_{+}
satisfying
h_{i}\downarrow 0
as
i\to\infty
and
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i}\in\partial f(\mathbf{z}+h_{i}\mathbf{d})
, one has

\limsup_{i\to\infty}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i}^{T}\mathbf{d}\geq\liminf_{i\to\infty}\frac{f(\mathbf{z}+h_{i}\mathbf{d})-f(\mathbf{z})}{h_{i}}.

The class of weakly upper semismooth functions encompasses a wide range of functions, including convex, concave, and functions of max- and min-type
[
30
,
31
,
32
]
. In fact, semismoothness is a popular assumption which is widely used in the context of general nonsmooth nonconvex optimization
[
6
,
10
,
33
]
.

3
Finding a descent direction

In this section, we develop an iterative procedure to find an efficient descent direction for problem (
1
) at a given point
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. A direction
\mathbf{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is called a descent direction for problem (
1
) at point
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
if there exists
z>0
such that

f_{i}(\mathbf{x}+t\mathbf{d})-f_{i}(\mathbf{x})<0,\quad\text{for all}\,\,t\in(0,z),\,\,\text{and}\,\,i=1,\ldots,p.

(2)

The following lemma, which is essentially well-known, presents a theoretical approach to find a descent direction. For the sake of completeness, we provide a proof. Before it, let us denote the normal cone
[
34
]
of the convex set
C\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
at a point
\mathbf{x}\in C
by
\mathcal{N}(C,\mathbf{x})
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Assume
\boldsymbol{\xi}^{*}\neq\mathbf{0}
solves the following minimization problem:

\min\big\{\lVert\boldsymbol{\xi}\rVert\,\,:\,\,\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\text{\rm{conv}}\{\cup_{i=1}^{p}\partial f_{i}(\mathbf{x})\}\big\}.

(3)

Then the normalized direction

{\bar{\mathbf{d}}}:=-\boldsymbol{\xi}^{*}/\lVert\boldsymbol{\xi}^{*}\rVert,

(4)

is a descent direction for multiobjective problem (
1
) at point
\mathbf{x}
.

Proof.

Compactness of
\texttt{conv}\{\cup_{i=1}^{p}\partial f_{i}(\mathbf{x})\}
yields the existence of
\boldsymbol{\xi}^{*}
. Since
\boldsymbol{\xi}^{*}
solves problem (
3
), we deduce
[
35
]

-\boldsymbol{\xi}^{*}\in\mathcal{N}\left(\texttt{conv}\{\cup_{i=1}^{p}\partial f_{i}(\mathbf{x})\},\boldsymbol{\xi}^{*}\right),

yielding

\boldsymbol{\xi}^{T}\boldsymbol{\xi}^{*}\geq\lVert\boldsymbol{\xi}^{*}\rVert^{2},\quad\text{for all}\,\,\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\texttt{conv}\{\cup_{i=1}^{p}\partial f_{i}(\mathbf{x})\},

which is equivalent to

-\boldsymbol{\xi}^{T}\frac{\boldsymbol{\xi}^{*}}{\lVert\boldsymbol{\xi}^{*}\rVert}\leq-\lVert\boldsymbol{\xi}^{*}\rVert<0,\quad\text{for all}\,\,\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\texttt{conv}\{\cup_{i=1}^{p}\partial f_{i}(\mathbf{x})\}.

Consequently,
\boldsymbol{\xi}^{T}\mathbf{d}<0
for all
\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\texttt{conv}\{\cup_{i=1}^{p}\partial f_{i}(\mathbf{x})\}
, which means
f^{\circ}_{i}(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{d})<0
, for
i=1,\ldots,p
, and the assertion follows immediately from the definition of
f^{\circ}(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{d})
.
∎

For some
i\in\{1,\ldots,p\}
, assume
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a continuously differentiable point for the objective
f_{i}
, which is close to a nonsmooth region. In this case, the subdifferential set
\partial f_{i}(\mathbf{x})
coincides with the singleton
\{\nabla f_{i}(\mathbf{x})\}
. In fact,
\partial f_{i}(\mathbf{x})
does not contain any useful information of the nearby nonsmooth region, and consequently the search direction
{\bar{\mathbf{d}}}
given by (
4
) may not be an appropriate descent direction. More precisely, when
\mathbf{x}
is near a point where
f_{i}
is not differentiable, this search direction fulfills decrease condition (
2
) only for very small values of
t
. In contrast,
\partial_{\varepsilon}f_{i}(\mathbf{x})
can capture some local information of the nearby nonsmooth region, and one can stabilize our choice of search direction by replacing
\partial f_{i}(\mathbf{x})
by
\partial_{\varepsilon}f_{i}(\mathbf{x})
. In this respect, the following minimization problem is considered:

\min\big\{\lVert\boldsymbol{\xi}\rVert\,\,:\,\,\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\texttt{conv}\{\cup_{i=1}^{p}\partial_{\varepsilon}f_{i}(\mathbf{x})\}\big\}.

(5)

One major difficulty with problem (
5
) is that, for each objective
f_{i}
, we need to know the entire subdifferential
\partial_{\varepsilon}f_{i}(\mathbf{x})
, which is not an easy task in many practical situations. In this regard, we develop an iterative algorithm to efficiently approximate
\partial_{\varepsilon}f_{i}(\mathbf{x})
, for
i=1,\ldots,p
.

For given
\varepsilon>0,m\in\mathbb{N}
and
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, let

\mathbf{\mathcal{S}}_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x}):=\left[\begin{array}[]{cccc}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1,1}&\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1,2}&\ldots&\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1,p}\\
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{2,1}&\boldsymbol{\xi}_{2,2}&\ldots&\boldsymbol{\xi}_{2,p}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{m,1}&\boldsymbol{\xi}_{m,2}&\ldots&\boldsymbol{\xi}_{m,p}\end{array}\right],

be a collection of subgradients computed throughout iterations
1
to
m
having the property that

\boldsymbol{\xi}_{j,i}\in\partial_{\varepsilon}f_{i}(\mathbf{x}),\quad\text{for all}\,\,i=1,\ldots,p,\,\,\text{and}\,\,j=1,\ldots,m.

In fact, the
i
-th column of
\mathbf{\mathcal{S}}_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})
stores a number of elements of
\partial_{\varepsilon}f_{i}(\mathbf{x})
. We denote the
i
-th column of
\mathbf{\mathcal{S}}_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})
by
\mathbf{\mathcal{S}}_{\varepsilon,i}(\mathbf{x})
. Then, for each
i\in\{1,\ldots,p\}
,

\texttt{conv}\{\mathbf{\mathcal{S}}_{\varepsilon,i}(\mathbf{x})\}\subset\partial_{\varepsilon}f_{i}(\mathbf{x}),

is an inner approximation of
\partial_{\varepsilon}f_{i}(\mathbf{x})
, and consequently a practical variant of problem (
5
) can be given by

\min\big\{\lVert\boldsymbol{\xi}\rVert\,\,:\,\,\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\texttt{conv}\{\cup_{i=1}^{p}\mathbf{\mathcal{S}}_{\varepsilon,i}(\mathbf{x})\}\big\}.

(6)

Suppose
\boldsymbol{\xi}^{*}\neq\mathbf{0}
solves problem (
6
), and define the normalized direction
{\mathbf{d}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
by

{\mathbf{d}}:=-\boldsymbol{\xi}^{*}/\lVert\boldsymbol{\xi}^{*}\rVert.

If
\texttt{conv}\{\cup_{i=1}^{p}\mathbf{\mathcal{S}}_{\varepsilon,i}(\mathbf{x})\}
is an adequate approximation of
\texttt{conv}\{\cup_{i=1}^{p}\partial_{\varepsilon}f_{i}(\mathbf{x})\}
, then one can find the step size
t>0
satisfying

f_{i}(\mathbf{x}+t{\mathbf{d}})-f_{i}(\mathbf{x})\leq-\beta t\lVert\boldsymbol{\xi}^{*}\rVert,\quad\text{for all}\,\,i=1,\ldots,p,\quad\text{and}\quad t\geq\bar{t},

(7)

in which
\beta\in(0,1)
is the Armijo parameter which guarantees a sufficient decrease in objectives
f_{i}
, and the lower bound
\bar{t}\in(0,\varepsilon)
excludes small values for the step size
t
. In the case that condition (
7
) is met, we employ the search direction
\mathbf{d}
for taking a serious step, i.e., the current point
\mathbf{x}
is updated by
\mathbf{x}^{+}:=\mathbf{x}+t\mathbf{d}.

Otherwise, we infer that our approximation of
\texttt{conv}\{\cup_{i=1}^{p}\partial_{\varepsilon}f_{i}(\mathbf{x})\}
must be improved. For this purpose, let

\mathcal{I}:=\left\{i\in\{1,\ldots,p\}\,\,:\,\,\nexists\,\,t\geq\bar{t}\,\,\,\,\text{\rm s.t.}\,\,\,\,f_{i}(\mathbf{x}+t{\mathbf{d}})-f_{i}(\mathbf{x})\leq-\beta t\lVert\boldsymbol{\xi}^{*}\rVert\right\}.

(8)

Then, for each
i\in\mathcal{I}
, we need to improve our approximation of
\partial_{\varepsilon}f_{i}(\mathbf{x})
. However, in practice, computing the index set
\mathcal{I}
is not possible because, for some
i
, checking the condition

\nexists\,\,t\geq\bar{t}\,\,\,\,\text{\rm s.t.}\,\,\,\,f_{i}(\mathbf{x}+t{\mathbf{d}})-f_{i}(\mathbf{x})\leq-\beta t\lVert\boldsymbol{\xi}^{*}\rVert,

is impractical. Therefore, in Algorithm
1
, we propose a limited backtracking line search to provide an estimation of the index set
\mathcal{I}
, namely
\tilde{\mathcal{I}}
. Regarding this algorithm, some explanations are as follows. The algorithm consists of two loops. The upper loop tests the sufficient decrease condition (
7
) for a limited number of step sizes. If the algorithm terminates within this loop, we conclude
\mathcal{I}=\emptyset
, and hence
\tilde{\mathcal{I}}=\emptyset
is returned. Moreover, one can employ the obtained step size
t\geq\bar{t}>0
to take a serious step. Otherwise, the algorithm executes the lower loop, in which the step size
\bar{t}>0
is used to provide the nonempty estimation
\tilde{\mathcal{I}}
of
\mathcal{I}
. Furthermore, the step size
t
is set to zero as an indication of taking a null step. Consequently, at termination of Algorithm
1
, one of the following cases occurs:

•

Case I. Algorithm
1
returns
\{\tilde{\mathcal{I}},t\}
with
\tilde{\mathcal{I}}=\emptyset
and
t\geq\bar{t}>0
.

•

Case II. Algorithm
1
returns
\{\tilde{\mathcal{I}},t\}
with
\tilde{\mathcal{I}}\neq\emptyset
and
t=0
.

Objectives
\{f_{i}\}_{i=1}^{p}
, current point

, search direction
{\mathbf{d}}=-\boldsymbol{\xi}^{*}/\lVert\boldsymbol{\xi}^{*}\rVert

\tilde{\mathcal{I}}\subset\{1,\ldots,p\}
.

=

(

)

\tilde{\mathcal{I}}:=\emptyset
;

3:

, for all
i=1,\ldots,p
,
then

4:

return
{
t,\tilde{\mathcal{I}}
} and
STOP
;

5:

end

6:

end

for

7:

for

i\in\{1,\ldots,p\}

do

8:

if

f_{i}(\mathbf{x}+\bar{t}{\mathbf{d}})-f_{i}(\mathbf{x})>-\beta\bar{t}\,\lVert\boldsymbol{\xi}^{*}\rVert
,
then

9:

\tilde{\mathcal{I}}:=\tilde{\mathcal{I}}\cup\{i\};

10:

end

if

11:

end

Set

;

{

} and

;

Algorithm 1

Limited Backtracking Line Search (LBLS)

If Case I takes place, as already mentioned, we take a serious step by setting
\mathbf{x}^{+}:=\mathbf{x}+t\mathbf{d}
. Thus, let us proceed with Case II. In this case, we append the following row of subgradients

[\boldsymbol{\xi}_{m+1,1},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{m+1,2},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\xi}_{m+1,p}],

to the current collection
\mathbf{\mathcal{S}}_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})
such that

\displaystyle\begin{split}&(\text{i})\,\,\boldsymbol{\xi}_{m+1,i}:=\boldsymbol{\xi}_{m,i},\,\,\text{for all}\,\,i\notin\tilde{\mathcal{I}}.\\
&(\text{ii})\,\,\boldsymbol{\xi}_{m+1,i}\in\partial_{\varepsilon}f_{i}(\mathbf{x})\,\,\text{and}\,\,\boldsymbol{\xi}_{m+1,i}\notin\texttt{conv}\{\mathcal{S}_{\varepsilon,i}(\mathbf{x})\},\,\,\text{for all}\,\,i\in\tilde{\mathcal{I}}.\end{split}

(9)

The first part of condition (
9
) states that for the objectives
f_{i}
with
i\notin\tilde{\mathcal{I}}
, we do not compute a new element of
\partial_{\varepsilon}f_{i}(\mathbf{x})
. By the second part of this condition, for each
i\in\tilde{\mathcal{I}}
, we compute a new element of
\partial_{\varepsilon}f_{i}(\mathbf{x})
which significantly improves our approximation of

(32)

for all
t\in(0,1)
. It is easy to see that

t^{*}:=2(1-c)\lVert\boldsymbol{\xi}^{*}_{k}\lVert^{2}/8\bar{C}^{2}\in(0,1),

is the minimum point of
\varphi(t)
and

\varphi(t^{*})=\left(1-\frac{(1-c)^{2}\lVert\boldsymbol{\xi}^{*}_{k}\lVert^{2}}{4\bar{C}^{2}}\right)\lVert\boldsymbol{\xi}^{*}_{k}\lVert^{2}.

In virtue of (
26
), the above equality gives

\varphi(t^{*})\leq\left(1-\frac{(1-c)^{2}\delta^{2}}{4\bar{C}^{2}}\right)\lVert\boldsymbol{\xi}^{*}_{k}\lVert^{2}.

(33)

Since
\delta\leq\bar{C}
and
c\in(0,1)
, we get

\alpha:=1-\frac{(1-c)^{2}\delta^{2}}{4\bar{C}^{2}}\in(0,1).

Next, it follows from (
32
) and (
33
) that

0\leq\lVert\boldsymbol{\xi}^{*}_{k+1}\rVert^{2}\leq\varphi(t^{*})\leq\alpha\lVert\boldsymbol{\xi}^{*}_{k}\lVert^{2},\quad\text{for all}\,\,k>\bar{k},

(34)

yielding the sequence
\{\lVert\boldsymbol{\xi}^{*}_{k}\rVert^{2}\}_{k>\bar{k}}
is bounded from below and decreasing. Thus, it converges. Let
\lVert\boldsymbol{\xi}^{*}_{k}\rVert^{2}\to A
as
k\to\infty
. Letting
k\to\infty
in inequality (
32
), we obtain
0\leq A\leq\alpha A
. Since
\alpha\in(0,1)
, we deduce
A=0
. Consequently,
\lVert\boldsymbol{\xi}^{*}_{k}\rVert^{2}\to 0
as
k\to\infty
, which contradicts (
26
).

∎

To study the asymptotic behavior of Algorithm

, one may assume

. In this case, the algorithm generates the infinite sequence
\{\mathbf{x}_{\nu}\}_{\nu}
. In the next theorem, it is proved that any accumulation point of the sequence
\{\mathbf{x}_{\nu}\}_{\nu}
is Clarke substationary for problem (

).

Theorem 4.3
.

Suppose that Assumption
4.1
holds. If
\rho=0
in Algorithm
4
, then any accumulation point of the sequence
\{\mathbf{x}_{\nu}\}_{\nu}
generated by this algorithm is Clarke substationary for problem (
1
).

Proof.

During each iteration
\nu\geq 0
of Algorithm
4
, the
DS-SP
algorithm is run and terminated after finite number of iterations. For any
\nu\geq 0
, suppose that this algorithm is terminated at iteration
k_{\nu}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Thus

\mathbf{x}_{\nu+1}=\mathbf{x}_{k_{\nu}}=\,\texttt{DS-SP}\,(\{f_{i}\}_{i=1}^{p},\mathbf{x}_{\nu},\varepsilon_{\nu},\delta_{\nu},\beta),

and therefore

\min\big\{\lVert\boldsymbol{\xi}\lVert\,\,:\,\,\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\text{\rm{conv}}\{\cup_{i=1}^{p}\mathbf{\mathcal{S}}^{k_{\nu}}_{\varepsilon_{\nu},i}(\mathbf{x}_{\nu+1})\}\big\}\leq\delta_{\nu},\quad\text{for all}\,\,\nu\geq 0.

(35)

We note that
\mathbf{x}_{\nu}\in lev_{f_{j}(\mathbf{x}_{0})}(f_{j})
, for all
\nu\geq 0
, and by Assumption
4.1
, the sub-level set
lev_{f_{j}(\mathbf{x}_{0})}(f_{j})
is bounded. Consequently, the sequence
\{\mathbf{x}_{\nu}\}_{\nu}
has at least one accumulation point, say
\mathbf{x}^{*}
, i.e., there exists
\mathcal{V}\subset\mathbb{N}_{0}
such that
\mathbf{x}_{\nu}\xrightarrow{\mathcal{V}}\mathbf{x}^{*}
. This fact along with (
35
) implies

\min\big\{\lVert\boldsymbol{\xi}\lVert\,\,:\,\,\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\text{\rm{conv}}\{\cup_{i=1}^{p}\mathbf{\mathcal{S}}^{k_{\nu}}_{\varepsilon_{\nu},i}(\mathbf{x}_{\nu+1})\}\big\}\leq\delta_{\nu},\quad\text{for all}\,\,\nu\in\mathcal{V}.

(36)

Let
\kappa>0
be arbitrary. As
\delta_{\nu}\downarrow 0
as
\nu\to\infty
, one can find
\bar{\nu}\in\mathcal{V}
sufficiently large such that
\delta_{\nu}<\kappa
, for all
\nu\geq\bar{\nu}
. Let
\mathcal{V}^{\prime}:=\{\nu\in\mathcal{V}\,:\,\nu\geq\bar{\nu}\}\subset\mathcal{V}
. Then, it immediately follows from (
36
) that, for all
\nu\in\mathcal{V}^{\prime}
,

\lVert\boldsymbol{\xi}^{*}_{\nu}\rVert:=\min\big\{\lVert\boldsymbol{\xi}\lVert\,\,:\,\,\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\text{\rm{conv}}\{\cup_{i=1}^{p}\mathbf{\mathcal{S}}^{k_{\nu}}_{\varepsilon_{\nu},i}(\mathbf{x}_{\nu+1})\}\big\}\leq\kappa.

(37)

In addition, since

\boldsymbol{\xi}^{*}_{\nu}\in\text{\rm{conv}}\{\cup_{i=1}^{p}\mathbf{\mathcal{S}}^{k_{\nu}}_{\varepsilon_{\nu},i}(\mathbf{x}_{\nu+1})\}\subset\text{\rm{conv}}\{\cup_{i=1}^{p}\partial_{\varepsilon_{\nu}}f_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{\nu+1})\},\quad\text{for all}\,\,\nu\in\mathcal{V}^{\prime},

there exist

\boldsymbol{\xi}^{*}_{\nu,i}\in\partial_{\varepsilon_{\nu}}f_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{\nu+1}),\,\,\lambda_{\nu,i}\in\mathbb{R},\quad i=1,\ldots,p,

satisfying

\boldsymbol{\xi}^{*}_{\nu}=\sum_{i=1}^{p}\lambda_{\nu,i}\boldsymbol{\xi}^{*}_{\nu,i},\quad\sum_{i=1}^{p}\lambda_{\nu,i}=1,\quad\lambda_{\nu,i}\geq 0,\,\,\,i=1,\ldots,p,

(38)

for all
\nu\in\mathcal{V}^{\prime}
. The fact that
\mathbf{x}_{\nu}\xrightarrow{\mathcal{V}^{\prime}}\mathbf{x}^{*}
along with uniformly compactness of the map
\partial_{\cdot}f_{i}(\cdot)
implies that the sequences
\{\boldsymbol{\xi}^{*}_{\nu,i}\}_{\nu\in\mathcal{V}^{\prime}}
, for
i=1,\ldots,p
, are bounded. Now, boundedness of the sequences
\{\lambda_{\nu,i}\}_{\nu\in\mathcal{V}^{\prime}}
, for
i=1,\ldots,p
, implies the existence of the infinite set
\mathcal{V}^{\prime\prime}\subset\mathcal{V}^{\prime}
such that

\boldsymbol{\xi}^{*}_{\nu,i}\xrightarrow{\mathcal{V}^{\prime\prime}}\boldsymbol{\xi}^{*}_{i},\quad\lambda_{\nu,i}\xrightarrow{\mathcal{V}^{\prime\prime}}\lambda_{i},\quad\lambda_{i}\geq 0,\quad\sum_{i=1}^{p}\lambda_{i}=1,\quad\text{for all}\,\,i=1,\ldots,p.

(39)

Furthermore, as the map
\partial_{\cdot}f_{i}(\cdot)
is upper semicontinuous, one can deduce

\boldsymbol{\xi}^{*}_{i}\in\partial f_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{*}),\quad\text{for all}\,\,i=1,\ldots,p.

(40)

Eventually, in view of (
38
), (
39
) and (
40
), we conclude

\boldsymbol{\xi}^{*}_{\nu}=\sum_{i=1}^{p}\lambda_{\nu,i}\boldsymbol{\xi}^{*}_{\nu,i}\xrightarrow{\mathcal{V}^{\prime\prime}}\sum_{i=1}^{p}\lambda_{i}\boldsymbol{\xi}^{*}_{i}\in\texttt{conv}\{\cup_{i=1}^{p}\partial f_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{*})\}.

(41)

Consequently, (
37
) and (
41
) yield

\min\big\{\lVert\boldsymbol{\xi}\rVert\,\,:\,\,\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\texttt{conv}\{\cup_{i=1}^{p}\partial f_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{*})\}\big\}\leq\kappa.

Since
\kappa>0
was arbitrary, we conclude
\mathbf{0}\in\texttt{conv}\{\cup_{i=1}^{p}\partial f_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{*})\}
.

HRS
provides a relative measure of this gap compared to the average gap size. Obviously, for these indicators, a lower value is desirable.

5.3
Sparse solution to an underdetermined linear system

In this experiment, we consider the following linear system

\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b},

(45)

in which,
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
with
m<n
, and
\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. This system is referred to as underdetermined and typically has an infinite number of solutions. Such systems appear in various practical problems, including data fitting, machine learning, denoising, and image processing. The main goal is to find an approximate solution to linear system (
45
) that achieves the highest level of sparsity. The classic approach to formulate this problem is as follows:

\displaystyle\min\,\,\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}\rVert_{2}^{2}+\lambda\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert_{1},\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

(46)

where
\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert_{1}
denotes the
l_{1}
norm of the vector
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and
\lambda>0
is a positive parameter. However, choosing an appropriate value for the parameter
\lambda
is a delicate and challenging issue in effectively solving this problem
[
41
]
. In this regard, one alternative interesting approach is to formulate the problem by the following bi-objective optimization problem:

\displaystyle\min\,\,(\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert_{1},\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}\rVert_{2}^{2})\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

(47)

This problem is convex, but nonsmooth due to the use of
l_{1}
norm. To create an instance of problem (
47
), we randomly generated the matrix
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{50\times 100}
and vector
\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{50}
. The
SUMOPT
was then applied to this problem using five randomly generated starting points from a unit box, and the optimality tolerance
\rho=10^{-2}
. For each run, the obtained results have been reported in Table
4
.

Conclusion

We have developed a descent subgradient method for finding a Clarke substationary point of a general multiobjective optimization problem, whose objectives are locally Lipschitz. As opposed to the multiobjective proximal bundle method proposed by Mäkelä et al., we do not turn the main problem into a single-objective one by using an improvement function. Instead, in order to find an effective descent direction for the main problem, the behavior of each individual objective is separately studied. This framework allows us to make use of a limited backtracking line search, which helps us determine the objectives for which improving the current approximation of the
\varepsilon
-subdifferential set is unnecessary. This strategy reduced the number of subgradient evaluations during the optimization process.

Regarding the enrichment process of the current collection of the subgradient information, the proposed method shares a common feature with bundle-type methods, in that the
\varepsilon
-subdifferential sets are sequentially improved. In this regard, we presented a new variant of Mifflin’s line search that provides a non-redundant subgradient, which significantly modifies the current working set associated with each objective in the improvement process. We proved that such a subgradient element can be found through a finite procedure by establishing a relationship between the limited backtracking line search and the proposed Mifflin’s line search. We studied the global convergence of the method to a Clarke substationary point, which strengthened the results derived in the method proposed by Gebken and Peitz.

In our numerical tests, we assessed the typical behavior of our algorithm,
SUMOPT
, and observed the efficiency of the proposed limited backtracking line search in excluding objective functions from the improvement process. Meanwhile, the proposed Mifflin’s line search showed its ability in modifying the inefficient search directions, after each null step. Next, we showed the efficiency of our method in generating a uniform approximation of the Pareto fronts. For finding a sparse solution of an underdetermined linear system, our method revealed a trade-off between the sparsity of the solution and the value of the remainder along the Pareto front of the problem. Eventually, our comparative results based on fifteen nonsmooth test problems showed that
SUMOPT
is a big rival to bundle-like methods, especially in case the computation of subgradients is a time-consuming process.
