Scalar product and distance in barycentric coordinates
Vladimir Zolotov

Abstract
. We give a formula for the scalar product and the distance in generalized barycentric coordinates for Euclidean and Pseudo-Euclidean spaces.

2.
Fixed objects

Suppose we have points
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
in an
m
-dimensional
Euclidean (or Pseudo-Euclidean) space
(M,\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)

such that the affine span of
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
is the whole
M
.
Let’s fix those
(M,\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)
and
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}

for the whole paper to avoid reintroducing
them in every single statement.

For a vector
v\in M
will use the notation
||v||^{2}:=\langle v,v\rangle
. Note that in the case when
M
is a
Pseudo-Euclidean space

||v||^{2}
can be negative.

We denote by
D
the distance matrix for
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
,
in other words,
D_{ij}=||x_{i}-x_{j}||^{2}
.

3.
Generalized barycentric coordinates

Definition 1
.

For a point
p\in M
, we say that
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\in{\mathbb{R}}
are
generalized non-normalized barycentric coordinates
of
p
iff

a_{1}+\dots+a_{n}\neq 0

and

(a_{1}+\dots+a_{n})p=a_{1}x_{1}+\dots+a_{n}x_{n}.

We say that
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}
are
generalized absolute (or normalized) barycentric coordinates
of
p
iff

a_{1}+\dots+a_{n}=1

and

p=a_{1}x_{1}+\dots+a_{n}x_{n}.

Remark 2
.

The world ”generalized” means that
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
do not have
to be in a general position.
If they are in a general position
then that’s just the usual barycentric coordinates.
In this case for the fixed point
p
absolute barycentric
coordinates are unique
and non-normalized barycentric coordinates are
unique up to a non-zero scaling factor.

Generalized barycentric coordinates have applications in finite
element methods, computer graphics, and the localization problem
for sensor networks.
For more information on generalized barycentric
coordinates see a survey
[
2
]
.

4.
Generalized displacement vector

Definition 3
.

For a vector
v\in M
, we say that a vector

\bar{v}=(\bar{v}_{1},\dots,\bar{v}_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

is a
generalized displacement vector
iff
there exist a point
p
with generalized absolute
barycentric coordinates
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}

such that the point
(v+p)

has generalized absolute barycentric
coordinates
a_{1}+\bar{v}_{1},\dots,a_{n}+\bar{v}_{n}
.

Remark 4
.

Using that for generalized absolute barycentric
coordinates
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}
for a point
p
we have

\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}=1

we get that for any generalized displacement
vector
\bar{v}=(\bar{v}_{1},\dots,\bar{v}_{n})
we have

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bar{v}_{i}=0.

Remark 5
.

If
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
are in general position (so our coordinates are not generalized)
then for any
v
, there is a unique displacement vector
\bar{v}
.
Otherwise, there are multiple generalized displacement
vectors for a single vector
v
.

5.
The formula for the scalar product (and the distance)

We remind that
D
denotes the distance matrix
for
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
in other words

D_{ij}=||x_{i}-x_{j}||^{2}
.

Theorem 1
.

Suppose we have vectors
u,v\in M
and
\bar{u}
,
\bar{v}

are their generalized displacement vectors. Then

(BSP)

\langle u,v\rangle=\bar{u}^{T}\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}D\right)\bar{v}.

Remark 6
.

A trivial corollary is that

||u||^{2}=\bar{u}^{T}\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}D\right)\bar{u}.

Before giving the proof of Theorem
1
we establish
a couple of lemmas providing more structure for the argument.

Lemma 7
.

Let
{\lambda}_{1},\dots,{\lambda}_{n}\geq 0
be such that

\sum_{i=0}^{n}{\lambda}_{i}=1
and
y\in M
then

\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\lambda}_{i}||y-x_{i}||^{2}=||y-\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\lambda}_{i}x_{i}||^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\lambda}_{i}||x_{i}-\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\lambda}_{j}x_{j}||^{2}.

Remark 8
.

The other way of stating this lemma is that for a point
p\in M
and a random point
X
supported on
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
we have

{\mathbb{E}\,}||y-X||^{2}=||y-{\mathbb{E}\,}X||^{2}+{\mathbb{E}\,}||X-{\mathbb{E}\,}X||^{2}.

Proof of Lemma
7
.

We start from the left-hand side and eventually transform
it into the right-hand side:

\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\lambda}_{i}||y-x_{i}||^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\lambda}_{i}\langle y-x_{i},y-x_{i}\rangle=

=\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\lambda}_{i}\langle(y-\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\lambda}_{j}x_{j})-(x_{i}-\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\lambda}_{j}x_{j}),(y-\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\lambda}_{j}x_{j})-(x_{i}-\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\lambda}_{j}x_{j})\rangle.

By applying
\langle u-v,u-v\rangle=||u||^{2}+||v||^{2}-2\langle u,v\rangle
to the above it rewrites as

||y-\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\lambda}_{j}x_{j}||^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\lambda}_{i}||x_{i}-\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\lambda}_{j}x_{j}||^{2}-2\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\lambda}_{i}\langle y-\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\lambda}_{j}x_{j},x_{i}-\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\lambda}_{j}x_{j}\rangle.

This is the right-hand side of our formula, except the last summand which is extra. But that’s not a problem because it is zero, indeed:

\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\lambda}_{i}\langle y-\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\lambda}_{j}x_{j},x_{i}-\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\lambda}_{j}x_{j}\rangle=

=\langle y-\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\lambda}_{j}x_{j},\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\lambda}_{i}(x_{i}-\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\lambda}_{j}x_{j})\rangle=

=\langle y-\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\lambda}_{j}x_{j},0\rangle=0.

∎

Lemma 9
.

Let
{\lambda}_{1},\dots,{\lambda}_{n}\geq 0
and
\nu_{1},\dots,\nu_{n}\geq 0
be such that
\sum_{i=0}^{n}{\lambda}_{i}=1
,
\sum_{i=0}^{n}\nu_{i}=1
.
Denote
\Lambda=({\lambda}_{1},\dots,{\lambda}_{n})^{T}
,
{\mathcal{V}}=(\nu_{1},\dots,\nu_{n})^{T}
then

\Lambda^{T}D{\mathcal{V}}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}{\lambda}_{i}\nu_{j}||x_{i}-x_{j}||^{2}=

=\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\lambda}_{i}||x_{i}-\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\lambda}_{j}x_{j}||^{2}+||\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\lambda}_{j}x_{j}-\sum_{j=1}^{n}\nu_{j}x_{j}||^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\nu_{i}||\sum_{j=1}^{n}\nu_{j}x_{j}-x_{i}||^{2}.

Remark 10
.

This statement also can be presented via probability language:
Let
X
and
Y
be two independent random variables supported on
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
. Then

{\mathbb{E}\,}||X-Y||^{2}={\mathbb{E}\,}||X-{\mathbb{E}\,}X||^{2}+||{\mathbb{E}\,}X-{\mathbb{E}\,}Y||^{2}+{\mathbb{E}\,}||{\mathbb{E}\,}Y-Y||^{2}.

Proof of Lemma
9
.

We start from the left-hand side and apply Lemma
7
:

\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}{\lambda}_{i}\nu_{j}||x_{i}-x_{j}||^{2}=\sum_{i}^{n}{\lambda}_{i}\sum_{j}^{n}\nu_{j}||x_{i}-x_{j}||^{2}=

=\sum_{i}^{n}{\lambda}_{i}\Big(||x_{i}-\sum_{k=1}^{n}\nu_{k}x_{k}||^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\nu_{j}||x_{j}-\sum_{k=1}^{n}\nu_{k}x_{k}||^{2}\Big)

=\sum_{i}^{n}{\lambda}_{i}\Big(||x_{i}-\sum_{k=1}^{n}\nu_{k}x_{k}||^{2}\Big)+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\nu_{j}||x_{j}-\sum_{k=1}^{n}\nu_{k}x_{k}||^{2}.

By applying Lemma 8 to the first summand, we get the right-hand side of the desired equality.

\sum_{i}^{n}{\lambda}_{i}||x_{i}-\sum_{k=1}^{n}{\lambda}_{k}x_{k}||^{2}+||\sum_{k=1}^{n}{\lambda}_{k}x_{k}-\sum_{k=1}^{n}\nu_{k}x_{k}||^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\nu_{j}||x_{j}-\sum_{k=1}^{n}\nu_{j}x_{k}||^{2}.

∎

Proof of Theorem
1
.

Since
\bar{u}
and
\bar{v}
are generalized displacement vectors
for
u
and
v
, then there
exist points
u_{b}
,
u_{e}=u_{b}+u
,
v_{b}
,
v_{e}=v_{b}+v
with generalized absolute barycentric
coordinates

{\lambda}_{1},\dots,{\lambda}_{n},

{\lambda}_{1}+\bar{u}_{1},\dots,{\lambda}_{n}+\bar{u}_{n},

\nu_{1},\dots,\nu_{n},

\nu_{1}+\bar{v}_{1},\dots,\nu_{n}+\bar{v}_{n}.

We denote

\Lambda^{b}=({\lambda}_{1},\dots,{\lambda}_{n})^{T},

\Lambda^{e}=({\lambda}_{1}+\bar{u}_{1},\dots,{\lambda}_{n}+\bar{u}_{n})^{T},

{\mathcal{V}}^{b}=(\nu_{1},\dots,\nu_{n})^{T},

{\mathcal{V}}^{e}=(\nu_{1}+\bar{v}_{1},\dots,\nu_{n}+\bar{v}_{n})^{T},

and now

\Lambda^{e}=\Lambda^{b}+\bar{u}
,
{\mathcal{V}}^{e}={\mathcal{V}}^{b}+\bar{v}
.
We start from the right-hand side of the desired equality and transform
it to the left-hand side:

\bar{u}^{T}\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}D\right)\bar{u}=(\Lambda^{e}-\Lambda^{b})^{T}\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}D\right)(\Lambda^{e}-\Lambda^{b})=

=-\frac{1}{2}\big(\Lambda^{e}D{\mathcal{V}}^{e}+\Lambda^{b}D{\mathcal{V}}^{b}-\Lambda^{e}D{\mathcal{V}}^{b}-\Lambda^{b}D{\mathcal{V}}^{e}\big).

We apply Lemma
9
to each of the four summands.
All the summands coming from the first and third parts
of the right-hand side of Lemma
9
will cancel
each other, and we get

-\frac{1}{2}\big(||u_{e}-v_{e}||+||u_{b}-v_{b}||-||u_{e}-v_{b}||-||u_{b}-v_{e}||).

Now we apply
||x-y||=||x||+||y||-2\langle x,y\rangle
to each of the four summands and get the left-hand side
from Theorem
1
.

\langle u_{e},v_{e}\rangle+\langle u_{b},v_{b}\rangle-\langle u_{e},v_{b}\rangle-\langle u_{b},v_{e}\rangle=

\langle u_{e}-u_{b},v_{e}-v_{b}\rangle=\langle u,v\rangle.

∎

6.2.
Relationship between Gram matrix and distance matrix.

Let
p=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n}x_{i}
and
G
be a Gram matrix
for vectors
x_{1}-p,x_{2}-p,\dots,x_{n}-p
, then

G=\left(I-\tfrac{1}{n}J\right)\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}D\right)\left(I-\tfrac{1}{n}J\right),

where
J
is an
n\times n
matrix of all ones.
This last formula is the key ingredient
in the classical Multidimensional Scaling(MDS).
This formula can be seen as
n^{2}
statements,
one for each element of the Gram matrix.
Denote by
\mathbb{1}_{n}
a vector of all ones.
Note that
(e_{i}-\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{1}_{n})
is a generalized displacement vector for
(x_{i}-p)
.
Thus one can see that those
n^{2}
statements are precisely the
formula from Theorem
1
for
u,v\in\{x_{1}-p,x_{2}-p,\dots,x_{n}-p\}
.

6.3.
Negative type inequalities and embeddability
in Euclidean spaces.

Consider a metric space
Y=\{y_{1},\dots,y_{n}\}
with a distance
matrix
D^{Y}
, i.e.,
D^{Y}_{i,j}=d(y_{i},y_{j})
.

Y
allows an isometric embedding into some Euclidean space iff
for every
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}=0
we have

(6.1)

\lambda^{T}D^{Y}\lambda\leq 0.

Those are called negative type inequalities, see Geometry of Cuts and Metrics

[
1
]
.
Let’s identify points
y_{1},\dots,y_{n}
with some set of points
in
\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
, which are in general position.
There exists a unique Pseudo-Euclidean scalar product on
\mathbb{R}^{n-1}

such that it agrees with the metric given by
D^{Y}

(for example, you can get it from Theorem
1
).
Theorem
1
says that (
6.1
) condition
is a condition that the ”squared length”
of each vector in
\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
is non-negative.

6.4.
Sturm and Alexandrov spaces of non-negative curvature.

Consider a metric space
Y=\{y_{1},\dots,y_{n}\}
with a distance
matrix
D^{Y}
, i.e.,
D^{Y}_{i,j}=d^{2}(y_{i},y_{j})
.

Y
has non-negative curvature in the sense of
K.T. Sturm
[
13
]
iff
for every
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}=0
, and only one of
{\lambda}_{i}
is negative
we have

(6.2)

\lambda^{T}D^{Y}\lambda\leq 0.

Once again, let’s identify
y_{1},\dots,y_{n}
with some set of points
in
\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
, which are in general position.
And again, there exists a unique Pseudo-Euclidean
scalar product on
\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
,
such that it agrees with the metric given by
D^{Y}
.
Theorem
1
says that
Y
has non-negative curvature in sense of
K.T. Sturm iff each vector going from a vertex to
the opposite face has a non-negative ”squared length”.

The above was previously noted by N. Lebedeva and A. Petrunin
in the work
[
6
]
,
where they prove that
5
-point subsets of Alexandrov spaces
of non-negative
curvature are exactly
5
-point Sturm spaces
of non-negative curvature. See also

[
8
]
.

6.5.
Distance geometry and skipping realizations (embeddings).

One natural application for distance formula in barycentric coordinates
comes from dealing with intrinsic triangulations

[
12
,
3
,
11
,
10
]
.
In this setting, one has length of the edges of a triangle given to him but does not have
a planar realization of this triangle readily available to him and interested
in computing distances between points specified by their barycentric coordinates
with respect to the triangle vertices. Although one can construct a planar realization,
compute barycenters and use the Pythagorean theorem, the distance formula in
barycentric coordinates provides a much cleaner path.

Distance geometry (see
[
7
]
) is a field of
geometry dealing with the setting described
above. Namely, all the information available is given as some distances,
and in particular, the Euclidean realization is not given.
Since Theorem
1

only relies on distances, it may be useful in this field to
do some geometry prior to or without constructing a realization.
