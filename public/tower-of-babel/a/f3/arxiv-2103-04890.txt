Quasimodular forms and modular differential equations which are not apparent at cusps: I
Chang-Shou Lin and Yifan Yang

Abstract
. In this paper, we explore a two-way connection between quasimodular forms of depth 1 1 and a class of second-order modular differential equations with regular singularities on the upper half-plane and the cusps. Here we consider the cases Γ = Γ 0 + ​ ( N ) \Gamma=\Gamma_{0}^{+}(N) generated by Γ 0 ​ ( N ) \Gamma_{0}(N) and the Atkin-Lehner involutions for N = 1 , 2 , 3 N=1,2,3 ( Γ 0 + ​ ( 1 ) = SL ⁡ ( 2 , ℤ ) \Gamma_{0}^{+}(1)=\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z}) ). Firstly, we note that a quasimodular form of depth 1 1 , after divided by some modular form with the same weight, is a solution of a modular differential equation. Our main results are the converse of the above statement for the groups Γ 0 + ​ ( N ) \Gamma_{0}^{+}(N) , N = 1 , 2 , 3 N=1,2,3 .

Introduction

Let
\Gamma
be a discrete subgroup of
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})
commensurable with

\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
. The aim of our study is to explore a two-way connection
between quasimodular forms with depth
\ell
on
\Gamma
and modular
differential equations of order
\ell+1
. In this paper, we shall
consider the case
\ell=1
.

The notion of quasimodular forms was first introduced by Kaneko and
Zagier
[
9
]
. For example, if
\Gamma=\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
, a
function
f(z)
on the upper half-plane
\mathbb{H}
is called a quasimodular
form of weight
k
and depth
\leq 1
if

(1.1)

f(z)=f_{1}(z)E_{2}(z)+f_{0}(z),\qquad z\in\mathbb{H},

for some modular forms
f_{0}(z)
and
f_{1}(z)
of weight
k
and
k-2
,
respectively, on
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
, where
E_{2}(z)
is the classical
Eisenstein series of weight
2
on
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
with
E_{2}(\infty)=1
.

For
f(z)
of (
1.1
),
Pellarin
[
10
]
introduced the Wronskian

(1.2)

W_{f}(z)=\det\begin{pmatrix}zf(z)+\frac{6}{\pi i}f_{1}(z)&f(z)\\
\left(zf(z)+\frac{6}{\pi i}f_{1}(z)\right)^{(1)}&f^{(1)}(z)\end{pmatrix},

where
g^{(k)}
is the
k
-th derivative of a function
g
with
respect to
z
, and a straightforward computation finds that
W_{f}(z)

is a modular form of weight
2k
(see
[
10
]
or
Section
2
for more details). Let

g_{1}(z)=\frac{zf(z)+6f_{1}(z)/\pi i}{\sqrt{W_{f}(z)}}\quad\text{and}\quad g_{2}(z)=\frac{f(z)}{\sqrt{W_{f}(z)}}.

Then we have
\det\left(\begin{smallmatrix}g_{1}&g_{2}\\
g_{1}^{\prime}&g_{2}^{\prime}\end{smallmatrix}\right)=1
, and hence

\det\left(\begin{smallmatrix}g_{1}&g_{2}\\
g_{1}^{\prime\prime}&g_{2}^{\prime\prime}\end{smallmatrix}\right)=0
, by a further
differentiation. Thus, both
g_{1}
and
g_{2}
are solutions of

(1.3)

y^{\prime\prime}=-4\pi^{2}Q(z)y,\qquad z\in\mathbb{H},

where

(1.4)

Q(z)=-\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}\frac{g_{1}^{\prime\prime}}{g_{1}}=-\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}\frac{g_{2}^{\prime\prime}}{g_{2}}.

Although generally,
g_{i}
,
i=1,2
, are not single-valued functions,
it is easy to see that
Q(z)
is a single-valued meromorphic function
on
\mathbb{H}
.

Equation (
1.3
) is a second-order ODE in a
complex variable. Such a differential equation is called
Fuchsian if the order of pole of
Q(z)
is
\leq 2
. Let
z_{0}

be a singular point and assume that

Q(z)=A(z-z_{0})^{-2}+O\left((z-z_{0})^{-1}\right)
. Then the
indicial equation at
z_{0}
is

\rho(\rho-1)=-4\pi^{2}A,

and its roots are
-\alpha
and
\alpha+1
for some

\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
. These two roots are called the
local exponents

of (
1.3
) (or of
Q(z)
). If

\alpha\in\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{Z}
, then equation (
1.3
) might
have a solution with a logarithmic singularity near
z_{0}
. The
(regular) singular point
z_{0}
is called
apparent
if (a)

\alpha\in\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{Z}
and (b) any solution of (
1.3
) has no logarithmic singularity at
z_{0}
. If (a) holds, but (b)
does not, we will simply say
z_{0}
is
not apparent
. In this
paper, we always assume that the local exponents of any singularity

z_{0}\in\mathbb{H}
are integers or half-integers, which will be denoted by

1/2\pm\kappa_{z_{0}}
,
\kappa_{z_{0}}\in\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
. It is easy
to see that (i) if
\kappa_{z_{0}}=0
, then
z_{0}
is always not
apparent, and (ii) if
\kappa_{z_{0}}=1/2
, then
z_{0}
is apparent if
and only if
Q(z)
is holomorphic at
z_{0}
.

.

Let

be defined (
1.4

is a

. Moreover,

.

(ii)

Any pole of

is an apparent

1.3

(iii)

The cusp

(iv)

Let

(
1.3
) at

This theorem is a special case of Theorem

and Proposition

(

H
Γ

).

(iii)

Let

In Section
3
, we will briefly explain the
basic notions related to ODE in a complex variable. (We refer to

(

H
Γ

of

2.
Quasimodular forms

For convenience of non-specialists, we shall give a quick introduction
of quasimodular forms in this section. See
[
2
,
18
]

for more detailed overview and discussion. Note that most of the
definitions and properties presented here are valid or have analogues
in the setting of general Fuchsian subgroups of the first kind of

\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})
, but for simplicity, here we restrict our attention to
subgroups
\Gamma
of
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})
commensurable with
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
,
which we assume throughout the section.

2.1.
Basic definitions and properties

The notion of quasimodular forms was first introduced by Kaneko and
Zagier
[
9
]
. It was defined through the notion of
nearly holomorphic modular forms (called almost holomorphic modular
forms in
[
9
]
).

Definition 2.1
.

A function
f:\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{C}
is said to be
nearly holomorphic
if it
is of the form

(2.1)

f(z)=\sum_{r=0}^{n}\frac{f_{r}(z)}{(z-\overline{z})^{r}}

for some holomorphic functions
f_{r}
.

Let
\Gamma
be a congruence subgroup of
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})
. A function

f:\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{C}
is said to be a
nearly holomorphic modular form

on
\Gamma
if it is nearly holomorphic, satisfies

(2.2)

f\left(\frac{az+b}{cz+d}\right)=(cz+d)^{k}f(z)

for all
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\Gamma
, and has a finite limit at each cusp of

\Gamma
. The largest integer
r
such that
f_{r}\not\equiv 0
in
(
2.1
) is called the
depth
of

f
. We let
\mathscr{M}_{k}^{\ast}(\Gamma)
denote the space of nearly
holomorphic modular forms of weight
k
on
\Gamma
.

On the spaces of nearly holomorphic modular forms, we have the
so-called Shimura-Maass operator.

Definition 2.2
.

For a nearly holomorphic function
f:\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{C}
and
an integer
k
, define the
Shimura-Maass operator

\partial_{k}

of weight

k
by

(\partial_{k}f)(z)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\left(f^{\prime}(z)+\frac{kf(z)}{z-\overline{z}}\right).

Also, for
\gamma=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\mathrm{GL}^{+}(2,\mathbb{R})
, the slash
operator of weight
k
is defined by

(f\big|_{k}\gamma)(z)=\frac{(\det\gamma)^{k/2}}{(cz+d)^{k}}f\left(\frac{az+b}{cz+d}\right).

Then we have the following properties.

Lemma 2.3

(
[
15
, Equations (1.5) and (1.8)]
)
.

For any nearly holomorphic functions
f,g:\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{C}
, any integers
k

and
\ell
, and any
\gamma\in\mathrm{GL}^{+}(2,\mathbb{R})
, we have

\partial_{k+\ell}(fg)=(\partial_{k}f)g+f(\partial_{\ell}g)

and

\partial_{k}\left(f\big|_{k}\gamma\right)=(\partial_{k}f)\big|_{k+2}\gamma.

In particular, the second property in the lemma says that if
f
is
a nearly holomorphic modular form of weight
k
on
\Gamma
, then

\partial_{k}f
is a nearly holomorphic form of weight

k+2
on
\Gamma
. That is,
\partial_{k}
is a linear transformation
from
\mathscr{M}^{\ast}_{k}(\Gamma)
to
\mathscr{M}^{\ast}_{k+2}(\Gamma)
.

Definition 2.4
.

We say a holomorphic function
f:\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{C}
is a

quasimodular form
of weight
k
and depth
r
on
\Gamma
if
it is the holomorphic part of a nearly holomorphic modular form of
weight
k
and depth
r
on
\Gamma
. We let

\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}_{k}^{\leq r}(\Gamma)
denote the space of quasimodular forms of
weight
k
and depth
\leq r
on
\Gamma
. We also let

\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}(\Gamma)=\bigoplus_{k}\bigcup_{r}\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}_{k}^{\leq r}(\Gamma)

denote the graded ring of quasimodular forms of all
weights and all depths on
\Gamma
.

Using the modular property (
2.2
), we can
show that if
f_{0}\in\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}_{k}(\Gamma)
is the holomorphic part of the
nearly holomorphic modular form

f(z)=\sum_{r=0}^{n}\frac{f_{r}(z)}{(z-\overline{z})^{r}},

then

(2.3)

\left(f_{0}\big|_{k}\gamma\right)(z)=\sum_{r=0}^{\infty}f_{r}(z)\left(\frac{c}{cz+d}\right)^{r}

for
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\Gamma
and vice versa.

The archetypal example of a quasimodular form is
E_{2}(z)
, which is
the holomorphic part of the nearly holomorphic modular form

E_{2}(z)+\frac{6}{\pi i(z-\overline{z})}=E_{2}(z)-\frac{3}{\pi\,\mathrm{Im\,}z}

of weight
2
and depth
1
on
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
. It can be shown that the
graded ring
\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}(\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}))
of quasimodular forms on

\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
is generated by
E_{2}(z)
,
E_{4}(z)
, and
E_{6}(z)
.
Also, by Ramanujan’s identities

D_{q}E_{2}=\frac{E_{2}^{2}-E_{4}}{12},\qquad D_{q}E_{4}=\frac{E_{2}E_{4}-E_{6}}{3},\qquad D_{q}E_{6}=\frac{E_{2}E_{6}-E_{4}^{2}}{2},

the ring
\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}(\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z}))
is closed under the differential
operator

D_{q}:=q\frac{d}{dq}=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\frac{d}{dz}.

More generally, we have the following description of
\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}_{k}^{\leq r}(\Gamma)
.

Proposition 2.5

(
[
18
, Proposition 20]
)
.

Let
\Gamma
be a subgroup of
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})
commensurable with
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
.

(i)

The graded ring
\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}(\Gamma)
of quasimodular forms on

\Gamma
is closed under differentiation. More precisely, we
have
D_{q}(\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}_{k}^{\leq r}(\Gamma))\subseteq\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}_{k+2}^{\leq r+1}(\Gamma)
for all
k,r\geq 0
.

(ii)

Let
\phi
be a quasimodular form of weight
2
and
depth
1
on
\Gamma
. Then

\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}_{k}^{\leq r}(\Gamma)=\bigoplus_{j=0}^{r}\phi^{j}\mathscr{M}_{k-2j}(\Gamma)

for all
k,r\geq 0
. In particular, we have

\dim\mathscr{M}_{k}^{\leq r}(\Gamma)=\sum_{j=0}^{r}\dim\mathscr{M}_{k-2j}(\Gamma).

The quasimodular form
\phi
in the proposition can be constructed by
taking the logarithmic derivative of a meromorphic modular form of
nonzero weight that is nonvanishing on
\mathbb{H}
. In general, the choice of

\phi
is not unique. For example, when
\Gamma=\Gamma_{0}(p)
, we can
take
\phi
to be the logarithmic derivative of any

\Delta(z)^{m}\Delta(pz)^{n}
, as long as
m+n\neq 0
.

More generally, we can define quasimodular forms on
\Gamma
with
character
\chi
to be the holomorphic parts of nearly holomorphic
function
f:\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{C}
satisfying

f(\gamma z)=\chi(\gamma)(cz+d)^{k}f(z),\qquad\gamma=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\in\Gamma,

and analytic conditions at cusps. (Thus, if
-I\in\Gamma
and

\chi(-I)=-1
, we will assume that
k
is odd.) We similarly let

\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}_{k}^{\leq r}(\Gamma,\chi)
denote the space of all quasimodular
forms on
\Gamma
of weight
k
with character
\chi
. In this paper,
we will only consider characters of finite order.

The first examples that provides a link between quasimodular forms and
modular differential equations were due to Kaneko and Koike

[
7
,
8
]
. Among other things, they proved
that if
6|k
, then there is a solution
f(z)
of the differential
equation

(2.4)

D_{q}^{2}y(z)-\frac{k}{6}E_{2}(z)D_{q}y(z)+\frac{k(k-1)}{12}(D_{q}E_{2}(z))y(z)=0,

such that
f
is an extremal quasimodular form in
\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}^{\leq 1}_{k}(\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z}))
, defined as an element whose vanishing order at

\infty
is
\dim\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}_{k}^{\leq 1}(\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z}))-1
.
Then by a simple transformation of the differential equation, we see
that
y(z)=f(z)/\Delta(z)^{k/12}
is
a solution of the differential equation

(2.5)

D_{q}^{2}y(z)=\left(\frac{k}{12}\right)^{2}E_{4}(z)y(z).

Analogues of (
2.4
) for the groups

\Gamma_{0}^{+}(N)
,
N=2,3
, and
\Gamma_{0}(N)
,
N=2,3,4
, were obtained
in
[
11
]
and
[
12
]
, respectively.
In Section
3
we will give a new proof of
these results, stated in the same form as (
2.5
).

2.2.
Atkin-Lehner decomposition of
\widetilde{M}_{k}^{\leq r}(\Gamma_{0}(N))

Again, for convenience of non-specialists, here we shall give a quick overview of the Atkin-Lehner decomposition of spaces of modular forms.

Let
N
be a positive integer. It is easy to see that if
\gamma\in\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})
normalizes
\Gamma_{0}(N)
, then
f\mapsto f|_{k}\gamma

defines an automorphism of
\mathscr{M}_{k}(\Gamma_{0}(N))
and
\mathscr{M}_{k}^{\ast}(\Gamma_{0}(N))
, where
f|_{k}\gamma
is the usual slash operator of weight
k
acting on
f
. Let
\mathrm{N}(\Gamma_{0}(N))
denote the normalizer of
\Gamma_{0}(N)
in
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})
. Inside
\mathrm{N}(\Gamma_{0}(N))/\Gamma_{0}(N)
, there is a special subgroup, called the Atkin-Lehner subgroup, whose definition is given as follows.

Let
e
be a positive divisor of
N
such that
(e,N/e)=1
. Such a divisor is often called a Hall divisor of
N
. We check that a matrix of the form

\frac{1}{\sqrt{e}}\begin{pmatrix}ae&b\\
cN&de\end{pmatrix},\qquad ade^{2}+bcN=e

normalizes
\Gamma_{0}(N)
. Let
W_{e}
denote the set of such
matrices. (In particular,
W_{1}=\Gamma_{0}(N)
.) It is easy to see that

W_{e}=w_{e}\Gamma_{0}(N)
for any element
w_{e}
of
W_{e}
. Also, if
e
and

e^{\prime}
are two Hall divisors of
N
,
\gamma\in W_{e}
, and
\gamma^{\prime}\in W_{e^{\prime}}
, then
\gamma\gamma^{\prime}\in W_{e^{\prime\prime}}
, where

e^{\prime\prime}=ee^{\prime}/(e,e^{\prime})^{2}
. Hence, if we let

\Gamma_{0}^{+}(N)=\bigcup_{e|N,(e,N/e)=1}W_{e},

then
\Gamma_{0}^{+}(N)
is a subgroup of
\mathrm{N}(\Gamma_{0}(N))
and
the quotient group
\Gamma_{0}^{+}(N)/\Gamma_{0}(N)
is an elementary
abelian
2
-group of order
2^{m}
, where
m
is the number of distinct
prime divisors of
N
. This quotient group
\Gamma_{0}^{+}(N)/\Gamma_{0}(N)

is called the
Atkin-Lehner subgroup
of

\mathrm{N}(\Gamma_{0}(N))/\Gamma_{0}(N)
.

Elements of the Atkin-Lehner groups acts on many things related to
\Gamma_{0}(N)
. For example, they give rise to automorphisms of the modular curve
X_{0}(N)
. They also act on
\mathscr{M}_{k}(\Gamma_{0}(N))
and
\mathscr{M}_{k}^{\ast}(\Gamma_{0}(N))
, as mentioned at the beginning of the section. In all case, they are called
Atkin-Lehner involutions
and denoted by
w_{e}
. Since every
w_{e}
has order
1
or
2
in
\mathrm{N}(\Gamma_{0}(N))/\Gamma_{0}(N)
and they commute with each other, the space
\mathscr{M}_{k}(\Gamma_{0}(N))
(respectively,
\mathscr{M}^{\ast}(\Gamma_{0}(N))
) decomposes into a direct sum of
2^{m}
subspaces that are simultaneous eigenspaces with eigenvalues
\pm 1
for all Atkin-Lehner involutions. For convenience, we introduce the following notation.

Definition 2.6
.

Let
G_{N}
denote the group of characters of

\Gamma_{0}^{+}(N)/\Gamma_{0}(N)
, that is, let
G_{N}
be the group of all
homomorphisms from
\Gamma_{0}^{+}(N)/\Gamma_{0}(N)
to
\{\pm 1\}
. We
shall let
\epsilon_{0}
denote the trivial character of

\Gamma_{0}^{+}(N)/\Gamma_{0}(N)
.

For
\epsilon\in G_{N}
, we let

\mathscr{M}_{k}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(N),\epsilon)=\left\{f\in\mathscr{M}_{k}(\Gamma_{0}(N)):f|_{k}w_{e}=\epsilon(W_{e})f\text{ for all }e\right\}.

The notation
\mathscr{M}_{k}^{\ast}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(N),\epsilon)
is similarly
defined. (The choice of the notation
\mathscr{M}_{k}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(N),\epsilon)

instead of
\mathscr{M}_{k}(\Gamma_{0}(N),\epsilon)
is to stress that we are
considering elements of
\mathscr{M}_{k}(\Gamma_{0}(N),\epsilon)
as modular forms
on
\Gamma_{0}^{+}(N)
with characters.)

With this notation, the Atkin-Lehner decomposition we discussed above can be written as

\mathscr{M}_{k}(\Gamma_{0}(N))=\bigoplus_{\epsilon\in G_{N}}\mathscr{M}_{k}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(N),\epsilon).

We remark that if
\Gamma
is a subgroup of
\Gamma_{0}^{+}(N)
containing
\Gamma_{0}(N)
, then

\mathscr{M}_{k}(\Gamma)=\bigoplus_{\epsilon\in G_{N},\Gamma/\Gamma_{0}(N)\in\ker\epsilon}\mathscr{M}_{k}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(N),\epsilon)

Thus, the dimension of each
\mathscr{M}_{k}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(N),\epsilon)
can be
derived from the following general formula and the inclusion-exclusion
principle.

Proposition 2.7

(
[
16
, Theorem 2.23]
)
.

Let
\Gamma
be a subgroup of
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})
commensurable with

\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
. Suppose that the modular curve
X(\Gamma)
has genus

g
,
c
cusps, and
m
elliptic points of order
e_{1},\ldots,e_{m}
,
respectively. Then for a nonnegative even integer
k
, we have

\dim\mathscr{M}_{k}(\Gamma)=(k-1)(g-1)+\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left\lfloor\frac{k}{2}\left(1-\frac{1}{e_{j}}\right)\right\rfloor+\frac{ck}{2}.

Example 2.8
.

Consider the case of
\Gamma_{0}(2)
. Since there is only one prime
divisor here, we shall use the following more intuitive notations
instead.

Let
\mathscr{M}_{k}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(2),\pm)
denote the Atkin-Lehner eigenspaces
with eigenvalue
\pm 1
, respectively. We observe that

\mathscr{M}_{k}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(2),+)
is simply
\mathscr{M}_{k}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(2))
. To
determine its dimension, we need to know how many cusps and elliptic
points
X_{0}(2)/w_{2}
has.

The modular curve
X_{0}(2)
has genus
0
,
2
cusps, and an elliptic
point
(1+i)/2
of order
2
. Thus,

\dim\mathscr{M}_{k}(\Gamma_{0}(2))=1+\left\lfloor\frac{k}{4}\right\rfloor.

To determine how many cusps and elliptic points
X_{0}(2)/w_{2}
has, we
note that
X_{0}(2)\to X_{0}(2)/w_{2}
is a covering of degree
2
. Thus, by
the Riemann-Hurwitz formula, the covering ramifies at
2
points and
these two points are fixed points of
w_{2}
. Now the matrix

\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\begin{smallmatrix}2&-1\\
2&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)
fixes
(1+i)/2
, i.e., the Atkin-Lehner

w_{2}
fixes
(1+i)/2
. Thus,
(1+i)/2
becomes an elliptic point of
order
4
on
X_{0}(2)/w_{2}
. Another fixed point of
w_{2}
is
i/\sqrt{2}
,
fixed by
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&-1\\
2&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)
. Since we have found two ramified
points, all other points are unramified. We conclude that
X_{0}(2)/w_{2}

has one cusp, one elliptic point of order
2
, and one elliptic point
of order
4
. Hence,

\dim\mathscr{M}_{k}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(2),+)=\dim\mathscr{M}_{k}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(2))=1-\frac{k}{2}+\left\lfloor\frac{k}{4}\right\rfloor+\left\lfloor\frac{3k}{8}\right\rfloor.

It follows that

\dim\mathscr{M}_{k}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(2),-)=\dim\mathscr{M}_{k}(\Gamma_{0}(2))-\dim\mathscr{M}_{k}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(2),+)=\frac{k}{2}-\left\lfloor\frac{3k}{8}\right\rfloor.

Now we consider the Atkin-Lehner decomposition of the spaces of nearly holomorphic modular forms and quasimodular forms. Just like
\mathscr{M}_{k}(\Gamma_{0}(N))
, we can decompose
\mathscr{M}_{k}^{\ast}(\Gamma_{0}(N))
into a direct sum

\mathscr{M}_{k}^{\ast}(\Gamma_{0}(N))=\bigoplus_{\epsilon\in G_{N}}\mathscr{M}_{k}^{\ast}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(N),\epsilon).

Definition 2.9
.

For
\epsilon\in G_{N}
, we let

\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}_{k}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(N),\epsilon)
denote the space of quasimodular
forms that are holomorphic parts of functions in

\mathscr{M}_{k}^{\ast}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(N),\epsilon)
.
Also, for a nonnegative integer
r
, we let
\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}_{k}^{\leq r}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(N),\epsilon)
denote the subspace of

\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}_{k}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(N),\epsilon)
consisting of quasimodular forms of
depth
\leq r
. When
\epsilon=\epsilon_{0}
is the trivial character,
we will often use the notation
\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}_{k}^{\leq r}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(N))
in place of
\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}_{k}^{\leq r}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(N),\epsilon_{0})
.

In addition, when
N=p^{n}
is a prime power, we will also use the
more intuitive notations
\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}_{k}^{\leq r}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(N),+)

(or simplfy
\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}_{k}^{\leq r}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(N))
) and

\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}_{k}^{\leq r}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(N),-)
to denote the spaces

\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}_{k}^{\leq r}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(N),\epsilon_{0})
and
\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}_{k}^{\leq r}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(N),\epsilon)
, where
\epsilon
is the nontrivial
element in
G_{N}
.

Remark 2.10
.

Let
N
be a positive integer. Let

\phi(z)=\sum_{e|N,(e,N/e)=1}eE_{2}(ez),

which is a scalar multiple of the logarithmic derivative of

\prod_{e}\Delta(ez)
and hence a quasimodular form of weight
2
and
depth
1
on
\Gamma_{0}^{+}(N)
.
Then for
\epsilon\in G_{N}
, it is easy to see that

\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}_{k}^{\leq r}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(N),\epsilon)=\bigoplus_{j=0}^{r}\phi^{j}\mathscr{M}_{k-2j}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(N),\epsilon).

and hence

\dim\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}_{k}^{\leq r}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(N),\epsilon)=\sum_{j=0}^{r}\dim\mathscr{M}_{k-2j}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(N),\epsilon).

Note that the choice of
\phi
is not unique
in general. For example, when
N=4
, we can choose

\phi(z)=E_{2}(2z)
. Then the statement
\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}_{k}^{\leq r}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(4),\epsilon)=\oplus_{j=0}^{r}\phi^{j}\mathscr{M}_{k-2j}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(4),\epsilon)

still holds.

We will need the following property in the subsequent discussion.

Lemma 2.11
.

Let
\epsilon\in G_{N}
. We have

D_{q}\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}_{k}^{\leq r}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(N),\epsilon)\subseteq\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}_{k+2}^{\leq r+1}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(N),\epsilon).

Proof.

This follows from Lemma
2.3
.
∎

2.3.
Extremal quasimodular forms

Several examples given in the paper are involved with the notion of
extremal quasimodular forms, introduced first in
[
8
]
,
so here we shall review their definition and discuss the existence and
uniqueness of such quasimodular forms.

Definition 2.12
.

Given
\epsilon\in G_{N}
, let
\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}=\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}_{k}^{\leq r}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(N),\epsilon)
be the Atkin-Lehner subspace of

\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}_{k}^{\leq r}(\Gamma_{0}(N))
corresponding to
\epsilon
. An
element
f\in\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}
is said to be

extremal
if its vanishing order at
\infty

is equal to
\dim\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}-1
. We say

f
is
normalized
if its leading Fourier coefficient is
1
.

Remark 2.13
.

Note that in
[
10
]
, Pellarin called a nonzero quasimodular form

f

analytically extremal
if its vanishing order at
\infty
is
the largest among all nonzero elements of the space
f
belongs
to. Pellarin’s definition of extremality seems to be closer to the
literal meaning of the word extremal. However, we will stick to the
definition introduced by Kaneko and Koike. It is clear that if
f
is
analytic extremal in a space
\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}
of quasimodular forms and its
vanishing order at
\infty
is
\dim\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}-1
, then it is extremal in
the sense of Kaneko and Koike.

In general, it is a complicated problem to show the existence and the
uniqueness of extremal quasimodular forms. In
[
10
]
,
Pellarin proved that if
r\leq 4
, then a normalized extremal
quasimodular form in
\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}_{k}^{\leq r}(\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z}))
exists and is
unique. His argument also works in some other cases. For the
convenience of the reader, we reproduce the proof, adapted to the
current setting.

Lemma 2.14
.

Let
\Gamma
be a subgroup of
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})
commensurable
with
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
and
\chi
be a character of
\Gamma
. Let
\phi

be a quasimodular form of weight
2
and depth
1
on
\Gamma
,
i.e.,

(cz+d)^{-2}\phi(\gamma z)=\phi(z)+\frac{\alpha c}{cz+d}

for all
\gamma=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\Gamma
for some nonzero complex number

\alpha
. For

f(z)=f_{0}(z)+\phi(z)f_{1}(z)\in\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}_{k}^{\leq 1}(\Gamma,\chi)
, where

k
is a positive integer and
f_{j}\in\mathscr{M}_{k-2j}(\Gamma,\chi)
,

j=1,2
, let

F_{f}(z)=\begin{pmatrix}zf(z)+\alpha f_{1}(z)\\
f(z)\end{pmatrix}

and define the Wronskian
W_{f}(z)
by

W_{f}(z)=\det(F_{f}(z),F_{f}^{\prime}(z))
. Then
W_{f}(z)
is a
modular form of weight
2k
with character
\chi^{2}
on
\Gamma
.
Moreover, we have

(2.6)

v_{\infty}(W_{f})=\begin{cases}2v_{\infty}(f),&\text{if }v_{\infty}(f)\leq v_{\infty}(f_{1}),\\
v_{\infty}(f)+v_{\infty}(f_{1}),&\text{if }v_{\infty}(f)>v_{\infty}(f_{1}),\end{cases}

where for a modular form or a quasimodular form
g
we let

v_{\infty}(g)
denote the vanishing order of
g
at
\infty
.

Note that in the case
\Gamma=\mathrm{SL}2,\mathbb{Z})
, a proof of the fact that

W_{f}(z)
is a modular form of weight
2k
can be found in

[
3
]
. (This is also mentioned in
[
10
]
, although
no proof is given.) Since our setting involves quasimodular forms with
characters on general
\Gamma
, we shall present a proof here.

Proof of Lemma
2.14
.

First of all, by the definition of
W_{f}(z)
, we have

W_{f}(z)=-f(z)^{2}+\alpha(f_{1}(z)f^{\prime}(z)-f_{1}^{\prime}(z)f(z)),

from which (
2.6
) immediately
follows. Also, by (an extension of) Proposition
2.5
(i), the function
W_{f}(z)
belongs to

\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}_{2k}^{\leq 2}(\Gamma,\chi^{2})
.
On the other hand, for
\gamma=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\Gamma
, we compute that

f(\gamma z)=\chi(\gamma)(cz+d)^{k}\left(f(z)+\frac{\alpha c}{cz+d}f_{1}(z)\right)

and

\begin{split}(\gamma z)f(\gamma z)+\alpha f_{1}(\gamma z)&=\chi(\gamma)\frac{az+b}{cz+d}(cz+d)^{k}\left(f(z)+\frac{\alpha c}{cz+d}f_{1}(z)\right)\\
&\qquad+\chi(\gamma)\alpha(cz+d)^{k-2}f_{1}(z).\end{split}

Using the relation
ad-bc=1
, we may simplify the last expression to

\chi(\gamma)(cz+d)^{k-1}\left((az+b)f(z)+\alpha af_{1}(z)\right).

Hence,

F_{f}(\gamma z)=\chi(\gamma)(cz+d)^{k-1}\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}F_{f}(z)

and

F_{f}^{\prime}(\gamma z)=\chi(\gamma)\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\left((k-1)(cz+d)^{k}F_{f}(z)+(cz+d)^{k+1}F_{f}^{\prime}(z)\right).

From the computation, we see that the Wronskian
W_{f}(z)
satisfies

W_{f}(\gamma z)=\chi(\gamma)^{2}(cz+d)^{2k}W_{f}(z).

That is,
W_{f}(z)
is a quasimodular form of weight
2k
and depth

\leq 2
with character
\chi^{2}
on
\Gamma
that is actually
modular. This proves that
W_{f}(z)\in\mathscr{M}_{2k}(\Gamma,\chi^{2})
.
∎

We now prove an extension of
[
10
, Theorem 1.3]
.

Proposition 2.15
.

Suppose that
\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}_{k}^{\leq 1}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(N),\epsilon)
is an Atkin-Lehner subspace of

\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}_{k}^{\leq 1}(\Gamma_{0}(N))
such that

(2.7)

\dim\mathscr{M}_{2k}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(N))=\dim\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}_{k}^{\leq 1}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(N),\epsilon)

and the maximal vanishing order at
\infty
of a nonzero element of

\mathscr{M}_{2k}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(N))
is equal to

\dim\mathscr{M}_{2k}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(N))-1
.
Then

(i)

extremal quasimodular forms exist in
\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}_{k}^{\leq 1}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(N),\epsilon)
and are unique up to scalars, and

(ii)

if
f
is an extremal quasimodular form in

\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}_{k}^{\leq 1}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(N),\epsilon)
, then we have

v_{\infty}(f)=v_{\infty}(W_{f})
, where
W_{f}
is the Wronskian
associated to
f
defined in Lemma
2.14
.

Proof.

The proof follows that of
[
10
, Theorem 1.3]
.
Set
\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}=\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}_{k}^{\leq 1}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(N),\epsilon)
. Let

\phi(z)=\sum_{e|N,(e,N/e)=1}eE_{2}(ez),

which is a scalar multiple of the logarithmic derivative of

\prod_{e}\Delta(ez)
. It is a holomorphic quasimodular form of weight

2
and depth
1
on
\Gamma_{0}^{+}(N)
and satisfies

\left(\phi\big|_{2}\gamma\right)(z)=\phi(z)+\frac{\alpha c}{cz+d}

for all
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\Gamma_{0}^{+}(N)
for some nonzero complex number

\alpha
. Assume that
f
is an element of

\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}
. According to Remark

2.10
, we have
f=f_{0}+\phi f_{1}
for some
f_{j}\in\mathscr{M}_{k-2j}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(N),\epsilon)
,
j=1,2
.
Let
W_{f}(z)
be
Wronskian associated to
f
defined in Lemma
2.14
. By the lemma,
W_{f}(z)
is a modular form in

\mathscr{M}_{2k}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(N))
. (Note that
\epsilon^{2}=\epsilon_{0}
for
any
\epsilon\in G_{N}
.) Moreover, (
2.6
) implies that
W_{f}\neq 0
whenever
f\neq 0
. Another
consequence of (
2.6
) is that, by
the assumptions that (
2.7
) holds and that
the maximal vanishing order of any nonzero element of

\mathscr{M}_{2k}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(N))
is

\dim\mathscr{M}_{2k}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(N))-1
, we have

\begin{split}\dim\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}-1&=\dim\mathscr{M}_{2k}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(N))-1\geq v_{\infty}(W_{f})\geq v_{\infty}(f)\end{split}

for all nonzero elements
f
of
\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}
. This implies that the
vanishing order at
\infty
of an analytically extremal quasimodular
form in
\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}
is
\dim\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}-1
and hence extremal quasimodular
forms exists in
\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}=\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}_{k}^{\leq 1}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(N),\epsilon)
and
are unique up to scalars. The argument above also shows that if
f

is an extremal quasimodular form in

\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}_{k}^{\leq 1}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(N),\epsilon)
, then

v_{\infty}(f)=v_{\infty}(W_{f})
.
∎

Now we use this result to prove the existence and uniqueness of
extremal quasimodular forms in the case
N=2
or
N=3
. Note that
Sakai
[
11
]
already established the existence in the case of

\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}_{k}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(N),+)
,
N=2,3
.

Corollary 2.16
.

Let
N=2
or
N=3
. Then for all positive even
integers
k
, extremal quasimodular forms exist in

\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}_{k}^{\leq 1}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(N))
and

\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}_{k}^{\leq 1}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(N),-)
and are unique up to scalars.

Proof.

In Example
2.8
, we have seen
that

(2.8)

\begin{split}\dim\mathscr{M}_{k}(\Gamma_{0}(2))&=1+\left\lfloor\frac{k}{4}\right\rfloor,\\
\dim\mathscr{M}_{k}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(2))&=1-\frac{k}{2}+\left\lfloor\frac{k}{4}\right\rfloor+\left\lfloor\frac{3k}{8}\right\rfloor,\\
\dim\mathscr{M}_{k}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(2),-)&=\frac{k}{2}-\left\lfloor\frac{3k}{8}\right\rfloor.\end{split}

Hence, according to Remark
2.10
, we have

(2.9)

\dim\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}^{\leq 1}_{k}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(2))=\begin{cases}1+k/4,&\text{if }k\equiv 0\ \mathrm{mod}\ 8,\\
(k+2)/4,&\text{if }k\equiv 2,6\ \mathrm{mod}\ 8,\\
k/4,&\text{if }k\equiv 4\ \mathrm{mod}\ 8,\end{cases}

and

(2.10)

\dim\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}^{\leq 1}_{k}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(2),-)=\begin{cases}k/4,&\text{if }k\equiv 0\ \mathrm{mod}\ 8,\\
(k+2)/4,&\text{if }k\equiv 2,6\ \mathrm{mod}\ 8,\\
1+k/4,&\text{if }k\equiv 4\ \mathrm{mod}\ 8.\end{cases}

It follows that if

k\not\equiv 4\ \mathrm{mod}\ 8
, then

\dim\mathscr{M}_{2k}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(2))=\dim\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}_{k}^{\leq 1}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(2))

and if
k\not\equiv 0\ \mathrm{mod}\ 8
, then

\dim\mathscr{M}_{2k}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(2))=\dim\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}_{k}^{\leq 1}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(2),-).

The condition (
2.7
) holds for these
spaces.

For the other condition of Proposition
2.15
, we observe that the condition is equivalent to
that there is a basis
\{g_{j}\}
for
\mathscr{M}_{2k}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(2))
such
that
v_{\infty}(g_{j})=j-1
for each

j=1,\ldots,\dim\mathscr{M}_{2k}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(2))
. Indeed, we
find that
g_{j}(z)=M_{4}(z)^{k/2-2j}M_{8}(z)^{j}
for

j=0,\ldots,\left\lfloor k/4\right\rfloor
, where

M_{4}(z)=\frac{1}{5}(4E_{4}(2z)+E_{4}(z)),\qquad M_{8}(z)=\eta(z)^{8}\eta(2z)^{8},

form such a basis. Therefore, by Proposition
2.15
, extremal quasimodular forms exist and are unique up to
scalars in the spaces above. For the remaining two cases

\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}_{k}^{\leq 1}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(2))
for
k\equiv 4\ \mathrm{mod}\ 8
and

\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}_{k}^{\leq 1}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(2),-)
for

k\equiv 0\ \mathrm{mod}\ 8
, we observe that when
k\equiv 4\ \mathrm{mod}\ 8
, we have

\dim\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}_{k-4}^{\leq 1}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(2))=\dim\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}_{k}^{\leq 1}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(2)),

and the map

f(z)\longmapsto M_{4}(z)f(z)

defines an isomorphism between the two spaces. It is clear that a
quasimodular form
f(z)

is extremal in
\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}_{k-4}^{\leq 1}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(2))
if and only if

M_{4}(z)f(z)
is extremal in

\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}_{k}^{\leq 1}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(2))
. Since we have seen that extremal
quasimodular forms exist and are unique up to scalars in the former
space, the same thing holds for the latter space. The same argument
works for the last case
\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}_{k}^{\leq 1}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(2),-)
,
k\equiv 0\ \mathrm{mod}\ 8
. This proves the case
N=2
.

For the case
N=3
, we note that
\Gamma_{0}(3)
has two cusps and one
elliptic point of order
3
, while
\Gamma_{0}^{+}(3)
has one cusp, one
elliptic point of order
2
, represented by
i/\sqrt{3}
with
stabilizer subgroup generated by
\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&-1\\
3&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)
, and one
elliptic point of order
6
, represented by
(3+\sqrt{-3})/6
with
stabilizer subgroup generated by
\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\left(\begin{smallmatrix}3&-1\\
3&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)
.
Thus, by Proposition
2.7
, we have

(2.11)

\begin{split}\dim\mathscr{M}_{k}(\Gamma_{0}(3))&=1+\left\lfloor\frac{k}{3}\right\rfloor,\\
\dim\mathscr{M}_{k}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(3))&=1-\frac{k}{2}+\left\lfloor\frac{k}{4}\right\rfloor+\left\lfloor\frac{5k}{12}\right\rfloor,\\
\dim\mathscr{M}_{k}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(3),-)&=\frac{k}{2}+\left\lfloor\frac{k}{3}\right\rfloor-\left\lfloor\frac{k}{4}\right\rfloor-\left\lfloor\frac{5k}{12}\right\rfloor.\end{split}

From these formulas, we deduce that

\dim\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}_{k}^{\leq 1}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(3))=\begin{cases}1+k/3,&\text{if }k\equiv 0\ \mathrm{mod}\ 12,\\
(k+1)/3,&\text{if }k\equiv 2,8\ \mathrm{mod}\ 12,\\
(k-1)/3,&\text{if }k\equiv 4\ \mathrm{mod}\ 12,\\
k/3,&\text{if }k\equiv 6\ \mathrm{mod}\ 12,\\
(k+2)/3,&\text{if }k\equiv 10\ \mathrm{mod}\ 12,\end{cases}

and

\dim\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}_{k}^{\leq 1}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(3),-)=\begin{cases}k/3,&\text{if }k\equiv 0\ \mathrm{mod}\ 12,\\
(k+1)/3,&\text{if }k\equiv 2,8\ \mathrm{mod}\ 12,\\
(k+2)/3,&\text{if }k\equiv 4\ \mathrm{mod}\ 12,\\
1+k/3,&\text{if }k\equiv 6\ \mathrm{mod}\ 12,\\
(k-1)/3,&\text{if }k\equiv 10\ \mathrm{mod}\ 12,\end{cases}

Therefore, the condition (
2.7
) in
Proposition
2.15

is met for
\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}_{k}^{\leq 1}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(3))
with

k\equiv 0,2,8,10\ \mathrm{mod}\ 12
and
\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}_{k}^{\leq 1}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(3),-)
with

k\equiv 2,4,6,8\ \mathrm{mod}\ 12
. For the other condition of the
proposition, we may use

\begin{split}M_{4}(z)&=\frac{1}{10}(9E_{4}(3z)+E_{4}(z)),\\
M_{8}(z)&=\frac{1}{2}(3E_{2}(3z)-E_{2}(z))\eta(z)^{6}\eta(3z)^{6},\\
M_{12}(z)&=\eta(z)^{12}\eta(3z)^{12}\end{split}

to construct a basis
\{g_{j}\}
for
\mathscr{M}_{2k}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(3))
with
the property
v_{\infty}(g_{j})=j-1
for all

j=1,\ldots,\dim\mathscr{M}_{2k}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(3))
. Hence, the second
condition of Proposition
2.15
also holds and
we conclude that extremal quasimodular forms exist in

\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}_{k}^{\leq 1}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(3))
for
k\equiv 0,2,8,10\ \mathrm{mod}\ 12
and

\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}_{k}^{\leq 1}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(3),-)
for
k\equiv 2,4,6,8\ \mathrm{mod}\ 12
and
are unique up to scalars.

Finally, similar to the case
N=2
, for
the remaining cases, we observe that

(2.12)

\begin{split}\dim\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}_{k-4}^{\leq 1}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(3))&=\dim\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}_{k}^{\leq 1}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(3)),\ \text{for }k\equiv 4\ \mathrm{mod}\ 12,\\
\dim\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}_{k-4}^{\leq 1}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(3),-)&=\dim\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}_{k}^{\leq 1}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(3),-),\ \text{for }k\equiv 10\ \mathrm{mod}\ 12,\end{split}

(2.13)

\begin{split}\dim\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}_{k-2}^{\leq 1}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(3),-)&=\dim\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}_{k}^{\leq 1}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(3)),\ \text{for }k\equiv 6\ \mathrm{mod}\ 12,\\
\dim\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}_{k-2}^{\leq 1}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(3))&=\dim\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}_{k}^{\leq 1}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(3),-),\ \text{for }k\equiv 0\ \mathrm{mod}\ 12,\end{split}

and that the map

f(z)\longmapsto(9E_{4}(3z)+E_{4}(z))f(z)

defines an isomorphism for the pairs of spaces in (
2.12
), while the map

f(z)\longmapsto(3E_{2}(3z)-E_{2}(z))f(z)

defines an isomorphism for the pairs of spaces in (
2.13
). From these observations we conclude that extremal
quasimodular forms exists and are unique up to scalars in all

\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}_{k}^{\leq 1}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(3))
and
\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}_{k}^{\leq 1}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(3),-)
.
∎

2.4.
Quasimodular forms of odd weights on
\Gamma_{1}(3)

In this paper, quasimodular forms of odd weights on

\Gamma_{1}(3):=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\in\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z}):c\equiv 0\ \mathrm{mod}\ 3,a,d\equiv 1\ \mathrm{mod}\ 3\right\}

will also appear as
solutions of modular differential equations when

\Gamma=\Gamma_{0}^{+}(3)
, so we will review
their properties in this section.

For a nonnegative integer
k
(even or odd), let

\mathscr{M}_{k}(\Gamma_{1}(3))
denote the set of
modular forms of weight
k
on
\Gamma_{1}(3)
.
Note that when
k
is odd, modular forms of weight
k
on

\Gamma_{1}(3)
can also be defined as modular forms on
\Gamma_{0}(3)

with nebentype character
\chi\left(\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{smallmatrix}\right)\right)=\left(\frac{-3}{d}\right)
.
By Theorem 2.25 of
[
16
]
, we have

(2.14)

\dim\mathscr{M}_{k}(\Gamma_{1}(3))=1+\left\lfloor\frac{k}{3}\right\rfloor.

Hence, we have

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\dim\mathscr{M}_{k}(\Gamma_{1}(3))x^{k}=\frac{1}{(1-x)(1-x^{3})},

and the graded ring
\bigoplus_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathscr{M}(\Gamma_{1}(3))

of modular forms is freely generated by

(2.15)

M_{1}(z)=\sum_{(m,n)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}q^{m^{2}+mn+n^{2}},\qquad q=e^{2\pi iz},

and

(2.16)

M_{3}(z)=\frac{9E_{4}(3z)-E_{4}(z)}{8M_{1}(z)}=\frac{\eta(z)^{9}}{\eta(3z)^{3}}-27\frac{\eta(3z)^{9}}{\eta(z)^{3}}.

Similar to the case of
\Gamma_{0}(N)
, the Atkin-Lehner involutions
normalize
\Gamma_{1}(N)
. Hence, we may decompose
\mathscr{M}_{k}(\Gamma_{1}(3))

into eigenspaces of the Atkin-Lehner involution
w_{3}
. Let

S=\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&-1\\
3&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)
. Because
S^{2}=-I
, the eigenvalues of

w_{3}
in the case of odd
k
can only be
\pm i
. For convenience, we
shall consider the eigenfunctions as modular forms
on
\Gamma_{0}^{+}(3)
with characters. More concretely, we recall that

\Gamma_{0}^{+}(3)
is generated by

S=\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\
3&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad R=TS=\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\begin{pmatrix}3&-1\\
3&0\end{pmatrix},

where
T=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&1\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)
. They satisfy
S^{2}=R^{6}=-I

and there is no other relation between
S
and
R
. In other words, a
presentation of the group
\Gamma_{0}^{+}(3)
is

\langle S,R:S^{4}=1,S^{2}=R^{6}\rangle
. We let
\chi
be the character of

\Gamma_{0}^{+}(3)
such that

(2.17)

\chi(S)=\chi(R)=-i,

and
\overline{\chi}
be
its complex conjugate. Then the eigenspace of
w_{3}
with eigenvalue

-i
(respectively,
i
) can be identified with

\mathscr{M}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(3),\chi)
(respectively,

\mathscr{M}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(3),\overline{\chi})
). Using the transformation formula
for the Dedekind eta function (see
[
17
, Pages 125–127]
), we
can show that

M_{3}(z)\in\mathscr{M}_{3}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(3),\chi)

and hence also

M_{1}(z)\in\mathscr{M}_{1}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(3),\chi),

where
M_{1}(z)
and
M_{3}(z)
are defined by (
2.15
) and (
2.16
), respectively.

Lemma 2.17
.

Let
k
be a positive odd integer. Then

\dim\mathscr{M}_{k}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(3),\chi)=\begin{cases}(k+5)/6,&\text{if }k\equiv 1\ \mathrm{mod}\ 12,\\
(k+3)/6,&\text{if }k\equiv 3,9\ \mathrm{mod}\ 12,\\
(k+1)/6,&\text{if }k\equiv 5,11\ \mathrm{mod}\ 12,\\
(k-1)/6,&\text{if }k\equiv 7\ \mathrm{mod}\ 12,\end{cases}

and

\dim\mathscr{M}_{k}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(3),\overline{\chi})=\begin{cases}(k-1)/6,&\text{if }k\equiv 1\ \mathrm{mod}\ 12,\\
(k+3)/6,&\text{if }k\equiv 3,9\ \mathrm{mod}\ 12,\\
(k+1)/6,&\text{if }k\equiv 5,11\ \mathrm{mod}\ 12,\\
(k+5)/6,&\text{if }k\equiv 7\ \mathrm{mod}\ 12.\end{cases}

Proof.

Observe that

\mathscr{M}_{k-1}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(3))\cup\mathscr{M}_{k-1}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(3),-)=\mathscr{M}_{k-1}(\Gamma_{0}(3))

and
\mathscr{M}_{k}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(3),\chi)\cup\mathscr{M}_{k}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(3),\overline{\chi})=\mathscr{M}_{k}(\Gamma_{1}(3))
. Also,

\begin{split}M_{1}\mathscr{M}_{k-1}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(3))&\subseteq\mathscr{M}_{k}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(3),\chi),\\
M_{1}\mathscr{M}_{k-1}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(3),-)&\subseteq\mathscr{M}_{k}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(3),\overline{\chi}).\end{split}

When
k
is not a multiple of
3
, by (
2.14
), we have

\dim\mathscr{M}_{k}(\Gamma_{1}(3))=\dim\mathscr{M}_{k-1}(\Gamma_{0}(3)).

Hence, both inclusions above are equality. When
3|k
, we use

\begin{split}M_{1}\mathscr{M}_{k}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(3),\chi)&\subseteq\mathscr{M}_{k+1}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(3),-),\\
M_{1}\mathscr{M}_{k}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(3),\overline{\chi})&\subseteq\mathscr{M}_{k+1}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(3))\end{split}

instead. Again, by the dimension formula (
2.14
), both inclusions are equality. This proves the lemma.
∎

Passing to quasimodular forms, we have the following dimension
formulas.

Lemma 2.18
.

Let
k
be a positive odd integer. We have

\dim\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}_{k}^{\leq 1}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(3),\chi)=\begin{cases}(k+2)/3,&\text{if }k\equiv 1\ \mathrm{mod}\ 12,\\
1+k/3,&\text{if }k\equiv 3\ \mathrm{mod}\ 12,\\
(k+1)/3,&\text{if }k\equiv 5,11\ \mathrm{mod}\ 12,\\
(k-1)/3,&\text{if }k\equiv 7\ \mathrm{mod}\ 12,\\
k/3,&\text{if }k\equiv 9\ \mathrm{mod}\ 12,\end{cases}

and

\dim\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}_{k}^{\leq 1}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(3),\overline{\chi})=\begin{cases}(k-1)/3,&\text{if }k\equiv 1\ \mathrm{mod}\ 12,\\
k/3,&\text{if }k\equiv 3\ \mathrm{mod}\ 12,\\
(k+1)/3,&\text{if }k\equiv 5,11\ \mathrm{mod}\ 12,\\
(k+2)/3,&\text{if }k\equiv 7\ \mathrm{mod}\ 12,\\
1+k/3,&\text{if }k\equiv 9\ \mathrm{mod}\ 12.\end{cases}

Proposition 2.19
.

For each positive odd integer
k
, an extremal
quasimodular form exists in
\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}_{k}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(3),\chi)
or

\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}_{k}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(3),\overline{\chi})
and is unique up to scalars.

Proof.

For a quasimodular form
f
in
\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}_{k}^{\leq 1}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(3),\chi)

or
\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}_{k}^{\leq 1}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(3),\overline{\chi})
, let
W_{f}
be the
Wronskian associated
f
defined in Lemma
2.14
. By the lemma, we have

W_{f}\in\mathscr{M}_{2k}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(3),\chi^{2})=\mathscr{M}_{2k}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(3),-)
.
Since

\dim\mathscr{M}_{2k}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(3),-)=\begin{cases}(k+2)/3,&\text{if }k\equiv 1\ \mathrm{mod}\ 6,\\
1+k/3,&\text{if }k\equiv 3\ \mathrm{mod}\ 6,\\
(k+1)/3,&\text{if }k\equiv 5\ \mathrm{mod}\ 6,\end{cases}

we find that

\dim\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}_{k}^{\leq 1}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(3),\chi)=\dim\mathscr{M}_{2k}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(3),-)

for
k\equiv 1,3,5,11\ \mathrm{mod}\ 12
, and

\dim\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}_{k}^{\leq 1}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(3),\overline{\chi})=\dim\mathscr{M}_{2k}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(3),-)

for
k\equiv 5,7,9,11\ \mathrm{mod}\ 12
. Hence, extremal quasimodular forms
exist in these spaces and is unique up to scalars. For the remaining
four spaces, we use the facts that

M_{1}\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}_{k-1}^{\leq 1}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(3))=\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}_{k}^{\leq 1}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(3),\chi)

for
k\equiv 1,7,9,11\ \mathrm{mod}\ 12
, and

M_{1}\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}_{k-1}^{\leq 1}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(3),-)=\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}_{k}^{\leq 1}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(3),\overline{\chi})

for
k\equiv 1,3,5,7\ \mathrm{mod}\ 12
to obtain the same conclusion.
∎

3.
Modular ordinary differential equations

The purpose of this section is to prove that for any quasimodular form

f(z)
of depth
1
,
f(z)/\sqrt{W_{f}(z)}
is a solution of a modular
differential equation. Throughout
the section, we assume that
\Gamma
is a discrete subgroup of

\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})
that is commensurable with
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
, and
\phi
is the
quasimodular form of weight
2
and depth
1
appearing in Proposition

2.5
, i.e.,

(3.1)

(cz+d)^{-2}\phi(\gamma z)=\phi(z)+\frac{\alpha c}{cz+d},\qquad\gamma=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\in\Gamma,

for some complex number
\alpha\neq 0
. Suppose that
f(z)
is a
quasimodular form of weight
k
and depth
1
with character

\chi
of finite order on
\Gamma
. We have

(3.2)

f(z)=f_{1}(z)\phi(z)+f_{0}(z)

for some
f_{0}\in\mathscr{M}_{k}(\Gamma,\chi)
and

f_{1}\neq 0\in\mathscr{M}_{k-2}(\Gamma,\chi)
. Let
W_{f}(z)
be the Wronskian
associated to
f
defined in Lemma
2.14
. It is a modular form of weight
2k
with character

\chi^{2}
on
\Gamma
. Define

(3.3)

g_{1}(z)=\frac{zf(z)+\alpha f_{1}(z)}{\sqrt{W_{f}(z)}}\quad\text{and}\quad g_{2}(z)=\frac{f(z)}{\sqrt{W_{f}(z)}}.

Then we have
\det\left(\begin{smallmatrix}g_{1}&g_{2}\\
g_{1}^{\prime}&g_{2}^{\prime}\end{smallmatrix}\right)=1
and hence

\det\left(\begin{smallmatrix}g_{1}&g_{2}\\
g_{1}^{\prime\prime}&g_{2}^{\prime\prime}\end{smallmatrix}\right)=0
. Therefore, both
g_{1}
and
g_{2}

are solutions of

(3.4)

y^{\prime\prime}(z)=-4\pi^{2}Q(z)y(z),

where

(3.5)

Q(z)=-\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}\frac{g_{1}^{\prime\prime}(z)}{g_{1}(z)}=-\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}\frac{g_{2}^{\prime\prime}(z)}{g_{2}(z)}.

Clearly,
Q(z)
is a single-valued meromorphic function not
identically
0
on
\mathbb{H}
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
Q(z)
be defined by (
3.5
). Then
Q(z)
is a meromorphic function on
\mathbb{H}
and
(
3.4
) is Fuchsian. Moreover, if

z_{0}\in\mathbb{H}
is a pole of
Q(z)
, then
z_{0}
is an apparent
singularity for (
3.4
).

Proof.

Clearly,
g_{i}(z)=(z-z_{0})^{\alpha_{i}}(c_{i}+O(z-z_{0}))
near

z_{0}
for some
c_{i}\neq 0
and
\alpha_{i}\in\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{Z}
. Thus,

Q(z)=\alpha_{i}(\alpha_{i}-1)(z-z_{0})^{-2}+O\left((z-z_{0})^{-1}\right)

near
z_{0}
and we have
\alpha_{1}(\alpha_{1}-1)=\alpha_{2}(\alpha_{2}-1)
,
which implies that the local exponents at
z_{i}
are
\alpha_{i}
and

-\alpha_{i}+1
. (The two sets
\{\alpha_{1},-\alpha_{1}+1\}
and

\{\alpha_{2},-\alpha_{2}+1\}
are identical.) Hence (
3.4
) is Fuchsian and the difference of the local
exponents are
1+2\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
. Since
g_{1}
and
g_{2}
are linearly
independent and has no logarithmic singularities near
z_{0}
,
z_{0}

is obviously an apparent singularity.
∎

Remark 3.2
.

Suppose that (
3.4
) has a
solution
y(z)
with a logarithmic singularity near
z_{0}
. Then the
local exponents
\rho_{1}
and
\rho_{2}
at
z_{0}
satisfy

\rho_{1}-\rho_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}
, and
z_{0}
is not apparent. This remark is used
in the proof of Theorem
3.3
. See below.

The following result is a generalization of Theorem
1.1
.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
Q(z)
be defined by (
3.5
). Then the following hold.

(i)

The function
Q(z)
is a meromorphic modular form of
weight
4
(with trivial character) on
\Gamma
.

(ii)

Moreover,
Q(z)
is holomorphic at
\infty
with

Q(\infty)=\kappa_{\infty}^{2}/N^{2}
for some

\kappa_{\infty}\in\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, where
N
is the width of
the cusp
\infty
of
\Gamma
. Also, the cusp
\infty
is not
apparent for (
3.4
).

(iii)

For any cusp
s
,
Q(z)
is holomorphic at
s
with

Q(s)\geq 0
and
s
is not apparent for (
3.4
).

Proof.

To prove the modularity of
Q(z)
, we consider

y(z)=\left(g_{2}|_{-1}\gamma\right)(z):=(cz+d)g_{2}(\gamma z)
for

\gamma=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\Gamma
. By the
definition of
g_{2}
,
y(z)
is well-defined up to
\pm 1
.
A direct computation show that

\left(g_{2}\big|_{-1}\gamma\right)^{\prime\prime}(z)=\left(g_{2}^{\prime\prime}\big|_{3}\gamma\right)(z)

(which is a special case of Bol’s identity
[
1
]
),
which implies that

y^{\prime\prime}(z)=\left(Q\big|_{4}\gamma\right)(z)y(z)

On the other hand, we have

\begin{split}y(z)&=(cz+d)g_{2}(\gamma z)=\frac{(cz+d)f(\gamma z)}{\sqrt{W_{f}(\gamma z)}}\\
&=\pm\frac{\chi(\gamma)(cz+d)(f(z)+\alpha cf_{1}(z)/(cz+d))}{\sqrt{\chi(\gamma)^{2}W_{f}(z)}}\\
&=\pm(cg_{1}(z)+dg_{2}(z)).\end{split}

Thus,
y(z)
is also a solution of (
3.4
),
which implies that
\left(Q|_{4}\gamma\right)(z)=Q(z)
for all

\gamma\in\Gamma
. This proves (i).

Let
N
be the width of the cusp
\infty
and
q_{N}=e^{2\pi iz/N}
.
By (
2.6
), the order of
g_{2}
at
\infty
is

\kappa_{\infty}=\frac{1}{2}v_{\infty}(f)-\frac{1}{2}\min(v_{\infty}(f),v_{\infty}(f_{1})),

which is in
\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
. (Note that
when
\chi\left(\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&N\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)\right)\neq 1
,
v_{\infty}(f)
and

v_{\infty}(f_{1})
are not integers, but we still have

v_{\infty}(f)\equiv v_{\infty}(f_{1})\ \mathrm{mod}\ 1
nonetheless.) Since

(3.6)

Q(z)=-\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}\frac{g_{2}^{\prime\prime}(z)}{g_{2}(z)}=\frac{1}{N^{2}}\frac{\left(q_{N}\frac{d}{dq_{N}}\right)^{2}g_{2}}{g_{2}},

we find that
Q(\infty)=\kappa_{\infty}^{2}/N^{2}
.

Notice that
z
appears in the expression of
g_{1}(z)
and
g_{1}(z)

is another solution of (
3.4
). This
obviously implies that
\infty
is not apparent. We now prove
(iii).

Let
s\neq\infty
be another cusp of
\Gamma
and
\sigma=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{smallmatrix}\right)

be an element of
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
such that
\sigma\infty=s
. Regarding

f(z)
as a quasimodular form on
\Gamma^{\prime}=\ker\chi\cap\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
, we
have
f(z)=h_{1}(z)E_{2}(z)+h_{0}(z)
for some
h_{j}\in\mathscr{M}_{k-2j}(\Gamma^{\prime})
.
We have

\begin{split}\left(g_{2}|_{-1}\sigma\right)(z)&=\frac{(cz+d)f(\sigma z)}{\sqrt{W_{f}(\sigma z)}}=\frac{(cz+d)\left(f|_{k}\sigma\right)(z)}{\sqrt{\left(W_{f}|_{2k}\sigma\right)(z)}}\\
&=\frac{(cz+d)\left(\left(h_{1}|_{k-2}\sigma\right)E_{2}(z)+\left(h_{0}|_{k}\sigma\right)\right)+6c\left(h_{1}|_{k-2}\sigma\right)/\pi i}{\sqrt{\left(W_{f}|_{2k}\sigma\right)(z)}}.\end{split}

Since all terms in the above expression except for
cz+d
has a

q_{M}
-expansion, where
M
is the width of the cusp
s
, we see that

z
appears in the expression of
g_{2}|_{-1}\gamma
. (Note that

c\neq 0
since
s
is assumed to be a cusp different from

\infty
.) This implies that
s
is not apparent and the local
exponents of (
3.4
) at
s
are

\pm\kappa_{s}
for some
\kappa_{s}\in\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
(see Remark

3.2
). As discussed earlier, near the cusp
s
,
there is a solution
y_{+}(z)
of (
3.4
) of
the form

y_{+}(z)=q_{M}^{\kappa_{s}}\left(1+\sum_{j\geq 1}c_{j}q_{M}^{j}\right).

By the same computation as (
3.6
), we see
that
Q(s)=M^{-2}\kappa_{s}^{2}\geq 0
. This completes the proof of the
theorem.
∎

Remark 3.4
.

We remark that for a given point
z_{0}\in\mathbb{H}
, the local exponents of
(
3.4
) at
z_{0}
and those at
\gamma z_{0}

are the same for any
\gamma\in\Gamma
. Also, if
z_{0}
is
apparent, then any equivalent point
\gamma z_{0}
,

\gamma\in\Gamma
, is also apparent. See
[
4
]
for a
proof of this fact.

For the special case
\Gamma
is one of
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
,
\Gamma_{0}^{+}(2)
,
and
\Gamma_{0}^{+}(3)
, we have the following informations about local
exponents at elliptic points. We first prove a lemma.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
\phi(z)
be a quasimodular form of weight
2
and depth
1
on

\Gamma
. Then
\phi(z)
does not vanish at any elliptic point of

\Gamma
.

Proof.

Let
\widetilde{\phi}(z)
be the nearly holomorphic modular form
of weight
2
corresponding to
\phi(z)
. We have

\widetilde{\phi}(z)=\phi(z)+\frac{\alpha}{z-\overline{z}}

for some nonzero complex number
\alpha
. Since
\widetilde{\phi}(z)

transforms like a modular form of weight
2
on
\Gamma
, it
vanishes at all elliptic points of
\Gamma
. It follows that

\phi(z)
does not vanish at any elliptic point of
\Gamma
.
∎

Proposition 3.6
.

We have the following properties about local exponents of
(
3.4
) at an elliptic point.

(i)

Let
f
be a quasimodular form of depth
1
in

\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}_{k}^{\leq 1}(\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z}))
. Let
1/2\pm\kappa_{\rho}
and

1/2\pm\kappa_{i}
,

\kappa_{\rho},\kappa_{i}\in\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{N}
, be the local exponents of
(
3.4
) at the elliptic points

\rho=(1+\sqrt{-3})/2
and
i=\sqrt{-1}
. Then

(2\kappa_{\rho},3)=(2\kappa_{i},2)=1
.

(ii)

Let
f
be a quasimodular form of depth
1
in

\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}_{k}^{\leq 1}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(2),\pm)
. Let
1/2\pm\kappa_{\rho_{j}}

with
\kappa_{\rho_{j}}\in\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{N}
,
j=1,2
, be the local
exponents of (
3.4
) at the elliptic
points
\rho_{1}=i/\sqrt{2}
and
\rho_{2}=(1+i)/2
of

\Gamma_{0}^{+}(2)
. Then
(2\kappa_{\rho_{1}},2)=(2\kappa_{\rho_{2}},4)=1
.

(iii)

Let
f
be a quasimodular form of depth
1
in

\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}_{k}^{\leq 1}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(3),\chi^{m})
with

m\in\{0,1,2,3\}
and
k\equiv m\ \mathrm{mod}\ 2
that
is not a modular form, where
\chi
is the character of

\Gamma_{0}^{+}(3)
defined by (
2.17
). Let

1/2\pm\kappa_{\rho_{j}}
with

\kappa_{\rho_{j}}\in\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{N}
,
j=1,2
, be the local exponents of
(
3.4
) at the elliptic points

\rho_{1}=i/\sqrt{3}
and
\rho_{2}=(3+\sqrt{-3})/6
of

\Gamma_{0}^{+}(3)
. Then
(2\kappa_{\rho_{1}},2)=(2\kappa_{\rho_{2}},6)=1
.

Proof.

We first make a general remark that since the local
exponents of (
3.4
) at a point
z_{0}\in\mathbb{H}

are of the form
1/2\pm\kappa_{z_{0}}
for some

\kappa_{z_{0}}\in\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{N}
, if the order of
f(z)/\sqrt{W_{f}(z)}
at

z_{0}
is
-n_{z_{0}}
for some
n_{z_{0}}\in\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, then we
must have
1/2-\kappa_{z_{0}}=-n_{z_{0}}
, i.e.,

\kappa_{z_{0}}=1/2+n_{z_{0}}
.

Assume that
f(z)\in\widetilde{\mathscr{M}}_{k}^{\leq 1}(\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z}))
that is
not a modular form. Write it as
f(z)=f_{1}(z)E_{2}(z)+f_{0}(z)
with

f_{j}\in\mathscr{M}_{k-2j}(\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z}))
. We first
observe that if
f_{0}(z)
and
f_{1}(z)
have a common zero at
i

(respectively,
\rho
), then
f_{j}(z)/E_{6}(z)
(respectively,

f_{1}(z)/E_{4}(z)
),
j=1,2
, are holomorphic modular forms, and

f(z)/E_{6}(z)
(respectively,
f(z)/E_{4}(z)
) is a quasimodular
form. Also, it is easy to check that
W_{f}(z)=E_{6}(z)^{2}W_{f/E_{6}}(z)

(respectively,
W_{f}(z)=E_{4}(z)^{2}W_{f/E_{4}}(z)
), so the differential
equation (
3.4
) from
f(z)

and that from
f(z)/E_{6}(z)
(respectively,
f(z)/E_{4}(z)
) are the
same. Therefore, without loss of generality, we may assume that

f_{0}(z)
and
f_{1}(z)
have no common zero at
i
or
\rho
. We note
that this assumption forces that the weight
k
to be congruent to

0
or
2
modulo
6
since any modular form on
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
whose
weight is not divisible by
6
must have a zero at
\rho
.

By Lemma
3.5
,
E_{2}(z)
does not vanish at

\rho
and
i
. Then using

\left(f|_{k}\gamma\right)(z)=f(z)+\frac{12cf_{1}(z)}{2\pi i(cz+d)},\qquad\gamma=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\in\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})

and the assumption that
f_{0}(z)
and
f_{1}(z)
have no common zero at

i
and
\rho
, we see that there exists an element
\gamma
in

\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
such that
f(\gamma\rho),f(\gamma i)\neq 0
. Since the
local exponents of (
3.4
) at
\rho

(respectively,
i
) and at
\gamma\rho
(respectively,
\gamma i
)
are the same, by the remark we made at the beginning of the proof,
we have

2\kappa_{\rho}=1+\operatorname{ord}_{\gamma\rho}W_{f}(z)=1+\operatorname{ord}_{\rho}W_{f}(z)

(respectively,
2\kappa_{i}=1+\operatorname{ord}_{i}W_{f}(z)
).

Now consider first the case where the weight
k
is congruent to
0

modulo
6
. In this case, the weight of

is a multiple of

12
and hence

\frac{\operatorname{ord}_{\rho}W_{f}(z)}{3}+\frac{\operatorname{ord}_{i}W_{f}(z)}{2}

must be an integer. It follows that

and

2|\operatorname{ord}_{i}W_{f}
and therefore
(2\kappa_{\rho},3)=(2\kappa_{i},2)=1
.
Similarly, when the weight

is congruent to
2
modulo

, we
have

\frac{\operatorname{ord}_{\rho}W_{f}(z)}{3}+\frac{\operatorname{ord}_{i}W_{f}(z)}{2}\equiv\frac{1}{3}\ \mathrm{mod}\ 1.

Then

and
\operatorname{ord}_{\rho}W_{f}\equiv 1\ \mathrm{mod}\ 3
, from which we
conclude that
(2\kappa_{\rho},3)=(2\kappa_{i},2)=1
. This proves Part
(i).

The proof of Part (ii) is similar. We may assume without loss of
generality that
f(z)
does not vanish at
\gamma\rho_{1}
and

\gamma\rho_{2}
for some
\gamma\in\Gamma_{0}^{+}(2)
. Then

2\kappa_{\rho_{j}}=1+\operatorname{ord}_{\rho_{j}}W_{f}(z),\qquad j=1,2,

and it suffices to prove that
\operatorname{ord}_{\rho_{j}}W_{f}
is always even.
Indeed, if the weight
k
is a multiple of
4
, then the weight of

W_{f}\in\mathscr{M}_{2k}(\Gamma_{0}^{+}(2))
is a multiple of

. The orders of

If the weight
k
is congruent to
2
modulo
4
so that the weight
of
W_{f}
is congruent to
4
modulo
8
, then

must be
divisible by
M_{4}(z)=(4E_{4}(2z)+E_{2}(z))/5
, which has a zero of order

2
at
\rho_{2}
and is nonvashing at
\rho_{1}
. Since
W_{f}/M_{4}
is
again a modular form of a weight congruent to
0
modulo
8
, we
conclude that the order of
W_{f}
at

4\ \mathrm{mod}\ 6,&\text{if }k\equiv 2\ \mathrm{mod}\ 3,\end{cases}

\gamma z_{0}

\begin{pmatrix}y_{1}\big|_{-1}\gamma\\
y_{2}\big|_{-1}\gamma\end{pmatrix}=\pm\rho(\gamma)\begin{pmatrix}y_{1}\\

For our purpose, we would like to lift

\hat{\rho}:\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})\to\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{C})

Define

Lemma 4.1

If

For any function

It is easy to see that if

\infty

Lemma 4.2

We have
\det\hat{\rho}(\gamma)=1
for all
\gamma\in\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})

\hat{\rho}
is a homomorphism from
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
to

Proof.

Let

W(z)=\det\begin{pmatrix}y_{1}&y_{1}^{\prime}\\
y_{2}&y_{2}^{\prime}\end{pmatrix},\qquad\hat{W}(z)=\det\begin{pmatrix}\hat{y}_{1}&\hat{y}_{1}^{\prime}\\
\hat{y}_{2}&\hat{y}_{2}^{\prime}\end{pmatrix}.

On the one hand, (

\hat{W}(z)\big|_{2(\ell+1)}\gamma=\det\hat{\rho}(\gamma)\hat{W}(z).

On the other hand, we have
\hat{W}(z)=F(z)^{2}W(z)
. Hence,

\begin{split}\hat{W}(z)\big|_{2(\ell+1)}&=\left(F(z)^{2}\big|_{2(\ell+1)}\gamma\right)\left(W(z)\big|_{0}\gamma\right)\\
&=\left(F(z)^{2}\big|_{2(\ell+1)}\gamma\right)\det\rho(\gamma)W(z)=\frac{\left(F(z)^{2}\big|_{2(\ell+1)}\gamma\right)}{F(z)^{2}}\hat{W}(z).\end{split}

Comparing the two expressions, we find that

for all

Lemma 4.4

The ODE (

Proof.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
y_{2}(z)=y_{+}(z)
and
y_{1}(z)=\left(\hat{y}_{+}\big|_{\ell}S\right)(z)/F(z)
.
Then the following hold:

(i)

y_{i}
,
i=1,2
, are linearly independent, and

(ii)

the ratio
h(z)=y_{1}(z)/y_{2}(z)
is equivariant.

Proof.

By Lemma
4.4
,
(
4.1
) is not apparent at

\infty
. Hence, we have a solution
y_{-}(z)
of the form

y_{-}(z)=dzy_{+}(z)+m(z)
with
m(z)=q^{-\kappa_{\infty}}(1+\cdots)
and

. Then with respect to the choice
\hat{Y}(z)=(\hat{y}_{+}(z),\hat{y}_{-}(z))^{t}
, we have
\hat{\rho}(T)=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\
d&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)
.

Now suppose that
y_{+}(z)
and
\left(\hat{y}_{+}\big|_{\ell}S\right)(z)/F(z)
are not
linearly independent, i.e.,
\left(\hat{y}_{2}\big|_{\ell}S\right)(z)=\lambda\hat{y}_{2}(z)
for some
\lambda
. Then

\hat{\rho}(S)=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\lambda&0\\
c&\overline{\lambda}\end{smallmatrix}\right)
,
c\in\mathbb{C}
. Because

\ell
is odd, we have
\hat{\rho}(S^{2})=\hat{\rho}(-I)=(-1)^{\ell}I=-I
. It follows that
\lambda=\pm i
and

\hat{\rho}(R)=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
d&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\lambda&0\\
c&\bar{\lambda}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda&0\\
d\lambda+c&\bar{\lambda}\end{pmatrix},

which implies
\lambda^{3}=-1
, a contradiction because
\lambda=\pm i
. Thus
{y}_{i}
,
i=1,2
, are linearly independent.

Since (
4.1
) is not apparent at
\infty
and we
have shown that
y_{2}(z)=y_{+}(z)
and

y_{1}(z)=\left(\hat{y}_{2}\big|_{\ell}S\right)(z)/F(z)
are linearly
independent, by (
1.11
), there is
d\neq 0

such that

y_{1}(z)=dzy_{2}(z)+m_{1}(z)
for some function

with
a_{0}\neq 0
. We claim that

(4.11)

d=1.

Obviously, with respect to the basis
(\hat{y}_{1}(z),\hat{y}_{2}(z))^{t}

\hat{\rho}(T)=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&d\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)
. On the other hand, since

\hat{y}_{1}(z)=\left(\hat{y}_{2}\big|_{\ell}S\right)(z)

-\hat{y}_{2}(z)=\left(\hat{y}_{2}\big|_{\ell}S^{2}\right)(z)=\left(\hat{y}_{1}\big|_{\ell}S\right)(z)
, we have

\hat{\rho}(S)=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}

and

\hat{\rho}(R)=\begin{pmatrix}1&d\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}d&-1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}.

By

1&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}d&-1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}d^{2}-1&-d\\

which yields

Using

This proves that

is equivariant.
∎

We recall the identity
\hat{y}_{1}(z)=\left(\hat{y}_{2}\big|_{\ell}S\right)(z)=z\hat{y}_{2}(z)+\hat{m}_{1}(z)

obtained in the proof of Lemma

Lemma 4.7
.

We have

(i)

\hat{m}_{1}(z+1)=\hat{m}_{1}(z)
, and

(ii)

\left(\hat{m}_{1}\big|_{\ell-1}S\right)(z)=\hat{m}_{1}(z)

Hence,
\hat{m}_{1}(z)
is a modular form of weight
\ell-1
on
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})

Proof.

The identity (i) is obvious.
By the definition,

of Lemma

(ii)
can be written as

(4.13)

We have

Thus by (

\displaystyle\frac{\hat{m}_{1}(-1/z)}{\hat{y}_{1}(z)}

\displaystyle=\frac{z^{\ell}\hat{m}_{1}(-1/z)}{\hat{y}_{2}(-1/z)}=z^{\ell}\left(\frac{1}{z}-\frac{1}{h(z)}\right)=\frac{(h(z)-z)z^{\ell}}{zh(z)}

∎

Lemma 4.8
.

Write
y_{+}(z)
as

with

\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})

By the previous lemma, we have

\displaystyle=z^{-\ell}\left(\frac{\pi i}{6}z^{\ell-1}\hat{m}_{1}\left(z\right)\left(z^{2}E_{2}(z)+\frac{6}{\pi i}z\right)+\hat{m}_{2}(-1/z)\right)

.
∎

Note that this is the only condition required.

is an elliptic point of

Proposition A.7
.

Assume that the stabilizer subgroup
\Gamma_{z_{0}}
of
z_{0}\in\mathbb{H}

in
\Gamma
has order
N
. Let

f(z)=(1-(1+A)\widetilde{w})^{k}\sum_{n=n_{0}}^{\infty}a_{n}\widetilde{w}^{n}

be the power series expansion in
\widetilde{w}
of a meromorphic modular
form
f
of weight
k
on
\Gamma
at
z_{0}
, where
\widetilde{w}
is given
by (
A.4
). Then
a_{n}=0
whenever

k+2n\not\equiv 0\ \mathrm{mod}\ N
.

Proof.

The analogous statement for (
A.2
) is already known in literature (for
example, see
[
6
]
for the case
k
is even). For the
convenience of the reader, we give a proof here.

Since any meromorphic modular form is the ratio of two holomorphic
modular forms, it suffices to prove the proposition under the
assumption that
f
is a holomorphic modular form. By
Proposition
A.4
,

a_{n}
is a multiple of
\vartheta^{[n]}f(z_{0})
and the proof reduces
to showing that
\vartheta^{[n]}f(z_{0})=0
whenever

k+2n\not\equiv 0\ \mathrm{mod}\ N
.

Recall that
\Gamma_{z_{0}}
is a finite cyclic group (see

[
16
, Proposition 1.16]
), say,
\Gamma_{z_{0}}
is
generated by
\gamma=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{smallmatrix}\right)
of order
N
. Since
z_{0}
is fixed by

\gamma
, we have

z_{0}=\frac{a-d+\sqrt{(d-a)^{2}+4bc}}{2c}=\frac{a-d+\sqrt{(a+d)^{2}-4}}{2c}

and

cz_{0}+d=\frac{a+d+\sqrt{(a+d)^{2}-4}}{2}.

Observe that it is a root of the characteristic polynomial

x^{2}-(a+d)x+1
of
\gamma
, i.e., an eigenvalue of
\gamma
. Hence,

cz_{0}+d
is a primitive
N
th root of unity. Now
\vartheta^{[n]}f

is a holomorphic modular form of weight
k+2n
on
\Gamma
, i.e.,

\vartheta^{[n]}f(\gamma z)=(cz+d)^{k+2n}\vartheta^{[n]}f(z).

Evaluating the two sides at
z_{0}
, we see that

\vartheta^{[n]}f(z_{0})=0
whenever
N\nmid(k+2n)
.
This proves the proposition.
∎

Using this proposition, we immediately obtain a sufficient condition
for (

Theorem A.8
.

Assume that

is an elliptic point of order

on

and the local exponents of (

) at

) is apparent at
z_{0}

Let
Q(z)=(1-(1+A)\widetilde{w})^{4}\sum_{n=-2}^{\infty}a_{n}\widetilde{w}^{n}

be the expansion of
Q(z)
in
\widetilde{w}
. Since
Q(z)
has weight
4
,
we may assume that
\Gamma
contains
-I
. Then the stabilizer
subgroup
\Gamma_{z_{0}}
of

in
\Gamma

(see

[
16

). Hence, by Proposition

whenever

if it has a solution of the form

By Lemma
A.5

A.8

∎
