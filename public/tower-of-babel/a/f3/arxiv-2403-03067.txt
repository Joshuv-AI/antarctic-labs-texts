MSO-Enumeration Over SLP-Compressed Unranked Forests


Abstract
We study the problem of enumerating the answers to a query formulated in monadic second order logic (MSO) over an unranked forest F F that is compressed by a straight-line program (SLP) 𝒟 \mathcal{D} . Our main result states that this can be done after 𝒪 ⁡ ( | 𝒟 | ) \bigO(|\mathcal{D}|) preprocessing and with output-linear delay (in data complexity). This is a substantial improvement over the previously known algorithms for MSO-evaluation over trees, since the compressed size | 𝒟 | |\mathcal{D}| might be much smaller than (or even logarithmic in) the actual data size | F | |F| , and there are linear time SLP-compressors that yield very good compressions on practical inputs. In particular, this also constitutes a meta-theorem in the field of algorithmics on SLP-compressed inputs: all enumeration problems on trees or strings that can be formulated in MSO-logic can be solved with linear preprocessing and output-linear delay, even if the inputs are compressed by SLPs. We also show that our approach can support vertex relabelling updates in time that is logarithmic in the uncompressed data. Our result extends previous work on the enumeration of MSO-queries over uncompressed trees and on the enumeration of document spanners over compressed text documents.

Introduction

The evaluation of queries formulated in
monadic second order logic
(MSO) is a classical problem in database theory and finite model theory. If we consider unrestricted relational structures as the queried data, then even fixed MSO-queries can express NP-hard problems. On the other hand, a seminal result by Doner
[
25
]
and Thatcher and Wright
[
74
]
shows that if the input data is given as a vertex labelled binary tree, then MSO-model checking (for a fixed formula) can be done in linear time. Moreover, the same holds if the data is given by structures of bounded treewidth, which is Courcelle’s famous meta-theorem
[
23
]
.

However, these positive algorithmic results are formulated for
Boolean
MSO-queries, which does not cover practical scenarios, where we wish to compute all answers to a query. Consequently, in the field of database theory, evaluation problems are nowadays mostly investigated in the context of enumeration algorithms that, after some preprocessing on the data and the query, enumerate all answers to the query without duplicates. In terms of running times, we measure the time for the preprocessing and the delay of the enumeration phase, i.e., the time needed to go from one answer to the next. The optimum is
linear preprocessing
and a delay that is always linear in the size of the next answer that is produced, which is called
output-linear delay
. As usual in database theory, we measure in data complexity, which means that the query is considered to be of constant size.

Enumeration algorithms with linear preprocessing and output-linear delay are known for several query evaluation settings, including MSO-evaluation, e.g., for MSO-queries on trees and structures with bounded treewidth
[
4
,
6
,
22
,
44
]
and for regular document spanners (which is a subclass of MSO-queries) on strings
[
3
,
31
]
. Moreover, for these cases the dynamic setting has been investigated as well, where we wish to update our data and then directly enumerate our query again without having to repeat the whole preprocessing (see
[
4
,
9
,
MMN22
,
62
]
).

In this work, we extend MSO-query evaluation on trees towards algorithmics on compressed data, where the input data is given in a
compressed
form and we wish to evaluate the MSO-query without decompressing our data.

1.1
Algorithmics on compressed data

The paradigm of
algorithmics on compressed data
(ACD) aims to solve fundamental computational tasks directly on compressed data objects, without prior decompression. This allows us to work in a completely compressed setting, where our data is always stored and processed in a compressed form. ACD works very well with respect to
grammar-based compression
using so-called
straight-line programs
(SLPs). Such SLPs use grammar-like formalisms in order to specify how to construct the data object from small building blocks. If the data is given by a finite string
w
, then an SLP is just a context-free grammar for the language
\{w\}
, which can be seen as a sequence of instructions that construct
w
from the terminal symbols. For instance, the SLP
S\to AA
,
A\to BBC
,
B\to ba
,
C\to cb
(where
S,A,B,C
are nonterminals and
a,b,c
are terminals) produces the string
babacbbabacb
. String SLPs (s-SLPs for short) are very popular and many results exist that demonstrate their wide-range applicability (see, e.g.,
[
7
,
19
,
33
,
35
,
37
]
for some recent publications and the survey
[
49
]
). Moreover, SLPs achieve very good compression rates in practice (exponential in the best case)
and are tightly related to dictionary based compression, in particular LZ77 and LZ78
[
20
,
65
]
.

An important point is that the ACD paradigm may lead to substantial running time improvements over the uncompressed setting. Indeed, the algorithm’s running time only depends on the size of the compressed input, so the smaller size of the input may directly translate into a lower running time. For example, if the same problem can be solved in linear time both in the uncompressed and in the compressed setting, then in the case that the input can be compressed from size
n
to size
\bigO(\log(n))
(this is possible with SLPs in the best case), the algorithm in the compressed setting is exponentially faster. This is not just hypothetically speaking. In the field of string algorithms several fundamental problems are known to show this behaviour. String pattern matching is a prominent example for this
[
35
]
.

Recently, the ACD paradigm has been combined with the enumeration perspective of query evaluation. In
[
59
,
70
,
67
,
68
]
, the information extraction framework of document spanners is investigated in the compressed setting, and it has been shown that the results of regular spanners over SLP-compressed text documents can be enumerated with linear preprocessing and constant delay.

Since document spanners output span-tuples of constant size, output-linear delay is the same as constant delay in this query evaluation setting.

Applying SLP-based ACD in the framework of document spanners suggests itself, since this is essentially a query model for string data (or sequences), and ACD is most famous in the realm of string algorithms.

An advantage of the grammar-based compression approach is that it can be easily extended to trees. More precisely, by using context-free tree grammars, s-SLPs can be extended to SLPs for ranked trees
[
50
,
54
,
56
]
. In this paper, we use so-called
forest SLPs
(f-SLPs for short) that were defined in
[
38
]
. Forest SLPs allow to compress
node-labelled, unranked and ordered
forests, i.e., ordered lists of trees, where every node has an ordered list of children of arbitrary length. A string is the special case where all trees have size one. An f-SLP can compress such a forest in the horizontal as well as the vertical dimension. Using the horizontal dimension, one can, for instance, compress the forest
aa\cdots a
(
n
many
a
-labelled roots without children) by an f-SLP of size
\bigO(\log n)
, whereas compression in the vertical dimension allows to represent the tree
a(a(\cdots a(a)\cdots))
(a vertical chain of
n

a
-labelled nodes) by an f-SLP of size
\bigO(\log n)
(note that such vertical compression cannot be achieved by simple DAG-compression, i.e., folding a tree into its natural DAG-representation (see also
Section 1.4
)). Formally, f-SLPs are based on the formalism of
forest algebras

[
12
]
. In the forest algebra a forest can be constructed using two operations: the horizontal concatenation
\mathbin{\varominus}
and the vertical concatenation
\mathbin{\varobar}
. The horizontal concatenation simply concatenates two forests horizontally, analogous to the string concatenation. The vertical concatenation of two forests
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
is only defined if
f_{1}
contains exactly one
\ast
-labelled leaf, and
f_{1}\mathbin{\varobar}f_{2}
is then obtained by appending
f_{2}
at the bottom of
f_{1}
, namely at the
\ast
-labelled leaf (i.e., the
\ast
-labelled leaf is replaced by
f_{2}
’s roots). A forest SLP is then a
directed acyclic graph
(DAG) that unfolds into a forest algebra expression. See Sections
4.3.1
and
4.3.2
for a formal treatment and examples (in particular,
Figure 5
shows the graphical representation of a forest algebra expression that evaluates to the tree on the right side of
Figure 6

Figure 6
shows the f-SLP induced by this forest algebra expression). Since typical tree-structured data is unranked (e.g., XML trees or tree decompositions), forest SLPs are a relevant compression scheme in our setting.

Forest SLPs share many of the desirable properties of s-SLPs. They cover other popular tree compression schemes like top dags
[
10
,
27
,
42
]
and tree SLPs
[
36
,
52
]
. Furthermore, there exist compressors such as TreeRePair
[
52
]
that can be used to produce forest SLPs and that show excellent compression ratios in practice. Other available grammar-based tree compressors are BPLEX
[
17
]
and CluX
[
13
]
.

Further motivation for the choice of SLPs as a compression scheme for strings and trees in the context of ACD shall be deferred to the end of this work (see
Section 8
, where we provide comprehensive background information on SLPs). Instead, let us move on to a summary of our main result.

1.2
Main result

Our main result lifts the linear preprocessing and output-linear delay enumeration algorithms for MSO-queries over trees to the case of MSO-queries over SLP-compressed unranked forests. We use the general notation
q[D]
to denote the result set of query
q
over data
D
, and our MSO-queries have the form
\Psi(X_{1},X_{2},\ldots,X_{k})
, where
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots,X_{k}
are free set variables. Complexity bounds in the following theorem and in the remainder of the introduction hold with respect to data complexity.

Theorem 1.1
.

Fix an MSO-query
\Psi(X_{1},X_{2},\ldots,X_{k})
. For an unranked forest
F
that is given in compressed form by a forest SLP
\FSLP
, one can enumerate
\Psi[F]

after linear preprocessing
\bigO(|\FSLP|)
and with output-linear delay.

The algorithm behind this theorem enumerates the answers of an MSO-query with linear preprocessing and output-linear delay (just like the algorithms from
[
6
,
44
]
), but the input is compressed by a forest SLP
\FSLP
and the preprocessing is linear only in the size
\lvert\FSLP\rvert
of the forest SLP instead of the size of the decompressed forest
F
.
Hence, depending on the size of the forest SLP
\FSLP
in comparison to the data
F
(which, theoretically, might be logarithmic, and in practical scenarios can be assumed to be rather small), this yields enumeration with optimal delay, but potentially much faster preprocessing.

Our result also covers and properly extends known results from the literature, e.g., the enumeration algorithms for regular document spanners on compressed and uncompressed strings
[
3
,
31
,
59
,
70
,
67
,
68
]
, and the enumeration algorithms for MSO-queries over uncompressed trees
[
4
,
6
,
22
,
44
]
. It can also be seen as a meta-theorem in the field of algorithmics on SLP-compressed forests (and therefore also strings): Any enumeration problem on SLP-compressed forests (or strings) that can be formulated in MSO-logic can be solved with output-linear delay after a preprocessing linear in the size of the SLP. This covers practically relevant tasks like enumerating all occurrences of a pattern (described by a single string or a regular expression) in a string, enumerating all tandem repeats
w^{k}
in a biosequence (with
k\leq c_{1}
and
|w|\leq c_{2}
for reasonable constants
c_{1},c_{2}
), enumerating the vertices of an unranked tree that have only children of the same kind, enumerating all pairs of close cousins of a phylogenetic tree, etc. All these problems have MSO-formulations. Hence,
Theorem 1.1
yields an algorithm for enumerating
the query result in the case that the data is SLP-compressed.

In addition to our main result, we also investigate the dynamic setting where we assume that after having enumerated the answers to a query, our data can be updated and after such an update, we still want to be able to enumerate the answers to our query (now with respect to the updated data), but without having to repeat the costly preprocessing.

Query evaluation under updates has received a lot of attention over the last decades in database theory, since it covers the practically relevant scenario where we repeatedly evaluate queries over data that is subject to only small changes. With respect to MSO-evaluation over uncompressed trees (and strings),
[
4
,
MMN22
,
62
]
present linear preprocessing and output-linear delay enumeration algorithms with update procedures handling insertions and deletions of leaves (symbols, respectively), and relabelling of vertices (symbols, respectively). The running time of these update procedures is logarithmic in the data size.
We can show that
Theorem 1.1
can be extended by vertex relabelling updates in time that is logarithmic in the uncompressed data; see
Theorem 7.6
for the formal result. Applying the techniques of
[
4
,
MMN22
,
62
]

for the other types of tree updates in the SLP-compressed setting seems rather challenging and we leave this for future research; see
Section 7.4
for a discussion.

1.3
Proof techniques and novel aspects

As explained above, algorithmics on SLP-compressed inputs is a large field with many theoretical and algorithmic results as well as practical implementations. However, most algorithms are tailored to particular computational problems and, to the best of our knowledge, our result is the first that treats the issue in form of a meta-theorem that yields an algorithm for any problem definable in MSO-logic. Moreover, the enumeration aspect is usually not a main focus in algorithmics on SLP-compressed inputs.

Query evaluation over SLP-compressed data can also be seen as an approach to sublinear query evaluation, which is interesting for big data scenarios, where even a linear dependency on the size of the data might be too expensive. More precisely, we spend linear time only once when we compress the data by an SLP. Then we can evaluate an arbitrary query in time that is only linear in the size of the SLP, which is potentially much smaller (logarithmic in the best case) than the actual data size. To our knowledge, this aspect has not yet been considered in the literature on database theory.

Existing algorithms for linear preprocessing and output linear delay of MSO-queries over uncompressed trees (see
[
6
,
4
]
) are similar in the sense that in the preprocessing the query (represented by an automaton) and the input tree are combined into a data structure that represents the whole query result. The enumeration phase then enumerates from this data structure the tuples of node sets. On the one hand, the underlying data structure must represent the exponentially large query result in a concise and implicit way, but, on the other hand, it explicitly contains the complete input tree. In the compressed setting, however, the data structure must respect the compression of the input data and it is therefore impossible for it to explicitly contain the nodes of the input tree. Consequently, the enumeration phase must not only derive all the elements of the query result from the data structure, it must also construct (or decompress) the actual nodes of the input tree that appear in the output tuples, and since this cannot be done in a preprocessing, it must be done on-the-fly during the enumeration. Let us give a high level description of how we solve this task, i.e., how we prove
Theorem 1.1
.

By several simplification steps, we first reduce the problem from
Theorem 1.1
to the following enumeration problem for binary trees: given a directed acyclic graph (DAG)
\mathcal{D}
that unfolds to the binary vertex-labelled tree
T
and a deterministic bottom-up tree automaton
\mathcal{B}
, enumerate all subsets
S\subseteq\mathsf{leaves}(T)
such that
\mathcal{B}
accepts the tree obtained from
T
by marking all leaves from
S
with a
1
. Since
T
is a binary tree, we could solve this problem by using Bagan’s algorithm from
[
6
]
, but
T
is given by the DAG
\mathcal{D}
and we cannot afford to explicitly construct
T
. Consequently, we have to adapt Bagan’s algorithm in such a way that it can be used directly on DAG-compressed trees, which is not a trivial task.
As mentioned above, we cannot afford to compute the data structure used by Bagan’s algorithm, but we can compute a compressed variant of this data structure, which is an edge-labelled DAG. In order to exploit this compressed data structure in a similar fashion as done by the enumeration phase of Bagan’s algorithm, we have to be able to enumerate with constant delay the labels of the paths of this DAG, since these edge labels represent the actual nodes of the uncompressed tree.
2
2

2

Actually, the edges of the DAG will be labelled with the morphisms of a category, but for the sake of an intuitive and high-level explanation we abstract here from this detail.
This enumeration procedure constitutes a non-trivial algorithmic result and is the main component of our algorithm (it will be proven as an independent algorithmic result in
Section 2.5
, where we also demonstrate that it has further applications of independent interest).

It is not unlikely that instead of extending Bagan’s algorithm from
[
6
]
in this fashion, we can also extend the algorithm of
[
4
]
to the compressed setting, i.e., we may be able to obtain from a compressed input tree in linear time a compressed version of the data structure used by the algorithm from
[
4
]
, and then find a way to employ this compressed data structure in the enumeration phase. However, this would most likely lead to similar challenges compared to extending Bagan’s algorithm. In particular, it is likely that extensions of other algorithms eventually also require a procedure that enumerates paths in a DAG (simply because in the SLP-compressed setting the nodes of the tree are represented by paths in a DAG and we cannot afford to precompute them in a preprocessing).

1.4
Further related work

A vast body of literature is concerned with straight-line programs and their application in the context of algorithmics on compressed data. We will give a comprehensive discussion of those aspects relevant for our work in
Section 8
towards the end of the paper.

Forest SLPs – the central compression scheme in our work – are based on forest algebra expressions. Forest algebras have been also used in the context of MSO-enumeration on uncompressed trees in
[
MMN22
]
for the purpose of enabling updates of the queried tree in logarithmic time by updating and re-balancing a forest algebra expression for the tree. However, no compression is handled in
[
MMN22
]
.

Relabelling updates have been also studied recently in the context of dynamic regular membership testing
[
2
]
.
For a fixed regular set of forests and a forest that undergoes relabelling updates, the goal is to maintain the information whether
the current forest belongs to
L
. It is shown that this can be done time in time
\bigO(\log n/\log\log n)
per update, where
n
is the number of nodes in the forest, and in constant time per update for so-called almost-commutative regular forest languages.
Also in
[
2
]
the concept of forest algebras plays an important role.

The arguably simplest way of compressing a tree is to fold it into a DAG. In the best case this allows to represent a tree of size
n
by
a DAG of size
\Theta(\log n)
(take for instance a perfect binary tree).
In the context of database theory, DAG-compression has been investigated in
[
16
,
32
]
for XPath and monadic datalog queries, but the enumeration perspective has not been investigated.

Forest SLPs subsume DAGs in the sense that a DAG for a tree
t
can be easily translated into a forest SLP for
t
of asymptotically the same size as the DAG. Moreover, there are also trees, where forest SLPs compress exponentially better than DAGs, e.g., unary trees of the form
a(a(\ldots a(a)\ldots))
. The experimental study of
[
52
]
also shows that in a practical setting, DAG-compression cannot compete with forest SLPs. Further work on the compression performance of DAGs for XML can be found in
[
15
,
53
]
.

There is a growing body of work on
factorised databases
that investigates the task of producing the result of a conjunctive relational query as a
factorised representation
, which is a compressed form obtained by exploiting redundancies (see
[
63
,
64
]
). The motivation is that such query results are often just intermediate data objects that are inputs for further computations within a larger pipeline, and computing them in factorised form can decrease the overall computation time. This approach, however, is slightly different from ours: In factorised databases, the compressibility is entailed by the fact that the object to compress is the result of a conjunctive query, while in our case tree structured input data is given in a compressed form.

2.3
Trees and forests

We will work with different types of rooted trees and acyclic graphs in this paper. All graphs (including trees) will be finite.
Forests are sequences of trees, and we should keep in mind that each definition of a certain tree model yields the corresponding concept of forests (which are just sequences of such trees).

2.3.1
Vertex-labelled ordered trees

Vertex-labelled ordered trees are trees, where vertices are labelled with symbols from some alphabet
\Sigma

T=(V,E,R,\lambda)
, where
V
is the set of vertices,
E
is the edge relation (i.e.,
(u,v)\in E
if and only if

is a child of

R

T
and one of its vertices
v

to denote

A

\lambda
).
We write
\mathsf{F}(\Sigma)
for the set of all forests with vertex labels from
\Sigma
. The size

of a forest

F
.

2.3.2
Vertex-labelled binary trees

Vertex-labelled binary trees (binary trees for short) are the special case of the trees from the previous paragraph, where every vertex is either

2.3.3
Unordered trees

Unordered trees are trees without vertex labels and without an order on the children of a vertex. They will be used
as auxiliary data structures in our algorithms. An unordered tree will
be defined as a pair

, where

is the set of vertices and

is the edge relation. An unordered forest is a disjoint union of unordered trees.

2.3.4
Decorated trees

We also have to consider (ordered as well as unordered) trees, where the vertices and edges are decorated with objects and morphisms, respectively, from a
category

. More precisely, a

of the tree to an object

and
every edge

be the unique path from a vertex

to a descendant

in the tree, i.e.,
(v_{i},v_{i+1})\in E
for all

. We then define
\gamma(v_{1},v_{d})
as the morphism
\gamma(v_{1},v_{2})\circ\gamma(v_{2},v_{3})\circ\cdots\circ\gamma(v_{d-1},v_{d})

of the tree

we define
\gamma^{*}(v)=\gamma(r,v)
,
where

is the root of

.

The outputs of our enumeration algorithms will be sets

for certain leaf sets

and a specific category

.

2.4
Directed acyclic graphs

As commonly defined, a

that has no cycles, i.e., there is no non-empty path from a vertex

back to

We will need DAGs with multiple edges between vertices. For this,

can be taken as a subset

for some

. Then we can have two different edges
(u,i,v)
and
(u,j,v)
(with

) from

to

.
The size

of

is defined as
|E|+|V|
.
The

(resp.,

is the number of edges of the form

(resp.,
(u,i,v)

Analogously to trees, vertices of a DAG with outdegree

(from

to

is a word

(v_{k},i_{k},v_{k+1})\in E
for all

.
The length of this path

We write

for the terminal vertex of the path

If

to some vertex in

for

Paths in

If in the above definition the index set

for a

The DAGs in this paper will mostly contain some of the following additional components, where
\mathcal{C}=(A,(M_{a,b})_{a,b\in A},\circ)

is a category and
\Sigma
is a finite set of vertex labels.

•

A decoration mapping
\gamma
that assigns an object

to every vertex

and a morphism
\gamma(e)\in M_{\gamma(u),\gamma(v)}
to every edge

For a path

we define its

For the empty path

at vertex

we set

.
3
3

3

The reader familiar with category theory may notice
that

to the category
\mathcal{C}
.

The resulting structure

is called a

-decorated DAG
.

•

A vertex labelling function

, which results in a vertex-labelled DAG.
We will need this concept only for a binary DAG

is the disjoint union of

and

and

for every leaf of

When a DAG contains one of the components

2.5
Enumeration algorithms

We use the standard RAM model with a special assumption about handling the morphisms of the category
\mathcal{C}
of
\mathcal{C}
-decorated DAGs (discussed towards the end of this section), and a restriction for the register length (discussed at the end of
subsubsection 4.3.2
).

An enumeration problem is a function
E
that maps an input
I
to a finite set
E(I)
of objects. We can assume that
I
and the objects in
E(I)

are written in RAM registers. An enumeration algorithm
A
for
E
is an algorithm that computes on input
I
a sequence

(s_{1},s_{2},\ldots,s_{m},s_{m+1})
, where
E(I)=\{s_{1},\ldots,s_{m}\}
,
s_{i}\neq s_{j}
for all
i\neq j
and

s_{m+1}=\mathsf{EOE}
is the
end-of-enumeration
marker. The algorithm produces this sequence in order, i.e., the algorithm only starts with
the computation of
s_{i+1}
, once it finishes outputting
s_{i}
.
The
preprocessing time
of
A
on input
I
is the time when the algorithm starts with outputting
s_{1}
.
The preprocessing time of
A
is the maximum preprocessing time over all possible inputs
I
of length at most
n
(viewed as a function of
n
).

Besides the preprocessing time, the other important time measure for an enumeration algorithm
A
is its
delay
, which measures
the maximal time between the computation of two consecutive outputs
s_{i}
and
s_{i+1}
. In our situation, the output objects
s_{i}
will be very large
(potentially much larger than the input
I
). Therefore, it does not make sense to measure the delay with respect to the input length
I
. In such situations,
the notion of
output-linear delay
makes sense. It requires that for every output sequence
(s_{1},s_{2},\ldots,s_{m},s_{m+1})
produced by the algorithm
A
,
if
t_{i}
(
1\leq i\leq m+1
) is the time when
A
starts with outputting
s_{i}
, then
t_{i+1}-t_{i}=\bigO(|s_{i}|)
for every
1\leq i\leq m
.
If every output
s_{i}
has constant size (which for the RAM model means that it occupies a constant number of registers), then output-linear delay is the same as constant delay.
The gold standard in the area of enumeration algorithms is (i)
linear preprocessing
(i.e., the preprocessing time is
\bigO(|I|)
) and
(ii)
output-linear delay
.

We are interested in enumeration algorithms that enumerate subsets
U\subseteq V
of a forest
F=(V,E,R,\lambda)
.
These subsets represent the results of a query
\mathcal{Q}
(that is given by an MSO-formula or a tree automaton; see
Section 5
).
The input is
F
, while the query
\mathcal{Q}
is fixed and not part of the input, i.e., we measure in data complexity. The special feature of this work is that the input forest
F
is not given explicitly, but in a potentially highly compressed form, and the enumeration algorithm must be able to handle this compressed representation rather than decompressing it. This aspect shall be explained in detail in
Section 4
.

Since we also deal with
\mathcal{C}
-decorated DAGs, where morphisms from a category
\mathcal{C}
are assigned to edges, we need the following assumption for the proof of our main result (recall that
\omega(\pi)
is the terminal vertex of a path
\pi
; see
Section 2.4
):

{assumption}

Let
\mathcal{C}=(A,(M_{a,b})_{a,b\in A},\circ)
be a category. If a
\mathcal{C}
-decorated DAG
\mathcal{D}=(V,E,\gamma)
is part of the input then

•

vertices from
V
as well as

•

all morphisms
\gamma(\pi)
for
\pi
a path in
\mathcal{D}

fit into a single register of our RAM.
For two such morphisms
\gamma(\pi_{1})

and
\gamma(\pi_{2})
such that
\omega(\pi_{1})
is the first vertex of
\pi_{2}
, the morphism
\gamma(\pi_{1})\circ\gamma(\pi_{2})

can be computed in constant time on the RAM.

For the specific setting that arises in the proof of
Theorem 1.1
, this assumption will be justified later.
Only in
Section 3.3
, where we discuss some further applications of our path enumeration
technique from
Section 3
, we will deviate from
Section 2.5
.

While this is not our algorithmic focus, it will occasionally also be convenient to talk about enumeration with duplicates, which we formalise as enumerating a multiset. More precisely, we say that an algorithm
A
on input
I
enumerates a multiset
S
if and only if the output sequence is
(s_{1},s_{2},\ldots,s_{m},\mathsf{EOE})
,
|S|=m
and
S=\{\!\{s_{1},s_{2},\ldots,s_{m}\}\!\}
. All other notions defined above apply in the same way also for enumerations of multisets.

Theorem 3.1
.

Fix a category

holds,
and let

be a distinguished set of target vertices. In time

one can compute a data structure that allows to enumerate for a given source vertex

in constant delay the multiset

\{\!\{\langle\omega(\pi),\gamma(\pi)\rangle:\pi\in\mathsf{path}_{\mathcal{D}}(s,V_{0})\}\!\}
.
4
4

4

In general, we might have
\langle\omega(\pi),\gamma(\pi)\rangle=\langle\omega(\pi^{\prime}),\gamma(\pi^{\prime})\rangle
for
different paths
\pi,\pi^{\prime}\in\mathsf{path}_{\mathcal{D}}(s,V_{0})
(although this does not happen for the decoration used later on in the application of this result in the context of

).
Therefore, we have to work with multisets.

Let us write
M
for the union of all the morphism sets

from
Theorem 3.1
. In the following, we let

be the source vertex that we get as input according to the statement of

\{\!\{\langle\omega(\pi),\gamma(\pi)\rangle:\pi\in\mathsf{path}_{\mathcal{D}}(s,V_{0})\}\!\}

3.1
Preprocessing

Let us start with some preprocessing for the DAG
\mathcal{D}=(V,E,\gamma)
. We first remove as long as possible vertices

of outdegree zero that do not belong to the target set
V_{0}
(and we remember these removed vertices so that in case the given source vertex

is one of them, we can simply output the empty list). We can therefore assume that all vertices of outdegree zero belong to
V_{0}
. If there is a vertex
v\in V_{0}
of non-zero outdegree, we can add a copy
v^{\prime}

together with a new edge
(v,i,v^{\prime})
and extend the decoration by
\gamma(v^{\prime})=\gamma(v)
and
\gamma(v,i,v^{\prime})=\iota_{\gamma(v)}
(recall that

is the identity
morphism for object

from

and
add
v^{\prime}
to
V_{0}
.
By this, we can assume that
V_{0}
consists of all vertices of outdegree zero (the leaves of the DAG).

We next eliminate vertices of outdegree one.
In time
\bigO(\lvert\mathcal{D}\rvert)
we first determine the set
V_{1}
of vertices of outdegree one.
For every vertex
u\in V_{1}
we then compute the unique vertex
f(u)
such that the outdegree of
f(u)
is not
1
and
f(u)
is reached
from

by the (unique) path
\pi_{u}
consisting of edges
(v_{1},i,v_{2})\in E
with

. We also compute the morphism
\gamma(\pi_{u})
for this

as follows: For every edge
(u,i,v)
with

we set

and
\gamma(\pi_{u})=\gamma(u,i,v)

(this includes the case where

and

. We then replace every edge

with

and set

.
After this step, there is no edge that ends in a vertex of outdegree one. In particular, if a vertex has outdegree one,
its indegree is zero. We can then remove all vertices of outdegree one and their outgoing edges from
\mathcal{D}
and store
them together with their decorations in a separate table. In case that the source vertex

has outdegree one and

is its unique outgoing edge, we run the enumeration algorithm for

instead of

-morphism that is printed in the enumeration for

We can now assume that all vertices of the DAG

have outdegree zero or at least two.

We next transform

into a binary DAG

and every vertex

For this, take a vertex

An

Take all words

such that

3.3.2
Permutation groups

In this section we briefly discuss another application of
Theorem 3.1
to permutation groups.
The set
\mathsf{Sym}(n)
of all permutations on
\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
is a group under the operation of composition (the so-called
symmetric group of degree
n
). A permutation group
G
is simply a subgroup of
\mathsf{Sym}(n)
(denoted by
G\leq\mathsf{Sym}(n)
), which is usually given by
a generating set
A\subseteq\mathsf{Sym}(n)
. Thus,
G
is the closure
\langle A\rangle
of the set
A
under composition.
There are many algorithmic results for permutation groups; see, e.g., the monograph
[
71
]
. Among others, there are polynomial
time algorithms for testing membership in permutation groups and computing the size of a permutation group.

We can use
Theorem 3.1
to enumerate a given permutation group
G=\langle A\rangle\leq\mathsf{Sym}(n)

with delay
\Theta(n)
after a polynomial preprocessing time. Like many other permutation group algorithms,
our enumeration method is based
on the stabiliser chain
1=G_{n}\leq G_{n-1}\leq\cdots\leq G_{1}\leq G_{0}=G
, where
G_{i}
the subgroup of
G
consisting
of all
g\in G
such that
g(a)=a
for all
1\leq a\leq i
. It is known that one can compute in polynomial time from
A
for every

0\leq i\leq n-1
a set of right coset representatives
R_{i}
of
G_{i+1}
in
G_{i}
; see
[
71
, Chapter 4]
. Thus, for every
g\in G_{i}
there is a unique
r\in R_{i}

such that
g\in G_{i+1}r
. The union
R=\bigcup_{0\leq i\leq n-1}R_{i}
is called a strong generating set of
G
. It has the property
that every element of
g\in G
can be uniquely written as
g=r_{n-1}r_{n-2}\cdots r_{0}
with
r_{i}\in R_{i}
for all
0\leq i\leq n-1
.
Hence, in order to enumerate
G
, we can first construct in polynomial time a strong generating set
R=\bigcup_{0\leq i\leq n-1}R_{i}
and a corresponding DAG

\mathcal{D}
consisting of nodes
0,1,\ldots,n
and
|R_{i}|
many edges from
i+1
to
i
that are labelled with the elements from
R_{i}

(
0\leq i\leq n-1
). We then use Algorithm
1
to enumerate all products labelling the paths from the source vertex
n
to the target vertex
0
. This will not produce duplicates.

For complexity considerations, we assume a RAM of register length
\Theta(\log n)
so that
elements from
\{1,\ldots,n\}
can be stored in a constant number of registers.
Hence, a permutation
g\in\mathsf{Sym}(n)
is stored in
\Theta(n)
registers and the multiplication of two permutations needs
time
\Theta(n)
. Under this assumption, the delay of Algorithm
1
is
\Theta(n)
, which is also the time needed to output
a single permutation. In other words: the enumeration algorithm works in output-linear delay.

Corollary 3.3
.

Given a set of permutations
A\subseteq\mathsf{Sym}(n)
, one can enumerate on a RAM with register length
\Theta(\log n)

the permutation group
\langle A\rangle
after polynomial preprocessing in delay
\Theta(n)
.

We now come back to the main topic of this paper: MSO-query enumeration in compressed forests.
In this section we will introduce
forest straight-line programs

For better motivation we start with the simpler concept of

(s-SLPs for short), which are a special

In

4.1

A simple but important concept for SLPs are the operations of folding a labelled tree into a DAG and unfolding a DAG

) and the label of

.
Moreover, if
(u,v)\in E_{d}
(
d\in\{\ell,r\}
)
then there is a DAG-edge
([u]_{\equiv},d,[v]_{\equiv})
. Intuitively, we merge all vertices of
T
where isomorphic subtrees are rooted. See
Figure 3
for an example.

A binary tree

with labels from

In the same way, we can define the DAG-folding of ordered trees with larger arity (we just need a larger index set

for the DAG-edges since there can be more than
two edges between two vertices). The DAG-folding of a syntax tree of an algebraic expression (which is a vertex-labelled ranked tree) is often called an algebraic circuit.
It can be easily seen that there is a one-to-one correspondence between the vertices of a tree
T
and the paths of its DAG-folding that start in the root (i.e., the unique vertex of in-degree

). For example, in

are represented by the paths

The DAG-folding

can be seen as a compressed representation of the tree

. DAG-compression is used in many different
areas of computer science (see
[
26
,
30
,
32
]
for further details), but it has also limitations.
For instance, the size of

is always lower
bounded by the height of

. In

we will introduce a compression scheme for trees (and forests) that overcomes this
limitation.

For our applications, more important than the DAG-folding is the opposite operation, i.e., the
unfolding
of a DAG. We first define this concept for a DAG
\mathcal{D}=(V,E)
without any further structure, where

for an index set

\mathsf{unfold}_{\mathcal{D}}(v)
for a vertex

(the unfolding of
\mathcal{D}
from vertex
v
) has the vertex set

\mathsf{path}_{\mathcal{D}}(v)
consisting of all paths
that start in
v
and end in an arbitrary vertex. For two paths

,
there is an edge
(\pi,\pi^{\prime})
if there are

and
v^{\prime}\in V
with
\pi^{\prime}=\pi iv^{\prime}
.
If
\mathcal{D}
is a binary DAG then
\mathsf{unfold}_{\mathcal{D}}(v)
becomes a binary tree in a natural way by
declaring the edge
(\pi,\pi iv^{\prime})
to be a left (resp., right) edge if

(resp.,
i=r
).
In addition, if the DAG
\mathcal{D}
is
\mathcal{C}
-decorated (for a category
\mathcal{C}
) with the decoration mapping
\gamma
then

\mathsf{unfold}_{\mathcal{D}}(v)
becomes a

-decorated tree by extending

to

\mathsf{unfold}_{\mathcal{D}}(v)

\gamma(\pi)=\gamma(\omega(\pi))
and

\gamma(\pi,\pi iv^{\prime})=\gamma(\omega(\pi),i,v^{\prime})
(recall that
\omega(\pi)
is the terminal vertex of
\pi
; see

Section 2.4
).
Similarly, if
\mathcal{D}
is vertex-labelled with the labelling function
\lambda:V\to\Sigma
then

\mathsf{unfold}_{\mathcal{D}}(v)
becomes also vertex-labelled by setting
\lambda(\pi)=\lambda(\omega(\pi))
for a path
\pi
.

Let
\mathcal{D}=(V,E,\lambda)
be the vertex-labelled binary DAG (without a decoration mapping

) on the right of

Obviously, according to the above definition,

on the left of

4.2
Straight-line programs for strings

Let us denote for the moment the concatenation operation for strings over a finite alphabet
\Sigma
by
\mathbin{\varominus}
(usually, we write
uv
for

u\mathbin{\varominus}v
, but in this section it will be convenient to treat string concatenation more prominently as a binary operation on strings). Then
(\Sigma^{*},\mathbin{\varominus})
is an algebraic structure that is usually called the free monoid over
\Sigma
. An expression (or syntax tree)
T
over
(\Sigma^{*},\mathbin{\varominus},(a)_{a\in\Sigma})
(the free monoid with all alphabet symbols added as constants) is a binary tree with leaves labelled by symbols from
\Sigma
and inner vertices labelled by
\mathbin{\varominus}
. This expression
T
naturally evaluates in

(\Sigma^{*},\mathbin{\varominus},(a)_{a\in\Sigma})
to a string
\llbracket T\rrbracket
. It is the word obtained by traversing the leaves of
T
from left to right and thereby writing the labels of the leaves.

Usually, string straight-line programs are defined as context-free grammars in Chomsky normal form that derive exactly one string; see
Section 8
. For our extension to forest straight-line programs the following
more algebraic definition is useful.
A
string straight-line program
(s-SLP for short)
is a (binary vertex-labelled) DAG
\SLP=(V,E,\lambda)
such that

\lambda(A)\in\Sigma
for every leaf
A
of
\SLP
and
\lambda(A)=\mathbin{\varominus}
for every non-leaf vertex
A
.
In other words, every tree
\mathsf{unfold}_{\SLP}(A)
for
A\in V
is a syntax tree over
(\Sigma^{*},\mathbin{\varominus},(a)_{a\in\Sigma})
.
We follow here the tradition that vertices of an s-SLP are denoted by capital letters (in the grammar-like definition of an s-SLP
they correspond to non-terminals). We then define for every vertex
A\in V
the string

\llbracket A\rrbracket_{\SLP}=\llbracket\mathsf{unfold}_{\SLP}(A)\rrbracket
.

Often in the literature one adds to an s-SLP
\SLP=(V,E,\lambda)
a distinguished start vertex
A\in V
(the start non-terminal in the grammar-like
formalization) and
defines
\llbracket\SLP\rrbracket=\llbracket\mathsf{unfold}_{\SLP}(A)\rrbracket
(the string
represented
or
compressed
by the s-SLP
\SLP
).
We then also say that
\SLP
is an
s-SLP for
\llbracket\SLP\rrbracket
.
For example, let
w=aabbabaabaabbab\in\Sigma^{*}
. Then

T=((((a\mathbin{\varominus}(a\mathbin{\varominus}b))\mathbin{\varominus}b))\mathbin{\varominus}(a\mathbin{\varominus}b))\mathbin{\varominus}((a\mathbin{\varominus}(a\mathbin{\varominus}b))\mathbin{\varominus}(((a\mathbin{\varominus}(a\mathbin{\varominus}b))\mathbin{\varominus}b)\mathbin{\varominus}(a\mathbin{\varominus}b)))

is an expression over
(\Sigma^{*},\mathbin{\varominus},a,b)
with
\llbracket T\rrbracket=w
(due to associativity, parentheses can be removed, but they determine the structure of
T
, which is obviously crucial for s-SLPs). The syntax tree
T
is shown on the left of
Figure 4
, and the right shows the DAG-folding of
T
and therefore an s-SLP for
w
.

for left and right edges are implicitly represented by the drawing of the s-SLP (i.e., the left edge of a vertex is always drawn to the left of the right edge).

4.3
Straight-line programs for unranked forests

Straight-line programs for trees or, more generally, forests can be defined in a similar way as for strings. We only have to replace the algebra for strings (i.e., the free monoid over
\Sigma^{*}
) by a suitable algebra
A
for forests. We start with the definition of this algebra.

4.3.1
Forest algebra

Recall that
\mathsf{F}(\Sigma)
is the set of all forests with vertex labels from
\Sigma
.
Let us fix a distinguished symbol
\ast\not\in\Sigma
.
The set of forests
F\in\mathsf{F}(\Sigma\cup\{\ast\})
such that
\ast
has a unique
occurrence in
F
and this occurrence is at a leaf vertex is denoted by
\mathsf{F}_{\ast}(\Sigma)
.
Elements of
\mathsf{F}_{\ast}(\Sigma)
are called
forest contexts
. Note that
\mathsf{F}(\Sigma)\cap\mathsf{F}_{\ast}(\Sigma)=\emptyset
.
Following
[
12
]
, we define the
forest algebra

as the 2-sorted algebra

(\mathsf{F}(\Sigma)\cup\mathsf{F}_{\ast}(\Sigma),\mathbin{\varominus},\mathbin{\varobar},\varepsilon,\ast)
,
where
\varepsilon\in\mathsf{F}(\Sigma)
is the empty forest,
\ast\in\mathsf{F}_{\ast}(\Sigma)
is the empty forest context, and

\mathbin{\varominus}
(
horizontal concatenation
) and
\mathbin{\varobar}
(
vertical concatenation
) are partially defined binary
operations on
\mathsf{F}(\Sigma)\cup\mathsf{F}_{\ast}(\Sigma)
that are defined as follows, where we view forests and forest contexts as parenthesised expressions built from the
binary symbols
\mathbin{\varobar}
and
\mathbin{\varominus}
, and the constants from
\Sigma\cup\{\ast\}
(see also
subsubsection 2.3.1
):

•

For
F_{1},F_{2}\in\mathsf{F}(\Sigma)\cup\mathsf{F}_{\ast}(\Sigma)
such that
F_{1}\in\mathsf{F}(\Sigma)
or
F_{2}\in\mathsf{F}(\Sigma)
,
we set
F_{1}\mathbin{\varominus}F_{2}=F_{1}F_{2}
(i.e., we concatenate the corresponding sequences of trees).

•

For
F_{1}\in\mathsf{F}_{\ast}(\Sigma)
and
F_{2}\in\mathsf{F}(\Sigma)\cup\mathsf{F}_{\ast}(\Sigma)
,
F_{1}\mathbin{\varobar}F_{2}
is obtained by replacing in
F_{1}
the unique
occurrence of
\ast
by
F_{2}
.

Consider
a(b\ast)\mathbin{\varobar}(a(bc)\mathbin{\varominus}b(ccb))=a(b\ast)\mathbin{\varobar}a(bc)b(ccb)=a(ba(bc)b(ccb))
as an example for these operations.

Note that
(\mathsf{F}(\Sigma),\mathbin{\varominus})
and
(\mathsf{F}_{\ast}(\Sigma),\mathbin{\varobar})
are monoids with the neutral elements
\varepsilon
(the empty forest) and

\ast
(the empty forest context) and that
(\Sigma^{*},\mathbin{\varominus})
is a submonoid of
(\mathsf{F}(\Sigma),\mathbin{\varominus})
.
For
a\in\Sigma
, we write
a_{\ast}
for the forest context
a(\ast)
which consists
of an
a
-labelled root with a single child labelled with
\ast
. Note that
a=a_{\ast}\mathbin{\varobar}\varepsilon
.
More generally, for a forest
F
,
a_{*}\mathbin{\varobar}F
yields the tree obtained from the forest
F
by adding an
a
-labelled
root vertex on top of the forest
F
.
In
[
12
]
the forest algebra is introduced as a two sorted algebra with the two sorts
\mathsf{F}(\Sigma)
and
\mathsf{F}_{\ast}(\Sigma)
.
Our approach with partially defined concatenation operators is equivalent.

A
forest algebra expression
is an expression over the algebra
(\mathsf{F}(\Sigma)\cup\mathsf{F}_{\ast}(\Sigma),\mathbin{\varominus},\mathbin{\varobar},\varepsilon,\ast)
with atomic subexpressions
of the form
a
and
a_{\ast}
for
a\in\Sigma
. Such an expression can be identified with a vertex-labelled binary tree, where
every internal vertex is labelled with the operator
\mathbin{\varominus}
or
\mathbin{\varobar}

and every leaf is labelled with a symbol
a
or
a_{\ast}
for
a\in\Sigma
.
Not all such trees are valid in the sense that they evaluate to an element from
\mathsf{F}(\Sigma)\cup\mathsf{F}_{\ast}(\Sigma)
, e.g.,
a_{\ast}\mathbin{\varominus}a_{\ast}
is not valid, since it would produce a forest with two occurrences of
\ast
.
We define
valid forest algebra expressions
and the
type

\tau(T)\in\{0,1\}
of a valid forest algebra expression inductively as follows:

•

For every
a\in\Sigma
,
a
and
a_{\ast}
are valid and
\tau(a)=0
and
\tau(a_{\ast})=1
.

•

If
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
are valid and
\tau(T_{1})+\tau(T_{2})\leq 1
then
T_{1}\mathbin{\varominus}T_{2}
is valid
and
\tau(T_{1}\mathbin{\varominus}T_{2})=\tau(T_{1})+\tau(T_{2})
.

•

If
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
are valid and
\tau(T_{1})=1
then
T_{1}\mathbin{\varobar}T_{2}
is valid
and
\tau(T_{1}\mathbin{\varobar}T_{2})=\tau(T_{2})
.

We will only consider valid forest algebra expressions
in the following. We write
\mathcal{E}(\Sigma)
for the set of all valid forest algebra expressions.
Elements of
\mathcal{E}(\Sigma)
will be denoted with
\phi
in the following. Moreover, we set
\mathcal{E}_{i}(\Sigma)=\{\phi\in\mathcal{E}(\Sigma):\tau(\phi)=i\}
for
i\in\{0,1\}
.

With
\llbracket\phi\rrbracket\in\mathsf{F}(\Sigma)\cup\mathsf{F}_{\ast}(\Sigma)
we denote the forest or forest context obtained by evaluating
\phi
in the forest algebra.
If
\tau(\phi)=0
then
\llbracket\phi\rrbracket\in\mathsf{F}(\Sigma)
and if
\tau(\phi)=1
then
\llbracket\phi\rrbracket\in\mathsf{F}_{\ast}(\Sigma)
.
The empty forest
\varepsilon
and the empty forest context
\ast
are not allowed in forest algebra expressions, which is not a restriction as long as we only want to produce non-empty forests and forest contexts; see
[
37
, Lemma 3.27]
.
Note that the vertices of
\llbracket\phi\rrbracket
can be mapped bijectively on the leaves of
\phi
.
For the rest of the paper we always assume that the vertices of
\llbracket\phi\rrbracket
are the leaves of
\phi
.
For a leaf
v
of
\phi
we write
\mathsf{po}(v)
for preorder number of the corresponding vertex of

\llbracket\phi\rrbracket
.

Figure 5
shows a forest algebra expression
\phi
, which evaluates to the forest on the right of
Figure 6
.
Every vertex of
\llbracket\phi\rrbracket
is labelled with its preorder number in blue and the vertex with preorder number
k
is the leaf
with number
k
in
Figure 5
. If
v
is the leaf of
\phi
identified by the green path in
Figure 5
, then

\mathsf{po}(v)=14
.

4.3.2
Forest straight-line programs

Based on the forest algebra, we can now define forest straight-line programs in an analogous way as we did for string straight-line programs (forest straight-line programs were introduced in
[
38
]
in a more grammar-like way that is nevertheless equivalent to the following approach). A
forest straight-line program
(over
\Sigma
), f-SLP for short, is a binary DAG
\FSLP=(V,E,\lambda)
such that
\mathsf{unfold}_{\FSLP}(A)\in\mathcal{E}(\Sigma)
for all
A\in V
. We can assign to every vertex
A\in V
its type
\tau(A)=\tau(\mathsf{unfold}_{\FSLP}(A))
.
We also say that
A
is a
forest vertex
if
\tau(A)=0
. Moreover, we define
\llbracket A\rrbracket_{\FSLP}=\llbracket\mathsf{unfold}_{\FSLP}(A)\rrbracket
. If
\FSLP
is clear from the context then we write

\llbracket A\rrbracket
instead of
\llbracket A\rrbracket_{\FSLP}
.
Note that an s-SLP is an f-SLP
\FSLP=(V,E,\lambda)
such that
\lambda(A)\in\{\mathbin{\varominus}\}\cup\Sigma
for every
A\in V
.

shows an f-SLP
\FSLP
on the left, where
A
is the topmost vertex. Then the forest

\llbracket A\rrbracket
(actually, a tree) is shown on the right. Every vertex is additionally labelled with its preorder number in blue.
The forest algebra expression

\mathsf{unfold}_{\FSLP}(A)
is shown in
Figure 5
.
The green path
\pi
in
\FSLP
(resp.,
\mathsf{unfold}_{\FSLP}(A)
) determines the green
b
-labelled vertex in the tree
\llbracket A\rrbracket
.
Recall that we identify the path
\pi
with a leaf of
\mathsf{unfold}_{\FSLP}(A)
(namely the leaf labelled with 14 in

Figure 5
).

An example f-SLP
\FSLP
(left side) that describes the tree
\llbracket A\rrbracket
on the right side. The forest algebra expression that corresponds to the unfolding
\mathsf{unfold}_{\FSLP}(A)
is shown in

.

Occasionally, (and when the underlying f-SLP
\FSLP=(V,E,\lambda)
is clear from the context)
we will write
N_{A}
for the number of vertices of
\llbracket A\rrbracket_{\FSLP}
(
A\in V
).
Since for every vertex
A\in V
with left child
B
and right child
C
we have

N_{A}\leq N_{B}+N_{C}
, we get the following simple fact:

Lemma 4.1
.

For every f-SLP
\FSLP=(V,E,\lambda)
and every vertex
A\in V
we have
N_{A}\leq 2^{\lvert V\rvert}\leq 2^{\lvert\FSLP\rvert}
.

We make the assumption that RAM-algorithms for an f-SLP
\FSLP=(V,E,\lambda)
have registers
of word length
\bigO(\lvert\FSLP\rvert)
. This is a standard assumption in the area of algorithms for compressed data
(see for instance also
[
11
,
37
]
).
With Lemma
4.1
it allows to store for every vertex
A\in V
and every vertex
v
of
\llbracket A\rrbracket_{\FSLP}

the preorder number of
v
in a single register.

We introduced f-SLPs without a distinguished root vertex
S
. In the literature such a root vertex
S
is often added to
an f-SLP
\FSLP
and one defines
\llbracket\FSLP\rrbracket=\llbracket S\rrbracket_{\FSLP}
.
Let us call such an
\FSLP
with a distinguished root vertex a
rooted f-SLP
.
Rooted f-SLPs will only appear in
Section 7.3
, all other f-SLPs in this paper are unrooted.

4.4
Computing preorder numbers

Recall that for a forest vertex
A
of an f-SLP
\FSLP
the vertices of the forest
\llbracket A\rrbracket_{\FSLP}
correspond to
A
-to-leaf paths in
\FSLP
(i.e., leaves of
\mathsf{unfold}_{\FSLP}(A)
). Hence, to every such path
\pi
we assign the preorder number
\mathsf{po}(\pi)
of the corresponding vertex
of
\llbracket A\rrbracket_{\FSLP}
.
In the enumeration produced by our algorithm for
Theorem 1.1
, every vertex of
\llbracket A\rrbracket_{\FSLP}

will be represented by its preorder number (see
Theorem 6.1
for a more precise version of

Theorem 1.1
). Hence, it is crucial that, after some preprocessing, we can compute
\mathsf{po}(\pi)
along the path
\pi
. In this section, we shall discuss the details of this technique, which, to the best knowledge of the authors, has not already been reported in the literature on f-SLPs.

For the further consideration we fix a forest algebra expression
\phi\in\mathcal{E}_{0}(\Sigma)
.
Recall that the vertices of
\llbracket\phi\rrbracket
are the leaves of
\phi
and that for a leaf
v
of
\phi
,

\mathsf{po}(v)
denotes the preorder number of the corresponding node of
\llbracket\phi\rrbracket
. These are the blue numbers in
Figure 5
.
When moving from the root
r
of
\phi
down to the leaf
v
we can compute the preorder number
\mathsf{po}(v)
.
To see this, consider the path from the root
r
of the expression tree
\phi
to the leaf
v
and let
u
be the current vertex.
Recall that
\phi(u)
is the subtree rooted in
u
. In accordance with our notation for f-SLPs we
write
\llbracket u\rrbracket\in\mathsf{F}(\Sigma)\cup\mathsf{F}_{\ast}(\Sigma)
for
\llbracket\phi(u)\rrbracket
and
\tau(u)\in\{0,1\}
for
\tau(\phi(u))
(the type of
u
).
In order to compute
\mathsf{po}(v)
we have to compute for every
u
on the path from
r
to
v

the so-called
preorder data

{\rm Phys.~Rev.~E}(u)
. It is a single natural number in case
\tau(u)=0

and it is a pair of natural numbers in
case
\tau(u)=1
.
The meaning of these numbers is the following, where we identify
\llbracket u\rrbracket
with the set of vertices of
\llbracket\phi\rrbracket

that belong to the subforest (resp., subcontext)
\llbracket u\rrbracket
. If
{\rm Phys.~Rev.~E}(u)=x\in\mathbb{N}
(resp.,

{\rm Phys.~Rev.~E}(u)=(x,y)\in\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}
) then

•

x
is the smallest preorder number of the vertices in
\llbracket u\rrbracket
and

•

y
(which only exists if
\tau(u)=1
)
is the size of the subforest of
\llbracket\phi\rrbracket
that replaces the special symbol
\ast
in the subcontext
\llbracket u\rrbracket
.

For a leaf
v
of
\phi
with
\tau(v)=0

we have
{\rm Phys.~Rev.~E}(v)=\mathsf{po}(v)
and for a leaf
v
with
\tau(v)=1

the first component of
{\rm Phys.~Rev.~E}(v)
is
\mathsf{po}(v)
.

In order to compute the preorder data for every vertex of
\phi
, we first have to compute the
leaf size

s(u)
and the
left size

\ell(u)
for every vertex
u
of
\phi
:

•

s(u)
is the number of leaves in the subexpression rooted in
u

(where a leaf labelled with
a_{\ast}
also counts as one leaf).
This is the same as the number of vertices of
\llbracket u\rrbracket
without the unique
\ast
-labelled vertex
in case
\tau(u)=1
.

•

\ell(u)
is only defined if
\tau(u)=1
and is the number of vertices of
\llbracket u\rrbracket
that are in preorder
smaller than the unique occurrence of the special symbol
\ast
in
\llbracket u\rrbracket
. Thus, it is the preorder number of
\ast

in
\llbracket u\rrbracket
.

The leaf sizes are computed bottom-up as follows: if
u
is a leaf of
\phi
then
s(u)=1
and if

u
has the left (resp., right) child
v_{1}
(resp.,
v_{2}
) then
s(u)=s(v_{1})+s(v_{2})
.

The left size for a leaf
u
of
\phi
with
\tau(u)=1
(i.e.,
u
is labelled with a symbol
a_{\ast}
) is
\ell(u)=1
.
Now assume that
u
has the left (resp., right) child
v_{1}
(resp.,
v_{2}
) and that
u
is labelled with the operator

\mathbin{\varodot}\in\{\mathbin{\varominus},\mathbin{\varobar}\}
:

•

Case
\mathbin{\varodot}=\mathbin{\varominus}
,
\tau(v_{1})=0
, and
\tau(v_{2})=1
:

\ell(u)=s(v_{1})+\ell(v_{2})

•

Case
\mathbin{\varodot}=\mathbin{\varominus}
,
\tau(v_{1})=1
, and
\tau(v_{2})=0
:

\ell(u)=\ell(v_{1})

•

Case
\mathbin{\varodot}=\mathbin{\varobar}
, and
\tau(v_{1})=\tau(v_{2})=1
:

\ell(u)=\ell(v_{1})+\ell(v_{2})

Finally, the preorder data are computed top-down as follows: For the root vertex
r
of
\phi
we set
{\rm Phys.~Rev.~E}(r)=0
(recall that
\tau(\phi)=0
).
Now assume that
u
is an internal vertex of
\phi
with left (resp., right) child
v_{1}
(resp.,
v_{2}
).
Moreover, let
u
be labelled with the operator
\mathbin{\varodot}\in\{\mathbin{\varominus},\mathbin{\varobar}\}
and let

{\rm Phys.~Rev.~E}(u)=x
in case
\tau(u)=0
and

{\rm Phys.~Rev.~E}(u)=(x,y)
in case
\tau(u)=1
:

Case (1)

\mathbin{\varodot}=\mathbin{\varominus}
, and

5.1
Monadic second order logic

We consider formulas of
monadic second order logic
(MSO)
[
24
]
that are interpreted over forests
F=(V,E,R,\lambda)
as described in
subsubsection 2.3.1
.
Since any first-order variable
x
(that takes elements from the universe as values) can be replaced by a set variable
X
(an MSO-formula can express that
X
is a singleton set), we can restrict to MSO-formulas where all free variables are set variables.

If
\Psi
is an MSO-formula over the signature of unranked forests with free set variables
X_{1},\ldots,X_{k}
(written as
\Psi(X_{1},\ldots,X_{k})
) and
S_{1},\ldots,S_{k}\subseteq V
are vertex sets of some forest
F=(V,E,R,\lambda)
, then we write
(F,S_{1},\ldots,S_{k})\models\Psi
if the formula
\Psi
holds in the forest
F
if the variable
X_{i}
is set to
S_{i}
. Hence, we can interpret MSO-formulas
\Psi(X_{1},\ldots,X_{k})
as
MSO-queries
that, for a given forest
F
, define a
result set

\Psi[F]=\{(S_{1},\ldots,S_{k}):(F,S_{1},\ldots,S_{k})\models\Psi\}
.

To make the exposition less technical, we further restrict to MSO-formulas with a single free set variable
X
. This is a common restriction that can be found elsewhere in the literature (see, e.g.,
[
5
,
32
]
) and it is without loss of generality. For the sake of a self-contained exposition, we will show next how to reduce the number of set variables in MSO-formulas to one.

Consider an MSO-formula
\Psi(X_{1},\ldots,X_{k})
with
k\geq 2
and let
F=(V,E,R,\lambda)
be a forest with vertex labels from
\Sigma
.
We then take the new set of vertex labels
\Sigma^{\prime}=\Sigma\times[k]
and define the new forest
F^{\prime}=(V^{\prime},E^{\prime},R^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime})
with

•

V^{\prime}=V\times[k]
,

•

E^{\prime}=\{((u,k),(v,i)):(u,v)\in E,i\in[k]\}
,

•

R^{\prime}=\{((u,i),(u,i+1)):u\in V,i\in[k-1]\}\cup\{((u,k),(v,1)):(u,v)\in R\}
, and

•

\lambda^{\prime}(u,i)=(\lambda(u),i)
.

So, intuitively, we add to each vertex
k-1
siblings on the left; the original vertex
u
corresponds to the vertex
(u,k)
.

It is then straightforward to translate
\Psi(X_{1},\ldots,X_{k})
into an MSO-formula
\Psi^{\prime}(X)
with a single free set variable such that
for all subsets
S_{1},\ldots,S_{k}\subseteq V
we have

(F,S_{1},\ldots,S_{k})\models\Psi
if and only if
(F^{\prime},S^{\prime})\models\Psi^{\prime}
, where

S^{\prime}=\{(v,i):v\in S_{i},i\in[k]\}
.
Hence, an algorithm that enumerates the set

\Psi^{\prime}[F^{\prime}]
with output-linear delay directly yields an algorithm that
enumerates the set
\Psi[F]
with output-linear delay. This is also true if we insist that forest vertices are represented by their preorder numbers:
If the algorithm for enumerating
\Psi^{\prime}[F^{\prime}]
outputs the set
S^{\prime}
, where
every
m\in S^{\prime}
is a preorder number in
F^{\prime}
, then the algorithm for enumerating
\Psi[F]
outputs
the tuple
(S_{0},\ldots,S_{k-1})
, where
S_{i}=\{\lfloor m/k\rfloor:m\in S^{\prime},m\bmod k=i\}
.

As mentioned before, our task is to enumerate the result set of an MSO-query on an f-SLP-compressed forest.
Consequently, we have to explain how the above reduction to the one-variable case can be done on the level of f-SLPs.
For this we will add additional vertices to an f-SLP
\FSLP=(V,E,\lambda)
such that the resulting f-SLP
\FSLP^{\prime}
has the following property
for every forest vertex
A\in V
:
\llbracket A\rrbracket_{\FSLP^{\prime}}
is the forest obtained by applying the above transformation
F\mapsto F^{\prime}

to
F=\llbracket A\rrbracket_{\FSLP}
. Moreover, the size of
\FSLP^{\prime}
should be linear in the size of
\FSLP
. This, however, is easy:
We can assume that in
\FSLP
there is for every
a\in\Sigma
exactly one vertex

A_{a}
labelled with
a
and exactly one vertex
A_{a_{\ast}}
labelled with
a_{\ast}
. We obtain
\FSLP^{\prime}

by adding in total
\bigO(|\Sigma|\cdot k)
many new vertices such that
\llbracket A_{a}\rrbracket_{\FSLP^{\prime}}=(a,1)(a,2)\cdots(a,k)

and
\llbracket A_{a_{\ast}}\rrbracket_{\FSLP^{\prime}}=(a,1)\cdots(a,k-1)(a,k)_{\ast}
.
This yields an f-SLP
\FSLP^{\prime}
of size
\bigO(\lvert\FSLP\rvert)
with the above property.

5.2
Tree automata

We consider two types of tree automata: deterministic bottom-up tree automata that work on binary trees and nondeterministic
stepwise tree automata that work on general unranked forests. Since they should implement queries on trees and forests, they will be interpreted as selecting vertices from trees or forests (this aspect is explained in more detail later on).

5.2.1
Deterministic bottom-up tree automata

A
deterministic bottom-up tree automaton
(over the alphabets
\Sigma_{0}
and
\Sigma_{2}
) is a 6-tuple
\mathcal{B}=(Q,\Sigma_{0},\Sigma_{2},\delta_{0},\delta_{2},Q_{f})
, where
Q
is a finite set of states,
\Sigma_{0}
is the set of leaf vertex labels,
\Sigma_{2}
is the set of labels for internal vertices,
Q_{f}\subseteq Q
is the set of final states,
\delta_{0}:\Sigma_{0}\to Q
assigns states to leaves of a tree, and
\delta_{2}:Q\times Q\times\Sigma_{2}\to Q
assigns states to internal vertices depending on the vertex label and the states of the two children. For a given binary tree
T
we define the state
\mathcal{B}(T)
as the unique state to which
\mathcal{B}
evaluates the tree
T
. It is inductively defined as follows, where
a\in\Sigma_{0}
and
f\in\Sigma_{2}
:

•

\mathcal{B}(a)=\delta_{0}(a)
and

•

\mathcal{B}(f(T_{1},T_{2}))=\delta_{2}(\mathcal{B}(T_{1}),\mathcal{B}(T_{2}),f)
for binary trees
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
.

The binary tree
T
is accepted by
\mathcal{B}
if and only if
\mathcal{B}(T)\in Q_{f}
. With
L(\mathcal{B})
we denote the set of binary trees accepted by
\mathcal{B}
. We use the acronym dBUTA for deterministic bottom-up tree automaton.

As an example, consider a dBUTA
\mathcal{B}_{\tau}
with
\Sigma_{0}=\{a,a_{\ast}:a\in\Sigma\}
,
\Sigma_{2}=\{\mathbin{\varominus},\mathbin{\varobar}\}
,
Q=\{0,1,\mathsf{failure}\}
,
Q_{f}=\{0,1\}
and the transition functions
\delta_{0}
and
\delta_{2}
are defined as follows:

\displaystyle\delta_{0}(a)=0,\delta_{0}(a_{\ast})=1

\displaystyle\delta_{2}(0,0,\mathbin{\varominus})=0,\delta_{2}(0,1,\mathbin{\varominus})=\delta_{2}(1,0,\mathbin{\varominus})=1,\delta_{2}(1,1,\mathbin{\varominus})=\mathsf{failure}

\displaystyle\delta_{2}(1,0,\mathbin{\varobar})=0,\delta_{2}(1,1,\mathbin{\varobar})=1,\delta_{2}(0,0,\mathbin{\varobar})=\delta_{2}(0,1,\mathbin{\varobar})=\mathsf{failure}

\displaystyle\delta_{2}(p,q,\mathbin{\varobar})=\mathsf{failure}\text{ if }p=\mathsf{failure}\text{ or }q=\mathsf{failure}

Obviously,
\mathcal{B}_{\tau}
accepts exactly the set of valid forest algebra expressions.

5.2.2

Stepwise tree automata are an automaton model for forests that is equivalent to MSO-logic
[
18
]
.

We follow the definition from

A

Let

is the left-most root and

(called

in

(called

in

) such that the following conditions hold (an intuitive explanation follows below):

•

\rho_{0}(v_{1})=q_{1}
,

•

,

•

\rho_{0}(v)\in\iota(\lambda(u))

•

•

A forest

if its has a

-run. With

we denote the set of forests accepted by

Let us explain this model on an intuitive level. To this end, we first observe that all the triples

from

over the alphabet

means that we can change from

to

. Now an nSTA processes an unranked tree by treating each sibling-sequence as a string that is then processed by this string automaton

be the children (ordered from left to right) of some vertex

is a leaf, or it is propagated from

via the condition
\rho_{1}(v_{i})=\rho_{f}(w_{i})
. Now we read the string
\rho_{1}(v_{1})\rho_{2}(v_{2})\ldots\rho_{k}(v_{k})
with

as follows. We start in some state
\rho_{0}(v_{1})\in\iota(\lambda(u))
(recall that
u
is
v_{1}
’s parent). Then reading
\rho_{1}(v_{1})
changes the state from
\rho_{0}(v_{1})
to
\rho_{f}(v_{1})
(

changes the state from
\rho_{0}(v_{2})

\rho_{f}(v_{2})

changes the state from
\rho_{0}(v_{3})
to
\rho_{f}(v_{3})
(

) and so on until we reach the state
\rho_{f}(v_{k})
, which then serves as the state associated to the parent vertex

, i.e.,
\rho_{f}(v_{k})=\rho_{1}(u)

Consequently, a computation of an nSTA can be seen as a preorder traversal of the trees of the forest: We read a sequence of siblings as described above until we reach a sibling
v
that is not a leaf. Then we go down one step and process
v

\rho_{1}(\cdot)
-state of its parent vertex

and we can therefore continue reading the sequence of siblings that contains

and so on until we end up with the
\rho_{f}(\cdot)
-state of the root of the rightmost tree of the forest.

In this work, stepwise tree automata will only serve as an intermediate model that, in the process of our algorithm, will be transformed into a deterministic bottom-up tree automaton. Hence, we will not further discuss the model of nSTA and refer the reader to
[
MMN22
]
for further details and explanations.

5.2.3
Representing MSO-queries by tree automata

Nondeterministic stepwise tree automata can represent queries on forests as follows.
For a forest
F
and a subset

of its vertices, we identify the pair
(F,S)
with the forest that is obtained from

by relabelling every

represents the forest

from which the vertices in

Our nSTAs become vertex-selecting, by taking

as the set of vertex labels. Such an nSTA

selects the vertex set

if and only if

(F,S)\in L(\mathcal{A})
.

Our dBUTAs only need the ability to select leaves of binary trees, which means that we define them over the alphabets
\Sigma_{0}\times\{0,1\}
(for leaf vertices) and
\Sigma_{2}
(for internal vertices), i.e., we run them on pairs

, where

In the following, we assume that all nSTAs and dBUTAs are vertex-selecting in the above sense.
For the forest

and an nSTA
\mathcal{A}

we write

\mathsf{select}(\mathcal{A},F)=\{S\subseteq V:(F,S)\in L(\mathcal{A})\}

Similarly, for a binary tree

we define

\mathsf{select}(\mathcal{B},T)=\{S\subseteq\mathsf{leaves}(T):(T,S)\in L(\mathcal{B})\}.

It is known that MSO-formulas (that are interpreted over forests) can be translated into equivalent automata (and vice versa).
More precisely, we use the following well-known fact:

(cf.
[

]
)

From an MSO-formula
\Psi(X)

such that for every forest

Our main goal is to enumerate the set of query results

By

We will therefore ignore MSO-logic in the following and directly start from an nSTA.

witness tree for

. For this, it suffices to enumerate witness trees themselves.
Thereby, every witness tree

\bigO(|W|)=\bigO(|S(W)|)
(see Lemma

).
To this end, we define a kind of lexicographical order on witness trees. For this, we have to fix some linear orders:
For every configuration
(v,q)\in\mathsf{Conf}^{a}(T)
we fix and precompute an arbitrary linear order on
\mathsf{succ}_{T}^{u}(v,q)
.
Moreover, for every configuration
(v,q)\in\mathsf{Conf}^{u}(T)
we additionally fix and precompute an arbitrary linear order on
\mathsf{succ}_{T}^{a}(v,q)
.

Consider next a witness tree
W
and a node
(v,q)
of
W
. We say that
(v,q)
is a maximal node if one of the following three cases holds:

•

(v,q)
is a leaf of
W
.

•

(v,q)
is a unary node whose unique child is the largest
(v^{\prime},q^{\prime})\in\mathsf{succ}_{T}^{u}(v,q)
.

•

(v,q)
is a binary node with left (resp., right) child
(v_{1},q_{1})
(resp.,
(v_{1},q_{2})
) and
(v_{1},q_{1},v_{2},q_{2})
is the largest
4-tuple in
\mathsf{succ}_{T}^{a}(v,q)
.

If in the second (resp., third) point we take the smallest
(v^{\prime},q^{\prime})\in\mathsf{succ}_{T}^{u}(v,q)
(resp., the smallest
(v_{1},q_{1},v_{2},q_{2})\in\mathsf{succ}_{T}^{a}(v,q)
),
then we speak of a minimal node. Leaves of a witness tree are maximal as well as minimal.

We say that the witness tree
W
is maximal (resp., minimal) if all nodes of
W
are maximal (resp., minimal).
Intuitively, this means that we construct the witness tree according to Definition
6.5
, but at each
extension step (where the children of a node are defined) we take the largest (resp., smallest) available choice.
Clearly, there is a unique maximal (resp., minimal) witness tree for every
(v,q)\in\mathsf{Conf}^{a}(T)
.

The enumeration algorithm for
\mathcal{S}^{a}(r,q_{f})
starts with producing the unique minimal witness tree
W_{0}
for
(r,q_{f})
in time
\bigO(|W_{0}|)
.
For a single enumeration step, assume that
W
is the previously produced witness tree for
(r,q_{f})
. If
W
is maximal, then the enumeration moves on to producing the witness trees for
(r,q^{\prime}_{f})
, where
q^{\prime}_{f}
is the next final state, or if
q_{f}
was the last final state, the algorithm stops and prints
EOE
.
Otherwise, we produce the lexicographically
next witness tree
W^{\prime}
as follows: Let
w_{1},w_{2},\ldots,w_{n}
be the set of nodes of
W
listed in preorder (left-to-right depth-first order).
8
8

8

Every order on the nodes of the witness tree would be suitable, as long as (i) one can traverse the nodes in the chosen order in constant time per node and
(ii) the parent node of a node
w
comes before
w
.
We can assume that this list was produced in the previous enumeration step in time

\bigO(|W|)
.
Let

be the last non-maximal node in the list, i.e.,
w_{i+1},\ldots,w_{n}

Also

. Note that the parent node of

We then extend the partial witness tree at node

if
(v,q)

(v^{\prime},q^{\prime})
in

to
the configuration that comes after

.
Now assume that
(v,q)
is binary and let
(v_{1},q_{1})
(resp.,
(v_{2},q_{2})

(v,q)

in

(v_{1},q^{\prime}_{1},v_{2},q^{\prime}_{2})

in

In the last step, it remains to complete the partial witness tree

below leaves

of

such that

During this extension we follow the recursive definition of witness trees (Definition

)
but always choose the smallest element from

(resp.,

) if

is the leaf where we currently extend the partial witness tree.
Since we spend constant time for each node of the final witness tree

6.2
Extending Bagan’s algorithm to DAG-foldings of binary trees

In this section we prove

.
For this, we have to take care of the setting where the input binary tree

\mathcal{C}
-decorated binary DAG

10
10

10

The first extension step in the witness tree construction, where one has to choose a vertex

By the previous discussion, the enumeration of witness trees for

7
Dealing with Updates

We have seen that even if unranked forests are highly compressed by f-SLPs, we can still enumerate the result set of an MSO-query with output-linear delay and after linear preprocessing. A natural question is whether our approach can be extended to the dynamic setting, i.e., after updating our data, we want to enumerate the query result again but with respect to the updated data and without having to repeat the whole preprocessing from scratch. Solving this task aims at two objectives: On the one hand, we have to be able to perform the update directly on the compressed data (clearly, we do not want to decompress our data, update it and then compress it again) and, on the other hand, our updates have to maintain the data structures that are necessary for running the enumeration algorithm.

7.1
The enumeration data structure

As mentioned above, in order to perform an update for an f-SLP-compressed forest, we do not only have to update the f-SLP accordingly, but we also have to update the data structures that are needed for our linear preprocessing and output-linear delay enumeration algorithm. Let us formally define these data structures.

First, let us recall that as a component of the enumeration algorithm of
Theorem 6.1
, we had to devise an algorithm for a certain enumeration problem on DAGs in
Section 3
. As a preprocessing of this algorithm, we first applied several simplification steps that eventually produced a binary version of the input DAG. In the following, we use the notation

for the binary

\mathcal{D}
by applying the construction from
Section 3
(recall that in the course of the overall algorithm from

, the decorated DAG

on which we applied this construction is the DAG
\FSLP\otimes\,\mathcal{B}
for an f-SLP
\FSLP
and a dBUTA

).

Let
\FSLP
be an f-SLP (viewed as a

-decorated DAG),

be an nSTA and

be the corresponding dBUTA from

The

for

7.3

We now argue that our enumeration algorithm can be easily extended with relabelling updates, i.e., updates

Formally, we define for a forest

.

7.4
Beyond relabelling updates

While relabelling a single vertex of an unranked forest is a natural update operation, it is also quite simple. In particular, it does not change the overall structure of the forest.

More complex tree updates were considered in
[
MMN22
]
in the context of query enumeration on uncompressed trees. In
[
MMN22
]
, the authors consider in addition to relabeling updates also certain insertion and deletion updates, where vertices are inserted (resp., deleted) at certain specified positions in the current forest. The authors in
[
MMN22
]
achieve time
\bigO(\log|F|)
for these updates. For this, they represent the input forest
F
by a forest algebra expression
\phi
of height
\bigO(\log|F|)
.

It is tempting to extend the approach from
[
MMN22
]
to an f-SLP
\FSLP
, i.e., to a forest algebra expression
\phi
that is represented by a DAG. We conjecture that this might be possible, but the technical difficulties are considerable. The main idea of
[
MMN22
]
is to maintain a certain balancing property of the forest algebra expression
\phi
that describes the tree
T
. With this balancing property, the update time depends on the height of
\phi
, which is logarithmic in the size of
T
. The approach for keeping
\phi
balanced is to perform certain rotations similar to the classical ones that are used for balanced search trees like AVL-trees. However, in order to do such rotations with respect to the syntax tree of an algebraic expression without changing the evaluation of the expression, one needs associativity of the respective algebra operations. For the two binary forest algebra operations, this is not the case:
F_{1}\mathbin{\varobar}(F_{2}\mathbin{\varominus}F_{3})
is in general not the same as
(F_{1}\mathbin{\varobar}F_{2})\mathbin{\varominus}F_{3}
. In
[
MMN22
]
the authors found a quite technical workaround for this problem in the uncompressed setting. It is not obvious that the same workaround can be also used in the situation, where the forest algebra expression is represented by a DAG.

8
More Background on Straight-Line Programs

Since this is the central concept of our work, let us provide more background on straight-line programs. A rather important motivation for our work is that compressing a given string or forest by an SLP is a problem that can be solved rather well in a practical context (i.e., we can compute SLPs with excellent compression ratios in linear time). Due to the relevance of this aspect, we shall discuss it in more detail in
Section 8.1
.

String SLPs (s-SLPs) date back several decades; see, e.g.,
[
60
,
72
]
. Nowadays, they are very popular and play a prominent role in the context of string algorithms and other areas of theoretical computer science. They are mathematically easy to handle and therefore very appealing for theoretical considerations. Independent of their applications in data compression, they have been used in many different contexts as a natural tool for representing (and reasoning about) hierarchical structure in sequential data; see, e.g.,
[
46
,
51
,
49
,
61
,
60
,
72
]
.
Good sources for further details on s-SLPs are the survey
[
49
]
, the PhD-thesis
[
21
]
and the comprehensive introductions of the papers
[
1
,
19
]
.

String SLPs are also of high practical relevance, mainly because many practically applied dictionary-based compression schemes (e.g., run-length encoding, and – most notably – the various Lempel-Ziv variants LZ77, LZ78, LZW, etc. which are relevant for practical tools like the built-in Unix utility
compress
or data formats like GIF, PNG, PDF and some ZIP archive file formats) can be converted efficiently into s-SLPs of similar size, i.e., with size blow-ups by only moderate constants or log-factors (see
[
1
,
21
,
41
,
49
,
65
]
). Hence, algorithms for SLP-compressed strings carry over to these practical formats.

A possible drawback of s-SLPs is that computing a minimal size s-SLP for a given string is intractable (even for fixed alphabets)
[
19
,
20
]
. However, this has never been an issue for the application of s-SLPs, since many
heuristical SLP-compressors achieve very good compression rates for practical inputs. This aspect will be discussed in more detail and tailored to our results in
Section 8.1
.

While in the early days of computer science fast compression and decompression was an important factor, it is nowadays common to also rate compression schemes according to how suitable they are for solving problems directly on the compressed data without prior decompression, a paradigm that is known as algorithmics on compressed strings. In this regard, s-SLPs have very good properties: Many basic problems on strings like comparison, pattern matching, membership in a regular language, retrieving subwords, etc. can all be efficiently solved
directly on s-SLPs
[
49
]
.

String SLPs are usually defined in terms of context-free grammars, i.e., an s-SLP for a word
w
is a context-free grammar in Chomsky normal form for the language
\{w\}
. It is straightforward to see that our definition from
Section 4.2
is equivalent:
We can interpret every vertex
A
of the DAG that represents the s-SLP as a non-terminal symbol with a context-free rule
A\to BC
, if it is an inner vertex with left edge
(A,\ell,B)
and right edge
(A,r,C))
, or with a context-free rule
A\to a
if it is an
a
-labelled leaf.
This context-free grammar based definition has the advantage that it can be easily extended to trees, by simply using a context-free grammar formalism for trees, which leads to tree SLPs
[
50
,
54
,
56
]
. Context-free tree grammars have rules of the form

A\to T
, where
A
labels a vertex
v
with children
u_{1},u_{2},\ldots,u_{k}
and
T
is a tree that, among others, has distinguished leaves
x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{k}
. The idea is that
v
is replaced by
T
in such a way that the subtree rooted in
u_{i}
is plugged in at the position of the leaf
x_{i}
of
T
. However, for such a formalism
A
-labelled vertices must have a fixed rank
k
. Thus, such tree SLPs can only compress ranked trees.
This is a disadvantage, since in the context of database theory, we are rather interested in unranked trees and forests as data model. A typical example of such data are XML tree structures or decision trees. Therefore, we use forest SLPs, which were introduced in
[
38
]
in a more grammar-like way that is nevertheless equivalent to our approach in
Section 4.3
.

These f-SLPs have many desirable properties, which make them a suitable compression scheme for our setting. Most importantly, they can compress vertex-labelled unranked forests, which cover a rather large class of tree structures (e.g., XML tree structures, decision trees, tree decompositions); in particular, they are not limited to ranked trees, which would be too restrictive for typical applications in data management. At the same time, f-SLPs share most of the desirable properties of s-SLPs, e.g., they are mathematically easy to handle and can achieve exponential compression rates. Moreover, f-SLPs are robust in the sense that they also cover other popular tree compression schemes like top dags
[
10
,
27
,
42
]
and tree straight-line programs
[
36
,
52
]
.

It is also possible to compute small f-SLPs for given forests in acceptable running times, mainly because compression techniques for s-SLPs can be adapted to the case of trees and forests.
12
12

12

Observe that the general intractability of computing a
smallest
f-SLP obviously carries over from the string case. However, just like in the string case, this is not an obstacle for practically relevant approximations and heuristics.
Since this aspect is very important for our results, we will discuss it in more detail in
Section 8.1
.

It is also known that for every forest with
n
vertices and
k
different vertex labels,
one can construct in linear time an f-SLP of size
\bigO(n\log k/\log n)
(so
\bigO(n/\log n)
for a fixed
k
)
[
36
]
. Finally, a recent balancing result for s-SLPs
[
37
]
also holds for f-SLPs, which we applied in the context of updates (see
Theorem 7.5
).

8.1
Practical algorithms for SLP-compression of strings and forests

Our whole work hinges on the assumption that we get our input data in SLP-compressed form, and that these SLPs are substantially smaller than the uncompressed data. Let us discuss now in a bit more detail why this assumption is justified for both strings and forests.

The problem of computing a smallest s-SLP for a given string
S
cannot be solved in polynomial time unless P=NP

[
19
,
20
]
. An algorithm for computing a smallest s-SLP that runs in time
\bigO(3^{\lvert S\rvert})
is presented in
[
19
]
. However, there is a large number of algorithms that compute small s-SLPs in linear time or low-degree polynomial time. For example, there exist several algorithms that compute for a given string
S
of length
n
in time
\bigO(n)
an s-SLP
of size
\bigO(g\cdot\log n)
, where
g
is the size of a smallest s-SLP for
S

[
20
,
43
,
65
]
.

\bigO(\log n)
is currently the best known approximation ratio of polynomial time grammar-based compressors.
Upper and lower bounds for the approximation ratios of several practical grammar-based compressors are studied in

[
7
,
20
]
. Some of these compressors (in particular RePair
[
47
]
) are known to perform very well in practical scenarios. Finally, it is also known that strings represented by many practical compression schemes (e.g., run length encoding, LZ77 and LZ78 encoding) can be transformed into s-SLPs with moderate size increase
[
41
]
. In summary, the problem of compressing a string by an s-SLP is theoretically well-understood and a rich toolbox of practical methods exists.

While the state-of-the-art for f-SLPs is not as developed as for s-SLPs, we can observe that compression by f-SLP can also be handled by existing algorithmic techniques. Most importantly, the above mentioned grammar-based string compressor RePair
can be adapted so that it computes an f-SLP for a given unranked forest. More precisely, the so-called TreeRePair algorithm
[
52
]
computes a tree SLP for a
ranked
tree, but it can also be used on the first-child-next-sibling encoding of an unranked forest
F
. The resulting tree SLP for the first-child-next-sibling encoding of
F
can then be transformed in linear time into an equivalent f-SLP for
F
(see
[
38
]
). This approach shows excellent compression ratios in practice, which is also demonstrated by an experimental study: For a corpus of typical XML documents, the number of edges of the original tree is reduced to approximately 3% using TreeRePair on the first-child next-sibling encoding of the XML tree
[
52
]
. Other available grammar-based tree compressors are BPLEX
[
17
]
and CluX
[
13
]
.

For maintaining relabelling updates, it is advantageous if our f-SLPs are balanced (i.e., the height is logarithmic in the size of the decompressed forest), since then the update procedure has a running time that is bounded logarithmically in the size of the data. Due to
Theorem 7.5
from
[
37
, Corollary 3.28]
, this can be achieved with a linear time preprocessing. Moreover, relabelling updates do not change the size of the decompressed forest or the height of the f-SLP, which implies that the f-SLP stays balanced after a relabelling update.

Conclusions

We remark that the special case of
Theorem 3.1
where the category
\mathcal{C}
is a groupoid (a category where
all morphisms are invertible)
can also be proven by using a known technique for the real-time traversal of SLP-compressed strings (see
[

,
54
]
). However, our category from
Section 4.4
is not a groupoid.
Another disadvantage is that the real-time traversal of SLP-compressed strings needs a tree data structure for so-called next link queries. While such data structures can be constructed in linear time, this is not straightforward and would significantly complicate an implementation of our algorithm. In general, we believe that our approach is simple to implement, which makes an experimental analysis in the vein of
[
52
]
possible.

An important open question is whether our enumeration algorithm for f-SLP-compressed unranked forests can also be extended by insertion and deletion updates in logarithmic time instead of only relabellings. We conjecture that this is indeed possible, but respective constructions will be technically rather involved.

SLPs have also been formulated for graphs (see
[
58
]
).
In a recent paper
[
55
]
we showed that the result sets of queries formulated in

of bounded degree (with a technical restriction on the graph SLPs).
This extends previous work for uncompressed graphs of bounded degree
[
28
,

]
.

For automaton-based queries on strings it has been recently shown that

[

. Moreover,

[

. Both these aspects are also worth investigating in the context of MSO-enumeration over SLP-compressed forests.
