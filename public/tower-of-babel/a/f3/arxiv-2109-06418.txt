Perfect state transfer in Grover walks between states associated to vertices of a graph
Sho Kubota, Etsuo Segawa

Abstract
We study perfect state transfer in Grover walks, which are typical discrete-time quantum walk models. In particular, we focus on states associated to vertices of a graph. We call such states vertex type states. Perfect state transfer between vertex type states can be studied via Chebyshev polynomials. We derive a necessary condition on eigenvalues of a graph for perfect state transfer between vertex type states to occur. In addition, we perfectly determine the complete multipartite graphs whose partite sets are the same size on which perfect state transfer occurs between vertex type states, together with the time. Keywords: perfect state transfer, Chebyshev polynomial, Grover walk MSC 2020 subject classifications: 05C50; 81Q99

Theorem 1.1
.

Let

See

Let
\Gamma=(V,E)

V

.

\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}(\Gamma)=\{(x,y),(y,x)\mid\{x,y\}\in E\}

2.1
Grover walks and related matrices

Let
\Gamma=(V,E)
be a graph.
We define several matrices on Grover walks.
The
boundary matrix

d=d(\Gamma)\in\mathbb{C}^{V\times\mathcal{A}}
is defined by

d_{x,a}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\deg x}}\delta_{x,t(a)}
,
where
\delta_{a,b}
is the Kronecker delta.
Note that

(2.1)

dd^{*}=I,

where
I
is the identity matrix.
The
shift matrix

S=S(\Gamma)\in\mathbb{C}^{\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{A}}

is defined by
S_{a,b}=\delta_{a,b^{-1}}
.
Clearly,

(2.2)

S^{2}=I.

Define the
time evolution matrix

U=U(\Gamma)\in\mathbb{C}^{\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{A}}

by
U=S(2d^{*}d-I)
.
Quantum walks defined by
U
is called
Grover walks
.
The
discriminant

P=P(\Gamma)\in\mathbb{C}^{V\times V}
is defined by
P=dSd^{*}
.

The discriminant is strongly related to the adjacency matrix of a graph,
and facilitates analysis of Grover walks.
The
adjacency matrix

A=A(\Gamma)\in\mathbb{C}^{V\times V}
of a graph
\Gamma=(V,E)
is defined by

A_{x,y}=\begin{cases}1\qquad&\text{$\{x,y\}\in E$,}\\
0\qquad&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Direct calculation leads to the following.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\Gamma
be a
k
-regular graph,
and let
A
and
P
be the adjacency matrix and the discriminant of
\Gamma
, respectively.
Then we have
P=\frac{1}{k}A
.
Therefore, the absolute values of eigenvalues of
P
does not exceed
1
.

We omit a proof.
A more general claim and its proof can be found
in Theorem 3.1 of
[
14
]
and Proposition 3.3 of
[
14
]
.

Since the discriminant
P
is a normal matrix,
it has a spectral decomposition.
Let
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{s}
be the distinct eigenvalues of
P
,
and let
E_{i}
be the projection matrices associated to the eigenvalue
\lambda_{i}
.
Then we have

P=\sum_{i=1}^{s}\lambda_{i}E_{i}
,
and the projection matrices satisfy

(2.3)

E_{i}E_{j}=\delta_{i,j}E_{i},

(2.4)

E_{i}^{*}=E_{i},

and

(2.5)

\sum_{i=1}^{s}E_{i}=I.

See
[
20
]
for the spectral decomposition of matrices.
In this study, we use polynomials of
P
.
The following can be obtained by using Equality (
2.3
).

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
M
be a normal matrix with the spectral decomposition
M=\sum_{i=1}^{s}\lambda_{i}E_{i}
,
and let
p(x)
be a polynomial.
Then we have
p(M)=\sum_{i=1}^{s}p(\lambda_{i})E_{i}
.

2.2
Perfect state transfer

Let
\Gamma
be a graph,
and let
U=U(\Gamma)
be the time evolution matrix.
A vector
\Phi\in\mathbb{C}^{\mathcal{A}}
is a
state
if
||\Phi||=1
.
We say that
perfect state transfer
occurs from a state
\Phi
to a state
\Psi
at time
\tau

if there exists
\gamma\in\mathbb{C}
with norm one such that
U^{\tau}\Phi=\gamma\Psi
.
As mentioned in Section 4 of
[
4
]
,
the occurrence of perfect state transfer can be restated as follows.

Lemma 2.3

(
[
4
]
)
.

Let
\Gamma
be a graph,
and let
U=U(\Gamma)
be the time evolution matrix.
Perfect state transfer occurs from a state
\Phi
to a state
\Psi
at time
\tau

if and only if
|\left\langle U^{\tau}\Phi,\Psi\right\rangle|=1
.

If no particular restriction is given to states,
we can find various perfect state transfer, including trivial cases.
For example,
if a state
\Phi
is an eigenvector of
U
associated to an eigenvalue
\lambda
,
then we have
U\Phi=\lambda\Phi
.
Mathematically, perfect state transfer certainly occurs from
\Phi
to
\Phi
at time
1
,
but it would not make sense for such a state
\Phi
.
Moreover, the state does not change.
On the other hand, perfect state transfer always occurs
when
\deg t(a)\in\{1,2\}
for some
a\in\mathcal{A}
in Grover walks.
We can find its explanation in Lemma 6.1 of
[
15
]
.
This is also perfect state transfer between different states, but it is still trivial.
From these points of view,
we should put some restrictions on states,
and the restricted states should be meaningful.
Let us consider states associated to vertices of a graph.

Let
\Gamma
be a graph with the vertex set
V
.
A state
\Phi
is said to be

if there exists a vertex

such that
\Phi=d^{*}{\bm{e}}_{x}
,
where
{\bm{e}}_{x}\in\mathbb{C}^{V}
is the unit vector defined by
({\bm{e}}_{x})_{z}=\delta_{x,z}
.
We denote by
\mathcal{X}
the set of all vertex type states
i.e.,
\mathcal{X}=\{d^{*}{\bm{e}}_{x}\mid x\in V\}
.
A vertex type state represents a state that has values only on arcs reaching toward some vertex.
Indeed,
letting

be the unit vector defined by
({\bm{e}}_{a})_{z}=\delta_{a,z}

we have

d^{*}{\bm{e}}_{x}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\deg x}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\in\mathcal{A}\\

3
Chebyshev polynomials of the first kind

In Grover walks,
perfect state transfer between vertex type states and Chebyshev polynomials are
closely related to each other.
We first introduce the polynomials.
The
Chebyshev polynomial of the first kind
, denoted by
T_{n}(x)
,
is the polynomial defined by
T_{0}(x)=1
,
T_{1}(x)=x
and

T_{n}(x)=2xT_{n-1}(x)-T_{n-2}(x)
for
n\geq 2
.
It is well-known that

(3.1)

T_{n}(\cos\theta)=\cos(n\theta).

This implies

(3.2)

|T_{n}(x)|\leq 1

for
|x|\leq 1
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\Gamma
be a graph with the time evolution matrix
U
and the discriminant
P
.
Then we have
dU^{\tau}d^{*}=T_{\tau}(P)
for
\tau\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
.

Proof.

We first claim that

(3.3)

dU^{\tau}d^{*}=dU^{\tau-1}Sd^{*}

for
\tau\geq 1
.
Indeed,

\displaystyle dU^{\tau}d^{*}

\displaystyle=dU^{\tau-1}S(2d^{*}d-I)d^{*}

\displaystyle=2dU^{\tau-1}Sd^{*}dd^{*}-dU^{\tau-1}Sd^{*}

(by (
2.1
))

\displaystyle=2dU^{\tau-1}Sd^{*}-dU^{\tau-1}Sd^{*}

\displaystyle=dU^{\tau-1}Sd^{*}.

We next show the statement by induction on
\tau
.
Equality (
2.1
) derives
dU^{0}d^{*}=I=T_{0}(P)
.
Equality (
3.3
) leads to
dUd^{*}=dSd^{*}=P=T_{1}(P)
.
Assume that the claim holds up to
\tau-1
for
\tau\geq 2
.
We have

(by (
3.3
))

\displaystyle dU^{\tau}d^{*}

\displaystyle=dU^{\tau-1}Sd^{*}

\displaystyle=dU^{\tau-2}\{S(2d^{*}d-I)\}Sd^{*}

(by (
2.2
))

\displaystyle=2dU^{\tau-2}Sd^{*}P-dU^{\tau-2}d^{*}

(by (
3.3
))

\displaystyle=2dU^{\tau-1}d^{*}P-T_{\tau-2}(P)

\displaystyle=2PT_{\tau-1}(P)-T_{\tau-2}(P)

\displaystyle=T_{\tau}(P).

The desired equality is obtained for any
\tau\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
.
∎

Let
M\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
be a normal matrix
with the spectral decomposition
M=\sum_{i=1}^{s}\lambda_{i}E_{i}
.
We denote by
\sigma(M)
the set of the distinct eigenvalues of
M
.
Remark that
\sigma(M)
is simply a set, so the multiplicities of the eigenvalues are ignored.
For a vector
x\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
, we define
\Theta_{M}(x)=\{\lambda_{i}\in\sigma(M)\mid E_{i}x\neq 0\}
.
This is called the
eigenvalue support
of the vector
x
with respect to
M
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\Gamma
be a graph with the discriminant
P
.
For a vertex
x\in V(\Gamma)
and
\tau\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
,
we have
||T_{\tau}(P){\bm{e}}_{x}||\leq 1
.
The equality holds if and only if
T_{\tau}(\lambda)=\pm 1
for any
\lambda\in\Theta_{P}({\bm{e}}_{x})
.

Proof.

Let the spectral decomposition of
P
be

P=\sum_{\lambda\in\sigma(P)}\lambda E_{\lambda}.

We note that
\sigma(P)\subset\mathbb{R}
since
P
is a symmetric matrix.
We have

\displaystyle||T_{\tau}(P){\bm{e}}_{x}||^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\langle T_{\tau}(P){\bm{e}}_{x},T_{\tau}(P){\bm{e}}_{x}\right\rangle

(by Lemma
2.2
)

\displaystyle=\left\langle\sum_{\lambda\in\sigma(P)}T_{\tau}(\lambda)E_{\lambda}{\bm{e}}_{x},\sum_{\lambda\in\sigma(P)}T_{\tau}(\lambda)E_{\lambda}{\bm{e}}_{x}\right\rangle

(by (
2.4
) and (
2.3
))

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda\in\sigma(P)}\left\langle T_{\tau}(\lambda)E_{\lambda}{\bm{e}}_{x},T_{\tau}(\lambda)E_{\lambda}{\bm{e}}_{x}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda\in\sigma(P)}T_{\tau}(\lambda)^{2}\left\langle E_{\lambda}{\bm{e}}_{x},E_{\lambda}{\bm{e}}_{x}\right\rangle

(by (
3.2
))

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\lambda\in\sigma(P)}\left\langle E_{\lambda}{\bm{e}}_{x},E_{\lambda}{\bm{e}}_{x}\right\rangle

(by (
2.4
) and (
2.3
))

\displaystyle=\left\langle{\bm{e}}_{x},\sum_{\lambda\in\sigma(P)}E_{\lambda}{\bm{e}}_{x}\right\rangle

(by (
2.5
))

\displaystyle=\left\langle{\bm{e}}_{x},{\bm{e}}_{x}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=1.

The equality holds if and only if
T_{\tau}(\lambda)^{2}\left\langle E_{\lambda}{\bm{e}}_{x},E_{\lambda}{\bm{e}}_{x}\right\rangle=\left\langle E_{\lambda}{\bm{e}}_{x},E_{\lambda}{\bm{e}}_{x}\right\rangle
for any
\lambda\in\sigma(P)
.
This is equivalent to the condition that
T_{\tau}(\lambda)=\pm 1
for any
\lambda\in\Theta_{P}({\bm{e}}_{x})

because
E_{\lambda}{\bm{e}}_{x}=0
for
\lambda\in\sigma(P)\setminus\Theta_{P}({\bm{e}}_{x})
.
∎

These two lemmas lead to a useful necessary condition for perfect state transfer to occur.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
\Gamma
be a graph with the discriminant
P
,
and let
x,y
be vertices of
\Gamma
.
If perfect state transfer occurs from
d^{*}{\bm{e}}_{x}
to
d^{*}{\bm{e}}_{y}
at time
\tau
,
then
T_{\tau}(\lambda)=\pm 1
holds for any
\lambda\in\Theta_{P}({\bm{e}}_{x})
.

Proof.

Indeed, if perfect state transfer occurs, then

(by Lemma
2.3
)

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle=\left|\left\langle U^{\tau}d^{*}{\bm{e}}_{x},d^{*}{\bm{e}}_{y}\right\rangle\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\left\langle dU^{\tau}d^{*}{\bm{e}}_{x},{\bm{e}}_{y}\right\rangle\right|

(by Lemma
3.1
)

\displaystyle=\left|\left\langle T_{\tau}(P){\bm{e}}_{x},{\bm{e}}_{y}\right\rangle\right|

\displaystyle\leq||T_{\tau}(P){\bm{e}}_{x}||\cdot||{\bm{e}}_{y}||

(by Lemma
3.2
)

\displaystyle\leq 1.

Thus, all inequalities are tight.
In particular, we have
||T_{\tau}(P){\bm{e}}_{x}||=1
.
From Lemma
3.2
,
we have
T_{\tau}(\lambda)=\pm 1
for any
\lambda\in\Theta_{P}({\bm{e}}_{x})
.
∎

The above theorem provides information on eigenvalues for perfect state transfer to occur.
In particular, when the discriminant has a rational eigenvalue,
then the eigenvalue is quite restricted.
The following are lemmas to describe this.

Lemma 3.4
.

We have

\bigcup_{\tau\in\mathbb{N}}\{x\in[-1,1]\mid T_{\tau}(x)=\pm 1\}\subset\cos\mathbb{Q}\pi.

Proof.

Take an element
x
from the left hand side.
There exists
\tau\in\mathbb{N}
such that
T_{\tau}(x)=\pm 1
.
We can put
x=\cos\theta
for some
\theta\in[0,\pi]
since
x\in[-1,1]
.
Thus we have
\cos(\tau\theta)=\pm 1
.
This is equivalent to
\theta\in\{\frac{j}{\tau}\pi\mid j\in\{0,1,\dots,\tau\}\}\subset\mathbb{Q}\pi
.
∎

A complex number
\alpha
is said to be an
algebraic integer

if there exists a monic polynomial
p(x)\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
such that
p(\alpha)=0
.
Let
\Omega
denote the set of algebraic integers.
We provide some basic facts on the algebraic integers.

Proposition 3.5

(
[
10
,
18
]
)
.

We have the following.

(i)

\Omega
is a subring of
\mathbb{C}
; and

(ii)

\Omega\cap\mathbb{Q}=\mathbb{Z}
.

Lemma 3.6
.

We have
\cos\mathbb{Q}\pi\cap\mathbb{Q}=\{\pm 1,\pm\frac{1}{2},0\}
.

Proof.

It is clear that the right hand side is included in the left hand side.
We show the reverse inclusion.
Without loss of generality,
we take
\cos\frac{2p}{q}\pi\in\cos\mathbb{Q}\pi\cap\mathbb{Q}
,
where
p,q\in\mathbb{Z}
.
Let
\zeta=e^{\frac{2p}{q}\pi i}
.
Since
\zeta^{q}=\zeta^{-q}=1
, we have
\zeta,\zeta^{-1}\in\Omega
.
By the assumption and Proposition
3.5
(i),
we have
\Omega\ni\zeta+\zeta^{-1}=2\cos\frac{2p}{q}\in\mathbb{Q}
.
Proposition
3.5
(ii) derives
2\cos\frac{2p}{q}\in\Omega\cap\mathbb{Q}=\mathbb{Z}
.
Therefore, we have
\cos\frac{2p}{q}\pi\in\{\pm 1,\pm\frac{1}{2},0\}
.
∎

For example,
we consider a situation that a rational eigenvalue
\lambda
of the discriminant
is in the eigenvalue support of some vertex type state.
If perfect state transfer occurs between vertex type states,
then the eigenvalue
\lambda
has to be
\pm 1,\pm\frac{1}{2}
or
0
.
Indeed,
Theorem
3.3
and Lemma
3.4
yield that

\mathbb{Q}\ni\lambda\in\bigcup_{\tau\in\mathbb{N}}\{x\in[-1,1]\mid T_{\tau}(x)=\pm 1\}\subset\cos\mathbb{Q}\pi,

and hence Lemma
3.6
derives

\lambda\in\cos\mathbb{Q}\pi\cap\mathbb{Q}=\{\pm 1,\pm\frac{1}{2},0\}
.

We note that a similar claim was provided in the context of periodicity of Grover walks.
In the proof of Theorem 1.2 of
[
25
]
,
Yoshie claimed that if the discriminant has a rational eigenvalue,
then it must be one of the five listed above.
We also find approaches that
rational eigenvalues restrict candidates of periodic graphs in
[
9
]
.
Considerations of quantum walks with algebraic integers were also given in
[
11
,
13
,
22
]
.

we determine perfect state transfer between vertex type states on the complete multipartite graphs.

4
Perfect state transfer on the complete multipartite graphs

Let
r,m\geq 2
and let
V_{1},V_{2},\dots,V_{r}
be sets of
m
elements
such that
V_{i}\cap V_{j}=\emptyset
for distinct
i,j\in\{1,\dots,r\}
.
Define the graph
\Gamma_{r,m}
by the vertex set

V(\Gamma_{r,m})=\bigcup_{i=1}^{r}V_{i},

where
x\in V_{i}
and
y\in V_{j}
are adjacent if and only if
i\neq j
.
The graph
\Gamma_{r,m}
is a kind of complete multipartite graph,
sometimes written as
K_{m,\dots,m}
, where
m
appears
r
times.
In other words, the graph
\Gamma_{r,m}
is the complement of
rK_{m}
.
We display as
V_{j}=\{v_{1}^{(j)},v_{2}^{(j)},\dots,v_{m}^{(j)}\}
for
j\in\{1,2,\dots,r\}
.
Note that the adjacency matrix of
\Gamma_{r,m}
is
A(\Gamma_{r,m})=A(K_{r})\otimes J_{m}
,
where
J_{m}
is the all-ones matrix of size
m
.
Since
\Gamma_{r,m}
is
m(r-1)
-regular,
we have
P(\Gamma_{r,m})=\frac{1}{m(r-1)}A(\Gamma_{r,m})=(\frac{1}{r-1}A(K_{r}))\otimes(\frac{1}{m}J_{m})
.
The spectral decompositions of
\frac{1}{r-1}A(K_{r})
and
\frac{1}{m}J_{m}
are respectively

(4.1)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{r-1}A(K_{r})

\displaystyle=1\cdot E_{1}+\left(-\frac{1}{r-1}\right)E_{2},

(4.2)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m}J_{m}

\displaystyle=1\cdot F_{1}+0\cdot F_{2},

where
E_{1}=\frac{1}{r}J_{r}
,
E_{2}=I_{r}-\frac{1}{r}J_{r}
,

F_{1}=\frac{1}{m}J_{m}
and
F_{2}=I_{m}-\frac{1}{m}J_{m}
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
P=P(\Gamma_{r,m})
.
Then we have
\Theta_{P}({\bm{e}}_{v_{i}^{(j)}})=\sigma(P)
for any vertex
v_{i}^{(j)}
in
\Gamma_{r,m}
.

Proof.

It is sufficient to show that
\Theta_{P}({\bm{e}}_{v_{i}^{(j)}})\supset\sigma(P)
.
By (
4.1
) and (
4.2
),
we have

(4.3)

P=1\cdot(E_{1}\otimes F_{1})+\left(-\frac{1}{r-1}\right)(E_{2}\otimes F_{1})+0\cdot(I\otimes F_{2})

as the spectral decomposition of
P
.
Since
{\bm{e}}_{v_{i}^{(j)}}={\bm{e}}_{j}\otimes{\bm{e}}_{i}
,
we have
(E_{1}\otimes F_{1})({\bm{e}}_{j}\otimes{\bm{e}}_{i})\neq 0
,

(E_{2}\otimes F_{1})({\bm{e}}_{j}\otimes{\bm{e}}_{i})\neq 0

and
(I\otimes F_{2})({\bm{e}}_{j}\otimes{\bm{e}}_{i})\neq 0
.
This implies the statement.
∎

First, we show that
r=2,3
if perfect state transfer occurs between vertex type states on
\Gamma_{r,m}
.

Lemma 4.2
.

If perfect state transfer occurs on
\Gamma_{r,m}

from
d^{*}{\bm{e}}_{x}
to
d^{*}{\bm{e}}_{y}
for some vertices
x,y
in
\Gamma_{r,m}
,
then
r=2,3
.

Proof.

Let
P
be the discriminant of
\Gamma_{r,m}
.
Equality (
4.3
) implies that the distinct eigenvalues of
P
are
1,0,-\frac{1}{r-1}
.
We have
\Theta_{P}({\bm{e}}_{x})=\sigma(P)
by Lemma
4.1
.
From Theorem
3.3
,
we have
T_{\tau}(-\frac{1}{r-1})=\pm 1
for some
\tau\in\mathbb{N}
.
Lemma
3.4
derives
-\frac{1}{r-1}\in\cos\mathbb{Q}\pi
,

-\frac{1}{r-1}

By Lemma
3.6

-\frac{1}{r-1}\in\{\pm 1,\pm\frac{1}{2},0\}

This implies
r=2,3
.
∎

From the above lemma,

r=2
and
r=3

Before discussing the specific cases,

Lemma 4.3
.

With the above notation, if

\left|\left\langle(I\otimes(F_{1}-F_{2})){\bm{e}}_{v_{1}^{(1)}},{\bm{e}}_{v_{i}^{(j)}}\right\rangle\right|=1,

then we have
m=2
and
v_{i}^{(j)}=v_{2}^{(1)}
.

Proof.

We note that
{\bm{e}}_{v_{i}^{(j)}}={\bm{e}}_{j}\otimes{\bm{e}}_{i}
.
From the assumption, we obtain

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle=\left|\left\langle(I\otimes(F_{1}-F_{2})){\bm{e}}_{v_{1}^{(1)}},{\bm{e}}_{v_{i}^{(j)}}\right\rangle\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\left\langle(I\otimes(F_{1}-F_{2}))({\bm{e}}_{1}\otimes{\bm{e}}_{1}),({\bm{e}}_{j}\otimes{\bm{e}}_{i})\right\rangle\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\left\langle({\bm{e}}_{1}\otimes(F_{1}-F_{2}){\bm{e}}_{1}),({\bm{e}}_{j}\otimes{\bm{e}}_{i})\right\rangle\right|

\displaystyle=\left|{\bm{e}}_{1}^{\top}{\bm{e}}_{j}\cdot((F_{1}-F_{2}){\bm{e}}_{1})^{\top}{\bm{e}}_{i}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\delta_{1,j}\left(\frac{2}{m}-\delta_{1,i}\right)\right|.

The above equality implies
j=1

i\neq 1

m\geq 2

\frac{2}{m}=1

m=2
.
This implies that
i=2

v_{i}^{(j)}=v_{2}^{(1)}
.
∎
