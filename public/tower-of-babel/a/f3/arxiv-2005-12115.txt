Must a primitive non-deficient number always have a small component?
Joshua Zelinsky

Abstract
Let n n be a primitive non-deficient number where n = p 1 a 1 p 2 a 2 ⋯ p k a k n=p_{1}^{a_{1}}p_{2}^{a_{2}}\cdots p_{k}^{a_{k}} where p 1 , p 2 ⋯ p k p_{1},p_{2}\cdots p_{k} are distinct primes. We prove that there exists an i i such that p i a i + 1 < 2 k ( p 1 p 2 p 3 ⋯ p k ) . p_{i}^{a_{i}+1}<2k(p_{1}p_{2}p_{3}\cdots p_{k}). We conjecture that in fact one can always find an i i such that p i a i + 1 < p 1 p 2 p 3 ⋯ p k {p_{i}}^{a_{i}+1}<p_{1}p_{2}p_{3}\cdots p_{k} .



Let
\sigma_{d}(n)
be the sum of the
d
th powers of divisors of
n
. We will write
\sigma(n)
for
\sigma_{1}(n)
.
Given a positive integer
n
, we will write
h(n)=\sigma_{-1}(n)=\frac{\sigma(n)}{n}
. We write

H(n)=\prod_{p|n,\mathrm{p\,\,prime}}\frac{p}{p-1}.

In what follows, we will write
n=p_{1}^{a_{1}}p_{2}^{a_{2}}\cdots p_{k}^{a_{k}}
where
p_{1},p_{2}\cdots p_{k}
are distinct primes and the
a_{i}
are positive integers. We will write
\mathrm{rad}(n)
to be the radical of

\mathrm{rad}(n)=p_{1}p_{2}\cdots p_{k}
. We will set

Recall, a number
n
is said to be deficient if
\sigma(n)<2n
. Deficient numbers form

. If
n
is non-deficient, then neither is
kn
for any
k
. Thus, there is an interest in non-deficient numbers which are primitive in the sense that such a number is non-deficient and also has no non-deficient divisors. Primitive non-deficient numbers are sequence
A006039

[
4
]
who proved that for any
k
there are only finitely many odd primitive non-deficient numbers. An asymptotic for the number of primitive non-deficient numbers which are at most
x
is still open. The best bound known bounds are due to Avidon
[
1
]
. In a different direction, the non-deficient numbers have a positive density and there are estimates of their natural density. Currently, the best lower bound is due to Kobayashi
[
6
]
, who showed that their natural density is at least
0.24760444
, while Deléglise
[
3
]
obtained an upper bound of
0.248.

We strongly suspect that

Conjecture 1
.

If
n
is a primitive non-deficient number with
n=p_{1}^{a_{1}}p_{2}^{a_{2}}\cdots p_{k}^{a_{k}}
, then there exists an
i
,

such that

.

We will prove the following weaker result:

Theorem 2
.

Let
n
be a primitive non-deficient number with
n=p_{1}^{a_{1}}p_{2}^{a_{2}}\cdots p_{k}^{a_{k}}
with
p_{1}<p_{2}\cdots<p_{k}
let
R=\mathrm{rad}(n)=p_{1}p_{2}\cdots p_{k}
. Then there is an
i
such that

p_{i}^{a_{i}+1}<2kR.

(1)

We recall the following basic fact about non-deficient numbers which has a straightforward proof.

Lemma 3
.

Let
n
be a number of the form
n=2^{a}p
where
p
is an odd prime. Then
n
is a primitive non-deficient number if and only if
2^{a}<p\leq 2^{a+1}-1
. Also, all odd numbers with exactly two distinct prime divisors are deficient.

One immediate consequence of Lemma
3
is that Theorem
1
holds whenever
n
is even and
i=1
. That is, we have an
a
such that
2^{a}||n
, and
2^{a+1}<R
.

The next Lemma is old, with a slightly weaker version due to Servais
[
8
]
. Although this Lemma often phrased just for odd perfect numbers, the Lemma applies to any non-deficient number.

Lemma 4
.

Let
n
be a non-deficient number with
n=p_{1}^{a_{1}}p_{2}^{a_{2}}\cdots p_{k}^{a_{k}}
with primes
p_{1}<p_{2}<\cdot<p_{k}
. Then
\frac{3}{2}p_{1}-2<k
.

Proof.

This is essentially just the argument from Grün
[
7
]
.
∎

Note that a stronger version of this inequality can be found in a prior paper by the author
[
9
]
but we will not need it here. We have then the following result for even primitive deficient numbers.

Proposition 5
.

Let
n
be an even primitive non-deficient number, with
R=\mathrm{rad}(n).
Let
a
be the integer such that
2^{a}||n
. Then
2^{a+1}<R
. Thus, every even primitive non-deficient number satisfies the radical inequality.

Proof.

Let
N
be an even primitive non-deficient number. Let
a
be the positive integer such that
2^{a}||N
. If
N
were a power of 2, it would be deficient, so
N
must have at least one odd prime divisor. Call that divisor
p
. From Lemma
3
, we must have
2^{a}<p
, so
2^{a+1}<2p\leq R
.
∎

Even primitive non-deficient numbers are much more common than odd primitive deficient numbers, so we can say that Conjecture
1
is at least true for most primitive non-deficient numbers. We will prove a weaker version of the conjecture but we will first need a few Lemmas.

Lemma 6
.

If
k
and
m
are real numbers such that
k\geq 2
and
0<m<\frac{1}{k(k+1)}
, then

\frac{1}{1-km}<1+(k+1)m.

(2)

Proof.

Assume
k
and
m
are real numbers satisfying the above inequalities. Note that
0<mk<\frac{1}{k+1}
, which implies that
|km|<1
, and thus we have

\frac{1}{1-km}=1+km+(km)^{2}+(km)^{3}\cdots

(3)

and so Inequality
2
is equivalent to the claim that

km+(km)^{2}+(km)^{3}\cdots<(k+1)m,

or equivalently that

\frac{k}{k+1}\left(1+km+k^{2}m^{2}+k^{3}m^{3}\cdots\right)<1.

(4)

Since
m<\frac{1}{k(k+1)},
, we have that

1+km+k^{2}m^{2}+k^{3}m^{3}\cdots<\left(1+\frac{k}{k(k+1)}+\frac{k^{2}}{k^{2}(k+1)^{2}}+\frac{k^{3}}{k^{3}(k+1)^{3}}\cdots\right).

But note that

\frac{k}{k+1}\left(1+\frac{1}{k+1}+\frac{1}{(k+1)^{2}}+\frac{1}{(k+1)^{3}}\cdots\right)=\frac{k}{k+1}\frac{1}{1-\frac{1}{k+1}}=1,

which completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 7
.

Let
n
be a positive integer, and let
p
be a prime such that
p^{a}||n
. Then

\frac{h(n)}{h(\frac{n}{p})}\leq 1+\frac{1}{p^{a}}.

Proof.

We break this into two cases, depending on whether
a=1
or
a\geq 2
. If
a=1
, since
h(n)
is multiplicative we have,

\frac{h(n)}{h(\frac{n}{p})}=h(p)=1+\frac{1}{p}.

Now, assume that
a\geq 2
. Then we have

\frac{h(n)}{h(\frac{n}{p})}=\frac{1+\frac{1}{p}\cdots\frac{1}{p^{a}}}{1+\frac{1}{p}\cdots\frac{1}{p^{a-1}}}.

Thus, we are done if we can show that

\frac{1+\frac{1}{p}\cdots\frac{1}{p^{a}}}{1+\frac{1}{p}\cdots\frac{1}{p^{a-1}}}\leq 1+\frac{1}{p^{a}}.

We have

\frac{1+\frac{1}{p}\cdots\frac{1}{p^{a}}}{1+\frac{1}{p}\cdots\frac{1}{p^{a-1}}}=\frac{1-\frac{1}{p^{a+1}}}{1-\frac{1}{p^{a}}}=\frac{p^{a}-\frac{1}{p}}{p^{a}-1}=1+\frac{1-\frac{1}{p}}{p^{a}-1},

and so our desired inequality is equivalent to the claim that

\frac{1-\frac{1}{p}}{p^{a}-1}<\frac{1}{p^{a}}.

(5)

But Inequality
5
is equivalent to

\frac{p^{a}-p^{a-1}}{p^{a}-1}<1,

which is trivially true.

∎

Lemma 8
.

Let
n
be a primitive non-deficient number with
n=p_{1}^{a_{1}}p_{2}^{a_{2}}\cdots p_{k}^{a_{k}}
with
p_{1}<p_{2}\cdots<p_{k}
. Let
R=\mathrm{rad}(n)=p_{1}p_{2}\cdots p_{k}
. Assume further that

H(n)\geq 2+\alpha

for some positive
\alpha<1
Then there exists an
i
,
1\leq i\leq k
such that

p_{i}^{a_{i}+1}<\max\{\frac{2\left(k+2+p_{1}\right)}{\alpha},k(k+1)\}.

Proof.

This proof is essentially an adaptation of part of the argument used in prior work by Heath-Brown
[
5
]
. Assume
n
is a primitive non-deficient number with
n=p_{1}^{a_{1}}p_{2}^{a_{2}}\cdots p_{k}^{a_{k}}
with
p_{1}<p_{2}\cdots<p_{k}
let
R=\mathrm{rad}(n)=p_{1}p_{2}\cdots p_{k}
. Assume that

H(n)\geq 2+\alpha

for some positive
\alpha<1
. So we have

H(n)=\prod_{i=1}^{k}\frac{p_{i}}{p_{i}-1}\geq 2+\alpha.

We also have that

h(n)=H(n)\prod_{i=1}^{k}\left(1-\frac{1}{p_{i}^{a_{i}+1}}\right)=\prod_{i=1}^{k}\left(\frac{p_{i}}{p_{i}-1}\right)\left(1-\frac{1}{p_{i}^{a_{i}+1}}\right).

But we also have

\prod_{i=1}^{k}\left(\frac{p_{i}}{p_{i}-1}\right)\left(1-\frac{1}{p_{i}^{a_{i}+1}}\right)\geq\left(\prod_{i=1}^{k}\frac{p_{i}}{p_{i}-1}\right)\left(1-\sum\frac{1}{p_{i}^{a_{i}+1}}\right),

and

\left(\prod_{i=1}^{k}\frac{p_{i}}{p_{i}-1}\right)\left(1-\sum\frac{1}{p_{i}^{a_{i}+1}}\right)\geq\left(2+\alpha\right)\left(1-k\max\frac{1}{p_{i}^{a_{i}+1}}\right).

Now, reducing any of the
a_{i}
by 1 or more to a get a new number
n^{\prime}
yields a deficient number since
n
is primitive non-deficient. Thus for any prime
p_{i}
we have

h(n/p_{i})<2,

which we combine with Lemma
7
to conclude that for any
p_{i}
,

h(n)<2\left(1+\frac{1}{p_{i}^{a_{i}}}\right).

We have then,

2\left(1+\min\frac{1}{p_{i}^{a_{i}}}\right)>\left(2+\alpha\right)\left(1-k\max\frac{1}{p_{i}^{a_{i}+1}}\right).

Set
m=\max\frac{1}{p_{i}^{a_{i}+1}}.
Note that
m=\frac{1}{\min p_{i}^{a_{i}+1}}
and that

\min\frac{1}{p_{i}^{a_{i}}}\leq p_{1}m
. Thus,

\frac{1+p_{1}m}{1-km}>1+\frac{\alpha}{2}.

If one has a concern about sign issues in the above, note that if
km\geq 1
, then one has that
\min p_{i}^{a_{i}+1}<k
, which is stronger than our desired inequality, so we can assume that
km<1
. Note that
k\geq p_{1}
by Servais’s Lemma, and the fact that no non-deficient number is a power of 2.

Now, we split into two cases, where
m<\frac{1}{k(k+1)}
and where
m\geq\frac{1}{k(k+1)}
. Notice that since
k(k+1)
cannot be a power of a prime, these two quantities cannot be equal.

Consider the case where
m>\frac{1}{k(k+1)}.
This means that we have

\min p_{i}^{a_{i}+1}<k(k+1),

which is one of the terms in our maximum needed. If

m<\frac{1}{k(k+1)},

then by Lemma
2
, we have

\frac{1}{1-km}<1+(k+1)m.

Hence,

\frac{1+p_{1}m}{1-km}<\left(1+mp_{1}\right)\left(1+(k+1)m\right)=1+(k+1)m+mp_{1}+m^{2}p_{1}(k+1).

(6)

Now, we use that
p_{1}<k
with Inequality
6
to obtain:

1+(k+1)m+mp_{1}+m^{2}p_{1}(k+1)<1+m(k+1+p_{1})+m^{2}k^{2}<1+m(k+2+p_{1}),

and so
1+m(k+2+p_{1})>1+\frac{\alpha}{2}
,
which yields

m(k+2+p_{1})>\frac{\alpha}{2}.

Thus,

\min p_{i}^{a_{i}+1}<2\frac{\left(k+2+p_{1}\right)}{\alpha}.

(7)

∎

We are now ready to prove Theorem
2
.

Proof.

We may, based on the earlier results, assume that
n
is odd, and thus that
p_{1}>2
. Since
n
is non-deficient, we have

2\leq h(n)<H(n).

We have

H(n)=\prod_{i=1}^{k}\frac{p_{i}}{p_{i}-1}\geq 2+\frac{1}{(p_{1}-1)(p_{2}-1)\cdots(p_{k}-1)}\geq 2+\frac{2}{\prod_{i=1}^{k}p_{i}-1}>2+\frac{2}{R}.

Note that on the last step in the above chain of inequalities, we are using that
\prod_{i=1}^{k}\frac{p_{i}}{p_{i}-1}>2
so
(p_{1}-1)(p_{2}-1)\cdots(p_{k}-1)<\frac{R}{2}.

We may then apply Lemma
8
with
\alpha=\frac{2}{R}
. Thus, there exists an
i
such that

p_{i}^{a_{i}+1}<\max\{R\left(k+2+p_{1}\right),k(k+1)\}.

(8)

The first term in the above maximum is clearly larger than the second term so we have

p_{i}^{a_{i}+1}<R\left(k+2+p_{1}\right).

(9)

We now apply Lemma
4
to get that

R\left(k+2+p_{1}\right)<2Rk.

and we are done.

∎

4
in the above argument with the sort of inequality used in section 5 of the author’s aforementioned paper

9
]
.

How tight is Conjecture
1
? Some of our odd primitive non-deficient numbers with 3 prime factors just barely manage to satisfy this inequality. However, the actual worst case scenario occurs when
n=2^{a}p
and
p
is a Fermat prime. That is, we have
p=2^{a}+1
. In this scenario,
2^{a+1}=R-2
. Notice that this is essentially the best possible case, since we cannot have
p^{a_{i}+1}=R-1
since
p|R
. Thus, our conjectured inequality being essentially best possible for infinitely many
n
is equivalent to there being infinitely many Fermat primes. The standard conjecture is that there are only finitely many such primes. If we are in the situation where
n
is odd, then so are
R
and
p_{i}^{a_{i}+1}
. In that situation we must have
p_{i}^{a_{i}+1}\leq R-2p_{i}
, since
R-p_{i}
would be even. Note that this happens for
n=131625=3^{4}5^{3}13
. In this case,
13
is the only prime divisor satisfying
p_{i}^{a_{i}+1}<R
, and in fact,
13^{2}=(3)(5)(13)-2(13)
.

It seems natural to ask for an asymptotic for

Although 945 is in

\lim_{x\rightarrow\infty}\frac{(B\cap C)(x)}{B(x)}=0.

We can also apply Lemma
8
to obtain the following result, which essentially says that if
n
is an odd primitive non-deficient number, then either its largest prime factor is small compared to the radical or it has a component which is small compared to the radical.

Proposition 15
.

Let
n
be an odd primitive non-deficient number with
n=p_{1}^{a_{1}}p_{2}^{a_{2}}\cdots p_{k}^{a_{k}}
with
p_{1}<p_{2}\cdots<p_{k}
.
Either

p_{k}<\sqrt{R}

(10)

or there exists an
i
such that

p_{i}^{a_{i}+1}<\frac{(k+2+p_{1})(p_{k}-1)}{2}.

(11)

Proof.

Assume as given. As before, if
n
is non-deficient we must have

H(n)=\prod_{i=1}^{k}\frac{p_{i}}{p_{i}-1}>2,

and thus

\frac{p_{1}p_{2}p_{3}\cdots p_{k}}{(p_{1}-1)(p_{2}-1)\cdots(p_{k}-1)}-2>0

which means that

p_{1}p_{2}\cdots p_{k}=2(p_{1}-1)(p_{2}-1)\cdots(p_{k}-1)+x

(12)

for some
x
. Note that
x
is itself a positive integer.
Now, Equation
12
modulo
p_{k}
becomes

0\equiv 2(p_{1}-1)(p_{2}-1)\cdots(p_{k-1}-1)(-1)+x\,\,\,(\mathrm{mod}\,\,\,p_{k}).

So
p_{k}|-2(p_{1}-1)(p_{2}-1)\cdots(p_{k-1}-1)+x.

If
-2(p_{1}-1)(p_{2}-1)\cdots(p_{k-1}-1)+x
is negative, then

p_{k}|2(p_{1}-1)(p_{2}-1)\cdots(p_{k-1}-1)-x.

Thus,

p_{k}\leq 2(p_{1}-1)(p_{2}-1)\cdots(p_{k-1}-1)-x<2(p_{1}-1)(p_{2}-1)\cdots(p_{k-1}-1)

(13)

We have then

2(p_{1}-1)(p_{2}-1)\cdots(p_{k-1}-1)\leq 2R\frac{p_{1}-1}{p_{1}}\frac{p_{2}-1}{p_{2}}\cdots\frac{p_{k-1}-1}{p_{k-1}}\frac{1}{p_{k}}

(14)

Since
n
is non-deficient we have

\frac{p_{1}-1}{p_{1}}\frac{p_{2}-1}{p_{2}}\cdots\frac{p_{k-1}-1}{p_{k-1}}\frac{p_{k-1}}{p_{k}}<2,

and so

\frac{p_{1}-1}{p_{1}}\frac{p_{2}-1}{p_{2}}\cdots\frac{p_{k-1}-1}{p_{k-1}}<\frac{1}{2}\frac{p_{k}}{p_{k}-1}.

(15)

We then combine inequalities
13
,
14
and
15
to get that

p_{k}\leq\frac{2R}{p_{k}}\frac{1}{2}\frac{p_{k}}{p_{k}-1}

which implies that

p_{k}(p_{k}-1)<R.

(16)

Since
R
is a multiple of
p_{k}
, we must have

p_{k}(p_{k}-1)\leq R-p_{k}

which implies that
p_{k}<\sqrt{R}.

Now, assume that

p_{k}\geq\sqrt{R}.

Thus, by our above reasoning we must that
-2(p_{1}-1)(p_{2}-1)\cdots(p_{k-1}-1)+x
is non-negative. Then we have that
x\geq 2(p_{1}-1)(p_{2}-1)\cdots(p_{k-1}-1).

Thus, we have that

p_{1}p_{2}\cdots p_{k}\geq 2(p_{1}-1)(p_{2}-1)\cdots(p_{k}-1)+2(p_{1}-1)(p_{2}-1)\cdots(p_{k-1}-1),

(17)

and so

\prod_{i=1}^{k}\frac{p_{i}}{p_{i}-1}\geq 2+\frac{2(p_{1}-1)(p_{2}-1)\cdots(p_{k-1}-1)}{(p_{1}-1)(p_{2}-1)\cdots(p_{k}-1)}=2+\frac{2}{p_{k}-1}.

The rest of the proof then continues from invoking Lemma
8
and proceeding as before.
∎

This raises the following question.

Question 16
.

For any
\epsilon>0
, does there exist an odd, primitive non-deficient number
n=p_{1}^{a_{1}}p_{2}^{a_{2}}\cdots p_{k}^{a_{k}}
with prime factors

(\mathrm{rad}(n))^{\frac{1}{2}}<\epsilon p_{k}?

We suspect that the answer to Question
16

10
.

It seems that most odd primitive on-deficient numbers satisfy both Inequality
10
and Inequality
11
. It is not too hard to show that there are infinitely many which satisfy both. In particular, there are infinitely many square free odd primitive non-deficient numbers made from taking a prime and then multiplying by each successive prime until the number is not deficient. All numbers generated this way satisfy both inequalities. Let
D
be the set of odd primitive non-deficient numbers which satisfy both Inequality

and Inequality

. Then we suspect the following.

Conjecture 17
.

\lim_{x\rightarrow\infty}\frac{D(x)}{C_{o}(x)}=1.
