The Convergence Rate of Vanishing Viscosity Approximations for Mean Field Games
Wenpin Tang, Yuming Paul Zhang

Abstract
. Motivated by numerical challenges in first-order mean field games (MFGs) and the weak noise theory for the Kardar–Parisi–Zhang equation, we consider the problem of vanishing viscosity approximations for MFGs. We provide the first results on the convergence rate to the vanishing viscosity limit in mean field games, with a focus on the dimension dependence of the rate exponent. Two cases are studied: MFGs with a local coupling and those with a nonlocal, regularizing coupling. In the former case, we use a duality approach and our results suggest that there may be a phase transition in the dimension dependence of vanishing viscosity approximations in terms of the growth of the Hamiltonian and the local coupling. In the latter case, we rely on the regularity analysis of the solution, and derive a faster rate compared to MFGs with a local coupling. A list of open problems are presented.



Key words:
Convergence rate, dimension dependence, duality, KPZ equation, local/nonlocal coupling, mean field games, vanishing viscosity approximations.

35K10, 35Q89, 49L25, 91A15

1.
Introduction

Mean Field Games (MFGs) are a mathematical framework used to model and analyze strategic interactions in a large population,
which was independently developed by Lasry and Lions
[
43
,
44
,
45
]
, and Caines, Huang and Malhamé
[
38
]
.
In MFGs, each individual makes decisions based on her own objective
as well as the behavior of the entire population,
represented by a mean field which describes the probability distribution of the collective state of the system.
MFGs are widely used to model complex systems in economics
[
18
,
40
]
and engineering
[
23
,
37
,
38
]
.

The standard form of MFGs is given by the following system of partial differential equations (PDEs):

\left\{\begin{aligned} &-\partial_{t}u_{\nu}-\nu\Delta u_{\nu}+H(x,Du_{\nu})=f(x,m_{\nu}),\\
&\partial_{t}m_{\nu}-\nu\Delta m_{\nu}-\nabla\cdot(m_{\nu}D_{p}H(x,Du_{\nu}))=0,\qquad\mbox{in }\Omega:=(0,T)\times{\mathbb{T}}^{d},\\
&m_{\nu}(0,x)=\bar{m}(x),\quad u_{\nu}(T,x)=\bar{u}(x),\end{aligned}\right.

(1.1)

where
{\mathbb{T}}^{d}:={\mathbb{R}}^{d}/{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
is the
d
-dimensional torus,

T>0
and
\nu\geq 0
.
The Hamiltonian
H(x,p)
is a convex function with respect to the second variable
p
.
MFGs are used to describe Nash equilibria in differential games with a continuum of players,
where
m_{\nu}(t,x)
is the density of players at time
t
and at position
x
.
The variable
u_{\nu}
is the value of a typical player’s optimal control problem,
so it is a solution to some Hamilton-Jacobi equation.
As
u_{\nu}
is the optimal value (that the player can possibly achieve),
the optimal strategy is
-D_{p}H(x,Du_{\nu})
.
If the density of the population flows to the direction preferred by the optimal strategy,
the game has a Nash equilibrium.
This amounts to solving (
1.1
).
When
\nu>0
, the system is of second order.
The system by sending
\nu
to zero is called the
vanishing viscosity limit
, which is of first order.

In this paper, we study the convergence rate of second-order MFGs to the vanishing viscosity limit as
\nu\rightarrow 0^{+}
.
A special focus is on the dependence of the rate on the dimension
d
,
and hence on whether or under what circumstances it induces the curse of dimensionality or the lack thereof.
For simplicity, we assume that the terminal data
\bar{u}
is independent of the density
m_{\nu}
.
We distinguish two cases for the coupling (or the running cost)
f
:

•

When
f(x,m)
depends on the pointwise value of the density
m(t,x)
,
the coupling is referred to as
local
.

•

When
f(x,m)
depends on the entire distribution of
m(t,\cdot)
and is uniformly smooth for all distributions,
it is referred to as a
regularizing, nonlocal coupling
.

We assume that
f
is increasing in
m
in the local case,
and satisfies the Lasry-Lions monotonicity condition in the nonlocal case, so that the uniqueness of solutions is guaranteed.
It is also worth mentioning that the convergence rate of vanishing viscosity approximations
of Hamilton-Jacobi equations was studied in
[
21
,
24
,
58
,
59
]
,
and the optimal rate is
\nu^{\frac{1}{2}}
.
The same problem was considered for hyperbolic systems in
[
3
,
5
,
6
]
,
and for Fokker-Planck equations with
\mathcal{C}^{1}
nonlocal drifts in
[
26
,
27
,
62
]
.

Before delving into the problem,
we digress a bit to explain the motivations to study the convergence of vanishing viscosity in MFGs.

(1)

In recent years, there has been a growing interest in modeling autonomous vehicles’ control and their macroscopic traffic flow
by first-order MFGs (
\nu=0
)
[
35
,
36
,
41
]
.
As pointed out in
[
35
]
, numerical methods converge slowly, or even fail to converge for first-order MFGs.
This is not surprising as iterative algorithms may be ill-posed due to irregular coefficients in the transport equation.
On the other hand, it is known
[
7
,
8
,
9
]
that
the policy iteration algorithm converges exponentially fast for second-order MFGs (
\nu>0
).
So a reasonable idea is to approximate first-order MFGs by second-order MFGs,
and a quantitative rate of second-order MFGs to the vanishing viscosity limit provides the approximation error.
Moreover, the convergence of the policy iteration algorithm is exponential in
\nu^{-1}
,
which yields a tradeoff between bias and algorithm efficiency.

(2)

The Kardar–Parisi–Zhang (KPZ) universality class describes the limiting behavior of a collection of random growth models,
and the underlying continuum object is the KPZ equation
[
20
,
57
]
.
Due to its nonlinear nature, the KPZ equation is hardly accessible
except for some initial conditions which lead to integrability.
Recently, there has been a line of work on large deviations of the stochastic heat equation (SHE),
and hence the (
1+1
)-dimensional KPZ equation by the Cole-Hopf transform,
under the
weak noise theory

[
42
,
50
]
.
Rigorous treatments have been developed in
[
28
,
47
,
48
,
61
]
.
Under narrow wedge initial condition, the “most probable” KPZ path
conditioned to be
\lambda
at time
T
is given by
h(t,x)=\log Z[\rho^{\texttt{m}}](t,x)
,
where
Z[\rho]
given
\rho=\rho(t,x)
solves the PDE

\partial_{t}Z=\frac{1}{2}\partial_{xx}Z+\rho Z,\mbox{ for }(t,x)\in(0,T]\times\mathbb{R},\quad Z(0,\cdot)=\delta_{0},

and
\rho^{\texttt{m}}
solves the variational problem

\inf\left\{\frac{1}{2}\|\rho\|_{L^{2}}^{2}:Z[\rho](T,0)=e^{\lambda}\right\}
.
Of particular interest is the lower-tail limit as
\lambda:=-\nu^{-1}\to-\infty
(so
\nu\to 0^{+}
).
Setting the scaling

Z_{\nu}[\rho](t,x):=Z[\nu^{-1}\rho(\cdot,\,\nu^{\frac{1}{2}}\cdot)](t,\,\nu^{-\frac{1}{2}}x)
,

\rho_{\nu}(t,x):=\nu\rho^{\texttt{m}}(t,\,\nu^{-\frac{1}{2}}x)
,
and
h_{\nu}(t,x):=\nu^{-1}\log Z_{\nu}[\rho_{\nu}](t,x)
,

[
48
,
61
]
showed that
h_{\nu}
converges locally uniformly to a limiting shape
h_{*}
as
\nu\to 0^{+}

(by integrability of
h_{*}
) but with no rate.
Curiously,
(h_{\nu},\rho_{\nu})
solves the PDEs:

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}h_{\nu}=\frac{\nu}{2}\partial_{xx}h_{\nu}+\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{x}h_{\nu})^{2}+\rho_{\nu},\\
&-\partial_{t}\rho_{\nu}=\frac{\nu}{2}\partial_{xx}\rho_{\nu}-\nabla\cdot(\rho_{\nu}\,\partial_{x}h_{\nu}),\end{aligned}\right.

(1.2)

with a suitable choice of the initial-terminal conditions (by a Riemann-Hilbert approach).
By taking
H(x,p)=\frac{1}{2}p^{2}
and
f(x,m)=m
(a local coupling), and letting

h_{\nu}(t,x):=-u_{\nu/2}(T-t,x),\quad\rho_{\nu}(t,x):=-m_{\nu/2}(T-t,x),

the equations (
1.1
) specify to (
1.2
).
Thus, our result on the convergence of second-order MFGs to the vanishing viscosity limit
stipulates how the lower-tail limit of the most probable KPZ path in (
1+1
)-dimension is obtained,
and gives a quantitative rate in the large deviation limit thereof.
Of course, the Cole-Hopf transform from the SHE to the KPZ equation and the weak noise theory is only valid in dimension
d=1
.
There is no obvious theory for dimension
d\geq 2
(see
[
12
,
19
,
49
]
for recent development),
so it is not clear how large deviations of the KPZ equation in dimension
(d+1)
with
d\geq 2
is connected to MFGs.
Nevertheless, our results for MFGs hold for general dimensions.

Now we turn back to MFGs.
The well-posedness of (
1.1
) has been thoroughly studied in the case of nonlocal, monotone, and regularizing couplings
for both second-order MFGs (
\nu>0
) and first-order MFGs (
\nu=0
)
[
43
,
44
,
45
]
,
and for time-dependent MFGs
[
25
]
.
When the function
f(x,m)
depends locally on the value of
m
and
\nu>0
,
the well-posedness problem has been addressed by
[
29
,
30
]
for classical solutions,
and by
[
53
,
54
]
for weak solutions. When
\nu=0
, in general, one cannot expect the existence of classical solutions.

[
14
]
exploited the variational method, and showed that first-order MFGs can be viewed as an optimality condition for two convex problems. This approach can also be used for quadratic Hamiltonian MFGs in the whole domain
[
52
]
.
Recently, allowing the terminal condition
\bar{u}
to depend on the density
m_{\nu}(T,\cdot)
,

[
51
]
obtained the weak solution as the limit of a sequence of classical solutions to strictly elliptic problems.

Here is an overview of our main results on the convergence rate of vanishing viscosity approximations for MFGs.
For both local and nonlocal couplings,
it is known that as
\nu\to 0^{+}
,
the solutions
(u_{\nu},m_{\nu})
converge to
(u,m)
,
where
(u,m)
are solutions to first-order MFGs
[
1
,
13
,
16
]
.
However, the convergence rate is not well understood.
This paper provides the first quantitative rate of vanishing viscosity approximations for MFGs.

(a)

Local coupling
.
When the coupling
f
is local,
we apply a duality approach which relies on the fact that
equation (
1.1
) is the optimality condition for two convex optimization problems.
This approach was used in
[
56
]
, and can be traced back to
[
4
]
.
To obtain a convergence rate of solutions as
\nu\to 0^{+}
, the main assumption is the coercivity of the Hamiltonian and the coupling,
as specified in the conditions (H5-1) and (H5-2).
Similar assumptions were made in
[
32
,
56
]
to get a Sobolev estimate for
m
.
With these assumptions, we prove that
(u_{\nu},m_{\nu})
converges in some Sobolev norm at a polynomial rate as
\nu\to 0^{+}
(see Theorem
4.3
).

To illustrate, take
H(x,p)=|p|^{r}
and
f(x,m)=m^{q-1}
for some
q,r>1

(while our results hold for more general Hamiltonian and local couplings).
Let

\beta:=\max\left\{\frac{qr}{2qr-q-r},1\right\}(d+1)-d\geq 1.

Our result shows that

\displaystyle\iint_{\Omega}(m_{\nu}^{q/2}-m^{q/2})^{2}dxdt\lesssim\nu^{\frac{1}{1+\beta}},

(1.3a)

\displaystyle\iint_{\Omega}\left||Du_{\nu}|^{r/2-1}Du_{\nu}-|Du|^{r/2-1}Du\right|^{2}mdxdt\lesssim\nu^{\frac{1}{1+\beta}}.

(1.3b)

In particular, we have
\iint_{\Omega}(m_{\nu}-m)^{2}dxdt\lesssim\nu^{\frac{2}{q(1+\beta)}}
for
q\geq 2
(see Remark
4.2
).
Note that if
\frac{1}{q}+\frac{1}{r}\leq 1
, then
\beta=1
and the rate in (
1.3b
) is
\nu^{\frac{1}{2}}
which is independent of the dimension
d
;
if
\frac{1}{q}+\frac{1}{r}>1
, then the rate becomes
\nu^{\frac{c}{d}}
for some
c>0

which decays slowly as the dimension
d
is large.
So there is no curse of dimensionality if the growth of
H
and
f
is sufficiently large (i.e.
\frac{1}{q}+\frac{1}{r}\leq 1
), while
the convergence may suffer from the curse of dimensionality otherwise.
However, we do not know whether the rate
\nu^{\frac{1}{1+\beta}}
is tight;
if it is, or the rate is
\nu^{\kappa(d)}
with any
\kappa
decreasing to
0
as
d\to\infty
for
\frac{1}{q}+\frac{1}{r}>1
,
then it implies a phase transition in the dimension dependence of vanishing viscosity approximations for MFGs
at
\frac{1}{q}+\frac{1}{r}=1
.

When the Hamiltonian is quadratic (
q=2
), we prove a stronger convergence result for
u_{\nu}
:

\iint_{\Omega}|u_{\nu}-u|^{2}mdxdt\lesssim\nu^{\frac{1}{2(1+\beta)}}\quad\mbox{if }r>\max\left\{2+\frac{d}{d+1},\frac{d^{2}}{2d+3}\right\},

(1.4)

(see Theorem
5.3
). This result, presented in Theorem
5.3
, cannot be directly deduced from (
1.3b
), as the weighted Poincaré inequality may not be applicable.
Instead, we use the higher regularity of
m_{\nu}
(see
[
32
]
) and the equations.
For
d=1
, the condition in (
1.4
) requires
r>\frac{5}{2}
, which fails to cover the KPZ Hamiltonian with
r=2
.
To address this, we further need
d\leq 3
to get uniform boundedness of
u_{\nu}
in the KPZ setting with
q=r=2
, as presented in Theorem
5.4
.

We also mention that if the terminal data
\bar{u}=\bar{u}(x,m_{\nu}(T,x))
depends on
m_{\nu}
,
the convergence rate of vanishing viscosity for MFGs remains open.
In this case, we have no longer the optimality condition characterization.

(b)

Nonlocal and regularizing coupling
.
When the coupling is nonlocal and regularizing, we adopt a different approach.
Instead of using the optimization structure, we rely on the duality of the two equations and some regularity properties of the solutions, both uniform and non-uniform with respect to
\nu
.
Our main tool is the uniform semi-concavity of the solution
u_{\nu}
, which results from the superlinear growth of the Hamiltonian and classical Hamilton-Jacobi equations
[
11
]
.
This property allows us to establish a uniform bound on the
W^{1,2}
norm of
\nu^{\frac{1}{2}}m_{\nu}
for all
\nu>0
.
With these findings, we first prove (
1.3b
) with
r=2
and
\beta=1
, and

\iint_{\Omega}(f(x,m_{\nu})-f(x,m))(m_{\nu}-m)dxdt\lesssim\nu^{\frac{1}{2}},

(1.5)

(see Theorem
6.2
). This method, while requiring less restrictive assumptions, leads to a faster convergence rate
compared to those with a local coupling.

Assuming that (
1.5
) implies pointwise convergence of
f(x,m_{\nu})
to
f(x,m)
as
\nu\to 0^{+}
,
we obtain a pointwise convergence of
u_{\nu}
with rate
\nu^{\frac{1}{4}}
(see Theorem
7.2
).
While under a weaker condition (H4”), the estimate (
1.5
) implies that
f(x,m_{\nu})
converges to
f(x,m)
in
L^{1}(\Omega)
with a rate
\nu^{1/4}
.
Then we use both the dual equation method (see
[
46
]
) and the viscosity solution method (see
[
21
]
) to prove that the HJ equation of
u
is stable under both
L^{1}
-perturbation of coefficients and vanishing viscosity. Indeed, we obtain for all
t\leq T
,

\|u_{\nu}(t,\cdot)-u(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}\lesssim\nu^{\frac{1}{4}},

(see Theorem
7.3
).

We also mention that in the literature,
\bar{m}
is often assumed to be strictly positive, see
[
15
,
16
,
51
,
55
]
for superlinear Hamiltonian, and
[
31
]
for linear Hamiltonian.
The condition is removed under extra requirements on
H
and
f

[
14
,
33
]
, or for the kinetic type first order MFGs (with continuous initial measure)
[
34
]
.
Relying on
[
15
,
16
]
,
we show that this condition can be dropped without further restrictions (see Theorem
3.2
).

2.
Assumptions and Preliminaries for Local Coupling

We first discuss the assumptions for the case of local coupling
f
. The assumptions are made so that (
1.1
) is well-posedness for all
\nu\geq 0
, and most of them can be found in
[
15
,
16
]
. We assume that there exists
C_{0}\geq 1
such that:

(H1)

(Conditions on the coupling)
f:{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\times[0,\infty)\to{\mathbb{R}}
is continuous in both variables, strictly increasing with respect to the second variable, and there exists
q>1
such that

C_{0}^{-1}m^{q-1}-C_{0}\leq f(x,m)\leq C_{0}m^{q-1}+C_{0}\quad\text{ for all $m\geq 0$ and $x\in{\mathbb{T}}^{d}$}.

Moreover, we make the normalization condition:

f(x,0)=0\quad\text{ for all }x\in{\mathbb{T}}^{d}.

(2.1)

(H2)

(Conditions on the Hamiltonian) The Hamiltonian
H:{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is continuous in both variables, strictly convex and differentiable in the second variable, with
D_{p}H
continuous, and satisfying for some
r>1
,

C_{0}^{-1}|p|^{r}-C_{0}\leq H(x,p)\leq C_{0}|p|^{r}+C_{0}\quad\text{ for all }(x,p)\in{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}.

(H3)

(Conditions on the initial and terminal data)
\bar{u}:{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is of class
{\mathcal{C}}^{2}
, and
\bar{m}:{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is a
{\mathcal{C}}^{1}
nonnegative density function.

Remark 2.1
.

1. As discussed in
[
16
]
, (
2.1
) is just a normalization condition, which can be assumed without loss of generality. In fact, if it does not hold, one can replace
f(x,m)
and
H(x,p)
by
f(x,m)-f(x,0)
and
H(x,p)-f(x,0)
, respectively.

2. Recall that the Fenchel conjugate
H^{*}(x,\cdot)
of
H(x,\cdot)
for each
x\in{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
is defined as
H^{*}(x,\xi):=\sup_{p\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}(\langle\xi,p\rangle-H(x,p))
. Then
H^{*}
is continuous and satisfies for some
C_{0}\geq 1
(without loss of generality let us still use
C_{0}
) such that

C_{0}^{-1}|\xi|^{r^{\prime}}-C_{0}\leq H^{*}(x,\xi)\leq C_{0}|\xi|^{r^{\prime}}+C_{0}\quad\text{ for all }(x,\xi)\in{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d},

where
r^{\prime}:=\frac{r}{r-1}
is the conjugate of
r
.
Later we also write
q^{\prime}:=\frac{q}{q-1}
as the conjugate of
q
.

3. Let
F
be defined as

F(x,m):=\int_{0}^{m}f(x,s)ds\quad\text{ if }m\geq 0,

and
F(x,m)=+\infty
if
m<0
. Then
(H2)
yields for some
C_{0}\geq 1
,

C_{0}^{-1}m^{q}-C_{0}\leq F(x,m)\leq C_{0}m^{q}+C_{0}\quad\text{ for all $m\geq 0$ and $x\in{\mathbb{T}}^{d}$}.

(2.2)

We define
F^{*}(x,\cdot)
to be the Fenchel conjugate of
F(x,\cdot)
. Then
F^{*}(x,\alpha)
is strictly convex in
\alpha
,
F^{*}(x,\alpha)=0
for
\alpha\leq 0
, and for some
C_{0}\geq 1
,

C_{0}^{-1}\alpha^{q^{\prime}}-C_{0}\leq F^{*}(x,\alpha)\leq C_{0}\alpha^{q^{\prime}}+C_{0}\quad\text{ for all $\alpha\geq 0$ and $x\in{\mathbb{T}}^{d}$}.

(2.3)

4. Unlike
[
15
,
16
]
, we do not need to assume
\bar{m}>0
. It was used in
[
15
,
16
]
to show the existence of a solution for the optimization problem (

).

5. One can consider equations with more general second order terms:

\left\{\begin{aligned} &-\partial_{t}u_{\nu}-\nu A_{ij}\partial_{ij}u_{\nu}+H(x,Du_{\nu})=f(x,m_{\nu}),\\
&\partial_{t}m_{\nu}-\nu\partial_{ij}(A_{ij}m_{\nu})-\nabla\cdot(m_{\nu}D_{p}H(x,Du_{\nu}))=0,\\
&m_{\nu}(0,x)=\bar{m}(x),\quad u_{\nu}(T,x)=\bar{u}(x)\end{aligned}\right.

where
A=A(x)
is assumed to be a Lipschitz continuous map, taking values in the set of symmetric, uniformly positive definite matrices. But for this, one needs to further assume

, see
[
16
]
.

2.1.
Optimization problems

We discuss two optimal control problems which are in duality, and we refer to
[
14
,
16
]
.
For any
\nu\geq 0
, the first problem is

\inf_{(m,w)\in{\mathcal{K}}_{1,\nu}}{\mathcal{B}}(m,w)

(2.4)

where

{\mathcal{B}}(m,w):=\iint_{\Omega}mH^{*}\left(x,-\frac{w}{m}\right)+F(x,m)dxdt+\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}\bar{u}(x)m(T,x)dx,

with
\Omega=(0,T)\times{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
, and

{\mathcal{K}}_{1,\nu}:=\left\{(m,w)\in L^{1}({\mathbb{T}}^{d})\times L^{1}({\mathbb{T}}^{d};{\mathbb{R}}^{d})\,\big|\,\partial_{t}m-\nu\Delta m+\nabla\cdot w=0,\,m(0)=\bar{m}\right\}

where the continuity equation holds in the sense of distributions.
When
m=0
, we use the usual convention:

mH^{*}\left(x,-\frac{w}{m}\right):=\left\{\begin{aligned} &+\infty,&\text{ if $m=0$ and $w\neq 0$},\\
&0,&\text{ if $m=0$ and $w=0$}.\end{aligned}\right.

Now we discuss the second minimization problem. Recall
q,r>1
from (H1)(H2) and
q^{\prime},r^{\prime}
are their conjugates, respectively. Set

\gamma:=\frac{rq^{\prime}(d+1)}{d-r(q^{\prime}-1)}\,\text{ if }q^{\prime}<1+\frac{d}{r}\quad\text{and}\quad\gamma:=\infty\,\text{ if }q^{\prime}>1+\frac{d}{r},

(2.5)

and
\gamma>0
can be an arbitrarily large constant when
q^{\prime}=1+\frac{d}{r}
.
Then we let

{\mathcal{A}}(u,\alpha):=\iint_{\Omega}F^{*}\left(x,\alpha(t,x)\right)dxdt-\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}{u}(0,x)\bar{m}(x)dx,

and let
{\mathcal{K}}_{2,\nu}
be the set of
(u,\alpha)\in L^{\gamma}(\Omega)\times L^{q^{\prime}}(\Omega)
such that
Du\in L^{r}(\Omega)
and the following holds in the sense of distributions

-\partial_{t}u-\nu\Delta u+H(x,Du)\leq\alpha,\quad u(T,\cdot)\leq\bar{u}.

The precise meaning of the inequality is given in
[
16
, Section 3]
.
The second optimization problem (also called the relaxed problem in
[
14
,
16
]
) is

\inf_{(u,\alpha)\in{\mathcal{K}}_{2,\nu}}{\mathcal{A}}(u,\alpha).

(2.6)

It turns out that for each
\nu\geq 0
, the optimization problems (
2.4
) and (
2.6
) are in duality. The first equality below is proved in
[
14
,
16
]
by the Fenchel-Rockafellar theorem.The second equality is due to the fact that one can always replace
\alpha
by
\max\{\alpha,0\}
as
F^{*}(x,\alpha)=0
for
\alpha\leq 0
.
Here we remark that
\bar{m}>0
is not needed in the proofs.

Theorem 2.1

(
[
16
]
)
.

For all
\nu\geq 0
,

-\min_{(m,w)\in{\mathcal{K}}_{1,\nu}}{\mathcal{B}}(m,w)=\inf_{(u,\alpha)\in{\mathcal{K}}_{2,\nu}}{\mathcal{A}}(u,\alpha)=\inf_{(u,\alpha)\in{\mathcal{K}}_{2,\nu},\,\alpha\geq 0\text{ a.e.}}{\mathcal{A}}(u,\alpha).

Moreover, the minimum of the first term is achieved by a unique pair in
(m,w)\in{\mathcal{K}}_{1,\nu}
satisfying
(m,w)\in L^{q}(\Omega)\times L^{\frac{r^{\prime}q}{r^{\prime}+q-1}}(\Omega)
.

The last statement that the minimum is achieved by a unique pair in
{\mathcal{K}}_{1,\nu}
is because
the set
{\mathcal{K}}_{1,\nu}
is convex, and the functions
F(x,\cdot)
and
H^{*}(x,\cdot)
are strictly convex for each

.

3.
Well-posedness for non-negative data

In this section, we show well-posedness of (
1.1
) without assuming
\bar{m}
to be strictly positive.
First we recall the notion of weak solutions in
[
16
]
. Let
\nu\geq 0
and
\gamma
be from (
2.5
).

Definition 3.1
.

We say that
(u_{\nu},m_{\nu})\in L^{\gamma}(\Omega)\times L^{q}(\Omega)
is a weak solution to (
1.1
) if

(i)

the following integrability conditions hold:

Du_{\nu}\in L^{r},\,m_{\nu}H^{*}(\cdot,D_{p}H(\cdot,Du_{\nu}))\in L^{1}\quad\text{and}\quad m_{\nu}D_{p}H(\cdot,Du_{\nu})\in L^{1}.

(ii)

The following (in)equalities hold in the sense of distribution:

-\partial_{t}u_{\nu}-\nu\Delta u_{\nu}+H(x,Du_{\nu})\leq f(x,m_{\nu})\quad\text{in }\Omega,

with
u_{\nu}(T,\cdot)\leq\bar{u}
, and

\partial_{t}m_{\nu}-\nu\Delta m_{\nu}-\nabla\cdot(m_{\nu}D_{p}(x,Du_{\nu}))=0\quad\text{in }\Omega\text{ with }m_{\nu}(0)=\bar{m}.

(iii)

We have

\iint_{\Omega}m_{\nu}f(x,m_{\nu})+H^{*}(x,D_{p}H(x,Du_{\nu}))dxdt+\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}m_{\nu}(T,x)\bar{u}(x)-\bar{m}(x)u_{\nu}(0,x)dx=0.

Here the last term is well-defined due to Lemma 5.1
[
16
]
.

Theorem 3.2
.

Assume
(H1)–(H3)
and let
\nu\geq 0
. The problem (
2.6
) has at least one solution in
{\mathcal{K}}_{2,\nu}
. Let
(m_{\nu},w_{\nu})\in{\mathcal{K}}_{1,\nu}
be the minimizer of (
2.4
) and
(u_{\nu},\alpha_{\nu})\in{\mathcal{K}}_{2,\nu}
be a minimizer of (
2.6
). Then:

(1)

(u_{\nu},m_{\nu})
is a weak solution to (
1.1
), and
w_{\nu}=-m_{\nu}D_{p}H(\cdot,Du_{\nu})
and
\alpha_{\nu}=f(\cdot,m_{\nu})
almost everywhere.

(2)

Conversely, any weak solution
(u_{\nu},m_{\nu})
of (
1.1
) is such that the pair
(m_{\nu},-m_{\nu}D_{p}H(\cdot,Du_{\nu}))
is the minimizer of (
2.4
) and
(u_{\nu},f(\cdot,m_{\nu}))
is a minimizer of (
2.6
).

(3)

The solution is unique in the following sense: if
(u_{\nu}^{\prime},m_{\nu}^{\prime})
is another weak solution to (
1.1
), then
m_{\nu}=m_{\nu}^{\prime}
a.e. and
u_{\nu}=u_{\nu}^{\prime}
in
\{m_{\nu}>0\}
.

The theorem is proved in
[
16
]
with an extra assumption that
\bar{m}
is strictly positive. The assumption is only used in their Proposition 5.4 to show the existence of a solution to the optimization problem (
2.6
). We use a uniform lower bound of
u_{\nu}
and a slightly different test function to remove the constraint. We state the result below.

Proposition 3.3
.

Under the assumptions
(H1)–(H3)
, for each
\nu\geq 0
, the problem (
2.6
) has at least one solution
(u_{\nu},\alpha_{\nu})\in{\mathcal{K}}_{2,\nu}
which is uniformly bounded from below by a constant depending only on the assumptions for all
\nu\in[0,1]
.

Moreover, there exists
C>0
depending only on the assumptions such that

\sup_{\nu\in[0,1]}\|u_{\nu}(0,\cdot)\|_{L^{1}({\mathbb{T}}^{d})}+\|Du_{\nu}\|_{L^{r}(\Omega)}+\|\alpha_{\nu}\|_{L^{q^{\prime}}(\Omega)}\leq C.

(3.1)

Remark 3.1
.

Let us comment that here
\|u_{\nu}(0,\cdot)\|_{L^{1}({\mathbb{T}}^{d})}
is well-defined due to Lemma 5.1
[
16
]
(
u_{\nu}
has a “trace” in a weak sense).

Proof.

The proof follows largely the one of Proposition 5.4
[
16
]
.

Step 1
. From the first paragraph of their argument, there exists a minimizing sequence
(u_{\nu}^{n},\alpha_{\nu}^{n})\in{\mathcal{K}}_{2,\nu}
for problem (
2.6
) such that
u_{\nu}^{n},\alpha_{\nu}^{n}
are continuous,
\alpha^{n}_{\nu}\geq 0
, and
u_{\nu}^{n}
is a viscosity solution to

-\partial_{t}u_{\nu}^{n}-\nu\Delta u_{\nu}^{n}+H(x,Du_{\nu}^{n})=\alpha_{\nu}^{n},\quad u_{\nu}^{n}(T,\cdot)=\bar{u}.

(3.2)

Since
H
is convex, this equality also holds in the sense of distribution
[
39
]
.
Moreover, since
\alpha^{n}_{\nu}\geq 0
, comparing the solution with
-\|\bar{u}\|_{\infty}-\|H(\cdot,0)\|_{\infty}(T-t)
yields

u_{\nu}^{n}(t,x)\geq-\|\bar{u}\|_{\infty}-\|H(\cdot,0)\|_{\infty}(T-t)\quad\text{ in }\Omega.

(3.3)

It is clear that the right-hand side is independent of
\nu
.

Step 2
. Next, we show some bounds for
\alpha_{\nu}^{n}
and
u_{\nu}^{n}
that are uniform in
n
and
\nu
. We integrate (
3.2
) against
\bar{m}+1
on
\Omega
(instead of
\bar{m}
that was used in
[
16
]
) to get

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}u_{\nu}^{n}(0,x)(\bar{m}(x)+1)dx+

\displaystyle\iint_{\Omega}\nu D\bar{m}Du_{\nu}^{n}+(\bar{m}+1)H(x,Du_{\nu}^{n})dxdt

\displaystyle=\iint_{\Omega}(\bar{m}(x)+1)\alpha_{\nu}^{n}(t,x)dxdt+\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}\bar{u}(\bar{m}+1)dx.

By (H2) and (H3) (
\bar{m},\bar{u}\in{\mathcal{C}}^{1}
), there exists
C\geq 1
depending only on (H3) and
C_{0}
such that

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}u_{\nu}^{n}(0,x)dx-

\displaystyle C\nu\iint_{\Omega}|Du_{\nu}^{n}|dxdt+C^{-1}\iint_{\Omega}|Du_{\nu}^{n}|^{r}dxdt

(3.4)

\displaystyle\leq-\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}u_{\nu}^{n}(0,x)\bar{m}(x)dx+C\iint_{\Omega}|\alpha_{\nu}^{n}|dxdt+C.

Then, we use (
3.3
) to get

\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}u_{\nu}^{n}(0,x)dx\geq\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}|u_{\nu}^{n}(0,x)|dx-C

for some
C
depending only on
\|\bar{u}\|_{\infty},\|H(\cdot,0)\|_{\infty}
and
T
.
Next, using that

and

, we get for any
\varepsilon>0
there is
C_{\varepsilon}
satisfying

\iint_{\Omega}|Du_{\nu}^{n}|dxdt\leq\varepsilon\iint_{\Omega}|Du_{\nu}^{n}|^{r}dxdt+C_{\varepsilon},

\iint_{\Omega}|\alpha_{\nu}^{n}|dxdt\leq\varepsilon\iint_{\Omega}|\alpha_{\nu}^{n}|^{q^{\prime}}dxdt+C_{\varepsilon}.

Applying these in (
3.4
) and taking
\varepsilon
to be sufficiently small, we obtain for some
C
independent of
n
and
\nu\in[0,1]
,

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}|u_{\nu}^{n}(0,x)|dx

\displaystyle+(2C)^{-1}\iint_{\Omega}|Du_{\nu}^{n}|^{r}dxdt+(2C_{0})^{-1}\iint_{\Omega}|\alpha_{\nu}^{n}|^{q^{\prime}}dxdt

\displaystyle\leq-\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}u_{\nu}^{n}(0,x)\bar{m}(x)dx+C_{0}^{-1}\iint_{\Omega}|\alpha_{\nu}^{n}|^{q^{\prime}}dxdt+C

where
C_{0}\geq 1
is from (
2.3
). It follows from (
2.3
) and the definition of
{\mathcal{A}}
that

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}|u_{\nu}^{n}(0,x)|dx+(2C)^{-1}\iint_{\Omega}|Du_{\nu}^{n}|^{r}dxdt+(2C_{0})^{-1}\iint_{\Omega}|\alpha_{\nu}^{n}|^{q^{\prime}}dxdt

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\leq-\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}u_{\nu}^{n}(0,x)\bar{m}(x)dx+C_{0}^{-1}\iint_{\Omega}F^{*}(x,\alpha_{\nu}^{n})dxdt+C_{0}+C\leq{\mathcal{A}}(u^{n}_{\nu},\alpha_{\nu}^{n})+C^{\prime}.

Step 3
. Since
(u^{n}_{\nu},\alpha_{\nu}^{n})
is a minimizing sequence, for all
n
sufficiently large and
\bar{\alpha}_{\nu}:=-\partial_{t}\bar{u}-\nu\Delta\bar{u}+H(x,D\bar{u})
we obtain

{\mathcal{A}}(u^{n}_{\nu},\alpha^{n}_{\nu})\leq\sup_{\nu\in[0,1]}\inf_{(u,\alpha)\in{\mathcal{K}}_{2,\nu}}{\mathcal{A}}(u,\alpha)+1\leq\sup_{\nu\in[0,1]}{\mathcal{A}}(\bar{u},\bar{\alpha}_{\nu})+1,

which is finite. So there exists
C>0
independent of

and
\nu\in[0,1]
such that for all large

,

\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}|u_{\nu}^{n}(0,x)|dx+\iint_{\Omega}|Du_{\nu}^{n}|^{r}dxdt+\iint_{\Omega}|\alpha_{\nu}^{n}|^{q^{\prime}}dxdt\leq C.

(3.5)

With the uniform bound, one can take weak limit of

along a subsequence of

. Following Step 2 and Step 3 in Proposition 5.4
[

]
, we obtain that the weak limit

is a minimizer of the optimization problem (

). Finally, (

∎

4.
Vanishing Viscosity Limit – Local Coupling

To study the convergence rate of vanishing viscosity limit of mean field games, we further need the following regularity and coercivity assumptions.

(H4)

(Regularity condition) There exist
C_{0},C_{1},C_{2}\geq 0
such that for all
x,y\in{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
and
m\geq 0
,

|F(x+y,m)-F(x,m)|\leq C_{0}(1+m^{q})|y|,

and for any
p\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and
\sigma\in(0,1]
,

\sup_{z\in B_{\sigma}}H\left(x+z,\frac{p}{1+C_{1}\sigma}\right)\leq\frac{H(x,p)}{1+C_{1}\sigma}+C_{2}\sigma.

(4.1)

(H5-1)

(Coecivity condition on
H
) There are
J_{1},J_{1}^{*}:{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and
c_{0}>0
such that for all
p,\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
we have

H(x,p)+H^{*}\left(x,\xi\right)-p\cdot\xi\geq c_{0}\,|J_{1}(x,p)-J_{1}^{*}(x,\xi)|^{2}.

(4.2)

(H5-2)

(Coecivity condition on
F
) There are
J_{2},J_{2}^{*}:{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
and
c_{0}>0
such that for all
m,\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}
we have

F(x,m)+F^{*}(x,\alpha)-m\alpha\geq c_{0}\,|J_{2}(x,m)-J_{2}^{*}(x,\alpha)|^{2}.

(4.3)

Remark 4.1
.

1. If
H(x,p)
is only a function of
p
, then we can take
C_{1}=C_{2}=0
in (H4) and (
4.1
) is trivial.

The condition (
4.1
) on
H
is stronger than the one on
F
, but it is still satisfied by a large class of Hamiltonian. For example, if
H(x,p)=h_{1}(x)|p|^{r}+h_{2}(x)
with two Lipschitz continuous functions
h_{1},h_{2}:{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
and
h_{1}\geq 0
, then (
4.1
) holds with
C_{1}:=\|Dh_{1}\|_{\infty}/(r-1)
and
C_{2}:=\|Dh_{2}\|_{\infty}
.

2. Note that
F^{*}(x,\alpha)=0
for
\alpha\leq 0
. For
\alpha\geq 0
, there exists a unique
m_{\alpha}\geq 0
such that

F^{*}(x,\alpha)=\sup_{m\geq 0}\{\alpha m-F(x,m)\}=\alpha m_{\alpha}-F(x,m_{\alpha}).

By (
2.2
),
m_{\alpha}\geq 0
satisfies

m_{\alpha}^{q}\leq C\alpha^{q^{\prime}}+C\quad\text{ for some $C>0$}.

(4.4)

So it follows from
(H4)
that for all
x,y\in{\mathbb{T}}^{d}

and
\alpha\geq 0
,

|F^{*}(x+y,\alpha)-F^{*}(x,\alpha)|\leq C(1+\alpha^{q^{\prime}})|y|\quad\text{for some $C>0$.}

(4.5)

It is not hard to see that
F^{*}
is increasing in
\alpha
. Indeed for any
\sigma\geq 0
,

\displaystyle F^{*}(x,\alpha+\sigma)\geq(\alpha+\sigma)m_{\alpha}-F(x,m_{\alpha})\geq F^{*}(x,\alpha).

(4.6)

Moreover, by (
4.4
), for any
\sigma\geq 0
,

\displaystyle F^{*}(x,\alpha)\geq F^{*}(x,\alpha+\sigma)-\sigma m_{\alpha+\sigma}\geq F^{*}(x,\alpha+\sigma)-C\sigma(1+(\alpha+\sigma)^{q^{\prime}/q}),

(4.7)

where
m_{\alpha+\sigma}
is such that
F^{*}(x,\alpha+\sigma)=\alpha m_{\alpha+\sigma}-F(x,m_{\alpha+\sigma})

3. Let
\tau:{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\to(0,\infty)
and
h:{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be continuous. If
H(x,p)=\frac{1}{r}|\tau(x)p|^{r}+h(x)
for some
r>1
, then
H^{*}(x,\xi)=\frac{1}{r^{\prime}}|\xi/\tau(x)|^{r^{\prime}}-h(x)
. In this case, (
4.2
) holds with

J_{1}(x,p)=\tau(x)^{r/2}|p|^{r/2-1}p,\quad J_{1}^{*}(x,\xi)=\tau(x)^{-r^{\prime}/2}|\xi|^{r^{\prime}/2-1}\xi\quad\text{and}\quad c_{0}=(\max\{r,r^{\prime}\})^{-1}.

If
F(x,m)=\frac{1}{q}(\tau(x)m)^{q}
for some
q>1
, then
F^{*}(x,\alpha)=\frac{1}{q^{\prime}}(\alpha/\tau(x))^{q^{\prime}}
, and (
4.3
) holds with

J_{2}(x,m)=\tau(x)^{q/2}m^{q/2},\quad J_{2}^{*}(x,\alpha)=\tau(x)^{-q^{\prime}/2}\alpha^{q^{\prime}/2}\quad\text{and}\quad c_{0}=(\max\{q,q^{\prime}\})^{-1}.

We only prove (
4.3
) with this selection of functions in the appendix. The proof for (
4.2
) is almost the same.

Finally, let us comment that in the above examples the value of
c_{0}
is optimal. Indeed, if
\tau\equiv 1,q=q^{\prime}=2
, it is easy to see that
c_{0}\leq\frac{1}{2}
. The optimality can also be seen in the proof.

The following lemma is a consequence of the coercivity condition. The proof is identical to the one of Lemma 3.2
[
56
]
, and we skip it.

Lemma 4.1
.

Assume
(H1)–(H4)
, and for
\nu\geq 0
, let
(u,\alpha)\in{\mathcal{K}}_{2,\nu}
and
(m,w)\in{\mathcal{K}}_{1,\nu}
. If
(H5-1)
holds, we have

\displaystyle{\mathcal{A}}(u,\alpha)+{\mathcal{B}}(m,w)\geq c_{0}\|m^{1/2}(J_{1}(\cdot,Du)-J_{1}^{*}(\cdot,-w/m))\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

(4.8)

And if
(H5-2)
holds, we have

\displaystyle{\mathcal{A}}(u,\alpha)+{\mathcal{B}}(m,w)\geq c_{0}\left\|J_{2}(\cdot,m)-J_{2}^{*}(\cdot,\alpha)\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

(4.9)

For
\nu\geq 0
, let
(m_{\nu},w_{\nu})\in{\mathcal{K}}_{1,\nu}
be the minimizer of (
2.4
) and
(u_{\nu},\alpha_{\nu})\in{\mathcal{K}}_{2,\nu}
be a minimizer of (
2.6
). Theorem
3.2
and Theorem

yield that
(u_{\nu},m_{\nu})
is a weak solution to (

), and

{\mathcal{A}}(u_{\nu},\alpha_{\nu})+{\mathcal{B}}(m_{\nu},w_{\nu})=0.

(4.10)

The following result is a direct consequence of Lemma
4.1
and (
4.10
).

Corollary 4.2
.

We have for any
\nu\geq 0
and for a.e.
(t,x)\in\Omega
such that

J_{1}^{*}(x,-w_{\nu}/m_{\nu})=J_{1}(x,Du_{\nu})\quad\text{and}\quad J_{2}^{*}(x,\alpha_{\nu})=J_{2}(x,m_{\nu}).

(4.11)

Now we prove the main theorem of the section.

Theorem 4.3
.

Assume
(H1)–(H4)
, and let
(m_{\nu},\alpha_{\nu})
be a weak solution to (
1.1
) with
\nu\geq 0
. Write
(u,\alpha,m,w)=(u_{0},\alpha_{0},m_{0},w_{0})
, and

Then if

holds, there exists

such that for all

we have

\iint_{\Omega}|J_{1}(\cdot,Du_{\nu})-J_{1}(\cdot,Du)|^{2}m\,dxdt\leq C\nu^{\frac{1}{1+\beta}},

(H5-2)
holds, for all

\iint_{\Omega}|J_{2}(\cdot,m_{\nu})-J_{2}(\cdot,m)|^{2}dxdt\leq C\nu^{\frac{1}{1+\beta}}.

Proof.

We use the optimization structure of (

) described in Theorem

.

Step 1
. By Theorem
2.1
and the proof of Proposition
3.3
, we can take a minimizing sequence
(u^{n},\alpha^{n})\in{\mathcal{K}}_{2,0}

\alpha^{n}\geq 0

-\partial_{t}u^{n}+H(x,Du^{n})=\alpha^{n},\quad u^{n}(T,\cdot)=\bar{u}

(4.13)

in the sense of distribution, and

{\mathcal{A}}(u^{n},\alpha^{n})\leq{\mathcal{A}}(u,\alpha)+1/n.

Due to (

C>0

sufficiently large we have

(4.14)

Step 2
. The goal is to show that

(4.15)

Below we construct
(u_{\varepsilon}^{n},\alpha_{\varepsilon}^{n})

Let
\varphi:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to[0,\infty)

\varphi

For

(4.16)

It is direct to see from the definition that

as

is supported in

.

Step 3
. Because

is convex, Jensen’s inequality yields for each fixed

,

H\left(x_{0},D\left(\frac{\varphi_{\varepsilon}*u^{n}}{1+C_{1}\varepsilon}\right)(t,x)\right)\leq\varphi_{\varepsilon}*H\left(x_{0},D\left(\frac{u^{n}}{1+C_{1}\varepsilon}\right)\right)(t,x).

By (
4.1
) with
\varepsilon
in place of

H(x,Du^{n}_{\varepsilon}(t,x))\leq\frac{\varphi_{\varepsilon}*H(x,Du^{n})(t,x)}{1+C_{1}\varepsilon}+C_{2}\varepsilon.

We note that if

is independent of

.
We get from (

-\partial_{t}u^{n}_{\varepsilon}-\nu\Delta u^{n}_{\varepsilon}+H(x,Du^{n}_{\varepsilon})\leq\frac{\varphi_{\varepsilon}*\alpha^{n}}{1+C_{1}\varepsilon}-\frac{\nu}{1+C_{1}\varepsilon}\Delta\varphi_{\varepsilon}*u^{n}=:\alpha_{\varepsilon}^{n}.

(4.17)

In particular, we have
(u^{n}_{\varepsilon},\alpha^{n}_{\varepsilon})\in{\mathcal{A}}_{2,\nu}
. Consequently, as

is a minimizer of (

) over

, we obtain

{\mathcal{A}}(u_{\nu},\alpha_{\nu})\leq{\mathcal{A}}(u^{n}_{\varepsilon},\alpha^{n}_{\varepsilon}).

It follows from the definition of

. We claim that

We postpone the proof to Lemma
4.4
below. As a consequence of the claim, passing

4.15

Step 4
. Now, we combine (

) with (

{\mathcal{A}}(u_{\nu},\alpha_{\nu})+{\mathcal{B}}(m,w)\leq{\mathcal{A}}(u,\alpha)+{\mathcal{B}}(m,w)+C\nu^{\frac{1}{1+\beta}}=C\nu^{\frac{1}{1+\beta}}.

Then using (

) and (H5-1) yields

\displaystyle c_{0}\,\|m^{1/2}(J_{1}(\cdot,Du_{\nu})-J_{1}^{*}(\cdot,-w/m))\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq{\mathcal{A}}(u_{\nu},\alpha_{\nu})+{\mathcal{B}}(m,w)\leq C\nu^{\frac{1}{1+\beta}}.

Using instead (
4.9
) and (H5-2) yields

\displaystyle c_{0}\,\|J_{2}(\cdot,m)-J_{2}^{*}(\cdot,\alpha_{\nu})\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq{\mathcal{A}}(u_{\nu},\alpha_{\nu})+{\mathcal{B}}(m,w)\leq C\nu^{\frac{1}{1+\beta}}.

Finally (
4.11
) yields the conclusion.
∎

Remark 4.2
.

Suppose that
J_{2}(\cdot,m)\equiv m^{q/2}
.
We obtain from the second conclusion of theorem that when

,

\displaystyle\iint_{\Omega}|m_{\nu}-m|^{2}dxdt

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\iint_{\Omega}|m_{\nu}-m|^{q}dxdt\right)^{2/q}

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\iint_{\Omega}|m_{\nu}^{q/2}-m^{q/2}|^{2}dxdt\right)^{2/q}\leq C\nu^{\frac{2}{q(1+\beta)}}.

Lemma 4.4
.

Suppose
\nu\varepsilon^{-\beta}\leq 1
. Then

{\mathcal{A}}(u^{n}_{\varepsilon},\alpha_{\varepsilon}^{n})\leq{\mathcal{A}}(u^{n},\alpha^{n})+C\nu\varepsilon^{-\beta}+C\varepsilon.

Proof.

In view of (
4.14
), Young’s convolution inequality yields if
\frac{q^{\prime}r^{\prime}}{q^{\prime}+r^{\prime}}\geq 1
(then
r\leq q^{\prime}
),

\|\Delta\varphi_{\varepsilon}*u^{n}\|_{L^{q^{\prime}}(\Omega)}\leq\|D\varphi_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{\beta_{1}}(\Omega)}\|Du^{n}\|_{L^{r}(\Omega)}\leq C\varepsilon^{-{\beta_{1}}(d+1)+d}\quad\text{with }\beta_{1}:=\frac{q^{\prime}r^{\prime}}{q^{\prime}+r^{\prime}}\geq 1,

and if
\frac{q^{\prime}r^{\prime}}{q^{\prime}+r^{\prime}}<1
(then

),

\|\Delta\varphi_{\varepsilon}*u^{n}\|_{L^{q^{\prime}}(\Omega)}\leq C\|\Delta\varphi_{\varepsilon}*u^{n}\|_{L^{r}(\Omega)}\leq C\|D\varphi_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}\|Du^{n}\|_{L^{r}(\Omega)}\leq C\varepsilon^{-1}.

Thus, overall, we get for
X_{\varepsilon}:=-\nu\Delta\varphi_{\varepsilon}*u^{n}
,

\|X_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{q^{\prime}}(\Omega)}\leq C\nu\varepsilon^{-\beta}\quad\text{ with }\beta=\max\left\{\beta_{1},1\right\}(d+1)-d.

(4.18)

In view of (
4.17
),

\alpha_{\varepsilon}^{n}-{\varphi_{\varepsilon}*\alpha^{n}}/(1+C_{1}\varepsilon)\leq(X_{\varepsilon})_{+}.

Using (
4.6
) and (
4.7
) yields

\displaystyle\iint_{\Omega}F^{*}(x,\alpha^{n}_{\varepsilon})dxdt

\displaystyle\leq\iint_{\Omega}F^{*}(x,\frac{\varphi_{\varepsilon}*\alpha^{n}}{1+C_{1}\varepsilon})+C(X_{\varepsilon})_{+}(1+(\alpha_{\varepsilon}^{n})^{q^{\prime}/q})dxdt

(4.19)

\displaystyle\leq\iint_{\Omega}F^{*}(x,\varphi_{\varepsilon}*\alpha^{n})+C(X_{\varepsilon})_{+}(1+(\varphi_{\varepsilon}*\alpha^{n}+(X_{\varepsilon})_{+})^{\frac{q^{\prime}}{q}})dxdt

\displaystyle\leq\iint_{\Omega}F^{*}(x,\varphi_{\varepsilon}*\alpha^{n})+C(X_{\varepsilon})_{+}^{q^{\prime}}+C(X_{\varepsilon})_{+}(1+(\varphi_{\varepsilon}*\alpha^{n})^{\frac{q^{\prime}}{q}})dxdt.

It follows from (
4.18
), Hölder’s and Young’s convolution inequality inequality that

\displaystyle\iint_{\Omega}(X_{\varepsilon})_{+}^{q^{\prime}}+(X_{\varepsilon})_{+}(1+(\varphi_{\varepsilon}*\alpha^{n})^{\frac{q^{\prime}}{q}})dxdt

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\leq\|X_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{q^{\prime}}(\Omega)}^{q^{\prime}}+\|X_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{q^{\prime}}(\Omega)}\left(\iint_{\Omega}1+(\varphi_{\varepsilon}*\alpha^{n})^{q^{\prime}}dxdt\right)^{1/q}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\leq C\nu^{q^{\prime}}\varepsilon^{-q^{\prime}\beta}+C\nu\varepsilon^{-\beta}\left(1+\|\alpha^{n}\|_{L^{q^{\prime}}(\Omega)}^{q^{\prime}/q}\right)\leq C\nu\varepsilon^{-\beta}

where the last inequality is due to (
4.14
) and
\nu\varepsilon^{-\beta}\leq 1
.
Since
F^{*}(x,\cdot)
is convex, Jensen’s inequality and (

) show

\displaystyle\iint_{\Omega}F^{*}(x,\varphi_{\varepsilon}*\alpha^{n})dxdt

\displaystyle\leq\iint_{\Omega}\varphi_{\varepsilon}*F^{*}(x,\alpha^{n})dxdt+C\varepsilon(1+(\alpha^{n})^{q^{\prime}})dxdt

\displaystyle\leq\iint_{\Omega}F^{*}(x,\alpha^{n})dxdt+C\varepsilon,

where we applied (
4.14
) again.
Using these in (
4.19
) yields

\iint_{\Omega}F^{*}(x,\alpha^{n}_{\varepsilon})dxdt\leq\iint_{\Omega}F^{*}(x,\alpha^{n})dxdt+C\nu\varepsilon^{-\beta}+C\varepsilon.

(4.20)

Note that, since
\bar{m}\in{\mathcal{C}}^{1}
, from (
4.16
) and (
3.5
) we get

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}u^{n}(0,x)\bar{m}(x)-u^{n}_{\varepsilon}(0,x)\bar{m}(x)dx

\displaystyle\leq C\varepsilon+\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}u^{n}(0,x)\bar{m}(x)-u^{n}(0,x)\frac{\varphi_{\varepsilon}*\bar{m}(x)}{1+C_{1}\varepsilon}dx

(4.21)

\displaystyle\leq C\varepsilon\left(1+\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}|u^{n}(0,x)|dx\right)\leq C\varepsilon.

Finally, we recall the definition of
{\mathcal{A}}
and apply (
4.20
) and (

) to get

\displaystyle{\mathcal{A}}(u^{n}_{\varepsilon},\alpha_{\varepsilon}^{n})

\displaystyle=\iint_{\Omega}F^{*}(x,\alpha_{\varepsilon}^{n})dxdt-\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}u^{n}_{\varepsilon}(0,\cdot)\bar{m}dx

\displaystyle\leq\iint_{\Omega}F^{*}(x,\alpha^{n})dxdt-\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}u^{n}(0,\cdot)\bar{m}dx+C\nu\varepsilon^{-\beta}+C\varepsilon

\displaystyle={\mathcal{A}}(u^{n},\alpha^{n})+C\nu\varepsilon^{-\beta}+C\varepsilon.

∎

Now we are ready to prove our main theorem of the section.

Theorem 5.3
.

Assume
(H2)–(H4)(H6)(H7)
and

r\geq\max\left\{2+\frac{d}{d+1},\,\frac{d^{2}}{2d+3}\right\}.

(5.6)

Suppose that

with

), and
(u,m)
is a weak solution to (

\iint_{\Omega}|u_{\nu}-u|^{2}m\,dxdt\leq C\nu^{\frac{1}{2(1+\beta)}},

where

Proof.

The proof is split into four steps.

Step 1
. For any
\nu^{\prime}\in(0,\nu)
, define

Later we will pass
\nu^{\prime}\to 0
. From the first equation in (

), it follows that that

\displaystyle-\partial_{t}U-\nu\Delta u_{\nu}+\nu^{\prime}\Delta u_{\nu^{\prime}}+H(x,Du_{\nu})-H(x,Du_{\nu^{\prime}})=f(x,m_{\nu})-f(x,m_{\nu^{\prime}}).

(5.7)

Since
(u_{\nu},m_{\nu})
and
(u_{\nu^{\prime}},m_{\nu^{\prime}})
are classical solutions, we can multiply (
5.7
) by
-2Um_{\nu^{\prime}}
, and use

as the test function against the equation of
m_{\nu^{\prime}}
. Then adding them up and integrating from
t
to
T

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}U^{2}m_{\nu^{\prime}}dx\,\big|_{t}^{T}-\int_{t}^{T}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}2(\nu Du_{\nu}-\nu^{\prime}Du_{\nu^{\prime}})D(Um_{\nu^{\prime}})+\nu^{\prime}Dm_{\nu^{\prime}}D(U^{2})\,dxdt

(5.8)

\displaystyle=\int_{t}^{T}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}2(H(x,Du_{\nu})-H(x,Du_{\nu^{\prime}}))Um_{\nu^{\prime}}-D_{p}H(x,Du_{\nu^{\prime}})D(U^{2})m_{\nu^{\prime}}

\displaystyle-2(f(x,m_{\nu})-f(x,m_{\nu^{\prime}}))Um_{\nu^{\prime}}\,dxdt.

Step 2
. Since

is convex in

H(x,Du_{\nu})-H(x,Du_{\nu^{\prime}})-D_{p}H(x,Du_{\nu^{\prime}})(DU)\geq 0.

Also using

) yields

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}U^{2}(t,\cdot)m_{\nu^{\prime}}(t,\cdot)dx

\displaystyle\leq\int_{t}^{T}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}2(\nu Du_{\nu}-\nu^{\prime}Du_{\nu^{\prime}})D(Um_{\nu^{\prime}})+\nu^{\prime}Dm_{\nu^{\prime}}D(U^{2})\,dxdt

(5.9)

\displaystyle+2\int_{t}^{T}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}(f(x,m_{\nu})-f(x,m_{\nu^{\prime}}))Um_{\nu^{\prime}}\,dxdt.

Step 3
. Now we estimate each term on the right-hand side of (
5.9
). Recall
\eta,\delta
from Lemma
5.2
(with

since

by (H7)). By Hölder’s inequality,

\int_{t}^{T}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}(Du_{\nu}DU)m_{\nu^{\prime}}dxdt\leq\|Du_{\nu}\|_{L^{r}(\Omega)}\|DU\|_{L^{r}(\Omega)}\|m_{\nu^{\prime}}\|_{L^{r_{1}}(\Omega)}

by (

). Thus, Lemma
5.2
and Proposition
3.3
yield

\int_{t}^{T}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}(Du_{\nu}DU)m_{\nu^{\prime}}dxdt\leq C

for some

\nu,\nu^{\prime}
. Similarly, we have

Due to Lemma
5.2

\int_{t}^{T}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}(Du_{\nu}Dm_{\nu^{\prime}})U\,dxdt\leq\|Du_{\nu}\|_{L^{r}(\Omega)}\|Dm_{\nu^{\prime}}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\|U\|_{L^{r_{2}}(\Omega)}\leq C

by (

). Similarly,

As for

\displaystyle\int_{t}^{T}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}(f(x,m_{\nu})-f(x,m_{\nu^{\prime}}))Um_{\nu^{\prime}}\,dxdt

\displaystyle\leq C\iint_{\Omega}|m_{\nu}-m_{\nu^{\prime}}||U|m_{\nu^{\prime}}dxdt

\displaystyle\leq C\|m_{\nu}-m_{\nu^{\prime}}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\|U\|_{L^{\delta}(\Omega)}\|m_{\nu^{\prime}}\|_{L^{r_{3}}}

. Putting these estimates into (

U
again yield

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\iint_{\Omega}2(H(x,Du_{\nu})-H(x,Du))Um_{\nu}-D_{p}H(x,Du_{\nu})D(U^{2})m_{\nu}dxdt

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=\iint_{\Omega}(H(x,Du)-H(x,Du_{\nu})-D_{p}H(x,Du_{\nu})(Du-Du_{\nu}))Um_{\nu}dxdt

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\leq C\iint_{\Omega}|Du_{\nu}-Du|^{2}m_{\nu}dxdt\leq C\nu^{\frac{1}{1+\beta}},

where in the last inequality, we applied Theorem
4.3
and Lemma
5.1
.

Step 3
. Putting the above estimates together into (
5.11
) yields

\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}U^{2}(t,x)m_{\nu}(t,x)dxdt\leq C\nu+C\nu^{\frac{1}{2(1+\beta)}}+C\|m_{\nu}\|_{\infty}\nu^{\prime}.

Passing
\nu^{\prime}\to 0
yields the conclusion.
∎

6.
Vanishing Viscosity Limit – Nonlocal Coupling

In this section, we discuss mean field games with cost functions that are nonlocal and are regularizing on the set of probability measures. We allow the terminal data of
u_{\nu}
to depend on
m_{\nu}(T,\cdot)
. Consider

\left\{\begin{aligned} &-\partial_{t}u_{\nu}-\nu\Delta u_{\nu}+H(x,Du_{\nu})=f(x,m_{\nu}(t,\cdot)),\\
&\partial_{t}m_{\nu}-\nu\Delta m_{\nu}-\nabla\cdot(m_{\nu}D_{p}H(x,Du_{\nu}))=0,\\
&m_{\nu}(0,x)=\bar{m}(x),\quad u_{\nu}(T,x)=\bar{u}(x,m_{\nu}(T,\cdot)).\end{aligned}\right.

(6.1)

Let
{\mathcal{P}}
be the set of Borel probability measures
\mu
on
{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
. The set
{\mathcal{P}}
can be endowed with the well-known Kantorovitch Rubinstein distance (or
1
-Wasserstein distance), that is for any
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}\in{\mathcal{P}}
,

\mathbf{d}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2}):=\sup_{\gamma\in\Pi(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{2d}}|x-y|d\gamma(x,y)

where
\Pi(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})
denotes all Borel probability measures on
{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
that have
\mu_{1}
as its first marginal and
\mu_{2}
as its second marginal.
We make the following assumptions (see
[
13
,
45
]
). There exists
C>0
such that the following holds.

(H1’)

(Regularizing condition)
f:{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\times{\mathcal{P}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
satisfies for any
\mu\in{\mathcal{P}}
,

\|f(\cdot,\mu)\|_{{\mathcal{C}}^{2}}\leq C,

and for any
x\in{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
and
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}\in{\mathcal{P}}
,

|f(x,\mu_{1})-f(x,\mu_{2})|\leq C\,\mathbf{d}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2}).

Moreover,
f
is strictly monotone in the second variable in the sense that for any
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}\in{\mathcal{P}}
, if
\mu_{1}\neq\mu_{2}
, then

\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}(f(x,\mu_{1})-f(x,\mu_{2}))d(\mu_{1}-\mu_{2})(x)>0.

If
\mu\in{\mathcal{P}}
has a density function
m
, we write

f(x,m):=f(x,\mu)
.

(H2’)

(Conditions on the Hamiltonian) Assume (H2), and that
H=H(x,p)
is
{\mathcal{C}}^{2}
in both variables. Moreover, for all
x\in{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
and
p\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, we have

|H_{x}(x,p)|\leq C(1+|p|^{r}),\quad|H_{p}(x,p)|\leq C(1+|p|^{r-1}).

For any
R>0
, there exists
C_{R}>0
such that for any
(x,p)\in{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
with
|p|\leq R
,

|D_{xx}H(x,p)|,\,|D_{xp}H(x,p)|\leq C_{R}\quad\text{and}\quad 0\leq D_{pp}H(x,p)\leq C_{R}I_{d}.

Moreover,
H
is convex in the second variable in the sense that there exists a non-negative function
c_{1}:{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\to[0,\infty)
such that for all
p,p^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and
x\in{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
,

H(x,p)-H(x,p^{\prime})-H_{p}(x,p)(p-p^{\prime})\geq c_{1}(x)|p-p^{\prime}|^{2}.

(H3’)

(Conditions on the initial and terminal data)
\bar{m}:{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is a
{\mathcal{C}}^{1}
non-negative density function.
\bar{u}:{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\times{\mathcal{P}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
satisfies for any
\mu\in{\mathcal{P}}
,

\|\bar{u}(\cdot,\mu)\|_{{\mathcal{C}}^{2}}\leq C,

and for any
x\in{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
and
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}\in{\mathcal{P}}
,

|\bar{u}(x,\mu_{1})-\bar{u}(x,\mu_{2})|\leq C\,\mathbf{d}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2}).

Moreover, we have the monotonicity condition: For any
m_{1},m_{2}\in{\mathcal{P}}
,

\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}(\bar{u}(x,\mu_{1})-\bar{u}(x,\mu_{2}))d(\mu_{1}-\mu_{2})(x)\geq 0.

If
\mu\in{\mathcal{P}}
has a density function
m
, we write

\bar{u}(x,m):=\bar{u}(x,\mu)
.

Remark 6.1
.

1. It is a classical result (see for example,
[
13
,
45
]
) that under the assumptions of
(H1’)(H2’)(H3’)
, there exists a unique classical solution to (
6.1
) when
\nu>0
. When
\nu=0
, (
6.1
) is still well-posed
[
13
,
45
]
, and the first equation in (
6.1
) is satisfied in the viscosity sense, and the second equation holds in the weak sense. It can be shown that
m_{\nu}(t,\cdot)
is continuous in time with respect to Kantorovich-Rubinstein distance, and so
f(x,m)
is continuous in time.

2. It was known that as
\nu\to 0
, the corresponding classical solutions
(u_{\nu},m_{\nu})
converge uniformly to the unique solution
(u,m)
of (
6.1
) with
\nu=0
(see
[
13
]
).

3. The typical example of
f
(of
\bar{u}
) is

f(x,m)=\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}g(y,(\phi*m)(y))\phi(x-y)dy,

where
\phi:{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is a smooth even kernel and
g:{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is a smooth function such that
g
is strictly increasing in the second variable. Indeed, it is direct to see (see also
[
10
, Example 4.1]
)

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}(f(x,m_{1}(\cdot))-f(x,m_{2}(\cdot)))(m_{1}(x)-m_{2}(x))dx

\displaystyle\qquad=\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}(g(y,(\phi*m_{1})(y))-g(y,(\phi*m_{2})(y)))(\phi*m_{1}(y)-\phi*m_{2}(y))dy\geq 0.

The following regularity results are consequences of (H1’)(H2’)(H3’).

Lemma 6.1
.

There exists
C>0
such that for all
\nu\geq 0
we have

m_{\nu},\,|u_{\nu}|,\,|Du_{\nu}|\leq C\quad\text{ in }\Omega,

(6.2)

\text{and }\quad D^{2}u_{\nu}\leq CI_{d}\quad\text{ in }\Omega.

(6.3)

Proof.

The comparison principle yields that
u_{\nu}
is uniformly finite for all
\nu\geq 0
. The proof for Lipschitz regularity of
u_{\nu}
follows from
[
2
]
(we also refer readers to
[
11
]
and
[
60
]
). Semiconcavity of
u_{\nu}
is given in Theorem 5.3.6
[
11
]
(as
u_{\nu}
is uniformly Lipschitz continuous, by modifying
H(x,p)
for large
p
one can assume that all second derivatives of
H
are uniformly finite).
Finally, from the results in
[
13
, Section 4.2]
, it follows that
m_{\nu}
is uniformly bounded.
∎

Now we prove the first main theorem of the section.

Theorem 6.2
.

Assume
(H1’)(H2’)(H3’)
and let
c_{1}=c_{1}(x)
be from
(H2’)
. For
\nu\in(0,1]
, let
(u_{\nu},m_{\nu})
solve (
6.1
) and
(u,m)
solve (
6.1
) with
\nu=0
. Then there exists
C>0
such that for all
\nu\in(0,1]
we have

\iint_{\Omega}(f(x,m_{\nu})-f(x,m))(m_{\nu}-m)dxdt\leq C\nu^{1/2},

(6.4)

\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}(\bar{u}(x,m_{\nu}(T,\cdot))-\bar{u}(x,m(T,\cdot)))(m_{\nu}(T,\cdot)-m(T,\cdot))dx\leq C\nu^{1/2},

(6.5)

and

\iint_{\Omega}c_{1}(m_{\nu}+m)|Du_{\nu}-Du|^{2}dxdt\leq C\nu^{1/2}.

(6.6)

Proof.

The proof consists of four steps.

Step 1
. We first show that the
W^{1,2}
norm of
\nu^{1/2}m_{\nu}
is uniformly bounded for all
\nu\in(0,1]
. Note that
(u_{\nu},m_{\nu})
is a classical solution
[
13
,
45
]
. So we can multiply the second equation in (
6.1
) by
m_{\nu}
and then integrate over
\Omega
to get

\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{2}}m_{\nu}(T,x)^{2}dx-\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{2}}m_{\nu}(0,x)^{2}dx+\nu\iint_{\Omega}|Dm_{\nu}|^{2}dxdt=-\iint_{\Omega}m_{\nu}D_{p}H(x,Du_{\nu})\cdot Dm_{\nu}dxdt.

Using (
6.2
) yields for some
C
independent of
\nu
,

\nu\iint_{\Omega}|Dm_{\nu}|^{2}dxdt\leq\frac{1}{2}\iint_{\Omega}\nabla\cdot D_{p}H(x,Du_{\nu})\,m_{\nu}^{2}\,dxdt+C.

Note that
m_{\nu},|Du_{\nu}|\leq C
uniformly in
\nu
by (
6.2
). Thus, it follows from (H2’) and (
6.3
) that

\nu\iint_{\Omega}|Dm_{\nu}|^{2}dxdt\leq C\iint_{\Omega}|D_{xp}H(x,Du_{\nu})|+\left[\tr\left(D_{pp}H(x,Du_{\nu})D^{2}u_{\nu}\right)\right]_{+}\,dxdt+C\leq C

(6.7)

for some constant
C
independent of
\nu\in(0,1]
.

Step 2
. Now we take any
\nu^{\prime}\in(0,\nu)
, and define

U(t,x):=u_{\nu}(t,x)-u_{\nu^{\prime}}(t,x),\quad M(t,x):=m_{\nu}(t,x)-m_{\nu^{\prime}}(t,x).

It follows from the equations in (
6.1
) that

\displaystyle-\partial_{t}U-\nu\Delta u_{\nu}+\nu^{\prime}\Delta u_{\nu^{\prime}}+H(x,Du_{\nu})-H(x,Du_{\nu^{\prime}})=f(x,m_{\nu})-f(x,m_{\nu^{\prime}}),

(6.8)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}M-\nu\Delta m_{\nu}+\nu^{\prime}\Delta m_{\nu^{\prime}}-\nabla\cdot(m_{\nu}D_{p}H(x,Du_{\nu}))+\nabla\cdot(m_{\nu^{\prime}}D_{p}H(x,Du_{\nu^{\prime}}))=0.

(6.9)

Let us multiply (
6.8
) by
-M
, and (
6.9
) by
U
, and integrate over
\Omega
. We can do these as
(u_{\nu},m_{\nu})
and
(u_{\nu^{\prime}},m_{\nu^{\prime}})
are classical solutions. Since
M(0,\cdot)=0
, we get

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}U(T,x)M(T,x)dx-\iint_{\Omega}\nu Du_{\nu}DM-\nu^{\prime}Du_{\nu^{\prime}}DM-\nu Dm_{\nu}DU+\nu^{\prime}Dm_{\nu^{\prime}}DU\,dxdt

(6.10)

\displaystyle=\iint_{\Omega}(H(x,Du_{\nu})-H(x,Du_{\nu^{\prime}}))M+(m_{\nu}D_{p}H(x,Du_{\nu})-m_{\nu^{\prime}}D_{p}H(x,Du_{\nu^{\prime}}))DU

\displaystyle-(f(x,m_{\nu})-f(x,m_{\nu^{\prime}}))M\,dxdt

\displaystyle=-\iint_{\Omega}m_{\nu}(H(x,Du_{\nu^{\prime}})-H(x,Du_{\nu})-D_{p}H(x,Du_{\nu})(Du_{\nu^{\prime}}-Du_{\nu}))

\displaystyle+m_{\nu^{\prime}}(H(x,Du_{\nu})-H(x,Du_{\nu^{\prime}})-D_{p}H(x,Du_{\nu^{\prime}})(Du_{\nu}-Du_{\nu^{\prime}}))

\displaystyle+(f(x,m_{\nu})-f(x,m_{\nu^{\prime}}))(m_{\nu}-m_{\nu^{\prime}})\,dxdt

\displaystyle\leq-\iint_{\Omega}c_{1}(x)(m_{\nu}+m_{\nu^{\prime}})|Du_{\nu}-Du_{\nu^{\prime}}|^{2}+(f(x,m_{\nu})-f(x,m_{\nu^{\prime}}))(m_{\nu}-m_{\nu^{\prime}})\,dxdt,

where we used (H2’) in the inequality.

Step 3
. Now we estimate the several terms in (
6.10
). By direct computations,

\displaystyle\iint_{\Omega}\nu Du_{\nu}DM-\nu^{\prime}Du_{\nu^{\prime}}DM-\nu Dm_{\nu}DU+\nu^{\prime}Dm_{\nu^{\prime}}DU\,dxdt

\displaystyle\qquad\quad=\iint_{\Omega}(\nu-\nu^{\prime})(Du_{\nu^{\prime}}Dm_{\nu}-Du_{\nu}Dm_{\nu^{\prime}})dxdt=\iint_{\Omega}(\nu-\nu^{\prime})(Du_{\nu^{\prime}}Dm_{\nu}+\Delta u_{\nu}m_{\nu^{\prime}})dxdt.

By (
6.2
) and (
6.3
),
u_{\nu}
and
u_{\nu^{\prime}}
are uniformly Lipschitz continuous and semiconcave in space, and
m_{\nu^{\prime}}
is uniformly bounded. Combining these with
\nu^{\prime}<\nu
and (
6.7
), we obtain

\displaystyle\iint_{\Omega}\nu Du_{\nu}DM-\nu^{\prime}Du_{\nu^{\prime}}DM-\nu Dm_{\nu}DU+\nu^{\prime}Dm_{\nu^{\prime}}DU\,dxdt

(6.11)

\displaystyle\leq C\nu\iint_{\Omega}|Dm_{\nu}|+m_{\nu^{\prime}}dxdt\leq C\nu\left(\iint_{\Omega}|Dm_{\nu}|^{2}dxdt\right)^{1/2}+C\nu\leq C\nu^{1/2}.

Thus (
6.10
) and (
6.11
) yield

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}U(T,x)M(T,x)dx+\iint_{\Omega}c_{1}(x)(m_{\nu}+m_{\nu^{\prime}})|Du_{\nu}-Du_{\nu^{\prime}}|^{2}dxdt

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+\iint_{\Omega}(f(x,m_{\nu})-f(x,m_{\nu^{\prime}}))(m_{\nu}-m_{\nu^{\prime}})dxdt\leq C\nu^{1/2}

In view of (H2’)(H3’), the three terms on the left-hand side of the above are non-negative. Thus passing
\nu^{\prime}\to 0
yields (
6.4
) and (
6.5
).

Step 4
. Finally, we pass
\nu^{\prime}\to 0
in

\iint_{\Omega}c_{1}(m_{\nu}+m_{\nu^{\prime}})|Du_{\nu}-Du_{\nu^{\prime}}|^{2}dxdt\leq C\nu^{1/2}.

Because
u_{\nu^{\prime}}
converges to
u
locally uniformly and
u_{\nu^{\prime}}
is uniformly semi-concave in
x
for all
\nu^{\prime}\geq 0
,
Du_{\nu^{\prime}}(t,\cdot)
converges to
Du(t,\cdot)
a.e.
x\in{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
. Thus passing
\nu^{\prime}
to
0
yields (
6.6
).
∎

7.
Convergence of
u_{\nu}
– Nonlocal Coupling

In this section, we proceed to show convergence results of
u_{\nu}
as
\nu\to 0
.
First, let us assume a strong condition (H4’) on
f
and
\bar{u}
, and we prove pointwise convergence of
u_{\nu}
to
u
. Later, we also consider a weaker condition (H4”).

(H4’)

There exists
C>0
such that for any
\varepsilon>0
, if
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}\in{\mathcal{P}}
satisfy

\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}(f(x,\mu_{2})-f(x,\mu_{1}))d(\mu_{2}-\mu_{1})(x)\leq\varepsilon,

then

\sup_{x\in{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}|f(x,\mu_{2})-f(x,\mu_{1})|\leq C\varepsilon^{1/2}.

And the same holds if we replace
f
by
\bar{u}
.

Remark 7.1
.

Note that if
f
(or
\bar{u}
) is of the form

f(x,m)=\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}g(y,(\phi*m)(y))\phi(x-y)dy
, where
\phi
is a smooth even function on
{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
and
g
is
{\mathcal{C}}^{1}
on
{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}
,
then the condition reduces to

\text{for all $(x,z)\in{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}$ we have }g_{z}(x,z)>0.

Indeed suppose
m_{1},m_{2}
are two probability density function in
{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
. Since
|\phi*m_{i}|\leq\|\phi\|_{\infty}
, we have

|f(x,m_{2})-f(x,m_{1})|\leq\|\phi\|_{\infty}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}|g(y,\phi*m_{1})-g(y,\phi*m_{2})|dy\leq C\|\phi*m_{1}-\phi*m_{2}\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{T}}^{d})}.

On the other hand, for
c:=\inf_{(x,z)\in{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\times[-\|\phi\|_{\infty},\|\phi\|_{\infty}]}g_{z}(x,z)>0
, we get

\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}(f(x,m_{2})-f(x,m_{1}))(m_{2}-m_{1})dx\geq c\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}|\phi*m_{1}-\phi*m_{2}|^{2}dx.

So there exists
C>0
independent of
m_{1},m_{2}
such that for all
x\in{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
,

|f(x,m_{2})-f(x,m_{1})|^{2}\leq C\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}(f(x,m_{2})-f(x,m_{1}))(m_{2}-m_{1})dx.

We will need the following lemma.

Lemma 7.1
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
, and let
H
be as before, and
g:[0,T]\times{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be
{\mathcal{C}}^{1}
in space and Lipschitz continuous in time. Suppose that
v
and
v_{\varepsilon}
are Lipschitz continuous and satisfy

\partial_{t}v+H(x,Dv)=g(t,x)\quad\text{and}\quad\partial_{t}v_{\varepsilon}-\varepsilon\Delta v_{\varepsilon}+H(x,Dv_{\varepsilon})=g(t,x)

in the sense of viscosity, and
v(0,\cdot)=v_{\varepsilon}(0,\cdot)
is a
{\mathcal{C}}^{2}
function. Then there exists
C>0
such that for all
\varepsilon>0
,

|v(t,x)-v_{\varepsilon}(t,x)|\leq C\varepsilon^{1/2}\quad\text{in }[0,T]\times{\mathbb{T}}^{d}.

Though the proof is given by the classical viscosity solution approach (see e.g.,
[
21
]
for the case when
H=H(p)
and
g\equiv 0
), for readers’ convenience, we provide it in the appendix.

Theorem 7.2
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
6.2
, assume
(H4’)
. Then there exists
C>0
such that for all
\nu\in(0,1]
we have

\sup_{(t,x)\in[0,T]\times{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}|u(t,\cdot)-u_{\nu}(t,\cdot)|\leq C\nu^{1/4}.

Proof.

For
\nu\in(0,1]
, let
w_{\nu}
be the unique solution to

-\partial_{t}w_{\nu}-\nu\Delta w_{\nu}+H(x,Dw_{\nu})=f(x,m)\quad\text{ with }w_{\nu}(T,x)=\bar{u}(x,m(T,\cdot)).

The condition (H4’) and Theorem
6.2
imply that

|f(x,m)-f(x,m_{\nu})|,\quad|w_{\nu}(T,x)-u_{\nu}(T,x)|=|\bar{u}(x,m(T,\cdot))-\bar{u}(x,m_{\mu}(T,\cdot))|\leq C\nu^{1/4}.

Thus by comparing
u_{\nu}
with
w_{\nu}\pm C\nu^{1/4}(T-t+1)
yields that

|u_{\nu}-w_{\nu}|\leq C\nu^{1/4}\quad\text{ in }\Omega.

Note
u
satisfies

-\partial_{t}u+H(x,Du)=f(x,m)\quad\text{ with }w(T,x)=\bar{u}(x,m(T,\cdot)),

in the sense of distribution, and
u
is also a viscosity solution by
[
39
]
.
Moreover, by
[
13
, Lemma 4.14]
and (H1’),

f(x,m)
is Lipschitz continuous in time. Then it follows from Lemma
7.1
that

\sup_{(t,x)\in\Omega}|w_{\nu}(t,x)-u(t,x)|\leq C\nu^{1/2},

which finishes the proof.
∎

Now we consider a weaker condition:

(H4”)

There exists
C>0
such that for any
\varepsilon>0
, if
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}\in{\mathcal{P}}
satisfy

\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}(f(x,\mu_{2})-f(x,\mu_{1}))d(\mu_{2}-\mu_{1})(x)\leq\varepsilon,

then

\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}|f(x,\mu_{2})-f(x,\mu_{1})|dx\leq C\varepsilon^{1/2}.

And the same holds if we replace
f
by
\bar{u}
.

Since, under (H4”), it is only known that
f(x,m(t,\cdot))
and
f(x,m_{\nu}(t,\cdot))
are close in average from Theorem
6.2
, we do not expect a pointwise strong convergence of
u_{\nu}
to
u
. We will apply a dual equation method to prove an
L^{\infty}_{t}L^{1}_{x}
convergence (see also
[

]
).

Theorem 7.3
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
6.2
, assume
(H4”)
. Then there exists
C>0
such that for all
\nu\in(0,1]
we have

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|u(t,\cdot)-u_{\nu}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{1}({\mathbb{T}}^{d})}\leq C\nu^{1/4}.

Proof.

For
\nu\in(0,1]
, let
w_{\nu}
be the unique solution to

-\partial_{t}w_{\nu}-\nu\Delta w_{\nu}+H(x,Dw_{\nu})=f(x,m)\quad\text{ with }w_{\nu}(T,x)=\bar{u}(x,m(T,\cdot)).

By Lemma
6.1
, there exists
C>0
such that for all
\nu\in(0,1]
,

|Du|,\,|Du_{\nu}|,\,|w_{\nu}|\leq C\quad\text{and}\quad D^{2}u_{\nu},D^{2}w\leq CI_{d}\quad\text{in }\Omega.

(7.1)

We will compare
u_{\nu}
with
w_{\nu}
and then
w_{\nu}
with
u
.

Step 1
. Consider
W:=w_{\nu}-u_{\nu}
, which then satisfies

-\partial_{t}W-\nu\Delta W+G\cdot DW=f(x,m)-f(x,m_{\nu})

(7.2)

with

W(T,x)=w_{\nu}(T,x)-u_{\nu}(T,x)=\bar{u}(x,m(T,\cdot))-\bar{u}(x,m_{\nu}(T,\cdot)),

and

G(t,x):=\int_{0}^{1}D_{p}H(x,sDw_{\nu}+(1-s)Du_{\nu})ds.

Recall that

-\partial_{t}u_{\nu}-\nu\Delta u_{\nu}+H(x,Du_{\nu})=f(x,m_{\nu})\quad\text{ with }w_{\nu}(T,x)=\bar{u}(x,m_{\nu}(T,\cdot)).

By (H4”) and Theorem
6.2
,

\displaystyle\iint_{\Omega}|f(x,m)-f(x,m_{\nu})|dxdt

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{t}C\left(\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}(f(x,m)-f(x,m_{\nu}))(m-m_{\nu})dx\right)^{1/2}dt

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\iint_{\Omega}(f(x,m)-f(x,m_{\nu}))(m-m_{\nu})dxdt\right)^{1/2}\leq C\nu^{1/4}.

Similarly, by (
6.5
) of Theorem
6.2

\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}|\bar{u}(x,m(T,\cdot))-\bar{u}(x,m_{\nu}(T,\cdot))|dx\leq C\nu^{1/4}.

(7.4)

Step 2
. For any fixed
t_{1}\in[0,T)
, we consider the dual equation of (

) in
{[t_{1},T]\times{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}
:

\psi_{t}-\nu\Delta\psi-\nabla\cdot(G\psi)=0\quad\text{with }\psi(t_{1},\cdot)=\psi_{0},

where
\psi_{0}
is a smooth function on
{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
. By Divergence Theorem, we have

\frac{d}{dt}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}W\psi dx=-\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}(f(x,m)-f(x,m_{\nu}))\psi dx.

In view of (
7.3
) and (
7.4
), integrating the above equality in the time interval
[t_{1},T]
yields

\displaystyle\left|\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}W(t_{1},x)\psi_{0}(x)dx\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}W(T,x)\psi(T,x)dx\right|+C\nu^{1/4}\|\psi\|_{L^{\infty}({[t_{1},T]\times{\mathbb{T}}^{d}})}

(7.5)

\displaystyle\leq C\nu^{1/4}\|\psi\|_{L^{\infty}({[t_{1},T]\times{\mathbb{T}}^{d}})}.

Step 3
. Now, we estimate
\|\psi\|_{L^{\infty}({[t_{1},T]\times{\mathbb{T}}^{d}})}
in terms of
\|\psi_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}}^{d})}
. It follows from the equation of
\psi
that for any
n\geq 2
an even number,

\frac{d}{dt}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}\psi^{n}dx+n(n-1)\nu\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}|D\psi|^{2}\psi^{n-2}dx=-n(n-1)\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}(G\cdot D\psi)\,\psi^{n-1}.

So for any
t\in[t_{1},T]
,

\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}\psi(t,x)^{n}dx\leq\|\psi_{0}\|_{\infty}^{n}+(n-1)\int_{t_{1}}^{t}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}(\nabla\cdot G(s,x))\psi^{n}(s,x)dxds.

This yields that if
\nabla\cdot G(s,x)
is uniformly bounded from above for all
\nu
, then by Gronwall’s inequality,

\|\psi(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{n}({\mathbb{T}}^{d})}\leq\|\psi_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}}^{d})}\exp\left(\|(\nabla\cdot G)_{+}\|_{L^{\infty}({[t_{1},T]\times{\mathbb{T}}^{d}})}(t-t_{1})\right).

Passing
n\to\infty
yields

\|\psi\|_{L^{\infty}({[t_{1},T]\times{\mathbb{T}}^{d}})}\leq\|\psi_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}}^{d})}\exp\left(\|(\nabla\cdot G)_{+}\|_{L^{\infty}({[t_{1},T]\times{\mathbb{T}}^{d}})}T\right).

Applying this in (
7.5
), we get for some
C>0
,

\left|\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}W(t_{1},x)\psi_{0}(x)dx\right|\leq C\nu^{1/4}\|\psi_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}}^{d})}

holds for all smooth
\psi_{0}
, which implies that

\|w_{\nu}(t_{1},\cdot)-u_{\nu}(t_{1},\cdot)\|_{L^{1}({\mathbb{T}}^{d})}\leq C\nu^{1/4}\quad\text{ for any }t_{1}\in[0,T).

(7.6)

Step 4
. To finish the proof of (
7.6
), we now show that
\nabla\cdot G(t,x)
is uniformly bounded from above. Indeed we have

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot G

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\sum_{i}\frac{\partial^{2}H}{\partial p_{i}\partial x_{i}}+\left(s\sum_{i,j}\frac{\partial^{2}H}{\partial p_{i}\partial p_{j}}\frac{\partial^{2}w_{\nu}}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}+(1-s)\sum_{i,j}\frac{\partial^{2}H}{\partial p_{i}\partial p_{j}}\frac{\partial^{2}u_{\nu}}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}\right)ds.

By (
7.1
) and (H2’), there exists
C>0
such that for all
\nu,s\in[0,1]
,

\frac{\partial^{2}H}{\partial p_{i}\partial x_{i}}(x,sDw_{\nu}+(1-s)Du_{\nu})\leq C,

\frac{\partial^{2}H}{\partial p_{i}\partial p_{j}},\quad\frac{\partial^{2}w_{\nu}}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}},\quad\frac{\partial^{2}u_{\nu}}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}\leq C.

Therefore, we obtain that
\nabla\cdot G\leq C
for some
C
independent of
\nu
.

Step 5
. Due to (
7.6
), to conclude the theorem, it suffices to show that

\sup_{(t,x)\in\Omega}|w_{\nu}(t,x)-u(t,x)|\leq C\nu^{1/2}.

This is a consequence of Lemma
7.1
(see also the proof of Theorem
7.2
).
∎

8.
Open problems

In this section, we collect a few open problems related to the convergence of vanishing viscosity approximations for MFGs
and its connection to the KPZ equation.

(1)

In Theorem
4.3
, we proved a rate of
\nu^{\frac{1}{2(1+\beta)}}
for the convergence of
(m_{\nu},Du_{\nu})
in some Sobolev norm
assuming that
H(\cdot,p)
grows as
|p|^{r}
, and
f(\cdot,m)
grows as
m^{r-1}
.
We know that if
\frac{1}{q}+\frac{1}{r}\leq 1
, this rate is
\nu^{\frac{1}{4}}
which is independent of the dimension
d
;
while
\frac{1}{q}+\frac{1}{r}>1
, the exponent
\frac{1}{2(1+\beta)}\asymp d^{-1}
.
Is the rate
\nu^{\frac{1}{2(1+\beta)}}
is tight,
or does the threshold
\frac{1}{q}+\frac{1}{r}=1
induces a phase transition in
the dimension dependence of the rate exponent of vanishing viscosity approximations for MFGs?

(2)

In Theorems
4.3
,
5.3
and
5.4
, we proved the convergence (rate) of
(m_{\nu},u_{\nu})
in
L^{2}
norm.
For the (1+1)-dimensional KPZ equation, it is known that under narrow wedge initial condition,

u_{\nu}
converges locally uniformly but without rate.

(a)

Does the convergence still hold in a stronger sense, e.g. locally uniformly for
u_{\nu}
,
and do we get the same convergence rate?

(b)

Can we relax the condition
q=2
, (
5.6
) and
r=2
in these theorems?

In Theorem
5.4
, we proved the convergence of
u_{\nu}
in
L^{2}
norm weighted by
m_{\nu}
.
Can we prove the same rate in
L^{2}
norm weighted by
m
(and further locally uniformly)?

(3)

For the
(1+1)
-dimensional KPZ equation, Theorem
4.3
implies that

\rho_{\nu}
converges in
L^{2}
norm with a rate
\nu^{\frac{1}{4}}
;
Theorem
5.4
shows that
h_{\nu}
converges in a weighted
L^{2}
norm with rate
\nu^{\frac{1}{8}}
.
This relies on the Cole-Hopf transform from the SHE to the KPZ equation, which underlies the weak noise theory.
Is there a higher dimensional weak noise theory to connect large deviations of the KPZ equation to
vanishing viscosity for MFGs?

(4)

There are also a few directions to extend this work from a PDE perspective.
For instance,

(a)

Is it possible to generalize our results to non-compact domains (e.g.
\mathbb{R}^{d}
)?

(b)

In the local coupling setting, can we allow the terminal data
u(T,\cdot)
to depend on
m(T,\cdot)
pointwise?

(c)

What is the convergence rate of vanishing viscosity approximations for kinetic MFGs (see
[
34
]
)?

We hope that our convergence analysis of vanishing viscosity approximations for MFGs may trigger further research
connecting the KPZ equation and MFGs in dimension
d\geq 2
,
and on the dimension effect as
d\to\infty
.
