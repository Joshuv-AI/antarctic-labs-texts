Abstract
. For a family of infinite-dimensional diffusions with degenerate noise, we develop a modified Γ \Gamma calculus on finite-dimensional projections of the equation in order to produce explicit functional inequalities that can be scaled to infinite dimensions. The choice of our Γ \Gamma operator appears canonical in our context, as the estimates depend only on the induced control distance. We apply the general analysis to a number of examples, exploring implications for quasi-invariance and uniqueness of stationary distributions.

1.
Introduction

Understanding when measures on infinite-dimensional spaces possess smoothness properties reminiscent of those on
\mathbf{R}^{n}
is a fundamental problem in the theory of diffusion processes. In the finite-dimensional context of
\mathbf{R}^{n}
, Hörmander’s bracket generating condition
[
35
]
provides the criteria to determine when the law of the diffusion with smooth
(C^{\infty})
coefficients has a smooth density with respect to Lebesgue measure. At the level of the associated diffusion, Hörmander’s condition translates to how the
external
randomness present in the equation propagates
internally
to produce a distribution with
C^{\infty}
density. Thus, provided there is sufficient randomness in the equation to ensure the needed propagation, the existence and smoothness of the density follows from Hörmander’s result.

While the mantra of
sufficient noise implies smoothness of the law
applies readily in the finite-dimensional setting via Hörmander’s classical hypoellipticity result, the story in infinite dimensions is more nuanced. For example, even in relatively simple settings when noise acts on every basis direction in an infinite-dimensional Hilbert space, which in particular acts as a
natural
phase space for the stochastic solution, the laws of two solutions started from close initial conditions may be mutually singular at any fixed time
t>0
. This, in turn, implies that the associated Markov semigroup is not strong Feller
[
32
, Example 3.15]
. Furthermore, while Hörmander’s condition in finite dimensions allows for the development of regularity theory for the corresponding PDEs even in the absence of ellipticity, such PDE techniques are not available in infinite dimensions. For example, even in the context of an infinite-dimensional Brownian motion, the classical Harnack inequality enjoyed by similar, non-degenerate finite-dimensional processes fails to hold
[
4
]
.

Because of the prevalence of degenerate noises in applications and these nuances, understanding what hypoellipticity means for infinite-dimensional diffusions continues to be an active area of research. On the one hand, significant progress has been made in this direction in the context of fluid mechanics. Indeed, building off of the pioneering works on unique ergodicity of stochastically-forced PDEs
[
14
,
16
,
24
,
37
]
as well as the known behavior of the finite-dimensional Galerkin approximations
[
23
,
46
]
, Hairer and Mattingly introduced the notion of
asymptotic strong Feller property
in order to prove unique ergodicity of the two-dimensional Navier–Stokes equations on the period box under highly degenerate stochastic forcing
[
32
]
. This property was further developed and investigated in the works
[
33
,
31
,
41
]
. In this context, there is just enough smoothing
at time infinity
, as defined by the asymptotic strong Feller property, to conclude uniqueness of steady states. We refer also to the work
[
25
]
which studies the Boussinesq equations under degenerate stochastic forcing, validating the asymptotic strong Feller property in that context.

On the other hand, smoothness of measures in infinite dimensions can be understood as quasi-invariance under transformations such as translations. This allows for the definition of smoothness even in the absence of a natural reference measure such as Lebesgue measure. This point of view was pioneered by Malliavin in
[
40
]
leading to development of Malliavin calculus. More relevant to the current paper is the connection between hypoellipticity in infinite dimensions to quasi-invariance properties and their relation to classical functional inequalities, especially in the context of heat kernel measures on infinite-dimensional Heisenberg-like groups
[
6
,
5
,
4
,
20
,
21
,
28
]
. In these settings, the noise structure in the equations is different than in the fluid models above, as the driving external randomness is infinite-dimensional as opposed to acting on a few low frequencies in Fourier space. Furthermore, techniques from Dirichlet forms, classical Cameron-Martin-Girsanov theorem and
\Gamma
calculus are often employed in place of Harris’ theorem and Malliavin calculus in the fluids setting. We refer also to
[
17
,
26
]
for related work.

The goal of this paper is to make progress on understanding the meaning of hypoellipticity in infinite-dimensions by developing a modified
\Gamma
calculus. We build off of understanding from previous work in
[
9
,
12
,
5
,
7
]
. Specifically, we design a modified
\Gamma
operator in order to obtain classical functional inequalities, e.g. Wang–Harnack and reverse log–Sobolev, for a class of infinite-dimensional diffusions arising as solutions to certain stochastic differential equations. These are generalizations of the Kolmogorov diffusion studied in
[
9
,
12
,
5
,
7
]
. An important contribution of this work is that the choice of our modified
\Gamma
operator appears canonical, as the constants in the functional inequality bounds are independent of both the noise and spatial dimension for finite-dimensional projections of the equation. However, the dependence on these parameters is intrinsic in the induced distance, which we show can be estimated in a variety of examples. The existence of a natural notion of distance here is notable, as previously there has been no geometry in which to work with this class of distributions. This is in contrast to other hypoelliptic models (like the Heisenberg group) where there is a natural geometric framework coming from the sub-Riemannian distance. For this class of diffusions, we also study large-time properties when the structure allows for it, developing a criteria for mutual absolute continuity of stationary distributions for the associated Markov semigroup. This is done by using the deduced Wang-Harnack type inequality in finite dimensions, and scaling it appropriately to infinite dimensions.

The organization of this paper is as follows. In
Section

2
, we introduce the finite-dimensional setting which will later be scaled to infinite dimensions in
Section

5
. In
Section

3
, we develop our modified
\Gamma
calculus in the context of the finite-dimensional setting. In particular, we derive our choice of
\Gamma
operator and deduce a number of functional inequalities based on this choice. In
Section

4
, we estimate the control distance associated to our choice of
\Gamma
operator in several concrete examples. Based on the derivations of these functional inequalities, the control distance is the only term one has left to estimate to produce fully explicit estimates. Finally in
Section

5
, we scale the functional inequalities and the finite-dimensional setting to infinite dimensions, obtaining criteria for quasi-invariance as well as mutual absolute continuity of invariant probability measures. At the end of
Section

5
, we revisit some of the examples discussed in
Section

4
, applying the results obtained in this section.

2.
The finite-dimensional setting

2.1.
The main equation and hypoellipticity

Let
\mathcal{B}
denote the Borel sigma field of subsets of
\mathbf{R}^{n}
. In the finite-dimensional setting, we consider the following stochastic differential equation (SDE) on
\mathbf{R}^{n}

(2.1)

\displaystyle dx_{t}=Ax_{t}\,dt+\sigma\,dB_{t},

where
A
and
\sigma
are
n\times n
real matrices, and
B_{t}
is a standard,
n
-dimensional Brownian motion defined on a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbf{P},\mathbf{E})
. For all initial conditions
x\in\mathbf{R}^{n}
, equation (
2.1
) has a unique pathwise solution
x_{t}(x)
, defined for all times
t\geqslant 0
, which can be explicitly written as

(2.2)

\displaystyle x_{t}(x)=e^{tA}x+\int_{0}^{t}e^{(t-s)A}\sigma dB_{s}.

Unless we must emphasize the initial condition, we will write
x_{t}
as shorthand notation for a generic solution of (
2.1
).

Solutions of (
2.1
) are Markovian and we let
\{P_{t}\}_{t\geqslant 0}
denote the corresponding Markov semigroup. We recall that
\{P_{t}\}_{t\geqslant 0}
acts on bounded,
\mathcal{B}
-measurable functions
f:\mathbf{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbf{R}
by

\displaystyle P_{t}f(x):=\mathbf{E}f(x_{t}(x)),\,\,t\geqslant 0,

and acts dually on a probability measure
\nu
on
\left(\mathbf{R}^{n},\mathcal{B}\right)
via

\displaystyle\nu P_{t}(A):=\int_{\mathbf{R}^{n}}\nu(dx)P_{t}\mathbf{1}_{A}(x),\,\,\,\,t\geqslant 0,\,\,A\in\mathcal{B}.

A probability measure
\nu
on
\left(\mathbf{R}^{n},\mathcal{B}\right)
is called a
stationary distribution
if
\nu P_{t}=\nu
for all
t\geqslant 0
.
For
x\in\mathbf{R}^{n}
,
t\geqslant 0
and
A\in\mathcal{B}
, we let

\displaystyle P_{t}(x,A):=P_{t}\mathbf{1}_{A}(x)=\mathbf{P}\{x_{t}(x)\in A\}

denote the
Markov transition probability
associated to
\{P_{t}\}_{t\geqslant 0}
. Throughout,
L
will denote the following second-order operator

(2.3)

\displaystyle L=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(Ax)_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}(\sigma\sigma^{\ast})_{ij}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}.

Note that
L
corresponds to the action of the infinitesimal generator of
\{P_{t}\}_{t\geqslant 0}
on a domain of sufficiently smooth functions, e.g.
C^{2}
functions
f:\mathbf{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbf{R}
with compact support. We offer the slight abuse of terminology and refer to
L
as the generator of the Markov process
x_{t}
throughout.

We are interested in the case when the noise in equation (
2.1
) is
degenerate
, i.e.
\text{rank}(\sigma)<n
, but the process
x_{t}
has a transition probability density function
p_{t}(x,y)
respect to Lebesgue measure which is
C^{\infty}
for all
(t,x,y)\in(0,\infty)\times\mathbf{R}^{n}\times\mathbf{R}^{n}
. When
\text{rank}(\sigma)<n
, the existence and smoothness of the transition density, which will be referred to throughout as
hypoellipticity
, is not immediate precisely because the noise is degenerate. However, in this context, hypoellipticity can be established under further conditions on the interaction between
A
and
\sigma
in essentially two ways. The first and perhaps most utilized way is to apply Hörmander’s hypoellipticity theorem
[
35
]
(see also
[
48
]
). However, Hörmander’s result is more powerful than needed in the context (
2.1
). For our purposes, a more direct way is to verify the
Kalman rank condition

[
36
]
(see
1
below) and show that this condition implies hypoellipticity. The Kalman rank condition is usually employed to ensure controllability of the resulting ordinary differential equation (ODE) when the independent Brownian motions in (
2.1
) are replaced by deterministic controls.

Assumption 1
.

The
Kalman rank condition
is satisfied; that is, if we define the matrix

\displaystyle A_{\sigma}:=\begin{bmatrix}\sigma&A\sigma&A^{2}\sigma&\ldots&A^{n-1}\sigma\end{bmatrix},

then

\displaystyle\text{rank}(A_{\sigma})=n.

Consider the mean
m_{t}(x)
and covariance
\Sigma_{t}
of the process (
2.2
) given by

(2.4)

\displaystyle m_{t}(x)=e^{tA}x\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\Sigma_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}e^{(t-s)A}\sigma\sigma^{\ast}e^{(t-s)A^{\ast}}\,ds.

A short argument (see
2.1
below) shows that
1
implies that
\Sigma_{t}
as in (
2.4
) is invertible for all
t>0
. This in turn implies that the process
x_{t}
is multivariate Gaussian with an explicit transition density given by

(2.5)

\displaystyle p_{t}(x,y)=\frac{1}{\det(2\pi\Sigma_{t})^{1/2}}\exp\big(-\tfrac{1}{2}\langle\Sigma_{t}^{-1}(y-m_{t}(x)),y-m_{t}(x)\rangle\big),\,\,\,x,y\in\mathbf{R}^{n},\,t>0,

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the standard inner product on
\mathbf{R}^{n}
. Smoothness of
p_{t}(x,y)
for
(t,x,y)\in(0,\infty)\times\mathbf{R}^{n}\times\mathbf{R}^{n}
can then be readily verified from (
2.4
) and (
2.5
). It should be noted that the proof of
2.1
is Malliavin’s probabilistic proof of Hörmander’s theorem
[
38
,
39
,
44
]
in the simplified context (
2.1
).

Lemma 2.1
.

Suppose that
1
is satisfied. Then for every
t>0
,
\Sigma_{t}
in (
2.4
) is invertible.

Proof.

Fix
t>0
and suppose that there exists
x\in\mathbf{R}^{n}
such that
\langle\Sigma_{t}x,x\rangle=0
. This implies

\displaystyle 0=\langle\Sigma_{t}x,x\rangle=\int_{0}^{t}|\sigma^{\ast}e^{sA^{\ast}}x|^{2}\,ds.

Hence, by continuity,
\sigma^{\ast}e^{sA^{\ast}}x=0
for all
s\in[0,t]
. Differentiating
\sigma^{\ast}e^{sA^{\ast}}x
repeatedly with respect to
s
and evaluating at
s=0
implies that

\displaystyle\sigma^{\ast}(A^{\ast})^{m}x=0\text{ for all }m=0,1,2,\ldots

Note by
1
this implies
x=0
since the rank of the matrix and its transpose are the same. ∎

2.2.
Elements of Bakry-Émery calculus

In
Section

3
, we provide a framework for establishing classical functional inequalities in the finite-dimensional hypoelliptic setting (
2.1
), where the constants in the bounds are independent of the spatial dimension
n
. Importantly, we employ a modification of the Bakry-Émery calculus
[
1
]
by proposing a
natural
gradient associated to the system (
2.1
) which plays the role analogous to the usual carré du champ in the uniformly elliptic setting, e.g when
\text{rank}(\sigma)=n
in equation (
2.1
)
[
1
]
. Here, we recall some of the basic elements of the Bakry-Émery calculus.

To the generator
L
as in (
2.3
), we associate the
carré du champ

\Gamma
and its
iterate

\Gamma_{2}
, which
for
f,g\in C^{\infty}(\mathbf{R}^{n};\mathbf{R})
are defined by

\displaystyle\Gamma(f,g):=\tfrac{1}{2}L(fg)-

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{2}gLf-\tfrac{1}{2}fLg,\qquad\Gamma(f):=\Gamma(f,f),

\displaystyle\Gamma_{2}(f)

\displaystyle:=\tfrac{1}{2}L\Gamma(f)-\Gamma(f,Lf).

Importantly,
\Gamma
and
\Gamma_{2}
arise naturally in relation to the Markov semigroup
\{P_{t}\}_{t\geqslant 0}
. If, for example, the Markov process
x_{t}
has a stationary distribution
\nu
and
f:\mathbf{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbf{R}
is bounded measurable, then a formal calculation using stationarity of
\nu
gives

(2.6)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\|P_{t}f\|_{L^{2}(\nu)}^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbf{R}^{n}}(P_{t}f)LP_{t}f\,d\nu=-\int_{\mathbf{R}^{n}}\Gamma(P_{t}f)\,d\nu,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\mathbf{R}^{n}}\Gamma(P_{t}f)\,d\nu

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbf{R}^{n}}\Gamma(LP_{t}f,P_{t}f)\,d\nu=-\int_{\mathbf{R}^{n}}\Gamma_{2}(P_{t}f)\,d\nu.

In particular, the forms
\Gamma,\Gamma_{2}
are the resulting objects that arise from iterating time with respect to the norm
\|\cdot\|_{L^{2}(\nu)}
. This calculation has been exploited in several situations in order to study convergence to equilibrium for SDEs with an explicit stationary distribution
\nu

[
9
,
1
,
12
,
19
,
34
,
30
,
49
]
. Such a function was also used to prove a logarithmic Sobolev inequality in
[
2
]
, and this approach has been employed to prove different functional inequalities as described in the monograph
[
3
]
.

Crucially in what follows, we employ a generalization of
\Gamma
and
\Gamma_{2}
. Recalling that
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the standard inner product on
\mathbf{R}^{n}
, for any
n\times n
real matrix
G
and
f,g\in C^{\infty}(\mathbf{R}^{n};\mathbf{R})
, we introduce the notation

(2.7)

\displaystyle\Gamma^{G}(f,g)=\langle G\nabla f,\nabla g\rangle,\qquad\Gamma^{G}(f)=\Gamma^{G}(f,f),

(2.8)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{2}^{G}(f)=\frac{1}{2}L\Gamma^{G}(f)-\Gamma^{G}(f,Lf).

We see that
\Gamma^{G}
is a generalization of
\Gamma
since

\displaystyle\Gamma(f,g)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\ell,m=1}^{n}(\sigma\sigma^{\ast})_{\ell m}\partial_{\ell}f\partial_{m}g=\Gamma^{\tfrac{\sigma\sigma^{\ast}}{2}}(f,g).

Similar functionals with a time-independent
G
were considered in
[
8
,
43
]
. Below, we find a convenient way to define a time-dependent,
natural

G
associated to
x_{t}
from which the desired functional inequalities can be obtained and such that the constants in the bounds of these inequalities are independent of the dimension
n
. Although we will not always need to assume it,
G
should be thought of as symmetric, positive-definite so that
\Gamma^{G}(f)
is equivalent to
|\nabla f|^{2}
, and so can be interpreted as changing the underlying metric.

3.
Functional inequalities by modified gradients

The goal of this section is to construct an appropriate, time-dependent matrix
G
so that, by using
\Gamma^{G}
and
\Gamma_{2}^{G}
defined in (
2.7
) and (
2.8
), we arrive at various functional inequalities where the constants in the inequalities are independent of the spatial dimension
n
. We will see that by using the framework outlined below, we arrive at a natural choice for
G
. Note that a similar strategy was employed using a diagonal matrix in the setting of the Kolmogorov diffusion in
[
5
,
13
]
. Our construction holds in the more general setting (
2.1
) for a convenient symmetric positive-definite matrix
G
. We also remark that a similar construction was used in the paper
[
42
]
in the context of chain of oscillators, but the matrix used there is not time-dependent. We finally point out
[
27
]
where dimension dependent Hardy-Littlewood-Sobolev inequalities for similar types of operators are obtained using heat kernel estimates.

We begin by computing
\Gamma_{2}^{G}
for a general, symmetric matrix
G
. Here and in what follows, all matrices below are assumed to be spatially constant.

Proposition 3.1
.

Suppose that
G
is an
n\times n
symmetric matrix. Then for all
f\in C^{\infty}(\mathbf{R}^{n};\mathbf{R})
, we have the formula

(3.1)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{2}^{G}(f)=-\Gamma^{AG}(f)+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\ell}\Gamma^{G}((\sigma^{\ast}\nabla f)_{\ell}).

If we furthermore assume that
G
is nonnegative-definite, then

(3.2)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{2}^{G}(f)\geqslant-\Gamma^{AG}(f)

for all
f\in C^{\infty}(\mathbf{R}^{n};\mathbf{R})
.

Proof.

Let
f\in C^{\infty}(\mathbf{R}^{n};\mathbf{R})
.
Standard calculations using symmetry of
G
give

\displaystyle L\Gamma^{G}(f)

\displaystyle=2\Gamma^{G}(f,Lf)-2\Gamma^{AG}(f)+\sum_{\ell,m}(\sigma\sigma^{\ast})_{\ell m}\langle G\nabla(\partial_{\ell}f),\nabla(\partial_{m}f)\rangle.

Relation (
3.1
) then follows using matrix arithmetic on the last term on the right-hand side above. Under the additional assumption that
G
is nonnegative-definite, the inequality (
3.2
) follows immediately from (
3.1
).
∎

To obtain a gradient estimate using a time-dependent matrix
G
in
\Gamma^{G}
, we fix a horizon time
t>0
, and
f\in L^{\infty}(\mathbf{R}^{n};\mathbf{R})
and consider the functional

(3.3)

\displaystyle\phi(s):=P_{s}\Gamma^{G(s)}(P_{t-s}f),\,\,\,s\in[0,t),

for a differentiable matrix
s\mapsto G(s)
on
[0,t]
which is
n\times n
, symmetric. Below, we see that the expression (
3.3
) plays a role similar to the
L^{2}(\nu)
norm in (
2.6
). The following lemma yields the expression which will, in turn, lead to the definition of a convenient
G
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Fix
t>0
. Suppose that
1
is satisfied and that
s\mapsto G(s)\in C^{1}([0,t];M_{n\times n})
is symmetric and nonegative-definite on
[0,t]
. For any
f\in L^{\infty}(\mathbf{R}^{n};\mathbf{R})
, let
\phi
be as in (
3.3
). Then
\phi
is defined and continuously differentiable on
(0,t)
and, moreover, for
s\in(0,t)
we have

(3.4)

\displaystyle\phi^{\prime}(s)=P_{s}2\Gamma_{2}^{G(s)}(P_{t-s}f)+P_{s}\Gamma^{G^{\prime}(s)}(P_{t-s}f)\geqslant P_{s}\Gamma^{G^{\prime}(s)-2AG(s)}(P_{t-s}f).

Remark 3.3
.

The fact that
\phi
as in (
3.3
) makes sense, is continuously differentiable on
(0,t)
and has
nice
properties permitting the calculations leading to the proof of
3.2
follows from
1
and the explicit representation

(3.5)

\displaystyle P_{t}f(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\det(2\pi\Sigma_{t})^{1/2}}\int_{\mathbf{R}^{n}}f(y)\exp\big(-\tfrac{1}{2}\langle\Sigma_{t}^{-1}(y-m_{t}(x)),y-m_{t}(x)\rangle\big)\,dy

(3.6)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}f(\Sigma_{t}^{1/2}Z+m_{t}(x)),

where
t>0
,
m_{t}(x)
and
\Sigma_{t}
are as in (
2.4
), and
Z
is the standard normal distribution on
\mathbf{R}^{n}
. Recalling that the covariance matrix
\Sigma_{t}
is invertible for
t>0
under
1
by
2.1
, it follows from (
3.5
) that the semigroup
\{P_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
is strong Feller and Mehler. Furthermore, one can use formula (
3.5
) to explicitly find derivatives of
P_{t}f(x)
, provided
t>0
, to fully justify the regularity of
\phi
on
(0,t)
and the remaining derivative calculations in the proof of
3.2
. Later we will also use formula (
3.6
) to do similar calculations under further regularity hypotheses on the test function
f
.

Proof of
3.2
.

Following
3.3
, we observe that for
s\in(0,t)
and
f\in L^{\infty}(\mathbf{R}^{n};\mathbf{R})
, symmetry of
G(s)
implies

\displaystyle\phi^{\prime}(s)

\displaystyle=P_{s}L\Gamma^{G(s)}(P_{t-s}f)-2P_{s}\Gamma^{G(s)}(P_{t-s}f,LP_{t-s}f)+P_{s}\Gamma^{G^{\prime}(s)}(P_{t-s}f)

\displaystyle=P_{s}2\Gamma_{2}^{G(s)}(P_{t-s}f)+P_{s}\Gamma^{G^{\prime}(s)}(P_{t-s}f).

The bound in the result follows after applying
3.1
.
∎

3.1.
Picking
G
based on
3.2

There are many workable choices for the matrix
G(s)
corresponding to the dynamics (
2.1
). Given the bound in relation (
3.4
), we argue that the choice given in the remainder of this section is canonical. In particular, fixing a horizon time
t>0
, we define the matrix-valued function
s\mapsto G(s,t)
on
[0,t]
by

(3.7)

\displaystyle G(s,t)=\int_{s}^{t}e^{(s-v)A}\sigma\sigma^{\ast}e^{(s-v)A^{\ast}}\,dv.

Note that this matrix satisfies the backward matrix-valued ODE

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{s}G(s,t)=-\sigma\sigma^{\ast}+AG(s,t)+G(s,t)A^{\ast},\,s\in[0,t]&\\
G(t,t)=0.&\end{cases}

By symmetry of
G(s,t)
, for any
x\in\mathbf{R}^{n}
we have that

\displaystyle\langle\partial_{s}G(s,t)x,x\rangle=-\langle\sigma\sigma^{\ast}x,x\rangle+\langle 2AG(s,t)x,x\rangle.

We observe that picking
G
in this way allows us to replace the right-hand side of the bound in (
3.4
) with

\displaystyle-P_{s}\Gamma^{\sigma\sigma^{\ast}}(P_{t-s}f)=-2P_{s}\Gamma(P_{t-s}f)=-\frac{d}{ds}P_{s}(P_{t-s}f)^{2}.

As a consequence, we produce a reverse Poincaré-type inequality/gradient bound for the semigroup associated to the modified operator
\Gamma^{G}
.

Proposition 3.4

(Reverse Poincaré-type inequality)
.

Let
t>0
and
s\mapsto G(s,t)
be as in (
3.7
) and suppose that
1
is satisfied. Then for all
f\in L^{\infty}(\mathbf{R}^{n};\mathbf{R})
we have the explicit bound

(3.8)

\displaystyle\Gamma^{G(0,t)}(P_{t}f)\leqslant P_{t}f^{2}-(P_{t}f)^{2}.

Remark 3.5
.

By a nearly identical argument to the one used in
2.1
, under
1
, the matrix
G(0,t)
is invertible for
t>0
. Thus the modified gradient on the lefthand side of (
3.8
) is comparable to the usual gradient.

Proof of Proposition
3.4
.

By
1
and a density argument, it suffices to prove estimate (
3.8
) for
f\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbf{R}^{n};\mathbf{R})
where
C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbf{R}^{n};\mathbf{R})
denotes the space of smooth real-valued functions with compact support in
\mathbf{R}^{n}
. If we let
\phi(s)
be as in (
3.3
) with
G(s):=G(s,t)
, then
3.2
implies

(3.9)

\displaystyle\phi^{\prime}(s)

\displaystyle=2P_{s}\Gamma_{2}^{G(s)}(P_{t-s}f)+P_{s}\Gamma^{G^{\prime}(s)}(P_{t-s}f)\geqslant-2P_{s}\Gamma(P_{t-s}f)

for
s\in(0,t)
.
Next, notice that for
s\in(0,t)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{ds}P_{s}(P_{t-s}f)^{2}=2P_{s}\Gamma(P_{t-s}f).

Thus for
\epsilon>0
sufficiently small, integrating both sides of (
3.9
) from
\epsilon
to
t-\epsilon
we arrive at the bound

\displaystyle P_{\epsilon}\Gamma^{G(\epsilon,t)}(P_{t-\epsilon}f)-P_{t-\epsilon}\Gamma^{G(t-\epsilon,t)}(P_{\epsilon}f)\leqslant P_{t-\epsilon}(P_{\epsilon}f)^{2}-P_{\epsilon}(P_{t-\epsilon}f)^{2}.

In order to pass to the limit as
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
in the above to arrive at (
3.8
) for
f\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbf{R}^{n};\mathbf{R})
, it remains to show that

\displaystyle P_{t-\epsilon}\Gamma^{G(t-\epsilon,t)}(P_{\epsilon}f)\rightarrow 0\text{ as }\epsilon\rightarrow 0.

Using (
3.6
) for
t=\epsilon
, we find that

\displaystyle\Gamma^{G(t-\epsilon,t)}(P_{\epsilon}f)=\langle G(t-\epsilon,t)\mathbf{E}e^{\epsilon A^{\ast}}\nabla f(\Sigma_{\epsilon}^{1/2}Z+m_{\epsilon}(x)),\mathbf{E}e^{\epsilon A^{\ast}}\nabla f(\Sigma_{\epsilon}^{1/2}Z+m_{\epsilon}(x))\rangle

is both uniformly bounded in
\epsilon
and converges to
0
as
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
. This now establishes the bound (
3.8
) for
f\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbf{R}^{n};\mathbf{R})
, finishing the proof.
∎

3.2.
Reverse log-Sobolev and Wang-Harnack inequalities

We now see that a similar calculation to the one used to prove the reverse Poincaré inequality in
Section

3.1
can also be used to establish a reverse log Sobolev inequality and the Wang-Harnack inequality.

To prove the log-Sobolev inequality, for computational ease, we introduce the spaces
C^{\infty}_{\epsilon}(\mathbf{R}^{n};[0,\infty))
,
\epsilon>0
, defined by

\displaystyle C_{\epsilon}^{\infty}(\mathbf{R}^{n};[0,\infty)):=\{f\,:\,f=\varphi+\epsilon,\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbf{R}^{n};[0,\infty))\}.

Here we recall again that
C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbf{R}^{n};[0,\infty))
denotes the space of smooth functions
\varphi:\mathbf{R}^{n}\rightarrow[0,\infty)
with compact support in
\mathbf{R}^{n}
.
Similar to
3.2
, we need the following computation.

Proposition 3.6
.

Fix
t,\epsilon>0
and suppose that
s\mapsto G(s)\in C^{1}([0,t];M_{n\times n})
is symmetric for any
s\in[0,t]
and that
1
is satisfied. For
f\in C_{\epsilon}^{\infty}(\mathbf{R}^{n};[0,\infty))
and
s\in(0,t)
, define

(3.11)

\displaystyle\psi(s):=P_{s}[(P_{t-s}f)\Gamma^{G(s)}(\log P_{t-s}f)].

Then

\displaystyle\psi^{\prime}(s)

\displaystyle=2P_{s}[(P_{t-s}f)\Gamma_{2}^{G(s)}(\log P_{t-s}f)]+P_{s}[(P_{t-s}f)\Gamma^{G^{\prime}(s)}(\log P_{t-s}f)].

Proof.

Let
t,\epsilon>0
and
f\in C_{\epsilon}^{\infty}(\mathbf{R}^{n};[0,\infty))
. Observe that for
s\in(0,t)

\displaystyle\psi^{\prime}(s)

\displaystyle=P_{s}L[(P_{t-s}f)\Gamma^{G(s)}(\log P_{t-s}f)]-P_{s}[L(P_{t-s}f)\Gamma^{G(s)}(\log P_{t-s}f)]

(3.12)

\displaystyle\qquad+P_{s}[(P_{t-s}f)\frac{d}{ds}(\Gamma^{G(s)}(\log P_{t-s}f))].

Next, notice that

\displaystyle\frac{d}{ds}(\Gamma^{G(s)}(\log P_{t-s}f))

\displaystyle=\Gamma^{G^{\prime}(s)}(\log P_{t-s}f)-2\Gamma^{G(s)}(LP_{t-s}f/P_{t-s}f,\log P_{t-s}f)

(3.13)

\displaystyle=\Gamma^{G^{\prime}(s)}(\log P_{t-s}f)-2\Gamma^{G(s)}(L(\log P_{t-s}f),\log P_{t-s}f)

\displaystyle\qquad-2\Gamma^{G(s)}(\Gamma(\log P_{t-s}f),\log P_{t-s}f).

Combining (
3.12
) with (
3.13
) gives

\displaystyle\psi^{\prime}(s)

\displaystyle=P_{s}L[(P_{t-s}f)\Gamma^{G(s)}(\log P_{t-s}f)]-P_{s}[L(P_{t-s}f)\Gamma^{G(s)}(\log P_{t-s}f)]

\displaystyle\qquad+P_{s}[(P_{t-s}f)\Gamma^{G^{\prime}(s)}(\log P_{t-s}f)]

\displaystyle\qquad-2P_{s}[(P_{t-s}f)\Gamma^{G(s)}(L(\log P_{t-s}f),\log P_{t-s}f)]

\displaystyle\qquad-2P_{s}[(P_{t-s}f)\Gamma^{G(s)}(\Gamma(\log P_{t-s}f),\log P_{t-s}f)]

(3.14)

\displaystyle=2P_{s}[(P_{t-s}f)\Gamma^{G(s)}_{2}(\log P_{t-s}f)]+2P_{s}[\Gamma(P_{t-s}f,\Gamma^{G(s)}(\log P_{t-s}f))]

\displaystyle\qquad+P_{s}[(P_{t-s}f)\Gamma^{G^{\prime}(s)}(\log P_{t-s}f)]-2P_{s}[(P_{t-s}f)\Gamma^{G(s)}(\Gamma(\log P_{t-s}f),\log P_{t-s}f)].

Now observe that if
B=\sigma\sigma^{\ast}/2
, we have

\displaystyle\Gamma(P_{t-s}f,\Gamma^{G(s)}(\log P_{t-s}f))

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell,m}B_{m\ell}\partial_{\ell}(P_{t-s}f)\partial_{m}(G\nabla(\log P_{t-s}f)\cdot\nabla(\log P_{t-s}f))

\displaystyle=2\sum_{\ell,m,j,k}B_{m\ell}\partial_{\ell}(P_{t-s}f)G_{jk}\partial_{km}^{2}(\log P_{t-s}f)\partial_{j}(\log P_{t-s}f)

and

\displaystyle(P_{t-s}f)\Gamma^{G(s)}(\Gamma(\log P_{t-s}f),\log P_{t-s}f)

\displaystyle=(P_{t-s}f)\sum_{j,k}G_{jk}\partial_{k}(\Gamma(\log P_{t-s}f))\partial_{j}(\log P_{t-s}f)

\displaystyle=2(P_{t-s}f)\sum_{\ell,m,j,k}G_{jk}B_{m\ell}\partial_{km}^{2}(\log P_{t-s}f)\partial_{\ell}(\log P_{t-s}f))\partial_{j}(\log P_{t-s}f)

\displaystyle=2\sum_{\ell,m,j,k}B_{m\ell}\partial_{\ell}(P_{t-s}f)G_{jk}\partial_{km}^{2}(\log P_{t-s}f)\partial_{j}(\log P_{t-s}f).

The result now follows after considering (
3.14
) since we just proved that

\displaystyle 2P_{s}[\Gamma(P_{t-s}f,\Gamma^{G(s)}(\log P_{t-s}f))]=2P_{s}[(P_{t-s}f)\Gamma^{G(s)}(\Gamma(\log P_{t-s}f),\log P_{t-s}f)].

∎

As a corollary of the previous computation, we obtain the following result. Below, we extend
x\mapsto x\log x
to
[0,\infty)
by defining it to be zero at
x=0
.

Corollary 3.7

(Reverse log Sobolev inequality)
.

Fix
t>0
, suppose
1
is satisfied and that
s\mapsto G(s,t)
is as in (
3.7
) . Then we have the following reverse log Sobolev inequality

(3.15)

\displaystyle(P_{t}f)\Gamma^{G_{t}}(\log P_{t}f)\leqslant 2P_{t}(f\log f)-2(P_{t}f)\log P_{t}f,

which is satisfied for all
f\in L^{\infty}(\mathbf{R}^{n};[0,\infty))
which are not identically zero.

Proof.

Fix
t,\epsilon>0
. We first prove the bound for
f\in C_{\epsilon}^{\infty}(\mathbf{R}^{n};[0,\infty))
. First note that if
\psi
is given by (
3.11
) with
G(s):=G(s,t)
where
G(s,t)
is as in (
3.7
), by
3.6
we have for
s\in(0,t)

\displaystyle\psi^{\prime}(s)

\displaystyle=2P_{s}[(P_{t-s}f)\Gamma_{2}^{G(s,t)}(\log P_{t-s}f)]+P_{s}[(P_{t-s}f)\Gamma^{\partial_{s}G(s,t)}(\log P_{t-s}f)]

\displaystyle\geqslant-2P_{s}[(P_{t-s}f)\Gamma(\log P_{t-s}f)]=-\frac{d}{ds}2P_{s}[(P_{t-s}f)\log P_{t-s}f].

For
\delta>0
small, integrating the previous inequality from
\delta
to
t-\delta
produces

\displaystyle P_{\delta}[(P_{t-\delta}f)\Gamma^{G(\delta,t)}(\log P_{t-\delta}f)]-\ P_{t-\delta}[(P_{\delta}f)\Gamma^{G(t-\delta,t)}(\log P_{\delta}f)]

\displaystyle\leqslant 2P_{t-\delta}[(P_{\delta}f)\log P_{\delta}f]-2P_{\delta}[(P_{t-\delta}f)\log P_{t-\delta}f].

Applying a line of reasoning similar to the one used in the proof of
3.6
, taking
\delta\rightarrow 0
gives the desired estimate (
3.15
) in the case when
f\in C_{\epsilon}^{\infty}(\mathbf{R}^{n};[0,\infty))
. Taking
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
, we obtain the bound for
f\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbf{R}^{n};[0,\infty))
which are not identically zero. Density arguments give the claimed bound for all
f\in L^{\infty}(\mathbf{R}^{n};[0,\infty))
which are not identically zero.
∎

Next, we turn our attention to establishing a Wang-Harnack inequality. Later, this will be used to prove a quasi-invariance result when we allow the spatial dimension
n\rightarrow\infty
. To setup the statement of the result, suppose that
K
is
n\times n
symmetric, positive-definite. We call a curve
\gamma\in C^{1}([0,T];\mathbf{R}^{n})

subunit
for
K
if for all
f\in C^{1}(\mathbf{R}^{n};\mathbf{R})

\displaystyle\bigg|\frac{d}{ds}f(\gamma(s))\bigg|\leqslant\sqrt{(\Gamma^{K}f)(\gamma(s))}.

For
x,y\in\mathbf{R}^{n}
and
t>0
, let

\displaystyle S_{T}^{K}(x,y):=\{\gamma:[0,T]\rightarrow\mathbf{R}^{n}\text{ subunit for }K\text{ and }\,\gamma(0)=x,\gamma(T)=y\}

and define the
control distance
between
x,y\in\mathbf{R}^{n}
associated to
K
:

\displaystyle\rho^{K}(x,y):=\inf\{T>0\,:\,S_{T}^{K}(x,y)\text{ nonempty}\}.

Note that, in our simplified setting of
\mathbf{R}^{n}
, a curve
\gamma\in C^{1}([0,T];\mathbf{R}^{n})
is subunit for
K
if and only if for every
t\in[0,T]
,
\|K^{-1/2}\gamma^{\prime}(t)\|\leq 1
and we therefore have

(3.16)

\displaystyle\rho^{K}(x,y)=\|K^{-1/2}(x-y)\|=\sqrt{\langle x-y,K^{-1}(x-y)\rangle}.

For simplicity, when
1
is satisfied, we use the notation

(3.17)

\displaystyle\rho_{t}(x,y):=\rho^{G_{t}}(x,y)

to denote the control distance associated to the matrix
G_{t}
as in (
3.10
).

Theorem 3.8

(Wang-Harnack inequality)
.

Fix
t>0
,
\alpha>1
and suppose that
1
is satisfied. Then we have the estimate

(3.18)

\displaystyle(P_{t}f(x))^{\alpha}\leqslant P_{t}f^{\alpha}(y)\exp\bigg(\frac{\alpha\rho_{t}^{2}(x,y)}{2(\alpha-1)}\bigg).

for all
x,y\in\mathbf{R}^{n}
and all
f\in L^{\infty}(\mathbf{R}^{n};[0,\infty))
.

Proof.

We use a similar proof as in
[
10
, Proposition 3.4]
. By standard approximation arguments, we may suppose without loss of generality that
f\in C_{\epsilon}^{\infty}(\mathbf{R}^{n};[0,\infty))
for some
\epsilon>0
. Let
x,y\in\mathbf{R}^{n}
. Since
G_{t}
is strictly positive-definite by
2.1
, there exists
T>0
and a curve
\gamma\in S_{T}^{G_{t}}(x,y)
. Let
\ell:[0,T]\rightarrow\mathbf{R}
be given by
\ell(s)=1+(\alpha-1)\tfrac{s}{T}
and set

\displaystyle\psi(s)=\frac{\alpha}{\ell(s)}\log P_{t}f^{\ell(s)}(\gamma(s)).

Then applying
3.7
and using subuniticity of
\gamma
, we find that

\displaystyle\psi^{\prime}(s)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha(\alpha-1)}{T\ell^{2}(s)}\frac{P_{t}f^{\ell(s)}\log f^{\ell(s)}(\gamma(s))-\log P_{t}f^{\ell(s)}(\gamma(s))P_{t}f^{\ell(s)}(\gamma(s))}{P_{t}f^{\ell(s)}(\gamma(s))}

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{\alpha}{\ell(s)}\frac{\nabla(P_{t}f^{\ell(s)})(\gamma(s))\cdot\gamma^{\prime}(s)}{P_{t}f^{\ell(s)}(\gamma(s))}

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{\alpha(\alpha-1)}{2T\ell^{2}(s)}\Gamma^{G_{t}}(\log P_{t}f^{\ell(s)}(\gamma(s)))-\frac{\alpha}{\ell(s)}\sqrt{\Gamma^{G_{t}}(\log P_{t}f^{\ell(s)}(\gamma(s)))}.

Now for every
\lambda>0
,

-\sqrt{\Gamma^{G_{t}}(\log P_{t}f^{\ell(s)}(\gamma(s)))}\geqslant-\frac{1}{2\lambda^{2}}\Gamma^{G_{t}}(\log P_{t}f^{\ell(s)}(\gamma(s)))-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}.

By choosing
\lambda^{2}=\frac{T\ell(s)}{(\alpha-1)}
we thus obtain

\displaystyle\psi^{\prime}(s)\geqslant-\frac{\alpha}{2(\alpha-1)}T

Integrating the inequality above from
0
to
T
we find that

\displaystyle\alpha\log P_{t}f(x)-\log P_{t}f^{\alpha}(y)\leqslant\frac{\alpha T^{2}}{2(\alpha-1)}.

Exponentiating both sides of the inequality above and optimizing over all such subunit curves
\gamma
produces the claimed estimate when
f\in C_{\epsilon}^{\infty}(\mathbf{R}^{n};[0,\infty))
, finishing the proof.
∎

Using similar computations to those for Corollary
3.7
and Theorem
3.8
, we can also obtain a bound on the total variation distance between the measures
P_{t}(x,\,\cdot\,)
and
P_{t}(y,\,\cdot\,)
for

using the control distance
\rho_{t}
.

Corollary 3.9
.

Let
t>0
and suppose that
1
is satisfied. Then we have

\displaystyle\|P_{t}(x,\,\cdot\,)-P_{t}(y,\,\cdot\,)\|_{TV}\leqslant\rho_{t}(x,y).

for all
x,y\in\mathbf{R}^{n}
.

Proof.

Let
f\in L^{\infty}(\mathbf{R}^{n})
with
\|f\|_{L^{\infty}}\leqslant 1
and observe that by
3.4
we have, for some
\xi\in\mathbf{R}^{n}
,

\displaystyle|P_{t}f(x)-P_{t}f(y)|=|\nabla(P_{t}f)(\xi)\cdot(x-y)|

\displaystyle=|G_{t}^{1/2}\nabla(P_{t}f)(\xi)\cdot G_{t}^{-1/2}(x-y)|

\displaystyle\leqslant\sqrt{\Gamma^{G_{t}}(P_{t}f)(\xi)}\rho_{t}(x,y)\leqslant\rho_{t}(x,y),

where in the final inequality we used (

) and

.
∎

3.3.
Transportation cost inequalities

Corollary
3.9
can be improved by using the Wasserstein(-Kantorovich-Rubinstein) distance in addition to the total variation distance. Indeed, functional inequalities like in Proposition
3.4
and
3.7
imply transportation cost inequalities as in
[
11
]
.

First recall the definitions of the Hellinger and
2
-Wasserstein distances. Denote by
\mathcal{P}\left(\mathbf{R}^{n}\right)
the set of Borel probability measures on
\mathbf{R}^{n}
and by
\mathcal{P}^{2}\left(\mathbf{R}^{n}\right)\subset\mathcal{P}\left(\mathbf{R}^{n}\right)
the space of probability measures having a finite second moment.

For
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}^{2}\left(\mathbf{R}^{n}\right)
the
2
-Wasserstein distance

W_{2}
is defined by

W_{2}\left(\mu,\nu\right)^{2}:=\inf\int_{\mathbf{R}^{n}\times\mathbf{R}^{n}}|x-y|^{2}\pi\left(dx,dy\right),

where the infimum is taken over all couplings
\pi\in\mathcal{P}\left(\mathbf{R}^{n}\times\mathbf{R}^{n}\right)
with marginals
\mu,\nu
.

We will also consider the
2
-Wasserstein distance

W^{\rho_{t}}_{2}
for the control distance associated to the matrix
G_{t}
as in (
3.10
), which is defined by

W^{\rho_{t}}_{2}\left(\mu,\nu\right)^{2}:=\inf\int_{\mathbf{R}^{n}\times\mathbf{R}^{n}}\rho_{t}(x,y)^{2}\pi\left(dx,dy\right),

where the infimum is taken over all couplings
\pi\in\mathcal{P}\left(\mathbf{R}^{n}\times\mathbf{R}^{n}\right)
with marginals
\mu,\nu
. Letting

(3.19)

\displaystyle\lambda(t):=\inf_{|x|=1}\langle G_{t}x,x\rangle,

we observe that
1
implies
\lambda(t)>0
for all
t>0
. Furthermore,
Note that

W^{\rho_{t}}_{2}\left(\mu,\nu\right)^{2}\leq\frac{1}{\lambda(t)}W_{2}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})^{2},\qquad\mu_{0},\mu_{1}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{R}^{n}).

The
2
-Hellinger distance

\mathsf{He}_{2}
is defined by

\mathsf{He}_{2}^{2}\left(\mu,\nu\right):=\int_{\mathbf{R}^{n}}\left(\sqrt{\frac{d\mu}{dm}}-\sqrt{\frac{d\nu}{dm}}\right)^{2}\,dm

where
m
is any measure that
\mu,\nu
are both absolutely continuous with respect to (for example,
m=\mu+\nu
). The definition of
\mathsf{He}_{2}
is independent of
m
. Convergence in the Hellinger distance is equivalent to convergence in total variation, and
\mathsf{He}_{2}^{2}\left(\mu,\nu\right)\leqslant 2
for all
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}\left(\mathbf{R}^{n}\right)
, with
\mathsf{He}_{2}^{2}\left(\mu,\nu\right)=2
if and only if
\mu
and
\nu
are mutually singular.

Proposition 3.10
.

Let
t>0
and suppose that
1
is satisfied. Then, we have

\mathsf{He}_{2}(\mu_{0}P_{t},\mu_{1}P_{t})^{2}\leqslant\frac{1}{4}W^{\rho_{t}}_{2}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})^{2},\qquad\mu_{0},\mu_{1}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{R}^{n}).

Proof.

This follows from Proposition
3.4
and
[
11
, Theorem 3.7]
.
∎

Let
\mathrm{Lip}_{b}(\mathbf{R}^{n})
denote the space of all bounded Lipschitz functions on
\mathbf{R}^{n}
, and for
a,b\geqslant 0
, let
\mathcal{E}_{a,b}
denote the class of all

positive
functions
\varphi\in C^{1}([0,1],\mathrm{Lip}_{b}(\mathbf{R}^{n}))
,
bounded and bounded away from
0
, satisfying the
differential inequality

\partial_{s}\varphi_{s}+a\varphi_{s}\Gamma^{G(t)}(\ln\varphi_{s})+b\varphi_{s}\ln\varphi_{s}\leqslant 0.

For probability measures
\mu_{0},\mu_{1}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{R}^{n})
, we define

T_{a,b}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1}):=\sup_{\varphi\in\mathcal{E}_{a,b}}\left[\int_{\mathbf{R}^{n}}\varphi_{1}\,d\mu_{1}-\int_{\mathbf{R}^{n}}\varphi_{0}\,d\mu_{0}\right].

Provided
b>0
, note by
[
11
, Proposition 5.11]
we have

T_{0,b}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})=\begin{cases}C_{b}\int_{\mathbf{R}^{n}}\left(\frac{d\mu_{1}}{d\mu_{0}}\right)^{\frac{e^{b}}{e^{b}-1}}\,d\mu_{0},&\mu_{1}\ll\mu_{0}\\
\infty,&\mu_{1}\not\ll\mu_{0}\end{cases}

where
C_{b}=\frac{e^{b}-1}{e^{be^{b}}}
.

Theorem 3.11
.

Let
t>0
and suppose that
1
is satisfied. Then

(3.20)

T_{0,\kappa/\lambda(t)}(\mu_{0}P_{t},\mu_{1}P_{t})\leqslant T_{\kappa,\kappa/\lambda(t)}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1}),\qquad\mu_{0},\mu_{1}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{R}^{n}),\quad\kappa>0.

In particular, for every
x,y\in\mathbf{R}^{n}
and
t>0

\int_{\mathbf{R}^{n}}\left(\frac{p_{t}(x,z)}{p_{t}(y,z)}\right)^{1/(p-1)}p_{t}(x,z)\,dz\leqslant\exp\left(\frac{p}{(p-1)^{2}}\frac{\rho_{t}(x,y)^{2}}{2}\right),\qquad p>1.

Proof.

The entropic transportation inequality (
3.20
) follows from Corollary
3.7
and
[
11
, Theorem 5.15]
since we have Wang-Harnack inequality (
3.18
) for any

(P_{t}f(x))^{p}\leqslant C^{p}P_{t}f^{p}(y),

with

C:=\exp\left(\frac{\rho_{t}^{2}(x,y)}{2(p-1)}\right).

As was observed in different settings in
[
22
, Lemma D1]
,
[
6
, Lemma 2.11]
and
[
29
, Proposition 4.1]
, this is equivalent to the integrated Harnack inequality with

:

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbf{R}^{n}}\left(\frac{p_{t}(x,z)}{p_{t}(y,z)}\right)^{p^{\prime}}p_{t}(x,z)\,dz\leqslant C^{p^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\frac{p^{\prime}\rho_{t}^{2}(x,y)}{2(p-1)}\right)=\exp\left(\frac{p}{(p-1)^{2}}\frac{\rho_{t}^{2}(x,y)}{2}\right).

∎

Remark 3.12
.

We now observe that

implies a quasi invariance result (see also

]
). Relying on
[

, Lemma 5.10]
which says that if for

we have

, then

3.4.
The iterate
G_{2}
of
G

Before proceeding onto concrete applications of the matrix
G(s,t)
as in (
3.7
), in this section, we discuss the
iterate

G_{2}
of this matrix. From a practical standpoint, the iterate
G_{2}
allows further stochastic mixing while retaining some structure similar to
G_{t}
. In particular in some applications, it will allows us to more easily control the smallest positive eigenvalue
\lambda(t)
of the matrix
G_{t}
introduced in (
3.19
).

Fixing
t>0
, we define the
iterate

s\mapsto G_{2}(s,t)
of the matrix (
3.7
) on the interval
[0,t]
by the formula

\displaystyle G_{2}(s,t):=\int_{s}^{t}e^{(s-v)A}G(v,t)e^{(s-v)A^{\ast}}\,dv.

Observe that
s\mapsto G_{2}(s,t)
is the unique solution of the backwards ODE

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{s}G_{2}(s,t)=-G(s,t)+AG_{2}(s,t)+G_{2}(s,t)A^{\ast}\\
G_{2}(t,t)=0.\end{cases}

Although we could re-do the Bakry-Émery calculus used in this section with
G_{2}(s,t)
in place of
G(s,t)
to produce similar functional inequalities, we find it more expedient to compare their respective spectra. In particular, our main result in this section is
3.13
below.

To this end, for
t>0
, let
\lambda_{2}(t):=\inf_{|x|=1}\langle G_{2}(0,t)x,x\rangle
be the smallest eigenvalue of the matrix
G_{2,t}:=G_{2}(0,t)
. We have the following:

Proposition 3.13
.

For
t>s>0
, we have the bound

(3.21)

\displaystyle\lambda(t)\geqslant\frac{\lambda_{2}(t)}{t}\geqslant\frac{\lambda(t-s)}{t}\inf_{|x|=1}\int_{0}^{s}|e^{-vA^{\ast}}x|^{2}\,dv.

Proof.

Fix
t>0
and
x\in\mathbf{R}^{n}
. We first prove the inequality
\lambda(t)\geqslant\lambda_{2}(t)/t
. Observe that

\displaystyle\langle G_{2,t}x,x\rangle=\int_{0}^{t}\langle G(v,t)e^{-vA^{\ast}}x,e^{-vA^{\ast}}x\rangle\,dv

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{v}^{t}|\sigma^{\ast}e^{(v-w)A^{\ast}}e^{-vA^{\ast}}x|^{2}\,dwdv

\displaystyle\leqslant\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{t}|\sigma^{\ast}e^{-wA^{\ast}}x|^{2}\,dw\,dv=t\langle G_{t}x,x\rangle.

Taking the infimum over
x\in\mathbf{R}^{n}
with
|x|=1
implies
\lambda(t)\geqslant\lambda_{2}(t)/t
.

For the remaining bound, fix
s>0
with
s<t
. For
x\in\mathbf{R}^{n}
, since
G(v,t)
is nonnegative, we have that

\displaystyle\langle G_{2,t}x,x\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\langle G(v,t)e^{-vA^{\ast}}x,e^{-vA^{\ast}}x\rangle\,dv

(3.22)

\displaystyle\geqslant\int_{0}^{s}\langle G(v,t)e^{-vA^{\ast}}x,e^{-vA^{\ast}}x\rangle\,dv.

Now, for
v\in[0,s]
we have for any
x\in\mathbf{R}^{n}

(3.23)

\displaystyle\langle G(v,t)x,x\rangle=\int_{v}^{t}|\sigma^{\ast}e^{(v-w)A^{\ast}}x|^{2}\,dw=\int_{0}^{t-v}|\sigma^{\ast}e^{-wA^{\ast}}x|^{2}\,dw\geqslant\lambda(t-s)|x|^{2}.

Combining (
3.22
) with (
3.23
) produces

\displaystyle\langle G_{2,t}x,x\rangle\geqslant\lambda(t-s)\int_{0}^{s}|e^{-vA^{\ast}}x|^{2}\,dv,

which implies the remaining inequality.
∎

Remark 3.14
.

The bound (
3.21
) allows to utilize the underlying dynamics driven by the ODE
\dot{x}=-A^{\ast}x
to lower bound
\lambda(t)
. In practice, dissipative dynamics for the system
\dot{x}=A^{\ast}x
leads to explosive dynamics for the time-reversed system
\dot{x}=-A^{\ast}x
. In such cases, it is often easy to show explicitly that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{s}|e^{-vA^{\ast}}x|^{2}\,dv

grows exponentially fast as
s\rightarrow\infty
. We refer the reader to
Section

4.2.3
for a concrete example.

4.
Estimating the distance
\rho_{t}
in examples

The goal of this section is to bound the control distance
\rho_{t}
as in (
3.17
) from above in a number of conrete examples in the form (
2.1
) where the noise is degenerate but
1
is satisfied. Of particular importance will be to produce explicit estimates depending on key parameters in the specific system. Looking ahead to the following section, how the estimates depend on the spatial dimension parameter will be critical as we consider an infinite-dimensional class of examples related to the examples treated in this section.

First, in
Section

4.1
, we outline the general form of each example and use it to deduce a basic uppers bounds for
\rho_{t}
in terms of the
underlying matrices
defined below. Importantly, the matrices
A
and
\sigma
will be written in tensored form from which some simplifications can be deduced. Then, in
Section

4.2
, we consider four, specific examples where we estimate
\rho_{t}
using the underlying matrices via
4.1
below.

4.1.
Kronecker product form of the examples

Letting
j,k\in\mathbf{N}
,
I=I_{k\times k}
denote the
k\times k
identity matrix and
Q
denote a
k\times k
, symmetric strictly positive-definite matrix, throughout this section
A
and
\sigma
are
n\times n
matrices,
n=jk
, of the following form

(4.1)

\displaystyle A=\begin{bmatrix}a_{11}I&a_{12}I&\ldots&a_{1j}I\\
a_{21}I&a_{22}I&\ldots&a_{2j}I\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ldots&\vdots\\
a_{j1}I&a_{j2}I&\ldots&a_{jj}I\end{bmatrix}\,\,\,\,\,\text{ and }\,\,\,\,\,\sigma=\begin{bmatrix}\sigma_{11}Q^{1/2}&\sigma_{12}Q^{1/2}&\ldots&\sigma_{1j}Q^{1/2}\\
\sigma_{21}Q^{1/2}&\sigma_{22}Q^{1/2}&\ldots&\sigma_{2j}Q^{1/2}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ldots&\vdots\\
\sigma_{j1}Q^{1/2}&\sigma_{j2}Q^{1/2}&\ldots&\sigma_{jj}Q^{1/2}\end{bmatrix}.

To
A
and
\sigma
, we associate
j\times j
real matrices
\underline{A}
and
\underline{\sigma}
, called the
underlying matrices
corresponding to
A
and
\sigma
, given by

\displaystyle\underline{A}=\begin{bmatrix}a_{11}&a_{12}&\ldots&a_{1j}\\
a_{21}&a_{22}&\ldots&a_{2j}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ldots&\vdots\\
a_{j1}&a_{j2}&\ldots&a_{jj}\end{bmatrix}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\underline{\sigma}=\begin{bmatrix}\sigma_{11}&\sigma_{12}&\ldots&\sigma_{1j}\\
\sigma_{21}&\sigma_{22}&\ldots&\sigma_{2j}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ldots&\vdots\\
\sigma_{j1}&\sigma_{j2}&\ldots&\sigma_{jj}\end{bmatrix}.

The parameter
j
will be called the
underlying dimension
. Observe that we can write
A
and
\sigma
in terms of the underlying matrices
\underline{A}
and
\underline{\sigma}
using the Kronecker product as

\displaystyle A=\underline{A}\otimes I\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\sigma=\underline{\sigma}\otimes Q^{1/2}.

Consequently, using symmetry of
Q
, we can write the corresponding matrix
G_{t}
as in (
3.10
) as

\displaystyle G_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}e^{-vA}\sigma\sigma^{\ast}e^{-vA^{\ast}}\,dv

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}(e^{-v\underline{A}}\otimes I)(\underline{\sigma}\otimes Q^{1/2})(\underline{\sigma}\otimes Q^{1/2})^{\ast}(e^{-v\underline{A}^{\ast}}\otimes I)\,dv

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}e^{-v\underline{A}}\underline{\sigma}\,\underline{\sigma}^{\ast}e^{-v\underline{A}^{\ast}}\,dv\otimes Q

(4.2)

\displaystyle=:\underline{G}_{t}\otimes Q.

Notationally, throughout this section, for any
x\in\mathbf{R}^{n}
and any
k\times k
matrix
B
we write

(4.3)

\displaystyle x=\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}\\
x_{2}\\
\vdots\\
x_{j}\end{bmatrix}\in(\mathbf{R}^{k})^{j}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\hat{B}x=\begin{bmatrix}Bx_{1}\\
Bx_{2}\\
\vdots\\
Bx_{j}\end{bmatrix}\in(\mathbf{R}^{k})^{j}

where the
x_{\ell}
’s above are
k
-dimensional column vectors.
Setting

(4.4)

\displaystyle\underline{A}_{\underline{\sigma}}

\displaystyle:=\begin{bmatrix}\underline{\sigma}&\underline{A}\,\underline{\sigma}&\ldots&\underline{A}^{j-1}\underline{\sigma}\end{bmatrix},

(4.5)

\displaystyle\underline{\lambda}(j,t)

\displaystyle:=\inf_{|x|_{\mathbf{R}^{j}}=1}\langle\underline{G}_{t}x,x\rangle_{\mathbf{R}^{j}},

we obtain the following result.

Proposition 4.1
.

Suppose that
\underline{A}_{\underline{\sigma}}
has full rank,
x,y\in\mathbf{R}^{n}
and
t>0
. Then
\underline{G}_{t}
is invertible,
\underline{\lambda}(j,t)>0
and

(4.6)

\displaystyle\rho_{t}(x,y)^{2}

\displaystyle=\rho^{\underline{G}_{t}\otimes I}(\widehat{Q^{-1/2}}x,\widehat{Q^{-1/2}}y)^{2}\leq\sum_{\ell=1}^{j}\frac{\langle Q^{-1}(x_{\ell}-y_{\ell}),x_{\ell}-y_{\ell}\rangle_{\mathbf{R}^{k}}}{\underline{\lambda}(j,t)}.

Proof.

The equality in (
4.6
) follows from (
3.16
) and properties of the Kronecker product. The estimate in (
4.6
) follows by definition of
\underline{\lambda}(j,t)
and the fact that if
v=(v^{1},\ldots,v^{j})\in\mathbf{R}^{j}
is an eigenvector of a
j\times j
matrix
C
with eigenvalue
\lambda
, then for every
i=1,2,\ldots,k
the vector

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}v^{1}e_{i}\\
v^{2}e_{i}\\
\vdots\\
v^{j}e_{i}\end{bmatrix},

with
e_{i}
denoting the standard orthonormal basis element of
\mathbf{R}^{k}
, is an eigenvector with eigenvalue
\lambda
for the matrix
C\otimes I
,
I=I_{k\times k}
.
∎

4.2.
Examples

We next consider a number of examples with
A
and
\sigma
in the form (
4.1
) in which we estimate the control distance
\rho_{t}
as in (
3.17
) using
4.1
and the structure afforded in the specific dynamics.

Example 4.1

(Kolmogorov diffusion)
.

Consider the Kolmogorov diffusion, whose SDE is of the form (
2.1
) with
n=2k
for some
k\in\mathbf{N}
, and

\displaystyle A=\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\
I&0\end{bmatrix}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\sigma=\begin{bmatrix}Q^{1/2}&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}

where
I=I_{k\times k}
and
Q
is
k\times k
, symmetric and strictly positive definite. In this case, the underlying dimension is
j=2
and the associated underlying matrices are given by

\displaystyle\underline{A}=\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\
1&0\end{bmatrix}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\underline{\sigma}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}.

Observe that

\displaystyle\underline{A}_{\underline{\sigma}}=\begin{bmatrix}\underline{\sigma}&\underline{A}\underline{\sigma}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0&0\\
0&0&1&0\end{bmatrix}

has full rank. Furthermore,
\underline{A}
is nilpotent with
\underline{A}^{2}=0
. Hence

\displaystyle e^{-v\underline{A}}\sigma=(I_{2\times 2}-v\underline{A})\sigma=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
-v&0\end{bmatrix},

so that

\displaystyle\underline{G}_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}e^{-v\underline{A}}\underline{\sigma}\,\underline{\sigma}^{\ast}e^{-v\underline{A}^{\ast}}\,dv=\int_{0}^{t}\begin{bmatrix}1&-v\\
-v&v^{2}\end{bmatrix}dv=\begin{bmatrix}t&-\frac{t^{2}}{2}\\[3.0pt]
-\frac{t^{2}}{2}&\frac{t^{3}}{3}\end{bmatrix}.

We observe that the matrix
\underline{G}_{t}
has inverse given by

\displaystyle\underline{G}_{t}^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{4}{t}&\frac{6}{t^{2}}\\
\frac{6}{t^{2}}&\frac{12}{t^{3}}\end{bmatrix},

so that by
4.1
, if
\hat{x}=\widehat{Q^{-1/2}}x
and
\hat{y}=\widehat{Q^{-1/2}}x
, we have

\displaystyle\rho_{t}(x,y)^{2}=\rho^{\underline{G}_{t}\otimes I}(\hat{x},\hat{y})^{2}

\displaystyle=\langle(\underline{G}_{t}^{-1}\otimes I)(\hat{x}-\hat{y}),\hat{x}-\hat{y}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{t}\|Q^{-1/2}(x_{1}-y_{1})\|^{2}_{\mathbf{R}^{k}}+\frac{12}{t^{2}}\langle Q^{-1}(x_{1}-y_{1}),x_{2}-y_{2}\rangle_{\mathbf{R}^{k}}

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{12}{t^{3}}\|Q^{-1/2}(x_{2}-y_{2})\|^{2}_{\mathbf{R}^{k}}.

Furthermore, we can calculate the smallest positive eigenvalue
\underline{\lambda}(2,t)>0
of
\underline{G}_{t}
to see that

\displaystyle\underline{\lambda}(2,t)=\frac{t+\tfrac{t^{3}}{3}-\sqrt{(t+t^{3}/3)^{2}-t^{4}/3}}{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{t^{4}}{6(t+\tfrac{t^{3}}{3}+\sqrt{(t+t^{3}/3)^{2}-t^{4}/3})}

\displaystyle\simeq\begin{cases}\frac{t}{4}&\text{ as }t\rightarrow\infty\\
\frac{t^{3}}{12}&\text{ as }t\rightarrow 0^{+}.\end{cases}

Observe that the expression above for the distance
\rho_{t}
also reveals a scale invariance for this particular example. That is, if we define for each
a>0
a dilation
\delta_{a}:\mathbf{R}^{2k}\to\mathbf{R}^{2k}
by

\displaystyle\delta_{a}x=\delta_{a}\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}\\
x_{2}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}ax_{1}\\
a^{3}x_{2}\end{bmatrix},

then we see that

\rho_{t}(x,y)=\rho_{1}(\delta_{t^{-1/2}}x,\delta_{t^{-1/2}}y).

This is appropriate for this example as it coincides with the natural scale invariance that the solution
x_{t}
inherits from the standard scaling relation for Brownian motion

\displaystyle\sqrt{a}B_{t}^{x}\overset{d}{=}B_{at}^{\sqrt{a}x},

where
B_{t}^{x}:=x+B_{t}
. To see this explicitly, note that for the given
A
and
\sigma
, we may express the solution of (
2.2
) as

x_{t}(x)=\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}+Q^{-1/2}B_{t}^{1}\\
x_{2}+tx_{1}+\int_{0}^{t}Q^{-1/2}B_{s}^{1}\,ds\end{bmatrix},

where
\{B_{t}^{1}\}_{t\geq 0}
is a standard
k
-dimensional Brownian motion. From this expression it follows that

\{\delta_{a}x_{t}(x)\}\overset{d}{=}\{x_{a^{2}t}(\delta_{a}(x))\}
.

Note that control distances in more general settings are not so easily computable. In particular, the scale invariance observed in Example
4.1
will not hold for general
A
and
\sigma
. However, there are other examples where such relations are possible.

Example 4.2

(Iterated Kolmogorov diffusion)
.

Consider next the SDE (
2.1
) with
n=jk
and
n\times n
matrices
A
and
\sigma
given by

\displaystyle A=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&\cdots&0&0\\
I&0&\cdots&0&0\\
0&I&\cdots&0&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&I&0\\
\end{bmatrix}\ \ \ \ \mbox{ and }\ \ \ \sigma=\begin{bmatrix}I&0&\cdots&0\\
0&0&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&0\end{bmatrix},

with
I=I_{k\times k}
. We observe that, in this case, equation (
2.1
) has explicit solution given by

x_{t}(x)=x+\left(B_{t},\int_{0}^{t}B_{s}\,ds,\ldots,\int_{\Delta_{j-1}(t)}B_{s_{j-1}}\,ds_{j-1}\cdots ds_{1}\right)

with
\{B_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
a standard
k
-dimensional Brownian motion and
\Delta_{\ell}(t)=\{0<s_{\ell}<s_{\ell-1}<\cdots<s_{1}<t\}
.
As with the Kolmogorov diffusion, it is also possible to explicitly compute
G_{t}
, but one may also see the scale invariance just from the above expression for
x_{t}
.
If we define
\delta_{a}:\mathbf{R}^{jk}\to\mathbf{R}^{jk}
by

\delta_{a}x=\delta_{a}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{j}):=\left(ax_{1},\ldots,a^{2j-1}x_{j}\right)

then the standard scaling relation for
\{B_{t}\}
implies that

\{\delta_{a}x_{t}(0)\}\overset{d}{=}\{x_{a^{2}t}(0)\}

and, similarly,

\{\delta_{a}(x_{t}(x))\}\overset{d}{=}\{x_{a^{2}t}(\delta_{a}(x))\}.

This can be expressed in semigroup form as

P_{t}(f\circ\delta_{a})=(P_{a^{2}t}f)\circ\delta_{a}.

In particular, taking
f=f\circ\delta_{a}
in the Wang-Harnack inequality, cf.
3.8
, yields

\displaystyle((P_{t}(f\circ\delta_{a}))(x))^{\alpha}

\displaystyle=(P_{a^{2}t}f)^{\alpha}(\delta_{a}(x))

\displaystyle\leqslant\exp\left(\frac{\alpha}{2(\alpha-1)}\rho_{a^{2}t}(\delta_{a}x,\delta_{a}y)\right)(P_{a^{2}t}(f^{\alpha}))(\delta_{a}(y))

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\frac{\alpha}{2(\alpha-1)}\rho_{a^{2}t}(\delta_{a}x,\delta_{a}y)\right)(P_{t}((f\circ\delta_{a})^{\alpha}))(y).

We may now take
f=f\circ\delta_{a^{-1}}
which gives

((P_{t}f)(x))^{\alpha}\leqslant\exp\left(\frac{\alpha}{2(\alpha-1)}\rho_{a^{2}t}(\delta_{a}x,\delta_{a}y)\right)(P_{t}(f^{\alpha}))(y).

Note that the choice of
a=t^{-1/2}
gives

((P_{t}f)(x))^{\alpha}\leqslant\exp\left(\frac{\alpha}{2(\alpha-1)}\rho_{1}(\delta_{t^{-1/2}}x,\delta_{t^{-1/2}}y)\right)(P_{t}(f^{\alpha}))(y).

4.2.1.
Linear kinetic Fokker-Planck equation

Recalling that
I=I_{k\times k}
is the identity and
Q
is
k\times k
and positive-definite, consider the case when
A
and
\sigma
are
2k\times 2k
of the following forms

\displaystyle A=\begin{bmatrix}0&I\\
-I&-\gamma I\end{bmatrix}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\sigma=\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\
0&\sqrt{\gamma}Q^{1/2}\end{bmatrix}

where
\gamma>0
is a constant, called the
friction
parameter, and the matrix
Q
is independent of
\gamma
.
In this case, the underlying matrices
\underline{A}
and
\underline{\sigma}
are
2\times 2
and satisfy

\displaystyle\underline{A}=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
-1&-\gamma\end{bmatrix}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\underline{\sigma}=\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\
0&\sqrt{\gamma}\end{bmatrix}.

Note that

(4.7)

\displaystyle\underline{A}_{\underline{\sigma}}=\begin{bmatrix}\underline{\sigma}&\underline{A}\underline{\sigma}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&0&\sqrt{\gamma}\\
0&\sqrt{\gamma}&0&-\gamma^{3/2}\end{bmatrix}

has full rank so that
\underline{\lambda}(2,t)>0
for all
t>0
. Our goal will be to study
\underline{\lambda}(2,t)
in the regimes where
\gamma\approx 0
and
\gamma\gg 1
. This analysis will be done on the appropriate time scale depending on the regime. That is, when
\gamma\approx 0
, we set
t=t_{*}/\gamma
and when
\gamma\gg 1
, we let
t=t_{*}\gamma
where
t_{*}\geqslant 1
. These timescales correspond to the correct scaling of the mixing rate of the Markovian dynamics with respect to
\gamma

[
15
]
. Below, we assume that
\gamma\neq 2
.

We find it convenient to diagonalize
\underline{A}
. First, observe that
\underline{A}
has distinct eigenvalues
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\in\mathbf{C}
given by

(4.8)

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}=\frac{-\gamma+\sqrt{\gamma^{2}-4}}{2}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\lambda_{2}=\frac{-\gamma-\sqrt{\gamma^{2}-4}}{2}

where
\sqrt{-1}=i
. Furthermore, we can write

\displaystyle\underline{A}=\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\
\lambda_{1}&\lambda_{2}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\lambda_{1}&0\\
0&\lambda_{2}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\
\lambda_{1}&\lambda_{2}\end{bmatrix}^{-1}.

Hence,

\displaystyle e^{-\underline{A}t}\underline{\sigma}=-\frac{\sqrt{\gamma}}{\sqrt{\gamma^{2}-4}}\begin{bmatrix}0&e^{-\lambda_{2}t}-e^{-\lambda_{1}t}\\
0&\lambda_{2}e^{-\lambda_{2}t}-\lambda_{1}e^{-\lambda_{1}t}\end{bmatrix}.

Thus, if
c_{\gamma}=\gamma/(\gamma^{2}-4)
, we have

\displaystyle\underline{G}_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\underline{A}s}\underline{\sigma}\underline{\sigma}^{\ast}e^{-\underline{A}^{\ast}s}\,ds

\displaystyle=c_{\gamma}\int_{0}^{t}\begin{bmatrix}e^{-2\lambda_{1}s}-2e^{-(\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2})s}+e^{-2\lambda_{2}s}&\lambda_{1}e^{-2\lambda_{1}s}-(\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2})e^{-(\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2})s}+\lambda_{2}e^{-2\lambda_{2}s}\\
\ \lambda_{1}e^{-2\lambda_{1}s}-(\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2})e^{-(\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2})s}+\lambda_{2}e^{-2\lambda_{2}s}&\lambda_{1}^{2}e^{-2\lambda_{1}s}-2\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}e^{-(\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2})s}+\lambda_{2}^{2}e^{-2\lambda_{2}s}\end{bmatrix}\,ds

\displaystyle=:c_{\gamma}\begin{bmatrix}G_{11}&G_{12}\\
G_{12}&G_{22}\end{bmatrix}.

Observe that for all
\gamma\neq 2
, by positive-definiteness of
\underline{G}_{t}
via (
4.7
), the discriminant

\displaystyle D:=G_{11}G_{22}-G_{12}^{2}

is strictly positive for all
t>0
.

Case 1
(
\gamma\gg 1
)
. In this case,
\lambda_{1}\neq\lambda_{2}
are both real and we set
t=\gamma t_{*}
where
t_{*}\geqslant 1
is independent of
\gamma
. Moreover, since
c_{\gamma}>0
,

\displaystyle\underline{\lambda}(j,t)

\displaystyle=c_{\gamma}\frac{G_{11}+G_{22}-\sqrt{(G_{11}+G_{22})^{2}-4D}}{2}=c_{\gamma}\frac{2D}{G_{11}+G_{22}+\sqrt{(G_{11}+G_{22})^{2}-4D}}.

Using the explicit expressions above along with the values of
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}
in (
4.8
), we obtain

(4.9)

\displaystyle G_{11}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\gamma}-\frac{\gamma}{2}-\frac{2}{\gamma}e^{\gamma t}-\frac{\lambda_{2}}{2}e^{-2\lambda_{1}t}-\frac{\lambda_{1}}{2}e^{-2\lambda_{2}t},

\displaystyle G_{22}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\gamma}-\frac{\gamma}{2}-\frac{2}{\gamma}e^{\gamma t}-\frac{\lambda_{1}}{2}e^{-2\lambda_{1}t}-\frac{\lambda_{2}}{2}e^{-2\lambda_{2}t},

\displaystyle G_{12}

\displaystyle=e^{\gamma t}-\frac{e^{-2\lambda_{1}t}}{2}-\frac{e^{-2\lambda_{2}t}}{2}.

Furthermore, note that as
\gamma\rightarrow\infty
we have the following asymptotic formulas

(4.10)

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\gamma}+O(\gamma^{-2})\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\lambda_{2}=-\gamma+\frac{1}{\gamma}+O(\gamma^{-2}).

Substituting in
t=t_{*}\gamma
into the expressions following (
4.9
) and using (
4.10
) produces the following bound

\displaystyle D

\displaystyle=G_{11}G_{22}-G_{12}^{2}

\displaystyle=\bigg\{\frac{e^{-4\lambda_{2}\gamma t_{*}}}{4}-e^{(-2\lambda_{2}+\gamma)\gamma t_{*}}+\frac{\lambda_{2}^{2}}{4}e^{2\gamma^{2}t_{*}}+\frac{\lambda_{2}\gamma}{4}e^{-2\lambda_{2}\gamma t_{*}}+R_{1}\bigg\}

\displaystyle\qquad-\bigg\{\frac{e^{-4\lambda_{2}\gamma t_{*}}}{4}-e^{(-2\lambda_{2}+\gamma)\gamma t_{*}}+\frac{3}{2}e^{2\gamma^{2}t_{*}}+R_{2}\bigg\}

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{\lambda_{2}^{2}}{4}e^{2\gamma^{2}t_{*}}\bigg\{1-e^{-t_{*}}-C\gamma^{-2}\bigg\}

which is satisfied for all
t_{*}\geqslant 1
and
\gamma\geqslant\gamma_{\ell}\gg 1
for some constant
C>0
independent of
t_{*},\gamma
.
In a similar fashion, by increasing
\gamma_{\ell}>0
and
C>0
if needed, we obtain

\displaystyle G_{11}+G_{22}+\sqrt{(G_{11}+G_{22})^{2}-4D}

\displaystyle\leqslant-\frac{3\lambda_{2}}{2}e^{-2\lambda_{2}\gamma t_{*}}\bigg\{1+C/\gamma\bigg\}

for all
t_{*}\geqslant 1,\gamma\geqslant\gamma_{\ell}
.
Consequently, by increasing
\gamma_{\ell}
again if needed, we see that for
t_{*}\geqslant 1
and
\gamma\geqslant\gamma_{L}

\displaystyle\lambda(2,t_{*}\gamma)\geqslant\frac{-c_{\gamma}\lambda_{2}}{6}e^{t_{*}}\frac{1-e^{-t_{*}}-C\gamma^{-2}}{1+C\gamma^{-1}}\geqslant\frac{e^{t_{*}}}{16}.

Case II
(
\gamma\approx 0
)
. In this case, we set
t=t_{*}/\gamma
where
t_{*}\geqslant 1
. In this case, since
c_{\gamma}<0
,

\displaystyle\underline{\lambda}(2,t)=c_{\gamma}\frac{(G_{11}+G_{22})+\sqrt{(G_{11}+G_{22})^{2}-4D}}{2}=|c_{\gamma}|\frac{2D}{-(G_{11}+G_{22})+\sqrt{(G_{11}+G_{22})^{2}-4D}}.

Again, since
\gamma\approx 0
, we find that if
\beta=|\sqrt{\gamma^{2}-4}|/2
, then

\displaystyle G_{11}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\gamma}{2}+\frac{2}{\gamma}[1-e^{\gamma t}]+\frac{\gamma}{2}e^{\gamma t}\cos(2\beta t)+\beta e^{\gamma t}\sin(2\beta t),

\displaystyle G_{22}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\gamma}{2}+\frac{2}{\gamma}[1-e^{\gamma t}]+\frac{\gamma}{2}e^{\gamma t}\cos(2\beta t)-\beta e^{\gamma t}\sin(2\beta t)

\displaystyle G_{12}

\displaystyle=e^{\gamma t}(1-\cos(2\beta t)).

Setting
t=t_{*}/\gamma
where
t_{*}\geqslant 1
we find that there exists a constant
C>0
so that

(4.11)

\displaystyle D\geqslant\frac{4}{\gamma^{2}}(1-e^{t_{*}})^{2}\{1-C\gamma\}

for all
t_{*}\geqslant 1
,
0<\gamma\leqslant\gamma_{s}
, where
\gamma_{s}>0
is sufficiently small and
C>0
is independent of
\gamma
. In a similar fashion, by decreasing
\gamma_{s}>0
and
C>0
if needed, we obtain the following bound

(4.12)

\displaystyle-(G_{11}+G_{22})+\sqrt{(G_{11}+G_{22})^{2}-4D}\leqslant\frac{8}{\gamma}(e^{t_{*}}-1)\{1+C\gamma\}

satisfied for all
t_{*}\geqslant 1
and
0<\gamma\leqslant\gamma_{s}
. Combining (
4.11
) with (
4.12
) and adjusting
\gamma_{s}
smaller if needed we obtain

\displaystyle\underline{\lambda}(2,t_{*}/\gamma)\geqslant\frac{|c_{\gamma}|}{\gamma}(e^{t_{*}}-1)\frac{1-C\gamma}{1+C\gamma}\geqslant\frac{e^{t_{*}}-1}{8}\geqslant\frac{e^{t_{*}}}{16}

for all
t_{*}\geqslant 1
and
\gamma\leqslant\gamma_{s}
.

4.2.2.
Coupled oscillators

For
a,d,b\in\mathbf{R}
, let
\text{Tri}_{j}(a,d,b)
denote the
j\times j
tridiagonal matrix satisfying

\displaystyle(\text{Tri}_{j}(a,d,c))_{\ell m}=\begin{cases}a&\text{ if }\,m=\ell-1\\
d&\text{ if }\,m=\ell\\
c&\text{ if }\,m=\ell+1\\
0&\text{ otherwise}\end{cases}

and let
E_{\ell m}
be the
j\times j
matrix with
(\ell,m)
th entry equal to
1
and all other entries equal to
0
. Let the underlying matrices
\underline{A}
and
\underline{\sigma}
be given by

\displaystyle\underline{A}=\text{Tri}_{j}(1,0,-1)\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\underline{\sigma}=E_{11}.

A short calculation shows that for
m=0,1,\ldots,j-1
and
\ell=1,2,\ldots,j

\displaystyle(\underline{A}^{m}\underline{\sigma})_{m+1,\ell}=E_{m+1,\ell}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad(\underline{A}^{m}\underline{\sigma})_{z,\ell}=0

for
m+2\leq z\leq j-1
.
Consequently, the matrix
\underline{A}_{\underline{\sigma}}
has full rank and so
\underline{\lambda}(j,t)>0
for all
t>0
.
The goal of this calculation will be to estimate
\underline{\lambda}(j,t)
for
t\gg 1
. We find it again convenient to diagonalize
\underline{A}
.

One can show that
\underline{A}
has distinct eigenvalues
\lambda_{\ell}
,
\ell=1,2,\ldots,j
, given by

(4.13)

\displaystyle\lambda_{\ell}=2i\cos\Big(\frac{\ell\pi}{j+1}\Big)

with corresponding (right) eigenvectors
v_{\ell}
,
\ell=1,2,\ldots,j
, defined by

\displaystyle(v_{\ell})_{m}=i^{m}\sin\Big(\frac{\ell m\pi}{j+1}\Big),\qquad m=1,2,\ldots,j.

See, for example,
[
45
,
47
]
. Moreover, using basic properties of trigonometric functions, it can be checked that the set of eigenvectors
\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{j}\}
forms an orthogonal family with identical lengths (see
[
50
]
)

\displaystyle|v_{\ell}|^{2}=\frac{j+1}{2},\qquad\ell=1,2,\ldots,j.

Therefore, we define an orthonormal family of (column) eigenvectors
\{w_{1},\ldots,w_{j}\}
by
w_{\ell}=v_{\ell}/|v_{\ell}|
,
\ell=1,2,\ldots,j
. Setting
P=[w_{1}\,\,w_{2}\,\,\ldots\,\,w_{j}]
and letting
P^{\ast}
denote its Hermitian transpose, for any
x\in\mathbf{R}^{j}_{\neq 0}
we have by symmetry of
\sigma
and antisymmetry of
\underline{A}

\displaystyle\langle\underline{G}_{t}x,x\rangle=\int_{0}^{t}|\underline{\sigma}^{\ast}e^{-v\underline{A}^{\ast}}x|^{2}\,dv=\int_{0}^{t}|\underline{\sigma}e^{v\underline{A}}x|^{2}\,dv=\int_{0}^{t}|E_{11}P\text{diag}(e^{v\lambda_{1}},e^{v\lambda_{2}},\ldots,e^{v\lambda_{j}})P^{\ast}x|^{2}.

Let
\xi=P^{\ast}x\in\mathbf{C}^{j}
and notice that

\displaystyle\langle\underline{G}_{t}x,x\rangle=\int_{0}^{t}\Big|\sum_{\ell=1}^{j}(w_{\ell})_{1}\xi_{\ell}e^{\lambda_{\ell}v}\Big|^{2}\,dv

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell,m=1}^{j}(w_{\ell})_{1}\overline{(w_{m})_{1}}\xi_{\ell}\overline{\xi_{m}}\int_{0}^{t}e^{v\lambda_{\ell}+v\overline{\lambda_{m}}}\,dv

\displaystyle=t\sum_{\ell}|(w_{\ell})_{1}|^{2}|\xi_{\ell}|^{2}+\sum_{\ell\neq m}(w_{\ell})_{1}\overline{(w_{m})_{1}}\xi_{\ell}\overline{\xi_{m}}\int_{0}^{t}e^{v\lambda_{\ell}+v\overline{\lambda_{m}}}\,dv.

By definition of the eigenvalues
\lambda_{m}
in (
4.13
), for
\ell\neq m
,
\ell,m\in\{1,2,\ldots,j\}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\bigg|\int_{0}^{t}e^{v\lambda_{\ell}+v\overline{\lambda_{m}}}\,dv\bigg|=\bigg|\frac{e^{2ti\cos(\frac{\ell\pi}{j+1})}-e^{2it\cos(\frac{m\pi}{j+1})}}{2i\cos\big(\frac{\ell\pi}{j+1}\big)-2i\cos\big(\frac{m\pi}{j+1}\big)}\bigg|\leqslant\frac{1}{\big|\cos\big(\frac{\ell\pi}{j+1}\big)-\cos\big(\frac{m\pi}{j+1}\big)\big|}.

Using the mean value theorem, we find that for
\ell\neq m
,
\ell,m\in\{1,2,\ldots,j\}
, there exists
p=p_{\ell m}
strictly between
\ell\pi/(j+1)
and
m\pi/(j+1)
such that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\big|\cos\big(\frac{\ell\pi}{j+1}\big)-\cos\big(\frac{m\pi}{j+1}\big)\big|}=\frac{1}{\big|\frac{(\ell-m)\pi}{j+1}\sin(p)\big|}\leqslant\frac{(j+1)}{\pi}\frac{1}{\sin(\tfrac{\pi}{j+1})}=:c_{j}.

Hence, applying the bound above and using Cauchy-Schwarz we obtain

\displaystyle\bigg|\sum_{\ell\neq m}(w_{\ell})_{1}\overline{(w_{m})_{1}}\xi_{\ell}\overline{\xi_{m}}\int_{0}^{t}e^{v\lambda_{\ell}+v\overline{\lambda_{m}}}\,dv\bigg|\leqslant c_{j}\bigg(\sum_{\ell}|(w_{\ell})_{1}\xi_{\ell}|\bigg)^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant c_{j}\sum_{\ell}|(w_{\ell})_{1}|^{2}\sum_{\ell}|\xi_{\ell}|^{2}

\displaystyle=c_{j}|x|^{2}.

Thus,

\displaystyle\langle G_{t}x,x\rangle\geqslant t\sum_{\ell}|(w_{\ell})_{1}|^{2}|\xi_{\ell}|^{2}-c_{j}|x|^{2}\geqslant\big(t\sin^{2}\big(\tfrac{\pi}{j+1}\big)-c_{j}\big)|x|^{2}

and so we obtain

\displaystyle\underline{\lambda}(j,t)\geqslant t\sin^{2}\big(\tfrac{\pi}{j+1}\big)-c_{j}

for all
t>0
.

4.2.3.
Oscillators with some damping

We revisit the previous example, but this time we place damping on the first coordinate. That is, we set

\displaystyle\underline{A}=\text{Tri}_{j}(1,0,-1)-E_{11}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\underline{\sigma}=E_{11}.

In this case, diagonalizing
\underline{A}
as before seems challenging because an explicit expression for eigenvalues and eigenvectors is not known. In order to analyze
\underline{\lambda}(j,t)
, we appeal to
3.13
.

Note that, in a similar fashion to the previous example, one can readily check that
\underline{A}_{\underline{\sigma}}
has full rank, so that
\underline{\lambda}(j,t)>0
for all
t>0
. We next seek to estimate

(4.14)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{s}|e^{-v\underline{A}^{\ast}}x|^{2}\,dv.

Observe that the integrand in (
4.14
) is precisely
|y(v)|^{2}
where
y(v)
is the solution of the following ODE on
\mathbf{R}^{j}
at time
v
:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\dot{y}=(E_{11}+\text{Tri}_{j}(1,0,-1))y\\
y_{0}=x\in\mathbf{R}^{j}.\end{cases}

We claim that

\displaystyle|y(v)|^{2}\geqslant d_{j}|x|^{2}e^{r_{j}v}\text{ for all }v\geqslant 0

where
d_{j},r_{j}>0
are constants. We will prove the claim using a convenient Lyapunov function.

To define our Lyapunov function, let
a_{\ell}
,
\ell=0,1,2,\ldots,j-1
, be positive constants to be determined and set
a_{j}=0
. Define and function
V:\mathbf{R}^{j}\rightarrow\mathbf{R}
by

\displaystyle V(y)=\frac{a_{0}}{2}|y|^{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}a_{i}y_{i}y_{i+1}.

We first pick a convenient form for the constants
a_{i}
; that is, we define

\displaystyle a_{i-1}=a_{i}+b_{i},\,\,\,\,i=2,\ldots,j,

for some positive constants
b_{i}
,
i=2,\ldots,j
, to be determined. Note that, in particular, this means that

\displaystyle a_{\ell}=\sum_{i=\ell+1}^{j}b_{i},\qquad\ell=1,\ldots,j,

with the convention that the empty sum is zero.

First observe that we have the following explicit bound:

\displaystyle\big(\tfrac{a_{0}}{2}+\textstyle{\sum_{i=2}^{j}}b_{i}\big)|y|^{2}

\displaystyle\geqslant(\tfrac{a_{0}}{2}+\tfrac{a_{1}}{2})y_{1}^{2}+\sum_{i=2}^{j}\big(\tfrac{a_{0}}{2}+\tfrac{a_{i-1}}{2}+\tfrac{a_{i}}{2}\big)y_{i}^{2}

(4.15)

\displaystyle\geqslant V(y)

\displaystyle\geqslant\big(\tfrac{a_{0}}{2}-\tfrac{a_{1}}{2}\big)y_{1}^{2}+\sum_{i=2}^{j}\big(\tfrac{a_{0}}{2}-\tfrac{a_{i-1}}{2}-\tfrac{a_{i}}{2}\big)y_{i}^{2}

\displaystyle\geqslant\big(\tfrac{a_{0}}{2}-\textstyle{\sum}_{i=2}^{j}b_{i}\big)|y|^{2}.

In particular, we need to choose

\displaystyle a_{0}>2\sum_{i=2}^{j}b_{i}

so that
V
is nonnegative.

Next, observe that

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}V(y(t))

\displaystyle=-a_{1}y_{1}(t)y_{2}(t)+\sum_{i=1}^{j}(a_{i-1}-a_{i})(y_{i}(t))^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{j-2}(a_{i}-a_{i+1})y_{i}(t)y_{i+2}(t)

\displaystyle=-a_{1}y_{1}(t)y_{2}(t)+(a_{0}-a_{1})y_{1}^{2}+\sum_{i=2}^{j}b_{i}(y_{i}(t))^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{j-2}b_{i+1}y_{i}(t)y_{i+2}(t)

\displaystyle\geqslant-a_{1}y_{1}(t)y_{2}(t)+(a_{0}-a_{1}-\tfrac{b_{2}}{2})y_{1}^{2}+(b_{2}-\tfrac{b_{3}}{2})y_{2}^{2}+(b_{j}-\tfrac{b_{j-1}}{2})y_{j}^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{i=3}^{j-1}(b_{i}-\tfrac{b_{i+1}}{2}-\tfrac{b_{i-1}}{2})(y_{i}(t))^{2}.

Pick
b_{i}=\log(i+1)
,
i=2,\ldots,j
, and notice by concavity we have

\displaystyle b_{i}>\frac{b_{i+1}}{2}+\frac{b_{i-1}}{2},\,\,i=2,\ldots,j.

On the other hand, for any
\beta>0
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}V(y(t))

\displaystyle\geqslant(a_{0}-a_{1}-\tfrac{a_{1}^{2}}{2\beta}-b_{2})y_{1}^{2}+(b_{2}-\tfrac{b_{3}}{2}-\tfrac{\beta}{2})y_{2}^{2}+(b_{j}-\tfrac{b_{j-1}}{2})y_{j}^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{i=3}^{j-1}(b_{i}-\tfrac{b_{i+1}}{2}-\tfrac{b_{i-1}}{2})(y_{i}(t))^{2}

Pick
\beta=\log(2)
, define
b_{j+1}=\log(j+2)
and let

\displaystyle a_{0}=a_{1}+\tfrac{a_{1}^{2}}{2\beta}+b_{2}+b_{j}-\tfrac{b_{j+1}}{2}-\tfrac{b_{j-1}}{2}.

Using the fact that the function
x\mapsto\log(x)-\log(x+1)/2-\log(x-1)/2
defined on
[2,\infty)
is strictly decreasing and using (
4.15
), we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}V(y(t))\geqslant(b_{j}-\tfrac{b_{j+1}}{2}-\tfrac{b_{j-1}}{2})|y(t)|^{2}\geqslant\frac{(b_{j}-\tfrac{b_{j+1}}{2}-\tfrac{b_{j-1}}{2})}{a_{0}+2\sum_{i=2}^{j}b_{i}}V(y(t)).

Hence, letting

(4.16)

\displaystyle r_{j}=\frac{(b_{j}-\tfrac{b_{j+1}}{2}-\tfrac{b_{j-1}}{2})}{a_{0}+2\sum_{i=2}^{j}b_{i}}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad c_{j}=\frac{\frac{a_{0}}{2}-\sum_{i=2}^{j}b_{i}}{\frac{a_{0}}{2}+\sum_{i=2}^{j}b_{i}},

we have that

\displaystyle|y(t)|^{2}\geqslant|x|^{2}c_{j}e^{r_{j}t}\,\,\,\text{ for all }t\geqslant 0.

Thus,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}|e^{-v\underline{A}^{\ast}}x|^{2}\,ds\geqslant|x|^{2}\frac{c_{j}}{r_{j}}(e^{r_{j}t}-1).

Applying
3.13
, we obtain the bound for
t\geq 2

(4.17)

\displaystyle\underline{\lambda}(j,t)\geqslant\underline{\lambda}(j,t/2)\frac{c_{j}}{tr_{j}}(e^{\frac{r_{j}t}{2}}-1)\geq\frac{\underline{\lambda}(j,1)}{tr_{j}}c_{j}(e^{\frac{r_{j}t}{2}}-1)

5.
From finite to infinite dimensions

The goal of this section is to move from the finite-dimensional setting in relation (
2.1
) to an infinite-dimensional version of the system where the noise becomes infinite-dimensional. In the context of the examples of the previous section, this means that if
n=jk
, where
j
is the underlying dimension
j
and
k
is the dimension of the noise, then we take
k\rightarrow\infty
leaving
j
fixed. Building off of the general analysis done with the modified gradients in
Section

3
, we will be able to extend the the Wang-Harnack inequality in
3.8
to an infinite-dimensional version. We will then use this infinite-dimensional version to conclude a quasi-invariance result as well as a “time-infinity” Wang-Harnack inequality. We conclude the section by revisiting some of the examples considered in
Section

4
as they relate to their associated infinite-dimensional versions.

5.1.
The infinite-dimensional setting

Fix a separable, infinite-dimensional Hilbert space
H
with inner product and norm respectively denoted by

\displaystyle\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{H}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\|\cdot\|_{H}=\sqrt{\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{H}}.

Below, we make several slight abuses of notation to help connect with the finite-dimensional setting (
2.1
) previously considered. In particular, we will intentionally reuse the notations
Q,A,\sigma
, which were previously used to denote operators on
\mathbf{R}^{k},\mathbf{R}^{n},\mathbf{R}^{n}
, respectively. Here, in this section, they will be linear operators on the respective spaces
H
,
H^{j}
,
H^{j}
. We let
W=H^{j}
denote the separable Banach space with norm given by

\displaystyle\|X\|_{W}:=\sqrt{\|X^{1}\|_{H}^{2}+\cdots+\|X^{j}\|_{H}^{2}}

where
X=(X^{1},X^{2},\ldots,X^{j})\in H^{j}
.

Throughout this section we will employ the following:

Assumption 2
.

The mapping
Q:H\rightarrow H
is a strictly positive symmetric bounded linear operator of trace-class.

Note that under
2
, we can diagonalize
Q
in
H
; that is, there is an orthonormal basis
\{e_{\ell}\}_{\ell\in\mathbf{N}}
of
H
and real numbers
\alpha_{\ell}>0
for which

(5.1)

\displaystyle Qe_{\ell}=\alpha_{\ell}e_{\ell}\,\,\text{ for all }\,\,\ell\in\mathbf{N}.

Note that
Q
being trace class translates to the summability condition

(5.2)

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{\ell}<\infty.

Again, offering slight abuses of notation, we assume that

\displaystyle B(t):=(B^{1}(t),B^{2}(t),\ldots,B^{j}(t))

where
B^{i}(t)
,
i=1,2,\ldots,j
, are independent,
Q
-Brownian motions on
H
. Note that this is the same as supposing that each
B^{i}
can be written as

\displaystyle B^{i}(t)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\sqrt{\alpha_{\ell}}\beta_{\ell}^{i}(t)e_{\ell}

where
\{\beta_{k}^{i}\}_{k=1,i=1}^{\infty,j}
is a collection of mutually independent standard, real-valued Brownian motions on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbf{P})

[
18
]
. The fact that each
B^{i}
has continuous paths in
\|\cdot\|_{H}
with probability one follows by path continuity of each
\beta_{k}^{i}
and the summability condition (
5.2
).

Recall that associated to
Q
is the Hilbert space
H_{Q}
consisting of elements
h
such that

\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\frac{\langle h,e_{\ell}\rangle_{H}^{2}}{\alpha_{\ell}}<\infty

equipped with the inner product
\langle h,k\rangle_{Q}:=\langle Q^{-1/2}h,Q^{-1/2}k\rangle_{H}
, where
Q^{-1/2}
denotes the pseudo-inverse of
Q^{1/2}
. The space
H_{Q}
is called the
Cameron-Martin space
associated to the Gaussian measure
\mu=\operatorname{Law}(B^{i}(1))
, and enjoys various important analytic properties with respect to
\mu
. For example, we recall the Cameron-Martin-Maruyama “quasi-invariance” theorem: The measure
\mu^{h}
defined by
\mu^{h}(\Gamma):=\mu(\Gamma+h)
is mutually absolutely continuous with respect to
\mu
if and only if
h\in H_{Q}
. If
h\notin H_{Q}
then
\mu^{h}
is singular with respect to
\mu
.
Let
\mathcal{H}_{Q}
denote the Hilbert space
(H_{Q})^{j}
equipped with inner product coming from the product structure

\langle X,Y\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{Q}}:=\langle X^{1},Y^{1}\rangle_{Q}+\cdots+\langle X^{j},Y^{j}\rangle_{Q}

and induced norm

\|X\|_{\mathcal{H}_{Q}}:=\sqrt{\|X^{1}\|_{Q}^{2}+\cdots+\|X^{j}\|_{Q}^{2}}=\|(I_{j\times j}\otimes Q^{-1/2})X\|_{W}.

Letting
I_{H}
denote the identity operator on
H
, we set

(5.3)

\displaystyle A=(\underline{A}\otimes I_{H})\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\sigma=(\underline{\sigma}\otimes I_{H})

where
\underline{A}
and
\underline{\sigma}
are
j\times j
real matrices. In relation to (
2.1
), in this context we consider the integral equation on
W:=H^{j}

(5.4)

\displaystyle X(t;X_{0})

\displaystyle=X_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}AX(s)\,ds+\sigma B(t),\,\,\,\,X_{0}\in W.

Using a standard iteration procedure, it is not hard to show that for every
X_{0}\in W
, relation (
5.4
) has a unique solution which is a stochastic process
X(t)
on
W
with continuous paths in the norm
\|\cdot\|_{W}
. Furthermore, the process
X(t)
is Markov and we let
\mathcal{P}_{t}
denote the corresponding Markov semigroup.

5.2.
Infinite-dimensional Wang-Harnack inequality

Next, consider the projection operator
\pi_{k}:H\rightarrow H
associated to the orthonormal basis
\{e_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbf{N}}
given by

\displaystyle\pi_{k}(h):=\pi_{k}\bigg(\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\langle h,e_{\ell}\rangle e_{\ell}\bigg)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}\langle h,e_{\ell}\rangle e_{\ell},

and then define
\Pi_{k}:W\rightarrow W
as

\displaystyle\Pi_{k}(h_{1},h_{2},\ldots,h_{j}):=(\pi_{k}(h_{1}),\ldots,\pi_{k}(h_{j})).

We observe that, by (
5.3
) and (
5.4
), the process
t\mapsto\Pi_{k}X(t)
satisfies the finite-dimensional integral equation

\displaystyle\Pi_{k}X(t;X_{0})=\Pi_{k}X_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}A\Pi_{k}X(s)\,ds+\sigma\Pi_{k}B_{t}.

Let
\mathcal{P}_{t}^{k}
denote the Markov semigroup corresponding to
\Pi_{k}X(t)
.

Proposition 5.1
.

Suppose that
\underline{A}_{\underline{\sigma}}
as in (
4.4
) is of full rank. Let
\alpha>1
. Then for all bounded, measurable
\varphi:W\rightarrow\mathbf{R}
,
t>0
,
X_{0},Y_{0}\in W
, and
k\in\mathbf{N}
we have

(5.5)

\displaystyle(\mathcal{P}_{t}^{k}\varphi(\Pi_{k}X_{0}))^{\alpha}\leqslant\mathcal{P}_{t}^{k}\varphi^{\alpha}(\Pi_{k}Y_{0})\exp\bigg(\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}\frac{\|(I_{j\times j}\otimes Q^{-1/2})\Pi_{k}(X_{0}-Y_{0})\|_{W}^{2}}{2\underline{\lambda}(j,t)}\bigg).

Proof.

This follows after combining
3.8
with
4.1
.
∎

The following infinite-dimensional Wang-Harnack inequality follows immediately from the previous result by allowing
k\to\infty
.

Theorem 5.2
.

Suppose that
\underline{A}_{\underline{\sigma}}
as in (
4.4
) is of full rank and that
X_{0},Y_{0}
are such that
X_{0}-Y_{0}\in\mathcal{H}_{Q}
. Let
\alpha>1
. Then for all bounded, measurable
\varphi:W\rightarrow\mathbf{R}
and
t>0
we have

\displaystyle(\mathcal{P}_{t}\varphi(X_{0}))^{\alpha}\leqslant\mathcal{P}_{t}\varphi^{\alpha}(Y_{0})\exp\bigg(\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}\frac{\|X_{0}-Y_{0}\|_{\mathcal{H}_{Q}}^{2}}{{2}\underline{\lambda}(j,t)}\bigg).

Proof.

One need only show that for any
X_{0}\in W
,

\Pi_{k}X(t)\xrightarrow{a.s.}X(t)

as
k\rightarrow\infty
, and this follows immediately by uniqueness of solutions and the definition of
\Pi_{k}
.
∎

The equivalence of Wang-Harnack inequalities with integrated Harnack inequalities has been established in
[
22
,
6
,
29
]
, along with how these estimates imply quasi-invariance results of the following kind.

Theorem 5.3
.

Fix
t>0
. The measure
\mu_{t}:=\operatorname{Law}(X_{t}(0))
is quasi-invariant under translations by elements of
\mathcal{H}_{Q}
, that is, for
X_{0}\in\mathcal{H}_{Q}
the measure
\mu_{t}(X_{0}):=\operatorname{Law}(X_{t}(X_{0}))
is mutually absolutely continuous with respect to
\mu_{t}
.
Moreover, for all
p>1

\left\|\frac{d\mu_{t}(X_{0})}{d\mu_{t}}\right\|_{L^{p}(W,\mu_{t})}\leqslant\exp\left(\frac{1+p}{{{2}\underline{\lambda}(j,t)}}\|X_{0}\|_{\mathcal{H}_{Q}}^{2}\right).

Remark 5.4
.

In each of the examples considered in
Section

4
we saw that
\underline{A}_{\underline{\sigma}}
has full rank. Thus

applies to the infinite-dimensional versions of these equations when the noise becomes infinite dimensional (

5.3.
Mutual absolute continuity at time infinity

Under further conditions, we will use the results of
Section

to deduce that the laws of the infinite-dimensional Markov process

solving (

) started from two initial conditions

and

To this end, for

\displaystyle\Delta_{k}:=(I_{W}-\Pi_{k}).

Assumption 3
.

The matrix
\underline{A}_{\underline{\sigma}}

(ii)

Under

Proposition 5.5
.

Suppose that the underlying matrix
\underline{A}

for all

.
Suppose, furthermore, that

\displaystyle t_{k}\sum_{\ell=k+1}^{\infty}\alpha_{\ell}\rightarrow 0\text{ as }k\rightarrow\infty.

Then

Remark 5.6
.

Suppose that
\underline{\lambda}(j,t)\geqslant c_{j}t^{3}-d_{j}
for all
t\geqslant 0
for some constants
c_{j},d_{j}>0
and that
\alpha_{k}=1/k^{2}
. Then in this case,

\displaystyle t_{k}\lesssim k^{2/3}

so that

(5.7)

\displaystyle t_{k}\sum_{\ell=k+1}^{\infty}\alpha_{\ell}\lesssim\frac{1}{k^{1/3}}\rightarrow 0\text{ as }k\rightarrow\infty.

In short, the smallest positive eigenvalue
\underline{\lambda}(j,t)
must grow sufficiently fast as
t\rightarrow\infty
with respect to the sequence
\alpha_{k}
so that (
5.6
) is satisfied.

Proof of
5.5
.

Let
Y_{k}(t):=\Delta_{k}X(t;X_{0})
and define

\displaystyle\tau_{k,m}=\inf\{t\geqslant 0\,:\,\|Y_{k}(t)\|_{W}\geqslant m\}.

Set
\tau_{k,m}(t)=t\wedge\tau_{k,m}
and observe that, by Itô’s formula and the condition
\langle\underline{A}x,x\rangle_{\mathbf{R}^{j}}\leqslant 0
we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\|Y_{k}(\tau_{k,m}(t))\|_{W}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|Y_{k}(0)\|_{W}^{2}+2\mathbf{E}\int_{0}^{\tau_{k,m}(t)}\sum_{\ell=1}^{j}\langle(AY_{k}(s))_{\ell},(Y_{k}(s))_{\ell}\rangle_{H}\,ds

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}=1}^{j}\underline{\sigma}_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}}^{2}\sum_{\ell=k+1}^{\infty}\alpha_{\ell}\mathbf{E}\tau_{k,m}(t)

\displaystyle\leqslant\|Y_{k}(0)\|_{W}^{2}+\sum_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}=1}^{j}\underline{\sigma}_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}}^{2}\sum_{\ell=k+1}^{\infty}\alpha_{\ell}t.

Taking
m\rightarrow\infty
and then plugging in
t=t_{k}
, we arrive at the following estimate

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\|Y_{k}(t_{k})\|_{W}^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\|Y_{k}(0)\|_{W}^{2}+\sum_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}=1}^{j}\underline{\sigma}_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}}^{2}t_{k}\sum_{\ell=k+1}^{\infty}\alpha_{\ell}.

Applying the hypothesis (
5.7
), we conclude the result.
∎

We are now prepared to state and prove our uniqueness result.

Corollary 5.7

(Uniqueness of stationary distributions via Wang-Harnack)
.

Suppose that
3
is satisfied and
\mu
and
\nu
are stationary distributions for the Markov process
X(t)
. Then for any
\alpha>1
there exists a constant
C=C(\mu,\nu,\alpha)>0
such that

(5.8)

\displaystyle(\mu(\varphi))^{\alpha}\leqslant C\nu(\varphi^{\alpha})

for all
\varphi:W\rightarrow[0,\infty)
bounded, measurable. Consequently, there is at most one stationary distribution corresponding to
X(t)
.

Proof.

Note that the final conclusion in the result follows immediately by symmetry of the bound (
5.8
) and ergodic decomposition, for any two such stationary distributions must be mutually absolutely continuous. Let
\alpha>1
.
In order to prove (
5.8
), it suffices to prove the bound for all
\varphi:W\rightarrow[0,\infty)
which are bounded, Lipschitz and such that
\varphi^{\alpha}
is also Lipschitz; that is,
\|\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}}<\infty
,

\displaystyle\|\varphi\|_{\text{Lip}}:=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}X,Y\in W\\
X\neq Y\end{subarray}}\frac{|\varphi(X)-\varphi(Y)|}{\|X-Y\|_{W}}<\infty\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\|\varphi^{\alpha}\|_{\text{Lip}}<\infty.

Let
\epsilon\in(0,1)
and pick
R=R(\mu,\nu,\alpha)>0
large enough so that
B_{R}:=\{\|X\|_{W}<R\}
satisfies

\displaystyle\mu(B_{R}^{c})<\tfrac{\epsilon}{2}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\nu(B_{R}^{c})<\tfrac{\epsilon}{2}.

Then by invariance and Jensen’s inequality we have that

\displaystyle\mu(\varphi)^{\alpha}=\bigg[\int\mu(dX)\mathcal{P}_{t_{k}}\varphi(X)\bigg]^{\alpha}

\displaystyle\leqslant\int\mu(dX)(\mathcal{P}_{t_{k}}\varphi(X))^{\alpha}\leqslant\int_{B_{R}}\mu(dX)(\mathcal{P}_{t_{k}}\varphi(X))^{\alpha}+\tfrac{\epsilon}{2}\|\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}}^{\alpha}.

Multiplying the previous inequality by
\nu(W)=1
and writing this as
\nu(W)=\nu(B_{R})+\nu(B_{R}^{c})
gives

\displaystyle\mu(\varphi)^{\alpha}

\displaystyle\leqslant\nu(B_{R})\int_{B_{R}}\mu(dX)(\mathcal{P}_{t_{k}}\varphi(X))^{\alpha}+\epsilon\|\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}}^{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\int_{B_{R}}\int_{B_{R}}(\mathcal{P}_{t_{k}}\varphi(X))^{\alpha}\mu(dX)\nu(dY)+\epsilon\|\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}}^{\alpha}.

In order to control the double integral above, observe that

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{t_{k}}\varphi(X)=\mathcal{P}_{t_{k}}^{k}\varphi(\Pi_{k}X)+\mathbf{E}(\varphi(X(t_{k};X))-\varphi(\Pi_{k}X(t_{k};X)))

and

\displaystyle|\mathbf{E}(\varphi(X(t_{k};X))-\varphi(\Pi_{k}X(t_{k};X)))|\leqslant 2\|\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}}\wedge\big\{\|\varphi\|_{\text{Lip}}(\mathbf{E}\|\Delta_{k}X(t_{k},X)\|^{p})^{1/p}\big\}=:C_{k}(\varphi,X).

By
3
, we have that
C_{k}(\varphi,X)\rightarrow 0
pointwise in
X
as
k\rightarrow\infty
.
Hence applying
5.1
we have

\displaystyle\int_{B_{R}}\int_{B_{R}}(\mathcal{P}_{t_{k}}\varphi(X))^{\alpha}\mu(dX)\nu(dY)

\displaystyle\leqslant 2^{\alpha}\int_{B_{R}}\int_{B_{R}}(\mathcal{P}_{t_{k}}^{k}\varphi(\Pi_{k}X))^{\alpha}\mu(dX)\nu(dY)+2^{\alpha}\int_{B_{R}}\mu(dX)C_{k}(\varphi,X)

\displaystyle\leqslant 2^{\alpha}C(\alpha,R)\int_{B_{R}}\int_{B_{R}}(\mathcal{P}_{t_{k}}^{k}\varphi^{\alpha}(\Pi_{k}Y))\mu(dX)\nu(dY)+2^{\alpha}\int_{B_{R}}\mu(dX)C_{k}(\varphi,X)

\displaystyle\leqslant 2^{\alpha}C(\alpha,R)\int_{B_{R}}\int_{B_{R}}(\mathcal{P}_{t_{k}}\varphi^{\alpha}(Y))\mu(dX)\nu(dY)+2^{\alpha}\int_{B_{R}}\mu(dX)C_{k}(\varphi,X)

\displaystyle\qquad+2^{2\alpha}C(\alpha,R)\int_{B_{R}}\nu(dY)C_{k}(\varphi^{\alpha},Y).

\displaystyle\leqslant 2^{\alpha}C(\alpha,R)\nu(\varphi^{\alpha})+2^{\alpha}\int_{B_{R}}\mu(dX)C_{k}(\varphi,X)+2^{2\alpha}C(\alpha,R)\int_{B_{R}}\nu(dY)C_{k}(\varphi^{\alpha},Y).

Taking
k\rightarrow\infty
using
3
and the bounded convergence theorem and then letting
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
we obtain the desired bound (
5.8
) for all
\varphi:W\rightarrow[0,\infty)
bounded, Lipschitz with
\varphi^{\alpha}
Lipschitz. This finishes the proof.
∎
