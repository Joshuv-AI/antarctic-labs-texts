Characterizing counterfactuals and dependencies over causal and generalized causal teams
Fausto Barbero, Fan Yang

Abstract
We analyze the causal-observational languages that were introduced in Barbero and Sandu (2018), which allow discussing interventionist counterfactuals and functional dependencies in a unified framework. In particular, we systematically investigate the expressive power of these languages in causal team semantics, and we provide complete natural deduction calculi for each language. Furthermore, we introduce a generalized semantics which allows representing uncertainty about the causal laws, and analyze the expressive power and proof theory of the causal-observational languages over this enriched semantics.

Introduction

Counterfactual conditionals express the modality of
irreality
: they describe what
would
or
might
be the case in circumstances that diverge from the actual state of affairs. Pinning down the exact meaning and logic of counterfactual statements has been the subject of a large literature (see e.g.
[
24
]
for a survey). We are interested here in a special case: the
interventionist
counterfactuals, which emerged from the literature on causal inference (
[
23
,
20
,
18
,
28
]
). Under this reading, a conditional
\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x}\boxright\psi
expresses that
\psi
would hold if we were to intervene on the given system, by subtracting the variables
\mathbf{X}
to their current causal mechanisms and forcing them to take the values
\mathbf{x}
.

The
logic
of interventionist counterfactuals has been mainly studied in the semantic context of
deterministic causal models
(
[
12
,
17
,
7
,
32
]
), which consist of an assignment of values to variables together with a system of
structural equations
that describe the causal connections among variables.
In
[
3
]
, causal models were generalized to
causal teams
, in the spirit of
team semantics
(
[
19
,
26
]
), by considering a
set
of assignments (a “
team
”) instead of a single assignment. This opens the possibility of describing e.g.
uncertainty
,
observations
, and
dependencies
(
[
26
,
11
,
15
,
13
,
27
,
30
,
14
,
3
,
4
]
).
In this paper we also consider a further generalization of causal team semantics. While causal teams only allow describing uncertainty about the current state of the variables (the assignment), their generalized version also encode uncertainty about the causal laws.

One of the main reasons for introducing causal teams was the possibility of comparing, within a unified semantic framework, the logic of dependencies of causal nature (those definable in terms of interventionist counterfactuals) against that of contingent dependencies (those that occur e.g. in sets of empirical data, and which have been thoroughly studied in the literature on team semantics).
In the context of team semantics the most commonly studied type of contingent dependency is functional determinacy, which is represented by the so-called
dependence atoms

=\hskip-3.0pt({\mathbf{X}};{Y})
, whose intended meaning is that the values of the variables in
\mathbf{X}
determine the value of
Y
. More precisely, this means that assignments in the team which agree over
\mathbf{X}
also agree over
Y
. On the causal side, there is a large variety of notions of causal dependence; see for example
[
28
]
for a taxonomy of the most common ones. A notable example is the notion of
direct cause
. A variable
X
is a direct cause of another variable
Y
if there is a way of manipulating
X
, while holding fixed all variables distinct from
Y
, so that the value of
Y
will change. This can be made precise and expressed as a boolean combination of counterfactuals:

\bigvee_{\mathbf{z},x\neq x^{\prime},y\neq y^{\prime}}\big((\mathbf{Z}=\mathbf{z}\land X=x)\boxright Y=y\land(\mathbf{Z}=\mathbf{z}\land X=x^{\prime})\boxright Y=y^{\prime}\big)

where
\mathbf{Z}
is the set of all variables distinct from
X
and
Y
, and the disjunction ranges over the possible values of
\mathbf{z},x,x^{\prime},y,y^{\prime}
(with
x\neq x^{\prime}
,
y\neq y^{\prime}
).

[
4
]
provided a complete axiomatization for a language
\mathcal{CO}
which describes causal dependencies (but no contingent dependencies)

In this paper we fill this gap in the literature by providing complete deduction systems (in natural deduction style) for the languages

All these results will also be extended to the generalized causal team semantics. We point out that the methods developed in the literature (

Syntax and semantics

2.1
Formal languages

In this paper, we consider three classes of causal languages, which were originally introduced in
[
3
]
.
Each of the causal languages is parametrized by a
signature

\sigma
, which is a pair
\sigma=(\mathrm{Dom},\mathrm{Ran})
with
\mathrm{Dom}
being a nonempty finite set of
variables
, and
\mathrm{Ran}
a function that associates to each variable
X\in\mathrm{Dom}
a nonempty finite set
\mathrm{Ran}(X)
(called the
range
of
X
) of
constant symbols
or
values

x
.

2
2

2

Note that we do not encode a distinction between exogenous and endogenous variables into the signatures, as done in
[
17
]
. Instead, we follow the style of Briggs
[
7
]
. Doing so will result in more general completeness results.

Throughout the paper we use the Greek letter
\sigma
to denote an arbitrary signature.

A boldface capital letter
\mathbf{X}
stands for a sequence
\langle X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\rangle
of variables; similarly, a boldface lower case letter
\mathbf{x}
stands for a sequence
\langle x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\rangle
of values. We will sometimes abuse notation and treat
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{x}
also as sets. We abbreviate the conjunction of equalities
X_{1}=x_{1}\land\dots\land X_{n}=x_{n}
as
\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x}
, where we always assume that
x_{i}\in Ran(X_{i})
, and also call
\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x}
an equality (over
\sigma
). We write
\mathrm{Ran}(\mathbf{X})
for
\mathrm{Ran}(X_{1})\times\cdots\times\mathrm{Ran}(X_{n})
.

Given a signature
\sigma=(\mathrm{Dom},\mathrm{Ran})
, formulas of our basic languages
\mathcal{CO}[\sigma]
are formed according to the grammar:

\alpha::=X=x\mid\neg\alpha\mid\alpha\land\alpha\mid\alpha\lor\alpha\mid\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x}\boxright\alpha

where
\mathbf{X}\cup\{X\}\subseteq\mathrm{Dom}
,
x\in\mathrm{Ran}(X)
,
\mathbf{x}\in\mathrm{Ran}(\mathbf{X})
.
3
3

3

We are identifying syntactical variables and values with their semantical counterpart, following the conventions of the literature on interventionist counterfactuals, e.g.
[
12
,
17
,
7
,
32
]
. In this convention, distinct symbols (e.g.
x,x^{\prime}
) denote distinct objects. Obviously, syntax and semantics could in principle be also kept completely separated; see
[
4
]
for details.
The connective
\boxright
is used to form
interventionist counterfactuals
, a peculiar type of conditional formula. Following the literature on team semantics, we also call the disjunction
\lor

tensor disjunction
. Throughout the paper, we reserve the first letters of the Greek alphabet,
\alpha
and
\beta
for
\mathcal{CO}[\sigma]
formulas.

We also consider two extensions of
\mathcal{CO}[\sigma]
, obtained by adding the
global disjunction

\mathop{\,{\mathbin{{\setminus}\mspace{-5mu}{\setminus}}\hskip-0.82521pt/}\,}
, or dependence atoms
=\hskip-3.0pt({\mathbf{X}};{Y})
:

-

\mathcal{CO}_{\footnotesize{\mathbin{{\setminus}\mspace{-5mu}{\setminus}}\hskip-0.66016pt/}}[\sigma]:~~\varphi::=X=x\mid\neg\alpha\mid\varphi\land\varphi\mid\varphi\lor\varphi\mid\varphi\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\mathop{\,{\mathbin{{\setminus}\mspace{-5mu}{\setminus}}\hskip-0.82521pt/}\,}$}\varphi\mid\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x}\boxright\varphi

-

\mathcal{COD}[\sigma]:~~\varphi::=X=x\mid\ =\hskip-3.0pt({\mathbf{X}};{Y})\mid\neg\alpha\mid\varphi\land\varphi\mid\varphi\lor\varphi\mid\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x}\boxright\varphi

where
\mathbf{X}\cup\{X,Y\}\subseteq\mathrm{Dom}
,
x\in\mathrm{Ran}(X)
,
\mathbf{x}\in\mathrm{Ran}(\mathbf{X})
,
\alpha\in\mathcal{CO}[\sigma]
. According to our syntax, negation is allowed to occur only in front of
\mathcal{CO}[\sigma]
-formulas
\alpha
.
4
4

4

Following the tradition in the field of logics of (in)dependence, we do not allow the negation to occur in front of formulas with occurrences of
\mathop{\,{\mathbin{{\setminus}\mspace{-5mu}{\setminus}}\hskip-0.82521pt/}\,}
or of dependence atoms. The main reason is that, if we apply to such formulas the usual semantic clause for negation, we obtain many trivializing results such as
\neg=\hskip-3.0pt({\mathbf{X}};{Y})\equiv\bot
and
\neg(\psi\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\mathop{\,{\mathbin{{\setminus}\mspace{-5mu}{\setminus}}\hskip-0.82521pt/}\,}$}\chi)\equiv\neg(\psi\lor\chi)
.
This syntax for negation is more liberal than that in
[
3
]
, where formulas are assumed to be always in negation normal form.

2.2
Team semantics over causal teams

We study two variants of team semantics for the three causal languages. In this section, we review (with some modifications) the causal team semantics from
[
4
]
.

An
assignment
over a signature
\sigma=(\mathrm{Dom},\mathrm{Ran})
is a map

s:\mathrm{Dom}\rightarrow\bigcup_{X\in\mathrm{Dom}}\mathrm{Ran}(X)
such that
s(X)\in\mathrm{Ran}(X)

for every

. Denote by
\mathbb{A}_{\sigma}
the set of all assignments over
\sigma
.
A (non-causal)

T^{-}
over
\sigma
is a set of assignments over
\sigma

instead of

A

Variables in the set

Denote by

Definition 2.3
.

A
causal team
over a signature
\sigma
is a pair
T=(T^{-},\mathcal{F})
consisting of

-

a team
T^{-}
over
\sigma
, called the
team component
of
T
, and

-

a system of functions
\mathcal{F}
over
\sigma
, called the
function component
of
T
,

where all assignments
s\in T^{-}
are compatible with the function component
\mathcal{F}
.

In particular, a pair
T=(\emptyset,\mathcal{F})
with the team component being the empty set
\emptyset
is also a causal team. For simplicity, over the same signature
\sigma
, we identify all teams with the empty team component and different function components, namely we stipulate
\emptyset:=(\emptyset,\mathcal{F})=(\emptyset,\mathcal{G})
for any function components
\mathcal{F},\mathcal{G}
over
\sigma
. We call the causal team
\emptyset
the
empty causal team
(over
\sigma
).

The graph of a causal team
T
is defined as the graph associated to its function component; we denote it as
G_{T}
.
We call
T

recursive
if
G_{T}
is acyclic. We stipulate that the empty causal team is recursive. Throughout this paper, we will only consider recursive causal teams.
The reasons behind this choice
are many. First of all, the recursive case might be of greater philosophical importance, as it is sometimes claimed that only the recursive systems of functions admit a causal interpretation (see e.g.
[
25
]
for a discussion). Secondly, the logic of counterfactuals in the general case is much more complex than in the recursive case (see
[
17
]
) and it requires a separate treatment.
6
6

6

In particular, it naturally involves the treatment of
might
-counterfactuals, which are not downward closed formulas in the sense explained in Theorem
2.15
. They are thus out of the reach of the methods used in the present paper.

Intuitively, a causal team
T
may be seen as representing an assumption concerning the causal relationships among the variables in
\mathrm{Dom}
(as encoded in
\mathcal{F}
) together with a range of hypotheses concerning the actual state of the system (as encoded in
T^{-}
).

Let

2.3
Team semantics over generalized causal teams

Causal team semantics was developed in order to support the introduction of counterfactuals and observations within the framework of team semantics. When we look at one of the main interpretations of teams – as a representation of
uncertainty
among possible states of affairs – we see an asymmetry: causal teams encode uncertainty about the state of the variables, and certainty about the causal laws. Yet, in all sciences the knowledge of the laws of causation may be as uncertain – if not more uncertain – than the knowledge of factual matters.
[
3
]
proposed to model uncertainty about causal laws by using the more general
partially defined causal teams
. In this section we introduce a more general and at the same time simpler approach: the
generalized causal teams
.

Given a signature
\sigma
, we write

\mathbb{S}\mathbbm{em}_{\sigma}:=\{(s,\mathcal{F})\in\mathbb{A}_{\sigma}\times\mathbb{F}_{\sigma}\mid s\text{ is compatible with }\mathcal{F}\}.

The pairs
(s,\mathcal{F})\in\mathbb{S}\mathbbm{em}_{\sigma}
can be easily identified with the
deterministic causal models
(also known as
deterministic structural equation models
) that are considered in the literature on causal inference (
[
23
]
,
[
20
]
, etc.).
Even though they are distinct mathematical objects, it is natural to identify a causal team
T=(T^{-},\mathcal{F})
with the set

T^{g}=\{(s,\mathcal{F})\mid s\in T^{-}\}

of deterministic causal models with a common function component
\mathcal{F}
. We now introduce the notion of
generalized causal team
, in which the function component
\mathcal{F}
does not have to be constant throughout the team.

Definition 2.10
.

A
generalized causal team

T
over a signature
\sigma
is a set of pairs
(s,\mathcal{F})\in\mathbb{S}\mathbbm{em}_{\sigma}
, that is,
T\subseteq\mathbb{S}\mathbbm{em}_{\sigma}
.
We call the set

T^{-}:=\{s\mid(s,\mathcal{F})\in T\text{ for some }\mathcal{F}\}

the
team component
of
T
.
A
causal subteam
of
T
is a subset
S
of
T
, denoted as
S\subseteq T
.
In particular, the empty set
\emptyset
is a generalized causal team, and
\emptyset\subseteq T
for all generalized causal teams
T
.

The
union

S\cup T
of two generalized causal teams
S,T
is their set-theoretic union.

Intuitively, a generalized causal team encodes uncertainty about which causal model governs the variables in
\mathrm{Dom}
- i.e., uncertainty both on the values of the variables and on the laws that determine them. Distinct elements
(s,\mathcal{F}),(t,\mathcal{G})
of the same generalized causal team may also disagree on what is the set of endogenous variables, or on whether the system is recursive or not. A generalized causal team is said to be
recursive
if, for each pair
(s,\mathcal{F})
in the team, the associated graph
G_{\mathcal{F}}
is recursive. In this paper we only consider recursive generalized causal teams.

Let us now discuss some possible applications of generalized causal teams as representation tools. Consider a situation in which we have observational data about some phenomenon. It is very common to organize the data in the form of a database or a table. When we are uncertain among alternative causal explanations

If one wants to make considerations of probabilistic nature, it might be necessary to allow multiple copies of the same causal model to occur in the model. We are not pursuing the probabilistic view in this paper, but see

In the first case, we know what causes what, and what does not – in other words, we are certain about what the causal graph

In the second case, the data arises from the observation of different individuals that may be of different types, and are thus bound to react differently to a possible intervention. Our uncertainty about these types and how the individual will be affected by interventions may be represented by associating multiple function components to an individual. In other words, we can use the generalized causal teams to represent the

3
Characterizing function components

As is common in the literature on causation, our formal languages only talk of variables and values; they do not explicitly mention the causal laws nor the functions that describe them. In order to understand the expressive power of these languages, we need to first understand to what extent they allow an implicit description of the laws. In this section we will answer to this question; the answer is: to a great extent. In the following subsection we begin addressing the aspects of the functional laws that can
not
be described by our languages.

3.1
Equivalence of function components

Consider a binary function
f
and a quaternary function
g
defined as
f(X,Y)=X+Y\text{ and }g(X,Y,Z_{1},Z_{2})=X+Z_{1}+0/Z_{2}+(Y-Z_{1}).
Essentially
f
and
g
are the same function:
Z_{1},Z_{2}
are dummy arguments of
g
. In some formal presentations of causal reasoning, the distinction between
f
and
g
is quotiented out. We preferred to maintain this distinction in the present paper, following the approaches in
[
7
,
3
,
4
]
. This type of distinction might be needed in case the framework is applied to realistic scenarios. Given a complex description of a function, it might be unfeasible to check whether some of its arguments are dummy in case the variable ranges are large. On the other hand, however, this additional degree of generality brings with it a burden of bookkeeping, which we now address.

First of all, we define formally what it means for two functions (and more generally, two function components) to be equivalent up to dummy arguments.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
\mathcal{F},\mathcal{G}
be two function components over
\sigma=(\mathrm{Dom},\mathrm{Ran})
.

-

Let
V\in\mathrm{Dom}
. The two functions
\mathcal{F}_{V}
and
\mathcal{G}_{V}
are said to be equivalent up to dummy arguments, denoted as
\mathcal{F}_{V}\sim\mathcal{G}_{V}
, if
for any
\mathbf{x}\in\mathrm{Ran}(PA_{V}^{\mathcal{F}}\cap PA_{V}^{\mathcal{G}})
,
\mathbf{y}\in\mathrm{Ran}(PA_{V}^{\mathcal{F}}\setminus PA_{V}^{\mathcal{G}})
and
\mathbf{z}\in\mathrm{Ran}(PA_{V}^{\mathcal{G}}\setminus PA_{V}^{\mathcal{F}})
,
we have that

\mathcal{F}_{V}(\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y})=\mathcal{G}_{V}(\mathbf{x}\mathbf{z})

(where we assume w.l.o.g. the shown orderings of the arguments of the functions).

-

Let
\mathrm{Cn}(\mathcal{F})
denote the set of endogenous variables
V
of
\mathcal{F}
for which
\mathcal{F}_{V}
is a constant function, i.e., for some fixed
c\in\mathrm{Ran}(V)
,

\mathcal{F}_{V}(\mathbf{p})=c\text{ for all }\mathbf{p}\in PA_{V}^{\mathcal{F}}.

We say that
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{G}
are
equivalent up to dummy arguments
, denoted as
\mathcal{F}\sim\mathcal{G}
, if

(a)

\mathrm{En}(\mathcal{F})\setminus\mathrm{Cn}(\mathcal{F})=\mathrm{En}(\mathcal{G})\setminus\mathrm{Cn}(\mathcal{G})
,

(b)

and
\mathcal{F}_{V}\sim\mathcal{G}_{V}
holds for all
V\in\mathrm{En}(\mathcal{F})\setminus\mathrm{Cn}(\mathcal{F})
.

It is easy to see that
\sim
is an equivalence relation. Furthermore, the relation
\sim
is preserved under interventions.

Fact 3.2
.

For any function components
\mathcal{F},\mathcal{G}\in\mathbb{F}_{\sigma}
and consistent equality
\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x}
over
\sigma
, we have that

\mathcal{F}\sim\mathcal{G}
implies
\mathcal{F}_{\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x}}\sim\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x}}
.

Proof.

Straightforward; see
[
6
]
.

\square

We now generalize the equivalence relation
\sim
to the causal team level. Two nonempty causal teams
T=(T^{-},\mathcal{F})
and
S=(S^{-},\mathcal{G})
of the same signature
\sigma
are said to be

similar
, denoted as
T\sim S
, if
\mathcal{F}\sim\mathcal{G}
;

equivalent
, denoted as
T\approx S
, if
T\sim S
and
T^{-}=S^{-}
. This definition of equivalence is meant to describe, semantically, the conditions under which
S
and
T
cannot be distinguished by our formal languages.

Let us now define equivalence also for generalized causal teams. For any generalized causal team
T
,
write

T^{\mathcal{F}}:=\{(s,\mathcal{G})\in T\mid\mathcal{G}\sim\mathcal{F}\}.

Two generalized causal teams
S
and
T
are said to be
equivalent
, denoted as
S\approx T
, if
(S^{\mathcal{F}})^{-}=(T^{\mathcal{F}})^{-}
for all
\mathcal{F}\in\mathbb{F}_{\sigma}
.

Moreover, we define the equivalence relation
\approx
between elements of
\mathbb{S}\mathbbm{em}_{\sigma}
by taking

(s,\mathcal{F})\approx(t,\mathcal{G})~\text{ iff }~\{(s,\mathcal{F})\}\approx\{(t,\mathcal{G})\}~~(\text{ iff }~s=t\text{ and }\mathcal{F}\sim\mathcal{G}).

We now show that formulas in the logics we consider in this paper are invariant under causal equivalence. In other words, none of our languages can distinguish causal teams which are equivalent up to dummy arguments.

Theorem 3.3

(Invariance under causal equivalence)
.

Let
T,S
be two (generalized) causal teams over
\sigma
with
T\approx S
. For any formula
\varphi
of
\mathcal{CO}[\sigma]
,
\mathcal{CO}_{\footnotesize{\mathbin{{\setminus}\mspace{-5mu}{\setminus}}\hskip-0.66016pt/}}[\sigma]
or
\mathcal{COD}[\sigma]
, we have that
T\models\varphi\iff S\models\varphi.

Proof.

The theorem is proved by induction on
\varphi
. The case

3.2
Characterization of the function components

In this subsection we show that, up to dummy arguments, every system of functions of signature
\sigma
is definable in
\mathcal{CO}[\sigma]
. This result is crucial for adapting the standard methods of team semantics to the causal context.

For any function component
\mathcal{F}
over
\sigma
, define a
\mathcal{CO}[\sigma]
-formula

\displaystyle\Phi^{\mathcal{F}}:=\bigwedge_{V\in\mathrm{En}(\mathcal{F})\setminus\mathrm{Cn}(\mathcal{F})}\eta_{\sigma}(V)\land\bigwedge_{V\notin\mathrm{En}(\mathcal{F})\setminus\mathrm{Cn}(\mathcal{F})}\xi_{\sigma}(V).

where

\displaystyle\eta_{\sigma}(V):=\bigwedge\big\{

\displaystyle(\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{w}\land PA_{V}^{\mathcal{F}}=\mathbf{p})\boxright V=\mathcal{F}_{V}(\mathbf{p})

\displaystyle\quad\quad\mid\mathbf{W}=\mathrm{Dom}\setminus(PA_{V}^{\mathcal{F}}\cup\{V\}),~\mathbf{w}\in\mathrm{Ran}(\mathbf{W}),~\mathbf{p}\in\mathrm{Ran}(PA_{V}^{\mathcal{F}})\big\}

\displaystyle\text{and }~\xi_{\sigma}(V):=\bigwedge\big\{

\displaystyle V=v\supset(\mathbf{W}_{V}=\mathbf{w}\boxright V=v)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\mid v\in\mathrm{Ran}(V),\mathbf{W}_{V}=\mathrm{Dom}\setminus\{V\},\mathbf{w}\in\mathrm{Ran}(\mathbf{W}_{V})\big\}.

Intuitively, for each non-constant endogenous variable
V
of
\mathcal{F}
, the formula
\eta_{\sigma}(V)
specifies that all assignments in the (generalized) causal team
T
in question behave exactly as required by the function
\mathcal{F}_{V}
. For each variable
V
which, according to
\mathcal{F}
, is exogenous or generated by a constant function, the formula
\xi_{\sigma}(V)
states that
V
is not affected by interventions on other variables. Notice that, if
V\in\mathrm{Cn}(\mathcal{F})
, then
V
has a
\xi
but not an
\eta
clause. Our languages cannot tell apart an endogenous variable that is constant “by law” from an exogenous variable that happens to be (contingently) constant in a team.

4
Characterizing
\mathcal{CO}

In this section, we characterize the expressive power of
\mathcal{CO}
over causal teams. We also present a system of natural deduction for
\mathcal{CO}
that is sound and complete over both (recursive) causal teams and generalized causal teams. The system is equivalent to the Hilbert-style system of the same logic over (recursive) causal teams introduced in
[
4
]
.

4.1
Expressivity

We show that
\mathcal{CO}
-formulas capture the flat class of (generalized) causal teams, up to
\approx
-equivalence. Our result is analogous to known characterizations of flat languages in propositional team semantics (
[
31
]
). But in the case of causal teams we have a twist, given by the fact that only the unions of similar causal teams can be reasonably defined. We define such unions as follows.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
S=(S^{-},\mathcal{F})
and
T=(T^{-},\mathcal{G})
be two causal teams over the same signature
\sigma
with
S\sim T
. The union of
S
and
T
is defined as the causal team
S\cup T=(S^{-}\cup T^{-},\mathcal{H})
over
\sigma
, where

-

\mathrm{En}(\mathcal{H})=\mathrm{En}(\mathcal{F})\setminus\mathrm{Cn}(\mathcal{F})

-

and for each
V\in\mathrm{En}(\mathcal{H})
,
PA^{\mathcal{H}}_{V}=PA^{\mathcal{F}}_{V}\cap PA^{\mathcal{G}}_{V}
, and

\mathcal{H}_{V}(\mathbf{p})=\mathcal{F}_{V}(\mathbf{p}\mathbf{x})

for any
\mathbf{p}\in PA_{V}^{\mathcal{F}}\cap PA_{V}^{\mathcal{G}}
and
\mathbf{x}\in PA_{V}^{\mathcal{F}}\setminus PA_{V}^{\mathcal{G}}
.

Clearly,
\mathcal{H}\sim\mathcal{F}\sim\mathcal{G}
and thus
S\cup T\sim S\sim T
. Let us also remark that even though the definition may seem to look asymmetric as it appears to rely more on
\mathcal{F}
than on
\mathcal{G}
in its details, the notion of union is indeed well-defined, and it guarantees that
S\cup T=T\cup S
. This is because the definition requires that
\mathcal{F}\sim\mathcal{G}
, from which it follows that
\mathrm{En}(\mathcal{H})=\mathrm{En}(\mathcal{F})\setminus\mathrm{Cn}(\mathcal{F})=\mathrm{En}(\mathcal{G})\setminus\mathrm{Cn}(\mathcal{G})
, and also that
\mathcal{H}_{V}(\mathbf{p})=\mathcal{F}_{V}(\mathbf{p}\mathbf{x})=\mathcal{G}_{V}(\mathbf{p}\mathbf{y})
for any
\mathbf{p}\in PA_{V}^{\mathcal{F}}\cap PA_{V}^{\mathcal{G}}
,

(for causal teams)

(for generalized causal teams)

.

-

closed under equivalence

and

.

Fact 4.3
.

A class
\mathcal{K}
of (generalized) causal teams over
\sigma
is flat iff
\mathcal{K}
contains the empty team, is causally downward closed and closed under causal unions.

The class
\mathcal{K}_{\varphi}
is always nonempty as the empty team is always in

(by Theorem

is flat and closed under equivalence.
The main result of this section is the following characterization theorem which gives also the converse direction.

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
\mathcal{K}
be a nonempty class of (generalized)
causal teams over some signature
\sigma

\mathcal{K}
is definable by a
\mathcal{CO}[\sigma]
-formula if and only if
\mathcal{K}
is flat and closed under equivalence.

Before we present the proof of the theorem, let us first introduce a relation

between causal teams that is of central use in this proof.
For any (generalized) causal teams

and

for some (generalized) causal team

for all (generalized) causal teams

Fact 4.5
.

A class
\mathcal{K}
of (generalized) causal teams over

\mathcal{K}
is closed under the relation
\succcurlyeq

and

We leave it for the reader to verify that

is a partial order (modulo

and

imply

. The next lemma lists some other useful properties of the relation

T
, we define
T^{\mathcal{F}}=\{(s,\mathcal{G})\in T\mid\mathcal{G}\sim\mathcal{F}\}
.

Lemma 4.6
.

The following holds in the context of generalized causal teams:

(i)

S\preccurlyeq T\iff\{(s,\mathcal{F})\}\preccurlyeq T
for all
(s,\mathcal{F})\in S
.

(ii)

If

is uniform and

is also uniform and

.

(iii)

If

for all
\mathcal{F}\in\mathbb{F}_{\sigma}
.

(iv)

If

are causal teams, then

(v)

If
S_{i}\preccurlyeq T_{i}

\bigcup_{i\in I}S_{i}\preccurlyeq\bigcup_{i\in I}T_{i}

(vi)

such that
S=\bigcup_{i\in I}S_{i}
.

Proof.

Item (i) is easy to prove,
items (ii) and (iii) are obvious, and item (iv) follows easily from the fact that both
S^{g}
and
T^{g}
are uniform. We only give detailed proof for items (v) and (vi).

Let us begin with (v). Suppose that
S_{i}\approx R_{i}\subseteq T_{i}
for some
R_{i}
(
i\in I
). Let
R=\bigcup_{i\in I}R_{i}
. Clearly,
\bigcup_{i\in I}R_{i}\subseteq\bigcup_{i\in I}T_{i}
. It remains to show that
S\approx R
for
S=\bigcup_{i\in I}S_{i}
. For every

and every

, we have that
(S_{i}^{\mathcal{F}})^{-}=(R_{i}^{\mathcal{F}})^{-}
. Thus,

(S^{\mathcal{F}})^{-}=\bigcup_{i\in I}(S_{i}^{\mathcal{F}})^{-}=\bigcup_{i\in I}(R_{i}^{\mathcal{F}})^{-}=(R^{\mathcal{F}})^{-}.

Now we prove (vi). By the assumption, there is an
R
such that
S\approx R\subseteq T=\bigcup_{i\in I}T_{i}
. For every

, define

and

S_{i}:=\{(s,\mathcal{F})\in S\mid\{(s,\mathcal{F})\}\preccurlyeq R_{i}\}.

Clearly,
R_{i}\subseteq T_{i}
. To see that
S_{i}\preccurlyeq T_{i}
, it remains to verify that

. For any

, we have that

\displaystyle s\in(S_{i}^{\mathcal{F}})^{-}

\displaystyle\iff(s,\mathcal{G})\in S_{i}\text{ for some }\mathcal{G}\sim\mathcal{F}\iff\{(s,\mathcal{G})\}\preccurlyeq R_{i}\text{ for some }\mathcal{G}\sim\mathcal{F}

\displaystyle\iff\{(s,\mathcal{G})\}\preccurlyeq R_{i}^{\mathcal{F}}\text{ for some }\mathcal{G}\sim\mathcal{F}\iff s\in(R_{i}^{\mathcal{F}})^{-}.

Finally, we show that

. By definition,

for every

. Conversely, for every

, we have by item (i) that

Thus,

.
\square

that defines the complement of the set of

-successors of a (generalized) causal team

in the pure team setting.

The formula

is a disjunction of three parts. The first disjunct requires some words of comment. Its purpose is to characterize the size of a (generalized) causal team. The size
of a causal team

can be (naturally) defined as the cardinality of its team component
T^{-}
. Generalized causal teams have a more complex structure, therefore the notion of size is more subtle. Consider for example the generalized causal team

\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{G}
are not similar (i.e.,
\mathcal{F}\not\sim\mathcal{G}
), the two elements of

should be counted as distinct; therefore, the cardinality of
T^{-}
(i.e.,
|T^{-}|=|\{s\}|=1
) is not a correct measure of the size of
T
. On the other hand,
if
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{G}
are similar (i.e.,
\mathcal{F}\sim\mathcal{G}
, or
(s,\mathcal{F})\approx(s,\mathcal{G})
), the two elements
(s,\mathcal{F})
and
(s,\mathcal{G})
cannot be told apart using our languages; thus our languages do not allow us to distinguish the size of
T
from that of one of its singleton subteams. We are then forced to say that
T
has size
1
; therefore also
|T|=2
is not a correct measure of the size of
T
.
For these reasons, for generalized causal teams
T
, we shall be more concerned with the cardinality
of the quotient team of
T
over
\approx
between pairs
(s,\mathcal{F})\in T
, denoted as
T/_{\approx}
.

If
T
is uniform, then clearly
|T/_{\approx}|=|T^{-}|
. In particular, if

is a causal team, then for the generalized causal team

|S^{-}|=|S^{g}/_{\approx}|
.

of having cardinality at most

6
Conclusion

We have answered the main questions concerning the expressive power and the existence of complete deduction calculi for the languages that were proposed in
[
3
]
and
[
4
]
, and which involve both interventionist counterfactuals and (contingent) dependencies. We have introduced a generalized causal team semantics that extends the causal discourse to cases in which there may be uncertainty about the causal laws; and we have analyzed the properties of the languages from
[
3
,
4
]
also when they are evaluated in this generalized semantics.
We believe the present work may provide guidelines for the investigation of further notions of dependence and causation in causal team semantics and its variants.

Our work shows that many of the methodologies developed in the literature on team semantics can be adapted to the (generalized) causal team semantics. At the same time, a number of peculiarities emerged that set apart these semantic frameworks from the usual team semantics. An interesting anomaly (as noted in
[
4
]
) is the existence of formulas whose interpretation only depends on the causal structure; this is the case for the “direct cause” formula mentioned in the introduction (call it
\beta_{\mathrm{DC}}(X,V)
), or for the following formula:

\beta_{\mathrm{En}}(V):=\bigvee_{X\in\mathbf{W}_{V}}\beta_{\mathrm{DC}}(X,V),

which can be shown to characterize the property of
V
being endogenous (via a non-constant function).
One unusual aspect of such formulas is that not only the class of causal teams they define is downward closed, but also its complement (taken together with the empty team) is.
Some more peculiarities have emerged that only concern the original causal team semantics: first, the failure of the disjunction property for
\mathop{\,{\mathbin{{\setminus}\mspace{-5mu}{\setminus}}\hskip-0.82521pt/}\,}
, and of analogous instantiation properties for the dependence atoms; and secondly, the collapse of
\mathop{\,{\mathbin{{\setminus}\mspace{-5mu}{\setminus}}\hskip-0.82521pt/}\,}
(global disjunction) to
\lor
(tensor disjunction) for some special classes of disjuncts. This kind of “collapse” is possible in pure team semantics (and over generalized causal teams) only if one of the disjuncts entails the other, which is however not the case in some of the examples we isolated.

We point out that our calculi are sound only for
recursive
systems, i.e., when the causal graph is acyclic. The general case (and special cases such as the “Lewisian” systems considered in
[
32
]
) will require a separate study. We point out, however, that each of our deduction systems can be adapted to the case of
unique-solution
(possibly generalized) causal teams by replacing the
Recur
rule with an inference rule that expresses the
Reversibility
axiom from
[
12
]
:

\Large\frac{(\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x}\land Y=y)\boxright W=w\hskip 25.0pt(\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x}\land W=w)\boxright Y=y}{\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x}\boxright Y=y}\normalsize\hskip 5.0pt\text{ (for $Y\neq W$) }.

See
[
4
]
for the details on how the semantics should be adapted for this case. The analysis of causal languages beyond the unique-solution case leads naturally to the consideration of operators (such as the
might
-counterfactuals) that do not preserve downward closure. It is difficult to guess, at this stage, to what extent the methodologies of team semantics can be adapted to the study of causal languages that are not downward closed. When this issue is solved, it might be interesting to investigate languages that also include non-downward closed contingent (in)dependencies, such as the
independence atoms
and
inclusion
atoms considered in the literature on pure team semantics (
[
15
,
13
]
). Finally, it would be interesting to consider counterfactuals with more complex antecedents – e.g., antecedents containing disjunctions or negations. There have been a number of (often conflicting) proposals in the literature for the semantics of such interventionist counterfactuals
[
7
,
1
,
10
,
22
,
21
]
; it is our hope that causal team semantics can shed some light on this issue. For example, a natural interpretation of a counterfactual
(X=1\lor X=2)\boxright\psi
is that
\psi
certainly holds, even if we are not sure which of the interventions
do(X=1)
or
do(X=2)
has been performed. This uncertainty may be modeled by taking the union of the two (generalized) causal teams resulting from applying each intervention.
