Convergence analysis of some tent-based schemes for linear hyperbolic systems Thanks: This work was supported in part by NSF grant DMS-1912779.
Dow Drake, Jay Gopalakrishnan, Joachim Schöberl, Christoph Wintersteiger

Abstract
. Finite element methods for symmetric linear hyperbolic systems using unstructured advancing fronts (satisfying a causality condition) are considered in this work. Convergence results and error bounds are obtained for mapped tent pitching schemes made with standard discontinuous Galerkin discretizations for spatial approximation on mapped tents. Techniques to study semidiscretization on mapped tents, design fully discrete schemes, prove local error bounds, prove stability on spacetime fronts, and bound error propagated through unstructured layers are developed.

65M12

1.
Introduction

Tent-based numerical methods for hyperbolic equations stratify a
spacetime simulation region in an unstructured manner, by tent-shaped
subregions, and advance solutions across them
progressively in time.
The partitioning into tents provides a rational design
for local time stepping, maintaining high order accuracy in both space
and time, and without any ad hoc projection or extrapolation steps, an
advantage that has been and continues to be effectively leveraged by
many researchers
[
1
,
2
,
10
,
17
,
18
,
20
,
22
]
.
Nevertheless, a drawback of tents is that they are not
tensor products of a spatial domain with a
time interval, necessitating development of new tent (spacetime)
discretizations coupling too many
spatiotemporal unknowns. In
[
13
]
, we overcame
this drawback by using a mapping technique that transforms spacetime
tents to tensor product spacetime cylinders. This opened avenues to
use standard techniques like the discontinuous Galerkin (DG)
discretizations for spatial discretization, together with efficient
matrix-free
time stepping schemes, on the mapped tents. Such methods, referred to
as
Mapped Tent Pitching
(MTP) schemes, have been applied to solve
a variety of linear and nonlinear hyperbolic
systems
[
12
,
13
,
14
]
.

This is the first paper to provide convergence theorems for MTP
schemes. Although the scope of the analysis here is limited to linear
hyperbolic systems, we identify what we consider to be the basic
ingredients for error analysis of MTP schemes, such as a norm in which
stability on spacetime fronts can be obtained. We use techniques to
bound the propagation of error through layers of tents similar to
those in
[
10
,
17
]
. However, a number of new
tools are needed to overcome difficulties arising from a
time-dependent mass matrix generated due to the mapping. As we already
noted in
[
12
]
, the use of classical explicit
Runge-Kutta time stepping on these mapped systems
leads to loss of higher orders of convergence due
to the complications created by the map. We outlined an algorithmic
solution in
[
12
]
, namely the
Structure-Aware
Taylor
(SAT) time stepping scheme, that accounts for the
specific structure of the time-dependent mass matrix. Here we shall
provide
a priori
error bounds for these as well as a few other
schemes.

Our analysis is divided into the next three sections. First, we borrow
a spatial DG discretization framework from
[
3
,
9
]
which permits the treatment of many important examples
of symmetric hyperbolic systems and many boundary condition choices,
all at once. The application of this framework to the mapped equation
(the pull back of the hyperbolic system from the physical tent to the
spacetime cylinder), is detailed in Section
2
. We
then combine it with a semidiscrete analysis in
Section
3
. While the analysis in that section
ignores errors due to
time discretization that are undoubtedly present in practice, it
immediately clarifies in what norms one may expect stability on
spacetime advancing fronts, and what error bounds might be provable
after time discretization. The main result of this section is that
under the conditions spelled out later, we may expect the error in the
numerical solution at the final time to be
O(h^{p+1/2})
where
h

represents a spatial mesh size parameter and
p
denotes the
spatial polynomial degree (used in the spatial DG discretization).
In this form,
the result is comparable to
[
17
, Theorem 5.1]
that
provides the same rate for their spacetime DG method using
spacetime polynomials of degree
p
on tents.

In Section
4
, we discuss several fully discrete
schemes that combine the spatial DG discretization on a mapped tent
with SAT or other time stepping. We find that proving stability of
the fully discrete schemes requires some trickery. Ever since
the classical work of
[
16
]
, we know that stability
regions and “naive
spectral stability analysis based on scalar eigenvalues arguments may
be misleading.” Many researchers have since pursued energy-type arguments
to prove stability of time stepping schemes with spatial DG
discretizations
[
3
,
5
,
21
,
23
]
. However, we are not able to directly apply existing
techniques due to the nonstandard nature of the system we obtain after
mapping the hyperbolic equation. Therefore, we start afresh,
beginning with the most basic scheme and proceeding to more
complicated cases. Namely, in §
4.2

we prove unconditional strong stability for a lowest order
tent-implicit scheme. Then, we proceed to analyze a lowest order
“iterated” explicit scheme in §
4.3
,
constructed as an iterative solver for the implicit scheme, for which
we prove a nonstandard conditional stability. Then, in
§
4.4
, we proceed to an
s
-stage SAT
scheme and show that its local error in a tent is
O(h^{s})
, which
is comparable to the
s
th power of the time step since the amount of
local time advance in tents
is tied to its spatial mesh size. We are
able to prove, in one case, stability under a traditional
Courant-Friedrichs-Levy (CFL) condition, by which we mean that the
amount of
local
time advance within a tent is limited by a
constant multiple of the
local
spatial mesh size. In another
case, we prove stability under a “3/2 CFL” condition.
In some non-tent-based
DG methods, others have encountered
a similar (4/3 CFL) limitation in stability
analyses
[
3
]
. We offer the above-mentioned cases
not as the last word on stability, but rather to spur further research
into this interesting topic.

In the remainder of this section, we establish notation and the lingua
franca of tents that we use throughout.
Consider a
cylindrical domain
{\varOmega}\times(0,T)

in the physical spacetime, where the spatial
domain
{\varOmega}
is an open bounded subset of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
.
We assume that
{\varOmega}
is subdivided by a
simplicial mesh
{\varOmega_{h}}
. The subscript
h
denotes the maximal element
diameter of the spatial mesh
{\varOmega_{h}}
. Spacetime tents are built atop
this spatial mesh, using the algorithms in
[
13
]

or
[
8
]
. We start by viewing the spatial mesh

{\varOmega_{h}}
at time
t=0
as the initial advancing front. When one mesh
vertex
{\mathtt{v}}
is moved forward in time, while keeping all other
vertices fixed, the advancing front is updated to the piecewise planar
surface formed by connecting the raised
{\mathtt{v}}
to its neighboring
vertices. The new front differs from the old by a tent-shaped region,
which we denote by
{T^{\mathtt{v}}}
. Its projection onto
{\varOmega}
gives the vertex
patch
{\varOmega^{{\mathtt{v}}}}
of all spatial simplices connected to the vertex

{\mathtt{v}}
. We shall refer to this process as
pitching
the tent

{T^{\mathtt{v}}}
. For concurrency, one pitches multiple tents simultaneously at
vertices whose vertex patches do not have a mesh element in their
pairwise intersections, as in Figure
1(b)
.
The canopies of these spacetime tents can be
represented as the graph of
\varphi_{1}(x)
, a continuous function that
is piecewise linear with respect to the mesh
{\varOmega_{h}}
(whose value is
zero in locations where tents are not yet erected). These canopies
together form the next advancing front:

C_{1}=\{(x,\varphi_{1}(x)):x\in{\varOmega}\}
. Note that the time coordinate of
a point in
C_{1}
is never less than that of
the corresponding point in the first front

C_{0}={\varOmega}\times\{0\}
. This process is repeated by pitching tents
atop
C_{1}
, and later atop the subsequent advancing fronts that result
from each step (as illustrated
in Figures
1(c)
–
1(f)
).

Reiterating, the
advancing front
at step
i
is
the graph of a lowest-order Lagrange finite element function

\varphi_{i}(x)
:

(1.1)

C_{i}=\{(x,\varphi_{i}(x)):x\in{\varOmega}\}.

We shall refer to the region between two successive advancing fronts
as a
layer
, i.e.,

(1.2)

L_{i}=\{(x,t)\in{\varOmega}\times(0,T):\;\varphi_{i-1}(x)\leq t\leq\varphi_{i}(x)\}

denotes the
i
th layer for
i=1,2,\ldots,m.
The layer
L_{i}

(see Figure
1
) is
made above
C_{i-1}
by pitching tents atop vertex patches associated
with a subset of mesh vertices of
{\varOmega_{h}}
, which form the
pitch
locations
at that stage. Let
V_{i}
denote the collection of such
vertices identifying the pitch locations on
C_{i-1}
. Then

L_{i}=\mathop{\bigcup}_{{\mathtt{v}}\in V_{i}}{T^{\mathtt{v}}}.

The spacetime will generally contain multiple tents pitched at the
same vertex
{\mathtt{v}}
at different time coordinates. Although referring
to a tent by its spatial pitch location alone, as in
{T^{\mathtt{v}}}
above, is
generally ambiguous, it will not confuse us since we will usually be
occupied with analyzing one tent at a time.

A tent can be expressed as

(1.3)

{T^{\mathtt{v}}}=\{(x,t):x\in{\varOmega^{{\mathtt{v}}}},\;\varphi_{\mathrm{bot}}^{\mathtt{v}}(x)\leq t\leq\varphi_{\mathrm{top}}^{\mathtt{v}}(x)\}

where

\varphi_{\mathrm{bot}}^{\mathtt{v}}
and
\varphi_{\mathrm{top}}^{\mathtt{v}}
are continuous functions on
{\varOmega^{{\mathtt{v}}}}
that are
piecewise linear with respect to the mesh elements forming the vertex
patch
{\varOmega^{{\mathtt{v}}}}.
The function
\delta^{\mathtt{v}}(x)=\varphi_{\mathrm{top}}^{\mathtt{v}}(x)-\varphi_{\mathrm{bot}}^{\mathtt{v}}(x)
on
{\varOmega^{{\mathtt{v}}}}

will feature often in the sequel. It arises as a weight in transformed
integrals and is degenerate at the points where tent top meets tent
bottom.

One reason for working with tents is the ease by which
causality
can
be imposed, simply by adjusting the height of the
tent pole
, the
line segment connecting
({\mathtt{v}},\varphi_{\mathrm{bot}}^{\mathtt{v}}({\mathtt{v}}))
to

({\mathtt{v}},\varphi_{\mathrm{top}}^{\mathtt{v}}({\mathtt{v}}))
. By the definition of hyperbolicity, the
maximal wave speed
c
is finite. When each spacetime tent encloses
the domain of dependence of all its points, causality holds. In other
words, if

\|(\mathop{\mathrm{grad}_{x}}\varphi_{i})(x)\|_{2}<\frac{1}{c},\qquad x\in{\varOmega},

on all advancing fronts, then causality holds. In this paper, in place
of the strict inequality, we assume we are given a strict upper bound

\hat{c}
for the maximal wave speed
c
and that our mesh of spacetime
tents is constructed so that

(1.4)

\|(\mathop{\mathrm{grad}_{x}}\varphi_{i})(x)\|_{2}\leq\frac{1}{\hat{c}}<\frac{1}{c}\qquad x\in{\varOmega}.

We will refer to (
1.4
) as the
causality
condition
. Algorithms for constructing tent meshes
satisfying (
1.4
) can be
found in
[
8
,
13
]
. They terminate
filling the spacetime
{\varOmega}\times(0,T)
with tents so that
the first and
the last advancing fronts,
C_{0}
and
C_{m}
, are flat, i.e.,

\varphi_{0}(x)\equiv 0
and
\varphi_{m}(x)\equiv T
.
In practice, we often select (as in Figure
1
)
a
t_{\mathrm{slab}}<T
such that

T=n_{\mathrm{slabs}}t_{\mathrm{slab}}
,
run the tent meshing algorithm to
fill the subregion

{\varOmega}\times(0,t_{\mathrm{slab}})
, and then translate the
same mesh to reuse it
n_{\mathrm{slabs}}-1
times to
cover the full spacetime domain
{\varOmega}\times(0,T)
without further
meshing overhead.
However, for the ensuing analysis, we ignore this extra subdivision
into smaller spacetime slabs (so
t_{\mathrm{slab}}=T
henceforth).
With this background
in mind, we proceed to show
how to map tents and construct an
MTP scheme after fixing a model problem.

2.
A model problem for analysis

In this section, we describe a model symmetric linear hyperbolic
system and its MTP discretization that we shall be occupied
with. Although MTP schemes can be applied much more generally (as
shown in
[
13
,
14
]
), we restrict to
this model for transparently presenting the essential new ideas
needed for a convergence analysis.

2.1.
A symmetric linear hyperbolic system

Let
L
be a positive integer (and let
N
, as before,
denote the spatial dimension). Suppose that

\mathcal{L}^{({{j}})}:{\varOmega}\to\mathbb{R}^{L\times L}
, for
j=1,\ldots,N
and

\mathcal{G}:{\varOmega}\to\mathbb{R}^{L\times L}
are symmetric bounded matrix-valued
functions and suppose
\mathcal{G}
is uniformly positive definite in

\bar{\varOmega}
. Our model problem is the following linear hyperbolic
system of
L
equations, in
L
unknowns, denoted by
u(x,t)
, or in
terms of scalar
components, by
u_{k}(x,t)
:

(2.1)

\mathop{\partial_{t}}g(u)+\mathop{\mathrm{div}_{x}}f(u)=0,

with

[g(u)]_{l}=\sum_{k=1}^{L}\mathcal{G}_{lk}u_{k},\qquad[f(u)]_{lj}=\sum_{k=1}^{L}\mathcal{L}^{({{j}})}_{lk}u_{k}.

We have restricted ourselves to time-independent coefficients, and
shall do so also for boundary conditions, which are expressed using a
matrix field
\mathcal{B}:\partial{\varOmega}\to\mathbb{R}^{L\times L}
. Boundary
conditions are considered in the form studied by
Friedrichs
[
11
]
, namely,

(2.2)

(\mathcal{D}-\mathcal{B})u=0\qquad\text{ on }\partial{\varOmega}

where

(2.3)

\mathcal{D}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}n_{j}\mathcal{L}^{({{j}})}.

Note that
\mathcal{D}
, in general, depends on the point
x\in\partial{\varOmega}
as
well as on
n(x)
, the spatial unit outward normal at
x
, and when
we wish to emphasize these dependencies, we shall denote it by
\mathcal{D}^{(n)}
,

\mathcal{D}(x)
,
or
\mathcal{D}(x)^{(n)}
. Note that later in the sequel
n
(or
n(x)
)
will also be used to
generically denote the outward unit normal on boundaries of other
domains (such as mesh elements). Of course, the hyperbolic
system (
2.1
) must also be supplemented with an initial
condition,

(2.4)

u(x,0)=u^{0}(x)\qquad x\in{\varOmega}

at time
t=0
for some given initial data
u^{0}
.

Friedrichs
[
11
]

identified conditions on the operator
\mathcal{B}
for obtaining
well-posed boundary value problems. We shall borrow the same
conditions and impose them pointwise on
\partial{\varOmega}\times(0,T)
. In
particular, at each
x\in\partial{\varOmega}
and
t\in(0,T)
, we assume

\ker(\mathcal{D}-\mathcal{B})+\ker(\mathcal{D}+\mathcal{B})=\mathbb{R}^{L}
and

\mathcal{B}+\mathcal{B}^{t}\geq 0.
(The latter
is to be interpreted as

(\mathcal{B}(x)+\mathcal{B}(x)^{t})y\cdot y=2\mathcal{B}(x)y\cdot y\geq 0

for all vectors
y\in\mathbb{R}^{L}
at any
x\in\partial{\varOmega}
.) To simplify the analysis, we shall
also assume that

(2.5)

\sum_{j=1}^{N}\partial_{j}\mathcal{L}^{({{j}})}=0

in the sense of distributions (so jumps in
\mathcal{L}^{({{j}})}(x)
are allowed so
long as (
2.5
) holds) and

(2.6)

\mathcal{G}\text{ and }\mathcal{L}^{({{j}})}\text{ are constant on each mesh element }K\in{\varOmega_{h}}.

Across a mesh facet
F
of normal
n_{F}
, it is easy to see
that (
2.5
) implies the continuity of

\mathcal{D}^{(n_{F})}.
As long as we obtain this normal continuity (which is
needed in our analysis),
assumption (
2.5
) can be relaxed to other forms (such
as what is suggested in
[
9
, equation (A.4)]
),
at the expense of a few
additional technicalities.
Assumption (
2.6
) allows us to zero out some projection
error
terms instead of tracking such small terms in error estimates.

2.2.
Mapping tents

Consider a tent expressed as in (
1.3
). We map to a tent
{T^{\mathtt{v}}}

from a cylindrical tensor product domain
{\hat{T}^{\mathtt{v}}}={\varOmega^{{\mathtt{v}}}}\times(0,1)

using the map
\varPhi^{\mathtt{v}}(x,\hat{t})=(x,\varphi^{\mathtt{v}}(x,\hat{t}))

where

\varphi^{\mathtt{v}}(x,\hat{t})=(1-\hat{t})\varphi_{\mathrm{bot}}^{\mathtt{v}}(x)+\hat{t}\varphi_{\mathrm{top}}^{\mathtt{v}}(x)=\varphi_{\mathrm{bot}}^{\mathtt{v}}(x)+\hat{t}\,\delta^{\mathtt{v}}(x)

(see
[
13
, Fig. 2]
for an illustration of this map).
We will drop the superscript
{\mathtt{v}}
when it is obvious from context.
Clearly,
\varPhi({\hat{T}^{\mathtt{v}}})={T^{\mathtt{v}}}
and the interior of
{\hat{T}^{\mathtt{v}}}
is mapped
one-to-one onto the interior of
{T^{\mathtt{v}}}
(but the map is not one-to-one
from the boundary of
{\hat{T}^{\mathtt{v}}}
to the boundary of
{T^{\mathtt{v}}}
). The coordinate

\hat{t}
in
{\hat{T}^{\mathtt{v}}}
will be referred to as the
pseudotime

coordinate. Note that the time coordinate
t
in the physical
spacetime twists space and pseudotime together since it is given by

t=\varphi(x,\hat{t})
.

The Jacobian matrix of the map
\varPhi
is easily computed:

(2.7)

\mathop{\mathrm{grad}_{x\hat{t}}}\varPhi=\begin{bmatrix}I&0\\
(\mathop{\mathrm{grad}_{x}}\varphi)^{T}&\delta\end{bmatrix}

Using it in a Piola transformation, it can be shown

[
13
, Theorem 2]
that
the mapped hyperbolic solution

\hat{u}=u\circ\varPhi
satisfies

(2.8)

\mathop{\partial_{\hat{t}}}\left[g({\hat{u}})-f({\hat{u}})\mathop{\mathrm{grad}_{x}}\varphi\right]+\mathop{\mathrm{div}_{x}}\big[\delta f({\hat{u}})\big]=0,\qquad\text{ in }{\hat{T}^{\mathtt{v}}},

whenever
u
solves (
2.1
). MTP schemes proceed by
solving (
2.8
) by various discretization strategies
(particularly those that leverage the tensor product nature of space
and pseudotime in
{\hat{T}^{\mathtt{v}}}
) and then pulling back the computed solution
to the physical spacetime. We now proceed to discuss a discretization
strategy that uses a DG spatial discretization on
{\varOmega^{{\mathtt{v}}}}
. We will
combine it with pseudotime discretizations later (in Section
4
).

2.3.
Spatial discretization on mapped tents

The mapped equation (
2.8
) can be approximated by any standard
scheme that allows for a time dependent mass matrix. Our focus is on
discontinuous Galerkin (DG) discretizations. Since there are numerous
flavors of DG schemes and numerical fluxes, for efficiently covering
various choices, we use the framework of
[
9
]
(see
also
[
3
]
), a simplified version of which, adapted
to our purposes, is described next. Their framework is motivated by
the previously mentioned early work of Friedrichs
[
11
]
, and
other previous authors have also been similarly motivated while
considering boundary conditions, notably
[
10
]

and
[
17
]
in the context of tents and spacetime methods.

We assume that
{\varOmega_{h}}
is a shape regular conforming simplicial mesh of
domain
{\varOmega}
. We use
a\lesssim b
to indicate that there is a
constant
C>0
such that
a\leq Cb
and that the value of
C
may be
chosen independently of any spatial mesh chosen from the shape regular
family. The value of the generic constant
C
in “
\lesssim
” may
differ at different occurrences and is allowed to depend on the wave
speed, material coefficients, spatial polynomial degree, etc. Let

P_{p}(K)
denote the space of polynomials of degree at most
p

restricted to the domain
K
and let
V_{h}=\{v:v|_{K}\in P_{p}(K)^{L}

for any mesh element
K\in{\varOmega_{h}}\}
. Let
{\varOmega_{h}^{{\mathtt{v}}}}
denote the collection
of elements in the vertex patch
{\varOmega^{{\mathtt{v}}}}
of a mesh vertex
v
. Let

V_{h}^{\mathtt{v}}
denote the restriction of the spatial DG space on
{\varOmega^{{\mathtt{v}}}}
and let

\psi_{j}(x)
denote a basis for
V_{h}^{\mathtt{v}}
. The semidiscrete approximation
of
{\hat{u}}
is of the form

{\hat{u}}_{h}(x,{\hat{t}})=\sum_{j}U_{j}({\hat{t}})\psi_{j}(x).

We consider a DG semidiscretization of (
2.8
) of the form
displayed next in (
2.9
). In the spatial integrals there and
throughout, we do not explicitly indicate the measure (volume or
boundary measure) as it will be understood from context. For each
fixed
0<\hat{t}\leq 1
, the function
{\hat{u}}_{h}(\cdot,\hat{t})
satisfies

(2.9)

\int_{\varOmega^{{\mathtt{v}}}}\mathop{\partial_{\hat{t}}}\big[g({\hat{u}}_{h})-f({\hat{u}}_{h})\mathop{\mathrm{grad}_{x}}\varphi\big]\cdot v=\sum_{K\in{\varOmega_{h}^{{\mathtt{v}}}}}\bigg[\int_{K}\delta f({\hat{u}}_{h}):\mathop{\mathrm{grad}_{x}}v-\int_{\partial K}\delta\hat{F}^{n}_{{\hat{u}}_{h}}\cdot v\bigg],

for all
v\in V_{h}^{\mathtt{v}},
where the numerical flux
\hat{F}^{n}_{{\hat{u}}_{h}}
on an
element boundary
\partial K
is defined using the values of
{\hat{u}}_{h}
from
the element
K
as well as from the neighboring element
K_{o}
, as
follows. For any
w\in V_{h}^{\mathtt{v}}
, at a point

x\in\partial K\cap\partial K_{o}
, letting
w_{o}=w|_{K_{o}}
, define

\{w\}(x)=\frac{1}{2}(w+w_{o}),\qquad\ldbrack{{w}}\rdbrack(x)=w-w_{o}.

Then,
\hat{F}^{n}_{w}
is assumed to take the form

(2.10)

\hat{F}^{n}_{w}=\begin{cases}\mathcal{D}\{w\}+S\ldbrack{{w}}\rdbrack&\text{ on }\partial K\setminus\partial{\varOmega},\\
\frac{1}{2}(\mathcal{D}+B)w&\text{ on }\partial K\cap\partial{\varOmega}.\end{cases}

Here,
\mathcal{D}
is defined by (
2.3
), with the vector

n
now denoting the
outward unit normal on
\partial K
,

S:\mathcal{F}_{i}\to\mathbb{R}^{L\times L}

denotes a
stabilization matrix on interior facets of

\mathcal{F}_{i}=\bigcup\{\partial K\setminus\partial{\varOmega}:\;K\in{\varOmega_{h}^{{\mathtt{v}}}}\}
,
and

B:\partial{\varOmega}\to\mathbb{R}^{L\times L}
,
is used to model the exact boundary condition
\mathcal{B}
with any needed extra
stabilization on boundary facets.
Note that
S
is
single-valued on
\mathcal{F}_{i}
(while
\mathcal{D}
is multivalued and
depends on the sign
of the normal on an element boundary).
Let
\|\cdot\|_{2}
denote the Euclidean norm (of a vector, or the
induced norm for a matrix) and let

|y|_{S}=(Sy\cdot y)^{1/2}
and
|y|_{B}=(By\cdot y)^{1/2}
.
We assume that

(2.11a)

\displaystyle\ker\left(\mathcal{D}(x)-\mathcal{B}(x)\right)\,

\displaystyle\subseteq\,\ker\left(\mathcal{D}(x)-B(x)\right)

\displaystyle x\in\partial{\varOmega},

(2.11b)

\displaystyle B(x)+B(x)^{t}

\displaystyle\geq 0,\qquad\|B(x)\|_{2}\lesssim 1,

\displaystyle x\in\partial{\varOmega},

(2.11c)

\displaystyle\left(\mathcal{D}(x)+B(x)\right)y\;\cdot z\;

\displaystyle\lesssim\;\|y\|_{2}\;|z|_{B},

\displaystyle x\in\partial{\varOmega},\;y,z\in\mathbb{R}^{L},

(2.11d)

\displaystyle S(x)+S(x)^{t}

\displaystyle\geq 0,\qquad\|S(x)\|_{2}\lesssim 1,

\displaystyle x\in\mathcal{F}_{i},

(2.11e)

\displaystyle S(x)y\cdot z\;

\displaystyle\lesssim\;|y|_{S}\;|z|_{S},

\displaystyle x\in\mathcal{F}_{i},\;y,z\in\mathbb{R}^{L}.

(2.11f)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(x)y\cdot z\;

\displaystyle\lesssim\;\|y\|_{2}\;|z|_{S},

\displaystyle x\in\mathcal{F}_{i},\;y,z\in\mathbb{R}^{L}.

These form a subset of the “design conditions for DG methods”
in

that we shall need for our analysis in
the next section.

2.4.
Examples

The following examples show a variety of equations, boundary
conditions, and their
well-known discretizations that conform to the framework
introduced above. We will work out the first example in some detail
and describe the rest telegraphically since similar examples can be
found in the literature
[

9
,

Example 2.1

(Maxwell equations with impedance boundary conditions)
.

Suppose we are given
electric permittivity
{\varepsilon}(x)
and magnetic
permeability
\mu(x)

as positive functions on
{\varOmega}
and let
Z>0
.
The Maxwell system for
the electric field
E(x,t)
and magnetic field
H(x,t)
, with
impedance boundary conditions, consists of

(2.12a)

\displaystyle{\varepsilon}\mathop{\partial_{t}}E-\mathop{\text{curl}}H=0,\quad\quad\mu\mathop{\partial_{t}}H+\mathop{\text{curl}}E

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\text{ in }{\varOmega}\times(0,T),

(2.12b)

\displaystyle n\times E-Zn\times(H\times n)

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\text{ on }\partial{\varOmega}\times(0,T).

To fit this into the prior setting, we put

N=3,

L=6,

and

\displaystyle u=\begin{bmatrix}E\\
H\end{bmatrix},\quad\mathcal{L}^{({{j}})}=\begin{bmatrix}0&[\epsilon^{j}]\\
[\epsilon^{j}]^{t}&0\end{bmatrix},\quad\mathcal{G}=\begin{bmatrix}{\varepsilon}I&0\\
0&\mu I\end{bmatrix},\quad\mathcal{B}=\begin{bmatrix}0&\mathcal{N}\\
\mathcal{N}&-2Z\mathcal{N}^{t}\mathcal{N}\end{bmatrix},

where
\epsilon^{j}
is the
3\times 3
matrix whose
(l,m)
th entry
equals the value of the Levi-Civita alternator
\epsilon_{jlm}
and

\mathcal{N}=\sum_{j=1}^{3}n_{j}\epsilon^{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}
. Noting
that
\mathcal{N}^{t}=-\mathcal{N},

\mathcal{N}E=E\times n,
and

\mathcal{N}^{t}\mathcal{N}H=n\times(H\times n),

it is easy to see that

(2.13)

\mathcal{D}=\begin{bmatrix}0&\mathcal{N}\\
\mathcal{N}^{t}&0\end{bmatrix},\quad(\mathcal{D}-\mathcal{B})\begin{bmatrix}E\\
H\end{bmatrix}=2\begin{bmatrix}0\\
n\times E-Zn\times(H\times n)\end{bmatrix},

so that (
2.2
) indeed imposes the impedance
boundary condition (
2.12b
).

Next, for the DG discretization, set

B=\frac{1}{1+Z}\begin{bmatrix}2\mathcal{N}\mathcal{N}^{t}&(1-Z)\mathcal{N}\\
(1-Z)\mathcal{N}&2Z\mathcal{N}\mathcal{N}^{t}\end{bmatrix},\quad S=\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{N}^{t}\mathcal{N}&0\\
0&\mathcal{N}^{t}\mathcal{N}\end{bmatrix}.

Since
\|n\times(E\times n)\|_{2}^{2}=\|\mathcal{N}E\|_{2}^{2}
,
setting
b=n\times E-Zn\times(H\times n)
, it is easy to
see that

(2.14)

(\mathcal{D}-B)\begin{bmatrix}E\\
H\end{bmatrix}=\frac{2}{1+Z}\begin{bmatrix}n\times b\\
b\end{bmatrix},\quad\left|\begin{bmatrix}E\\
H\end{bmatrix}\right|_{B}^{2}=\frac{2}{1+Z}\left(\|n\times E\|_{2}^{2}+Z\|n\times H\|_{2}^{2}\right),

and the latter shows (
2.11b
) since
Z>0
. The first identity
of (
2.14
), together with (
2.13
) implies
(
2.11a
). Similar computations establish (
2.11c
).
Finally, noting that
\left|[\begin{smallmatrix}E\\
H\end{smallmatrix}]\right|_{S}^{2}=\|n\times E\|_{2}^{2}+\|n\times H\|_{2}^{2},
the
remaining properties (
2.11d
)
and (
2.11f
), are easily established. Note that if the above

S
is scaled by 1/2, then the conditions of (
2.11
) continue
to hold and we get the “classic upwind” flux
[
15
, p. 434]

for Maxwell equations.

Example 2.2

(Maxwell equations with perfect electric boundary
conditions)
.

Reconsider Example
2.1
with
Z=0
. This yields
a Dirichlet boundary condition modeling the electrical isolation by
a perfect electric conductor. Substituting
Z=0
in previous
choices of

\mathcal{B},B,
and

\left|[\begin{smallmatrix}E\\
H\end{smallmatrix}]\right|_{B}^{2}=2\|n\times E\|_{2}^{2}
from (

).

Example 2.3

(Advection)
.

The advection problem with inflow boundary conditions,

\mathop{\partial_{t}}u+\mathop{\mathrm{div}_{x}}(bu)=0\quad\text{ in }{\varOmega}\times(0,T),\qquad u=0\quad\text{ on }{\partial_{\text{in}}}{\varOmega}\times(0,T),

where

is some given vector field,
fits the above setting with
L=1
(keeping the
spatial dimension

arbitrary),

\mathcal{L}^{({{j}})}=b_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times 1},

\mathcal{G}=1

\mathcal{D}=b\cdot n

Examples of

and (

lowest order Raviart-Thomas finite element space.
The inflow boundary condition is recovered by setting

The choices

are easily seen to verify (

Example 2.4

(Wave equation with Dirichlet boundary conditions)
.

Rewriting

(2.15)

\partial_{tt}\phi-\Delta\phi=0,\quad\text{ in }{\varOmega}\times(0,T),\qquad\phi=0\quad\text{ on }\partial{\varOmega}\times(0,T),

as a first order hyperbolic system for

,

\mathcal{G}

n^{t}&2\end{bmatrix}.

2.11

Example 2.5

(Wave equation with Robin boundary conditions)
.

We reconsider Example

Example 2.6

(Wave equation with Neumann boundary conditions)
.

This is the boundary condition obtained when

in (

of (

\mathcal{B}=\begin{bmatrix}0&n\\

Lemma 3.1

The causality condition implies that

\max\big(\|M_{0}\|_{\mathtt{v}},\|M_{0}^{-1}\|_{\mathtt{v}},\|M_{1}\|_{\mathtt{v}},\|M\|_{\mathtt{v}},\|M^{-1}\|_{\mathtt{v}}\big)\lesssim 1.

Proof.

Let

In accordance with (

Recall

and recalling that the maximal wave speed

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{(\nu)}v\cdot v

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{L}v_{i}\lambda_{i}\mathcal{G}e_{i}\cdot v=\sum_{i=1}^{L}\lambda_{i}\,|v_{i}|^{2}\leq c\sum_{i=1}^{L}|v_{i}|^{2}=c\,\langle{v,v}\rangle_{\mathcal{G}}.

Using this inequality with
\nu=(\mathop{\mathrm{grad}_{x}}\varphi)/\|\mathop{\mathrm{grad}_{x}}\varphi\|_{2}

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}v\cdot v-\sum_{j=1}^{N}(\partial_{j}\varphi)\mathcal{L}^{({{j}})}v\cdot v

4.
Analysis of fully discrete schemes

In this section we use time stepping schemes to arrive at practical
fully discrete schemes from the semidiscretization studied in the
previous section. Before studying these fully discrete schemes on a
mapped tent, it is useful to quickly make a few observations on the
time derivatives and Taylor expansion of the exact solution.

4.1.
Preparatory observations

The bilinear form
a(\cdot,\cdot)
defines an operator
A
from

H_{h}^{{\mathtt{v}}}
to its dual space
(H_{h}^{{\mathtt{v}}})^{\prime}
in the usual way:

(Aw)(v)=a(w,v)
for

. Recall the previously
defined

. Since

, the projector

to
(H_{h}^{{\mathtt{v}}})^{\prime}

(P_{h}Aw,v_{h})_{\mathtt{v}}=(Aw)(v_{h})=a(w,v_{h})
for all
w\in H_{h}^{{\mathtt{v}}}
and

v_{h}\in V_{h}^{\mathtt{v}}
. While describing fully discrete schemes,

A_{h}:V_{h}^{\mathtt{v}}\to V_{h}^{\mathtt{v}}
, defined by

(A_{h}w,v)_{\mathtt{v}}=a(w,v),
for all
w,v\in V_{h}^{\mathtt{v}}

will be useful.
One may also consider
\tilde{A}:{H^{{\mathtt{v}}}}\to L^{{\mathtt{v}}}

defined by
(\tilde{A}w,v)_{\mathtt{v}}=(\mathop{\mathrm{div}_{x}}\big[\delta f(w)\big],v)_{\mathtt{v}},

for all
w\in{H^{{\mathtt{v}}}}
and
v\in L^{{\mathtt{v}}}.
It is easy to see
from (
3.9
) of Lemma
3.2
that
A
coincides with

\tilde{A}
on functions
w\in H^{{\mathtt{v}}}
with
(\mathcal{D}-B)w=0
on
\partial{\varOmega}
. In
particular, on such functions
w
, we may view
Aw
as a function in

L^{{\mathtt{v}}}
. The pull back
{\hat{u}}
of
the exact hyperbolic solution
u
from a tent
{T^{\mathtt{v}}}
to
the cylinder
{\hat{T}^{\mathtt{v}}}
is one such function. Therefore the following
equation holds in
L^{{\mathtt{v}}}
:

(4.1)

\partial_{\hat{t}}(M\hat{u})=A\hat{u},\qquad 0\leq\hat{t}\leq 1.

We will proceed assuming that the exact solution

\hat{u}
is regular enough to admit
the Taylor expansion

(4.2)

{\hat{u}}(\tau)=\sum_{k=0}^{s}\frac{\tau^{k}}{k!}{\hat{u}}^{(k)}(0)+\rho_{s+1}(\tau),

for some
s\geq 1
. Here,
{\hat{u}}^{(k)}(\hat{t})
denotes the
k
th order
time derivative
d^{k}{\hat{u}}/d\hat{t}^{k}
(which is a function in

{H^{{\mathtt{v}}}}
when the solution is smooth—see
Lemma
4.1

\rho_{s+1}(\tau)
can be expressed as the
H^{{\mathtt{v}}}
-valued Riemann

(4.3)

\rho_{s+1}(\tau)=\frac{\tau^{s+1}}{s!}\int_{0}^{1}(1-\hat{t})^{s}{\hat{u}}^{(s+1)}(\hat{t}\tau)\;d\hat{t}.

It is well known that the expansion (
4.2
) holds for

\tau
in an interval containing
0
whenever
{\hat{u}}
is
s+1
times
continuously differentiable (as an
{H^{{\mathtt{v}}}}
-valued function) in that
interval. When applied to a spacetime hyperbolic solution
u
in the
physical domain, the smallness of the higher order terms
in (

function
\hat{u}
), is evident from the following lemma, since

\delta(x)\lesssim h_{{\mathtt{v}}}
.

Lemma 4.1
.

The function
\hat{u}=u\circ\varPhi
satisfies

{\hat{u}}^{(k)}=(\partial_{t}^{k}u\circ\varPhi)\,\delta^{k}.

Consequently, at each pseudotime
\hat{t}
, within each spatial element

K\in{\varOmega_{h}^{{\mathtt{v}}}}
, as a function of the spatial variable
x
,

{\hat{u}}^{(k)}(\hat{t})
is as smooth as

(\partial_{t}^{k}u)(x,\varphi(x,\hat{t}))
. Moreover,

{\hat{u}}^{(k)}(\hat{t})
is in
H^{{\mathtt{v}}}
if
\partial_{t}^{k}u
is continuously
differentiable in
{T^{\mathtt{v}}}
.

Proof.

Let
e
denote the spacetime unit vector in the time direction i.e.,
all its components are zero except for the last (time) component
which is 1. Then, at some fixed spacetime point
\hat{P}
in
{\hat{T}^{\mathtt{v}}}
,
we may write

{\hat{u}}^{(k)}(\hat{P})=D^{k}{\hat{u}}(\hat{P})(e,e,\ldots,e),
where

D^{k}{\hat{u}}
is the multilinear form representing the
k
th order
Fréchet derivative of
{\hat{u}}
, and
e
is repeated
k
times in its
argument list. Then, letting
P=\varPhi(\hat{P})
denote the mapped
point in
{T^{\mathtt{v}}}
, by standard arguments
[
4
]
for affine
maps,

\displaystyle{\hat{u}}^{(k)}(\hat{P})

\displaystyle=D^{k}(u\circ\varPhi)(\hat{P})(e,e,\ldots,e)

\displaystyle=D^{k}u(P)([\mathop{\mathrm{grad}_{x\hat{t}}}\varPhi]e,[\mathop{\mathrm{grad}_{x\hat{t}}}\varPhi]e,\ldots,[\mathop{\mathrm{grad}_{x\hat{t}}}\varPhi]e)

\displaystyle=D^{k}u(P)(\delta e,\delta e,\ldots,\delta e),

where we have used (

and the derivative

\partial^{k}u/\partial t^{k}

, the proof is complete.
∎

In view of Lemma

, when the exact

(4.4)

\begin{gathered}|w|_{{\mathtt{v}},l,m}=\sup_{0\leq\tau\leq 1}\sum_{k=0}^{m}\left|\hat{w}^{(k)}(\tau)\right|_{H^{l}({\varOmega_{h}^{{\mathtt{v}}}})^{L}},\\
\|w\|_{\infty,{\mathtt{v}}}=\sup_{0\leq\tau\leq 1}\|\hat{w}(\tau)\|_{\mathtt{v}},\qquad\|w\|_{s,\infty,{\mathtt{v}}}=\sum_{\ell=0}^{s}\|\partial_{t}^{\ell}w\|_{\infty,{\mathtt{v}}}.\end{gathered}

When

, the first seminorm coincides with the seminorm
in (
3.15
). The next result bounds the Taylor
remainder term in terms of the mapped time derivative

.

Lemma 4.2
.

The Taylor remainder term satisfies
\|\rho_{s}\|_{\mathtt{v}}\lesssim\tau^{s}h_{{\mathtt{v}}}^{s}\|\partial_{t}^{s}u\|_{\infty,{\mathtt{v}}}.

Proof.

Starting from (
4.3
), by Fubini’s theorem and
Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,

\displaystyle\tau^{-2s}\|\rho_{s}\|_{{\mathtt{v}}}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{0}^{1}(1-\hat{t})^{2s}\|{\hat{u}}^{(s)}(\hat{t}\tau)\|_{\mathtt{v}}^{2}\,d\hat{t}\leq\bigg(\sup_{0\leq\tau\leq 1}\|{\hat{u}}^{(s)}(\tau)\|_{\mathtt{v}}^{2}\bigg)\int_{0}^{1}(1-\hat{t})^{2s}\,d\hat{t}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sup_{0\leq\tau\leq 1}\|\delta^{s}(\partial_{t}^{s}u\circ\varPhi)(\tau)\|_{\mathtt{v}}^{2},

due to Lemma
4.1
. Since
\delta\lesssim h_{{\mathtt{v}}},
the result follows.
∎

Lemma 4.3
.

For any
k\geq 1,
whenever the exact time derivative

{\hat{u}}^{(k-1)}(0)
exists in
H^{{\mathtt{v}}}
, we have

{\hat{u}}^{(k)}(0)=M_{0}^{-1}(A+kM_{1})\,{\hat{u}}^{(k-1)}(0).

Differentiating both sides of (

)
k-1
times,

(M{\hat{u}})^{(k)}(\hat{t})=A{\hat{u}}^{(k-1)}(\hat{t})
. Simplifying the
left hand side by Leibniz rule and the linearity of

, we have

M(\hat{t}){\hat{u}}^{(k)}(\hat{t})-kM_{1}{\hat{u}}^{(k-1)}(\hat{t})=A{\hat{u}}^{(k-1)}(\hat{t}).

Evaluating at

and rearranging, the proof is complete.
∎

Note that
V_{h}^{\mathtt{v}}
is an invariant subspace of the previously defined
operators
M_{0}
and
M_{1}
, due to (

). It will be
understood from context whether we consider

as operators on

or as operators on
V_{h}^{\mathtt{v}}
. For operators on
V_{h}^{\mathtt{v}}
, we
define a discrete operator norm, analogous to (

), for
operators
\mathcal{O}_{h}
on

, by

\|\mathcal{O}_{h}\|_{{\mathtt{v}},h}:=\sup_{v_{h},w_{h}\in V_{h}^{\mathtt{v}}}\frac{(\mathcal{O}_{h}v_{h},w_{h})_{\mathtt{v}}}{\|v_{h}\|_{\mathtt{v}}\|w_{h}\|_{{\mathtt{v}}}}

for all
v_{h},w_{h}\in V_{h}^{\mathtt{v}}
.

Lemma 4.4
.

We have

\|A_{h}\|_{{\mathtt{v}},h}\lesssim 1,\;\|M_{1}\|_{{\mathtt{v}},h}\lesssim 1,\;\|M\|_{{\mathtt{v}},h}\lesssim 1,\;\|M^{-1}\|_{{\mathtt{v}},h}\lesssim 1.

Proof.

To prove the first inequality,
consider the terms that make up

a(v_{h},w_{h})=(A_{h}v_{h},w_{h})_{\mathtt{v}}

for any
v_{h},w_{h}\in V_{h}^{\mathtt{v}}
. On any
K\in{\varOmega_{h}^{{\mathtt{v}}}}
,
since
\mathcal{L}^{({{j}})}
is uniformly bounded and
\delta\lesssim h_{K}
,

(\delta\mathcal{L}^{({{j}})}v_{h},\partial_{j}w_{h})_{K}\lesssim\|v_{h}\|_{K}h_{K}\|\partial_{j}w_{h}\|_{K}\lesssim\|v_{h}\|_{K}\|w_{h}\|_{K}

where we have applied an inverse inequality in the last step.
Next, consider an element boundary term in
(A_{h}v_{h},w_{h})
, restricted
to say a facet

, shared with
the boundary of another element

in
{\varOmega_{h}^{{\mathtt{v}}}}
:

(\delta\,\mathcal{D}\{v_{h}\},w_{h}|_{\partial K})_{F}\lesssim\left(h_{K}\|v_{h}\|_{\partial K}^{2}+h_{K_{o}}\|v_{h}\|_{\partial K_{o}}^{2}\right)^{1/2}\left(h_{K}^{1/2}\|w_{h}\|_{\partial K}\right)\lesssim\|v_{h}\|_{{\varOmega^{{\mathtt{v}}}}}\|w_{h}\|_{K},

where we have again used
\delta\lesssim h_{K}
and local scaling
arguments. Continuing to use similar arguments on all the remaining
terms that make up
(A_{h}v_{h},w_{h})_{\mathtt{v}}
, we obtain

\|A_{h}\|_{{\mathtt{v}},h}\lesssim 1
. Finally, Lemma
3.1
shows
that
\|M_{1}\|_{{\mathtt{v}},h}
,
\|M(\tau)\|_{{\mathtt{v}},h}
, and

\|M(\tau)^{-1}\|_{{\mathtt{v}},h}
also admit mesh-independent bounds.
∎

The projector
P_{h}
enjoys the commutativity properties

M_{1}P_{h}=P_{h}M_{1},\quad M_{0}P_{h}=P_{h}M_{0},

because of (

). Although a similar commutativity
identity cannot be expected of

, we have the following lemma.

Lemma 4.5
.

For any
w\in H^{l}({\varOmega_{h}^{{\mathtt{v}}}})^{L}
,

, the function
\eta_{h}=(A_{h}P_{h}-P_{h}A)w
satisfies

\|\eta_{h}\|_{\mathtt{v}}\,\lesssim\,h_{{\mathtt{v}}}^{l}\,|w|_{H^{l}({\varOmega_{h}^{{\mathtt{v}}}})^{L}}.

Proof.

Since

\|\eta_{h}\|_{\mathtt{v}}^{2}=(A_{h}P_{h}w,\eta_{h})_{\mathtt{v}}-(P_{h}Aw,\eta_{h})_{\mathtt{v}}=a(P_{h}w,\eta_{h})_{\mathtt{v}}-a(w,\eta_{h})_{\mathtt{v}}
,

\displaystyle\|\eta_{h}\|_{\mathtt{v}}^{2}

\displaystyle=a(P_{h}w-w,\eta_{h})\,\lesssim\,h_{{\mathtt{v}}}^{l}|w|_{H^{l}({\varOmega_{h}^{{\mathtt{v}}}})^{L}}|\eta_{h}|_{d}

by Lemma

|\eta_{h}|_{d}^{2}=-((2A_{h}+M_{1})\eta_{h},\eta_{h})_{\mathtt{v}}\lesssim\|\eta_{h}\|_{\mathtt{v}}^{2}

∎

4.2.
Lowest order tent-implicit scheme

While the overall MTP strategy is a tent-by-tent time-marching
strategy akin to explicit methods, within a mapped tent, one may
choose between explicit or implicit schemes. By a “tent-implicit”
scheme, we mean a method that solves the
semidiscretization (
2.9
) on a mapped tent using implicit time
stepping. Although this requires matrix inversion, the size of the
matrix is only as large as the number of spatial degrees of freedom in
one tent (much smaller than the size of the global matrix that needs
to be inverted in standard implicit schemes for method of lines
discretizations). Numerical results using tent-implicit schemes of
various orders were reported first in
[
13
, §5.4]
. In
this subsection, we provide a convergence analysis of the lowest-order case.

To derive the lowest order tent-implicit method, we begin by
rewriting (
2.9
) in a form analogous to (
4.1
), i.e.,

\partial_{\hat{t}}(M{\hat{u}}_{h})=A_{h}{\hat{u}}_{h},\quad 0\leq\hat{t}\leq 1.

Then, putting
y_{h}=M{\hat{u}}_{h}
, we have

\partial_{\hat{t}}\,y_{h}=A_{h}M^{-1}y_{h}
. The implicit Euler
method applied to this defines
an approximation
y_{h1}(\tau)
to
y_{h}(\tau)

given by
y_{h1}(\tau)-y_{h}(0)=\tau A_{h}M^{-1}y_{h1}(\tau).
Since

{\hat{u}}_{h}=M^{-1}y_{h}
, an approximation to
{\hat{u}}_{h}(\tau)
is furnished
by
M^{-1}y_{h1}(\tau)
, which after simplification becomes

M^{-1}(I-\tau A_{h}M^{-1})^{-1}M_{0}{\hat{u}}(0).
This motivates the
following definition of the discrete propagator.

Definition 4.6

(Lowest order tent-implicit flow:
R^{\mathrm{imp}}_{h1}(\tau)
)
.

Define
R^{\mathrm{imp}}_{h1}(\tau):V_{h}^{\mathtt{v}}\to V_{h}^{\mathtt{v}}
by

R^{\mathrm{imp}}_{h1}(\tau)=M(\tau)^{-1}(I-\tau A_{h}M(\tau)^{-1})^{-1}M_{0}.

The two inverses required for this definition are both well defined:
first,

M
is invertible by Lemma
3.1
; second,

I-\tau A_{h}M^{-1}
is invertible because

\displaystyle(I-\tau A_{h}M^{-1})M

\displaystyle=M-\tau A_{h}=(M_{0}-\frac{\tau}{2}M_{1})-\frac{\tau}{2}(2A_{h}+M_{1}),

together with Lemmas
3.2
and
3.1

imply that for any
0\neq v\in V_{h}^{\mathtt{v}}
,

((I-\tau A_{h}M^{-1})Mv,v)_{\mathtt{v}}\geq\|v\|_{M(\tau/2)}^{2}+|v|_{d}^{2}>0.

We proceed to prove convergence of the scheme, beginning with the next
stability result that closely resembles the inequality
of Lemma
3.6
.

Lemma 4.7

(Unconditional strong stability)
.

For any
v\in V_{h}^{\mathtt{v}}
and any
0\leq\tau\leq 1
,

\|R^{\mathrm{imp}}_{h1}(\tau)v\|_{M(\tau)}^{2}\leq\|v\|_{M_{0}}^{2}.

Proof.

Let
v_{\tau}=R^{\mathrm{imp}}_{h1}(\tau)v
. Then
(M-\tau A_{h})v_{\tau}=M_{0}v.
Taking the inner product with
v_{\tau}
on both sides,

\displaystyle\|v_{\tau}\|_{M}^{2}

\displaystyle=(M_{0}v,v_{\tau})_{\mathtt{v}}+\tau(A_{h}v_{\tau},v_{\tau})_{\mathtt{v}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\|v\|_{M_{0}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|v_{\tau}\|_{M_{0}}^{2}+\tau(A_{h}v_{\tau},v_{\tau})_{\mathtt{v}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\|v\|_{M_{0}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|v_{\tau}\|_{M}^{2}+\frac{\tau}{2}((2A_{h}+M_{1})v_{\tau},v_{\tau})_{\mathtt{v}}.

Now, since
((2A_{h}+M_{1})v_{\tau},v_{\tau})_{\mathtt{v}}=-|v_{\tau}|_{d}^{2}

(see Lemma
3.2
), the proof is complete.
∎

When using any (spatial) polynomial degree
p\geq 0
, we obtain the
following bound for the lowest order method (showing that the rate is
limited by the time discretization error), which uses the
(semi)norms defined in (
3.15
)
and (
4.4
).

Lemma 4.8

(Local error bound)
.

Let
{\hat{u}}
denote the exact solution on
{\hat{T}^{\mathtt{v}}}
and let

\hat{u}^{\mathrm{imp}}_{h1}(\tau)=R^{\mathrm{imp}}_{h1}(\tau){\hat{u}}_{h}^{0}
for some
{\hat{u}}_{h}^{0}\in V_{h}^{\mathtt{v}}
.
Then,

\displaystyle\|{\hat{u}}(\tau)-\hat{u}^{\mathrm{imp}}_{h1}(\tau)\|_{M(\tau)}

\displaystyle\;\lesssim\;\|{\hat{u}}(0)-{\hat{u}}_{h}^{0}\|_{M(0)}+h_{{\mathtt{v}}}\big(\|u\|_{2,\infty,{\mathtt{v}}}+|u|_{{\mathtt{v}},1}\big).

Proof.

Let

X_{h}=M_{0}^{-1}(A_{h}+M_{1})
and
X=M_{0}^{-1}(A+M_{1})
.
By (
4.5
),

(4.6)

P_{h}X-X_{h}P_{h}=M_{0}^{-1}(P_{h}A-A_{h}P_{h}).

An alternate expression for the discrete propagator will also be
useful:
R^{\mathrm{imp}}_{h1}=M^{-1}(I-\tau A_{h}M^{-1})^{-1}M_{0}=(M-\tau A_{h})^{-1}M_{0}=(M_{0}-\tau(A_{h}+M_{1}))^{-1}M_{0},
i.e.,

(4.7)

R^{\mathrm{imp}}_{h1}(\tau)=(I-\tau X_{h})^{-1}.

With these preparations, we derive an “error equation” for

{\varepsilon}_{h}=\hat{u}^{\mathrm{imp}}_{h1}(\tau)-P_{h}{\hat{u}}(\tau)
.
Note that

{\varepsilon}_{h}
is a function in
V_{h}^{\mathtt{v}}
for each
\tau
. Writing

{\varepsilon}_{h}=R^{\mathrm{imp}}_{h1}\big[{\hat{u}}_{h}^{0}-P_{h}{\hat{u}}(0)\big]+\phi_{h},

with
\phi_{h}=R^{\mathrm{imp}}_{h1}P_{h}{\hat{u}}(0)-P_{h}{\hat{u}}(\tau),
we analyze

\phi_{h}
further as follows.

\displaystyle\phi_{h}

\displaystyle=(I-\tau X_{h})^{-1}P_{h}{\hat{u}}(0)-P_{h}{\hat{u}}(\tau)

\displaystyle\text{by}~\eqref{eq:30},

\displaystyle=\big[(I-\tau X_{h})^{-1}-I\big]P_{h}{\hat{u}}(0)-\tau P_{h}{\hat{u}}^{(1)}(0)-P_{h}\rho_{2}

by (
4.2
),

\displaystyle=\tau(I-\tau X_{h})^{-1}X_{h}P_{h}{\hat{u}}(0)-\tau P_{h}{\hat{u}}^{(1)}(0)-P_{h}\rho_{2}

\displaystyle=\tau(I-\tau X_{h})^{-1}\big[P_{h}X-M_{0}^{-1}(P_{h}A-A_{h}P_{h})\big]{\hat{u}}(0)

\displaystyle\quad-\tau P_{h}{\hat{u}}^{(1)}(0)-P_{h}\rho_{2}

by (
4.6
),

\displaystyle=\tau^{2}X_{h}(I-\tau X_{h})^{-1}P_{h}{\hat{u}}^{(1)}(0)-\tau(I-\tau X_{h})^{-1}M_{0}^{-1}\eta_{h}-P_{h}\rho_{2}

with
\eta_{h}=(P_{h}A-A_{h}P_{h}){\hat{u}}(0)
. We have used Lemma
4.3
in the last
step.

To bound
\phi_{h}
, first note that by
Lemma
4.4
,
\|X_{h}\|_{{\mathtt{v}},h}\lesssim 1.
Also,
by (
4.7
) and Lemma
4.7
,

\|R^{\mathrm{imp}}_{h1}(\tau)\|_{{\mathtt{v}},h}=\|(I-\tau X_{h})^{-1}\|_{{\mathtt{v}},h}\lesssim 1
, so

\|\phi_{h}\|_{\mathtt{v}}\lesssim\tau^{2}\|{\hat{u}}^{(1)}(0)\|_{\mathtt{v}}+\tau\|\eta_{h}\|_{{\mathtt{v}}}+\|\rho_{2}\|_{\mathtt{v}}.

Now, applying Lemmas
4.1
,

4.5
and
4.2
,

\displaystyle\|\phi_{h}\|_{\mathtt{v}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\tau^{2}h_{{\mathtt{v}}}\|\partial_{t}u\|_{\infty,{\mathtt{v}}}+\tau h_{{\mathtt{v}}}|u|_{{\mathtt{v}},1}+\tau^{2}h_{{\mathtt{v}}}^{2}\|\partial_{t}^{2}u\|_{\infty,{\mathtt{v}}}.

Together with the stability result of Lemma
4.7
,
this proves

\|{\varepsilon}_{h}\|_{M}\lesssim\|{\hat{u}}_{h}^{0}-P_{h}{\hat{u}}(0)\|_{M_{0}}+h_{{\mathtt{v}}}\big(\|\partial_{t}u\|_{\infty,{\mathtt{v}}}+\|\partial_{t}^{2}u\|_{\infty,{\mathtt{v}}}+|u|_{{\mathtt{v}},1}\big).

Using the triangle inequality,

\|{\hat{u}}(\tau)-\hat{u}^{\mathrm{imp}}_{h1}(\tau)\|_{M}\leq\|{\hat{u}}(\tau)-P_{h}{\hat{u}}(\tau)\|_{M}+\|{\varepsilon}_{h}\|_{M},
and the standard estimate for
L^{2}

projection,

\|{\hat{u}}(\tau)-P_{h}{\hat{u}}(\tau)\|_{\mathtt{v}}\lesssim h_{{\mathtt{v}}}|u|_{{\mathtt{v}},1}
,
the proof can now be completed.
∎

The previous two lemmas lead to a global convergence theorem, as we
shall now see.
The implicit scheme’s tent propagator on
{T^{\mathtt{v}}}
is the operator

R^{\mathrm{imp}}_{h1}(1)\circ P_{h}:L^{{\mathtt{v}}}\to V_{h}^{\mathtt{v}},
set using
R^{\mathrm{imp}}_{h1}(\tau)

evaluated at pseudotime
\tau=1
corresponding to the tent top.
Letting
\mathcal{R}_{h}^{\mathrm{imp}}
denote the collection of such tent propagators over
all tents, we use Definition
3.9
to set the global
propagator
R_{h,\mathrm{imp}}^{i,j}={\mathtt{T2G}}(i,j,\mathcal{R}_{h}^{\mathrm{imp}})
, and consider the
discrete solution
u_{h1}^{\mathrm{imp}}=R_{h,\mathrm{imp}}^{m,0}u^{0}
at the final time
T
.

Theorem 4.9

(Error estimate for the lowest order tent-implicit
scheme)
.

Under the same conditions as Theorem
3.12
, for any
spatial degree
p\geq 0
, the
fully discrete solution
u_{h1}^{\mathrm{imp}}
satisfies

\|u(T)-u_{h1}^{\mathrm{imp}}\|_{\varOmega}\;\lesssim\;\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{m}h_{j}\bigg)^{1/2}\bigg[\sum_{j=1}^{m}\sum_{{\mathtt{v}}\in V_{j}}h_{{\mathtt{v}}}\big(\|u\|_{2,\infty,{\mathtt{v}}}+|u|_{{\mathtt{v}},1}\big)^{2}\bigg]^{1/2}.

Proof.

First, due to Lemma
4.7
, we observe that in
complete analogy with

Lemma
3.11
, one
can prove that for
i>j
,

\|R_{h,\mathrm{imp}}^{i,j}w\|_{C_{i}}\leq\|w\|_{C_{j}}.

Defining

E_{h,\mathrm{imp}}^{i,j}=R^{i,j}-R_{h,\mathrm{imp}}^{i,j},

in analogy with Lemma
3.10
, we can show that

E_{h,\mathrm{imp}}^{m,0}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}R_{h,\mathrm{imp}}^{m,j}E_{h,\mathrm{imp}}^{j,j-1}R^{j-1,0}.

Hence the theorem can be proved along the same lines as the proof of
Theorem
3.12
, using
Lemma
4.8
in place of
Lemma
3.8
.
∎

As before, under the further assumption that (
3.22
) holds,
Theorem
4.9
gives an
O(h^{1/2})
rate of
convergence for the solution at the final time.

4.3.
Lowest order explicit scheme

A perhaps nonstandard route to derive an explicit scheme is to view it
as an iterative method for solving the equations of an implicit
scheme. Pursuing this approach using the tent-implicit scheme of
§
4.2
, we write

v_{\infty}=R^{\mathrm{imp}}_{h1}(\tau)v_{0}
, or equivalently,
using
the operator
X_{h}=M_{0}^{-1}(A_{h}+M_{1})
in (
4.7
),

(I-\tau X_{h})v_{\infty}=v_{0}.

Hence the Richardson iteration for solving this linear system for

v_{\infty}
takes the form

(4.8)

v_{\ell+1}=v_{\ell}+\left(v_{0}-(I-\tau X_{h})v_{\ell}\right),\qquad\ell=0,1,\ldots.

Definition 4.10

(Lowest order explicit discrete flows:

R^{\mathrm{exp}}_{h1}(\tau)
and
R^{\mathrm{exp}}_{h1q}(\tau)
)
.

Let
v_{0}\in V_{h}^{\mathtt{v}}
. The result
v_{1}
after one iteration
of (
4.8
) defines the operator

R^{\mathrm{exp}}_{h1}(\tau):V_{h}^{\mathtt{v}}\to V_{h}^{\mathtt{v}}
:

R^{\mathrm{exp}}_{h1}(\tau)v_{0}=v_{1}=(I+\tau X_{h})v_{0}.

The result
v_{q}
obtained after
performing
q\geq 1
iterations defines

R^{\mathrm{exp}}_{h1q}(\tau):V_{h}^{\mathtt{v}}\to V_{h}^{\mathtt{v}}
by

R^{\mathrm{exp}}_{h1q}(\tau)v_{0}=v_{q}=v_{0}+\tau X_{h}v_{q-1}.

Note that no matrix inversions are required for conducting these
q

iterations, except for one local mass matrix inversion (
M_{0}^{-1}
)
per tent.

Unlike the tent-implicit scheme, we are now able to obtain stability
for the explicit scheme
only under further conditions. From Lemma
4.4
, we know
that
\|X_{h}\|_{M_{0}}\lesssim 1
. Hence the condition (
4.9
) in
the next result can be met by performing sufficiently many iterations.

Lemma 4.11

(Conditional stability)
.

If
q
is large enough to admit

(4.9)

\|X_{h}\|_{M_{0}}\lesssim h_{{\mathtt{v}}}^{1/(q+1)},

then there is a
c_{q}>0
independent of
h_{{\mathtt{v}}}
such
that for all
v_{0}\in V_{h}^{\mathtt{v}}
,

\|R^{\mathrm{exp}}_{h1q}(\tau)v_{0}\|_{M(\tau)}\leq(1+c_{q}h_{{\mathtt{v}}})\|v_{0}\|_{M_{0}}.

Proof.

Recursively expanding
v_{q}=v_{0}+\tau X_{h}v_{q-1}
, we obtain

v_{q}=\sum_{j=0}^{q}(\tau X_{h})^{j}v_{0}.

Rewriting this, using (
4.7
), as

(4.10)

v_{q}=(I-\tau X_{h})^{-1}\big[I-(\tau X_{h})^{q+1}\big]v_{0}=R^{\mathrm{imp}}_{h1}\big[1-(\tau X_{h})^{q+1}\big]v_{0},

we apply
Lemma
4.7
. Hence

\displaystyle\|v_{q}\|_{M}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|v_{0}-(\tau X_{h})^{q+1}v_{0}\right\|_{M_{0}}\leq\|v_{0}\|_{M_{0}}+\|X_{h}\|_{M_{0}}^{q+1}\|v_{0}\|_{M_{0}}

and the result follows using (
4.9
).
∎

Lemma 4.12

(Local error bound)
.

Let
{\hat{u}}
denote the exact solution on
{\hat{T}^{\mathtt{v}}}
, let

\hat{u}^{\mathrm{exp}}_{h1q}(\tau)=R^{\mathrm{exp}}_{h1q}(\tau){\hat{u}}_{h}^{0}
for some

{\hat{u}}_{h}^{0}\in V_{h}^{\mathtt{v}}
, and suppose (
4.9
) holds.
Then,

\displaystyle\|{\hat{u}}(\tau)-\hat{u}^{\mathrm{exp}}_{h1q}(\tau)\|_{M(\tau)}

\displaystyle\;\lesssim\;\|{\hat{u}}(0)-{\hat{u}}_{h}^{0}\|_{M(0)}+h_{{\mathtt{v}}}\big(\|u\|_{2,\infty,{\mathtt{v}}}+\|u\|_{{\mathtt{v}},1}\big).

Proof.

Let
e_{h}=\hat{u}^{\mathrm{imp}}_{h1}(\tau)-\hat{u}^{\mathrm{exp}}_{h1q}(\tau)
. Since

{\hat{u}}(\tau)-\hat{u}^{\mathrm{exp}}_{h1q}(\tau)-e_{h}={\hat{u}}(\tau)-\hat{u}^{\mathrm{imp}}_{h1}(\tau)
can be bounded by Lemma
4.8
, it
suffices to bound
e_{h}
. By (
4.10
),

\displaystyle e_{h}

\displaystyle=(I-\tau X_{h})^{-1}{\hat{u}}_{h}^{0}-(I-\tau X_{h})^{-1}\big[I-(\tau X_{h})^{q+1}]{\hat{u}}_{h}^{0}

\displaystyle=(I-\tau X_{h})^{-1}(\tau X_{h})^{q+1}{\hat{u}}_{h}^{0}.

Thus, by Lemma
4.7
and (
4.9
),

\|e_{h}\|_{M}\lesssim h_{{\mathtt{v}}}\|{\hat{u}}_{h}^{0}\|_{M_{0}}
. We may further write

{\hat{u}}_{h}^{0}
as the sum of
{\hat{u}}_{h}^{0}-{\hat{u}}(0)
and

{\hat{u}}(0)
and apply triangle inequality to obtain the right
hand side of the stated bound.
∎

Letting
\mathcal{R}^{\mathrm{exp}}_{h1q}
denote the collection of explicit tent
propagator operators
R^{\mathrm{exp}}_{h1q}(1)\circ P_{h}:L^{{\mathtt{v}}}\to V_{h}^{\mathtt{v}}
on all
tents, we use Definition
3.9
to set the global propagators

{\mathtt{T2G}}(i,j,\mathcal{R}^{\mathrm{exp}}_{h1q})
, and consider the discrete solution

u^{\mathrm{exp}}_{h1q}={\mathtt{T2G}}(m,0,\mathcal{R}^{\mathrm{exp}}_{h1q})u^{0}
at the final time
T
.

Theorem 4.13

(Error estimate for iterated lowest order
explicit scheme)
.

Suppose (
4.9
), (
3.22
), and the conditions of
Theorem
3.12
hold. Then for any spatial degree

p\geq 0
, the fully discrete explicit solution
u^{\mathrm{exp}}_{h1q}

satisfies

\|u(T)-u^{\mathrm{exp}}_{h1q}\|_{\varOmega}^{2}\;\lesssim\;\sum_{j=1}^{m}\sum_{{\mathtt{v}}\in V_{j}}h_{{\mathtt{v}}}\big(\|u\|_{2,\infty,{\mathtt{v}}}+\|u\|_{{\mathtt{v}},1}\big)^{2}.

Proof.

The proof proceeds along the lines of the proof of
Theorem
3.12
, replacing the applications of
Lemmas
3.6

and
3.8
, respectively, by those of
Lemmas
4.11

and
4.12
instead. The main difference is that
we must now invoke the argument of Remark
3.14
due
to the weaker stability estimate of
Lemma
4.11
.
∎

The
O(h^{1/2})
rate of convergence given by
Theorem
4.13
is the same as the rate given by
Theorem
4.9
for the lowest order tent-implicit
scheme. Increasing the iteration number
q
can improve stability but
does not generally improve the order of convergence.

4.4.
Arbitrary order SAT schemes

Letting
X_{h}^{({0})}
denote the identity operator on
V_{h}^{\mathtt{v}}
, recursively
define further operators on
V_{h}^{\mathtt{v}}
by

(4.11)

X_{h}^{({k})}=M_{0}^{-1}(A_{h}+kM_{1})X_{h}^{({k-1})},\quad k\geq 1.

Similarly, let
X^{({0})}=1
and
X^{({k})}=M_{0}^{-1}(A+kM_{1})X^{({k-1})}

for
k\geq 1.
By
Lemma
4.3
, the time derivative of the exact
solution satisfies
{\hat{u}}^{(k)}(0)=X^{({k})}{\hat{u}}(0)
. Hence the
expansion (
4.2
) may be written as

(4.12)

{\hat{u}}(\tau)=\sum_{k=0}^{s}\frac{\tau^{k}}{k!}X^{({k})}{\hat{u}}(0)+\rho_{s+1}(\tau),

This motivates us to define the SAT flow by replacing
X^{({k})}
with
the discrete operator
X_{h}^{({k})}

as follows. (A gentler derivation can be found
in
[
12
]
and it can be seen easily that the
discrete flow defined there coincides with the one in the next
definition.)

Definition 4.14

(Discrete
s
-stage SAT flow:
R^{\mathrm{sat}}_{hs}(\tau)
for

s\geq 1
)
.

Define
R^{\mathrm{sat}}_{hs}(\tau):V_{h}^{\mathtt{v}}\to V_{h}^{\mathtt{v}}
by

R^{\mathrm{sat}}_{hs}(\tau)v=\sum_{k=0}^{s-1}\frac{\tau^{k}}{k!}X_{h}^{({k})}v+\frac{\tau^{s}}{s!}M(\tau)^{-1}M_{0}X_{h}^{({s})}v,\qquad v\in V_{h}^{\mathtt{v}}.

Lemma 4.15
.

Let
u
be the exact solution of (
3.12
) on a causal
tent
{T^{\mathtt{v}}}
and
{\hat{u}}=u\circ\varPhi\in C^{s+1}(0,1,H^{{\mathtt{v}}}\cap H^{p+1}({\varOmega_{h}^{{\mathtt{v}}}})^{L})
.
Then for any
s\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\|R^{\mathrm{sat}}_{hs}(\tau){\hat{u}}_{h}^{0}-P_{h}{\hat{u}}(\tau)\|_{M(\tau)}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|{\hat{u}}_{h}^{0}-P_{h}{\hat{u}}(0)\|_{M(0)}+\tau^{s}h_{{\mathtt{v}}}^{s}\|u\|_{s+1,\infty,{\mathtt{v}}}

\displaystyle+\tau h_{{\mathtt{v}}}^{p+1}|u|_{{\mathtt{v}},p+1,s-1}.

Proof.

Let
{\hat{u}}_{hs}(\tau)=R^{\mathrm{sat}}_{hs}(\tau){\hat{u}}_{h}^{0}
and let

{\varepsilon}_{k}=(X_{h}^{({k})}P_{h}-P_{h}X^{({k})}){\hat{u}}(0)
. Then projecting and
subtracting (
4.12
) from the expansion defining

R^{\mathrm{sat}}_{hs}(\tau){\hat{u}}_{h}^{0}
, we obtain

\displaystyle{\hat{u}}_{hs}(\tau)-P_{h}{\hat{u}}(\tau)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{s-1}\frac{\tau^{k}}{k!}\big[X_{h}^{({k})}({\hat{u}}_{h}^{0}-P_{h}{\hat{u}}(0))+{\varepsilon}_{k}\big]

\displaystyle+\frac{\tau^{s}}{s!}M^{-1}M_{0}\big[X_{h}^{({s})}({\hat{u}}_{h}^{0}-P_{h}{\hat{u}}(0))\big]

\displaystyle+\frac{\tau^{s}}{s!}\big[(M^{-1}M_{0}-I)P_{h}{\hat{u}}^{(s)}(0)+M^{-1}M_{0}{\varepsilon}_{s}\big]-P_{h}\rho_{s+1}.

Letting
\mu_{s}=(M^{-1}M_{0}-I)P_{h}{\hat{u}}^{(s)}(0)
,
and noting that
{\varepsilon}_{0}=0
,

(4.13)

{\hat{u}}_{hs}(\tau)-P_{h}{\hat{u}}(\tau)=R^{\mathrm{sat}}_{hs}(\tau)\big[{\hat{u}}_{h}^{0}-P_{h}{\hat{u}}(0)\big]+\sum_{k=1}^{s}\frac{\tau^{k}}{k!}{\varepsilon}_{k}+\frac{\tau^{s}}{s!}\big[\mu_{s}+M^{-1}M_{0}{\varepsilon}_{s}\big]-P_{h}\rho_{s+1}.

To estimate the terms on the right hand side, we first use
Lemma
4.4
to conclude that

\|R^{\mathrm{sat}}_{hs}(\tau)[{\hat{u}}_{h}^{0}-P_{h}{\hat{u}}(0)]\|_{M}\lesssim\|{\hat{u}}_{h}^{0}-P_{h}{\hat{u}}(0)\|_{M_{0}}.

By Lemma
4.1
,

\|\mu_{s}\|_{\mathtt{v}}\lesssim h_{{\mathtt{v}}}^{s}\|\partial_{t}^{s}u\|_{\infty,{\mathtt{v}}}.

To bound
{\varepsilon}_{k}
, note that

(4.14)

{\varepsilon}_{k}=M_{0}^{-1}(A_{h}+kM_{1}){\varepsilon}_{k-1}+\eta_{k-1}

where
\eta_{j}=M_{0}^{-1}(A_{h}P_{h}-P_{h}A){\hat{u}}^{(j)}(0)
. By
Lemma
4.5
,

\|\eta_{j}\|_{\mathtt{v}}\lesssim h_{{\mathtt{v}}}^{p+1}|u|_{{\mathtt{v}},p+1,j}.
Hence
recursively bounding
\|{\varepsilon}_{k}\|_{\mathtt{v}}
by

\|{\varepsilon}_{k-1}\|_{\mathtt{v}}
using (
4.14
), and noting that

{\varepsilon}_{0}=0
, we have

\|{\varepsilon}_{k}\|_{M}\lesssim h_{{\mathtt{v}}}^{p+1}|u|_{{\mathtt{v}},p+1,k-1}.
The
final term in (
4.13
) can be treated using
Lemma
4.2
, which yields

\|P_{h}\rho_{s+1}(\tau)\|_{\mathtt{v}}\lesssim\tau^{s+1}h_{{\mathtt{v}}}^{s+1}\|\partial_{t}^{s+1}u\|_{\infty,{\mathtt{v}}}.
When these estimates are used to
bound the terms in the right hand side of (
4.13
) (and noting
that
\tau
is a common factor in all terms except the first), we
obtain the stated inequality.
∎

In order to improve the stability of these explicit SAT schemes, we
shall now divide each tent into
r
subtents and apply the SAT scheme
in each subtent.

Definition 4.16

(Subtents)
.

Subdivide a tent
{T^{\mathtt{v}}}
into
r
subtents as follows. For

\ell=1,\ldots,r
,
define the
\ell
th subtent by

{T_{{[\ell]}}^{\mathtt{v}}}=\{(x,t):x\in{\varOmega^{{\mathtt{v}}}},\;\varphi^{{[\ell]}}(x)\leq t\leq\varphi^{{{[\ell+1]}}}(x)\},

where
\hat{t}^{{[\ell]}}=(\ell-1)/r
,

\varphi^{{[\ell]}}=\varphi(x,\hat{t}^{{[\ell]}}).

Let
\delta^{{[\ell]}}=\varphi^{{{[\ell+1]}}}-\varphi^{{[\ell]}}.
Using

\delta^{{[\ell]}}
in place of
\delta
in (
3.1
) and (
3.3
),
we define
a^{{[\ell]}}(w,v)
and
M_{1}^{{[\ell]}}
, respectively, and let

A_{h}^{{[\ell]}}:V_{h}^{\mathtt{v}}\to V_{h}^{\mathtt{v}}
be defined by

(A_{h}^{{[\ell]}}w,v)_{\mathtt{v}}=a^{{[\ell]}}(w,v)
for
w,v\in V_{h}^{\mathtt{v}}
. Finally let

M_{0}^{{[\ell]}}
be defined by (
3.2
) after replacing
\varphi_{\mathrm{bot}}
there
by
\varphi^{{[\ell]}}
and let
M^{{[\ell]}}(\tau)=M_{0}^{{[\ell]}}-\tau M_{1}^{{[\ell]}}
.
It is easy to see that

\delta^{{[\ell]}}=\delta/r
and

(4.15)

A_{h}^{{[\ell]}}=\frac{1}{r}A_{h},\quad M_{1}^{{[\ell]}}=\frac{1}{r}M_{1},\quad M^{{[\ell]}}(0)=M(\hat{t}^{{[\ell]}}),\quad M^{{[\ell]}}(1)=M(\hat{t}^{{{[\ell+1]}}}).

Definition 4.17

(Discrete
s
-stage SAT propagator using
r
subtents:
R^{\mathrm{sat}}_{rhs}
)
.

Define
X_{h,[\ell]}^{({k})}
by replacing
A_{h},M_{1},
and
M_{0},
by

A_{h}^{{[\ell]}},M_{1}^{{[\ell]}},
and
M_{0}^{{[\ell]}},
respectively,
in (
4.11
). Define
R^{\mathrm{sat}}_{{{[\ell]}},hs}(\tau)
on a
subtent
{T_{{[\ell]}}^{\mathtt{v}}}
by replacing
M_{0},X_{h}^{({k})}
and
M
by

M_{0}^{{[\ell]}},X_{h,[\ell]}^{({k})}
and
M^{{[\ell]}}
, respectively, in
Definition
4.14
. Applying on the
r
subtents
successively, we define

R^{\mathrm{sat}}_{rhs}=R^{\mathrm{sat}}_{[r],hs}(1)\circ R^{\mathrm{sat}}_{[r-1],hs}(1)\cdots\circ R^{\mathrm{sat}}_{[1],hs}(1).

Note that the constant in “
\lesssim
” will not be allowed to depend
on
r
(so that we may admit
examples with
h_{{\mathtt{v}}}
-dependent
r
), as
emphasized in the next lemma.

Lemma 4.18

(Local error in a tent)
.

Let
u
be the exact solution of (
3.12
) on a causal
tent
{T^{\mathtt{v}}}
,
{\hat{u}}=u\circ\varPhi\in C^{s+1}(0,1,H^{{\mathtt{v}}}\cap H^{p+1}({\varOmega_{h}^{{\mathtt{v}}}})^{L})
,
and let
{\hat{u}}_{rhs}=R^{\mathrm{sat}}_{rhs}{\hat{u}}_{h}^{0}.

Then there is a mesh-independent constant
c_{s,p}
that is also
independent of
r
such that

\displaystyle c_{s,p}\|{\hat{u}}_{rhs}-{\hat{u}}(1)\|_{M(1)}

\displaystyle\leq\|{\hat{u}}_{h}^{0}-P_{h}{\hat{u}}(0)\|_{M(0)}+h_{{\mathtt{v}}}^{s}\|u\|_{s+1,\infty,{\mathtt{v}}}+h_{{\mathtt{v}}}^{p+1}|u|_{{\mathtt{v}},p+1,s-1}.

Proof.

Denoting the discrete solutions by

{\hat{u}}_{1,hs}=R^{\mathrm{sat}}_{[1],hs}{\hat{u}}_{h}^{0}
and

{\hat{u}}_{\ell,hs}=R^{\mathrm{sat}}_{[\ell],hs}{\hat{u}}_{\ell-1,hs}
for
1\leq\ell\leq r
, we compare them with the subtent
exact solutions, denoted by

{\hat{u}}_{\ell}={\hat{u}}(\hat{t}^{{{[\ell+1]}}})
.
In the pseudotime coordinate of the un-split tent
{T^{\mathtt{v}}}
, the value

\tau=1/r
corresponds to the top of the first subtent, where the
exact solution is
{\hat{u}}_{1}={\hat{u}}(1/r)
.
Thus Lemma
4.15
with
\tau=1/r
gives

\|{\hat{u}}_{1,hs}-P_{h}{\hat{u}}_{1}\|_{M(1/r)}\lesssim\|{\hat{u}}_{h}^{0}-P_{h}{\hat{u}}(0)\|_{M(0)}+\frac{1}{r}\left(h_{{\mathtt{v}}}^{s}\|u\|_{s+1,\infty,{\mathtt{v}}}+h_{{\mathtt{v}}}^{p+1}|u|_{{\mathtt{v}},p+1,s-1}\right).

Similarly, on the
\ell
th subtent, for
\ell=1,2,\ldots,r
,

\displaystyle\|{\hat{u}}_{\ell,hs}-P_{h}{\hat{u}}_{\ell}\|_{M(\hat{t}^{{{[\ell+1]}}})}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|{\hat{u}}_{\ell-1,hs}-P_{h}{\hat{u}}_{\ell-1}\|_{M(\hat{t}^{{[\ell]}})}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{r}\left(h_{{\mathtt{v}}}^{s}\|u\|_{s+1,\infty,{\mathtt{v}}}+h_{{\mathtt{v}}}^{p+1}|u|_{{\mathtt{v}},p+1,s-1}\right).

Applying this estimate for
\ell=r,r-1,\ldots,1
, successively in
that order, where at each step the first term on the right hand side
is bounded using the next estimate,

\|{\hat{u}}_{rhs}-P_{h}{\hat{u}}(1)\|_{M(1)}\lesssim\|{\hat{u}}_{h}^{0}-P_{h}{\hat{u}}(0)\|_{M(0)}+\left(h_{{\mathtt{v}}}^{s}\|u\|_{s+1,\infty,{\mathtt{v}}}+h_{{\mathtt{v}}}^{p+1}|u|_{{\mathtt{v}},p+1,s-1}\right)\sum_{\ell=1}^{r}\frac{1}{r}

which completes the proof.
∎

Letting
\mathcal{R}^{\mathrm{sat}}_{rhs}
denote the collection of explicit tent
propagators
R^{\mathrm{sat}}_{rhs}\circ P_{h}:L^{{\mathtt{v}}}\to V_{h}^{\mathtt{v}}
on all
tents, we use Definition
3.9
to set the global propagators

{\mathtt{T2G}}(i,j,\mathcal{R}^{\mathrm{sat}}_{rhs})
, and consider the discrete solution

u^{\mathrm{sat}}_{rhs}={\mathtt{T2G}}(m,0,\mathcal{R}^{\mathrm{sat}}_{rhs})u^{0}
at the final time
T
.

Theorem 4.19

(Error estimate for the SAT scheme)
.

Assume that
there is a mesh-independent
C_{\mathrm{sta}}\geq 0
such that

(4.16)

\|R^{\mathrm{sat}}_{rhs}v\|_{M(1)}\leq(1+C_{\mathrm{sta}}h_{{\mathtt{v}}})\|v\|_{M(0)}

for all
v\in V_{h}^{\mathtt{v}}
on all tents
{T^{\mathtt{v}}}
. Suppose also that (
3.22
)
and the conditions of Theorem
3.12
hold. Then the
fully discrete explicit
s
-stage SAT solution
u^{\mathrm{sat}}_{rhs}
,
obtained using spatial polynomial degree

, satisfies

Proof.

Theorem

4.4.2.
Stability verification in the
p=0,\;s=1
case

This case is motivated by the many studies of the
p=0
case in the DG
literature (see e.g.
[
3
,
7
,
21
]
), often
called the finite volume case, and is illustrative of why special cases
are worth pursuing. We focus on the operator of the SAT
scheme, obtained by setting
s=1
in Definition
4.14
,
which can be simplified to

R^{\mathrm{sat}}_{h1}(\tau)=I+\tau M(\tau)^{-1}(A_{h}+M_{1}),

and the corresponding operator
R^{\mathrm{sat}}_{rh1}
obtained using
r

subtents, per Defintion
4.17
. Note that
R^{\mathrm{sat}}_{h1}(\tau)

differs slightly from the
q=1
case of
Definition
4.10
, namely,

R^{\mathrm{exp}}_{h1}(\tau)=I+\tau M_{0}^{-1}(A_{h}+M_{1})
. While

R^{\mathrm{exp}}_{h1q}
only requires one local mass matrix inversion for
q

iterations within a tent, the application of
R^{\mathrm{sat}}_{rh1}
requires
one local inversion per subtent. However,
R^{\mathrm{sat}}_{rh1}
admits a
stronger stability estimate that we shall prove after making the
following observation.

Lemma 4.20
.

When
p=0
, we have, for all
v,w\in V_{h}^{\mathtt{v}}
,

(4.17)

\displaystyle(A_{h}w,v)_{\mathtt{v}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|w\|_{\mathtt{v}}|v|_{d},

(4.18)

\displaystyle\|(A_{h}+M_{1})v\|_{{\mathtt{v}}}

\displaystyle\lesssim|v|_{d}.

Proof.

When
p=0
, the derivative terms in (
3.1
) vanish, so

\displaystyle(A_{h}w,v)_{\mathtt{v}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{K\in{\varOmega_{h}^{{\mathtt{v}}}}}-(\delta\hat{F}^{n}_{w},v)_{\partial K}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{F\in{\mathcal{F}^{\mathtt{v}}_{b}}}\frac{1}{2}(\delta(\mathcal{D}^{(n)}+B)w,v)_{F}

\displaystyle+\sum_{F\in{\mathcal{F}^{\mathtt{v}}_{i}}}(\delta\mathcal{D}^{(n_{F})}\{w\},\ldbrack{{v}}\rdbrack_{F})_{F}-(\delta S\ldbrack{{w}}\rdbrack_{F},\ldbrack{{v}}\rdbrack_{F})_{F}

where we have rearranged the sum to run over the
mesh facets. Now, by Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,
(
2.11
), and Lemma
3.2
, the estimate
of (
4.17
) follows.

Of course, (
4.17
) can also be written as

(w,A_{h}^{t}v)_{\mathtt{v}}\lesssim\|w\|_{\mathtt{v}}|v|_{d}
where
A_{h}^{t}
is
the
L^{{\mathtt{v}}}
-adjoint of
A_{h}
. When this is added to the
obvious inequality

(-(A_{h}+A_{h}^{t}+M_{1})v,w)_{\mathtt{v}}\leq(-(2A_{h}+M_{1})v,v)_{\mathtt{v}}^{1/2}(-(2A_{h}+M_{1})w,w)_{\mathtt{v}}^{1/2}=|v|_{d}|w|_{d},

we obtain
(-(A_{h}+M_{1})v,w)_{\mathtt{v}}\lesssim|v|_{d}\|w\|_{\mathtt{v}}
, so

\|(A_{h}+M_{1})v\|_{{\mathtt{v}}}=\sup_{0\neq w\in V_{h}^{\mathtt{v}}}\frac{((A_{h}+M_{1})v,w)_{\mathtt{v}}}{\|w\|_{\mathtt{v}}}\lesssim|v|_{d}

proves (
4.18
).
∎

Let
K_{h}
denote the kernel of
A_{h}+A_{h}^{t}+M_{1}:V_{h}^{\mathtt{v}}\to V_{h}^{\mathtt{v}}
and
let
K_{h}^{\perp}
denote its
L^{{\mathtt{v}}}
-orthogonal complement in
V_{h}^{\mathtt{v}}
. Set

(4.19)

\kappa=\sup_{0\leq\tau\leq 1}\,\sup_{\;0\neq v\in K_{h}^{\perp}}\frac{\|M(\tau)^{-1}(A_{h}+M_{1})v\|_{M(\tau)}^{2}}{|v|_{d}^{2}}.

Proposition 4.21

(Conditional strong stability)
.

In the case
p=0
and
s=1
, the constant
\kappa

of (
4.19
) satisfies
\kappa\lesssim 1
.
For all

(4.20)

0\leq\tau\leq 1/\kappa

and all
v\in V_{h}^{\mathtt{v}}
, we have

(4.21)

\|R^{\mathrm{sat}}_{h1}(\tau)v\|_{M(\tau)}\leq\|v\|_{M_{0}}.

Furthermore, if
r\geq\kappa
subtents are used, then

(4.22)

\|R^{\mathrm{sat}}_{rh1}v\|_{M(\tau)}\leq\|v\|_{M_{0}},

i.e., the stability assumption (
4.16
) of
Theorem
4.19
holds with
C_{\mathrm{sta}}=0
.

Proof.

Since
\|M(\tau)^{-1}\|_{{\mathtt{v}},h}\lesssim 1
by
Lemma
4.4
, the estimate (
4.18
) of
Lemma
4.20
shows that
\kappa\lesssim 1
whenever
p=0
.

Let
v_{\tau}=R^{\mathrm{sat}}_{h1}(\tau)v=v+\tau M^{-1}(A_{h}+M_{1})v.

Then expanding
\|v_{\tau}\|_{M}^{2}
,

\displaystyle\|v_{\tau}\|_{M}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|v\|_{M}^{2}+2\tau((A_{h}+M_{1})v,v)_{\mathtt{v}}+\tau^{2}\|M^{-1}(A_{h}+M_{1})v\|_{M}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|v\|_{M_{0}}^{2}+\tau((2A_{h}+M_{1})v,v)_{\mathtt{v}}+\tau^{2}\|M^{-1}(A_{h}+M_{1})v\|_{M}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|v\|_{M_{0}}^{2}-\tau|v|_{d}^{2}+\tau^{2}\kappa|v|_{d}^{2}

so (
4.21
) follows when
1-\tau\kappa\geq 0
.

Next, consider a subtent
{T_{{[\ell]}}^{\mathtt{v}}}
. By (
4.15
),

R^{\mathrm{sat}}_{{{[\ell]}},h1}(1)=I+M^{{[\ell]}}(1)^{-1}(A_{h}^{{[\ell]}}+M_{1}^{{[\ell]}})=I+r^{-1}M(\hat{t}^{{{[\ell+1]}}})(A_{h}+M_{1})
, so translating (
4.21
)
with
\tau=1/r\leq 1/\kappa
to this subtent, we obtain

\|R^{\mathrm{sat}}_{{{[\ell]}},h1}(1)v\|_{M(t^{{{[\ell+1]}}})}\leq\|v\|_{M(t^{{[\ell]}})}
. Successively applying these estimates over all
subtents, (
4.22
) is proved.
∎

Note that the inverse of
\kappa
appearing in (
4.20
) will
stay away from zero (since
\kappa\lesssim 1
) allowing for a
nontrivial advance in
\tau
. One can view (
4.20
) as the
analogue of a traditional “CFL condition”
within
a tent.
Indeed, the pseudotime restriction (
4.20
) may be interpreted
as a restriction on time advance in the physical spacetime by a small
subtent whose tent pole height is a scalar multiple of
h_{{\mathtt{v}}}
.
Even in the event (
4.20
)
forbids us to reach the tent-top pseudotime
(i.e., when
\tau=1
does not satisfy (
4.20
)), splitting
the tent into smaller subtents does allow
the analogue of (
4.20
) to hold throughout every subtent.

4.4.3.
Stability verification in the
s=2
case

We will now show how to prove stability under a stronger CFL condition
in the two-stage case. Definition
4.14
with
s=2
yields

R^{\mathrm{sat}}_{h2}(\tau)=I+\tau X_{h}^{({1})}+\frac{\tau^{2}}{2}M^{-1}M_{0}{X_{h}^{({2})}}.

Lemma 4.22
.

For any
v\in V_{h}^{\mathtt{v}}
,

\displaystyle\|R^{\mathrm{sat}}_{h2}(\tau)v\|_{M(\tau)}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|v\|_{M_{0}}^{2}-\tau\left|v+\frac{\tau}{2}X_{h}^{({1})}v\right|_{d}^{2}+\tau^{3}\,Z(\tau,v),

where
Z(\tau,v)=[2(M_{1}X_{h}^{({1})}v,X_{h}^{({1})}v)_{\mathtt{v}}-|X_{h}^{({1})}v|_{d}^{2}+\tau\|M(\tau)^{-1}M_{0}{X_{h}^{({2})}}v\|_{M}^{2}]/4.

Proof.

Let
w=\tau X_{h}^{({1})}v+(\tau^{2}/2)M^{-1}M_{0}{X_{h}^{({2})}}v.
Then

v_{\tau}=R^{\mathrm{sat}}_{h2}(\tau)v
can be written as

v_{\tau}=v+w
. Expanding
\|v+w\|_{M}^{2}
,

\displaystyle\|v_{\tau}\|_{M}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|v\|_{M_{0}}^{2}-\tau(M_{1}v,v)_{\mathtt{v}}+2(w,v)_{M}+\|w\|_{M}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|v\|_{M_{0}}^{2}-\tau(M_{1}v,v)_{\mathtt{v}}+2\tau((M_{0}-\tau M_{1})X_{h}^{({1})}v,v)_{\mathtt{v}}+\tau^{2}(M_{0}{X_{h}^{({2})}}v,v)_{\mathtt{v}}+\|w\|_{M}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|v\|_{M_{0}}^{2}+\tau((2A_{h}+M_{1})v,v)_{\mathtt{v}}+\tau^{2}(A_{h}X_{h}^{({1})}v,v)_{\mathtt{v}}+\|w\|_{M}^{2}.

Note that
(A_{h}X_{h}^{({1})}v,v)_{\mathtt{v}}=(X_{h}^{({1})}v,A_{h}^{t}v)_{\mathtt{v}}=(X_{h}^{({1})}v,(A_{h}^{t}+A_{h}+M_{1})v)_{\mathtt{v}}-\|X_{h}^{({1})}v\|_{M_{0}}^{2}
. Since

d(y,z)=-((A_{h}^{t}+A_{h}+M_{1})y,z)_{\mathtt{v}}
, we have

\displaystyle\|v_{\tau}\|_{M}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|v\|_{M_{0}}^{2}-\tau|v|_{d}^{2}-\tau^{2}d(X_{h}^{({1})}v,v)-\tau^{2}\|X_{h}^{({1})}v\|_{M_{0}}^{2}+\|w\|_{M}^{2}.

Next, letting
z=(1/2)M^{-1}M_{0}{X_{h}^{({2})}}v
,
expanding the last term above

\|w\|_{M}^{2}=\tau^{2}\|X_{h}^{({1})}v\|_{M}^{2}+\tau^{3}(X_{h}^{({1})}v,M_{0}{X_{h}^{({2})}}v)_{\mathtt{v}}+\tau^{4}\|z\|_{M}^{2},

noting that

M_{0}{X_{h}^{({2})}}=(A_{h}+2M_{1})X_{h}^{({1})}
, and simplifying,

\displaystyle\|v_{\tau}\|_{M}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|v\|_{M_{0}}^{2}-\tau|v|_{d}^{2}-\tau^{2}d(X_{h}^{({1})}v,v)+\tau^{3}((A_{h}+M_{1})X_{h}^{({1})}v,X_{h}^{({1})}v)_{\mathtt{v}}+\tau^{4}\|z\|_{M}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|v\|_{M_{0}}^{2}-\tau\big|v+\frac{\tau}{2}X_{h}^{({1})}v\big|_{d}^{2}+\frac{\tau^{3}}{4}((2A_{h}+3M_{1})X_{h}^{({1})}v,X_{h}^{({1})}v)_{\mathtt{v}}+\tau^{4}\|z\|_{M}^{2}

from which the stated identity follows.
∎

Proposition 4.23
.

Let
v\in V_{h}^{\mathtt{v}}
and
r
be chosen as the smallest integer not
smaller than
\kappa_{2}^{1/3}/h_{{\mathtt{v}}}^{1/2}
where
\kappa_{2}
is defined
using
Z(\tau,v)
of Lemma
4.22
by

\kappa_{2}=\sup_{0\leq\tau\leq 1}\;\sup_{0\neq v\in V_{h}^{\mathtt{v}}}\frac{Z(\tau,v)}{\|v\|_{M_{0}}^{2}}.

Then

\|R^{\mathrm{sat}}_{h2}(\tau)v\|_{M(\tau)}\leq(1+h_{{\mathtt{v}}}^{3/2})^{1/2}\|v\|_{M_{0}}\quad\text{ for all }\tau\leq 1/r,

and
R^{\mathrm{sat}}_{rh2}
satisfies
the stability assumption (
4.16
) of
Theorem
4.19
.

Proof.

By Lemma
4.22
and the definition of
\kappa_{2}
,

\|R^{\mathrm{sat}}_{h2}(\tau)v\|_{M(\tau)}^{2}\leq\|v\|_{M_{0}}^{2}+\tau^{3}\kappa_{2}\|v\|_{M_{0}}^{2}\leq(1+h_{{\mathtt{v}}}^{3/2})\|v\|_{M_{0}}^{2},

since
\tau^{3}\kappa_{2}=\kappa_{2}/r^{3}\leq h_{{\mathtt{v}}}^{3/2}.

Applying this successively on each subtent, we obtain

\|R^{\mathrm{sat}}_{rh2}v\|_{M(1)}\leq(1+h_{{\mathtt{v}}}^{3/2})^{r/2}\|v\|_{M_{0}}.

Next, we use the bound

(1+h_{{\mathtt{v}}}^{3/2})^{r/2}\leq\exp(h_{{\mathtt{v}}}^{3/2}r/2)
. Since the
argument of the exponential is bounded,

\exp(h^{3/2}r/2)-1\lesssim h_{{\mathtt{v}}}^{3/2}r/2\lesssim h_{{\mathtt{v}}}
.
Thus there is an
h_{{\mathtt{v}}}
-independent constant
C>0
such that

\|R^{\mathrm{sat}}_{rh2}v\|_{M(1)}\leq(1+Ch_{{\mathtt{v}}})\|v\|_{M_{0}}.

∎

Note that by Lemma
4.4
, the constant
\kappa_{2}

satisfies
\kappa_{2}\lesssim 1
. Hence Proposition
4.23

gives stability under a so-called “3/2-CFL” condition. The latter term
is an adaptation of the terminology
on CFL conditions in
[
3
]
for our tents,
in view of the fact
that our

\tau\leq 1/r
condition,
with
r
as in Proposition
4.23
, implies
that the amount of time advance
along a tent pole
(
\tau\delta
)
is limited by
O(h_{{\mathtt{v}}}^{3/2})
.

5.
Conclusion

We have developed a convergence theory for MTP schemes for a large
class of linear hyperbolic systems, covering the semidiscrete case
(§
3
), as well as a few fully discrete schemes
(§
4
). The convergence rate for the
semidiscretization was established to be
O(h^{p+1/2})
in
Theorem
3.12
under reasonable assumptions. When the
number of stages
s=p+1
, the fully discrete SAT scheme also gave
the same convergence rate (Theorem
4.19
) under the
stability assumption (
4.16
). Through a
selected numerical example, we showed in
§
4.4.1
that this convergence rate cannot be
improved in general. The stability of SAT scheme was verified in
§
4.4.2
for the
p=0,

s=1
case and in
§
4.4.3
for the (arbitrary
p
)
s=2
case. Proving the
stability of SAT schemes (verifying (
4.16
))
for other values of
s
is currently an open problem. It is however
possible to computationally verify stability within each tent by
solving a small eigenvalue problem as shown
in
[
14
]
. The numerical results there suggest that an
estimate of the form

\|R^{\mathrm{sat}}_{rhs}v\|_{M}\leq(1+Cr^{-s})\|v\|_{M_{0}}
might hold for
general
r
and
s
. If this is provable, then for larger
s
, a
slight modification of the argument of Proposition
4.23

would prove stability under a less stringent
(1+1/s)
-CFL
condition, which limits the amount of time advance by a
scalar multiple of
h_{{\mathtt{v}}}^{1+1/s}
. Also, if our analysis
in §
4.4.2
is any indication, it might be a worthwhile
future pursuit to seek further special cases where stability holds
under even weaker CFL conditions within a tent. The simplest cases of
the fully discrete analyses we presented are those of the lowest order
tent-implicit scheme in §
4.2
and the
lowest order iterated explicit scheme in
§
4.3
. The latter was obtained from a
nontraditional viewpoint of explicit schemes as iterative solvers for
implicit schemes.
