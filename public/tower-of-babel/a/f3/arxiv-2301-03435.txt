The Algebra of Nondeterministic Finite Automata
Roberto Gorrieri

Abstract
A process algebra is proposed, whose semantics maps a term to a nondeterministic finite automaton (NFA, for short). We prove a representability theorem: for each NFA N N , there exists a process algebraic term p p such that its semantics is an NFA isomorphic to N N . Moreover, we provide a concise axiomatization of language equivalence: two NFAs N 1 N_{1} and N 2 N_{2} recognize the same language if and only if the associated terms p 1 p_{1} and p 2 p_{2} , respectively, can be equated by means of a set of axioms, comprising 7 axioms plus 3 conditional axioms, only.

One advantage of this more concrete representability theorem is that the algebra SFM1 can be used as a basis to study
all the possible equivalences that can be defined over NFAs, not only language equivalence

The main contribution of this paper is the proof that
language equivalence can be characterized over SFM1 terms by means of a very concise axiomatization,
comprising only 7 axioms (4 for disjunction and 3 for prefixing) and 3 conditional axioms (for recursion).
The axioms we present are very similar to known axioms
[
17
,
27
,
18
,
20

21
,
14
]

In fact, for disjunction they are the usual ones: the choice operator is an idempotent, commutative monoid, with
0

The axioms for prefixing are similar to those for concatenation:
0
is an annihilator, the prefixing operator distributes over a choice term,
and the prefix
\epsilon
can be absorbed. The axioms for recursion are also similar to axioms developed for SFM
[

14

inspired to those for finite-state CCS
[

,

), and the

Section

Section

Let

Definition 1

A nondeterministic finite automaton (NFA for short) is a tuple
N=(Q,A,

1.

q

2.

3.

is the set of transitions, ranged over by

(possibly indexed);

4.

is the set of final states;

5.

is the initial state.

Given a transition

q
is called the source,
\alpha
the label of the transition (denoted also by
l(t)
), and

q^{\prime}
the target; this is usually represented as
q{\mbox{${\>\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\alpha}}{{\longrightarrow}}\>}$}}q^{\prime}

is deterministic if:

1.

\forall t\in T\;l(t)\in A
(i.e., there are no
\epsilon

2.

\forall q\in Q\,\forall a\in A
there exists exactly one state
q^{\prime}\in Q
such that
(q,a,q^{\prime})\in T
, i.e.,
T
can be equivalently represented as a function of type
Q\times A\rightarrow Q
.

A deterministic NFA is called a deterministic finite automaton - DFA, for short.

\Box

Definition 3

(Recognized language and equivalence)

Given

(Bisimulation)

Let

Definition 5

(Isomorphism)

Two NFAs

Congruence

In the following, for each SFM1 process
p
, its associated language
L(p)
is the language recognized by
the reduced NFA
\llbracket p\rrbracket_{\emptyset}
, computed according to the denotational semantics
in Table

.

Definition 6

Two SFM1 processes
p
and
q
are language equivalent, denoted
p\sim q
, if

L(p)=L(q)
, i.e., they recognize the same language.

\Box

Proposition 1

The following hold:

1)
L(\mbox{\bf 0})=\emptyset
.

2)
L(\mbox{\bf 1})=\{\epsilon\}
.

3) For each
p\in\mathcal{P}_{SFM1}
, for all
a\in A
, we have that

L(a.p)=\{aw\;\;\big|\;\;w\in L(p)\}
.

4) For each
p\in\mathcal{P}_{SFM1}
, we have that

L(\epsilon.p)=\{\epsilon w\;\;\big|\;\;w\in L(p)\}=L(p)
.

5) For each
p,q\in\mathcal{P}_{SFM1}^{grd}
,
L(p+q)=L(p)\cup L(q)
.

Proof 4

Obvious by construction in Table
2
.

\Box

Now we show that language equivalence is a congruence for all the SFM1 operators.

Proposition 2

1) For each
p,q\in\mathcal{P}_{SFM1}
, if
p\sim q
, then
\alpha.p\sim\alpha.q

for all
\alpha\in A\cup\{\epsilon\}
.

2) For each
p,q\in\mathcal{P}_{SFM1}^{grd}
, if
p\sim q
,
then
p+r\sim q+r\;

for all
r\in\mathcal{P}_{SFM1}^{grd}
.

Proof 5

By hypothesis, we know that
L(p)=L(q)
. Then the thesis follows easily by application of Proposition
1
.

Now, for case 1,
L(\alpha.p)=\{\alpha w\;\;\big|\;\;w\in L(p)\}=\{\alpha w\;\;\big|\;\;w\in L(q)\}=L(\alpha.q)
and so

\alpha.p\sim\alpha.q
.
While, for case 2,
L(p+r)=L(p)\cup L(r)=L(q)\cup L(r)=L(q+r)
and so

p+r\sim q+r
.

\Box

Note that the symmetric case
r+p\sim r+q
is implied by the fact that
the operator of choice is commutative w.r.t.
\sim
(see Proposition
3
).

Still there is one construct missing: recursion, defined over guarded terms only.
Consider an extension of SFM1 where terms can be constructed using variables, such as
x,y,\ldots

(which are in syntactic category
p
):
this defines
an “open” SFM1 that has the following syntax:

\begin{array}[]{lccccccccccl}s&::=&\mbox{\bf 1}&|&\mbox{\bf 0}&|&a.p&|&s+s&\\
p&::=&s&|&C&|&x&&\\
\end{array}

where the semantic of
x
is the NFA
(\{x\},\emptyset,\emptyset,\emptyset,x)
.

Sometimes we use the notation
p(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
to state explicitly that term
p
is open on the tuple of variables
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
.
For instance,
p_{1}(x)=a.(b.\mbox{\bf 1}+c.x)
and
p_{2}(x)=a.c.x+a.b.\mbox{\bf 1}
are open guarded SFM1 terms.
Similarly,
C_{1}(x)\doteq a.x
denotes explicitly that the constant
C_{1}
is defined by a term open on
x
.

An open term
p(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
can be closed by means of a substitution as follows (see also Section
6
for the definition of substitution application, also in the case of process constants):

p(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\{r_{1}/x_{1},\ldots,r_{n}/x_{n}\}

with the effect that each occurrence of the free variable
x_{i}
is replaced by the closed SFM1
process
r_{i}
, for
i=1,\ldots,n
. For instance,

p_{1}(x)\{d.\mbox{\bf 0}/x\}=a.(b.\mbox{\bf 1}+c.d.\mbox{\bf 0})
, as well as
C_{1}(x)\{d.\mbox{\bf 0}/x\}
is
C_{1}\doteq a.d.\mbox{\bf 0}
(i.e., we close the open
term
a.x
defining the body of
C_{1}
).
A natural extension of language equivalence
\sim
over open guarded terms is as follows:

p(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\sim q(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})

if for all tuples of closed SFM1 terms

(r_{1},\ldots,r_{n})
,

p(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\{r_{1}/x_{1},\ldots,r_{n}/x_{n}\}

\sim

q(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\{r_{1}/x_{1},\ldots,r_{n}/x_{n}\}
.

E.g., it is easy to see that
p_{1}(x)\sim p_{2}(x)
. As a matter of fact, for all
r
,

p_{1}(x)\{r/x\}=a.(b.\mbox{\bf 1}+c.r)

\sim

a.c.r+a.b.\mbox{\bf 1}
=
p_{2}(x)\{r/x\}
, which can be easily
proved by means of the algebraic properties (see the next section) and the congruence ones of
\sim
.

For simplicity’s sake, let us now restrict our attention to open guarded terms using a single undefined variable.
We can recursively close an open term
p(x)
by means of a recursively defined constant. For instance,

C\doteq p(x)\{C/x\}
. The resulting process constant

is a closed SFM1 process.
By saying that language equivalence is a congruence for
recursion we mean the following: If
p(x)\sim q(x)
and
C\doteq p(x)\{C/x\}
and
D\doteq q(x)\{D/x\}
,
then
C\sim D
.
The following theorem states this fact.
For simplicity’s sake, in the following a term
p
, open on a variable
x
, is annotated
as
p(x)
only when it is unclear from the context, because we work with real terms and not with annotated ones.

Remark 2

Throughout the paper, it is assumed that whenever a constant
C
is defined by

,
then the open guarded term
p
does not
contain occurrences of
C
, i.e.,

,
so that all the instances of

in

are due to substitution of

D\doteq q\{D/y\}

\Box

Theorem 3

Let
p
and
q
be two open guarded SFM1 terms, with one variable

at most.
Let
C\doteq p\{C/x\}
,
D\doteq q\{D/x\}

and

. Then

.

Proof 6

For the term
p
, open on
x
, we define the following languages:

1.

L_{p}^{\downarrow}=\{\sigma\in A^{*}\;\;\big|\;\;\exists p^{\prime}\in F.p{\mbox{${\>\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sigma}}{{\Longrightarrow}}\>}$}}p^{\prime}\}
is the set of the words recognized by
p
;

2.

L_{p}^{x}=\{\sigma\in A^{*}\;\;\big|\;\;p{\mbox{${\>\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sigma}}{{\Longrightarrow}}\>}$}}x\}
is the set of its maximal words reaching the variable

.

It is rather easy to see that for

, we have that
L(C)=(L_{p}^{x})^{*}\cdot L_{p}^{\downarrow}
,
where

[

It is not difficult to see that

and

\Box

Algebraic Properties

Now we list some algebraic properties of language equivalence and discuss whether they hold for bisimulation equivalence or isomorphism equivalence.

Proposition 3

(Laws of the choice op.)

For each
p,q,r\in\mathcal{P}_{SFM1}^{grd}
, the following hold:

\begin{array}[]{lrcllllll}&p+(q+r)&\sim&(p+q)+r&&\;\mbox{(associativity)}\\
&p+q&\sim&q+p&&\;\mbox{(commutativity)}\\
&p+\mbox{\bf 0}&\sim&p&&\;\mbox{(identity)}\\
&p+p&\sim&p&&\;\mbox{(idempotency)}\end{array}

Proof 7

The proof follows directly from Proposition
1
(5). For instance,

p+q\sim q+p
holds because
L(p+q)=L(p)\cup L(q)=L(q)\cup L(p)=L(q+p)
.

\Box

The laws of the choice operator hold also for bisimilarity
[
22
,
11
,
4
,
28
]
,
but they do not hold for isomorphism equivalence. For instance, consider
p=a.A
, with
A\doteq a.(b.B+a.A)
,
and
q=b.B
, with
B\doteq b.B+\mbox{\bf 1}
; then, it is easy to see that the NFA for
p+q
(four states)
is not isomorphic to the NFA for
q+p
(three states).

Proposition 4

(Laws of the prefixing operator)

For each
p,q\in\mathcal{P}_{SFM1}^{grd}
, for each
r\in\mathcal{P}_{SFM1}
,
for each
\alpha\in A\cup\{\epsilon\}
, the following hold:

\begin{array}[]{lrcllllll}&\alpha.\mbox{\bf 0}&\sim&\mbox{\bf 0}&&\;\mbox{(annihilation)}\\
&\alpha.(p+q)&\sim&\alpha.p+\alpha.q&&\;\mbox{(distributivity)}\\
&\epsilon.r&\sim&r&&\;\mbox{($\epsilon$-absorption)}\end{array}

Proof 8

The proof follows directly from Proposition
1
. The law

\alpha.\mbox{\bf 0}\sim\mbox{\bf 0}
holds because
L(\alpha.\mbox{\bf 0})=\{\alpha w\;\;\big|\;\;w\in L(\mbox{\bf 0})\}=\{\alpha w\;\;\big|\;\;w\in\emptyset\}=\emptyset=L(\mbox{\bf 0})
. The law
\alpha.(p+q)\sim\alpha.p+\alpha.q

holds because
L(\alpha.(p+q))=\{\alpha w\;\;\big|\;\;w\in L(p+q)\}=

\{\alpha w\;\;\big|\;\;w\in L(p)\cup L(q)\}=\{\alpha w\;\;\big|\;\;w\in L(p)\}\cup\{\alpha w\;\;\big|\;\;w\in L(q)\}

=L(\alpha.p)\cup L(\alpha.q)=L(\alpha.p+\alpha.q)
. The law

\epsilon.r\sim r
holds because
L(\epsilon.r)=\{\epsilon w\;\;\big|\;\;w\in L(r)\}=\{w\;\;\big|\;\;w\in L(r)\}=L(r)
.

\Box

It is easy to see that none of the laws for prefixing hold for bisimilarity, and so not even for isomorphism equivalence.

We now focus on the properties of constants. First, the unfolding law (which holds also for bisimilarity), then the excision law
(useful to remove loops of
\epsilon
transitions)
and finally, in the next subsection, the folding law, whose proof relies on some auxiliary technical machinery (fixed point theorems on a
complete lattice, see, e.g.,
[
8
,
28
]
).

Proposition 5

(Unfolding)

For each

5.1

The Folding Law

In order to prove the folding law, we first observe that
(\mathcal{P}(A^{*}),\subseteq)
is a complete lattice, i.e., with the property that
all the subsets of
\mathcal{P}(A^{*})
( e.g.,
\{L_{i}\}_{i\in I}
) have joins (i.e.,
\bigcup_{i\in I}L_{i}
) and meets
(i.e.,
\bigcap_{i\in I}L_{i}
), whose top element
\top
is
A^{*}
and bottom element
\bot
is
\emptyset
.

At a first approximation, the folding law states that if
C\doteq p\{C/x\}
and
q\sim p\{q/x\}
, then
C\sim q
.
Of course, we can easily realize that
L(C)=L_{p}^{\downarrow}\cup(L_{p}^{x}\cdot L(C))
as well as

L(q)=L_{p}^{\downarrow}\cup(L_{p}^{x}\cdot L(q))
. Therefore, both
L(C)
and
L(q)
are a fixed point of the
mapping
T(W)=L_{p}^{\downarrow}\cup(L_{p}^{x}\cdot W)
. We want to show that, under one mild condition,
there exists exactly one fixed point of

T
, so that
L(C)=L(q)
and so

C\sim q
, as required.

If the mapping
T
is continuous (i.e.,
for all increasing sequences
L_{0}\subseteq L_{1}\subseteq L_{2}\ldots
we have that
T(\bigcup_{i}L_{i})=\bigcup_{i}T(L_{i})
),
then the least fixed point
lfp
(T)=\bigcup_{n\geq 0}T^{n}(\emptyset)
, where
T^{0}(L)=L
and
T^{n+1}(L)=T(T^{n}(L))
.
Indeed,
T
is continuous because
T(\bigcup_{i}L_{i})=L_{p}^{\downarrow}\cup(L_{p}^{x}\cdot\bigcup_{i}L_{i})

=L_{p}^{\downarrow}\cup\bigcup_{i}(L_{p}^{x}\cdot L_{i})=

\bigcup_{i}(L_{p}^{\downarrow}\cup(L_{p}^{x}\cdot L_{i}))=\bigcup_{i}T(L_{i})
.
The least fixed point
lfp
(T)=\bigcup_{n\geq 0}T^{n}(\emptyset)
can be computed as follows:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}T^{0}(\emptyset)&=&\emptyset\\
T^{1}(\emptyset)&=&L_{p}^{\downarrow}\\
T^{2}(\emptyset)&=&L_{p}^{\downarrow}\cup(L_{p}^{x}\cdot L_{p}^{\downarrow})\\
T^{3}(\emptyset)&=&L_{p}^{\downarrow}\cup(L_{p}^{x}\cdot L_{p}^{\downarrow})\cup(L_{p}^{x}\cdot(L_{p}^{\downarrow}\cup(L_{p}^{x}\cdot L_{p}^{\downarrow}))=L_{p}^{\downarrow}\cup(L_{p}^{x}\cdot L_{p}^{\downarrow})\cup L_{p}^{x}\cdot(L_{p}^{x}\cdot L_{p}^{\downarrow})\\
\ldots\\
\end{array}

whose union is the language
lfp
(T)=(L_{p}^{x})^{*}\cdot L_{p}^{\downarrow}
.

Dually, if
T
is co-continuous (i.e.,
for all decreasing sequences
L_{0}\supseteq L_{1}\supseteq L_{2}\ldots
we have that
T(\bigcap_{i}L_{i})=\bigcap_{i}T(L_{i})
),
then the greatest fixed point
gfp
(T)=\bigcap_{n\geq 0}T^{n}(A^{*})
.
Indeed,
T
is co-continuous because
T(\bigcap_{i}L_{i})=L_{p}^{\downarrow}\cup(L_{p}^{x}\cdot\bigcap_{i}L_{i})

=L_{p}^{\downarrow}\cup\bigcap_{i}(L_{p}^{x}\cdot L_{i})=

\bigcap_{i}(L_{p}^{\downarrow}\cup(L_{p}^{x}\cdot L_{i}))=\bigcap_{i}T(L_{i})
.
The greatest fixed point
gfp
(T)=\bigcap_{n\geq 0}T^{n}(A^{*})
can be computed as follows:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}T^{0}(A^{*})&=&A^{*}\\
T^{1}(A^{*})&=&L_{p}^{\downarrow}\cup(L_{p}^{x}\cdot A^{*})\\
T^{2}(A^{*})&=&L_{p}^{\downarrow}\cup(L_{p}^{x}\cdot(L_{p}^{\downarrow}\cup(L_{p}^{x}\cdot A^{*}))=L_{p}^{\downarrow}\cup(L_{p}^{x}\cdot L_{p}^{\downarrow})\cup L_{p}^{x}\cdot(L_{p}^{x}\cdot A^{*})\\

&&L_{p}^{\downarrow}\cup(L_{p}^{x}\cdot L_{p}^{\downarrow})\cup L_{p}^{x}\cdot(L_{p}^{x}\cdot L_{p}^{\downarrow})\cup L_{p}^{x}\cdot(L_{p}^{x}\cdot(L_{p}^{x}\cdot A^{*}))\\
\ldots\\
\end{array}

whose intersection is the language

6

Equational Deduction with Process Constants

In the following, we show that it is possible to prove syntactically, i.e., by means of an equational deductive proof, when two SFM1 processes recognize the same language.
First, in this section we describe how equational deduction is implemented in a process algebra with process constants;
then in the next section we provide a sound and complete axiomatization of language equivalence for observationally guarded SFM1 processes, composed of 7 axioms and 2 conditional axioms;
finally, we show that each observationally unguarded process can be equated to an observationally guarded one with the help of one additional conditional axiom. Hence, the finite axiomatization of language equivalence over SFM1 is composed of 7 axioms and 3 conditional axioms, only.
Let us start with an equational deduction primer.

Given a (one-sorted) signature
\Sigma

Proposition 8

(Reduction to normal form)

Given an SFM1 process
p
, there exists a normal form
q
such that
B\vdash p=q
.

Proof 13

The proof is by induction on the structure of
p
, with the proviso to use a set

of already scanned constants,
in order to avoid looping on recursively defined constants, where

is initially empty.
We prove that for
(p,I)
there exists a term
q
such that
nf(q,I)
holds
and
(B,I)\vdash p=q
, where this means that the equality
p=q
can be derived by the axioms in
B
when
each constant
C\in I
is assumed to be equated to itself only. The thesis then follows by considering
(p,\emptyset)
.

The first base case is
(\mbox{\bf 1},I)
; in such a case,
q=\mbox{\bf 1}
, because

holds, and the thesis

(B,I)\vdash\mbox{\bf 1}=\mbox{\bf 1}

The second base case is
(\mbox{\bf 0},I)
and it is analogous to the previous one.

Case
(\alpha.p,I)
: by induction,
(p,I)
has an associated normal form

such that
(B,I)\vdash p=q
;
hence,
(B,I)\vdash\alpha.p=\alpha.q
by substitutivity. If
q
is a constant, then
\alpha.q
is already a

take a new constant

, so that
nf(C,I)
holds because
nf(q,I\cup\{C\})
holds
(note that
C
does not occur in

, which holds by induction).
The required normal form is
\alpha.C

holds; moreover,

(B,I)\vdash p=q

(B,I)\vdash p=C
by transitivity, so that

(B,I)\vdash\alpha.p=\alpha.C
by substitutivity.

Case
(p_{1}+p_{2},I)
: by induction, we can assume that for
(p_{i},I)
there exists a normal form

such that
(B,I)\vdash p_{i}=q_{i}
for

. Then,

holds and

(B,I)\vdash p_{1}+p_{2}=q_{1}+q_{2}
by substitutivity.

Case
(C,I)
, where
C\doteq r\{C/x\}
. If
C\in I
, then we can stop induction, by returning
C
itself:

holds

(B,I)\vdash C=C
by reflexivity.
Otherwise (i.e., if
C\not\in I
), by induction on
(r\{C/x\},I\cup\{C\})
, we can assume that there exists
a normal form
q\{C/x\}
such that

holds and

(B,I\cup\{C\})\vdash r\{C/x\}=q\{C/x\}

(B,I\cup\{C\})\vdash r\{C/x\}=q\{C/x\}

(B,I)\vdash r\{C/x\}=q\{C/x\}

(B,I)\vdash C=q\{C/x\}

Then, we take a new constant
D\doteq q\{D/x\}

nf(D,I)

(B,I)\vdash C=D

, where

(Reduction to observationally guarded normal form)

Proof 14

All the cases are trivial adaptation of the corresponding above, except the one for recursion.

Remark 6

(Normal forms as systems of equations)

As a matter of fact, we can restrict our attention only to
normal forms defined by constants: if
p
is a normal form and

p
is not a constant, then take a new constant
D\doteq p
, which is a normal form such that
B\vdash D=p

by axiom
R1
. For this reason,
in the following we restrict our attention to
normal forms that can be defined by means of a system of equations: a set

\widetilde{C}=\{C_{1},C_{2},\ldots,C_{n}\}
of defined constants in normal form,
such that
\mbox{{\it Const}$(C_{1})$}=\widetilde{C}
and
\mbox{{\it Const}$(C_{i})$}\subseteq\widetilde{C}
for
i=1,\ldots,n
.
The equations of the system
E(\widetilde{C})

are of the following form (where function

f(i,j)
returns a value
k
such that
1\leq k\leq n
):

\begin{array}[]{lcllcllcl}C_{1}&\doteq&\sum_{i=1}^{m(1)}\alpha_{1i}.C_{f(1,i)}\{+\mbox{\bf 1}\}\\
C_{2}&\doteq&\sum_{i=1}^{m(2)}\alpha_{2i}.C_{f(2,i)}\{+\mbox{\bf 1}\}\\
\ldots\\
C_{n}&\doteq&\sum_{i=1}^{m(n)}\alpha_{ni}.C_{f(n,i)}\{+\mbox{\bf 1}\}\\
\end{array}

where by the notation
\{+\mbox{\bf 1}\}
we mean that the additional summand
1
is optional, and where
we assume
C_{i}\doteq\mbox{\bf 0}
in case
m(i)=0
. We sometimes use the notation
body(C_{h})
to denote
the sumform
\sum_{i=1}^{m(h)}\alpha_{hi}.C_{f(h,i)}\{+\mbox{\bf 1}\}
.

\Box

Remark 7

(Observationally guarded system of equations)

We say that a system of equations
E(\widetilde{C})
, not necessarily in normal form
like the one above, is
observationally guarded if, for each
C_{i}\in\widetilde{C}
, there is no silent cycle
C_{i}{\mbox{${\>\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\epsilon}}{{\Longrightarrow}}\>}$}}C_{i}
.
This definition is coherent with Definition
7
.
For instance, consider the following example:

\begin{array}[]{lcllcllcl}C_{1}&\doteq&a.C_{2}+\epsilon.a.C_{1}\\
C_{2}&\doteq&b.C_{2}+\epsilon.C_{1}\\
\end{array}

Of course,
C_{i}{\mbox{${\>\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\epsilon}}{{\nRightarrow}}\>}$}}C_{i}
for
i=1,2
, so that the system is observationally guarded. And indeed
we can prove that
og(C_{1})
and
og(C_{2})
hold.

\Box

The next theorem shows that every observationally guarded system of equations
(not necessarily in normal form) has a unique solution up to provably equality.

Theorem 5

(Unique solution for
W_{g}
)

Let
\widetilde{X}=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})
be a tuple of variables and let

\widetilde{p}=(p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{n})
be a tuple of
open guarded SFM1 terms, using the variables in
\widetilde{X}
.
Let
\widetilde{C}=(C_{1},C_{2},\ldots,C_{n})
be a tuple of constants (not occurring in
\widetilde{p}
)
such that the system of equations

\begin{array}[]{lcllcllcl}C_{1}&\doteq&p_{1}\{\widetilde{C}/\widetilde{X}\}\\
C_{2}&\doteq&p_{2}\{\widetilde{C}/\widetilde{X}\}\\
\ldots\\
C_{n}&\doteq&p_{n}\{\widetilde{C}/\widetilde{X}\}\end{array}

is observationally guarded.
Then, for
i=1,\ldots,n
,

W_{g}\vdash C_{i}=p_{i}\{\widetilde{C}/\widetilde{X}\}
.

Moreover, if the same property holds for
\widetilde{q}=(q_{1},q_{2},\ldots,q_{n})
, i.e.,
for
i=1,\ldots,n

W_{g}\vdash q_{i}=p_{i}\{\widetilde{q}/\widetilde{X}\}
,
then

W_{g}\vdash C_{i}=q_{i}
.

Proof 15

By induction on
n
.

For
n=1
, we have
C_{1}\doteq p_{1}\{C_{1}/x_{1}\}
,
and so the result
W_{g}\vdash C_{1}=p_{1}\{C_{1}/x_{1}\}

follows immediately using axiom
R1
.
This solution is unique because
p_{1}
is observationally guarded
(cf. Remark
7
): if
W_{g}\vdash q_{1}=p_{1}\{q_{1}/x_{1}\}
,
by axiom
R2
we get
W_{g}\vdash C_{1}=q_{1}
.

Now assume a tuple
\widetilde{p}=(p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{n})
and the term
p_{n+1}
,
so that they are all open on
\widetilde{X}=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})
and the additional
x_{n+1}
.
Assume, w.l.o.g., that
x_{n+1}
occurs in
p_{n+1}
.
First, define

C_{n+1}\doteq p_{n+1}\{C_{n+1}/x_{n+1}\}
, so that
C_{n+1}
is now open on
\widetilde{X}
.
Therefore, also for
i=1,\ldots,n
, each
p_{i}\{C_{n+1}/x_{n+1}\}
is now
open on
\widetilde{X}
.
The resulting observationally guarded
system of equations is:

\begin{array}[]{lcllcllcl}C_{1}&\doteq&p_{1}\{C_{n+1}/x_{n+1}\}\{\widetilde{C}/\widetilde{X}\}\\
C_{2}&\doteq&p_{2}\{C_{n+1}/x_{n+1}\}\{\widetilde{C}/\widetilde{X}\}\\
\ldots\\
C_{n}&\doteq&p_{n}\{C_{n+1}/x_{n+1}\}\{\widetilde{C}/\widetilde{X}\}\\
\end{array}

so that now we can use induction on
\widetilde{X}
and

(p_{1}\{C_{n+1}/x_{n+1}\},\ldots,p_{n}\{C_{n+1}/x_{n+1}\})
, to conclude that
the tuple
\widetilde{C}=(C_{1},C_{2},\ldots,C_{n})
of closed constants
is such that for
i=1,\ldots,n
:

W_{g}\vdash C_{i}=(p_{i}\{C_{n+1}/x_{n+1}\})\{\widetilde{C}/\widetilde{X}\}=p_{i}\{\widetilde{C}/\widetilde{X},C_{n+1}\{\widetilde{C}/\widetilde{X}\}/x_{n+1}\}
.

Note that above by
C_{n+1}\{\widetilde{C}/\widetilde{X}\}
we have implicitly closed the definition of

C_{n+1}
as

C_{n+1}\doteq p_{n+1}\{C_{n+1}/x_{n+1}\}\{\widetilde{C}/\widetilde{X}\}=p_{n+1}\{\widetilde{C}/\widetilde{X}\}\{C_{n+1}/x_{n+1}\}
,

so that
W_{g}\vdash C_{n+1}=p_{n+1}\{\widetilde{C}/\widetilde{X}\}\{C_{n+1}/x_{n+1}\}

by axiom
R1
.

Unicity of the tuple
(\widetilde{C},C_{n+1})
can be proved by using axiom
R2
.
Assume to have another solution tuple
(\widetilde{q},q_{n+1})
. This means that, for
i=1,\ldots,n+1
,

W_{g}\vdash q_{i}=p_{i}\{\widetilde{q}/\widetilde{X},q_{n+1}/x_{n+1}\}
.

By induction, we can assume, for
i=1,\ldots,n
, that
W_{g}\vdash C_{i}=q_{i}
.

Since
W_{g}\vdash C_{n+1}=p_{n+1}\{\widetilde{C}/\widetilde{X}\}\{C_{n+1}/x_{n+1}\}

by axiom
R1
,
by substitutivity we get
W_{g}\vdash C_{n+1}=p_{n+1}\{\widetilde{q}/\widetilde{X}\}\{C_{n+1}/x_{n+1}\}
.

Note that
p_{n+1}\{\widetilde{q}/\widetilde{X}\}
is a term open on
x_{n+1}
which is observationally guarded.
Let
F
be a constant defined as follows:
F\doteq p_{n+1}\{\widetilde{q}/\widetilde{X}\}\{F/x_{n+1}\}
.
Then, by axiom
R2
,
C_{n+1}=F
.
Hence, since

W_{g}\vdash q_{n+1}=p_{n+1}\{\widetilde{q}/\widetilde{X}\}\{q_{n+1}/x_{n+1}\}

by axiom
R2
, we get
W_{g}\vdash F=q_{n+1}
; and so the thesis

W_{g}\vdash C_{n+1}=q_{n+1}
follows by transitivity.

\Box

A system of equations is
\epsilon
-free if there are no occurrences of the prefix
\epsilon
. Formally, an
\epsilon
-free
normal form must be of the form

\begin{array}[]{lcllcllcl}C_{1}&\doteq&\sum_{i=1}^{m(1)}a_{1i}.C_{f(1,i)}\{+\mbox{\bf 1}\}\\
C_{2}&\doteq&\sum_{i=1}^{m(2)}a_{2i}.C_{f(2,i)}\{+\mbox{\bf 1}\}\\
\ldots\\
C_{n}&\doteq&\sum_{i=1}^{m(n)}a_{ni}.C_{f(n,i)}\{+\mbox{\bf 1}\}\\
\end{array}

where all the actions
a_{ij}\in A
.

We want to show that an observationally guarded normal form can be equated to an
\epsilon
-free normal form
by using the axioms in
W_{g}
, in particular
T3
. In fact, assume that

C_{h}\doteq\sum_{i=1}^{m(h)}a_{hi}.C_{f(h,i)}+\sum_{i=1}^{m^{\prime}(h)}\epsilon.C_{g(h,i)}\{+\mbox{\bf 1}\}

and that
C_{g(h,i)}\doteq p_{g(h,i)}
. Then, by
R1, T3
and substitutivity, we have that

W_{g}\vdash C_{h}=\sum_{i=1}^{m(h)}a_{hi}.C_{f(h,i)}+\sum_{i=1}^{m^{\prime}(h)}p_{g(h,i)}\{+\mbox{\bf 1}\}
.

Of course, in
p_{g(h,i)}
there may be still occurrences of
\epsilon
, but, since the system of equations is observationally guarded, we
may proceed as above starting from the constant with no prefix
\epsilon
and then going backward till reaching the initial constant
C_{1}
,
as illustrated in the following example.

Example 6

Consider the following observationally guarded normal form:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}C_{1}&\doteq&a.C_{1}+\epsilon.C_{2}+\mbox{\bf 1}\\
C_{2}&\doteq&b.C_{2}+\epsilon.C_{3}\\
C_{3}&\doteq&a.C_{3}+\mbox{\bf 1}\\
\end{array}

It is easily seen that
W_{g}\vdash C_{3}=a.C_{3}+\mbox{\bf 1}
,

W_{g}\vdash C_{2}=b.C_{2}+a.C_{3}+\mbox{\bf 1}
and so also
W_{g}\vdash C_{1}=a.C_{1}+b.C_{2}+a.C_{3}+\mbox{\bf 1}
.
Therefore, we can define a new
\epsilon
-free system of equations:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}D_{1}&\doteq&a.D_{1}+b.D_{2}+a.D_{3}+\mbox{\bf 1}\\
D_{2}&\doteq&b.D_{2}+a.D_{3}+\mbox{\bf 1}\\
D_{3}&\doteq&a.D_{3}+\mbox{\bf 1}\\
\end{array}

Finally, by Theorem
5
, we get
W_{g}\vdash C_{i}=D_{i}
for
i=1,2,3
.

\Box

Similarly, also that

W_{g}\vdash D_{i}=q_{i}\{C_{n+1}/x_{n+1}\}\{\widetilde{D}/\widetilde{X}\}
for
i=1,\ldots,n
. By substitutivity, we also get

W_{g}\vdash D_{i}=q_{i}\{D_{n+1}/x_{n+1}\}\{\widetilde{D}/\widetilde{X}\}
for
i=1,\ldots,n
.
Finally,
we define a new
\epsilon
-free system of equations in normal form

\begin{array}[]{lcllcllcl}E_{1}&\doteq&q_{1}\{E_{n+1}/x_{n+1}\}\{\widetilde{E}/\widetilde{X}\}\\
E_{2}&\doteq&q_{2}\{E_{n+1}/x_{n+1}\}\{\widetilde{E}/\widetilde{X}\}\\
\ldots\\
E_{n}&\doteq&q_{n}\{E_{n+1}/x_{n+1}\}\{\widetilde{E}/\widetilde{X}\}\\
E_{n+1}&\doteq&q_{n+1}\{E_{n+1}/x_{n+1}\}\{\widetilde{E}/\widetilde{X}\}\end{array}

such that
W_{g}\vdash D_{i}=E_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,n+1
by Theorem
5
. The thesis,
W_{g}\vdash C_{i}=E_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,n+1
,
follows by transitivity.

\Box

7.3

Deterministic Normal Form

Given an alphabet
A=\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}\}
, we say that an
\epsilon
-free normal form is deterministic if it is of the form

\begin{array}[]{lcllcllcl}C_{1}&\doteq&\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}.C_{f(1,i)}\{+\mbox{\bf 1}\}\\
C_{2}&\doteq&\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}.C_{f(2,i)}\{+\mbox{\bf 1}\}\\
\ldots\\
C_{n}&\doteq&\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}.C_{f(n,i)}\{+\mbox{\bf 1}\}\\
\end{array}

i.e., the body of each constant contains exactly one summand for each action
a_{i}\in A
. Of course,
the NFA associated to
C_{1}
, according to the denotational semantics in Table
2
, is deterministic
and has exactly
A
as its alphabet.

Note that the concept of determinism heavily depends on the chosen alphabet.
For instance, according to the denotational semantics in Table
2
, the NFA for
C\doteq\mbox{\bf 0}
is deterministic, because its alphabet is empty.
However, it is not deterministic if we consider
a nonempty alphabet, say
A=\{a\}
,
because there is not an arc for
a
. In such a case, a DFA w.r.t.
A
, language equivalent to
C\doteq\mbox{\bf 0}
, could be the one associated to

E\doteq a.E
. Indeed, it is possible to prove that
W_{g}\vdash C=E
by means of axioms
T1, R1, R2
; in fact,
by axiom
T1
, we have
W_{g}\vdash\mbox{\bf 0}=a.\mbox{\bf 0}
; then, by axiom
R2
, from
E\doteq a.E
and

W_{g}\vdash\mbox{\bf 0}=a.\mbox{\bf 0}
, we get
W_{g}\vdash E=\mbox{\bf 0}
; then by axiom
R1
, we have
W_{g}\vdash C=\mbox{\bf 0}
, so that

W_{g}\vdash C=E
follows by transitivity.

Similarly, the NFA for
C\doteq\mbox{\bf 1}
is deterministic, because its alphabet is empty.
However, it is not deterministic if we consider
the nonempty alphabet
A=\{a\}
. In such a case, a DFA w.r.t.
A
, language equivalent to
C\doteq\mbox{\bf 1}
, could be the one associated to

D\doteq a.E+\mbox{\bf 1}
, where
E\doteq a.E
denotes a sink, i.e., an error state. We can prove that
W_{g}\vdash C=D
as follows.
First, we already know that
W_{g}\vdash E=\mbox{\bf 0}
; so, by substitutivity and
R1
, we have
W_{g}\vdash D=a.\mbox{\bf 0}+\mbox{\bf 1}
; then by
T1
,

W_{g}\vdash D=\mbox{\bf 0}+\mbox{\bf 1}
; then by axioms
A2,A3
, we have
W_{g}\vdash D=\mbox{\bf 1}
, so that
W_{g}\vdash C=D
follows easily.

Now we prove that, given an
\epsilon
-free normal form, it is possible to construct a provably equivalent deterministic normal form.
The idea behind the proof, that follows the classic Rabin-Scott subset construction
[
25
]
, is illustrated by the following example.

Example 7

Let us consider the following
\epsilon
-free normal form

\begin{array}[]{rcl}C_{1}&\doteq&a.C_{1}+a.C_{2}+a.C_{1}\\
C_{2}&\doteq&a.C_{2}+\mbox{\bf 1}\\
\end{array}

whose alphabet
A_{C}
is
\{a\}
. Let us take an alphabet
A=\{a,b\}
, w.r.t. which we want to define an equivalent
deterministic normal form. First, we define a collection of set-indexed
constants
B_{I}
, where
I\subseteq\{1,2\}
, as
B_{I}\doteq\sum_{i\in I}body(C_{i})
. More explicitly,

\begin{array}[]{rcl}B_{\{1\}}&\doteq&a.C_{1}+a.C_{2}+a.C_{1}\\
B_{\{2\}}&\doteq&a.C_{2}+\mbox{\bf 1}\\
B_{\{1,2\}}&\doteq&a.C_{1}+a.C_{2}+a.C_{1}+a.C_{2}+\mbox{\bf 1}\\
B_{\emptyset}&\doteq&\mbox{\bf 0}\\
\end{array}

Now, for each of them, we prove that it can be equated to a deterministic definition. First, we can prove that,
since
B_{\emptyset}=\mbox{\bf 0}
by axiom
R1
, also

W_{g}\vdash B_{\emptyset}=a.B_{\emptyset}+b.B_{\emptyset}
by axioms
T1,A3
.

Now, by axiom
R1
, we know that
W_{g}\vdash B_{\{1\}}=a.C_{1}+a.C_{2}+a.C_{1}
, so
that
W_{g}\vdash C_{1}=B_{\{1\}}
. Then, by axioms
A1-A4,T2,R1
,
we can derive
W_{g}\vdash B_{\{1\}}=a.(body(C_{1})+body(C_{2}))
. Then, we can also derive

W_{g}\vdash B_{\{1\}}=a.B_{\{1,2\}}+b.B_{\emptyset}
by axioms
R1,T1,A3
.

In the same way, we can prove that
W_{g}\vdash B_{\{2\}}=a.B_{\{2\}}+b.B_{\emptyset}+\mbox{\bf 1}
and that

W_{g}\vdash B_{\{1,2\}}=a.B_{\{1,2\}}+b.B_{\emptyset}+\mbox{\bf 1}
.

Finally, we define a new deterministic system of equations

\begin{array}[]{rcl}D_{\{1\}}&\doteq&a.D_{\{1,2\}}+b.D_{\emptyset}\\
D_{\{2\}}&\doteq&a.D_{\{2\}}+b.D_{\emptyset}+\mbox{\bf 1}\\
D_{\{1,2\}}&\doteq&a.D_{\{1,2\}}+b.D_{\emptyset}+\mbox{\bf 1}\\
D_{\emptyset}&\doteq&a.D_{\emptyset}+b.D_{\emptyset}\\
\end{array}

where
D_{\{2\}}
is redundant as
D_{\{2\}}\not\in\mbox{{\it Const}$(D_{\{1\}})$}
, such that, by Theorem
5
, we have
W_{g}\vdash B_{I}=D_{I}
for
I=\{1\},\{2\},\{1,2\},\emptyset
.
So,
W_{g}\vdash C_{1}=D_{\{1\}}
by transitivity.

\Box

Proposition 10

(Reduction to deterministic normal form)

Given the
\epsilon
-free normal form

\begin{array}[]{lcllcllcl}C_{1}&\doteq&\sum_{i=1}^{m(1)}a_{1i}.C_{f(1,i)}\{+\mbox{\bf 1}\}\\
C_{2}&\doteq&\sum_{i=1}^{m(2)}a_{2i}.C_{f(2,i)}\{+\mbox{\bf 1}\}\\
\ldots\\
C_{n}&\doteq&\sum_{i=1}^{m(n)}a_{ni}.C_{f(n,i)}\{+\mbox{\bf 1}\}\\
\end{array}

such that its alphabet is
A_{C}
,
it is possible to construct a deterministic normal form w.r.t. a chosen
A=\{a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{k}\}
,
with
A_{C}\subseteq A
, of the form

\begin{array}[]{lcllcllcl}D_{1}&\doteq&\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}.D_{g(1,i)}\{+\mbox{\bf 1}\}\\
D_{2}&\doteq&\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}.D_{g(2,i)}\{+\mbox{\bf 1}\}\\
\ldots\\
D_{m}&\doteq&\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}.D_{g(m,i)}\{+\mbox{\bf 1}\}\\
\end{array}

where
m\geq n
, such that
W_{g}\vdash C_{1}=D_{1}
.

Proof 17

We define
a set of set-indexed constants
B_{I}\doteq\sum_{i\in I}body(C_{i})
, one for each
I\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}
.
Note that
W_{g}\vdash C_{1}=B_{\{1\}}
by axiom
R1
.
Now for each of these constants
B_{I}
we want to show that it can be equated to a deterministic definition, i.e., a definition
where there is exactly one summand per symbol in
A
. First, let
In(B_{I})
denote the set of initial symbols of
B_{I}
, i.e.,

\cup_{i\in I}\{a_{i\,1},\ldots,a_{i\,m(i)}\}
. Then,
by using the axioms
A1-A4,R1,T2
and substitutivity, we can prove that

W_{g}\vdash B_{I}=\sum_{a_{h}\in In(B_{I})}a_{h}.(\sum_{i\in I_{h}}body(C_{i}))
,

where
I_{h}=\{f(i,j)\;\;\big|\;\;a_{i,j}=a_{h},a_{i,j}\in In(B_{I})\}
.
The next step is to recognize that
\sum_{i\in I_{h}}body(C_{i})
can be replaced by
B_{I_{h}}
by axiom
R1
, so that

W_{g}\vdash B_{I}=\sum_{a_{h}\in In(B_{I})}a_{h}.B_{I_{h}}
.

The next step is to add summands related to those symbols in
A
not in
In(B_{I})
. First note that
B_{\emptyset}\doteq\mbox{\bf 0}

can be equated, by means of axioms
R1,T1,A3
, to

W_{g}\vdash B_{\emptyset}=\sum_{a_{i}\in A}a_{i}.B_{\emptyset}
,

which is a deterministic definition for the error state
B_{\emptyset}
. Therefore, by axioms
T1,A3
we have

W_{g}\vdash B_{I}=\sum_{a_{h}\in In(B_{I})}a_{h}.B_{I_{h}}+\sum_{a_{h}\not\in In(B_{I})}a_{h}.B_{\emptyset}

which is a deterministic definition for constant
B_{I}
. Finally, we can define a deterministic system of equations
of the form

D_{I}\doteq\sum_{a_{h}\in In(B_{I})}a_{h}.D_{I_{h}}+\sum_{a_{h}\not\in In(B_{I})}a_{h}.D_{\emptyset}

for each
I\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}
, such that, by Theorem
5
, we have
W_{g}\vdash B_{I}=D_{I}
.
So,
W_{g}\vdash C_{1}=D_{\{1\}}
by transitivity.

\Box

Theorem 7

(Completeness of

over the whole of SFM1)

For every

, if

,

.

Proof 21

By Proposition

, there exists
p^{\prime}
and

Conclusion and Future Research

In this paper, we have shown how to use the process algebra SFM1, that truly represents NFAs up to isomorphism,
in order to find a sound and complete axiomatization of
language equivalence, and so also a sound and complete, finite axiomatization of regular languages, which is alternative
to those based on the Kleene algebra of regular expressions
[
27
,
7
,
18
]
. As a matter of fact,
our axiomatization, composed of 7 axioms and 3 conditional axioms, seems more concise than these.
On the one hand, the classic axiomatization
by Kozen
[
18
]
is composed of 11 axioms for disjunction and concatenation, and 4 axioms for iteration
(two of which are conditional). However, this is not the so far
optimal axiomatization of language equivalence with Kleene algebras; in fact,

[
19
]
showed that one of Kozen’s conditional axioms can
be removed at the price of adding a few equational axioms, and then
[
9
]
improved this result
by showing that language equivalence can be axiomatized by a (left-handed) Kleene algebra defined by 13 axioms,
only one of which is conditional.
On the other hand, it should be remarked that our axiomatization makes use of axiom schemata (in particular,
for recursion), while
[
18
,
9
]
do not.

NFAs without
\epsilon
-labeled transitions have been investigated by Alexandra Silva in her Ph.D. thesis
[
29
]
,
using co-algebraic techniques
[
26
]
. In chapter 5, based on a joint work with Bonsangue and Rutten
[
30
]
,
she considers a (co-)algebra to describe such NFAs that, similarly to SFM1, features an action prefixing operator
(but the prefix
\epsilon
is not considered)
instead of concatenation, and recursion (implemented by means of the standard fixpoint operator of
[
20
]
) instead of Kleene star, but this algebra also
contains a few additional operators. For this (co-)algebra, she proved a sort of representability theorem, up to language equivalence.
Moreover,
she defines an axiomatization of language equivalence that, for the syntactic peculiarities of her (co-)algebra, turns out to be rather
verbose, as it is composed
of 18 axioms (while our axiomatization for SFM1 without the prefix
\epsilon
, is composed of 8 axioms only,
as
T3
and
R3
are not necessary,
and, moreover,
R2
can be replaced by the simpler
R2
′
).

NFAs without
\epsilon
-labeled transitions are also the model of another process algebra, called
BSP_{gfrec}(A)
in
[
4
]
(cf. Corollary 5.8.3),
an algebra rather similar to SFM1 (without the prefix
\epsilon
).
However, the axiomatic theory presented there is only for bisimulation equivalence and, instead of using axiom
R2
, it resorts to some recursion principles,
in particular RSP (recursive specification principle, that ensures that each recursive specification has at most one solution), widely promoted in the ACP/BPA community.

Another goal of this paper is to show the usefulness
of representability theorems, up to isomorphism (like Theorem
2
), for the algebraic
study of semantic models, like NFAs. In fact, SFM1 has allowed us to study also bisimulation equivalence on NFAs and to
hint a sound and complete
axiomatization for it (the set
B
), composed of 4 axioms and 2 conditional axioms, only.
Future research may be devoted to extend the axiomatic theory of SFM1 to other behavioral equivalences in
the linear-time/branching-time
spectrum
[
10
,
11
]
. For instance, we claim that weak simulation equivalence
[
24
,
1
]
can be axiomatized by
replacing axiom
T2
in the set
W
with the axiom

T2
′

a.(x+y)=a.(x+y)+a.y
.

Interestingly enough, we have proposed similar representability theorems
about various classes of Petri nets in
[
12
]
,
offering for each class a specific process algebra truly representing the models of that class, up to isomorphism.
In
[
13
,
14
]
one of
these results has been exploited for axiomatizing bisimulation-based, behavioral equivalences over a small
class of finite Petri nets.

In the literature there are other process algebras that describe regular behaviors, the most prominent one being BPA
∗

(see, e.g.,
[
5
,
3
,
11
]
), which corresponds syntactically to the definition of regular expressions.
The abstract syntax of BPA
∗
is

p\;::=\;\mbox{\bf 0}\;\;\big|\;\;\mbox{\bf 1}\;\;\big|\;\;\alpha\;\;\big|\;\;p+p\;\;\big|\;\;p\cdot p\;\;\big|\;\;p^{*}

where

p_{1}\cdot p_{2}
is the operator of sequential composition,
and
p^{*}
is the iteration operator.
The semantic models used to give semantics to this process algebra are NFAs.
For instance, the semantics of
(a\cdot b)^{*}+a\cdot(b\cdot a)^{*}

is isomorphic to the NFA outlined in Figure
6
(b).
Note that the language recognized by
the NFA associated to the BPA
∗
process
(a\cdot b)^{*}+a\cdot(b\cdot a)^{*}
is exactly
the language associated to the regular expression
(a\cdot b)^{*}+a\cdot(b\cdot a)^{*}
.

Interestingly enough, this process algebra is strictly less expressive than SFM1.
In fact, Milner already observed in
[
20
]
that there are NFAs
that are not bisimulation equivalent to any process of BPA
∗
: for instance, the NFA in Figure
6
(a),
which instead can be represented in SFM1, up to isomorphism (hence, also up to bisimilarity), simply by the system of equations

\begin{array}[]{rcl}C_{0}&\doteq&a.C_{1}+\mbox{\bf 1}\\
C_{1}&\doteq&b.C_{0}+\mbox{\bf 1}\\
\end{array}

However, BPA
∗
can represent all the regular languages:
given a regular language
L
, there exists a BPA
∗
process
p
such that its associated NFA
recognizes
L
. For instance,
a BPA
∗
process, which is language equivalent to the one in Figure
6
(a), is actually

(a\cdot b)^{*}+a\cdot(b\cdot a)^{*}
, whose associated semantics is isomorphic to the NFA in Figure
6
(b).
This is not very surprising, as we already noted in the introduction (cf. Figure
1
) that regular expressions are a formalism that can
represent all the NFAs, but only up to language equivalence.
An interesting study about the subclass of NFAs that are actually expressible, up to bisimilarity,
with BPA
∗
is described in
[

]
