The Geometry of Rank Drop in a Class of Face-Splitting Matrix Products: Part I
Erin Connelly, Sameer Agarwal, Alperen Ergur, Rekha R. Thomas

Abstract
. Given k k points ( x i , y i ) ∈ ℙ 2 × ℙ 2 (x_{i},y_{i})\in\mathbb{P}^{2}\times\mathbb{P}^{2} , we characterize rank deficiency of the k × 9 k\times 9 matrix Z k Z_{k} with rows x i ⊤ ⊗ y i ⊤ x_{i}^{\top}\otimes y_{i}^{\top} in terms of the geometry of the point configurations { x i } \{x_{i}\} and { y i } \{y_{i}\} . In this paper we present results for k ≤ 6 k\leq 6 . For k ≤ 5 k\leq 5 , the geometry of the rank-drop locus is characterized by cross-ratios and basic (projective) geometry of point configurations. For the case k = 6 k=6 the rank-drop locus is captured by the classical theory of cubic surfaces. The results for k = 7 , 8 k=7,8 and 9 9 are presented in the sequel to this paper [ 7 ] .

A first answer to

\{y_{i}\}

To this end, we begin by observing that the rank of

\displaystyle Z^{\prime}_{k}=\begin{bmatrix}(H_{1}x_{1})^{\top}\otimes(H_{2}y_{1})^{\top}\\

(H_{1}x_{k})^{\top}\otimes(H_{2}y_{k})^{\top}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}(x_{1}^{\top}\otimes y_{1}^{\top})(H^{\top}_{1}\otimes H^{\top}_{2})\\
\vdots\\
(x_{k}^{\top}\otimes y_{k}^{\top})(H^{\top}_{1}\otimes H^{\top}_{2})\end{bmatrix}=Z_{k}(H^{\top}_{1}\otimes H^{\top}_{2}).

The matrix

Preprocessing the input

We begin by showing that one can make simplifying assumptions on the input to Problem

(x_{i},y_{i})\in\mathbb{P}^{2}\times\mathbb{P}^{2}

We sometimes refer to

as a

and

separately. It will also be

\mathbb{P}^{2}
containing

as
\mathbb{P}^{2}_{x}
and the
\mathbb{P}^{2}
containing
\{y_{i}\}
as
\mathbb{P}^{2}_{y}

\textup{PGL}(3)
be the group of invertible

\mathbb{C}
up to scale.
An element

induces a homography (projective isomorphism) on

In the introduction we informally proved that the rank of

is invariant under
\textup{PGL}(3)\times\textup{PGL}(3)
acting on
\mathbb{P}^{2}_{x}\times\mathbb{P}^{2}_{y}
by left multiplication. This allows us to change bases in
\mathbb{P}^{2}_{x}
and
\mathbb{P}^{2}_{y}
independently. We state the result formally below:

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
(x_{i},y_{i})\in\mathbb{P}^{2}\times\mathbb{P}^{2}
for
i=1,\ldots,k
and let
H_{1},H_{2}\in\textup{PGL}(3)
be homographies acting on
\mathbb{P}_{x}^{2}
and
\mathbb{P}^{2}_{y}
respectively (i.e.,
(x,y)\mapsto(H_{1}x,H_{2}y)
). Then
Z_{k}=\left(x^{\top}_{i}\otimes y^{\top}_{i}\right)_{i=1}^{k}
has the same rank as
Z^{\prime}_{k}=\left((H_{1}x_{i})^{\top}\otimes(H_{2}y_{i})^{\top}\right)_{i=1}^{k}
.

Lemma
2.1
allows us to make certain assumptions
on the configurations
\{x_{i}\}
and
\{y_{i}\}
which we phrase as corollaries to the lemma.
While our proofs do not need these assumptions, they become critical for computations in Macaulay2.
The first assumption is that the
x_{i}
’s and

y_{i}
’s in the input
to Problem
1.3
are finite points. With a view towards computations, in the following lemma and in several parts of the paper, we fix representatives of points in
\mathbb{P}^{2}
and write them out as vectors in
\mathbb{C}^{3}
.

Corollary 2.2
.

Without loss of generality, we can assume that the input to Problem
1.3

is of the form
x_{i}=(x_{i1},x_{i2},1)^{\top}
and

y_{i}=(y_{i1},y_{i2},1)^{\top}
for all
i=1,\ldots,k
, where

x_{i1},x_{i2},y_{i1},y_{i2}\in\mathbb{C}
. Hence

(3)

\displaystyle x_{i}^{\top}\otimes y_{i}^{\top}=(x_{i1}y_{i1},x_{i1}y_{i2},x_{i1},x_{i2}y_{i1},x_{i2}y_{i2},x_{i2},y_{i1},y_{i2},1).

Proof.

Let
x_{i}\in\mathbb{P}^{2}_{x}
and
y_{i}\in\mathbb{P}^{2}_{y}
be
k
pairs of points where

is finite, and suppose that some of these points lie on the line at infinity in their
\mathbb{P}^{2}

\ell
, common to both
\mathbb{P}^{2}
,
which contains none of these points. Let
H
be a homography that sends

\ell
to the line at infinity in both
\mathbb{P}^{2}
. Then
Hx_{i}
and
Hy_{i}
are finite points for all

. By Lemma

, the rank of
Z_{k}=(x_{i}^{\top}\otimes y_{i}^{\top})_{i=1}^{k}
equals the rank of

Z^{\prime}_{k}=((Hx_{i})^{\top}\otimes(Hy_{i})^{\top})_{i=1}^{k}
and hence we can work with the transformed points to understand
the rank deficiency of
Z_{k}
.
∎

The other assumption we can make is that sufficiently generic configurations
allow some of the points to be fixed, which will prove useful for computations.

Corollary 2.3
.

When no three of the
x_{i}
’s or
y_{i}
’s are collinear in their
respective
\mathbb{P}^{2}
, we may
fix the first four in each set to be:

(4)

\displaystyle\begin{split}x_{1}=y_{1}=(0,0,1),\,\,\,x_{2}=y_{2}=(1,0,1),\\
x_{3}=y_{3}=(0,1,1),\,\,\,x_{4}=y_{4}=(1,1,1).\end{split}

At certain times, for the sake of symmetry, we will instead fix the first four points as:

(5)

\displaystyle\begin{split}x_{1}=y_{1}=(1,0,0),\,\,\,x_{2}=y_{2}=(0,1,0),\\
x_{3}=y_{3}=(0,0,1),\,\,\,x_{4}=y_{4}=(1,1,1).\end{split}

Since the two fixings are related by a homography, results

Cross-ratios and brackets

By Lemma

is invariant under the action of
\textup{PGL(3)}\times\textup{PGL}(3)
on
\mathbb{P}^{2}\times\mathbb{P}^{2}

The intrinsic geometry of

are the properties of the configuration that remain intact/invariant under the action of

Since an element of

can be assumed to have determinant

is precisely the invariant properties, under the action of

, of the configuration
obtained by replacing each

with a representative in
\mathbb{C}^{3}
.

Let
\mathbb{C}[{\bf p}]:=\mathbb{C}[p_{i1},p_{i2},p_{i3},i=1,\ldots,k]
be the polynomial ring in the variables

for
i=1,\ldots,k

k

p_{1},\ldots,p_{k}
in
\mathbb{P}^{2}
written via representatives in
\mathbb{C}^{3}

is

under
\textup{SL}(3)
if
f(p_{1},\ldots,p_{k})=f(Hp_{1},\ldots,Hp_{k})
for all

It is a well-known classical result that the invariant ring
\mathbb{C}[{\bf p}]^{\textup{SL}(3)}
, which is the collection of all polynomials in
\mathbb{C}[{\bf p}]
that are invariant under
\textup{SL}(3)

p_{1}^{\top},\ldots,p_{k}^{\top}

\mathbb{C}[{\bf p}]^{\textup{SL}(3)}
is generated as an algebra by the
bracket polynomials

(6)

[ijl]:=\det\begin{bmatrix}p_{i1}&p_{i2}&p_{i3}\\
p_{j1}&p_{j2}&p_{j3}\\
p_{l1}&p_{l2}&p_{l3}\end{bmatrix}

for all distinct choices of
i,j,l\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
.
In our situation, we will write
[ijl]_{x}
(respectively,
[ijl]_{y}
) when the
input points come from
\{x_{i}\}
(respectively,
\{y_{i}\}
). Equations in brackets can be used to express geometry. For example,
three points
p_{i},p_{j},p_{l}\in\mathbb{P}^{2}
are collinear if and only if

[ijl]=0
, and
6
points lie on a conic if and only if they satisfy the bracket equation (

) in Lemma
3.5
.

Often we consider points in a line
\ell
in
\mathbb{P}^{2}
as points in
\mathbb{P}^{1}

6.2.
Geometric Characterizations of Rank Deficiency

We next explain where the algebraic results (and ideals) from the previous section come from by providing a geometric characterization of rank deficiency of
Z_{6}
. This relies on
the classical theory of smooth cubic surfaces in
\mathbb{P}^{3}
. For the convenience of the reader, we have collected the needed facts about cubic surfaces in Appendix
A
.

6.2.1.
Cubic surfaces and Schäfli double sixes

A cubic surface in
\mathbb{P}^{3}
is a hypersurface defined by a single homogenous polynomial of degree
3
in
\mathbb{C}[z_{0},z_{1},z_{2},z_{3}]
where
(z_{0},\ldots,z_{3})
are the coordinates of
\mathbb{P}^{3}
.

Definition 6.9
.

A pair of 6 lines
\{\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{6}\}
and

\{\ell_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,\ell_{6}^{\prime}\}
in
\mathbb{P}^{3}
is called a
Schläfli double six
if

(1)

the
6
lines in each set are mutually skew (i.e., they don’t intersect pairwise), and

(2)

\ell_{i}
intersects
\ell_{j}^{\prime}
if and only if
i\neq j
.

A smooth cubic surface has
36
Schläfli double sixes.
The rich and highly symmetric properties of these line configurations are listed in Appendix
A
and lie at the heart of all of our major results in this section.
Our main (geometric) theorem in this section is the following.

Theorem 6.10
.

Suppose
\{(x_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{6}\subset\mathbb{P}^{2}\times\mathbb{P}^{2}
is in general position. Then
Z_{6}
is
rank deficient if and only if there exists a cubic surface
\mathcal{S}
that can be obtained as the
blow up of both
\mathbb{P}^{2}_{x}
in
x_{1},\ldots,x_{6}
and
\mathbb{P}^{2}_{y}
in

y_{1},\ldots,y_{6}
such that their respective
exceptional curves

\{\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{6}\},\{\ell_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,\ell_{6}^{\prime}\}

form a Schläfli double six on
\mathcal{S}
.
When
Z_{6}
is rank deficient, this cubic surface has the determinantal representation
\mathcal{N}(Z_{6})\cap\mathcal{D}
, where
\mathcal{N}(Z_{6})
denotes the right null space of
Z_{6}
and
\mathcal{D}
is the cubic hypersurface of all rank deficient
3\times 3
matrices.

To prepare for the proof of this theorem, we begin by constructing a cubic surface from
5
points
\{(x_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{5}
in general position.
Recall that we can identify a point in
\mathbb{P}^{8}
with a
3\times 3
matrix
M
via its vectorization

\textup{vec}(M)
. Under this identification,
\mathcal{D}\subset\mathbb{P}^{8}
is the cubic hypersurface
\mathcal{D}=\{M\,:\,\det(M)=0\}
.
Let
\mathcal{N}(Z_{5})\subset\mathbb{P}^{8}
be the null space of
Z_{5}
. Since
\{(x_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{5}
is in general position,
\operatorname{rank}(Z_{5})=5
and so,
\mathcal{N}(Z_{5})\cong\mathbb{P}^{3}
. Therefore,

(49)

\displaystyle S:=\mathcal{N}(Z_{5})\cap\mathcal{D}

is a cubic surface in

\mathcal{N}(Z_{5})\cong\mathbb{P}^{3}\subset\mathbb{P}^{8}
. This surface is smooth by the general position assumption. (We note that if
\{x_{i}\}
and
\{y_{i}\}
were related by a homography then

would be degenerate (see Example

The surface

has a natural
determinantal representation
(see Section
A
for the definition): Pick a basis
M_{0},\ldots,M_{3}
of
\mathcal{N}(Z_{5})
and set

(50)

\displaystyle M(z)=z_{0}M_{0}+\cdots+z_{3}M_{3}

where

Since

is a parameterization of
\mathcal{N}(Z_{5})

is cut out by
\det(M(z))=0

is a determinantal representation of

Generically, the

of rank one matrices, and so a point

Next we construct a Schläfli double six on the cubic surface

We now note several facts about double sixes in
\mathbb{P}^{3}

Lemma A.2
.

(1)

Each double six in
\mathbb{P}^{3}
is contained in a unique non-singular cubic surface and thus forms part of the set of
27

(2)

Any set of

Next we talk about determinantal representations of a cubic surface.

Definition A.3
.

A
determinantal representation
of a cubic surface
\mathcal{S}
defined by a cubic equation
F(z)=F(z_{0},z_{1},z_{2},z_{3})=0
is a
3\times 3
matrix of linear forms

(79)

\displaystyle M(z)=M(z_{0},z_{1},z_{2},z_{3})=z_{0}M_{0}+z_{1}M_{1}+z_{2}M_{2}+z_{3}M_{3}

such that
\det(M(z))=\lambda F(z)
for some
\lambda\neq 0
. Two representations
M(z)
and
M^{\prime}(z)
are equivalent if
M^{\prime}(z)=AM(z)B
for
A,B\in\textup{GL}(3)
.

Note that if
M(z)
is a determinantal representation of
\mathcal{S}
, then so is
M(z)^{\top}
.
We present the following facts about determinantal representations of a cubic surface

[
4
]
.

Lemma A.4
.

(1)

Every smooth cubic surface
\mathcal{S}
has a determinantal representation.

(2)

The equivalence classes of determinantal representations of
\mathcal{S}
are in bijection with sets of
6
mutually skew lines on
\mathcal{S}
that are the
exceptional curves of the blow up of
\mathbb{P}^{2}
at the
6
points that gives
\mathcal{S}
.

(3)

If the determinantal representation
M
of
\mathcal{S}
corresponds to the skew lines
\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{6}
on
\mathcal{S}
, then
M^{\top}
corresponds to another
6
skew lines

\ell_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,\ell_{6}^{\prime}
on
\mathcal{S}
such that the pair of
6
lines form a Schläfli double six on
\mathcal{S}
. Thus there are

72
equivalence classes of determinantal representations of
\mathcal{S}
one for each half of the
36
Schläfli double sixes on
\mathcal{S}
.

(4)

The choice of a Schläfli double six
\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{6},\ell_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,\ell_{6}^{\prime}
on
\mathcal{S}
is equivalent to the choice of a pair of equivalence classes of determinantal representations of
\mathcal{S}
, namely
[M]
and
[M^{\top}]
. (A smooth cubic surface cannot have a symmetric determinantal representation and hence
[M]\neq[M^{\top}]
.)

Here are various recipes to explicitly realize some of the above assertions.

(1)

If
\mathcal{S}
is the blow up of
\mathbb{P}^{2}
at the
6

, then
\mathcal{S}
can be constructed as follows. Pick
a basis
\{f_{0},f_{1},f_{2},f_{3}\}
of the
4

and map

(80)

\displaystyle u\in\mathbb{P}^{2}\mapsto(f_{0}(u):f_{1}(u):f_{2}(u):f_{3}(u))\in\mathbb{P}^{3}.

(2)

The variety of
\langle f_{0},\ldots,f_{3}\rangle
is
\{p_{0},\ldots,p_{6}\}
. By the
Hilbert-Burch theorem

[
14
]
*Section 20.4 there is a
3\times 4
matrix
L(u)
of linear forms in

u_{0},u_{1},u_{2}
whose maximal minors are
f_{0},\ldots,f_{3}
. The matrix
L(u)
generically has
rank
3
and has rank
2
exactly when
u=p_{i}
for some
i=1,\ldots,6
. Define a
3\times 3
matrix

M(z)
of linear forms in
z_{0},z_{1},z_{2},z_{3}
by the relation

(81)

\displaystyle M(z)\cdot\begin{pmatrix}u_{0}\\
u_{1}\\
u_{2}\end{pmatrix}=L(u)\cdot\begin{pmatrix}z_{0}\\
z_{1}\\
z_{2}\\
z_{3}\end{pmatrix}.

Then
M(z)
is a determinantal representation of
\mathcal{S}
.

(3)

The blow up of
\mathbb{P}^{2}
at
p_{1},\ldots,p_{6}
can be seen via (
81
).
If
\bar{u}\in\mathbb{P}^{2}
is not one of
p_{1},\ldots,p_{6}
, then some
f_{i}(\bar{u})\neq 0
and

\operatorname{rank}(L(\bar{u}))=3
. Therefore, the null space of
L(\bar{u})
is a unique point

\bar{z}\in\mathbb{P}^{3}
and
M(\bar{z})\bar{u}=L(\bar{u})\bar{z}=0
. This means that
\det(M(\bar{z}))=0
and
\bar{z}\in\mathcal{S}
.
If
\bar{z}
is a generic point on
\mathcal{S}
, then
\operatorname{rank}(M(\bar{z}))=2
, and the blow up morphism
\pi
sends
\bar{z}
to the unique point
\bar{u}\in\mathbb{P}^{2}
that generates the right null space of
M(\bar{z})
.

(4)

The
6
exceptional curves
of the blow up are precisely

(82)

\displaystyle\ell_{i}:=\{z\in\mathbb{P}^{3}\,:\,M(z)p_{i}=0\}\textup{ for }i=1,\ldots,6.

By what is stated above,

\operatorname{rank}(L(u))=2
if and only if
u=p_{i}
for some
i=1,\ldots,6
if and only if (via
(
81

M(z)u=L(u)z=0
is a system of
2
linearly independent equations in

z
(i.e., defines a line in
\mathbb{P}^{3}
). This line is entirely on
\mathcal{S}
since

\det(M(z))=0
if
M(z)p_{i}=0
.

(5)

Corresponding results hold for the
determinantal representation
M(z)^{\top}
of
\mathcal{S}
.

(a)

To obtain the points
q_{1},\ldots,q_{6}\in\mathbb{P}^{2}
whose blow up is
\mathcal{S}
, compute

L^{\prime}(u)
via

(83)

\displaystyle M(z)^{\top}u=L^{\prime}(u)z.

The variety of the maximal minors of

is

.

(b)

A point
\bar{y}\in\mathbb{P}^{2}
not equal to any

is sent to the unique point
\bar{z}
in

\mathbb{P}^{3}
in the right nullspace of

since

\det(M(\bar{z}))^{\top}=0
.

(c)

The morphism
\pi^{\prime}\,:\,\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\mathbb{P}^{2}
sends
\bar{z}
to the unique point

\bar{y}\in\mathbb{P}^{2}
that
generates the right nullspace of
M(\bar{z})^{\top}

M(\bar{z})
.

(d)

The exceptional curves of the blow up are the lines

\displaystyle\ell_{j}^{\prime}:=\{z\in\mathbb{P}^{3}\,:\,q_{j}^{\top}M(z)=0\}\textup{ for }j=1,\ldots,6.

The following theorem follows from the above facts.

Theorem A.5

Given a determinantal representation

of

(84)

\displaystyle\ell_{i}:=\{z\in\mathbb{P}^{3}\,:\,M(z)p_{i}=0\}\textup{ for }i=1,\ldots,6,

(85)
