Information geometric bound on general chemical reaction networks Preprint: APS/123-QED
Tsuyoshi Mizohata, Tetsuya J. Kobayashi, Louis-S. Bouchard, Hideyuki Miyahara

Abstract
We investigate the dynamics of chemical reaction networks (CRNs) with the goal of deriving an upper bound on their reaction rates. This task is challenging due to the nonlinear nature and discrete structure inherent in CRNs. To address this, we employ an information geometric approach, using the natural gradient, to develop a nonlinear system that yields an upper bound for CRN dynamics. We validate our approach through numerical simulations, demonstrating faster convergence in a specific class of CRNs. This class is characterized by the number of chemicals, the maximum value of stoichiometric coefficients of the chemical reactions, and the number of reactions. We also compare our method to a conventional approach, showing that the latter cannot provide an upper bound on reaction rates of CRNs. While our study focuses on CRNs, the ubiquity of hypergraphs in fields from natural sciences to engineering suggests that our method may find broader applications, including in information science.

II.1
Definition of CRNs

We begin with a hypergraph
(\mathbb{V},\mathbb{E})
where
\mathbb{V}\coloneqq\{\mathbb{v}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N_{\mathbb{v}}}
and
\mathbb{E}\coloneqq\{\mathbb{e}_{e}\}_{e=1}^{N_{\mathbb{e}}}
as a hypergraph provides a mathematical framework to describe a chemical reaction.
Suppose that a CRN of interest involves
N_{\mathbb{X}}
chemicals, denoted as
\mathbb{X}_{1},\mathbb{X}_{2},\dots,\mathbb{X}_{N_{\mathbb{X}}}
.
In the case of a CRN, each hypervertex
\mathbb{v}_{i}
is composed of a combination of chemicals
\mathbb{X}_{1},\mathbb{X}_{2},\dots,\mathbb{X}_{N_{\mathbb{X}}}
and given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{v}_{i}\coloneqq\gamma_{1,i}\mathbb{X}_{1}+\gamma_{2,i}\mathbb{X}_{2}+\dots+\gamma_{N_{\mathbb{X}},i}\mathbb{X}_{N_{\mathbb{X}}}.

(2.1)

Each hyperedge
\mathbb{e}_{e}
corresponds to a chemical reaction and is defined by a directed pair of two hypervertices
\mathbb{e}_{e}\coloneqq(\mathbb{v}_{e}^{+},\mathbb{v}_{e}^{-})
, which can be expressed as

\displaystyle\alpha_{1,e}\mathbb{X}_{1}+\alpha_{2,e}\mathbb{X}_{2}+\dots+\alpha_{N_{\mathbb{X}},e}\mathbb{X}_{N_{\mathbb{X}}}

\displaystyle\quad\xrightarrow{\mathbb{e}_{e}}\beta_{1,e}\mathbb{X}_{1}+\beta_{2,e}\mathbb{X}_{2}+\dots+\beta_{N_{\mathbb{X}},e}\mathbb{X}_{N_{\mathbb{X}}}.

(2.2)

Here,
\mathbb{v}_{e}^{\pm}
are chosen from
\{\mathbb{v}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N_{\mathbb{v}}}
, and in Eq. (
2.2
),
\mathbb{v}_{e}^{+}=\alpha_{1,e}\mathbb{X}_{1}+\alpha_{2,e}\mathbb{X}_{2}+\dots+\alpha_{N_{\mathbb{X}},e}\mathbb{X}_{N_{\mathbb{X}}}
and
\mathbb{v}_{e}^{-}=\beta_{1,e}\mathbb{X}_{1}+\beta_{2,e}\mathbb{X}_{2}+\dots+\beta_{N_{\mathbb{X}},e}\mathbb{X}_{N_{\mathbb{X}}}
.
We also define the order of reaction as follows:

\displaystyle m

\displaystyle\coloneqq\max_{i,e}\{\alpha_{i,e},\beta_{i,e}\}.

(2.3)

To characterize CRNs,
m
in Eq. (
2.3
) will play an important role.

When a CRN involves multiple chemical reactions, the description provided above may be inadequate.
To describe a complex CRN, the stoichiometric matrix plays a crucial role.
The stoichiometric matrix
S
is defined as an
N_{\mathbb{X}}\times N_{\mathbb{e}}
matrix and is given by

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle\coloneqq[\bm{s}_{1},\bm{s}_{2},\dots,\bm{s}_{N_{\mathbb{e}}}],

(2.4)

where, for
e=1,2,\dots,N_{\mathbb{e}}
,

\displaystyle\bm{s}_{e}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}\beta_{1,e}-\alpha_{1,e}\\
\beta_{2,e}-\alpha_{2,e}\\
\vdots\\
\beta_{N_{\mathbb{X}},e}-\alpha_{N_{\mathbb{X}},e}\end{bmatrix}.

(2.5)

That is, the
(j,e)
-th element of
S
is given by
s_{j,e}=\beta_{j,e}-\alpha_{j,e}
for
j=1,2,\dots,N_{\mathbb{X}}
and
e=1,2,\dots,N_{\mathbb{e}}
.
In general, when a CRN involves multiple chemical reactions, the stoichiometric matrix provides a concise representation of the relationships between the reactants and products.

The stoichiometric matrix
S
is also expressed as
S=-\Gamma B
.
Here,
B\in\{1,0,-1\}^{N_{\mathbb{v}}\times N_{\mathbb{e}}}
is the incidence matrix whose
(i,e)
-th element is given for
i=1,2,\dots,N_{\mathbb{v}}
and
e=1,2,\dots,N_{\mathbb{e}}
by

\displaystyle b_{i,e}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\begin{cases}1&(\text{$\mathbb{v}_{i}$ is the head of hyperedge $\mathbb{e}_{e}$: $\mathbb{v}_{i}=\mathbb{v}_{e}^{+}$}),\\
-1&(\text{$\mathbb{v}_{i}$ is the tail of hyperedge $\mathbb{e}_{e}$: $\mathbb{v}_{i}=\mathbb{v}_{e}^{-}$}),\\
0&(\text{otherwise}),\end{cases}

(2.6)

and
\Gamma\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{N_{\mathbb{X}}\times N_{\mathbb{v}}}
is given by

\displaystyle\Gamma

\displaystyle\coloneqq[\bm{\gamma}_{1},\bm{\gamma}_{2},\dots,\bm{\gamma}_{N_{\mathbb{v}}}],

(2.7)

where, using
\gamma_{1,i},\gamma_{2,i},\dots,\gamma_{N_{\mathbb{X}},i}
in Eq. (
2.1
),
\bm{\gamma}_{i}
is defined as

\displaystyle\bm{\gamma}_{i}

\displaystyle\coloneqq[\gamma_{1,i},\gamma_{2,i},\dots,\gamma_{N_{\mathbb{X}},i}]^{\intercal},

(2.8)

for
i=1,2,\dots,N_{\mathbb{v}}
.
Having defined the necessary variables to describe CRNs, we will now derive the equation that characterizes the dynamics of CRNs in the remainder of this section.

II.2
Dynamics of CRNs

To analyze the dynamics of a CRN, we introduce fluxes associated with each hyperedge.
Let
j_{e}^{+}(\bm{x})
and
j_{e}^{-}(\bm{x})
denote the currents from the head to the tail and from the tail to the head of hyperedge
\mathbb{e}_{e}
, respectively, where
\bm{x}
is the chemical concentration vector.
We define
\bm{j}^{+}(\bm{x})\coloneqq[j_{1}^{+}(\bm{x}),j_{2}^{+}(\bm{x}),\dots,j_{N_{\mathbb{e}}}^{+}(\bm{x})]^{\intercal}
and
\bm{j}^{-}(\bm{x})\coloneqq[j_{1}^{-}(\bm{x}),j_{2}^{-}(\bm{x}),\dots,j_{N_{\mathbb{e}}}^{-}(\bm{x})]^{\intercal}
.

The law of mass action is widely observed to hold for CRNs and is considered one of the fundamental characteristics that differentiate CRNs from nonchemical hypergraphs.
Based on this, we make the assumption of mass action kinetics for the forward and reverse reaction fluxes on hyperedge
\mathbb{e}_{e}
in Eq. (
2.2
):

\displaystyle j_{e}^{\pm}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=k_{e}^{\pm}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mathbb{v}}}b_{i,e}^{\pm}\prod_{j=1}^{N_{\mathbb{X}}}x_{j}^{\gamma_{j,i}},

(2.9)

where, for
i=1,2,\dots,N_{\mathbb{X}}
and
e=1,2,\dots,N_{\mathbb{e}}
,

\displaystyle b_{i,e}^{+}\coloneqq\max(b_{i,e},0),

(2.10)

\displaystyle b_{i,e}^{-}\coloneqq-\min(b_{i,e},0),

(2.11)

and
k_{e}^{\pm}
are the reaction rate coefficients for the forward and backward currents on
\mathbb{e}_{e}
.
Expressed in vector notation, Eq. (
2.9
) can be written as

\displaystyle\bm{j}^{\pm}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\bm{k}^{\pm}\circ(B^{\pm})^{\intercal}{\bm{x}^{\Gamma}}^{\intercal}

(2.12)

\displaystyle=\bm{k}^{\pm}\circ{\bm{x}^{(\Gamma B^{\pm})}}^{\intercal},

(2.13)

where

\displaystyle B^{+}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\max(B,\mathbb{0}),

(2.14)

\displaystyle B^{-}

\displaystyle\coloneqq-\min(B,\mathbb{0}),

(2.15)

\displaystyle{\bm{x}^{\Gamma}}^{\intercal}

\displaystyle\coloneqq[\bm{x}^{\bm{\gamma}_{1}},\bm{x}^{\bm{\gamma}_{2}},\dots,\bm{x}^{\bm{\gamma}_{N_{\mathbb{v}}}}]^{\intercal},

(2.16)

\displaystyle\bm{x}^{\bm{\gamma}_{i}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\prod_{j=1}^{N_{\mathbb{X}}}x_{j}^{\gamma_{j,i}},

(2.17)

\displaystyle\bm{k}^{\pm}

\displaystyle\coloneqq[k_{1}^{\pm},k_{2}^{\pm},\dots,k_{N_{\mathbb{e}}}^{\pm}].

(2.18)

Here,
\mathbb{0}
represents the zero matrix, which has the same size as matrix
B
.
The functions
\max(\cdot,\cdot)
and
\min(\cdot,\cdot)
are applied elementwise, meaning that for each element
[A]_{i,j}
and
[B]_{i,j}
, we have
[\max(A,B)]_{i,j}=\max([A]_{i,j},[B]_{i,j})
and
[\min(A,B)]_{i,j}=\min([A]_{i,j},[B]_{i,j})
, respectively.
The notation
[\cdot]_{i,j}
represents the element located at the
i
-th row and
j
-th column.
Moreover, the symbol
\circ
denotes the element-wise product, which is defined as follows:

\displaystyle\bm{x}\circ\bm{y}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}y_{1}\\
x_{2}y_{2}\\
\vdots\\
x_{N_{\mathbb{X}}}y_{N_{\mathbb{X}}}\end{bmatrix},

(2.19)

where
\bm{x}\coloneqq[x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{N_{\mathbb{X}}}]^{\intercal}
,
\bm{y}\coloneqq[y_{1},y_{2},\dots,y_{N_{\mathbb{X}}}]^{\intercal}
.

The chemical concentration vector
\bm{x}_{t}
at time
t
satisfies the chemical rate equation (CRE) given by
Beard and Qian 2008
;
Feinberg 2019
;
Rao and Esposito 2016

\displaystyle\dot{\bm{x}}_{t}

\displaystyle=S\bm{j}(\bm{x}_{t}),

(2.20)

where
\bm{j}(\bm{x})\coloneqq\bm{j}^{+}(\bm{x})-\bm{j}^{-}(\bm{x})
.

II.3
Legendre duality of fluxes and forces

In the realm of physics, the relationship between fluxes and forces is commonly expressed through Legendre duality, a concept that describes how forces and fluxes are dual aspects of the same system.
Their product results in entropy production, denoted as
\langle\bm{j},\bm{f}\rangle
.
In the context of chemical thermodynamics, we define the force on a hyperedge
\mathbb{e}_{e}
in a manner consistent with entropy production:

\displaystyle f_{e}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\ln\frac{j_{e}^{+}(\bm{x})}{j_{e}^{-}(\bm{x})},

(2.21)

for
e=1,2,\dots,N_{\mathbb{e}}
.
The corresponding vector form is given by Eq. (
2.21

\bm{f}(\bm{x})\coloneqq[f_{1}(\bm{x}),f_{2}(\bm{x}),\dots,f_{N_{\mathbb{e}}}(\bm{x})]^{\intercal},

can be expressed as

\displaystyle\bm{f}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\ln\frac{\bm{j}^{+}(\bm{x})}{\bm{j}^{-}(\bm{x})},

(2.22)

where the division and the logarithmic function are computed elementwise.

We introduce a quantity called “frenetic activity,” particularly on hyperedge
\mathbb{e}_{e}

\displaystyle\omega_{e}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle\coloneqq 2\sqrt{j_{e}^{+}(\bm{x})j_{e}^{-}(\bm{x})},

(2.23)

.
The vector form of Eq. (

can be expressed as

\displaystyle\bm{\omega}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=2\sqrt{\bm{j}^{+}(\bm{x})\circ\bm{j}^{-}(\bm{x})}.

(2.24)

Then, the following strictly convex smooth function
\Psi_{\bm{\omega}(\bm{x})}^{*}(\bm{f}(\bm{x}))

2.22

2.24

\displaystyle\Psi_{\bm{\omega}(\bm{x})}^{*}(\bm{f}(\bm{x}))

\displaystyle\coloneqq\bm{\omega}(\bm{x})^{\intercal}[\cosh(\bm{f}(\bm{x}))-\bm{1}],

(2.25)

where

\displaystyle\cosh(\bm{f}(\bm{x}))

\displaystyle\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}\cosh(f_{1}(\bm{x}))\\
\cosh(f_{2}(\bm{x}))\\
\vdots\\
\cosh(f_{N_{\mathbb{e}}}(\bm{x}))\end{bmatrix},

(2.26)

\displaystyle\bm{f}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle\coloneqq[f_{1}(\bm{x}),f_{2}(\bm{x}),\dots,f_{N_{\mathbb{e}}}(\bm{x})]^{\intercal},

(2.27)

\displaystyle\bm{1}

\displaystyle\coloneqq[\underbrace{1,1,\dots,1}_{N_{\mathbb{e}}}]^{\intercal}.

(2.28)

As a result we have

\displaystyle=\partial_{\bm{f}}\Psi_{\bm{\omega}(\bm{x})}^{*}(\bm{f}(\bm{x})).

Chemical reaction dynamics

II.5
Geometric expression of an equilibrium CRE

III
Difficulty of constructing an upper bound on the reaction rates of CRNs

In this section, we briefly revisit Newton’s method and present a counterexample illustrating its limitations in establishing an upper bound on the reaction rates of CRNs.

III.1
Newton’s method

As stated in Sec.
I
, the objective of this paper is to determine an upper bound on the reaction rates of CRNs.
One might assume that straightforward optimization methods could achieve this.
However, before discussing NG, we elucidate the challenges of using Newton’s method
Hamming 2012
as an optimization technique for this purpose.
While the gradient method is another elementary optimization technique, its indeterminate step size precludes its consideration in this study.
We now turn to a specific optimization problem:

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{x}}f(\bm{x}).

\bm{x}_{t}
be the state at the

(3.3)

IV
Natural gradient

In this section, we explore the NG method and its applicability to the problem of constraining reaction rates in CRNs.
As our proposed methodology hinges on NG, understanding its theoretical underpinnings and its distinction from Newton’s method is crucial.

IV.1
Derivation of NG

In this section, we outline the derivation of the NG method, which is grounded in information geometry.
Specifically, we will elucidate how the dynamics of a given vector
\bm{x}_{t}
at time
t
are updated within the framework of NG:

\displaystyle\bm{x}_{t+\Delta t}

\displaystyle=\bm{x}_{t}+\Delta\bm{x}_{t}(\epsilon),

(4.1)

where
\Delta\bm{x}_{t}(\epsilon)
is defined as
5
5

5

We have used the following notation:
\partial_{\bm{x}}f(\bm{x}_{t})=\partial_{\bm{x}}f(\bm{x})|_{\bm{x}=\bm{x}_{t}}
.

\displaystyle\Delta\bm{x}_{t}(\epsilon)

\displaystyle=\argmin_{\Delta\bm{x}:\mathcal{D}_{\phi^{\prime}}(\bm{x}_{t}+\Delta\bm{x}\|\bm{x}_{t})\leq\epsilon}[f(\bm{x}_{t}+\Delta\bm{x})-f(\bm{x}_{t})]

(4.2)

\displaystyle\approx\argmin_{\Delta\bm{x}:\frac{1}{2}\Delta\bm{x}^{\intercal}G_{\phi^{\prime}}(\bm{x}_{t})\Delta\bm{x}\leq\epsilon}\partial_{\bm{x}}f(\bm{x}_{t})^{\intercal}\Delta\bm{x}.

(4.3)

Here,
G_{\phi^{\prime}}(\bm{x}_{t})
is the Hessian given by

\displaystyle[G_{\phi^{\prime}}(\bm{x}_{t})]_{i,j}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}\phi^{\prime}(\bm{x}_{t}),

(4.4)

where
[\cdot]_{i,j}
is the
(i,j)
-th element.
In the case of Eq. (
2.36
), Eq. (
4.4
) reads

\displaystyle[G_{\phi^{\prime}}(\bm{x}_{t})]_{i,j}

\displaystyle=\delta_{i,j}\frac{1}{[\bm{x}_{t}]_{i}},

(4.5)

where
\delta_{i,j}
is the Kronecker delta function and
[\cdot]_{i}
is the
i
-th element.
To derive Eq. (
4.3
), we have used the following expansion of the Bregman divergence:

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\phi^{\prime}}(\bm{x}_{t}+\Delta\bm{x}\|\bm{x}_{t})

\displaystyle\quad=\phi^{\prime}(\bm{x}_{t}+\Delta\bm{x})-\phi^{\prime}(\bm{x}_{t})-\langle(\bm{x}_{t}+\Delta\bm{x})-\bm{x}_{t},\partial_{\bm{x}}\phi^{\prime}(\bm{x}_{t})\rangle

(4.6)

\displaystyle\quad\approx\phi^{\prime}(\bm{x}_{t})+\partial_{\bm{x}}\phi(\bm{x}_{t})^{\intercal}\Delta\bm{x}+\frac{1}{2}\Delta\bm{x}^{\intercal}G_{\phi^{\prime}}(\bm{x}_{t})\Delta\bm{x}

\displaystyle\qquad-\phi^{\prime}(\bm{x}_{t})-\langle(\bm{x}_{t}+\Delta\bm{x})-\bm{x}_{t},\partial_{\bm{x}}\phi^{\prime}(\bm{x}_{t})\rangle

(4.7)

\displaystyle\quad=\frac{1}{2}\Delta\bm{x}^{\intercal}G_{\phi^{\prime}}(\bm{x}_{t})\Delta\bm{x}.

(4.8)

Note that
\Delta t
in Eq. (
4.1
) is set to unity in the conventional formulation of NG; in the following section, we will impose a specific relationship between
\Delta t
and
\epsilon
in Eq. (
4.1
) to connect NG and CRNs.

To find the solution of Eq. (
4.3
), we employ the method of Lagrange multipliers where the Lagrange function reads

\displaystyle L(\Delta\bm{x},\lambda)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\partial_{\bm{x}}f(\bm{x}_{t})^{\intercal}\Delta\bm{x}-\frac{\lambda}{2}(\Delta\bm{x}^{\intercal}G_{\phi^{\prime}}(\bm{x}_{t})\Delta\bm{x}-\epsilon).

(4.9)

The derivative of Eq. (
4.9

\Delta\bm{x}
takes the following form:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\Delta\bm{x}}L(\Delta\bm{x},\lambda)

\displaystyle=\partial_{\bm{x}}f(\bm{x}_{t})-\lambda G_{\phi^{\prime}}(\bm{x}_{t})\Delta\bm{x}.

(4.10)

Then, the solution of Eq. (
4.10
) is given by

\displaystyle\Delta\bm{x}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\lambda}G_{\phi^{\prime}}^{-1}(\bm{x}_{t})\partial_{\bm{x}}f(\bm{x}_{t}).

(4.11)

The derivative of Eq. (
4.9
) with respect to
\lambda
has the following form:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda}L(\Delta\bm{x},\lambda)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}(\Delta\bm{x}^{\intercal}G_{\phi^{\prime}}(\bm{x}_{t})\Delta\bm{x}-\epsilon)

(4.12)

\displaystyle=0.

(4.13)

Taking Eq. (
4.11
) into account, the solution of Eq. (
4.13
) is written as

\displaystyle\lambda^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial_{\bm{x}}f(\bm{x}_{t})^{\intercal}G_{\phi^{\prime}}^{-1}(\bm{x}_{t})\partial_{\bm{x}}f(\bm{x}_{t})}{\epsilon}.

(4.14)

Combining Eqs. (
4.11
) and (
4.14
) and taking account of the nature of the minimization problems, the solution of Eq. (
4.3
) takes the following form:

\displaystyle\Delta\bm{x}_{t}(\epsilon)

\displaystyle=-\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon}{\partial_{\bm{x}}f(\bm{x}_{t})^{\intercal}G_{\phi^{\prime}}^{-1}(\bm{x}_{t})\partial_{\bm{x}}f(\bm{x}_{t})}}G_{\phi^{\prime}}^{-1}(\bm{x}_{t})\partial_{\bm{x}}f(\bm{x}_{t}).

(4.15)

Note that
\phi^{\prime}(\cdot)
in Eq. (
4.15

\phi(\cdot)
appearing in Sec.
II
.
In the case of CRNs,
f(\bm{x}_{t})
in Eq. (
4.15
) represents
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\bm{x}_{t}\|\hat{\bm{x}})
.
As shown in Eq. (
4.15
),
\epsilon
is a key parameter in NG.
From the perspective of applying NG to CRNs, the relationship between
\epsilon
in NG and
\Delta t
in CRNs, when discretized, is still missing.
Therefore, NG cannot be directly applied to CRNs.
In the following section, we will explain how to address this challenge and develop a general upper bound on the dynamics of CRNs.

IV.2
Comparison with Newton’s method

In this section, we compare NG with Newton’s method.
Newton’s method is a special case of NG when Eq. (
4.15
) is adjusted according to certain conditions.
Specifically, the conditions are
\phi(\cdot)=\phi^{\prime}(\cdot)
and
\epsilon=\partial_{\bm{x}}f(\bm{x}_{t})^{\intercal}G_{\phi^{\prime}}^{-1}(\bm{x}_{t})\partial_{\bm{x}}f(\bm{x}_{t})

The equation thus becomes equivalent to Eq. (

This equivalency leads us to introduce a systematic NG-based method to determine the direction and step size for a gradient system that bounds CRNs of a specific class.

V.1
Upper bound system

Comparing discretized CRE dynamics with NG dynamics, represented by Eq. (

To address this issue, we propose the following relationship between

\displaystyle\epsilon

\displaystyle=\mathcal{D}_{\phi^{\prime}}(\bm{x}_{t}+\|\dot{\bm{x}}_{t}\|_{\mathrm{F}}\bm{e}_{t}\Delta t\|\bm{x}_{t}),

in Eq. (

Note that

From Eq. (

\displaystyle\|\dot{\bm{x}}_{t}\|_{\mathrm{F}}

\displaystyle\quad=\bigg\|S\partial_{\bm{f}}\Psi_{\bm{\omega}(\bm{x}_{t})}^{*}\bigg(-\frac{1}{2}S^{\intercal}\partial_{\bm{x}}\mathcal{D}_{\phi}(\bm{x}_{t}\|\hat{\bm{x}})\bigg)\bigg\|_{\mathrm{F}}

\displaystyle\|\bm{v}\|_{\mathrm{abs}}

\displaystyle\|\bm{v}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{N_{\mathbb{X}}\to N_{\mathbb{e}}}

\displaystyle[\|S^{\intercal}\bm{v}\|_{\mathrm{abs}}]_{e}

VIII
Conclusions

In this study, we developed a framework based on NG to establish an upper bound on the dynamics of a specific subset of CRNs.
The physical meaning of this bound relates to the concept of entropy production, which in turn is related to the speed of convergence of the chemical reaction.
The nonlinearity commonly present in CRNs presents a challenge, which is addressed here.
The optimization problem in the NG derivation was found to be related to entropy production, enriching the understanding of NG within a thermodynamic context.
While the primary focus has been on CRNs, the methods and discussions are applicable to a wider range of hypergraph dynamics.
The study holds implications for fields beyond chemistry and physics, including information science and machine learning.

Appendix A
Derivation of the KL divergence from the Bregman divergence

In this section, we show that the Bregman divergence, Eq. (
2.34
), with Eq. (
2.36
) is equivalent to the KL divergence, Eq. (
2.37
).
Let us define the following potential for
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
:

\displaystyle\phi_{\mathrm{KL}}^{(\alpha)}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mathbb{X}}}x_{i}(\ln x_{i}-\alpha)

(A.1)

The Bregman divergence, Eq. (
2.34
), with Eq. (
A.1
) is computed as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\phi_{\mathrm{KL}}^{(\alpha)}}(\bm{x}\|\bm{y})

\displaystyle=\phi_{\mathrm{KL}}^{(\alpha)}(\bm{x})-\phi_{\mathrm{KL}}^{(\alpha)}(\bm{y})-\langle(\bm{x}-\bm{y}),\nabla\phi_{\mathrm{KL}}^{(\alpha)}(\bm{y})\rangle

(A.2)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mathbb{X}}}x_{i}(\ln x_{i}-\alpha)-\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mathbb{X}}}y_{i}(\ln y_{i}-\alpha)

\displaystyle\quad-\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mathbb{X}}}(x_{i}-y_{i})(\ln y_{i}-\alpha+1)

(A.3)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mathbb{X}}}x_{i}\ln x_{i}-\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mathbb{X}}}y_{i}\ln y_{i}

\displaystyle\quad-\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mathbb{X}}}(x_{i}-y_{i})\ln y_{i}-\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mathbb{X}}}(x_{i}-y_{i})

(A.4)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mathbb{X}}}x_{i}\ln\frac{x_{i}}{y_{i}}-\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mathbb{X}}}(x_{i}-y_{i})

(A.5)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\bm{x}\|\bm{y})

(A.6)

Thus, the Bregman divergence, Eq. (
2.34
), with Eq. (
A.1
) is equivalent to the KL divergence, Eq. (
2.37
), independently from
\alpha
.
Furthermore, Eq. (
2.36
) is the special case of Eq. (
A.1
) with
\alpha=0
.

Appendix B
Upper bound system with the
L^{2}
constraint

In Sec.
V
, we have considered
\phi_{\mathrm{KL}}(\cdot)
, Eq. (
2.36
), as the potential of the Bregman divergence in the constraint term since the KL divergence is minimized in CRNs.
However, we are not limited to this choice, and it is expected that a different potential in the constraint may give us a different bound.
Another simple candidate for the potential of the Bregman divergence in the constraint is the
L^{2}
norm given by

\displaystyle\phi_{L^{2}}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mathbb{X}}}|x_{i}|^{2}.

(B.1)

In this case,
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\bm{x}_{t}+\|\dot{\bm{x}}_{t}\|_{\mathrm{F}}\bm{e}_{t}\Delta t\|\bm{x}_{t})
does not depend on
\bm{e}_{t}
and the Hessian
G_{\phi_{L^{2}}}(\bm{x}_{t})
becomes the identity matrix:
G_{\phi_{L^{2}}}(\bm{x}_{t})=\mathbb{1}
.
