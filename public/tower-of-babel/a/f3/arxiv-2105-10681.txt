1.
Introduction

The most popular definition of integrability for functions with values in a Banach space is the Bochner integrability, which is a direct generalization of the ordinary Lebesgue integration, see a very nice short exposition in
[
2
, Chapter 5]
. Bochner integrability assumes measurability, separability of the set of values outside of values on a set of measure 0, and Lebesgue integrability of the real function
t\mapsto\|f(t)\|
. This type of integration has very nice properties, but is restrictive. In particular, some Riemann integrable functions are not integrable in Bochner sense. Let us recall the definition and the corresponding well-known illustrative example (
[
12
]
,
[
13
, Pages 119-120]
).

Definition 1.1
.

Let
T=\{\Delta_{i},t_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
be a tagged partition of
[0,1]
(
t_{i}\in\Delta_{i}
). Denote
d(T)=\max\limits_{1\leqslant i\leqslant n}|\Delta_{i}|
. For a bounded function
f\colon[0;1]\rightarrow X
define the corresponding
Riemann
sum as
S(f,T)=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}|\Delta_{i}|F(t_{i})
. The function
f
is said to be
Riemann integrable
if there exists an
x\in X
(called
f
’s
Riemann integral
) such that for any
\varepsilon>0
there is a
\delta>0
such that for any tagged partition
T
with
d(T)<\delta
the corresponding Riemann integral sum is
\varepsilon
-close to
x
, that is,

\|S(f,T)-x\|<\varepsilon.

In other words,
x\in X
is integral of
f
(
x=\int\limits_{0}^{1}f(t){\rm dt}
) if
x=\lim\limits_{d(T)\to 0}S(f,T)
.

Recall that the space
\ell_{2}([0;1])
consists of all functions
x\colon[0;1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
with

\sum_{t\in[0;1]}|x(t)|^{2}<\infty
(in particular supports of these functions are at most countable subsets of
[0;1]
). The norm on
\ell_{2}([0;1])
is given by
\|x\|=(\sum_{t\in[0;1]}|x(t)|^{2})^{1/2}
. It is easy to see that
\ell_{2}([0;1])
is a non-separable Hilbert space. The standard orthonormal basis consists of continuum number of elements
e_{t}:=\mathds{1}_{\{t\}}
,
t\in[0,1]
.

Now consider the function
f\colon[0;1]\rightarrow\ell_{2}([0;1])
:
f(t)=e_{t}
. This function is not measurable: if
A
is a non-measurable subset of
[0;1]
, then
A
is the preimage of
\{e_{t}:t\in A\}
, but the later is a closed set and hence a Borel subset of
\ell_{2}([0;1])
.

However, this function is Riemann integrable. To see this, note that for any tagged partition partition
T
, if
d(T)\leqslant\varepsilon
then

\displaystyle\|S(f,\Pi,T)\|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\|\sum f(t_{i})\left|\Delta_{i}\right|\right\|=\left\|\sum e_{t_{i}}\left|\Delta_{i}\right|\right\|=\left(\sum\left|\Delta_{i}\right|^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\left(\sum\varepsilon\cdot\left|\Delta_{i}\right|\right)^{1/2}=\sqrt{\varepsilon}\Bigl(\sum_{i}\left|\Delta_{i}\right|\Bigr)^{1/2}=\sqrt{\varepsilon}.

Thus
f
is Riemann integrable with the zero integral.

In reality, outside of Bochner and Riemann integrals, there are a lot of other useful non-equivalent integrability concepts for functions with values in a Banach space: Pettis integral, Gelfand integral, Birkhoff integral, McShane integral, Henstock–Kurzweil integral, and others.The relationship between various kinds of integrability is a huge and popular area of research.

A substantial part of modern integration theory deals with set-valued functions. The most popular approaches to set-valued integration belong to Nobel laureates in economics Aumann
[
1
]
(through selectors) and Debreu
[
8
]
(through Rådström embedding which maps convex compact sets into points of the space of bounded functions on
B_{X^{*}}
, after which the multifunction transforms into an ordinary function and the ordinary Bochner integration is applicable). These approaches are proved to be very useful in various areas like optimization, differential inclusions and mathematical economics. Both approaches combined with different kinds of integrals for ordinary Banach space-valued functions lead to a number of integrals for set-valued functions, and, moreover, some vector-valued integrals like Pettis or Gelfand integral have direct generalizations to the set-valued case, that are not equivalent to those, obtained from Aumann’s and Debreu’s approaches (
[
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
]
and citations therein). For a detailed account on measurable selection results and set-valued integration we refer the reader to the monographs
[
7
,
14
]
and the survey
[
9
]
.

In this paper we concentrate on the Riemann integration of set-valued functions. The letter
X
is reserved for Banach spaces. We consider only real Banach spaces and use the standard Banach space theory notation like
X^{*}
for the dual space,
L(X,Y)
for the space of bounded linear operators from
X
to
Y
, or
B_{X}
for the closed unit ball without additional explanation. Below a
set-valued function
(another name –
multifunction
) with values in
X
is a map
F:[0,1]\to 2^{X}\setminus\{\varnothing\}
.

If
X
is a normed space, we can add elements of
2^{X}
and multiply them by a constant in a natural way: for subsets
A,B\subset X
their (Minkowski) sum is
A+B:=\{a+b\colon a\in A,b\in B\}
, and if
\lambda
is a scalar,
\lambda A:=\{\lambda a\colon a\in A\}
.

Denote by
{\rm b}{(X)}
the collection of all not empty bounded subsets of
X
and
{\rm clb}(X)
the subcollection of all closed bounded subsets. The convergence in
{\rm b}{(X)}
will be considered with respect to the Hausdorff distance
\rho_{H}
:

Definition 1.2
.

The
one-sided Hausdorff distance
between sets
A,B\in{\rm b}{(X)}
is the number
\widetilde{\rho}_{H}(A,B)=\sup_{b\in B}\rho(b,A)
. The
Hausdorff distance
between
A
and
B
is the number

\rho_{H}(A,B)=\max\{\widetilde{\rho}_{H}(A,B),\widetilde{\rho}_{H}(B,A)\}.

Remark, that
\rho_{H}
is a pseudometric on
{\rm b}{(X)}
but is not a metric:
\rho_{H}(A,\overline{A})=0
for every
A\in{\rm b}{(X)}
. This means that the limit in Hausdorff distance is not uniquely defined. In order to eliminate this inconvenience, below in the definition of the Riemann integral of a multifunction we demand that integral belongs to
{\rm clb}(X)
: since for two different closed sets the Hausdorff distance is non-zero, this makes the integral unique.

Definition 1.3
.

For a tagged partition
T=\{\Delta_{i},t_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
of
[0,1]
and a multifunction
F:[0,1]\to{\rm b}{(X)}
define the corresponding
Riemann sum

S(F,T)=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}|\Delta_{i}|F(t_{i})
. Then a set
A\in{\rm clb}(X)
is said to be the
Riemann integral
of
F
(
A=\int\limits_{0}^{1}F(t){\rm dt}
) if
A=\lim\limits_{d(T)\to 0}S(F,T)
, where the limit is taken in the sense of the Hausdorff distance. If the Riemann integral exists,
F
is called
Riemann integrable
.

We were able to trace this natural definition of the multi-valued Riemann integral to
[
10
]
for multifunctions with convex compact values and to
[
17
]
without the convexity assumption.

Remark 1.4
.

Since in our paper we consider only Riemann integrability, below we use words “integral” and “integrable” in the sense of “Riemann integral” and “Riemann integrable”.

Definition 1.5
.

Let
F:[0,1]\to 2^{X}
be a multifunction. Then the
convex hull
of
F
is the multifunction
{\rm conv}F:[0,1]\to 2^{X}:t\mapsto{\rm conv}(F(t))
.

Theorem 1.6
.

Let
F
be an integrable multifunction with
\int\limits_{0}^{1}F(t){\rm dt}=A
. Then its convex hull
{\rm conv}F
is also integrable and
\int\limits_{0}^{1}{{\rm conv}F(t)}{\rm dt}=\overline{{\rm conv}}A
.

Proof.

Since the convex hull of Minkowski sum is the Minkowski sum of convex hulls,
S({\rm conv}F,T)={\rm conv}S(F,T)
for every tagged partition
T
of
[0,1]
.
It remains to apply the evident inequality

\rho_{H}({\rm conv}U,{\rm conv}V)\leqslant\rho_{H}(U,V)

which is true for all
U,V\in{\rm b}{(X)}
:

\rho_{H}(S({\rm conv}F,T),{\overline{{\rm conv}}}A)=\rho_{H}({\rm conv}S(F,T),{\rm conv}A)\leqslant\rho_{H}(S(F,T),A)\xrightarrow[d(T)\to 0]{}0.

So
\int\limits_{0}^{1}{{\rm conv}F(t)}{\rm dt}={\overline{{\rm conv}}}A
.
∎

The converse to Theorem
1.6
result together with the stronger equality

(1.1)

\int\limits_{0}^{1}{{\rm conv}F(t)}{\rm dt}=\int\limits_{0}^{1}{F(t)}{\rm dt}

was demonstrated in
[
18
, Theorem 10]
under additional assumption of finite-dimensionality of
X
. Later it was extended to functions with compact values under assumption that
X
is superreflexive (see
[
11
, Corollary 3.1]
and the discussion around it).

The main results of our paper are:

1.

The integral of any Riemann integrable multifunction is convex.

2.

The converse to Theorem
1.6
holds true in any Banach space
X
with an infratype
p>1
(so-called B-convex spaces, see Definition
3.1
).

3.

If a Banach space
X
is not B-convex then there exists a multifunction
F:[0,1]\to{\rm b}{(X)}
which is not Riemann integrable, but whose convex hull is Riemann integrable.

4.

In any Banach space
X
if the convex hull of multifunction
F:[0,1]\to{\rm b}{(X)}
is Riemann integrable with compact
\int\limits_{0}^{1}{{\rm conv}F(t)}{\rm dt}
, then
F
is Riemann integrable and (
1.1
) holds true. In particular, this happens if all the values of
F
are precompact.

The structure of the paper is as follows. In the next section “
2.
The convexity of integral
” we list elementary properties of integral and demonstrate the first (and simplest) of the aforementioned main results. After that, in the section “
3.
The connection between a function and its convex hull
” we give the necessary preliminaries from the Banach space theory and demonstrate the second and the third of the main results; and the last section “
4.
The compact case
” is devoted to the last result from the above list.

2.
The convexity of integral

Let us list without proof some easy-to check properties.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
F:[0,1]\to{\rm b}{(X)}
be a Riemann integrable multi-valued function with

\int\limits_{0}^{1}F(t){{\rm dt}}=A
and
P\in L(X,Y)
. Then
P\circ F

(
the multifunction
t\mapsto P(F(t))
)
is also integrable and
\int\limits_{0}^{1}{P(F(t))}{\rm dt}=\overline{P(A)}
.

Proposition 2.2
.

Every Riemann integrable multifunction is bounded. That is, if
F
is integrable, then

\sup\{\|a\|\colon a\in F(t),t\in[0,1]\}<\infty.

Proposition 2.3
.

If
A\in{\rm b}{(X)}
is convex and
F(t)=A
for all
t\in[0,1]
, then
\int\limits_{0}^{1}{F}{\rm dt}=\overline{A}.

Theorem 2.4
.

The integral of any Riemann integrable multifunction is convex.

Proof.

Let
F
be a Riemann integrable multifunction,
A=\int\limits_{0}^{1}{F(t)}{\rm dt}
. Since
A
is closed, in order to demonstrate its convexity it is sufficient to prove that
\frac{1}{2}A+\frac{1}{2}A\subset A
.

Consider a sequence of partitions
\Pi_{n}=\{\Delta_{k,n}\}_{k=1}^{N_{n}}
with
\lim_{n\to\infty}d(\Pi_{n})=0,
. Let
\Delta_{k,n}=[x_{(k-1),n},x_{k,n}]
.
Define a sequence of partitions
\widetilde{\Pi}_{n}=\{\widetilde{\Delta}_{i,n}\}_{i=1}^{2N_{n}}
, where

\widetilde{\Delta}_{2k-1,n}=\left[x_{(k-1),n},\frac{1}{2}x_{(k-1),n}+\frac{1}{2}x_{k,n}\right],\,\widetilde{\Delta}_{2k,n}=\left[\frac{1}{2}x_{(k-1),n}+\frac{1}{2}x_{k,n},x_{k,n}\right],

that is we divide every segment of the partition
\Pi_{n}
into two segments of equal length.
Selecting arbitrary points
t_{i,n}\in\widetilde{\Delta}_{i,n}
we define the sequence of tagged partitions
\widetilde{T}_{n}=\{\widetilde{\Delta}_{i,n},t_{i,n}\}_{i=1}^{2N_{n}}
. Now the main trick comes: both points
t_{2k-1,n}
and
t_{2k,n}
lie in the original bigger interval
\Delta_{k,n}
, so for a given
n\in\mathbb{N}
we can define two tagged partitions based on the same selection of segments
\{\Delta_{k,n}\}_{k=1}^{N_{n}}
, namely
T_{a,n}=\{\Delta_{k,n},t_{2k-1,n}\}_{k=1}^{N_{n}}
and
T_{b,n}=\{\Delta_{k,n},t_{2k,n}\}_{k=1}^{N_{n}}
.
Then, for the corresponding Riemann sums

S_{a,n}=S(F,T_{a,n}),S_{b,n}=S(F,T_{b,n})\text{ and }S_{n}=S(F,T_{n})

we have that
S_{n}=\frac{1}{2}S_{a,n}+\frac{1}{2}S_{b,n}
, so

(2.1)

\rho_{H}\left(S_{n},\frac{1}{2}S_{a,n}+\frac{1}{2}S_{b,n}\right)=0.

By the integrability, all the three limits
\lim_{n\to\infty}S_{n}
,
\lim_{n\to\infty}S_{a,n}
, and
\lim_{n\to\infty}S_{b,n}
exist and are equal to
A
, so passing in (
2.1
) to the limit we obtain that

\rho_{H}\left(A,\frac{1}{2}A+\frac{1}{2}A\right)=0,

which means that
\overline{\frac{1}{2}A+\frac{1}{2}A}=A
, which gives the desired inclusion
\frac{1}{2}A+\frac{1}{2}A\subset A
.
∎

Combining this proposition with Theorem
1.6
we get:

Corollary 2.5
.

Suppose
F
is a Riemann integrable multifunction. Then
{\rm conv}F
is also integrable and
\int\limits_{0}^{1}{{\rm conv}F(t)}{\rm dt}=\int\limits_{0}^{1}{F(t)}{\rm dt}
.

3.
The connection between a function and its convex hull

Definition 3.1
.

A Banach space
X
is said to be of infratype
p
with a constant
C>0
if for every finite set
\{x_{k}\}_{k=1}^{n}\subset X
the following inequality holds:

\min\limits_{\alpha_{i}=\pm 1}\left\|\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}x_{i}\right\|\leqslant C\left(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\|x_{i}\|^{p}\right)^{1/p}.

In the sequel, we say that
X
is of infratype
p
if there exists a
C>0
such that
X
is of infratype
p
with the constant
C
. The theory of type and infratype was originated by Gilles Pisier
[
15
]
and is an important part of local theory of Banach spaces, see the survey
[
16
]
, and books
[
20
, Sections II.E, III.A]
and
[
13
, Chapter 5]
. It is easy to see that

•

any Banach space is of infratype
1
(for this reason the infratype 1 is called
the trivial infratype
);

•

infratype is an isomorphic invariant;

•

any Hilbert space is of infratype
2
, consequently finite-dimensional spaces have infratype
2
(in reality, finite dimensional spaces have a much stronger property
[
13
, Lemma 2.2.1]
);

•

the space
\ell_{1}
does not have a non-trivial infratype.

In this section we are going to prove the following theorem:

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
X
be a Banach space. Then the following assertions are equivalent:

(
1
)

X
has an infratype
p>1
;

(
2
)

For every multifunction
F:[0,1]\to{\rm b}{(X)}
if
{\rm conv}F
is integrable, then
F
is integrable itself.

The implications (1)
\Rightarrow
(2) and (2)
\Rightarrow
(1) in the above theorem are of very different nature, and will be proved as two separate theorems: Theorem
3.5
and Theorem
3.14
in two different subsections of this section.

3.1.
Spaces with a non-trivial infratype

Lemma 3.3

(
[
12
, Lemma before Theorem 3]
, or
[
13
, P. 133-134, Lemma 3]
)
.

Let
p>1
,
C>0
, and
X
be a Banach space with infratype
p
and constant
C
. Let
\{A_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
be an arbitrary collection of bounded subsets of
X
,
d_{i}=diam(A_{i})
, and
\{b_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
be a collection of points such that
b_{i}\in{\rm conv}A_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,n
.
Then one can choose points
a_{i}\in A_{i}
, so that

\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}(a_{i}-b_{i})\right\|\leqslant C_{1}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}^{p}\right)^{1/p},

where
C_{1}=\frac{2C}{2^{1-\frac{1}{p}}-1}
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
p>1
,
\{d_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\subset(0,+\infty)
with
\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}=1
. Denote
d=\max_{i}d_{i}
. Then
\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}^{p}\leqslant d^{p-1}
.

Proof
.
Since
d_{i}\leqslant d
and
p-1>0
,
d_{i}^{p-1}\leqslant d^{p-1}
. Then

[formula]

\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}^{p}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}^{p-1}\cdot d_{i}\leqslant\sum_{i=1}^{n}d^{p-1}\cdot d_{i}=d^{p-1}\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}=d^{p-1}.

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
X
be a space with an infratype
p>1
with a constant
C
,
F:[0;1]\to{\rm b}{(X)}
be Riemann integrable with
\int\limits_{0}^{1}{{\rm conv}F(t)}{\rm dt}=A
. Then
\int\limits_{0}^{1}{F(t)}{\rm dt}=A
.

Proof.

Since
{\rm conv}F
is integrable, it is bounded, hence,
F
is also bounded. Denote
M=\sup\limits_{t}\diam(F(t))
. For a tagged partition
T=\{\Delta_{i},t_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
of
[0,1]
consider an arbitrary
b\in S({\rm conv}(F),T)
. It can be written in the form
b=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}b_{i}
with
b_{i}\in{\rm conv}F(t_{i})|\Delta_{i}|
. Applying Lemma
3.3
to the collection
\{b_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
we obtain
\{a_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
such that
a_{i}\in F(t_{i})|\Delta_{i}|
and

\begin{split}\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_{i}\right\|=\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}(a_{i}-b_{i})\right\|\leqslant C_{1}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}(|\Delta_{i}|\diam(F(t_{i})))^{p}\right)^{1/p}\leqslant\\
C_{1}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}(M|\Delta_{i}|)^{p}\right)^{1/p}=C_{1}M\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\Delta_{i}|^{p}\right)^{1/p}\leqslant C_{1}\cdot M\cdot d(T)^{\frac{p-1}{p}},\end{split}

where
d(T)=\max\limits_{i}|\Delta_{i}|
and
C_{1}=\frac{2C}{2^{1-\frac{1}{p}}-1}
(on the last step we used Lemma
3.4
with
d_{i}=|\Delta_{i}|
).
So, for every
b\in S({\rm conv}(F),T)
there is
a=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\in S(F,T)
such that
\|a-b\|\leqslant C_{1}\cdot M\cdot d(T)^{\frac{p-1}{p}}
.
Consequently,

\widetilde{\rho}_{H}(S(F,T),S({\rm conv}(F),T))=\sup\limits_{b\in S({\rm conv}(F),T)}\rho(b,S(F,T))\xrightarrow[d(T)\rightarrow 0]{}0.

On the other hand,
S(F,T)\subset S({\rm conv}(F),T)
, so
\widetilde{\rho}_{H}(S({\rm conv}(F),T),S(F,T))=0
.
This means that
\rho_{H}(S(F,T),S({\rm conv}F,T))\xrightarrow[d(T)\rightarrow 0]{}0,
and

\int\limits_{0}^{1}{F(t)}{\rm dt}=\lim\limits_{d(T)\to 0}S(F,T)=\lim\limits_{d(T)\to 0}S({\rm conv}F,T)=A

as desired.
∎

3.2.
Spaces without non-trivial infratype

Definition 3.6
.

Let
X
and
Y
be Banach spaces. The
Banach-Mazur distance
between
X
and
Y
is the number
d(X,Y)=\inf\{\|T\|\cdot\|T^{-1}\|\}
, where the infinum is taken over all isomorphisms
T:X\to Y
. If
X
and
Y
are not isomorphic, we put
d(X,Y)=+\infty
.

Definition 3.7
.

A Banach space
Y
is said to be
finitely representable
in a space
X
(
Y\xrightarrow{f}X
for short) if for any
\varepsilon>0
and any finite-dimensional subspace
Z\subset Y
there exists a finite-dimensional subspace
Z_{1}\subset X
such that

d(Z,Z_{1})<1+\varepsilon.

Definition 3.8
.

X
is said to be
B-convex
if the space
\ell_{1}
is not finitely representable in
X
.

Theorem 3.9

(
[
15
]
)
.

A space
X
is B-convex if and only if it has an infratype
p>1
.

Definition 3.10
.

Let
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
. A subspace
Z
of a Banach space
X
is said to be
\varepsilon
-orthogonal
to a subspace
Y\subset X
if for all
y\in Y
and
z\in Z

(3.1)

\|y+z\|\geqslant(1-\varepsilon)\|y\|.

Remark, that
Z
and
Y
in the above definition intersect only by the zero element of
X
. Indeed, if there is a non-zero
y\in Y\cap Z
, then
z:=-y\in Z
but
\|y+z\|=0
, which contradicts (
3.1
).

Lemma 3.11

(
[
13
, Lemma 6.3.1]
)
.

Let
X
be a Banach space,
Y
be a finite-dimensional subspace of
X
, and
Z
be an infinite-dimensional subspace of
X
. Then for any
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
there exists a subspace
G\subset Z
of finite codimension in
Z
, such that
G
is
\varepsilon
-orthogonal to
Y
.

Lemma 3.12
.

Let
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and let a subspace
G
of
X
be
\varepsilon
-orthogonal to a subspace
E
. Then
E
is
\frac{1}{2-\varepsilon}
-orthogonal to
G
. That is, for all
e\in E,g\in G

\|e+g\|\geqslant\left(1-\frac{1}{2-\varepsilon}\right)\|g\|.

Proof
.
Since
G
is
\varepsilon
-orthogonal to
E
,
\|e+g\|\geqslant(1-\varepsilon)\|e\|
. On the other hand,
\|e+g\|\geqslant\|g\|-\|e\|
. Multiply the second inequality by
(1-\varepsilon)
and add it to the first:

(2-\varepsilon)\|e+g\|\geqslant(1-\varepsilon)\|g\|.

Then

[formula]

\|e+g\|\geqslant\frac{1-\varepsilon}{2-\varepsilon}\cdot\|g\|=\left(1-\frac{1}{2-\varepsilon}\right)\|g\|.

Lemma 3.13
.

Let
X
be a Banach space without a non-trivial infratype. Then for every
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
there are sequences
\{E_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
and
\{G_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}}
of subspaces of
X
such that for all
k\in\mathbb{N}

(
i
)

\dim E_{k}=2^{k-1}
,
\codim G_{k}<\infty
;

(
ii
)

G_{0}\supset G_{1}\supset G_{2}\supset\ldots
;

(
iii
)

E_{k}\subset G_{k-1}
;

(
iv
)

d(E_{k},\ell_{1}^{(2^{k-1})})<2
;

(
v
)

G_{k}
is
\varepsilon
-orthogonal to
E_{1}+E_{2}+\ldots+E_{k}
.

Proof.

Since
X
does not have an infratype, it is not B-convex. Hence
[
13
, Lemma 7.3.1]
, none of finite-codimensional subspaces of
X
is B-convex.
We are going to construct this sequences by induction. Set
G_{0}=X
.

The base of induction:
k=1
. Choose a one-dimensional subspace
E_{1}\subset X
. Then automatically
d(E_{1},\ell_{1}^{(1)})=1<2
. By Lemma
3.11
there exists a subspace
G_{1}\subset X
of finite codimension such that
G_{1}
is
\varepsilon
-orthogonal to
E_{1}
. Thus, the conditions above hold for
k=1
.

The inductive step
.
Suppose we have already constructed
E_{i}
and
G_{i}
for
i<k
such that the conditions above hold. Since
G_{k-1}
has finite codimension in
X
, it is not
B
-convex, so
\ell_{1}\xrightarrow{f}G_{k-1}
. Therefore there exists
E_{k}\subset G_{k-1}
with
\dim E_{k}=2^{k-1}
and
d(E_{k},\ell_{1}^{(2^{k-1})})<2
.
Then pick a subspace
G_{k}
of finite codimension in
G_{k-1}
such that it is
\varepsilon
-orthogonal to
E_{1}+E_{2}+\ldots+E_{k}
(it is possible because of Lemma
3.11
).
Also, since
\codim_{X}G_{k-1}<\infty
, we have
\codim_{X}G_{k}<\infty
. It can be easily seen that the constructed elements of the sequences satisfy the conditions above.
∎

Theorem 3.14
.

Let
X
be a Banach space without a non-trivial infratype. Then there exists a multifunction
F:[0,1]\to{\rm b}{(X)}
such that
{\rm conv}(F)
is integrable, but
F
is not.

Proof.

Let us apply the previous lemma with
\varepsilon=\frac{1}{2}
in order to get the corresponding subspaces
\{E_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
and
\{G_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}}
that satisfy the properties (i)-(v).
Remark that, by
\varepsilon
-orthogonality,
E_{k}\cap E_{j}=\{0\}
for
k\neq j
.

Since
d(\ell_{1}^{(2^{k-1})},E_{k})<2
, there exists a linear operator
T_{k}:\ell_{1}^{(2^{k-1})}\to E_{k}
such that
\|T_{k}\|\cdot\|T_{k}^{-1}\|<2
. Replacing
T_{k}
with
\lambda\cdot T_{k}
, we may assume that
\|T_{k}\|=1
. Then
\|T_{k}^{-1}\|<2
.

Let
\{\hat{e}_{j}\}
be the standard basis of
\ell_{1}
, then
\{\hat{e}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{2^{k-1}}
is a basis of
\ell_{1}^{(2^{k-1})}
. We can write
T_{k}\hat{e}_{j}
as
T_{k}\hat{e}_{j}=\tau_{2^{k-1}-1+j}e_{2^{k-1}-1+j}
, where
\|e_{2^{k-1}-1+j}\|=1
. Since
\|T_{k}\|=1
and
\|e_{j}\|=1
,
|\tau_{2^{n-1}-1+j}|\leqslant 1
.
T_{k}
is invertible, so
\{e_{j}\}_{j=2^{k-1}}^{2^{k}-1}
forms a normalized basis of
E_{k}
.

Now define the promised function
F:[0,1]\to{\rm clb}(X)
to be
F(t)=\{e_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
for all
t\in[0,1]
. Denote
A={\overline{{\rm conv}}}(\{e_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}})
. Since
({\rm conv}F)(t)={\rm conv}(\{e_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}})
is convex, Proposition
2.3
says that
{\rm conv}F
is Riemann integrable with
\int\limits_{0}^{1}{({\rm conv}F)(t)}{\rm dt}=A
. Therefore, in order to demonstrate that
F
is not integrable it is sufficient to show that the integral sums of
F
do not converge to
A
(Corollary
2.5
). Consider the tagged partition
T_{n}
of the segment
[0,1]
into
2^{n-1}-1
equal parts and denote
S_{n}
the corresponding integral sum:

(3.2)

S_{n}=S(F,T_{n})=\sum\limits_{l=1}^{2^{n-1}-1}\frac{1}{2^{n-1}-1}\{e_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}=\left\{\sum\limits_{l=1}^{2^{n-1}-1}\frac{1}{2^{n-1}-1}e_{j_{l}}:j_{l}\in\mathbb{N}\right\}.

For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
choose
y_{n}=\frac{1}{2^{n}-1}\sum\limits_{j=2^{n}}^{2^{n+1}-1}e_{j}\in E_{n+1}\in A
. Then

(3.3)

\rho_{H}(S_{n},A)\geqslant\rho(S_{n},y_{n})=\inf\limits_{s\in S_{n}}\|y_{n}-s\|.

Take an arbitrary
s\in S_{n}
. According to (
3.2
)
s
is of the form

s=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{\infty}t_{j}e_{j}
, where
\sum\limits_{j=1}^{\infty}t_{j}=1
and at most
2^{n-1}-1
of
t_{j}
are non-zero.
Denote
x=y_{n}-s=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{\infty}c_{j}e_{j}
. Then
s
has at most
2^{n-1}-1
non-zero coordinates and
y_{n}
has exactly
2^{n}-1
equal to
\frac{1}{2^{n}-1}
, so at least
2^{n-1}
coordinates of
x
with numbers between
2^{n}
-th and
2^{n+1}-1
are equal to
\frac{1}{2^{n}-1}
.
Denoting
x_{k}=\sum\limits_{j=2^{k-1}}^{2^{k}-1}c_{j}e_{j}\in E_{k}
for
k\leqslant n
and
x_{\infty}=\sum\limits_{j=2^{n+1}}^{\infty}c_{j}e_{j}\in E
we get
x=\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n+1}x_{k}+x_{\infty}
. Note that
x_{k}\in G_{k-1},x_{\infty}\in G_{n+1}
. Now we are ready to bound
\|x\|
from below.

(3.4)

\|x\|=\left\|\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n+1}x_{k}+x_{\infty}\right\|\geqslant(1-\varepsilon)\left\|\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n+1}x_{k}\right\|=(1-\varepsilon)\left\|\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n}x_{k}+x_{n+1}\right\|.

(We used the
\varepsilon
-orthogonality of
G_{n+1}
to
E_{1}+E_{2}+\ldots E_{n+1}
and the fact that
x_{\infty}\in G_{n+1}
and
\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n+1}x_{k}\in E_{1}+E_{2}+\ldots+E_{n+1}
).
Remark that
\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n}x_{k}\in E_{1}+E_{2}+\ldots+E_{n}
, and
x_{n+1}\in G_{n}
, which is
\varepsilon
-orthogonal to
E_{1}+E_{2}+\ldots+E_{n}
, so we may apply Lemma
3.12
:

\left\|\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n}x_{k}+x_{n+1}\right\|\geqslant\frac{1-\varepsilon}{2-\varepsilon}\|x_{n+1}\|=\frac{1}{3}\|x_{n+1}\|,

which together with (
3.4
) gives the estimate

(3.5)

\|x\|\geqslant\frac{1}{6}\|x_{n+1}\|.

It remains to bound from below the norm of
x_{n+1}=\sum\limits_{j=2^{n}}^{2^{n+1}-1}c_{j}e_{j}\in E_{n+1}
.

\displaystyle\|x_{n+1}\|

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{1}{\|T_{n+1}^{-1}\|}\cdot\|T_{n+1}^{-1}x_{n+1}\|>\frac{1}{2}\cdot\left\|T_{n+1}^{-1}\left(\sum\limits_{j=2^{n}}^{2^{n+1}-1}c_{j}e_{j}\right)\right\|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left\|\sum\limits_{j=2^{n}}^{2^{n+1}-1}c_{j}\cdot T_{k}^{-1}e_{j}\right\|=\frac{1}{2}\left\|\sum\limits_{j=2^{n}}^{2^{n+1}-1}c_{j}\cdot\frac{\hat{e}_{j-2^{n-1}+1}}{\tau_{j}}\right\|=\frac{1}{2}\sum\limits_{j=2^{n}}^{2^{n+1}-1}|c_{j}|\cdot\frac{1}{|\tau_{j}|}.

Plugging in the result into
(
3.5
)
and taking into account that
|\tau_{j}|\leqslant 1
, we obtain:

\|x\|\geqslant\frac{1}{6}\cdot\|x_{n+1}\|\geqslant\frac{1}{6}\cdot\frac{1}{2}\cdot\sum\limits_{k=2^{n}}^{2^{n+1}-1}|c_{k}|=\frac{1}{12}\cdot\sum\limits_{k=2^{n}}^{2^{n+1}-1}|c_{k}|

We know that at least
2^{n-1}
of
c_{j}
are equal to
\frac{1}{2^{n}-1}
. Replace the rest with
0
:

\|x\|\geqslant\frac{1}{12}\cdot 2^{n-1}\cdot\frac{1}{2^{n}-1}\geqslant\frac{1}{12}\cdot\frac{2^{n-1}}{2^{n}}=\frac{1}{24}

Thus
\rho_{H}(S_{n},A)
is bounded below by
\frac{1}{24}
and therefore
S_{n}
do not converge to
A
.
∎

4.
The compact case

Definition 4.1
.

A Banach space
X
is said to have the
approximation property
, if for every compact subset
A\subset X
and every
\varepsilon>0
there exists a finite-rank operator
P:X\to X
such that
\|Px-x\|<\varepsilon
for every
x\in A
.

We refer to
[
19
, Chapter 4]
for basic examples of spaces with the approximation property.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
X
be a Banach space that satisfies the approximation property.
Let
F:[0,1]\to{\rm b}{(X)}
be a multifunction such that
{\rm conv}F
is Riemann integrable with compact integral (that is,
A=\int\limits_{0}^{1}{\rm conv}F(t){\rm dt}
is compact). Then
F
is also Riemann integrable.

Proof.

Choose an
\varepsilon>0
.
Since
A
is compact, there exists a finite rank operator
P
such that
\|x-Px\|<\varepsilon
for all
x\in A
. Since
A=\int\limits_{0}^{1}{{\rm conv}F(t)}{\rm dt}
, there is
\delta_{1}>0
such that for all tagged partitions
T
of
[0,1]
, with
d(T)<\delta_{1}
the inequality
\rho_{H}(S({\rm conv}F,T),A)<\varepsilon
holds true.
Combining Theorem
1.6
, Proposition
2.1
and convexity of
A
we obtain that multifunction
{\rm conv}(P\circ F)
is integrable and

\int\limits_{0}^{1}{\rm conv}(P\circ F)(t){\rm dt}={\overline{{\rm conv}}}(\overline{P(A)})=\overline{P(A)}.

Now, using Theorem
3.14
in the finite-dimensional space
P(X)
, we obtain that
P\circ F
is also integrable and
\int\limits_{0}^{1}P(F(t)){\rm dt}=\overline{P(A)}
.
Therefore there exists
\delta_{2}>0
such that for all tagged partitions
T
of
[0,1]
, with
d(T)<\delta_{2}
the inequality
\rho_{H}(S(P\circ F,T),P(A))<\varepsilon
holds true.

Denote
Q=1-P
. By the same reason as above, one can find a constant
\delta_{3}>0
such that
\rho_{H}(S(Q({\rm conv}F),T),Q(A))<\varepsilon
for every tagged partition
T
of
[0,1]
, with
d(T)<\delta_{3}
.

Let
\delta=\min\{\delta_{1},\delta_{2},\delta_{3}\}
.
Fix tagged partition
T
with
d(T)<\delta
and denote
S=S(F,T)
and
\widetilde{S}=S({\rm conv}F,T)={\rm conv}(S)
. By the choice of
\delta
, we have that

\rho_{H}({\rm conv}(S),A)<\varepsilon,\rho_{H}(P(S),P(A))<\varepsilon,\rho_{H}(Q(\widetilde{S}),Q(A))=\rho_{H}({\rm conv}(Q(S)),Q(A))<\varepsilon

at once. Also we know that
\|x-Px\|=\|Qx\|<\varepsilon
for all
x\in A
, so
Q(A)\subset\varepsilon B_{X}
.
Since
\rho_{H}({\rm conv}(Q(S)),Q(A))<\varepsilon
, for every
x\in{\rm conv}(Q(S))
(and in particular for every
x\in Q(S)
) there exists
v\in Q(A)
with
\|x-v\|<\varepsilon
. Therefore
\|x\|\leqslant\|x-v\|+\|v\|<2\varepsilon
. Consequently, for
x\in Q(S)
,
y\in Q(A)

\|x-y\|\leqslant\|x\|+\|y\|<3\varepsilon.

Now let us demonstrate that
\rho_{H}(S,A)<4\varepsilon
.

Indeed, for every
s\in S
we can approximate up to
\varepsilon
the element
Ps\in P(S)
by an element of
P(A)
. In other words, there is
a_{0}\in A
such that
\|Ps-Pa_{0}\|<\varepsilon
. Then

\|s-a_{0}\|=\|(Ps+Qs)-(Pa_{0}+Qa_{0})\|\leqslant\|Ps-Pa_{0}\|+\|Qs-Qa_{0}\|<\varepsilon+3\varepsilon<4\varepsilon.

The same way, approximating for arbitrary
a\in A
the corresponding
Pa\in P(A)
by an element of
P(S)
we obtain
s_{0}\in S
with
\|Ps_{0}-Pa\|<\varepsilon
. Then

\|s_{0}-a\|=\|(Ps_{0}+Qs_{0})-(Pa+Qa)\|\leqslant\|Ps_{0}-Pa\|+\|Qs_{0}-Qa\|<\varepsilon+3\varepsilon<4\varepsilon.

Summarizing, for every
\varepsilon>0
we have found a
\delta>0
such that
\rho_{H}(S(F,T),A)<4\varepsilon
for all tagged partitions
T
with
d(T)<\delta
. This means that
\int\limits_{0}^{1}F(t){\rm dt}=A
.
∎

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
X
be a Banach space. Let
F:[0,1]\to{\rm b}{(X)}
be a multifunction such that its convex hull is Riemann integrable and
A=\int\limits_{0}^{1}{\rm conv}F(t){\rm dt}
is compact. Then the multifunction
F
is also Riemann integrable (and, thanks to Corollary
2.5
,
\int\limits_{0}^{1}F(t){\rm dt}=A
).

Proof.

Consider
K=\overline{B(X^{*})}
in the weak
∗
topology. By the Banach-Alaoglu theorem,
K
is compact. Define a map
U:X\to C(K)
:

(U(x))(x^{*})=x^{*}(x).

U
is linear,
\|Ux\|=\sup_{x\in K}|x^{*}(x)|=\sup_{x\in B_{X}}|x^{*}(x)|=\|x\|
for all
x\in X
, therefore
U
is an isometric embedding.
According to
[
19
, example 4.2]
,
C(K)
possesses the approximation property. Thus, by the previous proposition, our Theorem is true in
C(K)
. Consequently, the Theorem is true for every closed linear subspace of
C(K)
, in particular, it is true in
U(X)
, which is just an isometric copy of
X
.
∎

Since the closed limit of a sequence of precompacts is compact, we arrive at the following fact:

Corollary 4.4
.

Let
X
be a Banach space. Let
F:[0,1]\to{\rm b}{(X)}
be a multifunction with precompact values such that its convex hull is Riemann integrable. Then the multifunction
F
is also Riemann integrable.
