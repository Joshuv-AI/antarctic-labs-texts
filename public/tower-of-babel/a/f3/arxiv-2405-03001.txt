Old and new powerful tools for the normal ordering problem and noncommutative binomials
Kei Beauduin

Abstract
In this paper, we derive formal general formulas for noncommutative exponentiation and the exponential function, while also revisiting an unrecognized, and yet powerful theorem. These tools are subsequently applied to derive counterparts for the exponential identity e A + B = e A ​ e B e^{A+B}=e^{A}e^{B} and the binomial theorem ( A + B ) n = ∑ ( n k ) ​ A k ​ B n − k (A+B)^{n}=\sum\binom{n}{k}A^{k}B^{n-k} when the commutator [ B , A ] [B,A] is either an arbitrary quadratic polynomial or a monomial in A A or B B . Analogous formulas are found when the commutator is bivariate. Furthermore, we introduce a novel operator bridging between the normal and antinormal ordered forms.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be an associative
\mathbb{K}

\mathbb{K}

\mathbb{R}

\mathbb{C}

B

(AB)^{n}=A^{n}B^{n}=B^{n}A^{n}
. A less trivial but iconic normal ordering result in the case of commutativity is the normal ordering of a binomial
(A+B)^{n}
, also known as
Newton’s binomial theorem

(A+B)^{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}A^{k}B^{n-k}.

where
\binom{n}{k}\coloneqq\frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}
is the
binomial coefficient
. However, the process of normal ordering when the variables do not commute is known to be a challenging subject, as the findings are often only partial results. Numerous contributions addressing this topic are scattered throughout the literature (see, e.g.,

Normal ordering is predominantly studied in the context of univariate commutators, as the existence of a normal ordered form may be uncertain in the bivariate case (as discussed in

e^{A+B}=e^{A}e^{B},\qquad e^{AB}=(e^{A})^{B},

Preliminaries

2.1
Stirling numbers

We will denote the
falling factorial
by the Pochhammer symbol
(x)_{n}\coloneqq x(x-1)\ldots(x-n+1)
and define the
Stirling numbers of the first kind

\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}
(and its signed version
s(n,k)
) by

(x)_{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}(-1)^{n-k}x^{k}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}s(n,k)x^{k},

(2)

and the
Stirling number of the second kind

\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}
by
[
19
, Chapter 26.8]

x^{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}(x)_{k}.

(3)

Since these numbers are defined as coefficients of an expression on some basis, if
k
is an integer different from
0,\ldots,n
, then
\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}=\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}=0
.
The well-known Stirling generating functions are

\frac{(-\log(1-x))^{k}}{k!}=\sum_{n=k}^{\infty}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}\frac{x^{n}}{n!},

(4)

and

\frac{(e^{x}-1)^{k}}{k!}=\sum_{n=k}^{\infty}\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}\frac{x^{n}}{n!}.

(5)

Furthermore, the closed-form formula for the Stirling numbers of the second kind is as follows:

\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}=\frac{1}{k!}\sum_{i=0}^{k}\binom{k}{i}(-1)^{k-i}i^{n}.

(6)

2.2
Exponentiation

By generalizing the binomial coefficient

\binom{x}{n}\coloneqq\frac{(x)_{n}}{n!},

(7)

we can define, for
A,B\in\mathcal{A}
, the following formal power series

A^{B}\coloneqq\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(A-1)^{n}\binom{B}{n}.

(8)

Since we did not require the algebra to be commutative, in general, the form in which the power series is usually written is not true

A^{B}\neq\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\binom{B}{n}(A-1)^{n}.

The convention we have adopted may not be ideal in all situations however, it offers the advantage of readability, ensuring that the reading order of
A
and
B
in
A^{B}
aligns with the product order of its formula.

Remark 1
.

For most elements of most nonformal algebras, this series diverges (similar to divergence in
\mathbb{C}
). It should also diverge for
B=x
when
x
is negative and
A
is not invertible in
\mathcal{A}
. However, any representation of exponentiation that does converge in
\mathcal{A}
should satisfy
equation

8
as a valid formula when convergence occurs. Consequently, these representations should also exhibit the same properties as the one we will demonstrate below.

Proposition 2.1
.

For
A,B,C\in\mathcal{A}
satisfying
[A,B]=[B,C]=0

•

A^{B}A^{C}=A^{B+C}

•

(A^{B})^{C}=A^{BC}
.

Proof
.

We are able to perform the Cauchy product because
A
commutes with
B
and Chu-Vandermonde’s convolution because
B
commutes with
C

A^{B}A^{C}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(A-1)^{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{B}{k}\binom{C}{n-k}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(A-1)^{n}\binom{B+C}{n}=A^{B+C}.

Now, it is easy to deduce that for positive integers
n

(A^{B})^{n}=A^{B}A^{B}\cdots A^{B}=A^{B+B}\cdots A^{B}=\ldots=A^{nB},

which we can use to fully prove the second point

\displaystyle(A^{B})^{C}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}(A^{B}-1)^{m}\binom{C}{m}=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{m}\binom{m}{k}(-1)^{m-k}(A^{B})^{k}\binom{C}{m}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{m}\binom{m}{k}(-1)^{m-k}A^{kB}\binom{C}{m}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{m}\binom{m}{k}(-1)^{m-k}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(A-1)^{n}\binom{kB}{n}\binom{C}{m},

then we use
equation

2

\displaystyle(A^{B})^{C}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(A-1)^{n}}{n!}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{m}\binom{m}{k}(-1)^{m-k}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{n}s(n,j)(kB)^{j}\right)\binom{C}{m}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(A-1)^{n}}{n!}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\sum_{j=0}^{n}s(n,j)B^{j}\binom{C}{m}\sum_{k=0}^{m}\binom{m}{k}(-1)^{m-k}k^{j}.

In the inner sum,
equation

6
and (
3
) lead to

\displaystyle\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{(C)_{m}}{m!}\sum_{k=0}^{m}\binom{m}{k}(-1)^{m-k}k^{j}=\sum_{n=0}^{j}(C)_{m}\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{j}{m}=C^{j}.

The commutativity of
B
and
C
allows us to write
B^{j}C^{j}=(BC)^{j}
. Finally, applying
equation

2
once again, we arrive at

(A^{B})^{C}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(A-1)^{n}}{n!}\sum_{j=0}^{n}s(n,j)B^{j}C^{j}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(A-1)^{n}}{n!}(BC)_{n}=A^{BC}.

The following proposition can be seen as a kind of notation trick, facilitated by our definition of exponentiation, to express this infinite sum more succinctly. It also enables us to utilize the properties we have just proven.

Proposition 2.2
.

For
A,B\in\mathcal{A}

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!}A^{n}B^{n}=(e^{A})^{B}.

Proof
.

We make use of
equation

5
, exchange the sums and conclude with
equation

3

\displaystyle(e^{A})^{B}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(e^{A}-1)^{k}\binom{B}{k}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\sum_{n=k}^{\infty}\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}\frac{A^{n}}{n!}(B)_{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{A^{n}}{n!}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}(B)_{k}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{A^{n}}{n!}B^{n}.

Example 2.1
.

In the algebra of linear transformations over analytic functions, the most important operators are
x
and
D
, where
\text{x}f(x)\coloneqq xf(x)
and
Df(x)\coloneqq f^{\prime}(x)
, where
f^{\prime}
denotes the derivative of
f
. The product of the algebra is composition, and the exponents thus represent repeated self-composition. Using
Proposition

2.2
and Taylor’s formula, we can express the generalization of the
shift
map
f(x)\mapsto f(x+g(x))
in this manner:

f(x+g(x))=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{g(x)^{n}}{n!}f^{(n)}(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{g(\text{x})^{n}}{n!}D^{n}f(x)=(e^{g(\text{x})})^{D}f(x),

(9)

which appears to be new.
Equation

9
can also be seen as a
composition operator
, indeed

(e^{g(\text{x})-\text{x}})^{D}f(x)=f(g(x)).

(10)

2.3
The commutator and the
\mathfrak{L}
operator

The commutator
[B,A]=BA-AB
is a bilinear anticommutative operation that indicates the failure of commutativity between two elements of
\mathcal{A}
. These two properties combined allow for many manipulations that we will implicitly use throughout:

[A,B]=[A,A+B]=-[B,A]=[-B,A]=[B,-A].

The commutator yields the following simple identity

[B,A]=(A+B)^{2}-A^{2}-2AB-B^{2},

(11)

due to the noncommutative expansion
(A+B)^{2}=A^{2}+AB+BA+B^{2}
. The following lemma, reminiscent of the chain rule, is a classical result in normal ordering.

Lemma 2.1
.

If
f
is a power series and
[[B,A],A]=0
then

[B,f(A)]=[B,A]f^{\prime}(A).

Proof
.

First, we prove the result for monomials, and we see that the formula holds for
f(A)=1
and
f(A)=A
. We suppose for
n>0

BA^{n}=A^{n}B+n[B,A]A^{n-1},

now we multiply by
A
to the right

\displaystyle BA^{n+1}

\displaystyle=A^{n}BA+n[B,A]A^{n}

\displaystyle=A^{n}(AB+[B,A])+n[B,A]A^{n}

\displaystyle=A^{n+1}B+(n+1)[B,A]A^{n},

that is,
[B,A^{n+1}]=(n+1)[B,A]A^{n}
. Due to the linearity of all operations, the extension to power series is easily obtained.

The
adjoint
map is a derivation simply defined by
\ad_{B}(A)\coloneqq[B,A]
. With this definition in place, we can express the analogue of the Leibniz rule below, along with its antinormal form.

Lemma 2.2
.

If
A,B\in\mathcal{A}
then for all natural integers
n

B^{n}A=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}\ad_{B}^{k}(A)B^{n-k}\quad\mathbin{\textnormal{and }}\quad AB^{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}B^{n-k}(-\ad_{B})^{k}(A).

The proofs are omitted because they can readily be accomplished by induction. We are now prepared to demonstrate the main theorem of this section.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
p
be a power series and
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
be two algebras generated by
A,B
and
C,D
, respectively, satisfying
[B,A]=p(A)
and
[D,C]=p(D)
. Let
X\in\mathcal{A}
be uniquely written in normal-ordered form as

X=\sum_{i,j}x_{i,j}A^{i}B^{j}.

If we define the map
\mathfrak{L}:\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
by

\mathfrak{L}(X)\coloneqq\sum_{i,j}x_{i,j}C^{j}D^{i},

then

•

\mathfrak{L}
is linear

•

For
X,Y\in\mathcal{A}
,
\mathfrak{L}(XY)=\mathfrak{L}(Y)\mathfrak{L}(X)

•

For a power series
f
,
\mathfrak{L}(f(X))=f(\mathfrak{L}(X))
.

This theorem proves particularly valuable in the context of obtaining the antinormal form for normal-ordered expressions. We will illustrate this significance in
Section

5
.

Proof
.

The linearity property follows from the unique decomposition into the normal ordered form. To establish the other property, we first express
Y
in normal ordered form as
Y=\sum_{k,\ell}y_{k,\ell}A^{k}B^{\ell}
. Next, we replicate this process for
XY
using
Lemma

2.2
:

XY=\sum_{i,j,k,\ell}x_{i,j}y_{k,\ell}A^{i}B^{j}A^{k}B^{\ell}=\sum_{i,j,k,\ell}x_{i,j}y_{k,\ell}A^{i}\sum_{r=0}^{j}\binom{j}{r}\ad_{B}^{r}(A^{k})B^{j-r}B^{\ell}.

By repeatedly applying
Lemma

2.1
, we deduce that
\ad_{B}^{r}(A^{k})
is a power series in
A
, confirming that the expression above is indeed a normal ordered form. With this understanding and as we want to apply
\mathfrak{L}
, we now focus on elucidating the term
\mathfrak{L}(A^{i}\ad_{B}^{r}(A^{k})B^{j-r+\ell})
. Given that
[-C,D]=p(D)
is analogous to
[B,A]=p(A)
, it is straightforward to see that this term simplifies to
C^{j-r+\ell}D^{i}\ad_{-C}^{r}(D^{k})
. Thus, after applying
\mathfrak{L}
, we arrive at

\mathfrak{L}(XY)=\sum_{i,j,k,\ell}x_{i,j}y_{k,\ell}\sum_{r=0}^{j}\binom{j}{r}C^{j-r+\ell}D^{i}\ad_{-C}^{r}(D^{k}).

The above can be simplified using the simple fact that
\ad_{-C}=-\ad_{C}
and the second formula of
Lemma

2.2
:

\displaystyle\mathfrak{L}(XY)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j,k,\ell}x_{i,j}y_{k,\ell}C^{\ell}\sum_{r=0}^{j}\binom{j}{r}C^{j-r}(-\ad_{C})^{r}(D^{k})D^{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j,k,\ell}x_{i,j}y_{k,\ell}C^{\ell}D^{k}C^{j}D^{i}=\left(\sum_{k,\ell}y_{k,\ell}C^{\ell}D^{k}\right)\left(\sum_{i,j}x_{i,j}C^{j}D^{i}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{L}(Y)\mathfrak{L}(X),

as desired. Inducting on
n
, we then derive
\mathfrak{L}(X^{n})=\mathfrak{L}(X)^{n}
, which readily extends to power series due to the linearity of
\mathfrak{L}
.

Example 2.2
.

In the very special case where the power series
p
reduces to a constant
c
, we can simplify our choice for
C
and
D
by setting them to
A
and
B
respectively. The validity of the choice is ensured by
[B,A]=p(A)=p(B)=c
. We can opt for
A=\text{x}
and
B=D
(where, here,
D
is the derivative) since, in this context,
c=1
. This choice leads to the intriguing formula for the composition operator of
Example

2.1
:

\mathfrak{L}((e^{g(\text{x})-\text{x}})^{D})=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!}\mathfrak{L}((g(\text{x})-\text{x})^{n}D^{n})=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!}\text{x}^{n}(g(D)-D)^{n}=(e^{\text{x}})^{g(D)-D},

which, according to
[
5
, Proposition 21]
, is precisely an
umbral operator
when
g
represents power series with a compositional inverse.

2.4
Implications of the Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff formula

The
Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff formula
(BCH) is the solution for
Z
of the equation
e^{X}e^{Y}=e^{Z}
for non necessarily commuting
X,Y\in\mathcal{A}
, which takes the form of a (formal)
Lie series
, i.e., a series in iterated commutators of
X
and
Y
. Later discovered,
Dynkin’s formula
(BCHD) explicitly expresses the BCH. A clear exposition and proof of the BCHD are given in
[
18
]
.

Theorem 2.2

(Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff-Dynkin formula)
.

For
X,Y\in\mathcal{A}
,

\log(e^{X}e^{Y})=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k-1}}{k}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{1}+n_{1}>0\\[-4.0pt]
\vdots\\
m_{k}+n_{k}>0\end{subarray}}\frac{\overbrace{[X,[\ldots,[X}^{m_{1}},\overbrace{[Y,[\ldots,[Y}^{n_{1}},\overset{\begin{subarray}{c}\dots\\[8.0pt]
\end{subarray}}{[\ldots}\overbrace{[X,[\ldots,[X}^{m_{k}},\overbrace{[Y,[\ldots,[Y}^{n_{k}},[\ldots]\ldots]}{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}(m_{i}+n_{i})\right)\left(\prod_{i=1}^{k}m_{i}!\cdot n_{i}!\right)},

with the understanding that
[Y]\coloneqq Y
.

The reader should keep in mind that many terms in the expansion are zeros, for example, when the term
[Y,Y]
appears.

Example 2.3
.

The first few terms are

\displaystyle\log(e^{X}e^{Y})

\displaystyle=X+Y+\frac{1}{2}[X,Y]+\frac{1}{12}([X,[X,Y]]+[Y[Y,X]])-\frac{1}{24}[Y,[X,[X,Y]]]

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{720}([Y,[Y,[Y,[Y,X]]]]+[X,[X,[X,[X,Y]]]])

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{360}([X,[Y,[Y,[Y,X]]]]+[Y,[X,[X,[X,Y]]]])

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{120}([Y,[X,[Y,[X,Y]]]]+[X,[Y,[X,[Y,X]]]])

\displaystyle+\ldots

The BCH aids in deriving the following important existence lemma for noncommutative analogues of the first exponential identity of
equation

1
(EI for short).

Lemma 2.3
.

[B,A]
is independent of
B
if and only if there exists a formal series
\Psi_{A}:\mathbb{K}\to\mathcal{A}
depending on
A
and satisfying
\Psi_{A}(0)=1
and
\Psi^{\prime}_{A}(0)=A
, such that for all
t\in\mathbb{K}

e^{(A+B)t}=\Psi_{A}(t)e^{Bt}.

(12)

This lemma also ensures the existence of the binomial theorem in this special case. Indeed, the formula can be retrieved after applying the Cauchy product formula and identifying the coefficients of
t^{n}
.

Proof
.

(
\Rightarrow
)

We know that

[A+B,-B]=[B,A].

So using
Lemma

2.1
, we see that any combination of commutators of
A+B
and
-B
are functions of
A
.
Thus, by the BCHD, we have

\log(e^{(A+B)t}e^{-Bt})=(A+B)t-Bt+\ldots=At+\ldots,

where the dots are the commutator terms of higher order in
t
that are independent of
B
(see
Example

2.3
).
Thus we can write
e^{(A+B)t}=e^{At+\ldots}e^{Bt}
, and setting
\Psi_{A}(t)=e^{At+\ldots}
, we notice that
\Psi_{A}
has the properties we need.

(
\Leftarrow
)

Taking the second derivative at
t=0
in the equality (
12
), we arrive at

(A+B)^{2}=\Psi_{A}^{\prime\prime}(0)+2\Psi_{A}^{\prime}(0)B+\Psi_{A}(0)B^{2}=\Psi_{A}^{\prime\prime}(0)+2AB+B^{2},

and by using
equation

11
, we obtain
[B,A]=\Psi_{A}^{\prime\prime}(0)-A^{2}
, which is independent of
B
.

We can express the EI of
Lemma

2.3
in various equivalent forms, which enables us to extend the formula we know (and prove in the following sections) to other similar cases.

Proposition 2.3
.

If

\displaystyle[B,A]=f(A)

\displaystyle\implies e^{(A+B)t}=\Psi_{A}(t)e^{Bt}

then the following are also true

i.

Symmetry

\displaystyle[B,A]=f(B)

\displaystyle\implies e^{(A+B)t}=e^{At}\Psi_{B}(t)

ii.

Antinormal EI

\displaystyle[B,A]=-f(A)

\displaystyle\implies e^{(A+B)t}=e^{Bt}\Psi_{A}(t)

iii.

Special case of the BCH

\displaystyle[B,A]=-f(A+B)

\displaystyle\implies e^{At}e^{Bt}=\Psi_{A+B}(t).

Proof
.

For the symmetry, if
[B,A]=f(B)
then
[-A-B,B]=f(B)
. Thus, according to the hypothesis

e^{(B-A-B)t}=\Psi_{B}(t)e^{(-A-B)t}.

In other words,

e^{(A+B)t}=e^{At}\Psi_{B}(t).

The special case of the BCH is treated similarly by noticing that
[B,A]=-f(A+B)
is the same as
[-B,A+B]=f(A+B)
.
For the antinormal EI, we could just use the symmetry with
[A,B]=f(A)
. Alternatively, we can use the operator
\mathfrak{L}
of
Theorem

2.1
which was designed for this purpose, and make the following computation

\displaystyle\mathfrak{L}(e^{(A+B)t})

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{L}(\Psi_{A}(t)e^{Bt})

\displaystyle e^{\mathfrak{L}(A+B)t}

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{L}(e^{Bt})\mathfrak{L}(\Psi_{A}(t))

\displaystyle e^{(\mathfrak{L}(A)+\mathfrak{L}(B))t}

\displaystyle=e^{\mathfrak{L}(B)t}\Psi_{\mathfrak{L}(A)}(t)

\displaystyle e^{(C+D)t}

\displaystyle=e^{Dt}\Psi_{C}(t),

with
[C,D]=-f(D)
and
C
and
D
that we can freely rename to
A
and
B
respectively.

The following proposition is an additional general tool, for computing EIs, generalizing
Lemma

2.3
, that will prove useful later on.

Proposition 2.4
.

If
[B,A]
does not depend on
B
then for
\Psi_{A}
defined in
Lemma

2.3
, and for all
t,\lambda\in\mathbb{K}
, we have the following

e^{(\lambda A+B)t}=\Psi_{A}(t)^{\lambda}e^{Bt}.

Proof
.

We first prove the proposition for
\lambda=n\in\mathbb{N}
. Because
[(n-1)A+B,A]=[B,A]
does not depend on
B
, we have

e^{(nA+B)t}=e^{(A+(n-1)A+B)t}=\Psi_{A}(t)e^{((n-1)A+B)t}.

Repeating the process another
n-1
times, we recover the expected term
\Psi_{A}(t)^{n}e^{Bt}
.

Now to generalize this to any
\lambda
, we make a calculation similar to the proof of
Proposition

2.1
, where we first use the definition of exponentiation and the result above:

\Psi_{A}(t)^{\lambda}e^{Bt}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\binom{\lambda}{k}\sum_{j=0}^{k}\binom{k}{j}(-1)^{k-j}\Psi_{A}(t)^{j}e^{Bt}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\binom{\lambda}{k}\sum_{j=0}^{k}\binom{k}{j}(-1)^{k-j}e^{(jA+B)t}

Let us introduce the notation
\uline{A^{i}B^{j}}
which represents the sum of all possible product orders for
i

A

B

\uline{A^{2}B}=\uline{ABA}=\uline{BA^{2}}\coloneqq A^{2}B+ABA+BA^{2}
and the notation carries this property:
\uline{(\lambda A)^{i}(\mu B)^{j}}=\lambda^{i}\mu^{j}\uline{A^{i}B^{j}}
. We can now write the noncommutative expansion of a binomial as

(A+B)^{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\uline{A^{k}B^{n-k}},

thus

\displaystyle\Psi_{A}(t)^{\lambda}e^{Bt}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\binom{\lambda}{k}\sum_{j=0}^{k}\binom{k}{j}(-1)^{k-j}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{n}}{n!}\sum_{i=0}^{n}\uline{(jA)^{i}B^{n-i}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{n}}{n!}\sum_{i=0}^{n}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\binom{\lambda}{k}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{k}\binom{k}{j}(-1)^{k-j}j^{i}\right)\uline{A^{i}B^{n-i}}.

Using the closed form of the Stirling numbers of the second kind (
6

3
), we conclude the computation:

\displaystyle\Psi_{A}(t)^{\lambda}e^{Bt}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{n}}{n!}\sum_{i=0}^{n}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\binom{\lambda}{k}\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{i}{k}k!\right)\uline{A^{i}B^{n-i}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{n}}{n!}\sum_{i=0}^{n}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{i}\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{i}{k}(\lambda)_{k}\right)\uline{A^{i}B^{n-i}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{n}}{n!}\sum_{i=0}^{n}\lambda^{i}\uline{A^{i}B^{n-i}}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{n}}{n!}(\lambda A+B)^{n}

\displaystyle=e^{(\lambda A+B)t}.

3.1
The preliminary case

We will begin our investigation with what may appear as an arbitrary case: when

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
A
and
B
be two elements of
\mathcal{A}
and
\alpha
and
\varepsilon
two complex numbers such that
\alpha\neq 0
or
\varepsilon\neq 0
. Let
r,\rho
be the two roots of the polynomial
\alpha+\varepsilon X-X^{2}
.

Let
(u_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}},(v_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be two complex sequences defined by

\begin{cases}u_{0}=0,\,u_{1}=1\\
u_{n+2}=\varepsilon u_{n+1}+\alpha u_{n}\end{cases}\qquad\begin{cases}v_{0}=1,\,v_{1}=0\\
v_{n+2}=\varepsilon v_{n+1}+\alpha v_{n},\end{cases}

or equivalently by

u_{n}=\begin{cases}\dfrac{\rho^{n}-r^{n}}{\rho-r}&,\text{ if }r\neq\rho\\[8.0pt]
nr^{n-1}&,\text{ if }r=\rho\end{cases}\qquad\quad v_{n}=\begin{cases}\dfrac{\rho r^{n}-r\rho^{n}}{\rho-r}&,\text{ if }r\neq\rho\\[8.0pt]
(1-n)r^{n}&,\text{ if }r=\rho.\end{cases}

Then these statements are equivalent:

i.

[B,A]=\alpha+\varepsilon A-A^{2}

ii.

for
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

(A+B)^{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}\left(v_{k}+u_{k}A\right)B^{n-k}

iii.

for
t\in\mathbb{K}
,

e^{(A+B)t}=\Phi_{A}(t,r,\rho)e^{Bt}.

We notice this elegant structure where a single equation is equivalent to a countably infinite number of equations, which in turn holds equivalence to an uncountably infinite number of equations.

Proof
.

The relationships between the coefficients and roots of a polynomial read
\alpha=-r\rho
and
\varepsilon=r+\rho
. We can employ these alongside the explicit expressions of
u_{n}
and
v_{n}
to derive the relationship

v_{n+1}=\alpha u_{n}\quad\mathbin{\textnormal{and }}\quad u_{n+1}=v_{n}+\varepsilon u_{n}.

We proceed to prove by induction (i
\Rightarrow
ii). Since the base case
n=0
is satisfied, let us assume that the property holds for
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

\displaystyle(A+B)^{n+1}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}(A+B)\left(v_{k}+u_{k}A\right)B^{n-k}

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}\left(v_{k}A+u_{k}A^{2}+v_{k}B+u_{k}(AB+\alpha+\varepsilon A-A^{2})\right)B^{n-k}

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}(u_{k}AB+v_{k}B+(v_{k}+\varepsilon u_{k})A+\alpha u_{k})B^{n-k},

where we split the sum and apply
(*)
to obtain

\displaystyle(A+B)^{n+1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}(u_{k}A+v_{k})B^{n+1-k}+\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}(u_{k+1}A+v_{k+1}B)B^{n-k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n+1}\left(\binom{n}{k}+\binom{n}{k-1}\right)\left(v_{k}+u_{k}A\right)B^{n+1-k},

and finally, utilizing Pascal’s identity, we conclude the induction.

For the proof of (ii
\Rightarrow
i), first we compute

(A+B)^{2}=(v_{0}+u_{0}A)B^{2}+2(v_{1}+u_{1}A)B+v_{2}+u_{2}A=B^{2}+2AB+\alpha+\varepsilon A,

then we make use of
equation

11
to obtain
[B,A]=\alpha+\varepsilon A-A^{2}
, as expected.

For (iii
\Rightarrow
ii), we notice that by the explicit formula for
(u_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(v_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, we precisely have

\Phi_{A}(t,r,\rho)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{n}}{n!}(v_{n}+u_{n}A),

which is a Cauchy product with
e^{Bt}
and identification away from the desired result. Similarly, we derive (ii
\Rightarrow
iii) by summing the binomial theorem with the factor
t^{n}/n!
.

Mansour, Schork and Shattuck proved in

\mathfrak{S}_{0}(n,k)=\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k},\qquad\mathfrak{S}_{1}(n,k)=\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k},

Conclusion

In our research, we have developed exponentiation and general tools for exploring noncommutative algebra. This approach enabled us to derive exponential identities and certain binomial theorems in cases where the commutator
[B,A]=f(A)
takes the form of a polynomial of degree two or less. We also delved into the potential absence of a normal ordered form when the commutator involves two variables, providing illustrative examples to support our discussion.

We have shed light on an earlier theorem by Viskov
[
28
]
, which serves as a powerful tool for solving numerous problems in normal ordering. The antinormal form was proved via the new operator
\mathfrak{L}
. Viskov’s theorem facilitated a connection between the exponential identity in cases where
f
is a monomial of arbitrary degree and the normal ordered form of
(AB)^{n}
when
f
is one degree less, which was already documented
[
15
,
16
,
13
]
. This connection enabled the discovery of analogous formulas for both the exponential identity and the binomial theorem.

Further generalizing these findings by increasing the degree of the arbitrary polynomial
f
appears to be a challenging task, as discussed. Furthermore, exploring the case of a bivariate commutator remains an intriguing yet somewhat enigmatic area of study. Extending the work outlined in
Section

4
and in previous literature
[
21
,
11
,
24
,
25
,
8
