Trade-off between Information Gain and Disturbance in Local Discrimination of Entangled Quantum States
Youngrong Lim, Minki Hhan, Hyukjoon Kwon

Abstract
We establish an information gain-disturbance trade-off relation in local state discrimination. Our result demonstrates a fundamental limitation of local strategy to discriminate entangled quantum states without disturbance, which becomes more difficult as the entanglement of the states to be discriminated increases. For a set of maximally entangled states, the capability of local strategy is tightly suppressed, as random guessing without measurements saturates the bound provided by the trade-off relation. We also show that the trade-off can be circumvented when local operations are aided by pre-shared entanglement. To simultaneously achieve correct guessing of state and non-disturbance, an entirely different strategy from conventional state discrimination should be adopted to lower the cost of pre-shared entanglement. We explicitly propose an adaptive and non-destructive strategy based on the stabilizer formalism, which shows a strict advantage over conventional teleportation-based approaches in pre-shared entanglement cost for discriminating a set of maximally entangled states. As an application of the trade-off relation, we propose an entanglement certification protocol that is robust against depolarizing noise and generalize it to multipartite scenarios in a quantum network.



I
Introduction

Quantum state discrimination
Helstrom 1969
;
Holevo 1974
;
Barnett and Croke 2009
;
Bae and Kwek 2015
has served as a primitive for exploring the foundations of quantum theory, including the no-cloning theorem
Wootters and Zurek 1982
, nonlocality
Bennett et al. 1999
, and contextuality
Schmid and Spekkens 2018
.
In particular, when two physically separated parties are only allowed to perform local operations and classical communications (LOCC) to discriminate quantum states, often referred to as
local state discrimination
(LD), even orthogonal states cannot be definitively distinguished
Bennett et al. 1999
;
Ghosh et al. 2001
. Intriguing observations via LD have deepened our understanding of fundamental concepts in quantum theory, such as quantum nonlocality without entanglement
Bennett et al. 1999
and operational resource theory of imaginarity
Wu et al. 2021
.
Based on these observations, various applications in data hiding
Terhal et al. 2001
;
DiVincenzo et al. 2002
;
Eggeling and Werner 2002
;
Matthews et al. 2009
, secret sharing
Markham and Sanders 2008
;
Rahaman and Parker 2015
;
Banik et al. 2021
, and quantum error correction
Kribs et al. 2019
have been developed.

Nevertheless, characterizing the limitation of LD for a given set of states remains a non-trivial problem. Since there exists a set of product states that cannot be distinguished using LOCC, known as quantum nonlocality without entanglement
Bennett et al. 1999
, the entanglement of the states to be distinguished is not directly connected to the limitation of LD. Moreover, any two maximally entangled states (MESs) are perfectly distinguishable by LOCC, whenever they are orthogonal to each other
Walgate et al. 2000
. Despite these examples, extensive investigations have consistently demonstrated a prevailing trend that the ability of LOCC is highly restricted when discriminating entangled quantum states
Nathanson 2005
;
Yu et al. 2012
;
Nathanson 2013
;
Cosentino and Russo 2014
;
Li et al. 2015
;
Tian et al. 2016
;
Lugli et al. 2020
;
Yuan et al. 2022
. However, these results heavily rely on the choice of a set of states, leaving the complete understanding of their relationship with entanglement still open.

Meanwhile, quantum state discrimination necessarily involves measurements on the states to gain which-state information. The basic principle of quantum mechanics tells us that a quantum state can be disturbed by the impact of measurement. Hence, the state after the discrimination process can be different from the original input. Especially when it comes to local discrimination of entangled quantum states, disrupted entanglement after the discrimination process cannot be recovered by LOCC
Bennett et al. 1996
. Given the practical relevance of conserving and reusing entanglement in subsequent information processing
Kim et al. 2012
;
Kim et al. 2023
, it is important to understand an explicit trade-off relation between information gain and state disturbance. Although such trade-offs have been extensively studied from the fundamental and practical points of view
Fuchs and Peres 1996
;
Banaszek 2001
;
Buscemi and Sacchi 2006
;
Sacchi 2006
;
Buscemi et al. 2008
;
Kretschmann et al. 2008
;
Hong et al. 2022
, they have not been fully investigated in the context of state discrimination, except for specific cases: state discrimination without local strategies
Buscemi and Sacchi 2006
;
Skrzypczyk and Linden 2019
and LD with a strict constraint, such as entanglement preservation
Cohen 2007
.

In this manuscript, we establish a trade-off between distinguishability and disturbance in LD of entangled states. We introduce a score function that simultaneously captures the correct guessing probability and the state disturbance after the discrimination process. An upper bound on the score function is given in terms of the entanglement of states to be discriminated. This implies that more entanglement renders a tighter trade-off relation, i.e., correctly guessing the state without disturbance using LOCC becomes more difficult. For a set of MESs, the capability of LOCC is completely suppressed, as it cannot surpass random guessing without measurements. This significantly strengthens the restriction on LOCC beyond conventional LD, regardless of the structure of MESs
Nathanson 2005
.

The limitation of local operations in quantum state discrimination, as depicted by our trade-off relation, can be overcome by utilizing pre-shared entanglement
Cirac et al. 2001
;
Bandyopadhyay et al. 2009
;
Bandyopadhyay et al. 2015
;
Bandyopadhyay et al. 2016
.
For non-destructive local discrimination (NDLD) of orthogonal MESs,
we introduce an adaptive and fully non-destructive strategy that offers a strict advantage in pre-shared entanglement cost compared to the teleportation-based strategy, widely adopted in conventional LD
Bandyopadhyay et al. 2015
;
Bandyopadhyay and Russo 2021
.
Our strategy also has a benefit in conventional state discrimination. We demonstrate that for a specific set of MESs, adopting our strategy results in a net entanglement gain after state discrimination that is strictly positive, whereas it cannot be achieved by the conventional teleportation-based method. This provides a novel insight into reusing entanglement in subsequent information processing
Kim et al. 2023
, going beyond the catalytic usage of entanglement with zero gain
Yu et al. 2012
.

As a practical application of our trade-off relation, we construct an entanglement certification protocol, which can be readily extended to test quantum networks with multiple nodes. The proposed scheme can certify entanglement between distant parties even when classical communication is allowed and is robust against depolarizing noise.

Results

II.1
Guessing–disturbance trade-off in LD

Schematic of LD task (red dashed box) and the remaining quantum state (blue dashed box). The black (green) arrow represents quantum (classical) transmission. (a) Charlie chooses a state from the ensemble
\{p_{z},\left|\Psi_{z}\right>\}
and sends it to Alice and Bob. (b) Alice and Bob perform LOCC. (c) A classical answer
z_{\text{guess}}=f(\lambda)
from the local measurement outcomes determines the guessing score

.

In conventional LD
Barnett and Croke 2009
;
Bae and Kwek 2015
, the referee Charlie chooses a state from a set of
k
different quantum states
\{\left|\Psi_{z}\right>\}_{z=1}^{k}
with probability
p_{z}
and sends it to two local parties Alice and Bob (see Fig.
1
). Alice and Bob then guess the state by performing a measurement, described by a positive operator-valued measurement (POVM)
\Pi^{\lambda}
, satisfying
\Pi^{\lambda}\geq 0
and
\sum_{\lambda=1}^{N}\Pi^{\lambda}=1
with
N\geq k
. The probability to get an outcome
\lambda
for Charlie’s choice
z
is
p(\lambda|z)=\mbox{Tr}\left[\Pi^{\lambda}\rho_{z}\right]
with
\rho_{z}=\left|\Psi_{z}\right>\left<\Psi_{z}\right|
. For each measurement outcome
\lambda
, Alice and Bob guess the
z
with some guessing function
f(\lambda)\in\{1,2,\cdots,k\}
. The success probability of the correct guess then becomes

p_{\text{succ}}=\overline{G}=\sum_{z,\lambda}p(\lambda,z)G(\lambda,z),

(1)

which can be interpreted as an average of a score function
G(\lambda,z)=\delta_{f(\lambda),z}
over the joint distribution of
z
and
\lambda
,
p(\lambda,z)=p_{z}p(\lambda|z)
.
The success probability heavily depends on Alice and Bob’s ability to perform POVMs
\Pi^{\lambda}

Bennett et al. 1999
;
Ghosh et al. 2001
;
Yu et al. 2014
;
Bandyopadhyay et al. 2015
. The most generally adopted condition in LD is that Alice and Bob are only allowed to implement measurements that can be realized by LOCC
Barnett and Croke 2009
;
Bae and Kwek 2015
.

We extend this setup by considering not only the classical answer for guessing but also the quantum state after the measurements. For an outcome
\lambda
, the post-measurement state can be written as
\rho_{z}^{\lambda}=\frac{K^{\lambda}\rho_{z}K^{\lambda\dagger}}{p(\lambda|z)}
, where
K^{\lambda}
are Kraus operators composing the POVMs,
\Pi^{\lambda}=K^{\lambda\dagger}K^{\lambda}
. For each
\left|\Psi_{z}\right>
, state disturbance after the measurement with outcome
\lambda
is then captured by the fidelity between the initial state,
F(\lambda,z)=\left<\Psi_{z}\right|\rho^{\lambda}_{z}\left|\Psi_{z}\right>
. Consequently, the average fidelity after the discrimination can be expressed as
\overline{F}=\sum_{z,\lambda}p(\lambda,z)F(\lambda,z)
.

Figure 2:
(a) Illustration of the adaptive non-destructive scheme. In each round (green-dashed box), controlled-Pauli operations are performed in both local parties using a pre-shared Bell state
\left|\Phi^{0}\right>_{A^{\prime}B^{\prime}}
. From the sign information of
P_{A}P_{B}
, one can check whether the unambiguous determination of the state is possible so that the protocol ends. Otherwise, the next round of protocol proceeds for the sign of another stabilizer generator
P^{\prime}_{A}P^{\prime}_{B}
. This procedure continues until
z
is determined. (b) Average entanglement cost (in ebits) for randomly generated
10
-Bell pair states (local dimension
d=2^{10}
) as the number of states

k
varies. The cost of our strategy (see Appendix
D
) nearly saturates the lower bound
\log_{2}k
(black line).

In order to take into account both the correct guessing and disturbance, we introduce the total score

{\cal S}=\overline{G\cdot F}=\sum_{z,\lambda}p(\lambda,z)\delta_{f(\lambda),z}\left<\Psi_{z}\right|\rho^{\lambda}_{z}\left|\Psi_{z}\right>.

(2)

We note that the maximum value
{\cal S}=1
is achieved if and only if the correct guessing is possible without disturbing the state for every
z
.
Notably, we show a tight upper bound on the total score as follows:

Theorem 1
.

Suppose that
k
different bipartite states
\{\left|\Psi_{z}\right>_{AB}\}_{z=1}^{k}
with local dimensions
d_{A}
and
d_{B}
are distributed with probabilities
p_{z}
. For any separable operation in the form of
K^{\lambda}=K_{A}^{\lambda}\otimes K_{B}^{\lambda}
,

{\cal S}=\overline{G\cdot F}\leq d_{A}d_{B}\max_{z}\{p_{z}\xi_{z}^{4}\}\equiv{\cal B},

(3)

where
\xi_{z}
is the maximum Schmidt coefficient of
\left|\Psi_{z}\right>_{AB}
. Consequently, a trade-off between the average guessing probability and the average fidelity is given as

\overline{G}+\overline{F}\leq 1+{\cal B}.

(4)

The proof of the theorem can be found in Appendix
A
. As separable operations strictly include LOCC
Bennett et al. 1999
;
Chitambar et al. 2012
, the bound holds for any measurement using LOCC. Theorem
1
directly implies that perfect LD without disturbance is not possible for
{\cal B}<1
. We highlight that
{\cal B}
is directly related to the entanglement of the states to be discriminated, given in terms of the Schmidt coefficients
Aniello and Lupo 2009
;
Sperling and Vogel 2011
. In particular, Theorem
1
leads to a strong restriction on the local discrimination of MESs as follows:

Corollary 1.1
.

For equally distributed
k
different MESs with the same local dimension
d=d_{A}=d_{B}
, the following trade-off relation holds for any separable operation:

{\cal S}\leq\frac{1}{k}\Longrightarrow\overline{G}+\overline{F}\leq 1+\frac{1}{k}.

(5)

The trade-off relation states that any local measurement cannot simultaneously achieve perfect guessing and non-destructiveness for discriminating any MESs with
k\geq 2
. In particular, the capability of LOCC is completely suppressed when considering totally non-destructive discrimination, i.e.,
\overline{F}=1
, in which case
\overline{G}\leq 1/k
. This can be compared with the upper bound on the guessing probability in conventional state discrimination without considering state disturbance,
\overline{G}\leq d/k
, which scales in the local dimension
d

Nathanson 2005
. For example, any two orthogonal MESs (
k=2
) can be perfectly discriminated by local measurements
Walgate et al. 2000
, but our result implies that without disturbing the state, the guessing probability cannot exceed
1/2
. Furthermore, the bound in Eq. (
5
) tightly suppresses the capability of local measurements, as it can be saturated by random guessing without performing measurements. Our result also fully generalizes the previous studies on quantum delocalized interaction
Paige et al. 2020
;
Bilash et al. 2023
to be applied to any separable operations.

II.2
Pre-shared entanglement cost for perfect NDLD

While Theorem
1
and Corollary
1.1
state that local operations alone cannot perfectly discriminate orthogonal entangled states without disturbance, this limitation can be overcome aided by pre-shared entanglement. The most widely adopted strategy is using quantum teleportation to localize the target state to one of the parties, making perfect discrimination possible. This is proven to be the optimal strategy with the minimum entanglement cost in conventional LD for some cases
Bandyopadhyay et al. 2015
;
Bandyopadhyay and Russo 2021
. However, once teleportation is performed, the entanglement of the target state also vanishes. Thus, when taking into account the non-destructiveness condition, additional entanglement is required to reprepare the target state via LOCC after its correct guessing. This leads to the question of whether there exists a more efficient strategy than the teleportation to utilize pre-shared entanglement.

In this manuscript, we focus on the pre-shared entanglement cost for achieving perfect NDLD (
{\cal S}=1
) of
k
different orthogonal MESs. We derive a following lower bound on the entanglement cost:

Theorem 2
.

A perfect NDLD for equally distributed
k
different orthogonal MESs requires at least
\log_{2}k
ebits of pre-shared entanglement.

The lower bound also applies to the average entanglement cost when considering an adaptive strategy with different entanglement costs for each instance (see Appendix
B
).
We emphasize that the bound is independent in the local dimension
d
. This implies that a naïve teleportation of the entire local state might not be the optimal strategy when
d\gg k
, as it costs
2\log_{2}d
ebits including repreparation, resulting in a large gap between the lower bound.

Interestingly, we find an entirely non-destructive and adaptive strategy based on the stabilizer formalism
Gottesman 1997
that can be more efficient than the teleportation-based scheme in perfect NDLD. As an illustrative example, let us consider Bell states
\left|\Phi^{j}\right>_{AB}
with
j=0,1,2,3
in the standard Bell basis.
These four Bell states can be fully characterized by
(s_{X},s_{Z})
with
s_{X},s_{Z}\in\{+1,-1\}
based on the following relation:

\displaystyle X_{A}X_{B}\left|\Phi^{j}\right>

\displaystyle=s_{X}\left|\Phi^{j}\right>,

\displaystyle Z_{A}Z_{B}\left|\Phi^{j}\right>

\displaystyle=s_{Z}\left|\Phi^{j}\right>,

where
X_{A(B)}
and
Z_{A(B)}
are Pauli
X
and
Z
operators acting on the subsystem
A(B)
. For example,
\left|\Phi^{0}\right>=(\left|00\right>+\left|11\right>)/\sqrt{2}
satisfies
X_{A}X_{B}\left|\Phi^{0}\right>=(+1)\left|\Phi^{0}\right>
and
Z_{A}Z_{B}\left|\Phi^{0}\right>=(+1)\left|\Phi^{0}\right>
so that
(s_{X},s_{Z})=(+1,+1)
. In this case, we say
\left|\Phi^{0}\right>
is stabilized by a set of Pauli operators generated by
+X_{A}X_{B}
and
+Z_{A}Z_{B}
.
Similarly, the other Bell states are stabilized by a set of Pauli operators generated by
s_{X}X_{A}X_{B}
and
s_{Z}Z_{A}Z_{B}
with the different signs
(s_{X},s_{Z})
. Therefore, if we can determine the signs
s_{X}
and
s_{Z}
of the generators, we can specify the Bell state. This can be done in a non-destructive manner by using parity measurements introduced in the stabilizer quantum error correction codes
Gottesman 1997
. For example,
s_{Z}
can be determined by the measurement operator
\left\{\frac{\mathbb{1}\pm Z_{A}Z_{B}}{2}\right\}
.
If the Bell state
\left|\Phi^{j}\right>
is stabilized by
s_{Z}Z_{A}Z_{B}
, i.e.,
Z_{A}Z_{B}\left|\Phi^{j}\right>=s_{Z}\left|\Phi^{j}\right>
, then the probability of each outcome is given by
p_{\pm}=\left<\Phi^{j}\right|\frac{\mathbb{1}\pm Z_{A}Z_{B}}{2}\left|\Phi^{j}\right>=\frac{1\pm s_{Z}}{2}
. This results in a deterministic outcome with the same sign as
s_{Z}
, while the measurement is non-destructive, as the post-measurement state remains unchanged. The other sign,
s_{X}
, can be determined in similarly by taking the measurement operator
\left\{\frac{\mathbb{1}\pm X_{A}X_{B}}{2}\right\}
.

However, such a parity measurement is a nonlocal operator, which cannot be directly implemented via LOCC. Nevertheless, this can be done with the aid of a pre-shared Bell state
\left|\Phi^{0}\right>_{A^{\prime}B^{\prime}}
between the local parties. More precisely, one can apply a controlled unitary operator
U_{P_{A(B)}}=\mathbb{1}_{A(B)}\otimes\left|0\right>\left<0\right|_{A^{\prime}(B^{\prime})}+P_{A(B)}\otimes\left|1\right>\left<1\right|_{A^{\prime}(B^{\prime})}
for Pauli operators
P_{A(B)}=X_{A(B)}
or
P_{A(B)}=Z_{A(B)}
on both
AA^{\prime}
and
BB^{\prime}
followed by the measurements on the auxiliary systems
A^{\prime}
and
B^{\prime}
in
\{\left|\pm\right>=(\left|0\right>\pm\left|1\right>)/\sqrt{2}\}
basis. If both measurement outcomes are the same, the
AB
system is projected onto
\frac{\mathbb{1}+P_{A}P_{B}}{2}
, otherwise,
\frac{\mathbb{1}-P_{A}P_{B}}{2}
. Therefore,
1
ebit is consumed to determine each sign of generators, and the state after the measurement remains unchanged. We continue this procedure until the state is unambiguously deduced (see Fig.
2
(a) and Appendix
C
). Figure
2
(b) shows that for randomly generated
n
-Bell pairs
\left|\Phi^{j_{1}\cdots j_{n}}\right>_{AB}=\left|\Phi^{j_{1}}\right>_{A_{1}B_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\left|\Phi^{j_{n}}\right>_{A_{n}B_{n}}
with
j_{1},\cdots,j_{n}\in\{0,1,2,3\}
, the average entanglement cost of our strategy nearly saturates the lower bound in Theorem
2
(see Appendix
D
for the strategy).

To demonstrate a strict advantage of our scheme compared to the teleportation-based one, let us consider perfect NDLD of equally distributed three Bell states,
\{\left|\Phi^{0}\right>,\left|\Phi^{1}\right>,\left|\Phi^{2}\right>\}
. The signs
(s_{X},s_{Z})
distinguishing those Bell states are
\left|\Phi^{0}\right>:(+,+),\left|\Phi^{1}\right>:(-,+),\left|\Phi^{2}\right>:(+,-)
. We first determine the sign
s_{Z}
by consuming
1
ebit. When
s_{Z}=-1
, the state is deduced as
\left|\Phi^{2}\right>
and
the protocol terminates. When
s_{Z}=+1
,
we further check
s_{X}
to distinguish between
\left|\Phi^{0}\right>
and
\left|\Phi^{1}\right>
by consuming additional
1
ebit (i.e., total
2
ebits). The average entanglement cost of the overall process then becomes
\frac{1\times 1+2\times 2}{3}=\frac{5}{3}
ebits. This is strictly less than that of the teleportation-and-repreparation scheme, which costs a total of
2
ebits (
1
ebit each for the teleportation and repreparation). Such a gap arises as the stabilizer method can utilize the sign information more compactly by determining each sign adaptively, while the teleportation-based method can only access a pair of signs
(s_{X},s_{Z})
at once.

Furthermore, this gap is extendable by considering
n
-Bell pairs
\left|\Phi^{j_{1}\cdots j_{n}}\right>_{AB}

only with three indices for each
j_{i}\in\{0,1,2\}
. For a set of
n
-Bell pairs
\{\left|\Phi^{j_{1}\cdots j_{n}}\right>_{AB}\}
with all possible combinations of
(j_{1},\cdots,j_{n})
’s, i.e.,
k=3^{n}
, the entanglement cost of the stabilizer method is
5n/3
ebits, while teleportation-and-repreparation of each Bell-pair requires total
2n
ebits for the perfect NDLD. Hence, the gap
n/3
grows linearly by increasing the number of Bell pairs
n
(see Appendix
D
).

Our scheme can readily be generalized to multipartite entangled states. For example, we can show an advantage on the entanglement cost for perfect NDLD of GHZ states compared to the teleportation-based method (see Appendix
E
).

II.3
Entanglement earning from NDLD

We can show our protocol leads to an interesting implication even for conventional LD. This stems from the fact that one can reuse the entanglement during the discrimination process by utilizing the non-destructive nature of the protocol. More explicitly, let us consider the following
6
MESs,

\left\{\left|\Phi^{012}\right>,\left|\Phi^{021}\right>,\left|\Phi^{102}\right>,\left|\Phi^{120}\right>,\left|\Phi^{201}\right>,\left|\Phi^{210}\right>\right\}
.
The average entanglement cost for the perfect NDLD using the stabilizer method is
8/3
ebits, which is lower than the entanglement (3 ebits) of the target states (see Appendix
D
). In other words, a net gain of entanglement becomes strictly positive after the state discrimination, as one can reuse the Bell pair discriminated in the previous round for the next round of discrimination. This can be interpreted as
entanglement earning from discrimination
, further extending the notion of
entanglement discrimination catalysis

Yu et al. 2012
.

Although finding an exact condition for such phenomenon and computing the optimal entanglement cost are demanding, we note some lower bounds on the entanglement cost. In the above example, a lower bound on the average entanglement cost for the perfect NDLD is
\log_{2}6\approx 2.58
ebits from Theorem
2
, which is strictly smaller than that of the stabilizer method
8/3\approx 2.67
ebits.
However, we can show that the latter is the optimal cost under the stabilizer method:

Theorem 3
.

For equally distributed
k
orthogonal
n
-Bell pairs, a lower bound on the average entanglement cost for the perfect NDLD of the stabilizer method is
m+2-\frac{2^{m+1}}{k}
ebits,
where
m=\lfloor\log_{2}k\rfloor
.

Putting
k=6~(m=2)
, the cost of our strategy saturates the lower bound, 8/3 ebits. A proof of theorem
3
and comparison with a teleportation-based strategy are given in Appendix.
D
.

II.4
Application to entanglement certification

As a practical application, we discuss how our scheme can be utilized in entanglement certification. To do this, the referee, Charlie, first chooses one state among
k
orthogonal MESs, which can be done by performing a local unitary operation on any given MES.
Charlie then distributes the state to Alice and Bob. After the discrimination, each party returns the state to Charlie of the same dimension as the state they originally received. Then Charlie performs a projection measurement onto the initial state to evaluate the total score
{\cal S}
. When
{\cal S}>1/k
, entanglement between Alice and Bob is certified, as such a score cannot be achieved by LOCC from Corollary
1.1
. While transmission of entangled quantum states required in our protocol could be more demanding, it has some distinct advantages compared to other protocols based on nonlocality tests
Shalm et al. 2015
;
Bowles et al. 2018
that classical communication is fully allowed between local parties.

In a realistic situation, however, noisy communication channels and imperfect pre-shared entanglement can degrade the total score.
Nevertheless, for a local noise channel, the bound
{\cal S}>1/k
remains valid, as any local strategy including the local noise channel can still be represented by separable Kraus operators.
Specifically, let us consider a situation where depolarizing noises, i.e.,
{\cal E}_{\eta}(\rho)=(1-\eta)\rho+\eta\mathbb{1}/d^{2}
with
\eta\in[0,1]
, cause the MESs to evolve into Werner-like states
\rho_{\eta}^{i}={\cal E}_{\eta}(\left|\Phi^{i}\right>\left<\Phi^{i}\right|)=(1-\eta)\left|\Phi^{i}\right>\left<\Phi^{i}\right|+\eta\mathbb{1}/d^{2}
. Achievable total score for the noisy channel
{\cal E}_{\eta}
and noisy pre-shared entangled state
\rho^{0}_{\tau}
depicted in Fig.
3
shows the robustness of our scheme within a moderate range of parameters (for more details, see Appendix
F
). We note that when
k=d^{2}
, our protocol operates as long as the states evolved under the noise channel are entangled, i.e.,
\eta<d/(d+1)
. Conversely, when the channel is noiseless (
\eta=0
), our protocol can certify the entanglement of any entangled Werner-like states with
\tau<d/(d+1)
, which cannot be done by nonlocality tests
Werner 1989
;
Barrett 2002
;
Almeida et al. 2007
;
Oszmaniec et al. 2017
;
Hirsch et al. 2017
.

We extend this approach to multipartite scenarios. To certify entanglement between three nodes in a quantum network, the referee tests NDLD for
k
different GHZ states
(1/\sqrt{2})(\left|x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}\right>\pm\left|\bar{x}_{1}\bar{x}_{2}\bar{x}_{3}\right>)
, where
x_{i}\in\{0,1\}
and
\bar{x}=x+1~({\rm mod}~2)
. By noting that any multipartite LOCC is a subset of LOCC between the bipartition of the total system and any bipartition of GHZ states is maximally entangled with uniform Schmidt coefficients
1/\sqrt{2}
, the total score using LOCC is bounded above by
{\cal S}\leq 2/k
by Theorem
1
. Hence, choosing any three GHZ states is sufficient to certify entanglement by witnessing the total score higher than
2/3
. In contrast, without the state disturbance condition, some sets of up to four GHZ states can be perfectly discriminated by using LOCC
Hayashi et al. 2006
.

For a general
N
-node quantum network, we can adopt the absolutely maximally entangled (AME) states
Helwig et al. 2012
;
Helwig and Cui 2013
;
Goyeneche et al. 2015
, any bipartition of which leads to an MES. In particular, when
N
is even, the highest total score for
k
different AME states becomes
1/k
, which is a multiparty extension of Corollary
1.1
. This is because any multipartite local strategy for AME states is a subset of a bipartite local strategy for MESs with the same local dimension by taking the
N/2
node bipartition. We note that AME states exist for any number of parties with sufficiently high local dimension
Helwig and Cui 2013
(see also, Refs.
Scott 2004
;
Huber et al. 2018
for limitations in low dimensions) and can be systematically constructed
Helwig and Cui 2013
;
Raissi et al. 2018
;
Raissi et al. 2020
;
Raissi et al. 2022
.
Alternatively, we can exploit randomly generated stabilizer states, which approximately satisfy the AME property with high probability
Page 1993
;
Życzkowski and Sommers 2001
so that the bound on the total score slightly deviates from that of random guessing (see Appendix
G
).

III
Remarks

We have established a trade-off relation between guessing and disturbance in LD of entangled states. This imposes a strong restriction on LOCC, as it cannot perform better than random guessing for MESs, regardless of their choice. We have also presented a non-destructive and adaptive strategy for efficiently utilizing pre-shared entanglement to achieve perfect NDLD, surpassing the conventional strategy based on teleportation. Our results also provide practical applications to conventional state discrimination and entanglement certification.

From a thermodynamic perspective, the advantage of the proposed non-destructive strategy lies in overcoming the inherent irreversibility between measurement and repreparation of quantum states. In this direction, constructing a general formalism that precisely describes the difference between destructive and non-destructive strategies in terms of irreversibility will be an interesting future work. A possible approach is to analyze additional entanglement cost for the initial state repreparation, based on the irreversibility of entanglement concentration
Kumagai and Hayashi 2013
.

\displaystyle=\sum_{z=1}^{k}\sum_{\lambda}p_{z}p(\lambda|z)G(\lambda,z)F(\lambda,z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{z=1}^{k}p_{z}\sum_{\lambda}p(\lambda|z)\delta_{f(\lambda),z}\left<\Psi_{z}\right|\rho^{\lambda}_{z}\left|\Psi_{z}\right>

\displaystyle=\sum_{z=1}^{k}p_{z}\sum_{\lambda_{z}}\left|\left<\Psi_{z}\right|K^{\lambda_{z}}_{A}\otimes K^{\lambda_{z}}_{B}\left|\Psi_{z}\right>\right|^{2}

d_{A}
and

A

with

\displaystyle{\cal S}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{z=1}^{k}p_{z}\sum_{\lambda_{z}}\mbox{Tr}_{A}\left[K^{\lambda_{z}}_{A}K^{\lambda_{z}\dagger}_{A}\rho_{A,z}\right]\mbox{Tr}_{B}\left[K^{\lambda_{z}\dagger}_{B}K^{\lambda_{z}}_{B}\rho_{B,z}\right]

D.7

In this section, we show that the entanglement cost for the the perfect NDLD in the previous example, 8/3 ebits, is optimal for the stabilizer method. To do that, we provide a lower bound on the average entanglement cost of the stabilizer method for a given number of

We first note that for a given set of

Appendix E
Non-destructive local discrimination of GHZ states

Our strategy based on the stabilizer formalism for the perfect NDLD can be extended to multipartite scenarios. We illustrate this by investigating the pre-shared entanglement costs for the perfect NDLD of GHZ states, which can be determined by the signs of stabilizer generators

U_{P_{A}}\otimes U_{P_{B}}\left|\Psi_{z}\right>_{AB}(\mathbb{1}_{A^{\prime}}\otimes X_{B^{\prime}}^{i_{X}}Z_{B^{\prime}}^{i_{Z}})\left|\Phi^{0}\right>_{A^{\prime}B^{\prime}}\xrightarrow{\Pi^{(x,y)}_{A^{\prime}B^{\prime}}}\mathbb{1}_{A}\otimes P_{B}^{i_{X}}\frac{\mathbb{1}_{AB}+(-1)^{x+y+i_{Z}}P_{A}P_{B}}{2}\left|\Psi_{z}\right>_{AB},

\Pi_{A^{\prime}B^{\prime}}^{(x,y)}=H^{\otimes 2}\left(\Pi^{x}_{A^{\prime}}\otimes\Pi^{y}_{B^{\prime}}\right)H^{\otimes 2}
, and
\Pi^{x},\Pi^{y}
are the measurements on the computational basis with
x,y\in\{0,1\}
.
Suppose there are two states to be distinguished, such as
\left|\Psi_{0}\right>=\left|\Phi^{0}\right>
and
\left|\Psi_{1}\right>=\left|\Phi^{2}\right>
without loss of generality. In the noiseless case, we can distinguish them by measuring the parity of the stabilizer generator
Z_{A}Z_{B}
by using the pre-shared entanglement
\left|\Phi^{0}\right>_{A^{\prime}B^{\prime}}
. If the Pauli noise contaminates the states and pre-shared entanglement, however, the achievable total score is given by

\displaystyle{\cal S}_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{z=\{0,1\}}\sum_{(x,y)}\delta_{z,x\oplus y}\mbox{Tr}\left[{\cal N}\left(\left|\Psi_{z}\right>_{AB}\left<\Psi_{z}\right|\right)N\Pi^{(x,y)}_{A^{\prime}B^{\prime}}U_{P_{A}}\otimes U_{P_{B}}{\cal N}(\left|\Psi_{z}\right>_{AB}\left<\Psi_{z}\right|)\otimes{\cal N}(\left|\Phi^{0}\right>_{A^{\prime}B^{\prime}}\left<\Phi^{0}\right|)U^{\dagger}_{P_{A}}\otimes U^{\dagger}_{P_{B}}\Pi^{(x,y)}_{A^{\prime}B^{\prime}}\right]

(21)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{z=\{0,1\}}\sum_{(x,y)}\delta_{z,x\oplus y}N\mbox{Tr}\left[\sum_{ijk}q^{m}_{i_{X}i_{Z}}q^{s}_{j_{X}j_{Z}}q^{p}_{k_{X}k_{Z}}X^{i_{X}}Z^{i_{Z}}\left|\Psi_{z}\right>_{AB}\left<\Psi_{z}\right|Z^{i_{Z}}X^{i_{X}}\Pi^{(x,y)}_{A^{\prime}B^{\prime}}U_{P_{A}}\otimes U_{P_{B}}\right.

\displaystyle\left.\times(X^{j_{X}}Z^{j_{Z}}\left|\Psi_{z}\right>_{AB}\left<\Psi_{z}\right|Z^{j_{Z}}X^{j_{X}})\otimes(X^{k_{X}}Z^{k_{Z}}\left|\Phi^{0}\right>_{A^{\prime}B^{\prime}}\left<\Phi^{0}\right|Z^{k_{Z}}X^{k_{X}})U^{\dagger}_{P_{A}}\otimes U^{\dagger}_{P_{B}}\Pi^{(x,y)}_{A^{\prime}B^{\prime}}\right]

(22)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{z=\{0,1\}}\sum_{(x,y)}\delta_{z,x\oplus y}\mbox{Tr}\left[\sum_{ijk}q^{m}_{i_{X}i_{Z}}q^{s}_{j_{X}j_{Z}}q^{p}_{k_{X}k_{Z}}X^{i_{X}}Z^{i_{Z}}\left|\Psi_{z}\right>_{AB}\left<\Psi_{z}\right|Z^{i_{Z}}X^{i_{X}}Z_{B}^{k_{X}}\frac{\mathbb{1}_{AB}+(-1)^{x+y+k_{Z}}Z_{A}Z_{B}}{2}\right.

\displaystyle\left.\times X^{j_{X}}Z^{j_{Z}}\left|\Psi_{z}\right>_{AB}\left<\Psi_{z}\right|Z^{j_{Z}}X^{j_{X}}\frac{\mathbb{1}_{AB}+(-1)^{x+y+k_{Z}}Z_{A}Z_{B}}{2}Z_{B}^{k_{X}}\right]

(23)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{z=\{0,1\}}\sum_{(x,y)}\delta_{z,x\oplus y}\mbox{Tr}\left[\sum_{ijk}q^{m}_{i_{X}i_{Z}}q^{s}_{j_{X}j_{Z}}q^{p}_{k_{X}k_{Z}}X^{i_{X}}Z^{i_{Z}}\left|\Psi_{z}\right>_{AB}\left<\Psi_{z}\right|Z^{i_{Z}}X^{i_{X}}Z^{k_{X}}\left(\frac{1+e^{i\pi(x+y+z+k_{Z}+j_{X})}}{2}\right)\right.

\displaystyle\left.\times X^{j_{X}}Z^{j_{Z}}\left|\Psi_{z}\right>_{AB}\left<\Psi_{z}\right|Z^{j_{Z}}X^{j_{X}}Z^{k_{X}}\right]

(24)

\displaystyle=\sum_{z=\{0,1\}}\sum_{ijk}q^{m}_{i_{X}i_{Z}}q^{s}_{j_{X}j_{Z}}q^{p}_{k_{X}k_{Z}}\left(\frac{1+e^{i\pi(z+k_{Z}+j_{X})}}{2}\right)\mbox{Tr}\left[Z^{j_{Z}}X^{j_{X}}Z^{k_{X}}X^{i_{X}}Z^{i_{Z}}\left|\Psi_{z}\right>_{AB}\left<\Psi_{z}\right|Z^{i_{Z}}X^{i_{X}}Z^{k_{X}}X^{j_{X}}Z^{j_{Z}}\right.

\displaystyle\left.\times\left|\Psi_{z}\right>_{AB}\left<\Psi_{z}\right|\right],

(25)

where
N
is an appropriate normalization constant,
(q^{m}_{i_{X}i_{Z}},X^{i_{X}}Z^{i_{Z}})
,
(q^{s}_{j_{X}j_{Z}},X^{j_{X}}Z^{j_{Z}})
, and
(q^{m}_{k_{X}k_{Z}},X^{k_{X}}Z^{k_{Z}})
represent the channel noises for the measurement, input state, and pre-shared entanglement, respectively, and
\sum_{ijk}
is the summation over all
i_{X},i_{Z},j_{X},j_{Z},k_{X},k_{Z}\in\{0,1\}
and
\oplus
computed under modulus 2. The condition for the nonvanishing terms in the summation is given by

\displaystyle k_{Z}\oplus j_{X}=0~\wedge~i_{X}\oplus j_{X}=0~\wedge~i_{Z}\oplus k_{X}\oplus j_{Z}=0,

(26)

where the trace term is 1 when the condition is satisfied. For a depolarizing channel, the corresponding coefficients are
q^{m}_{00}=q^{s}_{00}=1-\frac{3}{4}\eta
,
q_{00}^{p}=1-\frac{3}{4}\tau
, and
q^{m}_{i_{X}i_{Z}}=q^{s}_{j_{X}j_{Z}}=\frac{1}{4}\eta
,
q^{p}_{k_{X}k_{Z}}=\frac{1}{4}\tau
when
(i_{X}i_{Z}),(j_{X}j_{Z}),(k_{X}k_{Z})\neq(00)
. Consequently, the total score is given by

{\cal S}_{2}=\left(1-\frac{3}{4}\eta\right)^{2}\left(1-\frac{3}{4}\tau\right)+\left(\frac{\eta}{4}\right)^{2}\left(1-\frac{3}{4}\tau\right)+2\left(1-\frac{3}{4}\eta\right)\frac{\eta}{4}\frac{\tau}{4}+4\left(\frac{\eta}{4}\right)^{2}\frac{\tau}{4}.

(27)

For the noiseless channel (
\eta=0
), this achievable score is larger than the local bound of
1/2
when
\tau<2/3
, which is satisfied as long as the pre-shared Werner-like state is entangled. On the other hand, even if we have a noiseless pre-shared entanglement, we need a slightly lower noise channel with
\eta<2/5
to surpass the local bound.

F.2
Example: 4 Bell states

Next, we examine the
k=4
case involving all four Bell states such that
\left|\Psi_{z_{X}z_{Z}}\right>=X^{z_{X}}Z^{z_{Z}}\left|\Phi^{0}\right>
with
z_{X},z_{Z}\in\{0,1\}
. Then the achievable score using the stabilizer method is given by

\displaystyle{\cal S}_{4}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{z_{X},z_{Z}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(x,y)\\
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\end{subarray}}\delta_{z_{X},x\oplus y}\delta_{z_{Z},x^{\prime}\oplus y^{\prime}}\mbox{Tr}\left[{\cal N}\left(\left|\Psi_{z}\right>_{AB}\left<\Psi_{z}\right|\right)N^{\prime}\Pi^{(x,y)}_{A^{\prime}B^{\prime}}\Pi^{(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})}_{A^{\prime}B^{\prime}}U_{P_{A}}\otimes U_{P_{B}}{\cal N}(\left|\Psi_{z}\right>_{AB}\left<\Psi_{z}\right|)\otimes{\cal N}^{\otimes 2}(\left|\Phi^{0}\right>_{A^{\prime}B^{\prime}}\left<\Phi^{0}\right|^{\otimes 2})\right.

\displaystyle\left.\times U^{\dagger}_{P_{A}}\otimes U^{\dagger}_{P_{B}}\Pi^{(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})}_{A^{\prime}B^{\prime}}\Pi^{(x,y)}_{A^{\prime}B^{\prime}}\right]

(28)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{z_{X},z_{Z}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(x,y)\\
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\end{subarray}}\delta_{z_{X},x\oplus y}\delta_{z_{Z},x^{\prime}\oplus y^{\prime}}\mbox{Tr}\left[\sum_{ijkl}q^{m}_{i_{X}i_{Z}}q^{s}_{j_{X}j_{Z}}q^{p_{1}}_{k_{X}k_{Z}}q^{p_{2}}_{l_{X}l_{Z}}X^{i_{X}}Z^{i_{Z}}\left|\Psi_{z}\right>_{AB}\left<\Psi_{z}\right|Z^{i_{Z}}X^{i_{X}}Z_{B}^{k_{X}}\frac{\mathbb{1}_{AB}+(-1)^{x+y+k_{Z}}Z_{A}Z_{B}}{2}\right.

\displaystyle\left.\times X_{B}^{l_{X}}\frac{\mathbb{1}_{AB}+(-1)^{x^{\prime}+y^{\prime}+l_{Z}}X_{A}X_{B}}{2}X^{j_{X}}Z^{j_{Z}}\left|\Psi_{z}\right>_{AB}\left<\Psi_{z}\right|Z^{j_{Z}}X^{j_{X}}\frac{\mathbb{1}_{AB}+(-1)^{x^{\prime}+y^{\prime}+l_{Z}}X_{A}X_{B}}{2}X_{B}^{l_{X}}\right.

\displaystyle\left.\times\frac{\mathbb{1}_{AB}+(-1)^{x+y+k_{Z}}Z_{A}Z_{B}}{2}Z_{B}^{k_{X}}\right]

(29)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{z_{X},z_{Z}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(x,y)\\
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\end{subarray}}\delta_{z_{X},x\oplus y}\delta_{z_{Z},x^{\prime}\oplus y^{\prime}}\mbox{Tr}\left[\sum_{ijkl}q^{m}_{i_{X}i_{Z}}q^{s}_{j_{X}j_{Z}}q^{p_{1}}_{k_{X}k_{Z}}q^{p_{2}}_{l_{X}l_{Z}}X^{i_{X}}Z^{i_{Z}}\left|\Psi_{z}\right>_{AB}\left<\Psi_{z}\right|Z^{i_{Z}}X^{i_{X}}Z^{k_{X}}\frac{1+e^{i\pi(x+y+z_{X}+l_{X}+k_{Z}+j_{X})}}{2}\right.

\displaystyle\left.\times X^{l_{X}}\frac{1+e^{i\pi(x^{\prime}+y^{\prime}+z_{Z}+l_{Z}+j_{Z})}}{2}X^{j_{X}}Z^{j_{Z}}\left|\Psi_{z}\right>_{AB}\left<\Psi_{z}\right|Z^{j_{Z}}X^{j_{X}}X^{l_{X}}Z^{k_{X}}\right]

(30)

\displaystyle=\sum_{z_{X},z_{Z}}\sum_{ijkl}q^{m}_{i_{X}i_{Z}}q^{s}_{j_{X}j_{Z}}q^{p_{1}}_{k_{X}k_{Z}}q^{p_{2}}_{l_{X}l_{Z}}\frac{1+e^{i\pi(l_{X}+k_{Z}+j_{X})}}{2}\frac{1+e^{i\pi(l_{Z}+j_{Z})}}{2}\mbox{Tr}\left[Z^{j_{Z}}X^{j_{X}}X^{l_{X}}Z^{k_{X}}X^{i_{X}}Z^{i_{Z}}\left|\Psi_{z}\right>_{AB}\left<\Psi_{z}\right|\right.

\displaystyle\left.\times Z^{i_{Z}}X^{i_{X}}Z^{k_{X}}X^{l_{X}}X^{j_{X}}Z^{j_{Z}}\left|\Psi_{z}\right>_{AB}\left<\Psi_{z}\right|\right].

(31)

The condition for nonvanishing terms can be written as

l_{X}\oplus k_{Z}\oplus j_{X}=0~\wedge~l_{Z}\oplus j_{Z}=0~\wedge~i_{Z}\oplus k_{X}\oplus j_{Z}=0~\wedge~i_{X}\oplus l_{X}\oplus j_{X}=0.

(32)

With the coefficients
q^{m}_{00}=q^{s}_{00}=1-\frac{3}{4}\eta
,
q_{00}^{p_{1}}=q_{00}^{p_{2}}=1-\frac{3}{4}\tau
, and
q^{m}_{i_{X}i_{Z}}=q^{s}_{j_{X}j_{Z}}=\frac{1}{4}\eta
when
(i_{X}i_{Z}),(j_{X}j_{Z})\neq(00)
,
q^{p_{1}}_{k_{X}k_{Z}}=q^{p_{2}}_{l_{X}l_{Z}}=\frac{1}{4}\tau
when
(k_{X}k_{Z}),(l_{X}l_{Z})\neq(00)
, the total score can be expressed as

\displaystyle{\cal S}_{4}

\displaystyle=\left(1-\frac{3}{4}\eta\right)^{2}\left(1-\frac{3}{4}\tau\right)^{2}+4\left(\frac{\eta}{4}\right)^{2}\left(1-\frac{3}{4}\tau\right)\frac{\tau}{4}+2\left(1-\frac{3}{4}\eta\right)\frac{\eta}{4}\left(1-\frac{3}{4}\tau\right)\frac{\tau}{4}+4\left(1-\frac{3}{4}\eta\right)\frac{\eta}{4}\left(\frac{\tau}{4}\right)^{2}+5\left(\frac{\eta}{4}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{\tau}{4}\right)^{2}.

(33)

Similar to the case of two Bell states, we can certify any entangled Werner-like state using a noiseless channel. Furthermore, the converse holds in this case: if the pre-shared Werner-like state is noiseless, we get a higher score than the local bound of
1/4
as long as the states evolved under the channel remain entangled, i.e.,
\eta<2/3
.

Appendix G
Local bound on the total score for randomly generated stabilizer states

In the main text, we consider the absolutely maximally entangled states, where any bipartition results in a maximally entangled state. Note that randomly generated pure states can have such a property approximately. In specific, for a large
N
(assuming even
N
), the reduced state converges to a maximally mixed state. Let us consider a randomly chosen stabilizer state
\left|\psi_{S}\right>
. Since any stabilizer state has a flat spectrum, i.e., all the Schmidt coefficients are equal, the largest Schmidt coefficient of the state is typically not far from
1/\sqrt{2^{N/2}}
when
N\gg 1
. Especially, the probability of the entanglement of
\left|\psi_{S}\right>
deviating from the maximum value is given by
Smith and Leung 2006

P\left(S(\sigma_{S})<\frac{N(1-\epsilon)}{2}\right)\leq\exp\left[-\frac{N\epsilon^{2}}{1024}\right],

(34)

where
\sigma_{S}=\mbox{Tr}_{N/2}\left|\psi_{S}\right>\left<\psi_{S}\right|
.
Consequently, the largest eigenvalue of
\sigma_{S}
is given by
\xi^{2}=\frac{1+\frac{N}{2}\epsilon+O(\epsilon^{2})}{2^{N/2}}
with an exponentially small failure probability as long as
\epsilon=O(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}})
.
Meanwhile, the total score for the
k
randomly generated stabilizer states is bounded above by Theorem 1 as

\displaystyle{\cal S}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{k}2^{N}\frac{(1+\frac{N}{2}\epsilon+O(\epsilon^{2}))^{2}}{2^{N}}=\frac{(1+\frac{N}{2}\epsilon)^{2}+O(\epsilon^{3})}{k}.

(35)

Therefore, if we set
\epsilon=O(\frac{1}{N})
, the bound slightly deviates from that of random guessing, i.e.,
1/k
, when
N\gg 1
.
