1.
Introduction

Let
G
be a finite group and let
N\trianglelefteqslant G
. A lot of properties of the group

are inherited by the quotient group
G/N
. On the other hand, the knowledge of
G/N
and
N
gives only a partial understanding on
G
. As an example, whenever
H
is a complement of
N
in
G
, we know that there exists a subgroup of
G
,
H
, which is isomorphic to
G/N
. An interesting question is whether for a group
G
and a normal subgroup
N\trianglelefteqslant G
, we can find a subgroup
H
such that
G=HN
and
H
preserve some properties of
G/N
, but not necessarily the isomorphism class. An instance of this problem is the following: if
G/N
belongs to a certain class of groups
\mathcal{C}
, can we find a subgroup
H
with
G=HN
such that
H
belongs to
\mathcal{C}
as well? If
\mathcal{C}
respects some properties, the answer is affirmative.

Proposition 1
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a class of finite groups which satisfies the following properties:

(1)

if
Y\trianglelefteqslant X
and
X\in\mathcal{C},
then
X/Y\in\mathcal{C};

(2)

if
X/\frat(X)\in\mathcal{C},
then
X\in\mathcal{C}.

If
N
is a normal subgroup of a finite group
G
and
G/N\in\mathcal{C},
then there exists
H\in\mathcal{C}
such that
G=HN.

Proof.

We prove the statement by induction on the order of the group. If
N\leqslant\frat(G),
then
G/\frat(G)
is an epimorphic image of
G/N
, so
by (1)
G/\frat(G)\in\mathcal{C}
and therefore it follows from (2)
that
G\in\mathcal{C}.

So we may assume
N\not\leqslant\frat(G).
In this case, there exists a maximal subgroup
M
of
G
such that
G=MN.
Moreover,
M/M\cap N\cong MN/\cap N=G/N\in\mathcal{C}
so there exists
H\in\mathcal{C}
such that

M=(M\cap N)H
and therefore
G=NM=N(M\cap N)H=NH.

∎

The previous statement does not in general hold if we do not assume that
\mathcal{C}
satisfies (2). For example if
\mathcal{C}
is the class of the finite abelian groups and

is the quaternion group of order

, then

does not admit an abelian supplement. However, Proposition

2.
Notation and preliminary results

In this section we will present the main strategy for the proof of Theorem
2
and prove some preliminary results which will also establish the main theorem for some families of almost simple groups.

Let
G_{0}
be a finite non-abelian simple group and let

\rho:\aut(G_{0})\to\out(G_{0})\cong\aut(G_{0})/G_{0}

be the canonical projection. The following definition will provide the language we will use in the proof of our main result.

Definition 5
.

Let
T
be an abelian subgroup of
\out(G_{0})
. We say that
\tilde{T}\leqslant\aut(G_{0})
is a
T
-abelian supplement if

(1)

\tilde{T}
is abelian.

(2)

\rho(\tilde{T})=T
.

Notice in particular that if
T
and
\tilde{T}
are as in the previous definition, if
G/G_{0}\cong T
, then
G=\tilde{T}G_{0}
with
\tilde{T}
abelian, therefore
proving Theorem
2
is equivalent to proving that for every non-abelian simple group
G_{0}
and every abelian
T\leqslant\out(G_{0})
, there exists a
T
-abelian supplement. The strategy of the proof of Theorem
2
is in fact the following: given
G_{0}
, we analyse all the abelian subgroups
T
of
\out(G_{0})
and prove case by case that there exists a
T
-abelian supplement. Actually, it is not necessary to check each abelian subgroup of
\out(G_{0})
, but only the maximal abelian ones, as it is shown by the following lemma.

Lemma 6
.

Let
T\leqslant S\leqslant\out(G_{0})
with
T
and
S
abelian. If there exists a
S
-abelian supplement, then there exists a
T
-abelian supplement as well.

Proof.

Let
\tilde{S}
be an
S
-abelian supplement. Let
\tilde{T}
be the preimage of
T
by the map
\rho|_{\tilde{S}}
. Then
\tilde{T}\leqslant\tilde{S}
and so it is abelian, moreover
\rho(\tilde{T})=\rho|_{\tilde{S}}(\tilde{T})=T
and so
\tilde{T}
is a
T
-abelian supplement.
∎

In particular, whenever
\out(G_{0})
is abelian, to prove Theorem
2
, it is enough to check that there exists a
\out(G_{0})
-abelian supplement.

We will now prove a couple of important lemmas which guarantee the existence of
T
-abelian supplements in some circumstances.

Lemma 7
.

Let
T
be a cyclic subgroup of
\out(G_{0})
. Then there exists a
T
-abelian supplement.

Proof.

Let
T=\left\langle t\right\rangle
and let
\tilde{t}\in\aut(G_{0})
be a preimage of
t
under
\rho
. Then
\tilde{T}=\langle\tilde{t}\rangle
is a
T
-abelian supplement.
∎

The previous lemma, together with Lemma
6
, shows that

Corollary 8
.

Theorem
2
is valid for all the almost simple groups with socle
G_{0}
such that
\out(G_{0})
is cyclic.

In particular, our main result is established whenever

•

G_{0}={\rm Alt}_{n}
,
n\geqslant 5
, with
n\neq 6
;

•

G_{0}={}^{3}\!D_{4}(q),E_{8}(q),F_{4}(q),G_{2}(q),\,{}^{2}B_{2}(2^{r}),{}^{2}G_{2}(3^{r}),{}^{2}F_{4}(2^{r})^{\prime}
;

•

G_{0}
is a sporadic simple group.

Noticing that
{\rm Alt}_{6}\cong\mathord{\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(9)}

If

Let

We are now ready to begin the investigation of the various types of almost simple groups, starting with the ones with linear socle.

3.
Linear groups

In this section we prove Theorem
2
in the linear case. We begin with the easiest case
n=2
, which is better understood on its own and gives us an explicit model for the more general setting. Then we deal with the case
n\geqslant 3
. More specifically, we prove some technical lemmas and analyse all the different types of abelian subgroups
T
of the outer automorpfism group, showing the existence of
T
-abelian supplements in each case. Finally, the main result of this section is contained in Theorem
17
.

Theorem 11
.

Let
G
a finite almost simple group with socle
G_{0}=\mathord{\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(q)}
. Then
G
contains an abelian subgroup
A
such that
G=AG_{0}.

Proof.

Let
Z:=\Z(\mathord{\mathrm{GL}_{2}(q)})
. We can suppose that
q
is odd, otherwise
d=1
and
\aut(G_{0})
splits over
G_{0}
. The outer automorphism group in this case is the following:

\out(G_{0})=\left\langle\delta\right\rangle\times\left\langle\phi\right\rangle,

where
\delta
is the diagonal automorphism with
|\delta|=2
and
\phi
is the field automorphism with
|\phi|=m
and
[\delta,\phi]=1
.

Let

A:=\begin{pmatrix}0&-\lambda\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}.\qquad B:=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda^{\frac{p-1}{2}}&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}.

We have

A^{\phi B}=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda^{\frac{1-p}{2}}&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0&-\lambda^{p}\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\lambda^{\frac{p-1}{2}}&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-\lambda^{\frac{p+1}{2}}\\
\lambda^{\frac{p-1}{2}}&0\end{pmatrix}=\lambda^{\frac{p-1}{2}}A.

Therefore

[A,\phi B]\in Z

and
\rho(AZ)=\delta
and
\rho(\phi BZ)
can be
\phi\delta
or
\phi
, but in any case

\rho(\left\langle AZ,\phi BZ\right\rangle)=\out(G_{0})

and therefore

\left\langle A,\phi B\right\rangle Z/Z

is a
\out(G_{0})
-abelian supplement.
∎

From now on,
G_{0}:=\mathord{\mathrm{PSL}_{n}(q)}
with
n\geqslant 3
, so
d=(n,q-1)
. We write also
Z:=\Z(\mathord{\mathrm{GL}_{n}(q)})
.

In this case, the outer automorphism group is the following:

\out(G_{0})=\left\langle\delta\right\rangle\rtimes\left\langle\phi,\gamma\right\rangle,

where
\delta
is a diagonal automorphism with
|\delta|=d
,
\phi
is the field automorphism which raises the coefficients of every matrix to the power of
p
and
\gamma
is the graph automorphism which transform each matrix in its inverse transpose. In particular we have
|\phi|=m
,
|\gamma|=2
,

[\phi,\gamma]=1
,
\delta^{\phi}=\delta^{p}
and

\delta^{\gamma}=\delta^{-1}
.

In the sequel we will use a lot the following special matrices defined from some integers
w,l,c\in\mathbb{Z}
with
w\geqslant 2
:

A_{w,l}:=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&\dots&0&(-1)^{w-1}\lambda^{l}\\
1&0&\dots&0&0\\
0&1&\dots&0&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&\dots&1&0\end{pmatrix}\in\mathord{\mathrm{GL}_{w}(q)}

and

X_{w,c}:=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda^{c(w-1)}&0&\cdots&0&0\\
0&\lambda^{c(w-2)}&\cdots&0&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&\lambda^{c}&0\\
0&0&\cdots&0&1\end{pmatrix}\in\mathord{\mathrm{GL}_{w}(q)}.

Notice that

\det{A_{w,l}}=\lambda^{l}.

We now introduce a technical lemma which is the key ingredient of the proofs in this section.

Lemma 12
.

Let
w,l,c\in\mathbb{Z}
be integers. Let

A:=A_{w,l}\qquad X:=X_{w,c}.

If

cw\equiv lp^{s}(-1)^{\varepsilon}-l\mod q-1,

then we have

A^{\phi^{s}\gamma^{\varepsilon}X}=\lambda^{c}A.

Proof.

First notice that

A^{\phi^{s}\gamma^{\varepsilon}}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&\dots&0&(-1)^{w-1}\lambda^{lp^{s}(-1)^{\varepsilon}}\\
1&0&\dots&0&0\\
0&1&\dots&0&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&\dots&1&0\end{pmatrix}.

To prove the equality, we will check that
A^{\phi^{s}\gamma^{\varepsilon}X}
and
\lambda^{c}A
act in the same way on the canonical basis’ vectors
\{e_{i}\mid 1\leqslant i\leqslant w\}
.

We have that

Xe_{i}=\lambda^{c(w-i)}e_{i},\qquad 1\leqslant i\leqslant w.

If
i<w
, then

A^{\phi^{s}\gamma^{\varepsilon}}e_{i}=e_{i+1}=Ae_{i},

therefore

A^{\phi^{s}\gamma^{\varepsilon}X}e_{i}=X^{-1}A^{\phi^{s}\gamma^{\varepsilon}}Xe_{i}=\lambda^{c(w-i)}X^{-1}A^{\phi^{s}\gamma^{\varepsilon}}e_{i}=

=\lambda^{c(w-i)}X^{-1}e_{i+1}=\lambda^{c(w-i)}\lambda^{-c(w-i-1)}e_{i+1}=\lambda^{c}e_{i+1}=\lambda^{c}Ae_{i}.

On the other hand, if
i=w
we have

A^{\phi^{s}\gamma^{\varepsilon}X}e_{w}=X^{-1}A^{\phi^{s}\gamma^{\varepsilon}}Xe_{w}=X^{-1}A^{\phi^{s}\gamma^{\varepsilon}}e_{w}=

(-1)^{w-1}\lambda^{lp^{s}(-1)^{\varepsilon}}X^{-1}e_{1}=(-1)^{w-1}\lambda^{lp^{s}(-1)^{\varepsilon}}\lambda^{-c(w-1)}e_{1}=

(-1)^{w-1}\lambda^{lp^{s}(-1)^{\varepsilon}-cw+c}e_{1}=(-1)^{w-1}\lambda^{l+c}e_{1},

since

cw\equiv lp^{s}(-1)^{\varepsilon}-l\mod q-1.

Finally,

\lambda^{c}Ae_{w}=\lambda^{c}\cdot(-1)^{w-1}\lambda^{l}e_{1}=(-1)^{w-1}\lambda^{l+c}e_{1},

so the two linear maps coincide on a basis and therefore they are equal in
\mathord{\mathrm{GL}_{w}(q)}
.
∎

We are now able to present the existence of
T
-abelian supplements for some of the possible choices of
T
.

Proposition 13
.

Let

T=\left\langle\delta^{k},\phi^{s}\gamma^{\varepsilon}\delta^{j}\right\rangle

with
k\mid d
and
k\not=d
. Then there exists a
T
-abelian supplement.

Proof.

Since
T
is abelian,

\delta^{k}=(\delta^{k})^{\phi^{s}\gamma^{\varepsilon}\delta^{j}}=\delta^{kp^{s}(-1)^{\varepsilon}},

which means
d\mid k((-1)^{\varepsilon}p^{s}-1)
or, equivalently

t\mid(-1)^{\varepsilon}p^{s}-1,\qquad t:=d/k.

First, suppose
t=n
, so
t=d=n
and
k=1
. In this case
T=\left\langle\delta,\phi^{s}\gamma^{\varepsilon}\right\rangle
and

\tilde{T}:=\left\langle A_{n,1},\phi^{s}\gamma^{\varepsilon}X_{n,\frac{(-1)^{\varepsilon}p^{s}-1}{n}}\right\rangle Z/Z

is a
T
-abelian supplement, since
\rho(A_{n,1}Z)=\delta
and by applying Lemma
12
with

(w,l,c)=\left(n,1,\frac{(-1)^{\varepsilon}p^{s}-1}{n}\right),

we have

\left[A_{n,1},\phi^{s}\gamma^{\varepsilon}X_{n,\frac{(-1)^{\varepsilon}p^{s}-1}{n}}\right]\in Z.

So in the sequel we can suppose
t\neq n
.

Step 1
.

We find an integer
y\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
yn\equiv d\mod{q-1}
and
(y,t)=1
.

Since
\left(\frac{n}{d},\frac{q-1}{d}\right)=1
, there exists
\overline{y}\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
\overline{y}\frac{n}{d}\equiv 1\mod\frac{q-1}{d}
. Now let

t=p_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\dots p_{\tilde{l}}^{\alpha_{\tilde{l}}}p_{\tilde{l}+1}^{\alpha_{\tilde{l}+1}}\dots p_{l}^{\alpha_{l}}

its prime factorization, where we ordered the primes in a way such that
p_{i}
divides
\overline{y}
if and only if
1\leqslant i\leqslant\tilde{l}
.

Let

y=\overline{y}+p_{\tilde{l}+1}\cdots p_{l}\frac{q-1}{d}.

For every
p_{i}
, we have that
p_{i}
does not divide
y
, because if
1\leqslant i\leqslant\tilde{l}
, then
\overline{y}
is divisible by
p_{i}
while
p_{\tilde{l}+1}\cdots p_{l}\frac{q-1}{d}
is not (since
(\overline{y},\frac{q-1}{d})=1
) and if
\tilde{l}<i\leqslant l
,
p_{i}
divides
p_{\tilde{l}+1}\cdots p_{l}\frac{q-1}{d}
but not
\overline{y}
. Therefore
(y,t)=1
, moreover
y\equiv\overline{y}\mod\frac{q-1}{d}
and
yn\equiv d\mod{q-1}
.

Step 2
.

We construct matrices
A,X\in\mathord{\mathrm{GL}_{n}(q)}
such that
\det{A}=\lambda^{k}
and
[A,\phi^{s}\gamma^{\varepsilon}X]\in Z
.

Since
t\mid n
and
t\neq 1,n
, we have that both
t\geqslant 2
and
n-t\geqslant 2
and so we can define

A:=\begin{pmatrix}A_{t,y}&0\\
0&A_{n-t,k-y}\end{pmatrix}.

First, notice that

\det{A}=\det{A_{t,y}}\det{A_{n-t,k-y}}=\lambda^{y}\lambda^{k-y}=\lambda^{k},

therefore
\rho(AZ)=\delta^{k}
.

Let
r:=((-1)^{\varepsilon}p^{s}-1)/t
and define

X:=\begin{pmatrix}X_{t,yr}&0\\
0&X_{n-t,yr}\end{pmatrix},

We have that

y(p^{s}(-1)^{\varepsilon}-1)=yrt\mod{q-1},

so applying Lemma
12
with
(w,l,c)=(t,y,yr)
we get

A_{t,y}^{\phi^{s}\gamma^{\varepsilon}X_{t,yr}}=\lambda^{yr}A_{t,y}.

Moreover, recalling that
kt=d\equiv ny\mod{q-1}
, we have that

(k-y)(p^{s}(-1)^{\varepsilon}-1)=(k-y)rt=krt-yrt=yr(n-t)\mod{q-1},

so applying Lemma
12
with
(w,l,c)=(n-t,k-y,yr)
we get

A_{n-t,k-y}^{\phi^{s}\gamma^{\varepsilon}X_{n-t,yr}}=\lambda^{yr}A_{n-t,k-y}.

Therefore we have

A^{\phi^{s}\gamma^{\varepsilon}X}=\lambda^{yr}A

or, equivalently,

[A,\phi^{s}\gamma^{\varepsilon}X]\in Z.

Step 3
.

We find a matrix
C\in\mathord{\mathrm{GL}_{n}(q)}
such that
[A,C]=1
with
\det{C}=\lambda
.

Recall that
(y,t)=1
, so there exist
a,b\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
ay+bt=1
.
Let

C_{0}:=\lambda^{b}A_{t,y}^{a}\in\mathord{\mathrm{GL}_{t}(q)}.

We have that
[A_{t,y},C_{0}]=1
and

\det{C_{0}}=\det{A_{t,y}}^{a}\lambda^{bt}=\lambda^{ay+bt}=\lambda.

Let

C:=\begin{pmatrix}C_{0}&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}.

We have
[A,C]=1
and
\det{C}=\det{C_{0}}=\lambda
.

Step 4
.

We complete the proof by constructing a
T
-abelian supplement.

Let
u\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
\rho(XZ)=\delta^{u}
.
Combining Steps
2
and
3
, we get

[A,\phi^{s}\gamma^{\varepsilon}XC^{j-u}]\in Z,

with
\rho(AZ)=\delta^{k}
and
\rho(XC^{j-u}Z)=\delta^{u}\delta^{j-u}=\delta^{j}
.
Therefore

\tilde{T}:=\left\langle A,\phi^{s}\gamma^{\varepsilon}XC^{j-u}\right\rangle Z/Z

is a
T
-abelian supplement.

∎

To continue our investigation, we need another couple of small lemmas.

Lemma 14
.

Let
A,B\in\mathord{\mathrm{GL}_{w}(q)}
. Then

[\phi^{s}A,\gamma B]=1

if and only if

B=A^{T}B^{\phi^{s}}A.

Proof.

Easy computation.
∎

Lemma 15
.

If
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{Z}
are such that

\beta\equiv 2\alpha+p^{s}\beta\mod{q-1},

then

[\phi^{s}X_{w,\alpha},\gamma X_{w,\beta}]=1.

Proof.

Since
X_{w,\alpha},X_{w,\beta}
are diagonal, for Lemma
14
we just need to check that

X_{w,\beta}=X_{w,\alpha}X_{w,\beta}^{p^{s}}X_{w,\alpha}.

By inspecting the coefficients on the diagonal, for every
1\leqslant i\leqslant w
we have

\lambda^{\beta(w-i)}=\lambda^{\alpha(w-i)}\lambda^{p^{s}\beta(w-i)}\lambda^{\alpha(w-i)}=\lambda^{(2\alpha+p^{s}\beta)(w-i)},

which is true because of the hypothesis on
\alpha
and
\beta
.
∎

We now show the existence of
T
-abelian supplements for other choices of
T
.

Proposition 16
.

Let
d
be even, then we can find a
T
-abelian supplement for
T
of the form
T=\left\langle\delta^{d/2},\phi^{s}\delta^{j},\gamma\delta^{k}\right\rangle
.

Proof.

As in the previous case, this proof is articulated in different steps.

Step 1
.

We find an integer
y\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
yn\equiv d\mod{q-1}
and
y
is odd.

Since
\left(\frac{n}{d},\frac{q-1}{d}\right)=1
, there exists
\overline{y}\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
\overline{y}\frac{n}{d}\equiv 1\mod\frac{q-1}{d}
. If
\overline{y}
is odd, define
y:=\overline{y}
; if
\overline{y}
is even, therefore
\frac{q-1}{d}
is odd, define
y:=\overline{y}+\frac{q-1}{d}
which is odd and such that
y\frac{n}{d}\equiv 1\mod\frac{q-1}{d}
. So, we have
yn\equiv d\mod{q-1}
with
y
odd.

Step 2
.

We construct matrices
A,X_{\phi},X_{\gamma}\in\mathord{\mathrm{GL}_{n}(q)}
such that
\det{A}=\lambda^{d/2}
and
\hat{T_{1}}:=\left\langle A,\phi^{s}X_{\phi},\gamma X_{\gamma}\right\rangle Z/Z
is abelian.

Since
n-2\geqslant 2
we can define

A:=\begin{pmatrix}A_{2,y}&0\\
0&A_{n-2,d/2-y}\end{pmatrix},

so that
\rho(AZ)=\delta^{d/2}
since

\det{A}=\det{A_{2,y}}\det{A_{n-2,d/2-y}}=\lambda^{y}\lambda^{d/2-y}=\lambda^{d/2}.

Let
r:=(p^{s}-1)/2
.
Considering the automrphisms
\phi^{s}
and
\gamma
let us now argue as in Step
2
of Propostion
13
and construct

X_{\phi}:=\begin{pmatrix}X_{2,yr}&0\\
0&X_{n-2,yr}\end{pmatrix},

so that

A^{\phi^{s}X_{\phi}}=\lambda^{yr}A

and

X_{\gamma}:=\begin{pmatrix}X_{2,-y}&0\\
0&X_{n-2,-y}\end{pmatrix}

so that

A^{\gamma X_{\gamma}}=\lambda^{-y}A.

Since

-y\equiv 2yr-yp^{s}\mod{q-1},

for Lemma
15
we have

\left[\phi^{s}X_{2,yr},\gamma X_{2,-y}\right]=\left[\phi^{s}X_{n-2,yr},\gamma X_{n-2,-y}\right]=1

and therefore

\left[\phi^{s}X_{\phi},\gamma X_{\gamma}\right]=1.

From this, we obtain that

\hat{T_{1}}:=\left\langle A,\phi^{s}X_{\phi},\gamma X_{\gamma}\right\rangle Z/Z

is abelian.

Step 3
.

We construct matrices
X_{\phi}^{\prime},X_{\gamma}^{\prime}\in\mathord{\mathrm{GL}_{n}(q)}
such that
\det{X_{\gamma}^{\prime}}=\lambda^{y}\det{X_{\gamma}}
and
\hat{T_{2}}:=\left\langle A,\phi^{s}X_{\phi}^{\prime},\gamma X_{\gamma}^{\prime}\right\rangle Z/Z
is abelian.

Let us define

Y_{\phi}:=X_{2,yr}A_{2,y}^{-r},\quad Y_{\gamma}:=X_{2,-y}A_{2,y}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix},

C_{\phi}:=\begin{pmatrix}A_{2,y}^{-r}&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},\quad C_{\gamma}:=\begin{pmatrix}A_{2,y}&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}

and

X_{\phi}^{\prime}:=X_{\phi}C_{\phi}=\begin{pmatrix}Y_{\phi}&0\\
0&X_{n-2,yr}\end{pmatrix},\quad X_{\gamma}^{\prime}:=X_{\gamma}C_{\gamma}=\begin{pmatrix}Y_{\gamma}&0\\
0&X_{n-2,-y}\end{pmatrix}.

Since
C_{\phi},C_{\gamma}\in\C_{\mathord{\mathrm{GL}_{n}(q)}}(A)
, we have

A^{\phi^{s}X_{\phi}^{\prime}}=\lambda^{yr}A\qquad A^{\gamma X_{\gamma}^{\prime}}=\lambda^{-y}A.

Notice that

X_{2,yr}^{T}Y_{\gamma}X_{2,yr}=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda^{yr}&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\lambda^{yr}&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}=\lambda^{yr}Y_{\gamma}

and

A_{2,y}^{T}Y_{\gamma}A_{2,y}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
-\lambda^{y}&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0&-\lambda^{y}\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-\lambda^{y}\\
\lambda^{y}&0\end{pmatrix}=\lambda^{y}Y_{\gamma}.

From this we deduce

Y_{\phi}^{T}Y_{\gamma}^{\phi^{s}}Y_{\phi}=\left(A_{2,y}^{T}\right)^{-r}X_{2,yr}^{T}Y_{\gamma}X_{2,yr}A_{2,y}^{-r}=

=\lambda^{yr}\left(A_{2,y}^{T}\right)^{-r}Y_{\gamma}A_{2,y}^{-r}=Y_{\gamma}.

Since
X_{n-2,yr}^{T}Y_{n-2,-y}^{\phi^{s}}X_{n-2,yr}=Y_{n-2,-y}
, this means that

\left[\phi^{s}X_{\phi}^{\prime},\gamma X_{\gamma}^{\prime}\right]=1.

Therefore

\det{X_{\gamma}^{\prime}}=\det{X_{\gamma}}\det{C_{\gamma}}=\det{X_{\gamma}}\det{A_{2,y}}=\lambda^{y}\det{X_{\gamma}}.

and

\hat{T_{2}}:=\left\langle A,\phi^{s}X_{\phi}^{\prime},\gamma X_{\gamma}^{\prime}\right\rangle Z/Z

is abelian.

Step 4
.

We complete the proof by constructing a
T
-abelian supplement.

Let
\rho(X_{\gamma}Z)=\delta^{u}
for some
u\in\mathbb{Z}
, so
\rho(X_{\gamma}^{\prime}Z)=\delta^{y+u}
. Given that
y
is odd, one of
u-k
or
y+u-k
is even. Since
\gamma\delta^{x}
is conjugate to
\gamma\delta^{y}
in
\left\langle\delta,\gamma\right\rangle
if
y-x
is even, one of
\gamma\delta^{u}
or
\gamma\delta^{u+y}
is conjugate to
\gamma\delta^{k}
.

Let

\hat{T}:=\begin{cases}\hat{T_{1}}\qquad\text{if $u-k$ is even}\\
\hat{T_{2}}\qquad\text{if $u+y-k$ is even}\end{cases},

so that there exists a matrix
R\in\mathord{\mathrm{GL}_{n}(q)}
such that
\rho(\hat{T}^{R})=\left\langle\delta^{d/2},\phi^{s}\delta^{l},\gamma\delta^{k}\right\rangle
for some
l\in\mathbb{Z}
. Notice that this group being abelian means
2l\equiv-k(p^{s}-1)\mod{d}
. In the same way, since
T=\left\langle\delta^{d/2},\phi^{s}\delta^{j},\gamma\delta^{k}\right\rangle
is abelian, it means that
2j\equiv-k(p^{s}-1)\mod{d}
, but then
2l\equiv 2j\mod{d}
, which means
l\equiv j\mod{d/2}
and
\rho(\hat{T}^{R})=T
. Therefore
\tilde{T}:=\hat{T}^{R}
is a
T
-abelian supplement.
∎

We are now able to prove Theorem
2
in the linear case.

Theorem 17
.

Let
G
a finite almost simple group with socle
G_{0}=\mathord{\mathrm{PSL}_{n}(q)}
. If
G/G_{0}
is abelian, then
G
contains an abelian subgroup
A
such that
G=AG_{0}.

Proof.

If
n=2
, this follows from Theorem
11
, so we can suppose
n\geqslant 3
. The statement in this case is equivalent to finding a
T
-abelian supplement for every abelian
T\leqslant\out(G_{0})
.
Let
\pi\colon\out(G_{0})\to\out(G_{0})/\left\langle\delta\right\rangle=\left\langle\phi,\gamma\right\rangle
. If
\pi(T)=\left\langle\phi^{s}\gamma^{\varepsilon}\right\rangle
is cyclic, then
T
is of the form
T=\left\langle\delta^{k},\phi^{s}\gamma^{\varepsilon}\delta^{j}\right\rangle
with
k\mid d
and we conclude by Proposition
13
. If
\pi(T)
is not cyclic, then
\pi(T)=\left\langle\phi^{s},\gamma\right\rangle
. Suppose
d
is odd. Then
T
is two generated and of the form
T=\left\langle\phi^{s}\delta^{j},\gamma\delta^{k}\right\rangle
. Since
d
is odd
\gamma\delta^{k}
is conjugate to
\gamma
in
\left\langle\delta,\gamma\right\rangle
, so, up to conjugation, we can assume
k=0
and therefore
\delta^{j}=1
, since
[\phi^{s}\delta^{j},\gamma]=1
; therefore
\tilde{T}:=T
is a
T
-abelian supplement. Suppose
d
is even. If
T
is
2
-generated it is of the form
T=\left\langle\phi^{s}\delta^{j},\gamma\delta^{k}\right\rangle
and since
\delta^{d/2}\in\Z(\out(G_{0}))
, it is contained in an abelian subgroup of
\out(G_{0})
of the form
\left\langle\delta^{d/2},\phi^{s}\delta^{j},\gamma\delta^{k}\right\rangle
and we conclude by Proposition
16
. If it is
3
-generated, it is of the form
T=\left\langle\delta^{l},\phi^{s}\delta^{j},\gamma\delta^{k}\right\rangle
and in order to be abelian we should have
[\delta^{l},\gamma\delta^{k}]=1
, therefore
l=d/2
and again we conclude by Proposition
16
.
∎

4.
Unitary groups

Throughout this section,
q=p^{m}
,
\nu
is a primitive element of the field
\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}
and
\omega:=\nu^{q-1}
so that
|\omega|=q+1
. Moreover,
G_{0}:=\mathord{\mathrm{PSU}_{n}(q)}
, and so
d=(n,q+1)
. Finally, we write
Z:=\Z(\mathord{\mathrm{GU}_{n}(q)})
.

We have

\out(G_{0})=\left\langle\delta\right\rangle\rtimes\left\langle\phi\right\rangle,

where
\delta
is a diagonal automorphism with
|\delta|=d
and
\phi
is the field automorphism which raises the coefficients of every matrix to the power
p
. In particular we have
|\phi|=2m
and
\delta^{\phi}=\delta^{p}
.

As in the linear case, to prove Theorem
2
for unitary groups, we use some special matrices defined from some integers
w,l,c\in\mathbb{Z}
with
w\geqslant 2
, which play a similar role that the ones in Section
3
, but with a slightly different definition, where we use
\omega
in place of
\lambda
:

A_{w,l}:=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&\dots&0&(-1)^{w-1}\omega^{l}\\
1&0&\dots&0&0\\
0&1&\dots&0&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&\dots&1&0\end{pmatrix}\in\mathord{\mathrm{GL}_{w}(q)}

and

X_{w,c}:=\begin{pmatrix}\omega^{c(w-1)}&0&\cdots&0&0\\
0&\omega^{c(w-2)}&\cdots&0&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&\omega^{c}&0\\
0&0&\cdots&0&1\end{pmatrix}\in\mathord{\mathrm{GL}_{w}(q)}.

Notice that, as in the previous case,

\det{A_{w,l}}=\omega^{l}

and moreover, keeping in mind that
|\omega|=q+1
, it can be easily checked that
A_{w,l},X_{w,c}\in\mathord{\mathrm{GU}_{w}(q)}
are unitary matrices, as well as the scalar matrix
\omega
in every dimension. Before proving the main result of this section, we present an analog of Lemma
12
.

Lemma 18
.

Let
w,l,c\in\mathbb{Z}
be integers. Let

A:=A_{w,l}\qquad X:=X_{w,c}.

If

cw\equiv l(p^{s}-1)\mod q+1,

then we have

A^{\phi^{s}X}=\omega^{c}A.

Proof.

To prove the statement is sufficient to carry out the same type of computations of the proof of Lemma
12
.
∎

We are now ready to prove Theorem
2
in the case in which
G_{0}
is a unitary group.

Proposition 19
.

Let
G
a finite almost simple group with socle
G_{0}=\mathord{\mathrm{PSU}_{n}(q)}
. If
G/G_{0}
is abelian, then
G
contains an abelian subgroup
A
such that
G=AG_{0}.

Proof.

The statement is equivalent to finding a
T
-abelian supplement for every abelian
T\leqslant\out(G_{0})
. The abelian subgroups of
\out(G_{0})
are of the form
T=\left\langle\delta^{k},\phi^{s}\delta^{j}\right\rangle
with
k\mid d
,
k\neq 1
, and since
T
is abelian,
t\mid p^{s}-1
with
t:=d/k
.

In its structure, this proof resembles the one of Proposition
13
and again is articulated in different steps which focus on one such
T
and build a
T
-abelian supplement accordingly.

First, suppose
t=n
, so
t=d=n
and
k=1
. In this case
T=\left\langle\delta,\phi^{s}\right\rangle
and

\tilde{T}:=\left\langle A_{n,1},\phi^{s}X_{n,\frac{p^{s}-1}{n}}\right\rangle Z/Z

is a
T
-abelian supplement, since
\rho(A_{n,1}Z)=\delta
and by applying Lemma
18
with

(w,l,c)=\left(n,1,\frac{p^{s}-1}{n}\right),

we have

\left[A_{n,1},\phi^{s}X_{n,\frac{p^{s}-1}{n}}\right]\in Z.

So in the sequel we can suppose
t\neq n
.

Step 1
.

We find an integer
y\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
yn\equiv d\mod{q+1}
and
(y,t)=1
.

Since
\left(\frac{n}{d},\frac{q+1}{d}\right)=1
, there exists
\overline{y}\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
\overline{y}\frac{n}{d}\equiv 1\mod\frac{q+1}{d}
. Now let

t=p_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\dots p_{\tilde{l}}^{\alpha_{\tilde{l}}}p_{\tilde{l}+1}^{\alpha_{\tilde{l}+1}}\dots p_{l}^{\alpha_{l}}

its prime factorization, where we ordered the primes in a way such that
p_{i}
divides
\overline{y}
if and only if
1\leqslant i\leqslant\tilde{l}
.

Let

y=\overline{y}+p_{\tilde{l}+1}\cdots p_{l}\frac{q+1}{d}.

For every
p_{i}
, we have that
p_{i}
does not divide
y
, because if
1\leqslant i\leqslant\tilde{l}
, then
\overline{y}
is divisible by
p_{i}
while
p_{\tilde{l}+1}\cdots p_{l}\frac{q+1}{d}
is not (since
(\overline{y},\frac{q+1}{d})=1
) and if
\tilde{l}<i\leqslant l
,
p_{i}
divides
p_{\tilde{l}+1}\cdots p_{l}\frac{q+1}{d}
but not
\overline{y}
. Therefore
(y,t)=1
, moreover
y\equiv\overline{y}\mod\frac{q+1}{d}
and
yn\equiv d\mod{q+1}
.

Step 2
.

We construct matrices
A,X\in\mathord{\mathrm{GU}_{n}(q)}
such that
\det{A}=\omega^{k}
and
[A,\phi^{s}X]\in Z
.

Since
t\mid n
and
t\neq 1,n
, we have that both
t\geqslant 2
and
n-t\geqslant 2
and so we can define

A:=\begin{pmatrix}A_{t,y}&0\\
0&A_{n-t,k-y}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathord{\mathrm{GU}_{n}(q)}.

First, notice that

\det{A}=\det{A_{t,y}}\det{A_{n-t,k-y}}=\omega^{y}\omega^{k-y}=\omega^{k},

therefore
\rho(AZ)=\delta^{k}
.

Let
r:=(p^{s}-1)/t
and define

X:=\begin{pmatrix}X_{t,yr}&0\\
0&X_{n-t,yr}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathord{\mathrm{GU}_{n}(q)},

We have that

y(p^{s}-1)=yrt\mod{q+1},

so applying Lemma
18
with
(w,l,c)=(t,y,yr)
we get

A_{t,y}^{\phi^{s}X_{t,yr}}=\omega^{yr}A_{t,y}.

Moreover, recalling that
kt=d\equiv ny\mod{q+1}
, we have that

(k-y)(p^{s}-1)=(k-y)rt=krt-yrt=yr(n-t)\mod{q+1},

so applying Lemma
18
with
(w,l,c)=(n-t,k-y,yr)
we get

A_{n-t,k-y}^{\phi^{s}X_{n-t,yr}}=\omega^{yr}A_{n-t,k-y}.

Therefore we have

A^{\phi^{s}X}=\omega^{yr}A

or, equivalently,

[A,\phi^{s}X]\in Z.

Step 3
.

We find a matrix
C\in\mathord{\mathrm{GU}_{n}(q)}
such that
[A,C]=1
with
\det{C}=\omega
.

Recall that
(y,t)=1
, so there exist
a,b\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
ay+bt=1
.
Let

C_{0}:=\omega^{b}A_{t,y}^{a}\in\mathord{\mathrm{GU}_{t}(q)}.

We have that
[A_{t,y},C_{0}]=1
and

\det{C_{0}}=\det{A_{t,y}}^{a}\omega^{bt}=\omega^{ay+bt}=\omega.

Let

C:=\begin{pmatrix}C_{0}&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\in\mathord{\mathrm{GU}_{n}(q)}.

We have
[A,C]=1
and
\det{C}=\det{C_{0}}=\omega
.

Step 4
.

We complete the proof by constructing a
T
-abelian supplement.

Let
u\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
\rho(XZ)=\delta^{u}
.
Combining Steps
2
and
3
, we get

[A,\phi^{s}XC^{j-u}]\in Z,

with
\rho(AZ)=\delta^{k}
and
\rho(XC^{j-u}Z)=\delta^{u}\delta^{j-u}=\delta^{j}
.
Therefore

\tilde{T}:=\left\langle A,\phi^{s}XC^{j-u}\right\rangle Z/Z

is a
T
-abelian supplement.
∎

5.
Notation for groups of Lie type

For the definitions and automorphisms of simple groups of Lie type we refer to
[
4
]
(see also
[

]
). We briefly recall that

over the field

of characteristic

\mathbb{F}_{q}

,

, one defines

n_{r}(t)=x_{r}(t)x_{-r}(-t^{-1})x_{r}(t)
,
n_{r}=n_{r}(1)
and the subgroup

of
L(q)
.

Let
\Delta=\{\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}\}
be a system of simple roots of
\Phi
. We shall use the numbering and the description of the simple roots in terms of the canonical basis
(e_{1},\ldots,e_{k})
of an appropriate
\mathbb{R}^{k}
as in
[
1
]
, Planches I-IX.
We denote by
Q
the root lattice, by
P
the weight lattice and by

the Weyl

s_{i}
is the simple reflection associated to
\alpha_{i}
,

\{\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{n}\}
are the fundamental weights,
w_{0}
is the longest element of

,

A=(a_{ij})
is the
Cartan matrix (hence
\alpha_{i}=\sum_{j}a_{ij}\omega_{j}
).

Let
\Hom(Q,\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times})
be the group of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-characters of
Q
(i.e. group homomorphisms of
Q
into
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
). For any
\chi\in\Hom(Q,\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times})

h(\chi)
of
L_{k}
. Let
\hat{H}=\{h(\chi)\mid\chi\in\Hom(Q,\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times})\}
. The map
\chi\mapsto h(\chi)
is an isomorphism of
\Hom(Q,\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times})
onto
\hat{H}
. We have
\hat{H}\leqslant N_{\mathrm{Aut}L_{k}}(L(q))
. Let
H=\hat{H}\cap L(q)
. Then
h(\chi)
lies in
H
if and only if
\chi
can be extended to an
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-character of
P
. The number
d
in Table
2
relative to the untwisted case is the order of
\hat{H}/H
. We have
H\triangleleft N
and
N/H\cong W
. For
w\in W
, we denote by
\dot{w}
a representative of
w
in
N
; for each
i=1,\ldots,n
,
n_{\alpha_{i}}
is a representative of
s_{i}
in
N
. For short we denote

n_{\alpha_{i}}
by
n_{i}
. Note that
n_{i}
lies in
L(p)
, so that it is fixed by field automorphisms of
L(q)
.

We give a short description of the twisted groups. Assume that the Dynkin diagram of
L
has a non-trivial symmetry
\tau
of order
s
. We stick to the cases
D_{n}
,
E_{6}
and

\displaystyle=[xn,\tau]^{\sigma}[\sigma,ym]^{\tau}=[x,\tau]^{\sigma}[\sigma,y]^{\tau}[n,\tau]^{\sigma}[\sigma,m]^{\tau}.

Since
[n,\tau]^{\sigma}[\sigma,m]^{\tau}=[n^{\sigma},\tau][\sigma,m^{\tau}],

we are looking for
n,m\in Z^{t}
such that

[x,\tau]^{\sigma}[\sigma,y]^{\tau}=[x\sigma,y\tau]=[\tau,n^{\sigma}][m^{\tau},\sigma].

Notice that
[x\sigma,y\tau]=(z_{1},\dots,z_{t})\in Z^{t},
with
z_{1}z_{2}\cdots z_{t}=1.
Let

\Lambda:=\{(z_{1},\dots,z_{t})\in Z^{t}\mid z_{1}z_{2}\cdots z_{t}=1\}.

In order to conclude our proof, it suffices to prove that for every
(z_{1},\dots,z_{t})\in\Lambda
there exist
\tilde{n},\tilde{m}\in Z^{t}
such that
(z_{1},\dots,z_{t})=[\tau,\tilde{n}][\tilde{m},\sigma].

Since
\langle\sigma,\tau\rangle
is a regular subgroup of
\perm(t),

\sigma=\sigma_{1}\cdots\sigma_{r}
is the product of
r
disjoint cycles of the same length
s
, with
rs=t.
First assume
r=1.
In that case for every
\lambda\in\Lambda,
there exists
\tilde{m}\in Z^{t}
such that
[\tilde{m},\sigma]=\lambda,
and our conclusion follows taking
\tilde{n}=1.

Finally, assume
r\neq 1.
In this case
\tau=\tau_{1}\cdots\tau_{u}
is the product of
u
disjoint cycles of the same length and
\tau
must permute cyclically the orbits
\Sigma_{1},\dots,\Sigma_{r}
of
\sigma.
It is not restrictive to assume that
i\in\Sigma_{i}
for
1\leqslant i\leqslant r
and that
\tau_{1}(j)=j+1
for
1\leqslant j\leqslant r-1.
Notice that
[Z^{t},\sigma]
consists of the elements
(k_{1},\dots,k_{t})\in Z^{t}
with the property that, for any
1\leqslant i\leqslant r
,
\prod_{\omega\in\Sigma_{i}}k_{\omega}=1.
Given
\lambda\in\Lambda,

we may choose
\tilde{m}
so that

\lambda[\tilde{m},\sigma]^{-1}=(v_{1},\dots,v_{t})\in Z^{t}
with

v_{1}\cdots v_{r}=1
and
v_{j}=1
if
j>r.
But then we may find
\tilde{n}=(w_{1},\dots,w_{r},1,\dots,1)
so that
[\tau,\tilde{n}]=[\tau_{1},\tilde{n}]=(v_{1},\dots,v_{t})
and therefore
\lambda=[\tau,\tilde{n}][\tilde{m},\sigma].

∎
