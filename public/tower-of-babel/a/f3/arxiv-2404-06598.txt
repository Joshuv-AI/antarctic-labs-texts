Abstract
. Generalized inversions X inv ( d ) X_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d)} and generalized descents X des ( d ) X_{\mathrm{des}}^{(d)} are an interesting combinatorial extension of the common inversion and descent statistics on permutation groups. By means of the root poset, they can be defined on all classical Weyl groups (i.e., symmetric groups, signed and even-signed permutation groups). In this paper, we investigate the bivariate normality of ( X inv ( d 1 ) , X des ( d 2 ) ) ⊤ (X_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d_{1})},X_{\mathrm{des}}^{(d_{2})})^{\top} for different regimes d 1 , d 2 d_{1},d_{2} that vary with the group size. Depending on the regimes, we classify the existence of the asymptotic correlation and provide explicit formulas. We also draw conclusions on the extreme value behavior of X inv ( d 1 ) X_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d_{1})} , X des ( d 2 ) , X_{\mathrm{des}}^{(d_{2})}, and ( X inv ( d 1 ) , X des ( d 2 ) ) ⊤ (X_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d_{1})},X_{\mathrm{des}}^{(d_{2})})^{\top} in suitable triangular arrays, discussing bounds on the number of i.i.d. samples k n k_{n} from a group of size n n so that the extreme values of these statistics are attracted to the Gumbel distribution.

1.
Introduction

The numbers of inversions and descents are two important characteristics of permutations. For a permutation
\pi\negmedspace:\{1,\ldots,n\}\rightarrow\{1,\ldots,n\},
an
inversion
is a pair
(i,j)
with
i<j,
but
\pi(i)>\pi(j)
. A
descent
is an index
i
with
\pi(i)>\pi(i+1)
, i.e., descents correspond to adjacent inversions. Equipping the symmetric group
S_{n}
with the discrete uniform probability measure induced by the point masses
\mathbb{P}(\{\pi\})=1/n!

\forall\pi\in S_{n}
, we denote the random numbers of inversions and descents by
X_{\mathrm{inv}}
and
X_{\mathrm{des}}
. These random variables can also be represented with help of i.i.d. variables
Z_{1},Z_{2},\ldots,\sim U(0,1)
by means of

(1)

\displaystyle X_{\mathrm{inv}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\textbf{1}\{Z_{i}>Z_{j}\}\,,

\displaystyle X_{\mathrm{des}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\textbf{1}\{Z_{i}>Z_{i+1}\}\,.

In (
1
), we are taking the sum of the indicators
\textbf{1}\{Z_{i}>Z_{j}\}
over all pairs
(i,j)
with
i<j
. A class of generalized inversion statistics can be constructed by restricting the sum in (
1
) to pairs
(i,j)
with
1\leq j-i\leq d
, for some
d\in\{1,\ldots,n-1\}
. Writing
\mathfrak{N}_{n,d}:=\{(i,j)\in\{1,\ldots,n\}^{2}\mid 1<j-i\leq d\}
, we say that

(2)

X_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d)}:=\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathfrak{N}_{n,d}}\textbf{1}\{Z_{i}>Z_{j}\}

counts the so-called
d
-
inversions
. The term
generalized inversions
is an umbrella term for all
d
-inversions. By choosing
d=n-1
or
d=1,
it is seen that this class includes common inversions and descents.

Generalized inversions were first introduced by de Mari & Shayman
[
11
]
to describe the Betti numbers of Hessenberg subvarieties in regular complex-valued matrices. The first probabilistic investigations of the random variables
X_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d)}
are due to Bona
[
2
]
and Pike
[
23
]
, who computed the mean and variance. Bona proved a central limit theorem (CLT) for
X_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d)}
with
d
remaining fixed, while Pike also addressed the case of
d=d(n)
varying with
n
, and proved bounds for the rate of convergence depending on both
n
and
d(n)
.

Symmetric groups belong to the class of finite irreducible Coxeter groups, which have been classified by
[
9
]
. The concept of inversions and descents can be transferred to these groups, see
[
1
, Section 1.4]
. Two other important subfamilies of the finite irreducible Coxeter groups are the
signed permutation groups

B_{n}
and the
even-signed permutation groups

D_{n}
. The group
B_{n}
consists of all maps
\pi\negthickspace:\{1,\ldots,n\}\longrightarrow\{1,\ldots,n\}\cup\{-1,\ldots,-n\}
for which
|\pi|
is a permutation. Such a map is called a
signed permutation
. The group
D_{n}
is the subgroup of
B_{n}
containing all signed permutations with an even number of negative signs. We refer to the three families
S_{n},B_{n},
and
D_{n}
as the
classical Weyl groups
.

Recently, Meier & Stump
[
22
]
introduced
generalized descents
alongside generalized inversions, and further extended the concept of both generalized inversions and generalized descents to the groups
B_{n}
and
D_{n}
. On the symmetric groups,
d
-
descents
or
generalized descents
are given by

(3)

X_{\mathrm{des}}^{(d)}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n-d}\textbf{1}\{Z_{i}>Z_{i+d}\}\,.

Meier & Stump extensively computed the mean and variance of
X_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d)}
and
X_{\mathrm{des}}^{(d)}
on the groups
B_{n}
and
D_{n},
and also stated CLTs for both
X_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d)}
and
X_{\mathrm{des}}^{(d)}
on all classical Weyl groups. As
d=d(n)
varies with
n,
the CLTs tacitly require that the number of summands in (
2
) or (
3
) tends to infinity.

The joint dependency structure of
X_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d_{1})}
and
X_{\mathrm{des}}^{(d_{2})}
for two different sequences
d_{1}=d_{1}(n),

d_{2}=d_{2}(n)
is interesting. For common inversions and descents, the asymptotic correlation of
X_{\mathrm{inv}}
and
X_{\mathrm{des}}
is zero and the CLT for
(X_{\mathrm{inv}},X_{\mathrm{des}})^{\top}
is satisfied, with the limit being the bivariate standard normal distribution. This was proven by Fang & Röllin
[
15
]
for symmetric groups and recently by Dörr & Heiny
[
12
]
for the other classical Weyl groups.

In this paper, we investigate the bivariate asymptotic normality of generalized inversions and descents. It turns out that the asymptotic correlation of
X_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d_{1})}
and
X_{\mathrm{des}}^{(d_{2})}
can be strictly positive if both
d_{1}
and
d_{2}
grow linearly, i.e., if
d_{1}/n
and
d_{2}/n
do not tend to zero. In that case, the asymptotic correlation explicitly depends on the limits of
d_{1}/n
and
d_{2}/n
. If these limits do not exist, then the limit of
\mathrm{Corr}(X_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d_{1})},X_{\mathrm{des}}^{(d_{2})})
commonly does not exist as well, hence, there is no CLT. Other interesting cases arise if
d_{1}
and
d_{2}
both remain bounded. Then, the asymptotic correlation turns out to be nonzero if
d_{1}\geq d_{2}
and if
d_{1}
remains constant. In the extreme case of
d_{1}=d_{2}=1,
the two statistics
X_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(1)}
and
X_{\mathrm{des}}^{(1)}
are identical. If
(d_{1}(n))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
has multiple accumulation points, then there is no CLT. We will investigate this behavior in thorough detail.

Moreover, the investigation of extreme value asymptotics for common inversions and descents was initiated in
[
13
,
12
]
, so we aim to derive the corresponding results for generalized inversions and descents. As these permutation statistics are discrete-valued, extreme value limit theorems are formulated for triangular arrays of i.i.d. copies of
X_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d_{1})}
and
X_{\mathrm{des}}^{(d_{2})}
. The
n
-th row of such a triangular array corresponds to a classical Weyl group of rank
n
, and the number
k_{n}
of i.i.d. copies is chosen depending on the rank. The row-wise maximum
M_{n}
of these i.i.d. copies is considered and one seeks to find deterministic constants
a_{n},b_{n}
and an
extreme value distribution

G
such that
a_{n}^{-1}(M_{n}-b_{n})\overset{\mathcal{D}}{\longrightarrow}G
holds.

For permutation statistics that satisfy the CLT, the typical extreme value distribution
G
is the Gumbel distribution, and the weak convergence
a_{n}^{-1}(M_{n}-b_{n})\overset{\mathcal{D}}{\longrightarrow}G
is simply called
Gumbel attraction
. Typically, the extreme value behavior in triangular arrays is degenerate if there are too many i.i.d. samples per row (i.e., if
k_{n}
is too large). We therefore seek to find asymptotic regimes of
k_{n}
as large as possible so that Gumbel attraction of
X_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d_{1})},

X_{\mathrm{des}}^{(d_{2})}
and the joint statistic
(X_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d_{1})},X_{\mathrm{des}}^{(d_{2})})^{\top}
is guaranteed. We thoroughly investigate the impact of
d_{1}
and
d_{2}
on these bounds for
k_{n}
as well.

This paper is structured as follows. Section
2
reviews basic properties on generalized inversions and descents. Section
3
is devoted to the study of the asymptotic correlation and the bivariate CLT of generalized inversions and descents. The CLT is achieved through high-dimensional Gaussian approximation rates and Hajék projections. Section
4
addresses their extreme value limit theorems. The appendix sections
A
,
B
,
C
collect all proofs. We use the shorthand notation
x\wedge y:=\min\{x,y\}
and we use typical Landau notation for positive sequences
a_{n},b_{n}
as follows:

•

a_{n}=O(b_{n})
means that

\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}a_{n}/b_{n}<\infty
.

•

a_{n}=o(b_{n})
means that
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}a_{n}/b_{n}=0
. This is also written as
b_{n}=\omega(a_{n})
or
b_{n}\gg a_{n}
.

•

a_{n}=\Theta(b_{n})
means that
a_{n}
and
b_{n}
have the same order of magnitude, i.e., both
a_{n}=O(b_{n})
and
b_{n}=O(a_{n})
hold.

•

a_{n}=b_{n}+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)
means that
a_{n},b_{n}
are sequences of random variables with
a_{n}-b_{n}\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\longrightarrow}0
.

3.
Bivariate Asymptotic Normality

The main work of this paper is to prove and investigate the asymptotic normality of the joint permutation statistic
\left(X_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d_{1})},X_{\mathrm{des}}^{(d_{2})}\right)^{\top}
, where
d_{1}=d_{1}(n),

d_{2}=d_{2}(n)
can depend on the group size
n
. All proofs in this section are deferred to the appendix.

We will find that for low regimes of
d_{1},
the dependency structure of
\left(X_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d_{1})},X_{\mathrm{des}}^{(d_{2})}\right)^{\top}
is sufficiently sparse, so that
\left(X_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d_{1})},X_{\mathrm{des}}^{(d_{2})}\right)^{\top}
can be tackled by Gaussian approximation. For larger regimes, the complexity of the dependency structure needs to be reduced, and we aim to do so by replacing
X_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d_{1})}
with its Hájek projection. In Section
3.1
, we introduce high-dimensional Gaussian approximation and prove that
\left(\hat{X}_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d_{1})},X_{\mathrm{des}}^{(d_{2})}\right)^{\top}
exhibits the same asymptotic behavior as
\left(X_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d_{1})},X_{\mathrm{des}}^{(d_{2})}\right)^{\top}
. Subsequently, in Section
3.2
we thoroughly investigate the covariance of
\hat{X}_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d_{1})}
and
X_{\mathrm{des}}^{(d_{2})}
and the limit behavior of the correlation
\mathrm{Corr}\left(\hat{X}_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d_{1})},X_{\mathrm{des}}^{(d_{2})}\right)=\mathrm{Cov}\left(\hat{X}_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d_{1})},X_{\mathrm{des}}^{(d_{2})}\right)/\left(\sigma(\hat{X}_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d_{1})})\sigma(X_{\mathrm{des}}^{(d_{2})})\right)
.
In Section
3.3
, we investigate the asymptotic behavior of the correlation of the original joint statistic
\left(X_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d_{1})},X_{\mathrm{des}}^{(d_{2})}\right)^{\top}
for the low regimes, where the use of the Hájek projection is not feasible.

3.1.
Gaussian approximation for sparse dependency structures

The classical multivariate CLT states that for a sequence or a triangular array
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{p}
and an appropriate
p
-variate normal distribution
\mathcal{N}
, under suitable conditions, the Gaussian approximation error

(5)

\sup_{\textbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}|\mathbb{P}(X_{n}\leq\textbf{x})-\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{N}\leq\textbf{x})|=\sup_{A=(-\infty,\textbf{x}]^{p}}|\mathbb{P}(X_{n}\in A)-\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{N}\in A)|

tends to zero as
n\rightarrow\infty
. Even stronger statements are obtained if the system
\mathcal{A}=\{(-\infty,\textbf{x}]^{p}\}
of negative orthants is extended to, e.g., the class of all hyperrectangles or all convex sets, and if the dimension
p
of the samples
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
can grow with
n
. The latter is commonly referred to as the
high-dimensional setting
and it encompasses the fixed-dimensional setting by artificially repeating components of a random vector in fixed dimension.

Seminal works for high-dimensional Gaussian approximation involving independent random vectors are due to
[
6
,
7
]
, followed by several efforts to reduce the rate of convergence in (
5
) and the growth rate of
p
, see
[
8
,
10
,
14
,
19
,
20
]
. The uniform consideration of all hyperrectangles yields not only CLTs but also extreme value limit results, which will be used in Section
4
.

An even more significant extension of Gaussian approximation is the consideration of more general dependency structures within the random vectors
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n},
dropping the assumption of independence. A seminal work in this direction was achieved by Chang
et al.

[
4
]
, which classifies the dependence of random vectors by dependency graphs. Let
\left(X_{j}^{(n)}\right)_{j=1,\ldots,n}
be a triangular array of
p
-dimensional centered random vectors (without restriction), and let

\displaystyle X^{(n)}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{t=1}^{n}X_{t}^{(n)},

\displaystyle\Sigma^{(n)}

\displaystyle:=\mathrm{Var}(X^{(n)})\,,

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{n}

\displaystyle\sim\mathrm{N}_{p}(0,\Sigma^{(n)})\,.

Let
\mathcal{A}^{\mathrm{re}}
be the system of all
p
-dimensional hyperrectangles including infinite bounds, i.e.,
\mathcal{A}^{\mathrm{re}}:=\Bigl\{\{\textbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}\negmedspace:\textbf{a}\leq\textbf{w}\leq\textbf{b}\}\mid\textbf{a},\textbf{b}\in[-\infty,\infty]^{p}\Bigr\}\,.
Let

r_{n}(\mathcal{A}^{\mathrm{re}}):=\sup_{A\in\mathcal{A}^{\mathrm{re}}}|\mathbb{P}(X^{(n)}\in A)-\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{N}_{n}\in A)|\,.

In particular,

is an upper bound for

which is relevant for the CLT, and for

which is relevant for the asymptotics of extreme values. Now, let

be a dependency graph for

which consists of all edges

Theorem 3.1

Let

and
\Delta_{n}^{*}
be as above. Under some regularity conditions which are satisfied by bounded and non-degenerate random variables, it holds that

r_{n}(\mathcal{A}^{\mathrm{re}})=O\left(\frac{(\Delta_{n}\Delta_{n}^{*})^{1/3}\log(p)^{7/6}}{n^{1/6}}\right).

In particular, if
\Bigl(X_{j}^{(n)}\Bigr)_{j=1,\ldots,n}
is
m
-dependent for some global constant
m\in\mathbb{N},
then
r_{n}(\mathcal{A}^{\mathrm{re}})=O\bigl(n^{-1/6}\log(p)^{7/6}\bigr)
.

In what follows, we consider the joint statistic
\left(X_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d_{1})},X_{\mathrm{des}}^{(d_{2})}\right)^{\top}
for two sequences
d_{1}=d_{1}(n),

d_{2}=d_{2}(n)
, with
d_{1}(n),d_{2}(n)\leq d_{\max}-1

\forall n\in\mathbb{N}
. Both
X_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d_{1})}
and
X_{\mathrm{des}}^{(d_{2})}
are based on a sequence of classical Weyl groups
(W_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}},
where each
W_{n}
is one of
S_{n},B_{n},
or
D_{n}
. We keep using
d
as an umbrella notation for
d_{1}
or
d_{2},
depending on the context.

If
d_{2}(n)
diverges as
n\rightarrow\infty
, then there is no
m\in\mathbb{N}
for which all
X_{\mathrm{des}}^{(d)}
are
m
-dependent, but the dependency structure of
X_{\mathrm{des}}^{(d)}
is still sparse. Recall that
X_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d)}
and
X_{\mathrm{des}}^{(d)}
are constructed from i.i.d. random variables
Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{n}
. According to (
3
), (
4c
), (
4d
), we can represent
X_{\mathrm{des}}^{(d)}
as a sum of indicator variables, each of which depends on at most three others. Therefore, the first-order and second-order maximum degrees
\Delta_{n},\Delta_{n}^{*}
are bounded in the way of
\Delta_{n}\leq 3
and
\Delta_{n}^{*}\leq 9
.
However, it has to be ensured that
X_{\mathrm{des}}^{(d)}
consists of a growing number of summands, which was already noted in
[
22
, Corollary 2.7]
. This number of summands equals the number of positive roots with height exactly
d
, which we denote by
N_{n,d}^{=}
.

The maximum degrees
\Delta_{n},\Delta_{n}^{*}
of the dependency graphs of the joint statistic
\left(X_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d_{1})},X_{\mathrm{des}}^{(d_{2})}\right)^{\top}
are bounded in the way of
\Delta_{n}\leq 4d_{1}
and
\Delta_{n}^{*}\leq 8d_{1}
. Moreover, we have to take into account that by (
2
), (
4a
), (
4b
),
X_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d_{1})}
is based on
\Theta(nd_{1})
summands, so we have to replace
n
with
nd
in Theorem
3.1
. Therefore, Theorem
3.1
gives an
o(1)
bound of
r_{n}(\mathcal{A}^{\mathrm{re}})
only if
d_{1}^{2/3}\log(nd_{1})^{7/6}=o((nd_{1})^{1/6})
, which leads to the condition
d_{1}=o\left(n^{1/3}\log(n^{4/3})^{-7/3}\right)
. The consideration of these
low regimes
is postponed to Section
3.3
.

For any larger regime of
d_{1},
the dependency structure of
\left(X_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d_{1})},X_{\mathrm{des}}^{(d_{2})}\right)^{\top}
is too complex to apply Theorem
3.1
. For these regimes, we resort to Hájek projections in order to approximate
X_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d)}
with a sum of independent variables.

Definition 3.2
.

Let
Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{n}
be independent random variables, and let
X
be another random variable. Then, the
Hájek projection
of
X
with respect to
Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{n}
is given by

\hat{X}:=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}(X\mid Z_{k})-(n-1)\mathbb{E}(X).

As every
\mathbb{E}(X\mid Z_{k})
is a measurable function only in
Z_{k}
, the Hájek projection is a sum of independent random variables. If
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a sequence of random variables with each
X_{n}
depending on
Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{n}
, then the Hájek projections
\hat{X}_{n}
should give a sufficiently accurate approximation to
X_{n}
. This can be ensured by the following criterion.

Theorem 3.3

(cf.
[
27
]
, Theorem 11.2)
.

Consider a sequence
(X_{n})_{n\geq 1}
of random variables and their associated Hájek projections
(\hat{X}_{n})_{n\geq 1}
. If
\mathrm{Var}(\hat{X}_{n})\sim\mathrm{Var}(X_{n})
as
n\rightarrow\infty,
then

\frac{X_{n}-\mathbb{E}(X_{n})}{\mathrm{Var}(X_{n})^{1/2}}=\frac{\hat{X}_{n}-\mathbb{E}(\hat{X}_{n})}{\mathrm{Var}(\hat{X}_{n})^{1/2}}+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1).

In the case of
d_{1}=\Omega\left(n^{1/3}\log(n^{4/3})^{-7/3}\right)
, we have to replace
X_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d_{1})}
with the Hájek projection
\hat{X}_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d_{1})}
, which is based on the representations (
2
), (
4a
), and (
4b
). The validity of the condition in Theorem
3.3
can be classified once we compute the variance of
\hat{X}_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d_{1})}
.

Lemma 3.4
.

For the Hájek projection
\hat{X}_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d)}
of generalized inversions on symmetric groups, it holds that

\mathrm{Var}\left(\hat{X}_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d)}\right)=\frac{1}{72}\left(4d^{3}+6d^{2}+2d\right)

if
d\leq n/2,
and

\mathrm{Var}\left(\hat{X}_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d)}\right)=-\frac{1}{6}d^{3}+\left(\frac{1}{3}n-\frac{1}{4}\right)d^{2}-\left(\frac{1}{6}n^{2}-\frac{1}{3}n+\frac{1}{12}\right)d+\frac{1}{36}n^{3}-\frac{1}{12}n^{2}+\frac{1}{18}n

if
d>n/2
.

Lemma 3.5
.

For the Hájek projection
\hat{X}_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d)}
of generalized inversions on both the signed permutation groups
B_{n}
and the even-signed permutation groups
D_{n},
it holds that

\mathrm{Var}\left(\hat{X}_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d)}\right)=\begin{cases}d^{3}/36+O(d^{2})\,,&d\leq n\\
-d^{3}/12+nd^{2}/3-n^{2}d/3+n^{3}/9\,,&d>n\end{cases}.

By recalling the asymptotic quantifications of
\mathrm{Var}(X_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d)})
given in Theorem
2.3
and Lemma
2.5
, we obtain the following conclusion:

Corollary 3.6
.

Consider the generalized inversion statistic
X_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d)}
on classical Weyl groups
(W_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}},
where each
W_{n}
is one of
S_{n},B_{n},
or
D_{n},
and
d=d(n)
satisfies
1\leq d\leq d_{\max}-1

\forall n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Then,
\mathrm{Var}\left(\hat{X}_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d)}\right)\sim\mathrm{Var}\left(X_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d)}\right)
holds if and only if
d\gg\sqrt{n}
.

From Theorem
3.1
, Corollary
3.6
and Slutsky’s lemma, we can conclude that
(X_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d_{1})},X_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d_{2})})
satisfies the CLT, provided that
N_{n,d_{2}}^{=}\longrightarrow\infty
, and
d_{1}
is in the aforementioned regimes
d_{1}=o\left(n^{1/3}\log(n^{4/3})^{-7/3}\right)
or
d_{1}=\omega(n^{1/2})
, and the correlation
\hat{\rho}_{n}
of
\hat{X}_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d_{1})}
and
X_{\mathrm{des}}^{(d_{2})}
has a limit, which is the same as the limit of
\rho_{n}=\mathrm{Corr}(X_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d_{1})},X_{\mathrm{des}}^{(d_{2})})
.

Corollary 3.7
.

If either
d_{1}=o\left(n^{1/3}\log(n^{4/3})^{-7/3}\right)
or
d_{1}\gg\sqrt{n},
and if
d_{2}=d_{2}(n)\in\{1,\ldots,d_{\max}-1\}
is chosen so that
N_{n,d_{2}}^{=}\longrightarrow\infty,
and if

\rho:=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\hat{\rho}_{n}:=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathrm{Corr}(\hat{X}_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d_{1})},X_{\mathrm{des}}^{(d_{2})})

exists, then
\left(X_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d_{1})},X_{\mathrm{des}}^{(d_{2})}\right)^{\top}
satisfies the CLT, that is,

\left(\frac{X_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d_{1})}-\mathbb{E}(X_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d_{1})})}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(X_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d_{1})})}},\,\frac{X_{\mathrm{des}}^{(d_{2})}-\mathbb{E}(X_{\mathrm{des}}^{(d_{2})})}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(X_{\mathrm{des}}^{(d_{2})})}}\right)^{\top}\overset{\mathcal{D}}{\longrightarrow}\mathcal{N}_{2}\left(0,I_{\rho}\right),\qquad I_{\rho}:=\begin{pmatrix}1&\rho\\
\rho&1\end{pmatrix}.

3.2.
Asymptotic correlation for large regimes of
d_{1}

We now investigate and classify the asymptotic correlation
\rho
of
\hat{X}_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d_{1})}
and
X_{\mathrm{des}}^{(d_{2})}
for all regimes
d_{1}\gg\sqrt{n}
.

Symmetric groups

Roughly speaking, the asymptotic correlation
\rho
is almost always zero if
d_{1}
or
d_{2}
are sublinear, or if the remainder
n-d_{2}
is sublinear. If both
d_{1}
and
d_{2}
grow linearly in the way of
d_{1}/n\rightarrow c_{1},d_{2}/n\rightarrow c_{2}
with
c_{1},c_{2}\in(0,1),
then
\rho
exists but explicitly depends on
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
. If the sequences
d_{1}/n,d_{2}/n
have multiple accumulation points, then the CLT almost never holds.

Lemma 3.8
.

For symmetric groups, the covariance of the Hájek projection
\hat{X}_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d_{1})}
and
X_{\mathrm{des}}^{(d_{2})}
is given as follows: If
d_{1}\leq d_{2},
then

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}\left(\hat{X}_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d_{1})},X_{\mathrm{des}}^{(d_{2})}\right)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}d_{1}(d_{1}+1)/12,&d_{1}+d_{2}\leq n\\
n(d_{1}+d_{2})/6-d_{1}d_{2}/6-d_{2}(d_{2}+1)/12-n(n-1)/12,&d_{1}+d_{2}>n\end{cases}.

In particular, if
d_{1}<d_{2}
, then
\mathrm{Cov}\left(\hat{X}_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d_{1})},X_{\mathrm{des}}^{(d_{2})}\right)=\mathrm{Cov}\left(X_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d_{1})},X_{\mathrm{des}}^{(d_{2})}\right)
. If
d_{1}>d_{2},
then

\mathrm{Cov}\left(\hat{X}_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d_{1})},X_{\mathrm{des}}^{(d_{2})}\right)=\begin{cases}d_{1}d_{2}/6-d_{2}(d_{2}-1)/12,&d_{1}+d_{2}\leq n\\
n(d_{1}+d_{2})/6-d_{2}^{2}/6-d_{1}(d_{1}+1)/12-n(n-1)/12,&d_{1}+d_{2}>n\end{cases}.

We can now precisely characterize the situations in which the limiting correlation
\rho
exists, and compute
\rho
accordingly.

Theorem 3.9
.

On the symmetric groups, the limiting correlation

\rho=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathrm{Corr}\left(\hat{X}_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d_{1})},X_{\mathrm{des}}^{(d_{2})}\right)

is classified as follows. First, assume
d_{1}=o(n),
while
d_{1}=\omega(n^{1/2})
.

a)

If either
n-d_{2}\ll d_{1}
or
n-d_{2}\gg d_{1},
then
\rho=0
.

b)

If
d_{2}\asymp n-cd_{1}
for some
c\geq 1,
then
\rho=1/(\sqrt{2}c)
.

c)

If
d_{2}\asymp n-cd_{1}
for some
c\in(0,1),
then
\rho=\sqrt{2c}(1-c/2)
.

In what follows, assume
d_{1}/n\rightarrow c_{1}
and
d_{2}/n\rightarrow c_{2}
with
0<c_{1}\leq 1
and
0\leq c_{2}\leq 1
. Put

\displaystyle p(c_{1})

\displaystyle:=-c_{1}^{3}/2+c_{1}^{2}-c_{1}/2+1/12\,,

\displaystyle f(c_{1},c_{2})

\displaystyle:=\sqrt{1-c_{2}}(2c_{1}+c_{2}-1)\,,

\displaystyle g(c_{1},c_{2})

\displaystyle:=(2c_{1}c_{2}-c_{2}^{2})/\sqrt{1+c_{2}}\,,

\displaystyle h(c_{1},c_{2})

\displaystyle:=(2c_{2}(1-c_{2})-(1-c_{1})^{2})/\sqrt{p(c_{1})}\,.

Then:

d)

If
c_{1}\leq c_{2}\leq 1/2,
then
\rho=\sqrt{6}/2\cdot\sqrt{c_{1}/(1+c_{2})}.

e)

If
c_{1}\leq 1/2
and
c_{2}>1/2,
then

\rho=\begin{cases}\sqrt{c_{1}}/\sqrt{2(1-c_{2})}\,,&c_{2}\leq 1-c_{1}\\
f(c_{1},c_{2})/(\sqrt{2}c_{1}^{3/2})\,,&c_{2}>1-c_{1}\end{cases}.

f)

If
1/2<c_{1}\leq c_{2},
then
\rho=\sqrt{3}/6\cdot f(c_{1},c_{2})/\sqrt{p(c_{1})}
.

g)

If
1/2\geq c_{1}>c_{2}>0,
then
\rho=\sqrt{6}g(c_{1},c_{2})/(2c_{1}^{3/2})
.

h)

If
c_{1}>1/2
and
c_{2}\leq 1-c_{1},
then
\rho=g(c_{1},c_{2})/(2\sqrt{p(c_{1})}).

i)

If
c_{1}>c_{2}>1-c_{1},
then

\rho=\begin{cases}h(c_{1},c_{2})/(2\sqrt{1+c_{2}})\,,&c_{2}\leq 1/2\\
\sqrt{3}/6\cdot h(c_{1},c_{2})/\sqrt{1-c_{2}}\,,&c_{2}>1/2\end{cases}.

In particular, if
c_{2}=0,
then always
\rho=0
. If
c_{2}=1
and
c_{1}>0,
then we have
\rho=0
as well.

Signed and even-signed permutation groups

The groups
B_{n}
and
D_{n}
do not differ in terms of the asymptotic behavior of
\mathrm{Cov}\left(\hat{X}_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d_{1})},X_{\mathrm{des}}^{(d_{2})}\right)
or the asymptotic correlation of
\hat{X}_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d_{1})}
and
X_{\mathrm{des}}^{(d_{2})}
. Therefore, we list the magnitudes of
\mathrm{Cov}\left(\hat{X}_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d_{1})},X_{\mathrm{des}}^{(d_{2})}\right)
and the asymptotic correlations only for the signed permutation groups
B_{n}
.

Lemma 3.10
.

Consider the generalized inversion and descent statistics
X_{\mathrm{inv}}^{B,(d_{1})},

X_{\mathrm{des}}^{B,(d_{2})}
on the signed permutation groups. Up to an error in the scale of
O(d_{1}),
the covariance of the Hájek projection
\hat{X}_{\mathrm{inv}}^{B,(d_{1})}
and
X_{\mathrm{des}}^{B,(d_{2})}
is given as follows: If
d_{1}\leq d_{2},
then

\mathrm{Cov}\left(\hat{X}_{\mathrm{inv}}^{B,(d_{1})},X_{\mathrm{des}}^{B,(d_{2})}\right)=\begin{cases}d_{1}(d_{1}+1)/24,&d_{1}+d_{2}\leq 2n\\
(2n-d_{2}+1)(d_{1}+d_{2}/2-n)/12,&d_{1}+d_{2}>2n\end{cases}.

If
d_{1}>d_{2}
and
d_{1}+d_{2}\leq 2n,
then

\mathrm{Cov}\left(\hat{X}_{\mathrm{inv}}^{B,(d_{1})},X_{\mathrm{des}}^{B,(d_{2})}\right)=\frac{1}{24}d_{2}(d_{2}+1)+\frac{1}{12}d_{2}(d_{1}-d_{2})\,.

If
d_{1}>d_{2}
and
d_{1}+d_{2}>2n,
then in case of
d_{2}<n,

\mathrm{Cov}\left(\hat{X}_{\mathrm{inv}}^{B,(d_{1})},X_{\mathrm{des}}^{B,(d_{2})}\right)=-\frac{1}{24}d_{1}(d_{1}-4n+3)-\frac{1}{12}d_{2}(d_{2}-2n+1)-\frac{n^{2}}{6}+\frac{n}{4}\,,

and in case of
d_{2}>n,

\mathrm{Cov}\left(\hat{X}_{\mathrm{inv}}^{B,(d_{1})},X_{\mathrm{des}}^{B,(d_{2})}\right)=-\frac{1}{24}d_{1}(d_{1}-4n+3)-\frac{1}{12}d_{2}(d_{2}-2n-1)-\frac{n^{2}}{6}+\frac{n}{12}\,.

We now make observations similar to those for symmetric groups regarding the limiting correlation
\rho
appearing in the CLT for
(X_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(B,d_{1})},X_{\mathrm{des}}^{(B,d_{2})})
and
(X_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(D,d_{1})},X_{\mathrm{des}}^{(D,d_{2})})
. The proof of the next theorem works analogously to the proof of Theorem
3.9
and is therefore omitted.

Theorem 3.11
.

On the signed and even-signed permutation groups, the limiting correlation of
\hat{X}_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d_{1})}
and
X_{\mathrm{des}}^{(d_{2})}
is classified as follows. First, assume
d_{1}=o(n),
while
d_{1}=\omega(n^{1/2})
. In that case, we observe the same results as in Theorem
3.9
,
i.e.,

•

If either
n-d_{2}\ll d_{1}
or
n-d_{2}\gg d_{1},
then
\rho=0
.

•

If
d_{2}\asymp n-cd_{1}
for some
c\geq 1,
then
\rho=1/(\sqrt{2}c)
.

•

If
d_{2}\asymp n-cd_{1}
for some
c\in(0,1),
then
\rho=\sqrt{2c}(1-c/2)
.

Now, assume
d_{1}/n\rightarrow c_{1}
and
d_{2}/n\rightarrow c_{2}
for some
c_{1},c_{2}\in(0,2]
. Put

\displaystyle p(c_{1})

\displaystyle:=-\frac{1}{12}c_{1}^{3}+\frac{1}{3}c_{1}^{2}-\frac{1}{3}c_{1}+\frac{1}{9}\,,

\displaystyle f(c_{1},c_{2})

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{4}c_{1}(c_{1}-4)+\frac{1}{2}c_{2}(c_{2}-2)+1\,.

Then:

•

If
c_{1}\leq c_{2}\leq 1,
then

\rho=\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{c_{1}}}{\sqrt{1+c_{2}/2}}\,.

•

If
c_{2}<c_{1}\leq 1,
then

\rho=\sqrt{3}\,\frac{c_{2}(c_{1}-c_{2}/2)}{c_{1}^{3/2}\sqrt{1+c_{2}/2}}\,.

•

If
c_{1}\leq 1,

c_{2}>1,
and
c_{1}+c_{2}\leq 2,
then

\rho=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\frac{\sqrt{c_{1}}}{\sqrt{2-c_{2}}}\,.

•

If
c_{1}\leq 1,c_{2}>1,
and
c_{1}+c_{2}>2,
then

\rho=\sqrt{2}\,\frac{\sqrt{2-c_{2}}}{c_{1}^{3/2}}\left(c_{1}+\frac{c_{2}}{2}-1\right).

•

If

then

\rho=\frac{1}{\sqrt{6}}\,\frac{\sqrt{2-c_{2}}}{\sqrt{p(c_{1})}}\left(c_{1}+\frac{c_{2}}{2}-1\right).

•

If
2\geq c_{1}>c_{2}>1,
then

\rho=-\frac{\sqrt{6}}{3}\frac{f(c_{1},c_{2})}{\sqrt{(2-c_{2})p(c_{1})}}\,.

•

If
c_{1}>1,c_{2}\leq 1,
and
c_{1}+c_{2}\leq 2,
then

\rho=\frac{1}{2}\,\frac{c_{2}(c_{1}-c_{2}/2)}{\sqrt{(1+c_{2}/2)p(c_{1})}}\,.

•

If
c_{1}>1,c_{2}\leq 1,
and
c_{1}+c_{2}>2,
then

\rho=-\frac{f(c_{1},c_{2})}{\sqrt{(1+c_{2}/2)p(c_{1})}}\,.

In conclusion, the cases
c_{2}=2
and
c_{2}=0,

c_{1}>0
yield
\rho=0
.

3.3.
Asymptotic correlation for low regimes of
d_{1}

For the low regimes

d_{1}=o\left(n^{1/3}\log(n^{4/3})^{-7/3}\right),

the replacement of
X_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d_{1})}
with
\hat{X}_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d_{1})}
is infeasible by Corollary
3.6
, and we obtain Gaussian approximation for the original joint statistic
(X_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d_{1})},X_{\mathrm{des}}^{(d_{2})})^{\top}
. Therefore, we now investigate the correlation
\rho_{n}=\mathrm{Corr}\left(X_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d_{1})},X_{\mathrm{des}}^{(d_{2})}\right)
and its limit behavior. An interesting observation is that
X_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d_{1})}
and
X_{\mathrm{des}}^{(d_{1})}
coincide if
d_{1}=d_{2}=1,
which implies
\rho=1
if
d_{1}\equiv d_{2}\equiv 1
throughout. We will generally notice a nonzero limit correlation
\rho=\rho(d_{1})
if both
d_{1},d_{2}
remain fixed. If
d_{1}(n)
is bounded but has multiple accumulation points, then
\rho
does not exist. On the contrary, if
d_{1}(n)
is not bounded, then we always have
\rho=0
, in analogy with the sublinear cases in Theorem
3.9
.

Symmetric groups

Similar as in Lemma
3.8
, we provide an exact computation and classification of the covariance
\mathrm{Cov}(X_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d_{1})},X_{\mathrm{des}}^{(d_{2})})
in the relevant regimes of
d_{1}
.

Theorem 3.12
.

For any two numbers
d_{1},d_{2}\in\{1,\ldots,n-1\}
with
d_{1}<n/2,
the covariance of
X_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d_{1})}
and
X_{\mathrm{des}}^{(d_{2})}
is given as follows, distinguishing by the relative order of
d_{1}
and
d_{2}\negmedspace:

If
d_{1}<d_{2},
then
\mathrm{Cov}\left(X_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d_{1})},X_{\mathrm{des}}^{(d_{2})}\right)=\mathrm{Cov}\left(\hat{X}_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d_{1})},X_{\mathrm{des}}^{(d_{2})}\right),
i.e.,

\mathrm{Cov}\left(X_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d_{1})},X_{\mathrm{des}}^{(d_{2})}\right)=\begin{cases}d_{1}(d_{1}+1)/12,&d_{1}+d_{2}\leq n\\
(n(d_{1}+d_{2})-d_{1}d_{2})/6-(d_{2}(d_{2}+1)+n(n-1))/12,&d_{1}+d_{2}>n\end{cases}.

If
d_{1}=d_{2}=:d<n/2,
then

\mathrm{Cov}\left(X_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d)},X_{\mathrm{des}}^{(d)}\right)=\frac{n+d^{2}}{12}\,.

If
d_{1}>d_{2},
then

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}\left(X_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d_{1})},X_{\mathrm{des}}^{(d_{2})}\right)=\frac{d_{1}d_{2}}{6}+\frac{n-d_{2}^{2}}{12}\,.

Theorem 3.13
.

Let
d_{1}=o\left(n^{1/3}\log(n^{4/3})^{-7/3}\right)
and let
d_{2}=d_{2}(n)\in\{1,\ldots,n-1\}
be arbitrary. Then, for the limiting correlation

\rho=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathrm{Corr}\left(X_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d_{1})},X_{\mathrm{des}}^{(d_{2})}\right),

the following holds:

a)

If
d_{1}<d_{2}
throughout, then
\rho=0
.

b)

If
d_{1}\geq d_{2}
throughout and
d_{1}
eventually remains constant, then
\rho=1/\sqrt{d_{1}}
. If
d_{1}
is bounded but has multiple accumulation points, then
\rho
does not exist.

b)

If
d_{1}\geq d_{2}
and
d_{1}\rightarrow\infty
as
n\rightarrow\infty,
then
\rho=0
.

In conclusion, if
d_{1}
and
d_{2}
both remain bounded, there is a sharp threshold between the cases
d_{1}<d_{2}
and
d_{1}\geq d_{2}
.

Signed and even-signed permutation groups

While the generalized inversions and descents statistics on the signed and even-signed permutation groups have a more complex structure, the asymptotic behavior of
\mathrm{Corr}(X_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d_{1})},X_{\mathrm{des}}^{(d_{2})})
is basically the same as for the symmetric groups.

Theorem 3.14
.

Consider the families of signed permutation groups
(B_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
or even-signed permutation groups
(D_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
. Let
d_{1}=o\left(n^{1/3}\log(n^{4/3})^{-7/3}\right)
and let
d_{2}=d_{2}(n)\in\{1,\ldots,d_{\max}-1\}
be arbitrary. Then, for the limiting correlation

\rho=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathrm{Corr}\left(X_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d_{1})},X_{\mathrm{des}}^{(d_{2})}\right),

the same results as in Theorem

hold:

a)

If
d_{1}<d_{2}
throughout, then
\rho=0
.

b)

If
d_{1}\geq d_{2}
throughout and
d_{1}
eventually remains constant, then

. If

is bounded but has multiple accumulation points, then

does not exist.

b)

If

and

then

4.
Extreme Value Limit Theorems

Extreme values of the univariate statistics
X_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d)}
and
X_{\mathrm{des}}^{(d)}

We now postulate the univariate and bivariate extreme value limit theorems (EVLTs) for
X_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d)}
and
X_{\mathrm{des}}^{(d)}
with the help of the methods used in
[
12
]
. Recall (e.g., by
[
21
]
, Theorem 1.5.3) that by use of

\displaystyle\alpha_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\log n}}\,,

\displaystyle\beta_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\alpha_{n}}-\frac{1}{2}\alpha_{n}\bigl(\log\log n+\log(4\pi)\bigr)\,,

the maximum of i.i.d. variables
N_{1},\ldots,N_{n}\sim\mathrm{N}(0,1)
is attracted to the Gumbel distribution, that is,
\forall x\in\mathbb{R}
:

\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{i=1,\ldots,n}N_{i}\leq\alpha_{n}x+\beta_{n}\right)\longrightarrow\exp(-\exp(-x))=:\Lambda(x).

Since the random numbers of generalized inversions and descents are discrete distributions, we are interested in the row-wise extremes of a triangular array
(X_{n1},\ldots,X_{nk_{n}}),
where for each
n\in\mathbb{N},
the block
X_{n1},\ldots,X_{nk_{n}}
consists of i.i.d. samples based on one of the classical Weyl groups
S_{n},B_{n}
or
D_{n}
. Each of the following EVLTs states a regime for the number of samples
k_{n}
for which Gumbel attraction is guaranteed.

For a univariate triangular array consisting of generalized descents, it is not necessary to use the Hájek projection. It has already been argued in
[
12
, Remark 4.2]
that a subexponential bound on
k_{n}
can be obtained if the Hájek projection is not needed. So, in the univariate EVLT for generalized descents, we can proceed in analogy to
[
12
, Remark 4.2]
:

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
(X_{nj})_{j=1,\ldots,k_{n}}
be a row-wise i.i.d. triangular array with
X_{n1}\overset{\mathcal{D}}{=}X_{\mathrm{des}}^{(d)}
for a sequence
d=d(n)
with
1\leq d\leq d_{\max}-1
and
N_{n,d}^{=}\longrightarrow\infty,
and let
M_{n}:=\max\{X_{n1},\ldots,X_{nk_{n}}\}
. Let
a_{n}:=\sigma\bigl(X_{\mathrm{des}}^{(d)}\bigr)\alpha_{k_{n}}
and let
b_{n}:=\sigma\bigl(X_{\mathrm{des}}^{(d)}\bigr)\beta_{k_{n}}+\mathbb{E}\bigl(X_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d)}\bigr).
If
k_{n}=\exp\left(o\bigl((N_{n,d}^{=})^{1/7}\bigr)\right),
then

\forall x\in\mathbb{R}\negmedspace:\quad\mathbb{P}(M_{n}\leq a_{n}x+b_{n})\longrightarrow\Lambda(x)\,.

Proof.

By (
3
), (
4c
), (
4d
), we can write each
X_{n1}
as a sum of
N_{n,d}^{=}
indicator variables with
\Delta_{n}\leq 3
and
\Delta_{n}^{*}\leq 9
. This means we apply Theorem
3.1
with respect to the numbers of summands
N_{n,d}^{=}
and for high-dimensional vectors built from
k_{n}
iterations of
X_{n1},
yielding

|\mathbb{P}(M_{n}\leq a_{n}x+b_{n})-\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{M}_{n}\leq\alpha_{n}x+\beta_{n})|=O\left((N_{n,d}^{=})^{-1/6}\log(k_{n})^{7/6}\right),

where
\mathcal{M}_{n}
is the maximum of
n
i.i.d. copies of the standard normal distribution. Thus we need to ensure

(N_{n,d}^{=})^{-1/6}\log(k_{n})^{7/6}=o(1)\,,

which exactly corresponds with the assumption of
k_{n}=\exp\left(o\bigl((N_{n,d}^{=})^{1/7}\bigr)\right)
.
∎

A similar statement applies for
X_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d)}
if
d
grows slowly enough.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
(X_{n1},\ldots,X_{nk_{n}})
be a row-wise i.i.d. triangular array with
X_{n1}\overset{\mathcal{D}}{=}X_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d)},
where
d=d(n)=o(n^{1/3})
. Let
M_{n},a_{n},b_{n}
be given in analogy to Theorem
4.1
. If
k_{n}=\exp\bigl(o(n^{1/7}d^{-3/7})\bigr),
then

\forall x\in\mathbb{R}\negmedspace:\quad\mathbb{P}(M_{n}\leq a_{n}x+b_{n})\longrightarrow\Lambda(x)\,.

Proof.

According to the above considerations, the maximum degrees
\Delta_{n},\Delta_{n}^{*}
in the dependency graphs of the representations (
2
), (
4a
), and (
4b
) are bounded in the way of
\Delta_{n}\leq 4d
and
\Delta_{n}^{*}\leq 8d
. These representations are based on
\Theta(nd)
summands. By analogy with the proof of Theorem
4.1
, an application of Theorem
3.1
with
k_{n}
i.i.d. iterations of
X_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d)}
yields

|\mathbb{P}(M_{n}\leq a_{n}x+b_{n})-\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{M}_{n}\leq\alpha_{n}x+\beta_{n})|=O(n^{-1/6}d^{1/2})\log(k_{n})^{7/6}\,.

Again, plugging
k_{n}=\exp\bigl(o(n^{1/7}d^{-3/7})\bigr)
according to the assumption yields

|\mathbb{P}(M_{n}\leq a_{n}x+b_{n})-\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{M}_{n}\leq\alpha_{n}x+\beta_{n})|=o(1)\,,

from which the claim follows.
∎

For any other growth rate of
d,
we can state an EVLT only for the cases covered by Lemma
3.4
, and due to the use of Hájek’s projection, we can only impose a strongly reduced asymptotic bound on

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
(X_{n1},\ldots,X_{nk_{n}})
be a row-wise i.i.d. triangular array with
X_{n1}\overset{\mathcal{D}}{=}X_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d)}

d=\omega(n^{1/2})

\forall x\in\mathbb{R}\negthickspace:\quad\mathbb{P}(M_{n}\leq a_{n}x+b_{n})\longrightarrow\Lambda(x)\,.

Proof.

The conditions on
d
ensure that
1-\mathrm{Var}(X_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d)})/\mathrm{Var}(\hat{X}_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d)})=o(1),
but the rate of convergence determines the bound on

by means of
[

\displaystyle 1-\frac{\mathrm{Var}(X_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d)})}{\mathrm{Var}(\hat{X}_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d)})}

\displaystyle=1-\frac{4d^{3}+6nd+3d^{2}-d}{4d^{3}+6d^{2}+2d}=\frac{4d^{2}+6n+3d-1}{4d^{2}+6d+2}

\displaystyle=1-\frac{4d^{2}}{4d^{2}+6d+2}-\frac{3d+1}{4d^{2}+6d+2}-\frac{6n}{4d^{2}+6d+2}

Apparently,

Again, the proof now follows the same steps as in
[

.
∎

5.
Conclusion and Outlook

We successfully investigated the bivariate CLT for the generalized inversion and descent statistics
X_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d_{1})}
and
X_{\mathrm{des}}^{(d_{2})}
on all classical Weyl groups. For low regimes
d_{1}\approx o(n^{1/3})
, the CLT was achieved through direct Gaussian approximation, while for larger regimes
d_{1}\gg n^{1/2}
, the CLT was achieved with help of the Hájek projection
\hat{X}_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d_{1})}
. Corresponding extreme value results for these two statistics were derived as well. There is a remaining gap consisting of the regimes
n^{1/3}\ll d_{1}\ll n^{1/2}
which could be closed upon availability of suitable Gaussian approximation results. Simulations suggested that the CLT applies for these regimes as well.

The investigation of the asymptotic correlation
\rho
showed several interesting phenomena. When
d_{1}
diverges to infinity as
n\rightarrow\infty
but remains sublinear, the correlation is always zero, with the exception of some ”complementary” cases where
d_{2}
is chosen so that
d_{1}+d_{2}=n(1+o(1))
. If
d_{2}
is sublinear,
\rho=0
applies as well. If both
d_{1}
and
d_{2}
are linear, then
\rho
is an explicit function of the limits of
d_{1}/n
and
d_{2}/n
. On the other hand, if
d_{1}
remains bounded, there is a clear distinction between the cases
d_{1}<d_{2}
and
d_{1}\geq d_{2}
. The former case guarantees
\rho=0,
while in the latter, the CLT can only be satisfied if
d_{1}
eventually remains constant, in which case
\rho=1/\sqrt{d_{1}}>0
.

There are several related questions beyond the scope of this paper. Other well-known permutation statistics can be generalized in a similar pattern. For example, on the symmetric groups, one can consider the
generalized major index

X_{\mathrm{maj}}^{(d)}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n-d}i\cdot\textbf{1}\{Z_{i}>Z_{i+d}\}

and the
generalized peaks

X_{\mathrm{peak}}^{(d,e)}:=\sum_{i=d+1}^{n-e}\textbf{1}\{Z_{i-d}<Z_{i},Z_{i+e}<Z_{i}\}\,.

While the common major index
X_{\mathrm{maj}}
is equidistributed to the common inversion statistic
X_{\mathrm{inv}},
this no longer applies for the generalized counterparts. Further generalized statistics arise by considering joint features of a permutation and its inverse. The most basic example is the
two-sided Eulerian statistic

X_{T}(\pi):=X_{\mathrm{des}}(\pi)+X_{\mathrm{des}}(\pi^{-1})
for
\pi\in S_{n}
(or
B_{n}
,
D_{n}
, see
[
3
,
5
,
24
]
). Its generalized version can be defined as
X_{T}^{(d,e)}(\pi):=X_{\mathrm{des}}^{(d)}(\pi)+X_{\mathrm{des}}^{(e)}(\pi^{-1})
. Even for the original statistic
X_{T}
, the joint asymptotic normality with
X_{\mathrm{inv}}
is not investigated yet due to the complex dependency structure of
X_{T}
.

Besides further generalized statistics, one can also drop the basic framework of drawing elements of classical Weyl groups uniformly at random. Prominent examples of non-uniform distributions on classical Weyl groups are the Ewens distribution (cf.
[
16
]
) and the Mallows distribution (cf.
[
17
,
26
]
).

\displaystyle=\frac{\omega_{d}(k)^{2}}{3}+\frac{1}{4}+2\omega_{d}(k)\underbrace{\mathrm{Cov}(Z_{k},\textbf{1}\{Z_{k}<0\})}_{=~-1/4}\,,

from which we see that even if
d<k,
the leading terms of
\mathrm{Var}\left(\hat{X}_{\mathrm{inv}}^{B,(d)}\right)
are not influenced by the third sum. In light of (
8
), we have to determine the linear coefficients
\omega_{d}(k)
stemming from

\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathfrak{N}_{n,d}}\mathbb{P}(Z_{i}>Z_{j}\mid Z_{k})+\sum_{(i,j)\in\widetilde{\mathfrak{N}}_{n,d}}\mathbb{P}(-Z_{i}>Z_{j}\mid Z_{k})\,.

We write
\omega_{d}(k)=\omega_{d}(k)^{+}+\omega_{d}(k)^{-}
, with
\omega_{d}(k)^{+}
stemming from the first sum and
\omega_{d}(k)^{-}
stemming from the second.
For
\displaystyle{\omega_{d}(k)^{+}},
we can use the counting method previously used in the proof of Lemma
3.4
. However, we have to take into account that, as
Z_{i}\sim U(-1,1)
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(Z_{k}>Z_{j}\mid Z_{k})

\displaystyle=\frac{Z_{k}+1}{2}\,,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(Z_{i}>Z_{k}\mid Z_{k})

\displaystyle=\frac{1-Z_{k}}{2}\,,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(-Z_{k}>Z_{j}\mid Z_{k})

\displaystyle=\frac{1-Z_{k}}{2}\,,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(Z_{i}>Z_{k}\mid Z_{k})

\displaystyle=\frac{1-Z_{k}}{2}\,.

In conclusion, the coefficients
\displaystyle{\omega_{d}(k)^{+}}
on
B_{n}
are half of the coefficients
\omega_{d}(k)
on
S_{n}
if
d<n
. Otherwise, for
d\geq n
, we always have
\omega_{d}(k)^{+}=(n-2k+1)/2
. Moreover,
\omega_{d}(k)^{-}=-\widetilde{N}_{n,d}^{(k)}/2,
where

\widetilde{N}_{n,d}^{(k)}:=|\{(i,j)\in\widetilde{\mathfrak{N}}_{n,d}\mid\text{either}~i=k~\text{or}~j=k\}|\,.

For all
d\in\{1,\ldots,d_{\max}\},
we can find that

\widetilde{N}_{n,d}^{(k)}=\begin{cases}n-1,&k\leq d-n\\
d-k-1,&d-n<k\leq d/2\\
d-k,&d/2<k\leq d\\
0,&k>d\end{cases}\,.

The first case
k\leq d-n
is redundant if
d\leq n,
and the last case
k>d
is redundant if
n\geq d
. We give illustrations for the computation of
\widetilde{N}_{n,d}^{(k)}
in the case
d>n
. Figure
4
displays the number of pairs
(i,k)
and the number of pairs
(k,j)
for the exemplary case of
n=12,d=16
.

.

(i,k)
(red) and
(k,j)
(blue) in
\widetilde{\mathfrak{N}}_{n,d}
. The sum of these two numbers is

which is displayed by the black crosses.

By analogy with the proofs of
[
22
, Theorems A.4 and A.13]
, we need to distinguish the four cases
d\leq n/2,

n/2\leq d\leq 2n/3
,
2n/3\leq d<n
, and
d\geq n
. If
d\leq n/2
, then all pairs in
\widetilde{\mathfrak{N}}_{n,d}
are located within
K_{1}
, yielding

\displaystyle\omega_{d}(k)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}(d-k+1)/2-(d-k-1)/2,&k\leq d/2\\
(d-k+1)/2-(d-k)/2,&d/2<k\leq d\\
0,&d<k\leq n-d\\
(n-d-k)/2,&n-d<k\leq n\end{cases}

(9a)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1,&k\leq d/2\\
1/2,&d/2<k\leq d\\
0,&d<k\leq n-d\\
(n-d-k)/2,&k>n-d\end{cases}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}(n-d-k)/2,&k>n-d\\
0,&d<k\leq n-d\\
O(1),&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}\,.

In conclusion, if
d\leq n/2,
then

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}\left(\hat{X}_{\mathrm{inv}}^{B,(d)}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\omega_{d}(k)^{2}+O(d^{2})=\frac{1}{12}\sum_{k=n-d+1}^{n}(n-d-k)^{2}+O(d^{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{12}\frac{d(d+1)(2d+1)}{6}+O(d^{2})=\frac{1}{36}d^{3}+O(d^{2}).

Due to
\mathrm{Var}\left(X_{\mathrm{inv}}^{B,(d)}\right)=d^{3}/36+nd/12+O(d^{2})
according to Lemma
2.5
, we again obtain the condition
d\gg\sqrt{n}
.

If
n/2<d<n
, then the pairs in
\widetilde{\mathfrak{N}}_{n,d}
also cover
K_{2}
. For
k\in K_{2},
there cannot be any pairs
(k,j)
if
n/2<d\leq 2n/3,
while this is possible if
d>2n/3
. However, the difference between these two subcases is only marginal. If
n/2<d\leq 2n/3,
we obtain

(9b)

\omega_{d}(k)=\begin{cases}1,&k\leq d/2\\
1/2,&d/2<k\leq n-d\\
(n-d-k+1)/2,&n-d<k\leq d\\
(n-d-k)/2,&k>d\end{cases}\,.

If
d>2n/3,
then

(9c)

\omega_{d}(k)=\begin{cases}1,&k\leq n-d\\
(n-d-k+2)/2,&n-d<k\leq d/2\\
(n-d-k+1)/2,&d/2<k\leq d\\
(n-d-k)/2,&k>d\end{cases}\,.

In conclusion, if
n/2<d<n,
then we obtain, as before,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}\left(\hat{X}_{\mathrm{inv}}^{B,(d)}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\omega_{d}(k)^{2}+O(d^{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{12}\sum_{k=n-d+1}^{d}(n-d-k+1)^{2}+\frac{1}{12}\sum_{k=d+1}^{n}(n-d-k)^{2}+O(d^{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{12}\sum_{k=n-d+1}^{d}(n-d-k+1)^{2}+\frac{1}{12}\sum_{k=1}^{2d-n}k^{2}+O(d^{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{12}\sum_{k=1}^{2d-n-1}k^{2}+\frac{1}{12}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{d}k^{2}-\sum_{k=1}^{2d-n}k^{2}\right)+O(d^{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{36}d^{3}+O(d^{2})\,.

(n-d-k+2)/2,&d-n<k\leq d/2,\\
(n-d-k+1)/2,&d/2<k\leq n.\end{cases}

We compute

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}\omega_{d}(k)^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{d-n}(k-1)^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\sum_{k=d-n+1}^{n}(n-d+1-k)^{2}+O(n^{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{6}\bigl(2d^{3}-3d^{2}(2n+1)+d(6n^{2}+6n+1)-n(2n^{2}+3n+1)\bigr)

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{1}{24}(2n-d)(14d^{2}-20dn-9d+8n^{2}+6n+1)+O(n^{2})\,.

Due to
\mathrm{Var}(Z_{k})=1/3,
we obtain

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(\hat{X}_{\mathrm{inv}}^{B,(d)})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{72}\Bigl(-6d^{3}+3d^{2}(8n-1)-3d(8n^{2}-1)+2n(4n^{2}-1)\Bigr)+O(n^{2})

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{12}d^{3}+\frac{1}{3}nd^{2}-\frac{1}{3}n^{2}d+\frac{1}{9}n^{3}+O(n^{2})\,.

By Lemma
2.5
, this also applies for
\mathrm{Var}(X_{\mathrm{inv}}^{B,(d)})
, completing the proof. ∎

Appendix B

Proof of Lemma
3.8

Recall that by (
6a
) and (
6b
), we have

\hat{X}_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d_{1})}=\sum_{k=1}^{d}\omega_{d_{1}}(k)Z_{k}

with
Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{n}\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}U(0,1)
and

\displaystyle d_{1}\leq n/2

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow\omega_{d_{1}}(k)=\begin{cases}d_{1}-k+1\,,&k\leq d_{1}\\
0,&d_{1}<k\leq n-d_{1}\\
n-d_{1}-k\,,&k>n-d_{1}\end{cases},

\displaystyle d_{1}>n/2

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow\omega_{d_{1}}(k)=\begin{cases}d_{1}-k+1,&k\leq n-d_{1}\\
n-2k+1,&n-d_{1}<k\leq d_{1}\\
n-d_{1}-k,&k>d_{1}\end{cases}.

Therefore,

\mathrm{Cov}(\hat{X}_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d_{1})},X_{\mathrm{des}}^{(d_{2})})=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\omega_{d_{1}}(j)\,\mathrm{Cov}\left(Z_{j},X_{\mathrm{des}}^{(d_{2})}\right).

Consider a fixed
j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
. For independence reasons, we have with slight abuse of notation:

\mathrm{Cov}\left(Z_{j},X_{\mathrm{des}}^{(d_{2})}\right)=\mathrm{Cov}\left(Z_{j},\textbf{1}\{Z_{j-d_{2}}>Z_{j}\}+\textbf{1}\{Z_{j}>Z_{j+d_{2}}\}\right).

By arguments in the proof of
[
12
, Lemma 2.6b)]
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(Z_{j}\textbf{1}\{Z_{j}<Z_{j-d_{2}}\})=\frac{1}{6}

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow\mathrm{Cov}(Z_{j},\textbf{1}\{Z_{j}<Z_{j-d_{2}}\})=-1/12\,,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(Z_{j}\textbf{1}\{Z_{j}>Z_{j+d_{2}}\})=\frac{1}{3}

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow\mathrm{Cov}(Z_{j},\textbf{1}\{Z_{j}>Z_{j+d_{2}}\})=1/12\,.

If both indicators
\textbf{1}\{Z_{j-d_{2}}>Z_{j}\}
and
\textbf{1}\{Z_{j}>Z_{j+d_{2}}\}
are applicable, i.e., if both
j>d_{2}
and
j\leq n-d_{2},
then
\mathrm{Cov}(Z_{j},X_{\mathrm{des}}^{(d_{2})})=0
. On the contrary, if
d_{2}>n/2,
then no indicator is applicable for all
j
with
n-d_{2}<j\leq d_{2},
also giving
\mathrm{Cov}(Z_{j},X_{\mathrm{des}}^{(d_{2})})=0
. In conclusion, we can write

\mathrm{Cov}\left(\hat{X}_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d_{1})},X_{\mathrm{des}}^{(d_{2})}\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\omega_{d_{1}}(j)c_{d_{2}}(j)\,,

where

\displaystyle d_{2}\leq n/2

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow c_{d_{2}}(j)=\begin{cases}+1/12\,,&j\leq d_{2}\\
0,&d_{2}<j\leq n-d_{2}\\
-1/12\,,&j>n-d_{2}\end{cases},

\displaystyle d_{2}>n/2

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow c_{d_{2}}(j)=\begin{cases}+1/12\,,&j\leq n-d_{2}\\
0,&n-d_{2}<j\leq d_{2}\\
-1/12\,,&j>d_{2}\end{cases}.

Since both
\omega_{d_{1}}
and
c_{d_{2}}
exhibit asymmetry in the way of
\omega_{d_{1}}(j)=-\omega_{d_{1}}(n+1-j)
and
c_{d_{2}}(j)=-c_{d_{2}}(n+1-j),
we may instead write

(10)

\mathrm{Cov}\left(\hat{X}_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d_{1})},X_{\mathrm{des}}^{(d_{2})}\right)=2\sum_{j=1}^{d_{2}\wedge(n-d_{2})}\omega_{d_{1}}(j)c_{d_{2}}(j)=\frac{1}{6}\sum_{j=1}^{d_{2}\wedge(n-d_{2})}\omega_{d_{1}}(j)\,.

We now distinguish the cases
d_{2}\leq n/2
and
d_{2}>n/2,
even though this later proves to be redundant. We analyze the range of zero and nonzero coefficients
\omega_{d_{1}}(j)c_{d_{2}}(j)
appearing in the sum in (
10
).

Case 1:

d_{2}\leq n/2
and
d_{1}\leq d_{2}
. Then,
c_{d_{2}}(j)=0
implies
\omega_{d_{1}}(j)=0
(figuratively, the range of zero coefficients solely depends on
d_{1}
), and

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}\left(\hat{X}_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d_{1})},X_{\mathrm{des}}^{(d_{2})}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{6}\sum_{j=1}^{d_{1}}(d_{1}+1-j)=\frac{1}{6}\sum_{j=1}^{d_{1}}j=\frac{d_{1}(d_{1}+1)}{12}\,.

Case 2:

d_{2}\leq n/2
and
d_{1}>d_{2}
. Then, the range of zero coefficients depends on
d_{2}
. In light of Figures
2
and
3
, we also need to distinguish whether
d_{2}\leq n-d_{1}\Longrightarrow d_{1}+d_{2}\leq n,
or
d_{1}+d_{2}>n
.

Case 2a:

d_{2}\leq n/2
and
d_{1}>d_{2},
but
d_{1}+d_{2}\leq n
. Then,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}\left(\hat{X}_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d_{1})},X_{\mathrm{des}}^{(d_{2})}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{6}\sum_{j=1}^{d_{2}}(d_{1}+1-j)=\frac{1}{6}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d_{1}}(d_{1}+1-j)-\sum_{j=d_{2}+1}^{d_{1}}(d_{1}+1-j)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{12}d_{1}(d_{1}+1)-\frac{1}{6}\Bigl(1+2+\ldots+(d_{1}-d_{2})\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{12}d_{1}(d_{1}+1)-\frac{1}{12}(d_{1}-d_{2})(d_{1}-d_{2}+1)

(11)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{6}d_{1}d_{2}-\frac{d_{2}^{2}}{12}+\frac{d}{12}=\frac{1}{6}d_{1}d_{2}-\frac{1}{12}d_{2}(d_{2}-1)\,.

Case 2b:

d_{2}\leq n/2
and
d_{1}+d_{2}>n
. Then,

\mathrm{Cov}\left(\hat{X}_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d_{1})},X_{\mathrm{des}}^{(d_{2})}\right)=\frac{1}{6}\sum_{j=1}^{n-d_{1}}(d_{1}+1-j)+\frac{1}{6}\sum_{j=n-d_{1}+1}^{d_{2}}(n-2j+1)\,.

On one hand, by substituting
d_{2}=n-d_{1}
in (
11
), we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{6}\sum_{j=1}^{n-d_{1}}(d_{1}+1-j)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{6}d_{1}(n-d_{1})-\frac{1}{12}(n-d_{1})(n-d_{1}+1)

(12a)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3}nd_{1}-\frac{1}{4}d_{1}^{2}-\frac{1}{12}(n^{2}-n+d_{1})\,.

On the other hand, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{6}\sum_{j=n-d_{1}+1}^{d_{2}}(n-2j+1)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{6}(n+1)(d_{2}+d_{1}-n)-\frac{1}{3}\sum_{j=n-d_{1}+1}^{d_{2}}j

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{6}(n+1)(d_{2}+d_{1}-n)-\frac{1}{3}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d_{2}}j-\sum_{j=1}^{n-d_{1}}j\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{6}(n+1)(d_{2}+d_{1}-n)-\frac{1}{6}d_{2}(d_{2}+1)+\frac{1}{6}(n-d_{1})(n-d_{1}+1)

(12b)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{6}\Bigl(d_{1}^{2}-nd_{1}-d_{2}^{2}+nd_{2}\Bigr)\,,

so overall,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}\left(\hat{X}_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d_{1})},X_{\mathrm{des}}^{(d_{2})}\right)

\displaystyle=\eqref{case2b}+\eqref{case2c}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{6}n(d_{1}+d_{2})-\frac{1}{6}d_{2}^{2}-\frac{1}{12}d_{1}(d_{1}+1)-\frac{1}{12}n(n-1)\,.

Case 3:

d_{2}>n/2
and
d_{1}\leq n-d_{2}\Longleftrightarrow d_{1}+d_{2}\leq n
. In that case, the range of zero coefficients depends on
d_{1},
and as in Case 1, we obtain

\mathrm{Cov}\left(\hat{X}_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d_{1})},X_{\mathrm{des}}^{(d_{2})}\right)=\frac{d_{1}(d_{1}+1)}{12}\,.

Case 4:

d_{2}>n/2
and
d_{1}>n-d_{2}
. Then, the range of zero coefficients depends on
d_{2},
but to determine whether the structural break of
\omega_{d_{1}}(j)
as seen in Figure
3
is relevant, we now need to distinguish the subcases
n-d_{2}\leq n-d_{1}\Longleftrightarrow d_{1}\leq d_{2}
(no structural break) and
d_{1}>d_{2}
(structural break). If
d_{1}\leq d_{2},
then by replacing
d_{2}
with
n-d_{2}
in (
11
), we get

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}\left(\hat{X}_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d_{1})},X_{\mathrm{des}}^{(d_{2})}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{6}d_{1}(n-d_{2})-\frac{1}{12}(n-d_{2})(n-d_{2}+1)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{6}n(d_{1}+d_{2})-\frac{1}{6}d_{1}d_{2}-\frac{1}{12}d_{2}(d_{2}+1)-\frac{1}{12}n(n-1)\,.

If
d_{1}>d_{2},
then we again replace
d_{2}
with
n-d_{2}
and obtain the same result as in Case 2b, because (
12a
) is independent of
d_{2}
and (
12b
) is invariant under replacing
d_{2}
with
n-d_{2}
. This completes the proof. ∎

Proof of Theorem
3.9

To shorten notation, let
\mathrm{Cov}
be an abbreviation for
\mathrm{Cov}(\hat{X}_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d_{1})},X_{\mathrm{des}}^{(d_{2})})
and let
\sigma_{\mathrm{inv}},

\sigma_{\mathrm{des}}
be abbreviations for
\mathrm{Var}(X_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d_{1})})^{1/2}
and
\mathrm{Var}(X_{\mathrm{des}}^{(d_{2})})^{1/2}
, respectively. Recall the notation
\hat{\rho}_{n}=\mathrm{Cov}/(\sigma_{\mathrm{inv}}\sigma_{\mathrm{des}})
. Moreover, we briefly write
e_{2}:=n-d_{2}=N_{n,d_{2}}^{=}
.

a): Let
n^{1/2}\ll d_{1}\ll n
. If
n-d_{2}\gg d_{1},
then we have
d_{1}+d_{2}\leq n
and therefore
\mathrm{Cov}=\Theta(d_{1}^{2}),

\sigma_{\mathrm{inv}}=\Theta(d_{1}^{3/2})
. If

we easily find

, both when
d_{1}\leq d_{2}
and when
d_{1}>d_{2}
. If
d_{2}>n/2,
then we have
\sigma_{\mathrm{des}}=\Theta(\sqrt{e_{2}}),
but by the assumption
d_{1}=o(e_{2})
, we find
\rho_{n}=o(1)
from the same arguments. If

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}\left(X_{\mathrm{inv}}^{(d_{1})},X_{\mathrm{des}}^{(d_{2})}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{nd_{1}+n^{2}-ne_{2}-d_{1}n+d_{1}e_{2}}{6}-\frac{(n-e_{2})(n-e_{2}+1)}{12}-\frac{n(n-1)}{12}

(13)

\displaystyle=\frac{d_{1}e_{2}}{6}-\frac{e_{2}^{2}}{12}+\frac{e_{2}}{12}\,,

yielding

for some

d): Under the given assumptions

with

we have

hence

e): If
c_{1}<1/2,
but
1/2<c_{2}\leq 1-c_{1},
then we have
\sigma_{\mathrm{des}}=\sqrt{(1-c_{1})n}/2
, while
\mathrm{Cov}
and
\sigma_{\mathrm{inv}}
are the same as in e), giving

\hat{\rho}_{n}\asymp\frac{c_{1}^{2}n^{2}/12}{c_{1}^{3/2}(n^{3/2}/\sqrt{18})\sqrt{(1-c_{2})n}/2}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\frac{\sqrt{c_{1}}}{\sqrt{1-c_{2}}}\,.

If
c_{2}>1-c_{1},
then it follows from (

) that

giving

f): Since now

i.e.,

Then, with the aid of (

), we have

g): If
c_{1}>c_{2},
but
c_{1}<1/2
, then
