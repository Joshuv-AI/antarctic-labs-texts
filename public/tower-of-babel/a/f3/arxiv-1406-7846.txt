Abstract
We present a construction that allows us to define a limit object of Banach space decorated graph sequences in a generalized homomorphism density sense. This general functional analytic framework provides a universal language for various combinatorial limit notions. In particular it makes it possible to assign limit objects to multigraph sequences that are convergent in the sense of node-and-edge homomorphism numbers, and it generalizes the limit theory for graph sequences with compact decorations.



1
Introduction

The motivation for this paper was to provide a framework for a theory
of convergence and limits of graphs with unbounded edge
multiplicities, along the lines of the limit theory for dense simple
graphs developed by Borgs, Chayes, Lovász, Sós and Vesztergombi

[
2
,
3
]
and Lovász and Szegedy
[
10
]
. Key
elements of this theory are the notions of cut distance and subgraph
densities, the definitions of convergence and limit objects, the
Regularity Lemma (in its weak form due to Frieze and Kannan

[
5
]
), along with the Counting Lemma.

In the paper
[
11
]
(posted on the Arxiv, but not published; see
also
[
9
]
, Section 17.1), the second and third authors worked out a
theory of convergence and limits of simple graphs whose edges are
decorated by points from some compact space. (Ordinary simple graphs
can be considered as complete graphs with edges decorated by elements
of a two-point space.) One could say that the theory of undecorated
simple graphs extends to this case in a rather straightforward manner
(at least as soon as the appropriate formulations are found).
Edge-weighted graphs and multigraphs fit in this framework, provided
the edge weights/multiplicities are bounded (but for unbounded
multiplicities or edge weights one has to do more, as we shall see).
We note that this paper took the approach of defining convergence via weak convergence of the distribution of samples, and this gives a link to exchangeability and Aldous’ representation theorem. It turns out that whilst sampling convergence is equivalent to homomorphism convergence notions
for compact decorations, this does not longer hold if the compactness condition is waived (see Section
4.4
).

A limit theory for convergence of multigraphs was worked out by
Kolossváry and Ráth
[
7
]
, and essentially the same
results can be derived from the limit theory of compact decorated
graphs using the one-point compactification of the set of integers to
encode the edge multiplicities. The limit objects can be described by
functions on
[0,1]^{2}
whose values are probability distributions on
nonnegative integers.

Let us describe in a few words the general framework for graph
convergence theories. We start with defining the number of
occurrences of a “small” graph
F
in a “big” graph
G
. In the
case of simple graphs, one can use the number of homomorphisms
(adjacency-preserving maps)
\hom(F,G)
from
F
to
G
. One also
needs the (normalized) homomorphism density

t(F,G)=\frac{\hom(F,G)}{|V(G)|^{|V(F)|}}.

(1)

A key notion in these theories is that of convergence of a graph
sequence, which is defined by specifying an appropriate family of

test graphs
, and then saying that a sequence of graphs

(G_{1},G_{2},\dots)
is
convergent
, if
t(F,G_{n})
is convergent
for every test graph
F
. In the theory of convergence of simple
graphs, the family of simple graphs is the right (in a sense, only
reasonable) choice for test graphs. The limiting values of these
densities can be represented by limit objects called
graphons
,
which in the case of simple graphs are symmetric measurable functions

[0,1]^{2}\to[0,1]
.

The motivation of this paper is to work out a limit theory for
convergence of multigraphs. Whether or not the results of
Kolossváry and Ráth
[
7
]
can be viewed as a solution of
the problem of multigraph convergence depends on how we define
homomorphisms between two multigraphs
F
and
G
.

One natural definition is that of
node-and-edge homomorphism
:
this is a pair of maps
\varphi:~V(F)\to V(G)
and
\psi:~E(F)\to E(G)

such that if
e\in E(G)
connects
i
and
j
, then
\psi(e)

connects
\varphi(i)
and
\varphi(j)
. A different definition is that of a

node-homomorphism
: a map
V(F)\to V(G)
such that the
multiplicity of the image of an edge is not less than the
multiplicity of the edge. If both
F
and
G
consist of two nodes
connected by two edges, then the number of node-homomorphisms
F\to G
is
2
, while the number of node-and-edge homomorphisms is
8
.

In this paper, we consider node-and-edge homomorphisms, and for two
multigraphs, we denote by
\hom(F,G)
their number. We define
homomorphism densities and convergence based on this definition. The
results of Kolossváry and Ráth are based on node-homomorphisms.
It turns out that these two notions of convergence are not equivalent
(see Section
2.3
, and also
[
9
]
, Chapter
17).

In fact, we consider a more general model, namely a limit theory of
graphs whose edges are decorated by elements from a Banach space, and
where the test graphs are decorated from the pre-dual space. This
will include the convergence theory of compact decorated graphs as
well. Along the way, we show that with a modified notion of cut
distance (which we call “jumble distance”) one can state a prove an
appropriate Weak Regularity Lemma and a Counting Lemma.

Recently Borgs, Chayes, Cohn and Zhao
[
1
]
developed a theory
for
L^{p}
-graphons (unbounded symmetric functions in the space

L^{p}([0,1]^{2})
for some
2<p<\infty
), and graph sequences convergent
to them. They prove appropriate versions of the Regularity and
Counting Lemmas. Not every graph has a finite density in such a
kernel, and accordingly, they limit the set of test-graphs to simple
graphs with degrees bounded by
p
. Their set-up is more general than
ours in the sense that we work with a more restricted family of
unbounded kernels, namely kernels in
\mathcal{L}=\bigcap_{1\leq p<\infty}L^{p}_{\mathrm{sym}}([0,1]^{2})
. On the other hand, we allow arbitrary multigraphs as
test graphs (more generally, decorating by elements of a Banach
space). So the two theories don’t seem to contain each other (but
perhaps a common generalization is possible).

Using random graphs generated by Banach space valued graphons, we show that every element of the space of limit objects that we define arises as a limit of a convergent sequence of decorated graphs, and that this space is closed under our convergence notion.

Although we do not in this paper investigate the question of uniqueness of Banach space graphons (we refer to
[
8
]
by the first author for details on that subject), we remark here that Examples
4.9
and
4.11
do show that indeterminacies in the Stieltjes/Hamburger moment problems are an extra natural obstacle to uniqueness for unbounded graphons, beyond the usual weak isomorphism equivalence (see, e.g.,
[
9
, Sections 7.3 and 10.7]
).

2
Decorated graphs and graphons

2.1
Decorated graphs and graphons

If
\mathcal{X}
is any set, an
\mathcal{X}
-decorated graph
is a graph where
every edge
ij
is decorated by an element
X_{ij}\in\mathcal{X}
. An

\mathcal{X}
-decorated graph will be denoted by
(G,g)
, where
G
is a
simple graph (possibly with loops), and
g:~E(G)\to\mathcal{X}
. We will see
several examples in Section
2.3
how decorations can be
used to express weights, multiple edges, and more.

In our setup, we will consider decorations by elements of Banach
spaces. Let
\mathcal{B}
be a separable Banach space, let
\mathcal{Z}
denote its dual. The elements of
\mathcal{B}
act on
\mathcal{Z}
as
bounded linear functionals in the canonical way, and vice versa; the
action of
b\in\mathcal{B}
on
z\in\mathcal{Z}
will be denoted by
\langle b,z\rangle
. We will use “small”
\mathcal{B}
-decorated graphs to probe
“large”
\mathcal{Z}
-decorated graphs.

Let
(G,g)
be an
X
-decorated graph, where
G
is a graph with
m

edges, and
X
is a Banach space. We define

\|g\|_{p}=\Bigl(\frac{1}{m}\sum_{e\in E(G)}\|g(e)\|_{X}^{p}\Bigr)^{1/p},\quad\|g\|_{\infty}=\max_{e\in E(G)}\|g(e)\|_{X},\quad\text{and}\quad\Pi_{g}=\prod_{e\in E(G)}\|g(e)\|_{X}.

Clearly
\Pi_{g}\leq\|g\|_{1}^{m}
.

To define “decorated graphons” we need to become more technical. We
set
\mathcal{L}=\bigcap_{1\leq p<\infty}L^{p}_{\mathrm{sym}}([0,1]^{2})
.
For
b\in\mathcal{B}
and
W:~[0,1]^{2}\to\mathcal{Z}
, let the function
\langle b,W\rangle:~[0,1]^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
be defined by

\langle b,W\rangle(x,y):=\langle b,W(x,y)\rangle.

Definition 2.1
.

A function
W:~[0,1]^{2}\to\mathcal{Z}
is called
weak-* measurable
if for any
b\in\mathcal{B}
, the function
\langle b,W\rangle
is measurable.

Definition 2.2
.

A symmetric weak-* measurable function
W:~[0,1]^{2}\to\mathcal{Z}
is called
a
\mathcal{Z}
-
graphon
if the function
(x,y)\mapsto\|W(x,y)\|_{\mathcal{Z}}

lies in
\mathcal{L}
. Note that this function is measurable, since
\mathcal{B}
is separable, and for a countable dense subset
\mathscr{F}\subset\mathcal{B}
we have

\|W(x,y)\|_{\mathcal{Z}}=\sup_{f\in\mathscr{F}\backslash\{0\}}\frac{|\langle f,W(x,y)\rangle|}{\|f\|_{\mathcal{B}}}.

Let the space of
\mathcal{Z}
-graphons be denoted by

\mathcal{W}_{\mathcal{Z}}
. We set

\|W\|_{p}:=\bigl\|\|W(.,.)\|_{\mathcal{Z}}\bigr\|_{p}.

(i.e., we take the
\mathcal{Z}
-norm of
W(x,y)
for every
x,y\in[0,1]
,
and then take the
L^{p}
-norm of the resulting function).

Also, if
W
is a
\mathcal{Z}
-graphon, then
\langle b,W\rangle\in\mathcal{L}
.
Indeed,

\|\langle b,W\rangle\|_{p}\leq\bigl\|\|b\|_{\mathcal{B}}\|W(.,.)\|_{\mathcal{Z}}\bigr\|_{p}=\|b\|_{\mathcal{B}}\|W\|_{p}<\infty.

(2)

Note that the above measurability notion is not one usually encountered when looking at Banach space valued functions, for the simple reason that generally a predual
\mathcal{B}
may not exist to the range space
\mathcal{Z}
of the functions at hand. The two usual notions are weak and strong/Bochner measurability, and we shall briefly hint at why weak-* measurability is instead the correct notion for our purposes.

Recall that a function
W:~[0,1]^{2}\to\mathcal{Z}
is called
weakly measurable
if for any
b\in\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}
, the function
\langle b,W\rangle
is measurable. Note that unless
\mathcal{B}
is reflexive (which in our applications it typically will not be), weak-* measurability is strictly weaker due to the natural identification of
\mathcal{B}
with a subspace of
\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}
.

A function
W:~[0,1]^{2}\to\mathcal{Z}
is called
Bochner measurable

if it equals to the limit of a sequence of measurable functions with
countable range almost everywhere. This in turn is by the Pettis measurability theorem equivalent to being weakly measurable and essentially separably valued (i.e., one may obtain a separable range by deleting a nullset from the domain). Note that whenever
\mathcal{Z}
is separable, Bochner and weak measurability are equivalent.

As will become clear in the next section, the homomorphism densities which will be at the core of our convergence notion can be defined just as well using
\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}
instead of
\mathcal{B}
. Also, our aim is to define a space of graphons that acts as a closure of the space of decorated graphs, and so is as small as possible. This indicates that we should opt for the strongest possible measurability restrictions, favouring strong or weak measurability. However, (norm) closed balls in
\mathcal{Z}
are not compact with respect to the weak topology, unless we have reflexivity and
\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}
anyway. Therefore, if we wish to use
\mathcal{Z}
-decorated graphs and test with elements from
\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}
, we might after the limit transition end up with something
\mathcal{Z}^{\prime\prime}
-valued, which again brings us to a range with a predual. In addition, there would also be some – related – issues defining the stepping operators (Section
2.6
), as weak/Pettis integrability does not follow from weak measurability even for bounded functions. For more details on the subtleties of measurability and integrability of Banach space valued functions, we refer to
[
13
]
.

2.2
Homomorphism densities

For every
\mathcal{B}
-decorated simple loopless graph
\mathbf{F}=(F,f)
and

\mathcal{Z}
-decorated completely looped complete graph
\mathbf{G}=(G,g)
, we define

\hom(\mathbf{F},\mathbf{G})=:\sum_{\varphi:V(F)\to V(G)}\prod_{e\in E(F)}\langle f(e),g(\varphi(e))\rangle.

Loops in
\mathbf{G}
are unavoidable if we want to allow non-injective vertex maps, whereas not having loops on
\mathbf{F}
conforms with the classical theory, and avoids having to cope with an extra component for the limit object (a “diagonal”).
The homomorphism density
t(\mathbf{F},\mathbf{G})
is defined by (
1
).
Note that we can write

t(\mathbf{F},\mathbf{G})={\sf E}\Bigl(\prod_{e\in E(F)}\langle f(e),g(\varphi(e))\rangle\Bigr),

(3)

where the expectation is taken over uniform random maps
\varphi:~V(F)\to V(G)
.

In the definition of the homomorphism number between finite graphs,
it is sometimes convenient to restrict the summation to injective
mappings. For every
\mathcal{B}
-decorated graph
\mathbf{F}=(F,f)
with
k
nodes
and
\mathcal{Z}
-decorated graph
\mathbf{G}=(G,g)
with
n
nodes, we define

{\rm inj}(\mathbf{F},\mathbf{G})=:\sum_{\varphi:V(F)\hookrightarrow V(G)}\prod_{e\in E(F)}\langle f(e),g(\varphi(e))\rangle,

and

t_{\rm inj}(\mathbf{F},\mathbf{G})=\frac{{\rm inj}(\mathbf{F},\mathbf{G})}{n(n-1)\dots(n-k+1)}.

We need some elementary estimates, summarized in the following lemma.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
\mathbf{F}=(F,f)
be a
\mathcal{B}
-decorated graph with
k
nodes and
l

edges, and let
\mathbf{G}=(G,g)
be a
\mathcal{Z}
-decorated graph with
n
nodes.
Then

|t(\mathbf{F},\mathbf{G})|\leq\Pi_{f}\|g\|_{l}^{l},\qquad|t_{\rm inj}(\mathbf{F},\mathbf{G})|\leq\frac{n}{n-1}\Pi_{f}\|g\|_{l}^{l}(\mbox{ for }n\geq 2),

and assuming
n\geq 2
and
g(u,u)=0\in\mathcal{Z}
for all
u\in V(G)
,

|t_{\rm inj}(\mathbf{F},\mathbf{G})-t(\mathbf{F},\mathbf{G})|\leq\frac{k(k-1)}{n-1}\Pi_{f}\|g\|_{l}^{l}.

Proof.

Let
{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}
be a random map
V(F)\to V(G)
, then

\displaystyle|t(\mathbf{F},\mathbf{G})|

\displaystyle\leq{\sf E}\Bigl(\prod_{e\in E(F)}|\langle f(e),g({\boldsymbol{\varphi}}(e))\rangle|\Bigr)\leq{\sf E}\Bigl(\prod_{e\in E(F)}\|f(e)\|_{\mathcal{B}}\|g({\boldsymbol{\varphi}}(e)\|_{\mathcal{Z}}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\Pi_{f}{\sf E}\Bigl(\prod_{e\in E(F)}\|g({\boldsymbol{\varphi}}(e))\|_{\mathcal{Z}}\Bigr).

Here
{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}(e)
is uniform over all pairs in
V(G)\times V(G)
. Using
Hölder’s Inequality,

\displaystyle|t(\mathbf{F},\mathbf{G})|\leq\Pi_{f}\Bigl(\prod_{e\in E(F)}{\sf E}\bigl(\|g({\boldsymbol{\varphi}}(e)\|_{\mathcal{Z}}^{l}\bigr)\Bigr)^{1/l}=\Pi_{f}\|g\|_{l}^{l}.

The second inequality follows by analogue computation, with the only modification being that working with injective maps,
{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}(e)
will be uniform on the off-diagonal elements of
V(G)\times V(G)
, hence the additional
n^{2}/n(n-1)=n/(n-1)
factor.

For the third inequality, we shall make use of the fact that injective maps are counted both in
\hom(\mathbf{F},\mathbf{G})
and
{\rm inj}(\mathbf{F},\mathbf{G})
, so a number of terms will cancel despite the different normalization. Also, by the Bernoulli inequality

(n)_{k}\geq n^{k}\left(1-\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\frac{j}{n}\right)=n^{k-1}\left(n-\frac{k(k-1)}{2}\right).

Therefore we have

\displaystyle|t(\mathbf{F},\mathbf{G})-t_{\rm inj}(\mathbf{F},\mathbf{G})|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{k}}\left|\hom(\mathbf{F},\mathbf{G})-\frac{n^{k}}{(n)_{k}}{\rm inj}(\mathbf{F},\mathbf{G})\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{k}}\left|\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\varphi:V(F)\to V(G)\\
\varphi\mathrm{\;not\;injective}\end{subarray}}\prod_{e\in E(F)}\langle f(e),g(\varphi(e))\rangle\right)-\frac{n^{k}-(n)_{k}}{(n)_{k}}{\rm inj}(\mathbf{F},\mathbf{G})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n^{k}}\left|\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\varphi:V(F)\to V(G)\\
\varphi\mathrm{\;not\;injective}\end{subarray}}\prod_{e\in E(F)}\langle f(e),g(\varphi(e))\rangle\right)\right|+\frac{n^{k}-(n)_{k}}{n^{k}}\left|t_{\rm inj}(\mathbf{F},\mathbf{G})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n^{k}}\left|\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\varphi:V(F)\to V(G)\\
\varphi\mathrm{\;not\;injective}\end{subarray}}\prod_{e\in E(F)}\langle f(e),g(\varphi(e))\rangle\right)\right|+\frac{k(k-1)}{2(n-1)}\Pi_{f}\|g\|_{l}^{l}

For the first term, an argument identical to the previous leads to

\frac{1}{n^{k}}\left|\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\varphi:V(F)\to V(G)\\
\varphi\mathrm{\;not\;injective}\end{subarray}}\prod_{e\in E(F)}\langle f(e),g(\varphi(e))\rangle\right)\right|\leq\frac{n^{k}-(n)_{k}}{n^{k}}\Pi_{f}\Bigl(\prod_{e\in E(F)}{\sf E}_{\mathrm{ninj}}\bigl(\|g({\boldsymbol{\varphi}}(e)\|_{\mathcal{Z}}^{l}\bigr)\Bigr)^{1/l},

where the expectation is taken over all non-injective maps. It remains to investigate the distribution of
{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}(e)
. Note that since the distribution under all maps would be uniform on
V(G)\times V(G)
, whereas under injective maps uniform on the off-diagonal elements, we see that for non-injective maps, we have
{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}(e)=(u,v)\in V(G)\times V(G)
with probability

\displaystyle\frac{n^{k}-(n)_{k}\frac{n}{n-1}}{n^{k}-(n)_{k}}\cdot\frac{1}{n^{2}}\qquad\mathrm{if}\;u\neq v;

\displaystyle\frac{n^{k}}{n^{k}-(n)_{k}}\cdot\frac{1}{n^{2}}\qquad\mathrm{if}\;u=v.

However
g(u,u)=0\in\mathcal{B}
for all
u\in V(G)
,
meaning

{\sf E}_{\mathrm{ninj}}\bigl(\|g({\boldsymbol{\varphi}}(e)\|_{\mathcal{Z}}^{l}\bigr)\leq\frac{n^{k}-(n)_{k}\frac{n}{n-1}}{n^{k}-(n)_{k}}\|g\|_{l}^{l}<\|g\|_{l}^{l},

and so

|t(\mathbf{F},\mathbf{G})-t_{\rm inj}(\mathbf{F},\mathbf{G})|\leq\frac{k(k-1)}{2n}\Pi_{f}\|g\|_{l}^{l}+\frac{k(k-1)}{2(n-1)}\Pi_{f}\|g\|_{l}^{l},

and the conclusion follows.
∎

Another type of decoration we consider is
\mathcal{L}
-decoration, given by
a map
w:~E(F)\to\mathcal{L}
(so every edge
ij
is decorated by a function

w_{ij}\in\mathcal{L}
). In this case, a “homomorphism density” can be
defined for a single graph: for an
\mathcal{L}
-decorated graph
(F,w)
on

V(F)=[k]
, we define

t(F,w):=\int\limits_{[0,1]^{V(F)}}\prod_{ij\in E(F)}w_{ij}(x_{i},x_{j})dx_{1}\ldots dx_{k}.

This integral is well defined: if
m=|E(F)|
, then by Hölder’s
Inequality,

\int\limits_{[0,1]^{V(F)}}\Bigl|\prod_{ij\in E(F)}w_{ij}(x_{i},x_{j})\Bigr|dx_{1}\ldots dx_{k}\leq\prod_{ij\in E(F)}\|w_{ij}\|_{m},

(4)

which is finite.

Most of the time we need the following special case. Consider a

\mathcal{B}
-decorated graph
\mathbf{F}=(F,f)
on
[k]
and a
\mathcal{Z}
-graphon
W
.
Then
w_{ij}=\langle f_{ij},W\rangle
defines an
\mathcal{L}
-decoration of

F
, and the previous definition specializes to

t(\mathbf{F},W):=t(F,w)=\int\limits_{[0,1]^{V(F)}}\prod_{ij\in E(F)}\langle f(ij),W(x_{i},x_{j})\rangle dx_{1}\ldots dx_{k}.

Finally, as a variation on the above, but without the dependence of the second variable on what simple graph
F
we chose. For any
\mathscr{F}
-decorated graph
\mathbf{F}
and
s\in\mathcal{L}^{\mathscr{F}}
we may define

t(\mathbf{F},s):=\int\limits_{[0,1]^{V(F)}}\prod_{ij\in E(F)}s_{f_{ij}}(x_{i},x_{j})dx_{1}\ldots dx_{k}.

We can think of getting information about a
\mathcal{Z}
-graphon
W
by
“probing” it with various “small”
\mathcal{B}
-decorated graphs
\mathbf{F}
.
It is often natural to restrict the decoration of our test graphs to
a subset
\mathscr{F}\subseteq\mathcal{B}
, for which homomorphism numbers of

\mathscr{F}
-decorated graphs carry special combinatorial information. The
family
\mathscr{F}
will be countable in our examples. We usually assume
that the set
\mathscr{F}
is
generating
in
\mathcal{B}
(meaning that

\overline{\operatorname{lin}\mathscr{F}}=\mathcal{B}
– this is sometimes also called total or fundamental). In this case, the values
t(\mathbf{F},W)
carry
the same information about
W
if we restrict
f
to

\mathscr{F}
-decorations than if we allow all
\mathcal{B}
-decorations. If
\mathcal{B}
is
finite dimensional, then any basis can be chosen for
\mathscr{F}
, but the
choice of the basis does not actually matter, as illustrated by the examples in
the next section.

2.3
Examples

A large variety of examples comes from
compact decorated
graphs
, i.e.,
\mathcal{K}
-decorated graphs where
\mathcal{K}
is a compact
Hausdorff space. To capture convergence of
\mathcal{K}
-decorated graphs, we
need to consider
\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{K})
-decorated graphs (where
\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{K})
is
the space of continuous real functions on
\mathcal{K}
, with the supremum
norm). The dual space
\mathcal{Z}=\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{K})
is the set of Radon measures on
the Borel sets of
\mathcal{K}
. This contains probability measures
concentrated on a single point, and thus
\mathcal{K}
-decorated graphs can
be thought of as special
\mathcal{Z}
-decorated graphs. We are also
interested in selecting a “nice” countable generating set

\mathscr{F}\subseteq\mathcal{B}
. It is interesting to note that different choices
of
\mathscr{F}
carry different combinatorial information.

Many examples of
\mathcal{K}
-decorated graphs with combinatorial
significance were discussed in
[
11
]
and also in

[
9
]
, Chapter 17, and we only mention them briefly.

Example 2.4

(Simple graphs)
.

Let
\mathcal{K}=\{0,1\}
be the discrete space with two elements
corresponding to “non-edge” and “edge”. The space
\mathcal{B}
consists
of all maps
\{0,1\}\to\mathbb{R}
, i.e., of all pairs
(f(0),f(1))
of real
numbers. Clearly, the dual space
\mathcal{Z}
can also be identified with

\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

A natural generating subset
\mathscr{F}
consists of the pairs
(1,1)
and

(0,1)
. Homomorphism density corresponds to that for simple graphs.
Every probability distribution on
\mathcal{K}
can be represented by a
number between
0
and
1
, which is the probability of the element
“edge”. So every symmetric measurable function
W:~[0,1]^{2}\mapsto[0,1]
defines a
\mathcal{Z}
-graphon.

One may, however, take another basis in
\mathcal{B}
, namely the pairs

(0,1)
and
(1,0)
. Then again
\mathscr{F}
-decorated graphs can be thought
of as simple graphs, and
\hom(F,G)
counts the number of maps that
preserve both adjacency and non-adjacency.

Example 2.5

(Bounded multigraphs and multi-test-graphs)
.

Let
G
be a multigraph with edge multiplicities at most
d
. Then

G
can be thought of as a
\mathcal{K}
-decorated graph, where

\mathcal{K}=\{0,1,\dots,d\}
. This can be modeled with

\mathcal{B}\cong\mathcal{Z}\cong\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
.

An interesting basis in
\mathcal{B}
consists of the functions

\mathscr{F}=\{1,x,\dots,x^{d}\}
. We can represent an
\mathscr{F}
-decorated graph by
a multigraph with edge multiplicities at most
d
, where an edge
decorated by
x^{i}
is represented by
i
parallel edges. The
advantage of this is that
\hom(F,G)
is then the number of
node-and-edge homomorphisms of
F
into
G
as multigraphs, and so

t(F,G)
is the node-and-edge homomorphism density.

Taking the standard basis
\{e_{0},e_{1},\dots,e_{d}\}
in
\mathcal{B}
, we can
represent an edge label
e_{i}
by
i
parallel edges. In this case,

\hom(F,G)
counts multiplicity-preserving homomorphisms of
F
into

G
.

As a third possibility, we can consider the basis vectors

f_{i}=e_{0}+\dots+e_{i}
(
i=0,\dots,d
). Representing an edge label
f_{i}

by
i
parallel edges,
\hom(F,G)
counts node-homomorphisms of
F

into
G
.

This shows that in the case of bounded edge-multiplicities, different
ways of counting homomorphisms between multigraphs are essentially
equivalent, they differ in a simple basis transformation in the space

\mathcal{B}
.

Example 2.6

(Bounded weighted graphs and multi-test-graphs)
.

This example is a rather straightforward extension
of the previous one. Let
\mathcal{K}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
be a bounded closed
interval. Let
\mathscr{F}
be the collection of monomial functions
x\mapsto x^{j}
for
j\in\mathbb{N}
on
\mathcal{K}
(we denote by
\mathbb{N}
the set of nonnegative
integers, and by
\mathbb{N}^{*}
, the set of positive integers). The linear
hull of
\mathscr{F}
is dense in
\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{K})
. It is natural to consider
an
\mathscr{F}
-decorated graph
F
as a multigraph, and then
t(F,G)
is
the weighted homomorphism number as defined e.g. in
[
9
]
.

Compact decorations do not utilize the full strength of the general
theory developed below. We discuss a couple of examples of this kind,
and will return to these examples in Section
4
.

Example 2.7

(Multigraphs and simple test-graphs)
.

Let us consider multigraphs with unbounded
edge-multiplicity, and simple graphs as test-graphs. It turns out
that in this case, multigraphs can be thought of as
\mathbb{N}
-decorated
simple graphs, and the fact that the edgeweights are nonnegative
integers plays no role; so we can take
\mathcal{Z}=\mathcal{B}=\mathbb{R}
, and consider the
basis
\mathscr{F}=\{1\}
in
\mathcal{B}
. If
F
is a simple (
\mathscr{F}
-decorated)
graph and
G
is an edge-weighted (complete) graph, then
\hom(F,G)

is the homomorphism number into
G
as a multigraph.

Example 2.8

(Multigraphs and multi-test-graphs)
.

Consider multigraphs with unbounded
edge-multiplicity, and multigraphs as test-graphs. We have already
seen that homomorphisms of a multigraph into another can be defined
in different ways. In the bounded case, these notions turned out to
be essentially the same, but in the unbounded case, the
correspondence is more subtle.

We sketch the idea how to fit convergence according to node-and-edge
homomorphism densities into our framework. Let
\mathcal{B}=\mathbb{R}[X]
be the
space of polynomials in one variable, and let
\mathcal{Z}
be the space of real
sequences with finite support. For
f\in\mathcal{B}
and

a=(a_{1},a_{2},\dots)\in\mathcal{Z}
, let us define

\langle f,a\rangle=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}a_{i}f(i).

We encode a multigraph
F
by decorating each edge
e\in E(F)
with
multiplicity
m
by the polynomial
X^{m}
, to get an edge-decorated
simple graph
\widehat{F}
. We encode a “target” multigraph
G
by
labeling each edge
e\in E(F)
with multiplicity
m
by sequence

e_{m}
with a single
1
in the
m
-th position, to get an
edge-decorated complete graph
\widetilde{G}
. Then
\hom(\widehat{F},\widetilde{G})=\hom(F,G)
.

The problem with this construction is that
\mathcal{B}
and
\mathcal{Z}
are not
Banach spaces, and our theory needs the Banach space structure. We
will describe how to work around this in Section

4.1
.

2.4
The jumble norm

The classical cut-norm, which plays a key role in the limit theory of
bounded graphons, is unfortunately not well suited for this general
setting that allows for unbounded functions. We introduce a variant
that serves this goal better.

Definition 2.9
.

Let the
jumble-norm
on the function space
\mathcal{L}
be defined by

\|u\|_{{}_{\boxtimes}}:=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}S,T\subseteq[0,1]\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda(S)\lambda(T)}}\Bigl|\int\limits_{S\times T}u(x,y)dx\,dy\Bigr|

(To motivate the name, we note that Thomason
[
14
]
uses this
normalization in the definition of “jumble graphs”, a version of
quasirandom graphs.)

Also, let

\|W\|_{{}_{\boxtimes}}:=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in\mathcal{B}\backslash\{0\}\\
\|f\|_{\mathcal{B}}=1\end{subarray}}\bigl\|\langle f,W\rangle\bigr\|_{{}_{\boxtimes}}.

We remark that this can also be written as

\sup_{S,T\subseteq[0,1]}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda(S)\lambda(T)}}\Bigl\|\int\limits_{S\times T}W(x,y)dx\,dy\Bigr\|_{\mathcal{Z}},

where the integral is to be taken in the weak-* sense, i.e.,
\int\limits_{S\times T}W
is the unique element
z\in\mathcal{Z}
such that for any
f\in\mathcal{B}
one has
\langle f,z\rangle=\int\limits_{S\times T}\langle f,W\rangle

(cf.
[
13
, Chap. XII., Prop. 3.3]
).

We note that for every
u\in\mathcal{L}
(in fact, for every
u\in L_{2}([0,1]^{2}
), the supremum in the definition above is finite.
Indeed,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda(S)\lambda(T)}}\Bigl|\int\limits_{S\times T}u(x,y)dx\,dy\Bigr|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda(S)\lambda(T)}}\bigl|\langle{\mathbbm{1}}_{S\times T},u\rangle\big|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda(S)\lambda(T)}}\|{\mathbbm{1}}_{S\times T}\|_{2}\|u\|_{2}=\|u\|_{2}.

(5)

A fortiori, we obtain
\|W\|_{{}_{\boxtimes}}\leq\|W\|_{2}
.

Let us also compare the jumble norm with the cut norm

\|u\|_{\square}:=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}S,T\subseteq[0,1]\end{subarray}}\Bigl|\int\limits_{S\times T}u(x,y)dx\,dy\Bigr|.

It is easy to see that

\|u\|_{\square}\leq\|u\|_{{}_{\boxtimes}}\leq\|u\|_{\square}^{1/2}\|u\|_{\infty}^{1/2},

showing that for bounded kernels the two norms define the same
topology.

In the case of stepfunctions, the sets attaining the supremum in the
definition can be chosen in a special way.

Lemma 2.10
.

Let
\mathscr{P}=\{S_{1},\ldots,S_{k}\}
be a measurable partition of
[0,1]
,
and
u:[0,1]^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
a stepfunction with steps in
\mathscr{P}\times\mathscr{P}
.
Then there exist
T_{1}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{\alpha}S_{a_{i}}
and

T_{2}=\bigcup_{j=1}^{\beta}S_{b_{j}}
such that

\|u\|_{{}_{\boxtimes}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda(T_{1})\lambda(T_{2})}}\Bigl|\int\limits_{T_{1}\times T_{2}}u\Bigr|.

Proof.

First note that since
u
is a stepfunction, given any
Q_{1},Q_{2}\subseteq[0,1]
, the value of

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda(Q_{1})\lambda(Q_{2})}}\Bigl|\int\limits_{Q_{1}\times Q_{2}}u\Bigr|

\displaystyle=\frac{\left|\sum_{j_{1},j_{2}=1}^{k}\lambda(Q_{1}\cap S_{j_{1}})\lambda(Q_{2}\cap S_{j_{2}})u|_{(Q_{1}\cap S_{j_{1}})\times(Q_{2}\cap S_{j_{2}})}\right|}{\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k}\lambda(Q_{1}\cap S_{j})\right)\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k}\lambda(Q_{2}\cap S_{j})\right)}

\displaystyle=:\mathcal{F}\left((\lambda(Q_{i}\cap S_{j}))_{1\leq j\leq k,i\in\{1,2\}}\right)

depends only on the
2k
variables

(\lambda(Q_{i}\cap S_{j}))_{1\leq j\leq k,i\in\{1,2\}}\in\left(\prod_{j=1}^{k}[0,\lambda(S_{j})]\right)\times\left(\prod_{j=1}^{k}[0,\lambda(S_{j})]\right),

and this dependence is continuous. By compactness there then exist some
\alpha_{i,j}\in[0,\lambda(S_{j})]
(
1\leq j\leq k,i\in\{1,2\}
) at which
\mathcal{F}
attains its maximum. Since the Lebesgue measure on
[0,1]
is non-atomic, there actually exist measurable subsets
R_{i,j}\subset S_{j}
with
\lambda(R_{i,j})=\alpha_{i,j}
for
1\leq j\leq k,i\in\{1,2\}
. Letting
R_{i}:=\bigcup_{j=1}^{k}R_{i,j}
(
i\in\{1,2\}
), we obtain

\|u\|_{{}_{\boxtimes}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda(R_{1})\lambda(R_{2})}}\Bigl|\int\limits_{R_{1}\times R_{2}}u\Bigr|.

(6)

Now assume that for some
1\leq\ell\leq k

0<\lambda(R_{1,\ell})<\lambda(S_{\ell}).

Let
R_{1}^{\prime}:=R_{1}\backslash S_{\ell}
and
R_{1}^{\prime\prime}:=R_{1}\cup S_{\ell}
, and set

a:=\int_{R_{1}^{\prime}\times R_{2}}u
,
d:=\lambda(R_{2})>0
,
c:=d\lambda(R_{1}^{\prime})\geq 0

and
b:=\int_{\{x_{0}\}\times R_{2}}u(x_{0},\cdot)
for some
x_{0}\in S_{\ell}
. Note that the value of
b
is well-defined since
u
is a
stepfunction. We may, without loss of
generality, assume that
a\geq 0
. If we were to have
a>0,b<0
, then the integral of
u
over
R_{1}^{\prime}\times R_{2}
and
R_{1,\ell}\times R_{2}
would have different signs, so at least one of the choices
R_{1}^{\prime}
or
R_{1,\ell}
instead of
R_{1}
would lead to a strictly larger value than
\|u\|_{{}_{\boxtimes}}
, which is a contradiction. Hence we may assume
b\geq 0
as well.
Let now
S_{\ell}^{\prime}
be any subset
of
S_{\ell}
of measure
\alpha
. With the notation

N(\alpha):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda(R_{1}^{\prime}\cup S_{\ell}^{\prime})\lambda(R_{2})}}\Bigl|\int\limits_{(R_{1}^{\prime}\cup S_{\ell}^{\prime})\times R_{2}}u\Bigr|=\frac{a+b\alpha}{\sqrt{c+d\alpha}}

we have that

N^{\prime}(\alpha)=\frac{bd\alpha+(2bc-ad)}{2(c+d\alpha)^{3/2}},

i.e., if
bd>0
, the function
N
is strictly monotone decreasing
until its minimum, then strictly monotone increasing, and hence if

N(0)<N(\lambda(R_{1}\cap S_{\ell}))
, then also
N(\lambda(R_{1}\cap S_{\ell}))<N(\lambda(S_{\ell}))
. If
bd=0
, then at least one of

N(0)\geq N(\lambda(R_{1}\cap S_{\ell}))
and
N(\lambda(R_{1}\cap S_{\ell}))\leq N(\lambda(S_{\ell}))
is true.

But note that

N(0)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda(R_{1}^{\prime})\lambda(R_{2})}}\Bigl|\int\limits_{R_{1}^{\prime}\times R_{2}}u\Bigr|,

N(\lambda(R_{1}\cap S_{\ell}))=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda(R_{1})\lambda(R_{2})}}\Bigl|\int\limits_{R_{1}\times R_{2}}u\Bigr|,

and

N(\lambda(S_{\ell}))=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda(R_{1}^{\prime\prime})\lambda(R_{2})}}\Bigl|\int\limits_{R_{1}^{\prime\prime}\times R_{2}}u\Bigr|.

This means that we may choose
R_{1}
so that it contains either all of

S_{\ell}
, or none of it, whilst still satisfying (
6
).
Iterating for all of the
S_{i}
we obtain the desired
T_{1}
, and
repeating for
R_{2}
yields us
T_{2}
.
∎

The next lemma shows how weighted integrals can be estimated in terms
of the jumble norm.

Lemma 2.11
.

Let
u\in L^{2}([0,1]^{2})
and
f,g\in L^{3}([0,1])
. Then

\Bigl|\int\limits_{[0,1]^{2}}u(x,y)f(x)g(y)dx\,dy\Bigr|\leq 8\|u\|_{{}_{\boxtimes}}\|f\|_{3}\|g\|_{3}.

Proof.

First, we prove the stronger inequality

\Bigl|\int\limits_{[0,1]^{2}}u(x,y)f(x)g(y)dx\,dy\Bigr|\leq 2\|u\|_{{}_{\boxtimes}}\|f\|_{3}\|g\|_{3}

(7)

for the case when
f,g\geq 0
. Writing
f(x)=\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}{\mathbbm{1}}(t\leq f(x))\,dt
and
g(x)=\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}{\mathbbm{1}}(s\leq g(x))\,ds
, we have

\displaystyle\int\limits_{[0,1]^{2}}

\displaystyle u(x,y)f(x)g(y)\,dx\,dy=\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\int\limits_{[0,1]^{2}}u(x,y){\mathbbm{1}}(f(x)\geq t){\mathbbm{1}}(g(y)\geq s)\,dx\,dy\,dt\,ds

\displaystyle\leq\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\|u\|_{{}_{\boxtimes}}\sqrt{\lambda\{x:f(x)\geq t\}\lambda\{y:g(y)\geq s\}}\,dt\,ds

\displaystyle=\|u\|_{{}_{\boxtimes}}\Bigl(\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\sqrt{\lambda\{x:f(x)\geq t\}}\,dt\Bigr)\Bigl(\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\sqrt{\lambda\{y:g(y)\geq s\}}\,ds\Bigr).

To estimate the integrals on the right, let
h
denote the monotone
decreasing reordering of
f
. Then, using Hölder’s Inequality,

\displaystyle\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\sqrt{\lambda\{x:f(x)\geq t\}}\,dt

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\sqrt{h^{-1}(t)}\,dt=\int\limits_{0}^{1}h(x^{2})\,dx=\int\limits_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{2\sqrt{y}}h(y)\,dy

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\Bigl\|\frac{1}{\sqrt{y}}\Bigr\|_{3/2}\|h\|_{3}=2^{1/3}\|f\|_{3}.

Using a similar estimate for
g
, then repeating for the function
-u
we obtain (
7
).

In the general case, let
f^{+}
and
g^{+}
denote the positive parts,
and
f^{-}
and
g^{-}
the negative parts of the functions
f
and
g
,
respectively, so that
f=f^{+}-f^{-}
and
g=g^{+}-g^{-}
. Then

\displaystyle\Bigl|\int\limits_{[0,1]^{2}}

\displaystyle u(x,y)f(x)g(y)dx\,dy\Bigr|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\,\int\limits_{[0,1]^{2}}u(x,y)f^{+}(x)g^{+}(y)dx\,dy\right|+\left|\,\int\limits_{[0,1]^{2}}u(x,y)f^{+}(x)g^{-}(y)dx\,dy\right|

\displaystyle+\left|\,\int\limits_{[0,1]^{2}}u(x,y)f^{-}(x)g^{+}(y)dx\,dy\right|+\left|\,\int\limits_{[0,1]^{2}}u(x,y)f^{-}(x)g^{-}(y)dx\,dy\right|

Each term can be estimated by (
7
), and using the trivial
facts that
\|f^{+}\|_{3},\|f^{-}\|_{3}\leq\|f\|_{3}
and
\|g^{+}\|_{3},\|g^{-}\|_{3}\leq\|g\|_{3}
, the lemma follows.
∎

Remark 2.12
.

A more careful computation would improve the factor of
8
to
4
.
One can strengthen the lemma in other ways as well. First, instead of
the
L^{3}
-norms on the right side, we could use the
L^{p}
-norm for
any
p>2
(but not
p=2
). Second, for
f\in L^{p}[0,1]
(
p>2
) we can
introduce the functional

K(f):=\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\sqrt{\lambda\{x:|f(x)|\geq t\}}\,dt.

It is not hard to see that
K(.)
is a norm on
L^{p}(0,1)
for
p>2
(it is in fact the Lorentz norm on the larger space
L^{2,1}
),
and it satisfies

K(f)\leq 2^{-1/p}\Bigl(\frac{p-1}{p-2}\Bigr)^{(p-1)/p}\|f\|_{p}.

In terms of the norm
K
, the conclusion of Lemma
2.11
can be
strengthened (with the same proof):

\Bigl|\int\limits_{[0,1]^{2}}u(x,y)f(x)g(y)dx\,dy\Bigr|\leq 4\|u\|_{{}_{\boxtimes}}K(f)K(g).

2.5
Counting Lemma

Our next goal is to prove appropriate generalizations of the Counting
Lemma from bounded kernels to unbounded ones (see Lemma 10.24 in

[
9
]
). It is clear that

|t(K_{2},w)-t(K_{2},w^{\prime})|=\Bigl|\int_{[0,1]^{2}}(w-w^{\prime})\Bigr|\leq\|w-w^{\prime}\|_{{}_{\boxtimes}}.

The following lemma generalizes this inequality to densities of other
graphs.

Lemma 2.13
.

Let
F
be a simple graph with
l\geq 2
edges, and let
w
and
w^{\prime}

be
\mathcal{L}
-decorations of
F
. For
a\in E(F)
, define

M_{a}:=\max\bigl\{\|w_{a}\|_{3l-3},\|w^{\prime}_{a}\|_{3l-3}\bigr\}.

Then we have

\displaystyle|t(F,w)-t(F,w^{\prime})|\leq 8\sum_{a\in E(F)}\|w_{a}-w^{\prime}_{a}\|_{{}_{\boxtimes}}\prod_{b\in E(F)\setminus\{a\}}M_{b}.

If we define
\|w\|_{0}=1
for every function
w
, then the lemma
remains valid for all graphs
F
with at least one edge.

Proof.

Let
V(F)=[k]
. It suffices to show that if
w^{\prime}
is obtained from
w
by
changing the decoration of a single edge
a=uv
, then

|t(F,w)-t(F,w^{\prime})|\leq 8\|w_{a}-w_{a}^{\prime}\|_{{}_{\boxtimes}}\,\prod_{b\in E(F)\setminus\{a\}}M_{b}.

We may assume that
u=1
and
v=2
. Then

t(F,w)-t(F,w^{\prime})=\int\limits\limits_{[0,1]^{k}}\bigl(w_{a}(x_{1},x_{2})-w^{\prime}_{a}(x_{1},x_{2})\bigr)\prod_{ij\not=e}w_{ij}(x_{i},x_{j})\,dx.

(8)

We can break the product into two parts:

\prod{}^{\prime}=\prod_{ij\in E(F),i=1,j>2},\qquad\prod{}^{\prime\prime}=\prod_{ij\in E(F),i,j>1}.

Let us fix
x_{3},\dots,x_{k}
, and integrate just with respect to
x_{1}

and
x_{2}
. Then
f=\prod{}^{\prime}
depends only on
x_{1}
and
g=\prod{}^{\prime\prime}

depends only on
x_{2}
, and clearly
f,g\in L^{p}(0,1)
for every

p\geq 1
. So we get by Lemma
2.11
that

\displaystyle\Bigl|\int\limits_{[0,1]^{2}}

\displaystyle\bigl(w_{a}(x_{1},x_{2})-w^{\prime}_{a}(x_{1},x_{2})\bigr)f(x_{1})g(x_{2})\,dx_{1}\,dx_{2}\Bigr|

\displaystyle\leq 8\|w_{a}-w_{a}^{\prime}\|_{{}_{\boxtimes}}\|f\|_{3}\|g\|_{3}.

Integrating this with respect to
x_{3},x_{4},\dots
, we get by
Hölder’s Inequality

\displaystyle|t(F,w)-t(F,w^{\prime})|

\displaystyle\leq 8\|w_{a}-w_{a}^{\prime}\|_{{}_{\boxtimes}}\int\limits_{[0,1]^{k-2}}\left(\int\limits_{0}^{1}\prod{}^{\prime}|w_{ij}(x_{i},x_{j})|^{3}\,dx_{1}\right)^{1/3}

\displaystyle\times\left(\int\limits_{0}^{1}\prod{}^{\prime\prime}|w_{ij}(x_{i},x_{j})|^{3}\,dx_{2}\right)^{1/3}dx_{3}\ldots dx_{k}

\displaystyle\leq 8\|w_{a}-w_{a}^{\prime}\|_{{}_{\boxtimes}}\left(\int\limits_{[0,1]^{k}}\prod{}^{\prime}|w_{ij}(x_{i},x_{j})|^{3}dx_{1}\ldots dx_{k}\right)^{1/3}

\displaystyle\times\left(\int\limits_{[0,1]^{k}}\prod{}^{\prime\prime}|w_{ij}(x_{i},x_{j})|^{3}dx_{1}\ldots dx_{k}\right)^{1/3}

\displaystyle\leq 8\|w_{a}-w_{a}^{\prime}\|_{{}_{\boxtimes}}\prod_{ij\not=e}\|w_{ij}\|_{3l-3}\leq 8\|w_{a}-w_{a}^{\prime}\|_{{}_{\boxtimes}}\prod_{b\not=a}M_{b}.

This proves the Lemma.
∎

As a special case, we get the following version of the Counting
Lemma.

Corollary 2.14

(
Counting Lemma for Unbounded Kernels).

Let
u,w\in\mathcal{L}
and let
F
be a simple graph with
l\geq 1

Then

|t(F,u)-t(F,w)|\leq 8l\|u-w\|_{{}_{\boxtimes}}\max\bigl(\|u\|_{3l-3},\|w\|_{3l-3}\bigr)^{l-1}.

As a further corollary, we can estimate the difference between the
homomorphism densities of a decorated graph in two
\mathcal{Z}
-graphons
with the help of distances in jumble norm.

Corollary 2.15
.

Let
\mathbf{F}=(F,f)
be a
\mathcal{B}
-decorated graph with
k

l

edges, and let
U,W\in\mathcal{W}_{\mathcal{Z}}

\displaystyle|t(\mathbf{F},U)-t(\mathbf{F},W)|\leq 8l\,\|f\|_{\infty}^{l}\,\|U-W\|_{{}_{\boxtimes}}\,\max\bigl(\|U\|_{3l-3},\|W\|_{3l-3}\bigr)^{l-1}.

Let
u_{a}:=\langle f_{a},U\rangle
,
w_{a}:=\langle f_{a},W\rangle

M_{a}:=\max\bigl\{\|u_{a}\|_{3l-3},\|w_{a}\|_{3l-3}\bigr\}

\displaystyle|t(\mathbf{F},U)-t(\mathbf{F},W)|=

\displaystyle|t(F,(u_{a})_{a\in E(F)})-t(F,(w_{a})_{a\in E(F)})|

\displaystyle 8\sum_{a\in E(F)}\|u_{a}-w_{a}\|_{{}_{\boxtimes}}\prod_{b\in E(F)\setminus\{a\}}M_{b}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 8l\,\|f\|_{\infty}\|U-W\|_{{}_{\boxtimes}}\max\bigl(\|f\|_{\infty}\|U\|_{3l-3},\|f\|_{\infty}\|W\|_{3l-3}\bigr)^{l-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 8l\,\|f\|_{\infty}^{l}\,\|U-W\|_{{}_{\boxtimes}}\,\max\bigl(\|U\|_{3l-3},\|W\|_{3l-3}\bigr)^{l-1}

∎

\displaystyle|t(\mathbf{F},s)-t(\mathbf{F},s^{\prime})|\leq 8\sum_{ij\in E(F)}\left(\mathchoice{\left\|s_{f_{ij}}-s^{\prime}_{f_{ij}}\right\|_{{{{{}_{\boxtimes}}}}}}{\left\|s_{f_{ij}}-s^{\prime}_{f_{ij}}\right\|_{{{{{}_{\boxtimes}}}}}}{\left\|s_{f_{ij}}-s^{\prime}_{f_{ij}}\right\|_{{{{{}_{\boxtimes}}}}}}{\left\|s_{f_{ij}}-s^{\prime}_{f_{ij}}\right\|_{{{{{}_{\boxtimes}}}}}}\prod_{\alpha\beta\in E(F)\backslash\{ij\}}M_{f_{\alpha\beta}}\right)

Lemma 2.17
.

Let
\mathbf{F}=(F,f)

2.6
The Weak Regularity Lemma

We generalize the Regularity Lemma to our setting, based on the
notion of the jumble norm. The proof is a straightforward
generalization from the bounded case, but since we work with a
different norm here, we include it for completeness.

The
stepping operator
associated with a measurable partition

\mathcal{P}=\{S_{1},\dots,S_{k}\}
of
[0,1]
assigns to every function

u\in\mathcal{L}
the stepfunction
u_{\mathcal{P}}
, where

u_{\mathcal{P}}(x,y)=\frac{1}{\lambda(S_{i})\lambda(S_{j})}\int\limits_{S_{i}\times S_{j}}u(x,y)\,dx\,dy\quad\text{for }x\in S_{i},y\in S_{j},

with the convention of assigning value
0
whenever one of the sets has measure
0
.

Also, again using weak-* integrals, the stepping operator can be extended to
\mathcal{Z}
-graphons as

W_{\mathcal{P}}(x,y):=\frac{1}{\lambda(S_{i})\lambda(S_{j})}\int\limits_{S_{i}\times S_{j}}W(x,y)\,dx\,dy\quad\text{for }x\in S_{i},y\in S_{j}

We note that by definition of the weak-* integral, the stepping operator commutes with applying a linear
functional: for every
W\in\mathcal{W}_{\mathcal{Z}}
and
f\in\mathcal{B}
, we have

\langle f,W\rangle_{\mathcal{P}}=\langle f,W_{\mathcal{P}}\rangle.

(9)

Let us summarize some basic properties of this operator; analogues of
these for the cut-norm and for bounded functions were proved in

[
9
]
.

Proposition 2.18
.

The stepping operators are contractive in the jumble norm.

Proof.

Let
u\in\mathcal{L}
and let
\mathcal{P}
be a measurable partition. Let
S
and

T
be sets attaining the supremum in the definition of

\|u_{\mathcal{P}}\|_{{}_{\boxtimes}}
, which by Lemma
2.10
can be assumed
to be unions of partition classes of
\mathcal{P}
. Then

\|u_{\mathcal{P}}\|_{{}_{\boxtimes}}=\frac{1}{\lambda(S)\lambda(T)}\Bigl|\int\limits_{S\times T}u_{\mathcal{P}}(x,y)\,dx\,dy\Bigr|=\frac{1}{\lambda(S)\lambda(T)}\Bigl|\int\limits_{S\times T}u(x,y)\,dx\,dy\Bigr|\leq\|u\|_{{}_{\boxtimes}}.

∎

It is easy to check that the stepping operator is contractive with
respect to the
L^{p}
-norm of functions in
L^{p}([0,1]^{2})
for all

p\geq 1
. In fact, it is contractive with respect to any
“reasonable” norm (cf. Proposition 14.13 in
[
9
]
).

The stepfunction
u_{\mathcal{P}}
is the best approximation of
u
in the

L^{2}
-norm among all stepfunctions with the same steps. This is no
longer true if the
L^{2}
-norm is replaced by the jumble norm, but it
is true up to a factor of
2
, as shown by the following
straightforward generalization of an observation of Frieze and Kannan

[
5
]
.

Proposition 2.19
.

Let
v\in\mathcal{L}
be a stepfunction and let
\mathcal{P}
be the partition of

[0,1]
that is finer than the partition into the steps of
v
. Then
for any
u\in\mathcal{L}
we have

\bigl\|u-u_{\mathcal{P}}\bigr\|_{{}_{\boxtimes}}\leq 2\bigl\|u-v\bigr\|_{{}_{\boxtimes}}.

Proof.

We have
v=v_{\mathcal{P}}
, and hence by Proposition
2.18
,

\displaystyle\|u-u_{\mathcal{P}}\|_{{}_{\boxtimes}}

\displaystyle\leq\|u-v\|_{{}_{\boxtimes}}+\|v-u_{\mathcal{P}}\|_{{}_{\boxtimes}}=\|u-v\|_{{}_{\boxtimes}}+\|v_{\mathcal{P}}-u_{\mathcal{P}}\|_{{}_{\boxtimes}}

\displaystyle=\|u-v\|_{{}_{\boxtimes}}+\|(v-u)_{\mathcal{P}})\|_{{}_{\boxtimes}}\leq 2\|u-v\|_{{}_{\boxtimes}}.

∎

After this preparation, we are able to state the main lemma in this
section.

Lemma 2.20

(
Weak Regularity Lemma for Unbounded Kernels).

For every symmetric function
w\in L_{2}([0,1]^{2})
and every
k\geq 1
there is a partition
\mathcal{P}
of

[0,1]
into
k
measurable sets such that

\|w-w_{\mathcal{P}}\|_{{}_{\boxtimes}}\leq\frac{4}{\sqrt{\log k}}\|w\|_{2}.

In the case of bounded kernels (or simple graphs), one may require
that the partition
\mathcal{P}
is an equipartition (allowing a little
larger error). This is, however, no longer true in the unbounded
setting: parts where the function is large must be partitioned into
more pieces.

Proof.

Let

j=\Bigl\lfloor\frac{{\log k}}{2}\Bigr\rfloor\quad\text{ and }\quad c=\frac{2j+2}{{\log k}}>1.

Let
S
and
T
be measurable subsets of
[0,1]
such that

\|w\|_{{}_{\boxtimes}}\leq\frac{c}{\sqrt{\lambda(S)\lambda(T)}}\Bigl|\int\limits_{S\times T}w\Bigr|=c\frac{\langle w,{\mathbbm{1}}_{S\times T}\rangle}{\|{\mathbbm{1}}_{S\times T}\|_{2}}.

Let
a:=\langle w,{\mathbbm{1}}_{S\times T}\rangle/\|{\mathbbm{1}}_{S\times T}\|_{2}^{2}
.
Clearly
a\geq\|w\|_{{}_{\boxtimes}}/(c\|{\mathbbm{1}}_{S\times T}\|_{2})
, and so

\|w-a{\mathbbm{1}}_{S\times T}\|^{2}_{2}=\|w\|_{2}^{2}-a^{2}\|{\mathbbm{1}}_{S\times T}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\|w\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{1}{c^{2}}\|w\|_{{}_{\boxtimes}}^{2}.

(10)

Applying this observation repeatedly, we get pairs of sets
S_{i},T_{i}

and real numbers
a_{i}
such that for all
r=1,2,\dots
, the
“remainder”
w_{r}=w-\sum_{i=1}^{r}a_{i}{\mathbbm{1}}_{S_{i}\times T_{i}}
satisfies

\|w_{r}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\|w\|^{2}_{2}-\sum_{i=0}^{r-1}\frac{1}{c^{2}}\|w_{i}\|_{{}_{\boxtimes}}^{2}.

Applying this inequality with
r=j
, using (
2.4
) and

c>1
, it follows that

\sum_{i=0}^{j}\|w_{i}\|_{{}_{\boxtimes}}^{2}\leq c^{2}\|w\|^{2}_{2}.

Hence there is a
0\leq m\leq j
with

\|w_{m}\|_{{}_{\boxtimes}}\leq\frac{c}{\sqrt{j+1}}\|w\|_{2}=\frac{2\sqrt{j+1}}{\log k}\|w\|_{2}\leq\frac{2\sqrt{2j}}{\log k}\|w\|_{2}\leq\frac{2}{\sqrt{\log k}}\|w\|_{2},

which means that the stepfunction
v=\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}{\mathbbm{1}}_{S_{i}\times T_{i}}
satisfies the inequality

\|w-v\|_{{}_{\boxtimes}}\leq\frac{2}{\sqrt{\log k}}\|w\|_{2}.

The stepfunction
v
has at most
2^{j}\leq\sqrt{k}
steps. It may not
be symmetric, but we can replace it by
(v(x,y)+v(y,x))/2
, which has
at most
4^{j}\leq k
steps. By Proposition
2.19
, we can
replace
v
by
w_{\mathcal{P}}
(where
\mathcal{P}
is the partition into the steps
of
v
) at the cost of doubling the error.
∎

Lemma 2.21

(
Weak Regularity Lemma for several Unbounded Kernels).

For every
t\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
,
\varepsilon\in(0,\infty)
,

u_{1},u_{2},\ldots,u_{t}\in\mathcal{L}
and measurable partition
\mathscr{P}_{1}
of

[0,1]
there exists a positive integer
q=q(|\mathscr{P}_{1}|,t,\varepsilon)
and a
measurable
q
-partition
\mathscr{P}_{2}
of
[0,1]
refining
\mathscr{P}_{1}
such
that

\left\|u_{i}-(u_{i})_{\mathscr{P}_{2}}\right\|_{{}_{\boxtimes}}\leq\varepsilon\|u_{i}\|_{2}

for each
1\leq i\leq t
.

Proof.

Using Lemma
2.20
we obtain measurable
partitions
\mathscr{R}_{1},\ldots,\mathscr{R}_{t}
of
[0,1]
into

2^{64\lceil\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\rceil}
sets such that for each
1\leq i\leq t
we have

\left\|u_{i}-(u_{i})_{\mathscr{R}_{i}}\right\|_{{}_{\boxtimes}}\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\|u_{i}\|_{2}.

Let
\mathscr{P}_{2}
be the common refinement of
\mathscr{P}_{1}
and all of the

\mathscr{R}_{i}
’s. Then

|\mathscr{P}_{2}|=|\mathscr{P}_{1}|\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{t}|\mathscr{R}_{i}|=|\mathscr{P}_{1}|\cdot 2^{4t\lceil\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\rceil}=:q(|\mathscr{P}_{1}|,t,\varepsilon),

and by Proposition

2.19

\left\|u_{i}-(u_{i})_{\mathscr{P}_{2}}\right\|_{{}_{\boxtimes}}\leq 2\left\|u_{i}-(u_{i})_{\mathscr{R}_{i}}\right\|_{{}_{\boxtimes}}\leq\varepsilon\|u_{i}\|_{2}

for each
1\leq i\leq t
.
∎

3.1
Dense sets

In this preliminary section we prove two lemmas which will allow us
to use countable “test sets” for certain properties.
The first lemma lets us prove weak-* measurability using only a countable dense subset of
\mathcal{B}

Lemma 3.1
.

Let

be a countable dense subset, and let

∎

Next we show that when dealing with convergence of homomorphism

3.2
Moment sequences

Let
\mathscr{F}
be a countable generating subset of
\mathcal{B}
.

Definition 3.3
.

The
\mathscr{F}
-
moment sequence
of an element
z\in\mathcal{Z}
is the
family
(\langle f,z\rangle:~f\in\mathscr{F})
of real numbers. The

\mathscr{F}
-
moment function sequence
of a function

U:~[0,1]^{2}\to\mathcal{Z}
is the family
(\langle f,U\rangle:~f\in\mathscr{F})
of
functions.

Proposition 3.4
.

A family of measurable symmetric functions
u_{f}:~[0,1]^{2}\to\mathbb{R}

(f\in\mathscr{F})
is the
\mathscr{F}
-moment sequence of a symmetric weak-* measurable function
W:~[0,1]^{2}\to\mathcal{Z}
if and only if

(u_{f}(x,y):f\in\mathscr{F})
is the
\mathscr{F}
-moment sequence of some element of

\mathcal{Z}
for every
(x,y)\in[0,1]^{2}
.

Proof.

The “only if” part is trivial. To prove the “if” part, notice
that by assumption we may define
W
pointwise in a unique way.
Indeed, by the definition of an
\mathscr{F}
-moment sequence, for every

(x,y)\in[0,1]^{2}
there is a
z\in\mathcal{Z}
such that
u_{f}(x,y)=\langle f,z\rangle
for every
f\in\mathscr{F}
. Thus element
z
is uniquely
determined; indeed, if
z_{1}
and
z_{2}
are two such elements, then by
linearity
\langle g,z_{1}\rangle=\langle g,z_{2}\rangle
for every

, and then by the density of
\operatorname{lin}(\mathscr{F})
in
\mathcal{B}
we have

\langle b,z_{1}\rangle=\langle b,z_{2}\rangle
for every
b\in\mathcal{B}
,
implying that
z_{1}=z_{2}
. Thus we can define
W(x,y)=z
. Uniqueness of

also implies that

is symmetric.

We have to show that this function
W
is weak-* measurable as a
function
[0,1]^{2}\to\mathcal{Z}
. Let
\mathscr{G}:=\operatorname{lin}_{\mathbb{Q}}\mathscr{F}
, then
\mathscr{G}
is a
dense countable set in
\mathcal{B}
. By linearity, for every
g\in\mathscr{G}
the function
\langle g,W\rangle
is measurable. By Lemma
3.1
, the function

is then weak-* measurable.
∎

Our next lemma says that, under appropriate conditions, the limit of
a sequence of
\mathscr{F}
-moment function sequences of
\mathcal{Z}
-graphons is the

\mathscr{F}
-moment function sequence of a
\mathcal{Z}
-graphon.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
W_{n}\in\mathcal{W}_{\mathcal{Z}}
(

) and suppose that there are
constants

such that
\|W_{n}\|_{p}\leq c_{p}
for every

. Let
u_{n,f}=\langle f,W_{n}\rangle
for

and

, and suppose that for every

,

for almost all

for some function

. Then there exists a

\mathcal{Z}
-graphon

such that

for every

, and
u_{f}=\langle f,W\rangle
for all

.

Proof.

First we note that the condition that
\|W_{n}\|_{p}
is bounded implies
that for almost all points
(x,y)\in[0,1]^{2}
, the sequence

does not tend to infinity. We also know that for
almost all
(x,y)\in[0,1]^{2}
,

for all

(in particular

is measurable). Let us call a point

satisfying these conditions

ordinary
.

Fix an ordinary point
(x,y)
. We can find a subsequence
\mathbb{N}_{1}
of
the indices

for which

remains bounded, and we
can select a further subsequence
\mathbb{N}_{2}
for which the sequence

(W_{n}(x,y):~n\in\mathbb{N}_{2})
is weak-* convergent. Let its limit be

. By the definition of weak-* convergence,
this limit satisfies

\langle f,z\rangle=\lim_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{2}}\langle f,W_{n}(x,y)\rangle=\lim_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{2}}u_{n,f}(x,y)=u_{f}(x,y)

for every

, and also

\displaystyle\|z\|_{\mathcal{Z}}

\displaystyle=\sup_{b\in\mathcal{B}\atop\|b\|_{\mathcal{B}}=1}\langle b,z\rangle=\sup_{b\in\mathcal{B}\atop\|b\|_{\mathcal{B}}=1}\lim_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{2}}\langle b,W_{n}(x,y)\rangle

) holds for any weak-*

Now Proposition

applies and yields a symmetric

that is weak-* measurable, and

for every

. We want to show that

By the remark about uniqueness above,

∎

3.3
Measure preserving transformations

As a last step before turning our attention to proving the existence of a limit graphon for convergent sequences, it will be useful to touch upon the subject of uniqueness of the graphon representation. In other words, to what extent can we expect a graphon to be uniquely determined by the homomorphism densities? In the bounded, real valued case, this question can be fully answered, giving a characterization of all “equivalent” graphons, and this strongly ties into the fact that a number of convergence notions (density convergence, cut distance convergence, sampling convergence) turn out to be equivalent. In this more general setting, however, only partial results are known, and we refer to
[
8
]
for details. In this section we shall concentrate on a sufficient condition under which two graphons exhibit the same densities, as this will play a pivotal role in the proof of our main theorem.

For a finite
\mathcal{Z}
-decorated graph
\mathbf{G}
on the vertex set
[n]
, there is a natural way of identifying it with a
\mathcal{Z}
graphon
W_{\mathbf{G}}
that has the exact same densities. Namely, let

W_{\mathbf{G}}(x,y):=g_{\lceil nx\rceil\lceil ny\rceil}.

Then
W_{\mathbf{G}}
is a stepfunction that essentially corresponds to the adjacency matrix of
\mathbf{G}
, and an easy computation shows that indeed
t(\mathbf{F},\mathbf{G})=t(\mathbf{F},W_{\mathbf{G}})
for any
\mathcal{B}
-decorated graph
\mathbf{F}
. Now, note that for a finite graph
\mathbf{G}
, the value of
t(\mathbf{F},\mathbf{G})
does not change if we replace it by an isomorphic graph
\mathbf{G}^{\prime}
(i.e., we relabel its vertices). However,
W_{\mathbf{G}}\neq W_{\mathbf{G}}
, so this immediately shows that two different graphons may exhibit the same densities.

The relabeling of the vertices of
\mathbf{G}
essentially amounts to a permutation of the
1/n
-length intervals corresponding to the steps of
W_{\mathbf{G}}
. Now note that for graphons, the homomorphism densities are defined via integrals, and in general, for any measure preserving map
\varphi:[0,1]\to[0,1]
and any integrable function
H:[0,1]^{k}\to\mathbb{R}
, we have that

\int_{[0,1]^{k}}H=\int_{[0,1]^{k}}H\circ\varphi,

where
H\circ\varphi(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{k}):=H\circ\varphi^{\otimes k}(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{k})=H(\varphi(x_{1}),\varphi(x_{2}),\ldots,\varphi(x_{k})
. In particular, for any
\mathcal{Z}
-graphon
W
and
\mathcal{B}
-decorated graph
\mathbf{F}
we have
t(\mathbf{F},W)=t(\mathbf{F},W\circ\varphi)
. Also, less trivially, we have the following invariance of the jumble norm as well.

Lemma 3.6
.

For any
u\in\mathcal{L}
we have
\|u\|_{{}_{\boxtimes}}=\|u\circ\varphi\|_{{{}_{\boxtimes}}}
.

Proof.

The proof works essentially as the proof for the cut norm in
[
6
, Lemma 5.5]
. The result is straightforward for any stepfunction due to Lemma
2.10
and the fact that any measure preserving map is actually a measure preserving bijection between the finite atomic
\sigma
-algebras generated by the steps of non-zero measure of
u
and
u\circ\varphi
, respectively.

For a general
u\in\mathcal{L}
, consider an arbitrary
\varepsilon>0
and a stepfunction
v\in\mathcal{L}
such that
\|u-v\|_{2}<\varepsilon
. Then we have the following:

\displaystyle\left|\|v\|_{{}_{\boxtimes}}-\|v\circ\varphi\|_{{{}_{\boxtimes}}}\right|=0;

\displaystyle\|u-v\|_{{}_{\boxtimes}}\leq\|u-v\|_{2}<\varepsilon;

\displaystyle\|u\circ\varphi-v\circ\varphi\|_{{}_{\boxtimes}}\leq\|u\circ\varphi-v\circ\varphi\|_{2}=\|(u-v)\circ\varphi\|_{2}=\|u-v\|_{2}<\varepsilon.

A few applications of the triangle inequality then imply
\left|\|u\|_{{}_{\boxtimes}}-\|u\circ\varphi\|_{{{}_{\boxtimes}}}\right|<2\varepsilon
.
∎

3.4
Limit graphon

The goal of this section is to prove our main result on the existence
of a limit graphon in the Banach space valued case. The proof follows the overall structure of the proof of the corresponding results in
[
9
,
10
]
.

Theorem 3.7
.

Let
\mathscr{F}
be a countable generating subset of
\mathcal{B}
and

W_{1},W_{2},\ldots
be
\mathcal{Z}
-graphons satisfying the following
conditions:

(i)
for every
1\leq p<\infty
there is a
c_{p}>0
such that

\|W_{n}\|_{p}\leq c_{p}
for all
n
;

(ii)
the sequence
(t(F,W_{n}):~n\in\mathbb{N}^{*})
is convergent for
every
\mathscr{F}
-decorated graph
F
.

Then there exists a
\mathcal{Z}
-graphon
W
such that

t(F,W_{n})\to t(F,W)
for every
\mathcal{B}
-decorated graph
F
.

Proof.

Let
\mathscr{F}=\{f_{1},f_{2},\dots\}
and
w_{n,m}=\langle f_{m},W_{n}\rangle
. For
each
k\geq 1
, define
h(k)
recursively by
h(1)=1
and
h(k)=q\bigl(h(k-1),k,1/(c_{2}k)\bigr)
, where
q
is the function in Lemma

2.21
. For every
k,n\geq 1
, we construct a partition

\mathcal{P}_{n,k}
of
[0,1]
so that
|\mathcal{P}_{n,k}|=h(k)
,
\mathcal{P}_{n,k+1}

refines
\mathcal{P}_{n,k}
, and the stepfunctions

u_{n,k,m}=(w_{n,m})_{\mathcal{P}_{n,k}}
satisfy

\bigl\|w_{n,m}-u_{n,k,m}\bigr\|_{{}_{\boxtimes}}\leq\frac{1}{k}\|f_{m}\|_{\mathcal{B}}\quad(m=1,\dots,k).

(16)

Let
\mathcal{P}_{n,k}=\{S_{n,k}^{1},\dots,S_{n,k}^{h(k)}\}
. Let

U_{n,k}=(W_{n})_{\mathcal{P}_{n,k}}
; then (
9
) implies that

u_{n,k,m}=\langle f_{m},U_{n,k}\rangle
, and by the contractivity of
the stepping operator,
\|U_{n,k}\|_{p}\leq c_{p}
for all
1\leq p<\infty
. Note that
u_{n,k,m}
is defined for all
n,k
and
m
,
but (
16
) only holds for
m\leq k
.

By selecting a subsequence
\mathbb{N}_{1}
of the indices
n
, we may assume
that for every fixed
k
and
1\leq j\leq h(k)
, the sequence

\lambda(S_{n,k}^{j})
converges to a value
s_{k,j}
, and in fact it
converges fast enough so that
\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{1}}|\lambda(S_{n,k}^{j})-s_{k,j}|<\infty
.

At this point, the issue is that even though the measures of the sets of a given index pair
(k,j)
converge as
n
tends to infinity, the sets themselves, pertaining to regularity partitions of different functions
W_{n}
, have no reason to exhibit any convergent behaviour. This is where the observations and Lemma
3.6
in the previous section become useful: since the homomorphism densities are not influenced by transforming the graphons via applying measure preserving transformations, nor are the various
L^{p}
(condition (i)) or jumble norm (equation
16
) bounds, we could try to find an appropriate measure preserving transformation
\varphi_{n}:[0,1]\to[0,1]
for each
n
that would “unscramble” the sets
S_{n,k}^{j}
and facilitate a natural limit transition for the partitions.

More precisely, there exist measure preserving transformations
\varphi_{n}:[0,1]\to[0,1]
(
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
)
such that for every
k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
there is a measurable
partition
\mathcal{P}_{k}=\{S_{k}^{1},\dots,S_{k}^{h(k)}\}
into intervals for which
\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{1}}\lambda(S_{k}^{j}\triangle\varphi_{n}(S_{n,k}^{j}))<\infty
for every
j
, and also

\mathcal{P}_{k+1}
refines
\mathcal{P}_{k}
.

Indeed, let
g_{n,k}:[0,1]\to\mathbb{N}^{*}
be the measurable function defined via
g_{n,k}(x):=j
whenever
x\in S^{j}_{n,k}
. Then the sequence
(g_{n,k}(x))_{k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}
corresponds to the sequence of indices of the partition classes
x
belongs to. Now let

g_{n}(x):=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{g_{n,k}(x)}{\prod_{m=1}^{k}(h(m)+1)}.

Note that
g_{n}:[0,1]\to[0,1]
is a measurable function, and so there exists a measure preserving transformation
\varphi_{n}
that yields a monotone increasing rearrangement of
g_{n}
(e.g.,
\varphi_{n}(x):=\lambda([g_{n}<g_{n}(x)])+\lambda([g_{n}=g_{n}(x)]\cap[0,x])
). As the denominators of the sum were chosen to grow fast enough, this will correspond to the “lexicographic ordering” of the points of
[0,1]
with regards to the index sequence
(g_{n,k}(\cdot))
. Thus the partitions
\varphi_{n}\circ\mathcal{P}_{n,k}
are interval partitions, and the convergence of the sequences
\left(\lambda(S_{n,k}^{j})\right)_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{1}}
finishes the argument.

Since replacing the graphons
W_{n}
by
W_{n}\circ\varphi_{n}
in the original statement would lead to an equivalent claim, we may without loss of any generality assume that each
\varphi_{n}
is the identity map, and avoid an extra layer of cumbersome notation.

Our next goal is to select a subsequence of the indices
n
so that
the stepfunctions
u_{n,k,m}
converge to some stepfunction with

\mathcal{P}_{k}
-steps almost everywhere. This is not obvious, as the sequence

(u_{n,k,m})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{1}}
may not be uniformly bounded. To prove it, we
have to treat “small” partition classes separately.

Let
J_{k}\subseteq[h(k)]
be the set of indices
j
such that

\lambda(S_{k}^{j})>0
. Let
a_{n,k,m,i,j}
be the value of
u_{n,k,m}

on
S_{k}^{i}\times S_{k}^{j}
(where
i,j\in[h(k)]
). Then we have that

|a_{n,k,m,i,j}|\,\lambda(S_{n,k}^{i})\,\lambda(S_{n,k}^{j})\leq\|u_{n,k,m}\|_{1}=\|\langle f_{m},U_{n,k}\rangle\|_{1}\leq\|f_{m}\|_{\mathcal{B}}\,\|U_{n,k}\|_{1}\leq c_{1}\|f_{m}\|_{\mathcal{B}},

and since
\lambda(S_{n,k}^{i})
and
\lambda(S_{n,k}^{j})
both converge to a non-zero value, the sequence
(a_{n,k,m,i,j})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{1}}
remains
bounded. Hence using the usual diagonal method, we
can select subsequence
\mathbb{N}_{2}\subseteq\mathbb{N}_{1}
so that
(a_{n,k,m,i,j})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{2}}

tends to some value
a_{k,m,i,j}
for all
m
and all
i,j\in J_{k}
.
We define
a_{k,m,i,j}=0
if
i\notin J_{k}
or
j\notin J_{k}
. (Note
that
\lambda(S_{i}\times S_{j})=0
in this case.) These values define a
stepfunction
u_{k,m}:~[0,1]^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
with
\mathcal{P}_{k}
-steps.

It is clear that
u_{n,k,m}(x,y)\to u_{k,m}(x,y)
for
(x,y)\in S_{k}^{i}\times S_{k}^{j}
for
i,j\in J_{k}
, provided
x
is contained in a
finite number of sets
S_{k}^{i}\triangle S_{n,k}^{i}
, and
y
is
contained in a finite number of sets
S_{k}^{j}\triangle S_{n,k}^{j}
.
Since
\sum_{n}\lambda(S_{k}^{j}\triangle S_{n,k}^{j})<\infty
, the
Borel-Cantelli Lemma implies that
u_{n,k,m}\to u_{k,m}
almost
everywhere
(n\in\mathbb{N}_{2},\ n\to\infty)
. Lemma
3.5

implies that for every
k
, the sequence
(u_{k,m})_{m\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}
is the

\mathscr{F}
-moment function sequence of some
\mathcal{Z}
-graphon
U_{k}
, and so

u_{k,m}=\langle f_{m},U_{k}\rangle
and
\|U_{k}\|_{p}\leq c_{p}
for all
1\leq p<\infty
.

Note that we have a finite number of steps and
\lim_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{h(k)}\lambda(S_{k}^{j}\triangle S_{n,k}^{j})=0
.
Also, whenever
i\not\in J_{k}
or

, we have that for each

,

\left\|u_{n,k,m}|_{S_{n,k}^{i}\times S_{n,k}^{j}}\right\|_{p}\to 0.

Indeed, if this were not the case for some

, then since the measures of the sets we restrict to tend to zero, the functions
u_{n,k,m}
would not be uniformly bounded in

for any
q>p
.
Thus, we have that for every
1\leq p<\infty
,
u_{n,k,m}\to u_{k,m}
in

(
n\in\mathbb{N}_{2}
,

).

Now
(u_{n,r,m})_{\mathcal{P}_{n,k}}=u_{n,k,m}
for

, and we have a finite (
|J(k)|
) number of partition class sequences

, each of which satisfy convergence in their measure to that of

, and a finite (

) number of value sequences

(u_{r,m})_{\mathcal{P}_{k}}=u_{k,m}
. So we can apply the Martingale

, and get that

tends to some symmetric function

as

.

Lemma
3.5
implies that the sequence
(u_{m})_{m\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}

is the
\mathscr{F}
-moment function sequence of a
\mathcal{Z}
-graphon
U
such
that
\|U\|_{p}\leq c_{p}
for all
1\leq p<\infty
.

But since the sequence

in

).

Next we show that for the subsequence of indices
n
we selected, we
have
t(F,\varphi,W_{n})\to t(F,\varphi,U)
for every
\mathscr{F}
-decorated simple
graph
(F,\varphi)
. To this end, let
\varepsilon>0
be given, and write

\displaystyle|t(F,\varphi,W_{n})-t(F,\varphi,U)|\leq

\displaystyle|t(F,\varphi,W_{n})-t(F,\varphi,U_{n,k})|+|t(F,\varphi,U_{n,k})-t(F,\varphi,U_{k})|

\displaystyle+|t(F,\varphi,U_{k})-t(F,\varphi,U)|.

We have a

such that every decoration in
(F,\varphi)
occurs
among
\{f_{1},\dots,f_{q}\}
. By the definition of

\bigl\|w_{n,m}-u_{n,k,m}\bigr\|_{{}_{\boxtimes}}\leq\frac{1}{k}\|f_{m}\|_{\mathcal{B}}

for every

and

. This means that

\|\langle f_{m},W_{n}\rangle-\langle f_{m},U_{n,k}\rangle\|_{{}_{\boxtimes}}\leq\frac{1}{k}\|f_{m}\|_{\mathcal{B}},

and by the Counting Lemma

|t(F,\varphi,W_{n})-t(F,\varphi,U_{n,k})|<\varepsilon/3

is large enough
(independently of

Since

in

2.17
implies that

, and
hence
|t(F,\varphi,U_{k})-t(F,\varphi,U)|<\varepsilon/3

is large enough. Let

2.17
, we get that
t(F,\varphi,U_{n,k})\to t(F,\varphi,U_{k})
, and hence
t|(F,\varphi,U_{n,k})-t(F,\varphi,U_{k})|<\varepsilon/3
if

is large enough.

This proves that

for

. Since

.

Finally, we show that the relation

-decorations

3.5
W
-random decorated graphs

Let
W
be a
\mathcal{Z}
-graphon. For every
n\geq 1
, we can generate a

W
-random
\mathcal{Z}
-decorated graph

\mathbb{G}(n,W)
on node set
[n]

as follows: we select
n
independent uniformly distributed points

x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
from
[0,1]
, and label every pair
ij

(1\leq i<j\leq n)
by
W(x_{i},x_{j})
, and label every pair
ii

(1\leq i\leq n)
by
0\in\mathcal{Z}
.

Theorem 3.8
.

For every
\mathcal{Z}
-graphon and every
\mathcal{B}
-decorated graph
\mathbf{F}
,

t(\mathbf{F},\mathbb{G}(n,W))\to t(\mathbf{F},W)\qquad(n\to\infty)

with probability
1
.

As an immediate corollary, we get:

Corollary 3.9
.

Every
\mathcal{Z}
-graphon is the limit of a convergent sequence of

\mathcal{Z}
-decorated graphs.

Proof.

The proof is similar to the proof for the unweighted case in

[
10
]
and in
[
9
]
, Section 11.2.1, but we have to be
somewhat more careful because of the unbounded values that occur.

Let us fix a countable generating set
\mathscr{F}\in\mathcal{B}
, and an

\mathscr{F}
-decorated graph
\mathbf{F}=(F,f)
on the node set
[k]
. It is easy to
compute the expected injective subgraph densities in
\mathbb{G}(n,W)
,
where
n\geq k
. We can generate a random map
[k]\to[0,1]
by
composing a random map
x:~[n]\to[0,1]
with a random injective map

\varphi:~[k]\to[n]
. This gives us that

\displaystyle t(\mathbf{F},W)={\sf E}_{x}{\sf E}_{\varphi}\prod_{ij\in E(F)}\langle f(ij),W(x_{\varphi(i)},x_{\varphi(j)})\rangle={\sf E}_{x}\bigl(t_{\rm inj}(\mathbf{F},\mathbb{G}(n,W))\bigr).

Lemma

2.3
implies that

{\sf E}_{x}t(\mathbf{F},\mathbb{G}(n,W)))\to t(\mathbf{F},W)\quad(n\to\infty).

This shows that
W
-random graphs yield a sequence with the desired
subgraph densities
in expectation
. To show that these values
are concentrated, we need to estimate the fourth moments
(unfortunately, estimating the variance would not be quite enough). Sharper bounds can be obtained based on the theory of U-statistics, but the simpler argument below will be sufficient for us.
For
\varphi:~V(F)\to[n]
, let
X_{\varphi}=t_{\varphi}(\mathbf{F},\mathbb{G}(n,W))-t(\mathbf{F},W)
.
Then
{\sf E}_{x}(X_{\varphi})=0
if
\varphi
is injective. Furthermore,

(t(\mathbf{F},\mathbb{G}(n,W))-t(\mathbf{F},W))^{4}=\frac{1}{n^{4k}}\sum_{\varphi_{1},\dots,\varphi_{4}}X_{\varphi_{1}}\dots X_{\varphi_{4}},

where
\varphi_{1},\dots,\varphi_{4}
range independently over all maps

V(F)\to[n]
.

Let us take expectation in
x
. If the range of any of the
\varphi_{i}
is
disjoint from the others, then the expectation of
X_{\varphi_{i}}
can be
taken separately, and if, in addition,
\varphi_{i}
is injective, then
this expectation is
0
. So only those terms remain in which for
every
i
, the range of
\varphi_{i}
intersects the range of at least one
other
\varphi_{j}
, or
\varphi_{i}
is not injective. This implies that the
range of
\varphi_{1}\cup\dots\cup\varphi_{4}
has at most
4k-2
elements, and
so the number of such terms
O(n^{4k-2})
. The expectation of such a
term is bounded by

2\Pi_{f}\cdot\|W\|_{4|E(F)|}^{4|E(F)|}

, and hence

{\sf E}_{x}\bigl((t(\mathbf{F},\mathbb{G}(n,W))-t(\mathbf{F},W))^{4}\bigr)=O\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right).

This implies that for every fixed
\varepsilon>0
,

\displaystyle\Pr\bigl(|t(\mathbf{F},\mathbb{G}(n,W))

\displaystyle-t(\mathbf{F},W)|>\varepsilon\bigr)=\Pr\bigl((t(\mathbf{F},\mathbb{G}(n,W))-t(\mathbf{F},W))^{4}>\varepsilon^{4}\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{{\sf E}_{x}\bigl((t(\mathbf{F},\mathbb{G}(n,W))-t(\mathbf{F},W))^{4}\bigr)}{\varepsilon^{4}}=O\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right).

Hence

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\Pr\left(|t(\mathbf{F},\mathbb{G}(n,W))-t(\mathbf{F},W)|>\varepsilon\right)<\infty,

and so by the Borel-Cantelli Lemma, with probability
1
,

|t(\mathbf{F},\mathbb{G}(n,W))-t(\mathbf{F},W)|\leq\varepsilon
if
n
is large enough. With
probability
1
, this holds for every
\varepsilon=1,1/2,1/3,\dots

simultaneously, which means that

t(\mathbf{F},\mathbb{G}(n,W))\to t(\mathbf{F},W).

(17)

Since there are only a countable number of
\mathscr{F}
-decorated simple
graphs, (
17
) holds, with probability
1
, for every

\mathscr{F}
-decorated graph
\mathbf{F}
simultaneously. We can use a similar
argument to show that in addition, with probability
1
, for each positive integer
k
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\|W_{n}\|_{k}=\|W\|_{k}
. Since for any
\mathcal{Z}
-graphon
U
and any

we have
\|U\|_{p}\leq\|U\|_{k}+1
, it follows that
\|W_{n}\|_{p}
remains bounded for every
p\geq 1
. In this case,
(
17
) also holds for every
\mathcal{B}
-decorated graph by
Lemma
3.2
, and so we get that, with probability
1
,

\mathbb{G}(n,W)\to W
as
n\to\infty
.
∎

4
Convergence of multigraphs

The main application of the above theory pertains to sequences of
multigraphs where there is no global bound on edge multiplicities,
and we are interested in convergence of node-and-edge homomorphism
numbers.

4.1
Multigraphs as Banach decorated graphs

To capture convergence of multigraphs, we would like to use
probability distributions on
\mathbb{N}
to decorate edges, because this
would allow us to generate a random multiplicity for the edge. We
want finite node-and-edge densities, and therefore we have to require
that these distributions have finite moments. Such distributions
generate the linear space
\mathcal{J}
of signed measures on
\mathbb{N}
having
finite moments. For
\gamma\in\mathcal{J}
, we denote by

\gamma^{(p)}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\gamma(n)n^{p}
its
p
-th moment
(
p\geq 0
). We can also think of signed measures as sequences indexed
by
\mathbb{N}
, and then

\mathcal{J}=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}:~\sum_{n}|x_{n}|n^{p}<\infty\text{ for all }p\geq 0\right\}.

Let
\mathcal{J}_{+}=\{x\in\mathcal{J}:~x_{n}\geq 0\ \forall n\in\mathbb{N}\}
. Note that

\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{N})=\{x\in\mathcal{J}_{+}:~\sum_{n}x_{n}=1\}
consists of those probability
distributions on
\mathbb{N}
having finite moments.

Based on the special case of multigraphs with bounded edge
multiplicity, the space
\mathcal{J}
(more exactly, its subset
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{N})
)
seems to be the right set to define limit graphons of multigraph
sequences. However, there are two technical difficulties with this.
First, there is no good way to turn
\mathcal{J}
into a Banach space with a
pre-dual, which we would need to be able to apply the machinery
developed above. Second, the moments of distributions on
\mathbb{N}
do not
determine the distribution (but they determine subgraph
multiplicities). In other words, we cannot (and should not)
distinguish between two distributions with the same moments.

The first problem will be addressed by introducing a weight function;
the second, by taking the factor space
\widehat{\mathcal{J}}
of
\mathcal{J}
, in which
two sequences are identified if they have the same moments.

Let us fix a weight function
\rho\in\mathcal{J}_{+}
(when we speak of a
weight function, we assume that
\rho(n)>0
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
). Let
the space

\mathcal{C}_{\rho}:=\{f:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}|f(m)\rho(m)\to 0\}

be equipped with the norm
\|f\|^{\rho}:=\|f\cdot\rho\|_{\infty}
. The
dual of this Banach space is

\mathcal{J}_{\rho}:=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}:~\left|~\sum_{m}\frac{|x_{m}|}{\rho(m)}<\infty\right.\right\}

with the norm

\|x\|_{\rho}:=\sum_{m}\frac{|x_{m}|}{\rho(m)}.

We note that
\mathcal{J}_{\rho}\subset\mathcal{J}
.

Since
\rho\in\mathcal{J}_{+}
, we have
(p(0),p(1),...)\in\mathcal{C}_{\rho}
for every
polynomial
p\in R[X]
; with some abuse of notation, we will denote
this sequence by
p
. Let
\mathcal{R}
denote the linear space of all such
sequences, and let
\mathcal{R}_{\rho}:=\overline{\mathcal{R}}^{\|\cdot\|^{\rho}}
. Then

\mathcal{R}_{\rho}
is a closed subspace of
\mathcal{C}_{\rho}
, and so its dual

\widehat{\mathcal{J}}_{\rho}
is a factor space of
\mathcal{J}_{\rho}
. The elements of

\widehat{\mathcal{J}}_{\rho}
are equivalence classes of sequences; two of these
sequences are equivalent if and only if they have the same moments (see, e.g.,
[
4
, Thm. III.10.1]
).

We need a lemma about the existence of weight functions
\rho
. We
call a family
\mathcal{M}
of probability distributions on
\mathbb{N}

moment-bounded
, if for every
p\geq 1
there is a constant
c_{p}>0

such that

\sum_{m\geq 0}\mu(m)m^{p}<c_{p}\qquad\text{for all~}\mu\in\mathcal{M}.

Given a weight function
\rho\in\mathcal{J}_{+}
, we call the family
\mathcal{M}

\rho
-smooth
, if for every
p\geq 1
there is a constant
c^{\prime}_{p}>0

such that

\sum_{m\geq 0}\frac{\mu(m)}{\rho(m)^{p}}<c^{\prime}_{p}\qquad\text{for all~}\mu\in\mathcal{M}.

Lemma 4.1
.

A family
\mathcal{M}
of probability distributions is moment-bounded if and
only if there exists a weight function
\rho\in\mathcal{J}_{+}
, such that

\mathcal{M}
is
\rho
-smooth.

Proof.

The “if” part is easy: Clearly
\rho(m)\leq 1/m
if
m
is large
enough (say
m\geq M
), and hence for any
p\geq 1

m^{p}\leq M^{p}+1/\rho(m)^{p}
. Thus

\sum_{m}\mu(m)m^{p}\leq\sum_{m}\mu(m)M^{p}+\sum_{m}\frac{\mu(m)}{\rho(m)^{p}}\leq M^{p}+c^{\prime}_{p}.

To prove the “only if” direction, note that for every
\mu\in\mathcal{M}
,

\sum_{m=s}^{\infty}m^{p}\mu(m)\leq\frac{1}{s}\sum_{m=s}^{\infty}m^{p+1}\mu(m)\leq\frac{c_{p+1}}{s}.

Hence for
s_{j}=2^{j}c_{j^{2}+1}
, we have

\sum_{m=s_{j}}^{\infty}m^{j^{2}}\mu(m)\leq\frac{1}{2^{j}}.

Let
\rho(m)=m^{-j}
for
s_{j}\leq m<s_{j+1}
(
0\leq j
) and
\rho(m)=1
for
m\leq s_{0}
. Clearly

\rho\in\mathcal{J}_{+}
, and

\displaystyle\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\rho(m)^{-p}\mu(m)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{s_{p}-1}\rho(m)^{-p}\mu(m)+\sum_{j=p}^{\infty}\sum_{m=s_{j}}^{s_{j+1}-1}\rho(m)^{-p}\mu(m)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{m=0}^{s_{p}}m^{p^{2}}\mu(m)+\sum_{j=p}^{\infty}\sum_{m=s_{j}}^{s_{j+1}-1}m^{j^{2}}\mu(m)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}m^{p^{2}}\mu(m)+\sum_{j=p}^{\infty}\sum_{m=s_{j}}^{\infty}m^{j^{2}}\mu(m)

\displaystyle\leq c_{p^{2}}+\sum_{j=p}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2^{j}}\leq c_{p^{2}}+1.

Letting
c^{\prime}_{p}=c_{p^{2}}+1
, the lemma is proved.
∎

Remark 4.2
.

Let
G
be a multigraph on
n
vertices such that its multiedge distribution
\mu
is
\rho
-smooth with constants
c_{p}^{\prime}
. Then with
\mathcal{Z}=\widehat{\mathcal{J}}_{\rho}
equipped with the
\rho
norm, we have that the corresponding
\mathcal{Z}
-graphon
W_{G}
satisfies

\displaystyle\|W_{G}\|_{p}^{p}=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{1\leq i\leq j\leq n}\left(\|\delta_{\left\{g_{ij}\right\}}\|^{\rho}\right)^{p}=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{1\leq i\leq j\leq n}\left(\frac{1}{\rho(g_{ij})}\right)^{p}=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\frac{\mu(\ell)}{\rho(\ell)^{p}}<c_{p}^{\prime},

meaning that the constants in the
\rho
-smoothness translate to the
p
-th power of the constants for the various
L^{p}
-bounds (
1\leq p<\infty
).

In particular a sequence
(G_{n})
of
\rho
-smooth multigraphs will remain uniformly
L^{p}
bounded as
\widehat{\mathcal{J}}_{\rho}
-decorated graphs
(\mathbf{G}_{n})
.

Let
\mathscr{F}:=\{X^{k}:~k\in\mathbb{N}\}\subseteq\mathcal{R}
. By definition, the set
\mathscr{F}

linearly generates
\mathcal{R}
, and hence it is generating in
\mathcal{R}_{\rho}
, which thus is a separable space.
We may turn a loopless multigraph
\mathbf{F}
into an
\mathscr{F}
-decorated graph

\mathbf{F}^{\prime}
by decorating each edge
ij
with multiplicity
m
by
X^{m}
.

To each multigraph
\mathbf{G}=(G,g)
(where
G
is the underlying simple
graph and
g_{uv}
is the multiplicity of the edge
uv
), we can
associate a
\mathcal{J}_{\rho}
-decorated complete graph by decorating
each edge with multiplicity
m
by the probability distribution
concentrated on
m
. Through the factor mapping we obtain a

\widehat{\mathcal{J}}_{\rho}
-decorated complete graph
\mathbf{G}^{\prime\prime}
. (Note that these
measures in
\mathcal{J}_{\rho}
have finite support, and therefore they
are determined by their moments. So the factor mapping distinguishes
the edge-labels in
\mathbf{G}^{\prime\prime}
.) For the node-and-edge homomorphism
density between two multigraphs
\mathbf{G}=(G,g)
and
\mathbf{F}=(F,f)
, we have

t(\mathbf{F},\mathbf{G})=t(\mathbf{F}^{\prime},\mathbf{G}^{\prime\prime})=t(\mathbf{F}^{\prime},W_{\mathbf{G}})
as defined for

\mathscr{F}
-decorated graphs and
\widehat{\mathcal{J}}_{\rho}
-graphons. This can be
expressed in terms of the moments as

\displaystyle t(\mathbf{F},\mathbf{G})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\varphi:V(F)\to V(G)}\prod_{ij\in E(F)}\langle X^{f_{ij}},g_{\varphi(i)\varphi(j)}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{\varphi:V(F)\to V(G)}\prod_{ij\in E(F)}g_{\varphi(i)\varphi(j)}^{(f_{ij})}.

(18)

Now we turn to convergence of multigraph sequences. Recall that a
sequence of
(\mathbf{G}_{n})
of multigraphs is
node-and-edge
convergent
, if
t(\mathbf{F},\mathbf{G}_{n})
is a convergent sequence for every
multigraph
\mathbf{F}
.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
(\mathbf{G}_{n})
be a sequence of multigraphs such that for every
multigraph
\mathbf{F}
, the sequence
t(\mathbf{F},\mathbf{G}_{n})
(in the node-and-edge
sense) is convergent. Then there exists a weight function
\rho\in\mathcal{J}_{+}

and a
\widehat{\mathcal{J}}_{\rho}
-graphon
W
such that
t(\mathbf{F},\mathbf{G}_{n})\to t(\mathbf{F}^{\prime},W)
.

Proof.

Let
\mu_{n}
denote the edge-multiplicity distribution of
\mathbf{G}_{n}
,
i.e.,
\mu_{n}(m)
is the probability that uniformly chosen random
nodes
i
and
j
of
\mathbf{G}_{n}
are connected by
m
edges. (Nodes
i=j

are connected by
0
edges.) Let us observe that every
moment of the probability measures
\mu_{n}
is uniformly bounded
(independently of
n
). Indeed, let
\mathbf{B}_{p}=(K_{2},p)
denote the graph
consisting of two nodes connected by
p
edges (the
p
-bond), then

\sum_{m}\mu_{n}(m)m^{p}=t(\mathbf{B}_{p},\mathbf{G}_{n})\leq c_{p},

since the sequence
(t(\mathbf{B}_{p},\mathbf{G}_{n}):~n\in\mathbb{N})
is convergent and hence
bounded.

Lemma
4.1
implies that there is a weight function

\rho\in\mathcal{J}_{+}
such that the family
\mathcal{M}=\{\mu_{n}\}
is
\rho
-smooth.

In light of Remark
4.2
we may apply
Theorem
3.7
to obtain a
\widehat{\mathcal{J}}_{\rho}
-graphon
W
such that

\lim_{n\to\infty}t(\mathbf{F},\mathbf{G}_{n})=\lim_{n\to\infty}t(\mathbf{F}^{\prime},\mathbf{G}^{\prime\prime}_{n})=t(\mathbf{F}^{\prime},W)

for every multigraph
\mathbf{F}
.
∎

An unpleasant point is that the space
\widehat{\mathcal{J}}_{\rho}
is awkward to
define and work with. One way out would be to ignore that several
distributions may have the same moments, and just select one
distribution as
W(x,y)
out of the equivalence class. We don’t know,
however, whether this can be done in a measurable way.

A better solution is to encode
W(x,y)\in\widehat{\mathcal{J}}_{\rho}
by its moment
sequence. Let us call a sequence
(a_{0},a_{1},\dots)
of real numbers a

\mathbb{N}
-moment sequence
, if there is a probability distribution

\mu
on
\mathbb{N}
such that
a_{p}=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\mu(m)m^{p}
. Define

W_{p}(x,y)=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\pi(m)m^{p},

where
\pi\in W(x,y)
is an arbitrary probability distribution from
the equivalence class. By the definition of equivalence, this is
independent of the choice of
\pi
, and in fact the sequence

(W_{0}(x,y),W_{1}(x,y),\dots)
uniquely determines the equivalence
class. Then for any multigraph
\mathbf{F}=(F,f)
,

t(\mathbf{F}^{\prime},W)=t(\mathbf{F},W_{0},W_{1},\dots):=\int\limits_{[0,1]^{V(F)}}\prod_{ij\in E(F)}W_{f(ij)}.

Thus we get the following corollary to Theorem
4.3
:

Corollary 4.4
.

For every node-end-edge convergent sequence
(\mathbf{G}_{n})
of multigraphs
there is a sequence
(W_{0},W_{1},\dots)
of measurable, symmetric
functions
\mathcal{L}\ni W_{i}:~[0,1]^{2}\to[0,\infty)
such that for every

(x,y)\in[0,1]^{2}
, the sequence
(W_{0}(x,y),W_{1}(x,y),\dots)
is an

\mathbb{N}
-moment sequence, and
t(\mathbf{F},\mathbf{G}_{n})\to t(\mathbf{F},W_{1},W_{2},\dots)
for
every multigraph
\mathbf{F}
.

4.2
W
-random multigraphs

We have seen how to sample from a
\widehat{\mathcal{J}}_{\rho}
-graphon
W
; the result
is a
\widehat{\mathcal{J}}_{\rho}
-decorated graph. However, in the case of simple
graphs, sampling goes one step further, and we get a simple graph
rather than a
[0,1]
-weighted graph. We can make the corresponding
step in the case of multigraphs as well: in this case the limit
objects are
\widehat{\mathcal{J}}_{\rho}
-graphons, and the first sampling, as described
in Section
3.5
, gives a
\widehat{\mathcal{J}}_{\rho}
-decorated graph

\mathbb{G}(n,W)
. For each edge
ij
(
i,j\in[n]
,
i\not=j
), we select a
representative
W^{\prime}(i,j)
from the equivalence class
W(i,j)
, and
generate a multiplicity
m(i,j)
from the distribution
W^{\prime}(i,j)
, to
get a multigraph
\mathbb{G}^{\mathfrak{R}}(n,W)
. Note the unfortunate
indeterminacy in selecting the probability distribution
W^{\prime}(i,j)
;
Corollary
4.6
below remains valid even if an
adversary selects these distributions.

To describe this more exactly, let
\mathbf{G}=(G,g)
be a
\mathcal{J}_{\rho}
-decorated
complete graph. By a
randomization
of
\mathbf{G}
, we mean a (random)
multigraph
\mathbf{G}^{\mathfrak{R}}
, where the multiplicity
G_{uv}
of an edge

uv
is randomly selected from the distribution
g_{uv}
,
independently for different edges. It will be convenient to assume
that
g_{uu}
is concentrated on
0
, so
G_{uu}=0
.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
\mathbf{F}
be a multigraph with
L
edges. Then there is a constant

c=c(\mathbf{F})>0
such that if
\mathbf{G}=(G,g)
is a
\mathcal{J}_{\rho}
-decorated complete
graph with
n
nodes and
\varepsilon>0
, then

|t(\mathbf{F},\mathbf{G})-t(\mathbf{F},\mathbf{G}^{\mathfrak{R}})|\leq\varepsilon
with probability at least

1-c\|g^{(4L)}\|_{1}/(\varepsilon^{4}n^{2})
.

Proof.

The proof is similar in structure to the proof of Theorem

3.8
, but the details are different. Let
\mathbf{F}=(F,f)
,
where
F
is a simple graph with
k
nodes and
l
edges,
f_{ij}
is
the multiplicity of edge
ij
, and
L=\sum_{ij}f_{ij}
is the number
of edges in the multigraph
\mathbf{F}
. Let
\mathbf{G}=(G,g)
, and let
\mathbf{G}
have

n
nodes. Let
g_{u,v}^{(p)}={\sf E}(G_{u,v}^{p})
denote the
p
-th
moment of
G_{u,v}
. This definition implies that for every
p\geq 1
,

{\sf E}(\|\mathbf{G}^{\mathfrak{R}}\|_{p}^{p})=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{u,v\in V(G)}{\sf E}(G_{u,v}^{p})=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{u,v\in V(G)}g_{u,v}^{(p)}=\|g^{(p)}\|_{1}.

(19)

Recall that

t(\mathbf{F},\mathbf{G})=\frac{1}{n^{k}}\sum_{\varphi:V(F)\to V(G)}\hom_{\varphi}(\mathbf{F},\mathbf{G}),

where

\hom_{\varphi}(\mathbf{F},\mathbf{G})=\prod_{ij\in E(F)}g^{(f_{ij})}_{\varphi(i),\varphi(j)}=\prod_{ij\in E(F)}{\sf E}\bigl(G_{\varphi(i),\varphi(j)}^{f_{ij}}\bigr).

Setting
X_{\varphi}=\hom_{\varphi}(\mathbf{F},\mathbf{G}^{\mathfrak{R}})-\hom_{\varphi}(\mathbf{F},\mathbf{G})
and using a
similar expression for
t(\mathbf{F},\mathbf{G}^{\mathfrak{R}})
, we have

t(\mathbf{F},\mathbf{G}^{\mathfrak{R}})-t(\mathbf{F},\mathbf{G})=\frac{1}{n^{k}}\sum_{\varphi:V(F)\to V(G)}X_{\varphi}.

If
\varphi
is injective, then

{\sf E}\bigl(\hom_{\varphi}(\mathbf{F},\mathbf{G}^{\mathfrak{R}})\bigr)=\hom_{\varphi}(\mathbf{F},\mathbf{G})
(the
expectation is taken over the randomization of the edge
multiplicities), and so
{\sf E}(X_{\varphi})=0
. This shows that

t(\mathbf{F},\mathbf{G}^{\mathfrak{R}})
will be close to
t(\mathbf{F},\mathbf{G})
in expectation.

To get that they are close with high probability, we need to estimate
higher moments. We can write

\bigl(t(\mathbf{F},\mathbf{G}^{\mathfrak{R}})-t(\mathbf{F},\mathbf{G})\bigr)^{4}=\frac{1}{n^{4k}}\Bigl(\sum_{\varphi:V(F)\to V(G)}X_{\varphi}\Bigr)^{4}=\frac{1}{n^{4k}}\sum_{\varphi_{1},\dots,\varphi_{4}}X_{\varphi_{1}}\dots X_{\varphi_{4}}.

(20)

Note that
X_{\varphi_{i}}
is independent (as a random variable) from all

X_{\varphi_{j}}
for which the range of
\varphi_{j}
is disjoint from the range
of
\varphi_{i}
. This implies that if we take expectation in
(
20
), only those terms remain where for every
i
,

\varphi_{i}
is non-injective or its range intersects the range of the
other
\varphi_{j}
. Let us also note that if
\varphi
identifies two adjacent
nodes of
F
, then
X_{\varphi}=0
, so to get a non-zero term, all the

\varphi_{i}
must map edges of
F
onto distinct nodes. Call these terms

bad
. It is easy to see that this implies that the ranges of

\varphi_{1},\dots,\varphi_{4}
cover at most
4k-2
nodes of
G
, and so the
number of bad
4
-tuples of maps is bounded by

\binom{n}{4k-2}(4k-2)^{4k}<(4k)^{4k}n^{4k-2}
.

For a particular map
\varphi
, we use the bound

|X_{\varphi}|\leq\hom_{\varphi}(\mathbf{F},\mathbf{G})+\hom_{\varphi}(\mathbf{F},\mathbf{G}^{\mathfrak{R}}),

which gives

\displaystyle|X_{\varphi_{1}}\dots X_{\varphi_{4}}|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{S\subseteq[4]}\prod_{s\in S}\hom_{\varphi_{s}}(\mathbf{F},\mathbf{G})\prod_{s\notin S}\hom_{\varphi_{s}}(\mathbf{F},\mathbf{G}^{\mathfrak{R}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{S\subseteq[4]}\prod_{s\in S}\prod_{ij\in E(F)}g_{\varphi_{s}(i),\varphi_{s}(j)}^{(f_{ij})}\prod_{s\notin S}\prod_{ij\in E(F)}G_{\varphi_{s}(i),\varphi_{s}(j)}^{f_{ij}}.

We think of every summand on the right side as a product of
4L

terms of the type
(g_{\varphi_{s}(i),\varphi_{s}(j)}^{(f_{ij})})^{1/f_{ij}}
and

G_{\varphi(i),\varphi(j)}
, and use the inequality between the geometric
mean and the power mean with exponent
4L
. We get

\displaystyle|X_{\varphi_{1}}\dots X_{\varphi_{4}}|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{S\subseteq[4]}\frac{1}{4L}\Bigl(\sum_{s\in S}\sum_{ij\in E(F)}f_{ij}\bigl(g_{\varphi_{s}(i),\varphi_{s}(j)}^{(f_{ij})}\bigr)^{4L/f_{ij}}+\sum_{s\notin S}\sum_{ij\in E(F)}f_{ij}G_{\varphi_{s}(i),\varphi_{s}(j)}^{4L}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{L}\Bigl(\sum_{s=1}^{4}\sum_{ij\in E(F)}f_{ij}\Bigr(\bigl(g_{\varphi_{s}(i),\varphi_{s}(j)}^{(f_{ij})}\bigr)^{4L/f_{ij}}+G_{\varphi_{s}(i),\varphi_{s}(j)}^{4L}\Bigr).

Using the inequality that
(g_{u,v}^{(a)})^{b}\leq g_{u,v}^{(ab)}
for

a,b\geq 1
, we get

|X_{\varphi_{1}}\dots X_{\varphi_{4}}|\leq\frac{2}{L}\sum_{s=1}^{4}\sum_{ij\in E(F)}f_{ij}\Bigl(g_{\varphi_{s}(i),\varphi_{s}(j)}^{(4L)}+G_{\varphi_{s}(i),\varphi_{s}(j)}^{4L}\Bigr).

Note that there are no terms here with
\varphi_{s}(i)=\varphi_{s}(j)
.
Furthermore, if
\varphi_{1},\dots,\varphi_{4}
gives a bad term, then so does

\pi\circ\varphi_{1},\dots\pi\circ\varphi_{4}
for any permutation of
V(G)
.
If we average this over all permutations
\pi
of
V(G)
, we get
every term
g_{u,v}^{(4L)}
and
G_{u,v}^{4L}
(
u\not=v
) the same
number of times:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\pi}|X_{\pi\circ\varphi_{1}}\dots X_{\pi\circ\varphi_{4}}|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{Ln(n-1)}\sum_{u\not=v\in V(G)}\sum_{ij\in E(F)}f_{ij}\bigl(g_{u,v}^{(4L)}+G_{u,v}^{4L}\bigr)

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{L}\sum_{ij\in E(F)}f_{ij}\bigl(\|g^{(4L)}\|_{1}+\|G\|_{4L}^{4L}\bigr)

\displaystyle=2\|g^{(4L)}\|_{1}+2\|G\|_{4L}^{4L}

(21)

Hence summing over bad terms, we get

\sum_{\text{bad}}|X_{\varphi_{1}}\dots X_{\varphi_{4}}|\leq 2(4k)^{4k}n^{4k-2}\bigl(\|g^{(4L)}\|_{1}+\|G\|_{4L}^{4L}\bigr).

Taking the expectation in (
20
) and using (
19
)
gives

\displaystyle{\sf E}\bigl((t(\mathbf{F},\mathbf{G}^{\mathfrak{R}})-

\displaystyle t(\mathbf{F},\mathbf{G}))^{4}\bigr)=\frac{1}{n^{4k}}\sum_{\varphi_{1},\dots,\varphi_{4}~\text{bad}}{\sf E}(X_{\varphi_{1}}\dots X_{\varphi_{4}})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2(4k)^{4k}}{n^{2}}\bigl(\|g^{(4L)}\|_{1}+{\sf E}(\|G\|_{4L}^{4L})\bigr)=\frac{4(4k)^{4k}}{n^{2}}\|g^{(4L)}\|_{1}.

Hence

\displaystyle\Pr(|t(\mathbf{F},\mathbf{G}^{\mathfrak{R}})-t(\mathbf{F},\mathbf{G})|>\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\Pr((t(\mathbf{F},\mathbf{G}^{\mathfrak{R}})-t(\mathbf{F},\mathbf{G}))^{4}>\varepsilon^{4})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{{\sf E}((t(\mathbf{F},\mathbf{G}^{\mathfrak{R}})-t(\mathbf{F},\mathbf{G}))^{4})}{\varepsilon^{4}}\leq\frac{4(4k)^{4k}}{n^{2}\varepsilon^{4}}\|g^{(4L)}\|_{1},

which proves the lemma.
∎

Combining this lemma with Theorem
3.8
, we get that
every
\widehat{\mathcal{J}}_{\rho}
-decorated graphon is the limit of multigraphs:

Corollary 4.6
.

For every
\widehat{\mathcal{J}}_{\rho}
-graphon
W
and every multigraph
\mathbf{F}
,

t(\mathbf{F},\mathbb{G}^{\mathfrak{R}}(n,W))\to t(\mathbf{F},W)\qquad(n\to\infty)

with probability
1
.

4.3
Node-convergence vs. node-and-edge convergence

When looking at sequences of multigraphs, several convergence notions
are available. From the combinatorial standpoint, we have convergence
of node-and-edge homomorphism densities and convergence of node
homomorphism densities. From a more abstract point of view, we have
convergence in the compactification sense
[
11
,
7
,
9
]
,
i.e., we take the one-point compactification
\overline{\mathbb{N}}
of
\mathbb{N}
,
and consider multigraphs as graphs decorated with measures from

\mathcal{R}(\overline{\mathbb{N}})
. Alternatively, we have convergence in the weighted
Banach space sense introduced in the previous section. As we have
seen, the latter convergence notion corresponds to the combinatorial
node-and-edge convergence. On the other hand, compactification
convergence corresponds to convergence in the node-homomorphism
sense. To be precise, let
\mathbf{G}^{(t)}
denote the multigraph obtained
from the multigraph
\mathbf{G}
by truncating its edge-multiplicities at

t
. The following proposition characterizes node-convergence

[
11
,
7
,
9
]
:

Proposition 4.7
.

For a sequence of multigraphs
(\mathbf{G}_{1},\mathbf{G}_{2},\dots)
, the following
are equivalent:

(i)
The sequence is convergent in terms of node-homomorphism
densities.

(ii)
The sequence is convergent as
\overline{\mathbb{N}}
-labeled
graphs.

(iii)
For every
t\in\mathbb{N}
, the truncated sequence

(\mathbf{G}_{1}^{(t)},\mathbf{G}_{1}^{(t)},\dots)
is convergent as a sequence of
multigraphs with bounded edge-multiplicities.

Indeed, convergence in the truncated multiplicity sense corresponds to
decorating our test graphs with the truncated polynomials

q_{k}(n):=\min\{n^{k},k^{k}\}
. The linear span of the truncated
polynomials is the same as the space generated by the characteristic
functions
f_{k}:={\mathbbm{1}}_{\{k\}}
for all
k
, together with the constant
1 function (which is actually
q_{0}
). These in turn generate

C(\overline{\mathbb{N}})
, and thus the three convergence notions are
equivalent.

In the case of graph sequences with uniformly bounded edge
multiplicities, convergence in the node-and-edge homomorphism sense
is also equivalent. How are the two types of convergence related
in the general case? We shall show that these notions are not
equivalent in general, but under appropriate conditions they are
closely related.

We start with two examples.

Example 4.8
.

Let
\mathbf{G}_{n}
be the multigraph on
[n]
with
c_{n}n^{2}
edges but with

o(n^{2})
distinct edges, where
(c_{n})
is an arbitrary bounded
sequence. This graph sequence is convergent in the node-sense. The
truncated graph
\mathbf{G}^{(t)}_{n}
has
o(n^{2})
edges, and hence it tends
to the identically zero graphon. However, the edge densities

t(K_{2},\mathbf{G}_{n})=c_{n}
do not form a convergent sequence in general, so
this sequence is not convergent in the node-and-edge sense.

Example 4.9
.

Let
\sigma
and
\tau
be two different probability distributions on
\mathbb{N}
with
finite moments and having the same moments (such distributions exist,
see, e.g.,
[
12
]
1
1

1

The explicit example given there is as follows: given a fixed integer
q\geq 2
, let
\sigma(\{a\})=e^{-q}q^{n}/n!
and

\tau(\{a\})=e^{-q}q^{n}\left(\frac{1}{n!}+\frac{(-1)^{n}}{\prod_{m=1}^{n}(q^{m}-1)}\right)

whenever
a=q^{n}
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, and let all other natural numbers have zero measure.

)
We consider
\sigma
and
\tau
as elements of

\mathcal{J}_{\rho}
for some appropriate weight function
\rho
.
Let
[\sigma]=[\tau]=:\mu\in\widehat{\mathcal{J}}_{\rho}
denote their equivalence class in the factor space.
Let
U(x,y)\equiv\sigma
,
W(x,y)\equiv\tau
, and
V(x,y)\equiv\mu
. Then
V
is a
\widehat{\mathcal{J}}_{\rho}
-graphon.
Next, we wish to apply Corollary
4.6
to the
\widehat{\mathcal{J}}_{\rho}
-graphon
V
. But as noted before, we may chose which probability distributions from the given equivalence class we wish to use to generate our random multigraphs.
Therefore we shall generate one family with the help of
U
, and one family with the help of
W
.
Consider the random multigraphs

\mathbf{G}_{n}=\mathbb{G}^{\mathfrak{R}}(n,[U])
and
\mathbf{H}_{n}=\mathbb{G}^{\mathfrak{R}}(n,[W])
. We know by Corollary
4.6
that
t(\mathbf{F},\mathbf{G}_{n})\to t(\mathbf{F},V)
and
t(\mathbf{F},\mathbf{H}_{n})\to t(\mathbf{F},V)
almost
surely.
This means that merging the two
sequences, we get a sequence

(\mathbf{G}_{1},\mathbf{H}_{1},\mathbf{G}_{2},\mathbf{H}_{2},\mathbf{G}_{3},\mathbf{H}_{3},\dots)
that is almost surely
convergent in the node-and-edge sense.

On the other hand, there is a
j\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\sigma_{j}\not=\tau_{j}
, and then the truncated distributions

\sigma^{(j+1)}_{i}=\begin{cases}\sigma_{i},&\text{if $i\leq j$},\\
\displaystyle\sum_{i>j}\sigma_{i},&\text{if $i\leq j+1$},\\
0,&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\tau^{(j+1)}_{i}=\begin{cases}\tau_{i},&\text{if $i\leq j$},\\
\displaystyle\sum_{i>j}\tau_{i},&\text{if $i\leq j+1$},\\
0,&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

are different. The truncated graphs
\mathbf{G}_{n}^{(j+1)}
can be considered
as random graphs
\mathbb{G}^{\mathfrak{R}}(n,[U^{\prime}])
(where
U^{\prime}(x,y)\equiv\sigma^{(j+1)}
),
and hence they converge to
[U^{\prime}]
almost surely, and similarly

\mathbf{H}_{n}^{(j+1)}\to[W^{\prime}]
almost surely (where
W^{\prime}(x,y)\equiv\tau^{(j+1)}
). But
\sigma^{(j+1)}
and
\tau^{(j+1)}
are different
distributions with a finite support, and hence their moments are not
the same; this means that
t(\mathbf{B}_{p},[U^{\prime}])\not=t(\mathbf{B}_{p},[W^{\prime}])
for an
appropriate
p\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, and hence
t(\mathbf{B}_{p},\mathbf{G}_{n}^{(j+1)})
and

t(\mathbf{B}_{p},\mathbf{H}_{n}^{(j+1)})
have different limits. So the truncated
sequence

(\mathbf{G}^{(j+1)}_{1},\mathbf{H}^{(j+1)}_{1},\mathbf{G}^{(j+1)}_{2},\mathbf{H}^{(j+1)}_{2},\mathbf{G}^{(j+1)}_{3},\mathbf{H}^{(j+1)}_{3},\dots)

is not convergent, which implies that

(\mathbf{G}_{1},\mathbf{H}_{1},\mathbf{G}_{2},\mathbf{H}_{2},\mathbf{G}_{3},\mathbf{H}_{3},\dots)
is almost surely not
convergent in the compactification sense.

Proposition 4.10
.

Suppose the multigraph sequence
\mathbf{G}_{n}
is convergent in the node
sense. If the numbers
t(\mathbf{B}_{p},\mathbf{G}_{n})
are bounded for each
p\in\mathbb{N}
,
then the graph sequence is also convergent in the node-and-edge
sense.

Proof.

By Lemma
4.1
, we can choose a weight function

\rho\in\mathcal{J}_{+}
such that the family of edge multiplicity
distributions is
\rho
-smooth. All polynomials and all truncated
polynomials lie in
C(\mathbb{N},\rho)
. For any polynomial
P
we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}P(n)\rho(n)=0
, and hence they all lie in the
closed linear span of the truncated polynomials. Hence the
compactification limit is also a limit in the multigraph sense.
∎

4.4
Node-and-edge vs. sample convergence and exchangeability

For simple graphs and bounded,
[0,1]
-valued graphons, it is well known that homomorphism density convergence is equivalent to sample convergence (cf.
[
9
, Section 5.2.4]
). In our formalism, following Example
2.4
, this means that given a sequence
W_{n}:[0,1]^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
of symmetric measurable functions with
W_{n}(x,y)\in\left\{(a,b)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\left|a,b\geq 0,a+b=1\right.\right\}
, we have that
(t(F,W_{n}))_{n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}
converges for every simple graph
F
if and only if the distribution of
\mathbb{G}^{\mathfrak{R}}(k,W_{n})
over the set of simple graphs on
k
vertices converges in the weak-* (or vague) topology – here equivalent to weak convergence of measures – as
n\to\infty
for every
k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
.

In more generality, Lovász and Szegedy showed (
[
11
, Theorem 2.3]
) the same equivalence in the setting of compact decorations, i.e., when
\mathcal{B}=C(\mathcal{K})
for some compact separable topological space
\mathcal{K}
(see Section
2.3
). It is thus a natural question to ask what happens in the case of mulitgraphs with unbounded edge multiplicities.

Convergence of the samples would provide a connection to exchangeable arrays and Aldous’ representation theorem for these.

The first non-trivial hurdle is what topology to choose for the distributions. As
\mathbb{G}^{\mathfrak{R}}(k,W_{n})
now lives on a locally compact, rather than a compact space, weak convergence (from the weak duality with
C_{b}(X)
) and weak-* convergence (from the Riesz-Markov duality
(C_{0}(X)*=M(X)
). The second is that the former convergence notion would preserve total measure (i.e. a sequence of probability distributions would have a limit that’s always a probability distribution), but compactness is lost, whereas the latter allows one to make use of weak-* compactness, but a sequence of probability distributions may actually end up with a zero limit.

Thirdly, no matter which we choose, even if we assume that the sequences
\mathbb{G}^{\mathfrak{R}}(k,W_{n})
have probability distributions as their limits, the density functions
t(\mathbf{F},\cdot)
are not bounded (except for zero-decorations), and even less in
C_{0}
, convergence of distributions does not immediately imply convergence in densities.

Issues are also present for the reverse direction, in that unboundedness naturally leads into moment indeterminacy problems. In particular the counter-example from
[
8
, Section 7.3]
can be adapted to this setting as well.

Example 4.11
.

Let
\sigma
and
\tau
be two different probability distributions on
\mathbb{N}
with
finite moments and having the same moments (as in Example
4.9
). Denote their n-th moments by
M_{n}

(n\geq 0)
.

Let further
\{S_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
and
\{T_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
be two partitions of
[0,1]
into measurable sets such that
\lambda(S_{i})=\sigma(\{i\})
and
\lambda(T_{j})=\tau(\{j\})
for all
i,j\in\mathbb{N}
. Consider the functions
g_{\sigma},g_{\tau}:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
defined by

\displaystyle g_{\sigma}(x):=n_{x}

whenever

\displaystyle x\in S_{n_{x}},

\displaystyle g_{\tau}(x):=m_{x}

whenever

\displaystyle x\in T_{m_{x}},

respectively, and let
W_{\sigma},W_{\tau}:[0,1]^{2}\rightarrow\widehat{\mathcal{J}}_{\rho}
be defined by
W_{\sigma}(x,y):=\delta_{\{g_{\sigma}(x)\cdot g_{\sigma}(y)\}}
and
W_{\tau}(x,y)=\delta_{\{g_{\tau}(x)\cdot g_{\tau}(y)\}}
, respectively, where
\rho\in\mathcal{J}_{+}
is chosen so that
W_{\sigma},W_{\tau}
both have finite
\|\cdot\|_{p}
-norms for all
1\leq p<\infty
(such a
\rho
can be guaranteed with arguments similar to the ones used in the proof of Lemma
4.1
).

Let
\mathbf{F}
be a multigraph, let the elements of
V(F)
be denoted by
v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{k}
, let
f_{ij}
denote the multiplicity of the edge
v_{i}v_{j}\in E(F)
, and let
d_{i}
denote the degree of vertex
v_{i}
(with multiplicities).
It can then easily be seen that we have

\displaystyle t(\mathbf{F},W_{\sigma})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\limits_{x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}\in[0,1]}\prod_{v_{i}v_{j}\in E(F)}(g_{\sigma}(x_{i})\cdot g_{\sigma}(x_{j}))^{f_{ij}}dx_{1}\ldots x_{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{k}\int_{[0,1]}g_{\sigma}(x_{i})^{d_{i}}dx_{i}=\prod_{i=1}^{k}M_{d_{i}}.

Similar calculations yield
t(\mathbf{F},W_{\sigma})=\prod_{i=1}^{k}M_{d_{i}}
, and so the two graphons have the exact same node-and-edge homomorphism densities. Yet,
\mathbb{G}^{\mathfrak{R}}(2,W_{\sigma})
is the random multiedge where the multiplicity distribution is the distribution of
g_{\sigma}(x)g_{\sigma}(y)
, whilst
\mathbb{G}^{\mathfrak{R}}(2,W_{\tau})
is the random multiedge where the multiplicity distribution is the distribution of
g_{\tau}(x)g_{\tau}(y)
, and these two are not equal.

To turn this into an actual counter-example with respect to the convergence notions at hand, we shall generate a sequence of multigraphs from each of the graphons. Let us first consider
W_{\sigma}
, the other sequence can be obtained in an analoguous way. For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
let
\sigma_{n}
be the probability distribution obtained from
\sigma
by cutting off it’s tail above
n
and setting it to zero, i.e.,
\sigma|_{\mathscr{P}(\{1,\ldots,n\})}=\sigma_{n}|_{\mathscr{P}(\{1,\ldots,n\})}
,
\sigma_{n}(\{n+1,n+2,\ldots\})=0
, and
\sigma_{n}(\{0\})=\sigma(\{0\}\cup\{n+1,n+2,\ldots\})
. Next, let the probability distribution
\widehat{\sigma}_{n}
be obtained from
\sigma_{n}
by choosing some large enough
k_{n}\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, and letting
\widehat{\sigma}_{n}(\{m\}):=\lfloor k_{n}\sigma_{n}(\{m\})\rfloor/k_{n}
for all
m\geq 1
in such a way that
\|\widehat{\sigma}_{n}-\sigma_{n}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}<1/n
. Finally, letting
W_{\sigma,n}
be the graphon obtained from
\widehat{\sigma}_{n}
the same way
W_{\sigma}
was constructed from
\sigma
, the graphon
W_{\sigma,n}
will be a stepfunction that represents a multigraph
G_{\sigma,n}
on
k_{n}
vertices. By construction, the total variational distance of
\widehat{\sigma}_{n}
and
\sigma
tends to zero as
n\to\infty
, meaning that
\mathbb{G}^{\mathfrak{R}}(k,W_{\sigma,n})
will tend in total variation distance to
\mathbb{G}^{\mathfrak{R}}(k,W_{\sigma})
. Note that convergence also holds in
L^{2}
, and so a fortiori in jumble norm as well. On the other hand,
\|W_{\sigma}-W_{\sigma,n}\|_{2}
will also tend to zero, and by Corollary
2.15
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}t(\mathbf{F},W_{\sigma,n})=t(\mathbf{F},W_{\sigma})
. In other words, the multigraph sequences
(G_{\sigma,n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}
and
(G_{\tau,n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}
have the same limit with respect to node-and-edge densities, but have two different limits with respect to convergence of samples or convergence in jumble norm.

We do expect to have settings (as in the previous section) where these convergence notions coincide, but it is beyond the scope of the present paper to go investigate the details.

5
Concluding remarks

5.1
.

An almost identical construction as in Section
4

can be used to define and study convergent sequences of edge-weighted
graphs with no universal bound on the weights. In this case we use as
a weight function an appropriate function
\rho:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
,
and we replace the set of natural numbers used in the previous
example by the set of nonnegative reals, summation by integral etc.
Caution: one has to distinguish more carefully functions and signed
measures (which were interchangeable in the discrete case above). We
don’t go into the details of this.

5.2
.

Using a generalized Hölder Inequality, Borgs, Chayes, Cohn and Zhao

[
1
]
proved the following inequality stronger than Lemma

2.3
and inequality (
4
):

t(F,w)\leq\prod_{ij\in E(F)}\|w_{ij}\|_{\Delta(F)},

where
\Delta(F)
is the maximum degree in
F
. We could improve
several of our bounds using similar methods. This has not been our
goal in this paper, but it remains an interesting open problem to
extend our results in this direction.

5.3
.

The theory of simple graph limits is closely related to the
characterization of homomorphism functions. Such characterizations
are known, among others, for simple graph parameters of the form

\hom(.,G)
(where
G
is a simple graph with loops, or an
edge-weighted graph, or a node-and-edge-weighted graph), and also for
parameters of the form
t(.,W)
, where
W
is (bounded) graphon.
Extending these characterizations to the Banach space decorated, or
compact decorated, case seems to be a challenging problem.
