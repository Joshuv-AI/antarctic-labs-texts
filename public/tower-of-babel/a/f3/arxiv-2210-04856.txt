A Posteriori Error Estimate and Adaptivity for QM/MM Models of Crystalline Defects
Yangshuai Wang, James R. Kermode, Christoph Ortner, Lei Zhang

Abstract
. Hybrid quantum/molecular mechanics (QM/MM) models play a pivotal role in molecular simulations. These models provide a balance between accuracy, surpassing pure MM models, and computational efficiency, offering advantages over pure QM models. Adaptive approaches have been developed to further improve this balance by allowing on-the-fly selection of the QM and MM subsystems as necessary. We propose a novel and robust adaptive QM/MM method for practical material defect simulations. To ensure mathematical consistency with the QM reference model, we employ machine-learning interatomic potentials (MLIPs) as the MM models [ 13 , 31 ] . Our adaptive QM/MM method utilizes a residual-based error estimator that provides both upper and lower bounds for the approximation error, thus indicating its reliability and efficiency. Furthermore, we introduce a novel adaptive algorithm capable of anisotropically updating the QM/MM partitions. This update is based on the proposed residual-based error estimator and involves solving a free interface motion problem, which is efficiently achieved using the fast marching method. We demonstrate the robustness of our approach via numerical tests on a wide range of crystalline defects.



1.
Introduction

Quantum mechanics and molecular mechanics (QM/MM) coupling methods have emerged as indispensable tools for simulating large molecular systems in both materials science and biology
[
4
,
16
,
28
,
35
,
39
,
56
]
. The essence of this methodology lies in the partition of the computational domain into QM and MM regions, where the region of primary interest is described by a QM model embedded in an ambient environment represented by a MM model. The goal of QM/MM coupling methods is to achieve (near-)QM accuracy at (near-)MM computational cost, to make quantitative large-scale atomistic simulations viable.

An important question in the field of QM/MM coupling methods concerns the optimal assignment of atoms to either QM or MM subsystems in order to balance accuracy and computational cost. To address this issue, adaptive QM/MM methods have been developed by providing an on-the-fly partition of QM/MM subsystems based on error estimators during simulations.

Adaptive QM/MM methods have been proposed for various applications such as the analysis of molecular fragments in macromolecules, monitoring of molecules entering/leaving binding sites and tracking proton transfer through the Grotthuss mechanism (as discussed in
[
20
]
and references therein). However, the majority of adaptive QM/MM methods developed thus far are for solute-solvent systems and rely on heuristic criteria. Further information can be found in
[
5
,
30
,
33
,
34
,
50
,
52
,
55
]
. In the context of materials featuring defects, adaptive computations are often guided by the distance to these defects
[
16
,
35
]
. Similar concepts can also be observed in the quasi-continuum methods for density functional theory
[
29
,
44
]
.

Adaptive QM/MM methods normally rely on empirical error estimators.
By contrast, Chen et al.
[
7
]
first introduced mathematically rigorous
a posteriori
error estimators for the QM/MM model residual inspired by classical adaptive finite element methods
[
18
,
49
]
. Their approach employed a weighted
\ell^{2}
-norm on the
residual
forces, providing an upper bound for the approximation error. However, it fell short in providing a lower bound, which is essential for ensuring efficiency of adaptive schemes. To overcome this limitation, Wang et al.
[
51
]
proposed a reliable and efficient
a posteriori
error estimator by connecting the natural dual norm of the residual with solving an auxiliary Poisson equation. They developed an inner-outer adaptive strategy with an outer adaptive algorithm for selecting QM and MM regions and an inner algorithm for computing the estimators with the desired accuracy. However, the inner algorithm necessitated a finite element mesh, which introduced additional errors requiring careful handling, not to mention additional algorithmic complexity. More importantly, this work was primarily centered on energy-mixing schemes for simple point defects and the adaptive algorithm only adjusts the radius of QM and MM sub-regions, limiting the ability to capture significant anisotropy in the defect core, elastic field, or defect nucleation observed in practical material simulations.

The purpose of the present work is to develop a more practical adaptive QM/MM method for material defect simulations, while maintaining the rigourous approach of
[
7
,
51
]
. To ensure
consistency
of the QM/MM scheme and improve computational efficiency, we employ state-of-art machine-learning interatomic potentials (MLIPs) as the MM models
[
13
,
31
]
.
Next, we propose a practical and flexible approach to obtain the error estimator, essentially replacing the PDE operator from
[
51
]
with a generalization of the graph-Laplacian
[
42
]
. Algorithmically, this approach fits much better into the setting of atomistic modeling. A practical error estimator is further developed by (i) truncating to a finite computational domain and (ii) facilitating the QM force constant to give a linear approximation of the residual force in the MM region.
Moreover, to evolve the QM/MM partitions anisotropically rather than only adjusting the radius, a free interface motion problem (i.e., Eikonal equation
[
57
]
) is formulated and solved using the fast marching method
[
14
,
57
]
, where the
practical
error estimator is regarded as the extending speed. We develop a novel strategy to assign atoms to QM or MM subsystems based on the solution of the corresponding Eikonal equation.

We test our algorithm by performing adaptive computations for three common defect types: edge dislocations, in-plane cracks, and di-interstitials. Our findings reveal that the practical error estimator we introduce attains convergence rates comparable to those of the approximation error, offering substantial computational cost reductions when employing a realistic electronic structure model. The adaptive algorithm showcases robustness by eliminating the need for user
a priori
input, thereby aligning with our objective of achieving a fully adaptive QM/MM scheme. The analysis and adaptive algorithm presented in this paper demonstrates a considerable degree of independence from the underlying approximation scheme, thereby rendering the proposed framework widely applicable to various coarse-graining or multiscale methods. As a proof of concept, we will focus solely on geometry equilibration problems (statics).

2.
QM/MM Coupling for Crystalline Defects

2.1.
Variational model for crystalline defects

A rigorous framework for modelling the geometric equilibration of crystalline defects has been developed in
[
9
,
21
]
. These works formulate the equilibration of a single crystalline defect as a variational problem in a discrete energy space and establish qualitatively sharp far-field decay estimates for the corresponding equilibrium configuration. This analytical foundation will serve as the basis for our
a posteriori
error estimates and the corresponding adaptive algorithm.

2.1.1.
Displacement space

Given
d\in\{2,3\}
, a homogeneous crystal reference configuration is given by the Bravais lattice

\Lambda^{\rm h}=\mathsf{A}\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, for some non-singular matrix
\mathsf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
. A reference lattice with a single defect is denoted by
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
. For the sake of simplicity we admit only single-species Bravais lattices. There are no conceptual obstacles to generalising this work to multi-lattices
[
40
]
, however, the notational and technical details become more involved.

A deformed configuration of the infinite lattice
\Lambda
is a map
y:\Lambda\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We can decompose the configuration
y
into

(2.1)

\displaystyle y(\ell)=\ell+u_{0}(\ell)+u(\ell)=y_{0}(\ell)+u(\ell),

where
u_{0}:\Lambda\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a
far-field predictor
solving a continuum linearised elasticity (CLE) equation
[
21
]
enforcing the presence of defect and
u:\Lambda\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a
core corrector
. For point defects we simply take
u_{0}(\ell)=0~\forall~\ell\in\Lambda
. The derivation of
u_{0}
for straight dislocations and cracks are reviewed in the Appendix
B
.

The set of admissible atomic configurations is

\displaystyle{\rm Adm}_{0}(\Lambda)

\displaystyle:=\bigcup_{\mathfrak{m}>0}{\rm Adm}_{\mathfrak{m}}(\Lambda)\qquad\text{with}

\displaystyle{\rm Adm}_{\mathfrak{m}}(\Lambda)

\displaystyle:=\left\{y:\Lambda\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d},~|y(\ell)-y(m)|>\mathfrak{m}|\ell-m|\quad\forall~\ell,m\in\Lambda\right\},

where the parameter
\mathfrak{m}>0
prevents the accumulation of atoms.

For
\ell\in\Lambda
and
\rho\in\Lambda-\ell
, we define the finite difference

D_{\rho}u(\ell):=u(\ell+\rho)-u(\ell)
. For a subset
\mathcal{R}\subset\Lambda-\ell
, we
define
D_{\mathcal{R}}u(\ell):=(D_{\rho}u(\ell))_{\rho\in\mathcal{R}}
, and we consider
Du(\ell):=D_{\Lambda-\ell}u(\ell)
to be a finite-difference stencil with infinite range.
For a stencil
Du(\ell)
, we define the stencil norms

(2.2)

\displaystyle\big|Du(\ell)\big|_{\mathcal{N}}:=\bigg(\sum_{\rho\in\mathcal{N}(\ell)-\ell}\big|D_{\rho}u(\ell)\big|^{2}\bigg)^{1/2}\quad{\rm and}\quad\|Du\|_{\ell^{2}_{\mathcal{N}}}:=\bigg(\sum_{\ell\in\Lambda}|Du(\ell)|_{\mathcal{N}}^{2}\bigg)^{1/2},

where
\mathcal{N}(\ell)
is the set containing nearest neighbours of site
\ell
,

(2.3)

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}(\ell):=

\displaystyle\left\{\,m\in\Lambda\setminus\ell\,~\Big|~\,\exists\,a\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\text{ s.t. }|a-\ell|=|a-m|\leq|a-k|\quad\forall\,k\in\Lambda\,\right\}.\,\,

We can then define the corresponding functional space of finite-energy displacements

(2.4)

\displaystyle{\mathscr{U}}^{1,2}(\Lambda):=\big\{u:\Lambda\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}~\big\lvert~\|Du\|_{\ell^{2}_{\mathcal{N}}(\Lambda)}<\infty\big\}

with the associated semi-norm
\|u\|_{{\mathscr{U}}^{1,2}}:=\|Du\|_{\ell^{2}_{\mathcal{N}}}
.
Then the associated class of admissible displacements is given by

\displaystyle\mathscr{A}(\Lambda):=\big\{u\in{\mathscr{U}}^{1,2}(\Lambda)~:~y_{0}+u\in{\rm Adm}_{0}(\Lambda)\big\}.

2.1.2.
The QM site potential

We consider the site potential to be a collection of mappings
V_{\ell}:(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\Lambda-\ell}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, which represent the energy distributed to each atomic site.
For technical reasons we make the following assumptions on the regularity and locality of the site potentials, which has been justified for some basic quantum mechanic models
[
8
,
10
,
12
,
41
]
, but we emphasize that it is not a universally valid assumption.

(RL)

Regularity and locality:

For all
\ell\in\Lambda
,
V_{\ell}\big(Du(\ell)\big)
possesses partial derivatives up to the third order. For
j=1,2,3
, there exist constants
C_{j}
and
\eta_{j}
such that

(2.5)

\displaystyle\big|V_{\ell,{\bm{\rho}}}\big(Du(\ell)\big)\big|\leq C_{j}\exp\Big(-\eta_{j}\sum^{j}_{l=1}|{\bm{\rho}}_{l}|\Big)

for all
\ell\in\Lambda
and
{\bm{\rho}}\in(\Lambda-\ell)^{j}
.

We refer to
[
9
, §2.3 and §4]
for a justification and discussion of this assumption.

If the reference configuration
\Lambda
is a homogeneous lattice,
\Lambda=\Lambda^{\rm h}
, then the site potential does not depend on site
\ell\in\Lambda^{\rm h}
due to the translation invariance. In this case,
we will denote the site potential by
V^{\rm h}:(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\Lambda^{\rm h}\setminus 0}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
.
for the homogeneous lattice. Although the site potentials are defined on infinite stencils
(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\Lambda-\ell}
, the setting also applies to finite systems or to finite range interactions. In particular, we denote by
V_{\ell}^{\Omega}
the site potential of a finite system with the reference configuration lying in
\Lambda\cap\Omega
.

2.1.3.
Equilibration of crystalline defects

Let the site potential
V_{\ell}
satisfy the assumptions
(RL)
. The energy-difference functional is then given by

(2.6)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(u):=\sum_{\ell\in\Lambda}\Big(V_{\ell}\big(Du_{0}(\ell)+Du(\ell)\big)-V_{\ell}\big(Du_{0}(\ell)\big)\Big).

It was shown in
[
9
, Theorem 2.1]
that after an elementary renormalisation, (
2.6
) is well-defined on the admissible displacements set
\mathscr{A}(\Lambda)
, and that it is
(\mathfrak{n}-1)
-times continuously differentiable with respect to the
\|\cdot\|_{{\mathscr{U}}^{1,2}}
norm.

Instead of the energy minimization problem
[
13
,
51
]
, we will focus on the (formally equivalent) force equilibrium formulation, that is,

(2.7)

\displaystyle{\rm Find}~\bar{u}\in\mathscr{A}(\Lambda),~~{\rm s.t.}\quad\mathcal{F}_{\ell}(\bar{u})=0,\qquad\forall~\ell\in\Lambda,

where
\mathcal{F}_{\ell}:=-\nabla_{\ell}\mathcal{E}(u)
represents the force on the atomic site
\ell
. For
u^{\rm h}\in\mathscr{A}(\Lambda^{\rm h})
on a homogeneous lattice
\Lambda^{\rm h}
, the force on each atomic site
\ell
satisfies
\mathcal{F}_{\ell}(u^{\rm h})=\mathcal{F}^{\rm h}\big(u^{\rm h}(\cdot-\ell)\big)
with some homogeneous force
\mathcal{F}^{\rm h}
that does not depend on site
\ell
.

2.2.
QM/MM Coupling

In this section, we describe the QM/MM models utilized in our study. Our approach is inspired by
[
11
,
13
]
, tailored to better align with the specific context of the present work.

2.2.1.
Domain decomposition

We first decompose the reference configuration
\Lambda
into three disjoint sets,

\Lambda=\Lambda^{\rm QM}\cup\Lambda^{\rm MM}\cup\Lambda^{\rm FF}
, where
\Lambda^{\rm QM}
and
\Lambda^{\rm MM}
denote the QM and MM regions, respectively, and
\Lambda^{\rm FF}
represents the far-field region where atom positions are frozen. This yields the approximated admissible set

(2.8)

\displaystyle\mathscr{A}^{\rm H}(\Lambda):=\big\{u\in\mathscr{U}^{1,2}(\Lambda)~:~y_{0}+u\in{\rm Adm}_{0}(\Lambda)~~{\rm and}~~u=0~{\rm in}~\Lambda^{\rm FF}\big\}.

Moreover, we define a buffer region
\Lambda^{\rm BUF}\subset\Lambda^{\rm MM}
surrounding
\Lambda^{\rm QM}
such that all atoms in
\Lambda^{\rm BUF}\cup\Lambda^{\rm QM}
are involved in the evaluation of the site energies defined on
\Lambda^{\rm QM}
.

In contrast to the method proposed in
[
7
,
51
]
, which employed approximately spherical regions centered at the defect core for domain partitioning, we introduce a more versatile domain decomposition scheme in our study. Rather than relying on region radii as model parameters, we utilize the number of atoms within the QM, buffer, and MM regions, denoted respectively by
N_{\rm QM}:=\#\Lambda^{\rm QM}
,
N_{\rm BUF}:=\#\Lambda^{\rm BUF}
, and
N_{\rm MM}:=\#\Lambda^{\rm MM}
. Figure
1
presents a typical QM/MM decomposition for a (001)[100] edge dislocation in Tungsten (W) with non-spherical subdomains.

Figure 1.
Decomposition of (001)[100] edge dislocation in W into QM, MM, buffer (BUF), and far-field (FF) regions.

2.2.2.
Specification of MM model

To represent interatomic interactions at a distance from the defect core in the MM region, it is desirable to construct the MM site energy (force) that is computationally efficient, accurately captures the underlying physical behavior, and is “compatible” with the QM model. These requirements have led to the adoption of machine-learned interatomic potentials (MLIPs) as the MM models
[
13
,
31
]
.

The concrete MLIPs ansatz we employ is the atomic cluster expansion (ACE) method
[
19
,
1
,
37
]
, although we emphasise the workflow is readily transferable to other MLIPs approaches. The ACE model stands out for its ability to achieve high accuracy, despite being a
linear
model
[
37
]
. The linear ACE model provides a parameterised site potential, for
\boldsymbol{g}\in\big(\mathbb{R}^{d}\big)^{\Lambda^{\rm h}\setminus 0}
,

(2.9)

\displaystyle V^{\rm ACE}(\boldsymbol{g};\{c_{B}\}_{B\in\boldsymbol{B}})=\sum_{B\in\boldsymbol{B}}c_{B}B(\boldsymbol{g}),

where
B
are the basis functions and
c_{B}
the parameters that we will estimate by minimizing a least square loss (cf. (
2.14
)). The resulting ACE forces are denoted by
\mathcal{F}^{\rm ACE}_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{g})
.

2.2.3.
The QM/MM hybrid model

As detailed in
[
13
, Section 4]
, the force-mixing approach is generally considered more computationally efficient and practically advantageous compared to the energy-mixing approach. We therefore focus on force-mixing schemes, while noting that our approach should be straightforward to adapt to energy-mixing methods.

The approximated equilibrium state of QM/MM hybrid models is obtained by solving the following hybrid force balance equations: Find
\bar{u}^{\rm H}\in\mathscr{A}^{\rm H}(\Lambda)
such that

(2.10)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\ell}^{\rm H}\big(\bar{u}^{\rm H}\big)=0\qquad\forall~\ell\in\Lambda^{\rm QM}\cup\Lambda^{\rm MM},

where
\mathcal{F}_{\ell}^{\rm H}
are the hybrid forces

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\ell}^{\rm H}(u)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathcal{F}^{\rm QM}_{\ell}(u),&\ell\in\Lambda^{\rm QM}\\[4.30554pt]
\mathcal{F}^{\rm ACE}_{\ell}(u),&\ell\in\Lambda^{\rm MM}\end{array}\right.,

with
\mathcal{F}^{\rm QM}_{\ell}(u):=\mathcal{F}^{\Lambda^{\rm QM}\cup\Lambda^{\rm BUF}}_{\ell}(u)
and
\mathcal{F}^{\rm ACE}_{\ell}(u):=\mathcal{F}^{\rm ACE}\big(u_{0}(\cdot-\ell)+u(\cdot-\ell)\big)
.

To ensure the consistency of hybrid models (cf. Remark
2.2.3
), construct the ACE potential by employing the matching conditions derived and analyzed in
[
13
]
. These matching conditions encode that the MM forces should match the QM forces to within a prescribed order off accuracy
K_{\rm F}
in the limit of infinitesimal displacements. A rigorous justification of this approach is based on far-field regularity estimates on atomic displacement fields
[
13
]
.

Concretely, for a prescribed
K_{\rm F}\geq 1
, we estimate the ACE model parameters by minimising a least square loss that matches the QM to the ACE forces, as well as higher order derivatives, at the origin,

(2.14)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\rm F}\big(\{c_{B}\}\big)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{j=0}^{K_{\rm F}}W^{\rm F}_{j}\big(\varepsilon_{j}^{\rm F}\big)^{2}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{j=0}^{K_{\rm F}}W^{\rm F}_{j}\sum_{\boldsymbol{\ell}=(\ell_{1},...,\ell_{j})\in(\Lambda^{\rm h})^{j}}\Big|\mathcal{F}^{\rm h}_{,\boldsymbol{\ell}}(\boldsymbol{0})-\mathcal{F}^{\rm ACE}_{,\boldsymbol{\ell}}(\boldsymbol{0};\{c_{B}\})\Big|^{2}{w}_{j}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\ell}).

Here,
w_{j}(\boldsymbol{\rho})
is the weight function defined by
w_{j}(\boldsymbol{\rho}):=\prod_{i=1}^{j}e^{-2\gamma|\rho_{i}|}
with
\gamma
a constant related to the locality of site potentials
(RL)
(cf.
[
13
, Section 3.1]
) and
W_{j}^{\rm F}
are additional empirical weights. The choices for
K_{\rm F},\gamma
and
W_{j}^{\rm F}
will be specified in Section
4
.

It is worth noting that the choice of loss functional is not unique and several variants of (
2.14
) are available. For instance, one can incorporate the matching conditions for the virial stress of the QM reference model in the loss functional (see (
4.5
)) to achieve improved accuracy. Further details can be found in
[
13
, Section 4]
. The training sets and the hyperparameters for constructing the ACE basis will be tailored to the specific defective systems tested in Section
4
.

Remark 2.1.

A QM/MM model constructed as described above is
consistent
with the reference QM model in the following sense
[

]
: Suppose that
\bar{u}
is a strongly stable solution of (

), i.e.,

a posteriori
residual estimates to

error estimates, as demonstrated in Lemma

.

3.
A posteriori error estimates

3.1.
An idealised error estimator

Following the analysis in
[
7
, Lemma 3.1]
and adapting it to the force-mixing scheme using the techniques in
[
11
, Appendix C]
, we deduce that the
residual
force
\mathcal{F}(\bar{u}^{\rm H})
evaluated at a solution
\bar{u}^{\rm H}
to (
2.10
) in dual norm characterizes the approximation error
\|\bar{u}-\bar{u}^{\rm H}\|_{{\mathscr{U}}^{1,2}}
. The proof can be found in the Appendix
A
.

Lemma 3.1.

Let
\bar{u}
be a strongly stable solution of (
2.7
).
For
\varepsilon_{1}^{\rm F}
sufficiently small and
N_{\rm QM},N_{\rm BUF}
sufficiently large, there exists a solution
\bar{u}^{\rm H}
of QM/MM force-mixing scheme (
2.10
) and constants
c,C
independent of the approximation parameters such that

(3.1)

\displaystyle c\|\bar{u}-\bar{u}^{\rm H}\|_{{\mathscr{U}}^{1,2}}\leq\|\mathcal{F}(\bar{u}^{\rm H})\|_{({\mathscr{U}}^{1,2})^{*}}\leq C\|\bar{u}-\bar{u}^{\rm H}\|_{{\mathscr{U}}^{1,2}}.

The estimate (
3.1
) shows that the dual norm of the residual,
\|\mathcal{F}(\bar{u}^{\rm H})\|_{({\mathscr{U}}^{1,2})^{*}}
, is a reliable and efficient
a posterior
error estimator. However, it is not directly computable and therefore not be used directly in practice.

In
[
51
, Section 3.3]
, we solved a partial differential equation to evaluate
\|\cdot\|_{({\mathscr{U}}^{1,2})^{*}}
, which introduced an additional finite element mesh and led to significant additional algorithmic complexity. Here, we propose a more practical and more flexible approach, essentially replacing the PDE operator from
[
51
]
with a generalisation of a graph Laplacian. The operator we use was previously proposed in
[
42
]
for preconditioning geometry optimization. Algorithmically, this approach fits much better into the setting of atomistic modelling. In addition, the operator is “aware” of the atomic bonding and can more faithfully represent the discrete dual norm.

To that end, we first construct the matrix
L^{\Lambda}
such that the elements satisfying

\displaystyle L^{\Lambda}_{ij}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-\mu\exp\Big(-A\Big(\frac{|y_{ij}|}{y_{\rm nn}}-1\Big)\Big),&i\neq j,~|y_{ij}|<r_{\rm cut}\\[1.29167pt]
0,&i\neq j,~|y_{ij}|\geq r_{\rm cut}\\[1.29167pt]
-\sum_{i\neq j}L_{ij}+\mu C_{\rm stab},&i=j\end{array}\right.,

where
y_{ij}=y_{i}-y_{j}
and the nearest-neighbour distance
r_{\rm nn}
is obtained as the maximum of nearest neighbour bond lengths, i.e.,
y_{\rm nn}=\max_{i}\min_{j\neq i}y_{ij}
. The parameters
\mu,A,r_{\rm cut}
can be user-specified (e.g.,
r_{\rm cut}=2.5y_{\rm nn},A=3
appear to be a good defaults). The parameters can also be estimated numerically from the forces
\mathcal{F}_{\ell}
to match the force jacobian as closely as possible.
Given a specific connectivity defined by
r_{\rm cut}
and setting
A=0
and
\mu=1
, the matrix
L^{\Lambda}
simplifies to the standard graph Laplacian matrix. To enforce positive definiteness in practice, we stabilize
L^{\Lambda}
by introducing a diagonal term
C_{\rm stab}
, as suggested in
[
42
]
. The empirical selection of
C_{\rm stab}=0.1
has proven to outperform alternative choices in the numerical experiments.

Let
\mathcal{F}(\bar{u}^{\rm H})
be the
residual
force evaluated at a solution
\bar{u}^{\rm H}
. We consider the equation

(3.6)

\displaystyle L^{\Lambda}\cdot\phi_{\rm a}(\bar{u}^{\rm H})=\mathcal{F}(\bar{u}^{\rm H}).

The following theorem establishes an equivalent representation of the residual force in dual norm through
\phi_{\rm a}(\bar{u}^{\rm H})
, the solution to (
3.6
). This result contributes to the derivation of the
ideal a posteriori
error estimator for the QM/MM scheme (
2.10
). We leave the proof to the Appendix
A
.

Theorem 3.2.

Let
\phi_{\rm a}
be the solution to (
3.6
). We define the a posteriori error estimator of the QM/MM scheme (
2.10
) by

(3.7)

\eta^{\rm ideal}(\bar{u}^{\rm H}):=\|D\phi_{\rm a}(\bar{u}^{\rm H})\|_{\ell^{2}_{\mathcal{N}}(\Lambda)}.

Then, there exist constants
c_{1},C_{1}
such that

(3.8)

c_{1}\|\mathcal{F}(\bar{u}^{\rm H})\|_{({\mathscr{U}}^{1,2})^{*}}\leq\eta^{\rm ideal}(\bar{u}^{\rm H})\leq C_{1}\|\mathcal{F}(\bar{u}^{\rm H})\|_{({\mathscr{U}}^{1,2})^{*}}.

Moreover, under the conditions of Lemma
3.1
, there exists another two constants
c_{2},C_{2}
such that

(3.9)

c_{2}\|\bar{u}-\bar{u}^{\rm H}\|_{{\mathscr{U}}^{1,2}}\leq\eta^{\rm ideal}(\bar{u}^{\rm H})\leq C_{2}\|\bar{u}-\bar{u}^{\rm H}\|_{{\mathscr{U}}^{1,2}}.

In light with (
3.9
), the error estimator
\eta^{\rm ideal}(\bar{u}^{\rm H})
is referred to as
ideal
as it provides both upper and lower bounds for the approximation error. However, practical computation of
\eta^{\rm ideal}(\bar{u}^{\rm H})
remains challenging, primarily due to two main reasons. Firstly, the equation (
3.6
) is defined on an infinite lattice, posing difficulties for explicit solution. Secondly, obtaining the source term (residual force)
\mathcal{F}(\bar{u}^{\rm H})
is a non-trivial task.

3.2.
A practical error estimator

To overcome the aforementioned challenges, we propose the following two approximations: (1) truncating the infinite lattice
\Lambda
to a finite lattice; (2) constructing the approximated force
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}(\bar{u}^{\rm H})
through a linear expansion of the residual force
\mathcal{F}(\bar{u}^{\rm H})
away from the defect core.

3.2.1.
Truncation

Let
\Omega
be a convex polygon or polyhedron in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with boundary
\partial\Omega
such that
\Lambda^{\rm QM}\cup\Lambda^{\rm MM}\subset\Omega
and
\Lambda^{\Omega}:=\Lambda\cap\Omega
. We interpret the lattice
\Lambda
as the vertex set of a simplicial grid
\mathcal{T}
leading to the
canonical partition
. For simplicity,
suppose that
\Omega
is compatible with
\mathcal{T}
, i.e., there exists a subset
\mathcal{T}_{\Omega}\subset\mathcal{T}
such that
\text{clos}(\Omega)=\cup\mathcal{T}_{\Omega}
.
As a truncation of (
3.1
), we denote the corresponding truncated Laplace matrix as
L
.

The error resulting from this truncation has already been analyzed in
[
51
, Section 3.5]
and in principle one can incorporate this to adapt the size of MM region. However, the adaptation of QM and buffer regions is much more important in practice. Therefore, in this paper, we assume that the computational domain
\Omega
(or equivalently
\Lambda^{\rm MM}
) is chosen to be sufficiently large to ensure that the corresponding truncation error is negligible for the error estimator.

3.2.2.
Approximated residual force

To reduce the computational cost of evaluating the residual force
\mathcal{F}(\bar{u}^{\rm H})
while maintaining its accuracy, an approximation scheme should be carefully designed. We note that the exact evaluation of residual force is necessary for accurate adjustment of the QM and buffer regions, which is in fact acceptable since the size of
\Lambda^{\rm QB}:=\Lambda^{\rm QM}\cup\Lambda^{\rm BUF}
is much smaller than that of the full computational domain
\Omega
. We therefore only need to consider the approximation of the residual force outside this region, where the displacement
\bar{u}^{\rm H}
varies smoothly.

To that end, we consider the linear approximation of residual force. Let
\delta\mathcal{F}^{B_{R_{\rm buf}}}({\bm{0}})
be the QM force constant defined on the homogeneous lattice inside the region
B_{R_{\rm buf}}
.
The residual force is then approximated in a mixed scheme

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}_{\ell}(\bar{u}^{\rm H})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathcal{F}_{\ell}(\bar{u}^{\rm H})&\ell\in\Lambda^{\rm QB}\\[4.30554pt]
\delta\mathcal{F}^{B_{R_{\rm buf}}}({\bm{0}})\Big(\big(u_{0}(\cdot-\ell)+\bar{u}^{\rm H}(\cdot-\ell)\big)\big|_{B_{R_{\rm buf}}(\ell)}\Big)&\ell\in\Lambda^{\Omega}\setminus\Lambda^{\rm QB}\end{array}\right..

Due to the locality of QM site potentials (cf.
(RL)
and
[
11
, Lemma 2.1]
), the accuracy of this approximation can therefore be guaranteed as long as the size of core region
\Lambda^{\rm QB}
and
R_{\rm BUF}
are chosen to be sufficiently large. The error caused by this approximation can be analyzed following the approach in
[
51
, Section 5.1]
, referred to as the data oscillation term. Although we omit the details here, it is important to note that this error can be controlled.

Hence, combined with the truncation and the approximation of the residual force, we obtain the approximation to (
3.6
), which is expressed as

(3.12)

\displaystyle L\cdot\phi(\bar{u}^{\rm H})=\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}(\bar{u}^{\rm H}).

The
practical a posteriori
error estimator of the QM/MM scheme (
2.10
) is then given by

(3.13)

\eta(\bar{u}^{\rm H}):=\|D\phi(\bar{u}^{\rm H})\|_{\ell^{2}_{\mathcal{N}}(\Lambda^{\Omega})}.

We will utilize it to formulate the main adaptive QM/MM algorithm in the following section.

In practical implementations, it is essential to accurately compute the residual force
\mathcal{F}_{\ell}(\bar{u}^{\rm H})
within
\Lambda^{\rm QB}
. To achieve this, an extended buffer region, twice the size of the original buffer region
R_{\rm buf}
, is employed for computing the residual force
\mathcal{F}_{\ell}(\bar{u}^{\rm H})
. This practice becomes particularly crucial when employing the electronic structure model as the reference model (cf. Section
4.5.2
).

4.
Adaptive algorithm and numerical tests

In this section, we present an adaptive QM/MM algorithm leveraging the
practical a posterior
error estimator
\eta(\bar{u}^{\rm H})
defined by (
3.13
), and conduct the numerical examples for three typical crystalline defects: in-plane crack in W, (110)[100] edge dislocation and di-interstitial in Si.

4.1.
Adaptive algorithm

The basic idea of our adaptive QM/MM algorithm is to repeat the following procedure before reaching the required accuracy:

\mbox{Solve}~\rightarrow~\mbox{Estimate}~\rightarrow~\mbox{Mark \& Refine}.

Compared to standard adaptive finite element methods
[
18
]
, our approach differs in that we integrate the “Mark” and “Refine” steps by employing a fast marching method for updating the QM and buffer regions, as presented in Algorithm
2
. Moreover, as discussed in Section
3.2.1
, the computational domain
\Omega
(or equivalently, MM region
\Lambda^{\rm MM}
) is fixed to be sufficiently large to mitigate the influence of the truncation error. Hence, in the “Mark & Refine” step, we only update QM and buffer regions.

To choose where to refine the models, it is natural to assign the global estimator
\eta(\bar{u}^{\rm H})
into local contributions (site-based),

(4.1)

\displaystyle\eta_{\ell}(\bar{u}^{\rm H}):=\frac{\big|D\phi(\bar{u}^{\rm H})(\ell)\big|^{2}_{\mathcal{N}}}{\eta(\bar{u}^{\rm H})}\qquad\forall\ell\in\Lambda^{\Omega},

where
|\cdot|_{\mathcal{N}}
is defined by (
2.2
) and it is straightforward to see that
\sum_{\ell\in\Lambda^{\Omega}}\eta_{\ell}(\bar{u}^{\rm H})=\eta(\bar{u}^{\rm H})
.

We first describe the adaptive QM/MM algorithm as follows and then give a detailed discussion.

The fast marching method is a numerical technique for finding the solutions of the Eikonal equation

5.
Conclusion

We proposed a novel adaptive QM/MM method for practical material defect simulations. To ensure
consistency
of the QM/MM method with the reference QM model, we employ “machine-learned interatomic potentials (MLIPs)” as the MM models
[
13
]
. Our adaptive QM/MM method utilizes a residual-based error estimator that provides both upper and lower bounds for the approximation error, thus indicating its reliability and efficiency. Furthermore, we introduce a novel adaptive algorithm capable of anisotropically updating the QM/MM partitions. This update is based on the proposed residual-based error estimator and involves solving a free interface motion problem, which is efficiently addressed using the fast marching method. To demonstrate the robustness of our approach, we performed numerical simulations involving a range of crystalline defects (point defects, dislocations, cracks).

Our results suggest that the proposed adaptive algorithm is generally applicable for other common multiscale coupling schemes and more complex crystalline defects, some open problems remain that deserve further mathematical and empirical analysis as well as further algorithmic developments, for example:

More complex crystalline defects:
More complex defect structures such as two partial dislocations connected by a stacking fault, or dislocation nucleation, are mcuh more difficult to include in a rigorous mathematical analysis, but we see no reason why our methods are not applicable in principle. Indeed, such scenarios are exactly where an adaptive QM/MM method can demonstrate the maximum gain.

Quasi-static and dynamical problems:
We plan to explore the generalization of this work to adaptive error control for quasi-static and dynamical problems (with moving defects). Our method is potentially more efficient and important for these problems, where both model refinement and coarsening must be carefully considered. This would require some adaptations to our methodology.

P(\ell_{1},\ell_{2})=(\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3})\quad\text{for}~\ell\in B\mathbb{Z}^{3}.

\displaystyle\mathbb{C}^{j\beta}_{i\alpha}\frac{\partial^{2}u^{\rm lin}_{i}}{\partial x_{\alpha}\partial x_{\beta}}=0\qquad

\displaystyle\text{for}~~x\in\Gamma\setminus\{\hat{x}\},

\displaystyle\text{for}~~x\in\Gamma\setminus\{\hat{x}\},

Appendix C
The Atomic Cluster Expansion

Following
[
1
]
, we briefly introduce the construction of the ACE potential.
Given
\mathcal{N}\in\mathbb{N}
, we first write the ACE site potential in the form of an
atomic body-order expansion
,
\displaystyle V^{\rm ACE}\big(\{\boldsymbol{g_{j}}\}\big)=\sum_{N=0}^{\mathcal{N}}\frac{1}{N!}\sum_{j_{1}\neq\cdots\neq j_{N}}V_{N}(\boldsymbol{g}_{j_{1}},\cdots,\boldsymbol{g}_{j_{N}})
, where the
N
-body potential
V_{N}:\mathbb{R}^{dN}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
can be approximated by using a tensor product basis
[
1
, Proposition 1]
,

\displaystyle\phi_{\boldsymbol{k\ell m}}\big(\{\boldsymbol{g}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N}\big):=\prod_{j=1}^{N}\phi_{k_{j}\ell_{j}m_{j}}(\boldsymbol{g}_{j})\quad

\displaystyle{\rm with}\quad\phi_{k\ell m}(\boldsymbol{r}):=P_{k}(r)Y^{m}_{\ell}(\hat{r}),~~\boldsymbol{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},~r=|\boldsymbol{r}|,~\hat{r}=\boldsymbol{r}/r,

where
P_{k},~k=0,1,2,\cdots
are radial basis functions (for example, Jacobic polynomials), and
Y_{\ell}^{m},~\ell=0,1,2,\cdots,~m=-\ell,\cdots,\ell
are the complex spherical harmonics.
The basis functions are further symmetrised to a permutation invariant form,

\displaystyle\tilde{\phi}_{N}=\sum_{(\boldsymbol{k,\ell,m})~{\rm ordered}}\sum_{\sigma\in S_{N}}\phi_{\boldsymbol{k\ell m}}\circ\sigma,

where
S_{N}
is the collection of all permutations, and by
\sum_{(\boldsymbol{k,\ell,m})~{\rm ordered}}
we mean that the sum is over all lexicographically ordered tuples
\big((k_{j},\ell_{j},m_{j})\big)_{j=1}^{N}
.
The next step is to incorporate the invariance under point reflections and rotations

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{k\ell}i}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{m}\in\mathcal{M}_{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}\mathcal{U}_{\boldsymbol{m}i}^{\boldsymbol{k\ell}}\sum_{\sigma\in S_{N}}\phi_{\boldsymbol{k\ell m}}\circ\sigma\quad{\rm with}\quad\mathcal{M}_{\boldsymbol{\ell}}=\big\{\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\mathbb{Z}^{N}~|~-\ell_{\alpha}\leq\mu_{\alpha}\leq\ell_{\alpha}\big\},

where the coefficients
\mathcal{U}_{mi}^{k\ell}
are given in
[
1
, Lemma 2 and Eq. (3.12)]
.
It was shown in
[
1
]
that the basis defined above is explicit but computational inefficient. The so-called “density trick” technique used in
[
3
,
19
,
47
]
can transform this basis into one that is computational efficient. The alternative basis is

\displaystyle B_{\boldsymbol{k\ell}i}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{m}\in\mathcal{M}_{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}\mathcal{U}_{\boldsymbol{m}i}^{\boldsymbol{k\ell}}A_{\boldsymbol{nlm}}\quad{\rm with~the~correlations}\quad A_{\boldsymbol{nlm}}:=\prod_{\alpha=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\phi_{n_{\alpha}l_{\alpha}m_{\alpha}}(\boldsymbol{g}_{j}),

which avoids both the
N!
cost for symmetrising the basis as well as the
C_{J}^{N}
cost of summation over all order
N
clusters within an atomic neighbourhood.
The resulting basis set is then defined by

(C.1)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{B}_{N}:=\big\{B_{\boldsymbol{k\ell}i}~|~(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{\ell})\in\mathbb{N}^{2N}~{\rm ordered},~\sum_{\alpha}\ell_{\alpha}~{\rm even},~i=1,\cdots,\boldsymbol{n}_{\boldsymbol{k\ell}}\big\},

where
\boldsymbol{n}_{\boldsymbol{k\ell}}
is the rank of body-orders (see
[
1
, Proposition 7 and Eq. (3.12)]
).

Once the finite symmetric polynomial basis set
\boldsymbol{B}\subset\bigcup^{\mathcal{N}}_{N=1}\boldsymbol{B}_{N}
is constructed, the ACE site potential can be expressed as

(C.2)

\displaystyle V^{\rm ACE}(\boldsymbol{g};\{c_{B}\}_{B\in\boldsymbol{B}})=\sum_{B\in\boldsymbol{B}}c_{B}B(\boldsymbol{g})

with the coefficients
c_{B}
. The corresponding force of this potential is denoted by
\mathcal{F}^{\rm ACE}
.

The family of potentials are systematically improvable (see
[
1
, Section 6.2]
): by increasing the body-order, cutoff radius and polynomial degree they are in principle capable of representing an arbitrary many-body potential energy surface to within arbitrary accuracy
[
54
]
.

Appendix D
A semi-empirical QM model: The NRL tight binding

In this paper, we use the tight binding model as the reference quantum mechanical model for simplicity of presentation. We note that our numerical scheme is in principle also suitable for general quantum mechanical models.

The NRL tight binding model is developed by Cohen, Mehl, and Papaconstantopoulos
[
15
]
. The energy levels are determined by the generalised eigenvalue problem

(D.1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}(y)\psi_{s}=\lambda_{s}\mathcal{M}(y)\psi_{s}\qquad\text{with}\quad\psi_{s}^{\rm T}\mathcal{M}(y)\psi_{s}=1,

where
\mathcal{H}
is the hamiltonian matrix and
\mathcal{M}(y)
is the overlap matrix. The NRL hamiltonian and overlap matrices are construct both from hopping elements as well as on-site matrix elements as a function of the local environment. For carbon and silicon they are parameterised as follows (for other elements the parameterisation is similar):

To define the on-site terms, each atom
\ell
is assigned a pseudo-atomic density

\displaystyle\rho_{\ell}:=\sum_{k}e^{-\lambda^{2}r_{\ell k}}f_{\rm c}(r_{\ell k}),

where the sum is over all of the atoms
k
within the cutoff
R_{\rm c}
of atom
\ell
,
\lambda
is a fitting parameter,
f_{\rm c}
is a cutoff function

\displaystyle f_{\rm c}(r)=\frac{\theta(R_{\rm c}-r)}{1+\exp\big((r-R_{\rm c})/l_{c}+L_{c}\big)},

with
\theta
the step function, and the parameters
l_{c}=0.5
,
L_{c}=5.0
for most elements.
Although, in principle, the on-site terms should have off-diagonal elements, but this would lead to additional computational challenges that we wished to avoid. The NRL model follows traditional practice and only include the diagonal terms.
Then, the on-site terms for each atomic site
\ell
are given by

(D.2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}(y)_{\ell\ell}^{\upsilon\upsilon}:=a_{\upsilon}+b_{\upsilon}\rho_{\ell}^{2/3}+c_{\upsilon}\rho_{\ell}^{4/3}+d_{\upsilon}\rho_{\ell}^{2},

where
\upsilon=s,p
, or
d
is the index for angular-momentum-dependent atomic orbitals and
(a_{\upsilon})
,
(b_{\upsilon})
,
(c_{\upsilon})
,
(d_{\upsilon})
are fitting parameters. The on-site elements for the overlap matrix are simply taken to be the identity matrix.

The off-diagonal NRL Hamiltonian entries follow the formalism of Slater and Koster who showed in
[
48
]
that all two-centre (spd) hopping integrals can be constructed from ten independent “bond integral” parameters
h_{\upsilon\upsilon^{\prime}\mu}
, where

\displaystyle(\upsilon\upsilon^{\prime}\mu)=ss\sigma,~sp\sigma,~pp\sigma,~pp\pi,~sd\sigma,~pd\sigma,~pd\pi,~dd\sigma,~dd\pi,~{\rm and}~dd\delta.

The NRL bond integrals are given by

(D.3)

\displaystyle h_{\upsilon\upsilon^{\prime}\mu}(r):=\big(e_{\upsilon\upsilon^{\prime}\mu}+f_{\upsilon\upsilon^{\prime}\mu}r+g_{\upsilon\upsilon^{\prime}\mu}r^{2}\big)e^{-h_{\upsilon\upsilon^{\prime}\mu}r}f_{\rm c}(r)

with fitting parameters
e_{\upsilon\upsilon^{\prime}\mu},f_{\upsilon\upsilon^{\prime}\mu},g_{\upsilon\upsilon^{\prime}\mu},h_{\upsilon\upsilon^{\prime}\mu}
. The matrix elements
\mathcal{H}(y)_{\ell k}^{\upsilon\upsilon^{\prime}}
are constructed from the
h_{\upsilon\upsilon^{\prime}\mu}(r)
by a standard procedure
[
48
]
.

The analogous bond integral parameterisation of the overlap matrix
is given by

(D.4)

\displaystyle m_{\upsilon\upsilon^{\prime}\mu}(r):=\big(\delta_{\upsilon\upsilon^{\prime}}+p_{\upsilon\upsilon^{\prime}\mu}r+q_{\upsilon\upsilon^{\prime}\mu}r^{2}+r_{\upsilon\upsilon^{\prime}\mu}r^{3}\big)e^{-s_{\upsilon\upsilon^{\prime}\mu}r}f_{\rm c}(r)

with the fitting parameters
(p_{\upsilon\upsilon^{\prime}\mu}),(q_{\upsilon\upsilon^{\prime}\mu}),(r_{\upsilon\upsilon^{\prime}\mu}),(s_{\upsilon\upsilon^{\prime}\mu})
and
\delta_{\upsilon\upsilon^{\prime}}
the Kronecker delta function.

The fitting parameters in the foregoing expressions are determined by fitting to some high-symmetry first-principle calculations: In the NRL method, a database of eigenvalues (band structures) and total energies were constructed for several crystal structures at several volumes. Then the parameters are chosen such that the eigenvalues and energies in the database are reproduced.
