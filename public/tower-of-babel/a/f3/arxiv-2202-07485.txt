Approaching the coupon collector’s problem with group drawings via Stein’s method
Carina Betken, Christoph Thäle

Abstract
In this paper the coupon collector’s problem with group drawings is studied. Assume there are n n different coupons. At each time precisely s s of the n n coupons are drawn, where all choices are supposed to have equal probability. The focus lies on the fluctuations, as n → ∞ n\to\infty , of the number Z n , s ​ ( k n ) Z_{n,s}(k_{n}) of coupons that have not been drawn in the first k n k_{n} drawings. Using a size-biased coupling construction together with Stein’s method for normal approximation, a quantitative central limit theorem for Z n , s ​ ( k n ) Z_{n,s}(k_{n}) is shown for the case that k n = n s ​ ( α ​ log ⁡ ( n ) + x ) k_{n}={n\over s}(\alpha\log(n)+x) , where 0 < α < 1 0<\alpha<1 and x ∈ ℝ x\in\mathbb{R} . The same coupling construction is used to retrieve a quantitative Poisson limit theorem in the boundary case α = 1 \alpha=1 , again using Stein’s method. Keywords . Central limit theorem, coupon collector’s problem, Poisson limit theorem, size-biased coupling, Stein’s method MSC . 60C05, 60F05

Introduction

The coupon collector’s problem is an old problem of probability theory which in its simplest form dates back to de Moivre, Laplace and Euler, see
[
4
]
,
[
8
]
and
[
12
]
. Whereas de Moivre used a die with
s
faces to pose the problem, Euler and Laplace used a lottery interpretation as motivation. However, a more recent example for a situation in which the coupon collector’s problem occurs is the collection of pictures of the participating players of all teams before and during every World Cup. Typically, fans can buy the pictures in packages of five or six. Two natural questions which arise are: How many packages does one need to buy to get the full or a specific portion of the full set of players? How many stickers are missing after one has bought
k
packages? The first question was for example studied in
[
3
]
,
[
6
]
,
[
7
]
or
[
16
]
. In the work at hand we will deal with the latter of the two problems.
The version of the coupon collector’s problem we consider can be described as follows. Assume there are
n
different coupons. At each time we draw
s
of these
n
coupons, where we assume that each of the
{n\choose s}
choices occurs with the same probability.
We are then interested in the distribution of the number
Z_{n,s}(k_{n})
of coupons that have not been drawn in the first
k=k_{n}
drawings. In a conceptually equivalent interpretation the
n
coupons are represented by
n
different cells numbered
1,\ldots,n
, and in each drawing we place
s
particles into
s
distinct cells, see Figure
1
. We are then interested in the distribution of the number
Z_{n,s}(k_{n})
of empty cells after
k=k_{n}
drawings.

Figure 1:
Illustration of the cell interpretation with
n=7
and
s=4
in the first three drawings.

The behaviour of
Z_{n,s}(k_{n})
for the different regimes of
k_{n}
has been subject to numerous research works over the years.
In
[
9
]
convergence towards a normal limit is proved for the sublinear and linear regime, that is, for
k_{n}=o(n)
and
k_{n}=\alpha n
, respectively. In
[
15
]
the author proves a central limit theorem for a generalized coupon collector’s problem allowing for
random
package sizes
S
in the lower superlinear regime, i.e., for
\frac{k_{n}}{n}\rightarrow\infty\text{ and }\limsup_{n}\frac{k_{n}}{n\log(n)}<\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}[S]}
using a martingale representation.
In
[
11
]
the method of moments is used to prove normal approximation for the case, where
n(\frac{k_{n}s}{n})^{r}\rightarrow\infty
for all
r\in\mathbb{N}
and
\mathbb{E}[Z_{n,s}(k_{n})]\rightarrow\infty
, which covers our regime of normal approximation introduced below. However, no rates of convergence are given in neither of the works mentioned so far. In
[
17
]
the authors deduce rates of convergence in the Kolmogorov distance towards a normal limit for
k_{n}=o(n\log(n))
which are of order
1/\sqrt{\operatorname{var}(Z_{n,s}(k_{n}))}
. We complement these bounds in the case where
k_{n}
is assumed to be of the form
k_{n}=\frac{n}{s}(\alpha\log(n)+x)
for some
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
x\in\mathbb{R}
. In the case
\alpha=1
, i.e. if
k_{n}=\frac{n}{s}(\log(n)+x)
, the author in
[
10
]
uses the Stein-Chen method to prove convergence towards a Poisson limit for the number of cells containing exactly
r
particles in a more general setting allowing for multiple particles being placed in one cell at each step. The same question is also studied in
[
14
]
and a Poisson limit is deduced. In the same work, the authors show that for the special case
r=0
the condition of equally probable group drawings can be relaxed to a certain extend without losing the limiting Poisson distribution. In
[
2
, Theorem 6.F]
the authors also prove rates of convergence of order
\frac{\log(n)}{n}
towards a Poisson distribution in this regime, which are optimal in view of
[
2
, Theorem 3.D]
. For
r=0
this covers our setting of Poisson approximation with the same rates, which are included here only for completeness and to demonstrate that both limit theorems can be based on the same coupling argument.

As we have explained above, there exists a sharp asymptotic distributional phase transition at
\alpha=1
in the sense that for
\alpha\in(0,1)
the random variable
Z_{n,s}(k_{n})
asymptotically follows a normal distribution whereas for
\alpha=1
we obtain a Poisson limit. However, in both cases we use Stein’s method in combination with the same size-biased coupling construction to prove upper bounds on the distance between
Z_{n,s}(k_{n})
and Gaussian or Poisson random variable, respectively. Our results are presented in the next section, while the coupling construction is explained in Section
3
. The proof of the normal approximation result for
\alpha\in(0,1)
is the content of Section
4
, while the Poisson limit theorem is derived in Section
5
.

2
Results

Denote by
(C_{i})_{i=1,\ldots n}
the collection of cells in the coupon collector’s problem and let
Z_{n,s}(k_{n})
be the number of empty cells after
k_{n}
drawings, i.e.

Z_{n,s}(k_{n})=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}_{E_{n,j}(k_{n})},

where
E_{n,j}(k)=\{|C_{j}|=0\text{ after }k_{n}\text{ drawings}\}
and where
|C_{j}|
stands for the number of particles in cell
C_{j}
. Moreover, we shall denote for two random variables
X
and
Y
by

d_{W}(X,Y):=\sup_{h\in{\rm Lip}(1)}\big|\mathbb{E}[h(X)]-\mathbb{E}[h(Y)]\big|

the Wasserstein distance between
X
and
Y
, where the supremum runs over all Lipschitz functions
h:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
with Lipschitz constant less than or equal to one.
We consider the case where
k_{n}=\frac{n}{s}(\alpha\log(n)+x)
for fixed
s\in\mathbb{N}
,
x\in\mathbb{R}
and
\alpha\in(0,1)
. Note that since
k_{n}
denotes the number of drawings we always assume implicitly that
k_{n}
is an integer, in particular we assume that
n
is large enough so that
k_{n}\geq 0
. Furthermore, we define the centred and normalized random variables

\displaystyle\tilde{Z}_{n,s}(k_{n}):=\frac{Z_{n,s}(k_{n})-\mathbb{E}[Z_{n,s}(k_{n})]}{\sqrt{\operatorname{var}(Z_{n,s}(k_{n}))}}.

(2.1)

Throughout the paper we use the notation
C(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots)
to indicate that a constant
C\in(0,\infty)
only depends on parameters
x_{1},x_{2},\ldots
of the model.

Theorem 1
.

Put
k_{n}=\frac{n}{s}(\alpha\log(n)+x)
for some
s\in\mathbb{N}
,
x\in\mathbb{R}
and
\alpha\in(0,1)
. Let
Z_{n,s}(k_{n})
be the number of empty cells after
k_{n}
drawings as introduced above and denote by
G
a standard Gaussian random variable. Then there exist constants
C=C(s,x)\in(0,\infty)
and
N=N(s,x,\alpha)\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
n\geq N
,

\displaystyle d_{W}(\tilde{Z}_{n,s}(k_{n}),G)\leq\begin{cases}C\frac{\sqrt{\log(n)}}{n^{\alpha}}&\text{ for }\alpha\in(0,\frac{1}{3}]\\[5.69046pt]
Cn^{-{1\over 2}(1-\alpha)}&\text{ for }\alpha\in(\frac{1}{3},1).\end{cases}

(2.2)

Remark 2
.

(i)

Since
n^{-\frac{1}{2}(1-\alpha)}
is of the same order as
1/\sqrt{\operatorname{var}(Z_{n,s}(k_{n}))}
we believe that the rate in Theorem
1
is optimal at least in the regime
\alpha\in(1/3,1)
. We leave it as an open problem to decide whether or not the rate is optimal for
\alpha\in(0,1/3]
. On the other hand, Remark
5
shows that in this situation the rate cannot be improved on the basis of the general normal approximation bound (
4.1
) we use.

(ii)

One might ask whether the Wasserstein distance can be replaced by the Kolmogorov distance
d_{K}(\tilde{Z}_{n,s}(k_{n}),N)=\sup_{u\in\mathbb{R}}|\mathbb{P}(\tilde{Z}_{n,s}(k_{n})\leq u)-\mathbb{P}(N\leq u)|
. As we shall explain in Remark
4
in more detail, this is not possible by means of the size-biased coupling approach of Stein’s method for normal approximation using our coupling construction. In fact, the resulting bound does in this case not even tend to zero with
n
. On the other hand, the presumably suboptimal bound
d_{K}(\tilde{Z}_{n,s}(k_{n}),N)\leq C(\log(n))^{1/4}n^{-\alpha/2}
for
0<\alpha\leq 1/3
and
d_{K}(\tilde{Z}_{n,s}(k_{n}),N)\leq Cn^{-\frac{1}{4}(1-\alpha)}
for
1/3<\alpha<1
follows directly from the fact that the Kolmogorov distance can always be bounded by the square-root of the Wasserstein distance.

The next result complements Theorem
1
by considering the case
\alpha=1
for which the upper bound (
2.2
) does not tend to zero, as
n\to\infty
. As emphasized already above, the result is known from
[
2
]
and is included here only for completeness. Similarly as above, for two random variables
X
and
Y
we denote by

d_{TV}(X,Y):=\sup_{A}\big|\mathbb{P}(X\in A)-\mathbb{P}(Y\in B)\big|

the total variation distance between
X
and
Y
, where the supremum is taken over all Borel sets
A\subset\mathbb{R}
.

Theorem 3
.

Put
k_{n}=\frac{n}{s}(\log(n)+x)
for some
s\in\mathbb{N}
and
x\in\mathbb{R}
. Let
Z_{n,s}(k_{n})
be the number of empty cells after
k_{n}
drawings as introduced above and denote by
W
a Poisson random variable with parameter
\lambda_{n}=\mathbb{E}[Z_{n,s}(k_{n})]
. Then there exist constants
\tilde{C}=\tilde{C}(s,x)\in(0,\infty)
and
\tilde{N}=\tilde{N}(s,x)\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
n\geq\tilde{N}
,

\displaystyle d_{TV}(Z_{n,s}(k_{n}),W)\leq\tilde{C}\,\frac{\log(n)}{n}.

For the proof of both results we use Stein’s method in combination with a size-biased coupling. We start by describing this coupling in the next section.

We are dealing with the coupon collector’s problem with
n
coupons, which can be interpreted as
n
distinct cells. At each time step we place a fixed number
s\leq n
of particles in
s
different cells. In order to keep track of when particles are placed into cells, we label all particles in the
m
th drawing with the letter
m
. Now, after
k_{n}
placements, we choose one of the
n
cells, which we denote by
C_{I}
, uniformly at random and take all particles out of it. For a particle labelled
j
taken from
C_{I}
we now choose one of the
n-s
cells not containing a particle with label
j
uniformly and place the particle into it. We proceed in the same manner until all particles from cell
I
have been redistributed into the remaining
n-1
cells, see Figure
2
. Denote by
F_{n,j}^{I}
the event that at least one particle from cell
I
is placed into cell
j
and put
\bar{F}_{n,j}^{I}:=(F_{n,j}^{I})^{c}
. Furthermore, we define
E^{I}_{n,j}(k_{n}):=E_{n,j}(k_{n})\cap\bar{F}_{n,j}^{I}.
For any
j\neq I
we then have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(E^{I}_{n,j}(k_{n}))

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=0}^{k_{n}}\mathbb{P}(E_{n,j}(k_{n})\cap\bar{F}_{n,j}^{I}\cap\{|C_{I}|=\ell\})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=0}^{k_{n}}\mathbb{P}(\bar{F}_{n,j}^{I}\,|\,E_{n,j}(k_{n})\cap\{|C_{I}|=\ell\})\ \mathbb{P}(|C_{I}|=\ell\,|\,E_{n,j}(k_{n}))\ \mathbb{P}(E_{n,j}(k_{n})).

(3.1)

Note that by construction we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(E_{n,j}(k_{n}))

\displaystyle=\Big(1-\frac{s}{n}\Big)^{k_{n}},

(3.2)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(|C_{I}|=\ell\,|\,E_{n,j}(k_{n}))

\displaystyle={k_{n}\choose\ell}\Big(\frac{s}{n-1}\Big)^{\ell}\Big(1-\frac{s}{n-1}\Big)^{k_{n}-\ell},

(3.3)

since conditioning on the event
E_{n,j}(k_{n})
simply means that we can only place particles in
n-1
instead of
n
cells. In addition,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\bar{F}_{n,j}^{I}\,|\,E_{n,j}(k_{n})\cap\{|C_{I}|=\ell\})

\displaystyle=\Big(1-\frac{1}{n-s}\Big)^{\ell},

since for each of the particles from cell
I
the probability that it is placed into an originally empty cell is
\frac{1}{n-s}
.
Putting these expressions back into (
3
) we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(E_{n,j}^{I}(k_{n}))

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=0}^{k_{n}}{k_{n}\choose\ell}\Big(\frac{s}{n-1}\Big)^{\ell}\Big(1-\frac{s}{n-1}\Big)^{k_{n}-\ell}\Big(1-\frac{1}{n-s}\Big)^{\ell}\,\Big(1-\frac{s}{n}\Big)^{k_{n}}

\displaystyle=\Big(1-\frac{s}{n-1}\Big)^{k_{n}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(E_{n,j}(k_{n})\,|\,E_{n,i}(k_{n}))

(3.4)

for any
i\neq j
. We thus conclude that the random variable

\displaystyle Z^{I}_{n,s}(k_{n}):=1+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq I\end{subarray}}^{n}\mathbf{1}_{E^{I}_{n,j}(k_{n})}

has the
Z_{n,s}(k_{n})-
size biased distribution, meaning that

\mathbb{P}(Z^{I}_{n,s}(k_{n})=y)={y\over\mathbb{E}[Z_{n,s}(k_{n})]}\mathbb{P}(Z_{n,s}(k_{n})=y),\qquad y\in\{0,1,\ldots,n\},

see
[
1
]
.

Remark 4
.

Theorem 5.6 in
[
1
]
provides a bound on the Kolmogorov distance between
\tilde{Z}_{n,s}(k_{n})
and a standard Gaussian random variable using a size-biased coupling. However, for this to yield a central limit theorem one needs that
|Z^{I}_{n,s}-Z_{n,s}|=o(\sqrt{n})
almost surely, as
n\to\infty
. For the coupling described above we have
|Z^{I}_{n,s}-Z_{n,s}|=n-s-1
if in all
k_{n}
drawings the same
s
cells are filled and the remaining
n-s
cells are filled when redistributing the
k_{n}
particles of one of the filled cells. Consequently, the result in
[
1
]
does not lead to a meaningful bound on the Komogorov distance as already explained in part (ii) of the remark after Theorem
1
.

Proof of Theorem
1

Following
[
5

the Wasserstein distance between
\tilde{Z}_{n,s}=\tilde{Z}_{n,s}(k_{n})
as defined in (
2.1
) and a standard Gaussian random variable
G
is bounded by

\displaystyle d_{W}(\tilde{Z}_{n,s},G)\leq\frac{\lambda_{n}}{\sigma_{n}^{2}}\sqrt{\operatorname{var}(\mathbb{E}[Z_{n,s}-Z^{I}_{n,s}|\mathcal{C}_{n}(k_{n})])}+\frac{\lambda_{n}}{\sigma_{n}^{3}}\mathbb{E}[(Z_{n,s}-Z^{I}_{n,s})^{2}],

(4.1)

where
\mathcal{C}_{n}(k_{n})
denotes the configuration of the
n
cells after
k_{n}
drawings,
\lambda_{n}=\mathbb{E}[Z_{n,s}(k_{n})]
and
\sigma_{n}^{2}=\operatorname{var}(Z_{n,s}(k_{n}))
. In the next sections we bound further the right-hand side of (
4.1
) by dealing with the individual terms.

4.1
Expectation and variance

We start by bounding from above the expectation and from below the variance of
Z_{n,s}
. First, we note that for
k_{n}=\frac{n}{s}(\alpha\log(n)+x)

we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(E_{n,j}(k_{n}))

\displaystyle=\Big(1-\frac{s}{n}\Big)^{k_{n}}=\bigg(\Big(1-\frac{s}{n}\Big)^{n/s}\bigg)^{\alpha\log(n)+x}\sim\ e^{-(\alpha\log(n)+x)}=\frac{\,e^{-x}}{n^{\alpha}},

(4.2)

where we write
f(n)\sim g(n)
for two functions
f,g:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}
if
\displaystyle{\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{f(n)}{g(n)}=1}
.
Thus,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Z_{n,s}(k_{n})]=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbb{P}(E_{n,j}(k_{n}))\sim e^{-x}n^{1-\alpha}.

(4.3)

In particular, we can find
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
, depending on
\alpha
, such that for all
n\geq n_{0}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(E_{n,j}(k_{n}))

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\,e^{-x}}{n^{\alpha}}

(4.4)

Similarly, we obtain

\displaystyle\Big(1-\frac{s}{n}\Big)^{k_{n}}-\Big(1-\frac{s}{n-1}\Big)^{k_{n}}

\displaystyle=\Big(1-\frac{s}{n}\Big)^{k_{n}}\bigg(1-\Big(\frac{(n-1-s)n}{(n-1)(n-s)}\Big)^{k_{n}}\bigg)

\displaystyle\leq\Big(1-\frac{s}{n}\Big)^{k_{n}}\bigg(-k_{n}\Big(\frac{(n-1-s)n}{(n-1)(n-s)}-1\Big)\bigg)

\displaystyle=\Big(1-\frac{s}{n}\Big)^{k_{n}}\,k_{n}\,\frac{(n-1)(n-s)-(n-1-s)n}{(n-1)(n-s)}

\displaystyle=\Big(1-\frac{s}{n}\Big)^{k_{n}}\frac{k_{n}\,s}{(n-1)(n-s)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2e^{-x}(\alpha\log(n)+x)}{n^{1+\alpha}},

(4.5)

for all
n\geq n_{1}
for some
n_{1}=n_{1}(\alpha,s)\in\mathbb{N}
,
where we have used that

\displaystyle 1-z^{k}\leq-k(z-1)

(4.6)

for
z\in(0,1)
.
Using that
\operatorname{var}({\bf 1}_{A})=\mathbb{P}(A)(1-\mathbb{P}(A))
and
\operatorname{Cov}({\bf 1}_{A},{\bf 1}_{B})=\mathbb{P}(A\cap B)-\mathbb{P}(A)\mathbb{P}(B)=\mathbb{P}(B)(\mathbb{P}(A|B)-\mathbb{P}(A))
, we see that

\displaystyle\operatorname{var}(Z_{n,s})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\operatorname{var}[\mathbf{1}_{E_{n,j}(k_{n})}]+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\\
i\neq j\end{subarray}}^{n}\operatorname{Cov}[\mathbf{1}_{E_{n,j}(k_{n})},\mathbf{1}_{E_{n,i}(k_{n})}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbb{P}(E_{n,j}(k_{n}))(1-\mathbb{P}(E_{n,j}(k_{n})))

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\\
i\neq j\end{subarray}}^{n}[\mathbb{P}(E_{n,i}(k_{n}))(\mathbb{P}(E_{n,j}(k_{n})\,|\,E_{n,i}(k_{n}))-\mathbb{P}(E_{n,j}(k_{n})))]

\displaystyle=\bigg(1-\Big(1-\frac{s}{n}\Big)^{k_{n}}+(n-1)\Big(\Big(1-\frac{s}{n-1}\Big)^{k_{n}}-\Big(1-\frac{s}{n}\Big)^{k_{n}}\Big)\bigg)\mathbb{E}[Z_{n,s}].

Applying now (
4.1
) we conclude that there exists a constant
c_{1}(s,x)\in(0,\infty)
such that for
n\geq\max\{n_{0},n_{1}\}
the lower variance bound

\displaystyle\operatorname{var}(Z_{n,s})

\displaystyle\geq\Big(1-\frac{2e^{-x}}{n^{\alpha}}-\frac{2e^{-x}\,(\alpha\log(n)+x)}{n^{\alpha}}\Big)\mathbb{E}[Z_{n,s}]\geq c_{1}(s,x)\,\mathbb{E}[Z_{n,s}],

(4.7)

holds.

4.2
Bounding
\operatorname{var}\big(\mathbb{E}[Z_{n,s}-Z^{I}_{n,s}|\mathcal{C}_{n}(k_{n})]\big)

For the variance of the conditional expectation on the right-hand side in (
4.1
) we obtain

\displaystyle\operatorname{var}\big(\mathbb{E}[Z_{n,s}-Z^{I}_{n,s}|\mathcal{C}_{n}(k_{n})]\big)=\operatorname{var}\Big(\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}_{E_{n,j}(k_{n})\cap F_{n,j}^{I}}|\mathcal{C}_{n}(k_{n})\Big]\Big)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}_{E_{n,j}(k_{n})\cap F_{n,j}^{I}}|\mathcal{C}_{n}(k_{n})]\Big)^{2}\Big]-\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}_{E_{n,j}(k_{n})\cap F_{n,j}^{I}}|\mathcal{C}_{n}(k_{n})]\Big]^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}_{E_{n,j}(k_{n})\cap F_{n,j}^{I}}|\mathcal{C}_{n}(k_{n})]^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle\quad+\Bigg(\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\\
i\neq j\end{subarray}}^{n}\,\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}_{E_{n,j}(k_{n})\cap F_{n,j}^{I}}|\mathcal{C}_{n}(k_{n})]\,\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}_{E_{n,i}(k_{n})\cap F_{n,i}^{I}}|\mathcal{C}_{n}(k_{n})]\Big]-\Big[\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}_{E_{n,j}(k_{n})\cap F_{n,j}^{I}}]\Big]^{2}\Bigg)

\displaystyle=:T_{1}+T_{2}.

(4.8)

We start by dealing with
T_{1}
. Note that conditionally on the event
E_{n,j}(k_{n})
we have

\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}_{F_{n,j}^{I}}|\mathcal{C}_{n}(k_{n})]=1-\Big(1-\frac{1}{n-s}\Big)^{|C_{I}|},

(4.9)

so that with (
4.6
) we obtain

\displaystyle T_{1}=\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}_{E_{n,j}(k_{n})}\Big(1-\Big(1-\frac{1}{n-s}\Big)^{|C_{I}|}\Big)^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{(n-s)^{2}}\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}_{E_{n,j}(k_{n})}|C_{I}|^{2}\Big].

Denoting by
D^{I}_{m}
the event that a particle is placed into cell
C_{I}
in the
m
th drawing for some
m\in\{1,\ldots,k_{n}\}
we see that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}_{E_{n,j}(k_{n})}|C_{I}|^{2}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big[\mathbf{1}_{E_{n,j}(k_{n})}\Big(\sum_{m=1}^{k_{n}}\mathbf{1}_{D_{m}^{I}}\Big)^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{m=1}^{k_{n}}\mathbf{1}_{E_{n,j}(k_{n})}\mathbf{1}_{D_{m}^{I}}\Big]+\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{m=1}^{k_{n}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r=1\\
r\neq m\end{subarray}}^{k_{n}}\mathbf{1}_{E_{n,j}(k_{n})}\mathbf{1}_{D_{m}^{I}}\mathbf{1}_{D_{r}^{I}}\Big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(E_{n,j}(k_{n}))\Big(\sum_{m=1}^{k_{n}}\mathbb{P}(D_{m}^{I}\big|E_{n,j}(k_{n}))+\sum_{m=1}^{k_{n}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r=1\\
r\neq m\end{subarray}}^{k_{n}}\mathbb{P}(D_{m}^{I}\cap D_{r}^{I}\big|E_{n,j}(k_{n}))\Big).

Since all
s
-placements occur with the same probability and conditioning on the event
E_{n,j}(k_{n})
simply means that we can only place particles into
n-1
cells, we have

\mathbb{P}(D_{m}^{I}\big|E_{n,j}(k_{n}))=\frac{s}{n-1}.

As the drawings are independent of each other we get

\displaystyle\sum_{m=1}^{k_{n}}\mathbb{P}(D_{m}^{I}\big|E_{n,j}(k_{n}))+\sum_{m=1}^{k_{n}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r=1\\
r\neq m\end{subarray}}^{k_{n}}\mathbb{P}(D_{m}^{I}\cap D_{r}^{I}\big|E_{n,j}(k_{n}))

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=1}^{k_{n}}\mathbb{P}(D_{m}^{I}\big|E_{n,j}(k_{n}))+\sum_{m=1}^{k_{n}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r=1\\
r\neq m\end{subarray}}^{k_{n}}\mathbb{P}(D_{m}^{I}\big|E_{n,j}(k_{n}))\mathbb{P}(D_{r}^{I}\big|E_{n,j}(k_{n}))=\frac{k_{n}s}{n-1}+\frac{k_{n}(k_{n}-1)s^{2}}{(n-1)^{2}}.

Using also (
4.2
) we obtain that there exists
a constant
c_{2}(s,x)\in(0,\infty)
such that we can bound
T_{1}
by

\displaystyle T_{1}\leq\Big(1-\frac{s}{n}\Big)^{k_{n}}\frac{n}{(n-s)^{2}}\Big(\frac{k_{n}s}{n-1}+\frac{k_{n}(k_{n}-1)s^{2}}{(n-1)^{2}}\Big)\leq c_{2}(s,x)\frac{\alpha^{2}\log(n)^{2}}{n^{1+\alpha}}.

(4.10)

To deal with the term
T_{2}
first note that by (
3.2
) and (
3.3
),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}_{E_{n,j}(k_{n})\cap F_{n,j}^{I}}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(E_{n,j}(k_{n}))\mathbb{P}(F_{n,j}^{I}|E_{n,j}(k_{n}))

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(E_{n,j}(k_{n}))\sum_{\ell=0}^{k_{n}}\mathbb{P}(F_{n,j}^{I}|E_{n,j}(k_{n})\cap\{|C_{I}|=\ell\})\mathbb{P}(C_{I}|=\ell|E_{n,j}(k_{n}))

\displaystyle=\Big(1-\frac{s}{n}\Big)^{k_{n}}\sum_{\ell=0}^{k_{n}}\Big(1-\big(1-\frac{1}{n-s}\big)^{\ell}\Big){k_{n}\choose\ell}\Big(\frac{s}{n-1}\Big)^{\ell}\Big(1-\frac{s}{n-1}\Big)^{k_{n}-\ell}

\displaystyle=\Big(1-\frac{s}{n}\Big)^{k_{n}}\Big(1-\sum_{\ell=0}^{k_{n}}\big(1-\frac{1}{n-s}\big)^{\ell}{k_{n}\choose\ell}\Big(\frac{s}{n-1}\Big)^{\ell}\Big(1-\frac{s}{n-1}\Big)^{k_{n}-\ell}\Big)

\displaystyle=\Big(1-\frac{s}{n}\Big)^{k_{n}}\Big(1-\Big(1-\frac{s}{(n-s)(n-1)}\Big)^{k_{n}}\Big).

(4.11)

Using (
4.9
) and slightly adapting (
3.3
), the first part of
T_{2}
can be handled in the following way:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}_{E_{n,j}(k_{n})\cap F_{n,j}^{I}}|\mathcal{C}_{n}(k_{n})]\,\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}_{E_{n,i}(k_{n})\cap F_{n,i}^{I}}|\mathcal{C}_{n}(k_{n})]\Big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big[\mathbf{1}_{E_{n,i}(k_{n})}\mathbf{1}_{E_{n,j}(k_{n})}\Big(1-\big(1-\frac{1}{n-s}\big)^{|C_{I}|}\Big)^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=0}^{k_{n}}\Big(1-\big(1-\frac{1}{n-s}\big)^{\ell}\Big)^{2}\,\mathbb{P}(|C_{I}|=\ell|E_{n,i}(k_{n})\cap E_{n,j}(k_{n}))\,\mathbb{P}(E_{n,i}(k_{n})\cap E_{n,j}(k_{n}))

\displaystyle=\Big(1-\frac{s}{n}\Big)^{k_{n}}\Big(1-\frac{s}{n-1}\Big)^{k_{n}}\sum_{\ell=0}^{k_{n}}\Big(1-\big(1-\frac{1}{n-s}\big)^{\ell}\Big)^{2}\,{k_{n}\choose\ell}\Big(\frac{s}{n-2}\Big)^{\ell}\Big(1-\frac{s}{n-2}\Big)^{k_{n}-\ell}

\displaystyle=\Big(1-\frac{s}{n}\Big)^{k_{n}}\Big(1-\frac{s}{n-1}\Big)^{k_{n}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\times\sum_{\ell=0}^{k_{n}}\Big(1-2\big(1-\frac{1}{n-s}\big)^{\ell}+\big(1-\frac{1}{n-s}\big)^{2\ell}\Big)\,{k_{n}\choose\ell}\Big(\frac{s}{n-2}\Big)^{\ell}\Big(1-\frac{s}{n-2}\Big)^{k_{n}-\ell}

\displaystyle=\Big(1-\frac{s}{n}\Big)^{k_{n}}\Big(1-\frac{s}{n-1}\Big)^{k_{n}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\times\Bigg(1-2\Big(1-\frac{s}{(n-2)(n-s)}\Big)^{k_{n}}+\Big(1-\frac{2s}{(n-2)(n-s)}+\frac{s}{(n-2)(n-s)^{2}}\Big)^{k_{n}}\Bigg),

independently of
i
and
j
. Combining this with (
4.2
) yields

\displaystyle T_{2}

\displaystyle\leq n^{2}\Big(1-\frac{s}{n}\Big)^{2k_{n}}\bigg[2\bigg(\Big(1-\frac{s}{(n-s)(n-1)}\Big)^{k_{n}}-\Big(1-\frac{s}{(n-s)(n-2)}\Big)^{k_{n}}\bigg)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+\Big(1-\frac{2s}{(n-s)(n-2)}+\frac{s}{(n-2)(n-s)^{2}}\Big)^{k_{n}}-\Big(1-\frac{s}{(n-s)(n-1)}\Big)^{2k_{n}}\bigg].

Now, there exists
n_{2}=n_{2}(\alpha,s)
such that

\displaystyle\Big(1-\frac{s}{(n-s)(n-1)}\Big)^{k_{n}}-\Big(1-\frac{s}{(n-s)(n-2)}\Big)^{k_{n}}\leq 2\frac{\alpha\log(n)+x}{n^{2}}

and

\displaystyle\Big(1-\frac{2s}{(n-s)(n-2)}+\frac{s}{(n-2)(n-s)^{2}}\Big)^{k_{n}}-\Big(1-\frac{s}{(n-s)(n-1)}\Big)^{2k_{n}}\leq 2\frac{\alpha\log(n)+x}{n^{2}}

holds for all
n\geq n_{2}
.
Combing this with (
4.2
) we can conclude that there exists a constant
c_{3}(s,x)\in(0,\infty)
such that for all
n\geq\max\{3,n_{2}\}
,

\displaystyle T_{2}\leq c_{3}(s,x)\frac{\alpha\log(n)}{n^{2\alpha}}.

(4.12)

Putting the bounds (
4.10
) and (
4.12
) into (
4.2
) we see that there exists a constant
c_{4}(s,x)\in(0,\infty)
such that

\displaystyle\operatorname{var}\big(\mathbb{E}[Z_{n,s}-Z^{I}_{n,s}|\mathcal{C}_{n}(k_{n})]\big)\leq c_{2}(s,x)\frac{\alpha^{2}\log(n)^{2}}{n^{1+\alpha}}+c_{3}(s,x)\frac{\alpha\log(n)}{n^{2\alpha}}\leq c_{4}(s,x)\frac{\alpha\log(n)}{n^{2\alpha}},

(4.13)

for all
n\geq\max\{3,n_{2}\}
, since
\alpha<1
by assumption.

Remark 5
.

Using (
4.2
) and the exact expressions for the probabilities appearing there, it can be shown that the order of (
4.13
) is optimal in the sense that one can find another constant
\tilde{c}_{4}(s,x)\in(0,\infty)
such that
\operatorname{var}(\mathbb{E}[Z_{n,s}-Z^{I}_{n,s}|\mathcal{C}_{n}(k_{n})])\geq\tilde{c}_{4}(s,x)\frac{\alpha\log(n)}{n^{2\alpha}}
for sufficiently large
n
.

4.3
Bounding
\mathbb{E}[(Z_{n,s}-Z^{I}_{n,s})^{2}]

For the second term in (
4.1
) it remains to bound
\mathbb{E}[(Z_{n,s}-Z^{I}_{n,s})^{2}]
. We have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[(Z_{n,s}-Z^{I}_{n,s})^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}[\Big(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq I\end{subarray}}^{n}\mathbf{1}_{E_{n,j}(k_{n})\cap F_{n,j}^{I}}-1\Big)^{2}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq I\end{subarray}}^{n}\mathbf{1}_{E_{n,j}(k_{n})\cap F_{n,j}^{I}}\Big)^{2}\Big]-2\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq I\end{subarray}}^{n}\mathbf{1}_{E_{n,j}(k_{n})\cap F_{n,j}}\Big]+1

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq I\end{subarray}}^{n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\\
i\neq j,I\end{subarray}}^{n}\mathbf{1}_{E_{n,j}(k_{n})\cap F_{n,j}^{I}}\mathbf{1}_{E_{n,i}(k_{n})\cap F_{n,i}^{I}}\Big]-\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq I\end{subarray}}^{n}\mathbf{1}_{E_{n,j}(k_{n})\cap F_{n,j}^{I}}\Big]+1,

(4.14)

where the
-1
in the first line comes from the artificially isolated cell with index
I
.
For the first sum note that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(E_{n,i}(k_{n})\cap F_{n,i}^{I}\cap E_{n,j}(k_{n})\cap F_{n,j}^{I})=\mathbb{P}(F^{I}_{n,i}(k_{n})\cap F_{n,j}^{I}|E_{n,j}(k_{n})\cap E_{n,i})\,\mathbb{P}(E_{n,j}(k_{n})\cap E_{n,j}),

where
\mathbb{P}(E_{n,j}(k_{n})\cap E_{n,j}(k_{n}))
can be bounded using (
3
) and (
4.2
).
To bound the remaining probability, we observe that similar to the considerations in (
4.2
) we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(F_{n,j}^{I}|E_{n,i}(k_{n})\cap E_{n,j}(k_{n}))

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=0}^{k_{n}}\mathbb{P}(F_{n,j}^{I}|E_{n,i}(k_{n})\cap E_{n,j}(k_{n})\cap\{|C_{I}|=\ell\})\mathbb{P}(|C_{I}|=\ell|E_{n,i}(k_{n})\cap E_{n,j}(k_{n}))

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=0}^{k_{n}}{k_{n}\choose\ell}\Big(1-\big(1-\frac{1}{n-s}\big)^{\ell}\Big)\Big(\frac{s}{n-2}\Big)^{\ell}\Big(1-\frac{s}{n-2}\Big)^{k_{n}-\ell}

\displaystyle=1-\Big(1-\frac{s}{(n-2)(n-s)}\Big)^{k_{n}},

independently of the choice of
i
and
j
.
So we are left to deal with
\mathbb{P}(F_{n,i}^{I}|E_{n,i}(k_{n})\cap E_{n,j}(k_{n})\cap F_{n,j}^{I})
. It holds that for
i\neq j

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(F_{n,i}^{I}|E_{n,j}(k_{n})\cap E_{n,i}(k_{n})\cap F_{n,j}^{I})\leq\mathbb{P}(F_{n,i}^{I}|E_{n,i}(k_{n})\cap E_{n,j}(k_{n})),

since the event
F_{n,j}^{I}
implies that at least one particle from cell
I
is placed into cell
C_{j},
reducing the chances of cell
C_{i}
receiving a particle. Hence,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(F_{n,i}^{I}\cap F_{n,j}^{I}|E_{n,i}(k_{n})\cap E_{n,j}(k_{n}))\leq\Big(1-\Big(1-\frac{s}{(n-2)(n-s)}\Big)^{k_{n}}\Big)^{2}

and we finally obtain that for
n\geq\max\{n_{0},s\}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq I\end{subarray}}^{n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\\
i\neq j,I\end{subarray}}^{n}\mathbf{1}_{E_{n,j}(k_{n})\cap F_{n,j}^{I}}\mathbf{1}_{E_{n,i}(k_{n})\cap F_{n,i}^{I}}\Big]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq I\end{subarray}}^{n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\\
i\neq j,I\end{subarray}}^{n}\mathbb{P}(F_{n,i}^{I}\cap F_{n,j}^{I}|E_{n,i}(k_{n})\cap E_{n,j}(k_{n}))\mathbb{P}(E_{n,i}(k_{n})\cap E_{n,j}(k_{n}))

\displaystyle\leq(n-1)(n-2)\Big(1-\Big(1-\frac{s}{(n-2)(n-s)}\Big)^{k_{n}}\Big)^{2}\Big(1-\frac{s}{n}\Big)^{2k_{n}}

\displaystyle\leq(n-1)(n-2)\Big(k_{n}\frac{s}{(n-2)(n-s)}\Big)^{2}\Big(1-\frac{s}{n}\Big)^{2k_{n}}

\displaystyle\leq(n-1)(n-2)\Big(\frac{3(s+1)(\alpha\log(n)+x)}{n}\Big)^{2}\Big(\frac{2e^{-x}\alpha\log(n)}{n^{\alpha}}\Big)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 36(s+1)^{2}e^{-2x}\frac{(\alpha\log(n)+x)^{2}}{n^{2\alpha}},

where we have used (
4.6
) to arrive at the third line.
Since according to (
4.2
) the negative sum in (
4.3
) is of order
n^{-(\alpha+1)}
, we conclude that there exists a constant
c_{5}(s,x)\in(0,\infty)
such that for all
n\geq\max\{n_{0},s\}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[(Z_{n,s}-Z^{I}_{n,s})^{2}]\leq c_{5}(s,x).

(4.15)

Combining the normal approximation bound (
4.1
) with the estimates (
4.3
), (
4.7
), (
4.13
) and (
4.15
) we arrive at

\displaystyle d_{W}(\tilde{Z}_{n,s},G)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\lambda_{n}}{c_{1}(s,x)\lambda_{n}}\sqrt{c_{4}(s,x)\frac{\alpha\log(n)}{n^{2\alpha}}}+\frac{\lambda_{n}}{(c_{1}(s,x)\lambda_{n})^{\frac{3}{2}}}c_{5}(s,x)

\displaystyle\leq\begin{cases}C(s,x)\frac{\sqrt{\log(n)}}{n^{\alpha}}&\text{ for }\alpha\in(0,\frac{1}{3}]\\[5.69046pt]
C(s,x)n^{-{1\over 2}(1-\alpha)}&\text{ for }\alpha\in(\frac{1}{3},1)\end{cases}

for some constant
C(s,x)\in(0,\infty)
and for all
n\geq N:=\max\{n_{0},n_{1},n_{2},3,s,e^{-x}\}
.
This proves Theorem
1
.∎

5
Proof of Theorem
3

As already mentioned in the introduction, rates of convergence towards a Poisson limit in the case
\alpha=1
can be concluded from
[
2
, Theorem 6.F]
. Nevertheless, we give an alternative self-contained proof using the coupling from Section
3
.

It follows from
[
13
, Theorem 4.13]
that the total variation distance between the number of empty cells in the coupon collector’s problem and a Poisson random variable
W
with parameter
\lambda_{n}=\mathbb{E}[Z_{n,s}(k_{n})]
can be bounded using the following inequality:

\displaystyle d_{TV}(Z_{n,s}(k_{n}),W)\leq\min\{1,\lambda_{n}\}\,\mathbb{E}[Z_{n,s}(k_{n})+1-Z_{n,s}^{I}(k_{n})].

(5.1)

For the expectation on the right the definitions of
Z_{n,s}(k_{n})
and
Z^{I}_{n,s}(k_{n})
yield

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Z_{n,s}(k_{n})+1-Z_{n,s}^{I}(k_{n})]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}_{E_{n,I}(k_{n})}]+\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{j\neq I}\mathbf{1}_{E_{n,j}(k_{n})}-\mathbf{1}_{E^{I}_{n,j}(k_{n})}\Big]

\displaystyle=\Big(1-\frac{s}{n}\Big)^{k}+(n-1)\bigg(\Big(1-\frac{s}{n}\Big)^{k}-\Big(1-\frac{s}{n-1}\Big)^{k}\bigg).

Combining (
4.2
) and (
4.1
) for
\alpha=1
with (
5.1
) yields

\displaystyle d_{TV}(Z_{n,s}(k_{n}),W)

\displaystyle\leq\min\{1,\lambda_{n}\}\,\mathbb{E}[Z_{n,s}(k_{n})+1-Z_{n,s}^{I}(k_{n})]

\displaystyle=\Big(1-\frac{s}{n}\Big)^{k}+(n-1)\bigg(\Big(1-\frac{s}{n}\Big)^{k}-\Big(1-\frac{s}{n-1}\Big)^{k}\bigg)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\,e^{-x}}{n}\Big(\log(n)+x+1\Big),

for
n\geq\tilde{N}:=\max\{n_{0},n_{1},e^{-(x+1)}\}
, which completes the proof of Theorem
3
.∎
