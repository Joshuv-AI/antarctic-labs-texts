Statistical properties of undulator radiation in the IOTA storage ring
Ihar Lobach, Valeri Lebedev, Sergei Nagaitsev, Aleksandr Romanov, Giulio Stancari, Alexander Valishev, Aliaksei Halavanau, Zhirong Huang, Kwang-Je Kim

Abstract
We study turn-by-turn fluctuations in the number of spontaneously emitted photons from an undulator, installed in the Integrable Optics Test Accelerator (IOTA) electron storage ring at Fermilab. A theoretical model is presented, showing the relative contributions due to the discrete nature of light emission and to the incoherent sum of fields from different electrons in the bunch. The model is compared with a previous experiment at Brookhaven and with new experiments we carried out at IOTA. Our experiments focused on the case of a large number of longitudinal and transverse radiation modes, a regime where photon shot noise is significant and the total magnitude of the fluctuations is very small. The experimental and data analysis techniques, required to reach the desired sensitivity, are detailed. We discuss how the model and the experiment provide insights into this emission regime, enable diagnostics of small beam sizes, and improve our understanding of beam lifetime in IOTA.



I
Introduction

In the last few decades there were several experiments regarding statistical properties of incoherent synchrotron radiation, produced by electron bunches in storage rings and linear accelerators
Teich et al. 1990
;
Sannibale et al. 2009
;
Catravas et al. 1999
;
Sajaev 2004
;
Sajaev 2000
. The fluctuation in the radiated energy (or the number of photons) from pulse to pulse was studied experimentally and theoretically. It was shown in
Sannibale et al. 2009
;
Catravas et al. 1999
that in some cases the rms electron bunch length can be measured via this fluctuation. Moreover, references
Sajaev 2004
;
Sajaev 2000
suggest that if the fluctuations in radiation spectrum are measured with a high resolution spectrometer, then even the shape of the electron bunch can be reconstructed. These observations, combined with the fact that fluctuations of the same nature are present in SASE FELs
Kim 1997a
;
Kim et al. 2017
;
Benson and Madey 1985
;
Saldin et al. 2013
;
Pellegrini et al. 2016
;
Becker and Zubairy 1982
, make the study of fluctuations in incoherent synchrotron radiation relevant for the understanding of beam dynamics and for beam diagnostics.

The number of photons radiated incoherently by an electron bunch in an external electromagnetic field (undulator, wiggler, dipole magnet, etc.) fluctuates from pass to pass due to the following two mechanisms
Goodman 2015
. The first mechanism is the photon shot noise, related to the quantum discrete nature of light. This effect would exist even if there was only one electron. Indeed, the electron would radiate light with Poisson statistics
Glauber 1963a
;
Glauber 1963b
;
Glauber 1951
. The second mechanism is due to the fact that the field produced by a bunch of electrons is an incoherent sum of fields produced by all the electrons in the bunch. If wave packets of radiation produced by different electrons overlap, the incoherent sum fluctuates from pass to pass because the positions of the electrons in the bunch change. In a storage ring, the effect arises because of betatron motion, synchrotron motion, radiation induced diffusion, etc.; in linacs, assuming exactly equal bunch charges, there is a new bunch of electrons at every pass, the positions of which are not correlated with the positions of electrons in the previous bunch.

For dense bunches, the fluctuations in the number of emitted photons are usually dominated by the incoherence contribution
Kim et al. 2017
, as it was the case in
Teich et al. 1990
;
Sajaev 2004
;
Sajaev 2000
;
Sannibale et al. 2009
;
Catravas et al. 1999
. In this paper, we present the results of studies of statistical properties of undulator radiation in the IOTA ring at Fermilab
Antipov et al. 2017
, where the contributions from both mechanisms are comparable. This also means that the fluctuations were very small (compared to
Teich et al. 1990
, for instance), and we present several critical improvements to the setup from
Teich et al. 1990
;
Sannibale et al. 2009
.

We start by reviewing the theory of fluctuations in synchrotron radiation. The theory is relevant for both storage rings and linear accelerators. However, below we assume a radiation coming from a single bunch in a storage ring, where the number of participating electrons does not fluctuate.
We derive an equation for the variance of the number of detected photons for a Gaussian electron bunch, taking into account the quantum efficiency of the detector. Then, the theoretical predictions are compared with the empirical data from
Teich et al. 1990
. Finally, we describe our experiment in IOTA
Lobach et al. 2019
, and how the empirical data from this experiment compare with our model.

II
Theoretical model

Consider incoherent synchrotron radiation (undulator, wiggler, bending-magnet, etc.), emitted by a Gaussian bunch consisting of many randomly located electrons. Let us assume that the synchrotron radiation is collected in a wide spectral range, and also in a large solid angle.

II.1
Quantum fluctuations

Whereas in general we consider a bunch with randomly located electrons, in this subsection we fix positions (phases) of all the electrons and derive the quantum contribution to the fluctuations. We take an ensemble average over random positions (phases) of the electrons in subsection
II.2
.

Conceptually, one can divide the detector into many detectors, each sensing only one mode of the produced radiation with the wave vector
\bm{k}
. The volume of the
\bm{k}
-space associated with this mode will be denoted by
d\bm{k}\equiv dk_{x}dk_{y}dk_{z}

L

L\rightarrow\infty
at some point, and all the sums over modes
\bm{k}
will be replaced by integrals. Upon this transition to integrals, it will also be valid to use
d\bm{k}=k^{2}dkd\Omega
, where
\Omega
is the solid angle.

It was shown in
Glauber 1963b
;
Glauber 1963a
that any classical current (corresponding to a negligible electron recoil) produces a coherent state of radiated electromagnetic field. The coherent state in the single mode
\bm{k}
is given by
Glauber 1963b

\ket{\alpha_{\bm{k}}}=e^{-\frac{1}{2}\left|\alpha_{\bm{k}}\right|^{2}}\sum_{n_{\bm{k}}}\frac{\alpha_{\bm{k}}^{n_{\bm{k}}}}{\sqrt{n_{\bm{k}}!}}\ket{n_{\bm{k}}},

(1)

where
\ket{n_{\bm{k}}}
is the state with
n_{\bm{k}}
photons in the mode
\bm{k}
and the exact formula for
\alpha_{\bm{k}}
is provided in
Glauber 1963b
. For our purposes, it is sufficient to mention that

\left|\alpha_{\bm{k}}\right|^{2}=\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{c}}^{\bm{k},d\bm{k}}=I_{\bm{k}}d\bm{k},

(2)

where
\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{c}}^{\bm{k},d\bm{k}}
is the quasi-classical number of photons emitted in the mode
\bm{k}
, and
I_{\bm{k}}
is the quasi-classical spectral-angular density of the number of emitted photons, defined in this paper by the second equality in Eq. (
2
). There are two orthogonal polarization components for each wavevector
\bm{k}
, usually denoted by
\sigma
and
\pi

Kim et al. 2017

In the coherent state Eq. (

\left|\alpha_{\bm{k}}\right|^{2}=I_{\bm{k}}d\bm{k}

\displaystyle\langle n_{\bm{k}}\rangle=\braket{\alpha_{\bm{k}}|\hat{n}_{\bm{k}}|\alpha_{\bm{k}}}=\left|\alpha_{\bm{k}}\right|^{2}=I_{\bm{k}}d\bm{k},

(3)

\displaystyle\mathrm{var}(n_{\bm{k}})=\braket{\alpha_{\bm{k}}|(\hat{n}_{\bm{k}}-\langle n_{\bm{k}}\rangle)^{2}|\alpha_{\bm{k}}}=\left|\alpha_{\bm{k}}\right|^{2}=I_{\bm{k}}d\bm{k},

(4)

where
\hat{n}_{\bm{k}}=\hat{a}_{\bm{k}}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{\bm{k}}
, with
\hat{a}_{\bm{k}}^{\dagger}
and
\hat{a}_{\bm{k}}

A conventional approach will be used to account for the
\bm{k}

\eta_{\bm{k}}

The beam splitter model for quantum efficiency of a non-ideal detector.

The input-output relations for the beam splitter take the form

\displaystyle\hat{b}_{\bm{k}}=\sqrt{\eta_{\bm{k}}}\hat{a}_{\bm{k}}+i\sqrt{1-\eta_{\bm{k}}}\hat{v}_{\bm{k}},

(5)

\displaystyle\hat{d}_{\bm{k}}=\sqrt{1-\eta_{\bm{k}}}\hat{v}_{\bm{k}}+i\sqrt{\eta_{\bm{k}}}\hat{a}_{\bm{k}},

(6)

where
\hat{a}_{\bm{k}}

corresponds to the second input port, which is in the vacuum state in this model,
\hat{b}_{\bm{k}}
and

\mathcal{N}_{\bm{k}}

Meda et al. 2017

\langle\mathcal{N}_{\bm{k}}\rangle={\vphantom{\braket{\alpha_{\bm{k}},0|\hat{b}_{\bm{k}}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{\bm{k}}|\alpha_{\bm{k}},0}}}_{a,v}\braket{\alpha_{\bm{k}},0|\hat{b}_{\bm{k}}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{\bm{k}}|\alpha_{\bm{k}},0}_{a,v}=\eta_{\bm{k}}\langle n_{\bm{k}}\rangle,

(7)

\mathrm{var}(\mathcal{N}_{\bm{k}})={\vphantom{\braket{\alpha_{\bm{k}},0|(\hat{b}_{\bm{k}}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{\bm{k}}-\langle\mathcal{N}_{\bm{k}}\rangle)^{2}|\alpha_{\bm{k}},0}}}_{a,v}\braket{\alpha_{\bm{k}},0|(\hat{b}_{\bm{k}}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{\bm{k}}-\langle\mathcal{N}_{\bm{k}}\rangle)^{2}|\alpha_{\bm{k}},0}_{a,v}=\\
\eta_{\bm{k}}\langle n_{\bm{k}}\rangle+\eta_{\bm{k}}^{2}(\mathrm{var}(n_{\bm{k}})-\langle n_{\bm{k}}\rangle).

(8)

In the second term in Eq. (
8
) one can see that it is important to have a high quantum efficiency to be able to observe the sub- or super-Poisson statistics
Chen 1999
;
Chen and Madey 2001
;
Madey 2014
of a quantum origin. However, for coherent states this term vanishes, and, using Eqs. (
3
) and (
4

\langle\mathcal{N}_{\bm{k}}\rangle=\mathrm{var}(\mathcal{N}_{\bm{k}})=\eta_{\bm{k}}I_{\bm{k}}d\bm{k}.

(9)

In fact, using Eq. (
5
) to find how the coherent state Eq. (
1
) is transformed by the beam splitter, one can show that the output state will be a coherent state, and the number of detected photons
\mathcal{N}_{\bm{k}}
will obey the Poisson statistics as well as

does. Furthermore, since a sum of independent random Poisson variables is Poissonian

, the total number of detected photons
\mathcal{N}
,

\mathcal{N}=\sum\limits_{\bm{k}}\mathcal{N}_{\bm{k}},

(10)

will obey the Poisson distribution with the mean and the variance given by

\langle\mathcal{N}\rangle=\mathrm{var}(\mathcal{N})=\int\eta_{\bm{k}}I_{\bm{k}}d\bm{k}=\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{c}},

(11)

where
\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{c}}
is the total number of detected photons, calculated in the quasi-classical model, the integration is performed over all
\bm{k}
. In Eq. (
11
) and below in this paper,
\langle\mathcal{N}\rangle
denotes the average of the random variable
\mathcal{N}
, not to be confused with
\langle n_{\bm{k}}\rangle
in Eq. (
3
) or
\langle\mathcal{N}_{\bm{k}}\rangle
in Eq. (
7
), where it denotes the expectation values of quantum operators
\hat{a}_{\bm{k}}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{\bm{k}}
and
\hat{b}_{\bm{k}}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{\bm{k}}
in corresponding coherent states. All sources of losses can be incorporated into
\eta_{\bm{k}}
, such as the detector’s quantum efficiency, losses in focusing lenses (if used), in the vacuum chamber windows, or due to the finite detector acceptance (
\eta_{\bm{k}}
can be set to zero for radiation angles that are not collected by the detector). If a spectral filter is used, its transmission function can be incorporated into
\eta_{\bm{k}}
and polarization filters can be accounted for in a similar manner.

In
Glauber 1951
, it was shown that the total number of photons emitted (not detected) by a classical current obeys the Poisson distribution. Equation (
11
) is in agreement with this result, and extends it to the counts in a non-ideal detector. It is also noteworthy that for
\langle\mathcal{N}\rangle\gg 1
the distribution for
\mathcal{N}
is essentially Gaussian.

Equation (
11
) is in agreement with intuitive understanding and is usually taken for granted. In the derivations presented above, we merely reviewed the proof of Eq. (
11
) for synchrotron radiation from the quantum optics perspective.

II.2
Classical fluctuations

The results of the previous subsection can be summarized as follows

\mathcal{N}=\Delta\left(\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{c}}\right)+\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{c}},

(12)

where
\mathcal{N}
is the number of photons detected at a certain turn;
\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{c}}
is the quasi-classical prediction for the number of detected photons produced by an electron bunch with fixed phases;
\Delta(\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{c}})
is a random variable describing the quantum fluctuation around the mean value
\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{c}}
. From Eq. (
11
) it follows that

\displaystyle\langle\Delta\left(\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{c}}\right)\rangle=0,

(13)

\displaystyle\mathrm{var}(\Delta\left(\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{c}}\right))=\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{c}}.

(14)

In this subsection we take into account the fact that in reality in a storage ring electron phases are random each turn. Hence,
\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{c}}
changes as well. One can still use Eq. (
12
) to represent the number of detected photons per turn, and we will use the same symbol for this variable
\mathcal{N}
in this subsection. However, it should be understood that the random variable
\mathcal{N}
in this subsection is different from that in Subsec.
II.1
. In this subsection,
\mathcal{N}
incorporates both the quantum fluctuation
\Delta\left(\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{c}}\right)
and the classical fluctuation of
\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{c}}
from turn to turn.

One can calculate
\mathrm{var}(\mathcal{N})
by taking the variance of Eq. (
12
):

\mathrm{var}(\mathcal{N})=\mathrm{var}(\Delta\left(\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{c}}\right)+\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{c}}).

(15)

We would like to use the fact that

\mathrm{var}(a+b)=\mathrm{var}(a)+\mathrm{var}(b)

(16)

for independent
a
and
b
. However,
\Delta\left(\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{c}}\right)
and
\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{c}}
are, in general, not independent. Nonetheless, if, on average, a large number of photons is detected per turn
\langle\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{c}}\rangle\gg 1
, then
\Delta\left(\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{c}}\right)
can be approximated by
\Delta\left(\langle\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{c}}\rangle\right)
, which is independent of
\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{c}}
. In this approximation, Eq. (
15
) becomes

\mathrm{var}(\mathcal{N})=\mathrm{var}(\Delta\left(\langle\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{c}}\rangle\right))+\mathrm{var}(\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{c}}).

(17)

Equation (
14
) can be used in Eq. (
17
) to obtain

\mathrm{var}(\mathcal{N})=\langle\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{c}}\rangle+\mathrm{var}(\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{c}}),

(18)

where
\langle\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{c}}\rangle
can be replaced with
\langle\mathcal{N}\rangle
according to Eqs. (
12
) and (
13

Equation (
18

;
Kim 1997b

\mathrm{var}(\mathcal{N})=\langle\mathcal{N}\rangle+\frac{1}{M}\langle\mathcal{N}\rangle^{2},

(19)

where the parameter
M
was introduced. In this paper it is defined as

\frac{1}{M}\equiv\frac{\mathrm{var}(\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{c}})}{\langle\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{c}}\rangle^{2}},

(20)

however, it can be identified with the number of coherent modes defined in
Kim et al. 2017
;
Kim 1989
, therefore we will use this name for the parameter
M
from now on.

In this subsection, we find
M
by explicitly calculating
\mathrm{var}(\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{c}})=\langle\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}\rangle-\langle\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{c}}\rangle^{2}
and using Eq. (
20
). To begin with, we introduce
I^{(1)}_{\bm{k}}
, i.e., the quasi-classical spectral-angular density of the number of emitted photons for the case when there is only one electron in the storage ring, by the following relation

\frac{d\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{c}}^{(1)}}{d\bm{k}}=\eta_{\bm{k}}I^{(1)}_{\bm{k}}.

(21)

Then, the total quasi-classical number of photons detected in this case is given by

\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{c}}^{(1)}=\int d\bm{k}\eta_{\bm{k}}I^{(1)}_{\bm{k}}.

(22)

In the derivations below, we will assume that the beam divergence is negligible compared to the radiation divergence
Kim et al. 2017
;
Kim 1989
;
Sannibale et al. 2009

\sigma_{x^{\prime}},\sigma_{y^{\prime}}\ll\sigma_{r^{\prime}}.

(23)

Also, it is assumed that the electrons’ momentum spread
\sigma_{p}
is sufficiently small, so that all the electrons in the bunch produce radiation with approximately the same spectrum

\left|\frac{\partial I^{(1)}_{\bm{k}}}{\partial p}\right|\sigma_{p}\ll\left|I^{(1)}_{\bm{k}}\right|.

(24)

If conditions (
23
) and (
24
) are fulfilled (typical for an electron storage ring,
Teich et al. 1990
;
Lobach et al. 2019
;
Sannibale et al. 2009
), one can use the following formula for
\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{c}}

;
Kim 1989
;
Sannibale et al. 2009

\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{c}}=\int d\bm{k}\eta_{\bm{k}}I^{(1)}_{\bm{k}}\left|\sum\limits_{m}e^{i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{r}_{m}}\right|^{2},

(25)

where

, with

being the number of electrons in the bunch;
\bm{r}_{m}\equiv(x_{m},y_{m},-ct_{m})

and

(\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{c}})^{2}=\int d\bm{k}_{1}d\bm{k}_{2}\eta_{\bm{k}_{1}}I^{(1)}_{\bm{k}_{1}}\eta_{\bm{k}_{2}}I^{(1)}_{\bm{k}_{2}}\times\\
\left|\sum\limits_{m}e^{i\bm{k}_{1}\cdot\bm{r}_{m}}\right|^{2}\left|\sum\limits_{n}e^{i\bm{k}_{2}\cdot\bm{r}_{n}}\right|^{2}.

(26)

We consider a Gaussian distribution of particles in the bunch along

and

,
\sigma_{y}
and

\langle\left|\sum\limits_{m}e^{i\bm{k}_{1}\cdot\bm{r}_{m}}\right|^{2}\left|\sum\limits_{n}e^{i\bm{k}_{2}\cdot\bm{r}_{n}}\right|^{2}\rangle=N_{e}^{2}+N_{e}(N_{e}-1)e^{-\bm{\Delta}_{12}\cdot\bm{\Sigma}}+\\

where the average is taken over each electron’s position,

Further, assuming that

\left|\frac{\partial I^{(1)}_{\bm{k}}}{\partial k_{z}}\right|\frac{1}{\sigma_{z}}\ll\left|I^{(1)}_{\bm{k}}\right|,

,

, and

. However, the form of the distribution can be suggested by a simple qualitative argument when the number of longitudinal modes

\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{c}}
can be found in

;
Kim et al. 2017
;

;
Kim 1997b
;
Catravas et al. 1999
;

It is practically impossible to find the exact reason for this disagreement now, because the measurements were taken about three decades ago, and it is difficult to reconstruct the exact conditions of the experiment. In part, this is what motivated us to carry out an independent study in IOTA.

III.2
IOTA experiment

The Integrable Optics Test Accelerator (IOTA), located at Fermilab’s Accelerator Science and Technology (FAST) facility, is a small storage ring designed for experiments with both electron and proton beams. We refer the reader to Ref.
Antipov et al. 2017
and Table
1
for the description of the ring and its parameters. In this experiment, the IOTA ring operated with electrons only.

In IOTA, the values of the parameters of the electron bunch and of the undulator are such that the quantum and the incoherence contributions to radiation fluctuations are comparable, whereas the latter is usually dominant. Also, due to significant intrabeam scattering, the bunch dimensions strongly depend on beam current. Through Eq. (
32
), the fluctuations themselves therefore acquire a complex dependence on beam current.

\Delta

\Sigma

, i.e., if the pulses in IOTA were perfectly identical, there would still be pulses in
\Delta
-channel with amplitude of
\kappa\approx$0.7\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}$
of the amplitude of the pulses in
\Sigma
-channel. However, this effect was easy to take into account.

To make a theoretical prediction for
M
and, consequently, for
\mathrm{var}(\mathcal{N})
, we had to know the dimensions of the electron bunch in IOTA as a function of bunch charge. To be able to estimate the bunch dimensions for the range of beam current values in our experiments, we developed a theoretical model of bunch evolution, including several effects and consistent with the available experimental data.

In addition to synchrotron radiation damping and radiation diffusion, there are three main effects, determining the bunch parameters in IOTA, namely, intrabeam scattering
Chao et al. 2013
, multiple Coulomb scattering in the background gas
Reiser and O’Shea 1994
, and longitudinal bunch self-focusing
Haïssinski 1973
due to space-charge. We use the method described in
Nagaitsev 2005
;
Chao et al. 2013
to compute emittance growth rates associated with intrabeam scattering. Let us define the intrabeam scattering growth rates in momentum
p
, in the horizontal
x
, and in the vertical
y
-directions as

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \frac{1}{T_{p}}=\frac{1}{\sigma_{p}^{2}}\frac{d\sigma_{p}^{2}}{dt},\quad\frac{1}{T_{x}}=\frac{1}{\epsilon_{x}}\frac{d\epsilon_{x}}{dt},\quad\frac{1}{T_{y}}=\frac{1}{\epsilon_{y}}\frac{d\epsilon_{y}}{dt},\end{aligned}

(36)

In our simulations, we keep the longitudinal bunch size constant,
\sigma_{z}=$38\text{\,}\mathrm{c}\mathrm{m}$
. This value was determined experimentally with a wall-current monitor and it remained approximately constant for any
I_{\mathrm{beam}}>$0.65\text{\,}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{A}$
. Most likely, this is due to the above mentioned self-focusing of the electron bunch in IOTA. The self-focusing also causes some deviations from Gaussian longitudinal bunch profile. However, Eq. (
32
) can still be used if one substitutes
\sigma_{z}
with
\sigma_{z}^{\mathrm{eff}}
, see Eq. (
33
). Since in our model we assume one specific constant value of
\sigma_{z}=$38\text{\,}\mathrm{c}\mathrm{m}$
, it will be valid only for
I_{\mathrm{beam}}>$0.65\text{\,}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{A}$
(
\langle\mathcal{N}\rangle>$1.75\text{\times}{10}^{6}\text{\,}$
).

The horizontal and vertical betatron tunes were decoupled and linear coupling was minimized. For typical bunch dimensions, the intrabeam scattering growth rate in the
y
-direction is much smaller than the synchrotron radiation damping rate
Lee 2018
. Therefore, vertical emittance is primarily determined by multiple Coulomb scattering in the residual gas. Moreover, this implies that vertical emittance
\epsilon_{y}
, and, consequently, vertical bunch size
\sigma_{y}
do not depend on beam current.

In the
x
-direction, the horizontal rms emittance at zero beam current, determined by quantum fluctuations, is
\epsilon_{x0}=$3.6\text{\times}{10}^{-2}\text{\,}\mu\mathrm{m}$
. The contribution from multiple Coulomb scattering is negligible compared to that from quantum fluctuations. For
I_{\mathrm{beam}}>$0.65\text{\,}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{A}$
, both the contribution from quantum fluctuations, and the contribution from multiple Coulomb scattering are negligible compared to the intrabeam scattering growth rate (it will be shown quantitatively below), and, hence, horizontal emittance
\epsilon_{x}
is defined solely by the balance between the synchrotron radiation damping rate
1/\tau_{x}
(see Table
1
) and the intrabeam scattering growth rate (
1/T_{x}
):

\frac{1}{\tau_{x}}=\frac{1}{T_{x}}.

(37)

In the longitudinal direction, the contribution from quantum fluctuations could in principle be neglected, since it was about one order of magnitude smaller than that from intrabeam scattering, but it was not difficult to account for it
Bane et al. 2002
, therefore it was taken into consideration. Thus, the rms momentum spread
\sigma_{p}
was determined by the balance between synchrotron radiation damping on one side and intrabeam scattering growth rate and quantum fluctuations on the other side:

\frac{\sigma_{p}^{2}}{\tau_{p}}=\frac{\sigma_{p}^{2}}{T_{p}}+\frac{\sigma_{p0}^{2}}{\tau_{p}},

(38)

where
\sigma_{p0}=$8.4\text{\times}{10}^{-5}\text{\,}$
is the momentum spread due to synchrotron radiation alone, in the absence of intrabeam scattering;
1/\tau_{p}
is the synchrotron radiation damping rate, see Table
1
.

At this point in our analysis, we have three unknowns, namely,
\epsilon_{x}
,
\epsilon_{y}
,
\sigma_{p}
and only two equations, i.e., Eqs. (
37
) and (
38
). We do not have an equation for the
y
-direction, since we do not know the exact composition of the background gas.

To resolve this uncertainty, we recorded several optical images of dipole-magnet synchrotron radiation from a circulating bunch at
I_{\mathrm{beam}}=$1.3\text{\,}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{A}$
. From these images and from the known betatron and dispersion functions, it was possible to determine the ratio of transverse emittances,
\epsilon_{y}/\epsilon_{x}=$9.5\text{\times}{10}^{-2}\text{\,}$
. Given this constraint, we had two equations and two unknowns at
I_{\mathrm{beam}}=$1.3\text{\,}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{A}$
. Therefore, we were able to find all the parameters of the bunch at this value of beam current,
\epsilon_{x}=$0.32\text{\,}\mu\mathrm{m}$
,
\epsilon_{y}=$31\text{\,}\mathrm{n}\mathrm{m}$
,
\sigma_{p}=$3.1\text{\times}{10}^{-4}\text{\,}$
,
\sigma_{x}=$815\text{\,}\mu\mathrm{m}$
,
\sigma_{y}=$75\text{\,}\mu\mathrm{m}$
,
\sigma_{z}=$38\text{\,}\mathrm{c}\mathrm{m}$
. These are the values given in Table
1
. This value of the beam current is marked by an orange diamond in Fig.

b and by a green vertical line in Fig.

III.2.4
Discussion

In Fig.
9
a the parameter
M
is constant. Since the measurements are taken at one specific value of beam current, the dimensions of the electron bunch are also the same during measurements with different neutral density filters. Therefore, the
M
parameter must also stay the same. The red solid curve and the blue dashed curve in Fig.
9
a are parabolas of the form of Eq. (
19
) with a fixed
M
.

However, in Fig.
9
b, the
M
parameter changes significantly, i.e., from
M=$3.5\text{\times}{10}^{6}\text{\,}$
at
\langle\mathcal{N}\rangle=$3.5\text{\times}{10}^{6}\text{\,}$
, to
M=$4.4\text{\times}{10}^{6}\text{\,}$
at
\langle\mathcal{N}\rangle=$1.1\text{\times}{10}^{7}\text{\,}$
, due to the changes in bunch dimensions. Thus, the red solid curve in Fig.
9
b is no longer a parabola.

The variation of
M
is related to one possible application, namely, estimating the bunch dimensions by looking at the fluctuations in synchrotron radiation in a storage ring. Clearly, in general, the amplitude of fluctuations depends on all three bunch sizes,
\sigma_{x}
,
\sigma_{y}
,
\sigma_{z}
. Nonetheless, in some cases additional constraints or relations may be available. For example, the ratio of transverse emittances may be known; or some bunch dimensions may be easily measurable, e.g.,
\sigma_{z}
in IOTA is large and it can be readily measured with a wall-current monitor. The method of estimating the bunch dimensions by measuring the fluctuations in synchrotron radiation may be especially useful when one of the bunch dimensions is very small, and it is difficult to measure it with conventional methods. Successful measurements of longitudinal bunch size with this technique were reported in
Sannibale et al. 2009
;
Catravas et al. 1999
. Moreover, if fluctuations data are available in a wide spectral range, the longitudinal bunch profile may be reconstructed
Sajaev 2004
;
Sajaev 2000
. However, it should be understood that for this method to work the radiation must be incoherent, i.e., in order to measure
\sigma_{z}
, the wavelength of the radiation should be significantly smaller than
\sigma_{z}
. In the opposite limit,
M_{L}
is equal to one, and the fluctuations are insensitive to
\sigma_{z}
.

In Fig.
9
a,b, one can see that the Poisson contribution (green dashed line) is comparable with the incoherence contribution (second term in Eq. (
19
)). Usually the incoherence contribution is dominant
Kim et al. 2017
.
There are several reasons why the two terms in Eq. (
19
) were comparable in IOTA: the small number of undulator periods
N_{u}=10
; a relatively small undulator parameter
K_{u}=1
; a relatively low beam current
I_{\mathrm{beam}}<$4\text{\,}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{A}$
(which means small
\langle\mathcal{N}\rangle
); and a relatively large
\sigma_{z}=$38\text{\,}\mathrm{c}\mathrm{m}$
(which means large
M_{L}
).
To our knowledge, the experiment in IOTA is the only one where the Poisson contribution was significant in undulator radiation, as opposed to
Teich et al. 1990
;
Sajaev 2004
;
Sajaev 2000
;
Catravas et al. 1999
, for example. However, in bending-magnet radiation, a situation similar to ours (with a considerable Poisson term) was observed in
Sannibale et al. 2009
.

IV
Conclusions and outlook

We derived relations (Eqs. (
19
) and (
32
)) to predict the fluctuations
\mathrm{var}(\mathcal{N})
in the incoherent synchrotron radiation for a Gaussian electron bunch in undulators, wigglers and bending-magnets. The formulas properly take into account the discrete nature of light and the quantum efficiency of the detector, which is in general a function of the radiation wavelength.
A spectral filter with any transmission function can be incorporated by including a transmission function into
\eta_{\bm{k}}
in Eq. (
32
). The detector acceptance can be taken into account by setting
\eta_{\bm{k}}
to zero outside of a given angular range.

The predicted variance vs. radiation intensity was compared with the empirical data from a previous experiment at Brookhaven
Teich et al. 1990
for the case of wiggler radiation with dominant incoherence contribution
and with new experimental data from IOTA for the case of comparable quantum
and incoherence contributions.
In
Teich et al. 1990
, the photoelectron count mean
\langle\mathcal{N}\rangle
was varied mainly by using different neutral density filters. In our experiment, in addition to varying
\langle\mathcal{N}\rangle
with different neutral density filters, we also varied
\langle\mathcal{N}\rangle
by changing the beam current in a wide range. The latter set of data was also used to refine the model of intrabeam scattering in IOTA.

The fact that the quantum Poisson contribution to fluctuations was significant in IOTA implied that the value of fluctuations was very small, about two orders of magnitude smaller than in
Teich et al. 1990
. Accordingly, we introduced several critical improvements to the experimental setup that dramatically increased the measurement sensitivity. In particular, we used a comb filter with a delay equal to one IOTA revolution and a special noise filtering algorithm.

In our present experimental configuration, the electron bunch size, undulator radiation direction, and the photodetector circuit parameters were the main sources of uncertainty.
Future experiments in IOTA with better diagnostics and control of the beam parameters may yield more stringent comparisons between model and data and deeper insights into these phenomena.

The cleanest way to compare theory and experiment is to collect the fluctuations data at fixed beam current, as in Fig.
9
a. In this case, the parameter
M
is constant, and provides an indirect measurement of the bunch dimensions.

In the future, we plan to use a larger electron bunch (larger
M
) and consider the case when the quantum Poisson contribution is dominant, i.e., to observe the green dashed line in Fig.
9
a,b experimentally. This will help calibrate the photodetector circuit, since the slope of the line is determined by the capacitance
C_{\mathrm{f}}
.

As it was pointed out in
Sajaev 2004
;
Sajaev 2000
;
Catravas et al. 1999
;
Sannibale et al. 2009
, the fluctuations in synchrotron radiation can be used to make measurements of the bunch length on a picosecond scale, and the proof of principle experiments were successful. In IOTA, the longitudinal bunch size is relatively large,
\sigma_{z}=$38\text{\,}\mathrm{c}\mathrm{m}$
, and can be easily measured with a wall-current monitor. On the other hand, the transverse bunch size can be quite small, down to a few tens of microns, where it may be hard to measure by conventional methods. The number of coherent modes
M
in undulator radiation in IOTA is very sensitive to the transverse bunch size. Therefore, it may be possible to estimate the transverse bunch dimensions from the fluctuations of the number of detected photons.

Appendix A
Estimation of number of coherent modes

An order of magnitude estimate of the number of coherent modes
M
can be made as the ratio of the radiation phase space volume
\Omega
and the coherent phase space volume
\Omega_{R}

Sannibale et al. 2009
;
Kim et al. 2017
:

M=\frac{\Omega}{\Omega_{R}},

(39)

where

\Omega_{R}=1/\left(2k\right)^{3},

(40)

\Omega=\sqrt{1/\left(2k\right)^{2}+\epsilon_{x}^{2}}\sqrt{1/\left(2k\right)^{2}+\epsilon_{y}^{2}}\\
\sqrt{1/\left(2k\right)^{2}+\epsilon_{z}^{2}},

(41)

where
\epsilon_{z}=\sigma_{z}\sigma_{\omega}/\omega
;
k
is the magnitude of the wave vector,
\epsilon_{x}
and
\epsilon_{y}
are transverse emittances, and
\sigma_{\omega}/\omega
describes the width of the radiation spectrum. It is approximately equal to the inverse of the number of undualtor periods
1/N_{\mathrm{u}}
.

In IOTA, for the fundamental harmonic of undulator radiation,
1/(2k)=$8.6\text{\times}{10}^{-8}\text{\,}\mathrm{m}$
,
\epsilon_{x}=$3.2\text{\times}{10}^{-7}\text{\,}\mathrm{m}$
,
\epsilon_{y}=$3.1\text{\times}{10}^{-8}\text{\,}\mathrm{m}$
,
\epsilon_{z}=$0.38\text{\,}\mathrm{m}$\times 1/10=$3.8\text{\times}{10}^{-2}\text{\,}\mathrm{m}$
. With these values, Eq. (
39
) gives
M=$1.8\text{\times}{10}^{6}\text{\,}$
. This number sets the scale for the expected values of
M
obtained in Subsec.
III.2.3
by using Eq. (
32
).

The measurement system was bench-tested with a special light source, which consisted of a fast laser diode (
1064\text{\,}\mathrm{n}\mathrm{m}

We also varied the mean photoelectron count (and photoelectron count variance) by placing various neutral density filters between the measurement system and the test light source. We believe that the fluctuations in the number of emitted photons in the test light source were mostly created by the jitter in the pulse generator amplitude. The unknown
\mathrm{var}(\mathcal{N})
of the test light source was determined experimentally in our bench tests. A typical waveform for
\Delta
- and
\Sigma
-channels for the test light source without any neutral density filters is shown in Fig.
11
a. The steps in
\Delta
- and
\Sigma
-signals at
\sim$80\text{\,}\mathrm{n}\mathrm{s}$
after the main peaks are likely produced by signal reflections and imperfections of the hybrid, which is designed to work at frequencies between
2\text{\,}\mathrm{M}\mathrm{H}\mathrm{z}
and
2\text{\,}\mathrm{G}\mathrm{H}\mathrm{z}
. The IOTA pulses come at
7.5\text{\,}\mathrm{M}\mathrm{H}\mathrm{z}
, which is close to the lower bandwidth limit of the hybrid. The signal at the integrator output does not show these steps, see Fig.
6
.

One can see in Fig.
11
a that the comb-filter technique works rather well. The amplitudes of the pulses in the
\Delta
-channel fluctuate significantly from pulse to pulse. And the range of these fluctuations is much larger than the noise in the
\Delta
-channel. Therefore, by analyzing these fluctuations for 11,000 periods, the relative fluctuation of photoelectron count for the test light source was quite reliably determined to be
\theta\equiv\mathrm{var}(\mathcal{N})/\langle\mathcal{N}\rangle^{2}=$3.35\text{\times}{10}^{-6}\text{\,}$
.

However, in the actual experiment with the undulator radiation in IOTA, the fluctuations in the pulse amplitudes in the
\Delta
-channel were smaller than for the test light source, see Fig.
11
b. Moreover, they were smaller than the noise in the
\Delta
-channel. Therefore, it was necessary to develop a method to filter out the instrumental noise and to extract the actual fluctuations of the photoelectron count.

The idea of the method is the following.
First, we analyze the
\Sigma
-channel to determine the period with which the pulses arrive with very high precision (
>7
significant figures). In IOTA, it is the revolution time (
133\text{\,}\mathrm{n}\mathrm{s}
); in the test light source, it is the period in the pulse generator, which was chosen to be
133\text{\,}\mathrm{n}\mathrm{s}
as well. Then, all the data in
\Sigma
- and
\Delta
-channel are mapped onto a single period. That is, the time stamp of each sample is mapped into the remainder of a division operation between itself and the IOTA revolution period. Further, these data are binned along the time axis into about
2660
bins, corresponding to the sampling period of the oscilloscope (
50\text{\,}\mathrm{p}\mathrm{s}
). The bins are numbered by index
i=1,\ldots,2660
; IOTA revolutions are numbered by index
n=1,\ldots,11000
.
After this procedure, one has

\displaystyle\Sigma_{i,n}=V_{i}(1+\delta_{n})+V_{i}(1+\delta_{n-1})+\mu\Delta_{i,n}+\xi_{i,n},

(42)

\displaystyle\Delta_{i,n}=V_{i}(1+\delta_{n})-V_{i}(1+\delta_{n-1})+\kappa\Sigma_{i,n}+\nu_{i,n},

(43)

where
V_{i}
represents the pulse signal from the integrator (up to a certain factor due to attenuation in signal splitter and hybrid) averaged over 11,000 IOTA revolutions;
\delta_{n}
and
\delta_{n-1}
are the relative fluctuations with respect to the average pulse signal in
n
th and
(n-1)
th turns, respectively; the parameters
\mu
and
\kappa
characterize the cross-talk between
\Sigma
- and
\Delta
-channels;
\xi_{i,n}
and
\nu_{i,n}
are the noise contributions in
\Sigma
- and
\Delta
-channels, respectively. Note that the noise contributions are assumed to be independent of the amplitudes of
\Delta
- and
\Sigma
-signals.

For convenience, we also introduce the averaged sum signal
\Sigma_{i}=2V_{i}
. Hence, Eq. (
43
) can be approximated as

\Delta_{i,n}=\frac{1}{2}\Sigma_{i}(\delta_{n}-\delta_{n-1})+\kappa\Sigma_{i}+\nu_{i,n}.

(44)

If one fixes index
i
in Eq. (
44
) and takes the variance with respect to index
n
, the following relation is obtained

\mathrm{var}(\Delta_{i})=\frac{1}{2}\Sigma_{i}^{2}\mathrm{var}(\delta)+\mathrm{var}(\nu_{i}).

(45)

The left-hand side of Eq. (
45

12

i

On the right-hand side of Eq. (
45

The noise filtering algorithm can remove noise that is independent of the amplitude of the signal and whose rms amplitude is constant with time. Sources of such contributions to noise are, for example, oscilloscope, op-amp in the integrator, photodiode, and most external noise sources. However, it is important to emphasize that the measurements with the test light source were performed in a lab, not in the IOTA ring enclosure. Therefore, we cannot completely eliminate the possibility that in the IOTA ring enclosure the results of our measurements were affected by some sources of nonlinear time-dependent noise.
