Curves on complete intersections and measures of irrationality
Nathan Chen, Benjamin Church, and Junyan Zhao with an Appendix by Mohan Swaminathan

Abstract
. We study the minimal degrees and gonalities of curves on complete intersections. We prove that the degree of any curve on a general complete intersection X ⊆ ℙ N X\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{N} of large multidegree is bounded from below by the degree of X X . As an application, we answer a problem of Bastianelli–De Poi–Ein–Lazarsfeld–Ullery on measures of irrationality for complete intersections.

1.
Introduction

Given a complex projective variety
X\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{N}
, it is always covered by curves obtained by taking intersections with linear subspaces. Often it is fruitful to ask: are these the “simplest” curves on
X
? More precisely, we ask if there are curves of smaller degree, genus, or gonality. For
X
a general complete intersection, we make a significant step towards showing there are none. This verifies two open conjectures regarding curves on general complete intersections.

Degrees of curves

Our first result establishes a folklore conjecture (cf.
[
41
]
and
[
9
, Conjecture 3.10]
).

Theorem A
.

Let
X\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n+r}
be a general complete intersection variety of dimension
n\geq 1
cut out by polynomials of degrees
d_{1},\ldots,d_{r}\geq 2n-1
. Then any curve
C\subseteq X
satisfies

\deg(C)\ \geq\ (d_{1}-2n+2)\cdots(d_{r}-2n+2).

Moreover, there exists
1
1

1

In fact one can choose
N=2^{27r(n+r)^{2}}\cdot(n+r)^{6(n+r)}
.
an integer
N\coloneqq N(n,r)
such that if
d_{1},\dots,d_{r}\geq N
, then

\deg(C)\ \geq\ d_{1}\cdots d_{r}.

By slicing with hyperplanes, note that the same degree bound appearing in Theorem
A
holds for any positive dimensional subvariety of
X
. For hypersurfaces in
{\mathbb{P}}^{4}
(
n=3
and
r=1
), the statement of Theorem
A
follows from a theorem of Wu
[
41
]
, and the higher dimensional case for hypersurfaces was recently shown by the first author and Yang
[
8
]
.

The story begins with the classical theorem of Noether–Lefschetz
[
27
]
, which states that if
d_{1}+\cdots+d_{r}\geq r+3
, then a very general complete intersection surface
X\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{r+2}
of multidegree
(d_{1},\ldots,d_{r})
satisfies
\Pic(X)=\mathbb{Z}\langle\mathdutchcal{O}_{X}(1)\rangle
. In particular, this implies that for any curve
C\subseteq X
, its degree
\deg(C)
is an integer multiple of
d_{1}\cdots d_{r}
. Motivated by the Noether–Lefschetz theorem, Griffiths–Harris later made a series of conjectures concerning curves on very general hypersurfaces in
\mathbb{P}^{4}

[
19
]
. The strongest form of their conjecture was disproved by Voisin
[
39
]
, but one notable case that remains open predicts that on a threefold hypersurface
X\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{4}
of degree
d\geq 6
, the degree of any curve
C\subseteq X
satisfies
d\mid\deg{C}
. Significant progress in this direction was initiated by Kollár
[
4
]
, who used degeneration methods to give counterexamples to the integral Hodge conjecture for smooth hypersurfaces. More recently, Paulsen refined these degeneration methods to show that for a positive density set of integers, the conjecture of Griffiths–Harris (and its generalization to higher-dimensional hypersurfaces) is true
[
31
]
.

Measures of irrationality

Another motivation for studying curves on varieties is as a measure of complexity. In recent years, measures of irrationality have emerged as interesting and subtle birational invariants for projective varieties, generalizing the notion of gonality for curves to higher dimensions. For a projective variety
X
of dimension
n
, the
degree of irrationality
and the
covering gonality
are defined as follows:

\irr(X)\ \coloneqq\ \min\big\{\delta>0\ |\ \exists\textup{ rational dominant map }X\dashrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{n}\textup{ of degree }\delta\big\};

\cov(X)\ \coloneqq\ \min\big\{c>0\ |\ \exists\textup{ a curve of gonality }c\textup{ through a general point }x\in X\big\}.

From their descriptions, we see that the degree of irrationality is a measure of how far
X
is from being rational, while the covering gonality is a measure of how far
X
is from being uniruled. These two quantities are related by:
\irr(X)\geq\cg(X)
. For an overview of the subject, we refer the reader to
[
7
]
and the many references therein.

Given a smooth projective non-degenerate variety
X\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n+r}
of dimension
n>0
and degree
d
, an elementary observation is that by generic projection from points on
X
, we always have
\irr(X)\leq d-r
. The most challenging difficulty in computing
\irr(X)
is often finding obstructions to the existence of low degree maps
X\dashrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{n}
. For very general hypersurfaces of large degree (i.e.
r=1
), we now have a fairly complete picture. In
[
5
]
, the authors computed their covering gonality. Around the same time,
[
6
,
7
]
proved that if
X
is a very general hypersurface of sufficiently large degree, then
\irr(X)=d-1
; in fact, they also showed that any dominant map
X\dashrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{n}
of degree
d-1
is given by projection from a point on
X
. The main idea that appears in these papers is to use the positivity of the canonical bundle
K_{X}
. Note that some of these results concerning the covering gonality have been extended to positive characteristic
[
34
]
.

For a (very) general complete intersection variety
X\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n+r}
cut out by polynomials of degrees
d_{1},\ldots,d_{r}
, it is natural to ask how both invariants behave. The methods of
[
7
]
only yield lower bounds that are additive in the
d_{i}
’s. However, the authors of
loc
.
cit
. conjectured that the degree of irrationality should behave
multiplicatively
in the degrees, namely there should be a positive constant
C(n,r)
depending only on
n
and
r
such that

(1)

\irr(X)\ \geq\ \cg(X)\ \geq\ C(n,r)\,d_{1}\cdots d_{r},

for sufficiently large degrees
d_{i}
(see
[
7
, Problem 4.1]
). From the previous paragraph, it follows that
\irr(X)\leq d_{1}\cdots d_{r}-r
so we must always have
C(n,r)<1
.

Our second result fully resolves this problem by exhibiting asymptotically sharp bounds:

Theorem B
.

Let
X\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n+r}
be a general complete intersection variety of dimension
n
cut out by polynomials of degrees
d_{1},\ldots,d_{r}\geq n
. Then

\covgon(X)\geq(d_{1}-2(n+1)\sqrt{d_{1}})(d_{2}-n+1)\cdots(d_{r}-n+1)+1.

In particular, for any
\epsilon>0
, there is an integer
N(\epsilon;n,r)
such that when
d_{i}\geq N(\epsilon;n,r)
we have

\covgon(X)\geq(1-\epsilon)d_{1}\cdots d_{r}.

Notably, Theorem
B
gives covering gonality bounds for
general
complete intersections and not just very general ones, as opposed to some of the results in
[
7
]
and
[
5
]
. In particular, the covering gonality bounds hold for almost all complete intersections defined over
\mathbb{Q}
.

The first evidence for a bound of the form (
1
) comes from a theorem of Lazarsfeld from the 1990s, where vector bundle techniques were used to establish sharp multiplicative bounds for complete intersection curves
[
24
, Exercise 4.12]
(this was later revisited in
[
21
]
). Unfortunately, these methods do not seem to generalize to higher dimensions. For
n\geq 2
, Stapleton derived superadditive bounds for the covering gonality of very general codimension two complete intersections
[
36
, Theorem 5.4]
. Subsequently, Stapleton and Ullery computed the degree of irrationality for codimension two complete intersections of type
(2,d)
and
(3,d)

[
35
, Theorem A & B]
. By relating the covering gonality of
X
to conjectures about the degrees and genera of curves on
X
, the first author produced multiplicative bounds for codimension two complete intersections (
r=2
) and complete intersection surfaces (
n=2
), but the constants were far from sharp. Most recently, the authors of
[
28
]
establish sharp multiplicative bounds for the degree of irrationality of complete intersections whose degrees are sufficiently spread out (i.e.
d_{1}\gg d_{2}\gg\cdots\gg d_{r}\gg 0
).

Separation of many points

An important ingredient in the proof of Theorem
B
which may be of independent interest is an Angehrn–Siu type result (cf.
[
3
]
) about separation of points for adjoint line bundles:

Theorem C

(Multi-point separation by adjoint line bundles)
.

Let
(X,H)
be a polarized normal Gorenstein variety. Suppose there exists a nonempty open subset
U\subseteq X^{\reg}
of the regular locus and a number
\alpha>0
such that any curve
C\subseteq X
meeting
U
satisfies
\deg_{H}{C}\geq\alpha
. Then there exists a constant
\delta\coloneqq\delta(X,H)
such that the linear series
|K_{X}+dH|
separates at least
(d-\delta\,\sqrt{d})\cdot\alpha
distinct points on
U
. Moreover, if
H
is very ample, then one can take
\delta=2\dim{X}
.

This technical strengthening greatly improves the reduction step that appears in
[
36
,
9
]
. Our approach gives different bounds than those arising from the main result of
[
3
]
, see Remark
5.4
, in that we optimize their techniques for separating asymptotically large numbers of points. Notably, unlike the assumptions in
[
3
, Corollary 0.4]
, the intersection-theoretic criterion above only involves curves and not higher-dimensional subvarieties. Theorem
C
gives the optimal leading term for
d\gg 0
(e.g. for
X
a general complete intersection as illustrated by Theorem
B
) but does not usually give the optimal error term. For example, a Reider-like argument
[
32
]
shows that when
X
is a surface, the adjoint linear series
|K_{X}+dH|
separates at least
\alpha(d-\alpha/H^{2})-1
points, where
\alpha\coloneqq\min_{C}(C\cdot H)
is the minimal degree of an effective curve
C\subset X
. This has an
O(1)
error term as opposed to
O(\sqrt{d})
as given in Theorem
C
.

Auxiliary results on bounds of other invariants

Theorem
A
can be used in concert with results in
[

,
13
,

,

,
30
,
11
]
and others to give an absolute lower bound on the

.

In §

, we apply Theorem

to bound the

Sketch of proofs and outline of the paper

Both Theorem
A
and Theorem
B
follow from a sharp lower bound on the degree of a curve passing through a
general
point on a general complete intersection
X
of sufficiently large degrees given in Theorem
3.1
. Moreover, our technique provides an explicit multiplicative bound for the covering degree and computes

\covdeg(X,\mathdutchcal{O}_{X}(1))\ =\ \deg{X}

(see Definition
2.3
) in the large degree regime. The proof of Theorem
3.1
proceeds by degenerating a complete intersection
X
into a union of two complete intersections
X_{1}\cup X_{2}
of smaller degrees meeting transversally along a complete intersection of one dimension less. Fixing a curve
C
on the generic such
X
, if we could show that the specialization of
C
has a component in each
X_{i}
, then we could apply induction to conclude. Unfortunately, this naive approach cannot work – consider the case of lines on a quadric hypersurface degenerating to a union of two hyperplanes. However, when the curve
C
belongs to a
covering family of curves
(see §2 for the precise definition), we show that either the curves break into components covering each
X_{i}
, or some component must cover the intersection
X_{1}\cap X_{2}
. This allows us to run a more subtle induction simultaneously on degrees, dimension, and codimension.

A crucial input in the argument above is a result on the structure of curves on the reducible central fiber of a flat family that deform to nearby fibers. This follows from ideas of Jun Li
[
29
]
, and we give a self-contained exposition in Appendix
A
. The Grassmannian technique developed by Reidl and Yang
[
33
]
then allows one to pass between the degree of
any
curve on a general complete intersection and the covering degree of a general complete intersection of the same multidegree but twice the dimension (see
Proof of Theorem

A
). Hence, Theorem
B
can be used to give similar bounds for the gonality of any curve on a general complete intersection.

In §2, we set up the machinery for degenerating stable maps when the ambient space degenerates to the union of two irreducible components meeting transversally and we exhibit
constraints on which curves can deform to the general fiber. In §3, we prove a lower bound on the covering degree of curves on a general complete intersection
X\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{N}
with respect to
\mathdutchcal{O}_{X}(1)
, and use this to prove Theorem
A
. In §4 we set up the relevant definitions of multiplier ideals, and we collect the necessary lemmas for modifying their log canonical centers. In §5, we then use these lemmas to prove Theorem
C
; at the end of the section, we also give the proof of Theorem
B
. Finally, in §6 we present a number of applications and questions that arise from our work.

Conventions

In this paper, we work over
\mathbb{C}
. By
variety
, we mean an integral separated scheme of finite type over
\mathbb{C}
. A
curve
is a
1
-dimensional geometrically reduced and connected separated scheme of finite type over a field (note that a reducible curve is not a variety according to our conventions). A
family of curves

\pi\colon\mathcal{C}\rightarrow T
is a flat proper morphism whose fibers are curves. A
polarized
variety
(X,L)
consists of a projective variety
X
together with an ample line bundle
L
. Recall that the
gonality
of a smooth projective irreducible curve
C
, denoted by
\gon(C)
, is the smallest degree of a finite morphism
C\rightarrow\mathbb{P}^{1}
. For an integral curve, we define the gonality to be the gonality of its normalization.

For a Cartier divisor
D
, we denote by
\big|D\big|_{\mathbb{Q}}
the
\mathbb{Q}
-linear series, which consists of effective
\mathbb{Q}
-divisors which are
\mathbb{Q}
-linearly equivalent to
D
. For additional properties about linear series and line bundles, we mainly follow
[
25
]
. By a collection of
general points

p_{1},\ldots,p_{m}
in
X
, we mean that each
p_{i}
is chosen arbitrarily and independently from some unspecified open subset of
X
. This should be distinguished from a collection of points
p_{1},\dots,p_{m}
in
general position
, by which we mean
(p_{1},\dots,p_{m})
is chosen from an open subset of
\Sym^{m}(X)
. For example, a linear series separating
n

points in general position
is an elementary consequence of the dimension of the linear series whereas separating
n

general points
is the more restrictive and subtle property of the linear series studied in §
4
-
5
.

When working with complete intersections
X\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n+r}
, we will use
n=\dim{X}
to denote the dimension and
r
to denote the codimension. Sometimes we will write
N=n+r
and
X\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{N}
. We say that
X
is a complete intersection of
multidegree

\underline{d}=(d_{1},\ldots,d_{r})
if it is pure dimension
n
and defined by polynomials of degrees
d_{1},\dots,d_{r}
.

Definition 2.2
.

A

Let

Remark 2.4
.

Alternatively, by standard compactification arguments (cf.

as the minimal

By a Hilbert scheme argument, it is straightforward to show that covering degree is lower semi-continuous in flat polarized families. It will be useful to set up notation to refer to the covering degree of a complete intersection of fixed degrees:

3.
Covering degree of complete intersections

In this section, we prove the following lower bound on the covering degree of complete intersections and show how it implies the bounds of Theorem
A
on the degree of
any
curve.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
X\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n+r}
be a general complete intersection variety of dimension
n
cut out by polynomials of degrees
d_{1},\ldots,d_{r}\geq n
. Then

\covdeg(X,\mathdutchcal{O}(1))\geq(d_{1}-n+1)\cdots(d_{r}-n+1).

Moreover, there exists
N\coloneqq N(n,r)
such that if
d_{1},\dots,d_{r}\geq N
then

\covdeg(X,\mathdutchcal{O}(1))=d_{1}\cdots d_{r}.

Remark 3.2
.

When
X\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n+1}
is a hypersurface of degree
d=n
, then
X
is covered by lines so
\covdeg(X)=1
. However, a basic dimension count shows that when

, the general such hypersurface is not covered by lines and hence
\covdeg(X)\geq 2
. This aligns with the constants in Theorem
3.1
. Moreover, if

then every hypersurface contains a line but if
d\geq 2n
then the general such hypersurface does not, aligning with the constants in Theorem
A
.

3.1.
The Main Induction

To prove this result, we will degenerate our complete intersection to the union of complete intersections of lower degrees and track how stable maps deform using the results of the previous section. This leads to the recursion below, which enables a simultaneous induction on the dimension and codimension of
X
.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
d_{1}=a+b
for positive integers

we have

\displaystyle\cd_{n,r}(d_{1},\dots,d_{r})

\displaystyle\geq\min\{\cd_{n,r}(a,d_{2},\dots,d_{r})+\cd_{n,r}(b,d_{2},\dots,d_{r}),

\displaystyle\hskip 31.2982pt\cd_{n-1,r+1}(a,b,d_{2},\dots,d_{r})\}.

Proof.

Given a very general hypersurface
X_{d_{1}}
in
\mathbb{P}^{n+r}
, there exists a degeneration to a union
X_{a}\cup X_{b}
of two very general hypersurfaces. More explicitly, we can find equations
F,G,H
of degrees
d_{1},a,b
such that
V(G)
and
V(H)
are isomorphic to the geometric generic hypersurface of degrees
a
and
b

V(F),V(G)
, and

all meet transversally. Choosing other fixed hypersurfaces

meeting each other and the aforementioned hypersurfaces all transversally, the family

\mathdutchcal{X}\ \coloneqq\ V(GH-tF)\cap X_{d_{2}}\cap\cdots\cap X_{d_{r}}\ \subseteq\ \mathbb{P}^{n+r}\times\mathbb{A}^{1}

satisfies the hypotheses in Theorem
2.12

is isomorphic to the geometric generic complete intersection of multidegree
\underline{d}
(cf.
[
37

). Let

denote the intersection of the two components of the special fiber:

Note that the singular locus

is the intersection

W\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}V(F)\cap V(G)\cap V(H)\cap X_{d_{2}}\cap\cdots\cap X_{d_{r}},

Let

and

. By the previous discussion,

(1)

\cd_{n,r}(d_{1},\dots,d_{r})=\covdeg({\mathdutchcal{X}}_{\bar{\eta}},\mathdutchcal{O}(1))

(2)

\cd_{n,r}(a,d_{2},\dots,d_{r})=\covdeg(X_{1},\mathdutchcal{O}(1))

(3)

Therefore, we may apply Theorem

The following will serve as the base case of an induction argument.

Example 3.4
.

Let
X\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}X_{\underline{d}}\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n+r}
be a general Fano index 1 complete intersection, i.e.
\sum d_{i}=n+r
. It is well known that the Fano variety of lines on

has dimension equal to the expected dimension

, so the locus of lines sweeps out at most a divisor on

. This implies that the covering degree of

is at least 2, and it turns out that conics do indeed cover

.

We are now ready to prove the first statement in Theorem
3.1
.

Theorem 3.5
.

For any fixed
n,r
, and any
\underline{d}
with
d_{i}\geq n
for all
i
, we have

(2)

\cd_{n,r}(d_{1},\dots,d_{r})\ \geq\ \prod_{i=1}^{r}(d_{i}-n+1).

Proof.

We apply induction on both
n
and
r
simultaneously. The case
n=1
of curves is clear. The case
r=0
is exactly the fact that
\covdeg(\mathbb{P}^{n})=1
since in this case the product in equation (
2
) is empty. Towards induction, assume the statement holds for
(n,r-1)
and
(n-1,r)
; we need to prove it holds for
(n,r)
. Splitting

, we obtain using Theorem

that

\displaystyle\cd_{n,r}(d_{1},\dots,d_{r})

\displaystyle\geq\min\big\{\cd_{n,r}(d_{1}-1,d_{2},\dots,d_{r})+\cd_{n,r}(1,d_{2},\dots,d_{r}),\cd_{n-1,r+1}(d_{1}-1,1,d_{2},\dots,d_{r})\big\}

\displaystyle=\min\big\{\cd_{n,r}(d_{1}-1,d_{2},\dots,d_{r})+\cd_{n,r-1}(d_{2},\dots,d_{r}),\cd_{n-1,r}(d_{1}-1,d_{2},\dots,d_{r})\big\}.

Now we may apply induction on the degrees to the first term in the expression above. For the second term, we use the inductive hypothesis
(n-1,r)
to conclude

\displaystyle\cd_{n-1,r}(d_{1}-1,d_{2},\dots,d_{r})

\displaystyle\geq((d_{1}-1)-(n-1)+1)\cdot\prod_{i=2}^{r}(d_{i}-(n-1)+1)

\displaystyle\geq\prod_{i=1}^{r}(d_{i}-n+1).

We use the following base case

for induction on

:

\displaystyle\cd_{n,r}(n,d_{2},\dots,d_{r})\geq\cd_{n,r}(1,d_{2},\dots,d_{r})

\displaystyle=\cd_{n,r-1}(d_{2},\dots,d_{r})

\displaystyle\geq\prod_{i=2}^{r}(d_{i}-n+1)=\prod_{i=1}^{r}(d_{i}-n+1),

where the first inequality follows from generic projection or from specialization to a union of complete intersections of multidegrees

\displaystyle\cd_{n,r}(d_{1}-1,d_{2},\dots,d_{r})+\cd_{n,r-1}(d_{2},\dots,d_{r})

\displaystyle\geq((d_{1}-1)-n+1)\cdot\prod_{i=2}^{r}(d_{i}-n+1)+\cd_{n,r-1}(d_{2},\dots,d_{r})

\displaystyle\geq(d_{1}-n)\cdot\prod_{i=2}^{r}(d_{i}-n+1)+\prod_{i=2}^{r}(d_{i}-n+1)=(d_{1}-n+1)\cdot\prod_{i=2}^{r}(d_{i}-n+1).\qed

3.2.
Proof of Theorem
A

In this section, we complete the proof of Theorem

Theorem 3.6

(

Let

Using this result, we complete the proof of Theorem

(b)

Granting that

\cd_{n-1,r+1}(a_{1},\dots,a_{r+1})\geq(1-\tfrac{1}{2})a_{1}\cdots a_{r+1}

\displaystyle\cd_{n,r}(d_{1},\dots,d_{r})

\displaystyle\geq\min\{\cd_{n,r}(a,d_{2},\dots,d_{r})+\cd_{n,r}(b,d_{2},\dots,d_{r}),

\displaystyle\hskip 31.2982pt\cd_{n-1,r+1}(a,b,d_{2},\dots,d_{r})\}

\displaystyle\geq\cd_{n,r}(a,d_{2},\dots,d_{r})+\cd_{n,r}(b,d_{2},\dots,d_{r}).

The second inequality follows from the fact that the second term in the “min” expression is automatically larger than

. By permutation symmetry in the inputs,

Using property

\mathdutchcal{Z}^{<b}_{n,\underline{d}}
has codimension
\geq n
inside the universal family. However, by definition, the fibers of the second projection from
\mathdutchcal{Z}^{<b}_{n,\underline{d}}
are covered by curves and hence
\mathdutchcal{X}_{n,\underline{d}}
cannot intersect the general fiber. This completes the proof.
∎

4.
Covering gonality and multiplier ideals

4.1.
Covering Gonality and Separation of Points

In this subsection, we recall how positivity of the canonical line bundle yields lower bounds on the covering gonality.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
X
be a normal projective variety. For a positive integer
m
, we say that a line bundle
L
on

separates
m
distinct general points
if there exists a Zariski-open

such that for every set of
m
distinct points
\xi\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{m}\}\subseteq U

H^{0}(X,L)\rightarrow H^{0}(X,L\otimes\mathdutchcal{O}_{\xi})

is surjective. If the choice of

is made explicit we say that

separates

distinct points on

.

The relevant result we need is the following:

Proposition 4.2

(
[
7
, Theorem 1.10]
&
[
36
, Remark 5.14]
)
.

Let
X
be a normal Gorenstein variety with at worst canonical singularities. If the canonical line bundle

separates

\cov(X)\geq m+1.

4.2.
Singularities, multiplier ideals and vanishing theorems

In this subsection, we will introduce the relevant multiplier ideal machinery (for more details, see
[
14
,
26
]
).

Although most of our results hold in a more general setting, for simplicity we will assume that

is
{\mathbb{Q}}
-Gorenstein.

Definition 4.3
.

Let
(X,D)
projective log pair such that

\mathbb{Q}
-Cartier
\mathbb{Q}
-divisor, and fix a log resolution

{\mathbb{Q}}
-divisor

Definition 4.4

Let
(X,D)
be a log canonical pair. A prime divisor

The closed subset

of

Definition 4.5

Consider a pair

of

Remark 4.6

In the literature, the LC-locus is sometimes referred to as the non-klt locus. In a neighborhood of

Proposition 4.7
.

(Semi-continuity of multiplier ideals,
[
26
, Corollary 9.5.39]
)

Let
p:X\rightarrow T
be a smooth morphism between smooth irreducible varieties. Let

be an effective
\mathbb{Q}
-divisor on

whose support does not contain any of the fibers

, so that for each

of

, and write
q_{t}\coloneqq(t)\in X_{t}
. If

.

The following well-known lemmas will be used in the proof of the main theorems.

Lemma 4.8
.

(
[
26
, Lemma 10.4.12]
)

Let
X
be an
n
-dimensional irreducible projective variety, and
p_{1},\dots,p_{m}\in X
be smooth points. Let

be an ample
\mathbb{Q}
-divisor on

such that
(H^{n})>m\cdot\gamma^{n}
for some rational number

. Then there exists an effective
\mathbb{Q}
-divisor

D\in\big|H\big|_{\mathbb{Q}}\hskip 10.00002pt\textup{with}\hskip 10.00002pt\mult_{p_{i}}(D)>\gamma\textup{ for each }1\leq i\leq m.

Proof.

This is the multi-point version of
[
25
, Lemma 10.4.12]
, and the proof is almost identical to
[
25
, Proposition 1.1.31]
. Choosing

sufficiently divisible, we have

and we need to impose at most

conditions to vanish to order

∎

Lemma 4.9

(
[
26
, Proposition 9.3.2]
)
.

Let

be a smooth point on

.

4.3.
Perturbation, cutting down, and lifting

From now on, let
(X,H)
be a normal polarized variety of dimension
n
such that the canonical divisor
K_{X}
is Cartier.

Let
p_{1},\ldots,p_{m}
be
m
distinct points on
X
. Consider a rational number
t_{1}>0
such that
(t_{1}H)^{n}>mn^{n}
. Then for sufficiently divisible and large
k\in\mathbb{N}
, by Lemma
4.8
one can find a divisor
D^{\prime}\in\big|kt_{1}H\big|
such that

\mult_{p_{i}}(D^{\prime})\ >\ kn\hskip 10.00002pt\textup{ for all }i=1,\ldots,m.

As a consequence, setting
D\coloneqq\frac{1}{k}D^{\prime}
, by Lemma
4.9
we have that

\mathdutchcal{J}\big(X,D\big)_{p_{i}}\ \subseteq\ \mathfrak{m}_{p_{i}}\hskip 10.00002pt\textup{for all }i=1,\ldots,m.

Proposition 4.10
.

Let
X
be a normal
{\mathbb{Q}}
-Gorenstein projective
n
-fold,
p_{1},\ldots,p_{m}\in X
distinct smooth points, and
D
an effective
\mathbb{Q}
-divisor such that
Z(D)
contains
p_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,m
. For any ample divisor
H
on
Y
, and any
\epsilon>0
, there exists a rational number
0\leq c<\epsilon
and an effective
\mathbb{Q}
-divisor
E\sim_{\mathbb{Q}}c\cdot H
such that

(1)

\LC_{p_{i}}(X,D+E)
is irreducible at
p_{i}
, and

(2)

\dim\LC_{p_{i}}(X,D+E)\leq\dim\LC_{p_{i}}(X,D)

for every
i=1,\ldots,m
.

Proof.

Set
Z_{i}\coloneqq\LC_{p_{i}}(X,D)
and
d_{i}(D)\coloneqq\dim_{p_{i}}Z_{i}
for each
i=1,\ldots,m
. Our proof will proceed by reducing the invariant

s\ \mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\ \max\big(\{0\}\cup\{d_{i}(D)\ |\ Z_{i}\textup{ is reducible}\}\big).

If
s=0
, then we may choose
E=0
and the theorem follows. Otherwise, assume that
s>0
and
d_{1}(D)
achieves the maximum
s
. Therefore,
Z_{1}
is reducible and
\dim_{p_{1}}Z_{1}\geq 1
. Choose an irreducible component
Z_{1}^{\prime}
of
Z_{1}
of minimal dimension passing through
p_{1}
, and fix an integer
\ell\gg 1
such that the sheaf
\mathdutchcal{I}_{Z_{1}^{\prime}}(\ell H)
is globally generated. Take a general element
f_{1}\in\Gamma(Y,\mathdutchcal{I}_{Z_{1}^{\prime}}(\ell H))
, and set
E_{1}\coloneqq\divisor(f_{1})
. Then for any rational number
0<\lambda_{1}\ll 1
, the support of
\LC_{p_{1}}(X,D+\lambda_{1}E_{1})
satisfies one of the following conditions:

(1)

it is exactly
Z_{1}^{\prime}
, or

(2)

it is contained in some proper algebraic subset of
Z_{1}^{\prime}
,

because
E_{1}
only contributes to the log canonical thresholds of the log canonical places whose centers are contained in
Z_{1}^{\prime}
. Fix an index
i\geq 2
. If
p_{i}\notin Z^{\prime}_{1}
, then
\LC_{p_{i}}(X,D+\lambda_{1}E_{1})=\LC_{p_{i}}(X,D)
. Now assume that
p_{i}
is contained in
Z^{\prime}_{1}
. If
\LC_{p_{i}}(X,D)
is irreducible, then by choosing
\lambda_{1}
sufficiently small, we can guarantee that
\LC_{p_{i}}(X,D+\lambda_{1}E_{1})=\LC_{p_{i}}(X,D)
. On the other hand, if
\LC_{p_{i}}(X,D)
is reducible, then there are two further cases:

(1)

Z_{1}^{\prime}
is a component of
\LC_{p_{i}}(X,D)
, in which case
\LC_{p_{i}}(X,D+\lambda_{1}E_{1})
is contained in
Z_{1}^{\prime}
(by the first sentence of the previous paragraph), or

(2)

Z_{1}^{\prime}
is not a component of
\LC_{p_{i}}(X,D)
, in which case
\LC_{p_{i}}(X,D+\lambda_{1}E_{1})
is contained in
\LC_{p_{i}}(X,D)
for
0<\lambda_{1}\ll 1
.

In both cases, we see that
d_{i}(D+\lambda_{1}D_{1})\leq d_{i}(D)
.

Replacing
D
by
D+\lambda_{1}E_{1}
for some fixed
0<\lambda_{1}<\frac{\epsilon}{nm}
, and repeating this reduction procedure, after at most

steps, one will get a
\mathbb{Q}
-divisor

E\ \coloneqq\ \sum\lambda_{j}E_{j}\ \sim_{\mathbb{Q}}\ c\cdot H

which satisfies the requisite properties.
∎

Lemma 4.11

(Generic lifting)
.

Let
(X,\mathdutchcal{O}_{X}(1))
be a normal polarized variety, and
Z\subseteq X
be a closed subvariety. Then for any
\mathbb{Q}
-divisor
D_{Z}\in\big|\mathdutchcal{O}_{Z}(d)\big|_{\mathbb{Q}}
on
Z
, there exists an effective
\mathbb{Q}
-divisor
D\in|\mathdutchcal{O}_{X}(d)|_{\mathbb{Q}}
on
X
such that
D|_{Z}=D_{Z}
.

Proof.

Pick a sufficiently large integer
p\gg 0
such that
H^{1}(X,\mathdutchcal{I}_{Z}(pd))=0
. Then the restriction map

H^{0}(X,\mathdutchcal{O}_{X}(pd))\ \longrightarrow\ H^{0}(Z,\mathdutchcal{O}_{Z}(pd))

is surjective. Hence there exists a divisor
D^{\prime}\in\big|\mathdutchcal{O}_{X}(pd)\big|
such that
D^{\prime}|_{Z}=pD_{Z}
, and the
\mathbb{Q}
-divisor
\frac{1}{p}D^{\prime}
is as desired. If

is sufficiently large and
D^{\prime}
is a general divisor satisfying the restriction condition, then we call

a
generic lifting
of

.
∎

Proposition 4.12

(Cutting down LC-locus)
.

Let
X
be a normal
{\mathbb{Q}}
-Gorenstein projective variety and
p\in X
be a point. Let
D
be an effective
\mathbb{Q}
-divisor on
X
with
\lct_{p}(X,D)=1
such that

Z\ =\ \LC_{p}(X,D)

is irreducible of dimension
d
at
p
. Fix
m
general smooth points
p_{1},\ldots,p_{m}\in Z
, and let
B
be any effective
\mathbb{Q}
-divisor on

with

. Assume that

\mult_{p_{i}}(B|_{Z})\ >\ d\ =\ \dim Z

for any

, where the multiplicity is computed on

. Then for any

, the multiplier ideal

\mathdutchcal{J}(X,(1-\epsilon)D+B)

is non-trivial at

. If moreover

Following the proof of

5.
Separation of points à la Angehrn–Siu

In this section, we will present the proof of Theorem
C
. The goal is to construct a
\mathbb{Q}
-divisor on
X
by cutting down the LC locus near a point and lifting the divisors, such that the Nadel vanishing theorem applies. This method is motivated by the proof of a weak version of Fujita’s conjecture by Angehrn–Siu
[
3
]
.

Proof of Theorem
C
.

Let
n\coloneqq\dim{X}
be the dimension of
X
. First we consider the case where
H
is very ample. By slicing down to curves, we get a bound
\deg_{H}W\geq\alpha
for any positive-dimensional subvariety
W\subset X
intersecting
U
from the hypothesis on curves.
For any choice of
m
distinct points
p_{1},\ldots,p_{m}\in U
, we will construct a
\mathbb{Q}
-divisor
D
which satisfies the following conditions:

•

D\sim_{\mathbb{Q}}c\cdot H
, where
0<c<d
is a rational number,

•

the support of
\mathdutchcal{O}_{X}/\mathdutchcal{J}(X,D)
contains
p_{1},\ldots,p_{m}
, and

•

some point
p_{j}
is an isolated point of
\Supp\mathdutchcal{O}_{X}/\mathdutchcal{J}(X,D)
.

Supposing that the above holds, we claim that this implies the statement of the theorem. Nadel vanishing
[
26
, Theorem 9.4.17]
gives

H^{1}\big(X,\mathdutchcal{O}_{X}(K_{X}+dH)\otimes\mathdutchcal{J}(X,D)\big)\ =\ 0,

and hence the restriction map

H^{0}\big(X,\mathdutchcal{O}_{X}(K_{X}+dH)\big)\longrightarrow H^{0}\big(X,\mathdutchcal{O}_{X}(K_{X}+dH)\otimes\mathdutchcal{O}_{X}/\mathdutchcal{J}(X,D)\big)

is surjective. Since
\mathdutchcal{O}_{X}/\mathdutchcal{J}(X,D)
has zero-dimensional support near
p_{j}
and contains all
p_{1},\dots,p_{m}
, there exists a section of
\mathdutchcal{O}_{X}(K_{X}+dH)
whose evaluation at
p_{j}
is
1
and at all other
p_{i}
is zero. Removing the point
p_{j}
from
\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{m}\}
and repeating the same construction for
\{p_{1},\ldots,\hat{p}_{j},\ldots,p_{m}\}
, this proves that the evaluation map

H^{0}\big(X,\mathdutchcal{O}_{X}(K_{X}+dH)\big)\longrightarrow H^{0}\big(X,\mathdutchcal{O}_{X}(K_{X}+dH)\otimes\mathdutchcal{O}_{X}/\mathdutchcal{I}_{p_{1},\dots,p_{m}}\big)

is an upper triangular matrix with
1
s along the diagonal when restricted to the span of the divisors constructed above. Hence the evaluation map is surjective.

Now let us begin with the construction of the desired divisor. Choose a positive rational number

a\ \coloneqq\ \sqrt[{}^{{}^{n}}]{\frac{mn^{n}}{\alpha}}+\delta\ =\ n\sqrt[{}^{{}^{n}}]{\frac{m}{\alpha}}+\delta,

where
\delta>0
is a sufficiently small real number. By Lemma
4.8
, there exists an effective
\mathbb{Q}
-divisor
D^{\prime}\in\big|aH\big|_{\mathbb{Q}}
such that
\mult_{p_{i}}(D^{\prime})>n
for each
i=1,\ldots,m
.
Recall that our assumption in particular says that
H^{n}=\deg_{H}{X}\geq\alpha
. Using Proposition
4.10
, one can replace
D^{\prime}
with a small perturbation
D^{\prime}+\eta H
such that
\LC_{p_{i}}(X,D^{\prime})
is irreducible at
p_{i}
for each
i
. Setting
\lambda_{i}\coloneqq\lct_{p_{i}}(X;D^{\prime})
, we may reorder

0\ <\ \lambda_{m}\ \leq\ \lambda_{m-1}\ \leq\ \cdots\ \leq\ \lambda_{1}\ \leq\ 1,

where the upper bound comes from the fact that each
p_{i}
is contained in
Z(D^{\prime})
by Lemma
4.9
.
Setting
D_{1}\coloneqq\lambda_{1}D^{\prime}
, we have produced a divisor
D_{1}\sim_{\mathbb{Q}}a_{1}H
, where
a_{1}\coloneqq\lambda_{1}\left(a+\eta\right)
. Since
\eta
can be made arbitrarily small, we may define a perturbation
\delta_{1}
such that

a_{1}\ =\ n\sqrt[{}^{{}^{n}}]{\frac{m}{\alpha}}+\delta_{1},

where
\delta_{1}
can be chosen to be
<\epsilon
for any fixed

. Note that

may be negative, and we are not claiming a lower bound.

By replacing
D^{\prime}
with
D_{1}
as in the previous paragraph, we may assume that

. Notice that there could be more than one point at which the log canonical threshold of

is equal to
1
. Let
W_{1},\ldots,W_{v}
be the distinct log canonical loci of
(X,D_{1})
near the points
p_{i}
for which we have
\lct_{p_{i}}(X;D_{1})=1
. Reordering the points, we may assume that
p_{1},\ldots,p_{\ell}
are exactly the points with
\lambda_{i}=1
and with
W_{1}
as the (local) log canonical center. By adding a small multiple of a general divisor in the linear series
\big|\mathdutchcal{I}_{W_{j}}(mH)\big|
for

, we may assume that

\lambda_{1}\ =\ \cdots\ =\ \lambda_{\ell}\ =\ 1\ >\ \lambda_{\ell+1},\ \ldots,\ \lambda_{m},

and
p_{1},\ldots,p_{\ell}
have the same log canonical center

.

Let
Z_{1}\coloneqq\NKLT(X,D_{1})
be the non-klt locus of
(X,D_{1})
, which contains
p_{1},\ldots,p_{m}
and contains
W_{1}
as a component. The dimension of

to construct the necessary divisor

such that

\mathbb{Q}
-divisor

on

\mathbb{Q}

in the singular locus of

Remark 5.4

When

Complements and further questions

In this section, we present a few interesting applications and state some related questions that arise from our work.

6.1.
Genus bounds

As mentioned in the introduction, Theorem
A
implies a lower bound on the geometric genus of any curve in a general complete intersection:

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{n+r}
be a very general complete intersection of type
(d_{1},\dots,d_{r})
with all

. Then any curve

satisfies

For

, as in Theorem
A
, one has that

p_{g}(C)\ \geq\ \tfrac{1}{2}(d_{1}+\cdots+d_{r}-2n-r)\cdot d_{1}\cdots d_{r}+1

Proof.

This follows immediately from
[
9
, Proposition 3.1]
and Theorem
A
.
∎

When the degrees are large, note that this bound is very close to the genus of a smooth curve coming from slicing by a linear subspace
\Lambda

p_{g}(X\cap\Lambda)\ =\ \tfrac{1}{2}(d_{1}+\cdots+d_{r}-n-r-1)\cdot d_{1}\cdots d_{r}+1.

6.2.
Measures of association

A recent paper of Lazarsfeld and Martin
[
23
]
introduces a measure of complexity between birational classes of varieties of dimension
n
. The starting point for their paper is to consider all
correspondences
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Z}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X^{\prime}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces a}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces b}

where both maps are generically finite and dominant, and
Z
is a smooth projective variety with

birational onto its image. The

between

and

is defined as

\mathrm{corr.deg}(X,X^{\prime})\ \mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\ \min_{(Z,a,b)}\{\deg a\cdot\deg b\}.

Then Theorem
C
implies a bound on the correspondence degree between two general complete intersections.

Theorem 6.2
.

Let
X,X^{\prime}\subset\mathbb{P}^{n+r}
be two very general complete intersections of type
(d_{1},\dots,d_{r})
. Assume that all

. Then

\mathrm{corr.deg}(X,X^{\prime})\ \geq\ \bigg(\big(d_{1}-2n\sqrt{d_{1}}\big)\big(d_{2}-n+1\big)\cdots\big(d_{r}-n+1\big)+1\bigg)^{2}.

Proof.

We follow the method of proof for
[
23
, Theorem A]
. A correspondence
Z\to X\times X^{\prime}
induces a trace map

\tr_{Z}\ :\ H^{0}(X,\Omega_{X}^{n})\ \longrightarrow\ H^{0}(X^{\prime},\Omega_{X^{\prime}}^{n})

given by the map of
\mathbb{Q}
-Hodge structures

Z_{*}:H^{n}(X,\mathbb{Q})_{\mathrm{prim}}\ \longrightarrow\ H^{n}(X^{\prime},\mathbb{Q}).

For very general

, these Hodge structures do not admit any non-zero morphism between each other, and hence the trace map is zero. However,
K_{X}
separates at least

R\ \coloneqq\ \big(d_{1}-2n\sqrt{d_{1}}\big)\cdot\big(d_{2}-n+1\big)\cdots\big(d_{r}-n+1\big)+1

distinct points on some open set

by the proof of Theorem

. As

is birational onto its image, the fibers of

and

generically only intersect at one point, so the trace map cannot be zero if

. Reversing the role of

and
X^{\prime}
, this proves the claim.
∎

are identified.

Remark A.2
.

Property (ii) in Proposition
A.1
implies that
\bigotimes_{i\in I}\sigma_{v,i}
is nowhere vanishing. This has a useful numerical consequence: for
v\in V
and
q_{e}\in\partial U_{v}
, we have
\sum_{i\in I}m_{i}(v,e)=0
.

Proof.

Since
\mu_{s}:C\to\mathdutchcal{X}_{s}
deforms to
\mathdutchcal{X}_{\eta}
, there is a commutative diagram
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{C}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathdutchcal{X}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces B}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Delta}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mu}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces g}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\nu}

of schemes over
\mathbb{C}
where

•

B=\Spec(S)
, where
S
is a DVR given by the local ring of a smooth affine curve over
\mathbb{C}
at a closed point, with
t,\xi\in B
being the closed and generic point respectively,

•

the morphism
\nu:B\to\Delta
maps
t\in B
to
s\in\Delta
and
\xi\in B
to
\eta\in\Delta
,

•

\mu:\mathcal{C}\to\mathdutchcal{X}
is a family of stable maps over
B
whose closed fiber is
\mu_{s}:C\to\mathdutchcal{X}_{s}
.

Let
\pi
be a uniformizer of
S
, let
\mathfrak{n}=(\pi)
be its maximal ideal and let
L
be its fraction field. After a base change of DVRs (inducing a finite extension of fraction fields), we ensure that the nodes of the generic fiber
\mathcal{C}_{\xi}
are defined over
L
. Each node
\Spec(L)\to\mathcal{C}_{\xi}
can be uniquely completed to a section of
\mathcal{C}\to B
, and this section specializes over
t
to a node of
C
. The nodes of
C
obtained in this manner from nodes of
\mathcal{C}_{\xi}
will be called
generic nodes
while the remaining nodes of
C
will be called
special nodes
.

Define the open subset
\mathdutchcal{U}:=\mu^{-1}(\mathdutchcal{X}^{\reg})\subseteq\mathcal{C}
and note that
\mathdutchcal{U}_{t}=U
. For convenience, we will denote the restrictions
\mu|{U}
and
g|{U}
simply by
\mu
and
g
respectively.

With these preparations, we define the desired data as follows.

(
a
)

Define
M:=T_{B,t}=(\mathfrak{n}/\mathfrak{n}^{2})^{\vee}
. Let
\Phi_{\Delta}:M^{\otimes n}\xrightarrow{\sim}T_{\Delta,s}
be the isomorphism induced by the ring map
R\to S
corresponding to
\nu:B\to\Delta
, where
n\geq 1
is defined by
\mathfrak{m}S=\mathfrak{n}^{n}
.

(
b
)

Let
e\in E
be an edge joining vertices
v,v^{\prime}\in V
. The discussion is somewhat different depending on whether
q_{e}
is a special node or a generic node.

Suppose
q_{e}\in U
is a special node. Then, étale locally near
q_{e}
, the family
\mathcal{C}\to B
is given by

(6)

\displaystyle\Spec\left(\frac{S[x,y]}{(xy-\pi^{\delta(e)})}\right)\ \longrightarrow\ \Spec(S),

and this defines
\delta(e)\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
.
We can phrase this intrinsically by saying
\mathcal{C}
has an
A_{\delta(e)-1}
singularity at
q_{e}
. If
v\neq v^{\prime}
, then we have a canonical isomorphism
\Phi_{e}:M^{\otimes\delta(e)}\xrightarrow{\sim}T_{U_{v},q_{e}}\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}T_{U_{v^{\prime}},q_{e}}
. In the étale local model (
6
), the map
\Phi_{e}
is given by
(\partial_{\pi})^{\otimes\delta(e)}\mapsto\partial_{x}\otimes\partial_{y}
.

Suppose
q_{e}\in U
is a generic node. Then, étale locally near
q_{e}
, the family
\mathcal{C}\to B
is given by

(7)

\displaystyle\Spec\left(\frac{S[x,y]}{(xy)}\right)\ \longrightarrow\ \Spec(S).

In this situation, we define the integer
\delta(e)\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
arbitrarily. When
v\neq v^{\prime}
, we also define the isomorphism
\Phi_{e}:M^{\otimes\delta(e)}\xrightarrow{\sim}T_{U_{v},q_{e}}\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}T_{U_{v^{\prime}},q_{e}}
arbitrarily.

(
c
)

Let
i\in I
and
v\in V
. Consider the section
\sigma_{i}:=\mu^{*}\tau_{i}
of the line bundle
\mu^{*}\mathdutchcal{L}_{i}
on
\mathdutchcal{U}
. By assumption,
\sigma_{i}
is non-vanishing on the generic fiber
\mathdutchcal{U}_{\xi}
of
\mathdutchcal{U}\to B
.

Define
n_{i}(v)\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
to be the generic order of vanishing of
\sigma_{i}
along
U_{v}
. To explain this in more detail, let
\xi_{v}
be the generic point of
U_{v}
and note that the local ring
\mathdutchcal{O}_{\mathdutchcal{U},\xi_{v}}
is a DVR, with uniformizer being the image of
\pi
under the injective map
S=\mathdutchcal{O}_{B,t}\to\mathdutchcal{O}_{\mathdutchcal{U},\xi_{v}}
induced by
g
. We can then define
n_{i}(v)
to be the valuation of
\sigma_{i}
in
\mathdutchcal{O}_{\mathdutchcal{U},\xi_{v}}
, where we use a local trivialization of
\mu^{*}\mathdutchcal{L}_{i}
near
\xi_{v}
to regard
\sigma_{i}
as an element of
\mathdutchcal{O}_{\mathdutchcal{U},\xi_{v}}
.

Define
\sigma_{v,i}
by first regarding
\sigma_{i}
as a rational section of the line bundle

\displaystyle\mathdutchcal{O}_{\mathdutchcal{U}}(-n_{i}(v)U)\otimes\mu^{*}\mathdutchcal{L}_{i}\ =\ g^{*}(\mathdutchcal{O}_{B}(-t))^{\otimes{n_{i}(v)}}\otimes\mu^{*}\mathdutchcal{L}_{i}

and then restricting it to
U_{v}
using the isomorphism
M=T_{B,t}\xrightarrow{\sim}\mathdutchcal{O}_{B}(t)|_{t}
. Equivalently, we can define
\sigma_{v,i}
by the explicit formula

(8)

\displaystyle\sigma_{v,i}\ =\ (d\pi)^{\otimes n_{i}(v)}\otimes(\pi^{-n_{i}(v)}\sigma_{i})|_{U_{v}}.

The ideal sheaf of
U_{v}\setminus\partial U_{v}\subseteq\mathdutchcal{U}\setminus\bigcup_{v^{\prime}\neq v}U_{v^{\prime}}
is generated by
\pi
and the section
\sigma_{i}
has no zeros on
\mathdutchcal{U}_{\xi}
. It follows that
\sigma_{v,i}
is regular and nowhere vanishing on
U_{v}\setminus\partial U_{v}
.

We now check that the above definitions have all the claimed properties.

(i)

By definition, we have
n_{i}(v)=0
if and only if
\sigma_{i}
is non-vanishing at the generic point
\xi_{v}
of
U_{v}
, i.e.,
\mu_{s}(U_{v})\not\subseteq X_{i}
. In this case, (
8
) implies that
\sigma_{v,i}=\sigma_{i}|_{U_{v}}=(\mu_{s}^{*}\tau_{i})|_{U_{v}}
.

(ii)

The valuation of the section
\bigotimes_{i\in I}\sigma_{i}
in the DVR
\mathdutchcal{O}_{\mathdutchcal{U},\xi_{v}}
is
\sum_{i\in I}n_{i}(v)
. Under the pullback of the isomorphism (
5
) under
\mu
, this section becomes
\mu^{*}(f^{*}\tau)=g^{*}(\nu^{*}\tau)
. Since
\pi
is simultaneously a uniformizer for both the DVRs
S
and
\mathdutchcal{O}_{\mathdutchcal{U},\xi_{v}}
, we conclude that
\sum_{i\in I}n_{i}(v)
is the same as the valuation of
\nu^{*}\tau
in
S
, which is
n
.

Regard
\nu^{*}\tau
as a section of the line bundle

\displaystyle\mathdutchcal{O}_{B}(-nt)\otimes\nu^{*}(\mathdutchcal{O}_{\Delta}(s)).

Then, its image under the isomorphism
(\mathdutchcal{O}_{B}(-nt)\otimes\varphi^{*}(\mathdutchcal{O}_{\Delta}(s)))|_{t}\xrightarrow{\sim}\Hom_{\mathbb{C}}(T_{B,t}^{\otimes n},T_{\Delta,s})
coincides with
\Phi_{\Delta}
. The claimed formula for
\bigotimes_{i\in I}\sigma_{v,i}
now follows from the fact that the section
(f^{*}\tau)|_{\mathdutchcal{X}^{\reg}}
maps to the section
\bigotimes_{i\in I}\tau_{i}
under the isomorphism (
5
).

(iii)

Fix
i\in I
and let
e
be an edge joining
v\neq v^{\prime}
. Using a local trivialization of
\mu^{*}\mathdutchcal{L}_{i}
near
q_{e}
, we regard
\sigma_{i}=\mu^{*}\tau_{i}
as a regular function defined on a neighborhood of
q_{e}
in
\mathdutchcal{U}
. Since the assertions to be checked are étale local, we will work in the local models (
6
) and (
7
). In terms of these local models, assume that
U_{v}
is given by
\pi=0
,
x=0
and that
U_{v^{\prime}}
is given by
\pi=0
,
y=0
.

Suppose
q_{e}
is a special node. Since
\sigma_{i}
has no zeros on
\mathdutchcal{U}_{\xi}
, we can write
\sigma_{i}=\pi^{a}x^{b}y^{c}h
for some
a,b,c\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
and
h\in\mathdutchcal{O}_{\mathdutchcal{U}}^{\times}
. Since
y\in\mathdutchcal{O}_{\mathdutchcal{U},\xi_{v}}^{\times}
, we can write
x=\pi^{\delta(e)}y^{-1}\in\mathdutchcal{O}_{\mathdutchcal{U},\xi_{v}}
which gives
\sigma_{i}=\pi^{a+\delta(e)b}y^{c-b}h
. From this, we deduce that
n_{i}(v)=a+\delta(e)b
,

\displaystyle\sigma_{v,i}=(d\pi)^{\otimes(a+\delta(e)b)}\otimes(y^{c-b}h)|_{\pi=0,\,x=0},

and
m_{i}(v,e)=c-b
. Similarly, we get
n_{i}(v^{\prime})=a+\delta(e)c
and
m_{i}(v^{\prime},e)=b-c
, which shows that
n_{i}(v^{\prime})=n_{i}(v)+m_{i}(v,e)\delta(e)
. Finally, note that the lowest order terms

\displaystyle\widetilde{\sigma}_{v,i}(q_{e})

\displaystyle=(d\pi)^{\otimes(a+\delta(e)b)}\otimes(dy)^{\otimes(c-b)}\otimes h(q_{e}),

\displaystyle\widetilde{\sigma}_{v^{\prime},i}(q_{e})

\displaystyle=(d\pi)^{\otimes(a+\delta(e)c)}\otimes(dx)^{\otimes(b-c)}\otimes h(q_{e})

coincide under the isomorphism
\Phi_{e}
, which identifies
\partial_{x}\otimes\partial_{y}
with
(\partial_{\pi})^{\otimes\delta(e)}
.

Suppose
q_{e}
is a generic node. Let
\widetilde{q}_{e}:B\to\mathcal{C}
be the completion of the node
\Spec(L)\to\mathcal{C}_{\xi}
which specializes to
q_{e}
over
t\in B
. The section
\widetilde{q}_{e}
corresponds to the section of (
7
) given by

\displaystyle\Spec(S)\ \longrightarrow\ \Spec\left(\frac{S[x,y]}{(xy)}\right),\hskip 10.00002pt\ x,y\mapsto 0.

Normalize an étale neighborhood of the section
\widetilde{q}_{e}
in
\mathdutchcal{U}
to break it into two components
\mathdutchcal{U}_{v}
and
\mathdutchcal{U}_{v^{\prime}}
such that
(\mathdutchcal{U}_{v})|_{t}=U_{v}
and
(\mathdutchcal{U}_{v^{\prime}})|_{t}=U_{v^{\prime}}
. The lifts of the section
\widetilde{q}_{e}
to
\mathdutchcal{U}_{v}
,
\mathdutchcal{U}_{v^{\prime}}
are given in the local model by

\displaystyle\Spec(S)\ \longrightarrow\ \Spec\left(\frac{S[x,y]}{(x)}\right)=\Spec\left(S[y]\right),\hskip 10.00002pt\ y\mapsto 0,

\displaystyle\Spec(S)\ \longrightarrow\ \Spec\left(\frac{S[x,y]}{(y)}\right)=\Spec\left(S[x]\right),\hskip 10.00002pt\ x\mapsto 0.

respectively.
Since
\sigma_{i}
has no zeros on
\mathdutchcal{U}_{\xi}
, we can find
h_{v}\in\mathdutchcal{O}_{\mathdutchcal{U}_{v}}^{\times}
and
h_{v^{\prime}}\in\mathdutchcal{O}_{\mathdutchcal{U}_{v^{\prime}}}^{\times}
with

\displaystyle\sigma_{i}|_{\mathdutchcal{U}_{v}}=\pi^{n_{i}(v)}h_{v}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\sigma_{i}|_{\mathdutchcal{U}_{v^{\prime}}}=\pi^{n_{i}(v^{\prime})}h_{v^{\prime}}.

Since these two expressions for
\sigma_{i}
must agree on the section
\widetilde{q}_{e}
, we must have
n_{i}(v)=n_{i}(v^{\prime})
,
h_{v}(q_{e})=h_{v^{\prime}}(q_{e})\neq 0
and
m_{i}(v,e)=m_{i}(v^{\prime},e)=0
. Thus,
n_{i}(v^{\prime})=n_{i}(v)+m_{i}(v,e)\delta(e)
and

\displaystyle\widetilde{\sigma}_{v,i}(q_{e})=\sigma_{v,i}(q_{e})=\sigma_{v^{\prime},i}(q_{e})=\widetilde{\sigma}_{v^{\prime},i}(q_{e}).

This concludes the proof.
∎

A.3.
Deducing Lemma
2.9
from Proposition
A.1

Restrict to the case where
\mathdutchcal{X}_{s}=X_{1}\cup_{Z}X_{2}
is the union of two smooth varieties
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
meeting along a smooth divisor
Z=X_{1}\cap X_{2}
.

Recall from the statement of Lemma
2.9
that
F\subseteq C
is a connected component of
\mu_{s}^{-1}(Z)
which is contracted to a point
z=\mu_{s}(F)\in\mathdutchcal{X}^{\reg}
. Moreover, for
i=1,2
, we define
C_{i}
to be the union of the irreducible components of
C
which map under
\mu_{s}
to
X_{i}
(but not
Z
) and meet
F
. Given that the non-constant stable map
\mu_{s}:C\to\mathdutchcal{X}_{s}
deforms to
\mathdutchcal{X}_{\eta}
, we need to show that

\displaystyle\sum_{p\in F\cap C_{1}}m_{p}(C_{1};Z)\ =\ \sum_{p\in F\cap C_{2}}m_{p}(C_{2};Z),

where
m_{p}(C_{i};Z)
is the multiplicity at
p
of the divisor on
C_{i}
obtained by pulling back
Z
under the map
\mu_{s}|_{C_{i}}:C_{i}\to X_{i}
for
i=1,2
.

Apply Proposition
A.1
to produce the data of (a), (b), (c) satisfying properties (i), (ii), (iii). As before, write
\Gamma=(V,E)
for the dual graph of
U=\mu_{s}^{-1}(\mathdutchcal{X}^{\reg})
, so that
F\subseteq U
. Fixing
i=1,2
, note that each irreducible component of
C_{i}
meets
U
. Let
V_{i}\subseteq V
be the set of vertices corresponding to the irreducible components of
U\cap C_{i}
. Since the irreducible components of
C_{i}
and
U\cap C_{i}
are in bijection, we will write
C_{v,i}
in place of
U_{v}
when
v\in V_{i}
.

Lemma A.3
.

Consider
p\in F\cap C_{v,1}
for some
v\in V_{1}
. Then, we have
n_{2}(v)=0
,
\sigma_{v,2}=(\mu_{s}^{*}\tau_{2})|_{C_{v,1}}
and
\ord_{p}(\sigma_{v,2})=m_{p}(C_{1};Z)
. Moreover, there exists
v^{\prime}\in V\setminus V_{1}
such that
p\in U_{v^{\prime}}
.

Proof.

Since
\mu_{s}(C_{v,1})\not\subseteq X_{2}
, property (i) in Proposition
A.1
gives
n_{2}(v)=0
and
\sigma_{v,2}=(\mu_{s}^{*}\tau_{2})|_{C_{v,1}}
. The restrictions of
\mathdutchcal{L}_{2}=\mathdutchcal{O}_{\mathdutchcal{X}^{\reg}}(\mathdutchcal{X}^{\reg}\cap X_{2})
and
\mathdutchcal{O}_{X_{1}}(Z)
to
\mathdutchcal{X}^{\reg}\cap X_{1}
are canonically isomorphic and their tautological sections get identified under this isomorphism. It follows that we have
\ord_{p}(\sigma_{v,2})=m_{p}(C_{1};Z)
.

Since
p\in F
, the section
\sigma_{v,2}
vanishes at
p
. Since
\sigma_{v,2}
is non-vanishing away from
\partial C_{v,1}
, the point
p
must lie on
U_{v^{\prime}}
for some
v\neq v^{\prime}\in V
. If we had
v^{\prime}\in V_{1}
, then the preceding paragraph shows that
\sigma_{v^{\prime},2}
is regular (and vanishes) at
p
, contradicting property (iii) of Proposition
A.1
, which gives
\ord_{p}(\sigma_{v,2})+\ord_{p}(\sigma_{v^{\prime},2})=0
. Thus,
v^{\prime}\in V\setminus V_{1}
.
∎

If
F
is
0
-dimensional, then write
F=\{p\}
. Since
p
lies on at most two irreducible components of
C
, we must have
|V_{1}|+|V_{2}|\leq 2
. As
\mu_{s}
is non-constant, at least one of
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
is non-empty. Without loss of generality, assume
V_{1}\neq\emptyset
and pick
v\in V_{1}
. By Lemma
A.3
, we get
v^{\prime}\in V\setminus V_{1}
such that
p\in U_{v^{\prime}}
. Since
F=\{p\}
is a connected component of
\mu_{s}^{-1}(Z)
, we must have
v^{\prime}\in V_{2}
. This shows
|V_{1}|=|V_{2}|=1
. By property (iii) of Proposition
A.1
, Remark
A.2
and Lemma
A.3
, we get

\displaystyle 0\ =\ \ord_{p}(\sigma_{v,2})+\ord_{p}(\sigma_{v^{\prime},2})\ =\ \ord_{p}(\sigma_{v,2})-\ord_{p}(\sigma_{v^{\prime},1})\ =\ m_{p}(C_{1};Z)-m_{p}(C_{2};Z).

If
F
is
1
-dimensional, let
V_{0}\subseteq V
be set of the vertices corresponding to the irreducible components of
U\cap F=F
. For
v\in V_{0}
, we will write
F_{v}
in place of
U_{v}
. Since
\mu_{s}(F)
is a point in
\mathdutchcal{X}^{\reg}\cap Z
, the bundles
\mu_{s}^{*}\mathdutchcal{L}_{i}
are trivial on
F_{v}
for all
v\in V_{0}
and
i=1,2
. Since
F_{v}
is a proper curve and
\sigma_{v,i}
is regular and non-vanishing at all nodes of
F_{v}
, we conclude that
\sigma_{v,i}
must have as many zeros as it has poles (counted with multiplicity) for all
v\in V_{0}
and
i=1,2
. Fixing
i=1
and taking the sum over all
v\in V_{0}
, we get the relation

\displaystyle\sum_{v\in V_{0}}\sum_{q_{e}\in\partial F_{v}}\ord_{q_{e}}(\sigma_{v,1})=0.

For distinct
v,v^{\prime}\in V_{0}
and
q_{e}\in F_{v}\cap F_{v^{\prime}}
, we have
\ord_{q_{e}}(\sigma_{v,1})+\ord_{q_{e}}(\sigma_{v^{\prime},1})=0
by property (iii) of Proposition
A.1
. Such terms therefore cancel in pairs and the above relation reduces to

\displaystyle\sum_{v\in V_{0}}\sum_{p\in F_{v}\cap C_{1}}\ord_{p}(\sigma_{v,1})+\sum_{v\in V_{0}}\sum_{p\in F_{v}\cap C_{2}}\ord_{p}(\sigma_{v,1})=0.

Using Remark
A.2
, we can rewrite this as

\displaystyle-\sum_{v\in V_{0}}\sum_{p\in F_{v}\cap C_{1}}\ord_{p}(\sigma_{v,2})+\sum_{v\in V_{0}}\sum_{p\in F_{v}\cap C_{2}}\ord_{p}(\sigma_{v,1})=0.

Using property (iii) in Proposition
A.1
, this becomes

\displaystyle\sum_{v\in V_{1}}\sum_{p\in F\cap C_{v,1}}\ord_{p}(\sigma_{v,2})-\sum_{v\in V_{2}}\sum_{p\in F\cap C_{v,2}}\ord_{p}(\sigma_{v,1})=0.

By Lemma
A.3
,
\ord_{p}(\sigma_{v,2})=m_{p}(C_{1};Z)
for all
v\in V_{1}
and
p\in F\cap C_{v,1}
. Similarly, for all
v\in V_{2}
and
p\in F\cap C_{v,2}
, we have
\ord_{p}(\sigma_{v,1})=m_{p}(C_{2};Z)
. This completes the proof of Lemma
2.9
.
