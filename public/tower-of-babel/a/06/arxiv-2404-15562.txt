Polyconvex neural network models of thermoelasticity
Jan N. Fuhg, Asghar Jadoon, Oliver Weeger, D. Thomas Seidl, Reese E. Jones

Abstract
Machine-learning function representations such as neural networks have proven to be excellent constructs for constitutive modeling due to their flexibility to represent highly nonlinear data and their ability to incorporate constitutive constraints, which also allows them to generalize well to unseen data. In this work, we extend a polyconvex hyperelastic neural network framework to thermo-hyperelasticity by specifying the thermodynamic and material theoretic requirements for an expansion of the Helmholtz free energy expressed in terms of deformation invariants and temperature. Different formulations which a priori ensure polyconvexity with respect to deformation and concavity with respect to temperature are proposed and discussed. The physics-augmented neural networks are furthermore calibrated with a recently proposed sparsification algorithm that not only aims to fit the training data but also penalizes the number of active parameters, which prevents overfitting in the low data regime and promotes generalization. The performance of the proposed framework is demonstrated on synthetic data, which illustrate the expected thermomechanical phenomena, and existing temperature-dependent uniaxial tension and tension-torsion experimental datasets.

Many machine learning models have been recently developed for hyperelastic materials, e.g.,

This gap is, in part, due to the need to develop a general thermo-hyperelastic representation that satisfies thermomechanical principles and constraints.
Generally speaking, embedding physical constraints guides training and provides confidence in the predictions of scientific machine learning (SciML) models.
Neural networks (NNs) are particularly flexible in this regard

This work is the first, to the authors’ knowledge, to embed the fundamental thermodynamic and material-theoretic constraints into a general, complete representation of thermo-hyperelasticity.
The neural network framework is based on a (Helmholtz) free energy that is a function of invariants and satisfies polyconvexity requirements.
It also has the generality to represent temperature-dependent stresses that are not self-similar, as well as thermal expansion, thermal softening and other expected behaviors of a thermoelastic material.
The imposed convexity also promotes smooth derivatives which lead directly to well-behaved stresses.

3
Thermoelasticity

A number of general conditions for ensuring a well-behaved material model have been postulated
[
42
]

To ensure ellipticity, polyconvexity is the most well-accepted condition, whereby the internal energy
\hat{\epsilon}

\epsilon(\mathbf{F})=\hat{\epsilon}(\mathbf{F},\mathbf{F}^{*},\det\mathbf{F})

(1)

must be convex in each of its arguments, namely the deformation gradient
\mathbf{F}
, its adjugate (matrix of cofactors)
\mathbf{F}^{*}\equiv\det(\mathbf{F})\mathbf{F}^{-T}
, and its determinant
\det\mathbf{F}
, which govern the deformation of line segments, areas and volumes, respectively.

For a thermoelastic material, the internal energy depends also on the entropy
\eta
, and for stable material behavior it is required to be polyconvex in these three deformation inputs and
\eta

[
42
]

\epsilon(\mathbf{F},\eta)=\hat{\epsilon}(\mathbf{F},\mathbf{F}^{*},\det\mathbf{F},\eta)\ .

(2)

If the response function is smooth enough, the convexity requirement can be translated into the requirement that the Hessian of
\hat{\epsilon}
with respect to each of its arguments is positive semi-definite.

Since entropy
\eta
is typically not directly observable, but (the heating measure) absolute temperature
T
is, it is more convenient to work with the Helmholtz free energy
\Psi
, which follows from the Legendre transform of the internal energy

\Psi(\mathbf{F},T)=\epsilon(\mathbf{F},\eta)-T\eta\ ,

(3)

where
T=\partial_{\eta}\epsilon
and
\eta=-\partial_{T}\Psi
.
This transform implies that the free energy
\hat{\Psi}

\Psi(\mathbf{F},T)=\hat{\Psi}(\mathbf{F},\mathbf{F}^{*},\det\mathbf{F},T)

(4)

is convex in the deformation measures and
concave
in
T
.

Objectivity requires
\mathbf{F}
to be replaced an objective strain measure
[
42
]
to eliminate the dependence on the local rotation.
We chose the right Cauchy–Green deformation tensor
\mathbf{C}\equiv\mathbf{F}^{T}\mathbf{F}
and also assume isotropic material behavior so that the formulation of the free energy can be reduced to dependence on three scalar deformation invariants and temperature:

\Psi(\mathbf{F},T)=\check{\Psi}(I_{1},I_{2},I_{3},T)\ ,

(5)

where, again, we have a choice in what invariants to use
[
4
]
.
The Cayley-Hamilton invariants

I_{1}=\operatorname{tr}\mathbf{C},\quad I_{2}=\operatorname{tr}\mathbf{C}^{*}=\tfrac{1}{2}(\operatorname{tr}^{2}\mathbf{C}-\operatorname{tr}\mathbf{C}^{2}),\quad I_{3}=\det\mathbf{C}

(6)

are sufficient, hence the function
\check{\Psi}(I_{1},I_{2},I_{3},T)
is required to be

\begin{matrix}[l]\bullet&\text{convex and monotonically increasing in}\ I_{1}\ \text{and}\ I_{2},\\
\bullet&\text{convex in}\ I_{3}>0\ \text{or}\ J=\sqrt{I_{3}},\\
\bullet&\text{concave in }\ T.\end{matrix}

(7)

We will call a free energy that satisfies these conditions polyconvex-concave (PCC).

With this formulation, the second Piola-Kirchhoff
\mathbf{S}
stress can be obtained from

\displaystyle\mathbf{S}=2\boldsymbol{\partial}_{\mathbf{C}}\Psi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\sum_{i=1}^{3}\partial_{I_{i}}\Psi\,\boldsymbol{\partial}_{\mathbf{C}}I_{i}

(8)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\partial_{I_{1}}\Psi\,\mathbf{I}+2\det(\mathbf{C})\left(\partial_{I_{2}}\Psi\,\left(\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{C}^{-1})\mathbf{C}^{-1}-\mathbf{C}^{-2}\right)+\partial_{I_{3}}\Psi\,\mathbf{C}^{-1}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\left((\partial_{I_{1}}\Psi+I_{1}\partial_{I_{2}}\Psi)\,\mathbf{I}-\partial_{I_{2}}\Psi\,\mathbf{C}+I_{3}\partial_{I_{3}}\Psi\,\mathbf{C}^{-1}\right)\ .

The corresponding Cauchy stress
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
is

\boldsymbol{\sigma}=J^{-1}\mathbf{F}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{F}^{T}=2\left(J\partial_{I_{3}}\Psi\,\mathbf{I}+\frac{1}{J}\left((\partial_{I_{1}}\Psi+I_{1}\partial_{I_{2}}\Psi)\,\mathbf{B}-\partial_{I_{2}}\Psi\,\mathbf{B}^{2}\right)\right)\ ,

(9)

where
\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{F}\mathbf{F}^{T}
is the left Cauchy-Green deformation tensor.

Some phenomenological expectations are also worth considering.
First, most solid materials expand with increasing temperature, i.e. the volume change at zero pressure is an increasing function of temperature:

\partial_{T}J_{p=0}>0\ \text{where}\ J_{p=0}=J\ |\ p(J,T)=0\ .

(10)

Second, many solid materials soften with increasing temperature i.e. their elastic moduli are decreasing functions of temperature, e.g.

\partial_{T}\kappa<0\ ,

(11)

where the Lamé moduli and bulk modulus are defined by

\displaystyle\lambda

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{30}\left(4\mathbb{C}:\mathbb{I}-\mathbb{C}:\mathbb{J}\right)\ ,

(12)

\displaystyle\mu

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{60}\left(3\mathbb{C}:\mathbb{J}-2\mathbb{C}:\mathbb{I}\right)\ ,

(13)

\displaystyle\kappa

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{9}\mathbb{C}:\mathbb{I}\ ,

(14)

and the elastic tensor is
\mathbb{C}=4\,\boldsymbol{\partial}^{2}_{\mathbf{C}}\Psi
evaluated at the reference configuration (
\mathbf{C}=\mathbf{I}
,
T=0
).
The components of the isotropic tensors are
\mathbb{I}_{ijkl}=\delta_{ij}\delta_{kl}
and
\mathbb{J}=\delta_{ik}\delta_{jl}+\delta_{il}\delta_{jk}
.
Third, the positive heat capacity
c_{V}\equiv-T\,\partial_{T}^{2}\Psi
is expected to increase (via the Debye model
[
43
]
) or remain constant (Dulong-Petit model for insulators
[
43
]
) with increasing temperature,
which also puts constraints on the form of the free energy.

Many thermoelastic formulations are predicated on the existence of a multiplicative split of deformation
[
11
]
:

\mathbf{F}=\mathbf{F}_{e}\mathbf{F}_{T}=\mathbf{F}_{e}\,\underbrace{J_{T}(T)\mathbf{I}}_{\mathbf{F}_{T}}=J_{T}(T)\mathbf{F}_{e}\ ,

(15)

which reduces to the last two equalities for an isotropic material.
The thermal deformation
\mathbf{F}_{T}
is effectively a change of the zero-stress reference configuration from a stress-free ground state
\mathbf{F}=\mathbf{I}
,
T=0
,
\mathbf{S}=\mathbf{0}
.
Hence, scaled invariants from
\bar{\mathbf{C}}=J^{-2/3}\mathbf{C}
that decouple the two deformations are commonly used:

\displaystyle J

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\det\mathbf{C}}=J_{T}J_{e}\ ,

(16)

\displaystyle\bar{I}_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle J^{-2/3}\operatorname{tr}\mathbf{C}\ ,

(17)

\displaystyle\bar{I}_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle J^{-4/3}\operatorname{tr}\mathbf{C}^{*}\ .

(18)

For the incompressible, isotropic case, Eq. (
15
) is essentially a spherical(thermal)-deviatoric(mechanical) split of the deformation gradient such that
\det\mathbf{F}\equiv J_{T}
.
For the geometric constraint of incompressibility
J_{e}=1
, the pressure
p=1/3\operatorname{tr}\boldsymbol{\sigma}
is indeterminant, so all volumetric changes can be attributed to
\mathbf{F}_{T}
.
Furthermore, if the free energy is a homogeneous function of temperature then

\Psi(\mathbf{F}=J_{T}\mathbf{F}_{e})=\phi(J_{T})\,\psi_{M}(\mathbf{F}_{e})\ ,

(19)

with a purely temperature-dependent potential
\phi
, and
\psi_{M}
, which depends only on the elastic deformation.
However, this form is constrained to have
self-similar
mechanical response with temperature, i.e.:

\mathbf{S}=f(J_{T}(T))(2\boldsymbol{\partial}_{\mathbf{C}}\psi_{M}(\mathbf{C}))\,

(20)

i.e. the stress has a separable dependence on
T
and
\mathbf{C}
.

4
Representation of the free energy

There are a number of somewhat general representations of thermo-hyperelastic free energy functions in the literature

[
24
,
16
,
17
,
30
]
that use the addition of three terms: (a) a temperature-independent/mechanical-only potential
\Psi_{M}
, (b) a deformation-independent/temperature-only potential
\Psi_{T}
, and (c) a thermomechanical coupling between temperature
T
and volumetric deformation
J
:

\Psi=\Psi_{M}(I_{1},I_{2},J)+\phi_{T}(T)\psi_{M}(I_{1},I_{2},J)+\Phi_{T}(T)\ .

(21)

Furthermore, many assume a linear dependence on
T
in the coupling term
\phi_{T}=-T
, ostensibly to satisfy the convexity/concavity requirements and to reproduce the basic thermomechanical phenomenology.

A general complete representation can be found in the tensor product of functions of temperature and deformation

\Psi=\sum_{i,j}\Phi_{i}(T)\Psi_{j}(I_{1},I_{2},I_{3})\ ,

(22)

where
\Phi_{i}
and
\Psi_{j}
are the basis for well-behaved (square-integrable and separable) Hilbert spaces over
T
and the deformation invariants, respectively.
This is essentially a Schmidt decomposition
[
44
]
, which will aid the imposition of the convexity-concavity conditions.
Furthermore, the functional singular value decomposition
[
45
,
46
]
allows for a representation in terms of a diagonalized tensor product of functions, hence we can assume that the finite convex sum

\Psi=\sum_{i}\phi_{i}(T)\psi_{i}(I_{1},I_{2},I_{3})

(23)

has the power to represent all free energy potentials to any selected accuracy.
Here,
\phi_{i}>0
and
\psi_{i}
are composed of scaled sums of the basis elements
\Phi_{i}
and
\Psi_{i}
, respectively.
Without loss of generality, we choose to fulfill the requirements in Eq. (
7
) by assuming a form

\Psi(I_{1},I_{2},J,T)=\Psi_{0}(I_{1},I_{2},J)+\sum_{i=1}^{N_{c}}\phi_{i}(T)\psi_{i}(I_{1},I_{2},J)+\Phi_{T}(T)\ ,

(24)

which is polyconvex-concave (PCC)if

•

\Psi_{0}
and
\psi_{i}
are positive and convex, non-decreasing in
I_{1}
and
I_{2}
and convex in
J
,

•

\phi_{i}
and
\Phi_{T}
are concave and positive.

Note, the first term
\Psi_{0}
can be seen as part of the sum where
\phi_{0}\equiv 1
.
The function
\Phi_{T}
will be undetermined by stress data, but it plays a role in determining the heat capacity and the overall concavity of
\Psi
with respect to temperature.
Also, if we constrain
\phi_{i}(0)\equiv 0
, then
\Psi_{0}
can be interpreted as the ground state elastic potential; however, this would hamper the discovery of a separable free energy function as in Sec.
5.2.1
.
We furthermore remark that if the second derivative of
\phi_{i}(T)
is zero, then
\Phi_{T}(T)
is no longer required to be positive.
The number of terms in the expansion in Eq. (
24
) is a hyperparameter of the material model that can be determined through regularization or a greedy algorithm with cross-validation.
Also, we impose the smoothness requirements
\psi_{i}\in C^{1}
such that a well-defined stress
\mathbf{S}=\partial_{\mathbf{C}}\Psi
can be obtained from
\Psi
.

We aim to describe all the functions in Eq. (
24
) by physics-augmented neural networks.
The general form of a feedforward neural network with
L-1
hidden layers, input
\bm{x}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n^{0}}
and scalar output
x_{L}\in\mathbb{R}
is

\displaystyle\bm{x}_{0}

\displaystyle\in\mathbb{R}^{n^{0}}\ ,

(25)

\displaystyle\bm{x}_{1}=\mathcal{A}_{1}\left(\bm{x}_{0}\bm{W}_{1}^{T}+\bm{b}_{1}\right)

\displaystyle\in\mathbb{R}^{n^{1}}\ ,

\displaystyle\bm{x}_{l}=\mathcal{A}_{l}\left(\bm{x}_{l-1}\bm{W}_{l}^{T}+\bm{b}_{l}\right)

\displaystyle\in\mathbb{R}^{n^{l}}\ ,\qquad l=2,\ldots,L-1\ ,

\displaystyle x_{L}=\bm{x}_{L-1}\bm{W}_{L}^{T}+\bm{b}_{L},

\displaystyle\in\mathbb{R}\ .

Here, we have defined the network activation functions as
\mathcal{A}_{l}:\mathbb{R}^{n^{l}}\to\mathbb{R}^{n^{l}}
, the weights as
\bm{W}_{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{n^{l}\times n^{l-1}}
, and the biases as
\bm{b}_{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{n^{l}}
.
We can constrain this model form to derive functions with our required properties.
By assuming a reparametrization of the trainable parameters using a smoothed
gating
system (c.f. Louizos
et al.
​
[
47
]
and Fuhg
et al.
​
[
48
]
) the number of non-zero weights and biases can be pruned using a form of smoothed
L^{0}
regularization. To regularize our networks and prevent overfitting, we have used this concept in the following when only limited experimental data in specific stress states is available.

Network construction for
\Psi_{0}
and
\psi_{i}
.

Following Ref.
[
31
]
, the output of the network
x_{L}
is convex with regards to its input
x_{0}
if the weights
\bm{W}_{l}
with
l=2,\ldots,L
are non-negative and all the activation functions are convex and non-decreasing.
In this work, we use the Softplus activation function
\mathcal{A}_{l}(x)=\log(1+\exp x)
for all the layers (in a component-wise fashion).
Additionally, due to the positivity of the Softplus function, if the bias vector of the output is positive
\bm{b}_{L}\geq 0
, then the network output is positive.
Lastly, the network output is non-decreasing if
\bm{W}_{1}\geq 0
is also enforced
[
1
]
.
Following Linden
et al.
​
[
41
]
, in order to be able to be able to represent negative stresses, the additional invariant
-2J
is used as an input to both networks.

Network construction for
\phi_{i}
.

We consider two different approaches to fulfill the requirements for polyconvexity-concavity (PCC):

1.

As discussed before, one option is to constrain the networks
\phi_{i}
to be positive and concave.
The first derivative of a scalar network output
x_{L}\in\mathbb{R}
with respect to a scalar positive input
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
is given by
[
49
,
50
]
:

\frac{dx_{L}}{dx_{0}}=\prod_{l=0}^{L-1}\left[({\mathcal{A}}_{L-l}^{{}^{\prime}}(\underbrace{\bm{x}_{L-l-1}\bm{W}_{L-l}^{T}+\bm{b}_{L-l}}_{\bm{y}_{L-l}}))^{T}\bm{j}_{L-l}\right]\circ\bm{W}_{L-l}\ ,

(26)

where
\bm{j}_{l}
are row vectors of ones with same size as
\bm{x}_{l-1}
.
The second derivative of the network output is given by:

\frac{d^{2}x_{L}}{d^{2}x_{0}}=\sum_{l=1}^{L}\bm{J}N^{l+1,L}\left(\left[\left\{(\mathcal{A}_{l}^{{}^{\prime\prime}})^{T}\circ\bm{m}\right\}\bm{j}_{l}\right]\circ\bm{W}_{l}\right)\bm{J}N^{1,l-1}\ ,

(27)

where

\displaystyle\bm{J}N^{p,q}

\displaystyle=\prod_{k=L-q}^{L-p}\left[\Big(\mathcal{A}_{L-k}^{{}^{\prime}}(\bm{x}_{L-k-1}\bm{W}_{L-k}^{T}+\bm{b}_{L-k})\Big)^{T}\bm{j}_{L-k}\right]\circ\bm{W}_{L-k}\ ,

(28)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{l}^{{}^{\prime\prime}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{A}_{l}^{{}^{\prime\prime}}(\bm{x}_{l-1}\bm{W}_{l}^{T}+\bm{b}_{l})\ ,

\displaystyle\bm{m}

\displaystyle=\bm{W}_{l}\bm{J}N^{1,l-1}\ .

In order for the networks to be positive and concave we require that
x_{L}>0
and
\frac{d^{2}x_{L}}{d^{2}x_{0}}\leq 0
.
We can achieve this by enforcing the constraints:

•

\bm{W}_{l}\geq 0
and
\bm{b}_{l}\geq 0
for
l=1,\ldots L
,

•

\mathcal{A}_{l}:\mathbb{R}_{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
for
l=1,\ldots L-1
,

•

\mathcal{A}_{l}^{\prime}:\mathbb{R}_{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
for
l=1,\ldots L-1
,

•

\mathcal{A}_{l}^{\prime\prime}:\mathbb{R}_{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{-}
for
l=1,\ldots L-1
.

A possible option for the activation function is the logistic function

\mathcal{A}(x)=\frac{1}{1+\exp(-x)}\ ,

(29)

which is positive and has the derivatives:

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\exp(-x)}{\left(1+\exp(-x)\right)^{2}}\ ,

(30)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}^{\prime\prime}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2\exp(-2x)}{\left(\exp(-x)+1\right)^{3}}-\frac{\exp(-x)}{\left(1+\exp(-x)\right)^{2}}\ .

(31)

One problematic aspect of these constraints is that the network is overconstrained to be positive, non-decreasing, and concave, which appears to be only possible over a finite range or by a constant function.
However, since the reciprocal of a positive, non-decreasing, and concave function is positive, non-increasing, and concave we can model decreasing functional forms of
\phi_{i}
by taking the reciprocal of the network output.
This would require
a priori
knowledge of the functional form of
\phi_{i}
.

2.

A second option is to enforce the functions
\phi_{i}
to be positive and
\partial^{2}_{T}\phi_{i}=0
.
This can, for example, be achieved by choosing a piecewise linear functional form that guarantees positivity.
We can construct a neural network that has similar characteristics by ensuring that the weight and bias of the output layer are positive
\bm{W}_{L}\geq 0
and
\bm{b}_{L}\geq 0
, by choosing a positive activation function in the last hidden layer
\mathcal{A}_{L-1}\geq 0
, and by guaranteeing that the second derivative of all activation functions is zero, i.e.
\mathcal{A}_{l}^{\prime\prime}=0
.
Possible options for
\mathcal{A}_{L-1}
are

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{L-1}(x)=\max(0,x),\quad\mathcal{A}_{L-1}(x)=|x|,\quad\mathcal{A}_{L-1}(x)=\max(-x,0)\ .

(32)

For the remaining activation functions
\mathcal{A}_{k}
with
k\neq L-1
, we can also include negative linear functions such as

\mathcal{A}_{k}(x)=\max(0,x)+a\min(0,x),\quad a\in\mathbb{R}\ .

(33)

In this case, in order to ensure polyconvexity,
\Psi_{T}
needs to be concave in
T
.
In the following, we pursue this second option and employ
ReLU
activation functions
\mathcal{A}_{l}(x)=\max(0,x)
throughout.

Network construction for
\Psi_{T}
.

As mentioned, in this work we assume that only stress data over different temperatures is available; hence,
\Psi_{T}
is left undetermined.
However, the function has to be positive and concave if the second derivative of at least one of the networks
\phi_{i}
is non-zero.
This can be achieved with the network architecture described for
\phi_{i}
. Otherwise a concave
\Psi_{T}
is sufficient for PCC.

To demonstrate the efficacy of the proposed machine learning framework for thermo-hyperelastic material modeling, we use two synthetic datasets where all components of strain and temperature are controlled and all stress components are observed, as well as three experimental datasets with limited observations
[

,

,
53
]
.
Although it is typical to use an empirical temperature scale, we will maintain our use of an absolute temperature

and employ

5.1
Synthetic data

For these examples, we use analytic free energy data generating models.
To obtain the training dataset we sample
(\mathbf{F},T)
uniformly as
[\mathbf{F}-\mathbf{I}]_{ij}\in\mathcal{U}[0.6,1.4]
and
T\in\mathcal{U}[0,2]
, and calculate the resulting
\mathbf{C}
and
\mathbf{S}
.
Here
\mathcal{U}(a,b)
is a uniform distribution over the range
[a,b]
and
T=1
is nominally room temperature.
For the training of the models on the generated data sets with
N_{D}
data points, we use a mean squared loss on training samples
\mathbf{S}_{i}
of the stress tensor:

\mathcal{L}=\frac{1}{N_{D}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{D}}\|\mathbf{S}_{i}-2\boldsymbol{\partial}_{\mathbf{C}}\Psi(\mathbf{C}_{i},T_{i})\|^{2}\ .

(34)

Recall that training on stress data implies that we only determine
\Psi
up to a function of temperature
\Psi_{T}(T)
, c.f. Eq. (
24
).
In the following, the neural network models related to the mechanical components
\Psi_{0}
and
\psi_{i}
are composed of 2 hidden layers with 30 neurons with Softplus activation function.
The temperature-dependent networks
\psi_{i}
consist of 2 layers with 40 neurons with ReLU as the activation function.
We employ the classical ADAM optimizer
[
54
]
with a learning rate of
10^{-3}
.

5.1.1
Neo-Hookean material

As mentioned in Sec.
4
, some thermo-hyperelastic models in the literature
[
17
]
have free energies of the form

\Psi=\Psi_{0}(\mathbf{C})-T\eta(J,T)+\Phi_{T}(T)\ ,

(35)

where the entropy contribution
-T\eta
provides coupling between the deformation and temperature.
For this type of decomposition, the second Piola-Kirchhoff stress
\mathbf{S}
is

\mathbf{S}=2\partial_{\mathbf{C}}\Psi_{0}(\mathbf{C})-2T\partial_{J}\eta\boldsymbol{\partial}_{\mathbf{C}}J=\underbrace{2\partial_{\mathbf{C}}\Psi_{0}(\mathbf{C})}_{\mathbf{S}_{0}}-TJ\partial_{J}\eta\mathbf{C}^{-1}\ ,

(36)

and the Cauchy stress
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
is

\boldsymbol{\sigma}=\underbrace{2J^{-1}\mathbf{F}\partial_{\mathbf{C}}\Psi_{0}(\mathbf{C})\mathbf{F}^{T}}_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{0}}+TJ\partial_{J}\eta\mathbf{I}\ .

(37)

Note that the entropy contribution is zero at the absolute temperature
T=0
so that
\mathbf{S}_{0}
and
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{0}
are the zero temperature stress response derived from
\Psi_{0}
.
Also, if the entropy is not temperature-dependent, the stress response is self-similar with respect to
T
.

In particular, we take
\Psi_{0}
to be the potential for a Rivlin-type
[
55
]
compressible neo-Hookean model

\Psi_{0}=\frac{1}{2}\mu\bar{I}_{1}+\frac{1}{2}\kappa(J-1)^{2}\ ,

(38)

where
\bar{I}_{1}=J^{-2/3}\operatorname{tr}\mathbf{C}=I_{3}^{-1/3}I_{1}>0
and
J=\sqrt{\det\mathbf{C}}>0
.
This potential is convex in
I_{1}
and
J
.
We take the material properties
\kappa=0.73
and
\mu=0.41
to be temperature-independent (normalized) constants representative of a polymer with Poisson’s ratio of 0.4.
The entropy contribution

T\eta=T\left(c_{v}\Gamma_{0}\frac{J^{q}-1}{q}\right)

(39)

follows from the Mie-Gruneisen model
[
56
,
57
]
.
We set
c_{v}\Gamma_{0}=0.1
and
q=1.5
.
This free energy satisfies the PCC requirements (
7
).
Finally the temperature-only contribution

\Phi_{T}=c_{V}(T-T_{0}-T\log(T/T_{0}))

(40)

is chosen to be consistent with expectations for a
constant
heat capacity
c_{V}=T\,\partial^{2}_{T}\Psi
, which does not affect the stress.

The resulting stress measures are

\mathbf{S}=\mu J^{-2/3}(\mathbf{I}-1/3I_{1}\mathbf{C}^{-1})+\left(\kappa(1-J^{-1})+Tc_{v}\Gamma_{0}J^{q}\right)\mathbf{C}^{-1}

(41)

and

\boldsymbol{\sigma}=\mu J^{-5/3}(\mathbf{B}-1/3I_{1}\mathbf{I})+\left(\kappa(J-1)+Tc_{v}\Gamma_{0}J^{q-1}\right)\mathbf{I}\ ,

(42)

where the partial derivatives of the potential are:

\displaystyle\partial_{I_{1}}\Psi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mu I_{3}^{-1/3}\ ,

(43)

\displaystyle\partial_{I_{3}}\Psi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{2}{3}\mu I_{1}I_{3}^{-4/3}+\kappa\frac{J-1}{J}+c_{v}\Gamma_{0}J^{q-2}T\ .

(44)

Thermal expansion is given by the solution of

p=3\left((\kappa(J^{2}-J)-c_{v}\Gamma_{0}J^{q}T\right)=0

(45)

for
J
, given
T
, which is the inverse of

T(J)=\frac{\kappa}{c_{v}\Gamma_{0}}\frac{J^{2}-J}{J^{q}}\ .

(46)

For the special case of
q=3/2
, Eq. (
46
) has the explicit inverse

J(T)=1+\frac{1}{2}\left(\left(\frac{c_{v}\Gamma_{0}}{\kappa}\right)^{2}T^{2}+\sqrt{\left(\frac{c_{v}\Gamma_{0}}{\kappa}\right)^{2}T^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\left(\frac{c_{v}\Gamma_{0}}{\kappa}\right)^{4}T^{4}}\right)\ .

(47)

Projections of the analytical potential are shown in Fig.
1
.
The trained model’s correspondence with held-out experimentally realizable loading modes: (a) isothermal uniaxial stretch
\mathbf{F}=\mathbf{I}+\lambda\mathbf{e}_{1}\otimes\mathbf{E}_{1}
, (b) unequal biaxial stretch
\mathbf{F}=\mathbf{I}+\lambda(\mathbf{e}_{1}\otimes\mathbf{E}_{1}+1/2\mathbf{e}_{1}\otimes\mathbf{E}_{1})
, and (c) volumetric stretch
\mathbf{F}=(1+\lambda)\mathbf{I}
is shown in Fig.
2
.
Both the selected stress components at a fixed temperature and the 11-stress component for a sequence of temperatures illustrate the accuracy of the model on this validation data.
Since the stress response was self-similar with temperature, one coupled term
N_{c}=1
was sufficient for an accurate representation.

5.1.2
Saint Venant material

A St. Venant model
[
14
]
has a free energy

\Psi=\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{E}:\mathbb{C}\mathbf{E}-T\mathbf{M}:\mathbf{E}+\Phi_{T}(T)\ ,

(48)

where
\mathbf{E}=1/2(\mathbf{C}-\mathbf{I})
is the Lagrange strain tensor and resembles the classical linear thermoelastic model in form.
For isotropic materials, the thermal expansion tensor
\mathbf{M}=\alpha\mathbf{I}
is proportional to the identity tensor
\mathbf{I}
and
\mathbb{C}=\lambda\mathbb{I}+\mu\mathbb{J}
, so that Eq. (
48
) reduces to

\Psi=\frac{1}{2}\lambda(T)\left(\operatorname{tr}\mathbf{E}\right)^{2}+\mu(T)\operatorname{tr}\mathbf{E}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\underbrace{T\alpha(T)}_{\gamma(T)}\operatorname{tr}\mathbf{C}+\Phi(T)\ ,

(49)

where we have absorbed
1/2\gamma(T)\operatorname{tr}\mathbf{I}
into
\Phi_{T}(T)
.
The second Piola-Kirchhoff stress is

\mathbf{S}=(\lambda\operatorname{tr}\mathbf{E}-\gamma)\mathbf{I}+\mu\mathbf{E}\ ,

(50)

which, like the previous model, has thermal expansion effects added to a temperature-independent elastic response.
However, the resulting Cauchy stress

\boldsymbol{\sigma}=\frac{1}{J}\left((\frac{1}{2}\lambda(\operatorname{tr}\mathbf{B}-3)-\gamma)\mathbf{B}+\frac{1}{2}\mu(\mathbf{B}^{2}-\mathbf{B})\right)

(51)

has the coefficient controlling thermal expansion
\gamma
paired with the right Cauchy-Green stretch
\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{F}\mathbf{F}^{T}
.
Since the deformation components are positive and convex, the coefficients
\lambda
,
\mu
, and
\kappa
need to be positive and concave with respect to
T
.
Although being convex in
I_{1}
and
I_{2}
this potential does not satisfy the monotonicity requirements (
7
) of a PCC free energy.

For the particular temperature dependence of the moduli we use:

\displaystyle\lambda

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda_{0}\frac{\tanh(T_{c}-T)+1}{\tanh(T_{c})+1}\ ,

(52)

\displaystyle\mu

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mu_{0}\frac{\tanh(T_{c}-T)+1}{\tanh(T_{c})+1}\ ,

(53)

\displaystyle\gamma

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\gamma_{0}\left(\frac{T}{T_{0}}\right)^{a}\ ,

(54)

with
\lambda_{0}=0.73
,
\mu_{0}=0.41
,
\kappa_{0}=\lambda_{0}+2/3\mu_{0}=1.0
,
\gamma_{0}=0.2
,
a=1/2
,
T_{0}=1.0
, and
T_{c}=2T_{0}
.
Note that
\tanh(a-x)
is concave for
x<a
so
\lambda
and
\mu
are concave in
T\in[0,T_{c}]
.
Furthermore, it is
\lambda(0)=\lambda_{0}
and
\lim_{T\to\infty}\lambda=0
, and likewise relations hold for
\mu
.
The coefficient controlling the thermal expansions starts with
\gamma(0)=0
but grows with temperature.
The different temperature dependencies of
\gamma
and
\kappa=\lambda+2/3\mu
ensure that the resulting stresses are not self-similar with respect to
T
.

Thermal expansion is given by the solution of

p=\frac{3}{2}J\bigl((J^{2/3}-1)\underbrace{\left(3\lambda(T)+2\mu(T)\right)}_{3\kappa(T)}-2\gamma(T)\bigr)=0

(55)

for
J
, given
T
, which is:

J(T)=\left(1+\frac{2\gamma(T)}{3\kappa(T)}\right)^{3/2}\ .

(56)

Projections of the analytical potential are shown in Fig.
3
.
We used a greedy algorithm based on cross-validation error to arrive at a two-coupled term
N_{c}=2
fit, c.f. Eq. (
24
).
An
L^{p}
regularization approach might have been equally effective at determining how many coupling terms are needed for a sufficient accurate representation.
The trained model correspondence with held-out validation data is given in Fig.
4
, which shows the ability of the representation to capture thermal softening and thermal expansion trends for a response that does not have a self-similar response with temperature.

For this demonstration, we employed a mean squared loss on the force
F
and moment
M
for the tension-torsion data

\mathcal{L}=\frac{1}{N_{D}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{D}}\big(|F_{i}-F(\gamma_{i},\tau_{i},T_{i})|^{2}+|M_{i}-M(\gamma_{i},\tau_{i},T_{i})|^{2}\big)

(57)

which is amended by an
L^{0}
regularization term following Fuhg

​

]
.

F
and moment
M

\gamma
and twist per stretched length

.

[

,

\displaystyle F

\displaystyle M

\displaystyle=

(59)

which is based on the ansatz for the motion:

(61)

\displaystyle z

(62)

5.2.2
Uniaxial tension of pig tissue

Zhang
et al.
​
[
51
]
collected tension data of pig ear tissue over a range of temperatures to guide cryosurgery practice.
Since only tensile data was provided, we employed a mean squared loss on the non-zero stress component of the Cauchy stress

\mathcal{L}=\frac{1}{N_{D}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{D}}\big\|[\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{i}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}(\mathbf{B}_{i},T_{i})]_{11}\big\|^{2}

(66)

with an additional term for the
L^{0}
regularization.
We assume that the deformation is incompressible, i.e.
J_{e}=1
.
To account for the incompressibility constraint we assume the adiabatic thermoelastic split
J=\det\mathbf{F}=J_{T}J_{e}=J_{T}
from Eq. (
15
).
Then, we assert
\overline{\Psi}(I_{1},I_{2},J,T)
to be the approximation of an unconstrained free energy function with a neural network.
We enforce the incompressibility constraint
J_{e}=1
by introducing the Lagrange multiplier
p
in

\displaystyle\Psi(I_{1},I_{2},J,T)=\overline{\Psi}(I_{1},I_{2},J,T)+(p+n)(J_{e}-1)\ ,

(67)

where
n
is used to set the stress response to be
\mathbf{S}=\mathbf{0}
at
\mathbf{F}=\mathbf{I}
and
T=0
.

Using the following derivatives

\frac{\partial I_{1}}{\partial\mathbf{C}}=\mathbf{I},\quad\frac{\partial I_{2}}{\partial\mathbf{C}}=I_{1}\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{C},\quad\frac{\partial I_{3}}{\partial\mathbf{C}}=J^{2}\mathbf{C}^{-1}=J_{T}^{2}J_{e}^{2}\mathbf{C}^{-1},\quad\frac{\partial J}{\partial I_{3}}=\frac{1}{2J}=\frac{1}{2J_{T}J_{e}},\quad\frac{\partial J_{e}}{\partial J}=\frac{1}{J_{T}}\ ,

(68)

and Eq. (
9
), we arrive at:

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{J}\mathbf{F}\left(\frac{\partial\overline{\Psi}}{\partial I_{1}}\mathbf{I}+\frac{\partial\overline{\Psi}}{\partial I_{2}}\left[I_{1}\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{C}\right]+\frac{\partial\overline{\Psi}}{\partial J}\frac{J_{T}}{2}\mathbf{C}^{-1}\right)\mathbf{F}^{T}+(p+n)\mathbf{I}\ .

(69)

The stress normalization constant
n
is defined as

\displaystyle n=\left.\left(-2\frac{\partial\overline{\Psi}}{\partial I_{1}}-\frac{\partial\overline{\Psi}}{\partial J}\right)\right\rvert_{\mathbf{F}_{e}=\mathbf{I},T=0}.

(70)

For uniaxial tension under incompressibility, the deformation gradient and the Cauchy stress reduce to

\mathbf{F}=\text{diag}\Bigl\{\lambda,\sqrt{\frac{J_{T}}{\lambda}},\sqrt{\frac{J_{T}}{\lambda}}\Bigr\}\quad\text{and}\quad\boldsymbol{\sigma}=\text{diag}\{\sigma_{11},0,0\}.

(71)

The conditions
\sigma_{22}=\sigma_{33}=0
restrict the Lagrange multiplier to be

p=-\frac{2}{\sqrt{I_{3}}\lambda}\left[\frac{\partial\overline{\Psi}}{\partial I_{1}}+\frac{\partial\overline{\Psi}}{\partial I_{2}}\left(\lambda^{2}+\frac{2\sqrt{I_{3}}}{\lambda}-\frac{I_{3}}{\lambda}\right)+\frac{\partial\overline{\Psi}}{\partial J}\frac{\lambda}{2\sqrt{I_{3}}}\right]-n\ .

(72)

The non-zero, uniaxial component of the Cauchy stress is then given by

\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}_{11}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\sqrt{I_{3}}}\lambda^{2}\left(\frac{\partial\overline{\Psi}}{\partial I_{1}}+\frac{\partial\overline{\Psi}}{\partial I_{2}}\frac{2\sqrt{I_{3}}}{\lambda}+\frac{\partial\overline{\Psi}}{\partial J}\frac{\sqrt{I_{3}}}{2}\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}\right)+p+n\ .

(73)

Due to the limited data, we validate the output of our models by plotting the response for equibiaxial tension under different temperatures.
Incompressible equibiaxial tension is defined by

\mathbf{F}=\text{diag}\Bigl\{\lambda,\lambda,\frac{J_{T}}{\lambda^{2}}\Bigr\}\quad\text{and}\quad\boldsymbol{\sigma}=\text{diag}\{T_{11},T_{22},0\}\ ,

(74)

with the invariants

\displaystyle I_{1}=2\lambda^{2}+\frac{J^{2}_{T}}{\lambda^{4}}=2\lambda^{2}+\frac{I_{3}}{\lambda^{4}},\quad I_{2}=\lambda^{2}+2\frac{I_{3}}{\lambda^{2}}\ .

(75)

Enforcing
\sigma_{33}=0
sets the Lagrange multiplier to

\displaystyle p=-\frac{2\sqrt{I_{3}}}{\lambda^{4}}\left(\frac{\partial\overline{\Psi}}{\partial I_{1}}+\frac{\partial\overline{\Psi}}{\partial I_{2}}2\lambda^{2}+\frac{\partial\overline{\Psi}}{\partial J}\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2\sqrt{I_{3}}}\right)-n\ .

(76)

Therefore the predicted equibiaxial stresses are

\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}_{11}=\hat{\sigma}_{22}=\frac{2\lambda^{2}}{\sqrt{I_{3}}}\left(\frac{\partial\overline{\Psi}}{\partial I_{1}}+\frac{\partial\overline{\Psi}}{\partial I_{2}}\left[\lambda^{2}+\frac{I_{3}}{\lambda^{4}}\right]+\frac{\partial\overline{\Psi}}{\partial J}\frac{\sqrt{I_{3}}}{2}\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}\right)+(p+n)\ .

(77)

The results shown in Fig.
7(a)
demonstrate that the model represents the data well while also predicting plausible responses for equibiaxial tension at various temperatures, see Fig.
7(b)
.
Using
L^{0}
regularization, the total sum of trainable parameters of the models reaches around 20 after training, as seen in Fig.
7(c)
.
Furthermore, the functional form of the temperature-dependent strain energy component
\phi_{1}(T)
, as plotted in Fig.

highlights that the model seems to be able to generalize plausibly far outside the range of temperatures in the training data.

6
Conclusion

We presented a complete representation of physics-constrained machine learning of thermoelastic stress behavior based on a thermomechanically constrained free energy function and demonstrated the ability of its neural network implementation to accurately model both synthetic and experimental data.
The model is capable of representing non-self-similar temperature dependence, general thermal expansion, thermal softening, and thermal inversion.
The series representation is amenable to both term-wise and parameter-wise sparsification that imparts a degree of generalization and interpretability to the resulting models.

The formalism is directly extensible to anisotropic thermo-hyperelasticity through the inclusion of additional invariants associated with a structure tensor that characterizes the symmetry group and use of the isotropization theorem.
As we have shown in Ref.
[
39
]
, these symmetries can be learned, as well as presupposed.
