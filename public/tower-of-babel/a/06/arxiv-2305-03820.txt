Modeling Model Predictive Control: A Category Theoretic Framework for Multistage Control Problems
Tyler Hanks, Baike She, Evan Patterson, Matthew Hale, Matthew Klawonn, James Fairbanks*

Abstract
Model predictive control (MPC) is an optimal control technique which involves solving a sequence of constrained optimization problems across a given time horizon. In this paper, we introduce a category theoretic framework for constructing complex MPC problem formulations by composing subproblems. Specifically, we construct a monoidal category — called Para ⁡ ( Conv ) \Para(\textnormal{{Conv}}) — whose objects are Euclidean spaces and whose morphisms represent constrained convex optimization problems. We then show that the multistage structure of typical MPC problems arises from sequential composition in Para ⁡ ( Conv ) \Para(\textnormal{{Conv}}) , while parallel composition can be used to model constraints across multiple stages of the prediction horizon. This framework comes equipped with a rigorous, diagrammatic syntax, allowing for easy visualization and modification of complex problems. Finally, we show how this framework allows a simple software realization in the Julia programming language by integrating with existing mathematical programming libraries to provide high-level, graphical abstractions for MPC.

MPC consists of specifying and repeatedly solving constrained optimization problems designed to model the response of a system to control inputs while minimizing a cost function over a finite prediction horizon. The generality of MPC gives it several benefits, including the ability to incorporate constraints on a system’s state and control inputs that must be satisfied at each timestep or across multiple different timesteps in the prediction horizon.

As the complexity of MPC problems has grown over time, so too has interest in the identification and use of structure inherent to such problems, such as sparsity or decoupling in a problem’s constraints. In this vein, we develop a novel perspective on the compositional nature of MPC problems. Specifically, we formalize the multistage structure inherent to MPC problems by utilizing tools from

]
, dynamical systems

Our specific technical contributions are as follows. We first show that convex bifunctions, which were introduced by Rockafellar
[

We then apply the

In addition to showing that MPC optimization problems are an instance of a general categorical abstraction, framing MPC in the unified mathematical language of category theory comes with a number of practical benefits, including a rigorous graphical syntax for compositionally building MPC problems, and a straightforward path to a clean and correct software implementation. Motivation and Problem Statements

In this section, we aim to build intuition behind the compositional structure of MPC optimization problems. MPC typically operates on a discretized version of the system dynamics
\dot{x}=f(x,u)
represented by
x_{i+1}=f_{d}(x_{i},u_{i})
for

can be generated from

using a
discretization technique such as Euler’s method or the Runge-Kutta method. When the dynamics are discretized, the MPC problem naturally exhibits a multistage structure which we discuss below.

2.1
Multistage MPC Problems

In this paper, we consider multistage MPC problems of the following form.

.

Let

and

Example 2.2
.

Suppose we want to implement a
constrained
linear-quadratic regulator for a linear system given by
x_{k+1}=Ax_{k}+Bu_{k}
by minimizing the cost

J=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}x_{k}^{T}Qx_{k}+u_{k}^{T}Ru_{k},

while remaining within operational bounds

14
]

\begin{array}[]{ll@{}ll}\text{minimize}&\sum_{i=k}^{k+N-1}x_{i}^{T}Qx_{i}+u_{i}^{T}Ru_{i}&\\
\text{subject to}&x_{i+1}=Ax_{i}+Bu_{i},\ i=k,\dots,k+N-1,\\
&x_{min}\leq x_{i}\leq x_{max},\ i=k,\dots,k+N-1,\\
&u_{min}\leq u_{i}\leq u_{max},\ i=k,\dots,k+N-1,\\
&x_{k}=x_{init}.\end{array}

This problem will serve as a simple, illustrative example for demonstrating the use of our framework.

Problem formulations such as these are typically referred to as having multistage or staged structure because they can intuitively be viewed as distinct subproblems linked together by some of the decision variables.

To illustrate this, consider the following parameterized problem for a fixed
i
representing a single stage of the MPC problem. We call this an
MPC subproblem
.

x_{i}\mapsto\begin{array}[]{ll@{}ll}\text{minimize}&\ell(x_{i},u_{i})&\\
\text{subject to}&x_{i+1}=f_{d}(x_{i},u_{i}),&\\
&g(x_{i},u_{i})\leq 0.&\\
\end{array}

(1)

When viewed this way in terms of individual stages, it is clear that the goal of the
i^{th}
subproblem is to find an optimal
u_{i}
and
x_{i+1}
given a perturbation to its constraints by
x_{i}
. Subsequently, the value of
x_{i+1}
becomes a perturbation to the constraints of the
(i+1)^{th}
subproblem. This is the multistage structure we need to capture in order to give a compositional perspective on MPC problems. Crucially, only the state variables couple subproblems through time; the control variables are independent to each subproblem. These observations motivate the central problems of this paper.

Category Theory Background

This section briefly reviews concepts from category theory used in the rest of this paper. We refer the reader to
[
15
]

The most basic notion in category theory is that of a category. Categories can be seen as directed graphs with extra structure and are often used to model a specific domain of mathematical objects and relationships between them. Different categories can then be related by functors, which are the structure-preserving maps between categories.

Definition 3.1
.

A
category

\mathcal{C}
consists of a collection of
objects

X,Y,Z,\dots
, and a collection of
morphisms
(also called
arrows
)
f,g,h,\dots
, together with the following data:

•

Each morphism has a specified
domain
and
codomain
object; the notation
f:X\rightarrow Y
signifies that
f
is a morphism with domain
X
and codomain
Y
.

•

Each object
X\in\mathcal{C}
has a distinguished
identity
morphism
\text{id}_{X}:X\rightarrow X
.

•

For any triple of objects
X,Y,Z\in\mathcal{C}
and any pair of morphisms
f:X\rightarrow Y
and
g:Y\rightarrow Z
, there is a specified
composite
morphism
g\circ f:X\rightarrow Z
.

This data is subject to the following two axioms:

•

(Unitality) For any morphism
f:X\rightarrow Y
, we have
f\circ\text{id}_{X}=f=\text{id}_{Y}\circ f
,

•

(Associativity) For any objects

and morphisms

,

The canonical example of a category is

, which has sets for objects and functions between sets for morphisms. Composition in this category is

\text{id}_{X}(x)=x
. The next example lends intuition to the composition operation that we will define for the category

. The category
Rel
has sets as objects and has binary relations of the form

as morphisms from

Y
.
The composite of two relations

is defined to be

S\circ R\coloneqq\{(x,z)\in X\times Z\mid\textnormal{there exists }y\in Y\\
\textnormal{such that }(x,y)\in R\textnormal{ and }(y,z)\in S\}.

(2)

The identity relation is
\text{id}_{X}=\{(x,x^{\prime})\in X\times X\mid x=x^{\prime}\}.

We can map the objects and morphisms of one category to those of another another using a

Definition 3.2
.

Given categories

•

a

These maps must satisfy the following equations:

•

As an example, there is a functor from

Categories of Convex Bifunctions

Now that we have established the necessary category-theoretic background, we are ready to solve Problem
1
, namely, modeling the composition of convex programs
using categories.
According to Definition
3.1
, morphisms in a category are required to be composable, so our solution to this problem will consist of constructing a category whose morphisms represent convex optimization problems.

4.1
Convex bifunctions

The framework we develop is based on a formalization of convex optimization problems, called
convex bifunctions
, introduced by Rockafellar
[
13
, §29]
.

Definition 4.1
.

A
convex bifunction

F\colon\mathbb{R}^{m}\,\mathaccent 0{\shortmid}\rightarrow\,\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a (jointly) convex function of form
F\colon\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{\pm\infty\}
.

Henceforth, we denote the extended reals
\mathbb{R}\cup\{\pm\infty\}
by
\overline{\mathbb{R}}
. Our purpose in introducing convex bifunctions is to represent minimization problems that are
parameterized in the constraint space. For example, consider a general convex program
(P)
over a vector of decision variables
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
:

\begin{array}[]{ll@{}ll}\text{minimize}&f_{0}&(x)&\\
\text{subject to}&f_{i}&(x)\leq u_{i},&i=1,\dots,r\\
&f_{j}&(x)=u_{j},&j=r+1,\dots,m.\\
\end{array}

Here
f_{i}
is a convex function
\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\overline{\mathbb{R}}
for each
i\in\{0,\dots,r\}
and
f_{j}
is an affine function
\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\overline{\mathbb{R}}
for each
j\in\{r+1,\dots,m\}
. Writing
u\coloneqq(u_{1},\dots,u_{m})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
for the constraint parameters, let
Su
denote the set of vectors
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

that satisfy the above constraints for that choice of
u
. Different values of
u
correspond to different parameterizations of the constraints of
(P)
.
We can encapsulate this whole family of optimization problems into a convex bifunction
F\colon\mathbb{R}^{m}\,\mathaccent 0{\shortmid}\rightarrow\,\mathbb{R}^{n}
by setting

F(u,x)\coloneqq f_{0}(x)+\delta(x\mid Su)
,
where
\delta(x\mid Su)
denotes the convex indicator function

x\mapsto\begin{cases}0&\text{if }x\in Su,\\
+\infty&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

We call
F
the convex bifunction associated with the convex program
(P)
.

Remark 4.2
.

In general, any convex program is uniquely determined by its associated bifunction (see
[
13
, §29]
). For our purposes, convex bifunctions will be sums where some terms in the sum are indicator functions and others are not. The convex program associated with a given convex bifunction is one whose hard constraints are satisfied when all indicator function terms take the value
0
, implying the program’s objective function is given by the non-indicator function terms of the bifunction.

It is useful to note that convex bifunctions generalize linear transformations. In other words, there is a canonical way to embed linear transformations as convex bifunctions. For a linear map
A\colon X\rightarrow Y
, the
convex indicator bifunction of the linear transformation
is the bifunction
\delta_{A}\colon X\,\mathaccent 0{\shortmid}\rightarrow\,Y
with

\delta_{A}(x,y)\coloneqq\delta(y\mid Ax)\coloneqq\left\{\begin{array}[]{cr}0&\text{if }y=Ax,\\
+\infty&\text{if }y\neq Ax.\\
\end{array}\right.

Since convex bifunctions can be identified with convex programs, defining a composition operation for bifunctions directly yields a composition operation for convex programs. Given convex bifunctions
F\colon U\,\mathaccent 0{\shortmid}\rightarrow\,X
and
G\colon X\,\mathaccent 0{\shortmid}\rightarrow\,Y
, their composite is defined by

(G\circ F)(u,y)\coloneqq\inf_{x\in X}\{F(u,x)+G(x,y)\},

(3)

for all
u\in U
and
y\in Y
. We refer to this operation as
bifunction composition
. We adopt the convention that
\inf f=-\infty
when
f
is unbounded below.

Bifunction composition generalizes composition of linear transformations, i.e.,
\delta_{A_{2}}\circ\delta_{A_{1}}=\delta_{A_{2}\circ A_{1}}
for composable linear maps
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
. It also generalizes the relational composition given by (
2
) when relations forming a convex subset are encoded by their convex indicator bifunctions. In that case bifunction composition agrees with relation composition.
Crucially, when viewed as representing convex programs, bifunction composition captures the notion that one problem’s decision variables parameterize the constraints of the subsequent problem.

The SMC of Convex Bifunctions

We next show that bifunction composition in fact

Theorem 4.3

There is a category,

•

Proof.

Given a composable pair of convex bifunctions, their composite is again convex (

Associativity is straightforward in the case that all infima in the resulting composites are attained.
Furthermore, using the convention that

For unitality, consider a bifunction

4.3
Parameterized convex bifunctions

The category
Conv
provides a formal setting for composing optimization problems such that one problem’s decisions parameterize another problem’s constraints. MPC optimization problems exhibit this compositional structure, as the state variables at time-step
k
are computed by the
k^{th}
MPC subproblem, while they are fixed from the perspective of the
(k+1)^{th}
subproblem and
serve to parameterize the constraints of the
(k+1)^{th}
subproblem. However, the setting of
Conv
leaves out the important role played by the control variables. These are decision variables which are independent for each subproblem, i.e., they do not couple across time like the state does.
As stated,
Conv
can model the coupling between state variables in MPC but cannot model this independence of the control variables.
To capture this semantic distinction, we review another categorical construction called a

[
17
]
and then apply it to

to produce the desired results.

•

and

,

•

the identity on

is
(I,\mathrm{id}_{X})
,

•

the composite of

and

is the pair
\big(U_{2}\otimes U_{1},g\circ(\mathrm{id}_{U_{2}}\otimes f)\big)
.

5
MPC Optimization Problems are Morphisms

With the ability to compose convex optimization problems according to the rules of
Conv
and the necessary semantic distinction between control variables and state variables formalized in
\Para(\textnormal{{Conv}})
, we are ready to describe the categorical structure of the MPC optimization problem. Specifically, given a convex MPC problem
(Q)
of the form in Definition
2.1
, there exists a morphism
(U,G_{Q})
in
\Para(\textnormal{{Conv}})
such that the optimization problem associated with
Q
for a prediction horizon of
N
is the
N
-fold composite
\underbrace{(U,G_{Q})\circ\dots\circ(U,G_{Q})}_{N\text{ times}}
which we denote
(U,G_{Q})^{N}
.

Recall that at a single time-step
i
in the prediction horizon, we have the one-step MPC subproblem

\begin{array}[]{ll@{}ll}\text{minimize}&\ell(x_{i},u_{i})&\\
\text{subject to}&x_{i+1}=f_{d}(x_{i},u_{i})\\
&g(x_{i},u_{i})\leq 0.\end{array}

(4)

This problem can be interpreted as optimizing over the control input at a single time-step, i.e.,
with a prediction horizon of length
N=1
. We can reformulate this single timestep problem into a bifunction.

Definition 5.1
.

Given a discrete dynamical system
f_{d}\colon X\times U\rightarrow X
, cost function
\ell\colon X\times U\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, and constraint function
g\colon X\times U\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
, the
one-step bifunction
representing the one-step subproblem (
4
) is
G\colon U\oplus X\,\mathaccent 0{\shortmid}\rightarrow\,X
, where

G((u,x),x^{\prime})\coloneqq\ell(x,u)+\delta\Bigl(x^{\prime}\mid f_{d}(x,u)\Bigr)\\
+\delta(x,u\mid g(x,u)\leq 0).

(5)

Here,
\delta(x,u\mid g(x,u)\leq 0)
denotes the indicator function of the set
\{(x,u)\in X\times U\mid g(x,u)\leq 0\}
.

We next show that, under suitable convexity conditions, the one-step bifunction associated with an MPC
subproblem can be regarded as belonging to
\Para(\textnormal{{Conv}})
.

Proposition 5.2
.

If
f_{d}\colon X\times U\rightarrow X
is affine and the functions
\ell\colon X\times U\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and
g\colon X\times U\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
are convex,
then the pair
(U,G\colon U\oplus X\,\mathaccent 0{\shortmid}\rightarrow\,X)
, where
G
is the one-step bifunction of the MPC subproblem associated with
f_{d},\ell,
and
g
, is a morphism from
X
to
X
in
\Para(\textnormal{{Conv}})
.

Proof.

Recall that by Definition
4.7
, a morphism
X\rightarrow X
in
\Para(\textnormal{{Conv}})
is a pair
(U^{\prime},F\colon U^{\prime}\oplus X\,\mathaccent 0{\shortmid}\rightarrow\,X)
for some choice of control space
U^{\prime}
and bifunction
F
. Choosing
U
as the control space and
G
as the bifunction,
(U,G)
has the correct signature of a morphism
X\rightarrow X
in
\Para(\textnormal{{Conv}})
. Convexity of
G
follows immediately from being the sum of a convex function with indicators of convex sets.
∎

Now that we have an endomorphism
3
3

3

Endormorphisms are morphisms whose domain and codomain are equal.

(U,G)\colon X\rightarrow X
in
\Para(\textnormal{{Conv}})

(U,G)
with itself an arbitrary number of times. We use this property to formally express the compositional structure of MPC optimization problems, thus solving Problem

Theorem 5.3
.

Let
\ell\colon X\times U\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and
g\colon X\times U\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
be convex functions and let
f_{d}\colon X\times U\rightarrow X
be an affine function. If
(U,G)\colon X\rightarrow X
is the one-step bifunction in
\Para(\textnormal{{Conv}})
generated by these functions, then
(U,G)^{N}
is the parameterized convex bifunction representation of the MPC optimization problem associated with
f_{d},\ell
, and
g
over a prediction horizon of
N
.

Proof.

This proof proceeds by induction on
N
. The base case of
N=1
is proven by Proposition
5.2
. For the inductive step, suppose
(U,G)^{k}
is the parameterized convex bifunction representation of the MPC optimization problem associated with
f_{d},\ell,
and
g
over a prediction horizon of
k
. We need to show that
(U,G)^{k+1}=(U,G)\circ(U,G)^{k}
is the parameterized convex bifunction representation of the MPC problem over a prediction horizon of
k+1
. By the inductive hypothesis, we have that

(U,G)^{k}((u_{k-1},\dots,u_{0},x_{0}),x_{k})=\\
\inf_{x_{1},\dots,x_{k-1}}\Big[\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\ell(x_{i},u_{i})+\delta\Bigl(x_{i+1}\mid f_{d}(x_{i},u_{i})\Bigr)\\
+\delta\Bigl(x_{i},u_{i}\mid g(x_{i},u_{i})\leq 0\Bigr)\Big].

(6)

Then, by the definition of bifunction composition, we have

(U,G)\circ(U,G)^{k}((u_{k},\dots,u_{0},x_{0}),x_{k+1})=\\
\inf_{x_{k}}\Big[\ell(x_{k},u_{k})+\delta\Bigl(x_{k+1}\mid f_{d}(x_{k},u_{k})\Bigr)\\
+\delta\Bigl(x_{k},u_{k}\mid g(x_{k},u_{k})\leq 0\Bigr)+\text{ RHS of~\eqref{IH}}\Big]\\
=\inf_{x_{1},\dots,x_{k}}\Big[\sum_{i=0}^{k}\ell(x_{i},u_{i})+\delta\Bigl(x_{i+1}\mid f_{d}(x_{i},u_{i})\Bigr)\\
+\delta\Bigl(x_{i},u_{i}\mid g(x_{i},u_{i})\leq 0\Bigr)\Big],

(7)

which is the parameterized convex bifunction representation of the MPC problem associated with
f_{d},\ell,
and
g
over a prediction horizon of
k+1

∎

The theorem shows that any MPC optimization problem of the form in Definition

\Para(\textnormal{{Conv}})

5.1
Modeling initial and terminal constraints and costs

The formulation so far of MPC optimization problems does not impose any
restrictions in terms of costs or constraints on the state achieved at the end of the time horizon, though such
restrictions often arise in practice.
For example, we may wish to modify the objective function of Example
2.2
to include a terminal cost term
x_{N}^{T}Q_{N}x_{N}
or add the constraint that
x_{N}=c
for some desired target vector
c
. Terminal costs and constraints can be represented by a morphism
(\mathbb{R}^{0},T)\colon X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{0}
in
\Para(\textnormal{{Conv}})
for some bifunction
T\colon\mathbb{R}^{0}\oplus X\,\mathaccent 0{\shortmid}\rightarrow\,\mathbb{R}^{0}
. Since
T
has trivial control space and codomain, it is equivalent to a convex function
X\rightarrow\overline{\mathbb{R}}
. This is precisely the signature needed to encode a terminal cost or constraint function. We can incoporate this bifunction into our problem by post-composition, i.e.,

(\mathbb{R}^{0},T)\circ(U,G)^{N}
.

Similarly, fixing the initial state to a value
x_{0}
is accomplished by pre-composition with a morphism
(\mathbb{R}^{0},\delta_{x_{0}})\colon\mathbb{R}^{0}\rightarrow X
where

\delta_{x_{0}}(x)\coloneqq\left\{\begin{array}[]{cr}0&\text{if }x=x_{0},\\
+\infty&\text{if }x\neq x_{0}.\\
\end{array}\right.

The formulation of MPC with initial conditions and required terminal state or cost is thus treated symmetrically by our formalism. The general case is
(\mathbb{R}^{0},T)\circ(U,G)^{N}\circ(\mathbb{R}^{0},x_{0})
, where composition in
\Para(\textnormal{{Conv}})
correctly models the coupling between initial, sequential, and terminal steps of the control process. This is represented graphically as

7
Conclusion and Future Work

We have developed a categorical framework for formally linking model predictive control problem formulations with convex optimization problem formulations that captures the categorical structure of MPC problems.
Future work will address the construction of specialized convex optimization routines that can exploit the categorical structure of MPC problems as identified in this paper. Finally, although we focused specifically on MPC problems for linear dynamical systems, there is nothing in principle which prevents this framework from being extended to the non-linear case. This is another direction for future work.
