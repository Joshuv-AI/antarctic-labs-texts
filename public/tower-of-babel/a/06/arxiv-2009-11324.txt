Local master equations bypass the secular approximation
Stefano Scali, Janet Anders, Luis A. Correa

Abstract
Master equations are a vital tool to model heat flow through nanoscale thermodynamic systems. Most practical devices are made up of interacting sub-systems and are often modelled using either local master equations (LMEs) or global master equations (GMEs). While the limiting cases in which either the LME or the GME breaks down are well understood, there exists a ‘grey area’ in which both equations capture steady-state heat currents reliably but predict very different transient heat flows. In such cases, which one should we trust? Here we show that, when it comes to dynamics, the local approach can be more reliable than the global one for weakly interacting open quantum systems. This is due to the fact that the secular approximation , which underpins the GME, can destroy key dynamical features. To illustrate this, we consider a minimal transport setup and show that its LME displays exceptional points (EPs). These singularities have been observed in a superconducting-circuit realisation of the model [ 1 ] . However, in stark contrast to experimental evidence, no EPs appear within the global approach. We then show that the EPs are a feature built into the Redfield equation, which is more accurate than the LME and the GME. Finally, we show that the local approach emerges as the weak-interaction limit of the Redfield equation, and that it entirely avoids the secular approximation.

Local master equations may be understood as a rough approximation to the true dissipative dynamics, valid in the limit of weak interactions between the sub-systems. One that is thermodynamically sound (GME), or a truncated series expansion with limited validity and serious thermodynamic deficiencies (LME)? Strikingly, our answer is that

within its error bars, which assume weak internal couplings. This holds for any multipartite quantum heat device which uses

Open-system dynamics

2.1
The global master equation

The Hamiltonian of a
generic
multipartite open system connected to various independent bosonic heat baths reads

\boldsymbol{H}=\boldsymbol{H}_{S}+\boldsymbol{H}_{B}+\boldsymbol{H}_{SB},

(1)

where
\boldsymbol{H}_{S}
is the Hamiltonian of the system,
\boldsymbol{H}_{B}
the Hamiltonian of the baths, each made of infinitely many uncoupled harmonic oscillators, and
\boldsymbol{H}_{SB}
stands for the interaction between system and baths. Specifically, let

\boldsymbol{H}_{S}=\sum\nolimits_{i}\boldsymbol{H}^{\text{(loc)}}_{i}+k\,\boldsymbol{V},

(2)

where
\boldsymbol{H}_{i}^{\text{(loc)}}
is the local Hamiltonian of each sub-system and
\boldsymbol{V}
denotes the interactions between them. The parameter
k
controls the magnitude of the latter. Every sub-system couples to its own independent bath, i.e.

\boldsymbol{H}_{SB}=\sum\nolimits_{\alpha}\lambda_{\alpha}\,\boldsymbol{S}_{\alpha}\otimes\boldsymbol{B}_{\alpha},

(3)

where
\boldsymbol{B}_{\alpha}
is a generic bath operator and
\boldsymbol{S}_{\alpha}
is a system operator which does not commute with
\boldsymbol{H}_{S}
, thus allowing for energy
dissipation
as well as
decoherence
. Here,
\lambda_{\alpha}
controls the strength of the interaction with bath
\alpha
.

The effective equation of motion for any arbitrary system observable
\boldsymbol{O}
can be cast in the standard GKSL form
[
15
,
16
]
.
Although its microscopic derivation is textbook material
[
21
]
, we provide it in Appendix
A
, making as few assumptions as possible. Essentially, these are:

(i)

that the dissipation strengths are small,

(ii)

that system and bath start uncorrelated,

(iii)

that the bath correlation functions are short-lived,

(iv)

and that there is a clear-cut timescale separation between (fast) coherent and (slow) dissipative processes.

The so-called (partial) Redfield equation
[
52
]
(cf. Appendix
A
) follows from these assumptions,

\frac{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{O}}{\mathrm{d}t}=i[\boldsymbol{H}_{S},\boldsymbol{O}]+\sum\nolimits_{\alpha}\mathscr{R}^{\dagger}_{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{O})+\pazocal{O}(\lambda_{\alpha}^{3}).

(4)

The super-operator
\mathscr{R}^{\dagger}_{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{O})
associated to bath
\alpha
is given by

\mathscr{R}_{\alpha}^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{O})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\omega_{i}\times\omega_{j}<0}\gamma_{\omega_{i}}^{(\alpha)}\Big(\boldsymbol{A}_{\omega_{i}}^{(\alpha)\dagger}\boldsymbol{O}\boldsymbol{A}_{\omega_{j}}^{(\alpha)}\\
-\boldsymbol{A}_{\omega_{j}}^{(\alpha)}\boldsymbol{A}_{\omega_{i}}^{(\alpha)\dagger}\boldsymbol{O}\Big)+\text{h.c.}

(5)

where the summation
\omega_{i}\times\omega_{j}<0
runs over the frequencies
\omega_{i}
and
\omega_{j}
of all
open decay channels

[
21
]
with different sign. The decay rates
\gamma_{\omega}^{(\alpha)}
are

\gamma_{\omega}^{(\alpha)}=2\,\lambda_{\alpha}^{2}\,\text{Re}\,\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}s\,e^{i\omega s}\,\langle\boldsymbol{B}_{\alpha}(s)\boldsymbol{B}_{\alpha}(0)\rangle,

(6)

where the notation
\langle\cdot\rangle
indicates thermal averaging at temperature
T_{\alpha}
. Finally,
\boldsymbol{A}_{\omega}^{(\alpha)}
is the ‘jump’ operator of the decay channel at frequency
\omega
from bath
\alpha
. These satisfy

\displaystyle[\boldsymbol{H}_{S},\boldsymbol{A}_{\omega}^{(\alpha)}]

\displaystyle=-\omega\,\boldsymbol{A}_{\omega}^{(\alpha)},

(7a)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{S}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\sum\nolimits_{\omega}\boldsymbol{A}_{\omega}^{(\alpha)},

(7b)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{A}_{\omega}^{(\alpha)\,\dagger}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{A}_{-\omega}^{(\alpha)}.

(7c)

In particular, Eq. (
6
) implies that
\mathscr{R}^{\dagger}_{\alpha}
is
\pazocal{O}(\lambda_{\alpha}^{2})
. Note as well that, to make sense of (
4
) and all the following adjoint master equations, we must always take expectation values, as we do in Eqs. (
33
), (
46a
) and (
48a
).

Pushing (iv) to its last consequences justifies the (full)
secular approximation
, and allows to bring Eq. (
4
) to the simpler GKSL form,

\frac{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{O}}{\mathrm{d}t}=i[\boldsymbol{H}_{S},\boldsymbol{O}]+\sum\nolimits_{\alpha}\mathscr{G}^{\dagger}_{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{O})+\pazocal{O}(\lambda_{\alpha}^{3}),

(8)

where the super-operators
\mathscr{G}^{\dagger}_{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{O})
are given by

\mathscr{G}_{\alpha}^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{O})=\sum\nolimits_{\omega}\gamma_{\omega}^{(\alpha)}\left(\boldsymbol{A}_{\omega}^{(\alpha)\,\dagger}\boldsymbol{O}\boldsymbol{A}_{\omega}^{(\alpha)}\right.\\
\left.-\frac{1}{2}\{\boldsymbol{A}_{\omega}^{(\alpha)\,\dagger}\boldsymbol{A}_{\omega}^{(\alpha)},\boldsymbol{O}\}_{+}\right).

(9)

Here,
\{\cdot,\cdot\}_{+}
stands for the anti-commutator. Eq. (
8
) is a
global
master equation. The name tag highlights the fact that the
\boldsymbol{A}_{\omega}^{(\alpha)}
enable jumps between eigenstates of the full multipartite
\boldsymbol{H}_{S}
, rather than between states of each sub-system
\boldsymbol{H}^{\text{(loc)}}_{\alpha}
(see Fig.
1
).

Since full diagonalisation of
\boldsymbol{H}_{S}
is needed to construct these jump operators, setting up Eq. (
8
) may become computationally unworkable; especially, when one wishes to scale up a many-body open quantum system. Furthermore, the GME suffers from another important issue, especially when applied to systems with a
dense
energy spectrum. In such cases, assumption (iv) from the list above is likely to break down
[
24
]
, which could invalidate the GME’s predictions
[
22
,
23
,
53
]
. On the ‘plus side’, constructing jump operators that fulfil Eqs. (
7
) is guaranteed to bring the system into a state of thermodynamic equilibrium whenever all temperatures coincide, i.e.,
T_{\alpha}=T\penalty\ \forall\alpha
. This is analogous to the Zeroth Law of thermodynamics
[
54
]
. Since, in addition, the dynamical map resulting from (
8
) is
completely positive

[
3
]
, it can be shown that

\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{\alpha}\frac{\dot{\pazocal{Q}}_{\alpha}}{T_{\alpha}}\leq 0,

(10a)

for

\displaystyle\dot{\pazocal{Q}}_{\alpha}\coloneqq\langle\mathscr{G}^{\dagger}_{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{H}_{S})\rangle_{\infty},

(10b)

where the steady-state heat currents
\dot{\pazocal{Q}}_{\alpha}
account for the stationary rate of energy influx from bath
\alpha
(i.e.,
\langle\cdot\rangle_{\infty}
denotes here
stationary
average). Eq. (
10
) is interpreted as the Second Law of thermodynamics, since it is formally identical to the statement of Clausius’ theorem
[
17
,
2
]
. Therefore, the global GKSL equation is particularly well suited to study the thermodynamics of (weakly dissipative) open quantum systems.

2.2
The local master equation

Another type of quantum master equation may be built by simply
adding up
dissipators that act locally on a specific part of the system

\frac{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{O}}{\mathrm{d}t}=i[\boldsymbol{H}_{S},\boldsymbol{O}]+\sum\nolimits_{\alpha}\mathscr{L}_{\alpha}^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{O}),

(11)

where
\mathscr{L}_{\alpha}^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{O})
takes the form

\mathscr{L}_{\alpha}^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{O})=\sum\nolimits_{\omega^{\prime}}\gamma_{\omega^{\prime}}^{(\alpha)}\left(\boldsymbol{L}_{\omega^{\prime}}^{(\alpha)\,\dagger}\boldsymbol{O}\boldsymbol{L}_{\omega^{\prime}}^{(\alpha)}\right.\\
-\left.\frac{1}{2}\{\boldsymbol{L}^{(\alpha)\,\dagger}_{\omega^{\prime}}\boldsymbol{L}^{(\alpha)}_{\omega^{\prime}},\boldsymbol{O}\}_{+}\right).

(12)

Here, the non-hermitian operators
\boldsymbol{L}_{\omega^{\prime}}^{(\alpha)}
have the properties,

\displaystyle[\boldsymbol{H}_{\alpha}^{\text{(loc)}},\boldsymbol{L}_{\omega^{\prime}}^{(\alpha)}]

\displaystyle=-\omega^{\prime}\boldsymbol{L}_{\omega^{\prime}}^{(\alpha)},

(13a)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{S}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\sum\nolimits_{\omega^{\prime}}\boldsymbol{L}_{\omega^{\prime}}^{(\alpha)},

(13b)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{L}_{\omega^{\prime}}^{(\alpha)\,\dagger}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{L}_{-\omega^{\prime}}^{(\alpha)}.

(13c)

in place of Eqs. (
7
). Note that the sum does not run over the Bohr frequencies
\omega
of
\boldsymbol{H}_{S}
, but over those of
\sum\nolimits_{\alpha}\boldsymbol{H}_{\alpha}^{\text{(loc)}}
; hence the notation
\omega^{\prime}
.

Such local equation is easily
scalable
, as the resonators must be diagonalised individually when searching for the operators
\boldsymbol{L}_{\omega^{\prime}}^{(\alpha)}
. It also shares with the global master equation its GKSL form, which means that the resulting dynamics is, again, completely positive.

‘Completely positive’ is often equated identically to ‘physical’ because at least it produces positive probabilities. We remark, however, that (completely positive) LMEs violate the Zeroth Law by construction
[
29
]
. Indeed, due to Eq. (
13a
), the dissipators
\mathscr{L}_{\alpha}^{\dagger}
‘try’ to pull the system towards the local thermal state
\propto\exp{(-\sum_{\alpha}\,\boldsymbol{H}^{\text{(loc)}}_{\alpha}/T_{\alpha})}
, which does not commute with the full
\boldsymbol{H}_{S}
appearing in the ‘coherent-evolution’ term of Eq. (
11
). Hence, according to the LME, the system would never thermalise, even if all temperatures
T_{\alpha}
are identical. As a result, also the Second Law as stated in Eq. (
10
), may be violated. In fact, in our example model of Eq. (
15
), the local approach invariably predicts unphysical cold-to-hot heat currents whenever
\omega_{c}/T_{c}<\omega_{h}/T_{h}

[
27
]
. This, however, would cease to be an issue if the environments were
active
, i.e., able to exchange both heat and work with the system
[
55
]
.

2.3
Comparing local and global approach

A local master equation can be motivated physically beyond merely “adding up dissipators”. For instance, we may obtain the LME directly from a formal collisional model, in the limit of instantaneous collisions

55
]
. On the other hand, for microscopic Hamiltonian models, the general Markovian weak-coupling master equation can be simplified by

.

The LME (

) can be alternatively viewed as the lowest order in an expansion of the GME (

)
[

]
. Namely, applying perturbation theory to the eigenstates and eigenvalues of
\boldsymbol{H}_{S}
for small

and following the procedure to obtain a GME

, gives

\mathscr{G}^{\dagger}_{\alpha}=\mathscr{G}_{\alpha}^{\dagger\,(0)}+k\,\mathscr{G}_{\alpha}^{\dagger\,(1)}+k^{2}\,\mathscr{G}_{\alpha}^{\dagger\,(2)}+\cdots.

Whenever
\mathscr{G}^{\dagger\,(0)}_{\alpha}=\mathscr{L}_{\alpha}^{\dagger}
, one can claim that the LME becomes equivalent to truncating the expansion at zeroth order in

. Since all terms

are
\pazocal{O}(\lambda^{2})
, the LME would thus be accurate within

is weak enough, this may be acceptable when compared with the intrinsic error of the GME, set at

(cf. Eq. (

[

]
.

However, if the degeneracy of
\boldsymbol{H}_{S}
changes depending on whether
k=0
or
k\neq 0
, the zeroth order of the global dissipator does not coincide with the local one (i.e.,
\mathscr{G}^{\dagger\,(0)}_{\alpha}\neq\mathscr{L}^{\dagger}_{\alpha}
)
[

]
. As we will see, this is precisely what happens to our example model (

) when
\omega_{c}=\omega_{h}=\omega

[

,

,

,

]
. Instead, we find that it is the zeroth order of the

Exceptional points

We now formally introduce the concept of exceptional points (EPs), before drawing the link to open-system dynamics. EPs are branch-point singularities which appear under variation of parameters of non-Hermitian matrices, such as those describing the dynamics of quantum dissipative systems. EPs have been used as resource for applications such as sensitivity amplification in micro-resonators
[
61
,
62
]
, laser-mode selectivity
[
63
]
, and topological chirality
[
64
,
65
]
. More recently, a gain in signal-to-noise-ratio has been shown in EP sensors
[
66
]
highlighting the practical relevance of exceptional points.

4.1
Formal definition and witnesses

Let
\mathsf{M}(k)\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
be an
N\times N
matrix dependent on some parameter (or set of parameters)
k
. We denote the right eigenvectors of this matrix by
\{\left|v_{j}\right\rangle\}_{j=1,\cdots,N}
; i.e.,

\mathsf{M}(k)\left|v_{j}\right\rangle=\mu_{j}\left|v_{j}\right\rangle.

(21)

The corresponding
left
eigenvectors
\{(v_{j}|\}_{j=1,\cdots,N}
are defined instead by

\mathsf{M}(k)^{\mathsf{T}}(v_{j}|^{\dagger}=\mu_{j}(v_{j}|^{\dagger}.

(22)

Notice that
(v_{j}|^{\dagger}
is a
column
vector (like
\left|v_{j}\right\rangle
) due to the Hermitian conjugation. These two indexed families of vectors form a
bi-orthogonal
set
[
67
]
; that is,

\displaystyle(v_{i}|v_{j}\rangle

\displaystyle=0\quad\text{for}\penalty\ i\neq j,

(23)

\displaystyle(v_{i}|v_{i}\rangle

\displaystyle\leq 1,

with
(v_{i}|v_{i}\rangle=1
being fulfilled only if
\mathsf{M}(k)
is Hermitian. Note that, in general,
(v_{i}|v_{i}\rangle
can be negative. We say that the matrix
\mathsf{M}(k)
has an exceptional point for those parameter choices

resulting in
(v_{i}|v_{i}\rangle=0
for two or more of the indices
i\in\{1,\cdots,N\}
. This phenomenon is called
self-orthogonality
and it is the hallmark of the coalescence of two or more right eigenvectors
[
68
]
.

In order to locate the exceptional points of
\mathsf{M}(k)
we could search for zeros of the
phase rigidities

\upphi_{i}(k)\coloneqq|(v_{i}|v_{i}\rangle|
for
i\in\{1,\cdots,N\}
as a function of
k
. However, analysing the behaviour of every single eigenvector can be time-consuming for large
N
. Luckily, we shall only be interested in finding
where
in parameter space an EP of
\mathsf{M}(k)
is located, and not in
which
or
how many
eigenvectors coalesce. We can thus exploit the fact that, at an EP, the set
\{\left|v_{i}\right\rangle\}_{i=1,\cdots,N}
does not form a complete basis. Therefore, the matrix
\mathsf{V}_{k}=(\left|v_{1}\right\rangle,\cdots,\left|v_{N}\right\rangle)
features a singularity and the norm of its inverse diverges. Consequently, the
condition number
of
\mathsf{V}_{k}
, denoted
\upkappa(\mathsf{V}_{k})
, could be a suitable witness for an EP. Namely,

\displaystyle\upkappa(\mathsf{V}_{k})\coloneqq\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mathsf{V}_{k}\right\rVert}\lVert\mathsf{V}_{k}^{-1}\rVert,

(24)

where the operator norm
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}
is defined as

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mathsf{O}\right\rVert}\coloneqq\max\nolimits_{\,\mathsf{x}}{\frac{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mathsf{O}\,\mathsf{x}\right\rVert}_{p}}{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mathsf{x}\right\rVert}_{p}}},

(25)

\mathsf{x}
is an arbitrary non-zero vector, and
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}_{p}
stands for the
p
-norm

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mathsf{x}\right\rVert}_{p}\coloneqq\big(\sum\nolimits_{i}|\mathsf{x}_{i}|^{p}\big)^{1/p}.

(26)

Here, the parameter
p
could take on any real value
p\geq 1
. In our numerical calculations, we use the 2-norm. Regardless of
p
,
\upkappa(\mathsf{V}_{k})
diverges
iff
there is an EP at position
k
in parameter space. Also, note that
\lVert\mathsf{V}_{k}^{-1}\rVert
can be alternatively cast as
\lVert\mathsf{V}_{k}^{-1}\rVert=\max_{\mathsf{x}}\lVert\mathsf{x}\rVert/\lVert\mathsf{V}_{k}\,\mathsf{x}\rVert
, which resolves the issue of inverting a singular matrix.

4.2
EPs in open quantum systems

In many cases of practical interest, an open system may be fully described by choosing a set of observables
\mathord{\text{j}}=(\boldsymbol{\upvartheta}_{1},\cdots,\boldsymbol{\upvartheta}_{m})^{\mathsf{T}}
and applying the corresponding
adjoint
quantum master equation to each of them; i.e.,

\frac{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\upvartheta}_{i}}{\mathrm{d}t}=i[\boldsymbol{H}_{S},\boldsymbol{\upvartheta}_{i}]+\sum\nolimits_{\alpha}\mathscr{D}_{\alpha}^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{\upvartheta}_{i}),

(27)

where the dissipators
\mathscr{D}^{\dagger}_{\alpha}
can take, e.g., the global
\mathscr{G}^{\dagger}_{\alpha}
, local
\mathscr{L}^{\dagger}_{\alpha}
, or Redfield
\mathscr{R}^{\dagger}_{\alpha}
form (cf. (
9
), (
12
), and (
A.15
)).

The aim is to pick observables
\boldsymbol{\upvartheta}_{j}
so that (
27
) becomes a
closed
set of equations
[
69
]
. This can then be expressed in compact form as

\frac{\mathrm{d}\langle\mathord{\text{j}}\rangle}{\mathrm{d}t}=\mathsf{M}_{\mathscr{D}}\,\langle\mathord{\text{j}}\rangle,

(28)

were the resulting matrix of coefficients
\mathsf{M}_{\mathscr{D}}\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times m}
is generally non-Hermitian. At an EP,
\mathsf{M}_{\mathscr{D}}
stops being diagonalizable, which has a detectable impact on the dynamics and thermodynamics of the open system
[
70
,
69
,
71
]
.

Specifically, in continuous-variable settings with linear
\boldsymbol{H}_{S}
—as our example model—it is always possible to write a set of equations like (
28
). For instance, we can build a 14-element
\mathord{\text{j}}
by grouping the four position and momentum operators

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{q}}\coloneqq(\boldsymbol{x}_{h},\boldsymbol{p}_{h},\boldsymbol{x}_{c},\boldsymbol{p}_{c})^{\mathsf{T}},

(29)

together with the
10
distinct combinations

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{C}}_{ij}\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{q}}_{i},\boldsymbol{\mathsf{q}}_{j}\}_{+},

(30)

e.g.,
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{C}}_{33}=\boldsymbol{x}_{c}^{2}
,
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{C}}_{12}=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{C}}_{21}=\frac{1}{2}\{\boldsymbol{x}_{h},\boldsymbol{p}_{h}\}_{+}
, or
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{C}}_{32}=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{C}}_{23}=\boldsymbol{x}_{c}\,\boldsymbol{p}_{h}
. Note that other choices of
\mathord{\text{j}}
are possible. Ordering the observables so that
\boldsymbol{x}_{h}
,
\boldsymbol{p}_{h}
,
\boldsymbol{x}_{c}
, and
\boldsymbol{p}_{c}
are the first elements of
\mathord{\text{j}}
results in the
14\times 14
coefficient matrix
\mathsf{M}_{\mathscr{D}}

\mathsf{M}_{\mathscr{D}}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathsf{M}_{\mathscr{D},1}&\hskip-5.0pt\vline\hskip-5.0pt&0\\
\hline\cr 0&\hskip-5.0pt\vline\hskip-5.0pt&\mathsf{M}_{\mathscr{D},2}\end{pmatrix}.

(31)

That is, for the Hamiltonian
\boldsymbol{H}_{S}
, one finds that
\mathsf{M}_{\mathscr{D}}
is the direct sum of sub-matrices
\mathsf{M}_{\mathscr{D},1}\in\mathbb{C}^{4\times 4}
and
\mathsf{M}_{\mathscr{D},2}\in\mathbb{C}^{10\times 10}
. Looking back at Eq. (
28
), we thus see that the dynamics of the first-order moments
\langle\boldsymbol{\mathsf{q}}_{i}\rangle
decouples from that of the second-order moments
\langle\boldsymbol{\mathsf{C}}_{ij}\rangle
. Note that the same block-diagonal structure is found for the local (
\mathscr{L}
), the global (
\mathscr{G}
), and Redfield (
\mathscr{R}
) equations. Since the exceptional points are properties of the dissipator
[
71
]
, it seems reasonable that their signatures appear both in
\mathsf{M}_{\mathscr{D},1}
and
\mathsf{M}_{\mathscr{D},2}
. In Sec.
5
below, we focus on the appearance of EPs in the sub-matrices
\mathsf{M}_{\mathscr{D},1}
while the discussion about
\mathsf{M}_{\mathscr{D},2}
has been deferred to Appendix
B
.

5.2
Global master equation

In order to find the jump operators
\boldsymbol{A}_{\omega}^{(\alpha)}
within the global dissipators
\mathscr{G}_{\alpha}^{\dagger}
(cf. (
9
) and Eqs. (
7
)), we must rotate our system into its normal-mode quadratures
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Q}}=(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{1},\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{1},\boldsymbol{\eta}_{2},\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{2})^{\mathsf{T}}
, so that

\boldsymbol{H}_{S}=\sum_{j=1}^{2}\left(\frac{\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{j}^{2}}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\Omega_{j}^{2}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{j}^{2}\right).

(38)

In resonance, the orthogonal transformation
(\boldsymbol{x}_{h},\boldsymbol{x}_{c})^{\mathsf{T}}=\mathsf{P}\,(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{1},\boldsymbol{\eta}_{2})^{\mathsf{T}}
between local and global modes has the form

\mathsf{P}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\begin{matrix}1&1\\
1&-1\end{matrix}\right),

(39)

and the normal-mode frequencies are

\Omega_{1,2}=\sqrt{\omega^{2}\pm k}.

(40)

We must decompose the system’s coupling operators
\boldsymbol{x}_{\alpha}
(cf. Eq. (
16
)) in eigenoperators of
\boldsymbol{H}_{S}
. Taking, for instance,
\boldsymbol{x}_{h}
, one can see that

\boldsymbol{x}_{h}=\frac{\boldsymbol{\eta}_{1}+\boldsymbol{\eta}_{2}}{\sqrt{2}}=\frac{\boldsymbol{b}_{1}}{2\sqrt{\Omega_{1}}}+\frac{\boldsymbol{b}_{2}}{2\sqrt{\Omega_{2}}}+\text{h.c.},

(41)

where
\boldsymbol{b}_{j}
is the annihilation operator associated with
\boldsymbol{\eta}_{j}
. Hence,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{A}_{\Omega_{j}}^{(h)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathsf{P}_{1j}}{\sqrt{2\Omega_{j}}}\,\boldsymbol{b}_{j}=\frac{\mathsf{P}_{1j}}{2}\left(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{j}+\frac{{i\mkern 1.0mu}}{\Omega_{j}}\,\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{j}\right),

(42)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{A}_{\Omega_{j}}^{(c)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathsf{P}_{2j}}{\sqrt{2\Omega_{j}}}\,\boldsymbol{b}_{j}=\frac{\mathsf{P}_{2j}}{2}\left(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{j}+\frac{{i\mkern 1.0mu}}{\Omega_{j}}\,\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{j}\right).

(43)

Also note that, unlike in the local approach, now there are two open decay channels into each bath, at frequencies
\Omega_{1}
and
\Omega_{2}
, respectively.

We can obtain the equations of motion for the normal-mode variables
\langle\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Q}}\rangle
from (
9
);

namely,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\langle\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Q}}\rangle}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{M}^{\prime}_{\mathscr{G},1}\langle\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Q}}\rangle,

\displaystyle\mathsf{M}^{\prime}_{\mathscr{G},1}

\displaystyle=\bigoplus_{j=1}^{2}\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{\Delta}_{j}/2&1\\
-\Omega_{j}^{2}&\tilde{\Delta}_{j}/2\end{pmatrix},

(44)

where we have introduced the new coefficients

\tilde{\Delta}_{j}\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\sum\nolimits_{\alpha}\Delta_{\Omega_{j}}^{(\alpha)}.

(45)

For completeness, we can rotate Eq. (
5.2
) back to the original coordinates
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{q}}
, which gives

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\langle\boldsymbol{\mathsf{q}}\rangle}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{M}_{\mathscr{G},1}\langle\boldsymbol{\mathsf{q}}\rangle,

(46a)

\displaystyle\mathsf{M}_{\mathscr{G},1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{2}(\tilde{\Delta}_{1}+\tilde{\Delta}_{2})&2&\frac{1}{2}(\tilde{\Delta}_{1}-\tilde{\Delta}_{2})&0\\
-2\omega^{2}&\frac{1}{2}(\tilde{\Delta}_{1}+\tilde{\Delta}_{2})&-2k&\frac{1}{2}(\tilde{\Delta}_{1}-\tilde{\Delta}_{2})\\
\frac{1}{2}(\tilde{\Delta}_{1}-\tilde{\Delta}_{2})&0&\frac{1}{2}(\tilde{\Delta}_{1}+\tilde{\Delta}_{2})&2\\
-2k&\frac{1}{2}(\tilde{\Delta}_{1}-\tilde{\Delta}_{2})&-2\omega^{2}&\frac{1}{2}(\tilde{\Delta}_{1}+\tilde{\Delta}_{2})\end{pmatrix}.

(46b)

The coefficient matrices
\mathsf{M}^{\prime}_{\mathscr{G},1}
and
\mathsf{M}_{\mathscr{G},1}
have the same condition number
\upkappa
, since they are connected via an orthogonal transformation. As a result, they also have the same EPs, since the norms involved in the calculation of
\upkappa
remain unaffected (see Eq. (
24
)).

As seen in the middle panel of Fig.
2
, the ‘exceptional lines’ of diverging condition number in the frequency–coupling space
disappear completely
, according to the global master equation.

One may question the
validity
of the GME for such parameters. Namely, in Fig.
2
we set
k\sim\lambda^{2}_{\alpha}
whereas, to be on the safe side when it comes to the secular approximation, we should ensure instead that
k\gg\max_{\alpha}\lambda^{2}_{\alpha}

[
22
]
. However, as we show in Sec.
5.4
, the GME does lead to the correct steady-state properties at all plotted points save for the fringe
|k|\lessapprox 0.1\,\lambda_{h}^{2}
. Hence the disappearance of the EPs cannot be simply attributed to the global approach breaking down.

Thinking of the local coefficient matrix
\mathsf{M}_{\mathscr{L},1}
as the lowest order
\mathsf{M}_{\mathscr{G},1}^{(0)}
of a perturbative expansion in
k
of the dissipative contributions to
\mathsf{M}_{\mathscr{G},1}
, i.e.

\mathsf{M}_{\mathscr{G},1}=\mathsf{M}_{\mathscr{G},1}^{(0)}+k\,\mathsf{M}_{\mathscr{G},1}^{(1)}+\cdots,

it would even be tempting to disregard the EP singularities predicted by the LME as mathematical artifacts, and trust instead in the
a priori
more physical GME. However, as advanced in Sec.
2.3
, this interpretation is
not
valid here. To see why, we only need to calculate
\mathsf{M}_{\mathscr{G},1}^{(0)}
from Eq. (
46b
) and show that it differs from
\mathsf{M}_{\mathscr{L},1}
in Eq. (
34
). Namely, we must set
k=0
in all terms arising from the global dissipators
\mathscr{G}_{\alpha}^{\dagger}
(
9
), while keeping those from the commutator part of Eq. (
8
) intact
[
47
]
. This is achieved by replacing all coefficients
\tilde{\Delta}_{j}
in
\mathsf{M}_{\mathscr{G},1}
by
(\Delta_{\omega}^{(h)}+\Delta_{\omega}^{(c)})/2
, i.e.,

\mathsf{M}_{\mathscr{G},1}^{(0)}=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{2}(\Delta_{\omega}^{(h)}+\Delta_{\omega}^{(c)})&2&0&0\\
-2\omega^{2}&\frac{1}{2}(\Delta_{\omega}^{(h)}+\Delta_{\omega}^{(c)})&-2k&0\\
0&0&\frac{1}{2}(\Delta_{\omega}^{(h)}+\Delta_{\omega}^{(c)})&2\\
-2k&0&-2\omega^{2}&\frac{1}{2}(\Delta_{\omega}^{(h)}+\Delta_{\omega}^{(c)})\end{pmatrix}\neq\mathsf{M}_{\mathscr{L},1}.

(47)

That is, even in the limit
k=0
, each heat bath continues to act globally on
both
resonators, rather than on the resonator directly coupled to it. This is a side-effect of the secular approximation, which introduces a ‘heat leak’ channel. And that is why the GME predicts steady-state heat flows in the resonant case even at vanishing coupling, as noted in Refs.
[
22
,
23
]
.

Eq. (
47
) confronts us with the fact that the LME is
not
, in general, a limiting case of the GME. But then,
what is it?
What we are after is a
microscopic justification
of the local equation (
33
) capable of explaining why it succeeds in capturing the EPs detected experimentally in
[
1
]
, while the global equation (
46a
) fails.

5.3
Redfield equation

We now resort to the Redfield equation, as introduced in Eqs. (
4
) and (
5
), to shed light on the nature of the LME. As outlined in Sec.
2.1
, this equation is the last step in the derivation of the GME, before forcing the GKSL form by means of the (full) secular approximation. Consequently, the Redfield equation is
always
more accurate than the GME. In the context of quantum thermodynamics, however, the main shortcoming of the Redfield equation is its lack of GKSL structure. Without it, it may fail to generate a completely positive dynamics
[
72
,
73
]
. Nonetheless, if used with caution, the Redfield equation can still yield thermodynamically sound results
[
74
,
75
,
76
]
.

To be more precise, (
4
) is a
partial Redfield equation

[
74
,
39
,
22
,
34
]
. That is, a simplified version of the equation in which its most rapidly oscillating terms—but
not all
oscillating terms—are averaged out under a coarse graining of time. Below, we also discard the
Lamb shifts
, defined in Appendix
A
. The resulting system is

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\langle\boldsymbol{\mathsf{q}}\rangle}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{M}_{\mathscr{R},1}\,\langle\boldsymbol{\mathsf{q}}\rangle,

(48a)

\displaystyle\mathsf{M}_{\mathscr{R},1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}[\mathsf{M}_{\mathscr{R},1}]_{11}&2&[\mathsf{M}_{\mathscr{R},1}]_{13}&0\\
-2\omega^{2}&\bar{\Delta}_{h}&-2k&\delta_{h}\\
[\mathsf{M}_{\mathscr{R},1}]_{31}&0&[\mathsf{M}_{\mathscr{R},1}]_{33}&2\\
-2\omega^{2}&\delta_{c}&-2\omega^{2}&\bar{\Delta}_{c}\end{pmatrix},

(48b)

where
\delta_{\alpha}\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}(\Delta_{1}^{(\alpha)}-\Delta_{2}^{(\alpha)})
,
\bar{\Delta}_{\alpha}\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}(\Delta_{1}^{(\alpha)}+\Delta_{2}^{(\alpha)})
, and

\displaystyle[\mathsf{M}_{\mathscr{R},1}]_{11}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Omega_{2}-\Omega_{1}}{4}\left(\frac{\Delta_{1}^{(c)}}{\Omega_{2}}-\frac{\Delta_{2}^{(c)}}{\Omega_{1}}\right)+\frac{\Omega_{2}+\Omega_{1}}{4}\left(\frac{\Delta_{1}^{(h)}}{\Omega_{2}}+\frac{\Delta_{2}^{(h)}}{\Omega_{1}}\right)

\displaystyle[\mathsf{M}_{\mathscr{R},1}]_{13}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Omega_{2}-\Omega_{1}}{4}\left(\frac{\Delta_{1}^{(c)}}{\Omega_{2}}+\frac{\Delta_{2}^{(c)}}{\Omega_{1}}\right)+\frac{\Omega_{2}+\Omega_{1}}{4}\left(\frac{\Delta_{1}^{(h)}}{\Omega_{2}}-\frac{\Delta_{2}^{(h)}}{\Omega_{1}}\right)

\displaystyle[\mathsf{M}_{\mathscr{R},1}]_{31}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Omega_{2}+\Omega_{1}}{4}\left(\frac{\Delta_{1}^{(c)}}{\Omega_{2}}-\frac{\Delta_{2}^{(c)}}{\Omega_{1}}\right)+\frac{\Omega_{2}-\Omega_{1}}{4}\left(\frac{\Delta_{1}^{(h)}}{\Omega_{2}}-\frac{\Delta_{2}^{(h)}}{\Omega_{1}}\right)

\displaystyle[\mathsf{M}_{\mathscr{R},1}]_{33}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Omega_{2}+\Omega_{1}}{4}\left(\frac{\Delta_{1}^{(c)}}{\Omega_{2}}+\frac{\Delta_{2}^{(c)}}{\Omega_{1}}\right)+\frac{\Omega_{2}-\Omega_{1}}{4}\left(\frac{\Delta_{1}^{(h)}}{\Omega_{2}}+\frac{\Delta_{2}^{(h)}}{\Omega_{1}}\right).

(48c)

In spite of the cumbersome expressions, one can see that the zeroth order term
\mathsf{M}_{\mathscr{R},1}^{(0)}
of the
k
-expansion of the dissipative part of
\mathsf{M}_{\mathscr{R},1}
,

\mathsf{M}_{\mathscr{R},1}=\mathsf{M}_{\mathscr{R},1}^{(0)}+k\,\mathsf{M}_{\mathscr{R},1}^{(1)}+k^{2}\,\mathsf{M}_{\mathscr{R},1}^{(2)}\cdots,

(49)

is identical to
\mathsf{M}_{\mathscr{L},1}
in Eq. (
34
) (or, equivalently,
\mathscr{R}_{\alpha}^{\dagger\,(0)}=\mathscr{L}_{\alpha}^{\dagger}
, see Appendix
D
). One only needs to set
k=0
in all dissipative contributions of Eq. (
4
), which is equivalent to setting
k=0
in the
\bar{\Delta}_{\alpha}
and
\delta_{\alpha}
terms, as well as in the four matrix elements written out in Eqs. (

).

We have thus shown that, in resonance,
the LME is the low-
k
limit of the Redfield equation; not of the GME
. In fact, it is easy to see that
\mathscr{R}_{\alpha}^{\dagger\,(0)}=\mathscr{L}_{\alpha}^{\dagger}
holds as well out of resonance for
any
multipartite model, as long as the coupling to the heat baths is mediated by ‘frequency filters’. More precisely, these are multipartite systems in which the coupling
\boldsymbol{H}_{SB}
is of the form, e.g.,
\boldsymbol{a}_{\alpha}\otimes\boldsymbol{B}_{\alpha}+\text{h.c.}
or
\left|\epsilon_{i}\right\rangle\left\langle\epsilon_{j}\right|\otimes\boldsymbol{B}_{\alpha}+\text{h.c.}
, where
\left|\epsilon_{i}\right\rangle
are eigenstates of the local Hamiltonian
\boldsymbol{H}_{\alpha}^{(\text{loc})}
(see Appendix
D
for details). In such settings, the LME emerges
directly
from the Redfield equation. This is why
\mathsf{M}_{\mathscr{R},1}
and
\mathsf{M}_{\mathscr{L},1}
share the same pattern of EPs in parameter space (cf. Fig.
2
).

Crucially, unlike the Redfield approach, the LME is guaranteed to generate a completely positive dissipative dynamics
[
45
]
. In addition, this explains the unlikely success of the LME over the GME at low
k
, when the secular approximation breaks down
[
22
,
23
]
. In this new light, we see that the LME simply
bypasses
the secular approximation. This is one of the main results of this paper.

Furthermore, note from Eq. (
39
) that the eigenstates of
\boldsymbol{H}_{S}
do
not
depend on
k
. It then becomes clear why the local approach remains accurate even at larger couplings in resonance
[
23
]
: A small-
k
approximation of the dissipators
\mathscr{R}_{\alpha}^{\dagger}
(or
\mathscr{G}_{\alpha}^{\dagger}
) is valid over a wider range of couplings if the expansion affects only the Bohr frequencies
\omega
, but not the jump operators
\boldsymbol{A}_{\omega}^{(\alpha)}
. Conversely, out of resonance, the LME loses validity at non-zero couplings, since the eigenstates of
\boldsymbol{H}_{S}
are then explicitly dependent on
k
(see Appendix
D
). We note as well that EPs can be studied exploiting the stochastic unraveling of GKSL master equations to define an effective non-Hermitian Hamiltonian, as shown in
[
71
]
.

The failure of the GME at capturing the correct dynamics is precisely due to the fact that the secular approximation—required by the GME—‘washes away’ relevant
dynamical
features. We have just illustrated this with the disappearance of the EPs. Next, we show that, even when the LME and GME do agree in their steady-state predictions, the local approach can generate more accurate heat-flow dynamics.

5.4
Heat currents

The
instantaneous
rate of heat flow into the system from each of the baths can be obtained by generalising Eq. (

) to

\dot{\pazocal{Q}}_{\alpha,\mathscr{D}}(t)\coloneqq\langle\mathscr{D}_{\alpha}^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{H}_{S})\rangle_{t},

(50)

where the transient heat currents
\dot{\pazocal{Q}}_{\alpha}(t)
generally
do not
sum up to zero, nor obey the Clausius-like inequality (
10a

6
Conclusions

We have analysed one of the central problems in quantum thermodynamics. Namely, the modelling of heat flows across open systems with quantum master equations. We have shown that the two most common approaches—the local and the global master equations—can make very different predictions. Firstly, our results illustrate that the local approach succeeds at capturing dynamical features, in the form of exceptional points, that escape the global master equation. Secondly, we have found that, when considering degenerate multipartite open quantum systems with weak internal coupling, the LME also yields much more accurate heat-flow dynamics than the GME, even when both agree in the steady-state.

Furthermore, we have shown that the LME follows directly from the more accurate Redfield equation, and is generally
not
a weak-coupling limit of the GME. This is always the case for any multipartite weakly-interacting open quantum system that couples to the environment(s) via single-frequency contacts, such as a qubit or a harmonic oscillator. Therefore, for such systems, the LME emerges as an accurate and computationally efficient alternative to the Redfield equation. It proves to be superior to the GME and, in contrast to the Redfield equation, it does guarantee positivity.

These results have profound consequences for quantum thermodynamics. Namely, modelling heat flow in a quantum thermal machine with the local approach instead of the global one, could make a sizeable difference in the predicted heat transfer in, e.g., any thermalising stroke of a finite-time thermodynamic cycle. This could result in a radically different assessment of both performance and power output. However, it is important to remember that the local approach has a limited range of validity. Specifically, it is unsuitable for open systems with
strong
internal couplings (i.e., large
k
), and it can lead to unphysical results at odds with thermodynamics. Finding an accurate and scalable master equation for such scenarios still remains an open challenge.

Appendix A
Derivation of the GME

Here, we derive a second-order master equation under the Markov and secular approximations. Essentially, we follow Ref.
[
73
]
, but simplify the derivation. Let the Hamiltonian of our system be

\boldsymbol{H}=\boldsymbol{H}_{S}+\boldsymbol{H}_{B}+\lambda\boldsymbol{H}_{SB},

(A.1)

where
\boldsymbol{H}_{SB}
stands for the dissipative interactions between system (S) and bath (B). Note that we are transferring the magnitude of these interactions into
\lambda
, which means that the rescaled
\boldsymbol{H}_{SB}
is now
\pazocal{O}(1)
, unlike in the main text (cf. Eq. (
16
)).

Our starting point will be the Liouville–von Neumann equation in the interaction picture with respect to
\boldsymbol{H}_{0}\coloneqq\boldsymbol{H}_{S}+\boldsymbol{H}_{B}
. This is

\frac{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\tilde{\rho}}}{\mathrm{d}t}=-i\,\lambda\,[\boldsymbol{\tilde{H}}_{SB}(t),\boldsymbol{\tilde{\rho}}(t)]\coloneqq\lambda\,\tilde{\pazocal{L}}(t)\,\boldsymbol{\tilde{\rho}}(t),

(A.2)

where
\boldsymbol{\tilde{O}}(t)\coloneqq e^{i\boldsymbol{H}_{0}t}\,\boldsymbol{O}\,e^{-i\boldsymbol{H}_{0}t}
. Here,
\boldsymbol{\tilde{\rho}}(t)
is the full system–environment state. Since we are only interested in the system’s marginal
\boldsymbol{\tilde{\varrho}}\coloneqq\tr_{B}\boldsymbol{\tilde{\rho}}(t)
, we trace out the bath, i.e.,

\frac{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\tilde{\varrho}}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\lambda\tr_{B}\tilde{\pazocal{L}}(t)\,\boldsymbol{\tilde{\rho}}(t)

(A.3)

and integrate formally. This gives us

\boldsymbol{\tilde{\varrho}}(t)=\boldsymbol{\varrho}(0)+\lambda\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}s\,\tr_{B}\tilde{\pazocal{L}}(s)\boldsymbol{\tilde{\rho}}(s),

(A.4)

and iterating,

\boldsymbol{\tilde{\varrho}}(t)=\boldsymbol{\varrho}(0)+\lambda\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}s\tr_{B}\tilde{\pazocal{L}}(s)\boldsymbol{\rho}(0)+\lambda^{2}\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}s\int_{0}^{s}\mathrm{d}s^{\prime}\tr_{B}\tilde{\pazocal{L}}(s)\tilde{\pazocal{L}}(s^{\prime})\boldsymbol{\tilde{\rho}}(s^{\prime}).

(A.5)

Replacing the state
\boldsymbol{\tilde{\rho}}(s^{\prime})
in Eq. (
A.5
) by the expression in (
A.4
) once again, we see that

\boldsymbol{\tilde{\varrho}}(t)=\boldsymbol{\varrho}(0)+\lambda\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}s\,\tr_{B}\tilde{\pazocal{L}}(s)\boldsymbol{\rho}(0)+\lambda^{2}\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}s\int_{0}^{s}\mathrm{d}s^{\prime}\,\tr_{B}\tilde{\pazocal{L}}(s)\tilde{\pazocal{L}}(s^{\prime})\boldsymbol{\rho}(0)\\
+\pazocal{O}(\lambda^{3}).

(A.6)

Assuming that
\lambda
is small enough so that all
\pazocal{O}(\lambda^{3})
terms or smaller can be neglected will be our first key approximation. This will lead to a
second-order quantum master equation
after taking the time derivative; namely,

\frac{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\tilde{\varrho}}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\lambda\,\tr_{B}\tilde{\pazocal{L}}(t)\boldsymbol{\rho}(0)+\lambda^{2}\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}s\,\tr_{B}\tilde{\pazocal{L}}(t)\tilde{\pazocal{L}}(s)\boldsymbol{\rho}(0).

(A.7)

Notice, however, that this master equation time-non-local and, still, of little practical use. To overcome this problem, we make two additional assumptions: First, we require the
initial
state to include no correlations between system’s and bath’s degrees of freedom. Namely,
\boldsymbol{\rho}(0)=\boldsymbol{\varrho}_{S}(0)\otimes\boldsymbol{\varrho}_{B}
. Secondly, we impose

\tr_{B}\tilde{\pazocal{L}}(s)\boldsymbol{\varrho}(0)\otimes\boldsymbol{\varrho}_{B}=0.

(A.8)

For instance, if the system–bath interaction is of the simple form
\boldsymbol{H}_{SB}=\boldsymbol{S}\otimes\boldsymbol{B}
(cf. Eq. (
16
)), Eq. (
A.8
) translates into
\langle\boldsymbol{B}\rangle=0
when averaged on the initial state of the environment.

Inserting (
A.8
) into (
A.6
), we see that
\boldsymbol{\varrho}(0)=\boldsymbol{\tilde{\varrho}}(t)+\pazocal{O}(\lambda^{2})
, which allows us to write our equation in the much more convenient time-local form

\frac{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\tilde{\varrho}}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\lambda^{2}\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}s\,\tr_{B}\tilde{\pazocal{L}}(t)\tilde{\pazocal{L}}(s)\tilde{\boldsymbol{\varrho}}(t)\otimes\boldsymbol{\varrho}_{B}

(A.9)

while still remaining accurate within
\pazocal{O}(\lambda^{3})
. It is customary to perform a change of variables in the integral, re-expressing it as

\frac{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\tilde{\varrho}}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\lambda^{2}\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}s\,\tr_{B}\tilde{\pazocal{L}}(t)\tilde{\pazocal{L}}(t-s)\boldsymbol{\tilde{\varrho}}(t)\otimes\boldsymbol{\varrho}_{B}.

(A.10)

We note that objects like

\tr_{B}\tilde{\pazocal{L}}(t)\tilde{\pazocal{L}}(t-s)\boldsymbol{\tilde{\varrho}}(t)\otimes\boldsymbol{\varrho}_{B}

(A.11)

enclose two-time correlation functions of bath operators, i.e.,
\tr_{B}\boldsymbol{\tilde{B}}(t)\boldsymbol{\tilde{B}}(t-s)\boldsymbol{\varrho}_{B}
. In many situations of practical interest, these decay extremely fast—much faster than any relevant timescale in the problem. Hence, we may replace the upper integration limit in (
A.10
) by infinity without substantial error

\frac{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\tilde{\varrho}}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\lambda^{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}s\,\tr_{B}\tilde{\pazocal{L}}(t)\tilde{\pazocal{L}}(t-s)\boldsymbol{\tilde{\varrho}}(t)\otimes\boldsymbol{\varrho}_{B}.

(A.12)

This is often referred-to as the
Markov approximation
and the resulting equation, as (Markovian)
Redfield master equation
. However, the use of the term ‘Markovian’ can be problematic since the dynamics generated by this equation is, in general, not even
positive

[
73
]
. This means that the corresponding dynamical map might not be
divisible
, and lack of divisibility is often associated with ‘
non
-Markovianity’
[
77
]
.

Before the final step in our derivation, we must transcribe the shorthand
\tr_{B}\tilde{\pazocal{L}}(t)\tilde{\pazocal{L}}(t-s)\boldsymbol{\tilde{\varrho}}(t)\otimes\boldsymbol{\varrho}_{B}
, which gives

\tilde{\pazocal{L}}(t)\tilde{\pazocal{L}}(t-s)\boldsymbol{\tilde{\varrho}}(t)\otimes\boldsymbol{\varrho}_{B}=-\boldsymbol{\tilde{H}}_{SB}(t)\boldsymbol{\tilde{H}}_{SB}(t-s)\boldsymbol{\tilde{\varrho}}(t)\otimes\boldsymbol{\varrho}_{B}+\boldsymbol{\tilde{H}}_{SB}(t-s)\boldsymbol{\tilde{\varrho}}(t)\otimes\boldsymbol{\varrho}_{B}\boldsymbol{\tilde{H}}_{SB}(t)+\text{h.c.}

We may always write the system–bath interaction term as
\boldsymbol{H}_{SB}=\sum_{\alpha}\boldsymbol{S}_{\alpha}\otimes\boldsymbol{B}_{\alpha}
. Here, we take
\boldsymbol{H}_{SB}=\boldsymbol{S}\otimes\boldsymbol{B}
for simplicity, and hence,

\tilde{\pazocal{L}}(t)\tilde{\pazocal{L}}(t-s)\boldsymbol{\tilde{\varrho}}(t)\otimes\boldsymbol{\varrho}_{B}=-\boldsymbol{\tilde{S}}(t)\boldsymbol{\tilde{S}}(t-s)\boldsymbol{\tilde{\varrho}}(t)\,\tr_{B}\boldsymbol{\tilde{B}}(t)\boldsymbol{\tilde{B}}(t-s)\boldsymbol{\varrho}_{B}+\boldsymbol{\tilde{S}}(t-s)\boldsymbol{\tilde{\varrho}}(t)\boldsymbol{\tilde{S}}(t)\,\tr_{B}\boldsymbol{\tilde{B}}(t-s)\boldsymbol{\varrho}_{B}\boldsymbol{\tilde{B}}(t)+\text{h.c.}

The explicit form of the interaction-picture system operator
\boldsymbol{\tilde{S}}(t)
can be found easily by exploiting the decomposition from Eqs. (
7
) in the main text. Namely, given the properties of the jump operators
\boldsymbol{A}_{\omega}
it is easy to see that
\boldsymbol{\tilde{S}}(t)=\sum\nolimits_{\omega}\boldsymbol{A}_{\omega}\,e^{-i\omega t}
. Putting together all the pieces

\frac{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\tilde{\varrho}}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\sum\nolimits_{\omega,\omega^{\prime}}\left(-\Gamma(\omega)\,\boldsymbol{A}_{\omega^{\prime}}\boldsymbol{A}_{\omega}e^{-i(\omega+\omega^{\prime})t}\boldsymbol{\tilde{\varrho}}(t)\right.\left.+\Gamma(\omega)\,\boldsymbol{A}_{\omega}\boldsymbol{\tilde{\varrho}}(t)\boldsymbol{A}_{\omega^{\prime}}e^{-i(\omega+\omega^{\prime})t}+\text{h.c.}\right)\\
=\sum\nolimits_{\omega,\omega^{\prime}}\left(-\Gamma(\omega)\,\boldsymbol{A}_{\omega^{\prime}}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{A}_{\omega}e^{i(\omega^{\prime}-\omega)t}\boldsymbol{\tilde{\varrho}}(t)\right.\left.+\Gamma(\omega)\,\boldsymbol{A}_{\omega}\boldsymbol{\tilde{\varrho}}(t)\boldsymbol{A}_{\omega^{\prime}}^{\dagger}e^{i(\omega^{\prime}-\omega^{\prime})t}+\text{h.c.}\right),

(A.13)

where

\Gamma(\omega)\coloneqq\lambda^{2}\int\nolimits_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}s\,e^{i\omega s}\tr_{B}\boldsymbol{\tilde{B}}(t)\boldsymbol{\tilde{B}}(t-s),

(A.14)

and the corresponding real and imaginary parts are
\Gamma(\omega)=\frac{1}{2}\gamma(\omega)+iS(\omega)
. The imaginary part
S(\omega)
is typically ignored. It introduces two effects—a displacement of the energy levels of
\boldsymbol{H}_{S}
through a
Lamb shift
term
\boldsymbol{H}_{L}=\sum\nolimits_{\omega}S(\omega)\boldsymbol{A}_{\omega}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{A}_{\omega}
(
[\boldsymbol{H}_{S},\boldsymbol{H}_{L}]=0
); but also, non-trivial dissipative terms. These are, however, typically very small.

Many terms in Eq. (
A.15
) can be dropped, since they are fast-oscillating and average out to zero over the time-scale defined by the dynamics of
\boldsymbol{\varrho}(t)

[
21
]
. Namely, we can drop all terms for which
\omega
and
\omega^{\prime}
have the same sign (
\omega\times\omega^{\prime}>0
), since these oscillate as
e^{\pm|\omega+\omega^{\prime}|t}
. Eq. (
A.13
) is often called ‘partial Redfield equation’
[
39
,
22
,
34
]

It is now time to abandon the interaction picture undoing the corresponding unitary transformation. This gives us

\frac{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\varrho}}{\mathrm{d}t}=-i[\boldsymbol{H}_{S},\boldsymbol{\varrho}]+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\omega\times\omega^{\prime}<0}\gamma(\omega)\left(\boldsymbol{A}_{\omega}\boldsymbol{\varrho}(t)\boldsymbol{A}_{\omega^{\prime}}^{\dagger}-\boldsymbol{A}_{\omega^{\prime}}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{A}_{\omega}\boldsymbol{\varrho}(t)\right)+\text{h.c.}

(A.15)

One final step is necessary to bring Eq. (
A.15
) into GKSL form—the
secular approximation
. This consists in removing all terms in which
\omega\neq\omega^{\prime}
from the double sum in (
A.15
). The rationale for this—seemingly arbitrary—simplification often involves again a time averaging which now ‘kills’
all
the remaining oscillating terms in Eq. (
A.13
)
[
21
]
. From all approximations involved in the process, this is certainly the most problematic and difficult to justify. Nonetheless, it does hold in many situations of practical interest
[
21
]
. This way, we finally arrive at the celebrated global GKSL master equation

\frac{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\varrho}}{\mathrm{d}t}=-i[\boldsymbol{H}_{S},\boldsymbol{\varrho}]+\sum_{\omega}\gamma(\omega)\left(\boldsymbol{A}_{\omega}\boldsymbol{\varrho}(t)\boldsymbol{A}_{\omega}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\{\boldsymbol{A}_{\omega}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{A}_{\omega}\boldsymbol{\varrho}(t)\}_{+}\right).

(A.16)

Appendix C
Conditions for the appearance of exceptional points

As mentioned in the main text, for the exceptional points to appear, dissipation must be asymmetric and the oscillators must be resonant. Here, we show the validity of this statement.

Consider the non-resonant case, where the resonators have bare frequencies
\omega_{h}
and
\omega_{c}
. The matrix of the dynamics of the first order moments is

\tilde{\mathsf{M}}_{\mathscr{L},1}=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}\Delta_{\omega_{h}}^{(h)}&2&0&0\\
-2\omega_{h}^{2}&\Delta_{\omega_{h}}^{(h)}&-2k&0\\
0&0&\Delta_{\omega_{c}}^{(c)}&2\\
-2k&0&-2\omega_{c}^{2}&\Delta_{\omega_{c}}^{(c)}\end{pmatrix},

(C.1)

where

\Delta_{\omega_{i}}^{(\alpha)}\coloneqq\frac{\gamma_{-\omega_{i}}^{(\alpha)}-\gamma_{\omega_{i}}^{(\alpha)}}{2\omega_{i}}.

(C.2)

To identify the exceptional points we look for square root singularities in the expressions of the eigenvalues. Two of the eigenvalues of the latter matrix are

\displaystyle\lambda_{1,2}=\frac{1}{4}\Big[\Delta_{\omega_{h}}^{(h)}+\Delta_{\omega_{c}}^{(c)}

\displaystyle+2\sqrt{-(\omega_{h}+\omega_{c})^{2}}+

(C.3)

\displaystyle\pm\sqrt{(\Delta_{\omega_{h}}^{(h)}-\Delta_{\omega_{c}}^{(c)})^{2}-4(\omega_{h}-\omega_{c})^{2}-\frac{4k^{2}}{\omega_{h}\omega_{c}}-4(\Delta_{\omega_{h}}^{(h)}-\Delta_{\omega_{c}}^{(c)})\frac{\omega_{h}^{2}-\omega_{c}^{2}}{\sqrt{-(\omega_{h}+\omega_{c})^{2}}}}\Big]

which coalesce when the latter square root vanishes. Therefore, we find the condition of exceptional points for the inter-resonators coupling
k
,

k=\pm\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\omega_{h}\omega_{c}}\sqrt{(\Delta_{\omega_{h}}^{(h)}-\Delta_{\omega_{c}}^{(c)})^{2}-4(\omega_{h}-\omega_{c})\left[(\omega_{h}-\omega_{c})-i(\Delta_{\omega_{h}}^{(h)}-\Delta_{\omega_{c}}^{(c)})\right]}.

(C.4)

Since the inter-resonators coupling
k
is assumed to be real, we have the only acceptable solution when the system is resonant,
\omega_{h}=\omega_{c}=\omega
and dissipation asymmetric,
\Delta_{h}\neq\Delta_{c}
. In this way, we recover the expression in Eq. (
36
).

Appendix D
Local, global, and Redfield dissipators out of resonance

We now show that the relationship between the Redfield and the local approach pertains even in the non-resonant case. First of all, note that, out of resonance, the transformation matrix
\mathsf{P}
that defines the rotation into normal modes generalises to
[
22
]

\mathsf{P}=\left(\begin{matrix}\sin{\zeta}&\cos{\zeta}\\
\cos{\zeta}&-\sin{\zeta}\end{matrix}\right),

(D.1)

where
\displaystyle\zeta=\arccos{\sqrt{\frac{\delta+\sqrt{4k^{2}+\delta^{2}}}{2\sqrt{4k^{2}+\delta^{2}}}}}
and
\delta=\omega_{h}^{2}-\omega_{c}^{2}
. The eigenfrequencies are

\Omega_{1,2}^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\omega_{h}^{2}+\omega_{c}^{2}\pm\sqrt{4k^{2}+\delta^{2}}\right).

(D.2)

Namely, in the non-resonant case
both
the eigenvectors and the Bohr frequencies of
\boldsymbol{H}_{S}
depend explicitly on the internal coupling strength
k
.

Moving now to the Redfield dissipators (
A.15
) we can write it out in adjoint form as

\displaystyle\mathscr{R}_{h}^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{O})+\mathscr{R}_{c}^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{O})=\sum_{i,j=1}^{2}\Big[

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma_{\Omega_{i}}^{(h)}}{2}\left(\boldsymbol{A}_{-\Omega_{j}}^{(h)}\boldsymbol{O}\boldsymbol{A}_{\Omega_{i}}^{(h)}+\boldsymbol{A}_{-\Omega_{i}}^{(h)}\boldsymbol{O}\boldsymbol{A}_{\Omega_{j}}^{(h)}-\boldsymbol{O}\boldsymbol{A}_{-\Omega_{j}}^{(h)}\boldsymbol{A}_{\Omega_{i}}^{(h)}-\boldsymbol{A}_{-\Omega_{i}}^{(h)}\boldsymbol{A}_{\Omega_{j}}^{(h)}\boldsymbol{O}\right)

(D.3)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma_{-\Omega_{i}}^{(h)}}{2}\left(\boldsymbol{A}_{\Omega_{j}}^{(h)}\boldsymbol{O}\boldsymbol{A}_{-\Omega_{i}}^{(h)}+\boldsymbol{A}_{\Omega_{i}}^{(h)}\boldsymbol{O}\boldsymbol{A}_{-\Omega_{j}}^{(h)}-\boldsymbol{O}\boldsymbol{A}_{\Omega_{j}}^{(h)}\boldsymbol{A}_{-\Omega_{i}}^{(h)}-\boldsymbol{A}_{\Omega_{i}}^{(h)}\boldsymbol{A}_{-\Omega_{j}}^{(h)}\boldsymbol{O}\right)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma_{\Omega_{i}}^{(c)}}{2}\left(\boldsymbol{A}_{-\Omega_{j}}^{(c)}\boldsymbol{O}\boldsymbol{A}_{\Omega_{i}}^{(c)}+\boldsymbol{A}_{-\Omega_{i}}^{(c)}\boldsymbol{O}\boldsymbol{A}_{\Omega_{j}}^{(c)}-\boldsymbol{O}\boldsymbol{A}_{-\Omega_{j}}^{(c)}\boldsymbol{A}_{\Omega_{i}}^{(c)}-\boldsymbol{A}_{-\Omega_{i}}^{(c)}\boldsymbol{A}_{\Omega_{j}}^{(c)}\boldsymbol{O}\right)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma_{-\Omega_{i}}^{(c)}}{2}\left(\boldsymbol{A}_{\Omega_{j}}^{(c)}\boldsymbol{O}\boldsymbol{A}_{-\Omega_{i}}^{(c)}+\boldsymbol{A}_{\Omega_{i}}^{(c)}\boldsymbol{O}\boldsymbol{A}_{-\Omega_{j}}^{(c)}-\boldsymbol{O}\boldsymbol{A}_{\Omega_{j}}^{(c)}\boldsymbol{A}_{-\Omega_{i}}^{(c)}-\boldsymbol{A}_{\Omega_{i}}^{(c)}\boldsymbol{A}_{-\Omega_{j}}^{(c)}\boldsymbol{O}\right)\Big]

To find the zeroth order term
\mathscr{R}^{\dagger\,(0)}
, we must simply set
k=0
in
\mathsf{P}
and the normal-mode frequencies. We thus find that the normal modes rotate back to the local coordinates
\boldsymbol{x}_{\alpha}
and
\Omega_{\alpha}
collapse into the bare frequencies
\omega_{\alpha}
. Importantly, as a result, frequency
\Omega_{1}^{(0)}=\omega_{h}
will only appear in the hot dissipator
\mathscr{R}_{h}^{\dagger}
and
\Omega_{2}^{(0)}=\omega_{c}
will be only linked to
\mathscr{R}^{\dagger}_{c}
. Therefore, the double sums in Eq. (
D.3
) directly transform into the local expression (
12
) which in the specific case of the system studied is

\displaystyle\mathscr{L}_{h}^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{O})+\mathscr{L}_{c}^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{O})=\;

\displaystyle\gamma_{\omega_{h}}^{(h)}\left(\boldsymbol{L}_{-\omega_{h}}^{(h)}\boldsymbol{O}\boldsymbol{L}_{\omega_{h}}^{(h)}-\frac{1}{2}\{\boldsymbol{L}^{(h)}_{-\omega_{h}}\boldsymbol{L}^{(h)}_{\omega_{h}},\boldsymbol{O}\}_{+}\right)

(D.4)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\gamma_{-\omega_{h}}^{(h)}\left(\boldsymbol{L}_{\omega_{h}}^{(h)}\boldsymbol{O}\boldsymbol{L}_{-\omega_{h}}^{(h)}-\frac{1}{2}\{\boldsymbol{L}^{(h)}_{\omega_{h}}\boldsymbol{L}^{(h)}_{-\omega_{h}},\boldsymbol{O}\}_{+}\right)

\displaystyle\gamma_{\omega_{c}}^{(c)}\left(\boldsymbol{L}_{-\omega_{c}}^{(c)}\boldsymbol{O}\boldsymbol{L}_{\omega_{c}}^{(c)}-\frac{1}{2}\{\boldsymbol{L}^{(c)}_{-\omega_{c}}\boldsymbol{L}^{(c)}_{\omega_{c}},\boldsymbol{O}\}_{+}\right)

In the low-

In the same way, it is easy to show that the relationship between the global and the local dissipators, proposed in

\displaystyle\mathscr{G}_{h}^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{O})+\mathscr{G}_{c}^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{O})=\sum_{i=1}^{2}\Big[

\displaystyle\gamma_{\Omega_{i}}^{(h)}\left(\boldsymbol{A}_{-\Omega_{i}}^{(h)}\boldsymbol{O}\boldsymbol{A}_{\Omega_{i}}^{(h)}-\frac{1}{2}\{\boldsymbol{A}_{-\Omega_{i}}^{(h)}\boldsymbol{A}_{\Omega_{i}}^{(h)},\boldsymbol{O}\}_{+}\right)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\gamma_{\Omega_{i}}^{(c)}\left(\boldsymbol{A}_{-\Omega_{i}}^{(c)}\boldsymbol{O}\boldsymbol{A}_{\Omega_{i}}^{(c)}-\frac{1}{2}\{\boldsymbol{A}_{-\Omega_{i}}^{(c)}\boldsymbol{A}_{\Omega_{i}}^{(c)},\boldsymbol{O}\}_{+}\right)
