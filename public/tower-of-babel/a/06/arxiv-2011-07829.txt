Controlling the direction of topological transport in a non-Hermitian time-reversal symmetric Floquet ladder Preprint: AIP/123-QED
B. Höckendorf, A. Alvermann, H. Fehske

Abstract
We propose a one-dimensional Floquet ladder that possesses two distinct topological transport channels with opposite directionality. The transport channels occur due to a ℤ 2 \mathbb{Z}_{2} non-Hermitian Floquet topological phase that is protected by time-reversal symmetry. The signatures of this phase are two pairs of Kramers degenerate Floquet quasienergy bands that are separated by an imaginary gap. We discuss how the Floquet ladder can be implemented in a photonic waveguide lattice and show that the direction of transport in the resulting waveguide structure can be externally controlled by focusing two light beams into adjacent waveguides. The relative phase between the two light beams selects which of the two transport channels is predominantly populated, while the angles of incidence of the two light beams determine which of the transport channels is suppressed by non-Hermitian losses. We identify the optimal lattice parameters for the external control of transport and demonstrate the robustness of this mechanism against disorder.



I
Introduction

The key feature of topological insulators is the existence of robust transport at the boundary which is protected by the non-trivial topology of the bulk
Hasan and Kane 2010
;
Qi and Zhang 2011
;
Chiu et al. 2016
. In solids, non-trivial topology can emerge through the electromagnetic interaction of the Bloch electrons with external or internal gauge fields
Klitzing, Dorda, and Pepper 1980
;
König et al. 2007
;
Hsieh et al. 2008
and through Kramers degeneracy which is induced by fermionic time-reversal symmetry of the spin
1/2
of the electrons
Kane and Mele 2005
. Naively, one would not expect that a photonic system is a suitable platform for topological insulators since photons are neutral bosons. However, both synthetic gauge fields
Haldane and Raghu 2008
;
Hafezi et al. 2011
;
Ozawa et al. 2019
and fermionic pseudo-spins
Maczewsky et al. 2020
can be encoded into photonic systems to realize non-trivial topology.

In lattices of evanescently coupled waveguides
Szameit and Nolte 2010
, spatially periodic modulation of the waveguides along the propagation direction has been used to achieve the encoding of gauge fields and fermionic degrees of freedom
via Floquet engineering
Kitagawa et al. 2010
;
Rudner et al. 2013
;
Maczewsky et al. 2017
;
Mukherjee et al. 2017
;
Rudner and Lindner 2020
. For example, helical waveguide arrays mimic the interaction of electrons in a solid with an external magnetic field
Rechtsman et al. 2013
, and Floquet protocols with pairwise coupling can faithfully reproduce the Kramers degeneracy of time-reversal symmetric spin
1/2
electrons
Maczewsky et al. 2020
;
Höckendorf, Alvermann, and Fehske 2019a
.
Beyond the emulation of solid state phenomena, topological phases in photonic media can be deliberately subjected to non-Hermiticity
Weimann et al. 2016
;
Weidemann et al. 2020
or non-linearity
Mukherjee and Rechtsman 2020a
;
Mukherjee and Rechtsman 2020b
, which is generally not possible in solids.

The interplay between non-Hermiticity and topology in static
Gong et al. 2018
;
Kawabata et al. 2019
;
Zhou and Lee 2019
;
Lee et al. 2019
;
Bergholtz, Budich, and Kunst 2020
and Floquet
Zhou and Gong 2018
;
Höckendorf, Alvermann, and Fehske 2019b
;
Zhou and Pan 2019
;
Li et al. 2019
;
Höckendorf, Alvermann, and Fehske 2020
;
Fedorova et al. 2020
;
Zhang and Gong 2020
;
Wu and An 2020
;
Zhou 2020
;
Höckendorf, Alvermann, and Fehske 2020
systems has attracted significant interest in the last couple of years. One example of the surprising results in this field is that topological transport occurs in one-dimensional non-Hermitian chains, in direct correspondence to the boundaries of a two-dimensional topological insulator
Lee et al. 2019
;
Höckendorf, Alvermann, and Fehske 2020
. The topological transport in a non-Hermitian chain does not depend on fine-tuned parameters and is unaffected by moderate disorder, hence robust. It is, however, only quantized if specific conditions are satisified
Höckendorf, Alvermann, and Fehske 2020
;
Fedorova et al. 2020
.
In non-Hermitian static chains, topological transport is associated with non-trivial point gaps in the complex-valued energy spectrum of the Hamiltonian
Gong et al. 2018
, in non-Hermitian Floquet chains with non-trivial imaginary gaps in the complex-valued quasienergy spectrum of the Floquet propagator
Höckendorf, Alvermann, and Fehske 2020
;
Fedorova et al. 2020
.

In both cases, we can distinguish between non-Hermitian chains that possess a unidirectional topological transport channel and non-Hermitian chains that possess two topological transport channels with opposite directionality. Due to the robustness of topological transport, it does not matter how the unidirectional chain is initially excited: Transport always goes in the same direction. On the other hand, the preparation of the initial state in a chain with bidirectional transport determines which of the two transport channels is predominantly populated. Therefore, the direction of the transport can be externally controlled by manipulating the initial state.

In this work, we introduce a one-dimensional non-Hermitian time-reversal symmetric Floquet ladder that possesses two topological transport channels with opposite directionality. The Floquet ladder is constructed in such a way that (i) it can be readily implemented in photonic waveguide lattices, and (ii) the direction of transport in the resulting waveguide structure can be externally controlled with the setup sketched in Fig.
1
(d). Two waveguides that form a rung of the ladder (indicated by the green ellipses) are simultaneously excited by two light beams that have the same intensity
|\psi_{0}|^{2}
but a variable relative phase
\phi
.
This initial excitation populates both the left-moving and right-moving transport channel in the waveguide lattice. Depending on the specific value of
\phi
, either the left- or right-moving transport channel is predominantly populated, influencing the output intensities
I_{l}(\phi)
,
I_{r}(\phi)
of the two channels.
By varying the relative phase, we can switch between equal transport in both directions (
I_{l}=I_{r}
), predominant transport to the left (
I_{l}>I_{r}
), or predominant transport to the right (
I_{l}<I_{r}
). The total output intensity
I_{\mathrm{tot}}=I_{l}(\phi)+I_{r}(\phi)
remains constant during this process.

Our Floquet ladder hosts a
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
topological phase that exclusively occurs in a non-Hermitian time-reversal symmetric Floquet system.
Before we specify the relevant Floquet protocol, let us explain why both non-Hermiticity and time-reversal symmetry are necessary to control the direction of topological transport in a one-dimensional chain.

II
Construction of the Floquet protocol

The simplest one-dimensional Hermitian Floquet protocol that realizes bidirectional transport is shown in Fig.
1
(a). It is implemented on a zigzag chain with lattice sites
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\bullet}
(filled red circles) and
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\circ}
(open red circles). The time-periodic Hamiltonian,
H(t+T)=H(t)
, cycles through two consecutive steps, each of which couples two different adjacent sites in the zigzag chain. The uniform (u) pairwise coupling can be described by the
2\times 2
Hamiltonian

H_{\mathrm{u}}=\begin{pmatrix}0&J_{\mathrm{u}}\\
J_{\mathrm{u}}&0\end{pmatrix}

(1)

with coupling strength
J_{\mathrm{u}}
. The associated propagator is
U_{\mathrm{u}}=\exp(-\mathrm{i}H_{\mathrm{u}}\delta t)=\cos(J_{\mathrm{u}}\delta t)\mathbbm{1}-\mathrm{i}\sin(J_{\mathrm{u}}\delta t)\sigma_{x}
, with the identity matrix
\mathbbm{1}
, the Pauli matrix
\sigma_{x}
, and the duration of a time step
\delta t
. In the following, we set
\delta t\equiv 1
for all time steps. At
J_{\mathrm{u}}=\pi/2
, we have
U_{\mathrm{u}}=-\mathrm{i}\sigma_{x}
. Consequently, a full amplitude transfer occurs between coupled sites. In this way, bidirectional transport is realized in the chain. An excitation starting on a
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\bullet}
site (
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\circ}
site) is transferred two sites to the right (left) in each period of the Floquet protocol. Note that this transport requires fine-tuning of the coupling strength
J_{\mathrm{u}}
. In addition, it is not robust against disorder
Höckendorf, Alvermann, and Fehske 2020
.

Robust transport is achieved if non-Hermiticity is added to the chain by replacing the uniform pairwise coupling (
1
) with the directional (d) pairwise coupling

H_{\mathrm{d}}=\begin{pmatrix}0&J_{\mathrm{d}}e^{\gamma_{\mathrm{d}}}\\
J_{\mathrm{d}}e^{-\gamma_{\mathrm{d}}}&0\end{pmatrix}\;,

(2)

as shown in Fig.
1
(b).
Here, the parameter
\gamma_{\mathrm{d}}
controls the directionality of the coupling.
For
\gamma_{\mathrm{d}}\neq 0
, the coupling incorporates gain for the transfer in one direction, leading to amplification of excitations, and loss for the transfer in the opposite direction, leading to suppression of excitations. Note that gain and loss in the directional coupling need only be specified relative to the uniform loss or gain of the entire system that does not favor a specific direction.
The associated propagator
U_{\mathrm{d}}=\exp(-\mathrm{i}H_{\mathrm{d}})
is now given by
U_{\mathrm{d}}=\cos(J_{\mathrm{d}})\mathbbm{1}-\mathrm{i}\sin(J_{\mathrm{d}})e^{\gamma_{\mathrm{d}}}\sigma_{+}-\mathrm{i}\sin(J_{\mathrm{d}})e^{-\gamma_{\mathrm{d}}}\sigma_{-}
, with the Pauli matrices
\sigma_{\pm}=\sigma_{x}/2\pm\mathrm{i}\sigma_{y}/2
. In the limit
e^{-\gamma_{\mathrm{d}}}\to 0
, we have
U_{\mathrm{d}}e^{-\gamma_{\mathrm{d}}}=-\mathrm{i}\sin(J_{\mathrm{d}})\sigma_{+}
. An excitation starting on a
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\bullet}
site is transferred two sites to the right, irrespective of the concrete value of the coupling strength
J_{\mathrm{d}}
. On the other hand, an excitation starting on a
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\circ}
site is suppressed during propagation. The analogous result, with inverted roles of the two lattice sites, holds in the limit
e^{\gamma_{\mathrm{d}}}\to 0
. To avoid redundancies, we will only consider the limit
e^{-\gamma_{\mathrm{d}}}\to 0
. Transport is now unaffected by the addition of disorder
Höckendorf, Alvermann, and Fehske 2020
, but is also unidirectional instead of bidirectional.

Bidirectional transport is recovered if a second Floquet zigzag chain with inverse directionality is added. Here, the two chains are arranged in such a way that they form the ladder shown in Fig.
1
(c).
On the second zigzag chain, excitations starting on
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\bullet}
sites (filled blue circles) get transferred to the left, while excitations starting on
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\circ}
sites (open blue circles) get suppressed for
e^{-\gamma_{\mathrm{d}}}\to 0
. Since the two zigzag chains are decoupled, there is no interference between excitations that start on adjacent filled sites
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\bullet}
,
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\bullet}
.

To enable the external control of topological transport depicted in Fig.
1
(d), the two zigzag chains have to be coupled. Simultaneously, the bidirectional transport has to retain its robustness. As with the boundary states in
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
topological insulators
Kane and Mele 2005
, this is only possible if Kramers degeneracy is enforced upon the two counter-propagating transport channels. To achieve this, the zigzag chains will be coupled in such a way that the combined ladder satisfies the relation

\theta H(t)\theta^{-1}=H^{t}(T-t)\;

(3)

of fermionic time-reversal symmetry, in which the unitary time-reversal operator
\theta
fullfills
\theta\theta^{*}=-1
and maps the red and blue sites onto each other as indicated by the green ellipses in Fig.
1
(d). In this case, the red and blue sublattice can be identified as the up and down components of a fermionic pseudo-spin
1/2
. The time-reversal operator acts on this pseudo-spin as
\theta=\sigma_{y}
. In this pseudo-spin notation, the initial state
|\Psi_{0}\rangle
used for the control of transport can be written as
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\langle x|\Psi_{0}\rangle}=\psi_{0}(x)(|{\uparrow}\rangle+e^{\mathrm{i}\phi}|{\downarrow}\rangle)
, where
\uparrow
(
\downarrow
) corresponds to the upper (lower) row of the Floquet ladder. The wavefunction
\psi_{0}(x)
has a non-zero value
\psi_{0}(x)=\psi_{0}
on a single filled site and is zero everywhere else.

In the symmetry relation (
3
),
H^{t}
denotes the transpose of the Hamiltonian.
Note that for a non-Hermitian Hamiltonian
H^{t}
does not coincide with the complex-conjugate
H^{*}
,
such that replacing
H^{t}
with
H^{*}
in Eq. (
3
) gives a different type of time-reversal symmetry
Kawabata et al. 2019
;
Zhou and Lee 2019
. We do not consider this symmetry because it enforces unidirectional instead of bidirectional transport
Höckendorf, Alvermann, and Fehske 2019b
.

Graphically, time reversal acts as follows on the Floquet ladder: (i) The order of the time steps is inverted, the directionality of each coupling line is inverted, and each coupling line is mirrored across the horizontal axis between the upper and lower row of the ladder. The Floquet protocol in Fig.
1
(c) thus satisfies the symmetry relation (
3
). Possible couplings between the red and blue sublattice additionally acquire a minus sign under time-reversal due to the constraint
\theta\theta^{*}=-1
.

We now couple the two zigzag chains by adding four steps to the Floquet protocol which consist of horizontal and vertical uniform pairwise couplings with coupling strength
\pm J_{\mathrm{u}}
, see steps
2
,
3
,
4
, and
5
in Fig.
2
(a). The four steps also satisfy Eq. (
3
). Both horizontal and vertical couplings are required to induce interference between excitations that start on adjacent red and blue sites. If, for example, we omit steps
2
and
5
from the protocol, time-reversal symmetry is still satisfied but the two zigzag chains are decoupled in the limit
e^{-\gamma_{\mathrm{d}}}\to 0
. Similarily, steps
2
and
5
cancel if we omit steps
3
and
4
.

In total, the Floquet protocol consists of six steps. Four of these steps utilize uniform pairwise couplings (
1
) (steps
2
,
3
,
4
,
5
) and two steps utilize directional pairwise couplings (
2
) (steps
1
,
6
). Both coupling types can be readily implemented in photonic waveguide lattices.

To implement uniform pairwise coupling, the distance between two waveguides must be locally reduced in a symmetric manner, as shown in Fig.
2
(b). In contrast to Eq. (
1
), the effective Hamiltonian
H_{\mathrm{u}}^{\mathrm{w}}=H_{\mathrm{u}}-\mathrm{i}\gamma_{\mathrm{u}}\mathbbm{1}
and propagator
U_{\mathrm{u}}^{\mathrm{w}}=U_{\mathrm{u}}\,e^{-\gamma_{\mathrm{u}}}
for the uniform coupling of two waveguides (w) include additional losses
\gamma_{\mathrm{u}}>0
which occur due to the bending of the waveguides.

The bending losses can be used to implement the directional coupling, as shown in Fig.
2
(c). The distance between two waveguides is again locally reduced but now in an asymmetric manner. In comparison to Eq. (
2
), the effective Hamiltonian
H_{\mathrm{d}}^{\mathrm{w}}=H_{\mathrm{d}}-\mathrm{i}\gamma_{\mathrm{d}}\mathbbm{1}
and propagator
U_{\mathrm{d}}^{\mathrm{w}}=U_{\mathrm{d}}\,e^{-\gamma_{\mathrm{d}}}
of the waveguides are also shifted by the bending losses
\gamma_{\mathrm{d}}>0
,
such that there is no amplification factor
e^{\gamma\mathrm{d}}
in the propagator
U_{\mathrm{d}}^{\mathrm{w}}
. Because of this, the propagator
U_{\mathrm{d}}^{\mathrm{w}}=\sin(J_{\mathrm{d}})\sigma_{+}
is finite for
e^{-\gamma_{\mathrm{d}}}\to 0
. In particular, directional couplings require no gain but can be realized purely with losses. This is important for passive systems such as waveguide lattices, where losses can be controlled through the curvature of the waveguides but the implementation of actual gain is significantly harder
Weimann et al. 2016
;
Ornigotti and Szameit 2014
.

The waveguide configuration in Fig.
2
(b) exlusively realizes positive couplings
J_{\mathrm{u}}>0
. To implement the protocol steps with negative couplings, the periodicity
U_{\mathrm{u}}^{\mathrm{w}}=(-1)^{n}e^{-\gamma_{\mathrm{d}}}[\cos(J_{\mathrm{u}}+n\pi)\mathbbm{1}-\mathrm{i}\sin(J_{\mathrm{u}}+n\pi)\sigma_{x}]
of the propagator can be exploited. Any negative coupling
J_{\mathrm{u}}<0
can be replaced with an equivalent positive coupling
J_{\mathrm{u}}+n\pi>0
for large enough
n\in N
. The additional global phase factor
(-1)^{n}
is irrelevant for the light propagation in the waveguides.

IV
Topological classification

Now, we show that the Floquet ladder hosts a
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
non-Hermitian Floquet topological phase which is protected by time-reversal symmetry. This phase can be fully characterized by the spectrum
e^{-\mathrm{i}\varepsilon(k)}
of the Floquet-Bloch propagator
U(k)
with the complex-valued quasienergies
\varepsilon(k)=\Re\varepsilon(k)+\mathrm{i}\Im\varepsilon(k)
. Note that the real part
\Re\varepsilon(k)
of the quasienergies is defined only up to multiples of
2\pi
. Here, we restrict it to the quasienergy zone
\Re\varepsilon\in[0,2\pi)
.

The Floquet-Bloch propagator is given by
U(k)=\prod_{i=1}^{6}U^{(i)}(k)
where
U^{(i)}(k)=\exp(-\mathrm{i}\,H^{(i)}(k))
are the Bloch propagators and
H^{(i)}(k)
the Bloch Hamiltonians of the six steps. The Bloch Hamiltonians and Bloch propagators can be deduced from the effective Hamiltonians
H^{\mathrm{w}}_{\mathrm{u}}
,
H^{\mathrm{w}}_{\mathrm{d}}
and propagators
U^{w}_{\mathrm{u}}
,
U^{w}_{\mathrm{d}}
of the coupled waveguides in combination with Fig.
2
(a). Since there is no gain in the waveguide lattice, the imaginary part of the quasienergies
\Im\varepsilon(k)\leq-4\gamma_{\mathrm{u}}
is bounded from above. Here,
-4\gamma_{\mathrm{u}}
are the uniform losses that all excitations in the Floquet ladder accumulate during one Floquet cycle due to steps
2
,
3
,
4
, and
5
.
Since the uniform losses act equally on all excitations, they are irrelevant for our discussion. In the following, we set
\gamma_{\mathrm{u}}\equiv 0
and thus omit the uniform losses in the specification of quasienergies
\varepsilon(k)
and output intensities
I_{l,r}(\phi)
.

The symmetry relation (
3
) implies that the four quasienergy bands of the ladder can be divided into two Kramers pairs, each of which satisfies

\varepsilon_{m}^{I}(k)=\varepsilon_{m}^{II}(-k)\;,

(4)

where
m=1,2
. The quasienergies
\varepsilon_{m}^{I}
,
\varepsilon_{m}^{II}
of each pair are degenerate at the invariant (
k\equiv-k
) momenta
k=0,\pi
and the corresponding eigenvectors are biorthogonal
Kawabata et al. 2019
;
Zhou and Lee 2019
. The Kramers degeneracy at the invariant momenta can only be lifted by breaking time-reversal symmetry.

To illustrate the main properties of the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
phase, let us consider the two parameter sets
J_{\mathrm{d}}=\pi/2,J_{\mathrm{u}}=0
and
J_{\mathrm{d}}=\pi/2
,
J_{\mathrm{u}}=\pi/2
, which realize the non-trivial and trivial phase, respectively. Figs.
2
(d,e)
show the patterns of motion and Figs.
2
(f,g) the quasienergy dispersions for the two parameter sets.

At
J_{\mathrm{d}}=\pi/2
,
J_{\mathrm{u}}=0
, any single-site excitation is transferred one unit cell to the right or left during one Floquet cycle. Correspondingly, each of the four quasienergy bands winds once around the quasienergy zone, two bands with positive chirality and two bands with negative chirality.
The four bands form two Kramers pairs with the dispersions
\varepsilon_{1}^{I,II}(k)=\pm k+\pi-4\mathrm{i}\gamma_{\mathrm{d}}
and
\varepsilon_{2}^{I,II}(k)=\pm k+\pi
. Due to the directional coupling, the two pairs are spectrally separated by an imaginary gap. The gap is located at the value
\Gamma=-2\gamma_{\mathrm{d}}
of the imaginary part
\Im\varepsilon(k)
of the quasienergy spectrum. For each pair, the bands only cross at the invariant momenta
k=0,\pi
. Due to the Kramers degeneracy, this crossing persists when the red and blue sublattice are coupled for
J_{\mathrm{u}}\neq 0
, see Fig.
2
(h). The quasienergy dispersion is symmetry-protected. As long as the symmetry relation (
3
) is not broken and the imaginary gap does not close, the paired bands must wind around the quasienergy zone with opposite chirality.

For
J_{\mathrm{d}}=\pi/2
,
J_{\mathrm{u}}=\pi/2
, excitations starting on the open sites
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\circ}
,
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\circ}
move in a closed loop, leading to the dispersionless Kramers pair
\varepsilon_{1}^{I,II}(k)=-4\mathrm{i}\gamma
. On the other hand, excitations that start on the filled sites
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\bullet}
,
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\bullet}
are transferred two unit cells to the right or left. Consequently, the two bands
\varepsilon_{2}^{I,II}(k)=\pm 2k+\pi
of the second Kramers pair wind twice around the quasienergy zone with two crossings at the non-invariant momenta
k=\pi/2
,
k=-\pi/2
. These crossings are not protected by Kramers degeneracy and thus are lifted for
J_{\mathrm{u}}\neq\pi/2
, see Fig.
2
(i). As a result, the second Kramers pair no longer winds around the quasienergy zone. Note that the imaginary gap at
\Gamma=-2\gamma_{\mathrm{d}}
does not close during this process. Here, the winding around the quasienergy zone is not symmetry-protected.

In general, the winding is only symmetry-protected if the paired bands wind an odd number of times around the quasienergy zone with opposite chirality. The number of windings modulo
2
is determined by the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-valued quantity

\displaystyle W_{\theta}(\Gamma)=\frac{1}{4\pi}\Bigg(

\displaystyle\sum_{\Gamma<\Im\varepsilon_{m}^{I}}\,\big[\Re\varepsilon_{m}^{I}(k)\big]_{k=-\pi}^{k=\pi}

(5)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\sum_{\Gamma<\Im\varepsilon_{m}^{II}}\big[\Re\varepsilon_{m}^{II}(k)\big]_{k=-\pi}^{k=\pi}\Bigg)\mod 2\;,

which includes all Kramers pairs above the imaginary gap
\mathrm{i}\Gamma
. Eq. (
5
) generalizes the
\mathbb{Z}
-valued winding number
W(\Gamma)
of Ref.
Höckendorf, Alvermann, and Fehske 2020
to systems with fermionic time-reversal symmetry.

For the trivial imaginary gap at
\Gamma=-\infty

W_{\theta}(-\infty)=0
. A single Kramers pair that winds an odd number of times around the quasienergy zone can not be generated by a time-reversal symmetric Hamiltonian. This directly follows from the invertibility of the propagator. Going in inverse time direction from

As a direct consequence, we have

for all

Non-zero values of

In the vicinity of
J_{\mathrm{d}}=\pi/2
and
J_{\mathrm{u}}=0
(
J_{\mathrm{u}}=\pi/2
), we have
W(\Gamma)=1
(
W(\Gamma)=0
) for the imaginary gap at
\Gamma=-2\gamma_{\mathrm{d}}
. The two phases are separated by an intermediate region

in which the imaginary gap between the two Kramers pairs closes (see Fig.

).
The two critical points
J_{\mathrm{u}}^{\mathrm{c},1}\leq J_{\mathrm{u}}^{\mathrm{c},2}
that mark the boundaries of the intermediate region are determined by the parameters
J_{\mathrm{d}}
and
\gamma_{\mathrm{d}}
. For
J_{\mathrm{d}}\neq 0
and
e^{-\gamma_{\mathrm{d}}}\to 0
, they coincide (
J_{\mathrm{u}}^{\mathrm{c},1}=J_{\mathrm{u}}^{\mathrm{c},2}=\pi/4
) and the intermediate region collapses to a single critical point. In the following, we will abbreviate the parameter values
e^{-\gamma_{\mathrm{d}}}\to 0
,
\lambda_{\mathrm{u}}=\pi/4
of this point with
p
. They are the optimal parameters for the external control of topological transport.

V
Controlling the direction of transport

The degree of control over the direction of transport in the setup of Fig.
1
(d) can be quantified with the peak intensity differences
b_{l}=\max(I_{l}(\phi)-I_{r}(\phi))/I_{\mathrm{tot}}
and
b_{r}=\max(I_{r}(\phi)-I_{l}(\phi))/I_{\mathrm{tot}}
of the two transport channels. The largest values of
b_{l,r}
are realized at
p
, where the interference between excitations starting on adjacent filled sites
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\bullet}
,
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\bullet}

VI
Conclusions

To summarize, we have shown that the one-dimensional Floquet ladder introduced in this work provides a promising platform for the external control of the direction of topological transport. Instead of controlling transport by modification of the waveguide lattice, i.e. by re-engineering the Floquet ladder, the direction of transport is simply and reliably controlled by changing the relative phase between the two input light beams of the initial excitation. The phase sensitivity is a specific quality of the present setup, by which it is distinguished from simpler setups without time-reversal symmetry. There, counter-propagating transport channels have to remain uncoupled to preserve their topological properties. In effect, this means that the direction of transport is fixed entirely by the initial population of each of the separate transport channels and cannot resolve the relative phase of the initial excitation.

Photonic waveguides provide a natural experimental setup for the implementation of our Floquet protocol and the realization of the transport control. The relative phase of the two input light beams can be controlled with a spatial light modulator as in, e.g., Ref.
Maczewsky et al. 2020
and the momenta of the inital excitation can be controlled via the incidence angles of the two light beams. Our calculations demonstrate that the control of transport is unaffected by the disorder inherent to waveguide lattices.

We have shown that the two distinct counter-propagating transport channels in the Floquet ladder are connected to a
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
non-Hermitian Floquet topological phase, and are thus protected by time-reversal symmetry. Similar to the relationship between two-dimensional
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
topological insulators and Chern insulators
Hasan and Kane 2010
, this
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
phase is a time-reversal symmetric generalization of the
\mathbb{Z}
topological phase that was recently discovered in non-Hermitian Floquet chains with unidirectional transport channels
Höckendorf, Alvermann, and Fehske 2020
;
Fedorova et al. 2020
. In both phases an imaginary gap separates quasienergy bands which wind around the quasienergy zone. The separation through the imaginary gap contributes to the robustness of the observed effects, even beyond the time-reversal symmetric situation. In the
\mathbb{Z}
phase, a large imaginary gap leads to quantized unidirectional transport
Höckendorf, Alvermann, and Fehske 2020
. For the present system, it remains an open question whether the two counter-propagatingpropagating transport channels become quantized in the limit of a large imaginary gap.

Interestingly, optimal conditions for the external control of transport are provided at the boundary of the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
phase, where the imaginary gap closes and the phase transition occurs. In a Hermitian Floquet system, this would indicate that transport is fragile, since a small perturbation can push the system into the topologically trivial phase. Here, in a non-Hermitian Floquet system, the transport remains robust when the system is pushed into the trivial phase because the imaginary part of the quasienergy is strongly momentum-dependent. Transport disappears only when this momentum-dependence flattens out
Höckendorf, Alvermann, and Fehske 2020
, which coincides with the closing of a point gap
Gong et al. 2018
in the quasienergy spectrum.
This phenomenon highlights the intrinsic differences between topologically robust transport in Hermitian and non-Hermitian Floquet systems.
