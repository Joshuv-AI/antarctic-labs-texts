Convergence of Continuous Normalizing Flows for Learning Probability Distributions
Yuan Gao, Jian Huang, Yuling Jiao, Shurong Zheng

Abstract
Continuous normalizing flows (CNFs) are a generative method for learning probability distributions, which is based on ordinary differential equations. This method has shown remarkable empirical success across various applications, including large-scale image synthesis, protein structure prediction, and molecule generation. In this work, we study the theoretical properties of CNFs with linear interpolation in learning probability distributions from a finite random sample, using a flow matching objective function. We establish non-asymptotic error bounds for the distribution estimator based on CNFs, in terms of the Wasserstein-2 distance. The key assumption in our analysis is that the target distribution satisfies one of the following three conditions: it either has a bounded support, is strongly log-concave, or is a finite or infinite mixture of Gaussian distributions. We present a convergence analysis framework that encompasses the error due to velocity estimation, the discretization error, and the early stopping error. A key step in our analysis involves establishing the regularity properties of the velocity field and its estimator for CNFs constructed with linear interpolation. This necessitates the development of uniform error bounds with Lipschitz regularity control of deep ReLU networks that approximate the Lipschitz function class, which could be of independent interest. Our nonparametric convergence analysis offers theoretical guarantees for using CNFs to learn probability distributions from a finite random sample.

Introduction

Let
\{\mathsf{X}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
be independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) random variables drawn from an underlying probability distribution
\nu
with support
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. The task of generative learning is to learn

\nu
from the data
\{\mathsf{X}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
by generating new samples

(
Salakhutdinov, 2015
)
. Several generative learning methods have been developed during the recent decade, including generative adversarial networks (GANs)
(
Goodfellow
et al., 2014
)
, variational autoencoders
(
Kingma and Welling, 2014
)
, diffusion models
(
Sohl-Dickstein et al., 2015
;
Ho, Jain and Abbeel, 2020
;
Song et al., 2021
)
, and
normalizing flows
(
Tabak and Turner, 2013
;
Rezende and
Mohamed, 2015
;
Chen et al., 2018
)
.
Deep neural networks
(
LeCun, Bengio and
Hinton, 2015
)
, as a powerful modeling tool,
have played an important role in the development of these methods. Among the generative learning methods, continuous normalizing flows (CNFs) use ordinary differential equations (ODEs) to determine a stochastic process for transporting a Gaussian distribution to the target distribution, achieving the goal of generative learning.

Simulation-free CNFs have achieved impressive empirical performance across various applications. These applications include large-scale image synthesis
(
Ma et al., 2024
)
, protein structure prediction
(
Jing, Berger and
Jaakkola, 2023
)
, and 3D molecule generation
(
Song et al., 2023
)
. Rectified flow
(
Liu, Gong and Liu, 2023
)
, a CNF model that linearly interpolates Gaussian noise and data, has been recently implemented in the large image
model Stable Diffusion 3
(
Esser et al., 2024
)
.
Simulation-free CNFs that use flow matching to learn probability distributions have been the focus of much recent attention
(
Albergo and
Vanden-Eijnden, 2023
;
Lipman et al., 2023
;
Liu, Gong and Liu, 2023
;
Neklyudov et al., 2023
)
.

An early model of CNFs was proposed by
Chen et al. (2018)
. This model is based on neural ODEs and employs a simulation-based maximum likelihood method to estimate velocity fields. However, simulation-based CNFs are computationally demanding in large-scale applications. To address the computational challenges of simulation-based CNFs, significant efforts have been made to develop simulation-free CNFs, where velocity fields can be represented in terms of conditional expectations. Noteworthy examples include the probability flows of diffusion models
(
Song et al., 2021
)
and denoising diffusion implicit models
(
Song, Meng and
Ermon, 2021
)
, which are trained using a denoising score matching objective function. In contrast, the flow matching method solves a least squares problem to estimate the conditional expectation that represents the velocity field
(
Albergo and
Vanden-Eijnden, 2023
;
Lipman et al., 2023
;
Liu, Gong and Liu, 2023
)
.

When a random sample from the target distribution is available, the process of learning simulation-free CNFs involves statistically and numerically solving a class of ODE-based initial value problems (IVPs). Specifically, consider the IVP on the unit time interval

\frac{\mathrm{d}X_{t}}{\mathrm{d}t}(x)=v(t,X_{t}(x)),\quad X_{0}(x)=x\sim\mu,\quad(t,x)\in[0,1]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d},

(1.1)

where
v
represents the velocity field, which can be estimated based on data. The solution to the IVP (
1.1
) is a family of flow maps
(X_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
indexed by the time variable, which generates a smoothing path between the source and target distributions.

Gao, Huang and Jiao (2023)
have studied the mathematical properties of these ODE-based IVPs under the framework of Gaussian interpolation flow. This defines a Gaussian smoothing path as a transport map that pushes forward a Gaussian distribution onto the target distribution in terms of measure transport. Simulation-free CNFs adopt a two-step “estimation-then-simulation” approach to learning the desired transport map based on a random sample. In the estimation stage, a deep learning model is trained to estimate the velocity field without simulating the ODE that defines the CNF. During the simulation stage, numerical solvers simulate the numerical solution of the ODE associated with the estimated velocity field, and the generated data is collected at the end time point.

In this work, we study the theoretical properties of simulation-free CNFs. We develop a general framework for error analyses of CNFs with flow matching for learning probability distributions based on a random sample. Central to simulation-free CNFs, deep ReLU networks are employed for function approximation and nonparametric estimation of the velocity field. We establish the approximation properties of deep ReLU networks with Lipschitz regularity control, which is essential for analyzing the impact of the estimated velocity field on the distribution of the data generated through the flow. In particular, it is crucial to control the Lipschitz regularity of the estimated velocity field to ensure that the associated IVP is well-posed.

1.1
Preview of main results

The following informal descriptions provide a preview of our main results. Our analysis is based on the key assumption that the target distribution satisfies one of the three conditions: it either has a bounded support, is strongly log-concave, or is a finite or infinite mixture of Gaussian distributions. The assumption of bounded support is practical, as all measurements can only be taken within a finite range. However, it is theoretically interesting to consider distributions with unbounded support. In such cases, we impose additional structures on the distributions, including mixtures of Gaussians and strong log-concavity. Mixtures of Gaussian distributions are multimodal and have a universal approximation capacity for smooth probability density functions. The assumption of strong log-concavity is crucial for the convergence analyses of Langevin Monte Carlo and Metropolis-Hastings algorithms when sampling from unnormalized distributions
(
Dalalyan, 2017
;
Durmus and
Moulines, 2017
;
Dwivedi et al., 2019
)
.

Our first main result concerns the regularity of the velocity fields of the CNFs constructed with linear interpolation. For a detailed definition of such CNFs, see Lemma
3.1
and equation (
3.4
).

Theorem 1.1
(Informal).

Assume that the target distribution either has a bounded support, is strongly log-concave, or is a mixture of Gaussians. Let
0<\underline{t}\ll 1
. The velocity fields of the CNFs with linear interpolation have the following regularity properties:

(i)

The velocity field
v^{*}
is Lipschitz continuous in the space variable
x
for
(t,x)\in[0,1]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, where the Lipschitz constant is uniformly bounded;

(ii)

The velocity field
v^{*}
is Lipschitz continuous in the time variable
t
for
(t,x)\in[0,1-\underline{t}]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, where the Lipschitz constant grows at the order of
{\mathcal{O}}(\underline{t}^{-2})
as
\underline{t}\downarrow 0
;

(iii)

The velocity field
v^{*}
spatially has a linear growth on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
for each
t\in[0,1]
.

Remark 1
.

The regularity properties of the velocity fields stated in Theorem
1.1
are derived from the assumptions made about the underlying target distribution. These properties are essential for studying the distributions generated by the corresponding CNFs.

Theorem 1.2
(Informal).

Suppose that the target distribution has a bounded support, or is strongly log-concave, or is a mixture of Gaussians.
Let
n
be the sample size and
0<\underline{t}\ll 1
satisfying

\underline{t}\asymp n^{-1/(d+5)}
.
By properly setting the deep ReLU network structure and the forward Euler discretization step sizes,
the distribution estimation error of the CNFs learned with linear interpolation and flow matching is evaluated by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathcal{W}_{2}(\hat{\nu}_{1-\underline{t}},\nu)=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(n^{-\frac{1}{d+5}}),

(1.2)

where the expectation is taken with respect to all random samples,
\hat{\nu}_{1-\underline{t}}
is the law of generated data,
\nu
is the law of target data,
\mathcal{W}_{2}(\cdot,\cdot)
is the Wasserstein-2 distance, and a polylogarithmic prefactor in
n
is omitted.

Remark 2
.

As can be seen from Theorem
1.1
or Theorem
5.1
below, the velocity fields associated with the CNFs based on linear interpolation may be singular in the time variable at
t=1
due to the exploding Lipschitz constant bound.
This singularity affects the convergence rate in (
1.2
). Without the time singularity of the velocity field, the distribution estimation error would be bounded by
\widetilde{{\mathcal{O}}}(n^{-1/(d+3)})
. The time singularity leads to a necessary trade-off regarding
\underline{t}
between the error due to velocity estimation and the early stopping error. The trade-off reduces the nonparametric convergence rate of the distribution estimator to
\widetilde{{\mathcal{O}}}(n^{-1/(d+5)})
.
See also Remark
10
for additional comments and explanation.

1.2
Our contributions

We present a comprehensive error analysis of simulation-free CNFs with linear interpolation, trained using flow matching. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first analysis of its kind in the context of simulation-free CNFs. Our results are based solely on assumptions about the target distribution, and all regularity conditions are rigorously derived from these assumptions. Our analysis accurately reflects the practical computational implementation of flow matching for learning simulation-free CNFs.
Although our focus is on CNFs based on linear interpolation due to their widespread use and for the sake of simplicity, our analytical framework can be applied to CNFs based on other types of interpolation as well.

We summarize our main contributions into four points:

(1)

We establish non-asymptotic error bounds for distribution estimators based on simulation-free CNFs with linear interpolation and flow matching. These bounds apply to distributions that satisfy one of the following conditions: (a) they have a bounded support, (b) they are strongly log-concave, or (c) they are mixtures of Gaussians. We present a convergence analysis framework that encompasses the error due to velocity estimation, the discretization error, and the early stopping error. We show that the nonparametric convergence rate of the distribution estimator is
\widetilde{{\mathcal{O}}}(n^{-1/(d+5)})
up to a polylogarithmic prefactor in the sample size
n
.

(2)

We derive regularity properties for the velocity field of the CNF with linear interpolation. We demonstrate the Lipschitz regularity properties of the velocity field in both the space and time variables and establish bounds for the Lipschitz constants. We also show that the velocity field grows at most linearly with respect to the space variable.

(3)

We establish error bounds for deep ReLU network approximation within the Lipschitz function class, demonstrating that the constructed approximation function maintains Lipschitz regularity. We also derive time-space approximation bounds for approximating the velocity field in both the time and space variables. These time-space approximation bounds are novel in three respects. Firstly, the approximation bounds are derived in terms of the
L^{\infty}
norm. Secondly, we demonstrate that the constructed time-space approximation function is Lipschitz in both the time and space variables, with Lipschitz constants of the same order as those of the target function. Lastly, the time-space approximation function can exhibit different Lipschitz regularity in the time and space variables. These neural network approximation results, which maintain Lipschitz regularity, could be of independent interest.

(4)

We establish the statistical consistency of the flow matching estimator for the velocity field. By rigorously bounding the stochastic and approximation errors, we show that the convergence rate of the flow matching estimator coincides with the minimax optimal rate of nonparametric estimation of regression functions belonging to the Sobolev space
W^{1,\infty}([0,1]^{d})
.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we present the preliminary materials required for subsequent sections. In Section
3
, we describe simulation-free CNFs and outline the steps for using these CNFs for generative learning. In Section
4
, we derive our main result concerning the error bounds for the distribution estimator based on CNFs with linear interpolation. In Section
5
, we first present some useful regularity properties of the velocity field and establish error bounds for the estimated velocity fields through flow matching. Section
6
contains discussions on related works in the existing literature. We conclude with remarks and discussions on potential future problems in Section
7
.

2.1
Definitions

We list a few useful definitions in this subsection.

Definition 2.1

(
Cattiaux and Guillin, 2014
)
.

A probability distribution
\mu(\mathrm{d}x)=\exp(-U)\mathrm{d}x
is
\kappa
-semi-log-concave for some
\kappa\in{\mathbb{R}}
if its support
\Omega\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is convex and its potential function
U\in C^{2}(\Omega;{\mathbb{R}})
satisfies

\nabla^{2}_{x}U(x)\succeq\kappa\mathrm{I}_{d},\quad\forall x\in\Omega.

Note that here
\kappa
is allowed to be negative.

Definition 2.2

(
Eldan and Lee, 2018
)
.

A probability distribution
\mu(\mathrm{d}x)=\exp(-U)\mathrm{d}x
is
\beta
-semi-log-convex for some
\beta>0
if its support
\Omega\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is convex and its potential function
U\in C^{2}(\Omega;{\mathbb{R}})
satisfies

\nabla^{2}_{x}U(x)\preceq\beta\mathrm{I}_{d},\quad\forall x\in\Omega.

The rectified linear unit (ReLU) activation function is defined as
\varrho(x):=\max(0,x)
for
x\in{\mathbb{R}},
which also operates coordinate-wise on elements of
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. For
k\geq 2
, the ReLU

activation function is given by
\varrho_{k}(x):=(\max(0,x))^{k}
.

Definition 2.3

(Deep ReLU networks)
.

Deep ReLU networks stand for a class of feed-forward artificial neural networks defined with ReLU activation functions.
The function
f_{\theta}(x):{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathtt{k}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathtt{d}}
implemented by a deep ReLU network with parameter
\theta
is expressed as composition of a sequence of functions

\displaystyle f_{\theta}(x):=l_{\mathtt{D}}\circ\varrho\circ l_{\mathtt{D}-1}\circ\varrho\circ\cdots\circ l_{1}\circ\varrho\circ l_{0}(x)

for any
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathtt{k}}
, where
\varrho(x)
is the ReLU activation function and the depth
\mathtt{D}
is the number of hidden layers.
For
i=0,1,\cdots,\mathtt{D}
, the
i
-th layer is represented by
l_{i}(x):=A_{i}x+b_{i}
, where
A_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{i+1}\times d_{i}}
is the weight matrix,
b_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{i+1}}
is the bias vector, and
d_{i}
is the width of the
i
-th layer.
The network
f_{\theta}
contains
\mathtt{D}+1
layers in all.
We use a
(\mathtt{D}+1)
-dimension vector
(d_{0},d_{1},\cdots,d_{\mathtt{D}})^{\top}
to describe the width of each layer.
In particular,
d_{0}=\mathtt{k}
is the dimension of the domain and
d_{\mathtt{D}}=\mathtt{d}
is the dimension of the codomain.
The width
\mathtt{W}
is defined as the maximum width of hidden layers, that is,
\mathtt{W}=\max\left\{d_{1},d_{2},\cdots,d_{\mathtt{D}}\right\}
.
The size
\mathtt{S}
denotes the total number of nonzero parameters in the network
f_{\theta}
.
The bound
\mathtt{B}
denotes the

bound of
f_{\theta}
, that is,
\sup_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathtt{k}}}\|f_{\theta}(x)\|_{\infty}\leq\mathtt{B}
.
We denote the function class
\{f_{\theta}:{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathtt{k}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathtt{d}}\}
implemented by deep ReLU networks with size
\mathtt{S}
, width
\mathtt{W}
, depth
\mathtt{D}
, and bound
\mathtt{B}
as
\mathcal{NN}(\mathtt{S},\mathtt{W},\mathtt{D},\mathtt{B},\mathtt{k},\mathtt{d})
.

Definition 2.4

(Wasserstein-
2
distance)
.

The Wasserstein-

distance between two probability distributions on

is the

optimal transportation cost defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{2}(\mu,\nu):=\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\left(\mathbb{E}_{(\mathsf{X},\mathsf{Y})\sim\pi}\|\mathsf{X}-\mathsf{Y}\|_{2}^{2}\right)^{1/2},

where
\Pi(\mu,\nu)
denotes the set of all joint probability distributions

and
\nu
. A distribution
\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)

.

2.2
Assumptions

We state the assumptions on the target distribution
\nu
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
to support the main results.
Below we use
\mathrm{supp}(\nu)
to denote the support of a probability distribution
\nu
. We also use
\mathrm{diam}(\Omega)
to represent the diameter of a set
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.

Assumption 1
.

The probability distribution
\nu
is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure and has a zero mean.

Assumption 2
.

Let
D:=(1/\sqrt{2})\mathrm{diam}(\mathrm{supp}(\nu))
.
The probability distribution
\nu
satisfies any one of the following conditions:

(i)

\nu
is
\kappa
-semi-log-concave for some
\kappa>0
with
D\in(0,\infty]
and
\beta
-semi-log-convex for some
\beta>0
;

(ii)

\nu
is
\kappa
-semi-log-concave for some
\kappa\leq 0
with
D\in(0,\infty)
and
\beta
-semi-log-convex for some
\beta>0
;

(iii)

\nu=\gamma_{d,\sigma^{2}}*\rho
where
\rho
is a probability distribution supported on a Euclidean ball of radius
R
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Remark 3

(Distribution classes)
.

Assumption
2
covers three classes of distributions that are of great interest in the literature on generative learning and sampling. Let us briefly remark on the properties of the distributions:

(1)

Distributions with a bounded support are covered in Assumption
2
-(ii). The boundedness provides a great source of regularity, which frees us from the requirement that the Hessian matrix of the potential function is positively lower bounded. A negative lower bound of the Hessian matrix
allows the distribution has a multimodal landscape.

(2)

Strongly log-concave distributions are considered in Assumption
2
-(i). For distributions in this class, the Hessian matrix of the potential function is both positively lower bounded and positively upper bounded.

(3)

Mixtures of Gaussians are considered in Assumption
2
-(iii). A mixture of Gaussians is notably a multimodal probability distribution, which is neither strongly log-concave nor bounded.

Remark 4

(Score Lipschitzness)
.

For any
\beta>0,\kappa\in{\mathbb{R}},
and
\kappa\leq\beta
, the
\beta
-semi-log-convexity and
\kappa
-semi-log-concavity measure the Lipschitzness of the smooth score function
S(x):=\nabla_{x}\log\frac{\mathrm{d}\nu}{\mathrm{d}x}(x)
in the sense that
\kappa\mathrm{I}_{d}\preceq\nabla_{x}S(x)\preceq\beta\mathrm{I}_{d}
.
The Lipschitzness of the score function for a probability distribution is a common assumption in the literature studying convergence properties of Langevin Monte Carlo algorithms and score-based diffusion models (cf.
Dalalyan (2017)
;
Durmus and
Moulines (2017)
;
Chen et al. (2023a)
;
Chen, Lee and Lu (2023)
).

Remark 5

(Sub-Gaussianity)
.

The probability distribution
\nu
considered in Assumption
2
satisfies the log-Sobolev inequality with a finite constant
C_{\mathrm{LSI}}
depending on
\kappa>0
or
(\kappa,D)
or
(\sigma,R)

(
Mikulincer and
Shenfeld, 2021
;
Dai et al., 2023
)
. Let
\mathsf{V}\sim\nu
and
\mathsf{V}=[\mathsf{V}_{1},\mathsf{V}_{2},\cdots,\mathsf{V}_{d}]^{\top}
. Then a standard Herbst’s argument
shows that
\mathsf{V}_{i}
is sub-Gaussian, and its sub-Gaussian norm
\|\mathsf{V}_{i}\|_{\psi_{2}}\asymp\sqrt{C_{\mathrm{LSI}}}
for
1\leq i\leq d
owing to
Ledoux (2001, Theorem 5.3)
.
In addition, a sub-Gaussian random variable has a finite fourth moment.

3
Simulation-free continuous normalizing flows

The basic idea of simulation-free CNFs is to construct an ODE-based IVP with a tractable velocity field. The flow map of the IVP pushes forward a simple source distribution onto the underlying target distribution. It is essential to be able to efficiently estimate the velocity field with a random sample from the target distribution. Since CNFs use ODEs to model a target distribution, the corresponding velocity fields depend on the target distribution and may have a complex, unknown structure. Therefore, it is natural to employ nonparametric methods with deep neural networks to estimate velocity fields.

In Table
1
, we summarize the four steps of
using simulation-free CNFs for generative learning, and we discuss each step in detail below.

Table 1:
Four steps to conduct generative learning via simulation-free CNFs.

Task
: Generating samples from a distribution approximating the target distribution
\nu
.

Step 1.

Construct a simulation-free CNF defined by the IVP such that
\nu={X_{T}}_{\#}\mu
:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}X_{t}}{\mathrm{d}t}(x)=v(t,X_{t}(x)),\quad X_{0}(x)=x\sim\mu,\quad(t,x)\in[0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d},

where
T>0,v:[0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is the velocity field, and
\mu
is a simple source distribution.

Step 2.

Estimate the velocity field
v(t,x)
with a deep neural network
\hat{v}(t,x)
.

Step 3.

Use a proper numerical solver to solve the IVP associated with
\hat{v}(t,x)
, and return the numerical solution
\hat{Y}_{T}(x)
at time
t=T
:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}Y_{t}}{\mathrm{d}t}(x)=\hat{v}(t,Y_{t}(x)),\quad Y_{0}(x)=x\sim\mu,\quad(t,x)\in[0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}.

Step 4.

Generate samples from
{\hat{Y}_{T}{}}_{\#}\mu
, which approximates
\nu={X_{T}}_{\#}\mu
.

3.1
Construction of simulation-free CNFs

Based on the concept of stochastic interpolation,
Liu, Gong and Liu (2023)
and
Albergo and
Vanden-Eijnden (2023)
proposed a new class of simulation-free CNFs.
Gao, Huang and Jiao (2023)

analyzed probability flows of diffusion models and denoising diffusion implicit models
in the framework of stochastic interpolation.
Let
a_{t}:[0,1]\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+},b_{t}:[0,1]\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
satisfy the following conditions:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\dot{a}_{t}\leq 0,\quad\dot{b}_{t}\geq 0,\quad a_{0}>0,\quad b_{0}\geq 0,\quad a_{1}=0,\quad b_{1}=1,\\
&a_{t}>0\ \ \text{for any $t\in(0,1)$},\quad b_{t}>0\ \ \text{for any $t\in(0,1)$},\\
&a_{t},b_{t}\in C^{2}([0,1)),\quad a_{t}^{2}\in C^{1}([0,1]),\quad b_{t}\in C^{1}([0,1]).\end{aligned}

Then a general class of simulation-free CNFs is constructed in Lemma
3.1
and satisfies the requirements of Step 1 in Table
1
.
For simplicity, we use
\mathrm{Law}(\mathsf{X})
to denote the distribution of a random variable
\mathsf{X}
.

Lemma 3.1
(Theorem 38 in
Gao, Huang and Jiao (2023)
).

Suppose that a probability distribution
\nu
satisfies Assumptions
1
and
2
.
Let
\mu=\mathrm{Law}(a_{0}\mathsf{Z}+b_{0}\mathsf{X}_{1})
with
\mathsf{Z}\sim\gamma_{d},\mathsf{X}_{1}\sim\nu
,
\mathsf{X}_{t}:=a_{t}\mathsf{Z}+b_{t}\mathsf{X}_{1}
for any
t\in(0,1).
Let the velocity field
v(t,x)
be defined by

\displaystyle v(t,x)

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}[\dot{a}_{t}\mathsf{Z}+\dot{b}_{t}\mathsf{X}_{1}|\mathsf{X}_{t}=x],\quad(t,x)\in(0,1)\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d},

(3.1)

\displaystyle v(0,x)

\displaystyle:=\lim_{t\downarrow 0}v(t,x),\quad v(1,x):=\lim_{t\uparrow 1}v(t,x),~~x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}.

(3.2)

Then there exists a unique solution
(X_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
to the IVP

\frac{\mathrm{d}X_{t}}{\mathrm{d}t}(x)=v(t,X_{t}(x)),\quad X_{0}(x)=x\sim\mu,\quad(t,x)\in[0,1]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}.

(3.3)

Moreover, the push-forward distribution satisfies
{X_{t}}_{\#}\mu=\mathrm{Law}(a_{t}\mathsf{Z}+b_{t}\mathsf{X}_{1})
with
\mathsf{Z}\sim\gamma_{d},\mathsf{X}_{1}\sim\nu
.

Lemma
3.1
shows that the velocity field
v(t,x)
of the simulation-free CNF (
3.3
) takes the form of conditional expectations. As a result, the least squares method, also known as the flow matching method for simulation-free CNFs
(
Lipman et al., 2023
)
, is an effective approach to estimating the velocity field
v(t,x)
.

Among various choices of the coefficients
a_{t}
and
b_{t}
,
the linear interpolation scenario, where

\displaystyle a_{t}=1-t,\ b_{t}=t,

(3.4)

has been shown to have excellent properties for generative learning tasks
(
Liu, Gong and Liu, 2023
;
Albergo, Boffi and
Vanden-Eijnden, 2023
)
. A CNF model with linear interpolation has been used in the implementation of a large image generative model
(
Esser et al., 2024
)
. The coefficients of the linear interpolation are the same as those of the displacement interpolation in optimal transport
(
McCann, 1997
;
Villani, 2009
)
. In this paper, we focus on the regularity and convergence properties of the CNF with linear interpolation (
3.4
).

Corollary 3.2
.

Suppose that a probability distribution
\nu
satisfies Assumptions
1
and
2
.
Let
\mathsf{X}_{0}=\mathsf{Z}\sim\gamma_{d}
,
\mathsf{X}_{1}\sim\nu
.
Consider the linear interpolation
\mathsf{X}_{t}:=(1-t)\mathsf{Z}+t\mathsf{X}_{1}
for any
t\in(0,1)
and the velocity field
v^{*}(t,x)
defined by

\displaystyle v^{*}(t,x)

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}[\mathsf{X}_{1}-\mathsf{Z}|\mathsf{X}_{t}=x],\quad(t,x)\in[0,1]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}.

(3.5)

Then there exists a unique solution
(X_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
to the IVP

\frac{\mathrm{d}X_{t}}{\mathrm{d}t}(x)=v^{*}(t,X_{t}(x)),\quad X_{0}(x)=x\sim\gamma_{d},\quad(t,x)\in[0,1]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d},

(3.6)

and the push-forward distribution satisfies
{X_{t}}_{\#}\gamma_{d}=\mathrm{Law}(\mathsf{X}_{t})
.

Corollary
3.2
implies that the push-forward distributions
({X_{t}}_{\#}\gamma_{d})_{t\in[0,1]}
coincide with the marginal distributions of the Gaussian channel
\mathsf{X}_{t}=(1-t)\mathsf{Z}+t\mathsf{X}_{1}
.
The connections between the velocity fields and Gaussian channels have inspired moment expressions for the derivatives of the velocity field. We show these expressions in Lemmas
3.3
and
3.4
for the purpose of examining the regularity properties of velocity fields.

Remark 6
.

Since
x=\mathbb{E}[(1-t)\mathsf{Z}+t\mathsf{X}_{1}|\mathsf{X}_{t}=x]
for
(t,x)\in[0,1]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, an alternative expression of the velocity field is given by

v^{*}(t,x)=-\frac{1}{1-t}x+\frac{1}{1-t}\mathbb{E}[\mathsf{X}_{1}|\mathsf{X}_{t}=x],\quad(t,x)\in[0,1)\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}.

(3.7)

Expression (
3.7
) shows that the velocity field
v^{*}(t,x)
only depends on the conditional expectation
\mathbb{E}[\mathsf{X}_{1}|\mathsf{X}_{t}=x].

Lemma 3.3
(Lemma 26 in
Gao, Huang and Jiao (2023)
).

The Jacobian matrix
\nabla_{x}v^{*}(t,x)
has a covariance expression as follows

\nabla_{x}v^{*}(t,x)=\frac{t}{(1-t)^{3}}\mathrm{Cov}(\mathsf{X}_{1}|\mathsf{X}_{t}=x)-\frac{1}{1-t}\mathrm{I}_{d},\quad(t,x)\in[0,1)\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}.

(3.8)

Remark 7
.

The covariance expression (
3.8
) has been used to derive regularity properties of the velocity field
v^{*}(t,x)
in the space variable
x
. For example, see Proposition 29 in
Gao, Huang and Jiao (2023)
.

Lemma 3.4
(Proposition 63 in
Gao, Huang and Jiao (2023)
).

The time derivative of the velocity field
v^{*}(t,x)
has a moment expression for any
t\in[0,1)
as follows

\displaystyle\partial_{t}v^{*}(t,x)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{(1-t)^{2}}x+\frac{1}{(1-t)^{2}}M_{1}+\frac{t+1}{(1-t)^{4}}M^{c}_{2}x-\frac{t}{(1-t)^{4}}(M_{3}-M_{2}M_{1}),

(3.9)

where
M_{1}:=\mathbb{E}[\mathsf{X}_{1}|\mathsf{X}_{t}=x],M_{2}:=\mathbb{E}[\mathsf{X}_{1}^{\top}\mathsf{X}_{1}|\mathsf{X}_{t}=x],M^{c}_{2}:=\mathrm{Cov}(\mathsf{X}_{1}|\mathsf{X}_{t}=x)
, and
M_{3}:=\mathbb{E}[\mathsf{X}_{1}\mathsf{X}_{1}^{\top}\mathsf{X}_{1}|\mathsf{X}_{t}=x]
with omitted dependence on
(t,x)
.

Remark 8
.

To quantify the regularity of the velocity field
v^{*}(t,x)
in the time variable
t
, one can try to bound the moments in Eq. (
3.9
) defined in Lemma
3.4
. Following this idea, we conduct regularity analyses on the velocity field in Appendix
A
and summarize the results in Theorem
5.1
.

3.2
Flow matching

This subsection concerns Step 2 in Table
1
, which estimates the velocity field of a CNF with linear interpolation.
As shown in (
3.5
), the velocity field
v^{*}(t,x)=\mathbb{E}[\mathsf{X}_{1}-\mathsf{Z}|\mathsf{X}_{t}=x]
for each
t\in[0,1].

For notational simplicity, let
\mathsf{Y}:=\mathsf{X}_{1}-\mathsf{Z}
, and note that
\mathsf{X}_{t}=(1-t)\mathsf{Z}+t\mathsf{X}_{1}
.
Given
\tau\in(0,1]
, we consider the time interval
[0,\tau]
.
For
t\in[0,\tau]
, we denote
\mathsf{X}_{t}\sim p_{t}
.
When the time is a random variable distributed as a uniform distribution on
[0,\tau]
, that is,
\mathsf{t}\sim\mathrm{U}(0,\tau)
, we denote
\mathsf{X}_{\mathsf{t}}|\mathsf{t}=t\sim p_{t}
.
Then the flow matching method
(
Lipman et al., 2023
;
Liu, Gong and Liu, 2023
)
solves a nonlinear least squares problem for estimating

v^{*}(t,x)=\mathbb{E}[\mathsf{Y}|\mathsf{X}_{t}=x]
on the domain
[0,\tau]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
:

\displaystyle v^{*}\in\argmin_{v}\Big\{\mathcal{L}(v):=\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{t}\sim\mathrm{U}(0,\tau)}\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{X}_{1}\sim\nu,\mathsf{Z}\sim\gamma_{d}}\|v(\mathsf{t},\mathsf{X}_{\mathsf{t}})-\mathsf{Y}\|_{2}^{2}\Big\}.

(3.10)

In practice,
\tau
is often taken as
1
. Here, we leave
\tau
as a quantity adaptive to the time regularity of the velocity field
v^{*}
. We will analyze the time regularity of
v^{*}
in Subsection
5.1
.

Let
\{\mathsf{Z}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
,
\{\mathsf{X}_{1,i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, and
\{\mathsf{t}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
be i.i.d. random samples from
\gamma_{d}
,
\nu
, and
\mathrm{U}(0,\tau)
, respectively.
For
i=1,2,\cdots,n
, we denote
\mathsf{X}_{\mathsf{t}_{i}}:=(1-\mathsf{t}_{i})\mathsf{Z}_{i}+\mathsf{t}_{i}\mathsf{X}_{1,i}
and
\mathsf{Y}_{i}:=\mathsf{X}_{1,i}-\mathsf{Z}_{i}
.
The population risk
\mathcal{L}(v)
defined in (
3.10
)
leads to the empirical risk

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{n}(v):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|v(\mathsf{t}_{i},\mathsf{X}_{\mathsf{t}_{i}})-\mathsf{Y}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}.

(3.11)

We approximate the velocity field
v^{*}
using deep neural networks.
We consider a deep ReLU network class with the input dimension
\mathtt{k}=d+1
and the output dimension
\mathtt{d}=d
.
Let
\mathcal{F}_{n}:=\mathcal{NN}(\mathtt{S},\mathtt{W},\mathtt{D},\mathtt{B},\mathtt{L}_{x},\mathtt{L}_{t},d+1,d)
denote a function class
\{f_{\theta}(t,x):{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\}
implemented by deep ReLU networks with size
\mathtt{S}
, width
\mathtt{W}
, depth
\mathtt{D}
, bound
\mathtt{B}
, and Lipschitz constants at most
\mathtt{L}_{x}
in
x
and
\mathtt{L}_{t}
in
t
over
(t,x)\in[0,\tau]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.
The network parameters can depend on the sample size
n
, and we show the fact by making
\mathcal{F}_{n}
depend on
n
.
For any
f\in\mathcal{F}_{n}
, the Lipschitz continuity of
f
implies that
\|f(t,x)-f(s,x)\|_{\infty}\leq\mathtt{L}_{t}|t-s|
and
\|f(t,x)-f(t,y)\|_{\infty}\leq\mathtt{L}_{x}\|x-y\|_{\infty}
for any
s,t\in[0,\tau]
and
x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.
It is easy to see that
\mathcal{F}_{n}\subseteq\mathcal{NN}(\mathtt{S},\mathtt{W},\mathtt{D},\mathtt{B},d+1,d)
, that is a deep ReLU network class without Lipschitz regularity control.
To estimate the velocity field within the hypothesis class
\mathcal{F}_{n}
, we consider the empirical risk minimization problem

\displaystyle\hat{v}_{n}\in\argmin_{v\in\mathcal{F}_{n}}\mathcal{L}_{n}(v),

(3.12)

where
\hat{v}_{n}
is a deep ReLU network estimator for the velocity field
v^{*}
.
We call
\hat{v}_{n}
the flow matching estimator because it is a minimizer of the empirical flow matching objective.

3.3
Forward Euler discretization

In this subsection, we proceed to Step 3 in Table
1
, where a numerical solver is used to solve the IVP:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\tilde{X}_{t}}{\mathrm{d}t}(x)=\hat{v}_{n}(t,\tilde{X}_{t}(x)),~~\tilde{X}_{0}(x)\sim\gamma_{d},~~(t,x)\in[0,\tau]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}.

(3.13)

The forward Euler method is a first-order numerical procedure for solving ODE-based IVPs, which is commonly used in algorithms for sampling and generative learning.
First, we set a time grid of
[0,\tau]
as
t_{0}=0<t_{1}<\cdots<t_{K}=\tau\leq 1
. The forward Euler method for solving the IVP (
3.13
)
yields the numerical iterations:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\hat{X}_{t}}{\mathrm{d}t}(x)=\hat{v}_{n}(t_{k-1},\hat{X}_{t_{k-1}}(x)),~~\hat{X}_{0}(x)\sim\gamma_{d},~~t\in[t_{k-1},t_{k}],\ k=1,2,\cdots,K.

(3.14)

Finally, Step 4 in Table
1
is accomplished by drawing samples from
\gamma_{d}
and running the numerical iterations given in (
3.14
).

In this section, we derive our main result, Theorem
4.4
, on the
error bounds for the distribution estimator based on CNFs with linear interpolation.

4.1
Error decomposition

We begin with three IVPs (
3.6
), (
3.13
), and (
3.14
) in Section
3
. The IVP (
3.6
) defines the true process without approximation of the velocity field
v^{*}
and discretization in time. The IVP (
3.13
) defines the neural process resulting from replacing the velocity field
v^{*}
with the flow matching estimator
\hat{v}_{n}
. The IVP (
3.14
) is the forward Euler discretization counterpart of the IVP (
3.13
).

Let
0<\underline{t}\ll 1
. As shown in Theorem
1.1
or Theorem
5.1
below, the Lipschitz constant bound of the velocity field in the time variable is of the order
{\mathcal{O}}(\underline{t}^{-2})
on the time interval
[0,1-\underline{t}]
.
This order shows that the bound explodes at the time
t=1
.
To maintain the Lipschitz regularity in the time variable for flow matching, we need to consider an early stopping time by letting the end time
\tau=1-\underline{t}
.

Solving the IVPs (
3.6
), (
3.13
), and (
3.14
), we
obtain the flow maps
(X_{t})_{t\in[0,1]},(\tilde{X}_{t})_{t\in[0,1-\underline{t}]}
, and
(\hat{X}_{t})_{t\in[0,1-\underline{t}]}
. To simplify the notations, we denote the push-forward distributions
{X_{t}}_{\#}\gamma_{d},{\tilde{X}_{t}{}}_{\#}\gamma_{d},{\hat{X}_{t}{}}_{\#}\gamma_{d}
by
p_{t},\tilde{p}_{t},\hat{p}_{t}
, respectively. We summarize the three processes (
3.6
), (
3.13
), and (
3.14
), their corresponding flow maps and push-forward distributions defined by the three IVPs, their corresponding velocity fields and density functions, and sources of errors in Table

.

Discretization

There are three sources of errors introduced in the generative learning process (
3.6
), (
3.13
), and (
3.14
).
The discretization error comes from the forward Euler discretization.
The error due to velocity estimation results from flow matching with deep ReLU networks.
The early stopping error is due to the time singularity of the velocity field at time
t=1
.
We use the Wasserstein-2 distance
{\mathcal{W}}_{2}
to measure the difference between the estimated
generative distribution
\hat{p}_{1-\underline{t}}
and the target distribution
p_{1}
.
We derive an upper bound for
\mathcal{W}_{2}(\hat{p}_{1-\underline{t}},p_{1}),
which
takes into account all the three sources of error.

It is important to consider the trade-off between the different sources of errors.
The early stopping error is reduced when the parameter
\underline{t}
gets smaller. However, a smaller value of
\underline{t}
increases the time singularity of the velocity field on the time interval
[0,1-\underline{t}]
, thus leads to a larger error due to velocity estimation.

Keeping the error trade-off in mind, we consider a basic decomposition of the
total error in terms of the Wasserstein-2 distance as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{2}(\hat{p}_{1-\underline{t}},p_{1})\leq\underbrace{\mathcal{W}_{2}(\hat{p}_{1-\underline{t}},\tilde{p}_{1-\underline{t}})}_{\text{discretization}}+\underbrace{\mathcal{W}_{2}(\tilde{p}_{1-\underline{t}},p_{1-\underline{t}})}_{\text{velocity estimation}}+\underbrace{\mathcal{W}_{2}(p_{1-\underline{t}},p_{1})}_{\text{early stopping}}.

(4.1)

In (
4.1
), the first term
\mathcal{W}_{2}(\hat{p}_{1-\underline{t}},\tilde{p}_{1-\underline{t}})
measures the discretization error,
the second term
\mathcal{W}_{2}(\tilde{p}_{1-\underline{t}},p_{1-\underline{t}})
measures the error due to velocity estimation, and the third term
\mathcal{W}_{2}(p_{1-\underline{t}},p_{1})
measures the early stopping error. We evaluate each error term in
Lemmas
4.1
,
4.2
, and
4.3
below, respectively.

Lemma 4.1
(Discretization error).

Suppose that Assumptions
1
and
2
hold.
Let
\Upsilon\equiv t_{k}-t_{k-1}
for
k=1,2,\cdots,K
.
Then the discretization error is evaluated by

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{2}(\hat{p}_{1-\underline{t}},\tilde{p}_{1-\underline{t}})=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{d}e^{\mathtt{L}_{x}}(\mathtt{L}_{x}\mathtt{B}+\mathtt{L}_{t})\Upsilon\right).

Lemma
4.1
shows that the error due to the forward Euler discretization is well controlled when the discretization step size
\Upsilon
is sufficiently small.
We use the perturbation analysis of ODE flows to derive the error bound in Lemma
4.1
, and the proof can be found in Appendix
D
.

Remark 9
.

Lemma
4.1
considers a uniform step size for the forward Euler discretization.
For more general choices of the step size, we need the general condition

\displaystyle\Upsilon^{2}=\sum\nolimits_{k=1}^{K}(t_{k}-t_{k-1})^{3}

(4.2)

to ensure that the error bound in Lemma
4.1
holds.
This can be shown following the proof of Lemma
4.1
.

In the sequel, we frequently take expectations over
(\mathsf{t},\mathsf{X}_{\mathsf{t}})
whose joint distribution is specified by
\mathsf{t}\sim\mathrm{U}(0,1-\underline{t})
and
\mathsf{X}_{\mathsf{t}}|\mathsf{t}=t\sim p_{t}
.
For ease of notation, we may omit the notation of the joint distribution when taking expectations over
(\mathsf{t},\mathsf{X}_{\mathsf{t}}).

Lemma 4.2
(Error due to velocity estimation).

Suppose that Assumptions
1
and
2
hold, and let
\hat{v}_{n}\in\mathcal{F}_{n}
satisfy

\|\hat{v}_{n}(t,x)-\hat{v}_{n}(t,y)\|_{\infty}\leq\mathtt{L}_{x}\|x-y\|_{\infty}
for any
t\in[0,1-\underline{t}]
and
x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.
Then the error due to velocity estimation is bounded by

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\tilde{p}_{1-\underline{t}},p_{1-\underline{t}})\leq\exp(2\mathtt{L}_{x}+1)\mathbb{E}_{(\mathsf{t},\mathsf{X}_{\mathsf{t}})}\|\hat{v}_{n}(\mathsf{t},\mathsf{X}_{\mathsf{t}})-v^{*}(\mathsf{t},\mathsf{X}_{\mathsf{t}})\|_{2}^{2}.

(4.3)

Lemma
4.2
states that the error due to velocity estimation is controlled by the excess risk of the flow matching estimator
\hat{v}_{n}
when the Lipschitz constant
\mathtt{L}_{x}
is bounded. We will analyze the excess risk of the flow matching estimator in Section
5
.

By combining the excess risk bound for
\hat{v}_{n}
and the
\mathcal{W}_{2}
distance bound (
4.3
), we can deduce the error bound attributable to the estimated velocity field. Generally, bounding the error due to velocity estimation involves establishing a perturbation error bound for the ODE flow associated with the velocity field
\hat{v}_{n}
or
v^{*}
, as well as an estimation error bound for the velocity field
v^{*}
.
We will use the Grönwall’s inequality to establish the perturbation error bound (
4.3
) based on the
\mathcal{W}_{2}
distance.
Similar perturbation error bounds have been obtained in
Albergo and
Vanden-Eijnden (2023, Proposition 3)
,
Benton, Deligiannidis and
Doucet (2024, Theorem 1)
, and
Gao, Huang and Jiao (2023, Proposition 54)
.
The proof of Lemma
4.2
is given in Appendix
D
.

Lemma 4.3
(Early stopping error).

Suppose that Assumptions
1
and
2
hold.
The early stopping error is evaluated by

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{2}(p_{1-\underline{t}},p_{1})\lesssim\underline{t},

where we omit a polynomial prefactor in
d
and
\mathbb{E}[\|\mathsf{X}_{1}\|_{2}^{2}]
.

The
\mathcal{W}_{2}
distance bound in Lemma
4.3
formalizes the intuition that the early stopping error scales with the early stopping parameter
\underline{t}
. The proof of Lemma
4.3
uses a coupling argument, and
is given in Appendix
D
.

4.2
Error bounds for the estimated distribution

We now apply the error bounds in the preceding subsection to derive
error bounds for the distribution estimator
\hat{\nu}_{1-\underline{t}}(\mathrm{d}x)=\hat{p}_{1-\underline{t}}(x)\mathrm{d}x
.

By Lemma
4.1
, it is clear that the discretization error can be controlled by choosing the step size
\Upsilon
properly.
Lemma
4.2
shows that the error due to
velocity estimation is upper bounded by the excess risk of the flow matching estimator
\hat{v}_{n}
.
Furthermore, we will provide a detailed nonparametric analysis of the flow matching estimator
\hat{v}_{n}
in Section
5
.

Before presenting our main result, we first describe the trade-off between the different sources of errors. By Theorem
5.12
, the excess risk of the flow matching estimator
\hat{v}_{n}
satisfies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{D}_{n}}\mathbb{E}_{(\mathsf{t},\mathsf{X}_{\mathsf{t}})}\|\hat{v}_{n}(\mathsf{t},\mathsf{X}_{\mathsf{t}})-v^{*}(\mathsf{t},\mathsf{X}_{\mathsf{t}})\|_{2}^{2}\lesssim(n\underline{t}^{2})^{-2/(d+3)}\mathrm{polylog}(n)\log(1/\underline{t}),

where
\mathrm{polylog}(n)
stands for a polylogarithmic prefactor in
n
.
Consequently, the error due to velocity estimation satisfies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{D}_{n}}\mathcal{W}_{2}(\tilde{p}_{1-\underline{t}},p_{1-\underline{t}})\lesssim(n\underline{t}^{2})^{-1/(d+3)}\mathrm{polylog}(n)\log(1/\underline{t}),

where we use Lemma
4.2
and the bound
\mathtt{L}_{x}\asymp\log(\log n)
.
According to Lemma
4.3
,
the early stopping error is upper bounded by

\mathcal{W}_{2}(p_{1-\underline{t}},p_{1})\lesssim\underline{t}
.
By substituting the error bounds into the error decomposition (
4.1
), it follows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{D}_{n}}\mathcal{W}_{2}(\hat{p}_{1-\underline{t}},p_{1})

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{W}_{2}(\hat{p}_{1-\underline{t}},\tilde{p}_{1-\underline{t}})+\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{D}_{n}}\mathcal{W}_{2}(\tilde{p}_{1-\underline{t}},p_{1-\underline{t}})+\mathcal{W}_{2}(p_{1-\underline{t}},p_{1})

\displaystyle\lesssim\Big\{\underbrace{e^{\mathtt{L}_{x}}(\mathtt{L}_{x}\mathtt{B}+\mathtt{L}_{t})\Upsilon}_{\text{controlled by }\Upsilon}+\underbrace{(n\underline{t}^{2})^{-1/(d+3)}+\underline{t}}_{\text{trade-off on }\underline{t}}\Big\}\mathrm{polylog}(n)\log(1/\underline{t})

\displaystyle\lesssim\Big\{\underline{t}^{-2}\Upsilon+(n\underline{t}^{2})^{-1/(d+3)}+\underline{t}\Big\}\mathrm{polylog}(n)\log(1/\underline{t}),

(4.4)

where we use the following bounds in deriving (
4.4
)

\displaystyle\mathtt{L}_{x}\asymp\log(\log n),\quad e^{\mathtt{L}_{x}}\asymp\log n,\quad\mathtt{B}\asymp\log(\log n),\quad\mathtt{L}_{t}\asymp\log(\log n)\underline{t}^{-2}.

Our main result stated below is obtained by balancing the error terms on the right-hand side of (
4.4
).

Theorem 4.4
(Distribution estimation error).

Suppose that Assumptions
1
and
2
hold.
Let us set
A\asymp\log(\log n)
,
NL\asymp n^{d/(2d+10)}
,
\underline{t}\asymp n^{-1/(d+5)}
, and
\Upsilon\lesssim n^{-3/(d+5)}
.
We consider the deep ReLU network class
{\mathcal{F}}_{n}=\mathcal{NN}(\mathtt{S},\mathtt{W},\mathtt{D},\mathtt{B},\mathtt{L}_{x},\mathtt{L}_{t},d+1,d)
whose parameters satisfy the following bounds

\displaystyle\mathtt{S}\asymp\underline{t}^{-2}(NL)^{2/d}(N\log N)^{2}L\log L,\quad\mathtt{W}\asymp\underline{t}^{-2}(NL)^{2/d}N\log N,

\displaystyle\mathtt{D}\asymp L\log L,\quad\mathtt{B}\asymp A,\quad\mathtt{L}_{x}\asymp A,\quad\mathtt{L}_{t}\asymp A\underline{t}^{-2}.

For any random sample
\mathbb{D}_{n}:=\{(\mathsf{Z}_{i},\mathsf{X}_{1,i},\mathsf{t}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
satisfying
n\geq\mathrm{Pdim}(\mathcal{F}_{n})
, the distribution estimation error of the CNF learned with linear interpolation and flow matching is upper bounded as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{D}_{n}}\mathcal{W}_{2}(\hat{p}_{1-\underline{t}},p_{1})=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(n^{-\frac{1}{d+5}}),

where we omit a polylogarithmic prefactor in
n
.

The proof of Theorem

is given in Appendix

.

Remark 10

5
Error analysis of flow matching

In this section, we first present some useful regularity properties of the velocity field
v^{*}
, which are essential to the convergence analysis of the flow matching estimator
\hat{v}_{n}
defined in (
3.12
). The error from the flow matching estimation constitutes a main source
of the total error for the distribution estimation given in Theorem
4.4
.

5.1
Regularity of velocity fields

The regularity properties of the velocity field are needed in studying the nonparametric estimation error of flow matching. We summarize the regularity results of the velocity field in Theorem
5.1
.

Theorem 5.1
.

Suppose that Assumptions
1
and
2
are satisfied, and let
0<\underline{t}\ll 1
.
Then the velocity field
v^{*}(t,x):[0,1]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
has the following regularity properties:

(1)

For any
s,t\in[0,1-\underline{t}]
and
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,

\|v^{*}(t,x)-v^{*}(s,x)\|_{\infty}\leq L_{t}|t-s|
with
L_{t}\lesssim\underline{t}^{-2}
;

(2)

For any
x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and
t\in[0,1]
,
\|v^{*}(t,x)-v^{*}(t,y)\|_{\infty}\leq L_{x}\|x-y\|_{\infty}
with
L_{x}\lesssim 1
;

(3)

\sup_{(t,x)\in[0,1]\times\Omega_{A}}\|v^{*}(t,x)\|_{\infty}\leq B
with
B\lesssim A
,

where we omit constants in
d,\kappa,\beta,\sigma,D,R
and denote
\Omega_{A}:=[-A,A]^{d}
.

Theorem
5.1
states that the Lipschitz regularity of the velocity field
v^{*}
holds in the time variable
t
and the space variable
x
. Moreover, the Lipschiz constant in
x
is uniformly bounded for any
(t,x)\in[0,1]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, and the Lipschitz constant in
t
is bounded for any
(t,x)\in[0,1-\underline{t}]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
but depends on
\underline{t}
. Due to the uniform Lipschitzness in
x
, the velocity field
v^{*}
further satisfies the linear growth property (3).

Remark 12
.

We note that the velocity field may be singular at time
t=1
,
since the Lipschitz constant bound of
L_{t}
explodes at time
t=1
.
We quantify the time singularity through the upper bound
L_{t}\lesssim\underline{t}^{-2}
where
0<\underline{t}\ll 1
.
Taking the time singularity into account, we set the end time
\tau=1-\underline{t}
in Subsection
4.1
.

Remark 13
.

The global Lipschitz continuity of the velocity field in
x

ensures that the associated IVP has a unique solution, according to the Cauchy-Lipschitz theorem
(
Hartman, 2002
)
.

Proof idea of Theorem
5.1
.

The proof of Theorem
5.1
can be found in Appendix
A.5
.
As shown in Lemmas
3.3
and
3.4
, the derivatives of the velocity field
v^{*}
can be expressed in terms of
the moments of
\mathsf{X}_{1}|\mathsf{X}_{t}
.
The key idea of the proof is to bound these moments.
Under Assumptions
1
and
2
,
Gao, Huang and Jiao (2023)
have
shown that the covariance matrix
\mathrm{Cov}(\mathsf{X}_{1}|\mathsf{X}_{t})
is both lower and upper bounded uniformly in
t\in[0,1]
(cf. Lemma
A.1
in Appendix
A
).
As a result, we can prove that the Lipschitz property (1) holds.
The linear growth property (3) follows from the Lipschitz property (1).
To prove the Lipschitz property (2), we derive bounds for the moments
M_{1}=\mathbb{E}[\mathsf{X}_{1}|\mathsf{X}_{t}],M_{2}=\mathbb{E}[\mathsf{X}_{1}^{\top}\mathsf{X}_{1}|\mathsf{X}_{t}]
, and
M_{3}=\mathbb{E}[\mathsf{X}_{1}\mathsf{X}_{1}^{\top}\mathsf{X}_{1}|\mathsf{X}_{t}]
by transforming the bounds of the covariance matrix
M^{c}_{2}=\mathrm{Cov}(\mathsf{X}_{1}|\mathsf{X}_{t})
.
In particular, the bound transformations can be derived based on the Hatsell-Nolte identity
(
Dytso, Poor and
Shamai Shitz, 2023
, Proposition 1)
, the Brascamp-Lieb inequality
(
Brascamp and
Lieb, 1976
)
, and a basic inequality on
M_{3}-M_{2}M_{1}
.
Moreover, we validate the sharpness of moment bounds using a Gaussian example.

5.2
Error decomposition of flow matching

The starting point of our analysis is the decomposition of the excess risk of
\hat{v}_{n}
below.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
\mathsf{t}\sim\mathrm{U}(0,1-\underline{t})
. For any random sample
\mathbb{D}_{n}:=\{(\mathsf{Z}_{i},\mathsf{X}_{1,i},\mathsf{t}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
, the excess risk of the flow matching estimator
\hat{v}_{n}
satisfies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{D}_{n}}\mathbb{E}_{(\mathsf{t},\mathsf{X}_{\mathsf{t}})}\|\hat{v}_{n}(\mathsf{t},\mathsf{X}_{\mathsf{t}})-v^{*}(\mathsf{t},\mathsf{X}_{\mathsf{t}})\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{D}_{n}}[\mathcal{L}(\hat{v}_{n})-\mathcal{L}(v^{*})]

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{stoc}}+2\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{appr}},

(5.1)

where the stochastic error
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{stoc}}:=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{D}_{n}}[\mathcal{L}(v^{*})-2\mathcal{L}_{n}(\hat{v}_{n})+\mathcal{L}(\hat{v}_{n})]
and the approximation error

\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{appr}}:=\inf_{v\in\mathcal{F}_{n}}\mathbb{E}_{(\mathsf{t},\mathsf{X}_{\mathsf{t}})}\|v(\mathsf{t},\mathsf{X}_{\mathsf{t}})-v^{*}(\mathsf{t},\mathsf{X}_{\mathsf{t}})\|_{2}^{2}
.

The proof of Lemma
5.2
is given in Appendix
C.1
.
The decomposition (
5.1
) of the excess risk can be considered a bias-variance decomposition.
The stochastic error
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{stoc}}
bounds the variance term of the flow matching estimator, and the approximation error
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{appr}}
represents the bias term of the flow matching estimator. We derive bounds for
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{stoc}}

and
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{appr}}
. Then the best bound for the excess risk under the decomposition (
5.1
) is obtained by balancing
these two error bounds.

5.3
Approximation error

We derive the error bounds for approximating Lipschitz functions using deep ReLU networks with Lipschitz regularity. The results are presented in Theorem
5.4
, which is crucial for bounding the approximation error of the flow matching estimator
\hat{v}_{n}
. To address the challenge posed by the unbounded support of the velocity field
v^{*}
in the space variable
x
, we use the standard technique of
truncated approximation
. This allows us to divide the approximation error into the truncated approximation error and the truncation error.

Lemma 5.3
.

For
\bar{v}\in\mathcal{F}_{n}
and any
A>0
, the approximation error satisfies a basic inequality as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{appr}}=\inf_{v\in\mathcal{F}_{n}}\mathbb{E}_{(\mathsf{t},\mathsf{X}_{\mathsf{t}})}\|v(\mathsf{t},\mathsf{X}_{\mathsf{t}})-v^{*}(\mathsf{t},\mathsf{X}_{\mathsf{t}})\|_{2}^{2}\lesssim\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{appr}}^{\mathrm{trunc}}+\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{trunc}},

(5.2)

where the truncated approximation error

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{appr}}^{\mathrm{trunc}}:=\mathbb{E}_{(\mathsf{t},\mathsf{X}_{\mathsf{t}})}\|[\bar{v}(\mathsf{t},\mathsf{X}_{\mathsf{t}})-v^{*}(\mathsf{t},\mathsf{X}_{\mathsf{t}})]\mathds{1}_{\Omega_{A}}(\mathsf{X}_{\mathsf{t}})\|_{2}^{2},

and the truncation error

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{trunc}}:=\mathbb{E}_{(\mathsf{t},\mathsf{X}_{\mathsf{t}})}\|[\bar{v}(\mathsf{t},\mathsf{X}_{\mathsf{t}})-v^{*}(\mathsf{t},\mathsf{X}_{\mathsf{t}})]\mathds{1}_{\Omega_{A}^{c}}(\mathsf{X}_{\mathsf{t}})\|_{2}^{2}.

Lemma
5.3
follows from the triangle inequality and the inequality
2ab\leq a^{2}+b^{2}
for any
a,b\in{\mathbb{R}}
.
We bound the truncated approximation error
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{appr}}^{\mathrm{trunc}}
by considering deep ReLU network approximation of the velocity field on the
(d+1)
-dimensional hypercube.
The truncation error
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{trunc}}
measures how fast the approximation error decays according to the tail property of the probability distribution
p_{t}
with
t\in[0,1-\underline{t}]
.

Approximation with Lipschitz regularity control.
We study the
capacity of an approximation function
\bar{v}(t,x)
implemented by a
deep ReLU network with Lipschitz regularity for approximating the velocity field
v^{*}(t,x).

For balancing the approximation error with the stochastic and discretization errors
to obtain an overall error bound for the distribution estimation error,
we construct
the approximation function
\bar{v}(t,x)
so that it satisfies the following three requirements:

(a)

Good approximation power
under the sup norm over the hypercube
\Omega_{\underline{t},A}:=[0,1-\underline{t}]\times[-A,A]^{d}
,

(b)

Lipschitz continuity with respect to both the time variable
t
and the space variable
x
,

(c)

Independent regularity in the time variable
t
and the space variable
x
.

Let us briefly comment on
each of these requirements.
Requirement (a) is needed for bounding the approximation error of the flow matching estimator
\hat{v}_{n}.
The time-space Lipschitz regularity of the approximation function
\bar{v}(t,x)

required in (b) is essential to bounding the discretization error and the error due to velocity estimation.
Requirement (c) stems from the time singularity of the velocity field at
t=1
and the different roles of the time regularity and the space regularity in the error analysis.

Theorem 5.4
.

For any
N,L\in{\mathbb{N}}
, there exists a function
\bar{v}(t,x)
implemented by a deep ReLU network with width
\mathcal{O}(\underline{t}^{-2}(NL)^{2/d}N\log N)
, depth
\mathcal{O}(L\log L)
, and size
\mathcal{O}(\underline{t}^{-2}(NL)^{2/d}(N\log N)^{2}L\log L)
such that the following properties hold simultaneously:

(i)

Boundedness and Lipschitz regularity: for any
s,t\in[0,1-\underline{t}]
and any
x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,

\displaystyle\sup_{(t,x)\in[0,1-\underline{t}]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\|\bar{v}(t,x)\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\lesssim A,

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\|\bar{v}(t,x)-\bar{v}(s,x)\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\lesssim A\underline{t}^{-2}|t-s|,

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,1-\underline{t}]}\|\bar{v}(t,x)-\bar{v}(t,y)\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\lesssim A\|x-y\|_{\infty}.

(ii)

Approximation error bound:

\displaystyle\sup_{(t,x)\in\Omega_{\underline{t},A}}\|\bar{v}(t,x)-v^{*}(t,x)\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\lesssim A(NL)^{-2/d}.

Note that we omit some prefactors in
d,\kappa,\beta,\sigma,D,R
and denote
\Omega_{\underline{t},A}:=[0,1-\underline{t}]\times[-A,A]^{d}
.

The proof of Theorem
5.4
is given in Appendix
B.3
.
Let
l\in\mathbb{N}
and
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{l}
denote a subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
We denote by
L^{p}(\Omega)
the standard Lebesgue space on
\Omega
with the Lebesgue norm
\|\cdot\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}
for
p\in[1,\infty]
.
Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
We show the definitions of the Sobolev space
W^{k,\infty}(\Omega)
, the Sobolev norm
\|\cdot\|_{W^{k,\infty}(\Omega)}
, and the Sobolev semi-norm
|\cdot|_{W^{k,\infty}(\Omega)}
in Appendix
E
.

Proof idea of Theorem
5.4
.

To fulfill Requirement (c), we use different approaches to approximation in the time variable and the space variable. Our approximation approaches can ensure the constructed approximation function to be global Lipschitz for fulfilling Requirement (b). We take four steps to construct the time-space approximation function
\bar{v}
with Lipschitz regularity control.

The first step is to derive an
L^{\infty}([0,1]^{d})
error bound of using deep ReLU networks for approximating a Lipschitz function in the space variable. We show that the constructed deep neural approximation function is globally Lipschitz in the space variable. The approximation results are presented in Appendix
B.1
, and we summarize them here.
Lemma
B.1
and Corollary
B.2
show that
for any
N,L\in\mathbb{N}
and any
f\in W^{1,\infty}((0,1)^{d})
, there exists a function
\phi
implemented by a deep ReLU network with width
\mathcal{O}(2^{d}dN\log N)
and depth
\mathcal{O}(d^{2}L\log L)
such that
|\phi|_{W^{1,\infty}((0,1)^{d})}\lesssim|f|_{W^{1,\infty}((0,1)^{d})}
and that
\|\phi-f\|_{L^{\infty}([0,1]^{d})}\lesssim(NL)^{-2/d},
omitting the prefactors depending only on
d
.

The second step is to derive an
L^{\infty}([0,1])
approximation error bound of deep ReLU networks for approximating a Lipschitz function in the time variable. We establish an
L^{\infty}([0,1])
approximation bound with Lipschitz regularity control in Appendix
B.2
.
Lemma
B.8
states the main results for approximation in time in such a way:
for any
M\in\mathbb{N}
and any
f\in W^{1,\infty}((0,1))
, there exists a function
\xi
implemented by a deep ReLU network with width
\mathcal{O}(M)
, depth
\mathcal{O}(1)
, and size
{\mathcal{O}}(M)
such that
|\xi|_{W^{1,\infty}((0,1))}\lesssim|f|_{W^{1,\infty}((0,1))}
and that
\|\xi-f\|_{L^{\infty}([0,1])}\lesssim|f|_{W^{1,\infty}((0,1))}/M
.

In the third step, we combine the constructed approximation in Lemmas
B.1
and
B.8
to establish an
L^{\infty}(\Omega_{\underline{t},A})
approximation bound for the time-space approximation of the velocity field
v^{*}
. This guarantees that Requirement (a) is fulfilled.

The last step is to show that the constructed time-space approximation satisfies the remaining Requirements (b) and (c). We summarize these discussions in Theorem
5.4
and present detailed construction and derivations in the proof of Theorem
5.4
.

Remark 14

(Optimality)
.

Under the assumption of continuous parameter selection,
DeVore, Howard and
Micchelli (1989, Theorem 4.2)
and
Yarotsky (2017, Theorem 3)
provided a lower bound
\Omega(\epsilon^{-d/k})
on the number of parameters for parametric approximations in the Sobolev space

approximation error is no more than
\epsilon
.
Our approximation rate for the time variable in the Sobolev space

matches this lower bound in the sense that a deep ReLU network with size
{\mathcal{O}}(S)
can yield an
L^{\infty}
approximation error no more than
{\mathcal{O}}(1/S)
.
Suppose that the deep ReLU network has width
{\mathcal{O}}(N)
, depth
{\mathcal{O}}(L)
, and size
{\mathcal{O}}(S)
with

(
Bartlett, Maiorov and
Meir, 1998
;
Bartlett et al., 2019
;
Lu et al., 2021
)
.
Our approximation rate for the space variable in the Sobolev space

is nearly optimal in the sense that a deep ReLU network with width
{\mathcal{O}}(N)
and depth
{\mathcal{O}}(L)
can yield an

approximation error no more than

5.5
Overall error bound for the estimated velocity field

By Lemma
5.2
, the overall error for the estimated velocity field is bounded by the sum of the approximation error
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{appr}}
and the stochastic error
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{stoc}}
.
The approximation error is analyzed in Subsection
5.3
and an upper bound is given in Corollary
5.9
.
It decreases at a fast rate as the depth and width of the deep ReLU networks grow.
The stochastic error
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{stoc}}
is analyzed in Subsection
5.4
and its upper bound is provided in Corollary
5.11
.
The stochastic error increases when the size and depth of the deep ReLU networks grow, as a result of the increasing complexity of the hypothesis class
{\mathcal{F}}_{n}
.
By balancing the bounds for
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{appr}}
and
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{stoc}}
, we obtain the best error bound
for the flow matching estimator
\hat{v}_{n}
under the error decomposition
in Lemma

.

Theorem 5.12
(Flow matching error).

Suppose that Assumptions
1
and
2
are satisfied.
Let

and

.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{D}_{n}}\mathbb{E}_{(\mathsf{t},\mathsf{X}_{\mathsf{t}})}\|\hat{v}_{n}(\mathsf{t},\mathsf{X}_{\mathsf{t}})-v^{*}(\mathsf{t},\mathsf{X}_{\mathsf{t}})\|_{2}^{2}\lesssim(n\underline{t}^{2})^{-2/(d+3)},

where we omit a polylogarithmic prefactor in
n
, a prefactor in
\log(1/\underline{t})
, and a prefactor in

and
R.

The proof of Theorem
5.12

is given in Appendix
C.4
.

Remark 15
.

In Theorem
5.12
, the polynomial prefactor in

is due to the singularity of

in the time variable

. Without the singularity at time

Related work

Process-based generative models aim to construct a stochastic process that
transports an easy-to-sample source probability distribution to the target distribution.
This goal is achieved by estimating a nonlinear transport map implemented through deep neural networks based on a random sample from the target distribution.
CNFs and diffusion models are two prominent approaches that have been developed for deep generative learning. Many researchers have considered the theoretical properties of various generative learning methods.
In this section, we discuss the connections and differences between our work and the existing studies.
We focus on the studies concerning CNFs and diffusion models that are most relevant to our work.
We also discuss the differences between the neural network approximation theory developed in this work and those in the existing literature, focusing on the regularity properties of the neural network functions. In particular, we highlight the fact that our approximation results concern velocity field functions that have different regularities in the space and time variables, while the existing results are only applicable to functions with the same regularity in all the variables.

6.1
Continuous normalizing flows

CNFs are an ODE-based generative learning approach which estimates a stochastic process for sampling from the target distribution.
Marzouk
et al. (2023)
conducted a nonparametric statistical convergence analysis for simulation-based CNF distribution estimators trained through likelihood maximization. However, this analysis does not extend to simulation-free CNFs. Probability flow ODEs
(
Song et al., 2021
)
, denoising diffusion implicit models (DDIMs)
(
Song, Meng and
Ermon, 2021
)
, and flow matching methods
(
Liu, Gong and Liu, 2023
;
Albergo and
Vanden-Eijnden, 2023
;
Lipman et al., 2023
)
all fall under the category of simulation-free CNFs.
In these models, either the score function or the velocity field is estimated. The overall error analysis needs to address both the estimation error of the velocity field (or score function) and the discretization error.

In existing literature, it is typical to assume strong regularity conditions directly on the velocity field (or score function) and its estimator. Furthermore, current studies often only consider certain sources of errors, neglecting either the discretization error or the estimation error of the velocity field (or score function). In contrast, our results are derived based on assumptions about the target distribution. Additionally, our analysis encompasses the error due to velocity estimation, the discretization error of the forward Euler solver, and the early stopping error.
These errors are included in the overall error bound. We provide a summary of the comparison between our work and relevant existing studies in Table

, with more detailed commentary provided below.

Chen, Daras and

conducted convergence analyses of DDIM-type samplers with the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence, assuming second-order smoothness in the space variable and Hölder-type regularity in the time variable for the score function, while ignoring the score estimation error.
Albergo, Boffi and

also derived a new perturbation error bound on the CNF distribution estimator using the KL divergence.

Chen et al. (2023b)
provided polynomial-time convergence guarantees for distribution estimation using the probability flow ODE trained with denoising score matching and simulated with additional randomness. To derive these convergence rates,

Benton, Deligiannidis and

Cui et al. (2023)
conducted convergence analyses of the Gaussian mixture distribution estimator in the asymptotic limit

Cheng et al. (2023)
presented a theoretical analysis of the distribution estimator defined by Jordan-Kinderleherer-Otto (JKO) flow models, which implements the JKO scheme in a normalizing flow network.
Gao and Zhu (2024)
assumed a small
L^{2}

Finally,
Chang et al. (2024)
considered a conditional generative learning model, in which the predictor
\mathsf{X}
and the response
\mathsf{Y}
are both random variables with bounded support. They provided an error analysis for learning the conditional distribution of
\mathsf{Y}|\mathsf{X}
via the Föllmer flow.

In this study, we derive non-asymptotic error bounds for the estimated velocity fields and discretization error bounds for the forward Euler sampler. These error bounds are incorporated into the end-to-end convergence analysis of the CNF distribution estimator with flow matching. Furthermore, we only stipulate general assumptions on the target distribution, rather than making assumptions on the velocity field (or score function) and its estimator. We believe that these theoretical contributions set our work apart from previous studies.

6.2
Diffusion models

Diffusion models
(
Sohl-Dickstein et al., 2015
;
Song and Ermon, 2019
;
Ho, Jain and Abbeel, 2020
;
Song et al., 2021
;
Song, Meng and
Ermon, 2021
)

have emerged as a powerful SDE-based framework for deep generative learning. The diffusion model estimators share a deep connection with the CNF distribution estimators due to the correspondence between an Itô SDE and its probability flow ODE. There has been a growing interest in the statistical analyses of diffusion model estimators, as evidenced by the works of
Lee, Lu and Tan (2022)
;
Bortoli (2022)
;
Chen et al. (2023a)
;
Lee, Lu and Tan (2023)
;
Chen, Lee and Lu (2023)
;
Oko, Akiyama and
Suzuki (2023)
;
Li et al. (2024)
, among others.

Unlike the deterministic sampler of CNF distribution estimators, diffusion model estimators employ a stochastic sampler (such as the Euler-Maruyama method) to simulate the time-reversed Itô SDEs. This stochasticity plays a crucial role in the discretization error analysis of diffusion model estimators and leads to the development of useful techniques such as Girsanov’s theorem
(
Chen et al., 2023a
)
, a chain rule-based variant
(
Chen, Lee and Lu, 2023
)
of the interpolation technique
(
Vempala and Wibisono, 2019
)
, and the stochastic interpolation formula
(
Bortoli, 2022
)
. However, it remains uncertain whether these techniques can be generalized for analyzing the CNF distribution estimators.

Compared to the CNF distribution estimators, the diffusion model estimators have been extensively investigated from a statistical perspective. For instance, the estimation error bounds of the score function have been established by
Oko, Akiyama and
Suzuki (2023)
;
Chen et al. (2023c)
;
Huang et al. (2023)
;
Cole and Lu (2024)
.
There is also a vast body of literature on analyzing the discretization error of diffusion model estimators, including works by
Wibisono and
Yang (2022)
;
Benton et al. (2024)
;
Pedrotti, Maas and
Mondelli (2023)
;
Gao, Nguyen and
Zhu (2023)
;
Bruno et al. (2023)
;
Shah, Chen and
Klivans (2023)
and others.
However, the absence of stochasticity presents significant challenges when attempting to analyze the ODE-based CNF distribution estimators using techniques developed for diffusion model estimators.

6.3
Neural network approximation with Lipschitz regularity control

The approximation theory of deep ReLU networks has developed rapidly since the seminal work of
Yarotsky (2017)
. Previous studies have shown that deep ReLU networks can efficiently approximate functions in a smooth function class, such as the Hölder class, the Sobolev class, and the Besov class, under the
L^{\infty}
norm
(
Yarotsky, 2017
;
Petersen and
Voigtlaender, 2018
;
Suzuki, 2019
;
Yarotsky, 2018
;
Gühring, Kutyniok and
Petersen, 2020
;
DeVore, Hanin and
Petrova, 2021
;
Daubechies
et al., 2022
;
Lu et al., 2021
;
Jiao et al., 2023
;
Siegel, 2023
)
.
Recent works have also considered nonparametric or semiparametric estimation using deep ReLU networks, including least squares regression
(
Bauer and Kohler, 2019
;
Schmidt-Hieber, 2020
;
Nakada and
Imaizumi, 2020
;
Kohler and Langer, 2021
;
Suzuki and Nitanda, 2021
;
Chen et al., 2022
;
Jiao et al., 2023
)
, quantile regression
(
Shen et al., 2022
;
Padilla, Tansey and
Chen, 2022
)

semiparametric inference
(
Farrell, Liang and
Misra, 2021
)
, factor augmented sparse throughput models
(
Fan and Gu, 2023
)
, among others. In the convergence analysis of these models, it is sufficient to know the error bounds of using deep neural networks for approximating smooth functions.

Analyzing deep generative distribution estimators becomes more challenging as it requires not only approximation error bounds but also additional regularity properties of the constructed neural network approximation functions. For instance, the error analysis of Wasserstein GANs necessitates an upper bound of the Lipschitz constant of the discriminator network
(
Chen et al., 2020
;
Huang et al., 2022
)
.

Chen et al. (2020)
demonstrated that the wide and shallow ReLU network constructed by
Yarotsky (2017)
, for which the depth grows logarithmically but the width grows polynomially, can approximate 1-Lipschitz functions with a uniformly bounded Lipschitz constant.

Huang et al. (2022)
provided a Lipschitz constant bound for the deep ReLU network approximation function proposed by
Lu et al. (2021)
. However, this bound increases with the width and depth of the network.
Furthermore,
Jiao, Wang and
Yang (2023)
succeeded in controlling the Lipschitz constant of deep ReLU networks by enforcing a norm constraint on the neural network weights, and applied the approximation bound to analyze the distribution estimation error of GANs. In addition to the error analyses of GANs, the convergence analysis of simulation-based CNFs by
Marzouk
et al. (2023)
, also requires a Lipschitz regularity control of the constructed approximation function to ensure the CNFs are well-posed.

In the current context, the Lipschitz regularity of the neural network approximation functions is crucial for analyzing the behavior of the estimated velocity field. Indeed, a key step in our error analysis involves constructing deep ReLU networks to approximate the Lipschitz velocity field
v^{*}(t,x)
for
(t,x)\in[0,1-\underline{t}]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}.
To achieve this target, we need to derive an
L^{\infty}
bound of the approximation error and demonstrate that the Lipschitz constant of the constructed deep ReLU network is uniformly bounded, regardless of the varying width and depth of the neural network. Establishing the Lipschitz regularity of the neural network approximation functions, in addition to the approximation error bounds, is a more challenging task that requires different techniques. Specifically, our uniform bounds of the Lipschitz constants are sharper than those obtained by
Huang et al. (2022)
for varying width and depth of the deep ReLU network. Compared to the approximation bound of

Chen et al. (2020)
, our approximation bound is valid for any network width and depth specified by the parameters
N
and
L.

Marzouk
et al. (2023)
considered the Lipschitz regularity of deep neural networks activated by the smooth function ReLU
k
with
k\geq 2
, which is based on spline approximation and technically differs from our work.

7
Conclusion and discussion

We have established non-asymptotic error bounds for the CNF distribution estimator trained via flow matching, using the Wasserstein-
2
distance. Assuming that the target distribution belongs to several rich classes of probability distributions, we have established Lipschitz regularity properties of the velocity field for simulation-free CNFs defined with linear interpolation. To meet the regularity requirements of flow matching estimators, we have developed
L^{\infty}
approximation bounds of deep ReLU networks for Lipschitz functions, along with Lipschitz regularity control of the constructed deep ReLU networks. By integrating the regularity results, the deep approximation bounds, and perturbation analyses of ODE flows, we have shown that the convergence rate of the CNF distribution estimator is
\widetilde{{\mathcal{O}}}(n^{-1/(d+5)}),
up to a polylogarithmic prefactor of
n
.
Our error analysis framework can be extended to study more general CNFs based on interpolation, beyond the CNFs constructed with linear interpolation.

There are several questions deserving further investigation. Firstly, it would be interesting to consider target distributions with general smoothness properties and investigate the resulting smoothness properties of the corresponding velocity fields. Secondly, the inevitability of the time singularity of the velocity field remains unclear and warrants further analysis, as we have not provided a lower bound on the Lipschitz constant in the time variable. This is a challenging problem that requires more effort and careful analyses. Lastly, it would be interesting to derive general non-asymptotic error bounds and convergence rates for CNF distribution estimators under general smoothness conditions.
For this purpose, we need to combine general smoothness properties of velocity fields with the deep neural network approximation theory.

\displaystyle\lesssim\|x\|_{2}\vee 1,

where a polynomial prefactor in

It further yields that

when omitting a polynomial prefactor in

Lastly, we bound

. For any

\displaystyle~~~~~~\|M_{3}-M_{2}M_{1}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{d}\left(\mathbb{E}[\mathsf{X}_{1,i}\mathsf{X}_{1}^{\top}\mathsf{X}_{1}|\mathsf{X}_{t}=x]-\mathbb{E}[\mathsf{X}_{1}^{\top}\mathsf{X}_{1}|\mathsf{X}_{t}=x]\mathbb{E}[\mathsf{X}_{1,i}|\mathsf{X}_{t}=x]\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{d}\left(\mathrm{Cov}(\mathsf{X}_{1}^{\top}\mathsf{X}_{1},\mathsf{X}_{1,i}|\mathsf{X}_{t}=x)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{d}\mathrm{Var}(\mathsf{X}_{1}^{\top}\mathsf{X}_{1}|\mathsf{X}_{t}=x)\mathrm{Var}(\mathsf{X}_{1,i}|\mathsf{X}_{t}=x)

(By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Var}(\mathsf{X}_{1}^{\top}\mathsf{X}_{1}|\mathsf{X}_{t}=x)\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{d}\mathrm{Var}(\mathsf{X}_{1,i}|\mathsf{X}_{t}=x)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Var}(\mathsf{X}_{1}^{\top}\mathsf{X}_{1}|\mathsf{X}_{t}=x)\Tr(M^{c}_{2})

\displaystyle\leq d\ \mathrm{Var}(\mathsf{X}_{1}^{\top}\mathsf{X}_{1}|\mathsf{X}_{t}=x)\|M^{c}_{2}\|_{2,2}.

Let

\kappa

where we omit a polynomial prefactor in
d,\kappa,\eta
.
This completes the proof.

B
Approximation error of the velocity field

In this section, we analyze the approximation error of the velocity field by a constructive approach.

Before proceeding, we present a few useful notations for the Sobolev function class.
A
d

d

\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\cdots,\alpha_{d})^{\top}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}

We define

We also use

B.1
Approximation in space with Lipschitz regularity

In this subsection, we study the approximation capacity of deep ReLU networks joint with an estimate of the Lipschitz regularity.
The strong expressive power of deep ReLU networks has been studied with the localized or averaged Taylor polynomials.
We follow the localized approximation approach, and establish the global Lipschitz continuity and non-asymptotic approximation estimate of deep ReLU networks.

Lemma B.1
.

Given any
f\in W^{1,\infty}((0,1)^{d})
with
\|f\|_{W^{1,\infty}((0,1)^{d})}\leq 1
, for any
N,L\in\mathbb{N}

\phi

\mathcal{O}(2^{d}dN\log N)
and depth
\mathcal{O}(d^{2}L\log L)
such that
\|\phi\|_{W^{1,\infty}((0,1)^{d})}\lesssim 1

\displaystyle\|\phi-f\|_{L^{\infty}([0,1]^{d})}\lesssim(NL)^{-2/d},

where we omit some prefactors depending only on
d
.

Corollary B.2
.

Given any
f\in W^{1,\infty}((0,1)^{d})
with
\|f\|_{W^{1,\infty}((0,1)^{d})}<\infty
, for any
N,L\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists a function
\phi
implemented by a deep ReLU network with width
\mathcal{O}(2^{d}dN\log N)
and depth
\mathcal{O}(d^{2}L\log L)

\displaystyle\|\phi-f\|_{L^{\infty}([0,1]^{d})}\lesssim\|f\|_{W^{1,\infty}((0,1)^{d})}(NL)^{-2/d},

d
.

Lemma B.6
.

Let
K\in\mathbb{N}
. For any
f\in W^{1,\infty}((0,1)^{d})
with
\|f\|_{W^{1,\infty}((0,1)^{d})}\leq 1
and
m\in\{1,2\}^{d}
, there exists a piecewise constant function
f_{K,m}
on
\Omega_{m}
satisfying

\displaystyle\|f_{K,m}-f\|_{W^{1,\infty}(\Omega_{m})}\lesssim 1,\quad\|f_{K,m}-f\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega_{m})}\lesssim 1/K

with prefactors in
d
omitted.

We leverage approximation properties of averaged Taylor polynomials
(
Brenner and
Scott, 2008
, Definition 4.1.3)
and the Bramble-Hilbert Lemma
(
Brenner and
Scott, 2008
, Lemma 4.3.8)
to deduce local estimates and then combine them through a partition of unity to obtain a global estimate.
The key observation is that the
L^{\infty}
approximation bound can be established while uniformly controlling the Lipschitz constant of the piecewise constant function with a mild regularity assumption on the target function such as
f\in W^{1,\infty}((0,1)^{d})
.

Without loss of generality, let us assume
m=m_{*}:=[1,1,\cdots,1]^{\top}
.
Following the proofs of
Gühring, Kutyniok and
Petersen (2020, Lemma C.4)
and
Yang, Yang and Xiang (2023, Theorem 6)
, we first define an extension operator
E:W^{1,\infty}((0,1)^{d})\to W^{1,\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
to handle the boundary.
Accordingly, let
\tilde{f}:=Ef
and
C_{E}
be the norm of the extension operator.
Then for any
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, it holds that

\displaystyle|\tilde{f}|_{W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)}\leq\|\tilde{f}\|_{W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)}\leq C_{E}\|f\|_{W^{1,\infty}((0,1)^{d})}\leq C_{E}.

Next, we define an average Taylor polynomial of order
1
over
B_{i,K}:=\mathbb{B}^{d}(\frac{8i+3}{8K},\frac{1}{4K},\|\cdot\|_{2})
by

\displaystyle p_{f,i}(x):=\int_{B_{i,K}}T_{y}^{1}\tilde{f}(x)\phi_{K}(y)\mathrm{d}y

where
\phi_{K}
is a cut-off function supported on
\bar{B}_{i,K}
as given in Example
1
.
By Definition
E.4
,
T_{y}^{1}\tilde{f}(x)=\tilde{f}(y)
.
Then, it implies that
p_{f,i}(x)
is a constant function as

\displaystyle p_{f,i}(x)=\int_{B_{i,K}}\tilde{f}(y)\phi_{K}(y)\mathrm{d}y.

Step 1. Get local estimates.
For any
i=[i_{1},i_{2},\cdots,i_{d}]^{\top}\in\{0,1,\cdots,K\}^{d}
, we would like to employ the Bramble-Hilbert lemma
E.8
on the subset

\displaystyle\Omega_{m_{*},i}=\bar{\mathbb{B}}^{d}\left(\frac{8i+3}{8K},\frac{3}{8K},\|\cdot\|_{\infty}\right)=\prod_{j=1}^{d}\left[\frac{i_{j}}{K},\frac{3+4i_{j}}{4K}\right].

It is easy to check the conditions of the Bramble-Hilbert lemma are fulfilled as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4K}\geq\frac{1}{2}\times\frac{3}{8K}=\frac{1}{2}r_{\max}(\Omega_{m_{*},i}),\quad\gamma(\Omega_{m_{*},i})=\frac{d_{\Omega_{m_{*},i}}}{r_{\max}(\Omega_{m_{*},i})}=2\sqrt{d}.

Hence, by the Bramble-Hilbert Lemma
E.8
, it yields that

\displaystyle\|\tilde{f}-p_{f,i}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega_{m_{*},i})}\leq C_{1}(d)|\tilde{f}|_{W^{1,\infty}(\Omega_{m_{*},i})}/K,

\displaystyle|\tilde{f}-p_{f,i}|_{W^{1,\infty}(\Omega_{m_{*},i})}\leq C_{1}(d)|\tilde{f}|_{W^{1,\infty}(\Omega_{m_{*},i})}.

Combining
|\tilde{f}|_{W^{1,\infty}(\Omega_{m_{*},i})}\leq C_{E}
and the inequalities above, it implies that

\displaystyle\|\tilde{f}-p_{f,i}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega_{m_{*},i})}\leq C_{1}(d)C_{E}/K,

(B.1)

\displaystyle\|\tilde{f}-p_{f,i}\|_{W^{1,\infty}(\Omega_{m_{*},i})}\leq C_{1}(d)C_{E}.

(B.2)

Step 2. Define a partition of unity. We construct a partition of unity in order to combine the local estimates.
Let
K\in\mathbb{N}
. For any
0\leq i\leq K
, we define
h_{i}:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
by

\displaystyle h_{i}(x)=h\left(4K\left(x-\frac{8i+3}{8K}\right)\right)\text{ where }h(x)=\begin{cases}1,&|x|<3/2,\\
0,&|x|>2,\\
4-2|x|,&3/2\leq|x|\leq 2.\end{cases}

One can verify that
\{h_{i}\}_{i=1}^{K}
is a partition of unity of
[0,1]
and
h_{i}(x)=1
for any
x\in\left[\frac{i}{K},\frac{3+4i}{4K}\right]
.
Considering the multidimensional case, for any
x=[x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{d}]^{\top}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and any
i=[i_{1},i_{2},\cdots,i_{d}]^{\top}\in\{0,1,\cdots,K\}^{d}
, let us define

\displaystyle h_{i}(x):=\prod\nolimits_{j=1}^{d}h_{i_{j}}(x_{j}).

Then a partition of unity of
[0,1]^{d}
is defined by
\{h_{i}:i\in\{0,1,\cdots,K\}^{d}\}
.
Moreover,
h_{i}(x)=1
for any
x\in\Omega_{m_{*},i}=\prod_{j=1}^{d}\left[\frac{i_{j}}{K},\frac{3+4i_{j}}{4K}\right]
and
i=[i_{1},i_{2},\cdots,i_{d}]^{\top}\in\{0,1,\cdots,K\}^{d}
.
By the definition of
h_{i}(x)
on
\Omega_{m_{*},i}
and (
B.1
), (
B.2
) , it yields that

\displaystyle\|h_{i}(\tilde{f}-p_{f,i})\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega_{m_{*},i})}\leq\|\tilde{f}-p_{f,i}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega_{m_{*},i})}\leq C_{1}(d)C_{E}/K,

\displaystyle\|h_{i}(\tilde{f}-p_{f,i})\|_{W^{1,\infty}(\Omega_{m_{*},i})}\leq\|\tilde{f}-p_{f,i}\|_{W^{1,\infty}(\Omega_{m_{*},i})}\leq C_{1}(d)C_{E}.

Step 3. Get global estimates.
To deduce the global estimates, we start with defining
f_{K,m_{*}}
over
\Omega_{m_{*}}
by

\displaystyle f_{K,m_{*}}:=\sum\nolimits_{i\in\{0,1,\cdots,K\}^{d}}h_{i}p_{f,i}.

The error bounds follow that

\displaystyle\|f_{K,m_{*}}-f\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega_{m_{*}})}\leq\max_{i\in\{0,1,\cdots,K\}^{d}}\|h_{i}(\tilde{f}-p_{f,i})\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega_{m_{*},i})}\leq C_{1}(d)C_{E}/K,

\displaystyle\|f_{K,m_{*}}-f\|_{W^{1,\infty}(\Omega_{m_{*}})}\leq\max_{i\in\{0,1,\cdots,K\}^{d}}\|h_{i}(\tilde{f}-p_{f,i})\|_{W^{1,\infty}(\Omega_{m_{*},i})}\leq C_{1}(d)C_{E}.

This completes the proof.
∎

Lemma B.7
.

Without loss of generality, we consider

.

Lastly, we calculate the width and depth of the deep ReLU network to implement

∎

\mathcal{O}(N)
and depth
\mathcal{O}(L)
;

•

\phi_{m}
with width
\mathcal{O}(dN)
and depth
\mathcal{O}(d^{2}L)
;

•

\psi_{m}
with width
{\mathcal{O}}(N\log N)
and depth
{\mathcal{O}}(L\log L)
.

Hence, the deep ReLU network implementing the function
\phi
has width
{\mathcal{O}}(2^{d}dN\log N)
and depth
{\mathcal{O}}(d^{2}L\log L)
.
∎

The proof is completed by employing Lemma
B.1
on
\bar{f}:=f/\|f\|_{W^{1,\infty}((0,1)^{d})}
.
∎

B.2
Approximation in time with Lipschitz regularity

To handle the singularity of the velocity field in time, we develop a new approximation result to approximate the velocity field in time.

Lemma B.8
.

Given any
f\in W^{1,\infty}((0,1))
with
\|f\|_{W^{1,\infty}((0,1))}\leq\infty
, for any
M\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists a function
\xi
implemented by a deep ReLU network with width
\mathcal{O}(M)
and depth
\mathcal{O}(1)
such that
|\xi|_{W^{1,\infty}((0,1))}\lesssim|f|_{W^{1,\infty}((0,1))}
and

\displaystyle\|\xi-f\|_{L^{\infty}([0,1])}\lesssim|f|_{W^{1,\infty}((0,1))}/M.

The proof consists of two steps. We start with the construction of a continuous piecewise linear function for approximating
1
-Lipschitz functions, which shall be implemented by a deep ReLU network. After that, we establish the global Lipschitz continuity of the constructed deep ReLU network, in addition to the approximation bounds in the
L^{\infty}([0,1])
norm.

Step 1. We construct a partition of unity following
Yarotsky (2017, Proof of Theorem 1)
.
Let
M\in\mathbb{N}
and
m\in\{0,1,\cdots,M\}
.
We collect a set of functions
\{\phi_{m}\}_{m=0}^{M}
that are defined as follows: for any
t\in[0,1]
, let

\displaystyle\phi_{m}(t):=\psi\left(3M\left(t-\frac{m}{M}\right)\right)\quad\text{with}\quad\psi(z)=\begin{cases}1,&|z|<1,\\
0,&|z|>2,\\
2-|z|,&1\leq|z|\leq 2,\end{cases}

(B.4)

that satisfies
\sum_{m=0}^{M}\phi_{m}(t)=1
.
It implies that
\{\phi_{m}\}_{m=0}^{M}
forms a partition of unity on the domain
[0,1]
.
We plot
\phi_{m}
and
\phi_{m+1}
in Figure
2
.
As in
Chen et al. (2020, Proof of Lemma 10)
, for each
m\in\{0,1,\cdots,M\}
, we consider a piecewise constant function
f_{m}:=f(m/M)
.
Actually, the piecewise constant approximation is specially the zero-degree Taylor polynomial for the function
f
at
x=m/M
in
Yarotsky (2017, Proof of Theorem 1)
.
We claim that

\displaystyle\tilde{f}(t):=\sum_{m=0}^{M}\phi_{m}(t)f_{m}

(B.5)

provides an approximation of
f
, and the approximation error is evaluated by

\displaystyle\|\tilde{f}-f\|_{L^{\infty}([0,1])}

\displaystyle=\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\Big|\sum\nolimits_{m=0}^{M}\phi_{m}(t)[f_{m}-f(t)]\Big|

\displaystyle=\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\Big|\sum\nolimits_{|t-\frac{m}{M}|\leq\frac{2}{3M}}\phi_{m}(t)[f(m/M)-f(t)]\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{3M}|f|_{W^{1,\infty}((0,1))},

where the Lipschitz continuity of
f
is used in the inequality.
It is clear that
\tilde{f}
can be implemented with a deep ReLU network.

Step 2. We establish the global Lipschitz continuity of
\tilde{f}
. Notice that for any
t,s\in[0,1]
,

\displaystyle|\tilde{f}(t)-\tilde{f}(s)|

\displaystyle\leq|\tilde{f}(t)-f(t)|+|f(t)-f(s)|+|f(s)-\tilde{f}(s)|

\displaystyle\leq 2\|\tilde{f}-f\|_{L^{\infty}([0,1])}+|f|_{W^{1,\infty}((0,1))}|t-s|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4}{3M}|f|_{W^{1,\infty}((0,1))}+|f|_{W^{1,\infty}((0,1))}|t-s|.

(1) If
|t-s|\geq\frac{1}{3M}
, it is clear that

|\tilde{f}(t)-\tilde{f}(s)|\leq 5|f|_{W^{1,\infty}((0,1))}|t-s|
.

(2) If
|t-s|<\frac{1}{3M}
, we try to directly bound the difference

\displaystyle|\tilde{f}(t)-\tilde{f}(s)|

\displaystyle=\Big|\sum\nolimits_{m=0}^{M}[\phi_{m}(t)-\phi_{m}(s)]f_{m}\Big|

\displaystyle=\Big|\sum\nolimits_{m=0}^{M}\left[\psi\left(3Mt-3m\right)-\psi\left(3Ms-3m\right)\right]f_{m}\Big|=:\mathcal{E}.

Next, we focus on bounding
\mathcal{E}
.
Without loss of generality, we assume
s>t
. Considering
|t-s|<\frac{1}{3M}
, we deduce that
s\in(t,t+\frac{1}{3M})
.
From Figure
2
, we can observe that there exist at most two numbers
m=\tilde{m}\in\{0,1,\cdots,M\}
or
m=\bar{m}:=\tilde{m}+1
such that
\psi(3Mt-3m)\not\equiv 0
or
\psi(3Ms-3m)\not\equiv 0
.
It follows that

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}

\displaystyle=\left|\left[\psi\left(3Mt-3\tilde{m}\right)-\psi\left(3Ms-3\tilde{m}\right)\right]f_{\tilde{m}}\right.

\displaystyle~~~~+\left.\left[\psi\left(3Mt-3\bar{m}\right)-\psi\left(3Ms-3\bar{m}\right)\right]f_{\bar{m}}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\left[\psi\left(3Mt-3\tilde{m}\right)-\psi\left(3Ms-3\tilde{m}\right)\right]f_{\tilde{m}}\right.

\displaystyle~~~~+\left.\left[(1-\psi\left(3Mt-3\tilde{m}\right))-(1-\psi\left(3Ms-3\tilde{m}\right))\right]f_{\bar{m}}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\left[\psi\left(3Mt-3\tilde{m}\right)-\psi\left(3Ms-3\tilde{m}\right)\right](f_{\tilde{m}}-f_{\bar{m}})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|f_{\tilde{m}}-f_{\bar{m}}\right|\cdot\left|\psi\left(3Mt-3\tilde{m}\right)-\psi\left(3Ms-3\tilde{m}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{M}|f|_{W^{1,\infty}((0,1))}\left|\psi\left(3Mt-3\tilde{m}\right)-\psi\left(3Ms-3\tilde{m}\right)\right|

\displaystyle=3|f|_{W^{1,\infty}((0,1))}|t-s|.

Hence, if
|t-s|<\frac{1}{3M}
, it holds that

|\tilde{f}(t)-\tilde{f}(s)|\leq 3|f|_{W^{1,\infty}((0,1))}|t-s|
.

To sum up, for any
t,s\in[0,1]
, it holds that
|\tilde{f}(t)-\tilde{f}(s)|\leq 5|f|_{W^{1,\infty}((0,1))}|t-s|
. It is easy to see from (
B.5
) that the deep ReLU network implementing
\tilde{f}
has width
\mathcal{O}(M)
and depth
\mathcal{O}(1)
. Then we complete the proof.
∎

B.3
Time-space approximation

In the subsection, we construct a time-space approximation while keeping the Lipschitz regularity both in the space variable and in the time variable.

Fig 3:
The clipping function
\beta_{A}
.

Lemma B.9
(Clipping functions).

Given
A>0
, we define
\beta_{A}:{\mathbb{R}}\to[-A,A]
by

\displaystyle\beta_{A}(z):=\begin{cases}-A,&z\in(-\infty,-A),\\
z,&z\in[-A,A],\\
A,&z\in(A,\infty).\end{cases}

There exists a clipping function
\mathcal{C}_{A}:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to[-A,A]^{d}
at level
A
implemented by a deep ReLU network with width
\mathcal{O}(d)
and depth
\mathcal{O}(1)
such that for any
x=[x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{d}]^{\top}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{A}(x)=[\beta_{A}(x_{1}),\beta_{A}(x_{2}),\cdots,\beta_{A}(x_{d})]^{\top}.

It is clear that
\mathcal{C}_{A}(x)=\varrho(x+A\mathbf{1}_{d})-\varrho(x-A\mathbf{1}_{d})-A\mathbf{1}_{d}
where
\varrho:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is the ReLU function. This expression implies that the clipping function
\mathcal{C}_{A}
can be implemented by a deep ReLU network with width
\mathcal{O}(d)
and depth
\mathcal{O}(1)
.
∎

The main idea of the time-space approximation on
[0,1-\underline{t}]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is based on Lemmas
B.1
,
B.8
, and
B.9
.

We derive a time-space approximation
\bar{v}
of the velocity field
v^{*}
on the domain
\Omega_{\underline{t},A}=[0,1-\underline{t}]\times[-A,A]^{d}
and bound the Lipschitz constants of
\bar{v}
on the domain
[0,1-\underline{t}]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.

First of all, we use the clipping function
\mathcal{C}
defined in Lemma
B.9
to clip the support of the space variable, that is, for each
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, we have
\mathcal{C}(x)\in[-A,A]^{d}
. We only need to consider approximation in
x
on the domain
[-A,A]^{d}
.

Then we can employ the mappings

\tilde{t}=\mathcal{T}_{1}(t):=t/(1-\underline{t})
and

\tilde{x}=\mathcal{T}_{2}(x):=(x+A\mathbf{1}_{d})/(2A)
to transform the domain
\Omega_{\underline{t},A}
into the domain
[0,1]^{d+1}
. When the domain
[0,1-\underline{t}]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is considered, the transformed domain is
[0,1]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.
Notice that both mappings are invertible and can be implemented by deep ReLU networks.
We denote their inverse functions as

t=\mathcal{T}_{1}^{-1}(\tilde{t})
and
x=\mathcal{T}_{2}^{-1}(\tilde{x})
.
We further define a new velocity field
v^{\diamond}
by

v^{\diamond}(\tilde{t},\tilde{x}):=v^{*}(\mathcal{T}_{1}^{-1}(\tilde{t}),\mathcal{T}_{2}^{-1}(\tilde{x}))
for any
(\tilde{t},\tilde{x})\in[0,1]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.
It is clear that
v^{*}(t,x)=v^{\diamond}(\mathcal{T}_{1}(t),\mathcal{T}_{2}(x))
for any
(t,x)\in[0,1-\underline{t}]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.
According to Theorem
5.1
, the new velocity field
v^{\diamond}
satisfies

(1)

For any
\tilde{s},\tilde{t}\in[0,1]
and
\tilde{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,
\|v^{\diamond}(\tilde{t},\tilde{x})-v^{\diamond}(\tilde{s},\tilde{x})\|_{\infty}\lesssim\underline{t}^{-2}|\tilde{t}-\tilde{s}|
;

(2)

For any
\tilde{x},\tilde{y}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and
\tilde{t}\in[0,1]
,
\|v^{\diamond}(\tilde{t},\tilde{x})-v^{\diamond}(\tilde{t},\tilde{y})\|_{\infty}\lesssim A\|\tilde{x}-\tilde{y}\|_{\infty}
;

(3)

\|v^{\diamond}\|_{L^{\infty}([0,1]^{d+1})}\lesssim A
,

where we omit constants in
d,\kappa,\beta,\sigma,D,R
.

In the following, we construct a time-space approximation of the new velocity field
v^{\diamond}
on the transformed domain
[0,1]^{d+1}
using deep ReLU networks.
Let
v^{\diamond}=[v^{\diamond}_{1},v^{\diamond}_{2},\cdots,v^{\diamond}_{d}]^{\top}
.
Given
M\in\mathbb{N}
, we uniformly partition the unit interval
[0,1]
into
M
non-overlapping sub-intervals with length
1/M
.
Let
\{\phi_{j}(\tilde{t})\}_{j=0}^{M}
form a partition of unity on
[0,1]
with the same definition as (
B.4
) in the proof of Lemma
B.8
.
For each
i\in\{1,2,\cdots,d\}
, we define a time approximation of
v^{\diamond}_{i}
by

\displaystyle\tilde{v}_{i}(\tilde{t},\tilde{x}):=\sum\nolimits_{j=0}^{M}v^{\diamond}_{i}\left(j/M,\tilde{x}\right)\phi_{j}\left(\tilde{t}\right).

Let
\tilde{v}:=[\tilde{v}_{1},\tilde{v}_{2},\cdots,\tilde{v}_{d}]^{\top}
.
Due to Lemma
B.8
, for any
\tilde{x}\in[0,1]^{d}
, it holds that

\displaystyle|\tilde{v}(\cdot,\tilde{x})|_{W^{1,\infty}((0,1);{\mathbb{R}}^{d})}\lesssim|v^{\diamond}(\cdot,\tilde{x})|_{W^{1,\infty}((0,1);{\mathbb{R}}^{d})}\lesssim\underline{t}^{-2}.

For
i=1,2,\cdots,d
and
j=0,1,\cdots,M
, let
\zeta_{ij}(\tilde{x})
be a space approximation of
v^{\diamond}_{i}\left(j/M,\tilde{x}\right)
implemented by a deep ReLU network constructed in Lemma
B.1
.
Then it holds that
\max_{i,j}\|\zeta_{ij}\|_{W^{1,\infty}((0,1)^{d})}\lesssim A
.
By Lemma
F.4
, we can construct a deep ReLU network
\phi_{\times,(B_{3},B_{4})}
with width
15(N+1)
and depth
8L
to approximate the product function such that

\|\phi_{\times,(B_{3},B_{4})}\|_{W^{1,\infty}((-B_{3},B_{3})\times(-B_{4},B_{4}))}\leq 12B_{3}B_{4}
,

\displaystyle\|\phi_{\times,(B_{3},B_{4})}(x,y)-xy\|_{W^{1,\infty}((-B_{3},B_{3})\times(-B_{4},B_{4}))}\leq 6B_{3}B_{4}(N+1)^{-4L},

(B.6)

and

\displaystyle\phi_{\times,(B_{3},B_{4})}(x,0)=\frac{\partial\phi_{\times,(B_{3},B_{4})}(x,0)}{\partial x}=0\text{ for $x\in(-B_{3},B_{3})$}.

(B.7)

Using the same partition of unity
\{\phi_{j}(\tilde{t})\}_{j=0}^{M}
on
[0,1]
, we define a time-space approximation of
v^{\diamond}_{i}
for each
i\in\{1,2,\cdots,d\}
by

\displaystyle v^{\natural}_{i}(\tilde{t},\tilde{x}):=\sum\nolimits_{j=0}^{M}\phi_{\times,(B_{3},B_{4})}\left(\zeta_{ij}(\tilde{x}),\phi_{j}\left(\tilde{t}\right)\right),

(B.8)

which can be implemented with a deep ReLU network.
We choose the parameters
B_{3},B_{4}
such that
B_{3}\asymp\max_{i,j}\|\zeta_{ij}\|_{L^{\infty}([0,1]^{d})}\lesssim A
and
B_{4}\asymp\max_{j}\|\phi_{j}\|_{L^{\infty}([0,1])}\lesssim 1
.

Claim B.10
.

There are at most two nonzero terms in the summation (
B.8
) defining the time-space approximation function
v^{\natural}_{i}
.

This claim holds because for any
\tilde{t}\in[0,1]
, there are at most two indexes
j
’s from
\{0,1,2,\cdots,M\}
such that
\phi_{j}(\tilde{t})
is nonzero according to the definition of the partition of unity
\{\phi_{j}(\tilde{t})\}_{j=0}^{M}
.
Then our claim follows from the property (
B.7
) of the approximation product function
\phi_{\times,(B_{3},B_{4})}
.

Before we study the properties of
v^{\natural}_{i}
, we introduce a surrogate function
\check{v}_{i}
defined by

\displaystyle\check{v}_{i}(\tilde{t},\tilde{x}):=\sum\nolimits_{j=0}^{M}\zeta_{ij}(\tilde{x})\phi_{j}\left(\tilde{t}\right).

The function
\check{v}_{i}
will be useful to study the approximation capacity and the regularity of
v^{\natural}_{i}
.
We derive the approximation rate and the regularity properties of
\check{v}_{i}
in the following.
Due to Lemma
B.1
, for any
\tilde{x}\in[0,1]^{d}
,
i=1,2,\cdots,d
, and
j=0,1,\cdots,M
, we have

\displaystyle|\zeta_{ij}(\tilde{x})-v^{\diamond}_{i}\left(j/M,\tilde{x}\right)|\lesssim A(NL)^{-2/d}.

We evaluate the approximation error of
\check{v}_{i}
by the following error decomposition:

\displaystyle\|\check{v}_{i}-v^{\diamond}_{i}\|_{L^{\infty}([0,1]^{d+1})}\leq\underbrace{\|\check{v}_{i}-\tilde{v}_{i}\|_{L^{\infty}([0,1]^{d+1})}}_{=:\mathcal{E}_{i}^{1}}+\underbrace{\|\tilde{v}_{i}-v^{\diamond}_{i}\|_{L^{\infty}([0,1]^{d+1})}}_{=:\mathcal{E}_{i}^{2}}.

(B.9)

By Lemma
B.1
, we bound
\mathcal{E}_{i}^{1}
by

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{i}^{1}

\displaystyle\leq\Big\|\sum\nolimits_{j=0}^{M}\left[\zeta_{ij}(\tilde{x})-v^{\diamond}_{i}\left(j/M,\tilde{x}\right)\right]\phi_{j}\left(\tilde{t}\right)\Big\|_{L^{\infty}([0,1]^{d+1})}

\displaystyle\leq\max\nolimits_{0\leq j\leq M}\|\zeta_{ij}(\tilde{x})-v^{\diamond}_{i}\left(j/M,\tilde{x}\right)\|_{L^{\infty}([0,1]^{d})}

\displaystyle\lesssim\max\nolimits_{0\leq j\leq M}\|v^{\diamond}_{i}(j/M,\tilde{x})\|_{W^{1,\infty}((0,1)^{d})}(NL)^{-2/d}

\displaystyle\lesssim A(NL)^{-2/d}.

(B.10)

By Lemma
B.8
, we bound
\mathcal{E}_{i}^{2}
by

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{i}^{2}\lesssim\sup_{\tilde{x}\in[0,1]^{d}}|v^{\diamond}_{i}(\cdot,\tilde{x})|_{W^{1,\infty}((0,1))}/M\ \lesssim\underline{t}^{-2}M^{-1}.

(B.11)

Combining (
B.9
), (
B.10
), and (
B.11
), we have

\displaystyle\|\check{v}_{i}-v^{\diamond}_{i}\|_{L^{\infty}([0,1]^{d+1})}\lesssim A(NL)^{-2/d}+\underline{t}^{-2}M^{-1}.

Suppose that
(NL)^{2/d}\asymp\underline{t}^{2}M
, and it yields that for
i=1,2,\cdots,d
,

\displaystyle\|\check{v}_{i}-v^{\diamond}_{i}\|_{L^{\infty}([0,1]^{d+1})}\lesssim A(NL)^{-2/d}.

Let
\check{v}:=[\check{v}_{1},\check{v}_{2},\cdots,\check{v}_{d}]^{\top}
.
We have the approximation power of
\check{v}
evaluated by

\displaystyle\|\check{v}-v^{\diamond}\|_{L^{\infty}([0,1]^{d+1})}\lesssim A(NL)^{-2/d}.

(B.12)

Moreover, the Lipschitz continuity of
\check{v}
in
\tilde{t}
and
\tilde{x}
can be verified.
Concretely, we have the Lipschitz estimate in the space variable
\tilde{x}
: for any
\tilde{x},\tilde{y}\in[0,1]^{d}
and
\tilde{t}\in[0,1]
,

\displaystyle\|\check{v}(\tilde{t},\tilde{x})-\check{v}(\tilde{t},\tilde{y})\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\max\nolimits_{1\leq i\leq d}\Big\|\sum\nolimits_{j=0}^{M}[\zeta_{ij}(\tilde{x})-\zeta_{ij}(\tilde{y})]\phi_{j}\left(\tilde{t}\right)\Big\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\max\nolimits_{1\leq i\leq d,\ 0\leq j\leq M}\|\zeta_{ij}(\tilde{x})-\zeta_{ij}(\tilde{y})\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\max\nolimits_{1\leq i\leq d,\ 0\leq j\leq M}\|\zeta_{ij}\|_{W^{1,\infty}((0,1)^{d})}\cdot\|\tilde{x}-\tilde{y}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|v^{\diamond}\|_{W^{1,\infty}((0,1)^{d};{\mathbb{R}}^{d})}\cdot\|\tilde{x}-\tilde{y}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\lesssim A\|\tilde{x}-\tilde{y}\|_{\infty},

It is somewhat tedious to derive the Lipschitz estimate in the time variable
\tilde{t}
.
For any
\tilde{s},\tilde{t}\in[0,1]
and
\tilde{x}\in[0,1]^{d}
,

\displaystyle~~~~~\|\check{v}(\tilde{t},\tilde{x})-\check{v}(\tilde{s},\tilde{x})\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\|\check{v}(\tilde{t},\tilde{x})-\tilde{v}(\tilde{t},\tilde{x})\|_{\infty}+\|\tilde{v}(\tilde{t},\tilde{x})-\tilde{v}(\tilde{s},\tilde{x})\|_{\infty}+\|\tilde{v}(\tilde{s},\tilde{x})-\check{v}(\tilde{s},\tilde{x})\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq 2\sup_{\vartheta\in[0,1]}\|\check{v}(\vartheta,\tilde{x})-\tilde{v}(\vartheta,\tilde{x})\|_{\infty}+|\tilde{v}(\cdot,\tilde{x})|_{W^{1,\infty}((0,1);{\mathbb{R}}^{d})}|\tilde{t}-\tilde{s}|

\displaystyle\leq 2\max\nolimits_{1\leq i\leq d}\mathcal{E}_{i}^{1}+|\tilde{v}(\cdot,\tilde{x})|_{W^{1,\infty}((0,1);{\mathbb{R}}^{d})}|\tilde{t}-\tilde{s}|

\displaystyle\lesssim A(NL)^{-2/d}+\underline{t}^{-2}|\tilde{t}-\tilde{s}|.

Considering
(NL)^{2/d}\asymp\underline{t}^{2}M
, we deduce that

\displaystyle\|\check{v}(\tilde{t},\tilde{x})-\check{v}(\tilde{s},\tilde{x})\|_{\infty}\lesssim A\underline{t}^{-2}M^{-1}+\underline{t}^{-2}|\tilde{t}-\tilde{s}|.

Then we consider two cases for bounding
\|\check{v}(\tilde{t},\tilde{x})-\check{v}(\tilde{s},\tilde{x})\|_{\infty}
.

Case 1. If
|\tilde{t}-\tilde{s}|\geq\frac{1}{3M}
, it is clear that

\|\check{v}(\tilde{t},\tilde{x})-\check{v}(\tilde{s},\tilde{x})\|_{\infty}\lesssim A\underline{t}^{-2}|\tilde{t}-\tilde{s}|
.

Case 2. If
|\tilde{t}-\tilde{s}|<\frac{1}{3M}
, for any
i\in\{1,2,\cdots,d\}
, we try to bound the difference

\displaystyle~~~~~|\check{v}_{i}(\tilde{t},\tilde{x})-\check{v}_{i}(\tilde{s},\tilde{x})|

\displaystyle=\Big|\sum\nolimits_{j=0}^{M}\zeta_{ij}(\tilde{x})\left[\phi_{j}\left(\tilde{t}\right)-\phi_{j}\left(\tilde{s}\right)\right]\Big|

\displaystyle=\Big|\sum\nolimits_{j=0}^{M}\zeta_{ij}(\tilde{x})\left[\psi\left(3M\tilde{t}-3j\right)-\psi\left(3M\tilde{s}-3j\right)\right]\Big|=:\mathcal{E}_{3}.

Then, we focus on bounding
\mathcal{E}_{3}
.
Without loss of generality, we assume
\tilde{t}<\tilde{s}
.
The remaining calculation is similar to the proof of Lemma
B.8
.
Let
m=\tilde{m}\in\{0,1,\cdots,M\}
or
m=\bar{m}:=\tilde{m}+1
be two possible numbers satisfying
\psi(3M\tilde{t}-3m)\not\equiv 0
or
\psi(3M\tilde{s}-3m)\not\equiv 0
. Then it holds that

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{3}

\displaystyle=\left|\left[\psi\left(3M\tilde{t}-3\tilde{m}\right)-\psi\left(3M\tilde{s}-3\tilde{m}\right)\right]\zeta_{i\tilde{m}}(\tilde{x})\right.

\displaystyle~~~~+\left.\left[\psi\left(3M\tilde{t}-3\bar{m}\right)-\psi\left(3M\tilde{s}-3\bar{m}\right)\right]\zeta_{i\bar{m}}(\tilde{x})\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\left[\psi\left(3M\tilde{t}-3\tilde{m}\right)-\psi\left(3M\tilde{s}-3\tilde{m}\right)\right]\zeta_{i\tilde{m}}(\tilde{x})\right.

\displaystyle~~~~+\left.\left[(1-\psi\left(3M\tilde{t}-3\tilde{m}\right))-(1-\psi\left(3M\tilde{s}-3\tilde{m}\right))\right]\zeta_{i\bar{m}}(\tilde{x})\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\left[\psi\left(3M\tilde{t}-3\tilde{m}\right)-\psi\left(3M\tilde{s}-3\tilde{m}\right)\right][\zeta_{i\tilde{m}}(\tilde{x})-\zeta_{i\bar{m}}(\tilde{x})]\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\zeta_{i\tilde{m}}(\tilde{x})-\zeta_{i\bar{m}}(\tilde{x})\right|\left|\psi\left(3M\tilde{t}-3\tilde{m}\right)-\psi\left(3M\tilde{s}-3\tilde{m}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq 3M\left|\zeta_{i\tilde{m}}(\tilde{x})-\zeta_{i\bar{m}}(\tilde{x})\right|\cdot|\tilde{t}-\tilde{s}|.

We bound the term
|\zeta_{i\tilde{m}}(\tilde{x})-\zeta_{i\bar{m}}(\tilde{x})|
by

\displaystyle~~~~~|\zeta_{i\tilde{m}}(\tilde{x})-\zeta_{i\bar{m}}(\tilde{x})|

\displaystyle\leq|\zeta_{i\tilde{m}}(\tilde{x})-v^{\diamond}_{i}\left(\tilde{m}/M,\tilde{x}\right)|+|v^{\diamond}_{i}\left(\tilde{m}/M,\tilde{x}\right)-v^{\diamond}_{i}\left(\bar{m}/M,\tilde{x}\right)|

\displaystyle~~~~+|v^{\diamond}_{i}\left(\bar{m}/M,\tilde{x}\right)-\zeta_{i\bar{m}}(\tilde{x})|

\displaystyle\lesssim A(NL)^{-2/d}+\underline{t}^{-2}M^{-1}.

Recall that
(NL)^{2/d}\asymp\underline{t}^{2}M
. It implies that

|\zeta_{i\tilde{m}}(\tilde{x})-\zeta_{i\bar{m}}(\tilde{x})|\lesssim A\underline{t}^{-2}M^{-1}.

Therefore, if
|\tilde{t}-\tilde{s}|<\frac{1}{3M}
, it holds that

\displaystyle|\check{v}_{i}(\tilde{t},\tilde{x})-\check{v}_{i}(\tilde{s},\tilde{x})|\leq 3M\left|\zeta_{i\tilde{m}}(\tilde{x})-\zeta_{i\bar{m}}(\tilde{x})\right||\tilde{t}-\tilde{s}|\lesssim A\underline{t}^{-2}|\tilde{t}-\tilde{s}|.

We summarize the Lipschitz properties of
\check{v}
as follows:

\displaystyle|\check{v}(\cdot,\tilde{x})|_{W^{1,\infty}((0,1);{\mathbb{R}}^{d})}\lesssim A\underline{t}^{-2},\quad|\check{v}(\tilde{t},\cdot)|_{W^{1,\infty}((0,1)^{d};{\mathbb{R}}^{d})}\lesssim A.

Let
v^{\natural}:=[v^{\natural}_{1},v^{\natural}_{2},\cdots,v^{\natural}_{d}]^{\top}
.
We use the approximation rate of
\check{v}
to derive that of
v^{\natural}
.
By the triangle inequality, it holds that

\displaystyle\|v^{\natural}-v^{\diamond}\|_{L^{\infty}([0,1]^{d+1})}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|v^{\natural}-\check{v}\|_{L^{\infty}([0,1]^{d+1})}+\|\check{v}-v^{\diamond}\|_{L^{\infty}([0,1]^{d+1})}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle A(N+1)^{-4L}+A(NL)^{-2/d}\quad\text{(By Claim \ref{claim:nonzero-terms}, Eq. (\ref{eq:approx-rate-times-nn}), and Eq. (\ref{eq:vf-surrogate-cube-approx}))}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle A(NL)^{-2/d}\quad\text{(By $(NL)^{2/d}\leq(N+1)^{4L}$ for any $N,L\in{\mathbb{N}}$)}.

Thus, the approximation rate of
v^{\natural}
is given by

\displaystyle\|v^{\natural}-v^{\diamond}\|_{L^{\infty}([0,1]^{d+1})}\lesssim A(NL)^{-2/d}.

(B.13)

Then we study the Lipschitz properties of
v^{\natural}
.
By Lemma
F.1
and Claim
B.10
, it holds that

\displaystyle~|v^{\natural}(\cdot,\tilde{x})-\check{v}(\cdot,\tilde{x})|_{W^{1,\infty}((0,1);{\mathbb{R}}^{d})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max_{i}|v_{i}^{\natural}(\cdot,\tilde{x})-\check{v}_{i}(\cdot,\tilde{x})|_{W^{1,\infty}((0,1))}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max_{i}\Big|\sum\nolimits_{j=0}^{M}\phi_{\times,(B_{3},B_{4})}\left(\zeta_{ij}(\tilde{x}),\phi_{j}(\cdot)\right)-\sum\nolimits_{j=0}^{M}\zeta_{ij}(\tilde{x})\phi_{j}(\cdot)\Big|_{W^{1,\infty}((0,1))}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\max_{i}|\phi_{\times,(B_{3},B_{4})}(x,y)-xy|_{W^{1,\infty}((-B_{3},B_{3})\times(-B_{4},B_{4}))}\cdot|\phi_{j}|_{W^{1,\infty}((0,1))}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle AM(N+1)^{-4L}\lesssim A\underline{t}^{-2}(NL)^{2/d}(N+1)^{-4L}\quad\text{(By $(NL)^{2/d}\asymp\underline{t}^{2}M$)}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle A\underline{t}^{-2}\quad\text{(By $(NL)^{2/d}\leq(N+1)^{4L}$ for any $N,L\in{\mathbb{N}}$)}.

By the triangle inequality, the Lipschitz property of
v^{\natural}
in the time variable
\tilde{t}
is evaluated by

\displaystyle|v^{\natural}(\cdot,\tilde{x})|_{W^{1,\infty}((0,1);{\mathbb{R}}^{d})}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle|v^{\natural}(\cdot,\tilde{x})-\check{v}(\cdot,\tilde{x})|_{W^{1,\infty}((0,1);{\mathbb{R}}^{d})}+|\check{v}(\cdot,\tilde{x})|_{W^{1,\infty}((0,1);{\mathbb{R}}^{d})}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle A\underline{t}^{-2}+A\underline{t}^{-2}\lesssim A\underline{t}^{-2}.

By Lemma
F.1
and Claim
B.10
, we derive the Lipschitz property of
v^{\natural}
in the space variable
\tilde{x}
as follows

\displaystyle~|v^{\natural}(\tilde{t},\cdot)|_{W^{1,\infty}((0,1)^{d})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max_{i}|v_{i}^{\natural}(\tilde{t},\cdot)|_{W^{1,\infty}((0,1)^{d})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max_{i}\Big|\sum\nolimits_{j=0}^{M}\phi_{\times,(B_{3},B_{4})}\left(\zeta_{ij}(\cdot),\phi_{j}(\tilde{t})\right)\Big|_{W^{1,\infty}((0,1)^{d})}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\max_{i}\Big\{|\phi_{\times,(B_{3},B_{4})}(\cdot,y)|_{W^{1,\infty}((-B_{3},B_{3}))}\cdot\max_{j}|\zeta_{ij}|_{W^{1,\infty}((0,1)^{d})}\Big\}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle A^{2}.

We claim that
\bar{v}(t,x):=v^{\natural}(\mathcal{T}_{1}(t),\mathcal{T}_{2}\circ\mathcal{C}_{A}(x))
provides a good approximation of the velocity field
v^{*}
on the domain
\Omega_{\underline{t},A}
, and
\bar{v}
can be implemented by a deep ReLU network.
According to the error bound (
B.13
), the approximation rate of
\bar{v}
is given by

\displaystyle\|\bar{v}(t,x)-v^{*}(t,x)\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega_{\underline{t},A})}\lesssim A(NL)^{-2/d},

where we omit constants in
d,\kappa,\beta,\sigma,D,R
.
Furthermore, we need to estimate the Lipschitz constants of
\bar{v}
.
Here, we use Lemma
F.1
to calculate the Sobolev semi-norms of the composite functions:

\displaystyle|\bar{v}(\cdot,x)|_{W^{1,\infty}((0,1-\underline{t});{\mathbb{R}}^{d})}\lesssim|v^{\natural}(\cdot,\mathcal{T}_{2}\circ\mathcal{C}_{A}(x))|_{W^{1,\infty}((0,1);{\mathbb{R}}^{d})}|\mathcal{T}_{1}|_{W^{1,\infty}((0,1-\underline{t});(0,1))}\lesssim A\underline{t}^{-2},

\displaystyle|\bar{v}(t,\cdot)|_{W^{1,\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{d};{\mathbb{R}}^{d})}\lesssim|v^{\natural}(\mathcal{T}_{1}(t),\cdot)|_{W^{1,\infty}((0,1)^{d};{\mathbb{R}}^{d})}|\mathcal{T}_{2}|_{W^{1,\infty}((-A,A)^{d};{\mathbb{R}}^{d})}|\mathcal{C}_{A}|_{W^{1,\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{d};[-A,A]^{d})}\lesssim A.

In addition, we have the
L^{\infty}
bound
\|\bar{v}\|_{L^{\infty}([1-\underline{t}]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d})}\lesssim A
.

In the end, it remains to calculate the complexity of the deep ReLU network implementing
\bar{v}
.
By the definition of
v^{\natural}
in (
B.8
), we know that
v^{\natural}
consists of
{\mathcal{O}}(\underline{t}^{-2}d(NL)^{2/d})
parallel subnetworks listed as follows:

•

\phi_{\times,(B_{3},B_{4})}
with width
\mathcal{O}(N)
and depth
\mathcal{O}(L)
;

•

\zeta_{ij}
with width
{\mathcal{O}}(2^{d}dN\log N)
and depth
{\mathcal{O}}(d^{2}L\log L)
;

•

\phi_{j}
with width
{\mathcal{O}}(1)
and depth
{\mathcal{O}}(1)
.

Hence, the deep ReLU network implementing
v^{\natural}
has width
{\mathcal{O}}(\underline{t}^{-2}2^{d}d^{2}(NL)^{2/d}N\log N)
and depth
{\mathcal{O}}(d^{2}L\log L)
.
By omitting polynomial prefactors in
d
, we obtain that the deep ReLU network implementing the function
\bar{v}
has width
{\mathcal{O}}(\underline{t}^{-2}2^{d}(NL)^{2/d}N\log N)
, depth
{\mathcal{O}}(L\log L)
, and size
{\mathcal{O}}(\underline{t}^{-2}4^{d}(NL)^{2/d}(N\log N)^{2}L\log L)
.
∎

C
Error analysis of flow matching

In the appendix, we present proofs for error analyses of flow matching.

C.1
Basic error decomposition

We present the proof of Lemma
5.2
.

We follow the proof of
Jiao et al. (2023, Lemma 3.1)
.
Due to that
v^{*}
is the minimizer of
\mathcal{L}
, direct calculation implies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{D}_{n}}\mathbb{E}_{(\mathsf{t},\mathsf{X}_{\mathsf{t}})}\|\hat{v}_{n}(\mathsf{t},\mathsf{X}_{\mathsf{t}})-v^{*}(\mathsf{t},\mathsf{X}_{\mathsf{t}})\|_{2}^{2}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{D}_{n}}[\mathcal{L}(\hat{v}_{n})-\mathcal{L}(v^{*})].

Since
\hat{v}_{n}
is the minimizer of the empirical risk, for any
v^{{\dagger}}\in\arg\inf_{v\in{\mathcal{F}}_{n}}\mathbb{E}_{(\mathsf{t},\mathsf{X}_{\mathsf{t}})}\|v(\mathsf{t},\mathsf{X}_{\mathsf{t}})-v^{*}(\mathsf{t},\mathsf{X}_{\mathsf{t}})\|_{2}^{2}
, it holds that

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{n}(\hat{v}_{n})-\mathcal{L}_{n}(v^{*})\leq\mathcal{L}_{n}(v^{{\dagger}})-\mathcal{L}_{n}(v^{*}).

Taking expectations over
\mathbb{D}_{n}
on both sides, it yields that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{D}_{n}}[\mathcal{L}_{n}(\hat{v}_{n})-\mathcal{L}(v^{*})]\leq\mathcal{L}(v^{{\dagger}})-\mathcal{L}(v^{*})=\inf_{v\in{\mathcal{F}}_{n}}\mathbb{E}_{(\mathsf{t},\mathsf{X}_{\mathsf{t}})}\|v(\mathsf{t},\mathsf{X}_{\mathsf{t}})-v^{*}(\mathsf{t},\mathsf{X}_{\mathsf{t}})\|_{2}^{2}.

(C.1)

Using the inequality (
C.1
), we deduce that

\displaystyle~\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{D}_{n}}\mathbb{E}_{(\mathsf{t},\mathsf{X}_{\mathsf{t}})}\|\hat{v}_{n}(\mathsf{t},\mathsf{X}_{\mathsf{t}})-v^{*}(\mathsf{t},\mathsf{X}_{\mathsf{t}})\|_{2}^{2}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{D}_{n}}[\mathcal{L}(\hat{v}_{n})-\mathcal{L}(v^{*})]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle~\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{D}_{n}}[\mathcal{L}(\hat{v}_{n})-\mathcal{L}(v^{*})]-2\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{D}_{n}}[\mathcal{L}_{n}(\hat{v}_{n})-\mathcal{L}(v^{*})]+2\inf_{v\in{\mathcal{F}}_{n}}\mathbb{E}_{(\mathsf{t},\mathsf{X}_{\mathsf{t}})}\|v(\mathsf{t},\mathsf{X}_{\mathsf{t}})-v^{*}(\mathsf{t},\mathsf{X}_{\mathsf{t}})\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle~\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{D}_{n}}[\mathcal{L}(v^{*})-2\mathcal{L}_{n}(\hat{v}_{n})+\mathcal{L}(\hat{v}_{n})]+2\inf_{v\in{\mathcal{F}}_{n}}\mathbb{E}_{(\mathsf{t},\mathsf{X}_{\mathsf{t}})}\|v(\mathsf{t},\mathsf{X}_{\mathsf{t}})-v^{*}(\mathsf{t},\mathsf{X}_{\mathsf{t}})\|_{2}^{2}.

This completes the proof.
∎

C.2
Truncation error

The truncation error is well controlled by the fast-decaying tail probability of
\mathsf{X}_{t}\sim p_{t}
.
We bound the tail probability in Lemma
5.7
and the truncation error in Lemma
5.8
.
For a sub-Gaussian random variable
\mathsf{X}
, we use
\|\mathsf{X}\|_{\psi_{2}}
to denote its sub-Gaussian norm.

Let
\mathsf{X}_{t}=[\mathsf{X}_{t}^{1},\mathsf{X}_{t}^{2},\cdots,\mathsf{X}_{t}^{d}]^{\top}
.
Similarly, let
\mathsf{Z}=[\mathsf{Z}^{1},\mathsf{Z}^{2},\cdots,\mathsf{Z}^{d}]^{\top}
and

\mathsf{X}_{1}=[\mathsf{X}_{1}^{1},\mathsf{X}_{1}^{2},\cdots,\mathsf{X}_{1}^{d}]^{\top}
.
By the general Hoeffding inequality
(
Vershynin, 2018
, Theorem 2.6.3)
, for any
1\leq i\leq d
, we bound the tail probability of
\mathsf{X}_{t}^{i}
by

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(|\mathsf{X}_{t}^{i}|>A)=\mathbb{P}(|(1-t)\mathsf{Z}^{i}+t\mathsf{X}_{1}^{i}|>A)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{C_{1}A^{2}}{K_{1}^{2}}\right),

where
C_{1}
is a universal constant and
K_{1}:=\|\mathsf{Z}^{1}\|_{\psi_{2}}\vee\max_{1\leq i\leq d}\|\mathsf{X}_{1}^{i}\|_{\psi_{2}}
with
\mathsf{Z}^{1}\sim\gamma_{1}
.
According to Remark
5
,
K_{1}\asymp\sqrt{C_{\mathrm{LSI}}}
is finite with dependence on parameters in
\mathcal{S}_{1}
.
By the union bound, it further yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathsf{X}_{t}\in\Omega_{A}^{c})=\mathbb{P}(\exists 1\leq i\leq d:|\mathsf{X}_{t}^{i}|>A)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathbb{P}(|\mathsf{X}_{t}^{i}|>A)\leq 2d\exp\left(-\frac{C_{2}A^{2}}{C_{\mathrm{LSI}}}\right),

where
C_{2}
is a universal constant and
C_{\mathrm{LSI}}
depends on parameters in
\mathcal{S}_{1}
.
This tail probability bound holds uniformly for
t\in[0,1]
.
∎

We decompose the truncation error by

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{trunc}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{(\mathsf{t},\mathsf{X}_{\mathsf{t}})}\|[\bar{v}(\mathsf{t},\mathsf{X}_{\mathsf{t}})-v^{*}(\mathsf{t},\mathsf{X}_{\mathsf{t}})]\mathds{1}_{\Omega_{A}^{c}}(\mathsf{X}_{\mathsf{t}})\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{(\mathsf{t},\mathsf{X}_{\mathsf{t}})}\|\bar{v}(\mathsf{t},\mathsf{X}_{\mathsf{t}})\mathds{1}_{\Omega_{A}^{c}}(\mathsf{X}_{\mathsf{t}})\|_{2}^{2}}_{=:\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{trunc}}^{1}}+\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{(\mathsf{t},\mathsf{X}_{\mathsf{t}})}\|v^{*}(\mathsf{t},\mathsf{X}_{\mathsf{t}})\mathds{1}_{\Omega_{A}^{c}}(\mathsf{X}_{\mathsf{t}})\|_{2}^{2}}_{=:\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{trunc}}^{2}}.

(C.2)

First, we bound
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{trunc}}^{1}
.
For any
A>0
and
t\in[0,1-\underline{t}]
, it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{X}_{t}}\|\bar{v}(t,\mathsf{X}_{t})\mathds{1}_{\Omega_{A}^{c}}(\mathsf{X}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{X}_{t}}[\|\bar{v}(t,\mathsf{X}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}\mathds{1}_{\Omega_{A}^{c}}(\mathsf{X}_{t})]

\displaystyle\leq\left(\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{X}_{t}}[\|\bar{v}(t,\mathsf{X}_{t})\|_{2}^{4}]\cdot\mathbb{P}(\mathsf{X}_{t}\in\Omega_{A}^{c})\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\lesssim A^{2}\mathbb{P}(\mathsf{X}_{t}\in\Omega_{A}^{c})^{1/2},

(C.3)

where the first inequality follows from the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, and the second inequality is due to
\|\bar{v}(t,x)\|_{L^{\infty}([0,1-\underline{t}]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d})}\lesssim A
given in Theorem
5.4
.
Combining (
5.3
) in Lemma
5.7
and (
C.3
) above, it follows

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{trunc}}^{1}=\mathbb{E}_{(\mathsf{t},\mathsf{X}_{\mathsf{t}})}\|\bar{v}(\mathsf{t},\mathsf{X}_{\mathsf{t}})\mathds{1}_{\Omega_{A}^{c}}(\mathsf{X}_{\mathsf{t}})\|_{2}^{2}\lesssim\sqrt{d}A^{2}\exp\left(-\frac{C_{3}A^{2}}{C_{\mathrm{LSI}}}\right),

(C.4)

where
C_{3}
is a universal constant.

Then, we bound
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{trunc}}^{2}
. Due to
v^{*}(t,x)=\mathbb{E}[\mathsf{X}_{1}-\mathsf{Z}|\mathsf{X}_{t}=x]
, it holds

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{X}_{t}}\|v^{*}(t,\mathsf{X}_{t})\|_{2}^{4}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{X}_{t}}\|\mathbb{E}[\mathsf{X}_{1}-\mathsf{Z}|\mathsf{X}_{t}=x]\|_{2}^{4}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{X}_{t}}\mathbb{E}[\|\mathsf{X}_{1}-\mathsf{Z}\|_{2}^{4}|\mathsf{X}_{t}=x]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}[\|\mathsf{X}_{1}-\mathsf{Z}\|_{2}^{4}]

\displaystyle\leq 8\mathbb{E}[\|\mathsf{Z}\|_{2}^{4}]+8\mathbb{E}[\|\mathsf{X}_{1}\|_{2}^{4}],

where the fourth moments in the last expression are finite by the property of the Gaussian distribution and the sub-Gaussian property of
\mathsf{X}_{1}
.
For any
A>0
and
t\in[0,1-\underline{t}]
, we further bound
\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{X}_{t}}\|v^{*}(t,\mathsf{X}_{t})\mathds{1}_{\Omega_{A}^{c}}(\mathsf{X}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}
by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{X}_{t}}\|v^{*}(t,\mathsf{X}_{t})\mathds{1}_{\Omega_{A}^{c}}(\mathsf{X}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{X}_{t}}[\|v^{*}(t,\mathsf{X}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}\mathds{1}_{\Omega_{A}^{c}}(\mathsf{X}_{t})]

\displaystyle\leq\left(\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{X}_{t}}\|[v^{*}(t,\mathsf{X}_{t})\|_{2}^{4}]\cdot\mathbb{P}(\mathsf{X}_{t}\in\Omega_{A}^{c})\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\mathbb{E}[\|\mathsf{X}_{1}\|_{2}^{4}]^{1/2}\cdot\mathbb{P}(\mathsf{X}_{t}\in\Omega_{A}^{c})^{1/2}.

(C.5)

Combining (
5.3
) in Lemma
5.7
and (
C.5
) above, it follows

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{trunc}}^{2}=\mathbb{E}_{(\mathsf{t},\mathsf{X}_{\mathsf{t}})}\|v^{*}(\mathsf{t},\mathsf{X}_{\mathsf{t}})\mathds{1}_{\Omega_{A}^{c}}(\mathsf{X}_{\mathsf{t}})\|_{2}^{2}\lesssim\sqrt{d}\exp\left(-\frac{C_{3}A^{2}}{C_{\mathrm{LSI}}}\right),

(C.6)

where we omit the dependence on the fourth moment of the target
\mathsf{X_{1}}
.

Finally, combining (
C.2
), (
C.4
), and (
C.6
), we get

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{trunc}}\lesssim\sqrt{d}A^{2}\exp\left(-\frac{C_{3}A^{2}}{C_{\mathrm{LSI}}}\right),

where we omit the dependence on the fourth moment of the target
\mathsf{X_{1}}
.
This completes the proof.
∎

C.3
Stochastic error

The stochastic error is known as generalization error in statistical machine learning. In this part, we study the stochastic error of flow matching with techniques in empirical processes and present the proof of Lemma
5.10
. Before that, we show necessary definitions from the content of empirical processes for establishing bounds of the stochastic error.

Definition C.1

(Uniform and empirical covering numbers)
.

Given the samples
\mathbb{X}_{n}:=\{\mathsf{X}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, we define the empirical
L^{\infty}
pseudometric
\|\cdot\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{X}_{n})}
on the samples
\mathbb{X}_{n}
by

\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{X}_{n})}:=\max_{1\leq i\leq n}|f(\mathsf{X}_{i})|.

A set
{\mathcal{F}}_{\delta}
is called an empirical
L^{\infty}

\delta
-cover of the function class
{\mathcal{F}}
on the samples
\mathbb{X}_{n}
if for each
f\in{\mathcal{F}}
, there exists
f^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{F}}_{\delta}
such that
\|f-f^{\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{X}_{n})}\leq\delta
. Furthermore,

\mathcal{N}_{\infty}(\delta,{\mathcal{F}},\mathbb{X}_{n}):=\inf\big\{|{\mathcal{F}}_{\delta}|:{\mathcal{F}}_{\delta}\text{ is an empirical $L^{\infty}$ $\delta$-cover of ${\mathcal{F}}$ on $\mathbb{X}_{n}$}\big\}

is called the empirical
L^{\infty}

\delta
-covering number of
{\mathcal{F}}
on
\mathbb{X}_{n}
.
Given
n
, the largest
L^{\infty}

\delta
-covering number over samples
\mathbb{X}_{n}
is referred to as the uniform
L^{\infty}

\delta
-covering number
\mathcal{N}_{\infty}(\delta,{\mathcal{F}},n):=\sup_{\mathbb{X}_{n}}\mathcal{N}_{\infty}(\delta,{\mathcal{F}},\mathbb{X}_{n})
.

Definition C.2
.

Let
{\mathcal{F}}
be a class of functions from a set
{\mathcal{Z}}
to
{\mathbb{R}}
. A set
\{z_{1},\cdots,z_{m}\}\subset{\mathcal{X}}
is said to be shattered by
{\mathcal{F}}
if there exist
t_{1},t_{2},\cdots,t_{m}\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that, for each
b\in\{0,1\}^{m}
, there exist a function
f_{b}\in{\mathcal{F}}
satisfying
\mathrm{sgn}\left(f_{b}\left(z_{i}\right)-t_{i}\right)=b_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq m
. We say that the threshold values
t_{1},t_{2},\cdots,t_{m}
witness the shattering.

Definition C.3

(Pseudo-dimension)
.

Let
{\mathcal{F}}
be a class of functions from a set
\Omega
to
{\mathbb{R}}
. The pseudo-dimension of
{\mathcal{F}}
, denoted by
\mathrm{Pdim}({\mathcal{F}})
, is the maximum cardinality of a subset of
\Omega
shattered by
{\mathcal{F}}
.

Let
\mathbb{D}_{n}=\{\mathsf{S}_{i}:=(\mathsf{Z}_{i},\mathsf{X}_{1,i},\mathsf{t}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
be a random sample from the distribution of
\mathsf{Z},\mathsf{X}_{1},\mathsf{t}
and
\mathbb{D}_{n}^{\prime}:=\{\mathsf{S}_{i}^{\prime}:=(\mathsf{Z}_{i}^{\prime},\mathsf{X}_{1,i}^{\prime},\mathsf{t}_{i}^{\prime})\}_{i=1}^{n}
be another ghost sample independent of
\mathbb{D}_{n}
.
We denote that
\mathsf{X}_{\mathsf{t}_{i}}:=(1-\mathsf{t}_{i})\mathsf{Z}_{i}+\mathsf{t}_{i}\mathsf{X}_{1,i}
,

\mathsf{X}_{\mathsf{t}_{i}}^{\prime}:=(1-\mathsf{t}_{i}^{\prime})\mathsf{Z}_{i}^{\prime}+\mathsf{t}_{i}^{\prime}\mathsf{X}_{1,i}^{\prime}
,

\mathsf{Y}_{i}:=\mathsf{X}_{1,i}-\mathsf{Z}_{i}
,
and
\mathsf{Y}_{i}^{\prime}:=\mathsf{X}_{1,i}^{\prime}-\mathsf{Z}_{i}^{\prime}
.
Define
{\mathcal{D}}(v,\mathsf{S}_{i}):=\|v(\mathsf{t}_{i},\mathsf{X}_{\mathsf{t}_{i}})-\mathsf{Y}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}-\|v^{*}(\mathsf{t}_{i},\mathsf{X}_{\mathsf{t}_{i}})-\mathsf{Y}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}
for any
v\in{\mathcal{F}}_{n}
and
\mathsf{S}_{i}
.
Notice that

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{D}_{n}}[\mathcal{L}(v^{*})-2\mathcal{L}_{n}(\hat{v}_{n})+\mathcal{L}(\hat{v}_{n})]=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{D}_{n}}\Big[\frac{1}{n}\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{n}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{D}_{n}^{\prime}}{\mathcal{D}}(\hat{v}_{n},\mathsf{S}_{i}^{\prime})-2{\mathcal{D}}(\hat{v}_{n},\mathsf{S}_{i})\right)\Big].

(C.7)

It is clear that the right-hand side of (
C.7
) defines an asymmetric empirical process.
Let
{\mathcal{G}}(v,\mathsf{S}_{i}):=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{D}_{n}^{\prime}}{\mathcal{D}}(v,\mathsf{S}_{i}^{\prime})-2{\mathcal{D}}(v,\mathsf{S}_{i})
for any
v\in{\mathcal{F}}_{n}
.
Then we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{D}_{n}}[\mathcal{L}(v^{*})-2\mathcal{L}_{n}(\hat{v}_{n})+\mathcal{L}(\hat{v}_{n})]=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{D}_{n}}\Big[\frac{1}{n}\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{n}{\mathcal{G}}(\hat{v}_{n},\mathsf{S}_{i})\Big].

Let
B_{n}\geq\mathtt{B}\geq B\geq 1
be a positive number that may depend on the sample size
n
.
We construct a clipping function
{\mathcal{C}}_{B_{n}}
at level
B_{n}
following the definition of clipping functions in Lemma
B.9
.
Let
v_{B_{n}}(t,x):=\mathbb{E}[{\mathcal{C}}_{B_{n}}(\mathsf{Y})|\mathsf{X}_{t}=x]
be the regression function of the truncated
\mathsf{Y}
. Similar to the definitions of
{\mathcal{D}}(v,\mathsf{S}_{i})
and
{\mathcal{G}}(v,\mathsf{S}_{i})
, we define
{\mathcal{D}}_{B_{n}}(v,\mathsf{S}_{i}):=\|v(\mathsf{t}_{i},\mathsf{X}_{\mathsf{t}_{i}})-{\mathcal{C}}_{B_{n}}(\mathsf{Y}_{i})\|_{2}^{2}-\|v_{B_{n}}(\mathsf{t}_{i},\mathsf{X}_{\mathsf{t}_{i}})-{\mathcal{C}}_{B_{n}}(\mathsf{Y}_{i})\|_{2}^{2}
and
{\mathcal{G}}_{B_{n}}(v,\mathsf{S}_{i}):=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{D}_{n}^{\prime}}{\mathcal{D}}_{B_{n}}(v,\mathsf{S}_{i}^{\prime})-2{\mathcal{D}}_{B_{n}}(v,\mathsf{S}_{i})
.
Then for any
v\in{\mathcal{F}}_{n}
we have

\displaystyle~|{\mathcal{D}}(v,\mathsf{S}_{i})-{\mathcal{D}}_{B_{n}}(v,\mathsf{S}_{i})|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\big|2\langle v(\mathsf{t}_{i},\mathsf{X}_{\mathsf{t}_{i}})-v^{*}(\mathsf{t}_{i},\mathsf{X}_{\mathsf{t}_{i}}),{\mathcal{C}}_{B_{n}}(\mathsf{Y}_{i})-\mathsf{Y}_{i}\rangle

\displaystyle+\|v_{B_{n}}(\mathsf{t}_{i},\mathsf{X}_{\mathsf{t}_{i}})-{\mathcal{C}}_{B_{n}}(\mathsf{Y}_{i})\|_{2}^{2}-\|v^{*}(\mathsf{t}_{i},\mathsf{X}_{\mathsf{t}_{i}})-{\mathcal{C}}_{B_{n}}(\mathsf{Y}_{i})\|_{2}^{2}\big|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\big|\big\langle v(\mathsf{t}_{i},\mathsf{X}_{\mathsf{t}_{i}})-v^{*}(\mathsf{t}_{i},\mathsf{X}_{\mathsf{t}_{i}}),{\mathcal{C}}_{B_{n}}(\mathsf{Y}_{i})-\mathsf{Y}_{i}\big\rangle\big|

\displaystyle+\big|\big\langle v_{B_{n}}(\mathsf{t}_{i},\mathsf{X}_{\mathsf{t}_{i}})-v^{*}(\mathsf{t}_{i},\mathsf{X}_{\mathsf{t}_{i}}),v_{B_{n}}(\mathsf{t}_{i},\mathsf{X}_{\mathsf{t}_{i}})+v^{*}(\mathsf{t}_{i},\mathsf{X}_{\mathsf{t}_{i}})-2{\mathcal{C}}_{B_{n}}(\mathsf{Y}_{i})\big\rangle\big|.

By considering coordinate-wise scalar expressions of the risks, we get

\displaystyle~|{\mathcal{D}}(v,\mathsf{S}_{i})-{\mathcal{D}}_{B_{n}}(v,\mathsf{S}_{i})|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{j=1}^{d}\Big\{2\big|[v_{j}(\mathsf{t}_{i},\mathsf{X}_{\mathsf{t}_{i}})-v^{*}_{j}(\mathsf{t}_{i},\mathsf{X}_{\mathsf{t}_{i}})]\cdot[({\mathcal{C}}_{B_{n}}(\mathsf{Y}_{i}))_{j}-(\mathsf{Y}_{i})_{j}]\big|

\displaystyle+\big|[(v_{B_{n}})_{j}(\mathsf{t}_{i},\mathsf{X}_{\mathsf{t}_{i}})-v^{*}_{j}(\mathsf{t}_{i},\mathsf{X}_{\mathsf{t}_{i}})]\cdot[(v_{B_{n}})_{j}(\mathsf{t}_{i},\mathsf{X}_{\mathsf{t}_{i}})+v^{*}_{j}(\mathsf{t}_{i},\mathsf{X}_{\mathsf{t}_{i}})-2({\mathcal{C}}_{B_{n}}(\mathsf{Y}_{i}))_{j}]\big|\Big\}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{j=1}^{d}\Big\{4\mathtt{B}\big|({\mathcal{C}}_{B_{n}}(\mathsf{Y}_{i}))_{j}-(\mathsf{Y}_{i})_{j}\big|+4B_{n}\big|(v_{B_{n}})_{j}(\mathsf{t}_{i},\mathsf{X}_{\mathsf{t}_{i}})-v^{*}_{j}(\mathsf{t}_{i},\mathsf{X}_{\mathsf{t}_{i}})\big|\Big\}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{j=1}^{d}\Big\{4\mathtt{B}\big|({\mathcal{C}}_{B_{n}}(\mathsf{Y}_{i}))_{j}-(\mathsf{Y}_{i})_{j}\big|+4B_{n}\mathbb{E}[|({\mathcal{C}}_{B_{n}}(\mathsf{Y}_{i}))_{j}-(\mathsf{Y}_{i})_{j}|\big|\mathsf{X}_{t_{i}}=\mathsf{X}_{\mathsf{t}_{i}}]\Big\}.

Note that
|({\mathcal{C}}_{B_{n}}(\mathsf{Y}_{i}))_{j}-(\mathsf{Y}_{i})_{j}|\leq|(\mathsf{Y}_{i})_{j}|\mathds{1}_{\{|(\mathsf{Y}_{i})_{j}|\geq B_{n}\}}
and
B_{n}\geq\mathtt{B}
. Then it follows that

\displaystyle~\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{D}_{n}}|{\mathcal{D}}(v,\mathsf{S}_{i})-{\mathcal{D}}_{B_{n}}(v,\mathsf{S}_{i})|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{D}_{n}}\Big[\sum\nolimits_{j=1}^{d}\Big\{4\mathtt{B}|(\mathsf{Y_{i}})_{j}|\mathds{1}_{\{|(\mathsf{Y_{i}})_{j}|\geq B_{n}\}}+4B_{n}\mathbb{E}[|(\mathsf{Y}_{i})_{j}|\mathds{1}_{\{|(\mathsf{Y}_{i})_{j}|\geq B_{n}\}}\big|\mathsf{X}_{t_{i}}=\mathsf{X}_{\mathsf{t}_{i}}]\Big\}\Big]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{j=1}^{d}8B_{n}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{D}_{n}}\big[|(\mathsf{Y}_{i})_{j}|\mathds{1}_{\{|(\mathsf{Y}_{i})_{j}|\geq B_{n}\}}\big]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{j=1}^{d}8B_{n}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{D}_{n}}\big[|(\mathsf{Y}_{i})_{j}|^{2}\big]\cdot\mathbb{P}\{|(\mathsf{Y}_{i})_{j}|\geq B_{n}\}.

By Assumption
2
and Remark
5
, the law of
\mathsf{Y}=\mathsf{X}-\mathsf{Z}
is sub-Gaussian.
Then there exist two constants
K_{1}
and
K_{2}
such that for each
i=1,2,\cdots,n
and
j=1,2,\cdots,d
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\{|(\mathsf{Y}_{i})_{j}|\geq B_{n}\}\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{B_{n}^{2}}{K_{1}^{2}}\right),\quad\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{D}_{n}}\big[|(\mathsf{Y}_{i})_{j}|^{2}\big]\leq 2K_{2}^{2}.

The bounds above further imply that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{D}_{n}}{\mathcal{D}}(v,\mathsf{S}_{i})\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{D}_{n}}{\mathcal{D}}_{B_{n}}(v,\mathsf{S}_{i})+\sum\nolimits_{j=1}^{d}8B_{n}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{D}_{n}}\big[|(\mathsf{Y}_{i})_{j}|^{2}\big]\mathbb{P}(|(\mathsf{Y}_{i})_{j}|\geq B_{n})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{D}_{n}}{\mathcal{D}}_{B_{n}}(v,\mathsf{S}_{i})+32dB_{n}K_{2}^{2}\exp\left(-\frac{B_{n}^{2}}{K_{1}^{2}}\right).

Therefore, we conclude that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{D}_{n}}\Big[\frac{1}{n}\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{n}{\mathcal{G}}(\hat{v}_{n},\mathsf{S}_{i})\Big]\leq\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{D}_{n}}\Big[\frac{1}{n}\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{n}{\mathcal{G}}_{B_{n}}(\hat{v}_{n},\mathsf{S}_{i})\Big]+K_{3}B_{n}\exp\left(-\frac{B_{n}^{2}}{K_{1}^{2}}\right),

(C.8)

where the constant
K_{3}
does not depend on
n
and
B_{n}
.

Next, we consider bounding a tail probability of the empirical process.
Before proceeding, we define
({\mathcal{D}}_{B_{n}})_{j}(v,\mathsf{S}_{i}):=[v_{j}(\mathsf{t}_{i},\mathsf{X}_{\mathsf{t}_{i}})-({\mathcal{C}}_{B_{n}}(\mathsf{Y}_{i}))_{j}]^{2}-[(v_{B_{n}})_{j}(\mathsf{t}_{i},\mathsf{X}_{\mathsf{t}_{i}})-({\mathcal{C}}_{B_{n}}(\mathsf{Y}_{i}))_{j}]^{2}
for any
j\in\{1,2,\cdots,d\}
.
It is clear that
{\mathcal{D}}_{B_{n}}(v,\mathsf{S}_{i})=\sum_{j=1}^{d}({\mathcal{D}}_{B_{n}})_{j}(v,\mathsf{S}_{i})
, and we have the following tail probability bounds

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big\{\frac{1}{n}\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{n}{\mathcal{G}}_{B_{n}}(\hat{v}_{n},\mathsf{S}_{i})>t\Big\}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big\{\exists v\in{\mathcal{F}}_{n}:\frac{1}{n}\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{n}{\mathcal{G}}_{B_{n}}(v,\mathsf{S}_{i})>t\Big\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big\{\exists v\in{\mathcal{F}}_{n}:\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{D}_{n}^{\prime}}{\mathcal{D}}_{B_{n}}(v,\mathsf{S}_{i}^{\prime})-\frac{2}{n}\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{n}{\mathcal{D}}_{B_{n}}(v,\mathsf{S}_{i})>t\Big\}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big\{\exists v\in{\mathcal{F}}_{n}\text{ and }\exists 1\leq j\leq d:\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{D}_{n}^{\prime}}({\mathcal{D}}_{B_{n}})_{j}(v,\mathsf{S}_{i}^{\prime})-\frac{2}{n}\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{n}({\mathcal{D}}_{B_{n}})_{j}(v,\mathsf{S}_{i})>\frac{t}{d}\Big\}.

Note that
|({\mathcal{C}}_{B_{n}}(\mathsf{Y}))_{j}|\leq B_{n}
,
\|(v_{B_{n}})_{j}\|_{\infty}\leq B_{n}
, and
B_{n}\geq\mathtt{B}\geq 1
.
By Theorem 11.4 of
Györfi
et al. (2002)
and letting
\epsilon=1/2,\alpha=\beta=t/(2d)
in
Györfi
et al. (2002, Theorem 11.4)
, it yields that for each
n\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big\{\frac{1}{n}\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{n}{\mathcal{G}}_{B_{n}}(\hat{v}_{n},\mathsf{S}_{i})>t\Big\}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big\{\exists v\in{\mathcal{F}}_{n}\text{ and }\exists 1\leq j\leq d:\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{D}_{n}^{\prime}}({\mathcal{D}}_{B_{n}})_{j}(v,\mathsf{S}_{i}^{\prime})-\frac{2}{n}\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{n}({\mathcal{D}}_{B_{n}})_{j}(v,\mathsf{S}_{i})>\frac{t}{d}\Big\}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 14\mathcal{N}_{\infty}(t/(80dB_{n}),{\mathcal{F}}_{n},n)\exp\left(-\frac{tn}{5136dB_{n}^{4}}\right).

(C.9)

Then we use the tail probability bound (
C.9
) to bound the stochastic error.
For any
\alpha_{n}>0
,

\displaystyle~\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{D}_{n}}\Big[\frac{1}{n}\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{n}{\mathcal{G}}_{B_{n}}(\hat{v}_{n},\mathsf{S}_{i})\Big]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\alpha_{n}+\int_{\alpha_{n}}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\Big\{\frac{1}{n}\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{n}{\mathcal{G}}_{B_{n}}(\hat{v}_{n},\mathsf{S}_{i})>t\Big\}\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\alpha_{n}+\int_{\alpha_{n}}^{\infty}14\mathcal{N}_{\infty}(t/(80dB_{n}),{\mathcal{F}}_{n},n)\exp\left(-\frac{tn}{5136dB_{n}^{4}}\right)\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\alpha_{n}+\int_{\alpha_{n}}^{\infty}14\mathcal{N}_{\infty}(\alpha_{n}/(80dB_{n}),{\mathcal{F}}_{n},n)\exp\left(-\frac{tn}{5136dB_{n}^{4}}\right)\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\alpha_{n}+14\mathcal{N}_{\infty}(\alpha_{n}/(80dB_{n}),{\mathcal{F}}_{n},n)\exp\left(-\frac{\alpha_{n}n}{5136dB_{n}^{4}}\right)\frac{5136dB_{n}^{4}}{n}.

By choosing
\alpha_{n}=\log(14\mathcal{N}_{\infty}(1/n,{\mathcal{F}}_{n},n))\cdot 5136dB_{n}^{4}/n
and noticing that
\alpha_{n}/(80dB_{n})\geq 1/n
and
\mathcal{N}_{\infty}(1/n,{\mathcal{F}}_{n},n)\geq\mathcal{N}_{\infty}(\alpha_{n}/(80dB_{n}),{\mathcal{F}}_{n},n)
,
we obtain that

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{D}_{n}}\Big[\frac{1}{n}\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{n}{\mathcal{G}}_{B_{n}}(\hat{v}_{n},\mathsf{S}_{i})\Big]\leq\frac{5136dB_{n}^{4}(\log(14\mathcal{N}_{\infty}(1/n,{\mathcal{F}}_{n},n))+1)}{n}.

(C.10)

Setting
B_{n}\asymp\mathtt{B}\log n
and combining (
C.8
) and (
C.10
), the stochastic error is upper bounded by the covering number of the hypothesis class
{\mathcal{F}}_{n}\subseteq\mathcal{NN}(\mathtt{S},\mathtt{W},\mathtt{D},\mathtt{B},d+1,d)
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{stoc}}\lesssim\frac{d}{n}(\log n)^{4}\mathtt{B}^{4}\log\mathcal{N}_{\infty}(1/n,{\mathcal{F}}_{n},n)

(C.11)

where we omit a constant not depending on
n
or
\mathtt{B}
.
By the relationship between the uniform covering number and the pseudo-dimension of the deep ReLU network class
{\mathcal{F}}_{n}

(
Anthony and
Bartlett, 1999
, Theorem 12.2)
, it yields that for

,

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{\infty}(1/n,{\mathcal{F}}_{n},n)\leq\left(\frac{2e\mathtt{B}n^{2}}{\mathrm{Pdim}({\mathcal{F}}_{n})}\right)^{\mathrm{Pdim}({\mathcal{F}}_{n})},

(C.12)

where
\mathrm{Pdim}({\mathcal{F}}_{n})
denotes the pseudo-dimension of
{\mathcal{F}}_{n}
.
By Theorems 3 and 7 of
Bartlett et al. (2019)
, the pseudo-dimension of the deep ReLU network class
{\mathcal{F}}_{n}\subseteq\mathcal{NN}(\mathtt{S},\mathtt{W},\mathtt{D},\mathtt{B},d+1,d)
satisfies

\displaystyle\mathtt{S}\mathtt{D}\log(\mathtt{S}/\mathtt{D})\lesssim\mathrm{Pdim}({\mathcal{F}}_{n})\lesssim\mathtt{S}\mathtt{D}\log(\mathtt{S}).

(C.13)

Combining (
C.11
), (
C.12
), (
C.13
), and
\mathtt{B}\lesssim A
, we complete the proof by showing

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{stoc}}\lesssim\frac{d}{n}(\log n)^{4}A^{4}\mathtt{S}\mathtt{D}\log(\mathtt{S})\log(An^{2}).

∎

D
Distribution estimation errors

In the appendix, we provide proofs for bounding distribution estimation errors.
The discretization error is bounded in Lemma
4.1
.

By the definition of Wasserstein-2 distance, it holds

\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\hat{p}_{t},\tilde{p}_{t})\leq\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\|\hat{X}_{t}(x)-\tilde{X}_{t}(x)\|_{2}^{2}p_{0}(x)\mathrm{d}x=:E_{t}.

It suffices to consider the propagation of error
E_{t}
in time
t\in[0,1-\underline{t}]
.
Recall that
(\hat{X}_{t})_{t\in[0,1-\underline{t}]}
is the linear interpolation of
(\hat{X}_{t_{k}})_{0\leq k\leq K}
, thus it is piecewise linear over
[0,1-\underline{t}]
.
To ease the arguments, we consider the dynamics of
E_{t}
over each time subinterval
[t_{k-1},t_{k}]
for
1\leq k\leq K
.
For
t\in[t_{k-1},t_{k}]
, it holds that

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}E_{t}}{\mathrm{d}t}=

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}2\langle\hat{v}_{n}(t_{k-1},\hat{X}_{t_{k-1}}(x))-\hat{v}_{n}(t,\tilde{X}_{t}(x)),\hat{X}_{t}(x)-\tilde{X}_{t}(x)\rangle p_{0}(x)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}2\langle\hat{v}_{n}(t_{k-1},\hat{X}_{t_{k-1}}(x))-\hat{v}_{n}(t,\hat{X}_{t_{k-1}}(x)),\hat{X}_{t}(x)-\tilde{X}_{t}(x)\rangle p_{0}(x)\mathrm{d}x

(D.1)

\displaystyle+\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}2\langle\hat{v}_{n}(t,\hat{X}_{t_{k-1}}(x))-\hat{v}_{n}(t,\hat{X}_{t}(x)),\hat{X}_{t}(x)-\tilde{X}_{t}(x)\rangle p_{0}(x)\mathrm{d}x

(D.2)

\displaystyle+\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}2\langle\hat{v}_{n}(t,\hat{X}_{t}(x))-\hat{v}_{n}(t,\tilde{X}_{t}(x)),\hat{X}_{t}(x)-\tilde{X}_{t}(x)\rangle p_{0}(x)\mathrm{d}x

(D.3)

For term (
D.1
), the basic inequality
2\langle a,b\rangle\leq\|a\|_{2}^{2}+\|b\|_{2}^{2}
and the fact that
\hat{v}_{n}
is
\mathtt{L}_{t}
-Lipschitz continuous in
t
imply that

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}2\langle\hat{v}_{n}(t_{k-1},\hat{X}_{t_{k-1}}(x))-\hat{v}_{n}(t,\hat{X}_{t_{k-1}}(x)),\hat{X}_{t}(x)-\tilde{X}_{t}(x)\rangle p_{0}(x)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\|\hat{v}_{n}(t_{k-1},\hat{X}_{t_{k-1}}(x))-\hat{v}_{n}(t,\hat{X}_{t_{k-1}}(x))\|_{2}^{2}p_{0}(x)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle+\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\|\hat{X}_{t}(x)-\tilde{X}_{t}(x)\|_{2}^{2}p_{0}(x)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\ d\mathtt{L}_{t}^{2}(t-t_{k-1})^{2}+E_{t}.

(D.4)

Note that
\hat{X}_{t}(x)=\hat{X}_{t_{k-1}}(x)+(t-t_{k-1})\hat{v}_{n}(t_{k-1},\hat{X}_{t_{k-1}}(x))
.
For term (
D.2
), we use
2\langle a,b\rangle\leq\|a\|_{2}^{2}+\|b\|_{2}^{2}
and the fact that
\hat{v}_{n}
is
\mathtt{L}_{x}
-Lipschitz continuous in
x
to deduce that

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}2\langle\hat{v}_{n}(t,\hat{X}_{t_{k-1}}(x))-\hat{v}_{n}(t,\hat{X}_{t}(x)),\hat{X}_{t}(x)-\tilde{X}_{t}(x)\rangle p_{0}(x)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\|\hat{v}_{n}(t,\hat{X}_{t_{k-1}}(x))-\hat{v}_{n}(t,\hat{X}_{t}(x))\|_{2}^{2}p_{0}(x)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle+\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\|\hat{X}_{t}(x)-\tilde{X}_{t}(x)\|_{2}^{2}p_{0}(x)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\ d\mathtt{L}_{x}^{2}(t-t_{k-1})^{2}\|\hat{v}_{n}\|_{L^{\infty}([0,1-\underline{t}]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d})}^{2}+E_{t}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\ d\mathtt{L}_{x}^{2}\mathtt{B}^{2}(t-t_{k-1})^{2}+E_{t}.

(D.5)

For term (
D.3
), by the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality and the fact that
\hat{v}_{n}
is
\mathtt{L}_{x}
-Lipschitz continuous in
x
, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}2\langle\hat{v}_{n}(t,\hat{X}_{t}(x))-\hat{v}_{n}(t,\tilde{X}_{t}(x)),\hat{X}_{t}(x)-\tilde{X}_{t}(x)\rangle p_{0}(x)\mathrm{d}x\leq 2\mathtt{L}_{x}E_{t}.

(D.6)

Combining (
D.4
), (
D.5
), and (
D.6
), we obtain

\frac{\mathrm{d}E_{t}}{\mathrm{d}t}\leq 2(\mathtt{L}_{x}+1)E_{t}+d(\mathtt{L}_{x}^{2}\mathtt{B}^{2}+\mathtt{L}_{t}^{2})(t-t_{k-1})^{2}\quad\text{ for $t\in[t_{k-1},t_{k}]$}.

By Grönwall’s inequality, it further yields

e^{-2(\mathtt{L}_{x}+1)t_{k}}E_{t_{k}}-e^{-2(\mathtt{L}_{x}+1)t_{k-1}}E_{t_{k-1}}\leq\frac{1}{3}d(\mathtt{L}_{x}^{2}\mathtt{B}^{2}+\mathtt{L}_{t}^{2})(t_{k}-t_{k-1})^{3}.

Taking sum over
k=1,2,\cdots,K
and letting
t_{K}=1-\underline{t}
, we obtain

E_{1-\underline{t}}\leq\frac{1}{3}de^{2(\mathtt{L}_{x}+1)(1-\underline{t})}(\mathtt{L}_{x}^{2}\mathtt{B}^{2}+\mathtt{L}_{t}^{2})\sum\nolimits_{k=1}^{K}(t_{k}-t_{k-1})^{3}.

Let
\Upsilon\equiv t_{k}-t_{k-1}
for
k=1,2,\cdots,K
.
It implies that

\mathcal{W}_{2}(\hat{p}_{1-\underline{t}},\tilde{p}_{1-\underline{t}})=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{d}e^{\mathtt{L}_{x}}(\mathtt{L}_{x}\mathtt{B}+\mathtt{L}_{t})\Upsilon\right).

This completes the proof.
∎

We denote by
L^{\infty}(\Omega)
the standard Lebesgue space on
\Omega
with
L^{\infty}
norm.

E.1
Sobolev spaces

We list some definitions for defining Sobolev spaces.

Definition E.1

(Sobolev space)
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Then the Sobolev space
W^{n,\infty}(\Omega)
is defined by

\displaystyle W^{n,\infty}(\Omega):=\{f\in L^{\infty}(\Omega):D^{\alpha}f\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)\text{ for all $\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}$ with $\|\alpha\|_{1}\leq n$ }\}.

Moreover, for any
f\in W^{n,\infty}(\Omega)
, we define the Sobolev norm
\|\cdot\|_{W^{n,\infty}(\Omega)}
by

\displaystyle\|f\|_{W^{n,\infty}(\Omega)}:=\max_{0\leq\|\alpha\|_{1}\leq n}\|D^{\alpha}f\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}.

Definition E.2

(Sobolev semi-norm)
.

Let
n,k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
with
k\leq n
.
For any
f\in W^{n,\infty}(\Omega)
, we define the Sobolev semi-norm
|\cdot|_{W^{k,\infty}(\Omega)}
by

\displaystyle|f|_{W^{k,\infty}(\Omega)}:=\max_{\|\alpha\|_{1}=k}\|D^{\alpha}f\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}.

Definition E.3

(Vector-valued Sobolev space)
.

Let
n,k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
with
k\leq n
, and
m\in\mathbb{N}
.
Then the vector-valued Sobolev space
W^{n,\infty}(\Omega;{\mathbb{R}}^{m})
is defined by

\displaystyle W^{n,\infty}(\Omega;{\mathbb{R}}^{m}):=\{(f_{1},f_{2},\cdots,f_{m}):f_{i}\in W^{n,\infty}(\Omega),1\leq i\leq m\}

Moreover, the Sobolev norm
\|\cdot\|_{W^{n,\infty}(\Omega;{\mathbb{R}}^{m})}
is defined by

\displaystyle\|f\|_{W^{n,\infty}(\Omega;{\mathbb{R}}^{m})}:=\max_{1\leq i\leq m}\|f_{i}\|_{W^{n,\infty}(\Omega)},

and the Sobolev semi-norm
|\cdot|_{W^{n,\infty}(\Omega;{\mathbb{R}}^{m})}
is defined by

\displaystyle|f|_{W^{k,\infty}(\Omega;{\mathbb{R}}^{m})}:=\max_{1\leq i\leq m}|f_{i}|_{W^{k,\infty}(\Omega)}.

E.2
Averaged Taylor polynomials

The following definitions and lemmas on averaged Taylor polynomials are collected from Chapter 4 of
Brenner and
Scott (2008)
.

Definition E.4

(Averaged Taylor polynomials)
.

Let
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
be a bounded, open subset and
f\in W^{m-1,\infty}(\Omega)
for some
m\in\mathbb{N}
, and let
x_{0}\in\Omega,r>0,B:=\mathbb{B}^{d}(x_{0},r,\|\cdot\|_{2})
with its closure
\bar{B}
compact in
\Omega
.
The Taylor polynomial of order
m
of
f
averaged over
B
is defined as

\displaystyle Q^{m}f(x):=\int_{B}T_{y}^{m}f(x)\phi(y)\mathrm{d}y,

where

\displaystyle T_{y}^{m}f(x):=\sum_{\|\alpha\|_{1}<m}\frac{1}{\alpha!}D^{\alpha}f(y)(x-y)^{\alpha},

and
\phi
is an arbitrary cut-off function supported in
\bar{B}
being infinitely differentiable, that is,
\phi\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
with
\mathrm{supp}(\phi)=\bar{B}
and
\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\phi(x)\mathrm{d}x=1
.

Example 1
.

Let
\psi(x)
be defined by

\displaystyle\psi(x):=\begin{cases}\exp\{-1/(1-(\|x-x_{0}\|_{2}/r)^{2})\},&\text{if }\|x-x_{0}\|_{2}<r,\\
0,&\text{if }\|x-x_{0}\|_{2}\geq r,\end{cases}

and let
c=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\psi(x)\mathrm{d}x
with
c>0
, then
\phi(x)=\psi(x)/c
is an example of the cut-off function on the ball
B:=\mathbb{B}^{d}(x_{0},r,\|\cdot\|_{2})
.
Moreover, it holds that
\|\phi\|_{L^{\infty}(B)}\leq C(d)r^{-d}
where
C(d)>0
is a constant in
d
.

Example 2
.

Let
\phi(x)
be defined by

\displaystyle\phi(x)=\begin{cases}\pi^{-d/2}\Gamma(d/2+1)r^{-d},&\text{if }\|x-x_{0}\|_{2}<r,\\
0,&\text{if }\|x-x_{0}\|_{2}\geq r,\end{cases}

then
\phi(x)
is another example of the cut-off function where
\phi
puts constant weight over the ball
B:=\mathbb{B}^{d}(x_{0},r,\|\cdot\|_{2})
.

Lemma E.5
(Lemma B.9 in
Gühring, Kutyniok and
Petersen (2020)
).

Let
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
be a bounded, open subset and
f\in W^{m-1,\infty}(\Omega)
for some
m\in\mathbb{N}
, and let
x_{0}\in\Omega,r>0,B:=\mathbb{B}^{d}(x_{0},r,\|\cdot\|_{2})
with its closure
\bar{B}
compact in
\Omega
.
The Taylor polynomial of order
m
of
f
averaged over
B
denoted by
Q^{m}f(x)
is a polynomial of degree less than
m
in
x
.

Definition E.6

(Star-shaped set)
.

Let
\Omega,B\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. We say
\Omega
is star-shaped with respect to
B
if for all
x\in\Omega
, the closed convex hull of
\{x\}\cup B
is a subset of
\Omega
.

Definition E.7

(Chunkiness parameter)
.

Suppose that
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
has diameter
d_{\Omega}
and is star-shaped with respect to a ball
B
.
Let

\displaystyle r_{\max}:=\sup\{r>0:\Omega\text{ is star-shaped with respect to a ball of radius }r\}.

Then the chunkiness parameter of
\Omega
is defined by
\gamma:=d_{\Omega}/r_{\max}
.

Lemma E.8
(Bramble-Hilbert, Lemma 4.3.8 in
Brenner and
Scott (2008)
).

Let
B
be a ball in
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
such that
\Omega
is star-shaped with respect to
B
and such that its radius
r>r_{\max}/2
, where
r_{\max}
is defined in Definition
E.7
.
Moreover, let
d_{\Omega}
be the diameter of
\Omega
,
\gamma
be the chunkiness parameter of
\Omega
, and
Q^{m}f
be the Taylor polynomial of order
m
of
f
averaged over
B
for any
f\in W^{m,\infty}(\Omega)
.
Then there exists a constant
C(d,m,\gamma)>0
such that

\displaystyle|f-Q^{m}f|_{W^{k,\infty}(\Omega)}\leq C(d,m,\gamma)d_{\Omega}^{m-k}|f|_{W^{m,\infty}(\Omega)},\quad k=0,1,\cdots,m.

F
Additional lemmas on approximation

Lemma F.1
(Corollary B.5 in
Gühring, Kutyniok and
Petersen (2020)
).

Let
d,m\in\mathbb{N}
and
\Omega_{1}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\Omega_{2}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
both be open, bounded, and convex.
If
f\in W^{1,\infty}(\Omega_{1};{\mathbb{R}}^{m})
and
g\in W^{1,\infty}(\Omega_{2})
with
\mathrm{rad}(f)\subset\Omega_{2}
, then for the composition
g\circ f
, it holds that
g\circ f\in W^{1,\infty}(\Omega_{1})
and we have

\displaystyle|g\circ f|_{W^{1,\infty}(\Omega_{1})}\leq\sqrt{d}m|g|_{W^{1,\infty}(\Omega_{2})}|f|_{W^{1,\infty}(\Omega_{1};{\mathbb{R}}^{m})}

and

\displaystyle\|g\circ f\|_{W^{1,\infty}(\Omega_{1})}\leq\sqrt{d}m\max\{\|g\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega_{2})},|g|_{W^{1,\infty}(\Omega_{2})}|f|_{W^{1,\infty}(\Omega_{1};{\mathbb{R}}^{m})}\}.

Lemma F.2
(Corollary B.6 in
Gühring, Kutyniok and
Petersen (2020)
).

Let
f\in W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)
and
g\in W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)
.
Then
fg\in W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)
and we have

\displaystyle|fg|_{W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)}\leq|f|_{W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)}\|g\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}+\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}|g|_{W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)}

and

\displaystyle\|fg\|_{W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)}\leq\|f\|_{W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)}\|g\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}+\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\|g\|_{W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)}.

Lemma F.3
(Proposition 4 in
Yang, Yang and Xiang (2023)
).

For any
N,L\in\mathbb{N}
and
a>0
, there exists a deep ReLU network
\phi_{\times,a}
with width
15N
and depth
2L
such that

\|\phi_{\times,a}\|_{W^{1,\infty}((-a,a)^{2})}\leq 12a^{2}
and

\displaystyle\|\phi_{\times,a}(x,y)-xy\|_{W^{1,\infty}((-a,a)^{2})}\leq 6a^{2}N^{-L}.

Furthermore, it holds that

\displaystyle\phi_{\times,a}(0,y)=\frac{\partial\phi_{\times,a}(0,y)}{\partial y}=0\text{ for $y\in(-a,a)$}.

Lemma F.4
.

For any
N,L\in\mathbb{N}
and
a,b>0
, there exists a deep ReLU network
\phi_{\times,(a,b)}
with width
15N
and depth
2L
such that

\|\phi_{\times,(a,b)}\|_{W^{1,\infty}((-a,a)\times(-b,b))}\leq 12ab
and

\displaystyle\|\phi_{\times,(a,b)}(x,y)-xy\|_{W^{1,\infty}((-a,a)\times(-b,b))}\leq 6abN^{-L}.

Furthermore, it holds that

\displaystyle\phi_{\times,(a,b)}(x,0)=\frac{\partial\phi_{\times,(a,b)}(x,0)}{\partial x}=0\text{ for $x\in(-a,a)$}.

The proof idea is similar to that of Proposition 4 in
Yang, Yang and Xiang (2023)
.
∎

Lemma F.5
(Proposition 5 in
Yang, Yang and Xiang (2023)
).

For any
N,L,s\in\mathbb{N}
with
s\geq 2
, there exists a deep ReLU network
\phi_{\times}
with width
\mathcal{O}(s\vee N)
and depth

\displaystyle\|\phi_{\times}(x)-x_{1}x_{2}\cdots x_{s}\|_{W^{1,\infty}((0,1)^{s})}\lesssim s(N+1)^{-7sL}.

Furthermore, for any

, if

\displaystyle\phi_{\times}(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{i-1},0,x_{i+1},\cdots,x_{s})=\frac{\partial\phi_{\times}(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{i-1},0,x_{i+1},\cdots,x_{s})}{\partial x_{j}}=0,\ i\neq j.

Lemma F.6
(Lemma 6 in
Yang, Yang and Xiang (2023)
).

Let
\{g_{m}\}_{m\in\{1,2\}^{d}}
be the partition of unity given in Definition
B.4
.
Then it satisfies:

(1)

\sum_{m\in\{1,2\}^{d}}g_{m}(x)=1
for every
x\in[0,1]^{d}
;

(2)

\mathrm{supp}(g_{m})\cap[0,1]^{d}\subset\Omega_{m}

B.3
;

(3)

For any

and

.

Lemma F.7
(Lemma 7 in
Yang, Yang and Xiang (2023)
).

For any
\chi(x)\in W^{1,\infty}((0,1)^{d})
, let
B_{5}:=\max\{\|\chi\|_{W^{1,\infty}((0,1)^{d})},\|\phi_{m}\|_{W^{1,\infty}((0,1)^{d})}\}
.
Then for any
m\in\{1,2\}^{d}
, it holds that

\displaystyle\|\phi_{m}(x)\cdot\chi(x)\|_{W^{1,\infty}((0,1)^{d})}=\|\phi_{m}(x)\cdot\chi(x)\|_{W^{1,\infty}(\Omega_{m})},

\displaystyle\|\phi_{m}(x)\cdot\chi(x)-\phi_{\times,B_{5}}(\phi_{m}(x),\chi(x))\|_{W^{1,\infty}((0,1)^{d})}

\displaystyle=\|\phi_{m}(x)\cdot\chi(x)-\phi_{\times,B_{5}}(\phi_{m}(x),\chi(x))\|_{W^{1,\infty}(\Omega_{m})}.

Lemma F.8
.

For any
\chi(x)\in L^{\infty}((0,1)^{d})
, let
B_{6}:=\max\{\|\chi\|_{L^{\infty}((0,1)^{d})},\|\phi_{m}\|_{L^{\infty}((0,1)^{d})}\}
.
Then for any
m\in\{1,2\}^{d}
, it holds that

\displaystyle\|\phi_{m}(x)\cdot\chi(x)\|_{L^{\infty}((0,1)^{d})}=\|\phi_{m}(x)\cdot\chi(x)\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega_{m})},

\displaystyle\|\phi_{m}(x)\cdot\chi(x)-\phi_{\times,B_{6}}(\phi_{m}(x),\chi(x))\|_{L^{\infty}((0,1)^{d})}

\displaystyle=\|\phi_{m}(x)\cdot\chi(x)-\phi_{\times,B_{6}}(\phi_{m}(x),\chi(x))\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega_{m})}.

The proof is similar to that of
(
Yang, Yang and Xiang, 2023
, Lemma 7)
.
To prove the equalities, we need to show that

\displaystyle\|\phi_{m}(x)\cdot\chi(x)\|_{L^{\infty}((0,1)^{d}\setminus\Omega_{m})}=0\quad\text{and}\quad\|\phi_{\times,B_{6}}(\phi_{m}(x),\chi(x))\|_{L^{\infty}((0,1)^{d}\setminus\Omega_{m})}=0.

In Lemma
B.5
, it is shown that

\displaystyle\phi_{m}(x):=\phi_{\times}(g_{m_{1}}(x_{1}),g_{m_{2}}(x_{2}),\cdots,g_{m_{d}}(x_{d}))=0

where
g_{m}(x)=[g_{m_{1}}(x_{1}),g_{m_{2}}(x_{2}),\cdots,g_{m_{d}}(x_{d})]^{\top}
is defined in Definition
B.4
.
Then by Lemma
F.6
, for any
x=[x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{d}]^{\top}\in(0,1)^{d}\setminus\Omega_{m}
, there exists
m_{j}
such that

g_{m_{j}}(x_{j})=0
.
By the definition of
\phi_{m}(x)
and Lemma
F.5
, it yields that

\displaystyle\phi_{m}(x)=0,\quad\forall x\in(0,1)^{d}\setminus\Omega_{m}.

Therefore, for any
x\in(0,1)^{d}\setminus\Omega_{m}
, it holds that
|\phi_{m}(x)\cdot\chi(x)|=0
.

Similarly, for any
x\in(0,1)^{d}\setminus\Omega_{m}
, it holds that

\displaystyle\phi_{\times,B_{6}}(\phi_{m}(x),\chi(x))=\phi_{\times,B_{6}}(0,\chi(x))=0.

This completes the proof.
∎

Lemma G.1
(Brascamp-Lieb inequality).

Let
\mu(\mathrm{d}x)=\exp(-U(x))\mathrm{d}x
be a probability distribution on a convex set
\Omega\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
whose potential function
U:\Omega\to{\mathbb{R}}
is of class
C^{2}
and strictly convex.
Then for every locally Lipschitz function
f\in L^{2}(\Omega,\mu)
,

\mathrm{Var}_{\mathsf{X}\sim\mu}(f)\leq\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{X}\sim\mu}\left[\langle\nabla_{x}f(\mathsf{X}),(\nabla^{2}_{x}U(\mathsf{X}))^{-1}\nabla_{x}f(\mathsf{X})\rangle\right].

(G.1)

Lemma G.2
(Tweedie’s formula).

Suppose that
\mathsf{X}\sim\mu
and
\epsilon\sim\gamma_{d,\sigma^{2}}
.
Let
\mathsf{Y}=\mathsf{X}+\epsilon
and
p(y)
be the marginal density of
\mathsf{Y}
.
Then it holds that
\mathbb{E}[\mathsf{X}|\mathsf{Y}=y]=y+\sigma^{2}\nabla_{y}\log p(y)
.

Lemma G.3
(Hatsell-Nolte identity).

Suppose that
\mathsf{X}\sim\mu
and
\epsilon\sim\gamma_{d,\sigma^{2}}
.
Let
\mathsf{Y}=\mathsf{X}+\epsilon
and
p(y)
be the marginal density of
\mathsf{Y}
.
Then it holds that

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}(\mathsf{X}|\mathsf{Y}=y)=\sigma^{2}\nabla_{y}\mathbb{E}[\mathsf{X}|\mathsf{Y}=y]=\sigma^{2}\mathrm{I}_{d}+\sigma^{4}\nabla^{2}_{y}\log p(y).
