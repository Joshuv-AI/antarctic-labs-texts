Hinged-rulers fold in 2 − Θ ⁡ ( 1 2 n / 4 ) 2-\Theta(\frac{1}{2^{n/4}})
Colin Tang

Abstract
A hinged-ruler is a sequence of line segments in the plane joined end-to-end with hinges, so each hinge joins exactly two segments, the first segment and last segment are adjacent to only one hinge each, and all other segments are adjacent to exactly two hinges. Hopcroft, Joseph, and Whitesides first posed the hinged-ruler-folding problem in [ HJW85 ] : given a hinged-ruler and a real number K K , can the hinged-ruler be folded so as to fit within a one-dimensional interval of length K K ? We show that if the segment lengths are constrained to be real numbers in the interval [ 0 , 1 ] [0,1] , then we can always fold the hinged-ruler so as to fit within a one-dimensional interval of length 2 − Ω ⁡ ( 1 2 n / 4 ) 2-\Omega(\frac{1}{2^{n/4}}) . On the other hand, we give a construction for a hinged-ruler which cannot be folded into any one-dimensional interval of length smaller than 2 − O ⁡ ( 1 2 n / 4 ) 2-O(\frac{1}{2^{n/4}}) .

Now define the

to be

f(a_{1},a_{2},\cdots,a_{n})\coloneqq\min_{e_{1},e_{2},\cdots,e_{n}\in\{-1,+1\}}g(e_{1},e_{2},\cdots,e_{n},a_{1},a_{2},\cdots,a_{n});

Define the
fit

p_{n}
to be

p_{n}\coloneqq\max_{a_{1},a_{2},\cdots,a_{n}\in[0,1]}f(a_{1},a_{2},\cdots,a_{n});

i.e. the fit is the largest real number

such that there exists a choice of
(a_{1},a_{2},\cdots,a_{n})
such that no matter how we choose the signs
(e_{1},e_{2},\cdots,e_{n})
, the range is forced to be at least

. Since we may pad excess range with zeroes, we conclude that
p_{n}\leq p_{n+1}
; i.e. the fit is monotonically nondecreasing in

.

Note that
f
is a continuous piecewise-linear function of
(a_{1},a_{2},\cdots,a_{n})
. More specifically,

is linear on each domain

, where the domain is defined to be the set of
(a_{1},a_{2},\cdots,a_{n})
such that

is minimized when
(e_{1},e_{2},\cdots,e_{n})=(e_{1}^{\prime},e_{2}^{\prime},\cdots,e_{n}^{\prime})
, and for that choice of
(e_{1},e_{2},\cdots,e_{n})
, we have
\max_{k}s_{k}=s_{i}
and
\min_{k}s_{k}=s_{j}
. Because each domain may be described as the intersection of half-planes whose defining equations have rational coefficients, and because
f
itself takes the form of a rational linear combination of the
(a_{1},a_{2},\cdots,a_{n})
on each domain, we conclude that the fit is a rational number.

In 1985, Hopcroft, Joseph, and Whitesides posed the following question in
[
HJW85
]
, in the context of robotic motion planning: given
(a_{1},a_{2},\cdots,a_{n})

Ruler-Folding
as follows: given rationals
(a_{1},a_{2},\cdots,

a_{n})

(a_{1},a_{2},\cdots,a_{n},

NP
-complete. The authors also show that the fit

is always at most
2
, by using a greedy algorithm. Indeed, given prescribed step sizes
(a_{1},a_{2},\cdots,a_{n})
, we may choose the
(e_{1},e_{2},\cdots,e_{n})
iteratively such that

holds, which implies that all the

are in the interval
[-1,1]
of length

. The authors also give an explicit construction showing that
p_{n}\geq 2-O(\frac{1}{n})
.

In 2005, Călinescu and Dumitrescu considered the two-dimensional ruler-folding problem in
[
CD05
]
, where the
e_{i}
are allowed to be arbitrary unit vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. In the hinged-ruler interpretation, the limitation that the hinges must follow
0^{\circ}
or

bending angles is removed, and each hinge may take on any bending angle from
0^{\circ}
through
360^{\circ}

The resulting hinged-ruler will not, in general, be contained in a one-dimensional interval, but we may define its two-dimensional range to be simply the smallest possible diameter of a convex set containing the hinged-ruler. The authors prove that the two-dimensional step-cover is always equal to
\max_{k}a_{k}
. In other words, any hinged-ruler may be folded so as to be contained in a convex set of diameter
1
(and the convex set in particular may be taken to be a Reuleaux triangle). We will not consider the two-dimensional problem in this paper, choosing to focus on the one-dimensional problem.

Proof of Theorem

.
Assume that
n=4m
is a multiple of
4
, and define
\epsilon=\frac{1}{2^{m+3}-7}
.

Let
a_{1},a_{2},\cdots,a_{n}\in[0,1]
be arbitrary; we aim to show that we may choose
(e_{1},e_{2},\cdots,e_{n})
such that the resulting range is at most
2-\epsilon
. First note that if there exists
i
such that
a_{i}+a_{i+1}\leq 1
, we may replace
(a_{1},a_{2},\cdots,a_{n})
with

(a_{1},a_{2},\cdots,a_{i-1},a_{i}+a_{i+1},a_{i+2},\cdots,a_{n},1).

i

a_{i}+a_{i+1}\leq 1

a_{i}+a_{i+1}>1

If
q:[-1,1]\rightarrow[0,+\infty)
is a probability distribution (for our purposes, this will be a piecewise-constant function with integral
1
), define
\Gamma_{-}(q,a)
to be the following measurable function:

•

If
-1\leq x\leq 1-2a
, then
(\Gamma_{-}(q,a))(x)=\frac{1}{2}q(x+a)
.

•

If
1-2a\leq x\leq 1-a

(\Gamma_{-}(q,a))(x)=q(x+a)
.

•

If
1-a\leq x\leq 1
, then
(\Gamma_{-}(q,a))(x)=0
.

Similarly, we define
\Gamma_{+}(q,a)
to be the following measurable function:

•

If
-1+2a\leq x\leq 1
, then
(\Gamma_{+}(q,a))(x)=\frac{1}{2}q(x-a)
.

•

If
-1+a\leq x\leq-1+2a
, then
(\Gamma_{+}(q,a))(x)=q(x-a)
.

•

If
-1\leq x\leq-1+a
, then
(\Gamma_{+}(q,a))(x)=0
.

Note that
\Gamma_{-}(q,a)
and
\Gamma_{+}(q,a)
are well-defined almost everywhere. Finally, we define the probability distribution
\Phi(q,a)
to be simply
\Phi(q,a)\coloneqq\Gamma_{-}(q,a)+\Gamma_{+}(q,a)
. It is easily verified that if
\int q\,dx=1
, then for any
a\in[0,1]
, we have
\int\Phi(q,a)\,dx=1
, so
\Phi(q,a)
is indeed a probability distribution.

Intuitively, we may describe
\Phi(q,a)
as follows. A particle
P
is initially placed at a location
x
in
[-1,1]
, where the probability distribution of
x
is given by
q
. Then:

•

If
-1\leq x\leq-1+a
,
P
moves to the right by a distance of exactly
a
with probability
1
.

•

If
-1+a\leq x\leq 1-a
,
P
moves left or right by a distance of exactly
a
, with the two directions chosen with probability
\frac{1}{2}
each.

•

If
1-a\leq x\leq 1
,
P
moves to the left by a distance of exactly
a
with probability
1
.

Then
\Phi(q,a)
is the probability distribution of the final position

of
P
.

Claim
.

Let
q:[-1,1]\rightarrow[0,+\infty)
be a probability distribution and let

be such that

. Define
q^{\prime}=\Phi(q,b)

\max_{-1\leq x\leq 1}q^{\prime\prime}(x)\leq 2\max_{-1\leq x\leq 1}q(x).

In other words,
q_{4m}

as “concentrated” as
q_{2m}

Proof.

We may assume that
q
is the uniform distribution, so
q(x)=\frac{1}{2}
for all
-1\leq x\leq 1
. We wish to show that
q^{\prime\prime}(x)\leq 1
for all
-1\leq x\leq 1
. This is a tedious case check; details are given below.

Note that for each
x
, we have
(\Gamma_{-}(q,b))(x)\leq\frac{1}{2}
and
(\Gamma_{+}(q,b))(x)\leq\frac{1}{2}
, so

q^{\prime}(x)=(\Phi(q,b))(x)=(\Gamma_{-}(q,b))(x)+(\Gamma_{+}(q,b))(x)\leq 1

for each
x
. Thus, for each
x
, if
(\Gamma_{-}(q^{\prime},b^{\prime}))(x)=0
or
(\Gamma_{+}(q^{\prime},b^{\prime}))(x)=0
, we would automatically have
q^{\prime\prime}(x)\leq 1
, as desired. It remains to check the case where
(\Gamma_{-}(q^{\prime},b^{\prime}))(x)>0
and
(\Gamma_{+}(q^{\prime},b^{\prime}))(x)>0
; this implies that
-1+b^{\prime}\leq x\leq 1-b^{\prime}
.

First suppose that
b\leq b^{\prime}
. Then note that since we have
-1+b^{\prime}\leq x\leq 1-b^{\prime}
, we also have
(\Gamma_{-}(q,b))(x-b^{\prime})=\frac{1}{2}q(x+b-b^{\prime})=\frac{1}{4}
and
(\Gamma_{+}(q,b))(x+b^{\prime})=\frac{1}{2}q(x-b+b^{\prime})=\frac{1}{4}
. Note that
b+b^{\prime}>1
, so at least one of
(\Gamma_{+}(q,b))(x-b^{\prime})
and
(\Gamma_{-}(q,b))(x+b^{\prime})
is equal to
0
. Furthermore,
(\Gamma_{+}(q,b))(x-b^{\prime})\leq\frac{1}{2}
and
(\Gamma_{-}(q,b))(x+b^{\prime})\leq\frac{1}{2}
, so their sum

(\Gamma_{+}(q,b))(x-b^{\prime})+(\Gamma_{-}(q,b))(x+b^{\prime})\leq\frac{1}{2}+0=\frac{1}{2}.

We conclude that

\displaystyle q^{\prime\prime}(x)

\displaystyle=(\Phi(q^{\prime},b^{\prime}))(x)

\displaystyle=(\Gamma_{-}(q^{\prime},b^{\prime}))(x)+(\Gamma_{+}(q^{\prime},b^{\prime}))(x)

\displaystyle\leq q^{\prime}(x-b^{\prime})+q^{\prime}(x+b^{\prime})

\displaystyle=(\Phi(q,b))(x-b^{\prime})+(\Phi(q,b))(x+b^{\prime})

\displaystyle=(\Gamma_{-}(q,b))(x-b^{\prime})+(\Gamma_{+}(q,b))(x-b^{\prime})+(\Gamma_{-}(q,b))(x+b^{\prime})+(\Gamma_{+}(q,b))(x+b^{\prime})

\displaystyle=(\Gamma_{-}(q,b))(x-b^{\prime})+(\Gamma_{+}(q,b))(x+b^{\prime})+((\Gamma_{+}(q,b))(x-b^{\prime})+(\Gamma_{-}(q,b))(x+b^{\prime}))

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{4}+\frac{1}{4}+\frac{1}{2}=1

as desired.

Now suppose that
b^{\prime}\leq b
. Observe that if
1-2b^{\prime}\leq x\leq 1-b^{\prime}
, then we have
1-b^{\prime}\leq x+b^{\prime}\leq 1
, which implies
1-b\leq x+b^{\prime}\leq 1
. Thus,
(\Gamma_{-}(q,b))(x+b^{\prime})=0
, which implies

\displaystyle(\Gamma_{-}(q^{\prime},b^{\prime}))(x)

\displaystyle\leq q^{\prime}(x+b^{\prime})

\displaystyle=(\Phi(q,b))(x+b^{\prime})

\displaystyle=(\Gamma_{-}(q,b))(x+b^{\prime})+(\Gamma_{+}(q,b))(x+b^{\prime})

\displaystyle=(\Gamma_{+}(q,b))(x+b^{\prime})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}.

On the other hand, if

and
-1+b^{\prime}\leq x\leq 1-2b^{\prime}
, then we have
(\Gamma_{-}(q^{\prime},b^{\prime}))(x)=\frac{1}{2}q^{\prime}(x+b^{\prime})
, which implies

\displaystyle(\Gamma_{-}(q^{\prime},b^{\prime}))(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}q^{\prime}(x+b^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(\Phi(q,b))(x+b^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}((\Gamma_{-}(q,b))(x+b^{\prime})+(\Gamma_{+}(q,b))(x+b^{\prime}))

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}.

. By reflecting the argument about

\delta=\frac{1}{3\cdot 2^{m-1}-1}
. We will explicitly construct
(a_{1},a_{2},\cdots,a_{n})
such that no matter how
(e_{1},e_{2},\cdots,e_{n})
are chosen, the resulting range is at least
2-\delta
.

Our construction will be as follows. For each
1\leq i\leq n
:

•

If
i
is odd, then
a_{i}=1
.

•

If
i=2m
, then
a_{i}=1-2^{m-1}\delta
.

•

If
2\leq i\leq 2m-2
and
i
is even, then
a_{i}=1-2^{(i/2)-1}\delta
.

•

If
2m+2\leq i\leq 4m-2
and
i
is even, then
a_{i}=1-2^{(2m-1)-(i/2)}\delta
.

For example, if
m=4
then our
(a_{1},a_{2},\cdots,a_{n})
will be

\left(1,\frac{22}{23},1,\frac{21}{23},1,\frac{19}{23},1,\frac{15}{23},1,\frac{19}{23},1,\frac{21}{23},1,\frac{22}{23},1\right).

Assume without loss of generality (by flipping the signs if needed) that
e_{2m}=-1
. If
e_{2m+1}=-1
also, then we cannot avoid having a range at least
2-\delta
even if we restrict our attention to only
(a_{2m},a_{2m+1},\cdots,a_{n})
. Similarly, if
e_{2m-1}=-1
, then we cannot avoid having a range at least
2-\delta
even if we restrict our attention to only
(a_{1},a_{2},\cdots,a_{2m})
. In fact, for any even
i\geq 2m
, if
e_{i}=e_{i+1}
then the range is at least
2-\delta
, and for any even
i\leq 2m
, if
e_{i}=e_{i-1}
then the range is at least
2-\delta
. (This is true because we may start at
a_{i}
and work towards the closest endpoint
a_{1}
or
a_{n}
.) It remains to check the case where
-e_{1}=e_{2}
,
-e_{3}=e_{4}
,
\cdots
,
-e_{2m-3}=e_{2m-2}
,
-e_{2m-1}=e_{2m}=-e_{2m+1}=-1
,
e_{2m+2}=-e_{2m+3}
,
\cdots
,
e_{n-3}=-e_{n-2}
,
e_{n-1}=-e_{n}
.

If we have
(e_{2m-1},e_{2m},e_{2m+1},e_{2m+2})=(+1,-1,+1,+1)
, then our range is already at least
2+(2^{m-1}-2^{m-2})\delta
. Similarly, if we have

(e_{2m-1},e_{2m},e_{2m+1},e_{2m+2},e_{2m+3},e_{2m+4})=(+1,-1,+1,-1,+1,+1),

then our range is already at least
2+(2^{m-1}+2^{m-2}-2^{m-3})\delta
. In general, if the sequence
(e_{1},e_{2},

\cdots,

e_{n})
is not perfectly alternating then the range will be at least
2-\delta
. It remains to check the case where the sequence
(e_{1},e_{2},\cdots,e_{n})
is perfectly alternating; i.e.
e_{i}=(-1)^{i+1}
for each
i
. We easily verify that the range in this case is

\displaystyle 1+(1+2+4+\cdots+2^{m-2}+2^{m-1}+2^{m-2}+\cdots+4+2+1)\delta

\displaystyle=1+(2^{m}+2^{m-1}-2)\delta

\displaystyle=2-\delta,

as desired.
\blacksquare
