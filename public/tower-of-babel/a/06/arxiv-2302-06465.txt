Calculus of functional centrality


Abstract
In this document, we present another perspective for the calculus of optimal geometrical primitives and functions according to the centrality requirements. The shortest paths expressed in spatial and temporal domains are studied. We show the effectiveness of this formulation by providing solutions that cannot be easily accessed by classical formulation when using the calculus of variations.

1
Introduction

The calculus of variations is widely used in mathematics, physics, image processing, and other areas of knowledge
[
2
,
3
,
4
]
. While the optimization aims to find a stationary point of a function defined over some domain, the calculus of variation seeks to find stationary geometrical primitives other than the point (e.g. line, surface, volume) or a stationary function according to a given functional. Let us concentrate on the simplest calculus of variations in which the unknown is univariate continuously derivable curves
u(x):[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}
and the first-order functional to be minimized depends upon
u(x)
, its first derivative
u^{\prime}(x)
, and
x
. This first-order functional is specified by a geometrical or physics feature
F(x,u(x),u^{\prime}(x))
, such as the curve length, its curvature or the time travelled by an object. It is given by:

J(u)=\int_{a}^{b}F(x,u(x),u^{\prime}(x))dx

(1)

The feature
F(x,u(x),u^{\prime}(x))
is assumed to be twice continuously differentiable with respect to
x
and the first and second variations with respect to
u
and
u^{\prime}
are continuous with respect to
x
. In this case, the solution to this problem can be obtained by solving the following Euler-Lagrange ordinary differential equation (ODE):

\frac{\partial F(x,u(x),u^{\prime}(x))}{\partial u}-\frac{d}{dx}\frac{\partial F(x,u(x),u^{\prime}(x))}{\partial u^{\prime}}=0

(2)

In order to formulate the condition so that the stationary curve to be a minimum of the functional
J(u)
, we need to establish the positive definiteness of the second variation. Straightforward computations lead to conclude that the critical curve u(x) is a minimum if the following inequality holds:

\frac{\partial^{2}F(x,u(x),u^{\prime}(x))}{\partial u^{2}}h^{2}+2\frac{\partial^{2}F(x,u(x),u^{\prime}(x))}{\partial u\partial u^{\prime}}hh^{\prime}+\frac{\partial^{2}F(x,u(x),u^{\prime}(x))}{\partial u^{\prime 2}}h^{\prime 2}>0

(3)

where
h(x)
is a non-constant variation such that
h(a)=h(b)=0
. The reader will find more about the calculus of variations in
[
2
]
and in other literature. Let us now consider things from another perspective. To this end, in this document we will limit ourselves to the case where
F(x,u(x),u^{\prime}(x))>0
. The functional in Eq.
1
is a prior knowledge expressing some arbitrariness because it is the result of intuition not subject to experimentation. For example, it can be the length of a plane curve, which can be measured in an infinite number of ways because there are an infinite number of ways to define distance. From this point of view, the variational problem in Eq.
1
can be expressed in an infinite number of ways. Let us consider the transformation of the feature
F(x,u(x),u^{\prime}(x))
yielding
F^{\alpha}(x,u(x),u^{\prime}(x))
, where
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
. For a given
x
,
F^{\alpha}(x,u(x),u^{\prime}(x))
is greater or lesser than
F(x,u(x),u^{\prime}(x))
depending upon the values of
F(x,u(x),u^{\prime}(x))
and
\alpha
. Similarly to Eq.
1
, the first-order functional can be written in the transformed space as:

C_{\alpha}^{\alpha}(u)=\frac{1}{\delta}\int_{a}^{b}F^{\alpha}(x,u(x),u^{\prime}(x))dx

(4)

where
\delta=b-a
.
The rational explanation of the exponent
\alpha
of
C_{\alpha}()
can be provided by the mean value theorem. By considering
F()
as an explicit or implicit function of
x
, then there exists
\eta\in]a,b[
such that
F^{\alpha}(\eta,u(\eta),u^{\prime}(\eta))=\frac{1}{\delta}\int_{a}^{b}F(x,u(x),u^{\prime}(x))^{\alpha}dx
. Instead of using
F(\eta,u(\eta),u^{\prime}(\eta))
, we rename it
C(u)
. Taking the
\alpha
-root allows us to write:

C_{\alpha}(u)=(\frac{1}{\delta}\int_{a}^{b}F^{\alpha}(x,u(x),u^{\prime}(x))dx)^{1/\alpha}

(5)

It is worth mentioning that when
\alpha=1
, the functional in Eq.
1
is a mean value up to the interval length. Because
C_{\alpha}(u)
is a limit when (
n\to+\infty
) of the Hölder mean of
n
samples, we call it the first-order functional centrality of Hölder. In this report, we will show that the first-order functional centrality of Hölder leads us to solutions that cannot be obtained by the variational formulation in Eq.
1
. We present some of its properties in the next section. In Section 3, we derive the ODE solution to the first-order functional centrality of Hölder. The shortest paths in both spatial and temporal domains are studied in Sections 4 and 5.

2
Properties

We announce and prove the following properties of the first-order functional centrality of Hölder in Eq.
5
under the assumption
F(x,u(x),u^{\prime}(x))>0
and it is bounded.

P1 :

\lim_{\alpha\to 0}C_{\alpha}(u)=e^{\frac{\int_{a}^{b}lnF(x,u(x),u^{\prime}(x))dx}{b-a}}
. Indeed, let us write the limit of
lnC_{\alpha}(u)
:

\lim_{\alpha\to 0}lnC_{\alpha}(u)=\lim_{\alpha\to 0}\frac{1}{\alpha}ln\frac{1}{\delta}\int_{a}^{b}F^{\alpha}(x,u(x),u^{\prime}(x))dx

(6)

The limit has the form 0/0, so by using the L’Hopital’s rule and the dominated convergence theorem, we obtain:

\displaystyle\lim_{\alpha\to 0}lnC_{\alpha}(u)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{\alpha\to 0}\frac{\int_{a}^{b}F^{\alpha}(x,u(x),u^{\prime}(x))lnF(x,u(x),u^{\prime}(x))dx}{\int_{a}^{b}F^{\alpha}(x,u(x),u^{\prime}(x))dx}

(7)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\int_{a}^{b}lnF(x,u(x),u^{\prime}(x))dx}{\delta}

(8)

P2:
The functional
C_{\alpha}(u)
is a increasing wrt
\alpha
. Indeed, the derivative of
C_{\alpha}(u)
wrt
\alpha

\frac{dlnC_{\alpha}(u)}{d\alpha}=\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}}\frac{\frac{1}{\delta}\int_{a}^{b}F^{\alpha}(x,u(x),u^{\prime}(x))(lnF^{\alpha}(x,u(x),u^{\prime}(x))-ln(\frac{1}{\delta}\int_{a}^{b}F^{\alpha}(x,u(x),u^{\prime}(x))dx)dx}{\frac{1}{\delta}\int_{a}^{b}F^{\alpha}(x,u(x),u^{\prime}(x))dx}

(9)

Equivalently,

\frac{dlnC_{\alpha}(u)}{d\alpha}=\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}}\frac{\frac{1}{\delta}\int_{a}^{b}F^{\alpha}(x,u(x),u^{\prime}(x))ln\frac{F^{\alpha}(x,u(x),u^{\prime}(x))}{\frac{1}{\delta}\int_{a}^{b}F^{\alpha}(x,u(x),u^{\prime}(x))dx}dx}{\frac{1}{\delta}\int_{a}^{b}F^{\alpha}(x,u(x),u^{\prime}(x))dx}

(10)

Since
lnr\geq 1-1/r
for all positive
x
, where the equality is true only if
r=1
. In our case,
r=F^{\alpha}(x,u(x),u^{\prime}(x))/

\frac{1}{\delta}\int_{a}^{b}F^{\alpha}(x,u(x),u^{\prime}(x))dx
cannot be one because
F^{\alpha}(x,u(x),u^{\prime}(x))
cannot be a constant. Hence,

\displaystyle\frac{dlnC_{\alpha}(u)}{d\alpha}>\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}}\frac{\frac{1}{\delta}\int_{a}^{b}F^{\alpha}(x,u(x),u^{\prime}(x))(1-\frac{\frac{1}{\delta}\int_{a}^{b}F^{\alpha}(x,u(x),u^{\prime}(x))dx}{F^{\alpha}(x,u(x),u^{\prime}(x))})dx}{\frac{1}{\delta}\int_{a}^{b}F^{\alpha}(x,u(x),u^{\prime}(x))dx}

(11)

It can also be rewritten as follows:

\displaystyle\frac{dlnC_{\alpha}(u)}{d\alpha}>\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}}\frac{\frac{1}{\delta}\int_{a}^{b}F^{\alpha}(x,u(x),u^{\prime}(x))dx-\frac{1}{\delta}\int_{a}^{b}F^{\alpha}(x,u(x),u^{\prime}(x))dx\frac{1}{\delta}\int_{a}^{b}\frac{F^{\alpha}(x,u(x),u^{\prime}(x))}{F^{\alpha}(x,u(x),u^{\prime}(x))}dx}{\frac{1}{\delta}\int_{a}^{b}F^{\alpha}(x,u(x),u^{\prime}(x))dx}

(12)

Calculating the leftmost integral allows us to write:

\displaystyle\frac{dlnC_{\alpha}(u)}{d\alpha}>\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}}\frac{\frac{1}{\delta}\int_{a}^{b}F^{\alpha}(x,u(x),u^{\prime}(x))dx-\frac{1}{\delta}\int_{a}^{b}F^{\alpha}(x,u(x),u^{\prime}(x))dx}{\frac{1}{\delta}\int_{a}^{b}F^{\alpha}(x,u(x),u^{\prime}(x))dx}=0

(13)

The derivative of
lnC_{\alpha}(u)
and
C_{\alpha}(u)
are both positives meaning
C_{\alpha}(u)
is increasing in
\alpha
.

P3:

C_{-\infty}(u)\leq C_{\alpha}(u)\leq C_{+\infty}(u)
. This is a corollary of
P2
.

P4:

C_{-\infty}(u)=\inf F(x,u(x),u^{\prime}(x))
and
C_{+\infty}(u)=\sup F(x,u(x),u^{\prime}(x))
. Let the sequence
\alpha_{1},\cdots,\alpha_{n}
such that
\alpha_{1}<\cdots<\alpha_{n}
, so according to P2 and Eq.
5
,
C_{\alpha_{1}}(u)<\cdots<C_{\alpha_{n}}(u)
. Thanks to the mean value theorem, the last inequality can be rewritten
C_{\alpha_{1}}(u)=F(\eta_{1},u(\eta_{1}),u^{\prime}(\eta_{1}))<\cdots<C_{\alpha_{n}}(u)=F(\eta_{n},u(\eta_{n}),u^{\prime}(\eta_{n}))
. The sequence is built such that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\alpha_{n}=\alpha
. If
\lim_{\alpha\to+\infty}C_{\alpha}(u)=F(\eta,u(\eta),u^{\prime}(\eta))
is finite, then it is the upper bound because
C_{\alpha}(u)
is increasing in
\alpha
. The same reasoning is used for the
\lim_{\alpha\to-\infty}C_{\alpha}(u)
.

The properties P2, P3 and P4 ensure that the
C_{\alpha}(u)()
is non decreasing in
\alpha
and it is bounded, i.e.
C_{\alpha}(u)\in[\inf F(),\sup F()]
for a given
\alpha
. The parameter
\alpha
implements a transform of the features
F()
to a new space in which the first-order functional centrality of Hölder is defined, i.e. Eq.
5
. This transform leads to specifying the contribution of the point
x
to Hölder’s first-order functional centrality using the parameter
\alpha
. In other words, the relevance of features is specified by the parameter
\alpha
, meaning that it is a trick for feature (data) selection
[
1
]
. Taken together these properties indicate that the functional
C_{\alpha}(u)
is a mean operator. The discrete case (i.e., counting measure) of this functional is the Hölder mean. By the analogy of the discrete case, we call it the functional arithmetic mean when
\alpha=1
, the functional harmonic mean when
\alpha=-1
, and according to P1 the functional geometric mean when
\alpha=0
.

3
Solutions of the First order functional centrality of Hölder

In a space of curves, we want to find a smooth, continuously derivable curve
u(x)
defined over
[a,b]
minimizing
C_{\alpha}(u)
in Eq.
5
. The curve is assumed to be known in two points,
P=(a,u(a))
and
Q=(b,u(b))
, where the values
a
and
b
are the data of the problem to be studied. In order to find the stationary curve
u(x)
, we vary
u(x)
by a small amount of
h(x)
(i.e.
u_{\epsilon}(x)=u(x)+\epsilon h(x)
and
u^{\prime}_{\epsilon}(x)=u^{\prime}(x)+\epsilon h^{\prime}(x)
), and calculate the first variation wrt
\epsilon
. Note that, all variations are ”glued” at the two points
P
and
Q
; that is the real function
h(x)
must verifies
h(a)=h(b)=0
. According to P1 and Eq.
5
, the two cases
\alpha\neq 0
and
\alpha=0
are considered separately.

3.1
Case
\alpha\neq 0

The first variation wrt
\epsilon
is:

\frac{dC_{\alpha}(u_{\epsilon})}{d\epsilon}=\frac{d}{d\epsilon}(\frac{1}{\delta}\int_{a}^{b}F(x,u_{\epsilon},u_{\epsilon}^{{}^{\prime}})^{\alpha}dx)^{1/\alpha}

(14)

Applying derivative rules, we obtain:

\displaystyle\frac{dC_{\alpha}(u_{\epsilon})}{d\epsilon}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\alpha}(\frac{1}{\delta}\int_{a}^{b}F(x,u_{\epsilon},u_{\epsilon}^{{}^{\prime}})^{\alpha}dx)^{1/\alpha-1}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\delta}\int_{a}^{b}\alpha F(x,u_{\epsilon},u_{\epsilon}^{{}^{\prime}})^{\alpha-1}\frac{dF(x,u_{\epsilon},u_{\epsilon}^{{}^{\prime}})}{d\epsilon}dx=0

Let us recall that
F()>0
, and therefore
(\int_{a}^{b}F(x,u_{\epsilon},u_{\epsilon}^{{}^{\prime}})^{\alpha}dx)^{1/\alpha-1}\neq 0
. So, the term is:

\int_{a}^{b}F(x,u_{\epsilon},u_{\epsilon}^{{}^{\prime}})^{\alpha-1}\frac{dF(x,u_{\epsilon},u_{\epsilon}^{{}^{\prime}})}{d\epsilon}dx=0

(15)

The total derivative wrt to
\epsilon
is:

\frac{d}{d\epsilon}F(x,u_{\epsilon},u_{\epsilon}^{{}^{\prime}})=\frac{\partial F(x,u_{\epsilon},u_{\epsilon}^{{}^{\prime}})}{\partial x}\frac{\partial x}{\partial\epsilon}+\frac{\partial F(x,u_{\epsilon},u_{\epsilon}^{{}^{\prime}})}{\partial u_{\epsilon}}\frac{\partial u_{\epsilon}}{\partial\epsilon}+\frac{\partial F(x,u_{\epsilon},u_{\epsilon}^{{}^{\prime}})}{\partial u_{\epsilon}^{{}^{\prime}}}\frac{\partial u_{\epsilon}^{{}^{\prime}}}{\partial\epsilon}

(16)

Because
\frac{\partial x}{\partial\epsilon}=0
, by substituting this equation in Eq.
15
and setting
\epsilon=0
, we obtain:

\int_{a}^{b}F(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})^{\alpha-1}(\frac{\partial F(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})}{\partial u}h(x)+\frac{\partial F(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})}{\partial u^{{}^{\prime}}}h^{{}^{\prime}}(x))dx=0

(17)

The integration by part of the second term leads to:

\int_{a}^{b}h(x)(F(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})^{\alpha-1}\frac{\partial F(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})}{\partial u}-\frac{d}{dx}F(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})^{\alpha-1}\frac{\partial F(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})}{\partial u^{{}^{\prime}}})dx=0

(18)

The fundamental lemma of the calculus of variations
[
2
]
ensures that the stationary curve
u(x)
is a solution to the ODE:

F(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})^{\alpha-1}\frac{\partial F(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})}{\partial u}-\frac{d}{dx}F(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})^{\alpha-1}\frac{\partial F(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})}{\partial u^{{}^{\prime}}}=0

(19)

This ODE can be rewritten as:

F(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})^{\alpha-2}(F(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})(\frac{\partial F(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})}{\partial u}-\frac{d}{dx}\frac{\partial F(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})}{\partial u^{{}^{\prime}}})-(\alpha-1)\frac{\partial F(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})}{\partial u^{{}^{\prime}}}\frac{d}{dx}F(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}}))=0

(20)

Because
F(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})^{\alpha-2}\neq 0
, the final ODE is written as follows:

F(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})(\frac{\partial F(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})}{\partial u}-\frac{d}{dx}\frac{\partial F(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})}{\partial u^{{}^{\prime}}})-(\alpha-1)\frac{\partial F(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})}{\partial u^{{}^{\prime}}}\frac{d}{dx}F(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})=0

(21)

Note that if
\alpha=1
, we have the well-known Euler-Lagrange ordinary differential equation (ODE).
The presence of the term
\frac{\partial F}{\partial u^{{}^{\prime}}}\frac{d}{dx}F(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})
when
\alpha\neq 1
could leads to other solutions. To assert that the stationary curve
u(x)
solution to Eq.
21
is a minimum, we need to test the positiveness of the second variation wrt
\epsilon
. Indeed, the curve
u(x)
is a minimum if
C_{\alpha}(u)<C_{\alpha}(u+\epsilon h)
. By using Maclaurin expansion wrt to
\epsilon
, we write:

f(\epsilon)=C_{\alpha}(u+\epsilon h)=C_{\alpha}(u)+\epsilon f^{\prime}(0)+0.5\epsilon^{2}f^{\prime\prime}(0)+O_{3}(u)

(22)

If
u(x)
is a stationary curve then the first variation
f^{\prime}(0)=0
. Consequently, the functional
C_{\alpha}(u+\epsilon h)>C_{\alpha}(u)
if
f^{\prime\prime}(0)
is positive. At
\epsilon=0
, the first variation can be rewritten as:

\frac{dC_{\alpha}(u_{\epsilon})}{d\epsilon}|_{\epsilon=0}=f^{\prime}(0)=\frac{1}{\alpha}(\frac{1}{\delta}\int_{a}^{b}F^{\alpha}(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})dx)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}-1}(\frac{1}{\delta}\int_{a}^{b}\frac{dF^{\alpha}(x,u_{\epsilon},u_{\epsilon}^{{}^{\prime}})}{d\epsilon}|_{\epsilon=0}dx)

(23)

The second variation is:

\frac{d^{2}C_{\alpha}(u_{\epsilon})}{d\epsilon^{2}}|_{\epsilon=0}=f^{\prime\prime}(0)=\begin{array}[]{ll}&\frac{1}{\alpha}\frac{1}{\delta^{2}}(\frac{1}{\delta}\int_{a}^{b}F^{\alpha}(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})dx)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}-2}(\int_{a}^{b}F^{\alpha}(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})dx\int_{a}^{b}\frac{d^{2}F^{\alpha}(x,u_{\epsilon},u_{\epsilon}^{{}^{\prime}})}{d\epsilon^{2}}|_{\epsilon=0}dx+\\
&~~~~(\frac{1}{\alpha}-1)(\int_{a}^{b}\frac{dF^{\alpha}(x,u_{\epsilon},u_{\epsilon}^{{}^{\prime}})}{d\epsilon}|_{\epsilon=0}dx)^{2})\end{array}

(24)

The stationary curve is a minimum if
\frac{d^{2}C_{\alpha}(u_{\epsilon})}{d\epsilon^{2}}|_{\epsilon=0}>0
. Knowing that both
\frac{1}{\delta}
and
\int_{a}^{b}F^{\alpha}(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})dx
are positive, and
\int_{a}^{b}\frac{dF^{\alpha}(x,u_{\epsilon},u_{\epsilon}^{{}^{\prime}})}{d\epsilon}|_{\epsilon=0}dx=0
, it follows that the stationary curve is a minimum if:

\frac{1}{\alpha}\int_{a}^{b}\frac{d^{2}F^{\alpha}(x,u_{\epsilon},u_{\epsilon}^{{}^{\prime}})}{d\epsilon^{2}}|_{\epsilon=0}dx>0

(25)

The inequality is fulfilled when
\alpha
and
\int_{a}^{b}\frac{d^{2}F^{\alpha}(x,u_{\epsilon},u_{\epsilon}^{{}^{\prime}})}{d\epsilon^{2}}|_{\epsilon=0}dx
have the same sign.
Two important cases that should be studied are the explicit independence of
F(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})
on
u(x)
and
x
. For the former case, substituting
\frac{\partial F(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})}{\partial u}=0
in Eq.
21
gives
F(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})\frac{d}{dx}\frac{\partial F(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})}{\partial u^{{}^{\prime}}}+(\alpha-1)\frac{\partial F(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})}{\partial u^{{}^{\prime}}}\frac{d}{dx}F(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})=0
. An interpretation in the general case seems to be not easy to draw. However, when
\alpha=1
, then
\frac{d}{dx}\frac{\partial F(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})}{\partial u^{{}^{\prime}}}=0
and therefore
\frac{\partial F(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})}{\partial u^{{}^{\prime}}}
is a constant. This outcome is known as the ignorable coordinates in the calculus of variations. When
\alpha=2
, Eq.
21
is rewritten as
\frac{d}{dx}F(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})\frac{\partial F}{\partial u^{{}^{\prime}}}=0
, then
F(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})\frac{\partial F}{\partial u^{{}^{\prime}}}
is a constant. For the last
\frac{\partial F(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})}{\partial x}=0
, straightforward manipulations leads to the ODE
F(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})\frac{d}{dx}(u^{\prime}\frac{\partial F(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})}{\partial u^{{}^{\prime}}}-F(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}}))+(\alpha-1)u^{\prime}\frac{\partial F(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})}{\partial u^{{}^{\prime}}}\frac{d}{dx}F(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})=0
. When
\alpha=1
, we obtain the well-known Beltrami identity; i.e.
u^{\prime}\frac{\partial F(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})}{\partial u^{{}^{\prime}}}-F(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})
is a constant. When
\alpha=2
, we have
\frac{d}{dx}F(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})u^{\prime}\frac{\partial F(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})}{\partial u^{{}^{\prime}}}=F(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})\frac{d}{dx}F(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})
and hence
u^{\prime}\frac{\partial F(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})}{\partial u^{{}^{\prime}}}-\frac{1}{2}F(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})
is a constant. The case
\alpha=2
generalizes the Beltrami identity. The study of other values of
\alpha
could lead to other outcomes.

3.2
Case
\alpha=0

We will now give the first and second variations in the case where
\alpha=0
. The first order variation is:

\displaystyle\frac{dC_{\alpha}(u)}{d\epsilon}|_{\epsilon=0}=C_{\alpha}(u)\int_{a}^{b}(\frac{\partial lnF(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})}{\partial u}h+\frac{\partial lnF(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})}{\partial u^{\prime}}h^{\prime})dx=0

(26)

The corresponding ODE is:

\frac{\partial lnF(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})}{\partial u}-\frac{d}{dx}\frac{\partial lnF(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})}{\partial u^{{}^{\prime}}}=0

(27)

If
u(x)
is an ignorable coordinate, then
\frac{\partial lnF(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})}{\partial u^{{}^{\prime}}}
is a constant. This is another outcome generalizing the Beltrami identity. When
F(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})
is explicitly independent upon
x
, then we deduce that
lnF(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})-u^{\prime}\frac{\partial lnF(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})}{\partial u^{{}^{\prime}}}
is a constant. Straightforward manipulations lead to writing the second variation:

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}C_{\alpha}(u)}{d\epsilon^{2}}|_{\epsilon=0}

\displaystyle=C_{\alpha}(u)((\int_{a}^{b}(\frac{\partial lnF(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})}{\partial u}h+\frac{\partial lnF(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})}{\partial u^{\prime}}h^{\prime})dx)^{2}+\int_{a}^{b}(\frac{1}{F(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})}(\frac{\partial^{2}F(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})}{\partial u^{2}}h^{2}+

(28)

\displaystyle 2\frac{\partial^{2}F(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})}{\partial u\partial u^{\prime}}hh^{\prime}+\frac{\partial^{2}F(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})}{\partial u^{\prime 2}}h^{\prime 2})-\frac{1}{F^{2}(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})}(\frac{\partial F(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})}{\partial u}h+\frac{\partial F(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})}{\partial u^{\prime}}h^{\prime})^{2})dx)

The second variation at the stationary curve is obtained by substituting Eq.
26
in Eq.
28
:

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}C_{\alpha}(u)}{d\epsilon^{2}}|_{\epsilon=0}=

\displaystyle C_{\alpha}(u)\int_{a}^{b}(\frac{1}{F(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})}(\frac{\partial^{2}F(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})}{\partial u^{2}}h^{2}+2\frac{\partial^{2}F(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})}{\partial u\partial u^{\prime}}hh^{\prime}+\frac{\partial^{2}F(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})}{\partial u^{\prime 2}}h^{\prime 2})

(29)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{F^{2}(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})}(\frac{\partial F(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})}{\partial u}h+\frac{\partial F(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})}{\partial u^{\prime}}h^{\prime})^{2})dx

Knowing that
C_{\alpha}(u)>0
, the second variation test leads to concluding that the stationary curve is a minimum if:

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{F(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})}(\frac{\partial^{2}F(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})}{\partial u^{2}}h^{2}+2\frac{\partial^{2}F(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})}{\partial u\partial u^{\prime}}hh^{\prime}+\frac{\partial^{2}F(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})}{\partial u^{\prime 2}}h^{\prime 2})dx>

(30)

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}\frac{1}{F^{2}(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})}(\frac{\partial F(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})}{\partial u}h+\frac{\partial F(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})}{\partial u^{\prime}}h^{\prime})^{2})dx

The RHS is non-negative, so we can write the second variation test as follows:

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}\frac{1}{F(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})}(\frac{\partial^{2}F(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})}{\partial u^{2}}h^{2}+2\frac{\partial^{2}F(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})}{\partial u\partial u^{\prime}}hh^{\prime}+\frac{\partial^{2}F(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})}{\partial u^{\prime 2}}h^{\prime 2})>0~~\mbox{if}~~\alpha=0

Shortest path problem

We would like to find the curve of the shortest path between the two points
P
and
Q
in the spatial domain. We can approximate the length of a plane curve defined on
[a,b]
by subdividing it into infinitesimal linear pieces, measuring the length of each, and adding up all the lengths. The infinitesimal piece length between
(x,u(x))
and
(x+dx,u(x+dx))
is the length of the hypotenuse of the right triangle having sides
dx
and
du(x)
, i.e.
\sqrt{dx^{2}+(u(x+dx)-u(x))^{2}}=dx\sqrt{1+\frac{(u(x+dx)-u(x))^{2}}{dx^{2}}}
. When
dx
is too small, the infinitesimal length is
\sqrt{1+u(x)^{\prime 2}}dx
. Let us define the feature
F(x,u,u^{\prime})=\sqrt{1+u(x)^{\prime 2}}
. It should be noted that
u(x)
and
x
are ignorable coordinates, i.e.
\frac{\partial F(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})}{\partial u}=0
and
\frac{\partial F(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})}{\partial x}=0
. The other terms involved in Eq.
21
are
\frac{\partial F(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})}{\partial u^{{}^{\prime}}}=\frac{u^{\prime}}{F}

\frac{dF(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})}{dx}=\frac{u^{\prime}u^{\prime\prime}}{F}
, and
\frac{d}{dx}\frac{\partial F(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})}{\partial u^{{}^{\prime}}}=\frac{F(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})u^{\prime\prime}-\frac{u^{\prime 2}u^{\prime\prime}}{F(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})}}{F^{2}(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})}
. The ODE in Eq.
21
is rewritten as:

-F(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})\frac{F(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})u^{\prime\prime}-\frac{u^{\prime 2}u^{\prime\prime}}{F(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})}}{F^{2}(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})}-(\alpha-1)\frac{u^{\prime}}{F(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})}\frac{u^{\prime}u^{\prime\prime}}{F(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})}=0

(32)

-F(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})(F(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})u^{\prime\prime}-\frac{u^{\prime 2}u^{\prime\prime}}{F(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})})-(\alpha-1)u^{\prime 2}u^{\prime\prime}=0

(33)

5
Fastest path problem

The Brachistochrone is an old problem known in the calculus of variations
[
2
]
. It describes a curve that carries a particle under gravity from one height to another in minimal time. Other variations
of this problem have included the effects of friction,
the motion of a disc on a hemisphere, the motion of a cyclist in a velodrome, and even the quantum Brachistochrone problem
[
3
]
.

Suppose that the particle is released from
P
at
t=0
, and then follows a curve
u(x)
which reaches
Q
, so that
u(b)-u(a)
is the height lost, and
b-a
is the horizontal distance traversed. The associated feature is
F(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})=\sqrt{(1+u^{\prime 2}(x))/u(x)}
. It should be noted that
\frac{\partial F(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})}{\partial x}=0
. The other derivatives in Eq.
21
are
\frac{\partial F(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})}{\partial u}=-\frac{\sqrt{1+u^{\prime 2}}}{2u^{3/2}}
,
\frac{\partial F(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})}{\partial u^{\prime}}=\frac{u^{\prime}}{\sqrt{u(1+u^{\prime 2})}}
,

\frac{d}{dx}\frac{\partial F(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})}{\partial u^{\prime}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{u(1+u^{\prime 2})}}(\frac{u^{\prime\prime}}{1+u^{\prime 2}}-\frac{u^{\prime 2}}{2u})
, and
\frac{dF(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})}{dx}=\frac{u^{\prime}u^{\prime\prime}}{\sqrt{u(1+u^{\prime 2})}}-\frac{u^{\prime}\sqrt{1+u^{\prime 2}}}{2u^{3/2}}
,

F(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})(\frac{\partial F(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})}{\partial u}-\frac{d}{dx}\frac{\partial F(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})}{\partial u^{\prime}})=-\frac{1+u^{\prime 2}}{2u^{2}}-\frac{1}{u}(\frac{u^{\prime\prime}}{1+u^{\prime 2}}-\frac{u^{\prime 2}}{2u})=-\frac{1}{2u^{2}}-\frac{u^{\prime\prime}}{u(1+u^{\prime 2})}
,

(\alpha-1)\frac{\partial F(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})}{\partial u^{\prime}}\frac{dF(x,u,u^{{}^{\prime}})}{dx}=(\alpha-1)\frac{u^{\prime 2}}{u}(\frac{u^{\prime\prime}}{1+u^{\prime 2}}-\frac{1}{2u})
.
The Euler-Lagrange ODE in Eq.
21
becomes:

-\frac{1}{2u}-\frac{u^{\prime\prime}}{1+u^{\prime 2}}-(\alpha-1)u^{\prime 2}(\frac{u^{\prime\prime}}{1+u^{\prime 2}}-\frac{1}{2u})=0

(35)

The solution in the calculus of variations (i.e.
\alpha=1
) is the cycloid with the parametric equations
x=r(\theta-sin\theta)
and
u(x)=r(1-cos\theta)
. It can be displayed by the path traced out by a point placed on a rolling wheel of radius
r
when it rotates by an angle
\theta
. The general solution to the ODE when
\alpha\neq 1
is challenging. For example, it is easy to check that

6
Conclusion

In this document, the calculus of variations is revisited from a new angle. Instead of the usual variational formulation, functional centrality based on Hölder’s mean is used. For a given problem, the solution is a family of curves, where each one corresponds to a certain data selection criterion induced by the centrality measure used. Some of these solutions cannot be obtained with the usual variational formulation.
To illustrate, the fastest and shortest paths are studied.
