Stochastic effects of waves on currents in the ocean mixed layer
Darryl D. Holm, and, Ruiao Hu

Abstract
This paper introduces an energy-preserving stochastic model for studying wave effects on currents in the ocean mixing layer. The model is called stochastic forcing by Lie transport (SFLT). The SFLT model is derived here from a stochastic constrained variational principle, so it has a Kelvin circulation theorem. The examples of SFLT given here treat 3D Euler fluid flow, rotating shallow water dynamics and the Euler-Boussinesq equations. In each example, one sees the effect of stochastic Stokes drift and material entrainment in the generation of fluid circulation. We also present an Eulerian-averaged SFLT model (EA SFLT), based on decomposing the Eulerian solutions of the energy-conserving SFLT model into sums of their expectations and fluctuations.

Introduction

Wave effects on currents (WEC).

In studies of the ocean mixing layer (OML) the problem of wave effects on currents (WEC) arises. For example, surface gravity waves can drive Langmuir circulations which have important influences on near-surface currents
[
50
]
. Langmuir circulations are horizontally oriented pairs of oppositely circulating vortex tubes aligned generally along the direction of the wind, as reviewed, e.g., in
[
46
,
26
,
70
,
24

Because they represent arrays of organised fluid transport, Langmuir circulation patterns can produce vertical transport which strongly entrains sediment and detritus into the OML from both above and below. This means they can have a strong effects, for example, on the dispersion of oil spills in a shallow sea,
[
69
]
.

Most previous studies of turbulence in the OML have been performed in the context of wave-averaged dynamics. For a recent review, see
[
1

Averaging the stratified Euler equations in three spatial dimensions over a time scale longer than the wave period produces a modified set of equations, known as the CL equations, after Craik and Leibovich,
[
15

The temporal-averaging basis of the CL equations introduces Stokes-drift effects which represent additional wave-averaged forces and material advection terms that emerge from multi-scale asymptotic theories
[
15
,
61
]
. Remarkably, the ideal CL equations preserve many of the properties of the original stratified Euler equations. For example, the ideal CL equations conserve energy and have a Hamiltonian formulation
[
34
]
.

The aim of the present paper is to develop a stochastic theory of WEC which encompasses the Craik-Leibovich theory and still preserves the original energy. Indeed, in deriving the stochastic theory, we will take an approach which can be adapted to select whichever primary conservation laws of the deterministic theory are desired. Specifically, our approach will use a stochastic version of a method from classical mechanics known as the Reduced Lagrange-d’Alembert-Pontryagin (RLDP) formulation of constrained dynamics which is reduced by a symmetry of the Lagrangian in Hamilton’s principle. Stochastic applications of the RLDP formulation are discussed, e.g., in

1.1
Motivating question and main results of the paper

The present paper addresses the geometric interplay between energy and circulation, when stochasticity is introduced into fluid dynamics, in both the incompressible flow of an ideal Euler fluid and in the flows of ideal fluids with advected quantities. This is a burgeoning area of research in fluid dynamics. For recent introductory surveys of stochastic fluid dynamics with applications, see, e.g.,
[
17
,
23
,
28
]
.

The question underlying the present work is, “What types of noise perturbations can be added to fluid dynamics which will preserve its fundamental properties of energy conservation, Kelvin-Noether circulation dynamics and conserved properties resulting from invariance under Lagrangian particle relabelling?” Since these properties all arise from the geometric structure of fluid dynamics, the noise perturbations we consider will be introduced in a geometrical framework.

The RLDP formulation of fluid dynamics leads to a constrained variational principle for stochastic ideal fluid dynamics through which noise may be introduced as a prescribed stochastic force. By
choosing
an appropriate form of the stochastic force, the RLDP formulation can be designed to preserve whatever conservation law one may desire among those of the deterministic ideal fluid equations in any number of dimensions.

For applications in fluid dynamics, the RLDP formulation results in the procedure mentioned above, called Stochastic Forcing by Lie Transport (SFLT). This is constructed by choosing the stochastic forces to Lie derivative of the transport velocity vector field acting on some prescribed one-form density which conserves the ideal fluid energy. Potential applications include a stochastic version of the Craik-Leibovich (CL) vortex force which presumably could generate stochastic Langmuir circulation. This result is introduced for the Euler fluid equations for 3D incompressible flow in Example
2.1
and Proposition
2.2
. The corresponding theory for ideal flows in general is also treated in section
2.2
where stochastic advected quantities are introduced. Using RLDP, the existence of Kelvin-Noether theorem is automatic due to the variational nature of the formulation. When the advection of the fluid density is assumed, the Kelvin circulation theorem becomes a simple corollary of the Kelvin-Noether theorem as proven in Theorem
2.7
.

SFLT has dual design capabilities.

Besides adding stochastic
forces
which drive the fluid motion equation, the SFLT framework can also be designed to distribute stochastic
sources
in the advective transport equations, as discussed in section
2.2
. The stochastic sources distributed in the advective transport equations by SFLT can be designed to model, for example, stochastic changes in the material properties of inertial fluid parcels which may be embedded in the flow. In particular, one can use SFLT to model the stochastic dynamics of a mixture of heavier and lighter parcels whose fluid paths deviate from passive tracers, which are carried by the drift flow velocity. In this case, the mass density would be changing stochastically in the material frame of the flow drift velocity, because of entrainment or detrainment of sediments. One can observe the entrainment or detrainment of sediments in Langmuir circulations, see for example,
[
50
]
. One can also imagine using SFLT to model the transport of a stochastically evolving, spatially distributed, algae bloom which has a distribution of shapes, so it is only partially embedded in a flow around an obstacle, such as an island in the ocean
[
30
,
63
]
.

Thus, SFLT has dual design capabilities. It can transport stochastic
sources
in the material frame, and it can also impose stochastic
non-inertial forces
arising from stochastic changes of the frame of motion, such as the Craik-Leibovich
vortex force

[
15
,
34
]
. The dual design capabilities of SFLT could potentially lead to a variety of important applications. For example, the use of SFLT for modelling entrainment and detrainment of various materials into flows in the ocean mixed layer (OML) can in principle be instrumental in modelling some important components of natural processes such as gas and nutrient exchanges. Besides its potential importance in modelling natural processes in the transport of materials such as sediment or algae blooms in the OML, the SFLT stochastic modelling framework may also find a role in data assimilation for predicting the transport of pollution such as oil droplets, microplastics, etc.

The energy preserving SFLT framework is extended to the Eulerian Averaged SFLT (EA SFLT) framework by applying an
Eulerian Average
on the Eulerian quantities in the equations. These systems are
non-local
in probability space in the sense that the expected momentum density and expected advected quantities are assumed to replace the drift momentum density and advected quantities respectively. These equations retain the fundamental properties of fluid dynamics and in addition, they have potential uses in climate change science since the quantities of interests have a clear sense of expectation and fluctuation, and the expected fluid motion is deterministic. For quadratic Hamiltonians, we find closed-form equations for the dynamics of the expectations and fluctuations of the Eulerian fluid variables. Total energy conservation enables us to show that, over time, the energy of the expected quantities is converted into the energy of the fluctuations, while the sum remains the same.

To illustrate the dual design capability of the proposed SLFT framework and the EA SFLT framework, explicit applications for the motion of fluids under gravity with SFLT noise will be given in the last part of the paper. Section
4.1
deals with the heavy top, which is a finite degree-of-freedom subsystem of fluid motion
[
33
]
; in section
4.2
for rotating shallow water dynamics; and in section
4.3
for the stochastic Euler-Boussinesq equations.

Slow + Fast decompositions of fluid flows.

In modelling geophysical fluid dynamics (GFD) in ocean, atmosphere or climate science, one tends to focus on balanced solution states which are near certain observed equilibrium
[
59
,
24
]
. These equilibria include hydrostatic and geostrophic balanced states, for example, in both the ocean and the atmosphere. Upsetting these balances can introduce both fast and slow temporal behaviour. The response depends on the range of the frequencies in the spectrum of excitations of the system away from equilibrium under the perturbations. When a separation in time scales exists in the response of the system to perturbations of one of its equilibria, then one may propose to average over the high frequency response and retain the remaining slow dynamics which remains near the equilibrium. This happens, for example, in the quasigeostrophic response to disturbances of geostrophic equilibria in the 2D rotating shallow water equations. Averaging over the high frequencies also often produces a slow
ponderomotive force
, due for example to a slowly varying envelope which modulates the high frequency response.
1

In taking these averages over the fast behaviour of the dynamics one must also deal properly with any resonances which would occur. However, the effects of resonances will be neglected in our discussion here.
In most situations in GFD, though, the solution only stays near the low-frequency
slow manifold
for a rather finite time before developing a high-frequency response, See, e.g., Lorenz
[

. In practice, for example in numerical weather prediction, the emergence of the high-frequency disturbances of the devoutly wished slow manifold introduces undeniable uncertainty which historically has often been handled by some sort of intervention, such as nonlinear re-initialisation
[

is commonly used in computational simulations to feed energy from the burgeoning unstable development of high-wavenumber excitations into large-scale coherent structures at low frequencies, so as to enhance the formation of eddies in ocean flows,

The geometric interplay between energy and circulation in ideal fluid dynamics.

Fluid dynamics transforms energy into circulation. As it turns out, this transformation is quite geometric. In particular, the solutions of Euler’s fluid equations for ideal incompressible flow describe geodesic curves parametrised by time on the manifold of volume-preserving diffeomorphisms (smooth invertible maps). These geodesic curves are defined with respect to the metric provided by the fluid’s kinetic energy, defined on the smooth, divergence-free, velocity vector fields which comprise the tangent space of the volume-preserving diffeomorphisms
{\rm SDiff}(\mathcal{D})
acting on the flow domain
\mathcal{D}
. This 1966 result of V. I. Arnold
[
4
]
introduced a fundamentally new geometric way of understanding energy and circulation in fluid dynamics. The incompressible Euler fluid case in
[
4
]
possesses the well-known conservation laws of energy and circulation, both of which arise via Noether’s theorem from symmetries of the Lagrangian in Hamilton’s variational principle under the right action of
{\rm SDiff}(\mathcal{D})
. Later, Arnold
[
5
]
noticed the topological nature of another conservation law for the Euler fluid equations which is known as helicity. In particular, the conserved helicity measures the topological linkage number of the vorticity field lines in Euler fluid dynamics.

Some of this geometric interplay between energy and circulation in ideal fluid dynamics already shows up in Kelvin’s circulation theorem
[
43
]
for ideal Euler fluids, which emerges as the Kelvin-Noether theorem from right-invariance (relabelling symmetry) of the Lagrangian in Hamilton’s principle when written in terms of the Eulerian representation,
[
41
]
.
In physical fluids, the particle-relabelling symmetry of the fluid Lagrangian in the Eulerian representation is broken from the full diffeomorphism group
G={\rm Diff}(\mathcal{D})
to its subgroup
G_{a}={\rm Diff}(\mathcal{D})|_{a_{0}}
which leaves invariant the initial conditions
a_{0}
for advected fluid variables, denoted
a
, such as the mass density and thermodynamic properties. The Lagrangian histories
x_{t}=g_{t}x_{0}
evolve as
\dot{x}_{t}=\dot{g}_{t}x_{0}=u(g_{t}x_{0},t)=u(x_{t},t)
, where the Eulerian velocity vector field given by
u:=\dot{g}_{t}g_{t}^{-1}
is right-invariant under the particle-relabelling
x_{0}\to y_{0}=h_{0}x_{0}
for any fixed
h_{0}\in G
.

In this geometrical setting for fluids in the Eulerian representation, the Legendre transform maps the Lagrangian variational formulation to the Hamiltonian formulation in which a Lie-Poisson bracket governs the motion generated by the Hamiltonian. This Lie-Poisson bracket is defined on the dual space of the Lie algebra of divergence-free vector fields
\mathfrak{X}_{\rm div}(\mathcal{D})
for Euler fluids. For ideal fluids with advected quantities, the Lie-Poisson bracket is defined on the dual space of the SDP Lie algebra
\mathfrak{X}(\mathcal{D})\circledS V(\mathcal{D})
.
As discussed in
[
41
]
, the fluid motion in each case represents the coadjoint action of the corresponding Lie algebra on its dual space.
For more details in the present context, refer to appendices
A
and
B
, particularly
B.3
.

Geometric formulation of advective transport.

The action of the full diffeomorphism group
G={\rm Diff}(\mathcal{D})
on its
order parameter
variables

represents fluid advection. Advection occurs by

(by right action by the inverse of the Lagrange-to-Euler map). This means the time-dependence of an advected quantity is given by the push-forward relation for composition of functions; namely,

Thus, an advected quantity

evolves by the

{\partial}_{t}a=-\mathcal{L}_{\dot{g}_{t}g_{t}^{-1}}a_{t}=-\mathcal{L}_{u_{t}}a\,,

in which
\mathcal{L}_{u_{t}}a
denotes the

of the advected quantity

by the time-dependent Eulerian velocity vector field,
u_{t}:=\dot{g}_{t}g_{t}^{-1}\in\mathfrak{X}(\mathcal{D})

2.1
Reduced Lagrange d’Alembert Pontryagin (RLDP) Principle

Stochastic non-inertial frames.

Newton’s law of motion in a non-inertial frame redefines momentum so as to introduce an additional force. The canonical example is the Coriolis force, which arises from redefining the momentum in a rotating frame of motion in terms of the velocity as viewed from an inertial frame. The CL vortex force arises in the fluid momentum equation by the addition of Stokes drift velocity to the frame of motion of the fluid momentum variable
m\in\mathfrak{X}^{*}
. In the present setting, it is natural to consider a stochastic addition to the momentum which corresponds to a stochastic change of referemce frame.

One variational principle which permits such additional forces is the
reduced Lagrange d’Alembert Pontryagin
(RLDP) principle. The general construction of fluid dynamics using the RLDP formulation is as follows

]

2.2
Energy preserving SFLT for semidirect-product motion and material entrainment

We next extend the SFLT approach to case of coadjoint motion of fluids carrying advected quantities such as mass and heat which are associated with potential energy.

Advected quantities are also known as order parameters in condensed matter physics.
The motion of advected quantities arises from the semidirect-product action of the diffeomorphisms on the vector spaces containing the advected quantities. The vector spaces containing the advected quantities comprise coset spaces obtained from symmetry-breaking of the full diffeomorphism group to the remaining isotropy subgroup of the initial conditions of the advected quantities.

This extension enables the derivation of stochastic vortex forces which model the uncertainty of unresolved slow-fast interaction effects as energy-preserving stochastic perturbations of fluid models which possess a potential energy. In addition to vortex forces, the introduction of stochasticity in the passive advection relations allows the modelling of quantities that do not passively follow the drift velocity field. \displaystyle\delta\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}{\left\langle{(m,a)}\,,\,{({\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}u,{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}b)}\right\rangle}-h(m,a)\,dt-\sum_{i}\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}{\left\langle{(F^{m}_{i},F^{a}_{i})}\,,\,{(\eta,w}\right\rangle}\circ dW^{i}_{t}=0\,,

where the variations have the same condition as principle (
2.21
).
The resulting equations are the following

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}m+{{\rm ad}}^{*}_{\frac{\delta h}{\delta m}}m\,dt+\frac{\delta h}{\delta a}\diamond a\,dt+\sum_{i}F^{m}_{i}\circ dW^{i}_{t}=0\,,\quad{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}a+\mathcal{L}_{\frac{\delta h}{\delta m}}a\,dt+\sum_{i}F^{a}_{i}\circ dW_{t}^{i}=0\,.

Remark 2.12

(Stochastic material entrainment modelled in equation (
2.19
))
.

When stochastic processes
f^{a}_{i}
are included in the Lie-derivative action on the fluid variables,
a(t)
, then one can no longer say that
a(t)
is passively advected by the flow
g\in G
, i.e.,
a(t)\neq a_{0}g^{-1}(t)
. Consider the case that the variable
a(t)
represents the mass density of the fluid. Then, the class of stochastic equations in (
2.19
) or (
2.20
) could model a fluid containing parcels whose density does not quite passively follow the drift velocity flow. Examples of such deviations from passive transport might include inertial fluid parcels whose density has a certain probability of being heavier or lighter than the ambient (or average, or expected) density. The motion of these inertial parcels would then be uncertain, as modelled by a stochastic variation in their density, relative to parcels undergoing passive advection by the flow. We include this feature of equation (
2.19
) because it may provide a useful single-fluid approach to dealing with stochastic entertainment of material particles into fluid flows such as Langmuir circulations. This stochastic model of material entrainment into fluid flows introduces probabilistic aspects into the theory, rather than dealing with the intricacies of multiphase flow models. The applications of this feature may include, for example, ice slurries in the Arctic Ocean, or dust clouds, or debris in tornadoes, or fluid flows with gas bubbles, or well-mixed oil spills, or transport of algae, or plastic detritus in the ocean. In the case where
f^{a}_{i}=0
for all
i
, then
a(t)
would be passively advected and would satisfy the standard relation
a(t)=a_{0}g^{-1}(t)
. For a recent review of deterministic LES turbulent models of this type of mixed-buoyancy fluid transport, see
[
11
]
.

Remark 2.13

(SFLT Kelvin-Noether theorem)
.

Note that the inclusion of forcing terms
f^{a}_{i}
on advected quantities
a
implies
a(t)\neq a_{0}g^{-1}
. This means one cannot take the pull back the Kelvin-Noether quantity
{\left\langle{\mathcal{K}(g(t)c_{0},a(t))}\,,\,{m(t)}\right\rangle}
by
g(t)
to the Kelvin-Noether quantity defined by the initial conditions
c_{0}
and
a_{0}
, i.e.
{\left\langle{\mathcal{K}(g(t)c_{0},a(t))}\,,\,{m(t)}\right\rangle}\neq{\left\langle{\mathcal{K}(c_{0},a_{0})}\,,\,{{{\rm Ad}}^{*}_{g}m(t)}\right\rangle}
. Hence there is no simple modification of the Kelvin-Noether theorem associated with (
2.19
). For fluids, the map
\mathcal{K}
is the circulation integral around a material loop
c
which is independent of the advected quantities
a
. Thus a Kelvin circulation theorem exists as formulated below.

Theorem 2.14

(SFLT Kelvin circulation theorem with entrainment)
.

Upon assuming that the fluid density
D
is also advected by the flow, so that
\partial_{t}D+\mathcal{L}_{u}D=0
, the Kelvin circulation theorem associated with (
2.19
) may be expressed as

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}\oint_{c(u)}\frac{m}{D}=\oint_{c(u)}\frac{1}{D}\left[-\sum_{i}{{\rm ad}}^{*}_{u}f^{m}_{i}\circ dW^{i}_{t}-\frac{\delta h}{\delta a}\diamond\left(a\,dt+\sum_{i}f^{a}_{i}\circ dW^{i}_{t}\right)\right],

(2.25)

where the notation of Lagrangian loop
c(u)
denotes the Lagrangian loop moving with the deterministic fluid velocity
u
, as in the deterministic case.

Remark 2.15
.

Note that the presence of the stochastic material entrainment terms
f^{i}_{a}
in equation (
2.25
) may have a significant effect as a source of circulation of the fluid flow.

Remark 2.16

(An unified variational approach)
.

By introducing stochastic Hamiltonians into the variational principle (
2.21
), one can formulate a stochastic RLDP principle which encompasses both SALT and SFLT. The augmented stochastic RLDP principle becomes

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\delta\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}{\left\langle{(m,a)}\,,\,{({\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}u,{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}b)}\right\rangle}-h(m,a)\,dt-\sum_{i}h_{i}(m,a)\circ dW^{i}_{t}\\
&\qquad\qquad-\sum_{i}\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}{\left\langle{(F^{m}_{i},F^{a}_{i})}\,,\,{(\eta,w)}\right\rangle}\circ dW^{i}_{t}=0\,.\end{split}

(2.26)

Here variations of
\delta(m,a)
of
(m,a)\in\mathfrak{s}^{*}
are arbitrary and variations
\delta(g,v)
of
(g,v)\in S
are given by

\displaystyle\delta{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}u={\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}\eta+{\left[{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}u}\,,\,{\eta}\right]}\quad\hbox{and}\quad\delta{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}b={\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}w-{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}b\,\eta+w{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}u\,,

where
(\eta,w)\in\mathfrak{s}
are arbitrary are vanishes at the boundaries.
The set of Hamiltonians

generates SALT type noise and the set of forces
(F^{m}_{i},F^{a}_{i})
generates SFLT type of noise.

3
Eulerian Averaged SFLT

Ed Lorenz captured the essence of the climate science problem in his celebrated unpublished paper
[
55
]
in which he motivated his discussion by invoking the old adage that

“Climate is what you expect. Weather is what you get.”

Lorenz’s lesson was that climate science is fundamentally probabilistic. Much later, this lesson inspired the derivation of the LA SALT fluid model, which also exploited an idea introduced in
[
21
]
to apply Lagrangian-averaging (LA) in
probability space
to the fluid equations governed by stochastic advection by Lie transport (SALT) which were introduced in
[
38
]
. The general theory of LA SALT and applications to 2D Euler-Boussinesq equations can be found in
[
22
]
and
[
3
]
respectively.

Here, we apply the probabilistic approach to derive the corresponding Eulerian-averaged SFLT model (EA SFLT) by decomposing the Eulerian solutions of the energy-preserving SFLT model into the sums of their expectations and their fluctuations. As for the energy-preserving SFLT models, the EA SFLT equations admit a Kelvin circulation theorem and preserve the deterministic energy. For systems resulting from quadratic Hamiltonians, this modification of the SFLT model allows the dynamics of the statistical properties of the solutions of EA-SFLT such as the evolution of the expectation of energy to be considered explicitly. As for the LA-SALT models, the EA-SFLT models can be viewed as the interaction of the expected quantities and the fluctuations in a conservative system. In this system, the energy of the expected quantities is dynamically converted into the energy of fluctuations whilst keeping the total energy invariant as shown in Theorem
3.2
. The Kelvin circulation theorem for EA-SFLT differs from that of SFLT by the presence of an additional forcing. This feature makes the EA-SFLT approach particularly apt for the examples of quadratic fluid Hamiltonians which are discussed in section
4
.

3.1
EA SFLT

In Eulerian Averaged SFLT, the expectation of the
Eulerian
quantity
m
from equation (
2.7
) is the taken over the underlying probability space, and the Eulerian Averaged Stochastic Forced Lie Poisson (EA SFLP) equation is proposed, as

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}m+{{\rm ad}}^{*}_{u}\mathbb{E}\left[{m}\right]\,dt+\sum_{i}{{\rm ad}}^{*}_{u}f^{i}\circ dW^{i}_{t}=0\,,\quad u=\frac{\delta h}{\delta m}.

(3.1)

This modification still preserves the deterministic energy of the original SFLP equation since

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}h(m)={\left\langle{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}m}\,,\,{u}\right\rangle}=-{\left\langle{{{\rm ad}}^{*}_{u}\mathbb{E}\left[{m}\right]\,dt+\sum_{i}{{\rm ad}}^{*}_{u}f^{i}\circ dW^{i}_{t}}\,,\,{u}\right\rangle}=0,

(3.2)

by anti-symmetry of of the
{{\rm ad}}
operation. The evolution of the expectation
\mathbb{E}[m]
can be determined by considering the Itô form of the equation (
3.1
)

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}m+{{\rm ad}}^{*}_{u}\mathbb{E}\left[{m}\right]\,dt+\sum_{i}{{\rm ad}}^{*}_{u}f^{i}\,dW^{i}_{t}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}{{\rm ad}}^{*}_{\sigma_{i}}f^{i}\,dt=0,

(3.3)

where
\sigma_{i}={\left({\frac{\delta^{2}h}{\delta m^{2}}}\,,\,{-{{\rm ad}}^{*}_{\frac{\delta h}{\delta m}}f_{i}}\right)}
. The proof of the Itô form is similar to the case of the SFLP equation in appendix
C
. Taking the expectation of (
3.3
) yields the following partial differential equation (PDE) for the expectation of the momentum density,
\mathbb{E}\left[{m}\right]
,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathbb{E}\left[{m}\right]+{{\rm ad}}^{*}_{\mathbb{E}\left[{u}\right]}\mathbb{E}\left[{m}\right]+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}{{\rm ad}}^{*}_{\mathbb{E}\left[{\sigma_{i}}\right]}f^{i}=0\,.

(3.4)

The expectation of (
3.3
) has produced a deterministic PDE because
{{\rm ad}}^{*}
is a linear operation and the expectation of
dW^{i}_{t}
vanishes by Itô’s Lemma. The PDE (
3.4
) closes, whenever the Hamiltonian is quadratic in
m
, i.e.
h=\frac{1}{2}{\left\langle{m}\,,\,{\mathbb{I}^{-1}m}\right\rangle}
where
I:\mathfrak{g}\rightarrow\mathfrak{g}^{*}
is a constant invertible symmetric operator which commutes with taking the expectation. Furthermore,
\mathbb{I}
is assumed to be positive definite. In this case, one finds the vector field

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{\sigma_{i}}\right]={\left({-{{\rm ad}}^{*}_{\mathbb{E}\left[{u}\right]}f^{i}}\,,\,{\frac{\delta^{2}h}{\delta m^{2}}}\right)}=-\mathbb{I}^{-1}{{\rm ad}}^{*}_{\mathbb{E}\left[{u}\right]}f^{i}\,.

Assuming quadratic Hamiltonian
h
, one can computes also the Hamiltonian function of the expectation of
m
,
\mathbb{E}\left[{m}\right]
to have

\displaystyle\partial_{t}h(\mathbb{E}\left[{m}\right])={\left\langle{\mathbb{E}\left[{u}\right]}\,,\,{\partial_{t}\mathbb{E}\left[{m}\right]}\right\rangle}=-\frac{1}{2}{\left\langle{\mathbb{I}^{-1}{{\rm ad}}^{*}_{\mathbb{E}\left[{u}\right]}f^{i}}\,,\,{{{\rm ad}}^{*}_{\mathbb{E}\left[{u}\right]}f^{i}}\right\rangle}<0\,,

(3.5)

Define fluctuations
u^{\prime}=u-\mathbb{E}\left[{u}\right]
,
m^{\prime}=m-\mathbb{E}\left[{m}\right]
and
\sigma_{i}^{\prime}=\sigma_{i}-\mathbb{E}\left[{\sigma_{i}}\right]
and obtain the fluctuation dynamics of
m
by subtracting (
3.4
) from (
3.3
) to obtain

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}m^{\prime}+{{\rm ad}}^{*}_{u^{\prime}}\mathbb{E}\left[{m}\right]\,dt+\sum_{i}{{\rm ad}}^{*}_{u}f^{i}\,dW^{i}_{t}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}{{\rm ad}}^{*}_{\sigma_{i}^{\prime}}f^{i}\,dt=0\,.

(3.6)

The time derivative of the Hamiltonian of the fluctuation of
m
,
h(m^{\prime})
is found as

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}h(m^{\prime})&={\left\langle{\frac{\delta h}{\delta m^{\prime}}}\,,\,{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}m^{\prime}}\right\rangle}+\frac{1}{2}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}{\left\langle{m^{\prime}}\,,\,{\frac{\delta h}{\delta m^{\prime}}}\right\rangle}_{t}\\
&={\left\langle{\frac{\delta h}{\delta m^{\prime}}}\,,\,{-{{\rm ad}}^{*}_{u^{\prime}}\mathbb{E}\left[{m}\right]\,dt-\sum_{k}{{\rm ad}}^{*}_{u}f^{k}\,dW^{k}_{t}-\frac{1}{2}{{\rm ad}}^{*}_{\sigma_{k}^{\prime}}f^{k}\,dt}\right\rangle}+\frac{1}{2}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}{\left\langle{m^{\prime}}\,,\,{\frac{\delta h}{\delta m^{\prime}}}\right\rangle}_{t}\,dt\end{split}

(3.7)

This expression simplifies dramatically when the Hamiltonian is quadratic, where

\displaystyle\frac{\delta h}{\delta m^{\prime}}=\mathbb{I}^{-1}m^{\prime}=u^{\prime},\quad\sigma_{i}^{\prime}=-\mathbb{I}^{-1}{{\rm ad}}^{*}_{u^{\prime}}f^{i}.

A dynamical expression can be calculated as

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}h(m^{\prime})&={\left\langle{u^{\prime}}\,,\,{-{{\rm ad}}^{*}_{u^{\prime}}\mathbb{E}\left[{m}\right]\,dt-\sum_{k}{{\rm ad}}^{*}_{u}f^{k}\,dW^{k}_{t}-\frac{1}{2}{{\rm ad}}^{*}_{\sigma_{k}^{\prime}}f^{k}\,dt}\right\rangle}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k}{\left\langle{{{\rm ad}}^{*}_{u}f^{k}}\,,\,{\mathbb{I}^{-1}{{\rm ad}}^{*}_{u}f^{k}}\right\rangle}\,dt\\
&=\sum_{k}{\left\langle{-{{\rm ad}}^{*}_{u}f^{k}\,dW^{k}_{t}-\frac{1}{2}{{\rm ad}}^{*}_{\sigma_{k}^{\prime}}f^{k}\,dt}\,,\,{u^{\prime}}\right\rangle}+\frac{1}{2}{\left\langle{{{\rm ad}}^{*}_{u}f^{k}}\,,\,{\mathbb{I}^{-1}{{\rm ad}}^{*}_{u}f^{k}}\right\rangle}\,dt\\
&=\sum_{k}{\left\langle{u^{\prime}}\,,\,{-{{\rm ad}}^{*}_{u}f^{k}}\right\rangle}\,dW^{k}_{t}+{\left\langle{\sigma_{k}^{\prime}}\,,\,{\frac{1}{2}{{\rm ad}}^{*}_{u^{\prime}}f^{k}}\right\rangle}\,dt+\frac{1}{2}{\left\langle{{{\rm ad}}^{*}_{u}f^{k}}\,,\,{\mathbb{I}^{-1}{{\rm ad}}^{*}_{u}f^{k}}\right\rangle}\,dt\\
&=\sum_{k}{\left\langle{u^{\prime}}\,,\,{-{{\rm ad}}^{*}_{u}f^{k}}\right\rangle}\,dW^{k}_{t}+{\left\langle{-\mathbb{I}^{-1}{{\rm ad}}^{*}_{u}f^{k}+\mathbb{I}^{-1}{{\rm ad}}^{*}_{\mathbb{E}\left[{u}\right]}f^{k}}\,,\,{\frac{1}{2}{{\rm ad}}^{*}_{u^{\prime}}f^{k}}\right\rangle}\,dt+\frac{1}{2}{\left\langle{{{\rm ad}}^{*}_{u}f^{k}}\,,\,{\mathbb{I}^{-1}{{\rm ad}}^{*}_{u}f^{k}}\right\rangle}\,dt\\
&=\sum_{k}{\left\langle{u^{\prime}}\,,\,{-{{\rm ad}}^{*}_{u}f^{k}}\right\rangle}\,dW^{k}_{t}+\frac{1}{2}{\left\langle{\mathbb{I}^{-1}{{\rm ad}}^{*}_{u}f^{k}}\,,\,{{{\rm ad}}^{*}_{\mathbb{E}\left[{u}\right]}f^{k}}\right\rangle}\,dt-\frac{1}{2}{\left\langle{\mathbb{I}^{-1}{{\rm ad}}^{*}_{\mathbb{E}\left[{u}\right]}f^{k}}\,,\,{{{\rm ad}}^{*}_{u}f^{k}}\right\rangle}\,dt\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad+\frac{1}{2}{\left\langle{\mathbb{I}^{-1}{{\rm ad}}^{*}_{\mathbb{E}\left[{u}\right]}f^{k}}\,,\,{{{\rm ad}}^{*}_{\mathbb{E}\left[{u}\right]}f^{k}}\right\rangle}\,dt\,.\end{split}

(3.8)

Taking the expectation yields

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathbb{E}\left[h(m^{\prime})\right]=\frac{1}{2}{\left\langle{\mathbb{I}^{-1}{{\rm ad}}^{*}_{\mathbb{E}\left[{u}\right]}f^{k}}\,,\,{{{\rm ad}}^{*}_{\mathbb{E}\left[{u}\right]}f^{k}}\right\rangle}\,.

(3.9)

The above calculation with (
3.5
) have proved the following.

Theorem 3.1

(Energy balance for EA SFLT)
.

The sum of energies
h(\mathbb{E}\left[{m}\right])+\mathbb{E}\left[{h(m^{\prime})}\right]
is preserved by the dynamics of EA SFLT in (
3.1
),

\displaystyle\partial_{t}h(\mathbb{E}\left[{m}\right])+\partial_{t}\mathbb{E}\left[{h(m^{\prime})}\right]=0\,.

(3.10)

Proof.

The previous calculation demonstrates this theorem by direct calculation. Having done so, an
alternative proof suggests itself, for quadratic Hamiltonians, one has
h(m)=h(\mathbb{E}\left[{m}\right]+m^{\prime})=const\Longrightarrow\mathbb{E}\left[{h(m)}\right]=h(\mathbb{E}\left[{m}\right])+\mathbb{E}\left[{h(m^{\prime})}\right]
.
∎

3.2

Then the stochastic time derivative of quadratic Hamiltonians of fluctuations can be computed in similar fashion as in the case without advected quantities.

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}h(m^{\prime},a^{\prime})&={\left\langle{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}m^{\prime}}\,,\,{\frac{\delta h}{\delta m^{\prime}}}\right\rangle}+{\left\langle{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}a^{\prime}}\,,\,{\frac{\delta h}{\delta a^{\prime}}}\right\rangle}+\frac{1}{2}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}{\left\langle{m^{\prime}}\,,\,{\frac{\delta h}{\delta m^{\prime}}}\right\rangle}_{t}+\frac{1}{2}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}{\left\langle{a^{\prime}}\,,\,{\frac{\delta h}{\delta a^{\prime}}}\right\rangle}_{t}\\
&=-\sum_{i}{\left\langle{{{\rm ad}}^{*}_{u}f^{i}+b\diamond f^{a}_{i}}\,,\,{u^{\prime}}\right\rangle}\,dW^{i}_{t}+{\left\langle{\mathcal{L}_{u}f^{a}_{i}}\,,\,{b^{\prime}}\right\rangle}dW^{i}_{t}\\
&\qquad-\frac{1}{2}{\left\langle{\mathbb{I}^{-1}\left({{\rm ad}}^{*}_{u^{\prime}}f^{i}+b^{\prime}\diamond f^{a}_{i}\right)}\,,\,{\left({{\rm ad}}^{*}_{u^{\prime}}f^{i}+b^{\prime}\diamond f^{a}_{i}\right)}\right\rangle}\,dt-\frac{1}{2}{\left\langle{\mathbb{J}^{-1}\mathcal{L}_{u^{\prime}}f^{a}_{i}}\,,\,{\mathcal{L}_{u^{\prime}}f^{a}_{i}}\right\rangle}\,dt\\
&\qquad+\frac{1}{2}{\left\langle{\mathbb{I}^{-1}\left({{\rm ad}}^{*}_{u}f^{i}+b\diamond f^{a}_{i}\right)}\,,\,{\left({{\rm ad}}^{*}_{u}f^{i}+b\diamond f^{a}_{i}\right)}\right\rangle}\,dt+\frac{1}{2}{\left\langle{\mathbb{J}^{-1}\mathcal{L}_{u}f^{a}_{i}}\,,\,{\mathcal{L}_{u}f^{a}_{i}}\right\rangle}\,dt\end{split}

(3.19)

Taking expectation gives

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathbb{E}\left[{h(m^{\prime},a^{\prime})}\right]=\sum_{i}\frac{1}{2}\left({\left\langle{\mathbb{I}^{-1}\left({{\rm ad}}^{*}_{\mathbb{E}\left[{u}\right]}f^{i}+\mathbb{E}\left[{b}\right]\diamond f^{a}_{i}\right)}\,,\,{\left({{\rm ad}}^{*}_{\mathbb{E}\left[{u}\right]}f^{i}+\mathbb{E}\left[{b}\right]\diamond f^{a}_{i}\right)}\right\rangle}+{\left\langle{\mathbb{J}^{-1}\mathcal{L}_{\mathbb{E}\left[{u}\right]}f^{a}_{i}}\,,\,{\mathcal{L}_{\mathbb{E}\left[{u}\right]}f^{a}_{i}}\right\rangle}\right)\,.

(3.20)

The above calculations with (
3.17
) proves the following theorem

Theorem 3.2

(Energy balance for EA SFLT with advected quantities)
.

Equations (
3.11
) lead to the energy balance,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}h(\mathbb{E}\left[{m}\right],\mathbb{E}\left[{a}\right])+\partial_{t}\mathbb{E}\left[{h(m^{\prime},a^{\prime})}\right]=0\,.

(3.21)

Proof.

The previous calculation demonstrates this theorem by direct calculation. Having done so, an
alternative proof suggests itself for quadratic Hamiltonians, since in this case one has
h(m,a)=h(\mathbb{E}\left[{m}\right]+m^{\prime},\mathbb{E}\left[{a}\right]+a^{\prime})=const\Longrightarrow\mathbb{E}\left[{h(m,a)}\right]=h(\mathbb{E}\left[{m}\right],\mathbb{E}\left[{a}\right])+\mathbb{E}\left[{h(m^{\prime},a^{\prime})}\right]
.
∎

Theorem 3.3

(EA SFLT Kelvin circulation theorem)
.

Upon assuming that the fluid density
D
is also advected by the flow, so that
\partial_{t}D+\mathcal{L}_{u}D=0
, the Kelvin circulation theorem may be expressed as

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}\oint_{c(u)}\frac{m}{D}=\oint_{c(u)}\mathcal{L}_{u}\left(\frac{m}{D}\right)\,dt+\frac{1}{D}\left[\frac{m}{D}\mathcal{L}_{u}D\,dt-{{\rm ad}}^{*}_{u}\mathbb{E}\left[{m}\right]\,dt-b\diamond\mathbb{E}\left[{a}\right]\,dt-\sum_{i}\left({{\rm ad}}^{*}_{u}f^{m}_{i}\circ dW^{i}_{t}+b\diamond f^{i}_{a}\circ dW^{i}_{t}\right)\right],

(3.22)

where the notation of Lagrangian loop
c(u)
denotes the Lagrangian loop moving with the deterministic fluid velocity
u
, as in the deterministic case.

Proof.

When
m\in\mathfrak{X}^{*}
, one can identify the
{{\rm ad}}^{*}
action as the Lie derivative, i.e.
{{\rm ad}}^{*}_{u}m=\mathcal{L}_{u}m
for all
u\in\mathfrak{u}
. Let
m=D\alpha
where
\alpha\in\Lambda^{1}
, the stochastic time derivative of
m
can be written as

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}m

\displaystyle=\alpha\,{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}D+D\,{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}\alpha=-\alpha\mathcal{L}_{u}D\,dt+D\,{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}\alpha,

(3.23)

thus

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}\alpha=\frac{\alpha}{D}\mathcal{L}_{u}D\,dt-\frac{1}{D}\left({{\rm ad}}^{*}_{u}\mathbb{E}\left[{m}\right]\,dt+b\diamond\mathbb{E}\left[{a}\right]\,dt+\sum_{i}({{\rm ad}}^{*}_{u}f^{i}\circ dW^{i}_{t}+b\diamond f^{a}_{i}\circ dW^{i}_{t})\right)

(3.24)

Inserting the expression of
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}\alpha
into the circulation integral to have the result.

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}\oint_{c(u)}\alpha&=\oint_{c(u)}\left({\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}+\mathcal{L}_{u\,dt}\right)\alpha\\
&=\oint_{c(u)}\mathcal{L}_{u}\alpha\,dt+\frac{\alpha}{D}\mathcal{L}_{u}D\,dt-\frac{1}{D}\left({{\rm ad}}^{*}_{u}\mathbb{E}\left[{m}\right]\,dt+b\diamond\mathbb{E}\left[{a}\right]\,dt+\sum_{i}({{\rm ad}}^{*}_{u}f^{i}\circ dW^{i}_{t}+b\diamond f^{a}_{i}\circ dW^{i}_{t})\right)\end{split}

(3.25)

∎

4
Examples of SFLT applications

4.1
Heavy Top

The motion of a heavy top under gravity is a good first example application for SFLT, because it is a finite degree-of-freedom subsystem of Euler-Boussinesq fluid motion
[
33
]
.
Following, e.g.,
[
37
]
, the configuration space of the heavy top can be taken as the semidirect-product Lie group of Euclidean motions by rotation and translation,
S={\rm SO}(3)\circledS\mathbb{R}^{3}
. The associated Lie algebra and its dual are, respectively,

\mathfrak{s}=\mathfrak{so}(3)\circledS\mathbb{R}^{3}\cong\mathbb{R}^{3}\circledS\mathbb{R}^{3}\quad\hbox{and}\quad\mathfrak{s}^{*}=\mathfrak{so}(3)^{*}\circledS\mathbb{R}^{3}\cong\mathbb{R}^{3}\circledS\mathbb{R}^{3}\,.

The natural pairing between
\mathbb{R}^{3}
and its dual is the dot-product, for the pairing of Euclidean vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
The heavy top Hamiltonian
H(\mbox{\boldmath{$\Pi$}},\mbox{\boldmath{$\Gamma$}})
comprises the sum of its rotational kinetic energy and its gravitational potential energy,

\displaystyle H(\mbox{\boldmath{$\Pi$}})=\frac{1}{2}\mbox{\boldmath{$\Pi$}}\cdot\mathbb{I}^{-1}\mbox{\boldmath{$\Pi$}}+mg\mbox{\boldmath{$\Gamma$}}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath{$\chi$}}\,.

(4.1)

The Hamiltonian for the heavy top is written in the body frame, in terms of the body angular momentum,
\Pi
, and the vertical unit vector as seen from the body,
\mbox{\boldmath{$\Gamma$}}=\mbox{\boldmath{$\widehat{z}$}}O(t)^{-1}
, where
O(t)\in SO(3)
is the time-dependent rotation from the reference configuration of the top to its current configuration.
Here,
\mathbb{I}
is the moment of inertia in the body frame and
\chi
is the vector from the point of support to the centre of mass in the body frame. Finally,
m
is the mass of the body and
g
is the constant acceleration of gravity.

The variational derivatives of the heavy top Hamiltonian are

{dH}/{d\mbox{\boldmath{$\Pi$}}}=\mathbb{I}^{-1}\mbox{\boldmath{$\Pi$}}=\mbox{\boldmath{$\Omega$}}\quad\hbox{and}\quad{dH}/{d\mbox{\boldmath{$\Gamma$}}}=mg\mbox{\boldmath{$\chi$}}\,.

Upon using the ‘hat’ map isomorphism
[
37
]
to identify the Lie derivative and
{{\rm ad}}^{*}
operation as the cross product in the
\mathbb{R}^{3}
representation,
the deterministic LP equation is written as

\displaystyle\frac{d\mbox{\boldmath{$\Pi$}}}{dt}=\mbox{\boldmath{$\Pi$}}\times\mbox{\boldmath{$\Omega$}}+\mbox{\boldmath{$\Gamma$}}\times mg\mbox{\boldmath{$\chi$}},\quad\frac{d\mbox{\boldmath{$\Pi$}}}{dt}=\mbox{\boldmath{$\Gamma$}}\times\mbox{\boldmath{$\Omega$}}\,,

which can also be written in Poisson operator form

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\begin{bmatrix}\mbox{\boldmath{$\Pi$}}\\
\mbox{\boldmath{$\Gamma$}}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\mbox{\boldmath{$\Pi$}}\times&\mbox{\boldmath{$\Gamma$}}\times\\
\mbox{\boldmath{$\Gamma$}}\times&0\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\delta H/\delta\mbox{\boldmath{$\Pi$}}\\
\delta H/\delta\mbox{\boldmath{$\Gamma$}}\end{bmatrix}.

Defining the force vectors
\mbox{\boldmath{$f^{m}_{i}$}},\mbox{\boldmath{$f^{a}_{i}$}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, the SFLP equations corresponding to (
2.19
) may be written as

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}\mbox{\boldmath{$\Pi$}}&=\left(\mbox{\boldmath{$\Pi$}}\,dt+\sum_{i}\mbox{\boldmath{$f^{m}_{i}$}}\circ dW^{i}_{t}\right)\times\mbox{\boldmath{$\Omega$}}+\left(\mbox{\boldmath{$\Gamma$}}\,dt+\sum_{i}\mbox{\boldmath{$f^{a}_{i}$}}\circ dW^{i}_{t}\right)\times mg\mbox{\boldmath{$\chi$}}\,,\\
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}\mbox{\boldmath{$\Gamma$}}&=\left(\mbox{\boldmath{$\Gamma$}}\,dt+\sum_{i}\mbox{\boldmath{$f^{a}_{i}$}}\circ dW^{i}_{t}\right)\times\mbox{\boldmath{$\Omega$}}\,.\end{split}

(4.2)

When written in Poisson bracket form, these equations become

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}\begin{bmatrix}\mbox{\boldmath{$\Pi$}}\\
\mbox{\boldmath{$\Gamma$}}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}(\mbox{\boldmath{$\Pi$}}\,dt+\sum_{i}\mbox{\boldmath{$f^{m}_{i}$}}\circ dW^{i}_{t})\times&(\mbox{\boldmath{$\Gamma$}}\,dt+\sum_{i}\mbox{\boldmath{$f^{a}_{i}$}}\circ dW^{i}_{t})\times\\
(\mbox{\boldmath{$\Gamma$}}\,dt+\sum_{i}\mbox{\boldmath{$f^{a}_{i}$}}\circ dW^{i}_{t})\times&0\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\delta H/\delta\mbox{\boldmath{$\Pi$}}\\
\delta H/\delta\mbox{\boldmath{$\Gamma$}}\end{bmatrix}.

(4.3)

One observes that these equations preserve the Hamiltonian in (
4.1
), which follows because of skew symmetry of the matrix Poisson operator.

Eulerian averaged heavy top

Consider the EA SFLP equations associated to (
4.2
), which read

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}\mbox{\boldmath{$\Pi$}}&=\left(\mathbb{E}\left[{\mbox{\boldmath{$\Pi$}}}\right]\,dt+\sum_{i}\mbox{\boldmath{$f^{m}_{i}$}}\circ dW^{i}_{t}\right)\times\mbox{\boldmath{$\Omega$}}+\left(\mathbb{E}\left[{\mbox{\boldmath{$\Gamma$}}}\right]\,dt+\sum_{i}\mbox{\boldmath{$f^{a}_{i}$}}\circ dW^{i}_{t}\right)\times mg\mbox{\boldmath{$\chi$}}\,,\\
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}\mbox{\boldmath{$\Gamma$}}&=\left(\mathbb{E}\left[{\mbox{\boldmath{$\Gamma$}}}\right]\,dt+\sum_{i}\mbox{\boldmath{$f^{a}_{i}$}}\circ dW^{i}_{t}\right)\times\mbox{\boldmath{$\Omega$}}\,.\end{split}

(4.4)

Energy conservation is immediate since the Poisson struture is preserved. In Itô form, these equation reads

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}\mbox{\boldmath{$\Pi$}}&=\left(\mathbb{E}\left[{\mbox{\boldmath{$\Pi$}}}\right]\,dt+\sum_{i}\mbox{\boldmath{$f^{m}_{i}$}}\,dW^{i}_{t}\right)\times\mbox{\boldmath{$\Omega$}}+\left(\mathbb{E}\left[{\mbox{\boldmath{$\Gamma$}}}\right]\,dt+\sum_{i}\mbox{\boldmath{$f^{a}_{i}$}}\,dW^{i}_{t}\right)\times mg\mbox{\boldmath{$\chi$}}\\
&\qquad\qquad+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}\mbox{\boldmath{$f^{m}_{i}$}}\times\mathbb{I}^{-1}\left(\mbox{\boldmath{$f^{m}_{i}$}}\times\mbox{\boldmath{$\Omega$}}+\mbox{\boldmath{$f^{a}_{i}$}}\times mg\mbox{\boldmath{$\chi$}}\right)\,,\\
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}\mbox{\boldmath{$\Gamma$}}&=\left(\mathbb{E}\left[{\mbox{\boldmath{$\Gamma$}}}\right]\,dt+\sum_{i}\mbox{\boldmath{$f^{a}_{i}$}}\,dW^{i}_{t}\right)\times\mbox{\boldmath{$\Omega$}}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}\mbox{\boldmath{$f^{m}_{i}$}}\times\mathbb{I}^{-1}\left(\mbox{\boldmath{$f^{m}_{i}$}}\times\mbox{\boldmath{$\Omega$}}+\mbox{\boldmath{$f^{a}_{i}$}}\times mg\mbox{\boldmath{$\chi$}}\right)\,.\end{split}

(4.5)

Taking expectations to have the time evolution of expectations
\mathbb{E}\left[{\mbox{\boldmath{$\Pi$}}}\right]
and
\mathbb{E}\left[{\mbox{\boldmath{$\Gamma$}}}\right]
as

\displaystyle\begin{split}\partial_{t}\mathbb{E}\left[{\mbox{\boldmath{$\Pi$}}}\right]&=\mathbb{E}\left[{\mbox{\boldmath{$\Pi$}}}\right]\times\mathbb{E}\left[{\mbox{\boldmath{$\Omega$}}}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[{\mbox{\boldmath{$\Gamma$}}}\right]\times mg\mbox{\boldmath{$\chi$}}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}\mbox{\boldmath{$f^{m}_{i}$}}\times\mathbb{I}^{-1}\left(\mbox{\boldmath{$f^{m}_{i}$}}\times\mathbb{E}\left[{\mbox{\boldmath{$\Omega$}}}\right]+\mbox{\boldmath{$f^{a}_{i}$}}\times mg\mathbb{E}\left[{\mbox{\boldmath{$\chi$}}}\right]\right)\,,\\
\partial_{t}\mathbb{E}\left[{\mbox{\boldmath{$\Gamma$}}}\right]&=\mathbb{E}\left[{\mbox{\boldmath{$\Gamma$}}}\right]\times\mathbb{E}\left[{\mbox{\boldmath{$\Omega$}}}\right]+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}\mbox{\boldmath{$f^{m}_{i}$}}\times\mathbb{I}^{-1}\left(\mbox{\boldmath{$f^{m}_{i}$}}\times\mathbb{E}\left[{\mbox{\boldmath{$\Omega$}}}\right]+\mbox{\boldmath{$f^{a}_{i}$}}\times mg\mathbb{E}\left[{\mbox{\boldmath{$\chi$}}}\right]\right)\,.\end{split}

(4.6)

As the Hamiltonian is quadratic, the above equations close and we have

\displaystyle\partial_{t}H(\mathbb{E}\left[{\mbox{\boldmath{$\Pi$}}}\right],\mathbb{E}\left[{\mbox{\boldmath{$\Gamma$}}}\right])=-\frac{1}{2}\left(\mbox{\boldmath{$f^{m}_{i}$}}\times\mathbb{E}\left[{\mbox{\boldmath{$\Omega$}}}\right]+\mbox{\boldmath{$f^{a}_{i}$}}\times mg\mathbb{E}\left[{\mbox{\boldmath{$\chi$}}}\right]\right)\mathbb{I}^{-1}\left(\mbox{\boldmath{$f^{m}_{i}$}}\times\mathbb{E}\left[{\mbox{\boldmath{$\Omega$}}}\right]+\mbox{\boldmath{$f^{a}_{i}$}}\times mg\mathbb{E}\left[{\mbox{\boldmath{$\chi$}}}\right]\right)\,.

(4.7)

If the inertial tensor
\mathbb{I}
is positive definite, the energy of the expectations
\mathbb{E}\left[{\mbox{\boldmath{$\Pi$}}}\right]
and
\mathbb{E}\left[{\mbox{\boldmath{$\Gamma$}}}\right]
will decay to zero. Defining the fluctuation of
\Pi
and
\Gamma
as
\mbox{\boldmath{$\Pi^{\prime}$}}:=\mbox{\boldmath{$\Pi$}}-\mathbb{E}\left[{\mbox{\boldmath{$\Pi$}}}\right]
and
\mbox{\boldmath{$\Gamma^{\prime}$}}:=\mbox{\boldmath{$\Gamma$}}-\mathbb{E}\left[{\mbox{\boldmath{$\Gamma$}}}\right]
respectively, the evolution of the fluctuation can be written in Itô form as

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}\mbox{\boldmath{$\Pi^{\prime}$}}&=\left(\mbox{\boldmath{$\Pi^{\prime}$}}\,dt+\sum_{i}\mbox{\boldmath{$f^{m}_{i}$}}\,dW^{i}_{t}\right)\times\mbox{\boldmath{$\Omega^{\prime}$}}+\left(\mbox{\boldmath{$\Gamma^{\prime}$}}\,dt+\sum_{i}\mbox{\boldmath{$f^{a}_{i}$}}\,dW^{i}_{t}\right)\times mg\mbox{\boldmath{$\chi^{\prime}$}}\\
&\qquad\qquad+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}\mbox{\boldmath{$f^{m}_{i}$}}\times\mathbb{I}^{-1}\left(\mbox{\boldmath{$f^{m}_{i}$}}\times\mbox{\boldmath{$\Omega^{\prime}$}}+\mbox{\boldmath{$f^{a}_{i}$}}\times mg\mbox{\boldmath{$\chi^{\prime}$}}\right)\,,\\
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}\mbox{\boldmath{$\Gamma^{\prime}$}}&=\left(\mathbb{E}\left[{\mbox{\boldmath{$\Gamma$}}}\right]\,dt+\sum_{i}\mbox{\boldmath{$f^{a}_{i}$}}\,dW^{i}_{t}\right)\times\mbox{\boldmath{$\Omega^{\prime}$}}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}\mbox{\boldmath{$f^{m}_{i}$}}\times\mathbb{I}^{-1}\left(\mbox{\boldmath{$f^{m}_{i}$}}\times\mbox{\boldmath{$\Omega^{\prime}$}}+\mbox{\boldmath{$f^{a}_{i}$}}\times mg\mbox{\boldmath{$\chi^{\prime}$}}\right)\,.\end{split}

(4.8)

The energy of the fluctuations
h(\mbox{\boldmath{$\Pi^{\prime}$}},\mbox{\boldmath{$\Gamma^{\prime}$}})
has evoluation equation after using Itô’s Lemma

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}h(\mbox{\boldmath{$\Pi^{\prime}$}},\mbox{\boldmath{$\Gamma^{\prime}$}})&=\sum_{i}\mbox{\boldmath{$\Omega^{\prime}$}}\cdot\left(\mbox{\boldmath{$f^{m}_{i}$}}\times\mbox{\boldmath{$\Omega^{\prime}$}}+\mbox{\boldmath{$f^{a}_{i}$}}\times mg\mbox{\boldmath{$\chi^{\prime}$}}\right)\,dW^{i}_{t}+mg\mbox{\boldmath{$\chi^{\prime}$}}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath{$f^{a}_{i}$}}\times\mbox{\boldmath{$\Omega^{\prime}$}}\,dW^{i}_{t}\\
&\qquad\qquad-\frac{1}{2}\left(\mbox{\boldmath{$f^{m}_{i}$}}\times\mbox{\boldmath{$\Omega^{\prime}$}}+\mbox{\boldmath{$f^{a}_{i}$}}\times mg\mbox{\boldmath{$\chi^{\prime}$}}\right)\mathbb{I}^{-1}\left(\mbox{\boldmath{$f^{m}_{i}$}}\times\mbox{\boldmath{$\Omega^{\prime}$}}+\mbox{\boldmath{$f^{a}_{i}$}}\times mg\mbox{\boldmath{$\chi^{\prime}$}}\right)\\
&\qquad\qquad+\frac{1}{2}\left(\mbox{\boldmath{$f^{m}_{i}$}}\times\mbox{\boldmath{$\Omega$}}+\mbox{\boldmath{$f^{a}_{i}$}}\times mg\mbox{\boldmath{$\chi$}}\right)\mathbb{I}^{-1}\left(\mbox{\boldmath{$f^{m}_{i}$}}\times\mbox{\boldmath{$\Omega$}}+\mbox{\boldmath{$f^{a}_{i}$}}\times mg\mbox{\boldmath{$\chi$}}\right)\end{split}

(4.9)

Taking the expectation yields

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathbb{E}\left[{h(\mbox{\boldmath{$\Pi^{\prime}$}},\mbox{\boldmath{$\Gamma^{\prime}$}})}\right]=\frac{1}{2}\left(\mbox{\boldmath{$f^{m}_{i}$}}\times\mathbb{E}\left[{\mbox{\boldmath{$\Omega$}}}\right]+\mbox{\boldmath{$f^{a}_{i}$}}\times mg\mathbb{E}\left[{\mbox{\boldmath{$\chi$}}}\right]\right)\mathbb{I}^{-1}\left(\mbox{\boldmath{$f^{m}_{i}$}}\times\mathbb{E}\left[{\mbox{\boldmath{$\Omega$}}}\right]+\mbox{\boldmath{$f^{a}_{i}$}}\times mg\mathbb{E}\left[{\mbox{\boldmath{$\chi$}}}\right]\right)\,.

(4.10)

Together with the expression of
\partial_{t}H(\mathbb{E}\left[{\mbox{\boldmath{$\Pi$}}}\right],\mathbb{E}\left[{\mbox{\boldmath{$\Gamma$}}}\right])
, we have exemplified Theorem
3.2
for the EA HT dynamics.

4.2
Energy-preserving stochastic rotating shallow water equations (RSW)

Let
S=\text{Diff}(\mathcal{D})\circledS\text{Den}(\mathcal{D})
, where
\text{Diff}(\mathcal{D})
denotes the group of diffeomorphisms acting on the planar domain
\mathcal{D}
. Let
\eta\in\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{D})
, and let
\eta\,dV\in\text{Den}(\mathcal{D})
denote the density on
\mathcal{D}
. The associated Lie algebra and its dual are
\mathfrak{s}=\mathfrak{X}(\mathcal{D})\circledS\text{Den}(\mathcal{D})
and
\mathfrak{s}^{*}=(\Lambda^{1}(\mathcal{D})\otimes\text{Den}(\mathcal{D}))\circledS\text{Den}(\mathcal{D})
.
We denote
{\bf u}\cdot\frac{\partial}{\partial{\bf x}}=u\in\mathfrak{X}(\mathcal{D})
and
m={\bf m}\cdot d{\bf x}\otimes dV\in\Lambda^{1}(\mathcal{D})\otimes\text{Den}(\mathcal{D})
. In this notation, the LP equations can be written in Cartesian coordinates as the following partial differential equations,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\frac{{\bf m}}{\eta}\right)+({\bf u}\cdot{\bm{\nabla}})\frac{{\bf m}}{\eta}+\frac{m_{j}}{\eta}{\bm{\nabla}}u^{j}+{\bm{\nabla}}\frac{\delta H}{\delta\eta}=0\,,\qquad\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\eta+{\bm{\nabla}\cdot}(\eta{\bf u})=0\,,

(4.11)

and the LP operator can be written as

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\begin{bmatrix}m_{i}\\
\eta\end{bmatrix}=-\begin{bmatrix}\partial_{j}m_{i}+m_{j}\partial_{i}&\eta\partial_{i}\\
\partial_{j}\eta&0\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\delta H/\delta m_{j}\\
\delta H/\delta\eta\\
\end{bmatrix}\,.

(4.12)

For rotating shallow water equations (RSW) in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, the Hamiltonian
H
is given by

\displaystyle H=\int_{\mathcal{D}}\frac{1}{2\epsilon\eta}\left|{\bf m}-\eta{\bf R}\right|^{2}+\frac{(\eta-B)^{2}}{\epsilon\mathcal{F}}\,d^{2}x\,,

(4.13)

in which
\epsilon\ll 1
denotes Rossby number and
\mathcal{F}=O(1)
denotes the Froude number. The mean depth is
B
and the surface elevation is
(\eta-B)
.
The reduced Legendre transform yields
{\bf u}={\delta H}/{\delta{\bf m}}=({\bf m}-\eta{\bf R})/{\epsilon\eta}
. The variational derivatives of the RSW Hamiltonian are obtained as

\displaystyle\delta H=\int_{\mathcal{D}}{\bf u}\cdot\delta{\bf m}+\left(\frac{\eta-B}{\epsilon\mathcal{F}}-\frac{\epsilon}{2}|{\bf u}|^{2}-{\bf u}\cdot{\bf R}\right)\cdot\delta\eta\,d^{2}x\,.

Substituting into (
4.11
) and using the relation
(\hbox{curl}{\bf u}){\bm{\times}}{\bf v}+{\bm{\nabla}}({\bf u}\cdot{\bf v})=({\bf u}\cdot{\bm{\nabla}}){\bf v}+u_{i}{\bm{\nabla}}v^{i}
yields the standard set of RSW equations governing motion and continuity,

\displaystyle\epsilon\frac{\partial{\bf u}}{\partial t}+\left(\hbox{curl}{\bf R}+\epsilon\,\hbox{curl}{\bf u}\right){\bm{\times}}{\bf u}+{\bm{\nabla}}\left(\frac{\eta-B}{\epsilon\mathcal{F}}+\frac{\epsilon}{2}|{\bf u}|^{2}\right)=0\,,\quad\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\eta+{\bm{\nabla}\cdot}(\eta{\bf u})=0\,.

(4.14)

Consider the Poisson operator of the form (
2.20
) applied to
\mathfrak{s}^{*}
. It reads

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}\begin{bmatrix}m_{i}\\
\eta\end{bmatrix}=-\begin{bmatrix}\partial_{j}m_{i}+m_{j}\partial_{i}&\eta\partial_{i}\\
\partial_{j}\eta&0\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\delta H/\delta m_{j}\\
\delta H/\delta\eta\\
\end{bmatrix}\,dt-\sum_{k}\begin{bmatrix}\partial_{j}f^{k}_{i}+f^{k}_{j}\partial_{i}&g^{k}\partial_{i}\\
\partial_{j}g^{k}&0\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\delta H/\delta m_{j}\\
\delta H/\delta\eta\\
\end{bmatrix}\circ dW_{t}^{k}\,,

(4.15)

where
f^{k}_{i}
are the components of
{\bf f}^{k}
such that
f^{k}={\bf f}^{k}\cdot d{\bf x}\otimes dV\in\Lambda^{1}(\mathcal{D})\otimes\text{Den}(\mathcal{D})
and
g^{k}\in\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{D})
for all
k
. Then the SFLP equations become

\displaystyle\begin{split}&{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}m_{i}+(\partial_{j}(m_{i}u^{j})+m_{j}\partial_{i}u^{j})\,dt+(\partial_{j}(f^{k}_{i}u^{j})+\sum_{k}f^{k}_{j}\partial_{i}u^{j})\circ dW_{t}^{k}+\eta\partial_{i}\frac{\delta H}{\delta\eta}\,dt+g^{k}\partial_{i}\frac{\delta H}{\delta\eta}\circ dW_{t}^{k}=0,\\
&{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}\eta+{\bm{\nabla}\cdot}(\eta{\bf u})\,dt+\sum_{k}{\bm{\nabla}\cdot}(g^{k}{\bf u})\circ dW_{t}^{k}=0.\end{split}

(4.16)

Remark 4.1
.

When noise is introduced into the continuity equation for density
\eta
, one can still write the momentum equation in terms of
{{\bf m}}/{\eta}
. Namely, it reads

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}\frac{m_{i}}{\eta}&+\left(u^{j}\partial_{j}\frac{m_{i}}{\eta}+\frac{m_{j}}{\eta}\partial_{i}u^{j}\right)\,dt+\partial_{i}\frac{\delta H}{\delta\eta}\,dt\\
&+\sum_{k}\frac{1}{\eta}\left(-\frac{m_{i}}{\eta}\partial_{j}(g^{k}u^{j})+\partial_{j}(f^{k}_{i}u^{j})+f^{k}_{j}\partial_{i}u^{j}+g^{k}\partial_{i}\frac{\delta H}{\delta\eta}\right)\circ dW_{t}^{k}=0,\end{split}

(4.17)

where the inhomogenous term
-\eta^{-2}{m_{j}}\partial_{i}(g^{k}u^{j})
in the second line of (
4.17
) is due to the modified advection relation for
\eta\,dV
. The SFLP equations for RSW dynamics in (
4.16
) written more succinctly using Lie derivatives and denoting the momentum 1-form as
\alpha={\bf m}\cdot d{\bf x}
, then

\displaystyle\begin{split}&{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}\frac{\alpha}{\eta}+\left(\mathcal{L}_{u}\frac{\alpha}{\eta}+{\bf d}\frac{\delta H}{\delta\eta}\right)\,dt+\sum_{k}\frac{1}{\eta\,dV}\left(-\frac{\alpha}{\eta}\mathcal{L}_{u}g^{k}+\mathcal{L}_{u}f^{k}+\frac{\delta H}{\delta\eta}\diamond g^{k}\right)\circ dW^{k}_{t}=0,\\
&{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}(\eta\,dV)+\mathcal{L}_{u}(\eta\,dV)\,dt+\sum_{k}\mathcal{L}_{u}(g^{k}_{i}\,dV)\circ dW_{t}^{i}=0\,.\end{split}

(4.18)

In vector calculus notation, the stochastic RSW equation obtained by substituting the variational derivatives of
H
into equation (
4.15
) yields

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\epsilon{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}{\bf u}+\left(\hbox{curl}{\bf R}+\epsilon\,\hbox{curl}{\bf u}\right){\bm{\times}}{\bf u}\,dt+{\bm{\nabla}}\left(\frac{\eta-B}{\epsilon\mathcal{F}}+\frac{\epsilon}{2}|{\bf u}|^{2}\right)\,dt\\
&\quad+\sum_{k}\frac{1}{\eta}\left(-\left(\epsilon{\bf u}+{\bf R}\right){\bm{\nabla}\cdot}(g^{k}{\bf u})+(\hbox{curl}{\bf f}^{k}){\bm{\times}}{\bf u}+{\bm{\nabla}}({\bf f}^{k}\cdot{\bf u})+{\bf f}^{k}{\bm{\nabla}\cdot}{\bf u}+g^{k}{\bm{\nabla}}\pi_{RSW}\right)\circ dW_{t}^{k}=0\,,\\
&{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}\eta+{\bm{\nabla}\cdot}(\eta{\bf u})\,dt+\sum_{k}{\bm{\nabla}\cdot}(g^{k}{\bf u})\circ dW_{t}^{k}=0,\end{split}

(4.19)

where
\pi_{RSW}=\left(\frac{\eta-B}{\epsilon\mathcal{F}}-\frac{\epsilon}{2}|{\bf u}|^{2}-{\bf u}\cdot{\bf R}\right)
.

The corresponding Kelvin circulation theorem follows easily from (
4.18
) in geometric form, as

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}\oint_{c(u)}\frac{\alpha}{\eta}=-\oint_{c(u)}\sum_{k}\frac{1}{\eta\,dV}\left(-\frac{\alpha}{\eta}\mathcal{L}_{u}g^{k}+\mathcal{L}_{u}f^{k}+g^{k}d\frac{\delta H}{\delta\eta}\right)\circ dW^{k}_{t}\,.

(4.20)

The stochastic RSW equations in (
4.19
) enable the Kelvin circulation equation (
4.20
) to be written in vector calculus form as

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}\oint_{c(u)}(\epsilon{\bf u}+{\bf R})\cdot d{\bf x}

(4.21)

\displaystyle\quad=-\oint_{c(u)}\sum_{k}\frac{1}{\eta}\left(-\left(\epsilon{\bf u}+{\bf R}\right){\bm{\nabla}\cdot}(g^{k}{\bf u})+(\hbox{curl}{\bf f}^{k}){\bm{\times}}{\bf u}+{\bm{\nabla}}({\bf f}^{k}\cdot{\bf u})+{\bf f}^{k}({\bm{\nabla}\cdot}{\bf u})+g^{k}{\bm{\nabla}}\pi_{RSW}\right)\cdot d{\bf x}\circ dW_{t}^{k}.

Here, ones sees the effects of the material entrainment terms proportional to
g^{k}
appearing in the generation of Kelvin circulation.
Setting
g^{k}=0
simplifies equation (
4.20
) to

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}\oint_{c(u)}\frac{\alpha}{\eta}=-\oint_{c(u)}\sum_{k}\frac{1}{\eta\,dV}\left(\mathcal{L}_{u}f^{k}\right)\circ dW^{k}_{t}\,.

(4.22)

The vector form of the motion equation also simplifies for
g^{k}=0
to

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\epsilon{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}{\bf u}+\left(\hbox{curl}{\bf R}+\epsilon\,\hbox{curl}{\bf u}\right){\bm{\times}}{\bf u}\,dt+{\bm{\nabla}}\left(\frac{\eta-B}{\epsilon\mathcal{F}}+\frac{\epsilon}{2}|{\bf u}|^{2}\right)\,dt\\
&\qquad=-\sum_{k}\frac{1}{\eta}\left((\hbox{curl}{\bf f}^{k}){\bm{\times}}{\bf u}+{\bm{\nabla}}({\bf f}^{k}\cdot{\bf u})+{\bf f}^{k}{\bm{\nabla}\cdot}{\bf u}\right)\circ dW_{t}^{k}\,,\end{split}

(4.23)

whose right hand side may be regarded as a compressible version of the CL vortex force. The Ito form is

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\epsilon{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}{\bf u}+\left(\hbox{curl}{\bf R}+\epsilon\,\hbox{curl}{\bf u}\right){\bm{\times}}{\bf u}\,dt+{\bm{\nabla}}\left(\frac{\eta-B}{\epsilon\mathcal{F}}+\frac{\epsilon}{2}|{\bf u}|^{2}\right)\,dt\\
&\qquad+\sum_{k}\frac{1}{\eta}\left((\hbox{curl}{\bf f}^{k}){\bm{\times}}{\bf u}+{\bm{\nabla}}({\bf f}^{k}\cdot{\bf u})+{\bf f}^{k}{\bm{\nabla}\cdot}{\bf u}\right)\,dW_{t}^{k}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k}\frac{1}{\eta}\left((\hbox{curl}{\bf f}^{k}){\bm{\times}}{\bm{\sigma}}^{k}+{\bm{\nabla}}({\bf f}^{k}\cdot{\bm{\sigma}}^{k})+{\bf f}^{k}{\bm{\nabla}\cdot}{\bm{\sigma}}^{k}\right)\,dt\,,\end{split}

(4.24)

where
{\bm{\sigma}}^{k}=\frac{1}{\eta}\left((\hbox{curl}{\bf f}^{k}){\bm{\times}}{\bf u}+{\bm{\nabla}}({\bf f}^{k}\cdot{\bf u})+{\bf f}^{k}{\bm{\nabla}\cdot}{\bf u}\right)
. By taking the exterior derivative of equation (
4.18
) and noting
{\bf d}\frac{\alpha}{\eta}=\eta q\,dV
where
q
is the potential vorticity and
dV
is the area element, one finds that the vorticity density
\eta q\,dV
satisfies

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}(\eta q\,dV)+\mathcal{L}_{u}(\eta q\,dV)\,dt+\sum_{k}{\bf d}\frac{1}{\eta\,dV}\left[-\frac{\alpha}{\eta}\mathcal{L}_{u}g^{k}+\mathcal{L}_{u}f^{k}+g^{k}{\bf d}\frac{\delta H}{\delta\eta}\right]=0\,.

(4.25)

Remark 4.2
.

In coordinates, the last two summands in equation (
4.25
) can be written as divergences, so one finds the pathwise conservation law
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}\int_{\mathcal{D}}(\eta q\,dV)=0
for appropriate (homogeneous, or periodic) boundary conditions.

4.3
Euler-Boussinesq (EB) equations

Consider the case where
S=\text{Diff}(\mathcal{D})\circledS\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{D})\circledS\text{Den}(\mathcal{D})
, where
D\,dV\in\text{Den}(\mathcal{D})
and
b\in\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{D})
. The Poisson operator form of the LP equation in this case is

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\begin{bmatrix}m_{i}\\
D\\
b\end{bmatrix}=-\begin{bmatrix}\partial_{j}m_{i}+m_{j}\partial_{i}&D\partial_{i}&-b_{,i}\\
\partial_{j}D&0&0\\
b_{,j}&0&0\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\delta H/\delta m_{j}\\
\delta H/\delta D\\
\delta H/\delta b\end{bmatrix}\,.

(4.26)

The stochastic extension of equation (
2.20
) to include buoyancy reads

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}\begin{bmatrix}m_{i}\\
D\\
b\end{bmatrix}=-\begin{bmatrix}\partial_{j}m_{i}+m_{j}\partial_{i}&D\partial_{i}&-b_{,i}\\
\partial_{j}D&0&0\\
b_{,j}&0&0\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\delta H/\delta m_{j}\\
\delta H/\delta D\\
\delta H/\delta b\end{bmatrix}\,dt-\sum_{k}\begin{bmatrix}\partial_{j}f^{k}_{i}+f^{k}_{j}\partial_{i}&g^{k}\partial_{i}&-a^{k}_{,i}\\
\partial_{j}g^{k}&0&0\\
a^{k}_{,j}&0&0\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\delta H/\delta m_{j}\\
\delta H/\delta D\\
\delta H/\delta b\end{bmatrix}\circ dW_{t}^{k}\,.

(4.27)

Here,
f^{k}_{i}
are the components of
{\bf f}^{k}
such that
f^{k}={\bf f}^{k}\cdot d{\bf x}\otimes dV\in\Lambda^{1}(\mathcal{D})\otimes\text{Den}(\mathcal{D})
,
g^{k}\in\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{D})
and
a^{k}\in\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{D})
for all
k
. For simplicity, let us consider incompressible flows with
D=1
and neglect the stochastic part of the advection of
D
. That is, we set
g^{k}=0
. This case will yield the standard incompressibility condition,
{\bm{\nabla}\cdot}{\bf u}=0
.

The EB Hamiltonian is

\displaystyle H=\int_{\mathcal{D}}\bigg[\frac{1}{2D}\big|{\bf m}-D{\bf R}\big|^{2}+gDbz+p(D-1)\bigg]d^{3}x\,,

(4.28)

where the momentum density
{\bf m}=D\Big({\bf u}+{\bf R}({\bf x})\Big)
and the pressure
p
is a Lagrange multiplier which enforces incompressibility. The variational derivatives of
H
are given by

\displaystyle\delta H=\int_{\mathcal{D}}{\bf u}\cdot\delta{\bf m}+\delta D\Big(gbz+p-\frac{1}{2}|{\bf u}|^{2}-{\bf u}\cdot{\bf R}({\bf x})\Big)+(gDz)\delta b\,d^{3}x\,.

(4.29)

The deterministic EB equations then follow as

\displaystyle\begin{split}\partial_{t}{\bf u}-{\bf u}\times{\rm curl}\big({\bf u}+{\bf R}({\bf x})\big)&=-\,\nabla\Big(p+\frac{1}{2}|{\bf u}|^{2}\Big)-gb\,\mathbf{\hat{z}}\,,\\
\partial_{t}D+{\rm div}(D{\bf u})&=0\,,\quad\hbox{with}\quad D=1\,,\\
\partial_{t}b+{\bf u}\cdot\nabla b&=0\,.\end{split}

(4.30)

One can obtain the energy preserving stochastic EB equations by substituting the variational derivatives into stochastic Poisson operator in (
4.27
) to find

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}{\bf u}&-{\bf u}{\bm{\times}}\hbox{curl}({\bf u}+{\bf R})\,dt+{\bm{\nabla}}\left({\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}p+\frac{1}{2}|{\bf u}|^{2}\,dt\right)+gb\mathbf{\hat{z}}\,dt+\sum_{k}\left[-{\bf u}{\bm{\times}}\hbox{curl}({\bf f}^{k})+{\bm{\nabla}}({\bf f}^{k}\cdot{\bf u})-gz\cdot{\bm{\nabla}}a^{k}\right]\circ dW_{t}^{k}=0\,,\\
&\partial_{t}D+{\rm div}(D{\bf u})=0\,,\quad\hbox{with}\quad D=1\,,\\
&{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}b+{\bf u}\cdot{\bm{\nabla}}b\,dt+\sum_{k}{\bf u}\cdot{\bm{\nabla}}a^{k}\circ dW_{t}^{k}=0\,.\end{split}

(4.31)

Remark 4.3
.

Following
[
66
]
, the change of pressure
p\rightarrow{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}p=p\,dt+\sum_{i}p_{i}\circ dW_{t}^{i}
has been made, so that the incompressibility condition,
{\bm{\nabla}\cdot}{\bf u}=0
will be enforced in both the drift and stochastic parts of
{\bf u}
.

Similar to the case of
S=\text{Diff}(\mathcal{D})\circledS\text{Den}(\mathcal{D})
for energy-preserving stochastic RSW equation, a convenient way of considering the Kelvin circulation theorem for the Euler-Boussinesq equations is to write the associated SFLP equations in terms of the momentum 1-form
\alpha={\bf m}\cdot d{\bf x}
. These equations then read

\displaystyle\begin{split}&{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}\alpha+\left(\mathcal{L}_{u}\alpha-\frac{\delta H}{\delta b}{\bf d}b\right)\,dt+{\bf d}\left({\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}p-\frac{1}{2}|u|^{2}\,dt-{\bf u}\cdot{\bf R}\,dt\right)+\sum_{k}\frac{1}{D}\left(\mathcal{L}_{u}\beta^{k}-\frac{\delta H}{\delta b}{\bf d}a^{k}\right)\circ dW^{k}_{t}=0\,,\\
&\partial_{t}\left(D\,dV\right)+\mathcal{L}_{u}(D\,dV)=0\,,\quad\hbox{with}\quad D=1\,,\\
&{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}b+{\bf u}\cdot{\bm{\nabla}}b\,dt+\sum_{k}{\bf u}\cdot{\bm{\nabla}}a^{k}\circ dW_{t}^{k}=0\,,\end{split}

(4.32)

where the 1-form
\beta^{k}:={\bf f}^{k}\cdot d{\bf x}
. The Kelvin circulation theorem for these equations is immediate, as

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}\oint_{c(u)}\alpha=\oint_{c(u)}\frac{\delta H}{\delta b}{\bf d}b\,dt-\sum_{k}\oint_{c(u)}\left(\mathcal{L}_{u}\beta^{k}-\frac{\delta H}{\delta b}{\bf d}a^{k}\right)\circ dW_{t}^{k}\,.

(4.33)

In vector calculus notation, this Kelvin circulation theorem is written as

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}\oint_{c(u)}({\bf u}+{\bf R})\cdot d{\bf x}=\oint_{c(u)}gz{\bm{\nabla}}b\cdot d{\bf x}\,dt-\sum_{k}\oint_{c(u)}\left((\hbox{curl}{\bf f}^{k}){\bm{\times}}{\bf u}-gz{\bm{\nabla}}a^{k}\right)\cdot d{\bf x}\circ dW^{k}_{t}

(4.34)

Remark 4.4
.

In the case where
{\bf f}^{0}=-{\bf u}^{S}({\bf x})
,
dW_{t}^{0}=dt
, and
a^{k}=0
for
k=0,\ldots
, one finds that the energy-preserving stochastic EB equations in (
4.31
) produce a stochastic contribution to the vortex force in the Craik-Leibovich equations
[
15
]
whose formulation with Hamilton’s principle is discussed in
[
34
]
. Namely, they reduce as follows,

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}{\bf u}-{\bf u}\times{\rm curl}\big({\bf u}-{\bf u}^{S}({\bf x})+{\bf R}({\bf x})\big)dt&=-\,\nabla\Big(p+\frac{1}{2}|{\bf u}|^{2}+{\bf u}\cdot{\bf u}^{S}\Big)dt-gb\,\mathbf{\hat{z}}\,dt\\
&\qquad+\sum_{k>0}\Big({\bf u}\times{\rm curl}\,{\bf f}^{k}-\,\nabla\big({\bf u}\cdot{\bf f}^{k}\big)\Big)\circ dW^{k}_{t}\,,\\
\partial_{t}D+{\rm div}(D{\bf u})&=0\,,\quad\hbox{with}\quad D=1\,,\\
\partial_{t}b+{\bf u}\cdot\nabla b&=0\,,\end{split}

(4.35)

where one interprets the semimartingale
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}{\bf u}^{S}={\bf u}^{S}({\bf x})\,dt+\sum_{k}{\bf f}^{k}({\bf x})\circ dW^{k}_{t}
as a stochastic augmentation of the usual steady prescribed Stokes drift velocity.

Physical interpretation of the functions
{\bf f}^{k}
and
a^{k}
in terms of stochastic PV fluxes

The energy-preserving stochastic EB equations in (
4.31
) imply the following equation for potential vorticity density,
q\,dV
, defined by
q\,dV:={\bf d}\alpha\wedge{\bf d}b=(\hbox{curl}{\bf m})\cdot{\bm{\nabla}}b\,dV=(\bm{\omega}+2\bm{\Omega})\cdot{\bm{\nabla}}b\,dV
, where
{\bf m}={\bf u}+{\bf R}
for
D=1
. Namely,

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}(q\,dV)+\mathcal{L}_{u}(q\,dV)\,dt&={\bf d}\left({\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}\alpha+\mathcal{L}_{u}\alpha\,dt\right)\wedge{\bf d}b+{\bf d}\alpha\wedge{\bf d}\left({\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}b+\mathcal{L}_{u}b\,dt\right)\\
&=-\sum_{k}{\bf d}\bigg[\big(\mathcal{L}_{u}\beta^{k})-gz{\bf d}a^{k}\big)\wedge{\bf d}b-\big(\mathcal{L}_{u}a^{k}\big){\bf d}\alpha\bigg]\circ dW_{t}^{k}\,,\end{split}

(4.36)

where we recall that
\beta^{k}:={\bf f}^{k}\cdot d{\bf x}
is a 1-form.
In vector calculus notation, after using the incompressibility condition
{\bm{\nabla}\cdot}{\bf u}=0
, the potential vorticity equation (
4.36
) can be written in terms of
q=(\hbox{curl}\,{\bf m})\cdot{\bm{\nabla}}b
as, cf.
[
8
]

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}q+{\bf u}\cdot{\bm{\nabla}}q\,dt&=\sum_{k}{\rm div}\Big[\big({\bf u}{\bm{\times}}\hbox{curl}\,{\bf f}^{k}+gz\nabla a^{k}\big)\times{\bm{\nabla}}b+(\bm{\omega}+2\bm{\Omega})\cdot{\bm{\nabla}}({\bf u}\cdot{\bm{\nabla}}a^{k})\Big]\circ dW^{k}_{t}\\
&=\sum_{k}{\rm div}\Big[\mathbf{F}^{k}\times\nabla b+(\bm{\omega}+2\bm{\Omega})\cdot\big(\nabla\mathfrak{D}^{k}\big)\Big]\circ dW^{k}_{t}=:-\,\sum_{k}{\rm div}\mathbf{J}^{k}\circ dW^{k}_{t}\,,\end{split}

(4.37)

where
\hbox{curl}\,{\bf m}=\bm{\omega}+2\bm{\Omega}
is the total vorticity, and the quantities
\mathbf{F}^{k}
and
\mathfrak{D}^{k}
are defined as

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}^{k}:={\bf u}{\bm{\times}}\hbox{curl}\,{\bf f}^{k}+gz\nabla a^{k}\quad\hbox{and}\quad\mathfrak{D}^{k}:={\bf u}\cdot{\bm{\nabla}}a^{k}\,.

(4.38)

The summands in
\mathbf{J}^{k}=-\,\mathbf{F}^{k}\times\nabla b-(\bm{\omega}+2\bm{\Omega})\cdot\big(\nabla\mathfrak{D}^{k})
are called the “
J
-fluxes of PV” and are identified with “frictional” and “diabatic” effects, respectively, in
[
32

56

. See also
[
8
]
for LES turbulence interpretations of these fluxes.

In summary, while the energy-preserving stochastically-augmented CL vortex force and entrainment effects in equations (

4.35

Q=\int_{\mathcal{D}}q\,dV
will be preserved for appropriate boundary conditions.
See
[
67
]
for more information about Langmuir circulations and their importance in the mixing processes in the upper ocean boundary layer. The sub-mesoscale excitations created by the
J
-fluxes of PV are a subject of intense present research aimed at understanding the effects of turbulence on oceanic frontogenesis, as well as wave forcing which transports materials such as sediment, gases, algae (carbon), oil spills and plastic detritus,
[

,
57
,

,

,

,

,

.

\displaystyle\partial_{t}H(\mathbb{E}\left[{{\bf m}}\right],\mathbb{E}\left[{b}\right])=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}\int_{\mathcal{D}}\left|-\mathbb{E}\left[{{\bf u}}\right]{\bm{\times}}\hbox{curl}{\bf f}^{i}+{\bm{\nabla}}(\mathbb{E}\left[{{\bf u}}\right]\cdot{\bf f}^{i})-\mathbb{E}\left[{gz}\right]\cdot{\bm{\nabla}}a^{i}\right|^{2}\,d^{3}x

5
Conclusion and outlook

The motivation of this paper has been to determine what type of stochastic perturbations can be added to fluid dynamics that will preserve the fundamental properties of energy conservation, Kelvin circulation theorem and conserved quantities arising from the Lagrangian particle relabelling symmetry. The geometric framework employed in this paper introduces stochastic forcing by Lie transport (SFLT) noise as a series of perturbations which automatically produce a Kelvin circulation theorem and can be chosen to satisfy either energy, or Casimir preservation. In this paper, we have mainly focused on preserving energy conservation. These stochastic external forces can be seen as the slow
+
fast decomposition of external forces corresponding to the slow
+
fast decomposition of fluid flow. For Euler fluid equations, the stochastic CL vortex force will always be energy conserving and its physical interpretation as a wave-averaged forces fits well into the external forces considered in the reduced Lagrange-d’Alembert-Pontryagin (RLDP) principle.
In comparison with the location uncertainty (LU) approach by Mémin
[
62
]
, the present paper gives an
alternative
set of fluid equations which are energy preserving. The relation to LU has been left for future work. Numerical simulations of the stochastically forced Lie-Poisson (SFLP) equations (
2.19
) will be needed to classify solution behaviours of these new stochastic extension of classical fluid equations. As in the applications of the stochastic advection by Lie transport (SALT) and LU approaches, computational simulations of Langmuir fluid circulations and their material entrainment using equations (
4.31
) and (
4.35
) will require the calibration of the functions
(f^{m}_{i},f^{a}_{i})
, perhaps via data analysis methods similar to those used in the approach detailed in
[
12
,
13
]
. Computational simulations of the equations resulting from the SFLT and SFLP modelling approaches introduced here, as well as simulations of the EA SFLT equations in section
3
have all been left for future work.

Appendix C
Itô form of the SFLP equation

Proposition C.1
.

The Itô form of the SFLP equation (
2.7
)

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}m+{{\rm ad}}^{*}_{\frac{\delta h}{\delta m}}m\,dt+\sum_{i}{{\rm ad}}^{*}_{\frac{\delta h}{\delta m}}f_{i}\circ dW^{i}_{t}=0\,,

is given by

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}m+{{\rm ad}}^{*}_{\frac{\delta h}{\delta m}}m\,dt+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}{{\rm ad}}^{*}_{\sigma_{i}}f_{i}\,dt+\sum_{i}{{\rm ad}}^{*}_{\frac{\delta h}{\delta m}}f_{i}dW_{t}^{i}=0,\quad\sigma_{i}={\left({-{{\rm ad}}^{*}_{\frac{\delta h}{\delta m}}f_{i}}\,,\,{\frac{\delta^{2}h}{\delta m^{2}}}\right)},

(C.1)

where the brackets
{\left({\cdot}\,,\,{\cdot}\right)}
in the definition of
\sigma_{i}
denotes contraction, not
L^{2}
pairing.

Proof.

This can be shown via direct computation. We ignore the drift term and suppress the indices on constants
f_{i}
for ease of notation, choosing an arbitrary
\phi=\phi(x)\in\mathfrak{X}
, we have the Stratonovich stochastic equation

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}{\big\langle{m}\,,\,{\phi}\big\rangle}={\Big\langle{-{{\rm ad}}^{*}_{\frac{\delta h}{\delta m}}f}\,,\,{\phi}\Big\rangle}\circ dW_{t}={\left\langle{{{\rm ad}}^{*}_{\phi}f}\,,\,{\frac{\delta h}{\delta m}}\right\rangle}\circ dW_{t}

(C.2)

The corresponding Itô form is then

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}{\big\langle{\phi}\,,\,{m}\big\rangle}&={\left\langle{{{\rm ad}}^{*}_{\phi}f}\,,\,{\frac{\delta h}{\delta m}}\right\rangle}\,dW_{t}+\frac{1}{2}{\left[{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}{\left\langle{\frac{\delta h}{\delta m}}\,,\,{{{\rm ad}}^{*}_{\phi}f}\right\rangle}}\,,\,{dW_{t}}\right]}\\
&={\left\langle{{{\rm ad}}^{*}_{\phi}f}\,,\,{\frac{\delta h}{\delta m}}\right\rangle}\,dW_{t}+\frac{1}{2}{\left[{{\left\langle{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}\frac{\delta h}{\delta m}}\,,\,{{{\rm ad}}^{*}_{\phi}f}\right\rangle}}\,,\,{dW_{t}}\right]}\\
&={\left\langle{{{\rm ad}}^{*}_{\phi}f}\,,\,{\frac{\delta h}{\delta m}}\right\rangle}\,dW_{t}+\frac{1}{2}{\left[{{\left\langle{{\left({-\frac{\delta^{2}h}{\delta m^{2}}}\,,\,{{{\rm ad}}^{*}_{\frac{\delta h}{\delta m}}f}\right)}\,dW_{t}}\,,\,{{{\rm ad}}^{*}_{\phi}f}\right\rangle}}\,,\,{dW_{t}}\right]}\\
&=-{\left\langle{{{\rm ad}}^{*}_{\frac{\delta h}{\delta m}}f}\,,\,{\phi}\right\rangle}\,dW_{t}-\frac{1}{2}{\left\langle{{{\rm ad}}^{*}_{\sigma}f}\,,\,{\phi}\right\rangle}{\left[{dW_{t}}\,,\,{dW_{t}}\right]}\\
\end{split}

(C.3)

Since
\phi
is arbitrary, the Itô form of (
2.7
) is

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}m+{{\rm ad}}^{*}_{\frac{\delta h}{\delta m}}m\,dt+\sum_{i}{{\rm ad}}^{*}_{\frac{\delta h}{\delta m}}f_{i}\,dW_{t}^{i}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j}{{\rm ad}}^{*}_{\sigma_{j}}f_{i}{\left[{dW_{t}^{i}}\,,\,{dW_{t}^{j}}\right]}=0.

(C.4)

For Brownian motion, the quadratic variation term simplifies to
{\left[{dW_{t}^{i}}\,,\,{dW_{t}^{j}}\right]}=\delta^{ij}dt
which recovers Itô form as presented.
∎

Proposition C.2
.

The Itô form of the SFLP equation with advected quantity (
2.19
)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}m+{{\rm ad}}^{*}_{\frac{\delta h}{\delta m}}m\,dt+\frac{\delta h}{\delta a}\diamond a\,dt+\sum_{i}{{\rm ad}}^{*}_{\frac{\delta h}{\delta m}}f^{i}\circ dW^{i}_{t}+\frac{\delta h}{\delta a}\diamond g^{i}\circ dW^{i}_{t}=0\\
&{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}a+\mathcal{L}_{\frac{\delta h}{\delta m}}a\,dt+\sum_{i}\mathcal{L}_{\frac{\delta h}{\delta m}}g^{i}\circ dW^{i}_{t}=0\end{split}

(C.5)

is given by

\displaystyle\begin{split}&{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}m+{{\rm ad}}^{*}_{\frac{\delta h}{\delta m}}m\,dt+\frac{\delta h}{\delta a}\diamond a\,dt+\sum_{i}{{\rm ad}}^{*}_{\frac{\delta h}{\delta m}}f^{i}\,dW^{i}_{t}+\frac{\delta h}{\delta a}\diamond g^{i}\,dW^{i}_{t}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}({{\rm ad}}^{*}_{\sigma_{i}}f^{i}+\theta_{i}\diamond g^{i})\,dt=0\\
&{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}a+\mathcal{L}_{\frac{\delta h}{\delta m}}a\,dt+\sum_{i}\mathcal{L}_{\frac{\delta h}{\delta m}}g^{i}\,dW^{i}_{t}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}\mathcal{L}_{\sigma_{i}}g^{i}\,dt=0\end{split}

(C.6)

where
\sigma_{i}
and
\theta_{i}
are given as

\displaystyle\sigma_{i}:={\left({\frac{\delta^{2}h}{\delta m^{2}}}\,,\,{-{{\rm ad}}^{*}_{\frac{\delta h}{\delta m}}f^{m}_{i}-\frac{\delta h}{\delta a}\diamond f^{a}_{i}}\right)}\quad\hbox{and}\quad\theta_{i}:={\left({\frac{\delta^{2}h}{\delta a^{2}}}\,,\,{-\mathcal{L}_{\frac{\delta h}{\delta m}}f^{a}_{i}}\right)}.

Proof.

Consider similarly by ignoring drift terms and suppress the indices on the
f^{i}\in\mathfrak{X}^{*}
,
g^{i}\in V^{*}
,
\sigma_{i}\in\mathfrak{X}
and
\theta_{i}\in V
. Consider constants
\phi=\phi(x)\in\mathfrak{X}
and
\psi=\psi(x)\in V
, the following Stratonovich stochastic equations hold

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}{\left\langle{m}\,,\,{\phi}\right\rangle}=-{\left\langle{{{\rm ad}}^{*}_{\frac{\delta h}{\delta m}}f+\frac{\delta h}{\delta a}\diamond g}\,,\,{\phi}\right\rangle}\circ dW_{t},\quad{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}{\left\langle{a}\,,\,{\psi}\right\rangle}=-{\left\langle{\mathcal{L}_{\frac{\delta h}{\delta m}}g}\,,\,{\psi}\right\rangle}\circ dW_{t}=0

(C.7)

The Itô form thus satisfies

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}{\left\langle{m}\,,\,{\phi}\right\rangle}

\displaystyle=-{\left\langle{{{\rm ad}}^{*}_{\frac{\delta h}{\delta m}}f+\frac{\delta h}{\delta a}\diamond g}\,,\,{\phi}\right\rangle}dW_{t}-\frac{1}{2}{\left[{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}{\left\langle{{{\rm ad}}^{*}_{\frac{\delta h}{\delta m}}f+\frac{\delta h}{\delta a}\diamond g}\,,\,{\phi}\right\rangle}}\,,\,{dW_{t}}\right]}

\displaystyle=-{\left\langle{{{\rm ad}}^{*}_{\frac{\delta h}{\delta m}}f+\frac{\delta h}{\delta a}\diamond g}\,,\,{\phi}\right\rangle}dW_{t}+\frac{1}{2}{\left[{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}{\left\langle{{{\rm ad}}^{*}_{\phi}f}\,,\,{\frac{\delta h}{\delta m}}\right\rangle}+{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}{\left\langle{\mathcal{L}_{\phi}g}\,,\,{\frac{\delta h}{\delta a}}\right\rangle}}\,,\,{dW_{t}}\right]}

\displaystyle=-{\left\langle{{{\rm ad}}^{*}_{\frac{\delta h}{\delta m}}f+\frac{\delta h}{\delta a}\diamond g}\,,\,{\phi}\right\rangle}dW_{t}+\frac{1}{2}{\left[{{\left\langle{{{\rm ad}}^{*}_{\phi}f}\,,\,{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}\frac{\delta h}{\delta m}}\right\rangle}+{\left\langle{\mathcal{L}_{\phi}g}\,,\,{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}\frac{\delta h}{\delta a}}\right\rangle}}\,,\,{dW_{t}}\right]}

\displaystyle=-{\left\langle{{{\rm ad}}^{*}_{\frac{\delta h}{\delta m}}f+\frac{\delta h}{\delta a}\diamond g}\,,\,{\phi}\right\rangle}dW_{t}+\frac{1}{2}{\left[{{\left\langle{{{\rm ad}}^{*}_{\phi}f}\,,\,{\sigma_{i}\,dW_{t}}\right\rangle}+{\left\langle{\mathcal{L}_{\phi}g}\,,\,{\theta_{i}\,dW_{t}}\right\rangle}}\,,\,{dW_{t}}\right]}

\displaystyle=-{\left\langle{{{\rm ad}}^{*}_{\frac{\delta h}{\delta m}}f+\frac{\delta h}{\delta a}\diamond g}\,,\,{\phi}\right\rangle}dW_{t}-\frac{1}{2}{\left\langle{{{\rm ad}}^{*}_{\sigma}f+\mathcal{L}_{\theta}g}\,,\,{\phi}\right\rangle}\,{\left[{dW_{t}}\,,\,{dW_{t}}\right]}

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}{\left\langle{a}\,,\,{\psi}\right\rangle}

\displaystyle=-{\left\langle{\mathcal{L}_{\frac{\delta h}{\delta m}}g}\,,\,{\psi}\right\rangle}\,dW_{t}-\frac{1}{2}{\left[{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}{\left\langle{\mathcal{L}_{\frac{\delta h}{\delta m}}g}\,,\,{\psi}\right\rangle}}\,,\,{dW_{t}}\right]}

\displaystyle=-{\left\langle{\mathcal{L}_{\frac{\delta h}{\delta m}}g}\,,\,{\psi}\right\rangle}\,dW_{t}+\frac{1}{2}{\left[{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}{\left\langle{\psi\diamond g}\,,\,{\frac{\delta h}{\delta m}}\right\rangle}}\,,\,{dW_{t}}\right]}

\displaystyle=-{\left\langle{\mathcal{L}_{\frac{\delta h}{\delta m}}g}\,,\,{\psi}\right\rangle}\,dW_{t}-\frac{1}{2}{\left[{{\left\langle{\psi\diamond g}\,,\,{\sigma\,dW_{t}}\right\rangle}}\,,\,{dW_{t}}\right]}

\displaystyle=-{\left\langle{\mathcal{L}_{\frac{\delta h}{\delta m}}g}\,,\,{\psi}\right\rangle}\,dW_{t}-\frac{1}{2}{\left\langle{\mathcal{L}_{\sigma}g}\,,\,{\psi}\right\rangle}\,{\left[{dW_{t}}\,,\,{dW_{t}}\right]}

Since
\phi
and
\psi
are arbitrary, the Itô form of
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}m
and
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}a
with drift are

\displaystyle\begin{split}&{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}m+{{\rm ad}}^{*}_{\frac{\delta h}{\delta m}}m\,dt+\frac{\delta h}{\delta a}\diamond a\,dt+\sum_{i}\left({{\rm ad}}^{*}_{\frac{\delta h}{\delta m}}f^{i}+\frac{\delta h}{\delta a}\diamond g^{i}\right)\,dW^{i}_{t}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j}({{\rm ad}}^{*}_{\sigma_{j}}f^{i}+\theta_{j}\diamond g^{i}){\left[{dW^{i}_{t}}\,,\,{dW^{j}_{t}}\right]}=0\\
&{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rm d}}a+\mathcal{L}_{\frac{\delta h}{\delta m}}a\,dt+\sum_{i,j}\mathcal{L}_{\sigma_{j}}g^{i}\,{\left[{dW^{i}_{t}}\,,\,{dW^{j}_{t}}\right]}=0\end{split}

(C.8)

For Brownian motion, the quadratic variation term simplifies to
{\left[{dW_{t}^{i}}\,,\,{dW_{t}^{j}}\right]}=\delta^{ij}dt
which completes the proof.
∎
