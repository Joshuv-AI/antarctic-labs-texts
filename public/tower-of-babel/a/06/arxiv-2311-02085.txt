Abstract
. Preference elicitation plays a central role in interactive recommender systems . Most preference elicitation approaches use either item queries that ask users to select preferred items from a slate, or attribute queries that ask them to express their preferences for item characteristics. Unfortunately, users often wish to describe their preferences using soft attributes ( Balog et al. 2021 ) for which no ground-truth semantics is given. Leveraging concept activation vectors ( Göpfert et al. 2022 ) for soft attribute semantics, we develop novel preference elicitation methods that can accommodate soft attributes and bring together both item and attribute-based preference elicitation. Our techniques query users using both items and soft attributes to update the recommender system’s belief about their preferences to improve recommendation quality. We demonstrate the effectiveness of our methods vis-à-vis competing approaches on both synthetic and real-world datasets.



1.
Introduction

Recommender systems (RSs) play a central role in connecting users to products, content and services by predicting user
preferences
for candidate items. While practical RSs are often trained using implicit user feedback on recommended items (e.g., clicks, consumption, ratings), increasingly interactive and conversational systems explicitly elicit user preferences to more effectively guide recommendations. Approaches to
preference elicitation (PE)
include both item-based
(
Sarwar et al. 2001
;
Adomavicius and Tuzhilin 2005
)

and attribute-based methods
(
Chen and Pu 2004
;
Viappiani et al. 2006
)
, but rarely are these considered in conjunction. Moreover, attribute-based methods generally rely on
hard attributes
, those for which a definitive source of ground truth associates attribute values (e.g., actors in movies, color of a product) with items.
Unfortunately, attributes with which users often wish to describe their preferences are
soft

(
Balog et al. 2021
)
—there is
no source of ground truth associating such attribute values to items; instead this association, or
semantics
, must be learned. For instance,
information about movie attributes like ‘funny,’ ‘thought-provoking,’ or ‘inspiring’ may only be learnable via sparse, noisy user comments, reviews, or tags. Because of this, practical attribute-based PE must account for the inherent noise and uncertainty in the semantics of soft attributes.
1
1

1

Soft attribute usage may also be
subjective

(
Göpfert et al. 2022
)
, an issue we set aside in this work.

In this work, we develop PE methods that can accommodate such soft attributes in item-based and attribute-based PE. To do so, we leverage the recent method by
Göpfert et al. 2022
, who use
concept activation vectors (CAVs)

(
Kim et al. 2018
)
to discover the semantics of soft attributes w.r.t. a RS’s item representation. We show how to incorporate this semantics into interactive RSs with
active critiquing
: given a slate of items, our techniques determine the soft attributes about which to elicit user feedback (e.g., “do you prefer a funnier or a less funny movie than this slate of movies?”), interpret the user’s response relative to this semantics, and update RS beliefs about the user’s preferences.
Because the semantics are learned from sparse and noisy data, the RS may have varying degrees of certainty or confidence in the CAV semantics of different soft attributes.
This should, in turn, impact how the RS interprets user responses to attribute-based PE-queries and updates its beliefs. We extend the CAV semantics to handle such uncertainty and incorporate this into PE-driven belief updates.

Our key contributions are as follows: (i) we leverage CAV semantics to extend attribute-based PE for interactive RSs to handle soft attributes (in contrast to
Göpfert et al. 2022
, who model user-initiated critiques); (ii) we propose two novel query types: queries over attributes, and their combination with well-studied item queries, along with the human response models to these new query types, which enable us to efficiently update RS beliefs over user preferences in our PE framework; (iii) we propose several PE (query) selection schemes and optimization methods that balance information gathering and recommendation quality; and (iv) we extend our techniques to incorporate uncertainty in an attribute’s CAV semantics.
We illustrate the efficacy of our methods on both synthetic data and MovieLens data
(
Harper and Konstan 2016
)
.

2.
Problem Formulation

In this section, we outline our problem formulation and key assumptions, then briefly discuss related work.

2.1 Preference Prediction.

We assume a standard
collaborative filtering (CF)
setting
(
Hu et al. 2008
)
, where users
u\in\mathcal{U}
rate items
i\in\mathcal{I}
with
r_{u,i}\in\mathcal{R}\cup\{0\}
. Here
\mathcal{R}
is the set of possible ratings (e.g.,
1
–
5
stars) and
r_{u,i}=0
indicates that user
u
has not rated item
i
. Let the
ratings dataset
be
\mathbf{R}=\{(u,i,r_{u,i}):r_{u,i}\neq 0\}
.

The RS learns user and item representations from the ratings dataset
\mathbf{R}

using some form of CF. Let
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a user-item embedding space. An
item embedding

\phi_{I}:\mathcal{I}\rightarrow\mathcal{X}
maps each item
i\in\mathcal{I}
into a vector representation
\phi_{I}(i)
of its (latent) attributes in
\mathcal{X}
; note that this embedding is typically not interpretable. We assume that user preferences are captured by a similar mapping
\phi_{U}:\mathcal{U}\rightarrow\mathcal{X}
. However, to capture the preference variations of users due to their latent state (e.g., mood, activity, location), the RS learns an
embedding distribution
for each user, i.e.,
P_{U}:\mathcal{U}\rightarrow\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{X})
. As such, the embedding
\phi_{U}(u)=\phi_{u}\sim P_{U}(u)
in
u
’s session is a sample from her embedding distribution. Since
\phi_{u}
is unknown to the RS, we use PE to uncover it.
2
2

2

Our techniques can be applied,
mutatis mutandis
, to the RS’s uncertainty about a user’s
stationary preferences
and its gradual refinement of beliefs over time, rather than assuming that preferences actually change (are resampled) with each session.

To learn user and item embeddings, we use a
two-tower model
(or
dual encoder
) in which users and items are passed through separate but co-trained deep neural networks (DNNs)
(
Yi et al. 2019
;
Yang et al. 2020
)
. The item encoder outputs
\phi_{I}(i)
for any item
i\in\mathcal{I}
, while the user encoder outputs a
d
-dimensional multivariate Gaussian distribution
P_{U}(u)=\mathcal{N}(\phi_{\mu,U}(u),\phi_{\sigma,U}(u))
for any user
u\in\mathcal{U}
, where
\phi_{\mu,U}(u)
is
the mean user embedding vector and
\phi_{\sigma,U}(u)
is a (lower-triangular) scale matrix. Suitable methods
include probabilistic matrix factorization
(
Salakhutdinov and Mnih 2007
)
and certain forms of neural CF
(
Beutel et al. 2018
;
Yang et al. 2020
)
. These methods often assume linear user utility, i.e., (predicted) user-item affinity is
\hat{r}_{u,i}=\phi_{u}^{\top}\phi_{I}(i)
. Hence, we treat
\phi_{u}
as the parameters of
u
’s utility function (w.r.t. the learned item representation).
Our Gaussian user embedding gives the following
belief state
over the user’s utility function:
P(r_{u,i}\mid P_{U},\phi_{I})=\mathcal{N}(\phi_{\mu,U}(u)^{\top}\phi_{I}(i),\phi_{I}(i)^{\top}\phi_{\sigma,U}(u)^{\top}\phi_{\sigma,U}(u)\phi_{I}(i))
.
We train these encoders by minimizing negative log-likelihood:

\mathcal{L}=-\frac{1}{|\mathbf{R}|}\sum_{(u,i,r_{u,i})\in\mathbf{R}}\log P(r_{i,u}\mid P_{U},\phi_{I})
.

2.2  Soft Attributes & Tags.

In addition to
hard (known or objective) attributes
, users often describe items using
soft attributes
which have no “ground truth” semantics or association with items.
Unlike hard attributes, such as ‘genre,’ ‘artist,’ or ‘director,’ these terms are not part of an agreed-upon formal specification of an item. They are neither applied universally to all items, nor by all users.

Some RSs support user-supplied
tags
(e.g., MovieLens), which may be specified directly in the RS, or extracted from user descriptions, reviews, or other sources. A tag can denote a hard or soft attribute. We let
\mathcal{T}
be the set of tags that users may adopt to describe items.
We assume that tags are used in a binary way—users simply choose to apply a tag or not. However, the actual attributes corresponding to tags can be ordinal or cardinal.
Indeed, the user study in
Balog et al. 2021
shows that soft attributes often exhibit “relative” degrees.
For example, applying the tag ‘violent’ may reflect that a user believes a movie exhibits a
degree of violence
exceeding her tolerance.
Let
t_{u,i,g}=1
if user
u
applies tag
g\in\mathcal{T}
to item
i
, and
t_{u,i,g}=0
otherwise.
For each
g\in\mathcal{T}
, let
\mathbf{T}_{g}=\{(u,i):t_{u,i,g}=1\}
and
\mathbf{T}_{\overline{g}}=\{(u,i):t_{u,i,g}=0,\;\exists g^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}:t_{u,i,g^{\prime}}=1,\;\exists i^{\prime}\in\mathcal{I}:t_{u,i^{\prime},g}=1\}
. Pair
(u,i)
belongs to
\mathbf{T}_{g}
, if user
u
applies tag
g
to item
i
, and belongs to
\mathbf{T}_{\overline{g}}
, if
u
applies a tag
g^{\prime}\neq g
to
i
and tags another item
i^{\prime}\neq i
with
g
.
Tags are usually strictly sparser than ratings, so we assume

\sum_{g}|\mathbf{T}_{g}|\ll|\mathbf{R}|
.

2.3  Concept Activation Vectors in RSs.

Modern ML models usually learn complex and non-transparent representations of concepts, an issue addressed by work on interpretable representations
(
Sundararajan et al. 2017
)
.
Kim et al. 2018
develop an approach that tries to find a correspondence between internal state of an ML model (e.g., a DNN used for image classification)—in the form of a
concept activation vector (CAV)
—and a human-interpretable concept.

Göpfert et al. 2022
apply CAVs to identify the semantics of tags w.r.t. the item representation learned by CF models in RSs. We adopt this approach in our work: for a tag
g\in\mathcal{T}
, we attempt to find a CAV
\phi_{g}\in\mathcal{X}
in the embedding space, and use it to determine if the CF model has learned a representation of (the attribute corresponding to)
g
.
3
3

3

While a tag
g
is simply a
token
used by users to label items, its corresponding soft attribute is a
property
of the items themselves, which can take a real-value. For conciseness, we sometimes refer to the semantics (or representation or CAV) of a tag
g
as shorthand for the semantics of the attribute corresponding to
g
.
We train the CF model and learn CAVs separately, similar to methods that build attribute models on top of embeddings for cold-start
(
Rendle and Schmidt-Thieme 2010
;
Cohen et al. 2017
)
, and in contrast to those that jointly train attribute models
(
Wu et al. 2019
;
Luo et al. 2020
)
. This separation is motivated by the hypothesis that if a tag is useful for understanding user preferences (and thus for PE), the CF model will have learned a representation of it
(
Göpfert et al. 2022
)
. After training a two-tower model, we use its item tower
\phi_{I}
to learn CAVs. For a tag
g\in\mathcal{T}
, we construct a training set
\mathbf{D}_{g}
in which positive instances (
y=+1
) are items in
\mathbf{T}_{g}
and negatives (
y=-1
) are those in
\mathbf{T}_{\overline{g}}
. We then learn the CAV
\phi_{g}
for
g
by learning a (regularized) logistic regressor:

(1)

\mathcal{L}(\phi_{g};\mathbf{D}_{g})=\sum_{(i,y)\in\mathbf{D}_{g}}\log(1+e^{-y\phi_{g}^{\top}\phi_{I}(i)})+\frac{\lambda}{2}\phi_{g}^{\top}\phi_{g}.

The induced CAV
\phi_{g}
is the normal to the separating hyperplane of this classifier, and offers a directional semantics for (the attribute corresponding to) tag
g
in the item embedding space. Specifically, its dot product with
\phi_{I}(i)
, or
g
-score

c_{g}(i)=\phi_{g}^{\top}\phi_{I}(i)
, quantifies the degree to which item
i
satisfies tag
g
, (e.g., how violent or funny a movie is). We use the
g
-score to measure the
quality
(or usefulness) of CAV
\phi_{g}
, defining
Q(\phi_{g};\mathbf{D}_{g})
to be the fraction of the item pairs
\{(i_{1},i_{2})\mid(i_{1},+1)\in\mathbf{D}_{g},(i_{2},-1)\in\mathbf{D}_{g}\}
for which
c_{g}(i_{1})\geq c_{g}(i_{2})
.
Given sparsity and noise in the tag data, we may be more confident in some CAVs than in others. We discuss
CAV uncertainty below.

2.4  Preference Elicitation.

Traditional CF-based RSs uncover user preferences indirectly, using indirect feedback (e.g., clicks, ratings, purchases).
Interactive RSs
, by contrast, actively elicit a user’s preference by asking her questions, responses to which are used to refine its estimate (or
belief
) about her utility function and improve quality of future recommendations
(
He et al. 2016
)
. At suitable points, an interactive RS selects a
query

q
from a
query space

\mathcal{Q}
that reveals some aspects of the user’s preferences. For example, in item-based PE, users may be asked to rate a particular item
(
Boutilier et al. 2003
;
Zhao et al. 2013
)
, in which case
\mathcal{Q}=\mathcal{I}
, or to choose an item from a slate
S
that she most prefers
(
Viappiani and Boutilier 2010
)
, in which case
\mathcal{Q}
is the set of all subsets of
\mathcal{I}
of size
|S|
. In attribute-based PE
(
Chen and Pu 2012
)
, a user can express her preferences with more abstract semantic attributes.
We describe several types of elicitation queries
in Section
3
.

2.5  Related Work.

Our work assumes an underlying CF method, and can be applied to matrix factorization methods
(
Salakhutdinov and Mnih 2007
)
or more recent DNN-based approaches
(
Yi et al. 2019
;
Yang et al. 2020
)
. A number of approaches have been proposed for interpreting tags or attributes in RSs, often using tag or review data
(
Gantner et al. 2010
;
McAuley et al. 2012
;
Luo et al. 2020
)
. Some works focused on learning tag semantics w.r.t. a learned recommendation model, including those that jointly learn both, or that derive tag meanings using a pre-trained CF model
(
Rendle et al. 2009
;
Cohen et al. 2017
;
Nema et al. 2021
)
. The model of
Göpfert et al. 2022
, which we employ in this work, is of the latter form.

The literature on PE is substantial. Most related to our work are those which use Bayesian methods for eliciting user preferences
(
Bourdache et al. 2019
;
Adam and Destercke 2021
)
. They maintain a
belief state
over user preferences and refine it using PE queries. Item-based methods include, say, active CF methods that ask users to explicitly rate specific items
(
Boutilier et al. 2003
;
Zhao et al. 2013
)
, and item-selection methods that ask users to compare two items or state which item is most preferred within some set
(
Chajewska et al. 2000
;
Boutilier 2002
;
Viappiani and Boutilier 2010
)
.
Attribute-based techniques include, for instance, example-critiquing schemes
(
Viappiani et al. 2006
;
Chen and Pu 2012
)
. Most work in PE deals with hard attributes, while we focus on soft
attributes. One exception is the work of
Radlinski et al. 2019
, who develop a methodology for relating soft attribute usage in conversational RSs to user preferences.

3.
Queries and User Responses

We first describe two novel
query
types that we develop as well as the item queries. These queries can be used
to elicit information about a user’s preferences for specific items or their (hard or soft) attributes. For each type, we develop
response models
that relate a user’s (stochastic) response to their underlying preferences, using the CAV semantics to interpret the user’s behaviour w.r.t. attributes.
For ease of exposition, we assume that the CAV semantics is
known
by both the RS and the user, but derive updates when there is uncertainty in the CAV semantics below.

3.1  Attribute Queries.

An
attribute query

q=(S,g)
consists of a
slate
of items
S
and a tag
g
. The RS presents the slate to the user and asks her if she prefers items, relative to those in
S
, that are more/less—i.e., exhibit a greater degree of—
g
’s attribute (e.g., “Would you prefer movies that are more/less thought-provoking than those in
S
?”). The user responds to positively
\rho=+1
(i.e., more) or negatively
\rho=-1
(i.e., less). Slate
S
can also be viewed as a recommendation set, not just a query (see Section
5
).

Our user response models for attribute queries assume user
u
relates items in
S
to her “most preferred” or
target
item given her underlying utility function
\phi_{u}
.While
u
would like to guide the RS to her most preferred item

\argmax_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\phi_{u}^{\top}\phi_{I}(i)
, this would assume an unrealistic level of familiarity with available items
\mathcal{I}
by
u
. Instead, we consider a model where
u
targets a hypothetical
ideal
item,
\phi^{*}_{I,u}\in\argmax_{\phi\in\Gamma}\,\phi_{u}^{\top}\phi
,
w.r.t. some mildly constrained space
\Gamma\subset\mathcal{X}
unrelated to
\mathcal{I}
.
4
4

4

For example, a user will not target a movie that is simultaneously maximally funny, though-provoking, and scary.
In lieu of a detailed familiarity model for
u
, we capture some familiarity with
\mathcal{I}
by constraining her
target
item as follows:

\phi^{*}_{I,u}\in\argmax_{\phi\in\Gamma}\,\phi_{u}^{\top}\phi
,
s.t.
\|\phi\|_{2}\leq\max_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\|\phi_{I}(i)\|_{2}
,
whose solution is

(2)

\phi^{*}_{I,u}=\max_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\|\phi_{I}(i)\|_{2}\cdot\phi_{u}/\|\phi_{u}\|_{2}.

The target item
\phi^{*}_{I,u}
may fall outside of
\mathcal{I}
, but this allows the user to direct the RS more meaningfully.

For attribute query
q=(S,g)
, in our
mean-slate response model
,
u
compares her target item
\phi^{*}_{I,u}
with the mean embedding of items in
S
,
\phi_{I,\overline{S}}=\frac{1}{|S|}\sum_{i\in S}\phi_{I}(i)
.
If the target exhibits more of attribute
g
than the mean slate, i.e., the target’s
g
-score
c_{g}(\phi^{*}_{I,u})
is greater than
c_{g}(\phi_{I,\overline{S}})
, then

u
responds positively (
\rho=+1
). User response is estimated by the following
probit
model
(
Train 2009
;
Chaptini 2005
)
:

(3)

\displaystyle P(\rho=+1\mid q,\phi_{u})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{\Phi}\left(c_{g}\left(\phi^{*}_{I,u}-\phi_{I,\overline{S}}\right)/\sigma_{g}\right),

where
\epsilon_{g}
is a zero-mean Gaussian noise with variance
\sigma_{g}^{2}
,
and
\mathbf{\Phi}(\cdot)
is the standard Gaussian CDF.

In our second
mean-probability response model
,
u
compares the
g
-score of her target with that of each item
i\in S
, and responds using the weighted average of differences:

(4)

\displaystyle P(\rho=+1\mid q,\phi_{u})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|S|}\sum_{i\in S}\lambda_{i}\cdot\mathbf{\Phi}\left(c_{g}\left(\phi^{*}_{I,u}-\phi_{I}(i)\right)/\sigma_{g}\right)\;,

where
\lambda_{i}>0
is a response weight
s.t.
\sum_{i\in S}\lambda_{i}=1
.
While mean-slate model is intuitive, the flexibility of mean-probability may better capture nuances in user behavior (see
IpA
below).

3.2  Item Queries.

An
item query
(or choice query)
q=S
presents a slate
S
to
u
and asks her which item is preferred
(
Ben-Akiva et al. 1985
;
Viappiani and Boutilier 2010
)
. User response
\rho=i

is given by a standard
multinomial logit
model w.r.t.
\phi_{u}

(
Chaptini 2005
;
Ben-Akiva et al. 1985
)
:

(5)

P(\rho=i\mid q,\phi_{u})=\frac{\exp(\phi_{I}(i)^{\top}\phi_{u}/T)}{\sum_{j\in S}\exp(\phi_{I}(j)^{\top}\phi_{u}/T)},

where
T
is a temperature parameter.

3.3  Item-plus-Attribute Queries.

Item-plus-Attribute (IpA) queries
combine attribute and item queries, and have the same form
q=(S,g)
as attribute queries. The user
is first asked to select her preferred item
i^{\ast}_{S}
in
S
(per item queries), then asked to critique
i^{\ast}_{S}
w.r.t.
g

(per attribute queries).
The user responds with
\rho=(i,+1)
or
\rho=(i,-1)
by comparing

\phi^{*}_{I,u}
to
i^{\ast}_{S}
(rather than the slate) via
g
-scores, and otherwise responds as in attribute queries:

\displaystyle P(\rho_{1}=i\mid S,\phi_{u})\!\times\!P(\rho_{2}=y\mid g,\phi_{u},\rho_{1}=i)

(6)

\displaystyle\!=\!\frac{\exp(\phi_{I}(i)^{\top}\phi_{u}/T)}{\sum_{j}\exp(\phi_{I}(j)^{\top}\phi_{u}/T)}\!\times\!\mathbf{\Phi}\!\left(\!y\cdot c_{g}\phi^{*}_{I,u}\!-\!\phi_{I}(i)/\sigma_{g}\!\right).

3.4  CAV Uncertainty.

In contrast to hard attributes, the semantics of soft attributes are typically estimated using sparse, noisy data (e.g., as in our use of tag data above). As a result, CAVs constructed for different tags may be characterized by varying degrees of uncertainty. For instance, if CAV
\phi_{g}
for tag
g
is based on a large amount of tag data with little disagreement in usage, the RS should be more confident in
\phi_{g}
than in the CAV
\phi_{g^{\prime}}
for a tag
g^{\prime}
whose data is sparser or less consistent. Abstractly, we assume that the RS has
a

CAV belief (distribution)

P_{g}(\phi_{g}\mid\mathbf{D}_{g})
reflecting this uncertainty (where
\mathbf{D}_{g}
is the tag data used to train
\phi_{g}
). We do not require a specific mechanism for generating this belief,
but Bayesian logistic regression (augmenting Eq.
1
)
(
Jaakkola and Jordan 1997
)
or Bayesian learning-to-rank
(
Kuo et al. 2009
)
are suitable methods.

The response models for attribute and IpA queries above depend on the CAV/semantics of the attribute/tag in question. In what follows, we assume that user
u
responds using some true, underlying CAV
\phi_{g}
.
5
5

5

Different users may have
different interpretations of an attribute. Such
subjectivity
can be uncovered using CAVs
(
Göpfert et al. 2022
)
, but we do not consider this here.
To reflect this additional uncertainty in a user response, the RS interprets it w.r.t. its CAV belief. Specifically,
P(\rho\mid q,\phi_{u})
in Eqs.
3
and
3
requires taking an expectation over possible CAVs
\phi_{g}
w.r.t.
P_{g}(\phi_{g}|\mathbf{D}_{g})
. These response probabilities are then used to update RS beliefs about
u
’s utility (Section
4
) and to choose queries (Section
5
) when the CAVs are uncertain.

Specifically,
P(\rho\mid q,\phi_{u})
in Eqs.
3
,
4
and
3
requires taking an expectation over possible CAVs
\phi_{g}
(or equivalently, scoring functions
c_{g}
) w.r.t.
P_{g}(\phi_{g}|\mathbf{D}_{g})
. These response probabilities are then used to update beliefs about
u
’s utility (Section
4
, by Bayesian methods) and selecting optimal queries (Section
5
) when (some or all) the CAVs are uncertain.
See Appendix
A
for further details.

4.
User Belief State Modeling

Bayesian methods
generally maintain a
belief state
or distribution over a user’s utility function

(
Vendrov et al. 2020
;
Boutilier 2002
)
.
In our setting, the belief state for user
u
is initially
P_{U}(u)
, and is refined as
u
responds to PE queries.
Given response
\rho
to query
q
, we update our belief about
u
in the standard Bayesian fashion:

P_{U}(u\mid\rho,q):=P(\phi_{u}\mid\rho,q)\propto P(\rho\mid q,\phi_{u})P_{U}(u).

The prior
P_{U}(u)
is the Gaussian user embedding learned by our two-tower model (Section
2
). Let
\mathcal{H}^{(K)}=\{(q^{(K)},\rho^{(K)})\}\cup\mathcal{H}^{(K-1)}
with
\mathcal{H}^{(0)}=\emptyset
, be a history
of
K
queries and user responses. Given
\mathcal{H}^{(K)}
, the RS’s posterior for
u
is

(7)

P_{U}(u\!\mid\!\mathcal{H}^{(K)})\!=\!P(\phi_{u}\!\mid\!\mathcal{H}^{(K)})\!\propto\!P_{U}(u)\prod_{k=1}^{K}P(\rho^{(k)}\!\mid\!q^{(k)},\!\!\phi_{u}).

This assumes conditional independence of
responses
given
\phi_{u}
.
Unlike the prior, generally the posterior is not Gaussian, and thus, we use the following two methods to tractably approximate it.

4.1.
Parameterized Posterior

We can approximate the posterior with some parameterized distribution
P(u;\theta)
.
To do so, we sample from the true unnormalized posterior using Metropolis-Hastings
(
Chib and Greenberg 1995
)
or Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC). These samples
\{\phi_{u,i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
are then used to estimate parameter
\theta

by maximizing the log-likelihood

(8)

\displaystyle L(\theta\mid\mathcal{H}^{(K)})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log P(\phi_{u,i}\mid\mathcal{H}^{(K)};\theta)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log P(\rho^{(K)}|q^{(K)}\!,\phi_{u,i};\theta)\!+\!\log P(\phi_{u,i}|\mathcal{H}^{(K-1)}\!;\!\theta).

We test two different variants of the sampling and posterior update methods in this scheme. In the first
batch
method, we generate a large set of samples and use them to update the posterior offline, i.e., for each query no additional sample of posterior belief is generated before the posterior is updated. In the second
iterative
method, we generate fewer samples from our initial posterior, update the posterior with these samples, and then re-generate new samples with this updated posterior. This process is repeated several times for each query. While the iterative method is more computationally expensive, our experiments (Section
6
, Figure
5
) demonstrate its improved data efficiency over the batched counterpart.

4.2.
Gaussian Posterior

The posterior can be “assumed” to be Gaussian
\mathcal{N}(\hat{\phi}_{\mu,U}(u),\hat{\phi}_{\sigma,U}(u))
using the Laplace approximation
(
Williams and Rasmussen 2006
;
Biyik et al. 2020
;
Li et al. 2021
)
. For this, we treat the posterior mean
\hat{\phi}_{\mu,U}(u)
as the mode of the true log-posterior, i.e.,

(9)

\displaystyle\hat{\phi}_{\mu,U}(u)

\displaystyle\in\argmax_{\phi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Big[\sum_{k=1}^{K}\log P\big(\rho^{(k)}\mid q^{(k)},\phi\big)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\big(\phi-\phi_{\mu,U}(u)\big)^{\top}\big(\phi_{\sigma,U}(u)^{\top}\phi_{\sigma,U}(u)\big)^{-1}\big(\phi-\phi_{\mu,U}(u)\big)\Big].

This optimization may not be convex, but its local optima can be found efficiently because its gradient can be written in closed-form.
\hat{\phi}_{\mu,U}(u)
. In Figure
2
in Section 6, we numerically compare the performance of the Gaussian posterior with the parameterized (categorical) posterior. The discrete posterior model outperforms its Gaussian counterpart with both attribute and IpA response models. We therefore use the parameterized posterior as the default belief state model for all PE methods described below.

4.3.
CAV Uncertainty

When the CAV used by an attribute or IpA query is uncertain, the response probabilities used in our belief state updates (Eqs.
7
,
9
or
8
) are computed using expectations over CAV samples w.r.t. its belief distribution
P_{g}(\phi_{g}|\mathbf{D}_{g})
(see Section 3.4). Similarly, we update the belief about
u
with responses:

P_{U}(u\mid\rho,\tilde{q}):=P(\phi_{u}\mid\rho,\tilde{q})\propto P(\rho\mid\tilde{q},\phi_{u})P_{U}(u).

Given any query-response pair
(\tilde{q},\rho)
under the current belief state
P_{U}(u)
, the posterior belief is updated by Bayes rule:

(10)

P(\rho\mid\tilde{q},\phi_{u})=\frac{P(\rho\mid\tilde{q},\phi_{u})P(\phi_{u})}{\int_{\phi_{u}}P(\rho\mid\tilde{q},\phi_{u})P(\phi_{u})d\phi_{u}}.

5.
Query Optimization

In this section, we develop methods to optimize the choice of queries.
A key objective is to find queries whose responses quickly refine the posterior
to improve recommendation quality. However, the RS may also want to use queries whose slates contain ‘‘good’’ recommendations w.r.t. the current posterior, not just ‘‘good’’ information for posterior update, since the user may select/consume one of these items at any time. We address the trade-off between information gathering and recommendation quality below.
6
6

6

For item queries without attributes, the optimal recommendation and query slates
are identical under several natural response models, resolving this tension
(
Viappiani and Boutilier 2010
)
.

5.1.
Pure Preference Elicitation

In
pure preference elicitation (PPE)
, we focus on the
information
a query response provides about user
u
’s utility (and ignore the predicted utility of items in
S
).
We consider several
acquisition functions (AFs)
to find the query with the most useful information w.r.t. reducing uncertainty in
the belief state
P_{U}(u)
.
7
7

7

An ideal AF would be a
policy
that optimizes the
sequence
of queries
(
Boutilier 2002
;
Holloway and White 2003
)
. However, such a sequential formulation is generally intractable; thus, we consider
myopic
approaches and optimize the AF w.r.t. the immediate query only (as is common in PE
(
Chajewska et al. 2000
)
).

Random Query Selection:

This is a natural baseline that samples queries uniformly at random
from
\mathcal{Q}
.

Entropy:

To make posterior beliefs more informative,
one can use conditional Shannon entropy
(
Cover 1999
)
:

(11)

H(\rho\mid q,\mathcal{H})=-\mathbb{E}_{\rho|q,P_{U}(u|\mathcal{H})}[\log P(\rho|q,P_{U}(u|\mathcal{H}))].

Computing the expected posterior entropy for a given query in closed-form is generally hard, but one can estimate it by taking an expectation over all sampled posterior user responses. This metric measures the amount of latent user information that
remains after a PE query
q
.

Mutual Information:

We can measure the information content of
q
using
mutual information (MI)
between
u
’s target
\phi_{I,u}^{*}
and her response
\rho
, given
q
and
\mathcal{H}
:

(12)

MI(\phi_{I,u}^{*};\rho\!\mid\!q,\mathcal{H})\!:=\!H(\rho\!\mid\!q,\mathcal{H})\!-\!\mathbb{E}_{\phi_{I,u}^{*}\!\mid\!\mathcal{H}}\big[H(\rho\!\mid\!q,\phi_{I,u}^{*})\big].

We can also express the distribution over target items as
P(\phi_{I,u}^{*}|\mathcal{H})=\int P(\phi_{I,u}^{*},\phi_{u}|\mathcal{H})d\phi_{u}=\mathbb{E}_{\phi_{u}\sim P_{U}}\!\big[P(\phi^{*}_{I,u}|\phi_{u})\big]
.

Using (
2
), the second term is equal to
\mathbb{E}_{\phi_{I,u}^{*}|\mathcal{H}}\big[H(\rho|q,\phi_{I,u}^{*})\big]=\mathbb{E}_{\phi_{u}\sim P_{U}(u|\mathcal{H})}\big[H(\rho|q,\max_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\frac{\|\phi_{I}(i)\|_{2}\cdot\phi_{u}}{\|\phi_{u}\|_{2}})\big]
,
which is estimated by sampling
\phi_{u}\sim P_{U}(u\mid\mathcal{H})
.
We choose a query that minimizes this measure to
make our posterior model correlated with
u
’s target.

Expected Value of Information (EVOI).

The EVOI acquisition function
(
Chajewska et al. 2000
;
Boutilier 2002
;
Viappiani and Boutilier 2010
)
measures the improvement in
u
’s expected utility resulting from a response to query
q
. The EVOI of
q
(given
\mathcal{H}
) is

(13)

\mathit{EVOI}(q\mid\mathcal{H})=\mathit{PEU}(q\mid\mathcal{H})-\mathit{EU}^{*}\big(P_{U}(u\mid\mathcal{H})\big),

where

(14)

EU^{*}(P_{U}(u))=\max_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\;\mathbb{E}_{\phi_{u}\sim P_{U}(u)}\big[\phi_{u}^{\top}\phi_{I}(i)\big]

and
PEU(q\mid\mathcal{H})
is the
posterior expected utility
of
q
,

(15)

PEU(q\mid\mathcal{H})=\sum_{\rho}P(\rho\mid q,\mathcal{H})\cdot EU^{*}\big(P_{U}(u\mid\mathcal{H}\cup\{(\rho,q)\})\big).

EVOI extracts information that offers maximum expected improvement in recommendation quality. It can be also used to decide when to stop elicitation (e.g., once it falls below some tolerance or exceeds the cost of interaction).
The direct computation of PEU can be expensive, so we approximate it by sampling from the prior
P_{U}(u\mid\mathcal{H})
. With CAV uncertainty, the response probabilities used in PEU and other AFs are computed using expectation over CAV. The details are deferred to App.
A
.

CAV Uncertainty.

With CAV uncertainty, response probabilities used in PEU and other AFs are computed using expectation over CAVs as discussed above. For example, the EVOI of a soft attribute query
\tilde{q}
(given
\mathcal{H}
) is:

(16)

\mathit{EVOI}(\tilde{q}\mid\mathcal{H})=\mathit{PEU}(\tilde{q}\mid\mathcal{H})-\mathit{EU}^{*}\big(P_{U}(u\mid\mathcal{H})\big).

where
EU^{*}(P_{U}(u))
is still the same as Eq (10)
but
PEU(\tilde{q}\mid\mathcal{H})
is the
posterior expected utility
of w.r.t.
\tilde{q}
,

(17)

PEU(\tilde{q}\mid\mathcal{H})=\sum_{\rho}P(\rho\mid\tilde{q},\mathcal{H})\cdot EU^{*}\big(P_{U}(u\mid\mathcal{H}\cup\{(\rho,\tilde{q})\})\big).

Other acquisition functions can also be extended analogously but their details will be omitted for the sake of brevity.

5.2.
Blended Elicitation & Recommendation

In
blended PE and recommendation (BPER)
, we include the quality of the slate
S
used in query
q

when assessing
q
, not just its information value.
BPER blends two objectives,
information gathering

\mathit{IG}(q|\mathcal{H})
and
recommendation quality

\mathit{RQ}(q|\mathcal{H})
.
\mathit{IG}(q|\mathcal{H})
measures the utility information extracted by
q
and can use any pure PE AF (e.g., Entropy, MI, EVOI).

\mathit{RQ}(q|\mathcal{H})
measures slate quality (i.e., expected utility) of
S
:
\mathit{RQ}(q|\mathcal{H}):=\sum_{i\in S}\mathbb{E}_{\phi_{u}\sim P_{U}(u\ |\mathcal{H})}[\phi_{u}^{\top}\phi_{I}(i)]
.
To balance
the two,
we define the BPER AF as
\gamma\mathit{IG}(q|\mathcal{H})+(1-\gamma)\mathit{RQ}(q|\mathcal{H})
, where
\gamma\in[0,1]
.
We expect an RS to focus on IG earlier in an interaction sequence to learn about
u
, and gradually shift focus to RQ. While decaying
\gamma
seems natural, experiments suggest that a well-tuned
constant

\gamma
suffices, since IG
tends to decrease over time as the posterior
converges to
u
’s true embedding, after which the BPER AF would focus more on recommendation.

5.3.
Query Optimizers

Query selection requires optimizing the chosen AF
over query space
\mathcal{Q}
. The size of
\mathcal{Q}
depends linearly on the number of tags
|\mathcal{T}|
and combinatorially on the number of items
|\mathcal{I}|
, where usually
|\mathcal{T}|<<|\mathcal{I}|
. Optimal tags can be found by searching over
\mathcal{T}
, while slate optimization is demanding when
\mathcal{I}
is large, hence requires approximation.
We consider two approaches to slate-attribute selection: optimizing slate
S
(or an item inside
S
) and then tag
g
; and optimizing both jointly.

Thompson Sampling.

Slate
S
is constructed using sequential TS
(
Gopalan et al. 2014
)
:
S_{0}=\emptyset
and
S_{j}=S_{j-1}\cup\argmax_{i\in\mathcal{I}\setminus S_{j-1}}\phi_{u}^{\top}\phi_{I}(i)
, where
\phi_{u}\sim P_{U}(u\mid\mathcal{H})
and
j\in\{1,\ldots,\lvert S\rvert\}
.
Given
S
,
g
is randomly selected.

Sequential Greedy.

Each item in the slate is selected greedily over the item set (excluding items that have already been added).
The first item in
S
is the “best” item; at each subsequent step, we alternate between updating
g
and the next item in
S
using the AF until
S
is complete.

Random Search.

We randomly generate a certain number of slates-attribute pairs, then apply the AF to select the query with the highest score. With CAV uncertainty, we compute score of each query by averaging over sampled CAVs.

Continuous Relaxation.

Inspired by gradient-based PE in
(
Vendrov et al. 2020
)
, we relax the combinatorial query selection problem into a continuous one and solve it using first- or second-order methods. Given this continuous representation of
q
, we project it back onto the true query space.
To recover
S
and
g
, we project using Euclidean distance. The main challenge in continuous optimization lies in the discontinuous nature of our AFs, which require maximizing over the discrete item set. We circumvent this with a normalization assumption
(see Appendix
A.3
).
With CAV uncertainty, since tag
g
is a random variable, we have to represent
P_{g}
by a multivariate normal distribution and apply the re-parameterization trick (see Appendix
A.2
). We project
P_{g}
using KL-divergence.

We conduct experiments to test the effectiveness of our PE methods, combining various query types, belief update methods, query selection schemes, and slate optimization methods. Synthetic Environment.

Following

Five of the
25

While the RS can learn about user preferences and improve its recommendations with most PE methods, the joint optimizers provide better recommendations (slates with greater NDCG across any number of queries) by extracting more useful information (greater cosine). As in RecSim NG, PE with random queries performs relatively well, likely due to two factors: (i) With an inaccurate prior model (e.g., during the early phases of PE), a random query of diverse items may effectively reduce belief-state entropy; (ii) With a combinatorial space of decision variables (slates), none of the optimization strategies converge to local optima (w.r.t. BPER) that are much better than the random query. This suggests that smartly switching between random and jointly optimized queries may be “practically optimal” in terms of computational complexity and PE, but detailed studies are left for future work.

Finally, we explore different query types with both random search and gradient approximation. Figure
10
shows that PE with IpA queries is much more effective than PE with simpler queries, an observation consistent with that seen in the RecSim NG setting. With the additional information collected at each PE step, IpA queries offer a 10–20% NDCG gain over item queries. PE with attribute queries performs worst w.r.t. both IG and RQ because, without allowing users to express nuanced preferences through item selection, it may fail to capture preferences regarding important but untaggable latent attributes.

7.
Conclusions and Future Work

We have proposed a framework for preference elicitation in interactive recommenders that allows users to navigate item space or critique recommendations using soft attributes. Our techniques exploit concept-activation vectors (CAVs) to uncover the semantics of soft attributes w.r.t. the RS’s item representation, and to support Bayesian updates of the RS’s beliefs about a user’s underlying preferences.
We considered uncertainty in the semantics of soft attributes and develop scalable, continuous relaxation methods for query optimization.
We studied a variety of query types, both item and attribute-based, and response models, developed belief update and query optimization methods (i.e., acquisition functions) for each, and demonstrated the efficacy of these methods on both simulated and MovieLens 20M data. Our work has fundamental implications for designing the next-generation of recommenders (e.g., dialogue-based RSs), since our methods allows RSs to interact and learn about users more naturally with soft attributes.

There are a number of avenues for future research. With insufficient tag data, CAV noise may render PE ineffective, requiring active learning methods for attribute semantics—integrating these into the PE process should prove valuable.
Subjectivity
, where users may use terms in different ways
(
Göpfert et al. 2022
;
Radlinski et al. 2022
)
, means methods to elicit a user’s “personalized” semantics for an attribute should further enhance PE. Multi-modal user belief model, reflecting more diverse preferences, are of interest. While our current PE methods optimize for myopic information gathering and recommendation, multi-step lookahead PE methods that use reinforcement learning remain an important challenge. Finally, experiments with real users are needed to further evaluate our methods with more diverse user preferences, attribute semantics, and responses.

A.1.
User Belief State Update and AFs

To incorporate the CAV uncertainty, we consider a soft attribute query
\tilde{q}=(S,P_{g})

However, the RS has to compute the response probabilities for updating user belief state according to its CAV belief.

For a (soft) attribute query
\tilde{q}=(S,P_{g})

we define

\displaystyle P(\rho\mid\tilde{q},\phi_{u})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\phi_{g}\sim P_{g}}P(\rho\mid(S,\phi_{g}),\phi_{u})

(18)

\displaystyle=\int_{\phi_{g}}P_{g}(\phi_{g}|D_{g})P(\rho\mid(S,\phi_{g}),\phi_{u})d\phi_{g}

as the probability of observing response
\rho
under a CAV belief
P_{g}

u

P_{U}(u\mid\rho,\tilde{q}):=P(\phi_{u}\mid\rho,\tilde{q})\propto P(\rho\mid\tilde{q},\phi_{u})P_{U}(u).

Given any query-response pair
(\tilde{q},\rho)
under the current belief state
P_{U}(u)

(19)

P(\rho\mid\tilde{q},\phi_{u})=\frac{P(\rho\mid\tilde{q},\phi_{u})P(\phi_{u})}{\int_{\phi_{u}}P(\rho\mid\tilde{q},\phi_{u})P(\phi_{u})d\phi_{u}}.

We also need to consider the CAV uncertainty in computing acquisition functions. For example, the EVOI of
\tilde{q}

\mathcal{H}

(20)

\mathit{EVOI}(\tilde{q}\mid\mathcal{H})=\mathit{PEU}(\tilde{q}\mid\mathcal{H})-\mathit{EU}^{*}\big(P_{U}(u\mid\mathcal{H})\big).

where

but

is the

of w.r.t.
\tilde{q}

(21)

PEU(\tilde{q}\mid\mathcal{H})=\sum_{\rho}P(\rho\mid\tilde{q},\mathcal{H})\cdot EU^{*}\big(P_{U}(u\mid\mathcal{H}\cup\{(\rho,\tilde{q})\})\big).

Other acquisition functions can also be extended analogously but their details will be omitted for the sake of brevity.

A.2.
Continuous Relaxation for AFs

Recall that the acquisition function can be expressed as

(22)

F(q)=\gamma\mathit{IG}(q\mid\mathcal{H})+(1-\gamma)\mathit{RQ}(q\mid\mathcal{H}),

where

(23)

\mathit{RQ}(q\mid\mathcal{H}):=\sum_{i\in S}\mathbb{E}_{\phi_{u}\sim P_{U}(u\mid\mathcal{H})}[\phi_{u}^{\top}\phi_{I}(i)]

measures the recommendation quality and
\mathit{IG}
is the information gathering metric (i.e., Entropy, MI or EVOI). In the following we take EVOI as an example and show how we obtain an differentiable objective function. The same derivation applies to Entropy and MI. For IG=EVOI, computing
q^{*}=\arg\max_{q}F(q)
is equivalent to finding

(24)

q^{*}=\arg\max_{q}\big\{\gamma PEU(q|\mathcal{H})+(1-\gamma)RQ(q|\mathcal{H})\big\}.

PEU
requires computing the expected belief w.r.t. possible responses
\rho
,
P_{U}(u\mid\mathcal{H}\cup\{(\rho,q)\})
. This computation can be expensive, so we approximate PEU by sampling from the prior
P_{U}(u\mid\mathcal{H})
:

(25)

\!\!\!\!PEU^{\prime}(q\mid\mathcal{H})\!:=\!\sum_{\rho}\max_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\mathbb{E}_{\phi_{u}\sim P_{U}(u\mid\mathcal{H})}\big[\phi_{u}^{\top}\phi_{I}(i)\cdot P(\rho\mid q,\phi_{u})\big].

With CAV uncertainty, the response probabilities used in PEU and other AFs are computed using expectation over CAV. The PEU function in Eq (
25

m

\{\phi_{u,j}\}_{j=1}^{m}
from the posterior
P_{U}(u|\mathcal{H})

(26)

\!\!\!\!PEU(q\mid\mathcal{H})\approx\frac{1}{m}\sum_{\rho}\max_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\big[\sum_{j=1}^{m}\phi_{u,j}^{\top}\phi_{I}(i)P(\rho\mid q,\phi_{u,j})\big].

And similarly, the
RQ
function can be approximated by

(27)

\!\!\!\!RQ(q\mid\mathcal{H})\approx\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i\in S}\big[\sum_{j=1}^{m}\phi_{u,j}^{\top}\phi_{I}(i)\big].

In the following we use the concatenation of attribute vector
g
and item embeddings in
S
to represent
q
, immediately
RQ(q\mid\mathcal{H})
is differentiable w.r.t.
q
. However, computing

requires enumerating over
\mathcal{I}
and thus is not differentiable. To derive a differentiable objective function

\mathcal{I}
is rich enough such that for each query-response pair
(q,\rho)
we can find
i^{*}_{(q,\rho)}=\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{m}\phi_{u,j}P(\rho\mid q,\phi_{u,j})}{\|\sum_{j=1}^{m}\phi_{u,j}P(\rho\mid q,\phi_{u,j})\|}\max_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\|\phi_{I}(i)\|

(28)

PEU(q\mid\mathcal{H})\!\approx\!\frac{\max_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\|\phi_{I}(i)\|}{m}\sum_{\rho}\Big\|\sum_{j=1}^{m}\phi_{u,j}P(\rho\mid q,\phi_{u,j})\Big\|_{2},

which is differentiable w.r.t.
q
because the response model
P(\cdot|q,\cdot)
is differentiable w.r.t.
q

Now we extend the continuous relaxation framework to account for queries with an uncertain CAV vector as its attribute. We may apply the re-parameterization trick to derive a continuous acquisition function. Suppose the CAV vector
\phi_{g}
in the attribute query
\tilde{q}=(S,P_{g})
follows a Multivariate normal
\mathcal{N}(\mu_{g},\Sigma_{g})

\Sigma_{g}=L_{g}^{\top}L_{g}

PEU(\tilde{q}\mid\mathcal{H})\approx\frac{\max_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\|\phi_{I}(i)\|}{mn}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{\rho}\Big\|\sum_{j=1}^{m}\phi_{u,j}P(\rho\mid q_{i},\phi_{u,j})\Big\|_{2},

where

\displaystyle P(\rho=+1\mid q_{i},\phi_{u})

\displaystyle=P(\rho=+1\mid S,\mu_{g},\Sigma_{g},\epsilon_{i},\phi_{u})

(29)

\displaystyle=\Phi\Big(\frac{(\mu_{g}+L_{g}\epsilon_{i})^{\top}(\phi^{*}_{I,u}-\phi_{I,\bar{S}})}{\sigma_{g}}\Big),

Eq. (

Suppose

A.3.
Gradient-based Query Optimization

Once we approximate the acquisition function in a continuous form
F(q)
, standard gradient-based method can be applied to find the optimal query
q*=\arg\max_{q}F(q)
. In our empirical study we use both first and second-order optimization. First we generate an initial query
q_{0}
from random search with 20 trials and then apply either the following updates for a fixed number of steps:

(1)

First-order optimization:

q_{t}=q_{t-1}+\eta\nabla F(q_{t-1}),1\leq t\leq T,

(2)

Second-order optimization:

q_{t}=q_{t-1}+[\lambda I+\nabla^{2}F(q_{t-1})]^{-1}\nabla F(q_{t-1}),1\leq t\leq T,

where
\eta
is the learning rate,
\lambda
is a regularization constant used to guarantee the non-singularity of the Hessian matrix
\nabla^{2}F
. In our experiments we take
T=2,\eta=1e-3,\lambda=1e-4
.
