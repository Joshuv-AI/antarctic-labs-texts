Bounds on scattering of neutral Goldstones
Francesca Acanfora, Andrea Guerrieri, Kelian Häring, and Denis Karateev

Abstract
We study the space of 2 → 2 2\to 2 scattering amplitudes of neutral Goldstone bosons in four space-time dimensions. We establish universal bounds on the first two non-universal Wilson coefficients of the low energy Effective Field Theory (EFT) for such particles. We reconstruct the analytic, crossing-symmetric, and unitary amplitudes saturating our bounds, and we study their physical content. We uncover non-perturbative Regge trajectories by continuing our numerical amplitudes to complex spins. We then explore the consequence of additional constraints arising when we impose the knowledge about the EFT up to the cut-off scale. In the process, we improve on some aspects of the numerical S S -matrix bootstrap technology for massless particles.

2.1
Phenomenology of the boundary

At each point on the boundary of the allowed region, given by the orange line in figure
1
, there is a unique amplitude which we can reconstruct numerically. The amplitudes on the boundary are called extremal. Our goal in this subsection is to understand the physics contained in these extremal amplitudes.

The simplest way to analyze a scattering process is to plot partial amplitudes
\mathcal{T}_{\ell}(s)
(also called partial waves) for several values of angular momentum
\ell=0,2,4,\ldots
. Recall, that in the physical region the scattering amplitudes in
d=4
space-time dimensions is written in terms of the partial amplitudes as

\mathcal{T}(s,t)=2\pi\sum_{\ell=0,2,\dots}(1+2\ell)P_{\ell}\left(1+\frac{2t}{s}\right)\,\mathcal{T}_{\ell}(s),

(15)

where
P_{\ell}(x)
is the Legendre polynomial. For more details, see appendix
A
.

In order to diagnose how the various degrees of freedom in the amplitude distribute in the spin channels, it is useful to look at the spin decomposition of some observables. A convenient choice is given by
g_{2}
and its sum-rule representation, derived in appendix
F
. One has

g_{2}=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}ds\frac{\operatorname{Im}\mathcal{T}(s,0)}{s^{3}}=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}ds\frac{\sigma_{\text{tot}}}{s^{2}}\geq 0,

(16)

where
\sigma_{\text{tot}}
is the total cross section.
If we decompose the amplitude in the forward direction in partial waves inside the integral, we can define the following dimensionless coefficients that can be thought as
branching ratios
of the cross section in each spin channel
EliasMiro:2022xaa

\bar{g}_{2}^{\ell}\equiv 2g_{2}^{-1}\,(1+2\ell)\int_{0}^{\infty}ds\frac{\operatorname{Im}\mathcal{T}_{\ell}(s)}{s^{3}}\geq 0\,.

(17)

The values
\bar{g}_{2}^{\ell}

In figure

The sum rule (

S-matrix machinery for massless particles and its improvements

In this section we introduce and explain our improvements of the numerical

3.1
Machinery review

In order to study scattering amplitudes numerically in the
generic scenario
we use the machinery introduced in
Paulos:2017fhb
combined with an extension of this machinery introduced in
Guerrieri:2020bto
needed for working with massless particle. It consists of writing the following ansatz

\text{Ansatz 1: }\quad\mathcal{T}_{\text{ansatz}}(s,t,u)=\sum_{a,b,c}\alpha_{abc}\,\rho^{a}(s)\rho^{b}(t)\rho^{c}(u)+\mathbb{N}(s,t,u).

(34)

where
\alpha_{abc}
are real coefficients. Crossing requires that they are fully symmetric in their indices. Due to the relation among the Mandelstam variables
s+t+u=0
, not all terms are independent in the expansion (
34
), it is standard to require

\forall(abc)\neq 0:\qquad\alpha_{abc}=0\,,

(35)

which removes the redundant terms.
The
\rho
-variable is defined as

\rho(z)\equiv\frac{\sqrt{4m^{2}-z_{0}}-\sqrt{4m^{2}-z}}{\sqrt{4m^{2}-z_{0}}+\sqrt{4m^{2}-z}},

(36)

where
m
is the mass of the external particle (in our case
m=0
) and
z_{0}
is an arbitrary parameter, numerical results do not depend on it. We choose
z_{0}=-1
. The infinite sum in (
34
) is truncated in practice in such a way that
\max(a+b+c)\leq N_{\text{max}}
. The numerics is ran for several values
N_{\text{max}}
. This allows for an
N_{\text{max}}\rightarrow\infty
extrapolation.

The first term in the ansatz (
34
) has the square root branch point at
s=0
, however the effective amplitude of Goldstones given by (
2
) - (
4
) has a log branch-point because of the log-terms in (
4
). The latter can never be represented by the former at finite
N_{\text{max}}
. In order to deal with this issue we have added the additional term
\mathbb{N}(s,t,u)
in (
34
). Let us explain how to obtain its explicit expression.
Let us first denote the non-analytic part of the effective amplitude (
2
) - (
4
) (which contains the log-terms) by

N(s,t,u)\equiv-\frac{g_{2}^{2}}{480\pi^{2}}\Big(s^{2}(41s^{2}+t^{2}+u^{2})\log\left(-s\sqrt{g_{2}}\right)+\\
t^{2}(s^{2}+41t^{2}+u^{2})\log\left(-t\sqrt{g_{2}}\right)+u^{2}(s^{2}+t^{2}+41u^{2})\log\left(-u\sqrt{g_{2}}\right)\Big).

(37)

We can then also define

\mathbb{N}(s,t,u)\equiv-\frac{g_{2}^{2}s_{0}^{4}}{480\pi^{2}}\left[\chi_{s}^{2}\,\mathbb{H}(s|t,u)\log\chi_{s}+\chi_{t}^{2}\,\mathbb{H}(t|s,u)\log\chi_{t}+\chi_{u}^{2}\,\mathbb{H}(u|t,s)\log\chi_{u}\right],

(38)

where we the
\chi
-variable is defined as

\chi_{s}\equiv\frac{1}{4}(\rho_{s}-1)^{2}-\frac{1}{4}(\rho_{s}-1)^{3}=\frac{s}{s_{0}}-3\left(\frac{s}{s_{0}}\right)^{2}+\mathcal{O}(s^{5/2})

(39)

and the
\mathbb{H}
-function is given by

\mathbb{H}(s|,t,u)=41\chi_{s}^{2}+\chi_{t}^{2}+\chi_{u}^{2}.

(40)

The non-analytic expression (
37
) has an unlimited growth at large energies and, thus, violates unitarity. The term (
38
) instead remains finite at large energies. It is constructed in such a way that at low energies it reproduces (
37
), namely

\mathbb{N}(s,t,u)=N(s,t,u)-s^{2}\left[\beta_{1,1}s^{2}+\beta_{1,2}tu+\beta_{1,3}(t^{2}+u^{2})\right]\log(-s_{0}\sqrt{g_{2}})+\mathcal{O}(s^{5}).

(41)

Next, we require that at low energies the ansatz (
34
) precisely reproduces the effective amplitude (
2
). Performing the expansion of (
34

\mathcal{T}(s,t,u)=N(s,t,u)+\alpha_{000}+k_{1}(\alpha,x)s^{1/2}+k_{2}(\alpha,x)s+k_{3}(\alpha,x)s^{3/2}+k_{4}(\alpha,x)s^{2}\\
+k_{5}(\alpha,x)s^{5/2}+k_{6}(\alpha,x)s^{3}+k_{7}(\alpha,x)s^{7/2}+k_{8}(\alpha,x)s^{8}+O(s^{9/2}),

(42)

where

are some functions which depend on the coefficients of the ansatz and
x\equiv\cos\theta

\theta
is the scattering angle. These functions can be easily obtained in Mathematica, their form, however, is too large (and depends on
N_{\text{max}}
) in order to be written here.
Comparing this expansion with (

\alpha_{000}=0,\quad k_{1}=0,\quad k_{2}=0,\quad k_{3}=0,\quad k_{4}=g_{2},\\
k_{5}=0,\quad k_{6}=g_{3},\quad k_{7}=0,\quad k_{8}=g_{4}.

(43)

After solving these constraints and plugging the solution back to (

4
Model-dependent bounds

Let us now study the allowed space of amplitudes in the scenario in which we have an EFT-inspired model. We impose that the effective field theory approximation of the amplitude is valid in the extended region
s\in[0,M^{2}]
for all angles. This is imposed by the condition (
11
). In practice we impose non-linear unitarity in the whole range of energies
s\in[0,\infty]
. Thus, we can impose the condition (
11
) only on the imaginary part of the amplitude (the real part will be adjusted by the numerics accordingly), namely

s\in[0,M^{2}]:\qquad|\text{Im}_{s}\mathcal{T}(s,\cos\theta)-\text{Im}_{s}\mathcal{T}_{\text{EFT}}(s,\cos\theta)|\leq\text{err}(s,\cos\theta).

(51)

We emphasize here that the model is defined by a ‘band’ given by
\text{Im}_{s}\mathcal{T}_{\text{EFT}}(s,\cos\theta)\pm\text{err}(s,\cos\theta)
constraining the amplitude up to a cut-off scale
M^{2}
. For example, experimental measure of the differential cross-section could be used to define the model. In that follow, we will describe how this can be implemented numerically in subsection
4.1
and present the result for a simple model in subsection
4.2
.

5
Conclusions and outlook

In this paper we initiated the exploration of the space of
2\to 2
scattering amplitudes of massless neutral Goldstone bosons in four dimensions. We numerically determined the allowed region of the first two non-universal Wilson coefficients appearing in the
2\to 2
scattering, dubbed
\bar{g}_{3}
and
\bar{g}_{4}
both in the
universal scenario
, and by imposing a low energy model for the amplitude.

In the
universal scenario
we used the numerical approach of
Paulos:2017fhb
;
Guerrieri:2020bto
where an analytic ansatz is written for the amplitude and unitarity is imposed numerically. In this paper we proposed two new improvements of the ansatz in section
3
. The first improvement allows us to use the usual Bootstrap ansatz to match the fixed-
s
, small
t
behaviour of the amplitude predicted by the EFT expansion in (
3
). The second removes the
ramp
instability for large number of angular momentum constraints
L_{\text{max}}
discovered in
Haring:2022sdp
.
We presented the main result in the
universal scenario
in figure
1
.

On the boundary given by the orange line in figure
1
we can reconstruct the amplitudes numerically.
Analyzing the branching ratios of the cross section in various spin channels and the behaviour of the lightest spin zero resonance, we identified three different regions along the boundary. The two asymptotic regions correspond respectively to weakly coupled string amplitudes with approximately linear Regge trajectories, and weakly coupled scalar exchanges. In between these two regimes, the amplitude resembles a strongly coupled theory like QCD.
For the special point that minimizes
\bar{g}_{4}
, we performed for the first time a thorough analysis of the spectrum of resonances continuing the scattering amplitude to complex spins. We could interpolate the first two Regge trajectories by following the resonances in spin, and extract reliably their intercepts without fitting. We observed a curious crossing phenomenon among the two trajectories.

The amplitudes we extracted on the boundary for large values of
\bar{g}_{3}
and
\bar{g}_{4}
are not fully converged for the finite size ansatz we used. This is rather unsatisfactory. In the future, one could attempt to solve this issue by centering the
\rho
-variables at multiple different points. One of the realizations of this idea called the
wavelet ansatz
was advocated for in
EliasMiro:2022xaa
. Numerical convergence is controlled also by the number of spin constraints that should be taken to be very large.
However, computing the partial wave projection with high precision and for very large spin is numerically expensive. In
Guerrieri:2022sod
, the authors introduced the notion of
unitarity in the sky
, and showed how dramatically improves the convergence in the spin cutoff. We believe that by combining the improvements introduced in this paper with both the ideas mentioned in this paragraph might pave the way to more efficient numerical studies of massless scattering amplitudes.

The universal bounds determined in this paper apply to the dilatons of
\mathcal{N}=4
SYM on the Coulomb branch. It would be interesting to consider also the other massless Goldstones of the
R
-symmetry breaking, and study the mixed system of dilatons and Goldstones. Another possible direction is to generalize the Bootstrap of neutral Goldstones to other dimensions. In three dimensions, we could study the low energy dynamics of
\mathcal{M}_{2}
branes, using maximal supersymmetry to obtain the bounds on the Wilson coefficients from the scattering of neutral massless scalars.
On the other hand, in higher dimensions such bounds could be used to constrain the Wilson coefficients of the effective action of various strings compactifications and to test the consistency of the available non-perturbative results with the principles of analyticity, crossing, and unitarity.

In the
model-dependent scenario
we assumed that the amplitude is described by an EFT-inspired model at energies below a chosen
cut-off scale
M
. In section
4.1
we proposed a concrete numerical implementation of this idea inspired by
Chen:2021pgx
. The main result in the
model-dependent scenario
is given in figure
15
.
This method is not limited to massless particles. One could use this approach also for the scattering of physical pions, and improve the results obtained in
Guerrieri:2018uew
by injecting the available experimental or lattice data as low energy constraints into the bootstrap setup.

As briefly reviewed in the end of appendix
C
the Wilson coefficients of the dilaton scattering in six dimesions is related to the difference of the UV and IR
a
-anomalies denoted by
\Delta a
. In general, as it is well known, there are no bounds on
\Delta a
in 6d from the
2\to 2
scattering of dilatons. However, adding an extra assumption/knowledge about their IR behaviour up to some energy scale one can apply our
model-dependent scenario
to get some bounds on
\Delta a
. In QFTs with explicit conformal symmetry breaking one can introduce a massless scalar probe field (also often called the dilaton) which carries information about
\Delta a
. No new bounds can be constructed on
\Delta a
in this case using our technique.

Appendix A
Non-linear unitarity

The partial amplitudes for the scattering of identical particles in a general number of dimensions have the following form

\mathcal{S}_{\ell}(s)=1+\frac{i}{\mathcal{N}_{d}(s)}\mathcal{T}_{\ell}(s),

(58)

where the interacting part of the partial amplitudes are defined as

\mathcal{T}_{\ell}(s)\equiv\int_{-1}^{+1}dx\,\mu_{d,\ell}(x)\mathcal{T}(s,t(x),u(x)).

(59)

Here
\ell=0,2,4,\ldots
is the angular momentum and
x\equiv\cos\theta
is the cosine of the scattering angle. The object
\mathcal{N}_{d}(s)
for massless particles is given by

\mathcal{N}_{d}(s)\equiv 2^{d-1}s^{\frac{4-d}{2}},

(60)

and the measure reads as

\mu_{d,\,\ell}(x)=\frac{j!\,\Gamma\left(\frac{d-3}{2}\right)}{4\,\pi^{(d-1)/2}\Gamma(d-3+\ell)}\times(1-x^{2})^{\frac{d-4}{2}}C_{\ell}^{(d-3)/2}(x).

(61)

The relation between the
(t,u)
and
(s,x)
variables for massless particles is given by

t=-\frac{s}{2}\,(1-x),\quad u=-\frac{s}{2}\,(1+x).

(62)

The relation (
59
) can be inverted. As a result the interacting part of the scattering amplitude can be written as a sum of interacting partial amplitudes as follows

\mathcal{T}(s,t)=\sum_{\ell=0,2,\dots}c_{\ell}^{(d)}P_{\ell}^{(d)}\left(1+\frac{2t}{s}\right)\,\mathcal{T}_{\ell}(s),

(63)

where the
d
-dimensional Legendre polynomials are defined as

P^{(d)}_{\ell}(x)\equiv{}_{2}F_{1}\left(-\ell,\ell+d-3,\frac{d-2}{2},\frac{1-x}{2}\right).

(64)

By using the properties of the hypergeometric functions we can rewrite the
d
-dimensional Legendre polynomials as

P^{(d)}_{\ell}(x)={\Gamma(1+\ell)\Gamma(d-3)\over\Gamma(\ell+d-3)}C^{({d-3\over 2})}_{\ell}(x)=\frac{C^{({d-3\over 2})}_{\ell}(x)}{C^{({d-3\over 2})}_{\ell}(1)},

(65)

where
C_{\ell}^{(d)}
are the Gegenbauer polynomials. Notice that
P^{(d)}_{\ell}(1)=1
.
The coefficients
c_{\ell}^{(d)}
in the decomposition (
63
) read as

c_{\ell}^{(d)}=\frac{2\,\pi^{\frac{d}{2}-1}(d+2\ell-3)\Gamma(d+\ell-3)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{d}{2}-1\right)\Gamma(\ell+1)}\,,

(66)

For further discussion of the above expressions see either
Karateev:2019ymz
or
Correia:2020xtr
. Notice that in equation (
60
) we have an additional factor of
2^{d-1}
compared to the conventions of
Correia:2020xtr
.

Unitarity of theory leads to the following non-linear constraint on the partial amplitudes

\forall s\geq 0,\quad\forall\ell:\qquad|\mathcal{S}_{\ell}(s)|^{2}\leq 1.

(67)

In this paper we refer to the condition (
67
) as the non-linear unitarity. This condition is equivalent to

2\mathcal{N}_{d}(s)\,\text{Im}\mathcal{T}_{\ell}(s)\geq|\mathcal{T}_{\ell}(s)|^{2}.

(68)

The unitarity constraint (
68
) can be written in a positive semi-definite form as

\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
1&1\end{pmatrix}+\frac{1}{\mathcal{N}_{d}(s)}\begin{pmatrix}0&-i\mathcal{T}_{\ell}^{*}(s)\\
i\mathcal{T}_{\ell}(s)&0\end{pmatrix}\succeq 0

(69)

or equivalently

\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}-\frac{1}{2\mathcal{N}_{d}}\operatorname{Im}\mathcal{T}_{\ell}&\,\,\mathcal{N}_{d}^{-1/2}\operatorname{Re}\mathcal{T}_{\ell}\\[2.0pt]
\mathcal{N}_{d}^{-1/2}\operatorname{Re}\mathcal{T}_{\ell}&2\operatorname{Im}\mathcal{T}_{\ell}\end{pmatrix}\succeq 0

(70)

The condition (
70
) has an advantage of consisting of purely real matrices compared to (
69
).

The non-linear unitarity condition (
68
) contains the following simple condition

\text{Im}\mathcal{T}_{\ell}(s)\geq 0.

(71)

It is called positivity. The positivity condition is a necessary, but not sufficient condition to have a unitary theory.

Appendix B
Effective amplitude from unitarity

Consider the following amplitude

\mathcal{T}^{\text{tree}}(s,t,u)=g_{2}\,(s^{2}+t^{2}+u^{2})+g_{3}\,stu+g_{4}\left(s^{2}+t^{2}+u^{2}\right)^{2}\\
+g_{5}\,stu\left(s^{2}+t^{2}+u^{2}\right)+g_{6}\left(s^{2}+t^{2}+u^{2}\right)^{3}+g_{6}^{\prime}\left(stu\right)^{2}+O(s^{7}),

(72)

where
g_{n}
are real coefficients.
This amplitude arises in a typical tree level computation of the scattering of Goldstone bosons described by the Lagrangian density (
1
). The tree level amplitude (
72
) does not satisfy (
68

68

\mathcal{T}^{\text{full}}(s,t,u)=\mathcal{T}^{\text{tree}}(s,t,u)+N(s,t,u).

(73)

Here the tree level amplitude in the small energy expansion is given by (
72
) and the function

Loop computation is a hard task. Luckily there is a simpler way to determine the function

The main idea is to use (

\text{Im}N_{\ell}(s)=2^{-d}s^{\frac{d-4}{2}}\big|\mathcal{T}^{\text{full}}_{\ell}(s)\big|^{2}+\text{(particle production)}\,,

(74)

where we have denote the partial wave projection of the function
N(s,t,u)
by

N_{\ell}(s)\equiv\int_{-1}^{+1}dx\mu_{d,\ell}(x)N(s,t(x),u(x)).

(75)

We solve this equation iteratively order by order in small energy expansion by simply requiring non-linear unitarity (
68
).

21

21

s=0
where the variables

and

Off course, this procedure stops at the order in which particle production appears as emphasize in (

(79)

Analogously to (
63
) we can write the function

as a sum of its partial waves, namely

N(s,t,u)=\sum_{\ell=0,2,\dots}c_{\ell}^{(d)}P_{\ell}^{(d)}\left(1+\frac{2t}{s}\right)\,N_{\ell}(s).

Plugging here the result (

\text{Im}_{s}N(s,t,u)=s^{\tfrac{d}{2}}h(s|t,u),

where the function

h(s|t,u)\equiv\frac{g_{2}^{2}}{4^{d+1}\pi^{\tfrac{d-3}{2}}\Gamma(\tfrac{d+3}{2})}\Big((9d^{2}+6d-4)s^{2}+4(t^{2}+u^{2})\Big)+O(s^{3}).

(82)

Now we can simply guess the form of the function

). We get

(83)

where the function

is defined as

\displaystyle d=\text{even}:\qquad

\displaystyle L_{d}(s)\equiv-\frac{1}{\pi}\log(-s\sqrt{g_{2}})s^{\tfrac{d}{2}},

(84)

\displaystyle d=\text{odd}:\qquad

\displaystyle L_{d}(s)\equiv-(-s)^{\tfrac{d}{2}}.

(85)

Appendix E
Inverse amplitude method

Using elastic unitarity it is possible to unitarize tree-level amplitudes in the
s
-channel. One method for doing this is called the Inverse Amplitude Method (IAM) (see for example
Truong:1988zp
;
Dobado:1996ps
). The main idea is to
assume
elastic unitarity which implies

2\mathcal{N}_{d}(s)\operatorname{Im}\mathcal{T}_{\ell}(s)=|\mathcal{T}_{\ell}(s)|^{2}\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad\operatorname{Im}\left(\frac{1}{\mathcal{T}_{\ell}(s)}\right)=-\frac{1}{2\mathcal{N}_{d}(s)}.

(109)

This equation fixes the imaginary part of the amplitude and leaves the real part free. At this point we are left to choose how the imaginary part arises. The simplest way to solve the above equation is by requiring

\frac{1}{\mathcal{T}_{\ell}(s)}=-\frac{i}{2\mathcal{N}_{d}(s)}+\frac{1}{\alpha_{\ell}(s)}\,,\qquad\alpha_{\ell}(s)\in\mathbb{R}.

(110)

Equivalently, this can be rewritten as

\text{IAM 1:}\qquad\mathcal{S}_{\ell}(s)=\frac{1+i\frac{\alpha_{\ell}(s)}{2\mathcal{N}_{d}(s)}}{1-i\frac{\alpha_{\ell}(s)}{2\mathcal{N}_{d}(s)}}\,.

(111)

The partial amplitude (
111
) is clearly a pure phase and, thus, obeys elastic unitarity. The real function
\alpha_{\ell}(s)
is called the
seed
. Its explicit form depends on the model under consideration. We refer to the partial amplitude (
111
) as the amplitudes obtained with the IAM 1.

In the case of massless particles, we know that the imaginary part arises from the
\log(-s)
term. Using this information we can, thus, make another choice for solving (
109
). Using
\operatorname{Im}(\log(-s))=-\pi
for
s>0
one can rewrite (
109
) as

\operatorname{Im}\left(\frac{1}{\mathcal{T}_{\ell}(s)}\right)=\operatorname{Im}\left(\frac{\log(-s)}{2\pi\mathcal{N}_{d}(s)}\right).

(112)

Solving this equation we obtain

\text{IAM 2:}\qquad\mathcal{T}_{\ell}(s)=\frac{\tilde{\alpha}_{\ell}(s)}{1+\tilde{\alpha}_{\ell}(s)\frac{\log(-s)}{2\pi\mathcal{N}_{d}(s)}}\,,\qquad\tilde{\alpha}_{\ell}(s)\in\mathbb{R}\,.

(113)

We refer to the interacting part of the partial amplitude (
113
) as the one obtained with the IAM 2.
The two solutions (
111
) and (
113
) are equivalent upon the following redefinition of the seeds

\frac{1}{\alpha_{\ell}(s)}=\frac{1}{\tilde{\alpha}_{\ell}(s)}+\frac{\log(s)}{2\mathcal{N}_{d}(s)\pi}\,.

(114)

The natural choice for the seeds reads as

\displaystyle\text{IAM 1}:\quad\alpha_{\ell}(s)=\mathcal{T}_{\ell}^{tree}(s),

(115)

\displaystyle\text{IAM 2}:\quad\tilde{\alpha}_{\ell}(s)=\mathcal{T}_{\ell}^{tree}(s).

(116)

In figure
9
we have plotted the spin zero partial amplitudes obtained using IAM1 and IAM2 for the three-level amplitude (
32
). Both IAM1 and IAM2 expressions have a similar behaviour.
The advantage of IAM 2 is that it naturally reproduces the correct low energy behaviour (
2
) up to
O(s^{4})
order.

\displaystyle\geq 2^{-d}\,\left|B^{0}_{0}+\frac{B^{0}_{1}}{s^{1/2}}+\frac{B^{0}_{2}}{s}+O(s^{-3/2})\right|^{2},

(143)

\displaystyle\ell\geq 2:\qquad\frac{\text{Im}B^{\ell}_{1}}{s^{1/2}}+\frac{\text{Im}B^{\ell}_{2}}{s}+O(s^{-3/2})

\displaystyle\geq 2^{-d}\,\left|\frac{B^{\ell}_{1}}{s^{1/2}}+\frac{B^{\ell}_{2}}{s}+O(s^{-3/2})\right|^{2}.

Recall that
B^{\ell}_{0}=0
for
\ell\geq 2
due to (
139
). The above conditions can be satisfied only if we require
B_{0}^{0}=0
. Taking into account (
139
) we conclude that in 4d the following condition must be obeyed

d=4:\qquad\sum_{a,b,c}\alpha_{abc}\,(-1)^{a+b+c}=0.

(144)

Requiring this condition is equivalent to setting
A_{0}=0
in the
s\rightarrow\infty

The case of 6d.

In the case of

(147)
