Non-uniqueness times for the maximizer of the KPZ fixed point
Duncan Dauvergne

Abstract
. Let 𝔥 t \mathfrak{h}_{t} be the KPZ fixed point started from any initial condition that guarantees 𝔥 t \mathfrak{h}_{t} has a maximum at every time t t almost surely. For any fixed t t , almost surely max ⁡ 𝔥 t \max\mathfrak{h}_{t} is uniquely attained. However, there are exceptional times t ∈ ( 0 , ∞ ) t\in(0,\infty) when max ⁡ 𝔥 t \max\mathfrak{h}_{t} is achieved at multiple points. Let 𝒯 k ⊂ ( 0 , ∞ ) \mathcal{T}_{k}\subset(0,\infty) denote the set of times when max ⁡ 𝔥 t \max\mathfrak{h}_{t} is achieved at exactly k k points. 1.
Introduction

The KPZ (Kardar-Parisi-Zhang) universality class is a large collection of
1
-dimensional models of random interface growth and
2
-dimensional random metrics. The past twenty-five years have seen a period of intense and fruitful research on this class, with progress propelled by the discovery of a handful of exactly solvable models, including tasep, last passage percolation, directed polymers in a random environment, and the KPZ equation itself. See the books and expository articles
[
19
,
33
,
12
,
34
,
5
]
and references therein for background on the KPZ universality class and related areas.

Growth models in the KPZ universality class have a height function
t\mapsto h_{t},t\geq 0
, which at every time
t
returns a one-dimensional interface, often simply a continuous function from
\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
. Typically, the height function is a Markov process in
t
started from some initial condition
h_{0}
. In
[
26
]
, Matetski, Quastel, and Remenik identified a limiting continuous-time Markov process for these height functions known as the
KPZ fixed point
. This object is a fundamental limit within the KPZ universality class, and height functions of all KPZ models should converge to it. This was shown for tasep in
[
26
]
and extended to other integrable models in
[
27
,
16
,
32
,
39
]
.

The domain for the KPZ fixed point is the space of upper semicontinuous functions
h:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty\}

such that
h\not\equiv-\infty
. We write
\mathfrak{h}_{t}(y;h_{0})
for the value of the KPZ fixed point at time
t\geq 0
and location
y\in\mathbb{R}
started from an initial condition
h_{0}
. The KPZ fixed point is well-defined for all times
t\geq 0
as long as the initial condition grows sub-parabolically at
\infty
, see
[
38
]
. Its transition probabilities can be described in terms of a Fredholm determinant, see
[
26
]
, or alternately through a variational problem involving a random metric introduced in
[
13
]
. Moreover,
\mathfrak{h}_{t}(y;h_{0})
is real-valued for all
t>0
, locally Brownian in
y
, and Hölder-
1/3^{-}
continuous in
t
for
t>0
.

As with many scaling limits in probability, the KPZ fixed point exhibits remarkable fractal geometry.
In
[
11
]
, Corwin, Hammond, Hegde, and Matetski started to investigate one of these fractal aspects related to an old conjecture of Johansson and the uniqueness of fixed point maximizers.

1.1.
Johansson’s conjecture and non-uniqueness times

While the construction of the KPZ fixed point is quite recent, some of its marginals have been known for much longer. For example, when the initial condition is a narrow wedge
\delta_{0}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
, given by
\delta_{0}(0)=0
and
\delta_{0}(x)=-\infty
for all other
x
, then for every fixed
t
we have
\mathfrak{h}_{t}(y;\delta_{0})=t^{1/3}\mathcal{A}(t^{-2/3}x)-x^{2}/t
, where
\mathcal{A}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is an Airy (or Airy
2
) process.

Prähofer and Spohn
[
30
]
first identified the Airy process as a scaling limit of the polynuclear growth model (PNG). Shortly afterwards, Johansson
[
25
]
proved an analogous convergence statement for a discrete version of PNG. In that paper, Johansson also made the following conjecture.

Conjecture 1.1

(Conjecture, 1.5,
[
25
]
)
.

Almost surely, the parabolically shifted Airy process
x\mapsto\mathcal{A}(x)-x^{2}
attains its maximum at a unique point
Y\in\mathbb{R}
.

Johansson’s interest in Conjecture
1.1
was in understanding the point-to-line geodesic in a related KPZ model known as geometric last passage percolation. He proved that given Conjecture
1.1
, the scaling limit of the endpoint of the point-to-line geodesic in geometric lat passage percolation is given by
Y
. Conjecture
1.1
was proven by Corwin and Hammond
[
9
]
by showing that
\mathcal{A}
is locally absolutely continuous with respect to Brownian motion of variance
2
1
1

1

Here we say a Brownian motion has variance
2
if its quadratic variation on an interval
[a,b]
is
2(b-a)
. Throughout the paper, whenever we refer to a Brownian motion (or a Bessel process) it will have variance
2
, as this is the convention for KPZ limits.
. Alternate proofs were found by
[
28
,
18
]
.

Rephrased in terms of the KPZ fixed point, Johansson’s conjecture states that
\mathfrak{h}_{1}(\cdot;\delta_{0})
has a unique argmax almost surely. The same turns out to be true for
\mathfrak{h}_{1}(\cdot;h_{0})
for any
h_{0}
as long as
\mathfrak{h}_{1}(\cdot;h_{0})
has a maximum. This follows from an extension of the Corwin-Hammond absolute continuity result by Sarkar and Virág
[
38
]
, which shows that

\mathfrak{h}_{1}(\cdot;h_{0})
is locally absolutely continuous with respect to Brownian continous for essentially any
h_{0}
.

A more nuanced situation occurs when we start to vary
t
. Indeed, it turns out that there is a fractal structure to the set of times when the KPZ fixed point attains its maximum at multiple points, first investigated in
[
11
]
.
To describe this more precisely, we first restrict to the set of initial conditions
h_{0}
such that

(1)

\mathbb{P}(\{\mathfrak{h}_{t}(x;h_{0}):x\in\mathbb{R}\}\text{ has a maximum for all }t>0)=1.

This is the set of initial conditions for which it makes sense to consider questions of non-unique maxima.
It is not difficult to give concrete bounds on the types of initial conditions that satisfy (
1
). Indeed, Lemma
3.1
shows that the condition

\limsup_{|x|\to\infty}\frac{h_{0}(x)}{\log^{2/3}|x|}=-\infty

implies (
1
), and (
1
) implies that
h_{0}(x)\to-\infty
as
|x|\to\infty
. These bounds are not optimal but give a good sense for the types of initial conditions we can work with.

Next, for a KPZ fixed point
\mathfrak{h}=\mathfrak{h}(\cdot;h_{0})
where
h_{0}
satisfies (
1
), let

\argmax\mathfrak{h}_{t}=\{y\in\mathbb{R}:\max_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\mathfrak{h}_{t}(x)=\mathfrak{h}_{t}(y)\}

and for
k\in\mathbb{N}
define sets of
non-uniqueness times

(2)

\mathcal{T}_{k}(h_{0})=\{t\in(0,\infty):|\argmax\mathfrak{h}_{t}|=k\},\quad\mathcal{T}_{\geq k}(h_{0})=\{t\in(0,\infty):|\argmax\mathfrak{h}_{t}|\geq k\}.

In
[
11
, Theorem 1.3]
, Corwin, Hammond, Hegde, and Matetski showed that for one-sided initial conditions
h_{0}
satisfying a certain parabolic decay condition (Assumption 1.1 in
[
11
]
), for any
b>0
we have

(3)

\mathbb{P}(\dim(\mathcal{T}_{\geq 2}(h_{0})\cap[0,b])=2/3\mid\mathcal{T}_{\geq 2}(h_{0})\cap[0,b]\neq\emptyset)=1\quad\text{ and }\quad\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{T}_{\geq 2}(h_{0})\neq\emptyset)>0.

Here and throughout
\dim A
refers to the Hausdorff dimension of a set
A
. They also showed that
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{T}_{\geq 2}(h_{0})\cap[0,b]\neq\emptyset)=1
when
h_{0}
is the narrow wedge and hence that
\mathbb{P}(\dim(\mathcal{T}_{\geq 2}(h_{0})\cap[0,b])=2/3)=1
in this special case. The heuristics in their paper suggested two natural conjectures regarding the behaviour of the
\mathcal{T}_{k}
that would refine (
3
). These can be loosely summarized as follows.

Conjecture 1.2

(Conjectures 1.5, 1.6,
[
11
]
)
.

For nice enough initial conditions
h_{0}
, almost surely
\mathcal{T}_{\geq 2}(h_{0})
is dense in
[0,\infty)
,
\mathcal{T}_{\geq 5}(h_{0})
is empty, and
\mathcal{T}_{2}(h_{0}),\mathcal{T}_{3}(h_{0}),
and
\mathcal{T}_{4}(h_{0})
have Hausdorff dimensions
2/3,1/3,
and
0
respectively.

The main result of this paper establishes Conjecture
1.2
.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
\mathfrak{h}(\cdot;h_{0})
be a KPZ fixed point where
h_{0}
satisfies (
1
). Then almost surely, we have the following assertions:

1.

For any interval
I=[a,b]\subset(0,\infty)
with
a<b
, we have
\dim(\mathcal{T}_{2}(h_{0})\cap I)=2/3
and
\dim(\mathcal{T}_{3}(h_{0})\cap I)=1/3
.

2.

\dim(\mathcal{T}_{4}(h_{0}))=0
and
\mathcal{T}_{\geq 5}(h_{0})=\emptyset
.

Our proof method also goes a bit further towards understanding the fractal structure of the sets
\mathcal{T}_{k}(h_{0})
by relating them to shift and scale-invariant ergodic processes, see Lemma
3.5
and Section
4
.

One question left open by Theorem
1.3
is whether
\mathcal{T}_{4}
is almost surely empty. I believe this to be the case (as do
[
11
]
, see the line following Conjecture 1.5 therein), but the tools in this paper are not currently refined enough to handle this border case. Note that we
can
show that either
\mathcal{T}_{4}
is almost surely empty for every
h_{0}
satisfying (
1
), or almost surely dense for every satisfying (
1
), see Proposition
4.5
.

Just as Johansson’s original conjecture is connected with point-to-line geodesics, so is our Theorem
1.3
. There is a way in which the maximum of the KPZ fixed point can be written as follows:

\max_{y\in\mathbb{R}}\mathfrak{h}_{t}(y;h_{0})=\max_{y\in\mathbb{R}}\max_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\max_{g:(x,0)\to(y,t)}\|g\|_{\mathcal{L}}+h_{0}(x).

Here the rightmost maximum is over all paths
g
between spacetime points
(x,0)
and
(y,t)
, and
\|g\|_{\mathcal{L}}
is the length of
g
as measured in a certain limiting random directed metric
\mathcal{L}
, the directed landscape. A function-to-line geodesic (or point-to-line if
h_{0}
is the narrow wedge) is any
g
realizing the above maximum. Theorem
1.3
then describes times when there are function-to-line geodesics with
different
endpoints realizing this maximum. We refer the reader to Section
2
and
[
13
,
16
]
for more detail on this perspective.

A broader question is to describe the set of times when there are multiple function-to-line geodesics realizing this maximum, possibly even with
the same
endpoint. While this question is not explored in this paper, I believe that almost surely for all
t
, any pair of distinct function-to-line geodesics have different endpoints and so Theorem
1.3
still describes Hausdorff dimensions in this broader setting.

1.2.
Proof sketch

Unsurprisingly, the proof of Theorem
1.3
can be broken into two distinct parts:

(i)

Upper bounds on the Hausdorff dimension and size of the sets
\mathcal{T}_{\geq k}(h_{0})
for
k=2,3,4,5
.

(ii)

Lower bounds on the Hausdorff dimension of the sets
\mathcal{T}_{\geq k}(h_{0})
for
k=2,3
.

When thinking about (i) and (ii), I found
that some initial conditions lent themselves to proving upper bounds and some lent themselves to proving lower bounds (and most initial conditions resisted any clean analysis at all). Because of this, a key ingredient in the paper is a transfer principle for going between initial conditions, based on the Brownian absolute continuity result of
[
38
]
.

Let us explain how the transfer principle works in a simpler setting. Let
B
be a Brownian motion, let
A
be a set of continuous functions, and suppose that by some
0
-
1
law we can show that
\mathbb{P}(B\in A)\in\{0,1\}
. If we can then find some process
Y
which is absolutely continuous with respect to Brownian motion and for which
\mathbb{P}(Y\in A)>0
, then
\mathbb{P}(X\in A)=1
for all processes
X
which are absolutely continuous with respect to Brownian motion. In the setting of the KPZ fixed point there are additional technical complications, but nonetheless we can use
[
38
]
to show the following for many sets
A
:

(4)

\text{If $\mathbb{P}(\mathfrak{h}_{\cdot}(\cdot;h_{0})\in A)>0$ from some $h_{0}$, then $\mathbb{P}(\mathfrak{h}_{\cdot}(\cdot;h_{0})\in A)=1$ for all $h_{0}$}.

We prove a version of (
4
) tailored to our setting in Proposition
4.5
. Because of (
4
), to prove our main theorems it will suffice to prove each of the statements (i) and (ii) for exactly one choice of
h_{0}
.

As is typical, the proof of the upper bound (i) is easier. In this case, we work with the random initial condition
h_{0}=-R
, where
R
is a two-sided Bessel-
3
process. The advantage of this choice is that the evolution of
\mathfrak{h}_{t}(\cdot;-R),t\geq 0
is stationary in
t
after recentering at the maximum, see Theorem
4.1
. Because of this, the upper bound reduces to a simple bound on a standard Bessel-
3
process and a Hölder-
1/3^{-}
continuity estimate for the KPZ fixed point. Moreover,
\mathfrak{h}_{t}(\cdot;-R)
has scale invariance and ergodicity properties that allow us to prove the kind of
0
-
1
laws that underlie the transfer principle (
4
).

The proof of the lower bound (ii) is much harder. Here, the correct choice is to let
h_{0}
consist of a sum of
k
narrow wedges, where
k=2,3
. At small time scales,
\mathfrak{h}_{t}(\cdot;h_{0})
splits into
k
independent narrow wedges, which will create competing maxima that evolve independently. The proof then boils down to estimating the transition probability from time
t
to time
t+s
for the maximum of
\mathfrak{h}_{t}
started from a single narrow wedge.

This is the sort of task for which the Fredholm determinant formula from
[
26
]
is ideally suited. However, there turns out to be a major difficulty here in that the estimate coming from the Fredholm determinant formula increases as a double exponential in one parameter direction.
To deal with this, we find a way of thinning out the sets
\mathcal{T}_{\geq k}(h_{0})
to mitigate the effect from this weak bound. It turns out that a law of the iterated logarithm for the KPZ fixed point can be used in the thinning to just about nullify the effects of the double exponential, see Section
6
for details.

We note that our method is quite different than the method used in
[
11
]
to prove (
3
). In
[
11
]
, the computations required for bounding Hausdorff dimension are tackled fairly directly. In this paper we take a more indirect route, using the ideas discussed above (e.g. transfer principle, finding a stationary process, set thinning) to shift difficulty away from the final computations. One advantage of this approach is that because the final computations are straightforward, we are able to strengthen (
3
) and prove Theorem
1.3
without additional work.

Some related work

This paper follows a recent line of research based around exploiting and understanding the locally Brownian structure of the Airy process
\mathcal{A}
and the KPZ fixed point
\mathfrak{h}_{t}
. For example, quantitative Brownian comparisons from
[

]
underlie the construction of the directed landscape
[

]
.
Many other recent results about the KPZ fixed point, the directed landscape, and related objects, e.g. see
[
22
,
20
,
8
,
17
]
, have been proven with the aid of quantitative comparisons between
\mathcal{A}
and Brownian motion from
[
23
]
and
[
10
]
.
The absolute continuity for
\mathfrak{h}_{t}
from
[
38
]
seems harder to quantify, but
nonetheless has proved useful for showing qualitative results, e.g. see
[
14
]
.

This paper and
[
11
]
are also not the first to study fractal geometry in a KPZ limit. For example,
[
6

7
]
use Brownian absolute continuity results to study fractal geometry in the directed landscape. One major difference between the problem studied here and in

and those previous works is that here, we are trying to understand what happens as the

Transferring results from one initial condition to others is also a frequently used idea in KPZ, though not in the exact guise of (

[

and the papers

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
The KPZ fixed point and the directed landscape

The KPZ fixed point can be described in two ways. The original description in
[
26
]
is through a Fredholm determinant formula. We will only require this formulation for the proof of one key proposition in the paper, so we will set this description aside for now and return to it when it is required in Section
7
.

For the most part, we will understand the KPZ fixed point by appealing to a variational formula in terms of another KPZ limit, the directed landscape.
The
directed landscape
is a random continuous function
\mathcal{L}:\mathbb{R}^{4}_{\uparrow}\to\mathbb{R}
, where
\mathbb{R}^{4}_{\uparrow}:=\{(x,s;y,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{4}:s<t\}
. It is best thought of as a metric on the spacetime plane, where
\mathcal{L}(p;q)
is thought of as a distance from
p=(x,s)
to
q=(y,t)
. However, it is not a true metric: it is not necessarily positive, it is not symmetric, and it satisfies the triangle inequality backwards:

(5)

\mathcal{L}(p;q)\leq\mathcal{L}(p;r)+\mathcal{L}(r;q).

For any initial condition
h_{0}
, the KPZ fixed point
\mathfrak{h}(\cdot;h_{0})
can be expressed using a variational problem involving
\mathcal{L}
:

(6)

\mathfrak{h}_{t}(y)=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}h_{0}(x)+\mathcal{L}(x,0;y,t).

Unless otherwise stated, we always assume
\mathfrak{h}_{t}
and
\mathcal{L}
are related by (
6
).

The directed landscape is built from a random continuous function
\mathcal{S}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
known as the
Airy sheet
whose explicit description we do not require in this paper. The directed landscape is the unique function (in distribution) satisfying the following almost sure properties:

I.

(Metric composition) For any
s<r<t
and
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
we have

\mathcal{L}(x,s;y,t)=\max_{z\in\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{L}(x,s;z,r)+\mathcal{L}(z,r;y,t).

II.

(Independent increments) For any disjoint intervals
(s_{i},t_{i}),i=1,\dots,k
, the random functions
\mathcal{L}(\cdot,s_{i};\cdot,t_{i}):\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R},i=1,\dots,k
are independent.

III.

(Airy sheet marginals) For any
t\in\mathbb{R},s>0
we have

s^{-1}\mathcal{L}(s^{2}x,t;s^{2}y,t+s^{3})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\mathcal{S}(x,y),

where the equality in distribution is as a function of
x
and
y
.

Like many scaling limits, the directed landscape has many symmetries that we will use throughout the paper, frequently without reference.

Proposition 2.1

(Lemma 10.2,
[
13
]
)
.

We have the following equalities in distribution as random continuous functions from
\mathbb{R}^{4}_{\uparrow}\to\mathbb{R}
. Here
r,z\in\mathbb{R}
, and
q>0
.

1.

KPZ scale invariance:

\mathcal{L}(x,t;y,t+s)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}q\mathcal{L}(q^{-2}x,q^{-3}t;q^{-2}y,q^{-3}(t+s)).

2.

Time stationarity:

\mathcal{L}(x,t;y,t+s)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\mathcal{L}(x,t+r;y,t+s+r).

3.

Spatial stationarity:

\mathcal{L}(x,t;y,t+s)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\mathcal{L}(x+z,t;y+z,t+s).

4.

Skew stationarity:

\mathcal{L}(x,t;y,t+s)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\mathcal{L}(x+zt,t;y+zt+sz,t+s)+s^{-1}[(x-y)^{2}-(x-y-sz)^{2}].

In particular, these symmetries imply that for any fixed
x,s,y,t
we have
\mathcal{L}(x,t;y,t+s)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}s^{1/3}\mathcal{L}(0,0;0,1)-(x-y)^{2}/s
. The random variable
\mathcal{L}(0,0;0,1)
is a standard GUE Tracy-Widom random variable; this goes back to
[
3
,
24
]
.

In order to more easily follow arguments in the paper, the reader should also have a good sense of the rough shape of the directed landscape and some of its continuity properties. Propositions
2.2
and
2.3
record estimates on how close the directed landscape and Airy sheet are to their expected parabolic shapes. Proposition
2.4
records a continuity estimate for
\mathcal{L}
.

Proposition 2.2

(Corollary 10.7,
[
13
]
)
.

For all
u=(x,t;y,t+s)\in\mathbb{R}^{4}_{\uparrow}
, we have

\left|\mathcal{L}(x,t;y,t+s)+\frac{(x-y)^{2}}{s}\right|\leq Cs^{1/3}\log^{4/3}\left(\frac{2(\|u\|_{2}+2)^{3/2}}{s}\right)\log^{2/3}(\|u\|_{2}+2).

Here

C>0
is a random constant with
\mathbb{E}a^{C^{3/2}}<\infty
for some
a>1
.

Proposition 2.3

(Lemma 5.3,
[
14
]
)
.

There exists a constant
c>0
such that for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
we have

|\mathcal{S}(x,y)+(x-y)^{2}|\leq C+c\log^{2/3}(2+|x|+|y|).

Here

C>0
is a random constant with
\mathbb{E}a^{C^{3/2}}<\infty
for some
a>1
.

Proposition 2.4

(Proposition 1.6,
[
13
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{R}(x,t;y,t+s)=\mathcal{L}(x,t;y,t+s)+(x-y)^{2}/s
denote the stationary version of the directed landscape. Let
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{4}_{\uparrow}
be a compact set. Then

|\mathcal{R}(u)-\mathcal{R}(v)|\leq C_{K}\left(\tau^{1/3}\log^{2/3}(\tau^{-1}+1)+\xi^{1/2}\log^{1/2}(\xi^{-1}+1)\right)

for all points
u=(x,s;y,t),v=(x^{\prime},s^{\prime};y^{\prime},t^{\prime})\in K
where
\xi:=\|(x,y)-(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\|_{2},\tau:=\|(s,t)-(s^{\prime},t^{\prime})\|_{2}
. Here

C_{K}>0
is a random constant depending on
K
with
\mathbb{E}a^{C_{K}^{3/2}}<\infty
for some
a>1
.

The key thing to remember from Proposition
2.4
is that
\mathcal{L}
is Hölder-
1/3^{-}
as we vary the time coordinates. This estimate is crucial for understanding how the Hausdorff dimensions in Theorem
1.3
arise.

The directed landscape also has mixing properties both as we shift in time and space and as we rescale. We will only need a few of these properties moving forward.

Proposition 2.5
.

For
t>0
, define
\mathcal{F}_{t}=\sigma\{\mathcal{L}(x,s;y,r):x,y\in\mathbb{R},s<r\in[0,t]\}
. Then for any
A\in\mathcal{F}_{0}^{+}:=\bigcap_{t>0}\mathcal{F}_{t}
, we have
\mathbb{P}A\in\{0,1\}
.

Proof.

Almost surely, for any
(x,s;y,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{4}_{\uparrow}
with
0\leq s
, we have

\mathcal{L}(x,s;y,t)=\lim_{r\to 0^{+}}\mathcal{L}(x,r;y,t)

by continuity. For
r>0
,
\mathcal{L}(x,r;y,t)
is independent of
\mathcal{F}_{0}^{+}
by the independent increment property of
\mathcal{L}
. Therefore the whole process
\mathcal{L}(\cdot,s;\cdot,\cdot),0\leq s
is independent of
\mathcal{F}_{0}^{+}
. On the other hand,
\mathcal{F}_{0}^{+}
is contained in the
\sigma
-algebra generated by
\mathcal{L}(\cdot,s;\cdot,\cdot),0\leq s
, so
\mathcal{F}_{0}^{+}
is independent of itself, yielding the result.
∎

Proposition 2.6
.

Fix
k\in\mathbb{N}
. For every
i=1,\dots,k
and
\epsilon>0
, let

K_{i,\epsilon}=\{(x,s;y,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{4}_{\uparrow}:s,t\in[0,\epsilon],x,y\in[i-1/4,i+1/4]\}.

We can couple
k+1
copies of the directed landscape
\mathcal{L}_{0},\mathcal{L}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{L}_{k}
so that
\mathcal{L}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{L}_{k}
are independent and almost surely for all small enough
\epsilon>0
and
i=1,\dots,k
we have
\mathcal{L}_{0}|_{K_{i,\epsilon}}=\mathcal{L}_{i}|_{K_{i,\epsilon}}
.

The proof of Proposition
2.6
follows a standard, but rather tedious, approximation from a prelimiting last passage model. We leave it to Appendix
A
.

We will require one more result about the KPZ fixed point.
For this next theorem and throughout the paper, for random continuous functions
F,G
with a common domain
D
we write
F\ll_{c}G
if the law of
F|_{K}
is absolutely continuous with respect to the law of
G|_{K}
for any compact set
K\subset D
. We will also write
X\ll Y
for two random variables
X,Y
if the law of
X
is absolutely continuous with respect to the law of
Y
.

Theorem 2.7

(special case of Theorem 1.2,
[
38
]
)
.

Let
\mathfrak{h}_{\cdot}
denote the KPZ fixed point run from any initial condition
h_{0}
which is bounded above. Then for any
t>0
, we have
\mathfrak{h}_{t}(\cdot)-\mathfrak{h}_{t}(0)\ll_{c}B
, where
B:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is a two-sided Brownian motion.

2.2.
Absolute continuity facts

Theorem 2.8

(Theorem 2.15,
[
14
]
)
.

Let
B
be a Brownian motion on
[0,1]
, and let
T
be the unique point of maximum of
B
. Let
\mu
of be the law of

B(T)-B(T+t),\qquad t\in[-T,1-T]\,.

Note that
\mu
is the law of a random function defined on random interval.
Let
R
be a two-sided Bessel-
3
process independent of
T
, and let
\nu
be the law of
R
on the interval
[-T,1-T]
.
Then
\mu
is absolutely continuous with respect to
\nu
.

In Theorem
2.8
, we can think of continuous functions on a random closed interval
I\subset[-1,1]
as continuous functions on
[-1,1]
by extending the functions to be constant off of
I
. Equipping these extensions with the uniform norm (and identifying functions with the same extension) makes the space of such functions a complete separable metric space.

Theorems
2.7
and
2.8
together imply that the Bessel-
3
process should play a crucial role in studying the KPZ fixed point near its maximum. We end this section with two more facts that will be useful for going between local absolute continuity statements and global ones.

Lemma 2.9
.

Let
X,Y
be two random variables, and suppose that
X_{n}
is a sequence of random variables such that there exists a random
N\in\mathbb{N}
with
X_{N}=X
and such that
X_{n}\ll Y
for all
n
. Then
X\ll Y
.

Proof.

Consider an event
A
with
\mathbb{P}(X\in A)>0
. Then there exists
n\in\mathbb{N}
for which
\mathbb{P}(X\in A,N=n)>0
and hence
\mathbb{P}(X_{n}\in A)>0
. Since
X_{n}\ll Y
,
\mathbb{P}(Y\in A)>0
as well.
∎

Lemma 2.10

(Lemma 4.3,
[
14
]
)
.

Let
D:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be any stochastic process, let
B
be an
n
-dimensional Brownian motion, and suppose that
D\ll_{c}B
. Then

D_{\epsilon}(t):=\epsilon^{-1}D(\epsilon^{2}t)

converges in law to
B
as
\epsilon\to 0
in the uniform-on-compact topology.

Lemma
2.10
also applies if
B
is replaced by a two-sided Bessel-
3
process

, since
R=\|B\|_{2}
, where
B
is a
3
-dimensional Brownian motion.

3.
Always decaying initial conditions and absolute continuity

We say that an initial condition
h_{0}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty\}
is
always decaying
if for any fixed
t>0
, a.s. we have

(7)

\lim_{|x|\to\infty}\mathfrak{h}_{t}(x;h_{0})\to-\infty.

The goal of this section is to prove a few basic properties of the KPZ fixed point run from an always decaying initial condition, culminating in two absolute continuity results relating always decaying initial conditions to the initial condition
h_{0}=-R
, where
R
is a Bessel-
3
process. As part of this effort, we show that
h_{0}
is always decaying if and only if it satisfies (
1
).

Our first lemma uses basic bounds to give criteria for when initial conditions are always decaying. We have not attempted to refine this lemma to get an optimal result.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
h_{0}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty\}
be an upper semicontinuous function.

(i)

If
\displaystyle\lim_{|x|\to\infty}\frac{h_{0}(x)}{\log^{2/3}(|x|)}=-\infty

then
h_{0}
is always decaying.

(ii)

Suppose that
\limsup_{|x|\to\infty}h_{0}(x)>-\infty
. Then for any
t>0
, a.s. we have

\limsup_{|x|\to\infty}\mathfrak{h}_{t}(x)=\infty.

In particular, if
h_{0}
is always decaying, then
\displaystyle\lim_{|x|\to\infty}h_{0}(x)=-\infty
.

Proof.

Part (i) follows immediately from the bound in Proposition
2.3
on the Airy sheet, the fact that
\mathcal{L}
has Airy sheet marginals, and the variational representation (
6
) for
\mathfrak{h}_{t}
.

For part (ii), suppose that
\limsup_{|x|\to\infty}h_{0}(x)>-\infty
. Then there is an unbounded sequence
x_{n},n\in\mathbb{N}
and a constant
c\in\mathbb{R}
such that
h_{0}(x_{n})\geq c
for all
n
. We have

\limsup_{|x|\to\infty}\mathfrak{h}_{t}(x)\geq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathfrak{h}_{t}(x_{n})\geq c+\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{L}(x_{n},0;x_{n},1).

Now, the process
\mathcal{L}(x,0;x,1),x\in\mathbb{R}
is stationary (Proposition
2.1
.3) and
\mathcal{L}(0,0;0,1)
is a standard Tracy-Widom and hence has unbounded support. Therefore the limsup above is
\infty
if we can show that the joint distribution of
\mathcal{L}(0,0;0,1),\mathcal{L}(x,0;x,1)
converges as
x\to\pm\infty
to that of two independent Tracy-Widom random variables. This follows from the independence established in Proposition
2.5
and KPZ rescaling (Proposition
2.1
.1):

(\mathcal{L}(0,0;0,1),\mathcal{L}(x,0;x,1))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}x^{1/2}(\mathcal{L}(0,0;0,x^{-3/2}),\mathcal{L}(1,0;1,x^{-3/2})).\qed

Next, we use the independent increment property of
\mathcal{L}
prove two results about the KPZ fixed point from an always decaying initial condition, including the equivalence of (
7
) and (
1
).

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
h_{0}
be an always decaying initial condition. Then for any compact interval
[a,b]\subset(0,\infty)
, a.s. we have

(8)

\displaystyle\lim_{|x|\to\infty}\sup_{t\in[a,b]}\mathfrak{h}_{t}(x)=-\infty.

Proof.

Suppose that the left side of (
8
) is at least some constant
c+1
with positive probability. For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let

T_{n}=\inf\{t\in[a,b]:\sup_{x\notin[-n,n]}\mathfrak{h}_{t}(x)\geq c+1/2\}.

Let
X_{n}\in[-n,n]^{c}
be any (measurable) point such that
\mathfrak{h}_{T_{n}}(X_{n})\geq c
. Now, since each
T_{n}
is a stopping time with respect to the filtration
\mathcal{F}_{t}=\{\mathcal{L}(x,s;y,r):r\leq t\}
, by the independent increment property of
\mathcal{L}
, each of the random variables
\mathfrak{h}_{b}(X_{n}),n\in\mathbb{N}
satisfies

\mathfrak{h}_{b}(X_{n})\geq\mathfrak{h}_{T_{n}}(X_{n})+\mathcal{L}(X_{n},T_{n};X_{n},b)\geq c+\mathcal{L}(X_{n},T_{n};X_{n},b)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}c+(b-T_{n})^{1/3}Y,

where
Y
is an independent Tracy-Widom GUE random variable and we use the convention that
\mathcal{L}(X_{n},T_{n};X_{n},b)=0
if
T_{n}=b
. In particular,

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(\mathfrak{h}_{b}(X_{n})\geq c)>0,

and so with positive probability
\mathfrak{h}_{b}(x)\not\to-\infty
as
|x|\to\infty
. This is a contradiction.
∎

Corollary 3.3
.

An initial condition
h_{0}
is always decaying if and only if (
1
) holds.

Proof.

Condition (
7
) implies condition (
1
) by Lemma
3.2
. We prove the opposite implication with the contrapositive. Fix
t>0
and suppose that (
7
) does not hold almost surely at
t
. Then by Lemma
3.1
(ii), with positive probability
\limsup_{|x|\to\infty}\mathfrak{h}_{2t}(x)=\infty
, since we can view
\mathfrak{h}_{2t}
as a KPZ fixed point at time
t
started from the random initial condition
\mathfrak{h}_{t}
. This contradicts (
1
).
∎

We now turn our attention to proving absolute continuity results for the KPZ fixed point. The key point for these results is Theorem
2.7
, which is applicable since Lemma
3.1
(ii) implies that always decaying initial conditions are bounded above. For
y\in\mathbb{R}
define the shift operator
T_{y}
on functions
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
by

T_{y}f(x)=f(x+y)-f(y).

Lemma 3.4
.

If
h_{0}
is an always decaying initial condition, then for any fixed
t>0
, almost surely
\mathfrak{h}_{t}=\mathfrak{h}_{t}(\cdot;h_{0})
attains its maximum at a unique point
Y_{t}
. Moreover,
T_{Y_{t}}\mathfrak{h}_{t}\ll_{c}-R
, where
R:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is a two-sided Bessel-
3
process.

Proof.

By Theorem
2.7
,
T_{0}\mathfrak{h}_{t}\ll_{c}B
, where
B
is a Brownian motion. Therefore almost surely on every interval
[-m,m],m\in\mathbb{N}
,
\mathfrak{h}_{t}
attains its maximum at a unique point
Y^{m}\in[-m,m]
.
Since
h_{0}
is always decaying and
\mathfrak{h}_{t}
is continuous,
\mathfrak{h}_{t}
must have a global maximum. Any global argmax for
\mathfrak{h}_{t}
must coincide with
Y^{m}
for all large enough
m
. This yields the first part of the lemma, and gives that a.s.
Y^{m}=Y_{t}
for all large enough
m
.

Next, for
m\in\mathbb{N}
we define random continuous functions
f_{m}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
as follows. First set
f_{m}
equal to
T_{Y^{m}}\mathfrak{h}_{t}
on
[-m-Y^{m},m-Y^{m}]
. Next, define
f_{m}
on
(-\infty,-m-Y^{m}]\cup[m-Y^{m},\infty)
so that the conditional law of
-f_{m}|_{(-\infty,-m-Y^{m}]\cup[m-Y^{m},\infty)}
given
f_{m}|_{[-m-Y^{m},m-Y^{m}]}
is that of a two-sided Bessel-
3
process equal to
T_{Y^{m}}\mathfrak{h}_{t}(\pm m-Y^{m})
at
\pm m-Y^{m}
.

By Theorems
2.7
and
2.8
, for every
m\in\mathbb{N}
we have
f_{m}\ll-R
, where
R
is a two-sided Bessel-
3
process. This absolute continuity is global, not local (i.e. it is as continuous functions from
\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
).
Moreover, for large enough
m
, on any compact set
K
we have
f_{m}|_{K}=T_{Y_{t}}\mathfrak{h}_{t}
. Therefore by Lemma
2.9
,
T_{Y_{t}}\mathfrak{h}_{t}\ll_{c}-R
.
∎

Next, consider an always decaying initial condition
h_{0}
and let
\mathfrak{h}_{t}
be the KPZ fixed point evolved from
h_{0}
via a directed landscape
\mathcal{L}
. For
t\in(0,\infty]
, define the set

\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}^{t}(h_{0})

\displaystyle=\{(s,y)\in(0,t]\times\mathbb{R}:y\in\argmax(\mathfrak{h}_{s}(\cdot;h_{0}))\}.

When
t=\infty
we simply write
\mathcal{Y}(h_{0})
.
This next lemma concerns the distribution of the set
\mathcal{Y}^{s}(T_{Y_{t}}\tilde{\mathfrak{h}}_{t})
. Here
we are using an evolved KPZ fixed point
T_{Y_{t}}\tilde{\mathfrak{h}}_{t}(\cdot;h_{0})
as an initial condition, and the KPZ fixed point
\mathfrak{h}
used in the definition of the set
\mathcal{Y}^{s}
is independent of this initial condition.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
h_{0}
be an always decaying initial condition, and let
R
be a two-sided independent Bessel-
3
process. For any
t,s\in(0,\infty)
we have that

\mathcal{Y}^{s}(T_{Y_{t}}\tilde{\mathfrak{h}}_{t})\ll\mathcal{Y}^{s}(-R).

Proof.

For ease of notation, let
F=T_{Y_{t}}\tilde{\mathfrak{h}}_{t}
. By Lemma
3.4
,
F\ll_{c}-R
. Next, for each
n
, define a continuous function
F_{n}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
so that
F_{n}|_{[-n,n]}=F|_{[-n,n]}
, and the conditional law of
-F_{n}|_{[-n,n]^{c}}
given
F_{n}|_{[-n,n]}
is that of a two-sided Bessel-
3
process whose endpoints at
\pm n
are chosen so that
F_{n}
is continuous. As in the proof of Lemma
3.4
,
F_{n}\ll-R
.

Next, we aim to show that for any
s>0
, a.s. there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}
for which

(9)

\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}^{s}(F_{N})=\mathcal{Y}^{s}(F).

If we can establish (
9
), then the result will follow from Lemma
2.9
. First, observe that
F
is a.s. always decaying by the definition (
7
) and since
\mathfrak{h}_{t+r}(\cdot+Y_{t};h_{0})-\mathfrak{h}_{t}(Y_{t})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\mathfrak{h}_{r}(\cdot;F)
for any
r\geq 0
. Each
F_{n}
is a.s. always decaying by Lemma
3.1
(i) and a standard bound on a Bessel process (Lemma
B.1
). We require this for both sides of (
9
) to be a.s. well-defined.
To prove (
9
), we will show that for every
\epsilon>0
we can find
m\in\mathbb{N},c>0
such that for all large enough
n\in\mathbb{N}
, with probability at least
1-\epsilon
all of the following statements hold:

(i)

For all
r\in(0,s]
and
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
with
|y-x|\geq c\log(2+|x|+|y|)
we have
\mathcal{L}(x,0;y,r)<-c<\mathcal{L}(0,0;0,r)
.

(ii)

For
r\in[0,s]
, we have

\sup_{x\notin[-m,m]}\mathfrak{h}_{r}(x;F)<-c
,

(iii)

For
r\in[0,s]
, we have
\sup_{x\notin[-m,m]}\mathfrak{h}_{r}(x;F_{n})<-c
.

Let us first explain why these together imply (
9
). Let
r\in(0,s]
and assume (i)-(iii) hold for some sufficiently large
n
. First, since
F(0)=0
, we have that
\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\mathfrak{h}_{r}(x;F)\geq\mathcal{L}(0,0;0,r)>-c
. Therefore

(10)

\max_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\mathfrak{h}_{r}(x;F)>\max_{x\in A^{c}}\mathfrak{h}(x;F),\qquad\text{ where }A=\{x:\mathfrak{h}_{r}(x;F)\geq-c\}.

By (ii), the set
A=\{x:\mathfrak{h}_{r}(x;F)\geq-c\}
is contained in the interval
[-m,m]
. Moreover, since
F\leq 0
everywhere, condition (i) implies that at every
x\in A
we have

\displaystyle\mathfrak{h}_{r}(x;F)

\displaystyle=\sup\{F(y)+\mathcal{L}(y,0;x,r):|y-x|\leq c\log(2+|x|+|y|)\}.

Since
x\in[-m,m]
, as long as
n
is sufficiently large given
m,c
this implies

(11)

\mathfrak{h}_{r}(x;F)=\sup\{F(y)+\mathcal{L}(y,0;x,r):|y|\leq n\}.

Putting together (
10
) and (
11
), we get that

(12)

\mathcal{Y}^{s}(F)=\mathcal{Y}^{s}(F|_{[-n,n]}),

where we use the notation
F|_{K}
for the function equal to
F
on
K
and
-\infty
elsewhere. By analogous reasoning with (ii) in place of (iii), equation (
12
) also holds with
F_{n}
in place of
F
. Since
F|_{[-n,n]}=F_{n}|_{[-n,n]}
, this implies (
9
).

We turn our attention to proving (i)-(iii).
The existence of a constant
c
satisfying (i) with probability
1-\epsilon/4
follows from Proposition
2.2
. Given
c
, condition (ii) holds with probability at least
1-\epsilon/4
for large enough
m
by the fact that

\sup_{r\in[0,s],x\notin[-m,m]}\mathfrak{h}_{r}(x;F)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\sup_{r\in[t,t+s],x\notin[-m,m]}\mathfrak{h}_{r}(x+Y_{t};h_{0})-\mathfrak{h}_{t}(Y_{t})\to-\infty

a.s. as
m\to\infty
(Lemma
3.2
). The proof of (iii) is slightly more involved. First, by a standard bound on Bessel processes (Lemma
B.1
) for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
x\geq n
we have

F_{n}(x)\leq X_{n}-(x-n)^{1/4}-|F(n)|^{1/4},

where
X_{n},n\in\mathbb{N}
is a tight sequence of random variables. Combining this bound with Proposition
2.2
, we find that there is a tight sequence of random variables
C_{n}>0
such that for all
x\geq n,y\in\mathbb{R}
and
r\in[0,s]
we have

(13)

\mathcal{L}(x,0;y,r)+F_{n}(x)\leq C_{n}\log^{2}(2+|y-n|+|x-n|)-(x-y)^{2}+X_{n}-(x-n)^{1/4}-|F(n)|^{1/4}.

Here we are able to center the logarithmic error at the point
(n,n)
using the spatial stationarity of
\mathcal{L}
(Proposition
2.1
.3). By (
13
), the tightness of
X_{n},C_{n}
and the fact that
F(n)\to-\infty
almost surely we get that

\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R},x\geq n,r\in[0,s]}\mathcal{L}(x,0;y,r)+F_{n}(x)\to-\infty\qquad\text{in probability as $n\to\infty$.}

A symmetric statement holds when we take the supremum over

y\in\mathbb{R},x\leq-n
. Therefore

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\mathfrak{h}_{r}(x;F_{n}|_{[-n,n]^{c}})<-c

for all
r\in[0,s]
with probability tending to
1
as
n\to\infty
. Moreover,

\sup_{x\notin[-m,m]}\mathfrak{h}_{r}(x;F_{n}|_{[-n,n]})=\sup_{x\notin[-m,m]}\mathfrak{h}_{r}(x;F|_{[-n,n]})\leq\sup_{x\notin[-m,m]}\mathfrak{h}_{r}(x;F).

This is less than
-c
with probability at least
1-\epsilon/4
by (ii). Combining these implies that for all large enough
n
, (iii) holds with probability at least
1-\epsilon/2
, and so (i)-(iii) simultaneously hold with probability
1-\epsilon
, as desired.
∎

4.
The Bessel Initial Condition and a transfer principle

Lemma
3.5
demonstrates the importance of the negative
2
-sided Bessel initial condition
-R
for analyzing times where
|\argmax\mathfrak{h}_{t}|>1
. In this section we study the KPZ fixed point from this special initial condition and use this to give a precise formulation of the transfer principle (
4
) in our setting.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\mathfrak{h}_{t}=\mathfrak{h}_{t}(\cdot;-R)
. For any fixed
t>0
, almost surely
\mathfrak{h}_{t}
has a unique argmax
Y_{t}
and

(14)

T_{Y_{t}}\mathfrak{h}_{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}-R.

The main step in the proof of Theorem
4.1
is the following.

Lemma 4.2
.

For
t>0
, let
Y_{t}=\argmax_{y\in\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{L}(0,0;y,t)
and let

F_{t,\epsilon}(x)=\epsilon^{-1}\left(\mathcal{L}(0,0;\epsilon^{2}x+Y_{t},t)-\mathcal{L}(0,0;Y_{t},t)\right).

Similarly, let

\mathcal{L}_{\epsilon}(x,s;y,t)=\epsilon^{-1}\left(\mathcal{L}(\epsilon^{2}x+Y_{1},1+\epsilon^{3}s;\epsilon^{2}y+Y_{1},1+\epsilon^{3}t)\right)

denote the shifted and rescaled landscape. Then letting
K=\{(x,s;y,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{4}_{\uparrow}:s,t\in[0,1]\}
,
we have

(F_{1,\epsilon},\mathcal{L}_{\epsilon}|_{K},F_{1+\epsilon^{3},\epsilon})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}(-R,\mathcal{L}^{\prime}|_{K},-R^{\prime})

as
\epsilon\to 0
, where
R,R^{\prime}
are Bessel-
3
processes,
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
is a directed landscape independent of
R
, and

(15)

-R^{\prime}=T_{\tilde{Y}_{1}}\mathfrak{h}_{1}(\cdot;-R),

where
\mathfrak{h}_{t}
is driven by the noise
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}|_{K}
and
\tilde{Y}_{1}
is the a.s. unique argmax of
\mathfrak{h}_{1}(\cdot;-R)
. Here the underlying topology is uniform-on-compact convergence of continuous functions from
\mathbb{R}\times K\times\mathbb{R}
to
\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

The convergence of the marginals
F_{1,\epsilon},F_{1+\epsilon^{3},\epsilon}
to Bessel processes
R,R^{\prime}
is immediate from Lemma
3.4
and the comment following Lemma
2.10
. Also,
\mathcal{L}_{\epsilon}|_{K}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\mathcal{L}^{\prime}|_{K}
by shift and scale invariance of
\mathcal{L}
and
F_{1,\epsilon}
and
L_{\epsilon}|_{K}
are independent for all
\epsilon
.

Putting all these observations together, we get that the collection
(F_{1,\epsilon},\mathcal{L}_{\epsilon}|_{K},F_{1+\epsilon^{3},\epsilon}),\epsilon>0
is tight in
\epsilon
, and any subsequential distributional limit
(-R,\mathcal{L}^{\prime}|_{K},-R^{\prime})
, has the desired marginal distributions for
-R,\mathcal{L}^{\prime}|_{K},-R^{\prime}
and has
-R,\mathcal{L}^{\prime}|_{K}
independent. To complete the proof we just need to establish (
15
). We start with the version of (
15
) that holds in the prelimit. Setting

(16)

\mathfrak{h}_{1,\epsilon}(y):=\max_{x\in\mathbb{R}}F_{1,\epsilon}(x)+\mathcal{L}_{\epsilon}(x,0;y,1),

we have

F_{1+\epsilon^{3},\epsilon}=T_{Y_{1,\epsilon}}\mathfrak{h}_{1,\epsilon},

where
Y_{1,\epsilon}
is the (almost surely unique) argmax of
\mathfrak{h}_{1,\epsilon}
. Concretely then, it suffices to show that
(F_{1,\epsilon},\mathcal{L}_{1,\epsilon}|_{K},\mathfrak{h}_{1,\epsilon},Y_{1,\epsilon})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}(-R,\mathcal{L}^{\prime}|_{K},\mathfrak{h}_{1},\tilde{Y}_{1})
, where
\mathfrak{h}_{1}=\mathfrak{h}_{1}(\cdot;-R)
and
\tilde{Y}_{1}
are as in the statement of the lemma. Here the topology of convergence for
\mathfrak{h}_{1,\epsilon}
is the uniform-on-compact topology.
Since
Y_{1,\epsilon}=\argmax\mathfrak{h}_{1,\epsilon}
and
Y_{1}=\argmax\mathfrak{h}_{1}
, we can break this convergence up into two steps:

(i)

Showing
(F_{1,\epsilon},\mathcal{L}_{\epsilon}|_{K},\mathfrak{h}_{1,\epsilon})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}(-R,\mathcal{L}^{\prime}|_{K},\mathfrak{h}_{1})

(ii)

For (i), for

for a collection of identically distributed random variables

and some

and Proposition

(c)

For any

We move on to (ii). By the convergence in (i),

where
\mathfrak{h}_{1}
is a copy of the KPZ fixed point started from

. The function

is almost surely an always decaying initial condition by Lemma

and standard Bessel properties (Lemma

), and hence the argmax

of

is a.s. unique. Point (ii) will then follow if we can show that

is tight. Define

we have

for all

for some random

. Now fix

and choose

Next we turn to the main object of the paper: non-uniqueness times for the KPZ fixed point. Recalling the notation

Proposition 4.5
.

For every

, there exists a constant

and values
\beta_{k}\in\{0,\infty\}

\displaystyle\dim(\mathcal{T}_{\geq k}(h_{0})\cap I)

\displaystyle=\alpha_{k},\qquad\;\text{and}\;\qquad\#(\mathcal{T}_{\geq k}(h_{0})\cap I)=\beta_{k}.

Proof.

We begin by proving the proposition for the random initial condition

and restricting to the case when

. We start with the first equality in (

).
It suffices to prove that the statement holds a.s. for every rational interval

k\in\mathbb{N}
and

\dim(\mathcal{T}_{\geq k}(-R)\cap[0,b])=\sup_{q=0,1,2,\dots}\dim(\mathcal{T}_{\geq k}(-R)\cap[0,2^{-3q}b]).

By Lemma

, and the fact that Hausdorff dimension is unchanged under linear dilations of a set, the sequence

\sup_{q\in\mathbb{N}}\dim(\mathcal{T}_{\geq k}(-R)\cap[0,2^{-3q}b])=\alpha_{k},

We modify the above proof to establish the second equality in (

. First, the function

is monotone with respect to set inclusion, and unchanged under linear dilations of a set, so the proof above goes through verbatim to show that there exists
\beta_{k}\in\{0,1,\dots,\infty\}
such that the second equality in (

for any interval

. We can rule out the possibility that

since by the inequality

Now consider a general initial condition

. By the result for

and the absolute continuity in Lemma

, a.s. (

∎

Proposition

allows us to transfer information about Hausdorff dimension and set size from one initial condition to all others. In particular, Proposition

.

We now address the probability that a Bessel process has multiple near-minima.

Lemma 5.3
.

Fix
k\in\mathbb{N}
and a
k
-tuple of disjoint closed intervals
I=(I_{1},\dots,I_{k})
, where
I_{j}=[a_{j},b_{j}]
, ordered so that
b_{i-1}<a_{i}
for all
i\geq 2
. Set

m_{I}=\min\{a_{i}-b_{i-1}:i\in\{2,\dots,k\}\}.

Let
R
be a two-sided Bessel-
3
process and define the event

A(R,I,\epsilon)=\{\forall i\in\{1,\dots,k\},\min_{x\in I_{i}}R(x)\leq\epsilon\}.

Then for all
\epsilon>0
we have

\mathbb{P}A(R,I,\epsilon)\leq(\epsilon/\sqrt{m_{I}})^{k-1}.

Proof.

Let
j\in\{1,\dots,k\}
be chosen so that
d(I_{j},0)
is minimized, and let
I^{-}=(I_{1},\dots,I_{j-1})
and
I^{+}=(I_{j+1},\dots,I_{k})
. By construction, all intervals in
I^{-}
are contained in
(-\infty,-m_{I}/2]
and all intervals in
I^{+}
are contained in
[m_{I}/2,\infty)
.
Therefore since
A(R,I,\epsilon)\subset A(R,I^{-},\epsilon)\cap A(R,I^{+},\epsilon)
and
R(\cdot)|_{[0,\infty)}
and
R(-\;\cdot)|_{[0,\infty)}
are independent Bessel processes, we have that

\mathbb{P}A(R,I,\epsilon)\leq\mathbb{P}A(R,I^{-},\epsilon)\mathbb{P}A(R,I^{+},\epsilon),

and the lemma will follow if we can show that
\mathbb{P}A(R,I^{-},\epsilon)\leq(\epsilon/\sqrt{m_{I}})^{j-1}
and
\mathbb{P}A(R,I^{+},\epsilon)\leq(\epsilon/\sqrt{m_{I}})^{k-j}
. The proofs of these two claims are identical, so we just focus on the latter.

Letting
L_{i}=\min_{x\in I_{i}}R(x)
, it is enough to show that for every
i=j+1,\dots,k
we have

(21)

\mathbb{P}(L_{i}<\epsilon\;|\;R|_{[0,b_{i-1}\vee 0]})\leq\epsilon/\sqrt{m_{I}}.

First, by the Markov property for
R
,
L_{i}
only depends on
R|_{[0,b_{i-1}\vee 0]}
through
R(a_{i})
. Moreover,
L_{i}\geq L_{i}^{\prime}:=\min_{x\in[a_{i},\infty)}R(x)
and the distribution of
L_{i}^{\prime}
given
R(a_{i})
is
UR(a_{i})
, where
U
is an independent uniform random variable on
[0,1]
, see
[
35
, Chapter VI, Corollary 3.4]
. Next, the conditional distribution of
R(a_{i})
given
R|_{[0,b_{i-1}\vee 0]}
stochastically dominates the (unconditional) distribution of
R(a_{i}-b_{i-1}\vee 0)
. In particular, using the representation of
R
as the magnitude of
3
-dimensional Brownian motion of variance
2
, we have

R(a_{i})\succeq\sqrt{2a_{i}-2b_{i-1}\vee 0}\sqrt{N_{1}^{2}+N_{2}^{2}+N_{3}^{2}}\geq\sqrt{m_{I}}\sqrt{N_{1}^{2}+N_{2}^{2}+N_{3}^{2}}.

where the notation
\succeq
denotes stochastic domination given
R|_{[0,b_{i-1}\vee 0]}
, and the
N_{i}
are independent standard normals. Therefore

\mathbb{P}(L_{i}<\epsilon\;|\;R|_{[0,b_{i-1}\vee 0]})\leq\mathbb{P}(U\sqrt{m_{I}}\sqrt{N_{1}^{2}+N_{2}^{2}+N_{3}^{2}}\leq\epsilon),

and a computation then yields (
21
).
∎

This is similar to the proof of Proposition

Our remaining task is to estimate

Proposition 6.4

Let

be any initial condition bounded above by

We would like to use Proposition

Lemma 6.5
.

Let

\inf_{t\in(0,1/2]}\frac{f(t)}{(t\log\log(2+t^{-1}))^{1/3}}>-\infty.

We prepare for the proof of Lemma

Lemma 6.6
.

For an interval

and

\mathbb{P}(Y_{[a,b],s,t}>mt^{1/3}\log^{2/3}(t^{-1}))\leq c(b-a+1)e^{-dm^{3/2}}
for universal

Proof.

The distribution of

does not change if we shift

so by a union bound it suffices to prove the lemma when

2.4
to the compact set

∎

Proof of Lemma
6.5
.

For all

3.2
all the

Let

\mathbb{P}(f^{n}(t)\leq-mt^{1/3})\leq ce^{-dm^{3}}

for absolute constants

Next, with notation as in Lemma

\displaystyle\min_{r\in[0,t]}f^{n}(s-r)=f^{n}(s)-\max_{r\in[0,t]}(M^{n}_{1-s+r}-M^{n}_{1-s})\geq f(s)-Y_{[-n,n],1-s,t}.

Lemma 6.7
.

Let
\mathfrak{h}_{t}
be the KPZ fixed point started from an always decaying initial condition, and let

and

.
Define

by

\sup_{t\in(0,1/2]}\frac{g(t)}{t^{2/3}\log^{16+\epsilon}(t^{-1})}<\infty.

Note that by Lemma
3.4
, for any fixed

, almost surely

, so we could alternately the define

with

instead of

.

Proof.

First, by Lemma
3.5
, it suffices to prove the result when

is started from the Bessel initial condition
-R
. Next, for the Bessel initial condition, by Theorem

((S_{t+s}-S_{t},I_{t+s}-S_{t}):s\in(0,\infty))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}((S_{s},I_{s}):s\in(0,\infty))

for any

. Moreover, we have the scale invariance

((S_{s},I_{s}):s\in(0,\infty))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}(q^{-2}(S_{q^{3}s},I_{q^{3}s}):s\in(0,\infty))

for any

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[2^{-k},2^{-k+1}]}

\displaystyle g(t)

\displaystyle=\sup_{t\in[2^{-k},2^{-k+1}]}|(S_{1}-S_{1-2^{-k+1}})-(S_{1-t}-S_{1-2^{-k+1}})|\vee|(S_{1}-S_{1-2^{-k+1}})-(I_{1-t}-S_{1-2^{-k+1}})|

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\sup_{t\in[2^{-k},2^{-k+1}]}|S_{2^{-k+1}}-S_{{2^{-k+1}}-t}|\vee|S_{2^{-k+1}}-I_{{2^{-k+1}}-t}|

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}2^{-2k/3}\sup_{t\in[1,2]}|S_{2}-S_{2-t}|\vee|S_{2}-I_{2-t}|

\displaystyle\leq 2^{-2k/3}\Big(|S_{2}|+\sup_{t\in[0,1]}|S_{t}|\vee|I_{t}|\Big).

Therefore by a union bound over intervals of the form

\mathbb{P}(|S_{2}|+\sup_{t\in[0,1]}|S_{t}|\vee|I_{t}|>m)\leq cm^{-1/16}

. Also, by a standard bound on Bessel processes (Lemma

), the random variable

\sup_{t\in[0,1]}|S_{t}|\vee|I_{t}|\geq a\quad\implies\quad C>a^{1/8}\text{ or }X>a^{1/4}/2,

from which the result follows.
∎

Lemma 6.10
.

Let
\mu

\mu^{k}(x\in\mathbb{R}^{k}:|x_{i}-x_{j}|\leq\epsilon\text{ for all }i\neq j)\geq\frac{\epsilon^{k-1}}{2^{3k-2}b^{k-1}}.

Proof.

First, we have

\displaystyle\mu^{k}(x\in\mathbb{R}^{k}:|x_{i}-x_{j}|\leq\epsilon\text{ for all }i\neq j)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}\cap[-{\left\lfloor b/\epsilon\right\rfloor}-1,{\left\lfloor b/\epsilon\right\rfloor}]}\left(\mu[i\epsilon,(i+1)\epsilon)\right)^{k}.

We can recognize the right hand side above as the

-norm on

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}\cap[-{\left\lfloor b/\epsilon\right\rfloor}-1,{\left\lfloor b/\epsilon\right\rfloor}]}\left(\mu[i\epsilon,(i+1)\epsilon)\right)^{k}\geq\frac{\left(\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}\cap[-{\left\lfloor b/\epsilon\right\rfloor}-1,{\left\lfloor b/\epsilon\right\rfloor}]}\mu[i\epsilon,(i+1)\epsilon)\right)^{k}}{(2{\left\lfloor b/\epsilon\right\rfloor}+2)^{k-1}}

which gives the result after simplification, using that

Proof of Lemma

We move on to the lower bound. Fix

7.1.
A Fredholm determinant formula

To prove Proposition
6.4
we will use a Fredholm determinant formula for the KPZ fixed point from
[
26
]
. We introduce only the minimum background that the reader will need to understand our manipulations. The interested reader should refer to
[
26
,
31
]
and references therein for more background.

To set up the formula, let
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
be the space of square integrable functions
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}
. If
K
is an operator on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
acting through its kernel
(Kf)(x)=\int f(y)K(x,y)dy
, its
Fredholm determinant
is given by

\det(I+K)=1+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!}\int\det[K(x_{i},x_{j})]_{i,j=1}^{n}dx_{1}\dots dx_{n}.

This Fredholm determinant is well-defined and finite if
K
has finite trace norm
\|K\|_{1}=\operatorname{tr}(\sqrt{K^{*}K})
, where
\sqrt{K^{*}K}
is the unique positive square root of the operator
K^{*}K
. If
\|K\|_{1}<\infty
, we say that
K
is trace class. We note for later use the standard estimates

(40)

\displaystyle\|ABC\|_{1}\leq\|A\|_{\operatorname{op}}\|C\|_{\operatorname{op}}\|B\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\|A\|_{1}\|B\|_{1}\|C\|_{1},

(41)

\displaystyle|\det(I+A)-\det(I+B)|

\displaystyle\leq\|A-B\|_{1}e^{1+\|A\|_{1}+\|B\|_{1}},

see the first few sections of
[
36
, Chapter 1]
for the first inequality and
[
36
, Theorem 3.4]
for the second one.

Now, define
\operatorname{UC}
to be the set of upper semicontinuous functions
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty\}
that are not identically equal to
-\infty
and for all
x
satisfy
f(x)\leq\alpha+\gamma|x|
for some constants
\alpha,\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
. Let
\operatorname{LC}=\{f:-f\in\operatorname{UC}\}
. For any
f\in\operatorname{UC},g\in\operatorname{LC},
and
t>0
there are trace class
hypo
and
epi
operators
\mathbf{K}^{\operatorname{hypo}(f)}_{t}
and
\mathbf{K}^{\operatorname{epi}(g)}_{-t}
on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})

such that

(42)

\mathbb{P}(\mathfrak{h}_{t}(y;f)\leq g(y)\text{ for all }y\in\mathbb{R})=\det\left(I-\mathbf{K}^{\operatorname{hypo}(f)}_{t/2}\mathbf{K}^{\operatorname{epi}(g)}_{-t/2}\right).

Note that the product
\mathbf{K}^{\operatorname{hypo}(f)}_{t/2}\mathbf{K}^{\operatorname{epi}(g)}_{-t/2}
is trace class by (
40
) so the Fredholm determinant is well-defined. The hypo and epi operators are related through the formula

(43)

\mathbf{K}^{\operatorname{epi}(-f)}_{-t}=\rho\mathbf{K}^{\operatorname{hypo}(f)}_{t}\rho,

where
\rho
is the reflection operator on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
given by
\rho f(x):=f(-x)
(see
[
26
, Proposition 4.4]
, so to describe (
42
) explicitly we just need to describe
\mathbf{K}^{\operatorname{hypo}(f)}_{t}
.
We give a definition of the operator
\mathbf{K}^{\operatorname{hypo}(f)}_{t}
that matches that of
[
11
, Section 2]
, which differs from the presentation in
[
26
]
by a conjugation that does not affect the determinant.
For
f\in\operatorname{UC}
and
t>0
the operator
\mathbf{K}^{\operatorname{hypo}(f)}_{t}
is a trace class limit of a product of operators:

(44)

\mathbf{K}^{\operatorname{hypo}(f)}_{t}=\lim_{\ell_{1}\to-\infty,\ell_{2}\to\infty}\Gamma_{t}(\mathbf{S}_{t,\ell_{1}})^{*}\mathbf{P}_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}}^{\operatorname{Hit}f}\mathbf{S}_{t,-\ell_{2}}\Gamma_{t}.

It remains to define the operators on the right side above. The operator
\Gamma_{t}
is multiplication by the function

\Gamma_{t}(z)=\exp(\kappa_{t}\operatorname{sgn}(z)|z|^{3/2}),

where
\kappa_{t}>0
is any sufficiently small constant. Next, for
t>0
and
x\in\mathbb{R}
let
\mathbf{S}_{t,x}
be the integral operator with kernel
\mathbf{S}_{t,x}(z,y)=\mathbf{S}_{t,x}(z-y)
, where

\mathbf{S}_{t,x}(z)=t^{-1/3}\exp(\tfrac{2x^{3}}{3t^{2}}-\tfrac{zx}{t})\operatorname{Ai}(-t^{1/3}z+t^{-4/3}x^{2}),

and
\operatorname{Ai}(z)
is the classical Airy function. Finally, for
\ell_{1}<\ell_{2}
and
f\in\operatorname{UC}
we define

\mathbf{P}_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}}^{\operatorname{Hit}(f)}=I-\mathbf{P}_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}}^{\operatorname{No\;hit}(f)},

where
\mathbf{P}_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}}^{\operatorname{No\;hit}(f)}
is an integral operator with kernel

\mathbf{P}_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}}^{\operatorname{No\;hit}(f)}(u_{1},u_{2})=\mathbb{P}_{B(\ell_{1})=u_{1},B(\ell_{2})=u_{2}}(B(y)>f(y)\text{ for }y\in[\ell_{1},\ell_{2}])\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi(\ell_{2}-\ell_{1})}}e^{-\tfrac{(u_{2}-u_{1})^{2}}{4(\ell_{2}-\ell_{1})}}.

Here the probability above denotes the probability that a Brownian bridge (of variance

starting at

and finishing at

stays above the function
f
. Noting the Gaussian factor, the whole expression above can be equivalently thought of as a transition density for Brownian motion from
(u_{1},\ell_{1})
to
(u_{2},\ell_{2})
, killed if it goes below

. While the limit definition (

is

, the expression under the limit in (

, see the discussion after (4.1) in
[
26
]
.

Now, by (

F_{M}(a)=\det(1-\mathbf{K}^{\operatorname{hypo}(h_{0})}_{1/2}\mathbf{K}^{\operatorname{epi}(g_{a})}_{-1/2})
, where

\infty,\qquad\text{ else.}\end{cases}

and some trace class estimates from

allow us to reduce Proposition

\|\mathbf{K}^{\operatorname{hypo}(-g_{b})}_{1/2}-\mathbf{K}^{\operatorname{hypo}(-g_{a})}_{1/2}\|_{1}\leq c^{\prime}(b-a)e^{c^{\prime}(a^{-})^{3/2}}.

Proof of Lemma
7.1
.

The method here is the same as in
[
26
, Appendix A.1]
. There are only superficial differences in the required calculations.
Let
\mathbf{O}_{a,b}:=\mathbf{K}^{\operatorname{hypo}(-g_{a})}_{1/2}-\mathbf{K}^{\operatorname{hypo}(-g_{b})}_{1/2}
. We have

\mathbf{O}_{a,b}=\Gamma_{1/2}(\mathbf{S}_{1/2,-1})^{*}\mathbf{P}_{a,b}\mathbf{S}_{1/2,-1}\Gamma_{1/2}

where

\mathbf{P}_{a,b}=\mathbf{P}_{-1,1}^{\operatorname{No\;hit}(-g_{b})}-\mathbf{P}_{-1,1}^{\operatorname{No\;hit}(-g_{a})}.

Since
g_{a},g_{b}
are constant on
[-1,1]
, the kernel of
\mathbf{P}_{a,b}
can be computed explicitly by the reflection principle:

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{a,b}(w,v)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{1}(w\wedge v>-a)\frac{1}{\sqrt{8\pi}}\left(e^{-(w+v+2a)^{2}/8}-e^{-(w+v+2b)^{2}/8}\right)

\displaystyle+\mathbf{1}(w\wedge v\in(-b,-a])\frac{1}{\sqrt{8\pi}}\left(e^{-(w-v)^{2}/8}-e^{-(w+v+2b)^{2}/8}\right).

Now, the key idea is to view
\mathbf{O}_{a,b}
as an integral of rank one operators integrated against the positive measure
\mathbf{P}_{a,b}(w,v)dwdv
. Indeed, we can write out the kernel of
\mathbf{O}_{a,b}
as:

\mathbf{O}_{a,b}(t,u)=\int\Gamma_{1/2}(t)\mathbf{S}_{1/2,-1}(w,t)\Gamma_{1/2}(u)\mathbf{S}_{1/2,-1}(v,u)\mathbf{P}_{a,b}(w,v)dwdv.

Let
\mathbf{R}_{w,v}
be the operator with kernel

\mathbf{R}_{w,v}(t,u):=R_{w}(t)R_{v}(u),\quad\text{ where }\quad R_{w}(t)=\Gamma(t)\mathbf{S}_{1/2,-1}(w,t).

We can then upper bound
\|\mathbf{O}_{a,b}\|_{1}
by an integral of the trace norms of
\mathbf{R}_{w,v}
:

(47)

\|\mathbf{O}_{a,b}\|_{1}\leq\int\|\mathbf{R}_{w,v}\|_{1}\mathbf{P}_{a,b}(w,v)dwdv=\int\|R_{w}\|_{2}\|R_{v}\|_{2}\mathbf{P}_{a,b}(w,v)dwdv.

For the second equality we have used that
\mathbf{R}_{v,w}
is rank one, and hence its trace norm is simply the product of the
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
-norms of the functions
R_{v},R_{w}
. At this point it just remains to estimate the final integral. In the remainder of the proof the constant
c>0
depends only on
\kappa_{1/2}
and may change from line to line.

To bound
\|R_{w}\|_{2}
we will use the standard Airy function bound

\operatorname{Ai}(z)\leq c\exp(-\frac{2}{3}(z^{+})^{3/2})
, see
[
1
]
, formulas 10.4.59–10.4.60. This yields the bound

\displaystyle\|R_{w}\|^{2}_{2}

\displaystyle\leq c\int_{\mathbb{R}}\exp(2\operatorname{sgn}(t)\kappa_{1/2}|t|^{3/2}-4(t-w)-\frac{2\sqrt{2}}{3}((t-w)^{+})^{3/2})dt.

As long as
\kappa_{1/2}<\sqrt{2}/3
, this gives the bound

\|R_{w}\|^{2}_{2}\leq c\exp(2w+c(w^{+})^{3/2}).

We can now turn to bounding the right side of (
47
). We first look at at the portion of the integral where
w\wedge v\in(-b,-a]
. Using the fact that the integrand is symmetric in
w
and
v
and making the change of variables
w\mapsto x=w-v
, we can write

\displaystyle\int_{w\wedge v\in(-b,-a)}\|R_{w}\|_{2}\|R_{v}\|_{2}\mathbf{P}_{a,b}(w,v)dwdv=2\int_{-b}^{-a}\int_{0}^{\infty}\|R_{x+v}\|_{2}\|R_{v}\|_{2}\mathbf{P}_{a,b}(x+v,v)dxdv.

Using the bound on
\|R_{w}\|_{2}
and the fact that
\mathbf{P}_{a,b}(x+v,v)\leq e^{-x^{2}/8}
in the domain of integration, this is bounded above by

\displaystyle c\int_{-b}^{-a}\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp(2x+4v+c(v^{+})^{3/2}+

\displaystyle c((x+v)^{+})^{3/2}-x^{2}/8)dxdv

\displaystyle\leq c(b-a)\exp(c(a^{-})^{3/2}).

To complete the proof of the lemma, it just remains to prove the same bound on the portion of the integral where
w\wedge v>-a
. In this region we use that

\mathbf{P}_{a,b}(w,v)\leq c(b-a)e^{-(w+v+2a)^{2}/9},

which follows from a straightforward bound on the derivative of
e^{-(u_{1}+u_{2}+2a)^{2}/8}
with respect to
a
. Then using the bound on
\|R_{w}\|_{2}
and the change of variables
x=w+a,y=v+a
we have

\displaystyle\int_{-a}^{\infty}\int_{-a}^{\infty}\|R_{w}\|_{2}\|R_{v}\|_{2}\mathbf{P}_{a,b}(w,v)dwdv

\displaystyle\leq c(b-a)\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp\Big(2(x+y)-4a+c((x-a)^{+})^{3/2}+c((y-a)^{+})^{3/2}-(x+y)^{2}/9\Big)dxdy

\displaystyle\leq c(b-a)\exp(c(a^{-})^{3/2}),

as desired.
∎

Facts about Bessel processes

Lemma B.1
.

Let
R_{a}:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
be a Bessel-
3
process with
R_{a}(0)=a
and define the random variable

X_{a}:=\inf_{x\geq 0}R_{a}(x)-x^{1/4}-\sqrt{a}

X_{a}\wedge 0,a\geq 0
is tight.

Proof.

To prove the lemma, we will use that conditional on
R_{a}(t)
, the minimum of
R_{a}
on
[t,\infty)
is uniform on
[0,R_{a}(t)]
, see
[
35

. Combining this with the representation of
R_{a}
as the magnitude of a
3
-dimensional Brownian motion (of variance
2
) started at the point
(a,0,0)
gives that for fixed
t\geq 0
,

(50)

\min_{x\geq t}R_{a}(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}U\sqrt{(N_{1}+a)^{2}+N_{2}^{2}+N_{3}^{2}}

where

and all random variables are independent.

We first use this to show
\tilde{X}_{0}:=\inf_{x\geq 0}R_{0}(x)-2x^{1/4}\neq-\infty
almost surely. Consider the sequence of random variables

I_{n}=\min_{x\in[2^{n},\infty)}2^{-n/2}R_{0}(x),\qquad n=0,1,2,\dots

By (
50
), this is a sequence of identically distributed non-negative random variables with Lebesgue density bounded above. Therefore by a union bound,

I:=\inf_{n\geq 1}n^{2}I_{n}

is non-zero almost surely, and hence so is the random variable
\inf_{x\geq 1}\log^{2}(x)x^{-1/2}R_{0}(x)
. Noting also that

gives that

Now, for

Lemma B.2
.

Let
R:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
be a Bessel-
3
process started at
0
, and let
X_{0}=\inf_{x\geq 0}R(x)-x^{1/4}
. Then for some
c>0
we have

\mathbb{P}(X_{0}<-m)\leq cm^{-1/4}.

Proof.

Using the notation from the previous proof, by a union bound we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X_{0}<-m)\leq\sum_{n={\left\lfloor\log_{2}m\right\rfloor}}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(\min_{y\in[2^{n},2^{n+1}]}R_{0}(x)-2^{(n+1)/4}<0)\leq\sum_{n={\left\lfloor\log_{2}m\right\rfloor}}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(2^{n/2}I_{n}<2^{(n+1)/4}).

This is bounded above by
cm^{-1/4}
since the
I_{n}
are identically distributed with Lebesgue density bounded above by (
50
).
∎
