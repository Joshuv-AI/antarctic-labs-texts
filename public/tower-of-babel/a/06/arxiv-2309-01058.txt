Abstract
. This paper offers an extensive exploration of nonradiating sources for the two- and three-dimensional biharmonic wave equations. Various equivalent characterizations are derived to reveal the nature of a nonradiating source. Additionally, we establish the connection between nonradiating sources in the biharmonic wave equation and those in the Helmholtz equation as well as the modified Helmholtz equation. Several illustrative examples are explicitly constructed to showcase the existence of nonradiating sources. One significant implication of the existence of nonradiating sources is that it undermines the uniqueness of the inverse source problem when utilizing boundary data at a fixed frequency.

1.
Introduction

In the study of wave propagation, two distinct types of wave sources come to light: radiating and nonradiating. Radiating sources emit waves that disperse into free space and can travel indefinitely. In contrast, nonradiating sources generate waves within a specific medium, which remain confined to a particular region in space without extending into free space. The exploration of nonradiating sources holds considerable importance across multiple domains of physics and engineering, such as geological exploration, electromagnetic detection, and stealth technology. Moreover, it plays an important role in tackling challenges posed by the inverse source problem.

Extensive research efforts have been devoted to exploring the mathematical and computational aspects of the inverse source problem concerning acoustic, electromagnetic, and elastic wave phenomena. From a mathematical perspective, the matter of uniqueness represents a substantial challenge when addressing inverse source problems. It is essential to emphasize that the inverse source problem at a specific frequency is inherently ill-posed, primarily due to the existence of nonradiating sources. Consequently, attempting to deduce the wave source uniquely based solely on a single far-field pattern or boundary data at a fixed frequency becomes an infeasible endeavor
[
11
]
. The issue of nonuniqueness was explored in
[
6
]
with regard to the inverse source problem in the fields of acoustics and electromagnetics. This work notably introduced the concept of nonradiating sources. The work presented in
[
1
]
addressed the inverse source problem associated with Maxwell’s equations. The study demonstrated that the inverse problem of determining a volume current density from surface measurements lacks a unique solution. This nonuniqueness in solutions was clarified through the variational approach. In
[
7
,
8
]
, nonradiating sources were investigated under a fixed frequency condition for both the acoustic and elastic wave equations, utilizing the enclosure method. The findings of the study established that nonradiating sources exhibiting a convex or non-convex corner or edge along their boundary are required to undergo vanishing behavior in those regions. In order to address the challenge posed by nonuniqueness, the work presented in
[
3
]
provides a comprehensive review of the subject matter concerning inverse scattering problems, centering on the utilization of multi-frequency boundary measurements as a strategic approach. Moreover, originating from the work in

and the references cited therein.

The examination of scattering problems associated with biharmonic waves has attracted substantial interest, largely attributed to their essential applications in the field of thin plate elasticity
[

Nevertheless, it is worth highlighting that the inverse source problems for biharmonic wave equations demonstrate limited findings in contrast to the more extensively studied acoustic, elastic, and electromagnetic wave equations. This limitation arises due to the complex nature of solutions inherent to biharmonic equations. As a result, certain methodologies that have proven effective for second-order wave equations are rendered inadequate in this context

For the latest advancements within this research domain, we direct attention to

Inspired by the research conducted by
[
6

, the present paper aims to explore the detailed characterizations of nonradiating sources for the biharmonic wave equation in both two and three dimensions. We establish a set of necessary and sufficient conditions that determine the nonradiating characteristic of a source. This is achieved by employing the integral representation of the field and expanding the fundamental solution in special functions. Additionally, we deduce a fundamental identity that establishes a direct connection between the integral transform of the source distribution and the ensuing near-field data. This outcome provides us with the capability to extract the integral transform of the source distribution by utilizing near-field data acquired from the boundary. Moreover, we conduct an extensive discussion concerning the integral equation inherent to the problem and examine the nonuniqueness associated with the inverse source problem. Our investigation reveals the presence of a nontrivial null space within the integral operator, thereby illuminating the ill-posed nature of the inverse source problem. This result offers invaluable insights for future research endeavors and practical applications. Finally, we construct several illustrative examples explicitly to demonstrate the existence of nonradiating sources.

2.
The biharmonic wave equation

The biharmonic wave equation characterizes wave behavior by considering both the curvature and deflection of the wavefront. Mathematically, the time-harmonic biharmonic wave equation can be expressed as

\displaystyle\Delta^{2}u-\kappa^{4}u=-f\quad\text{in}~\mathbb{R}^{d},

(2.1)

where the spatial dimension is denoted as
d=2
or
d=3
, the operator
\Delta^{2}
stands for the biharmonic operator, capturing effects of curvature and deformation, the scalar function
u
describes the displacement amplitude of the wave, the parameter
\kappa>0
denotes the wavenumber, related to oscillation frequency and wave propagation speed, the term
f
represents the source function, which is assumed to have a compact support
D
contained in the ball
B_{R}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:|x|<R\}
. To ensure the well-posedness of the problem, the Sommerfeld radiation condition is imposed on both the wave field
u
and its Laplacian
\Delta u
(cf.
[
24
]
):

\displaystyle\lim_{r\to\infty}r^{\frac{d-1}{2}}(\partial_{r}u-{\rm i}\kappa u)=0,\quad\quad\lim_{r\to\infty}r^{\frac{d-1}{2}}(\partial_{r}\Delta u-{\rm i}\kappa\Delta u)=0,\quad\quad r=|x|,

(2.2)

uniformly in all directions
{\hat{x}}=x/|x|
.

The analysis of far-field patterns has received significant emphasis in the field of inverse scattering theory. Considering a solution
u
of (
2.1
)–(
2.2
), it has the asymptotic behavior

\displaystyle u(x)=-\frac{\mu_{d}}{8\kappa^{2}}\frac{e^{{\rm i}\kappa|x|}}{(\pi|x|)^{\frac{d-1}{2}}}\bigg[u_{\infty}({\hat{x}})+O\left(\frac{1}{|x|}\right)\bigg],\quad\ |x|\to\infty,

where
u_{\infty}
is called the far-field pattern of the wave field
u
and

\mu_{d}=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\left(\frac{2}{\kappa}\right)^{1/2}e^{{\rm i}\frac{\pi}{4}}&\quad\text{if }~d=2,\\
&1&\quad\text{if }~d=3.\end{aligned}\right.

Let
G(x,y)
be the Green’s function of the biharmonic wave equation, which satisfies

\displaystyle\Delta^{2}G(x,y)-\kappa^{4}G(x,y)=-\delta(x-y),\quad x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d},~x\neq y.

(2.3)

It can be verified that
G(x,y)
is given by

\displaystyle G(x,y)=-\frac{1}{2\kappa^{2}}\left(\Phi_{\rm H}(x,y)-\Phi_{\rm M}(x,y)\right),

(2.4)

where
\Phi_{\rm H}(x,y)
and
\Phi_{\rm M}(x,y)
represent the Green’s functions corresponding to the Helmholtz equation and the modified Helmholtz equation, respectively, and they satisfy

\Delta\Phi_{\rm H}(x,y)+\kappa^{2}\Phi_{\rm H}(x,y)=-\delta(x-y),\quad\Delta\Phi_{\rm M}(x,y)-\kappa^{2}\Phi_{\rm M}(x,y)=-\delta(x-y).

(2.5)

Using (
2.1
) and (
2.3
), and noting that the source has a compact support
D
contained in
B_{R}
, we obtain that the solution to (
2.1
)–(
2.2
) can be written as

\displaystyle u(x)=\int_{B_{R}}G(x,y)f(y){\rm d}y,\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(2.6)

Nonradiating sources, referring to sources where the field remains consistently zero beyond a finite region, have a strong association with the nonuniqueness issues encountered in the inverse source problems. The inverse source problem aims to determine the source function
f
by utilizing measurements associated with the wave field
u
on the boundary

\partial B_{R}
. Moving forward, our focus is directed towards the introduction and subsequent examination of nonradiating sources for the biharmonic wave equation.

Let us present a formal definition of a nonradiating source in the context of the biharmonic wave equation.

Definition 2.1
.

Consider a source denoted as
f
, having a compact support designated as
D
, which is entirely confined within the ball
B_{R}
. A source is classified as nonradiating if the solution
u
to equation (
2.6
) is identically zero outside of the ball
B_{R}
.

In the following two sections, we examine the two- and three-dimensional problems, respectively. In each scenario, we provide distinct yet equivalent characterizations of nonradiating sources. We then proceed to investigate these characterizations utilizing near-field data, which is followed by a discussion of the nonuniqueness inherent in the inverse source problem. Our analysis relies on the utilization of expansions involving Bessel functions of various types within the framework of the fundamental solutions for the Helmholtz and modified Helmholtz equations. This method finds its roots in the work conducted by
[
6
]
, where it was employed to analyze nonradiating sources within the context of the three-dimensional Helmholtz equation.

3.
The two-dimensional problem

In this section, we discuss different characterizations of nonradiating sources for the two-dimensional biharmonic wave equation.

3.1.
Characterizations of nonradiating sources

In two dimensions, the explicit expressions for the Green’s functions corresponding to the Helmholtz and modified Helmholtz equations, as defined in (
2.5
), are given by

\displaystyle\Phi_{\rm H}(x,y)=\frac{\rm i}{4}H_{0}^{(1)}(\kappa|x-y|),\quad\Phi_{\rm M}(x,y)=\frac{\rm i}{4}H_{0}^{(1)}({\rm i}\kappa|x-y|),

where
H_{0}^{(1)}
is the Hankel function of the first kind with order
0
.

Define a pair of auxiliary functions

\displaystyle f_{\rm H}(x)=-\int_{B_{R}}\Phi_{\rm H}(x,y)f(y){\rm d}y,\quad f_{\rm M}(x)=-\int_{B_{R}}\Phi_{\rm M}(x,y)f(y){\rm d}y,\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}.

(3.1)

It is clear to note that
f_{\rm H}
and
f_{\rm M}
are the radiation solutions to the Helmholtz and modified Helmholtz equations, respectively, i.e., they satisfy

\Delta f_{\rm H}+\kappa^{2}f_{\rm H}=f,\quad\Delta f_{\rm M}-\kappa^{2}f_{\rm M}=f.

Furthermore, both solutions satisfy the Sommerfeld radiation condition.

Combining (
2.4
), (
2.6
), and (
3.1
), we obtain

\displaystyle u(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{B_{R}}G(x,y)f(y){\rm d}y=-\frac{1}{2\kappa^{2}}\int_{B_{R}}\big(\Phi_{\rm H}(x,y)-\Phi_{\rm M}(x,y)\big)f(y){\rm d}y

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\kappa^{2}}\big(f_{\rm H}(x)-f_{\rm M}(x)\big).

(3.2)

First, we establish the connection between a nonradiating source associated with the biharmonic wave equation and a nonradiating source related to both the Helmholtz equation and the modified Helmholtz equation through the subsequent theorem.

Theorem 3.1
.

The source
f
is nonradiating for the biharmonic wave equation if and only if
f
is nonradiating for both the Helmholtz equation and the modified Helmholtz equation, i.e.,
f_{\rm H}
and
f_{\rm M}
defined in (
3.1
) are identically zero outside of the ball
B_{R}
.

Proof.

If
f
is nonradiating for both the Helmholtz equation and the modified Helmholtz equation, then we have from (
3.1
) that
f_{\rm H}(x)=f_{\rm M}(x)=0
for
|x|>R
. It follows from (
3.1
) that
u(x)=0
for
|x|>R
, which shows that
f
is radiating for the biharmonic wave equation.

Conversely, if the source
f
is nonradiating concerning the biharmonic wave equation, then based on (
3.1
), it can be deduced that
u(x)=0
for
|x|>R
. Upon considering (
3.1
), two scenarios emerge: (1) where
f_{\rm H}(x)
and
f_{\rm M}(x)
are both non-zero for
|x|>R
, yet satisfy
f_{\rm H}(x)=f_{\rm M}(x)
for all
x
satisfying
|x|>R
; and (2) where
f_{\rm H}(x)=f_{\rm M}(x)=0
for all
|x|>R
. We demonstrate that the former scenario is impossible, while the latter scenario indicates that
f
is nonradiating in relation to both the Helmholtz equation and the modified Helmholtz equation.

When
f_{\rm H}(x)\neq 0,f_{\rm M}(x)\neq 0
for
|x|>R
, it is clear to note that the source
f(x)
does not qualify as a nonradiating source for either the Helmholtz equation or the modified Helmholtz equation. By the asymptotic expansion

\displaystyle|x-y|=|x|-{\hat{x}}\cdot y+O\left(\frac{1}{|{x}|}\right),\quad|x|>|y|,~|x|\to\infty,

(3.3)

it can be verified for
|x|>|y|,|x|\to\infty
that the Green’s functions
\Phi_{\rm H}
and
\Phi_{\rm M}
admit the asymptotic expansions

\displaystyle\Phi_{\rm H}(x,y)=\frac{1}{4}\left(\frac{2}{\pi\kappa|{x}|}\right)^{1/2}e^{{\rm i}(\kappa|x|+\frac{\pi}{4})}\bigg[e^{-{\rm i}\kappa{\hat{x}}\cdot y}+O\left(\frac{1}{|{x}|}\right)\bigg]

(3.4)

and

\displaystyle\Phi_{\rm M}(x,y)=\frac{1}{4}\left(\frac{2}{{\rm i}\pi\kappa|{x}|}\right)^{1/2}e^{(-\kappa|x|+{\rm i}\frac{\pi}{4})}~\bigg[e^{\kappa{\hat{x}}\cdot y}+O\left(\frac{1}{|{x}|}\right)\bigg].

(3.5)

Hence, we have

\displaystyle G(x,y)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{8\kappa^{2}}e^{{\rm i}(\kappa|x|+\frac{\pi}{4})}\left(\frac{2}{\pi\kappa|{x}|}\right)^{1/2}\bigg[e^{-{\rm i}\kappa{\hat{x}}\cdot y}+O\left(\frac{1}{|{x}|}\right)\bigg],\quad\ |x|>|y|,~|x|\to\infty.

(3.6)

Combining (
3.1
) and (
3.6
) yields

\displaystyle u(x)=-\frac{1}{8\kappa^{2}}e^{{\rm i}(\kappa|x|+\frac{\pi}{4})}\left(\frac{2}{\pi\kappa|{x}|}\right)^{1/2}\bigg[\int_{B_{R}}e^{-{\rm i}\kappa{\hat{x}}\cdot y}f(y){\rm d}y+O\left(\frac{1}{|{x}|}\right)\bigg],\quad|x|\to\infty,

which implies that
u(x)\not\equiv 0
for
|x|>R
. Consequently, the first scenario is thereby eliminated, leading to the conclusion of the proof.
∎

In order to gain a more comprehensive understanding of the nonradiating source, we undertake an additional analysis, exploring how its reliance on the projection of specific coefficients influences the nonradiating source.

Let
H_{n}^{(1)}=J_{n}+{\rm i}Y_{n}
be the Hankel function of the first kind with order
n
, where
J_{n}
and
Y_{n}
are the Bessel and Neumann functions of order
n
, respectively. When
|x|>|y|
, we utilize Graf’s addition theorem (cf.
[
10
,
(3.65)
]
) to express the functions
\Phi_{\rm H}(x,y)
and
\Phi_{\rm M}(x,y)
as a combination of multipolar sources centered at the origin:

\displaystyle\Phi_{\rm H}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\frac{\rm i}{4}H_{0}^{(1)}(\kappa|x-y|)=\frac{\rm i}{4}\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}H_{n}^{(1)}(\kappa|x|)e^{{\rm i}n{\rm arg}(x)}J_{n}(\kappa|y|)e^{-{\rm i}n{\rm arg}(y)},

(3.7)

\displaystyle\Phi_{\rm M}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\frac{\rm i}{4}H_{0}^{(1)}({\rm i}\kappa|x-y|)=\frac{\rm i}{4}\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}H_{n}^{(1)}({\rm i}\kappa|x|)e^{{\rm i}n{\rm arg}(x)}J_{n}({\rm i}\kappa|y|)e^{-{\rm i}n{\rm arg}(y)},

(3.8)

where
{\rm arg}(x)
represents the counterclockwise oriented angle formed by the vector
(1,0)
and the vector
x
.
It is noteworthy to mention that in (
3.8
), the Hankel function
H_{n}^{(1)}
and the Bessel function
J_{n}
with purely imaginary arguments can be substituted with the modified Hankel functions
K_{n}
and
I_{n}
.

Assume
f\in L^{2}(B_{R})
and let

f_{n}(r)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}f(x)e^{-{\rm i}n\theta}{\rm d}\theta.

It is evident that the coefficients
f_{n}
represent the Fourier coefficients of the function
f
in the following expansion:

f(x)=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}f_{n}(r)e^{{\rm i}n\theta},\quad|x|<R.

By employing the Fourier coefficients
f_{n}
, we define the parameters
\alpha_{n}
and
\beta_{n}
as follows:

\alpha_{n}=\int_{0}^{R}f_{n}(r)J_{n}(\kappa r)r{\rm d}r,\quad\beta_{n}=\int_{0}^{R}f_{n}(r)J_{n}({\rm i}\kappa r)r{\rm d}r.

(3.9)

Presented below is an alternative characterization of nonradiating sources.

Theorem 3.2
.

Assume that
f\in L^{2}(B_{R})
. Then the source
f
is nonradiating if and only if
\alpha_{n}=\beta_{n}=0
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Proof.

Combining (
3.1
) and (
3.7
), we obtain

\displaystyle f_{\rm H}(x)

\displaystyle=-\int_{B_{R}}\Phi_{\rm H}(x,y)f(y){\rm d}y=-\frac{\rm i}{4}\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}H_{n}^{(1)}(\kappa|x|)e^{{\rm i}n{\rm arg}(x)}\int_{B_{R}}f(y)J_{n}(\kappa|y|)e^{-{\rm i}n{\rm arg}(y)}{\rm d}y

\displaystyle=-\frac{\rm i}{4}\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}H_{n}^{(1)}(\kappa|x|)e^{{\rm i}n{\rm arg}(x)}\int_{0}^{R}\int_{0}^{2\pi}f(y)J_{n}(\kappa r)e^{-{\rm i}n\theta}r{\rm d}\theta{\rm d}r

\displaystyle=-\frac{{\rm i}\pi}{2}\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\alpha_{n}H_{n}^{(1)}(\kappa|x|)e^{{\rm i}n{\rm arg}(x)},\quad|x|>R.

(3.10)

Similarly, we have from (
3.1
) and (
3.8
) that

\displaystyle f_{\rm M}(x)

\displaystyle=-\int_{B_{R}}\Phi_{\rm M}(x,y)f(y){\rm d}y=-\frac{\rm i}{4}\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}H_{n}^{(1)}({\rm i}\kappa|x|)e^{{\rm i}n{\rm arg}(x)}\int_{B_{R}}f(y)J_{n}({\rm i}\kappa|y|)e^{-{\rm i}n{\rm arg}(y)}{\rm d}y

\displaystyle=-\frac{\rm i}{4}\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}H_{n}^{(1)}({\rm i}\kappa|x|)e^{{\rm i}n{\rm arg}(x)}\int_{0}^{R}\int_{0}^{2\pi}f(y)J_{n}({\rm i}\kappa r)e^{-{\rm i}n\theta}r{\rm d}\theta{\rm d}r

\displaystyle=-\frac{{\rm i}\pi}{2}\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\beta_{n}H_{n}^{(1)}({\rm i}\kappa|x|)e^{{\rm i}n{\rm arg}(x)},\quad|x|>R.

(3.11)

The proof is completed by noting Theorem
3.1
in conjunction with (
3.1
)–(
3.1
).
∎

If
f\in L^{2}(B_{R})
, we can deduce from
[
10
, Theorem 8.2]
and (
3.1
) that both
f_{\rm H}
and
f_{\rm M}
belong to the space
H^{2}_{\rm loc}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
. Consequently, the traces of the functions
f_{\rm H}|_{\partial B_{R}}
and
f_{\rm M}|_{\partial B_{R}}
can be found in the space
H^{\frac{3}{2}}(\partial B_{R})
, and their respective normal derivatives,
\partial_{\nu}f_{\rm H}|_{\partial B_{R}}
and
\partial_{\nu}f_{\rm M}|_{\partial B_{R}}
, are contained in
H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\partial B_{R})
.

Proposition 3.3
.

Assume that
f\in L^{2}(B_{R})
. Then
f_{\rm M}(x)|_{\partial B_{R}}=0
if and only if
\beta_{n}=0
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Proof.

Given that the source
f
has a compact support
D
confined within
B_{R}
, the function
f_{\rm M}
satisfies the equation
\Delta f_{\rm M}-\kappa^{2}f_{\rm M}=0
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\overline{B_{R}}
. By (
3.1
) and (
3.5
), it becomes evident that
f_{\rm M}
satisfies the Sommerfeld radiation condition. With the information that
f_{\rm M}(x)|_{\partial B_{R}}=0
and considering the Sommerfeld radiation condition, we conclude that
f_{\rm M}(x)=0
for
|x|>R
. It follows from (
3.1
) that
\beta_{n}=0
holds true for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Conversely, based on the fact that
\beta_{n}=0
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
and (
3.1
), it can be deduced that
f_{\rm M}(x)=0
when
|x|>R
, which, with the information that
f_{\rm M}|_{\partial B_{R}}\in H^{\frac{3}{2}}(\partial B_{R})
, leads us to the conclusion that

f_{\rm M}|_{\partial B_{R}}=0
.
∎

Similarly, the following proposition can be demonstrated.

Proposition 3.4
.

Assume that
f(x)\in L^{2}(B_{R})
. Then
f_{\rm H}(x)|_{\partial B_{R}}=0
if and only if
\alpha_{n}=0
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
.

By combining Theorem
3.2
with Propositions
3.3
and
3.4
, we formulate an equivalent characterization of nonradiating sources.

Corollary 3.5
.

The source
f\in L^{2}(B_{R})
is nonradiating if and only if
f_{\rm H}(x)|_{\partial B_{R}}=f_{\rm M}(x)|_{\partial B_{R}}=0
.

Subsequently, we move forward to establish an alternative characterization of nonradiating sources from the perspective of integral transforms.

First, we consider the Fourier transform of the source, i.e.,

\displaystyle\hat{f}(\xi)=\int_{B_{R}}f(x)e^{-{\rm i}\xi\cdot x}{\rm d}x,

(3.12)

where
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
is the spatial frequency. Recall the Jacobi–Anger expansion for the plane wave (cf.
[
10
,
(3.89)
]
):

\displaystyle e^{{\rm i}\kappa{x}\cdot{d}}=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{+\infty}{\rm i}^{n}e^{-{\rm i}n{\rm arg}(d)}J_{n}(\kappa|x|)e^{{\rm i}n{\rm arg}(x)},\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{2},

(3.13)

where
d\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
is the unit vector of propagation direction.

Using (
3.13
), we have from a simple calculation that

\displaystyle e^{-{\rm i}{\xi}\cdot{x}}=\overline{e^{{\rm i}{\xi}\cdot{x}}}=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{+\infty}(-{\rm i})^{n}e^{{\rm i}n{\rm arg}(\hat{\xi})}J_{n}(|{\xi}||x|)e^{-{\rm i}n{\rm arg}(x)},

(3.14)

where
\hat{\xi}={\xi}/|{\xi}|
. Substituting (
3.14
) into (
3.12
) yields

\displaystyle\hat{f}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\int_{B_{R}}f(x)\bigg(\sum_{n=-\infty}^{+\infty}(-{\rm i})^{n}e^{{\rm i}n{\rm arg}(\hat{\xi})}J_{n}(|{\xi}||x|)e^{-{\rm i}n{\rm arg}(x)}\bigg){\rm d}x

\displaystyle=2\pi\sum_{n=-\infty}^{+\infty}(-{\rm i})^{n}e^{{\rm i}n{\rm arg}(\hat{\xi})}\int_{0}^{R}J_{n}(|{\xi}|r)r\bigg(\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}f(x)e^{-{\rm i}n\theta}{\rm d}\theta\bigg){\rm d}r

\displaystyle=2\pi\sum_{n=-\infty}^{+\infty}(-{\rm i})^{n}e^{{\rm i}n{\rm arg}(\hat{\xi})}\int_{0}^{R}f_{n}(r)J_{n}(|{\xi}|r)r{\rm d}r,

which implies, upon employing the definition of
\alpha_{n}
in (
3.9
), that

\displaystyle\hat{f}(\xi)=2\pi\sum_{n=-\infty}^{+\infty}(-{\rm i})^{n}\alpha_{n}e^{{\rm i}n{\rm arg}(\hat{\xi})},\quad|\xi|=\kappa.

(3.15)

It follows from (
3.13
) and
[
25
, Proposition 3.1]
that

\displaystyle e^{-\kappa{x}\cdot{d}}=e^{{\rm i}({\rm i}\kappa){x}\cdot{d}}=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{+\infty}{{\rm i}}^{n}e^{-{\rm i}n{\rm arg}(d)}J_{n}({\rm i}\kappa|x|)e^{{\rm i}n{\rm arg}(x)},\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}.

Then, for
|x|=r\leq R
and
0<|s|<\kappa+K
, where
s\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
K>0
is a bounded constant, we have

\displaystyle e^{-{s}\cdot{x}}=e^{-{|x|s}\cdot{{\hat{x}}}}=e^{-{rs}\cdot{{\hat{x}}}}=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{+\infty}{{\rm i}}^{n}e^{{\rm i}n{\rm arg}(s)}J_{n}({\rm i}|s|r)e^{-{\rm i}n{\rm arg}({\hat{x}})}.

(3.16)

Define an integral transform

\displaystyle\check{f}(s)=\int_{B_{R}}f(x)e^{-s\cdot x}{\rm d}x,\quad s\in\mathbb{R}^{2},~0<|s|<\kappa+K.

(3.17)

Combining (
3.16
) and (
3.17
), we obtain

\displaystyle\check{f}(s)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{R}\int_{0}^{2\pi}rf(x)\bigg(\sum_{n=-\infty}^{+\infty}{{\rm i}}^{n}e^{{\rm i}n{\rm arg}(s)}J_{n}({\rm i}|s|r)e^{-{\rm i}n{\rm arg}({\hat{x}})}\bigg){\rm d}r{\rm d}\theta

\displaystyle=2\pi\sum_{n=-\infty}^{+\infty}{{\rm i}}^{n}e^{{\rm i}n{\rm arg}(s)}\int_{0}^{R}rJ_{n}({\rm i}|s|r)\bigg(\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}f(x)e^{-{\rm i}n\theta}{\rm d}\theta\bigg){\rm d}r

\displaystyle=2\pi\sum_{n=-\infty}^{+\infty}{{\rm i}}^{n}e^{{\rm i}n{\rm arg}(s)}\int_{0}^{R}f_{n}(r)J_{n}({\rm i}|s|r)r{\rm d}r,\quad 0<|s|<\kappa+K,

which, together with the definition of
\beta_{n}
as given in (
3.9
), results in

\displaystyle\check{f}(s)=2\pi\sum_{n=-\infty}^{+\infty}{{\rm i}}^{n}\beta_{n}e^{{\rm i}n{\rm arg}(s)},\quad|s|=\kappa.

(3.18)

Therefore, utilizing Theorem
3.2
, along with (
3.15
) and (
3.18
), we arrive at a different yet equivalent description of nonradiating sources.

Theorem 3.6
.

Assume that
f\in L^{2}(B_{R})
. Then the source
f
is nonradiating if and only if
\hat{f}(\xi)=\check{f}(s)=0
when
|\xi|=|s|=\kappa
.

3.2.
Near-field data

We proceed to derive integral identities that establish connections between the integral transform of the source function and the near-field data
u
,
\partial_{\nu}u
,
\Delta u
, and
\partial_{\nu}\Delta u
measured on the boundary
\partial B_{R}
.

By employing Green’s theorem to the functions
u
and
\Delta G
in the domain
D
, with the help of
(
2.3
), we get

\displaystyle\int_{\partial B_{R}}\big[\Delta G(x,y)\partial_{\nu}u(y)-u(y)\partial_{\nu(y)}\Delta G(x,y)\big]{\rm d}{s_{{y}}}

\displaystyle=\int_{B_{R}}\big[\Delta G(x,y)\Delta u(y)-\kappa^{4}u(y)G(x,y)\big]{\rm d}{{y}}+\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}u(x),&&x\in B_{R},\\
0,&&x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\overline{B_{R}}.\end{array}\right.

where
\nu
denotes the unit normal vector to the boundary
\partial B_{R}
, oriented outward from the interior of
B_{R}
.
Similarly, in the case of
\Delta u
and
G
, we can deduce from Green’s theorem, coupled with (
2.1
) and (
2.3
), that

\displaystyle\int_{\partial B_{R}}\big[\Delta u(y)\partial_{\nu(y)}G(x,y)-G(x,y)\partial_{\nu}\Delta u(y)\big]{\rm d}{s_{{y}}}

\displaystyle=\int_{B_{R}}\big[\Delta G(x,y)\Delta u(y)-\kappa^{4}u(y)G(x,y)\big]{\rm d}{{y}}+\int_{B_{R}}G(x,y)f(y){\rm d}{y}.

(3.21)

Subtracting (
3.2
) from (
3.2
) leads to

\displaystyle\int_{\partial B_{R}}\big[\Delta G(x,y)\partial_{\nu}u(y)-u(y)\partial_{\nu(y)}\Delta G(x,y)\big]{\rm d}{s_{{y}}}

\displaystyle-\int_{\partial B_{R}}\big[\Delta u(y)\partial_{\nu(y)}G(x,y)-G(x,y)\partial_{\nu}\Delta u(y)\big]{\rm d}{s_{{y}}}+\int_{B_{R}}G(x,y)f(y){\rm d}{y}

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}u(x),&&x\in B_{R},\\
0,&&x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\overline{B_{R}}.\end{array}\right.

Noting that
\Phi_{\rm M}
is a real-valued function, we let

\displaystyle G^{*}(x,y)=-\frac{1}{2\kappa^{2}}\left(\Phi_{\rm H}^{*}(x,y)-\Phi_{\rm M}(x,y)\right),

(3.24)

where
\Phi_{\rm H}^{*}(x,y)=-\frac{{\rm i}}{4}H_{0}^{(2)}(\kappa|x-y|)
, with
H_{0}^{(2)}
being the Hankel function of the second kind with order zero, satisfies

\displaystyle\Delta\Phi_{\rm H}^{*}(x,y)+\kappa^{2}\Phi_{\rm H}^{*}(x,y)=-\delta(x-y).

(3.25)

It can be verified from (
3.24
)–(
3.25
) that

\displaystyle(\Delta^{2}-\kappa^{4})G^{*}(x,y)=-\delta(x-y).

By substituting
G
with
G^{*}
in (
3.2
) and then subtracting the resulting equation from (
3.2
), we can deduce

\displaystyle\int_{B_{R}}\Psi(x,y)f(y){\rm d}{y}

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial B_{R}}\big[\partial_{\nu(y)}\Psi(x,y)\Delta u(y)-\Psi(x,y)\partial_{\nu}\Delta u(y)\big]{\rm d}{s_{{y}}}

\displaystyle\quad-\int_{\partial B_{R}}\big[\Delta\Psi(x,y)\partial_{\nu}u(y)-\partial_{\nu(y)}\Delta\Psi(x,y)u(y)\big]{\rm d}{s_{{y}}},

(3.26)

where

\displaystyle\Psi(x,y)=G(x,y)-G^{*}(x,y)=-\frac{{\rm i}}{4\kappa^{2}}J_{0}(\kappa|x-y|).

(3.27)

Substituting (
3.27
) into (
3.2
), we have from a straightforward calculation that

\displaystyle\int_{B_{R}}J_{0}(\kappa|x-y|)f(y){\rm d}{y}

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial B_{R}}\big[\partial_{\nu(y)}J_{0}(\kappa|x-y|)\Delta u(y)-J_{0}(\kappa|x-y|)\partial_{\nu}\Delta u(y)\big]{\rm d}{s_{y}}

\displaystyle\quad-\int_{\partial B_{R}}\big[\Delta J_{0}(\kappa|x-y|)\partial_{\nu}u(y)-\partial_{\nu(y)}\Delta J_{0}(\kappa|x-y|)u(y)\big]{\rm d}{s_{y}}

\displaystyle=-\int_{\partial B_{R}}\Big[\left(\nu\cdot\nabla_{x}J_{0}(\kappa|x-y|)\right)\Delta u(y)+J_{0}(\kappa|x-y|)\partial_{\nu}\Delta u(y)\Big]{\rm d}{s_{y}}

\displaystyle\quad-\int_{\partial B_{R}}\Big[\Delta J_{0}(\kappa|x-y|)\partial_{\nu}u(y)+\left(\nu\cdot\nabla_{x}\Delta J_{0}(\kappa|x-y|)\right)u(y)\Big]{\rm d}{s_{y}}.

(3.28)

Since
f
is compactly supported in
B_{R}
, we let
J(x)=J_{0}(\kappa|x|)
and obtain from taking the Fourier transform on both sides of (
3.2
) that

\hat{J}(\xi)\hat{f}(\xi)=\hat{J}(\xi)\hat{U}(\xi),

(3.29)

where the expression for
\hat{U}
is given in terms of the near-field data of the wave field
u
as follows:

\hat{U}(\xi)=\int_{\partial B_{R}}\Big[\left(-({\rm i}\xi\cdot\nu)\Delta u(y)-\partial_{\nu}\Delta u(y)+|\xi|^{2}\partial_{\nu}u(y)+|\xi|^{2}({\rm i}\xi\cdot\nu)u(y)\right)e^{-{\rm i}\xi\cdot y}\Big]{\rm d}{s_{y}}.

As evident from (
3.29
), it becomes apparent that
\hat{f}(\xi)=\hat{U}(\xi)
when
\hat{J}(\xi)\neq 0
. In such cases, the source function
f
can be reconstructed by performing the inverse Fourier transform on
\hat{U}(\xi)
. However, if
\hat{J}(\xi)=0
, the information about
\hat{f}(\xi)
is not present. As a result, attempting to determine
f
by applying the inverse Fourier transform to
\hat{U}(\xi)
becomes unfeasible.

Using (
3.14
), we deduce through a straightforward calculation that

\displaystyle\hat{J}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}J_{0}(\kappa|x|)e^{-{\rm i}\xi\cdot x}{\rm d}x=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}J_{0}(\kappa|x|)\left(\sum_{n=-\infty}^{+\infty}(-{\rm i})^{n}e^{{\rm i}n{\rm arg}(\hat{\xi})}J_{n}(|{\xi}||x|)e^{-{\rm i}n{\rm arg}(x)}\right){\rm d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{2\pi}J_{0}(\kappa r)\left(\sum_{n=-\infty}^{+\infty}(-{\rm i})^{n}e^{{\rm i}n{\rm arg}(\hat{\xi})}J_{n}(|{\xi}|r)e^{-{\rm i}n{\rm arg}(x)}\right)r{\rm d\theta}{\rm dr}

\displaystyle=2\pi\int_{0}^{\infty}J_{0}(\kappa r)J_{0}(|{\xi}|r)r{\rm d}r

\displaystyle=\frac{2\pi}{\kappa}\delta(|{\xi}|-\kappa)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\infty,&&|\xi|=\kappa,\\
0,&&|\xi|\neq\kappa,\end{array}\right.

where
\delta
is the Dirac delta function. Based on (
3.2
), it is evident that
\hat{J}(\xi)=0
for
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
except on the circle
|\xi|=\kappa
. By (
3.29
), we deduce that the information regarding
\hat{f}(\xi)
can be determined only on the circle
|\xi|=\kappa
, in the following manner:

\displaystyle\hat{f}(\xi)=\hat{U}(\xi),\quad|\xi|=\kappa.

(3.32)

Furthermore, by applying Green’s second theorem and using (
2.1
) and (
3.17
), we derive

\displaystyle\check{f}(s)

\displaystyle=\int_{B_{R}}f(y)e^{-s\cdot y}{\rm d}y=-\int_{B_{R}}[(\Delta-\kappa^{2})(\Delta+\kappa^{2})u(y)]e^{-s\cdot y}{\rm d}y

\displaystyle=-\int_{B_{R}}[(\Delta+\kappa^{2})u(y)][(\Delta-\kappa^{2})e^{-s\cdot y}]{\rm d}y

\displaystyle\quad-\int_{\partial B_{R}}\Big[e^{-s\cdot y}\partial_{\nu}\left(\Delta+\kappa^{2})u(y)\right)-\left((\Delta+\kappa^{2})u(y)\right)\partial_{\nu}e^{-s\cdot y}\Big]{\rm d}{s_{y}}

\displaystyle=-\int_{B_{R}}[(\Delta+\kappa^{2})u(y)][(|s|^{2}-\kappa^{2})e^{-s\cdot y}]{\rm d}y

\displaystyle\quad-\int_{\partial B_{R}}\Big[\partial_{\nu}\Delta u(y)+\kappa^{2}\partial_{\nu}u(y)+({s\cdot\nu})\Delta u(y)+\kappa^{2}({s\cdot\nu})u(y)\Big]e^{-s\cdot y}{\rm d}{s_{y}},

which implies, when considering the case
|s|=\kappa
, that

\check{f}(s)=\check{V}(s),

(3.33)

where
\hat{V}(s)
is solely based on the near-field data of the function
u
on the boundary
\partial B_{R}
:

\displaystyle\check{V}(s)=-\int_{\partial B_{R}}\Big[\partial_{\nu}\Delta u(y)+\kappa^{2}\partial_{\nu}u(y)+({s\cdot\nu})\Delta u(y)+\kappa^{2}({s\cdot\nu})u(y)\Big]e^{-s\cdot y}{\rm d}{s_{y}}.

Hence, by employing Theorem
3.6
, in conjunction with (
3.32
) and (
3.33
), we are able to establish the following alternative characterization of nonradiating sources using the near-field data.

Theorem 3.7
.

Assume that
f\in L^{2}(B_{R})
. Then the source
f
is nonradiating if and only if
\hat{U}(\xi)=\check{V}(s)=0
for
|\xi|=|s|=\kappa
.

3.3.
Nonuniqueness

In this section, we examine the topic of nonuniqueness of the inverse source problem. As the inverse source problem exhibits linearity, the presence of nonuniqueness indicates that a nonzero source function has the capability to produce a localized wave field. In other words, the nonuniqueness issue originates from the existence of nonradiating sources. We begin by introducing the null space, a concept directly related to the nonuniqueness aspects of the inverse source problem.

Definition 3.1
.

The source function
f\in L^{2}(B_{R})
is said to be in the null space
\mathcal{N}(R)
if it satisfies

\displaystyle\int_{B_{R}}J_{0}(\kappa|x-y|)f(y){\rm d}{y}=0,\quad\int_{B_{R}}\Phi_{\rm M}(x,y)f(y){\rm d}y=0,\quad|x|>R.

(3.34)

For
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, it is noteworthy that
J_{0}
has a cylindrical harmonic expansion, expressed as

\displaystyle J_{0}(\kappa|x-y|)=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}J_{n}(\kappa|x|)e^{{\rm i}n{\rm arg}(x)}J_{n}(\kappa|y|)e^{-{\rm i}n{\rm arg}(y)},\quad|x|>|y|.

(3.35)

Hence, for
|x|>R
, we can deduce from (
3.34
) and (
3.35
) that

\displaystyle\int_{B_{R}}J_{0}(\kappa|x-y|)f(y){\rm d}{y}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}J_{n}(\kappa|x|)e^{{\rm i}n{\rm arg}(x)}\int_{B_{R}}J_{n}(\kappa|y|)e^{-{\rm i}n{\rm arg}(y)}f(y){\rm d}{y}

\displaystyle=2\pi\bigg[\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}J_{n}(\kappa|x|)e^{{\rm i}n{\rm arg}(x)}\int_{0}^{R}J_{n}(\kappa r)r\bigg(\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}f(y)e^{-{\rm i}n\theta}{\rm d}\theta\bigg){\rm d}r\bigg]

\displaystyle=2\pi\bigg[\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}J_{n}(\kappa|x|)e^{{\rm i}n{\rm arg}(x)}\int_{0}^{R}f_{n}(r)J_{n}(\kappa r)r{\rm d}r\bigg],

which implies that

\displaystyle\int_{B_{R}}J_{0}(\kappa|x-y|)f(y){\rm d}{y}=2\pi\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\alpha_{n}J_{n}(\kappa|x|)e^{{\rm i}n{\rm arg}(x)},\quad|x|>R.

(3.36)

Proposition 3.8
.

Assume that
f\in L^{2}(B_{R})
. Then the source
f
is nonradiating if and only if
f\in\mathcal{N}(R)
.

Proof.

If
f\in L^{2}(B_{R})
is a nonradiating source for the biharmonic wave equation, according to Theorem
3.2
, Proposition
3.3
, and (
3.36
), we can derive (
3.34
), which implies that
f
belongs to
\mathcal{N}(R)
.

Conversely, if
f\in L^{2}(B_{R})
belongs to
\mathcal{N}(R)
, it satisfies (
3.34
). By invoking (
3.36
) and utilizing Proposition
3.3
, we can conclude that
\alpha_{n}=\beta_{n}=0
holds for all
\mathbb{Z}
, thereby confirming that
f
is indeed a nonradiating source.
∎

Proposition
3.8
demonstrates that the null space includes a set of nonradiating sources. In Section
5
, we establish the non-emptiness of
\mathcal{N}(R)
by providing explicit examples of nonradiating sources. Consequently, the presence of nonradiating sources leads to the lack of uniqueness in the inverse source problem.

4.
The Three-Dimensional problem

This section is dedicated to examining the characterization of nonradiating sources within the context of the three-dimensional biharmonic wave equation. The analysis presented here is analogous to that of the two-dimensional case.

4.1.
Characterizations of nonradiating sources

In three dimensions, the Green’s functions for the Helmholtz and modified Helmholtz equations are given by

\displaystyle\Phi_{\rm H}(x,y)=\frac{e^{{\rm i}\kappa|x-y|}}{4\pi|x-y|},\quad\quad\Phi_{\rm M}(x,y)=\frac{e^{-\kappa|x-y|}}{4\pi|x-y|}.

By the addition theorem (cf.
[
10
, Theorem 2.11]
), they admit the following expansions for
|x|>|y|
:

\displaystyle\Phi_{\rm H}(x,y)

\displaystyle={\rm i}\kappa\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-n}^{n}h_{n}^{(1)}(\kappa|x|)Y_{n}^{m}({\hat{x}})j_{n}(\kappa|y|)\overline{Y_{n}^{m}({\hat{y}})},

(4.1)

\displaystyle\Phi_{\rm M}(x,y)

\displaystyle=-\kappa\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-n}^{n}h_{n}^{(1)}({\rm i}\kappa|x|)Y_{n}^{m}({\hat{x}})j_{n}({\rm i}\kappa|y|)\overline{Y_{n}^{m}({\hat{y}})},

(4.2)

where
{\hat{x}}=x/|x|,{\hat{y}}=y/|y|
, the spherical harmonics
\{Y_{n}^{m}:m=-n,\dots,n,n=0,1,\dots\}
form a complete orthonormal system in the space of square integrable functions on the unit sphere,
j_{n}
denotes spherical Bessel function of order
n
, and
h_{n}^{(1)}
stands for the spherical Hankel function of the first kind with order
n
.

Let

\displaystyle g_{\rm H}(x)=-\int_{B_{R}}\Phi_{\rm H}(x,y)f(y){\rm d}y,\quad\quad g_{\rm M}(x)=-\int_{B_{R}}\Phi_{\rm M}(x,y)f(y){\rm d}y.

(4.3)

We have from (
2.4
)–(
2.6
) and (
4.3
) that

\displaystyle u(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{B_{R}}G(x,y)f(y){\rm d}y=-\frac{1}{2\kappa^{2}}\left(\int_{B_{R}}\Phi_{\rm H}(x,y)f(y){\rm d}y-\int_{B_{R}}\Phi_{\rm M}(x,y)f(y){\rm d}y\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\kappa^{2}}\left(g_{\rm H}(x)-g_{\rm M}(x)\right).

(4.4)

Theorem 4.1
.

The source
f
is nonradiating if and only if
g_{\rm H}(x)=g_{\rm M}(x)=0
for
|x|>R
.

Proof.

It is obvious from (
4.1
) that
f
is nonradiating if
g_{\rm H}(x)=g_{\rm M}(x)=0
for
|x|>R
. Conversely, if
f
is nonradiating, then from (
4.1
), it is evident that
u(x)=0
for
|x|>R
, and this dependence solely relies on
g_{\rm H}(x)
and
g_{\rm M}(x)
. In the scenario where
g_{\rm H}(x)\neq 0
and
g_{\rm M}(x)\neq 0
for
|x|>R
, we can confirm, based on (
3.3
), that as
|x|\to\infty
, the following asymptotic expansions hold:

\displaystyle\Phi_{\rm H}(x,y)=\frac{e^{{\rm i}\kappa|x-y|}}{4\pi|x-y|}=\frac{e^{{\rm i}\kappa|x|}}{4\pi|x|}\bigg[e^{-{\rm i}\kappa{\hat{x}}\cdot y}+O\bigg(\frac{1}{|{x}|}\bigg)\bigg]

(4.5)

and

\displaystyle\Phi_{\rm M}(x,y)=\frac{e^{-\kappa|x-y|}}{4\pi|x-y|}=\frac{e^{{\rm i}\kappa|x|}}{4\pi|x|}\bigg[e^{-\kappa({\rm i}|x|+|x|-{\hat{x}}\cdot y)}\bigg(1+O\bigg(\frac{1}{|{x}|}\bigg)\bigg)\bigg].

(4.6)

Hence, from (
4.5
) and (
4.6
), we have

\displaystyle G(x,y)=-\frac{1}{8\pi\kappa^{2}}\frac{e^{{\rm i}\kappa|x|}}{|x|}\bigg[e^{-{\rm i}\kappa{\hat{x}}\cdot y}+O\bigg(\frac{1}{|{x}|}\bigg)\bigg],\quad|x|\to\infty.

(4.7)

Substituting (
4.7
) into (
4.1
) yields

\displaystyle u(x)=-\frac{1}{8\pi\kappa^{2}}\frac{e^{{\rm i}\kappa|x|}}{|x|}\bigg[\int_{D}e^{-{\rm i}\kappa{\hat{x}}\cdot y}f(y){\rm d}y+O\bigg(\frac{1}{|{x}|}\bigg)\bigg],\quad|x|\to\infty.

which implies that
u(x)\not\equiv 0
for
|x|>R
. Therefore, ensuring
u(x)=0
for
|x|>R
is equivalent to the condition
g_{\rm H}(x)=g_{\rm M}(x)=0
for
|x|>R
.
∎

According to Theorem
4.1
, a source
f(x)
is deemed nonradiating for the biharmonic wave equation if and only if it satisfies the condition of being a nonradiating source for both the Helmholtz equation and the modified Helmholtz equation.

Combining (
4.1
)–(
4.2
) and (
4.1
), we obtain

\displaystyle u(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{B_{R}}G(x,y)f(y){\rm d}y=\frac{1}{2\kappa^{2}}\left(g_{\rm H}(x)-g_{\rm M}(x)\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2\kappa^{2}}\bigg[{\rm i}\kappa\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-n}^{n}h_{n}^{(1)}(\kappa|x|)Y_{n}^{m}({\hat{x}})\int_{B_{R}}j_{n}(\kappa|y|)\overline{Y_{n}^{m}({\hat{y}})}f(y){\rm d}y

\displaystyle\quad+\kappa\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-n}^{n}h_{n}^{(1)}({\rm i}\kappa|x|)Y_{n}^{m}({\hat{x}})\int_{B_{R}}j_{n}({\rm i}\kappa|y|)\overline{Y_{n}^{m}({\hat{y}})}f(y){\rm d}y\bigg]

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2\kappa^{2}}\bigg[{\rm i}\kappa\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-n}^{n}h_{n}^{(1)}(\kappa|x|)Y_{n}^{m}({\hat{x}})\int_{0}^{R}j_{n}(\kappa r)r^{2}\bigg(\int_{0}^{\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}f(r,\theta,\phi)\overline{Y_{n}^{m}(\theta,\phi)}\sin\theta{\rm d}\theta{\rm d}\phi\bigg){\rm d}r\bigg]

\displaystyle\quad+\kappa\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-n}^{n}h_{n}^{(1)}({\rm i}\kappa|x|)Y_{n}^{m}({\hat{x}})\int_{0}^{R}j_{n}({\rm i}\kappa r)r^{2}\bigg(\int_{0}^{\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}f(r,\theta,\phi)\overline{Y_{n}^{m}(\theta,\phi)}\sin\theta{\rm d}\theta{\rm d}\phi\bigg){\rm d}r\bigg]

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2\kappa^{2}}\bigg[{\rm i}\kappa\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-n}^{n}h_{n}^{(1)}(\kappa|x|)Y_{n}^{m}({\hat{x}})\int_{0}^{R}f_{n}^{m}(r)j_{n}(\kappa r)r^{2}{\rm d}r

\displaystyle\quad+\kappa\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-n}^{n}h_{n}^{(1)}({\rm i}\kappa|x|)Y_{n}^{m}({\hat{x}})\int_{0}^{R}f_{n}^{m}(r)j_{n}({\rm i}\kappa r)r^{2}{\rm d}r\bigg]

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2\kappa^{2}}\bigg[{\rm i}\kappa\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-n}^{n}\alpha_{n}^{m}h_{n}^{(1)}(\kappa|x|)Y_{n}^{m}({\hat{x}})+\kappa\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-n}^{n}\beta_{n}^{m}h_{n}^{(1)}({\rm i}\kappa|x|)Y_{n}^{m}({\hat{x}})\bigg],

(4.8)

where

\displaystyle f_{n}^{m}(r)=\int_{0}^{\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}f(r,\theta,\phi)\overline{Y_{n}^{m}(\theta,\phi)}\sin\theta{\rm d}\theta{\rm d}\phi,

and

\displaystyle\alpha_{n}^{m}=\int_{0}^{R}f_{n}^{m}(r)j_{n}(\kappa r)r^{2}{\rm d}r,\quad\quad\beta_{n}^{m}=\int_{0}^{R}f_{n}^{m}(r)j_{n}({\rm i}\kappa r)r^{2}{\rm d}r.

It is evident that
f_{n}^{m}
also represents the Fourier coefficients of
f
in the expansion

\displaystyle f(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-n}^{n}f_{n}^{m}(r)Y_{n}^{m}(\theta,\phi)

with respect to the complete set of spherical harmonics
Y_{n}^{m}(\theta,\phi)
.

It can be seen from (
4.1
) that the wave field
u(x)
for
|x|>R
relies on the coefficients
\alpha_{n}^{m}
and
\beta_{n}^{m}
, which represent the projections of the coefficients
f_{n}^{m}
onto the functions
j_{n}(\kappa r)r^{2}
and
j_{n}({\rm i}\kappa r)r^{2}
, respectively. As a result, it becomes possible to construct a source
f
that does not yield any radiated field by nullifying these projections. By closely examining the expansions (
4.1
) of the solution and referring to Theorem
4.1
, we are able to derive the subsequent theorem.

Theorem 4.2
.

The source
f
is nonradiating if and only if
\alpha_{n}^{m}=\beta_{n}^{m}=0
for all
n=0,1,\dots,m=-n,\dots,n.

Proposition 4.3
.

Assume that
f\in L^{2}(B_{R})
. Then
g_{\rm M}(x)|_{\partial B_{R}}=0
if and only if
\beta_{n}^{m}=0
for all
n=0,1,\cdots,m=-n,\cdots,n
.

Proof.

From (
4.2
)–(
4.3
) and (
4.6
), for
f\in L^{2}(B_{R})
, we know that
g_{\rm M}
satisfies the modified Helmholtz equation

\Delta g_{\rm M}-\kappa^{2}g_{\rm M}=f\quad\text{in}~\mathbb{R}^{3}

(4.9)

and

\displaystyle g_{\rm M}(x)=-\int_{B_{R}}\Phi_{\rm M}(x,y)f(y){\rm d}y=-\kappa\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-n}^{n}\beta_{n}^{m}h_{n}^{(1)}({\rm i}\kappa|x|)Y_{n}^{m}({\hat{x}}),\quad|x|>R.

(4.10)

It follows from (
4.9
) and the boundary condition
g_{\rm M}=0
on
\partial B_{R}
and the radiation condition, we have
g_{\rm M}(x)=0
for
|x|>R
, which implies that
\beta_{n}^{m}=0
for all
n=0,1,\dots,m=-n,\cdots,n
.

From
\beta_{n}^{m}=0
for all
n=0,1,\dots,m=-n,\cdots,n
and (
4.10
), we have
g_{\rm M}(x)=0
for
|x|>R
. Then, combining
g_{\rm M}(x)=0
for
|x|>R
and
g_{\rm M}\in H^{\frac{3}{2}}(\partial B_{R})
, we find that
g_{\rm M}(x)=0
on
\partial B_{R}
.
∎

Similarly, we may also show the following result.

Proposition 4.4
.

Assume that
f\in L^{2}(B_{R})
. Then
g_{\rm H}(x)|_{\partial B_{R}}=0
if and only if
\alpha_{n}^{m}=0
for all
n=0,1,\dots,m=-n,\dots,n
.

Combining Theorem
4.2
, Proposition
4.3
and
4.4
, we find that the source
f\in L^{2}(B_{R})
is nonradiating if and only if
g_{\rm H}(x)|_{\partial B_{R}}=g_{\rm M}(x)|_{\partial B_{R}}=0
.

Next we derive an alternative characterization of nonradiating sources in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Recall the Jacobi–Anger expansion for the plane wave (cf.
[

\displaystyle e^{{\rm i}\kappa{x}\cdot{d}}

\displaystyle=4\pi\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\sum_{m=-n}^{n}{{\rm i}}^{n}j_{n}(\kappa|x|)Y_{n}^{m}({\hat{x}})\overline{Y_{n}^{m}({d})},\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{3},

(4.11)

where
d\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
is a unit propagation direction vector. Then, for
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{3}

\displaystyle e^{-{\rm i}{\xi}\cdot{x}}=4\pi\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\sum_{m=-n}^{n}(-{{\rm i}})^{n}j_{n}(|{\xi}||x|)\overline{Y_{n}^{m}({\hat{x}})}Y_{n}^{m}({\hat{\xi}}),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{3},

(4.12)

where
\hat{\xi}={\xi}/|{\xi}|
. Using (

), we obtain

\displaystyle\hat{f}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\int_{B_{R}}f(x)e^{-{\rm i}\xi\cdot x}{\rm d}x=\int_{B_{R}}f(x)\left(4\pi\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\sum_{m=-n}^{n}(-{{\rm i}})^{n}j_{n}(|{\xi}||x|)\overline{Y_{n}^{m}({\hat{x}})}Y_{n}^{m}({\hat{\xi}})\right){\rm d}x

\displaystyle=4\pi\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\sum_{m=-n}^{n}(-{\rm i})^{n}Y_{n}^{m}({\hat{\xi}})\int_{0}^{R}j_{n}(|{\xi}|r)r^{2}\bigg(\int_{0}^{\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}f(r,\theta,\phi)\overline{Y_{n}^{m}(\theta,\phi)}\sin\theta{\rm d}\theta{\rm d}\phi\bigg){\rm d}r

\displaystyle=4\pi\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\sum_{m=-n}^{n}(-{\rm i})^{n}Y_{n}^{m}({\hat{\xi}})\int_{0}^{R}f_{n}^{m}(r)j_{n}(|{\xi}|r)r^{2}{\rm d}r,

which implies

\displaystyle\hat{f}(\xi)=4\pi\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\sum_{m=-n}^{n}(-{\rm i})^{n}\alpha_{n}^{m}Y_{n}^{m}({\hat{\xi}}),\quad|\xi|=\kappa.

(4.13)

From (
4.11
) and
[
25
, Proposition 3.1]
, we have

\displaystyle e^{-\kappa{x}\cdot{d}}=e^{{\rm i}({\rm i}\kappa){x}\cdot{d}}=4\pi\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\sum_{m=-n}^{n}{{\rm i}}^{n}j_{n}({\rm i}\kappa|x|)Y_{n}^{m}({\hat{x}})\overline{Y_{n}^{m}({d})}.

Then, for
|x|=r\leq R
and
0<|s|<\kappa+K
,
K>0
is a bounded constant, we get

\displaystyle e^{-{s}\cdot{x}}=e^{-{|x|s}\cdot{{\hat{x}}}}=e^{-{rs}\cdot{{\hat{x}}}}=4\pi\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\sum_{m=-n}^{n}{{\rm i}}^{n}j_{n}({\rm i}|s|r)Y_{n}^{m}({\hat{s}})\overline{Y_{n}^{m}({\hat{x}})},

where
\hat{s}=s/|s|
.

Define

\displaystyle\check{f}(s)

\displaystyle=\int_{B_{R}}f(x)e^{-s\cdot x}{\rm d}x,\quad 0<|s|<\kappa+K.

(4.14)

By (
4.14
), we have

\displaystyle\check{f}(s)

\displaystyle=\int_{B_{R}}f(x)\left(4\pi\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\sum_{m=-n}^{n}{{\rm i}}^{n}j_{n}({\rm i}|s|r)Y_{n}^{m}({\hat{s}})\overline{Y_{n}^{m}({\hat{x}})}\right){\rm d}x

\displaystyle=4\pi\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\sum_{m=-n}^{n}{{\rm i}}^{n}Y_{n}^{m}({\hat{s}})\int_{0}^{R}j_{n}({\rm i}|{s}|r)r^{2}\bigg(\int_{0}^{\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}f(r,\theta,\phi)\overline{Y_{n}^{m}(\theta,\phi)}\sin\theta{\rm d}\theta{\rm d}\phi\bigg){\rm d}r

\displaystyle=4\pi\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\sum_{m=-n}^{n}{{\rm i}}^{n}Y_{n}^{m}({\hat{s}})\int_{0}^{R}f_{n}^{m}(r)j_{n}({\rm i}|{s}|r)r^{2}{\rm d}r,\quad 0<|s|<\kappa+K,

(4.15)

which implies

\displaystyle\check{f}(s)=4\pi\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\sum_{m=-n}^{n}{{\rm i}}^{n}\beta_{n}^{m}Y_{n}^{m}({\hat{s}}),\quad|s|=\kappa.

(4.16)

According to Theorem
4.2
, together with (
4.13
) and (
4.16
), we arrive at the following conclusion.

Theorem 4.5
.

Assume that
f\in L^{2}(B_{R})
. Then the source
f
is nonradiating if and only if
\hat{f}(\xi)=\check{f}(s)=0
when
|\xi|=|s|=\kappa
.

4.2.
Near-field data

First, applying Green’s second theorem, from (
2.1
), we have

\displaystyle\hat{f}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\int_{B_{R}}f(y)e^{-{\rm i}\xi\cdot y}{\rm d}y=-\int_{B_{R}}[(\Delta+\kappa^{2})(\Delta-\kappa^{2})u(y)]e^{-{\rm i}\xi\cdot y}{\rm d}y

\displaystyle=-\int_{B_{R}}[(\Delta-\kappa^{2})u(y)][(\Delta+\kappa^{2})e^{-{\rm i}\xi\cdot y}]{\rm d}y

\displaystyle\quad-\int_{\partial B_{R}}\big[e^{-{\rm i}\xi\cdot y}\partial_{\nu}\big((\Delta-\kappa^{2})u(y)\big)-\big((\Delta-\kappa^{2})u(y)\big)\partial_{\nu}e^{-{\rm i}\xi\cdot y}\big]{\rm d}{s_{y}}

\displaystyle=-\int_{B_{R}}[(\Delta-\kappa^{2})u(y)][(-|\xi|^{2}+\kappa^{2})e^{-{\rm i}\xi\cdot y}]{\rm d}y

\displaystyle\quad-\int_{\partial B_{R}}\big[\partial_{\nu}\Delta u(y)-\kappa^{2}\partial_{\nu}u(y)+({{\rm i}\xi\cdot\nu})\Delta u(y)-\kappa^{2}({{\rm i}\xi\cdot\nu})u(y)\big]e^{-{\rm i}\xi\cdot y}{\rm d}{s_{y}},\quad\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{3}.

(4.17)

By (
4.2
), it is evident that we can only determine
\hat{f}(\xi)
on the sphere
|\xi|=\kappa,\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
as

\displaystyle\hat{f}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\hat{U}(\xi)

\displaystyle:

\displaystyle=-\int_{\partial B_{R}}\big[\partial_{\nu}\Delta u(y)-\kappa^{2}\partial_{\nu}u(y)+({{\rm i}\xi\cdot\nu})\Delta u(y)-\kappa^{2}({{\rm i}\xi\cdot\nu})u(y)\big]e^{-{\rm i}\xi\cdot y}{\rm d}{s_{y}}.

(4.18)

Furthermore, applying Green’s second theorem, from (
2.1
) and (
4.1
), we get

\displaystyle\check{f}(s)

\displaystyle=\int_{B_{R}}f(y)e^{-s\cdot y}{\rm d}y=-\int_{B_{R}}[(\Delta-\kappa^{2})(\Delta+\kappa^{2})u(y)]e^{-s\cdot y}{\rm d}y

\displaystyle=-\int_{B_{R}}[(\Delta+\kappa^{2})u(y)][(\Delta-\kappa^{2})e^{-s\cdot y}]{\rm d}y

\displaystyle\quad-\int_{\partial B_{R}}\big[e^{-s\cdot y}\partial_{\nu}\big((\Delta+\kappa^{2})u(y)\big)-\big((\Delta+\kappa^{2})u(y)\big)\partial_{\nu}e^{-s\cdot y}\big]{\rm d}{s_{y}}

\displaystyle=-\int_{B_{R}}[(\Delta+\kappa^{2})u(y)][(|s|^{2}-\kappa^{2})e^{-s\cdot y}]{\rm d}y

\displaystyle\quad-\int_{\partial B_{R}}\big[\partial_{\nu}\Delta u(y)+\kappa^{2}\partial_{\nu}u(y)+({s\cdot\nu})\Delta u(y)+\kappa^{2}({s\cdot\nu})u(y)\big]e^{-s\cdot y}{\rm d}{s_{y}},\quad 0<|s|<\kappa+K.

Hence, we can only determine
\check{f}(s)
on
|s|=\kappa,s\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
as

\displaystyle\check{f}(s)

\displaystyle=\check{V}(s)

\displaystyle:=-\int_{\partial B_{R}}\big[\partial_{\nu}\Delta u(y)+\kappa^{2}\partial_{\nu}u(y)+({s\cdot\nu})\Delta u(y)+\kappa^{2}({s\cdot\nu})u(y)\big]e^{-s\cdot y}{\rm d}{s_{y}}.

(4.19)

By Theorem
4.5
, (
4.2
) and (
4.2
), we have the following Theorem.

Theorem 4.6
.

Assume that
f\in L^{2}(B_{R})
. Then the source
f
is nonradiating if and only if
\hat{U}(\xi)=\check{V}(s)=0
when
|\xi|=|s|=\kappa
.

4.3.
Nonuniqueness

For the three-dimensional case, we define the following null space.

Definition 4.1
.

The source function
f\in L^{2}(B_{R})
is said to be in the null space
\mathcal{N}(R)
if

\displaystyle\int_{B_{R}}j_{0}(\kappa|x-y|)f(y){\rm d}{y}=0,\quad\int_{B_{R}}\Phi_{\rm M}(x,y)f(y){\rm d}{y}=0,\quad|x|>R.

(4.20)

For
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, we note that
j_{0}
has the spherical harmonic expansion

\displaystyle j_{0}(\kappa|x-y|)=4\pi\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-n}^{n}j_{n}(\kappa|x|)Y_{n}^{m}({\hat{x}})j_{n}(\kappa|y|)\overline{Y_{n}^{m}({\hat{y}})},\quad|x|>|y|.

(4.21)

Hence, we have from (
4.20
) and (
4.21
) that

\displaystyle\int_{B_{R}}j_{0}(\kappa|x-y|)f(y){\rm d}{y}=4\pi\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-n}^{n}j_{n}(\kappa|x|)Y_{n}^{m}({\hat{x}})\int_{B_{R}}j_{n}(\kappa|y|)\overline{Y_{n}^{m}({\hat{y}})}f(y){\rm d}{y}

\displaystyle=4\pi\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-n}^{n}j_{n}(\kappa|x|)Y_{n}^{m}({\hat{x}})\int_{0}^{R}f_{n}^{m}(r)j_{n}(\kappa r)r^{2}{\rm d}r

\displaystyle=4\pi\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-n}^{n}\alpha_{n}^{m}j_{n}(\kappa|x|)Y_{n}^{m}({\hat{x}}).

(4.22)

By Theorem
4.1
,
4.2
and (
4.3
), we conclude the following Theorem.

Theorem 4.7
.

Assume that
f\in L^{2}(B_{R})
. Then the source
f
is nonradiating if and only if
f\in\mathcal{N}(R)
.

Proof.

If
f\in L^{2}(B_{R})
is a nonradiating source for the biharmonic wave equation, then, as a consequence of Theorems
4.1
and
4.2
, along with the relation (
4.3
), it follows that (
4.20
) can be derived, which in turn implies
f
belongs to the set
\mathcal{N}(R)
.

Conversely, if
f\in L^{2}(B_{R})
is an element of
\mathcal{N}(R)
, it satisfies (
4.20
). Additionally, according to (
4.1
) and (
4.3
), the conditions
\alpha_{n}^{m}=\beta_{n}^{m}=0
hold for all
n=0,1,\cdots,m=-n,\cdots,n
. By virtue of Theorem
4.2
, it is established that
f
qualifies as a nonradiating source.
∎

5.
Examples of nonradiating sources

In this section, we explicitly construct several examples of nonradiating sources to illustrate that the null space
\mathcal{N}(R)
is not empty.

5.1.
General nonradiating sources

Consider a smooth function
u\in C_{0}^{\infty}(D)
, where
D
represents a domain with compact support. By applying the operator
-(\Delta^{2}-\kappa^{4})
to the function
u
, resulting in the function
f
, we obtain

f(x)=-(\Delta^{2}-\kappa^{4})u(x),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(5.1)

By Definition
2.1
, it is evident that the function
f
given in (
5.1
) also belongs to the space
C_{0}^{\infty}(D)
and constitutes a nonradiating source for the biharmonic wave equation. Below, we demonstrate through equivalent characterizations that the function
f
is a nonradiating source.

For
u\in C_{0}^{\infty}(B_{R})
, we have

\partial_{\nu}\big((\Delta+\kappa^{2})u(y)\big)|_{\partial B_{R}}=\big((\Delta+\kappa^{2})u(y)\big)|_{\partial B_{R}}=0
. It follows from the integration by parts that

\displaystyle f_{\rm M}(x)

\displaystyle=-\int_{B_{R}}\Phi_{\rm M}(x,y)f(y){\rm d}y=\int_{B_{R}}\Phi_{\rm M}(x,y)\big((\Delta-\kappa^{2})(\Delta+\kappa^{2})u(y)\big){\rm d}y

\displaystyle=\int_{B_{R}}\big((\Delta-\kappa^{2})\Phi_{\rm M}(x,y)\big)\big((\Delta+\kappa^{2})u(y)\big){\rm d}y

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{\partial B_{R}}\big[\Phi_{\rm M}(x,y)\partial_{\nu}\big((\Delta+\kappa^{2})u(y)\big)-\big((\Delta+\kappa^{2})u(y)\big)\partial_{\nu(y)}\Phi_{\rm M}(x,y)\big]{\rm d}s_{y}

\displaystyle=\int_{B_{R}}\big((\Delta-\kappa^{2})\Phi_{\rm M}(x,y)\big)\big((\Delta+\kappa^{2})u(y)\big){\rm d}y=0,\quad|x|>R.

Similarly, we may show that

\displaystyle f_{\rm H}(x)=-\int_{B_{R}}\Phi_{\rm H}(x,y)f(y){\rm d}y=0,\quad|x|>R.

According to Theorem
3.1
or Theorem
4.1
, the source function
f
is nonradiating.

Subsequently, we validate Theorem
3.2
or Theorem
4.2
by showing that
\alpha_{n}=\beta_{n}=0
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. For the sake of brevity and generality, we focus our attention on the case where
d=2
.

For all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, from (
5.1
), we have from the integration by parts that

\displaystyle\alpha_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{B_{R}}f(y)J_{n}(\kappa|y|)e^{-{\rm i}n{\rm arg}({y})}{\rm d}y=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{B_{R}}\big((\Delta+\kappa^{2})(\Delta-\kappa^{2})u(y)\big)J_{n}(\kappa|y|)e^{-{\rm i}n{\rm arg}({y})}{\rm d}y

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{B_{R}}\big((\Delta-\kappa^{2})u(y)\big)\big[(\Delta+\kappa^{2})\big(J_{n}(\kappa|y|)e^{-{\rm i}n{\rm arg}({y})}\big)\big]{\rm d}y

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{R}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\bigg[\frac{1}{r}\bigg(\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}r}\bigg(r\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}r}\bigg)+\frac{1}{r}\frac{{\rm d}^{2}}{{\rm d}\theta^{2}}-\kappa^{2}r\bigg)u(r,\theta)\bigg]

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt\times\bigg[\frac{1}{r}\bigg(\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}r}\bigg(r\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}r}\bigg)+\frac{1}{r}\frac{{\rm d}^{2}}{{\rm d}\theta^{2}}+\kappa^{2}r\bigg)\big(J_{n}(\kappa r)e^{-{\rm i}n\theta}\big)\bigg]r{\rm d}\theta{\rm d}r

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{R}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\bigg[\frac{1}{r}\bigg(\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}r}\bigg(r\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}r}\bigg)+\frac{1}{r}\frac{{\rm d}^{2}}{{\rm d}\theta^{2}}-\kappa^{2}r\bigg)u(r,\theta)\bigg]

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt\times\bigg[\bigg(\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}r}\bigg(r\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}r}\bigg)+\kappa^{2}r-\frac{n^{2}}{r}\bigg)J_{n}(\kappa r)\bigg]e^{-{\rm i}n\theta}{\rm d}\theta{\rm d}r=0,

where we utilize the property

\bigg[\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}r}\left(r\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}r}\right)+\kappa^{2}r-\frac{n^{2}}{r}\bigg]J_{n}(\kappa r)=0\quad\forall\,n\in\mathbb{Z}.

We can similarly show that
\beta_{n}=0
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
by noting

\bigg[\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}r}\bigg(r\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}r}\bigg)-\kappa^{2}r-\frac{n^{2}}{r}\bigg]J_{n}({\rm i}\kappa r)=0.

5.2.
A two-dimensional nonradiating source

For
\kappa>0
and
R>0
, we consider the condition
J_{0}(\kappa R)=0
, indicating that
\kappa R
is a root of the Bessel function of order zero. Define

f_{\rm M}(x)=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{J_{0}^{3}(\kappa|x|)}{\int_{0}^{R}J_{0}^{4}(\kappa r)r{\rm d}r}-\frac{J_{0}^{2}(\kappa|x|)}{\int_{0}^{R}J_{0}^{3}(\kappa r)r{\rm d}r},&\quad|x|<R,\\
&0,&\quad|x|\geq R.\end{aligned}\right.

(5.2)

It is clear to note
f_{\rm M}(x)|_{\partial B_{R}}=0
. Based on the identity
J_{0}^{\prime}(z)=-J_{1}(z)
, a straightforward calculation gives

\displaystyle\partial_{\nu}f_{\rm M}(y)|_{\partial B_{R}}

\displaystyle=\lim_{h\to 0^{+}}\nu(y)\cdot\nabla f_{\rm M}(y-h\nu(y))=\frac{-3\kappa J_{0}^{2}(\kappa R)J_{1}(\kappa R)}{\int_{0}^{R}J_{0}^{4}(\kappa r)r{\rm d}r}+\frac{2\kappa J_{0}(\kappa R)J_{1}(\kappa R)}{\int_{0}^{R}J_{0}^{3}(\kappa r)r{\rm d}r}=0,

which indicates that
f_{\rm M}(x)\in C_{0}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
.

Consider the expression

f(x)=(\Delta-\kappa^{2})f_{\rm M}(x),

(5.3)

which is shown to be a nonradiating source for the two-dimensional biharmonic wave equation.

It follows from (
5.2
) and (
5.3
) that

\displaystyle f(x)

\displaystyle=(\Delta-\kappa^{2})f_{\rm M}(x)=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{(\Delta-\kappa^{2})J_{0}^{3}(\kappa|x|)}{\int_{0}^{R}J_{0}^{4}(\kappa r)r{\rm d}r}-\frac{(\Delta-\kappa^{2})J_{0}^{2}(\kappa|x|)}{\int_{0}^{R}J_{0}^{3}(\kappa r)r{\rm d}r},&\quad|x|<R,\\
&0,&\quad|x|\geq R.\end{aligned}\right.

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{\frac{1}{r}\big(\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}r}\big(r\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}r}\big)-\kappa^{2}r\big)J_{0}^{3}(\kappa r)}{\int_{0}^{R}J_{0}^{4}(\kappa r)r{\rm d}r}-\frac{\frac{1}{r}\big(\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}r}\big(r\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}r}\big)-\kappa^{2}r\big)J_{0}^{2}(\kappa r)}{\int_{0}^{R}J_{0}^{3}(\kappa r)r{\rm d}r},&\quad|x|<R,\\
&0,&\quad|x|\geq R.\end{aligned}\right.

(5.4)

Using the integration by parts and the boundary conditions
f_{\rm M}(x)|_{\partial B_{R}}=\partial_{\nu}f_{\rm M}(x)|_{\partial B_{R}}=0
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{B_{R}}\Phi_{\rm H}(x,y)f(y){\rm d}y=\int_{B_{R}}\Phi_{\rm H}(x,y)\left((\Delta-\kappa^{2})f_{\rm M}(y)\right){\rm d}y

\displaystyle=\int_{B_{R}}\big((\Delta-\kappa^{2})\Phi_{\rm H}(x,y)\big)f_{\rm M}(y){\rm d}y+\int_{\partial B_{R}}\big(\Phi_{\rm H}(x,y)\partial_{\nu}f_{\rm M}(y)-f_{\rm M}(y)\partial_{\nu(y)}\Phi_{\rm H}(x,y)\big){\rm d}s_{y}

\displaystyle=\int_{B_{R}}\big((\Delta-\kappa^{2})\Phi_{\rm H}(x,y)\big)f_{\rm M}(y){\rm d}y=-2\kappa^{2}\int_{B_{R}}\Phi_{\rm H}(x,y)f_{\rm M}(y){\rm d}y,\quad|x|>R.

(5.5)

On the other hand, we have from a straightforward calculation that

\displaystyle\int_{B_{R}}\Phi_{\rm H}(x,y)f_{\rm M}(y){\rm d}y=\int_{B_{R}}\frac{{\rm i}}{4}H_{0}^{(1)}(\kappa|x-y|)\left(\frac{J_{0}^{3}(\kappa|y|)}{\int_{0}^{R}J_{0}^{4}(\kappa r)r{\rm d}r}-\frac{J_{0}^{2}(\kappa|y|)}{\int_{0}^{R}J_{0}^{3}(\kappa r)r{\rm d}r}\right){\rm d}y

\displaystyle=\frac{{\rm i}}{4}\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}H_{n}^{(1)}(\kappa|x|)e^{{\rm i}n{\rm arg}(x)}\int_{B_{R}}\left(\frac{J_{0}^{3}(\kappa|y|)}{\int_{0}^{R}J_{0}^{4}(\kappa r)r{\rm d}r}-\frac{J_{0}^{2}(\kappa|y|)}{\int_{0}^{R}J_{0}^{3}(\kappa r)r{\rm d}r}\right)J_{n}(\kappa|y|)e^{-{\rm i}n{\rm arg}(y)}{\rm d}y

\displaystyle=\frac{{\rm i}}{4}H_{0}^{(1)}(\kappa|x|)\left(\frac{2\pi\int_{0}^{R}J_{0}^{4}(\kappa r)r{\rm d}r}{\int_{0}^{R}J_{0}^{4}(\kappa r)r{\rm d}r}-\frac{2\pi\int_{0}^{R}J_{0}^{3}(\kappa r)r{\rm d}r}{\int_{0}^{R}J_{0}^{3}(\kappa r)r{\rm d}r}\right)=0,\quad|x|>R.

(5.6)

Combining (
5.2
) and (
5.2
) yields

\displaystyle f_{\rm H}(x)=-\int_{B_{R}}\Phi_{\rm H}(x,y)f(y){\rm d}y=0,\quad|x|>R.

(5.7)

Similarly, we obtain from the integration by parts and (
2.5
) that

\displaystyle f_{\rm M}(x)

\displaystyle=-\int_{B_{R}}\Phi_{\rm M}(x,y)f(y){\rm d}y=-\int_{B_{R}}\Phi_{\rm M}(x,y)\big((\Delta-\kappa^{2})f_{\rm M}(y)\big){\rm d}y

\displaystyle=-\int_{B_{R}}\big((\Delta-\kappa^{2})\Phi_{\rm M}(x,y)\big)f_{\rm M}(y){\rm d}y

\displaystyle\quad-\int_{\partial B_{R}}\big(\Phi_{\rm M}(x,y)\partial_{\nu}f_{\rm M}(y)-f_{\rm M}(y)\partial_{\nu(y)}\Phi_{\rm M}(x,y)\big){\rm d}s_{y}

\displaystyle=-\int_{B_{R}}[(\Delta-\kappa^{2})\Phi_{\rm M}(x,y)]f_{\rm M}(y){\rm d}y=0,\quad|x|>R.

(5.8)

Combining (
5.7
) and (
5.2
) leads to

\displaystyle u(x)=\int_{B_{R}}G(x,y)f(y)dy=\frac{1}{2\kappa^{2}}(f_{\rm H}(x)-f_{\rm M}(x))=0,\quad|x|>R.

which implies that
f(x)
is a nonradiating source for the two-dimensional biharmonic wave equation.

Next, we verify that
\alpha_{n}=\beta_{n}=0
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. Noting

\displaystyle J_{0}^{k}(\kappa r)\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}r}\bigg(r\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}r}J_{0}(\kappa r)\bigg)+\kappa^{2}rJ_{0}^{k+1}(\kappa r)

\displaystyle=\bigg[\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}r}\bigg(r\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}r}J_{0}(\kappa r)\bigg)+\kappa^{2}rJ_{0}(\kappa r)\bigg]J_{0}^{k}(\kappa r)

\displaystyle=0\times J_{0}^{k}(\kappa r)=0,

we have for
k\geq 2
that

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{R}\big[\kappa^{2}rJ_{0}^{k}(\kappa r)\big]J_{0}(\kappa r){\rm d}r=\int_{0}^{R}\bigg[\bigg(\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}r}\bigg(r\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}r}J_{0}(\kappa r)\bigg)\bigg]J_{0}^{k}(\kappa r){\rm d}r

\displaystyle=\bigg(r\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}r}J_{0}(\kappa r)\bigg)J_{0}^{k}(\kappa r)\bigg|^{R}_{0}-\int_{0}^{R}\bigg(r\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}r}J_{0}(\kappa r)\bigg)\bigg(\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}r}J_{0}^{k}(\kappa r)\bigg){\rm d}r

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{R}\bigg(\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}r}J_{0}(\kappa r)\bigg)\bigg(r\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}r}J_{0}^{k}(\kappa r)\bigg){\rm d}r

\displaystyle=-J_{0}(\kappa r)\bigg(r\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}r}J_{0}^{k}(\kappa r)\bigg)\bigg|^{R}_{0}+\int_{0}^{R}J_{0}(\kappa r)\bigg[\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}r}\bigg(r\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}r}J_{0}^{k}(\kappa r)\bigg)\bigg]{\rm d}r

6.
Conclusion

In this study, we undertake a thorough investigation on nonradiating sources of the two- and three-dimensional biharmonic wave equation. The goal is to reveal both their existence and inherent characteristics. Nonradiating sources, defined as sources that result in a field being identically zero beyond a finite region, share a close relationship with the issue of nonuniqueness in the inverse source problem. Specifically, we demonstrate that a source conforms to nonradiating behavior in the biharmonic wave equation if and only if it satisfies the condition of being a nonradiating source of both the Helmholtz equation and the modified Helmholtz equation. By utilizing the integral transform and the series representation of the fundamental solution, we deduce fundamental identities, which establish connections between the integral transform of the source and the near-field data through the integral representation of the solution for the biharmonic wave equation. We provide these conditions, which serve as valuable criteria for assessing the nonradiating attributes of sources, enabling researchers to facilitate the more efficient identification and characterization of nonradiating sources. Furthermore, we demonstrate that the integral operator possesses a nontrivial null space, constituting the collection of nonradiating sources. This observation holds implications for the nonuniqueness inherent in the inverse source problem.
