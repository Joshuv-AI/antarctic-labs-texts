Spectral approximation of a class of stochastic time-fractional evolution equations Thanks: 1 Department of Mathematical Sciences, NTNU, Trondheim, Norway Thanks: ✉ S. Knutsen Furset: simen.k.furset@ntnu.no

Abstract
. A method for numerical approximation of a class of fractional parabolic stochastic evolution equations is introduced and analysed. This class of equations has recently been proposed as a space-time extension of the SPDE-method in spatial statistics. A truncation of the spectral basis function expansion is used to discretise in space, and then a quadrature is used to approximate the temporal evolution of each basis coefficient. Strong error bounds are proved both for the spectral and temporal approximations. The method is tested and the results are verified by several numerical experiments.

Introduction

Motivation

We introduce a spectral method for approximating a class of stochastic fractional parabolic evolution equation

(1)

\begin{cases}\left(\partial_{t}+A\right)^{\gamma}X(t)=\dot{W}_{Q}(t)\quad\text{for}\quad t>0\\
X(t)=0\quad\text{for}\quad t\leq 0\end{cases}\,,

where
A
is an unbounded linear operator such that
-A
generates a
C_{0}
-semigroup,
Q
is a bounded linear operator,
W_{Q}
is a (generalised)
Q
-Wiener process,
t\in[0,T]
, and
\gamma>\frac{1}{2}
. Our method consists of truncating an eigenfunction expansion of
\{X(t)\}_{t}
and computing the coefficients by quadrature. The quadrature in time allows us to achieve an improvement in computational complexity compared to direct simulation using Cholesky decompositions. We are primarily interested in the case where
A
and
Q^{-1}
are elliptic operators defined by

(2)

A=r^{-1}(\kappa^{2}-\Delta)^{\alpha}\,,\quad Q=\sigma^{2}r^{-2\gamma}(\kappa^{2}-\Delta)^{-\beta}\,,

where
\alpha>0
,
\beta>0
,
\sigma>0
,
r>0
,
\kappa>0
and
\Delta
is the Laplacian on
\mathcal{D}
, equipped with zero Dirichlet boundary condition. (
1
) with these operators has recently been suggested as a way to extend the so-called SPDE-method in spatial statistics to the case where the random fields are varying in time
[
39
]
. The SPDE-method, i.e., using stochastic partial differential equations (SPDE’s) to construct spatial covariance models, is a popular approach in spatial statistics and has seen applications in a wide range of fields, see for example
[
3
,
24
,
41
,
40
]
. Motivating a statistical model through an SPDE creates a bridge between statistics and numerics by allowing the use of numerical methods developed for PDE’s in the construction of covariance models. Compared to the classical method of specifying covariance functions directly, the SPDE-method allows easy generalisation to non-Euclidean spaces, for example the sphere. This is because covariance functions need to be non-negative definite, which is a difficult property to verify for functions on non-Euclidean spaces. However, covariance functions that derive from SPDE’s automatically satisfy this requirement.

Solutions
\{X(t)\}_{t}
to (
1
) with the operators in (
2
) have several properties that makes them useful for the modelling of spatio-temporal phenomena. First, it has been shown that
\{X(t)\}_{t}
has spatial covariance operator that converges to a Whittle-Mátern operator as
t\rightarrow\infty

[
29
, Corollary 4.2]
. This is of interest because Mátern fields are the most commonly used covariance models in spatial statistics; see for example
[
3
,
17
]
. The alternative time-fractional SPDE
\partial_{t}^{\gamma}X(t)+AX(t)=\dot{W}_{Q}(t)
has also been studied; see
[
11
,
22
]
, but solutions to this SPDE are not asymptotically Whittle-Mátern. Secondly, the parameters
\gamma
,
\alpha
,
\beta
,
r
,
\kappa
, and
\sigma
are few in number, and they allow independent control over several important properties of the process
\{X(t)\}_{t}
, namely the spatial smoothness
\nu_{s}
, the temporal smoothness
\nu_{t}
, the spatial range
r_{s}
, and the temporal range
r_{t}
. The smoothness is here a measure of the regularity of the solutions, interpreted in the sense that if the solution has a smoothness of
\nu
, it has
n:=\lceil\nu\rceil-1
mean-square derivatives, and the
n
-th derivative is mean-square
(\nu-n)
-Hölder continuous (up to an
\epsilon
if
\nu
is an integer) on
[0,T]
. The range is a measure of the rate at which correlation decays to zero. It is possible to parameterise the SPDE in terms of these properties, see
[
39
]
or Section
4
for details.

Advantages and disadvantages of spectral approximations

We are using a spectral approximation in this work. Finite element methods are also common in SPDE literature. The advantage of using a spectral method on (
1
) is that we can easily use any value of
\alpha
and
\beta
also non-integer, constrained only by the desire that
\{X(t)\}_{t}
has
\mathcal{L}^{2}
-solutions. Since fractional operators are non-local they are more costly to discretise using finite-element methods, see for example
[
10
,
8
,
9
,
12
,
25
]
. In addition, spectral methods generally have very fast convergence when solutions are smooth; we see later that this also is the case for the SPDE in (
1
). A drawback of the spectral approach is that we need the eigenfunctions of
A
and
Q
on the domain
\mathcal{D}
. In the case of the operators in (
2
) the eigenfunctions are equal to the eigenfunctions of the Laplacian, which are known in some special cases, for example rectangles in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and the sphere
S^{2}
, both of which are important in applications. In the numerical section of the work we therefore restrict our attention to these two cases. However, in general one would need to numerically compute the eigenfunctions, which can be computationally expensive. This makes it difficult to extend the method to more complicated domains. It also makes it difficult to use more complicated operators, e.g., operators with spatially varying coefficients.

Contributions

To the authors knowledge, no one has yet considered methods for numerical approximation of (
1
) for fractional
\gamma
. We therefore introduce a new numerical method for approximating (
1
), based on discretising a Duhamel-type representation formula for the solutions derived in
[
29
]
. The representation formula is in the form of a stochastic convolution. Our method is spectral in space, we approximate
X(t)
by truncating its eigenfunction expansion. The spectral approximation is standard, and the main contribution of this work is a quadrature method for approximating the resulting Itô integrals on each spectral basis function. The eigenfunctions and eigenvalues are assumed known. We prove strong error bounds for both the spectral and temporal discretisations and then verify these error estimates with numerical experiments. We also do simulations of
\{X(t)\}_{t}
on the unit sphere, providing the first visualisation of the solutions of (
1
) for fractional
\gamma
. This allows us to visually verify how the smoothness of the solutions depend on the parameters. Though we are primarily interested in the spatial operators in (
2
), we achieve a more general theory with little extra effort by using slightly more general assumptions on
A
and
Q
. These assumptions can be found in Assumption
2.1
below. One weakness of our approach is that the convergence rate of our temporal approximation goes to zero as
\gamma\rightarrow\frac{1}{2}
due to a singularity in the Itô integrals. This is hard to avoid since quadrature methods with accumulating meshes are not readily applicable, see Remark
3.9
for details.

2.
Spectral decomposition

As mentioned above (
1
) has mild solution given by the stochastic convolution

(3)

X(t):=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\gamma)}\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{\gamma-1}S(t-s)\,\textup{d}W_{Q}(s)\,,\quad t\geq 0\,,

where
\{S(t)\}_{t}
is the
C_{0}
-semigroup generated by
-A

[
29
]
. This stochastic convolution is the basis for our numerical approximation. We make the following assumptions

Assumption 2.1
.

Let
A:\mathcal{L}^{2}(\mathcal{D})\supset D(A)\rightarrow\mathcal{L}^{2}(\mathcal{D})
, where
D(A)
is the domain of
A
, and
Q:\mathcal{L}^{2}(\mathcal{D})\rightarrow\mathcal{L}^{2}(\mathcal{D})
. We assume the following:

(i)

A
and
Q
are linear operators with a joint orthonormal basis of eigenfunctions
\{e_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
, spanning
\mathcal{L}^{2}(\mathcal{D})
, with corresponding real eigenvalues
\{\mu_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
and
\{\lambda_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
respectively.

(ii)

There exists an
r_{\mu}>0
and a
C_{1}>0
such that
\{\mu_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
satisfies

\mu_{k}\geq C_{1}k^{r_{\mu}},

where the eigenvalues
\{\mu_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
are indexed so that they are non-decreasing in
k
.

(iii)

There exists an
r_{\lambda}<0
and a
C_{2}>0
such that
\{\lambda_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
satisfies

0<\lambda_{k}\leq C_{2}k^{r_{\lambda}}.

where the eigenvalues
\{\lambda_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
are indexed so that they are non-increasing in
k
.

Under Assumption
2.1
it follows from
[
29
, Theorem 3.12]
that if
\gamma>\frac{1}{2}
, we have
\mathcal{L}^{2}(\mathcal{D})
-existence of (
1
) if
1+r_{\lambda}-r_{\mu}(2\gamma-1)<0
. Additionally,
-A
generates a
C_{0}
-semigroup
S(t)=\textup{e}^{-tA}
on
\mathcal{L}^{2}(\mathcal{D})
defined by
S(t)e_{k}:=\textup{e}^{-\mu_{k}t}e_{k}

[
35
, Section 6.1]
. We denote

\left\langle u,v\right\rangle:=\int_{\mathcal{D}}uv\,dx\,,\quad\left\|u\right\|^{2}:=\left\langle u,u\right\rangle\,.

Since the eigenfunctions
\{e_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
span
\mathcal{L}^{2}(\mathcal{D})
, and since
X(t)
is almost surely in
\mathcal{L}^{2}(\mathcal{D})
, we can for each
t\in[0,T]
expand the solution
X(t)
of (
3
) in terms of the eigenfunctions of
A
, so that

(4)

X(t):=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}c_{k}(t)e_{k}\,,

almost surely in
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}(\mathcal{D})}
, where, for each
k
,
c_{k}
is a
[0,T]
-indexed stochastic processes on
\mathbb{R}
. In the following lemma we apply (
3
) to calculate
c_{k}(t)
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Assume that Assumption
2.1
holds and that
\{X(t)\}_{t}
satisfies (
3
). Define
\omega_{k}(t):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda_{k}}}\left\langle W_{Q}(t),e_{k}\right\rangle
. Then for all
t\in[0,T]
and for all
k=1,2,3,...
,

(5)

c_{k}(t)=\frac{\sqrt{\lambda_{k}}}{\Gamma(\gamma)}\int_{0}^{t}\textup{e}^{-\mu_{k}(t-s)}(t-s)^{\gamma-1}\text{d}\omega_{k}(s)\,,

almost surely, where
c_{k}
is as defined in (
4
). Furthermore, the random processes
\{c_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
are pairwise independent
\mathbb{R}
-valued, standard Wiener-processes.

Proof.

By (
3
) we have that

\displaystyle c_{k}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}c_{j}(t)\left\langle e_{j},e_{k}\right\rangle=\left\langle\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}c_{j}(t)e_{j},e_{k}\right\rangle=\left\langle X(t),e_{k}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle\frac{1}{\Gamma(\gamma)}\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{\gamma-1}S(t-s)\textup{d}W_{Q}(s),e_{k}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\gamma)}\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{\gamma-1}\left\langle\textup{d}W_{Q}(s),S(t-s)e_{k}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\gamma)}\int_{0}^{t}\textup{e}^{-\mu_{k}(t-s)}(t-s)^{\gamma-1}\left\langle\textup{d}W_{Q}(s),e_{k}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{\lambda_{k}}}{\Gamma(\gamma)}\int_{0}^{t}\textup{e}^{-\mu_{k}(t-s)}(t-s)^{\gamma-1}\text{d}\omega_{k}(s)\,,

where the fifth step is justified by
[
18
, Proposition 4.30]
. Furthermore, by definition
\{\omega_{k}(t)\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
are
\mathbb{R}
-valued Wiener-processes. The
\{\omega_{k}(t)\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
’s are also independent, standard Wiener-processes since

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\omega_{k}(t)\omega_{j}(s)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda_{k}\lambda_{j}}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\langle W_{Q}(t),e_{k}\right\rangle\left\langle W_{Q}(s),e_{j}\right\rangle\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda_{k}\lambda_{j}}}\left\langle\min(t,s)Q^{\frac{1}{2}}e_{k},Q^{\frac{1}{2}}e_{j}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\min(t,s)\delta_{kj}\,.\qed

3.
Discretisation of the stochastic evolution equation (
1
)

In this section we discuss how to discretise problem (
1
)
in Section
3.1
. In Section
3.2
we derive a bound on the spectral approximation error, and in Section
3.3
we derive an error bound for our temporal scheme. In Section
3.4
we combine these results to get an error bound for the full discretisation and in Section
3.5
we discuss the complexity of the method.

3.1.
The numerical method

We truncate the spectral expansion (
4
) of the solution
X(t)
of the SPDE (
1
) given by the stochastic convolution formula (
3
),

(6)

\tilde{X}_{M}(t):=\sum_{k=1}^{M}c_{k}(t)e_{k}\,.

We then approximate the coefficient processes
c_{k}(t)
by replacing
(t-s)^{\gamma-1}
in (
5
) by a piecewise polynomial
p_{t}(s)
(of order
m
) on a discretisation grid
0=t_{0}<t_{1}<....<t_{N}=T
, with
h:=\max_{\ell}(t_{\ell}-t_{\ell-1})
, i.e.

\tilde{c}_{k}^{(m)}(t):=\frac{\sqrt{\lambda_{k}}}{\Gamma(\gamma)}\int_{0}^{t}\textup{e}^{-\mu_{k}(t-s)}p_{t}(s)\text{d}\omega_{k}(s)\,.

The full discretisation of the solution
X(t)
at
t_{n}
in the discretisation grid is then:

\displaystyle\tilde{X}^{h}_{M}(t_{n})

\displaystyle:=\sum_{k=1}^{M}\tilde{c}^{(m)}_{k}(t_{n})e_{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{M}\frac{\sqrt{\lambda_{k}}}{\Gamma(\gamma)}\left(\int_{0}^{t_{n}}\textup{e}^{-\mu_{k}(t_{n}-s)}p_{t_{n}}(s)\text{d}\omega_{k}(s)\right)e_{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{M}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\frac{\sqrt{\lambda_{k}}}{\Gamma(\gamma)}\left(\int_{{t_{\ell-1}}}^{t_{\ell}}\textup{e}^{-\mu_{k}(t_{n}-s)}p_{t_{n}}(s)\text{d}\omega_{k}(s)\right)e_{k}

(7)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\gamma)}\sum_{k=1}^{M}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{m}\sqrt{\lambda_{k}}\textup{e}^{-\mu_{k}(t_{n}-t_{\ell})}b_{n,\ell,j}t_{\ell}^{j}{w_{\ell,j,k}}e_{k}\,,

where
\sum_{j=0}^{m}b_{n,\ell,j}s^{j}:={p_{t_{n}}(s)}\big|_{s\in[t_{\ell-1},t_{\ell})}
, and

(8)

{w_{\ell,j,k}}:=\int_{t_{\ell-1}}^{t_{\ell}}\left(\frac{s}{t_{\ell}}\right)^{j}\textup{e}^{-\mu_{k}(t_{\ell}-s)}\text{d}\omega_{k}(s)\,.

For fixed
\ell
and
k
,
(w_{\ell,0,k},w_{\ell,1,k},...,w_{\ell,m,k})
is a multivariate Gaussian random variable with mean vector
0
and covariance matrix
\Sigma
defined by

\Sigma_{i,j}:=\int_{t_{\ell-1}}^{t_{\ell}}\left(\frac{s}{t_{\ell}}\right)^{i+j}\textup{e}^{-2\mu_{k}(t_{\ell}-s)}\text{d}s\,.

The random vectors are independent in
\ell
and
k
and have bounded variance over
\ell
and
k
and and can therefore be simulated fast with complexity
O(m^{3})
using standard methods. An additional benefit is that the components of the covariance matrix
\Sigma_{i,j}
can be computed exactly using integration by parts. This procedure therefore gives us a practical method to simulate
\tilde{X}^{h}_{M}(t_{n})\approx X(t_{n})
.

To use our method in practice, one must also choose a method for selecting the piecewise polynomial
p
. The simplest is to use a constant approximation and choose
p_{t}(s)\big|_{s\in[t_{\ell-1},t_{\ell})}=(t-t_{\ell-1}-\theta(t_{\ell}-t_{\ell-1}))^{\gamma-1}
for some
\theta\in[0,1]
. Another option is to use an
\mathcal{L}^{2}
-projection to minimise
\int_{t_{\ell-1}}^{t_{\ell}}(p_{t}(s)-(t-s)^{\gamma-1})^{2}\,\text{d}s
on each interval
[t_{\ell-1},t_{\ell}]
. Lemma
3.11
below gives a practical to compute the coefficients of these projection polynomials.

3.2.
Convergence of the spectral truncation (
6
)

Theorem 3.1
.

Assume Assumption
2.1
holds, and let
X
and
\tilde{X}
be as defined in (
4
) and (
6
) respectively. If
1+r_{\lambda}-(2\gamma-1)r_{\mu}<0
, then for all
t\in[0,T]
we have
X(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}c_{k}(t)e_{k}\in\mathcal{L}^{2}(\Omega,\mathcal{L}^{2}(\mathcal{D}))
and

\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|X(t)-\tilde{X}_{M}(t)\right\|^{2}\right]\leq CM^{-\frac{2\nu}{d}}\,,

where
C=\frac{C_{2}\Gamma(2\gamma-1)}{C_{1}^{2\gamma-1}\Gamma(\gamma)^{2}}\frac{d}{2\nu}
, and
\nu=-\frac{d}{2}(1+r_{\lambda}-(2\gamma-1)r_{\mu})
.

The proof of this theorem can be found in Section
5
.
We are especially interested in the case of the operators in (
2
), i.e.,
A=r^{-1}(\kappa^{2}-\Delta)^{\alpha}
and
Q=\sigma r^{-\gamma}(\kappa^{2}-\Delta)^{-\beta}
. To analyse this case we use the following lemma, adapted from
[
19
, Theorem 6.3.1]
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\mathcal{D}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be an open, non-empty, bounded, and connected domain. Let
\Delta:=\sum_{k=1}^{d}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{k}^{2}}
be defined on a subset
D(\Delta)
of
\mathcal{L}^{2}(\mathcal{D})
of sufficiently smooth functions which are zero on
\partial\mathcal{D}
, the boundary of
\mathcal{D}
. Then the operator
A:=-\Delta
has a set of orthonormal eigenfunctions
\{e_{k}\}_{k}
spanning
\mathcal{L}^{2}(\mathcal{D})
, with a corresponding non-decreasing sequence of positive eigenvalues
\{\xi_{k}\}_{k}
satisfying the estimates

C_{\Delta}k^{\frac{2}{d}}\leq\xi_{k}\leq D_{\Delta}k^{\frac{2}{d}}\,,

for constants
C_{\Delta}>0
and
D_{\Delta}>0
.

This lemma can be used to show that the operators in (
2
) satisfy Assumption
2.1
with
r_{\mu}=\frac{2\alpha}{d}
, and
r_{\lambda}=-\frac{2\beta}{d}
. The criterion
1+r_{\lambda}-r_{\mu}(2\gamma-1)<0
for existence of solutions to (
3
) then becomes
\beta+(2\gamma-1)\alpha-\frac{d}{2}>0
. In addition we can use
[
29
, Theorem 3.12]
to find that
\{X(t)\}_{t\in[0,T]}
has at most
\beta+(2\gamma-1)\alpha-\frac{d}{2}
spatial mean-square derivatives and at most
\gamma-\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2\alpha}\min(\beta-\frac{d}{2},0)
temporal mean-square derivatives.

Corollary 3.3
.

Let
\mathcal{D}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a bounded domain. Assume
\alpha>0
,
\beta>0
,
\kappa>0
,
r>0
,
\sigma>0
, and
\gamma>\frac{1}{2}
. Let
A=r^{-1}(\kappa^{2}-\Delta)^{\alpha}
and
Q=\sigma^{2}r^{-2\gamma}(\kappa^{2}-\Delta)^{-\beta}
and assume that
\{X(t)\}_{t\in[0,T]}
satisfies (
3
). Assume further that
\beta+(2\gamma-1)\alpha-\frac{d}{2}>0
. Then for all
t\in[0,T]

\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|X(t)-\tilde{X}_{M}(t)\right\|^{2}\right]\leq CM^{-\frac{2\nu}{d}}\,,

where
C=\frac{\sigma r^{\gamma-1}\Gamma(2\gamma-1)}{C_{\Delta}^{\nu+\frac{d}{2}}\Gamma(\gamma)^{2}}\frac{d}{2\nu}
, and
\nu=\beta+(2\gamma-1)\alpha-\frac{d}{2}
.

Proof.

By Theorem
3.2

-\Delta
has an orthonormal basis of eigenfunctions
\{e_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
with corresponding eigenvalues
\{\xi_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
, satisfying
C_{\Delta}k^{\frac{2}{d}}\leq\xi_{k}
by Theorem
3.2
. Thus
A
and
Q
have eigenvalues
\mu_{k}=r^{-1}(\kappa^{2}+\xi_{k})^{\alpha}
and
\lambda_{k}=\sigma r^{-\gamma}(\kappa^{2}+\xi_{k})^{-\beta}
and
\{\mu_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
and
\{\lambda_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
satisfy the bounds

and

A

with

,

,

, and

3.1
to conclude.
∎

Remark 3.9

3.4.
Convergence of the full discretisation

We can derive an error bound for the full discretisation by combining Theorem
3.1
and Theorem
3.8
. A proof can found in Section
5
.

Theorem 3.14
.

Assume that Assumption
2.1
and Assumption
3.7
holds and that
\{X(t)\}_{t\in[0,T]}
satisfies (
3
). Assume
1+r_{\lambda}-(2\gamma-1)r_{\mu}<0
and let
t\in[0,T]
, and
\gamma>\frac{1}{2}
. Then

\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|X(t)-\tilde{X}^{h}_{M}(t)\right\|^{2}\right]\leq C^{{}^{\prime}}M^{-\frac{2\nu}{d}}+C^{{}^{\prime\prime}}_{M}\delta_{\gamma,h}h^{\min(2\gamma-1,2m+2)}\,,

where
C^{{}^{\prime}}=\frac{C_{2}\Gamma(2\gamma-1)}{C_{1}^{2\gamma-1}\Gamma(\gamma)^{2}(1+r_{\lambda}-(2\gamma-1)r_{\mu})}
, and
C^{{}^{\prime\prime}}_{M}=C(C_{\Pi},t,\gamma)\sum_{k=1}^{M}\lambda_{k}
, and
\nu=-\frac{d}{2}(1+r_{\lambda}-(2\gamma-1)r_{\mu})
, and
\delta_{\gamma,h}
is as defined in Theorem
3.8
. If in addition the assumptions of Theorem
3.8
b) holds, then
C^{{}^{\prime\prime}}_{M}=\frac{C}{\Gamma(\gamma)^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{M}\mu_{k}^{-\frac{2}{q}}\lambda_{k}
.

Theorem
3.14
gives us the convergence of the full discretisation under reasonably general assumptions. The fact that
C_{M}^{{}^{\prime\prime}}
is potentially unbounded in
M
is a difficulty. In the case where
\text{Tr}(Q)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{k}<\infty
we can bound
C_{M}^{{}^{\prime\prime}}
by
\text{Tr}(Q)
. If we have enough temporal smoothness, i.e., if
\gamma
is large enough, we can use the second part of Theorem
3.14
to get the improved constant
C_{M}^{{}^{\prime\prime}}=\frac{C}{\Gamma(\gamma)^{2}}\sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^{M}\mu_{k}^{-\frac{2}{q}}\lambda_{k}}
, allowing for a more degenerate
Q
. In many practical applications
A^{-1}
will be trace-class, i.e.,
\sum_{k=1}^{M}\mu_{k}^{-1}<\infty
, so that in the extreme case of
\gamma>m+\frac{3}{2}+\frac{1}{2}
we have convergence of
\sum_{k=1}^{M}\mu_{k}^{-1}\lambda_{k}
assuming only that
Q
is bounded. Of course, we have two degrees of freedom, so even if
C_{M}^{{}^{\prime\prime}}
is not bounded in
M
the full discretisation still convergences, since we can first control the spatial error
C^{{}^{\prime}}M^{-\frac{2\nu}{d}}
by making
M
large and then control the temporal error
C^{{}^{\prime\prime}}_{M}\delta_{\gamma,h}h^{\min(2m+2,2\gamma-1)}
by making
h
small. The following corollary tells us how small
h
must be in order to achieve the optimal spatial convergence order
M^{-\frac{2\nu}{d}}
. A proof can found in Section
5
.

Corollary 3.15
.

Let the assumptions of Theorem
3.14
hold. Then choosing
h=M^{-\zeta}
, where

\zeta=\begin{cases}r_{\mu}-\min(0,\frac{\delta}{b})&\text{when}\,\gamma\leq m+2\,,\\[5.69046pt]
\frac{2\gamma-2}{2m+2}r_{\mu}-\min(0,\frac{\delta}{b})&\text{when}\,\gamma>m+2\,,\end{cases}

where
b=\min(2\gamma-1,2m+2)
and

\delta=\begin{cases}1+r_{\lambda}&\text{when}\,\gamma\in(\frac{1}{2},m+\frac{3}{2}]\,,\\
1+r_{\lambda}-(2\gamma-2m-3)r_{\mu}&\text{when}\,\gamma\in(m+\frac{3}{2},m+2]\,,\\
1+r_{\lambda}-r_{\mu}&\text{when}\,\gamma\in(m+2,\infty)\,,\end{cases}

then

\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|X(t)-\tilde{X}^{h}_{M}(t)\right\|^{2}\right]\lesssim M^{-\frac{2\nu}{d}}\,,

where
\nu
is as defined in Theorem
3.1
and depends on
\gamma
,
r_{\lambda}
, and
r_{\mu}
. In the special case of the operators in we have
r_{\mu}=\frac{2\alpha}{d}
and
r_{\lambda}=-\frac{2\beta}{d}
.

3.5.
Computational complexity and cost

We are also interested in the computational complexity of the method. Consider a set of times
t_{1}^{*},t_{2}^{*},...,t_{J}^{*}
, on which we are interested in simulating
X(t)
. We then apply our method on a potentially finer mesh
t_{1},t_{2},...t_{N}
. Simulating the stochastic integrals in (
8
) and computing the sum in (
7
) then takes
Mnm^{3}\leq MNm^{3}
operations, so computing
X_{M}^{h}(t_{\ell})
for each
\ell=0,...,J
has complexity
O(JNMm^{3})
, or
O(JNM)
if we consider the method order
m
as fixed. We can use this bound on the complexity and Corollary
3.15
to derive another bound on the computational complexity in terms of the number of operations needed required to achieve an error of
\epsilon
. A proof can found in Section
5
.

Corollary 3.16
.

Let the assumptions of Theorem
3.14
hold, and let
J
be as defined above. The computational cost
\mathcal{C}
required to achieve an error
\epsilon:=\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|X(t)-\tilde{X}^{h}_{M}(t)\right\|^{2}\right]
can be bounded by

(12)

\mathcal{C}\lesssim J\epsilon^{-\frac{d}{2\nu}(1+\zeta)}\,,

where
\zeta
is as defined in Corollary
3.15
, and
\nu
is as defined in Theorem
3.1
(alternatively Corollary
3.3
).

For comparison, the ’naive’ method of using a Cholesky decomposition of the covariance matrix to simulate (
5
) has a complexity of
O(J^{3}M)
. The cost to achieve an error of
\epsilon
is then

\mathcal{C}_{\text{chol}}\lesssim J^{3}M\lesssim J^{3}\epsilon^{-\frac{d}{2\nu}}\,.

This highlights both a strength and a weakness of the proposed method. If we consider
J
fixed, then as the acceptable error
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
, Cholesky decomposition will eventually be cheaper than our method, due to the additional factor of
1+\zeta>1
in the exponent in (
12
). However, our method scales linearly in
J
, so if we fix an error
\epsilon
that we deem ’acceptable’, then as
J\rightarrow\infty
our method will eventually be cheaper than Cholesky decomposition. This improvement is sufficient for our method to allow for simulation for much larger
J
’s compared to what is feasible with the standard method. Note for example that in Section
4.1
below we simulate our reference solution on a reference mesh of
2^{17}=131072
nodes. This would have been completely unfeasible using Cholesky decomposition.

Remark 3.17
.

Its likely possible to get complexity
O(JM)
by replacing
\Sigma_{\ell=1}^{n}
with
\Sigma_{\ell=\max(1,n-d)}^{n}
in (
7
) for some fixed lag
d
. Bounding the error of this additional approximation is beyond the scope of this work.

4.
Numerical examples

We now conduct numerical experiments to demonstrate the convergence rate of our method. We first test the temporal convergence order established in Theorem
3.8
. We consider two specific methods: the one described in Corollary
3.13
with
\theta=0
, and the one described in Corollary
3.10
with
m=1
. For the purpose of the numerical experiment we refer to these two methods respectively as the left-point method and the projection method. We also use a numerical experiment to demonstrate the spectral convergence order established in Theorem
3.8
. For this purpose we use the domain
[0,1]^{2}
and spatial operators
A=r^{-1}(\kappa^{2}-\Delta)^{\alpha}
and
Q=\sigma^{2}r^{-2\gamma}(\kappa^{2}-\Delta)^{-\beta}
based on the Laplacian. We also do some simulations on the unit sphere and use these simulations to demonstrate our ability to control the spatial and temporal smoothness separately.

Throughout the numerical experiment we will make use of the reparametrisation suggested in
[
39
]
. This reparametrisation specifies
A
and
Q
through the spatial smoothness
\nu_{s}>0
, the temporal smoothness
\nu_{t}>0
, the spatial range
r_{s}>0
, the temporal range
r_{t}>0
, and a “non-separability” parameter
0\leq\beta_{s}\leq 1
. These parameters are given by

\nu_{s}=\beta+(2\gamma-1)\alpha-\frac{d}{2},\quad\nu_{t}=\gamma-\frac{1}{2}+{\frac{1}{2\alpha}}\min\left(\beta-\frac{d}{2},0\right),\quad\beta_{s}=\frac{(2\gamma-1)\alpha}{\beta+(2\gamma-1)\alpha},

r_{s}=\kappa^{-1}\sqrt{8\nu_{s}},\quad r_{t}=r\kappa^{-2\alpha}\sqrt{8\left(\gamma-\frac{1}{2}\right)}\,,

or alternatively

(13)

\gamma=\nu_{t}\max\left(1,\frac{\beta_{s}}{\beta^{*}}\right)+\frac{1}{2},\quad\alpha=\frac{\nu_{s}}{2\nu_{t}}\min\left(1,\frac{\beta_{s}}{\beta^{*}}\right),\quad\beta=\frac{1-\beta_{s}}{\beta^{*}}\nu_{s}\,,

\kappa=\frac{\sqrt{8\nu_{s}}}{r_{s}},\quad r=\frac{r_{t}\kappa^{2\alpha}}{\sqrt{8(\gamma-\frac{1}{2})}}\,,

where
\beta^{*}=\frac{\nu_{s}}{\nu_{s}+\frac{d}{2}}
. The parameter
\sigma
still controls the variance scaling.

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

Let
N>M
. Clearly
\tilde{X}_{N}(t)-\tilde{X}_{M}(t)=\sum_{k=M+1}^{N}c_{k}(t)e_{k}
. Using Lemma
2.2
, the Itô isometry, the definition of the gamma function, Assumption
2.1
, and
1+r_{\lambda}-(2\gamma-1)r_{\mu}<0
, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\tilde{X}_{N}(t)-\tilde{X}_{M}(t)\right\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\sum_{k=M+1}^{N}c_{k}(t)e_{k}\right\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{k=M+1}^{N}\left\|\frac{\sqrt{\lambda_{k}}}{\Gamma(\gamma)}\int_{0}^{t}\textup{e}^{-\mu_{k}(t-s)}(t-s)^{\gamma-1}\text{d}\omega_{k}(s)e_{k}\right\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\gamma)^{2}}\sum_{k=M+1}^{N}\lambda_{k}\int_{0}^{t}\textup{e}^{-2\mu_{k}(t-s)}(t-s)^{2\gamma-2}\,\text{d}s

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\gamma)^{2}}\sum_{k=M+1}^{N}\frac{\lambda_{k}}{(2\mu_{k})^{2\gamma-1}}\int_{0}^{2\mu_{k}t}\textup{e}^{-u}u^{2\gamma-2}\,\text{d}u

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\Gamma(2\gamma-1)}{\Gamma(\gamma)^{2}}\sum_{k=M+1}^{N}\lambda_{k}\mu_{k}^{1-2\gamma}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C_{2}}{C_{1}^{2\gamma-1}}\frac{\Gamma(2\gamma-1)}{\Gamma(\gamma)^{2}}\sum_{k=M+1}^{N}k^{r_{\lambda}-(2\gamma-1)r_{\mu}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C_{2}C_{1}^{1-2\gamma}\Gamma(2\gamma-1)}{\Gamma(\gamma)^{2}((2\gamma-1)r_{\mu}-r_{\lambda}-1)}M^{1+r_{\lambda}-(2\gamma-1)r_{\mu}}\,,

so
\{X_{M}(t)\}_{M=1}^{\infty}
is a Cauchy sequence in
\mathcal{L}^{2}(\Omega,\mathcal{L}^{2}(\mathcal{D}))
. It follows that
X(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}c_{k}(t)e_{k}\in\mathcal{L}^{2}(\Omega,\mathcal{L}^{2}(\mathcal{D}))
. By taking the limit as
N\rightarrow\infty
we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|X(t)-\tilde{X}_{M}(t)\right\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C_{2}C_{1}^{1-2\gamma}\Gamma(2\gamma-1)}{\Gamma(\gamma)^{2}((2\gamma-1)r_{\mu}-r_{\lambda}-1)}M^{1+r_{\lambda}-(2\gamma-1)r_{\mu}}\,.\qed

To prove Theorem
3.8
we first need a preliminary lemma.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
t\in[0,T]
,
\mu_{k}>0
,
\gamma>\frac{1}{2}
, and
{\rho}\in\mathcal{L}^{2}([0,t))
. Let
\{\omega_{k}(s)\}_{s\in[0,T]}
be an
\mathbb{R}
-valued, standard Wiener-process. Then

\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\int_{0}^{t}\textup{e}^{-\mu_{k}(t-s)}((t-s)^{\gamma-1}-{\rho}(s))\text{d}\omega_{k}(s)\right|^{2}\right]\leq\left\|(t-s)^{\gamma-1}-{\rho}(s)\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}([0,t))}^{2}\,.

In addition for
p\in(2,\infty]
we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\int_{0}^{t}\textup{e}^{-\mu_{k}(t-s)}((t-s)^{\gamma-1}-{\rho}(s))\text{d}\omega_{k}(s)\right|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\left(q\mu_{k}\right)^{-\frac{2}{q}}\left\|(t-s)^{\gamma-1}-{\rho}(s)\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{p}([0,t))}^{2}\,,

where
q\in[2,\infty)
is such that
\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}=\frac{1}{2}
.

Proof.

By the Itô isometry
[
18
, Theorem 4.27]

\displaystyle\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\int_{0}^{t}\textup{e}^{-\mu_{k}(t-s)}((t-s)^{\gamma-1}-{\rho}(s))\text{d}\omega_{k}(s)\right|^{2}\right]\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\left(\int_{0}^{t}\textup{e}^{-2\mu_{k}(t-s)}((t-s)^{\gamma-1}-{\rho}(s))^{2}\text{d}s\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\left\|\textup{e}^{-\mu_{k}(t-s)}((t-s)^{\gamma-1}-{\rho}(s))\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}([0,t))}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\,\left\|\textup{e}^{-\mu_{k}(t-s)}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{q}([0,t))}\left\|(t-s)^{\gamma-1}-{\rho}(s)\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{p}([0,t))}\,,

where
\frac{1}{q}+\frac{1}{p}=\frac{1}{2}
for
p,q\in[2,\infty]
. For
q=\infty
and
p=2
we have
\left\|\textup{e}^{-\mu_{k}(t-s)}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{q}([0,t))}=1
and thus

\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\int_{0}^{t}\textup{e}^{-\mu_{k}(t-s)}((t-s)^{\gamma-1}-{\rho}(s))\text{d}\omega_{k}(s)\right|^{2}\right]\leq\left\|(t-s)^{\gamma-1}-{\rho}(s)\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}([0,t))}^{2}\,.

For
q<\infty
we calculate

\displaystyle\left\|\textup{e}^{-\mu_{k}(t-s)}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{q}([0,t))}

\displaystyle=\left(\int_{0}^{t}\textup{e}^{-q\mu_{k}(t-s)}\,\text{d}s\right)^{\frac{1}{q}}=\left(\frac{1}{q\mu_{k}}(1-\textup{e}^{-q\mu_{k}t})\right)^{\frac{1}{q}}\leq(q\mu_{k})^{-1/q}\,,

so that

\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\int_{0}^{t}\textup{e}^{-\mu_{k}(t-s)}((t-s)^{\gamma-1}-{\rho}(s))\text{d}\omega_{k}(s)\right|^{2}\right]\leq\left(q\mu_{k}\right)^{-\frac{2}{q}}\left\|(t-s)^{\gamma-1}-{\rho}(s)\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{p}([0,t))}^{2}\,.

∎

Proof of Theorem
3.8
.

We divide the proof into four parts. In Part 1 we consider the case where
\gamma>m+\frac{3}{2}
. In Part 2, we prove the second part of the theorem where
\gamma>m+\frac{3}{2}+\frac{1}{q}
. In Part 3 we consider the case
\gamma<m+\frac{3}{2}
. Finally, in Part 4, we consider the case
\gamma=m+\frac{3}{2}
.

Part 1:
Assume
\gamma>m+\frac{3}{2}
, then
f_{t}\big|_{[t_{\ell-1},t_{\ell}]\cap[0,t)}\in\mathcal{H}^{m+1}([t_{\ell-1},t_{\ell})\cap[0,t))
, where
\mathcal{H}^{m+1}
is the
(m+1)
th Sobolev space with
p=2
. We can then apply the Bramble-Hilbert lemma
[
47
, Chapter 3, Remark 2]
, to show that for each
\ell=1,2,...N
there exists a polynomial
g_{\ell}
of degree
m
satisfying

\left\|f_{t}-g_{\ell}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}([t_{\ell-1},t_{\ell}])}\leq C\left\|f\right\|_{\mathcal{H}^{m+1}([t_{\ell-1},t_{\ell}])}(t_{\ell}-t_{\ell-1})^{m+1}\,.

Define
g:[0,t)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
to be the function that is equal to
g_{\ell}
when restricted to
[t_{\ell-1},t_{\ell})\cap[0,t)
. Then
\left\|f_{t}-g\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}([0,t))}\leq C\left\|f\right\|_{\mathcal{H}^{m+1}([0,t))}h^{m+1}
. Further

\left\|f_{t}-\Pi_{m}f_{t}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}([0,t))}\leq\left\|f_{t}-g\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}([0,t))}+\left\|g-\Pi_{m}g\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}([0,t))}+\left\|\Pi_{m}(g-f_{t})\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}([0,t))}\,.

Since
g\in\mathcal{P}_{m}(\mathcal{I})
we have that
\Pi_{m}g=g
, so that

\displaystyle\left\|f_{t}-\Pi_{m}f_{t}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}([0,t))}

\displaystyle\leq(1+C_{\Pi})\left\|f_{t}-g\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}([0,t))}

\displaystyle\leq C(1+C_{\Pi})\left\|f_{t}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}^{m+1}([0,t))}h^{m+1}\,.

By the first part of Lemma
5.1

\displaystyle\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\left|c_{k}(t)-\tilde{c}^{(m)}_{k}(t)\right|^{2}\right]\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{\lambda_{k}}}{\Gamma(\gamma)}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\int_{0}^{t}\textup{e}^{-\mu_{k}(t-s)}((t-s)^{\gamma-1}-\Pi_{m}f_{t}(s))\,\text{d}\omega_{k}(s)\right|\right]^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{\lambda_{k}}}{\Gamma(\gamma)}\left\|f_{t}-\Pi_{m}f_{t}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}([0,t))}

\displaystyle\leq C\frac{\sqrt{\lambda_{k}}}{\Gamma(\gamma)}(1+C_{\Pi})\left\|f_{t}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}^{m+1}([0,t))}h^{m+1}

Part 2:
Assume
\gamma>m+\frac{3}{2}+\frac{1}{q}
, then
\gamma>m+2-\frac{1}{p}
, where
\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}=\frac{1}{2}
. Thus
f_{t}\big|_{[t_{\ell-1},t_{\ell}]\cap[0,t)}\in\mathcal{W}^{m+1,p}([t_{\ell-1},t_{\ell})\cap[0,t))
, where
\mathcal{W}^{m+1,p}
is the
(m+1)
th order Sobolev space, so we can modify the proof by using the Bramble-Hilbert lemma on
\mathcal{L}^{p}([0,t))
above to get

\displaystyle\left\|f_{t}-\Pi_{m}f_{t}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{p}([0,t))}

\displaystyle\leq C(1+C_{\Pi})h^{m+1}\left\|f_{t}\right\|_{\mathcal{W}^{m+1,p}([0,t))}\,.

Thus by the second part of Lemma
5.1

\displaystyle\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\left|c_{k}(t)-\tilde{c}^{(m)}_{k}(t)\right|^{2}\right]\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\sqrt{\lambda_{k}}\mu_{k}^{-\frac{1}{q}}}{\Gamma(\gamma)}\left\|f_{t}-\Pi_{m}f_{t}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{p}([0,t))}

\displaystyle\leq C\frac{\sqrt{\lambda_{k}}\mu_{k}^{-\frac{1}{q}}}{\Gamma(\gamma)}(1+C_{\Pi}^{p})\left\|f_{t}\right\|_{\mathcal{W}^{m+1,p}([0,t))}h^{m+1}.

Part 3:
Assume
\gamma<m+\frac{3}{2}
. Let
\epsilon>0
and define
T_{m}(s):=\sum_{k=0}^{m}\frac{1}{k!}f_{t}^{(k)}(t-\epsilon)(s-t+\epsilon)^{k}
; the Taylor polynomial expansion of
f_{t}
around
t-\epsilon
. Further we define
f_{t}^{\epsilon}:[0,t)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
as

f_{t}^{\epsilon}(s):=\begin{cases}(t-s)^{\gamma-1},\quad&\text{if}\quad s<t-\epsilon,\\
T_{m}(s),\quad&\text{else.}\end{cases}

We then consider the approximation

\left\|f_{t}-g\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}([0,t))}\leq\left\|f_{t}-f_{t}^{\epsilon}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}([0,t))}+\left\|f_{t}^{\epsilon}-g\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}([0,t))}=(I)+(II)\,,

for some
g\in\mathcal{P}_{m}(\mathcal{I})
. For
s\in[t-\epsilon,t)
the Schlömilch form of the Taylor remainder is
f_{t}(s)-f_{t}^{\epsilon}(s)=f_{t}(s)-T_{m}(s)=\frac{f_{t}^{(m+1)}(\xi)}{pm!}(s-\xi)^{m+1-p}(s-t+\epsilon)^{p}
, where
\xi\in(t-\epsilon,s)
and
p>0

[
5
, p.66]
. The
(m+1)
th derivative of
f_{t}
is
f_{t}^{(m+1)}(s)=K_{m+1}(t-s)^{\gamma-2-m}
, where
K_{m+1}:=(-1)^{m+1}\Pi_{i=1}^{m+1}(\gamma-i)
. We now fix
\delta>0
and select
p=\gamma-\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\delta}{2}
, where
\delta\in(0,1)
is chosen such that
p>0
as required. We then obtain the following expression for the error
f_{t}(s)-f_{t}^{\epsilon}(s)
:

\displaystyle f_{t}(s)-f_{t}^{\epsilon}(s)

\displaystyle=\frac{K_{m+1}(t-\xi)^{\gamma-2-m}}{pm!}(s-\xi)^{m+1-p}(s-t+\epsilon)^{p}

\displaystyle=\frac{K_{m+1}(t-\xi)^{\gamma-2-m}}{(\gamma-\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\delta}{2})m!}(s-\xi)^{m+\frac{3}{2}-\gamma+\frac{\delta}{2}}(s-t+\epsilon)^{\gamma-\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\delta}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{K_{m+1}(t-\xi)^{\gamma-2-m}}{(\gamma-\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\delta}{2})m!}(t-\xi)^{m+\frac{3}{2}-\gamma+\frac{\delta}{2}}\epsilon^{\gamma-\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\delta}{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{K_{m+1}}{(\gamma-\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\delta}{2})m!}(t-\xi)^{-\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\delta}{2}}\epsilon^{\gamma-\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\delta}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{K_{m+1}}{(\gamma-\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\delta}{2})m!}(t-s)^{-\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\delta}{2}}\epsilon^{\gamma-\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\delta}{2}}

The bound
(s-t+\epsilon)^{\gamma-\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\delta}{2}}<\epsilon^{\gamma-\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\delta}{2}}
is justified by
s-t+\epsilon<t-t+\epsilon=\epsilon
and the fact that
\gamma-\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\delta}{2}=p>0
. The bound
(s-\xi)^{m+\frac{3}{2}-\gamma+\frac{\delta}{2}}<(t-\xi)^{m+\frac{3}{2}-\gamma+\frac{\delta}{2}}
is justified by
s-\xi<t-\xi
and the fact that
m+\frac{3}{2}-\gamma+\frac{\delta}{2}>0
since
\gamma-\frac{\delta}{2}<\gamma<m+\frac{3}{2}
by assumption. Finally
(t-\xi)^{-\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\delta}{2}}<(t-s)^{-\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\delta}{2}}
is justified by
t-\xi>t-s
together with the fact that
-\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\delta}{2}<0
. We are now ready to approximate
(I)
:

\displaystyle\left\|f_{t}-f_{t}^{\epsilon}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}([0,t))}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{t-\epsilon}^{t}\left(\frac{K_{m+1}}{(\gamma-\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\delta}{2})m!}\right)^{2}(t-s)^{-1+\delta}\epsilon^{2\gamma-1-\delta}\,\text{d}s

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\delta}\left(\frac{K_{m+1}}{(\gamma-\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\delta}{2})m!}\right)^{2}\epsilon^{2\gamma-1}\,.

If we optimize for the smallest possible constant in terms of
\delta
we get the bound

(I)=\left\|f_{t}-f_{t}^{\epsilon}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}([0,t))}\leq\frac{C_{op}(\gamma)K_{m+1}}{m!}\epsilon^{\gamma-\frac{1}{2}}\,,

where

C_{op}(\gamma):=\begin{cases}\sqrt{\frac{27}{8}}(\gamma-\frac{1}{2})^{-\frac{3}{2}},\quad\text{if}\quad\frac{1}{2}<\gamma\leq 2\\
(\gamma-1)^{-1},\quad\text{if}\quad\gamma>2\end{cases}\,.

Further the
(m+1)
th order weak derivative of
f_{t}^{\epsilon}
can be represented by

\frac{\text{d}^{m+1}}{\text{d}s^{m+1}}f_{t}^{\epsilon}(s):=\begin{cases}K_{m+1}(t-s)^{\gamma-2-m},\quad\text{if}\quad s<t-\epsilon\\
0,\quad\text{else}\end{cases}\,,

where
K_{m+1}:=(-1)^{m+1}\Pi_{i=1}^{m+1}(\gamma-i)
. Since
\gamma<m+\frac{3}{2}
by assumption, it then follows that

\displaystyle\left\|f_{t}^{\epsilon}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}^{m+1}([0,t))}^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t-\epsilon}K_{m+1}^{2}(t-s)^{2\gamma-2m-4}\,\text{d}s

\displaystyle=\frac{K_{m+1}^{2}}{2m+3-2\gamma}(\epsilon^{2\gamma-2m-3}-t^{2\gamma-2m-3})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{K_{m+1}^{2}}{2m+3-2\gamma}\epsilon^{2\gamma-2m-3}<\infty\,,

where the last bound is possible due to the fact that
2m+3-2\gamma>0
since by assumption
\gamma<m+\frac{3}{2}
. Thus
f_{t}^{\epsilon}\in\mathcal{H}^{m+1}([0,t))
. We can therefore apply the Bramble-Hilbert lemma as we did above to approximate
f_{t}^{\epsilon}
by a piecewise polynomial function
g^{\epsilon}\in\mathcal{P}_{m}(\mathcal{I})
. Choosing
g=g^{\epsilon}
we can therefore approximate
(II)
by

\displaystyle(II)=\left\|f_{t}^{\epsilon}-g^{\epsilon}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}([0,t))}

\displaystyle\leq Ch^{m+1}\left\|f_{t}^{\epsilon}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}^{m+1}([0,t))}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C\left|K_{m+1}\right|}{(2m+3-2\gamma)^{\frac{1}{2}}}\epsilon^{\gamma-m-\frac{3}{2}}h^{m+1}\,.

Putting
(I)
and
(II)
together and selecting
\epsilon=h
we then get

\displaystyle\left\|f_{t}-g^{\epsilon}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}([0,t))}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|f_{t}-f_{t}^{\epsilon}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}([0,t))}+\left\|f_{t}^{\epsilon}-g^{\epsilon}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}([0,t))}

\displaystyle\leq\left|K_{m+1}\right|\left(\frac{C_{op}(\gamma)}{m!}+\frac{C}{(2m+3-2\gamma)^{\frac{1}{2}}}\right)h^{\gamma-\frac{1}{2}}\,.

Further we can approximate

\displaystyle\left\|f_{t}-\Pi_{m}f_{t}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}([0,t))}

\displaystyle\leq(1+C_{\Pi})\left\|f_{t}-g^{\epsilon}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}([0,t))}

\displaystyle\leq(1+C_{\Pi})K_{m+1}\left(\frac{C_{op}(\gamma)}{m!}+\frac{C}{(2m+3-2\gamma)^{\frac{1}{2}}}\right)h^{\gamma-\frac{1}{2}}\,.

Thus again by Lemma
5.1

\displaystyle\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\left|c_{k}(t)-\tilde{c}^{(m)}_{k}(t)\right|^{2}\right]\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\sqrt{\lambda_{k}}}{\Gamma(\gamma)}\left\|f_{t}-\Pi_{m}f_{t}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}([0,t))}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\sqrt{\lambda_{k}}}{\Gamma(\gamma)}(1+C_{\Pi})\left|K_{m+1}\right|\left(\frac{C_{op}(\gamma)}{m!}+\frac{C}{(2m+3-2\gamma)^{\frac{1}{2}}}\right)h^{\gamma-\frac{1}{2}}

Part 4:
Assume
\gamma=m+\frac{3}{2}
. We then repeat the proof for Case 3, but for
(II)
we calculate

\displaystyle\left\|f_{t}^{\epsilon}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}^{m+1}([0,t))}^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t-\epsilon}K_{m+1}^{2}(t-s)^{-1}\,\text{d}s

\displaystyle=K_{m+1}^{2}\log(t\epsilon^{-1})<\infty\,,

so that
(II)=\left\|f_{t}^{\epsilon}-g^{\epsilon}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}([0,t))}\leq C\left|K_{m+1}\right|\sqrt{\log(t\epsilon^{-1})}h^{m+1}
. Thus, putting
(I)
and
(II)
together and selecting

, we get

\displaystyle\left\|f_{t}-g^{\epsilon}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}([0,t))}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|f_{t}-f_{t}^{\epsilon}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}([0,t))}+\left\|f_{t}^{\epsilon}-g^{\epsilon}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}([0,t))}

\displaystyle\leq\left|K_{m+1}\right|\left(\frac{C_{op}(\gamma)}{m!}+C\sqrt{\log(th^{-1})}\right)h^{m+1}

\displaystyle\leq\left|K_{m+1}\right|\max\left(\frac{C_{op}(\gamma)}{m!},C\right)\left(1+\sqrt{\log(th^{-1})}\right)h^{m+1}\,,

so that

\displaystyle\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\left|c_{k}(t)-\tilde{c}^{(m)}_{k}(t)\right|^{2}\right]\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\sqrt{\lambda_{k}}}{\Gamma(\gamma)}\left\|f_{t}-\Pi_{m}f_{t}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}([0,t))}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\sqrt{\lambda_{k}}}{\Gamma(\gamma)}(1+C_{\Pi})\left|K_{m+1}\right|\max\left(\frac{C_{op}(\gamma)}{m!},C\right)\left(1+\sqrt{\log(th^{-1})}\right)h^{m+1}\,,

∎

Proof of Lemma
3.11
.

First observe that

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}s^{k}(t-s)^{\gamma-1}\text{d}s

\displaystyle=\int_{a}^{b}(t-(t-s))^{k}(t-s)^{\gamma-1}\text{d}s

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\binom{k}{j}(-1)^{j}t^{k-j}\int_{a}^{b}(t-s)^{j}(t-s)^{\gamma-1}\text{d}s

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\binom{k}{j}\frac{(-1)^{j}t^{k-j}}{\gamma+j}\left[(t-a)^{\gamma+j}-(t-b)^{\gamma+j}\right]=i_{k}

Since
\{q_{j}\}_{j=0}^{m}
is a basis for
\mathbb{P}_{m}([a,b))
we can express
p
as
\sum_{j=0}^{m}\beta_{j}q_{j}
. We can then calculate

\displaystyle\beta_{k}=\int_{a}^{b}pq_{k}\text{d}s=\int_{a}^{b}(t-s)^{\gamma-1}q_{k}\text{d}s=\sum_{j=0}^{m}q_{k}^{(j)}\int_{a}^{b}s^{j}(t-s)^{\gamma-1}\text{d}s=\sum_{j=0}^{m}q_{k}^{(j)}i_{j}\,.

Then

p=\sum_{k=0}^{m}\beta_{k}q_{k}=\sum_{k=0}^{m}\left(\sum_{l=0}^{m}q_{k}^{(l)}i_{l}\right)\left(\sum_{j=0}^{m}q_{k}^{(j)}s^{j}\right)=\sum_{j=0}^{m}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{m}\sum_{l=0}^{m}q_{k}^{(l)}q_{k}^{(j)}i_{l}\right)s^{j}=:\sum_{j=0}^{m}\alpha_{j}s^{j}\,,

so that
\alpha_{j}=\sum_{k=0}^{m}\sum_{l=0}^{m}q_{k}^{(l)}q_{k}^{(j)}i_{l}
.
∎

We now turn to proving Lemma
3.12
. To prove this result, we first need another lemma.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
\tilde{s}_{\ell}=t_{\ell-1}+\theta(t_{\ell}-t_{\ell-1})
,
\alpha>0
, and
{\eta}:=\frac{\max_{\ell}(h_{\ell})}{\min_{\ell}(h_{\ell})}
. Fix
t_{n}\in\mathcal{I}
. If
\theta\in[0,1]
then for all
0<\ell<n
we have that

\displaystyle(t_{n}-\tilde{s}_{\ell})^{\alpha}

\displaystyle\leq(t_{n}-t_{\ell-1})^{\alpha}\leq{\eta}^{\alpha}\left(\frac{2}{2-\theta}\right)^{\alpha}(t_{n}-\tilde{s}_{\ell})^{\alpha}

\displaystyle(t_{n}-t_{\ell})^{\alpha}

\displaystyle\leq(t_{n}-\tilde{s}_{\ell})^{\alpha}\leq{\eta}^{\alpha}\left(2-\theta\right)^{\alpha}(t_{n}-t_{\ell})^{\alpha}

Consequently, under stated assumptions, for all
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle(t_{n}-\tilde{s}_{\ell})^{\alpha}

\displaystyle\lesssim(t_{n}-t_{\ell-1})^{\alpha}\lesssim(t_{n}-\tilde{s}_{\ell})^{\alpha}

\displaystyle(t_{n}-t_{\ell})^{\alpha}

\displaystyle\lesssim(t_{n}-\tilde{s}_{\ell})^{\alpha}\lesssim(t_{n}-t_{\ell})^{\alpha}

Proof.

We first assume that
\theta\in[0,1]
and that
0<\ell<n
. It is trivial that
(t_{n}-\tilde{s}_{\ell})^{\alpha}\leq(t_{n}-t_{\ell-1})^{\alpha}
and
(t_{n}-t_{\ell})^{\alpha}\leq(t_{n}-\tilde{s}_{\ell})^{\alpha}
. Since
\ell<n
, then
t_{n}-t_{\ell}>0
, so that

\displaystyle\left(\frac{t_{n}-\tilde{s}_{\ell}}{t_{n}-t_{\ell}}\right)^{\alpha}=\left(\frac{(1-\theta)h_{\ell}+\sum_{i=\ell+1}^{n}h_{i}}{\sum_{i=\ell+1}^{n}h_{i}}\right)^{\alpha}\leq{\eta}^{\alpha}\left(\frac{1-\theta+n-\ell}{n-\ell}\right)^{\alpha}\leq{\eta}^{\alpha}\left(2-\theta\right)^{\alpha}\,.

It follows that
(t_{n}-\tilde{s}_{\ell})^{\alpha}\leq{\eta}^{\alpha}\left(2-\theta\right)^{\alpha}(t_{n}-t_{\ell})^{\alpha}
. Similarly, since
\ell<n
, then
t_{n}-\tilde{s}_{\ell}>0
, so that

\displaystyle\left(\frac{t_{n}-t_{\ell-1}}{t_{n}-\tilde{s}_{\ell}}\right)^{\alpha}=\left(\frac{\sum_{i=\ell}^{n}h_{i}}{(1-\theta)h_{\ell}+\sum_{i=\ell+1}^{n}h_{i}}\right)^{\alpha}\leq{\eta}^{\alpha}\left(\frac{n-\ell+1}{1-\theta+n-\ell}\right)^{\alpha}\leq{\eta}^{\alpha}\left(\frac{2}{2-\theta}\right)^{\alpha}\,.

It follows that
(t_{n}-t_{\ell-1})^{\alpha}\leq{\eta}^{\alpha}\left(\frac{2}{2-\theta}\right)^{\alpha}(t_{n}-\tilde{s}_{\ell})^{\alpha}
. The conclusion follows.
∎

Proof of Lemma
3.12
.

Let
h(s)=g(s)+\alpha f_{t_{n}}(s)
, where
g\in\mathcal{P}_{0}(\mathcal{I})
, and
f_{t}(s)=(t-s)^{\gamma-1}
. Then
g
is of the form
g(s)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{N}g_{\ell}\mathbb{I}_{[t_{\ell-1},t_{\ell})}(s)
, so that

\left\|g+\alpha f_{t_{n}}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}([0,t_{n}))}^{2}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\left[g_{\ell}^{2}(t_{\ell}-t_{\ell-1})+2\alpha g_{\ell}\int_{t_{\ell-1}}^{t_{\ell}}f_{t_{n}}(s)\,\text{d}s+\alpha^{2}\int_{t_{\ell-1}}^{t_{\ell}}f_{t_{n}}^{2}\,\text{d}s\right]\,.

Also

\left\|\Pi_{0}(g+\alpha f_{t})\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}([0,t_{n}))}^{2}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}(t_{\ell}-t_{\ell-1})\left[g_{\ell}^{2}+2\alpha g_{\ell}(t-\tilde{s}_{\ell})^{\gamma-1}+\alpha^{2}(t-\tilde{s}_{\ell})^{2\gamma-2}\right]\,,

where
\tilde{s}_{\ell}:=t_{\ell-1}+\theta(t_{\ell}-t_{\ell-1})
. We now consider

\displaystyle 2\alpha g_{\ell}(t_{n}-\tilde{s}_{\ell})^{\gamma-1}(t_{\ell}-t_{\ell-1})

\displaystyle=2\alpha g_{\ell}((t_{n}-\tilde{s}_{\ell})^{\gamma-1}(t_{n}-t_{\ell-1})-(t_{n}-\tilde{s}_{\ell})^{\gamma-1}(t_{n}-t_{\ell}))=(*)

If
\ell<n
, then by Lemma
5.2
we have that

(*)\lesssim 2\alpha g_{\ell}((t_{n}-t_{\ell-1})^{\gamma}-(t_{n}-t_{\ell})^{\gamma})=2\alpha g_{\ell}\gamma\int_{t_{\ell-1}}^{t_{\ell}}f_{t_{n}}(s)\,\text{d}s\,.

If
n=\ell
, then
t_{n}-t_{\ell}=0
and thus

(*)=2\alpha g_{\ell}(1-\theta)^{\gamma-1}(t_{n}-t_{n-1})^{\gamma}=2\alpha g_{\ell}(1-\theta)^{\gamma-1}\gamma\int_{t_{n-1}}^{t_{n}}f_{t_{n}}(s)\,\text{d}s\,.

Note that if
\gamma<1
, then we must assume
\theta\neq 1
, else
(1-\theta)^{\gamma-1}
will be undefined. We can use an almost identical argument to show that
\alpha^{2}(t-\tilde{s}_{\ell})^{2\gamma-2}\lesssim\alpha^{2}\int_{t_{\ell-1}}^{t_{\ell}}f_{t}(s)^{2}\,\text{d}s
. Thus, if
\theta\in[0,1]
and
\gamma\leq 1
, or if
\theta\in[0,1)
, we have that

\displaystyle\left\|\Pi_{0}(g+\alpha f_{t_{n}})\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}([0,t_{n}))}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}(t_{\ell}-t_{\ell-1})\left[g_{\ell}^{2}+2\alpha g_{\ell}(t_{n}-\tilde{s}_{\ell})^{\gamma-1}+\alpha^{2}(t_{n}-\tilde{s}_{\ell})^{2\gamma-2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq C_{\Pi}^{2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\left[g_{\ell}^{2}(t_{\ell}-t_{\ell-1})+2\alpha g_{\ell}\int_{t_{\ell-1}}^{t_{\ell}}f_{t_{n}}(s)\,\text{d}s+\alpha^{2}\int_{t_{\ell-1}}^{t_{\ell}}f_{t_{n}}(s)^{2}\,\text{d}s\right]

\displaystyle=C_{\Pi}^{2}\left\|g+\alpha f_{t_{n}}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}([0,t_{n}))}^{2}\,,

where
C_{\Pi}
depends on
\gamma
,
\theta
and
{\eta}=\frac{\max_{\ell}(h_{\ell})}{\min_{\ell}(h_{\ell})}
. ∎

Proof of Corollary
3.15
.

From Theorem
3.14
we know that the error
\epsilon\lesssim M^{-\frac{2\nu}{d}}+C(M,\gamma)h^{b}
, where
b:=\min(2\gamma-1,2m+2)
and

C(M,\gamma)=\begin{cases}\sum_{k=1}^{M}\lambda_{k}&\text{when}\,\gamma\in(\frac{1}{2},m+\frac{3}{2}]\,,\\
\sum_{k=1}^{M}\lambda_{k}\mu_{k}^{-2\gamma+2m+3}&\text{when}\,\gamma\in(m+\frac{3}{2},m+2]\,,\\
\sum_{k=1}^{M}\lambda_{k}\mu_{k}^{-1}&\text{when}\,\gamma\in(m+2,\infty)\,.\end{cases}\,.

Note that
C(M,\gamma)\lesssim\sum_{k=1}^{M}k^{\delta-1}
by Assumption
2.1
. If
\delta<0
this sum converges, so
C(M,\gamma)\lesssim 1
. If
0<\delta<1
, then

C(M,\gamma)\lesssim 1+\int_{1}^{M}k^{\delta-1}=1-\frac{1}{\delta}+\frac{1}{\delta}M^{\delta}\lesssim M^{\delta}\,.

If
\delta=0
, then similarly
C(M,\gamma)\lesssim 1+\ln(M)\lesssim\ln(M)
. Finally if
\delta\geq 1
, then

C(M,\gamma)\lesssim\sum_{k=1}^{M}k^{\delta-1}\leq\sum_{k=1}^{M}M^{\delta-1}=M^{\delta}
. Thus

\epsilon\lesssim\begin{cases}M^{-\frac{2\nu}{d}}+M^{\delta}h^{b}&\text{when}\,\delta>0\,,\\
M^{-\frac{2\nu}{d}}+\ln(M)h^{b}&\text{when}\,\delta=0\,,\\
M^{-\frac{2\nu}{d}}+h^{b}&\text{when}\,\delta<0\,,\\
\end{cases}\,.

Note that we can always achieve the optimal rate
M^{-\frac{2\nu}{d}}
by setting
h=M^{-\zeta}
and choosing
\zeta
appropriately. The optimal choice of
\zeta
is given by

\zeta=\begin{cases}\frac{\delta}{b}+\frac{2\nu}{db}&\text{when}\,\delta>0\,,\\
\frac{2\nu}{db}&\text{when}\,\delta\leq 0\,,\\
\end{cases}\,.

though note that in the case
\delta=0
this choice of
\zeta
only gives us the optimal rate up to
\ln(M)
. To compute
\zeta
we will consider the cases
\gamma\in(\frac{1}{2},m+\frac{3}{2})
,
\gamma\in(m+\frac{3}{2},m+2]
, and
\gamma\in(m+2,\infty)
separately.
We first consider the case
\gamma\in(\frac{1}{2},m+\frac{3}{2})
, where
\delta=1+r_{\lambda}
. The above then becomes

\displaystyle\zeta

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\frac{1+r_{\lambda}}{2\gamma-1}-\frac{1+r_{\lambda}-(2\gamma-1)r_{\mu}}{2\gamma-1}&\text{when}\,\delta>0\,,\\
-\frac{1+r_{\lambda}-(2\gamma-1)r_{\mu}}{2\gamma-1}&\text{when}\,\delta\leq 0\,,\\
\end{cases}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}r_{\mu}&\text{when}\,\delta>0\,,\\
-\frac{\delta}{b}+r_{\mu}&\text{when}\,\delta\leq 0\,,\\
\end{cases}\,.

Secondly we have the case
\gamma\in(m+\frac{3}{2},m+2)
, where
\delta=1+r_{\lambda}-(2\gamma-2m-3)r_{\mu}
. The above then becomes

\displaystyle\zeta

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\frac{1+r_{\lambda}-(2\gamma-2m-3)r_{\mu}}{2m+2}-\frac{1+r_{\lambda}-(2\gamma-1)r_{\mu}}{2m+2}&\text{when}\,\delta>0\,,\\
-\frac{1+r_{\lambda}-(2\gamma-1)r_{\mu}}{2m+2}&\text{when}\,\delta\leq 0\,,\\
\end{cases}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}r_{\mu}&\text{when}\,\delta>0\,,\\
-\frac{\delta}{b}+r_{\mu}&\text{when}\,\delta\leq 0\,,\\
\end{cases}\,.

Finally we have the case
\gamma\in(m+2,\infty)
, where
\delta=1+r_{\lambda}-r_{\mu}
. The above then becomes

\displaystyle\zeta

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\frac{1+r_{\lambda}-r_{\mu}}{2m+2}-\frac{1+r_{\lambda}-(2\gamma-1)r_{\mu}}{2m+2}&\text{when}\,\delta>0\,,\\
-\frac{1+r_{\lambda}-(2\gamma-1)r_{\mu}}{2m+2}&\text{when}\,\delta\leq 0\,,\\
\end{cases}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\frac{2\gamma-2}{2m+2}r_{\mu}&\text{when}\,\delta>0\,,\\
-\frac{\delta}{b}+\frac{2\gamma-2}{2m+2}r_{\mu}&\text{when}\,\delta\leq 0\,,\\
\end{cases}\,.

We summarize this as

\zeta=\begin{cases}r_{\mu}-\min(0,\frac{\delta}{b})&\text{when}\,\gamma\leq m+2\,,\\
\frac{2\gamma-2}{2m+2}r_{\mu}-\min(0,\frac{\delta}{b})&\text{when}\,\gamma>m+2\,,\end{cases}\,.\qed

Proof of Corollary
3.16
.

We know from Corollary
3.15
that if
h=M^{-\zeta}
then the error
\epsilon\lesssim M^{-\frac{2\nu}{d}}
, or equivalently
\epsilon^{-\frac{d}{2\nu}}\gtrsim M
. Since the complexity is
O(JMN)=O(JMh^{-1})
, we thus have

\mathcal{C}\lesssim JMh^{-1}=JM^{1+\zeta}\lesssim J\epsilon^{-\frac{d}{2\nu}(1+\zeta)}\,.\qed
