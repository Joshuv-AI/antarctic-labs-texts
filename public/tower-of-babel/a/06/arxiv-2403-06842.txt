Hybrid optimal control with mixed-integer Lagrangian methods
Viktoriya Nikitina, Alberto De Marchi, Matthias Gerdts

Abstract
Models involving hybrid systems are versatile in their application but difficult to optimize efficiently due to their combinatorial nature. This work presents a method to cope with hybrid optimal control problems which, in contrast to decomposition techniques, does not require relaxing the integrality constraints. Based on the discretize-then-optimize approach, our scheme addresses mixed-integer nonlinear problems under mild assumptions. The proposed numerical algorithm builds upon the augmented Lagrangian framework, whose subproblems are handled using successive mixed-integer linearizations with trust regions. We validate the performance of the numerical routine with extensive investigations using hybrid optimal control problems from different fields of application. Promising preliminary results are presented for a motion planning task with hysteresis and a Lotka-Volterra fishing problem with total variation.



Keywords.
Hybrid dynamics, Optimal control, Mixed-integer programming, Constrained optimization, Augmented Lagrangian.

We consider hybrid optimal control problems (OCPs), that is,
optimization problems involving possibly
nonsmooth dynamics, mixed-integer states or controls, or logical constraints.
Hybrid OCPs were investigated, e.g., by
[
13
,
11
]

where hybrid dynamics are modeled with disjunctive programming.
An important subclass of hybrid OCPs are mixed-integer OCPs with discrete-valued controls.
Numerical methods often use variable time transformations, relaxation, or decomposition techniques; compare
[
9
,
17
,
2
,
14
]

for important developments.
Such approaches are often tailored to discrete-valued controls and exploit the time structure.

In this work, we permit more general hybrid OCPs and apply a direct discretization method to transform the dynamic optimization problem into a finite dimensional mixed-integer nonlinear program (MINLP).
Our focus is not on the direct discretization method itself,
which exists in various versions,
compare
[
10
]
for an overview,
nor on taking advantage of the time structure.
We investigate instead the numerical viability of an optimization scheme
that handles hybrid features as they stand,
namely without relaxing integrality constraints.
Moreover, since solving these problems to global optimality is in general computationally intractable, we do not seek global solutions and are content with suboptimal, yet practical ones, as in
[
7
,
15
]
.

We study a method for the numerical solution of a class of MINLPs of the following type:

\displaystyle\minimize~

\displaystyle f(x)

\displaystyle\stt~

\displaystyle x\in\mathcal{X},\hskip 10.00002ptc(x)\in\mathcal{C}

(P)

with

and

and
\mathcal{X}
a nonempty closed set with a mixed-integer linear (MIL) structure.
In particular, we assume
\mathcal{X}
to be described by linear inequalities and integrality

\mathcal{X}:=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,\middle|\,\begin{array}[]{l}Ax\leq b,\\
x_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}\quad\forall i\in\mathcal{I}\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}\end{array}\right\}

(1)

2
Optimality Conditions

This section is dedicated to developing suitable solution concepts for (
P
).
First of all, we seek
feasible
points, namely some
\bar{x}\in\mathcal{X}
such that
c(\bar{x})\in\mathcal{C}
.
Then, a
global
minimizer
\bar{x}
for (
P
) can be readily characterized by

\displaystyle\bar{x}\in\mathcal{X},\hskip 10.00002ptc(\bar{x})\in\mathcal{C},\hskip 10.00002pt\forall x\in\mathcal{X},\,c(x)\in\mathcal{C}\colon~f(\bar{x})\leq f(x).

Since we aim at developing an affordable numerical method, possibly at the price of global optimality,
we are interested in
local
optimality notions too.
However, as these are sensitive to how neighborhoods are defined,
local notions can be weak and fragile in the mixed-integer setting considered here.
Indeed, considering simple balls, sufficiently small neighborhoods of feasible
points may contain only one integer configuration,
making it locally optimal, in a weak sense.
To avoid this naive declaration of local optimality,
we develop some stronger conditions by
constructing neighborhoods based on
a polyhedral norm applied to the real-valued components only,
denoted
\|\cdot\|_{\textup{PL}}
as in

[
6
, §2]
.
For instance, it can take on the forms

\displaystyle\|x\|_{\textup{PL}}:={}

\displaystyle\sum\{|x_{i}|\,|\,i\notin\mathcal{I}\}

for

\displaystyle\ell_{1}\text{-type},

\displaystyle\text{or}\hskip 10.00002pt\|x\|_{\textup{PL}}:={}

\displaystyle\max\{|x_{i}|\,|\,i\notin\mathcal{I}\}

for

\displaystyle\ell_{\infty}\text{-type}.

Although not a norm,
\|\cdot\|_{\textup{PL}}
induces compact neighborhoods according to

\mathbb{B}_{\textup{PL}}(\bar{x},\Delta):=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,|\,\|x-\bar{x}\|_{\textup{PL}}\leq\Delta\},

owing to the boundedness of integer-valued variables by
1.1
.
Now, analogously to the “unconstrained” case,
local minimizers for (
P
) can be defined as follows.

Definition 2.1
.

A point
\bar{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is called a
local minimizer

for (
P
) if it is feasible
and
there exists
\Delta>0
such that

f(\bar{x})\leq f(x)
for all feasible
x\in\mathbb{B}_{\textup{PL}}(\bar{x},\Delta)
.
If the latter property additionally holds for all
\Delta>0
, then

\bar{x}
is called a
global minimizer
for (
P
).

Notice that non-polyhedral norms for
\|\cdot\|_{\textup{PL}}
are deliberately avoided so that, in practice, local models of (
P
) take the form of mixed-integer
linear
programs.
However, this specification is not necessary to the definition of suitable neighborhoods nor to identify stronger local minimizers.

We now focus on first-order necessary optimality conditions for (
P
).
These should be useful and practical in order to characterize and numerically detect
points that are (at least) candidates for global (or local) minimizers
[
1
, Chapter 3]
.

2.1
Mixed-integer polyhedral constraints

Let us first consider the minimization of some smooth function
\varphi\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
over
\mathcal{X}
:

\displaystyle\minimize\hskip 10.00002pt

\displaystyle\varphi(x)

\displaystyle\wrt\hskip 10.00002pt

\displaystyle x\in\mathcal{X}.

(2)

Recalling
[
6
, Def. 2.3, Prop. 2.4]
, a first-order optimality measure associated to (
2
)
is defined
for all
\bar{x}\in\mathcal{X}
and
\Delta\geq 0
by

\Psi_{\varphi,\mathcal{X}}(\bar{x},\Delta):=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\mathcal{X}\\
\|x-\bar{x}\|_{\textup{PL}}\leq\Delta\end{subarray}}\langle\nabla\varphi(\bar{x}),\bar{x}-x\rangle\geq 0.

(3)

Then, a first-order optimality concept for (
2
) is that in the following definition,
which also provides an approximate counterpart thereof.

Definition 2.2
.

Given some
\varepsilon\geq 0
,
a point
\bar{x}\in\mathcal{X}
is said
\varepsilon
-critical
for (
2
) if there exists some
\Delta>0
such that

\Psi_{\varphi,\mathcal{X}}(\bar{x},\Delta)\leq\varepsilon
.
A
0
-critical point is simply called
critical
.

As discussed in
[
6
, §2.2]
,
criticality is a necessary optimality condition for (
2
) and, as such, plays a crucial role when it comes to designing an affordable numerical method for solving (
2
).
The denomination
criticality
, in contrast to
stationarity
, emphasizes that in general the former notion implies (hence, is stronger than) the latter.

2.2
Nonlinear constraints

The “unconstrained” notion of
Definition

2.2
is useful to characterize solutions of a problem with simple constraints.
But what is a “critical point” for a problem such as
(
P
)?
A stationarity characterization that resembles, at least in spirit, the classical Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) conditions in nonlinear programming
has been proposed and analysed by
[
4
, §2.2]
.

Let the Lagrangian function
\mathcal{L}\colon\mathcal{X}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}
associated to (
P
) be defined by

\mathcal{L}(x,y):=f(x)+\langle y,c(x)\rangle

(4)

where
y
denotes a Lagrange multiplier.
From the viewpoint of nonlinear programming, where local minima correspond to saddle points of the Lagrangian functions, we consider the following notion for KKT-like points of (
P
),
building upon
Definition

2.2
.

Definition 2.3
.

A point
\bar{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is said
KKT-critical
for (
P
) if

\bar{x}\in\mathcal{X}
and there exists a multiplier
y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and some
\Delta>0
such that

\Psi_{\mathcal{L}(\cdot,y),\mathcal{X}}(\bar{x},\Delta)=0\hskip 20.00003pt\text{and}\hskip 20.00003pty\in\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{C}}(c(\bar{x})).

Notice that KKT criticality demands feasibility since the normal cone
\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{C}}(c(\bar{x}))
to
\mathcal{C}
at
c(\bar{x})
would be empty otherwise.
By (
3
)–(
4
), the first condition in
Definition

2.3

can be rewritten as

\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\mathcal{X}\\
\|x-\bar{x}\|_{\textup{PL}}\leq\Delta\end{subarray}}\langle\nabla f(\bar{x})+c^{\prime}(\bar{x})^{\top}y,x-\bar{x}\rangle=0,

meaning that the Lagrangian function at
\bar{x}
cannot be (locally) further minimized with respect to
x
while maintaining MIL feasibility, in the sense of
Definition

2.2
.
Thanks to the
\|\cdot\|_{\textup{PL}}
-localization, this condition is in general stronger than the inclusion

0\in\nabla_{x}\mathcal{L}(\bar{x},y)+\mathcal{N}^{\textup{lim}}_{\mathcal{X}}(\bar{x})
,
corresponding to mere stationarity,
where
\mathcal{N}^{\textup{lim}}_{\mathcal{X}}(\bar{x})
denotes the limiting normal cone to
\mathcal{X}
at
\bar{x}
.
Equivalence holds when the set
\mathcal{X}
is convex, which is not the case as soon as
\mathcal{I}\neq\emptyset
.

A criterion for numerical termination arises from
relaxing criticality and feasibility requirements
in
Definition

2.3
.
Analogously to
\varepsilon
-criticality of
Definition

2.2
,
we consider the following concept,
akin to
[
5
, Def. 3.2]
.

Definition 2.4
.

Given some
\varepsilon\geq 0
,
a point
\bar{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is said
\varepsilon
-KKT-critical
for (
P
) if
\bar{x}\in\mathcal{X}
and there exists a multiplier
y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, a vector
z\in\mathcal{C}
, and some
\Delta>0
such that

\displaystyle\Psi_{\mathcal{L}(\cdot,y),\mathcal{X}}(\bar{x},\Delta)\leq{}

\displaystyle\varepsilon,

\displaystyle y\in{}

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{C}}(z),

\displaystyle\|c(\bar{x})-z\|\leq{}

\displaystyle\varepsilon.

Consistently with
Definition

2.3
, a
0
-KKT-critical point is KKT-critical.
Possibly under some constraint qualifications (e.g., LICQ), KKT-criticality provides a necessary optimality condition for (
P
).
However, regardless of additional regularity, each local minimizer of (
P
) is
asymptotically
KKT-critical, cf.
[
4
, Thm 2.6]
.

3
Algorithmic Framework

We employ an augmented Lagrangian (AL) scheme to seek a numerical solution for (
P
) under
1.1
.
This design choice is to exploit the fact that the AL framework maintains the original problem’s structure and it does not rely on smoothness of the objective nor on convexity of the feasible set
[
1
]
.
Building upon a sequence of shifted-penalty subproblems,
AL algorithms can be described with a nested structure.
An outer loop generates a sequence of primal-dual estimates,
adapts the penalty parameter,
and monitors convergence toward KKT-critical points.
Each subproblem associated to a set of parameters is addressed by an iterative procedure, effectively an inner loop.
Although the AL framework comprises several algorithms and variants,
all these share a core element:
the successive parametric minimization of an AL function.
The safeguarded AL scheme for (
P
) stated in
Algorithm

3.1

patterns that of
[
1
, §4.1]
and
proceeds with a sequence of subproblems of the form

\displaystyle\minimize\hskip 10.00002pt

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mu}(x,\widehat{y})

\displaystyle\wrt\hskip 10.00002pt

\displaystyle x\in\mathcal{X}

(5)

for some given penalty parameter
\mu>0
and multiplier estimate
\widehat{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
.
Herein, the MIL constraints
x\in\mathcal{X}
are kept implicit, and the AL function is defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mu}(x,\widehat{y}):={}

\displaystyle f(x)+\frac{1}{2\mu}\dist_{\mathcal{C}}^{2}\left(c(x)+\mu\widehat{y}\right)-\frac{\mu}{2}\|\widehat{y}\|^{2},

(6)

cf.
[
5
, §4]
.
Feasibility of (
5
) follows from
\mathcal{X}
being nonempty
whereas well-posedness is due to continuity of
\mathcal{L}_{\mu}(\cdot,\widehat{y})
and can be guaranteed, e.g., by coercivity or (level) boundedness arguments.
Notice that
\mathcal{L}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\mu}
are differentiable with respect to both primal and dual variables, thanks to convexity of
\mathcal{C}
, with derivatives

\displaystyle\nabla_{x}\mathcal{L}_{\mu}(x,\widehat{y})={}

\displaystyle\nabla f(x)+c^{\prime}(x)^{\top}y_{\mu}(x,\widehat{y}),

(7a)

\displaystyle\nabla_{y}\mathcal{L}_{\mu}(x,\widehat{y})={}

\displaystyle c(x)-s_{\mu}(x,\widehat{y})

(7b)

where

\displaystyle s_{\mu}(x,\widehat{y}):={}

\displaystyle\proj_{\mathcal{C}}(c(x)+\mu\widehat{y}),

(8a)

\displaystyle y_{\mu}(x,\widehat{y}):={}

\displaystyle\widehat{y}+\frac{c(x)-s_{\mu}(x,\widehat{y})}{\mu}.

(8b)

Furthermore, note that the input requirement in
Algorithm

3.1
is not a hard restriction:
given some
x^{0}\notin\mathcal{X}
, it is enough to project it onto
\mathcal{X}
by solving a MILP, for instance
\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\|x-x^{0}\|_{1}
.

Input:

x^{0}\in\mathcal{X}
,
y^{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
\epsilon_{\textup{p}},\epsilon_{\textup{d}}>0
,

\mu_{1},\varepsilon_{1}>0
,
\kappa_{\mu},\theta_{\mu},\kappa_{\varepsilon}\in(0,1)
,
\mathcal{Y}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
compact

for

j=1,2\ldots

do

1

\widehat{y}^{j}\leftarrow\proj_{\mathcal{Y}}(y^{j-1})

2

x^{j}\leftarrow
compute an
\varepsilon_{j}
-critical point for
\mathcal{L}_{\mu_{j}}(\cdot,\widehat{y}^{j})
over
\mathcal{X}
starting from
x^{j-1}

3

s^{j}\leftarrow\proj_{\mathcal{C}}(c(x^{j})+\mu_{j}\widehat{y}^{j})

and

v^{j}\leftarrow c(x^{j})-s^{j}

4

y^{j}\leftarrow\widehat{y}^{j}+v^{j}/\mu_{j}

5

if

\varepsilon_{j}\leq\epsilon_{\textup{d}}

and

\|v^{j}\|\leq\epsilon_{\textup{p}}

then

return

(x^{j},y^{j})

6

if

j=1

or

\|v^{j}\|\leq\max\{\epsilon_{\textup{p}},\theta_{\mu}\|v^{j-1}\|\}

then

\mu_{j+1}\leftarrow\mu_{j}
,
else

\mu_{j+1}\leftarrow\kappa_{\mu}\mu_{j}

7

\varepsilon_{j+1}\leftarrow\max\{\epsilon_{\textup{d}},\kappa_{\varepsilon}\varepsilon_{j}\}

8

Algorithm 3.1

Safeguarded augmented Lagrangian routine for (
P
)

The major computational toll is taken by
Algorithm

3.1
, which aims to minimize
\mathcal{L}_{\mu_{j}}(\cdot,\widehat{y}^{j})
over
\mathcal{X}
.
In fact, only approximate criticality is required, and the sequential mixed-integer linearization scheme of
[

can be readily adopted to compute a suitable
\varepsilon_{j}
-critical point

, in the sense of
Definition

2.2
.
Although the performance of the algorithm might be improved by warm-starting the inner solver and guaranteeing descent behaviour,
these properties are not strictly required in the convergence analysis
[
4
]
.
The classical first-order dual estimate update takes place at
Algorithms

3.1
and
3.1
.
Given a (possibly inexact, first-order) solution

5
), the dual update rule in
Algorithm

3.1

is designed toward the identity

\nabla_{x}\mathcal{L}_{\mu}(x,\widehat{y})={}\nabla f(x)+c^{\prime}(x)^{\top}y={}\nabla_{x}\mathcal{L}(x,y),

This relationship allows to monitor the “outer” convergence for (

5
).
Since the inclusion
y\in\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{C}}(s)
in

and
y^{j}
in
Algorithms

and
3.1

.
The dual safeguard takes place in

,
where the compact set
\mathcal{Y}

Remark 3.1
.

A favorable attribute of AL methods is that if the AL subproblems are solved to approximate global optimality, then the iterates accumulate at feasible, globally optimal points

.
Thus, whenever additional knowledge or problem structure allow to solve the AL subproblems to approximate global optimality, this general property of AL methods automatically provides global guarantees for (

4
Numerical Results

In this section, we present and compare the results achieved by the proposed numerical approach for two hybrid optimal control problems.
Before considering the simulations, we highlight some aspects regarding the computational procedure behind
Algorithm

3.1
(ALM).

ALM is initialized with zero dual estimate
y^{0}
, penalty parameter
\mu_{1}=10^{-2}
and inner tolerance
\varepsilon_{1}=10^{-2}
.
The choice of
x^{0}
is based on a more sophisticated approach.
Admissible points for the set
\mathcal{X}
are computed as projections onto it, in the
\ell_{1}
-norm sense.
Another routine was implemented to generate reasonable starting points and to refine solutions provided by ALM.
While integer variables are relaxed in the former case,
they are fixed (to an admissible value) in the latter case so that only real-valued variables are further optimized.
In both scenarios, one ends up with a nonlinear problem, which is then tackled by Ipopt
[
18
]
.
We use the numerical routine of
[
6
, §4]
for executing
Algorithm

3.1
, with Gurobi for solving the MIL subproblems there.

4.1
Car with hysteretic turbo charger and drag

The first problem handled by the proposed method is inspired by one from

[
6
, §4.1]
.
The aim is to perform point-to-point one-dimensional motion planning of a car with hybrid nonlinear dynamics,
extending the previous model to accommodate a drag force term.
The underlying double-integrator point-mass model is equipped with a hysteretic turbo accelerator.
The car is described by its time-dependent position
x(t)
, velocity
v(t)
and a turbo state
w(t)\in\{0,1\}
for each time point
t\in[0,T]
,
T>0
.
The control variables correspond to the inputs to the acceleration
a(t)
and brake
b(t)
pedals.
The turbo mode becomes active or inactive once the velocity exceeds
v^{+}:=10
or falls below the threshold
v^{-}:=5
, respectively.
An activated turbo mode entails an effective increase of the nominal thrust by three times but leaves the braking force unaffected.
These dynamics read

\displaystyle\dot{x}(t)={}

\displaystyle v(t),

\displaystyle\dot{v}(t)={}

\displaystyle\tau(w(t),a(t))-b(t)-c_{\rm d}v(t)^{2}

where
c_{\rm d}\geq 0
denotes the drag coefficient, and
the thrust
\tau
has two modes of operation depending on the turbo state:

\tau(w,a)=a
if
w=0
, and
\tau(w,a)=3a
if
w=1
.
The velocity is limited by
|v(t)|\leq v_{\max}:=25
.
Boundary conditions, state and control bounds, and the minimum-control objective function are chosen following
[
6
, §4.1]
.
The same holds for the hysteresis characteristic describing the turbo behaviour,
whose logical implications are encoded as MIL constraints.

Simulations

Considering the final time
T=10
, the problem is fully discretized using the explicit Euler scheme on a uniform time grid with
N=100

The resulting problem in the form (

) has
100

ALM is initialized with an all-zero primal input.
The solutions returned for different values of the drag coefficient

are displayed in

,

]
for the linear case with

, the turbo car problem was in addition solved by ALM combined with a subsequent fixed-integer NLP refinement to examine the impact of the problem size on the computational effort.

4.2
Lotka-Volterra fishing with total variation

Let us now consider the classical Lotka-Volterra fishing problem complemented with a total variation (
\totalvariation
) term
[
16
]
.
For two states
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
, which can be interpreted as the biomasses of prey and predator populations, respectively, a binary optimal control is sought.
The dynamics are described with the model

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{1}(t)={}

\displaystyle x_{1}(t)-x_{1}(t)x_{2}(t)-c_{1}^{\top}w(t),

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{2}(t)={}

\displaystyle x_{1}(t)x_{2}(t)-x_{2}(t)-c_{2}^{\top}w(t)

for some given parameters
c_{1},c_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{5}
and control inputs
w(t)\in\{0,1\}^{5}
.
For every time
t\in\mathbb{R}
, the pointwise constraint
\sum_{i=1}^{5}w_{i}(t)=1
encodes the choice of one among five control options.
The primary objective is formulated as

J(x):=\min\hskip 10.00002pt\int_{0}^{T}\|x(t)-x_{r}\|^{2}\mathrm{d}t

for tracking the reference value
x_{r}=(1,1)
, starting from the initial state
x_{1}(0)=0.5
,
x_{2}(0)=0.7
and with final time
T=12

[
17
, §5]
.

To limit the well-known chattering behaviour of the optimal control,
we monitor the total variation (in fact, its discrete counterpart, after time discretization)

\totalvariation(w):=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{5}\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}|w_{i,k+1}-w_{i,k}|,

possibly imposing an upper bound
\totalvariation(w)\leq U_{\totalvariation}
or including a penalty term
\alpha_{\totalvariation}\totalvariation(w)
in the objective.
By including additional
(N-1)
real-valued variables and
4(N-1)
linear inequality constraints for each binary-valued control, the nonsmooth
\totalvariation
term can be expressed as a linear function of the unknowns.

Conclusions

This work presents an efficient method to address hybrid optimal control problems without any integrality relaxations.
The proposed approach is based on the augmented Lagrangian framework combined with a routine for nonlinear optimization with mixed-integer linear constraints.
The robustness and performance of the algorithm were assessed on two problem examples from different fields of application, validating the proposed strategy.
Future research should consider the integration of other techniques for handling nonlinear constraints.
