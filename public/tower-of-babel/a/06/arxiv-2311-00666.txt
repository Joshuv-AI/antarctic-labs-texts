Uconnect: Synergistic Spectral CT Reconstruction with U-Nets Connecting the Energy bins
Zhihan Wang, Alexandre Bousse, Franck Vermet, Jacques Froment, Béatrice Vedel, Alessandro Perelli, Jean-Pierre Tasu, Dimitris Visvikis

Abstract
Spectral computed tomography offers the possibility to reconstruct attenuation images at different energy levels, which can be then used for material decomposition. However, traditional methods reconstruct each energy bin individually and are vulnerable to noise. In this paper, we propose a novel synergistic method for spectral computed tomography reconstruction, namely Uconnect. It utilizes trained convolutional neural networks to connect the energy bins to a latent image so that the full binned data is used synergistically. We experiment on two types of low-dose data: simulated and real patient data. Qualitative and quantitative analysis show that our proposed Uconnect outperforms state-of-art model-based iterative reconstruction techniques as well as convolutional neural network-based denoising.



I
Introduction

Spectral computed tomography is fast becoming an important technology in medical imaging for its potential to differentiate between different types of tissues and structures based on their unique spectral properties
[
1
]
. Its applications include lesion detection
[
2
]
, material decomposition
[
3
,
4
]
, automated bone removal
[
5
]
, etc. As an emerging technology to implement photon-counting CT imaging, photon-counting detectors are capable of collecting multiple sets of measured data with different spectral information in a single exposure by distinguishing photon energies during data acquisition. On the other hand, with the increasing number of energy bins, the number of X-ray photons in each energy bin is limited, which reduces signal-to-noise ratio in spectral projections acquired by photon-counting detectors. At the same time efforts to reduce radiation dose, largely motivated by the introduction of potential clinical applications such as lung cancer screening, are associated with decreasing number of projection angles and/or reduced X-ray source flux. In this situation, traditional analytic reconstruction methods such as filtered backprojection will cause severe artifacts and noise. Therefore, many studies have focused on developing methods to improve the reconstruction quality.

Over the past few years, several sparsity-exploiting methods have been applied to low-dose computed tomography reconstruction, such as total variation
[
6
,
7
,
8
]
, dictionary learning
[
9
]
, and total generalized variation
[
10
]
. These methods can be extended to photon-counting CT multi-energy reconstruction by applying the sparse regularization to each energy bin separately. However, this kind of strategy ignores dependencies between images acquired at different energy bins, which may lead to suboptimal results. Many algorithms, thus, have been proposed to take advantage of the similarities between different energy bins.

Synergistic regularization terms have been designed to promote structural similarities across channels. Examples include joint TV
[
11
,
12
]
which promotes joint sparsity of the gradients of images, total nuclear variation
[
13
,
14
]
which encourages gradient-sparse solutions in the same way as the conventional total variation and also favors solutions that have common edge directions in all channels, and parallel level sets
[
15
]
which promotes images with similar contours. In addition, the low-rank property is often used as a prior assumption to exploit the correlation among different energy bins. Specifically, the spectral computed tomography data can be represented as a low-rank matrix, where each row corresponds to an energy bin and each column corresponds to a voxel in the image. In this way, it is possible to obtain more accurate and robust spectral computed tomography images during reconstruction
[
16
,
17
,
18
,
19
]
. Another class of approach consists in enforcing structural similarities with a reference clean image
[
20
,
21
,
22
,
23
]
.

Synergistic regularization can also be trained as opposed to the use of a fixed analytical model. Tensor DiL
[
24
,
25
,
26
]
has also emerged as a promising technique for spectral computed tomography reconstruction. It is a powerful extension of traditional dictionary learning, where instead of representing the data using a single dictionary, a collection of dictionaries is learned, such that information can be conveyed across channels. However, they rely on the patch-based sparse representation that may be suboptimal as the trained atoms are often shifted version of each other. To remedy this problem, multichannel convolutional dictionary learning techniques can be deployed
[
27
]
. More details can be found in a recent review
[
28
]
.

Recently, there has been growing interest in applying deep learning techniques to computed tomography reconstruction
[
29
,
30
,
31
]
. Several studies have shown promising results using various deep learning architectures, such as convolutional neural networks, generative adversarial networks, autoencoders, and unrolling architectures. However, few investigations have been carried out in the domain of spectral computed tomography. For example,
[
32
,
33
]
have proposed deep learning-based spectral computed tomography multichannel post-processing techniques. Recently, an unrolling architecture, namely SOUL-Net
[
34
]
, was proposed. However the training of such models is challenging, especially with a large number of channels.

In this paper, we propose a novel spectral computed tomography reconstruction technique, namely Uconnect, where images at each energy bin are connected to a reference image by a collection of U-Nets. We considered the case of photon-counting CT with 6 energy bins, although our method can also be applied to dual-energy computed tomography. The training is performed on an image basis, and therefore does not require a computationally expensive supervised training for sinogram-to-image mappings such as unrolling architectures. The U-Nets are pretrained on a separate dataset to map the attenuation image at a reference energy to other energies and are incorporated into a plug-and-play penalty term which can be used in any model-based iterative reconstruction technique, for any system geometry. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first method that employs a deep-learned penalty for multi-energy computed tomography synergistic model-based iterative reconstruction. This work follows previous work we presented at the 2022 IEEE Medical Imaging Conference
[
35
]
.

The rest of the paper is structured as follows. Section
II
briefly reviews the physical model and several existing approaches for spectral computed tomography reconstruction, and presents the details on our proposed approach (Uconnect). Section
III
shows the details of experiments on synthetic data and real patient data, as well as qualitative and quantitative comparison results with other approaches. Section
IV
discusses several technical issues, while Section
V
provides our concluding remarks.

II
Methods

II-A

Forward Model

In this work we consider a standard photon-counting CT set-up. We consider 2-dimensional images, although the proposed method can generalize to the 3-dimensional case.

The discrete anatomical image takes the form of an energy-dependent vector
\bm{x}(E)=\left[x_{1}(E),\dots,x_{J}(E)\right]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{J}
, where for all
j=1,\dots,J
,
x_{j}(E)
is the linear attenuation at pixel
j
and energy
E
,
J
is the total number of pixels (or voxels) in the image, and ‘
⊤
’ denotes the matrix transposition.

The spectral computed tomography system performs measurements along a collection of
I
rays (from the source to the detectors), with
I=N_{\mathrm{d}}\times N_{\mathrm{s}}
,
N_{\mathrm{d}}
and
N_{\mathrm{s}}
being, respectively, the number of detectors and the number of source positions. The measurements are regrouped into
K
energy bins, each bin
k
corresponding to an interval
\left[E_{k-1},E_{k}\right]
with
E_{0}<E_{1}<\dots<E_{K}
. For each bin
k=1,\dots,K
and each ray
i=1,\dots,I
, the expected number of detected photons is given by the Beer-Lambert law as

\bar{y}_{i,k}(\bm{x})=\int_{0}^{+\infty}h_{k}\left(E\right)\cdot\mathrm{e}^{-\left[\bm{A}\bm{x}(E)\right]_{i}}\,\mathrm{d}E

(1)

where
h_{k}\colon\mathbb{R}^{+}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
is the photon flux X-ray intensity associated to energy bin
k
. For simplicity we ignored background events such as scatter but they can be incorporated in the model without affecting the rational of the proposed approach. The matrix
\bm{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{I\times J}
is the system matrix modeling line integrals along each beam in the discrete case, and for ray
i
and pixel
j
,
[\bm{A}]_{i,j}
is the contribution of pixel
j
to beam
i
.

Instead of reconstructing
\bm{x}(E)
as a function of

, we reconstruct a multichannel image
\left\{\bm{x}_{k}\right\}\in\left(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{J}\right)^{K}
where for each
k=1,\dots,K
the image
\bm{x}_{k}
is an “average” attenuation image corresponding to energy bin

, using the simplified forward model

\bar{y}_{i,k}\left(\bm{x}_{k}\right)=\bar{h}_{k}\cdot\mathrm{e}^{-\left[\bm{A}\bm{x}_{k}\right]_{i}}

(2)

where
\bar{h}_{k}=\int h_{k}\left(E\right)\mathrm{d}E
is the mean photon flux X-ray intensity for energy bin
k
. The number of detected photons at each bin
k
and ray
i
is modeled as a Poisson random variable denoted
y_{i,k}
, i.e.,

y_{i,k}\sim\mathrm{Poisson}\left(\bar{y}_{i,k}\left(\bm{x}_{k}\right)\right)\,,

(3)

and for all
i,i^{\prime},k,k^{\prime}
,
y_{i,k}
and
y_{i^{\prime},k^{\prime}}
are independent if
(i,k)\neq\left(i^{\prime},k^{\prime}\right)
. We denote by
\bm{y}_{k}=\left[y_{1,k},\dots,y_{I,k}\right]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{I}
the vector of measurement at bin
k
.

II-B

Conventional Reconstruction

Each
\bm{x}_{k}
can be reconstructed by minimizing the negative log-likelihood of the measurement
\bm{y}_{k}
. To simplify the reconstruction, we approximate the negative log-likelihood with a weighted least squares loss function
L_{k}
as proposed in
[
36
]
:

\displaystyle L_{k}\left(\bm{x}_{k}\right)={}

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{I}\frac{1}{2}y_{i,k}\left([\bm{A}\bm{x}_{k}]_{i}-b_{i,k}\right)^{2}

(4)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left\|\bm{A}\bm{x}_{k}-\bm{b}_{k}\right\|^{2}_{\bm{W}_{k}}

(5)

with
\bm{b}_{k}=\left[b_{1,k},\dots,b_{I,k}\right]^{\top}
,
b_{i,k}=\log\left(\bar{h}_{k}/y_{i,k}\right)
,
where we assumed
y_{i,k}>0
for all
(i,k)
, and
\bm{W}_{k}=\mathrm{diag}\left\{\bm{y}_{k}\right\}
is a diagonal matrix of statistical weights. Finally, each
\bm{x}_{k}
can be reconstructed by penalized WLS with positivity constraint:

\hat{\bm{x}}_{k}=\operatornamewithlimits{arg\,min}_{\bm{x}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{J}_{+}}\,L_{k}\left(\bm{x}_{k}\right)+\beta R\left(\bm{x}_{k}\right)

(6)

where
\beta>0
is a weight and
R
is an edge-preserving regularization term. A popular choice is the Huber regularizer which enforces piecewise smoothness
[
37
]
, defined as

\displaystyle R_{\mathrm{H}}(\bm{x})=\sum_{j=1}^{J}\sum_{l\in\mathcal{N}_{j}}\omega_{j,l}\psi_{\delta}\left(x_{j}-x_{l}\right),

(7)

\displaystyle\psi_{\delta}(t)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{1}{2}t^{2}&\text{if $|t|\leq\delta$}\\
\delta|t|-\frac{1}{2}\delta^{2}&\text{otherwise}\end{array}\right.

where
\delta>0
,
\mathcal{N}_{j}
is the index set of the neighbor of pixel
j
, and
\omega_{j,l}
is the inverse Euclidean distance between
j
and
l
.

II-C

Synergistic Penalties: Existing Approaches

Instead of reconstructing each
\bm{x}_{k}
independently following (
6
), the entire collection of images
\left\{\bm{x}_{k}\right\}
can be reconstructed simultaneously in a single optimization problem as

\{\hat{\bm{x}}_{k}\}=\operatornamewithlimits{arg\,min}_{\{\bm{x}_{k}\}\in\left(\mathbb{R}^{J}_{+}\right)^{K}}\,\sum_{k=1}^{K}L_{k}(\bm{x}_{k})+\beta R_{\mathrm{syn}}\left(\left\{\bm{x}_{k}\right\}\right)

(10)

where in this case
R_{\mathrm{syn}}
is a synergistic regularization term for the multichannel image
\left\{\bm{x}_{k}\right\}
. Note that the sum of the
L_{k}
s can be weighted in order to equally smooth the
\bm{x}_{k}
s. In this section we describe existing categories of methods for spectral computed tomography reconstruction and we highlight the methods we implemented for comparison with our proposed approach outlined in the following section
II-D
.

A first category of methods consists in using a regularizer that enforces structural similarities between channels. An example is joint TV, whose regularizer is defined as

R_{\mathrm{JTV}}\left(\left\{\bm{x}_{k}\right\}\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{J}\sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left\|\left[\nabla\bm{x}_{k}\right]_{j}\right\|^{2}_{2}}

(11)

where
\nabla\colon\mathbb{R}^{J}\to\left(\mathbb{R}^{J}\right)^{2}
denotes the discrete gradient. This promotes joint sparsity of the gradient of the
\bm{x}_{k}
s. Other examples include total nuclear variation
[
13
,
14
]
and parallel level sets
[
15
]
.

A second category involves controlling the rank of the multichannel image to promote linear dependencies between the channels. It is reasonable to assume some form of “low rankness” due to the fact that the energy dependency of human tissues can be expressed as a linear combination of only two materials. In
[
38
]
, a low-rank and sparse (LRS) decomposition was proposed for the multichannel image matrix
\bm{X}=[\bm{x}_{1},\dots,\bm{x}_{K}]\in\mathbb{R}^{J\times K}
such that
\bm{X}=\bm{L}+\bm{S}
, where the low-rank matrix
\bm{L}
represents the underlying structure or common information between channels and the sparse matrix
\bm{S}
reflects distinct spectral features across channels. A synergistic penalty is then defined as

R_{\mathrm{LRS}}\left(\bm{X}\right)=\gamma\left\|\bm{L}\right\|_{*}+\left\|\bm{T}\bm{S}\right\|_{1}

(12)

where
\bm{T}
is a sparsifying transform (such as the gradient or a wavelet transform) with
\gamma>0
and the nuclear norm
\|\cdot\|_{*}
is a convex relaxation of the rank for an efficient optimization. The method aided by self-similarity in image-spectral tensors in
[
19
]
is a generalization of this approach that selects the
M
most similar
d
-dimensional patches within a search window of a clean reference (or prior) image
\bm{x}^{\mathrm{prior}}
, to form a collection of
d\times M\times K
tensor units of which the nuclear norm is computed.

A third category of methods consists in reconstructing each channel
\bm{x}_{k}
independently by solving (
6
) with a single-channel penalty that enforces similarities with a reference low-noise prior image
\bm{x}^{\mathrm{prior}}\in\mathbb{R}^{J}

[
20
,
21
,
22
,
23
]
. For example in
[
20
]
the directional TV regularizer promotes common edge directions with the prior image, and is defined for all
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{J}
as

R_{\mathrm{DTV}}(\bm{x})=\sum_{j=1}^{J}\left\|\bm{\Pi}_{j}[\nabla\bm{x}]_{j}\right\|_{2}

(13)

where
\bm{\Pi}_{j}=\bm{I}-\bm{\xi}_{j}\otimes\bm{\xi}_{j}
,
\bm{I}
being the
2\times 2
identity matrix and
\otimes

\bm{\xi}_{j}=\eta\frac{[\nabla\bm{x}^{\mathrm{prior}}]_{j}}{\sqrt{\left\|[\nabla\bm{x}^{\mathrm{prior}}]_{j}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\epsilon}}

(14)

with

\left(1-\eta^{2}\right)R_{\mathrm{TV}}\leq R_{\mathrm{DTV}}\leq R_{\mathrm{TV}}

are collinear for all

R_{\mathrm{DTV}}(\bm{x})
is minimized when the gradient vanishes and therefore potential artifacts in

41

which use the gradient of the difference between each

II-D

Proposed Learned Synergistic Penalty: Uconnect

The traditional regularization techniques presented in Section
II-C
enforce a fixed handcrafted penalty on the reconstructed image due to certain assumptions about its structure, such as sparsity or smoothness. However, these assumptions may not always be valid in practice. By contrast, learned penalty functions adjust adaptively according to the data’s specific characteristics, providing more accurate and adaptable reconstructions.

II-D
1
General principle

Learned synergistic penalties can be derived from multichannel dictionary learning, which consists in training a collection of dictionaries
\bm{D}_{1},\dots,\bm{D}_{K}
, i.e., over-complete bases comprising
S
atoms, to approximately represent each image
\bm{x}_{k}
with a fraction of their columns with a single sparse code
\bm{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{S}
, that is to say,

\bm{x}_{k}\approx\bm{D}_{k}\bm{z}
for all
k
, in order to convey the information across channels. Unfortunately, a large number of atoms is required to accurately represent all possible spectral images, which can compromise training. Therefore, to reduce the complexity, the image is split into
P
smaller
d
-dimensional “patches” with
d\ll J
. The synergistic penalty is therefore defined as follows:

\displaystyle R_{\mathrm{dict}}\left(\{\bm{x}_{k}\}\right)={}

\displaystyle\min_{\{\bm{z}_{p}\}\in(\mathbb{R}^{S})^{P}}\,\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{p=1}^{P}\frac{1}{2}\|\bm{P}_{p}\bm{x}_{k}-\bm{D}_{k}\bm{z}_{p}\|^{2}_{2}

\displaystyle+\alpha\|\bm{z}_{p}\|_{q}

(16)

where
\bm{P}_{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times J}
is the
p
th
patch extractor,
\bm{D}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times S}
is the
k
th
trained dictionary,
\bm{z}_{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{S}
is the sparse coding (same for all channel
k
),
\|\cdot\|_{q}
is either the
\ell_{1}
-norm or the
\ell_{0}
semi-norm, and
\alpha>0
. In
[
24
]
the authors used tensor dictionaries to represent the spectral image based on canonical polyadic decomposition, which is similar to (
16
). Similar coupled dictionary models have been used in multimodal imaging synergistic reconstruction, for example in positron emission tomography/magnetic resonance imaging
[
42
,
43
]
and multi-contrast magnetic resonance imaging
[
44
]
.

Despite the possibility to extract patches at random locations over the image, patch-based dictionary learning may suffer from inefficiency due to the shift-variant nature of the atoms that can generate duplicates during training. Furthermore, the utilization of many neighboring/overlapping patches across the training images is not optimal for sparse representation, as sparsification is performed independently on each patch.

In this work we propose an alternative to patch-based dictionary learning. We assume that each image
\bm{x}_{k}
is acquired from a single object
\bm{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
(non necessarily sparse),
N
being the dimension of the latent space (in dictionary learning
N
corresponds to the number of atoms
S
), with a collection of trained convolutional neural networks
\left\{\bm{f}_{k}\right\}
, such that

\bm{f}_{k}(\bm{z})\approx\bm{x}_{k},\quad\forall k=1,\dots,K\,.

(17)

The mappings
\left\{\bm{f}_{k}\right\}
play the role of the “non-linear” dictionaries for the entire image (without patches), which connect all energy bins through
\bm{z}
. The proposed joint regularization term is therefore defined as

R_{\mathrm{NN}}\left(\{\bm{x}_{k}\}\right)=\min_{\bm{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\,\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{1}{2}\gamma_{k}\left\|\bm{f}_{k}(\bm{z})-\bm{x}_{k}\right\|^{2}_{2}+\alpha H(\bm{z})

(18)

where
H
is a penalty term on
\bm{z}
. This representation is a generalization of (
16
) with non-linear mappings
\left\{\bm{f}_{k}\right\}
instead of dictionaries, and without patch extraction. The regularizer
R_{\mathrm{NN}}\left(\left\{\bm{x}_{k}\right\}\right)
is small if (i) each image
\bm{x}_{k}
is close to
\bm{f}_{k}(\bm{z})
for some
\bm{z}
and (ii)
\bm{z}
is smooth (in the sense of

The choice of the reference image

, i.e., the lowest-energy bin, is not arbitrary. It is generally accepted that computed tomography images of the same anatomical part but acquired at different energies exhibit similar patterns despite different attenuation values. To take advantage of this similarity, we designated the computed tomography image obtained from the lowest-energy bin as the reference image owing to the fact that low-energy attenuation images contain more features than high-energy ones, therefore high-energy to low-energy mappings would have to be trained to reveal features, which is more challenging than the other way around. By leveraging this approach, the shared characteristics across energy levels can enhance the accuracy and quality of spectral computed tomography reconstructions. Note that the model (
19

III
, the lowest-energy image can reasonably predict the images at other energies. Similar observation was made in
[
49
]
.

To finish,
\bm{z}

is the amount of labeled data for training. Note that we also train the network for
k=1
, i.e., bin 1 to bin 1, in order to take advantage of the denoising properties of the
\bm{f}_{k}
. The training was achieved with mean squared error and Adam optimizer.

To account for the scaling factors, a modified version of the synergistic penalty (
18
) is used for our experiments as follows:

R_{\mathrm{NN}}\left(\left\{\bm{x}_{k}\right\}\right)=\operatornamewithlimits{arg\,min}_{\bm{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\,\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{1}{2}\gamma_{k}\left\|\bm{f}_{k}(\bm{z})-\hat{s}_{k}\bm{x}_{k}\right\|^{2}_{2}{}+\alpha H(\bm{z})

(21)

where
\hat{s}_{k}
is an estimated scaling factor, which can be obtained from scout reconstructions, for example by solving (
6
) for each bin independently. Note that this factor is object-dependent, although we treat it here as a constant.

II-D
3
Reconstruction Algorithm

Solving (
10
) using the synergistic penalty (
21
) is performed by alternating minimization with respect to
\bm{z}
and
\{\bm{x}_{k}\}
, i.e., given the estimates
\left\{\bm{x}_{k}^{(n)}\right\}
and
\bm{z}^{(n)}
at iteration
n
, the new estimates at iteration
n+1
are given by

\displaystyle\bm{z}^{(n+1)}={}

\displaystyle\operatornamewithlimits{arg\,min}_{\bm{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{J}_{+}}\,\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{1}{2}\gamma_{k}\left\|\bm{f}_{k}(\bm{z})-\hat{s}_{k}\bm{x}_{k}^{(n)}\right\|^{2}_{2}+\alpha H(\bm{z})

(22)

\displaystyle\bm{x}_{k}^{(n+1)}={}

\displaystyle\operatornamewithlimits{arg\,min}_{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{J}_{+}}\,L_{k}(\bm{x})+\beta\gamma_{k}\frac{1}{2}\left\|\bm{f}_{k}\left(\bm{z}^{(n+1)}\right)-\hat{s}_{k}\bm{x}\right\|_{2}^{2}\,,

\displaystyle\forall k=1,\dots,K

(23)

We treat each sub-problem with iterative algorithms.

When
H
is differentiable (e.g.,
H=R_{\mathrm{H}}
), the
\bm{z}
-update (
22
) is achieved with a limited-memory Broyden-Fletcher-Goldfarb-Shanno algorithm
[
50
]
(initialized from
\bm{z}^{(n)}
) with non-negativity constraints, which is a quasi-Newton optimization algorithm that only requires the gradient of the objective function. We used the
GradientTape
function in TensorFlow to compute the gradient of a function with respect to its input variables. When
H
is non-differentiable (e.g., total variation), the minimization can achieved with a Chambolle-Pock algorithm
[
51
]
, combined with limited-memory Broyden-Fletcher-Goldfarb-Shanno for the proximal of
\left\|\bm{f}_{k}(\cdot)-\hat{s}_{k}\bm{x}_{k}^{(n)}\right\|^{2}_{2}
.

For the
\bm{x}_{k}
-update (
23
), we used a separable quadratic surrogate algorithm
[
36
]
, i.e., using a diagonal matrix
\bm{H}_{k}
that majorizes
\bm{A}^{\top}\bm{W}_{k}\bm{A}
(e.g.,
\bm{H}_{k}=\mathrm{diag}(\bm{A}^{\top}\bm{W}_{k}\bm{A}\bm{1})
where
\bm{1}
is a vector containing
1
only, see
[
52
]
). Let
\bm{x}_{k}^{(n,m)}
be the current estimate at iteration
n
, sub-iteration
m
.
The new image at sub-iteration
m+1
is given by

\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{x}}_{k}={}

\displaystyle\bm{x}_{k}^{(n,m)}-\bm{H}_{k}^{-1}\bm{A}^{\top}\bm{W}_{k}\left(\bm{A}\bm{x}_{k}^{(n,m)}-\bm{b}_{k}\right)

\displaystyle\bm{x}_{k}^{(n,m+1)}={}

\displaystyle\left[\frac{\bm{H}_{k}\tilde{\bm{x}}_{k}+\beta\gamma_{k}\hat{s}_{k}\bm{f}_{k}\left(\bm{z}^{(n+1)}\right)}{\bm{H}_{k}+\beta\gamma_{k}\hat{s}_{k}^{2}}\right]_{+}

(24)

where
[\cdot]_{+}
denotes the positive part of a vector.

\bm{z}

\{\bm{x}_{k}\}

III
Experiments

We conducted experiments on two types of data: synthetic data and real patient images. In both cases, we considered 2-dimensional computed tomography images at
K=6
energies:
E_{1}=40
keV,
E_{2}=60
keV,
E_{3}=80
keV,
E_{4}=100
keV,
E_{5}=120
keV and
E_{6}=140
keV.

U-Net architectures were built in Python 3.8 with Tensorflow 2.10 using NVIDIA GeForce GTX 1660 Ti (6 GB memory). In both experiments, the models were trained on one dataset, and were tested on a separate dataset of the same type.

We compared the approaches mentioned in Section
II
, i.e., Huber, joint TV, directional TV, aided by self-similarity in image-spectral tensors, as well as our proposed Uconnect method with
H=R_{\mathrm{H}}
(Huber), for two different tasks: image reconstruction in Section
III-A
and image denoising in Section
III-B
. In addition, we also conducted a comparison with a deep learning approach called residual encoder-decoder convolutional neural network
[
53
]
in terms of image denoising task for patient data in Section
III-B
. We used a separable quadratic surrogate algorithm
[
36
]
for the individual reconstructions with Huber penalty and the Chambolle-Pock algorithm
[
51
,
54
]
for joint TV and directional TV. All algorithms were implemented in Python programming language.

The objective measures structural similarity index measure and peak signal-to-noise ratio, with respect to a reference image
\bm{x}^{\mathrm{ref}}=\left[x_{1}^{\mathrm{ref}},\dots,x_{J}^{\mathrm{ref}}\right]^{\top}
, were employed for quantitative evaluation of the image quality. We utilized the functions
structural_similarity
and
peak_signal_noise_ratio
from the Python package
skimage.metrics
.

We used the extended cardiac-torso software
[

512\times{}512\times{}280

L=280

\bm{f}_{k}

For each energy, we utilized a constant intensity

\bm{x}_{k}=\bm{x}_{k}^{\mathrm{gt}}

\bm{y}=[y_{1},\dots,y_{I}]^{\top}

III-B

Patient Data

III-B
1
Data Preparation, Training, and Setup

2 collections of real patient data images of size
512\times 512
from 2 different patients were obtained by the Philips IQon Spectral computed tomography scanner from the Poitiers University Hospital, Poitiers, France. The Philips IQon Spectral computed tomography is a dual energy scanner that produces 2 images at 80 kVp and 120 kVp from which 6 virtual mono-energetic images were obtained by interpolation (same energies as in Section
III-A
).
L=360
slices of the first dataset were used to train the
\bm{f}_{k}
s throughout 500 epochs. The batch size was set to 5 and the learning rate was set to 0.0002.

Owing to the fact that these images do not represent ground truths attenuation of real patients, reconstructing from simulated measurements
\{\bm{y}_{k}\}
with Poisson noise added, as we did with the extended cardiac-torso phantom, was not considered. Therefore we assessed the different penalties for a denoising task with a least-squares data-fidelity term, i.e.

L_{k}(\bm{x}_{k})=\frac{1}{2}\left\|\bm{x}_{k}-\bm{x}_{k}^{\mathrm{noisy}}\right\|^{2}_{2}

(29)

where
\bm{x}_{k}^{\mathrm{noisy}}
is the noisy image simulated as

\bm{x}_{k}^{\mathrm{noisy}}=\bm{x}_{k}^{\mathrm{patient}}+\bm{\varepsilon}_{k},\quad\bm{\varepsilon}_{k}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\bm{0},\,\bm{\Sigma}_{k}^{2}\right),

(30)

and
\bm{x}_{k}^{\mathrm{patient}}
a patient image from the second dataset, and
\bm{\Sigma}_{k}^{2}=\mathrm{diag}\left\{\sigma_{1,k}^{2},\dots,\sigma_{J,k}^{2}\right\}
. The Uconnect approach for denoising corresponds to solving (
25
). The other methods were implemented by using the same approach with different penalties.

We estimated realistic
\sigma_{j,k}
values with a Monte-Carlo approach. We created
Q=30
independent measurements with random Poisson noise following (
3
) with
\bm{x}_{k}=\left\{\bm{x}_{k}^{\mathrm{patient}}\right\}
for each energy
k=1,\dots,K
, with intensity
\bar{h}_{k}=\frac{2}{3}\cdot 10^{5}
for all
k
, from which we reconstructed the spectral images
\bm{x}_{k}^{[q]}=\left[x_{1,k}^{[q]},\dots,x_{J,k}^{[q]}\right]^{\top}
,
k=1,\dots,K
and
q=1,\dots,Q
, by weighted least squares (i.e., (
6
) with
\beta=0
). The variance
\sigma_{j,k}^{2}
at each pixel
j
for each energy
k
was estimated as

\sigma_{j,k}^{2}=\frac{1}{Q-1}\sum_{q=1}^{Q}\left(x_{j,k}^{[q]}-\bar{x}_{j,k}\right)^{2},\quad\bar{x}_{j,k}=\frac{1}{Q}\sum_{q=1}^{Q}x_{j,k}^{[q]}

(31)

The weights
\gamma_{k}
in our method (i.e., Eq. (
21
) and (
25
)) were defined as

\gamma_{k}=\frac{\frac{1}{\sum_{j=1}^{J}\sigma_{j,k}^{2}}}{\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{1}{\sum_{j=1}^{J}\sigma_{j,k}^{2}}}

(32)

in order to give more weight to the less noisy images. Although these weights are in principle unknown, they can be estimated
[
57
]
.

For the deep learning-based denoising method residual encoder-decoder convolutional neural network, a total of
K
networks need to be trained for each energy
k=1,\dots,K
. The training dataset for each energy

IV
Discussion

We proposed a novel method that takes the form of a trained penalty to solve the problem of synergistic reconstruction for spectral computed tomography. In this paper, we used the pretrained U-Nets to “connect” images at all energies to a reference
\bm{z}
, so that the training can be supervised. We considered as reference the image at the lowest energy. Our U-Net based regularization, referred to as Uconnect, showed enhanced performance for both image reconstruction and image denoising tasks. The mappings
\left\{\bm{f}_{k}\right\}
provide regularization (i) by their training, as they are trained on “clean” images (as initially suggested in
[
48
]
), and (ii) by sharing the information across channels, in a similar fashion as tensor DiL but without patch extraction. Note that our method can be generalized to a single architecture with
K
outputs, each of which corresponding to an energy bin, in order to further leverage the inter-energy information during training.

As pointed out in Section
II-D1
, the pretrained mappings
\left\{\bm{f}_{k}\right\}
do not exactly reflect the physics, as
\bm{z}
represents an attenuation image. Alternatively, models such as Wasserstein GAN or variational autoencoder, where the latent space is “trained” to encode the physical properties of the patient, should be used
[
46
]
. These models have been applied in positron emission tomography/computed tomography
[
58
]
and positron emission tomography/magnetic resonance imaging
[
59
]
. Similar models may also be used to generate material images and could be used for one-step material decomposition. However unsupervised training with a large number of channels remains challenging.

The results of the proposed approach can be potentially improved by fine-tuning all parameters, including
\alpha
and
\left\{\gamma_{k}\right\}
in (
21
), which we have not yet investigated. Additionally, we can explore other regularizers for the reference
\bm{z}
in our method, such as by replacing Huber with total variation or directional TV.

An major limit to our validation is that our phantom data were based on virtual mono-energetic images, which correspond to an oversimplified setting. In addition, virtual mono-energetic images are artificially correlated, which may bias the results. Further validation should include real raw photon-counting CT projection data and models trained from photon-counting CT binned images.

Finally, our comparison can be expanded beyond other synergistic reconstruction methods, such as tensor DiL
[
24
]
and SOUL-Net
[
34
]
, a recent work using an unrolling architecture.

V
Conclusion

In this work, we used a convolutional neural network to derive a novel synergistic penalty term for spectral computed tomography reconstruction. The proposed approach is a combination on the convolutional neural network representation technique
[
48
]
and multichannel dictionary learning
[
24
]
.

The presented results demonstrate that Uconnect has the ability to reduce noise while preserving structures. Both visual inspection and quantitative measurements show significant improvements for our proposed method in synergistic spectral computed tomography reconstruction. While there is certainly room for improvement, we believe that the proposed methodological framework provides a solid foundation for future research in this field. Further refinement of our approach may be achieved by unrolling our architecture, a development that warrants further investigation.
