Meromorphic Jacobi Forms of Half-Integral Index and Umbral Moonshine Modules On leave from CNRS, France.
Miranda C. N. Cheng, John F. R. Duncan

Abstract
In this work we consider an association of meromorphic Jacobi forms of half-integral index to the pure D-type cases of umbral moonshine, and solve the module problem for four of these cases by constructing vertex operator superalgebras that realise the corresponding meromorphic Jacobi forms as graded traces. We also present a general discussion of meromorphic Jacobi forms with half-integral index and their relationship to mock modular forms.

1
Introduction

Moonshine connects modular objects and finite groups in an interesting way.
Starting from 2010, the discovery of Mathieu moonshine for
M_{24}

[
1
]
has initiated a new wave of activity in the study of moonshine.
One development arising is the realisation that this
M_{24}
moonshine is but one of 23 instances of the so-called umbral moonshine
[
2
,
3
]
.
Umbral moonshine associates
a vector-valued mock modular form
H^{(\ell)}_{g}=(H^{(\ell)}_{g,r})
, with a certain level and multiplier system, to each conjugacy class
[g]
of a finite group
G^{(\ell)}
, where
\ell
is a symbol (called lambency) which indexes the 23 Niemeier lattices (and also indexes certain genus zero groups of isometries of the upper half-plane, cf.
[
4
]
). The group
G^{(\ell)}
can be defined explicitly as the group of outer automorphisms of the Niemeier lattice corresponding to
\ell
(cf. §2.4 of
[
3
]
).
One of the umbral moonshine conjectures, proven for Mathieu moonshine in
[
5
]
and proven for umbral moonshine in general in
[
6
]
,
then states that the
H^{(\ell)}_{g}
can be identified with the graded characters of a certain infinite-dimensional
G^{(\ell)}
-module.

This is reminiscent of monstrous moonshine
[
7
]
, which attaches to each conjugacy class
[g]
of the monster group a certain Hauptmodul
T_{g}(\tau)
for a certain subgroup
\Gamma_{g}
of
\operatorname{\textsl{SL}}_{2}({\mathbb{R}})
that defines a genus zero quotient of the upper half-plane. These functions
T_{g}(\tau)
were ultimately shown by Borcherds
[
8
]
to coincide with the graded characters of a certain infinite-dimensional module
V^{\natural}
for the monster group, which was constructed earlier by Frenkel–Lepowsky–Meurman
[
9
,
10
]
and which famously possesses vertex operator algebra structure
[
11
,
12
]
.

We would like to have a similar understanding of the modules for umbral moonshine. Arguably, this is the most important outstanding question in the study of umbral moonshine. The first case of umbral moonshine for which the module problem was solved is the case where
\ell=30+6,10,15
, the Niemeier lattice is
E_{8}^{\oplus 3}
, and
G^{(\ell)}\cong S_{3}

[
13
]
. This is the unique case for which the Niemeier lattice coincides with its root lattice.
So far the techniques of
[
13
]
have not been successfully extended to other cases.

In the more recent work
[
14
]
, and in the present paper, the focus is shifted from
the vector-valued mock modular forms
H^{(\ell)}_{g}

to certain meromorphic Jacobi forms.
The former may be obtained from the latter
once a canonically defined polar part that captures the poles of the latter
is removed. We explain this in detail in §
2
. The meromorphic Jacobi forms
involved in the cases of umbral moonshine discussed here and in
[
14
]
admit expressions as sums of ratios of infinite products, and this makes it possible to realise them as (twined) partition functions of certain free chiral conformal field theories. Using this strategy, vertex operator superalgebras are constructed that possess automorphisms containing the corresponding umbral groups
G^{(\ell)}
, and are such that the twined partition functions arising coincide with the meromorphic Jacobi forms
specified by umbral moonshine. In this way, the umbral moonshine modules for two of the pure A-type cases, corresponding to
\ell=7
and
\ell=13
, have been constructed in
[
14
]
(while partial solutions to the module problem are given in loc. cit. for
\ell=4
and
\ell=5
). The corresponding Niemeier lattices are those with the root systems
A_{6}^{\oplus 4}
and
A_{12}^{\oplus 2}
, respectively.

The main objective of this work is the construction of umbral moonshine modules for four of the five pure D-type umbral moonshine cases, corresponding to
\ell=10+5
,
\ell=14+7
,
\ell=22+11
and
\ell=46+23
, for which the corresponding Niemeier lattices are those whose roots systems are
D_{6}^{\oplus 4}
,
D_{8}^{\oplus 3}
,
D_{12}^{\oplus 2}
and
D_{24}
, respectively.
We achieve this in §
3
. See Theorems
3.1
,
3.2
,
3.3
and
3.4
. Our methods are similar to those of
[
14
]
, but diverge from loc. cit. in an apparently powerful aspect, by considering meromorphic Jacobi forms of half-integral index, rather than those of integral index which are attached to the pure D-type cases of umbral moonshine in
[
3
]
.

In order to support our approach we present results on meromorphic Jacobi forms of half-integral index and their relationship to mock modular forms in §
2
. Theorem
2.4
represents an extension to half-integral index of the canonical splitting of a meromorphic Jacobi form into polar and finite parts, which was first given for integral index in
[
16
]
. Theorem
2.6
precisely formulates the relationship between mock modular forms and meromorphic Jacobi forms of weight
1
and half-integral index that have simple poles only at torsion points, and satisfy a certain growth condition.

The paper is structured in the following way. In §
2
we discuss meromorphic Jacobi forms of half-integral index and their relationship to mock modular forms. We review elliptic forms, theta series and Eicher–Zagier operators of half-integral index in §
2.1
. We review holomorphic, skew-holomorphic and meromorphic Jacobi forms in §
2.2
, and also review there the construction of the finite part of a meromorphic Jacobi form due to Dabholkar–Murthy–Zagier
[
16
]
. In §
2.3
we review the construction of the polar part of a meromorphic Jacobi form from its poles, assuming that they are simple and constrained to lie at torsion points. This extends the discussion of §8.2 of
[
16
]
to half-integral indexes. In §
2.4
we specialise to meromorphic Jacobi forms of weight
1
satisfying a growth condition, and prove our main result (see Theorem
2.6
) on the mock modularity of the finite parts and theta coefficients of such forms. In §
2.5
we explain the construction which converts the vector-valued mock modular forms of pure D-type umbral moonshine into meromorphic Jacobi forms of half-integral index.

Our main constructions appear in §
3
, where we obtain vertex algebraic realisations of the half-integral index meromorphic Jacobi forms presented in §
2.5
, for four of the pure D-type cases of umbral moonshine.
The case of
D_{6}^{\oplus 4}
is treated in §
3.1
(see Theorem
3.1
), the case of
D_{8}^{\oplus 3}
is treated in §
3.2
(see Theorem
3.2
), the case of
D_{12}^{\oplus 2}
is treated in §
3.3
(see Theorem
3.3
), and
D_{24}
is treated in §
3.4
(see Theorem
3.4
).
We conclude in §
4
with discussions on the relation between the known modules for pure A-type and pure D-type umbral moonshine, as well as the possible physical relevance of our constructions.

2
Meromorphic and Mock Jacobi Forms

In this section we describe a relationship between meromorphic Jacobi forms and mock modular forms. This relationship goes back to work of Zwegers
[
15
]
, which was subsequently developed by Dabholkar–Murthy–Zagier in
[
16
]
. Here we follow
[
16
]
quite closely, but extend the discussion to include half-integral index, and refine it for meromorphic Jacobi forms with weight
1
.

2.1
Elliptic Forms

We first discuss elliptic forms with half-integral index.
For this set
\operatorname{e}(x):=e^{2\pi ix}
and let
{\mathbb{H}}:=\{\tau\in{\mathbb{C}}\mid\Im(\tau)>0\}
denote the upper half-plane.
For
m\in{1\over 2}{\mathbb{Z}}
define the index
m

elliptic action
of

{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
on functions
\phi:{\mathbb{H}}\times{\mathbb{C}}\to{\mathbb{C}}
by setting

(\phi\lvert_{m}(\lambda,\mu))(\tau,z):=e\left(m\lambda^{2}\tau+2m\lambda z+{(\lambda+\mu)m}\right)\,\phi(\tau,z+\lambda\tau+\mu)

(2.1)

for
(\lambda,\mu)\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
. With this definition we call a smooth function
\phi:{\mathbb{H}}\times{\mathbb{C}}\to{\mathbb{C}}
an
elliptic form
1
1

1

Our nomenclature follows
[
16
]
, except that elliptic forms are assumed to be holomorphic in that work, and the focus there is on integral index.

with index
m
if
(\phi\lvert_{m}(\lambda,\mu))=\phi
for all
(\lambda,\mu)\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
.
Write
\mathcal{E}_{m}
for the space of elliptic forms of index
m\in\frac{1}{2}{\mathbb{Z}}
.

For
m\in{1\over 2}{\mathbb{Z}}
and
r\in{\mathbb{Z}}+m
define the
index
m
theta function

\theta_{m,r}:{\mathbb{H}}\times{\mathbb{C}}\to{\mathbb{C}}
by setting

\displaystyle\theta_{m,r}(\tau,z):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell\in{\mathbb{Z}}+m\\
\ell=r{\rm\;mod\;}2m\end{subarray}}\operatorname{e}(m\ell)y^{\ell}q^{\frac{\ell^{2}}{4m}}

(2.2)

where
q:=\operatorname{e}(\tau)
and
y:=\operatorname{e}(z)
.
Also
define
\theta^{1}_{m,r}(\tau):=\frac{1}{2\pi i}{\partial\over\partial z}\theta_{m,r}(\tau,z)\lvert_{z=0}
.
Then a smooth function
\phi:{\mathbb{H}}\times{\mathbb{C}}\to{\mathbb{C}}
belongs to
\mathcal{E}_{m}
if and only if it can be written in the form

\displaystyle\phi(\tau,z)=\sum_{r{\rm\;mod\;}2m}h_{r}(\tau)\theta_{m,r}(\tau,z)

(2.3)

for some
2m

theta coefficient
functions
h_{r}:{\mathbb{H}}\to{\mathbb{C}}
. We emphasise here that
m\in\frac{1}{2}{\mathbb{Z}}
, and the summation in (
2.3
) is over representatives for
{\mathbb{Z}}+m
modulo translation by
2m
.

For an explicit example note that the
m=r=\frac{1}{2}
case of (
2.2
) recovers a classical Jacobi theta function

\displaystyle\begin{split}\theta_{\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}}(\tau,z)&=\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}i(-1)^{n+1}y^{n+\frac{1}{2}}q^{\frac{1}{2}(n+\frac{1}{2})^{2}}\\
&=-iy^{\frac{1}{2}}q^{\frac{1}{8}}\prod_{n>0}(1-y^{-1}q^{n-1})(1-yq^{n})(1-q^{n}).\end{split}

(2.4)

We will denote
\theta_{\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}}(\tau,z)
by
\theta_{1}(\tau,z)
in §§
3
,
4
(cf. (
3.5
)).

Elliptic forms come equipped with some naturally defined symmetry. To explain this
define a function

m\mapsto\tilde{m}
on
\frac{1}{2}{\mathbb{Z}}
by setting

\displaystyle\tilde{m}:=\begin{cases}m&\text{ if $m\in{\mathbb{Z}}$,}\\
2m&\text{ if $m\in{\mathbb{Z}}+\frac{1}{2}$.}\end{cases}

(2.5)

Then the group
O_{\tilde{m}}:=\{a\in{\mathbb{Z}}/2\tilde{m}{\mathbb{Z}}\mid a^{2}=1{\rm\;mod\;}4\tilde{m}\}
acts naturally on
{\mathcal{E}}_{m}
via

\displaystyle\phi\cdot a:=\sum_{r{\rm\;mod\;}2m}h_{r}\theta_{m,ra}

(2.6)

when
\phi=\sum_{r{\rm\;mod\;}2m}h_{r}\theta_{m,r}
is the theta decomposition (
2.3
) of
\phi\in{\mathcal{E}}_{m}
.

It develops that
O_{\tilde{m}}
is naturally isomorphic to the group
\operatorname{Ex}(\tilde{m})
of exact divisors of
\tilde{m}
. We obtain an explicit isomorphism
O_{\tilde{m}}\to\operatorname{Ex}(\tilde{m})
, to be denoted
n\mapsto a(n)
, by letting
a(n)
be the unique element of
{\mathbb{Z}}/2\tilde{m}{\mathbb{Z}}
such that
a(n)=1{\rm\;mod\;}2\frac{\tilde{m}}{n}
and
a(n)=-1{\rm\;mod\;}2n
.
With this definition we have

\displaystyle\phi|\mathcal{W}_{m}(n)=\phi\cdot a(n)

(2.7)

for
\phi\in\mathcal{E}_{m}
and
n\in\operatorname{Ex}(\tilde{m})
, where

\displaystyle(\phi|\mathcal{W}_{m}(n))(\tau,z):=\tfrac{1}{n}\sum_{a,b=0}^{n-1}\operatorname{e}(m(\tfrac{a^{2}}{n^{2}}\tau+2\tfrac{a}{n}z+\tfrac{ab}{n^{2}}+ab+a+b))\phi(\tau,z+\tfrac{a}{n}\tau+\tfrac{b}{n}).

(2.8)

We call
\mathcal{W}_{m}(n)
an
Eichler–Zagier operator
of index
m
. The
\mathcal{W}_{m}(n)
for integral index first appeared in
[
17
]
. Note that (
2.8
) is well-defined and stabilises
\mathcal{E}_{m}
for
n
an arbitrary divisor of
\tilde{m}
.

2.2
Meromorphic Jacobi Forms

We now explain precisely what we mean by a meromorphic Jacobi form.
Recall that a
holomorphic Jacobi form
of weight
k\in{\mathbb{Z}}
and index
m\in\frac{1}{2}{\mathbb{Z}}
for a group
\Gamma<\operatorname{\textsl{SL}}_{2}({\mathbb{Z}})
is an elliptic form
\phi=\sum_{r{\rm\;mod\;}2m}h_{r}\theta_{m,r}
of index
m

that is invariant for the weight
k
index
m

modular action

\displaystyle\left(\phi|_{k,m}\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{smallmatrix}\right)\right)(\tau,z):=\phi\left(\tfrac{a\tau+b}{c\tau+d},\tfrac{z}{c\tau+d}\right)e\left(-m\tfrac{cz^{2}}{c\tau+d}\right)(c\tau+d)^{-k}

(2.9)

of
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\Gamma
, and whose theta coefficients (
2.3
) are holomorphic and bounded near any cusp of
\Gamma
.
A
skew-holomorphic Jacobi form
is defined similarly, just replacing (
2.9
) with the weight
k
index
m

skew-modular action

\displaystyle\left(\phi|_{k,m}^{\rm sk}\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{smallmatrix}\right)\right)(\tau,z):=\phi\left(\tfrac{a\tau+b}{c\tau+d},\tfrac{z}{c\tau+d}\right)e\left(-m\tfrac{cz^{2}}{c\tau+d}\right)(c\tau+d)^{-k+1}|c\tau+d|^{-1}

(2.10)

of
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\Gamma
, and requiring the theta coefficients (
2.3
) to be anti-holomorphic and bounded near any cusp of
\Gamma
.
For us a
meromorphic Jacobi form

is a quotient
\psi=\frac{\phi_{1}}{\phi_{2}}
of holomorphic Jacobi forms
such that for any fixed
\tau\in{\mathbb{H}}
the function

z\mapsto\phi_{2}(\tau,z)
is not identically zero. With this definition
z\mapsto\psi(\tau,z)

is meromorphic on
{\mathbb{C}}
for all
\tau\in{\mathbb{H}}
.
If
k_{i}
and
m_{i}
are the weight and index (respectively) of
\phi_{i}
then
k=k_{1}-k_{2}
and
m=m_{1}-m_{2}
are the weight and index (respectively) of
\psi
.

A
weak holomorphic Jacobi form
of weight
k
and index
m
is a holomorphic function
\phi\in\mathcal{E}_{m}
that is invariant for the weight
k
modular action (
2.9
), and is such that
\tau\mapsto(\phi|_{k,m}\gamma)(\tau,z)
is bounded as
\Im(\tau)\to\infty
for any fixed
z\in{\mathbb{C}}
and
\gamma\in\operatorname{\textsl{SL}}_{2}({\mathbb{Z}})
.
We will say that a meromorphic Jacobi form
\psi
of weight
k
and index
m
is a
weak meromorphic Jacobi form
if, for fixed
z\in{\mathbb{C}}
and arbitrary
\gamma\in\operatorname{\textsl{SL}}_{2}({\mathbb{Z}})
, the function

\tau\mapsto(\psi|_{k,m}\gamma)(\tau,z)
is bounded as
\Im(\tau)\to\infty
along any path that avoids poles. In general we have that
\tau\mapsto(\psi|_{k,m}\gamma)(\tau,z)
is
O(e^{C\Im(\tau)})
as
\Im(\tau)\to\infty
(along paths that avoid poles), for some
C>0
that is independent of
\gamma
and
z
.
In the rest of this section we will focus on meromorphic Jacobi forms for
\operatorname{\textsl{SL}}_{2}({\mathbb{Z}})

\Gamma<\operatorname{\textsl{SL}}_{2}({\mathbb{Z}})
will appear in §
3

If
\psi
is a meromorphic Jacobi form then from invariance under the index

z\mapsto z+1

r\in{\mathbb{Z}}+m

\tau\in{\mathbb{H}}

\displaystyle h_{r}(\tau):=\operatorname{e}(mr)q^{\frac{r^{2}}{4m}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}}}\psi(\tau,z-\tfrac{r}{2m}\tau)\operatorname{e}(-rz){\rm d}z

is well-defined for

, so long as
z\mapsto\psi(\tau,z)
has no poles of the form

\beta\in{\mathbb{R}}
. If
z=-\tfrac{r}{2m}\tau+\beta
is a pole for some
\beta\in{\mathbb{R}}
then choose
\beta_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}
so that
z=-\tfrac{r}{2m}\tau+\beta_{0}
is not a pole, let
\gamma
be a deformation of the line segment
[\beta_{0},\beta_{0}+1]
that passes just above the poles of

\bar{\gamma}
be the image of

under complex conjugation, and replace
\int_{{\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}}}
with
\frac{1}{2}\left(\int_{\gamma}+\int_{\bar{\gamma}}\right)

). For

[
16
]

\psi

If
\psi

\psi

\displaystyle\psi^{F}(\tau,z):=\sum_{r{\rm\;mod\;}2m}h_{r}(\tau)\theta_{m,r}(\tau,z),

2.3
Residues

In order to relate meromorphic Jacobi forms to mock modular forms we follow
[
16
]
by restricting to meromorphic Jacobi forms

\psi
such that the poles of
z\mapsto\psi(\tau,z)
, for fixed
\tau\in{\mathbb{H}}
, are constrained to lie in the set of
torsion points

{\mathbb{Q}}\tau+{\mathbb{Q}}
. For simplicity we further restrict to meromorphic Jacobi forms
whose poles are simple. (Single poles and double poles are discussed in
[
16
]
.)
So for
m\in\frac{1}{2}{\mathbb{Z}}
let
J^{\rm mst}_{k,m}
denote the space of meromorphic Jacobi forms
\psi
of weight
k
and index
m
for
\Gamma=\operatorname{\textsl{SL}}_{2}({\mathbb{Z}})

such that the poles of the functions
z\mapsto\psi(\tau,z)
are simple, and occur only at torsion points
z\in{\mathbb{Q}}\tau+{\mathbb{Q}}
.

Our next objective is to present an explicit construction of the difference
\psi^{P}=\psi-\psi^{F}
, which is called the
polar part
of
\psi
.
To prepare for this
observe that
{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}\rtimes\operatorname{\textsl{SL}}_{2}({\mathbb{Z}})
acts naturally from the right on
{\mathbb{Q}}^{2}
according to the rules

\displaystyle(\alpha,\beta)\cdot(\lambda,\mu):=(\alpha+\lambda,\beta+\mu),\quad(\alpha,\beta)\cdot\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{smallmatrix}\right):=(\alpha a+\beta c,\alpha b+\beta d).

(2.13)

Here
(\alpha,\beta)\in{\mathbb{Q}}^{2}
,
(\lambda,\mu)\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
and
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\operatorname{\textsl{SL}}_{2}({\mathbb{Z}})
.
It will be useful to have a description of the orbits of this action. With this in mind we
set

\displaystyle S_{n}:=\left\{(\tfrac{k}{n},\tfrac{l}{n})\in{\mathbb{Q}}^{2}\mid k,l\in{\mathbb{Z}},\,\gcd(k,l,n)=1\right\}

(2.14)

for
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{+}
.

Lemma 2.1
.

The sets
S_{n}
for
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{+}
are the orbits for the action of
{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}\rtimes\operatorname{\textsl{SL}}_{2}({\mathbb{Z}})
on
{\mathbb{Q}}^{2}
.

Proof.

It is straightforward to check that
S_{n}
is stable under the action of

{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}\rtimes\operatorname{\textsl{SL}}_{2}({\mathbb{Z}})
.
To see that
{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}\rtimes\operatorname{\textsl{SL}}_{2}({\mathbb{Z}})
acts transitively observe that if
s=(\tfrac{k}{n},\tfrac{l}{n})\in S_{n}
where
k,l\in{\mathbb{Z}}
and
\gcd(k,l,n)=1
then there exists
j\in{\mathbb{Z}}
such that
\gcd(k,l+jn)=1
. So after replacing
s
with
s\cdot(0,j)
we may assume that
\gcd(k,l)=1
. Then for
x,y\in{\mathbb{Z}}
such that

s\cdot\left(\begin{smallmatrix}x&-l\\
y&k\end{smallmatrix}\right)=(\frac{1}{n},0)
. So
S_{n}
is just the orbit of
(\frac{1}{n},0)
under
{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}\rtimes\operatorname{\textsl{SL}}_{2}({\mathbb{Z}})
.

To see that the
S_{n}
exhaust
{\mathbb{Q}}^{2}
let
S\subset{\mathbb{Q}}^{2}
be an arbitrary orbit for
{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}\rtimes\operatorname{\textsl{SL}}_{2}({\mathbb{Z}})
. If
(\frac{k}{n},\frac{l}{n})\in S
where
k,l,n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
(and the components are not necessarily fractions in lowest form), then
n\alpha
and
n\beta
are integers for every
(\alpha,\beta)\in S
. So there is a smallest positive integer
n
such that
n\alpha
and
n\beta
are integers for every
(\alpha,\beta)\in S
. For this
n
there must be
(\frac{k}{n},\frac{l}{n})\in S
such that
\gcd(k,l,n)=1
. Then
S=S_{n}
by the above.
∎

Given
\psi\in J^{\rm mst}_{k,m}

define
S(\psi)\subset{\mathbb{Q}}^{2}
to be the set of pairs

such that

has a pole
at

for some
\tau\in{\mathbb{H}}
. When there is no risk of confusion we write

in place of

.
For
s=(\alpha,\beta)\in S(\psi)
we follow
[
16
]
in defining

\displaystyle D_{s}(\tau):=2\pi i\operatorname{e}(m\alpha z_{s})\operatorname{Res}_{z=z_{s}}\psi(\tau,z).

(2.15)

From the invariance of
\psi
under the elliptic (
2.1
) and modular (
2.9
) actions
we deduce that

\displaystyle D_{s\cdot(\lambda,\mu)}(\tau)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{e}(m(\alpha\mu-\beta\lambda+\lambda\mu+\lambda+\mu))D_{s}(\tau),

(2.16)

\displaystyle D_{s\cdot\gamma}(\tau)

\displaystyle=D_{s}(\gamma\tau)(c\tau+d)^{1-k},

(2.17)

for
(\lambda,\mu)\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}

Proposition 2.2

Let

for

is weak

for
\Gamma(2n^{2})

Proof.

The growth conditions on

near cusps follow from the definitions of meromorphic and weak meromorphic Jacobi form (cf. §

). To check modularity write

\gamma=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1+2n^{2}a^{\prime}&2n^{2}b^{\prime}\\
2n^{2}c^{\prime}&1+2n^{2}d^{\prime}\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\Gamma(2n^{2})

Then

\displaystyle s\cdot\gamma=(\tfrac{k}{n}+2n\lambda,\tfrac{l}{n}+2n\mu)=s\cdot(2n\lambda,2n\mu)

∎

The next result will be applied in §

Lemma 2.3
.

Let

. Then for

\displaystyle D_{s}(\tau)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}N\in{\mathbb{Z}}\\

Proof.

By Proposition

\displaystyle D_{s}(\tau+n)=D_{s\cdot\gamma}(\tau)=D_{s\cdot(0,1)}(\tau)=\operatorname{e}(m(\tfrac{1}{n}+1))D_{s}(\tau).

This shows that

4
Discussion

In this section we discuss a few features and a possible interpretation
of the pure D-type umbral moonshine module constructions presented in §
3
.

Relation to
{\cal N}=2
Superconformal Algebra

Note that all the meromorphic Jacobi forms

\psi^{({M\over 4})}_{g}
discussed in §
3
have the property that
\psi^{({M\over 4})}_{g}(\Psi_{1,-\frac{1}{2}})^{-1}
is a weight 0 weak (holomorphic) Jacobi form for
\operatorname{\textsl{SL}}_{2}({\mathbb{Z}})
of index
{M\over 4}+{1\over 2}
, where

\Psi_{1,-{1\over 2}}(\tau,z):=i{\eta^{3}(\tau)\over\theta_{1}(\tau,z)}

(4.1)

(cf. (
3.5
)). Then the splitting of meromorphic Jacobi forms into polar and finite parts as described by Theorem
2.4

corresponds precisely to the decomposition of the weight 0 weak Jacobi form

\psi^{({M\over 4})}_{g}(\Psi_{1,-\frac{1}{2}})^{-1}

into characters of the
{\cal N}=2
superconformal algebra. In particular, the universal factor
(\Psi_{1,-\frac{1}{2}})^{-1}
multiplying the polar and finite parts give the contributions from the massless and massive representations (respectively) of the
{\cal N}=2
superconformal algebra of central charge
c=6({M\over 4}+{1\over 2})
.
We refer to §7 of
[
19
]
for details.

This is reminiscent of the situation in the case
X=A_{1}^{\oplus 24}
,
\ell=2
of umbral moonshine (cf.
[
3
]
), namely Mathieu moonshine for
M_{24}
. There, analogous to the other 22 cases of umbral moonshine, we can regard the weight
\frac{1}{2}
mock modular form
H^{(2)}_{g}
for all
g\in M_{24}
as arising from splitting a certain weight 1 index 2 meromorphic Jacobi form
\psi^{(2)}_{g}
into its polar and finite parts (see §3.4 of
[
3
]
). Alternatively, we can regard
H^{(2)}_{g}
as arising from decomposing a weight 0 index 1 weak Jacobi form
\psi^{(2)}_{g}(\Psi_{1,1})^{-1}
into characters of the
{\cal N}=4
superconformal algebra at central charge
6
(see §4.3 of
[
3
]
), where

\Psi_{1,1}(\tau,z):=i{\eta^{3}(\tau)\theta_{1}(\tau,2z)\over\theta_{1}(\tau,z)^{2}}.

(4.2)

Famously, for the identity class
g=e
, the weak Jacobi form
\psi^{(2)}_{g}(\Psi_{1,1})^{-1}
equals the elliptic genus of K3 surfaces, and its
{\cal N}=4
decomposition is precisely the context in which this
M_{24}
moonshine was initially discovered
[
1
]
.

Relation to Pure A-Type Umbral Moonshine Modules

For the other cases of pure A-type, corresponding to the Niemeier root systems
A_{2}^{\oplus 12}
,
A_{3}^{\oplus 8}
,
A_{4}^{\oplus 6}
,
A_{6}^{\oplus 4}
,
A_{8}^{\oplus 3}
,
A_{12}^{\oplus 2}
and
A_{24}
, there is for general group elements
g
no weight 0 weak Jacobi form related to
\psi^{(\ell)}_{g}
via a multiplication of
(\Psi_{1,1})^{-1}
