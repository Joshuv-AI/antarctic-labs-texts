Continuous symmetry breaking of low-dimensional systems driven by inhomogeneous oscillatory driving forces Preprint: AIP/123-QED
Harukuni Ikeda, Yuta Kuroda

Abstract
The driving forces of chiral active particles and deformations of cells are often modeled by spatially inhomogeneous but temporally periodic driving forces. Such inhomogeneous oscillatory driving forces have only recently been proposed in the context of active matter, and their effects on the systems are not yet fully understood. In this work, we theoretically study the impact of spatially inhomogeneous oscillatory driving forces on continuous symmetry breaking. We first analyze the linear model for the soft modes in the ordered phase to derive the lower critical dimension of the model, and then analyze the spherical model to investigate more detailed phase behaviors. Interestingly, our analysis reveals that symmetry breaking occurs even in one and two dimensions, where the Hohenberg–Mermin–Wagner theorem prohibits continuous symmetry breaking in equilibrium. Furthermore, fluctuations of conserved quantities, such as density, are anomalously suppressed in the long-wavelength, i.e. , show hyperuniformity.



I
Introduction

As the temperature is lowered, gas becomes liquid and the liquid becomes
solid. These dramatic changes in physical properties are called phase
transitions
Nishimori and Ortiz 2010
. The phase transitions in
equilibrium systems have been actively studied using powerful tools of
statistical mechanics such as the renormalization
groups
Wilson 1971
, scaling theories
Kadanoff 1966
, exact
solutions
Onsager 1944
;
BRUSH 1967
, and extensive numerical
simulations
Alder and Wainwright 1957
;
Hukushima and Nemoto 1996
;
Frenkel and Smit 2001
.
The phase transitions in non-equilibrium systems are also actively
investigated, though the theoretical development is still in its infancy
compared to that of the equilibrium phase transitions.

One of the most well-known no-go theorems in the theory of equilibrium
phase transitions is the Hohenberg–Mermin–Wagner
theorem
Mermin and Wagner 1966
;
Hohenberg 1967
. This theorem claims that
short-range interacting systems having continuous degrees of freedom do
not show long-range order in one and two dimensions. On the other hand,
the theorem does not hold for non-equilibrium systems, and indeed there
are several examples of low-dimensional systems showing the long-range
order. Examples include the XY model driven by anisotropic
noise
Bassler and Rácz 1995
;
Reichl et al. 2010
,
O(n)
model driven by
shear
Corberi et al. 2002
;
Nakano et al. 2021
;
Ikeda
, and models driven
by anti-correlated noise
Galliano et al. 2023
;
Ikeda 2023a
, polar active
fluids
Vicsek et al. 1995
;
Toner and Tu 1995
;
Ikeda 2024
, and non-reciprocal
systems
Loos et al. 2023
;
Dadhichi et al. 2020
.

A popular class of non-equilibrium systems is periodically driven
systems. For instance, spin systems in an AC field have been
investigated extensively to understand hysteresis of
ferromagnets
Rao et al. 1989
;
Rao et al. 1990
;
Dhar and Thomas 1992
;
Thomas and Dhar 1993
.
Other examples include tapping experiments of granular
systems
Knight et al. 1995
, and emulsions subjected to an oscillatory
shear strain
Hébraud et al. 1997
;
Berthier et al. 2001
. In the above examples,
the systems are driven by the spatially
homogeneous
driving
force. For instance, in a typical setting of magnetic hysteresis, all
spins
i=1,\cdots,N
are driven by the same external field such as

h_{i}(t)=h(t)=a\sin(\omega_{0}t)
, where
a
and
\omega_{0}
denote the
strength and frequency of the driving force, respectively. Recently, a
different type of driving forces have been proposed in the context of
active matter, namely, spatially
inhomogeneous
but temporally
periodic driving forces
Berthier and Kurchan 2019
. For instance,
tissues are often driven by oscillatory deformations of constituent
cells
Zehnder et al. 2015
;
Tjhung and Berthier 2017
. To model this behavior,
Tjhung and Berthier introduced a model consisting of actively-deforming
particles
Tjhung and Berthier 2017
. The diameter
\sigma_{i}
of the

i
-th particle of the model oscillates around its mean value

\sigma_{i}^{0}
as follows:

\displaystyle\sigma_{i}(t)=\sigma_{i}^{0}(1+a\cos(\omega_{0}t+\psi_{i})),

(1)

where
a
and
\omega_{0}
denote the strength and frequency of the
oscillation, respectively, and
\psi_{i}
denotes a random phase
shift
Tjhung and Berthier 2017
. When
\psi_{i}=0
,
Eq. (
1
) represents the affine
compression/decompression, while when
\psi\neq 0
, it leads spatially
inhomogeneous deformations. Another example of inhomogeneous driving
force appears in chiral active matter, where constituent particles
spontaneously rotate due to the periodic nature of the driving
force
van Teeffelen and Löwen 2008
;
Debets et al. 2023
. A popular numerical model to
describe the rotational motion of a chiral active particle
in two dimensions
is
Callegari and Volpe 2019

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{i}=-\frac{\partial V}{\partial x_{i}}+h\cos\theta_{i}+\xi_{i,x},

\displaystyle\dot{y}_{i}=-\frac{\partial V}{\partial y_{i}}+h\sin\theta_{i}+\xi_{i,y},

\displaystyle\dot{\theta}_{i}=\omega_{0}+\xi_{\theta},

(2)

where
(x_{i},y_{i})
denotes position of the
i
-th particle,

\xi_{i,x,y,\theta}
denotes the noise, and
V
denotes the interaction
potential. In the absence of the noise
\xi_{i,x,y,\theta}=0
, the
equations are reduced to

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{i}=-\frac{\partial V}{\partial x_{i}}+h_{i,x},

\displaystyle\dot{y}_{i}=-\frac{\partial V}{\partial y_{i}}+h_{i,y}.

(3)

Here the particle is driven by the inhomogeneous driving force

h_{i,x}=h\cos(\omega_{0}t+\psi_{i})
and
h_{i,y}=h\sin(\omega_{0}t+\psi_{i})
, where the phase shift
\psi_{i}
is determined by the initial
condition
\psi_{i}=-\omega_{0}t_{\rm ini}+\theta_{i}(t_{\rm ini})
. The
phase behavior of this type of system, including actively deforming
particles and chiral active matter, has been studied
recently
Liebchen and Levis 2022
;
Huang et al. 2020
;
Huang et al. 2021
;
Lin et al. 2022
;
Semwal et al. 2022
;
Tjhung and Berthier 2017
;
Zhang and Fodor 2023
;
Li et al. 2024
.
However, the effects of the inhomogeneous driving force on the phase
transition have not been fully understood yet.

This study focuses on continuous symmetry breakings such as
crystallization and nematic transition systems driven by inhomogeneous
oscillatory driving forces. In particular, we show that continuous
symmetry-breaking can occur even in low dimensions
d\leq 2
for systems
driven solely by inhomogeneous oscillatory driving forces. To achieve
this goal, we first investigate the linear model and derive the lower
critical dimension. Here we only assume that the model has at least one
massless mode in the ordered phase, and thus, the result should be
applied for general types of continuous symmetry breaking such as
crystallization and nematic phase transitions. Next, we investigate the
spherical model in order to discuss more detailed phase
behaviors
Joyce 1966
;
Nishimori and Ortiz 2010
.

The spherical model was first introduced by Berlin and Kac as a
simplification of the Ising model
Berlin and Kac 1952
. The model
can be solved analytically in any dimension, both in equilibrium and
nonequilibrium
Stanley 1968
;
Nishimori and Ortiz 2010
;
Henkel et al. 2008
.
In equilibrium, the model undergoes the second-order phase transition
from the paramagnetic to the ferromagnetic phase at finite
temperatures. The critical exponents of the transition agree with those
of the
n\to\infty
limit of the
O(n)

model
Nishimori and Ortiz 2010
. In previous studies, the spherical
model driven by the homogeneous oscillatory driving forces has been
investigated extensively in the context of the magnetic
hysteresis
Rao et al. 1989
;
Rao et al. 1990
;
Dhar and Thomas 1992
;
Thomas and Dhar 1993
,
and also structural glasses
Berthier et al. 2001
. However, to the best of
our knowledge, the spherical model driven by the inhomogeneous
oscillatory driving force has not been investigated so far.

The structure of the manuscript is as follows. In
Sec.
II
, we investigate the linear model by using the
scaling analysis. In Sec.
III
, we investigate the
spherical model. In Sec.
IV
, we shortly discuss the
behavior of the conserved order parameter such as density. In
particular, we show that the fluctuations of the conserved quantity are
highly suppressed, implying that the model exhibits
hyperuniformity
Torquato 2018
. In
Sec.
V
, we conclude the work.

II
Linear model

When continuous symmetries are spontaneously broken, there arise soft
modes called the Nambu–Goldstone (NG)
modes
Nambu 1960
;
Goldstone et al. 1962
. In equilibrium, the fluctuations
of NG modes diverge in
d\leq 2
, which destroys the long-range order.
Therefore, continuous symmetries are not spontaneously broken for
d\leq 2
in equilibrium systems. This fact is nowadays widely known as the
Hohenberg–Mermin–Wagner theorem
Hohenberg 1967
;
Mermin and Wagner 1966
. We
here investigate the scaling behavior of the NG modes to discuss the
stability of the ordered phase of systems driven by inhomogeneous
oscillatory driving forces. Let
u
be the displacement along one of the
NG modes. We assume that
u
follows the following phenomenological
linear equation:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial u(\bm{x},t)}{\partial t}=\nabla^{2}u(\bm{x},t)+f(\bm{x},t),

(4)

\displaystyle f(\bm{x},t)=\xi(\bm{x},t)+h(\bm{x},t),

(5)

where
\xi(\bm{x},t)
denotes a Gaussian white noise satisfying

\displaystyle\left\langle\xi(\bm{x},t)\right\rangle=0,

\displaystyle\left\langle\xi(\bm{x},t)\xi(\bm{x}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\right\rangle=2T\delta(\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\prime})\delta(t-t^{\prime}),

(6)

and
h(\bm{x},t)
represents an inhomogeneous oscillatory driving force:

\displaystyle h(\bm{x},t)=\sqrt{2D}\cos(\omega_{0}t+\psi(\bm{x}))\left|d\bm{x}\right|^{-1/2},

(7)

where
\left|d\bm{x}\right|
denotes the volume of the unit cell of the
discretization.
T
and
D
stand for the strength of the noise and the
driving force, respectively. In Eq. (
7
), we
introduced the random phase shift
\psi(\bm{x})
distributed uniformly in

[0,2\pi]

Berthier and Kurchan 2019
. The case with
\psi(\bm{x})=0

corresponds to a homogeneous driving force. The mean and variance of

h(\bm{x},t)
are
Zwanzig 2001

\displaystyle\left\langle h(\bm{x},t)\right\rangle=0,

\displaystyle\left\langle h(\bm{x},t)h(\bm{x}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\right\rangle=D\delta(\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\prime})\cos(\omega_{0}(t-t^{\prime})),

(8)

where we used
\delta(\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\prime})=\lim_{\left|d\bm{x}\right|\to 0}\delta_{\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime}}/\left|d\bm{x}\right|
in the continuum limit. In
Eqs. (
6
) and (
8
), the symbol

\left\langle\bullet\right\rangle
represents either the average over the noise
\xi
or
the randomness
\psi
. Equation (
8
) implies that the
time translational symmetry is restored after taking the average for the
quenched randomness
\psi(\bm{x})
. To investigate the scaling behavior,
we consider the following scaling
transformation
Medina et al. 1989
;
Toner and Tu 1995

\displaystyle x\to bx,\ t\to b^{z}t,\ u\to b^{\chi}u.

(9)

To calculate the scaling dimension of the driving force
f(\bm{x},t)
, we
observe the fluctuation induced by
f(\bm{x},t)
in
d+1
dimensional
Euclidean space
[0,l]^{d}\times[0,t]

Torquato 2018
:

\displaystyle\sigma(l,t)

\displaystyle=\left\langle\left(\int_{\bm{x}^{\prime}\in[0,l]^{d}}\hskip-14.22636ptd\bm{x}^{\prime}\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}f(\bm{x}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\right)^{2}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sigma_{\xi}(l,t)+\sigma_{h}(l,t),

(10)

where

\displaystyle\sigma_{\xi}(l,t)

\displaystyle=\left\langle\left(\int_{\bm{x}^{\prime}\in[0,l]^{d}}\hskip-14.22636ptd\bm{x}^{\prime}\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\xi(\bm{x}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\right)^{2}\right\rangle,

\displaystyle\sigma_{h}(l,t)

\displaystyle=\left\langle\left(\int_{\bm{x}^{\prime}\in[0,l]^{d}}\hskip-14.22636ptd\bm{x}^{\prime}\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}h(\bm{x}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\right)^{2}\right\rangle.

(11)

For
T>0
and
\omega_{0}\neq 0
, we get
\sigma_{\xi}\sim l^{d}t
and

\sigma_{h}\sim l^{d}t^{0}
, leading to
\sigma\sim\sigma_{\xi}\sim l^{d}t
for
l\gg 1
and
t\gg 1
. Since
\sigma\sim t^{2}l^{2d}f^{2}
,
we have
{f(\bm{x},t)\to b^{-z/2}b^{-d/2}f(\bm{x},t)}
for
T>0
and

\omega_{0}\neq 0
. On the contrary, for
T=0
and
\omega_{0}\neq 0
, we
get
\sigma=\sigma_{h}\sim l^{d}t^{0}
, leading to
{f(\bm{x},t)\to b^{-z}b^{-d/2}f(\bm{x},t)}
. For
\omega_{0}=0
,
h(\bm{x},t)\propto\cos\psi(\bm{x})
is a time-independent constant force, which leads to the
ballistic behavior
\sigma\sim\sigma_{h}\sim l^{d}t^{2}
. Therefore, we
get
{f(\bm{x},t)\to b^{-d/2}f(\bm{x},t)}
for
\omega_{0}=0
. In summary,
after the scaling transformation, Eq. (
4
) reduces to

\displaystyle b^{\chi-z}\frac{\partial u(\bm{x},t)}{\partial t}=b^{\chi-2}\nabla^{2}u(\bm{x},t)+b^{z_{f}}f(\bm{x},t),

\displaystyle z_{f}=\begin{cases}-\frac{z}{2}-\frac{d}{2}&T>0,\omega_{0}\neq 0\\
-z-\frac{d}{2}&T=0,\omega_{0}\neq 0\\
-\frac{d}{2}&\omega_{0}=0\end{cases}.

(12)

Assuming all terms have the same scaling dimension, we get the following
scaling relations
Nishimori and Ortiz 2010
;
Maggi et al. 2022
:

\displaystyle\chi-z=\chi-2=z_{f},

(13)

leading to

\displaystyle z=2,

\displaystyle\chi=\begin{cases}1-\frac{d}{2}&T>0,\omega_{0}\neq 0\\
-\frac{d}{2}&T=0,\omega_{0}\neq 0\\
2-\frac{d}{2}&\omega_{0}=0\end{cases}.

(14)

To see the stability of the ordered phase,
we observe the fluctuations of the NG modes
Toner and Tu 1995
:

\displaystyle\left\langle\delta u^{2}\right\rangle\sim b^{2\chi}.

(15)

For the ordered phase to be stable,
\chi
must be negative; otherwise,
the fluctuations diverge in the thermodynamic limit
b\to\infty
, and
the long-range order is destroyed
Toner and Tu 1995
. Therefore, the
lower-critical dimension can be determined by setting
\chi=0
:

\displaystyle d_{l}=\begin{cases}2&T>0,\omega_{0}\neq 0\\
0&T=0,\omega_{0}\neq 0\\
4&\omega_{0}=0\end{cases}.

(16)

The above equation is the main result of this paper. For
T>0
and

\omega_{0}\neq 0
, we get
d_{l}=2
, as in
equilibrium
Mermin and Wagner 1966
;
Hohenberg 1967
, meaning that the periodic
driving forces do not change the lower critical dimension at finite

T
. On the contrary, for
T=0
and
\omega_{0}\neq 0
, we get
d_{l}=0
,
meaning that the long-range order can appear even in
d=1
and
d=2
.
For
\omega_{0}=0
, we get
d_{l}=4
. This is natural since

h(\bm{x},t)\propto\cos\psi(\bm{x})
plays the role of the random field,
which destroys the order in the systems with continuous symmetry for

d\leq 4
according to the Imry–Ma
arguments
Imry and Ma 1975
;
Nishimori and Ortiz 2010
.
Equation (
16
) should also be applied for an arbitrary
function
h(\bm{x},t)
that is temporally periodic and spatially
uncorrelated for which
\sigma_{h}\sim l^{d/2}t^{0}
.

The argument so far is phenomenological but quite general, where we only
assume the existence of the NG modes. Eq. (
16
) should
be applied for general types of continuous symmetry breaking such as the
ferromagnetic phase transition of the
O(n)
model with
n\geq 2
,
crystalization, and nematic phase transition.

However, the above linear model cannot describe the phase transitions.
To investigate the phase behaviors more closely, in the next section, we
investigate the mean-spherical model
Berlin and Kac 1952
, which
corresponds to the
n\to\infty
limit of the
O(n)
model and can be
solved exactly
Nishimori and Ortiz 2010
.

III
Spherical model

For concreteness, we here introduce and investigate the mean-spherical
model and discuss more detailed phase behaviors. Let
\phi(\bm{x},t)
be a
non-conserved order parameter such as the magnetization. We assume that
the time evolution of
\phi(\bm{x},t)
follows the model-A
dynamics
Hohenberg and Halperin 1977
:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\phi(\bm{x},t)}{\partial t}=-\frac{\delta{\mathcal{F}}[\phi]}{\delta\phi(\bm{x},t)}+f(\bm{x},t),

(17)

where the driving force
f(\bm{x},t)
is defined by
Eq. (
5
). The free-energy
{\mathcal{F}}[\phi]

of the mean-spherical model
is defined as
Crisanti et al. 2019
;
Ikeda 2023a
:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{F}}[\phi]=\int d\bm{x}\left[\frac{(\nabla\phi)^{2}}{2}+\frac{\mu\phi^{2}}{2}\right],

(18)

where
\mu
denotes the Lagrange multiplier to impose the mean-spherical
constraint :

\displaystyle\int d\bm{x}\left\langle\phi(\bm{x})^{2}\right\rangle=N.

(19)

The model has been studied extensively in equilibrium
D=0
, where the
critical exponents agree with those of the large
n
limit of the
O(n)

model
Henkel et al. 2008
;
Nishimori and Ortiz 2010
. Below we investigate
the phase behaviors of the model in the thermodynamics limit:

N\to\infty
and
\int d\bm{x}=V\to\infty
with fixed ratio
\rho=N/V
.

We shall make some comments on
\mu
.
Equation (
17
) is written as

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\phi(\bm{x},t)}{\partial t}=-(\mu-\nabla^{2})\phi(\bm{x},t)+f(\bm{x},t).

(20)

By integrating Eq. (
20
) w.r.t.
\bm{x}
, we get

\dot{m}(t)=-\mu m(t)
, where
m(t)=V^{-1}\int d\bm{x}\phi(\bm{x},t)
. The
Lagrange multiplier should satisfy
\mu\geq 0
since otherwise, the
steady state becomes unstable. In the steady state
\dot{m}=-\mu m=0
.
This condition is automatically satisfied in the disordered phase
m=0
.
In the ordered phase
m>0
, on the contrary,
\mu
should vanish

\mu=0
. Therefore, in the ordered phase Eq. (
20
)
reduces to the linear equation Eq. (
4
) analyzed in the
previous section. As a consequence, the lower critical dimension
d_{l}

of the mean-spherical model is given by Eq. (
16
), as we
will confirm later.

We decompose the order parameter
\phi(\bm{x},t)
into the mean value
m

and fluctuations
\tilde{\phi}(\bm{x},t)\equiv\phi(\bm{x},t)-m
. Since
\dot{m}=-\mu m=0
in the steady state,
\tilde{\phi}(\bm{x},t)
satisfies the
same equation as Eq. (
20
). Thus, the Fourier transform
of the equation leads to

\displaystyle i\omega\tilde{\phi}(\bm{q},\omega)=-(q^{2}+\mu)\tilde{\phi}(\bm{q},\omega)+f(\bm{q},\omega),

(21)

where
q=\left|\bm{q}\right|
and

\displaystyle\tilde{\phi}(\bm{q},\omega)=\int d\bm{x}dte^{-i\bm{q}\cdot\bm{x}-i\omega t}\tilde{\phi}(\bm{x},t),

(22)

From Eq. (
21
), we get

\displaystyle\tilde{\phi}(\bm{q},\omega)=\frac{f(\bm{q},\omega)}{i\omega+(q^{2}+\mu)}.

(23)

The two-point correlation is then calculated as

\displaystyle\left\langle\phi(\bm{q},\omega)\phi(\bm{q}^{\prime},\omega^{\prime})\right\rangle

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{d+1}\delta(\bm{q}+\bm{q}^{\prime})\delta(\omega+\omega^{\prime})S(\bm{q},\omega),

(24)

where
\phi(\bm{q},\omega)
denotes the Fourier transform of
\phi(\bm{x},t)
,
and

\displaystyle S(\bm{q},\omega)

\displaystyle=m^{2}(2\pi)^{d+1}\delta(\omega)\delta(\bm{q})+\frac{2T}{\omega^{2}+(q^{2}+\mu)^{2}}

\displaystyle+\frac{D\pi}{\omega_{0}^{2}+(q^{2}+\mu)^{2}}\left[\delta(\omega+\omega_{0})+\delta(\omega-\omega_{0})\right].

(25)

The static correlation is obtained by integrating
Eq. (
25
) over
\omega
:

\displaystyle S(\bm{q})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int d\omega S(\bm{q},\omega)

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{d}m^{2}\delta(\bm{q})+\frac{T}{q^{2}+\mu}+\frac{D}{\omega_{0}^{2}+(q^{2}+\mu)^{2}}.

(26)

The remaining task is to determine the Lagrange multiplier
\mu
by the
spherical constraint:

\displaystyle N=\int d\bm{x}\left\langle\phi(\bm{x},t)^{2}\right\rangle=\frac{V}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int d\bm{q}S(\bm{q}).

(27)

Substituting Eq. (
26
) into Eq. (
27
), we
get

\displaystyle 1=\frac{m^{2}}{\rho}+TF(\mu)+DG(\mu),

(28)

where

\displaystyle F(\mu)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Omega_{d}}{(2\pi)^{d}\rho}\int_{0}^{q_{D}}\frac{dqq^{d-1}}{q^{2}+\mu},

\displaystyle G(\mu)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Omega_{d}}{(2\pi)^{d}\rho}\int_{0}^{q_{D}}\frac{dqq^{d-1}}{\omega_{0}^{2}+(q^{2}+\mu)^{2}}.

(29)

Here
q_{D}
denotes the phenomenological cutoff, and
\Omega_{d}

denotes the
d
-dimensional solid angle.

Now we discuss the phase behavior for finite
T
. For sufficiently high

for the model-A and
d>6
for the
model-B. For simplicity we here set
\rho=\Omega_{d}/(2\pi)^{d}
,
q_{D}=1
.

Finally, we consider the
\omega_{0}
dependence of the phase behavior. For

\omega_{0}\neq 0
, all qualitative phase behaviors discussed above remain
unchanged. On the contrary, when
\omega_{0}=0
,
G(0)
diverges for

d\leq 4
, leading to
D_{c}\to 0
and
T_{c}\to-\infty
. Therefore, the
phase transition does not occur for all
d\leq 4
. This is a natural
result because
h(\bm{x},t)
for
\omega_{0}=0
plays the role of a random
field, which destroys the long-range order for
d\leq 4
according to
the Imry–Ma argument for continuous variables
Imry and Ma 1975
. In
Fig.
3
(a), we show
D
–
d
phase diagram for
various
\omega_{0}
to visualize how the ordered phase for
d\leq 4

disappears in the limit
\omega_{0}\to 0
. The above result implies

d_{l}=4
, which is consistent with the linear model
Eq. (
16
).

IV
Conserved order parameter

Figure 4:
S(q)
for model-B. The
solid lines denote
S(q)
for
T=0
,
10^{-5}
,
10^{-3}
,
10^{-1}

from bottom to top. The black dashed line denotes
S(q)\propto q^{2}
. When
T=0
, the model exhibits hyperuniformity
S(q)\propto q^{2}

for
q\ll 1
. For simplicity, here we set
D=1
,
\omega_{0}=1
, and

\mu=1
.

So far, we discussed the phase behavior for the non-conserved order
parameter. Below we discuss what will happen for the conserved order
parameter such as density. A conserved quantity
\phi(\bm{x},t)
follows
the continuity equation:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\phi(\bm{x},t)}{\partial t}=-\nabla\cdot\bm{J}(\bm{x},t),

(32)

where
\bm{J}
denotes the flux. In the model-B dynamics,
\bm{J}
is
written as
Hohenberg and Halperin 1977
;
Onuki 2002
;
Cates 2019

\displaystyle\bm{J}(\bm{x},t)=-\nabla\frac{\delta{\mathcal{F}}[\phi]}{\delta\phi(\bm{x},t)}+\bm{f}(\bm{x},t),

\displaystyle\bm{f}(\bm{x},t)=\bm{\xi}(\bm{x},t)+\bm{h}(\bm{x},t),

(33)

where
{\mathcal{F}}[\phi]
is the free-energy of the mean-spherical model Eq. (
18
),
and
\bm{\xi}=\{\xi_{a}\}_{a=1,\cdots,d}

and
\bm{h}=\{h_{a}\}_{a=1,\cdots,d}
satisfy

\displaystyle\left\langle\xi_{a}(\bm{x},t)\right\rangle=0,

\displaystyle\left\langle\xi_{a}(\bm{x},t)\xi_{b}(\bm{x}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\right\rangle=2T\delta_{ab}\delta(\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\prime})\delta(t-t^{\prime}),

\displaystyle\left\langle h_{a}(\bm{x},t)\right\rangle=0,

\displaystyle\left\langle h_{a}(\bm{x},t)h_{b}(\bm{x}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\right\rangle=\delta_{ab}\delta(\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\prime})D\cos(\omega_{0}(t-t^{\prime})).

(34)

A calculation very similar to that in the previous section yields

\displaystyle S(\bm{q})

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{d}m^{2}\delta(\bm{q})+\frac{T}{q^{2}+\mu}+\frac{Dq^{2}}{\omega_{0}^{2}+q^{4}(q^{2}+\mu)^{2}}.

(35)

When
T=0
for
q\neq 0
, we get

\displaystyle\lim_{T\to 0}S(\bm{q})=\frac{Dq^{2}}{q^{4}(q^{2}+\mu)^{2}+\omega_{0}^{2}}.

(36)

For
q\ll 1
, we observe
S(q)\sim q^{2}
: the large-scale fluctuations
are highly suppressed in the limit
T\to 0
, see
Fig.
4
. This anomalous suppression of the
fluctuations is referred to as
hyperuniformity
Torquato 2018
. Hyperuniformity has
been also reported in other periodically driven systems, such as chiral
active matter
Huang et al. 2021
;
Zhang and Snezhko 2022
and pulsating
epithelial tissues
Li et al. 2024
. In previous theoretical works, the
authors in Refs.
Lei and Ni 2019
;
Lei et al. 2019

discussed that hyperuniformity of the chiral active matter is a
consequence of the conservation of the center of
mass
Hexner and Levine 2017
. Our model instead assets that this
phenomenon is induced by the periodic nature of the driving force. A
recent theoretical calculation by the fluctuating hydrodynamics also
supports this conclusion, see
Ref.
Kuroda and Miyazaki 2023
. However, it is not necessary that all
instances of hyperuniformity come from a single underlying cause. For
instance, the long-range hydrodynamic interaction is another source of
hyperuniformity
Torquato 2021
;
Huang et al. 2021
;
Oppenheimer et al. 2022
. Further studies would be beneficial to
elucidate this point.

When
\omega_{0}=0
,
S(\bm{q})
shows the power-low divergence
S(q)\sim q^{-2}
for
q\ll 1
, meaning that the large-scale fluctuations
are significantly enhanced even in the disordered phase. The same power-low
behavior has been previously reported for the scalar active matter in
quenched random potentials
Ro et al. 2021
.

The Lagrange multiplier
\mu
is to be determined by

\displaystyle 1=\frac{m^{2}}{\rho}+TF_{B}(\mu)+DG_{B}(\mu),

(37)

where
\rho=N/V
and

\displaystyle F_{B}(\mu)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Omega_{d}}{(2\pi)^{d}\rho}\int_{0}^{q_{D}}\frac{dqq^{d-1}}{q^{2}+\mu},

\displaystyle G_{B}(\mu)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Omega_{d}}{(2\pi)^{d}\rho}\int_{0}^{q_{D}}\frac{dqq^{d-1}q^{2}}{\omega_{0}^{2}+q^{4}(q^{2}+\mu)^{2}}.

(38)

Then, one can draw the critical line by
1=m^{2}/\rho+TF_{B}(0)+DG_{B}(0)

as in the case of the model-A. Note that
m
should be considered as the
control parameter since the model-B dynamics conserves
m
. We here
only discuss the behavior for
m=0
. We show the phase diagrams in
Figs 1–3 (b), and the lower critical dimension is

\displaystyle d_{l}=\begin{cases}2&T>0,\omega_{0}\neq 0\\
0&T=0,\omega_{0}\neq 0\\
6&\omega_{0}=0\end{cases}.

(39)

For
\omega_{0}\neq 0
, the qualitative phase behaviors agree with that
of the model-A. However, for
\omega_{0}=0
,
G_{B}(0)
diverges for
d\leq 6
, leading to
d_{l}=6
. Therefore, the ordered phase does not appear
for
d\leq 6
, see Fig.
3
(b). This result also
consistent with the Imry–Ma argument for the continuous symmetry
breaking of the active matter in quenched random
fields
Ro et al. 2021
.

V
Summary and discussions

In summary, we have introduced and investigated
the linear and spherical model driven by the temporally periodic but
spatially inhomogeneous driving forces. We found that in the absence of
the thermal noise, the lower critical dimension is
d_{l}=0
, meaning that
the models show the continuous symmetry breaking even in
d=1
and

2
. Furthermore, the model for the conserved order parameter (model-B)
shows hyperuniformity.

It is well-known from the Imry–Ma argument that time-independent
inhomogeneous external fields prohibit the continuous symmetry breaking
for
d\leq 4

Imry and Ma 1975
. One may expect that the time dependence
of the external field introduces extra complexity, which makes the
system more disordered. However, our result demonstrated that the
inhomogeneous oscillatory external field allows the continuous symmetry
breaking for
d\leq 4
. To the best of our knowledge, this
counter-intuitive result has not been reported before.

It is also surprising that our model undergoes continuous symmetry
breaking even in
d=1
. Although there are several known examples of
non-equilibrium phase transitions in one
dimension
Czirók et al. 1999
;
O’Loan and Evans 1999
;
Solon and Tailleur 2013
;
Evans and Hanney 2005
;
Henkel et al. 2008
;
Hexner and Levine 2017
,
the continuous symmetry breaking is hardly observed in
d=1
, though our
recent studies reported several other promising
candidates
Ikeda 2023a
;
Ikeda 2023b
.

To derive the lower critical dimension, Eq. (
16
), we
only assumed the existence of the NG modes. Therefore, the result

d_{l}=0
should be generally applied for continuous symmetry breaking of
periodically driven systems. For instance, our theory can be tested in
crystal phases of chiral active
particles
Callegari and Volpe 2019
;
Liebchen and Levis 2022
and actively-deforming
particles
Tjhung and Berthier 2017
;
Parisi et al. 2023
by observing the
translational order parameters. We hope our results will be verified in
numerical simulations and experiments of those systems.

Note added: After the previous version was submitted, one of the authors
confirmed that chiral active particles can indeed exhibit long-range
crystalline order even in two dimensions
Kuroda et al. 2024
.
