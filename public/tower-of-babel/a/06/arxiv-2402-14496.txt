First-principle tight-binding approach to angle-resolved photoemission spectroscopy simulations: importance of light-matter gauge and ubiquitous interference effects
Yun Yen, Gian Parusa, Michael Schüler

Abstract
Angle-resolved photoemission spectroscopy (ARPES) is one of the most powerful techniques to study the electronic structure of materials. To go beyond the paradigm of band mapping and extract aspects of the Bloch wave-functions, the intricate interplay of experimental geometry, crystal structure, and photon polarization needs to be understood. In this work we discuss several model approaches to computing ARPES signals in a unified fashion. While we represent the Bloch wave-functions by first-principle Wannier functions, we introduce different approximations to the final states and discuss the implications for the predictive power. We also introduce various light-matter gauges and explain the role of the inevitable breaking of gauge invariance. Finally, we benchmark the different models for the two-dimensional semiconductor WSe 2 , known for its strong Berry curvature, orbital angular momentum (OAM), and nontrivial orbital texture. The models are compared based on their ability to simulate photoemission intensity and interpret circular dichroism in ARPES (CD-ARPES). We show that interference effects are crucial to understanding the circular dichroism, and explain their photon-energy dependence. Our in-depth analysis provides insights into the advantages and limitations of various model approaches in clarifying the complex interplay between experimental observables and underlying orbital texture in materials.



I
Introduction

The rise of materials with nontrival topological properties
Hasan and Kane 2010
was paralleled and driven by the advances of angle-resolved photoemission spectroscopy (ARPES)
Schönhense et al. 2018
;
Sobota et al. 2021
;
Rader et al. 2021
;
Lv et al. 2019
;
Dil 2019
;
Lu et al. 2012
. This experimental techniques provides direct access to the band structure of the occupied Bloch states in periodic solids. More recently, pump-probe time-resolved ARPES (trARPES) extended the capability to map out also the excited states
Boschini et al. 2020
.

Beyond extracting the band energies
\varepsilon_{\alpha}(\mathbf{k})
, the focus has shifted more recently towards accessing aspects of the underlying wave-function
\psi_{\mathbf{k}\alpha}(\mathbf{r})
. In particular, the intricate dependence of the spectra on the polarization of the photons and the experimental geometry has been shown to contain fingerprints of the wave-function
Hwang et al. 2011
;
Beaulieu et al. 2021
;
Beaulieu et al. 2020
;
Ünzelmann et al. 2021
;
Yen et al. 2023
. The most prominent example is circular dichroism in ARPES (CD-ARPES)
Schönhense 1990
;
Fedchenko et al. 2019
. For simple systems, a link between the circular dichroism in the angular distribution (CDAD) and the orbital angular momentum (OAM) can be established
Park et al. 2012a
;
Gierz et al. 2012
;
Wang and Gedik 2013
;
Cho et al. 2018
;
Cho et al. 2021
;
Schüler et al. 2020a
;
Schüler et al. 2020b
. For more complex systems however, the extraction of the precise orbital texture is difficult due to a number of complicating effects.
Multiple interference of the partial waves originating from the same and from different atoms give rise to pronounced dependence on the photon energy
Kern et al. 2023
;
Gierz et al. 2011
;
Heider et al. 2023
while the scattering of the photoelectrons off the lattice imprint a complex energy dependence onto the final states of the photoemission process
Strocov et al. 2023
.

While first-principle methods
Krasovskii and Schattke 1999
;
Krasovskii et al. 2007
;
Ebert et al. 2011
;
De Giovannini et al. 2017
;
Ono et al. 2021
– akin numerical experiments – are available and can help understand experiments, a more transparent method would be beneficial to clarify the connection between spectra and the Bloch wave-functions. To this end, various model approaches have been employed with the goal of disentangling the contributions to the photoemission signal
Moser 2017
;
Day et al. 2019
;
Schüler et al. 2022
;
Feidt et al. 2019
;
Schüler and Beaulieu 2022
;
Moser 2023
. However, potentially uncontrolled approximations, inequivalent schemes of incorporating the light-matter coupling, and unnecessary assumptions make it unclear which model provides the best compromise of accuracy and transparency.

In this paper we tackle this problem by a systematic assessment of the different approximation schemes. We start by a rigorous derivation, where we introduce the different light-matter gauges. In particular, we employ the modern theory of polarization to construct the dipole operator
Resta 1998
from a quantum geometrical perspective.
The Bloch wave-functions
\psi_{\mathbf{k}\alpha}(\mathbf{r})
of the occupied bands are obtained from first-principle density-function theory (DFT), whereas the photoelectron states are treated approximately.

Finally, we benchmark the different models against each other for the paradigmatic two-dimensional semiconductor WSe
2
, which exhibits strong Berry curvature, OAM, and nontrivial orbital texture in the valence bands.
This system allows us to display the advantage of the model approach to ARPES, as we can analyze the various contributions to the simulated photoemission intensity. In particular, we discuss the importance of interference effects in the photoemission process, which gives rise to a pronounced photon-energy dependence of the CDAD and a complex texture. We then discuss to what extend the CDAD can be linked to the OAM under typical experimental conditions.

II
Wannier function approach

We consider a 2D material or the surface of a bulk material, described by the effective single-particle Hamiltonian

\displaystyle\hat{H}=-\frac{\nabla^{2}}{2}+\hat{V}\ .

(1)

Here, and it what follows, we use atomic units (a.u.) consistently unless stated otherwise.
Typically,
\hat{H}
is taken as the Kohn-Sham Hamiltonian within the framework of density-functional theory (DFT), but other choices such as Hartree-Fock are possible. For the sake of clarity we assume the potential energy operator
\hat{V}
represents a local potential
\hat{V}\rightarrow V(\mathbf{r})
(the generalization to non-local potentials is straightforward). The potential is periodic in the plane parallel to the surface,
V(\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{R})=V(\mathbf{r})
, with
\mathbf{R}
denoting the in-plane lattice vectors. According to the Bloch theorem, the eigenstates of the Hamiltonian (
1
) are the Bloch states
\hat{H}|\psi_{\mathbf{k}\alpha}\rangle
with the Bloch-phase condition
\psi_{\mathbf{k}\alpha}(\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{R})=e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{R}}\psi_{\mathbf{k}\alpha}(\mathbf{r})
. The quasi-momentum
\mathbf{k}
is a vector from the 2D Brillouin zone (BZ). While it seems that this formulation is geared towards 2D materials, bulk materials are also captured by considering a slab geometry, where the information about the layers translates into, in principle infinitely, many bands
\alpha
.

II.1
Bloch states: bound states and photoelectron states

The Bloch states
|\psi_{\mathbf{k}\alpha}\rangle
can be grouped into bound states with a discrete band index
\alpha
, and photoelectron states (also known as inverse LEED states) in the continuum. For bound states, we can employ the Wannier representation

\displaystyle\psi_{\mathbf{k}\alpha}(\mathbf{r})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{\mathbf{R}j}e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{R}}C_{j\alpha}(\mathbf{k})\phi_{j}(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}_{j}-\mathbf{R})\ .

(2)

Here,
\phi_{j}(\mathbf{r})
denote the Wannier orbitals, which are localized at the Wannier center
\mathbf{r}_{j}
. Within empirical TB approaches such as the Slater-Koster theory, the Wannier orbitals can be approximated by atom-like orbitals centered at the atomic sites
\mathbf{r}_{j}
. The Wannier orbitals can also be constructed from the Bloch states
\psi_{\mathbf{k}\alpha}(\mathbf{r})
by the Wannierization procedure, delivering projective or maximally localized Wannier functions
Marzari et al. 2012
. The other ingredients in Eq. (
2
) are the number of lattice sites
N\rightarrow\infty
, and the rotation coefficients
C_{j\alpha}(\mathbf{k})
which contain the amplitude of Wannier orbital
j
in the band
\alpha
.

The Hamiltonian (
1
) also hosts photoelectron states, which we label as
|\chi_{\mathbf{p}}\rangle
with the three-dimensional momentum
\mathbf{p}=\mathbf{p}_{\parallel}+p_{\perp}\hat{z}
and energy
\varepsilon_{\mathbf{p}}=\mathbf{p}^{2}/2
. The photoelectron states also obey the Bloch phase condition
\chi_{\mathbf{p}}(\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{R})=e^{i\mathbf{p}_{\parallel}\cdot\mathbf{R}}\chi_{\mathbf{p}}(\mathbf{r})
. Computing
|\chi_{\mathbf{p}}\rangle
from first principles is challenging, but possible within the one-step approach to ARPES.

II.2
Fermi’s Golden Rule

With the eigenstates of the Hamiltonian defined, we can compute the photoemission intensity from Fermi’s Golden Rule

\displaystyle I(\mathbf{p})=\sum_{\mathbf{k}\alpha}f_{\alpha}(\mathbf{k})|\langle\chi_{\mathbf{p}}|\hat{\Delta}|\psi_{\mathbf{k}\alpha}\rangle|^{2}\delta(\varepsilon_{\alpha}(\mathbf{k})+\omega-\varepsilon_{\mathbf{p}})\ .

(3)

Here,
f_{\alpha}(\mathbf{k})
denotes the occupation of the Bloch state
|\psi_{\mathbf{k}\alpha}\rangle
(band energy
\varepsilon_{\alpha}(\mathbf{k})
). The Dirac delta function in Eq. (
3
)
ensures the energy conservation of the photoemission process. The light-matter coupling is incorporated through the operator
\hat{\Delta}
. Within the minimal coupling scheme, it is given by

\displaystyle\hat{\Delta}=\mathbf{e}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{p}}\ ,

(4)

where
\mathbf{e}
denotes the polarization vector.

The Bloch-phase condition for both initial and final states in the matrix element in Eq. (
3
) implies the conservation of the in-plane momentum, as

\displaystyle\langle\chi_{\mathbf{p}}|\hat{\Delta}|\psi_{\mathbf{k}\alpha}\rangle

\displaystyle=\int d\mathbf{r}\,\chi^{*}_{\mathbf{p}}(\mathbf{r})\hat{\Delta}\psi_{\mathbf{k}\alpha}(\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{R}}\int_{\mathrm{UC}}d\mathbf{r}\,\chi^{*}_{\mathbf{p}}(\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{R})\hat{\Delta}\psi_{\mathbf{k}\alpha}(\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{R})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{R}}e^{i(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{p}_{\parallel})\cdot\mathbf{R}}\int_{\mathrm{UC}}d\mathbf{r}\,\chi^{*}_{\mathbf{p}}(\mathbf{r})\hat{\Delta}\psi_{\mathbf{k}\alpha}(\mathbf{r})\ .

Here,
\int_{\mathrm{UC}}
denotes the integration over the 2D unit cell. The sum over
\mathbf{R}
in the last line yields
N\delta_{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{G},\mathbf{p}_{\parallel}}
with a reciprocal lattice vector
\mathbf{G}
. Focusing on the first BZ (
\mathbf{G}=0)
, we can thus re-label
|\chi_{\mathbf{p}}\rangle\rightarrow|\chi_{\mathbf{k},E}\rangle
and simplify Fermi’s Golden rule (
3
) to

\displaystyle I(\mathbf{k},E)=\sum_{\alpha}f_{\alpha}(\mathbf{k})|M_{\alpha}(\mathbf{k},E)|^{2}\delta(\varepsilon_{\alpha}(\mathbf{k})+\omega-E)\ .

(5)

Here,
E
denotes the energy of the photoelectron
E=(\mathbf{k}^{2}+p^{2}_{\perp})/2
, while
M_{\alpha}(\mathbf{k},E)=\langle\chi_{\mathbf{k},E}|\hat{\Delta}|\psi_{\mathbf{k}\alpha}\rangle
stands for the photoemission matrix element.

The advantage of the Wannier representation (
2
) displays itself in the transparent exansion of the matrix elements. Exploiting the momentum conservation, one finds

\displaystyle M_{\alpha}(\mathbf{k},E)=\sqrt{N}\sum_{j}C_{j\alpha}(\mathbf{k})M^{\mathrm{orb}}_{j}(\mathbf{k},E)

(6)

Eq. (
6
) allows us to interpret the photoemission matrix element as a coherent sum of
orbital
matrix elements

\displaystyle M^{\mathrm{orb}}_{j}(\mathbf{k},E)=\int d\mathbf{r}\,\chi^{*}_{\mathbf{k},E}(\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{r}_{j})\hat{\Delta}\phi_{j}(\mathbf{r})

(7)

in terms of the Wannier orbitals. Whenever a band
\alpha
is dominant by a particular orbital character, only one coefficient
C_{j\alpha}(\mathbf{k})
enters the sum in Eq. (
6
). In this case, the symmetries of the relevant orbital directly manifest in the photoemission matrix elements. In contrast, if several coefficients
C_{j\alpha}(\mathbf{k})
contribute, interferences of the different orbital channels complicate the photoemission signal.

While we have employed a particular choice for the light-matter coupling (
4
) directly following the minimal coupling scheme (known as velocity gauge), Fermi’s Golden rule remains valid for other choices.
As long as the initial states
|\psi_{\mathbf{k}\alpha}\rangle
and the final states
|\chi_{\mathbf{k},E}\rangle
are eigenstates of the same Hamiltonian (
1
), gauge-invariance is guaranteed. Choosing the light-matter coupling operator
\hat{\Delta}=\mathbf{e}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{r}}

(dipole gauge) or
\hat{\Delta}=\mathbf{e}\cdot\nabla V(\mathbf{r})

(acceleration gauge) yields (up to a prefactor) the identical expression for the intensity (
5
). Additional approximations break the gauge invariance, rendering the choice of the gauge an important step in practical calculations.

II.3
Dipole gauge

In the dipole gauge the light-matter coupling is represented by
\hat{\Delta}=\mathbf{e}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{r}}
. While the dipole operator
\hat{\mathbf{r}}
itself is ill-defined for periodic solids, the matrix elements with respect to different Bloch states is well-defined
Resta 1998
;
Resta 2010
:

\displaystyle\langle\psi_{\mathbf{k}\alpha}|\hat{\mathbf{r}}|\psi_{\mathbf{k}\alpha^{\prime}}\rangle=i\langle u_{\mathbf{k}\alpha}|\nabla_{\mathbf{k}}u_{\mathbf{k}\alpha^{\prime}}\rangle\quad\mathrm{for}\quad\alpha\neq\alpha^{\prime}\ ,

(8)

where
u_{\mathbf{k}\alpha}(\mathbf{r})=e^{-i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r}}\psi_{\mathbf{k}\alpha}(\mathbf{r})
denote the cell-periodic Bloch states.
Eq. (
8
) establishes a link between the inter-band dipole matrix element and quantum geometric properties: the right-hand side of Eq. (
8
) is nothing else than the Berry connection
\mathbf{A}_{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}(\mathbf{k})
.

Since the photoelectron states
|\chi_{\mathbf{k},E}\rangle
are eigenstates of
\hat{H}
, we can interpret the photoemission matrix element as an inter-band dipole matrix element. Separating the polarization in the in-plane (
\mathbf{e}_{\parallel}
) and out-of-plane (
e_{\perp}
) components, one finds

\displaystyle M_{\alpha}(\mathbf{k},E)

\displaystyle=i\mathbf{e}_{\parallel}\cdot\langle\tilde{\chi}_{\mathbf{k},E}|\nabla_{\mathbf{k}}u_{\mathbf{k}\alpha}\rangle+e_{\perp}\langle\chi_{\mathbf{k},E}|z|\psi_{\mathbf{k}\alpha}\rangle\ ,

(9)

where
\tilde{\chi}_{\mathbf{k},E}(\mathbf{r})=e^{-i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r}}\chi_{\mathbf{k},E}(\mathbf{r})
is the cell-periodic part of photoelectron state. Note that the broken periodicity in the
z
direction renders the corresponding dipole operator the same as for finite systems. For evaluating the first term on the right-hand side of Eq. (
9
), we employ the Wannier representation (
2
) for the cell-periodic Bloch wave-function. It is convenient for this purpose to re-define the switch the convention of the coefficients,
C_{j\alpha}(\mathbf{k})=e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r}_{j}}\tilde{C}_{j\alpha}(\mathbf{k})
. Taking the
\mathbf{k}
-derivative of
u_{\mathbf{k}\alpha}(\mathbf{r})
and exploiting the translational invariance, one then finds the dipole matrix element

\displaystyle\mathbf{D}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{k},E)\equiv i\langle\tilde{\chi}_{\mathbf{k},E}|\nabla_{\mathbf{k}}u_{\mathbf{k}\alpha}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sqrt{N}\sum_{j}e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r}_{j}}\tilde{C}_{j\alpha}(\mathbf{k})\int d\mathbf{r}\,\chi^{*}_{\mathbf{k},E}(\mathbf{r})(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}_{j})\phi_{j}(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}_{j})

\displaystyle\quad+i\sqrt{N}\sum_{j}e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r}_{j}}\nabla_{\mathbf{k}}\tilde{C}_{j\alpha}(\mathbf{k})\int d\mathbf{r}\,\chi^{*}_{\mathbf{k},E}(\mathbf{r})\phi_{j}(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}_{j})\ .

(10)

The first contribution to the dipole matrix elements (
II.3
) is analogous to the coherent sum (
6
) of orbital matrix elements (
7
). We refer to it as atom-centered contribution
\mathbf{D}^{\mathrm{AC}}(\mathbf{k},E)
, whereas the second term on the right-hand side of Eq. (
II.3
) originates from the quantum geometry of the Bloch states as encoded in the
\mathbf{k}
-dependence of the coefficients
\tilde{C}_{j\alpha}(\mathbf{k})
. While such quantum geometric contributions to dipole matrix elements play an important role in linear and nonlinear excitation phenomena in solids
Ahn et al. 2022
;
von Gersdorff and Chen 2021
;
Baykusheva et al. 2021
, for in example in transport and high-harmonic generation, they typically play only a minor role in the photoemission process. First of all, the
\mathbf{k}
-derivative acts only on the in-plane coordinates,
which means that quantum geometric contributions are only relevant for in-plane photon polarization. Furthermore, the magnitude of the second term in Eq. (
II.3
) is small compared to
\mathbf{D}^{\mathrm{AC}}(\mathbf{k},E)
unless the coefficients
\tilde{C}_{j\alpha}(\mathbf{k})
exhibit a strong momentum dependence.

In the following, we will refer to
\mathbf{D}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{k},E)\approx\mathbf{D}^{\mathrm{AC}}(\mathbf{k},E)
as the atom-centered approximation (ACA). As we will show below, the ACA as presented in this section provides the formal foundation for the ad-hoc approaches often encountered in the literature
Park et al. 2012b
;
Day et al. 2019
;
Devereaux et al. 2021
.

Exploiting the orthogonality of photoelectron and bound states, we can simplify the photoemission matrix element within the ACA as

\displaystyle M^{\mathrm{ACA}}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{k},E)=\sqrt{N}\sum_{j}C_{j\alpha}(\mathbf{k})M^{\mathrm{orb,ACA}}_{j}(\mathbf{k},E)

(11)

with

\displaystyle M^{\mathrm{orb,ACA}}_{j}(\mathbf{k},E)=\int d\mathbf{r}\,\chi^{*}_{\mathbf{k},E}(\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{r}_{j})\mathbf{e}\cdot\mathbf{r}\phi_{j}(\mathbf{r})\ .

(12)

Eqs. (
11
)–(
12
) are identical to the matrix elements within the minimal coupling scheme (
6
)–(
7
) upon replacing
\hat{\Delta}\rightarrow\mathbf{e}\cdot\mathbf{r}
. However, we stress that gauge invariance is broken when employing the ACA, and velocity gauge and dipole gauge will yield different results.

II.4
Plane-wave approximation

Up to this point, we have assumed exact photoelectron states, i. e.
\hat{H}|\chi_{\mathbf{k},E}\rangle=E|\chi_{\mathbf{k},E}\rangle
. However, explicitly calculating
|\chi_{\mathbf{k},E}\rangle
is challenging. Finding approximations to the photoelectron states that balance accuracy and simplicity is the practical path forward that we will take here.

The simplest possible approximation is the plane-wave approximation (PWA):
\chi_{\mathbf{k},E}(\mathbf{r})\approx e^{i\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{r}}
. The PWA is based on the fact that at high energy
E
the crystal potential plays only a minor role, rendering
|\chi_{\mathbf{k},E}\rangle
an approximate eigenstate of the kinetic energy only. While the PWA generally becomes more accurate at large
E
, this trend is not monotonic
Strocov et al. 2023
. Assuming the PWA to be applicable for now, we can make use of the property

\displaystyle\chi_{\mathbf{k},E}(\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{r}_{j})=e^{i\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{r}}e^{z_{j}/\lambda}\chi_{\mathbf{k},E}(\mathbf{r})\ .

(13)

Here, we have additionally taken into account the mean-free path
\lambda
of the photoelectrons, resulting in the attenuation factor
e^{z_{j}/\lambda}
, which suppresses the photoemission signal from atoms far away from the surface.

Due to
\hat{\mathbf{p}}|\chi_{\mathbf{k},E}\rangle\approx\mathbf{p}|\chi_{\mathbf{k},E}\rangle
, the matrix elements in the velocity gauge simplify to

\displaystyle M_{\alpha}(\mathbf{k},E)=\mathbf{e}\cdot\mathbf{p}\sum_{j}e^{-i\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{r}_{j}}e^{z_{j}/\lambda}C_{j\alpha}(\mathbf{k})\tilde{\phi}_{j}(\mathbf{p})\ ,

(14)

where
\tilde{\phi}_{j}(\mathbf{p})=\int d\mathbf{r}\,e^{-i\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{r}}\phi_{j}(\mathbf{r})
denotes the Fourier transform of the Wannier orbital
\phi_{j}(\mathbf{r})
.
The matrix elements (
14
) vanish for perpendicular polarization vector
\mathbf{e}
and photoelectron momentum
\mathbf{p}
, which is a serious shortcoming of the approximation. Furthermore, there is no circular dichroism as long as
\mathbf{p}
is real
Moser 2017
. Despite the shortcomings of the PWA in the velocity gauge, the interference effects inherent to the photoemission process are already present in Eq. (
14
): the phase factors
e^{-i\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{r}_{j}}
incorporate position of the atomic sites in the unit cell.

Using the dipole gauge and the ACA, we can evaluate the orbital matrix elements (
12
):

\displaystyle M^{\mathrm{orb,ACA}}_{j}(\mathbf{k},E)=e^{-i\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{r}_{j}}e^{z_{j}/\lambda}\int d\mathbf{r}\,e^{-i\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{r}}\mathbf{e}\cdot\mathbf{r}\phi_{j}(\mathbf{r})\ .

(15)

Inserting the orbital matrix elements (
15
) into Eq. (
11
) then yields the band-dependend photoemission matrix elements.
Similarly, the geometric phase factors
e^{-i\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{r}_{j}}
give rise to interference effects of different partial waves emitted from the different sub-lattice sites in the crystal. The orbital matrix elements (
15
) are not necessarily zero for
\mathbf{e}\perp\mathbf{p}
, and circular dichroism is also possible (depending on the orbital character of the Wannier orbitals
\phi_{j}(\mathbf{r})
). Therefore, the combination of dipole gauge, ACA, and PWA already provide a realistic description of photoemission beyond the corresponding velocity-gauge formulation.

II.5
Locally distorted wave approximation

While the PWA includes basic features of the photoemission process, such as the coherent superposition of atomic transition amplitudes, many important effects are missing. In particular, the effective potential
V(\mathbf{r})
has strong dips close to the atomic positions, which distorts the partial waves originating from each atom. To account for this effect, we introduce the locally distorted wave approximation (LDWA), where the photoelectron wave-function around each atomic site
\mathbf{r}_{j}
is expanded in spherical harmonics:

\displaystyle\chi_{\mathbf{k},E}(\mathbf{r})=4\pi\sum^{\infty}_{\ell=0}\sum^{\ell}_{m=-\ell}i^{\ell}\frac{w_{j,\ell}(r)}{r}Y_{\ell m}(\Omega_{\mathbf{r}})Y^{*}_{\ell m}(\Omega_{\mathbf{p}})\ .

(16)

Here,
\Omega_{\mathbf{r}}
(
\Omega_{\mathbf{p}}
) denotes the solid angle of the vector
\mathbf{r}
(
\mathbf{p}
), while
Y_{\ell m}(\Omega)
stand for the spherical harmonics. The physical picture behind the expansion (
16
) is that the potential around each atom is spherically symmetric:
V(\mathbf{r})\approx v_{j}(|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}_{j}|)
for
\mathbf{r}\approx\mathbf{r}_{j}
. The radial wave-functions are then determined by

\displaystyle\left[-\frac{1}{2}\frac{d^{2}}{dr^{2}}+\frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{2r^{2}}+v_{j}(r)\right]w_{j,\ell}(r)=Ew_{j,\ell}(r)\ ,

(17)

together with the outgoing scattering boundary conditions.

A useful special case of the LDWA is obtained by approximating the local atomic potential by an effective Coulomb potential
v_{j}(r)\approx-Z_{j}/r
. In this case, the radial wave-functions can be found explicitly
Joachain 1975
:

\displaystyle w_{j,\ell}(r)=\frac{1}{p}\exp\left(-i\sigma_{\ell}(\eta_{j})\right)F_{\ell}(\eta_{j},pr)\ ,

(18)

where
\sigma_{\ell}(\eta)=\mathrm{arg}[\Gamma(\ell+1+i\eta)]
is the Coulomb phase shift,
\eta_{j}=-Z_{j}/p
is the Sommerfeld parameter, and
F_{\ell}(\eta,x)
denotes the regular Coulomb function
Olver et al. 2010
. We refer to Eq. (
18
) as local Coulomb-wave approximation (LCWA). Note that the LCWA includes the PWA in the limit
\eta\rightarrow 0
, since
F_{\ell}(\eta,x)\rightarrow rj_{\ell}(x)
and
\sigma_{\ell}\rightarrow 0
. In contrast to the PWA, the phase property (
13
) must be assumed as additional approximation to proceed.

Assuming the validity Eq. (
13
), the orbital matrix elements in the velocity gauge (
7
) are transformed to

\displaystyle M^{\mathrm{orb}}_{j}(\mathbf{k},E)

\displaystyle=4\pi e^{-i\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{r}_{j}}e^{z_{j}/\lambda_{j}}\sum_{\ell m}(-i)^{\ell}Y_{\ell m}(\Omega_{\mathbf{p}})

\displaystyle\quad\times\int d\mathbf{r}\,\frac{w^{*}_{j,\ell}(r)}{r}Y^{*}_{\ell m}(\Omega_{\mathbf{r}})\mathbf{e}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{p}}\phi_{j}(\mathbf{r})\ .

(19)

Similarly, we obtain the corresponding orbital matrix element within the dipole gauge and the ACA by replacing
\mathbf{e}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{p}}\rightarrow\mathbf{e}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{r}}
.

The phase of radial wave-functions
w_{j,\ell}(r)
(which is given by
e^{i\sigma_{j,\ell}(p)}
within the LCWA) introduces an energy-dependent phase shift between the partial waves originating from each atomic site, which is absent within the PWA. Therefore, the LDWA can reproduce full-fledged first-principle simulations or experimental spectra better than the PWA. In practice, the LCWA has been tested for a few systems in ref.
Moser 2023
and ref.
Tanaka et al. 2023
with promising results.

II.6
Radial integrals

One of the main advantage of the Wannier representation (
2
) is the fact that the complicated wave-like Bloch state
\psi_{\mathbf{k}\alpha}(\mathbf{r})
can be interpreted as a superposition of quasi-atomic orbitals
\phi_{j}(\mathbf{r})
. Indeed, within empirical TB approaches such as the Slater-Koster method, the orbitals
\phi_{j}(\mathbf{r})
are chosen as atom-like orbitals with spherical symmetry. Within the first-principle Wannierization approach, atom-like orbitals can also be imposed or obtained approximately in the framework of projective Wannier functions. Upon maximizing the projectiblity
Qiao et al. 2023
, the atom-like orbitals that best span the Bloch states can be found. Either way, if we assume

\displaystyle\phi_{j}(\mathbf{r})=\frac{u_{j}(r)}{r}Y_{\ell_{j}m_{j}}(\Omega_{\mathbf{r}})\ ,

(20)

the expressions for the orbital matrix elements can be simplified significantly. Furthermore, the interpretation in terms of the different cross sections of the dipole-allowed transitions becomes even more transparent.

In the velocity gauge and using the commutation relation
\hat{\mathbf{p}}=-i[\hat{\mathbf{r}},\hat{\mathbf{p}}^{2}]/2
, one finds

\displaystyle\int d\mathbf{r}\,\frac{w^{*}_{j,\ell}(r)}{r}Y^{*}_{\ell m}(\Omega_{\mathbf{r}})\hat{p}_{\mu}\phi_{j}(\mathbf{r})=-i(-1)^{m}C_{\mu}(\ell m,\ell_{j}m_{j})\left[J^{(1)}_{j,\ell}+\left(\frac{\ell_{j}(\ell_{j}+1)-\ell(\ell+1)}{2}\right)J^{(2)}_{j,\ell}\ .\right]

(21)

Here we have defined the angular coefficients

\displaystyle C_{\mu}(\ell_{1}m_{1},\ell_{2}m_{2})=\sqrt{\frac{4\pi}{3}}\sum^{1}_{s-1}u^{s}_{\mu}\begin{bmatrix}\ell_{1}&1&\ell_{2}\\
-m_{1}&s&m_{2}\end{bmatrix}

(22)

with the vectors
\mathbf{u}^{-1}=(1,i,0)^{T}/\sqrt{2}
,
\mathbf{u}^{0}=(0,0,1)^{T}
,
\mathbf{u}^{+1}=(1,-i,0)^{T}/\sqrt{2}
, while the matrix in square brackets denote the Gaunt coefficients
Joachain 1975
. The only information about the radial wave-function
R_{j}(r)
is encoded in the radial integrals

\displaystyle J^{(1)}_{j,\ell}=\int^{\infty}_{0}dr\,w^{*}_{j,\ell}(r)u^{\prime}_{j}(r)\ ,\ J^{(2)}_{j,\ell}=\int^{\infty}_{0}dr\,w^{*}_{j,\ell}(r)\frac{1}{r}u_{j}(r)\ .

(23)

The derivation for the dipole gauge is similar. One finds

\displaystyle\int d\mathbf{r}\,\frac{w^{*}_{j,\ell}(r)}{r}Y^{*}_{\ell m}(\Omega_{\mathbf{r}})\hat{e}_{\mu}\phi_{j}(\mathbf{r})=(-1)^{m}C_{\mu}(\ell m,\ell_{j}m_{j})I_{j,\ell}

(24)

with the radial integral

\displaystyle I_{j,\ell}=\int^{\infty}_{0}dr\,w^{*}_{j,\ell}(r)ru_{j}(r)\ .

(25)

Substituting either Eq. (
24
) or (
21
)
into Eq. (
II.5
) then yields the orbital matrix elements.

As in atomic physics, the dipole selection rule (encoded in the coefficients (
22
)) constrains the angular momentum of the outgoing wave to
\ell=\ell_{j}\pm 1
, while the magnetic quantum number
m=m_{j},m_{j}\pm 1
depending on the polarization. Thus, the orbital matrix element (
II.5
) is fully characterized by the integrals
J^{(1)}_{j,\ell_{j}\pm 1}
and
J^{(2)}_{j,\ell_{j}\pm 1}
in velocity gauge, and by
I_{j,\ell_{j}\pm 1}
in dipole gauge.

In practice, the radial integrals (
23
) or (
25
) can be computed from the Wannier orbitals (or the closest approximation by atom-like orbitals), providing a first-principle framework to computing the photoemission matrix elements within the various approximations to the final states. Alternatively, one can treat the integrals as fitting parameters to model photoemission spectra obtained from experiments.

II.7
Gauge invariance considerations

While the equivalence of dipole gauge in velocity gauge is broken by invoking the ACA, the orbital matrix elements are still equivalent if the same partial-wave expansion for the final states
w_{\ell}(r)
is used around each atom. This equivalence is an expression of
local
gauge invariance. Consider the purely atomic problem with a spherically symmetric potential
v(r)
. If both the bound states
u_{j}(r)
and the final states
w_{\ell}(r)
obey the same radial Schrödinger equation,

\displaystyle\left[-\frac{1}{2}\frac{d^{2}}{dr^{2}}+\frac{\ell_{j}(\ell_{j}+1)}{2r^{2}}+v(r)\right]u_{j}(r)=\varepsilon_{j}u_{j}(r)

\displaystyle\left[-\frac{1}{2}\frac{d^{2}}{dr^{2}}+\frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{2r^{2}}+v(r)\right]w_{\ell}(r)=\varepsilon w_{\ell}(r)\ ,

(26)

the radial integrals in both gauges are related by

\displaystyle J^{(1)}_{j,\ell}+\left(\frac{\ell_{j}(\ell_{j}+1)-\ell(\ell+1)}{2}\right)J^{(2)}_{j,\ell}=(\varepsilon_{j}-\varepsilon)I_{j,\ell}\ .

(27)

The constraint (
27
) is useful for ensuring consistency between the gauge choices, which is especially important when invoking additional approximations to the partial waves of the photoelectron states
w_{\ell}(r)
. As an important application, Eq. (
27
) allows us to choose the effective charges
Z_{j}
within the LCWA in a consistent manner. Assuming we have access to the orbital energies
\varepsilon_{j}
and (a guess of) the radial wave-functions
u_{j}(r)
,
Z_{j}
can be chosen such that the violation of Eq. (
27
) is minimal. We demonstrate the power of this approach below.

Illustrative example: monolayer tungsten diselenide

III.3
Interference effects

From the discussion of the final state channels it becomes clear that both the W and the Se atoms contribute to the photoemission signal and the CDAD in particular. This is especially important to explain the oscillatory photon energy dependence observed in Fig.

With the Wannier-ARPES models we can analyze the various interference channels in detail. To this end we rewrite the CDAD directly in terms of the orbital matrix elements

IV
Conclusions

We have presented a detailed derivation and discussion of the various model approaches to calculating ARPES intensities. Accurately representing the Bloch wave-functions in terms of quasi-atomic Wannier functions allowed us to highlight the role of approximating the final states in combination with choosing the light-matter gauge. In case the potential
V(\mathbf{r})
around each atom is available, one can directly compute the locally distorted waves, yielding realistic radial integrals and phase shifts.
Comparison to experiments
Yen et al. 2023
shows that complementing the resulting LDWA with such first-principle input provides a very accurate description of ARPES, as long as multi-band effects in the final states do not play an important role. As good proxy for the local potential we suggested to compute the distorted waves for Kohn-Sham potential of the isolated atoms, which will not deviate much from the potential in the crystal. The quality of this approximation can be assessed by inspecting how well the Bloch wave-function can be represented in the basis of isolated atoms. For our benchmark system, monolayer WSe
2
, this criterion is fulfilled with high accuracy.

Taking the LDWA as a benchmark, we tested the different other approximations. While the popular PWA provides a reasonable overall description, it fails to reproduce the correct photon-energy dependence. This shortcoming is explained by the incorrect behavior of the radial integrals, predicting the wrong energy dependence of the ratio of contributions from W and Se atoms. Interestingly, the approximating the final states as local Coulomb waves around each atom yields results comparable to the first-principle LDWA, provided the violation of the local gauge invariance condition is minimized. This principle is expected to be applicable generally, and should be the preferred approximation in case the local potential around each atom is not available.

We then utilized the main advantage of Wannier-ARPES models as compared to one-step calculations: the ability to distenangle and understand the various contributions to the total signal. In particular, we highlighted the ubiquitous interference effects in the CDAD. Besides intra-atomic inteference, which already complicates the signal if
d
orbitals are involved, inter-atomic interference plays the main role for the photon-energy dependence of the CDAD observed discussed here (and observed in experiments
Scholz et al. 2013
;
Fedchenko et al. 2019
;
Yen et al. 2023
).

With the inevitable interferences, how can one link the OAM as property of the Bloch wave-function to the CDAD? In general, a one-to-one correspondence can not be established. Nevertheless, the interference analysis presented in this work enables a few possible strategies. First of all, inter-atomic interference only plays a role if (1) the relevant Bloch states are comprised of orbitals localized at different lattice sites, and (2) the cross sections of these orbitals are comparable. Since at high photon energies one atomic cross section typically dominates, performing experiments in the X-ray range reduces the inteferences. This strategy works for systems like
WSe
2
, where the relative contribution of the single W atom per unit cell can be enhanced. For materials with multiple atoms of the same species in the unit cell, the inter-atomic interference can not be eliminated even at large photon energy. A more promising, albeit material-specific, strategy is to exploit crystal symmetries. For instance, rotating WSe
2
by 60
∘
emulates a time-reversal operation
Beaulieu et al. 2020
. Taking the difference of the circular (or linear) dichroism partially removes interference terms
Schusser et al. 2024
, as the anti-symmetric contribution is enhanced. A third option is to identify specific features in the CDAD that remain robust when changing the photon energy, as demonstrated in ref.
Yen et al. 2023
.

Ultimately, combining experiments with accurate modeling, as presented in this paper, is most promising route to extracting detailed information from ARPES measurements.Disentangling the various intereference channels greatly facilitates the separation of intrinsic material properties and extrinsic effects. Combining the Wannier-ARPES methods presented in this work with first-principle input provides an excellent tool for zeroing in on this goal.
