The relationship between bipolar magnetic regions and their sunspots
K. L. Yeo, S. K. Solanki, N. A. Krivova, J. Jiang

Abstract
Context. The relationship between bipolar magnetic regions (BMRs) and their sunspots is an important property of the solar magnetic field, but it is not well constrained. One consequence is that it is a challenge for surface flux transport models (SFTMs) based on sunspot observations to determine the details of BMR emergence, which they require as input, from such data. Aims. We aimed to establish the relationship between the amount of magnetic flux in newly emerged BMRs and the area of the enclosed sunspots, and examine the results of its application to an established SFTM. Methods. Earlier attempts to constrain BMR magnetic flux were hindered by the fact that there is no extensive and reliable record of the magnetic and physical properties of newly emerged BMRs currently available. We made use of the empirical model of the relationship between the disc-integrated facular and network magnetic flux and the total surface coverage by sunspots reported in a recent study. The structure of the model is such that it enabled us to establish, from these disc-integrated quantities, an empirical relationship between the magnetic flux and sunspot area of individual newly emerged BMRs, circumventing the lack of any proper BMR database. Results. Applying the constraint on BMR magnetic flux derived here to an established SFTM retained its key features, in particular its ability to replicate various independent datasets and the correlation between the model output polar field at the end of each cycle and the observed strength of the following cycle. The SFTM output indicates that facular and network magnetic flux rises with increasing sunspot magnetic flux at a slowing rate such that it appears to gradually saturate. This is analogous to what earlier studies comparing disc-integrated quantities sensitive to the amount of faculae and network present to sunspot indices had reported. The activity dependence of the ratio of facular and network flux to sunspot flux is consistent with the findings of recent studies: although the Sun is faculae-dominated (such that its brightness is mostly positively correlated with activity), it is only marginally so as facular and network brightening and sunspot darkening appear to be closely balanced.



1
Introduction

The global solar dynamo produces the cyclic emergence of bipolar magnetic regions (BMRs) on the solar surface which underlies the 11-year activity cycle
(
Charbonneau 2020
)
. Emergent BMRs range from the larger sunspot-bearing magnetic bipoles, which go on to form active regions, to the smaller spot-free and relatively short-lived (timescale of hours) ephemeral regions (ERs)
(
Harvey 1993
;
Harvey 2000
;
Hagenaar 2001
;
Hagenaar et al. 2003
;
Thornton & Parnell 2011
)
. How the larger BMRs relate to the sunspots they enclose and how ER emergence relates to the number of sunspots present are of interest as they connect directly to how solar magnetism and activity relate to sunspot prevalence, with implications for the study of a wide range of solar phenomena.

The large-scale photospheric magnetic field evolves as BMRs emerge, their magnetic flux is transported and dispersed by systematic and turbulent motions, and magnetic elements of opposite polarity approach and cancel each other
(
van Driel-Gesztelyi & Green 2015
)
. This process can be described via the surface flux transport equation
(
Leighton 1964
;
DeVore et al. 1984
)
or, at a simpler level, by a closed system of ordinary differential equations
(
Solanki et al. 2000
;
Solanki et al. 2002
)
. Models have been developed to reproduce the past and forecast the future evolution of the large-scale photospheric magnetic field by solving the transport equation for a given set of initial conditions and details of BMR emergence, aptly termed surface flux transport models (SFTMs)
(
Jiang et al. 2014
)
. Some studies have simulated the evolution over the past few centuries by prescribing to SFTMs the details of BMR emergence over this period, as determined from sunspot number or sunspot area records
(
Wang et al. 2005
;
Cameron et al. 2010
;
Jiang et al. 2011a
;
Jiang et al. 2011b
, e.g.)
. Sunspot number and sunspot area records are employed as they are the only direct observations of solar surface magnetism to go this far back in time. Otherwise, SFTMs usually make use of solar magnetograms
(
Whitbread et al. 2018
;
Virtanen et al. 2018
;
Yeates 2020
, e.g.)
or Ca II K spectroheliograms
(
Virtanen et al. 2019
, e.g.)
instead. While magnetogram and Ca II K spectroheliogram observations do not extend as far back in time as sunspot observations, the details of BMR emergence can be established more directly and accurately from such data.

Since the large-scale photospheric magnetic field is the observable part of the global dynamo and determines the structure of the coronal magnetic field, SFTMs represent a powerful tool in the study of these solar phenomena
(
Cameron et al. 2012
;
Mackay & Yeates 2012
;
Jiang et al. 2013
)
. Models of solar irradiance variability are often employed to generate the historical solar forcing input required by climate simulations
(
Haigh 2007
)
and to investigate how the brightness variability of the Sun compares to that of other cool stars
(
Shapiro et al. 2014
;
Shapiro et al. 2015
;
Shapiro et al. 2016
;
Nèmec et al. 2020a
;
Nèmec et al. 2020b
)
. Through their incorporation into models of solar irradiance variability, discussed next, SFTMs are also relevant to the study of the Earth’s climate and of the solar-stellar connection.

Solar irradiance variability on timescales greater than a day is believed to be mainly driven by the variation in the prevalence and distribution of dark sunspots and bright faculae and network on the solar disc as the photospheric magnetic field evolves
(
Solanki et al. 2013
;
Yeo et al. 2017
)
. Accordingly, solar irradiance models describe the variation in this quantity due to the intensity deficit from sunspots and the excess from faculae and network, and this information is determined from observations of solar magnetism
(
Domingo et al. 2009
;
Yeo et al. 2014a
)
.

The variation in solar irradiance over the past few centuries, a period of particular interest to climate studies, is often modelled from sunspot number and sunspot area records
(
Lean 2000
;
Krivova et al. 2007
;
Krivova et al. 2010
;
Dasi-Espuig et al. 2014
;
Dasi-Espuig et al. 2016
;
Coddington et al. 2016
;
Wu et al. 2018
, e.g.)
. Modelling solar irradiance variability with sunspot observations means inferring not just the effect of sunspots, but also the effect of faculae and network on solar irradiance from information about sunspots alone. This can be aided by SFTMs aimed at reproducing the evolution of the large-scale photospheric magnetic field from the same sunspot observations. For example,
Coddington et al. 2016
incorporated into their solar irradiance reconstruction the decadal variability in the SFTM reported by
Wang et al. 2005
. In another instance, the solar irradiance reconstruction by
Dasi-Espuig et al. 2014
;
Dasi-Espuig et al. 2016
is based on synthetic full-disc magnetograms generated from sunspot area and sunspot number records using the SFTM of
Cameron et al. 2010
and
Jiang et al. 2011a
;
Jiang et al. 2011b
(hereafter CJSS).

Various studies found the facular area to increase with increasing sunspot area at a diminishing rate such that it appears to gradually saturate
(
Foukal 1993
;
Foukal 1996
;
Foukal 1998
;
Chapman et al. 1997
;
Shapiro et al. 2014
)
.
Solanki & Fligge 1998
and
Yeo et al. 2020
(hereafter YSK), compared various disc-integrated quantities that are sensitive to the amount of faculae and network present to sunspot area and/or sunspot number and returned analogous results. As first pointed out by
Foukal 1993
, these results suggest that the balance between the competing effects of bright faculae and network and of dark sunspots on solar irradiance might shift towards the latter as we go from low to high activity levels. This is believed to extend to cool stars in general, such that as we go from less to more active stars, we transit from a faculae-dominated regime where facular and network brightening dominates starspot darkening to a spot-dominated regime where the converse is true. This explains why less active stars appear to become brighter with increasing activity, while more active stars exhibit the opposite trend
(
Lockwood et al. 2007
;
Hall et al. 2009
;
Shapiro et al. 2014
;
Radick et al. 2018
)
. The balance between bright faculae and network and dark sunspots on the solar disc, and how that might change with the activity level and the inclination of the Sun to the observer, is of interest for the relevance to the understanding of the brightness variability of the Sun and other cool stars. In recent studies
Nèmec et al. 2020a
;
Nèmec et al. 2020b
extended the model of solar irradiance variability by
Dasi-Espuig et al. 2014
;
Dasi-Espuig et al. 2016
, which incorporates the SFTM by CJSS, to probe the inclination-dependence of this balance.

In order to reproduce the evolution of the large-scale photospheric magnetic field over a given period of time, SFTMs require as input the physical properties of each BMR that had emerged over this time interval, including the timing, location, and amount of magnetic flux at emergence. However, deriving this information from observations of sunspots is less than straightforward. As ERs do not contain or emerge synchronously with sunspots and are difficult to observe due to their relatively small sizes and short lifetimes, the relationship between ER emergence and sunspot prevalence is not exactly known. As for sunspot-bearing BMRs, it is less than straightforward to infer the details of their emergence from sunspot number records as these are essentially sunspot and sunspot group counts, and therefore do not contain information on individual sunspot groups. While sunspot area records do tabulate the location and area of individual sunspot groups, how the amount of magnetic flux in a given newly emerged sunspot-bearing BMR,
\Phi_{\rm BMR}
, depends on the area of the enclosed sunspots,
A_{\rm BMR,S}
, is not well constrained. By the term ‘newly emerged’ we refer to the point where the sunspot-bearing BMR has fully emerged but not yet started to decay. This paper focuses on the issue of the relationship between
\Phi_{\rm BMR}
and
A_{\rm BMR,S}
. For the sake of brevity, hereafter the term BMR denotes sunspot-bearing BMRs alone, excluding ERs.

The difficulty in establishing BMR emergence from sunspot observations leaves SFTMs to make varying assumptions. The
Wang et al. 2005
model, based on the
Hoyt & Schatten 1998
sunspot group number record, accommodated the fact that there is no information on individual sunspot groups in such data by assigning the same amount of magnetic flux to each of the emergent BMRs in a given solar cycle. In the CJSS model, detailed in Sect.
3.1
, the
A_{\rm BMR,S}
of each BMR is taken directly from sunspot area records. We let
\Delta\beta
denote the separation between the two poles of a BMR. The model combines an empirical relationship between
\Delta\beta
and
A_{\rm BMR,S}
and the assumption that
\Phi_{\rm BMR}
is linearly proportional to
\left(\Delta\beta\right)^{2}
to determine
\Phi_{\rm BMR}
from
A_{\rm BMR,S}
.

Few studies have attempted to constrain the relationship between
\Phi_{\rm BMR}
and
A_{\rm BMR,S}
. From an examination of the faculae in active regions and newly emerged BMRs,
Schrijver & Harvey 1994
postulated that
\Phi_{\rm BMR}
scales linearly with the total area extended by the faculae and sunspots in each BMR,
A_{\rm BMR}
. This can be combined with the empirical relationship between facular area and sunspot area
(
Foukal 1993
;
Foukal 1996
;
Foukal 1998
;
Chapman et al. 1997
;
Shapiro et al. 2014
)
to roughly relate
\Phi_{\rm BMR}
to
A_{\rm BMR,S}
. In a more recent study,
Lemerle et al. 2015
compared the database of newly emerged BMRs compiled by
Wang & Sheeley 1989
to the USAF-NOAA sunspot area record and found
\Phi_{\rm BMR}
to conform to a power-law relationship with
A_{\rm BMR,S}
with a power-law exponent of 0.71. The analyses of
Schrijver & Harvey 1994
and
Lemerle et al. 2015
(hereafter SH and LCC), were restricted by certain limitations of the observations available to them, discussed in Sect.
2.3
. It would be straightforward to derive the relationship between
\Phi_{\rm BMR}
and
A_{\rm BMR,S}
from an extensive and reliable record of the magnetic and physical properties of newly emerged BMRs. There is, however, no such record currently available, as also discussed in Sect.
2.3
.

In this study we aim to establish the relationship between the amount of magnetic flux in each newly emerged BMR,
\Phi_{\rm BMR}
, and the area of the enclosed sunspots,
A_{\rm BMR,S}
. We make use of the empirical model relating the disc-integrated facular and network magnetic flux,
\Sigma\Phi_{\rm FN}
, to the total surface coverage by sunspots,
\Sigma{}A_{\rm S}
presented by YSK
(
Yeo et al. 2020
)
, which builds on the earlier work by
Preminger & Walton 2005
;
Preminger & Walton 2006a
;
Preminger & Walton 2006b
;
Preminger & Walton 2007
, abbreviated here as PW. As we demonstrate, the set-up of the YSK model allows us to establish from it an empirical relationship between
\Phi_{\rm BMR}
and
A_{\rm BMR,S}
while circumventing the lack of a proper database of newly emerged BMRs. In the following, we describe the YSK model (Sect.
2.1
), derive the empirical relationship between
\Phi_{\rm BMR}
and
A_{\rm BMR,S}
(Sect.
2.2
), and compare it to the SH and LCC results (Sect.
2.3
). Then, we apply each of these constraints on
\Phi_{\rm BMR}
to the SFTM by CJSS (Sect.
3.1
) and examine the effect on the model output (Sect.
3.2
). Making use of the surface flux transport simulation based on the empirical relationship between
\Phi_{\rm BMR}
and
A_{\rm BMR,S}
derived here, we will look at the balance between the amount of faculae and network and the amount of sunspots on the solar disc, and how that changes with solar activity level (Sect.
4
). Finally, we provide a summary of the study in Sect.
5
.

2
The relationship between the amount of magnetic flux in newly emerged bipolar magnetic regions and the area of the enclosed sunspots

2.1
Yeo et al. 2020
model

In this section we derive an empirical relationship between the amount of magnetic flux in each newly emerged bipolar magnetic region or BMR,
\Phi_{\rm BMR}
, and the area of the enclosed sunspots,
A_{\rm BMR,S}
. For this purpose we employ the empirical model of the relationship between the disc-integrated facular and network magnetic flux,
\Sigma\Phi_{\rm FN}
, and the total surface coverage by sunspots,
\Sigma{}A_{\rm S}
, recently reported by YSK
(
Yeo et al. 2020
)
. The YSK model is given by

\Sigma\Phi_{\rm FN}=\left(\Sigma{}A_{\rm S}\right)^{h_{1}}\otimes{}H+h_{2},

(1)

where
h_{1}
and
h_{2}
are fit parameters. The convolution of
\left(\Sigma{}A_{\rm S}\right)^{h_{1}}
with
H
, a finite impulse response (FIR) filter, describes the variation in
\Sigma\Phi_{\rm FN}
associated with sunspot-bearing BMRs. The
h_{2}
term represents network magnetic flux that did not originate from sunspot-bearing BMRs; it is assumed in the model to be constant. The FIR filter is given by

H\left(t\right)=h_{3}\max\left[\exp\left(-\frac{|t|}{h_{4}}\right)\cos\left(\frac{2\pi{}t}{t_{\sun}}\right),0\right],

(2)

where
-0.25t_{\sun}\leq{}t\leq 14.25t_{\sun}
,
t_{\sun}=26.24\ {\rm days}
denotes the synodic rotation period of the Sun, and
h_{3}
and
h_{4}
are fit parameters.

Yeo et al. 2020
extended the composite time series of daily
\Sigma{}A_{\rm S}
by
Balmaceda et al. 2009
(Fig.
1
a) and of daily
\Sigma\Phi_{\rm FN}
by
Yeo et al. 2014b
(Fig.
1
b) to June 2017 and constrained the fit parameters with the result.
Yeo et al. 2014b
had derived daily
\Sigma\Phi_{\rm FN}
back to 1974 by taking daily full-disc magnetograms from the Kitt Peak Vacuum Telescope
(
Livingston et al. 1976
;
Jones et al. 1992
)
, SoHO/MDI
(
Scherrer et al. 1995
)
, and SDO/HMI
(
Scherrer et al. 2012
)
, accounting for the systematic differences between the observations from the various telescopes due to instrumental factors and isolating the faculae and network present in each magnetogram. YSK could not extend the
Balmaceda et al. 2009
time series beyond June 2017 as it is based on USAF-NOAA sunspot areas for the period of 1986 on, and these measurements are available only up to this time. For
\Sigma{}A_{\rm S}
in millionths of the solar hemisphere (
\mu
Hem) and
\Sigma\Phi_{\rm FN}
in units of Weber (Wb),
h_{1}=0.843
,
h_{2}=1.82\times 10^{14}{\rm\ Wb}
,
h_{3}=1.99\times{}10^{11}{\rm\ Wb}
, and
h_{4}=33.5{\rm\ days}
. The robustness of the model is visibly evident in Fig.
1
b; the model reconstruction of
\Sigma\Phi_{\rm FN}
from
\Sigma{}A_{\rm S}
(red) is very similar to the
Yeo et al. 2014b
time series (black), and the 365-day running mean of the difference between the two (blue) is close to null and does not indicate any clear systematic departures. The Pearson’s correlation coefficient,
R
, between the two time series is 0.950, meaning the model reproduces about
90\%
of the observed variability. We refer the reader to
Yeo et al. 2020
for a detailed discussion of the uncertainties in the model and how it compares to earlier models.

The FIR filter
H
(Equation
2
) is based on the earlier findings of PW
(
Preminger & Walton 2005
;
Preminger & Walton 2006a
;
Preminger & Walton 2006b
;
Preminger & Walton 2007
)
. Sunspots form some time after the BMRs they belong to have started emerging on the solar surface; they are short-lived compared to active regions and their decay products, such that the latter persists long after their sunspots have decayed
(
van Driel-Gesztelyi & Green 2015
)
. Consequently, the response of any quantity that is sensitive to the presence of BMRs, active regions, and their decay products, including
\Sigma\Phi_{\rm FN}
, to the appearance of a sunspot similarly starts some time before it appears and persists for a period after it dissipates. PW deconvolved
\Sigma{}A_{\rm S}
from the average unsigned magnetic flux density over the solar disc, solar irradiance, and various chromospheric and coronal indices, deriving FIR filters that elucidate the response of each quantity to the appearance of a sunspot and how this response varies with time due to the phenomenon just described.
Preminger & Walton 2007
captured the key features of these empirical FIR filters with a model FIR filter (Equation 2 in their paper), which YSK modified slightly to more accurately reflect the form of the empirical FIR filters, yielding
H
. Depicted in Fig.
1
c,
H
describes a series of sinusoidal lobes,
0.5t_{\sun}
wide and
t_{\sun}
apart, of diminishing amplitude, as delineated by the exponential envelope of time constant
h_{4}
(dotted line). The first lobe has an amplitude of
h_{3}
and is centred on
t=0
. The sequence of lobes is truncated at
t=14.25t_{\sun}
. The FIR filter charts the amount of facular and network magnetic flux associated with a sunspot-bearing BMR, per unit sunspot area, from
0.25t_{\sun}
before it fully emerges to
14.25t_{\sun}
after. The rising edge of the first lobe corresponds to the BMR emerging, the peak of the first lobe to full emergence, and the falling edge and the subsequent lobes to its decay and modulation with solar rotation.

If the amount of facular and network magnetic flux associated with a sunspot-bearing BMR scales linearly with the area of the enclosed sunspots, then the variation in
\Sigma\Phi_{\rm FN}
due to sunspot-bearing BMRs would be given by
\Sigma{}A_{\rm S}\otimes{}H
. However, as noted in the introduction, various studies have found the facular area to increase with increasing sunspot area at a diminishing rate, such that it is at a lower level than if the two quantities are linearly related
(
Foukal 1993
;
Foukal 1996
;
Foukal 1998
;
Chapman et al. 1997
;
Shapiro et al. 2014
)
. This implies that the larger an active region, the higher (lower) the proportion of the total area occupied by sunspots (faculae). Based on this observation, YSK argued that it is unlikely that the amount of facular and network magnetic flux associated with a sunspot-bearing BMR conforms to a linear relationship with the area of the enclosed sunspots. They introduced the fit parameter
h_{1}
, such that the variation in
\Sigma\Phi_{\rm FN}
is given by
\left(\Sigma{}A_{\rm S}\right)^{h_{1}}\otimes{}H
instead. This and the modifications introduced to the model FIR filter reported by PW produced a closer agreement between modelled and measured
\Sigma\Phi_{\rm FN}
, detailed in
Yeo et al. 2020
.

After the time of the YSK study, the
\Sigma{}A_{\rm S}
composite by
Balmaceda et al. 2009
was revised by
Mandal et al. 2020
. Both the
Balmaceda et al. 2009
and
Mandal et al. 2020
time series are based on, up to 1976, Royal Greenwich Observatory (RGO) sunspot area measurements. They are therefore exactly identical to one another up to this time, and differ only in the observations used to extend the RGO time series to more recent times.
Mandal et al. 2020
noted that these two time series differ from one another by about 6% between 1977 and 1985, and that there are otherwise no noticeable systematic differences between the two. The revision of the YSK model with the
Mandal et al. 2020
time series is outside the scope of the current study. In any case, we do not expect the result of applying the
Mandal et al. 2020
time series to the YSK model to differ significantly from what was obtained with the
Balmaceda et al. 2009
time series as input. The disparity between these two time series between 1977 and 1985 is relatively small, especially in comparison to the random error in sunspot area measurements
(
Sofia et al. 1982
, 20% to 50%,)
. In addition, less than 10% of the data points in the daily
\Sigma\Phi_{\rm FN}
composite by
Yeo et al. 2014b
lie in this period.

2.2
Derivation

The total magnetic flux in each newly emerged BMR is the sum of the magnetic flux in its faculae and in its sunspots, or

\Phi_{\rm BMR}=\Phi_{\rm BMR,F}+\Phi_{\rm BMR,S}.

(3)

Here we establish how
\Phi_{\rm BMR,F}
and
\Phi_{\rm BMR,S}
relate to
A_{\rm BMR,S}
in turn. To derive the relationship between
\Phi_{\rm BMR,F}
and
A_{\rm BMR,S}
, let us isolate from the YSK model the contribution to
\Sigma\Phi_{\rm FN}
by the faculae in newly emerged BMRs,
\Sigma\Phi_{\rm BMR,F}
. As noted in Sect.
2.1
, the peak of the first lobe of the FIR filter, which is at
t=0
, corresponds to the response of
\Sigma\Phi_{\rm FN}
to a sunspot-bearing BMR when it has fully emerged and has not started to decay. Taking Equation
1
, we isolate
\Sigma\Phi_{\rm BMR,F}
by replacing
H
with
H\left(t=0\right)=h_{3}
and factoring out the basal level,
h_{2}
, arriving at

\Sigma\Phi_{\rm BMR,F}=h_{3}\left(\Sigma{}A_{\rm S}\right)^{h_{1}}.

(4)

The PW and YSK approach, by modelling the sunspot-related variation in a given disc-integrated quantity as the convolution of
\Sigma{}A_{\rm S}
with a FIR filter and with the various quantities at daily cadence, treats the appearance of sunspots as one-day events. In other words, it implicitly assumes that each sunspot exists for just one day, such that the total surface coverage by sunspots,
\Sigma{}A_{\rm S}
, is treated in the model as the total surface coverage by sunspots in newly emerged BMRs,
\Sigma{}A_{\rm BMR,S}
. Replacing
\Sigma{}A_{\rm S}
in Equation
4
with
\Sigma{}A_{\rm BMR,S}
, we get

\Sigma\Phi_{\rm BMR,F}=h_{3}\left(\Sigma{}A_{\rm BMR,S}\right)^{h_{1}}.

(5)

Assuming this relationship applies to individual BMRs, then

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Phi_{\rm BMR,F}&=h_{3}A_{\rm BMR,S}^{h_{1}}\\
&=1.99\times{}10^{11}A_{\rm BMR,S}^{0.843}{\rm\ Wb}.\end{split}

(6)

Cho et al. 2015
examined how the amount of magnetic flux in sunspots and pores varies with their size. The authors noted that for fully formed sunspots, the amount of magnetic flux appears to scale linearly with its area, confirming the early result by
Sheeley 1966
. Assuming the mean magnetic flux density over sunspots is 1350 G or
4.11\times{}10^{11}\ {\rm Wb}\ \mu{\rm Hem^{-1}}

(
Solanki & Schmidt 1993
;
Lites et al. 1993
;
Keppens & Martinez Pillet 1996
)
, this means

\Phi_{\rm BMR,S}=4.11\times{}10^{11}A_{\rm BMR,S}\ {\rm Wb}.

(7)

Taking Equations
6
and
7
into Equation
3
, we get

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Phi_{\rm BMR}&=4.11\times{}10^{11}A_{\rm BMR,S}+1.99\times{}10^{11}{}A_{\rm BMR,S}^{0.843}\ {\rm Wb}\\
&=4.11\times{}10^{11}\left(A_{\rm BMR,S}+0.485A_{\rm BMR,S}^{0.843}\right)\ {\rm Wb}\\
&=4.11\times{}10^{11}\tilde{A}_{\rm BMR}\ {\rm Wb},\end{split}

(8)

which relates the amount of magnetic flux in newly emerged BMRs to the area of the enclosed sunspots. The
\tilde{A}_{\rm BMR}
term, representing
A_{\rm BMR,S}+0.485A_{\rm BMR,S}^{0.843}
, denotes for a given BMR the area of the sunspot that would contain the same amount of magnetic flux (compare Equations
7
and
8
). It is worth noting here that the YSK model (Sect.
2.1
) and the analysis based on this model that we have just presented pertains only to sunspot-bearing BMRs and does not apply to ephemeral regions.

In Fig.
2
we chart
\Phi_{\rm BMR,S}
,
\Phi_{\rm BMR}
(dashed and solid black lines, top panel), and the ratio of the two,
\frac{\Phi_{\rm BMR,S}}{\Phi_{\rm BMR}}
(black line, bottom panel) as a function of
A_{\rm BMR,S}
. As is visibly evident, the empirical relationship between
\Phi_{\rm BMR}
and

presented in Equation

\frac{\Phi_{\rm BMR,S}}{\Phi_{\rm BMR}}
increases gradually from about 0.7 at
A_{\rm BMR,S}=1\ \mu{}{\rm Hem^{-1}}
to 0.9 at
6000\ \mu{}{\rm Hem^{-1}}
(left axis, Fig.

Schrijver 1987
established an empirical relationship between the proportion of the magnetic flux in active regions contained in their sunspots and the proportion of the area of active regions occupied by their sunspots. Assuming it applies to newly emerged BMRs, then

\frac{\Phi_{\rm BMR,S}}{\Phi_{\rm BMR}}=5.1\frac{A_{\rm BMR,S}}{A_{\rm BMR}},

(9)

meaning the trend in
\frac{\Phi_{\rm BMR,S}}{\Phi_{\rm BMR}}
with
A_{\rm BMR,S}
(left axis, Fig.

\frac{A_{\rm BMR,S}}{A_{\rm BMR}}
rising in proportion from about 0.14 to 0.18 (right axis). In other words, the smallest newly emerged BMRs are about 70
\%
sunspots by magnetic flux and 14
\%
sunspots by area, and this increases steadily to about 90
\%
and 18
\%
, respectively for the largest newly emerged BMRs.

We note the following. Firstly, active regions and newly emerged BMRs are not equivalent to one another, meaning the application of the findings of
Schrijver 1987
to the latter (Equation

) is not strictly correct. Secondly, some of the smallest BMRs might contain pores instead of fully developed sunspots
(

, such that the amount of sunspot magnetic flux they enclose might be smaller than indicated by Equation
7
. Nonetheless, the assertion here that the sunspots in a given newly emerged BMR can enclose most of its magnetic flux while occupying only a small proportion of its area is reasonable and within expectation given that sunspots and pores are magnetically denser than faculae.

2.3
Comparison to
Schrijver & Harvey 1994
and
Lemerle et al. 2015

In this section we compare the empirical relationship between
\Phi_{\rm BMR}
and
A_{\rm BMR,S}
, derived here (Equation
8
) to the constraints on
\Phi_{\rm BMR}
reported by SH
(
Schrijver & Harvey 1994
)
and by LCC
(
Lemerle et al. 2015
)
. Taking Kitt Peak magnetograms, SH isolated the faculae in active regions and newly emerged BMRs and found the facular magnetic flux in individual regions to scale linearly with the area extended by these features. Based on this result and assuming an average BMR magnetic flux density of 100 G or
1.52\times{}10^{10}\ {\rm Wb}\ \mu{\rm Hem^{-1}}
, SH estimated that

\Phi_{\rm BMR}=1.52\times{}10^{10}kA_{\rm BMR}\ {\rm Wb},

(10)

where
k
is a free parameter that the authors did not constrain. SH interpreted the quantity
k-1
for a given newly emerged BMR as the sum of
\Phi_{\rm BMR,S}
, and the magnetic flux that dissipated before the BMR had fully formed as a proportion of
\Phi_{\rm BMR}
. Since this ratio is unlikely to exceed unity, at least not by a wide margin,
k
most probably lies between one and two. Instead of establishing the relationship between
\Phi_{\rm BMR}
and
A_{\rm BMR}
directly, SH had estimated it from the relationship between facular magnetic flux and area. This was necessary because, as noted in precursor studies to the SH study conducted by the same authors
(
Schrijver 1987
;
Harvey & Zwaan 1993
)
, sunspot magnetic flux cannot be taken directly, at least not reliably, from Kitt Peak magnetograms.

Ideally, to relate
\Phi_{\rm BMR}
to
A_{\rm BMR,S}
using the relationship between
\Phi_{\rm BMR}
and
A_{\rm BMR}
reported by SH (Equation
10
), it should be combined with the relationship between
A_{\rm BMR}
and
A_{\rm BMR,S}
. Unfortunately, the relationship between
A_{\rm BMR}
and
A_{\rm BMR,S}
is not known. Instead, let us assume the empirical relationship between the total surface coverage by faculae and by sunspots reported by
Chapman et al. 1997
(hereafter CCD) can be taken as the relationship between the area of the faculae and of the sunspots in individual newly emerged BMRs, such that

A_{\rm BMR}=414+22A_{\rm BMR,S}-0.0036A_{\rm BMR,S}^{2}.

(11)

Inserting Equation
11
into Equation
10
, we obtain

\Phi_{\rm BMR}=1.52\times{}10^{10}k\left[414+22A_{\rm BMR,S}-0.0036A_{\rm BMR,S}^{2}\right]\ {\rm Wb}.

(12)

The estimate of
\Phi_{\rm BMR}
from this expression at
k=1
and
k=2
represents the lower and upper bound of this quantity based on the combination of the SH and CCD findings.

Lemerle et al. 2015
compared the
\Phi_{\rm BMR}
measurements in the database of newly emerged BMRs compiled by
Wang & Sheeley 1989
to USAF-NOAA sunspot areas. They could not match each newly emerged BMR in the
Wang & Sheeley 1989
database to the corresponding sunspot group in the USAF-NOAA record, which excluded a direct comparison and compelled them to compare the distribution of values in the two records instead. From this, LCC estimated that

\Phi_{\rm BMR}=1.12\times{}10^{12}A_{\rm BMR,S}^{0.714}\ {\rm Wb}.

(13)

Taking Equations
12
and
13
, we recalculate
\Phi_{\rm BMR}
and
\frac{\Phi_{\rm BMR,S}}{\Phi_{\rm BMR}}
, drawn in Fig.
2
(blue and red lines) along the values from the empirical relationship between
\Phi_{\rm BMR}
and
A_{\rm BMR,S}
derived in this study (black solid lines).

Figure
2
a shows, at lower levels of
A_{\rm BMR,S}
, that the estimates of
\Phi_{\rm BMR}
based on the SH (blue) and LCC results (red) are higher than the current estimate (black solid line). However,
\Phi_{\rm BMR}
increases with increasing
A_{\rm BMR,S}
at a slower rate in the former, such that at higher levels of
A_{\rm BMR,S}
the estimates of
\Phi_{\rm BMR}
based on the SH and LCC results drop below the current estimate and also below
\Phi_{\rm BMR,S}
(dashed line). This translates into the analogous behaviour of
\frac{\Phi_{\rm BMR,S}}{\Phi_{\rm BMR}}
illustrated in Fig.
2
b. At low
A_{\rm BMR,S}
the estimates of
\frac{\Phi_{\rm BMR,S}}{\Phi_{\rm BMR}}
based on the SH (blue) and LCC results (red) are lower than the current estimate (black), but they rise more rapidly with increasing
A_{\rm BMR,S}
such that they eventually exceed not just the current estimate, but also unity. The amount of magnetic flux in a given BMR has to be greater than the amount carried by the sunspots it encloses, meaning
\Phi_{\rm BMR}
going below
\Phi_{\rm BMR,S}
or, equivalently,
\frac{\Phi_{\rm BMR,S}}{\Phi_{\rm BMR}}
going above unity is unphysical. The observation here that the estimates of
\Phi_{\rm BMR}
based on the SH and LCC results drop below
\Phi_{\rm BMR,S}
towards higher
A_{\rm BMR,S}
is unlikely to be from any uncertainty in the latter. The margin is so wide that even if the mean magnetic flux density over sunspots is only one-quarter the value 1350 G we adopt here,
\Phi_{\rm BMR,S}
will still be greater than the estimates of
\Phi_{\rm BMR}
based on the SH and LCC results for the largest BMRs. The constraint on
\Phi_{\rm BMR}
reported by SH, at least when combined with the findings of CCD as we do here (Equation
11
), and that by LCC appear to grossly underestimate the amount of magnetic flux in larger BMRs.

The discrepancies between the results reported in this study and earlier by SH and LCC that we have just highlighted could have arisen from the following. SH examined active regions and newly emerged BMRs together even though the two are not equivalent. Furthermore, SH had to constrain
\Phi_{\rm BMR}
indirectly as sunspot magnetic flux cannot be determined directly and reliably from the magnetogram observations they utilised. How the SH results compare to the LCC and current results could have also been affected by the fact that we had to combine the SH result with the findings of CCD in order to make this comparison, and the latter does not strictly apply to newly emerged BMRs.

As for the LCC study, the authors could not compare the
Wang & Sheeley 1989
and USAF-NOAA records directly because they could not match each newly emerged BMR in the former to the corresponding sunspot group in the latter. The
Wang & Sheeley 1989
database includes spot-free BMRs, which are not represented in the USAF-NOAA record and should have been excluded.
Wang & Sheeley 1989
had also determined
\Phi_{\rm BMR}
approximately from photographic magnetograms, introducing further uncertainty to the LCC analysis. SH and LCC were hampered by limitations in the data available to them. As stated in the introduction, it would be straightforward to derive the relationship between
\Phi_{\rm BMR}
and
A_{\rm BMR,S}
from an extensive and reliable database of the magnetic and physical properties of newly emerged BMRs, but no such record currently exists. The
Wang & Sheeley 1989
database is clearly not ideal for this purpose. The BMR databases compiled more recently by
Yeates et al. 2007
and by
Yeates 2020
, although of excellent quality, are not suitable either as they tabulate the physical properties of each BMR at the time it crosses the central meridian, not when it newly emerges.

The YSK model and the analysis based on it presented in this study have their own assumptions and limitations, discussed in Sects.
2.1
and
2.2
. Nonetheless, it is worth noting that the YSK model allowed us to circumvent the lack of any proper database of newly emerged BMRs, an issue that severely limited the SH and LCC analyses, and constrains the relationship between
\Phi_{\rm BMR}
and
A_{\rm BMR,S}
from
\Sigma\Phi_{\rm FN}
and
\Sigma{}A_{\rm S}
(i.e. disc-integrated observations) instead.

3.1
Simulation set-up

The governing equation of the CJSS model is

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{\partial{}B}{\partial{}t}=&-\Omega\left(\lambda\right)\frac{\partial{}B}{\partial\phi}-\frac{1}{R_{\odot}\cos\lambda}\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda}\left[\upsilon\left(\lambda\right)B\cos\lambda\right]\\
&+\eta_{\rm S}\left[\frac{1}{R^{2}_{\odot}\cos\lambda}\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda}\left(\cos\lambda\frac{\partial{}B}{\partial\lambda}\right)+\frac{1}{R^{2}_{\odot}\cos^{2}\lambda}\frac{\partial^{2}B}{\partial\phi^{2}}\right]\\
&+D\left(\eta_{\rm R}\right)+S\left(\lambda,\phi,t\right),\end{split}

(14)

where
R_{\sun}
,
\lambda
, and
\phi
denote the radius of the Sun, latitude, and longitude, respectively. This describes the passive transport of the radial component of the photospheric magnetic field,
B
, under the influence of differential rotation,
\Omega
; meridional flow,
\upsilon
; and diffusion, which is characterised by the surface and radial diffusivity,
\eta_{\rm S}
and
\eta_{\rm R}

(
DeVore et al. 1984
;
Wang et al. 1989
;
Mackay et al. 2000
;
Schrijver et al. 2002
;
Baumann et al. 2004
)
. Radial diffusion is introduced through a linear operator,
D

(
Baumann et al. 2006
)
, and BMR emergence through a source term,
S
. As noted in the previous paragraph, we set up the simulation runs following the reference case presented by
Cameron et al. 2010
. We refer the reader to this earlier paper for details on the definition of the initial condition and the
\Omega
,
\upsilon
,
\eta_{\rm S}
, and
\eta_{\rm R}
parameters. For the CJSS-SH run, we set up the source term following
Cameron et al. 2010
. For the CJSS-LCC and CJSS-YEA runs, we modified the source term in such a way as to conform the model to the constraints on
\Phi_{\rm BMR}
reported by LCC (Equation
13
) and derived here (Equation
8
). We describe the definition of the source term and these modifications next.

The source term (i.e. details of BMR emergence) is inferred from the composite of the RGO and USAF-NOAA sunspot area records by
Hathaway & Choudhary 2008
. The location and area of each sunspot group when the latter is at its maximum is taken to denote the location and
A_{\rm BMR,S}
of a newly emerged BMR. Following
van Ballegooijen et al. 1998
, each BMR is described as two circular magnetic patches of opposite polarity. Let
B_{+}\left(\lambda,\phi\right)
represent the magnetic flux density of the positive polarity patch at
\left(\lambda,\phi\right)
,
\left(\lambda_{+},\phi_{+}\right)
the centre of the patch, and
\beta_{+}\left(\lambda,\phi\right)
the heliocentric angle between
\left(\lambda,\phi\right)
and
\left(\lambda_{+},\phi_{+}\right)
, and let
B_{-}\left(\lambda,\phi\right)
,
\left(\lambda_{-},\phi_{-}\right)
and
\beta_{-}\left(\lambda,\phi\right)
represent the same for the negative polarity patch. The magnetic flux density profile is given by

B_{\pm}\left(\lambda,\phi\right)=B_{\rm max}\left(\frac{0.4\Delta\beta}{\delta}\right)^{2}\exp\left(-\frac{2\left[1-\cos\beta_{\pm}\left(\lambda,\phi\right)\right]}{\delta^{2}}\right),

(15)

where
\Delta\beta=\beta_{+}\left(\lambda_{-},\phi_{-}\right)=\beta_{-}\left(\lambda_{+},\phi_{+}\right)
denotes the heliocentric angle between the two patches and
\delta=4^{\circ}
the size of each patch. The constant factor
B_{\rm max}
modulates the absolute scale of the model output and is fixed at 374 G by matching the output average unsigned magnetic flux density to the observed values from the Mount Wilson Observatory (MWO) and Wilcox Solar Observatory (WSO)
(
Arge et al. 2002
)
, illustrated in Fig.
3
a. We note that BMR magnetic flux density,
B_{\pm}
, and therefore the amount of magnetic flux in each BMR,
\Phi_{\rm BMR}
, are assumed to scale linearly with the square of the angular separation,
\left(\Delta\beta\right)^{2}
. The angular separation,
\Delta\beta
, is given by

\Delta\beta=6.46\times 10^{-2}\sqrt{A_{\rm BMR}},

(16)

where
\Delta\beta
and
A_{\rm BMR}
are in units of degrees and
\mu
Hem, respectively, and
A_{\rm BMR}
is calculated from
A_{\rm BMR,S}
using the CCD relationship (Equation
11
).
Cameron et al. 2010
established this empirical relationship with the
\Delta\beta
and sunspot umbral area measurements from the Kodaikanal Solar Observatory (KSO) and MWO. The tilt angle of each BMR is calculated from the observed cycle-average tilt angle, also from KSO and MWO, through the procedure detailed in
Cameron et al. 2010
. The source term, and therefore the simulation runs, extend from 1913 to 1986 (solar cycle 15 to 21), the period over which sunspot group location, area, and tilt angle measurements are available. The simulation runs and the analysis based on them (see the following sections) are not sensitive to which sunspot area composite we take into the CJSS model as input. The
Hathaway & Choudhary 2008
,
Balmaceda et al. 2009
, and
Mandal et al. 2020
composites are essentially different efforts to extend the RGO record, which ends in 1976, to more recent times with sunspot area measurements from other observatories. The various composites are therefore exactly identical to one another up to this time (i.e. over most of the simulation period of 1913 to 1986).

The CJSS model relies on the combination of the assumption that
B_{\pm}
scales linearly with
\left(\Delta\beta\right)^{2}
(Equation
15
) and the empirical relationship between
\Delta\beta
and
A_{\rm BMR}
(Equation
16
) to define
B_{\pm}
. Substituting Equation
16
into Equation
15
we obtain

B_{\pm}\left(\lambda,\phi\right)=\tilde{B}_{\rm max}A_{\rm BMR}\exp\left(-\frac{2\left[1-\cos\beta_{\pm}\left(\lambda,\phi\right)\right]}{\delta^{2}}\right),

(17)

where
\tilde{B}_{\rm max}=B_{\rm max}0.4^{2}\left(6.46\times 10^{-2}\right)^{2}\delta^{-2}=0.0156\ {\rm G}\ \mu{}{\rm Hem^{-1}}
is a constant factor that serves the same purpose as
B_{\rm max}
. The consequence of this approach is that
B_{\pm}
, and therefore also
\Phi_{\rm BMR}
, scales linearly with
A_{\rm BMR}
in the model. Incidentally, this is similar to the constraint on
\Phi_{\rm BMR}
reported by SH, where this quantity also scales linearly with
A_{\rm BMR}
(Equation
10
).

Let us generalise Equation
17
by rewriting it as

B_{\pm}\left(\lambda,\phi\right)=\tilde{B}_{\rm max}{}f\exp\left(-\frac{2\left[1-\cos\beta_{\pm}\left(\lambda,\phi\right)\right]}{\delta^{2}}\right),

(18)

where
f=A_{\rm BMR}
determines the
A_{\rm BMR,S}
-dependence of
B_{\pm}
and
\Phi_{\rm BMR}
. In the CJSS-LCC run we modify the CJSS model to comply with the constraint on
\Phi_{\rm BMR}
reported by LCC (Equation
13
) by redefining
f
as
A_{\rm BMR,S}^{0.71}
. Similarly, in the CJSS-YEA run we apply the constraint on
\Phi_{\rm BMR}
derived in this study (Equation
8
) to the CJSS model by redefining
f
as
\tilde{A}_{\rm BMR}
. By this redefinition of
f
, we replace the assumption in the CJSS model that
B_{\pm}
scales linearly with
\left(\Delta\beta\right)^{2}
with direct observational constraints on the relationship between
\Phi_{\rm BMR}
and
A_{\rm BMR,S}
. As in the CJSS-SH run,
\tilde{B}_{\rm max}
is set in the CJSS-LCC and CJSS-YEA runs at the value that brings the modelled average unsigned magnetic flux density to the absolute scale of the observed values (Fig.
3
a). The value of
\tilde{B}_{\rm max}
and the definition of
f
in each simulation run is summarised in Table

.

Simulation results

3.2.1
Total photospheric magnetic flux

In terms of the total photospheric magnetic flux, represented in Fig.
3

\tilde{B}_{\rm max}
is set in each run at the level that brings the model output to the absolute scale of the observed values (circles and boxes). What is notable is that the time variability is also very similar even with the different

-dependence of

imposed for each run.

3.2.2
Solar open flux and polar field reversals

Cameron et al. 2010
and
Jiang et al. 2011b
had verified their model by demonstrating its ability to reproduce the evolution of the solar open flux and the timing of the polar field reversals. The authors derived the open flux corresponding to the model output photospheric magnetic field by extrapolating it into the heliosphere using the Current Sheet Source Surface (CSSS) model by
Zhao & Hoeksema 1995b
;
Zhao & Hoeksema 1995a
. This was matched to the independent reconstruction of the open flux from the
aa
geomagnetic activity index by
Lockwood et al. 2009
. They compared the timing of the polar field reversals in the model output to the timings reported by
Makarov et al. 2004
based on an examination of the polar filaments in H
\alpha
synoptic charts.

Following CJSS, we applied the CSSS model to each simulation run to calculate the corresponding solar open flux. In Fig.
3
b we compare the results (black, blue, and red) to the
Lockwood et al. 2009
reconstruction (green solid line) and the update by
Lockwood et al. 2014
(green dashed line). The various runs do not differ from one another more than they do from the
Lockwood et al. 2009
;
Lockwood et al. 2014
time series.

In Fig.
3
c we chart the evolution of the polar field in the simulation runs as given by the mean magnetic flux density over the north (
\lambda>75^{\circ}
, solid lines) and the south polar caps (
\lambda<-75^{\circ}
, dashed lines). Polar field reversal is indicated by the mean polar field switching polarity. In the same plot we indicate the observed timing of the polar field reversals reported by
Makarov et al. 2004
(green crosses). Analogous to the solar open flux, the various runs do not differ more from one another than they do from the
Makarov et al. 2004
observations. Applying the constraints on
\Phi_{\rm BMR}
presented by LCC (Equation
13
) and derived in this study (Equation
8
) to the CJSS model retained its ability to reproduce the evolution of the solar open flux and the timing of the polar field reversals.

3.2.3
Correlation between the polar field and solar cycle strength

A key finding of the CJSS studies is that the amount of polar flux at the end of each solar cycle in the model appears to be highly correlated to the observed strength of the succeeding cycle. This is consistent with the existence of a Babcock–Leighton-type solar dynamo
(
Babcock 1961
;
Leighton 1964
;
Leighton 1969
)
, where the polar field, which peaks around the end of each cycle, is the main source of the toroidal field in the convection zone
(
Cameron & Schüssler 2015
, see discussion in)
. We examine if this feature of the CJSS model is sensitive to the
A_{\rm BMR,S}
-dependence of
\Phi_{\rm BMR}
assumed. Taking each simulation run, we compare the peak of the mean unsigned magnetic flux density over both polar caps at the end of each cycle to the maximum total sunspot area over the following cycle, representing modelled polar flux and measured cycle strength (Fig.
4
). The Pearson’s correlation coefficient,
R
, between the two quantities is 0.933 for the CJSS-SH run, 0.925 for the CJSS-LCC run, and 0.937 for the CJSS-YEA run. The different
A_{\rm BMR,S}
-dependence of
\Phi_{\rm BMR}
imposed on the various runs made no appreciable difference to the correlation between the model output polar field at the end of each cycle and the observed strength of the succeeding cycle.

Whether in terms of the time variation of the total photospheric magnetic flux (Fig.

a) and the solar open flux (Fig.

A_{\rm BMR,S}
-dependence of
\Phi_{\rm BMR}
assumed. This is likely, at least in part, due to the fact that the magnetic flux in each newly emerged BMR can persist for weeks or months, such that the state of a simulation run at a given time depends not only on the BMRs injected then but also on all the BMRs that were introduced in the weeks to months prior. The amount of magnetic flux in individual BMRs depends strongly on the
A_{\rm BMR,S}
-dependence of
\Phi_{\rm BMR}
assumed. However, the aggregate magnetic flux introduced over an extended period by all the BMRs that had emerged then would not be as sensitive to the
A_{\rm BMR,S}
-dependence of
\Phi_{\rm BMR}
assumed due to the summation. Nonetheless, it is noteworthy that the application of the empirical relationship between
\Phi_{\rm BMR}
and
A_{\rm BMR,S}
presented here to what is an established SFTM retained its key features, including its ability to replicate various independent datasets.

Balance between bright faculae and network and dark sunspots at various solar activity levels

As discussed in the Introduction, the balance between bright faculae and network and dark sunspots on the solar disc and how that changes with solar activity level is of interest for the relevance to the understanding of the brightness variability of the Sun and other cool stars. Making use of the CJSS-YEA run (Sect.

5
Summary

The relationship between sunspot-bearing bipolar magnetic regions (BMRs) and the sunspots they enclose, connected to how solar magnetism and activity relate to sunspot prevalence, is important. It is particularly relevant to surface flux transport models (SFTMs) that derive the details of BMR emergence they require as input from sunspot observations. However, attempts to constrain how the amount of magnetic flux in each newly emerged BMR,
\Phi_{\rm BMR}
, relates to its area
(
Schrijver & Harvey 1994
)
or the area of the enclosed sunspots,
A_{\rm BMR,S}

(
Lemerle et al. 2015
)
, were limited by the fact that there is no extensive and reliable database of the magnetic and physical properties of newly emerged BMRs currently available. In the absence of proper observational constraints, existing SFTMs adopt various simplifications to determine the details of BMR emergence from sunspot observations. In this study we aimed to establish the relationship between
\Phi_{\rm BMR}
and
A_{\rm BMR,S}
.

To this end, we made use of the empirical model of the relationship between the disc-integrated facular and network magnetic flux,
\Sigma\Phi_{\rm FN}
, and the total surface coverage by sunspots,
\Sigma{}A_{\rm S}
, presented by
Yeo et al. 2020
. The structure of the
Yeo et al. 2020
model enabled us to circumvent the lack of any proper BMR database, and instead derive an empirical relationship between
\Phi_{\rm BMR}
and
A_{\rm BMR,S}
(Equation
8
) from
\Sigma{}A_{\rm FN}
and
\Sigma{}A_{\rm S}
(i.e. disc-integrated quantities). The empirical relationship between
\Phi_{\rm BMR}
and
A_{\rm BMR,S}
indicates that for newly emerged BMRs, the enclosed sunspots occupy a small proportion of their area (14
\%
to 18
\%
), but contain most of their magnetic flux (70
\%
to 90
\%
) (Fig.
2
b). We found evidence that the earlier results by
Schrijver & Harvey 1994
(Equation
10
) and
Lemerle et al. 2015
(Equation
13
) might underestimate the
\Phi_{\rm BMR}
of larger BMRs (Fig.
2
a).

To verify the empirical relationship between
\Phi_{\rm BMR}
and
A_{\rm BMR,S}
presented here, we examined the result of applying this and the earlier results by
Schrijver & Harvey 1994
and
Lemerle et al. 2015
to a SFTM. We made use of the SFTM reported by
Cameron et al. 2010
and
Jiang et al. 2011a
;
Jiang et al. 2011b
. This model, incidentally and not by design, already complies with the
Schrijver & Harvey 1994
result. We generated three simulation runs, one with the model as it is and the other two with the model modified to comply with the
Lemerle et al. 2015
results and the current results. We compared the evolution of the solar open flux to the independent reconstruction of this quantity from the
aa
geomagnetic activity index by
Lockwood et al. 2009
;
Lockwood et al. 2014
(Fig.
3
b) and the timing of the polar field reversals to the observed timing reported by
Makarov et al. 2004
(Fig.
3
c). The various runs reproduce the
Lockwood et al. 2009
;
Lockwood et al. 2014
and
Makarov et al. 2004
results relatively well and almost equally. An important feature of the
Cameron et al. 2010
and
Jiang et al. 2011a
;
Jiang et al. 2011b
model is that the polar field at the end of each cycle is highly correlated to the observed strength of the following cycle. This is significant as it is consistent with the existence of a Babcock–Leighton-type solar dynamo. In terms of this correlation, the various runs are again very similar (Fig.
4
). Surface flux transport simulations do not appear to be, to the extent tested here, particularly sensitive to the
A_{\rm BMR,S}
-dependence of
\Phi_{\rm BMR}
assumed. Nonetheless, it is worth noting that applying the current result to what is an established SFTM retained its key features.

Making use of the surface flux transport simulation based on the relationship between
\Phi_{\rm BMR}
and
A_{\rm BMR,S}
presented here, we examined how the amount of magnetic flux on the solar disc in faculae and network,
\Sigma\Phi_{\rm FN}
, and in sunspots,
\Sigma\Phi_{\rm S}
, relate to one another and the relationship between the ratio
\frac{\Sigma\Phi_{\rm FN}}{\Sigma\Phi_{\rm S}}
and the solar activity level. We found
\Sigma\Phi_{\rm FN}
to increase with increasing
\Sigma\Phi_{\rm S}
at a diminishing rate such that it appears to gradually saturate (Fig.
5
). This complies with the analogous results from the earlier studies that compared facular area and various disc-integrated quantities sensitive to the amount of faculae and network present to sunspot area and number
(
Foukal 1993
;
Foukal 1996
;
Foukal 1998
;
Chapman et al. 1997
;
Solanki & Fligge 1998
;
Shapiro et al. 2014
;
Yeo et al. 2020
)
. Taking
\Sigma{}A_{\rm S}
and
\log{}R^{\prime}_{\rm HK}
as proxies of solar activity, we found
\frac{\Sigma\Phi_{\rm FN}}{\Sigma\Phi_{\rm S}}
to decrease with increasing activity at a diminishing rate such that it appears to approach a horizontal asymptote (Fig.
6
). We modelled
\frac{\Sigma\Phi_{\rm FN}}{\Sigma\Phi_{\rm S}}
as conforming to a power-law relationship with activity (Equations
22
and
23
). The power-law model indicates that, at least within the range of activity examined, the proportion of the magnetic flux on the solar disc that is in sunspots remains smaller than that in faculae and network. This does not contradict our claim that most of the magnetic flux in newly emerged BMRs is in the enclosed sunspots (see Sect.
4
). The extrapolation of the power-law model to higher activity levels indicates that the
\frac{\Sigma\Phi_{\rm FN}}{\Sigma\Phi_{\rm S}}
level of the Sun might be close to the level where facular and network brightening and sunspot darkening would cancel one another out, consistent with
Shapiro et al. 2016
and
Radick et al. 2018
.
