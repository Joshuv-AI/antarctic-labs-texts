Nonexpansive maps with surjective displacement
Brian Lins

Abstract
. We investigate necessary and sufficient conditions for a nonexpansive map f f on a Banach space X X to have surjective displacement, that is, for f − id f-\mathrm{id} to map onto X X . In particular, we give a computable necessary and sufficient condition when X X is a finite dimensional space with a polyhedral norm. We give a similar computable necessary and sufficient condition for a fixed point of a polyhedral norm nonexpansive map to be unique. We also consider applications to nonlinear Perron-Frobenius theory and suggest some additional computable sufficient conditions for surjective displacement and uniqueness of fixed points.



2010 Mathematics Subject Classification
Primary 47H09, 47H10, 47H07; Secondary 54D35

1.
Introduction

Given a nonexpansive map on a metric space, it is not always easy to determine whether the map has any fixed points.
In Banach spaces with the fixed point property, any nonexpansive map that leaves a closed, bounded, convex set invariant has a fixed point.
However, in applications it can be difficult to determine whether a nonexpansive map
has any invariant, bounded, closed, convex sets.

For finite dimensional Banach spaces, necessary and sufficient conditions for nonexpansive maps to have a nonempty and bounded set of fixed points were given in
[
21
]
. When
X
is a finite dimensional normed space with either a smooth norm or a polyhedral norm,
[
21
, Section 4]
describes a non-deterministic algorithm that eventually halts (almost certainly) if and only if the fixed point set is nonempty and bounded.

Somewhat surprisingly, it may be easier to determine whether a nonexpansive map
f:X\rightarrow X
has the stronger property that the map
f+u
has a fixed point for every
u\in X
. This is equivalent to
f
having surjective displacement, that is
f-\mathrm{id}
being onto. The main result of this paper is a computable test involving a finite set of limits which determines whether or not
f
has surjective displacement when
X
is a polyhedral normed space. A similar test can also determine whether a fixed point of a nonexpansive map on a polyhedral normed space is unique.

A motivation for this investigation comes from nonlinear Perron-Frobenius theory which seeks conditions for the existence and also uniqueness of eigenvectors of order-preserving and homogeneous maps. Some general results in this area include
[
7
,
23
,
25
,
24
,
12
,
3
]
.
Many of these results rely on the close connection between nonlinear Perron-Frobenius theory and the theory of nonexpansive maps, see e.g.
[
20
,
22
]
.

Our results are inspired by a hypergraph condition from
[
3
]

that determines whether or not an order-preserving, additively homogeneous function
T:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
has several properties, one of which is that the functions
T+u
have an additive eigenvector for every
u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
(see also
[
2
]
where the hypergraph condition was first introduced for Shapley operators associated with certain two-player games).
Order-preserving and additively homogeneous maps on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
are nonexpansive with respect to the variation seminorm, so the results in
[
2
]
and
[
3
]
can be interpreted as fixed point results for a special class of nonexpansive maps.
We will show that the results in
[
3
]
can be derived from our general test for surjective displacement of a polyhedral norm nonexpansive map.

The paper is organized as follows. We begin by collecting some facts about nonexpansive maps, Banach spaces, and the metric compactification and horofunctions of a Banach space. We use these facts in Section
3
to identify necessary and sufficient conditions for a nonexpansive map to have surjective displacement. The main result of Section
3
is Theorem
3.7
which summarizes these conditions
for finite and infinite dimensional Banach spaces. In Section
4
we prove our main result which is a computable necessary and sufficient condition for a polyhedral norm nonexpansive map to have surjective displacement. We look at applications of our main result to nonlinear Perron-Frobenius theory in Section
5
and in Section
6
we give a computable necessary and sufficient condition for a fixed point of a polyhedral norm nonexpansive map to be unique. We conclude with a look at other computable sufficient conditions for a nonexpansive map to have surjective displacement or a unique fixed point.

2.
Preliminaries

For any metric space
(X,d)
, a map
f:X\rightarrow X
is
nonexpansive
if
d(f(x),f(y))\leq d(x,y)
for all
x,y\in M
. Here we focus on nonexpansive maps
f
defined on a real Banach space. For a Banach space
X
, we use the following notation for the norm
\|\cdot\|
, dual space
X^{*}
, and dual norm
\|\cdot\|_{*}
. Let
B_{R}
denote the closed ball with radius
R
and center
0
in
X
and let
B^{*}_{R}
denote the closed ball of radius
R
around
0
in
X^{*}
. The identity map on
X
is
\mathrm{id}
, and
J
denotes the set-valued
duality map

J:X\rightrightarrows X^{*}
given by

J(x):=\{x^{*}\in X^{*}:\|x^{*}\|_{*}=\|x\|,\left\langle x,x^{*}\right\rangle=\|x\|\,\|x^{*}\|_{*}\}.

For any function
f:X\rightarrow X
, we refer to
f-\mathrm{id}
as the
displacement map
associated with
f
. We say that
f
has
surjective displacement
if
f-\mathrm{id}
maps onto
X
and
f
has
dense displacement
if the range of
f-\mathrm{id}
is dense in
X
. For any
\delta\geq 0
, we define
F_{\delta}(f)=\{x\in X:\|f(x)-x\|\leq\delta\}
. When
\delta>0
, we refer to
F_{\delta}(f)
as an
approximate fixed point set
of
f
. Of course,
F_{0}(f)
is the set of fixed points of
f
.

A convex subset
F
of a convex set
C
is called a
face
of
C
if
tx+(1-t)y\in F
with
x,y\in C
and
0<t<1
implies that
x
and
y
are contained in
F
. If
F
is a face of
C
and
F\neq C
, then
F
is a
proper face
. The intersection of a supporting hyperplane with
C
is called an
exposed face
. All exposed faces are proper faces, but there are simple examples where proper faces are not exposed faces (see
[
29
, Section 18]
). For any convex set
C
, we denote the extreme points of
C
by
\operatorname{ext}C
and the relative interior of
C
by
\operatorname{ri}C
. For a proper face of
F
of the unit ball in a real Banach space, the
dual face

F^{*}
is the image of
\operatorname{ri}F
under the duality mapping
J
.

A norm
\|\cdot\|
on
X
is
polyhedral
if the unit ball
B_{1}
is a convex polytope, that is, if it has a finite number of extreme points. A real Banach space
X
is
strictly convex
if
\|x+y\|<2
whenever
x,y\in X
with
x\neq y
and
\|x\|=\|y\|=1
. It is
uniformly convex
if for every
0<\epsilon\leq 2
, there is a
\delta>0
such that for any
x,y\in X
with
\|x\|=\|y\|=1
and
\|x-y\|\geq\epsilon
,
\|x+y\|\leq 2-\delta
. A real Banach space
X
is
smooth
if
X^{*}
is strictly convex, and
uniformly smooth
if
X^{*}
is uniformly convex. Note that uniformly convex and uniformly smooth Banach spaces are always reflexive
[
8
, Corollary 3.21 and Theorem 3.31]
.

A Banach space
X
has the
fixed point property
if for every closed, bounded, convex subset
C\subset X
and every nonexpansive map
f:C\rightarrow C
,
f
has a fixed point. Browder
[
9
]
and Göhde
[
14
]
independently proved that uniformly convex Banach spaces have the fixed point property, while Kirk proved that reflexive Banach spaces with normal structure have the fixed point property
[
19
]
. It turns out that all uniformly convex Banach spaces have normal structure
[
5
, Theorem 4.1]
, while there are examples of reflexive Banach spaces with normal structure that are not isomorphic to any uniformly convex Banach space
[
6
]
. Therefore Kirk’s result is more general than the results of Browder and Göhde. Since then, other conditions have been found that are sufficient for a Banach space to have the fixed point property. It is a famous open problem whether or not all reflexive Banach spaces have the fixed point property. See
[
11
]
and the references therein for some recent contributions and a good introduction to the topic.

2.1.
The metric compactification and horofunctions.

In any metric space
(X,d)
, we can choose an arbitrary base point
b
, and consider the functions

h_{y}(x)=d(x,y)-d(b,y).

Each of these functions is 1-Lipschitz and
h_{y}(b)=0
. Therefore
h_{y}(x)
takes values in the closed interval
[-d(x,b),d(x,b)]\subset\mathbb{R}
for each
x\in X
, so each function
h_{y}
can be identified with an element of the product space

\prod_{x\in X}[-d(x,b),d(x,b)]

which is a compact topological space by Tychonoff’s theorem. The topology on the product space is equivalent to the topology of pointwise convergence, and the closure of the set
\{h_{y}:y\in X\}
in this topology is known as the
metric compactification of

(X,d)
. The elements of the metric compactification are called
metric functionals
. Metric functionals include both the functions
h_{y}
described above, and also functions on the boundary of the metric compactification, which are known as
horofunctions
. Any horofunction
h
is a pointwise limit

h(x)=\lim_{\beta}d(x,y_{\beta})-d(b,y_{\beta})

where
y_{\beta}
is a net in
(X,d)
. For 1-Lipschitz functions, the topology of pointwise convergence is equivalent to the topology of uniform convergence on compact subsets of
X
by the Arzelà-Ascoli theorem. If the metric space
(X,d)
is proper, i.e., all closed balls in
X
are compact, then the topology of uniform convergence on compact subsets of
X
is metrizable. In that case, we can use sequences
y_{k}
to define horofunctions rather than nets. This is true, in particular, if
X
is a finite dimensional Banach space. In addition,
d(b,y_{k})\rightarrow\infty
for any sequence
y_{k}
that defines a horofunction in a proper metric space. Note that horofunctions are always 1-Lipschitz. In a Banach space, horofunctions are also convex. Since the base point
b\in X
is arbitrary, it is standard practice to choose
b=0
when
X
is a Banach space.

In a finite dimensional real Banach space, Walsh has shown (implicitly in
[
31
, Theorem 1.1]
), that a subset of the horofunctions known as the Busemann points are given by

(2.1)

h(x)=\sup_{\nu\in F^{*}}\left\langle u-x,\nu\right\rangle,

where
F^{*}
is a proper face of
B_{1}^{*}
and
u\in X
satisfies
\sup_{\nu\in F^{*}}\left\langle u,\nu\right\rangle=0
. For some finite dimensional Banach spaces all horofunctions are Busemann points, but this is not true in general. The following is
[
31
, Theorem 1.2]
.

Theorem 2.1

(Walsh)
.

Let
X
be a finite dimensional real Banach space. All horofunctions on
X
are Busemann points given by (
2.1
) if and only if the faces of the dual unit ball
B_{1}^{*}
are closed in the Painlevé-Kuratowski topology. This is true, in particular, if
X
has a polyhedral norm or if
X
is smooth.

Even when a horofunction on a finite dimensional normed space is not a Busemann point, we can still make the following observation. Note that this result was part of the proof of
[
21
, Theorem 3.4]
, however we reproduce the proof here for the reader’s convenience.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
X
be a finite dimensional real Banach space. For any horofunction
h
on
X
, there is a
v\in X
with
\|v\|=1
such that

h(x+tv)=h(x)-t

for all
x\in X
and
t\in\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

There is a sequence
x_{k}
with
\|x_{k}\|\rightarrow\infty
such that

h(x)=\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\|x-x_{k}\|-\|x_{k}\|.

We may assume, by passing to a subsequence if necessary, that
\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}x_{k}/\|x_{k}\|=v
. Then

\displaystyle\left|\left|x+t\frac{x_{k}-x}{\|x_{k}-x\|}-x_{k}\right|\right|-\|x_{k}\|

\displaystyle=\|x-x_{k}\|\left(1-\frac{t}{\|x_{k}-x\|}\right)-\|x_{k}\|

\displaystyle=\|x-x_{k}\|-\|x_{k}\|-t.

In the limit as
k\rightarrow\infty
, this equation becomes
h(x+tv)=h(x)-t
.

∎

Unfortunately, Lemma
2.2
does not hold for infinite dimensional Banach spaces. Gutiérrez
[
15
]
has given a complete description of the horofunctions on infinite dimensional Hilbert spaces and
\ell_{p}
spaces with
1\leq p<\infty
. In some Banach spaces (including Hilbert spaces), there are non-trivial horofunctions
h
that are bounded below. Even when a horofunction is not bounded below, it is possible that there is no direction
v\in X
such that
\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}h(tv)=-\infty
as the following example shows.

Example 2.3
.

By
[
15
, Theorem 3.6]
, the function

h(x)=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}(|x_{i}-1|-1)

is a horofunction on the real Banach space
\ell_{1}(\mathbb{N})
. At the same time, it can be shown that
\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}h(tv)=+\infty
for all nonzero
v\in\ell_{1}(\mathbb{N})
even though
\inf_{x\in\ell_{1}(\mathbb{N})}h(x)=-\infty
.

However, when
X
is uniformly smooth, the following result is an immediate consequence of
[
15
, Lemma 5.3]
.

Lemma 2.4

(Gutiérrez)
.

Let
X
be a uniformly smooth real Banach space. If
h
is a horofunction on
X
with
\inf_{x\in X}h(x)=-\infty
, then there is a nonzero linear functional
\nu\in B_{1}^{*}
such that
h(x)=-\left\langle x,\nu\right\rangle
for all
x\in X
.

In the sequel, we will use horofunctions to help understand nonexpansive maps. One important result we will use is the following special case of
[
13
, Theorem 1]
. Note that all Banach spaces are metrically star-shaped, as is any convex subset of a Banach space.

Theorem 2.5

(Gaubert-Vigeral)
.

Let
T
be a nonexpansive self-map of a complete metrically star-shaped metric space
(X,d)
. If
\rho(T)=\inf_{y\in X}d(y,T(y))>0
, then there is a horofunction
h
on
X
such that
h(T(x))\leq h(x)-\rho(T)
for all
x\in X
.

3.
Surjective displacement

The following theorem characterizes when nonexpansive maps on a finite dimensional normed space have surjective displacement. The equivalence of conditions (2), (3), and (4) was already observed by Hochart in
[
16
, Corollary 4]
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
X
be a finite dimensional real Banach space and suppose
f:X\rightarrow X
is nonexpansive. The following are equivalent:

(1)

There is no horofunction
h
and constant
c\in\mathbb{R}
such that
h(f(x))\leq h(x)+c
for all
x\in X
.

(2)

The approximate fixed point sets
F_{\delta}(f)
are bounded for all
\delta>0
.

(3)

f+u
has a nonempty and bounded set of fixed points for all
u\in X
.

(4)

f-\mathrm{id}
maps onto
X
.

Proof.

(1)
\Rightarrow
(2). Suppose
F_{\delta}(f)
is not bounded for some
\delta>0
. Then there is a sequence
x_{k}
such that
\|x_{k}\|\rightarrow\infty
while
\|f(x_{k})-x_{k}\|\leq\delta
for all
k\geq 0
. Replacing
x_{k}
with a subsequence if necessary, we may assume that the horofunction
h(x)=\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\|x-x_{k}\|-\|x_{k}\|
is defined on
X
. Then

\displaystyle h(f(x))

\displaystyle=\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\|f(x)-x_{k}\|-\|x_{k}\|

\displaystyle\leq\liminf_{k\rightarrow\infty}\|f(x)-f(x_{k})\|+\|f(x_{k})-x_{k}\|-\|x_{k}\|

\displaystyle(\text{triangle inequality})

\displaystyle\leq\liminf_{k\rightarrow\infty}\|x-x_{k}\|+\delta-\|x_{k}\|

\displaystyle(\text{by nonexpansiveness})

\displaystyle=h(x)+\delta

for all
x\in X
. This contradicts (1).

(2)
\Rightarrow
(3). Choose any
u\in X
. Let
x_{k}
be defined recursively by
x_{0}=0
and
x_{k+1}=f(x_{k})+u
. Since
f+u
is nonexpansive,

\displaystyle\|f(x_{k})-x_{k}\|

\displaystyle\leq\|f(x_{k})+u-x_{k}\|+\|u\|

\displaystyle(\text{triangle inequality})

\displaystyle\leq\|f(0)+u\|+\|u\|

\displaystyle(\text{by nonexpansiveness})

for all
k
. Let
\delta=\|f(0)+u\|+\|u\|
. Since the set
F_{\delta}(f)
is bounded, it follows that
x_{k}
is a bounded orbit of
f+u
, and therefore
f+u
must have a fixed point
[
22
, Proposition 3.2.4]
. The fixed point set for
f+u
must also be bounded because it is contained in
F_{\delta}(f)
.

(3)
\Rightarrow
(4). This is obvious since
-u
is in the range of
f-\mathrm{id}
if and only if
f+u
has a fixed point.

(4)
\Rightarrow
(1). Consider any horofunction
h
on
X
. By Lemma
2.2
, there is a
v\in X
such that
h(x+tv)=h(x)-t
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
and
x\in X
. Since
f-\mathrm{id}
is onto, we can choose
x\in X
such that
f(x)=x+tv
. Then
h(f(x))=h(x)-t
, which proves (1).
∎

In the rest of this section, we will consider necessary and sufficient conditions on any (finite or infinite dimensional) Banach space to have surjective displacement. Before proving the main result of the section, we need the following minor results.

Lemma 3.2
.

Suppose that
X
is a real Banach space and
f:X\rightarrow X
is nonexpansive. For any
x\in X
with
\|x\|=1
, and any
x^{*}\in J(x)
, the real function
t\mapsto\left\langle f(tx)-tx,x^{*}\right\rangle
is monotone decreasing. Consequently,
\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\left\langle f(tx)-tx,x^{*}\right\rangle
is either
-\infty
or it converges to a finite value.

Proof.

Consider
t_{1}<t_{2}
and observe that

\displaystyle\left\langle f(t_{2}x)-f(t_{1}x),x^{*}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\|f(t_{2}x)-f(t_{1}x)\|

\displaystyle\text{ (since }\|x^{*}\|_{*}=1)

\displaystyle\leq\|t_{2}x-t_{1}x\|

(by nonexpansiveness)

\displaystyle=\left\langle t_{2}x-t_{1}x,x^{*}\right\rangle.

\displaystyle\text{ (since }t_{2}>t_{1}\text{ and }x^{*}\in J(x))

Rearranging terms, we have
\left\langle f(t_{2}x)-t_{2}x,x^{*}\right\rangle\leq\left\langle f(t_{1}x)-t_{1}x,x^{*}\right\rangle
, as claimed.
∎

The next lemma is a semidifferentiability property of Banach space norms that is not new, but we could not find a reference. Before stating the result, we review some terminology about convex functions. Let
X
be a real Banach space and suppose that
f:X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a convex function. An
x^{*}\in X^{*}
is called a
subgradient
of
f
at
x
if
f(z)-f(x)\geq\left\langle z-x,x^{*}\right\rangle

for all
z\in X
. The set of all subgradients at
x
is called the
subdifferential
of
f
at
x
. For any
x\in X
with
\|x\|=1
, the subdifferential for the norm at
x
is precisely
J(x)
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
X
be a real Banach space and let
x,y\in X
with
\|x\|=1
. Then there exists
x^{*}\in J(x)
such that

(3.1)

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\|tx-y\|-\left\langle tx-y,x^{*}\right\rangle=0.

Proof.

If
X
is finite dimensional, then
[
29
, Theorem 23.4]
says that

\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{\|x-\epsilon y\|-\|x\|}{\epsilon}=\sup_{x^{*}\in J(x)}\left\langle-y,x^{*}\right\rangle

since
J(x)
is the subdifferential of the norm at
x
.
Furthermore
J(x)
is compact, so there is an
x^{*}\in J(x)
where the supremum is attained, and then

\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{\|x-\epsilon y\|-\|x\|}{\epsilon}=-\left\langle y,x^{*}\right\rangle.

By replacing
\epsilon
with
t^{-1}
, we have (
3.1
). The infinite dimensional version follows by first applying the result to the finite dimensional subspace spanned by
x
and
y
, and then extending the linear functional
x^{*}
to all of
X
using the Hahn-Banach theorem.
∎

For any nonzero
x\in X
, let
W_{x}=\{w\in X:\sup_{x^{*}\in J(x)}\left\langle w,x^{*}\right\rangle<0\}
. Note that each
W_{x}
is a nonempty open convex cone in
X
. The following result says that if certain limits are bounded below, then the range of the displacement map does not intersect a translate of
W_{x}
for some
x
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
X
be a real Banach space and let
f:X\rightarrow X
be nonexpansive. Let
x\in X
with
\|x\|=1
.
If
\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\left\langle f(tx)-tx,x^{*}\right\rangle\geq c

for all
x^{*}\in J(x)
, then
\operatorname{range}(f-\mathrm{id})
does not intersect the open affine cone
W_{x}+cx
.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.3
, for any
y\in X
there is an
x^{*}\in J(x)
such that

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\|tx-y\|-\left\langle tx-y,x^{*}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\geq\limsup_{t\rightarrow\infty}\|f(tx)-f(y)\|-\left\langle tx-y,x^{*}\right\rangle

\displaystyle(\text{nonexpansiveness})

\displaystyle\geq\limsup_{t\rightarrow\infty}\left\langle f(tx)-f(y),x^{*}\right\rangle-\left\langle tx-y,x^{*}\right\rangle

\displaystyle(\text{since }\|x^{*}\|_{*}=1)

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\left\langle f(tx)-tx,x^{*}\right\rangle-\left\langle f(y)-y,x^{*}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\geq c-\left\langle f(y)-y,x^{*}\right\rangle.

This means that
\sup_{x^{*}\in J(x)}\left\langle f(y)-y,x^{*}\right\rangle\geq c
so
f(y)-y\notin W_{x}+cx
.
∎

The next observation is also about the range of displacement maps, this time when
f
is nonexpansive with respect to a uniformly smooth norm. This lemma can be proved directly from a more general result
[
27
, Theorem 5.2]
which says that the closure of the range of an
m
-accretive operator on a smooth Banach space is always convex. Here we take a different approach using horofunctions.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
X
be a uniformly smooth real Banach space and suppose that
f:X\rightarrow X
is nonexpansive. The closure of the range of
f-\mathrm{id}
is convex.

Proof.

Suppose that
u\in X\backslash\overline{\operatorname{range}}(f-\mathrm{id})
. Then
\inf_{x\in X}\|f(x)-u-x\|=\rho>0
. By Theorem
2.5
, there is a horofunction
h
on
X
such that
h(f(x)-u)\leq h(x)-\rho
for all
x\in X
. This horofunction cannot be bounded below since the iterates
(f-u)^{k}(0)
have
h((f-u)^{k}(0))\rightarrow-\infty
. So Lemma
2.4
implies that there is a nonzero linear functional
\nu\in B_{1}^{*}
such that
h(x)=-\left\langle x,\nu\right\rangle
for all
x
. Then

-\left\langle f(x)-u,\nu\right\rangle\leq-\left\langle x,\nu\right\rangle-\rho

for all
x\in X
. Equivalently,

\left\langle f(x)-x,\nu\right\rangle\geq\left\langle u,\nu\right\rangle+\rho.

Therefore the linear functional
\nu
separates
u
from
\overline{\operatorname{range}}(f-\mathrm{id})
. This means that
\overline{\operatorname{range}}(f-\mathrm{id})
is the intersection of an infinite family of closed half-spaces (one for each
u\notin\overline{\operatorname{range}}(f-\mathrm{id})
), and as such, it must be a convex set.
∎

The last preliminary observation before the main result of this section is the following fixed point theorem
[
17
, Theorem 2.4]
.

Theorem 3.6

(Kaewcharoen-Kirk)
.

Let
X
be a Banach space which has the fixed point property, let
C
be a closed, convex subset of
X
, and suppose that
f:C\rightarrow C
is nonexpansive. If
F_{\delta}(f)=\{x\in C:\|f(x)-x\|\leq\delta\}
is bounded and nonempty for some
\delta\geq 0
, then
f
has a fixed point.

The main result of this section is the following theorem. Condition
1
in the theorem is sufficient for a nonexpansive map to have surjective displacement when the Banach space has the fixed point property, while condition
5
is necessary for surjective displacement on any Banach space.

Theorem 3.7
.

Let
f:X\rightarrow X
be a nonexpansive map on a real Banach space
X
. Consider the following possible conditions on
f
.

(1)

The approximate fixed point sets
F_{\delta}(f)
are bounded for all
\delta>0
.

(2)

f+u
has a nonempty and bounded set of fixed points for all
u\in X
.

(3)

f-\mathrm{id}
maps onto
X
.

(4)

The range of
f-\mathrm{id}
is dense in
X
.

(5)

For every
x\in X
with
\|x\|=1
, there is an
x^{*}\in J(x)
such that

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\left\langle f(tx)-tx,x^{*}\right\rangle=-\infty.

For these conditions, the following implications are true.

•

Condition
1
implies
4
,
2
implies
3
, and
4
implies
5
.

•

If
X
has the fixed point property, then
1
implies
2
.

•

If
X
is uniformly smooth, then conditions
4
and
5
are equivalent.

•

If
X
is a Hilbert space, then conditions
2
and
3
are equivalent.

•

If
X
is finite dimensional with a smooth or polyhedral norm, then all five conditions are equivalent.

Proof.

1

\Rightarrow

4
. Suppose by way of contradiction that condition
4
is false. Then there is a
u\in X\backslash\overline{\operatorname{range}}(f-\mathrm{id})
and
\rho=\inf_{x\in X}\|f(x)-u-x\|>0
. By Theorem
2.5
, there is a horofunction
h
on
X
such that
h(f(x)-u)\leq h(x)-\rho
for all
x\in X
. Let the sequence
x_{k}
be defined recursively by
x_{0}=0
and
x_{k+1}=f(x_{k})-u
. Then
h(x_{k})\leq-\rho k
for all
k\geq 0
. Since
h
is 1-Lipschitz, this implies that
\|x_{k}\|\rightarrow\infty
. At the same time, for all
k
we have

\displaystyle\|f(x_{k})-x_{k}\|

\displaystyle\leq\|f(x_{k})-u-x_{k}\|+\|u\|

(triangle inequality)

\displaystyle\leq\|f(0)-u\|+\|u\|.

\displaystyle\text{(since }f-u\text{ is nonexpansive)}

So
x_{k}
is an unbounded sequence contained in the set
F_{\delta}(f)
with
\delta=\|f(0)-u\|+\|u\|
which contradicts
1
.

4

\Rightarrow

5
. Suppose that condition
5
fails, that is, there is a constant
c\in\mathbb{R}
and
x\in X
with
\|x\|=1
such that
\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\left\langle f(tx)-tx,x^{*}\right\rangle\geq c
for all
x^{*}\in J(x)
. By Lemma
3.4
, the range of
f-\mathrm{id}
does not contain any elements in the nonempty open set
\{y\in X:\max_{y^{*}\in J(x)}\left\langle y,y^{*}\right\rangle<c\}
. This contradicts
4
.

1

\Rightarrow

2
. We assume here that
X
has the fixed point property. Let
x,u\in X
. Then
\|f(x)-x\|\leq\|f(x)+u-x\|+\|u\|
by the triangle inequality. Therefore
F_{\delta}(f+u)\subseteq F_{(\delta+\|u\|)}(f)
is bounded for every
\delta\geq 0
. By choosing
\delta
large enough so that
F_{\delta}(f+u)
is nonempty and letting
C=X
, we see that Theorem
3.6
implies that
f+u
has a fixed point. In addition, the fixed point set
F_{0}(f+u)
is bounded because it is contained in
F_{\|u\|}(f)
.

5

\Rightarrow

4
. Suppose now that
X
is uniformly smooth.
By Lemma
3.5
,
\overline{\operatorname{range}}(f-\mathrm{id})
is convex. Suppose there exists
u\in X\backslash\overline{\operatorname{range}}(f-\mathrm{id})
. By the Hahn-Banach theorem, there is a linear functional
x^{*}\in X^{*}
such that
\left\langle f(x)-x,x^{*}\right\rangle>\left\langle u,x^{*}\right\rangle
for all
x\in X
. We may assume that
\|x^{*}\|_{*}=1
. Since any uniformly smooth Banach space is reflexive, there exists
x\in X
such that
x^{*}\in J(x)
, and since
X^{*}
is uniformly convex,
x^{*}
is the only element of
J(x)
. Then the fact that
\left\langle f(tx)-tx,x^{*}\right\rangle>\left\langle u,x^{*}\right\rangle
for all
t>0
contradicts
5
.

It is obvious that
2
implies
3
, so we will just prove the converse when
X
is a Hilbert space.

3

\Rightarrow

2
. Let
X
be a Hilbert space. It suffices to prove that the fixed point set
F_{0}(f)
is bounded since the same argument will apply to
f+u
for any
u
. Choose any
w\in F_{0}(f)
and
y\in X
. Since
f
has surjective displacement, there is an
x\in X
such that
f(x)=x+y
. Then
\|x+y-w\|^{2}=\|f(x)-f(w)\|^{2}\leq\|x-w\|^{2}
by nonexpansiveness. Using the inner-product to expand the norms in this inequality, and then canceling terms, we find that
\left\langle w,y\right\rangle\geq\left\langle x,y\right\rangle+\tfrac{1}{2}\|y\|^{2}
. We can also find a corresponding upper bound
\left\langle w,y\right\rangle\leq\left\langle x,y\right\rangle-\tfrac{1}{2}\|y\|^{2}
by finding an
x\in X
such that
f(x)=x-y
. So there is a constant
b_{y}>0
, depending only on
y
, such that
|\left\langle w,y\right\rangle|\leq b_{y}
for all
w\in F_{0}(f)
. Thus
F_{0}(f)
is weakly bounded which means it must also be strongly bounded
[
8
, Corollary 2.4]
.

Now suppose that
X
is finite dimensional and is either smooth or has a polyhedral norm. By Theorem
3.1
, conditions
1
,
2
, and
3
are equivalent, and the above results tell us that
1
implies
4
and
4
implies
5
. Therefore, it suffices to prove that
5
implies
1
.

5

\Rightarrow

1
. Suppose that condition
1
is not true. By Theorem
3.1
, there is a horofunction
h
on
X
and a constant
c\in\mathbb{R}
such that
h(f(x))\leq h(x)+c
for all
x\in X
. Since we are assuming that either
X
is smooth or
X
has a polyhedral norm, Theorem
2.1
implies that
h
is given by (
2.1
) for some proper face
F^{*}
of
B_{1}^{*}
and some
u\in X
such that
\sup_{\nu\in F^{*}}\left\langle u,\nu\right\rangle=0
. Also, when
X
is smooth or has a polyhedral norm, all proper faces of
B^{*}_{1}
are exposed faces. Thus, there is an
x\in X
with
\|x\|=1
such that
F^{*}=J(x)
.
Now, for any
x^{*}\in F^{*}
and
t>0
,

\displaystyle\left\langle u-f(tx),x^{*}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\nu\in F^{*}}\left\langle u-f(tx),\nu\right\rangle

\displaystyle=h(f(tx))

\displaystyle(\text{by }\eqref{eq:Cormac})

\displaystyle\leq h(tx)+c

\displaystyle=\sup_{\nu\in F^{*}}\left\langle u-tx,\nu\right\rangle+c

\displaystyle(\text{by }\eqref{eq:Cormac}\text{ again})

\displaystyle=-t+c.

\displaystyle(\text{since }J(x)=F^{*})

By rearranging terms, we have
\left\langle u,x^{*}\right\rangle-c\leq\left\langle f(tx),x^{*}\right\rangle-t=\left\langle f(tx)-tx,x^{*}\right\rangle
. Since this is true for all
x^{*}\in J(x)
and
t>0
, we have contradicted condition
5
.
∎

Remark 3.8
.

The implication
1

\Rightarrow

4
appears to already be known. In fact, the following is
[
10
, Exercise 13.6(a)]
: If

is an

-accretive operator (also known as a hyperaccretive operator) on a Banach space

such that

is bounded for all

, then

has dense range. For any nonexpansive map

, the map
\mathrm{id}-f
is
m
-accretive, so this exercise implies that
1

\Rightarrow

4
. If
X
has the fixed point property, then
1

\Rightarrow

3
is also known. In that case, if
F
is an
m
-accretive operator such that
\{x\in X:\|F(x)\|\leq\alpha\}
is bounded for all

, then
[

Remark 3.9
.

If
X
is a Hilbert space and
f:X\rightarrow X
is nonexpansive, then the multivalued map
u\mapsto F_{0}(f+u)
is maximally monotone. Since monotone operators on a Hilbert space are locally bounded on the interior of their domains
[
10
, Theorem 23.2]
, this gives an alternative proof of the equivalence of conditions
2
and
3
. This also implies that if
f
has surjective displacement, then the set of
u
such that
F_{0}(f+u)
is single-valued is a dense
G_{\delta}
subset of
X

[
10
, Theorem 23.6]
.

In infinite dimensions, it is easy to construct nonexpansive maps with dense displacement, but not surjective displacement.

Example 3.10
.

Let
T:\ell_{2}(\mathbb{N})\rightarrow\ell_{2}(\mathbb{N})
be the right shift operator

T(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},\ldots)=(0,x_{1},x_{2},\ldots).

If
e_{1}=(1,0,0,\ldots)
, then the map
T+e_{1}
is an isometry with no fixed points in
\ell_{2}(\mathbb{N})
. Thus
T
does not have surjective displacement. However,
\|T(x)-x\|^{2}=2\|x\|^{2}-2\left\langle T(x),x\right\rangle>0
for all nonzero
x\in\ell_{2}(\mathbb{N})
. Therefore,
\left\langle T(tx)-tx,x\right\rangle=t\left\langle T(x)-x,x\right\rangle\rightarrow-\infty
as
t\rightarrow\infty
for all nonzero
x
. This shows that
T
has dense displacement by Theorem
3.7
.

Condition
1
is sufficient for a nonexpansive map to have surjective displacement, but the following example shows that it is not necessary in infinite dimensions.

Example 3.11
.

Let
X=\ell_{2}(\mathbb{N})
, let
\{e_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
denote the standard basis for
\ell_{2}(\mathbb{N})
, and let
f:X\rightarrow X
be the map defined entrywise by

f_{j}(x)=\begin{cases}\tfrac{j}{j+1}(x_{j}+1)&\text{if }x_{j}<-1\\
0&\text{if }|x_{j}|\leq 1\\
\tfrac{j}{j+1}(x_{j}-1)&\text{if }x_{j}>1.\end{cases}

Then
f
is nonexpansive and
f-\mathrm{id}
is onto. After all, for any
u\in X
,
|u_{j}|\leq 1
for all but finitely many
j\in\mathbb{N}
. Therefore, we can choose
x\in X
as follows:

x_{j}=\begin{cases}-(j+1)u_{j}+j&\text{if }u_{j}>1\\
-u_{j}&\text{if }|u_{j}|\leq 1\\
-(j+1)u_{j}-j&\text{if }u_{j}<-1,\end{cases}

so that
f(x)-x=u
. On the other hand, observe that the approximate fixed point set
F_{2}(f)
contains
(k+2)e_{k}
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Therefore
f
has surjective displacement even though the approximate fixed point set
F_{2}(f)
is not bounded.

For linear nonexpansive maps, condition
3
implies condition
1
from Theorem
3.7
.

Lemma 3.12
.

Suppose
X
is a real Banach space,
T:X\rightarrow X
is linear and nonexpansive, and
T
has surjective displacement. Then all approximate fixed point sets
F_{\delta}(T)
are bounded.

Proof.

For any nonzero
x\in X
,

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\left\langle T(tx)-tx,x^{*}\right\rangle=\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}t\left\langle T(x)-x,x^{*}\right\rangle=-\infty

for some
x^{*}\in J(x)
by Theorem
3.7
. Therefore
T(x)-x\neq 0
and so the kernel of
T-\mathrm{id}
is
\{0\}
. This means that
T-\mathrm{id}
is one-to-one and onto, so it is invertible with a bounded inverse. Then
F_{\delta}(T)=(T-\mathrm{id})^{-1}B_{\delta}
, so
F_{\delta}(T)
is bounded for all
\delta\geq 0
.
∎

An interesting corollary of Theorem
3.7
is the following.

Corollary 3.13
.

Let
X
be a real Banach space and suppose that
f
and
g
are both nonexpansive maps on
X
. If
X
is uniformly smooth and
f
has dense displacement, then
cf+(1-c)g
is a nonexpansive map with dense displacement for all
0<c<1
. If
f
has surjective displacement and
X
is finite dimensional with either a smooth or polyhedral norm, then
cf+(1-c)g
is a nonexpansive map with surjective displacement for all
0<c<1
.

Proof.

It is easy to verify that
cf+(1-c)g
is nonexpansive for all
0<c<1
. By Lemma
3.2
,

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\left\langle cf(tx)+(1-c)g(tx)-tx,x^{*}\right\rangle

exists and is contained in
[-\infty,\infty)
for all
x\in X
with
\|x\|=1
and
x^{*}\in J(x)
. Suppose
f
has dense displacement. Then
f
satisfies condition
5
of Theorem
3.7
and so must
cf+(1-c)g
for all
0<c<1
. Then if
X
is uniformly smooth, it follows that from Theorem
3.7
that
cf+(1-c)g
has dense displacement. If
X
is finite dimensional and smooth or
X
has a polyhedral norm, then Theorem
3.7
implies that
cf+(1-c)g
has surjective displacement.
∎

Some of the implications from Theorem
3.7
may not be best possible. It is not clear whether condition
1
implies surjective displacement on Banach spaces without the fixed point property or whether condition
5
implies surjective displacement on every finite dimensional real Banach space. We also don’t know whether conditions
2
and
3
are equivalent on Banach spaces that are not Hilbert spaces.

4.
Polyhedral norm nonexpansive maps

The following theorem gives a computational procedure involving a finite number of limits that determine whether or not a nonexpansive map on a space with a polyhedral norm has surjective displacement.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
X
be a finite dimensional real Banach space with a polyhedral norm and suppose that
f:X\rightarrow X
is nonexpansive. For every face
F
of the unit ball
B_{1}
, choose an
x_{F}
in the relative interior of
F
and an
x_{F}^{*}
in the relative interior of the corresponding dual face
F^{*}
of
B_{1}^{*}
. Then
f-\mathrm{id}
maps onto
X
if and only if

(4.1)

lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\left\langle f(tx_{F})-tx_{F},x_{F}^{*}\right\rangle=-\infty

for every proper face
F
.

Of course, Theorem
4.1
is only useful as a test for the existence of fixed points if it is possible to calculate the limits in (
4.1
). However, given an explicit expression for
f
, this is frequently the case. Before proving the theorem, we make the following general observation.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
X
be a real Banach space, let
x,y\in X
with
\|x\|=\|y\|=1
, and suppose that
J(x)\subseteq J(y)
. Then for any
x^{*}\in J(x)
,

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\left\langle f(tx)-tx,x^{*}\right\rangle\leq\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\left\langle f(ty)-ty,x^{*}\right\rangle.

In particular, if
J(x)=J(y)
, then the two limits are equal.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.3
, for any
s>0
there is a
z^{*}\in J(x)
such that

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\|tx-sy\|-\left\langle tx-sy,z^{*}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\|tx-sy\|-t+s

\displaystyle\text{(since }z^{*}\in J(x)\subseteq J(y))

\displaystyle\geq\limsup_{t\rightarrow\infty}\|f(tx)-f(sy)\|-t+s

\displaystyle(\text{nonexpansiveness})

\displaystyle\geq\limsup_{t\rightarrow\infty}\left\langle f(tx)-f(sy),x^{*}\right\rangle-t+s

\displaystyle(\text{since }\|x^{*}\|_{*}=1)

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\langle f(tx)-tx,x^{*}\rangle-\left\langle f(sy)-sy,x^{*}\right\rangle.

We have shown that
\left\langle f(sy)-sy,x^{*}\right\rangle\geq\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\langle f(tx)-tx,x^{*}\rangle
for all
s>0
. The conclusion follows by letting
s\rightarrow\infty
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
4.1
.

Choose any proper face
F
of
B_{1}
.
If
f-\mathrm{id}
maps onto
X
, then Theorem
3.7
implies that

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\left\langle f(tx_{F})-tx_{F},x^{*}\right\rangle=-\infty

for at least one
x^{*}\in F^{*}
. Any
x_{F}^{*}
in the relative interior of
F^{*}
can be expressed as a non-trivial convex combination of
x^{*}
with some other
y^{*}\in F^{*}
, that is,
x_{F}^{*}=cx^{*}+(1-c)y^{*}
where
0<c<1
. Since the
\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\left\langle f(tx_{F})-tx_{F},y^{*}\right\rangle<\infty
by Lemma
3.2
, it follows that
\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\left\langle f(tx_{F})-tx_{F},x_{F}^{*}\right\rangle=-\infty
. This proves that the condition given by (
4.1
) is necessary in order for
f-\mathrm{id}
to map onto
X
.

Now, suppose that (
4.1
) holds for one
x_{F}\in\operatorname{ri}F
. For any other
y_{F}\in\operatorname{ri}F
,
J(x_{F})=J(y_{F})
. Then by Lemma
4.2

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\left\langle f(ty_{F})-ty_{F},x_{F}^{*}\right\rangle=\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\left\langle f(tx_{F})-tx_{F},x_{F}^{*}\right\rangle=-\infty

for all
y_{F}\in\operatorname{ri}F
. Therefore confirming (
4.1
) for one representative
x_{F}
in the relative interior of each proper face
F
is sufficient to confirm it for all
y_{F}\in\operatorname{ri}F
. Since every
x\in X
with
\|x\|=1
is contained in the relative interior of some face
F
, verifying (
4.1
) for every face
F
will establish condition
5
in Theorem
3.7
. That is sufficient to show that
f
has surjective displacement.
∎

One might wonder if checking (
4.1
) for a subset of the faces of
B_{1}
might be sufficient to prove that (
4.1
) holds for all proper faces? For the supremum norm on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, this is not the case. The following example shows that for any proper face
F
of the unit ball in
(\mathbb{R}^{n},\|\cdot\|_{\infty})
, there is an
\ell_{\infty}
nonexpansive map such that (
4.1
) holds for every face except
F
.

Example 4.3
.

Let
X=\mathbb{R}^{n}
with the supremum norm
\|x\|_{\infty}
. Let
N=\{1,\ldots,n\}
.
For any subset
I\subseteq N
, let
e_{I}
be the vector in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
defined by

(e_{I})_{i}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }i\in I\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

The faces of the unit ball in
X
are the sets

F_{IJ}=\{x\in B_{1}:x_{i}=1\text{ if }i\in I,x_{i}=-1\text{ if }i\in J\}

where
I
and
J
are disjoint subsets of
N
. Note that
F_{IJ}
is a proper face if at least one of the sets
I,J
is nonempty. The corresponding dual faces are

F_{IJ}^{*}=\{x\in B_{1}^{*}:\sum_{i\in I}x_{i}-\sum_{j\in J}x_{j}=1\}.

For each face
F_{IJ}
, let
x_{IJ}=e_{I}-e_{J}
and
x_{IJ}^{*}=\tfrac{1}{|I|+|J|}(e_{I}-e_{J})
.
Observe that
x_{IJ}\in\operatorname{ri}(F_{IJ})
and
x_{IJ}^{*}\in\operatorname{ri}(F_{IJ}^{*})
.

In order to apply Theorem
4.1
to a nonexpansive map
f
on
X
, we must check whether

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\left\langle f(tx_{IJ})-tx_{IJ},x_{IJ}^{*}\right\rangle=-\infty

for every disjoint pair
I,J\subseteq N
with
I\cup J\neq\varnothing
.

Fix disjoint sets
K,L\subset N
such that
K\cup L
is nonempty.
Let
P_{KL}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
be the map

P_{KL}(x)_{i}=\begin{cases}\max\{x_{i},0\}&\text{if }i\in K\cup L\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Let
D_{L}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
be the transformation

D_{L}(x)_{i}=\begin{cases}-x_{i}&\text{if }i\in L\\
x_{i}&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Finally, let
\sigma
be a permutation of
N
that is a cyclic permutation of the elements of
K\cup L
and let
S:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
be the permutation operator corresponding to
\sigma
, that is
S(x)_{i}=x_{\sigma(i)}
.

Each of the maps
P_{KL}
,
D_{L}
, and
S
are nonexpansive on
X
, and therefore so is the composition
f=D_{L}\circ S\circ P_{KL}\circ D_{L}
. Observe that
tx_{KL}
is a fixed point of
f
for all
t>0
. Therefore
f
cannot have surjective displacement. But every one of the limits (
4.1
) holds, except for the one corresponding to the face
F_{KL}
.

When
\|\cdot\|
is either the
\ell_{1}
or the
\ell_{\infty}
norm on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the condition of Theorem
4.1
involves checking
3^{n}-1
limit inequalities.
Interestingly, according to Kalai’s
3^{d}
conjecture
[
18
,
30
]
, any centrally symmetric
n
-dimensional polytope should have at least
3^{n}
nonempty faces. Thus the number of limits needed to check the conditions of Theorem
4.1
for other polyhedral norms on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
should always be at least
3^{n}-1
.

Unfortunately, there is no way to show that an
\ell_{2}
-nonexpansive map on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
has surjective displacement using a finite set of limits of the type in Theorem
4.1
. To see why, let
f
be an orthogonal projection onto a one-dimensional subspace spanned by
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
\|v\|_{2}=1
. For any
x
that is not a scalar multiple of
v
(that is, almost every
x
), we have
|\left\langle x,v\right\rangle|<\|x\|_{2}
, so
\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\left\langle f(tx)-tx,x\right\rangle=t(|\left\langle x,v\right\rangle|^{2}-\|x\|_{2}^{2})\rightarrow-\infty
. This is despite the fact that
f
has an unbounded set of fixed points.
In section
7
, we will give a computable sufficient condition for confirming surjective displacement when
f
is a nonexpansive map on a smooth finite dimensional normed space.

5.
Perron-Frobenius theory

Let
N=\{1,\ldots,n\}
. For any subset
I\subseteq N
, let
e_{I}
be the vector in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
defined by

(e_{I})_{i}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }i\in I\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

We use
\geq
to denote the natural partial ordering on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
:
x\geq y
if and only if
x_{i}\geq y_{i}
for all
i\in N
. A map
T:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
is
order-preserving
if
T(x)\geq T(y)
whenever
x\geq y
.
T
is
additively homogeneous
if
T(x+te_{N})=T(x)+te_{N}
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
t\in\mathbb{R}
and
T
is
additively subhomogeneous
if
T(x+te_{N})\leq T(x)+te_{N}
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
t\geq 0
.

If
T:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
is order-preserving, then
T
is nonexpansive with respect to the
\ell_{\infty}
-norm if and only if it is additively subhomogeneous
[
22
, Lemma 2.7.2]
. Such maps are called
subtopical
. Order-preserving maps on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
that are additively homogeneous are called
topical
.

5.1.
Surjective displacement for subtopical maps

The following necessary and sufficient condition for a subtopical map to have surjective displacement only requires computing
2(2^{n}-1)
limits as opposed to the
3^{n}-1
limits needed by the condition in Theorem
4.1
for
\ell_{\infty}
-nonexpansive maps that are not order-preserving.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
T:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
be subtopical. Then
T
has surjective displacement if and only if for every nonempty
I\subseteq N
,

(5.1)

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\left\langle T(te_{I}),e_{I}\right\rangle-t|I|=-\infty\text{ and }\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\left\langle T(-te_{I}),e_{I}\right\rangle+t|I|=+\infty.

Proof.

Since
T
is subtopical, it is nonexpansive with respect to the supremum norm
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
. We use the notation from Example
4.3
for the faces
F_{IJ}
, dual faces
F_{IJ}^{*}
and representative points
x_{IJ}\in\operatorname{ri}F_{IJ}
and
x_{IJ}^{*}\in\operatorname{ri}F_{IJ}^{*}
of the unit ball
B_{1}
in
(\mathbb{R}^{n},\|\cdot\|_{\infty})
.
By Theorem
4.1
,
T
has surjective displacement if and only if

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\left\langle T(tx_{IJ})-tx_{IJ},x_{IJ}^{*}\right\rangle=-\infty

for every disjoint pair
I,J\subseteq N
with
I\cup J\neq\varnothing
. Since
x_{IJ}=(|I|+|J|)x_{IJ}^{*}
, the limit above is equivalent to

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\left\langle T(tx_{IJ}),x_{IJ}\right\rangle-t(|I|+|J|)=-\infty.

In order for
T
to have surjective displacement, it is necessary that both limits in (
5.1
) hold true, as those two limits correspond to the limit condition above for
x_{I\varnothing}
and
x_{\varnothing I}
. We will now show that the limits in (
5.1
) are also sufficient for
T
to have surjective displacement.

Note that
-e_{J}\leq x_{IJ}\leq e_{I}
. Therefore
T(-te_{J})\leq T(tx_{IJ})\leq T(te_{I})
for all
t>0
. So

\displaystyle\left\langle T(tx_{IJ}),x_{IJ}\right\rangle-t(|I|+|J|)

\displaystyle=\left\langle T(tx_{IJ}),e_{I}\right\rangle-t|I|-\left\langle T(tx_{IJ}),e_{J}\right\rangle-t|J|

\displaystyle\leq\left\langle T(te_{I}),e_{I}\right\rangle-t|I|-\left\langle T(-te_{J}),e_{J}\right\rangle-t|J|

\displaystyle=\left(\left\langle T(te_{I}),e_{I}\right\rangle-t|I|\right)-\left(\left\langle T(-te_{J}),e_{J}\right\rangle+t|J|\right).

If (
5.1
) holds for all nonempty
I
, then the inequality above implies that

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\left\langle T(tx_{IJ}),x_{IJ}\right\rangle-t(|I|+|J|)=-\infty

for all disjoint sets
I,J\subseteq N
with
I\cup J\neq\varnothing
. Hence
T
has surjective displacement by Theorem
4.1
.
∎

5.2.
Existence of additive eigenvectors for topical maps

The results in this subsection are not new, but rather give alternative proofs of some necessary and sufficient conditions from
[
3
]
for a topical map
T:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
to have the property that
T+u
has an additive eigenvector for all
u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The results in
[
3
]
were proved using a game theoretic approach involving dominions of players in certain one and two-player games. Here we will show how those results follow from Theorem
4.1
.

For
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, let
\mathsf{t}(x)=\max_{i\in N}x_{i}
and
\mathsf{b}(x)=\min_{i\in N}x_{i}
. If
T:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
is topical, then
T
is nonexpansive with respect to the variation seminorm
\|x\|_{\text{var}}=\mathsf{t}(x)-\mathsf{b}(x)

[
12
, Section 2.2]
. Note that
\|\cdot\|_{\text{var}}
is not a norm on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, but it is a norm on the subspace
V_{0}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\left\langle x,e_{N}\right\rangle=0\}
. The normalized map
f(x)=T(x)-\tfrac{1}{n}\left\langle T(x),e_{N}\right\rangle e_{N}
maps
V_{0}
into itself and is nonexpansive with respect to
\|\cdot\|_{\text{var}}
. If
f
has a fixed point
x
in
V_{0}
, then
x
is an
additive eigenvector
of
T
, that is,
T(x)=x+\lambda e_{N}
for some
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
. In fact,
T+u
has an additive eigenvector if and only if
f+u_{0}
with
u_{0}=u-\tfrac{1}{n}\left\langle u,e_{N}\right\rangle e_{N}
has a fixed point. Since the variation norm is a polyhedral norm on
V_{0}
, Theorem
4.1
gives a testable necessary and sufficient condition to see if
T+u
has an additive eigenvector for every
u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We will see below that the condition ends up being equivalent to the one given in
[
3
, Theorem 4.2]
.

The proper faces of
B_{1}
in
(V_{0},\|\cdot\|_{\text{var}})
are the sets

F_{IJ}=\{x\in V_{0}\,:\,\|x\|_{\text{var}}=1,\,x_{i}=\mathsf{t}(x)\,\forall i\in I,\,x_{j}=\mathsf{b}(x)\,\forall j\in J\}

where
I,J\subset N
are nonempty and disjoint.
The dual norm is
\|x\|_{\text{var}}^{*}=\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{i\in N}|x_{i}|
, and the faces of the dual unit ball are

F_{IJ}^{*}=\{x\in V_{0}\,:\,\|x\|_{\text{var}}^{*}=1,\textstyle{\sum_{i\in I}}x_{i}=1,\textstyle{\sum_{j\in J}}x_{j}=-1\}.

Note that if
x\in F_{IJ}^{*}
, then
x_{i}>0
for all
i\in I
,
x_{i}<0
for
i\in J
and
x_{i}=0
for
i\in(I\cup J)^{c}
.
Let
x_{IJ}^{*}=\frac{1}{|I|}e_{I}-\frac{1}{|J|}e_{J}
, and let
\delta=\|x_{IJ}^{*}\|_{\text{var}}=\left(\frac{1}{|I|}+\frac{1}{|J|}\right).
By construction,
x_{IJ}^{*}\in\operatorname{ri}F_{IJ}^{*}
.
If we let
x_{IJ}=\tfrac{1}{\delta}x_{IJ}^{*}
, then
x_{IJ}\in\operatorname{ri}F_{IJ}
.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
T:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
be topical. Let
f:V_{0}\rightarrow V_{0}
be the corresponding normalized map
f(x)=T(x)-\tfrac{1}{n}\left\langle T(x),e_{N}\right\rangle e_{N}
. Let
I,J
be disjoint, nonempty subsets of
N
. Then

(5.2)

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\left\langle f(tx_{IJ})-tx_{IJ},x_{IJ}^{*}\right\rangle=-\infty

if and only if

(5.3)

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\left\langle T(-te_{I^{c}}),e_{I}\right\rangle=-\infty\text{ or }\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\left\langle T(te_{J^{c}}),e_{J}\right\rangle=\infty.

Proof.

Start by observing that

-\tfrac{1}{\delta|J|}e_{J}\leq x_{IJ}\leq\tfrac{1}{\delta|I|}e_{I}.

This implies that

-e_{I^{c}}\leq x_{IJ}-\tfrac{1}{\delta|I|}e_{N}\leq-\tfrac{1}{\delta|I|}e_{I^{c}}

and

\tfrac{1}{\delta|J|}e_{J^{c}}\leq x_{IJ}+\tfrac{1}{\delta|J|}e_{N}\leq e_{J^{c}}.

Also,

\displaystyle\left\langle f(tx_{IJ})-tx_{IJ},x_{IJ}^{*}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle T(tx_{IJ}),x_{IJ}^{*}\right\rangle-t

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{|I|}\left\langle T(tx_{IJ}),e_{I}\right\rangle-\tfrac{1}{|J|}\left\langle T(tx_{IJ}),e_{J}\right\rangle-t

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{|I|}\left\langle T(tx_{IJ}-\tfrac{1}{\delta|I|}e_{N}),e_{I}\right\rangle-\tfrac{1}{|J|}\left\langle T(tx_{IJ}+\tfrac{1}{\delta|J|}e_{N}),e_{J}\right\rangle.

Combining these facts, we see that

(5.4)

\left\langle f(tx_{IJ})-tx_{IJ},x_{IJ}^{*}\right\rangle\geq\tfrac{1}{|I|}\left\langle T(-te_{I^{c}}),e_{I}\right\rangle-\tfrac{1}{|J|}\left\langle T(te_{J^{c}}),e_{J}\right\rangle

and

(5.5)

\left\langle f(tx_{IJ})-tx_{IJ},x_{IJ}^{*}\right\rangle\leq\tfrac{1}{|I|}\left\langle T(-\tfrac{t}{\delta|I|}e_{I^{c}}),e_{I}\right\rangle-\tfrac{1}{|J|}\left\langle T(\tfrac{t}{\delta|J|}e_{J^{c}}),e_{J}\right\rangle.

As
T
is order-preserving, both
\left\langle T(-te_{I^{c}}),e_{I}\right\rangle
and
\left\langle T(te_{J^{c}}),e_{J}\right\rangle
are monotone functions of
t
. So both
\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\left\langle T(-te_{I^{c}}),e_{I}\right\rangle
and
\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\left\langle T(te_{J^{c}}),e_{J}\right\rangle
exist. From this and the last two inequalities, the conclusion follows.
∎

The next theorem is equivalent to
[
3
, Theorem 4.2]
.

Theorem 5.3
.

Let
T:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
be topical. Then
T+u
has an additive eigenvector for all
u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
if and only if for every pair of nonempty, disjoint sets
I,J\subset N
either

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\left\langle T(-te_{I^{c}}),e_{I}\right\rangle=-\infty\text{ or }\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\left\langle T(te_{J^{c}}),e_{J}\right\rangle=+\infty.

Proof.

The functions
T+u
have an additive eigenvector for every
u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
if and only if the corresponding normalized map
f:V_{0}\rightarrow V_{0}
defined by
f(x)=T(x)-\tfrac{1}{n}\left\langle T(x),e_{N}\right\rangle e_{N}
has surjective displacement. By Theorem
4.1
, this happens if and only if (
5.2
) holds for every disjoint pair of nonempty subsets
I,J\subset N
. This, in turn, is equivalent to (
5.3
) holding for each pair
I,J
by Lemma
5.2
.
∎

The condition of Theorem
5.3
only requires computing
2(2^{n}-1)
limits, however one also needs to check that for each nonempty, disjoint pair
I,J\subset N
, one of the limits in (
5.3
) is infinite. There are almost
3^{n}
such pairs. However, a combinatorial approach using hypergraphs reduces the number of computations necessary to check the conditions above to
O(2^{n}n^{2})
. We’ll give a short explanation of the idea here, for more details see
[
3
, Section 4.2.3]
.

A
directed hypergraph
consists of a set of nodes
N
and a collection of directed hyperarcs. A
hyperarc
is an ordered pair
(\mathbf{t},\mathbf{h})
where
\mathbf{t},\mathbf{h}\subseteq N
. Following
[
3
]
, we introduce two directed hypergraphs
\mathcal{H}^{+}_{\infty}(T)
and
\mathcal{H}^{-}_{\infty}(T)
both of which have nodes
N=\{1,\ldots,n\}
. The hyperarcs of
\mathcal{H}^{+}_{\infty}(T)
are the pairs
(J,\{i\})
with
i\notin J
such that

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}T_{i}(te_{J})=+\infty.

The hyperarcs of
\mathcal{H}^{-}_{\infty}(T)
are
(J,\{i\})
with
i\notin J
such that

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}T_{i}(-te_{J})=-\infty.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be any directed hypergraph with nodes
N
. For
I\subseteq N
, we say that
I
is
invariant
in
\mathcal{H}
if there are no hyperarcs from a subset of
I
to a subset of
I^{c}
. Since
T
is order-preserving, if
I
is not invariant in
\mathcal{H}^{-}_{\infty}(T)
or
\mathcal{H}^{+}_{\infty}(T)
, then there must be a
j\in I^{c}
such that
(I,\{j\})
is a hyperarc in the hypergraph. Note that
\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\left\langle T(-te_{I^{c}}),e_{I}\right\rangle=-\infty
if and only if
I^{c}
is not invariant in
\mathcal{H}^{-}_{\infty}(T)
and
\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\left\langle T(te_{J^{c}}),e_{J}\right\rangle=\infty
if and only if
J^{c}
is not invariant in
\mathcal{H}^{+}_{\infty}(T)
.

The
reach
of
I
in a hypergraph
\mathcal{H}
is the smallest invariant subset of
N
that contains
I
.
Using the notion of reach, we can give an improved version of Theorem
5.3
. This next result is described in the comments preceding
[
3
, Theorem 4.6]
.

Theorem 5.4
.

Let
T:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
be topical. Then
T+u
has an additive eigenvector for all
u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
if and only if for every nonempty proper subset
J\subset N
either

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\left\langle T(te_{J^{c}}),e_{J}\right\rangle=+\infty\text{ or }\operatorname{reach}(J,\mathcal{H}^{-}_{\infty}(T))=N.

Proof.

If
\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\left\langle T(te_{J^{c}}),e_{J}\right\rangle
is finite for some nonempty proper subset
J\subset N
and if
I=N\backslash\operatorname{reach}(J,\mathcal{H}^{-}_{\infty}(T))
is nonempty, then
I^{c}=\operatorname{reach}(J,\mathcal{H}^{-}_{\infty}(T))
is invariant in
\mathcal{H}^{-}_{\infty}(T)
so
\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\left\langle T(-te_{I^{c}}),e_{I}\right\rangle>-\infty
. Therefore Theorem
5.3
implies that
T+u
does not have an additive eigenvector for all
u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. On the other hand, if
\operatorname{reach}(J,\mathcal{H}^{-}_{\infty}(T))=N
, then for any nonempty
I
disjoint from
J
, we have
J\subseteq I^{c}
, so
\operatorname{reach}(I^{c},\mathcal{H}^{-}_{\infty}(T))=N
. This means that
I^{c}
is not invariant in
\mathcal{H}^{-}_{\infty}(T)
, so
\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\left\langle T(-te_{I^{c}}),e_{I}\right\rangle=-\infty
for all nonempty
I
disjoint from
J
. Then Theorem
5.3
implies that
T+u
has an additive eigenvector for all
u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
∎

Since the reach of a set in
\mathcal{H}^{-}_{\infty}(T)
can be computed relatively quickly (see
[
3
, Lemma 4.5]
), the condition in Theorem
5.4
can be checked in
O(2^{n}n^{2})
computations including limits as a single computation. See
[
3
, Theorem 4.6]
for details.

Although we chose to state Theorem
5.4
using the hypergraph
\mathcal{H}^{-}_{\infty}(T)
, the following dual result is also true.

T+u
has additive eigenvector for all
u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
if and only if for every nonempty proper subset
I\subset N
either

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\left\langle T(-te_{I^{c}}),e_{I}\right\rangle=-\infty\text{ or }\operatorname{reach}(I,\mathcal{H}^{+}_{\infty}(T))=N.

The proof is essentially the same. It turns out that working with the hypergraph
\mathcal{H}^{-}_{\infty}(T)
, as in Theorem
5.4
, is more convenient for convex topical maps.

5.2.1.
Convex topical maps

If
T:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
is convex in addition to being topical, then we can determine whether or not
T+u
has an additive eigenvector for all
u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
much more quickly. Let
\mathcal{G}_{\infty}(T)
be the directed graph
with nodes
N
and an arc from
i
to
j
if
\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}T_{i}(te_{\{j\}})=\infty
. Note that the arcs in
\mathcal{G}_{\infty}(T)
point in the opposite direction of the corresponding hyperarcs in
\mathcal{H}^{+}_{\infty}(T)
. The graph
\mathcal{G}_{\infty}(T)
was introduced in
[
12
]
and used in
[
3
]
to prove the following theorem. Recall that the nodes of a directed graph can be partitioned into their strongly connected components, and a strongly connected component is called a
final class
if there are no arcs from that component to any other.

Theorem 5.5

(
[
3
, Corollary 4.4]
)
.

Let
T:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
be convex and topical. Then
T+u
has an additive eigenvector for every
u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
if and only if
\mathcal{G}_{\infty}(T)
has a unique final class
C
and
\operatorname{reach}(C,\mathcal{H}^{-}_{\infty}(T))=N
.

Because the strongly connected components of
\mathcal{G}_{\infty}(T)
can be computed in
O(n^{2})
time and
\operatorname{reach}(C,\mathcal{H}^{-}_{\infty}(T))
can also be computed in
O(n^{2})

[
3
, Lemma 4.5]
, the conditions in Theorem
5.5
are much faster to check than the conditions of Theorems
5.3
/
5.4
when
n
is large. See
[
3
, Corollary 4.7]
. The graph
\mathcal{G}_{\infty}(T)
also leads to the following (already known) generalization of
[
12
, Theorem 2]
.

Theorem 5.6
.

Let
T:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
be topical. If
\mathcal{G}_{\infty}(T)
is strongly connected, then
T+u
has an additive eigenvector for every
u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

We will prove both Theorem
5.5
and Theorem
5.6
using the following lemma which relates the graph
\mathcal{G}_{\infty}(T)
with the hypergraphs
\mathcal{H}^{+}_{\infty}(T)
and
\mathcal{H}^{-}_{\infty}(T)
.

Lemma 5.7
.

Let
T:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
be topical. If
I\subset N
is invariant in
\mathcal{H}^{+}_{\infty}(T)
, then
I^{c}
is invariant in
\mathcal{G}_{\infty}(T)
. If
T
is also convex, then for any
J\subset N
that is invariant in
\mathcal{G}_{\infty}(T)
,
J^{c}
is invariant in both
\mathcal{H}^{+}_{\infty}(T)
and
\mathcal{H}^{-}_{\infty}(T)
.

Proof.

If
I\subset N
is invariant in
\mathcal{H}^{+}_{\infty}(T)
, then
\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\left\langle T(te_{I}),e_{I^{c}}\right\rangle<\infty
. Since
T
is order-preserving,

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}T_{j}(te_{\{i\}})\leq\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}T_{j}(te_{I})<\infty

for all
i\in I
and
j\in I^{c}
. Therefore
I^{c}
is invariant in
\mathcal{G}_{\infty}(T)
.

Now suppose that
T
is convex and
J\subset N
is invariant in
\mathcal{G}_{\infty}(T)
. This means that

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}T_{i}(te_{\{j\}})<\infty

for all
i\in J
and
j\in J^{c}
. By the convexity of
T
,

T_{i}\left(\tfrac{t}{|J^{c}|}e_{J^{c}}\right)\leq\tfrac{1}{|J^{c}|}\sum_{j\in J^{c}}T_{i}(te_{\{j\}})

therefore
\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}T_{i}(te_{J^{c}})<\infty
for all
i\in J
. This proves that
J^{c}
is invariant in
\mathcal{H}^{+}_{\infty}(T)
.

Since
T
is additively homogeneous,
T_{i}(-te_{J^{c}})=T_{i}(te_{J})-t
for all
i\in N
. By convexity

T_{i}(0)+\tfrac{1}{2}t=T_{i}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}te_{N}\right)\leq\tfrac{1}{2}(T_{i}(te_{J})+T_{i}(te_{J^{c}})).

Rearranging and doubling the inequality above gives

T_{i}(-te_{J^{c}})=T_{i}(te_{J})-t\geq 2T_{i}(0)-T_{i}(te_{J^{c}}).

We have already shown that
\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}T_{i}(te_{J^{c}})<\infty
when
i\in J
. So we have
\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}T_{i}(-te_{J^{c}})>-\infty

for all
i\in J
. Hence
J^{c}
is invariant in
\mathcal{H}^{-}_{\infty}(T)
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
5.5
.

If
\mathcal{G}_{\infty}(T)
has two different final classes
I
and
J
, then both
I
and
J
are invariant in
\mathcal{G}_{\infty}(T)
so by Lemma
5.7

I^{c}
is invariant in
\mathcal{H}^{-}_{\infty}(T)
and
J^{c}
is invariant in
\mathcal{H}^{+}_{\infty}(T)
. This means that

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\left\langle T(te_{J^{c}}),e_{J}\right\rangle<\infty\text{ and }\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\left\langle T(-te_{I^{c}}),e_{I}\right\rangle>-\infty.

So
T+u
does not have an additive eigenvector for all
u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
by Theorem
5.3
.

Now suppose that
C
is the unique final class of
\mathcal{G}_{\infty}(T)
. Since
C
is invariant in
\mathcal{G}_{\infty}(T)
,
C^{c}
is invariant in
\mathcal{H}^{+}_{\infty}(T)
by Lemma
5.7
. This means that

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\left\langle T(te_{C^{c}}),e_{C}\right\rangle<\infty,

so by Theorem
5.4
it is necessary that
\operatorname{reach}(C,\mathcal{H}^{-}_{\infty}(T))=N
in order for
T+u
to have an additive eigenvector for every
u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

We will now show that
\operatorname{reach}(C,\mathcal{H}^{-}_{\infty}(T))=N
is also sufficient. Let
J
be any nonempty proper subset of
N
such that
\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\left\langle T(te_{J^{c}}),e_{J}\right\rangle<\infty
, i.e.,
J^{c}
is invariant in
\mathcal{H}^{+}_{\infty}(T)
. By Lemma
5.7
,
J
is invariant in
\mathcal{G}_{\infty}(T)
.
Any nonempty set that is invariant in
\mathcal{G}_{\infty}(T)
must contain
C
, so if
\operatorname{reach}(C,\mathcal{H}^{-}_{\infty}(T))=N
, then
\operatorname{reach}(J,\mathcal{H}^{-}_{\infty}(T))=N
as well. Thus
T+u
has an additive eigenvector for all
u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
by Theorem
5.4
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
5.6
.

If
T:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
is topical and
\mathcal{G}_{\infty}(T)
is strongly connected, then Lemma
5.7
implies that there are no nontrivial invariant subsets of
N
in the hypergraph
\mathcal{H}^{+}_{\infty}(T)
. Thus
\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\left\langle T(te_{J^{c}}),e_{J}\right\rangle=\infty
for all nonempty proper subsets
J\subset N
, and therefore
T+u
has an additive eigenvector for all
u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
by Theorem
5.3
.
∎

6.
Uniqueness of fixed points

In this section, we give necessary and sufficient conditions for the uniqueness of fixed points of polyhedral norm nonexpansive maps.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
X
be a finite dimensional real Banach space with a polyhedral norm and suppose that
F
is a proper face of the unit ball
B_{1}
. For any
x\in\operatorname{ri}F
, there is a constant
c<1
such that

\|Rx-ry\|=R-r

whenever
y\in F
and
0\leq r\leq\frac{1}{2}(1-c)R
.

Proof.

Since
x,y\in F
, it follows that
\left\langle x,\nu\right\rangle=\left\langle y,\nu\right\rangle=1
for all
\nu
in the dual face
F^{*}
. Let
c=\max\{\left\langle x,\nu\right\rangle\,:\,\nu\in(\operatorname{ext}B_{1})\backslash F^{*}\}
. Since
x\in\operatorname{ri}F
, it follows that
c<1
. If
0\leq r\leq\frac{1}{2}(1-c)R
, then
cR+r\leq R-r
. Observe that

\left\langle Rx-ry,\nu\right\rangle=R-r

if
\nu\in F^{*}
, and
\left\langle Rx-ry,\nu\right\rangle\leq cR+r
if
\nu\in\operatorname{ext}B_{1}^{*}
and
\nu\notin F^{*}
. Therefore
\|Rx-ry\|=\max_{\nu\in\operatorname{ext}B_{1}^{*}}\left\langle Rx-ry,\nu\right\rangle=R-r
.
∎

Theorem 6.2
.

Let
X
be a finite dimensional real Banach space with a polyhedral norm, let
f:X\rightarrow X
be nonexpansive, and suppose that
f(0)=0
. Then the following are equivalent.

(1)

f
has a nonzero fixed point
u\in X
.

(2)

For some
R>0
, there is an
x
contained in the relative interior of a proper face
F_{R}
of
B_{R}
such that
f(x)\in F_{R}
.

(3)

There is a proper face
G
of the unit ball
B_{1}
and an
\epsilon>0
such that
rG
is invariant under
f
for all
0\leq r\leq\epsilon
.

Proof.

(1)
\Rightarrow
(2). If
u\neq 0
is a fixed point of
f
, let
R=\|u\|
and let
F_{R}
be the face of
B_{R}
such that
u\in\operatorname{ri}F
. Then
f(u)=u\in F_{R}
.

(2)
\Rightarrow
(3). Suppose
x\in\operatorname{ri}F_{R}
and
f(x)\in F_{R}
. Let
G_{R}\subseteq F_{R}
be the face of
B_{R}
such that
f(x)\in\operatorname{ri}G_{R}
. Let
F=\frac{1}{R}F_{R}
and
G=\frac{1}{R}G_{R}
so that
F
and
G
are faces of the unit ball
B_{1}
. Let
G^{*}
be the dual face corresponding to
G
. Choose any
v\in G
. Since
G\subseteq F
, we have
\|x-rv\|=R-r
for all
r>0
sufficiently small by Lemma
6.1
. Then
\|f(x)-f(rv)\|\leq R-r
by nonexpansiveness. Observe that
\left\langle f(x),\nu\right\rangle=\|f(x)\|=R
for all
\nu\in G^{*}
. At the same time,
\left\langle f(rv),\nu\right\rangle\leq\|f(rv)\|\leq r
for all
\nu\in G^{*}
. Therefore, for any
\nu\in G^{*}
we have

R-r\leq\left\langle f(x)-f(rv),\nu\right\rangle\leq\|f(x)-f(rv)\|\leq\|x-rv\|=R-r.

This proves that
\left\langle f(rv),\nu\right\rangle=r
for all
\nu\in G^{*}
and therefore
f(rv)\in rG
. Since
v\in G
was arbitrary, this proves that
rG
is invariant under
f
.

(3)
\Rightarrow
(1). The faces of
B_{1}
are compact and convex. Therefore, if
rG
is invariant for some
r>0
, then it contains a nonzero fixed point of
f
by the Brouwer fixed point theorem.
∎

Corollary 6.3
.

Let
X
be a finite dimensional real Banach space with a polyhedral norm, let
f:X\rightarrow X
be nonexpansive, and suppose
u\in X
is a fixed point of
f
.
For each face
F
of the unit ball
B_{1}
, choose
x_{F}\in\operatorname{ri}F
and
x_{F}^{*}\in\operatorname{ri}F^{*}
. Then
u
is the unique fixed point of
f
if and only if

(6.1)

\left\langle f(u+tx_{F})-u-tx_{F},x_{F}^{*}\right\rangle<0

for all
t>0
and every face
F
of
B_{1}
.

Proof.

We may assume without loss of generality that
u=0
by replacing
f
with the map
x\mapsto f(u+x)-u
. Since
f
is nonexpansive and
f(0)=0
, it follows that
\|f(tx_{F})\|\leq t
for all
t>0
. Therefore
\left\langle f(tx_{F})-tx_{F},x_{F}^{*}\right\rangle\leq 0
. If (
6.1
) fails for any face
F
and
t>0
, then
\left\langle f(tx_{F}),x_{F}^{*}\right\rangle=t
. This means that
f(tx_{F})\in tF
and so
f
has a nonzero fixed point by Theorem
6.2
. Conversely, if (
6.1
) holds for every face
F
and
t>0
, then none of the faces
tF
are invariant under
f
. Therefore Theorem
6.2
implies that
0
is the unique fixed point of
f
.
∎

Note that the functions
t\mapsto\left\langle f(u+tx_{F})-u-tx_{F},x_{F}^{*}\right\rangle
in (
6.1
) are monotone decreasing by Lemma
3.2
. Therefore it suffices to verify that

\lim_{t\rightarrow 0^{+}}\operatorname{sign}(\left\langle f(u+tx_{F})-u-tx_{F},x_{F}^{*}\right\rangle)=-1

for each face
F
in order to confirm that
u
is the unique fixed point of
f
.

For subtopical maps, we have the following criterion for a fixed point to be unique.

Theorem 6.4
.

Let
T:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
be subtopical and suppose that
u
is a fixed point of
T
. Then
u
is the unique fixed point of
T
if and only if for every nonempty
I\subseteq N
,

\left\langle T(u+te_{I})-u,e_{I}\right\rangle-t|I|<0\text{ and }\left\langle T(u-te_{I})-u,e_{I}\right\rangle+t|I|>0

for all
t>0
.

Proof.

We can assume without loss of generality that
u=0
by replacing
T
with the map
x\mapsto T(u+x)-u
which is also subtopical. All subtopical maps are nonexpansive with respect to the
\ell_{\infty}
norm on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We use the same notation as in Example
4.3
for the faces
F_{IJ}
of the
\ell_{\infty}
unit ball
B_{1}
, as well as representative points
x_{IJ}\in\operatorname{ri}F_{IJ}
and
x_{IJ}^{*}\in\operatorname{ri}F_{IJ}^{*}
. In the proof of Theorem
5.1
, we showed that

\displaystyle(|I|+|J|)\langle T(tx_{IJ})

\displaystyle-tx_{IJ},x_{IJ}^{*}\rangle=\left\langle T(tx_{IJ}),x_{IJ}\right\rangle-t(|I|+|J|)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\left\langle T(te_{I}),e_{I}\right\rangle-t|I|\right)-\left(\left\langle T(-te_{J}),e_{J}\right\rangle+t|J|\right)

for all disjoint
I,J\subset N
with
I\cup J\neq\varnothing
. Therefore, (
6.1
) holds for every face
F_{IJ}
(including
F_{\varnothing I}
and
F_{I\varnothing}
) if and only if

\left\langle T(te_{I}),e_{I}\right\rangle-t|I|<0\text{ and }\left\langle T(-te_{I}),e_{I}\right\rangle+t|I|>0

for all nonempty
I\subseteq N
and
t>0
.
∎

We can also use the results of this section to give an alternative proof of
[
3
, Theorem 4.8]
. Combining Corollary
6.3
with (
5.4
) and (
5.5
) we have the following.

Theorem 6.5
.

Let
T:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
be topical. Suppose that
u
is an additive eigenvector of
T
. Then
u
is the unique additive eigenvector of
T
up to an additive constant if and only if for every pair of nonempty disjoint sets
I,J\subset N
, and all
t>0
, either

\left\langle T(u-te_{I^{c}})-u,e_{I}\right\rangle<0\text{ or }\left\langle T(u+te_{J^{c}})-u,e_{J}\right\rangle>0.

As is the case with the condition in Theorem
5.3
, determining whether an additive eigenvector is unique can be done more quickly using a hypergraph approach, particularly when

is convex in addition to topical. See
[
3
, Subsection 4.3]
and
[
1
]
.

7.
Recession maps and semiderivatives

Let
X
be a real Banach space and
f:X\rightarrow X
be nonexpansive. If

f_{\infty}(x)=\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}t^{-1}f(tx)

exists for all
x\in X
, then we call
f_{\infty}
the
recession map
for
f
. Recession maps were introduced in
[
12
]
for topical functions, but the same definition applies more broadly. Recession maps don’t always exist, but when one does it can provide a sufficient condition for
f
to have surjective displacement. The following theorem is based on a similar result for topical maps on
\mathbb{R}^{n}

[
12
, Theorem 13]
. Related infinite dimensional results with additional compactness assumptions can be found in
[
17
, Theorem 2.7]
and
[
26
, Theorem 12]
.

Theorem 7.1
.

Let
X
be a finite dimensional real Banach space and
f:X\rightarrow X
be nonexpansive. If the recession map
f_{\infty}
exists and 0 is the only fixed point of
f_{\infty}
, then
f
has surjective displacement.

Proof.

For each
t>1
, let
g_{t}(x)=t^{-1}f(tx)-x
. Since
t^{-1}f(tx)
is nonexpansive, each of the functions
g_{t}
is 2-Lipschitz. If
f_{\infty}
exists, then the functions
g_{t}
converge pointwise to
f_{\infty}-\mathrm{id}
, and therefore they converge uniformly on the unit sphere
\partial B_{1}
. If
0
is the only fixed point of
f_{\infty}
, then there must be an
\epsilon>0
such that

\inf_{x\in\partial B_{1}}\|f_{\infty}(x)-x\|=\epsilon.

Then for
t
sufficiently large,

\inf_{x\in\partial B_{1}}\|t^{-1}f(tx)-x\|\geq\tfrac{1}{2}\epsilon,

or equivalently,

\|f(tx)-tx\|\geq\tfrac{1}{2}\epsilon t

for all
x\in\partial B_{1}
.
This means that the approximate fixed point sets
F_{\delta}(f)
are bounded for all
\delta>0
, which proves that
f-\mathrm{id}
is onto by Theorem
3.1
.
∎

The condition in Theorem
7.1
is sufficient, but not necessary for
f
to have surjective displacement. For example, the
(\mathbb{R},|\cdot|)
-nonexpansive function

f(x)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }x\leq 1\\
x-\sqrt{x}&\text{ if }x>1\end{cases}

has surjective displacement even though
f_{\infty}(x)=x
for all
x>0
.

Theorem
7.1
fails in infinite dimensions. For example, the right shift operator on
\ell_{2}(\mathbb{N})
from Example
3.10
is linear, so it is its own recession map. Clearly, 0 is the only fixed point of the right shift operator, and yet the right shift operator only has dense, not surjective, displacement on
\ell_{2}(\mathbb{N})
. However, when
X
is a uniformly smooth and strictly convex Banach space, we can prove the following.

Theorem 7.2
.

Let
X
be a real Banach space that is uniformly smooth and strictly convex. Suppose that
f:X\rightarrow X
is nonexpansive and the recession map
f_{\infty}
exists for
f
. If 0 is the unique fixed point of
f_{\infty}
, then
f
has dense displacement.

Proof.

Choose any
x\in X
with
\|x\|=1
. Since
X
is uniformly smooth,
J(x)
contains a single element,
x^{*}
. Since
X
is strictly convex,
x
is the only element in
B_{1}
with
\left\langle x,x^{*}\right\rangle=1
. Therefore,
\left\langle f_{\infty}(x),x^{*}\right\rangle<\left\langle x,x^{*}\right\rangle
. This means that

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}t^{-1}\left\langle f(tx)-tx,x^{*}\right\rangle<0

so

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\left\langle f(tx)-tx,x^{*}\right\rangle=-\infty,

which proves that
f
has dense displacement by Theorem
3.7
.
∎

Another simple observation about recession maps is that they are composable.

Lemma 7.3
.

If
X
is a real Banach space and
f,g:X\rightarrow X
are nonexpansive maps that have recession maps
f_{\infty}
and
g_{\infty}
respectively, then the recession map for
f\circ g
is
f_{\infty}\circ g_{\infty}
.

Proof.

Let
f_{t}(x)=t^{-1}f(tx)
and
g_{t}(x)=t^{-1}g(tx)
. Note that
f_{t}
is nonexpansive for all
t>0
. Therefore

\displaystyle\|t^{-1}f(g(tx))

\displaystyle-f_{\infty}(g_{\infty}(x))\|=\|f_{t}(g_{t}(x))-f_{\infty}(g_{\infty}(x))\|

\displaystyle\leq\|f_{t}(g_{t}(x))-f_{t}(g_{\infty}(x))\|+\|f_{t}(g_{\infty}(x))-f_{\infty}(g_{\infty}(x))\|

\displaystyle\leq\|g_{t}(x)-g_{\infty}(x)\|+\|f_{t}(g_{\infty}(x))-f_{\infty}(g_{\infty}(x))\|.

Since both
\|g_{t}(x)-g_{\infty}(x)\|
and
\|f_{t}(g_{\infty}(x))-f_{\infty}(g_{\infty}(x))\|
approach
0
as
t\rightarrow\infty
, we conclude that
f_{\infty}\circ g_{\infty}
is the recession map for
f\circ g
.
∎

Just like recession maps provide a sufficient condition for surjective displacement, the semiderivative of a nonexpansive map can give a sufficient condition to check uniqueness of fixed points. Nonexpansive maps aren’t always differentiable. When they aren’t, it is sometimes possible to define a semiderivative as follows. Let
X
be a real Banach space, and
f:X\rightarrow X
be a function. For any
u\in X
, the
semiderivative
of
f
at
u
is the function
f^{\prime}_{u}:X\rightarrow X
given by

f^{\prime}_{u}(x)=\lim_{t\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{f(u+tx)-f(u)}{t},

assuming those limits exist for all
x\in X
. Semiderivatives are similar to recession functions. For example, if
0
is a fixed point of
f
, then the semiderivative
f^{\prime}_{0}
and the recession function
f_{\infty}
are both limits of the expression
t^{-1}f(tx)
, one as
t\rightarrow 0^{+}
, the other as
t\rightarrow\infty
. When a semiderivative exists at a fixed point, it can be used to check if the fixed point is unique. The following is a special case of
[
4
, Theorem 6.1]
.

Theorem 7.4
.

Suppose that
X
is a finite dimensional real Banach space,
f:X\rightarrow X
is nonexpansive, and
u\in X
is fixed point of
f
. If the semiderivative
f^{\prime}_{u}
exists and 0 is the unique fixed point of
f^{\prime}_{u}
, then
u
is the unique fixed point of
f
.

Note that semiderivatives are composable. In fact, they obey the chain rule. If
f,g
are nonexpansive maps on a Banach space
X
and if
g
is semidifferentiable at
u
and
f
is semidifferentiable at
g(u)
, then
(f\circ g)^{\prime}_{u}=f_{g(u)}^{\prime}\circ g_{u}^{\prime}
(see
[
4
, Lemma 3.4]
).

7.1.
Homogeneous nonexpansive maps

If
f
is nonexpansive, then both the recession function
f_{\infty}
and all semiderivatives
f^{\prime}_{u}
are nonexpansive, assuming they exist. They are also
homogeneous
, that is,
f_{\infty}(cx)=cf_{\infty}(x)
and
f^{\prime}_{u}(cx)=cf^{\prime}_{u}(x)
for all
c>0
and
x\in X
. Homogeneous nonexpansive maps have some interesting properties that are worth mentioning here. As motivation, note that all of the theorems so far in this section involve determining whether 0 is the unique fixed point of a homogeneous nonexpansive map.

Let
g:X\rightarrow X
be homogeneous and nonexpansive. By continuity,
0
must be a fixed point of
g
. When
X
is finite dimensional, Theorem
7.1
implies that 0 is the only fixed point of
g
if and only if
g
has surjective displacement since
g
is its own recession map. On the other hand, if
0
is not the only fixed point of
g
, then there is a nonzero
u\in X
such that
tu
is a fixed point for all
t>0
. Therefore determining whether
0
is the unique fixed point of a homogeneous nonexpansive map is equivalent to determining whether the fixed point set is bounded. For many finite dimensional real Banach spaces, this question can be answered using a non-deterministic algorithm proposed in
[
21
]
. We will give a brief description of the idea here.

We say that a vector
v\in X

illuminates
a point
w
on the boundary of the unit ball if
\|w+\epsilon v\|<1
for all sufficiently small
\epsilon>0
. Combining
[
21
, Theorem 3.4 and Corollary 4.5]
with the observation above, we have the following result.

Proposition 7.5
.

Let
X
be a finite dimensional real Banach space and
g:X\rightarrow X
be homogeneous and nonexpansive. Then 0 is the unique fixed point of
g
if and only if there are points
w_{1},\ldots,w_{m}\in X
such that every extreme point of
B_{1}
is illuminated by
g(w_{i})-w_{i}
for some
i
. If
X
is smooth, then this happens if and only if
0
is in the interior of the convex hull of
\{g(w_{i})-w_{i}:1\leq i\leq m\}
.

This proposition leads immediately to a simple algorithm to check whether
0
is the unique fixed point of
g
. Randomly choose
w_{i}\in X
and calculate
g(w_{i})-w_{i}
. Repeat until the set
\{g(w_{i})-w_{i}\}
is sufficient to illuminate every extreme point of the unit ball. If this process eventually stops, then
0
is the unique fixed point. For an
n
-dimensional space with a smooth norm, it only requires
n+1
vectors to illuminate every extreme point on the unit ball since a set of vectors
\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{m}\}
illuminates every extreme point exactly when
0\in\operatorname{int}\operatorname{conv}\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{m}\}

[
21
, Lemma 4.3]
. Therefore there is hope that this illumination algorithm would halt fairly quickly for many homogeneous nonexpansive maps. By applying the illumination algorithm to the recession map
f_{\infty}
or a semiderivative
f^{\prime}_{u}
, we can check whether a nonexpansive map has surjective displacement or a unique fixed point.

For polyhedral norm nonexpansive maps, we already have computable necessary and sufficient conditions for surjective displacement in Theorem
4.1
and for uniqueness of fixed points in Corollary
6.3
. In some cases however, the illumination algorithm might be quicker to check. In particular, in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with the
\ell_{1}
-norm, the unit ball only has
2n
extreme points, so the illumination algorithm might only need to check on the order of
2n
limits in order to verify surjective displacement or uniqueness. For other spaces, such as
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with the
\ell_{\infty}
-norm, this algorithm would require at least
2^{n}
limit computations (see
[
21
, Proposition 4.7]
), and therefore would not have much, if any, advantage over the conditions in Theorem
4.1
and Corollary
6.3
.

On polyhedral normed spaces, homogeneous nonexpansive maps have additional structure that is worth mentioning. Let
X
be a finite dimensional real Banach space with a polyhedral norm. Let
g:X\rightarrow X
be homogeneous and nonexpansive. Note that
g(B_{1})\subseteq B_{1}
since
0
is a fixed point of
g
. For any
x\in B_{1}
, we will use the notation
F_{x}
to indicate the closed face of
B_{1}
with
x
in its relative interior. There is a simple partial order that
B_{1}
inherits from its faces. For
x,y\in B_{1}
, we write
x\preceq y
if
F_{x}\subseteq F_{y}
. We will also say that
x
and
y
are
comparable
, and write
x\sim y
, if
x\preceq y
and
y\preceq x
, that is if
x
and
y
are both in the relative interior of the same face of
B_{1}
.

Lemma 7.6
.

Let
X
be a finite dimensional real Banach space with a polyhedral norm. Let
g:X\rightarrow X
be nonexpansive and homogeneous. Then
g
is order-preserving with respect to
\preceq
. That is, if
x,y\in B_{1}
and
F_{x}\subseteq F_{y}
, then
F_{g(x)}\subseteq F_{g(y)}
. Consequently, if
x\sim y
, then
g(x)\sim g(y)
.

Proof.

Suppose that
x\preceq y
for
x,y\in B_{1}
. If
\|g(y)\|<1
, then
g(x)\preceq g(y)
trivially since
F_{g(y)}=B_{1}
. Let us assume therefore that
\|g(y)\|=1
. By Lemma
6.1
, for any
t>0
large enough,

\|ty-x\|=t-1
. Choose any
\nu\in J(g(y))
. To prove that
F_{g(x)}\subseteq F_{g(y)}
, it suffices to prove that
\left\langle g(x),\nu\right\rangle=1
. For
t>0
large enough,

\displaystyle t-\left\langle g(x),\nu\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle tg(y)-g(x),\nu\right\rangle

\displaystyle(\text{since }\nu\in J(g(y)))

\displaystyle=\left\langle g(ty)-g(x),\nu\right\rangle

\displaystyle(\text{homogeneity})

\displaystyle\leq\|g(ty)-g(x)\|

\displaystyle(\text{since }\|\nu\|_{*}=1)

\displaystyle\leq\|tx-y\|=t-1.

\displaystyle(\text{nonexpansiveness})

From this, we conclude that
\left\langle g(x),\nu\right\rangle\geq 1
, which means that
\left\langle g(x),\nu\right\rangle=1
since
\|\nu\|_{*}=1
. Therefore
g(x)\in F_{g(y)}
as claimed.
∎

An immediate consequence of Lemma
7.6
is that homogeneous nonexpansive maps on polyhedral normed spaces induce a well-defined order-preserving mapping on the face lattice of the unit ball as follows: define
\hat{g}(F_{x})
to be
F_{g(x)}
for any face
F_{x}
of
B_{1}
. Then by Theorem
6.2
,
0
is the unique fixed point of
g
if and only if
B_{1}
is the only fixed point of
\hat{g}
. This suggests another possible route to check for surjective displacement or uniqueness of fixed points. Since the face lattice is a finite partially ordered set, one can search the face lattice for a fixed point of
\hat{g}
. In some cases it may be possible to use the order-preserving property of
\hat{g}
to speed up the search.
