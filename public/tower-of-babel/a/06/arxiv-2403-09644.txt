An extension of Wilson’s Theorem.
Gaitanas Konstantinos

Abstract




Abstract:
Let
\mathcal{N}[k]
be the multiset containing the
\binom{n-1}{k}
products of
k
-subsets of
\{1,\ldots,n-1\}
. We show that if
n\geq(2c+3)^{2}
, then

\displaystyle\left((-1)^{c}+\sum_{M\in\mathcal{N}[n-1-c]}M\right)\cdot(c+1)\equiv 0\pmod{n},

if and only if
n=(c+1)p
, where
p
is prime. This provides a combinatorial extension of Wilson’s Theorem, which is the special case where
c=0
.

Keywords:
Wilson’s Theorem, prime numbers.

1
Introduction

The following theorem, known as
Wilson’s Theorem
provides probably the oldest and most famous non-trivial primality criterion:

Theorem 1.1
.

A positive integer
n>1
is prime if and only if

(n-1)!\equiv-1\pmod{n}.

(1)

Proof.

A straightforward computation shows that the result holds true for
n=2
, so we may assume that
n\geq 3
.

If
n
is prime, then
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
is a field. This implies that the numbers
1,2,\ldots,n-1
with the exception of
\pm 1
, can be arranged in disjoint pairs
(x,x^{\prime})
, such that
x\cdot x^{\prime}\equiv 1\pmod{n}
. Thus,
(n-1)!\equiv-1\pmod{n}
, which shows that (
1
) holds true if
n
is prime.

If
n
is composite, there is an integer
d>1
such that
d\mid n
. Thus,
\gcd\left((n-1)!,n\right)\geq d
, which implies that
(n-1)!\equiv-1\pmod{n}
is impossible. Thus, (
1
) does not hold true for composite numbers. This completes the proof.
∎

It is not easy to determine who was the first to give a valid proof since there is evidence that the result was known before the Middle Ages, but it was J. L. Lagrange who provided the first
published
proof. For a historical account of Wilson’s Theorem, we refer the reader to the well-known book
History of the Theory of Numbers

[
1
]
by Leonard Eugene Dickson.

Let
\mathcal{N}=\{1,\ldots,n-1\}
and
\mathcal{N}[k]
be the multiset containing the
\binom{n-1}{k}
products of
k
-subsets of
\mathcal{N}
, that is

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}[k]=\{M:M=\prod_{m_{j_{1}},\ldots,m_{j_{k}}\in\mathcal{N}}m_{j_{1}}\cdots m_{j_{k}}\}.

Since
\mathcal{N}[n-1]=\{(n-1)!\}
, Wilson’s Theorem evidently states that
n>1
is prime, if and only if

\displaystyle\sum_{M\in\mathcal{N}[n-1]}M\equiv-1\pmod{n}.

We would like to investigate whether some similar result holds true if we consider other values of
k
less than
n-1
, namely when
k=n-1-c
. In particular, the primary objective of this paper is to extend Wilson’s Theorem through the following question:

Let
c\geq 1
. What is the behavior of
\displaystyle\sum_{M\in\mathcal{N}[n-1-c]}M\pmod{n}
?

Despite the popularity of Wilson’s Theorem, it seems that there has been no effort to extend it in this direction.

2
Notation and preliminaries

This section is a quick-reference guide to the notation and background information that will be assumed throughout this paper.

Throughout this paper,
p
will always denote a prime number. The largest integer less than or equal to
x
(usually called the
floor
of
x
) will be denoted by
\lfloor x\rfloor
. Below we mention some useful properties of the floor function:

1.

\lfloor x\rfloor>x-1
.

2.

If
k\nmid n
, then
\left\lfloor\frac{n-1}{k}\right\rfloor=\left\lfloor\frac{n}{k}\right\rfloor
.

3.

\left\lfloor\frac{n-1}{k}\right\rfloor\geq\frac{n}{k}-1
, for every
n,k\geq 2
.

We briefly prove (
3
): If
k\mid n
, then
\frac{n}{k}
is a positive integer and
k\nmid n-1
; thus,
\left\lfloor\frac{n-1}{k}\right\rfloor=\frac{n}{k}-1
. On the other hand, if
k\nmid n
, then (using the first two properties)
\left\lfloor\frac{n-1}{k}\right\rfloor=\left\lfloor\frac{n}{k}\right\rfloor>\frac{n}{k}-1
. In any case, property (
3
) holds true.

Let
m\in\mathbb{N}
. We will write
p^{a}\mid\mid m
, if
a
is the exponent of the largest power of
p
that divides
m
. Legendre’s formula states that
p^{\nu_{p}(m!)}\mid\mid m!
, where

\displaystyle\nu_{p}(m!)=\left\lfloor\frac{m}{p}\right\rfloor+\cdots+\left\lfloor\frac{m}{p^{x}}\right\rfloor,\quad p^{x}\leq m<p^{x+1}.

A simple counting argument shows that

\sum_{M\in\mathcal{N}[n-1-c]}M=\sum_{M\in\mathcal{N}[c]}\frac{(n-1)!}{M}.

(2)

If we denote by

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{1}[c]=\{M\in\mathcal{N}[c]:M=M^{\prime}\cdot\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\mid m\\
m<n\end{subarray}}m,\quad M^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}\}

the multiset which contains all
M\in\mathcal{N}[c]
which are divisible by all multiples of
p
less than
n
, we may rewrite (
2
) in the following way:

\displaystyle\sum_{M\in\mathcal{N}[n-1-c]}M=\sum_{M\in\mathcal{N}_{1}[c]}\frac{(n-1)!}{M}+\sum_{M\not\in\mathcal{N}_{1}[c]}\frac{(n-1)!}{M}.

Observe that if
M\not\in\mathcal{N}_{1}[c]
, then
\frac{(n-1)!}{M}\equiv 0\pmod{p}
; this is obvious, since there is at least one multiple of
p
less than
n
which divides
(n-1)!
but not
M
. Hence,

\sum_{M\in\mathcal{N}[n-1-c]}M\equiv\sum_{M\in\mathcal{N}_{1}[c]}\frac{(n-1)!}{M}\pmod{p}.

(3)

Note that this holds true, even if
\mathcal{N}_{1}[c]
is empty.
1
1

1

We adopt the convention that the empty sum is zero.

In closing, we will also make use of the following well-known congruence:

(x-1)\cdots(\left(x-(p-1)\right)\equiv x^{p-1}-1\pmod{p},\quad

(4)

which means that the coefficients of the corresponding powers of
x
are congruent modulo
p
. A proof of this result can be found in many mathematical contexts (such as
[
2
]
).

As for other prerequisites, the reader is expected to be familiar only with basic congruence rules and some standard tools from analysis.

3
Some useful Lemmas

We present two lemmas which will be useful for the proof of our main result, Theorem(
4.1
). Both of them are obtained using only basic analysis.

Lemma 3.1
.

For every
n\geq 25
, the following inequality holds:

\displaystyle\frac{15n}{16}>\frac{(\sqrt{n}+1)\ln n}{2\ln 2}.

Proof.

Let
f(x)=\frac{x}{\sqrt{x}+1}-\frac{8\ln x}{15\ln 2},x\geq 25
. It’s derivative is equal to

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(x)=\frac{\frac{\sqrt{x}}{2}+1}{(\sqrt{x}+1)^{2}}-\frac{8}{15\ln 2\cdot x}.

But
8<15\ln 2
, which implies that
-\frac{8}{15\ln 2\cdot x}>-\frac{1}{x}
. Thus,

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(x)>\frac{\frac{\sqrt{x}}{2}+1}{(\sqrt{x}+1)^{2}}-\frac{1}{x}=\frac{\sqrt{x}(x-4)-2}{2x(\sqrt{x}+1)^{2}}.

The denominator is always positive and the numerator is clearly positive since by assumption,
x\geq 25
. This implies that
f(x)
is strictly increasing, thus, if
n\geq 25
, then
f(n)\geq f(25)>0.9
. This implies that
f(n)>0
, hence

\displaystyle\frac{n}{\sqrt{n}+1}>\frac{8\ln n}{15\ln 2}\Leftrightarrow\frac{15n}{16}>\frac{(\sqrt{n}+1)\ln n}{2\ln 2}.

∎

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
3\leq p\leq\sqrt{n}
. Then

\displaystyle\left(\frac{n}{p}-1\right)\frac{\ln p}{\ln n}\geq\frac{\sqrt{n}-1}{2}.

Proof.

By assumption,
p\leq\sqrt{n}
, which implies that
\ln p\leq\frac{1}{2}\ln n
. With a little patience we can see that this is equivalent to

\displaystyle\left(\frac{n}{p}-1\right)\frac{\ln p}{\ln n}\geq\frac{n\ln p}{p\ln n}-\frac{1}{2},

therefore it suffices to prove that
\frac{n\ln p}{p\ln n}\geq\frac{\sqrt{n}}{2}
. We observe that the function

\displaystyle f(x)=\frac{\ln x}{x}

is strictly decreasing for
x\geq e
, since
f^{\prime}(x)=\frac{1-\ln x}{x^{2}}
. But
e<3\leq p\leq\sqrt{n}
, therefore

\displaystyle f(p)\geq f(\sqrt{n})\Leftrightarrow\frac{\ln p}{p}\geq\frac{\ln n}{2\sqrt{n}}.

Finally, we multiply both sides by
\frac{n}{\ln n}
, to obtain

\displaystyle\frac{n\ln p}{p\ln n}\geq\frac{n}{\ln n}\cdot\frac{\ln n}{2\sqrt{n}}=\frac{\sqrt{n}}{2}.

This completes the proof.
∎

4
Main results

In this section we state our main results and provide some key proof techniques and insights. We begin with the following:

Theorem 4.1
.

Suppose that
n\geq(2c+3)^{2}
and
n
is not of the form
(c+1)p
, where
p
is prime. Then

\sum_{M\in\mathcal{N}[n-1-c]}M\equiv 0\pmod{n}.

(5)

Proof.

The main idea of the proof is to show that for every prime power
p^{a}
with
p^{a}\mid\mid n
, then
p^{a}\mid M
, for every
M\in\mathcal{N}[n-1-c]
.
With the aim of doing this, suppose that
p
is a prime divisor of
n
, where

\displaystyle n=kp^{a},\quad p^{a}\mid\mid n\quad\text{and}\quad p^{x}\leq n<p^{x+1}.

It is important to note that
n=kp^{a}
and
n\neq(c+1)p
, imply that either
a\geq 2
or
k\neq c+1
; thus, it suffices to consider the following cases:

Case 1
.

a\geq 2
.

Recall that (
2
) states

\displaystyle\sum_{M\in\mathcal{N}[n-1-c]}M=\sum_{M\in\mathcal{N}[c]}\frac{(n-1)!}{M}.

Every
M\in\mathcal{N}[c]
is a product of
c
positive integers less than
n
. Thus, the largest power of
p
that divides
M
is at most
p^{cx}
. Since
\nu_{p}\left((n-1)!\right)
is the exponent of the largest power of
p
that divides
(n-1)!
, it will be enough to show that

\nu_{p}\left((n-1)!\right)-cx\geq a.

(6)

Let
p\geq 3
. The assumption
a\geq 2
implies
p\leq\sqrt{n}
, hence, applying Lemma (
3.2
) we get

\displaystyle\left(\frac{n}{p}-1\right)\frac{\ln p}{\ln n}\geq\frac{\sqrt{n}-1}{2}\geq c+1.

The last part of the inequality comes from the fact that
n\geq(2c+3)^{2}
. It follows that

\frac{n}{p}-1\geq(c+1)\frac{\ln n}{\ln p}\geq(c+1)x,

(7)

because by assumption,
p^{x}\leq n
which implies
x\leq\frac{\ln{n}}{\ln{p}}
. Legendre’s formula yields

\displaystyle\nu_{p}\left((n-1)!\right)\geq\left\lfloor\frac{n-1}{p}\right\rfloor=\frac{n}{p}-1,

since
p\mid n
. From (
7
) and the previous inequality it follows that
\nu_{p}\left((n-1)!\right)\geq(c+1)x
, which implies that
\nu_{p}\left((n-1)!\right)-cx\geq x\geq a
. Therefore, (
6
) is proved.

If
p=2
, we can prove (
6
) fairly easily (even if
a<2
): From Legendre’s formula and property (
3
),

\nu_{2}\left((n-1)!\right)=\left\lfloor\frac{n-1}{2}\right\rfloor+\cdots+\left\lfloor\frac{n-1}{2^{x}}\right\rfloor\geq\frac{n}{2}-1+\cdots+\frac{n}{2^{x}}-1=n\cdot\frac{2^{x}-1}{2^{x}}-x.

(8)

It is easy to check that
n\geq(2c+3)^{2}
and
c\geq 1
imply that
n\geq 25
. By assumption,
2^{x}\leq n<2^{x+1}
, thus
x\geq 4
and consequently,
\frac{2^{x}-1}{2^{x}}\geq\frac{15}{16}
. Therefore, (
8
) yields

\nu_{2}\left((n-1)!\right)\geq\frac{15n}{16}-x.

(9)

Moreover,
n\geq(2c+3)^{2}
and
2^{x}\leq n
are equivalent to
c+2\leq\frac{\sqrt{n}+1}{2}
and
x\leq\frac{\ln x}{\ln 2}
, respectively. Thus, from Lemma (
3.1
) we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{15n}{16}>\frac{(\sqrt{n}+1)\ln n}{2\ln 2}\geq(c+2)\frac{\ln n}{\ln 2}\geq(c+2)x,

which implies that
\frac{15n}{16}-x-cx\geq x
. From (
9
), it follows that
\nu_{2}\left((n-1)!\right)-cx\geq x\geq a
, which completes the proof of the first case.

Case 2
.

a=1,k\neq c+1
.

Recall that from (
3
),

\displaystyle\sum_{M\in\mathcal{N}[n-1-c]}M\equiv\sum_{M\in\mathcal{N}_{1}[c]}\frac{(n-1)!}{M}\pmod{p}.

Since
a=1
, then
n=kp
. Thus, there are
\left\lfloor\frac{n-1}{p}\right\rfloor=k-1
multiples of
p
less than
n
.

If
k>c+1
, then
k-1>c
. Thus, by definition,
\mathcal{N}_{1}[c]=\emptyset
. Hence,

\displaystyle\sum_{M\in\mathcal{N}[n-1-c]}M\equiv 0\pmod{p}.

If
k<c+1
, then
k-1<c
. For every
M\in\mathcal{N}_{1}[c]
,
\frac{(n-1)!}{M}
is not divisible by
p
, since the
k-1
multiples of
p
are canceled from the numerator and the denominator. In particular, if

\displaystyle(n-1)!_{p}=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\nmid m\\
m<n\end{subarray}}m,

then

\sum_{M\in\mathcal{N}_{1}[c]}\frac{(n-1)!}{M}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}M\in\mathcal{N}[c-(k-1)]\\
p\nmid M\end{subarray}}\frac{(n-1)!_{p}}{M}.

(10)

A simple counting argument shows that there are
k(p-1)
integers less than
n
, not divisible by
p
. Thus, the right-hand side of (
10
), is equal to the sum of the products of these numbers, taken
k(p-1)-(c-(k-1))
at a time, which is the coefficient of
x^{c-(k-1)}
in the polynomial

\displaystyle P(x)=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\leq n-1\\
p\nmid m\end{subarray}}(x-m)=\prod_{j=0}^{k-1}\left(x-(jp+1)\right)\cdots\left(x-(jp+p-1)\right).

But

\displaystyle\left(x-(jp+1)\right)\cdots\left(x-(jp+p-1)\right)\equiv(x-1)\cdots(\left(x-(p-1)\right)\pmod{p},

thus, (
2
), yields

\displaystyle P(x)\equiv(x^{p-1}-1)^{k}=\sum_{i=0}^{k}\binom{k}{i}(-1)^{i}x^{(p-1)(k-i)}\pmod{p}.

Therefore, in order to show that
\displaystyle\sum_{M\in\mathcal{N}_{1}[c]}\frac{(n-1)!}{M}\equiv 0\pmod{p}
, it suffices to show that the coefficient of
x^{c-(k-1)}
vanishes. Since the only coefficients which are (possibly) non-zero modulo
p
correspond to the power
x^{(p-1)(k-i)}
, it suffices to show that
c-(k-1)\neq(p-1)(k-i)
.

Aiming for a contradiction, we suppose that
c-(k-1)=(k-i)(p-1)
. This implies that
p-1\mid c-k+1
, hence,
p-1\leq c-k+1
. But
k\geq 1
, thus
p\leq c+1
. We combine this with the hypothesis that
k<c+1
and
n\geq(2c+3)^{2}
, to conclude

\displaystyle(2c+3)^{2}\leq n=kp<(c+1)^{2}

which is absurd since
c\geq 1
. The proof is complete.
∎

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
n=(c+1)p
. Then

\displaystyle\left((-1)^{c}+\sum_{M\in\mathcal{N}[n-1-c]}M\right)\cdot(c+1)\equiv 0\pmod{n}.

Proof.

It suffices to prove that

\displaystyle(-1)^{c}+\sum_{M\in\mathcal{N}[n-1-c]}M\equiv 0\pmod{p}.

If
n=(c+1)p
, there are exactly
c
multiples of
p
less than
n
, which implies that
\mathcal{N}_{1}[c]
contains only one element. In particular,
\mathcal{N}_{1}[c]=\{p\cdot 2p\cdots cp\}
and

\displaystyle\sum_{M\in\mathcal{N}_{1}[c]}\frac{(n-1)!}{M}=(n-1)!_{p}.

Using Wilson’s theorem we obtain

\displaystyle(n-1)!_{p}=\prod_{j=0}^{c}(jp+1)\cdots(jp+p-1)\equiv\left((p-1)!\right)^{c+1}\equiv(-1)^{c+1}\pmod{p}.

Thus,

\displaystyle\sum_{M\in\mathcal{N}[n-1-c]}M\equiv(-1)^{c+1}\pmod{p}.

Consequently,

\displaystyle\left((-1)^{c}+\sum_{M\in\mathcal{N}[n-1-c]}M\right)\cdot(c+1)\equiv 0\pmod{n},

which completes the proof.
∎

In this way, we obtain the following corollary:

Corollary 4.3
.

If
n\geq(2c+3)^{2}
, then

\displaystyle\left((-1)^{c}+\sum_{M\in\mathcal{N}[n-1-c]}M\right)\cdot(c+1)\equiv 0\pmod{n},

if and only if
n=(c+1)p
, where
p
is prime.

Proof.

If
c=0
, the claim is equivalent to Wilson’s Theorem. Therefore, we assume that
c\geq 1
and it suffices to show that the above congruence does not hold if
n\neq(c+1)p
. We observe that if
n\neq(c+1)p
, Theorem (
4.1
) implies that

\displaystyle\left((-1)^{c}+\sum_{M\in\mathcal{N}[n-1-c]}M\right)\cdot(c+1)\equiv(-1)^{c}\cdot(c+1)\pmod{p^{a}},

for every
p^{a}\mid\mid n
. This is equivalent to
(-1)^{c}\cdot(c+1)\equiv 0\pmod{n}
and consequently,
n\leq c+1
. This is absurd, since
n\geq(2c+3)^{2}
. This completes the proof and the paper.
∎
