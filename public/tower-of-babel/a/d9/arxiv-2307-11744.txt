Optimal importance sampling for overdamped Langevin dynamics
M. Chak, T. Lelièvre, G. Stoltz, U. Vaes

Abstract
Calculating averages with respect to multimodal probability distributions is often necessary in applications. Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods to this end, which are based on time averages along a realization of a Markov process ergodic with respect to the target probability distribution, are usually plagued by a large variance due to the metastability of the process. In this work, we mathematically analyze an importance sampling approach for MCMC methods that rely on the overdamped Langevin dynamics. Specifically, we study an estimator based on an ergodic average along a realization of an overdamped Langevin process for a modified potential. The estimator we consider incorporates a reweighting term in order to rectify the bias that would otherwise be introduced by this modification of the potential. We obtain an explicit expression in dimension 1 for the biasing potential that minimizes the asymptotic variance of the estimator for a given observable, and propose a general numerical approach for approximating the optimal potential in the multi-dimensional setting. We also investigate an alternative approach where, instead of the asymptotic variance for a given observable, a weighted average of the asymptotic variances corresponding to a class of observables is minimized. Finally, we demonstrate the capabilities of the proposed method by means of numerical experiments.

Context

In many applications ranging from Bayesian inference to statistical physics and computational biology,

Asymptotic variance

In the whole

2
,
we suppose that

2.3
Explicit optimal potential

In order to prepare the proof of the main result of this section,

Proposition

4
,
we first give a preparatory lemma.
We introduce the functional
\widehat{s}^{2}_{f}\colon\mathcal{U}\to\real\cup\{\infty\}
given by

\widehat{s}^{2}_{f}[U]=\frac{Z[U]}{Z^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{D}^{d}}\lvert f-I\rvert^{2}\mathrm{e}^{U-V}.

(12)

Given the convention that
0\cdot+\infty=+\infty\cdot 0=0
,
the quantity
\widehat{s}^{2}_{f}[U]
is well defined as an element of

.
Note that
\widehat{s}_{f}^{2}[U]
coincides with the asymptotic variance

if and only if

Lemma 2
.

It holds that

\min_{U\in\mathcal{U}}\widehat{s}^{2}_{f}[U]=\frac{1}{Z^{2}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{D}^{d}}\lvert f-I\rvert\,\mathrm{d}\mu\right)^{2}=:s^{*}_{f}.

In addition, the minimum is achieved for

U=U_{*}^{\rm iid}:=-\log\lvert f-I\rvert\in\mathcal{U},

(14)

with the convention that
\log(0)=-\infty

Proof.

We first prove that
\widehat{s}^{2}_{f}[U]\geqslant s^{*}_{f}
for all

.
If
\widehat{s}^{2}_{f}[U]=\infty
,
then this inequality is clear, so we assume from now on that

Using the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality,

The statement that the infimum is achieved for

Note that

.

We divide the proof into three parts,

First item

The idea here is to construct an importance distribution

denote the mollifier

\varrho_{\varepsilon}(z)=\varepsilon^{-1}\varrho\bigl(\varepsilon^{-1}z\bigr),\qquad\varrho(z)=\begin{cases}k\exp\left(-\frac{1}{1-\lvert z\rvert^{2}}\right)&\text{if }\lvert z\rvert\leqslant 1,\\
0&\text{if }\lvert z\rvert>1,\end{cases}

(16)

with
k>0
such that
\int\varrho(z)\,\mathrm{d}z=1
.
Let us introduce the smooth regularization of the absolute value function given by
\lvert z\rvert_{\varepsilon}=\varrho_{\varepsilon}\star{\rm abs}(z)
,
where
{\rm abs}(z)=|z|
is the absolute value function.
Notice that
\lvert z\rvert_{\varepsilon}=\lvert z\rvert
for
\lvert z\rvert\geqslant\varepsilon
and that

\forall z\in\real,\qquad 0\leqslant|z|_{\varepsilon}-\lvert z\rvert\leqslant|0|_{\varepsilon}\leqslant\varepsilon.

The first inequality follows from the convexity of the absolute value function,
and the second inequality comes from an application of the reverse triangle inequality:

|z|_{\varepsilon}-|z|=\int\left(\lvert z-y\rvert-\lvert z\rvert\right)\varrho_{\varepsilon}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\leqslant\int\lvert y\rvert\,\varrho_{\varepsilon}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y=|0|_{\varepsilon}.

Moreover,
let
U_{\varepsilon}\colon\mathbb{D}\to\real
be the smooth biasing potential given by

U_{\varepsilon}(x)=\begin{cases}\chi_{\varepsilon}(x)\Bigl(-\log\bigl\lvert f-I\bigr\rvert_{\varepsilon}\Bigr)&\textrm{if }\mathbb{D}=\real,\\
-\log\bigl\lvert f-I\bigr\rvert_{\varepsilon}&\textrm{if }\mathbb{D}=\mathbb{T},\end{cases}

where
\chi_{\varepsilon}=\varrho\star\mathds{1}_{[-\varepsilon^{-1},\varepsilon^{-1}]}
.
Since
\lvert z\rvert_{\varepsilon}\geqslant\lvert 0\rvert_{\varepsilon}>0
for all
z\in\real
,
the function
U_{\varepsilon}
,
which is a regularization of (
14
),
is well-defined everywhere and uniformly bounded from above.
The choice of
U_{*}^{\rm iid}
as the function we regularize is natural in view of
Lemma

2

and the fact that the inequality in (
15
) is an equality for
U=U_{*}^{\rm iid}
.

We now show that
s^{2}_{f}[U_{\varepsilon}]
converges to the lower bound in (
15
) in the limit as
\varepsilon\to 0
.
We focus in this proof on the case where
\mathbb{D}=\real
,
but the reasoning applies verbatim to the case where
\mathbb{D}=\mathbb{T}
.
Using (
11
), we have that

s^{2}_{f}[U_{\varepsilon}]=\frac{Z[U_{\varepsilon}]}{Z^{2}}\int_{\real{}^{d}}\left(\frac{\lvert f-I\rvert}{\lvert f-I\rvert_{\varepsilon}^{\chi_{\varepsilon}}}\right)^{2}\lvert f-I\rvert_{\varepsilon}^{\chi_{\varepsilon}}\mathrm{e}^{-V}.

Since
0\leqslant\chi_{\varepsilon}(x)\leqslant 1
for all
x\in\mathbb{D}^{d}
,
it holds for all
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
that

\left\{\begin{aligned} \lvert f-I\rvert_{\varepsilon}^{\chi_{\varepsilon}}&\leqslant\left(\lvert f-I\rvert+1\right)^{\chi_{\varepsilon}}\leqslant\lvert f-I\rvert+1\\
\frac{\lvert f-I\rvert^{2}}{\lvert f-I\rvert_{\varepsilon}^{\chi_{\varepsilon}}}&\leqslant\lvert f-I\rvert^{2-\chi_{\varepsilon}}\leqslant\bigl(\lvert f-I\rvert+1\bigr)^{2-\chi_{\varepsilon}}\leqslant\bigl(\lvert f-I\rvert+1\bigr)^{2}.\end{aligned}\right.

Since
f\in L^{2}\left(\mathrm{e}^{-V}\right)
by assumption,
the right-hand sides of these inequalities are integrable.
Therefore, using dominated convergence,
we obtain that

Z[U_{\varepsilon}]=\int_{\real{}^{d}}\lvert f-I\rvert_{\varepsilon}^{\chi_{\varepsilon}}\mathrm{e}^{-V}\xrightarrow[\varepsilon\to 0]{}\int_{\real{}^{d}}\lvert f-I\rvert\mathrm{e}^{-V}

and

\int_{\real{}^{d}}\left(\frac{\lvert f-I\rvert}{\lvert f-I\rvert_{\varepsilon}^{\chi_{\varepsilon}}}\right)^{2}\lvert f-I\rvert_{\varepsilon}^{\chi_{\varepsilon}}\mathrm{e}^{-V}=\int_{\real{}^{d}}\frac{\lvert f-I\rvert^{2}}{\lvert f-I\rvert_{\varepsilon}^{\chi_{\varepsilon}}}\mathrm{e}^{-V}\xrightarrow[\varepsilon\to 0]{}\int_{\real{}^{d}}\lvert f-I\rvert\mathrm{e}^{-V}.

and so
s^{2}_{f}[U_{\varepsilon}]\to s^{*}_{f}
in the limit as
\varepsilon\to 0
.
Since
s^{2}_{f}[U]\geqslant s^{*}_{f}
for all
U\in C^{\infty}_{\rm c}(\real^{d})
by (
15
),
we deduce the statement.
∎

3.1
Mathematical setting

We will use the following functional spaces:

L^{2}_{0}(\mu_{U})=\left\{\varphi\in L^{2}(\mu_{U})\,\middle|\,\int_{\mathbb{D}^{d}}\varphi\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{U}=0\right\},\qquad H^{1}(\mu_{U})=\left\{\varphi\in L^{2}(\mu_{U})\,\middle|\,\nabla\varphi\in\bigl(L^{2}(\mu_{U})\bigr)^{d}\right\},

In the i.i.d. setting, the condition

There does not exist such a simple condition in the MCMC setting,

Specifically, we denote by

Assumption 7
.

•

The function

is smooth on
\mathbb{D}^{d}
.

•

The function

is Lebesgue integrable over
\mathbb{D}^{d}
.

•

The probability measure

satisfies a Poincaré inequality:
there exists

such that

\forall\varphi\in H^{1}(\mu_{U})\cap L^{2}_{0}(\mu_{U}),\qquad\lVert\varphi\rVert_{L^{2}(\mu_{U})}^{2}\leqslant\frac{1}{R[U]}\lVert\nabla\varphi\rVert_{L^{2}(\mu_{U})}^{2}.

(18)

•

It holds that
(f-I)\,\mathrm{e}^{U}\in L^{2}(\mu_{U})
.

•

For any
x_{0}\in\mathbb{D}^{d}
, there exists a unique strong solution
X_{t}
to (
6
) with
X_{0}=x_{0}
.

In this section, the functions
V
and
f
are considered fixed data of the problem,
so the dependence of
\mathfrak{U}_{0}
on these data is omitted in the notation.
However, in
Section

4
we will write

to emphasize this dependence where necessary.
Just like the set

in

,
the set

in this section contains “nice” biasing potentials.
Indeed, the asymptotic behavior of the estimator (

∎

3.2
Asymptotic variance

The following lemma gives an expression of the asymptotic variance of the estimator
\mu^{T}_{U}(f)
given in (
5
)
in terms of the solution to a Poisson equation.

Lemma 11

(Asymptotic variance)
.

Suppose that
U\in\mathfrak{U}_{0}
.
Then there exists a unique distributional solution
\phi_{U}\in H^{1}(\mu_{U})\cap L^{2}_{0}(\mu_{U})
to

-\mathcal{L}_{U}\phi_{U}=(f-I)\mathrm{e}^{U}.

(20)

The solution
\phi_{U}
is smooth and,
for Lebesgue almost all initial condition,
it holds that

\sqrt{T}\bigl(\mu^{T}_{U}(f)-I\bigr)\xrightarrow[T\to\infty]{\rm Law}\mathcal{N}\bigl(0,\sigma^{2}_{f}[U]\bigr),

where

\sigma^{2}_{f}[U]:=\frac{2Z[U]^{2}}{Z^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{D}^{d}}\left\lvert\nabla\phi_{U}\right\rvert^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu_{U}=\frac{2Z[U]^{2}}{Z^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{D}^{d}}\phi_{U}(f-I)\,\mathrm{e}^{U}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{U}.

(21)

Proof.

By density of
C^{\infty}_{\rm c}(\mathbb{D}^{d})
in
H^{1}(\mu_{U})
,
a function
\phi_{U}\in H^{1}(\mu_{U})\cap L^{2}_{0}(\mu_{U})
is a distributional solution to (
20
) if and only if

\forall\varphi\in H^{1}(\mu_{U})\cap L^{2}_{0}(\mu_{U}),\qquad\int_{\mathbb{D}^{d}}\nabla\phi_{U}\cdot\nabla\varphi\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{U}=\int_{\mathbb{D}^{d}}(f-I)\mathrm{e}^{U}\,\varphi\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{U}.

(22)

The validity of a Poincaré inequality for
\mu_{U}
implies that
the function space
H^{1}(\mu_{U})\cap L^{2}_{0}(\mu_{U})
endowed with the inner product

(\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2})\mapsto\int_{\mathbb{D}^{d}}\nabla\varphi_{1}\cdot\nabla\varphi_{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{U}

is a Hilbert space,
and that the right-hand side of (
22
) is a bounded linear functional on this space.
Therefore, the Lax–Milgram theorem (or the Riesz representation theorem)
yields the existence of a unique solution
\phi_{U}
in
H^{1}(\mu_{U})\cap L^{2}_{0}(\mu_{U})
.
Elliptic regularity theory
[
20
,
18
]

then implies that
\phi_{U}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{D}^{d})
.
From the definition (
5
) of
\mu^{T}_{U}(f)
,
we have

\sqrt{T}\bigl(\mu^{T}_{U}(f)-I\bigr)=\frac{\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\int_{0}^{T}\left((f-I)\mathrm{e}^{U}\right)(X_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t}{\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\left(\mathrm{e}^{U}\right)({X}_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t}.

The numerator converges in law to
\mathcal{N}\bigl(0,2\int_{\mathbb{D}^{d}}\left\lvert\nabla\phi_{U}\right\rvert^{2}d\mu_{U}\bigr)
,
for instance by
[
10
, Theorem 3.1]

(the setting there is
d
,
but for
\mathbb{T}^{d}
, the argument using (
3
) and the martingale central limit theorem,
that is essentially
[
24
, Theorem VIII.3.11]
,
works in the same way),
while the denominator converges almost surely to
Z/Z[U]
.
The claimed convergence in law then follows from Slutsky’s lemma.
The last equality in (
21
) follows from the definition (
22
) of a weak solution.
∎

\lVert\nabla\phi_{U}\rVert_{L^{2}(\mu_{U})}\leqslant\frac{1}{\sqrt{R[U]}}\left\lVert(f-I)\mathrm{e}^{U}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mu_{U})}.

(23)

Remark 13

It is instructive to write the counterpart of

For this estimator,

Lemma 14

(Explicit expression for the asymptotic variance in dimension 1)
.

For
U\in\mathfrak{U}_{0}
and in dimension

the asymptotic variance (

) writes

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}_{f}[U]

\displaystyle=\frac{2Z[U]^{2}}{Z^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{D}}\left\lvert\bigl(F-A_{\mathbb{D}}[U]\bigr)\,\mathrm{e}^{V+U}\right\rvert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{U},

(28)

where

is given by

F(x)=\int_{0}^{x}\bigl(f(\xi)-I\bigr)\mathrm{e}^{-V(\xi)}\mathrm{d}\xi,

and

A_{\mathbb{D}}[U]=\begin{cases}\displaystyle-\int_{-\infty}^{0}\bigl(f-I\bigr)\mathrm{e}^{-V}&\text{ if $\mathbb{D}=\real$, }\\[11.38092pt]
\displaystyle\frac{\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}}F\,\mathrm{e}^{V+U}}{\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}}\mathrm{e}^{V+U}}&\text{ if $\mathbb{D}=\mathbb{T}$. }\end{cases}

(29)

Note that
A[U]
is independent of
U
;
we shall henceforth drop the dependence in the notation.
It seems from (

and

have very different expressions.
In fact, the constant

may be obtained as a limit of
A_{\mathbb{T}}[U]
for an increasingly large torus;

Proof.

In dimension one, the Poisson equation (
20
) reads

-\mathrm{e}^{V+U}\left(\mathrm{e}^{-(V+U)}\phi_{U}^{\prime}\right)^{\prime}=(f-I)\mathrm{e}^{U}.

(30)

By integration of (

),
it holds that

\phi_{U}^{\prime}(x)=-\left(\int_{0}^{x}\bigl(f(\xi)-I\bigr)\mathrm{e}^{-V(\xi)}\mathrm{d}\xi-A\right)\mathrm{e}^{(V+U)(x)}=-\bigl(F(x)-A\bigr)\mathrm{e}^{(V+U)(x)},

(31)

and so

\phi_{U}(x)=B-\int_{0}^{x}\bigl(F(\xi)-A\bigr)\mathrm{e}^{(V+U)(\xi)}\,\mathrm{d}\xi,

(32)

for some constants
A\in\real
and
B\in\real
.
The requirement that
\phi_{U}\in H^{1}(\mu_{U})
enables to determine the constant
A
:

•

When
\mathbb{D}=\mathbb{T}
, the embedding

gives that

\phi_{U}(-\pi)=\phi_{U}(\pi)
, which leads to the equation for

in (
29
).

•

When
\mathbb{D}=\real
,
the requirement that
\phi_{U}\in H^{1}(\mu_{U})
implies that

A=\lim_{x\to\infty}F(x),

(33)

where

\lim_{x\to\infty}F(x)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\bigl(f(x)-I\bigr)\mathrm{e}^{-V(x)}\,\mathrm{d}x=-\int_{-\infty}^{0}\bigl(f(x)-I\bigr)\mathrm{e}^{-V(x)}\,\mathrm{d}x,

because otherwise
\phi_{U}^{\prime}
in (

) is not in

.
Indeed, assume for contradiction that

\lim_{x\to\infty}F(x)=:L\neq A.

(The limit exists because

by

.)
Then there is

such that

\inf_{x\geqslant K}\lvert F(x)-A\rvert\geqslant\frac{1}{2}\lvert L-A\rvert,

and so by (

\int\lvert\phi_{U}^{\prime}\rvert^{2}\,\mathrm{e}^{-(V+U)}\,\mathrm{d}x\geqslant\frac{1}{4}\lvert L-A\rvert^{2}\int_{K}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{V+U}\,\mathrm{d}x.

The right-hand side of this equation is infinite because,
by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality,

+\infty=\int_{K}^{\infty}\sqrt{\mathrm{e}^{V+U}}\sqrt{\mathrm{e}^{-V-U}}\,\mathrm{d}x\leqslant\int_{K}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{V+U}\,\mathrm{d}x\int_{K}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-V-U}\,\mathrm{d}x.

Equation (

) in (

).

it contains elements which violate

For biasing potentials not in

the quantity (

In particular,

Example 17
.

Consider the setting where
V\colon\mathbb{T}\to\real
is zero and

f(x)=\begin{cases}\sgn(x)\qquad&\text{if $\lvert x\rvert\geqslant\frac{\pi}{2}$},\\
0\qquad&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases},\qquad\qquad\sgn(x):=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if $x>0$,}\\
0&\text{ if $x=0$,}\\
-1&\text{ if $x<0$.}\\
\end{cases}

(35)

Here we identify
[-\pi,\pi]
with its image under the quotient map
\real\to\mathbb{T}
.
If
U
is a potential such that (i) there exists a unique strong solution to (

and (ii) this solution satisfies

with probability 1 for all times,
then (

However
\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{f}[U]>0

∎

Although
\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{f}
does not in general correspond to an asymptotic variance,
obtaining a bound from below on

over

will be useful in order to motivate the proof of

just like

proved useful for establishing

Lemma 18
.

It holds that

\min_{U\in\mathfrak{U}}\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{f}[U]=\frac{2}{Z^{2}}\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{D}}\bigl\lvert F(x)-A^{*}_{\mathbb{D}}\bigr\rvert\mathrm{d}x\bigg)^{2}=:\sigma^{*}_{f},

(36)

with
A^{*}:=A
and

A^{*}_{\mathbb{T}}:=\sup\left\{A\in\real:\int_{\mathbb{T}}\sgn(F-A)\geqslant 0\right\}.

(37)

In addition, the infimum is achieved for

U=U_{*}(x):=-V(x)-\log\left\lvert F(x)-A^{*}_{\mathbb{D}}\right\rvert\in\mathfrak{U}.

(38)

In this case

\mathrm{e}^{-V-U_{*}}\propto\left\lvert F(x)-A^{*}_{\mathbb{D}}\right\rvert.

Proof.

It is sufficient to show that, for all
A\in\real
,

\frac{2Z[U]}{Z^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{D}}\lvert F-A\rvert^{2}\mathrm{e}^{V+U}\geqslant\frac{2}{Z^{2}}\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{D}}\bigl\lvert F(x)-A^{*}_{\mathbb{D}}\bigr\rvert\,\mathrm{d}x\bigg)^{2}.

(39)

If the left-hand side of (
39
) is infinite,
then the inequality is trivially satisfied.
On the other hand,
if the left-hand side is finite,
in which case the set on which
\lvert F-A\rvert^{2}\mathrm{e}^{V+U}=\infty
is of measure zero,
then we have by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality that

\displaystyle\frac{2Z[U]}{Z^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{D}}\lvert F-A\rvert^{2}\mathrm{e}^{V+U}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{Z^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{D}}\mathrm{e}^{-V-U}\int_{\mathbb{D}}\lvert F-A\rvert^{2}\mathrm{e}^{V+U}\geqslant\frac{2}{Z^{2}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{D}}\lvert F-A\rvert\right)^{2}.

In the case
\mathbb{D}=\real
,
the right-hand side is finite only if
A=A
,
which leads to (
36
).
In the case
\mathbb{D}=\mathbb{T}
,
the inequality (
36
) is obtained by noting that

\int_{\mathbb{D}}\bigl\lvert F-A\bigr\rvert=\mathbb{E}\bigl\lvert F(X)-A\bigr\rvert,

where
X\sim\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{T})
is a random variable uniformly distributed on the torus.
It is well known,
see for example
[
49
, Exercise 1.4.23]
,
that the expectation on the right-hand side is minimized for any
A
that is a median of
F(X)
.
Here
F
is continuous, so the median of
F(X)
is unique and given by
A^{*}_{\mathbb{T}}
,
which implies that (
36
) holds.

The fact that the lower bound is achieved for
U
in (
38
)
follows from the inequality

\displaystyle\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{f}[U_{*}]

\displaystyle=\frac{2Z[U_{*}]}{Z^{2}}\inf_{A\in\real}\int_{\mathbb{D}}\left\lvert F-A\right\rvert^{2}\mathrm{e}^{V+U_{*}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{2Z[U_{*}]}{Z^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{D}}\left\lvert F-A_{\mathbb{D}}^{*}\right\rvert^{2}\mathrm{e}^{V+U_{*}}=\frac{2}{Z^{2}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{D}}\left\lvert F-A_{\mathbb{D}}^{*}\right\rvert\right)^{2},

which concludes the proof.
∎

Remark 19
.

The singularities in the biasing potential
U_{*}
coincide with zeros of the function
F(x)-A^{*}_{\mathbb{D}}
.
Consider for simplicity the case where
\mathbb{D}=\real

If
x_{*}
denotes a zero of the function

In other words,
the average of

with respect to the measure
\mu
restricted to
[-\infty,x_{*}]

coincides with its average with respect to
\mu
over the real line.
When singular, the biasing potential (
38
) effectively divides the domain into regions that suffice for the estimation of

.
Several numerical experiments illustrating this behavior are presented in
Section

5
.
∎

Proposition 21
.

Suppose that
0\in\mathfrak{U}_{0}
.
Then,

•

It holds that

\inf_{U\in\mathfrak{U}_{0}}\sigma^{2}_{f}[U]=\sigma^{*}_{f}.

•

It holds that

\inf_{U\in C^{\infty}_{\rm c}(\mathbb{D})}\sigma^{2}_{f}[U]=\sigma^{*}_{f}.

Proof.

Since
\sigma^{2}_{f}[U]=\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{f}[U]
for
U\in\mathfrak{U}_{0}
and
C^{\infty}_{\rm c}(\mathbb{D}^{d})\subset\mathfrak{U}_{0}
,

Lemma

18
and the second statement of
Proposition

21
imply the first statement.
In order to prove the second statement,
we use the same notation in this proof as in
Proposition

4
.
Let
U_{\varepsilon}\colon\mathbb{D}\to\real
be the smooth biasing potential given by

U_{\varepsilon}(x)=\begin{cases}-\chi_{\varepsilon}(x)\Bigl(V(x)+\log\bigl\lvert F(x)-A^{*}\bigr\rvert_{\varepsilon}\Bigr)&\textrm{if }\mathbb{D}=\real,\\
-V(x)-\log\bigl\lvert F(x)-A^{*}_{\mathbb{T}}\bigr\rvert_{\varepsilon}&\textrm{if }\mathbb{D}=\mathbb{T},\end{cases}

where for
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
,
\chi_{\varepsilon}=\varrho\star\mathds{1}_{[-\ell_{\varepsilon},\ell_{\varepsilon}]}
with
\ell_{\varepsilon}=\varepsilon^{-1/2}-1
.
(This choice of
\ell_{\varepsilon}
enables to write the bound in (
42

The probability distribution
\mu_{U_{\varepsilon}}
,
with density proportional to
\mathrm{e}^{-V-U_{\varepsilon}}
,
satisfies a Poincaré inequality.
This follows from the fact that
\mathrm{e}^{-V-U_{\varepsilon}}
is uniformly bounded from below when
\mathbb{D}=\mathbb{T}
,
and from the classical Holley–Stroock biasing argument when
\mathbb{D}=\real
;
see
[
23
]
, as for example reviewed in
[
31
, Theorem 2.11]
.
Therefore
U_{\varepsilon}\in\mathfrak{U}_{0}
and so,
by
Lemma

14
,
the associated asymptotic variance is given by

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}_{f}[U_{\varepsilon}]

\displaystyle=\frac{2Z[U_{\varepsilon}]}{Z^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{D}}\bigl(F-A_{\varepsilon}\bigr)^{2}\,\mathrm{e}^{V+U_{\varepsilon}},

(40)

where
A_{\varepsilon}:=A_{\mathbb{D}}[U_{\varepsilon}]
is given by (
29
)
with
U=U_{\varepsilon}
.
We now prove, separately for
\mathbb{D}=\mathbb{T}
and
\mathbb{D}=\real
,
that

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}Z[U_{\varepsilon}]\to\int_{\mathbb{D}}\lvert F-A^{*}_{\mathbb{D}}\rvert\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{\mathbb{D}}\bigl(F-A_{\varepsilon}\bigr)^{2}\,\mathrm{e}^{V+U_{\varepsilon}}\leqslant\int_{\mathbb{D}}\bigl\lvert F(x)-A^{*}_{\mathbb{D}}\bigr\rvert\,\mathrm{d}x.

Given these results,
taking the limit superior as
\varepsilon\to 0
in (
40
) gives

\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\sigma^{2}_{f}[U_{\varepsilon}]\leqslant\frac{2}{Z^{2}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{D}}\bigl\lvert F(x)-A^{*}_{\mathbb{D}}\bigr\rvert\,\mathrm{d}x\right)^{2}.

Since the right-hand side is a lower bound on
\sigma^{2}_{f}[U_{\varepsilon}]=\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{f}[U_{\varepsilon}]
by (
36
),
the result will be proved.

Case
\mathbb{D}=\mathbb{T}
.

In this setting,
it holds by dominated convergence,
with an argument similar to the one used to prove the third item of
Proposition

4
,
that

Z[U_{\varepsilon}]=\int_{\mathbb{T}}\bigl\lvert F-A^{*}_{\mathbb{T}}\bigr\rvert_{\varepsilon}\xrightarrow[\varepsilon\to 0]{}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\bigl\lvert F-A^{*}_{\mathbb{T}}\bigr\rvert.

In addition, since
A_{\varepsilon}
is the average of
F
over
\mathbb{T}
with respect to the probability measure with density proportional to
\mathrm{e}^{V+U_{\varepsilon}}
,
it holds for all
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}}\bigl\lvert F-A_{\varepsilon}\bigr\rvert^{2}\,\mathrm{e}^{V+U_{\varepsilon}}

\displaystyle=\inf_{C\in\real}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\bigl\lvert F-C\bigr\rvert^{2}\,\mathrm{e}^{V+U_{\varepsilon}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\int_{\mathbb{T}}\bigl\lvert F-A_{\mathbb{T}}^{*}\bigr\rvert^{2}\,\mathrm{e}^{V+U_{\varepsilon}}=\int_{\mathbb{T}}\frac{\bigl\lvert F-A_{\mathbb{T}}^{*}\bigr\rvert^{2}}{\bigl\lvert F-A_{\mathbb{T}}^{*}\bigr\rvert_{\varepsilon}}\leqslant\int_{\mathbb{T}}\bigl\lvert F-A_{\mathbb{T}}^{*}\bigr\rvert,

which enables to conclude.

Case
\mathbb{D}=\real
.

The numerator of the fraction on the right-hand side of (
40
) can be written as

\displaystyle Z[U_{\varepsilon}]

\displaystyle=\int\exp\Big(-V(x)+\chi_{\varepsilon}(x)\left(V(x)+\log\bigl\lvert F(x)-A^{*}\bigr\rvert_{\varepsilon}\right)\Big)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int\exp\bigl((\chi_{\varepsilon}-1)V\bigr)\lvert F-A^{*}\rvert_{\varepsilon}^{\chi_{\varepsilon}}.

(41)

By convexity of the exponential function,
it holds that

\displaystyle\exp\bigl((1-\chi_{\varepsilon})(-V)+\chi_{\varepsilon}\log\lvert F-A^{*}\rvert_{\varepsilon}\bigr)

\displaystyle\leqslant(1-\chi_{\varepsilon})\mathrm{e}^{-V}+\chi_{\varepsilon}\lvert F-A^{*}\rvert_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathrm{e}^{-V}+\chi_{\varepsilon}\bigl(\lvert F-A^{*}\rvert+\varepsilon\bigr)

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathrm{e}^{-V}+\lvert F-A^{*}\rvert+\varepsilon\chi_{\varepsilon}.

Since
\varepsilon=(\ell_{\varepsilon}+1)^{-2}
,
all three terms on the right-hand side are dominated by an integrable function over independent of
\varepsilon
,
because

\forall x\in\real,\qquad\varepsilon\chi_{\varepsilon}(x)\leqslant\frac{\mathds{1}_{[-\ell_{\varepsilon}-1,\ell_{\varepsilon}+1]}(x)}{(\ell_{\varepsilon}+1)^{2}}\leqslant\min\left\{1,\frac{1}{x^{2}}\right\}.

(42)

Therefore,
by dominated convergence,
we deduce from (
41
) that
Z[U_{\varepsilon}]\to\int\lvert F-A^{*}\rvert
in the limit as
\varepsilon=(\ell_{\varepsilon}+1)^{-2}\to 0
.
For the integral on the right-hand side of (

noting that

and recalling that this constant is independent of

we have that

\displaystyle\int(F-A_{\varepsilon})^{2}\mathrm{e}^{V+U_{\varepsilon}}

\displaystyle=\int\lvert F-A^{*}\rvert^{2}\exp\Bigl((1-\chi_{\varepsilon})V+\chi_{\varepsilon}\bigl(-\log\lvert F-A^{*}\rvert_{\varepsilon}\bigr)\Bigr)

\displaystyle\leqslant\int\lvert F-A^{*}\rvert^{2}\left((1-\chi_{\varepsilon})\mathrm{e}^{V}+\frac{\chi_{\varepsilon}}{\lvert F-A^{*}\rvert_{\varepsilon}}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\int_{\real\setminus B_{\ell_{\varepsilon}-1}}\lvert F-A^{*}\rvert^{2}\mathrm{e}^{V}+\int\lvert F-A^{*}\rvert,

(43)

where we used the convexity of the exponential function and the notation
B_{\ell_{\varepsilon}-1}=[-\ell_{\varepsilon}+1,\ell_{\varepsilon}-1]

Since

is finite, so is
\int\lvert F-A^{*}\rvert^{2}\mathrm{e}^{V}

implying that the first term on the right-hand side of (

\varepsilon\to 0
.

3.4
Optimal
U
in the multi-dimensional setting via steepest descent

In the multi-dimensional setting,
obtaining an explicit expression for the optimal biasing
U
is not possible.
However, analogously to
[
12
]
, the functional derivative of the asymptotic variance with respect to the biasing potential can be expressed
in terms of the solution to a Poisson equation.
This enables a numerical strategy based on a steepest descent for finding a good biasing potential.

Computation of the functional derivative

In the following,
the directional derivative of a functional
E:C^{\infty}(\mathbb{D}^{d})\rightarrow\real
at
U\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{D}^{d})

in the direction
\delta U\in C^{\infty}_{\rm c}(\mathbb{D}^{d})
is denoted by

\mathrm{d}E[U]\cdot\delta U=\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\bigl(E[U+\varepsilon\delta U]-E[U]\bigr),

(44)

whenever the limit exists.

Theorem 25

(Functional derivative of the asymptotic variance)
.

Suppose that
U\in\mathfrak{U}_{0}
and let
\phi_{U}
be as in
Lemma

11
.
Then for all
\delta U\in C^{\infty}_{\rm c}(\mathbb{D}^{d})
,
it holds that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{d}\sigma^{2}_{f}[U]\cdot\delta U

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2}\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\bigl(\sigma^{2}_{f}[U+\varepsilon\delta U]-\sigma^{2}_{f}[U]\bigr)

\displaystyle=\frac{Z[U]^{2}}{Z^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{D}^{d}}\delta U\bigg(\left\lvert\nabla{\phi_{U}}\right\rvert^{2}-\int_{\mathbb{D}^{d}}\left\lvert\nabla{\phi_{U}}\right\rvert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{U}\bigg)\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{U}.

(45)

Proof.

We first rewrite (
21
) as

\sigma^{2}_{f}[U]=\frac{2Z[U]^{2}}{Z^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{D}^{d}}\phi_{U}(f-I)\mathrm{e}^{U}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{U}=\frac{2Z[U]}{Z^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{D}^{d}}\phi_{U}(f-I)\mathrm{e}^{-V},

so that the only factors depending on
U
are
Z[U]
and
\phi_{U}
.
By definition of the functional derivative,
using (
22
) for the first integral term on the right-hand side,
we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{d}\sigma^{2}_{f}[U]\cdot\delta U

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{d}Z[U]\cdot\delta U}{Z^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{D}^{d}}\left\lvert\nabla{\phi_{U}}\right\rvert^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-V-U}+\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\frac{Z[U]}{\varepsilon Z^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{D}^{d}}(\phi_{U+\varepsilon\delta U}-\phi_{U})(f-I)\mathrm{e}^{-V},

(46)

where
\phi_{U+\varepsilon\delta U}
is the solution to the perturbed Poisson equation

-\mathcal{L}_{U+\varepsilon\delta U}\phi_{U+\varepsilon\delta U}=(f-I)\mathrm{e}^{U+\varepsilon\delta U}.

(47)

The function
(f-I)\mathrm{e}^{U+\varepsilon\delta U}
has zero mean with respect to
\mu_{U+\varepsilon\delta U}
.
By
7
and the fact that
\delta U\in C^{\infty}_{\rm c}(\mathbb{D}^{d})
,
it holds that
(f-I)\mathrm{e}^{U+\varepsilon\delta U}\in L^{2}(\mathrm{e}^{-V-U-\varepsilon\delta U})

and that
\mu_{U+\varepsilon\delta U}
satisfies a Poincaré inequality,
by the Holley–Stroock theorem.
Consequently, there exists a unique solution in
L^{2}_{0}(\mathrm{e}^{-V-U-\varepsilon\delta U})
to (
47
) by
Lemma

11
.
A simple calculation using the fact that
\delta U\in C^{\infty}_{\rm c}(\mathbb{D}^{d})
gives

\mathrm{d}Z[U]\cdot\delta U=-\int_{\mathbb{D}^{d}}\delta U\mathrm{e}^{-V-U}.

(48)

For the second term on the right-hand side of (
46
),
we have by
Lemma

48
that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\mathbb{D}^{d}}(\phi_{U+\varepsilon\delta U}-\phi_{U})(f-I)\mathrm{e}^{-V}=-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\mathbb{D}^{d}}(\phi_{U+\varepsilon\delta U}-\phi_{U})(\mathcal{L}_{U}\phi_{U})\,\mathrm{e}^{-V-U}

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\mathbb{D}^{d}}\nabla(\phi_{U+\varepsilon\delta U}-\phi_{U})\cdot\nabla\phi_{U}\,\mathrm{e}^{-V-U}\xrightarrow[\varepsilon\to 0]{}\int_{\mathbb{D}^{d}}\nabla\psi_{U,\delta U}\cdot\nabla\phi_{U}\,\mathrm{e}^{-V-U}

\displaystyle\qquad=\int_{\mathbb{D}^{d}}(\mathcal{L}_{U}\psi_{U,\delta U})\phi_{U}\,\mathrm{e}^{-V-U}=\int_{\mathbb{D}^{d}}\Bigl(-\mathrm{e}^{U+V}\nabla\cdot\left(\mathrm{e}^{-U-V}\delta U\nabla\phi_{U}\right)\Bigr)\phi_{U}\,\mathrm{e}^{-V-U}

\displaystyle\qquad=\int_{\mathbb{D}^{d}}\delta U\lvert\nabla\phi_{U}\rvert^{2}\,\mathrm{e}^{-V-U},

where
\psi_{U,\delta U}
is the solution to the Poisson equation (
62
).
The equalities before and after the limit follow from the definitions of
\phi_{U}
and
\psi_{U,\delta U}
as weak solutions to (
20
) and (
62
),
respectively.
The last inequality is obtained by integration by parts,
which is justified because
\delta U
is compactly supported.
Combining this equation with (
46
) and (
48
),
we deduce (
45
).
∎

Before presenting the numerical method for approaching the optimal biasing potential
U
,
we mention two corollaries of
Theorem

25
.

Corollary 26

(Critical points)
.

Suppose that
U\in\mathfrak{U}_{0}
.
Then
U
is a critical point of the asymptotic variance viewed as a functional of
U

if and only if the corresponding solution to the Poisson equation (
20
) satisfies

\left\lvert\nabla{\phi_{U}}\right\rvert^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{D}^{d}}\left\lvert\nabla\phi_{U}\right\rvert^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu_{U}.

(49)

In other words, the norm of
\nabla{\phi_{U}}
is constant over
\mathbb{D}^{d}
.

Corollary 27

(No smooth minimizer)
.

Let
\mathbb{D}=\mathbb{T}
.
Then there is no biasing potential
U\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
that is a critical point of the asymptotic variance
\sigma^{2}_{f}[U]
.

Proof.

Assume for contradiction that there is a smooth biasing potential
U
for which (
49
) holds.
By elliptic regularity, the corresponding solution
\phi_{U}
to the Poisson equation (
20
) is also smooth and so,
by the extreme value theorem,
it attains its minimum at some point in
\mathbb{T}^{d}
, where
\nabla\phi_{U}
vanishes.
By (
49
), this implies that
\nabla\phi_{U}=0
for all
x\in\mathbb{T}^{d}
and, therefore,
-\mathcal{L}_{U}\phi_{U}=0=(f-I)\,\mathrm{e}^{U}
.
This is a contradiction because, by
1
,
the observable
f
is not everywhere equal to
I
.
∎

Corollary

27
highlights a limitation of the target-oriented approach taken in this section,
as singular potentials are impractical at the numerical level and unlikely to be of any use for different observables.
This motivates the approach taken in
Section

4
,
which aims at finding a biasing potential
U
that leads to a reduction in variance for not just one but a family of observables.

Steepest descent method

To conclude this section,
we present an iterative approach for approximating a minimizer of
\sigma^{2}_{f}[U]
.
We focus on the case where
\mathbb{D}=\mathbb{T}
for simplicity,
but the approach is easy to generalize to other settings (see
Section

4.2
).
Since an expression for the functional derivative of
\sigma^{2}_{f}[U]
is available by
Theorem

25
,
we employ a method based on steepest descent.
Each step of the method may be decomposed into three stages:

•

First, an approximate solution to the Poisson equation (
20
) is computed.
A number of numerical methods can be employed to this end.
Given that the optimal potential always exhibits singularities when
\mathbb{D}=\mathbb{T}
,
we opt for a finite difference approach rather than,
for example, a spectral method
[
2
,
48
]
.
The details of the finite difference method are presented in
Appendix

D
,
together with a convergence proof in the setting where
U
is regular.

•

Then, from the solution to (
20
),
an approximation of the gradient
G
of the asymptotic variance is calculated based on
Theorem

25
.

•

Finally, the potential
U
is updated according to
U\leftarrow U-\eta\,G
,
where
\eta
is found by backtracking line search following Armijo’s method
[
6
]
.

These steps are repeated until the
L^{2}(\mathbb{T})
norm of the gradient is sufficiently small.
A couple of comments are in order.
First, the expression of
G
depends on the considered Hilbert functional space.
By (
45
), the functions

\displaystyle G_{L^{2}(\mathrm{e}^{-V-U})}=\frac{Z[U]}{Z^{2}}\bigg(\left\lvert\nabla{\phi_{U}}\right\rvert^{2}-\int\left\lvert\nabla{\phi_{U}}\right\rvert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{U}\bigg),

\displaystyle G_{L^{2}(\mathrm{e}^{-V})}=\frac{Z[U]}{Z^{2}}\bigg(\left\lvert\nabla{\phi_{U}}\right\rvert^{2}-\int\left\lvert\nabla{\phi_{U}}\right\rvert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{U}\bigg)\mathrm{e}^{-U},

(50)

\displaystyle G_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T})}=\frac{Z[U]}{Z^{2}}\bigg(\left\lvert\nabla{\phi_{U}}\right\rvert^{2}-\int\left\lvert\nabla{\phi_{U}}\right\rvert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{U}\bigg)\mathrm{e}^{-U-V},

(51)

are all ascent directions for
\sigma^{2}_{f}[U]
,
corresponding to the gradients in
L^{2}(\mathrm{e}^{-V-U})
, in
L^{2}(\mathrm{e}^{-V})
and in
L^{2}(\mathbb{T})
,
respectively.
Of these three options, the latter two are better suited for use in an optimization method.
Indeed, employing the
L^{2}(\mathrm{e}^{-V-U})
derivative would lead to a change of metric with each update of
U
,
which precludes the use of methods that rely on information from multiple steps,
such as the Barzilai–Borwein method
[
9
]
.
In the numerical experiments presented in
Section

5
,
we use (
51
),
but good results can also be obtained by using (
50
).

Second,
the gradient needs to be discretized in practice.
In order to avoid convergence issues,
it is desirable that the discretized gradient is itself the gradient of a function.
Therefore, we use the discretization given in (
88
),
which is guaranteed to be the gradient of an appropriate discretization of the asymptotic variance;
see
Proposition

63
.

4
Minimizing the asymptotic variance for a class of observables

Assume that the set of observables of which we want to compute the expectation is well described by a Gaussian random field

f=\sum_{j=1}^{J}\sqrt{\lambda_{j}}u_{j}f_{j},\qquad u_{j}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1),\qquad\lambda_{j}\in(0,\infty),

(52)

where
(f_{j})_{1\leqslant j\leqslant J}
are given functions from
\mathbb{D}^{d}
to and the random variables
(u_{j})_{1\leqslant j\leqslant J}
are independent.
This equation defines a probability distribution
\mathcal{F}
on the space of observables
as the pushforward of the finite-dimensional Gaussian measure
\mathcal{G}=\mathcal{N}(0,\mathrm{id}_{J})
on
J
;
the probability measure
\mathcal{F}
assigns a probability 1 to
\Span(f_{1},\dotsc,f_{J})
.
One may wonder whether it is possible to minimize the average asymptotic variance for observables drawn from this distribution.
For clarity, we denote by
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{F}}
expectations with respect to observables.
Within this section,
we also use the notation

\mathfrak{U}_{0}=\bigcap_{j=1}^{J}\mathfrak{U}_{0}(V,f_{j}),\qquad\mathfrak{U}=\bigcap_{j=1}^{J}\mathfrak{U}(V,f_{j}),

where
\mathfrak{U}_{0}(V,f_{j})
and
\mathfrak{U}(V,f_{j})
are the sets defined before
7
and after (
34
), respectively.
These sets are assumed to be non-empty in this section.
Denoting by
\phi
the solution in
H^{1}(\mu_{U})\cap L^{2}_{0}(\mu_{U})
to

-\mathcal{L}_{U}\phi=(f-I)\mathrm{e}^{U},

and assuming that
U\in\mathfrak{U}_{0}
,
we have by
Lemma

11
that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{F}}\bigl[\sigma^{2}_{f}[U]\bigr]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{F}}\left[\frac{2Z[U]}{Z^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{D}^{d}}\phi(f-I)\mathrm{e}^{-V}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{2Z[U]}{Z^{2}}\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{G}}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{J}\sum_{k=1}^{J}u_{j}u_{k}\sqrt{\lambda_{j}\lambda_{k}}\int_{\mathbb{D}^{d}}\phi_{k}(f_{j}-I_{j})\mathrm{e}^{-V}\right],

where, for
1\leqslant j\leqslant J
,
the function
\phi_{j}
is the unique solution in
H^{1}(\mu_{U})\cap L^{2}_{0}(\mu_{U})
to the Poisson equation
-\mathcal{L}_{U}\phi_{j}=(f_{j}-I_{j})\mathrm{e}^{U}
,
with
I_{j}:=\mu(f_{j})
.
Since
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{G}}[u_{j}u_{k}]=\delta_{jk}
,
we obtain by rearranging the previous expression that

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}[U]:=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{F}}\bigl[\sigma^{2}_{f}[U]\bigr]=\frac{2Z[U]}{Z^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\lambda_{j}\int_{\mathbb{D}^{d}}\phi_{j}(f_{j}-I_{j})\mathrm{e}^{-V}=\sum_{j=1}^{J}\lambda_{j}\sigma^{2}_{f_{j}}[U].

(53)

Therefore, minimizing the expectation
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{F}}[\sigma^{2}_{f}]
amounts to minimizing the sum on the right-hand side of (
53
).

4.1
Optimal biasing potential in the one-dimensional setting with
\mathbb{D}=\real

In the one-dimensional setting with
\mathbb{D}=\real
,
an explicit expression for the infimum of the asymptotic variance can be obtained,
similar to that
Lemma

18
in the case of a single observable.
In order to state a precise result,
we introduce the notation

\widehat{\sigma}^{2}[U]=\sum_{j=1}^{J}\lambda_{j}\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{f_{j}}[U],

where
\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{g}
for an observable
g
was defined in (
34
).
Let us recall that, by the reasoning in
Section

3.3
,
the quantity
\widehat{\sigma}^{2}[U]
coincides with
\sigma^{2}[U]
in (
53
) when
U\in\mathfrak{U}_{0}
,
but
\widehat{\sigma}[U]
is well-defined more generally for any
U\in\mathfrak{U}
.
In
Lemma

29
below,
we give a bound from below on
\widehat{\sigma}^{2}[U]
in the particular case where
d=1
and
\mathbb{D}=\real
.
Before presenting this result,
we introduce the notation

F_{j}(x)=\int_{0}^{x}\bigl(f_{j}(\xi)-I_{j}\bigr)\mathrm{e}^{-V(\xi)}\mathrm{d}\xi,\qquad A_{\real,j}=-\int_{-\infty}^{0}\bigl(f_{j}(\xi)-I_{j}\bigr)\mathrm{e}^{-V(\xi)}\mathrm{d}\xi.

Remark 28
.

In the rest of this section,

F_{j}
and
A_{\real,j}
always appear together as
F_{j}-A_{\real,j}
,
which can be rewritten as an integral over
(-\infty,x]
with the same integrand as in the definition of
F_{j}
,
that is to say

F_{j}(x)-A_{\real,j}=\int_{-\infty}^{x}\bigl(f_{j}(\xi)-I_{j}\bigr)\mathrm{e}^{-V(\xi)}\,\mathrm{d}\xi.

For the sake of conciseness,
we could introduce a new notation to refer to
F_{j}-A_{\real,j}
,
but we refrain from doing so in order to keep the notation consistent with that used in
Section

3
.
∎

Lemma 29
.

Assume that
d=1
and
\mathbb{D}=\real
.
Then, for all
U\in\mathfrak{U}
, it holds that

\min_{U\in\mathfrak{U}}\widehat{\sigma}^{2}(U)=\frac{2}{Z^{2}}\left(\int\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{J}\lambda_{j}\lvert F_{j}-A_{\real,j}\rvert^{2}}\right)^{2}.

(54)

The minimum is achieved for

U=U_{*}:=-V-\log\left(\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{J}\lambda_{j}\lvert F_{j}-A_{\real,j}\rvert^{2}}\right)\in\mathfrak{U}.

(55)

In this case

\mathrm{e}^{-V-U_{*}}\text{ is proportional to }\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{J}\lambda_{j}\lvert F_{j}-A_{\real,j}\rvert^{2}}.

Proof.

We first show that
\widehat{\sigma}^{2}[U]
is bounded from below by the right-hand side of (
54
).
This is trivial if
\widehat{\sigma}^{2}[U]
is infinite,
so we assume from now on that
\widehat{\sigma}^{2}[U]<\infty
.
Using the definition of
\widehat{\sigma}^{2}[U]
,
we have

\displaystyle\widehat{\sigma}^{2}[U]=\sum_{j=1}^{J}\lambda_{j}\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{f_{j}}[U]=\frac{2Z[U]}{Z^{2}}\int\left(\sum_{j=1}^{J}\lambda_{j}\lvert F_{j}-A_{\real,j}\rvert^{2}\right)\mathrm{e}^{V+U}.

(56)

Here, we used that the infimum in the definition (
34
) of
\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{f_{j}}
is achieved for
A=A_{\real,j}
,
as explained in
Lemma

16
.
Let us introduce the notation

G=\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{J}\int\lambda_{j}\lvert F_{j}-A_{\real,j}\rvert^{2}}.

Since
\widehat{\sigma}^{2}[U]<\infty
by assumption,
the set over which
G^{2}\mathrm{e}^{V+U}
takes an infinite value is of zero Lebesgue measure.
Therefore, by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality,

\widehat{\sigma}^{2}[U]=\frac{2Z[U]}{Z^{2}}\int\lvert G\rvert^{2}\mathrm{e}^{U+V}\geqslant\frac{2}{Z^{2}}\left(\int\lvert G\rvert\sqrt{\mathrm{e}^{U+V}}\sqrt{\mathrm{e}^{-V-U}}\right)^{2}=\frac{2}{Z^{2}}\left(\int G\right)^{2}.

The claim that
U_{*}
in (
55
) achieves the lower bound can be verified by substitution in (
56
).
∎

Remark 30
.

Notice that, unless the functions
(F_{j}-A_{\real,j})_{1\leqslant j\leqslant J}
share a common root,
the optimal biasing potential
U_{*}
in (
55
) is a smooth function.
∎

In the same way that we obtained
Proposition

21
from
Lemma

18
,
we deduce from

The proof is a simple adaptation of that of

and

,

.
Then

\inf_{U\in\mathfrak{U}_{0}}\sigma^{2}[U]=\inf_{U\in C^{\infty}_{\rm c}(\real)}\sigma^{2}[U]=\frac{2}{Z^{2}}\left(\int\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{J}\lambda_{j}\lvert F_{j}-A_{\real,j}\rvert^{2}}\right)^{2}.

4.2
Numerical optimization

A result similar to
Lemma

29
is not easily available when
d=1
and
\mathbb{D}=\mathbb{T}
,
because the constant
A_{\mathbb{T}}
given in (
29

U
.
In this case or in the multi-dimensional setting,
one can resort to a steepest descent approach in order to find the optimal biasing potential.
It is easy to prove,
based on

that the functional derivative of
\sigma^{2}[U]
is given by

\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{d}\sigma^{2}[U]\cdot\delta U=\frac{Z[U]^{2}}{Z^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{D}^{d}}\left(\delta U-\int_{\mathbb{D}^{d}}\delta U\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{U}\right)\left(\sum_{j=1}^{J}\lambda_{j}\left\lvert\nabla{\phi_{j}}\right\rvert^{2}\right)\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{U}.

(57)

The approach presented in
Section

3.4
can then be applied
mutatis mutandis
.
Numerical experiments illustrating the potential found as a result of this procedure are presented in
Section

5
.

4.3
Free energy biasing

In the molecular dynamics literature,
variance reduction over a compact state space is often achieved by
free energy biasing
,
a heuristic approach which,
in the absence of coarse graining via a reaction coordinate,
amounts to setting
U=-V
;
see
[
31
]
and the references therein.
To conclude this section, we address the following related question:
is there a probability distribution
\mathcal{F}
on observables such that
U=-V
is a minimizer of the average asymptotic variance
\sigma^{2}[U]
,
i.e. for which energy biasing is optimal?
While we will not be able to provide a definite answer to this question,
we shall prove in
Proposition

32
that,
for an appropriate probability measure on observables, the biasing
U=-V
corresponds to a critical point of
\sigma^{2}[U]
.
This does not imply that
U=-V
is necessarily optimal,
because
\sigma^{2}[U]
is not convex in general;
see
56
in
Appendix

C
.

Proposition 32
.

Suppose that
\mathbb{D}=\mathbb{T}
with
d=1
and assume,
for
J\in 2\mathbb{N}_{>0}
,
that
(f_{j},\lambda_{j})_{1\leqslant j\leqslant J}

are the
J
first eigenfunctions and eigenvalues of the operator
\mathcal{K}=\mathrm{e}^{V}(-\Delta+\tau^{2}\mathcal{I})^{-\alpha}\mathrm{e}^{-V}
,
where
\alpha\in(0,\infty)
and
\tau\in\real
are parameters of the random field
and
\mathcal{K}
is viewed as a compact self-adjoint operator on the following space of functions defined on
\mathbb{T}
:

\left\{f\in L^{2}\left(\mathrm{e}^{-2V}\right)\colon\int_{\mathbb{T}}f\mathrm{e}^{-V}=0\right\}.

Then the average asymptotic variance
\sigma^{2}[U]
admits a critical point for
U=-V
.

Proof.

The eigenfunctions of the operator
\mathcal{K}
are given by

f_{j}(x)=\mathrm{e}^{V}\left\{\begin{aligned} &\sin\left(\frac{j+1}{2}x\right),\quad&&\text{if $j$ is odd},\\
&\cos\left(\frac{j}{2}x\right),\quad&&\text{if $j$ is even}.\end{aligned}\right.

(58)

Note that
I_{j}=0
for all
1\leqslant j\leqslant J
.
When
U=-V
,
the generator of the Markov semigroup associated with (
6
) is just the Laplacian operator.
Therefore, for a given
j\in\{1,\dotsc,J\}
,
the solution to the Poisson equation
-\mathcal{L}_{U}\phi_{j}=(f_{j}-I_{j})\mathrm{e}^{U}
is given by

\phi_{j}(x)=\left\lceil\frac{j}{2}\right\rceil^{-2}f_{j}(x).

Since
\lambda_{j}=\lambda_{j+1}
for all odd values of
j

and since
\sin^{2}+\cos^{2}=1
,
we deduce that the sum on the right-hand side of (
57
) is constant,
implying that the functional derivative of
\sigma^{2}[U]
is zero when evaluated at the biasing potential
U=-V
.
∎

Remark 33
.

The choice of the operator
\mathcal{K}
is motivated by the form (
48
) of the desired observables,
which is itself motivated by the fact that these observables lead to Poisson equations with explicit trigonometric solutions when
U=-V
.
∎

Remark 34
.

In this section,
we assumed for simplicity that the random observable admitted a finite expansion of the form (
52
).
The results we obtained could in principle be extended to the case of a more general Gaussian field,
with an infinite Karhunen–Loève series.
For background on Gaussian variables in infinite dimension,
see for example
[
41
, Section 1.5]
.
The reference
[
13
]
is also useful for understanding the regularity of infinite series of the form (
52
).
∎

Remark 42
.

In all the examples presented in this subsection,
the optimal biasing potential effectively partitions the domain into several regions that suffice for the estimation of
I
.
It is natural to wonder whether this observation holds true in general:
is it
always
the case that,
when such partitioning of the domain occurs,
averages of the observable with respect to the corresponding conditioned measures coincide with the target average
I
?
We gave in
19
a positive answer to this question in the one-dimensional setting.
Although we are not able to provide an equally rigorous answer in the multi-dimensional setting,
we motivate hereafter our belief that the answer is also positive in this case.
To this end, suppose that
U_{*}
partitions the domain into a number of regions,
corresponding to the connected components of
\{U_{*}<\infty\}
.
Suppose also that there exists an ensemble
(U_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon>0}
of smooth biasing potentials such that

\sigma^{2}_{f}[U_{\varepsilon}]\to\sigma^{2}_{f}[U_{*}]
and
\mathrm{e}^{-V-U_{\varepsilon}}\to\mathrm{e}^{-V-U_{*}}
in
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{D}^{d})
as
\varepsilon\to 0
.
In particular, it holds under this assumption that
U_{\varepsilon}(x)\to U_{*}(x)
for almost all
x\in\{U_{*}<\infty\}
.
It is well known, see e.g.
[
41
, Section 7.3]
,
that the average escape time from a potential well for the overdamped Langevin dynamics (
6
) scales exponentially with respect to the height of the potential barrier.
Therefore, for very small
\varepsilon
,
it would take a very long time for the dynamics to visit all the regions of the state space.
In these conditions,
the asymptotic variance would be very large,
unless the averages of the observable with respect to the probability measure
\mu
conditioned to each of the regions happen to coincide.
More precisely, if
\sigma^{2}_{f}[U_{\varepsilon}]
does not diverge as
\varepsilon\to 0
,
then the conditional averages in all the regions must necessarily coincide.
∎

Conclusions and perspectives for future works

In this work,
we considered an importance sampling method based on the overdamped Langevin dynamics in a perturbed potential
and present a novel approach for constructing the biasing potential.
Under appropriate assumptions,
this potential is optimal,
in the sense that it leads to the minimum asymptotic variance
when employed for calculating the average of one or a class of observables with respect to the target probability measure.
The optimal biasing potential is explicit in dimension 1,
and may be approximated by steepest descent in higher dimensions.

We demonstrated the performance of the method by means of numerical experiments in dimensions 1 and 2.
In the multimodal setting, in particular,
using the optimal importance distribution enables a considerable reduction in asymptotic variance.
Finally, our numerical experiments show that,
while minimizing the asymptotic variance for just one observable leads to singularities in the potential,
targeting a number of observables simultaneously leads to smooth potentials which can more easily be employed in numerical schemes.

A drawback of the proposed methodology is that the construction of the optimal biasing potential relies on an iterative method which,
at each step, requires the solution of a Poisson equation.
While feasible in low dimension,
this approach is computationally too costly in a high-dimensional setting.
A possible approach in this case is to
reduce the dimension of the problem by requiring that the biasing potential
is a function of only a few well-chosen degrees of freedom (so-called collective variables),
which ideally capture the metastable behavior of the dynamics.
This corresponds to the setting of free energy computation
[
30
]
,
and suggests to consider the variance as a functional of some free energy,
which particularly makes sense when the observable under investigation itself depends only on the collective variables.
Investigation of this approach will be the subject of future work.
Another direction for future work would be to investigate whether a similar approach can be employed to minimize the asymptotic variance of estimators based on discrete-time MCMC schemes using overdamped Langevin dynamics.
We expect the resulting optimal biasing potentials in that case to be close to those considered here.

\phi_{U+\varepsilon\delta U}\to\phi_{U}
in
L^{2}(\mu_{U})
in the limit as
\varepsilon\to 0
.
Additionally, it holds by

48
and the Poincaré inequality that

\frac{\phi_{U+\varepsilon\delta U}-\phi_{U}}{\varepsilon}-\int_{\mathbb{D}^{d}}\frac{\phi_{U+\varepsilon\delta U}-\phi_{U}}{\varepsilon}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{U}\xrightarrow[\varepsilon\to 0]{}\psi_{U,\delta U}\penalty\ \text{in $H^{1}(\mu_{U})$},

but these statements are not useful for our purposes in this paper.
∎

Appendix B
Connection between
(A,A^{*})
and
(A_{\mathbb{T}},A^{*}_{\mathbb{T}})

In this section,
we discuss the links between the constants defined in (
29
) and (
37
).

Connection between
A
and
A_{\mathbb{T}}
.

The constant
A^{*}=A
is recovered as a limit of
A^{*}_{\mathbb{T}}
for an increasingly large torus.
More precisely, it holds that

A=\lim_{L\to\infty}\frac{\displaystyle\int_{-L}^{L}F\,\mathrm{e}^{V+U}}{\displaystyle\int_{-L}^{L}\mathrm{e}^{V+U}}.

(69)

Indeed, for any
\ell>0
and
L>\ell
,
it holds that

\displaystyle\frac{\displaystyle\int_{-L}^{L}(F-A)\,\mathrm{e}^{V+U}}{\displaystyle\int_{-L}^{L}\mathrm{e}^{V+U}}=\,

\displaystyle\frac{\displaystyle\int_{[-L,-\ell)\cup(\ell,L]}(F-A)\,\mathrm{e}^{V+U}}{\displaystyle\int_{[-L,-\ell)\cup(\ell,L]}\mathrm{e}^{V+U}}\frac{\displaystyle\int_{[-L,-\ell)\cup(\ell,L]}\mathrm{e}^{V+U}}{\displaystyle\int_{[-L,L]}\mathrm{e}^{V+U}}

\displaystyle+\frac{\displaystyle\int_{[-\ell,\ell]}(F-A)\,\mathrm{e}^{V+U}}{\displaystyle\int_{[-\ell,\ell]}\mathrm{e}^{V+U}}\frac{\displaystyle\int_{[-\ell,\ell]}\mathrm{e}^{V+U}}{\displaystyle\int_{[-L,L]}\mathrm{e}^{V+U}}.

(70)

The right-hand side is a convex combination of the averages of
F-A
restricted to the sets
[-L,-\ell)\cup(\ell,L]
, for the first term,
and
[-\ell,\ell]
, for the second term.
In the proof of
Lemma

14
,
we proved that

\int\mathrm{e}^{U+V}=\infty.

Therefore, since
F
is uniformly bounded,
the second summand on the right-hand side of (
70
) converges to 0 in the limit as
L\to\infty
,
and so

\limsup_{L\to\infty}\left\lvert\frac{\displaystyle\int_{-L}^{L}F\,\mathrm{e}^{V+U}}{\displaystyle\int_{-L}^{L}\mathrm{e}^{V+U}}-A\right\rvert=\limsup_{L\to\infty}\left\lvert\frac{\displaystyle\int_{[-L,-\ell)\cup(\ell,L]}(F-A)\,\mathrm{e}^{V+U}}{\displaystyle\int_{[-L,-\ell)\cup(\ell,L]}\mathrm{e}^{V+U}}\right\rvert\leqslant\sup_{\lvert x\rvert\geqslant\ell}\lvert F(x)-A\rvert.

Since
\lim_{\lvert x\rvert\to\infty}F(x)=A
by definition of
A
in (
33
),
the right-hand side of this equation can be made arbitrarily small by taking
\ell
sufficiently large,
and so the limit (
69
) follows.

Connection between
A^{*}
and
A^{*}_{\mathbb{T}}
.

The constant
A^{*}=A
coincides with

\lim_{\ell\to\infty}\sup\left\{A\in\real:\int_{-\ell}^{\ell}\sgn(F-A)\geqslant 0\right\}.

(71)

Indeed, since
\lim_{|x|\to\infty}F(x)=A
,
there exists for any
\varepsilon>0
a constant
\ell_{\varepsilon}>0
such that

\forall\ell\geqslant\ell_{\varepsilon},\qquad\int_{-\ell}^{\ell}\sgn(F-A+\varepsilon)>0\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\int_{-\ell}^{\ell}\sgn(F-A-\varepsilon)<0.

Therefore, for all
\ell\geqslant\ell_{\varepsilon}
,
the supremum in (
71
) is contained in the interval
[A-\varepsilon,A+\varepsilon]
.
Since
\varepsilon
was arbitrary, the claim is proved.

Appendix C
Second variation of the asymptotic variance

Since the method we propose in
Section

3.4
relies on a steepest descent for the asymptotic variance
viewed as a functional of
U
,
it is natural to wonder whether this functional is convex,
in order to provide guarantees on the convergence of the method.
We provide a partial answer to this question in
Proposition

53
and
56
below.
Specifically, we prove that the asymptotic variance is convex when the domain is the one-dimensional real line but possibly non-convex when the domain is
\mathbb{T}
. We have not managed to prove or rule out the convexity of the asymptotic variance in the multi-dimensional setting.

We emphasize that the convexity of the asymptotic variance in the case where the domain is
does not imply the uniqueness (up to an additive constant) of the minimizer.
The most straightforward example is that of the constant observable,
in which case the asymptotic variance is equal to 0 for any smooth biasing potential
U
.

Proposition 53
.

Suppose that
7
is satisfied and let
\phi_{U}
be the solution to the Poisson equation as in
Lemma

11
.
Then, for all
\delta U,\delta W\in C^{\infty}_{\rm c}(\mathbb{D}^{d})
,
it holds that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{d}(\mathrm{d}\sigma^{2}_{f}[U]\cdot\delta U)\cdot\delta W=\frac{Z[U]}{Z^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{D}^{d}}\delta U_{0}\delta W_{0}\left(\left\lvert\nabla\phi_{U}\right\rvert^{2}+\int_{\mathbb{D}^{d}}\left\lvert\nabla\phi_{U}\right\rvert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{U}\right)\mathrm{e}^{-V-U}

\displaystyle\qquad-\frac{2Z[U]}{Z^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{D}^{d}}\bigl(\nabla\psi_{U,\delta U_{0}}-\delta U_{0}\nabla\phi_{U}\bigr)\cdot\bigl(\nabla\psi_{U,\delta W_{0}}-\delta W_{0}\nabla\phi_{U}\bigr)\mathrm{e}^{-V-U},

(72)

where, for a perturbation
\delta X\in\{\delta U,\delta W\}
,

\delta X_{0}:=\delta X-\mu_{U}(\delta X),\qquad\mu_{U}(\delta X):=\int_{\mathbb{D}^{d}}\delta X\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{U}.

and
\psi_{U,\delta X_{0}}\in H^{1}(\mu_{U})\cap L^{2}_{0}(\mu_{U})
is the solution to (
62
) with
\delta U=\delta X_{0}
.
In addition, the second term in (
72
) is zero in dimension 1 when
\mathbb{D}=\real
,
and so the asymptotic variance
\sigma^{2}_{f}[U]
is a convex functional in this case.

Proof.

We begin by rewriting the expression (
45
) as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{d}\sigma^{2}_{f}[U]\cdot\delta U

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Z^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{D}^{d}}\left(Z[U]\delta U-\int_{\mathbb{D}^{d}}\delta U\,\mathrm{e}^{-V-U}\right)\left\lvert\nabla\phi_{U}\right\rvert^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-V-U}

\displaystyle=\frac{Z[U]}{Z^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{D}^{d}}\delta U\left\lvert\nabla\phi_{U}\right\rvert^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-V-U}-\left(\int_{\mathbb{D}^{d}}\delta U\,\mathrm{e}^{-V-U}\right)\frac{\sigma^{2}_{f}[U]}{2Z[U]}=:T_{1}[U;\delta U]+T_{2}[U;\delta U].

Using the chain rule, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}T_{1}[U;\delta U]\cdot\delta W=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{Z^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{D}^{d}}\delta W\mathrm{e}^{-V-U}\int_{\mathbb{D}^{d}}\delta U\left\lvert\nabla\phi_{U}\right\rvert^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-V-U}

\displaystyle+\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{Z[U]}{\varepsilon Z^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{D}^{d}}\delta U\left(\left\lvert\nabla\phi_{U+\varepsilon\delta W}\right\rvert^{2}-\left\lvert\nabla\phi_{U}\right\rvert^{2}\right)\mathrm{e}^{-V-U}-\frac{Z[U]}{Z^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{D}^{d}}\delta U\delta W\left\lvert\nabla\phi_{U}\right\rvert^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-V-U}.

Similarly, for the second term we obtain

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}T_{2}[U;\delta U]\cdot\delta W=\frac{1}{Z^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{D}^{d}}\delta U\delta W\mathrm{e}^{-V-U}\int_{\mathbb{D}^{d}}\left\lvert\nabla\phi_{U}\right\rvert^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-V-U}-\int_{\mathbb{D}^{d}}\delta U\mathrm{e}^{-V-U}\mathrm{d}\bigg(\frac{\sigma^{2}_{f}[U]}{2Z[U]}\bigg)\cdot\delta W.

The functional derivative in the last term on the right-hand side is calculated as in the proof of
Theorem

25
;
specifically,

\mathrm{d}\bigg(\frac{\sigma^{2}_{f}[U]}{2Z[U]}\bigg)\cdot\delta W=\mathrm{d}\left(\frac{1}{Z^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{D}^{d}}\phi_{U}(f-I)\mathrm{e}^{-V}\right)\cdot\delta W=\frac{1}{Z^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{D}^{d}}\delta W\lvert\nabla\phi_{U}\rvert^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-V-U}.

By
Lemma

48
and the fact that
\delta U\in C^{\infty}_{\rm c}(\mathbb{D}^{d})
,
we have that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\mathbb{D}^{d}}\delta U\left(\left\lvert\nabla\phi_{U+\varepsilon\delta W}\right\rvert^{2}-\left\lvert\nabla\phi_{U}\right\rvert^{2}\right)\mathrm{e}^{-V-U}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=\int_{\mathbb{D}^{d}}\delta U\left(\frac{\nabla\phi_{U+\varepsilon\delta W}-\nabla\phi_{U}}{\varepsilon}\right)\cdot\left(\nabla\phi_{U+\varepsilon\delta W}+\nabla\phi_{U}\right)\mathrm{e}^{-V-U}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\xrightarrow[\varepsilon\to 0]{}2\int_{\mathbb{D}^{d}}\delta U\nabla\psi_{U,\delta W}\cdot\nabla\phi_{U}\,\mathrm{e}^{-V-U}.

Collecting all the terms,
we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{d}(\mathrm{d}\sigma^{2}_{f}[U]\cdot\delta U)\cdot\delta W

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{Z^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{D}^{d}}\delta U\mathrm{e}^{-V-U}\int_{\mathbb{D}^{d}}\delta W\left\lvert\nabla\phi_{U}\right\rvert^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-V-U}

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{1}{Z^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{D}^{d}}\delta W\mathrm{e}^{-V-U}\int_{\mathbb{D}^{d}}\delta U\left\lvert\nabla\phi_{U}\right\rvert^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-V-U}

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{2Z[U]}{Z^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{D}^{d}}\delta U\nabla\psi_{U,\delta W}\cdot\nabla\phi_{U}\mathrm{e}^{-V-U}

(73)

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{Z[U]}{Z^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{D}^{d}}\delta U\delta W\left(-\left\lvert\nabla\phi_{U}\right\rvert^{2}+\int_{\mathbb{D}^{d}}\left\lvert\nabla\phi_{U}\right\rvert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{U}\right)\mathrm{e}^{-V-U}.

By rewriting the last term on the right-hand side as

\frac{Z[U]}{Z^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{D}^{d}}\delta U\delta W\left(\left\lvert\nabla\phi_{U}\right\rvert^{2}+\int_{\mathbb{D}^{d}}\left\lvert\nabla\phi_{U}\right\rvert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{U}\right)\mathrm{e}^{-V-U}-\frac{2Z[U]}{Z^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{D}^{d}}\delta U\delta W\left\lvert\nabla\phi_{U}\right\rvert^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-V-U},

and substituting
\delta U\delta W=\delta U_{0}\delta W_{0}+\delta U\mu_{U}(\delta W)+\delta W\mu_{U}(\delta U)-\mu_{U}(\delta U)\mu_{U}(\delta W)
in the first term of the latter expression,
the second variation may be further simplified to

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{d}(\mathrm{d}\sigma^{2}_{f}[U]\cdot\delta U)\cdot\delta W

\displaystyle=\frac{Z[U]}{Z^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{D}^{d}}\delta U_{0}\delta W_{0}\left(\left\lvert\nabla\phi_{U}\right\rvert^{2}+\int_{\mathbb{D}^{d}}\left\lvert\nabla\phi_{U}\right\rvert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{U}\right)\mathrm{e}^{-V-U}

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{2Z[U]}{Z^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{D}^{d}}\delta U\nabla\phi_{U}\cdot(\nabla\psi_{U,\delta W}-\delta W\nabla\phi_{U})\,\mathrm{e}^{-V-U}.

(74)

Using (
67
),
both for
\psi_{U,\delta U}
and
\psi_{U,\delta W}
,
we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{D}^{d}}\delta U\nabla\phi_{U}\cdot\bigl(\nabla\psi_{U,\delta W}-\delta W\nabla\phi_{U}\bigr)\mathrm{e}^{-V-U}

\displaystyle\qquad=-\int_{\mathbb{D}^{d}}\bigl(\nabla\psi_{U,\delta U}-\delta U\nabla\phi_{U}\bigr)\cdot\bigl(\nabla\psi_{U,\delta W}-\delta W\nabla\phi_{U}\bigr)\mathrm{e}^{-V-U}.

From (
62
),
it is simple to see that
\nabla\psi_{U,\delta U}=\nabla\psi_{U,\delta U_{0}}+\nabla\psi_{U,\mu_{U}(\delta U)}=\nabla\psi_{U,\delta U_{0}}+\mu_{U}(\delta U)\nabla\phi_{U}
.
Similarly,
\nabla\psi_{U,\delta W}=\nabla\psi_{U,\delta W_{0}}+\mu_{U}(\delta W)\nabla\phi_{U}
.
Substituting these expressions in (
74
) leads to the claimed result (
72
).

One-dimensional setting.

In dimension 1 when
\mathbb{D}=\real
,
it holds that
\psi_{U,\delta W}^{\prime}=\delta W\phi_{U}^{\prime}
by (
68
),
and so the second term in (
74
) cancels out,
which proves the last part of the statement.
∎

Remark 56

(One-dimensional case with
\mathbb{D}=\mathbb{T}
)
.

The asymptotic variance is not a convex functional when
\mathbb{D}=\mathbb{T}
and

.
Indeed, we construct in this remark a potential
V
, a smooth function
\phi
,
and a direction
\delta U
such that
the second variation of the asymptotic variance
\sigma^{2}_{f}
for the observable
f=-\mathcal{L}\phi

(with
\mathcal{L}
the generator
\mathcal{L}_{U}
given in (

) with

)
in the direction

is negative when evaluated at the biasing potential

.
In the setting we consider, since
\phi
is the solution to (
20
) when
U=0
,
it holds by
52
that

\psi_{U=0,\delta U_{0}}^{\prime}=\delta U_{0}\phi^{\prime}-\left(\frac{\int_{\mathbb{T}}\delta U_{0}\phi^{\prime}}{\int_{\mathbb{T}}\mathrm{e}^{V}}\right)\mathrm{e}^{V}.

Here
\delta U_{0}:=\delta U-\mu(\delta U)
.
Therefore, by substitution in (

\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{d}\bigl(\mathrm{d}\sigma^{2}_{f}[0]\cdot\delta U\bigr)\cdot\delta U=\frac{1}{Z}\left(\int_{\mathbb{T}}\delta U_{0}^{2}\left(\left\lvert\phi^{\prime}\right\rvert^{2}+\int_{\mathbb{T}}\left\lvert\phi^{\prime}\right\rvert^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu\right)\mathrm{e}^{-V}-2\frac{\left(\int_{\mathbb{T}}\delta U_{0}\phi^{\prime}\right)^{2}}{\int_{\mathbb{T}}\mathrm{e}^{V}}\right),

The right-hand side of this equation is not always positive.

\delta U_{0}=\phi^{\prime}\mathrm{e}^{V}.

Note that
\delta U_{0}
indeed has average 0 with respect to

\int_{\mathbb{T}}\delta U_{0}\mathrm{e}^{-V}=\int_{\mathbb{T}}\phi^{\prime}=0.

\frac{Z}{2}\mathrm{d}\bigl(\mathrm{d}\sigma^{2}_{f}[0]\cdot\delta U\bigr)\cdot\delta U=\int_{\mathbb{T}}\lvert\phi^{\prime}\rvert^{4}\mathrm{e}^{V}+\frac{\int_{\mathbb{T}}\left\lvert\phi^{\prime}\right\rvert^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-V}}{\int_{\mathbb{T}}\mathrm{e}^{-V}}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\left\lvert\phi^{\prime}\right\rvert^{2}\mathrm{e}^{V}-2\frac{\left(\int_{\mathbb{T}}\lvert\phi^{\prime}\rvert^{2}\mathrm{e}^{V}\right)^{2}}{\int_{\mathbb{T}}\mathrm{e}^{V}}.

Appendix D
Numerical discretization of the Poisson equation

We consider here the case where the domain is
\mathbb{T}^{2}
for simplicity,
noting that the method may be generalized to any spatial dimension.
In order to numerically solve the Poisson equation (
20
),
we use a finite difference approach on a grid of size
N\times N
.
For a given
\delta>0
, the discretization nodes are arranged linearly according to

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{\ell}:=(-\pi+i\delta,-\pi+j\delta)\in\mathbb{T}^{2},\qquad j=\left\lfloor\frac{\ell-1}{N}\right\rfloor,\qquad i=\ell-1-jN,\qquad\delta=\frac{2\pi}{N},

(75)

for
\ell\in\{1,\dotsc,N^{2}\}
.
Note that the indices
i
and
j
each run from
0
to
N-1
;
the largest value of either coordinate over the set of discretization nodes is
\pi-\delta
,
which is sufficient given that
-\pi
and
\pi
coincide under the quotient map
\real{}^{2}\to\mathbb{T}^{2}
.

Before we present the method,
we introduce additional notation.
We denote by
\Pi_{N}
the discretization operator which associates to a function its values at the grid points (
75
),
and for a function
h\colon\mathbb{T}^{2}\to\real
,
we write
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{h}}=\Pi_{N}h\in\real^{N^{2}}
.
The notation
\exp.(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{h}})
refers the vector obtained by applying the exponential function element-wise to
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{h}}
,
and
\diag(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{h}})
refers to the diagonal matrix with diagonal entries given by
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{h}}
.
The notation
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{1}}\in\real^{N^{2}}
refers to a column vector containing only ones.
We also introduce the one-dimensional backward and forward difference operators,
which act on vectors in
N
:

\displaystyle\mathit{D}_{\rm B}=\frac{1}{\delta}\begin{pmatrix}1&&&&-1\\
-1&1\\
&-1&1\\
&&\ddots&\ddots\\
&&&-1&1\\
\end{pmatrix},\qquad\mathit{D}_{\rm F}=\frac{1}{\delta}\begin{pmatrix}-1&1\\
&-1&1\\
&&\ddots&\ddots\\
&&&-1&1\\
1&&&&-1\\
\end{pmatrix}.

From these operators,
we construct difference operators along the
x
and
y
directions by taking Kronecker products with the
N×N
identity matrix
\mathrm{id}_{N}
:

\mathit{D}_{\rm B}^{x}=\mathrm{id}_{N}\otimes\mathit{D}_{\rm B},\qquad\mathit{D}_{\rm B}^{y}=\mathit{D}_{\rm B}\otimes\mathrm{id}_{N},\qquad\mathit{D}_{\rm F}^{x}=\mathrm{id}_{N}\otimes\mathit{D}_{\rm F},\qquad\mathit{D}_{\rm F}^{y}=\mathit{D}_{\rm F}\otimes\mathrm{id}_{N}.

We recall that, for two matrices
\mathit{A},\mathit{B}\in\real^{N\times N}
,
the Kronecker product
A\otimes B
is defined as

\mathit{A}\otimes\mathit{B}=\begin{pmatrix}a_{11}\mathit{B}&\cdots&a_{1N}\mathit{B}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
a_{N1}\mathit{B}&\cdots&a_{NN}\mathit{B}\end{pmatrix}.

We denote by
\nabla_{\rm F}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{h}}
the
N^{2}\times 2
matrix

\nabla_{\rm F}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{h}}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathit{D}_{\rm F}^{x}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{h}}&\mathit{D}_{\rm F}^{y}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{h}}\end{pmatrix}.

For a weight function
w\colon\mathbb{T}^{2}\to\real
,
we introduce the weighted inner product
\langle\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,\rangle_{w}\colon\real^{N^{2}}\times\real^{N^{2}}\to\real
given for
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{h}}\in\real^{N^{2}}
by

\langle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{h}}\rangle_{w}=\delta^{2}\,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}}^{\mathsf{T}}\diag\bigl(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}\bigr)\boldsymbol{\mathbf{h}}=\delta^{2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{N^{2}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}}_{\ell}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{h}}_{\ell}\,w(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{\ell}),

(76)

with corresponding norm
\lVert\,\cdot\,\rVert_{w}
.
We include the factor
\delta^{2}
in this definition so that,
if
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{h}}
contain the values taken by continuous functions
g
and
h
when evaluated at the discretization points
and
w
is continuous,
then

\langle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{h}}\rangle_{w}\xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}g(x)h(x)w(x)\,\mathrm{d}x.

Finally, let
\lVert\nabla_{\rm F}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{h}}\rVert_{w}^{2}=\lVert\mathit{D}_{\rm F}^{x}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{h}}\rVert_{w}^{2}+\lVert\mathit{D}_{\rm F}^{y}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{h}}\rVert_{w}^{2}

and let
\lvert\nabla_{\rm F}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{h}}\rvert^{2}
denote the
N^{2}\times 1
column vector obtained by taking the squared Euclidean norm of each row of
\nabla_{\rm F}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{h}}
.
In the remainder of this section,
the notation (
76
) and corresponding norm are usually employed with the weight function
w=\mathrm{e}^{-V-U}
and so,
in order to simplify notation, we omit the subscript in this case.
We are now ready to write the discrete formulation of the Poisson equation (
20
).

Proposition 57
.

For
V,U,f\colon\mathbb{T}^{2}\to\real
, there exists a unique solution
(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}_{N},I_{N})\in\real^{N^{2}}\times\real
to

-\widetilde{\mathit{L}}\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}_{N}\\
I_{N}\end{pmatrix}:=\begin{pmatrix}-\mathit{L}&\exp.(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}})\\
\delta^{2}\exp.(-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{V}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}})^{\mathsf{T}}&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}_{N}\\
I_{N}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\diag\bigl(\exp.(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}})\bigr)\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}\\
0\end{pmatrix},

(77)

where

\displaystyle\mathit{L}

\displaystyle=\diag\bigl(\exp.(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{V}}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}})\bigr)\mathit{D}_{\rm B}^{x}\diag\bigl(\exp.(-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{V}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}})\bigr)\mathit{D}_{\rm F}^{x}

\displaystyle\qquad+\diag\bigl(\exp.(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{V}}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}})\bigr)\mathit{D}_{\rm B}^{y}\diag\bigl(\exp.(-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{V}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}})\bigr)\mathit{D}_{\rm F}^{y}.

(78)

Remark 58
.

The first rows in (
77
) may be rewritten as

-\mathit{L}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}_{N}=\diag\bigl(\exp.(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}})\bigr)(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}-I_{N}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{1}}),

which resembles the Poisson equation (
20
).
The last row in (
77
) may be rewritten as

\delta^{2}\exp.(-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{V}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}})^{\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}_{N}=\langle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{1}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}_{N}\rangle=0.

It expresses the requirement that the vector
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}_{N}
should be mean-zero with respect to the discrete measure
\exp.(-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{V}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}})
.
∎

Remark 59
.

We use the notation
I_{N}
for the scalar unknown in (
77
)
because solving (
77
) yields
both an approximate solution to the Poisson equation and an approximation of
I=\mu(f)
.
∎

Proof.

In order to prove the statement,
it is sufficient to show that the homogeneous equation

\begin{pmatrix}-\mathit{L}&\exp.(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}})\\
\delta^{2}\exp.(-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{V}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}})^{\mathsf{T}}&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}\\
\sigma\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{0}}\\
0\end{pmatrix}

(79)

admits only the trivial solution
=(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{0}},0)
.
We assume by contradiction that
(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}},\sigma)
is a nonzero solution.
Then

-\mathit{L}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}+\sigma\exp.(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}})=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{0}},

implying that

-\langle\mathit{L}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{1}}\rangle+\sigma\langle\exp.(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}),\boldsymbol{\mathbf{1}}\rangle=0.

(80)

The linear operator on
\real{}^{N^{2}\times N^{2}}
induced by
\mathit{L}
is self-adjoint for the inner product
\langle\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,\rangle
,
because

\displaystyle-\langle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}},\mathit{L}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{h}}\rangle

\displaystyle=-\delta^{2}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}}^{\mathsf{T}}\left(\mathit{D}_{\rm B}^{x}\diag\bigl(\exp.(-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{V}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}})\bigr)\mathit{D}_{\rm F}^{x}+\mathit{D}_{\rm B}^{y}\diag\bigl(\exp.(-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{V}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}})\bigr)\mathit{D}_{\rm F}^{y}\right)\boldsymbol{\mathbf{h}}

\displaystyle=\langle\mathit{D}_{\rm F}^{x}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}},\mathit{D}_{\rm F}^{x}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{h}}\rangle+\langle\mathit{D}_{\rm F}^{y}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}},\mathit{D}_{\rm F}^{y}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{h}}\rangle,

(81)

where we used the relation
\mathit{D}_{\rm B}^{\mathsf{T}}=-\mathit{D}_{\rm F}
.
Therefore, going back to (
80
),
we deduce that

0=-\langle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}},\mathit{L}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{1}}\rangle+\sigma\langle\exp.(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}),\boldsymbol{\mathbf{1}}\rangle=\sigma\langle\exp.(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}),\boldsymbol{\mathbf{1}}\rangle=\delta^{2}\sigma\boldsymbol{\mathbf{1}}^{\mathsf{T}}\exp.(-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{V}}).

Therefore
\sigma=0
,
but then
\mathit{L}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{0}}
by (
79
) and so
\langle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}},\mathit{L}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}\rangle=0
.
By the relation (
81
),
this implies that
D_{\rm F}^{x}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}=D_{\rm F}^{y}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}=0
,
so the vector
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}
is constant.
The last equation in (
79
) then implies that
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{0}}
.
Note that (
81
) implies that

\kernel(L^{\mathsf{T}})=\Span\bigl\{\exp.(-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{V}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}})\bigr\},

which will be useful in the proof of
Lemma

61
.
∎

It is possible to prove the convergence of the solution to (
77
)
to the exact solution of the Poisson equation (
20
) in the limit as
N\to\infty
.
To this end,
we begin by showing the following Poincaré-like inequality.

Lemma 60

(Discrete Poincaré inequality)
.

Assume that
V+U\colon\mathbb{T}^{2}\to\real
is uniformly bounded.
Then there exists a constant
R_{\rm disc}[U]>0
independent of
N
such that

\displaystyle\forall\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}}\in\left\{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{h}}\in\real^{N^{2}}:\boldsymbol{\mathbf{h}}^{\mathsf{T}}\exp.(-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{V}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}})=0\right\},\qquad\lVert\nabla_{\rm F}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}}\rVert^{2}\geqslant R_{\rm disc}[U]\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}}\rVert^{2}.

(82)

Proof.

It is sufficient to show (
82
) for
V+U=0
.
Indeed, assuming that the inequality holds in this particular case and denoting by
C
a constant
which depends only on
V+U
and is allowed to change from line to line,
we have that

\displaystyle\forall\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}}\in\real^{N^{2}},\qquad

\displaystyle\langle\mathit{D}_{\rm F}^{x}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}},\mathit{D}_{\rm F}^{x}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}}\rangle+\langle\mathit{D}_{\rm F}^{y}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}},\mathit{D}_{\rm F}^{y}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}}\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad\geqslant C\Bigl(\langle\mathit{D}_{\rm F}^{x}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}},\mathit{D}_{\rm F}^{x}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}}\rangle_{1}+\langle\mathit{D}_{\rm F}^{y}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}},\mathit{D}_{\rm F}^{y}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}}\rangle_{1}\Bigr)

\displaystyle\qquad\geqslant C\langle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}}}\rangle_{1},\qquad\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}}}=\frac{\langle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{1}}\rangle_{1}}{\langle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{1}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{1}}\rangle_{1}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{1}},

where we employed the equivalence between
\langle\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,\rangle_{1}
,
which is given by (
76
) in the particular case where
V+U=0
,
and
\langle\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,\rangle_{\mathrm{e}^{-V-U}}
,
noting that both constants in this equivalence can be fixed independently of
N
.
Using this equivalence in the other direction,
we obtain

\displaystyle\langle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}}}\rangle_{1}

\displaystyle\geqslant C\langle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}}}\rangle\geqslant C\inf_{s\in\real}\langle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}}-s\boldsymbol{\mathbf{1}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}}-s\boldsymbol{\mathbf{1}}\rangle

\displaystyle=C\langle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}}-\overline{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}}-\overline{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}}}\rangle,\qquad\overline{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}}}=\frac{\langle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{1}}\rangle}{\langle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{1}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{1}}\rangle}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{1}}.

Finally, equation (
82
) when
V+U=0
follows from its one-dimensional counterpart by using a standard tensorization argument (as for the proof of
[
31
, Proposition 2.6]
for instance).
It only remains to show the one-dimensional inequality

\forall\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}}\in\real^{N},\qquad\langle\mathit{D}_{\rm F}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}},\mathit{D}_{\rm F}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}}\rangle_{1}\geqslant R_{\rm disc}[U]\langle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}}}\rangle_{1},\qquad\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}}}=\frac{\langle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{1}}\rangle_{1}}{\langle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{1}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{1}}\rangle_{1}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{1}},

(83)

for a constant
R_{\rm disc}[U]
independent of
N
for sufficiently large
N
.
To this end,
we notice that

\big\langle\mathit{D}_{\rm F}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}},\mathit{D}_{\rm F}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}}\big\rangle_{1}=\big\langle\mathit{D}_{\rm B}\mathit{D}_{\rm F}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}}}),\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}}}\big\rangle_{1}.

The matrix
D_{\rm B}\mathit{D}_{\rm F}
is given by

D_{\rm B}\mathit{D}_{\rm F}=\frac{1}{\delta^{2}}\begin{pmatrix}2&-1&&&-1\\
-1&2&-1\\
&-1&\ddots&\ddots\\
&&\ddots&\ddots&-1\\
-1&&&-1&2\end{pmatrix}.

This is a circulant matrix
[
14
]
with explicit eigenvalues given by

\lambda_{k}=\frac{4}{\delta^{2}}\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi k}{N}\right)=\frac{N^{2}}{\pi^{2}}\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi k}{N}\right),\qquad k=0,1,\dotsc,N-1.

The minimum eigenvalue of this matrix is
\lambda_{0}=0
,
and the associated eigenvector is
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{1}}
,
to which
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}}}
is orthogonal.
Therefore, equation (
83
) implies that

\forall\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}}\in\real^{N},\qquad\big\langle\mathit{D}_{\rm F}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}},\mathit{D}_{\rm F}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}}\big\rangle_{1}\geqslant\lambda_{1}\big\langle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}}}\big\rangle_{1},

which implies that, for fixed
N
,
equation (
83
) holds with constant
R_{\rm disc}(N)=(N/\pi)^{2}\sin^{2}(\pi/N)
,
which converges to
1
in the limit
N\to\infty
.
See also
[
42
, Lemma 12.2]
for a Poincaré inequality for discrete functions on a bounded interval that are zero at the endpoints.
∎

Proposition

57
implies that
\widetilde{\mathit{L}}
is invertible.
Using
Lemma

60
,
we show that
\widetilde{\mathit{L}}^{-1}
does not diverge in the limit as
N\to\infty
,
in an appropriate norm.

Lemma 61
.

Assume that
V,U\colon\mathbb{T}^{2}\to\real
are continuous.
Then the matrix
\widetilde{\mathit{L}}^{-1}
is bounded uniformly in
N\in\mathbb{N}
,
for the operator norm induced by the following norm on
\real{}^{N^{2}}\times\real
:

(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}},\sigma)\mapsto\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}\rVert_{\mathrm{e}^{-U-V}}+\lvert\sigma\rvert.

(84)

Proof.

The strategy of proof is similar to that used in
[
47
, Section 2.2]
.
Since by the Fredholm alternative
\range(\mathit{L})=\kernel(\mathit{L}^{\mathsf{T}})^{\perp}
,
the range of
\mathit{L}
is given by
\left\{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{h}}\in\real^{N^{2}}:\boldsymbol{\mathbf{h}}^{\mathsf{T}}\exp.(-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{V}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}})=0\right\}
.
It then follows by
Lemma

60
that the following inequality holds for all
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}}\in\range(\mathit{L})
:

\lVert\mathit{L}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}}\rVert\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}}\rVert\geqslant-\langle\mathit{L}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}}\rangle=\lVert\mathit{D}_{\rm F}^{x}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}}\rVert^{2}+\lVert\mathit{D}_{\rm F}^{y}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}}\rVert^{2}\geqslant R_{\rm disc}[U]\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}}\rVert^{2},

and so we deduce that that
\lVert\mathit{L}^{-1}\rVert\leqslant\frac{1}{R_{\rm disc}[U]}
over
\range(\mathit{L})
.
Denote by
(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}},\sigma)
the solution to

\begin{pmatrix}-\mathit{L}&\exp.(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}})\\
\delta^{2}\exp.(-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{V}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}})^{\mathsf{T}}&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}\\
\sigma\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}}\\
s\end{pmatrix}.

(85)

This solution satisfies

-\langle\mathit{L}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{1}}\rangle+\sigma\langle\exp.(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}),\boldsymbol{\mathbf{1}}\rangle=\langle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{1}}\rangle,

(86)

and since
\langle\mathit{L}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{1}}\rangle=\langle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}},\mathit{L}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{1}}\rangle=0
,
this implies

\lvert\sigma\rvert=\left\lvert\frac{\langle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{1}}\rangle}{\langle\exp.(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}),\boldsymbol{\mathbf{1}}\rangle}\right\rvert\leqslant\frac{\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}}\rVert\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{1}}\rVert}{\langle\exp.(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}),\boldsymbol{\mathbf{1}}\rangle}.

We then deduce that

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}-\overline{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}}=-\mathit{L}^{-1}\bigl(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}}-\sigma\exp.(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}})\bigr),\qquad\overline{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}}=\frac{\langle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{1}}\rangle}{\langle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{1}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{1}}\rangle}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{1}},

and then the last equation in (
85
) gives
\overline{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}}=s\boldsymbol{\mathbf{1}}/\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{1}}\rVert^{2}
.
This leads to the bound

\displaystyle\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}\rVert

\displaystyle\leqslant\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}-\overline{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}}\rVert+\lVert\overline{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\gamma}}}\rVert

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{R_{\rm disc}[U]}\bigl(\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}}\rVert+\lvert\sigma\rvert\lVert\exp.(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}})\rVert\bigr)+\lvert s\rvert\frac{\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{1}}\rVert}{\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{1}}\rVert^{2}}\leqslant\frac{\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}}\rVert}{R_{\rm disc}[U]}\left(1+\frac{\lVert\exp.(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}})\rVert\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{1}}\rVert}{\langle\exp.(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}),\boldsymbol{\mathbf{1}}\rangle}\right)+\lvert s\rvert\frac{\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{1}}\rVert}{\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{1}}\rVert^{2}}.

In the limit
N\to\infty
, it holds that

\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{1}}\rVert\to\sqrt{\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\mathrm{e}^{-V-U}},\qquad\lVert\exp.(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}})\rVert\to\sqrt{\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\mathrm{e}^{-V+U}},\qquad\langle\exp.(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}),\boldsymbol{\mathbf{1}}\rangle\to\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\mathrm{e}^{-V},

which enables to conclude the proof.
∎

We are now ready to prove the convergence of the solution of the discretized Poisson equation (
77
) in the limit
N\to\infty
.

Proposition 62
.

Suppose that
7
is satisfied.
Let
\phi
denote the exact solution to (
20
) and let
I=\mu(f)
.
Let also
(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}_{N},I_{N})
denote the solution to the discretized equation (
77
).
Then it holds that

\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}_{N}-\Pi_{N}\phi\rVert\xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{}0,\qquad I_{N}\xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{}I.

Proof.

The proof is an application of the standard Lax equivalence theorem.
We denote the matrix of the linear system (
77
) by
\widetilde{\mathit{L}}_{N}
to emphasize its dependence on
N
.
Convergence follows from the usual argument:

\left\lVert\begin{pmatrix}\Pi_{N}\phi-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}_{N}\\
I-I_{N}\end{pmatrix}\right\rVert\leqslant C\left\lVert{\widetilde{\mathit{L}}_{N}}\begin{pmatrix}\Pi_{N}\phi-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}_{N}\\
I-I_{N}\end{pmatrix}\right\rVert=\left\lVert{\widetilde{\mathit{L}}_{N}}\begin{pmatrix}\Pi_{N}\phi\\
I\end{pmatrix}-\begin{pmatrix}\Pi_{N}\left(f\mathrm{e}^{U}\right)\\
0\end{pmatrix}\right\rVert\xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{}0,

where the norm in this equation is that defined in (
84
).
The first inequality follows from the stability statement of
Lemma

61
,
while the limit follows from the consistency of the discretization,
which is simple to check given that
\phi
is a smooth function under
7
,
and relying on the presence of the factor
\delta^{2}
in the definition (
76
).
∎

The main interest of the discretization (
77
) lies in the following statement,
which may be viewed as a result on the commutation of the discretization and derivative operators.
In order to be more precise,
we denote by
(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}_{N},I_{N})
the solution to (
77
) and let

\sigma^{2}_{f,N}[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}]=\frac{2Z_{N}[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}]}{Z_{N}^{2}}\lVert\nabla_{\rm F}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}_{N}\rVert^{2},\qquad\text{ where }\left\{\begin{aligned} Z_{N}&:=\delta^{2}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{1}}^{\mathsf{T}}\exp.(-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{V}}),\qquad\\
Z_{N}[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}]&:=\delta^{2}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{1}}^{\mathsf{T}}\exp.(-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{V}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}).\end{aligned}\right.

(87)

The following statement shows that the functional derivative of
\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{f}[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}]

has a structure similar to that of
\sigma^{2}_{f}[U]
given in (
21
);
it may be viewed as a discretization thereof.

Proposition 63

(Functional derivative of
\sigma^{2}_{f,N}
)
.

Suppose that
V,U\colon\mathbb{T}^{2}\to\real
are uniformly bounded.
The functional derivative with respect to
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}
of
\sigma^{2}_{f,N}
is given by

\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{d}\sigma^{2}_{f,N}[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}]\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\delta}U}=\frac{Z_{N}[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}]}{Z_{N}^{2}}\left\langle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\delta}U},\lvert\nabla_{\rm F}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}_{N}\rvert^{2}-\overline{\lvert\nabla_{\rm F}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}_{N}\rvert^{2}}\right\rangle,\qquad\overline{\lvert\nabla_{\rm F}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}_{N}\rvert^{2}}:=\frac{\lVert\nabla_{\rm F}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}_{N}\rVert^{2}}{Z_{N}[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}]}.

(88)

Notice that (
88
) is very similar to the formula (
45
) for the functional derivative of
\sigma^{2}_{f}[U]
.

Proof.

The proof mirrors that of
Theorem

25
.
In view of (
81
), we first rewrite

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}_{f,N}[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}]

\displaystyle=\frac{2Z_{N}[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}]}{Z_{N}^{2}}\lVert\nabla_{\rm F}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}_{N}\rVert^{2}=-\frac{2Z_{N}[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}]}{Z_{N}^{2}}\langle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}_{N},\mathit{L}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}_{N}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{2Z_{N}[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}]}{Z_{N}^{2}}\langle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}_{N},\diag\bigl(\exp.(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}})\bigr)(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}-I_{N}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{1}})\rangle=\frac{2Z_{N}[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}]}{Z_{N}^{2}}\langle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}_{N},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}-I_{N}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{1}}\rangle_{\mathrm{e}^{-V}}.

Let
(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}_{N}^{\varepsilon},I_{N}^{\varepsilon})
denote the solution to (
77
) with
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}+\varepsilon\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\delta}U}
in place of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}
everywhere
and
\mathit{L}^{\varepsilon}
the corresponding matrix (
78
).
It is simple to check, using a reasoning similar to (
86
) as well as the equation
\langle L^{\varepsilon}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}_{N}^{\varepsilon},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{1}}\rangle=0
,
that the scalar term
I^{\varepsilon}_{N}=I_{N}
is fact independent of the potential
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}
.
Therefore,
we obtain that

-\mathit{L}^{\varepsilon}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}_{N}^{\varepsilon}=\diag\bigl(\exp.(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}+\varepsilon\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\delta}U})\bigr)(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}-I_{N}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{1}}).

By definition of the functional derivative,
we then have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{d}\sigma^{2}_{f,N}[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}]\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\delta}U}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{d}Z_{N}[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}]\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\delta}U}}{Z_{N}^{2}}\lVert\nabla_{\rm F}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}_{N}\rVert^{2}+\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\frac{Z_{N}[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}]}{\varepsilon Z_{N}^{2}}\langle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}^{\varepsilon}_{N}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}_{N},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}-I_{N}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{1}}\rangle_{\mathrm{e}^{-V}}.

(89)

The functional derivative in the first term is given by

\mathrm{d}Z_{N}[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}]\cdot{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\delta}}U}=-\langle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\delta}U},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{1}}\rangle.

(90)

For the second term in (
89
),
we obtain

\displaystyle\langle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}^{\varepsilon}_{N}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}_{N},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}-I_{N}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{1}}\rangle_{\mathrm{e}^{-V}}

\displaystyle=\left\langle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}^{\varepsilon}_{N}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}_{N},\diag\bigl(\exp.(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}})\bigr)(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}-I_{N}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{1}})\right\rangle_{\mathrm{e}^{-V-U}}

\displaystyle=-\langle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}^{\varepsilon}_{N}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}_{N},\mathit{L}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}_{N}\rangle_{\mathrm{e}^{-V-U}}

\displaystyle=-\langle\mathit{L}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}^{\varepsilon}_{N}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}_{N}),\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}_{N}\rangle_{\mathrm{e}^{-V-U}}

\displaystyle=-\langle\mathit{L}^{\varepsilon}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}^{\varepsilon}_{N},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}_{N}\rangle_{\mathrm{e}^{-V-U-\varepsilon\delta U}}+\langle\mathit{L}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}_{N},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}_{N}\rangle_{\mathrm{e}^{-V-U}}

\displaystyle\qquad-\langle\mathit{L}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}^{\varepsilon}_{N},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}_{N}\rangle_{\mathrm{e}^{-V-U}}+\langle\mathit{L}^{\varepsilon}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}^{\varepsilon}_{N},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}_{N}\rangle_{\mathrm{e}^{-V-U-\varepsilon\delta U}}.

The first two terms in the last expression cancel out,
and after substituting the expressions of
\mathit{L}
and
\mathit{L}^{\varepsilon}

given in (
78
) in the other two terms,
we obtain

\displaystyle\langle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}^{\varepsilon}_{N}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}_{N},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}-I_{N}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{1}}\rangle_{\mathrm{e}^{-V}}

\displaystyle=-\langle\mathit{D}_{\rm B}^{x}(\mathit{M}-\mathit{M}^{\varepsilon})\mathit{D}_{\rm F}^{x}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}^{\varepsilon}_{N},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}_{N}\rangle_{1}-\langle\mathit{D}_{\rm B}^{y}(\mathit{M}-\mathit{M}^{\varepsilon})\mathit{D}_{\rm F}^{y}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}^{\varepsilon}_{N},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}_{N}\rangle_{1},

where

\mathit{M}-\mathit{M}^{\varepsilon}:=\diag\bigl(\exp.(-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{V}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}})\bigr)-\diag\bigl(\exp.(-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{V}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}-\varepsilon\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\delta}U})\bigr).

Noting that

\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\frac{\mathit{M}-\mathit{M}^{\varepsilon}}{\varepsilon}=\diag\bigl(\exp.(-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{V}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}})\bigr)\diag(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\delta}U}),

we deduce that

\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\frac{Z_{N}[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}]}{\varepsilon Z_{N}^{2}}\langle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}^{\varepsilon}_{N}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}_{N},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}-I_{N}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{1}}\rangle_{\mathrm{e}^{-V}}=\frac{Z_{N}[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}]}{Z_{N}^{2}}\left\langle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\delta}U},\lvert\nabla_{\rm F}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}_{N}\rvert^{2}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{e}^{-V-U}}.

Combining this equation with (
89
) and (
90
),
we deduce (
88
).
∎
