Symmetrization results for general nonlocal linear ellipitic and parabolic problems
Vincenzo Ferone, Gianpaolo Piscitelli, Bruno Volzone

Abstract
We establish a Talenti-type symmetrization result in the form of mass concentration ( i.e. integral comparison) for very general linear nonlocal elliptic problems, equipped with homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions. In this framework, the relevant concentration comparison for the classical fractional Laplacian can be reviewed as a special case of our main result, thus generalizing the previous results in [ 21 ] . Finally, using an implicit time discretization techniques, similar results are obtained for the solutions of Cauchy-Dirichlet nonlocal linear parabolic problems.



1
Introduction

The aim of the present work is to establish some estimates, in the form of mass concentration comparisons, for solutions to general nonlocal elliptic homogeneous Dirichlet problems.

To be more specific, let us assume that
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
,
N\geq 1
, is an open and bounded set and
K,J
be measurable nonnegative function such that
J\not\equiv 0
and

\displaystyle K(x,y)=K(y,x)\quad\forall x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N},

(1.1)

\displaystyle x\mapsto\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}K(x,y)\min\{|x-y|^{2},1\}dy\in L_{loc}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{N}),

(1.2)

\displaystyle K(x,y)\geq J(x-y).

(1.3)

Let us denote by
\Omega^{\sharp}
the ball with the same measure of
\Omega
and by
J^{\sharp}
the Schwartz rearrangement of
J
(see Section
2.1
for details); the main result of the paper consists in developing new symmetrization techniques in order to achieve comparison results for equations involving the following nonlocal operator:

\mathcal{L}u(x)=\mathrm{P.V.}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}K(x,y)(u(x)-u(y))dy,

(1.4)

where the principal value (P.V.) integral is meant in the sense that

\mathcal{L}u(x)=\frac{1}{2}\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus B_{\varepsilon}(x)}K(x,y)(u(x)-u(y))dy.

The dual variational interpretation of the operator
\mathcal{L}
will be specified in Subsection
2.2
. We associate to
\mathcal{L}
the following, suitable symmetrized operator
{\mathcal{L}}^{\sharp}
, whose kernel depends on the kernel
J
introduced in the lower bound (
1.3
):

{\mathcal{L}}^{\sharp}v(x)=\frac{1}{2}\,\mathrm{P.V.}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}J^{\sharp}(x-y)(v(x)-v(y))dy.

(1.5)

We denote
c_{\sharp}
the radially increasing rearrangement of a nonnegative measurable function
c
(see Section
2.1
for details). The main Theorem of the paper is the following Talenti-type (see
[
38
]
) symmetrization result.

Let
\mathcal{L}
and
\mathcal{L}^{\sharp}

1.4
) and (
1.5
) with the kernels

satisfying (

)-(
1.2
)-(
1.3
),

,

such that

in

. If
u
and
v
are the solutions of:

\begin{cases}\mathcal{L}u+cu=f\ &\text{in }\Omega,\\
u=0&\text{on }\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega,\end{cases}

(1.6)

and

\begin{cases}{\mathcal{L}}^{\sharp}v+c_{\sharp}v=f^{\sharp}\ &\text{in }\Omega^{\sharp},\\
v=0&\text{on }\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega^{\sharp},\end{cases}

(1.7)

respectively, then

\int_{B_{r}}u^{\sharp}(x)\,dx\leq\int_{B_{r}}v(x)\,dx\quad\forall r>0,

(1.8)

where

is the ball centered in the origin.
Moreover we have the following energy estimate:

\begin{split}&\frac{1}{2}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}K(x,y)\left(u(x)-u(y)\right)^{2}dydx+\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}c(x)u^{2}(x)dx\\

(1.9)

For what concerns the nonlocal
parabolic

{\mathbb{R}}^{N+1}
given by
\Omega\times[0,T]
for

and give a related comparison result. We will use the following convention: if

is defined in
\Omega\times[0,T]
, then
f^{\sharp}(x,t)
and
f_{\sharp}(x,t)
denotes the symmetrized function with respect to
x
, for
t
fixed (
i.e.
Steiner symmetrizations of

):

\mathcal{L}
and

satisfying (

Main novelties of the paper.
We mention that a symmetrization result, in the form of mass concentration comparison, for the fractional Laplacian has been already obtained, for example, in
[
21
]
, where the singular kernel is then given by

K(y)=\frac{C}{|x|^{N+2s}},

with
0<s<1
and
C
a proper normalization constant. Moreover, analogous comparison results has been also obtained for singular nonlocal elliptic problem
[
10
]
.

The main difficulty in proving Theorem
1.1
relies with no doubt in the
very
general form of the kernel
K
described by (
1.1
)-(
1.2
)-(
1.3
). Indeed, despite of
[
21
,
10
]
, the rearrangement estimates in the weak formulation of our problem
can not
yield an explicit expression in radial coordinates in terms of hypergeometric functions, for which the principal features, such as the main monotonicity and asymptotic behaviors, are known. Thus we had to look for new and more flexible techniques which could be adapted to the present general context.

The remarkable features of the new techniques can be identified in the tools used in the proof of Theorem
1.1
, which is divided in two relevant steps. In the first one, choosing the classical Talenti’s truncature of the weak solution as a test function, we first apply a
Riesz type rearrangement inequality
, in the form described Proposition
2.1
, directly on the weak formulation of the problem. This step can be seen as an usage of a
nonlocal
Pólya-Szegő type inequality for the achievement of a suitable energy estimate (see
[
30
]
for a similar approach in the case of local operators). At this stage, an exponential integrability of the involved kernels, derived by the Lévy property (
1.3
) in Lemma
2.6
, is required. Therefore, we use a nonlocal variant of the coarea formula on the super and sub level sets of the solution (see Proposition
2.9
), which turns out to be essential in treating the behavior on the level sets at height
h>0
; particularly, without this property the passage to the limit as
h\rightarrow 0
looks quite difficult in such general context.

In the second step, we finalize a comparison result. To this aim, we introduce two key functions defined through the convolutions
\Phi_{1}
and
\Phi_{2}
of
J^{\sharp}
with the characteristic functions of the ball or its complementary set, whose radial monotonicity is achieved in Proposition
2.13
by suitable and nontrivial geometric considerations. Besides, the main argument of the comparison result is based on subtle contradiction argument applied to nonlocal estimates derived from the weak formulations of the initial problem (
1.6
) and the symmetrized one (
1.7
). In this point, the monotonicity of the functions
\Phi_{i}
plays an essential role. Finally, we close the argument by invoking a special maximum/minimum principle in Proposition
2.11
applied on the lower order term.

The proof of the parabolic mass concentration comparison exhibited in Theorem
1.2
is based on the implicit time discretization scheme, employed in
[
4
,
6
]
for linear problems and firstly introduced in
[
39
]
,
[
40
]
for symmetrization in nonlinear diffusion equations. More precisely, we reduce the problem (
1.10
) to a sequence of elliptic problems, to which we can apply the comparison result (
1.8
). See also the survey
[
40
]
.

Some comments on relevant previous results in the literature.

Actually, the effect of symmetrization on the fractional Laplacian operator

(-\Delta)^{s}u=\gamma(N,s)\,\text{P.V.}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}\frac{u(x)-u(y)}{|x-y|^{N+2s}}dy

(1.13)

being
\gamma(N,s)
a suitabile normalization constant, has already been exploited in
[
16
]
,
[
19
]
for fractional elliptic equations and then in
[
41
]
,
[
42
]
,
[
36
]
,
[
44
]
in the context of nonlocal diffusion equations of porous medium type. In those papers a symmetrization result in terms of mass concentration of the form (
1.8
) is obtained employing the
local
interpretation of (
1.13
) in terms of an extension problem settled on an infinite cylinder
\mathcal{C}_{\Omega}=\Omega\times(0,\infty)
, which was established in the classical result by Caffarelli and Silvestre
[
11
]
and generalized in
[
37
]
. In such setting, the extra variable
y>0
is fixed in the symmetrization techniques, therefore the Steiner symmetrization approach can be handled in the extensions problems associated to the nonlocal problems (
1.6
) and (
1.7
). Finally, the mass concentration estimate (
1.8
) is established in the limit as
y\rightarrow 0
. We explicitly remark that such an approach is
not
available for our context, since, to our knowledge, no extension problem can be in principle associated to nonlocal operators with general kernels. Moreover, the
direct
symmetrization approach has the notable benefit to highlight the considerable differences with respect the local results, see for instance the explicit counterexamples in
[
21
]
for the fractional laplacian operator.

We also mention that in the recent paper
[
23
]
some
L^{p}
estimates of solutions of nonlocal elliptic an parabolic problems with
integrable
kernels
K
are obtained by Talenti’s type symmetrization techniques. We remark that these results are consequence of our general Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
.

Potential applications of the results and generalizations.

The class of equations we are treating appears in several
contexts, and has attracted a lot of interests in different fields, particularly where anomalous diffusions appear. Examples of the central role played by nonlocal operators in the Applied Sciences can be found, for instance, in Probability theory (since they are generators of stochastic Lévy processes,
i.e.
special stochastic processes with jumps), Fluid mechanics (for example, in the SQG equation) or in
Mathematical physics (relativistic Schrödinger operators or the Boltzmann equation), peridynamics theory (see
e.g.
in
[
1
,
9
,
17
,
31
,
32
]
). For a very rich of more detailed references we refer to the book
[
20
, Sec. 1.1]
or
[
33
]
.

In particular, we observe that the results established in the present work allows to consider operators of the form
\mathcal{L}
which are
stable-like operators
, that is infinitesimal generators of processes associated with kernels
K
such that
K(x,y)=\mathsf{K}(x-y)
, where
\mathsf{K}
is
comparable
to the kernel
|x|^{-N-2s}
of the fractional Laplacian (also called rough kernels). In this case, the kernel
\mathsf{K}
needs not to be homogeneous but it satisfies a strong ellipticity condition

\frac{C_{1}}{|y|^{N+2s}}\leq\mathsf{K}(y)\leq\frac{C_{2}}{|y|^{N+2s}},

(1.14)

for some positive constants
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
,
C_{1}\leq C_{2}
. Such kind of operators are widely studied in the literature, see
[
20
, Sec. 2.1.6]
and the references therein. The importance of such kernels in the theory is confirmed, for instance by recent important results on obstacle problems
[
35
]
,
[
34
]
and the regularity of the free boundaries
[
22
]
.

To this concern, some possible, future applications of our result might regard the study of decay estimates and the asymptotics related to nonlocal nonlinear diffusion equations with symmetric kernels verifying (
1.14
), namely nonlinear parabolic equations of the form

u_{t}+\mathcal{L}\phi(u)=0,

for some increasing odd diffusivity
\phi(t)
: for instance, the choice
\psi(t)=|t|^{m-1}t
gives rise to a nonlocal equation of porous medium type. Such types of models attracted a lot of attention in the recent literature, see for instance
[
14
]
. Also operators with more general Lévy kernels satisfying
upper and lower, weak scaling conditions might be considered: this types of kernels with explicit examples were studied for instance in
[
8
]
(see also
[
26
]
).
Just to give some explicit, interesting examples, we can consider for instance symmetric kernels of the form

\mathsf{K}(y)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{1}{|y|^{N+2s_{i}}},\quad k\in\mathbf{N},s_{i}\in(0,1)\text{ for }i=1,...,k:

in this case, it is clear that the radial kernel in (
1.3
) is just
J(r)=\mathsf{K}(r)
, being
r
the radial coordinate, and the operators
\mathcal{L},\mathcal{L}^{\sharp}
is the sum of fractional Laplacians

\sum_{i=1}^{k}(-\Delta)^{s_{i}}.

We might also take into account kernels of the logarithmic type in the form

\mathsf{K}(y)=\frac{\log^{\varepsilon}(1+|y|)}{r^{N+2}},

for some small
\varepsilon>0
. For more interesting examples of kernels and properties of the related diffusion equations, we refer to the forthcoming paper
[
24
]
.

We finally observe that our techniques might also contribute to give results in the interesting framework of nonlocal anisotropic equations, see for instance
[
33
]
and results in the related nonlinear diffusion theory
[
15
]
.

2.1
Rearrangements

In this subsection we recall the definiton of rearrangements and some properties which will be used in the following. For a more exhaustive treatment of the argument we refer, for example, to
[

]
,
[

]

[

Let us consider a real measurable function
f
on an open set
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
and, for any
t\geq 0
, the set

\Omega_{f}^{t}=\left\{x\in\Omega:\left|f\left(x\right)\right|>t\right\}.

We assume that the

distribution function

\mu_{f}
of
f
is such that

\mu_{f}(t):=\left|\Omega_{f}^{t}\right|<+\infty\qquad\text{for every }t>0,

(2.1)

We recall that
\mu_{f}(\cdot)
is a right-continuous function, decreasing from
\mu_{f}(0)=|\operatorname{supp}(f)|
to
\mu_{f}(+\infty)=0
as
t
increases from 0 to
+\infty
. It presents a discontinuity at every value
t
which is assumed by
|f|
on a set of positive measure, and, for such a value of
t
, it holds

\mu_{f}(t^{-})-\mu_{f}(t)=|\{x\in\Omega:\left|f\left(x\right)\right|=t\}|.

For every
t\geq 0
, we put
r_{f}(t)=\left(\frac{\mu_{f}(t)}{\omega_{N}}\right)^{\frac{1}{N}}
and it is clear that
(\Omega_{f}^{t})^{\sharp}=B_{r_{f}(t)}
. Furthermore, we observe that
r_{f}(t)
is also right-continuous. Obviously we put
r_{f}(t^{-})=\left(\frac{\mu_{f}(t^{-})}{\omega_{N}}\right)^{\frac{1}{N}}
.

The
one dimensional decreasing rearrangement
of
f
is

f^{\ast}\left(\sigma\right)=\sup\left\{t\geq 0:\mu_{f}\left(t\right)>\sigma\right\}\qquad\sigma\in\left[0,+\infty\right),

(2.2)

that is,
f^{*}
is the distribution function of
\mu_{f}
, so it is a right-continuous function.
We stress that if
\mu_{f}
is strictly decreasing, then
f^{*}
extends to the whole of the half line
[0,+\infty[
the inverse function of
\mu_{f}
. In the general case we have that
f^{*}(\mu_{f}(t))\leq t
, for
t\in[0,+\infty[
, and
\mu_{f}(f^{*}(s))\leq s
, for
s\in[0,+\infty[
. We also observe that, if
\mu_{f}(t)
has a jump, i.e.,
\mu_{f}(t)<\mu_{f}(t^{-})
for some

, then
f^{*}(s)
has a flat zone, i.e.,
f^{*}(s)=t,\ \forall s\in[\mu_{f}(t),\mu_{f}(t^{-})]
(see Fig.
2.1
). Similarly, if
\mu_{f}(t)
has a flat zone then
f^{*}(s)
has a jump.

If
\Omega
is bounded, the

one dimensional increasing rearrangement
of
f
is

f_{\ast}\left(\sigma\right)=f^{\ast}(|\Omega|-\sigma)\qquad\sigma\in\left(0,\left|\Omega\right|\right).

We call the
radially decreasing rearrangement
(or
Schwarz decreasing rearrangement
) of

, the function

f^{\sharp}\left(x\right)=f^{\ast}(\omega_{N}\left|x\right|^{N})\qquad x\in\Omega^{\sharp};

and we call the
radially increasing rearrangement
of
f
, the

f_{\sharp}\left(x\right)=f_{\ast}(\omega_{N}\left|x\right|^{N})\qquad x\in\Omega^{\sharp}.

From the definitions (see in particular (
2.2
)), we deduce that
f^{*}
,
f_{*}
,
f^{\sharp}
and
f_{\sharp}
have the same distribution function as

, consequently, rearrangements preserve

\|f\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}=\|f^{\ast}\|_{L^{p}(0,|\Omega|)}=\|f^{\sharp}\|_{L^{p}(\Omega^{\sharp})}.

Furthermore, for any couple of measurable functions

[

\int_{\Omega}|f(x)\,g(x)|dx\leq\int_{0}^{\left|\Omega\right|}f^{\ast}(\sigma)\,g^{\ast}(\sigma)d\sigma=\int_{\Omega^{\sharp}}f^{\sharp}(x)\,g^{\sharp}(x)\,dx\,;

(2.3)

and

\int_{\Omega^{\sharp}}f^{\sharp}(x)\,g_{\sharp}(x)\,dx\,=\int_{0}^{\left|\Omega\right|}f^{\ast}(\sigma)\,g_{\ast}(\sigma)d\sigma\leq\int_{\Omega}|f(x)\,g(x)|dx.

(2.4)

Now, we recall a generalization of the

Proposition 2.1

Let
F:{\mathbb{R}}^{+}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{+}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{+}
be a continuous function such that
F(0,0)=0
and

F(u_{2},v_{2})+F(u_{1},v_{1})\geq F(u_{2},v_{1})+F(u_{1},v_{2})

(2.5)

whenever
u_{2}\geq u_{1}>0
and
v_{2}\geq v_{1}>0
.
Assume that
u,v
are two nonnegative, measurable functions on
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
satisfying (
2.1
), then

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}F(u(x),v(y))W(ax+by)\,dydx\leq\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}F(u^{\sharp}(x),v^{\sharp}(y))W^{\sharp}(ax+by)\,dydx

(2.6)

for any nonnegative function
W\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{N})
and any choice of nonzero numbers

and

.

The comparison of mass concentrations (

(for the proof we refer to

]

]

Proposition 2.2

Let
u,v\in L^{p}(\Omega)
,
p\geq 1
, be two nonnegative function. Then the following are equivalent:

(i)

\int_{B_{r}}u(x)\,dx\leq\int_{B_{r}}v(x)\,dx\quad\forall r>0,

(ii) for all nonnegative

\int_{\Omega}u(x)\varphi(x)\,dx\leq\int_{\Omega^{\sharp}}v(x)\varphi^{\#}(x)\,dx,

(iii) for all convex, nonnegative Lipschitz functions

From this Lemma it easily follows that if (i) holds,

\|f\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\leq\|g\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\quad\forall p\in[1,\infty].

2.2
The nonlocal problems

In this Section, we recollect some definitions and properties of the nonlocal problems we are dealing with.

Assume that the nonnegative kernel
K
satisfies the assumptions (
1.1
)-(
1.2
)-(
1.3
).
From
[
18
]
, we recall the following definitions and properties of the related functional spaces. We define the Sobolev space
H({\mathbb{R}}^{N},K)
as

H({\mathbb{R}}^{N},K)=\left\{u\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{N})\text{ such that }[u]_{H({\mathbb{R}}^{N},K)}<\infty\right\}

where
[u]_{H({\mathbb{R}}^{N},K)}
is the
K
-Gagliardo seminorm of
u
, that is

[u]_{H({\mathbb{R}}^{N},K)}=\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}|u(x)-u(y)|^{2}K(x,y)\,dydx\right)^{1/2}.

The space
H({\mathbb{R}}^{N},K)
is equipped with the norm

||u||_{H_{\Omega}({\mathbb{R}}^{N},K)}=\left(||u||^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+[u]^{2}_{H_{\Omega}({\mathbb{R}}^{N},K)}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Moreover, we define the subspace
H_{\Omega}({\mathbb{R}}^{N},K)
of
H({\mathbb{R}}^{N},K)
encoding the exterior homogeneous Dirichlet condition for
\Omega
, namely defined by means of

H_{\Omega}({\mathbb{R}}^{N},K)=\{u\in H({\mathbb{R}}^{N},K)\ :\ u=0\text{ a.e. }{\mathbb{R}}^{N}\setminus\Omega\}.

Under assumptions (
1.1
)-(
1.2
)-(
1.3
),
[
18
, Lemma 2.9]
guarantees that the following Poincaré-Friedrichs inequality holds:

||u||^{2}_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{N})}\leq C\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}K(x,y)\left(u(x)-u(y)\right)^{2}dx\,dy

(2.7)

where
C>0
is a proper constant, for all
u\in H_{\Omega}({\mathbb{R}}^{N},K)
. This allows to equip
H_{\Omega}({\mathbb{R}}^{N},K)
of the equivalent norm

||u||_{H_{\Omega}({\mathbb{R}}^{N},K)}=[u]_{H_{\Omega}({\mathbb{R}}^{N},K)}.

Then the operator
\mathcal{L}
is defined by duality on the space
H_{\Omega}({\mathbb{R}}^{N},K)
by the identity

\langle\mathcal{L}u,\varphi\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}K(x,y)\left(u(x)-u(y)\right)\left(\varphi(x)-\varphi(y)\right)dydx,

which defines
\mathcal{L}
as a linear, continuous operator from
H_{\Omega}({\mathbb{R}}^{N},K)
to its dual
(H_{\Omega}({\mathbb{R}}^{N},K))^{*}
.

Once this notation fixed, we are able to define the solutions of problem problem (
1.6
).

Definition 2.3

Let
K
be a nonnegative measurable function satisfying (
1.1
) and (
1.2
). For all
u,\varphi\in H_{\Omega}({\mathbb{R}}^{N},K)
, we define

\mathbb{E}(u,\varphi;K,c):=\frac{1}{2}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}K(x,y)\left(u(x)-u(y)\right)\left(\varphi(x)-\varphi(y)\right)dydx+\int_{\Omega}c(x)u(x)\varphi(x)dx.

Then
u\in H_{\Omega}({\mathbb{R}}^{N},K)
is called a weak solution of (
1.6
) if

\mathbb{E}(u,\varphi;K,c)=\int_{\Omega}f(x)\varphi(x)dx

(2.8)

for all
\varphi\in H_{\Omega}({\mathbb{R}}^{N},K)
.

The existence and uniqueness of the solutions of problem (
1.6
) have been extensively studied in many papers; particularly, we will refer to
[
18
, Proposition 3.4]
.

Proposition 2.4

Let
K
be a nonnegative measurable function satisying (
1.1
) and (
1.2
),
c\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
a nonnegative function,
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
. Then there exists a unique weak solution
u\in H_{\Omega}({\mathbb{R}}^{N},K)
to (
1.6
). Furthermore,
u
is characterized by the property:

\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}(u,u;K,c)-\int_{\Omega}f(x)u(x)dx=\min_{v\in H_{\Omega}({\mathbb{R}}^{N},K)}\left\{\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}(v,v;K,c)-\int_{\Omega}f(x)v(x)dx\right\}.

(2.9)

Proof.

It is easily seen that

\mathbb{E}(u,\varphi;K,c)\leq C_{1}[u]_{H_{\Omega}({\mathbb{R}}^{N},K)}\,[\varphi]_{H_{\Omega}({\mathbb{R}}^{N},K)}

for a proper positive constant
C_{1}
. On the other hand, by using the Poincaré-Friedrichs inequality (
2.7
), we have

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}(u,u;K,c)\geq\frac{C}{2}[u]^{2}_{H_{\Omega}({\mathbb{R}}^{N},K)}.\end{split}

Therefore by Lax-Milgram Lemma, there is a unique
u\in H_{\Omega}({\mathbb{R}}^{N},K)
such that

\mathbb{E}(u,\varphi;K,c)=\int_{\Omega}f(x)\varphi(x)dx\qquad\forall\varphi\in H_{\Omega}({\mathbb{R}}^{N},K),

that is the weak formulation (
2.8
). Finally, observing that
\mathbb{E}(u,\varphi;K,c)
is symmetric, then Lax-Milgram Lemma also implies (
2.9
).

\;\square

Furthermore, the following weak maximum principle is satisfied.

Proposition 2.5

Let
K
be a nonnegative measurable function satisying (
1.1
) and (
1.2
),
c\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
a nonnegative function,
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
and
u\in H_{\Omega}({\mathbb{R}}^{N},K)
satisfying

\begin{cases}\mathcal{L}u+cu=f\ &\text{in }\Omega,\\
u=0&\text{on }\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega.\end{cases}

If
f\leq 0
in
\Omega
,
then
u\leq 0
in
\Omega
.

Proof.

By choosing

\varphi(x)=u^{+}(x)=\begin{cases}u(x)\quad&\text{if }u(x)>0\\
0&\text{if }u(x)\leq 0,\end{cases}

in the weak formulation (
2.8
), we have

\begin{split}\frac{1}{2}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}K(x,y)\left(u(x)-u(y)\right)&\left(u^{+}(x)-u^{+}(y)\right)dydx\\
&+\int_{\Omega}c(x)u(x)u^{+}(x)dx=\int_{\Omega}f(x)u^{+}(x)\leq 0.\end{split}

(2.10)

We observe that each term in the l.h.s of (
2.10
) is nonnegative. Indeed, regarding the first integrand term we observe that

\left(u(x)-u(y)\right)\left(u^{+}(x)-u^{+}(y)\right)=\begin{cases}\left(u(y)-u(x)\right)^{2}\quad&\text{if }u(x)\geq 0\text{ and }u(y)\geq 0,\\
\left(u(x)-u(y)\right)u(x)\quad&\text{if }u(x)\geq 0\text{ and }u(y)\leq 0,\\
\left(u(y)-u(x)\right)u(y)\quad&\text{if }u(x)\leq 0\text{ and }u(y)\geq 0,\\
0&\text{if }u(x)\leq 0\text{ and }u(y)\leq 0;\end{cases}

the second integrand term is also nonnegative since can be written as
\int_{u\geq 0}c(x)u^{2}(x)dx
.

Therefore, the l.h.s of (
2.10
) is null and hence
u^{+}=0
.

\;\square

In order to prove a Pólya-Szegő type inequality, the following exponential integrability of the involved kernel will turn out essential.

Lemma 2.6

Let
J
be a nonnegative measurable function such that

x\mapsto\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}J(x-y)\min\{|x-y|^{2},1\}dy\in L_{loc}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{N}).

(2.11)

Then

e^{-\frac{t}{J(x)}}\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{N})\qquad\forall t\geq 0.

(2.12)

Proof.

Firstly, let us observe that the function
\min\{|x|^{2},1\}
is radially increasing. This means that
J^{\sharp}
also satisfies (
2.11
), in view of

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}J(x-y)\min\{|x-y|^{2},1\}dy\geq\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}J^{\sharp}(x-y)\min\{|x-y|^{2},1\}dy.

Therefore, it is sufficient to prove (
2.12
) when
J
is replaced by
J^{\sharp}
, since

\|e^{-\frac{t}{J^{\sharp}(\cdot)}}\|_{L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{N})}=\|e^{-\frac{t}{J(\cdot)}}\|_{L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{N})}.

(2.13)

Let us observe that
e^{-\frac{t}{J^{\sharp}(x)}}\leq 1
for any
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
and
t\geq 0
, that implies
e^{-\frac{t}{J^{\sharp}(x)}}\in L^{1}(B_{r})
for any
r>0
. Hence it remains to prove that
e^{-\frac{t}{J^{\sharp}(x)}}\in L^{1}(B_{r}^{c})
.

By assumption, we deduce that
\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}J^{\sharp}(x-y)\min\{|x-y|^{2},1\}dy<+\infty
for a.e.
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
; since this quantity is independent by
x
, we have that
\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}J^{\sharp}(y)\min\{|y|^{2},1\}dy<+\infty
and hence, for any
R
large enough, we have

\int_{B_{R}^{c}}J^{\sharp}(y)dy<+\infty.

Calling
\mathsf{j}
the radial profile of
J^{\sharp}
, the radial monotonicity of
J^{\sharp}
gives, for
\rho=|x|>R
,

\int_{R}^{\rho}r^{N-1}\mathsf{j}(r)dr\geq\mathsf{j}(\rho)\,\frac{\rho^{N}-R^{N}}{N}

that is

\mathsf{j}(\rho)\leq\frac{C}{\rho^{N}-R^{N}}\int_{B_{R}^{c}}J^{\sharp}(y)dy

thus, for some positive constant
C^{\prime}

e^{-\frac{t}{J^{\#}(x)}}\leq e^{-C^{\prime}t(|x|^{N}-R^{N})}\qquad\text{ as }|x|\rightarrow+\infty.

Therefore, it remains proven that
e^{-\frac{t}{J^{\sharp}(x)}}\in L^{1}(B_{R}^{c})
and hence, by (
2.13
),
we get the conclusion.

\;\square

Now, we state a Pólya-Szegő type inequality, that will be useful in deriving suitable an energy estimates.

Proposition 2.7

Let
K,J
be two nonnegative measurable functions satisfing the assumptions (
1.1
), (
1.2
), (
1.3
). Then, for any
u\in H_{\Omega}({\mathbb{R}}^{N},K)
, we have that
u^{\sharp}\in H_{\Omega^{\sharp}}({\mathbb{R}}^{N},J^{\sharp})
and

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}K(x,y)\left(u(x)-u(y)\right)^{2}dydx\geq\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}J^{\sharp}(x-y)\left(u^{\sharp}(x)-u^{\sharp}(y)\right)^{2}dydx

(2.14)

Proof.

Let us observe that by (
1.3
), we directly have

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}K(x,y)\left(u(x)-u(y)\right)^{2}dydx\geq\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}J(x-y)\left(u(x)-u(y)\right)^{2}dydx.

Using (
1.3
) and the arguments in
[
2
, Section 9]
, we have

\begin{split}&\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}J(x-y)\left(u(x)-u(y)\right)^{2}dydx=\int_{0}^{+\infty}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}e^{-\frac{t}{J(x-y)}}\left(u(x)-u(y)\right)^{2}dydxdt\\
&=\int_{0}^{+\infty}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}e^{-\frac{t}{J(x-y)}}u^{2}(x)dydxdt+\int_{0}^{+\infty}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}e^{-\frac{t}{J(x-y)}}u^{2}(y)dydxdt\\
&\qquad-2\int_{0}^{+\infty}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}e^{-\frac{t}{J(x-y)}}u(x)u(y)dydxdt.\end{split}

(2.15)

We observe that, in view of Lemma
2.6
, the function
e^{-\frac{t}{J(x)}}
belongs to
L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{N})
for any
t>0
. This means that we can use inequality (
2.6
) of Proposition
2.1
, with
F(u,v)=uv
,
a=1
,
b=-1
and
W(ax+by)=e^{-\frac{t}{J(x-y)}}
. Observing that
W^{\sharp}(x)=e^{-\frac{t}{J^{\sharp}(x)}}
, for the last term in (

) it holds

\int_{0}^{+\infty}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}e^{-\frac{t}{J(x-y)}}u(x)u(y)dy\,dx\,dt\leq\int_{0}^{+\infty}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}e^{-\frac{t}{J^{\sharp}(x-y)}}u^{\sharp}(x)u^{\sharp}(y)dy\,dx\,dt.

Using the same properties, we have that the first two term on the r.h.s. of (
2.15

2.14

\;\square

2.3
A coarea formula and a Maximum Principle

In this section, we recollect two result that, on the one hand, are very useful to prove the main Theorems, and, on the other hand, have an interest on their own.

Firstly, we prove a coarea formula; we adapt the proof from
[
7
, Lemma 10]
(and
[
43
]
) for the fractional Laplacian to our general case.

Proposition 2.9

Let
K
satisfy the assumptions (
1.1
) and (
1.2
), and
u
be a nonnegative measurable function such that

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}K(x,y)|u(x)-u(y)|dydx<+\infty.

Then

\frac{1}{2}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}K(x,y)|u(x)-u(y)|dydx=\int_{0}^{\sup u}\int_{u>t}\int_{u\leq t}K(x,y)dydxdt.

Proof.

Let us consider the function

t\in[0,\sup u]\mapsto\chi_{u>t}(x)-\chi_{u>t}(y)\in\{-1,0,1\}.

We have

|\chi_{u>t}(x)-\chi_{u>t}(y)|=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0&\text{if }t<\min\{u(x),\>u(y)\}\\
\\
0&\text{if }t\geq\max\{u(x),\>u(y)\}\\
\\
1&\text{if }\min\{u(x),\>u(y)\}\leq t<\max\{u(x),\>u(y)\},\end{array}\right.

(2.18)

and

|\chi_{u>t}(x)-\chi_{u>t}(y)|=\chi_{u>t}(x)\chi_{u\leq t}(y)+\chi_{u\leq t}(x)\chi_{u>t}(y).

(2.19)

It follows that

|u(x)-u(y)|=\int_{0}^{\sup u}|\chi_{u>t}(x)-\chi_{u>t}(y)|dt,

and, furthermore, by Fubini Theorem, we have

\begin{split}&\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}K(x,y)|u(x)-u(y)|dydx\\
&\quad=\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}K(x,y)\int_{0}^{\sup u}|\chi_{u>t}(x)-\chi_{u>t}(x)|dtdydx\\
&\quad=2\int_{0}^{u_{M}}\int_{u>t}\int_{u\leq t}K(x,y)dydxdt.\end{split}

\;\square

The following variant of the previous result involving the truncature of a suitable integrable function will be useful in the proof of the main Theorem.

Proposition 2.10

Let
K
satisfy the assumptions (
1.1
) and (
1.2
), and
u
be a nonnegative measurable function such that

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}K(x,y)(u(x)-u(y))^{2}dydx<+\infty.

Then, for every
t\geq 0
, we have

\begin{split}\frac{1}{2}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}K(x,y)(u(x)-u(y))\left((u(x)-t)^{+}-(u(y)-t)^{+}\right)dydx\\
=\int_{t}^{+\infty}\int_{u>\tau}\int_{u\leq\tau}K(x,y)(u(x)-u(y))dydxd\tau.\end{split}

(2.20)

Proof.

Let us consider, for
t\geq 0
, the function

A(t)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}K(x,y)\left(u(x)-u(y)\right)\left((u(x)-t)^{+}-(u(y)-t)^{+}\right)dydx,

that is finite because

\left(u(x)-u(y)\right)\left((u(x)-t)^{+}-(u(y)-t)^{+}\right)\leq\left(u(x)-u(y)\right)^{2}.

Using (
2.18
) and (
2.19
), we have

|(u(x)-t)^{+}-(u(y)-t)^{+}|=\int_{t}^{+\infty}\Bigl(\chi_{u\leq\tau}(x)\chi_{u>\tau}(y)+\chi_{u>\tau}(x)\chi_{u\leq\tau}(y)\Bigr)d\tau.

Therefore, we can write

\displaystyle A(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{t}^{+\infty}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}K(x,y)|u(x)-u(y)|\Bigl(\chi_{u\leq\tau}(x)\chi_{u>\tau}(y)+\chi_{u>\tau}(x)\chi_{u\leq\tau}(y)\Bigr)dydxd\tau

\displaystyle=\int_{t}^{+\infty}\Biggl(\int_{u(x)\leq\tau}\int_{u(y)>\tau}K(x,y)|u(x)-u(y)|dydx+

\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{u(x)>\tau}\int_{u(y)\leq\tau}K(x,y)|u(x)-u(y)|dydx\Biggr)dt=

\displaystyle=2\int_{t}^{+\infty}\int_{u(x)>\tau}\int_{u(y)\leq\tau}K(x,y)\bigl(u(x)-u(y)\bigr)dydxd\tau.

Therefore (
2.20
) is proved.

\;\square

[

, whose proof is mainly based on

and

.

Proposition 2.11

Let
u,v
be two nonnegative, radial and summable functions on
B_{R}
. Let us assume that the function

r\in[0,R]\mapsto\int_{B_{r}}(u(x)-v(x))dx

admits a positive maximum point at
\bar{r}>0
, that is,

0<\int_{B_{\bar{r}}}(u(x)-v(x))dx=\max_{r\in[0,R]}\left(\int_{B_{r}}(u(x)-v(x))dx\right).

Then, if
\mathfrak{h}_{1}
is a positive radially increasing function such that
(u(x)-v(x))\mathfrak{h}_{1}(x)
is summable on

, we have

\int_{B_{\bar{r}}}(u(x)-v(x))\mathfrak{h}_{1}(x)dx>0.

Analogously, let us assume that the function

0>\int_{B_{R}\setminus B_{\bar{r}}}(u(x)-v(x))dx=\min_{r\in[0,R]}\left(\int_{B_{R}\setminus B_{r}}(u(x)-v(x))dx\right).

Then, if
\mathfrak{h}_{2}

is summable on

\int_{B_{R}\setminus B_{\bar{r}}}(u(x)-v(x))\mathfrak{h}_{2}(x)dx<0.

2.4
A Monotonicity Result

In this section, we give a monotonicity result that is a key tool to prove the main Theorem. Firstly, for any
r>0
, let us set

\displaystyle\Phi_{1}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{B_{r}^{c}}J^{\sharp}(x-y)dy=\int_{B_{r}^{c}(x)}J^{\sharp}(y)dy\qquad|x|<r,

(2.21)

\displaystyle\Phi_{2}(y)

\displaystyle=\int_{B_{r}}J^{\sharp}(x-y)dx=\int_{B_{r}(y)}J^{\sharp}(x)dx\qquad|y|>r.

(2.22)

We observe that under the assumptions made on
J^{\sharp}
, the functions
\Phi_{1}
and
\Phi_{2}
are well defined.
Indeed, as observed in the proof of Lemma
2.12
, we have

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}J^{\sharp}(y)\min\{|y|^{2},1\}dy<+\infty.

Then,
J^{\sharp}
is summable in
B_{r}^{c}(x)
when
|x|<r
because
B_{r}^{c}(x)
does not contain a suitable neighborhood of the origin. Similarly,
J^{\sharp}
is summable in
B_{r}(y)
when
|y|>r
for the same reason.

Our aim is to prove that the function
\Phi_{1}
is radially increasing and that the function
\Phi_{2}
is radially decreasing with respect to any direction passing through the origin. To gain this result, we first give the following.

Lemma 2.12

Let us fix
r>0
, then for any
\rho
,
\rho^{\prime}
such that
0<\rho<\rho^{\prime}
, we have:

\max_{\overline{B_{r}(\rho e_{1})\setminus B_{r}(\rho^{\prime}e_{1})}}|y|^{2}\leq\min_{\overline{B_{r}(\rho^{\prime}e_{1})\setminus B_{r}(\rho e_{1})}}|y|^{2},

(2.23)

where
e_{1}
is the first vector of the standard basis.

Furthermore, if
\rho^{\prime}-\rho\leq 2r
, then

\max_{\overline{B_{r}(\rho e_{1})\setminus B_{r}(\rho^{\prime}e_{1})}}|y|^{2}=\min_{\overline{B_{r}(\rho^{\prime}e_{1})\setminus B_{r}(\rho e_{1})}}|y|^{2}=r^{2}+\rho\rho^{\prime}.

(2.24)

Proof.

Let us observe that in the case where
\rho^{\prime}-\rho>2r
the balls
B_{r}(\rho e_{1})
and
B_{r}(\rho^{\prime}e_{1})
are disjoint, so:

\max_{\overline{B_{r}(\rho e_{1})\setminus B_{r}(\rho^{\prime}e_{1})}}|y|^{2}=\max_{\overline{B_{r}(\rho e_{1})}}|y|^{2}=(\rho+r)^{2},

and

\min_{\overline{B_{r}(\rho^{\prime}e_{1})\setminus B_{r}(\rho e_{1})}}|y|^{2}=\min_{\overline{B_{r}(\rho^{\prime}e_{1})}}|y|^{2}=(\rho^{\prime}-r)^{2}.

Hence, the inequality (
2.23
) follows because

(\rho+r)^{2}-(\rho^{\prime}-r)^{2}=(\rho^{\prime}+\rho)(\rho^{\prime}-\rho-2r)>0.

So, in order to complete the proof, we have to prove (
2.24
) when
\rho^{\prime}-\rho\leq 2r
.

We firstly observe that

y\in\overline{B_{r}(\rho e_{1})\setminus B_{r}(\rho^{\prime}e_{1})}\ \ \Longrightarrow\begin{cases}|y-\rho e_{1}|^{2}\leq r^{2}\\
|y-\rho^{\prime}e_{1}|^{2}\geq r^{2}\end{cases}\Longrightarrow\begin{cases}&|y|^{2}\leq r^{2}+2y_{1}\rho-\rho^{2}\\
&|y|^{2}\geq r^{2}+2y_{1}\rho^{\prime}-\rho^{\prime 2}.\end{cases}

(2.25)

By subtracting each other the two estimates above, we have that

2y_{1}(\rho-\rho^{\prime})-\rho^{2}+\rho^{\prime 2}\geq 0

and consequently that

y_{1}\leq\frac{\rho+\rho^{\prime}}{2}.

Moreover, from the first inequality in (
2.25
), it follows that

|y|^{2}\leq r^{2}+\rho\rho^{\prime}.

Observing that the points
y\in\mathbb{{\mathbb{R}}}^{N}
such that

|y|^{2}=r^{2}+\rho\rho^{\prime}\qquad\text{and}\qquad y_{1}=\frac{\rho+\rho^{\prime}}{2}

belong to
\overline{B_{r}(\rho e_{1})\setminus B_{r}(\rho^{\prime}e_{1})}
we have that

\max_{\overline{B_{r}(\rho e_{1})\setminus B_{r}(\rho^{\prime}e_{1})}}|y|^{2}=r^{2}+\rho\rho^{\prime}.

(2.26)

As regards the minimum in (
2.24
), we observe that, for any
y\in\overline{B_{r}(\rho^{\prime}e_{1})\setminus B_{r}(\rho e_{1})}
, the inequalities in (
2.25
) hold with the reverse order relation. By proceeding as before, we get

|y|^{2}\geq r^{2}+\rho\rho^{\prime}\qquad\text{and}\qquad y_{1}\geq\frac{\rho+\rho^{\prime}}{2},

and, hence, we have

\min_{\overline{B_{r}(\rho^{\prime}e_{1})\setminus B_{r}(\rho e_{1})}}|y|^{2}=r^{2}+\rho\rho^{\prime}.

(2.27)

Finally, the equality chain (
2.24
) follows by (
2.26
) and (
2.27
).

\;\square

At this point, we are in position to state the monotonicity results for (
2.21
) and (
2.22
).

Proposition 2.13

Let
r>0
, then

•

\Phi_{1}(x)
is radially increasing for any
|x|<r
;

•

\Phi_{2}(y)
is radially decreasing for any
|y|>r
.

Proof.

We firstly observe that
\Phi_{i}
,
i=1,2
, are radial. Indeed, for any
x_{1},x_{2}
such that
|x_{1}|=|x_{2}|
, there exists a orthogonal rotation matrix
G
, such that
x_{2}=G(x_{1})
and
\det|G|=1
. By using the change of variables
z=G(y)
, we obtain

\Phi_{1}(x_{1})=\int_{B_{r}^{c}}J^{\sharp}(G(x_{1}-y))dy=\int_{B_{r}^{c}}J^{\sharp}(x_{2}-y)dy=\Phi_{1}(x_{2}).

Similarly, for
\Phi_{2}
we have

\Phi_{2}(y_{1})=\int_{B_{r}}J^{\sharp}(G(x-y_{1}))dx=\int_{B_{r}}J^{\sharp}(x-y_{2})dx=\Phi_{1}(y_{2}),

and therefore both
\Phi_{1}
and
\Phi_{2}
are radial.

Being
J^{\sharp}
a radial function, it immediately follows that
\Phi_{1}(x)
is a radial function, that is
\Phi_{1}(x)=\Phi_{1}(|x|)
, for any
|x|<r
.

Therefore, we can study the monotonicity of
\Phi_{1}
along a fixed direction passing through the origin. Namely, we will prove that for any
0<\rho<\rho^{\prime}<r
, we have
\Phi_{1}(\rho e_{1})\leq\Phi_{1}(\rho^{\prime}e_{1})
, where
e_{1}
is the first vector of the standard basis.

Let us observe that since
\rho<\rho^{\prime}<r
, the sets
B_{r}(\rho^{\prime}e_{1})\setminus B_{r}(\rho e_{1})
and
B_{r}(\rho e_{1})\setminus B_{r}(\rho^{\prime}e_{1})
are not empty and have the same measure. We have that

\begin{split}\Phi_{1}(\rho e_{1})-\Phi_{1}(\rho^{\prime}e_{1})&=\int_{B_{r}^{c}(\rho e_{1})}J^{\sharp}(y)dy-\int_{B_{r}^{c}(\rho^{\prime}e_{1})}J^{\sharp}(y)dy=\\
&=\int_{B_{r}(\rho^{\prime}e_{1})\setminus B_{r}(\rho e_{1})}J^{\sharp}(y)dy-\int_{B_{r}(\rho e_{1})\setminus B_{r}(\rho^{\prime}e_{1})}J^{\sharp}(y)dy\\
&\leq\left(\max_{\overline{B_{r}(\rho^{\prime}e_{1})\setminus B_{r}(\rho e_{1})}}J^{\sharp}(y)-\min_{\overline{B_{r}(\rho e_{1})\setminus B_{r}(\rho^{\prime}e_{1})}}J^{\sharp}(y)\right)|B_{r}(\rho^{\prime}e_{1})\setminus B_{r}(\rho e_{1})|\leq 0,\end{split}

(2.28)

in view of Lemma
2.12
. Indeed, we notice that since

is radially decreasing, the minimum of

is achieved in points
\overline{\overline{y}}\in\overline{B_{r}(\rho e_{1})\setminus B_{r}(\rho^{\prime}e_{1})}
such that

|\overline{\overline{y}}|^{2}=\max_{\overline{B_{r}(\rho e_{1})\setminus B_{r}(\rho^{\prime}e_{1})}}|y|^{2},

while the maximum of
J^{\sharp}
in (
2.28
) is achieved in the point
\bar{y}\in\overline{B_{r}(\rho^{\prime}e_{1})\setminus B_{r}(\rho e_{1})}
such that

|\bar{y}|^{2}=\min_{\overline{B_{r}(\rho^{\prime}e_{1})\setminus B_{r}(\rho e_{1})}}|y|^{2}.

Therefore, the desired monotonicity result follows from Lemma
2.12
where we have proven that

\max_{\overline{B_{r}(\rho e_{1})\setminus B_{r}(\rho^{\prime}e_{1})}}|y|^{2}=\min_{\overline{B_{r}(\rho^{\prime}e_{1})\setminus B_{r}(\rho e_{1})}}|y|^{2}.

Indeed, from this property follows that for the choice of the points

and
y_{2}
as above, we have

and thus

) vanishes (see also Figure

We have

\begin{split}\Phi_{2}(\rho e_{1})-\Phi_{2}(\rho^{\prime}e_{1})&=\int_{B_{r}(\rho e_{1})}J^{\sharp}(x)dx-\int_{B_{r}(\rho^{\prime}e_{1})}J^{\sharp}(x)dx=\\

At this stage, for any

is monotone decreasing in view of the fact that, for

(3.8)

and then

Putting

\displaystyle I(h)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2h}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}J^{\sharp}(x-y)\left(u^{\sharp}(x)-u^{\sharp}(y)\right)\left(\mathcal{G}_{t,h}(u^{\sharp}(x))-\mathcal{G}_{t,h}(u^{\sharp}(y))\right)dydx

On the other hand, by Lemma

\displaystyle\Psi(t)=2\int_{t}^{+\infty}\int_{u^{\sharp}(x)>\tau}\int_{u^{\sharp}(y)\leq\tau}J^{\sharp}(x-y)\bigl(u^{\sharp}(x)-u^{\sharp}(y)\bigr)dydxd\tau,

Taking into account (

\begin{split}&\frac{1}{2}\int_{u^{\sharp}(x)>t}\int_{u^{\sharp}(y)\leq t}J^{\sharp}(x-y)\left(u^{\sharp}(x)-u^{\sharp}(y)\right)dydx\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+\int_{u^{\sharp}>t}c_{\sharp}(x)u^{\sharp}(x)dx\leq\int_{u^{\sharp}>t}f^{\sharp}(x)dx.\end{split}

Hence, for any

Let us observe that if

)

Step 2.

The comparison result.

As regards the weak solution
v
to problem (

), we observe that, integrating the equation on a generic ball

, we have

\frac{1}{2}\int_{B_{r}}\int_{B_{r}^{c}}J^{\sharp}(x-y)\left(v(x)-v(y)\right)dydx+\int_{B_{r}}c_{\sharp}(x)v(x)dx=\int_{B_{r}}f^{\sharp}(x)dx.

(3.11)

By (

) and (
3.11
), for any
r\in[0,+\infty[
such that
r=r_{u}(t)
or
r=r_{u}(t^{-})
for some

, it follows that

\begin{split}\frac{1}{2}\int_{B_{r}}\int_{B_{r}^{c}}J^{\sharp}(x-y)\left(u^{\sharp}(x)-u^{\sharp}(y)\right)dydx-\frac{1}{2}\int_{B_{r}}\int_{B_{r}^{c}}J^{\sharp}(x-y)\left(v(x)-v(y)\right)dydx\\
+\int_{B_{r}}c_{\sharp}(x)(u^{\sharp}(x)-v(x))dx\leq 0.\end{split}

(3.12)

At this stage, it remains to prove that

\int_{B_{r}}u^{\sharp}(x)dx\leq\int_{B_{r}}v(x)dx\qquad\forall r\geq 0.

Let us suppose, by contradiction, that the function

r\mapsto\int_{B_{r}}u^{\sharp}(x)\,dx-\int_{B_{r}}v(x)\,dx

(3.13)

has a positive maximum point at
\bar{r}\in(0,R]
, i.e.,

0<\int_{B_{\bar{r}}}u^{\sharp}(x)\,dx-\int_{B_{\bar{r}}}v(x)\,dx=\max_{r\in[0,R]}\left(\int_{B_{r}}u^{\sharp}(x)\,dx-\int_{B_{r}}v(x)\,dx\right).

(3.14)

We observe that the function defined in (
3.13
) can be written as a function of the variable
s=\omega_{N}r^{N}

\int_{B_{r}}u^{\sharp}(x)\,dx-\int_{B_{r}}v(x)\,dx=\int_{0}^{s}u^{*}(\sigma)\,d\sigma-\int_{0}^{s}v^{*}(\sigma)\,d\sigma

and the function

s\mapsto\int_{0}^{s}u^{*}(\sigma)\,d\sigma-\int_{0}^{s}v^{*}(\sigma)\,d\sigma

(3.15)

is convex in any interval where
u^{*}
is constant. Thus, if for some
t\in[0,\sup u]
we have
r_{u}(t)<r_{u}(t^{-})
, then the function defined in (
3.15
) attains its maximum on
\big[\omega_{N}\big(r_{u}(t)\big)^{N},\>\allowbreak\omega_{N}\big(r_{u}(t^{-})\big)^{N}\big]
at the boundary. It follows that also the function defined in (
3.13
) attains its maximum on
[r_{u}(t),\>r_{u}(t^{-})]
at the boundary.

In view of the above observation, we have that the value
\bar{r}
can be chosen in such a way that for some

, then either
\bar{r}=r_{u}(t)
or
\bar{r}=r_{u}(t^{-})
.

We also observe that at the point
\bar{r}
the function

r\mapsto\int_{B_{r}^{c}}u^{\sharp}(x)\,dx-\int_{B_{r}^{c}}v(x)\,dx

admits a nonpositive minimum point, that is,

0\geq\int_{B_{\bar{r}}^{c}}u^{\sharp}(x)\,dx-\int_{B_{\bar{r}}^{c}}v(x)\,dx=\min_{r\in[0,R]}\left(\int_{B_{r}^{c}}u^{\sharp}(x)\,dx-\int_{B_{r}^{c}}v(x)\,dx\right).

(3.16)

Indeed, for every
r\in[0,R]
,

\displaystyle\int_{B_{r}^{c}}u^{\sharp}(x)\,dx-\int_{B_{r}^{c}}v(x)\,dx=\int_{B_{R}}\bigl(u^{\sharp}(x)-v(x)\bigr)\,dx-\int_{B_{r}}\bigl(u^{\sharp}(x)-v(x)\bigr)\,dx

\displaystyle\qquad\geq\int_{B_{R}}\bigl(u^{\sharp}(x)-v(x)\bigr)\,dx-\int_{B_{\bar{r}}}\bigl(u^{\sharp}(x)-v(x)\bigr)\,dx=\int_{B_{\bar{r}}^{c}}u^{\sharp}(x)\,dx-\int_{B_{\bar{r}}^{c}}v(x)\,dx

where the right-hand side in the above inequality is nonpositive because
\bar{r}
is a maximum point for the function (
3.13
).

We have

\begin{split}&\int_{B_{\bar{r}}}\int_{B_{\bar{r}}^{c}}J^{\sharp}(x-y)\bigl(u^{\sharp}(x)-u^{\sharp}(y)\bigr)dydx=\\
&=\int_{|x^{\prime}|=1}\left(\int_{0}^{\bar{r}}\left(\int_{\bar{r}}^{+\infty}\left(\int_{|y^{\prime}|=1}J^{\sharp}(\rho x^{\prime}-\tau y^{\prime})\bigl(u^{\sharp}(\rho)-u^{\sharp}(\tau)\bigr)dH^{N-1}(y^{\prime})\right)\right.\right.\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad\tau^{N-1}d\tau\biggr)\rho^{N-1}d\rho\biggr)dH^{N-1}(x^{\prime})\\
&=\int_{|x^{\prime}|=1}\left(\int_{0}^{\bar{r}}\left(\Phi_{1}(\rho)u^{\sharp}(\rho)\rho^{N-1}\right.\right.\\
&\quad\left.\left.-\rho^{N-1}\int_{\bar{r}}^{+\infty}\left(\int_{|y^{\prime}|=1}J^{\sharp}(\rho x^{\prime}-\tau y^{\prime})u^{\sharp}(\tau)\,dH^{N-1}(y^{\prime})\right)\tau^{N-1}d\tau\right)d\rho\right)dH^{N-1}(x^{\prime}).\end{split}

(3.17)

Let us observe that, in view of (
3.14
),
we have

\displaystyle\Phi_{1}(\rho)u^{\sharp}(\rho)\rho^{N-1}=\left(-\Phi_{1}(\rho)\int_{\rho}^{\bar{r}}u^{\sharp}(\sigma)\sigma^{N-1}d\sigma\right)^{\prime}+\Phi_{1}^{\prime}(\rho)\int_{\rho}^{\bar{r}}u^{\sharp}(\sigma)\sigma^{N-1}d\sigma

\displaystyle\qquad\geq\left(-\Phi_{1}(\rho)\int_{\rho}^{\bar{r}}u^{\sharp}(\sigma)\sigma^{N-1}d\sigma\right)^{\prime}+\Phi^{\prime}_{1}(\rho)\int_{\rho}^{\bar{r}}v(\sigma)\sigma^{N-1}d\sigma,

where we have used that
\Phi_{1}^{\prime}(\rho)\geq 0
and the fact that being
\bar{r}
a maximum point for the function (
3.13
) we have

\int_{\rho}^{\bar{r}}u^{\sharp}(\sigma)\sigma^{N-1}d\sigma\geq\int_{\rho}^{\bar{r}}v(\sigma)\sigma^{N-1}d\sigma.

Moreover, we also have that

\int_{0}^{\bar{r}}\left(-\Phi_{1}(\rho)\int_{\rho}^{\bar{r}}u^{\sharp}(\sigma)\sigma^{N-1}d\sigma\right)^{\prime}d\rho=\Phi_{1}(0)\int_{0}^{\bar{r}}u^{\sharp}(\sigma)\sigma^{N-1}d\sigma>\Phi_{1}(0)\int_{0}^{\bar{r}}v(\sigma)\sigma^{N-1}d\sigma.

Therefore, by using this inequality in (
3.17
), we have

\displaystyle\int_{B_{\bar{r}}}\int_{B_{\bar{r}}^{c}}J^{\sharp}(x-y)\bigl(u^{\sharp}(x)-u^{\sharp}(y)\bigr)dydx

\displaystyle>\int_{|x^{\prime}|=1}\left(\int_{0}^{\bar{r}}\left(\Phi_{1}(\rho)v(\rho)\rho^{N-1}\right.\right.

\displaystyle\quad\left.\left.-\rho^{N-1}\int_{\bar{r}}^{+\infty}\left(\int_{|y^{\prime}|=1}J^{\sharp}(\rho x^{\prime}-\tau y^{\prime})u^{\sharp}(\tau)\,dH^{N-1}(y^{\prime})\right)\tau^{N-1}d\tau\right)d\rho\right)dH^{N-1}(x^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\int_{B_{\bar{r}}}\int_{B_{\bar{r}}^{c}}J^{\sharp}(x-y)\bigl(v(x)-u^{\sharp}(y)\bigr)dydx.

We can repeat a similar argument for the integral on
B_{\bar{r}}^{c}

\begin{split}&\int_{B_{\bar{r}}}\int_{B_{\bar{r}}^{c}}J^{\sharp}(x-y)\bigl(v(x)-u^{\sharp}(y)\bigr)dydx=\\
&=\int_{|y^{\prime}|=1}\left(\int_{\bar{r}}^{+\infty}\left(\int_{0}^{\bar{r}}\left(\int_{|x^{\prime}|=1}J^{\sharp}(\rho x^{\prime}-\tau y^{\prime})\bigl(v(\rho)-u^{\sharp}(\tau)\bigr)dH^{N-1}(x^{\prime})\right)\right.\right.\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\rho^{N-1}d\rho\biggr)\tau^{N-1}d\tau\biggr)dH^{N-1}(y^{\prime})\\
&=\int_{|y^{\prime}|=1}\left(\int_{\bar{r}}^{+\infty}\left(\int_{0}^{\bar{r}}\left(\int_{|x^{\prime}|=1}J^{\sharp}(\rho x^{\prime}-\tau y^{\prime})v(\rho)dH^{N-1}(x^{\prime})\right)\rho^{N-1}d\rho\right)\tau^{N-1}\right.\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad-\Phi_{2}(\tau)u^{\sharp}(\tau)\tau^{N-1}\biggr)d\tau\biggr)dH^{N-1}(y^{\prime}).\end{split}

(3.18)

Indeed, let us observe that, in view of (
3.16
), we have

\displaystyle\Phi_{2}(\tau)u^{\sharp}(\tau)\tau^{N-1}=\left(\Phi_{2}(\tau)\int_{\bar{r}}^{\tau}u^{\sharp}(\sigma)\sigma^{N-1}d\sigma\right)^{\prime}-\Phi_{2}^{\prime}(\tau)\int_{\bar{r}}^{\tau}u^{\sharp}(\sigma)\sigma^{N-1}d\sigma\leq

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\left(\Phi_{2}(\tau)\int_{\bar{r}}^{\tau}u^{\sharp}(\sigma)\sigma^{N-1}d\sigma\right)^{\prime}-\Phi^{\prime}_{2}(\tau)\int_{\bar{r}}^{\tau}v(\sigma)\sigma^{N-1}d\sigma.

where we have used that
\Phi_{2}^{\prime}(\tau)\leq 0
, and we also have that

\int_{\bar{r}}^{R}\left(\Phi_{2}(\tau)\int_{\bar{r}}^{\tau}u^{\sharp}(\sigma)\sigma^{N-1}d\sigma\right)^{\prime}d\rho=\Phi_{2}(R)\int_{\bar{r}}^{R}u^{\sharp}(\sigma)\sigma^{N-1}d\sigma\leq\Phi_{2}(R)\int_{\bar{r}}^{R}v(\sigma)\sigma^{N-1}d\sigma.

Therefore, by using this inequality in (
3.18
), we have

\begin{split}&\int_{B_{\bar{r}}}\int_{B_{\bar{r}}^{c}}J^{\sharp}(x-y)\bigl(v(x)-u^{\sharp}(y)\bigr)dydx\geq\\
&\geq\int_{|y^{\prime}|=1}\left(\int_{\bar{r}}^{+\infty}\left(\int_{0}^{\bar{r}}\left(\int_{|x^{\prime}|=1}J^{\sharp}(\rho x^{\prime}-\tau y^{\prime})v(\rho)dH^{N-1}(x^{\prime})\right)\rho^{N-1}d\rho\right)\tau^{N-1}\right.\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad-\Phi_{2}(\rho)v(\tau)\tau^{N-1}\biggr)d\tau\biggr)dH^{N-1}(y^{\prime})\\
&=\int_{B_{\bar{r}}}\int_{B_{\bar{r}}^{c}}J^{\sharp}(x-y)\bigl(v(x)-v(y)\bigr)dydx.\end{split}

(3.19)

Hence, by joining (
3.17
), (
3.18
) and (

), we have then proved

\int_{B_{\bar{r}}}\int_{B_{\bar{r}}^{c}}J^{\sharp}(x-y)\bigl(u^{\sharp}(x)-u^{\sharp}(y)\bigr)dydx>\int_{B_{\bar{r}}}\int_{B_{\bar{r}}^{c}}J^{\sharp}(x-y)\bigl(v(x)-v(y)\bigr)dydx.

Finally, by using Proposition
2.11
, we have

\int_{B_{\bar{r}}}c_{\sharp}(x)(u^{\sharp}(x)-v(x))dx>0,

that contradicts (
3.12
) at
r=\bar{r}
.

Step 3.

The general case.

If there are no assumptions on the sign of

, Theorem
1.1
can be applied to the weak solution
w
to the equation

\mathcal{L}w+cw=|f|\ \text{ in }\Omega.

(3.20)

On the other hand, we know that

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}u+cu=f\ \text{ in }\Omega.

(3.21)

By the linearity of the operator

, by subtracting (
3.20
) to (
3.21
) we have

\mathcal{L}(u-w)+c(u-w)=f-|f|\leq 0\ \text{ in }\Omega.

By Proposition
2.5
, we deduce that
u-w\leq 0
in
\Omega
, that is

u\leq w\ \text{ in }\Omega.

(3.22)

Furthermore, by summing (
3.20
)
and (
3.21
), we have

\mathcal{L}(u+w)+c(u+w)=f+|f|\geq 0\ \text{ in }\Omega.

Again Proposition
2.5
implies that
u+w\geq 0
in
\Omega
, that is

-w\leq u\ \text{ in }\Omega.

(3.23)

Therefore (
3.22
) and (
3.23
) implies that
|u|\leq w
. Since (
1.8
) holds for

in place of

, we have

\int_{B_{r}}u^{\sharp}(x)dx\leq\int_{B_{r}}w^{\sharp}(x)dx\leq\int_{B_{r}}v(x)dx\qquad\forall r\geq 0,

that gives the conclusion.

Step 4.

The energy estimate (

).

By using the weak formulation (

) of problems (

) and (

) with

and
\varphi=v
, respectively, and employing Proposition
2.2
(ii), we have

\begin{split}&\frac{1}{2}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}K(x,y)\left(u(x)-u(y)\right)^{2}dydx+\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}c(x)u^{2}(x)dx=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}f(x)u(x)dx\\
&\quad\leq\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}f^{\sharp}(x)v^{2}(x)dx=\frac{1}{2}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}J^{\sharp}(x-y)\left(v(x)-v(y)\right)dydx+\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}c_{\sharp}(x)v^{2}(x)dx.\end{split}

Remark 3.1

We also observe that from the proof of Theorem
1.1
that (
1.8
) still holds when the symmetric rearrangement
f^{\sharp}
in problem (
1.7
) is replaced by a radially decreasing function
g

more concentrated than
f
,
i.e.
when

\int_{B_{r}}f^{\sharp}(x)dx\leq\int_{B_{r}}g(x)dx\qquad\forall r>0.

Remark 3.2

The main Theorem
1.1
gives us the possibility to transfer the study of the
L^{p}
regularity scale of the solution
u
to (
1.6
) to the same regularity for the solution
v
to the radial problem (
1.7
). For instance, assume that
\mathcal{L}
is a stable-like operator, whose kernel
K(x,y)=\mathsf{K}(|x-y|)
, where
\mathsf{K}
satisfies (
1.14
). Then
\mathcal{L}^{\sharp}=(-\Delta)^{s}
and we can take the advantage that
v
can be written in the integral form in terms of the explicit Green function of the fractional Laplacian on the ball, see the proof of
[
21
, Th. 3.2]
. More precisely, we have that for any
N\geq 2
,
f\in L^{p}(\Omega)
, with
p\geq 2N/(N+2s)
, the following properties hold:

1.

if
p<N/(2s)
then
u\in L^{q}(\Omega)
, with

q=\frac{Np}{N-2sp}

and there exists a constant
C
such that:

\|u\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}\leq C\|f\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)};

2.

if
p>N/(2s)
then
u\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
and there exists a constant
C
such that:

\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq C\|f\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)};

3.

if
p=N/(2s)
, then
u\in L_{\Phi_{p}(\Omega)}
and there exists a constant
C
such that:

\|u\|_{L_{\Phi_{p}(\Omega)}}\leq C\|f\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)},

where
L_{\Phi_{p}(\Omega)}
is the Orlicz space generated by the
N
-function

\Phi_{p}(t)=\exp(|t|^{p^{\prime}})-1.

Similar arguments can be used also in case
N=1
.

4
Concentration comparisons for linear parabolic problems

We extend our results to the parabolic case, as stated in (
1.12
), by using the method of discretization in time by induction along with the implicit Euler method
[
29
]
.

Moreover, to give a weak formulation of problem (
1.10
), we introduce the following Banach space

\mathcal{W}(0,T;H_{\Omega}({\mathbb{R}}^{N},K)):=\{u\in L^{2}(0,T;H_{\Omega}({\mathbb{R}}^{N},K))\ :\ u_{t}\in L^{2}(0,T;H_{\Omega}(K,{\mathbb{R}}^{N})^{\prime})\},

endowed with the norm

||u||_{\mathcal{W}(0,T;H_{\Omega}({\mathbb{R}}^{N},K))}=\int_{0}^{T}||u(x;t)||^{2}_{H_{\Omega}({\mathbb{R}}^{N},K)}dt+\int_{0}^{T}||u_{t}(x;t)||^{2}_{H_{\Omega}({\mathbb{R}}^{N},K)^{*}}dt.

Once fixed the notations, we give the following definition.

Definition 4.1

Let
u_{0}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
,
c\in L^{\infty}(\Omega\times(0,T))
be a nonnegative function,
f\in L^{2}(\Omega\times(0,T))
and
u_{0}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
. We say that
u\in\mathcal{W}(0,T;H_{\Omega}({\mathbb{R}}^{N},K))
is a weak solution of (
1.10
) if for every
\varphi\in H_{\Omega}({\mathbb{R}}^{N},K)
, we have

\begin{split}<u_{t}(t),\varphi>_{H_{\Omega}({\mathbb{R}}^{N},K)^{{}^{\prime}}}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}&\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}K(x,y)\left(u(x,t)-u(y,t)\right)\left(\varphi(x)-\varphi(y)\right)dx\,dy\\
&+\int_{\Omega}c(x,t)u(x,t)\varphi(x)dx=\int_{\Omega}f(x,t)\varphi(x)dx\quad\text{ a.e. }t\in(0,T),\end{split}

and
u(0,x)=u_{0}(x)
a.e. in
\Omega
.

At this stage, we give the proof of the main Theorem in the parabolic case.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Theorem
[
18
, Th.5.3]
assures the existence and the uniqueness of problems (
1.10
) and (
1.11
) in the sense of Definition
4.1
. Our aim now is first to introduce the
implicit time discretization scheme
, which shall provides the existence of suitable approximating solutions, to which the elliptic concentration comparison Theorem (
1.1
) can be applied. The main weak convergences in the approximation procedures will allow to pass to the limit in the final mass concentration estimate.
We divide the proof in three steps.

Step 1.

The implicit time discretization scheme.

Let us fix
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
define
\Delta t:=\frac{T}{N}
,
t_{n}:=n\Delta t
, for
n=0,...,N
, and

c_{n}(x):=\frac{1}{\Delta t}\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n+1}}c(x,\tau)d\tau\qquad f_{n}(x):=\frac{1}{\Delta t}\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n+1}}f(x,\tau)d\tau\qquad x\in\Omega,\ \ n=0,...,N-1.

Let us consider the problem where the time derivative
u_{t}
is replaced by a difference quotient

\begin{cases}\mathcal{L}u_{n+1}+c_{n}u_{n+1}+\dfrac{u_{n+1}}{\Delta t}=f_{n}+\dfrac{u_{n}}{\Delta t}\quad&\text{in }\Omega\\
u_{n+1}\in H_{\Omega}({\mathbb{R}}^{N},K),\end{cases}

(4.1)

where
n=0,...,N-1
and
u_{0}=u_{0}(x)
is given in the initial conditions.
Moreover, let us set

d_{n}=(c_{n})_{\sharp}\quad\text{in }\Omega\\

and

g_{n}=f_{n}^{\sharp}\quad\text{in }\Omega\\

and consider the corresponding symmetrized problems

\begin{cases}\mathcal{L}^{\sharp}v_{n+1}+d_{n}v_{n+1}+\dfrac{v_{n+1}}{\Delta t}=g_{n}+\dfrac{v_{n}}{\Delta t}\quad&\text{in }\Omega^{\sharp}\\
v_{n+1}\in H_{\Omega^{\sharp}}({\mathbb{R}}^{N},K),\end{cases}

(4.2)

with
v_{0}=u_{0}^{\sharp}(x)
.
Now we define the piecewise constant interpolations

u_{N}(x,t)=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}u_{n+1}(x)\chi_{[t_{n},t_{n+1}]}(t),\quad v_{N}(x,t)=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}v_{n+1}(x)\chi_{[t_{n},t_{n+1}]}(t),

c_{N}(x,t)=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}c_{n}(x)\chi_{[t_{n},t_{n+1}]}(t),\quad d_{N}(x,t)=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}d_{n}(x)\chi_{[t_{n},t_{n+1}]}(t),

f_{N}(x,t)=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}f_{n}(x)\chi_{[t_{n},t_{n+1}]}(t),\quad g_{N}(x,t)=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}g_{n}(x)\chi_{[t_{n},t_{n+1}]}(t).

Step 2.

Convergence of the approximating sequences.

We show that
\{u_{N}\}_{N\in{\mathbb{N}}}
and
\{v_{N}\}_{N\in{\mathbb{N}}}
converge to the weak solutions
u
and
v
of problems (
1.10
) and (
1.11
). Though the argument is rather classical and is based on achieving suitable discrete energy estimates, we reproduce here the main steps for the sake of completeness. We write problem (
4.1
) for
n=k
,
n\leq N
and test with
u_{k+1}
, in order to obtain

\frac{\Delta t}{2}||u_{k+1}||_{H_{\Omega}({\mathbb{R}}^{N},K)}^{2}+(u_{k+1}-u_{k},u_{k+1})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\int_{\Omega}c_{k}u_{k+1}^{2}dx=\Delta t\,(f_{k},u_{k+1})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

Since
c_{k}
is nonnegative, summing the previous equality on
k
from
0
to
n
, we find

\frac{\Delta t}{2}\sum_{k=0}^{n}||u_{k+1}||_{H_{\Omega}({\mathbb{R}}^{N},K)}^{2}+\sum_{k=0}^{n}(u_{k+1}-u_{k},u_{k+1})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq\Delta t\sum_{k=0}^{n}\,(f_{k},u_{k+1})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

Using the identity

(u_{k+1}-u_{k},u_{k+1})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\|u_{k+1}-u_{k}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|u_{k+1}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}-\|u_{k}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\right),

we can write the previous estimate in the following form

\sum_{k=0}^{n}\|u_{k+1}-u_{k}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|u_{n+1}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}-\|u_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\Delta t\,\sum_{k=0}^{n}||u_{k+1}||_{H_{\Omega}({\mathbb{R}}^{N},K)}^{2}\leq 2\Delta t\sum_{k=0}^{n}\,(f_{k},u_{k+1})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

(4.3)

On the other hand, using Poincaré and Young inequalities we have

(f_{k},u_{k+1})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq\frac{1}{2}||u_{k+1}||_{H_{\Omega}({\mathbb{R}}^{N},K)}^{2}+C\frac{\|f_{k}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}}{2},

for some constant
C>0
, hence estimate (
4.3
) leads

\sum_{k=0}^{n}\|u_{k+1}-u_{k}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|u_{n+1}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\Delta t\,\sum_{k=0}^{n}||u_{k+1}||_{H_{\Omega}({\mathbb{R}}^{N},K)}^{2}\leq\|u_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+C\Delta t\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\|f_{k}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}.

(4.4)

Now we introduce the piecewise linear interpolation

\hat{u}_{N}(x,t)=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\left(u_{n}(x)+\frac{t-t_{n}}{\Delta t}(u_{n+1}(x)-u_{n}(x))\right)\chi_{[t_{n},t_{n+1}]}(t).

It is clear that
u_{N}\in L^{2}(0,T;H_{\Omega}({\mathbb{R}}^{N},K))
, while
\hat{u}_{N}\in C([0,T];H_{\Omega}({\mathbb{R}}^{N},K))
. Moreover

\max_{t\in[0,T]}\|\hat{u}_{N}(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}=\max_{0\leq n\leq N}\|u_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2},\quad\int_{0}^{T}\|u_{N}(t)\|^{2}_{H_{\Omega}({\mathbb{R}}^{N},K)}dt=\Delta t\sum_{n=1}^{N}\|u_{n}\|^{2}_{H_{\Omega}({\mathbb{R}}^{N},K)},

hence (
4.4
) gives

\max_{t\in[0,T]}\|\hat{u}_{N}(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\|u_{N}(t)\|^{2}_{H_{\Omega}({\mathbb{R}}^{N},K)}dt\leq C\left(\|u_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\Delta t\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\|f_{k}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\right).

(4.5)

Moreover, an explicit calculation gives

\int_{0}^{T}\|\hat{u}_{N}(t)-u_{N}(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}dt=\frac{\Delta t}{3}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\|u_{n+1}-u_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}

and using (
4.4
) again we find

\int_{0}^{T}\|\hat{u}_{N}(t)-u_{N}(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}dt\leq C\,\Delta t\left(\|u_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\Delta t\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\|f_{k}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\right).

(4.6)

Observe moreover that by the integral Young inequality we find

\Delta t\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\|f_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}=\int_{0}^{T}\|f_{N}(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}dt\leq\int_{0}^{T}\|f(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}dt=\|f\|_{L^{2}(\Omega\times(0,T))}^{2}.

Hence estimates (
4.5
) and (
4.6
) imply

\|\hat{u}_{N}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}+\|u_{N}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H_{\Omega}({\mathbb{R}}^{N},K))}\leq C,\quad\|\hat{u}_{N}(t)-u_{N}(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega\times(0,T))}\leq C\sqrt{\Delta t}.

(4.7)

From (
4.7
) we have in particular that, up to subsequences,

\hat{u}_{N}\overset{\ast}{\rightharpoonup}u\quad\text{in }L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega)),

(4.8)

u_{N}\rightharpoonup z\quad\text{weakly in }L^{\infty}(0,T;H_{\Omega}({\mathbb{R}}^{N},K))).

and by the second estimate in (
4.7
) we find
u=z
, therefore

u_{N}\rightharpoonup u\quad\text{weakly in }L^{2}(0,T;H_{\Omega}({\mathbb{R}}^{N},K))).

(4.9)

Now we observe that problems (
4.1
) can be rewritten in the following unified form

\hat{u}_{N}^{\prime}(t)+\mathcal{L}u_{N}(t)+c_{N}(t)u_{N}(t)=f_{N}(t),\quad\text{in }(0,T).

(4.10)

Observe that from (
4.8
) we have

\hat{u}_{N}^{\prime}\rightarrow u^{\prime}\text{ in }\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(0,T;H_{\Omega}({\mathbb{R}}^{N},K))^{\prime})

while from (
4.9
)

\mathcal{L}u_{N}\ \rightharpoonup\mathcal{L}u\quad\text{weakly in }L^{2}(0,T;H_{\Omega}({\mathbb{R}}^{N},K)^{\prime})

and it is not difficult to show that

c_{N}\rightarrow c,\quad f_{N}\rightarrow f\text{ strongly in }L^{2}(\Omega\times(0,T)).

Passing to the limit as
N\rightarrow\infty
in (
4.10
) we find that
u
satisfies

u^{\prime}(t)+\mathcal{L}u(t)+c(t)u(t)=f(t),\quad\text{in }\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(0,T;H_{\Omega}({\mathbb{R}}^{N},K)^{\prime}),

which gives in particular that
u^{\prime}\in L^{2}(0,T;H_{\Omega}({\mathbb{R}}^{N},K)^{\prime})
. Choosing the function
\eta(x,t)=\varphi(x)\xi(t)
with
\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)
and
\xi(t)\in C_{0}^{\infty}(0,T)
we have that
u
satisfies relation (
4.1
). It remains to show that
u
verifies the initial condition. Notice that since

\hat{u}_{N}^{\prime}=-\mathcal{L}u_{N}-c_{N}u_{N}+f_{N}(t)\rightharpoonup-\mathcal{L}u-cu+f=u^{\prime}\quad\text{weakly in }L^{2}(0,T;H_{\Omega}({\mathbb{R}}^{N},K)^{\prime}),

we have that

\hat{u}_{N}\rightharpoonup u\quad\text{weakly in }\mathcal{W}(0,T;H_{\Omega}({\mathbb{R}}^{N},K))

and since

\mathcal{W}(0,T;H_{\Omega}({\mathbb{R}}^{N},K))\hookrightarrow C([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega))

with continuous injection, we have

\hat{u}_{N}(0)\rightharpoonup u(0)\quad\text{weakly in }L^{2}(\Omega)

but
\hat{u}_{N}(0)=u_{0}
hence
u(0)=u_{0}
andd
u
is a weak energy solution to problem (
1.10
).

In order to show that
\{v_{N}\}_{N\in{\mathbb{N}}}
converges (up to a subsequence) to the weak solution
v
to problem (
1.11
) we use similar arguments, once we simply observe that

d_{N}\rightarrow c_{\sharp},\quad g_{N}\rightarrow f^{\sharp}\text{ strongly in }L^{2}(\Omega^{\sharp}\times(0,T)):

indeed, the contractivity of the map
\eta\rightarrow\eta^{\sharp}
from
L^{p}(\Omega)
to
L^{p}(\Omega^{\sharp})
,
p\in[1,\infty]
implies that

\|d_{N}-c_{\sharp}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega^{\sharp}\times(0,T))}\leq\|c_{N}-c\|_{L^{2}(\Omega\times(0,T))},\quad\|g_{N}-f^{\#}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega^{\sharp}\times(0,T))}\leq\|f_{N}-f\|_{L^{2}(\Omega\times(0,T))}.

Step 3.

The comparison result.

Let us look at the discretized elliptic problems (
4.1
)-(
4.2
) as
n=0,1,...,N-1
. For
n=0
, we observe that

\left(c_{0}(x)+\frac{1}{\Delta t}\right)_{\sharp}=(c_{0})\sharp(x)+\frac{1}{\Delta t}=d_{0}(x)+\frac{1}{\Delta t}\quad\text{in }\Omega.

Moreover, we observe that, by using the Hardy inequality (
2.3
), the following inequality holds true

\int_{B_{r}}\left(f_{0}(x)+\frac{u_{0}(x)}{\Delta t}\right)^{\sharp}dx\leq\int_{B_{r}}\left(f_{0}^{\sharp}(x)+\frac{u^{\sharp}_{0}(x)}{\Delta t}\right)dx\qquad\forall r>0.

So, from Theorem
1.1
and Remark
3.1
, the following inequality holds

\int_{B_{r}}u_{1}^{\sharp}(x)dx\leq\int_{B_{r}}v_{1}(x)dx\qquad\forall r>0.

Assume by induction that

\int_{B_{r}}u_{n}^{\sharp}(x)dx\leq\int_{B_{r}}v_{n}(x)dx\qquad\forall r>0.

holds for some
n\leq(N-1)
.
Therefore

\int_{B_{r}}\left(f_{n}(x)+\frac{u_{n}(x)}{\Delta t}\right)^{\sharp}dx\leq\int_{B_{r}}\left(f_{n}^{\sharp}(x)+\frac{u^{\sharp}_{n}(x)}{\Delta t}\right)dx\leq\int_{B_{r}}\left(f_{n}^{\sharp}(x)+\frac{v_{n}(x)}{\Delta t}\right)dx\qquad\forall r>0.

Thus Theorem
1.1
and Remark
3.1
give again

\int_{B_{r}}u_{n+1}^{\sharp}(x)dx\leq\int_{B_{r}}v_{n+1}(x)dx\qquad\forall r>0.

Taking into account the form of the piecewise constant interpolation functions
u_{N}
and
v_{N}
, this concentration comparison can be written into the form

\int_{B_{r}}u_{N}^{\sharp}(x,t)dx\leq\int_{B_{r}}v_{N}(x,t)dx\qquad\text{ for all }r>0,t\in(0,T).

(4.11)

Now we wish to pass to the limit as
N\rightarrow\infty
in (
4.11
). By Proposition (
2.2
) we find

\int_{\Omega}u_{N}(x,t)\,\varphi(x)\,dx\leq\int_{\Omega^{\sharp}}v_{N}(x,t)\,\varphi^{\sharp}(x)\,dx,

for all nonnegative
\varphi\in L^{2}(\Omega)
. Observe that
u_{N}
converges weakly to
u
in
L^{2}(\Omega\times(0,T))
and
v_{N}
converges weakly to
v
in
L^{2}(\Omega^{\sharp}\times(0,T))
. Taking a nonnegative bounded function
\xi
in
(0,T)
we can write

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}u_{N}(x,t)\,\varphi(x)\xi(t)\,dx\,dt\leq\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega^{\sharp}}v_{N}(x,t)\,\varphi^{\sharp}(x)\xi(t)\,dx\,dt

hence we can pass to the limit as
N\rightarrow\infty
in order to obtain

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}u(x,t)\,\varphi(x)\xi(t)\,dx\,dt\leq\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega^{\sharp}}v(x,t)\,\varphi^{\sharp}(x)\xi(t)\,dx\,dt.

for all
\xi\in L^{\infty}_{+}(0,T)
.
Setting

\psi(t)=\int_{\Omega}u(x,t)\,\varphi(x)\,dx-\int_{\Omega^{\sharp}}v(x,t)\,\varphi^{\sharp}(x)dx

the previous inequality implies

\int_{0}^{T}\psi_{+}(t)\xi(t)dt=\int_{0}^{T}\psi(t)\chi_{\left\{\psi(t)\geq 0\right\}}dt\leq 0,

that is
\psi_{+}(t=0
for a.e.
t>0
, namely

\int_{\Omega}u(x,t)\,\varphi(x)\,dx\leq\int_{\Omega^{\sharp}}v(x,t)\,\varphi^{\sharp}(x)dx

for all
\varphi\in L^{2}_{+}(\Omega)
. Using Proposition (
2.2
) again, we find (recall that
u,\,v
are
L^{2}
-valued maps continuous in time)

\int_{B_{r}}u^{\sharp}(x,t)dx\leq\int_{B_{r}}v(x,t)dx\qquad\text{ for all }r>0,t\in(0,T)

and we achieve the desired conclusion.
