AntibodyFlow : Normalizing Flow Model for Designing Antibody Complementarity-Determining Regions
Bohao Xu Yanbo Wang Wenyu Chen, Shimin Shan

Abstract
Therapeutic antibodies have been extensively studied in drug discovery and development in the past decades. Antibodies are specialized protective proteins that bind to antigens in a lock-to-key manner. The binding strength/affinity between an antibody and a specific antigen is heavily determined by the complementarity-determining regions (CDRs) on the antibodies. Existing machine learning methods cast in silico development of CDRs as either sequence or 3D graph (with a single chain) generation tasks and have achieved initial success. However, with CDR loops having specific geometry shapes, learning the 3D geometric structures of CDRs remains a challenge. To address this issue, we propose AntibodyFlow , a 3D flow model to design antibody CDR loops. Specifically, AntibodyFlow first constructs the distance matrix, then predicts amino acids conditioned on the distance matrix. Also, AntibodyFlow conducts constraint learning and constrained generation to ensure valid 3D structures. Experimental results indicate that AntibodyFlow outperforms the best baseline consistently with up to 16.0% relative improvement in validity rate and 24.3% relative reduction in geometric graph level error (root mean square deviation, RMSD).

Introduction

Therapeutic antibodies have been extensively studied in drug discovery and development in the past decades

Compared to small-molecule drugs, therapeutic antibodies have many advantages such as minimal adverse effects, and can bind to many previously “undruggable” targets due to different biochemical mechanisms

The majority of the affinity and specificity of antibodies is modulated by a set of binding loops called the Complementarity Determining Regions (CDRs) found on the variable domain of each of the two chains.

Traditionally, antibody sequences are discovered either

In silico

Earlier methods are primarily based on heuristic searching and require extensive wet-lab validation

In recent years, several sequence/3D graph generation methods have been proposed for antibody CDR loop discovery,

However, several challenges remain:

1.

Most of the existing methods generate amino acids sequentially, which is hard to model long-term dependency and impose global constraints on the geometric shape of CDR loops.

2.

Rotating and translating the 3D graph would not change the underlying 3D graph and it is challenging to directly generate rotation- and translation-invariant 3D graphs

]
.

To address these challenges, we propose

, a 3D flow model that generates antibody CDR loops in two phases: (i) build the geometric structure of the CDR loop with spatial constraints; and (ii) predict the amino acids as the graph nodes.
The main contributions are summarized as follows.

1.

AntibodyFlow
generates amino acid sequence and pairwise distance matrix (between amino acids) in a

Unlike the sequential manner, the flow model with one-hot generation is more flexible to impute the global constraints of geometric shape.
Concretely,

2.

To circumvent the direct generation of 3D graphs, we build a pairwise distance matrix that measures Euclidean distances between amino acids. The distance matrix is invariant w.r.t. rotation and translation on a 3D graph.
After that, we generate 3D coordinates based on both distance matrix and geometric constraints.

3.

Experiments confirmed

2.2
Background and Overview

Flow model background
.
The objective of flow model is to learn an invertible model
f_{\theta}
(with learnable parameter
\theta
) that maps the input data
{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
into the latent variable
{\mathbf{z}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, i.e.,
{\mathbf{z}}=f_{\theta}({\mathbf{x}})
.

f_{\theta}
is invertible, i.e.,
{\mathbf{x}}=f^{-1}_{\theta}({\mathbf{z}})
.
The latent variable has the same size as the input data.
We suppose the latent variable
{\mathbf{z}}
is drawn from a known prior distribution (e.g., isotropic Gaussian distribution), i.e.,
{\mathbf{z}}\sim p_{{\mathbf{z}}}({\mathbf{z}})
.
Using the change-of-variable formula, the log-probability of
{\mathbf{x}}
becomes

\log p_{{\mathbf{x}}}({\mathbf{x}})=\log p_{\mathbf{z}}({\mathbf{z}})+\log\bigg(\bigg|\det\frac{\partial{\mathbf{z}}}{\partial{\mathbf{x}}}\bigg|\bigg),

(3)

where
\frac{\partial{\mathbf{z}}}{\partial{\mathbf{x}}}
is the Jacobian of
f_{\theta}
at
\mathbf{x}
,
\det
is the determinant of a square matrix.
Recall input data
\mathbf{x}
is
\mathbf{x}=(\mathbf{D},\mathbf{S}),\mathbf{D}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N},\mathbf{S}\in\{0,1\}^{N\times|V|}
.
The design of
f
follows two strategies to learn complex transformations: (i) “easy logarithm of determinant of the Jacobian” (
\log\big|\det\frac{\partial\mathbf{z}}{\partial\mathbf{x}}\big|
) and (ii) “easy inverse” (
f^{-1}
)
[
18
]
.

Background of affine coupling
. Affine coupling layer is a commonly-used neural architecture in flow model to learn complex transformation, which naturally meets the two criteria above
[
18
,
19
,
20
]
.
Specifically, in affine coupling layer, the input
\mathbf{I}\in\mathbb{R}^{P\times Q}
is decomposed into
\mathbf{I}=(\mathbf{I}_{1},\mathbf{I}_{2}),\mathbf{I}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{P_{1}\times Q},\mathbf{I}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{(P-P_{1})\times Q}
, where
P_{1}=\lceil{\frac{P}{2}}\rceil
(ceiling function) or
P-\lceil{\frac{P}{2}}\rceil
. The mapping is

\mathbf{O}_{1}=\mathbf{I}_{1},\ \ \mathbf{O}_{2}=\mathbf{I}_{2}\odot\text{sigmoid}(R(\mathbf{I}_{1}))+T(\mathbf{I}_{1}),

(4)

where
\odot
denotes element-wise multiplication.
Outputs
\mathbf{O}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{P_{1}\times Q},\mathbf{O}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{(P-P_{1})\times Q}
has same shape with the inputs
\mathbf{I}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{P_{1}\times Q},\mathbf{I}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{(P-P_{1})\times Q}
, respectively.

R(\cdot),T(\cdot):\mathbb{R}^{P_{1}\times Q}\xrightarrow{}\mathbb{R}^{(P-P_{1})\times Q}
share the same neural architectures but different parameters.
The sigmoid function is applied element-wisely.
The logarithm of the Jacobian determinant is easy to evaluate,

\displaystyle\log\bigg|\det\frac{\partial\mathbf{O}}{\partial\mathbf{I}}\bigg|=\log\bigg|\det\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{I}&0\\
0&\text{sigmoid}(R(\mathbf{I}_{1}))\\
\end{pmatrix}\bigg|=\sum\nolimits_{j=1}^{(P-P_{1})Q}\log\text{sigmoid}(R(\mathbf{I}_{1}))_{j}.

(5)

where
\mathbb{I}
is the identity matrix.
The inverse mapping is

\displaystyle\mathbf{I}_{1}=\mathbf{O}_{1},\ \ \mathbf{I}_{2}=\big(\mathbf{O}_{2}-T(\mathbf{I}_{1})\big)/\text{sigmoid}\big(R(\mathbf{I}_{1})\big).

(6)

Multiple affine coupling layers are usually concatenated (e.g.,
\mathcal{F}_{1}
,
\cdots
,
\mathcal{F}_{K}
in Eq.
7
).
Consecutive affine coupling layers swap the elements in
\mathbf{I}_{1}
and
\mathbf{I}_{2}
so that every element occurs in
\mathbf{I}_{1}
and
\mathbf{I}_{2}
in turn.

2.5
Constraint Learning

Constraint learning encourages the flow model to learn the validity constraints of the CDR loop.
To proceed, we design a
constraint loss
function, a function of the generated distance matrix
\mathbf{D}
. It penalizes the distance matrix that does not satisfy the validity constraints.
However, the constraints in Eq. (
1
) and (
2
) are hard constraints and not differentiable w.r.t. the distance matrix.
To bridge the gap, we design the following
constraint surrogate function

H(y;a,b,\delta)
that transforms the hard constraints into soft constraints.

The
constraint surrogate function

H(y;a,b,\delta)
is a function of
y
(a scalar), where
a,b,\delta
are all scalars and satisfy
a<b
and
\delta>0
.
H
is designed to encourage the variable
y
to be between scalar
a
and
b
.
To make
H
differentiable everywhere and enable the continuous optimization process,
we borrow the idea from Huber loss
[
23
]
.
The basic idea of Huber loss comes from the fact that square loss is sensitive to outliers but differentiable everywhere, whereas L1 loss is more robust to outliers but not differentiable at 0.
Combining the advantage of both, the Huber loss function is quadratic for small values (square loss), and linear for large values (L1 loss).
In our scenario, as long as the scalar
y
is between
a
and
b
, the constraints are satisfied with the loss equal to 0.
Thus, we design
H(y;a,b,\delta)
as

\displaystyle H(y;a,b,\delta)=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\delta(-y+a-\frac{1}{2}\delta),&&y\leq a-\delta\\
&\frac{1}{2}(y-a)^{2},&&a-\delta<y\leq a,\\
&0,&&a<y\leq b,\\
&\frac{1}{2}(y-b)^{2},&&b<y\leq b+\delta,\\
&\delta(y-b-\frac{1}{2}\delta),&&b+\delta<y.\\
\end{aligned}\right.

(13)

It has equal values and slopes of the different sections at the four connection points (
a-\delta,a,b,b+\delta
), so it is continuous and differentiable everywhere.
When
a=b
,
H
loss function reduces to Huber loss function.
Thus, the hard constraints in Eq. (
1
) and (
2
) can be transformed into surrogate functions as follows

\displaystyle h_{1}(\mathbf{D})=\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{N-1}H(\mathbf{D}_{i,i+1};\eta_{1},\eta_{2},\eta_{3}),\ \ \ \ \ h_{2}(\mathbf{D})=H(\mathbf{D}_{1,N};\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2},\epsilon_{3}).

(14)

where
\eta_{1}/\eta_{2}
and
\epsilon_{1}/\epsilon_{2}
are defined in Eq. (
1
) and (
2
), respectively. We also have
\eta_{3}=\eta_{2}-\eta_{1}
and
\epsilon_{3}=\epsilon_{2}-\epsilon_{1}
.

\{\mathbf{D}_{i,j}\}
(
i,j\in\{1,\cdots,N\}
) as follows,

\displaystyle|\mathbf{D}_{i,j}-\mathbf{D}_{(i+1),j}|\leq\mathbf{D}_{i,(i+1)}\leq\eta_{2},\ \ \ |\mathbf{D}_{i,j}-\mathbf{D}_{i,(j+1)}|\leq\mathbf{D}_{j,(j+1)}\leq\eta_{2},

(15)

\displaystyle\text{for}\ i,j\in\{1,2,\cdots,N-1\},

where
\eta_{2}
is defined in Eq. (
1
).
It indicates that the elements of
\mathbf{D}
are smooth, we design the following
constraint loss
to encourage the smoothness,

\displaystyle h_{3}(\mathbf{D})=\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}(\mathbf{D}_{i,j}-\mathbf{D}_{(i+1),j})^{2}+(\mathbf{D}_{i,j}-\mathbf{D}_{i,(j+1)})^{2}.

(16)

We design
constraint loss
function as follows, which is a function of distance matrix
\mathbf{D}
,

\displaystyle h(\mathbf{D})=\xi_{1}h_{1}(\mathbf{D})+\xi_{2}h_{2}(\mathbf{D})+\xi_{3}h_{3}(\mathbf{D}),

(17)

where
\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\xi_{3}>0
are the hyperparameters that control the weights of various terms.
When generating new distance matrix, we draw latent variable
{\mathbf{z}}_{\mathbf{D}}
from isotropic Gaussian distribution
p_{{\mathbf{z}}_{\mathbf{D}}}(\cdot)
, the learning objective is designed as expectation of
h(\mathbf{D})
under the distribution of
\mathbf{z}_{\mathbf{D}}

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{con}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\big[{\mathbf{z}}_{\mathbf{D}}\sim p_{{\mathbf{z}}_{\mathbf{D}}}(\cdot),\mathbf{D}=(f_{\theta})_{\mathbf{D}}^{-1}({\mathbf{z}}_{\mathbf{D}})\big]}h(\mathbf{D}).

(18)

We use Monte Carlo sampling to get a noisy but unbiased estimate of the learning objective,

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{L}_{\text{con}}(\theta)}=\frac{1}{M}\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{M}h(\mathbf{D}^{(i)}),\ \text{where}\ {\mathbf{z}}^{(i)}_{\mathbf{D}}\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}p_{{\mathbf{z}}_{\mathbf{D}}}(\cdot),

(19)

\displaystyle i=1,\cdots,M,\ \ \ \ \ \ \mathbf{D}^{(i)}=(f_{\theta})_{\mathbf{D}}^{-1}({\mathbf{z}}^{(i)}_{\mathbf{D}}),

where
M
is the number of Monte Carlo samples.
Gradient descent is leveraged to minimize the noisy objective
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}_{\text{con}}(\theta)}
.
We alternatively optimize log-likelihood in Eq. (
3
) and constraint loss in Eq. (
17
).
Constraint learning is only conducted during learning and not utilized in inference.

2.6
Constrained 3D Coordinates Generation

This section discusses how to transform the generated distance matrix
\mathbf{D}
into 3D coordinates
\mathbf{G}
that satisfy validity constraints simultaneously.
Specifically, given the generated distance matrix
\mathbf{D}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
, we build the 3D graph via optimizing the coordinates of all the amino acids, i.e.,
\mathbf{G}=[\mathbf{g}_{1},\mathbf{g}_{2},\cdots,\mathbf{g}_{N}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times 3}
.
We optimize the amino acids’ coordinates by minimizing the following objective under the validity constraints given in Eq. (
1
) and (
2
):

\displaystyle\underset{\mathbf{g}_{1},\cdots,\mathbf{g}_{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}}{\arg\min}\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{N}\sum\nolimits_{j=1,j\neq i}^{N}\big|\|\mathbf{g}_{i}-\mathbf{g}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}-\mathbf{D}_{i,j}^{2}\big|,

(20)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}\ \eta_{1}\leq||\mathbf{g}_{i}-\mathbf{g}_{i+1}||_{2}\leq\eta_{2},\ \epsilon_{1}\leq||\mathbf{g}_{1}-\mathbf{g}_{N}||_{2}\leq\epsilon_{2},

where
\eta_{1},\eta_{2}
are defined in Eq. (
1
),
\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2}
are defined in Eq. (
2
),
||\cdot||_{2}
represents
{l}_{2}
norm of a vector.

Then, in order to simplify the optimization process, we used the
constraint surrogate function

H(\cdot)
(Eq. (
13
)) to turn the hard constraints in Eq. (
20
) into soft constraints.
Then we augment the learning objective and reformulate an unconstrained optimization problem as follows,

\displaystyle\underset{\mathbf{g}_{1},\cdots,\mathbf{g}_{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}}{\arg\min}\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{N}\sum\nolimits_{j=1,j\neq i}^{N}\big|\|\mathbf{g}_{i}-\mathbf{g}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}-\mathbf{D}_{i,j}^{2}\big|

(21)

\displaystyle+\lambda_{1}\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}H(||\mathbf{g}_{i}-\mathbf{g}_{i+1}||_{2};\eta_{1},\eta_{2},\eta_{3})+\lambda_{2}H(||\mathbf{g}_{1}-\mathbf{g}_{N}||_{2};\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2},\epsilon_{3}),

where
H(\cdot)
is the loss function defined in Eq. (
13
); hyperparameters
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\gg 1
emphasize the importance of the constraints to make sure that they are satisfied, hyperparameters
\eta_{3}
and
\epsilon_{3}
are set to
\eta_{3}=\eta_{2}-\eta_{1}
,
\epsilon_{3}=\epsilon_{2}-\epsilon_{1}
.

To optimize Eq. (
21
), we leverage random coordinate descent
[
24
]
to update
\mathbf{g}_{1},\cdots,\mathbf{g}_{N}
(i.e., randomly selecting a
\mathbf{g}_{i}
from
\{\mathbf{g}_{1},\cdots,\mathbf{g}_{N}\}
to update and fixing the remaining ones).

H(y;a,b,\delta)
is convex w.r.t.
y
.
Thus, when we update each
\mathbf{g}_{i}
(
i=1,\cdots,N
) in coordinate descent, when fixing other
\mathbf{g}
variables, Eq. (
21
) is convex w.r.t.
\mathbf{g}_{i}
, which makes the optimization efficient.
Worth mentioning that the constrained 3D coordinates generation is not conducted during the learning procedure and only during inference.
Algorithm
1
summarizes the essential steps.

3.2
Results and Analysis

De novo
design
. We report the performance of
de novo
CDR H1, H2, and H3 generations in Table
1
. For PPL and RMSD, we report the average value and standard deviations of all the data points in the test set.
We find that (i) in all three tasks, our method achieves over 90% validity rate (96.3% on H1, 92.8% on H2, 91.0% on H3) and obtains the best performance among all the methods in both sequences (PPL, perplexity) and geometric graph metrics (RMSD & validity rate).
Compared to IR-GNN (the best baseline), the relative improvements are up to 5.4% in PPL (perplexity) (10.81 v.s. 11.43, H3), 24.3% in RMSD (1.28 v.s. 1.69, H1), and 16.0% in VR (validity rate) (91.0% v.s. 78.4%, H3);
(ii) among all the three tasks (H1, H2, H3), almost all the methods get the highest perplexity, RMSD, and lower validity in the H3 generation task, thus validating the fact that CDR H3 loops have the highest variability and most challenging to design
[
13
,
11
]
;
(iii) removing constraint learning from
AntibodyFlow
(“
AntibodyFlow
- constraint” v.s.
AntibodyFlow
) significantly degrades the performance, especially in geometric graph metrics (RMSD and validity rate (VR)). For example, in H3 loop generation (Table

), it leads to 1.4%, 22.1%, and 35.1% relative drops in PPL, RMSD, and VR, respectively.
It validates that learning constraint plays a crucial role in geometric graph generation, especially generating a valid CDR loop; (iv)
AntibodyFlow
achieves the highest diversity in H2 & H3 design tasks, and second highest diversity in H1 generation, indicating our method is able to generate diverse amino acid sequences.

4
Conclusion

This paper proposed
AntibodyFlow
model to generate 3D antibody CDR loops.
Specifically, our method first builds the distance matrix of amino acids, and then constructs the amino acid sequence conditioned on the distance matrix.
Also, to enhance the geometric validity of the generated CDR loops, we design constraint learning and constrained 3D coordinate generation to impose the 3D geometric constraints.
Empirical studies validate
AntibodyFlow
’s superiority.

(I)

For protein design, variational autoencoder (VAE) based methods

,

[

proposed an

to jointly design 3D graph structure and amino acid sequence.
However, almost all of these methods incrementally grow the sequence by adding a single amino acid at a time. In contrast, our method generates the whole CDR loop together so that the global information (geometric constraints) is considered.

(II) Flow model
(a.k.a. normalizing flow model) is a special category of deep generative models that designs invertible mapping so that the input data can be exactly reconstructed
[
36
]
.
The advantage of a flow model is its ability to reconstruct input data exactly and directly optimize the likelihood, instead of optimizing a surrogate, e.g., VAE optimizes the lower bound of likelihood
[
37
]
.
It is successfully applied to image generation/impainting
[
38
,
39
]
, machine translation
[
40
]
, and small molecule generation
[
41
,
19
,
20
,
14
]
.
The conditional flow model is a well-known variant of the flow model and is also widely applied
[
39
,
19
,
20
]
.
In this paper, we address some challenges of the flow model in 3D graph generation.
Besides,
[
39
]
proposes conditional flow on computer vision.

(III) 3D graph generative model
have been proposed for drug discovery recently, e.g.,
[
11
]
(mentioned in I) and
[
29
]
.

[
29
]
leveraged autoregressive 3D graph neural network to design small-molecule conformation conditioned on the pocket conformation of the target protein.
However, these methods focus on generating a single component (e.g., an amino acid or an atom) at a time based on conditional distribution without global constraints.
In contrast, our method generates the 3D graph at once and imposes global constraints that encode the geometric shape of CDR loops.

Appendix C
Proof of Corollary
1

Corollary 1
.
Suppose
\mathbf{G}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times 3}
are the 3D coordinates of the graph by rotating and translating the original graph whose coordinates are
\mathbf{G}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times 3}
,
\mathbf{D}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
and
\mathbf{D}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
are the distance matrix of
\mathbf{G}
and
\mathbf{G}^{\prime}
, respectively, then we have
\mathbf{D}=\mathbf{D}^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Suppose
\mathbf{D}
and
\mathbf{D}^{\prime}
are the distance matrix before and after transformation (rotation/translation), respectively.

First, regarding rotation, without loss of generalization, we suppose the rotation matrix is
\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}
, which is an orthogonal matrix satisfying
\mathbf{Q}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{Q}^{\top}=\mathbf{I}_{3}
,
\mathbf{I}_{3}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}
is identity matrix.
Rotating 3D graph whose coordinates are
\mathbf{G}=[\mathbf{g}_{1},\cdots,\mathbf{g}_{N}]
results in a new 3D graph whose coordinates are
\mathbf{G}^{\prime}=[\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{g}_{1},\cdots,\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{g}_{N}]
. Then based on the definition of the distance matrix, we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{D}^{\prime}_{i,j}

\displaystyle=||\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{g}_{i}-\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{g}_{i}||_{2}

(22)

\displaystyle=||\mathbf{Q}(\mathbf{g}_{i}-\mathbf{g}_{j})||_{2}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{(\mathbf{g}_{i}-\mathbf{g}_{j})^{\top}\mathbf{Q}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}(\mathbf{g}_{i}-\mathbf{g}_{j})}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{(\mathbf{g}_{i}-\mathbf{g}_{j})^{\top}(\mathbf{g}_{i}-\mathbf{g}_{j})}

\displaystyle=||\mathbf{g}_{i}-\mathbf{g}_{j}||_{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{D}_{i,j}.

Secondly, translating 3D graph (whose coordinates are
\mathbf{G}=[\mathbf{g}_{1},\cdots,\mathbf{g}_{N}]
) by
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
results in a new 3D graph whose coordinates are
\mathbf{G}^{\prime}=[\mathbf{g}_{1}+\mathbf{v},\cdots,\mathbf{g}_{N}+\mathbf{v}]
.
We have

\mathbf{D}^{\prime}_{i,j}=||(\mathbf{g}_{i}+\mathbf{v})-(\mathbf{g}_{j}+\mathbf{v})||_{2}=||\mathbf{g}_{i}-\mathbf{g}_{j}||_{2}=\mathbf{D}_{i,j}.

(23)

Third, the joint transformation of rotation and translation can be represented as

\mathbf{G}^{\prime}=[\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{g}_{1}+\mathbf{v},\cdots,\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{g}_{N}+\mathbf{v}].

Then based on definition of distance matrix and combining Equation (
22
) and (
23
), we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{D}^{\prime}_{i,j}

\displaystyle=||(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{g}_{i}+\mathbf{v})-(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{g}_{i}+\mathbf{v})||_{2}

(24)

\displaystyle=||\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{g}_{i}-\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{g}_{i}||_{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{D}_{i,j}.

In sum, the distance matrix
\mathbf{D}
is invariant w.r.t. translation and rotation on the 3D graph.

∎

Appendix D
Learning and Inference

In this section, we describe the learning and inference process.
The key steps of our method are summarized in Algorithm
1
.

During the learning process, we alternatively conduct maximum likelihood learning and constraint learning.
Given the data sample
\mathbf{x}
, maximum likelihood learning (MLE) maximizes the exact log-likelihood (Equation.
3
) of input data w.r.t. parameters
\theta
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{MLE}}(\theta)=\log p_{{\mathbf{x}}}({\mathbf{x}})

(25)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\log p_{\mathbf{z}}({\mathbf{z}})+\log\big(\big|\det\frac{\partial{\mathbf{z}}}{\partial{\mathbf{x}}}\big|\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\log p_{{\mathbf{z}}_{\mathbf{D}}}({\mathbf{z}}_{\mathbf{D}})+\log p_{{\mathbf{z}}_{\mathbf{S}|\mathbf{D}}}({\mathbf{z}}_{\mathbf{S}|\mathbf{D}})

\displaystyle+\log\bigg(\bigg|\det\frac{\partial f_{\mathbf{D}}}{\partial\mathbf{D}}\bigg|\bigg)+\log\bigg(\bigg|\det\frac{\partial f_{\mathbf{S}|\mathbf{D}}}{\partial\mathbf{S}}\bigg|\bigg),

where latent variable
\mathbf{z}
satisfies
\mathbf{z}=f_{\theta}(\mathbf{x})
,
the first two term
p_{{\mathbf{z}}_{\mathbf{D}}}(\cdot)
,
p_{{\mathbf{z}}_{\mathbf{S}|\mathbf{D}}}(\cdot)
follow isotropic Gaussian distribution, the third term
\log\big(\big|\det\frac{\partial f_{\mathbf{D}}}{\partial\mathbf{D}}\big|\big)
is given in Section
2.3
; the fourth term
\log\big(\big|\det\frac{\partial f_{\mathbf{S}|\mathbf{D}}}{\partial\mathbf{S}}\big|\big)
is defined in Section
2.4
.

During inference process, we sample latent variable
{\mathbf{z}}=({\mathbf{z}}_{\mathbf{D}},{\mathbf{z}}_{\mathbf{S}|\mathbf{D}})
from isotropic Gaussian distribution, generate the distance matrix
\mathbf{D}
, and transform
\mathbf{D}
into 3D coordinates
\mathbf{G}
following Section
2.3
.
Then we generate
\mathbf{S}
conditioned on

Appendix F
Evaluation Metrics

In this section, we describe the metrics for evaluating the performance of all the methods.

•

(1)
Protein Perplexity (PPL)
.
Protein perplexity is the exponential of the average log-likelihood and measures how well a probability model predicts amino acid sequence
[
27
,
42
]
. We train an LSTM model as language model. Lower perplexity indicates better performance.

•

(2)
Root-Mean-Square Deviation (RMSD)
measures the alignment between tested conformations
R\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times 3}
and reference conformation
{R}^{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times 3}
,
N
is the number of points in the conformation, defined as

\text{RMSD}(R,\hat{R})=\big(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}||R_{i}-\hat{R}_{i}||_{2}^{2}\big)^{\frac{1}{2}},

where
R_{i}
is the
i
-th row of conformation
R
.
The conformation
\hat{R}
is obtained by an alignment function
\hat{R}=A(R,R^{r})
, which rotates and translates the reference conformation
R^{r}
to have the smallest distance to the generated R according to the RMSD metrics, which is calculated by the Kabsch algorithm
[
43
]
.
Lower RMSD indicates better performance.

•

(3)
Validity Rate (VR)
is the percentage of valid CDR loops in all the generated CDRs. A CDR loop is valid if it satisfies two criteria in Equation (
1
) and (
2
).
Higher validity is more desirable.

•

(4)
Similarity
is evaluated on amino acid sequence level.
The similarity between two sequences
s_{1}
and
s_{2}
, and is defined as

\text{sim}(s_{1},s_{2})=\frac{|\text{LCS}(s_{1},s_{2})|}{\max\{|s_{1}|,|s_{2}|\}},

(26)

where
|s|
denotes the length of sequence
s
,
\text{LCS}(s_{1},s_{2})
denotes the longest common substring between
s_{1}
and
s_{2}
, computed by dynamic programming.
The similarity value ranges from 0 to 1, a higher value indicates more similarity.
The distance is defined as one minus similarity.
The similarity is not used directly in our experiment.
It is used for evaluating diversity.

•

(5)
Diversity (Div)
is defined as the average pairwise distance between the CDR loops.
Specifically, suppose
\mathcal{S}
is the set of all the generated CDR loops, we have

\text{diversity}(\mathcal{S})=1-\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}|(|\mathcal{S}|-1)}\sum_{s_{1},s_{2}\in\mathcal{S},s_{1}\neq s_{2}}\text{sim}(s_{1},s_{2}),

where
\text{sim}(\cdot,\cdot)
is the similarity function defined above.
Diversity ranges from 0 to 1, higher diversity is more desirable.

•

(6)
SARS-CoV Neutralization Ability
.
Following
[
11
]
, we train a machine learning model to predict the neutralization ability of a CDR-H3 sequence to either SARS-CoV-1 or SARS-CoV-2 viruses.
The predictor takes as CDR-H3 sequence of an antibody and outputs a neutralization probability for the SARS-CoV-1 and SARS-CoV-2 viruses.
Each residue is embedded into a 100-dimensional vector.
Then the embeddings are fed to a three-layer bi-LSTM (bidirectional Long Short Term Memory)
[
44
]
, followed by average-pooling and a two-layer MLP (multi-layer perceptron).
The hidden dimension of bi-LSTM is 100.
The activate functions in the hidden layers and output layer are ReLU and sigmoid, respectively.
The prediction is very accurate, achieving over 0.99 ROC-AUC and over 0.95 F1 score.
The neutralization predictor yields neutralization probability (ranging from 0 to 1), higher probability is desirable.

Appendix G
Baseline Methods

In this section, we describe the baseline methods and briefly introduce the implementation details.
All these baselines are state-of-the-art approaches in antibody design.
We use the default setup following the original paper.

1.

Reference
evaluates the metrics of real CDR loops in the test set.

2.

LSTM

[
10
,
9
]
leverages LSTM (long short-term memory) to generate amino acid sequence and does not model 3D structure.
Following the original paper, it is trained by the Adam optimizer and has a learning rate of 0.01. Dropout rates were chosen from 0.1 and 0.2 to regularize all layers
The hidden dimension of LSTM is set to 256.

3.

GA (genetic algorithm)
starts from a population of CDR loops. Given the data in the current population, to generate offspring for the next generation, we leverage the following two operations: (i) crossover (switching subsequence between two loops) and (ii) mutation (randomly changing one amino acid) to produce new candidate in each iteration
[
8
]
. The population size of GA is set to 100, and the number of generations is set to 1K, which is sufficient for GA to converge. In each generation, when producing the offspring, we generate 1K offsprings, where half of them are produced through crossover operation, and half are produced through mutation operation. We use perplexity as the oracle to select the most promising offspring for the next generation.

4.

IR-GNN
(Iterative refinement graph neural network)
[
11
]
first unravels amino acid sequence and iteratively refine its predicted global structure. The inferred structure in turn guides subsequent amino acid choices. Following their original paper, for the GNN, both its structure and sequence MPN have four message passing layers, with a hidden dimension of 256 and block size b = 4. It is trained by the Adam optimizer with a dropout of 0.1 and a learning rate of 0.001.

5.

AR-GNN
(auto-regressive graph neural network)
[
28
,
29
]
was proposed for molecule generation and was adapted for antibody CDR design
[
11
]
.
The neural architecture of AR-GNN follows the setups of IR-GNN.
Different from IR-GNN that uses iterative refinement mechanism, AR-GNN generates the amino acid sequence and 3D structural graph autoregressively.

6.

LSTM, IR-GNN and AR-GNN fall into the category of deep generative models while GA are belonging to traditional heuristic searching method.
