Interferometric Lensless Imaging: Rank-one Projections of Image Frequencies with Speckle Illuminations
Olivier Leblanc 1, Matthias Hofer 2, Siddharth Sivankutty 3, Hervé Rigneault 2, Laurent Jacques 1

Abstract
Lensless illumination single-pixel imaging with a multicore fiber (MCF) is a computational imaging technique that enables potential endoscopic observations of biological samples at cellular scale. In this work, we show that this technique is tantamount to collecting multiple symmetric rank-one projections (SROP) of an interferometric matrix—a matrix encoding the spectral content of the sample image. In this model, each SROP is induced by the complex sketching vector shaping the incident light wavefront with a spatial light modulator (SLM), while the projected interferometric matrix collects up to 𝒪 ⁡ ( Q 2 ) \mathcal{O}(Q^{2}) image frequencies for a Q Q -core MCF. While this scheme subsumes previous sensing modalities, such as raster scanning (RS) imaging with beamformed illumination, we demonstrate that collecting the measurements of M M random SLM configurations—and thus acquiring M M SROPs—allows us to estimate an image of interest if M M and Q Q scale log-linearly with the image sparsity level. This demonstration is achieved both theoretically, with a specific restricted isometry analysis of the sensing scheme, and with extensive Monte Carlo experiments. On a practical side, we perform a single calibration of the sensing system robust to certain deviations to the theoretical model and independent of the ​ s ​ k ​ e ​ t ​ c ​ h ​ i ​ n ​ g \emph{sketching} vectors used during the imaging phase. Experimental results made on an actual MCF system demonstrate the effectiveness of this imaging procedure on a benchmark image.

II
MCF lensless imaging

We here develop the sensing model associated with an MCF lensless imager (MCF-LI) in the same speckle imaging conditions as provided in
[
12
]
,
i.e.
, interfering coherent light components output by the cores of the MCF with random relative delays. As illustrated in Fig.
1
(top), an MCF-LI consists of four main parts: a wavefront shaper (SLM), optics, an MCF and a single photo-detector. The SLM shapes the phase of the light that is injected into the cores. The optics include mirrors and lenses used to focus the light into the center of each core, hence preventing multimodal effects.

As explained below, under a common far-field assumption, MCF-LI can be described as a two-component sensing system applying SROP of a specific interferometric matrix. We show how this model subsumes previous descriptions of the MCF-LI, and end this section highlighting that the SROP and interferometric nature of the model hold beyond the far-field assumptions.

\textstyle\bar{y}_{\bm{\theta}}=\bm{\gamma}_{\bm{\theta}}^{*}\bm{\mathcal{I}}_{\Omega}[f^{\circ}]\bm{\gamma}_{\bm{\theta}}=\bm{1}^{\top}\bm{\mathcal{I}}_{\Omega}[\mathcal{T}_{-\bm{\theta}}f^{\circ}]\bm{1}

for any tilt
\bm{\theta}
, meeting the convolution interpretation in (
9
).

Despite its conceptual simplicity, the RS mode has a few drawbacks
[
12
]
:
(i)
it requires as many illuminations as the target image resolution;
(ii)
due to limited MCF diameter and the chosen core arrangement, the related convolution kernel
\varphi
is actually spatially varying, which limits the validity of (
9
).

Speckle Illumination mode

In the SI mode, the sample
f
is illuminated with random light patterns called
speckles
. These are generated with random core complex amplitudes
\bm{\alpha}
. Conversely to the RS mode, by recording all speckles illuminations at calibration, SI does not require to know the MCF transfer matrix.

One can interpret SI as a compressive imaging system
[
32
,
33
,
34
]
. By considering that both
f
and each illumination intensity
S(\bm{x};\bm{\alpha})
are discretized and vectorized as
\bm{f}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
and
\bm{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, respectively, and gathering in a matrix
\bm{S}:=(\bm{s}_{1},\,\ldots,\bm{s}_{M})\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times M}
the
M
discretized speckles obtained from the sketching vectors
\{\bm{\alpha}_{m}\}_{m=1}^{M}
, the model (
5
) becomes

\bar{y}_{m}=\bm{s}_{m}^{\top}\bm{f},\ m\in[M],\ \text{or}\ \bm{\bar{y}}=\bm{S}^{\top}\bm{f}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}_{+}.

(10)

If
M
is adjusted to the sparsity level of
\bm{f}
(with
M<N
), the recovery of
\bm{f}
from
\bm{y}
becomes a classical compressive sensing (CS) problem with the sensing matrix
\bm{S}
.

To characterize the properties of the sensing model (
10
) in this CS framework, the authors in
[
12
]
propose to first to center (or
debiase
) this model by computing
\bm{y}^{\rm c}=\bm{y}-y^{\rm a}\bm{1}_{M}
with the measurement average
y^{\rm a}:={\textstyle\frac{1}{M}}\sum_{j=1}^{M}y_{j}
(we reinterpret this operation in Sec.
IV-C
). This provides, from (
10
),
the model

\bm{y}^{\rm c}=\sqrt{M}\bm{\Phi}\bm{\bar{S}f}+\bm{n}^{\rm c},

(11)

with a centered noise
\bm{n}^{\rm c}:=\bm{n}-({\textstyle\frac{1}{M}}\sum_{j=1}^{M}n_{j})\bm{1}_{M}
,
\sqrt{M}\bm{\Phi}:=\bm{D}\bm{S}^{\top}\bar{\bm{S}}^{-1}
and the debiasing matrix
\bm{D}:=(\Id_{M}-{\textstyle\frac{1}{M}}\bm{1}_{M}\bm{1}_{M}^{\top})
,
\bm{\bar{S}}:=\diag(\bm{\bar{s}})\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
, and
\bm{\bar{s}}:=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\alpha}}\bm{s}
. The map
\bm{\bar{S}}\bm{f}
relates to the discretization of the vignetted image
f^{\circ}
defined above.

The debiasing above allowed the authors of
[
12
]
to hypothesize that
\bm{\Phi}
satisfies the Restricted Isometry Property (RIP), a crucial property in the classical CS problem
[
32
]
ensuring the success of recovery procedures such as the basis pursuit denoise program (see Sec.
IV
).
SI both improves the quality of the reconstructed images and reduces the acquisition time compared to RS. However, the RIP of the related sensing matrix which relies on specific random speckle configurations has not been established, keeping the sample complexity unknown for stable and robust image recovery
Moreover, in SI mode, we must prerecord
M
—object free—illumination speckles to build
\bm{\Phi}
, before observing the sample in the imaging plane with the same speckles.

II-C

Generalized MCF-LI sensing

We can extend the MCF-LI model (
3
) beyond the far-field and identical core diameter assumptions by replacing the interferometric matrix
\bm{\mathcal{I}}_{\Omega}[f^{\circ}]
with a more general matrix function
\bm{G}[f]
.

Given the wavefield
E_{q}(\bm{x})
of the
q
-th core of the MCF in the plane
\mathcal{Z}
, the illumination reads

\textstyle S(\bm{x};\bm{\alpha}):=\big|\!\sum_{q=1}^{Q}\alpha_{q}E_{q}(\bm{x})\big|^{2},

(12)

and similar developments to Sec.
II-A
provide

\bar{y}_{m}=\bm{\alpha}_{m}^{*}\,\bm{G}[f]\,\bm{\alpha}_{m}=\big\langle\bm{\alpha}_{m}\bm{\alpha}_{m}^{*},\bm{G}[f]\big\rangle_{F}

(13)

where we defined, for any function
h:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
, the Hermitian matrix
\bm{G}[h]\in\mathcal{H}^{Q}
with entries

\textstyle G_{jk}[h]:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}E_{j}^{*}(\bm{x})E_{k}(\bm{x})h(\bm{x})\mathrm{d}\bm{x}.

(14)

By recording a spatial discretization of the fields
\{E_{q}(\bm{x})\}_{q=1}^{Q}
, we can thus estimate the forward model (
14
)—and thus
\bm{\mathcal{H}}[h]:=(\bm{\alpha}_{m}^{*}\,\bm{G}[h]\,\bm{\alpha}_{m})_{m=1}^{M}
—for any function
h
, as imposed to solve the inverse problem (
13
) with practical algorithms.
While slower than the computation of
\mathcal{I}_{\Omega}[f^{\circ}]
(
e.g.
, with a FFT boosting) and its
M
SROP, estimating
\bm{\mathcal{H}}
directly integrates many deviations to the interferometric model, with a calibration limited to the observation of
\mathcal{O}(Q)
discretized spatial intensities aimed to yield
\{E_{q}(\bm{x})\}_{q=1}^{Q}
. We detail in Sec.
V-B
how to practically achieve this calibration.

III
Interferometric matrix reconstruction

It is possible to recover the interferometric matrix
\bm{\mathcal{I}}_{\Omega}
from its SROPs
\bm{y}
, which allows for subsequent image estimation from that matrix. However, as made clear below, that procedure provides a suboptimal sample complexity compared to the direct sensing approach (combining SROPs and the interferometric sensing) proposed in Sec.
IV
.

With Prop.
3
provided in Appendix
A
, we first show that
\mathcal{O}(Q^{2})
deterministic sketching vectors suffice to reconstruct any interferometric matrix
\bm{\mathcal{I}}_{\Omega}
in a noiseless scenario,
i.e.
, a sample complexity upper bound to any further compressive measurements of this matrix.

Recovering
\bm{\mathcal{I}}_{\Omega}
in less than
\mathcal{O}(Q^{2})
SROP is possible if this matrix, and thus
f
, respects specific low-complexity models. First,
\bm{\mathcal{I}}_{0}:=\bm{\mathcal{I}}_{\Omega}[f^{\circ}]
is Hermitian. Moreover, if
f^{\circ}
is non-negative, this matrix is positive semi-definite since from (
4
), for any
\bm{v}\in\mathbb{C}^{Q}
,

\begin{gathered}\textstyle\bm{v}^{*}\bm{\mathcal{I}}_{0}\bm{v}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}f^{\circ}(\bm{x})\sum_{j,k}v_{j}^{*}v_{k}e^{\frac{\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu2\pi}{\lambda z}(\bm{p}_{k}-\bm{p}_{j})^{\top}\bm{x}}\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\\
\textstyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}f^{\circ}(\bm{x})|\bm{v}^{*}\bm{\rho}(\bm{x})|^{2}\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\geqslant 0,\end{gathered}

(15)

where
\bm{\rho}=(\rho_{1},\ldots,\rho_{Q})\in\mathbb{C}^{Q}
with
\rho_{j}(\bm{x}):=e^{-\frac{\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu2\pi}{\lambda z}\bm{p}_{j}^{\top}\bm{x}}
.

Second, if
f^{\circ}
is composed of a few Dirac spikes,
i.e.
, if
f^{\circ}(\bm{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{K}u_{i}\delta(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{i})
for
K
coefficients and locations
\{(u_{i},\bm{x}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{K}
, the interferometric matrix has rank-
K
since (
4
) reduces to the sum of
K
rank-one matrices,
i.e.
,

\textstyle\bm{\mathcal{I}}_{\Omega}[f^{\circ}]=\sum_{i=1}^{K}u_{i}\,\bm{\rho}(\bm{x}_{i})\bm{\rho}^{*}(\bm{x}_{i}).

(16)

Under this structural assumption, or if
\bm{\mathcal{I}}_{0}
is well approximated by a rank-
K
matrix
(\bm{\mathcal{I}}_{0})_{K}
, we can recover
\bm{\mathcal{I}}_{0}
with high probability provided the sketching vectors
\{\bm{\alpha}_{m}\}_{m=1}^{M}
, and thus
\bm{\mathcal{A}}
, are random,
i.e.
, their entries are i.i.d. from a centered sub-Gaussian distribution
[
21
, Thm 1]
. In particular, with

M\geqslant M_{0}=O(KQ),

(17)

and probability exceeding
1-\exp(-cM)
, any matrix
\bm{\mathcal{I}}_{0}
observed through the model

\bm{y}=\bm{\mathcal{A}}(\bm{\mathcal{I}}_{0})+\bm{\eta}
, with bounded noise
\|\bm{\eta}\|_{1}\leqslant\varepsilon
, can be estimated from

\tilde{\bm{\mathcal{I}}}\in\arg\min_{\bm{\mathcal{I}}}\|\bm{\mathcal{I}}\|_{\ast}\ \ {\rm s.t.}\ \ \bm{\mathcal{I}}\succcurlyeq 0,\ \|\bm{y}-\bm{\mathcal{A}}(\bm{\mathcal{I}})\|_{1}\leqslant\varepsilon.

This solution is instance optimal,
i.e.
, for some
C,D>0
,

\textstyle\|\tilde{\bm{\mathcal{I}}}-\bm{\mathcal{I}}_{0}\|_{F}\leqslant C\frac{\|\bm{\mathcal{I}}_{0}-(\bm{\mathcal{I}}_{0})_{K}\|_{\ast}}{\sqrt{K}}+D\frac{\varepsilon}{M}.

(18)

The sample complexity in (
17
) is, however, not optimal since for a
K
-sparse
f^{\circ}
,
\bm{\mathcal{I}}_{0}
depends only on
\mathcal{O}(K)
parameters in (
16
). While
[
21
]
provides similar results with reduced sample complexity provided
\bm{\mathcal{I}}_{0}
is,
e.g.
, sparse or circulant, these models are not applicable here and we show in Sec.
IV
that a smaller sample complexity is achievable under certain simplifying assumptions.

IV
Image reconstruction

Let us consider a compressive sensing framework whose objective is to explore the imaging capability of MCF-LI,
i.e.
, we study the problem of directly estimating sparse images from their rank-one projected partial Fourier sampling, as driven by the two sensing components in (
3
). As proved in Sec.
IV-D
, from simplifying assumptions made on both
f^{\circ}
and the sensing scenario (see Sec.
IV-A
), this method achieved reduced sample complexities compared to the approach in Sec.
III
, which are also confirmed numerically in Sec.
IV-E
.

IV-A

Working assumptions

We first assume a bounded field of view in MCF-LI.

Assumption A.1

(Bounded FOV)
.

The support of the vignetting window
w(\bm{x})
in (
1
) is contained in a domain
\mathcal{D}:=[-L/2,L/2]\times[-L/2,L/2]
with
L:=\frac{c\lambda z}{d}
, for
c>0
depending on the (spectrum of the) output wavefield
E_{0}
in (
1
), and
w=0
on the frontier of
D
.

Therefore, supposing
f
bounded, we have
{\rm supp}\,f^{\circ}\subset\mathcal{D}
and
f^{\circ}=0
over the frontier of
D
.

We also need to discretize
f^{\circ}
by assuming it bandlimited.

Assumption A.2

(Bounded and bandlimited image)
.

The image
f
is bounded, and
f^{\circ}
is bandlimited with bandlimit
\frac{W}{2}
, with
W:=\frac{N_{1}}{L}
and
N_{1}\in\mathbb{N}
,
i.e.
,
\mathcal{F}[f^{\circ}](\bm{\chi})=0
for all
\bm{\chi}
with
\|\bm{\chi}\|_{\infty}\geqslant\frac{W}{2}
.

As will be clear below, this assumption enables the computation of the interferometric matrix
\bm{\mathcal{I}}_{\Omega}[f^{\circ}]
from the discrete Fourier transform of the following discretization of
f^{\circ}
.

From (
A.1
) and (
A.2
) the function
f^{\circ}
can be identified with a vector
\bm{f}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
of
N=N_{1}^{2}
components. Up to a pixel rearrangement, each component
f_{j}
of
\bm{f}
is related to one specific pixel of
f^{\circ}
taken in the
N
-point grid

\textstyle\mathcal{G}_{N}:=\frac{L}{N_{1}}\{(s_{1},s_{2})\}_{s_{1},s_{2}=-\frac{N_{1}}{2}}^{\frac{N_{1}}{2}-1}\subset\mathcal{D}.

The discrete Fourier transform (DFT)
\hat{\bm{f}}
of
\bm{f}
is then computed from the 2-D DFT matrix
\bm{F}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
,
i.e.
,
\hat{\bm{f}}=\bm{F}\bm{f}\in\mathbb{C}^{N},\quad\bm{F}:=\bm{F}_{1}\otimes\bm{F}_{1}
,
with
(\bm{F}_{1})_{kl}=e^{-\frac{\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu2\pi}{N_{1}}kl}/\sqrt{N_{1}}
,
k,l\in[N_{1}]
, and the Kronecker product
\otimes
. Each component of
\hat{\bm{f}}
is related to a 2-D frequency of

\textstyle\hat{\mathcal{G}}_{N}:=\frac{W}{N_{1}}\{\chi_{1},\chi_{2}\}_{\chi_{1},\chi_{2}=-\frac{N_{1}}{2}}^{\frac{N_{1}}{2}-1}\subset[-\frac{W}{2},\frac{W}{2}]\times[-\frac{W}{2},\frac{W}{2}].

We need now to simplify our selection of the visibilities.

Assumption A.3

(Distinct on-grid non-zero visibilities)
.

All non-zero visibilities in
\mathcal{V}_{0}=\mathcal{V}\setminus\{\bm{0}\}
belong to the regular grid
\hat{\mathcal{G}}_{N}
,
i.e.
,
\mathcal{V}_{0}\subset\hat{\mathcal{G}}_{N}
, and are unique, which means that
|\mathcal{V}_{0}|=Q(Q-1)
.

Anticipating over Sec.
IV-D
, assumptions
A.1
and
A.2
show that
\bm{\mathcal{I}}_{\Omega}[f^{\circ}]
can be computed from
\bm{F}\bm{f}
;
for each visibility
\bm{\nu}_{jk}\in\mathcal{V}
, there is an index
\bar{l}=\bar{l}(j,k)\in[N]
such that

\textstyle(\bm{\mathcal{I}}_{\Omega}[f^{\circ}])_{j,k}=\varpi\,(\bm{F}\bm{f})_{\bar{l}}
, where
\varpi:=\frac{L^{2}}{\sqrt{N}}
can be found from the continuous interpolation formula of the Shannon-Nyquist sampling theorem.

Moreover, from
A.3
,
\bar{l}(j,k)
is unique for all
j\neq k
(
i.e.
,
\nu_{j,k}\in\mathcal{V}_{0}
), and since
(\bm{\mathcal{I}}_{\Omega}[f^{\circ}])_{j,j}=\varpi(\bm{F}\bm{f})_{0}
(
i.e.
,
\bar{l}(j,j)=0
) for all
j\in[Q]
, we get the equivalence

\bm{\mathcal{I}}_{\Omega}[f^{\circ}]=\varpi\,\mathcal{T}(\bm{F}\bm{f}),

(19)

where the operator
\mathcal{T}
is such that, for all
j,k\in[Q]
and
\bm{u}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
,
(\mathcal{T}(\bm{u}))_{jk}
equals
u_{0}
if
j=k
, and
u_{\bar{l}(j,k)}
otherwise.

Consequently, if
f^{\circ}
has zero mean,
(\bm{F}\bm{f})_{0}=0
and

\textstyle\frac{1}{\varpi^{2}}\|\bm{\mathcal{I}}_{\Omega}[f^{\circ}]\|_{F}^{2}=\|\bm{R}_{\overline{\mathcal{V}}_{0}}\bm{F}\bm{f}\|^{2},

(20)

with
\bm{R}_{\mathcal{S}}=\Id_{\mathcal{S}}^{\top}
the restriction operator defined for any
\mathcal{S}\subset[N]
, and
\overline{\mathcal{V}}_{0}=\{\,\bar{l}(j,k):j,k\in[Q],j\neq k\}\subset[N]
the index set of
\bm{F}\bm{f}
related to the off-diagonal entries of
\bm{\mathcal{I}}_{\Omega}[f^{\circ}]
(with
|\mathcal{V}_{0}|=|\overline{\mathcal{V}}_{0}|
from
A.3
).

We need to
regularize
the (ill-posed) MCF-LI imaging problem by supposing that
\bm{f}
is
sparse
in the canonical basis.

Assumption A.4

(Sparse sample image)
.

The discrete image
\bm{f}
is
K
-sparse (in the canonical basis):

\bm{f}\in\Sigma_{K}:=\{\bm{v}:\|\bm{v}\|_{0}\leqslant K\}
.

While restrictive, our experiments in Sec.
V
show experimentally that other sparsity priors are compatible with our sensing scheme,
e.g.
, the TV norm, postponing to a future work a theoretical justification of such possible extensions.

Our theoretical analysis leverages the tools of compressive sensing theory
[
24
,
25
]
. In particular, as stated in the next assumption, we require that the interferometric matrix—actually, its non-diagonal entries encoded in the visibilities of
\mathcal{V}_{0}
—captures enough information about any sparse image
\bm{f}
.

Assumption A.5

(RIP
{}_{\ell_{2}/\ell_{2}}
for visibility sampling)
.

Given a sparsity level
K
, a distortion
\delta>0
, and provided

|\mathcal{V}_{0}|=Q(Q-1)\geqslant\delta^{-2}K\,{\rm plog}(N,K,\delta),

(21)

for some polynomials
{\rm plog}(N,K,1/\delta)
of
\log N
,
\log K
and
\log 1/\delta
, the matrix
\bm{\Phi}:=\sqrt{N}\bm{R}_{\overline{\mathcal{V}}_{0}}\bm{F}
respects the
(\ell_{2}/\ell_{2})
-restricted isometry, or RIP
{}_{\ell_{2}/\ell_{2}}(\Sigma_{K},\delta)
,
i.e.
,

\textstyle(1-\delta)\|\bm{v}\|^{2}\leqslant{\textstyle\frac{1}{|\mathcal{V}_{0}|}}\|\bm{\Phi}\bm{v}\|^{2}_{2}\leqslant(1+\delta)\|\bm{v}\|^{2},\ \forall\bm{v}\in\Sigma_{K}.

(22)

As will be clear later, combined with (
20
), this assumption ensures that two different sparse images lead to two distinct interferometric matrices, a key element for stably estimating images from our sensing model (see Prop.
1
).

We specify now the distribution of the sketching vectors
\bm{\alpha}
.

Assumption A.6

(Random sketches with unit modules)
.

The sketching vectors
\{\bm{\alpha}_{m}\}_{m=1}^{M}
involved in (
5
) have components i.i.d. as the random variable
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
, with
\mathbb{E}\alpha=0
and
|\alpha|=1
.

The sketching vectors are thus sub-Gaussian, since the sub-Gaussian norm
\|\alpha_{q}\|_{\psi_{2}}=\||\alpha_{q}|\|_{\psi_{2}}=1
is bounded (see
[
35
, Sec 5.2.3]
). While motivated by the MCF-LI application where the SLM mainly acts on the phase of the core complex amplitudes, this assumption enables a
debiasing trick
, described in Sec.
IV-C
, which simplifies the theoretical analysis detailed in Sec.
IV-D

IV-B

Rationale and limitations of our assumptions

We here discuss the rationale and limitations of our assumptions. First, both Assumptions
A.3
and
A.5
are built on the multiset
\mathcal{V}_{0}
(listing all the non-zero visibilities) and not
\mathcal{V}
. Anticipating over Sec.
IV-D
, this choice is imposed by the SROP measurements; they are strongly biased by the diagonal elements of the projected matrices (see also Lemma.
4
in App.
C
). As a result, we need a
debiased
sensing model (see Sec.
IV-C
) removing the diagonal of the interferometric matrix, and hence the zero image frequency.

Second, while we do not prove that the visibility set
\mathcal{V}_{0}
defined by the Fermat’s spiral core arrangement
\Omega
in MCF-LI verifies
A.5
, we invoke existing results characterizing compressive sensing with partial Fourier sampling—as established for instance in the context of tomographic and radio interferometric applications
[
33
,
13
]
—to prove the existence of a visibility set respecting
A.5
.
For example, from
[
25
, Thm 12.31]
, if
Q(Q-1)\geqslant C\delta^{-2}K\log^{4}(N)
(for some constant
C>0
) and the set of
Q(Q-1)
visibilities
\overline{\mathcal{V}}_{0}
are picked uniformly at random in
[N]
, then
\bm{\Phi}
respects the RIP
{}_{\ell_{2}/\ell_{2}}(\Sigma_{K},\delta)
with probability exceeding
1-N^{-\log^{3}N}
.

Third, as stated by Assumption
A.3
, our analysis expects that each visibility, except the 0 frequency, is only observed once. However, for large values of
Q
(and certainly if
Q(Q-1)>N
), low-frequency visibilities tend to occupy the same points in the gridded frequency domain
\hat{\mathcal{G}}_{N}
. For instance, for a 1-D configuration, the pdf of the visibilities would be centered and triangular—with thus increasing multiplicity at low-frequency—if the frequencies were drawn uniformly at random in a given frequency interval.

Relaxing
A.3
would require adapting our developments in new directions. To account for possible multiplicities in the visibilities, we can indeed introduce a weighting matrix
\bm{W}
encoding the number of entries in
\bm{\mathcal{I}}_{\Omega}[f^{\circ}]
that are related to the same frequency,
i.e.
, using the notations defined above,
W_{qq}:=|\{(j,k):\bar{l}(j,k)=q\}|
,
1\leqslant q\leqslant N
. In that case, (
20
) becomes

\textstyle\frac{1}{\varpi^{2}}\|\bm{\mathcal{I}}_{\Omega}[f^{\circ}]\|_{F}^{2}=\|\bm{W}\bm{R}_{\overline{\mathcal{V}}_{0}}\bm{F}\bm{f}\|^{2}.

However, as our analysis requires that
\|\bm{W}\bm{R}_{\overline{\mathcal{V}}_{0}}\bm{F}\bm{f}\|^{2}
is approximately proportional to the norm of
\|\bm{f}\|^{2}
if
\bm{f}
is sparse,
i.e.
, the matrix
\bm{\Phi}^{\prime}:=\bm{W}\bm{R}_{\overline{\mathcal{V}}_{0}}\bm{F}
should respect the RIP
[
32
]
, we then need to adapt Assumption
A.5
to that matrix. Unfortunately, as soon as
\bm{W}\neq\Id
, recovering sparse signals from their random partial Fourier sampling imposes to reweight
\bm{\Phi}^{\prime}
to cancel out the impact of
\bm{W}

[
36
,
40
]
, both for ensuring the RIP of this matrix and for the stability of the numerical reconstructions. Unfortunately, as we only access to the SROP of the interferometric matrix, it is unclear how to introduce this cancellation in our sensing model.

Finally, Assumption
A.4
is restricted to sparse signals in the canonical basis. Generalizing our recovery guarantees developed in Sec.
IV-D
to general sparsifying bases
\bm{\Psi}\neq\Id
is, however, challenging. Certain bases, such as the Haar wavelet basis
\bm{\Psi}
, includes the constant vector. Assuming it placed on the 1-st column of
\bm{\Psi}
, we easily check that
the RIP
{}_{\ell_{2}/\ell_{2}}
in (
22
) cannot hold anymore for
\bm{\Phi}=\sqrt{N}\bm{R}_{\bar{\mathcal{V}}_{0}}\bm{F}\bm{\Psi}
; since
\bar{\mathcal{V}}_{0}
excludes the DC frequency, taking
\bm{v}=\lambda\bm{e}_{1}+\bm{e}_{k}\in\Sigma_{2}
with a sufficiently large value
\lambda
breaks (
22
) as
\bm{\Phi}\bm{v}=\sqrt{N}\bm{R}_{\bar{\mathcal{V}}_{0}}\bm{F}\bm{\Psi}\bm{e}_{k}
.

IV-C

Debiased sensing model

As made clear in Sec.
IV-D
, the estimation of
\bm{f}
requires a
debiasing
of the MCF-LI measurements imposed by the properties of the SROP operator
\bm{\mathcal{A}}
in (
3
). We follow a debiasing inspired by
[
12
]
(and allowed by
A.6
), with a reduced number of measurements compared to the method proposed in
[
21
]
.

From (
5
), we define the
debiased
measurements

\displaystyle\textstyle y^{\rm c}_{m}:=y_{m}-\tfrac{1}{M}\sum_{j=1}^{M}y_{j}=\langle\bm{A}^{\rm c}_{m},\bm{\mathcal{I}}\rangle_{F}+n^{\rm c}_{m},

(23)

with the centered and the average matrices
\bm{A}^{\rm c}_{m}=\bm{\alpha}_{m}\bm{\alpha}_{m}^{*}-\bm{A}^{\rm a}
and
\bm{A}^{\rm a}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\bm{\alpha}_{j}\bm{\alpha}_{j}^{*}
, respectively,
\bm{\mathcal{I}}:=\bm{\mathcal{I}}_{\Omega}[f^{\circ}]
, and noise
n^{\rm c}_{m}:=n_{m}-\tfrac{1}{M}\sum_{j=1}^{M}n_{j}
with
\mathbb{E}|n^{\rm c}_{m}|^{2}=(1-\frac{1}{M})\mathbb{E}|n_{m}|^{2}
.

Introducing the debiased sensing operator

\bm{\mathcal{A}}^{\rm c}:\bm{\mathcal{J}}\in\mathcal{H}^{Q}\mapsto\big(\langle\bm{A}^{\rm c}_{m},\bm{\mathcal{J}}\rangle\big)_{m=1}^{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{M},

(24)

which respects
\bm{\mathcal{A}}^{\rm c}(\bm{\mathcal{J}})=\bm{\mathcal{A}}^{\rm c}(\bm{\mathcal{J}}_{\rm h})
with the
hollow
matrix
\bm{\mathcal{J}}_{\rm h}:=\bm{\mathcal{J}}-\bm{\mathcal{J}}_{\rm d}
(
i.e.
,
\diag(\bm{\mathcal{J}}_{\rm h})=\bm{0}
) since each
\bm{A}^{\rm c}_{m}
is hollow from
A.6
, we can compactly write

\textstyle\bm{y}^{\rm c}=(y^{\rm c}_{1},\,\ldots,y^{\rm c}_{M})^{\top}=\bm{\mathcal{A}}^{\rm c}(\bm{\mathcal{I}}_{\rm h})+\bm{n}^{\rm c}.

We have tested our approach on proof-of-concept imaging system set in a transmission mode so as to limit both light power loss and Poisson noise

V-A

Setup

In the setup explained in Fig.
4
, a continuous wave laser operating at
\lambda=1053
nm, (YLM-1, IPG Photonics) is expanded and impinges upon a Spatial Light Modulator (SLM X10468-07, Hammamatsu) used to code the incident wavefront to the MCF. The MCF is made of
Q=110
cores arranged in Fermat’s golden spiral, each exhibiting a single mode at the laser wavelength
[
12
]
. The MCF exhibits an inter-core coupling term less than 20 dB
[
16
]
. Unlike multimode fibers with stronger core coupling, the focused or speckle patterns generated by an MCF are resilient to thermal and mechanical external perturbations.

The SLM consists of a
800\times 600
grid of liquid-crystal phase modulators that control the phase of reflected light. As shown in Fig.
4
(a), by mapping specific pixel groups (segments) on the SLM to individual cores of the fiber, an orthogonal basis of input modes is created to modulate the light entering each core. After calibrating the SLM’s phase response, any phase pattern in the range of [
0
-
2\pi
] can be conveniently represented as an 8-bit grayscale image.
The phase pattern on each segment
q
comprises three terms
(i)
a blazed grating ensures to shift the modulated light to the first-order of the SLM, preventing unmodulated beam from entering the fibers,
(ii)
a convex lens and a series of telescopes produce a focused spot array aligned with the fiber cores, achieving single-modal behavior with a demagnification factor of 64; and
(iii)
a constant phase-offset for each segment which controls the relative phases between the segments.

The light coming from the SLM is focused into the MCF proximal end by Obj
1
(
20\times
/
0.75
NA, Nikon), then re-expanded on the distal end side with Obj
2
(
20\times
/
0.45
NA, Olympus). The dependency to fiber bending is avoided by placing the MCF straight. In these conditions, the transmission matrix remains constant and a single calibration phase is sufficient. However, it is possible to greatly attenuate the MCF sensitivity to external perturbation such as mechanical bending by twisting the MCF along the fiber during its manufacture
[
17
]
. To ensure the validity of the scalar model described in Sec.
II-A
, a linear polarizer is placed after the fiber end to eliminate any polarization effects.
To satisfy the far-field approximation, the object is positioned at the front-focal plane of a lens while the fiber’s distal end is placed at the back-focal plane of the same lens
[
38
]
. In our setup, the fiber is positioned at the focal plane of Objective lens (Obj
2
), and lenses L
1
and L

(

The conjugate focal plane is re-imaged onto a
{1\,920\times 1\,200}

V-B

VI
Conclusion and Perspectives

In this paper, we extended the modeling of MCF-LI with speckle illumination by including the physics of light propagation. This new model highlights that the sensing of a 2-D refractive index map of interest is limited both by the number of applied illuminations and the number (and arrangement) of cores at the distal end of the MCF. We provided recovery guarantees and observed the derived sample complexities in both numerical and experimental conditions.

A future research could open our recovery guarantees in Sec.
IV
to general sparsifying bases
\bm{\Psi}\neq\Id
. This would require particularizing our proofs to sparse signals with zero mean,
i.e.
, belonging to
\Sigma_{K}^{0}:=\{\bm{v}\in\Sigma_{K}:\sum_{j}(\bm{\Psi}\bm{v})_{j}=0\}
.

As announced in Sec.
IV-B
, a limitation of our approach lies in the distinct visibility Assumption
A.3
. By construction, the density of the visibilities—as achieved by a difference set—cannot be uniform. As shown in Fig.
1
(b), this is also true for the golden Fermat’s spiral arrangement. Therefore, when
Q
grows on a fixed frequency resolution, close visibilities are hardly distinguishable. A more promising sensing model could integrate a variable density sampling (VDS) of the image spectral domain
[
39
,
40
]
. In the same time, this could also allow for more general sparsifying basis by accounting for their variable local coherence with the Fourier basis. However, combining this aspect inside the ROP model is an open question.

Future works about MCF-LI include experimental proof of concept in reflective/endoscopic conditions, extension of the model to vector diffraction theory, and imaging of 3-D maps with generalized ROP models.

\|\xi_{j}\|_{\psi_{1}}\leqslant\kappa^{2}
. Therefore, using again
[
35
, Cor. 5.17]
, we have, with a failure probability lower than
2\exp(-cM\min(\frac{t^{2}}{\kappa^{4}},\frac{t}{\kappa^{2}}))
,

\textstyle-t\ \leqslant\langle\frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\bm{\alpha}_{j}\bm{\alpha}_{j}^{*},\bm{\mathcal{J}}_{\rm h}\rangle\leqslant t,

for some
c>0
. The result follows from a union bound on the failure of this event and the event (
46
) in Lemma
6
, both inequalities and (
48
) justifying (
47
).
∎

As a simple corollary of the previous lemma, we can now establish the concentration of

\bm{\mathcal{B}}(\bm{f}):=\varpi\bm{\mathcal{A}}^{\rm c}\big(\mathcal{T}(\bm{F}\bm{f})\big)\in\mathbb{R}^{M}_{+}

in the
\ell_{1}
-norm for an arbitrary
K
-sparse vector
\bm{f}\in\Sigma_{K}
.

Corollary 1

(Concentration of
\bm{\mathcal{B}}
in the
\ell_{1}
-norm)
.

In the context of Lemma
7
, suppose that
A.1
-
A.6
are respected, with
A.5
set with sparsity level
{K_{0}}>0
and distortion
\delta=1/2
. Given
\bm{f}\in\Sigma_{K_{0}}
, and the operator
\bm{\mathcal{B}}
defined in (
27
) from the
M
SROP measurements and the
|\mathcal{V}_{0}|=Q(Q-1)
non-zero visibilities with

Q(Q-1)\geqslant 4{K_{0}}\,{\rm plog}(N,{K_{0}},\delta),

we have, with a failure probability smaller than
2\exp(-c^{\prime}M)
(for some
c^{\prime}>0
depending only on the distribution of
\alpha
),

\textstyle\frac{\varpi c_{\alpha}}{2\sqrt{2}}\frac{\sqrt{|\mathcal{V}_{0}}|}{\sqrt{N}}\,\|\bm{f}\|\leqslant{\textstyle\frac{1}{M}}\|\bm{\mathcal{B}}(\bm{f})\|_{1}\leqslant 2\varpi\frac{\sqrt{|\mathcal{V}_{0}}|}{\sqrt{N}}\,\|\bm{f}\|.

Proof.

Given
\bm{f}\in\Sigma_{K_{0}}
and
\bm{\mathcal{J}}=\mathcal{T}(\bm{F}\bm{f})\in\mathcal{H}^{Q}
, let us assume that (
47
) holds on this matrix with
t=c_{\alpha}/(6\kappa^{2})<1/6
, an event with probability of failure smaller than
2\exp(-c^{\prime}M)
with
c^{\prime}>0
depending only on
c_{\alpha}
and
\kappa
,
i.e.
, on the distribution of
\alpha
. We first note that
\|\bm{\mathcal{J}}_{\rm h}\|_{F}=\|\bm{R}_{\overline{\mathcal{V}}_{0}}\bm{F}\bm{f}\|
from (
20
). Second,

\textstyle{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\|\bm{f}\|^{2}\leqslant\tfrac{N}{|\mathcal{V}_{0}|}\|\bm{R}_{\overline{\mathcal{V}}_{0}}\bm{F}\bm{f}\|^{2}\leqslant\frac{3}{2}\|\bm{f}\|^{2}.

(49)

since from
A.5
the matrix
\bm{\Phi}:=\sqrt{N}\bm{R}_{\overline{\mathcal{V}}_{0}}\bm{F}
respects the RIP
{}_{\ell_{2}/\ell_{2}}(\Sigma_{K_{0}},\delta=1/2)
as soon as
|\mathcal{V}_{0}|=Q(Q-1)\geqslant 4{K_{0}}\,{\rm plog}(N,{K_{0}},\delta)
.
Therefore, since
\bm{\mathcal{B}}(\bm{f})=\varpi\bm{\mathcal{A}}^{\rm c}(\bm{\mathcal{J}})=\varpi\bm{\mathcal{A}}^{\rm c}(\bm{\mathcal{J}}_{\rm h})
, combining (
47
) and (
49
) gives

\textstyle{\textstyle\frac{1}{M}}\|\bm{\mathcal{B}}(\bm{f})\|_{1}\geqslant(c_{\alpha}-3t\kappa^{2})\varpi\|\bm{\mathcal{J}}_{\rm h}\|_{F}\\
\textstyle={\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}c_{\alpha}\varpi\|\bm{R}_{\overline{\mathcal{V}}_{0}}\bm{F}\bm{f}\|\geqslant\frac{\varpi c_{\alpha}}{2\sqrt{2}}\,\frac{\sqrt{|\mathcal{V}_{0}|}}{\sqrt{N}}\,\|\bm{f}\|.

Similarly, using
\sqrt{\frac{3}{2}}(1+3t\kappa^{2})<(\frac{3}{2})^{3/2}<2
, we get

\textstyle{\textstyle\frac{1}{M}}\|\bm{\mathcal{B}}(\bm{f})\|_{1}\leqslant\sqrt{\frac{3}{2}}(1+3t\kappa^{2})\varpi\,\frac{\sqrt{\mathcal{V}_{0}}}{\sqrt{N}}\,\|\bm{f}\|<2\varpi\frac{\sqrt{|\mathcal{V}_{0}}|}{\sqrt{N}}\,\|\bm{f}\|.

∎

We are now ready to prove Prop.
2
. We will follow the standard proof strategy developed in
[
42
]
. By homogeneity of the RIP
{}_{\ell_{2}/\ell_{1}}
in (
28
), we restrict the proof to unit vectors
\bm{f}
of
\Sigma_{K_{0}}
,
i.e.
,
\bm{f}\in\Sigma_{K_{0}}^{*}:=\Sigma_{K_{0}}\cap\mathbb{S}^{N-1}_{2}
.

Given a radius
0<\lambda<1
, let
\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}\subset\Sigma^{*}_{K_{0}}
be a
\lambda
covering of
\Sigma^{*}_{K_{0}}
,
i.e.
, for all
\bm{f}\in\Sigma^{*}_{K_{0}}
, there exists a
\bm{f}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}
, with
{\rm supp}\,\bm{f}^{\prime}={\rm supp}\,\bm{f}
, such that
\|\bm{f}-\bm{f}^{\prime}\|\leqslant\lambda
. Such a covering exists and its cardinality is smaller than
{N\choose{K_{0}}}(1+\frac{2}{\lambda})^{{K_{0}}}\leqslant(\frac{3eN}{{K_{0}}\lambda})^{K_{0}}

[
42
]
.

Invoking Cor.
1
, we can apply the union bound to all points of the covering so that

\textstyle\ \forall\bm{f}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{G}_{\lambda},\ \textstyle\frac{\varpi c_{\alpha}}{2\sqrt{2}}\frac{\sqrt{|\mathcal{V}_{0}|}}{\sqrt{N}}\leqslant{\textstyle\frac{1}{M}}\|\bm{\mathcal{B}}(\bm{f}^{\prime})\|_{1}\leqslant 2\varpi\frac{\sqrt{|\mathcal{V}_{0}|}}{\sqrt{N}},

(50)

holds with failure probability smaller than

\textstyle 2(\frac{3eN}{{K_{0}}\lambda})^{K_{0}}\exp(-c^{\prime}M)\leqslant 2\exp({K_{0}}\ln(\frac{3eN}{{K_{0}}\lambda})-c^{\prime}M).

Therefore, there exists a constant
C>0
such that, if
M\geqslant C{K_{0}}\ln(\frac{3eN}{{K_{0}}\lambda})
, then (
50
) holds with probability exceeding
1-2\exp(-c^{\prime\prime}M)
, for some
c^{\prime\prime}>0
.

Let us assume that this event holds. Then, for any
\bm{f}\in\Sigma_{K_{0}}
,

\displaystyle\textstyle{\textstyle\frac{1}{M}}\|\bm{\mathcal{B}}(\bm{f})\|_{1}

\displaystyle\textstyle\leqslant{\textstyle\frac{1}{M}}\|\bm{\mathcal{B}}(\bm{f}^{\prime})\|_{1}+{\textstyle\frac{1}{M}}\|\bm{\mathcal{B}}(\bm{f}-\bm{f}^{\prime})\|_{1}

\displaystyle\textstyle\leqslant 2\varpi\frac{\sqrt{|\mathcal{V}_{0}|}}{\sqrt{N}}+{\textstyle\frac{1}{M}}\|\bm{\mathcal{B}}(\frac{\bm{f}-\bm{f}^{\prime}}{\|\bm{f}-\bm{f}^{\prime}\|})\|_{1}\|\bm{f}-\bm{f}^{\prime}\|

\displaystyle\textstyle\leqslant 2\varpi\frac{\sqrt{|\mathcal{V}_{0}|}}{\sqrt{N}}+{\textstyle\frac{1}{M}}\|\bm{\mathcal{B}}(\bm{r})\|_{1}\lambda,

with the unit vector
\bm{r}:=\frac{\bm{f}-\bm{f}^{\prime}}{\|\bm{f}-\bm{f}^{\prime}\|}
. However, this vector
\bm{r}
is itself
{K_{0}}
-sparse since
\bm{f}
and
\bm{f}^{\prime}
share the same support. Therefore, applying recursively the same argument on the last term above, and using the fact that
\|\bm{\mathcal{B}}(\bm{w})\|_{1}
is bounded for any unit vector
\bm{w}
, we get

{\textstyle\frac{1}{M}}\|\bm{\mathcal{B}}(\bm{r})\|_{1}\lambda\leqslant 2\varpi\frac{\sqrt{|\mathcal{V}_{0}|}}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{j\geqslant 1}\lambda^{j}=2\frac{\lambda}{1-\lambda}\varpi\frac{\sqrt{|\mathcal{V}_{0}|}}{\sqrt{N}}
.

Consequently, since we also have

\displaystyle\textstyle{\textstyle\frac{1}{M}}\|\bm{\mathcal{B}}(\bm{f})\|_{1}

\displaystyle\textstyle\geqslant{\textstyle\frac{1}{M}}\|\bm{\mathcal{B}}(\bm{f}^{\prime})\|_{1}-{\textstyle\frac{1}{M}}\|\bm{\mathcal{B}}(\bm{f}-\bm{f}^{\prime})\|_{1}

\displaystyle\textstyle\geqslant\frac{\varpi c_{\alpha}}{2\sqrt{2}}\frac{\sqrt{|\mathcal{V}_{0}|}}{\sqrt{N}}-{\textstyle\frac{1}{M}}\|\bm{\mathcal{B}}(\bm{r})\|_{1}\lambda,

we conclude that

\textstyle\frac{\varpi c_{\alpha}}{2\sqrt{2}}(\frac{1-2\lambda}{1-\lambda})\frac{\sqrt{|\mathcal{V}_{0}|}}{\sqrt{N}}\leqslant{\textstyle\frac{1}{M}}\|\bm{\mathcal{B}}(\bm{f})\|_{1}\leqslant 2\varpi\frac{1}{1-\lambda}\frac{\sqrt{|\mathcal{V}_{0}|}}{\sqrt{N}},

Picking
\lambda=1/4
finally shows that, under the conditions described above,
\bm{\mathcal{B}}
respects the RIP
{}_{\ell_{2}/\ell_{1}}(\Sigma_{K_{0}},m_{K_{0}},M_{K_{0}})
with
m_{K_{0}}>\frac{\varpi c_{\alpha}}{3\sqrt{2}}\frac{\sqrt{|\mathcal{V}_{0}|}}{\sqrt{N}}
, and
M_{K_{0}}<\frac{8\varpi}{3}\frac{\sqrt{|\mathcal{V}_{0}|}}{\sqrt{N}}
.
