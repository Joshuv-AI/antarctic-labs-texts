Termination in Convex Sets of Distributions
Ana Sokolova, Harald Woracek

Abstract
. Convex algebras, also called (semi)convex sets, are at the heart of modelling probabilistic systems including probabilistic automata. Abstractly, they are the Eilenberg-Moore algebras of the finitely supported distribution monad. Concretely, they have been studied for decades within algebra and convex geometry. In this paper we study the problem of extending a convex algebra by a single point. Such extensions enable the modelling of termination in probabilistic systems. We provide a full description of all possible extensions for a particular class of convex algebras: For a fixed convex subset D D of a vector space satisfying additional technical condition, we consider the algebra of convex subsets of D D . This class contains the convex algebras of convex subsets of distributions, modelling (nondeterministic) probabilistic automata. We also provide a full description of all possible extensions for the class of free convex algebras, modelling fully probabilistic systems. Finally, we show that there is a unique functorial extension, the so-called black-hole extension.

As a consequence, on the concrete side, convexity has notably appeared in the semantics of probabilistic systems, in particular probabilistic automata

Additionally, on the abstract side, coalgebras over (categories of) algebras have attracted significant attention

For coalgebras over convex algebras, the most important observation is that convex algebras are the Eilenberg-Moore algebras of the finitely supported distribution monad

One contribution of

Stated algebraically, the questions we address in this paper are:

(1)

Given a convex algebra
\mathbb{X}
, is it possible to extend it by a single point?

(2)

If yes, what are all possible one-point extensions?

(3)

Which one-point extensions are functorial, i.e., do they provide a functor on
\mathcal{E}{\kern-0.86108pt}\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{D}{\mkern-3.5mu}_{f})
that could then be used for modelling probabilistic automata as coalgebras over

Observe that extensions by a single point are different from the coproduct

in
\mathcal{E}{\kern-0.86108pt}\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{D}_{f})

Despite a large body of work on convex algebras, to the best of our knowledge, the problem of extending a convex algebra by a single element has not been studied, except for the black-hole extension mentioned above, see
[

We answer the stated questions, and in particular our answers and main results are:

(1)

Yes, it is possible and there are many possible extensions in general. One of them is the mentioned black-hole extension.

(2)

We describe all possible extensions for the free convex algebra
\mathbb{D}_{S}
of probability distributions over a set

, see
Theorem

in Section

(3)

We prove that only the black-hole extension is functorial, see Theorem

In addition, we provide many smaller results, observations, and examples that add to the vast knowledge on convex algebras.

2.
Convex Algebras

By
\mathcal{C}
we denote the signature of convex algebras

\mathcal{C}=\{(p_{i})_{i=0}^{n}\mid n\in\mathbb{N},p_{i}\in[0,1],\sum_{i=0}^{n}p_{i}=1\}.

Intuitively, the
(n+1)
-ary operation symbol
(p_{i})_{i=0}^{n}
will be interpreted by a convex combination with coefficients
p_{i}

for
i=0,\dots,n
. For a real number
p\in[0,1]
we set
\bar{p}=1-p
.

{defi}

A
convex algebra

\mathbb{X}
is an algebra with signature
\mathcal{C}
, i.e., a set
X
together with an
operation
\sum_{i=0}^{n}p_{i}(-)_{i}
for each operational symbol
(p_{i})_{i=0}^{n}\in\mathcal{C}
,
such that the following two axioms hold:

•

Projection:
\sum_{i=0}^{n}p_{i}x_{i}=x_{j}
if
p_{j}=1
.

•

Barycenter:
\sum_{i=0}^{n}p_{i}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{m}q_{i,j}x_{j}\right)=\sum_{j=0}^{m}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{n}p_{i}q_{i,j}\right)x_{j}
.

We remark that the terminology in the literature is far from uniform. To give a few examples,
convex algebras are called “convex modules in
[
23
]
, “positive convex structures”
in
[
9
]
(where
X
is taken to be endowed with the discrete topology),
“finitely positively convex spaces” in
[
37
]
, and “sets with a convex structure”
in
[
35
]
.

Convex algebras are the Eilenberg-Moore algebras of the finitely-supported distribution monad
\mathcal{D}{\mkern-3.5mu}_{f}
on

\mathbf{Sets}
, cf.
[
35
, 4.1.3]
and
[
30
]
, see also
[
9
,
10
]
or

[
16
, Theorem 4]
where a concrete and simple proof is given.
A convex algebra homomorphism is a morphism in the Eilenberg-Moore category
\mathcal{E}{\kern-0.86108pt}\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{D}{\mkern-3.5mu}_{f})
.
Concretely, a convex algebra homomorphism
h
from
\mathbb{X}
to
\mathbb{Y}
is a
convex
(synonymously,
affine
) map,
i.e.,
h\colon X\to Y
with the property
h\left(\sum_{i=0}^{n}p_{i}x_{i}\right)=\sum_{i=0}^{n}p_{i}h(x_{i})
.

Remark 1
.

Let
\mathbb{X}
be a convex algebra. Then (for
p_{n}\neq 1
)

\sum_{i=0}^{n}p_{i}x_{i}=\overline{p_{n}}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\frac{p_{j}}{\overline{p_{n}}}x_{j}\right)+p_{n}x_{n}.

(1)

Hence, an
(n+1)
-ary convex combination can be written as a binary convex combination using an
n
-ary convex
combination. As a consequence, if
X
is a set that carries two convex algebras
\mathbb{X}_{1}
and
\mathbb{X}_{2}
with
operations
\sum_{i=0}^{n}p_{i}(-)_{i}
and
\bigoplus_{i=0}^{n}p_{i}(-)_{i}
, respectively (and binary versions
+
and
\oplus
,
respectively) such that
px+\bar{p}y=px\oplus\bar{p}y
for all
p,x,y
, then
\mathbb{X}_{1}=\mathbb{X}_{2}
.

One can also see Equation (
1
) as a definition – the classical definition of Stone
[
34
, Definition 1]
.
We make the connection explicit with the next proposition.

Proposition 2
.

Let
X
be a set with binary operations
px+\bar{p}y
for
x,y\in X
and
p\in(0,1)
.
Assume

•

Idempotence:
px+\bar{p}x=x
for all
x\in X,p\in(0,1)
.

•

Parametric commutativity:
px+\bar{p}y=\bar{p}y+px
for all
x,y\in X,p\in(0,1)
.

•

Parametric associativity:
p(qx+\bar{q}y)+\bar{p}z=pqx+\overline{pq}\left(\frac{p\bar{q}}{\overline{pq}}y+\frac{\bar{p}}{\overline{pq}}z\right)

for all
x,y,z\in X,p,q,\in(0,1)
.

Define
n
-ary convex operations inductively by the projection axiom and the formula (
1
). Then
X
becomes
a convex algebra.

Proof 2.1
.

The proof is carried out by induction along the lines of
[
34
, Lemma 1–Lemma 4]
.

This allows us to focus on binary convex combinations whenever more convenient.

{defi}

Let
\mathbb{X}
be a convex algebra, and
C\subseteq X
. Then

is called
convex
if
it is the carrier of a subalgebra of
\mathbb{X}
, i.e., if
px+\bar{p}y\in C
for all
x,y\in C
and
p\in(0,1)
.

Given convex algebras
\mathbb{X}_{i}
,

, the direct product
\prod_{i\in I}X_{i}
is a convex algebra

\prod_{i\in I}\mathbb{X}_{i}
with operations defined componentwise.
We call a relation on the carrier of a convex algebra

a

,
if it is a convex subset of
\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{X}
.

{defi}

Let
\mathbb{X}
be a convex algebra. An element

is
\mathbb{X}
-

\forall x,y\in X.\,\,\forall p\in(0,1).\,\,px+\bar{p}z=py+\bar{p}z\Rightarrow x=y.

The convex algebra

is

if every element of

is
\mathbb{X}
-cancellable.

{defi}

Let
\mathbb{X}
be a convex algebra. An element

adheres to
an element

,
notation
x\raisebox{-1.25pt}{$\TransformHoriz$}y

.

Observe that for a cancellative algebra the adherence relation equals the identity relation.
As examples show, the converse need not hold.
The following simple properties of adherence will be needed on multiple occasions.

Lemma 3
.

Let
\mathbb{X}
be a convex algebra. The following properties hold.

(1)

For all
x,y\in X
,
x\raisebox{-1.25pt}{$\TransformHoriz$}y
if and only if
px+\bar{p}y=y
for some
p\in(0,1)
.

(2)

The adherence relation is reflexive and convex.

(3)

For all
x,y\in X
, if
x\raisebox{-1.25pt}{$\TransformHoriz$}y
then
pz+\bar{p}x\raisebox{-1.25pt}{$\TransformHoriz$}pz+\bar{p}y

and
p\in(0,1)
.

(4)

If
z
is
\mathbb{X}
-cancellable, then for all

and

pz+\bar{p}x\raisebox{-1.25pt}{$\TransformHoriz$}pz+\bar{p}y\,\,\,\Rightarrow\,\,\,x\raisebox{-1.25pt}{$\TransformHoriz$}y.

Proof 2.2
.

(1)

Let
x,y\in X
.
Consider the map
\varphi\colon[0,1]\to\mathbb{X}
defined by
\varphi(p)=px+\bar{p}y

Easy calculations show that

(qp+\bar{q}r)x+\overline{(qp+\bar{q}r)}y=q(px+\bar{p}y)+\bar{q}(rx+\bar{r}y),

(2)

showing that

The implication

for some

is an equivalence

This shows that for all

and hence
x\raisebox{-1.25pt}{$\TransformHoriz$}y
.

(2)

Reflexivity is a direct consequence of idempotence.
Let

and assume

and

q(px+\bar{p}u)+\bar{q}(py+\bar{p}v)=p(qx+\bar{q}y)+\bar{p}(qu+\bar{q}v)=py+\bar{p}v.

(3)

Direct consequence of reflexivity and convexity of adherence.

(4)

Assume

and

is
\mathbb{X}
-cancellable. Let

pz+\bar{p}y=q(pz+\bar{p}x)+\bar{q}(pz+\bar{p}y)=pz+\bar{p}(qx+\bar{q}y)

{defi}

Let
\mathbb{X}
be a convex algebra, and

.

•

\forall x\in P.\,\,\forall y\in X.\,\,\forall p\in(0,1).\,\,px+\bar{p}y\in P

•

•

•

Lemma 4
.

Let
\mathbb{X}
be a convex algebra, and

is an extremal set.

Proof 2.3

Assume

is an ideal. Let

If

or

, then since

Hence

For the converse, assume

If

, then

Hence,

.

3.
The Problem and Some Example Solutions

Let
\mathbb{X}
be a convex algebra.
Can one extend it for one element to a convex algebra
\mathbb{X}_{*}
with carrier
X\cup\{*\}
where
*\notin X
?
If yes, what are all possible extensions?

We will show that an arbitrary convex algebra
\mathbb{X}
can be extended in many ways,
and describe all possible ways of extending
\mathbb{X}=\mathbb{D}_{S}
and
\mathbb{X}=\textsc{css}\mathbb{D}_{S}
.

First, we provide four examples of extensions, two of which are instances of the semilattice construction
from Example
2
.

{exa}

Let
\mathbb{X}
be a convex algebra and

. We denote the operations of
\mathbb{X}
as before by

.
In each of the examples we construct a convex algebra
\mathbb{X}_{*}
with operations denoted by

,

.

(1)

Black-hole behaviour, cf.
[

In this example,

.
To be precise, consider the semilattice

with

. Let

Extensions of Convex Algebras - The Prime Ideal

The following two notions provide a crucial characteristic of an extension

{defi}

Let

.
By Lemma

The next lemma gives a way to conclude that

imitates an element.

Lemma 6

Let

In this section we formulate and prove Theorem

We start with some algebraic preliminaries. First, we recall the notion of linear boundedness, see e.g.

[
3

.

{defi}

A convex algebra
\mathbb{X}
is
linearly bounded
, if every homomorphism of the convex algebra
(0,\infty)

into
\mathbb{X}
is constant.

Intuitively, a convex algebra is linearly bounded if it does not contain an infinite ray.
A large class of examples of linearly bounded algebras is given by topologically bounded subsets of a topological vector space.
Recall that a topological vector space is a vector space endowed with a topology such that addition and scalar
multiplication are continuous.
Our standard reference for the theory of topological vector spaces is
[
26
]
.

{defi}

Let
\mathbb{V}
be a topological vector space. A subset
D\subseteq V
is
bounded
, if
for every neighbourhood
U
of
0
there exists
r_{0}>0
such that
D\subseteq rU
,
r>r_{0}

(cf.
[
26
, p.8]
).

For example, if
\mathbb{V}
is a normed space (with a norm denoted by
\|.\|
), then a subset
D
is bounded in this sense
if and only if
\sup_{x\in D}\|x\|<\infty
.

We could not find an explicit reference for the following (intuitive) fact, and hence provide a complete proof.

Lemma 12
.

Let
\mathbb{V}
be a topological vector space. Then for every bounded convex subset

Proof 6.1

Let

, and let

By

Set

7.
Termination in Probabilistic Automata

A (simple)
probabilistic automaton
(PA, for short)
[
29
,
28
]
is a triple

M=(S,A,\to)
where
S
is the set of states,
A
the set of actions, and
\to\,\,\subseteq S\times A\times\mathcal{D}{\mkern-3.5mu}_{f}S
the transition relation. As usual, we write
a\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a}}{{\to}}\xi
for
(s,a,\xi)\in\,\to
and say that
s
makes an
a
-step to
\xi
.

A probabilistic automaton
M=(S,A,\to)
can be identified
[
1
,
32
]
with a
(\mathcal{P}\mathcal{D}{\mkern-3.5mu}_{f})^{A}
-coalgebra
(S,c_{M})
where
c_{M}\colon S\to(\mathcal{P}\mathcal{D}{\mkern-3.5mu}_{f}S)^{A}
on
\mathbf{Sets}
and
s\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a}}{{\to}}\xi
in
M
iff
\xi\in c_{M}(s)(a)
.

We say that a PA
M
is
input enabled
, if for all
s\in S
and all
a\in A
, the set
\{\xi\in\mathcal{D}{\mkern-3.5mu}_{f}S\mid s\stackrel{{\scriptstyle s}}{{\to}}\xi\}
is nonempty. Input-enabledness implies that the associated coalgebra
c_{M}
is actually a
(\mathcal{P}_{\neq\emptyset}\mathcal{D}{\mkern-3.5mu}_{f})^{A}
-coalgebra where
\mathcal{P}_{\neq\emptyset}
denotes the nonempty powerset functor.

We call this view on PA (their standard definition and the observation that they are coalgebras on
\mathbf{Sets}
) the classical view.

In the classical view, the canonical semantics for PA is bisimilarity. However, it is possible to give PA more intricate and useful
distribution
semantics. This was noticed in the last decade by many authors, and studied from a coalgebraic perspective in
[
2
]
, by viewing them as
belief-state transformers
and making the underlaying convex algebra structure explicit. To be precise, we state the following property, which is an instance of
[
2
, Lemma 25]
. By

we denote the forgetful functor from
\mathcal{E}{\kern-0.86108pt}\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{D}{\mkern-3.5mu}_{f})
to
\mathbf{Sets}
.

Lemma 20
.

There is a 1-1 correspondence between input enabled PA, i.e.,

(\mathcal{P}_{\neq\emptyset}\mathcal{D}{\mkern-3.5mu}_{f})^{A}
-coalgebras on
\mathbf{Sets}
, and
\textsc{css}^{A}
-coalgebras on

\mathcal{E}{\kern-0.86108pt}\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{D}{\mkern-3.5mu}_{f})
with carriers free algebras:

\begin{array}[]{c}{c_{M}\colon S\to(\mathcal{P}_{\neq\emptyset}\mathcal{D}{\mkern-3.5mu}_{f}S)^{A}\,\,\,\text{~in~}\,\,\,\mathbf{Sets}}\raisebox{-6.0pt}{\rule{0.0pt}{0.0pt}}\\
\hline\cr{c_{M}^{\#}\colon\mathbb{D}_{S}\to(\textsc{css}\mathbb{D}_{S})^{A}\,\,\,\text{~in~}\,\,\,\mathcal{E}{\kern-0.86108pt}\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{D}{\mkern-3.5mu}_{f})}\raisebox{13.0pt}{\rule{0.0pt}{0.0pt}}\end{array}

Given

for

being the convex algebra structure on

\{A\in X\mid A\raisebox{-1.25pt}{$\TransformHoriz$}C\}=\{A\in X\mid\overline{A}=\overline{C}\},\quad I=\CoH(\{A\in X\mid\overline{A}\neq\overline{C}\}).

Assume that
\overline{C}\in\Ext\mathcal{K}(D)
. Then
I
itself is a prime ideal, and we may choose
P=I
in
(4)
.
There are also other possible choices for
P
. For example,
P=X\setminus\{\overline{C}\}
is a prime ideal.

If
\overline{C}\not\in\Ext\mathcal{K}(D)
, we have no general result telling how large
I
will be.
In some concrete low-dimensional examples, we saw that
I\neq X
may happen and there are many
prime ideals admissible for
(4)
.

To summarize, we exhibited two families of different extensions:
1. A unique extension is associated with each extremal point
C
of
\mathcal{K}(D)
.
Points
C\in\mathcal{K}(D)\setminus\Ext\mathcal{K}(D)
are not eligible;
2. Every relatively open subset
C
with
\overline{C}\in\Ext\mathcal{K}(D)
is eligible and each such set
gives rise to several extensions. If
\overline{C}\not\in\Ext\mathcal{K}(D)
, examples indicate that
C
can be
eligible and give rise to different extensions.

Remark 22
.

Describing
\Ext\mathcal{K}(D)
is an open problem in convex geometry.

\Ext\mathcal{K}(D)
must be large in the sense that
\CoH\Ext\mathcal{K}(D)
is dense in
\mathcal{K}(D)

w.r.t. the Hausdorff metric.
This fact is shown by considering
\mathcal{K}(D)
as a compact convex subset of the space of continuous functions on
the
n
-dimensional sphere via passing to support functions (e.g.
[

, Theorems 1.7.1,1.8.11 and p.150]
),
and applying the Krein-Milman theorem (e.g.
[
26
, Theorem 3.23]
).

The only situation we know where an explicit description of
\Ext\mathcal{K}(D)
is possible is

[
13
, Theorem]
where
D
is a strictly convex subset of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with nonempty interior.

When
D
is a simplex (defined below), a bit more can be said about
\Ext\mathcal{K}(D)
.

{exa}

Let
n\geq 2
, let
S
be an
n
-element set, and consider
\mathcal{K}(\mathcal{D}{\mkern-3.5mu}_{f}S)
.
The algebra
\mathcal{D}{\mkern-3.5mu}_{f}S
is isomorphic to the standard
(n-1)
-simplex

The extremal points of

are its corner points, the canonical basis vectors

in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Hence a singleton
\{\xi\}
belongs to
\Ext\mathcal{K}(\mathcal{D}{\mkern-3.5mu}_{f}S)
if and only if

is a Dirac measure
\delta_{x}
concentrated
at one of the points
x
of
S
. The set

\mathcal{E}:=\{A\in\Ext\mathcal{K}(\mathcal{D}{\mkern-3.5mu}_{f}S)\mid A\text{ is not singleton}\}

(14)

corresponds bijectively to the homothety classes of indecomposable convex bodies in
\mathbb{R}^{n-1}

which are not singletons (see Corollary
29
, Lemma
30
in Section
9
).
Let us point out that this property is particular for the algebra
\mathcal{K}(\Delta^{n-1})
.
For example, it does not hold for
\mathcal{K}(D)
when
D
is a square in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
cf.
[
13
, Remark 2]
.

For
n=2
and

the set

is known explicitly; the case

is trivial and the case

is elaborated in

[

, Theorem 3.2.11]
.
Concretely, for

\Ext\mathcal{K}(\mathcal{D}{\mkern-3.5mu}_{f}\{1,2\})=\big\{\{\delta_{1}\},\{\delta_{2}\}\big\}\cup\big\{\CoH\{\delta_{1},\delta_{2}\}\big\}=\big\{\{\delta_{1}\},\{\delta_{2}\},\mathcal{D}{\mkern-3.5mu}_{f}\{1,2\}\big\}.

For

consists of

,
the line segments connecting one Dirac measure with any convex combination of the other two,

.

For

,

Remark 23
.

Giving description of indecomposable convex bodies in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
n\geq 3
is an open problem in convex geometry.
By Baire’s category theorem most convex bodies are indecomposable (being
a dense set). However, almost no
indecomposable bodies are explicitly known.,
cf. the discussion
[
27
, p.153]
. For polytopes, however, there are easy to check conditions for
indecomposability. For example, if all
2
-dimensional faces of a polytope are triangles, then it is indecomposable,
cf.
[
27
, Corollary 3.2.13]
.

Proofs of Geometric Arguments in Example
8
and Example
8

In this section we provide evidence for the statements made in Example
8
and Example
8
.

Example
8
relies on knowing the sets
\{A\in\textsc{css}\mathbb{D}\mid A\raisebox{-1.25pt}{$\TransformHoriz$}C\}
when
C
is either
closed or relatively open, and on the fact that an extremal point of
\mathcal{K}(D)
is automatically
extremal in the larger algebra
\textsc{css}\mathbb{D}
.
The next three results contain all the details.

Lemma 24
.

Let
D\in\mathcal{K}^{n}
.

(1)

The closure operator
\overline{\rule{0.0pt}{5.0pt}.}:\textsc{css}\mathbb{D}\to\mathcal{K}(D)
is a homomorphism of convex algebras.

(2)

Let
A,B\in\textsc{css}\mathbb{D}
. If
A\raisebox{-1.25pt}{$\TransformHoriz$}B
, then
\overline{A}=\overline{B}
.

(3)

Let
C\in\textsc{css}\mathbb{D}
be relatively open. Then

\{A\in\textsc{css}\mathbb{D}\mid A\raisebox{-1.25pt}{$\TransformHoriz$}C\}=\{A\in\textsc{css}\mathbb{D}\mid\overline{A}=\overline{C}\}.

Proof 9.1
.

(1)

Let
A,B\in\textsc{css}\mathbb{D}
,
p\in(0,1)
, and set
C=pA+\bar{p}B
. Continuity of linear operations ensures that

p\overline{A}+\bar{p}\overline{B}\subseteq\overline{C}
. However,
\overline{A}
and
\overline{B}
are compact,
so also
p\overline{A}+\bar{p}\overline{B}
is compact, hence, in particular, closed. We conclude that

\overline{C}\subseteq p\overline{A}+\bar{p}\overline{B}
.

(2)

If
A\raisebox{-1.25pt}{$\TransformHoriz$}B
, by 1., also
\overline{A}\raisebox{-1.25pt}{$\TransformHoriz$}\overline{B}
, i.e.,
p\overline{A}+\bar{p}\overline{B}=\overline{B}
for
all
p\in(0,1)
. This implies
\overline{A}=\lim_{p\to 1}(p\overline{A}+\bar{p}\overline{B})=\overline{B}
, where
the limit is understood w.r.t. the Hausdorff metric. For the first equality recall that the operations are
continuous, cf. Remark
21
.1.

(3)

The inclusion “
\subseteq
” holds by 2. For the reverse inclusion, let
A\in\textsc{css}\mathbb{D}
with

\overline{A}=\overline{C}
be given.
Then
C=\relint(\overline{C})=\relint(\overline{A})=\relint A\subseteq A
, cf.
[
24
, Theorem 6.3]
.
Thus
pA+\bar{p}C\supseteq C
. The inclusion
pA+\bar{p}C\subseteq C
holds by
[
24
, Theorem 6.1]
.

Lemma 25
.

Let
C\in\mathcal{K}^{n}
.

(1)

If
A,B\in\textsc{css}\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
p\in(0,1)
, with
A,B\subseteq C
and
pA+\bar{p}B=C
, then
A=B=C
.

(2)

{\displaystyle\{A\in\textsc{css}\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid A\raisebox{-1.25pt}{$\TransformHoriz$}C\}=\{C\}}
.

Proof 9.2
.

(1)

Let
x\in\Ext C
, and choose
a\in A
,
b\in B
, with
x=pa+\bar{p}b
. Since
a,b\in C
, it
follows that
a=b=x
. We see that
\Ext C\subseteq A\cap B
. Using Minkowski’s theorem,
cf.
[
27
, Corollary 1.4.5]
, yields

C=\CoH(\Ext C)\subseteq A\cap B
, from which
A=B=C
follows.

(2)

Let
a\in A
and choose
c\in C
. Then, for each
p\in(0,1)
, the element
pa+\bar{p}c
belongs to
C
.
Since
C
is closed,

a=\lim_{p\to 1}(pa+\bar{p}c)\in C.

Thus
A\subseteq C
, and 1. shows that
A=C
.

Lemma 26
.

Let
D\in\mathcal{K}^{n}
and
C\in\mathcal{K}(D)
.
Then
C
is an extremal point of
\mathcal{K}(D)
if and only if it is an extremal point of
\textsc{css}\mathbb{D}
.

Proof 9.3
.

Since
\mathcal{K}(D)
is a subalgebra of
\textsc{css}\mathbb{D}
, we have
\Ext(\textsc{css}\mathbb{D})\cap\mathcal{K}(D)\subseteq\Ext\mathcal{K}(D)
.
Assume
C\in\Ext\mathcal{K}(D)
and that
C=pA+\bar{p}B
with some
A,B\in\textsc{css}\mathbb{D}
and
p\in(0,1)
.
Then
p\overline{A}+\bar{p}\overline{B}=\overline{C}=C
, and we obtain
\overline{A}=\overline{B}=C
. In particular,

A,B\subseteq C
, and Lemma
25
.1 shows
A=B=C
.

We turn to the proof of the statement made in Example
8
, that the set
\mathcal{E}
from (
14
)
corresponds to (classes of) indecomposable bodies.

First, let us introduce some notation.
Let
\pi_{i}\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
denote the
i
-th projection, and set
\sigma=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\pi_{i}
.
Denote by
e_{i}
the
i
-th canonical basis vector of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Then the standard
n
-simplex can be written as

\Delta^{n}=\CoH\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}\}=\sigma^{-1}(\{1\})\cap\bigcap_{i=1}^{n}\pi_{i}^{-1}([0,1]).

A central role is played by the subset of
\mathcal{K}(\Delta^{n})
of all bodies which touch each face of
\Delta^{n}
.

{defi}

Set
\mathcal{L}(\Delta^{n})=\{A\in\mathcal{K}(\Delta^{n})\mid\min\pi_{i}(A)=0,i=1,\ldots,n\}
.

Lemma 27
.

The set
\mathcal{L}(\Delta^{n})
contains no singletons.
It is a convex and extreme subset of
\mathcal{K}(\Delta^{n})
.

Proof 9.4
.

Assume
\{x\}\in\mathcal{L}(\Delta^{n})
. Since
\min\pi_{i}(\{x\})=\pi_{i}(x)
, it follows that
x=0
which contradicts

\sigma(x)=\max\sigma(\{x\})=1
.

It holds that
\min\pi_{i}(pA+\bar{p}B)=p\min\pi_{i}(A)+\bar{p}\min\pi_{i}(B)
. The fact that
\mathcal{L}(\Delta^{n})
is
convex follows immediately.
To show that
\mathcal{L}(\Delta^{n})
is extreme, let
A\in\mathcal{K}(\Delta^{n})\setminus\mathcal{L}(\Delta^{n})
.
Choose
j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
with
\min\pi_{j}(A)>0
. Then, for each
p\in(0,1)
and
B\in\mathcal{K}(\Delta^{n})
we have

\min\pi_{i}(pA+\bar{p}B)\geq p\min\pi_{i}(A)>0
, and conclude that
pA+\bar{p}B\not\in\mathcal{L}(\Delta^{n})
.

Next we prove that each extremal point of
\mathcal{K}(\Delta^{n})
which is not a singleton
must touch each face of
\Delta^{n}
.
This property fits our intuition, but surprisingly it only holds for the algebra
\mathcal{K}(\Delta^{n})
.
For example, it does not hold in
\mathcal{K}(D)
for
D
a square in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, cf.
[
13
, Remark 2]
.

Lemma 28
.

We have
\big\{A\in\Ext\mathcal{K}(\Delta^{n})\mid A\text{ is not singleton}\big\}\subseteq\mathcal{L}(\Delta^{n})
.

Proof 9.5
.

Let
A\in\mathcal{K}(\Delta^{n})\setminus\mathcal{L}(\Delta^{n})
have more than one point.
Our aim is to show that
A
can be written as a convex combination of two elements of
\mathcal{K}(\Delta^{n})
not both
equal to
A
.
Choose
j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
with
\epsilon=\min\pi_{j}(A)>0
. Since
A
is not a singleton, we have
\epsilon<1
.
Consider the convex map
f\colon x\mapsto(1-\epsilon)x+\epsilon e_{j}
and its inverse

f^{-1}\colon x\mapsto\frac{1}{1-\epsilon}x-\frac{\epsilon}{1-\epsilon}e_{j}
.
Obviously,
f(\Delta^{n})\subseteq\Delta^{n}
. We prove that
f^{-1}(A)\subseteq\Delta^{n}
.
Let
x\in A
, then

\displaystyle\sigma(f^{-1}(x))=\frac{1}{1-\epsilon}-\frac{\epsilon}{1-\epsilon}=1,

\displaystyle\pi_{i}(f^{-1}(x))=\frac{1}{1-\epsilon}\pi_{i}(x)\geq 0,\ i\neq j,\quad\pi_{j}(f^{-1}(x))=\frac{1}{1-\epsilon}\big(\pi_{i}(x)-\epsilon\big)\geq 0.

Thus
f^{-1}(x)\in\Delta^{n}
.

Set
p=\frac{1}{2-\epsilon}
. Then
p\in(0,1)
,
\bar{p}=\frac{1-\epsilon}{2-\epsilon}
, and

pf(x)+\bar{p}f^{-1}(y)=\Big(\frac{1-\epsilon}{2-\epsilon}x+\frac{\epsilon}{2-\epsilon}e_{j}\Big)+\Big(\frac{1}{2-\epsilon}x-\frac{\epsilon}{2-\epsilon}e_{j}\Big)=px+\bar{p}y.

It follows that
pf(A)+\bar{p}f^{-1}(A)=A
. Since
\min\pi_{j}(f^{-1}(A))=0
, we have
f^{-1}(A)\neq A
.

Corollary 29
.

We have
\Ext\mathcal{K}(\Delta^{n})=\{\{e_{1}\},\ldots,\{e_{n}\}\}\,\cup\,\Ext\mathcal{L}(\Delta^{n})
. The union is disjoint.

Proof 9.6
.

We already determined the extremal points of
\mathcal{K}(\Delta^{n})
which are singletons.
Clearly,
\mathcal{L}(\Delta^{n})\cap\Ext\mathcal{K}(\Delta^{n})\subseteq\Ext\mathcal{L}(\Delta^{n})
.
Since
\mathcal{L}(\Delta^{n})
is an extremal set,
\Ext\mathcal{L}(\Delta^{n})\subseteq\Ext\mathcal{K}(\Delta^{n})
.
If
A\in\Ext\mathcal{K}(\Delta^{n})
is not a singleton, the above lemma says
A\in\mathcal{L}(\Delta^{n})
.

To make the connection with indecomposable bodies, we pass to an isomorphic situation.
Slightly overloading notation, let again
\pi_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\to\mathbb{R}

be the
i
-th projection, and
\sigma
and
e_{i}
be defined as above (with
n-1
instead of
n
).
Set

D^{n-1}=\big\{(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\mid t_{i}\geq 0,\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}t_{i}\leq 1\big\}.

Then
D^{n-1}=\CoH\{0,e_{1},\ldots,e_{n-1}\}=\sigma^{-1}([0,1])\cap\bigcap_{i=1}^{n-1}\pi_{i}^{-1}([0,1])
. The standard simplex

\Delta^{n}
is isomorphic to
D^{n-1}
via the convex map
\phi
taking the basis vector
e_{i}
of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
to the
corresponding basis vector in
\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
if
i<n
and mapping
e_{n}
to
0
.
This map lifts in the natural (pointwise) way to the isomorphism
\Phi\colon A\mapsto\phi(A)
of the convex algebra

\mathcal{K}(\Delta^{n})
onto
\mathcal{K}(D^{n-1})
.
The image of
\mathcal{L}(\Delta^{n})
under this isomorphism is

\Phi\big(\mathcal{L}(\Delta^{n})\big)=\big\{A\in\mathcal{K}(D^{n-1})\mid\max\sigma(A)=1,\ \min\pi_{i}(A)=0,i=1,\ldots,n-1\big\},

which we shall denote as
\mathcal{L}(D^{n-1})
.
Being an isomorphism,
\Phi
maps extremal points to extremal points, and we obtain

\Phi\big(\Ext\mathcal{L}(\Delta^{n})\big)=\Ext\mathcal{L}(D^{n-1}).

Further note that also
\mathcal{L}(D^{n-1})
contains no singletons.

Lemma 30
.

(1)

The set
\mathcal{L}(D^{n-1})
is a complete system of representatives modulo homothety
of the set of convex bodies in
\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
having more than one point.

(2)

Let
A\in\mathcal{L}(D^{n-1})
. Then
A\in\Ext\mathcal{L}(D^{n-1})
if and only if

A
is indecomposable in
\mathcal{K}^{n-1}
.

Proof 9.7
.

(1)

Let
A\in\mathcal{K}^{n-1}
with more than one point, and set
t_{i}=\min\pi_{i}(A)
,
x=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}t_{i}e_{i}
.
Since
A
is not a singleton,
\max\sigma(A)>\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}t_{i}
. The body

\Psi(A)=\big(\max\sigma(A)-\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}t_{i}\big)^{-1}\big(A-x\big)

is homothetic to
A
and belongs to
\mathcal{L}(D^{n-1})
.

Now assume that
s>0
,
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
, and that both
A
and
sA+x
belong to
\mathcal{L}(D^{n-1})
.
Then

0=\min\pi_{i}(sA+x)=s[\min\pi_{i}(A)]+\pi_{i}(x)=\pi_{i}(x),

whence
x=0
. Now
1=\max\sigma(sA)=s\max\sigma(A)=s
.

(2)

Assume
A\in\mathcal{L}(D^{n-1})\setminus\Ext\mathcal{L}(D^{n-1})
, then
A
has a representation

A=pB+\bar{p}C
with some
p\in(0,1)
and
B,C\in\mathcal{L}(D^{n-1})
where not both of
B,C
equal
A
.
Then
pB
and
\bar{p}C
are not both homothetic to
A
by 1.,
and we conclude that
A
is decomposable in
\mathcal{K}^{n-1}
.

To show the converse, we first establish the following:
If
B,C\in\mathcal{K}^{n-1}
are not singletons, then there exists
p\in(0,1)
with

\Psi(B+C)=p\Psi(B)+\bar{p}\Psi(C).

To see this, denote

\displaystyle t_{i}^{B}=\min\pi_{i}(B),s^{B}=\max\sigma(B)-\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}t_{i}^{B},\quad t_{i}^{C}=\min\pi_{i}(C),s^{C}=\max\sigma(C)-\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}t_{i}^{C},

\displaystyle t_{i}=\min\pi_{i}(B+C),s=\max\sigma(B+C)-\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}t_{i}.

Then
t_{i}=t_{i}^{B}+t_{i}^{C}
and
s=s^{B}+s^{C}
, which gives

\Psi(B+C)=\frac{1}{s^{B}+s^{C}}\Big[(B+C)+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}(t_{i}^{B}+t_{i}^{C})\Big]=\frac{s^{B}}{s^{B}+s^{C}}\Psi(B)+\frac{s^{C}}{s^{B}+s^{C}}\Psi(C).

Since
B
and
C
are not singletons,
s^{B}
and
s^{C}
are both nonzero. Thus
p=\frac{s^{B}}{s^{B}+s^{C}}\in(0,1)
.

Now let
A\in\mathcal{L}(D^{n-1})
, and assume that
A=B+C
with
B,C\in\mathcal{K}^{n-1}
not both
homothetic to
A
. Then

A=\Psi(A)=p\Psi(B)+\bar{p}\Psi(C),

and not both of
\Psi(B)
and
\Psi(C)
are equal to
A
by 1.

Putting together Corollary
29
and Lemma
30
, we see that indeed the set
\mathcal{E}
from (
14
)
corresponds bijectively to the homothety classes of indecomposable convex bodies in
\mathbb{R}^{n-1}

which are not singletons.

10.
Conclusions

We have studied the possibility of extending a convex algebra by a single
element. We have proven that many different extensions are possible of which
only one gives rise to a functor on
\mathcal{E}{\kern-0.86108pt}\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{D}{\mkern-3.5mu}_{f})
. We have described all
extensions of
\mathbb{D}_{S}
, the free convex algebra of probability distributions
over a set
S
, and of
\textsc{css}\mathbb{D}
, the convex algebra of convex subsets of a particular kind of
convex subset of a vector space. As a consequence of the latter result, we have
described all extensions of
\textsc{css}\mathbb{D}_{S}
used for modelling probabilistic
automata.

It would be interesting to investigate whether the methods developed here could be useful in the study of Eilenberg-Moore algebras
of the Giry monad on measurable spaces, or on subcategories of measurable spaces like Polish or analytic spaces.
