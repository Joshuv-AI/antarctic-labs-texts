On finding optimal collective variables for complex systems by minimizing the deviation between effective and full dynamics
Wei Zhang, Christof Schütte

Abstract
This paper is concerned with collective variables, or reaction coordinates, that map a discrete-in-time Markov process X n X_{n} in ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} to a (much) smaller dimension k ≪ d k\ll d . We define the effective dynamics under a given collective variable map ξ \xi as the best Markovian representation of X n X_{n} under ξ \xi . The novelty of the paper is that it gives strict criteria for selecting optimal collective variables via the properties of the effective dynamics. In particular, we show that the transition density of the effective dynamics of the optimal collective variable solves a relative entropy minimization problem from certain family of densities to the transition density of X n X_{n} . We also show that many transfer operator-based data-driven numerical approaches essentially learn quantities of the effective dynamics. Furthermore, we obtain various error estimates for the effective dynamics in approximating dominant timescales / eigenvalues and transition rates of the original process X n X_{n} and how optimal collective variables minimize these errors. Our results contribute to the development of theoretical tools for the understanding of complex dynamical systems, e.g. molecular kinetics, on large timescales. These results shed light on the relations among existing data-driven numerical approaches for identifying good collective variables, and they also motivate the development of new methods.



Keywords—
molecular dynamics, transfer operator, collective variable, reaction coordinate, effective dynamics

Introduction

In many realistic applications, e.g. molecular dynamics (MD), materials
science, and climate simulation, the systems of interest are often very high-dimensional
and exhibit extraordinarily complex phenomena on vastly different time scales.
For instance, it is common that molecular systems, e.g. proteins, undergo noise-induced transitions among several
long-lived (metastable) conformations, and these essential transitions occur on timescales that are much larger compared to the timescale of noisy fluctuations experienced by the systems.
Understanding the dynamics of such systems is important for
scientific discoveries, e.g. in drug design. However, the characteristics of these systems, e.g. their
high dimensionality and the existence of multiple time/spatial scales, bring
considerable challenges, as many traditional experimental/computational
approaches become either inefficient or completely inapplicable. A great amount of research efforts have thus been devoted to developing theoretical tools as well as advanced numerical approaches for tackling these challenges.

A commonly adopted strategy is to utilize the fact that the dynamics of
high-dimensional systems, e.g. molecular systems in MD and materials science,
can often be well characterized using only a few observables, often called
collective variables (CVs) or reaction coordinates, of the systems. In
particular, many enhanced sampling methods
(see
[
22
]
for a review) utilize the
knowledge about the system’s CVs to perform accelerated sampling and efficient
free energy calculations
[
37
]
, and various methods for
model reduction rely on this knowledge to construct simpler
(lower-dimensional) surrogate
models
[
44
,
33
,
3
]
.
While these methods have proven to be greatly helpful in understanding
dynamical behaviors of complex systems, the accuracy of estimated quantities
and surrogate models offered by these methods crucially depend on the choices
of CVs. Given a set of CVs, analyzing the accuracy of surrogate models in
approximating the true dynamics is an important research topic that has
attracted considerable attentions in the past years;
see
[
33
,
34
,
66
,
35
,
14
,
39
]

for studies on effective dynamics constructed using conditional expectation.
These work extend the classical averaging technique for multiscale processes to
the setting where the slow variable is nonlinear
(see
[
47
, Chapter 10]
for averaging techniques and

[
39
,
35
]
for discussions on the connection
between averaging and effective dynamics by conditional expectation).
Furthermore, efficient algorithms which allow for automatic identification of “good” CVs are currently under rapid development; see for instance
[
69
,
7
,
62
]
for recent progresses in finding CVs using machine learning and deep learning techniques.

As generating data for large and complex systems becomes much easier thanks to the significant advances in the developments of both computer hardware and efficient numerical algorithms in the past decades, many data-driven numerical methods have been proposed, which can be applied to understanding dynamical behaviors of complex systems by analyzing their trajectory data. A large class of these data-driven methods are based on the theory of transfer operators
[
53
,
54
]
or Koopman operators
[
11
]
, in which the dynamics of a underlying system is analyzed using the eigenvalues and the corresponding eigenfunctions of the transfer (or Koopman) operator associated to the system.
Notable examples of methods in this class include standard Markov state models (MSMs)
[
50
,
24
]
, the variational approach to conformational dynamics
[

,

]
, time lagged independent component analysis (tICA)
[

]
, variational approach for Markov processes (VAMP)
[

, extended dynamic mode decompositions

,

, and many others. Recent development in this direction includes kernel-tICA

This paper is concerned with collective variables of discrete-in-time Markov

in
\mathbb{R}^{d}

may also
be understood as resulting from a continuous-time Markov process by subsampling with constant lag-time. Collective variables are described by CV maps

16

Main results

The novelty of the paper is that it gives strict criteria for selecting
optimal
collective variables via the properties of the effective dynamics. Concretely, we give three optimality statements: First, we show that the effective dynamics introduced in this paper (Definition
43
) is the optimal surrogate model in the sense that the Kullback-Leibler (KL) “distance” to the transition density of

is minimized (Proposition

). Next, we obtain quantitative error estimates for the effective dynamics in approximating the eigenvalues and transition rates of the original process. Based on these
results, optimal CVs for minimizing these approximation errors are characterized, see Theorems

and
4.3

Algorithmic implications

We show that our theoretical results also impact transfer operator-based data-driven numerical approaches.
For instance, in order to reduce computational complexity or pertain certain properties, e.g. rotation and translation invariance in molecular dynamics,
many data-driven numerical algorithms such as the deep learning approach VAMPnets for learning eigenfunctions
[

,
12
]
, often employ features, e.g., internal variables, of the underlying systems (instead of working directly with coordinate data) for function representation.
We show that in the case where such system’s features are employed, the numerical methods implicitly learn the quantities (e.g. timescales, transition rates) of the effective dynamics, see Remark
3.2
for formal discussions.
Moreover, the result on the relative entropy minimization property of the effective dynamics of the optimal CV uncovers a close connection to the transition manifold framework
[

, especially to its variational formulation
[

Transfer operator approach

In this section, we present the transfer operator approach for Markov processes.
We introduce necessary quantities in Section
2.1
,
then we study the spectrum of the transfer operator in Section
2.2
, and finally we present an extension of TPT in Section
2.3
. This section provides theoretical basis for the study of effective dynamics in Sections
3
–
4
.

2.1
Definitions and basic properties

Consider a stationary, discrete-in-time Markov process
X_{n}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}

with a positive transition density function
p(x,y)
for
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, i.e.
\mathbb{P}(X_{n+1}\in D|X_{n}=x)=\int_{D}p(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}{y}
for any measurable subset
D\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
and any integer
n\geq 0
. We assume that
X_{n}
is
ergodic with respect to a unique invariant distribution
\mu(\mathrm{d}{x})=\pi(x)\mathrm{d}{x}
, where the probability density
\pi
is positive
(see
[
31
, Chapter 1]
and
[
2
, Section 2]
for ergodicity of Markov processes).
The invariance of
\mu
means

\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}p(x,y)\mu(\mathrm{d}{x})\right)\mathrm{d}{y}=\mu(\mathrm{d}{y}),~\mbox{or equivalently,}~\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}p(x,y)\mu(\mathrm{d}{x})=\pi(y),\quad y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,.

(1)

A key object in analyzing the dynamical properties of the process
X_{n}
is the transfer operator
\mathcal{T}
, which is defined by its action on functions
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, i.e.

(\mathcal{T}f)(x)=\mathbb{E}(f(X_{1})|X_{0}=x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}p(x,y)f(y)\,\mathrm{d}{y}\,,\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,,

(2)

where
\mathbb{E}(\cdot|X_{0}=x)
denotes the expectation conditioned on the
initial state
X_{0}=x
(see Remark
2.1
below for discussions on different operators studied in the literature as well as their connections).
It is natural to study
\mathcal{T}
in the Hilbert space

\mathcal{H}=L^{2}_{\mu}(\mathbb{R}^{d})=\left\{f\,\middle|\,f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R},\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f^{2}(x)\mu(\mathrm{d}{x})<+\infty\right\}

endowed
with the weighted inner product (and the norm denoted by
\|\cdot\|_{\mu}
)

\langle f,h\rangle_{\mu}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x)h(x)\mu(\mathrm{d}{x})\,,\quad\forall f,h\in\mathcal{H}\,.

(3)

With the above preparation we study the reversibility of
X_{n}
.
Denote by
\mathcal{T}^{*}
the adjoint of
\mathcal{T}
in
\mathcal{H}
, such
that
\langle\mathcal{T}f,h\rangle_{\mu}=\langle f,\mathcal{T}^{*}h\rangle_{\mu}
, for all
f,h\in\mathcal{H}
. It is straightforward to verify that

(\mathcal{T}^{*}f)(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}p^{*}(x,y)f(y)\,\mathrm{d}{y}\,,\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

(4)

for test functions
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, where
p^{*}
is defined by

p^{*}(x,y)=\frac{p(y,x)\pi(y)}{\pi(x)}\,,\quad x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,.

(5)

Moreover, from (
1
) and (
5
) we can easily verify that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}p^{*}(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}{y}=1
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}p^{*}(x,y)\,\mu(\mathrm{d}{x})=\pi(y)
for all
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Therefore,
p^{*}
is a transition density function and it defines another Markov process (i.e. the adjoint of
X_{n}
) which has the same invariant distribution
\mu
.

Note that
\mathcal{T}
can be decomposed into the reversible and non-reversible parts, i.e.

\mathcal{T}=\mathcal{T}^{rev}+\mathcal{T}^{non}

(6)

where

\mathcal{T}^{rev}=\frac{1}{2}(\mathcal{T}+\mathcal{T}^{*}),\quad\mathcal{T}^{non}=\frac{1}{2}(\mathcal{T}-\mathcal{T}^{*}),

(7)

or, more explicitly,

\displaystyle(\mathcal{T}^{rev}f)(x)=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{1}{2}\big(p(x,y)+p^{*}(x,y)\big)f(y)\,\mathrm{d}{y}\,,

(8)

\displaystyle(\mathcal{T}^{non}f)(x)=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{1}{2}\big(p(x,y)-p^{*}(x,y)\big)f(y)\,\mathrm{d}{y}

for test functions
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. From
(
4
), (
5
), and the simple decomposition in
(
6
)–(
8
), we obtain the following equivalent conditions.

\displaystyle\begin{split}&~p^{*}\equiv p\hskip 56.9055pt\mbox{(detailed balance)}\\
\Longleftrightarrow&~\mathcal{T}=\mathcal{T}^{*}=\mathcal{T}^{rev}\hskip 17.07182pt\mbox{(self-adjointness)}\\
\Longleftrightarrow&~\mathcal{T}^{non}=0\,.\end{split}

(9)

We will refer to (
9
) as reversibility conditions and say that
X_{n}
is reversible if any of the equivalent conditions in (
9
) is satisfied.

Let us define the Dirichlet form
[
19
, Chapter 1]

\mathcal{E}(f,h):=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(f(y)-f(x))(h(y)-h(x))p(x,y)\mathrm{d}{y}\,\right)\mu(\mathrm{d}{x})\,,

(10)

for two test functions
f,h:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, and the Dirichlet energy

\mathcal{E}(f):=\mathcal{E}(f,f)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(f(y)-f(x))^{2}p(x,y)\mathrm{d}{y}\,\right)\mu(\mathrm{d}{x})\,.

(11)

The following lemma summarizes basic properties of these quantities.

Lemma 1
.

For any
f,h\in\mathcal{H}
, we have

\mathcal{E}(f,h)=\langle(\mathcal{I}-\mathcal{T}^{rev})f,h\rangle_{\mu}\,,

(12)

where
\mathcal{I}
and
\mathcal{T}^{rev}
denote the identity operator and the reversible part of
\mathcal{T}
in (
7
), respectively. Let
X_{0},X_{1},X_{2},\dots
be an infinitely long trajectory of the process
X_{n}
. Then, we have almost surely

\mathcal{E}(f)=\lim_{N\rightarrow+\infty}\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}|f(X_{n+1})-f(X_{n})|^{2}\,.

(13)

Proof.

Concerning (
12
), from (
10
) we can derive

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(f,h)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Big(f(y)h(y)+f(x)h(x)-f(y)h(x)-f(x)h(y)\Big)\,p(x,y)\mathrm{d}{y}\right)\mu(\mathrm{d}{x})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x)h(x)\,\mu(\mathrm{d}{x})-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Big(f(y)h(x)+f(x)h(y)\Big)p(x,y)\mathrm{d}{y}\right)\mu(\mathrm{d}{x})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x)h(x)\,\mu(\mathrm{d}{x})-\frac{1}{2}\Big(\langle\mathcal{T}f,h\rangle_{\mu}+\langle f,\mathcal{T}h\rangle_{\mu}\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle(\mathcal{I}-\mathcal{T}^{rev})f,h\rangle_{\mu}

where we used (
1
),(
2
) and (
7
) to obtain the second, the third and the last equality, respectively.
Since we assume that the process is ergodic, the identity (
13
)
follows directly from (
11
) and Birkhoff’s ergodic theorem (see
[
31
, Theorem 4.4 in Chapter 1]
).
∎

In the following remark we discuss the relations among different operators that were studied in the literature.

Remark 2.1

(Alternative operators and terminologies)
.

Different operators have been used under different names in the literature for the study of stochastic dynamical systems. The transfer
operator
\mathcal{T}
in (
2
) is also called backward transfer operator
[
53
]
or Koopman
operator
[
61
,
54
,
29
]
, whereas
its adjoint
\mathcal{T}^{*}
in (
4
) is called forward
transfer operator in
[
53
]
. In the reversible case,
we have
\mathcal{T}=\mathcal{T}^{*}
and hence all these operators coincide with
each other. In this work, we focus on the operator
\mathcal{T}
and stick to the name transfer operator. The analysis can be straightforwardly applied to Koopman operator and
forward transfer operator thanks to the relations among these operators.

We conclude this section with a discussion on the concrete examples that are most interesting to this work.

Example 2.1
.

Transfer operator approach is often used as a theoretical tool in data-driven numerical methods in order to analyze time-series

X_{0}=x(0),X_{1}=x(\tau),X_{2}=x(2\tau),\dots,X_{N-1}=x((N-1)\tau)
of a system that is governed by a underlying SDE

\mathrm{d}{x}(t)=b(x(t))\,\mathrm{d}{t}+\sigma(x(t))\,\mathrm{d}{\eta}(t)\,,\quad t\geq 0\,,

(14)

with some drift and diffusion coefficients
b:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\sigma:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
,
respectively, where
\tau>0
is called the lag-time
[
50
]
,

x(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
denotes the system’s state at time
t
, and
\eta(t)
is a
d
-dimensional Brownian motion.
In this case, the transition density
p(x,y)
of
X_{n}
is related to the
transition density
p^{\mathrm{SDE}}(t,y\,|x)
of
x(t)
in (
14
)
at time
t=\tau
, starting from
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
at time
t=0
. The latter
solves the Fokker-Planck equation
[
48
,
52
]

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\frac{\partial p^{\mathrm{SDE}}}{\partial t}=\mathcal{L}^{\top}p^{\mathrm{SDE}},\quad t>0\,,\\
&p^{\mathrm{SDE}}(0,y\,|x)=\delta(x-y),\quad t=0\end{split}

(15)

for
t\in(0,+\infty)
and
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where the operator
\mathcal{L}^{\top}
, given by
\mathcal{L}^{\top}f=-\mathrm{div}(bf)+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}\frac{\partial^{2}\big((\sigma\sigma^{\top})_{ij}f\big)}{\partial x_{i}x_{j}}
for a test function
f
,
is the adjoint (with respect to the Lebesgue measure) of the generator of (
14
), and
\delta(\cdot)
denotes the Dirac delta function.
In many cases, the solution to (
15
) is smooth for
t>0
(see
[
48
, Chapter 4]
and
[
8
]
for a comprehensive study).
In the following, we consider the transition density
p(x,y)
in three concrete examples.

1.

Brownian dynamics. Assume that SDE (
14
) has the form

\mathrm{d}{x}(t)=-\nabla V(x(t))\,\mathrm{d}{t}+\sqrt{2\beta^{-1}}\mathrm{d}{\eta}(t)\,,\quad t\geq 0

(16)

where
V:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a smooth potential function and
\beta>0
is a constant related to the system’s temperature.
The transition density of
X_{n}
is then
p(x,y)=p^{\mathrm{BD}}(\tau,y\,|x)
,
where
p^{\mathrm{BD}}(t,y\,|x)
is the solution to (
15
)
with the generator
\mathcal{L}f=-\nabla V\cdot\nabla f+\frac{1}{\beta}\Delta f
. Under certain conditions on
V
,
X_{n}
is
ergodic with the unique invariant distribution
\mu
whose density is
given by
\pi(x)=\frac{1}{Z}\mathrm{e}^{-\beta V(x)}
, where
Z=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathrm{e}^{-\beta V(x)}\,\mathrm{d}{x}
is a normalizing constant.
In particular,
X_{n}
is reversible and the corresponding transfer
operator defined in (
2
) is given by

\mathcal{T}f=\mathrm{e}^{\tau\mathcal{L}}f
(the solution to the
backward Kolmogorov equation associated to
\mathcal{L}

[
46
, Chapter 8]
).

2.

Langevin dynamics. Assume that the evolution of
x(s)
is governed by the (underdamped) Langevin dynamics
[
37
, Section 2.2.3]

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathrm{d}{x}(t)=&v(t)\mathrm{d}{t}\\
\mathrm{d}{v}(t)=&-\nabla V(x(t))\mathrm{d}{t}-\gamma\,v(t)\mathrm{d}{t}+\sqrt{2\gamma\beta^{-1}}\mathrm{d}{\eta}(t)\end{split}

(17)

where
v(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
denotes the system’s velocity and
\gamma>0
is
a friction constant. In this case, if we make the assumption that the (position) data
X_{0},X_{1},X_{2},\dots
comes from a Markov process and estimate its transition density using a density estimation method, then the ideal transition density (obtained as data size goes to infinite) is given by

p(x,y)=(\frac{\beta}{2\pi})^{\frac{d}{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}p^{\mathrm{Lan}}(\tau,y,v^{\prime}|x,v)\,\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\beta|v|^{2}}{2}}\,\mathrm{d}{v}\,\mathrm{d}{v^{\prime}}\,,

(18)

where
p^{\mathrm{Lan}}(\tau,y,v^{\prime}|x,v)
denotes the transition density of (
17
).
We refer to Appendix
A
for detailed derivations.
In particular, we show that the Markov process described by
p(x,y)
in (
18
) is reversible, although the original Langevin dynamics is non-reversible.

3.

Numerical scheme. Assume that the time-series data comes from sampling
the SDE (
16
) using a discretization scheme

X_{n+1}=X_{n}-\nabla V(X_{n})\,\Delta t+\sqrt{2\beta^{-1}\Delta t}\,W_{n},\quad n=0,1,\dots,

(19)

where
(W_{n})_{n\geq 0}
are independent standard Gaussian variables in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\Delta t
is the step-size (here, for simplicity we assume Euler-Maruyama scheme
[
26
, Chapter 9]
and
\tau=\Delta t
). In this case, the transition density of

X_{n}
is given by
p(x,y)=\big(\frac{\beta}{4\pi\Delta t}\big)^{\frac{d}{2}}\exp(-\frac{\beta|y-x+\nabla V(x)\Delta t|^{2}}{4\Delta t})
, for
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
The process
X_{n}
is in general not reversible, but in practice this
irreversibility due to numerical discretization is often ignored whenever
\Delta t
is small.
Alternatively, reversibility can be retained by adding a Matropolis-Hasting acceptance/rejection step
[
37
, Chapter 2]
.

2.2
Spectrum and timescales

In this section, we study the spectrum of
\mathcal{T}
as well as its implications on the speed of convergence of the dynamics
X_{n}
to equilibrium.

We assume that
X_{n}
is reversible. By (
9
),
\mathcal{T}
is
self-adjoint with respect to the inner product (
3
), and therefore its spectrum
\sigma(\mathcal{T})
is real.
To simplify the discussion, we further confine ourself to the case where
each nonzero element of
\sigma(\mathcal{T})
is an eigenvalue
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, for which the (integral) equation

\mathcal{T}\varphi=\lambda\varphi\,,

(20)

has a nonzero solution (eigenfunction). In the following proposition, we make our assumptions precise and give a characterization of the spectrum
\sigma(\mathcal{T})
.

Proposition 1
.

Assume that the transition density
p(x,y)
is positive for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the operator
\mathcal{T}
is self-adjoint with respect to
(
3
), and the invariant density
\pi
is positive. Also assume that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}p(x,y)p(y,x)\,\mathrm{d}{x}\,\mathrm{d}{y}<+\infty\,.

(21)

Then, the following claims hold.

1.

\mathcal{T}
is a compact operator on
\mathcal{H}
. Any nonzero value in
\sigma(\mathcal{T})
is an eigenvalue of (
20
).

2.

Any eigenvalue
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
of (
20
) satisfies that
-1<\lambda\leq 1
. Moreover, the only eigenfunction corresponding to
\lambda=1
is the constant function.

The proof of Proposition
1
is presented in Appendix
B
.
The next proposition gives conditions which guarantee that all eigenvalues are nonnegative.

Proposition 2
.

Under the assumptions in Proposition
1
, the following conditions are equivalent.

(a)

All eigenvalues of
\mathcal{T}
are nonnegative.

(b)

The Markov chain
X_{n}
satisfies
\mathbb{E}_{X_{0}\sim\mu}(f(X_{0})f(X_{1}))\geq 0
, for any
f\in\mathcal{H}
.

(c)

There is a unique self-adjoint linear operator
\mathcal{A}
on
\mathcal{H}
such that
\mathcal{A}\geq 0
and
\mathcal{T}=\mathcal{A}^{2}
.

Proof.

Note that by the definition of
\mathcal{T}
in (
2
) we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}\langle\mathcal{T}f,f\rangle_{\mu}=&\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Big(\mathbb{E}(f(X_{1})|X_{0}=x)\Big)f(x)\mu(\mathrm{d}{x})=\mathbb{E}_{X_{0}\sim\mu}(f(X_{0})f(X_{1}))\,,~f\in\mathcal{H}\,,\end{split}

(22)

where the second equality follows from the law of total expectation.
Therefore, the equivalence between (a) and (b) follows from the variational
principle for the smallest eigenvalue of
\mathcal{T}
(see
[
56
, Theorem 2.20]
).
To prove the equivalence between (b) and (c), we notice that (
22
) implies that (b) is equivalent to
\mathcal{T}\geq 0
.
Also, it is easy to verify that
\mathcal{T}
is bounded. Therefore, applying the square root lemma
[
51
, Theorem VI.9]
we see that (b) implies (c).
Conversely, assuming (c), we have
\langle\mathcal{T}f,f\rangle_{\mu}=\langle\mathcal{A}^{2}f,f\rangle_{\mu}=\langle\mathcal{A}f,\mathcal{A}f\rangle_{\mu}\geq 0
and therefore (b) holds.
This shows that (b) and (c) are equivalent.
∎

In the following remark we discuss the conditions in Proposition
2
.

Remark 2.2

(Positivity of eigenvalues)
.

Roughly speaking, the condition (b) of
Proposition
2
describes that “on average,
f(X_{n})
does not change sign in two consecutive steps”.

A situation under which the condition (c) is met is when the chain
X_{n}
can
be embedded into another reversible Markov process
Y_{m}
, such that
X_{n}=Y_{2n}
for all
n\geq 0
.
In particular, suppose that
X_{n}
corresponds to the state of a reversible diffusion process governed by a SDE with a generator
\mathcal{L}

and let
\tau>0
be the lag-time (see the first case in Example
2.1
). Then,
\mathcal{T}=\mathrm{e}^{\tau\mathcal{L}}=\mathcal{A}^{2}
with
\mathcal{A}=\mathrm{e}^{\frac{\tau}{2}\mathcal{L}}
. In fact, in this case all eigenvalues of
\mathcal{T}
are positive.

In general, it is possible that a transfer operator
\mathcal{T}
has negative eigenvalues
\lambda\in(-1,0)
under the assumptions in Proposition
1
.
Such negative eigenvalues (and the corresponding eigenfunctions) may even be important in studying the large-time dynamics of
X_{n}
, if they are close to
-1
.
Similar to Theorem
2.2
below, variational characterization for those negative eigenvalues (which are close to
-1
)
can be obtained and applied to developing numerical methods. However, we will not
consider negative eigenvalues, since we are mainly interested in the case where the Markov chains come from reversible SDEs and the eigenvalues in this case are all positive according to the discussion above.

Let us assume that the assumptions in Proposition
1
, as well as one of the conditions in Proposition
2
, are satisfied.
In this case, the eigenvalues
(\lambda_{i})_{i\geq 0}
to the problem (
20
) are nonnegative, and they can be ordered in a way such that

1=\lambda_{0}>\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\dots\,\geq 0\,.

(23)

Moreover, by Hilbert-Schmidt theorem
[
51
, Theorem VI.16]
,
we can choose the corresponding eigenfunctions
(\varphi_{i})_{i\geq 0}
, with

\varphi_{0}\equiv 1
, such that they form an orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{H}
.
For a function
f\in\mathcal{H}
, from (
2
) we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(f(X_{n})|X_{0}=x)=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big(\mathbb{E}(f(X_{n})|X_{n-1})|X_{0}=x\big)=\mathbb{E}((\mathcal{T}f)(X_{n-1})|X_{0}=x)

(24)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dots=(\mathcal{T}^{n}f)(x)=\mathbb{E}_{\mu}(f)+\sum_{i=1}^{+\infty}\lambda_{i}^{n}\langle f,\varphi_{i}\rangle_{\mu}\varphi_{i}(x)\,,\quad n\geq 0\,,

where
\mathbb{E}_{\mu}(f)
denotes the mean value of
f
under
\mu
. Therefore, as the Markov chain evolves, the expectation

\mathbb{E}(f(X_{n})|X_{0}=x)
converges to
\mathbb{E}_{\mu}(f)
(i.e. the value at equilibrium), and the speed of convergence is determined
by the leading eigenvalues of
\mathcal{T}
that are closest to
1
. Estimating these leading eigenvalues and computing the corresponding eigenfunctions help
to understand the long-time dynamical behaviors (e.g. identifying large timescales and metastable states) of the process
X_{n}
.
In this regard, let us present the following variational characterization of the large eigenvalues and the corresponding eigenfunctions of
\mathcal{T}
(see
[
69
, Theorem 2.1]
).

Theorem 2.2
.

Assume that the spectrum of
\mathcal{T}
consists of discrete eigenvalues

(\lambda_{i})_{i\geq 0}
in (
23
) with the corresponding (normalized) eigenfunctions
(\varphi_{i})_{i\geq 0}
.
Given an integer
m\geq 1
and positive constants
\omega_{1}\geq\dots\geq\omega_{m}>0
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}\omega_{i}(1-\lambda_{i})=\min_{f_{1},\dots,f_{m}\in\mathcal{H}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\omega_{i}\mathcal{E}(f_{i})\,,

(25)

where
\mathcal{E}
is the Dirichlet energy in (
11
), and the
minimization is over all
(f_{i})_{1\leq i\leq m}
in
\mathcal{H}
under the constraints

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mu}(f_{i})=0,\quad\langle f_{i},f_{j}\rangle_{\mu}=\delta_{ij}\,,\quad\forall\,i,j\,\in\{1,\dots,m\}\,.

(26)

Moreover, the minimum of
(
25
)–(
26
) is achieved when
f_{i}=\varphi_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq m
.

We mention that similar variational characterizations for eigenvalues of generator have been obtained in
[
68
, Theorem 3]
and
[
67
, Theorem 1]
.
The proof of Theorem
2.2
, which we present in
Appendix
B
, is adapted from the proof of
[
67
, Theorem 1, in Appendix A]
.

In the following remark we discuss the connection between Theorem
2.2
and the variational formulations in VAMP
[
61
]
.
Despite the similarities, we emphasize that, by employing the Dirichlet energy
\mathcal{E}
, we are able to draw a connection between eigenfunctions and slow CVs using Theorem
2.2
(see
Theorem
2.3
and the discussions after
Remark
2.3
), and obtain a unified framework that characterizes both
the spectrum and transition rates (see Proposition
4
in Section
2.3
) of the process.

Remark 2.3

(Connections to VAMP)
.

Theorem
2.2
is similar to the variational formulation in VAMP in the reversible case (see
[
61
, Appendix D]

and
[
63
, Section II.A]
). In fact, using the identity
(
12
) in Lemma
13
and the normalization constraints in (
26
), we can write (
25
) as

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}\omega_{i}\lambda_{i}=\max_{f_{1},\dots,f_{m}\in\mathcal{H}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\omega_{i}\langle f_{i},\mathcal{T}f_{i}\rangle_{\mu}\,,

(27)

which recovers the VAMP-1 score defined in
[
61
, Theorem 2]
when
\omega_{i}=1
for

1\leq i\leq m
(the eigenvalue
\lambda_{0}=1
and its eigenfunction are
excluded by imposing the first constraint in (
26
)).

Based on Theorem
2.2
, we discuss the optimality of eigenfunctions as system’s CV maps.
We also refer to Remark
4.1
for discussions on a numerical approach (based on Theorem
2.2
and the effective dynamics in the next section) for learning CV maps that can parametrize eigenfunctions.

To explain the idea, let us first assume
k=1
and consider scalar CV maps.
Our observation is that, for a CV map
\xi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

to be indicative of essential transitions of
X_{n}
on large timescales, it is
necessary that
\xi
is insensitive to the noisy fluctuations on the smallest timescales. In
other words,
\xi
should be a slow variable such that the observations
\xi(X_{0}),\xi(X_{1}),\dots
vary slowly (in most of the time unless essential transitions occur) as the dynamics
X_{n}
evolves.
This reasoning suggests that a natural way to find a good CV map
\xi
is by minimizing the average of squared variations

\lim_{N\rightarrow+\infty}\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}|f(X_{n+1})-f(X_{n})|^{2}

(28)

among all non-constant (i.e. non-trivial) functions
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
that are properly normalized, where the constant
2
is introduced for convenience.

The above reasoning can be extended to the case of vector-valued CV maps. Let us summarize it in the following result.

Theorem 2.3
.

Assume that
\omega=(\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{m})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
has positive components, where
\omega_{1}\geq\omega_{2}\geq\dots\geq\omega_{m}>0
.
Denote by
|z|_{\omega}:=(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\omega_{i}|z_{i}|^{2})^{1/2}
the weighted
l^{2}
-norm of vectors
z=(z_{1},\dots,z_{m})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
.
The CV map
\xi=(\varphi_{1},\dots,\varphi_{m})^{\top}
defined by the first eigenfunctions
\varphi_{1},\dots,\varphi_{m}
minimizes the average of squared variations

\min_{f}\lim_{N\rightarrow+\infty}\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}|f(X_{n+1})-f(X_{n})|^{2}_{\omega}

(29)

among functions
f=(f_{1},\dots,f_{m})^{\top}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
whose components belong to
\mathcal{H}
and satisfy
(
26
).

Proof.

Using the identity (
13
) in Lemma
13
,
we can derive the objective in (
29
) as

\displaystyle\lim_{N\rightarrow+\infty}\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}|f(X_{n+1})-f(X_{n})|^{2}_{\omega}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}\omega_{i}\left(\lim_{N\rightarrow+\infty}\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}|f_{i}(X_{n+1})-f_{i}(X_{n})|^{2}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\omega_{i}\mathcal{E}(f_{i})\,.

Therefore, Theorem
2.2
implies that the
minimum of (
29
) under the constraints
(
26
) is achieved when
f
is defined by the first
m
eigenfunctions.
∎

Note that the norm in (
29
) is an extension of the norm in (
28
) when
k>1
.
The weights
\omega
are introduced in (
29
) in order to remove the non-uniqueness of
the minimizer of (
29
) due to reorderings of
f_{1},f_{2},\dots,f_{m}
.
Condition (
26
) guarantees that
f_{1},f_{2},\dots,f_{m}
are non-constant, normalized, and pairwise orthogonal (therefore linearly independent).

2.3
Transition rates

In this section, we study the transition of the (discrete-in-time and
continuous-in-space) process
X_{n}
between two subsets
A,B\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
in the framework of
TPT
[
57
,
15
,
16
]
. We refer to
[
59
,
21
,
20
]
for extensions of TPT to different types of Markov chains.

Let us assume that
A,B\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
are two closed subsets with smooth boundaries
\partial A
and
\partial B
, respectively, such that

A\cap B=\emptyset
and the complement of their union is nonempty, i.e.
(A\cup B)^{c}:=\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus(A\cup B)\neq\emptyset
.
Recall that TPT focuses on the
reactive segments
in the trajectory of
X_{n}
.
Specifically, a trajectory segment
X_{m},X_{m+1},\dots,X_{m+j}
, where
m\geq 0
and
j\geq 1
, is called reactive, if it starts from a state in
A
and it goes
to
B
without returning to
A
, or equivalently, if
X_{m}\in A
,
X_{m+j}\in B
, and
X_{i}\in(A\cup B)^{c}
for all
m<i<m+j
.
See Figure
2
for illustration. Denote by
M_{N}^{R}
the number of reactive
segments that occur within the first
N
steps of the trajectory (the last reactive segment is allowed to end after the
N
th step).
Then, the transition rate of the process
X_{n}
from
A
to
B
is defined as

k_{AB}=\lim_{N\rightarrow+\infty}\frac{M_{N}^{R}}{N}\,.

(30)

Let us also recall the (forward)
committor

q
associated to the sets
A
and
B
, which is defined by

q(x)=\mathbb{P}(\text{starting from $x$, $X_{n}$ enters $B$ before it enters
$A$}),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,.

(31)

In mathematical literatures, the committor
q
is called equilibrium potential, which plays a central role in the study of metastability
[
9
,
10
,
5
]
. Calculating the probability in (
31
) by considering the state in the next step,
it is straightforward to verify that
q
satisfies the equation (see
[
42
, Appendix A]
for the proof in the case of Markov jump processes)

\displaystyle(\mathcal{T}q)(x)=q(x),\quad\forall~x\in(A\cup B)^{c}\,,

(32)

\displaystyle q|_{A}\equiv 0,\quad q|_{B}\equiv 1\,,

where
\mathcal{T}
is the transfer operator of the process
X_{n}
.

In the following, we give alternative expressions for the transition rate
k_{AB}
.

Proposition 3
.

Let
p(x,y)
be the transition density of
X_{n}
and
\mathcal{E}
be the Dirichlet energy defined in (
11
). Then, the following identities hold.

1.

k_{AB}=k_{BA}
.

2.

k_{AB}=\int_{A}\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}p(x,y)q(y)\,\mathrm{d}{y}\right]\mu(\mathrm{d}{x})=\int_{B}\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}p(x,y)(1-q(y))\,\mathrm{d}{y}\right]\mu(\mathrm{d}{x})
.

3.

k_{AB}=\mathcal{E}(q)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}p(x,y)(q(y)-q(x))^{2}\mathrm{d}{y}\right]\mu(\mathrm{d}{x})
.

The proof of Proposition
3
is presented in Appendix
B
.
It is worth mentioning that the proof of Proposition
3
does not
rely on reversibility and hence Proposition
3
holds for non-reversible ergodic Markov
processes as well. In particular, the third claim of
Proposition
3
relates the transition rate
k_{AB}
to the Dirichlet energy of the committor
q
.
In the reversible setting (see (
9
)), we can further obtain the following variational characterization of the committor.

Proposition 4
.

Assume that
X_{n}
is reversible. Let
q
be the committor
(
31
) associated to the transitions of
X_{n}
from
A
to

B
, and let
\mathcal{E}
denote the Dirichlet energy defined in
(
11
). Define the space

\mathcal{F}_{AB}=\Big\{f\in\mathcal{H}\,\Big|\,f|_{A}\equiv 0,~f|_{B}\equiv 1\Big\}\,.

(33)

We have

\mathcal{E}(f)=k_{AB}+\mathcal{E}(f-q)\,,\quad\forall~f\in\mathcal{F}_{AB}\,.

(34)

In particular,
q
solves the minimization problem

\min_{f\in\mathcal{F}_{AB}}\mathcal{E}(f)\,,

(35)

and the minimum value of (
35
) is
k_{AB}
.

Proof.

It suffices to prove (
34
). Let us derive

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(f)=

\displaystyle\langle f,(\mathcal{I}-\mathcal{T})f\rangle_{\mu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\big\langle q+(f-q),(\mathcal{I}-\mathcal{T})\big(q+(f-q)\big)\big\rangle_{\mu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle q,(\mathcal{I}-\mathcal{T})q\rangle_{\mu}+\langle f-q,(\mathcal{I}-\mathcal{T})(f-q)\rangle_{\mu}+2\langle q,(\mathcal{I}-\mathcal{T})(f-q)\rangle_{\mu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(q)+\mathcal{E}(f-q)+2\langle(\mathcal{I}-\mathcal{T})q,f-q\rangle_{\mu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle k_{AB}+\mathcal{E}(f-q)\,,

where the first, the third, and the fourth equalities follow from (
12
) in Lemma
13
and the
self-adjointness of
\mathcal{T}
, the last equality follows from the third
claim of Proposition
3
, the fact that
f\equiv q
on
A\cup B

thanks to the boundary condition in (
33
) (which is met by both
f
and
q
), as well as the identity
(\mathcal{I}-\mathcal{T})q=0
on
(A\cup B)^{c}
as stated in the equation (
32
).
∎

We note that the variational formulation (
35
) is well-known in the literatures on TPT
[
16
]
and on metastability
[
9
]
.

Similar to Theorem
2.3
which concerns the leading eigenfunctions, we have the following characterization of the committor as a slow variable for the study of transitions from
A
to
B
.

Theorem 2.4
.

Assume that
X_{n}
is reversible. Given two disjoint subsets
A,B\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the CV map defined by the committor
q
minimizes the average of squared variations

\min_{f\in\mathcal{F}_{AB}}\lim_{N\rightarrow+\infty}\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}|f(X_{n+1})-f(X_{n})|^{2}\,,

where
\mathcal{F}_{AB}
is the space defined in (
33
).

Proof.

The conclusion follows directly from Proposition
4
and Lemma
13
.
∎

3
Effective dynamics for a given CV map

In this section, we study the effective dynamics of
X_{n}
for a given CV map.
We give the definition of the effective dynamics in Section
3.1
and then study its properties in Section
3.2
.
In particular, we show that its transition density can be characterized via
a relative entropy minimization to the transition density of
X_{n}
and discuss
the application of this fact in finding CVs (see Proposition
6

and Remark
3.1
). Unless explicitly stated, in this section we do not require the process
X_{n}
to be reversible.

3.1
Definitions

Before we define the effective dynamics for
X_{n}
, we need to introduce several quantities associated to the CV map.
Given
1\leq k<d
, let us define the space

\Xi=\Big\{\xi\in C^{3}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{k})\,\Big|\,\mbox{$\xi$ is onto, $\mbox{rank}(\nabla\xi(x))=k$ at each $x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$}\Big\}\,,

(36)

where
\nabla\xi(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times k}
denotes the Jacobian matrix of
\xi
.
For
\xi\in\Xi
and given
z\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
, the level set

\Sigma_{z}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,|\,\xi(x)=z\}

(37)

is a
(d-k)
-dimensional submanifold in
\mathbb{R}^{d}

(see
[
39
, Remark 1]
and

[
38
, Section 1]
; see Figure
1
for illustration). We denote by
\sigma_{\Sigma_{z}}
the surface measure of
\Sigma_{z}

and we define the probability measure
\mu_{z}
on
\Sigma_{z}
as

\mu_{z}(\mathrm{d}{x})=p_{z}(x)\left[\mathrm{det}(\nabla\xi(x)^{\top}\nabla\xi(x))\right]^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sigma_{\Sigma_{z}}(\mathrm{d}{x})\,,\quad p_{z}(x)=\frac{\pi(x)}{\widetilde{\pi}(z)}\,,

(38)

where
\widetilde{\pi}(z)
is the normalizing constant defined by

\widetilde{\pi}(z)=\int_{\Sigma_{z}}\pi(x)[\mathrm{det}(\nabla\xi(x)^{\top}\nabla\xi(x))]^{-\frac{1}{2}}\,\sigma_{\Sigma_{z}}(\mathrm{d}{x})\,.

(39)

A determinant term is introduced in (
38
) and (
39
), so that by co-area formula (see
[
18
, Theorem 3.11]
and
[
37
, Lemma 3.2]
) we have, for all integrable functions
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
,

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x)\mu(\mathrm{d}{x})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}\Big(\int_{\Sigma_{z}}f(x)\,\mu_{z}(\mathrm{d}{x})\Big)\widetilde{\mu}(\mathrm{d}{z})\,,

(40)

where
\widetilde{\mu}
is the pushforward measure of
\mu
by the map
\xi
, given by

\widetilde{\mu}(\mathrm{d}{z})=\widetilde{\pi}(z)\mathrm{d}{z}\,,\quad z\in\mathbb{R}^{k}\,.

(41)

Note that (
40
) is identical to the identity in the disintegration theorem for probability measures
[
1
, Theorem 5.3.1]
.
The measure
\mu_{z}
is often termed as the conditional probability measure, since by (
40
) and the law of total expectation it holds

\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{z}}(f)=\int_{\Sigma_{z}}f(x)\,\mu_{z}(\mathrm{d}{x})=\mathbb{E}_{\mu}(f(x)\,|\,\xi(x)=z)\,,

for all integrable functions
f
.
In particular, when
\xi
is a linear map, the level set
\Sigma_{z}
is a linear subspace and the expressions of quantities introduced above can be largely simplified
[
34
,
35
]
.

To define the effective dynamics for the process
X_{n}
, we borrow the idea
from
[
33
]
for the effective dynamics of SDEs.
Specifically, notice that
\xi(X_{n})
is in general a non-Markovian process in

\mathbb{R}^{k}
, since the probability that
\xi(X_{n+1})\in\widetilde{D}
at
(n+1)
th step for a subset
\widetilde{D}\subset\mathbb{R}^{k}
depends on the
full state
X_{n}=x
at
n
th step rather than
\xi(X_{n})=z
only, i.e.

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\xi(X_{n+1})\in\widetilde{D}|X_{n}=x)=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}p(x,y)\mathbbm{1}_{\widetilde{D}}(\xi(y))\,\mathrm{d}{y}

(42)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\widetilde{D}}\left(\int_{\Sigma_{w}}p(x,y)[\mathrm{det}(\nabla\xi(y)^{\top}\nabla\xi(y))]^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sigma_{\Sigma_{z}}(\mathrm{d}{y})\right)\,\mathrm{d}{w}\,,

where
\mathbbm{1}_{\widetilde{D}}
denotes the indicator function of the set

\widetilde{D}
and the expression on the second line follows from co-area formula
[
37
, Lemma 3.2]
.
Following the idea in
[
33
]
, one way to define a transition
density in
\mathbb{R}^{k}
is to replace the expression on the right hand side
of (
42
) by its average over the states
X_{n}=x
on
\Sigma_{z}
with respect to the conditional measure
\mu_{z}
in (
38
).
Specifically, given the CV map
\xi
, we define the effective dynamics of
X_{n}
as follows.

Definition 1

(Effective dynamics)
.

The effective dynamics of
X_{n}
associated to the CV map
\xi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{k}
is defined as the Markov process
Z_{n}
in
\mathbb{R}^{k}
with the transition density

\widetilde{p}(z,w)
, where

\displaystyle\widetilde{p}(z,w)=

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma_{z}}\Big[\int_{\Sigma_{w}}p(x,y)[\mathrm{det}(\nabla\xi(y)^{\top}\nabla\xi(y))]^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sigma_{\Sigma_{w}}(\mathrm{d}{y})\Big]\,\mu_{z}(\mathrm{d}{x})

(43)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\widetilde{\pi}(w)\int_{\Sigma_{z}}\left(\int_{\Sigma_{w}}\frac{p(x,y)}{\pi(y)}\mu_{w}(\mathrm{d}{y})\right)\,\mu_{z}(\mathrm{d}{x})\,,\quad z,w\in\mathbb{R}^{k}\,.

Note that the second equality in (
43
) follows from the expression in (
38
).
Using (
40
) and the fact that
p(x,\cdot)
is a probability
density in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we can verify that
\widetilde{p}(z,\cdot)
in
(
43
) indeed defines a probability density for
z\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
, i.e.
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}\widetilde{p}(z,w)\,\mathrm{d}{w}=1
. As we show in the next subsection,
the invariant distribution of
Z_{n}
is related to the free energy associated to

\xi

[
37
, Section 3.2.1]
and its transition density

\widetilde{p}
gives the best approximation of
p
in the sense of relative
entropy minimization
[
13
, Section 2.3]
. As discussed above, the effective dynamics defined in Definition
43
can be viewed as
the counterpart to the effective dynamics
[
33
]
for discrete-in-time and continuous-in-space Markov processes.
It also shares similarities with the effective dynamics of Markov chains in
previous works
[
64
,
23
]

and with the asymptotic Markov chains considered in the study of metastability
[
4
,
5
,
32
]
.

Figure 1
:
Illustration of level sets of the CV map
\xi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{k}
for values
z,w\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
.

3.2
Properties of effective dynamics

In this section, we study properties of the effective dynamics
Z_{n}
in Definition
43
whose transition density
\widetilde{p}
is defined in (
43
). Let us first identify its invariant probability measure.

Proposition 5
.

The probability measure
\widetilde{\mu}
defined in (
41
) is an invariant measure of
Z_{n}
.

Proof.

To show the invariance of

\displaystyle\widetilde{\pi}(w)\mathrm{d}{w}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\int_{\Sigma_{w}}\frac{p(x,y)}{\pi(y)}\mu_{w}(\mathrm{d}{y})\right)\,\mu(\mathrm{d}{x})

\displaystyle\widetilde{\pi}(w)\mathrm{d}{w}\left(\int_{\Sigma_{w}}\frac{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}p(x,y)\mu(\mathrm{d}{x})}{\pi(y)}\,\mu_{w}(\mathrm{d}{y})\right)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\pi}(w)\mathrm{d}{w}=\widetilde{\mu}(\mathrm{d}{w})\,,

where the first equality follows from the second equality in (

Proposition 6

For

Proof.

For any

with the expression

we show the following identities hold.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mu}\Big(D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(p(x,\cdot)\,\|\,g(x,\cdot)\big)\Big)-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}p(x,y)\ln p(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}{y}\bigg)\mu(\mathrm{d}{x})

\displaystyle-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}p(x,y)\ln\widetilde{g}(\xi(x),\xi(y))\mathrm{d}{y}\bigg)\mu(\mathrm{d}{x})-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\ln g_{\xi(x)}(x)\mu(\mathrm{d}{x})

\displaystyle-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}p(x,y)\ln\widetilde{g}(\xi(x),\xi(y))\mathrm{d}{y}\bigg)\mu(\mathrm{d}{x})+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_{z}\,\|\,g_{z})\widetilde{\mu}(\mathrm{d}{z})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}\bigg(D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(\widetilde{p}(z,\cdot)\,\|\,\widetilde{g}(z,\cdot)\big)+D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_{z}\,\|\,g_{z})\bigg)\widetilde{\mu}(\mathrm{d}{z})

In the above,

denotes the KL divergence of

from

, both of which are densities on
\Sigma_{z}

with respect to

We start by calculating the first term on the first line of (

[
13
, Section

with expression in (

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mu}\Big(D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(p(x,\cdot)\,\|\,g(x,\cdot)\big)\Big)

(50)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}p(x,y)\ln\frac{p(x,y)}{g(x,y)}\,\mathrm{d}{y}\bigg)\mu(\mathrm{d}{x})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}p(x,y)\ln p(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}{y}\bigg)\mu(\mathrm{d}{x})-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}p(x,y)\ln g(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}{y}\bigg)\mu(\mathrm{d}{x})\,,

where the first term on the last line is independent of

\displaystyle-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}p(x,y)\ln g(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}{y}\bigg)\mu(\mathrm{d}{x})

(51)

\displaystyle=\,

\displaystyle-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}p(x,y)\left(\ln\widetilde{g}(\xi(x),\xi(y))+\ln g_{\xi(y)}(y)\right)\,\mathrm{d}{y}\bigg)\mu(\mathrm{d}{x})

\displaystyle-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}p(x,y)\ln\widetilde{g}(\xi(x),\xi(y))\mathrm{d}{y}\bigg)\mu(\mathrm{d}{x})-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\ln g_{\xi(x)}(x)\mu(\mathrm{d}{x})

\displaystyle I_{1}+I_{2}\,,

where the first equality follows from the expression of

of
\mu
in (

and

For

\displaystyle I_{1}=

\displaystyle-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}p(x,y)\ln\widetilde{g}(\xi(x),\xi(y))\mathrm{d}{y}\bigg)\mu(\mathrm{d}{x})

(52)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}\widetilde{p}(z,w)\ln\widetilde{g}(z,w)\,\mathrm{d}{w}\bigg)\widetilde{\mu}(\mathrm{d}{z})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(\widetilde{p}(z,\cdot)\,\|\,\widetilde{g}(z,\cdot)\big)\widetilde{\mu}(\mathrm{d}{z})-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}(\widetilde{p}\ln\widetilde{p})(z,w)\,\mathrm{d}{w}\bigg)\widetilde{\mu}(\mathrm{d}{z})\,.

where the first equality follows from the co-area formula, the identity (

Similarly, for

\displaystyle-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\ln g_{\xi(x)}(x)\mu(\mathrm{d}{x})

\displaystyle-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}\left(\int_{\Sigma_{z}}\ln g_{z}(x)\mu_{z}(\mathrm{d}{x})\right)\widetilde{\mu}(\mathrm{d}{z})

\displaystyle-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}\left(\int_{\Sigma_{z}}\ln g_{z}(x)p_{z}(x)\left[\mathrm{det}(\nabla\xi(x)^{\top}\nabla\xi(x))\right]^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sigma_{\Sigma_{z}}(\mathrm{d}{x})\right)\widetilde{\mu}(\mathrm{d}{z})

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_{z}\,\|\,g_{z})\widetilde{\mu}(\mathrm{d}{z})-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}\bigg(\int_{\Sigma_{z}}\ln p_{z}(x)\mu_{z}(\mathrm{d}{x})\bigg)\widetilde{\mu}(\mathrm{d}{z})

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_{z}\,\|\,g_{z})\widetilde{\mu}(\mathrm{d}{z})-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\ln p_{\xi(x)}(x)\mu(\mathrm{d}{x})\,.

Theorem 3.1

The CV map

\displaystyle\min_{\xi\in\Xi,\widetilde{g}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}}\bigg[-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Big(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}p(x,y)\ln\widetilde{g}(\xi(x),\xi(y))\,\mathrm{d}{y}\Big)\mu(\mathrm{d}{x})+\max_{\widetilde{f}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\ln\widetilde{f}(\xi(x))\mu(\mathrm{d}{x})\bigg]\,,

,
\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}

Proof.

We apply the equalities in (

The equivalence between (
54
) and (

Remark 3.1

(Algorithmic realization)
.

Theorem

provides two possible data-driven numerical approaches for

\widetilde{p}
of the effective dynamics.
(see
[

for alternative objective loss functions involving weighted

1.

The first approach is to solve (

) (or (
55

This is close to the loss function proposed in

]
for learning CV maps based on normalizing flows
[

]

2.

The second approach is to solve (

\displaystyle\begin{split}\min_{\xi\in\Xi}\Big[&-\max_{\widetilde{g}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\ln\widetilde{g}(\xi(X_{n}),\xi(X_{n+1}))+\max_{\widetilde{f}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\ln\widetilde{f}(\xi(X_{n}))\Big]\,.\end{split}

), learning

of

For any two functions

and

Proof.

Concerning the first claim, given

\displaystyle(\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}\,\widetilde{f})(z)=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}\widetilde{p}(z,w)\widetilde{f}(w)\,\mathrm{d}{w}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}\left[\widetilde{\pi}(w)\int_{\Sigma_{z}}\left(\int_{\Sigma_{w}}\frac{p(x,y)}{\pi(y)}\mu_{w}(\mathrm{d}{y})\right)\,\mu_{z}(\mathrm{d}{x})\right]\widetilde{f}(w)\,\mathrm{d}{w}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma_{z}}\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}\left(\int_{\Sigma_{w}}\frac{p(x,y)}{\pi(y)}\mu_{w}(\mathrm{d}{y})\right)\,\widetilde{f}(w)\,\widetilde{\mu}(\mathrm{d}{w})\right]\mu_{z}(\mathrm{d}{x})

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma_{z}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{p(x,y)}{\pi(y)}\widetilde{f}(\xi(y))\mu(\mathrm{d}{y})\right)\mu_{z}(\mathrm{d}{x})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma_{z}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}p(x,y)\widetilde{f}(\xi(y))\mathrm{d}{y}\right)\mu_{z}(\mathrm{d}{x})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{z}}(\mathcal{T}f)\,,

where
f=\widetilde{f}\circ\xi
, and we have used
(
61
), the second equality in (

), as well as (

) to derive the above equalities.
Concerning the second claim, using (

), (

),
the first claim, and the identity (

), we can compute

\displaystyle\langle\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}\,\widetilde{f},\widetilde{h}\rangle_{\widetilde{\mu}}=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}\big(\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{z}}(\mathcal{T}f)\big)\widetilde{h}(z)\widetilde{\mu}(\mathrm{d}{z})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}\left(\int_{\Sigma_{z}}(\mathcal{T}f)(x)\mu_{z}(\mathrm{d}{x})\right)\widetilde{h}(z)\widetilde{\mu}(\mathrm{d}{z})

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(\mathcal{T}f)(x)\widetilde{h}(\xi(x))\mu(\mathrm{d}{x})

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{T}f,h\rangle_{\mu}\,,

Z_{n}
and its connection to the
reversibility of
X_{n}
. Denote by
\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}^{*}
the adjoint of

\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}
in
\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}
, which satisfies

\langle\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}\widetilde{f},\widetilde{h}\,\rangle_{\widetilde{\mu}}=\langle\widetilde{f},\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}^{*}\widetilde{h}\,\rangle_{\widetilde{\mu}}
for any
\widetilde{f},\widetilde{h}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}
.
The following result gives the concrete expression of
\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}^{*}
.

Proposition 7
.

For
\widetilde{f}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}
, we have the following identity

(\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}^{*}\widetilde{f}\,)(z)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}\widetilde{p}^{*}(z,w)\widetilde{f}(w)\,\mathrm{d}{w}\,,\quad z\in\mathbb{R}^{k}\,,

(65)

with the transition density

\displaystyle\widetilde{p}^{*}(z,w)=\frac{\widetilde{p}(w,z)\widetilde{\pi}(w)}{\widetilde{\pi}(z)}=

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma_{z}}\left(\int_{\Sigma_{w}}p^{*}(x,y)[\mathrm{det}(\nabla\xi(y)^{\top}\nabla\xi(y))]^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sigma_{\Sigma_{w}}(\mathrm{d}{y})\right)\mu_{z}(\mathrm{d}{x})

(66)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\widetilde{\pi}(w)\int_{\Sigma_{z}}\left(\int_{\Sigma_{w}}\frac{p^{*}(x,y)}{\pi(y)}\mu_{w}(\mathrm{d}{y})\right)\mu_{z}(\mathrm{d}{x})\,,

for
z,w\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
, where
p^{*}
is given in (
5
).

Proof.

For any
\widetilde{f},\widetilde{h}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}
, direct calculation gives

\displaystyle\langle\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}^{*}\widetilde{f},\widetilde{h}\rangle_{\widetilde{\mu}}=\langle\widetilde{f},\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}\widetilde{h}\rangle_{\widetilde{\mu}}=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}\bigg[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}\widetilde{p}(z,w)\widetilde{h}(w)\,\mathrm{d}{w}\bigg]\widetilde{f}(z)\widetilde{\mu}(\mathrm{d}{z})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}\bigg[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}\frac{\widetilde{p}(z,w)\widetilde{\pi}(z)}{\widetilde{\pi}(w)}\widetilde{f}(z)\,\mathrm{d}{z}\bigg]\widetilde{h}(w)\,\widetilde{\mu}(\mathrm{d}{w})\,,

from which we obtain (
65
) with
\widetilde{p}^{*}
given by the first equality in (
66
).
To prove the second equality in (
66
), let us derive

\displaystyle\widetilde{p}^{*}(z,w)=

\displaystyle\frac{\widetilde{p}(w,z)\widetilde{\pi}(w)}{\widetilde{\pi}(z)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\widetilde{\pi}(w)\int_{\Sigma_{w}}\left(\int_{\Sigma_{z}}\,\frac{p(y,x)}{\pi(x)}\,\mu_{z}(\mathrm{d}{x})\right)\mu_{w}(\mathrm{d}{y})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\widetilde{\pi}(w)\int_{\Sigma_{z}}\left(\int_{\Sigma_{w}}\,\frac{p^{*}(x,y)}{\pi(y)}\,\mu_{w}(\mathrm{d}{y})\right)\mu_{z}(\mathrm{d}{x})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma_{z}}\left(\int_{\Sigma_{w}}(p^{*}(x,y)\,[\mathrm{det}(\nabla\xi(y)^{\top}\nabla\xi(y))]^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sigma_{\Sigma_{w}}(\mathrm{d}{y})\right)\,\mu_{z}(\mathrm{d}{x})\,,

where the second equality follows from the second equality in (
43
), the third equality follows from the definition of
p^{*}

in (
5
) and an exchange of integrals, the fourth equality
follows from the definition of
\mu_{w}
in (
38
).
∎

Proposition
7
implies that

\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}^{*}
is the transfer operator associated to the
effective dynamics of the adjoint process of
X_{n}
(the process defined by the transition density
p^{*}
). As a simple application of Proposition

, we have the following result on the reversibility of
Z_{n}
.

Corollary 1
.

Suppose that
X_{n}
is reversible. Then, the effective process
Z_{n}
is also reversible.

Proof.

The fact that
X_{n}
is reversible implies
p^{*}=p
(see (
9
)). In this case, we obtain

\widetilde{p}^{*}=\widetilde{p}
by comparing (
66
) to (
43
),
and (
65
) implies that
\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}^{*}=\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}
.
Therefore,
\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}
is self-adjoint and the effective process
Z_{n}
is reversible.
∎

Before concluding this section, let us introduce the Dirichlet energy
associated to the effective dynamics
Z_{n}
(assuming reversibility).
Similar to the Dirichlet energy (
11
) associated to
X_{n}
, for a test function
\widetilde{f}:\mathbb{R}^{k}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, we define its Dirichlet energy associated to

as

\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}(\widetilde{f})=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}\widetilde{p}(z,w)(\widetilde{f}(w)-\widetilde{f}(z))^{2}\mathrm{d}{w}\right)\widetilde{\mu}(\mathrm{d}{z})\,.

(67)

In analogy to (
12
), applying Lemma

we have (for the first identity see the proof of Lemma

)

\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}(\widetilde{f})=\langle(\mathcal{I}-\widetilde{\mathcal{T}})\widetilde{f},\widetilde{f}\rangle_{\widetilde{\mu}}=\langle(\mathcal{I}-\mathcal{T})(\widetilde{f}\circ\xi),\widetilde{f}\circ\xi\rangle_{\mu}=\mathcal{E}(\widetilde{f}\circ\xi)\,.

(68)

Both (

) and (

to those of

.

In this section, we study the approximation of the effective dynamics to the original process

We assume that

In Section

4.1
Timescales

First, we compare the timescales of the effective dynamics

to the timescales of
X_{n}
.
Similar to the eigenvalue problem (
20
) in
Section
2.2
associated to
X_{n}
, we introduce the eigenvalue
problem associated to the effective dynamics
Z_{n}
in
\mathbb{R}^{k}
:

\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}\,\widetilde{f}=\widetilde{\lambda}\,\widetilde{f}\,,

(69)

where

\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}
is the transfer operator in
(
61
),
\widetilde{\lambda}\in\mathbb{R}
, and the function
\widetilde{f}:\mathbb{R}^{k}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is normalized such that

\langle\widetilde{f},\widetilde{f}\rangle_{\widetilde{\mu}}=1
.
In analogy to the discussions in Section
2.2
, we also assume
that the spectrum of
\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}
consists of eigenvalues
(\widetilde{\lambda}_{i})_{i\geq 0}
of (
69
),
which are within the range
(0,1]
and can be sorted in a way such that

1=\widetilde{\lambda}_{0}>\widetilde{\lambda}_{1}\geq\widetilde{\lambda}_{2}\geq\dots\geq 0\,,

(70)

with the corresponding orthonormal eigenfunctions
(\widetilde{\varphi}_{i})_{i\geq 0}
, where
\widetilde{\varphi}_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{k}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
for
i\geq 0
and
\widetilde{\varphi}_{0}\equiv 1
.
This assumption is true when the density
\widetilde{p}
and the transfer
operator
\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}
satisfy the conditions of Proposition
1
and Proposition
2
.

We have the following result comparing the eigenvalues of
\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}
to the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{T}
.

Theorem 4.1
.

For each
i=1,2,\dots
, let
\lambda_{i}
be the eigenvalue of

\mathcal{T}
in (
23
) and
\varphi_{i}
be the
corresponding normalized eigenfunction. Let
\widetilde{\lambda}_{i}
be the
eigenvalue of
\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}
in (
70
) and

\widetilde{\varphi}_{i}
be the corresponding normalized eigenfunction. Let

\mathcal{E}
denote the Dirichlet energy (
11
) associated to
X_{n}
.
The following claims hold.

1.

\widetilde{\lambda}_{i}\leq\lambda_{i}
.

2.

For any
j=1,2,\dots
, we have
\lambda_{i}-\widetilde{\lambda}_{j}=\mathcal{E}(\widetilde{\varphi}_{j}\circ\xi-\varphi_{i})-(1-\lambda_{i})\|\widetilde{\varphi}_{j}\circ\xi-\varphi_{i}\|^{2}_{\mu}
.

3.

Assume that there is a function
\widetilde{h}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}
such that
\varphi_{i}=\widetilde{h}\circ\xi
. Then,
\lambda_{i}
is an eigenvalue
of
\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}
and
\widetilde{h}
is the corresponding
eigenfunction, i.e.
\widetilde{\lambda}_{j}=\lambda_{i}
and
\widetilde{\varphi}_{j}=\widetilde{h}
for some integer
j\geq 1
.

Proof.

The proof is similar to the proof of
[
66
, Proposition 5]
.
Concerning the first claim, notice that
1-\lambda_{i}
is the
(i+1)
th smallest eigenvalue of
\mathcal{I}-\mathcal{T}
for
i\geq 1
.
Therefore, the min-max theorem
[
56
, Theorem 4.14]
implies

1-\lambda_{i}=\inf_{H_{i}}\sup_{\psi\in H_{i}}\Big\{\langle(\mathcal{I}-\mathcal{T})\psi,\psi\rangle_{\mu},\langle\psi,\psi\rangle_{\mu}=1\Big\}\,,

(71)

where
H_{i}
goes over all
(i+1)
-dimensional linear subspace of

\mathcal{H}
. In particular, let
\widetilde{H}_{i}
be any
(i+1)
-dimensional linear subspace of

\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}
and define
H_{i}=\{\widetilde{f}\circ\xi\,|\,\widetilde{f}\in\widetilde{H}_{i}\}
. Then, it is easy to show that
H_{i}
is an

(i+1)
-dimensional linear subspace of
\mathcal{H}
. Therefore, for such
H_{i}
, from (
71
) we obtain

1-\lambda_{i}\leq\sup_{\psi\in H_{i}}\Big\{\langle(\mathcal{I}-\mathcal{T})\psi,\psi\rangle_{\mu},\langle\psi,\psi\rangle_{\mu}=1\Big\}\,.

(72)

Applying Lemma
2
, we have

\sup_{\psi\in H_{i}}\Big\{\langle(\mathcal{I}-\mathcal{T})\psi,\psi\rangle_{\mu},\langle\psi,\psi\rangle_{\mu}=1\Big\}=\sup_{\widetilde{\psi}\in\widetilde{H}_{i}}\Big\{\langle(\mathcal{I}-\widetilde{\mathcal{T}})\widetilde{\psi},\widetilde{\psi}\rangle_{\widetilde{\mu}},\langle\widetilde{\psi},\widetilde{\psi}\rangle_{\widetilde{\mu}}=1\Big\}\,.

(73)

Combining (
72
) and (
73
), and applying the min-max theorem to
\mathcal{I}-\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}
, we get

1-\lambda_{i}\leq\inf_{\widetilde{H}_{i}}\sup_{\widetilde{\psi}\in\widetilde{H}_{i}}\Big\{\langle(\mathcal{I}-\widetilde{\mathcal{T}})\widetilde{\psi},\widetilde{\psi}\rangle_{\widetilde{\mu}},\langle\widetilde{\psi},\widetilde{\psi}\rangle_{\widetilde{\mu}}=1\Big\}=1-\widetilde{\lambda}_{i}\,,

which implies the first claim.

Concerning the second claim, let us derive, for any
j\geq 1
,

\displaystyle 1-\widetilde{\lambda}_{j}=

\displaystyle\langle(\mathcal{I}-\widetilde{\mathcal{T}})\widetilde{\varphi}_{j},\widetilde{\varphi}_{j}\rangle_{\widetilde{\mu}}

(74)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle(\mathcal{I}-\mathcal{T})(\widetilde{\varphi}_{j}\circ\xi),\widetilde{\varphi}_{j}\circ\xi\rangle_{\mu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle(\mathcal{I}-\mathcal{T})(\widetilde{\varphi}_{j}\circ\xi-\varphi_{i}+\varphi_{i}),\widetilde{\varphi}_{j}\circ\xi-\varphi_{i}+\varphi_{i}\rangle_{\mu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle(\mathcal{I}-\mathcal{T})(\widetilde{\varphi}_{j}\circ\xi-\varphi_{i}),\widetilde{\varphi}_{j}\circ\xi-\varphi_{i}\rangle_{\mu}+\langle(\mathcal{I}-\mathcal{T})\varphi_{i},\varphi_{i}\rangle_{\mu}

\displaystyle+2\langle(\mathcal{I}-\mathcal{T})\varphi_{i},\widetilde{\varphi}_{j}\circ\xi-\varphi_{i}\rangle_{\mu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\widetilde{\varphi}_{j}\circ\xi-\varphi_{i})+1-\lambda_{i}+2(1-\lambda_{i})\langle\varphi_{i},\widetilde{\varphi}_{j}\circ\xi-\varphi_{i}\rangle_{\mu}\,,

where we have used the fact that
\widetilde{\varphi}_{i}
is normalized to get the first equality, Lemma
2
to obtain the second
equality, and the fact that
\varphi_{i}
is the eigenfunction of
\mathcal{I}-\mathcal{T}
corresponding to the eigenvalue
1-\lambda_{i}
to arrive at the last equality.
For the last term on the last line of (
74
), we notice that

2\langle\varphi_{i},\widetilde{\varphi}_{j}\circ\xi-\varphi_{i}\rangle_{\mu}=2\langle\varphi_{i},\widetilde{\varphi}_{j}\circ\xi\rangle_{\mu}-2=-\langle\widetilde{\varphi}_{j}\circ\xi-\varphi_{i},\widetilde{\varphi}_{j}\circ\xi-\varphi_{i}\rangle_{\mu}=-\|\widetilde{\varphi}_{j}\circ\xi-\varphi_{i}\|_{\mu}^{2}\,,

(75)

which follows from the fact that both eigenfunctions
\varphi_{i}
and

\widetilde{\varphi}_{j}
are normalized, and the equality

\|\widetilde{\varphi}_{j}\circ\xi\|_{\mu}=\|\widetilde{\varphi}_{j}\|_{\widetilde{\mu}}=1
as implied by
Lemma
2
. The identity in the second claim is obtained by combining and reorganizing (
74
)–(
75
).

Concerning the third claim, using the identity in the first claim of Lemma
2
we obtain

(\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}\widetilde{h})(z)=\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{z}}(\mathcal{T}(\widetilde{h}\circ\xi))=\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{z}}(\mathcal{T}\varphi_{i})=\lambda_{i}\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{z}}(\varphi_{i})=\lambda_{i}\widetilde{h}(z)
, from which we can conclude.
∎

Let us discuss the implications of Theorem
4.1
. The first
claim of Theorem
4.1
implies that the eigenvalues of
Z_{n}
are
smaller than (or equal to) the corresponding eigenvalues of
X_{n}
. In other
words, the convergence of the effective dynamics to equilibrium is faster than
(or equal to) the convergence of the original process (see
(
24
)). The second claim of
Theorem
4.1
implies that the true eigenvalue
\lambda_{i}
(or
equivalently, timescale) of
X_{n}
can be well approximated by an eigenvalue of

Z_{n}
, if the corresponding eigenfunction
\varphi_{i}
is approximately a
function of the CV map
\xi
. In particular, if a component of
\xi
is capable of representing the eigenfunction
\varphi_{i}
, e.g.
\varphi_{i}=\xi_{1}
, then the third claim of Theorem
4.1
implies
that the corresponding eigenvalue
\lambda_{i}
is preserved by the effective
dynamics. Recall that, in data-driven numerical approaches for timescales estimations, employing system’s features corresponds to estimating
timescales of effective dynamics (see Remark
3.2
). Therefore,
Theorem
4.1
suggests that, in order to obtain accurate timescale estimations,
features should be selected such that they can parametrize the corresponding eigenfunctions.

We conclude this section with a theorem on a numerical approach for learning the CV map
\xi
by optimizing timescale estimations.

Theorem 4.2

(Optimizing CV maps by timescale estimations)
.

The variational formulation (
25
)–(
26
) in Theorem
2.2

can be used to learn the CV map
\xi
, by optimizing
the objective in (
25
) with functions
f_{1},\dots,f_{m}
, which are parametrized by a common CV map
\xi
,
i.e.
f_{i}=\widetilde{f}_{i}\circ\xi
for some function
\widetilde{f}_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{k}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, where
1\leq i\leq m
.
In this case, we have

\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}f_{i}=\widetilde{f}_{i}\circ\xi,\xi\in\Xi\\
\widetilde{f}_{i}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{H}},1\leq i\leq m\end{subarray}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\omega_{i}\mathcal{E}(f_{i})=\min_{\xi\in\Xi}\min_{\widetilde{f}_{1},\dots,\widetilde{f}_{m}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\omega_{i}\mathcal{E}(\widetilde{f}_{i}\circ\xi)=\min_{\xi\in\Xi}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\omega_{i}(1-\widetilde{\lambda}_{i}),

(76)

where the functions
\widetilde{f}_{i}
satisfy the constraints (
64
).

Proof.

The constraints (
64
) on
\widetilde{f}_{i}
follow from the constraints in (
26
) and Lemma
2
.
To prove the second equality in (
76
), we derive

\min_{\xi\in\Xi}\min_{\widetilde{f}_{1},\dots,\widetilde{f}_{m}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\omega_{i}\mathcal{E}(\widetilde{f}_{i}\circ\xi)=\min_{\xi\in\Xi}\min_{\widetilde{f}_{1},\dots,\widetilde{f}_{m}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\omega_{i}\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}(\widetilde{f}_{i})=\min_{\xi\in\Xi}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\omega_{i}(1-\widetilde{\lambda}_{i}),

where the first equality follows from (
68
) and the second equality follows from the variational formulation in
Theorem
2.2
when applied to the eigenfunctions of the effective dynamics.
∎

Remark 4.1

(Interpretation and algorithmic realization)
.

The rightmost expression in (
76
) shows that optimizing functions
f_{1},\dots,f_{m}
of the form

f_{i}=\widetilde{f}_{i}\circ\xi
for
1\leq i\leq m
by minimizing
the objective in (
25
) (under the
constraints (
26
)) is equivalent to optimizing the CV map
\xi
such that the quantity
\sum_{i=1}^{m}\omega_{i}(1-\lambda_{i})
associated to
X_{n}
is best approximated by the quantity
\sum_{i=1}^{m}\omega_{i}(1-\widetilde{\lambda}_{i})
associated to the effective dynamics
built using the map
\xi
. We refer to
[
36
, Section 2.7]
for a numerical algorithm that learns an encoder (i.e. the map
\xi
) using a loss function that combines reconstruction error and the objective in (
25
).

4.2
Transition rates

To begin with, recall that in TPT the transition rate

Theorem 4.3
.

Assume that the sets

\xi
by (

and

from

is the Dirichlet energy in (

.

\widetilde{k}_{\widetilde{A}\widetilde{B}}=k_{AB}

Proof.

We prove the claims by applying Proposition
4
. Consider the

5
Conclusion and Discussions

One of the ultimate goals in complex system science is to find a faithful
low-dimensional representation of the system’s dynamics. The effective
dynamics given by (a set of) collective variables as defined here offers such
a low-dimensional representation. In this paper, we have studied this
effective dynamics for discrete-in-time Markov processes and presented a
detailed study on its properties, including invariances and approximation
quality with respect to main timescales, transition rates, and KL-distance to the full dynamics. The results characterize which CVs are optimal regarding the respective minimization of these errors.

There are manifold connections between the work presented herein and similar
approaches proposed in the literature. For example, the effective dynamics of
diffusion processes described by SDEs have been studied in several works and
different types of error estimates were obtained, e.g.,
in
[
34
,
66
,
35
,
14
,
39
]
.
The error estimates in Section
4
of this work are closely related to the estimates in
[
66
]
. Beyond this, the quality of effective dynamics in approximating the original process can also be studied by considering the relative entropy or pathwise error between the effective dynamics and the original process, as is done, e.g., in
[
34
,
35
,
14
,
39
]
for diffusion processes.
However, to our best knowledge, there are no similar results on the optimal
characterization for effective dynamics of diffusion processes in terms of relative entropy.

While the main results of this work are theoretical, we discussed the
relevance of effective dynamics in data-driven numerical approaches, such as
in VAMPnets
[
40
,
12
]
, deep learning of transition manifolds
[
7
]
and existing algorithms for learning CV maps
[
62
,
36
]
.
The approach taken herein, however, may open the horizon for algorithmic approaches for learning “good” CVs and the associated effective dynamics
simultaneously
where the already learned effective dynamics might also be utilized for exploring state space, a key step that is still the main bottleneck of available algorithmic approaches. In future work, this approach will be further studied and applied to concrete systems in MD applications.

Appendix A
Transfer operator for Langevin dynamics

In this section, we derive the expression of transfer operator for the
evolution of the positional variables of Langevin dynamics (see Example
2.1
in Section
2.1
).

The transition density
p^{\mathrm{Lan}}
of the Langevin dynamics (
17
) satisfies the Fokker-Planck equation

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{\mathrm{d}{}}{\mathrm{d}{t}}p^{\mathrm{Lan}}(t,y,v^{\prime}|x,v)=&-\mathrm{div}_{y}(v^{\prime}\,p^{\mathrm{Lan}})+\mathrm{div}_{v^{\prime}}(\nabla V(y)p^{\mathrm{Lan}})\\
&+\gamma\mathrm{div}_{v^{\prime}}(v^{\prime}\,p^{\mathrm{Lan}})+\frac{\gamma}{\beta}\Delta_{v^{\prime}}p^{\mathrm{Lan}},\quad t>0\\
p^{\mathrm{Lan}}(0,y,v^{\prime}|x,v)=&\delta(y-x)\delta(v^{\prime}-v)\,,\end{split}

(85)

where
t\in[0,+\infty)
,
x,y,v,v^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
\mathrm{div}_{y}
,

\mathrm{div}_{v^{\prime}}
,
\Delta_{v^{\prime}}
denote the divergence with respect
to
y
, the divergence with respect to
v^{\prime}
, the Laplacian with respect to
v^{\prime}
, respectively. Under certain
conditions on
V
, the process is ergodic and the transition density
p^{\mathrm{Lan}}
converges to the invariant density

p^{\mathrm{Lan}}_{\infty}(x,v)=Z^{-1}(\frac{\beta}{2\pi})^{\frac{d}{2}}\mathrm{e}^{-\beta\big(V(x)+\frac{|v|^{2}}{2}\big)}
as
t\rightarrow+\infty
, where
Z=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathrm{e}^{-\beta V(x)}\,\mathrm{d}{x}
is a
normalizing constant
[
58
,
17
,
65
,
25
]
.

Suppose that we want to construct a Markov chain for the data
(X_{n})_{0\leq n\leq N}
, where
X_{n}=x(n\tau)
is the position of (
17
) at time
t=n\tau
and
\tau>0
is the lag-time.
Let
p(x,y)
be the transition density of the Markov chain estimated from the data when the length
N\rightarrow+\infty
.
Given
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, let us denote by
D_{1},D_{2}
small neighborhoods of
x
and
y
, respectively.
We can derive

\displaystyle\int_{D_{2}}p(x,\bar{y})\,\mathrm{d}{\bar{y}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{|D_{1}|\rightarrow 0}\frac{1}{|D_{1}|}\int_{D_{2}}\int_{D_{1}}p(\bar{x},\bar{y})\,\mathrm{d}{\bar{x}}\,\mathrm{d}{\bar{y}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{|D_{1}|\rightarrow 0}\lim_{N\rightarrow+\infty}\frac{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\mathbbm{1}_{D_{1}}(X_{n})\mathbbm{1}_{D_{2}}(X_{n+1})}{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\mathbbm{1}_{D_{1}}(X_{n})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\big(\frac{\beta}{2\pi}\big)^{\frac{d}{2}}\lim_{|D_{1}|\rightarrow 0}\frac{\int_{D_{2}}\int_{D_{1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}p^{\mathrm{Lan}}(\tau,\bar{y},v^{\prime}|\bar{x},v)\,\mathrm{e}^{-\beta(V(\bar{x})+\frac{|v|^{2}}{2})}\,\mathrm{d}{v}\,\mathrm{d}{v^{\prime}}\,\mathrm{d}{\bar{x}}\,\mathrm{d}{\bar{y}}}{\int_{D_{1}}\mathrm{e}^{-\beta V(\bar{x})}\,\mathrm{d}{\bar{x}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\big(\frac{\beta}{2\pi}\big)^{\frac{d}{2}}\int_{D_{2}}\Big[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}p^{\mathrm{Lan}}(\tau,\bar{y},v^{\prime}|x,v)\,\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\beta|v|^{2}}{2}}\,\mathrm{d}{v}\,\mathrm{d}{v^{\prime}}\Big]\,\mathrm{d}{\bar{y}}\,,

where the first and the last equalities follow from the continuity of densities
p
and
p^{\mathrm{Lan}}
, respectively,
the second equality follows from the definition of
p
(i.e. the way in which
p
is estimated from data), the third equality follows from
Birkhoff’s ergodic theorem (see
[
31
, Theorem 4.4 in Chapter 1]
) and the ergodicity of the process. Since the above derivation is true for any neighborhood
D_{2}
of
y
, we conclude that

\displaystyle p(x,y)=\big(\frac{\beta}{2\pi}\big)^{\frac{d}{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}p^{\mathrm{Lan}}(\tau,y,v^{\prime}|x,v)\,\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\beta|v|^{2}}{2}}\,\mathrm{d}{v}\,\mathrm{d}{v^{\prime}}\,.

(86)

Next, we show that
p(x,y)
satisfies the detailed balance condition with respect to the density
\frac{1}{Z}\mathrm{e}^{-\beta V}
:

p(x,y)\,\mathrm{e}^{-\beta V(x)}=p(y,x)\,\mathrm{e}^{-\beta V(y)}\,,\quad\forall~x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,.

(87)

For this purpose, notice that the Langevin dynamics is reversible up to the
momentum (velocity) flip. In fact, we have, for
t>0
,

p^{\mathrm{Lan}}(t,y,v^{\prime}|x,v)\,p^{\mathrm{Lan}}_{\infty}(x,v)=p^{\mathrm{Lan}}(t,x,-v|y,-v^{\prime})\,p^{\mathrm{Lan}}_{\infty}(y,-v^{\prime})\,,\quad\forall x,y,v,v^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

(88)

which can be proven using (
85
) and the Kolmogorov backward
equation satisfied by

when considered as a function of
(t,x,v)
.
Applying (
86
) and (
88
), we can derive

\displaystyle p(x,y)\,\frac{1}{Z}\mathrm{e}^{-\beta V(x)}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{Z}\big(\frac{\beta}{2\pi}\big)^{\frac{d}{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}p^{\mathrm{Lan}}(\tau,y,v^{\prime}|x,v)\,\mathrm{e}^{-\beta\big(V(x)+\frac{|v|^{2}}{2}\big)}\,\mathrm{d}{v}\,\mathrm{d}{v^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}p^{\mathrm{Lan}}(\tau,y,v^{\prime}|x,v)\,p^{\mathrm{Lan}}_{\infty}(x,v)\,\mathrm{d}{v}\,\mathrm{d}{v^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}p^{\mathrm{Lan}}(\tau,x,-v|y,-v^{\prime})\,p^{\mathrm{Lan}}_{\infty}(y,-v^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}{v}\,\mathrm{d}{v^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{Z}\big(\frac{\beta}{2\pi}\big)^{\frac{d}{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}p^{\mathrm{Lan}}(\tau,x,-v|y,-v^{\prime})\,\mathrm{e}^{-\beta\big(V(y)+\frac{|v^{\prime}|^{2}}{2}\big)}\,\mathrm{d}{v}\,\mathrm{d}{v^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle p(y,x)\,\frac{1}{Z}\mathrm{e}^{-\beta V(y)}\,,

which gives (
87
).
