The limit point in the Jante’s law process has an absolutely continuous distribution
Edward Crane, Stanislav Volkov

Abstract
We study a stochastic model of consensus formation, introduced in 2015 by Grinfeld, Volkov and Wade, who called it a multidimensional randomized Keynesian beauty contest . The model was generalized by Kennerberg and Volkov, who called their generalization the Jante’s law process . We consider a version of the model where the space of possible opinions is a convex body ℬ \mathscr{B} in ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} . N N individuals in a population each hold a (multidimensional) opinion in ℬ \mathscr{B} . Repeatedly, the individual whose opinion is furthest from the centre of mass of the N N current opinions chooses a new opinion, sampled uniformly at random from ℬ \mathscr{B} . Kennerberg and Volkov showed that the set of opinions that are not furthest from the centre of mass converges to a random limit point. We show that the distribution of the limit opinion is absolutely continuous, thus proving the conjecture made after Proposition 3.2 in Grinfeld et al.

Jante’s law process, consensus formation,
Keynesian beauty contest, rank-driven process, interacting particle system

1.3
Outline and results

In §
2

Our proof of Theorem

In §

1.4
Related models

In the paper
[
4
]
, the authors generalized the original model of
[
2
]
by allowing the common distribution of the new points

to be an arbitrary absolutely continuous distribution on

. They also allowed for more than one point to be replaced simultaneously. It was shown that if the distribution of

is certainly a subset of the support of

\zeta

\zeta_{t}
is uniform on
[0,1]\cup[2,3]
and a.s. all coordinates of

lie in
[0,1]
, then

for all

, and after passing to the subsequence of times at which
\zeta_{t}\in[0,1]
, we recover the original process with
\zeta\sim U([0,1])
. In particular, in this case, the support of
\xi
is
[0,1]
. In case
\zeta
has unbounded support,
[
4
]
shows that a.s. either
X^{\prime}(t)
converges to a random limit point

or

, i.e.

. However, no examples of distributions of
\zeta_{t}
are known to satisfy

with positive probability. For an arbitrary common distribution of the random variables
\zeta_{t}
, and an arbitrary distribution of

on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, such that there is a positive probability that

converges, one can ask whether the distribution of the limit
\xi
conditional on its existence is absolutely continuous with respect to the distribution of the
\zeta_{t}

\zeta_{t}
has an absolutely continuous distribution with a sufficiently well-behaved density.

In another paper
[
5
]
, the authors studied asymptotic properties of a modified version of Jante’s law process. In this model, called the

-contest
, at each moment of time the point farthest from
p\mu
, where
\mu
is the current centre of mass, is removed and replaced by an independent

\zeta

would correspond to the original Jante’s law process. Finally, in
[
6

, a local version of the process was considered. In this version,
N

. At each unit of time, the vertex whose fitness deviates most from the average of the fitnesses of its two immediate neighbours has its fitness replaced by a random value drawn independently according to some distribution
\zeta

\zeta
has a uniform or a discrete uniform distribution, all fitnesses but at most one converge to the same limit. We would also like to mention that there is a related continuous-time model, called “Brownian bees”, which involves a fixed number
N
of particles which perform independent Brownian motions, and branch from time to time simultaneously with the removal of the particle most distant from the origin, thus keeping the total number of particles constant; see e.g.
[

]
and
[

. It would be in the spirit of the current paper to identify extrema in the Brownian bees from the centre of mass of the swarm, rather than from the origin; and have exponential killing of the extreme bee with introducing a new bee at a random location.

such that

\left\|z-\frac{z+\Sigma(X)}{M+1}\right\|<\left\|x_{j}-\frac{z+\Sigma(X)}{M+1}\right\|\,.

Straightforward algebraic manipulation shows that each of the following three inequalities is equivalent to (

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left\|Mz-\Sigma(X)\right\|&<\left\|(M+1)x_{j}-z-\Sigma(X)\right\|\\
\sum_{i:i\neq j}\|z-x_{i}\|^{2}&<\sum_{i:i\neq j}\|x_{j}-x_{i}\|^{2}\\
\left\|z-\frac{\Sigma(X)-x_{j}}{M-1}\right\|&<\left\|x_{j}-\frac{\Sigma(X)-x_{j}}{M-1}\right\|=\frac{M}{M-1}\|x_{j}-\mu(X)\|\,.\end{split}

(10)

The expression (

Lemma 3
.

Let
X=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{M}\}\subset\mathscr{B}
, where

. Then

\mathrm{Keep}(X;\mathscr{B})\subseteq\mathscr{B}\cap B\left(\mu(X),\frac{M+1}{M-1}A(X)\right)\,.

Proof.

For every

\left\|\mu(X)-\frac{\Sigma(X)-x_{j}}{M-1}\right\|=\frac{1}{M-1}\|x_{j}-\mu(X)\|\leq\frac{1}{M-1}A(X)\,.

(12)

Suppose

and let

hold. Then by (

we have

\left\|z-\frac{\Sigma(X)-x_{j}}{M-1}\right\|<\frac{M}{M-1}\|x_{j}-\mu(X)\|\leq\frac{M}{M-1}A(X)\,.

Summing the inequalities (

\|z-\mu(X)\|\leq\left\|z-\frac{\Sigma(X)-x_{j}}{M-1}\right\|+\left\|\mu(X)-\frac{\Sigma(X)-x_{j}}{M-1}\right\|<\frac{M+1}{M-1}\cdot A(X)\,,

Lemma 4
.

Let
X=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{M}\}\subset\mathscr{B}
, where
M\geq 2
and the

are distinct. Then

Proof.

Let

be a point of
X
furthest from

Lemma 9

From Lemma

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{y_{t}-r_{t}}{M}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(1+\frac{M+1}{M-1}\right)\frac{A(Y(t-1))}{M}=\frac{2}{M-1}A(Y(t-1))

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{2}{M\sqrt{M-1}}\sqrt{F(Y(t-1))}\,.

Since

3
Reduction to the case of uniform geometry

Following the terminology of
[
7
, Ch. 3]
, a convex body
\mathscr{B}
is said to have
uniform geometry
when there exists some
r_{0}>0
such that

b(r_{0}):=\inf_{x\in\mathscr{B}}\lambda(B(x,r_{0})\cap\mathscr{B})>0

(19)

(see
[
7
, Ch. 3]
.) Since the volume
\lambda(B(x,r_{0})\cap\mathscr{B})
depends continuously on
x
, when
\mathscr{B}
is compact the infimum is achieved and is positive because
\mathscr{B}
is the closure of its interior. That is, every bounded convex body has uniform geometry. Examples of unbounded convex bodies with bounded geometry are
\mathbb{R}^{d}
itself, and any convex body which is the intersection of finitely many closed half-spaces. However, not every unbounded convex body has uniform geometry, see for example
[
7
, Example 3.12]
.

Suppose
\mathscr{B}
is a convex body of uniform geometry, with
r_{0}
and
b(r_{0})>0
as in (
19
). Let
V(d)
denote the volume of the unit ball in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and define

c=c(\mathscr{B},r_{0}):=\frac{b(r_{0})}{V(d)r_{0}^{d}}\,.

(20)

Note that in the case
\mathscr{B}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
we have
c=1
, and in all other cases we have
c\leq 1/2
. For the case
\mathscr{B}=[0,1]^{d}
which was studied in
[
2
]
, we have
c=2^{-d}
. By Lemma
7
, using
(
19
) and (
20
),
for all
r\in(0,r_{0})
and all
x\in\mathscr{B}
we have

\frac{\lambda(\mathscr{B}\cap B(x,r))}{\lambda(B(x,r))}\geq c>0\,.

(21)

Recalling Lemma
3
and Lemma
4
, for
X=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{M}\}\subset\mathscr{B}
with
M\geq 2
we have

\mathscr{B}\cap B\left(\mu(X),A(X)\right)\subseteq\mathrm{Keep}(X;\mathscr{B})\subseteq\mathscr{B}\cap B\left(\mu(X),\frac{M+1}{M-1}A(X)\right).

(22)

It follows from (
21
) and (
22
) that when
A(X)\leq r_{0}
,

\frac{\lambda(\mathrm{Keep}(X;\mathscr{B}))}{\lambda\left(B\left(\mu(X),\frac{M+1}{M-1}A(X)\right)\right)}\,\geq\,\frac{\lambda(\mathscr{B}\cap B(\mu(X),A(X)))}{\left(\frac{M+1}{M-1}\right)^{d}\lambda(B(\mu(X),A(X)))}\,\geq\,\left(\frac{M-1}{M+1}\right)^{d}c\,>\,0\,.

This means that conditional on
Y(n)=X
, we may sample from the distribution of
y_{n+1}
by rejection sampling with success probability bounded away from
0
. That is, we repeatedly sample a point uniformly from
B\left(\mu(X),\frac{M+1}{M-1}A(X)\right)
until we obtain a sample that lies in
\mathrm{Keep}(X;\mathscr{B})
, and for each trial the probability of success is at least
\left(\frac{M-1}{M+1}\right)^{d}c
. This property will be used several times. If
\mathscr{B}
did not have uniform geometry then the success probability for this rejection sampling procedure would not be bounded away from
0
as
X
ranges over all sets with sufficiently small

. Fortunately, we may reduce Theorem
2
to the case where
\mathscr{B}
is bounded and therefore has uniform geometry, as follows.

Suppose the conclusion of Theorem

Lemma 12

For every set

M

, there exists a finite

We will identify a trajectory meeting the conditions of the lemma through a combination of probabilistic and constructive reasoning. Note that the process
Z(\cdot)

Z(0)=X
, a.s. obeys

, implying that
D(Z_{n})\to 0
. Let
\tau_{A}
be the least
n

. Then
\tau_{A}<\infty
a.s., and a.s. the points

are distinct and not in
X
. Therefore there exists some

and a trajectory
Z_{0}^{*},\dots,Z_{m}^{*}
, with
Z_{i}^{*}\setminus Z_{i-1}^{*}=\{z_{i}^{*}\}
and
Z_{i-1}^{*}\setminus Z_{i}^{*}=\{s_{i}^{*}\}
for

The end of the construction falls into two cases, depending on whether

from the point

\mu_{0}:=\frac{M\mu(Z_{m}^{*})+x_{\smalldiamond}}{M+1}.

First, suppose we are in case 1, where

from the point

. If we had

. Therefore
x_{\smalldiamond}\neq\mu(Z_{m}^{*})

is not in

is the unique furthest point of

\mu_{\epsilon}:=\frac{(M+\epsilon)\mu(Z_{m}^{*})+(1-\epsilon)x_{\smalldiamond}}{M+1}\,.

\|z_{m+1}^{*}-\mu_{\epsilon}\|=\frac{M}{M+1}(1-\epsilon)\|x_{\smalldiamond}-\mu(Z_{m}^{*})\|>0\,,

\|x_{\smalldiamond}-\mu_{\epsilon}\|=\frac{M+\epsilon}{M+1}\|x_{\smalldiamond}-\mu(Z_{m}^{*})\|\,.

\|x_{\smalldiamond}-\mu_{\epsilon}\|>\|z_{m+1}^{*}-\mu_{\epsilon}\|>0

. Hence

. Hence the point

is the unique point which may be removed, and we obtain

. The trajectory

is a permissible trajectory with no tiebreaks and no repeated points, and

, as required.

Case 2 is the case where
x_{\smalldiamond}
is not a furthest point in
Z_{m}^{*}
from the point
\mu_{0}
. For the rest of the proof, we assume that we are in this case. We claim that if

, where

Appendix 1: Absolute continuity of probability distributions

Here are the basic facts which we use about absolutely continuous
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued random variables.

1.

Definitions.
Let
\mathscr{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be the Borel
\sigma
-algebra of Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Let
W:M\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a Borel-measurable random variable on a probability space
(M,\mathfrak{S},\mathbb{P})
, meaning that for every
A\in\mathscr{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
W^{-1}(A)\in\mathfrak{S}
. The distribution of
W
is the unique Borel probability measure
\nu
on
\mathscr{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that for every
A\in\mathscr{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
we have
\nu(A)=\mathbb{P}(W\in A)
. (It suffices to check that this holds for every open set
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, or for every closed set.) We call both
W
and
\nu

absolutely continuous
when
\nu
is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure
\lambda
. This means that for every Borel set
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

(\lambda(A)=0)\implies(\nu(A)=\mathbb{P}(W\in A)=0)\,.

Equivalently, there exists an element
f\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\lambda)
of norm
1
such that for every Borel set
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
we have

\mathbb{P}(W\in A)=\nu(A)=\int_{A}f(x)\,d\lambda(x).

This element
f
is called the density of
\nu
, or the density of
W
. Every element of
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\lambda)
has a Borel-measurable representative, so we may assume that
f
is Borel-measurable. The density of an absolutely continuous random variable is not necessarily representable by a continuous function, and if
M
happens to be equipped with a topology which generates
\mathfrak{S}
, the absolute continuity of
W
as a random variable is unrelated to the continuity of
W
as a function.

2.

Mixtures of absolutely continuous random variables are absolutely continuous.
Suppose that
\pi
is a Borel probability measure on
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
\nu
is a random absolutely continuous probability measure defined on
(M,\mathfrak{S},\mathbb{P})
whose density
d\nu/d\lambda
with respect to Lebesgue measure
\lambda
is distributed according to
\pi
. Then for any Lebesgue measurable set
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
we have (by Tonelli’s theorem)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{\pi}(\nu)(A):=\int\nu(A)\,d\pi(\nu)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\int\frac{d\nu}{d\lambda}(x)\mathbf{1}_{A}(x)\,d\lambda(x)\,d\pi(\nu)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\int\frac{d\nu}{d\lambda}(x)\,d\pi(\nu)\mathbf{1}_{A}(x)\,d\lambda(x)\,,

so the mixture
\mathbb{E}^{\pi}(\nu)
is an absolutely continuous probability measure with density
\int\frac{d\nu}{d\lambda}\,d\pi(\nu)
. To see that the integral of this density with respect to
\lambda
is
1
, take
A=\mathbb{R}^{d}
above. Even if
\nu
a.s. has a
C^{\infty}
density, it need not be true that
\mathbb{E}^{\pi}(\nu)
has a continuous density.

3.

Conditioning on positive probability events.
Let
\nu
be a Borel probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
W
an
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued random variable on
(M,\mathfrak{S},\mathbb{P})
with distribution
\nu
. If
E\in\mathfrak{S}
is an event such that
\mathbb{P}(E)>0
, then the conditional distribution of
W
given
E
is also absolutely continuous.
Indeed, let
\nu_{E}(A)={\mathbb{P}}(W\in A\mid E)
. Then

\nu_{E}(A)=\frac{{\mathbb{P}}(E\cap\{W\in A\})}{{\mathbb{P}}(E)}\leq\frac{\nu(A)}{{\mathbb{P}}(E)}

so that
\nu(A)=0
implies
\nu_{E}(A)=0
, so that
\nu_{E}\ll\nu\ll\lambda
where
\lambda
is the Lebesgue measure.

Finally, we prove (

Appendix 3: Beyond convex bodies

For a bounded measurable set
\mathscr{B}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we will explain below how some reasonable geometric assumptions on
\mathscr{B}
, which are more general than convexity, are sufficient to define the
\mathscr{B}
-valued Jante process (so that it is almost surely well-defined for all times) and to show that it converges almost surely to a random limit. However, it appears to be more difficult to show that the limit point has an absolutely continuous distribution, even in the nicest possible non-convex setting, where
\mathscr{B}
is a region bounded by a compact smooth hypersurface in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In this case, the boundary of
\mathscr{B}
is Lebesgue null, so to prove absolute continuity of the distribution of the limit point one must prove in particular that the limit point almost surely lies in the interior of
\mathscr{B}
. In Lemma
22
below, which applies to a reasonable class of non-convex bodies, we show that the
only obstacle
is proving that the limit point almost surely lies in the interior of
\mathscr{B}
.

First, the notion of
\mathscr{B}
-valued Jante process may be extended to any subset
\mathscr{B}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfying the following condition:

Condition A
:
\mathscr{B}
is Lebesgue measurable and non-empty, and
\overline{\mathscr{B}}=\mathrm{support}(\lambda|_{\mathscr{B}})
, where
\lambda
denotes Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. (In particular this implies that
\lambda(\mathscr{B})>0
.)

Condition
A
holds for a non-empty Lebesgue measurable subset
\mathscr{B}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
if and only if for every
x\in\mathscr{B}
and
\epsilon>0
we have
\lambda(B(x,\epsilon)\cap\mathscr{B})>0
. For example, condition
A
holds whenever is
\mathscr{B}
is the closure of its interior.

Lemma 21
.

Suppose
\mathscr{B}
satisfies condition A and
X=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{M}\}
is a set of
M
distinct points of
\mathscr{B}
. Then the Lebesgue measure of
\mathrm{Keep}(X;\mathscr{B})
is positive.

Proof.

Let
x_{j}
be a point of
X
such that
d(x_{j},\mu(X))=A(X)
, i.e.
x_{j}
is a point of
X
that is as far as possible from
\mu(X)
. Let
x_{i}
(
i\neq j
) be another point of
X
. Since
x_{i}
lies in the closure of the ball
B(\mu(X),A(X))
, which is internally tangent to the ball
B\left(\frac{\Sigma(X)-x_{j}}{M-1},\frac{M}{M-1}A(X)\right)
at
x_{j}
, but
x_{i}\neq x_{j}
, we see that
x_{i}
lies in the (open) ball
B\left(\frac{\Sigma(X)-x_{j}}{M-1},\frac{M}{M-1}A(X)\right)
and hence there is an
\epsilon>0
such that

B(x_{i},\epsilon)\subset B\left(\frac{\Sigma(X)-x_{j}}{M-1},\frac{M}{M-1}A(X)\right)\,,

and hence by Lemma 2 we have
B(x_{i},\epsilon)\cap\mathscr{B}\subset\mathrm{Keep}(X;\mathscr{B})
. Condition A implies that
B(x_{i},\epsilon)\cap\mathscr{B}
has positive Lebesgue measure, so
\mathrm{Keep}(X;\mathscr{B})
has positive Lebesgue measure, as required.
∎

Under condition
A
, the
\mathscr{B}
-valued Jante process is well-defined starting at any set
Y(0)
of
M\geq 2
distinct points of
\mathscr{B}
. Indeed, when
Y(n)
is any set of
M\geq 2
distinct points in
\mathscr{B}
, it makes sense to sample a point uniformly from
\mathrm{Keep}(Y(n);\mathscr{B})
in order to define
Y(n+1)
, because this set has positive but finite Lebesgue measure. Moreover, almost surely
Y(n+1)
will also consist of
M
distinct points.

To ensure that the
\mathscr{B}
-valued Jante process almost surely converges to a limit point, it is helpful to assume a stronger condition.

Definition 4
.

Let
\zeta
be an
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued random variable. Any subset
A\subset\mathrm{support}(\zeta)
is called
regular
with parameters
\delta_{A}\in(0,1),\sigma_{A},r_{A}>0
if for every
x\in A
and
0<r\leq r_{A}
we have

\mathbb{P}(\zeta\in B(x,r\delta_{A})\,|\,\zeta\in B(x,r))\geq\sigma_{A}\,.

\zeta
is called
regular
if for every
x\in\mathrm{support}(\zeta)
, there exists a
\gamma_{x}>0
such that the subset
B_{\gamma}(x)\cap\mathrm{support}(\zeta)
is regular (with any parameters).

This regularity condition is Assumption 2 in Kennerberg and Volkov
[
4
]
. Specializing to the case at hand, we introduce

Condition B:

\mathscr{B}
is a bounded measurable subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
\lambda(B)>0
and such that the random variable
\zeta
whose distribution is
\frac{1}{\lambda(\mathscr{B})}\lambda|_{\mathscr{B}}
is regular.

We remark that condition B is closely related to the uniform geometry condition that we defined for the case of convex sets. Under conditions A and B, the
\mathscr{B}
-valued Jante process is a special case of the process studied by Kennerberg and Volkov, and
[
4
, Theorem 2]
implies that the
\mathscr{B}
-valued Jante process almost surely has a (random) limit
y_{\infty}
in the closure of
\mathscr{B}
.

To make our coupling approach to generalize to a non-convex set
\mathscr{B}
, in addition to conditions A and B, we need two more conditions:

Condition C:

\mathscr{B}
is the closure of its interior.

Condition D:
The limit point
y_{\infty}
of the
\mathscr{B}
-valued Jante process almost surely lies in the interior of
\mathscr{B}
.

Unfortunately, we do not know whether
in general
conditions A, B and C imply condition D. In some special circumstances other than the convex case, it might be possible to prove a substitute for Lemma
16
and hence also a substitute for Corollary
4
. For example, if
B
is bounded by a compact smooth hypersurface, then conditions A, B and C hold, and one might also hope for a substitute for Lemma
16
to hold with uniform constants because the principal curvatures of the bounding hypersurface would be bounded.

Lemma 22
.

Suppose
\mathscr{B}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfies conditions A, B, and C. Consider the
\mathscr{B}
-valued Jante process with initial state
Y(0)\subset\mathscr{B}
consisting of
M\geq 2
distinct points of
\mathscr{B}
. Then if condition D also holds, the distribution of
y_{\infty}
is absolutely continuous.

Proof.

Assume conditions A, B, C, and D hold. Take a countable cover of the interior of
\mathscr{B}
by distinct open balls,

\mathscr{C}=\{U_{i}:i\in\mathbb{N}\}\,,

where each
U_{i}\subseteq\mathscr{B}
. Then define a random element
U_{\infty}
of
\mathscr{C}
by letting
U_{\infty}=U_{i}
where
i\in\mathbb{N}
is minimal such that
y_{\infty}\in U_{i}
. By the assumptions, almost surely at least one such index
i
exists.

For any
U\in\mathscr{C}
and
T\geq 0
, let
K(U,T)
be the event that for all
t\geq T
,
\mathrm{Keep}(Y(t);\mathscr{B})\subset U
. Let
T_{0}
be the earliest time
T
such that
K(U_{\infty},T)
holds. (Note that
T_{0}
is not a stopping time, but it is almost surely finite.) Then the distribution of
y_{\infty}
is a mixture of the conditional distributions of
y_{\infty}

conditioned on
U_{\infty}=U
and
T_{0}=T
, as
(U,T)
ranges over the countable set

\{(U,T)\in\mathscr{C}\times\mathbb{N}_{0}\,:\,\mathbb{P}(U_{\infty}=U,T_{0}=T)>0\}\,.

It therefore suffices to prove that each of these conditional distributions is absolutely continuous. So suppose that that
U\in\mathscr{C}
and
T\geq 0
satisfy
\mathbb{P}(U_{\infty}=U,T_{0}=T)>0
. The conditional distribution of
y_{\infty}
given
U_{\infty}=U
and
T_{0}=T
is obtained from the conditional distribution of
y_{\infty}
given only
K(U,T)
by further conditioning on the
positive probability
event that
y_{\infty}
is not contained in any
U^{\prime}\in\mathscr{C}
which precedes
U
in the enumeration. So it suffices to prove that the conditional distribution of
y_{\infty}
given
K(U,T)
is absolutely continuous.

The conditional distribution of
y_{\infty}
given
K(U,T)
is a mixture of the distributions of
y_{\infty}
given
Y(T)
and
K(U,T)
, where the mixture is with respect to the distribution of
Y(T)
conditioned on the positive probability event
K(U,T)
. So it suffices to prove the absolute continuity of the conditional distribution of
y_{\infty}
given
Y(T)=X
and
K(U,T)
, for every configuration
X
such that
\mathbb{P}(K(U,T)\,|\,Y(T)=X)>0
.

Using a coupling argument, we will show that this distribution is absolutely continuous with respect to the distribution of the limit point of
U
-valued Jante process started at
X
, which is absolutely continuous by our Theorem
2
. Since the Jante process is homogeneous, we may replace
T
with
0
without loss of generality in the rest of the proof.

Let
Y^{\prime}
be a
U
-valued Jante process started in configuration
X\subset U
, and let
Y
be a
\mathscr{B}
-valued Jante process started in configuration
X
. Define the events

K(U):=\{(\forall t\geq 0)(\mathrm{Keep}(Y(t);\mathscr{B})\subset U)\}

and

K^{\prime}(U):=\{(\forall t\geq 0)(\mathrm{Keep}(Y^{\prime}(t);\mathscr{B})\subset U)\}\,.

We claim that
\mathbb{P}(K^{\prime}(U))=\mathbb{P}(K(U))
. Indeed, there is a coupling of
\left(Y(t)\right)_{t\geq 0}
and
\left(Y^{\prime}(t)\right)_{t\geq 0}
such that
Y(t)=Y^{\prime}(t)
for all times
t
up to and including the first time when
\mathrm{Keep}(Y(t);\mathscr{B})\not\subset U
, or for all
t
if there is no such time. (After such a time, we may let
Y^{\prime}
and
Y
evolve independently.) Let
y^{\prime}_{\infty}
be the limit point of
Y^{\prime}(\cdot)
, which exists almost surely. Let
\mu_{U,X}
be the (defective) positive Borel measure defined by

\displaystyle\mu_{U,X}(\cdot)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(y_{\infty}\in\cdot\text{ and }K(U))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(y^{\prime}_{\infty}\in\cdot\text{ and }K^{\prime}(U))\,.

(The equality here is a consequence of the coupling.)
Since
\mathbb{P}(K(U))=\mathbb{P}(K^{\prime}(U))
, we deduce that the conditional distribution of
y_{\infty}
given
K(U)
is equal to the conditional distribution of
y^{\prime}_{\infty}
given
K^{\prime}(U)
. (Indeed, both are obtained by normalizing
\mu_{U,X}
.)
Finally, note that
\mu_{U,X}
is absolutely continuous with respect to the unconditional distribution of

, with the Radon-Nikodym derivative at

being
\mathbb{P}(K^{\prime}(U)\,|\,y^{\prime}_{\infty}=y)
. Finally, the unconditional distribution of

is absolutely continuous, by Theorem
2

Putting this all together we conclude that
y_{\infty}
has an absolutely continuous distribution.
∎
