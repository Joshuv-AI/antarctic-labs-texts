Multivariate Symmetry: Distribution-Free Testing via Optimal Transport
Zhen Huang Department of Statistics, Columbia University e-mail: zh2395@columbia.edu and Bodhisattva Sen Department of Statistics, Columbia University e-mail: bodhi@stat.columbia.edu

Abstract
The sign test (Arbuthnott, 1710) and the Wilcoxon signed-rank test (Wilcoxon, 1945) are among the first examples of a nonparametric test. These procedures — based on signs, (absolute) ranks and signed-ranks — yield distribution-free tests for symmetry in one-dimension. In this paper we propose a novel and unified framework for distribution-free testing of multivariate symmetry (that includes central symmetry, sign symmetry, spherical symmetry, etc.) based on the theory of optimal transport. Our approach leads to notions of distribution-free generalized multivariate signs, ranks and signed-ranks. As a consequence, we develop analogues of the sign and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests that share many of the appealing properties of their one-dimensional counterparts. In particular, the proposed tests are exactly distribution-free in finite samples with an asymptotic normal limit, and adapt to various notions of multivariate symmetry. We study the consistency of the proposed tests and their behavior under local alternatives, and show that the proposed generalized Wilcoxon signed-rank (GWSR) test is particularly powerful against location shift alternatives. We show that in a large class of such models, our GWSR test suffers from no loss in (asymptotic) efficiency, when compared to Hotelling’s T 2 T^{2} test, despite being nonparametric and exactly distribution-free. An appropriately score transformed version of the GWSR statistic leads to a locally asymptotically optimal test. Further, our method can be readily used to construct distribution-free confidence sets for the center of symmetry.

Introduction

Symmetry is everywhere, from the bilateral symmetry in Greek sculpture, to the description of crystalline structure in nature by modern group theory
[
103
,
93
]
. The study of symmetry in statistics also has an old and rich history. Perhaps the first published report of a nonparametric test goes back to the landmark paper
[
4
]
by John Arbuthnott where he essentially performed a statistical hypothesis test for univariate
symmetry
, computing a
p
-value via the
sign test
; see e.g.,
[
7
,
41
]
. Some statisticians also date the beginning of (classical) nonparametric statistics, in particular rank-based methods, to the appearance of Wilcoxon’s 1945 paper
[
104
]
, which introduced the
Wilcoxon signed-rank test
(WSR) for testing univariate symmetry. Both these classical tests are
distribution-free
and have had an enormous impact in the development of nonparametric statistics.

A univariate random variable
X
is
symmetric
(around 0) if
X
has the same distribution as
-X
.
Given
n
observations
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
from the distribution of
X
,
the sign test rejects the null hypothesis
{\rm H}_{0}:X\overset{d}{=}-X
when
\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\rm sign}(X_{i})\right|
is large; here
{\rm sign}(X_{i})=1
if
X_{i}\geq 0
and is
-1
otherwise.
In contrast, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test first sorts
|X_{1}|,\ldots,|X_{n}|
to obtain their (absolute) ranks
\{{\rm rank}(|X_{i}|)\}_{i=1}^{n}
, and rejects
{\rm H}_{0}
for large values of
\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\rm sign}(X_{i}){\rm rank}(|X_{i}|)\right|
.
When
X
has a continuous distribution, the classical sign and signed-rank tests enjoy several properties that make them powerful and useful in practice, including: (i)
distribution-freeness
, (ii)
independence
of signs and absolute ranks (under
{\rm H}_{0}
), (iii) simple asymptotic normal limiting distributions, and (iv) high (relative) efficiency compared to the
t
-test
[
57
,
22
]
; see
[
99
, Chapter 15]
for a detailed discussion of these results.

It is natural to ask if generalizations of these tests, that satisfy the above mentioned desirable properties, exist in multi-dimensions. In this paper, we answer this question in the affirmative by proposing a novel framework for testing
multivariate symmetry
, using the theory of optimal transport
[
100
,
101
]
.

While symmetry in one dimension is unambiguous (i.e.,
X\overset{d}{=}-X
), in the multivariate setting there are various notions of symmetry. The most prominent ones include: (a)
central symmetry

[
91
,
13
,
80
,
83
,
32
]
, (b)
sign symmetry

[
31
,
84
,
83
]
, and (c)
spherical symmetry

[
6
,
39
,
52
,
2
]
; a thorough review of the topic can be found in
Serfling, 2014
.

In this paper, we consider the following general setting for testing multivariate symmetry. Let
\mathcal{G}
be a compact subgroup of the orthogonal group
{\rm O}(p)
(for
p\geq 1
) — the group of all
p\times p
real orthogonal matrices, where the group operation is the usual matrix multiplication.
For a random vector

{\bf X}\sim\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{\rm ac}(\mathbb{R}^{p})
, where
\mathcal{P}_{\rm ac}(\mathbb{R}^{p})
denotes the class of all Borel probability measures on
\mathbb{R}^{p}
with a Lebesgue density, we are interested in testing the hypothesis of
\mathcal{G}
-symmetry
:

(1.1)

\textrm{H}_{0}:{\bf X}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}Q{\bf X}{\rm\ \ for\ all\ \ }Q\in\mathcal{G}\qquad\qquad\textrm{versus}\qquad\qquad\textrm{H}_{1}:\textrm{not H}_{0}.

In particular, the prominent examples of symmetry mentioned earlier correspond to the following groups
\mathcal{G}
.

(a)

Central symmetry
: Here
\mathcal{G}=\{I,-I\}
, where
I\equiv I_{p}
is the identity matrix of order
p
.

Central symmetry (or “reflective” or “diagonal” or “simple” or “antipodal” symmetry
[
91
,
13
,
80
,
83
,
32
]
) arises naturally from paired data
(see e.g.,
[
40
, Section 5]
):
Given a sample of
n
pairs
\{(\mathbf{Y}_{i},\mathbf{Z}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
,
it is often of interest to study the differences

\mathbf{X}_{i}:=\mathbf{Y}_{i}-\mathbf{Z}_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,n
.
Under the null hypothesis that
\mathbf{Y}_{i}
and
\mathbf{Z}_{i}
are exchangeable,
\mathbf{X}_{i}
will have a centrally symmetric distribution
[
67
,
40
]
.
Extensions of central symmetry include
angular symmetry

[
86
]
,
which corresponds to
\frac{{\bf X}}{\|{\bf X}\|}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}-\frac{{\bf X}}{\|{\bf X}\|}
; here
\|\cdot\|
denotes the usual Euclidean norm.

(b)

Sign symmetry
: Here
\mathcal{G}=\{Q\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}:Q{\rm\ is\ a\ diagonal\ orthogonal\ matrix}\}
; thus each diagonal entry of
Q
is
\pm 1
.

Sign symmetry is also called
orthant symmetry
or
marginal symmetry

[
31
,
84
,
83
]
. Such sign-change invariance is often assumed in the
location-scatter
model, which provides tools for robust estimation of the regular mean vector and covariance matrix
[
83
]
; also see the book by Puri and Sen
[
85
]
which gives a comprehensive account of multivariate analysis methods based on marginal signs and ranks.

(c)

Spherical symmetry
: Here
\mathcal{G}={\rm O}(p)
.

Spherically symmetric distributions are natural generalizations of the Gaussian distribution, and have drawn substantial interest
[
33
,
2
]
.
It is also known that many estimation results are robust when departures from the normality assumption are in the direction of the spherically symmetric family, with applications in the general linear model
[
62
,
10
,
36
]
, sufficient dimension reduction
[
69
,
105
]
, etc.
Tests for spherical symmetry have been considered in
[
6
,
39
,
52
,
2
]
.

Clearly, other subgroups of
{\rm O}(p)
, such as the
signed permutation group

The signed permutation group consists of all matrices having exactly one nonzero entry in each row and exactly one nonzero entry in each column, and the nonzero entries are either 1 or
-1
.

[
35
]
, also correspond to a notion of symmetry.
The
\mathcal{G}
-symmetric model described by
{\rm H}_{0}
in (
1.1
) is motivated by the work of
Beran and Millar, 1997
, and it provides a general framework for testing many notions of multivariate symmetries in a unified fashion.

To develop distribution-free tests for the general hypothesis of
\mathcal{G}
-symmetry in (
1.1
) we first observe (in
Section

2
) that the one-dimensional signs, ranks and signed-ranks — which are the building blocks for distribution-free inference in the univariate setting — can be obtained as solutions to an optimization problem which can be generalized to multi-dimensions. This resulting optimization is an optimal transport (OT) problem
[
100
,
101
]
with a specific cost function that can be solved efficiently (see
Section

2.3
).

Specifically, given observations
\mathbf{X}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{X}_{n}
from
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{\rm ac}(\mathbb{R}^{p})
and (known) candidate rank vectors
\mathbf{h}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{h}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, the
generalized sign

S_{n}(\mathbf{X}_{i})
is defined as an element of
\mathcal{G}
and the collection of
generalized ranks

(R_{n}(\mathbf{X}_{1}),\ldots,R_{n}(\mathbf{X}_{n}))
is a permutation of
(\mathbf{h}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{h}_{n})
; see
Section

2.2
for the formal definitions. Our approach leads to notions of (
\mathcal{G}
-symmetric specific) generalized multivariate signs, ranks and signed-ranks that are also distribution-free (under
{\rm H}_{0}
). In the one-dimensional case, if
(\mathbf{h}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{h}_{n})
is chosen as
(\frac{1}{n},\ldots,\frac{n}{n})
, then we recover the usual notion of signs and ranks. Further, mirroring the one-dimensional setting, we define the
generalized sign
test statistic as
\sum_{i=1}^{n}S_{n}(\mathbf{X}_{i})
, and the statistic for the
generalized Wilcoxon signed-rank
(GWSR) test will be defined as

\sum_{i=1}^{n}S_{n}(\mathbf{X}_{i})R_{n}(\mathbf{X}_{i})
; see
Section

3
for the details.

Over the past few years, multivariate ranks
[
43
,
27
]
, defined via the theory of OT, have been effectively used to design distribution-free testing procedures in two-sample problems
[
15
,
27
]
, for independence testing
[
27
,
95
,
96
,
94
]
, in multivariate linear models
[
44
,
45
]
, for directional data
[
46
]
, etc. Our framework, although inspired by these recent developments, is novel and more general and includes the usual notion of OT based multivariate ranks
[
43
,
27
]
as a special case. We achieve this by considering a more general cost function in the OT problem that naturally arises from the hypothesis testing problem (
1.1
) and reduces to the usual squared Euclidean distance cost when
\mathcal{G}
is the trivial group
\{I\}
. Further, in Section
2.4
, using the theory of OT, we provide characterizations of the population analogues of the empirical generalized signs, ranks and signed-ranks (see
Theorem

2.1
). Moreover, we show the consistency of these sample estimates to their population analogues in
Theorem

2.2
. Our treatment does not need any moment assumptions on the data distribution
\mu
as we appeal to geometric characterizations of OT (as pioneered by Robert McCann
[
73
]
).

Among the important reasons for the popularity of the classical Wilcoxon signed-rank test are
the striking results of
Hodges and Lehmann, 1956
and
Chernoff and Savage, 1958
, where the authors show that the asymptotic (Pitman) relative efficiency of Wilcoxon’s test with respect
to (w.r.t.) the one-sample Student’s
t
-test, under location-shift alternatives, never falls below 0.864 (with the identity score function) and 1 (with the Gaussian score function) respectively, despite the former being exactly distribution-free for all sample sizes. Motivated by these results, we study in
Section

3.4
the (asymptotic relative) efficiency, in the sense of Pitman
[see e.g.,
99
, Section 14.3]
, of our proposed multivariate generalization of the Wilcoxon signed-rank test compared to Hotelling’s
T^{2}
test — the multivariate generalization of the usual
t
-test. In particular, we show that the proposed GWSR test satisfies Hodges-Lehmann and Chernoff-Savage-type efficiency lower bounds over natural sub-families of multivariate distributions, despite being entirely agnostic to the underlying data generating mechanism; see Theorems
3.5
-
3.7
.

Let us now briefly highlight the main results in this paper.

1.

Distribution-freeness: under
{\rm H}_{0}
,

(a)

S_{n}(\mathbf{X}_{1}),\ldots,S_{n}(\mathbf{X}_{n})
are i.i.d. following the uniform distribution over
\mathcal{G}
.

(b)

The ranks
(R_{n}(\mathbf{X}_{1}),\ldots,R_{n}(\mathbf{X}_{n}))
are uniformly distributed over all
n!
permutations of
(\mathbf{h}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{h}_{n})
; see
2.2
.

2.

Independence:
(S_{n}(\mathbf{X}_{1}),\ldots,S_{n}(\mathbf{X}_{n}))
is independent of
(R_{n}(\mathbf{X}_{1}),\ldots,R_{n}(\mathbf{X}_{n}))
under
\textrm{H}_{0}
(
2.2
).

3.

Asymptotic normality: Both
\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}S_{n}(\mathbf{X}_{i})
and
\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}S_{n}(\mathbf{X}_{i})R_{n}(\mathbf{X}_{i})
converge in distribution to multivariate normal distributions, under
{\rm H}_{0}
(
Theorem

3.1
).

4.

Relative efficiency against location shift alternatives for suitable sub-classes of multivariate distributions (e.g., product distributions, elliptically symmetric distributions, etc.):

(a)

(Hodges-Lehmann phenomenon
[
57
]
) The relative efficiency of the GWSR test w.r.t. Hotelling’s
T^{2}
test never falls below 0.864, if
\mathbf{h}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{h}_{n}
and
\mathcal{G}
determine a uniform
effective reference distribution
(see Theorem
3.5
for the precise statement).

(b)

(Chernoff-Savage phenomenon
[
22
]
) The relative efficiency of the GWSR test against Hotelling’s
T^{2}
test is lower bounded by 1, if
\mathbf{h}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{h}_{n}
and
\mathcal{G}
determine a Gaussian
effective reference distribution
(see Theorems
3.5
-
3.7
).

5.

Efficiency (against location shift alternatives): The family of score transformed GWSR tests (with statistic
\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}S_{n}(\mathbf{X}_{i})J(R_{n}(\mathbf{X}_{i}))
for some score function
J:\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathbb{R}^{p}
) contains a
locally asymptotically optimal test
(see
Theorem

3.9
), i.e., a test which has maximum (asymptotic) local power among all possible tests with a fixed Type I error. This is in parallel with the analogous result in univariate setting, cf.
Van der Vaart, 1998
.

We say that
\mathbf{X}
is
\mathcal{G}
-symmetric around
{\bm{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
(usually referred to as the
center of
\mathcal{G}
-symmetry
) if
(\mathbf{X}-{\bm{\theta}})\overset{d}{=}Q(\mathbf{X}-{\bm{\theta}})
, for all
Q\in\mathcal{G}
. Our framework can be immediately extended to test the hypothesis of
\mathcal{G}
-symmetry around any fixed and known
{\bm{\theta}}
. To test symmetry around a general center
{\bm{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, one may just translate the data
\{\mathbf{X}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
to
\{\mathbf{X}_{i}-{\bm{\theta}}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, thereby reducing the problem to (
1.1
). Further, if
{\bm{\theta}}
is unknown, our proposed testing framework can be used to construct
distribution-free confidence sets
for
{\bm{\theta}}
(see
Section

5
). Further, in Section
4
we demonstrate the superior performance of our proposed tests (especially for location shift models) via simulation studies, illustrate the implications of our efficiency results for data sets with small to moderate sample sizes, and compare our proposals with existing methods that test for different notions of symmetry.

From the above properties, we believe that our generalized multivariate signs and ranks (defined via OT) are natural extensions of the classical concepts. As far as we are aware, our work is the first to propose notions of multivariate distribution-free signs and ranks (relative to any group
\mathcal{G}
) and develop distribution-free testing for (
1.1
) in multi-dimensions with the above efficiency lower bounds, analogous to the classical Wilcoxon’s signed-rank test.

1.1
Literature Review

Multivariate symmetry is an important concept in nonparametric statistics
[
106
]
. Testing for multivariate symmetry has been studied extensively in the literature, see, e.g.,
Baringhaus, 1991
,
Diks and Tong, 1999
and
Serfling, 2014
.

Extensions of sign in multi-dimension.

One of the earliest and most popular tests for symmetry is possibly the sign test
[
4
]
.
Since then, several notions of multivariate signs have been proposed in the literature. The component-wise multivariate sign, which is a popular generalization,
dates back to
Bennett, 1962
,
Chatterjee, 1966
. The asymptotic normality and Pitman efficiency of the component-wise sign test against Hotelling’s
T^{2}
was derived by
Bickel, 1965
.
Component-wise ranks and signed-ranks have also been used for testing
[
85
,
59
]
.
One drawback of the component-wise sign is that its distribution-freeness requires
\mathbf{X}
to be sign symmetric (it is not distribution-free under central symmetry).
We will show that our generalized sign (see (
2.4
)) reduces to the component-wise sign when
\mathcal{G}
is the group corresponding to sign symmetry (see
Remark

2.4
).

A multivariate generalization of the sign test based on
interdirections
was proposed in
Randles, 1989
, generalizing the two-sided univariate sign test and Blumen’s bivariate sign test
[
14
]
. This test statistic is invariant under nonsingular linear transformations and it has a finite-sample distribution-free property over distributions with elliptical directions
[
86
]
.

Chaudhuri and Sengupta, 1993
expressed Randles’s statistic in terms of a data-dependent sign, and further unified a number of previous affine invariant generalized sign tests
[
56
,
102
,
1
]
.
Such transformation re-transformation technique was later investigated to define affine equivariant multivariate median and quantiles

[
18
,
19
,
17
,
92
]
, and affine invariant multivariate sign test
[
87
]
.
Locally asymptotically maximin tests based on interdirections were derived in
Hallin and Paindaveine, 2002
under elliptical symmetry.

Möttönen and Oja, 1995
defined the multivariate sign as the unit vector in the direction of a given point, which is called the
spatial sign
. The Pitman efficiencies of the corresponding tests and a generalization of the Hodges-Lehmann estimator were further analyzed in
Möttönen et al., 1997
,
Möttönen et al., 2005
. However, none of the above notions of signs are distribution-free beyond spherical symmetry.

Hallin et al., 2021
proposed a novel center-outward definition of ranks and signs based on measure transportation with attractive properties, similar to our framework with
\mathcal{G}=\{I\}
. However, their center-outward signs differ from the classical signs and our proposal in multiple ways; e.g., the center-outward signs are not i.i.d. uniform over
\{\pm 1\}
for symmetric distributions in one dimension.

Tests for central symmetry.

Going beyond the sign test, there are tests for central symmetry which are consistent against all alternatives.
In the univariate setting,
McWilliams, 1990
presented a simple test based on a runs statistic.
Dyckerhoff et al., 2015
extended McWilliams’s procedure to test for bivariate central symmetry using the notion of data depth. In
Einmahl and Gan, 2016
, omnibus tests for central symmetry of a bivariate probability distribution are proposed that are asymptotically distribution-free. Another class of approaches explores the properties of the characteristic function of a centrally symmetric distribution and uses the
empirical characteristic function to construct test statistics, including both in the univariate setting
[
24
,
34
]
and in multi-dimensional scenarios
[
39
,
51
,
53
,
80
,
54
]
.
Affine invariant tests for central symmetry are proposed in
Henze et al., 2003
using kernel density estimators; also see
[
54
]
.
However, these tests are in general not asymptotically distribution-free, and the limiting distributions need to be estimated from the data for inference
[
80
,
54
]
.

Tests for spherical symmetry.

A direct extension of McWilliams’s procedure for testing spherical symmetry in multi-dimension is given in
Marden, 1999
, which has a simple asymptotic
\chi^{2}_{1}
distribution under the null. In
Baringhaus, 1991
, rotationally invariant tests based on test statistics of the von Mises type are proposed, which give rise to tests that are consistent against all alternatives.
The characteristic function has also been used for testing
spherical symmetry in multi-dimensional scenarios
[
52
,
39
]
.
Kariya and Eaton, 1977
consider two classes of alternatives to test spherical symmetry, and provide uniformly most powerful tests. A new testing procedure based on the fact that
\mathbf{X}
is spherically symmetric if and only if
\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbf{X}|\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{X}]=0
for any two perpendicular vectors
\mathbf{u}
and
\mathbf{v}
is given in
Albisetti et al., 2020
, with its rejection region being found via bootstrap.

Tests for sign symmetry.

The analysis of the classical Hotelling’s
T^{2}
test has also been extended to sign symmetric distributions by

Efron, 1969
,
Pinelis, 1994
, etc.
In
Oja, 2010
, a book-length treatment on tests based on spatial signs and ranks is given;
sign-change versions of the spatial sign test and spatial signed-rank test are proposed when the observations are sign symmetric.
These tests also have good efficiency properties with respect to Hotelling’s
T^{2}
in the multivariate
t
-distribution settings
[
83
, Chapter 8]
. However, Hodges-Lehmann
[
57
]
and Chernoff-Savage
[
22
]
type lower bounds are not available for these methods.

2
Multivariate Generalized Signs, Ranks and Signed-ranks

2.1
Preliminaries

Let
{\rm O}(p)
, the orthogonal group in dimension
p
, denote the group of all
\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
orthogonal matrices under the group operation of matrix multiplication. Let
\mathcal{G}
be a compact subgroup of the orthogonal group
{\rm O}(p)
.
In particular,
\mathcal{G}
satisfies the following group axioms: (i)
I\in\mathcal{G}
; here
I\equiv I_{p}
is the identity matrix of order
p
; (ii) if
Q\in\mathcal{G}
, then
Q^{-1}\in\mathcal{G}
; (iii) if
Q_{1}
,
Q_{2}\in\mathcal{G}
, then
Q_{1}Q_{2}\in\mathcal{G}
; (iv) if
Q_{1}
,
Q_{2}
,
Q_{3}\in\mathcal{G}
, then
(Q_{1}Q_{2})Q_{3}=Q_{1}(Q_{2}Q_{3})
.
The compactness assumption ensures that there exists a unique uniform distribution (also called the Haar measure) over
\mathcal{G}
; see e.g.,
Eaton, 1989
.

Definition 2.1

(Orbit)
.

The orbit of an element
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
is the set of elements (in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
) to which
\mathbf{x}
can be moved by the elements of
\mathcal{G}
, i.e.,
\{Q\mathbf{x}:Q\in\mathcal{G}\}
.

Note that, if
\mathbf{y}
is in the orbit of
\mathbf{x}
, then
\mathbf{x}
is also in the orbit of
\mathbf{y}
. Thus,

\mathbb{R}^{p}
can be partitioned into a disjoint union of orbits.
Let
B\subset\mathbb{R}^{p}
. We define the set

(2.1)

\mathcal{G}B:=\{Q\mathbf{x}:Q\in\mathcal{G},\mathbf{x}\in B\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{p}

as the image of the group action of
\mathcal{G}
on
B
.

Definition 2.2

(Group action)
.

A (left) group action of
\mathcal{G}
on
\mathcal{G}B
is a function
\cdot:\mathcal{G}\times\mathcal{G}B\to\mathcal{G}B
which satisfies the following two axioms: (i)
I\cdot\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{x}
, and (ii)
Q_{1}\cdot(Q_{2}\cdot\mathbf{x})=(Q_{1}Q_{2})\cdot\mathbf{x}
, for all
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{G}B
, and
Q_{1},Q_{2}\in\mathcal{G}
. In our setting we take the group action
\cdot
to be the usual matrix multiplication operation.

Definition 2.3

(Free group action)
.

We say that
\mathcal{G}
acts freely on
\mathcal{G}B
if for
\mathbf{y}\in B
and
Q\in\mathcal{G}
,
Q\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{y}
implies
Q=I
(i.e., for any
\mathbf{x}
in
\mathcal{G}B
, only the identity in
\mathcal{G}
leaves
\mathbf{x}
fixed).

Intuitively, a free group action helps identify the unique element in
\mathcal{G}
that maps a point to another point (i.e.,
Q_{1}\mathbf{x}=Q_{2}\mathbf{x}
for
Q_{1},Q_{2}\in\mathcal{G}
and
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{G}B
implies that
Q_{1}=Q_{2}
).
Let
\mathcal{P}_{\rm ac}(\mathbb{R}^{p})
denote the class of all Borel probability measures on
\mathbb{R}^{p}
with a Lebesgue density.
We say that a probability measure
\mu
is concentrated on a set
B
if
\mu(B)=1
.
If
\mu
is a Borel measure, the support of
\mu
is the smallest closed set on which
\mu
is concentrated.

Definition 2.4

(Push forward distribution)
.

Let
F:\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathbb{R}^{k}
(for
k\geq 1
) be a function and
\mu
be a distribution on
\mathbb{R}^{p}
. The push forward distribution
F\#\mu
is defined as the distribution of
F(\mathbf{X})
, where
\mathbf{X}\sim\mu
.

Definition 2.5

(Cyclical monotonicity)
.

Let
c:\mathbb{R}^{p}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}\to(-\infty,+\infty]
be a function. A subset
\Gamma\subset\mathbb{R}^{p}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}
is said to be
c
-cyclically monotone if for any
N\in\mathbb{N}
and any family
(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{y}_{1}),\ldots,(\mathbf{x}_{N},\mathbf{y}_{N})
of points in
\Gamma
, the following inequality holds:

\sum_{i=1}^{N}c(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{y}_{i})\leq\sum_{i=1}^{N}c(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{y}_{i+1}),

with the convention
\mathbf{y}_{N+1}=\mathbf{y}_{1}
.

2.2
Connection to Optimal Transport

For a random vector
{\bf X}\sim\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{\rm ac}(\mathbb{R}^{p})
and a compact subgroup
\mathcal{G}
of the orthogonal group
{\rm O}(p)
, we are interested in distribution-free testing of the hypothesis of
\mathcal{G}
-symmetry (
1.1
). In this subsection we define new distribution-free (in finite samples) multivariate generalizations of signs and ranks that will form the building blocks for distribution-free inference. Further, these generalizations of signs and ranks — which reduce to the usual classical notions of sign and rank when
p=1
and
\mathcal{G}=\{I,-I\}
— adapt to different notions of multivariate symmetry, and share many properties similar to their classical counterparts.
We borrow ideas from the theory of optimal transport (OT) to define our multivariate generalizations.
Although our approach is inspired by the notion of multivariate ranks defined via OT
[
43
,
27
]
, our framework is novel and more general.

Let
\nu
be some known and fixed Borel measure on
\mathbb{R}^{p}
, which will be referred to as the
reference distribution
; see
Remark

3.3
for concrete examples. For each
n
, we consider a quasi-Monte Carlo
[
48
,
66
,
81
]
or Monte Carlo
[
50
,
88
]
sequence
\{\mathbf{h}_{n,j}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}:\,j=1,2,\ldots,n\}
assumed to be known and fixed, such that the empirical distribution over
\{\mathbf{h}_{n,j}\}_{j=1}^{n}
, denoted by
\nu_{n}
, converges in distribution to
\nu
as
n\to\infty
. So
\nu_{n}
provides a discretization of
\nu
.
For notational brevity, we hide
n
and write
\mathbf{h}_{j}\equiv\mathbf{h}_{n,j}
.
For instance,
\{\mathbf{h}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}
can be taken as
n
i.i.d. observations from
\nu
and then fixed. If
\nu={\rm Uniform}(0,1)^{p}
, we can also let
\{\mathbf{h}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}
be the first
n
points in the Halton sequence
[
48
]
; other choices are also available (see e.g.,
Deb and Sen, 2023
).
As we will see later, to recover the classical one-dimensional sign and signed-rank, one can take
\nu={\rm Uniform}(0,1)
and
\mathbf{h}_{j}=\frac{j}{n}
, for
j=1,2,\ldots,n
.

Let
\mathcal{S}_{n}
be the set of all
n!
permutations of
\{1,\ldots,n\}
. Consider the following optimization problem:

(2.2)

\min\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lVert Q_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{\sigma(i)}-\mathbf{h}_{i}\rVert^{2}:Q_{i}\in\mathcal{G},\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{n}\right\}.

Let
(\{\widehat{Q}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n},\widehat{\sigma})
be a minimizer of (
2.2
).
In fact, under mild assumptions,
\widehat{\sigma}
is actually a.s. unique, and
\{\widehat{Q}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
is also a.s. unique if
\mathcal{G}
induces a free group action (see
2.1
). Define the cost function

(2.3)

c(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{h}):=\min_{Q\in\mathcal{G}}\|Q^{\top}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{h}\|^{2},\qquad\mbox{for}\;\;\mathbf{x},\mathbf{h}\in\mathbb{R}^{p},

so that (
2.2
) reduces to
\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}c(\mathbf{X}_{\sigma(i)},\mathbf{h}_{i})
— the optimal transport (OT) problem from
\{\mathbf{X}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{X}_{n}\}
to
\{\mathbf{h}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{h}_{n}\}
under the cost function
c(\cdot,\cdot)
. Observe that the cost function satisfies
c(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{h})=c(\mathbf{x},Q\mathbf{h})=c(Q\mathbf{x},\mathbf{h})
for any
Q\in\mathcal{G}
, i.e., it is invariant under transformations in
\mathcal{G}
.

Define the
generalized sign

S_{n}:\{\mathbf{X}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{X}_{n}\}\to\mathcal{G}
and
generalized rank

R_{n}:\{\mathbf{X}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{X}_{n}\}\to\{\mathbf{h}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{h}_{n}\}
as:

(2.4)

S_{n}(\mathbf{X}_{i})=\widehat{Q}_{\widehat{\sigma}^{-1}(i)},\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad R_{n}(\mathbf{X}_{i})=\mathbf{h}_{\widehat{\sigma}^{-1}(i)},\qquad\mbox{for}\;\;i=1,\ldots,n.

The
generalized signed-rank
of the
i
-th observation
\mathbf{X}_{i}
is then defined as
S_{n}(\mathbf{X}_{i})R_{n}(\mathbf{X}_{i})
.

Remark 2.1

(When
p=1
)
.

Note that when
p=1
and
\mathcal{G}=\{1,-1\}
, the generalized signs and ranks (in (
2.4
)) reduce to the classical one-dimensional concepts. In fact, problem (
2.2
) directly yields the usual signs and ranks in this case. To see this observe that, for
x\in\mathbb{R}
and
h\in[0,1]
, from (
2.3
),
c(x,h)=\min\{(x-h)^{2},(-x-h)^{2}\}
, which (due to the linear ordering of the real line) reduces to
c(x,h)=(|x|-h)^{2}
. It is now easy to see that (
2.2
), with
h_{j}=\frac{j}{n}
for all
j
, simplifies to

\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(|X_{i}|-\frac{\sigma(j)}{n}\right)^{2}=\max_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|X_{i}|\frac{\sigma(j)}{n},

which, by the rearrangement inequality (see e.g.,
[
49
, Theorem 368]
), yields that
\widehat{\sigma}(i)
will equal the usual (absolute) rank of

, for

with the

will be 1 if

When

) does not have an explicit solution. However, the approach is similar; optimization problem (
2.2
) assigns the data points

\{\mathbf{X}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
to the canonical multivariate rank vectors

\{\mathbf{h}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}
in a bijective fashion such that the objective
function in (
2.2
) is minimized. To develop some intuition for the generalized signs and signed-ranks (as in (

)), maybe it is easier to express the cost function as
c(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{h})=\min_{Q\in\mathcal{G}}\|\mathbf{x}-Q\mathbf{h}\|^{2}
. Thus, if

, then

and hence the signed-rank

S_{n}(\mathbf{X}_{i})R_{n}(\mathbf{X}_{i})\equiv S_{n}(\mathbf{X}_{i})\mathbf{h}_{j}
is the closest element (in Euclidean norm) to

\mathbf{X}_{i}
in the orbit of
\mathbf{h}_{j}

\mathbf{X}_{i}

\|\mathbf{X}_{i}-Q\mathbf{h}_{j}\|^{2}
over

— it is the orthogonal matrix in

that transforms the rank vector

to

2.3
Computational Complexity

The optimization problem (
2.2

Recall that
c(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{h})=\min_{Q\in\mathcal{G}}\|Q^{\top}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{h}\|^{2}
is the cost function (see (
2.3

O(1)
time if
\mathcal{G}
is finite. Then
the ranks
R_{n}(\mathbf{X}_{i})
can be found by solving the assignment problem
[
78
,
11
]
of
\mathbf{X}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{X}_{n}
to
\mathbf{h}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{h}_{n}
under the cost
c(\cdot,\cdot)
,
for which algorithms with worst case complexity

are available
[
63
]
.
After obtaining the ranks,

can be obtained (see the discussion in
Section

3.3
and

3.1
on how to obtain this minimizer for the three main examples of symmetry considered in this paper).

Remark 2.5

(Fast computation for spherical symmetry)
.

When
\mathcal{G}={\rm O}(p)
, the computation of the ranks (and the signed-ranks) is even faster (i.e., in

time) since

, where the last step follows from the fact that there exists
Q\in\mathcal{G}
that maps
\mathbf{h}
to the direction of
\mathbf{x}

[
58
]
. Thus, if

has the

will have

The Population Analogues

So far, we have defined the sample version of the multivariate generalized signs and ranks,
S_{n}(\cdot)
and

, via the optimization problem (

).
We will now show that, under finite second order moment assumptions on

\nu
, the sample rank map

converges to a population rank map

such that

[

(2.5)

\inf_{(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{H}):(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{H})\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\mathbb{E}\left[c(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{H})\right],\quad{\rm with}\ c(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{h})=\min_{Q\in\mathcal{G}}\|Q^{\top}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{h}\|^{2}.

Here,
\Pi(\mu,\nu)
consists of all joint Borel distributions
(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{H})
with marginals
\mathbf{X}\sim\mu
and
\mathbf{H}\sim\nu
. It can be seen that the optimization problem (
2.5
) is a natural population analogue of (
2.2
).

To establish the convergence of

and

to satisfy certain natural compatibility assumptions. In particular, we assume that:

Assumption 1
.

There exists a Borel set

\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}

B
) from any orbit of
\mathcal{G}

This is because transporting
\mathbf{x}
to any point in an orbit of
\mathcal{G}
has the same cost due to the form of the cost function

(see (
2.3
)),
and there is no need to include two representative points from an orbit, which may break the uniqueness of the population OT map.

3.1
Generalized Sign Test

Recall the hypothesis of
\mathcal{G}
-symmetry in (
1.1
), and that
under
{\rm H}_{0}
, the generalized signs
S_{n}(\mathbf{X}_{1}),\ldots,S_{n}(\mathbf{X}_{n})
are i.i.d. from
{\rm Uniform}(\mathcal{G})
.
Hence, any test of uniformity over
\mathcal{G}
can be applied with the generalized signs to define a multivariate analogue of the sign test.

Example 3.1

(Chi-squared test)
.

If
\mathcal{G}=\{g_{1},\ldots,g_{k}\}
is a finite group of size
k
, then we can count the observed frequencies of each element in
\mathcal{G}
, i.e.,
Y_{i}:=\sum_{j=1}^{n}I(S_{n}(\mathbf{X}_{j})=g_{i})
,
i=1,\ldots,k
.
Under
{\rm H}_{0}
,
(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{k})
follows

{\rm Multinomial}\left(n,\frac{1}{k}\mathbf{1}_{k}\right)
. Further, we can perform an asymptotic Pearson’s chi-squared test:

\widetilde{T}_{n}:=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{(Y_{i}-n/k)^{2}}{n/k}\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}\chi^{2}_{k-1},\quad{\rm as\ }n\to\infty.

{\rm H}_{0}
can be rejected for a large value of
\widetilde{T}_{n}
.

However, for a group with large size
k
(e.g.,
\mathcal{G}
corresponding to sign symmetry has size
2^{p}
, which can be very large for even moderate
p
), the convergence of the chi-squared test statistic
\widetilde{T}_{n}
to its limiting distribution can be slow.
Hence, in this paper we propose
the following
generalized sign test
:

(3.1)

T_{n}:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}S_{n}(\mathbf{X}_{i}).

We reject the null hypothesis
{\rm H}_{0}
for large values of
\|T_{n}\|^{2}_{F}
where
\|\cdot\|_{F}
denotes the matrix Frobenius norm. The generalized sign test is distribution-free under
{\rm H}_{0}
as the signs
S_{n}(\mathbf{X}_{i})
are i.i.d. from
{\rm Uniform}(\mathcal{G})
. We give below a simple result on the asymptotic behavior of
\|T_{n}\|^{2}_{F}
under
{\rm H}_{0}
. Note that this test statistic is also a direct generalization of the classical sign test.

Proposition 3.1

(Generalized sign test)
.

We have the following consequences:

1.

Central symmetry:
S_{n}(\mathbf{X}_{i})
are i.i.d. uniform over
\mathcal{G}=\{I,-I\}
under
{\rm H}_{0}
, and
T_{n}
(in (
3.1
)) is thus equivalent to a binomial random variable (up to a linear transformation) in finite samples. The asymptotic distribution of
T_{n}
follows from the usual one-dimensional CLT:

\frac{1}{p}\|T_{n}\|^{2}_{F}\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}\chi^{2}_{1},\quad{\rm as}\ n\to\infty.

2.

Sign symmetry:
S_{n}(\mathbf{X}_{i})
are i.i.d. uniform over diagonal orthogonal matrices under the null. Equivalently,
S_{n}(\mathbf{X}_{i})
can be viewed as being i.i.d. uniform over
\{1,-1\}^{p}
. In such a case,

\|T_{n}\|^{2}_{F}\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}\chi^{2}_{p},\quad{\rm as}\ n\to\infty.

3.

Spherical symmetry:
S_{n}(\mathbf{X}_{i})
are i.i.d. uniform over the orthogonal group
{\rm O}(p)
under
{\rm H}_{0}
. In this case, our generalized sign test behaves as:

{p}\|T_{n}\|^{2}_{F}\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}\chi^{2}_{p^{2}},\quad{\rm as}\ n\to\infty.

Possibly the simplest test of uniformity over
{\rm O}(p)
is given by the Rayleigh test
[
72

, which rejects the uniformity assumption when
pn{\rm Tr}\left(\overline{S}^{\top}\overline{S}\right)
is large, where

\overline{S}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}S_{n}(\mathbf{X}_{i})

3.1
-3. We also observe that all the above three cases reduce to the classical two-sided sign test when
p=1
.

Lemma 3.1

(Hájek representation)

Suppose Assumptions

,

,

Theorem 3.4

(Asymptotic distribution under local alternatives)

Let
\mathbf{X}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{X}_{n}

{\rm H}_{0}:{\bm{\theta}}=\mathbf{0}_{p}\qquad\textrm{versus}\qquad{\rm H}_{1}:{\bm{\theta}}=\frac{{\bm{\xi}}_{n}}{\sqrt{n}},

2

4

\mathbf{W}_{n}\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}N\left({\bm{\gamma}},\Sigma_{\rm ERD}\right),\quad\qquad\mbox{where}\;\;{\bm{\gamma}}:=-\mathbb{E}_{{\rm H}_{0}}\left[J(\nabla\psi(\mathbf{X}))\frac{{\bm{\xi}}^{\top}\nabla f(\mathbf{X})}{f(\mathbf{X})}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{p}.

Definition 3.2

(Asymptotic relative efficiency)

For

{\rm H}_{0}:{\bm{\theta}}=\mathbf{0}_{p}\qquad\textrm{versus}\qquad{\rm H}_{1}:{\bm{\theta}}=\varepsilon_{n}{\bm{\xi}}_{n},\quad{\rm where\ }\varepsilon_{n}\to 0,\ {\bm{\xi}}_{n}\to{\bm{\xi}}

{\bm{\xi}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, we have

\displaystyle\inf_{{\bm{\xi}}:\|I^{1/2}_{{\bm{\theta}}_{0}}{\bm{\xi}}\|=c}\lim_{n\to\infty}\pi_{n}\left(\frac{{\bm{\xi}}}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\leq\inf_{{\bm{\xi}}:\|I^{1/2}_{{\bm{\theta}}_{0}}{\bm{\xi}}\|=c}\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}_{{\bm{\theta}}_{0}+\frac{{\bm{\xi}}}{\sqrt{n}}}\left(\mathbf{W}_{n}^{\top}\Sigma_{\rm ERD}^{-1}\mathbf{W}_{n}\geq\chi_{p}^{2}(\alpha)\right),

for all
c>0
.

2.

(Locally most powerful test) Let
\zeta:\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathbb{R}
be differentiable at
{\bm{\theta}}_{0}=\mathbf{0}_{p}
and
\zeta({\bm{\theta}}_{0})=0
.
Let
{\bm{\xi}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
be such that
{\bm{\xi}}^{\top}\nabla\zeta({\bm{\theta}}_{0})>0
.
For any sequence of level
\alpha\in(0,1)
tests
\{\phi_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
for

(3.8)

{\rm H}_{0}:\zeta({\bm{\theta}})\leq 0\qquad{\rm versus}\qquad{\rm H}_{1}:\zeta({\bm{\theta}})>0

with power function
\pi_{n}({\bm{\theta}})=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\theta}}[\phi_{n}]
, we have

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}\pi_{n}\left(\frac{{\bm{\xi}}}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\leq\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}_{{\bm{\theta}}_{0}+\frac{{\bm{\xi}}}{\sqrt{n}}}\left(\frac{\nabla\zeta({\bm{\theta}}_{0})^{\top}I_{{\bm{\theta}}_{0}}^{-1}\mathbf{W}_{n}}{\sqrt{\nabla\zeta({\bm{\theta}}_{0})^{\top}I_{{{\bm{\theta}}_{0}}}^{-1}\nabla\zeta({\bm{\theta}}_{0})}}\geq z_{\alpha}\right),

where
z_{\alpha}
satisfies
\Phi(z_{\alpha})=1-\alpha
, and

\frac{\nabla\zeta({\bm{\theta}}_{0})^{\top}I_{{\bm{\theta}}_{0}}^{-1}\mathbf{W}_{n}}{\sqrt{\nabla\zeta({\bm{\theta}}_{0})^{\top}I_{{{\bm{\theta}}_{0}}}^{-1}\nabla\zeta({\bm{\theta}}_{0})}}\geq z_{\alpha}
is the appropriate (asymptotic) level
\alpha
test based on the GWSR statistic
\mathbf{W}_{n}
for (
3.8
).

The first result above shows that the GWSR test maximizes the minimum (local) power over the contour
\{{\bm{\xi}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}:\|I^{1/2}_{{\bm{\theta}}_{0}}{\bm{\xi}}\|=c\}
for any
c>0
, while the second result tells us that if the hypothesis is one-sided, then a test based on the GWSR statistic
\mathbf{W}_{n}
would achieve maximum (local) power for any fixed direction
{\bm{\xi}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
.
Although tests based on
\mathbf{W}_{n}
can be locally asymptotically optimal, the optimal score function
J(\cdot)
(in (
3.6
)) depends on the underlying distribution
f
, and is in general unknown.

Example 3.2

(Gaussian case)
.

Suppose it is known that
f(\cdot)
(in
Theorem

3.9
) follows a Gaussian distribution
N(\mathbf{0}_{p},\Sigma)
with unknown nonsingular covariance matrix
\Sigma
.
Then with
S\mathbf{H}\sim N(\mathbf{0},I_{p})
,
Theorem

3.9
states that the optimal score function (in (
3.6
)) is
J(\mathbf{x})=\Sigma^{-1/2}\mathbf{x}
.
Although
\Sigma
is unknown, it may be estimated by the sample covariance matrix
\widehat{\Sigma}
. Define

\widehat{J}(\mathbf{x})=\widehat{\Sigma}^{-1/2}\mathbf{x}
. Then,

\mathbf{W}_{n}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}S_{n}(\mathbf{X}_{i})J(R_{n}(\mathbf{X}_{i}))=\Sigma^{-1/2}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}S_{n}(\mathbf{X}_{i})R_{n}(\mathbf{X}_{i})\right)

has the same asymptotic distribution as

(3.9)

\widehat{\Sigma}^{-1/2}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}S_{n}(\mathbf{X}_{i})R_{n}(\mathbf{X}_{i})\right).

This means that using the data-driven score function
\widehat{J}(\cdot)
has the same asymptotic distribution as using
J(\cdot)
, and thus the GWSR test based on (
3.9
) also has the same local asymptotic optimal properties.

Generalizing the above example, if we can estimate optimal score function
J(\cdot)=-\frac{\nabla f(\nabla\psi^{*}(\cdot))}{f(\nabla\psi^{*}(\cdot))}
in
Theorem

3.9
sufficiently well such that

\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}S_{n}(\mathbf{X}_{i})J(R_{n}(\mathbf{X}_{i}))=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}S_{n}(\mathbf{X}_{i})\widehat{J}(R_{n}(\mathbf{X}_{i}))+o_{p}(1),

then the GWSR test using
\widehat{J}
will be locally asymptotically optimal as described in
Theorem

3.9
.

However, in general, estimating the optimal
J(\cdot)=-\frac{\nabla f(\nabla\psi^{*}(\cdot))}{f(\nabla\psi^{*}(\cdot))}
faces two main obstacles, which are both hard problems without additional information.
First,
\frac{\nabla f(\cdot)}{f(\cdot)}
is unknown and may be estimated empirically.
Estimating the gradient of a log-density function is in principle a difficult non-parametric problem
[
60
]
. A positive result was given in
Hyvärinen, 2005
, where the author proposed the
score matching
method which minimizes an empirical version of

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}\sim f}\left[\|\mathbf{s}_{\tau}(\mathbf{X})-\nabla\log f(\mathbf{X})\|_{2}^{2}\right]
over
\tau
,
with
\mathbf{s}_{\tau}:\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathbb{R}^{p}
is parametrized by
\tau
. In
Song and Ermon, 2019
,
\mathbf{s}_{\tau}
is taken as a neural network;

\mathbf{s}_{\tau}
can also be taken to belong to a parametric family
[
60
]
.
Another obstacle is the estimation of
\nabla\psi^{*}(\cdot)
(which is the transport map from
\nu_{S}
to
P_{{\bm{\theta}}_{0}}
). Though with enough data from
P_{{\bm{\theta}}_{0}}
, we can estimate
\nabla\psi^{*}(\cdot)
up to arbitrary precision
[
26
,
70
]
, the convergence rate may still suffer from the curse of dimensionality
[
26
,
70
]
.

Distribution-Free Confidence Sets

We say that
\mathbf{X}\sim\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{\rm ac}(\mathbb{R}^{p})
is
\mathcal{G}
-symmetric around
\bm{\theta}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
if
\mathbf{X}-\bm{\theta}^{*}\overset{d}{=}Q(\mathbf{X}-\bm{\theta}^{*})
for all
Q\in\mathcal{G}
. Here
\bm{\theta}^{*}
is called the center of symmetry for the distribution
\mu
. As a direct consequence of our results, the GWSR test (or the generalized sign test) can be used to obtain a distribution-free confidence set for
\bm{\theta}^{*}
. The idea is just to invert the collection of hypotheses
\mathrm{H}_{0,\bm{\theta}}
defined as:

\mathrm{H}_{0,\bm{\theta}}:(\mathbf{X}-\bm{\theta})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}Q(\mathbf{X}-\bm{\theta}),\quad\mbox{ for all }\;Q\in\mathcal{G},

for
\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, using the distribution-free (see Proposition
2.2
) GWSR test with data
\{\mathbf{X}_{i}-\bm{\theta}\}_{i=1}^{n}
at level
\alpha\in(0,1)
. This yields an exact
(1-\alpha)
distribution-free confidence set for
\bm{\theta}^{*}
:

\mathcal{C}:=\left\{\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}:\mathrm{H}_{0,\bm{\theta}}\mbox{ is accepted using data }\{\mathbf{X}_{i}-\bm{\theta}\}_{i=1}^{n}\right\},

without making any distributional assumptions on
\mathbf{X}\sim\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{\rm ac}(\mathbb{R}^{p})
except the assumption of
\mathcal{G}
-symmetry. For implementational purposes, we recommend using a fine grid of
\bm{\theta}
’s to test
\mathrm{H}_{0,\bm{\theta}}
and approximate
\mathcal{C}
. For
p\geq 2
, an alternative to ‘gridding’ (which may be computationally infeasible) is to test the hypothesis of
\mathcal{G}
-symmetry
\mathrm{H}_{0,\bm{\theta}}
at
\bm{\theta}=\mathbf{X}_{i}
, for each
i=1,\ldots,n
, and then to consider the (possibly larger) convex hull of the accepted
\bm{\theta}
’s.

Figure

5
shows the distribution-free 95% confidence sets (colored in yellow; obtained via a fine gridding of
\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
) using the GWSR test when
p=2
with the Gaussian ERD and the identity score function, when: (i)
\mathcal{G}=\{-I,I\}
(corresponding to central symmetry), and (ii)
\mathcal{G}=O(p)
(corresponding to spherical symmetry); see
Remark

3.3
for our choice of
\nu
(here we used a random sample from
\nu
to obtain
\nu_{n}
as in
Section

4.2
).
The first two rows show data points drawn from the Gaussian distribution
N(0.5\cdot\mathbf{1}_{2},I_{2})
; the third row depicts data points obtained from the multivariate
t
-distribution with 1 degree of freedom with location parameter
0.5\cdot\mathbf{1}_{2}
and scale parameter
I_{2}
.
In each example, the true center of symmetry
\bm{\theta}^{*}
is marked by “
+
”.

It can be seen that our GWSR based confidence sets successfully cover the true unknown center
\bm{\theta}^{*}
in the depicted cases. Moreover, for testing spherical symmetry with Gaussian data (see the first row of
Figure

5
), the two confidence sets — our proposal based on the GWSR test and the one obtained from Hotelling’s
T^{2}
test — are almost indistinguishable even for sample size
n=20
. For testing central symmetry (see the second row of
Figure

5
) using Gaussian data we observe a similar phenomenon, although for

When the Gaussian assumption is violated and we have heavy-tailed observations (see the third row of

A.1
Regularity Conditions

In this section, we describe the regularity conditions that are assumed in
Theorem

3.4
, in order to study the power performance against local contiguous alternatives.
We adopt the standard smooth parametric model assumptions that guarantee local asymptotic normality (LAN) (see e.g.,
Van der Vaart, 1998
).
Consider the parametric family consisting of all locational shifts of a symmetric distribution
f
, i.e.,

\{\mathcal{P}_{\bm{\theta}}\}_{{\bm{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}=\{f(\cdot-{\bm{\theta}}):{\bm{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}\}
. Assume:

1.

The family
\{\mathcal{P}_{\bm{\theta}}\}_{{\bm{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}
is
quadratic mean differentiable
(QMD) at
{\bm{\theta}}_{0}=\mathbf{0}
(see
[
68
, Definition 12.2.1]
for relevant definitions).

2.

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}\sim\mathcal{P}_{{\bm{\theta}}_{0}}}\left(\|\eta(\mathbf{X},{\bm{\theta}}_{0})\|^{2}\right)<\infty
, where
\eta(\cdot,{\bm{\theta}}):=\frac{\nabla f(\cdot-{\bm{\theta}})}{f(\cdot-{\bm{\theta}})}
is the
score function
. Also assume that the Fisher information at
{\bm{\theta}}_{0}
, that is,
I_{{\bm{\theta}}_{0}}:=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}\sim\mathcal{P}_{{\bm{\theta}}_{0}}}\left[\eta(\mathbf{X},{\bm{\theta}})\eta(\mathbf{X},{\bm{\theta}})^{\top}\right]
exists and is invertible.

A.2
Generating a Random Sign for Spherical Symmetry

To show that the procedure described in
Remark

3.1
in
Section

3.3
generates a sample from the conditional distribution of
{\rm Uniform(O}(p))
given that it maps
\mathbf{v}
to
\mathbf{w}
,
we note that a uniform distribution over
{\rm O}(p)
can be generated in the following way
[
30
, Example 4.1]
:

1.

First draw
\mathbf{\omega}
from the uniform distribution over the sphere
\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}:\|\mathbf{x}\|=1\}
.

2.

Then draw
p(p-1)
i.i.d. standard normal random variables
\varepsilon\in\mathbb{R}^{p(p-1)}
independent of
\mathbf{\omega}
.

3.

Apply Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization to the columns of
[\mathbf{\omega}\ \varepsilon]
in the order of the columns. This yields an orthogonal matrix
{\rm GS}([\mathbf{\omega}\ \varepsilon])
following the uniform distribution over
{\rm O}(p)

[
30
, Example 4.1]
.

4.

Define
f(\mathbf{\omega},\varepsilon):={\rm GS}([\mathbf{\omega}\ \varepsilon])[\mathbf{v}\ V]^{\top}
which also follows the uniform distribution over
{\rm O}(p)
(as
[\mathbf{v}\ V]\in{\rm O}(p)
), by the definition of the Haar measure (invariant under group action).

Note that the set
\argmin_{Q\in\mathcal{G}}\|Q^{\top}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{h}\|^{2}
is described by
\{Q\in{\rm O}(p):\mathbf{w}=Q\mathbf{v}\}
.
If
f(\omega,\varepsilon)\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{w}
, we know that this condition is equivalent to
{\rm GS}([\mathbf{\omega}\ \varepsilon])\mathbf{e}_{1}=\mathbf{w}
, where
\mathbf{e}_{1}=(1,0,\ldots,0)^{\top}
, and it is further equivalent to
\omega=\mathbf{w}
. Therefore, by the independence of
\omega
and
\varepsilon
, given
\omega=\mathbf{w}
, the conditional distribution of
f(\omega,\varepsilon)
is given by the unconditional distribution of
f(\mathbf{w},\varepsilon)
. Thus the validity of the procedure is shown.
∎

A second way to generate the conditional distribution.
From

\{Q\in\mathrm{O}(p):\;\mathbf{w}=Q\mathbf{v}\}=\{\mathbf{w}\mathbf{v}^{\top}+WUV^{\top}:\;U\in\mathrm{O}(p-1)\},

where
V
and
W
are
p\times(p-1)
matrices such that
V^{\top}V=W^{\top}W=I_{p-1}
,
V^{\top}\mathbf{v}=W^{\top}\mathbf{w}=\mathbf{0}
,
we can first generate
U
from
\mathrm{O}(p-1)
and then
\mathbf{w}\mathbf{v}^{\top}+WUV^{\top}
has the desired conditional distribution. The validity of this procedure can be shown as follows:

1.

Draw
p^{2}
i.i.d. standard normal variables
\varepsilon\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
to form the
p\times p
matrix
[\varepsilon_{ij}]
.

2.

Apply Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization to the columns of
[\varepsilon_{ij}]
in the order of the columns. This yields an orthogonal matrix
{\rm GS}([\varepsilon_{ij}])
that follows the uniform distribution over
{\rm O}(p)

[
30
, Example 4.1]
.

3.

Define
f(\varepsilon):=[\mathbf{w}\ W]{\rm GS}([\varepsilon_{ij}])[\mathbf{v}\ V]^{\top}
which also follows the uniform distribution over
{\rm O}(p)
by the invariance property of the Haar measure.

If
f(\varepsilon)\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{w}
, we know that this condition is equivalent to saying that the first column of
{\rm GS}([\varepsilon_{ij}])
is
\mathbf{e}_{1}
. From the Gram-Schmidt procedure we know that this is further equivalent to the event
\{\varepsilon_{j1}=0,{\ \rm for\ }j=2,3,\ldots,p,\ {\rm and}\ \varepsilon_{11}>0\}
which only depends on the first column of
[\varepsilon_{ij}]
. By independence, given this condition, other
\varepsilon_{ij}
from the second column to the last column are still i.i.d. standard normal. Hence this implies the bottom right
(p-1)\times(p-1)
sub-matrix of
{\rm GS}([\varepsilon_{ij}])
(denoted by
U
) conditionally follows the uniform distribution over
{\rm O}(p-1)
, and
{\rm GS}([\varepsilon_{ij}])=\begin{pmatrix}1&\mathbf{0}^{\top}\\
\mathbf{0}&U\\
\end{pmatrix}.

Hence the conditional distribution of the uniform distribution

f(\varepsilon)
given
\{f(\varepsilon)\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{w}\}
has the distribution of
[\mathbf{w}\ W]\begin{pmatrix}1&\mathbf{0}^{\top}\\
\mathbf{0}&U\\
\end{pmatrix}[\mathbf{v}\ V]^{\top}=\mathbf{w}\mathbf{v}^{\top}+WUV^{\top}

with
U\sim{\rm Uniform}({\rm O}(p-1))
.
Therefore, the validity of the second generating procedure is shown.∎

Appendix B
Proofs

B.1
Proof of
2.1

We first show the uniqueness of
R_{n}(\cdot)
. If the uniqueness fails, then necessarily, there exist two different (non-random) permutations
\sigma_{1}
and
\sigma_{2}
such that

\sum_{i=1}^{n}c(\mathbf{X}_{i},\mathbf{h}_{\sigma_{1}(i)})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}c(\mathbf{X}_{i},\mathbf{h}_{\sigma_{2}(i)})
.
Without loss of generality, suppose
\sigma_{1}(1)\neq\sigma_{2}(1)
.
Then

(B.1)

c(\mathbf{X}_{1},\mathbf{h}_{\sigma_{1}(1)})-c(\mathbf{X}_{1},\mathbf{h}_{\sigma_{2}(1)})=\sum_{i=2}^{n}\left(c(\mathbf{X}_{i},\mathbf{h}_{\sigma_{2}(i)})-c(\mathbf{X}_{i},\mathbf{h}_{\sigma_{1}(i)})\right).

Define the distance from a point
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
to a set
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{p}
as
d(\mathbf{x},A):=\inf_{\mathbf{y}\in A}\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|
.
Note that
c(\mathbf{X}_{1},\mathbf{h}_{\sigma_{1}(1)})-c(\mathbf{X}_{1},\mathbf{h}_{\sigma_{2}(1)})=d(\mathbf{X}_{1},\mathcal{G}\mathbf{h}_{\sigma_{1}(1)})^{2}-d(\mathbf{X}_{1},\mathcal{G}\mathbf{h}_{\sigma_{2}(1)})^{2}
.
The following lemma shows that as long as the orbits

such that for all
Q\in V
and
\mathbf{y}\in B(\mathbf{x},\varepsilon/2)
,
we have
Q\mathbf{y}\in N_{\mathbf{x}}
.
Therefore, by independence,

\mathbb{P}(S\mathbf{X}\in N_{\mathbf{x}})\geq\mathbb{P}(S\in V,\mathbf{X}\in B(\mathbf{x},\varepsilon/2))=\mathbb{P}(S\in V)\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{X}\in B(\mathbf{x},\varepsilon/2))>0
, where we have used that
I
,
\mathbf{x}
are in the supports of
{\rm Uniform}(\mathcal{G})
and
\mu
, respectively. Hence, every point in the support of
\mathbf{X}
is in the support of
S\mathbf{X}\sim\mu_{S}
.

Note that
g(\cdot)
is Lebesgue a.e. positive on
{\rm supp}(\mu_{S})
. Thus for any Borel set
B\subset\mathbb{R}^{p}
,

\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{X}\in B)=\int_{{\rm supp}(\mu_{S})\cap B}f(\mathbf{x})\,{\rm d}\mathbf{x}=\int_{{\rm supp}(\mu_{S})\cap B}\frac{f(\mathbf{x})}{g(\mathbf{x})}g(\mathbf{x})\,{\rm d}\mathbf{x}=\int_{{\rm supp}(\mu_{S})\cap B}\frac{f(\mathbf{x})}{g(\mathbf{x})}\,{\rm d}\mu_{S}(\mathbf{x}).

Thus,
\mathbf{X}
is absolutely continuous w.r.t.
S\mathbf{X}
with Radon-Nikodym derivative
\frac{f(\cdot)}{g(\cdot)}
. ∎

Proof of
Theorem

2.1
.

By
C.1
,
the continuity of
c(\cdot,\cdot)
implies that there exists
\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)
with a
c
-cyclically monotone support. By
C.2
, for any
\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)
with a
c
-cyclically monotone support, there exists a
c
-convex function
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
such that the support of

c
-subdifferential set

\partial_{c}\psi:=\left\{(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\in\mathbb{R}^{p}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}:\psi^{c}(\mathbf{y})-\psi(\mathbf{x})=c(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\right\}.

Since
\psi
is
c
-convex, and

is continuous,
\psi
is a l.s.c. convex function in the usual sense. Thus,
\psi
is Lebesgue a.e. differentiable in its domain (which contains the support of
\mu
).
Fix any pair of
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
in the support of
\pi
and suppose
\psi

\mathbf{x}

Let
\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\left(-c(\textbf{x},\textbf{y})\right)

which clearly exists (as

\mathbf{x}

[

As

is differentiable at
\mathbf{x}
, for
\mathbf{h}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}

\mathbf{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, we have

(B.6)

\psi(\textbf{x}+\textbf{h})=\psi(\textbf{x})+\nabla\psi(\textbf{x})\cdot\textbf{h}+o(\textbf{h}).

Now, using the definition of
c
-transform (see (

)):

\displaystyle\psi(\textbf{x}+\textbf{h})

\displaystyle\geq\psi^{c}(\textbf{y})-c(\textbf{x}+\textbf{h},\textbf{y})

\displaystyle\geq\;\psi^{c}(\textbf{y})-c(\textbf{x},\textbf{y})+\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\left(-c(\textbf{x},\textbf{y})\right)\cdot\textbf{h}\quad\text{ (by the definition\ of\ sub-gradient of }g(\cdot))

\displaystyle=\;\psi(\textbf{x})+\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\left(-c(\textbf{x},\textbf{y})\right)\cdot\textbf{h}.

Since

can take arbitrary directions, comparing the above display with (

\nabla\psi(\textbf{x})=\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\left(-c(\textbf{x},\textbf{y})\right).

This also implies that the sub-gradient

\{\widetilde{Q}\mathbf{y}:\widetilde{Q}\in\argmax_{Q\in\mathcal{G}}\mathbf{x}^{\top}Q\mathbf{y}\}

\nabla\psi(\textbf{x})=Q_{0}\mathbf{y}
, so that
\textbf{y}=Q_{0}^{-1}\nabla\psi(\textbf{x})
.
Since the orbit
\{Q\nabla\psi(\textbf{x}):Q\in\mathcal{G}\}
intersects

at one point at most, we have
\mathbf{y}=q\left(\nabla\psi(\textbf{x})\right)
(as
\mathbf{y}\in B

\nu

).

Thus, for
\mu
-a.e.
x
, there is only one

(\textbf{x},\textbf{y})\in\partial_{c}\psi
. So,
\pi
is given by a deterministic coupling, i.e.,
({\rm identity},q\circ\nabla\psi)\#\mu
and the existence of

is shown.
Although

\psi

\mu
-a.e. unique Borel measurable version (see the proof of

) which we define to be

Let

S\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}SQ

\mu
-a.e.
\mathbf{x}

\nabla\psi(\mathbf{x})
and
R(\mathbf{x})=q(\nabla\psi(\mathbf{x}))

Since
\psi

As

to

[

(which does not need any moment assumptions)

-a.e. uniquely determined (by the absolute continuity of

As

To show

and thus,

.
However, as shown in (

. This implies that

.

Finally,

Lemma B.4

(Continuity and uniqueness of
S(\cdot,\cdot)
when
\mathcal{G}
acts freely on
\mathcal{G}B
)
.

Under the assumptions of
Theorem

2.1
, if
\mathcal{G}
acts freely on
\mathcal{G}B
, then
S(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}):=\argmin_{Q\in\mathcal{G}}\|Q^{\top}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|^{2}
is unique and continuous for
({\rm identity},{R})\#\mu
-a.e.
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
.

Proof.

We first show the uniqueness. Suppose that there are two minimizers
Q_{1},Q_{2}
of
\|Q^{\top}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|^{2}
. Then
Q_{1},Q_{2}
are also maximizers of
\mathbf{x}^{\top}Q\mathbf{y}
. Since for
\mu
-a.e.
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}=R(\mathbf{x})
, from the proof of
Theorem

2.1
,
\{Q\mathbf{y}:Q{\rm\ is\ a\ maximizer\ of\ }\max_{S\in\mathcal{G}}\mathbf{x}^{\top}Q\mathbf{y}\}
has only one element
\nabla\psi(\mathbf{x})
, we must have
Q_{1}\mathbf{y}=Q_{2}\mathbf{y}=\nabla\psi(\mathbf{x})
.
This implies
Q_{1}^{-1}Q_{2}\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{y}
and thus
Q_{1}=Q_{2}
, as
\mathcal{G}
acts freely on
\mathcal{G}B
.
Hence
\argmin_{Q\in\mathcal{G}}\|Q^{\top}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|^{2}
is unique
({\rm identity},{R})\#\mu
-a.s.

Next, we show the continuity.
Suppose
(\mathbf{x}_{n},\mathbf{y}_{n})\to(\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{y}_{0})
as
n\to\infty
and
S(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
is uniquely defined at
(\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{y}_{0})
.
Consider the function
f_{n}:\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}\to\mathbb{R}
defined by
f_{n}(Q)=\|Q^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{n}-\mathbf{y}_{n}\|^{2}
if
Q\in\mathcal{G}
, and
f_{n}(Q)=+\infty
otherwise.
Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}\to\mathbb{R}
be the function defined by
f(Q)=\|Q^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{0}-\mathbf{y}_{0}\|^{2}
if
Q\in\mathcal{G}
and
f(Q)=+\infty
otherwise. For any
Q\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
, it is easy to see that
for every sequence
\{Q_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
such that
\|Q_{n}-Q\|_{F}\to 0
,

\liminf_{n}f_{n}(Q_{n})\geq f(Q)
(by using the form of
f_{n}
, which is continuous on
\mathcal{G}
, and the compactness of
\mathcal{G}
). Moreover, for some sequence
\{Q_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
such that
\|Q_{n}-Q\|_{F}\to 0
, we also have
\limsup_{n}f_{n}(Q_{n})\leq f(Q)
.
This implies
f_{n}

epi-converges
to
f

[
89
, Proposition 7.2]
.
Note that
f_{n}
and
f
are l.s.c. with bounded level sets
\{Q\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}\,:\,f_{n}(Q)\leq\alpha\}
for all
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
.
By
Rockafellar and Wets, 1998
, the minimizer(s) of

must converge to the minimizer of
f
.
Hence, the continuity of
S(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
is shown.
∎

Lemma B.5

(Joint weak convergence of empirical ranks and signed-ranks)
.

Suppose Assumptions
1
and
2
hold. Let
\mu_{n}
denote the empirical distribution on
\{\mathbf{X}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{X}_{n}\}
and
\mathcal{X}_{n}\sim\mu_{n}
.
With probability 1, we have

(\mathcal{X}_{n},R_{n}(\mathcal{X}_{n}))\overset{w}{\longrightarrow}(\mathbf{X},R(\mathbf{X}))
and
(\mathcal{X}_{n},S_{n}(\mathcal{X}_{n})R_{n}(\mathcal{X}_{n}))\overset{w}{\longrightarrow}(\mathbf{X},\nabla\psi(\mathbf{X}))
.

Proof.

It is known that
\mu_{n}
converges weakly to
\mu
with probability 1.
Fix
\omega\in\Omega
(the sample space) such that
\mu_{n}(\omega)\overset{w}{\longrightarrow}\mu
.
Consider the sequence of random measures
({\rm identity},{R}_{n})\#\mu_{n}(\omega)
, where
{R}_{n}
is the empirical rank map and the randomness comes from the random draw according to
\mu_{n}(\omega)
.
As
{R}_{n}\#\mu_{n}=\nu_{n}
converges weakly (to
\nu
, by
2
) and
\mu_{n}(\omega)
converges weakly (to
\mu
), we have that
({\rm identity},{R}_{n})\#\mu_{n}(\omega)
is tight. Hence by Prokhorov’s theorem, every subsequence of
({\rm identity},{R}_{n})\#\mu_{n}(\omega)
has
a further subsequence that converges weakly.
We will show that this limit is
({\rm identity},{R})\#\mu
regardless of what sub-sequences are chosen. Consequently, since weak convergence is metrizable,
({\rm identity},{R}_{n})\#\mu_{n}(\omega)
converges weakly to the same limit.

Now suppose the convergent sub-sequence is
({\rm identity},{R}_{n_{k}})\#\mu_{n_{k}}(\omega)
. We know that
({\rm identity},{R}_{n_{k}})\#\mu_{n_{k}}(\omega)
is supported on a
c
-cyclically monotone set, following the optimality of
{R}_{n_{k}}
(see e.g., the proof of
C.1
). This, together with the continuity of
c(\cdot,\cdot)
, implies that its weak limit must also be supported on a
c
-cyclically monotone set (this is also shown in the proof of
C.1
).
From
Theorem

2.1
, the unique
c
-cyclically monotone measure in
\Pi(\mu,\nu)
is
({\rm identity},{R})\#\mu
;
this establishes
({\rm identity},{R}_{n})\#\mu_{n}(\omega)\overset{w}{\longrightarrow}({\rm identity},{R})\#\mu
.
Or equivalently, with
\mathcal{X}_{n}\sim\mu_{n}
, we have
(\mathcal{X}_{n},R_{n}(\mathcal{X}_{n}))\overset{w}{\longrightarrow}(\mathbf{X},R(\mathbf{X}))
.

Next, we use the above weak convergence to show that

(\mathcal{X}_{n},S_{n}(\mathcal{X}_{n})R_{n}(\mathcal{X}_{n}))\overset{w}{\longrightarrow}(\mathbf{X},\nabla\psi(\mathbf{X}))
.
Recall that the signed-rank is the point in the orbit of the rank vector that is closest to the data point. So by defining
f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}):={\rm Proj}_{\mathcal{G}\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{x})
as the projection of
\mathbf{x}
onto the orbit of
\mathbf{y}
, we can write
S_{n}(\mathcal{X}_{n})R_{n}(\mathcal{X}_{n})=f(\mathcal{X}_{n},R_{n}(\mathcal{X}_{n}))
,
and
\nabla\psi(\mathbf{X})=f(\mathbf{X},R(\mathbf{X}))
.
We will show that
f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
is continuous at
({\rm identity},{R})\#\mu
a.e.-
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
.
We have shown in the proof of
Theorem

2.1
that the signed-rank
f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})={\rm Proj}_{\mathcal{G}\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{x})
is

({\rm identity},{R})\#\mu
a.e.-
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
uniquely defined. In case where the nearest point in
\mathcal{G}\mathbf{y}
to
\mathbf{x}
is not unique,
f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
can be chosen as any projection, and we can show that the continuity still holds.

Let
(\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{y}_{0})
be a point where the projection
{\rm Proj}_{\mathcal{G}\mathbf{y}_{0}}(\mathbf{x}_{0})
is unique. We argue by contradiction. If
f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
is not continuous at
(\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{y}_{0})
, then there exist
\delta>0
and a sequence
(\mathbf{x}_{n},\mathbf{y}_{n})\to(\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{y}_{0})
such that
\|{\rm Proj}_{\mathcal{G}\mathbf{y}_{n}}(\mathbf{x}_{n})-{\rm Proj}_{\mathcal{G}\mathbf{y}_{0}}(\mathbf{x}_{0})\|\geq\delta
for all
n
.
Define the distance from a point
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
to a set
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{p}
as
d(\mathbf{x},A):=\inf_{\mathbf{y}\in A}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
. Let
d_{0}:=d(\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathcal{G}\mathbf{y}_{0})
, and suppose
{\rm Proj}_{\mathcal{G}\mathbf{y}_{0}}(\mathbf{x}_{0})=g_{0}\mathbf{y}_{0}
is the point in
\mathcal{G}\mathbf{y}_{0}
that achieves this minimal distance, i.e.,
\|\mathbf{x}_{0}-g_{0}\mathbf{y}_{0}\|=d_{0}
.
Observe that
d(\mathbf{x}_{n},\mathcal{G}\mathbf{y}_{n})\leq d(\mathbf{x}_{n},\mathbf{x}_{0})+d(\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathcal{G}\mathbf{y}_{n})\leq d(\mathbf{x}_{n},\mathbf{x}_{0})+d(\mathbf{x}_{0},g_{0}\mathbf{y}_{n})\leq d(\mathbf{x}_{n},\mathbf{x}_{0})+d(\mathbf{x}_{0},g_{0}\mathbf{y}_{0})+d(g_{0}\mathbf{y}_{0},g_{0}\mathbf{y}_{n})=\|\mathbf{x}_{n}-\mathbf{x}_{0}\|+d_{0}+\|\mathbf{y}_{n}-\mathbf{y}_{0}\|
. Therefore,
\|{\rm Proj}_{\mathcal{G}\mathbf{y}_{n}}(\mathbf{x}_{n})-\mathbf{x}_{0}\|\leq\|{\rm Proj}_{\mathcal{G}\mathbf{y}_{n}}(\mathbf{x}_{n})-\mathbf{x}_{n}\|+\|\mathbf{x}_{n}-\mathbf{x}_{0}\|=d(\mathbf{x}_{n},\mathcal{G}\mathbf{y}_{n})+\|\mathbf{x}_{n}-\mathbf{x}_{0}\|\leq 2\|\mathbf{x}_{n}-\mathbf{x}_{0}\|+d_{0}+\|\mathbf{y}_{n}-\mathbf{y}_{0}\|\to d_{0}
, as
n\to\infty
.
Hence,
{\rm Proj}_{\mathcal{G}\mathbf{y}_{n}}(\mathbf{x}_{n})
must have a sub-sequence that converges to a point in
\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}:\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{0}\|\leq d_{0}\}\cap\mathcal{G}\mathbf{y}_{0}
, which consists of one single point
{\rm Proj}_{\mathcal{G}\mathbf{y}_{0}}(\mathbf{x}_{0})
, by the uniqueness of the projection
{\rm Proj}_{\mathcal{G}\mathbf{y}_{0}}(\mathbf{x}_{0})
. This is a contradiction to
\|{\rm Proj}_{\mathcal{G}\mathbf{y}_{n}}(\mathbf{x}_{n})-{\rm Proj}_{\mathcal{G}\mathbf{y}_{0}}(\mathbf{x}_{0})\|\geq\delta>0
for all
n
.

Now, by continuous mapping applied to
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\mapsto(\mathbf{x},f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}))
and
(\mathcal{X}_{n},R_{n}(\mathcal{X}_{n}))\overset{w}{\longrightarrow}(\mathbf{X},R(\mathbf{X}))
, we have
(\mathcal{X}_{n},S_{n}(\mathcal{X}_{n})R_{n}(\mathcal{X}_{n}))\overset{w}{\longrightarrow}(\mathbf{X},\nabla\psi(\mathbf{X}))
.
∎

Finally, we present a simple lemma which serves as a convenient tool in proving
Theorem

2.2
.

Lemma B.6
.

Suppose Assumption
2
holds. For a continuous function
f:B\to\mathbb{R}
, if we have
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|f(\mathbf{h}_{n,i})|\to\int|f(\mathbf{x})|{\rm d}\nu(\mathbf{x})
, then
f\#\nu_{n}
is uniformly integrable.

Proof.

Since
\mathbf{H}_{n}\sim\nu_{n}
converges weakly to
\nu
, by choosing an almost sure representative, we may assume

\mathbf{H}_{n}\overset{a.s.}{\longrightarrow}\mathbf{H}
, and by continuous mapping
f(\mathbf{H}_{n})\overset{a.s.}{\longrightarrow}f(\mathbf{H})
. Since the moments also converge, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}|f(\mathbf{H}_{n})|\to\mathbb{E}|f(\mathbf{H})|
, Scheffé’s lemma implies

f(\mathbf{H}_{n})
converges to
f(\mathbf{H})
in
L^{1}
. This implies
\{f(\mathbf{H}_{n})\}_{n\geq 1}
is uniformly integrable.
∎

Proof of
Theorem

2.2
.

We first state a continuity property of gradients of convex functions. Note that the gradient of convex function
\nabla\psi(\cdot)
is uniquely defined Lebesgue a.e.
In the negligible case where the gradient does not exist at some point
\mathbf{x}
, we may define
\nabla\psi(\mathbf{x})
as any subgradient at
\mathbf{x}
, i.e., an element of the subdifferential set
\partial\psi(\mathbf{x})
(see e.g.
[
73
]
).
A subgradient always exists in the interior of the domain of a convex function. This notion of
\nabla\psi(\cdot)
is
continuous
in the following sense: if
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
is a differentiable point of
\psi(\cdot)
, and
\mathbf{x}_{n}\to\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}_{n}\in\partial\psi(\mathbf{x}_{n})
, then
\mathbf{y}_{n}\to\nabla\psi(\mathbf{x})
.

Let us now prove part-1. of
Theorem

2.2
. From
Lemma

B.5
, we have with probability 1,
(\mathcal{X}_{n},R_{n}(\mathcal{X}_{n}))\overset{w}{\longrightarrow}(\mathbf{X},R(\mathbf{X}))
and
(\mathcal{X}_{n},S_{n}(\mathcal{X}_{n})R_{n}(\mathcal{X}_{n}))\overset{w}{\longrightarrow}(\mathbf{X},\nabla\psi(\mathbf{X}))
, where

\mathcal{X}_{n}\sim\mu_{n}
. As
q(\cdot)
is continuous,
R(\cdot)=q(\nabla\psi(\cdot))
is
\mu
-a.e. continuous. Hence, by the continuous mapping theorem applied to
f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\rho(R(\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{y})
,

(\mathcal{X}_{n},R_{n}(\mathcal{X}_{n}))\overset{w}{\longrightarrow}(\mathbf{X},R(\mathbf{X}))
implies

\rho(R(\mathcal{X}_{n}),R_{n}(\mathcal{X}_{n}))\overset{w}{\longrightarrow}\rho(R(\mathbf{X}),R(\mathbf{X}))=0
.
Note that
R_{n}(\mathcal{X}_{n})\sim\nu_{n}
.

\mathbb{E}\rho(\mathbf{H}_{n},\mathbf{0})\to\mathbb{E}\rho(\mathbf{H},\mathbf{0})
together with
Lemma

B.6
implies

\rho(R_{n}(\mathcal{X}_{n}),\mathbf{0})
is uniformly integrable.
Hence,
\rho(R(\mathbf{X}),\mathbf{0})
, as the weak limit of
\rho(R_{n}(\mathcal{X}_{n}),\mathbf{0})
, is integrable by Fatou’s lemma.
The strong law of large numbers then implies

\mathbb{E}\rho(R(\mathcal{X}_{n}),\mathbf{0})
converges to
\mathbb{E}\rho(R(\mathbf{X}),\mathbf{0})
. Since
\rho(R(\mathcal{X}_{n}),\mathbf{0})
converges in distribution to
\rho(R(\mathbf{X}),\mathbf{0})
,
Scheffé’s lemma again implies
\rho(R(\mathcal{X}_{n}),\mathbf{0})
is uniformly integrable.
Hence,
\rho(R(\mathcal{X}_{n}),R_{n}(\mathcal{X}_{n}))\lesssim\rho(R(\mathcal{X}_{n}),\mathbf{0})+\rho(R_{n}(\mathcal{X}_{n}),\mathbf{0})
is uniformly integrable and its mean converges to the mean of 0, i.e.,

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\rho(R(\mathbf{X}_{i}),R_{n}(\mathbf{X}_{i}))\to 0.

Next, we show the convergence of signs.
By
Lemma

B.4
and the continuous mapping theorem applied to
f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\widetilde{\rho}(S(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}),S(\mathbf{x},R(\mathbf{x})))
,

(\mathcal{X}_{n},R_{n}(\mathcal{X}_{n}))\overset{w}{\longrightarrow}(\mathbf{X},R(\mathbf{X}))
implies

\widetilde{\rho}(S(\mathcal{X}_{n},R_{n}(\mathcal{X}_{n})),S(\mathcal{X}_{n},R(\mathcal{X}_{n})))\overset{w}{\longrightarrow}0
.
As
\widetilde{\rho}
is continuous and the signs are bounded, we can apply the bounded convergence theorem which implies that the expectation also converges, i.e.,

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widetilde{\rho}(S(\mathbf{X}_{i},R_{n}(\mathbf{X}_{i})),S(\mathbf{X}_{i},R(\mathbf{X}_{i})))\to 0\qquad\mbox{as }n\to\infty.

Since
S(\mathbf{X}_{i},R_{n}(\mathbf{X}_{i}))=S_{n}(\mathbf{X}_{i})

∎

B.5
Proof of

Under the assumptions of

and

transports
\mu_{S}
to
{\rm Uniform}(-1,1)

and appears as the gradient of a convex function.
Hence from

3.2
, the GWSR test is consistent against
\mathbb{E}\left[{\rm sign}(X)F_{|X|}(|X|)\right]\neq 0
. Suppose
f
is the density function of
X

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{\rm sign}(X)F_{|X|}(|X|)\right]>0

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\int_{0}^{\infty}\left[\frac{f(x)}{f(x)+f(-x)}-\frac{f(-x)}{f(x)+f(-x)}\right]F_{|X|}(x)\left[f(x)+f(-x)\right]{\rm d}x>0

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\int_{0}^{\infty}\left[f(x)-f(-x)\right]F_{|X|}(x){\rm d}x>0

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\int_{0}^{\infty}\left[f(x)-f(-x)\right]\int_{0}^{x}\left[f(y)+f(-y)\right]{\rm d}y{\rm d}x>0

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\int_{0\leq y\leq x}\left[f(x)-f(-x)\right]\left[f(y)+f(-y)\right]{\rm d}x{\rm d}y>0

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\int_{0\leq y\leq x}f(x)\left[f(y)+f(-y)\right]{\rm d}x{\rm d}y>\int_{0\leq y\leq x}f(-x)\left[f(y)+f(-y)\right]{\rm d}x{\rm d}y

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\mathbb{P}(-X_{1}\leq X_{2}\leq X_{1})>\mathbb{P}(X_{1}\leq X_{2}\leq-X_{1})

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\mathbb{P}(X_{1}+X_{2}\geq 0,X_{2}\leq X_{1})>\mathbb{P}(X_{1}+X_{2}\leq 0,X_{1}\leq X_{2})

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{P}(X_{1}+X_{2}\geq 0)>\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{P}(X_{1}+X_{2}\leq 0)

which concludes the proof.

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\textrm{H}_{0}}\left[\frac{\nabla f(\mathbf{X})}{f(\mathbf{X})}\frac{\nabla f(\mathbf{X})^{\top}}{f(\mathbf{X})}\right]{\bm{\xi}}=I_{{\bm{\theta}}_{0}}{\bm{\xi}},

\displaystyle\Sigma_{\rm ERD}

\displaystyle={\rm Var}_{\rm H_{0}}\left(J(\nabla\psi(\mathbf{X}))\right)={\rm Var}_{\rm H_{0}}\left(\frac{\nabla f(\mathbf{X})}{f(\mathbf{X})}\right)=I_{{\bm{\theta}}_{0}}.

Hence, under
{\rm H}_{1}
,

\mathbf{W}_{n}\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}N\left(I_{{\bm{\theta}}_{0}}{\bm{\xi}},I_{{\bm{\theta}}_{0}}\right)
, and therefore

\mathbb{P}_{{\bm{\theta}}_{0}+\frac{{\bm{\xi}}}{\sqrt{n}}}\left(\mathbf{W}_{n}^{\top}\Sigma_{\rm ERD}^{-1}\mathbf{W}_{n}\geq\chi_{p}^{2}(\alpha)\right)\to\mathbb{P}\left(\|N(I_{{\bm{\theta}}_{0}}^{1/2}{\bm{\xi}},I_{p})\|^{2}\geq\chi_{p}^{2}(\alpha)\right).

Combining with (
B.13
), we have

\inf_{{\bm{\xi}}:\|I^{1/2}_{{\bm{\theta}}_{0}}{\bm{\xi}}\|=c}\lim_{n\to\infty}\pi_{n}\left(\frac{{\bm{\xi}}}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\leq\inf_{{\bm{\xi}}:\|I^{1/2}_{{\bm{\theta}}_{0}}{\bm{\xi}}\|=c}\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}_{{\bm{\theta}}_{0}+\frac{{\bm{\xi}}}{\sqrt{n}}}\left(\mathbf{W}_{n}^{\top}\Sigma_{\rm ERD}^{-1}\mathbf{W}_{n}\geq\chi_{p}^{2}(\alpha)\right),

which yields the desired result.

2.
Recall that
\pi_{n}(\cdot)
is the power function of a sequence of level
\alpha
tests for (
3.8
). By
Van der Vaart, 1998
,

(B.14)

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\pi_{n}\left({\bm{\theta}}_{0}+\frac{{\bm{\xi}}}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\leq 1-\Phi\left(z_{\alpha}-\frac{{\bm{\xi}}^{\top}\nabla\zeta({\bm{\theta}}_{0})}{\sqrt{\nabla\zeta({\bm{\theta}}_{0})^{\top}I_{{{\bm{\theta}}_{0}}}^{-1}\nabla\zeta({\bm{\theta}}_{0})}}\right),

where
\Phi(\cdot)
is the cdf of a standard normal distribution, and
z_{\alpha}
satisfies
\Phi(z_{\alpha})=1-\alpha
.

From the proof of part-1.,
\mathbf{W}_{n}\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}N\left(I_{{\bm{\theta}}_{0}}{\bm{\xi}},I_{{\bm{\theta}}_{0}}\right)
under
{\bm{\theta}}={{\bm{\theta}}_{0}+\frac{{\bm{\xi}}}{\sqrt{n}}}
, and therefore

\nabla\zeta({\bm{\theta}}_{0})^{\top}I_{{\bm{\theta}}_{0}}^{-1}\mathbf{W}_{n}\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}N\big({\bm{\xi}}^{\top}\nabla\zeta({\bm{\theta}}_{0}),\nabla\zeta({\bm{\theta}}_{0})^{\top}I_{{{\bm{\theta}}_{0}}}^{-1}\nabla\zeta({\bm{\theta}}_{0})\big).

This implies

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}_{{\bm{\theta}}_{0}+\frac{{\bm{\xi}}}{\sqrt{n}}}\left(\frac{\nabla\zeta({\bm{\theta}}_{0})^{\top}I_{{\bm{\theta}}_{0}}^{-1}\mathbf{W}_{n}}{\sqrt{\nabla\zeta({\bm{\theta}}_{0})^{\top}I_{{{\bm{\theta}}_{0}}}^{-1}\nabla\zeta({\bm{\theta}}_{0})}}\geq z_{\alpha}\right)=1-\Phi\left(z_{\alpha}-\frac{{\bm{\xi}}^{\top}\nabla\zeta({\bm{\theta}}_{0})}{\sqrt{\nabla\zeta({\bm{\theta}}_{0})^{\top}I_{{{\bm{\theta}}_{0}}}^{-1}\nabla\zeta({\bm{\theta}}_{0})}}\right),

which attains the upper bound in (
B.14
).

∎

Appendix C
Some Auxiliary Results

In the following, we state and prove some auxiliary results. Results of a similar form can be found in
McCann, 1995
and
Villani, 2009
.

Proposition C.1
.

If
c(\cdot,\cdot)
is continuous, then there exists
\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)
with a
c
-cyclically monotone support (see
Definition

2.5
).

Proof.

Let
\mu_{n}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{\mathbf{x}_{i}}
and
\nu_{n}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{\mathbf{y}_{i}}
be such that
\mu_{n}\overset{w}{\longrightarrow}\mu
and
\nu_{n}\overset{w}{\longrightarrow}\nu
(for example, we can draw
\mathbf{x}_{i}
i.i.d. from
\mu
and
\mathbf{y}_{i}
i.i.d. from
\nu
).
For each
n
, solve

(C.1)

\min_{a_{ij}\geq 0}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}a_{ij}c(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{y}_{j}),\qquad{\rm\ s.t.\ }\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{ij}=1,i=1,\ldots,n,{\rm\ and\ }\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{ij}=1,j=1,\ldots,n

to obtain a minimizer
(a_{ij}^{n})
. In fact, this is the assignment problem
[
78
,
11
]
and there exists an optimal solution such that
a_{ij}^{n}\in\{0,1\}
for all
i,j
.
Let
\pi_{n}:=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}a_{ij}^{n}\delta_{(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{y}_{j})}
.
Then
\pi_{n}
is supported on
S
, the set of all couples
(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{y}_{j})
such that
a_{ij}^{n}>0
.
To show that
\pi_{n}\in\Pi(\mu_{n},\nu_{n})
has a
c
-cyclically monotone support, we argue by contradiction. Suppose that there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}
and
(\mathbf{x}_{i_{1}},\mathbf{y}_{i_{1}}),\ldots,(\mathbf{x}_{i_{N}},\mathbf{y}_{i_{N}})
in
S
such that (with
\mathbf{y}_{i_{N+1}}=\mathbf{y}_{i_{1}}
)

c(\mathbf{x}_{i_{1}},\mathbf{y}_{i_{2}})+c(\mathbf{x}_{i_{2}},\mathbf{y}_{i_{3}})+\ldots+c(\mathbf{x}_{i_{N}},\mathbf{y}_{i_{N+1}})<c(\mathbf{x}_{i_{1}},\mathbf{y}_{i_{1}})+\ldots+c(\mathbf{x}_{i_{N}},\mathbf{y}_{i_{N}}).

Define

\widetilde{\pi}_{n}:=\pi_{n}+\varepsilon\sum_{l=1}^{N}\left(\delta_{(\mathbf{x}_{i_{l}},\mathbf{y}_{i_{l+1}})}-\delta_{(\mathbf{x}_{i_{l}},\mathbf{y}_{i_{l}})}\right).

Suppose
\widetilde{\pi}_{n}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\widetilde{a}_{ij}^{n}\delta_{(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{y}_{j})}
. Then, for a sufficiently small
\varepsilon>0
,

\widetilde{a}_{ij}^{n}\geq 0
, and
(\widetilde{a}_{ij}^{n})
has a smaller cost than
({a}_{ij}^{n})
in (
C.1
), which yields a contradiction.

Hence,
\pi_{n}\in\Pi(\mu_{n},\nu_{n})
has a
c
-cyclically monotone support. The weak convergence of
\mu_{n}
and
\nu_{n}
implies that
\{\mu_{n}\}
and
\{\nu_{n}\}
are tight, and thus
\{\pi_{n}\}
must be tight (
Villani, 2009
). By Prokhorov’s theorem, there is a subsequence
\{\pi_{n_{k}}\}_{k\geq 1}
which converges weakly to some probability measure
\pi
.
Since
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\mapsto\mathbf{x}
and
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\mapsto\mathbf{y}
are continuous, by continuous mapping theorem,
\pi
has marginals
\mu
and
\nu
. For each
n
, the
c
-cyclically monotonicity of
\pi_{n}
implies that for all
N\in\mathbb{N}
and
\pi_{n}^{\otimes N}
-almost all
(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{y}_{1}),\ldots,(\mathbf{x}_{N},\mathbf{y}_{N})
, we have (with
\mathbf{y}_{N+1}=\mathbf{y}_{1}
)

(C.2)

\sum_{i=1}^{N}c(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{y}_{i})\leq\sum_{i=1}^{N}c(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{y}_{i+1}).

In other words,
\pi_{n}^{\otimes N}
is concentrated on the set
\mathcal{C}(N)
of all
((\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{y}_{1}),\ldots,(\mathbf{x}_{N},\mathbf{y}_{N}))\in(\mathbb{R}^{p}\times\mathbb{R}^{p})^{N}
satisfying (
C.2
). Since
c(\cdot,\cdot)
is continuous,
\mathcal{C}(N)
is a closed set, so the weak limit

\pi^{\otimes N}
of
\pi^{\otimes N}_{n_{k}}
is also concentrated on
\mathcal{C}(N)
by Portmanteau theorem. Let
\Gamma
be the support of
\pi
. Then
\Gamma^{N}=({\rm supp}(\pi))^{N}={\rm supp}(\pi^{\otimes N})\subset\mathcal{C}(N)
. Since this holds true for all
N
,
\Gamma
is
c
-cyclically monotone.
∎

Proposition C.2
.

If
c(\cdot,\cdot)
is continuous and
\pi\in\Gamma(\mu,\nu)
has
c
-cyclically monotone support, then there exists a
c
-convex function
\psi
such that
\partial_{c}\psi
(see
Definition

B.1
) contains the support of
\pi
.

Proof.

Let
\Gamma
denote the support of
\pi
. Fix
(\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{y}_{0})\in\Gamma
. Define

\displaystyle\psi(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle:=\sup_{m\in\mathbb{N}}\sup\big\{[c(\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{y}_{0})-c(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{y}_{0})]+[c(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{y}_{1})-c(\mathbf{x}_{2},\mathbf{y}_{1})]

\displaystyle+\ldots+[c(\mathbf{x}_{m},\mathbf{y}_{m})-c(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{m})]:(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{y}_{1}),\ldots,(\mathbf{x}_{m},\mathbf{y}_{m})\in\Gamma\big\}.

From the definition, with
m=1
and
(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{y}_{1})=(\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{y}_{0})
, we have

\psi(\mathbf{x}_{0})\geq 0
. On the other hand, the
c
-cyclical monotonicity of
\Gamma
implies that

\psi(\mathbf{x}_{0})\leq 0
, and hence
\psi(\mathbf{x}_{0})=0
.
By renaming
\mathbf{y}_{m}
as
\mathbf{y}
,

\displaystyle\psi(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\sup_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\sup_{m\in\mathbb{N}}\sup_{(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{y}_{1}),\ldots,(\mathbf{x}_{m-1},\mathbf{y}_{m-1}),\mathbf{x}_{m}}\big\{[c(\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{y}_{0})-c(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{y}_{0})]+[c(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{y}_{1})-c(\mathbf{x}_{2},\mathbf{y}_{1})]

\displaystyle+\ldots+[c(\mathbf{x}_{m},\mathbf{y})-c(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})]:(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{y}_{1}),\ldots,(\mathbf{x}_{m},\mathbf{y})\in\Gamma\big\}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}[\zeta(\mathbf{y})-c(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})],

where
\zeta(\mathbf{y}):=-\infty
if there does not exist
\mathbf{x}_{m}
such that
(\mathbf{x}_{m},\mathbf{y})\in\Gamma
, and otherwise

\displaystyle\zeta(\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle:=\sup_{m\in\mathbb{N}}\sup_{(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{y}_{1}),\ldots,(\mathbf{x}_{m-1},\mathbf{y}_{m-1}),\mathbf{x}_{m}}\big\{[c(\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{y}_{0})-c(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{y}_{0})]+[c(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{y}_{1})-c(\mathbf{x}_{2},\mathbf{y}_{1})]

\displaystyle+\ldots+[c(\mathbf{x}_{m},\mathbf{y})-c(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})]:(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{y}_{1}),\ldots,(\mathbf{x}_{m},\mathbf{y})\in\Gamma\big\}.

Thus
\psi(\cdot)
is a
c
-convex function.
For any
(\overline{\mathbf{x}},\overline{\mathbf{y}})\in\Gamma
, with
(\mathbf{x}_{m},\mathbf{y}_{m})=(\overline{\mathbf{x}},\overline{\mathbf{y}})
in the definition of
\psi
,

\displaystyle\psi(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle\geq\sup_{m\geq 2}\sup_{(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{y}_{1}),\ldots,(\mathbf{x}_{m-1},\mathbf{y}_{m-1})\in\Gamma}\big\{[c(\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{y}_{0})-c(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{y}_{0})]+[c(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{y}_{1})-c(\mathbf{x}_{2},\mathbf{y}_{1})]

\displaystyle+\ldots+[c(\mathbf{x}_{m-1},\mathbf{y}_{m-1})-c(\overline{\mathbf{x}},\mathbf{y}_{m-1})]+[c(\overline{\mathbf{x}},\overline{\mathbf{y}})-c(\mathbf{x},\overline{\mathbf{y}})]\big\}

\displaystyle=\psi(\overline{\mathbf{x}})+[c(\overline{\mathbf{x}},\overline{\mathbf{y}})-c(\mathbf{x},\overline{\mathbf{y}})].

Hence,
\psi(\mathbf{x})+c(\mathbf{x},\overline{\mathbf{y}})\geq\psi(\overline{\mathbf{x}})+c(\overline{\mathbf{x}},\overline{\mathbf{y}})
. Taking the infimum over
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, we deduce that

\psi^{c}(\overline{\mathbf{y}})\geq\psi(\overline{\mathbf{x}})+c(\overline{\mathbf{x}},\overline{\mathbf{y}})
. Since the reverse inequality always holds from the definition of
\psi^{c}
(
Definition

B.1
), we have
\psi^{c}(\overline{\mathbf{y}})=\psi(\overline{\mathbf{x}})+c(\overline{\mathbf{x}},\overline{\mathbf{y}})
. This means that
(\overline{\mathbf{x}},\overline{\mathbf{y}})\in\partial_{c}\psi
, and so
\Gamma\subset\partial_{c}\psi
.
∎
