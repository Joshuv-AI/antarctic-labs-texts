Identifiability and Characterization of Transmon Qutrits Through Bayesian Experimental Design
Sohail Reddy

Abstract
Robust control of a quantum system is essential to utilize the current noisy quantum hardware to their full potential, such as quantum algorithms. To achieve such a goal, systematic search for an optimal control for any given experiment is essential. Design of optimal control pulses require accurate numerical models, and therefore, accurate characterization of the system parameters. We present an online, Bayesian approach for quantum characterization of qutrit systems which automatically and systematically identifies the optimal experiments that provide maximum information on the system parameters, thereby greatly reducing the number of experiments that need to be performed on the quantum testbed. Unlike most characterization protocols that provide point-estimates of the parameters, the proposed approach is able to estimate their probability distribution. The applicability of the Bayesian experimental design technique was demonstrated on test problems where each experiment was defined by a parameterized control pulse. In addition to this, we also presented an approach for iterative pulse extension which is robust under uncertainties in transition frequencies and coherence times, and shot noise, despite being initialized with wide uninformative priors. Furthermore, we provide a mathematical proof of the theoretical identifiability of the model parameters and present conditions on the quantum state under which the parameters are identifiable. The proof and conditions for identifiability are presented for both closed and open quantum systems using the Schroedinger equation and the Lindblad master equation respectively.

Such classical-quantum algorithms typically require accurate modeling of the quantum systems. These numerical models can be parameterized by a few quantities of the QPUs, thus, accurate determination of these parameters is paramount for high-fidelity simulation of the QPU. However, the noisy behavior of current devices poses a great challenge to identifying the testbed characteristics, particularly, providing point-estimates of these parameters. Under such circumstance, posing the characterization problem within a probabilistic Bayesian framework is ideal. Regardless of the approach used for characterization, the identifiability of the model parameters must first be established. Although crucial, such
priori

Furthermore, they establish a set of conditions on the experiments/measurements that make parameters identifiable, thereby informing the design of experiments. One such approach involves characterization (i.e., parameter estimation) through online Bayesian experimental design (BExD). The BExD technique uses classical optimization techniques to identify experimental parameters (or experiments) that provide maximum
information
about the parameters being identified and iteratively updates the prior distribution of the parameters. Estimating complete probability distributions provide insight on the correlation between parameters and allow for design of control pulses that are robust to slight variances in parameters. Although demonstrated on a single, qubit problems, its performance for characterizing quantum systems with higher energy levels has yet to be thoroughly investigated. This work aims to address this scarcity and apply the BExD technique for characterizing multi-level quantum systems. Quantum Mechanical Model

Consider an open quantum system whose dynamics are modeled by the Lindblad master equation
[
27
]

\dfrac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}=-i\left[H,\rho\right]+L(\rho)

(1)

where
\rho\in\mathbb{C}^{3\times 3}
is the density matrix,
[\cdot,\cdot]
is the commutator,
H(t)\in\mathbb{C}^{3\times 3}
is the Hamiltonian governing the unitary dynamics,
L(\rho)
is the Lindbladian governing the dissipative dynamics. The Hamiltonian in the lab frame consists of the time-independent system Hamiltonian
H_{s}
and a time-dependent control Hamiltonian
H_{c}(t)

\displaystyle H(t):=\underbrace{\omega a^{\dagger}a-\dfrac{\chi}{2}a^{\dagger}a^{\dagger}aa}_{H_{s}}+\underbrace{f(t)(a+a^{\dagger})}_{H_{c}(t)}

(2)

where
a
and
a^{\dagger}
are the lowering and raising operators,
\omega
is the 0-1 transition frequency,
\chi
is the anharmonicity, and
f(t)=2\mathrm{Re}(\Omega(t)e^{iw_{d}t})
is the control function with drive frequency
\omega_{d}
and amplitude
\Omega(t):=p(t)+iq(t)
where
p(t),q(t)\in\mathbb{R}
. Under the rotating wave approximation (RWA), we obtain the Hamiltonian in the rotating frame

\displaystyle H(t)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\left(\omega-\omega_{d}\right)a^{\dagger}a-\dfrac{\chi}{2}a^{\dagger}a^{\dagger}aa}_{H_{s}}+\underbrace{\Omega(t)a+\overline{\Omega}(t)a^{\dagger}}_{H_{c}(t)}

(3)

\displaystyle=\Delta a^{\dagger}a-\dfrac{\chi}{2}a^{\dagger}a^{\dagger}aa+p(t)\left(a+a^{\dagger}\right)+iq(t)\left(a-a^{\dagger}\right)

where the rotation frequency in the RWA is taken to be the drive frequency
\omega_{d}
, and
\Delta:=\omega-\omega_{d}
is the detuning between the qubit 0-1 transition and the drive frequencies. This model was experimentally validated for a transmon qutrit at Quantum Device and Integration Testbed (QuDIT) at LLNL
[
28
]
. The decoherence is introduced through the Lindblad jump operators for decay (
L_{1}:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{T_{1}}}a
) and dephasing (
L_{2}:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{T_{2}}}a^{\dagger}a
) where
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
are the decay and dephasing times, respectively. The Lindbladian is then written as

L(\rho):=\sum_{i=1}^{2}L_{i}\rho L^{\dagger}_{i}-\dfrac{1}{2}\left\{L^{\dagger}_{i}L_{i},~\rho\right\}

(4)

where
\left\{\cdot,\cdot\right\}
is the anti-commutator. The control pulse is modeled as a set of piece-wise constant pulses, each with
p(t)
,
q(t)
, and duration
\Delta t
. The pulse resolution can be increased by increasing the number of segments. In general, real experiments require 1 ns resolution to play pulses on hardware. This generalization can be seen as a bang-bang control where each application uses a different amplitude and duration for the control. Equation
1
is vectorized

\dfrac{\partial\tilde{\rho}}{\partial t}=\mathcal{L}(t)\tilde{\rho}

with
\tilde{\rho}=\mathrm{vec}(\rho)
and evolved until final time
T
using matrix exponentiation by means of the Padé approximant

\tilde{\rho}(T)=\prod_{i=1}^{N_{T}}\exp(\mathcal{L}(t_{i})\Delta t_{i})\tilde{\rho}(0)

(5)

where
N_{T}
is the number of piecewise-constant segments and the Liouvillian superoperator
\mathcal{L}(t)
is defined as

\mathcal{L}(t):=-i\left(I\otimes H(t)-H^{\dagger}(t)\otimes I\right)+\sum_{i=1}^{2}L_{i}\otimes L^{\dagger}_{i}-\dfrac{1}{2}\left(I\otimes L^{\dagger}_{i}L_{i}+L^{\dagger}_{i}L_{i}\otimes I\right)

(6)

and
I
is rank-3 identity matrix. Given a density matrix, the probability of measuring the system in a particular state
y
is given as

\mathrm{Pr}{}(y|\rho)=\mathrm{Tr}\left(M_{y}\rho\right)

(7)

where
M_{y}
is the measurement operator corresponding to the observable
y
. In this work, we assume projective measurements in the
z
-basis and take
M_{y}=\left|e_{y}\right\rangle\left\langle e_{y}\right|,~y=0,1,2
where
\left|e_{i}\right\rangle
is the
i^{th}
canonical unit vector; then each element of
\mathrm{Pr}{}(\mathrm{\boldsymbol{y}}|\rho)=\mathrm{diag}(\rho)
denotes the probability of measuring that state and will be used to compute the likelihood in the subsequent sections.

3
Identifiability of Quantum Mechanical Models

Before characterizing a system described by a mathematical model, it is imperative to determine the global and unique (termed
global structural
) identifiability of the parameters in such a model. Some techniques for determining the identifiability of a model include those based on Laplace transforms
[
29
]
, similarity transformation
[
30
]
, generating series
[
31
]
, differential algebra
[
32
,
33
]
and the Taylor series approach
[
34
]
. To determine the identifiability of the models of open and closed quantum systems, we will employ the Taylor series approach
[
34
,
35
,
36
]
. The approach relies on the observations being a unique analytic function of time, hence their derivatives must also be unique. The uniqueness of this representation will guarantee the structural identifiability of the model. Consider an observable/measurable quantity
y(t;\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}})
that is dependent on model parameters
\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
. The Taylor series approach expands this quantity about time
t_{0}

y_{i}\left(t;\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\right)\approx\sum_{k=0}^{m}\dfrac{t^{k}}{k!}a_{i,k}(\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}}),\quad\mathrm{with}~a_{i,k}=\dfrac{d^{k}}{d^{k}t}y_{i}\left(t_{0};\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\right)

(8)

A sufficient condition for global structural identifiability is given by the uniqueness of the expansion coefficients

a_{i,k}(\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}})=a_{i,k}(\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\star}),~~k=1,...,m;i=0,1,2\Rightarrow\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\star}

(9)

where
m
is the smallest positive integer such that the resulting system is solvable for the parameters. We emphasize that the order of expansion here is used only to establish identifiability conditions. This minimum order of expansion is not known a
priori
but as will be shown, first order and second order expansions are sufficient for closed and open quantum systems, respectively. We should mention that the identifiability conditions are derived for models in the lab-frame and, therefore, do not make any approximations such as neglecting the rapidly oscillating terms in the RWA.

3.1
Model Identifiability: Schroedinger Equation

Consider a closed quantum system modeled by the Schroedinger equation
\dot{\left|\psi\right\rangle}=-iH\left|\psi\right\rangle
, driven by a
known
pulse
f(t)
with a pulse amplitude
\Omega(t)
and the probability of measuring state
\left|i\right\rangle
by
y_{i}(t;\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}}):=\mathrm{Pr}{}(\left|i\right\rangle,t;\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}})=\left\langle\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\psi}}(t;\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}})\right|M_{i}\left|\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\psi}}(t;\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}})\right\rangle
. The pulse amplitude
\Omega(t)
is taken to be constant for all times. An alternative parameterization for
\Omega(t)
can also be used, in which case, the resulting Taylor series expansion will yield additional pulse dependent terms that simply scale the coefficients
a_{i,k}
in Eq. (
8
) and, therefore, do not alter the identifiability conditions. We expand the measurement about
t_{0}=0
with
\mathcal{F}:=f(t_{0})
, which yields the first three expansion coefficients

\displaystyle a_{i,0}(\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}})

\displaystyle=\left\langle\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\psi}}(t_{0})\right|M_{i}\left|\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\psi}}(t_{0})\right\rangle

(10)

\displaystyle a_{i,1}(\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}})

\displaystyle=i\left\langle\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\psi}}(t_{0})\right|\left[H,M_{i}\right]\left|\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\psi}}(t_{0})\right\rangle

\displaystyle a_{i,2}(\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}})

\displaystyle=\left\langle\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\psi}}(t_{0})\right|\left[\left[H,M_{i}\right],H\right]\left|\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\psi}}(t_{0})\right\rangle

We note the zeroth order terms (
a_{i,0}(\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}}),~i=0,1,2
) do not yield any information on the parameters of the model. Defining
\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\psi}}(t_{0})=\left\{\psi_{0},\psi_{1},\psi_{2}\right\}\in\mathbb{C}^{3}
, the first order terms can be written as

\displaystyle a_{0,1}(\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}})

\displaystyle=2\mathcal{F}~\mathrm{Im}(\overline{\psi_{0}}\psi_{1})

(11)

\displaystyle a_{1,1}(\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}})

\displaystyle=2\mathcal{F}\left(\mathrm{Im}(\psi_{0}\overline{\psi_{1}})+\sqrt{2}\mathrm{Im}(\overline{\psi_{1}}\psi_{2})\right)

\displaystyle a_{2,1}(\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}})

\displaystyle=2\sqrt{2}\mathcal{F}~\mathrm{Im}(\psi_{1}\overline{\psi_{2}})

which uniquely define
\mathcal{F}
when
\mathrm{Im}(\psi_{i}\overline{\psi_{j}})\neq 0
for
i\neq j
. The second order terms are given by

\displaystyle a_{0,2}(\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}})

\displaystyle=2\mathcal{F}^{2}\left(\left|{\psi_{1}}\right|^{2}-\sqrt{2}\mathrm{Re}(\psi_{0}\overline{\psi_{2}})-\left|{\psi_{0}}\right|^{2}\right)-2\omega\mathcal{F}\left(\mathrm{Re}(\psi_{0}\overline{\psi_{1}})\right)

(12)

\displaystyle a_{1,2}(\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}})

\displaystyle=2\mathcal{F}^{2}\left(\left|{\psi_{0}}\right|^{2}-3\left|{\psi_{1}}\right|^{2}+2\left|{\psi_{2}}\right|^{2}+2\sqrt{2}\mathrm{Re}(\psi_{0}\overline{\psi_{2}})\right)+2\omega\mathcal{F}\left(\mathrm{Re}(\psi_{0}\overline{\psi_{1}})-\sqrt{2}\mathrm{Re}(\psi_{1}\overline{\psi_{2}})\right)

\displaystyle+2\sqrt{2}\chi\mathcal{F}\left(\mathrm{Re}(\psi_{1}\overline{\psi_{2}})\right)

\displaystyle a_{2,2}(\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}})

\displaystyle=2\mathcal{F}^{2}\left(2\left|{\psi_{1}}\right|^{2}-2\left|{\psi_{2}}\right|^{2}-\sqrt{2}\mathrm{Re}(\psi_{0}\overline{\psi_{2}})\right)+2\sqrt{2}\omega\mathcal{F}\left(\mathrm{Re}(\psi_{1}\overline{\psi_{2}})\right)-2\sqrt{2}\chi\mathcal{F}\left(\mathrm{Re}(\psi_{1}\overline{\psi_{2}})\right)

Collecting all the known and identified terms in a right-hand-side
\mathcal{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, we can write the identifiability conditions in matrix form

\displaystyle\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{Re}(\psi_{0}\overline{\psi_{1}})&0\\
\mathrm{Re}(\psi_{0}\overline{\psi_{1}})-\sqrt{2}\mathrm{Re}(\psi_{1}\overline{\psi_{2}})&\sqrt{2}\mathrm{Re}(\psi_{1}\overline{\psi_{2}})\\
\mathrm{Re}(\psi_{1}\overline{\psi_{2}})&-\mathrm{Re}(\psi_{1}\overline{\psi_{2}})\end{pmatrix}}_{A_{c}}\begin{Bmatrix}\omega\\
\chi\end{Bmatrix}=\mathcal{S}

(13)

Notice that the rows of
A_{c}
are linearly dependent. Since the drive
\mathcal{F}
is known (or can be identified as indicated by the first order terms), the sufficient conditions to identify
\omega
and
\chi
are
\mathrm{Re}(\psi_{0}\overline{\psi_{1}})\neq 0
and
\mathrm{Re}(\psi_{1}\overline{\psi_{2}})\neq 0
, respectively. Furthermore, the sufficient conditions for identifiability of
\chi
requires that the conditions for
\omega
also be satisfied. Hence, the parameters are globally and uniquely identifiable and the model is
globally structrually identifiable
, when the control pulse is able to prepare a state that satisfies these conditions.

3.2
Model Identifiability: Lindblad Master Equation

We now determine the identifiability of the Lindblad master equation. Consider an open quantum system defined by Eq. (
1
) with observables defined as
y_{i}(t;\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}})=\mathrm{Tr}\left(M_{i}\rho(t;\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}})\right)
where
M_{i}
is a measurement operator defined in Sec.
2
. As before, we let
\mathcal{F}:=f(t_{0})
and expand the observable about
t_{0}=0
(Eq. (
8
)) with coefficients

\displaystyle a_{i,0}(\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}\left(M_{i}\rho(t_{0})\right)

(14)

\displaystyle a_{i,1}(\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}})

\displaystyle=-i\mathrm{Tr}\left(M_{i}\left[H(t),\rho(t_{0})\right]\right)+\sum_{k=1}^{2}\mathrm{Tr}\left(M_{i}\left(L_{k}\rho(t_{0})L^{\dagger}_{k}-\dfrac{1}{2}\left\{L^{\dagger}_{k}L_{k},~\rho(t_{0})\right\}\right)\right)

\displaystyle a_{i,2}(\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}})

\displaystyle=-i\mathrm{Tr}\left(M_{i}\left[H(t),\dot{\rho}(t_{0})\right]\right)+\sum_{k=1}^{2}\mathrm{Tr}\left(M_{i}\left(L_{k}\dot{\rho}(t_{0})L^{\dagger}_{k}-\dfrac{1}{2}\left\{L^{\dagger}_{k}L_{k},~\dot{\rho}(t_{0})\right\}\right)\right)

The second order terms
a_{i,2}(\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}})
are obtained by substituting the right-hand side of Eq. (
1
) into
a_{i,2}(\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}})
. Letting

\rho_{0}:=\rho(t_{0})=\begin{pmatrix}\rho_{00}&\overline{\rho_{10}}&\overline{\rho_{20}}\\
\rho_{10}&\rho_{11}&\overline{\rho_{21}}\\
\rho_{20}&\rho_{21}&\rho_{22}\end{pmatrix}

(15)

Letting
\tau_{1}=\frac{1}{T_{1}}
and
\tau_{2}=\frac{1}{T_{2}}
, the first order terms can be expanded as

\displaystyle a_{0,1}(\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}})

\displaystyle=\tau_{1}\rho_{11}-i\mathcal{F}\left(\rho_{10}-\overline{\rho_{10}}\right)=\tau_{1}\rho_{11}+2\mathcal{F}~\mathrm{Im}(\rho_{10})

(16)

\displaystyle a_{1,1}(\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}})

\displaystyle=\tau_{1}\left(2\rho_{22}-\rho_{11}\right)+i\mathcal{F}\left(\rho_{10}-\overline{\rho_{10}}+\sqrt{2}\left(\overline{\rho_{21}}-\rho_{21}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\tau_{1}\left(2\rho_{22}-\rho_{11}\right)+2\mathcal{F}\left(\sqrt{2}\mathrm{Im}(\rho_{21})-\mathrm{Im}(\rho_{10})\right)

\displaystyle a_{2,1}(\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}})

\displaystyle=-2\tau_{1}\rho_{22}+i\sqrt{2}\mathcal{F}\left(\rho_{21}-\overline{\rho_{21}}\right)=-2\tau_{1}\rho_{22}-2\sqrt{2}\mathcal{F}~\mathrm{Im}(\rho_{21})

Note that
a_{2,1}(\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}})=-(a_{0,1}(\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}})+a_{1,1}(\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}}))
. We can construct the linear system using
a_{0,1}(\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}})
and
a_{2,1}(\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}})

\displaystyle\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}2\mathrm{Im}(\rho_{10})&\rho_{11}\\
\sqrt{2}\mathrm{Im}(\rho_{21})&\rho_{22}\end{pmatrix}}_{A_{1}}\begin{Bmatrix}\mathcal{F}\\
\tau_{1}\end{Bmatrix}=\mathcal{S}_{1}

(17)

Hence, if
A_{1}
is non-singular, the first order terms prove global and unique identifiability of
\mathcal{F}
and
\tau_{1}
. The second order terms are given as

\displaystyle a_{0,2}(\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}})=

\displaystyle-2\omega\mathcal{F}~\mathrm{Re}(\rho_{10})+\tau_{1}\left(\rho_{11}+\mathcal{F}\left(2\sqrt{2}\mathrm{Im}(\rho_{21})-\mathrm{Im}(\rho_{10})\right)\right)-\tau_{2}\mathcal{F}~\mathrm{Im}(\rho_{10})

(18)

\displaystyle+2\mathcal{F}^{2}\left(\rho_{11}-\rho_{00}-\sqrt{2}\mathrm{Re}(\rho_{3})\right)

\displaystyle a_{1,2}(\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}})=

\displaystyle 2\omega\mathcal{F}~\left(\mathrm{Re}(\rho_{10})-\sqrt{2}\mathrm{Re}(\rho_{21})\right)+2\sqrt{2}\mathcal{F}~\chi\mathrm{Re}(\rho_{21})

\displaystyle+\tau_{1}\left(2\rho_{22}+\tau_{1}\left(\rho_{11}-2\rho_{22}\right)+\mathcal{F}\left(3\mathrm{Im}(\rho_{10})-7\sqrt{2}\mathrm{Im}(\rho_{21})\right)\right)

\displaystyle+\tau_{2}\underbrace{\left(\rho_{11}-\tau_{1}\left(2\rho_{22}-\rho_{11}\right)+\mathcal{F}\left(3\mathrm{Im}(\rho_{10})-3\sqrt{2}\mathrm{Im}(\rho_{21})\right)\right)}_{A_{12}}

\displaystyle+2\mathcal{F}^{2}\left(\rho_{1}-3\rho_{11}+2\rho_{22}+2\sqrt{2}\mathrm{Re}(\rho_{20})\right)

\displaystyle a_{2,2}(\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}})=

\displaystyle 2\sqrt{2}\omega\mathcal{F}~\mathrm{Re}(\rho_{21})-2\sqrt{2}\mathcal{F}~\chi\mathrm{Re}(\rho_{21})+\tau_{1}\left(4\tau_{1}\rho_{22}+7\sqrt{2}\mathcal{F}~\mathrm{Im}(\rho_{21})\right)

\displaystyle+\tau_{2}\underbrace{\left(4\rho_{22}\left(1+2\tau_{1}\right)+9\sqrt{2}\mathcal{F}~\mathrm{Im}(\rho_{21})\right)}_{A_{22}}+2\mathcal{F}^{2}\left(2\rho_{11}-2\rho_{22}-\sqrt{2}\mathrm{Re}(\rho_{20})\right)

\tau_{1}

\displaystyle\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}2\mathcal{F}\mathrm{Re}(\rho_{10})&0&-\mathcal{F}\mathrm{Im}(\rho_{10})\\

and
\tau_{1}

since
y_{i}(t)=\mathrm{Tr}\left(UM_{i}U^{\dagger}\rho(t)\right)=\mathrm{Tr}\left(M_{i}U^{\dagger}\rho(t)U\right)=\mathrm{Tr}\left(M_{i}\widetilde{\rho}(t)\right)
where the conditions on the quantum state now apply in the transformed basis. These conditions show that the model parameters are identifiable by measuring the population in a single basis without needing to estimate the full density matrix via state or process tomography, which can be expensive.

4
Bayesian Experimental Design

Having demonstrated the identifiability of the model parameters is dependent on the quantum state, and therefore, the control applied (or experiment performed), we present an approach for automatic, online design of experiments that allow the parameters to be identified. Consider a set of model parameters
\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
to be identified, set of measurements
\mathrm{\boldsymbol{y}}
from an experiment parameterized by a set of controls (i.e. controllable parameters)
\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
. We begin with the Bayes’ theorem

\mathrm{Pr}{}(\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}}|\mathrm{\boldsymbol{y}};\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\xi}})=\dfrac{\mathrm{Pr}{}(\mathrm{\boldsymbol{y}}|\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}};\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\mathrm{Pr}{}(\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}})}{\mathrm{Pr}{}(\mathrm{\boldsymbol{y}}|\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\xi}})}

(20)

where
\mathrm{Pr}{}(\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}})
,
\mathrm{Pr}{}(\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}}|\mathrm{\boldsymbol{y}};\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\xi}})
, and
\mathrm{Pr}{}(\mathrm{\boldsymbol{y}}|\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}};\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\xi}})
are the prior, posterior distribution and likelihood, respectively. The normalizing constant is the marginalized probability distribution over parameter space (also referred to as the evidence) and is defined as

\mathrm{Pr}{}(\mathrm{\boldsymbol{y}}|\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\xi}})=\int_{\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}{\mathrm{Pr}{}(\mathrm{\boldsymbol{y}}|\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}};\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\mathrm{Pr}{}(\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}})~d\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}

(21)

The Bayesian experimental design (BExD) framework aims to estimate the probability distributions of the model parameters (i.e. the posterior distribution) by identifying the optimal controls of an experiment that provides maximum information on the parameters. The measure of effectiveness of the experiment (parameterized by
\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
) if the outcome
y
is observed is called the utility function
U(y|\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\xi}})
. Hence, an optimal experiment is one that maximizes the expectation of this utility function over all possible observations

\displaystyle U(\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\xi}})=\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{Pr}{}(\mathrm{\boldsymbol{y}}|\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\xi}})}\left[U(\mathrm{\boldsymbol{y}}|\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathrm{\boldsymbol{y}}}{U(\mathrm{\boldsymbol{y}}|\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\mathrm{Pr}{}(\mathrm{\boldsymbol{y}}|\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\xi}})~d\mathrm{\boldsymbol{y}}}

(22)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}{\int_{\mathrm{\boldsymbol{y}}}{U(\mathrm{\boldsymbol{y}}|\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\mathrm{Pr}{}(\mathrm{\boldsymbol{y}}|\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}};\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\mathrm{Pr}{}(\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}})~d\mathrm{\boldsymbol{y}}}~d\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}

Typical choices for the utility function include: 1) negative variance of the posterior, 2) Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence and 3) Wasserstein distance; other utility functions are also possible
[
37
,
38
]
. Minimizing the variance seeks to minimize the spread about the mean, thereby reducing the uncertainty in the model parameters. Meanwhile, maximizing the KL-divergence and Wasserstein distance functions seeks controls that maximize the distance between the prior and the posterior distributions, thereby yielding the largest update over the prior distribution.

For all such utility functions, the integrals in Eq. (
22
) are often analytically intractable and the
curse of dimensionality
renders standard numerical integration inefficient. Hence, the integral is often approximated using Monte Carlo sampling by drawing
N
samples
\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{i}\sim\mathrm{Pr}{}(\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}})

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\right]\approx\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_{i}\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{i}

(23)

where
w_{i}
are the weights and
\sum_{i}w_{i}=1
. These MC samples are also used to construct an approximate discrete representation of the PDF

\mathrm{Pr}{}(\mathrm{\boldsymbol{y}}|\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}})\approx\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_{i}(\mathrm{\boldsymbol{y}})\delta\left(\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{i}\right)

(24)

where
\delta(\cdot)
is the Dirac delta function. The search for such optimal control
\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\star}
requires the solution to an optimization problem Eq. (
25
) that can be high-dimensional and typically non-convex and multi-modal
[
39
]
.

\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\star}:=\arg\max_{\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\in\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\Xi}}}~~U(\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\xi}})

(25)

For these reasons, we solve the optimization problem using a non-gradient based differential evolution algorithm. The applicability of the evolutionary method for optimization of utility function in BExD was demonstrated by Hamada
et al.
[
40
]
. Schoneberger
et al.
[
39
]
demonstrated the robustness of non-gradient based optimization algorithm for optimal experimental design. An experiment is then performed using the optimal control
\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\star}
, and the measurements
\mathrm{\boldsymbol{y}}
are used to construct the likelihood and update the posterior distribution at the
n^{th}
epoch/iteration as

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}{}(\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}}|\mathrm{\boldsymbol{y}}_{n};\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{n}^{\star})

\displaystyle\propto\mathrm{Pr}{}(\mathrm{\boldsymbol{y}}_{n}|\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}};\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{n}^{\star})\mathrm{Pr}{}(\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}}|\mathrm{\boldsymbol{y}}_{n-1};\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{n-1}^{\star})

(26)

\displaystyle\propto\mathrm{Pr}{}(\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}})\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{Pr}{}(\mathrm{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}|\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}};\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{i}^{\star})

To reduce computational cost associated with evaluating the likelihood in regions of low posterior density, the Monte Carlo samples are resampled to redistribute the particles to regions of high posterior density. We make use of the resampling algorithm proposed by Liu and West
[
41
]
. The expectation of the inferred posterior
\widehat{\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}:=\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{Pr}{}(\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}}|\mathrm{\boldsymbol{y}})}\left[\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\right]
yields the unbiased estimator for the model parameters. Figure
1
presents the complete workflow for Bayesian experimental design.

4.1
Utility Function: Kullback-Leibler (KL) Divergence

The Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence is a measure of distance between two probability distributions and is defined as

\displaystyle D_{KL}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}{\mathrm{Pr}{}(\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}}|\mathrm{\boldsymbol{y}};\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\log\left(\dfrac{\mathrm{Pr}{}(\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}}|\mathrm{\boldsymbol{y}};\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\xi}})}{\mathrm{Pr}{}(\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}})}\right)~d\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}

(27)

Note that the KL divergence is not symmetric in the two distributions. At the
n^{th}
epoch,
\mathrm{Pr}{}(\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}}|\mathrm{\boldsymbol{y}};\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\equiv\mathrm{Pr}{}(\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}}|\mathrm{\boldsymbol{y}}_{n};\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\xi}})
is the posterior (i.e. Eq. (
26
) for some control vector
\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
) and
\mathrm{Pr}{}(\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}})\equiv\mathrm{Pr}{}(\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}}|\mathrm{\boldsymbol{y}}_{n-1};\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\xi^{\star}_{n-1}}})
is now the prior (i.e. posterior from previous epoch). Taking the expectation of the KL divergence over all possible outcomes
y
yields an expression for the
mutual information

\displaystyle U(\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\xi}})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathrm{\boldsymbol{y}}}{\mathrm{Pr}{}(\mathrm{\boldsymbol{y}}|\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\int_{\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}{\mathrm{Pr}{}(\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}}|\mathrm{\boldsymbol{y}};\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\log\left(\dfrac{\mathrm{Pr}{}(\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}}|\mathrm{\boldsymbol{y}};\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\xi}})}{\mathrm{Pr}{}(\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}})}\right)~d\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}d\mathrm{\boldsymbol{y}}}

(28)

Hence, maximizing the mutual information amounts to identifying control parameters that provide the maximum
information
gain over the prior distribution.

4.2
Likelihood Function

For clarity of presentation, we denote by
\widetilde{\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}
the true distribution of the parameters (i.e. the parameters of the quantum testbed). For a total of
N
independent shots, let
y_{i}\sim\mathrm{Pr}{}(\mathrm{\boldsymbol{y}}|\widetilde{\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}}};\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\xi}})
be a possible outcome of a shot (i.e., the state measured for a shot) after applying control
\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
,
n_{i}
the number of times
y_{i}
is observed with
\sum_{i}n_{i}=N
. Then, the likelihood can be modeled by a multinomial distribution
[
17
,
38
]
defined by

\mathrm{Pr}{}(\mathrm{\boldsymbol{y}}|\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}};\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\xi}},N)=\begin{pmatrix}N\\
n_{1},n_{2},\ldots,n_{M}\end{pmatrix}\prod_{i=1}^{N}\mathrm{Pr}{}(y_{i}|\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}};\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\xi}})

(29)

where
\begin{pmatrix}N\\
n_{1},n_{2},\ldots,n_{M}\end{pmatrix}:=\dfrac{N!}{\prod_{i=1}^{\left|{\mathbb{S}}\right|}n_{i}!}
is the multinomial coefficient,
y_{i}\sim\mathrm{Pr}{}(y_{i}|\widetilde{\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}}};\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\xi}})=\mathrm{Pr}{}(y_{i}|\rho(\widetilde{\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}}};\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\xi}}))\approx\dfrac{n_{i}}{N}
is the probability, estimated from experiments, of observing
y_{i}
and
\mathrm{Pr}{}(y_{i}|\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}};\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\xi}})
is the probability of observing
y_{i}
computed using Eq. (
7
) and the numerical model for a given
\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
and
\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
. It should be mentioned that the multinomial coefficient can be omitted since the weights in Eq. (
24
) are renormalized at each epoch. For a large number of draws
N
, this omission may lead to numerical underflow as the resulting expression would produce near zero probabilities, particularly when
\mathrm{Pr}{}(y_{i}|\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}};\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\xi}})
is small. Alternatively, under certain assumptions, the multinomial distribution asymptotically tends to a Gaussian distribution
[
42
]

\mathrm{Pr}{}(\mathrm{\boldsymbol{y}}|\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}};\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\xi}})=\mathcal{N}\left(\mathrm{Pr}{}(\mathrm{\boldsymbol{y}}|\widetilde{\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}}};\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\xi}}),\Sigma\right)

(30)

where
\Sigma
is the covariance that accounts for errors between the numerical model and the ‘true’ model (i.e. experiment). This model of the likelihood has been used extensively in Bayesian experimental design
[
37
,
43
,
38
]
and in Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods. The exact form of
\Sigma
is not known a priori and must be estimated. Let
\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Y}}}(\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}};\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\xi}})=\left\{\mathrm{Pr}{}(y_{i}|\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}};\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\xi}}),i=1,\ldots,N\right\}
and
\widetilde{\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Y}}}}(\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\xi}})=\left\{\mathrm{Pr}{}(y_{i}|\widetilde{\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}}};\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\xi}}),i=1,\ldots,N\right\}
be vectors of probabilities computed using the numerical model and experimentally measured, respectively, after applying control
\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
. Then, we estimate
\Sigma
by means of Monte Carlo sampling over the prior for
\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
with
N_{\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}
samples and random control
\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
with
N_{\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}
samples. The sample covariance matrix is then computed as

\Sigma=\dfrac{1}{N_{\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}\dfrac{1}{N_{\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}}\left(\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Y}}}(\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{i};\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{j})-\widetilde{\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Y}}}}(\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{j})\right)\left(\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Y}}}(\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{i};\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{j})-\widetilde{\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Y}}}}(\mathrm{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{j})\right)^{T}

(31)

Conclusion

The conventional techniques for quantum characterization, whether probabilistic or deterministic, rely on measurements from a set of experiments that are designed
a-priori
. These measurements are then used to perform an offline calibration of the mathematical model to reconstruct the measurements from the experimental testbed. In most cases, the theoretical identifiability of the model parameters is neglected. Even when the identifiability conditions are known, manual design of experiments satisfying such conditions is not trivial. Hence, this work presented an automatic, online Bayesian framework for characterizing open qutrit systems described by the Lindblad master equation. The approach automatically identifies optimal robust experiments that provide maximum information on the model parameters to be estimated. Furthermore, we proved the theoretical identifiability of the Schroedinger and Lindblad equations given a measured observable, and derived the sufficient conditions for the quantum state to establish the global, unique identifiability of the model parameters.

We demonstrated our approach to identify the frequencies and decoherence parameters on a numerical test problem subjected to both parameter uncertainty and shot noise. The characterization study demonstrated that different parameters exhibit different rates of convergence and are dependent on both the pulse parameterization and magnitude of shot noise. It was seen that accurate estimation of the frequencies required shortened pulse sequences whereas estimation of decoherence parameters required longer pulse sequences. We introduced an iterative pulse extension approach to improve the convergence of all estimates. The results show that the Bayesian experiment design framework with iterative pulse extensions is able to accurately estimate all four parameters in only 500 epochs (i.e. 500 different experiments). The approach is better able to estimate the distribution of the system frequencies than the decoherence parameters. Desipite this, the BExD approach yielded robust estimates of the parameters such that the mean prediction error and variances were on the order of
10^{-4}
. The results showed that the online BExD approach is able to design robust, optimal experiments to accurately characterize the quantum system described by the Lindblad master equation. Ongoing and future work includes experimentally demonstrating the technique on multi-qubit systems.
