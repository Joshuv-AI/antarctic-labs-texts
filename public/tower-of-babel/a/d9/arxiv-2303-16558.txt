On the number of equilibria of the replicator-mutator dynamics for noisy social dilemmas
L. Chen, C. Deng, M. H. Duong, T. A. Han

Abstract
In this paper, we consider the replicator-mutator dynamics for pairwise social dilemmas where the payoff entries are random variables. The randomness is incorporated to take into account the uncertainty, which is inevitable in practical applications and may arise from different sources such as lack of data for measuring the outcomes, noisy and rapidly changing environments, as well as unavoidable human estimate errors. We analytically and numerically compute the probability that the replicator-mutator dynamics has a given number of equilibria for four classes of pairwise social dilemmas (Prisoner’s Dilemma, Snow-Drift Game, Stag-Hunt Game and Harmony Game). As a result, we characterise the qualitative behaviour of such probabilities as a function of the mutation rate. Our results clearly show the influence of the mutation rate and the uncertainty in the payoff matrix definition on the number of equilibria in these games. Overall, our analysis has provided novel theoretical contributions to the understanding of the impact of uncertainty on the behavioural diversity in a complex dynamical system.

2.1
The replicator-mutator dynamics

The replicator-mutator equation describes the evolution dynamics in a population of different strategies being in co-presence, where selection and mutation are both captured. It is an well established mathematical framework that integrates the unavoidable mutation observed in various biological and social settings
[
29
,
57
,
62
,

,
32
,

]
.
This framework has been utilised in many application domains, including evolution of collective behaviours
[

,

]
, social networks dynamics
[

]
, language evolution

[
53
]
, population genetics
[
42
]
, and autocatalytic reaction networks

[

]
.

We consider an infinitely large population consisting of
n
different strategies
S_{1},\cdots,S_{n}
. Their frequencies are denoted, respectively, by
x_{1},\cdots,x_{n}
, where
\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}=1
. These strategies undergo selection where their frequency,
S_{i}
, is determined by its fitness (i.e. average payoff),
f_{i}
, obtained through interactions with others in the population. Such interactions happen within randomly selected pairs of individuals playing a social dilemma game (see details below).
By means of mutation, individuals in the population might change their strategy to another randomly selected strategy, given by the so-called mutation matrix:
Q=(q_{ji}),j,i\in\{1,\cdots,n\}
. Here,
q_{ji}
stands for the probability of an
S_{j}
individual changing its strategy to
S_{i}
, satisfying that

\sum_{j=1}^{n}q_{ji}=1,\quad\forall 1\leq i\leq n.

Denoting vector
\textbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n})
and
\bar{f}(\textbf{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}f_{i}(\textbf{x})
the population’s average fitness, we can describe the replicator-mutator equation as follows
[
49
,
47
,
48
,
56
]

\dot{x}_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}x_{j}f_{j}(\textbf{x})q_{ji}-x_{i}\bar{f}(\textbf{x}),\qquad 1\leq i\leq n.

(1)

It is important to note that the replicator dynamics can be reproduced from (
1
) with
q=0
(i.e. no mutation). This paper investigates the equilibrium points of the replicator-mutator dynamics, which are solutions in
[0,1]^{n}
of the following system of equations

g_{i}(\mathbf{x})=0,\quad 1\leq i\leq n,

where
g_{i}(\textbf{x})
,
i=1,\ldots,n
, denotes the right-hand side of (
1
)

g_{i}(\textbf{x}):=\sum_{j=1}^{n}x_{j}f_{j}(\textbf{x})q_{ji}-x_{i}\bar{f}(\textbf{x}).

In general, knowing equilibrium points in a dynamical system allows us to study states where different strategies might co-exist in the population, indicating the possibility of polymorphism.

2.2
Pairwise social dilemmas

Now let us consider a pairwise game with two strategies

-x\Big(a_{11}x^{2}+(a_{12}+a_{21})x(1-x)+a_{22}(1-x)^{2}\Big).

Using the identities

, Equation (

\displaystyle\dot{x}

\displaystyle=\Big(a_{12}+a_{21}-a_{11}-a_{22}\Big)x^{3}+\Big(a_{11}-a_{21}-2(a_{12}-a_{22})+q(a_{22}+a_{12}-a_{11}-a_{21})\Big)x^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\Big(a_{12}-a_{22}+q(a_{21}-a_{12}-2a_{22})\Big)x+qa_{22}.

(3)

Next, we consider a well-established parameterisation of pairwise social dilemmas, for random game analysis
[
24
,
30
,
28
]
. In these games, players can choose to cooperate or defect in each interaction. Mutual cooperation (punishment) would lead to a payoff

(

(iii)

As motivated in the introduction, to be more realistic and to capture the various possible uncertainty, we will consider random games, where

or

or both T and S, are random variables.

Suppose that

3.1
Equilibrium points

By simplifying the right hand side of (
3
), equilibria of a social dilemma game are roots in the interval
[0,1]
of the following cubic equation

\displaystyle\Big(T+S-1\Big)x^{3}+\Big(1-T-2S+q(S-1-T)\Big)x^{2}+\Big(S+q(T-S)\Big)x=0.

(5)

It follows that
x=0
is always an equilibrium. If
q=0
, (
5
) reduces to

(T+S-1)x^{3}+(1-T-2S)x^{2}+Sx=0,

which has solutions

x=0,\quad x=1,\quad x^{*}=\frac{S}{S+T-1}.

Note that for SH and SD games
x^{*}\in(0,1)
, thus it is always an (internal) equilibrium. On the other hand, for PD-games and H-games,
x^{*}\not\in(0,1)
, thus it is not an equilibrium.

If
q=\frac{1}{2}
then the above equation has two solutions
x_{1}=\frac{1}{2}
and
x_{2}=\frac{T+S}{T+S-1}
. In PD, SD and H games,
x_{2}\not\in(0,1)
, thus they have two equilibria
x_{0}=0
and
x_{1}=\frac{1}{2}
. In the SH game: if
T+S<0
then the game has three equilibria
x_{0}=0,x_{1}=\frac{1}{2}
and
0<x_{2}<1
; if
T+S\geq 0
then the game has only two equilibria
x_{0}=0,x_{1}=\frac{1}{2}
.

Now we consider the case
0<q<\frac{1}{2}
. For non-zero equilibrium points we solve the following quadratic equation

h(x):=(T+S-1)x^{2}+(1-T-2S+q(S-1-T))x+S+q(T-S)=:ax^{2}+bx+c=0,

(6)

where we define

a=T+S-1,\quad b=1-T-2S+q(S-1-T),\quad c=S+q(T-S).

(7)

Set
t:=\frac{x}{1-x}
, then we obtain

\frac{h(x)}{(1-x)^{2}}=(a+b+c)t^{2}+(b+2c)t+c=-qt^{2}+(-q-a+c)t+c:=g(t).

Thus, an equilibrium point of a social dilemma can be found from a positive solution of the following quadratic equation

g(t)=-qt^{2}+(-q-a+c)t+c=-qt^{2}-((1-q)(T-1)+qS)t+S+q(T-S).

(8)

Using this relation, subsequently we provide numerical simulations and analytical results for the probability
p_{k}
that each of the game mentioned above has a certain number,
k\in\{1,2,3\}
, of equilibria. For the quadratic function
g(t)
, the discriminant is given by

\Delta=(q+a-c)^{2}+4qc,

(9)

where
a
and
c
are defined in terms of
T
and
S
in (
7
). The number of positive roots of
g
is characterized in the following three cases:

(a)

g
has no positive roots, which happens when
g
has no real roots (
\Delta<0
) or
g
has only negative roots (
\Delta\geq 0,t_{1}\leq 0,t_{2}\leq 0
). In this case, the replicator-mutator equation has only one equilibrium
x=0
.

(b)

g
has one positive root, which happens when
g
has a positive double root (
\Delta=0,t_{1}=t_{2}>0
) or when
g
has one positive and one negative root (
\Delta>0,t_{1}t_{2}<0
). In this case, the replicator-mutator equation has two equilibria.

(c)

g
has two positive roots (
\Delta>0,t_{1}+t_{2}>0,t_{1}t_{2}>0
), thus the replicator-mutator equation has three equilibria.

Using (
7
) we can write

\displaystyle\Delta

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(q+a-c)^{2}+4qc

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle[q+T+S-1-(S+qT-qS)]^{2}+4q[S+q(T-S)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1-q)^{2}T^{2}+2[(q-q^{2})S+q^{2}+2q-1]T+q^{2}S^{2}+2q(1-q)S+(q-1)^{2},

which is a quadratic function of

whose discriminant is given by

2q^{3}-q^{2}-(2q^{3}-3q^{2}+q)S=q(2q-1)[q-(q-1)S].

Therefore

(i)

If

, then
\Delta=0
has two distinct solutions.

(ii)

If
2q^{3}-q^{2}-(2q^{3}-3q^{2}+q)S=0
, then
\Delta=0
has only one solution.

(iii)

If
2q^{3}-q^{2}-(2q^{3}-3q^{2}+q)S<0
, then
\Delta=0
has no real solutions.

If
(q-1)S\leq q
, then

has solutions

\displaystyle T_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{-((1-q)qS+q^{2}+2q-1)-2\sqrt{2q^{3}-q^{2}-(2q^{3}-3q^{2}+q)S}}{(1-q)^{2}},

(10)

\displaystyle T_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{-((1-q)qS+q^{2}+2q-1)+2\sqrt{2q^{3}-q^{2}-(2q^{3}-3q^{2}+q)S}}{(1-q)^{2}}.

(11)

In addition, we also have the following:

\displaystyle t_{1}+t_{2}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{q+a-c}{-q}=\dfrac{q+T+S-1-(S+qT-qS)}{-q}=\dfrac{q+T-qT+qS-1}{-q},

\displaystyle t_{1}t_{2}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{c}{-q}=\dfrac{S+qT-qS}{-q}.

Define

s_{1}=\dfrac{-q(1-q)}{1-2q},\quad s_{2}=\dfrac{q}{q-1},\quad\text{and}\quad T_{3}=\dfrac{(q-1)S}{q}.

(12)

Note that since

3.2
Probability that the replicator-mutator has two equilibria

We first compute the probability
p_{2}
that the replicator-mutator equation has two equilibria, which amounts to compute the probability that
\Delta=0,t_{1}=t_{2}>0
or
\Delta>0,t_{1}t_{2}<0
:

p_{2}=\mathbb{P}\Big\{\Delta=0,t_{1}=t_{2}>0\}+\mathbb{P}\Big\{\Delta>0,t_{1}t_{2}<0\}=p_{21}+p_{22}.

We note that when
\Delta=0
has no solution,
\mathbb{P}\{\Delta=0,t_{1}=t_{2}>0\}=0
. When
\Delta=0
has two solutions
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
(
T_{1}
may equal to
T_{2}
here, but it does not matter), then
\mathbb{P}\{\Delta=0,t_{1}=t_{2}>0\}
is given by

P(T=T_{1})+P(T=T_{2}).

Since in both cases (Gaussian and uniform distributions)
T
follows a continuous distribution, we have
P(T=T_{1})=P(T=T_{2})=0
. It follows that
p_{21}=0
. Thus

p_{2}=p_{22}=\mathbb{P}\{\Delta>0,t_{1}t_{2}<0\},

which is equivalent to the probability that
T>T_{3}
.
Since
T\sim\mathcal{N}(T_{0},\sigma^{2})
, we obtain

\displaystyle p_{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(T>T_{3})

(13)

\displaystyle=\int_{T_{3}}^{\infty}\dfrac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\Big({\dfrac{-(x-T_{0})^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}\Big)dx

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{\frac{T_{3}-T_{0}}{\sigma\sqrt{2}}}^{\infty}\exp(-t^{2})dt\quad(\text{by changing of variable}~t=\frac{x-T_{0}}{\sigma\sqrt{2}})

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp(-t^{2})dt-\int_{0}^{\frac{T_{3}-T_{0}}{\sigma\sqrt{2}}}\exp(-t^{2})dt\right]

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2}-\dfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{erf}\Big(\dfrac{T_{3}-T_{0}}{\sigma\sqrt{2}}\Big)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2}-\dfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{erf}\Big(\dfrac{(q-1)S/q-T_{0}}{\sigma\sqrt{2}}\Big),

where
\mathrm{erf}(\cdot)
is the error function

\mathrm{erf}(z):=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{0}^{z}e^{-t^{2}}\,dt.

The following lemma presents some interesting qualitative properties of
p_{2}
.

Lemma 1
.

As a function of
q
, the probability
p_{2}
is decreasing in SD and H games, but is increasing in PD and SH games. As a consequence, for SD and H games, it holds that

p_{2}\geq\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{erf}\Big(-\frac{S+T_{0}}{\sigma\sqrt{2}}\Big)>\frac{1}{2}.

(14)

In addition,

\lim_{q\rightarrow 0}p_{2}=\begin{cases}1,\quad\text{in SD and H games},\\
0,\quad\text{in PD and SH games}.\end{cases}

It is worth mentioning that the monotonicity and the small mutation limits above are independent of the specific values of
S,T_{0}
and
\sigma
. The lower bound (
14
) indicates that in SD and H games,
p_{2}
is always dominant over
p_{1}
and
p_{3}
since
p_{1}+p_{2}+p_{3}=1
. For instance, in the SD game, with the specific values
S=0.5,T_{0}=1.5,\sigma=1
we obtain

p_{2}\geq\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{erf}(-\sqrt{2})\approx 0.97725,

and in the H game with the specific values
S=0.5,T=0.5,\sigma=1
, we obtain

p_{2}\geq\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{erf}(-1/\sqrt{2})\approx 0.8413.

Proof.

Let
z=\dfrac{(q-1)S/q-T_{0}}{\sigma\sqrt{2}}
, then

p_{2}=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{erf}{(z)}.

Therefore, using the chain rule and the fact that
\frac{d}{dz}\mathrm{erf}(z)=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}e^{-z^{2}}
, we obtain

\frac{dp_{2}}{dq}=\frac{dp_{2}}{dz}\frac{dz}{dq}=-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}e^{-z^{2}}\frac{S}{\sigma q^{2}\sqrt{2}}.

Since
S
is positive in SD and H games but is negative in PD and SH games,
\frac{dp_{2}}{dq}
is negative in SD and H games but is positive in PD and SH games. Thus the probability
p_{2}
, as a function of
q
, is decreasing in SD and H games, but is increasing in PD and SH games. As a consequence, for SD and H games, we have

p_{2}\geq p_{2}|_{q=1/2}=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{erf}\Big(-\frac{S+T_{0}}{\sigma\sqrt{2}}\Big).

Now we establish the limit of
p_{2}
as
q
tends to
0
. Since
0<q<\frac{1}{2}
we have

\lim_{q\rightarrow 0}z=\begin{cases}-\infty,\quad\text{if}~~S>0,\\
+\infty,\quad\text{if}~~S<0.\end{cases}

Together with the fact that

\lim_{z\rightarrow\pm\infty}\mathrm{erf}(z)=\pm 1,

we obtain

\lim_{q\rightarrow 0}p_{2}=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\lim_{q\rightarrow 0}\mathrm{erf}(z)=\begin{cases}1,\quad\text{if}~~S>0,\\
0,\quad\text{if}~~S<0.\end{cases}

Applying this to the underlying games, we achieve

\lim_{q\rightarrow 0}p_{2}=\begin{cases}1,\quad\text{in SD and H games},\\
0,\quad\text{in PD and SH games}.\end{cases}

∎

3.3
Probability that the replicator-mutator has three equilibria

Now we compute the probability
p_{3}
that the replicator-mutator equation has three equilibria. We consider the following cases, depending on the ordering between
S
and
s_{1}<s_{2}
.

(C1)
S>q/(q-1)=s_{2}

Then, recalling that when
0<q<1/2
, then
2q^{3}-q^{2}-(2q^{3}-3q^{2}+q)S=q(2q-1)[q-(q-1)S]<0
. Therefore,
\Delta>0
always holds. It also holds that
T_{3}<\dfrac{1-(S+1)q}{1-q}
. Therefore,

\Big\{T:\Delta>0,T<\dfrac{1-(S+1)q}{1-q},T<T_{3}\Big\}=\{T:T<T_{3}\}.

Thus, when
(q-1)S>q
, the probability
p_{3}
that the replicator-mutator dynamics has three equilibria is given by

\displaystyle p_{3}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(T<T_{3})=1-\mathbb{P}(T>T_{3})=1-p_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{erf}\Big(\frac{T_{3}-T_{0}}{\sigma\sqrt{2}}\Big).

It implies that in this case,
p_{1}=0
. Note that, SD and H games always satisfy this case since
s_{2}=\frac{q}{q-1}<0<S
. Thus, we obtain for SD and H games:

p_{1}=0,\quad p_{2}=\dfrac{1}{2}-\dfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{erf}\Big(\dfrac{(q-1)S/q-T_{0}}{\sigma\sqrt{2}}\Big),\quad p_{3}=1-p_{2}=\dfrac{1}{2}+\dfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{erf}\Big(\dfrac{(q-1)S/q-T_{0}}{\sigma\sqrt{2}}\Big).

(C2)
S=s_{2}

Then
2q^{3}-q^{2}-(2q^{3}-3q^{2}+q)S=0
, we obtain that

T\neq-\dfrac{q(1-q)S+q^{2}+2q-1}{(1-q)^{2}}\Leftrightarrow\Delta>0.

(15)

A direct computation shows that
-\dfrac{q(1-q)S+q^{2}+2q-1}{(1-q)^{2}}<T_{3}
. Thus, the probability that the replicator-mutator equation has 3 equilibria is again
p_{3}=\mathbb{P}(T<T_{3})
. Thus we obtain the same results as in the previous case.

(C3)
s_{1}<S<s_{2}

In this case since
2q^{3}-q^{2}-(2q^{3}-3q^{2}+q)S>0
,
\Delta=0
has two distinct solutions
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
. In addition, we have

\dfrac{1-(S+1)q}{1-q}>T_{3}>T_{2}.

It implies that the probability of the replicator-mutator equation having three equilibria is

\displaystyle p_{3}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(T<T_{1})+\mathbb{P}(T_{2}<T<T_{3})

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{T_{1}}\dfrac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\Big({\dfrac{-(x-T_{0})^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}\Big)dx+\int_{T_{2}}^{T_{3}}\dfrac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\Big({\dfrac{-(x-T_{0})^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}\Big)dx

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left[1+\mathrm{erf}\Big(\frac{T_{1}-T_{0}}{\sigma\sqrt{2}}\Big)+\mathrm{erf}\Big(\frac{T_{3}-T_{0}}{\sigma\sqrt{2}}\Big)-\mathrm{erf}\Big(\frac{T_{2}-T_{0}}{\sigma\sqrt{2}}\Big)\right].

(C4)
S\leq s_{1}

Similarly to case
(C3)
,
\Delta=0
has two distinct solutions
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
and

\dfrac{1-(S+1)q}{1-q}\leq T_{3},\quad T_{1}\leq\dfrac{1-(S+1)q}{1-q}\leq T_{2}.

Thus the probability that replicator-mutator equation has three equilibria is given by

\displaystyle p_{3}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(T<T_{1})

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{T_{1}}\dfrac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\Big({\dfrac{-(x-T_{0})^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}\Big)dx

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{erf}\Big(\frac{T_{1}-T_{0}}{\sigma\sqrt{2}}\Big).

Bringing all cases together, we obtain, in PD and SH games, the probability that the replicator-mutator equation has three equilibria is

p_{3}=\begin{cases}\dfrac{1}{2}\left[1+\mathrm{erf}\Big(\dfrac{T_{1}-T_{0}}{\sigma\sqrt{2}}\Big)\right],\quad\text{if}\quad S\leq s_{1},\\
\\
\dfrac{1}{2}\left[1+\mathrm{erf}\Big(\dfrac{T_{1}-T_{0}}{\sigma\sqrt{2}}\Big)+\mathrm{erf}\Big(\dfrac{T_{3}-T_{0}}{\sigma\sqrt{2}}\Big)-\mathrm{erf}\Big(\dfrac{T_{2}-T_{0}}{\sigma\sqrt{2}}\Big)\right],\quad\text{if}\quad s_{1}<S<s_{2},\\
\\
\dfrac{1}{2}\left[1+\mathrm{erf}\Big(\dfrac{T_{3}-T_{0}}{\sigma\sqrt{2}}\Big)\right],\quad\text{if}\quad S\geq s_{2}.\end{cases}

The three cases in the formula above can be written in terms of
q
as follows

p_{3}=\begin{cases}\dfrac{1}{2}\left[1+\mathrm{erf}\Big(\dfrac{T_{1}-T_{0}}{\sigma\sqrt{2}}\Big)\right],\quad\text{if}\quad 0\leq q\leq q_{1},\\
\\
\dfrac{1}{2}\left[1+\mathrm{erf}\Big(\dfrac{T_{1}-T_{0}}{\sigma\sqrt{2}}\Big)+\mathrm{erf}\Big(\dfrac{T_{3}-T_{0}}{\sigma\sqrt{2}}\Big)-\mathrm{erf}\Big(\dfrac{T_{2}-T_{0}}{\sigma\sqrt{2}}\Big)\right],\quad\text{if}\quad q_{1}<q<q_{2},\\
\\
\dfrac{1}{2}\left[1+\mathrm{erf}\Big(\dfrac{T_{3}-T_{0}}{\sigma\sqrt{2}}\Big)\right],\quad\text{if}\quad q_{2}\leq q\leq\frac{1}{2},\end{cases}

where

We note that when

.

The probability that the replicator-mutator has two equilibria

In this section, we compute the probability that the replicator-mutator has 2 equilibria, which amounts to computing the probability that the quadratic polynomial

(C1)

\Delta=0
,
q+a-c<0
.

Let
\hat{T}:=T-1
and

\displaystyle X(\hat{T},S)

\displaystyle:=q+a-c=(1-q)(T_{0}-1)+qS,

(16)

\displaystyle Y(\hat{T},S)

\displaystyle:=c-q=q(T_{0}-1)+(1-q)S.

(17)

Then, the equation
\Delta=0
can be rewritten as

X^{2}+4qY=-4q^{2}.

(18)

Substituting
X
and

in (
16
) and (

Since

\Delta>0
and

From (

have two real solutions,

To proceed, we need to compare

\displaystyle\frac{\frac{-qT_{0}}{1-q}}{S_{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{-qT_{0}}{1-q}\frac{q}{(q-1)(T_{0}+1)+2\sqrt{T_{0}-2qT_{0}}}

6
Summary and Outlook

In this paper, we have studied pair-wise social dilemmas where the payoff entries are random variables. The randomness is necessary to capture the uncertainty that is unavoidable in practical applications, which may come from different sources, both subjective and objective, such as lack of data, fluctuating environment as well as human estimate errors. We have focused on four important social dilemma games, namely Prisoner’s Dilemma, the Snow-Drift game, the Stag-Hunt game and the Harmony game. For each game, we have analytically computed, and numerically validated, the probability that the replicator-mutator dynamics has a certain number of equilibria, studying their qualitative behaviour as a function of the mutation rate.
Our results have clearly shown that the mutation rate and randomness from the payoff matrix have a strong impact on the equilibrium outcomes. Thus, our analysis has provided novel theoretical contributions to the understanding of the impact of uncertainty on the behavioural diversity in a complex dynamical system.

One natural and challenging problem for future work is to generalize the equilibrium analysis of the present work to multi-player and multi-strategy games where the payoff entries satisfy more complex conditions. Another direction is to trajectorially characterize statistical properties of the full dynamical systems.
