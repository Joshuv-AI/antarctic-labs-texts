Mean-preserving Rounding Integer-valued ARMA Models
Christian H. Weiß, Fukang Zhu

Abstract
In the past four decades, research on count time series has made significant progress, but research on ℤ \mathbb{Z} -valued time series is relatively rare. Existing ℤ \mathbb{Z} -valued models are mainly of autoregressive structure, where the use of the rounding operator is very natural. Because of the discontinuity of the rounding operator, the formulation of the corresponding model identifiability conditions and the computation of parameter estimators need special attention. It is also difficult to derive closed-form formulae for crucial stochastic properties. We rediscover a stochastic rounding operator, referred to as mean-preserving rounding, which overcomes the above drawbacks. Then, a novel class of ℤ \mathbb{Z} -valued ARMA models based on the new operator is proposed, and the existence of stationary solutions of the models is established. Stochastic properties including closed-form formulae for (conditional) moments, autocorrelation function, and conditional distributions are obtained. The advantages of our novel model class compared to existing ones are demonstrated. In particular, our model construction avoids identifiability issues such that maximum likelihood estimation is possible. A simulation study is provided, and the appealing performance of the new models is shown by several real-world data sets. Key words: ARMA model; Mean-preserving; Rounding operator; ℤ \mathbb{Z} -valued time series. MSC 2020: 62M05, 62M10

Introduction

During the last decades, there was a lot of research on discrete-valued time series, see
Weiß 2018
for a survey, where the large majority of contributions focused on count time series, i. e., quantitative time series having a range contained in the set of non-negative integers,
\mathbb{N}_{0}=\{0,1,\ldots\}
. Only during the last fifteen years
(
Li et al. 2024
, see)
, there was increasing interest also on time series that have the full set of integers,
\mathbb{Z}=\{\ldots,-1,0,1,\ldots\}
, as their range, i. e., where also negative integer outcomes are possible. Since the (actually applicable) term “integer-valued time series” is often used in the context of count time series, it became practice to use “
\mathbb{Z}
-valued time series” instead in such a case.

The first model for
\mathbb{Z}
-valued time series appears to be the autoregressive (AR) model by
Kim & Park 2008
, where the traditional AR-recursion is adapted to the integer case by using so-called “thinning operators”
(
Scotto et al. 2015
, see)
. Since that time, several thinning-based ARMA-like models (autoregressive moving average) have been proposed, commonly referred to as integer-valued ARMA (INARMA) models, see
Scotto et al. 2015
;
Li et al. 2024
for surveys. These models, however, have the disadvantage that the computation of probabilities and likelihoods can get demanding as convolutions of distributions are involved. Another approach for
\mathbb{Z}
-valued time series are rounded ARMA models as suggested by
Kachour and Yao 2009
;
Kachour 2014
, i. e., the outcome of the ARMA recursion is rounded to the next integer value (also see the related approach by
Kowal & Canale 2020
). In this case, however, it is difficult to derive closed-form formulae for crucial stochastic properties, in particular, moment formulae are not available. A third popular approach (besides several further proposals as surveyed by
Li et al. 2024
) are
\mathbb{Z}
-valued INGARCH models (integer-valued generalized autoregressive conditional heteroscedasticity), see
Xu and Zhu 2022
and
Liu et al. 2023
, but these only specify the one-step ahead conditional distribution while other stochastic properties are difficult to derive (if possible at all). So none of the proposed model classes is entirely satisfactory from a practical point of view.

Therefore, in this work, we propose a new class of ARMA-like models for
\mathbb{Z}
-valued time series, which combines ideas from both INARMA models and rounded ARMA models, and which is constructed in such a way that the main disadvantages of INARMA and rounded ARMA models are simultaneously avoided. Inspired by
Liu and Yuan 2013
, the crucial idea is to use a particular type of randomized rounding operator (i. e., which combines the randomness of a thinning operator with the rounding operation) together with the ARMA recursion. This randomized rounding operator is constructed such that we get simple closed-form formulae for (conditional) moments, autocorrelation function (acf), and conditional distributions.

Definition and properties of this operator are presented in Section
2
, while the proposed time series models are discussed in Section
3
. In particular, the advantages of our novel model class compared to existing ones are demonstrated. Among others, our model construction avoids identifiability issues such that maximum likelihood estimation is possible; the corresponding finite-sample performance is investigated by simulations, see Section
4
as well as the Supplement. In Section
5
, the practical benefit of our model class is demonstrated by three real-world data applications, namely to
\mathbb{Z}
-valued time series from finance, demography, and ecology (three further data examples are provided by the Supplement). Finally, we conclude in Section
6
and outline directions for future research.

2
A Mean-preserving Rounding Operator

In this section, we define and investigate the randomized rounding operator, mapping real numbers from
\mathbb{R}
onto integers from
\mathbb{Z}
, which is later used for defining integer-valued ARMA-type time series models.
More precisely, we define
\langle\cdot\rangle:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{Z}
as a random operator which does a kind of mean-preserving rounding operation:

\langle z\rangle\ :=\ \left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\lfloor z\rfloor&\text{with probability }1-(z-\lfloor z\rfloor),\\
\lfloor z\rfloor+1&\text{with probability }z-\lfloor z\rfloor,\\
\end{array}\right\}\ =\ \lfloor z\rfloor\ +\ \textup{Bin}(1,\ z-\lfloor z\rfloor).

(2.1)

Here,
\textup{Bin}(n,\pi)
abbreviates the binomial distribution with parameters
n\in\mathbb{N}=\{1,2,\ldots\}
and
\pi\in(0,1)
, see
Johnson et al. 2005
.
The operator
\langle\cdot\rangle
was referred to as “first-order random rounding” by
Liu and Yuan 2013
and as “stochastic rounding” by
Gupta et al. 2015
.
The last equality in (
2.1
) shows that the real number
z
is split into its integer part
\lfloor z\rfloor
and fractional part
z-\lfloor z\rfloor
, in analogy to the so-called “binomial multiplicative operator” in
Weiß & Zhu 2024
. As the common notation “
\{z\}
” for the fractional part is easily confused with the one for a one-point set (singleton), we use the abbreviation
\widetilde{z}:=z-\lfloor z\rfloor
instead. The distribution in (
2.1
) can also be summarized in terms of its probability generating function (pgf), then

\textstyle\textup{pgf}_{\langle z\rangle}(s)\ =\ s^{\lfloor z\rfloor}\cdot\mathbb{E}\big[s^{\textup{Bin}(1,\widetilde{z})}\big]\ =\ (1-\widetilde{z})\,s^{\lfloor z\rfloor}+\widetilde{z}\,s^{\lfloor z\rfloor+1}.

(2.2)

The last representation in (
2.1
) immediately implies that

\mathbb{E}\big[\langle z\rangle\big]\ =\ \lfloor z\rfloor\ +\ (z-\lfloor z\rfloor)\ =\ z,

(2.3)

so the mean is equal to
z
, which implies the mean-preserving property mentioned before: if
Z
is a real-valued random variable, then
\mathbb{E}\big[\langle Z\rangle\ |\ Z\big]=Z
, such that the law of total expectation implies that
\mathbb{E}\big[\langle Z\rangle\big]=\mathbb{E}[Z]
. This property was also highlighted by
Gupta et al. 2015
, who characterized
\langle\cdot\rangle
as an “unbiased rounding scheme”, as well as by
Liu and Yuan 2013
.

Using the last representation in (
2.1
), we also get the variance as

\textrm{Var}\big[\langle z\rangle\big]\ =\ 0\ +\ \textrm{Var}\big[\textup{Bin}(1,\ \widetilde{z})\big]\ =\ \widetilde{z}\,(1-\widetilde{z}).

(2.4)

As a consequence, if
Z
is a real-valued random variable, then

\textrm{Var}\big[\langle Z\rangle\ |\ Z\big]=\widetilde{Z}\,\big(1-\widetilde{Z}\big)
. By the law of total variance, we get

\displaystyle\textrm{Var}\big[\langle Z\rangle\big]\

\displaystyle=\ \textrm{Var}\big[\mathbb{E}[\langle Z\rangle\ |\ Z]\big]+\mathbb{E}\big[\textrm{Var}[\langle Z\rangle\ |\ Z]\big]\ =\ \textrm{Var}[Z]+\mathbb{E}\big[\widetilde{Z}\,\big(1-\widetilde{Z}\big)\big].

While a simple closed-form expression for the last term is missing, it is obviously from the interval
[0,0.25]
, i. e.,
\textrm{Var}\big[\langle Z\rangle]
deviates from
\textrm{Var}[Z]
by at most 0.25.

Note that
\langle z\rangle
can be equal to some
y\in\mathbb{Z}
iff
z\in(y-1,y+1)
holds.
Analogously, for given
z\in\mathbb{R}
, it also holds that
y\in(z-1,z+1)
.
As a consequence, if
x\in\mathbb{Z}
, then

\langle\alpha\cdot x\rangle=y\hskip 10.00002pt\text{possible iff}\hskip 10.00002pt\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}x\in\Big\{\lfloor\tfrac{y-1}{\alpha}\rfloor+1,\ldots,\lceil\tfrac{y+1}{\alpha}\rceil-1\Big\}&\text{if }\alpha\in(0,1),\\[8.61108pt]
x\in\Big\{\lfloor\tfrac{y+1}{\alpha}\rfloor+1,\ldots,\lceil\tfrac{y-1}{\alpha}\rceil-1\Big\}&\text{if }\alpha\in(-1,0).\\
\end{array}\right.

(2.5)

The case
\alpha=0
is degenerate as then
\langle 0\cdot x\rangle=0
holds without exception.
The set for
\alpha\in(0,1)
in (
2.5
) can be further split by considering the cases
\alpha x\geq y\ \Leftrightarrow\ x\geq\lceil\tfrac{y}{\alpha}\rceil
and
\alpha x<y\ \Leftrightarrow\ x\leq\lceil\tfrac{y}{\alpha}\rceil-1
. This distinction is important in view of the definition of the operator
\langle\cdot\rangle
: if
\alpha x\geq y
, then
\langle\alpha\cdot x\rangle=y
can only happen by selecting
\lfloor\alpha\cdot x\rfloor
, while
\alpha x<y
requires
\lfloor\alpha\cdot x\rfloor+1
for obtaining
\langle\alpha\cdot x\rangle=y
.
For
\alpha\in(-1,0)
, an analogous splitting is possible, now between
\alpha x\geq y\ \Leftrightarrow\ x\leq\lfloor\tfrac{y}{\alpha}\rfloor
and
\alpha x<y\ \Leftrightarrow\ x\geq\lfloor\tfrac{y}{\alpha}\rfloor+1
.

Hence, if
X
is a
\mathbb{Z}
-valued random variable, if
\alpha\in(0,1)
, and if
\langle\cdot\rangle
is executed independently of
X
, we get

\begin{array}[]{@{}l}P\big(\langle\alpha\cdot X\rangle=y\big)\ =\sum\limits_{x=\lfloor\tfrac{y-1}{\alpha}\rfloor+1}^{\lceil\tfrac{y+1}{\alpha}\rceil-1}P\big(\langle\alpha\cdot x\rangle=y\big)\cdot P(X=x)\\[8.61108pt]
\ =\sum\limits_{x=\lfloor\tfrac{y-1}{\alpha}\rfloor+1}^{\lceil\tfrac{y}{\alpha}\rceil-1}P\big(\langle\alpha\cdot x\rangle=y\big)\cdot P(X=x)\ +\sum\limits_{x=\lceil\tfrac{y}{\alpha}\rceil}^{\lceil\tfrac{y+1}{\alpha}\rceil-1}P\big(\langle\alpha\cdot x\rangle=y\big)\cdot P(X=x)\\[8.61108pt]
\ =\sum\limits_{x=\lfloor\tfrac{y-1}{\alpha}\rfloor+1}^{\lceil\tfrac{y}{\alpha}\rceil-1}\widetilde{\alpha x}\cdot P(X=x)\ +\sum\limits_{x=\lceil\tfrac{y}{\alpha}\rceil}^{\lceil\tfrac{y+1}{\alpha}\rceil-1}\big(1-\widetilde{\alpha x}\big)\cdot P(X=x),\end{array}

(2.6)

which is a convolution between two discrete distributions.
Analogously, if
\alpha\in(-1,0)
, then

\begin{array}[]{@{}l}P\big(\langle\alpha\cdot X\rangle=y\big)\ =\sum\limits_{x=\lfloor\tfrac{y+1}{\alpha}\rfloor+1}^{\lfloor\tfrac{y}{\alpha}\rfloor}\big(1-\widetilde{\alpha x}\big)\cdot P(X=x)\ +\sum\limits_{x=\lfloor\tfrac{y}{\alpha}\rfloor+1}^{\lceil\tfrac{y-1}{\alpha}\rceil-1}\widetilde{\alpha x}\cdot P(X=x).\end{array}

(2.7)

For
\alpha=0
, we certainly get
P\big(\langle 0\cdot X\rangle=y\big)=\delta_{0y}
, where
\delta_{ab}
denotes the Kronecker delta, which equals 1 (0) if
a=b
(
a\not=b
).

3
A Novel Class of
\mathbb{Z}
-valued ARMA Models

3.1
Model Definition

In what follows, we present the main contribution of our article, namely a novel ARMA-like model for
\mathbb{Z}
-valued time series using the mean-preserving rounding operator
\langle\cdot\rangle
from (
2.3
), referred to as mean-preserving rounded ARMA (MRARMA) model. There are several ways how such integer-valued MRARMA models can be defined. In our opinion, the most convenient approach is to define the MRARMA
(p,q)
model by the recursive scheme

X_{t}\ =\ \epsilon_{t}+\big\langle Z_{t-1}\big\rangle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{with }Z_{t-1}\ =\ \alpha_{1}\cdot X_{t-1}+\ldots+\alpha_{p}\cdot X_{t-p}+\beta_{1}\cdot\epsilon_{t-1}+\ldots+\beta_{q}\cdot\epsilon_{t-q},

(3.1)

where
(\epsilon_{t})
constitutes a sequence of independent and identically distributed (i. i. d.)
\mathbb{Z}
-valued innovations, and where the operator
\langle\cdot\rangle
at each time
t
is executed independently of the innovations and the available process history.
The parameters
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\beta_{q}\in\mathbb{R}
have to be chosen appropriately, see Theorem
3.2.1
below.
Note that
Z_{t-1}
only depends on information being available up to time
t-1
, although the innovations are usually not observed in practice. The model order
(p,q)\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{2}
covers the pure mean-preserving rounded autoregressive (MRAR) model (if
q=0
) or pure mean-preserving rounded moving average (MRMA) model (if
p=0
) as special cases, while
p=q=0
corresponds to i. i. d. integers. Comparing (
3.1
) to the existing literature on integer time series models, it becomes clear that this model definition is inspired by the rounded autoregressive (RAR) models of
Kachour and Yao 2009
;
Kachour 2014
, more precisely by the model formulation in
Kachour 2014
as this version avoids the possible identifiability problems of
Kachour and Yao 2009
. The major difference of (
3.1
) to
Kachour 2014
is given by the use of the mean-preserving rounding operator
\langle\cdot\rangle
from (
2.1
) instead of the ordinary rounding operator, as this shall allow us to derive closed-form expressions for marginal moments and acf.

3.1.1 Remark

Our novel MRARMA model (
3.1
) is also related to the “random rounded INARCH (RRINARCH) model” (1.7) in
Liu and Yuan 2013
, where mean-preserving rounding is used as well. However, there are also important differences between our model and the one of
Liu and Yuan 2013
. First, the RRINARCH model does not have an MA-part but an ARCH-type feedback term instead. Second, even in the pure AR-case (
q=0
in (
3.1
) and
h_{t}=1
in model (1.7) of
Liu and Yuan 2013
, respectively), both model approaches differ as
Liu and Yuan 2013
restrict to centered innovations while including a real-valued mean parameter within the rounding operator. The latter construction is analogous to the model of
Kachour and Yao 2009
, but it may cause identifiability problems as mentioned before. Such problems are avoided by our model formulation (
3.1
).

As indicated before (
3.1
), there would be further options for defining an ARMA-type model for integer time series by using the operator
\langle\cdot\rangle
from (
2.1
). One particularly obvious approach is later discussed in Appendix
A
. But as we shall recognize there, this alternative ARMA-type model has more sophisticated stochastic properties. Therefore, we focus on definition (
3.1
) in the sequel.

3.1.2 Remark

At first glance, it appears that the MRARMA model (
3.1
) (and also model (
A.1
) from Appendix
A
) could also be useful for modeling count time series, which could be achieved by requiring the innovations
(\epsilon_{t})
to be count random variables and the parameters
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\beta_{q}
to be non-negative. The sample paths being generated by such a recursive scheme, however, are restricted by a limited possibility for downward movements. Given the values of
X_{t-1},\ldots,\epsilon_{t-q}
, the term
\big\langle Z_{t-1}\big\rangle
in (
3.1
)
will often be truly positive, because downward movements beyond the floor operation are not possible. Thus, transitions towards zero will often be impossible as
\epsilon_{t}\geq 0
.

3.2
Existence of Stationary Solution

To establish the existence and stationarity of the MRARMA
(p,q)
process (
3.1
), we define the following
(p+q)
-dimensional vectorized process

\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}_{t}\ :=\ \left(\begin{array}[]{c}X_{t}\\
X_{t-1}\\
\vdots\\
X_{t-p+1}\\
\epsilon_{t}\\
\epsilon_{t-1}\\
\vdots\\
\epsilon_{t-q+1}\end{array}\right)\ =\ \left(\begin{array}[]{c}\big\langle Z_{t-1}\big\rangle+\epsilon_{t}\\
X_{t-1}\\
\vdots\\
X_{t-p+1}\\
\epsilon_{t}\\
\epsilon_{t-1}\\
\vdots\\
\epsilon_{t-q+1}\end{array}\right).

Then, the process
(\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}_{t})
denotes a homogeneous Markov chain with state space
E^{\ast}=\mathbb{Z}^{p+q}
and transition probabilities

\textstyle\pi(\mbox{\boldmath$x$},\mbox{\boldmath$y$})=P\left(\epsilon_{1}=y_{1}-\Big\langle\sum\limits_{i=1}^{p}\alpha_{i}x_{i}+\sum\limits_{j=1}^{q}\beta_{j}x_{p+j}\Big\rangle\right)\,\mathds{1}_{\{y_{2}=x_{1},\ldots,y_{p}=x_{p-1},y_{p+2}=x_{p+1},\ldots,y_{p+q}=x_{p+q-1}\}},

where
\mbox{\boldmath$x$}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{p+q})^{\top}\in E^{\ast}
and
\mbox{\boldmath$y$}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{p+q})^{\top}\in E^{\ast}
, and where
\mathds{1}_{\cdot}
denotes the indicator function.
Then, the process (
3.1
) can be written in the following form:

\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}_{t}\ =\ \langle\mbox{{{A}}}\,\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}_{t-1}\rangle+\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}_{t},

where rounding is done component-wise,
\langle\mbox{\boldmath$x$}\rangle=\left(\langle x_{1}\rangle,\ldots,\langle x_{p+q}\rangle\right)^{\top}
, where the
(p+q)\times(p+q)
-matrix
\mbox{{{A}}}=(a_{kl})
is defined as

\displaystyle a_{1i}=\alpha_{i},i=1,\ldots,p;~~~a_{1,j+p}=\beta_{j},j=1,\ldots,q;

\displaystyle a_{i+1,i}=1,i\neq p,i=1,2,\ldots,p+q-1;~~~a_{kl}=0,~\hbox{otherwise},

and where
\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}_{t}=(\epsilon_{t},0,\ldots,0,\epsilon_{t},0,\ldots,0)^{\top}
, i. e., with
\epsilon_{t}
being the value of the first and
(p+1)
-th coordinates, and zero being the value of the remaining coordinates.

The following theorem gives the conditions that ensure the ergodicity and
the stationarity of the process (
3.1
).
For
\mbox{\boldmath$x$}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{p+q})^{\top}
, let
\|\mbox{\boldmath$x$}\|_{1}=\sum_{i=1}^{p+q}|x_{i}|
denote the
L_{1}
-norm. In addition, let
\rho(\mbox{{{A}}})
denote the spectral radius of
A
. For any measure
\lambda
and function
g
on
E^{\ast}
, we set
\lambda(g)=\int g(x)\,d\lambda(x)
.

3.2.1 Theorem

Suppose that
(i) the probability law of the i. i. d. noise
\epsilon_{t}
charges all points of
E^{\ast}
, i. e.,
P(\epsilon_{t}=a)>0
for any
a\in\mathbb{Z}
;
(ii)
E|\epsilon_{t}|^{k}<\infty
for some
k\geq 1
;
(iii)
\alpha_{p}\neq 0,\beta_{q}\neq 0,\rho(\mbox{{{A}}})<1
.
Then:

(1)

The process (
3.1
)
(X_{t})
has an unique invariant probability measure
\lambda
which has a moment of order
k
, i. e.,
\lambda(\|\cdot\|_{1}^{k})<\infty
.

(2)

For any
\mbox{\boldmath$y$}\in E^{\ast}
and
f\in L^{1}(\lambda)
, we have

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}f(\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}_{k})\rightarrow\lambda(f),~~~~P_{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$y$}}~a.s.,

where
P_{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$y$}}
denotes the conditional probability
P(\cdot|\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}_{0}=\mbox{\boldmath$y$})
.

The proof of Theorem
3.2.1
is provided by Appendix
B
.

3.2.2 Remark

Assumption (i) is just used to show the irreducibility of
(X_{t})
.
Let the left upper corner
p\times p
-submatrix of
A
be
\mbox{{{A}}}^{\ast}
, i. e.,
\mbox{{{A}}}^{\ast}=(a_{kl})_{1\leq k,l\leq p}
.
According to
Dion et al. 1995
, we know that
\rho(\mbox{{{A}}})=\rho(\mbox{{{A}}}^{\ast})
.
Compared to
Kachour 2014
, although we have different models, rounding operators, and coefficient matrix
A
, the assumptions and conclusions in Theorem
3.2.1
and Theorem 1 in
Kachour 2014
are comparable.

3.2.3 Remark

Dion et al. 1995
provided conditions for the existence of a stationary distribution for thinning-based generalized INARMA process of general orders based on the theory of multi-type branching processes with immigration, whose results are similar to Theorem
3.2.1
, i. e.,
\rho(\mbox{{{M}}})<1
, where
M
is the mean matrix of the offspring distribution and
M
is the transpose of
A
considered here. This condition can be further simplified to
0<\sum_{i=1}^{p}\alpha_{i}<1
if the autoregressive coefficients are positive, which are the same for the existence of a stationary distribution for the thinning-based generalized integer-valued autoregressive process without the moving average terms. This phenomenon also happens for the real-valued ARMA models, see the Remark on page 1123 of
Ling 1999
.

For thinning-based INARMA process,
Neal and Subba Rao 2007
also assumed that
\sum_{j=1}^{p}\beta_{j}<1
. For a real-valued ARMA process, this condition is sufficient for the time series to be invertible. But it is an open question whether this condition is sufficient for the INARMA process to be invertible. Here, we also make a similar assumption: we require that
\beta=(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{q})^{\top}
are such that the roots of the
q
th-order polynomial

1-\beta_{1}x-\beta_{2}x^{2}-\ldots-\beta_{q}x^{q}

are outside the unit circle.

3.3
Moments and Probabilistic Properties

As the operator
\langle\cdot\rangle
from (
2.1
) is mean-preserving, recall (
2.3
), model definition (
3.1
) leads to the linear conditional mean

\mathbb{E}[X_{t}\ |\ X_{t-1},\ldots,\ \epsilon_{t-1},\ldots]\ =\ \mu_{\epsilon}+\alpha_{1}\cdot X_{t-1}+\ldots+\alpha_{p}\cdot X_{t-p}+\beta_{1}\cdot\epsilon_{t-1}+\ldots+\beta_{q}\cdot\epsilon_{t-q},

(3.2)

where
\mu_{\epsilon}=\mathbb{E}[\epsilon_{t}]
. Such a closed-form expression does not exist for the models of
Kachour and Yao 2009
;
Kachour 2014
. It was one of the main motivations for our model proposal(s) as it implies a set of Yule–Walker equations for the acf, see equations (
3.4
) and (
3.5
) below. Furthermore, the unconditional mean
\mu=\mathbb{E}[X_{t}]
takes the same form as for ordinary ARMA models, namely

\mu\ =\ \mu_{\epsilon}\,\frac{1+\beta_{1}+\ldots+\beta_{q}}{1-\alpha_{1}-\ldots-\alpha_{p}},

(3.3)

where
\alpha_{1}+\ldots+\alpha_{p}\ <1
is required.

The autocovariance at lag
h\in\mathbb{N}
,
\gamma(h):=\textrm{Cov}[X_{t},X_{t-h}]
, is computed by using the law of total covariance:

\begin{array}[]{rl}\gamma(h)\ =&\textrm{Cov}\big[\mathbb{E}[X_{t}\ |\ X_{t-1},\ldots,\ \epsilon_{t-1},\ldots],\ X_{t-h}\big]\\[4.30554pt]
=&\sum_{i=1}^{p}\alpha_{i}\,\gamma(h-i)+\sum_{j=1}^{q}\beta_{j}\,c(h-j),\end{array}

(3.4)

where the mixed covariances
c(s):=\textrm{Cov}[X_{r},\epsilon_{r-s}]
,
s\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, satisfy
c(0)=\textrm{Cov}[X_{r},\epsilon_{r}]=\textrm{Var}[\epsilon_{r}]=:\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}
,
while for
s\in\mathbb{N}
, we get

\begin{array}[]{rl}c(s)=\textrm{Cov}[X_{r},\epsilon_{r-s}]\ =&\textrm{Cov}\big[\mathbb{E}[X_{r}\ |\ X_{r-1},\ldots,\ \epsilon_{r-1},\ldots],\ \epsilon_{r-s}\big]\\
=&\sum_{i=1}^{p}\alpha_{i}\,\textrm{Cov}[X_{r-i},\epsilon_{r-s}]+\sum_{j=1}^{q}\beta_{j}\,\textrm{Cov}[\epsilon_{r-j},\epsilon_{r-s}]\\
=&\sum_{i=1}^{\min\{s,p\}}\alpha_{i}\,c(s-i)+\sum_{j=1}^{q}\beta_{j}\,\delta_{js}\,\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}.\end{array}

(3.5)

Equations (
3.4
) and (
3.5
) just correspond to the Yule–Walker equations of an ordinary ARMA model, i. e., the MRARMA model (
3.1
) has the same autocorrelation structure as an ordinary ARMA model.

\textrm{Var}[X_{t}\ |\ X_{t-1},\ldots,\ \epsilon_{t-1},\ldots]\ =\ \sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}+\widetilde{Z_{t-1}}\,\big(1-\widetilde{Z_{t-1}}\big),

(3.6)

which can be used in practice for computing, e. g., the standardized Pearson residuals as a popular tool for model diagnostics

Weiß 2018

.

\widetilde{Z_{t-1}}\,\big(1-\widetilde{Z_{t-1}}\big)
takes a value from
[0,0.25]
, we have relatively strong heteroscedasticity for low
\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}
and vice versa.

The unconditional variance of the MRARMA model (
3.1
) satisfies

\begin{array}[]{rl}\sigma^{2}:=\textrm{Var}[X_{t}]\ =&\textrm{Var}\big[\mathbb{E}[X_{t}\ |\ X_{t-1},\ldots,\ \epsilon_{t-1},\ldots]\big]+\mathbb{E}\big[\textrm{Var}[X_{t}\ |\ X_{t-1},\ldots,\ \epsilon_{t-1},\ldots]\big]\\
=&\textrm{Var}[Z_{t-1}]+\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}+\mathbb{E}\big[\widetilde{Z_{t-1}}\,\big(1-\widetilde{Z_{t-1}}\big)\Big].\end{array}

(3.7)

Here, the first summand
\textrm{Var}[Z_{t-1}]=\textrm{Var}[\alpha_{1}\cdot X_{t-1}+\ldots+\alpha_{p}\cdot X_{t-p}+\beta_{1}\cdot\epsilon_{t-1}+\ldots+\beta_{q}\cdot\epsilon_{t-q}]
requires to evaluate the Yule–Walker equations (
3.4
) and (
3.5
) for the autocovariance function, whereas the last term
\mathbb{E}\big[\widetilde{Z_{t-1}}\,\big(1-\widetilde{Z_{t-1}}\big)\big]
takes a value from the interval
[0,0.25]
. Exactly if
Z_{t-1}
is integer-valued, the last term becomes zero as for ordinary ARMA models, but the possible deviation from ordinary ARMA models is bounded from above by 0.25.

For purely autoregressive models, also conditional distributions can be derived. For the MRAR

\textstyle P(X_{t}=x\ |\ X_{t-1}=x_{1},\ldots)\ =\ \sum\limits_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}P(\epsilon_{t}=x-k)\,P\big(\langle z\rangle=k\big)\ \hskip 10.00002pt\text{with }z=\alpha_{1}x_{1}+\ldots+\alpha_{p}x_{p}.

denotes the innovations’ pgf, and where the second factor is computed via (

The aforementioned symmetry in Figure
3
(a) can also be proven formally by utilizing the following properties of the floor operation and the fractional part: for an arbitrary real number
z\in\mathbb{R}
, it holds that
\lfloor-z\rfloor=-\lfloor z\rfloor-\mathds{1}_{z\not\in\mathbb{Z}}
and
\widetilde{-z}=-\widetilde{z}+\mathds{1}_{z\not\in\mathbb{Z}}
. Thus, if
\alpha_{1}y\not\in\mathbb{Z}
, we get for the transition probabilities that

\begin{array}[]{rl}p(-x|-y)\ =&(1-\widetilde{-\alpha_{1}y})\cdot P\big(\epsilon_{t}=-x-\lfloor-\alpha_{1}y\rfloor\big)+\widetilde{-\alpha_{1}y}\cdot P\big(\epsilon_{t}=-x-\lfloor-\alpha_{1}y\rfloor-1\big)\\[3.87498pt]
\ =&\widetilde{\alpha_{1}y}\cdot P\big(\epsilon_{t}=-x+\lfloor\alpha_{1}y\rfloor+1\big)+(-\widetilde{\alpha_{1}y}+1)\cdot P\big(\epsilon_{t}=-x+\lfloor\alpha_{1}y\rfloor\big)\\[3.87498pt]
\ =&(1-\widetilde{\alpha_{1}y})\cdot P\big(\epsilon_{t}=-(x-\lfloor\alpha_{1}y\rfloor)\big)+\widetilde{\alpha_{1}y}\cdot P\big(\epsilon_{t}=-(x-\lfloor\alpha_{1}y\rfloor-1)\big),\end{array}

which equals
p(x|y)
if
\epsilon_{t}
has a symmetric distribution. For
\alpha_{1}y\in\mathbb{Z}
, by contrast, the transition probabilities have a more simple form anyway,
p(x|y)=P\big(\epsilon_{t}=x-\alpha_{1}y\big)
, such that
p(-x|-y)=P\big(\epsilon_{t}=-(x-\alpha_{1}y)\big)
again equals
p(x|y)
if
\epsilon_{t}
has a symmetric distribution.
These symmetries imply the symmetry of the marginal distribution.

4
Performance of Parameter Estimation

First, we discuss estimation methods for the general MRARMA model, then focus on the MRAR model.

Due to its close relationship to the ordinary ARMA model, the parameters of the MRARMA model can be estimated by analogous estimation approaches.
First, recall that a direct proof for the consistency and asymptotic normality of maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) for causal and invertible real-valued ARMA was given by
Yao and Brockwell 2006
, which is difficult to be generalized to the general MRARMA model considered here. Now we use three-stage least squares (LS) estimation method in
Koreisha and Pukkila 1990
to estimate regression parameters
(\alpha_{1},...,\alpha_{p},\beta_{1},...,\beta_{q})
. At the first stage, an autoregression of order
\lfloor\sqrt{n}\rfloor
is fitted to data
X_{1},...,X_{n}
, wherein the autoregressive parameters are estimated using LS. Denote
\hat{\epsilon}_{t}
the estimate of
\epsilon_{t}
obtained from the residuals from this AR. At the second stage, consider the following regression model

X_{t}-\hat{\epsilon}_{t}=\sum_{i=1}^{p}\alpha_{i}X_{t-i}+\sum_{j=1}^{q}\beta_{j}\hat{\epsilon}_{t-j}+\beta(B)(\epsilon_{t}-\hat{\epsilon}_{t}),

(4.1)

where
B
is the backshift operator
B\epsilon_{t}=\epsilon_{t-1}
, and
\beta(B)=1+\sum_{j=1}^{q}\beta_{j}B^{j}
. The error term is
\beta(B)(\epsilon_{t}-\hat{\epsilon}_{t})
, and
a linear regression of
X_{t}-\hat{\epsilon}_{t}
on
X_{t-1},...,X_{t-p},\hat{\epsilon}_{t-1},...,\hat{\epsilon}_{t-q}
gives LS parameter estimates. At the third stage, the above regression (
4.1
) is recalculated but now using a generalized LS procedure as if the errors in the regression were generated by a moving average of order
q
with parameters
\beta_{1},...,\beta_{q}
, and the estimators in the second stage are used to calculate the covariance matrix of the errors. The above three-stage LS estimation was improved by
Kapetanios 2003
, who suggested to iterate this LS operation using the new estimate of the error sequence until the estimate of the error sequence converges, and the superiority of this method lies in the fact that it eliminates the random noise
component underlying the justification of the generalized LS application.

In the following, we will focus on the pure autoregressive case for ease of computations, i.e., the MRAR
(p)
model.
Using the Yule–Walker equations (
3.4
) and (
3.5
) together with (
3.3
) for the mean, the method of moments (MM) is easily implement, whereas (
3.2
) can be used to compute conditional LS (CLS) estimates.
The consistency and asymptotic normality of the CLS estimator can be easily established using arguments in
Tjøstheim 1986
.

If, in addition, the distribution of the innovations
(\epsilon_{t})
is specified, also MLE is possible by using the conditional probabilities (
3.8
). Since no closed-form formulae are available for MLE, the practical computation is done by numerically maximizing the (conditional) log-likelihood function, where MM or CLS estimates can be used as starting values.
If the time series
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
origins from an MRAR
(p)
model, and if
\theta
denotes the vector of all model parameters, then the conditional log-likelihood function equals

\textstyle\ell_{p}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})\ =\ \sum_{t=p+1}^{n}\ln{P(X_{t}\ |\ X_{t-1},\ldots,X_{t-p})},

which is to be maximized over
\theta
. Since our randomized rounding does not cause identifiability issues, we can also compute approximate standard errors (s. e.) from the inverse Hessian at the maximum, see
Weiß 2018
for details. Finally, the maximal value of
\ell_{p}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})
, say
\ell_{\max,p}
, can be used for model identification, in the case that multiple candidate models are fitted to the time series. This is done by computing an appropriate information criterion, such as Akaike’s (AIC) or the Bayesian one (BIC). Here, we follow the recommendation in
Weiß 2018
and account for the conditioning in MLE computation by using the corrective factor
n/(n-p)
, i. e., AIC or BIC are computed by using
\frac{n}{n-p}\,\ell_{\max,p}
as the maximized log-likelihood value.

6
Conclusions and Future Research

In this paper, we rediscover the mean-preserving rounding operator and propose a corresponding class of
\mathbb{Z}
-valued ARMA models.
The existence of stationary solutions and important stochastic properties are derived. The various advantages of the novel model class compared to existing ones are demonstrated. These advantages are further confirmed by several real-world data examples, where the fitted MRARMA models show an appealing performance.

We conclude with some suggestions for possible future research.
First, one could consider a semiparametric model specification in analogy to
Liu et al. 2021
to provide more flexibility for model fitting. Second, one could construct INGARCH-type model like in
Liu and Yuan 2013
to provide another alternative to existing INGARCH models. Finally, it would be interesting to develop multivariate, spatial, or spatio-temporal extensions of the MRARMA class.

Appendix A
On an Alternative Model Definition

In view of the existing literature on integer time series models, another option for model definition would be

X_{t}\ =\ \epsilon_{t}+\big\langle\alpha_{1}\cdot X_{t-1}\big\rangle+\ldots+\big\langle\alpha_{p}\cdot X_{t-p}\big\rangle+\big\langle\beta_{1}\cdot\epsilon_{t-1}\big\rangle+\ldots+\big\langle\beta_{q}\cdot\epsilon_{t-q}\big\rangle,

(A.1)

i. e., different rounding operators (executed independently of each other) are used for each part of the model recursion. This type of model definition is inspired by thinning-based ARMA models (“INARMA models”), see
Weiß 2018
for comprehensive information and references on such models. We use the acronym MRARMA
*
for this kind of modified MRARMA model. Except for low model orders with
p+q\leq 1
, the definitions (
3.1
) and (
A.1
) differ from each other. INARMA models are quite popular for modeling integer time series, so the MRARMA
*
definition (
A.1
) might look more familiar at a first glance. However, as we shall see in the remainder of this appendix, our original definition (
3.1
) leads to considerably simpler expressions for important stochastic properties.

But let us first start with an important commonness: both model definitions (
3.1
) and (
A.1
) lead to the same linear conditional mean (
3.2
), which implies that they also share the same unconditional mean (
3.3
) as well as the same Yule–Walker equations (
3.4
) and (
3.5
) concerning the autocovariance structure.
Although (
3.1
) and (
A.1
) have the same (conditional) means, their conditional variances differ. While model (
3.1
) has the simple expression (
3.6
), the conditional variance gets more complex for the MRARMA
*
model (
A.1
):

V[X_{t}\ |\ X_{t-1},\ldots,\ \epsilon_{t-1},\ldots]\ =\ \displaystyle\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{p}\widetilde{\alpha_{i}X_{t-i}}\,\Big(1-\widetilde{\alpha_{i}X_{t-i}}\Big)+\sum_{j=1}^{q}\widetilde{\beta_{j}\epsilon_{t-j}}\,\Big(1-\widetilde{\beta_{j}\epsilon_{t-j}}\Big).

(A.2)

This additional complexity in conditional variance is one of the reasons why we prefer definition (
3.1
) over (
A.1
). Note that the heteroscedasticity in (
A.2
) is more pronounced than in (
3.6
), because each summand after
\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}
takes a value from
[0,0.25]
, i. e.,
\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}
may be increased by up to
(p+q)\cdot 0.25
. An illustrative simulated sample path is shown in Figure

.

While (

p=2

\begin{array}[]{rl}P(X_{t}=x\ |\ X_{t-1}=x_{1},\ldots)\ =&\sum\limits_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}P(\epsilon_{t}=x-k)\,P\big(\langle\alpha_{1}x_{1}\rangle+\langle\alpha_{2}x_{2}\rangle=k\big)\\[3.87498pt]

k-l\in(\alpha_{1}x_{1}-1,\alpha_{1}x_{1}+1)

\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt=\ \big(1-\widetilde{\alpha_{1}x_{1}}\big)\big(1-\widetilde{\alpha_{2}x_{2}}\big)\cdot P\big(\epsilon_{t}=x-\lfloor\alpha_{1}x_{1}\rfloor-\lfloor\alpha_{2}x_{2}\rfloor\big)

\displaystyle\hskip 18.49988pt+\ \Big(\widetilde{\alpha_{1}x_{1}}\big(1-\widetilde{\alpha_{2}x_{2}}\big)+\widetilde{\alpha_{2}x_{2}}\big(1-\widetilde{\alpha_{1}x_{1}}\big)\Big)\cdot P\big(\epsilon_{t}=x-\lfloor\alpha_{1}x_{1}\rfloor-\lfloor\alpha_{2}x_{2}\rfloor-1\big)

Appendix B
Proof of Theorem
3.2.1

We use arguments similar to those in Theorem 1 of
Kachour 2014
. Note that we can also establish existence and ergodicity of the process (
3.1
)
(X_{t})
by using
Theorem 3.3 of
Menshikov 1994
. For all
\mbox{\boldmath$y$}_{0}\in E^{\ast}
, we define the empirical measure
\lambda_{n}
by

\lambda_{n}(\cdot)=\tfrac{1}{n}\big[\pi^{1}(\mbox{\boldmath$y$}_{0},\cdot)+\ldots\pi^{n}(\mbox{\boldmath$y$}_{0},\cdot)\big],

where
\pi^{n}
denotes the
n
-step transition probability of the Markov chain
(\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}_{t})
. We shall prove that the sequence of probability distributions
(\lambda_{n})
has a sub-sequence converging to some probability distribution
\lambda
, and this limit will
automatically be a stationary probability distributions of the Markov chain
(\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}_{t})
.
Then, the process (
3.1
) can be written in the following form

\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}_{t}=\mbox{{{A}}}\,\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}_{t-1}+\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}_{t}+\mbox{\boldmath$B$}_{t},

(B.1)

where
\mbox{\boldmath$B$}_{t}=\left(\langle Z_{t-1}\rangle-Z_{t-1},0,\ldots,0\right)^{\top}
. Note that
\|\mbox{\boldmath$B$}_{t}\|_{1}\leq 1
. Iterating equation (
B.1
), we have

\textstyle\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}_{t}=\mbox{{{A}}}^{n}\,\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}_{t-n}+\sum\limits_{j=0}^{n-1}\mbox{{{A}}}^{j}\,\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{t-j},

where
\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{t}=\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}_{t}+\mbox{\boldmath$B$}_{t}
. Define the function
V
as
V(\mbox{\boldmath$y$})=\|\mbox{\boldmath$y$}\|_{1}
. Since
V
is positive and as
k\geq 1
, we have

\lim_{\|\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$y$}\|_{1}\rightarrow\infty}V(\mbox{\boldmath$y$})=\infty
, so
V
is a Lyapunov function
(see
Duflo 1997
, p. 41)
. Then,

\textstyle(\pi^{n}V)(\mbox{\boldmath$y$})=E_{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$y$}}\left[\Big\|\mbox{{{A}}}^{n}\,\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}_{t-n}+\sum\limits_{j=0}^{n-1}\mbox{{{A}}}^{j}\,\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{t-j}\Big\|_{1}\right]^{k}.

Note that
\|\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{t-j}\|_{1}\leq 2|\epsilon_{t-j}|+1
,
0\leq j\leq n-1
. Let
b=\big(\mathbb{E}[2|\epsilon_{t-j}|+1]^{k}\big)^{1/k}
, then it follows that
b<\infty
as
E|\epsilon_{t}|^{k}<\infty
.
We denote by
\vvvert\cdot\vvvert_{1}
the matrical norm
associated to
\|\cdot\|_{1}
. Thus, from Minkowski’s inequality, we obtain

[(\pi^{n}V)(\mbox{\boldmath$y$})]^{1/k}\ \leq\ \vvvert\mbox{{{A}}}^{n}\vvvert_{1}\ \|\mbox{\boldmath$y$}\|_{1}+S_{n}(\mbox{{{A}}})\,b,

where
S_{n}(\mbox{{{A}}})=\vvvert\mbox{{{A}}}^{n-1}\vvvert_{1}+\ldots+\vvvert\mbox{{{A}}}\vvvert_{1}+1
. From Gelfand’s formula, we know that for any matrix norm
\vvvert\cdot\vvvert
, we have

\vvvert\mbox{{{A}}}^{n}\vvvert^{1/n}\rightarrow\rho(\mbox{{{A}}})
as
n\rightarrow\infty
. Recall that
\rho(\mbox{{{A}}})\leq\vvvert\mbox{{{A}}}\vvvert
for any matrix norm
\vvvert\cdot\vvvert
. As
\rho(\mbox{{{A}}})<1
,
there exists an
n_{0}
such that for any
n\geq n_{0}
, we have
\vvvert\mbox{{{A}}}^{n}\vvvert_{1}<1
. On the other hand, for
n\geq n_{0}
, we have
\vvvert\mbox{{{A}}}^{n}\vvvert_{1}\leq(\rho(\mbox{{{A}}})+\varepsilon)^{n}
with
\varepsilon
being arbitrarily small and
\rho(\mbox{{{A}}})+\varepsilon<1
. Then,
S_{n}(\mbox{{{A}}})
is convergent using the property of geometric series.

For
n=n_{0}
, we have
\vvvert\mbox{{{A}}}^{n_{0}}\vvvert_{1}=\alpha^{\ast}<1
. Then,

\big[(\pi^{n_{0}}V)(\mbox{\boldmath$y$})\big]^{1/k}\ \leq\ \alpha^{\ast}\|\mbox{\boldmath$y$}\|_{1}+\beta^{\ast}\ =\ \|\mbox{\boldmath$y$}\|_{1}\left(\alpha^{\ast}+\frac{\beta^{\ast}}{\|\mbox{\boldmath$y$}\|_{1}}\right),

where
\beta^{\ast}=b\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\vvvert\mbox{{{A}}}^{j}\vvvert_{1}
and
0<\beta^{\ast}<\infty
. It follows that

\frac{\big[(\pi^{n_{0}}V)(\mbox{\boldmath$y$})\big]^{1/k}}{V(\mbox{\boldmath$y$})}\ \leq\ \left(\alpha^{\ast}+\frac{\beta^{\ast}}{\|\mbox{\boldmath$y$}\|_{1}}\right)^{k}.

Then,
(\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}_{t})
satisfies the Lyapunov criterion with
V(\mbox{\boldmath$y$})
as Lyapunov function, see Proposition 2.1.6 in
Duflo 1997
. Moreover, the law of the i. i. d. noise
\epsilon_{t}
charges all points of
E^{\ast}
, so
(\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}_{t})
is irreducible. Thus,
(\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}_{t})
is positive recurrent with a unique invariant probability measure, denoted by
\lambda
, with
\lambda(V)<\infty
. On the other hand, part (2) of the theorem follows from the classical ergodic theorem for Markov chains.
