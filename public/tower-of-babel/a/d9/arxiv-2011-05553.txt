Analog quantum simulation of non-Condon effects in molecular spectroscopy
Hamza Jnane, Nicolas P. D. Sawaya, Borja Peropadre, Alan Aspuru-Guzik, Raul Garcia-Patron, Joonsuk Huh

Abstract
In this work, we present a linear optical implementation for analog quantum simulation of molecular vibronic spectra, incorporating the non-Condon scattering operation with a quadratically small truncation error. Thus far, analog and digital quantum algorithms for achieving quantum speedup have been suggested only in the Condon regime, which refers to a transition dipole moment that is independent of nuclear coordinates. For analog quantum optical simulation beyond the Condon regime (i.e., non-Condon transitions) the resulting non-unitary scattering operations must be handled appropriately in a linear optical network. In this paper, we consider the first and second-order Herzberg-Teller expansions of the transition dipole moment operator for the non-Condon effect, for implementation on linear optical quantum hardware. We believe the method opens a new way to approximate arbitrary non-unitary operations in analog and digital quantum simulations. We report in-silico simulations of the vibronic spectra for naphthalene, phenanthrene, and benzene to support our findings.

II
Non-Condon profile

When a molecule absorbs a photon, the molecule undergoes simultaneously vibrational and electronic state changes, also called vibronic transitions. Within the BO approximation (separation of electronic and nuclear degrees of freedom), determining the vibronic spectral profile involves computing the transition probabilities between any two vibrational states of the initial and excited PESs. The PESs are generally anharmonic; however, in this work, we approximate them as harmonic PESs.

Because the vibronic transition profile at finite temperature can always be transformed into the zero-temperature profile by purifying the initial thermal state
Huh and Yung 2017b
, we focus on the zero-temperature non-Condon transition profile for simplicity, which is given as a Fermi’s golden rule type equation:

\displaystyle P(\omega)=\frac{1}{\mathcal{N}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{m^{{}^{\prime}}}=~\mathbf{0}\\
r=x,y,z\end{subarray}}^{\bm{\infty}}|\langle\mathbf{m^{{}^{\prime}}}|\hat{U}_{\mathrm{Dok}}\hat{\mu}_{r}(\hat{\mathbf{Q}})|\mathbf{0}\rangle|^{2}\delta(\omega-\mathbf{m^{{}^{\prime}}}\cdot\bm{\omega}^{\prime}),

(1)

where
\mathcal{N}=\sum_{r=x,y,z}\mathcal{N}_{r}
is a normalization constant, that depends on the TDM operator
\hat{\bm{\mu}}(\mathbf{\hat{Q}})(=(\hat{\mu}_{x}(\hat{\mathbf{Q}}),\hat{\mu}_{y}(\hat{\mathbf{Q}}),\hat{\mu}_{z}(\hat{\mathbf{Q}}))^{\mathrm{t}})

\mathbf{\hat{Q}}=(\hat{Q}_{1},\ldots,\hat{Q}_{M})^{\mathrm{t}}
, where the corresponding frequency vector is
\bm{\omega}=(\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{M})^{\mathrm{t}}
.

and

M

The normal coordinates of the final and initial electronic states are linearly related by the Duschinsky transformation

is a molecular geometric displacement vector. The Duschinsky transformation can be represented by the Doktorov operator

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{a}}^{{}^{\prime}\dagger}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathbf{J}-(\mathbf{J}^{\mathrm{t}})^{-1}\right)\hat{\mathbf{a}}+\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathbf{J}+(\mathbf{J}^{\mathrm{t}})^{-1}\right)\hat{\mathbf{a}}^{\dagger}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\bm{\delta}

(7)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{cosh}(\bm{\Xi})\hat{\mathbf{a}}+\mathrm{sinh}(\bm{\Xi})\hat{\mathbf{a}}^{\dagger},

(8)

\displaystyle\hat{D}(\bm{\alpha})^{\dagger}\hat{\mathbf{a}}\hat{D}(\bm{\alpha})

IV
Numerical examples

As proof of principle, we present the vibronic spectra of naphthalene and phenanthrene for the first-order HT expansion, and benzene for the first-order and second-order HT expansions. We note that the vibronic spectra of molecules are not the full spectra of the molecules. We considered only a limited number of vibrational modes belonging to a certain symmetry block: a portion of the spectrum that contributes to the whole spectrum. Usually, in the classical simulation, the vibronic profile belonging to each vibrational symmetry block is collected separately, and the entire vibronic profile is obtained by convoluting all vibronic profiles of symmetry blocks. Therefore, if we can obtain the non-Condon profile of a symmetry block with a linear optical device, we can construct the entire vibronic spectrum by convolution. To clearly demonstrate the non-Condon effects, we generated the Franck-Condon profiles to compare the profiles with the non-Condon cases by simply ignoring the TDM operator (i.e., by assuming
\hat{\mu}=1
) and considering only the Doktorov operators.

V
Conclusion and outlook

Without consideration of practical applications, boson sampling was introduced as an easily implementable device whose computational power can surpass that of classical computers. However, the proposal by Huh et al.
Huh et al. 2015
revealed that boson sampling can also be useful for quantum simulation. Our work generalizes the aforementioned study by simulating more complex molecular processes beyond the Condon regime, thereby increasing the number of practical applications of boson sampling
Bromley et al. 2020
. The non-Condon profile was approximated with linear optics simply by using a linear combination of Gaussian boson samplers that we introduced. We note here that our new development is not just an extension of the theoretical proposal of Huh and coworkers
Huh et al. 2015
. The method opened a new way to approximate non-unitary operations in a linear optical network that it can be generalized to arbitrary non-unitary operators and digital quantum simulations. Our technique can potentially enable the extraction of relevant molecular properties that exhibit strong non-Condon effects without resorting to the use of digital quantum computers for the Condon case
Sawaya and Huh 2019
;
Sawaya et al. 2020a
;
Sawaya et al. 2020b
.

Focusing on small molecules to motivate experimental implementations, we evaluated our method for both linear and quadratic HT expansions through numerical examples. In each case, we successfully simulated the non-Condon profiles with a small error. However, because we studied probability densities, we did not consider errors due to sampling. In fact, we had infinite precision in the estimation of the probabilities. In an experiment in which the profile is reconstructed by sampling from the approximated probability density, there will be errors due to the finite number of samples considered.

Our work can be extended to other types of vibronic transitions such as resonance Raman spectroscopy
Santoro et al. 2011
, internal conversion
Peng et al. 2007
, and intersystem crossing
Etinski 2011
, as all of these are related by the computation of a non-Condon integral to evaluate the probability of a transition.
Furthermore, we can also generalize the work performed in
Sawaya and Huh 2019
for the quantum circuit model to include non-Condon effects based on the current development or to describe the non-adiabatic molecular quantum dynamics
Pauline J. Ollitrault 2020
. Finally, we can reuse the concepts developed in this paper to improve current classical algorithms used for molecular vibronic spectroscopy in the non-Condon case; for example, we can combine the current approach with the time-dependent method
Huh 2011
;
Baiardi et al. 2013
.

\displaystyle 1=\sum_{\omega=0}^{\infty}P(\omega)=\frac{1}{\mathcal{N}}\sum_{r=x,y,z}\sum_{\omega=0}^{\infty}\sum_{\mathbf{m^{{}^{\prime}}}=\mathbf{0}}^{\bm{\infty}}|\langle\mathbf{m^{{}^{\prime}}}|\hat{U}_{\mathrm{Dok}}\hat{\mu}_{r}|\mathbf{0}\rangle|^{2}\delta(\omega-\mathbf{m^{{}^{\prime}}}\cdot\bm{\omega}^{\prime}).

But,

\displaystyle\sum_{\omega=0}^{\infty}\sum_{\mathbf{m^{{}^{\prime}}}=\mathbf{0}}^{\bm{\infty}}|\langle\mathbf{m^{{}^{\prime}}}|\hat{U}_{\mathrm{Dok}}\hat{\mu}_{r}|\mathbf{0}\rangle|^{2}\delta(\omega-\mathbf{m^{{}^{\prime}}}\cdot\bm{\omega}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\omega=0}^{\infty}\sum_{\mathbf{m^{{}^{\prime}}}=\mathbf{0}}^{\bm{\infty}}\bra{\mathbf{0}}\hat{\mu}_{r}^{\dagger}\hat{U}_{\mathrm{Dok}}^{\dagger}\ket{\mathbf{m^{{}^{\prime}}}}\bra{\mathbf{m^{{}^{\prime}}}}\hat{U}_{\mathrm{Dok}}\hat{\mu}_{r}\ket{\mathbf{0}}\delta(\omega-\mathbf{m^{{}^{\prime}}}\cdot\bm{\omega}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\bra{\mathbf{0}}\hat{\mu}_{r}^{\dagger}\hat{U}_{\mathrm{Dok}}^{\dagger}\left(\sum_{\omega=0}^{\infty}\sum_{\mathbf{m^{{}^{\prime}}}=\mathbf{0}}^{\bm{\infty}}\ket{\mathbf{m^{{}^{\prime}}}}\bra{\mathbf{m^{{}^{\prime}}}}\delta(\omega-\mathbf{m^{{}^{\prime}}}\cdot\bm{\omega}^{\prime})\right)\hat{U}_{\mathrm{Dok}}\hat{\mu}_{r}\ket{\mathbf{0}}.

(28)

Appendix B
Derivation of
\exp(\kappa\hat{\mu})|\mathbf{0}\rangle

If
\kappa
was purely imaginary, then
\exp(\kappa\hat{\mu})
would be a unitary (as
\hat{\mu}
is Hermitian) and it would be straightforward to implement the linear optical setup. However, it is necessary to generalize the application of the operator for
\kappa
can be a real number. Therefore, we have,

\displaystyle\exp(\kappa\hat{\mu})|\mathbf{0}\rangle

\displaystyle=\hat{R}(\mathbf{U}^{\mathrm{t}})\exp(\kappa(\mu^{(0)}+\mathbf{b}^{\mathrm{t}}\hat{\mathbf{q}}+\hat{\mathbf{q}}^{\mathrm{t}}\mathbf{D}\hat{\mathbf{q}}))|\mathbf{0}\rangle

\displaystyle=\exp(\kappa\mu^{(0)})\hat{R}(\mathbf{U}^{\mathrm{t}})\bigotimes_{j=1}^{M}\exp(\kappa b_{j}\hat{q}_{j}+\kappa d_{j}\hat{q}_{j}^{2})|0\rangle_{j}.

Now, we need to find the expression of
\exp(\kappa b_{j}\hat{q}_{j}+\kappa d_{j}\hat{q}_{j}^{2})|0\rangle_{j}=\exp(\kappa b_{j}\hat{q}_{j})\exp(\kappa d_{j}\hat{q}_{j}^{2})|0\rangle_{j}
(as
[\hat{q}_{j},\hat{q}_{j}^{2}]=0
) in terms of Gaussian operators.
On the one hand
Fan 2003
,

\displaystyle\exp(\kappa d_{j}\hat{q}_{j}^{2})\ket{0}_{j}

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}q_{j}\exp(\kappa d_{j}q_{j}^{2})|q_{j}\rangle\langle q_{j}|\ket{0}_{j}

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}q_{j}}{\sqrt{\pi}}\exp(\kappa d_{j}q_{j}^{2}):\exp(-(q_{j}-\hat{q}_{j})^{2}):\ket{0}_{j}

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}q_{j}}{\sqrt{\pi}}:\exp\left(-(1-\kappa d_{j})q_{j}^{2}+2\hat{q}_{j}q_{j}-\hat{q}_{j}^{2}\right):\ket{0}_{j}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\kappa d_{j}}}:\exp\left(\frac{\kappa d_{j}}{1-\kappa d_{j}}\hat{q}_{j}^{2}\right):\ket{0}_{j}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\kappa d_{j}}}\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}\frac{\kappa d_{j}}{1-\kappa d_{j}}\hat{a}_{j}^{\dagger 2}\right)\ket{0}_{j},

(33)

where : : denotes normal ordering. On the other hand, the squeezing operator can be arranged in a normal ordered form,

\displaystyle\hat{S}_{j}(\xi_{j})\ket{0}_{j}

\displaystyle=\exp(\tfrac{1}{2}(\xi_{j}\hat{a}_{j}^{\dagger 2}-\xi_{j}^{*}\hat{a}_{j}^{2}))\ket{0}_{j}

\displaystyle=|s_{j}|^{\tfrac{1}{2}}\exp(\tfrac{1}{2}t_{j}\hat{a}_{j}^{\dagger 2}):\exp((s_{j}-1)\hat{a}_{j}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{j}):\exp(-\tfrac{1}{2}t_{j}\hat{a}_{j}^{2})\ket{0}_{j}

\displaystyle=|s_{j}|^{\tfrac{1}{2}}\exp(\tfrac{1}{2}t_{j}\hat{a}_{j}^{\dagger 2})\ket{0}_{j},

(34)

where
\xi_{j}=r_{j}\exp(\mathrm{i}\theta_{j})
,
t_{j}=\tanh(r_{j})\exp(\mathrm{i}\theta_{j})
,
s_{j}=\mathrm{sech}(r_{j})
. Then by identification,

\displaystyle\exp(\kappa d_{j}\hat{q}_{j}^{2})\ket{0}_{j}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{|s_{j,\kappa}|(1-\kappa d_{j})}}\hat{S}_{j}(\xi_{j,\kappa})\ket{0}_{j}.

(35)

with
t_{j,\kappa}=\tfrac{\kappa d_{j}}{1-\kappa d_{j}}
.
r_{j,\kappa},\theta_{j,\kappa}
and
\xi_{j,\kappa}
stem from

\displaystyle\exp(\kappa b_{j}\hat{q}_{j}+\kappa d_{j}\hat{q}_{j}^{2})\ket{0}_{j}

By using the formulas
