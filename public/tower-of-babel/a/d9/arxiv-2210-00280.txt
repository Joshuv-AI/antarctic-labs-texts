Joé Brendel, Felix Schlenk

Abstract
. The complex projective plane ℂ ​ P 2 \operatorname{\mathbb{C}P}^{2} contains certain Lagrangian CW-complexes called pinwheels , which have interesting rigidity properties related to solutions of the Markov equation, see for example [ 16 ] . We compute the Gromov width of the complement of pinwheels and show that it is strictly smaller than the Gromov width of ℂ ​ P 2 \operatorname{\mathbb{C}P}^{2} , meaning that pinwheels are Lagrangian barriers in the sense of Biran [ 4 ] . The accumulation points of the set of these Gromov widths are in a simple bijection with the Lagrange spectrum below 3 3 .



1.
Introduction and main results

1.1.
The problem

One measure for the size of a symplectic manifold
(M,\omega)

is the Gromov width

w_{\operatorname{G}}(M,\omega)\,=\,\sup\left\{a\mid B^{2n}(a)\mbox{ symplectically embeds into $(M,\omega)$}\right\},

where
B^{2n}(a)
denotes the open ball of capacity
a=\pi r^{2}
equipped with the standard symplectic form.
In some cases, the Gromov width is much smaller than the obstruction given by volume, see
[
18
]
.

Biran
[
4
]
discovered that there are Lagrangian submanifolds such that
w_{\operatorname{G}}(M\setminus L,\omega)<w_{\operatorname{G}}(M,\omega)
. These are called
Lagrangian barriers
. Our main result is the following.

Theorem 1.1
.

The Gromov width of the complement of a Markov pinwheel
L_{p}^{{\mathfrak{m}}}\subset\operatorname{\mathbb{C}P}^{2}
is equal to

(1.1)

w_{\operatorname{G}}\left(\operatorname{\mathbb{C}P}^{2}\setminus L_{p}^{{\mathfrak{m}}}\right)\,=\,\frac{bc}{p},

where
(p,b,c)
is the unique Markov triple with
p\geqslant b\geqslant c
which can be obtained from
{\mathfrak{m}}
by mutations preserving
p
.

In particular, since
\frac{bc}{p}<1
for all Markov triples
(p,b,c)\neq(1,1,1)
,
Markov pinwheels are Lagrangian barriers.
Before introducing the relevant terminology used in Theorem
1.1
, let us briefly motivate this result.

As a simple example, take the round sphere
S^{2}
of area
1
,
and let
C_{a}
be an embedded circle enclosing area
a\leqslant\frac{1}{2}
.
Then
w_{\operatorname{G}}(S^{2})=1
while
w_{\operatorname{G}}(S^{2}\setminus C_{a})=1-a
.
Hence
C_{a}
is the stronger a Lagrangian barrier the larger
a
is.
In the extreme case
a=\frac{1}{2}
, the Lagrangian
C_{a}
is
monotone
.
Recall that a Lagrangian is called monotone if the corresponding area and Maslov homomorphisms are positively proportional.
From now on, we restrict our attention to
M=\operatorname{\mathbb{C}P}^{2}
, which contains the Clifford torus
T_{\operatorname{Cliff}}
and the real projective plane
\operatorname{\mathbb{R}P}^{2}

as monotone Lagrangians.
Both can be viewed as four-dimensional generalizations of the equatorial circle in
S^{2}
,
and their relative Gromov widths are
w_{\operatorname{G}}(\operatorname{\mathbb{C}P}^{2}\setminus\operatorname{\mathbb{R}P}^{2})=\frac{1}{2}
(see
[
4
]
)
and
w_{\operatorname{G}}\bigl(\operatorname{\mathbb{C}P}^{2}\setminus\operatorname{T}_{\operatorname{Cliff}}\bigr)=\frac{2}{3}
(see
[
5
, Corollary 1.2.5]
). There seem to be no known examples of monotone Lagrangian surfaces in
\operatorname{\mathbb{C}P}^{2}
for which
the quotient
w_{\operatorname{G}}(M\setminus L)/w_{\operatorname{G}}(M)
is not
\frac{1}{2}
or
\frac{2}{3}
, and the only other known value for this quotient in a closed symplectic four-manifold is
1

(the Clifford torus in
S^{2}\times S^{2}
is not a barrier).
Although Markov pinwheels are not smooth, they are monotone in an appropriate sense – see the appendix.
Theorem
1.1
thus illustrates that many more quotients of Gromov widths can be realized for monotone Lagrangians if one allows them to be slightly singular. In Section
3
we study the set of numbers which appear more thoroughly and establish a connection with the Lagrange and Markov spectra below
3
.

1.2.
Markov triples

We give a brief overview on Markov triples, highlighting the facts that will be used in the paper.
For more details, see for example the book
[
1
]
.
A
Markov triple
is a triple
(a,b,c)
of natural numbers (called
Markov numbers
)
satisfying the
Markov equation
,

(1.2)

a^{2}+b^{2}+c^{2}\,=\,3abc.

We consider Markov triples to be ordered in the sense that we write
(a,b,c)

if
a\geqslant b\geqslant c
. We denote the set of Markov triples by
\mathfrak{M}
.
Fixing two out of three numbers in a triple turns (
1.2
) into a quadratic equation with two integer solutions.
This means that, from one triple
(a,b,c)
one can obtain three new triples by
so-called
mutations
,

(1.3)

(a,b,c)\xrightarrow{\,c\;}(3ab-c,a,b),\quad(a,b,c)\xrightarrow{\,b\;}(3ac-b,a,c),\quad(a,b,c)\xrightarrow{\,a\;}(b,c,3bc-a).

Therefore, Markov triples can be naturally arranged in a trivalent tree with root
(1,1,1)
, and one can show that this tree contains all solutions. The tree is degenerate at the first two entries, in the sense that it contains repeated Markov triples (which we omit).

The following conjecture was first stated by Frobenius in 1913 and is still open.

Conjecture 1.2

(Uniqueness Conjecture)
.

Every Markov triple is uniquely determined by its maximal Markov number.

Note that if the uniqueness conjecture holds, then the statement of Theorem
1.1
can be simplified. Indeed, the relevant Markov triple
(p,b,c)\in{\mathfrak{M}}
is then simply the unique one
in which
p
appears as the maximal entry.
Let us check that the Markov triple we refer to in the statement of Theorem
1.1

is well-defined, independently of the Uniqueness conjecture.
Let
p\in{\mathfrak{m}}
be a Markov number in a Markov triple
{\mathfrak{m}}\in{\mathfrak{M}}
.
By
\bigwedge({\mathfrak{m}},p)
we denote the set of all Markov triples which can be obtained from
{\mathfrak{m}}

by mutations preserving
p
. This set forms a bivalent subtree of the Markov tree,
since at each triple in
\bigwedge({\mathfrak{m}},p)
there are two mutations preserving
p
.
The subtree is rooted in a unique Markov triple containing
p
as maximal element –
this is the element we refer to in Theorem
1.1
.
Indeed, take
{\mathfrak{m}}^{\prime}\in\bigwedge({\mathfrak{m}},p)
. If
p
is not the maximal entry in
{\mathfrak{m}}
,
we can repeatedly apply the third mutation in (
1.3
).
Under this mutation, the maximal entry strictly decreases and hence
p
will end up being maximal.
The triple we obtain is unique, since the maximal entry strictly increases when applying one of the
other two mutations in (
1.3
).

1.3.
Lagrangian pinwheels

We consider the symplectic manifold
(\operatorname{\mathbb{C}P}^{2},\omega_{\operatorname{FS}})
, where
\omega_{\operatorname{FS}}
is the Fubini–Study form normalized
so that
\int_{\operatorname{\mathbb{C}P}^{1}}\omega_{\operatorname{FS}}=1
. We are interested in
Lagrangian pinwheels
in
\operatorname{\mathbb{C}P}^{2}
. We give a brief overview here, see
[
16
]
and Section
2.1
for more details.

Definition 1.3
.

Let
p\in\mathbb{N}_{\geqslant 2}
. The topological space obtained from the unit disk
D

by quotienting out the action of the group of the
p
-th roots of unity on
\partial D
is called
p
-
pinwheel
.

For example, the
2
-pinwheel is
\operatorname{\mathbb{R}P}^{2}
. The image of
\partial D
in the quotient is called the
core circle
. For
p>2
the
p
-pinwheel is not smooth at points of the core circle. Pinwheels appear as skeleta of certain rational homology balls
B_{p,q}
such that the image of the interior of the disk is Lagrangian. The number
q\in\mathbb{Z}
describes the winding of the Lagrangian tangent plane about the core circle and is only determined up to sign and modulo
p
. We call a pinwheel
L_{p}\subset M

Lagrangian
(p,q)
-pinwheel
if it admits a neighbourhood which is symplectomorphic to the normal form of
L_{p}\subset B_{p,q}
as discussed in
[
16
, 2.1]
. Evans–Smith
[
16
]
have studied Lagrangian embeddings of disjoint unions of pinwheels,

(1.4)

L_{p_{1}}\sqcup\dots\sqcup L_{p_{N}}\hookrightarrow\operatorname{\mathbb{C}P}^{2}.

In fact, they give a complete classification of these embeddings in terms of the set of pairs
\{(p_{i},q_{i})\}_{i\in 1,\ldots,N}
.

Theorem 1.4

(Evans–Smith
[
16
]
)
.

Let there be a Lagrangian embedding of a disjoint union of pinwheels into
\operatorname{\mathbb{C}P}^{2}
as in (
1.4
). Then
N\leqslant 3
, and if
N=3
, then
(p_{1},p_{2},p_{3})
is a Markov triple. If
N\in\{1,2\}
, then the
p_{i}
can be completed to a Markov triple.

See
[
16
, Theorem 1.2]
for a more detailed statement.

Note that in Theorem
1.1
we restrict our attention to certain types of Lagrangian pinwheels,
which we call
Markov pinwheels
. This comes from the fact that it is unknown whether Lagrangian pinwheel embeddings are unique up to symplectomorphism
(except for
L_{2}^{(2,1,1)}=\operatorname{\mathbb{R}P}^{2}
), meaning that we do not know if Lagrangian pinwheels can be mapped to one another by an ambient symplectomorphism, see also Remark
1.5
.
Therefore, given a Markov triple
{\mathfrak{m}}=(a,b,c)\in{\mathfrak{M}}
, we fix a particular embedding

(1.5)

L_{a}\sqcup L_{b}\sqcup L_{c}\hookrightarrow\operatorname{\mathbb{C}P}^{2}.

This embedding comes from almost toric fibrations of
\operatorname{\mathbb{C}P}^{2}
, see
[
32
,
35
,
36
]
.
Markov pinwheels are defined as certain subsets fibering over segments in the almost toric base diagrams of
\operatorname{\mathbb{C}P}^{2}
,
see for example
[
16
, Remark 2.9]
.
This will be discussed in detail in Section
2.1
, and a precise definition of Markov pinwheels is given in
Definition
2.1
.
We denote Markov pinwheels obtained as connected components of the image of
(
1.5
)

by
L_{a}^{{\mathfrak{m}}},L_{b}^{{\mathfrak{m}}},L_{c}^{{\mathfrak{m}}}\subset\operatorname{\mathbb{C}P}^{2}
.

Remark 1.5

(Uniqueness of Lagrangian pinwheels)
.

We prove in Lemma
2.2
that the Markov pinwheels
L_{p}^{{\mathfrak{m}}}
and
L_{p}^{{\mathfrak{m}}^{\prime}}
are equal as sets
whenever
{\mathfrak{m}}^{\prime}\in\bigwedge({\mathfrak{m}},p)
.
The related question whether
L_{p}^{{\mathfrak{m}}}
is symplectomorphic to every
L_{p}^{{\mathfrak{m}}^{\prime}}
seems transcendent,
because it is equivalent to the Uniqueness Conjecture
1.2
.
Indeed, the positive answer to this question would imply that
p
determines the extrinsic parameter
q

(up to sign and modulo
p
),
and it is known that this implies the Uniqueness Conjecture,
see
[
16
, Section 2.1]
and
[
1
, Proposition 3.15]
.
A much more approachable conjecture is that Lagrangian
(p,q)
-pinwheels in
\operatorname{\mathbb{C}P}^{2}

are unique up to symplectomorphism.

.

Almost toric fibrations

Almost toric fibrations (ATFs) were introduced in
[

and later used to great effect in

In

which are in bijection with Markov triples.
Furthermore, using the ATF-toolkit one can realize the algebraic mutations of Markov triples (

2.1.
Markov pinwheels

The main goal of this subsection is to define Markov pinwheels
L^{{\mathfrak{m}}}_{p}\subset\operatorname{\mathbb{C}P}^{2}
in terms of almost toric fibrations.
Let us stress again that it is unknown whether these pinwheels are unique up to Hamiltonian isotopy.
We therefore first define them as sets,
and then prove that the definition is invariant under mutations preserving
p
(Lemma
2.2
)
and that pinwheels can be made to lie arbitrarily close to the vertices of the ATF-base diagram by modifying the fibration structure
(Lemma
2.3

Both Lemmata will be used in the proof of Theorem
1.1

We fix almost toric fibrations

(2.1)

\pi_{{\mathfrak{m}}}\colon\operatorname{\mathbb{C}P}^{2}\rightarrow\Delta_{{\mathfrak{m}}},\quad{\mathfrak{m}}\in{\mathfrak{M}},

Every triangle

Lemma 2.2

Let

Proof.
The claim follows straightforwardly from the ATF-toolkit. Let

and suppose that we apply the mutation

which preserves

(the Markov number which is eliminated in the triple). When applying an ATF-mutation to a vertex of
\Delta_{{\mathfrak{m}}}
, the fibration structure changes only on an arbitrarily small neighbourhood of the segment defined by the dashed line at the vertex in question and passing through the central point of
\Delta_{{\mathfrak{m}}}
. In our normalization, the central point lies at integral affine distance
\frac{1}{3}
to all three edges of
\Delta_{{\mathfrak{m}}}

does not change the fibration structure over

and hence the general claim follows from iterating.

\square

Proof.

.

Relation with the Lagrange spectrum, and order in the Markov tree

3.1.
Accumulation at the Lagrange spectrum below 3

Dirichlet proved that for every irrational number
\alpha

there exist infinitely many rational numbers
\frac{p}{q}
such that

\left|\alpha-\frac{p}{q}\right|\,\leq\,\frac{1}{q^{2}},

and Roth proved that, in general, one cannot improve on the exponent
2
.
So one tries to improve the constant
1
:
The Lagrange number of
\alpha
is defined as the supremum over
L\geq 1

such that

\left|\alpha-\frac{p}{q}\right|\,\leq\,\frac{1}{L\;\!q^{2}}

for infinitely many
\frac{p}{q}
.
The set
{\mathcal{L}}=\left\{L(\alpha)\mid\mbox{$\alpha$ irrational}\right\}\subset[1,\infty]

is called the Lagrange spectrum.
Hurwitz showed that for the Golden ratio,
L\bigl((1+\sqrt{5})/2\bigr)=\sqrt{5}
,
and that this is the minimum of
{\mathcal{L}}
.
The next numbers in
{\mathcal{L}}
are
\sqrt{8}
and
\sqrt{221}/5
.

Let
f(x,y)=a\;\!x^{2}+b\;\!xy+c\;\!y^{2}
with
a,b,c\in\mathbb{R}

be a quadradic form on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, and assume that
f
is indefinite:

\Delta(f)=b^{2}-4ac>0
.
Set

m(f)\,=\,\inf\left\{|f(x,y)|:f(x,y)\neq 0,\;(x,y)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\right\},

and define the Markov spectrum as the set of numbers
\sqrt{\Delta(f)}/m(f)

obtained in this way.

Markov proved that the Markov spectrum below
3
is equal to the Lagrange spectrum below
3
.
Furthermore, he showed that this set is parametrized by the set of Markov numbers
{\mathcal{M}}

(as defined in Subsection
1.2
) by the bijection

\lambda\colon{\mathcal{M}}\to{\mathcal{L}}\cap[1,3),\quad\lambda(a)=\sqrt{9-4/a^{2}}\,.

We refer to
[
1
]
,
[
7
]
, and
[
10
, Chapter 2]
for much more on this beautiful story.

Now fix a Markov number
a
.
Take a Markov triple
{\mathfrak{m}}
with
a\in{\mathfrak{m}}
,
and as in the introduction consider the subtree
\bigwedge({\mathfrak{m}},a)
of Markov triples
obtained from
{\mathfrak{m}}
by mutations that preserve
a
.
Hence
\bigwedge({\mathfrak{m}},1)
is the left-most branch of the Markov tree
(the Fibonacci branch, formed by the triples
(F_{2n+1},F_{2n-1},1)
with odd-index Fibonacchi numbers),

is the right-most branch of the Markov tree
(the Pell branch, formed by the triples

and for

the tree

is a
\bigwedge

.

.

Proof of Proposition

It suffices to prove the claim for the case that
