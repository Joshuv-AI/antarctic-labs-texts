A surrogate by exchangeability approach to the Curie-Weiss model
Yacine Barhoumi-Andréani, Marius Butzek, Peter Eichelsbacher

Abstract
. We introduce a new general concept of surrogate random variable, the “surrogate by exchangeability” that allows to study the class of random variables that can be decomposed by means of an independent randomisation. As an example, we treat the case of the Curie-Weiss model using the explicit construction of its De Finetti measure of exchangeability. Writing the magnetisation as a sum of i.i.d.’s randomised by the underlying De Finetti random variable, the surrogate study shows that the appearance of a phase transition can be understood as a competition between these two sources of randomness, the Gaussian regime corresponding to a marginally relevant disordered system.

The Curie-Weiss model of

\displaystyle(X_{k})_{1\leqslant k\leqslant n}\sim\textrm{i.i.d. Ber}_{\left\{\pm 1\right\}}(1/2),\qquad\qquad S_{n}:=\sum_{k=1}^{n}X_{k}.

One classical modification of this model consists in adding an additional parameter, the external magnetic field

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\!\left(X_{1}^{(\beta,\mu)}\!,\dots,\,X_{n}^{(\beta,\mu)}\right)}:=\frac{e^{\frac{\beta}{2n}S_{n}^{2}+\mu S_{n}}}{\mathbb{E}\!\left(e^{\frac{\beta}{2n}S_{n}^{2}+\mu S_{n}}\right)}\bullet\mathbb{P}_{(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})}.

This simple model of statistical mechanics was originally introduced by Pierre Curie in 1895

Replacing a complex model with a simpler one whose overall behavior may be examined through explicit computations allows to get an intuition of the features that can be inferred from the original model, sometimes with no alteration. This is the case of the Curie-Weiss model when it comes to spontaneous magnetisation and metastability. In particular, it does exhibit a phase transition with three distinct behaviours at high, critical and low temperature.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left(M_{n}^{(\beta)}-\widehat{M}_{n}^{(\beta)}\Big|M_{n}^{(\beta)}\right)=\mathbb{E}\!\left(X_{I}^{(\beta)}-\widehat{X}_{I}^{(\beta)}\Big|M_{n}^{(\beta)}\right)=\frac{M_{n}^{(\beta)}}{n}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\!\left(\widehat{X}_{i}^{(\beta)}\Big|M_{n}^{(\beta)}\right).

The proof of Theorem

We hope that future works will expand this philosophy to the variety of available statistical mechanical models in addition to go beyond the scope of the sole fluctuations.

1.2.2.
A quick overview of the philosophy

In a broad sense, exchangeability is concerned with the action of a particular group or algebra, usually infinite. This action leaves invariant the law of the sequence of random variables, which, by duality between functions and measures, is nothing but a probabilistic manifestation of representation theory: the measure underlying the sequence can be disintegrated with the extremal points of the convex set of measures in a similar way representations are decomposed by means of irreducible representations
2
2

2

Characters of representations (i.e. their traces) are harmonic functions for a relevant Laplacian, and a mixture of measures can be seen as a decomposition of harmonic functions on the Martin boundary of an underlying Markov field, see e.g.
[
28
]
.

. Problems having such group actions hidden in their core are famously known to be solvable; this is the theory of
integrable systems
. The recent field of integrable probability is another instance of such a phenomenon in probability theory
3
3

3

As noted by A. Borodin and V. Gorin
[
4
]

(…) the “solvable” or
integrable
members of this class [of models] should be viewed as projections of much more powerful objects whose origins lie in representation theory. In a way, this is similar to integrable systems that can also be viewed as projections of representation theoretic objects; and this is one reason we use the words integrable probability to describe the phenomenon.

.

In some sense that will be made precise in this article, this phenomenon will be reproduced: the knowledge of a structure of mixture will be used to decompose the magnetisation with two levels of randomness, one of the random variables being a sum of i.i.d.’s conditionally to the other one, as in the field of random walks in random environment. We will then perform a probabilistic approximation that will, quite surprisingly, preserve all the information of the phase transition picture, and even more at the level of the speed of convergence (in a continuous or Kolmogorov distance). The Curie-Weiss model is already the mean-field approximation of the Ising model that retains its phase transition aspect; the surrogate random variables that we will introduce constitute a last piece of approximation (arguably,
the
last piece of approximation) that will again conserve this picture.

We will describe in detail this philosophy in §
2.2
, but we can already give an overview of the procedure. Consider a mixture

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})}=\int\mathbb{P}_{(Y_{1}(p),\dots,Y_{n}(p))}\nu_{n}(dp),\qquad\nu_{n}(dp)=\mathbb{P}\!\left({\boldsymbol{P}}_{\!\!n}\in dp\right)

with mixing measure
\nu_{n}
and independent random variables
(Y_{k}(p))_{k}
for all
p
. Then, one has

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{X_{1}+\cdots+X_{n}}=\int\mathbb{P}_{Y_{1}(p)+\cdots+Y_{n}(p)}\nu_{n}(dp)

and one can study the additional structure that consists in the sum of i.i.d.’s
S_{n}(p):=\sum_{k=1}^{n}Y_{k}(p)
and the “random environment” given by the mixture measure
\nu_{n}
, or equivalently, the randomisation
{\boldsymbol{P}}_{\!\!n}\sim\nu_{n}
. Suppose now that, for fixed
p
,
S_{n}(p)
can be approximated in distribution by another random variable
\widetilde{S}_{n}(p)
, for instance
\mu_{n}(p)+\sigma_{n}(p)G
with
G\sim\mathscr{N}(0,1)
in the continuous setting, or
\operatorname{Po}(\mu_{n}(p))
in the discrete setting (with
\mu_{n}(p):=\mathbb{E}\!\left(S_{n}(p)\right)
, etc.). If one can make sense, quantitatively, of the approximation

\displaystyle\int\mathbb{P}_{S_{n}(p)}\nu_{n}(dp)\approx\int\mathbb{P}_{\widetilde{S}_{n}(p)}\nu_{n}(dp)

then, one can study instead the replacement random variable coming from the CLT, or
CLT surrogate

\widetilde{S}_{n}({\boldsymbol{P}}_{\!\!n})\sim\int\mathbb{P}_{\widetilde{S}_{n}(p)}\nu_{n}(dp)
. As one can see, this philosophy is very general and applies to a broad class of models enjoying a mixture property (see e.g. a list in §
5
).

The rationale for Theorem
1.1
will then take the form of an interplay between the CLT approximation
S_{n}(p)\approx\widetilde{S}_{n}(p)
and the mixture approximation
{\boldsymbol{P}}_{\!\!n}\approx{\boldsymbol{P}}^{\prime}_{\!\!n}
for some
{\boldsymbol{P}}^{\prime}_{\!\!n}
:

(1)

when the randomisation
{\boldsymbol{P}}_{\!\!n}
is “weak”, the CLT approximation dominates and the limit is Gaussian; this corresponds to the case
\beta<1
. In this particular regime, the randomisation is also shown to converge in law to a Gaussian, and the algebraic structure underlying the construction of the CLT surrogate entails that the limiting random variable is the
sum
of these two Gaussians.

The limiting Gaussian is thus made out of two independent Gaussians, one coming from the CLT for sums of i.i.d.’s and another one coming from the randomisation, a dichotomy that went unnoticed in all previous proofs of Theorem
1.1
. This subtle mixture of two sources of randomness with survival at the limit of a part of the disorder (i.e. the randomisation considered as a random environment) is named, in the language of statistical mechanics, a
marginally relevant disordered system
. It is a reminiscence of the standard energy-entropy competition, the randomness being naturally associated to the entropy, see
[
21
,
22
,
23
]
.

One classical such case concerns the convergence of the rescaled KPZ equation towards the Edwards-Wilkinson universality class, see Remark
3.2
and
[
6
,
53
]
. To the best of the authors’ knowledge, this is the first time that the Gaussian limit in the Curie-Weiss model is analysed through the lens of such a framework which lies at the heart of statistical mechanics.

(2)

The case
\beta\geqslant 1
being non Gaussian, it can be assumed that the randomisation will no longer be marginally relevant (since the sum of i.i.d.’s is approximately Gaussian), and indeed, the surrogate behaviour is dominated by the randomisation with a total disapperance of the Gaussian sum.

(3)

The transition around
\beta=1
consists in a competition between CLT and randomisation, i.e. between the disordered environment and the random walk given by the sum of i.i.d.’s. The proportion of both sources of randomness is particularly visible in their contribution to the speed of convergence (in any type of norm), and one has to design a more refined type of surrogate to analyse it correctly, using the known Bernoulli coupling in
p
. We refer to §
3.3
for a precise description.

In order to control exactly the error originated from the replacement of the original random variable by its surrogate, the approximation in law must be quantitative. This is the difference between Theorem
1.1
and Theorem
1.2
: we will not only be concerned with the limits in law but also with their speed of convergence, in the Fortet-Mourier norm whose test function admit a certain degree of smoothness and in the Kolmogorov norm whose test functions are indicators of half infinite intervals of the real line.

Here, not only can one save a considerable computational effort by using the classical Berry-Esséen theorem for sums of i.i.d.’s, but in addition comes an unexpected bonus that arises as a byproduct of the use of such a surrogate: by integrating a fraction of the randomness of the surrogate, the indicator functions in the Kolmogorov distance are replaced by smooth functions. This transfert from randomness to smoothness is a very agreable surprise that reduces the discontinuous norm estimate to a smooth one for a related random variable, allowing thus to bypass the usual pathologies of discontinuous test functions distances (see §
4
). This is one of the advantages of the surrogate by exchangeability approach: it does not differentiate between the discontinuous and the continuous probability norms.

2.
General theory

2.1.
Reminders on the De Finetti measure of the Curie-Weiss model

The celebrated De Finetti theorem
[
14
]
can be stated as follows:

Theorem 2.1

(De Finetti, 1969)
.

Every exchangeable infinite sequence of random variables is a mixture of an i.i.d. sequence.

Here, exchangeability for an infinite sequence is understood in the sense of the action of the inductive limit of the symmetric group, i.e. every
finite
sub-sequence of the sequence is invariant by a (finite) permutation. This notion has been extended in several ways, in a finite-dimensional version by Diaconis-Freedman
[
16
]
, using other groups such as the projective limit of symmetric groups by Gnedin-Olshanski
[
30
]
and orthogonal groups by Olshanski-Vershik
[
41
]
and Schoenberg
[
46
]
, etc. See e.g. Accardi
[
1
]
for an account of subtleties on this theorem and further references, or Diaconis-Freedman
[
16
]
for a presentation of the topic in relation with the computation of total variation distances.

The Curie-Weiss spins are clearly exchangeable since the measure (
1
) is invariant by permutation of the spins. Since
n
is fixed, the De Finetti theorem
a priori
does not apply, but Lemma
B.1
shows nevertheless that this is the case. There exists a measure
\widetilde{\nu}_{n,\beta}:\!\left[0,1\right]\to\!\left[0,1\right]
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{n}^{(\beta)}\equiv\mathbb{P}_{(X_{1}^{(\beta)},\dots,X_{n}^{(\beta)})}=\int_{\!\left[0,1\right]}\mathbb{P}_{(X_{1}(p),\dots,X_{n}(p))}\,\widetilde{\nu}_{n,\beta}(dp),

(6)

where
(X_{k}(p))_{1\leqslant k\leqslant n}\sim i.i.d.\operatorname{Ber}_{\left\{\pm 1\right\}}(p)
.

Classically writing

\displaystyle X_{k}(p)=2\mathds{1}_{\left\{U_{k}<p\right\}}-1,\qquad U_{k}\sim\mathscr{U}([0,1]),

(7)

one can rewrite (
6
) in a more probabilistic way using a random variable
\widetilde{V}_{n,\beta}\sim\widetilde{\nu}_{n,\beta}
independent of
(X_{k}(p))_{1\leqslant k\leqslant n,p\in[0,1]}
. We then have the randomisation equality

\displaystyle\hskip-11.38092pt(X_{1}^{(\beta)},\dots,X_{n}^{(\beta)})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{L}}}{{=}}(X_{1}(\widetilde{V}_{n,\beta}),\dots,X_{n}(\widetilde{V}_{n,\beta}))=\!\left(2\mathds{1}_{\{U_{1}<\widetilde{V}_{n,\beta}\}}-1,\dots,2\mathds{1}_{\{U_{n}<\widetilde{V}_{n,\beta}\}}-1\right).

(8)

The measure
\widetilde{\nu}_{n,\beta}
(or the random variable
\widetilde{V}_{n,\beta}
) is well-known for the Curie-Weiss model. It is given by (see
[
34
, thm. 5.6, (164), (165)]
or Lemma
B.1
for a derivation)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\nu}_{n,\beta}(dp)=\widetilde{f}_{n,\beta}(p)dp,\qquad\widetilde{f}_{n,\beta}(p):=\frac{1}{\mathcal{Z}_{n,\beta}}e^{-\frac{n}{2\beta}\operatorname{Argtanh}(2p-1)^{2}-(n/2+1)\ln(1-(2p-1)^{2})},

(9)

with an explicit renormalisation constant
\mathcal{Z}_{n,\beta}
defined by the equality
\int_{0}^{1}\widetilde{f}_{n,\beta}(p)dp=1
. We recall here that
\operatorname{Argtanh}(x)=\frac{1}{2}\log\left|\frac{1+x}{1-x}\right|
for
\left|x\right|<1
. For the sake of completeness, we give a proof of this result in Annex
B
.

If instead of considering the parameter
p\in[0,1]
of the Bernoulli random variables, one encodes the De Finetti measure with the expectation parameter
t:=2p-1\in[-1,1]
, one gets

\displaystyle\nu_{n,\beta}(dt)=f_{n,\beta}(t)dt,\qquad f_{n,\beta}(t):=\frac{1}{\mathcal{Z}_{n,\beta}}e^{-\frac{n}{2\beta}\operatorname{Argtanh}(t)^{2}-(n/2+1)\ln(1-t^{2})},

(10)

and a randomisation by an independent random variable
V_{n,\beta}\sim\nu_{n,\beta}
.

2.2.
Surrogate random variables in probability theory

Consider the following classical problem in extreme value theory: compute the fluctuations of
{\boldsymbol{M}}_{\!n}:=\max_{1\leqslant k\leqslant n}Z_{k}
for independent random variables
(Z_{k})_{k\geqslant 1}
when
n\to+\infty
. One way to proceed is to note that

\displaystyle\left\{{\boldsymbol{M}}_{\!n}\leqslant x\right\}=\left\{\forall\,k\leqslant n,\,Z_{k}\leqslant x\right\}=\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathds{1}_{\left\{Z_{k}>x\right\}}=0\right\}.

(11)

The problem amounts thus to analyse the fluctuations of the parametric random variable

\displaystyle S_{n}(x_{n}):=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathds{1}_{\left\{Z_{k}>x_{n}\right\}},\qquad x_{n}:=\mu_{n}+\sigma_{n}x,

for given numbers
\mu_{n},\sigma_{n}
that one has to tune in order to get the correct result. Since
S_{n}(x_{n})
is a sum of independent
\left\{0,1\right\}
-Bernoulli random variables, one can proceed to a Poisson approximation
S_{n}(x_{n})\approx\operatorname{Po}(f(x))
, using for instance the Chen-Stein method
[
24
]
, resulting in:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\!\left({\boldsymbol{M}}_{\!n}\leqslant x_{n}\right)=\mathbb{P}\!\left(S_{n}(x_{n})=0\right)=\mathbb{P}\!\left(\operatorname{Po}(f(x))=0\right)+o(1)=e^{-f(x)}+o(1).

By doing so, one has replaced the estimation of a non linear functional of
(Z_{k})_{k}
, a maximum, by a simpler problem, the estimation of a sum. Such a sum is a
surrogate
random variable. Its study is equivalent to the original problem while being arguably simpler.

The equality (
11
) allows to use a strict equality to replace the original problem by the surrogate problem, and the approximation is only performed at the level of
S_{n}(x_{n})
, but one could reverse the steps or add an additional approximation step in between (replacing the equality (
11
) by an approximation) as long as the original problem is not fundamentally impacted. This is what we perform now.

3.
Application to the Curie-Weiss magnetisation in smooth distance

3.1.
The case
\beta<1

\left|h_{n}(x)\right|\leqslant\left|\!\left|h\right|\!\right|_{\infty}

2\left|\!\left|h\right|\!\right|_{\infty}
but since
\int_{[-\sqrt{n},\sqrt{n}]}g_{\beta}=\mathbb{P}\!\left(\left|G_{\beta}\right|\leqslant\sqrt{n}\right)=O(1-e^{-n/2})=O(1)
which does not tend to
0
, one must work on
\left|\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\int_{[-\sqrt{n},\sqrt{n}]}(h_{n}-h_{G})g_{\beta}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\right|
direclty. Since one has the same random variables
G
and
G_{\beta}
, one has a coupling that allows to write

\displaystyle\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\int_{[-\sqrt{n},\sqrt{n}]}(h_{n}-h_{G})g_{\beta}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\!\left(h\!\left(G\sqrt{1-\frac{G_{\beta}^{2}}{n}}+G_{\beta}\right)\mathds{1}_{\left\{\left|G_{\beta}\right|\leqslant\sqrt{n}\right\}}-h(G+G_{\beta})\mathds{1}_{\left\{\left|G_{\beta}\right|\leqslant\sqrt{n}\right\}}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip-28.45274pt=\mathbb{E}\!\left(G\!\left(\!\sqrt{1-\tfrac{G_{\beta}^{2}}{n}}-1\right)\mathds{1}_{\left\{\left|G_{\beta}\right|\leqslant\sqrt{n}\right\}}h^{\prime}\!\left(\!G_{\beta}+G+U\,G\!\left(\!\sqrt{1-\tfrac{G_{\beta}^{2}}{n}}\,-1\right)\!\right)\!\right)

with
U\sim\mathscr{U}([0,1])
independent of
(G,G_{\beta})
. Using
x_{+}:=\max\{x,0\}=x\mathds{1}_{\left\{x\geqslant 0\right\}}
and
\left\{\left|G_{\beta}\right|\leqslant\sqrt{n}\right\}=\left\{1-G_{\beta}^{2}/n\geqslant 0\right\}
, one then gets

\displaystyle\left|\int_{[-\sqrt{n},\sqrt{n}]}(h_{n}-h_{G})g_{\beta}\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathbb{E}\!\left(\left|G\!\left(\sqrt{\!\left(1-\frac{G_{\beta}^{2}}{n}\right)_{+}}-1\right)\right|\right)\,\left|\!\left|h^{\prime}\right|\!\right|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\!\left(\left|G\right|\right)\,\left|\!\left|h^{\prime}\right|\!\right|_{\infty}\times\mathbb{E}\!\left(\left|\sqrt{\!\left(1-\frac{G_{\beta}^{2}}{n}\right)_{+}}-1\right|\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{\mathbb{E}\!\left(G_{\beta}^{2}\right)}{n}\,\left|\!\left|h^{\prime}\right|\!\right|_{\infty},

where we have used
1-\sqrt{1-x}\leqslant x
for
0\leqslant x\leqslant 1
and
\mathbb{E}\!\left(\left|G\right|\right)\leqslant\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\!\left(G^{2}\right)}=1
.

The important quantity to bound is thus

\displaystyle\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\left|\!\left|h_{n}\right|\!\right|_{\infty}\int_{[-\sqrt{n},\sqrt{n}]}\left|g_{n}-g_{\beta}\right|\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\leqslant\left|\!\left|h\right|\!\right|_{\infty}\int_{[-\sqrt{n},\sqrt{n}]}\left|g_{n}-g_{\beta}\right|.

One thus needs to estimate carefully

\displaystyle\delta(g_{n},g_{\beta})

\displaystyle:=\int_{[-\sqrt{n},\sqrt{n}]}\left|g_{n}-g_{\beta}\right|

\displaystyle=\int_{[-\sqrt{n},\sqrt{n}]}\left|\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}f_{n,\beta}\!\left(\frac{x}{\sqrt{n}}\right)-\sqrt{\frac{1-\beta}{2\pi\beta}}e^{-\frac{1-\beta}{\beta}\frac{x^{2}}{2}}\right|dx

\displaystyle=2\int_{[0,\sqrt{n}]}\left|\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}f_{n,\beta}\!\left(\frac{x}{\sqrt{n}}\right)-\sqrt{\frac{1-\beta}{2\pi\beta}}e^{-\frac{1-\beta}{\beta}\frac{x^{2}}{2}}\right|dx

by symmetry of
g_{n}
and
g_{\beta}
.

Define

\varphi_{n}(x):=\frac{n}{2\beta}\operatorname{Argtanh}\!\left(\frac{x}{\sqrt{n}}\right)^{2}+\!\left(\frac{n}{2}+1\right)\ln\!\left(1-\frac{x^{2}}{n}\right),\quad C_{\beta}:=\frac{1-\beta}{\beta},

so that

f_{n,\beta}\!\left(\frac{x}{\sqrt{n}}\right)=\frac{1}{\mathcal{Z}_{n,\beta}}e^{-\varphi_{n}(x)},\quad g_{\beta}(x)=\sqrt{\frac{C_{\beta}}{2\pi}}e^{-C_{\beta}\frac{x^{2}}{2}}.

One then has

\displaystyle\delta(g_{n},g_{\beta})

\displaystyle\leqslant\left|\frac{1}{\mathcal{Z}_{n,\beta}\sqrt{n}}-\sqrt{\frac{C_{\beta}}{2\pi}}\right|\int_{[-\sqrt{n},\sqrt{n}]}e^{-\varphi_{n}(x)}dx+\sqrt{\frac{C_{\beta}}{2\pi}}\int_{[-\sqrt{n},\sqrt{n}]}\left|e^{-\varphi_{n}(x)}-e^{-C_{\beta}\frac{x^{2}}{2}}\right|dx.

The first quantity is

\displaystyle\left|\sqrt{\frac{C_{\beta}}{2\pi}}\times\mathcal{Z}_{n,\beta}\sqrt{n}-1\right|\int_{[-\sqrt{n},\sqrt{n}]}g_{n}

\displaystyle=\left|\sqrt{\frac{C_{\beta}}{2\pi}}\times\mathcal{Z}_{n,\beta}\sqrt{n}-1\right|\mathbb{P}\!\left(\left|X_{n}\right|\leqslant\sqrt{n}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\left|\sqrt{\frac{C_{\beta}}{2\pi}}\times\mathcal{Z}_{n,\beta}\sqrt{n}-1\right|

and an analysis of its speed of convergence to 0 is performed in Lemma
A.1
.

The important quantity is the second one. Define

\gamma_{n}:=\int_{(-\sqrt{n},\sqrt{n})}\left|e^{-\varphi_{n}(x)}-e^{-C_{\beta}\frac{x^{2}}{2}}\right|dx=\int_{(-\sqrt{n},\sqrt{n})}\left|e^{-(\varphi_{n}(x)-C_{\beta}\frac{x^{2}}{2})}-1\right|e^{-C_{\beta}\frac{x^{2}}{2}}dx.

Supposing that the quantity inside the absolute value in the second line was bounded by a constant
D_{n}
on
(-\sqrt{n},\sqrt{n})
, one would have
\gamma_{n}=O\!\left(D_{n}\int_{(-\sqrt{n},\sqrt{n})}e^{-C_{\beta}\frac{x^{2}}{2}}dx\right)=O\!\left(D_{n}\mathbb{P}\!\left(\left|G_{\beta}\right|\leqslant\sqrt{n}\right)\right)=O\!\left(D_{n}\right)
. This is not the case as
\operatorname{Argtanh}^{2}(\frac{x}{\sqrt{n}})\to+\infty
when
x\to\sqrt{n}
; nevertheless, the integral is still definite. By symmetry, we will now work on
[0,\sqrt{n})
and use a factor
2
.

Let
\varepsilon\in(0,\sqrt{n})
to be choosen later. We split
[0,\sqrt{n})
according to

\displaystyle[0,\sqrt{n}):=[0,\sqrt{n}-\varepsilon)\cup[\sqrt{n}-\varepsilon,\sqrt{n})

and estimate the integral on each of these subintervals.

\bullet

Main interval:
On
[0,\sqrt{n}-\varepsilon)
, the function
\widetilde{\kappa}_{n}:x\mapsto\varphi_{n}(x)-C_{\beta}\frac{x^{2}}{2}
is not monotone. This is mainly due to the fact that the second derivatives of the two functions occuring in the difference do not match in
0
. Indeed, one has
\varphi_{n}(0)=\varphi_{n}^{\prime}(0)=0
but
\varphi_{n}^{\prime\prime}(0)=C_{\beta}-\frac{2}{n}
.

Up to comparing with a triangle inequality
G_{\beta}
and
G_{\beta,n}\sim\mathscr{N}(0,(C_{\beta}-\frac{2}{n})^{-1})
, one defines then for
n\geqslant n_{0}(\beta):=\left[2C_{\beta}^{-1}\right]

C_{\beta,n}:=C_{\beta}-\frac{2}{n},\quad\kappa_{n}(x):=\varphi_{n}(x)-C_{\beta,n}\frac{x^{2}}{2}.

(23)

The replacement of
G_{\beta}\sim\mathscr{N}(0,C_{\beta}^{-1})
by
G_{\beta,n}\sim\mathscr{N}(0,C_{\beta,n}^{-1})
can be done up to
O(1/n)
. Indeed,

\displaystyle\gamma_{n}

\displaystyle=\int_{(-\sqrt{n},\sqrt{n})}\left|e^{-\varphi_{n}(x)}-e^{-C_{\beta}\frac{x^{2}}{2}}\right|dx

\displaystyle=\int_{(-\sqrt{n},\sqrt{n})}\left|e^{-\varphi_{n}(x)}-e^{-C_{\beta,n}\frac{x^{2}}{2}}+e^{-C_{\beta,n}\frac{x^{2}}{2}}-e^{-C_{\beta}\frac{x^{2}}{2}}\right|dx

\displaystyle\leqslant\int_{(-\sqrt{n},\sqrt{n})}\left|e^{-\varphi_{n}(x)}-e^{-C_{\beta,n}\frac{x^{2}}{2}}\right|dx+\int_{(-\sqrt{n},\sqrt{n})}\left|e^{-C_{\beta,n}\frac{x^{2}}{2}}-e^{-C_{\beta}\frac{x^{2}}{2}}\right|dx

\displaystyle=:2\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{C_{\beta,n}}}\,\widetilde{\gamma}_{n}+\int_{(-\sqrt{n},\sqrt{n})}\left|e^{-C_{\beta,n}\frac{x^{2}}{2}}-e^{-C_{\beta}\frac{x^{2}}{2}}\right|dx

with

\widetilde{\gamma}_{n}:=\sqrt{\frac{C_{\beta,n}}{2\pi}}\int_{0}^{\sqrt{n}}\left|e^{-\varphi_{n}(x)}-e^{-C_{\beta,n}\frac{x^{2}}{2}}\right|dx=\sqrt{\frac{C_{\beta,n}}{2\pi}}\int_{0}^{\sqrt{n}}\left|1-e^{-\kappa_{n}(x)}\right|e^{-C_{\beta,n}\frac{x^{2}}{2}}dx

and

\displaystyle\int_{-\sqrt{n}}^{\sqrt{n}}\left|e^{-C_{\beta,n}\frac{x^{2}}{2}}-e^{-C_{\beta}\frac{x^{2}}{2}}\right|dx

\displaystyle=\int_{-\sqrt{n}}^{\sqrt{n}}\left|e^{\frac{x^{2}}{n}}-1\right|e^{-C_{\beta}\frac{x^{2}}{2}}dx

\displaystyle\leqslant 3\int{R}\frac{x^{2}}{n}e^{-C_{\beta}\frac{x^{2}}{2}}dx

\displaystyle=\frac{3}{n}\frac{\sqrt{2\pi}}{C_{\beta}^{3/2}}.

Here, we have used
\left|e^{x}-1\right|\leqslant e^{\left|x\right|}-1\leqslant\left|x\right|e^{\left|x\right|}
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
,
e^{\frac{x^{2}}{n}}\leqslant 3
for
x\in(-\sqrt{n},\sqrt{n})
and the second moment of a Gaussian.

The function
\kappa_{n}
thus defined in (
23
) is now strictly increasing and positive on
[0,\sqrt{n})
, hence, so is
1-e^{-\kappa_{n}}
. Moreover, thanks to the matching of the derivatives and the fact that
\varphi_{n}^{\prime\prime\prime}(0)=0
, the Taylor formula with integral remainder gives at the fourth order

\displaystyle\kappa_{n}(x)=\frac{x^{4}}{6}\int_{0}^{1}(1-\alpha)^{3}\kappa_{n}^{(4)}(\alpha x)d\alpha=\frac{x^{4}}{6}\int_{0}^{1}(1-\alpha)^{3}\varphi_{n}^{(4)}(\alpha x)d\alpha,

where
\kappa_{n}^{(4)}(x):=\!\left(\frac{d}{dx}\right)^{4}\kappa_{n}(x)
. The only singularity of all the derivatives of
\kappa_{n}
is in
\sqrt{n}
, which is not in
(0,\sqrt{n}-\varepsilon)
. One can thus write for all

\displaystyle 0\leqslant\kappa_{n}(x)\leqslant\frac{x^{4}}{6}\int_{0}^{1}(1-\alpha)^{3}d\alpha\left|\!\left|\kappa_{n}^{(4)}\mathds{1}_{[0,\sqrt{n}-\varepsilon)}\right|\!\right|_{\infty}=\frac{x^{4}}{24}\left|\!\left|\kappa_{n}^{(4)}\mathds{1}_{[0,\sqrt{n}-\varepsilon)}\right|\!\right|_{\infty}=:M_{n}(\varepsilon)\frac{x^{4}}{24}.

Since
\kappa_{n}^{(4)}
is positive and increasing on
[0,\sqrt{n}-\varepsilon)
, one has

\displaystyle M_{n}(\varepsilon)=\kappa_{n}^{(4)}(\sqrt{n}-\varepsilon),

hence

\displaystyle\int_{[0,\sqrt{n}-\varepsilon)}\left|1-e^{-\kappa_{n}(x)}\right|e^{-C_{\beta,n}\frac{x^{2}}{2}}dx

\displaystyle\leqslant\int_{[0,\sqrt{n}-\varepsilon)}\!\left(1-e^{-M_{n}(\varepsilon)\frac{x^{4}}{24}}\right)e^{-C_{\beta,n}\frac{x^{2}}{2}}dx

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{M_{n}(\varepsilon)}{24}\int_{[0,\sqrt{n}-\varepsilon)}x^{4}e^{-C_{\beta,n}\frac{x^{2}}{2}}dx

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{M_{n}(\varepsilon)}{24}\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{C_{\beta,n}}}\,\mathbb{E}\!\left((G_{\beta,n})^{4}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{M_{n}(\varepsilon)}{8}\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{C_{\beta,n}}}\,C_{\beta,n}^{-2},

where we have used the scaling of the Gaussian and its fourth moment equal to
3
.

Last, a computation with SageMath

]
gives

\displaystyle\kappa_{n}^{(4)}(x)=\varphi_{n}^{(4)}(x)=\frac{2}{n\beta}\frac{P(x/\sqrt{n})\operatorname{Argtanh}(x/\sqrt{n})-Q_{n,\beta}(x/\sqrt{n})}{(1-(x/\sqrt{n})^{2})^{4}}

with explicit polynomials

\displaystyle P(x)

\displaystyle:=12\,x(x^{2}+1),

\displaystyle Q_{n,\beta}(x)

\displaystyle:=3\beta(1+2n^{-1})x^{4}-18(1-\beta-2n^{-1})x^{2}-(4-3\beta-6\beta n^{-1})

\displaystyle=:Q_{\beta}(x)+\frac{1}{n}\widetilde{Q}_{\beta}(x),

\displaystyle Q_{\beta}(x)

\displaystyle:=3\beta x^{4}-18(1-\beta)x^{2}-(4-3\beta),

\displaystyle\widetilde{Q}_{\beta}(x)

\displaystyle:=6(\beta x^{4}+6x^{2}+6\beta).

Define

t:=\frac{\varepsilon}{\sqrt{n}}\in(0,1)
, then

\displaystyle M_{n}(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\varphi_{n}^{(4)}(\sqrt{n}-\varepsilon)=\frac{2}{n\beta}\frac{P\!\left(1-t\right)\operatorname{Argtanh}(1-t)-Q_{n,\beta}(1-t)}{(1-(1-t)^{2})^{4}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{n\beta}\frac{P\!\left(1-t\right)\operatorname{Argtanh}(1-t)-Q_{\beta}(1-t)}{t^{4}(2-t)^{4}}-\frac{2}{n^{2}\beta}\frac{\widetilde{Q}_{\beta}(1-t)}{t^{4}(2-t)^{4}}.

(24)

\bullet

Remaining interval:
As
\kappa_{n}
is positive on
[\sqrt{n}-\varepsilon,\sqrt{n})
, we have
1-e^{-\kappa_{n}(x)}\leqslant 1
and

\displaystyle\int_{[\sqrt{n}-\varepsilon,\sqrt{n})}\left|1-e^{-\kappa_{n}(x)}\right|e^{-C_{\beta,n}\frac{x^{2}}{2}}dx

\displaystyle\leqslant\int_{[\sqrt{n}-\varepsilon,\sqrt{n})}e^{-C_{\beta,n}\frac{x^{2}}{2}}dx

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\int_{0}^{1}e^{-C_{\beta,n}\frac{(\sqrt{n}-\varepsilon u)^{2}}{2}}du

\displaystyle\leqslant\varepsilon\,e^{-C_{\beta,n}\frac{\sqrt{n}}{2}}\leqslant 3\varepsilon\,e^{-C_{\beta}\frac{\sqrt{n}}{2}},

where we have used
(\sqrt{n}-\varepsilon u)^{2}=n(1-tu)^{2}\geqslant\sqrt{n}
for

big enough and for all

, in addition to
C_{\beta,n}\frac{\sqrt{n}}{2}=C_{\beta}\frac{\sqrt{n}}{2}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}
and
e^{1/\sqrt{n}}\leqslant 3
.

\bullet

General contribution:
One finally gets

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{C_{\beta,n}}}\,\widetilde{\gamma}_{n}:=\int_{(0,\sqrt{n})}\left|1-e^{-\kappa_{n}(x)}\right|e^{-C_{\beta,n}\frac{x^{2}}{2}}dx\leqslant M_{n}(\varepsilon)\frac{C_{\beta,n}^{-2}}{8}+3\sqrt{\frac{C_{\beta,n}}{2\pi}}\varepsilon\,e^{-C_{\beta}\frac{\sqrt{n}}{2}}.

It remains to choose

in order to get the order of convergence. In view of (

, yielding

\displaystyle M_{n}(\tfrac{3}{4}\sqrt{n})

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{n\beta}\frac{P\!\left(\tfrac{1}{4}\right)\operatorname{Argtanh}(\tfrac{1}{4})-Q_{\beta}(\tfrac{1}{4})}{(3/4)^{4}(3/2-1)^{4}}-\frac{2}{n^{2}\beta}\frac{\widetilde{Q}_{\beta}(\tfrac{1}{4})}{(3/4)^{4}(3/2-1)^{4}}

\displaystyle=:\frac{K_{1}(\beta)}{n}+\frac{K_{2}(\beta)}{n^{2}}.

As a result, one gets for explicit constants
K_{3}(\beta),K_{4}(\beta)>0

\bullet

Conclusion:
We have for all
k\geqslant 1

\displaystyle\widetilde{\delta}_{n}(h)\leqslant\frac{\mathbb{E}\!\left(\left|G_{\beta}\right|^{2k}\right)}{n^{k}}\left|\!\left|h\right|\!\right|_{\infty}+\frac{\mathbb{E}\!\left(G_{\beta}^{2}\right)}{n}\,\left|\!\left|h^{\prime}\right|\!\right|_{\infty}+\delta(g_{n},g_{\beta})\left|\!\left|h\right|\!\right|_{\infty}

and using Lemma

, one has

which gives the result.

Remark 3.2
.

The surrogate approach here defined allows to understand in a better way the apparition of the limiting Gaussian random variable. In the case
\beta<1
, the Gaussian CLT is present through the random variable
G
, and it is the adjunction of the random variable
G_{\beta}
coming from the fluctuations of the randomisation that finally gives
{\boldsymbol{Z}}_{\!\beta}=G+G_{\beta}
. It is thus a subtle mixture of the two structures, sums of i.i.d.’s and randomisation, that gives the final distribution in this case. In the language of statistical mechanics of phase transitions, when a disorder is present in a statistical system and has a marginal effect, one talks about a
marginally relevant disordered system
(see e.g.
[
6
,
53
]
in the context of the KPZ equation or random polymers). It is typically the case here with the decomposition
{\boldsymbol{Z}}_{\!\beta}=G+G_{\beta}
(analogous to the convergence of the rescaled KPZ equation to the Edwards-Wilkinson universality class which is Gaussian).

Nevertheless, when looking at the speed in (
18
) and (
17
), one sees that it is only the Berry-Esséen bound for sums of i.i.d.’s that gives its footprint to the first order and not at all the randomisation in this case (the speed coming from the randomisation only appears at the second order). This will be the opposite in the next case
\beta=1-\frac{\gamma}{\sqrt{n}}
and in particular its special case
\beta=1
. The regime
\beta<1
can thus be considered as the regime where the independent CLT dominates at the level of the speed (and the randomisation is marginally relevant); the regime
\beta=1-\frac{\gamma}{\sqrt{n}}
will be the one where the randomisation dominates at the level of the fluctuations (non Gaussian behaviour). From this perspective, the transition is interesting: there is a competition between randomisation and sums of independent random variables.

3.2.
The case
\beta_{n}=1\pm\frac{\gamma}{\sqrt{n}}
,
\gamma>0

Theorem 3.3

(Fluctuations of the
unnormalised
magnetisation for
\beta_{n}=1-\frac{\gamma}{\sqrt{n}}
,
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
)
.

Let
{\boldsymbol{F}}_{\!\!\gamma}
be a random variable of law given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\!\left({\boldsymbol{F}}_{\!\!\gamma}\in dx\right):=\frac{1}{\mathcal{Z}_{{\boldsymbol{F}}_{\!\gamma}}}e^{-\frac{x^{4}}{12}-\gamma\frac{x^{2}}{2}}dx,\qquad\mathcal{Z}_{{\boldsymbol{F}}_{\!\gamma}}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-\frac{x^{4}}{12}-\gamma\frac{x^{2}}{2}}dx

Then, for all
h\in\mathcal{C}^{1}
with
\left|\!\left|h\right|\!\right|_{\infty},\left|\!\left|h^{\prime}\right|\!\right|_{\infty}<\infty
it holds that

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}\!\left(h\!\left(\frac{M_{n}^{(\beta_{n})}}{n^{3/4}}\right)\right)-\mathbb{E}\!\left(h({\boldsymbol{F}}_{\!\!\gamma})\right)\right|\leqslant\!\left(\frac{C}{\sqrt{n}}+O\!\left(\frac{1}{n^{3/4}}\right)\right)\!\left(\vphantom{a^{a^{a}}}\left|\!\left|h\right|\!\right|_{\infty}+\left|\!\left|h^{\prime}\right|\!\right|_{\infty}\right),

(25)

where
C>0
is an explicit constant.

Proof.

With
{\boldsymbol{T}}_{\!n}^{(\beta)}
defined in (
14
), set

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{F}}_{\!\!n,\gamma}:=n^{1/4}\,{\boldsymbol{T}}_{\!n}^{(\beta_{n})},\qquad\beta_{n}:=1-\tfrac{\gamma}{\sqrt{n}}.

(26)

Then, the law of
{\boldsymbol{F}}_{\!\!n,\gamma}
is given by

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathbb{P}\!\left({\boldsymbol{F}}_{\!\!n,\gamma}\in dt\right)&=f_{n,\beta_{n}}\!\left(\frac{t}{n^{1/4}}\right)\frac{dt}{n^{1/4}}\\
&=\frac{1}{n^{1/4}\,\mathcal{Z}_{n,\beta_{n}}}e^{-\frac{n}{2\beta_{n}}\operatorname{Argtanh}(t/n^{1/4})^{2}-(n/2+1)\ln(1-t^{2}/\sqrt{n})}\mathds{1}_{\left\{\left|t\right|\leqslant n^{1/4}\right\}}dt.\end{aligned}

(27)

Randomising (
16
) and rescaling it by a factor
n^{3/4}
(which amounts to do
h\leftarrow h(\frac{\cdot}{n^{3/4}})
hence
h^{\prime}\leftarrow\frac{1}{n^{3/4}}h^{\prime}(\frac{\cdot}{n^{3/4}})
) yields

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}\!\left(h\!\left(\frac{M_{n}^{(\beta_{n})}}{n^{3/4}}\right)\right)-\mathbb{E}\!\left(h\!\left(\frac{\mathcal{M}_{n}^{(\beta_{n})}}{n^{3/4}}\right)\right)\right|\leqslant\frac{C}{n^{3/4}}\left|\!\left|h^{\prime}\right|\!\right|_{\infty},

(28)

with

\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{M}_{n}^{(\beta_{n})}}{n^{3/4}}=\frac{G}{n^{1/4}}\,\sqrt{1-({\boldsymbol{T}}_{\!n}^{(\beta_{n})})^{2}}+n^{1/4}\,{\boldsymbol{T}}_{\!n}^{(\beta_{n})},

or rewritten to

\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{M}_{n}^{(\beta_{n})}}{n^{3/4}}=\frac{G}{n^{1/4}}\,\sqrt{1-\frac{{\boldsymbol{F}}_{\!\!n,\gamma}^{2}}{\sqrt{n}}}+{\boldsymbol{F}}_{\!\!n,\gamma},

(29)

where
G\sim\mathscr{N}(0,1)
independent of
M_{n}^{(\beta_{n})}
and
{\boldsymbol{F}}_{\!\!\gamma}
, and the triangle inequality gives then the analogue of (
18
)

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}\!\left(h\!\left(\frac{M_{n}^{(\beta_{n})}}{n^{3/4}}\right)\right)-\mathbb{E}\!\left(h({\boldsymbol{F}}_{\!\!\gamma})\right)\right|\leqslant\frac{C}{n^{3/4}}\left|\!\left|h^{\prime}\right|\!\right|_{\infty}+\left|\mathbb{E}\!\left(h\!\left(\frac{\mathcal{M}_{n}^{(\beta_{n})}}{n^{3/4}}\right)\right)-\mathbb{E}\!\left(h({\boldsymbol{F}}_{\!\!\gamma})\right)\right|.

(30)

Nevertheless, one sees from the expression of (
29
) that if
{\boldsymbol{F}}_{\!\!n,\gamma}\to{\boldsymbol{F}}_{\!\!\gamma}
in law,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{M}_{n}^{(\beta_{n})}}{n^{3/4}}

\displaystyle=\frac{G}{n^{1/4}}\sqrt{1-\frac{{\boldsymbol{F}}_{\!\!n,\gamma}^{2}}{\sqrt{n}}}+{\boldsymbol{F}}_{\!\!n,\gamma}

\displaystyle\approx{\boldsymbol{F}}_{\!\!\gamma}+\frac{G}{n^{1/4}}\!\left(1-\frac{{\boldsymbol{F}}_{\!\!\gamma}^{2}}{2\sqrt{n}}\right)

\displaystyle\approx{\boldsymbol{F}}_{\!\!\gamma}+\frac{G}{n^{1/4}}+O{P}\!\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right).

As a result, one will always have at best
\mathbb{E}\!\left(h\!\left(\frac{\mathcal{M}_{n}^{(\beta_{n}))}}{n^{3/4}}\right)\right)-\mathbb{E}\!\left(h({\boldsymbol{F}}_{\!\!\gamma})\right)=O\!\left(\frac{\left|\!\left|h^{\prime}\right|\!\right|_{\infty}}{n^{1/4}}\right)
which is incompatible with the results of
[
12
,
19
]
that give a speed in
O\!\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right)
for the case
\gamma=0
. Such a discrepancy between this result and (
28
) shows that we have used the “wrong” random variable to compare to, when using the triangle inequality. One should instead incorporate another random variable at a distance
\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}
to decrease the distance in
\frac{1}{n^{1/4}}
, possibly at the cost of increasing the distance in
\frac{1}{n^{3/4}}
in (
28
). Such a replacement can be performed by introducing another related surrogate.

Recall that for i.i.d.’s
(B_{k})_{k}
, one has
S_{n}(p):=\sum_{k=1}^{n}B_{k}(p)
. A classical representation of
B_{k}(p)
is given by

\displaystyle B_{k}(p):=\mathds{1}_{\left\{U_{k}<p\right\}}-\mathds{1}_{\left\{U_{k}>p\right\}}=2\,\mathds{1}_{\left\{U_{k}<p\right\}}-1,\qquad(U_{k})_{k\geqslant 1}\sim\textrm{i.i.d.}\mathscr{U}\!\left([0,1]\right),

(31)

and this representation allows in addition to visualise the randomisation of the parameter
p
in a functional way.

One can thus use a coupling of
S_{n}(p)
and
S_{n}(q)
using these uniform random variables. This very coupling is said to be
totally dependent
in the sense that these are the same uniform random variables that are used (i.e.
B_{k}(q)
is a measurable function of
B_{k}(p)
and vice versa).

Define for
p,q\in[0,1]

\displaystyle\lambda_{n}

\displaystyle:=2n(p-q)=\mathbb{E}\!\left(S_{n}(p)-S_{n}(q)\right),

\displaystyle S_{n}(p,q)

\displaystyle:=S_{n}(p)-S_{n}(q)-\lambda_{n},

\displaystyle\sigma(p)^{2}

\displaystyle:=4p(1-p).

Then,

(36)

Bound on
\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(\gamma,7)}_{n}
:

Define

\displaystyle:=\Phi_{n,\gamma}(x)-\widehat{\Phi}_{n,\gamma}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{n}{2\beta_{n}}\operatorname{Argtanh}\!\left(\frac{x}{n^{1/4}}\right)^{2}+\!\left(\frac{n}{2}+1\right)\ln\!\left(1-\frac{x^{2}}{\sqrt{n}}\right)-\alpha_{n}^{(1)}\frac{x^{4}}{12}-\alpha_{n}^{(2)}\frac{x^{2}}{2}.

, hence

with SageMath

on

(37)

We consider the transcendent equation

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{X}}_{\!\beta}\sim\operatorname{Ber}_{\pm x_{\beta}}\!\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right),\qquad{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}}^{(\beta)}\sim\operatorname{Ber}_{\pm t_{\beta}}\!\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right),\qquad t_{\beta}:=\frac{x_{\beta}}{\beta}=\tanh(x_{\beta}).

Theorem 3.8

(Fluctuations of the
unnormalised
magnetisation for
\beta>1
)
.

If
\beta>1
, one has for all
h\in\mathcal{C}^{1}
with
\left|\!\left|h\right|\!\right|_{\infty},\left|\!\left|h^{\prime}\right|\!\right|_{\infty}<\infty

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}\!\left(h\!\left(\frac{M_{n}^{(\beta)}}{n}\right)\right)-\mathbb{E}\!\left(h\!\left({\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}}^{(\beta)}\right)\right)\right|\leqslant\!\left(\frac{C}{\sqrt{n}}+O_{\beta}\!\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)\right)\left|\!\left|h^{\prime}\right|\!\right|_{\infty}

(40)

for an explicit constant
C>0
.

Proof.

Rescaling
M_{n}^{(\beta)}
by
n
and substituting in (
16
) yields

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}\!\left(h\!\left(\frac{M_{n}^{(\beta)}}{n}\right)\right)-\mathbb{E}\!\left(h\!\left(\frac{\mathcal{M}_{n}^{(\beta)}}{n}\right)\right)\right|\leqslant\frac{C^{\prime}}{n}\left|\!\left|h^{\prime}\right|\!\right|_{\infty}

and the triangle inequality implies the following adaptation of (
18
):

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}\!\left(h\!\left(\frac{M_{n}^{(\beta)}}{n}\right)\right)-\mathbb{E}\!\left(h\!\left({\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}}^{(\beta)}\right)\right)\right|\leqslant\frac{C^{\prime}}{n}\left|\!\left|h^{\prime}\right|\!\right|_{\infty}+\left|\mathbb{E}\!\left(h\!\left(\frac{\mathcal{M}_{n}^{(\beta)}}{n}\right)\right)-\mathbb{E}\!\left(h\!\left({\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}}^{(\beta)}\right)\right)\right|.

(41)

Moreover, one has

\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{M}_{n}^{(\beta)}}{n}=\frac{G}{\sqrt{n}}\,\sqrt{1-({\boldsymbol{T}}_{\!n}^{(\beta)})^{2}}+{\boldsymbol{T}}_{\!n}^{(\beta)}

and, since
{\boldsymbol{T}}_{\!n}^{(\beta)}\in[-1,1]
a.s., one has
\frac{\left|G\right|}{\sqrt{n}}\,\sqrt{1-({\boldsymbol{T}}_{\!n}^{(\beta)})^{2}}\leqslant\frac{\left|G\right|}{\sqrt{n}}\to 0
in law, hence

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}\!\left(h\!\left(\frac{\mathcal{M}_{n}^{(\beta)}}{n}\right)\right)-\mathbb{E}\!\left(h\!\left({\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}}^{(\beta)}\right)\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{\left|\!\left|h^{\prime}\right|\!\right|_{\infty}}{\sqrt{n}}+\left|\mathbb{E}\!\left(h\!\left({\boldsymbol{T}}_{\!n}^{(\beta)}\right)-\mathbb{E}\!\left(h\!\left({\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}}^{(\beta)}\right)\right)\right)\right|.

It thus remains to show that
{\boldsymbol{T}}_{\!n}^{(\beta)}\!\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{L}}}{{\longrightarrow}}{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}}^{(\beta)}
and to control its norm. For this, remark that

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}\!\left(h\!\left({\boldsymbol{T}}_{\!n}^{(\beta)}\right)-\mathbb{E}\!\left(h\!\left({\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}}^{(\beta)}\right)\right)\right)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\mathbb{E}\!\left(h\!\left(\tanh({\boldsymbol{R}}_{\!n}^{(\beta)})\right)-\mathbb{E}\!\left(h\!\left(\tanh({\boldsymbol{X}}_{\!\beta}\right))\right)\right)\right|

\displaystyle=:\left|\mathbb{E}\!\left(\widetilde{h}\!\left({\boldsymbol{R}}_{n}^{(\beta)}\right)-\mathbb{E}\!\left(\widetilde{h}\!\left({\boldsymbol{X}}_{\!\beta}\right)\right)\right)\right|,\qquad\widetilde{h}:=h\!\circ\!\tanh,

where

has a law given by

\displaystyle\mu_{n,\beta}(dy):=\mathbb{P}\!\left({\boldsymbol{R}}_{n}^{(\beta)}\in dy\right)=e^{-n\varphi_{\beta}(y)}\frac{dy}{\mathcal{Z}_{n,\beta}},\qquad\varphi_{\beta}(y):=\frac{y^{2}}{2\beta}-\log\cosh(y).

(42)

We now adapt the Laplace method (in the easier case of a global minimum) to show that
\mu_{n,\beta}\to\frac{1}{2}(\delta_{t_{\beta}}+\delta_{-t_{\beta}})
weakly. Since
\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}d\mu_{n,\beta}=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{-}}d\mu_{n,\beta}=\frac{1}{2}
, one has

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}\!\left(\widetilde{h}\!\left({\boldsymbol{R}}_{n}^{(\beta)}\right)-\mathbb{E}\!\left(\widetilde{h}\!\left({\boldsymbol{X}}_{\!\beta}\right)\right)\right)\right|

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\!\left[\widetilde{h}(y)-\widetilde{h}(x_{\beta})\right]\mu_{n,\beta}(dy)+\int_{\mathbb{R}_{-}}\!\left[\widetilde{h}(y)-\widetilde{h}(-x_{\beta})\right]\mu_{n,\beta}(dy)

\displaystyle=:\delta_{n}(\widetilde{h})+\delta_{n}(\widetilde{h}(-\cdot)).

It is thus enough to treat the case of
\delta_{n}(\widetilde{h})
. For this, note that (
39
) is equivalent to
\varphi_{\beta}^{\prime}(x_{\beta})=0
, hence that for all
x\geqslant 0

\displaystyle\varphi_{\beta}(x)=\varphi_{\beta}(x_{\beta})+(x-x_{\beta})^{2}\int_{0}^{1}\varphi_{\beta}^{\prime\prime}(\alpha x+\overline{\alpha}x_{\beta})\alpha d\alpha,\qquad\overline{\alpha}:=1-\alpha.

As a result

\displaystyle\delta_{n}(\widetilde{h})

\displaystyle=e^{-n\varphi_{\beta}(x_{\beta})}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\!\left[\widetilde{h}(x)-\widetilde{h}(x_{\beta})\right]e^{-n(\varphi_{\beta}(x)-\varphi_{\beta}(x_{\beta}))}\frac{dx}{\mathcal{Z}_{n,\beta}}

\displaystyle=e^{-n\varphi_{\beta}(x_{\beta})}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\!\left[\widetilde{h}(x)-\widetilde{h}(x_{\beta})\right]e^{-n(x-x_{\beta})^{2}\int_{0}^{1}\varphi_{\beta}^{\prime\prime}(\alpha x+\overline{\alpha}x_{\beta})\alpha d\alpha}\frac{dx}{\mathcal{Z}_{n,\beta}}

\displaystyle=e^{-n\varphi_{\beta}(x_{\beta})}\int_{-x_{\beta}\sqrt{n}}^{+\infty}\!\left[\widetilde{h}\!\left(x_{\beta}+\frac{w}{\sqrt{n}}\right)-\widetilde{h}(x_{\beta})\right]e^{-w^{2}\int_{0}^{1}\varphi_{\beta}^{\prime\prime}(\alpha w/\sqrt{n}+x_{\beta})\alpha d\alpha}\frac{dw}{\mathcal{Z}_{n,\beta}\sqrt{n}}

\displaystyle\leqslant e^{-n\varphi_{\beta}(x_{\beta})}\frac{|\!|\widetilde{h}^{\prime}|\!|_{\infty}}{\sqrt{n}}\int_{-x_{\beta}\sqrt{n}}^{+\infty}\left|w\right|e^{-w^{2}\int_{0}^{1}\varphi_{\beta}^{\prime\prime}(\alpha w/\sqrt{n}+x_{\beta})\alpha d\alpha}\frac{dw}{\mathcal{Z}_{n,\beta}\sqrt{n}}

\displaystyle=:\frac{|\!|\widetilde{h}^{\prime}|\!|_{\infty}}{\sqrt{n}}\frac{\mathcal{Z}_{n,\beta}^{(1)}\sqrt{n}}{\mathcal{Z}_{n,\beta}\sqrt{n}},

with (
53
).

Lemma
A.5
for
k=1
gives moreover

\displaystyle\int_{-x_{\beta}\sqrt{n}}^{+\infty}\left|w\right|e^{-w^{2}\int_{0}^{1}\varphi_{\beta}^{\prime\prime}(\alpha w/\sqrt{n}+x_{\beta})\alpha d\alpha}dw\underset{n\rightarrow+\infty}{\hskip 0.43057pt-\hskip-4.73611pt-\hskip-4.73611pt-\hskip-4.73611pt\longrightarrow}\int{R}\left|w\right|e^{-\frac{w^{2}}{2}\varphi^{\prime\prime}(x_{\beta})}dw=\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{\varphi_{\beta}^{\prime\prime}(x_{\beta})^{3}}}\mathbb{E}\!\left(\left|G\right|\right),

with
G\sim\mathscr{N}(0,1)
.

In the end, Lemma
A.5
for
k=1
yields

\displaystyle\delta_{n}(\widetilde{h})

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{|\!|\widetilde{h}^{\prime}|\!|_{\infty}}{\sqrt{n}}\!\left(\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{\varphi_{\beta}^{\prime\prime}(x_{\beta})}}\mathbb{E}\!\left(\left|G\right|\right)+o(1)\right)\times\frac{e^{-n\varphi_{\beta}(x_{\beta})}}{\sqrt{n}\mathcal{Z}_{n,\beta}}

\displaystyle=\frac{|\!|\widetilde{h}^{\prime}|\!|_{\infty}}{2\sqrt{n}}\!\left(\frac{\mathbb{E}\!\left(\left|G\right|\right)}{\varphi_{\beta}^{\prime\prime}(x_{\beta})}+o(1)\right).

Last,

Remark 3.9
.

One can also consider with
[
19
]
the law of

\displaystyle(X_{1}^{(\beta,+)},\dots,X_{n}^{(\beta,+)})

\displaystyle:\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{L}}}{{=}}\!\left(X_{1}(\widetilde{V}_{n,\beta}),\dots,X_{n}(\widetilde{V}_{n,\beta})\Big|\widetilde{V}_{n,\beta}\geqslant\tfrac{1}{2}\right),

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{n}^{(\beta,+)}:=\sqrt{n}\,G\,\sqrt{1-({\boldsymbol{T}}_{\!n}^{(\beta,+)})^{2}}+n\,{\boldsymbol{T}}_{\!n}^{(\beta,+)},\qquad{\boldsymbol{T}}_{\!n}^{(\beta,+)}:=\!\left({\boldsymbol{T}}_{\!n}^{(\beta)}\Big|{\boldsymbol{T}}_{\!n}^{(\beta)}\geqslant 0\right).

in probability and in

. In the end, one gets

\displaystyle\frac{M_{n}^{(\beta,+)}-t_{\beta}\,n}{\sqrt{n}\sqrt{1-t_{\beta}^{2}}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{L}}}{{\underset{n\,\rightarrow\,+\infty}{\hskip 0.43057pt-\hskip-4.73611pt-\hskip-4.73611pt-\hskip-4.73611pt\longrightarrow}}}\mathscr{N}(0,1),

with a speed in

in smooth norm. Details are left to the interested reader.

4.
Application to the Curie-Weiss magnetisation in Kolmogorov distance

Recall the definition of the Kolmogorov distance given in (
5
).
This norm is a functional one in the same vein as the Fortet-Mourier one, using test functions
h=\mathds{1}_{(-\infty,x]}
. The main difference is nevertheless the lack of differentiability of these test functions that prevents the use of (
12
). An extension of Theorem
3.1
to the Kolmogorov distance case requires thus to find an analogue of this inequality for indicator functions. This is furnished by the classical Berry-Esséen bound for sums of i.i.d. random variables (see e.g.
[
43
, thm. 3.39]
)

\displaystyle d_{\operatorname{Kol}}\!\left(S_{n},\,\sigma\sqrt{n}\,G+n\mu\right)=O\!\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right).

(43)

Here,
(Z_{k})_{k}
is a sequence of i.i.d. random variables satisfying
\mathbb{E}\!\left(\left|Z\right|^{3}\right)<\infty
,
S_{n}:=\sum_{k=1}^{n}Z_{k}
,
{\operatorname{Var}}(S_{n})=:n\sigma^{2}
,
\mathbb{E}\!\left(S_{n}\right)=n\mu
and
G\sim\mathscr{N}(0,1)
.

Of course, a randomisation of this inequality will give the same result as in §
2.2
, since one has just changed test functions.

4.1.
The case
\beta<1

Theorem 4.1

(Kolmogorov distance to the Gaussian for the
unnormalised
magnetisation with
\beta<1
)
.

With
{\boldsymbol{Z}}_{\!\beta}\sim\mathscr{N}\!\left(0,\frac{1}{1-\beta}\right)

, we have

\displaystyle d_{\operatorname{Kol}}\!\left(\frac{M_{n}^{(\beta)}}{\sqrt{n}},{\boldsymbol{Z}}_{\!\beta}\right)=\frac{C}{\sqrt{n}}+O_{\beta}\!\left(\frac{1}{n}\right).

(44)

Proof.

We use (
43
) in the particular case of
\left\{\pm 1\right\}
-Bernoulli random variables
(B_{k})_{k}
of parameter
p:=\mathbb{P}\!\left(B=1\right)
(with
\mathbb{E}\!\left(B\right)=2p-1
and
{\operatorname{Var}}(B)=4p(1-p)
) and then randomise
p
. Taking
p
distributed as in (
13
), i.e.
{\boldsymbol{P}}_{n}^{(\beta)}\sim\widetilde{\nu}_{n,\beta}
, or equivalently taking
t:=2p-1
distributed as in (
14
), i.e.
{\boldsymbol{T}}_{\!n}^{(\beta)}\sim\nu_{n,\beta}
yields

\displaystyle d_{\operatorname{Kol}}\!\left(M_{n}^{(\beta)},G\sqrt{n}\sqrt{1-({\boldsymbol{T}}_{\!n}^{(\beta)})^{2}}+n\,{\boldsymbol{T}}_{\!n}^{(\beta)}\right)=O\!\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right)

\displaystyle\ \Longleftrightarrow\

\displaystyle d_{\operatorname{Kol}}\!\left(\frac{M_{n}^{(\beta)}}{\sqrt{n}},\frac{\mathcal{M}_{n}^{(\beta)}}{\sqrt{n}}\right)=O\!\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right)

by invariance of the norm and using the definition of the surrogate
\mathcal{M}_{n}^{(\beta)}
given in (
15

The triangle inequality then yields

\displaystyle d_{\operatorname{Kol}}\!\left(\frac{M_{n}^{(\beta)}}{\sqrt{n}},{\boldsymbol{Z}}_{\!\beta}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant d_{\operatorname{Kol}}\!\left(\frac{M_{n}^{(\beta)}}{\sqrt{n}},\frac{\mathcal{M}_{n}^{(\beta)}}{\sqrt{n}}\right)+d_{\operatorname{Kol}}\!\left(\frac{\mathcal{M}_{n}^{(\beta)}}{\sqrt{n}},{\boldsymbol{Z}}_{\!\beta}\right)

\displaystyle=d_{\operatorname{Kol}}\!\left(\frac{\mathcal{M}_{n}^{(\beta)}}{\sqrt{n}},{\boldsymbol{Z}}_{\!\beta}\right)+O\!\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right)

and one is led to analyse

\displaystyle d_{\operatorname{Kol}}\!\left(\frac{\mathcal{M}_{n}^{(\beta)}}{\sqrt{n}},{\boldsymbol{Z}}_{\!\beta}\right)=:\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\delta_{n}(x),

with

\displaystyle\delta_{n}(x):=\left|\mathbb{P}\!\left(\frac{\mathcal{M}_{n}^{(\beta)}}{\sqrt{n}}\leqslant x\right)-\mathbb{P}\!\left({\boldsymbol{Z}}_{\!\beta}\leqslant x\right)\right|.

Recall that
{\boldsymbol{Z}}_{\!\beta}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{L}}}{{=}}G+G_{\beta}
with
G\sim\mathscr{N}(0,1)
independent of
G_{\beta}\sim\mathscr{N}(0,\beta/(1-\beta))
. One thus has

\displaystyle\delta_{n}(x)=\left|\mathbb{P}\!\left(G\sqrt{1-\frac{({\boldsymbol{X}}_{n}^{(\beta)})^{2}}{n}}+{\boldsymbol{X}}_{n}^{(\beta)}\leqslant x\right)-\mathbb{P}\!\left(G+G_{\beta}\leqslant x\right)\right|,

with
{\boldsymbol{X}}_{\!n,\beta}:=\sqrt{n}\,{\boldsymbol{T}}_{\!n}^{(\beta)}\to G_{\beta}
(in law).

Integrating on
G
and using
\Phi(x):=\mathbb{P}\!\left(G\leqslant x\right)
,
{\boldsymbol{X}}_{\!n,\beta}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{L}}}{{=}}-{\boldsymbol{X}}_{\!n,\beta}
and
G_{\beta}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{L}}}{{=}}-G_{\beta}
yields

\displaystyle\delta_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=\left|\mathbb{E}\!\left(\Phi\!\left(\frac{x+{\boldsymbol{X}}_{n}^{(\beta)}}{\sqrt{1-\frac{({\boldsymbol{X}}_{n}^{(\beta)})^{2}}{n}}}\right)\right)-\mathbb{E}\!\left(\Phi(x+G_{\beta})\right)\right|.

Set

\displaystyle Y_{x}

\displaystyle:=x+G_{\beta},\quad\Psi_{x,n}(u):=\frac{x+u}{\sqrt{1-\frac{u^{2}}{n}}},\quad\Xi_{n}(u):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\frac{u^{2}}{n}}},

\displaystyle Y_{x,n}

\displaystyle:=\Psi_{x,n}\!\left({\boldsymbol{X}}_{n}^{(\beta)}\right),\quad\mathcal{Y}_{x,n}:=\Psi_{x,n}(G_{\beta})=Y_{x}\,\Xi_{n}(G_{\beta}).

Recall that the support of the law of
{\boldsymbol{X}}_{n}^{(\beta)}
is
(-\sqrt{n},\sqrt{n})
. One has

\displaystyle\delta_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=\left|\mathbb{E}\!\left(\Phi\!\left(Y_{x}\right)\right)-\mathbb{E}\!\left(\Phi\!\left(Y_{x,n}\right)\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\left|\mathbb{E}\!\left(\Phi\!\left(Y_{x}\right)\mathds{1}_{\left\{\left|G_{\beta}\right|\leqslant\sqrt{n}\right\}}\right)-\mathbb{E}\!\left(\Phi\!\left(\mathcal{Y}_{x,n}\right)\mathds{1}_{\left\{\left|G_{\beta}\right|\leqslant\sqrt{n}\right\}}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\left|\mathbb{E}\!\left(\Phi\!\left(\mathcal{Y}_{x,n}\right)\mathds{1}_{\left\{\left|G_{\beta}\right|\leqslant\sqrt{n}\right\}}\right)-\mathbb{E}\!\left(\Phi\!\left(Y_{x,n}\right)\right)\right|+\left|\mathbb{E}\!\left(\Phi\!\left(Y_{x}\right)\mathds{1}_{\left\{\left|G_{\beta}\right|>\sqrt{n}\right\}}\right)\right|

\displaystyle=:\delta_{n}^{(1)}(x)+\delta_{n}^{(2)}(x)+\delta_{n}^{(3)}(x).

Since
\Phi(x):=\mathbb{P}\!\left(G\leqslant x\right)\leqslant 1
, the Markov inequality gives for all
k\geqslant 1

\displaystyle\delta_{n}^{(3)}(x)\leqslant\mathbb{P}\!\left(\left|G_{\beta}\right|>\sqrt{n}\,\right)\leqslant\frac{\mathbb{E}\!\left(\left|G_{\beta}\right|^{2k}\right)}{n^{k}}.

Moreover, using the notation
g_{\beta}
(resp.
g_{n}
) for the Lebesgue density of
G_{\beta}
(resp.
{\boldsymbol{X}}_{n}^{(\beta)}
) as in the proof of Theorem
3.1
, one gets

\displaystyle\delta_{n}^{(2)}(x)

\displaystyle=\left|\mathbb{E}\!\left(\Phi\circ\Psi_{x,n}(G_{\beta})\mathds{1}_{\left\{\left|G_{\beta}\right|\leqslant\sqrt{n}\right\}}\right)-\mathbb{E}\!\left(\Phi\circ\Psi_{x,n}({\boldsymbol{X}}_{n}^{(\beta)})\mathds{1}_{\left\{\left|{\boldsymbol{X}}_{n}^{(\beta)}\right|\leqslant\sqrt{n}\right\}}\right)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{(-\sqrt{n},\sqrt{n}\,)}\Phi\circ\Psi_{x,n}\cdot(g_{\beta}-g_{n})\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\left|\!\left|\Phi\circ\Psi_{x,n}\right|\!\right|_{\infty}\left|\!\left|(g_{\beta}-g_{n})\mathds{1}_{(-\sqrt{n},\sqrt{n}\,)}\right|\!\right|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}

\displaystyle=:\left|\!\left|\Phi\circ\Psi_{x,n}\right|\!\right|_{\infty}\delta_{n}(g_{n},g_{\beta}).

Since
0\leqslant\Phi\leqslant 1
, one has
\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\left|\!\left|\Phi\circ\Psi_{x,n}\right|\!\right|_{\infty}\leqslant 1
, and (
3.1
) yields then for all
x\in\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle\delta_{n}^{(2)}(x)=O_{\beta}\!\left(\frac{1}{n}\right).

We now estimate
\delta_{n}^{(1)}(x)
. Setting
\varphi:=\Phi(\cdot+x)-\Phi\circ\Psi_{x,n}
, we get

\displaystyle\delta_{n}^{(1)}(x)

\displaystyle=\left|\mathbb{E}\!\left(\Phi(Y_{x})\mathds{1}_{\left\{\left|G_{\beta}\right|\leqslant\sqrt{n}\right\}}\right)-\mathbb{E}\!\left(\Phi(\mathcal{Y}_{x})\mathds{1}_{\left\{\left|G_{\beta}\right|\leqslant\sqrt{n}\right\}}\right)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\mathbb{E}\!\left(\!\left(\Phi(x+G_{\beta})-\Phi\circ\Psi_{x,n}(G_{\beta})\right)\mathds{1}_{\left\{\left|G_{\beta}\right|\leqslant\sqrt{n}\right\}}\right)\right|

\displaystyle=:\left|\mathbb{E}\!\left(\varphi(G_{\beta})\!\left(\mathds{1}_{\left\{\left|G_{\beta}\right|\leqslant(1-\varepsilon)\sqrt{n}\right\}}+\mathds{1}_{\left\{(1-\varepsilon)\sqrt{n}\leqslant\left|G_{\beta}\right|\leqslant\sqrt{n}\right\}}\right)\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\left|\mathbb{E}\!\left(\varphi(G_{\beta})\mathds{1}_{\left\{\left|G_{\beta}\right|\leqslant(1-\varepsilon)\sqrt{n}\right\}}\right)\right|+\left|\!\left|\varphi\right|\!\right|_{\infty}\mathbb{P}\!\left((1-\varepsilon)\sqrt{n}\leqslant\left|G_{\beta}\right|\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\left|\mathbb{E}\!\left(\varphi(G_{\beta})\mathds{1}_{\left\{\left|G_{\beta}\right|\leqslant(1-\varepsilon)\sqrt{n}\right\}}\right)\right|+2\,\frac{\mathbb{E}\!\left(\left|G_{\beta}\right|^{2k}\right)}{n^{k}(1-\varepsilon)^{2k}},

for all
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, using the Markov inequality and
\left|\!\left|\varphi\right|\!\right|_{\infty}\leqslant 2
.

Recall that
\Phi^{\prime}(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-x^{2}/2}\geqslant 0
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
and that
\mathcal{Y}_{n,x}=Y_{x}\,\Xi_{n}(G_{\beta})
. One has then with
U\sim\mathscr{U}([0,1])
independent of
G_{\beta}

\displaystyle\delta_{n}^{(4)}(x)

\displaystyle:=\left|\mathbb{E}\!\left(\varphi(G_{\beta})\mathds{1}_{\left\{\left|G_{\beta}\right|\leqslant(1-\varepsilon)\sqrt{n}\right\}}\right)\right|=\left|\mathbb{E}\!\left(\!\left(\vphantom{a^{a^{a}}}\Phi(Y_{x})-\Phi(\mathcal{Y}_{n,x})\right)\mathds{1}_{\left\{\left|G_{\beta}\right|\leqslant(1-\varepsilon)\sqrt{n}\right\}}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathbb{E}\!\left(\left|\mathcal{Y}_{x,n}-Y_{x}\right|\Phi^{\prime}\!\left(Y_{x}+U(\mathcal{Y}_{x,n}-Y_{x})\right)\mathds{1}_{\left\{\left|G_{\beta}\right|\leqslant(1-\varepsilon)\sqrt{n}\right\}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\!\left(\left|1-\Xi_{n}(G_{\beta})\right|\times\left|Y_{x}\right|\Phi^{\prime}\!\left(Y_{x}+Y_{x}U(\Xi_{n}(G_{\beta})-1)\right)\mathds{1}_{\left\{\left|G_{\beta}\right|\leqslant(1-\varepsilon)\sqrt{n}\right\}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\,\mathbb{E}\!\left(\!\left(\vphantom{a^{a^{a}}}\Xi_{n}(G_{\beta})-1\right)\times\left|Y_{x}\right|e^{-\frac{Y_{x}^{2}}{2}(1+U(\Xi_{n}(G_{\beta})-1))^{2}}\mathds{1}_{\left\{\left|G_{\beta}\right|\leqslant(1-\varepsilon)\sqrt{n}\right\}}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\,\mathbb{E}\!\left(\!\left(\vphantom{a^{a^{a}}}\Xi_{n}(G_{\beta})-1\right)\times\left|Y_{x}\right|e^{-\frac{Y_{x}^{2}}{2}}\mathds{1}_{\left\{\left|G_{\beta}\right|\leqslant(1-\varepsilon)\sqrt{n}\right\}}\right),

since on
\left\{\left|G_{\beta}\right|\leqslant(1-\varepsilon)\sqrt{n}\right\}
, one has
\Xi_{n}(G)-1=(1-G^{2}/n)^{-1/2}-1\geqslant 0
. In particular,

\displaystyle\delta_{n}^{(4)}(x)

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\,\mathbb{E}\!\left(\!\left(\vphantom{a^{a^{a}}}\Xi_{n}(G_{\beta})-1\right)\times\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\left\{\left|Y_{x}\right|e^{-\frac{Y_{x}^{2}}{2}}\right\}\mathds{1}_{\left\{\left|G_{\beta}\right|\leqslant(1-\varepsilon)\sqrt{n}\right\}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\,\mathbb{E}\!\left(\!\left(\vphantom{a^{a^{a}}}\Xi_{n}(G_{\beta})-1\right)\mathds{1}_{\left\{\left|G_{\beta}\right|\leqslant(1-\varepsilon)\sqrt{n}\right\}}\right)\times\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}}\left\{\left|y\right|e^{-\frac{y^{2}}{2}}\right\}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi e}}\mathbb{E}\!\left(\!\left(\vphantom{a^{a^{a}}}\Xi_{n}(G_{\beta})-1\right)\mathds{1}_{\left\{\left|G_{\beta}\right|\leqslant(1-\varepsilon)\sqrt{n}\right\}}\right),

since
Y_{x}=x+G_{\beta}
and the supremum of the function
y\mapsto\left|y\right|e^{-y^{2}/2}
is easily seen to be reached uniquely in
y=1
.

Last, the function
x\mapsto\Xi_{n}(\sqrt{n}x)-1
is clearly integrable on
(-1+\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon)
, and as a result, using
0\leqslant\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-t}}-1\leqslant t\,\sup_{\left|u\right|\leqslant 1-\varepsilon}\left|\frac{d}{du}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-u}}\right|=\frac{t}{2\varepsilon^{3/2}}
and
\mathbb{E}\!\left(G_{\beta}^{2}\right)=\frac{\beta}{1-\beta}
, one finally gets for all
x\in\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle\delta_{n}^{(4)}(x)\leqslant\frac{\beta}{4\varepsilon^{3/2}(1-\beta)\sqrt{2\pi e}}\times\frac{1}{n}.

In the end, we obtain

\displaystyle\delta_{n}(x)

\displaystyle\leqslant\delta_{n}^{(1)}(x)+\delta_{n}^{(2)}(x)+\delta_{n}^{(3)}(x)

\displaystyle\leqslant\delta_{n}^{(4)}(x)+2\,\frac{\mathbb{E}\!\left(\left|G_{\beta}\right|^{2k}\right)}{n^{k}(1-\varepsilon)^{2k}}+\delta_{n}^{(2)}(x)+\delta_{n}^{(3)}(x)

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{\beta}{4\varepsilon^{3/2}(1-\beta)\sqrt{2\pi e}}\times\frac{1}{n}+2\,\frac{\mathbb{E}\!\left(\left|G_{\beta}\right|^{2k}\right)}{n^{k}(1-\varepsilon)^{2k}}+O_{\beta}\!\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)+\frac{\mathbb{E}\!\left(\left|G_{\beta}\right|^{2k}\right)}{n^{k}}

\displaystyle=O_{\beta}\!\left(\frac{1}{n}\right),

having choosen
\varepsilon=\frac{3}{4}
for instance. Taking the supremum over
x\in\mathbb{R}
ends the proof.
∎

Remark 4.2
.

Analogously with the case of a smooth norm analysed in Remark
3.2
, one sees that
d_{\operatorname{Kol}}\!\left(\frac{\mathcal{M}_{n}^{(\beta)}}{\sqrt{n}}\,,{\boldsymbol{Z}}_{\!\beta}\right)=O_{\beta}\!\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)
which is faster than the speed coming from the sum of i.i.d.s (i.e. from the CLT). Here again, we can check in Kolmogorov distance the phenomenon of “CLT domination” over the randomisation.

Theorem 4.3

(Kolmogorov distance to

magnetisation with

.

Let
{\boldsymbol{F}}_{\!\!\gamma}
be a random variable of law given in Theorem

(45)

Proof.

Starting with the approach (
43
) with
\beta=\beta_{n}
yields

\displaystyle d_{\operatorname{Kol}}\!\left(\frac{M_{n}^{(\beta_{n})}}{n^{3/4}},\frac{\mathcal{M}_{n}^{(\beta_{n})}}{n^{3/4}}\right)=O\!\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right),

(46)

where the surrogate
\mathcal{M}_{n}^{(\beta_{n})}
is given in (
15
). With the coupling (
31
) and the notations in the proof of Theorem
3.3
, we get

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\delta}^{\gamma}_{n}(x)

\displaystyle:=\left|\mathbb{P}\!\left(\frac{S_{n}({\boldsymbol{P}})}{n^{3/4}}\leqslant x\right)-\mathbb{P}\!\left({\boldsymbol{F}}_{\!\!\gamma}\leqslant x\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\,\,\left|\mathbb{P}\!\left(\frac{S_{n}({\boldsymbol{P}})}{n^{3/4}}\leqslant x\right)-\mathbb{P}\!\left(\frac{S_{n}({\boldsymbol{Q}})+\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{n}}{n^{3/4}}\leqslant x\right)\right|

\displaystyle\hskip 42.67912pt+\left|\mathbb{P}\!\left(\frac{S_{n}({\boldsymbol{Q}})+\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{n}}{n^{3/4}}\leqslant x\right)-\mathbb{P}\!\left(\frac{2\,{\boldsymbol{Q}}(1-{\boldsymbol{Q}})\sqrt{n}\,G+n(2{\boldsymbol{Q}}-1)+\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{n}}{n^{3/4}}\leqslant x\right)\right|

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt+\left|\mathbb{P}\!\left(\frac{2\,{\boldsymbol{Q}}(1-{\boldsymbol{Q}})\sqrt{n}\,G+n(2{\boldsymbol{Q}}-1)+\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{n}}{n^{3/4}}\leqslant x\right)-\mathbb{P}\!\left({\boldsymbol{F}}_{\!\!\gamma}\leqslant x\right)\right|

\displaystyle=:\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(\gamma,1)}_{n}(x)+\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(\gamma,2)}_{n}(x)+\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(\gamma,3)}_{n}(x).

\bullet

\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(\gamma,2)}_{n}(x)
:
the Berry-Esséen bound (

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(\gamma,2)}_{n}(x)

\displaystyle\leqslant\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(\gamma,2)}_{n}(x)=\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}}\left|\mathbb{P}\!\left(\frac{S_{n}({\boldsymbol{Q}})}{n^{3/4}}\leqslant y\right)-\mathbb{P}\!\left(\frac{2\,{\boldsymbol{Q}}(1-{\boldsymbol{Q}})\sqrt{n}\,G+n(2{\boldsymbol{Q}}-1)}{n^{3/4}}\leqslant y\right)\right|

\displaystyle=O\!\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right).

\bullet

Bound on
\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(\gamma,3)}_{n}(x)
:
One has

\displaystyle\frac{2\,{\boldsymbol{Q}}(1-{\boldsymbol{Q}})\sqrt{n}\,G+n(2{\boldsymbol{Q}}-1)+\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{n}}{n^{3/4}}=\frac{G}{n^{1/4}}\!\left(\sqrt{1-\frac{{\boldsymbol{F}}_{\!\!n,\gamma}^{2}}{\sqrt{n}}}-1\right)+{\boldsymbol{F}}_{\!\!n,\gamma}+\frac{G}{n^{1/4}}\mathds{1}_{\left\{\left|G\right|>\sqrt{n}\right\}}.

Similarly to the proof of Theorem
3.3
, set

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{n,\gamma}

\displaystyle:=\frac{G}{n^{1/4}}\!\left(\sqrt{1-\frac{{\boldsymbol{F}}_{\!\!n,\gamma}^{2}}{\sqrt{n}}}-1\right),

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(\gamma,4)}_{n}(x)

\displaystyle:=\left|\mathbb{P}\!\left(\mathcal{F}_{n,\gamma}+{\boldsymbol{F}}_{\!\!n,\gamma}+\frac{G}{n^{1/4}}\mathds{1}_{\left\{\left|G\right|>\sqrt{n}\right\}}\leqslant x\right)-\mathbb{P}\!\left(\mathcal{F}_{n,\gamma}+{\boldsymbol{F}}_{\!\!n,\gamma}\leqslant x\right)\right|,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(\gamma,5)}_{n}(x)

\displaystyle:=\left|\mathbb{P}\!\left(\mathcal{F}_{n,\gamma}+{\boldsymbol{F}}_{\!\!n,\gamma}\leqslant x\right)-\mathbb{P}\!\left({\boldsymbol{F}}_{\!\!n,\gamma}\leqslant x\right)\right|,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(\gamma,6)}_{n}(x)

\displaystyle:=\left|\mathbb{P}\!\left({\boldsymbol{F}}_{n,\gamma}\leqslant x\right)-\mathbb{P}\!\left({\boldsymbol{F}}_{\!\!\gamma}\leqslant x\right)\right|,

so that

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(\gamma,3)}_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=\left|\mathbb{P}\!\left(\mathcal{F}_{n,\gamma}+{\boldsymbol{F}}_{\!\!n,\gamma}+\frac{G}{n^{1/4}}\mathds{1}_{\left\{\left|G\right|>\sqrt{n}\right\}}\leqslant x\right)-\mathbb{P}\!\left({\boldsymbol{F}}_{\!\!\gamma}\leqslant x\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(\gamma,4)}_{n}(x)+\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(\gamma,5)}_{n}(x)+\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(\gamma,6)}_{n}(x).

\bullet

Bound on
\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(\gamma,5)}_{n}(x)
:
Integrating on
{\boldsymbol{F}}_{\!\!n,\gamma}
and using
F_{{\boldsymbol{F}}_{\!n,\gamma}}(x):=\mathbb{P}\!\left({\boldsymbol{F}}_{\!\!n,\gamma}\leqslant x\right)=\int_{-\infty}^{x}f_{\!{\boldsymbol{F}}_{\!\!n,\gamma}}
and
\mathcal{F}_{n,\gamma}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{L}}}{{=}}-\mathcal{F}_{n,\gamma}
yields

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(\gamma,5)}_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=\left|\mathbb{E}\!\left(F_{{\boldsymbol{F}}_{\!n,\gamma}}\!\left(x+\mathcal{F}_{n,\gamma}\right)\right)-\mathbb{E}\!\left(F_{{\boldsymbol{F}}_{\!n,\gamma}}(x)\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\left|\!\left|f_{{\boldsymbol{F}}_{\!n,\gamma}}\right|\!\right|_{\infty}\mathbb{E}\!\left(\left|\mathcal{F}_{n,\gamma}\right|\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\left|\!\left|f_{{\boldsymbol{F}}_{\!n,\gamma}}\right|\!\right|_{\infty}\frac{\mathbb{E}\!\left(\left|G\right|\right)\mathbb{E}\!\left({\boldsymbol{F}}_{\!\!n,\gamma}^{2}\right)}{n^{3/4}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\!\left(\frac{1}{\mathcal{Z}_{{\boldsymbol{F}}_{\!\gamma}}}+O\!\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\right)\frac{\mathbb{E}\!\left({\boldsymbol{F}}_{\!\!\gamma}^{2}\right)+o(1)}{n^{3/4}},

where we have used Lemma
A.3
for the last inequality.

\bullet

Bound on
\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(\gamma,4)}_{n}(x)
:
Similarly, using
G\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{L}}}{{=}}-G
, we get

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(\gamma,4)}_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=\left|\mathbb{E}\!\left(F_{{\boldsymbol{F}}_{\!n,\gamma}}\!\left(x+\mathcal{F}_{n,\gamma}+\frac{G}{n^{1/4}}\mathds{1}_{\left\{\left|G\right|>\sqrt{n}\right\}}\right)\right)-\mathbb{E}\!\left(F_{{\boldsymbol{F}}_{\!n,\gamma}}(x)\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\!\left(\frac{1}{\mathcal{Z}_{{\boldsymbol{F}}_{\!\gamma}}}+O\!\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\right)\!\left(\frac{\mathbb{E}\!\left({\boldsymbol{F}}_{\!\!\gamma}^{2}\right)+o(1)}{n^{3/4}}+\frac{\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\!\left(G^{2k}\right)}}{n^{k+1/4}}\right).

\bullet

Bound on
\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(\gamma,6)}_{n}(x)
:
One has for all
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and setting
\overline{\varepsilon}:=1-\varepsilon

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(\gamma,6)}_{n}(x)

\displaystyle:=\left|\mathbb{P}\!\left({\boldsymbol{F}}_{\!\!n,\gamma}\leqslant x\right)-\mathbb{P}({\boldsymbol{F}}_{\!\!\gamma}\leqslant x)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{-\infty}^{x}(f_{{\boldsymbol{F}}_{\!n,\gamma}}-f_{{\boldsymbol{F}}_{\!\gamma}})\right|\leqslant\int{R}\left|f_{{\boldsymbol{F}}_{\!n,\gamma}}-f_{{\boldsymbol{F}}_{\!\gamma}}\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\left|\!\left|f_{{\boldsymbol{F}}_{\!n,\gamma}}-f_{{\boldsymbol{F}}_{\!\gamma}}\right|\!\right|_{L^{1}([-\overline{\varepsilon}n^{1/4},\overline{\varepsilon}n^{1/4}])}+\frac{2\mathbb{E}\!\left({\boldsymbol{F}}_{\!\!\gamma}^{4k}\right)+o(1)}{(1-\varepsilon)^{4k}\,n^{k}},

using the triangle inequality and the Markov inequality for all
k\geqslant 1
. We then conclude with (
35
), (
36
) and (
3.2
).

\bullet

Bound on
\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(\gamma,1)}_{n}(x)
:
Recall that
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{n}=2n({\boldsymbol{P}}-{\boldsymbol{Q}})=n^{3/4}({\boldsymbol{F}}_{\!\!n,\gamma}^{\prime}-{\boldsymbol{F}}_{\!\!n,\gamma})
. Then,

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(1)}_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\left|\mathbb{P}\!\left(\frac{S_{n}({\boldsymbol{P}})}{n^{3/4}}\leqslant x\right)-\mathbb{P}\!\left(\frac{S_{n}({\boldsymbol{Q}})+\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{n}}{n^{3/4}}\leqslant x\right)\right|

\displaystyle=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\Big|\mathbb{P}\!\left(S_{n}({\boldsymbol{P}})-n(2{\boldsymbol{P}}-1)\leqslant n^{3/4}x-n(2{\boldsymbol{P}}-1)\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt-\mathbb{P}\!\left(S_{n}({\boldsymbol{Q}})-n(2{\boldsymbol{Q}}-1)\leqslant n^{3/4}x-n(2{\boldsymbol{P}}-1)\right)\Big|

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathbb{E}\Bigg(\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\Bigg|\mathbb{P}\!\left(S_{n}({\boldsymbol{P}})-n(2{\boldsymbol{P}}-1)\leqslant n^{3/4}x-n(2{\boldsymbol{P}}-1)\Big|{\boldsymbol{P}}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt-\mathbb{P}\!\left(S_{n}({\boldsymbol{Q}})-n(2{\boldsymbol{Q}}-1)\leqslant n^{3/4}x-n(2{\boldsymbol{Q}}-1)\Big|{\boldsymbol{P}},{\boldsymbol{Q}}\right)\Bigg|\Bigg)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\!\left(\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}}\Big|\mathbb{P}\!\left(S_{n}({\boldsymbol{P}})-n(2{\boldsymbol{P}}-1)\leqslant y\Big|{\boldsymbol{P}}\right)-\mathbb{P}\!\left(S_{n}({\boldsymbol{Q}})-n(2{\boldsymbol{Q}}-1)\leqslant y\Big|{\boldsymbol{Q}}\right)\Big|\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathbb{E}\!\left(\left|{\boldsymbol{P}}-{\boldsymbol{Q}}\right|\left|\frac{1-({\boldsymbol{P}}+{\boldsymbol{Q}})}{{\boldsymbol{P}}(1-{\boldsymbol{P}})}\right|\right)+O\!\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right),

by Lemma
A.6
.

We recall the following definitions from the proof of Theorem
3.3
:

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{Q}}=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2n^{1/4}}{\boldsymbol{F}}_{\!\!n,\gamma},\quad{\boldsymbol{P}}=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2n^{1/4}}{\boldsymbol{F}}_{\!\!n,\gamma}^{\prime},

\displaystyle 2({\boldsymbol{P}}-{\boldsymbol{Q}})=\frac{1}{n^{1/4}}\!\left({\boldsymbol{F}}_{\!\!n,\gamma}^{\prime}-{\boldsymbol{F}}_{\!\!n,\gamma}\right)=-\frac{G}{\sqrt{n}}\mathds{1}_{\left\{\left|G\right|\leqslant\sqrt{n}\right\}}.

As a result

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(\gamma,1)}_{n}(x)

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{4n^{3/4}}\mathbb{E}\!\left(\left|G\right|\left|\frac{{\boldsymbol{F}}_{\!\!n,\gamma}+{\boldsymbol{F}}_{\!\!n,\gamma}^{\prime}}{\!\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2n^{1/4}}{\boldsymbol{F}}_{\!\!n,\gamma}^{\prime}\right)\!\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2n^{1/4}}{\boldsymbol{F}}_{\!\!n,\gamma}^{\prime}\right)}\right|\right)+O\!\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{n^{3/4}}\!\left(\mathbb{E}\!\left(\!\left(\frac{{\boldsymbol{F}}_{\!\!\gamma}+{\boldsymbol{F}}_{\!\!\gamma}^{\prime}}{1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}{\boldsymbol{F}}_{\!\!\gamma}^{2}}\right)^{\!\!2}\right)+o(1)\right)^{1/2}+O\!\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{\sqrt{2\,\mathbb{E}\!\left({\boldsymbol{F}}_{\!\!\gamma}^{2}\right)}}{n^{3/4}}\!\left(1+O\!\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\right)+O\!\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right)=O\!\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right).

\bullet

Final bound on
\boldsymbol{\delta}^{\gamma}_{n}(x)
:

In the end, we obtain

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}^{\gamma}(x)

\displaystyle\leqslant\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}^{(\gamma,1)}(x)+\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}^{(\gamma,2)}(x)+\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}^{(\gamma,3)}(x)

\displaystyle\leqslant O\!\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right)+O\!\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right)+\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}^{(\gamma,4)}(x)+\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}^{(\gamma,5)}(x)+\boldsymbol{\delta}_{n}^{(\gamma,6)}(x)

\displaystyle\leqslant O\!\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right)+\!\left(\frac{1}{\mathcal{Z}_{{\boldsymbol{F}}_{\!\gamma}}}+O\!\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\right)\!\left(\frac{2\mathbb{E}\!\left({\boldsymbol{F}}_{\!\!\gamma}^{2}\right)+o(1)}{n^{3/4}}+\frac{\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\!\left(G^{2k}\right)}}{n^{k+1/2}}\right)+\frac{\mathbb{E}\!\left({\boldsymbol{F}}_{\!\!\gamma}^{4k}\right)}{(1-\varepsilon)^{4k}\,n^{k}}

\displaystyle=O\!\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right),

which concludes the proof.
∎

Theorem 4.4

Let

\displaystyle d_{\operatorname{Kol}}\!\left(\frac{M_{n}^{(1)}}{n^{3/4}},{\boldsymbol{F}}\right)=O\!\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right).

Theorem 4.5

(Fluctuations of the
unnormalised
magnetisation for
\beta>1
)
.

If
\beta>1
, one has

\displaystyle d_{\operatorname{Kol}}\!\left(\frac{M_{n}^{(\beta)}}{n},{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}}^{(\beta)}\right)=O\!\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right),

(48)

for an explicit constant
C>0
.

Proof.

Using (
43
) and the invariance of
d_{\operatorname{Kol}}
gives

\displaystyle d_{\operatorname{Kol}}\!\left(M_{n}^{(\beta)},G\sqrt{n}\sqrt{1-({\boldsymbol{T}}_{\!n}^{(\beta)})^{2}}+n\,{\boldsymbol{T}}_{\!n}^{(\beta)}\right)=O\!\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right)

\displaystyle\ \Longleftrightarrow\

\displaystyle d_{\operatorname{Kol}}\!\left(\frac{M_{n}^{(\beta)}}{n},\frac{\mathcal{M}_{n}^{(\beta)}}{n}\right)=O\!\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right)

(49)

and the triangle inequality then implies

\displaystyle d_{\operatorname{Kol}}\!\left(\frac{M_{n}^{(\beta)}}{n},{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}}^{(\beta)}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant d_{\operatorname{Kol}}\!\left(\frac{M_{n}^{(\beta)}}{n},\frac{\mathcal{M}_{n}^{(\beta)}}{n}\right)+d_{\operatorname{Kol}}\!\left(\frac{\mathcal{M}_{n}^{(\beta)}}{n},{\boldsymbol{T}}_{\!n}^{(\beta)}\right)+d_{\operatorname{Kol}}\!\left({\boldsymbol{T}}_{\!n}^{(\beta)},{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}}^{(\beta)}\right)

\displaystyle=d_{\operatorname{Kol}}\!\left(\frac{\mathcal{M}_{n}^{(\beta)}}{n},{\boldsymbol{T}}_{\!n}^{(\beta)}\right)+d_{\operatorname{Kol}}\!\left({\boldsymbol{T}}_{\!n}^{(\beta)},{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}}^{(\beta)}\right)+O\!\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right).

One has

\displaystyle d_{\operatorname{Kol}}\!\left(\frac{\mathcal{M}_{n}^{(\beta)}}{n},{\boldsymbol{T}}_{\!n}^{(\beta)}\right)

\displaystyle=d_{\operatorname{Kol}}\!\left({\boldsymbol{T}}_{\!n}^{(\beta)}+\frac{G}{\sqrt{n}}\sqrt{1-({\boldsymbol{T}}_{\!n}^{(\beta)})^{2}},\,{\boldsymbol{T}}_{\!n}^{(\beta)}\right)

\displaystyle=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\left|\mathbb{P}\!\left({\boldsymbol{T}}_{\!n}^{(\beta)}+\frac{G}{\sqrt{n}}\sqrt{1-({\boldsymbol{T}}_{\!n}^{(\beta)})^{2}}\leqslant x\right)-\mathbb{P}\!\left({\boldsymbol{T}}_{\!n}^{(\beta)}\leqslant x\right)\right|

\displaystyle=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\left|\mathbb{P}\!\left(\frac{G}{\sqrt{n}}\leqslant\mathcal{Y}_{n,x,\beta}\right)-\mathbb{P}\!\left(0\leqslant\mathcal{Y}_{n,x,\beta}\right)\right|=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{P}\!\left(0\leqslant\mathcal{Y}_{n,x,\beta}\leqslant\frac{G}{\sqrt{n}}\right),

with

\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}_{n,x,\beta}

\displaystyle:=\frac{x-{\boldsymbol{T}}_{\!n}^{(\beta)}}{\sqrt{1-({\boldsymbol{T}}_{\!n}^{(\beta)})^{2}}}=x\cosh\!\left({\boldsymbol{R}}_{n}^{(\beta)}\right)-\sinh\!\left({\boldsymbol{R}}_{n}^{(\beta)}\right)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\sinh\!\left(\operatorname{Argtanh}(x)-{\boldsymbol{R}}_{n}^{(\beta)}\right)&\mbox{if }\left|x\right|<1\\
s_{x}e^{-s_{x}{\boldsymbol{R}}_{n}^{(\beta)}}&\mbox{if }\left|x\right|=1,\qquad s_{x}:=\operatorname{sign}(x),\\
\sqrt{x^{2}-1}\cosh\!\left(\operatorname{Argtanh}(x^{-1})-{\boldsymbol{R}}_{n}^{(\beta)}\right)&\mbox{if }\left|x\right|>1.\end{cases}

Thus, for
\varepsilon>0
small enough so that
\min_{\left|x-1\right|<\varepsilon}(x-t_{\beta})_{+}>0
, one has

\displaystyle d_{\operatorname{Kol}}\!\left(\frac{\mathcal{M}_{n}^{(\beta)}}{n},{\boldsymbol{T}}_{\!n}^{(\beta)}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\sup_{\left|x-1\right|\leqslant\varepsilon}\mathbb{P}\!\left(0\leqslant\mathcal{Y}_{n,x,\beta}\leqslant\frac{G}{\sqrt{n}}\right)+\sup_{\left|x-1\right|>\varepsilon}\mathbb{P}\!\left(0\leqslant\mathcal{Y}_{n,x,\beta}\leqslant\frac{G}{\sqrt{n}}\right)

One has

\displaystyle\sup_{\left|x-1\right|>\varepsilon}\mathbb{P}\!\left(0\leqslant\mathcal{Y}_{n,x,\beta}\leqslant\frac{G}{\sqrt{n}}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathbb{P}\!\left(\frac{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}{2}e^{\operatorname{Argtanh}(\varepsilon)-{\boldsymbol{R}}_{n}^{(\beta)}}\leqslant\frac{G}{\sqrt{n}}\right)+\mathbb{P}\!\left(\sqrt{2\varepsilon}\leqslant\frac{G}{\sqrt{n}}\right)

\displaystyle=O\!\left(e^{-nC\varepsilon}\right),\qquad C>0,

and

\displaystyle\sup_{\left|x-1\right|<\varepsilon}\mathbb{P}\!\left(0\leqslant\mathcal{Y}_{n,x,\beta}\leqslant\frac{G}{\sqrt{n}}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\sup_{\left|x-1\right|<\varepsilon}\mathbb{P}\!\left((\mathcal{Y}_{n,x,\beta})_{+}\leqslant\frac{G}{\sqrt{n}}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\sup_{\left|x-1\right|<\varepsilon}\mathbb{E}\!\left(e^{G}\right)\mathbb{E}\!\left(e^{-\sqrt{n}(x\cosh({\boldsymbol{R}}_{n}^{(\beta)})-\sinh({\boldsymbol{R}}_{n}^{(\beta)}))_{+}}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\sqrt{e}\sup_{\left|x-1\right|<\varepsilon}e^{-\sqrt{n}[(x\cosh(x_{\beta})-\sinh(x_{\beta}))_{+}+o(1)]}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{e}\,\exp\!\left(-\sqrt{n}\cosh(x_{\beta})\!\left(\min_{\left|x-1\right|<\varepsilon}(x-\tanh(x_{\beta}))_{+}+o(1)\right)\right)

\displaystyle=O\!\left(e^{-C_{\varepsilon}\sqrt{n}}\right),\qquad C_{\varepsilon}>0.

It then remains to analyse

\displaystyle d_{\operatorname{Kol}}\!\left({\boldsymbol{T}}_{\!n}^{(\beta)},{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}}^{(\beta)}\right)

\displaystyle:=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\left|\mathbb{P}\!\left({\boldsymbol{T}}_{\!n}^{(\beta)}\leqslant x\right)-\mathbb{P}\!\left({\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}}^{(\beta)}\leqslant x\right)\right|

\displaystyle=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\left|\mathbb{P}\!\left(\tanh({\boldsymbol{R}}_{n}^{(\beta)})\leqslant x\right)-\mathbb{P}\!\left(\tanh({\boldsymbol{X}}_{\!\beta})\leqslant x\right)\right|

\displaystyle=\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}}\left|\mathbb{P}\!\left({\boldsymbol{R}}_{n}^{(\beta)}\leqslant y\right)-\mathbb{P}\!\left({\boldsymbol{X}}_{\!\beta}\leqslant y\right)\right|

\displaystyle=\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}}\left|\int_{-\infty}^{y}e^{-n\varphi_{\beta}(x)}\frac{dx}{\mathcal{Z}_{n,\beta}}-\frac{1}{2}\!\left(\mathds{1}_{\left\{y\geqslant-x_{\beta}\right\}}+\mathds{1}_{\left\{y\geqslant x_{\beta}\right\}}\right)\right|.

Since
\max_{A\cup B}f=\max\{\max_{A}f,\max_{B}f\}
, it is enough to consider the following quantities:

\displaystyle I_{+}(y)

\displaystyle:=\int_{y}^{+\infty}e^{-n\varphi_{\beta}(x)}\frac{dx}{\mathcal{Z}_{n,\beta}},\qquad y>x_{\beta},

\displaystyle I_{0}(y)

\displaystyle:=\int_{-\infty}^{y}e^{-n\varphi_{\beta}(x)}\frac{dx}{\mathcal{Z}_{n,\beta}}-\frac{1}{2}=\int_{0}^{y}e^{-n\varphi_{\beta}(x)}\frac{dx}{\mathcal{Z}_{n,\beta}},\qquad-x_{\beta}<y\leqslant x_{\beta},

\displaystyle I_{-}(y)

\displaystyle:=\int_{-\infty}^{y}e^{-n\varphi_{\beta}(x)}\frac{dx}{\mathcal{Z}_{n,\beta}}=I_{+}(-y),\qquad y<-x_{\beta}.

By symmetry, it is enough to consider the case
0<y<x_{\beta}
in the case of
I_{0}(y)
. One thus has for
y\in(0,x_{\beta})

and with Lemma
A.5

I_{0}(y)=\int_{0}^{y}e^{-n\varphi_{\beta}(x)}\frac{dx}{\mathcal{Z}_{n,\beta}}\leqslant y\frac{e^{-n\varphi_{\beta}(y)}}{\mathcal{Z}_{n,\beta}}=O\!\left(\sqrt{n}\,e^{-n(\varphi_{\beta}(x_{\beta}-\varepsilon)-\varphi_{\beta}(x_{\beta}))}\right)=O\!\left(\sqrt{n}\,e^{-n\varepsilon^{2}\varphi^{\prime\prime}_{\beta}(x_{\beta})/2}\right),

having set
y:=x_{\beta}-\varepsilon
with
\varepsilon>0
.

In the case of
I_{+}(y)
,
y=x_{\beta}+\varepsilon
, one has with the Markov inequality

\displaystyle I_{+}(x_{\beta}+\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\!\left({\boldsymbol{R}}_{n}^{(\beta)}\geqslant x_{\beta}+\varepsilon\right)\leqslant\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\mathbb{E}\!\left(\left|{\boldsymbol{R}}_{n}^{(\beta)}-x_{\beta}\right|\right)=O\!\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right),

using the computations at the end of the proof of Theorem
3.8
. This concludes the proof.
∎

5.
Conclusion and perspectives

This work was concerned with the study of complex models having particular symmetries encoded into a structure of mixture. It opens the path to study other such models, at several other levels:

(1)

Large or moderate deviations in the vein of
[
18
,
21
]
, mod-
\phi
convergence
[
25
,
26
]
, local CLT
[
27
]
and functional CLT in the vein of
[
32
,
42
]
for the magnetisation with the De Finetti randomisation and the surrogate approach. Interesting questions are e.g.: will the LDP surrogate coming from the randomisation of the precise Cramer/Bahadur-Rao theorem for sums of i.i.d.’s give the correct LDP for the magnetisation? The same questions can be asked for the local CLT or the functional CLT, starting from the one for sums of i.i.d.’s followed by a randomisation. In the case of the LDP, one does not have a surrogate random variable but a surrogate probability (i.e. the randomisation of a probability); one will have to rescale again the randomised LDP to get the correct rate (which should imply a fair piece of analysis). In the case of a local CLT, one can use a surrogate with values in a lattice, such as the Poisson surrogate in Remark
2.2
. In the case of the functional CLT, the results of Jeon and Papangelou
[
32
,
42
]
can certainly be obtained with the surrogate approach with a modification of the arguments presented here, at least in the Bernoulli case. Nevertheless, the functional rescaling of the i.i.d. sums will give a Brownian motion and not a Brownian bridge, i.e. the functional surrogate will then be of the form
m_{n}({\boldsymbol{P}}_{\!\!n})+\sigma_{n}({\boldsymbol{P}}_{\!\!n})B_{t}
where
B
is a standard Brownian motion. As a result, the critical case
\beta=1
will require an additional argument to turn the Brownian motion into a Brownian bridge (the rescaling performed by Papangelou uses a random shift given precisely by the randomisation). As a by-product of such a study, one will get as a bonus the speed of convergence in Kolmogorov distance (say), a question that is not treated in
[
32
,
42
]
.

(2)

The functional CLTs studied in
[
9
,
32
,
42
]
or the extension of the Ellis-Newman Theorem
1.1
for general measures on spins
show the emergence of a general condition on the class of random variables considered, called the
class L
in
[
9
,
22
]
: their cumulant generating series
\Lambda_{X}(s):=\log\mathbb{E}\!\left(e^{sX}\right)
must satisfy “
s\mapsto\frac{s^{2}}{2}+\Lambda_{X}(s)
has a unique minimiser”. On the other hand, the general surrogate approach will be concerned with the replacement of the expectation and variance of the random variables
X_{k}
generalising the Bernoulli random variables
B_{k}(p)
, implying a surrogate of the form
m_{X}(V_{n})+\sigma_{X}(V_{n})G
where
V_{n}
will be the new De Finetti randomisation. Finding conditions on the expectation and variance
(m_{X},\sigma_{X}^{2})
so that the (functional) CLT remains valid and linking them with the conditions defining the class
L
random variables is an interesting question.

(3)

Change of model with exchangeability, e.g. the Curie-Weiss-Potts model
[
20
]
or the inhomogeneous Curie-Weiss model
[
17
]
, the Curie-Weiss-Heisenberg or Curie-Weiss clock model
[
38
]
. Such models will still be exchangeable and Lemma
B.1
will give their De Finetti measure in a straightforward way. More generally, all models with exchangeable pairs can be scrutinised to see if a De Finetti measure is present, and the philosophy of this article will then apply.

(4)

Dynamical Curie-Weiss model and dynamical De Finetti randomisation: replacing the spins
(X_{k})_{k}
by
(X_{k}(t))_{k}
for a good dynamic such as the spin-flip dynamics of
[
37
]
will lead to a dynamical exchangeability randomisation whose dynamic will certainly be interesting. The interplay between
t\to+\infty
and
n\to+\infty
could lead to interesting phenomena in the underlying surrogate, in particular concerning the marginally relevant disorder.

(5)

Change of model with a weight that reads as a Laplace transform, to mimic the proof of Lemma
B.1
and obtain the De Finetti measure in the same way. For instance, replacing the Gaussian by a Poisson random variable gives the following
Poissonian Curie-Weiss model
:

\displaystyle\qquad\mathbb{P}_{PoCW(\beta,n)}=\frac{e^{\psi(S_{n})}}{\mathbb{E}\!\left(e^{\psi(S_{n})}\right)}\bullet\mathbb{P}_{(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})},\quad\psi(x):=e^{\lambda x}-1-\mu x=\log\mathbb{E}\!\left(e^{x(\operatorname{Po}(\lambda)-\mu)}\right)

and this time, the De Finetti randomisation will be discrete, of law
\mu_{n}(k)=\frac{1}{\mathcal{Z}_{n}(\lambda,\mu)\,k!}\cosh(k)^{n}e^{k(\ln(\lambda)-\mu)}
, hence the relevance of a Poisson surrogate.

(6)

Self-Organised Criticality, in short, SOC
[
7
]
: the SOC-Curie-Weiss model is of the type

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{SOC(\beta,n)}=\frac{e^{\beta H(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})}}{\mathbb{E}\!\left(e^{\beta H(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})}\right)}\bullet\mathbb{P}_{(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})},\qquad H({\boldsymbol{x}})=\frac{\!\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}x_{k}\right)^{2}}{1+\sum_{k=1}^{n}x_{k}^{2}},\quad\beta=1.

H
is again a symmetric function, hence, the vector is exchangeable. There is thus a surrogate coming from the CLT (the same as ours), but with a different De Finetti measure, not immediately straightforward to compute.

We hope to come back to some of these questions in the future.

(50)

that follows from the termwise comparison of the Taylor series of each function. As a result, the previous quantity is positive on
\mathbb{R}^{*}
. Moreover, in the same vein as for

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{n^{1/4}\mathcal{Z}_{n,\beta_{n}}}e^{-\frac{n}{2}\operatorname{Argtanh}\,\!\left(\frac{x}{n^{1/4}}\right)^{2}-(\frac{n}{2}+1)\ln\,\!\left(1-\frac{x^{2}}{\sqrt{n}}\right)}\mathds{1}_{\left\{\left|x\right|<n^{1/4}\right\}}

\displaystyle=:\frac{e^{\xi_{n}(x)}}{n^{1/4}\mathcal{Z}_{n,\beta_{n}}}\mathds{1}_{\left\{\left|x\right|<n^{1/4}\right\}}

and

\displaystyle\xi_{n}^{\prime}(x)=-n^{3/4}\,\frac{\beta_{n}^{-1}\operatorname{Argtanh}\!\left(\frac{x}{n^{1/4}}\right)-\!\left(1+\frac{2}{n}\right)\frac{x}{n^{1/4}}}{1-(\frac{x}{n^{1/4}})^{2}}.

We have
\xi_{n}^{\prime}(0)=0
, and
\xi_{n}^{\prime\prime}(0)=\frac{2}{\sqrt{n}}
, hence,
0
is a minimum of
\xi_{n}
and
f_{\!{\boldsymbol{F}}_{\!\!n,\gamma}}
. To find the maxima, set

y:=\operatorname{Argtanh}\!\left(\frac{x}{n^{1/4}}\right)
. One needs to analyse the solutions of the equation

\displaystyle\frac{\tanh(y)}{y}=\frac{\beta_{n}}{1+\frac{2}{n}}.

(52)

This equation is well known in the study of the Curie-Weiss model as it gives the limiting magnetisation when
\beta>1
(see e.g.
[
35
, prop. 8]
). An easy study shows that (
52
) has a unique solution
y_{n}
on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
and by symmetry a unique solution
-y_{n}
on
\mathbb{R}_{-}
, both being global maxima.

Define

\displaystyle G(w)

\displaystyle:=1-\frac{\tanh(\sqrt{w})}{\sqrt{w}}=\frac{w}{3}+O(w^{2})\qquad\mbox{when }w\to 0,

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{n}

\displaystyle=1-\frac{\beta_{n}}{1+\frac{2}{n}}=1-\frac{1-\frac{\gamma}{\sqrt{n}}}{1+\frac{2}{n}}\ \ {\underset{n\to+\infty}{\sim}}\ \frac{\gamma}{\sqrt{n}}\mathds{1}_{\left\{\gamma\neq 0\right\}}+\frac{2}{n}\mathds{1}_{\left\{\gamma=0\right\}}.

Then, the solution
y_{n}
of (

, where
G(w_{n})=\varepsilon_{n}
.
Since
G
is bijective on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
, one can define its inverse
G^{-1}
for the composition
\circ
of functions, and, both functions being
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}

\displaystyle w_{n}

\displaystyle=G^{-1}(\varepsilon_{n})=G^{-1}(0)+(G^{-1})^{\prime}(0)\varepsilon_{n}+O(\varepsilon_{n}^{2})=3\varepsilon_{n}+O(\varepsilon_{n}^{2}),

hence

\displaystyle y_{n}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{w_{n}}=\sqrt{3\varepsilon_{n}}\!\left(1+O\!\left(\varepsilon_{n}^{2}\right)\right)=\sqrt{3\varepsilon_{n}}+O\!\left(\varepsilon_{n}^{3/2}\right),

\displaystyle\frac{x_{n}}{n^{1/4}}

\displaystyle:=\tanh(y_{n})=\tanh\!\left(\sqrt{3\varepsilon_{n}}+O\!\left(\varepsilon_{n}^{3/2}\right)\right)=\sqrt{3\varepsilon_{n}}+O\!\left(\varepsilon_{n}\right).

In the end, using
H(x):=x+\log\!\left(1-\tanh(\sqrt{x})^{2}\right)=\frac{x^{2}}{6}+O(x^{3})
, one gets

\displaystyle\left|\!\left|f_{\!{\boldsymbol{F}}_{\!\!n,\gamma}}\right|\!\right|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=f_{\!{\boldsymbol{F}}_{\!\!n,\gamma}}(x_{n})=\frac{1}{n^{1/4}\mathcal{Z}_{n,\beta_{n}}}e^{-\frac{n}{2\beta_{n}}\operatorname{Argtanh}\,\!\left(\frac{x_{n}}{n^{1/4}}\right)^{2}-(\frac{n}{2}+1)\ln\,\!\left(1-\frac{x_{n}^{2}}{\sqrt{n}}\right)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{1/4}\mathcal{Z}_{n,\beta_{n}}}e^{-\frac{n}{2\beta_{n}}H(w_{n})-\ln\,\!\left(1-\,\tanh(\sqrt{w_{n}})^{2}\right)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{1/4}\mathcal{Z}_{n,\beta_{n}}}e^{-\frac{n}{12\beta_{n}}w_{n}^{2}+O(nw_{n}^{3})+\frac{6}{n}+O(n^{-3/2})}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{1/4}\mathcal{Z}_{n,\beta_{n}}}e^{\frac{\gamma^{2}}{4}\mathds{1}_{\left\{\gamma\neq 0\right\}}+\frac{9}{n}\mathds{1}_{\left\{\gamma=0\right\}}+o(n^{-1})}=\frac{1}{n^{1/4}\mathcal{Z}_{n,\beta_{n}}}\!\left(1+O\!\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\right)

and Lemma

(Asymptotic analysis of
\mathcal{Z}_{n,\beta_{n}}

Set

Lemma A.5

(Asymptotic analysis of
\mathcal{Z}_{n,\beta}^{(k)}
for

)
.

For all

and with

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}\,e^{n\varphi_{\beta}(x_{\beta})}\mathcal{Z}_{n,\beta}^{(k)}

\displaystyle=\!\left(2\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{\varphi_{\beta}^{\prime\prime}(x_{\beta})^{k+1}}}\,\mathbb{E}\!\left(\left|G\right|^{k}\right)+O\!\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\right).

A.2.
Kolmogorov distance estimates

Lemma A.6

(Kolmogorov Distance between two centered binomials)
.

For all
t\in(0,1)
, define
\stackrel{{{}_{\circ}}}{{S_{n}}}\!\!(t):=S_{n}(t)-n(2t-1)
with
S_{n}(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}B_{k}(t)
and
B_{k}(t)\sim\mathrm{i.i.d.}\operatorname{Ber}_{\pm 1}(t)
. Then, for all
p,q\in(0,1)
, one has

\displaystyle d_{\operatorname{Kol}}\!\left(\stackrel{{{}_{\circ}}}{{S_{n}}}\!\!(p),\stackrel{{{}_{\circ}}}{{S_{n}}}\!\!(q)\right)\leqslant\left|p-q\right|\left|\frac{1-(p+q)}{p(1-p)}\right|+O\!\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right).

Proof.

Recall that
\mathbb{E}\!\left(\stackrel{{{}_{\circ}}}{{S_{n}}}\!\!(t)\right)=0
and set
\sigma_{n}^{2}(q):={\operatorname{Var}}(\stackrel{{{}_{\circ}}}{{S_{n}}}\!\!(q))=4q(1-q)n
.
We define
G_{p}\sim\mathscr{N}(0,\sigma_{n}^{2}(p))
and
G_{q}\sim\mathscr{N}(0,\sigma_{n}^{2}(q))
. The triangle inequality yields

\displaystyle d_{\operatorname{Kol}}\!\left(\stackrel{{{}_{\circ}}}{{S_{n}}}\!\!(p),\stackrel{{{}_{\circ}}}{{S_{n}}}\!\!(q)\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant d_{\operatorname{Kol}}\!\left(\stackrel{{{}_{\circ}}}{{S_{n}}}\!\!(p),G_{p}\right)+d_{\operatorname{Kol}}\!\left(\stackrel{{{}_{\circ}}}{{S_{n}}}\!\!(q),G_{q}\right)+d_{\operatorname{Kol}}\!\left(G_{p},G_{q}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant d_{\operatorname{Kol}}\!\left(G_{p},G_{q}\right)+O\!\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right)

by the Berry-Esséen theorem (
43
). It remains to prove that

\displaystyle d_{\operatorname{Kol}}\!\left(G_{p},G_{q}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\left|p-q\right|\left|\frac{1-(p+q)}{p(1-p)}\right|.

Stein’s method for
G_{p}
gives
[
43
, (2.2)]

\displaystyle d_{\operatorname{Kol}}\!\left(G_{p},G_{q}\right)

\displaystyle:=\sup_{w\in\mathbb{R}}\left|\mathbb{P}\!\left(G_{p}\leqslant w\right)-\mathbb{P}\!\left(G_{q}\leqslant w\right)\right|

\displaystyle=\sup_{w\in\mathbb{R}}\left|\mathbb{E}\!\left(f_{w,p}^{\prime}(G_{q})-\frac{G_{q}}{\sigma_{n}^{2}(p)}f_{w,p}(G_{q})\right)\right|,

where

\displaystyle f_{w,p}(x):=\frac{1}{f_{G_{p}}(x)}\mathbb{E}\!\left(\mathds{1}_{\left\{G_{p}\leqslant x\right\}}\!\left(\mathds{1}_{\left\{G_{p}\leqslant w\right\}}-\mathbb{P}\!\left(G_{p}\leqslant w\right)\right)\right),\quad f_{G_{p}}(x):=\frac{1}{\sigma_{n}(p)\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{2\sigma_{n}(p)^{2}}}

is the solution of the Stein equation for
G_{p}
(
[
50
, (19)]
/
[
43
, (2.1)]
).

Moreover, the Gaussian integration by parts gives for
f_{w,p}\in\mathcal{C}^{1}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left(G_{q}f_{w,p}(G_{q})\right)=\sigma_{n}^{2}(q)\mathbb{E}\!\left(f_{w,p}^{\prime}(G_{q})\right),

implying

\displaystyle d_{\operatorname{Kol}}\!\left(G_{p},G_{q}\right)

\displaystyle=\sup\limits_{w\in\mathbb{R}}\left|\mathbb{E}\!\left(f_{w,p}^{\prime}(G_{q})-\frac{\sigma_{n}^{2}(q)}{\sigma_{n}^{2}(p)}f_{w,p}^{\prime}(G_{q})\right)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|1-\frac{\sigma_{n}^{2}(q)}{\sigma_{n}^{2}(p)}\right|\sup_{w\in\mathbb{R}}\left|\mathbb{E}\!\left(f_{w,p}^{\prime}(G_{q})\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\left|1-\frac{\sigma_{n}^{2}(q)}{\sigma_{n}^{2}(p)}\right|\sup_{w\in\mathbb{R}}\left|\!\left|f_{w,p}^{\prime}\right|\!\right|_{\infty}

and (see e.g.
[
50
, (22)]
)
\sup_{w\in\mathbb{R}}\left|\!\left|f_{w,p}^{\prime}\right|\!\right|_{\infty}\leqslant 1
.
Finally

\displaystyle\left|1-\frac{\sigma_{n}^{2}(q)}{\sigma_{n}^{2}(p)}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|1-\frac{4q(1-q)n}{4p(1-p)n}\right|=\left|p-q\right|\left|\frac{1-(p+q)}{p(1-p)}\right|,

which gives the result.
∎

We define the

\displaystyle\psi(\alpha)

Remark B.2
.

Notice the difference between the bias definition (
1
) and the mixture property (
58
). Another way to rephrase the whole philosophy of the surrogate approach would be: when adding a particular penalisation to an i.i.d. sum is equivalent to perform an independent randomisation, one should only use the randomisation framework. Such a situation is of course extremely particular, but one could try to describe more complicated systems as approximation of “mixture systems”, with a replacement method for instance. We leave this study for a future work.

We now show that the magnetisation is solution to a fixed point equation in law, which allows to simulate it with a Markov chain approximating the fixed point:

Lemma B.3

(The magnetisation as a solution to an equation in law)
.

We have

\displaystyle M_{n}^{(\beta)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{L}}}{{=}}\widetilde{S}_{n}\!\left[\sqrt{\frac{\beta}{n}}G+\mu+\frac{\beta}{n}M_{n}^{(\beta)}\right],

(59)

where
(\widetilde{S}_{n},G,M_{n}^{(\beta)})
are independent.

Proof.

Let
\widetilde{S}_{n},S_{n},G
independent random variables and
f
a bounded function. One can write with
\frac{\beta}{n}:=\alpha^{2}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left(f\!\left(M_{n}^{(\beta)}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{E}\!\left(e^{\frac{\beta}{2n}S_{n}^{2}+\mu S_{n}}f(S_{n})\right)}{\mathbb{E}\!\left(e^{\frac{\beta}{2n}S_{n}^{2}+\mu S_{n}}\right)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{E}\!\left(e^{(\alpha G+\mu)S_{n}}f(S_{n})\right)}{\mathbb{E}\!\left(e^{(\alpha G+\mu)S_{n}}\right)}\quad\mbox{with $G$ independent of $S_{n}$}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{E}\!\left(\mathbb{E}_{S_{n}\!}\!\left(e^{(\alpha G+\mu)S_{n}}\right)f(S_{n}\!\left[\alpha G+\mu\right])\right)}{\mathbb{E}\!\left(e^{(\alpha G+\mu)S_{n}}\right)}\quad\mbox{by \eqref{Eq:BernoulliBias}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{E}\!\left(e^{(\alpha G+\mu)\widetilde{S}_{n}}f\!\left(S_{n}\!\left[\alpha G+\mu\right]\right)\right)}{\mathbb{E}\!\left(e^{(\alpha G+\mu)\widetilde{S}_{n}}\right)}\quad\mbox{with $\widetilde{S}_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{L}}}{{=}}S_{n}$ independent of }G,S_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{E}\!\left(e^{(\alpha G+\mu)\widetilde{S}_{n}}f\!\left(S_{n}\!\left[\alpha G+\mu\right]\right)\right)}{\mathbb{E}\!\left(e^{(\alpha G+\mu)\widetilde{S}_{n}}\right)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{E}\!\left(\mathbb{E}_{G\,}\!\left(e^{(\alpha G+\mu)\widetilde{S}_{n}}\right)f\!\left(S_{n}\!\left[\alpha G+\mu+\alpha^{2}\widetilde{S}_{n}\right]\right)\right)}{\mathbb{E}\!\left(e^{(\alpha G+\mu)\widetilde{S}_{n}}\right)}\quad\mbox{by \eqref{Eq:GaussianBias} with $\gamma=\alpha\widetilde{S}_{n}$, }

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{E}\!\left(e^{\alpha\widetilde{S}_{n}^{2}+\mu\widetilde{S}_{n}}f\!\left(S_{n}\!\left[\alpha G+\mu+\alpha^{2}\widetilde{S}_{n}\right]\right)\right)}{\mathbb{E}\!\left(e^{\alpha\widetilde{S}_{n}^{2}+\mu\widetilde{S}_{n}}\right)}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\!\left(f\!\left(S_{n}\!\left[\alpha G+\mu+\alpha^{2}\widetilde{S}^{(\beta)}_{n}\right]\right)\right),

which gives the result.
∎

This result gives a way to simulate
M_{n}^{(\beta)}
(hence the whole sequence of spins) with a sequence of i.i.d. Binomial and Gaussian random variables
(\operatorname{Bin}_{k})_{k}
and
(G_{k})_{k}
. One can form the Markov chain
(M_{k})_{k\geqslant 0}
given, with obvious notations, by:

\displaystyle M_{k+1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{L}}}{{=}}\operatorname{Bin}_{k}\!\left(n,\,\psi\!\left(\sqrt{\frac{\beta}{n}}G_{k}+\mu+\frac{\beta}{n}M_{k}\right)\right),\qquad M_{0}\sim\operatorname{Bin}\!\left(n,\tfrac{1}{2}\right).

An interesting question is to find its rate of convergence to equilibrium (i.e. to
M_{n}^{(\beta)}
) that would give the precise number of iterations to simulate it, and in particular if a cut-off phenomenon appears in total variation distance.
