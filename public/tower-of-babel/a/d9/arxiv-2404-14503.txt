The CO-to-H 2 Conversion Factor in the Barred Spiral Galaxy M83 Facilities: JVLA, GBT, ALMA, Herschel, IRSA, NED
Amanda M Lee, Jin Koda, Akihiko Hirota, Fumi Egusa, Mark Heyer

Abstract
We analyze the CO-to-H 2 conversion factor ( α CO {\alpha_{\rm CO}} ) in the nearby barred spiral galaxy M83. We present new HI observations from the JVLA and single-dish GBT in the disk of the galaxy, and combine them with maps of CO(1-0) integrated intensity and dust surface density from the literature. α CO {\alpha_{\rm CO}} and the gas-to-dust ratio ( δ GDR {\delta_{\rm GDR}} ) are simultaneously derived in annuli of 2 kpc width from R = 1-7 kpc. We find that α CO {\alpha_{\rm CO}} and δ GDR {\delta_{\rm GDR}} both increase radially, by a factor of ∼ \sim 2-3 from the center to the outskirts of the disk. The luminosity-weighted averages over the disk are α CO = 3.14 {\alpha_{\rm CO}}=3.14 (2.06, 4.96) M ⊙ ​ pc − 2 ​ [ K ⋅ km ​ s − 1 ] − 1 {\rm M_{\odot}}\,\,{\rm pc}^{-2}[{\rm K\cdot}\,{\rm km\,s^{-1}}]^{-1} and δ GDR {\delta_{\rm GDR}} = 137 (111, 182) at the 68% (1 σ \sigma ) confidence level. These are consistent with the α CO {\alpha_{\rm CO}} and δ GDR {\delta_{\rm GDR}} values measured in the Milky Way. In addition to possible variations of α CO {\alpha_{\rm CO}} due to the radial metallicity gradient, we test the possibility of variations in α CO {\alpha_{\rm CO}} due to changes in the underlying cloud populations, as a function of galactic radius. Using a truncated power-law molecular cloud CO luminosity function and an empirical power-law relation for cloud-mass and luminosity, we show that the changes in the underlying cloud population may account for a factor of ∼ 1.5 − 2.0 \sim 1.5-2.0 radial change in α CO {\alpha_{\rm CO}} .

II.1
Jansky Very Large Array (JVLA)

New and Archival Data

We used a total of 30 measurement sets (MSs) from the JVLA,

\nu_{\rm HI}

21.1061141 cm),
the primary beam of the JVLA antennae has a full-width at half power (FWHP) of 29.6’.

The MSs include 10 spectral windows (SPWs).

II.2
Green Bank Telescope (GBT)

II.2.1
Observations

GBT observations were also made (project 20A-432).
We used the single-element L-band dual-polarization receiver
and the VErsatile GBT Astronomical Spectrometer (VEGAS).
We employed one of the eight banks in the narrowest bandwidth, 11.72 MHz with 32768 channels.
The corresponding velocity coverage is about 2,400
\,{\rm km\,s^{-1}}
at a 0.08
\,{\rm km\,s^{-1}}
channel width.
The beam size at the full width half maximum (FWHM) is
\sim 8.7\arcmin
at the HI frequency/wavelength.

A total of 5.25 hours was allocated over two sessions in May 2020.
Each session started from receiver tuning, and telescope pointing and focus corrections using 1311-2216.
A region of about
1.2\arcdeg\times 1.5\arcdeg
around M83 was mapped
with the On-The-Fly (OTF) mapping technique in the RA and DEC directions,
with a step size of
3.6\arcmin
(
=\frac{1}{2}\lambda_{\rm HI}/D
, between OTF scans, where
D=100\,\rm m
is the dish diameter).
Each OTF mapping in RA and DEC required 26 and 22 scans to cover the whole region,
and each scan took 72 and 90 seconds, respectively.
After every 4-5 scans, we integrated an OFF position of (RA,DEC)=(13:30:00.0,-29:51:19.0) for 30 seconds.
We obtained a total of 6 OTF maps (3 in RA and 3 in DEC).
We observed 3C286 for flux calibration.

II.2.2
Data Reduction

The data were reduced in a standard way with the GBTIDL software and pipeline.
Time and elevation-dependent amplitude variations were calibrated
using the noise diode in the telescope, ON/OFF sky measurements, and
T_{\rm sys}
measurements.
The calibrated spectra were gridded spatially to produce a data cube using
the GaussBessel kernel function.
We adopted spatial and velocity pixel scales of
1.47\arcmin
and 0.75 km/s.

In order to correct for relative flux variations,
we made 12 data cubes for the two polarizations of the 6 OTF maps.
We measured their flux ratios and inverted the ratio matrix to derive relative flux scaling factors.
Their range was small and was 0.98-1.02 (peak-to-peak) after setting the median to be unity.
We applied these factors to the data and made the integrated data cube.

The absolute flux scale was calibrated using 3C286.
This calibrator had been monitored and confirmed to be stable
[
52
]

with the flux density of
S_{\nu}
= 14.97 Jy at 1.420 GHz
[
53
]
.

II.3
Combination of JVLA and GBT Data

The JVLA and GBT cubes were combined with the “feather” task in CASA.
We regridded the GBT cube, applied the JVLA primary beam to it, combined it with the JVLA cube,
and applied the JVLA primary beam correction to the combined cube.
The final JVLA+GBT data cube has a beam size of
9\arcsec.89\times 8\arcsec.77
and
a sensitivity of about 1.1 mJy/beam in each
5\,{\rm km\,s^{-1}}
channel at the field center.
This HI data covers a large area, and in this paper,
we focus only on the disk of M83 (Figure
1
).

We applied a mask to the final cube for generating an integrated intensity map for a 11’
\times
11’ box around the galaxy center (the region analyzed in Section
II.4
; see Figure
2
).
We made the mask in two steps.
First, we utilized the clean component cube from the JVLA imaging (Section 2.1.2), smoothed it with a Gaussian kernel with a FWHM of
60\arcsec
(about the width of the HI spiral arms), and took regions with fluxes
>5\,\rm mJy/beam
in the smoothed cube to make the first mask with pixel values of either 0 or 1. The first mask encloses significant emission in the JVLA imaging.
Second, we expanded this mask by smoothing it with a Gaussian kernel with a FWHM of
90\arcsec
and by taking the regions
>0.1
.
We start from the clean component cube because, by definition, it includes only the flux components identified as emission.
This operation is to include any extended emission from the GBT data.
The parameters in these operations were chosen manually by looking at the emission distributions in the cubes, and conservatively, so that the final mask may include blank sky but does not miss any emission.
We used this mask also to calculate a noise map using the standard noise propagation based on the number of velocity pixels in each spatial pixel.
Within the 11’
\times
11’ region, we calculated the total flux with and without applying the mask in the cube, and found that the ratio is
\sim
1.09. Thus, the mask likely encloses all of the emission.

The
\chi^{2}
Minimization

Using eqs. (
1
) and (
2
),
a solution of (
{\alpha_{\rm CO}}
,
{\delta_{\rm GDR}}
) is derived
with the observed variables of (
\Sigma_{\rm HI}
,
I_{\rm CO}
,
\Sigma_{\rm d}
).
We rewrite eq. (
1
) as,

\Sigma_{\rm HI}={\delta_{\rm GDR}}\Sigma_{\rm d}-{\alpha_{\rm CO}}I_{\rm CO}.

(5)

and define
\chi^{2}
as

\chi^{2}=\sum_{i}\frac{(\Sigma_{\mathrm{HI}}-{\delta_{\rm GDR}}\Sigma_{\rm d}+{\alpha_{\rm CO}}I_{\rm CO})^{2}}{\sigma_{\Sigma_{\rm HI}}^{2}+({\delta_{\rm GDR}}\sigma_{\Sigma_{\rm d}})^{2}+({\alpha_{\rm CO}}\sigma_{I_{\rm CO}})^{2}}.

(6)

\sigma_{\Sigma_{\rm HI}}
,
\sigma_{I_{\rm CO}}
, and
\sigma_{\Sigma_{\rm d}}
are
the errors in
\Sigma_{\rm HI}
,
I_{\rm CO}
, and
\Sigma_{\rm d}
respectively.
The summations are over all image pixels,
i
, within a region of interest in the galaxy.

The
\chi^{2}
values are calculated on a 2-dimensional grid of
(
{\alpha_{\rm CO}}
,
{\delta_{\rm GDR}}
).
The minimum
\chi^{2}
is searched in this space.
Figure
5
a shows an example for an annulus of
R=3
-
5\,{\rm kpc}
.
If the errors follow the normal distribution,
the
\chi^{2}
values can be converted to confidence intervals to assess the uncertainties (errors).
The figure shows three contours at
\Delta\chi^{2}\equiv\chi^{2}-\chi_{\rm min}^{2}=1
, 4, and 9, which correspond to
the confidence levels (CLs) of 68, 95, and 99.7% when they are projected to the
{\alpha_{\rm CO}}
or
{\delta_{\rm GDR}}
axis.
The results of the fitting are discussed in Section
V.1
.

IV.3.2
The
\log{\delta_{\rm GDR}}
Scatter Minimization

A solution can also be derived by minimizing the scatter,
\Delta\log{\delta_{\rm GDR}}
,
in
\log{\delta_{\rm GDR}}
over a region of interest.
For a fixed
{\alpha_{\rm CO}}
, we calculate a map of

{\delta_{\rm GDR}}=\frac{\Sigma_{\rm g}}{\Sigma_{\rm d}}=\frac{\Sigma_{\rm HI}+{\alpha_{\rm CO}}I_{\rm CO}}{\Sigma_{\rm d}}

(7)

and evaluate the scatter
\Delta\log{\delta_{\rm GDR}}
.
This is repeated for a range of
\log{\alpha_{\rm CO}}\in(-1.0,+2.0)
,
hence for a wide range of
{\alpha_{\rm CO}}\in(0.1,100.0)
,
to find the
{\alpha_{\rm CO}}
that minimizes the scatter.
Following
Sandstrom et al. [63]
, the scatter and mean of
\log{\delta_{\rm GDR}}
are calculated
as the scale and location of the robust statistics, respectively.

Sandstrom et al. [63]
showed, in their Figure 1c, that the minimization of
\Delta\log{\delta_{\rm GDR}}

is equivalent to adopting a constant
{\delta_{\rm GDR}}
(i.e., the inverse of their dust-to-gas ratio - DGR)
independent of gas phase (i.e.,
I_{\rm CO}/\Sigma_{\rm HI}
, the ratio between
I_{\rm CO}
and
\Sigma_{\rm HI}
).

Figure
5
b shows an example for the same region (
R=3
-
5\,{\rm kpc}
) as in Figure
5
a.
The color in the background shows
\Delta\log{\delta_{\rm GDR}}
as a function of
{\alpha_{\rm CO}}
: each
{\alpha_{\rm CO}}
corresponds to one value of
\Delta\log{\delta_{\rm GDR}}
, and hence,

\Delta\log{\delta_{\rm GDR}}
changes only in the horizontal direction.
In addition, an average
\log{\delta_{\rm GDR}}
at each
{\alpha_{\rm CO}}
can be evaluated with eq. (
7
).
We adopt the biweight location to approximate the average
following
Sandstrom et al. [63]
, which is shown as the white line in Figure
5
b.
Along this line, the point at the minimum of
\Delta\log{\delta_{\rm GDR}}
, in the color background, is the solution.
The result of the fitting is discussed in Section
V.1
.

V.2
Galaxy-wide Averages

The average
{\alpha_{\rm CO}}
and
{\delta_{\rm GDR}}
give the representative values of the whole galaxy.
Since both of these parameters vary systematically along the galactic radius,
their averages depend on how the annuli are weighted in averaging.
Here we calculate the galaxy-average values by applying
different weightings on the annulus-based solutions (Table
1
)
or by applying the fitting methods in Section
IV.3
to the whole galaxy.
The best averaging scheme may depend on the purpose of usage.
For example, to derive the total molecular gas mass over a galaxy, the luminosity-weighted average
{\alpha_{\rm CO}}
may be the best.

We first calculate the averages from the annulus-based analysis (Table
1
).
With the
\chi^{2}
method, the averages weighting by luminosity and area are 3.14 (2.06 - 4.96) and 3.96 (2.73 - 5.81), respectively, with the values within the parenthesis indicating the 68% confidence points.
With the
\Delta{\rm log}{\delta_{\rm GDR}}
method, the average
{\alpha_{\rm CO}}
are 3.13 and 3.93 for the luminosity and area weightings, respectively.
Since the luminosity is concentrated more towards the inner galaxy,
the luminosity-weighted galaxy average tends to weight the inner part of the disk more, and results in a smaller
{\alpha_{\rm CO}}
.
On the other hand, there is more area in the outer galaxy, and the area-weighted average skews more towards the values of the outer disk,
leading to the larger
{\alpha_{\rm CO}}
.
Nevertheless, all these averages are consistent within an expected factor of 2 uncertainty.

The luminosity and area weighted
{\delta_{\rm GDR}}
using the
\chi^{2}
method are 137 (111, 182) and 167 (140, 209), while the
\Delta
log
{\delta_{\rm GDR}}
method gives 136 and 164.
The
{\delta_{\rm GDR}}
increases with radius, and the luminosity weighting weighs the inner disk more, while the area weighting weighs the outer disk more.

We also apply the
\chi^{2}
and
\Delta\log{\delta_{\rm GDR}}
methods to the whole area (
R=
1-7
\,{\rm kpc}
; excluding the
R<1\,{\rm kpc}
area).
The results are in the last row of Table
1
.
The
\chi^{2}
fitting gives
{\alpha_{\rm CO}}=6.03
with
\Delta\chi=1
range of 5.19-6.90.
The minimization of
\log{\delta_{\rm GDR}}
also gives a very similar value
of
{\alpha_{\rm CO}}=5.95
.
These values are almost a factor of 2 larger than the annulus-based averages,
and are likely due to the non-linearity between
I_{\rm CO}
and the molecular gas surface density

\Sigma_{{\rm H}_{2}}
, calculated as the difference between
the dust-based total gas and atomic gas surface densities (i.e.,
\Sigma_{\rm H_{2}}={\delta_{\rm GDR}}\Sigma_{\rm d}-\Sigma_{\rm HI}
; see Section
VI.2
). In other words, the assumed linear fit equation (
5
) may break down on the scales of the entire galactic disk in M83, as most environmental parameters (such as metallicity, cloud populations, dominant gas phase - molecular vs. atomic) can vary across the disk.

This is a potential limitation of this method, but in our annulus-based analysis in Section
V.1
, this limitation is mitigated.

Bolatto et al. [9]
summarized the various measurements of
{\alpha_{\rm CO}}
in the MW and recommended
\alpha_{\rm MW}={\alpha_{\rm CO}}=4.35{\rm M_{\odot}}\,\,{\rm pc}^{-2}[{\rm K}\,{\rm km\,s^{-1}}]^{-1}
with uncertainties of
\pm
30%
within the inner MW disk (R=1-9 kpc) and a factor of 2 for “normal” galactic disks with solar metallicity.
Taking into account our own uncertainties,
the derived
{\alpha_{\rm CO}}
averages are consistent within the
{\alpha_{\rm CO}}
in the solar metallicity (
{\alpha_{\rm CO}}\sim
2.2-8.7, i.e.,
1/2\alpha_{\rm MW}
to
2\alpha_{\rm MW}
).

VI
Discussion

VI.1
Previous Measurements in M83

In this study, we derived
{\alpha_{\rm CO}}
and
{\delta_{\rm GDR}}
, simultaneously in M83.
Previous studies of this galaxy derived
{\alpha_{\rm CO}}
by fixing
{\delta_{\rm GDR}}

[
39
]
, derived
{\delta_{\rm GDR}}
by fixing
{\alpha_{\rm CO}}

[
25
]
, or derived
{\alpha_{\rm CO}}
and
{\delta_{\rm GDR}}
together
[
77
]
.

Jiao et al. [39]
estimated the CO-to-H
2
and CI-to-H
2
conversion factors in M83, also using the dust-based method as we do in this study (eq.
1
), but with THINGS HI and Nobeyama CO(1-0) data
[
73
,
44
]
and the
Draine & Li [22]
dust model.
They fixed
{\delta_{\rm GDR}}
, assuming three forms of metallicity-dependent
{\delta_{\rm GDR}}
, and derived

\alpha_{CO}
finding an increase from
\sim
1.0 to
\sim
3.2 from the central region to their CO detection radius limit of
R\sim 4
kpc (their Figure 2).
These appear consistent with the results derived in Section
V
, but with caution. As demonstrated in Figure
2
, the ratios of the archival HI and CO(1-0) fluxes used in their study over our fluxes are, on average, only 40 and 80%, respectively.
These ratios also vary radially and azimuthally, e.g. between the arm and interarm regions.
If we simplistically take the average differences and compare them with eq. (
1
),
their
{I_{\rm CO}}/\Sigma_{\rm HI}
ratio would be a factor of two larger than ours, which should result in twice smaller
{\alpha_{\rm CO}}
than ours.
The apparent consistency may indicate that the dust-based method, which both their and our studies employed, has an intrinsic uncertainty of a factor of two.

Yasuda et al. [77]
analyzed the same HI and CO(1-0) data as
Jiao et al. [39]
and adopted the SED fitting by
Casey [12]
for
\Sigma_{\rm d}
.
They derived both
{\alpha_{\rm CO}}
and
{\delta_{\rm GDR}}
together.
They obtained an average of
{\alpha_{\rm CO}}=0.84
for
R\lesssim 4.7\,{\rm kpc}
(0.60 - 1.23, within the 68% confidence level in the
{\alpha_{\rm CO}}
-
{\delta_{\rm GDR}}
plane),
and a radial increase in
{\alpha_{\rm CO}}
by about a factor of two from 0.34 (0.18 - 0.80) to 0.78 (0.43 - 1.42) between the inner (
R<1.7\,{\rm kpc}
) and outer (
>1.7\,{\rm kpc}
, up to
R\sim 4.7\,{\rm kpc}
) disk.
The radial increase is consistent with the factor of two increase in our results, from 1.82 to 3.91 (Table
1
).
The values of their
{\alpha_{\rm CO}}
are about
twice smaller than our results, which can be explained by the flux errors in their HI and CO data (as discussed above).

Yasuda et al. [77]
also found
{\delta_{\rm GDR}}=
73 (59-94) on average, with an increasing trend from 33 (21-66) in
R<1.7\,{\rm kpc}
to 72 (55-102) in
>1.7\,{\rm kpc}
.
These are about three times smaller than our results (Table
1
).
This discrepancy could also be explained by the factor of 2.5 smaller HI flux in their data,
which reduces
{\delta_{\rm GDR}}
by the same factor (see eq.
1
).

Foyle et al. [25]
fixed
{\alpha_{\rm CO}}
and derived
{\delta_{\rm GDR}}
on spatial scales of
\sim 0.8\rm kpc
. They used a modified blackbody fit for
\Sigma_{\rm d}
and applied both a constant (MW value) and metallicity-dependent
{\alpha_{\rm CO}}
.
They used THINGS HI and CO(3-2) emission from the JCMT for the atomic and molecular gas, respectively.
From their Figure 10, the radial
{\delta_{\rm GDR}}
profile looks roughly constant, with
\delta_{GDR}
values of
\sim
80 and
\sim
60 when using a constant and metallicity-dependent
{\alpha_{\rm CO}}
(their equation 6) respectively. These values could be corrected upwards, possibly by a factor of two, if the factor of 2 flux error in the THINGS HI data is taken into account (see eq.
5
). To that end, the
\delta_{GDR}
derived in this study are consistent with their values.

VI.2
Possible Origin of Radial Gradients

We find that
{\alpha_{\rm CO}}
and
{\delta_{\rm GDR}}
systematically increase with galactic radius (Section
V.1
).
Such variations are often attributed to the dependence on metallicity
[
74
,
3
,
36
,
47
]
, and we discuss their possible metallicity-dependence in Section
VI.2.1
.
At the same time, many galactic parameters vary radially (e.g., stellar density and radiation field, gravitational potential, strength of galactic structures, cosmic ray intensity, gas amount, and phase, as well as metallicity),
and the
{\alpha_{\rm CO}}
and
{\delta_{\rm GDR}}
could correlate with any of those radially-variable parameters.
It is
valuable to discuss other possible origins of the
{\alpha_{\rm CO}}
and
{\delta_{\rm GDR}}
variations.
In Section
VI.2.2
, we discuss another possibility: a dependence on the underlying molecular cloud populations.
Identifying the exact origin is beyond the scope of this work, and here we aim to investigate a possible origin.

Figure
7
presents a clue to understanding the radial gradient of
{\alpha_{\rm CO}}
.
This plot shows molecular and atomic gas surface densities vs.
I_{\rm CO}
. The top panel is for the disk (
R=
1-7
\,{\rm kpc}
), and the subsequent panels show the same data, but for each
2\,{\rm kpc}
-width annulus
separately.
If
{\alpha_{\rm CO}}
is constant, then we expect a straight line for the molecular gas, with
{\alpha_{\rm CO}}
as the slope.
The blue points “
{\delta_{\rm GDR}}\Sigma_{\rm d}-\Sigma_{\rm HI}
” represent the molecular gas surface density
(equivalent to
\Sigma_{\rm H_{2}}
when
{\delta_{\rm GDR}}
is calibrated; see eq.
1
).
Again,
{\alpha_{\rm CO}}=\Sigma_{\rm H_{2}}/I_{\rm CO}
is the ratio between the
y
-axis and the
x
-axis in this plot
and is the CO-to-H
2
conversion factor,
when the
y
-intercept is zero.
For comparison, the figure also includes the total HI+H
2
gas surface density “
{\delta_{\rm GDR}}\Sigma_{\rm d}
” (yellow)
and
\Sigma_{\rm HI}
(magenta).

The blue points show non-linear (curved) distributions.
The black solid lines are 2-d polynomial fits to the blue data points for
R=
1-7
\,{\rm kpc}
(top panel) and are the same in all panels for reference.
The curved distributions indicate that the slope
{\alpha_{\rm CO}}
decreases with increasing
I_{\rm CO}
.
At the same time, these panels also show that the range of
I_{\rm CO}
that each annulus occupies declines radially,
and hence,
{\alpha_{\rm CO}}
increases with galactic radius.

This suggests that if the non-linear dependence of
{\alpha_{\rm CO}}
on
I_{\rm CO}
reflects intrinsic gas properties,
the range of
I_{\rm CO}
that each radius covers determines the average
{\alpha_{\rm CO}}
in that radius.
The non-linearity thus could explain the radial increase of
{\alpha_{\rm CO}}
.

5
5

5

This radial trend (in Figure
7
) would be amplified further if the radially-increasing
{\delta_{\rm GDR}}
is also taken into account:
in a relative sense, the yellow data points (
{\delta_{\rm GDR}}\Sigma_{\rm d}
) would move downwards in smaller radii and upwards in larger radii, and hence,
the blue data points would also move downwards (in the lower
{\alpha_{\rm CO}}

direction) in smaller radii and upwards (in the larger
{\alpha_{\rm CO}}
direction) in larger radii.

The above discussion relies on an assumption that the other parameters,
especially
\Sigma_{\rm d}
, are determined accurately.
It is also possible that the determination of
\Sigma_{\rm d}
could suffer from some systematic errors
even after significant efforts, due to the inherent difficulty
in constraining dust properties.
The relation between
I_{\rm CO}
and
{\delta_{\rm GDR}}\Sigma_{\rm d}
(note that
{\delta_{\rm GDR}}
is constant in the plots) is already non-linear in a wide range of
I_{\rm CO}
,
which is inherited by the molecular gas surface density “
{\delta_{\rm GDR}}\Sigma_{\rm d}-\Sigma_{\rm HI}
” (blue).

VI.2.2
Cloud Population Dependence

As discussed in Section
VI.2
(see also Figure
7
),
{\alpha_{\rm CO}}
appears to depend on the range of
I_{\rm CO}
that each annulus traces, and hence depends on the range of
\Sigma_{\rm H_{2}}
. It therefore seems natural to consider variations of the physical conditions of the gas, i.e., the average properties of the underlying molecular cloud populations, as another potential origin of the
{\alpha_{\rm CO}}
variation.

Dickman et al. [20]
calculated
{\alpha_{\rm CO}}
as an average over a cloud population (see Appendix
C
). We extend this idea and examine an effect of varying cloud populations on
{\alpha_{\rm CO}}
.
While our
56.6\arcsec
(1.23 kpc) resolution does not resolve individual molecular clouds,
it has been known that the cloud population in M83 varies radially: massive (luminous) clouds are populated more in the inner disk than in the outer disk
[
27
, Hirota et al. in prep.]
.

The dynamical state of molecular clouds is often characterized with the virial parameter
\alpha_{\rm vir}
,
which is the ratio between the kinetic energy
K
and gravitational potential energy
U
of cloud
[
7
]
,

\alpha_{\rm vir}\equiv\frac{2K}{|U|}.

(8)

A cloud is gravitationally-bound when
\alpha_{\rm vir}\leq 2
(i.e.,
K\leq|U|
), and is in virial equilibrium when
\alpha_{\rm vir}=1
(
2K=|U|
, the virial theorem).
Hence, gravitationally-bound clouds should show
\alpha_{\rm vir}=
1-2.

For a spherical cloud with the mass
M
, line-of-sight velocity dispersion
\sigma_{\rm v}
, and density profile
\rho(r)\propto r^{-\xi}
truncated at
r=R
, we have
U=-(3-\xi)/(5-2\xi)(GM^{2}/R)
and
K=3/2(M\sigma_{\rm v}^{2})
.
Thus, with a definition of the virial mass
M_{\rm vir}=3(5-2\xi)/(3-\xi)(R\sigma_{\rm v}^{2}/G)
,

\alpha_{\rm vir}=\frac{M_{\rm vir}}{M}.

(9)

Molecular clouds in the Milky Way (MW) show central concentrations (
\xi>0
) but do not have as steep a profile as the isothermal sphere (
\xi<2
). If we adopt
\xi=1
as done by
Solomon et al. [67]
,
M_{\rm vir}=9/2(R\sigma_{\rm v}^{2}/G)
.
6
6

6

We take this definition of
M_{\rm vir}
so that the virialized state is represented by
\alpha_{\rm vir}=1
, since this seems to be a natural definition of the virial parameter. Some studies define
M_{\rm vir}^{\prime}\equiv 5R\sigma_{\rm v}^{2}/G

independent of
\xi
and
in turn their virial parameter is
\alpha_{\rm vir}^{\prime}\equiv M_{\rm vir}^{\prime}/M=(5/3)((3-\xi)/(5-2\xi))\alpha_{\rm vir}
. In their case, the virialized state is
\alpha_{\rm vir}^{\prime}
=1.00, 1.11, and 1.67 for
\xi
= 0, 1, and 2, respectively.

The true cloud mass
M
is often represented by the luminous mass
M_{\rm lum}
(
={\alpha_{\rm CO}}L_{\rm CO}
) using the cloud CO luminosity
L_{\rm CO}
, and hence,

\alpha_{\rm vir}{\alpha_{\rm CO}}=\frac{M_{\rm vir}}{L_{\rm CO}}.

(10)

Solomon et al. [67]
analyzed a population of molecular clouds with masses
>10^{4}{\rm M_{\odot}}
in the MW
and found a power-law relation of
M_{\rm vir}=39L_{\rm CO}^{0.81}
with
M_{\rm vir}
and
L_{\rm CO}

in units of
{\rm M_{\odot}}
and
\rm K\,\,{\rm km\,s^{-1}}\,\,{\rm pc}^{2}
, respectively.
Using the same data but conducting a separate analysis,

Scoville et al. [65]
obtained a linear relation,
M_{\rm vir}=7.9L_{\rm CO}
, for clouds with
>10^{5}{\rm M_{\odot}}
.
In a generalized form, this empirical relation, along with eq. (
10
), is

\alpha_{\rm vir}{\alpha_{\rm CO}}=\frac{M_{\rm vir}}{L_{\rm CO}}=\alpha_{\rm ref}\left(\frac{L_{\rm CO}}{L_{\rm ref}}\right)^{\beta}.

(11)

We adopt a CO reference luminosity of
L_{\rm ref}=10^{5}\,\rm K\cdot\,{\rm km\,s^{-1}}\,{\rm pc}^{2}
.
The relations found by
Solomon et al. [67]
and
Scoville et al. [65]
translate to
\beta=-0.19
and
\beta=0
, respectively.

In the case of
\beta\neq 0
, eq. (
11
) means that
{\alpha_{\rm CO}}
and/or
\alpha_{\rm vir}
change with the cloud luminosity.
It has been discussed in two ways, by assuming either
{\alpha_{\rm CO}}
or
\alpha_{\rm vir}
to be constant.

Chevance et al. [13]
assumed a constant
{\alpha_{\rm CO}}
and discussed that
\alpha_{\rm vir}
, the dynamical state of clouds, changes with cloud luminosity, and hence with mass.

Bolatto et al. [9]
adopted
\alpha_{\rm vir}=1
(all clouds are in virial equilibrium) and explained that

{\alpha_{\rm CO}}
decreases with cloud luminosity and mass.
If
\alpha_{\rm vir}
is constant, the average
{\alpha_{\rm CO}}
in a region should depend on the underlying cloud population.

In order to evaluate the average
{\alpha_{\rm CO}}
in each annulus,
we adopt the simplest form of commonly used luminosity functions, a truncated power-law cloud luminosity function
[
61
,
67
,
59
,
58
,
15
]

defined between a maximum and minimum cloud luminosity,
L_{\rm max}
and
L_{\rm min}

\displaystyle\Phi({L_{\rm CO}}){\rm{d}}{L_{\rm CO}}=\Phi_{\rm max}\left(\frac{{L_{\rm CO}}}{L_{\rm max}}\right)^{\gamma}{\rm{d}}{L_{\rm CO}},

(12)

where
\Phi({L_{\rm CO}})\,{\rm{d}}{L_{\rm CO}}
is the number of clouds with luminosity
{L_{\rm CO}}
in a volume and

\Phi_{\rm max}
is the number density of clouds with
L_{\rm max}
.
We adopt a luminosity function instead of a mass function because a mass function would require assuming a fixed conversion factor.

The total luminosity and mass in an annulus are

L_{tot}=\int{L_{\rm CO}}\Phi\,{\rm d}{L_{\rm CO}}/\int\Phi\,{\rm d}{L_{\rm CO}}
and

M_{tot}=\int\alpha_{CO}{L_{\rm CO}}\Phi\,{\rm d}{L_{\rm CO}}/\int\Phi\,{\rm d}{L_{\rm CO}}
, respectively.
With eq. (
11
) and constant
\alpha_{\rm vir}
, we derive the average
\langle{\alpha_{\rm CO}}\rangle=M_{tot}/L_{tot}
for the cloud population,

\displaystyle\langle{\alpha_{\rm CO}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{\alpha_{\rm vir}}\right)\alpha_{\rm ref}\frac{\gamma+2}{\beta+\gamma+2}\left(\frac{L_{\rm max}}{L_{\rm ref}}\right)^{\beta}

(13)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\left[\left(1-\left(\frac{L_{\rm min}}{L_{\rm max}}\right)^{\beta+\gamma+2}\right)/\left(1-\left(\frac{L_{\rm min}}{L_{\rm max}}\right)^{\gamma+2}\right)\right].

Note that
\alpha_{\rm ref}
is in the units of
{\alpha_{\rm CO}}
, and
\alpha_{\rm vir}
is a dimensionless parameter.

L_{\rm min}/L_{\rm max}
is small
[
67
,
65
,
\sim 10^{-2}
, ]
, and
the last term within the bracket is around 1.
Thus, in the limit where
L_{\rm min}/L_{\rm max}\rightarrow 0
,

\langle{\alpha_{\rm CO}}\rangle
depends on the luminosity of the brightest and hence most massive cloud.

Hirota et al. (in prep.) generated a catalog of molecular clouds in M83. In their analysis,
\sim
91% of the total CO luminosity within the mapped area was assigned to molecular clouds.
They fitted a truncated power-law cloud luminosity function and obtained (
\gamma
,
L_{\rm max}
) in each annulus (Table
2
).
Using their cloud catalog, we
determine (
\alpha_{\rm ref}
,
\beta
) of eq. (
11
)
in Appendix
A
.
These parameters are listed in Table
4
in the appendix.
In this catalog, small clouds are not entirely spatially resolved.
Hirota et al. (in prep.)
uses their peak brightness temperature
T_{\rm peak}
as a rough measure to separate likely-resolved clouds (
T_{\rm peak}>2
K) from likely-unresolved ones (
<2
K) (Appendix
A
).
Therefore, we obtained (
\alpha_{\rm ref}
,
\beta
)
for all clouds (denoted “M83

T_{\rm peak}>2
K (“M83

As an example of unambiguously-resolved clouds, we also use the clouds in the MW
[
67

.
For consistency, we made our own fit to the MW clouds (Appendix

)
and use the MW relation for the M83 clouds as well.

With these parameters, we derive the population-averaged
\langle\alpha_{CO}\rangle
for clouds in M83.
The results in all cases are listed in Table

The

If we adopt the case of gravitationally-bound clouds (

the

increases radially from

= 3.84 to 7.58 using (

).
Figure

compares the

We should note that because
\langle\alpha_{CO}\rangle\propto 1/\alpha_{\rm vir}
(eq.
13
)
the absolute values increase by a factor of two if we adopt

(all clouds are in virial equilibrium; see Table

columns 6-8), instead of
\alpha_{\rm vir}=2
(the clouds are merely gravitationally-bound; columns 9-11).
This uncertainty stems from our lack of knowledge on the exact dynamical state of molecular clouds.

Here, we tested the idea that
{\alpha_{\rm CO}}
may depend on the underlying cloud population and its variations,
and showed that the cursory estimation can explain the radial increase of
{\alpha_{\rm CO}}
.
There are of course some caveats.
For example, we did not consider a potential dependence of the cloud luminosity function (eq.
12
) on metallicity.
It is possible that previous studies might have already unintentionally treated the possible dependence on cloud population as a dependence on other parameters, since the variation of cloud population itself could depend on other parameters, e.g., dynamical environment, metallicity, etc. These dependencies make it difficult to isolate the origin of the
{\alpha_{\rm CO}}
variations. Put simply, if the properties of molecular clouds vary, e.g., as in eq. (
11

the
{\alpha_{\rm CO}}
averaged over a cloud population should also change accordingly.

VII
Summary and Conclusions

We present new HI observations in the disk of M83 combining JVLA and single-dish GBT data. In the region we analyzed in this study, the new observations recover a factor of 2 more flux than the distributed THINGS moment 0 map used in previous studies.
We compare these HI observations to CO(J=1-0) and a dust surface density map derived
with the conversion equation from a 500
\mu
m image
[
2
]
. We derive (
{\alpha_{\rm CO}}
,
{\delta_{\rm GDR}}
) simultaneously with the dust-based approach, i.e., using dust masses as a tracer of total gas masses via two methods: (1)
\chi^{2}
minimization (plane-fitting) and (2) log
{\delta_{\rm GDR}}
minimization. These gave similar results. The dust-based approach requires accurate
\Sigma_{\rm{HI}}
measurements as
\Sigma_{\rm HI}
determines the absolute scale of
{\delta_{\rm GDR}}
, which calibrates the scale for
{\alpha_{\rm CO}}
.

We find that
{\alpha_{\rm CO}}
and
{\delta_{\rm GDR}}
both increase as a function of radius, by a factor of
\sim
2-3 from R = 1-3 to 5-7 kpc. These radial changes are consistent with the ones found in previous studies, when the errors in flux calibration in the previous studies are taken into account.
As representative values for the galactic disk, we also average the annulus values and derive, e.g., luminosity-weighted averages of
{\alpha_{\rm CO}}
= 3.14 (2.06, 4.96) and
{\delta_{\rm GDR}}
= 137 (111, 182).
These are similar to the values measured in the Milky Way.

The variations of
{\alpha_{\rm CO}}
and
{\delta_{\rm GDR}}
in galaxies are often explained by metallicity dependence.
We show that the metallicity dependence could explain a factor of
1.2-1.3

radial increase in
{\alpha_{\rm CO}}
.
We also present another possibility: the radial variation of
{\alpha_{\rm CO}}
as due to the variation of the underlying cloud population. This idea stems from the observed nonlinearity between
\Sigma_{\rm{d}}
and
\Sigma_{\rm{H}_{2}}

(assuming it primarily arises from gas properties, and not potential systematic uncertainties in dust),
and the tracing of different ranges of
I_{\rm{CO}}
in each annulus. We test this possibility by assuming constant
\alpha_{\rm vir}
for clouds, and using a
truncated power-law cloud luminosity function and an empirical dependence of
{\alpha_{\rm CO}}
on cloud CO luminosity. We show that the cloud averaged
\langle{\alpha_{\rm CO}}\rangle
can explain a factor of
1.5-2.0
change in
{\alpha_{\rm CO}}
, compared to the factor of
2-3
change
from the annulus-based fits.
Beyond the relative radial changes, the absolute values of
{\alpha_{\rm CO}}
predicted by the metallicity and cloud population-dependences are consistent with the results we obtained, if we adopt a particular calibration for the metallicity measurements and
\alpha_{\rm vir}
value (Figure
8
).
This is suggestive, but must be revisited in the future, as there is no strong evidence to support the particular calibration nor value.

The exact origin of
{\alpha_{\rm CO}}
variations in nearby galaxies remains difficult to disentangle as most environmental parameters vary with galactic radius and correlate with each other.
While the dependence on cloud population
may in fact embed dependencies on dynamical environment, disk potential, metallicity, etc., it is important to recognize that
{\alpha_{\rm CO}}
could depend on cloud population,
especially as we move towards more spatially resolved analyses of molecular clouds.
Similarly, in terms of cloud population and their properties, we discussed only about the dependence of
{\alpha_{\rm CO}}
on cloud luminosity.

{\alpha_{\rm CO}}
is also known to vary with cloud temperature
T
and density
\rho
(i.e.,
{\alpha_{\rm CO}}\propto\rho^{1/2}/T
, ee Appendix
C
)
[
20
,
67
,
33
]
.
These parameters could also depend on each other.

Appendix B
Consideration of Metallicity Dependence

Variations in
{\alpha_{\rm CO}}
are often attributed to a dependence on metallicity.
Recent observational studies have suggested
{\alpha_{\rm CO}}
prescriptions, i.e.,
{\alpha_{\rm CO}}

\propto\left(\frac{Z}{Z_{\odot}}\right)^{\eta}
, with a range of power-law indices
[
64
,
35
,
1
,
49
,
\eta
= -0.7 to -3.4; ]
.
While there are not many alternative ways to constrain
{\alpha_{\rm CO}}
especially in low-metallicity environments, many of these studies adopt assumptions that could be questioned (e.g., the universality of the star formation process/Kennicutt-Schmidt relation independent of environments, the accuracy of chemical models that assume geometry, density, and temperature of the gas).
There are also theoretical works that analyzed simulations to understand the metallicity-dependence of
{\alpha_{\rm CO}}

[
30
,
34
]
,
which reproduced the observed
{\alpha_{\rm CO}}
-metallicity dependences with power-law indices of -0.80 and -0.70.
Hence, instead of adopting a single relation directly, we explore a range of indices encompassing suggested
{\alpha_{\rm CO}}-Z
relations in the literature. For this, we adopt the power-law model
{\alpha_{\rm CO}}
=
\alpha_{{\rm{MW}}}\left(\frac{Z}{Z_{\odot}}\right)^{\eta}
with
\eta=0.0,-1.0,-2.0
and
-3.0
. We use the metallicity gradient for M83 and calibrations described in section
VI.2.1
. In Table
5
, we show the results from this model as a function of radius. In Figure
10
, we compare them with the
\chi^{2}
results obtained in section
V
. The absolute value of the predicted
{\alpha_{\rm CO}}
depends on the metallicity calibration adopted, but
\eta=

-2.0
or
-3.0
(the steepest slopes among the suggested) with the B07 calibration, may potentially explain the factor of
\sim
2-3 radial variation in
{\alpha_{\rm CO}}
derived in section
V
. For
\eta=

-2.0
or
-3.0
with the B07 calibration to match in absolute value with the
\chi^{2}
results,
\alpha_{{\rm{MW}}}
would have to be
\sim
2 times smaller.

Appendix C
Dickman et al. 1986

Dickman et al. [20]
calculated
{\alpha_{\rm CO}}
as an average over the cloud population across the disk of the MW. In essence, they adopted [1] one empirical relation, the size-velocity dispersion relation
\sigma_{v}\propto R^{\gamma}
, and assumed [2] virial equilibrium
\alpha_{\rm vir}=1
(hence,
M_{\rm{vir}}\propto R\sigma_{v}^{2}
), [3]
L_{\rm{CO}}\propto T\sigma_{v}R^{2}
, and [4] a constant surface brightness temperature
T
for all clouds. From these, they derived

\displaystyle{\alpha_{\rm CO}}=\frac{M_{\rm{vir}}}{L_{\rm{CO}}}\propto\frac{1}{T}R^{\gamma-1},

(C1)

i.e., a dependence of
{\alpha_{\rm CO}}
on cloud size (radius) under constant
T
. With this
{\alpha_{\rm CO}}
-
R
relation, they averaged
{\alpha_{\rm CO}}
over the distribution of
R
in the MW cloud population, and obtained
{\alpha_{\rm CO}}

\sim
4.
We extended this idea, applied it to varying cloud populations, and explained the variations of
{\alpha_{\rm CO}}
with galactic radius in M83.
We used [1] an empirical relation of eq. (
11
) under the assumptions equivalent to [2] and [3] – in our case, we set
\alpha_{\rm vir}
=1 or 2 and used
L_{\rm{CO}}
measured by integrating
T
over a cloud area (equivalent to the
R^{2}
term) and velocity (
\sigma_{v}
).
By starting from eq. (
11
) instead of eq. (
C1
), we did not need to adopt the assumption [4] of constant
T
.

As a further note, from assumptions [1]-[3], the volume density of cloud is
\rho\propto M_{\rm{vir}}/R^{3}\propto R^{2(\gamma-1)}
. With eq. (
C1
), we can derive

{\alpha_{\rm CO}}\propto\frac{\rho^{1/2}}{T}

(C2)

independent of
\gamma
.
