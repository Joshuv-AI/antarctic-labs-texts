Abstract
. In the Bidder Selection Problem (BSP) there is a large pool of n n potential advertisers competing for ad slots on the user’s web page. Due to strict computational restrictions, the advertising platform can run a proper auction only for a fraction k < n k<n of advertisers. We consider the basic optimization problem underlying BSP: given n n independent prior distributions, how to efficiently find a subset of k k with the objective of either maximizing expected social welfare or revenue of the platform. We study BSP in the classic multi-winner model of position auctions for welfare and revenue objectives using the optimal (respectively, VCG mechanism, or Myerson’s auction) format for the selected set of bidders. This is a natural generalization of the fundamental problem of selecting k k out of n n random variables in a way that the expected highest value is maximized. Previous PTAS results ([Chen, Hu, Li, Li, Liu, Lu, NIPS 2016], [Mehta, Nadav, Psomas, Rubinstein, NIPS 2020], [Segev and Singla, EC 2021]) for BSP optimization were only known for single-item auctions and in case of [Segev and Singla 2021] for l l -unit auctions. More importantly, all of these PTASes were computational complexity results with impractically large running times, which defeats the purpose of using these algorithms under severe computational constraints. We propose a novel Poisson relaxation of BSP for position auctions that immediately implies that 1) BSP is polynomial-time solvable up to a vanishingly small error as the problem size k k grows; 2) there is a PTAS for position auctions after combining our relaxation with the trivial brute force algorithm. Unlike all previous PTASes, we implemented our algorithm and did extensive numerical experiments on practically relevant input sizes. First, our experiments corroborate the previous experimental findings of Mehta et al. that a few simple heuristics used in practice (e.g., Greedy for general submodular maximization) perform surprisingly well in terms of approximation factor. Furthermore, our algorithm outperforms Greedy both in running time and approximation on medium and large-sized instances, i.e., its running time scales better with the instance size.

1.1.
Our Results

We propose a novel relaxation of the BSP for a more general position auction environment, which we call Poisson (or Poisson-Chernoff) relaxation.
It has the following theoretical guarantees.

(1)

The relaxation is a continuous maximization problem with a concave objective that can be solved in time polynomial in
n
and
k
. In fact, the objective of this relaxation is a nicely structured algebraic function that lends itself to efficient convex minimization solvers.

(2)

With small adjustments (summarized in
Algorithm

1
), the relaxed objective
converges
at the rate
1-O(k^{-1/4})
to the actual social welfare of fractional BSP as the problem size
k
grows (
Theorem

4.4
). The standard rounding of this fractional solution suffers only a small loss of
O(k^{-1/2})
, yielding
\bigl(1-O(k^{-1/4})\bigr)
-approximation (
Theorem

4.5
) for the integral BSP.

(3)

For the special case of the single-item auction, our algorithm achieves
1-O\bigl(\hskip-1.00006pt\sqrt{\ln k/k}\bigr)
approximation (see
Appendix

C
).

These results have immediate theoretical implications and can be implemented in practice unlike all previous PTAS algorithms, as our approach has far superior running time to the point where it outperforms some of the existing heuristics used in practice, such as the Greedy algorithm for general submodular maximization. Furthermore, our results bring new theoretical insight for BSP: the BSP converges to a polynomial-time solvable optimization problem as the problem size

Relevance in Practice.

Our relaxation is a white-box approach that can be easily adapted to different scenarios. E.g., if some bidders have to be included in the final solution (which is often the case in industry because of contract obligations), then our approach gives the same approximation with these additional constraints

5

In fact, the approximation guarantee holds not only for the global objective, but also for the surplus objective, i.e., the additional gain to welfare from the non-fixed bidders.

Also, depending on the type of problem instances, a few steps of our algorithm can be removed or simplified to fit the specific domain, which results in simpler and more efficient solutions.

Furthermore, unlike the case with all previous PTASes, we actually implemented our algorithm and did numerical experiments

In the context of BSP any reasonable heuristic is preferable to a complex and slow algorithm with good approximation efficiency guarantees.

Preliminaries

A set of

The seminal VCG mechanism (a second-price auction in the case of single-item auction) is an example of incentive compatible mechanism that also maximizes

We study auctions in the Bayesian setting, where we assume that bidder values are drawn independently from known prior distributions

The auction designer is usually concerned about two objectives: the expected

\textup{{SW}}=\operatorname{\mathbf{E}}_{\mathbf{v}\sim\mathbf{\mathbf{D}}}\mathchoice{\left[\textup{{SW}}(\mathbf{a}(\mathbf{v}),\mathbf{v})\right]}{[\textup{{SW}}(\mathbf{a}(\mathbf{v}),\mathbf{v})]}{[\textup{{SW}}(\mathbf{a}(\mathbf{v}),\mathbf{v})]}{[\textup{{SW}}(\mathbf{a}(\mathbf{v}),\mathbf{v})]}

4.
Our Algorithm

Poisson approximation
is the central idea of this paper. We use the Poisson Objective to approximate Bernoulli objective terms.

Definition 4.1 (Poisson Objective).

For
\mathbf{q}\in[0,1]^{n}
and
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
, the
Poisson objective term
is a function
\textup{H}_{\textup{pois}}(\mathbf{q},\ell)
given by

\displaystyle\textup{H}_{\textup{pois}}(\mathbf{q},\ell)

\displaystyle\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=\mathrel{\mkern-3.0mu}=}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbf{E}}\limits_{\mathbf{y}\sim\textup{Pois}(\mathbf{q})}\left[\min\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i},\,\ell\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbf{E}}\limits_{Y\sim\textup{Pois}(\sum_{i=1}^{n}q_{i})}\mathchoice{\left[\min\left(Y,\,\ell\right)\right]}{[\min\left(Y,\,\ell\right)]}{[\min\left(Y,\,\ell\right)]}{[\min\left(Y,\,\ell\right)]}.

Note that the latter equality is an important property of Poisson distribution: the sum of independent Poisson random variables
y_{i}\sim\textup{Pois}(q_{i})
follows the Poisson distribution
\textup{Pois}(\sum_{i=1}^{n}q_{i})
. Another crucial property is the concavity of Poisson approximation.

Claim 4.2 (Concavity of Poisson).

\textup{H}_{\textup{pois}}(\mathbf{q},\ell)
is a concave function in
\mathbf{q}\in[0,1]^{n}
for
\forall\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}
.

Proof.

Let
\lambda=\sum_{i=1}^{n}q_{i}
. Notice that
\textup{H}_{\textup{pois}}(\mathbf{q},\ell)
only depends on
\lambda
, which is linear in
\mathbf{q}
. We use
\textup{H}_{\textup{pois}}(\lambda)
to represent this function. Then, we only need to prove that
\textup{H}_{\textup{pois}}(\lambda)
is concave in
\lambda
.
Rewrite

\textup{H}_{\textup{pois}}(\lambda)=\ell-\sum_{j=0}^{\ell-1}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbf{Pr}}\limits_{Y\sim\textup{Pois}(\lambda)}\mathchoice{\left[Y=j\right]}{[Y=j]}{[Y=j]}{[Y=j]}\cdot(\ell-j)=\ell-\sum_{j=0}^{\ell-1}\frac{\lambda^{j}}{j!}e^{-\lambda}\cdot(\ell-j).

By a straightforward differentiation of the partial series we get

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\lambda}\textup{H}_{\textup{pois}}=\sum_{j=0}^{\ell-1}\frac{\lambda^{j}}{j!}e^{-\lambda},\quad\quad\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}\lambda^{2}}\textup{H}_{\textup{pois}}=-e^{-\lambda}\frac{\lambda^{\ell-1}}{(\ell-1)!}<0.

Therefore,
\textup{H}_{\textup{pois}}(\mathbf{q},\ell)
is concave in
\lambda
, and thus concave in
\mathbf{q}
.
∎

6.
Concluding Remarks

In this paper, we studied a more general setting of position auction than all previous work on the Bidder Selection Problem. We proposed a new relaxation that can be solved in time polynomial in
n
and
k
, and the polynomial is rather small. The proposed Poisson approximation approach is much simpler than previous PTAS
complexity results
for
k
-MAX or non-adaptive probing, and it can be implemented in practice. We also did extensive numerical experiments on inputs with practically relevant sizes and observed that our algorithm outperforms some commonly used heuristics, such as Greedy for general submodular maximization.
Furthermore, we showed that the Poisson approximation approach also yields good theoretical guarantees. Namely, that BSP becomes solvable in polynomial time for any fixed
\varepsilon
, when the problem size
k
grows. Our algorithm is the first one with a nearly perfect efficiency guarantee of PTAS, that is relevant in the application domain of the BSP and can be used by a company. Indeed, all previous PTASes had enormous running times and high implementation complexities that made them completely irrelevant to the problem, which was motivated by getting a speedup of
n/k
magnitude.

A natural next step would be to consider BSP of various auction environments under richer sets of feasibility constraints such as matroid, matching, and intersection of matroids. Another interesting direction is to identify conditions where it is possible to efficiently optimize the revenue of BSP for VCG/GSP auction formats.

Appendix A
Poisson and Chernoff Approximation of Bernoulli Objective

In this section, we present approximation guarantees for Bernoulli objective term by the Poisson and Chernoff objectives.
Poisson approximation is the central idea of this paper. It is defined in
Section

4
as:

\textup{H}_{\textup{pois}}(\mathbf{q},\ell)\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=\mathrel{\mkern-3.0mu}=}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbf{E}}\limits_{Y\sim\textup{Pois}(\sum_{i=1}^{n}q_{i})}\mathchoice{\left[\min\left(Y,\,\ell\right)\right]}{[\min\left(Y,\,\ell\right)]}{[\min\left(Y,\,\ell\right)]}{[\min\left(Y,\,\ell\right)]}.

We also
will solve a special case of fractional Bidder Selection Problem for
\ell
-unit auction to demonstrate how Poisson approximation can be useful. In particular, we will assume that each bidder’s value has only
\leq\delta
probability to be nonzero. Our main goal here will be to illustrate our approach and analysis ideas rather than to derive independent results for this special case.

Approximation Guarantees.

There are many known Poisson approximation results (see, e.g., a survey
(
Novak 2019
)
) for the sum of independent Bernoulli random variables, e.g., in total variation, earth mover’s, uniform (a.k.a. Kolmogorov) distances. These are typically absolute approximation guarantees, while we need relative approximations similar to Chernoff approximations from
Lemma

A.3
. As our goal is to handle small probability tail events, we assume that each bidder’s value
{v_{i}}
has only a small probability
\delta
to be greater than zero, i.e.,
\forall i\in[n],\;\operatorname{\mathbf{Pr}}\mathchoice{\left[{v_{i}}\!>\!0\right]}{[{v_{i}}\!>\!0]}{[{v_{i}}\!>\!0]}{[{v_{i}}\!>\!0]}\leq\delta
. The following Poisson absolute approximation result will be useful to us:

Lemma A.1 (
(
Daley and Vere-Jones 2008
, Lemma 11.3.V, p
.
162)
).

Let
(z_{i}\sim\textup{Ber}(q_{i}))_{i=1}^{n}
be
n
independent Bernoulli random variables with
q_{i}\leq\delta
for
\forall i\in[n]
. Let
Z\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=\mathrel{\mkern-3.0mu}=}\sum_{i=1}^{n}z_{i}
and
Y\sim\textup{Pois}(\lambda)
, where
\lambda\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=\mathrel{\mkern-3.0mu}=}\sum_{i=1}^{n}q_{i}
.
Then

(10)

\displaystyle\textup{(total variation distance)}\quad\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\left\lvert\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[Z=j]-\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[Y=j]\right\rvert\leq\delta.

With
Lemma

A.1
we can derive relative approximations:

Lemma A.2.

Suppose
\mathbf{q}\in[0,\delta]^{n}
and
\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Then

(a)

\displaystyle\left\lvert\textup{H}_{\textup{ber}}(\mathbf{q},\ell)-\textup{H}_{\textup{pois}}(\mathbf{q},\ell)\right\rvert\leq 17.5\cdot\delta\cdot\textup{H}_{\textup{ber}}(\mathbf{q},\ell)

\displaystyle(\forall\delta,\,\ell),

(b)

\displaystyle 0\leq\textup{H}_{\textup{ber}}(\mathbf{q},1)-\textup{H}_{\textup{pois}}(\mathbf{q},1)\leq\delta\cdot\textup{H}_{\textup{ber}}(\mathbf{q},1)

\displaystyle(\ell=1).

Proof.

(a).
Let
z_{i}\sim\textup{Ber}(q_{i})
be Bernoulli random variables and
Z=\sum_{i=1}^{n}z_{i}
be their sum. Then we can rewrite

\textup{H}_{\textup{ber}}(\mathbf{q},\ell)=\operatorname{\mathbf{E}}\mathchoice{\left[\min(Z,\ell)\right]}{[\min(Z,\ell)]}{[\min(Z,\ell)]}{[\min(Z,\ell)]}=\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[Z\geq j].

Also let
y_{i}\sim\textup{Pois}(q_{i})
and define
Y=\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}
. Clearly,
Y\sim\textup{Pois}(\lambda)
for
\lambda=\sum_{i=1}^{n}q_{i}
. Then

\textup{H}_{\textup{pois}}(\mathbf{q},\ell)=\operatorname{\mathbf{E}}\mathchoice{\left[\min(Y,\ell)\right]}{[\min(Y,\ell)]}{[\min(Y,\ell)]}{[\min(Y,\ell)]}=\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[Y\geq j].

Hence, we have

\left\lvert\textup{H}_{\textup{ber}}(\mathbf{q},\ell)-\textup{H}_{\textup{pois}}(\mathbf{q},\ell)\right\rvert\leq\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}\left\lvert\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[Z\geq j]-\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[Y\geq j]\right\rvert.

The RHS is similar to the earth mover’s distance between the sum of Bernoulli and Poisson random variables (the difference is that the summation instead of
+\infty
goes only up to
\ell
). Next, we shall prove the following inequality:

\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}\left\lvert\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[Z\geq j]-\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[Y\geq j]\right\rvert\leq 2.5\,\delta\cdot\min(\lambda,\ell),

which together with
Lemma

A.3
(a) immediately implies the desired result:

\left\lvert\textup{H}_{\textup{ber}}(\mathbf{q},\ell)-\textup{H}_{\textup{pois}}(\mathbf{q},\ell)\right\rvert\leq 2.5\,\delta\cdot\min(\lambda,\ell)\leq 2.5\,\delta\cdot 7\,\textup{H}_{\textup{ber}}(\mathbf{q},\ell)=17.5\,\delta\cdot\textup{H}_{\textup{ber}}(\mathbf{q},\ell).

We consider two cases. First, when
\lambda\geq\ell
. Then for any
j\in{\mathbb{N}}^{+}
we have by (
10
)

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}\left\lvert\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[Z\geq j]-\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[Y\geq j]\right\rvert\leq\ell\cdot\sum_{j^{\prime}=0}^{\infty}\left\lvert\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[Z=j^{\prime}]-\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[Y=j^{\prime}]\right\rvert\leq\ell\cdot\delta=\delta\cdot\min(\lambda,\ell).

Second, when
\min(\lambda,\,\ell)=\lambda<\ell

d_{\textup{G}}(Z,Y)=\sum_{j=0}^{+\infty}\left\lvert\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[Z\geq j]-\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[Y\geq j]\right\rvert

\displaystyle d_{\textup{G}}(Z,Y)\leq\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbf{E}}\limits_{(z_{i},y_{i})_{i=1}^{n}}\mathchoice{\left[|Z-Y|\right]}{[|Z-Y|]}{[|Z-Y|]}{[|Z-Y|]}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbf{E}}\limits_{(z_{i},y_{i})}\mathchoice{\left[\left\lvert z_{i}-y_{i}\right\rvert\right]}{[\left\lvert z_{i}-y_{i}\right\rvert]}{[\left\lvert z_{i}-y_{i}\right\rvert]}{[\left\lvert z_{i}-y_{i}\right\rvert]}.

\operatorname{\mathbf{E}}\mathchoice{\left[|z_{i}-y_{i}|\right]}{[|z_{i}-y_{i}|]}{[|z_{i}-y_{i}|]}{[|z_{i}-y_{i}|]}

\displaystyle\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbf{E}}\limits_{(z_{i},y_{i})}\mathchoice{\left[\left\lvert z_{i}-y_{i}\right\rvert\right]}{[\left\lvert z_{i}-y_{i}\right\rvert]}{[\left\lvert z_{i}-y_{i}\right\rvert]}{[\left\lvert z_{i}-y_{i}\right\rvert]}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{q_{i}^{2}}{2}+q_{i}^{2}\sum_{j=2}^{+\infty}2^{-(j-1)}(j-1)\leq 2.5\,q_{i}^{2},

(b). As
\ell=1

\textup{H}_{\textup{pois}}(\mathbf{q},1)=f(\lambda)

\mathbf{q}\in[0,1]^{n},\,\ell\in\mathbb{N}^{+}

(b)

(e)

M\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=\mathrel{\mkern-3.0mu}=}[n]\setminus S_{\textup{fix}}
let the Poisson approximation
\widetilde{\textup{{SW}}}(\mathbf{x}_{\scriptscriptstyle M})
be

\qquad\widetilde{\textup{{SW}}}(\mathbf{x}_{\scriptscriptstyle M})\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=\mathrel{\mkern-3.0mu}=}\left(1-\frac{1}{k}\right)\eta+\int_{\eta}^{+\infty}\widetilde{\textup{H}}_{\textup{pois}}\!\left(\mathbf{q}(\mathbf{x}_{\scriptscriptstyle M},\tau)\right)\,\mathrm{d}\tau,\qquad\text{where}

\widetilde{\textup{H}}_{\textup{pois}}\!\left(\mathbf{q}_{\scriptscriptstyle M}\right)\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=\mathrel{\mkern-3.0mu}=}r_{\tau}+(1-r_{\tau})\cdot\textup{H}_{\textup{pois}}\!\left(\mathbf{q}_{\scriptscriptstyle M},\,\ell=1\right),\quad\text{and}\quad r_{\tau}\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=\mathrel{\mkern-3.0mu}=}\operatornamewithlimits{\mathbf{Pr}}\limits_{\mathbf{v}\sim\mathbf{\mathbf{D}}}\mathchoice{\left[\exists i\in S_{\textup{fix}}:{v_{i}}\geq\tau\right]}{[\exists i\in S_{\textup{fix}}:{v_{i}}\geq\tau]}{[\exists i\in S_{\textup{fix}}:{v_{i}}\geq\tau]}{[\exists i\in S_{\textup{fix}}:{v_{i}}\geq\tau]}.

(3)

Return
\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}^{*}=(\mathbf{1}_{S_{\textup{fix}}},\,\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}_{\scriptscriptstyle M}^{*})
, where
\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}_{\scriptscriptstyle M}^{*}
is the solution to the concave program in
\mathbf{x}_{\scriptscriptstyle M}
:

\begin{array}[]{ll}\text{Maximize}&\widetilde{\textup{{SW}}}(\mathbf{x}_{\scriptscriptstyle M})\\
\text{Subject To}&\sum_{i\in M}{x_{i}}\leq k-\varepsilon\cdot k,\quad{x_{i}}\in[0,1]\quad\forall i\in M.\end{array}

In the algorithm, we ignore thresholds
\tau\in[0,\eta]
, as by taking
S_{\textup{fix}}
we have already achieved high success probability of at least
1-\frac{1}{k}
by
C.1
. For the high range thresholds
\tau>\eta
, we first observe that as the result of fixing set
S_{\textup{fix}}
, the probability that there is a bidder with value greater than the threshold
\tau
becomes

\!\!\textup{H}_{\textup{ber}}(\mathbf{q},1)=\operatorname{\mathbf{Pr}}\mathchoice{\left[\exists i\in[n]:{v_{i}}\geq\tau\right]}{[\exists i\in[n]:{v_{i}}\geq\tau]}{[\exists i\in[n]:{v_{i}}\geq\tau]}{[\exists i\in[n]:{v_{i}}\geq\tau]}=r_{\tau}+\left(1-r_{\tau}\right)\cdot\operatorname{\mathbf{Pr}}\mathchoice{\left[\exists i\in M:{v_{i}}\geq\tau\right]}{[\exists i\in M:{v_{i}}\geq\tau]}{[\exists i\in M:{v_{i}}\geq\tau]}{[\exists i\in M:{v_{i}}\geq\tau]},

where
r_{\tau}
is a constant that we can easily compute.
Hence, we respectively adjust the Poisson approximation term
\widetilde{\textup{H}}_{\textup{pois}}(\mathbf{q}_{\scriptscriptstyle M})
in the algorithm according to (
2
) (we slightly abuse notations by writing
\textup{H}_{\textup{pois}}(\mathbf{q}_{\scriptscriptstyle M})
instead of
\textup{H}_{\textup{pois}}(\mathbf{q})
: for the coordinates
i\notin M
we let
q_{i}=0
).

Theorem C.2.

Algorithm

4
for single-item auction works in polynomial time and is a
(1-2\varepsilon)
-approximation, i.e., a
\bigl(1-O\big(\sqrt{\ln k/k}\big)\bigr)
-approximation to the fractional BSP.

Proof.

We first verify that
Algorithm

4
is polynomial. Note that the step (1) works in polynomial time by

C.1
. For each
\tau
in the support of
{D_{i}}
we calculate in polynomial time the constants
r_{\tau}\in[0,1]
. Both
\widetilde{\textup{H}}_{\textup{pois}}(\mathbf{q}_{\scriptscriptstyle M})
for each
\mathbf{x}_{\scriptscriptstyle M}
and
\tau
in the support and
\widetilde{\textup{{SW}}}(\mathbf{x}_{\scriptscriptstyle M})
for each
\mathbf{x}_{\scriptscriptstyle M}
can be computed in polynomial time.
Moreover, all first and second order partial derivatives of
\widetilde{\textup{{SW}}}(\mathbf{x}_{\scriptscriptstyle M})
can be computed in the same way as the integral of respective derivatives of
\widetilde{\textup{H}}_{\textup{pois}}(\mathbf{x}_{\scriptscriptstyle M})
. Furthermore, it is easy to see that
\widetilde{\textup{{SW}}}(\mathbf{x}_{\scriptscriptstyle M})
is a concave function in
\mathbf{x}_{\scriptscriptstyle M}
, since it is a positive linear combination of constant terms (such as
(1-1/k)\eta
and
r_{\tau}
) and concave functions
\textup{H}_{\textup{pois}}(\mathbf{x}_{\scriptscriptstyle M})
according to
4.2
. Hence, we can find the optimal solution
\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}_{\scriptscriptstyle M}^{*}
in polynomial time using standard concave (first or second order) maximization methods.

To prove an approximation guarantee of
1-2\varepsilon
for
Algorithm

4
, we first derive the following approximations of
\textup{{SW}}(\mathbf{x}_{\scriptscriptstyle M},\mathbf{1}_{S_{\textup{fix}}})
by
\widetilde{\textup{{SW}}}(\mathbf{x}_{\scriptscriptstyle M})
similar to (
11
) (but in a special case of
\ell=1
).

Lemma C.3.

\forall\mathbf{x}_{\scriptscriptstyle M}\in[0,1]^{|M|}
,
0\leq\textup{{SW}}(\mathbf{x}_{\scriptscriptstyle M},\mathbf{1}_{S_{\textup{fix}}})-\widetilde{\textup{{SW}}}(\mathbf{x}_{\scriptscriptstyle M})\leq\varepsilon\cdot\textup{{SW}}(\mathbf{x}_{\scriptscriptstyle M},\mathbf{1}_{S_{\textup{fix}}}).

Proof.

Recall that by (
3
) the Social Welfare
\textup{{SW}}(\mathbf{x})
for
\mathbf{x}=(\mathbf{x}_{\scriptscriptstyle M},\mathbf{1}_{S_{\textup{fix}}})
and
\ell=1
is

\textup{{SW}}(\mathbf{x})=\int_{0}^{\eta}\textup{H}_{\textup{ber}}(\mathbf{q}(\mathbf{x},\tau))\,\mathrm{d}\tau+\int_{\eta}^{+\infty}\textup{H}_{\textup{ber}}(\mathbf{q}(\mathbf{x},\tau))\,\mathrm{d}\tau.

For the low range
\tau\in[0,\eta]
we have
\textup{H}_{\textup{ber}}(\mathbf{q}(\mathbf{x},\tau))\in[1-\frac{1}{k},\,1]
due to the choice of
S_{\textup{fix}}
in
C.1
. It is well approximated by the respective term
\left(1-\frac{1}{k}\right)\eta
in (
2
).

For the high range
\tau>\eta
, we apply
Lemma

A.2
(b) with
\delta=\varepsilon
and get the following bound:

\displaystyle\textup{H}_{\textup{ber}}(\mathbf{q})-\widetilde{\textup{H}}_{\textup{pois}}(\mathbf{q})={}

\displaystyle(1-r_{\tau})\left(\textup{H}_{\textup{ber}}(\mathbf{q}_{\scriptscriptstyle M})-\textup{H}_{\textup{pois}}(\mathbf{q}_{\scriptscriptstyle M})\right)\leq(1-r_{\tau})\varepsilon\cdot\textup{H}_{\textup{ber}}(\mathbf{q}_{\scriptscriptstyle M})\leq\varepsilon\cdot\textup{H}_{\textup{ber}}(\mathbf{q}).

On the other hand,
\textup{H}_{\textup{ber}}(\mathbf{q})-\widetilde{\textup{H}}_{\textup{pois}}(\mathbf{q})\geq 0
, as
\textup{H}_{\textup{ber}}(\mathbf{q}_{\scriptscriptstyle M})\geq\textup{H}_{\textup{pois}}(\mathbf{q}_{\scriptscriptstyle M})
by
Lemma

A.2
(b). Thus

\displaystyle 0\leq\textup{{SW}}(\mathbf{x})-\widetilde{\textup{{SW}}}(\mathbf{x}_{\scriptscriptstyle M})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\eta}{k}+\varepsilon\cdot\int_{\eta}^{+\infty}\textup{H}_{\textup{ber}}(\mathbf{q}(\mathbf{x},\tau))\,\mathrm{d}\tau\leq\max\left(\frac{1}{k-1},\varepsilon\right)\cdot\textup{{SW}}(\mathbf{x})=\varepsilon\cdot\textup{{SW}}(\mathbf{x}),

where the last equality holds since
\varepsilon\geq\sqrt{\frac{\ln k}{k}}
.
∎

Now we are ready to complete the proof of
Theorem

C.2
. Let
\mathbf{x}^{*}
be the optimal solution to fractional BSP. We consider
\mathbf{x}^{*}_{+}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\geq 0}
defined as
\mathbf{x}^{*}_{+}\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=\mathrel{\mkern-3.0mu}=}(\mathbf{x}_{\scriptscriptstyle M}^{*},\mathbf{1}_{S_{\textup{fix}}})
, so that
\mathbf{x}^{*}_{+}\succeq\mathbf{x}^{*}
. Then, by
Lemma

C.3
for
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{x}^{*}_{+}
we have

\widetilde{\textup{{SW}}}(\mathbf{x}_{\scriptscriptstyle M}^{*})\geq(1-\varepsilon)\cdot\textup{{SW}}(\mathbf{x}^{*}_{+})\geq(1-\varepsilon)\cdot\textup{{SW}}(\mathbf{x}^{*}).

On the other hand, by
Lemma

C.3
for
\mathbf{x}=\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}^{*}
we have

\displaystyle\textup{{SW}}(\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}^{*})\geq\widetilde{\textup{{SW}}}(\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}_{\scriptscriptstyle M}^{*})

\displaystyle\geq\widetilde{\textup{{SW}}}\left(\frac{k-\varepsilon\cdot k}{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{\scriptscriptstyle M}^{*}\right)\geq(1-\varepsilon)\cdot\widetilde{\textup{{SW}}}(\mathbf{x}_{\scriptscriptstyle M}^{*})\geq(1-2\varepsilon)\cdot\textup{{SW}}(\mathbf{x}^{*}),

where the second inequality holds, as
\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}_{\scriptscriptstyle M}^{*}
is the optimal solution to (
3
) and
\frac{k-\varepsilon\cdot k}{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{\scriptscriptstyle M}^{*}
is a feasible solution; the third inequality holds, as
\widetilde{\textup{{SW}}}(\mathbf{x})
is a concave function in
\mathbf{x}
by
4.2
; the last inequality holds, as

\widetilde{\textup{{SW}}}(\mathbf{x}_{\scriptscriptstyle M}^{*})\geq(1-\varepsilon)\cdot\textup{{SW}}(\mathbf{x}^{*})
and
(1-\varepsilon)^{2}\geq 1-2\varepsilon
. This concludes the proof.

D.2.
Notes on the Discretization

Recall that in
Section

5
, we discretized each generated distribution to a common, finite support
\{0\}\cup\{1+\frac{i}{50}\mid i=0,1,\dots,49\}
by moving probability mass on each discretized interval inside
[0,2]
to its left point and by redistributing the mass on
(2,+\infty)
to the discrete points, proportional to their respective probabilities.

We picked this unusual discretization to make instances more challenging for Greedy, as without it Greedy and other heuristics like our algorithm produce solutions with nearly optimal approximation efficiency (over
99\%
for both algorithms). Indeed, two distributions in a well-structured family of log-normal distributions are likely to have strong dominance relation: a distribution
{D_{1}}=\mathrm{Lognormal}(\mu_{1},\sigma_{1}^{2})
is always preferable to
{D_{2}}=\mathrm{Lognormal}(\mu_{2},\sigma_{2}^{2})
, whenever
\mu_{1}\geq\mu_{2}
and
\sigma_{1}\geq\sigma_{2}
. Our choice of discretization was solely based on the comparison between Greedy and Local Search, i.e., after a few empirical trials of different discretizations we simply stopped once the average efficiency of Greedy was less than
99\%
that of the Local Search.
