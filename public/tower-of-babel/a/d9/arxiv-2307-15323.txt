Massive Thirring Model: Inverse Scattering and Soliton Resolution
Cheng He, Jiaqi Liu, Changzheng Qu

Abstract
. In this paper the long-time dynamics of the massive Thirring model is investigated. Firstly the nonlinear steepest descent method for Riemann-Hilbert problem is explored to obtain the soliton resolution of the solutions to the massive Thirring model whose initial data belong to some weighted-Sobolev spaces. Secondly, the asymptotic stability of multi-solitons follow as a corollary. The main difficulty in studying the massive Thirring model through inverse scattering is that the corresponding Lax pair has singularities at the origin and infinity. We overcome this difficulty by making use of two transforms that separate the singularities.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
General introduction

In this paper we apply the inverse scattering transform (IST) to study the long time asymptotics of the massive Thirring model (MTM):

(1.1)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}i\left(u_{t}+u_{x}\right)+v+|v|^{2}u=0\\
i\left(v_{t}-v_{x}\right)+u+|u|^{2}v=0\end{array}\right.

subject to an initial condition

(1.2)

(u(x,0),v(x,0)):=(u_{0},v_{0})\in\mathcal{I}\times\mathcal{I},

where

(1.3)

\mathcal{I}:=\left\{f:f,f^{\prime},f^{\prime\prime}\in L^{2,1}(\mathbb{R})\right\}

with the space defined by

(1.4)

\|f\|_{L^{2,1}}:=\|(1+|\cdot|)f(\cdot)\|_{L^{2}}.

The massive Thirring model was first introduced by W. Thirring in
[
26
]
. Such a model arises as a modification of a nonlinear Schrödinger equation to relativistic velocities. We first point out that (
1.1
) is one type of the
nonlinear Dirac equation
. For a general exposition, see
[
14
]
. For the global well-posedness of the MTM through PDE methods, we refer the reader to the work of Candy
[
5
]
. Also, in
[
21
]
, Pelinovsky and Saalmann established well-posedness by studying the bijective property of the direct and inverse scattering transform. They follow the work of Zhou
[
28
]
, where the author developed a rigorous analysis of the direct and inverse scattering transform of the AKNS system
for a class of initial conditions
u_{0}(x)=u(x,t=0)
belonging to the space
H^{i,j}(\mathbb{R})
.
Here,
H^{i,j}(\mathbb{R})
denotes
the completion of
C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
in the norm

(1.5)

\left\|{u}\right\|_{H^{i,j}(\mathbb{R})}=\left(\left\|{(1+|x|^{j})u}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\left\|{\frac{\partial^{i}u}{\partial x^{i}}}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

Besides well-posedness, another fundamental question for dispersive PDEs is the long-time asymptotics. In
[
6
]
, Candy and Lindblad calculated the long-time scattering of the solution to (
1.1
) of small initial condition in
Sobolev
space using PDE methods. In the current paper, we shall making use of the completely integrable structure of the MTM first introduced by
[
17
]
.

Using the complete integrability of the modified KdV equation, Deift and Zhou in their seminal work
[
11
]
developed the celebrated nonlinear steepest descent method for oscillatory Riemann-Hilbert problems. Since then, analysis of long-time behavior
of integrable systems have been extensively treated by
many authors. The nonlinear steepest descent method provides a systematic way to
reduce the original RHP
to a canonical model RHP whose solution is calculated in terms of special functions. Dieng and McLaughlin in
[
12
]
(see also an extended version
[
13
]
)later developed a variant of Deift-Zhou method. In their approach
rational approximation of the reflection coefficient is replaced by some
non-analytic extension of the jump matrices off the real axis, which leads to a
\bar{\partial}
-problem to
be solved in some regions of the complex plane.
This method has shown its robustness in its application to other integrable models. Notably, for focussing NLS, this method was
successfully applied to address the soliton resolution in
[
4
]
. Later, in
[
16
]
and
[
8
]
, soliton resolution for the derivative NLS equation and sine-Gordon equation were established respectively. The massive Thirring model admits a family of soliton solutions:

(1.6)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}u_{\lambda}(x,t)=i\delta^{-1}\sin\gamma\operatorname{sech}\left[\alpha(x+ct)-i\frac{\gamma}{2}\right]e^{-i\beta(t+cx)}\\
v_{\lambda}(x,t)=-i\delta\sin\gamma\operatorname{sech}\left[\alpha(x+ct)+i\frac{\gamma}{2}\right]e^{-i\beta(t+cx)}\end{array}\right.

We first mention that the solitons do not exist in MTM for small initial data and the first result of this was obtained in the last section of
[
20
]
. Later in
[
7
]
(see also
[
23
]
), the authors proved
L^{2}
orbital stability of the solitons above, through the use of the auto-Bäcklund transformation. In the current paper, we shall employ the
nonlinear steepest descent
method to to analyze the large
t
asymptotics of
the MTM in weighted Sobolev space given by (
1.3
). Consequentially, we obtain the soliton resolution and multi-soliton asymptotic stability of the MTM.

1.2.
Main results

Theorem 1.1

(Soliton resolution)
.

Let the initial data
(u_{0},v_{0})=(u(x,0),v(x,0))\in\mathcal{I}\times\mathcal{I}

be
generic
in the sense of Definition
2.15
and let
(u(x,t),v(x,t))

be the unique solution to the massive Thirring (
1.1
) obtained by solving the Riemann-Hilbert Problem
2.13
with initial data
(u_{0},v_{0})
. Then the
solution
(u(x,t),v(x,t))
can be written as the superposition of solitons and radiation. More precisely, there exist a non negative
integer
N_{1}
, and two sets of complex parameters

\left\{\lambda_{j}\right\}_{j=1,\ldots N_{1}},\ \left\{\widetilde{c}_{j}\right\}_{j=1,\ldots N_{1}}

such that the solutions
u
and
v
have the following representation

\displaystyle u(x,t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{N_{1}}u^{sol}\left(x,t;\lambda_{j},\widetilde{c}_{j}\right)+u_{r}(x,t)

\displaystyle v(x,t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{N_{1}}v^{sol}\left(x,t;\lambda_{j},\widetilde{c}_{j}\right)+v_{r}(x,t)

where the radiation term
u_{r}
and
v_{r}
decay to
0
and it exhibits the modified scattering inside the light-cone.

Remark 1.2
.

We remark that
u_{r}(x,t)
and
v_{r}(x,t)
are computed explicitly in terms of stationary points and the reflection coefficient. In particualr the error term from
u_{r}(x,t)
and
v_{r}(x,t)
depend on the weighted norm of the initial data
\mathcal{I}
. We would like to point out that strictly inside, near and strictly outside of the line-cone, the error terms depend on
\frac{x}{t}
in different manners.
For the explicit expressions of the radiation terms, see Theorem
9.1
.

Remark 1.3
.

From the inverse scattering point of view, the analysis of the MTM (
1.1
) combines techniques from that of the derivative NLS equation and the sine-Gordon equation in the following sense:

•

the spectral dependence of the linear problem is rational which leads to singularities at both the origin and infinity. We mention that, for the derivative NLS, the singularity is at infinity while the sine-Gordon equation has singularity at the origin. These difficulties are overcome by introducing a pair of transforms that divide the analysis into two different linear problems (see
[
21
]
).

•

the phase function of the reflection coefficient has singularity at the origin which makes the direct scattering map between Sobolev spaces more involved. Here we follow the arguments developed in
[
8
]
to show
the following map:

(1.7)

\mathcal{I}\times\mathcal{I}\ni(u_{0},v_{0})\mapsto r\in H^{1,1}_{0}(\mathbb{R})\subset H^{1}(\mathbb{R}).

which is crucial in determining the long time asymptotics. And this fills in the gap previously left out by
[
25
]
(see
[
25
, p.28]
).

Remark 1.4
.

Compared with
[
24
]
-
[
25
]
in which the author also discussed the long time asymptotics of the MTM using
nonlinear steepest descent
, the current paper is different in the following aspects:

•

we reconstruct both
u(x,t)
and
v(x,t)
from a single Riemann-Hilbert problem.

•

We follow the argument developed in
[
15
]
to show the existence of solutions to the Riemann-Hilbert problem without relying on Darboux transform.

•

Instead of showing the mapping properties of the inverse scattering
\widetilde{r}\mapsto(u(x,t),v(x,t))
as in
[
21
]

we use techniques from pde theory to show global existence of solutions in (
1.3
).

2.
Direct and Inverse scattering

2.1.
The direct scattering

As an integrable system, the massive Thirring model (
1.1
) is the compatibility condition for the following Lax pair:

(2.1)

\begin{array}[]{c}\psi_{x}=L\psi\\
\psi_{t}=A\psi\end{array}

in the sense that

(2.2)

L_{t}-A_{x}+[L,A]=0

where

\displaystyle L

\displaystyle=\frac{i}{4}\left(|u|^{2}-|v|^{2}\right)\sigma_{3}-\frac{i\zeta}{2}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&\bar{v}\\
v&0\end{array}\right)+\frac{i}{2\zeta}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&\bar{u}\\
u&0\end{array}\right)+\frac{i}{4}\left(\zeta^{2}-\frac{1}{\zeta^{2}}\right)\sigma_{3}

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle=-\frac{i}{4}\left(|u|^{2}+|v|^{2}\right)\sigma_{3}-\frac{i\zeta}{2}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&\bar{v}\\
v&0\end{array}\right)-\frac{i}{2\zeta}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&\bar{u}\\
u&0\end{array}\right)+\frac{i}{4}\left(\zeta^{2}+\frac{1}{\zeta^{2}}\right)\sigma_{3}

and
\sigma_{3}
is the third
Pauli
matrix

\sigma_{3}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}{1}&{0}\\
{0}&{-1}\end{array}\right).

We now define the simultaneous solutions (
2\times 2
matrix-valued
Jost
functions)

\psi^{\pm}=\left[\psi_{1}^{(\pm)},\psi_{2}^{(\pm)}\right]

to the Lax pair (
2.1
) in the column-wise form and prescribe the boundary conditions at
\pm\infty
:

\displaystyle\psi_{1}^{(-)}(x;\zeta)

\displaystyle\sim\left(\begin{array}[]{l}1\\
0\end{array}\right)e^{{ix}\left(\zeta^{2}-\zeta^{-2}\right)/4},\quad\psi_{2}^{(-)}(x;\zeta)\sim\left(\begin{array}[]{l}0\\
1\end{array}\right)e^{{-ix}\left(\zeta^{2}-\zeta^{-2}\right)/4}\quad\text{ as }x\rightarrow-\infty

\displaystyle\psi_{1}^{(+)}(x;\zeta)

\displaystyle\sim\left(\begin{array}[]{l}1\\
0\end{array}\right)e^{{ix}\left(\zeta^{2}-\zeta^{-2}\right)/4},\quad\psi_{2}^{(+)}(x;\zeta)\sim\left(\begin{array}[]{c}0\\
1\end{array}\right)e^{-ix\left(\zeta^{2}-\zeta^{-2}\right)/4}\quad\text{ as }x\rightarrow+\infty.

We further normalize the Jost functions at
x=\pm\infty
:

(2.5a)

\displaystyle m_{1}^{(\pm)}(x;\zeta)

\displaystyle=\psi_{1}^{(\pm)}(x;\zeta)e^{-ix\left(\zeta^{2}-\zeta^{-2}\right)/4}

(2.5b)

\displaystyle m_{2}^{(\pm)}(x;\zeta)

\displaystyle=\psi_{2}^{(\pm)}(x;\zeta)e^{ix\left(\zeta^{2}-\zeta^{-2}\right)/4}

such that

(2.6a)

\displaystyle\lim_{x\rightarrow\pm\infty}m_{1}^{(\pm)}(x;\zeta)

\displaystyle=e_{1}=(1,0)^{\textrm{T}}

(2.6b)

\displaystyle\lim_{x\rightarrow\pm\infty}m_{2}^{(\pm)}(x;\zeta)

\displaystyle=e_{2}=(0,1)^{\textrm{T}}.

It is easy to see from (
2.1
) that the normalized Jost functions (
2.5a
)-(
2.5b
) satisfy the following
Volterra
integral equations:

(2.7)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} m^{(\pm)}(x;\zeta):&=\left[m_{1}^{(\pm)}(x;\zeta),m_{2}^{(\pm)}(x;\zeta)\right]\\
&=I+\int^{x}_{\pm\infty}e^{\frac{i}{4}(\zeta^{2}-\zeta^{-2})(x-y)\ad\sigma_{3}}\left[Q(u,v;\zeta)m^{(\pm)}(y;\zeta)\right]dy\end{aligned}

where

(2.8)

Q(u,v;\zeta)=\frac{i}{4}\left(|u|^{2}-|v|^{2}\right)\sigma_{3}-\frac{i\zeta}{2}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&\bar{v}\\
v&0\end{array}\right)+\frac{i}{2\zeta}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&\bar{u}\\
u&0\end{array}\right)

and
I
is the
2\times 2
identity matrix.
The following lemma follows from standard
Volterra
theory which can be found in
[
3
]
:

Lemma 2.1
.

For every
\zeta\in\left(\mathbb{R}\cup i\mathbb{R}\backslash\{0\}\right)
, if
Q(u,v;\zeta)\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
, then Volterra integral equations (
2.7
) admits unique solutions
m^{(\pm)}_{1}(\cdot,\zeta)
and
m^{(\pm)}_{2}(\cdot,\zeta)
in
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
.

For every
\zeta\in(\mathbb{R}\cup i\mathbb{R})\backslash\{0\}
, by standard ODE theory, there is a matrix
S(\zeta)
, the scattering matrix, with

(2.9)

\left[\psi^{(+)}_{1},\psi^{(+)}_{2}\right]=\left[\psi_{1}^{(-)},\psi_{2}^{(-)}\right]S(\zeta).

The matrix

takes the form

(2.10)

S(\zeta)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}a(\zeta)&{b}(\zeta)\\
\breve{b}(\zeta)&\breve{a}(\zeta)\end{array}\right)

and the determinant relation gives

(2.11)

a(\zeta)\breve{a}(\zeta)-b(\zeta)\breve{b}(\zeta)=1.

By uniqueness we have

(2.12)

\psi_{11}^{(\pm)}(\zeta)=\overline{\psi_{22}^{(\pm)}(\bar{\zeta})},\quad\psi_{12}^{(\pm)}(\zeta)=-\overline{\psi_{21}^{(\pm)}(\bar{\zeta})}.

This leads to the symmetry relation of the entries of
S(\zeta)
:

(2.13)

\breve{a}(\zeta)=\overline{a(\bar{\zeta})},\quad\breve{b}(\zeta)=-\overline{b(\bar{\zeta})}

and

(2.14)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}a(\zeta)=\text{det}\left[\psi^{(+)}_{1},\psi^{(-)}_{2}\right]\\
\\
{{\breve{b}(\zeta)}}=\text{det}\left[\psi^{(-)}_{1},\psi^{(+)}_{1}\right].\end{array}\right.

For
\zeta\in(\mathbb{R}\cup i\mathbb{R})\backslash\{0\}
, the determinant of
S(\zeta)
is given by

(2.15)

a(\zeta)\overline{a(\bar{\zeta})}+b(\zeta)\overline{b(\bar{\zeta})}=1.

and

2.1.1.
Transformation of the Jost functions for small

Define the transformation matrix by:

(2.16)

T(u;\zeta)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}{1}&{0}\\
{u}&{\zeta^{-1}}\end{array}\right)

and set

Direct computation shows that

{\Psi}_{x}={\mathcal{L}}{\Psi}

(2.18)

and

\displaystyle{Q}_{1}(u,v)

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}-\dfrac{i}{4}\left(|u|^{2}+|v|^{2}\right)&\dfrac{i}{2}\bar{u}\\

\displaystyle\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt{Q}_{2}(u,v)

, we define a new set of normalized Jost functions

\displaystyle={T}(u(x);\zeta)m_{1}^{(\pm)}(x;\zeta)

(2.20b)

\displaystyle{n}_{2}^{(\pm)}(x;\lambda)

\displaystyle=\zeta{T}(u(x);\zeta)m_{2}^{(\pm)}(x;\zeta)

subject to the constant boundary conditions at infinity:

(2.21a)

\displaystyle\lim_{x\rightarrow\pm\infty}{n}_{1}^{(\pm)}(x;\lambda)

\displaystyle=e_{1}=(1,0)^{\text{T}}

(2.21b)

\displaystyle\lim_{x\rightarrow\pm\infty}{n}_{2}^{(\pm)}(x;\lambda)

\displaystyle=e_{2}=(0,1)^{\text{T}}.

\displaystyle{n}^{(\pm)}(x;\lambda):

The following lemma will be useful:

Lemma 2.2
.

[

, then for every

(2.24b)

\displaystyle\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow 0}\frac{{n}_{2}^{(\pm)}(x;\lambda)}{{n}_{2}^{\pm 0}(x)}

\displaystyle=e_{2}=(0,1)^{\text{T}}

where

(2.25a)

\displaystyle{n}_{1}^{\pm 0}(x)

\displaystyle=e^{-\frac{i}{4}\int_{\pm\infty}^{x}\left(|u|^{2}+|v|^{2}\right)dy}

(2.25b)

\displaystyle{n}_{2}^{\pm 0}(x)

2iu_{x}+u|v|^{2}+v\end{array}\right)

\int_{\pm\infty}^{x}\left[\bar{u}\left(u_{x}-\frac{i}{2}u|v|^{2}-\frac{i}{2}v\right)-\frac{i}{2}u\bar{v}\right]dy\end{array}\right).

\left[n^{(+)}_{1}e^{ix(\lambda-\lambda^{-1})/4},n^{(+)}_{2}e^{-ix(\lambda-\lambda^{-1})/4}\right]=\left[n^{(-)}_{1}e^{ix(\lambda-\lambda^{-1})/4},n^{(-)}_{2}e^{-ix(\lambda-\lambda^{-1})/4}\right]S_{g}(\lambda).

It is clear that

(2.29a)

\displaystyle\alpha(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\text{det}\left[n^{(+)}_{1}e^{ix(\lambda-\lambda^{-1})/4},n^{(-)}_{2}e^{-ix(\lambda-\lambda^{-1})/4}\right]=\overline{\breve{\alpha}(\overline{\lambda})}

10.
well-posedness

In the direct scattering process, it requires that initial data to be in
\mathcal{I}\times\mathcal{I}
. Here we provide a sketch of the proof of the global well-posedness in this space for the sake of completeness. First of all, we record the well-posedness of the MTM in the energy space.

Theorem 10.1
.

[
5
]

Let
s\geq 0
and
u_{0},v_{0}\in H^{s}(\mathbb{R})
. There exists a global solution
(u,v)\in C(\mathbb{R},H^{s}(\mathbb{R}))
to (
1.1
) such that the charge is conserved, so

(10.1)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \|u_{0}\|_{L_{x}^{2}}^{2}+\|v_{0}\|_{L_{x}^{2}}^{2}=\|u\|_{L_{x}^{2}}^{2}+\|v\|_{L_{x}^{2}}^{2}\end{aligned}

for every
t\in\mathbb{R}
. Moreover, the solution is unique in a subsequence of
C(\mathbb{R},L^{2}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}))
and we have continuous dependence on initial data.

This theorem can be established by decomposing the solution into an approximately linear component and a component with improved integrability. For detailed proof , see Timothy Candy
[
5
]
. Next, the following theorem will show the global well-posedness of the massive Thirring model in the weighted energy space.

Theorem 10.2
.

Given
(u_{0},v_{0})\in\mathcal{I}\times\mathcal{I}
, there exists a unique solution

(10.2)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} (u,v)\in C\left(\mathbb{R},\mathcal{I}\times\mathcal{I}\right)\end{aligned}

to the massive Thirring model with initial data
(u_{0},v_{0})
.

Proof.

Given
u,v\in H^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, following the result in Timothy Candy
[
5
]
, there exists a unique solution
(u,v)\in C(\mathbb{R},H^{2}(\mathbb{R})\times H^{2}(\mathbb{R}))
.

With unweighted results above, one can pass to the weighted space directly. Given
(u_{0},v_{0})\in\mathcal{I}\times\mathcal{I}
, following notations above, one can find the solution
(u,v)
such that
u,v\in H^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. Our goal is to show that
(u,v)
is in the weighted Sobolev space.
We use the equation and then directing differentiation leads to

(10.3)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \dfrac{d}{dt}E_{2}^{\epsilon}(u,v)\lesssim E_{2}(u,v)+E_{2}^{\epsilon}(u,v)+E_{1}^{\epsilon}(u,v),\end{aligned}

where
E_{2}(u,v)
and
E_{2}^{\epsilon}(u,v)
are defined via

(10.4)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} E_{k}^{\epsilon}(u,v)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(|\partial^{k}_{x}u|^{2}+|\partial^{k}_{x}v|^{2}\right)x^{2}e^{-\epsilon|x|}dx\end{aligned}

and

(10.5)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} E_{k}(u,v)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(|\partial^{k}_{x}u|^{2}+|\partial^{k}_{x}v|^{2}\right)dx.\end{aligned}

If we differentiate the weighed energy directly and integrate by parts, then we will obtain these first order weighted energies, which can not be controlled by
E_{2}^{\epsilon}(u,v)
. Thus, the first step is to show

(10.6)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \dfrac{d}{dt}E_{0}^{\epsilon}(u,v)\lesssim E_{0}(u,v)+E_{0}^{\epsilon}(u,v)\end{aligned}

Indeed, differentiating the weighed energy and integrating by parts, one has

(10.7)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \dfrac{d}{dt}E_{0}^{\epsilon}(u,v)=&\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left((|v|^{2})_{x}-(|u|^{2})_{x}\right)x^{2}e^{-\epsilon|x|}dx\\
=&\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(|u|^{2}-|v|^{2}\right)\partial_{x}(x^{2}e^{-\epsilon|x|})dx\\
\leq&\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(|u_{0}|^{2}+|v_{0}|^{2}\right)dx+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(|u|^{2}+|v|^{2}\right)x^{2}e^{-\epsilon|x|}dx\end{aligned}

Gronwall’s inequality, we get

(10.8)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} E_{0}^{\epsilon}(u,v)\leq C_{T}E_{0}^{\epsilon}(u_{0},v_{0}).\end{aligned}

The same argument above results in

(10.9)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} E_{1}^{\epsilon}(u,v)\leq C_{T}E_{1}^{\epsilon}(u_{0},v_{0}).\end{aligned}

The next step is to estimate the weighted energy
E_{2}^{\epsilon}
. Differentiating the weighed modified norm and integrating by parts, one has

(10.10)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \dfrac{d}{dt}E_{2}^{\epsilon}(u,v)&=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(u_{xx}\bar{u}_{xxt}+u_{xxt}\bar{u}_{xx}+v_{xx}\bar{v}_{xxt}+v_{xxt}\bar{v}_{xx}\right)x^{2}e^{-\epsilon|x|}dx\\
&=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(|u_{xx}|^{2}-|v_{xx}|^{2}\right)\partial_{x}(x^{2}e^{-\epsilon|x|})dx+2\operatorname{Im}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(u_{xx}(|v|^{2}\bar{u})_{xx}+v_{xx}(|u|^{2}\bar{v})_{xx}\right)x^{2}e^{-\epsilon|x|}dx\\
&\lesssim E_{2}^{\epsilon}(u,v)+E_{2}(u,v)+E_{1}^{\epsilon}(u,v)\end{aligned}

Finally, combining (
10.9
) and (
10.10
) together, Gronwall’s inequality gives

(10.11)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} E_{2}^{\epsilon}(u,v)\leq C_{T}E_{2}^{\epsilon}(u_{0},v_{0}).\end{aligned}

Passing
\epsilon
to
0
by the monotone convergence theorem, we get the following result,

(10.12)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(|u_{xx}|^{2}+|v_{xx}|^{2}\right)x^{2}dx\leq C_{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(|u_{0,xx}|^{2}+|v_{0,xx}|^{2}\right)x^{2}dx<\infty,\end{aligned}

for
t\in[-T,T]
for all
T\in\mathbb{R}
.
∎
