Abstract
. We discuss some of our work on averages along polynomial sequences in nilpotent groups of step 2. Our main results include boundedness of associated maximal functions and singular integrals operators, an almost everywhere pointwise convergence theorem for ergodic averages along polynomial sequences, and a nilpotent Waring theorem. Our proofs are based on analytical tools, such as a nilpotent Weyl inequality, and on complex almost-orthogonality arguments that are designed to replace Fourier transform tools, which are not available in the non-commutative nilpotent setting. In particular, we present what we call a nilpotent circle method that allows us to adapt some of the ideas of the classical circle method to the setting of nilpotent groups.



Dedicated to David Jerison, on the occasion of his 70th birthday.

1.
Introduction

The goal of this paper is twofold. We first review some recent results on averages of functions along polynomial sequences in discrete nilpotent Lie groups of step 2, and the main ideas in the proofs. Then we use one of the main ingredients, a nilpotent Weyl inequality, to prove a new theorem on a nilpotent version of the Waring problem.

The natural general setting for our analysis consists of a
discrete nilpotent group
\mathbb{G}
of step
d
, which by definition is assumed to be a discrete, co-compact subgroup of a connected and simply connected nilpotent Lie group
\mathbb{G}^{\#}
of step
d
, and a
polynomial sequence

A:\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{G}
, which is a map satisfying
A(0)=1
and
D^{k_{0}}A\equiv 1
for some
k_{0}\geq 1
. Here
D^{k}
is the
k
-fold differencing operator defined recursively by

D^{0}A(n):=A(n),\qquad D^{k+1}A(n):=D^{k}A(n)^{-1}D^{k}A(n+1),\qquad n\in\mathbb{Z}.

We consider a class of operators defined by taking averages along polynomial sequences in discrete nilpotent groups. As in the continuous case, one can consider discrete maximal operators, which have applications to pointwise ergodic theorems, and discrete Calderón–Zygmund operators.

1.1.
The main theorem

Our main theorem in this paper concerns
L^{p}
boundedness of maximal averages along polynomial sequences in discrete nilpotent groups of step 2,
L^{p}
pointwise ergodic theorems, and
L^{2}
boundedness of singular integrals. More precisely:

Theorem 1.1

(Main result)
.

Assume that
\mathbb{G}
is a discrete nilpotent group
\mathbb{G}
of step
2
and
A:\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{G}
is a polynomial sequence. Then:

(i) (
\ell^{p}
boundedness of maximal averages) Assume
f:\mathbb{G}\to\mathbb{C}
is a function and let

\mathcal{M}f(g):=\sup_{N\geq 0}\frac{1}{2N+1}\sum_{|n|\leq N}|f(A^{-1}(n)\cdot g)|,\qquad g\in\mathbb{G}.

Then, for any
p\in(1,\infty]
,

\|\mathcal{M}f\|_{\ell^{p}(\mathbb{G})}\lesssim_{p}\|f\|_{\ell^{p}(\mathbb{G})}.

(ii) (
L^{p}
pointwise ergodic theorems) Assume
\mathbb{G}
acts by measure-preserving transformations on a
\sigma
-finite measure space
X
,
f\in L^{p}(X)
,
p\in(1,\infty)
, and let

(1.1)

A_{N}f(x):=\frac{1}{2N+1}\sum_{|n|\leq N}f(A^{-1}(n)\cdot x),\qquad x\in X.

Then the sequence
A_{N}f
converges pointwise almost everywhere and in the
L^{p}
norm as
N\to\infty
.

(iii) (
\ell^{2}
boundedness of singular averages) Assume
K:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is a Calderón–Zygmund kernel, i.e. a
C^{1}
function satisfying

(1.2)

\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}[(1+|t|)|K(t)|+(1+|t|)^{2}|K^{\prime}(t)|]\leq 1,\qquad\sup_{N\geq 0}\Big|\int_{-N}^{N}K(t)\,dt\Big|\leq 1.

Assume that
f:\mathbb{G}\to\mathbb{C}
is a (compactly supported) function, and let

Hf(g):=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}K(n)f(A^{-1}(n)\cdot g),\qquad g\in\mathbb{G}.

Then

\|Hf\|_{\ell^{2}(\mathbb{G})}\lesssim\|f\|_{\ell^{2}(\mathbb{G})}.

The theorem follows by combining the main results in
[
36
]
for parts (i) and (ii), and
[
38
]
for part (iii). We discuss now some connections between this theorem and other related results in the literature.

1.1.1.
Continuous Radon transforms

The discrete maximal averages and the discrete singular averages defined in Theorem
1.1
can be thought of as discrete analogues of the continuous Radon transforms, which are averages along suitable curves or surfaces. The theory of continuous Radon transforms has been extensively studied, motivated mainly by problems at the interface of Fourier analysis and geometry of surfaces in Euclidean spaces or nilpotent groups, and is very well understood. This includes
L^{q}
estimates for the full range of exponents
q>1
and multidimensional averages, see for example
[
17
]
,
[
55
]
,
[
19
]
.

1.1.2.
The Furstenberg–Bergelson–Leibman conjecture

Discrete averages, both of the maximal and singular type, have been considered motivated mainly by open problems in ergodic theory. A fundamental problem in ergodic theory is to establish convergence in norm and pointwise almost everywhere for the polynomial ergodic averages as in (
1.1
) as
N\to\infty
for functions
f\in L^{p}(X)
,
1\leq p\leq\infty
.
The problem goes back to at least the early 1930’s with von Neumann’s mean ergodic theorem
[
60
]
and Birkhoff’s pointwise ergodic theorem
[
10
]
and led to profound extensions such as Bourgain’s polynomial pointwise ergodic theorem
[
11
,
12
,
13
]
and Furstenberg’s ergodic proof
[
26
]
of Szemerédi’s theorem
[
58
]
in particular. Furstenberg’s proof was also the starting point of ergodic Ramsey theory, which resulted in many natural generalizations
of Szemerédi’s theorem, including a polynomial Szemerédi theorem of Bergelson and Leibman
[
7
]
.

This motivates the following far reaching conjecture known as the Furstenberg–Bergelson–Leibman conjecture
[
8
, Section 5.5, p. 468]
.

Conjecture 1.2
.

Assume that
d,k\geq 1
are integers,
(X,\mathcal{B}(X),\mu)
is a probability space, and assume that
T_{1},\ldots,T_{d}:X\to X
is a given family of invertible measure-preserving transformations on the space
(X,\mathcal{B}(X),\mu)
that generates a nilpotent group of step
k
. Assume that
m\geq 1
is an integer and

P_{1,1},\ldots,P_{i,j},\ldots,P_{d,m}:\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}
are polynomial maps with integer coefficients such that
P_{i,j}(0)=0
. Then for any
f_{1},\ldots,f_{m}\in L^{\infty}(X)
, the non-conventional multilinear
polynomial averages

(1.3)

\displaystyle A_{N;X,T_{1},\ldots,T_{d}}^{P_{1,1},\ldots,P_{d,m}}(f_{1},\ldots,f_{m})(x)=\frac{1}{2N+1}\sum_{n\in[-N,N]\cap\mathbb{Z}}\prod_{j=1}^{m}f_{j}(T_{1}^{P_{1,j}(n)}\cdots T_{d}^{P_{d,j}(n)}x)

converge for
\mu
-almost every
x\in X
as
N\to\infty
.

Conjecture
1.2
is a major open problem in ergodic theory that was promoted in person by Furstenberg, see
[
1
, p. 6662]
, before being published in
[
8
]
. Our main result Theorem
1.1
(ii) proves this conjecture in the linear case
m=1
, provided that the family of transformations
T_{1},\ldots,T_{d}:X\to X
generates a nilpotent group of step
k=2
.

1.1.3.
Earlier pointwise ergodic theorems

The basic linear case
m=d=k=1
with
P_{1,1}(n)=n
follows from Birkhoff’s original ergodic theorem
[
10
]
. On the other hand, the commutative case
m=d=k=1
with an arbitrary polynomial
P=P_{1,1}
with integer coefficients was a famous open problem of Bellow
[
3
]
and Furstenberg
[
27
]
, solved by Bourgain in his breakthrough papers
[
11
,
12
,
13
]
.

Some particular examples of averages (
1.3
) with
m=1
and polynomial mappings with degree at most two in the step two nilpotent setting were studied in
[
37
,
47
]
.

The multilinear theory
m\geq 2
, in contrast to the linear theory, is widely open even in the commutative case
k=1
. Only a few results in the bilinear
m=2
and commutative
d=k=1
setting are known. Bourgain
[
14
]
proved pointwise convergence when
P_{1,1}(n)=an
and
P_{1,2}(n)=bn
,
a,b\in\mathbb{Z}
. More recently, Krause–Mirek–Tao
[
42
]
established pointwise convergence for the polynomial Furstenberg–Weiss averages
[
28
,
29
]

corresponding to
P_{1,1}(n)=n
and
P_{1,2}(n)=P(n)
,
{\rm deg}\,P\geq 2
.

1.1.4.
Norm convergence

1.3

A breakthrough paper of Walsh

is a nilpotent group
of transformations of a probability space. Prior to this, there was an extensive body of research towards
establishing

norm convergence, including groundbreaking works of Host–Kra

[
32
]
, Ziegler
[
63
]
, Bergelson
[
4
]
,
and Leibman
[
44
]
. See also
[

,
20
,
25
,
33
,
59
]
and the survey articles
[
5
,
6
,
24
]

1.1.5.
Additional remarks

Bergelson and Leibman
[
8
]
showed that convergence may fail if the transformations
T_{1},\ldots,T_{d}
generate a solvable group, so the
nilpotent setting is probably the appropriate setting for Conjecture
1.2
. The restriction

is necessary in the case of nonlinear polynomials as was shown in
[

43
]

If
(X,\mathcal{B}(X),\mu)
is a probability space and the family of measure preserving transformations
(T_{1},\ldots,T_{d_{1}})
is

, then
Theorem

(ii) implies that

(1.4)

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}A_{N;X}^{P_{1},\ldots,P_{d_{1}}}(f)(x)=\int_{X}f(y)d\mu(y)

\mu
-almost everywhere on

. We recall that a family of measure preserving transformations

is called ergodic on

for all

1.2.
The universal step-two group
\mathbb{G}_{0}

The proof of Theorem
1.1
will follow from our second main result, Theorem
1.3
below, for averages on universal nilpotent groups of step two. We start with some definitions. For integers
d\geq 1
, we define

Y_{d}:=\{(l_{1},l_{2})\in\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}:0\leq l_{2}<l_{1}\leq d\}

and the “universal” step-two nilpotent Lie groups
\mathbb{G}_{0}^{\#}=\mathbb{G}_{0}^{\#}(d)

(1.5)

\mathbb{G}_{0}^{\#}:=\{(x_{l_{1}l_{2}})_{(l_{1},l_{2})\in Y_{d}}:x_{l_{1}l_{2}}\in\mathbb{R}\},

with the group multiplication law

(1.6)

[x\cdot y]_{l_{1}l_{2}}:=\begin{cases}x_{l_{1}0}+y_{l_{1}0}&\text{ if }l_{1}\in\{1,\ldots,d\}\text{ and }l_{2}=0,\\
x_{l_{1}l_{2}}+y_{l_{1}l_{2}}+x_{l_{1}0}y_{l_{2}0}&\text{ if }l_{1}\in\{1,\ldots,d\}\text{ and }l_{2}\in\{1,\ldots,l_{1}-1\}.\end{cases}

Alternatively, we can also define the group
\mathbb{G}_{0}^{\#}
as the set of elements

(1.7)

g=(g^{(1)},g^{(2)}),\qquad g^{(1)}=(g_{l_{1}0})_{l_{1}\in\{1,\ldots,d\}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\qquad g^{(2)}=(g_{l_{1}l_{2}})_{(l_{1},l_{2})\in Y^{\prime}_{d}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}},

where
d^{\prime}:=d(d-1)/2
and
Y^{\prime}_{d}:=\{(l_{1},l_{2})\in Y_{d}:\,l_{2}\geq 1\}
. Letting

(1.8)

R_{0}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}\quad\text{ denote the bilinear form }\quad[R_{0}(x,y)]_{l_{1}l_{2}}:=x_{l_{1}0}y_{l_{2}0},

we notice that the product rule in the group
\mathbb{G}_{0}^{\#}
is given by

(1.9)

[g\cdot h]^{(1)}:=g^{(1)}+h^{(1)},\qquad[g\cdot h]^{(2)}:=g^{(2)}+h^{(2)}+R_{0}(g^{(1)},h^{(1)})

if
g=(g^{(1)},g^{(2)})
and
h=(h^{(1)},h^{(2)})
. For any
g=(g^{(1)},g^{(2)})\in\mathbb{G}_{0}^{\#}
, its inverse is given by

g^{-1}=\big(-g^{(1)},-g^{(2)}+R_{0}(g^{(1)},g^{(1)})\big).

The second variable of
g=(g^{(1)},g^{(2)})\in\mathbb{G}_{0}^{\#}
is called the central variable. Based on the product structure (
1.9
) of the group
\mathbb{G}_{0}^{\#}
, it is not difficult to see that
g\cdot h=h\cdot g
for any
g=(g^{(1)},g^{(2)})\in\mathbb{G}_{0}^{\#}
and
h=(0,h^{(2)})\in\mathbb{G}_{0}^{\#}
.

Let
\mathbb{G}_{0}=\mathbb{G}_{0}(d)
denote the discrete subgroup

(1.10)

\displaystyle\mathbb{G}_{0}:=\mathbb{G}_{0}^{\#}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{|Y_{d}|}.

Let
A_{0}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{G}_{0}^{\#}
denote the canonical polynomial map (or the moment curve on
\mathbb{G}_{0}^{\#}
)

(1.11)

[A_{0}(x)]_{l_{1}l_{2}}:=\begin{cases}x^{l_{1}}&\text{ if }l_{2}=0,\\
0&\text{ if }l_{2}\neq 0,\end{cases}

and notice that
A_{0}(\mathbb{Z})\subseteq\mathbb{G}_{0}
. For
x=(x_{l_{1}l_{2}})_{(l_{1},l_{2})\in Y_{d}}\in\mathbb{G}_{0}^{\#}
and
\Lambda\in(0,\infty)
, we define

(1.12)

\Lambda\circ x:=(\Lambda^{l_{1}+l_{2}}x_{l_{1}l_{2}})_{(l_{1},l_{2})\in Y_{d}}\in\mathbb{G}_{0}^{\#}.

Notice that the dilations
\Lambda\circ
are group homomorphisms on the group
\mathbb{G}_{0}
that are compatible with the map
A_{0}
, i.e.
\Lambda\circ A_{0}(x)=A_{0}(\Lambda x)
.

Let
\chi:\mathbb{R}\to[0,1]
be a smooth function supported on the interval
[-2,2]
. Given any real number
N\geq 1
and a function
f:\mathbb{G}_{0}\to\mathbb{C}
, we can define a smoothed average along the moment curve
A_{0}
by the formula

(1.13)

\displaystyle M_{N}^{\chi}(f)(x):=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}N^{-1}\chi(N^{-1}n)f(A_{0}(n)^{-1}\cdot x),\qquad x\in\mathbb{G}_{0}.

The main advantage of working on the group
\mathbb{G}_{0}
with the polynomial map
A_{0}
is the presence of the compatible dilations
\Lambda\circ
defined in (
1.12
), which lead to a natural family of associated balls. This can be efficiently exploited by noting that
M_{N}^{\chi}
is a convolution operator on
\mathbb{G}_{0}
.

The convolution of functions on the group
\mathbb{G}_{0}
is defined by the formula

(1.14)

(f\ast g)(x):=\sum_{y\in\mathbb{G}_{0}}f(y^{-1}\cdot x)g(y)=\sum_{z\in\mathbb{G}_{0}}f(z)g(x\cdot z^{-1}).

Then it is not difficult to see that
M_{N}^{\chi}(f)(x)=f*G_{N}^{\chi}(x)
, where

(1.15)

\displaystyle G_{N}^{\chi}(x):=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}N^{-1}\chi(N^{-1}n)\mathds{1}_{{\{A_{0}(n)\}}}(x),\qquad x\in\mathbb{G}_{0}.

We are now ready to state our second main result.

Theorem 1.3

(Boundedness on
\mathbb{G}_{0}
)
.

Let
\mathbb{G}_{0}=\mathbb{G}_{0}(d)
,
d\geq 1
, be the discrete nilpotent group defined in (
1.10
) and
A_{0}
the polynomial sequence defined in (
1.11
). Then

(i) (Maximal estimates) If
1<p\leq\infty
and
f\in\ell^{p}(\mathbb{G}_{0})
then

(1.16)

\displaystyle\big\|\sup_{N\geq 1}|M_{N}^{\chi}(f)|\big\|_{\ell^{p}(\mathbb{G}_{0})}\lesssim_{p}\|f\|_{\ell^{p}(\mathbb{G}_{0})},

where
M_{N}^{\chi}
is defined as in (
1.13
).

(ii) (Long variational estimates)
If
1<p<\infty
and
\rho>\max\big\{p,\frac{p}{p-1}\big\}
, and
\tau\in(1,2]
then

(1.17)

\displaystyle\big\|V^{\rho}\big(M_{N}^{\chi}(f):N\in\mathbb{D}_{\tau}\big)\big\|_{\ell^{p}(\mathbb{G}_{0})}\lesssim_{p,\rho,\tau}\|f\|_{\ell^{p}(\mathbb{G}_{0})},

where
\mathbb{D}_{\tau}:=\{\tau^{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\}
. See (
1.18
) for the definition of the
\rho
-variation seminorms
V^{\rho}
.

(iii) (Singular integrals) If
K:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is a Calderón–Zygmund kernel as in (
1.2
),
f:\mathbb{G}_{0}\to\mathbb{C}
is a (compactly supported) function, and

H_{0}f(g):=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}K(n)f(A_{0}^{-1}(n)\cdot g),\qquad g\in\mathbb{G}_{0},

then

\|H_{0}f\|_{\ell^{2}(\mathbb{G})}\lesssim\|f\|_{\ell^{2}(\mathbb{G})}.

1.3.
Remarks and overview of the proof

We discuss now some of the main ideas in the proofs of Theorems
1.1
and
1.3
.

1.3.1.
The Calderón transference principle

One can show that Theorem
1.1
is a consequence of Theorem
1.3

upon performing lifting arguments and adapting the Calderón transference principle. Indeed, if
\mathbb{G}^{\#}
is a connected and simply connected nilpotent Lie group of step 2, with Lie algebra
\mathcal{G}
, then one can choose so-called exponential coordinates of the second kind associated to a Malcev basis of the Lie algebra
\mathcal{G}
(see
[
21
]
, Sec. 1.2) in such a way that

\mathbb{G}^{\#}\simeq\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{b_{1}}\times\mathbb{R}^{b_{2}}:(x,y)\cdot(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})=(x+x^{\prime},y+y^{\prime}+R(x,x^{\prime})\},

where
b_{1},b_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
depend on the Lie algebra
\mathcal{G}
and
R:\mathbb{R}^{b_{1}}\times\mathbb{R}^{b_{1}}\to\mathbb{R}^{b_{2}}
is a bilinear form.

Moreover, if
\mathbb{G}\leq\mathbb{G}^{\#}
is a discrete co-compact subgroup, then one can choose the Malcev basis such that the discrete subgroup
\mathbb{G}
is identified with the integer lattice
\mathbb{Z}^{b}=\mathbb{Z}^{b_{1}}\times\mathbb{Z}^{b_{2}}
(see
[
21
]
, Thm. 5.1.6 and Prop. 5.3.2). Recall that
A:\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{G}
is a polynomial sequence satisfying
A(0)=1
. The main point is that one can choose
d
sufficiently large and a group morphism
T:\mathbb{G}_{0}\to\mathbb{G}^{\#}
such that

A(n)=T(A_{0}(n))\qquad\text{ for any }n\in\mathbb{Z}.

Then one can use this group morphism to transfer bounds on operators on the universal group
\mathbb{G}_{0}
to bounds on operators on the group
\mathbb{G}
. Theorem
1.1
is thus a consequence of Theorem
1.3
and our main goal therefore is to prove Theorem
1.3
.

1.3.2.
The variation spaces
V^{\rho}

For any family
(a_{t}:t\in\mathbb{I})
of elements of
\mathbb{C}

indexed by a totally ordered set
\mathbb{I}
, and any exponent

1\leq\rho<\infty
, the
\rho
-variation seminorm is defined by

(1.18)

\displaystyle V^{\rho}(a_{t}:t\in\mathbb{I}):=\sup_{J\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}t_{0}<\dotsb<t_{J}\\
t_{j}\in\mathbb{I}\end{subarray}}\Big(\sum_{j=0}^{J-1}|a(t_{j+1})-a(t_{j})|^{\rho}\Big)^{1/\rho},

where the supremum is taken over all finite increasing sequences in

\mathbb{I}
. It is easy to see that
\rho\mapsto V^{\rho}
is non-increasing, and
for every
t_{0}\in\mathbb{I}
one has

(1.19)

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in\mathbb{I}}|a_{t}|\leq|a_{t_{0}}|+V^{\rho}(a_{t}:t\in\mathbb{I})\leq\sup_{t\in\mathbb{I}}|a_{t}|+V^{\rho}(a_{t}:t\in\mathbb{I}).

In particular, the maximal estimate (
1.16
) follows from the variational estimate (
1.17
). The main point of proving stronger variational estimates such as (
1.17
), with general parameters
\tau\in(1,2]
, is that it gives an elegant path to deriving pointwise ergodic theorems (which would not follow directly just from maximal estimates such as (
1.16
)). At the same time, the analysis of variational inequalities has many similarities with the analysis of maximal inequalities, and is not substantially more difficult. This is due in large part to the Rademacher–Menshov inequality (see
[
50
, Lemma 2.5]
): for any

2\leq\rho<\infty
and
j_{0},m\in\mathbb{N}
so that
j_{0}<2^{m}
and any sequence of complex numbers
(\mathfrak{a}_{k}:k\in\mathbb{N})
we have

(1.20)

V^{\rho}(\mathfrak{a}_{j}:j_{0}\leq j\leq 2^{m})\leq\sqrt{2}\sum_{i=0}^{m}\bigg(\sum_{j\in[j_{0}2^{-i},2^{m-i}-1]\cap\mathbb{Z}}\Big|\mathfrak{a}_{(j+1)2^{i}}-\mathfrak{a}_{j2^{i}})\Big|^{2}\bigg)^{1/2}.

1.3.3.
\ell^{p}
theory

The problem of passing from
\ell^{2}
estimates to
\ell^{p}
estimates in the context of discrete polynomial averages has been investigated extensively in recent years (see, for example,
[
48
]
and the references therein).

The full
\ell^{p}(\mathbb{G}_{0})
bounds in Theorem
1.3
rely on first proving
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{G}_{0})
bounds. In fact, we first establish (
1.17
) for
p=2
and
\rho>2
. Then we use the positivity of the operators
M_{N}^{\chi}
(i.e.
M_{N}^{\chi}(f)\geq 0
if
f\geq 0
) to prove the maximal operator bounds (
1.16
) for all
p\in(1,\infty]
. Finally, we use vector-valued interpolation between the bounds (
1.17
) with
p=2
and
\rho>2
and (
1.16
) with
p\in(1,\infty]
to complete the proof of Theorem
1.3
.

1.3.4.
Some technical remarks

Theorem
1.3
(i) and (ii) extends the results of
[
48
,
51
]
to the non-commutative, nilpotent setting. Its conclusions remain true for rough averages, i.e. when
\chi=\mathds{1}_{{[-1,1]}}
in (
1.13
), but it is more convenient to work with smooth averages.

The restriction
p>1
in Theorem
1.3
(i) and (ii) is sharp due to
[
15
,
43
]
. However, the range of
\rho>\max\big\{p,\frac{p}{p-1}\big\}
is only sharp when
p=2
due to Lépingle’s inequality
[
45
]
. One could hope to improve this to the full range
\rho>2
, but we do not address this here since the limited range
\rho>\max\big\{p,\frac{p}{p-1}\big\}
is already sufficient for us to establish Theorem
1.1
.

The restriction
p=2
in the singular integral bounds in part (ii) is probably not necessary. In the commutative case one can prove boundedness in the full range
p\in(1,\infty)
(see
[
39
]
), but the proof depends on exploiting certain Fourier multipliers and we do not know at this time if a similar definitive result holds in the nilpotent case.

1.4.
The main difficulty and a nilpotent circle method

Bourgain’s seminal papers
[
11
,
12
,
13
]
generated a large amount of research and progress in the field. Many other discrete operators have been analyzed by many authors motivated by problems in Analysis and Ergodic Theory. See, for example,
[
15
,
37
,
39
,
41
,
42
,
43
,
47
,
48
,
50
,
51
,
53
,
54
,
57
]
for some results of this type and more references. A common feature of all of these results, which plays a crucial role in the proofs, is that one can use Fourier analysis techniques, in particular, the powerful framework of the classical circle method, to perform the analysis.

Our situation in Theorem
1.3
is different. The main conceptual issue is that
there is no good Fourier transform on nilpotent groups, compatible with the structure of the underlying convolution operators and at the level of analytical precision of the classical circle method. At a more technical level, there is no good resolution of the delta function compatible with the group multiplication on the group
\mathbb{G}_{0}
. This prevents us from using a naive implementation of the circle method. The classical delta function resolution

\displaystyle\mathds{1}_{{\{0\}}}(x^{-1}\cdot y)=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{T}^{d^{\prime}}}\mathfrak{e}((y^{(1)}-x^{(1)}){.}\theta^{(1)})\mathfrak{e}((y^{(2)}-x^{(2)}){.}\theta^{(2)})\,d\theta^{(1)}d\theta^{(2)},

does not detect the group multiplication correctly.
Here
(y^{(1)}-x^{(1)}){.}\theta^{(1)}
and
(y^{(2)}-x^{(2)}){.}\theta^{(2)}
denote the usual scalar product of vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}
, respectively.

These issues lead to very significant difficulties in the proof and require substantial new ideas.
Our main new construction in
[
36
]
is what we call a
nilpotent circle method
, an iterative procedure, starting from the center of the group and moving down along its central series. At every stage we identify “minor arcs”, and bound their contributions using Weyl’s inequalities (the classical Weyl inequality as well as a nilpotent Weyl inequality which was proved in
[
38
]
). The final stage involves “major arcs” analysis, which relies on a combination of continuous harmonic analysis on groups
\mathbb{G}_{0}^{\#}
and arithmetic harmonic analysis over finite integer rings modulo
Q\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
. We outline this procedure in Section
3
below.

At the implementation level, classical Fourier techniques are replaced with almost orthogonality methods based on exploiting high order
T^{\ast}T
arguments for
operators defined on the discrete group
\mathbb{G}_{0}
. Investigating high powers of
T^{\ast}T
(i.e.
(T^{\ast}T)^{r}
for a large
r\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
) is consistent with a general heuristic lying behind the proof of Waring-type problems, which says that the more variables that occur in Waring-type equations, the easier it is to find solutions, and we are able to make this heuristic rigorous in our problem. Manipulating the parameter
r
, by taking
r
to be very large, we can always decide how many variables we have at our disposal, making our operators “smoother and smoother”.

1.5.
General discrete nilpotent groups

The primary goal is, of course, to remove the restriction that the discrete nilpotent groups
\mathbb{G}
in Theorem
1.1
are of step 2, and thus establish the full Conjecture
1.2
in the linear
m=1
case for arbitrary invertible measure-preserving transformations
T_{1},\ldots,T_{d}
that generate a nilpotent group of any step
k\geq 2
. The iterative argument we outline in Section
3
below could, in principle, be extended to higher step groups, at least as long as the group and the polynomial sequence have suitable “universal”-type structure, as one could try to go down along the central series of the group and prove minor arcs and transition estimates at every stage.

However, this is only possible if one can prove suitable analogues of the nilpotent Weyl’s inequalities in Proposition
2.1
on general nilpotent groups of step
k\geq 3
. The point is to have a small (not necessarily optimal, but nontrivial) gain for bounds on oscillatory sums over many variables, corresponding to the kernels of high power
(T^{\ast}T)^{r}
operators, whenever frequencies are restricted to the minor arcs. In our case, the formulas are explicit, see the identities (
2.10
), and we can use ideas of Davenport
[
22
]
and Birch
[
9
]
for Diophantine forms in many variables to control the induced oscillatory sums, but the analysis seems to be more complicated for the higher step nilpotent groups.

This is an interesting problem in its own right, corresponding to Waring-type problems on nilpotent groups.
A qualitative variant of the Waring problem on nilpotent groups was recently investigated in
[
34
,
35
]
, see also the references given there. We prove a quantitative version on our nilpotent group
\mathbb{G}_{0}
in Theorem
4.1
below.

1.6.
Organization

The rest of this paper is organized as follows: in section 2 we present several nilpotent Weyl estimates proved in
[
38
]
, which play a key role in the analysis of minor arcs. In section 3 we outline our main new method, the nilpotent circle method, developed in
[
36
]
to prove maximal and variational estimates on nilpotent groups. In section 4 we prove a new Waring-type theorem on the nilpotent group
\mathbb{G}_{0}
, as an application of the nilpotent Weyl estimates discussed earlier.

2.
A nilpotent Weyl inequality on the group
\mathbb{G}_{0}

In this section we derive explicit formulas used in high order
T^{\ast}T
arguments and discuss a key ingredient in our analysis, namely Weyl inequalities on the group
\mathbb{G}_{0}
.

2.1.
High order
T^{\ast}T
arguments and product kernels

Many of our
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{G}_{0})
estimates will be based on high order
T^{\ast}T
arguments. Assume that

S_{1},T_{1},\ldots,S_{r},T_{r}:\ell^{2}(\mathbb{G}_{0})\to\ell^{2}(G_{0})

are convolution operators defined by some
\ell^{1}(\mathbb{G}_{0})
kernels
L_{1},K_{1},\ldots,L_{r},K_{r}:\mathbb{G}_{0}\to\mathbb{C}
, i.e.
S_{j}f=f\ast L_{j}
and
T_{j}f=f\ast K_{j}
for
j\in\{1,\ldots,r\}
. Then the adjoint operators
S_{1}^{\ast},\ldots,S_{r}^{\ast}
are also convolution operators, defined by the kernels
L_{1}^{\ast},\ldots,L_{r}^{\ast}
given by

L_{j}^{\ast}(g):=\overline{L_{j}(g^{-1})}.

Moreover, using (
1.14
), for any
f\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{G}_{0})
and
x\in\mathbb{G}_{0}
, we have

(2.1)

\begin{split}(S_{1}^{\ast}T_{1}\ldots S_{r}^{\ast}T_{r}f)(x)=\sum_{h_{1},g_{1},\ldots,h_{r},g_{r}\in\mathbb{G}_{0}}\Big\{\prod_{j=1}^{r}L_{j}^{\ast}(h_{j})K_{j}(g_{j})\Big\}f(g_{r}^{-1}\cdot h_{r}^{-1}\cdot\ldots\cdot g_{1}^{-1}\cdot h_{1}^{-1}\cdot x).\end{split}

In other words
(S_{1}^{\ast}T_{1}\ldots S_{r}^{\ast}T_{r}f)(x)=(f\ast A^{r})(x)
,
where the kernel
A^{r}
is given by

(2.2)

A^{r}(y):=\sum_{h_{1},g_{1},\ldots,h_{r},g_{r}\in\mathbb{G}_{0}}\Big\{\prod_{j=1}^{r}\overline{L_{j}(h_{j})}K_{j}(g_{j})\Big\}\mathds{1}_{{\{0\}}}(g_{r}^{-1}\cdot h_{r}\cdot\ldots\cdot g_{1}^{-1}\cdot h_{1}\cdot y).

To use these formulas we decompose
h_{j}=(h_{j}^{(1)},h_{j}^{(2)}),\,g_{j}=(g_{j}^{(1)},g_{j}^{(2)})
as in (
1.7
). Then

(2.3)

[h_{1}^{-1}\cdot g_{1}\cdot\ldots\cdot h_{r}^{-1}\cdot g_{r}]^{(1)}=\sum_{1\leq j\leq r}(-h_{j}^{(1)}+g_{j}^{(1)}),

(2.4)

\begin{split}[h_{1}^{-1}\cdot g_{1}\cdot\ldots\cdot h_{r}^{-1}\cdot g_{r}]^{(2)}&=\sum_{1\leq j\leq r}\big\{-(h_{j}^{(2)}-g_{j}^{(2)})+R_{0}(h_{j}^{(1)},h_{j}^{(1)}-g_{j}^{(1)})\big\}\\
&+\sum_{1\leq l<j\leq r}R_{0}(-h_{l}^{(1)}+g_{l}^{(1)},-h_{j}^{(1)}+g_{j}^{(1)}),\end{split}

as a consequence of applying (
1.9
) inductively.

In many of our applications the operators
S_{1},T_{1},\ldots,S_{r},T_{r}
are equal and, more importantly, are defined by a kernel
K
that has product structure, i.e.

(2.5)

\begin{split}&S_{1}f=T_{1}f=\ldots=S_{r}f=T_{r}f=f\ast K,\\
&K(g)=K(g^{(1)},g^{(2)})=K^{(1)}(g^{(1)})K^{(2)}(g^{(2)}).\end{split}

In this case we can derive an additional formula for the kernel
A^{r}
. We use the identity

\mathds{1}_{{\{0\}}}(x^{-1}\cdot y)=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{T}^{d^{\prime}}}\mathfrak{e}((y^{(1)}-x^{(1)}){.}\theta^{(1)})\mathfrak{e}((y^{(2)}-x^{(2)}){.}\theta^{(2)})\,d\theta^{(1)}d\theta^{(2)},

where
\mathfrak{e}(z):=e^{2\pi iz}
. The formula (
2.2
) shows that

(2.6)

A^{r}(y)=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{T}^{d^{\prime}}}\mathfrak{e}\big(y^{(1)}.\theta^{(1)}\big)\mathfrak{e}\big(y^{(2)}.\theta^{(2)}\big)\Sigma^{r}\big(\theta^{(1)},\theta^{(2)}\big)\,d\theta^{(1)}d\theta^{(2)},

where

\begin{split}\Sigma^{r}\big(\theta^{(1)},\theta^{(2)}\big)&:=\sum_{h_{j},g_{j}\in\mathbb{G}_{0}}\Big\{\prod_{j=1}^{r}\overline{K(h_{j})}K(g_{j})\Big\}\prod_{i=1}^{2}\mathfrak{e}\big(-[h_{1}^{-1}\cdot g_{1}\cdot\ldots\cdot h_{r}^{-1}\cdot g_{r}]^{(i)}{.}\theta^{(i)}\big).\end{split}

Recalling the product formula (
2.5
) we can write

(2.7)

\Sigma^{r}\big(\theta^{(1)},\theta^{(2)}\big)=\Pi^{r}\big(\theta^{(1)},\theta^{(2)}\big)\Omega^{r}\big(\theta^{(2)}\big),

for any
(\theta^{(1)},\theta^{(2)})\in\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{T}^{d^{\prime}}
,
where

(2.8)

\begin{split}\Pi^{r}&\big(\theta^{(1)},\theta^{(2)}\big):=\sum_{h_{j}^{(1)},g_{j}^{(1)}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\Big\{\prod_{j=1}^{r}\overline{K^{(1)}(h_{j}^{(1)})}K^{(1)}(g_{j}^{(1)})\Big\}\mathfrak{e}\big(\theta^{(1)}{.}\sum_{1\leq j\leq r}(h_{j}^{(1)}-g_{j}^{(1)})\big)\\
&\times\mathfrak{e}\Big(-\theta^{(2)}{.}\big\{\sum_{1\leq j\leq r}R_{0}(h_{j}^{(1)},h_{j}^{(1)}-g_{j}^{(1)})+\sum_{1\leq l<j\leq r}R_{0}(-h_{l}^{(1)}+g_{l}^{(1)},-h_{j}^{(1)}+g_{j}^{(1)})\big\}\Big)\end{split}

and

(2.9)

\begin{split}\Omega^{r}\big(\theta^{(2)}\big)&:=\sum_{h_{j}^{(2)},g_{j}^{(2)}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d^{\prime}}}\Big\{\prod_{j=1}^{r}\overline{K^{(2)}(h_{j}^{(2)})}K^{(2)}(g_{j}^{(2)})\Big\}\mathfrak{e}\big(\theta^{(2)}{.}\sum_{1\leq j\leq r}(h_{j}^{(2)}-g_{j}^{(2)})\big)\\
&=\Big|\sum_{g^{(2)}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d^{\prime}}}K^{(2)}(g^{(2)})\mathfrak{e}\big(-\theta^{(2)}{.}g^{(2)}\big)\Big|^{2r}.\end{split}

2.2.
Weyl estimates

After applying high order
T^{\ast}T
arguments we often need to estimate exponential sums and oscillatory integrals involving polynomial phases. With the notation in Section
1.2
, for
r\geq 1
let
D,\widetilde{D}:\mathbb{R}^{r}\times\mathbb{R}^{r}\to\mathbb{G}_{0}^{\#}
be defined by

(2.10)

\begin{split}&D((n_{1},\ldots,n_{r}),(m_{1},\ldots,m_{r})):=A_{0}(n_{1})^{-1}\cdot A_{0}(m_{1})\cdot\ldots\cdot A_{0}(n_{r})^{-1}\cdot A_{0}(m_{r}),\\
&\widetilde{D}((n_{1},\ldots,n_{r}),(m_{1},\ldots,m_{r})):=A_{0}(n_{1})\cdot A_{0}(m_{1})^{-1}\cdot\ldots\cdot A_{0}(n_{r})\cdot A_{0}(m_{r})^{-1}.\end{split}

By definition, we have

[A_{0}(n)]_{l_{1}l_{2}}=\begin{cases}n^{l_{1}}&\text{ if }l_{2}=0,\\
0&\text{ if }l_{2}\geq 1,\end{cases}\qquad[A_{0}(n)^{-1}]_{l_{1}l_{2}}=\begin{cases}-n^{l_{1}}&\text{ if }l_{2}=0,\\
n^{l_{1}+l_{2}}&\text{ if }l_{2}\geq 1.\end{cases}

Thus, using (
2.3
) and (
2.4
), for
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{r})\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
and
y=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{r})\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
one has

(2.11)

[D(x,y)]_{l_{1}l_{2}}=\begin{cases}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{r}(y_{j}^{l_{1}}-x_{j}^{l_{1}})&\text{ if }l_{2}=0,\\
\sum\limits_{1\leq j_{1}<j_{2}\leq r}(y_{j_{1}}^{l_{1}}-x_{j_{1}}^{l_{1}})(y_{j_{2}}^{l_{2}}-x_{j_{2}}^{l_{2}})+\sum\limits_{j=1}^{r}(x_{j}^{l_{1}+l_{2}}-x_{j}^{l_{1}}y_{j}^{l_{2}})&\text{ if }l_{2}\geq 1,\end{cases}

and

(2.12)

[\widetilde{D}(x,y)]_{l_{1}l_{2}}=\begin{cases}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{r}(x_{j}^{l_{1}}-y_{j}^{l_{1}})&\text{ if }l_{2}=0,\\
\sum\limits_{1\leq j_{1}<j_{2}\leq r}(x_{j_{1}}^{l_{1}}-y_{j_{1}}^{l_{1}})(x_{j_{2}}^{l_{2}}-y_{j_{2}}^{l_{2}})+\sum\limits_{j=1}^{r}(y_{j}^{l_{1}+l_{2}}-x_{j}^{l_{1}}y_{j}^{l_{2}})&\text{ if }l_{2}\geq 1.\end{cases}

For
P\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
assume
\phi_{P}^{(j)},\psi_{P}^{(j)}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
,
j\in\{1,\ldots,r\}
, are
C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
functions with the properties

(2.13)

\sup_{1\leq j\leq r}\big[\big|\phi_{P}^{(j)}\big|+\big|\psi_{P}^{(j)}\big|\big]\leq\mathds{1}_{{[-P,P]}},\qquad\sup_{1\leq j\leq r}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\big|[\phi^{(j)}_{P}]^{\prime}(x)\big|+\big|[\psi^{(j)}_{P}]^{\prime}(x)\big|\,dx\leq 1.

For
\theta=(\theta_{l_{1}l_{2}})_{(l_{1},l_{2})\in Y_{d}}\in\mathbb{R}^{|Y_{d}|}
,
r\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, and
P\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
let

S_{P,r}(\theta)=\sum_{n,m\in\mathbb{Z}^{r}}\mathfrak{e}(-D(n,m){.}\theta)\Big\{\prod_{j=1}^{r}\phi_{P}^{(j)}(n_{j})\psi_{P}^{(j)}(m_{j})\Big\}

and

\widetilde{S}_{P,r}(\theta)=\sum_{n,m\in\mathbb{Z}^{r}}\mathfrak{e}(-\widetilde{D}(n,m){.}\theta)\Big\{\prod_{j=1}^{r}\phi_{P}^{(j)}(n_{j})\psi_{P}^{(j)}(m_{j})\Big\},

where
D
and
\widetilde{D}
are defined as in (
2.11
)–(
2.12
).

The following key estimates are proved in
[
38
, Proposition 5.1 and Lemma 3.1]
:

Proposition 2.1
.

(i) (Nilpotent Weyl estimate) For any
\varepsilon>0
there is
r=r(\varepsilon,d)\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
sufficiently large such that for all
P\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
we have

(2.14)

|S_{P,r}(\theta)|+|\widetilde{S}_{P,r}(\theta)|\lesssim_{\varepsilon}P^{2r}P^{-1/\varepsilon},

provided that there is
(l_{1},l_{2})\in Y_{d}
and an irreducible fraction
a/q\in\mathbb{Q}
,
q\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, such that

(2.15)

|\theta_{l_{1}l_{2}}-a/q|\leq 1/q^{2}\text{ and }q\in[P^{\varepsilon},P^{l_{1}+l_{2}-\varepsilon}].

(ii) (Nilpotent Gauss sums) For any irreducible fraction
a/q\in\mathbb{Q}^{|Y_{d}|}
,
a=(a_{l_{1}l_{2}})_{(l_{1},l_{2})\in Y_{d}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{|Y_{d}|}
,
q\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, we define the arithmetic coefficients

(2.16)

G(a/q):=q^{-2r}\sum_{v,w\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{r}}\mathfrak{e}\big(-D(v,w){.}(a/q)\big),\qquad\widetilde{G}(a/q):=q^{-2r}\sum_{v,w\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{r}}\mathfrak{e}\big(-\widetilde{D}(v,w){.}(a/q)\big).

Then for any
\varepsilon>0
there is
r=r(\varepsilon,d)\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
sufficiently large such that

(2.17)

|G(a/q)|+|\widetilde{G}(a/q)|\lesssim_{\varepsilon}q^{-1/\varepsilon}.

We also need a related integral estimate, see Lemma 5.4 in
[
38
]
:

Proposition 2.2
.

Given
\varepsilon>0
there is
r=r(\varepsilon,d)
sufficiently large as in Proposition
2.1
such that

(2.18)

\begin{split}\Big|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{r}\times\mathbb{R}^{r}}\Big\{\prod_{j=1}^{r}\phi_{j}(x_{j})\psi_{j}(y_{j})\Big\}\mathfrak{e}(-D(x,y){.}\beta\big)\,dxdy\Big|\lesssim\langle\beta\rangle^{-1/\varepsilon},\\
\Big|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{r}\times\mathbb{R}^{r}}\Big\{\prod_{j=1}^{r}\phi_{j}(x_{j})\psi_{j}(y_{j})\Big\}\mathfrak{e}(-\widetilde{D}(x,y){.}\beta\big)\,dxdy\Big|\lesssim\langle\beta\rangle^{-1/\varepsilon},\end{split}

for any
\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{|Y_{d}|}
; here and later on we use the Japanese bracket notation
\langle\beta\rangle:=(1+|\beta|^{2})^{1/2}
, and for any
C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
functions
\phi_{1},\psi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{r},\psi_{r}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}
satisfying, for any
j\in\{1,\ldots,r\}
, the bounds

|\phi_{j}|+|\psi_{j}|\leq\mathds{1}_{{[-1,1]}}(x),\qquad\int_{\mathbb{R}}\big[|\partial_{x}\phi_{j}(x)|+|\partial_{x}\psi_{j}(x)|\big]\,dx\leq 1.

These statements should be compared with classical Weyl-type estimates, which are proved for example in
[
57
, Proposition 1]
:

Proposition 2.3
.

(i) Assume that
P\geq 1
is an integer and
\phi_{P}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is a
C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
function satisfying

(2.19)

|\phi_{P}|\leq\mathds{1}_{{[-P,P]}},\qquad\int_{\mathbb{R}}\big|\phi^{\prime}_{P}(x)\big|\,dx\leq 1.

Assume that
\varepsilon>0
and
\theta=(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
has the property that there is
l\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
and an irreducible fraction
a/q\in\mathbb{Q}
with
q\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, such that

(2.20)

|\theta_{l}-a/q|\leq 1/q^{2}\,\,\text{ and }\,\,q\in[P^{\varepsilon},P^{l-\varepsilon}].

Then there is a constant
\overline{C}=\overline{C}_{d}\geq 1
such that

(2.21)

\Big|\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\phi_{P}(n)\mathfrak{e}\big(-(\theta_{1}n+\ldots+\theta_{d}n^{d})\big)\Big|\lesssim_{\varepsilon}P^{1-\varepsilon/\overline{C}}.

(ii) For any irreducible fraction
\theta=a/q\in(\mathbb{Z}/q)^{d}
,
a=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{d})\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
,
q\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, we have

(2.22)

\Big|q^{-1}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}}\mathfrak{e}\big(-(\theta_{1}n+\ldots+\theta_{d}n^{d})\big)\Big|\lesssim q^{-1/\overline{C}}.

Notice a formal similarity between Proposition
2.1
and
2.3
. They both involve a small but non-trivial gain of a power of
P
as soon as one of the coefficients of the relevant polynomials is far from rational numbers with small denominators. These estimates can therefore be used efficiently to estimate minor arcs contributions.

We note, however, that the proof of the nilpotent Weyl estimates in Proposition
2.1
is much more involved than the proof of Proposition
2.3
. It relies on some classical ideas of Davenport
[
22
]
and Birch
[
9
]
on treating polynomials in many variables, but one has to identify and exploit suitable non-degeneracy properties of the explicit (but complicated) polynomials
D
and
\widetilde{D}
in (
2.11
)–(
2.12
) to make the proof work. All the details of the proof are provided in
[
38
, Section 5]
.

3.
A nilpotent circle method

To illustrate our main method, we focus on a particular case of Theorem
1.3
, namely on proving boundedness of the maximal function
M_{N}^{\chi}
on
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{G}_{0})
. For simplicity of notation, for
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
x\in\mathbb{G}_{0}
, let

(3.1)

\begin{split}\mathcal{M}_{k}f(x)&:=M_{2^{k}}^{\chi}f(x)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}2^{-k}\chi(2^{-k}n)f(A_{0}(n)^{-1}\cdot x)=(f\ast K_{k})(x),\\
K_{k}(x)&:=G_{2^{k}}^{\chi}(x)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}2^{-k}\chi(2^{-k}n)\mathds{1}_{{\{A_{0}(n)\}}}(x),\end{split}

see (
1.13
) and (
1.15
) for the definitions
M_{N}^{\chi}
and
G_{N}^{\chi}
respectively. With this new notation, our main goal is to prove the following:

Theorem 3.1
.

For any
f\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{G}_{0})
we have

(3.2)

\displaystyle\big\|\sup_{k\geq 0}|\mathcal{M}_{k}f|\big\|_{\ell^{2}(\mathbb{G}_{0})}\lesssim\|f\|_{\ell^{2}(\mathbb{G}_{0})}.

In the rest of this section we outline the proof of this theorem. Our main new construction is an iterative procedure, starting from the center of the group and moving down along its central series, that allows us to use some of the ideas of the classical circle method recursively at every stage. In our case of nilpotent groups of step two, the procedure consists of two basic stages and one additional step corresponding to “major arcs”.

Notice that the kernels
K_{k}
have product structure

(3.3)

K_{k}(g):=L_{k}(g^{(1)})\mathds{1}_{{\{0\}}}(g^{(2)}),\qquad L_{k}(g^{(1)}):=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}2^{-k}\chi(2^{-k}n)\mathds{1}_{{\{0\}}}(g^{(1)}-A^{(1)}_{0}(n)),

where
A^{(1)}_{0}(n):=(n,\ldots,n^{d})\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
and
g=(g^{(1)},g^{(2)})\in\mathbb{G}_{0}
as in (
1.7
).

3.1.
First stage reduction

We first decompose the singular kernel
\mathds{1}_{{\{0\}}}(g^{(2)})
in the central variable
g^{(2)}
into smoother kernels. For any
s\in\mathbb{N}
and
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
we define the set of rational fractions

(3.4)

\mathcal{R}_{s}^{m}:=\{a/q:\,a=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{m})\in\mathbb{Z}^{m},\,q\in[2^{s},2^{s+1})\cap\mathbb{Z},\,\mathrm{gcd}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{m},q)=1\}.

We define also
\mathcal{R}^{m}_{\leq a}:=\bigcup_{0\leq s\leq a}\mathcal{R}_{s}^{m}
. For
x^{(1)}=(x^{(1)}_{l_{1}0})_{l_{1}\in\{1,\ldots,d\}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
x^{(2)}=(x^{(2)}_{l_{1}l_{2}})_{(l_{1},l_{2})\in Y^{\prime}_{d}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}
and
\Lambda\in(0,\infty)
we define the partial dilations

(3.5)

\Lambda\circ x^{(1)}=(\Lambda^{l_{1}}x^{(1)}_{l_{1}0})_{l_{1}\in\{1,\ldots,d\}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\qquad\Lambda\circ x^{(2)}=(\Lambda^{l_{1}+l_{2}}x^{(2)}_{l_{1}l_{2}})_{(l_{1},l_{2})\in Y^{\prime}_{d}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}},

which are induced by the group-dilations defined in (
1.12
).

We fix
\eta_{0}:\mathbb{R}\to[0,1]
a smooth even function
such that
\mathds{1}_{{[-1,1]}}\leq\eta_{0}\leq\mathds{1}_{{[-2,2]}}
. For
t\in\mathbb{R}
and integers
j\geq 1
we define

(3.6)

\eta_{j}(t):=\eta_{0}(2^{-j}t)-\eta_{0}(2^{-j+1}t),\qquad 1=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\eta_{j}.

For any
A\in[0,\infty)
we define

(3.7)

\eta_{\leq A}:=\sum_{j\in[0,A]\cap\mathbb{Z}}\eta_{j}.

By a slight abuse of notation we also let
\eta_{j}
and
\eta_{\leq A}
denote the smooth radial functions on
\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
m\geq 1
, defined by
\eta_{j}(x)=\eta_{j}(|x|)
and
\eta_{\leq A}(x)=\eta_{\leq A}(|x|)
. We fix also two small constants
\delta=\delta(d)\ll\delta^{\prime}=\delta^{\prime}(d)
such that
\delta^{\prime}\in(0,(10d)^{-10}]
and
\delta\in(0,{(\delta^{\prime})}^{4}]
, and a large constant
D=D(d)\gg\delta^{-8}
, which depend on arithmetic properties of the polynomial sequence
A_{0}
(more precisely on the structural constants in Propositions
2.1
–
2.2
) such that

(3.8)

1\ll 1/\delta^{\prime}\ll 1/\delta\ll r=r(\delta,\delta^{\prime},d)\ll D.

For
k\geq D^{2}
we fix two cutoff functions
\phi_{k}^{(1)}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to[0,1]
,
\phi_{k}^{(2)}:\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}\to[0,1]
, such that

(3.9)

\phi_{k}^{(1)}(g^{(1)}):=\eta_{\leq\delta k}(2^{-k}\circ g^{(1)}),\qquad\phi_{k}^{(2)}(g^{(2)}):=\eta_{\leq\delta k}(2^{-k}\circ g^{(2)}).

For
k\in\mathbb{N}
so that
k\geq D^{2}
and for any
1
-periodic sets of rationals
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathbb{Q}^{d}
,
\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathbb{Q}^{d^{\prime}}

we define the periodic Fourier multipliers by

(3.10)

\begin{split}\Psi_{k,\mathcal{A}}(\xi^{(1)})&:=\sum_{a/q\in\mathcal{A}}\eta_{\leq\delta^{\prime}k}(2^{k}\circ(\xi^{(1)}-a/q)),\qquad\xi^{(1)}\in\mathbb{T}^{d},\\
\Xi_{k,\mathcal{B}}(\xi^{(2)})&:=\sum_{b/q\in\mathcal{B}}\eta_{\leq\delta k}(2^{k}\circ(\xi^{(2)}-b/q)),\qquad\xi^{(2)}\in\mathbb{T}^{d^{\prime}}.\end{split}

For
k\geq D^{2}
and
s\in[0,\delta k]\cap\mathbb{Z}
we define the periodic Fourier multipliers
\Xi_{k,s}:\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}\to[0,1]
,

(3.11)

\Xi_{k,s}(\xi^{(2)}):=\Xi_{k,\mathcal{R}_{s}^{d^{\prime}}}(\xi^{(2)})=\sum_{a/q\in\mathcal{R}_{s}^{d^{\prime}}}\eta_{\leq\delta k}(2^{k}\circ(\xi^{(2)}-a/q)).

For
k\geq D^{2}
we write

(3.12)

\begin{split}\mathds{1}_{{\{0\}}}(g^{(2)})&=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d^{\prime}}}\mathfrak{e}(g^{(2)}.\xi^{(2)})\,d\xi^{(2)}\\
&=\sum_{s\in[0,\delta k]\cap\mathbb{Z}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d^{\prime}}}\mathfrak{e}(g^{(2)}.\xi^{(2)})\Xi_{k,s}(\xi^{(2)})\,d\xi^{(2)}+\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d^{\prime}}}\mathfrak{e}(g^{(2)}.\xi^{(2)})\Xi_{k}^{c}(\xi^{(2)})\,d\xi^{(2)},\end{split}

recall that
g^{(2)}.\xi^{(2)}
denotes the usual scalar product of vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}
and

(3.13)

\Xi_{k}^{c}:=1-\sum_{s\in[0,\delta k]\cap\mathbb{Z}}\Xi_{k,s}.

Then we decompose
K_{k}=K_{k}^{c}+\sum_{s\in[0,\delta k]\cap\mathbb{Z}}K_{k,s}
, where, with the notation in (
3.3
), we have

(3.14)

K_{k,s}(g):=L_{k}(g^{(1)})N_{k,s}(g^{(2)}),\qquad K_{k}^{c}(g):=L_{k}(g^{(1)})N_{k}^{c}(g^{(2)}),

and

(3.15)

\begin{split}N_{k,s}(g^{(2)})&:=\phi_{k}^{(2)}(g^{(2)})\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d^{\prime}}}\mathfrak{e}(g^{(2)}.\xi^{(2)})\Xi_{k,s}(\xi^{(2)})\,d\xi^{(2)},\\
N_{k}^{c}(g^{(2)})&:=\phi_{k}^{(2)}(g^{(2)})\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d^{\prime}}}\mathfrak{e}(g^{(2)}.\xi^{(2)})\Xi_{k}^{c}(\xi^{(2)})\,d\xi^{(2)}.\end{split}

We first show that we can bound the contributions of the minor arcs in
the central variables:

Lemma 3.2
.

For any integer
k\geq D^{2}
and
f\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{G}_{0})
we have

(3.16)

\|f\ast K_{k}^{c}\|_{\ell^{2}(\mathbb{G}_{0})}\lesssim 2^{-k/D^{2}}\|f\|_{\ell^{2}(\mathbb{G}_{0})}.

Then we prove our first transition estimate, i.e. we show that we can bound the
contributions of the kernels
K_{k,s}
corresponding to scales
k\geq 0
not very large. More precisely, for any
s\geq 0
we define

(3.17)

\kappa_{s}:=2^{2D(s+1)^{2}}.

Lemma 3.3
.

For any integer
s\geq 0
and
f\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{G}_{0})
we have

(3.18)

\big\|\sup_{\max(D^{2},s/\delta)\leq k<2\kappa_{s}}|f\ast K_{k,s}|\big\|_{\ell^{2}(\mathbb{G}_{0})}\lesssim 2^{-s/D^{2}}\|f\|_{\ell^{2}(\mathbb{G}_{0})}.

In the commutative setting, minor arcs estimates such as (
3.16
) follow using Weyl estimates and the Plancherel theorem. As we do not have a useful Fourier transform on the group
\mathbb{G}_{0}
, our main tool to prove the bounds (
3.16
) is a high order
T^{\ast}T
argument. More precisely, we analyze the kernel of the convolution operator
\{(\mathcal{K}_{k}^{c})^{\ast}\mathcal{K}_{k}^{c}\}^{r}
, where
\mathcal{K}_{k}^{c}f:=f\ast K_{k}^{c}
and
r
is sufficiently large, and show that its
\ell^{1}(\mathbb{G}_{0})
norm is
\lesssim 2^{-k}
. The main ingredient in this proof is the non-commutative Weyl estimate in Proposition
2.1
(i).

To prove the transition estimates (
3.18
), we use the Rademacher-Menshov inequality and Khintchine’s inequality (leading to logarithmic losses) to reduce to proving the bounds

(3.19)

\Big\|\sum_{k\in[J,2J]}\varkappa_{k}(f\ast H_{k,s})\Big\|_{\ell^{2}(\mathbb{G}_{0})}\lesssim 2^{-4s/D^{2}}\big\|f\big\|_{\ell^{2}(\mathbb{G}_{0})}

for any
J\geq\max(D^{2},s/\delta)
and any coefficients
\varkappa_{k}\in[-1,1]
, where
H_{k,s}:=K_{k+1,s}-K_{k,s}
. For this, we use a high order version of the Cotlar–Stein lemma, which relies again on precise analysis of the kernel of the convolution operator
\{(\mathcal{H}_{k,s})^{\ast}\mathcal{H}_{k,s}\}^{r}
, where
\mathcal{H}_{k,s}f:=f\ast H_{k,s}
and
r
is sufficiently large. The key exponential gain of
2^{-4s/D^{2}}
in (
3.19
) is due to the non-commutative Gauss sums estimate, see Proposition
2.1
(ii).

3.2.
Second stage reduction

In view of Lemmas
3.2
–
3.3
it remains to prove that

(3.20)

\big\|\sup_{k\geq\kappa_{s}}|f\ast K_{k,s}|\big\|_{\ell^{2}(\mathbb{G}_{0})}\lesssim 2^{-s/D^{2}}\|f\|_{\ell^{2}(\mathbb{G}_{0})}

for any fixed integer
s\geq 0
. The kernels
K_{k,s}
are now reasonably well adapted to a natural family of non-isotropic balls in the central variables, at least when
2^{s}\approx 1
, and we need to start decomposing in the non-central variables.

We examine the kernels
L_{k}(g^{(1)})
defined in (
3.3
), and rewrite them in the form

(3.21)

\begin{split}L_{k}(g^{(1)})&=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}2^{-k}\chi(2^{-k}n)\mathds{1}_{{\{0\}}}(-A^{(1)}_{0}(n)+g^{(1)})\\
&=\phi_{k}^{(1)}(g^{(1)})\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\mathfrak{e}(g^{(1)}{.}\xi^{(1)})S_{k}(\xi^{(1)})\,d\xi^{(1)},\end{split}

where
g^{(1)}.\xi^{(1)}
denotes the usual scalar product of vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and

(3.22)

\displaystyle\begin{split}S_{k}(\xi^{(1)})&:=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}2^{-k}\chi(2^{-k}n)\mathfrak{e}(-A^{(1)}_{0}(n){.}\xi^{(1)}).\end{split}

For any integers
Q\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
and
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
we define the set of fractions

(3.23)

\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}^{m}_{Q}:=\{a/Q:\,a=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{m})\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}\}.

For any integer
s\geq 0
we fix a large denominator

(3.24)

Q_{s}:=\big(\big\lfloor 2^{D(s+1)}\big\rfloor\big)!=1\cdot 2\cdot\ldots\cdot\big\lfloor 2^{D(s+1)}\big\rfloor,

and using (
3.10
) define the periodic multipliers

(3.25)

\begin{split}\Psi_{k,s}^{\rm low}(\xi^{(1)})&:=\Psi_{k,\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}^{d}_{Q_{s}}}(\xi^{(1)})=\sum_{a/q\in\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}^{d}_{Q_{s}}}\eta_{\leq\delta^{\prime}k}(2^{k}\circ(\xi^{(1)}-a/q)),\\
\Psi_{k,s,t}(\xi^{(1)})&:=\Psi_{k,\mathcal{R}^{d}_{t}\setminus\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}^{d}_{Q_{s}}}(\xi^{(1)})=\sum_{a/q\in\mathcal{R}^{d}_{t}\setminus\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}^{d}_{Q_{s}}}\eta_{\leq\delta^{\prime}k}(2^{k}\circ(\xi^{(1)}-a/q)),\\
\Psi_{k}^{c}(\xi^{(1)})&:=1-\Psi_{k,s}^{\rm low}(\xi^{(1)})-\sum_{t\in[0,\delta^{\prime}k]\cap\mathbb{Z}}\Psi_{k,s,t}(\xi^{(1)})\ =1-\sum_{a/q\in\mathcal{R}^{d}_{\leq\delta^{\prime}k}}\eta_{\leq\delta^{\prime}k}(\tau^{k}\circ(\xi^{(1)}-a/q)).\end{split}

Since
k\geq\kappa_{s}=2^{2D(s+1)^{2}}
we see that
Q_{s}\leq 2^{\delta^{2}k}
. Therefore the supports of the cutoff functions
\eta_{\leq\delta^{\prime}k}(2^{k}\circ(\xi^{(1)}-a/q))
are all disjoint and the multipliers
\Psi_{k,s}^{\rm low},\Psi_{k,s,t},\Psi_{k}^{c}
take values in the interval
[0,1]
. Notice also that
\Psi_{k,s,t}\equiv 0
unless
t\geq D(s+1)
, and that the cutoffs used in these definitions depend on
\delta^{\prime}k
not on
\delta k
as in the case of the central variables.

We examine the formula (
3.21
) and define the kernels
L_{k,s}^{\rm low},L_{k,s,t},L_{k}^{c}:\mathbb{Z}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}
by

(3.26)

L_{\ast}(g^{(1)})=\phi_{k}^{(1)}(g^{(1)})\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\mathfrak{e}(g^{(1)}{.}\xi^{(1)})S_{k}(\xi^{(1)})\Psi_{\ast}(\xi^{(1)})\,d\xi^{(1)},

where
(L_{\ast},\Psi_{\ast})\in\{(L_{k,s}^{\rm low},\Psi_{k,s}^{\rm low}),(L_{k,s,t},\Psi_{k,s,t}),(L_{k}^{c},\Psi_{k}^{c})\}
. For any
k\geq\kappa_{s}
we
obtain
K_{k,s}=G_{k,s}^{\rm low}+\sum_{t\leq\delta^{\prime}k}G_{k,s,t}+G_{k,s}^{c}
, where
the kernels
G_{k,s}^{\rm low},G_{k,s,t},G_{k,s}^{c}:\mathbb{Z}^{|Y_{d}|}\to\mathbb{C}
are defined by

(3.27)

\begin{split}G_{k,s}^{\rm low}(g)&:=L_{k,s}^{\rm low}(g^{(1)})N_{k,s}(g^{(2)}),\\
G_{k,s,t}(g)&:=L_{k,s,t}(g^{(1)})N_{k,s}(g^{(2)}),\\
G_{k,s}^{c}(g)&:=L_{k}^{c}(g^{(1)})N_{k,s}(g^{(2)}).\end{split}

Our next step is to show that the contributions of the minor arcs corresponding to the kernels
G_{k,s}^{c}
can be suitably bounded:

Lemma 3.4
.

For any integers
s\geq 0
and
k\geq\kappa_{s}
, and for any
f\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{G}_{0})
we have

(3.28)

\|f\ast G_{k,s}^{c}\|_{\ell^{2}(\mathbb{G}_{0})}\lesssim 2^{-k/D^{2}}\|f\|_{\ell^{2}(\mathbb{G}_{0})}.

Then we prove our second transition estimate, bounding the contributions of the operators defined by the kernels
G_{k,s,t}
for intermediate values of
k
.

Lemma 3.5
.

For any integers
s\geq 0
, and
t\geq D(s+1)
, and
f\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{G}_{0})
we have

(3.29)

\big\|\sup_{\max(\kappa_{s},t/\delta^{\prime})\leq k<2\kappa_{t}}|f\ast G_{k,s,t}|\big\|_{\ell^{2}(\mathbb{G}_{0})}\lesssim 2^{-t/D^{2}}\|f\|_{\ell^{2}(\mathbb{G}_{0})},

where
\kappa_{t}=2^{2D(t+1)^{2}}
as in (
3.17
).

The proofs of these estimates are similar to the proofs of the corresponding first stage estimates (
3.16
) and (
3.18
), using high order
T^{\ast}T
arguments. However, instead of using the nilpotent oscillatory sums estimates in Proposition
2.1
, we use the classical estimates from Proposition
2.3
here. We emphasize, however, that the underlying nilpotent structure is very important and that these estimates are only possible after performing the two reductions in the first stage, namely, the restriction to major arcs corresponding to denominators
\approx 2^{s}
and the restriction to parameters
k\geq\kappa_{s}
.
We finally remark that the circle method could not have been applied simultaneously to both central and non-central variables, as we would not have been able control efficiently the phase functions arising in the corresponding exponential sums and oscillatory integrals, especially on major arcs.

3.3.
Final stage: major arcs contributions

After these reductions, it remains to bound the contributions of the “major arcs” in both the central and the non-central variables. More precisely, we prove the following bounds:

Lemma 3.6
.

(i) For any integer
s\geq 0
and
f\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{G}_{0})
we have

(3.30)

\big\|\sup_{k\geq\kappa_{s}}|f\ast G^{\rm low}_{k,s}|\big\|_{\ell^{2}(\mathbb{G}_{0})}\lesssim 2^{-s/D^{2}}\|f\|_{\ell^{2}(\mathbb{G}_{0})}.

(ii) For any integers
s\geq 0
,
t\geq D(s+1)
, and
f\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{G}_{0})
we have

(3.31)

\big\|\sup_{k\geq\kappa_{t}}|f\ast G_{k,s,t}|\big\|_{\ell^{2}(\mathbb{G}_{0})}\lesssim 2^{-t/D^{2}}\|f\|_{\ell^{2}(\mathbb{G}_{0})}.

The main idea here is different: we write the kernels
G^{\rm low}_{k,s}
and
G_{k,s,t}
as tensor products of two components up to acceptable errors. One of these components is essentially a maximal average operator on a continuous group, which can be analyzed using the classical method of Christ
[
17
]
. The other component is an arithmetic operator-valued analogue of the classical Gauss sums, which leads to the key factors
2^{-s/D^{2}}
and
2^{-t/D^{2}}
in (
3.30
) and (
3.31
).

More precisely, for any integer
Q\geq 1
we define the subgroup

(3.32)

\mathbb{H}_{Q}:=\{h=(Qh_{l_{1}l_{2}})_{(l_{1},l_{2})\in Y_{d}}\in\mathbb{G}_{0}:\,h_{l_{1},l_{2}}\in\mathbb{Z}\}.

Clearly
\mathbb{H}_{Q}\subseteq\mathbb{G}_{0}
is a normal subgroup. Let
\mathbb{J}_{Q}
denote the coset

(3.33)

\mathbb{J}_{Q}:=\{b=(b_{l_{1}l_{2}})_{(l_{1},l_{2})\in Y_{d}}\in\mathbb{G}_{0}:\,b_{l_{1},l_{2}}\in\mathbb{Z}\cap[0,Q-1]\},

with the natural induced group structure. Notice that

(3.34)

\text{ the map }(b,h)\mapsto b\cdot h\text{ defines a bijection from }\mathbb{J}_{Q}\times\mathbb{H}_{Q}\text{ to }\mathbb{G}_{0}.

Assume that
Q\geq 1
and
2^{k}\geq Q
. For any
a\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
and
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
let

(3.35)

\begin{split}&J_{k}(\xi):=2^{-k}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\chi(2^{-k}x)\mathfrak{e}[-A_{0}^{(1)}(x){.}\xi]\,dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\chi(y)\mathfrak{e}[-A_{0}^{(1)}(y){.}(2^{k}\circ\xi)]\,dy,\\
&S(a/Q):=Q^{-1}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{Q}}\mathfrak{e}[-A_{0}^{(1)}(n){.}a/Q].\end{split}

The point is that the kernels
G^{\rm low}_{k,s}
and
G_{k,s,t}
can be decomposed as tensor products. Indeed, to decompose
G_{k,s,t}
(the harder case) we set
Q:=Q_{t}=\big(\big\lfloor 2^{D(t+1)}\big\rfloor\big)!
as in (
3.24
). Then we show that if
k\geq\kappa_{t}
(so
2^{k}\gg Q_{t}^{4}
),
h\in\mathbb{H}_{Q_{t}}
and
b_{1},b_{2}\in\mathbb{G}_{0}
satisfy
|b_{1}|+|b_{2}|\leq Q^{4}
then

(3.36)

G_{k,s,t}(b_{1}\cdot h\cdot b_{2})\approx W_{k,Q_{t}}(h)V_{\mathcal{R}^{d}_{t}\setminus\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}^{d}_{Q_{s}},\mathcal{R}_{s}^{d^{\prime}},Q_{t}}(b_{1}\cdot b_{2}),

up to acceptable summable errors. Here

W_{k,Q}(h):=Q^{d+d^{\prime}}\phi_{k}(h)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}}\eta_{\leq\delta^{\prime}k}(2^{k}\circ\xi)\eta_{\leq\delta k}(2^{k}\circ\theta)\mathfrak{e}(h{.}(\xi,\theta))J_{k}(\xi)\,d\xi d\theta,

V_{\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B},Q}(b):=Q^{-d-d^{\prime}}\Big\{\sum_{\sigma^{(1)}\in\mathcal{A}\cap[0,1)^{d}}S(\sigma^{(1)})\mathfrak{e}[b^{(1)}{.}(\sigma^{(1)})]\Big\}\Big\{\sum_{\sigma^{(2)}\in\mathcal{B}\cap[0,1)^{d^{\prime}}}\mathfrak{e}[b^{(2)}.(\sigma^{(2)})]\Big\},

and
\phi_{k}(h):=\phi_{k}^{(1)}(h^{(1)})\phi_{k}^{(2)}(h^{(2)})
,
h=(h^{(1)},h^{(2)})\in\mathbb{H}_{Q}
, is defined in (
3.9
),
b=(b^{(1)},b^{(2)})\in\mathbb{G}_{0}
, and the functions
J_{k}
and
S
are defined in (
3.35
).

Finally, we show that the kernels
V_{s,t}:=V_{\mathcal{R}^{d}_{t}\setminus\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}^{d}_{Q_{s}},\mathcal{R}_{s}^{d^{\prime}},Q_{t}}
(which can be interpreted as an operator-valued Gauss sums) define bounded operators on
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{J}_{Q_{t}})
,

\|f\ast_{\mathbb{J}_{Q_{t}}}V_{s,t}\|_{\ell^{2}(\mathbb{J}_{Q_{t}})}\lesssim 2^{-t/D}\|f\|_{\ell^{2}(\mathbb{J}_{Q_{t}})}.

Moreover, the kernels
W_{k,Q_{t}}
are close to classical maximal operators and one can show that

\big\|\sup_{k\geq\kappa_{t}}|f\ast_{\mathbb{H}_{Q_{t}}}W_{k,Q_{t}}|\big\|_{\ell^{2}(\mathbb{H}_{Q_{t}})}\lesssim\|f\|_{\ell^{2}(\mathbb{H}_{Q_{t}})}.

The desired bounds (
3.31
) follow using the approximation formula (
3.36
).

4.
A nilpotent Waring theorem on the group
\mathbb{G}_{0}

The classical Waring problem, solved by Hilbert
[
31
]
in 1909, concerns the possibility of writing any positive integer as a sum of finitely many
p
powers: for any integer
p\geq 1
there is
N(p)
such that any integer
y\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
can be written in the form

(4.1)

y=\sum_{i=1}^{N(p)}x_{i}^{p},\qquad\text{ for some non-negative integers }x_{1},\ldots,x_{N(p)}.

There is a vast amount of literature on this problem and its many possible extensions. We are interested here in understanding the analogous question on our discrete nilpotent Lie group
\mathbb{G}_{0}
and for our given polynomial sequence
A_{0}
: can one represent elements
g\in\mathbb{G}_{0}
in the form

(4.2)

g=A_{0}(n_{1})^{-1}\cdot A_{0}(m_{1})\cdot\ldots\cdot A_{0}(n_{r})^{-1}\cdot A_{0}(m_{r}),

for some integers
n_{1},m_{1},\ldots,n_{r},m_{r}
, provided that
r
is large enough? We are, in fact, interested in proving a quantitative statement on the number of such representations, for integers
n_{1},m_{1},\ldots,n_{r},m_{r}\in[N]:=[-N,N]\cap\mathbb{Z}
.

We make two observations. First, many group elements
g
cannot be written in the form (
4.2
), due to local obstructions; for instance, if
g
can be represented in the form (
4.2
) then necessarily
g_{10}\equiv g_{20}\equiv\ldots\equiv g_{d0}\,(\mathrm{mod}\,2)
,
g_{10}=g_{30}=\ldots\,(\mathrm{mod}\,3)
etc. Second, there is a significant difference between the classical Waring problem (
4.1
) and its nilpotent analogue (
4.2
), namely the positivity of the
p
-powers which imposes size restrictions on the variables
x_{i}
in terms of the prescribed output value
y
.

For integers
r,N\geq 1
and
g\in\mathbb{G}_{0}
let

(4.3)

\displaystyle S_{r,N}(g):=\big|\big\{(m,n)\in[N]^{2r}:\,A_{0}(n_{1})^{-1}\cdot A_{0}(m_{1})\cdot\ldots\cdot A_{0}(n_{r})^{-1}\cdot A_{0}(m_{r})=g\big\}\big|.

Our main result in this section is the following:

Theorem 4.1
.

(i) There is an integer
r_{0}(d)\geq 1
such that if
r\geq r_{0}(d)
is sufficiently large and
g\in\mathbb{G}_{0}
then

(4.4)

\displaystyle S_{r,N}(g)

\displaystyle=N^{2r}\Big(\prod_{(l_{1},l_{2})\in Y_{d}}N^{-\lvert l_{1}\rvert-\lvert l_{2}\rvert}\Big)\Big[\mathfrak{S}(g)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d+d^{\prime}}}\Phi(\zeta)\mathfrak{e}(-(N^{-1}\circ g).\zeta)\,d\zeta+O_{r}(N^{-1/2})\Big],

uniformly in
N\in\mathbb{N}
. Here the singular series
\mathfrak{S}
is defined by

(4.5)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{S}(g):=\sum_{a/q\in\mathcal{R}_{\infty}^{d+d^{\prime}}\cap[0,1)^{d+d^{\prime}}}\overline{G(a/q)}\mathfrak{e}(-g.a/q)

and the singular integral
\Phi
is defined by

(4.6)

\displaystyle\Phi(\xi)=\int_{[-1,1]^{2r}}\mathfrak{e}\big(D(z,w).\xi\big)\,dz\,dw,\qquad\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d+d^{\prime}}.

In particular, all elements
g\in\mathbb{G}_{0}
cannot be represented in the form (
4.2
) more than a constant times the expected number of representations, i.e.

(4.7)

S_{r,N}(g)\lesssim_{r}N^{2r}\Big(\prod_{(l_{1},l_{2})\in Y_{d}}N^{-\lvert l_{1}\rvert-\lvert l_{2}\rvert}\Big)\qquad\text{ for any }g\in\mathbb{G}_{0}.

(ii) For
r_{0}(d)
as above, if
r\geq r_{0}(d)
and
r
is even, then there is a sufficiently large integer
Q=Q(r)
and an element
g_{0}\in\mathbb{J}_{Q}
(see definitions (
3.32
)–(
3.33
)) such that

(4.8)

S_{r,N}(g)=N^{2r}\Big(\prod_{(l_{1},l_{2})\in Y_{d}}N^{-\lvert l_{1}\rvert-\lvert l_{2}\rvert}\Big)\Big[c_{r}(g)+O_{r,g}(N^{-1/2})\Big],

for any
g\in g_{0}\mathbb{H}_{Q}
, where
c_{r}(g)\approx_{r}1
uniformly in
g
.

Proof.

Observe that
D(n,m)=A_{0}(n_{1})^{-1}\cdot A_{0}(m_{1})\cdot\ldots\cdot A_{0}(n_{r})^{-1}\cdot A_{0}(m_{r})
, see (
2.10
). Using the classical delta function we can write

(4.9)

S_{r,N}(g)=\sum_{m,n\in[N]^{r}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d+d^{\prime}}}\mathfrak{e}\big(D(n,m).\xi\big)\mathfrak{e}(-g.\xi)\,d\xi.

Step 1.
We start by decomposing the integration in
\xi
into major and minor arcs.
For any integer
m\geq 1
and any positive number
M>0
, we define the set of rational fractions

(4.10)

\mathcal{R}_{\leq M}^{m}:=\{a/q:\,a=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{m})\in\mathbb{Z}^{m},\,q\in[1,M]\cap\mathbb{Z},\,\mathrm{gcd}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{m},q)=1\}.

Notice that we use a bit different definition of
\mathcal{R}_{\leq M}^{m}
than in (
3.4
).
We fix a small constant
\delta=\delta(d)\ll 1
and a smooth radial function
\eta_{0}\colon\mathbb{R}^{|Y_{d}|}\to[0,1]
such that
\mathds{1}_{{|x|\leq 1}}\leq\eta_{0}(x)\leq\mathds{1}_{{|x|\leq 2}}
,
x\in\mathbb{R}^{|Y_{d}|}
. For
A>0
let
\eta_{\leq A}(x):=\eta_{0}\big(A^{-1}x\big)
,
x\in\mathbb{R}^{|Y_{d}|}
;
here we use a bit different definition of
\eta_{\leq A}
than in (
3.7
).
Then we introduce the projections

(4.11)

\displaystyle\Xi_{N}(\xi):=\sum_{a/q\in\mathcal{R}_{\leq N^{\delta}}^{d+d^{\prime}}}\eta_{\leq N^{\delta}}\big(N\circ(\xi-a/q)\big),\qquad\xi\in\mathbb{T}^{d+d^{\prime}},\quad N\in\mathbb{N},

and decompose the integration in (
4.9
) into major and minor arcs, i.e. we define

(4.12)

\displaystyle S_{r,N,\mathrm{maj}}(g)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{m,n\in[N]^{r}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d+d^{\prime}}}\mathfrak{e}\big(D(n,m).\xi\big)\mathfrak{e}(-g.\xi)\Xi_{N}(\xi)\,d\xi,

(4.13)

\displaystyle S_{r,N,\mathrm{min}}(g)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{m,n\in[N]^{r}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d+d^{\prime}}}\mathfrak{e}\big(D(n,m).\xi\big)\mathfrak{e}(-g.\xi)\big(1-\Xi_{N}(\xi)\big)\,d\xi,

Notice that
S_{r,N}(g)=S_{r,N,\mathrm{min}}(g)+S_{r,N,\mathrm{maj}}(g)
. Moreover

(4.14)

\displaystyle\lvert S_{r,N,\mathrm{min}}(g)\rvert\lesssim_{r}N^{2r-1}\Big(\prod_{(l_{1},l_{2})\in Y_{d}}N^{-\lvert l_{1}\rvert-\lvert l_{2}\rvert}\Big),\qquad N\in\mathbb{N},\quad g\in\mathbb{G}_{0}

provided that
r
is sufficiently large, as a consequence of Proposition
2.1
(i) and the Dirichlet principle; in fact we use Proposition
2.1
(i) with
\phi_{N}^{(j)}=\psi_{N}^{(j)}=\mathds{1}_{{[N]}}
,
1\leq j\leq r
, which is still valid as can be seen by careful reading of the proof of this result contained in
[
38
]
. Therefore the contribution of the minor arcs
S_{r,N,\mathrm{min}}(g)
can be absorbed by the error term in (
4.4
).

Step 2.
Next, we deal with the major arcs contributions. Notice that

(4.15)

\displaystyle S_{r,N,\mathrm{maj}}(g)=\sum_{a/q\in\mathcal{R}_{\leq N^{\delta}}^{d+d^{\prime}}\cap[0,1)^{d+d^{\prime}}}\mathfrak{e}(-g.a/q)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d+d^{\prime}}}\eta_{\leq N^{\delta}}\big(N\circ\xi\big)I_{r,N,a/q}(\xi)\mathfrak{e}(-g.\xi)\,d\xi,

where

(4.16)

\displaystyle I_{r,N,a/q}(\xi)=\sum_{m,n\in[N]^{r}}\mathfrak{e}\big(D(n,m).(a/q)\big)\mathfrak{e}\big(D(n,m).\xi\big).

Observe that for
a/q\in\mathcal{R}_{\leq N^{\delta}}^{d+d^{\prime}}\cap[0,1)^{d+d^{\prime}}
and

\lvert N\circ\xi\rvert\lesssim N^{\delta}
we have

\displaystyle I_{r,N,a/q}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m,n\in[N/q]^{r}}\sum_{u,v\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{r}}\mathfrak{e}\big(D(v,w){.}(a/q)\big)\mathfrak{e}\big(D(qn,qm).\xi\big)+O(qN^{2r-1+\delta})

\displaystyle=N^{2r}\overline{G(a/q)}\Phi(N\circ\xi)+O(qN^{2r-1+\delta}),

where
G(a/q)
is defined in (
2.16
) and
\Phi
is defined in (
4.6
).

Therefore, if
\delta\leq(10d)^{-4}
then we have

(4.17)

\begin{split}&S_{r,N,\mathrm{maj}}(g)=N^{2r}\Big(\prod_{(l_{1},l_{2})\in Y_{d}}N^{-\lvert l_{1}\rvert-\lvert l_{2}\rvert}\Big)\\
&\quad\times\Big[\sum_{a/q\in\mathcal{R}_{\leq N^{\delta}}^{d+d^{\prime}}}\overline{G(a/q)}\mathfrak{e}(-g.a/q)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d+d^{\prime}}}\eta_{\leq N^{\delta}}\big(\xi\big)\Phi(\xi)\mathfrak{e}(-g.(N^{-1}\circ\xi))\,d\xi+O_{r}(N^{-1/2})\Big].\end{split}

It follows from Proposition
2.1
(ii) and Proposition
2.2
that

(4.18)

\displaystyle\lvert G(a/q)\rvert\lesssim_{r}q^{-1/\delta^{2}},\qquad(a,q)=1,

and

(4.19)

\displaystyle\lvert\Phi(\zeta)\rvert\lesssim_{r}\langle\zeta\rangle^{-1/\delta^{2}},\qquad\zeta\in\mathbb{R}^{d+d^{\prime}},

provided that
r
is sufficiently large. Therefore, recalling the definition (
4.5
),

(4.20)

\begin{split}&|\mathfrak{S}(g)|\lesssim_{r}1,\\
&\Big\lvert\mathfrak{S}(g)-\sum_{a/q\in\mathcal{R}_{\leq N^{\delta}}^{d+d^{\prime}}}\overline{G(a/q)}\mathfrak{e}(-g.a/q)\Big\rvert\lesssim_{r}\sum_{q\geq N^{\delta}}q^{d+d^{\prime}-1/\delta^{2}}\lesssim_{r}N^{-1/(2\delta)}.\end{split}

Moreover, we have

(4.21)

\begin{split}&\Big|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d+d^{\prime}}}\eta_{\leq N^{\delta}}\big(\xi\big)\Phi(\xi)\mathfrak{e}(-g.(N^{-1}\circ\xi))\,d\xi\Big|\lesssim_{r}1,\\
&\Big|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d+d^{\prime}}}\eta_{\leq N^{\delta}}\big(\xi\big)\Phi(\xi)\mathfrak{e}(-g.(N^{-1}\circ\xi))\,d\xi-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d+d^{\prime}}}\Phi(\xi)\mathfrak{e}(-g.(N^{-1}\circ\xi))\,d\xi\Big|\lesssim_{r}N^{-1/(2\delta)}.\end{split}

It follows from (
4.17
), (
4.20
), and (
4.21
) that

(4.22)

\begin{split}&S_{r,N,\mathrm{maj}}(g)=N^{2r}\Big(\prod_{(l_{1},l_{2})\in Y_{d}}N^{-\lvert l_{1}\rvert-\lvert l_{2}\rvert}\Big)\Big[\mathfrak{S}(g)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d+d^{\prime}}}\Phi(\xi)\mathfrak{e}(-g.(N^{-1}\circ\xi))\,d\xi+O_{r}(N^{-1/2})\Big].\end{split}

The desired conclusion (
4.4
) follows using also (
4.14
). This completes the proof of part (i) of the theorem.

Step 3.
We analyze now the singular series
\mathfrak{S}
defined in (
4.5
). Observe that

(4.23)

\mathfrak{S}(h)=\sum_{q\geq 1}A(q,h),\qquad A(q,h):=\sum_{(a,q)=1}\overline{G(a/q)}\mathfrak{e}(-h.a/q),

for any
h\in\mathbb{G}_{0}
. Notice that the sequence
A(q,h)
is multiplicative in the sense that
A(q_{1}q_{2},h)=A(q_{1},h)A(q_{2},h)
provided that
(q_{1},q_{2})=1
and
h\in\mathbb{G}_{0}
. Therefore, letting
\mathbb{P}
denote the set of primes,

(4.24)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{S}(h)=\prod_{p\in\mathbb{P}}B(p,h),\qquad B(p,h):=1+\sum_{n\geq 1}A(p^{n},h).

For
h\in\mathbb{G}_{0}
and
q\geq 1
let

(4.25)

\displaystyle M(q,h):=\big|\big\{(m,n)\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{2r}:D(n,m)=h\,(\mathrm{mod}\,q)\big\}\big|.

We prove that for any
h\in\mathbb{G}_{0}
,
p\in\mathbb{P}
and integer
n\geq 1
we have

(4.26)

\displaystyle 1+\sum_{v=1}^{n}A(p^{v},h)=\frac{M(p^{n},h)}{p^{n(2r-d-d^{\prime})}}.

Indeed, for any integer
q\geq 1
we have

\displaystyle M(q,h)

\displaystyle=q^{-d-d^{\prime}}\sum_{t\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{d+d^{\prime}}}\sum_{m,n\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{r}}\mathfrak{e}\big((D(n,m)-h).(t/q)\big)

\displaystyle=q^{-d-d^{\prime}}\sum_{q_{1}|q}\sum_{w\in\mathbb{Z}_{q/q_{1}}^{d+d^{\prime}},\,(w,q/q_{1})=1}\sum_{m,n\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{r}}\mathfrak{e}\big((D(n,m)-h).(wq_{1}/q)\big)

\displaystyle=q^{-d-d^{\prime}}\sum_{q_{2}|q}\sum_{w\in\mathbb{Z}_{q_{2}}^{d+d^{\prime}},\,(w,q_{2})=1}\sum_{m,n\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{r}}\mathfrak{e}\big((D(n,m)-h).(w/q_{2})\big)

\displaystyle=q^{-d-d^{\prime}}\sum_{q_{2}|q}\sum_{w\in\mathbb{Z}_{q_{2}}^{d+d^{\prime}},\,(w,q_{2})=1}q^{2r}\overline{G(w/q_{2})}\mathfrak{e}\big(-h.(w/q_{2})\big)

\displaystyle=q^{2r-d-d^{\prime}}\sum_{q_{2}|q}A(q_{2},h).

The identity (
4.26
) follows by applying this with
q=p^{n}
,
p\in\mathbb{P}
. In particular
\mathfrak{S}(h)
and
B(p,h)
are real non-negative numbers,

(4.27)

\mathfrak{S}(h),\,B(p,h)\in[0,\infty)\qquad\text{ for any }h\in\mathbb{G}_{0},\,p\in\mathbb{P}.

We would like to show now that
\mathfrak{S}(h)\gtrsim_{r}1
for a large set of elements
h\in\mathbb{G}_{0}
, in order to be able to exploit the expansion (
4.4
). We notice first that for any integer
r
sufficiently large there is
p_{0}(r)\in\mathbb{P}
such that

(4.28)

\displaystyle 1/2\leq\prod_{p\in\mathbb{P},\,p\geq p_{0}(r)}B(p,h)\leq 3/2,

for any
h\in\mathbb{G}_{0}
, due to the rapid decay of the coefficients
G(a/q)
in (
4.18
). Moreover, using the formulas (
4.24
) and (
4.26
),

B(p,h)=\frac{M(p^{n},h)}{p^{n(2r-d-d^{\prime})}}+O_{r}(2^{-n/\delta})

for any
n\geq 1
,
h\in\mathbb{G}_{0}
and
p\in\mathbb{P}
,
p\leq p_{0}(r)
. In view of the definition (
4.25
),

\sum_{h\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{d+d^{\prime}}}M(q,h)=q^{2r}\qquad\text{ for any }q\geq 1.

Therefore we can fix
n=n(r)
sufficiently large and
h_{p^{n}}\in\mathbb{Z}_{p^{n}}^{d+d^{\prime}}
such that
B(p,h_{p^{n}})\geq 1/2
for any prime
p\leq p_{0}(r)
. Therefore we can fix

Q=Q(r)=\prod_{p\in\mathbb{P},\,p<p_{0}(r)}p^{n(r)},\qquad g_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}_{Q}^{d+d^{\prime}},\qquad g_{0}\equiv h_{p^{n}}\,(\mathrm{mod}\,p^{n}),

with the property that
B(p,g)\geq 1/2
for any prime
p<p_{0}(r)
and
g\in g_{0}\mathbb{H}_{Q}
. Thus

(4.29)

\mathfrak{S}(g)\gtrsim_{r}1\text{ for any }g\in g_{0}\mathbb{H}_{Q}.

Step 4.
Finally we analyze the contribution of the singular integral. Since

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d+d^{\prime}}}\Phi(\zeta)\mathfrak{e}(-(N^{-1}\circ g).\zeta)\,d\zeta=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d+d^{\prime}}}\Phi(\zeta)\,d\zeta+O_{r,g}(N^{-1}),

due to (
4.19
), to prove the approximate identity (
4.8
) it suffices to prove that

(4.30)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d+d^{\prime}}}\Phi(\zeta)\,d\zeta\gtrsim_{r}1.

We fix a smooth function
\chi:\mathbb{R}^{d+d^{\prime}}\to[0,1]
, satisfying
\chi(x)=1
if
|x|\leq 1/2
,
\chi(x)=0
if
|x|\geq 2
, and
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d+d^{\prime}}}\chi(x)\,dx=1
. For
\epsilon\leq\epsilon(r)
sufficiently small we write

(4.31)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d+d^{\prime}}}\Phi(\zeta)\widehat{\chi}(\epsilon\zeta)\,d\zeta=\int_{[-1,1]^{2r}}\epsilon^{-(d+d^{\prime})}\chi(D(z,w)/\epsilon)\,dzdw,

using the definition (
4.6
). In particular, by letting
\epsilon\to 0
,
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d+d^{\prime}}}\Phi(\zeta)\,d\zeta
is a real non-negative number. Moreover, the lower bound (
4.30
) follows from (
4.31
) provided that we can show that there is a point
(z_{0},w_{0})\in[-1,1]^{2r}
such that

(4.32)

D(z_{0},w_{0})=0\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\mathrm{rank}[\nabla_{\!z,w}D(z_{0},w_{0})]=d+d^{\prime}.

We notice that this follows easily from Lemma
4.2
below.
∎

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
r\geq r_{0}(d)
. Then there exists
(n,m)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2r}
such that

(4.33)

\mathrm{rank}[\nabla_{\!x,y}D(n,m)]=d+d^{\prime}.

Indeed, writing
D_{r}(x,y)=D(x,y):\mathbb{R}^{2r}\to\mathbb{G}_{0}^{\#}
, we have that

D_{r}(x,y)\cdot D_{r}(n,m)^{-1}=D_{2r}((x,m^{\prime}),(y,n^{\prime}))
with
n^{\prime}=(n_{r},\ldots,n_{1}),\ m^{\prime}=(m_{r},\ldots,m_{1})
. Assuming (
4.33
) it is clear that the map
D_{2r}((x,m^{\prime}),(y,n^{\prime}))
has maximal rank at
z_{0}=(n,m^{\prime}),\ w_{0}=(m,n^{\prime})
and (
4.32
) follows.

The proof of Lemma
4.2
is based on counting points
(n,m)\in[N]^{2r}
at which the rank of the map
\nabla_{\!x,y}D
drops. This was also crucial in obtaining the nilpotent Weyl estimate (
2.14
).

Proof of Lemma
4.2
.

Let
N
be sufficiently large with respect to
r,d
. It is enough to show that there is a constant
C_{d}>0
(
C_{d}=2d(d+d^{\prime})^{2}
works here) such that

(4.34)

|\{n\in[N]^{r}:\mathrm{rank}[\nabla_{\!x}D(n,m)]<d+d^{\prime}\}|\lesssim_{d,r}N^{(r+1)/2+C_{d}},

holds uniformly for
m\in[N]^{r}
. Fix
m\in[N]^{r}
. If

\ \mathrm{rank}[\nabla_{\!x}D(n,m)]<d+d^{\prime}
then by Cramer’s rule there exists
b_{l_{1}l_{2}}\in\mathbb{Z}
,
|b_{l_{1}l_{2}}|\lesssim N^{(2d-1)(d+d^{\prime})}
with
b_{l_{1}l_{2}}\neq 0
for at least one
0\leq l_{2}<l_{1}\leq d
, such that

(4.35)

\sum_{0\leq l_{2}<l_{1}\leq d}b_{l_{1}l_{2}}\,[\partial_{j}D(n,m)]_{l_{1}l_{2}}=0\quad\quad\textit{for all}\quad 1\leq j\leq r.

From (
2.11
) we have that
[\partial_{j}D(n,m)]_{l_{1}0}=-l_{1}n_{j}^{l_{1}-1}
, and for
1\leq l_{2}
,

(4.36)

\displaystyle[\partial_{j}D(n,m)]_{l_{1}l_{2}}

\displaystyle=l_{1}n_{j}^{l_{1}-1}\sum_{k>j}(n_{k}^{l_{2}}-m_{k}^{l_{2}})+l_{2}n_{j}^{l_{2}-1}\sum_{k<j}(n_{k}^{l_{1}}-m_{k}^{l_{1}})

\displaystyle\quad-l_{1}n_{j}^{l_{1}-1}m_{j}^{l_{2}}+(l_{1}+l_{2})n_{j}^{l_{1}+l_{2}-1}.

We want to only include terms
k\leq j
and to achieve that we introduce the parameters

T_{l}=T_{l}(n,m)=\sum_{k=1}^{r}(n_{k}^{l}-m_{k}^{l}),\quad\textit{for}\quad\quad 1\leq l<d.

Note that
T_{l}\in[-2rN^{d-1},2rN^{d-1}]
. For fixed
T=(T_{l})_{1\leq l<d}
, write

\sum_{k>j}(n_{k}^{l_{2}}-m_{k}^{l_{2}})=T_{l_{2}}(n,m)-\sum_{k\leq j}(n_{k}^{l_{2}}-m_{k}^{l_{2}}),

Substituting into (
4.36
), we obtain, up to lower degree terms in the variables
n=(n_{1},\ldots,n_{r})
,

(4.37)

[\partial_{j}D(n,m)]_{l_{1}l_{2}}=-l_{1}\sum_{k\leq j}n_{j}^{l_{1}-1}n_{k}^{l_{2}}+l_{2}\sum_{k<j}n_{j}^{l_{2}-1}n_{k}^{l_{1}}+(l_{1}+l_{2})n_{j}^{l_{1}+l_{2}-1},

for
1\leq l_{2}<d
.
Thus the system in (
4.35
) takes the form

(4.38)

\sum_{0\leq l_{2}<l_{1}\leq d}b_{l_{1}l_{2}}\,P^{j,T,m}_{l_{1}l_{2}}(n_{1},\ldots,n_{j})=0,\quad\quad 1\leq j\leq r.

Notice that for fixed
n_{1},\ldots,n_{2j-2}
with
j\leq r/2
, the left side of (
4.38
) with
j
replaced by
2j
contains the monomials
-b_{l_{1}0}l_{1}n_{2j}^{l_{1}-1}
and
b_{l_{1}l_{2}}l_{2}n_{2j}^{l_{2}-1}n_{2j-1}^{l_{1}}
, and hence is nonvanishing in the variables
n_{2j-1},n_{2j}
.
This, thanks to
[
38
, Lemma 5.3]
, implies that number of solutions to (
4.38
) is at most
2(d+d^{\prime})(N+1)
in the variables
n_{2j-1},n_{2j}
.

As the number of choices for parameters
b=(b_{l_{1}l_{2}})_{0\leq l_{2}<l_{1}\leq d}
and
T=(T_{l})_{1\leq l<d}
is
\lesssim_{r,d}N^{C_{d}}
(with, say
C_{d}=2d(d+d^{\prime})^{2}
), (
4.33
) follows.
∎

Remark 4.3
.

We remark that (
4.33
) together with the argument proving (
4.32
) also implies that the map
D_{2r}:\mathbb{R}^{4r}\to\mathbb{G}_{0}^{\#}
is surjective.
Indeed, the image of the map
D_{r}
must contain an open ball
B(g,\delta)
thus the image of
D_{2r}
must contain an open ball
B(0,\delta^{\prime})\subseteq B(g,\delta)B(g,\delta)^{-1}
centered at the origin, then by homogeneity the whole space
\mathbb{G}_{0}^{\#}
.

Finally, we have in fact shown that for
r\geq r_{0}(d)
the equations
D_{r}(n,m)=0
have a non-singular integer solution and hence a non-singular
p
-adic solutions for each prime
p
. It is well-known from a general form of Hensel’s Lemma, see
[
30
, Lemma 5.21]
, that all local factors
B(p,0)
are non-vanishing and hence the singular series
\mathfrak{S}(0)>0
. Thus Theorem
4.1
and in particular asymptotic formula (
4.8
) holds for
g=0
with
c_{r}(0)>0
. This gives the precise nilpotent analogue of the asymptotic formulae for the number of solutions to the Vinogradov system:
\sum_{j=1}^{r}(n_{j}^{l}-m_{j}^{l})=0
for all
1\leq l\leq d
, see
[
23
,
62
]
for both historical and recent breakthrough developments.
