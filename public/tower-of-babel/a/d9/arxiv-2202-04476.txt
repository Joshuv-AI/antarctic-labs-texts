Counting Kernels in Directed Graphs with Arbitrary Orientations
Bruno Jartoux

Abstract
A kernel of a directed graph is a subset of vertices that is both independent and absorbing (every vertex not in the kernel has an out-neighbour in the kernel). Not all directed graphs contain kernels, and computing a kernel or deciding that none exist is 𝐍𝐏 \mathbf{NP} -complete even on low-degree planar digraphs. The existing polynomial-time algorithms for this problem are all obtained by restricting both the undirected structure and the edge orientations of the input: for example, to chordal graphs without bidirectional edges (Pass-Lanneau, Igarashi and Meunier, Discrete Appl Math 2020) or to permutation graphs where each clique has a sink (Abbas and Saoula, 4OR 2005). By contrast, we count the kernels of a fuzzy circular interval graph in polynomial time, regardless of its edge orientations, and return a kernel when one exists. (Fuzzy circular interval graphs were introduced by Chudnovsky and Seymour in their structure theorem for claw-free graphs.) We also consider kernels on cographs, where we establish 𝐍𝐏 \mathbf{NP} -hardness in general but linear-time solvability on the subclass of threshold graphs.

2.2
The structure of kernels in fuzzy linear interval graphs

We start with some results about the structure of FLIGs and their kernels. In all that follows, fix an FLIG
D
and a nice model
(f\colon\operatorname{\mathsf{V}}(D)\to[0;1],\mathcal{I})
. We will build a weak ordering of
\operatorname{\mathsf{V}}(D)
whose restriction to each independent set (in particular, to each kernel) is a total order. Intuitively, we will then construct kernels of
D

‘guessing’

Weak vertex ordering.

The model gives

and

Further write

\displaystyle[x;y]\coloneqq\{z\in\operatorname{\mathsf{V}}(D)\colon x\preceq z\preceq y\},

Further remarks

3.1
Counting maximal and maximum independent sets

An independent set of a graph is
maximal
when it has no independent superset, and
maximum
when its cardinality is largest among independent sets. Clearly, the latter entails the former.

The kernels of a digraph with all edges bidirectional are exactly its maximal independent sets. Hence
Theorem

2
translates into polynomial-time algorithms for counting maximal independent sets, maximal independent sets of a certain size, and thus also maximum independent sets in FCIGs; see
Corollaries

2
and
3
. Compare with chordal graphs and other quasi-line graphs:

Lemma 8

(
[
OUU08
]
)
.

Counting maximal independent sets is
\mathbf{\#P}
-complete on chordal graphs.

Lemma 9
.

Counting maximum independent sets is
\mathbf{\#P}
-complete on line graphs of bipartite graphs.

Proof.

Let
G
be a bipartite graph. In polynomial time, we decide whether
G
has perfect matchings by building a largest matching (e.g. by the Ford–Fulkerson algorithm) and checking whether it is perfect. In the affirmative, the perfect matchings of
G
are the maximum independent sets of its line graph. Now an algorithm that counts the latter also counts the former, but counting the perfect matchings of bipartite graphs is
\mathbf{\#P}
-complete
[
Val79
]
.
∎

3.2
Tree-width, clique-width

The tree-width of a digraph
D
is an integer
\operatorname{tw}(D)\in\mathbf{N}
measuring its complexity on a scale on which trees are simplest
[
CE12
]
. Note that
\operatorname{tw}(D)
does not depend on edge orientations.

A set of vertices
k
being a kernel is expressible in the
monadic second-order logic of directed graphs
(see e.g.
[
ALS91
]
), for example by the formula:

\forall v\quad(v\notin k\iff\exists u\quad u\in k\land Evu),

where the relation symbol
E
encodes directed edges, so the counting version of Courcelle’s theorem
[
CE12
, Th. 6.56]
gives

Theorem 3
.

For some computable function
f
there exist algorithms that, given a digraph
D
, compute
\lvert\ker D\rvert
, and, if
\ker D\neq\varnothing
, build a largest and a smallest kernel, all of it in time
\mathcal{O}(f(\operatorname{tw}(D))\cdot\lvert\lvert D\rvert\rvert)
.

The proofs, constructive but quite involved, yield fast growing functions
f
, making said algorithms impractical. Still, this approach has interesting consequences, such as a polynomial-time algorithm for deciding the existence of kernels of size
\mathcal{O}(\log^{2}\lvert\lvert D\rvert\rvert)
in planar digraphs
[
Gut+05
]
.

As for our
Theorem

2
, there is little overlap with these results since even LIGs have arbitrarily large cliques, hence unbounded tree-width.

Clique-width
, the other most common complexity measure, is as coarse as tree-width in our orientation-agnostic setting. Indeed, unlike tree-width, clique-width strongly depends on edge orientations: if a class
\mathcal{C}
of undirected graphs has unbounded tree-width (say, if
\mathcal{C}
contains all complete graphs) then the digraph class obtained by taking all possible edge orientations on members of
\mathcal{C}
has unbounded clique-width
[
CE12
, Prop. 2.117]
. In fact, we show that the existence of kernels is
\mathbf{NP}
-complete on cographs (which, when undirected, are the graphs of clique-width at most
2
): see
Theorem

4
below. This contrasts with our
Theorem

2
, and with the positive results on adequately oriented cographs
[
AS05
]
.

Likewise,
bi-min-width
is another complexity measure with applications to kernel-finding and related problems
[
JKT22
]
which, being unbounded already on orientations of complete graphs, cannot serve our purposes.

3.3

One may define

inductively as being either a single-vertex graph, or the union of
G_{1}
and
G_{2}

4
4

4

One obtains the

Theorem 4
.

The existence of kernels is
\mathbf{NP}
-complete on cographs with bidirectional edges.

Proof of
Theorem

4
.

The problem is clearly in
\mathbf{NP}
.

Fix an instance of the Boolean satisfiability problem (SAT) in conjunctive normal form: a set of variables
\mathcal{V}
which defines a set of literals
L=\{v,\bar{v}\colon v\in\mathcal{V}\}\simeq\mathcal{V}\times\{0,1\}
, and a set of disjunctive clauses
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathcal{P}(L)
. A solution of the SAT instance is a subset
S\subset L
such that
v\in S\iff\bar{v}\notin S
for each variable
v
and
S
intersects every clause in
\mathcal{C}
.

Build a digraph on
\mathcal{C}\sqcup L
by putting a bidirectional edge between every pair of clauses in
\mathcal{C}
, and between every variable
v\in\mathcal{V}
and its negation
\bar{v}
. Also add two special vertices
\alpha
and
\omega
with an edge from
\alpha
to
\omega
. Finally, for each clause
c\in\mathcal{C}
put a directed edge from
c
to the literals that appear in
c
and to
\alpha
, as well as directed edges to
c
from the literals that do not appear in
c
and from
\omega
. The resulting digraph on
\mathcal{C}\sqcup L\sqcup\{\alpha,\omega\}
, which we will call
D
, has the following properties:

•

D
has
\lvert\mathcal{V}\rvert+\lvert\mathcal{C}\rvert+2
vertices;

•

(the undirected graph underlying)
D
is a cograph, as the join of a complete graph on
\mathcal{C}
and a perfect matching on
L\sqcup\{\alpha,\omega\}
;

•

If
k
is a kernel of
D
then
\omega\in k
and
k\subset L\sqcup\{\omega\}
. Indeed, since
\operatorname{N}^{+}(\alpha)=\{\omega\}
, either
\alpha\in k
or
\omega\in k
, but in the first case
\operatorname{N}^{+}(\omega)=\mathcal{C}\subset\operatorname{N}(\alpha)\subset\operatorname{N}(k)
contradicts the independence of

, since
\omega
must have an out-neighbour in
k
. Hence
\omega\in k
, which implies
(\mathcal{C}\sqcup\{\alpha\})\cap k=\operatorname{N}(\omega)\cap k=\varnothing
;

•

In view of the previous fact, kernels are subsets of literals (plus
\omega
) containing exactly one of

and
\bar{v}
for each variable

, and such that each

has at least one out-neighbour in the kernel. Thus the kernels of

are in bijection with the solutions of the SAT instance.

Hence this construction is a polynomial-time parsimonious reduction of SAT to the existence of kernels in cographs.
∎

We conclude by noting that the counting problem is not only polynomial but linear on a particular subclass of cographs, even though said subclass, having unbounded tree-width, does not fall under the purview of

A cograph is a

) the three cases above give

\displaystyle R(n,m)

\displaystyle\leq C+R(n-1,m)+\max_{v\in\operatorname{\mathsf{V}}(T)}(C\cdot(\deg v+n)+R(n-1,m-\deg v))

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}(m+n).

For enumerating
\ker_{\mathcal{F}}T
the same reasoning applies, except that each
‘disjoint union’
step spends constant time appending a vertex to each set in
\ker_{\mathcal{F}}T^{\prime}
. As a threshold graph,
T
has
\omega(T)
maximal independent sets
[
GR20
]
, so
\lvert\ker_{\mathcal{F}}T^{\prime}\rvert\leq\lvert\ker_{\mathcal{F}}T\rvert\leq\omega(T)
and we obtain

\displaystyle R^{\prime}(n,m)

\displaystyle\leq C+(R^{\prime}(n-1,m)+C\cdot\omega(T))

\displaystyle\quad+\max_{v\in\operatorname{\mathsf{V}}(T)}(C\cdot(\deg v+n)+R^{\prime}(n-1,m-\deg v))

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}(\omega(T)\cdot n+m).\qed

Remark 4
.

The maximal independent sets of a graph are enumerable with polynomial delay, hence, if there are
\mu
such sets, enumerable in total time
\mu\cdot n^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
. This is unlikely to extend to kernels. Even if only on cographs, a polynomial-delay algorithm for kernels would solve SAT and (because of the parsimonious reduction) an enumeration algorithm polynomial in the total size of its output would solve unambiguous-SAT. This would respectively establish
\mathbf{P}=\mathbf{NP}
and, by the Valiant–Vazirani theorem,
\mathbf{RP}=\mathbf{NP}

[
VV86
]
.

3.4
Other graph classes

Concave-round graphs.

A graph is
concave-round
if its vertices have a circular ordering in which every
closed
neighbourhood (that is,
\{x\}\sqcup\operatorname{N}(x)
) is an interval
[
BHY00
]
. Concave-round graphs are quasi-line. They form a subclass of normal circular-arc graphs
[
Saf20
]
and a proper superclass of CIGs. Unknowingly, we have already solved the problem for this class, because of the following easy but unpublished inclusion.

Remark 5
.

Concave-round graphs form a proper subclass of the fuzzy circular interval graphs. More precisely, they are either fuzzy linear interval graphs or circular interval graphs (proper circular arc graphs).

Proof.

A concave-round graph is (at least) one of circular interval and co-bipartite
[
Tuc71
,
Saf20
]
. In turn, co-bipartite graphs (i.e. graphs with a partition into two cliques) are clearly FLIGs: they admit a single-interval model, with each vertex mapped to one endpoint.
∎

interval digraphs

[

]
. However, said interval digraphs are quite different from arbitrarily-directed interval graphs: interval graphs are chordal, whereas every directed cycle is an interval digraph in the sense of
[
FHJ21
]
. Hence there is little overlap with our results.
