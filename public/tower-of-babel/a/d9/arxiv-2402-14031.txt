Autoencoder with Ordered Variance for Nonlinear Model Identification
Midhun T. Augustine, Parag Patil, Mani Bhushan, Sharad Bhartiya

Abstract
This paper presents a novel autoencoder with ordered variance (AEO) in which the loss function is modified with a variance regularization term to enforce order in the latent space. Further, the autoencoder is modified using ResNets, which results in a ResNet AEO (RAEO). The paper also illustrates the effectiveness of AEO and RAEO in extracting nonlinear relationships among input variables in an unsupervised setting.

Introduction

In general,
real-world high dimensional data is likely to concentrate in the vicinity of lower dimensional manifolds due to system constraints
[
1
]
. This has resulted in the subfield of unsupervised learning known as
dimensionality reduction
. Two major problems of interest in dimensionality reduction are:

•

P1:
Feature extraction
: where the objective is to extract lower dimensional features that are desired to be ordered in terms of reconstruction error contribution, variance, etc.

•

P2:
Model extraction
: deals with identifying relationships amongst variables of the high-dimensional data set.

In the case that linear relationships exist between the variables of the data set, both P1 and P2 can be simultaneously solved using
principal component analysis
(PCA)
[
2
,
3
,
4
]
. In PCA, the objective is to find an optimal linear transformation that maps its high-dimensional input vector (unlabeled) to a lower-dimensional latent (feature) space. Even though PCA identifies and eliminates collinearity in the latent space, this ability degrades when the functional dependence in the input variables becomes nonlinear or equivalently the input variables lie in a non-Euclidean manifold.
This leads to the introduction of nonlinear dimensionality reduction methods such as
autoencoder
(AE)
[
5
,
6
]
, principal curve
[
7
]
, Kernel PCA
[
8
,
9
]
, isomap
[
10
]
, locally linear embedding
[
11
,
12
]
, laplacian eigenmap
[
13
]
, diffusion map
[
14
]
, etc. Amongst these, AEs are popular because of their connection to neural networks and deep learning
[
15
]
. AEs use an encoder to transform its high-dimensional input vector (in input space) to a latent vector (in latent space) followed by a a decoder to reconstruct the input vector from the latent vector. The encoder and decoder are usually nonlinear functions represented using neural networks (NNs). AEs can be considered as a nonlinear counterpart of PCA. However, unlike PCA, in AEs, the latent variables are not ordered and uncorrelated. Consequently, determining the optimal size or dimension of the latent space in AE is difficult.

To address these challenges, various modifications of AEs are presented in the literature, which are mostly based on modifying the loss function with different regularization terms.
Variational AE is introduced in
[
16
]
, in which the objective is to enforce a prior distribution in the latent space by modifying the loss function with a variational Bayes term. In sparse AE
[
17
]
and k-sparse AE
[
18
]
, the objective is to have as few non-zero latent variables as possible.
Contractive AE
[
19
]
and its variants
[
20
,
21
]
use the norm of the encoder’s Jacobian as a regularization term.
Relational AE
[
22
]
uses a regularization term based on the covariance matrices of the input and reconstructed input.
Neural component analysis
[
23
]
is a combination of AE and PCA, which uses an NN as the encoder function and a linear mapping as the decoder function. Orthogonal AE is proposed in
[
24
]
in which orthogonality in the latent space is achieved using regularization. In
[
25
]
, a PCA-AE approach is proposed, which uses a covariance loss term and sequential algorithm based on progressively increasing the size of the latent space. Further,
[
26
]
proposes an efficient structuring of the latent space in AEs using Shapley values, which results in ordering based on reconstruction contribution.
Deep AEs are introduced in
[
27
]
, which use deep NNs in the encoder and decoder.
Residual networks (ResNets) are proposed in
[
28
]
, which use residual or skip connections for dealing with the performance degradation issue in deep NNs.
Recently, ResNet autoencoder (RAE) and convolutional RAE (CRAE) for image classification are introduced
[
29
]
, which use ResNets as encoder functions. Even though ordering of latent variables in terms of reconstruction error is achieved in
[
25
,
26
]
, ordering in terms of variances is not achieved in any of the above approaches.

Apart from feature extraction, an important aspect of PCA is that the residual equation in PCA represents a linear equation, and hence, equivalently, provides a linear model between the variables directly from the data set
[
3
,
4
]
. However, the extension of this idea to nonlinear model identification has not been elucidated.
Although authors in
[
30
]
presented a kernel principal component regression based nonlinear model identification and data reconciliation, it requires the data to be labeled in the form of input and output variables. Further, in kernel-based methods, the identified models are non-parametric. When it comes to AEs, the problem of model extraction has not been explored in the literature.

To summarize, the ordering of latent variables in terms of variance and
nonlinear model identification using AEs are open problems to the best of the authors’ knowledge. This motivates the current work, wherein we attempt to solve both of these open problems. For that, we introduce an
autoencoder with ordered variance
(AEO), which is then modified using ResNets (RAEO). Further, the paper illustrates the effectiveness of AEO and RAEO in extracting nonlinear relationships among the input variables. Compared to the literature, the contributions in this paper are highlighted below:

1.

The proposed AEO and RAEO with regularized loss function result in ordered latent variables in terms of decreasing variance. Here, the ordering is achieved by
modifying the loss function with a regularization term containing a non-uniform weighted sum of the latent variable variances.

2.

The AEO and RAEO extract nonlinear relationships amongst input variables in
implicit
form and are thus used for nonlinear model identification. Further, RAEO provides an opportunity to extract
explicit
nonlinear relationships when dependent variables are user-defined.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows.
Section 2 discusses the relevant preliminary concepts from PCA and AE. The proposed AEO approach is presented in Section 3, which also discusses the nonlinear model extraction using AEO with a numerical example. Section 4 presents the extension of AEO with
ResNet (RAEO) and its effectiveness in nonlinear model identification.
Section 5 illustrates the numerical implementation of AEO and RAEO approaches and the analysis of the simulation results. Finally, conclusions and future directions are discussed in Section 6.

Preliminaries

2.1
Principal Component Analysis (PCA)

PCA is a dimensionality reduction method and belongs to unsupervised learning algorithms
[
2
,
3
]
.
In PCA, the objective is to linearly transform the input data into a latent space where most of the information or variation in the data can be described with fewer dimensions. Consider
the input data:

\textbf{X}=\left[\begin{matrix}\textbf{x}_{1}&\textbf{x}_{2}&\dots&\textbf{x}_{N}\end{matrix}\right]

(1)

where
\textbf{x}=\textbf{x}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

j=1,..,N
are the
N
samples which are assumed to be mean-centered, and
\textbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times N}
. Each sample
x
consists of measurements of
n
variables.
Dimensionality reduction is achieved in PCA by linearly transforming the input vector to the latent vector:

\textbf{y}=\textbf{P}^{T}\textbf{x}

(2)

where
\textbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the input vector,
\textbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
is the latent vector,
m\leq n,
and
\textbf{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
contains
m
orthonormal columns which are also called principal components. From
\textbf{y},
the reconstructed input is computed as:

\hat{\textbf{x}}=\textbf{P}\textbf{y}=\textbf{P}\textbf{P}^{T}\textbf{x}.

(3)

where
\hat{\textbf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the reconstructed input vector. We define the latent and reconstructed data as:

\displaystyle\textbf{Y}=\left[\begin{matrix}\textbf{y}_{1}&\textbf{y}_{2}&\dots&\textbf{y}_{N}\end{matrix}\right]

(4)

\displaystyle\hat{\textbf{X}}=\left[\begin{matrix}\hat{\textbf{x}}_{1}&\hat{\textbf{x}}_{2}&\dots&\hat{\textbf{x}}_{N}\end{matrix}\right]

where
\textbf{Y}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times N},\hat{\textbf{X}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times N}
represent
N
samples of the latent vectors, and reconstructed input vectors, respectively.

Given the input data
\textbf{X},
the PCA transformation matrix
P
can be obtained by solving the
constrained
optimization problem
[
31
]
:

\displaystyle\underset{\textbf{P}}{\min}\hskip 5.69046pt{\parallel\textbf{X}-\hat{\textbf{X}}\parallel}_{F}^{2}\hskip 25.6073ptor\hskip 22.76228pt\underset{\textbf{P}}{\min}\hskip 5.69046pt{\parallel\textbf{X}-\textbf{P}\textbf{P}^{T}\textbf{X}\parallel}_{F}^{2}

(5)

\displaystyle\text{subj. to}~~\textbf{P}^{T}\textbf{P}=\textbf{I}_{m}\hskip 48.36958pt\text{subj. to}\hskip 8.5359pt\textbf{P}^{T}\textbf{P}=\textbf{I}_{m}

where the loss function is chosen for minimizing the reconstruction error and the equality constraint is used for obtaining an orthonormal transformation. If the reconstruction error in the objective function in Eq. (
5
) is negligible, then the identification of the
m
latent variables from the
n
input variables with
m<n
can be interpreted as dimensionality reduction or feature extraction.
Further,
PCA can also be used for identifying linear relationships amongst the input variables, as discussed next.

2.2
Linear Model Extraction using PCA

One of the applications of PCA is to identify the linear relationships among the input variables. Such relationships can be identified using latent variables with (nearly) zero variances, known as residual variables
[
4
]
.
Since the latent variables in PCA are ordered in terms of decreasing variance, the residual variables will correspond to the last few elements of
y
with zero variance. During model extraction using PCA, we set
m=n.
Further, we consider that the first
p
latent variables have nonzero variance (or significant) while the remaining
n-p
are with zero variance (or residual). Thus,
the first
p
principal components are treated as significant and the rest
n-p
as residual components, i.e.,
\textbf{P}=\left[\begin{matrix}\textbf{P}_{p}&\textbf{P}_{r}\end{matrix}\right]
where
\textbf{P}_{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
and
\textbf{P}_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times(n-p)}.

Based on this, the latent variables can be partitioned into:

\textbf{y}=\left[\begin{matrix}\textbf{y}_{p}\\
\textbf{y}_{r}\end{matrix}\right]=\left[\begin{matrix}\textbf{P}_{p}^{T}\\
\textbf{P}_{r}^{T}\end{matrix}\right]\textbf{x}

(6)

where
\textbf{y}_{p}\in\mathbb{P}_{p},

\textbf{y}_{r}\in\mathbb{P}_{r}
contain the significant and residual variables, and

\mathbb{P}_{p},\mathbb{P}_{r}
are subspaces of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with dimension
n
and
n-p,
respectively. The residual variables have zero mean and zero variance, which implies
\textbf{y}_{r}=0.
Substituting this in Eq. (
6
) results in:

\textbf{P}_{r}^{T}\textbf{x}=\textbf{0}

(7)

which gives a linear model containing
n-p
relationships amongst the input variables. In general, any set of
n-p
variables in
x
corresponding to linearly independent columns in
\textbf{P}_{r}
can be solved using the above equation, given the remaining
p
variables in
\textbf{x}.

We denote the subspace containing all the vectors satisfying Eq. (
7
) as
\mathbb{P}_{l}
. In PCA, the principal components are orthogonal which implies
\mathbb{P}_{p}=\mathbb{P}_{r}^{\perp}.
Further, Eq. (
7
) results in:

\mathbb{P}_{l}=\mathbb{P}_{r}^{\perp}=\mathbb{P}_{p}

(8)

In practice, the input data contains measurement noise and outliers. Consequently, the elements of
\textbf{y}_{r}
can be defined as the latent variables with
V_{y_{i}}<\epsilon
where
V_{y_{i}}
is the variance of the latent variable
y_{i}
and
\epsilon>0
is a tolerance value.
This is illustrated in Fig.
1
(b), where it can be observed that most of the data is oriented along
\textbf{p}_{1}
and the variance of the data in the direction of
\textbf{p}_{2}
is small, i.e.,
V_{y_{2}}
is on account of measurement noise and can be neglected. Further, since the data is mean-centered (centered around the origin in Fig.
1
(b)), the mean
\bar{\textbf{x}}=0\implies\bar{y}_{2}=0.
This results in:

y_{2}=\textbf{p}_{2}^{T}\textbf{x}=0

(9)

which gives a linear model as in Eq. (
7
) where
\mathbb{P}_{p}=span(\{\textbf{p}_{1}\})
and
\mathbb{P}_{r}=span(\{\textbf{p}_{2}\})
. Next, we discuss the AE as a nonlinear extension of the PCA.

2.3
Autoencoder (AE)

AEs come under unsupervised learning and are used for efficient low-dimensional representation of unlabeled data
[
5
,
6
]
.
AEs can be considered as two sequentially connected NNs that are trained together using the unlabelled input data
X
(see Fig.
2
(a)). The first NN is called the encoder, which maps the input vector into a (possibly) low-dimensional space. The second NN or decoder reconstructs the input vector from its low-dimensional representation. The encoder and decoder can be mathematically represented as:

\displaystyle\textbf{y}=\textbf{e}(\textbf{x})

(10)

\displaystyle\hat{\textbf{x}}=\textbf{d}(\textbf{y})=\textbf{d}(\textbf{e}(\textbf{x}))

where
\textbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the input vector,
\textbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
is the encoded vector or latent vector,
\hat{\textbf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the reconstructed input vector,
\textbf{e}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
is the encoder function, and
\textbf{d}:\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the decoder function. In general,
m\leq n,
whereas, in the case of dimensionality reduction,
m<n.

In AEs, the objective is to find the encoder and decoder functions that result in the reconstructed vector
\hat{\textbf{x}}
such that the reconstruction error is minimized. This results in the
unconstrained
optimization problem for AE
[
23
]
:

\underset{\textbf{e},\textbf{d}}{\min}\hskip 5.69046pt{\parallel\textbf{X}-\hat{\textbf{X}}\parallel}_{F}^{2}\hskip 19.91684ptor\hskip 19.91684pt\underset{\textbf{e},\textbf{d}}{\min}\hskip 5.69046pt{\parallel\textbf{X}-\textbf{d}(\textbf{e}(\textbf{X})\parallel}_{F}^{2}.

(11)

AEs can be considered as nonlinear extensions of PCA in which the linear transformations in Eqs. (
2
) and (
3
) are replaced by nonlinear functions in Eq. (
10
).
This is illustrated in Fig.
2
(a). Generally, AEs are suitable for datasets containing nonlinear relationships amongst input variables as in Fig.
2
(b).

Figure 2:
(a) AE block diagram (b) AE illustration.

Several variants of AEs have been proposed in the literature. Linear autoencoders (LAEs), which use linear activation functions are studied in
[
31
,
32
,
33
]
. In LAEs, the encoder and decoder functions are linear:
\textbf{e}(\textbf{x})=\textbf{W}_{1}\textbf{x}
and
\textbf{d}(\textbf{y})=\textbf{W}_{2}\textbf{y}
where
\textbf{W}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n},\textbf{W}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}.
Consequently, with suitable regularization
[
32
]
the weights in LAE can learn the principal components:
\textbf{W}_{1}=\textbf{P}^{T}
and
\textbf{W}_{2}=\textbf{P}
.
In
[
34
]
, a PCA-boosted AE approach is presented, which uses the PCA solution as the initial condition for AE.
In nonlinear PCA using AEs, the encoder and decoder are nonlinear functions such as NNs with nonlinear activation functions
[
5
,
35
]
. Generally, AEs (or PCA) can be implemented either sequentially or simultaneously
[
5
]
. The loss function for simultaneous AE based on Eq. (
11
) is:

J={\parallel\textbf{X}-\hat{\textbf{X}}\parallel}_{F}^{2}={\parallel\textbf{X}-\textbf{d}(\textbf{e}(\textbf{X}))\parallel}_{F}^{2}

(12)

where weights and biases for encoder and decoder functions are computed by optimizing the loss function.
In sequential AE
[
5
]
, a series of

m
networks containing a single latent variable are trained.
The residual
from the
i^{th}
network becomes the input for the
(i+1)^{th}
network resulting in:

\displaystyle\textbf{Y}_{i}=\textbf{e}_{i}(\textbf{X}_{i})

(13)

\displaystyle\textbf{X}_{i+1}=\textbf{X}_{i}-\hat{\textbf{X}}_{i}=\textbf{X}_{i}-\textbf{d}_{i}(\textbf{Y}_{i})

where
\textbf{X}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times N},

\textbf{Y}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times N},\hat{\textbf{X}}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times N},

i=1,2,...,m
and
\textbf{X}_{1}=\textbf{X}.

Even though sequential PCA identifies latent variables with ordered variance, the same cannot be ensured in the sequential AE. However, there exist a few approaches that ensure ordering in the latent space of AE in terms of reconstruction error
[
25
,
26
]
. In PCA-AE
[
25
]
, a regularization term with the covariance matrix
\textbf{V}_{\textbf{y}}
of the latent vector is included in the loss function:

J={\parallel\textbf{X}-\hat{\textbf{X}}\parallel}_{F}^{2}+\alpha{\parallel\textbf{V}_{\textbf{y}}\parallel}_{F}^{2},\hskip 28.45274pt\alpha>0

(14)

using which the AE is trained sequentially.
Similarly, in relational AE
[
22
]
, the relationship amongst the variables (in terms of covariances) is incorporated into the loss function as a regularization term.
Apart from this,
orthogonal AE modifies the loss function as
[
24
]
:

J={\parallel\textbf{X}-\hat{\textbf{X}}\parallel}_{F}^{2}+\alpha{\parallel\textbf{Y}^{T}\textbf{Y}-\textbf{I}_{N}\parallel}_{F}^{2},\hskip 14.22636pt\alpha>0

(15)

which imposes orthogonality on the
learned representation in the latent space.
However, none of the above approaches achieve the ordering of latent variables in terms of variance.

The extension of AEs in the direction of manifold learning is studied in contractive AE
[
19
]
and its variants
[
20
,
21
]
. The motivation behind manifold learning is the unsupervised learning hypothesis
[
1
]
, which states that real-world data in
high dimensional spaces is likely to concentrate in the vicinity of a lower dimensional non-linear manifold.
Manifold learning is achieved in contractive AE by modifying the loss
function as
[
19
]
:

J={\parallel\textbf{X}-\hat{\textbf{X}}\parallel}_{F}^{2}+\alpha{\parallel\nabla_{\textbf{x}}\textbf{y}\parallel}_{F}^{2},\hskip 14.22636pt\alpha>0

(16)

where the encoder’s Jacobian
\nabla_{\textbf{x}}\textbf{y}
is the gradient of
y
with respect to
\textbf{x}.

Here, the regularization term forces the latent variables to be constant, i.e., the gradient with respect to the input variables to be nearly zero. Generally, the manifold learning algorithms
[
10
]
-
[
12
]

are used for learning a function that transforms the input vector into a
lower-dimensional manifold in the latent space. The extension of these methods in the direction of model extraction is not explored in the literature. In summary, the use of AEs has primarily been restricted to feature extraction. The notion of order is also limited to the identification of dominant features in terms of reconstruction error. To the best of the authors’ knowledge, no attempt has been made in the literature to extract nonlinear relationships using AEs (analogous to PCA).

These motivate the current work where the main objectives are:
1.
Ordering of latent variables in terms of variance,
2.
Nonlinear model identification
using AEs. In the next section, we present the autoencoder with ordered variance (AEO)
which can achieve the above objectives.

3
Autoencoder with Ordered variance (AEO)

In this section, we propose the AEO in which the loss function is modified with a variance regularization term to enforce order in the latent space. The encoder and decoder functions for the AEO are chosen as NNs, which results in the latent and reconstructed vectors:

\displaystyle\textbf{y}=\textbf{e}_{\textbf{NN}}(\textbf{x})=\textbf{f}_{E}(\textbf{f}_{E-1}(...(\textbf{f}_{1}(\textbf{x}))))

(17)

\displaystyle\hat{\textbf{x}}=\textbf{d}_{\textbf{NN}}(\textbf{y})=\textbf{f}_{E+D}(\textbf{f}_{E+D-1}(...(\textbf{f}_{E+1}(\textbf{y}))))

where
\textbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\textbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{m},

\textbf{f}_{1},...,\textbf{f}_{E}
are layers of the encoder,
\textbf{f}_{E+1},...,\textbf{f}_{E+D}
are layers of the decoder,

E
and
D
are the number of layers in the encoder and decoder, respectively. Let the input and output of the
i^{th}
layer be denoted by
\textbf{u}_{i}
and
\textbf{v}_{i}.
Then,
each layer
\textbf{f}_{i}
can be represented as:

\textbf{v}_{i}=\textbf{f}_{i}(\textbf{u}_{i})=\bm{\sigma}_{i}(\textbf{A}_{i}\textbf{u}_{i}+\textbf{b}_{i})

(18)

where
\bm{\sigma}_{i}={\left[\begin{matrix}\sigma_{i_{1}}\\
\vdots\\
\sigma_{i_{m_{i}}}\end{matrix}\right]}
contains the element-wise activation functions for the
i^{th}
layer,
m_{i}
is the number of neurons in
i^{th}
layer,
\textbf{A}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{i}\times m_{i-1}}
is the weight matrix,
\textbf{b}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{i}}
is the bias vector,

\textbf{v}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{i}},

\textbf{u}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{i-1}},

i=1,2,..E+D
,
m_{0}=n,m_{E}=m,

m_{E+D}=n,

\textbf{u}_{1}=\textbf{x},\textbf{v}_{E}=\textbf{y},
and
\textbf{v}_{E+D}=\hat{\textbf{x}}
.

Let
A
contain the weights and biases for all layers in the encoder and decoder. Now,
the loss function for the AEO is defined as:

J={\underbrace{\alpha{\parallel\textbf{X}-\hat{\textbf{X}}\parallel}_{F}^{2}}_{J_{1}}+\underbrace{\beta{\parallel\textbf{Q}^{\frac{1}{2}}[\textbf{Y}-\bar{\textbf{Y}}]\parallel}_{F}^{2}}_{J_{2}}+\underbrace{\gamma{\parallel\textbf{A}\parallel}_{F}^{2}}_{J_{3}}}

(19)

where
\bar{\textbf{Y}}=\left[\begin{matrix}\bar{\textbf{y}}&\bar{\textbf{y}}&\dots&\bar{\textbf{y}}\end{matrix}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times N}
contains the mean latent vector
\bar{\textbf{y}}
as its elements,
\alpha,\beta,\gamma>0
are the tuning parameters, and
\textbf{Q}=diag(q_{1},q_{2},...,q_{m})
is the weighting matrix. The elements of
Q

are chosen as
0\leq q_{1}<q_{2}<...<q_{m}<\infty
so that the latent variables can be ordered in terms of decreasing variance. Here, the loss function consists of three components:

1.

Reconstruction error term

J_{1}
: denotes the sum of square error between the given data and reconstructed data.

2.

Variance regularization term

J_{2}
: is used for ordering the latent variables in terms of their variances
V_{y_{i}},
i.e.,

J_{2}=\beta\hskip 2.84544ptTrace([\textbf{Y}-\bar{\textbf{Y}}]^{T}\textbf{Q}[\textbf{Y}-\bar{\textbf{Y}}])=\beta(N-1)(q_{1}V_{y_{1}}+...+q_{m}V_{y_{m}}).

3.

Weight regularization term

J_{3}
: is used for avoiding overfitting and large weights (mostly in decoders).

Now, the optimization problem for AEO becomes:

\underset{\textbf{A}}{\min}\hskip 5.69046ptJ

(20)

where
J
is the loss function in Eq. (
19
).

We have seen that PCA can be used for extracting a lower dimensional linear model as in Eq. (
7
) directly from data. An analogous approach to extract a nonlinear model using AEO is presented next.

3.1
Extracting Nonlinear Relationships using AEO

In this section, the application of AEO in nonlinear model identification is discussed. As discussed in the context of PCA, the latent variables with
V_{y_{i}}<\epsilon
are identified using AEO, and the nonlinear relationship in the manifold
\mathbb{P}_{nl}
is extracted.
For that, the AEO with
m=n
is considered, which is trained using the available input data to identify those latent variables
y_{i},

i=p+1,...,n
whose variance
V_{y_{i}}<\epsilon
.
Without loss of generality, we assume the input data is mean-centered, and the bias terms are chosen as zero. Further, the activation function for the encoder and decoder output layers:
\bm{\sigma}_{E}
and
\bm{\sigma}_{E+D}
are chosen as linear. This simplifies
y
and
\hat{\textbf{x}}
in Eq. (
17
) as:

\displaystyle\textbf{y}=\textbf{A}_{E}\bm{\sigma}_{E-1}(\textbf{A}_{E-1}...\bm{\sigma}_{1}(\textbf{A}_{1}\textbf{x}))

(21)

\displaystyle\hat{\textbf{x}}=\textbf{A}_{E+D}\bm{\sigma}_{E+D-1}(\textbf{A}_{E+D-1}...\bm{\sigma}_{1}(\textbf{A}_{E+1}\textbf{y})).

Since the variance of latent variables in AEO is ordered,
the latent variables from
p+1
to
n
can be considered to have zero variance. Then, the elements in
y
and

\textbf{A}_{E}
can be grouped as follows:

{\textbf{y}=\left[\begin{matrix}\textbf{y}_{p}\\
\textbf{y}_{r}\end{matrix}\right]},\hskip 5.69046pt{\textbf{A}_{E}=\left[\begin{matrix}\textbf{A}_{E_{p}}\\
\textbf{A}_{E_{r}}\end{matrix}\right]}

(22)

where
\textbf{y}_{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{p},\textbf{y}_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{n-p},\textbf{A}_{E_{p}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times m_{E-1}},

\textbf{A}_{E_{r}}\in\mathbb{R}^{(n-p)\times m_{E-1}}.

Using these, the latent vector in Eq. (
21
) can be rewritten as:

\textbf{y}=\left[\begin{matrix}\textbf{y}_{p}\\
\textbf{y}_{r}\end{matrix}\right]=\left[\begin{matrix}\textbf{A}_{E_{p}}\\
\textbf{A}_{E_{r}}\end{matrix}\right]\bm{\sigma}_{E-1}(\textbf{A}_{E-1}...\bm{\sigma}_{1}(\textbf{A}_{1}\textbf{x})).

(23)

Now,
\textbf{V}_{\textbf{y}_{r}}=\textbf{0}
implies
\textbf{y}_{r}=\bar{\textbf{y}}_{r},
which results in:

\textbf{A}_{E_{r}}\bm{\sigma}_{E-1}(\textbf{A}_{E-1}...\bm{\sigma}_{1}(\textbf{A}_{1}\textbf{x}))-\bar{\textbf{y}}_{r}=\textbf{0}.

(24)

The above equation gives
n-p
nonlinear relationships among the variables in
x
in implicit form. Given an appropriate set of
p
variables in
\textbf{x},
one can solve Eq. (
24
) for the remaining
n-p
variables using numerical solvers for nonlinear system of equations
[
36
]
. The solutions of the above equation define a
p
- dimensional nonlinear manifold
\mathbb{P}_{nl}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Remark 1
.

The advantages and potential applications of AEO are listed below:

1.

AEO does not require a sequential implementation to achieve ordering, i.e., ordering is achieved with simultaneous training where all the nodes in the bottleneck layer are trained together. Ordering of latent variables has applications in the following areas:

•

Dimensionality reduction
: where ordering helps in determining the optimal size or dimension of the latent
space.

•

Image classification
: where ordering helps in prioritizing different features in an image.

2.

AEO is successful in identifying the nonlinear relationship with sufficient accuracy.
Identifying nonlinear models from data has applications in the following areas:

•

Data reconciliation

[
30
]
: where models can be used for identifying faulty sensors, improving the sensor data or noisy measurements.

•

Soft sensing
[
37
]
: where models can be used for estimating non-measurable variables from noisy measurements.

•

Process monitoring
[
38
,
39
]
: where models can be used to improve fault detection and diagnosis performance.

•

Real-time optimization
[
40
]
: where models are used to specify process operating constraints.

Remark 2
.

Even though AEO can extract nonlinear relationships, its performance depends on the parameters
\alpha,\beta,\gamma
and
\textbf{Q},
which need to be tuned properly. Apart from this, AEO has the following shortcomings:

1.

It is observed that AEO can result in the trivial solution
\textbf{A}_{E_{r}}=\textbf{0},

\bar{\textbf{y}}_{r}=\textbf{0}
(Eq. (
24
)) and in that case identifying the nonlinear relationships is not possible. One way to avoid this issue is to add constraints on
\textbf{A}_{E_{r}}
such as
{\parallel\textbf{A}_{E_{r}}\parallel}_{F}^{2}=1
and retrain AEO.

2.

The nonlinear relationship obtained from AEO is in implicit form. Even though such relationships can be solved numerically using various solvers, their accuracy depends on the initial conditions, uniqueness of solutions, etc. Further, obtaining an explicit relationship is beneficial in real-time applications such as soft sensing, data reconciliation, etc.

This motivates the extension of AEO, and in the next section, we propose the ResNet autoencoder with ordered variance (RAEO) in which a ResNet is used as encoder and decoder functions. RAEO can
extract an explicit relationship between the input variables which will be illustrated using a numerical example.

4
ResNet Autoencoder with Ordered variance (RAEO)

Residual neural networks or residual networks (ResNets) are neural networks that have a residual connection or skip connection, as shown in Fig.
4
. Two commonly used forms of ResNet functions are
[
28
]
:

1.

ResNet with a direct skip connection, denoted as
\textbf{f}_{\textbf{RN}}:

\textbf{y}=\textbf{f}_{\textbf{RN}}(\textbf{x})=\textbf{x}+\textbf{f}_{\textbf{NN}}(\textbf{x})

(27)

where
\textbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\textbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{m},

\textbf{f}_{\textbf{NN}}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m},
and
m=n.

2.

ResNet with a weighted skip connection (weighted ResNet), denoted as
\textbf{f}_{\textbf{WRN}}:

\textbf{y}=\textbf{f}_{\textbf{WRN}}(\textbf{x})=\textbf{A}_{\textbf{R}}\textbf{x}+\textbf{f}_{\textbf{NN}}(\textbf{x})

(28)

where
\textbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\textbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{m},

\textbf{A}_{\textbf{R}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n},

\textbf{f}_{\textbf{NN}}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m},
and
m\leq n.

In the proposed RAEO, the encoder and decoder functions are selected as weighted ResNets (Fig.
5
). Here
\textbf{e}_{\textbf{WRN}},\textbf{d}_{\textbf{WRN}}
stands for encoder and decoder functions with weighted ResNet.
This gives
y
and
\hat{\textbf{x}}
for RAEO as:

\displaystyle\textbf{y}

\displaystyle=\textbf{e}_{\textbf{WRN}}(\textbf{x})=\textbf{A}_{\textbf{e}}\textbf{x}+\textbf{e}_{\textbf{NN}}(\textbf{x})

(29)

\displaystyle=\textbf{A}_{\textbf{e}}\textbf{x}+\textbf{f}_{E}(\textbf{f}_{E-1}(...(\textbf{f}_{1}(\textbf{x}))))

\displaystyle\hat{\textbf{x}}

\displaystyle=\textbf{d}_{\textbf{WRN}}(\textbf{y})=\textbf{A}_{\textbf{d}}\textbf{y}+\textbf{d}_{\textbf{NN}}(\textbf{y})

\displaystyle=\textbf{A}_{\textbf{d}}\textbf{y}+\textbf{f}_{E+D}(\textbf{f}_{E+D-1}(...(\textbf{f}_{E+1}(\textbf{y}))))

where
\textbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\textbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{m},

\textbf{A}_{\textbf{e}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
and
\textbf{A}_{\textbf{d}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
. In this paper we consider
\textbf{A}_{e}
and
\textbf{A}_{d}
as fixed (user specified) parameters. The loss function for the RAEO
is chosen as in Eq. (
19
).
Now, the optimization problem for RAEO can be defined as in Eq. (
20
).

The weighted ResNet
[
28
]
in Fig.
4
(b) can be considered as a generalized ResNet, which leads to the following special cases:

1.

\textbf{f}_{\textbf{NN}}
: for which
\textbf{A}_{\textbf{R}}=\textbf{0}.

2.

\textbf{f}_{\textbf{RN}}
: for which
\textbf{A}_{\textbf{R}}=\textbf{I}_{[m\times n]}.

3.

\textbf{f}_{\textbf{PCA-RN}}
: for which
\textbf{A}_{\textbf{R}}=\textbf{P}_{[m\times n]}^{T}
where
\textbf{P}^{T}
comes from the PCA.

4.

\textbf{f}_{\textbf{WRN}}
: for which
\textbf{A}_{\textbf{R}}
is a trainable parameter.

Now, in RAEO, the encoder and decoder functions can be chosen in the form of any of the above four functions. This leads to 16 variants of RAEO, which are named as RAEO 1-1, RAEO 1-2,…, RAEO 4-4. Out of these 16 combinations, the following two versions are investigated in this paper.

1.

RAEO 1-1: for which
\textbf{A}_{\textbf{e}}=\textbf{0}
, and
\textbf{A}_{\textbf{d}}=\textbf{0}
and is same as AEO (see Section 3).

2.

RAEO 2-1: for which
\textbf{A}_{\textbf{e}}=\textbf{I}_{[m\times n]}
, and
\textbf{A}_{\textbf{d}}=\textbf{0}
.

4.1
Extracting Nonlinear Relationships using RAEO

Here, we are interested in identifying nonlinear relationships among the input variables. For that, RAEO is trained with the available input data to find the variables in
y
with zero variance.
Similar to AEO, we assume the input data is mean-centered, and the bias terms are chosen as zero. This simplifies
y
and
\hat{\textbf{x}}
for RAEO as:

\displaystyle\textbf{y}=\textbf{x}+\textbf{A}_{E}\bm{\sigma}_{E-1}(\textbf{A}_{E-1}...\bm{\sigma}_{1}(\textbf{A}_{1}\textbf{x}))

(30)

\displaystyle\hat{\textbf{x}}=\textbf{y}+\textbf{A}_{E+D}\bm{\sigma}_{E+D-1}(\textbf{A}_{E+D-1}...\bm{\sigma}_{1}(\textbf{A}_{E+1}\textbf{y})).

Let the variables from
p+1
to
n
have zero variance. Then elements in
y
and
\textbf{A}_{E}
can grouped as in Eq. (
22
). Further, the elements in
x
and
\textbf{A}_{1}
are grouped as:

\textbf{x}=\left[\begin{matrix}\textbf{x}_{p}\\
\textbf{x}_{r}\end{matrix}\right],\hskip 14.22636pt\textbf{A}_{1}=\left[\begin{matrix}\textbf{A}_{1_{p}}&\textbf{A}_{1_{r}}\end{matrix}\right]

(31)

where
\textbf{x}_{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{p},\textbf{x}_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{n-p},\textbf{A}_{1_{p}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{1}\times p},

\textbf{A}_{1_{r}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{1}\times(n-p)}.

Using this, Eq. (
29
) can be rewritten as:

\displaystyle\textbf{y}

\displaystyle={\left[\begin{matrix}\textbf{y}_{p}\\
\textbf{y}_{r}\end{matrix}\right]=\textbf{x}+\textbf{A}_{E}\bm{\sigma}_{E-1}(\textbf{A}_{E-1}...\bm{\sigma}_{1}(\textbf{A}_{1}\textbf{x}))}

(32)

\displaystyle={\left[\begin{matrix}\textbf{x}_{p}\\
\textbf{x}_{r}\end{matrix}\right]+\left[\begin{matrix}\textbf{A}_{E_{p}}\\
\textbf{A}_{E_{r}}\end{matrix}\right]\bm{\sigma}_{E-1}(\textbf{A}_{E-1}...\bm{\sigma}_{1}(\textbf{A}_{1_{p}}\textbf{x}_{p}+\textbf{A}_{1_{r}}\textbf{x}_{r}))}.

Now,
\textbf{V}_{\textbf{y}_{r}=\textbf{0}}
results in:

\displaystyle\textbf{x}_{r}+\textbf{A}_{E_{r}}\bm{\sigma}_{E-1}(\textbf{A}_{E-1}...\bm{\sigma}_{1}(\textbf{A}_{1_{p}}\textbf{x}_{p}+\textbf{A}_{1_{r}}\textbf{x}_{r}))-\bar{\textbf{y}}_{r}=\textbf{0}

(33)

which represents an implicit relationship among input variables. Further, in the case of RAEO, it is possible to obtain an explicit relationship as well where the objective is to represent
\textbf{x}_{r}
as a nonlinear function of
\textbf{x}_{p}.

For that, select
\textbf{A}_{1_{r}}=\textbf{0}
and retrain the RAEO to make
\textbf{V}_{\textbf{y}_{r}}=\textbf{0}
which modifies Eq. (
33
) to an explicit relationship between
\textbf{x}_{r}
and
\textbf{x}_{p}
as:

\textbf{x}_{r}=\bar{\textbf{y}}_{r}-\textbf{A}_{E_{r}}\bm{\sigma}_{E-1}(\textbf{A}_{E-1}...\bm{\sigma}_{1}(\textbf{A}_{1_{p}}\textbf{x}_{p})).

(34)

Here, one can also use other versions: RAEO 3-3, RAEO 4-4, etc, and derive relationships similar to Eq. (
34
).

Remark 3
.

The advantages of RAEO in comparison with AEO are as follows:

1.

The ResNet makes it easier to extract nonlinear relationships in explicit form. However, the success of getting an explicit relation cannot be guaranteed, i.e., achieving
\textbf{V}_{\textbf{y}_{r}}=\textbf{0}
with the constraint
\textbf{A}_{1_{r}}=0
may not be possible always. In such cases, one can rearrange the variables in
x
(so that the last
n-p
variables can represented as a function of the first
p
variables) and retrain RAEO.

2.

The skip connection in RAEO avoids the trivial solutions of zero weights. This can be observed from Eq. (
33
) where
\textbf{x}_{r}
comes from the input data which makes
\textbf{x}_{r}\neq 0,
in practice. Consequently, the trivial solution
\textbf{A}_{E_{r}}=\textbf{0},

\bar{\textbf{y}}_{r}=0
is avoided.

6
Conclusions

This paper proposed an autoencoder with an ordered variance of latent variables (AEO), which is then modified using ResNets (RAEO).
The performance of AEO and RAEO are compared using various numerical simulations. The simulation results show that AEO and RAEO are useful for nonlinear model identification in an unsupervised setting.
The future works are designing an algorithm for finding the optimal tuning parameters (
\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\textbf{Q}
), obtaining theoretical guarantees on model identification accuracy, and exploring other versions of RAEO.
