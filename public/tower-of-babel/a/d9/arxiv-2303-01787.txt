The curse of dimensionality for the L p L_{p} -discrepancy with finite p p
Erich Novak, Friedrich Pillichshammer

Abstract
The L p L_{p} -discrepancy is a quantitative measure for the irregularity of distribution of an N N -element point set in the d d -dimensional unit cube, which is closely related to the worst-case error of quasi-Monte Carlo algorithms for numerical integration. Its inverse for dimension d d and error threshold ε ∈ ( 0 , 1 ) \varepsilon\in(0,1) is the minimal number of points in [ 0 , 1 ) d [0,1)^{d} such that the minimal normalized L p L_{p} -discrepancy is less or equal ε \varepsilon . It is well known, that the inverse of L 2 L_{2} -discrepancy grows exponentially fast with the dimension d d , i.e., we have the curse of dimensionality, whereas the inverse of L ∞ L_{\infty} -discrepancy depends exactly linearly on d d . The behavior of inverse of L p L_{p} -discrepancy for general p ∉ { 2 , ∞ } p\not\in\{2,\infty\} has been an open problem for many years. In this paper we show that the L p L_{p} -discrepancy suffers from the curse of dimensionality for all p p in ( 1 , 2 ] (1,2] which are of the form p = 2 ​ ℓ / ( 2 ​ ℓ − 1 ) p=2\ell/(2\ell-1) with ℓ ∈ ℕ \ell\in\mathbb{N} . This result follows from a more general result that we show for the worst-case error of numerical integration in an anchored Sobolev space with anchor 0 of once differentiable functions in each variable whose first derivative has finite L q L_{q} -norm, where q q is an even positive integer satisfying 1 / p + 1 / q = 1 1/p+1/q=1 .



Keywords:
Discrepancy, numerical integration, curse of dimensionality, tractability, quasi-Monte Carlo

1
Introduction and main result

For a set
\mathcal{P}
consisting of
N
points
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{2},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{N}
in the
d
-dimensional unit-cube
[0,1)^{d}
the local discrepancy function
\Delta_{\mathcal{P}}:[0,1]^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is defined as

\Delta_{\mathcal{P}}({\boldsymbol{t}})=\frac{|\{k\in\{1,2,\ldots,N\}\ :\ {\boldsymbol{x}}_{k}\in[{\boldsymbol{0}},{\boldsymbol{t}})\}|}{N}-{\rm volume}([{\boldsymbol{0}},{\boldsymbol{t}})),

for
{\boldsymbol{t}}=(t_{1},t_{2},\ldots,t_{d})
in
[0,1]^{d}
, where
[{\boldsymbol{0}},{\boldsymbol{t}})=[0,t_{1})\times[0,t_{2})\times\ldots\times[0,t_{d})
. For a parameter
p\in[1,\infty]
the
L_{p}
-discrepancy of the point set
\mathcal{P}
is defined as the
L_{p}
-norm of the local discrepancy function
\Delta_{\mathcal{P}}
, i.e.,

L_{p,N}(\mathcal{P}):=\left(\int_{[0,1]^{d}}|\Delta_{\mathcal{P}}({\boldsymbol{t}})|^{p}\,{\rm d}{\boldsymbol{t}}\right)^{1/p}\quad\mbox{for $p\in[1,\infty)$,}

and

L_{\infty,N}(\mathcal{P}):=\sup_{{\boldsymbol{t}}\in[0,1]^{d}}|\Delta_{\mathcal{P}}({\boldsymbol{t}})|\quad\mbox{for $p=\infty$.}

Traditionally, the
L_{\infty}
-discrepancy is called star-discrepancy and is denoted by
D_{N}^{\ast}(\mathcal{P})
rather than
L_{\infty,N}(\mathcal{P})
. The study of
L_{p}
-discrepancy has its roots in the theory of uniform distribution modulo one; see
[
2
,
13
,
21
,
23
]
for detailed information. It is of particular importance because of its close relation to numerical integration. We will refer to this issue in Section
2
.

Since one is interested in point sets with
L_{p}
-discrepancy as low as possible it is obvious to study for
d,N\in\mathbb{N}
the quantity

{\rm disc}_{p}(N,d):=\min_{\mathcal{P}}L_{p}(\mathcal{P}),

where the minimum is extended over all
N
-element point sets
\mathcal{P}
in
[0,1)^{d}
. This quantity is called the
N
-th minimal
L_{p}
-discrepancy in dimension
d
.

Traditionally, the
L_{p}
-discrepancy is studied from the point of view of a fixed dimension
d
and one asks for the asymptotic behavior for increasing sample sizes
N
.
The celebrated result of Roth
[
31
]
is the most famous result in this direction and can be seen as the initial point of discrepancy theory. Later, Schmidt
[
36
]
extended Roth’s lower bound to arbitrary
p>1
. For
p\in(1,\infty)
it is known that for every dimension
d\in\mathbb{N}
there exist positive reals
c_{d,p},C_{d,p}
such that for every
N\geq 2
it holds true that

c_{d,p}\frac{(\log N)^{\frac{d-1}{2}}}{N}\leq{\rm disc}_{p}(N,d)\leq C_{d,p}\frac{(\log N)^{\frac{d-1}{2}}}{N}.

The upper bound was proven by Davenport
[
7
]
for
p=2
,
d=2
, by Roth
[
32
]
for
p=2
and arbitrary
d
and finally by Chen
[
4
]
in the general case. For more details see
[
2
]
. Explicit constructions of point sets can be found in
[
6
,
7
,
8
,
11
,
22
,
33
]
.

Similar results, but less accurate, are available also for
p\in\{1,\infty\}
. See the above references for further information. The currently best
asymptotical lower bound in the
L_{\infty}
-case can be found in
[
3
]
.

All the classical bounds have a poor dependence on the dimension
d
.
For large
d
these bounds are only meaningful in an asymptotic sense
(for very large
N
) and do not give any information about the
discrepancy in the pre-asymptotic regime (see, e.g.,
[
27
,
28
]
or
[
10
, Section 1.7]
for discussions). Nowadays, motivated by applications of point sets with low discrepancy for numerical integration, there is dire need of information about the dependence of discrepancy on the dimension.

This problem is studied with the help of the so-called inverse of
L_{p}
-discrepancy (or, in a more general context, the information complexity; see Section
2
). This concept compares the minimal
L_{p}
-discrepancy with the initial discrepancy

{\rm disc}_{p}(0,d):=\left(\int_{[0,1]^{d}}\left({\rm volume}([{\boldsymbol{0}},{\boldsymbol{t}}))\right)^{p}\,{\rm d}{\boldsymbol{t}}\right)^{1/p},

which can be interpreted as the
L_{p}
-discrepancy of the empty point set, and asks for the minimal number
N
of nodes that is necessary in order to achieve that the
N
-th minimal
L_{p}
-discrepancy is smaller than
\varepsilon
times
{\rm disc}_{p}(0,d)
for a threshold
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
. In other words, for
d\in\mathbb{N}
and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
the inverse of the minimal
L_{p}
-discrepancy is defined as

N_{p}^{{\rm disc}}(\varepsilon,d):=\min\{N\in\mathbb{N}\ :\ {\rm disc}_{p}(N,d)\leq\varepsilon\ {\rm disc}_{p}(0,d)\}.

The question is now how fast
N_{p}^{{\rm disc}}(\varepsilon,d)
increases, when
d\rightarrow\infty
and
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
.

It is well known and easy to check that for the initial
L_{p}
-discrepancy we have

{\rm disc}_{p}(0,d)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{1}{(p+1)^{d/p}}&\mbox{if $p\in[1,\infty)$},\\[5.0pt]
1&\mbox{if $p=\infty$}.\end{array}\right.

(1)

Here we observe a difference in the cases of finite and infinite
p
. While for
p=\infty
the initial discrepancy equals 1 for every dimension
d
, for finite values of
p
the initial discrepancy tends to zero exponentially fast with the dimension.

For
p\in\{2,\infty\}
the behavior of the inverse of the minimal
L_{p}
-discrepancy is well understood. In the
L_{2}
-case it is known that for all
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
we have

(1.125)^{d}(1-\varepsilon^{2})\leq N_{2}^{{\rm disc}}(\varepsilon,d)\leq 1.5^{d}\varepsilon^{-2}.

Here the lower bound was first shown by Woźniakowski in
[
38
]
(see also
[
26
,
35
]
) and the upper bound follows from an easy averaging argument, see, e.g.,
[
28
, Sec. 9.3.2]
.

In the
L_{\infty}
-case it was shown by Heinrich, Novak, Wasilkowski and Woźniakowski in
[
16
]
that there exists an absolute positive constant
C
such that for every
d\in\mathbb{N}
and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
we have

N_{\infty}^{{\rm disc}}(\varepsilon,d)\leq Cd\varepsilon^{-2}.

The currently smallest known value of
C
is
6.23401\ldots
as shown in
[
15
]
(thereby improving on other results from
[
1
,
12
,
14
,
29
]
). On the other hand, there exist numbers
c>0
and
\varepsilon_{0}\in(0,1)
such that for all
d\in\mathbb{N}
and all
\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{0})
we have

N_{\infty}^{{\rm disc}}(\varepsilon,d)\geq cd\varepsilon^{-1},

as shown by Hinrichs in
[
17
]
.

So while the inverse of
L_{2}
-discrepancy grows exponentially fast with the dimension
d
, the inverse of star-discrepancy depends only linearly on the dimension
d
. One says that the
L_{2}
-discrepancy suffers from the curse of dimensionality. In information based complexity theory the behavior of the inverse of star-discrepancy is called “polynomial tractability” (see, e.g.,
[
27
,
28
]
).

As we see, the situation is clear (and quite different) for
p\in\{2,\infty\}
. But what happens for all other
p\not\in\{2,\infty\}
? This question has been open for many years.

Just for completeness we remark that the problem has been considered also for other (semi) norms of the local discrepancy function rather than
L_{p}
-norms, that are in some sense “close” to the
L_{\infty}
-norm. Positive results (tractability) have been obtained for exponential Orlicz norms in
[
9
]
. On the other hand, for the BMO-seminorm the curse of dimensionality has been shown in
[
30
]
.

All known proofs for lower bounds on the inverse of
L_{2}
-discrepancy are based on Hilbert-space methods. A very powerful tool in this context developed in
[
26
]
(see also
[
28
, Chapter 12]
) is the method of decomposable reproducing kernels. Unfortunately, it is not obvious how these
L_{2}
-methods could be applied to the general
L_{p}
-case directly. So one has to find a new way or one has to figure out the essence of the Hilbert-space based proofs with the hope to get rid of all
L_{2}
-specific factors in order to find a way to extend these proofs to the general
L_{p}
-case. We shall follow the latter path.

Our main result is formulated in the following theorem.

Theorem 1
.

For every
p
of the form
p=\frac{2\ell}{2\ell-1}
with
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
there exists a real
C_{p}
that is strictly larger than 1, such that for all
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
we have

N_{p}^{{\rm disc}}(\varepsilon,d)\geq C_{p}^{d}(1+o(1))\quad\mbox{for $d\rightarrow\infty$}.

In particular, for all these
p
the
L_{p}
-discrepancy suffers from the curse of dimensionality.

At first glance, one would think that the result could be easily extended to any number
p\in(1,2]
by means of monotonicity of the
L_{p}
-norm and squeezing any
p
between two values of the form given in the theorem above. However, notice that we have to take the normalized discrepancy into account, which destroys monotonicity. Having a more careful look at the problem shows that it might be not so easy to follow this first intuition and in fact, so far we did not succeed in showing the curse of dimensionality for all
p\in(1,2]
.

Theorem
1
will follow from a more general result about the integration problem in the anchored Sobolev space with a
q
-norm that will be introduced and discussed in the following Section
2
. This result will be stated as Theorem
3
.

Sometimes a generalized notion of
L_{p}
-discrepancy is studied, where every point
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k}
is equipped with an own weight
a_{k}
(rather than the weight
1/N
for every point). The result from Theorem
1
even holds for this generalized
L_{p}
-discrepancy. This more general conclusion will be stated in Section
4
as Theorem
4
.

We add a conjecture: We guess that the curse holds for all
p
with
1<p<\infty
.

2
Relation to numerical integration

It is well known that the
L_{p}
-discrepancy is related to multivariate integration (see, e.g.,
[
28
, Chapter 9]
). From now on let
p,q\geq 1
be such that
1/p+1/q=1
. For
d=1
let
W_{q}^{1}([0,1])
be the space of absolutely continuous functions whose first derivatives belong to the space
L_{q}([0,1])
. For
d>1
consider the
d
-fold tensor product space which is denoted by

W_{q}^{\boldsymbol{1}}:=W_{q}^{(1,1,\ldots,1)}([0,1]^{d})

and which is the Sobolev space of functions on
[0,1]^{d}
that are once differentiable in each variable and whose first derivative
\partial^{d}f/\partial{\boldsymbol{x}}
has finite
L_{q}
-norm, where
\partial{\boldsymbol{x}}=\partial x_{1}\partial x_{2}\ldots\partial x_{d}
. Now consider the subspace of functions that satisfy the boundary conditions
f({\boldsymbol{x}})=0
if at least one component of
{\boldsymbol{x}}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})
equals 0 and equip this subspace with the norm

\|f\|_{d,q}:=\left(\int_{[0,1]^{d}}\left|\frac{\partial^{d}}{\partial{\boldsymbol{x}}}f({\boldsymbol{x}})\right|^{q}\,{\rm d}{\boldsymbol{x}}\right)^{1/q}\quad\mbox{for $q\in[1,\infty)$,}

and

\|f\|_{d,\infty}:=\sup_{{\boldsymbol{x}}\in[0,1]^{d}}\left|\frac{\partial^{d}}{\partial{\boldsymbol{x}}}f({\boldsymbol{x}})\right|\quad\mbox{for $q=\infty$.}

That is, consider the space

F_{d,q}:=\{f\in W_{q}^{\boldsymbol{1}}\ :\ f({\boldsymbol{x}})=0\ \mbox{if $x_{j}=0$ for some $j\in[d]$ and $\|f\|_{d,q}<\infty$}\},

where here and throughout this paper we write
[d]:=\{1,2,\ldots,d\}
.

Now consider multivariate integration

I_{d}(f):=\int_{[0,1]^{d}}f({\boldsymbol{x}})\,{\rm d}{\boldsymbol{x}}\quad\mbox{for $f\in F_{d,q}$}.

We approximate the integrals
I_{d}(f)
by algorithms based on
N
function evaluations of the form

A_{d,N}(f)=\varphi(f({\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}),f({\boldsymbol{x}}_{2}),\ldots,f({\boldsymbol{x}}_{N})),

(2)

where
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is an arbitrary function and where
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{2},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{N}
are points in
[0,1]^{d}
, called integration nodes. Since
f\equiv 0
belongs to
F_{d,q}
we choose
\varphi
such that
\varphi(0,0,\ldots,0)=0
. Typical examples are linear algorithms of the form

A_{d,N}^{{\rm lin}}(f)=\sum_{k=1}^{N}a_{j}f({\boldsymbol{x}}_{k}),

(3)

where
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{2},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{N}
are in
[0,1]^{d}
and
a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{N}
are real weights that we call integration weights. If
a_{1}=a_{2}=\ldots=a_{N}=1/N
, then the linear algorithm (
3
) is a so-called quasi-Monte Carlo algorithm, for which we will write
A_{d,N}^{{\rm QMC}}
.

Define the worst-case error of an algorithm (
2
) by

e(F_{d,q},A_{d,N})=\sup_{f\in F_{d,q}\atop\|f\|_{d,q}\leq 1}\left|I_{d}(f)-A_{d,N}(f)\right|.

(4)

For a quasi-Monte Carlo algorithm
A_{d,N}^{{\rm QMC}}
it is well known (see, e.g.,
[
35
]
) that

e(F_{d,q},A_{d,N}^{{\rm QMC}})=L_{p}(\overline{\mathcal{P}}),

where
L_{p}(\overline{\mathcal{P}})
is the
L_{p}
-discrepancy of the point set
1
1

1

We remark that in
[
35
]
the anchored space with anchor
\boldsymbol{1}
is considered which results in a worst case error of exactly

L_{p}(\mathcal{P})
, where
\mathcal{P}
is the node set of the QMC rule. Here we have chosen the anchor as
\boldsymbol{0}
, and therefore
in the formula for the worst-case error the point set
\overline{\mathcal{P}}
appears. For details see also
[
28
, Section 9.5.1]
for the case
p=2
.

\overline{\mathcal{P}}=\{\boldsymbol{1}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k}\ :\ k=1,2,\ldots,N\},

(5)

where
\boldsymbol{1}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k}
is defined as the component-wise difference of the vector containing only ones and
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k}
. From this point of view we now study the more general problem of numerical integration in
F_{d,q}
rather than only the
L_{p}
-discrepancy (which corresponds to quasi-Monte Carlo algorithms – although with suitably “reflected” points).

We define the
N
-th minimal worst-case error as

e_{q}(N,d):=\min_{A_{d,N}}|e(F_{d,q},A_{d,N})|

where the minimum is extended over all algorithms of the form (
2
) based on
N
function evaluations along points
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{2},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{N}
from
[0,1)^{d}
. Note that for all
d,N\in\mathbb{N}
we have

e_{q}(N,d)\leq{\rm disc}_{p}(N,d).

(6)

The initial error is

e_{q}(0,d)=\sup_{f\in F_{d,q}\atop\|f\|_{d,q}\leq 1}\left|I_{d}(f)\right|.

Lemma 2
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
and let
q\in(1,\infty]
and
p\in[1,\infty)
with
1/p+1/q=1
. Then we have

e_{q}(0,d)=\frac{1}{(p+1)^{d/p}}

and the worst-case function in
F_{d,q}
is given by
h_{d}({\boldsymbol{x}})=h_{1}(x_{1})\cdots h_{1}(x_{d})
for
{\boldsymbol{x}}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})\in[0,1]^{d}
, where
h_{1}(x)=1-(1-x)^{p}
.

Proof.

Since we are dealing with tensor products of one-dimensional spaces it suffices to prove the result for
d=1
. For
f\in F_{1,q}
we have

\int_{0}^{1}f(x)\,{\rm d}x=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{x}f^{\prime}(t)\,{\rm d}t\,{\rm d}x=\int_{0}^{1}f^{\prime}(t)g(t)\,{\rm d}t,

where
g(t)=1-t
. Applying Hölder’s inequality we obtain

\left|\int_{0}^{1}f(x)\,{\rm d}x\right|\leq\|f^{\prime}\|_{L_{q}}\|g\|_{L_{p}}=\|f\|_{1,q}\|g\|_{L_{p}}

with equality if
f^{\prime}(t)=c(g(t))^{p-1}=c(1-t)^{p-1}
for some
c
. This holds for
f(t)=h_{1}(t):=1-(1-t)^{p}
.

We have

\int_{0}^{1}h_{1}(t)\,{\rm d}t=\frac{p}{p+1}

and

\|h_{1}\|_{1,q}=\left(\int_{0}^{1}(px^{p-1})^{q}\,{\rm d}x\right)^{1/q}=p\left(\frac{1}{(p-1)q+1}\right)^{1/q}=p(1+p)^{1/p-1}.

Hence,

e_{q}(0,1)=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{h_{1}(t)}{\|h_{1}\|_{1,q}}\,{\rm d}t=\frac{1}{(p+1)^{1/p}}.

∎

Note that for all
q\in(1,\infty]
and
p\in[1,\infty)
with
1/p+1/q=1
and for all
d\in\mathbb{N}
we have

e_{q}(0,d)={\rm disc}_{p}(0,d).

Now we define the information complexity as the minimal number of function evaluations necessary in order to reduce the initial error by a factor of
\varepsilon
. For
d\in\mathbb{N}
and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
put

N^{{\rm int}}_{q}(\varepsilon,d):=\min\{N\in\mathbb{N}\ :\ e_{q}(N,d)\leq\varepsilon\ e_{q}(0,d)\}.

From (
6
), (
1
) and Lemma
2
it follows that for all
q\in(1,\infty]
and
p\in[1,\infty)
with
1/p+1/q=1
and for all
d\in\mathbb{N}
and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)^{d}
we have

N^{{\rm int}}_{q}(\varepsilon,d)\leq N^{{\rm disc}}_{p}(\varepsilon,d).

Hence, Theorem
1
follows from the following more general result.

Theorem 3
.

For every positive even integer
q
there exists a real
C=C(q)
, which depends on
q
and which is strictly larger than 1, such that for all
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
we have

N_{q}^{{\rm int}}(\varepsilon,d)\geq C^{d}(1+o(1))\quad\mbox{for $d\rightarrow\infty$}.

(7)

In particular, for all even integer
q
the integration problem in
F_{d,q}
suffers from the curse of dimensionality.

The proof of this result will be given in the following section.

Information about the base
C=C(q)
of the exponentiation in Theorem
3
will be given in Section
4
.

3
The proof of Theorem
3

The proof of Theorem
3
is based on a suitable decomposition of the worst-case function
h_{1}
from Lemma
2
. This decomposition depends on
q
and
p
, respectively, and will determine the base
C
of the exponentiation in (
7
).

Proof of Theorem
3
.

Let
q
be an even, positive interger and let
p
be such that
1/p+1/q=1
. Obviously, then
p=q/(q-1)
and
p\in(1,2]
. According to Lemma
2
the univariate worst-case function equals

h_{1}(x)=1-(1-x)^{p}

and

\|h_{1}\|_{1,q}=\frac{p}{(p+1)^{(p-1)/p}}.

Now we decompose
h_{1}
in a suitable way into a sum of three functions. For real parameters
c
and
a
, with
a\in(0,1)
and
c\in(0,a/11)
that will be determined later put

h_{1,2,(0)}(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{1-(1-x)^{p}}{2}&\mbox{if $x\in[0,c)$,}\\[5.0pt]
\frac{1-(1-c)^{p}}{2}&\mbox{if $x\in[c,a-10c)$,}\\[5.0pt]
\frac{1-(1-c)^{p}}{20c}(a-x)&\mbox{if $x\in[a-10c,a)$,}\\[5.0pt]
0&\mbox{if $x\in[a,1]$,}\end{array}\right.

and

h_{1,2,(1)}(x)={\bf 1}_{[a,1]}(x)\left((1-a)^{p}-(1-x)^{p}\right),

and

h_{1,1}(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1-(1-x)^{p}-h_{1,2,(0)}(x)&\mbox{if $x\in[0,a)$,}\\[5.0pt]
1-(1-a)^{p}&\mbox{if $x\in[a,1]$.}\end{array}\right.

Then we have

h_{1,1}(x)+h_{1,2,(0)}(x)+h_{1,2,(1)}(x)=1-(1-x)^{p}=h_{1}(x)

and
h_{1,2,(0)}(x)
and
h_{1,2,(1)}(x)
have disjoint support as well as the derivatives
h_{1,1}^{\prime}
and
h_{1,2,(1)}^{\prime}
. See Figure
1
for a graphical illustration of the decomposition.

Figure 1:
Plot of the functions
h_{1},h_{1,1},h_{1,2,(0)}
, and
h_{1,2,(1)}
for
q=4
(hence
p=4/3
) and
a=0.8
and
c=0.04
(later we will use a different choice of parameters; but with the ones chosen here one gets a better impression of the principle situation).

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
. For
\mathfrak{u}\subseteq[d]
let
\mathfrak{u}^{c}=[d]\setminus\mathfrak{u}
. Now, for
{\boldsymbol{x}}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})\in[0,1]^{d}
let

\displaystyle h_{d}({\boldsymbol{x}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^{d}h_{1}(x_{j})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^{d}(h_{1,1}(x_{j})+h_{1,2,(0)}(x_{j})+h_{1,2,(1)}(x_{j}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathfrak{u}\subseteq[d]}\prod_{j\in\mathfrak{u}^{c}}h_{1,1}(x_{j})\sum_{\mathfrak{v}\subseteq\mathfrak{u}}\prod_{j\in\mathfrak{v}}h_{1,2,(0)}(x_{j})\prod_{j\in\mathfrak{u}\setminus\mathfrak{v}}h_{1,2,(1)}(x_{j}).

We have

\|h_{d}\|_{d,q}=\|h_{1}\|_{1,q}^{d}=\left(\frac{p}{(p+1)^{(p-1)/p}}\right)^{d}.

For a point
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in[0,1]^{d}
and for
\mathfrak{u}\subseteq[d]
let
{\boldsymbol{x}}(\mathfrak{u})
denote the projection of
{\boldsymbol{x}}
on the coordinates
j\in\mathfrak{u}
.

Now let
N\in\mathbb{N}
and let
\mathcal{P}=\{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{2},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{N}\}
be a set of
N
quadrature nodes in
[0,1]^{d}

\alpha_{3}=\frac{\alpha_{2}}{\alpha_{1}}=\frac{2I_{1,2,(0)}(a^{*},c)}{\frac{p}{p+1}(1-(1-a^{*})^{p+1})-I_{1,2,(0)}(a^{*},c)},

where
c
is choosen according to (

I_{1,2,(0)}(a^{*},c)=I_{1,2,(1)}(a^{*})=\frac{p}{p+1}(1-a^{*})^{p+1}.

Hence,

\alpha_{3}=\frac{2(1-a^{*})^{p+1}}{1-2(1-a^{*})^{p+1}}

and further

\frac{\alpha_{3}}{1+\alpha_{3}}=2(1-a^{*})^{p+1}=\frac{2}{10^{q(p+1)}}\quad\mbox{ and }\quad 2^{\alpha_{3}/(1+\alpha_{3})}=4^{10^{-q(p+1)}}.

History and related problems.

During the proof of Theorem
3
we already observed that, formally, we prove a lower bound for the integration problem and a
3^{d}
-dimensional tensor product space. We give some history of such problems, in particular (in)tractability results. The first results appeared in 1997. In
[
25
]
it is proved that some non-trivial problems are easy (tractable) and for other problems the curse of dimensionality is present. An important case is the space of trigonometric polynomials of degree one in each variable; Sloan and Woźniakowski
[
34
]
prove the curse for this space. The same space, but with different norms, was studied later in
[
24
]
. To prove the curse for this norm, a new technique was developed in
[
37
]
and further studied in
[
18
]
and
[
19
]
.

As we explain in the next section, also the method of decomposable kernels (see, e.g.,
[
28
, Theorem 11.12]
)
can be seen as a contribution to this problem, even if the authors at that time did not present it that way. This technique works fine in the case of finite smoothness and is related to the technique of bump functions, see
[
20
]
for a discussion and new results. Bump functions do not work for analytic functions, see again
[
18
,
19
]
, and surprisingly they also do not work for functions of very low degree of smoothness, see
[
20
]
.

Generalized
L_{p}
-discrepancy.

Sometimes a more general version of
L_{p}
-discrepancy is considered. Here for points
\mathcal{P}=\{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{2},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{N}\}
and corresponding coefficients
\mathcal{A}=\{a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{N}\}
the discrepancy function is

\overline{\Delta}_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}}({\boldsymbol{t}})=\sum_{k=1}^{N}a_{k}{\bf 1}_{[0,{\boldsymbol{t}})}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{k})-t_{1}t_{2}\cdots t_{d}

for
{\boldsymbol{t}}=(t_{1},t_{2},\ldots,t_{d})
in
[0,1]^{d}
and the generalized
L_{p}
-discrepancy is

\overline{L}_{p,N}(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A})=\left(\int_{[0,1]^{d}}|\overline{\Delta}_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}}({\boldsymbol{t}})|^{p}\,{\rm d}{\boldsymbol{t}}\right)^{1/p}\quad\mbox{for $p\in[1,\infty)$,}

with the usual adaptions for
p=\infty
. If
a_{1}=a_{2}=\ldots=a_{N}=1/N
, then we are back to the classical definition of
L_{p}
-discrepancy from Section
1
.

Now the
N
-th minimal generalized
L_{p}
-discrepancy in dimension
d
is defined as

\overline{{\rm disc}}_{p}(N,d):=\min_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}}\overline{L}_{p}(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A})

where the minimum is extended over all
N
-element point sets
\mathcal{P}
in
[0,1)^{d}
and over all corresponding coefficients
\mathcal{A}
, and its inverse is

N_{p}^{\overline{{\rm disc}}}(\varepsilon,d):=\min\{N\in\mathbb{N}\ :\ \overline{{\rm disc}}_{p}(N,d)\leq\varepsilon\ \overline{{\rm disc}}_{p}(0,d)\},

where, obviously,
\overline{{\rm disc}}_{p}(0,d)={\rm disc}_{p}(0,d)
.

Turn to the integration problem in
F_{d,q}
, then the worst-case error (
4
) of a linear algorithm (
3
) is

e(F_{d,q},A_{d,N}^{{\rm lin}})=\overline{L}_{p}(\overline{\mathcal{P}},\mathcal{A}),

where
\overline{\mathcal{P}}
is given in (
5
) and
\mathcal{A}
consists of exactly the coefficients from the given linear algorithm. Again, this is well known (see
[
28
]
or
[
35
]
).

With these preparations it follows from Theorem
3
that also the generalized
L_{p}
-discrepancy suffers for all

p
of the form
p=\frac{2\ell}{2\ell-1}
with
\ell\in\mathbb{N}

from the curse of dimensionality.

Theorem 4
.

For every
p
of the form
p=\frac{2\ell}{2\ell-1}
with
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
there exists a real
C_{p}
that is strictly larger than 1, such that for all
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
we have

N_{p}^{\overline{{\rm disc}}}(\varepsilon,d)\geq C_{p}^{d}(1+o(1))\quad\mbox{for $d\rightarrow\infty$}.

In particular, for all these
p
the generalized
L_{p}
-discrepancy suffers from the curse of dimensionality.

5
The case
p=q=2
revisited and an outlook on the general problem

As already mentioned, the case
p=q=2
is well established and can be tackled with the method of decomposable kernels (see
[
26
]
and also
[
28
, Chapter 12]
). This method can be mimicked with the method presented here by taking the decomposition

\displaystyle h_{1,1}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(2-a)\min(x,a),

\displaystyle h_{1,2,(0)}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{1}_{[0,a]}(x)x(a-x),

\displaystyle h_{1,2,(1)}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{1}_{[a,1]}(x)\left(x(2-x)-a(2-a)\right)

for some
a\in(0,1)
(compare this with
[
28
, p. 192]
). See Figure
2
.

Figure 2:
Plot of the functions
h_{1},h_{1,1},h_{1,2,(0)}
, and
h_{1,2,(1)}
for
q=p=2
and
a=4^{1/3}/(1+4^{1/3})
.

Define the fooling function
g_{d}
like in (
8
), but with the present decomposition, it follows almost immediately from orthogonality arguments that
\|g_{d}\|_{d,2}\leq\|h_{d}\|_{d,2}
. Choosing
a=4^{1/3}/(1+4^{1/3})
(compare again with
[
28
, p. 192]
) gives in addition that
\int_{0}^{1}h_{1,2,(0)}(x)\,{\rm d}x=\int_{0}^{1}h_{1,2,(1)}(x)\,{\rm d}x
such that the whole proof of Theorem
3
goes through with the present decomposition, but now one obtains the improved quantity

2^{\alpha_{3}/(1+\alpha_{3})}=1.08332\ldots,

i.e.,

N_{2}^{{\rm int}}(\varepsilon,d)\geq(1.08332)^{d}(1+o(1)),

which is exactly the value obtained in
[
28
, p. 193]
. This reproves the result for
p=q=2
, but without using the method of decomposable kernels.

From this point of view, the following decomposition for general
q\in(1,\infty)
would be somehow obvious:

\displaystyle h_{1,1}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1-(1-a)^{p}}{a}\min(x,a),

\displaystyle h_{1,2,(0)}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{1}_{[0,a]}(x)\left((1-(1-x)^{p})-\frac{x}{a}(1-(1-a)^{p})\right),

\displaystyle h_{1,2,(1)}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{1}_{[a,1]}(x)\left((1-(1-x)^{p})-(1-(1-a)^{p})\right).

Choosing
a=2^{\frac{p}{p+1}}/(1+2^{\frac{p}{p+1}})
one obtains again that
\int_{0}^{1}h_{1,2,(0)}(x)\,{\rm d}x=\int_{0}^{1}h_{1,2,(1)}(x)\,{\rm d}x
. However, choosing the fooling function
g_{d}
like in (
8
) the problem is to show the estimate
\|g_{d}\|_{d,q}\leq\|h_{d}\|_{d,q}
. As soon as this could be done, one can tackle the general
q
case with the proof method from Theorem
3
.
