1.
introduction and preliminaries

Gröbner bases of an ideal, as special kinds of generating sets of the ideal, give rise to computational tools to determine many properties of the ideal. It is one of the main practical tools for solving systems of polynomial equations and computing the images of algebraic varieties under projections or rational maps. Gröbner bases were introduced in 1965, together with
an algorithm to compute them (Buchberger’s algorithm), by Bruno Buchberger in his Ph.D. thesis. This notion has been introduced for ideals in the polynomial rings and extended to various noncommutative algebras such as free non-commutative algebra and exterior algebra
[
2
]
.

Let
I
be an ideal in the polynomial ring
R=\mathbb{K}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
. We denote by
\mathrm{Mon}(R)
the set of all monomials in
R
, that is the set
\{x_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots x_{n}^{\alpha_{n}}\colon\;\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}\}
. Let
\leq
be a monomial ordering on
R
by which we mean
\leq
is a total ordering on
\mathrm{Mon}(R)
such that:

(i)

1<u
for all
1\neq u\in\mathrm{Mon}(R)
;

(ii)

if
u,v\in\mathrm{Mon}(R)
and
u<v
, then
uw<vw
for all
w\in\mathrm{Mon}(R)
.

Some well-known monomial orders are lexicographic
\leq_{\mathrm{lex}}
, degree lexicographic
\leq_{\mathrm{dlex}}
, and degree reverse lexicographic
\leq_{\mathrm{revlex}}
ordering (see
[
1
]
for detailed descriptions).

Let
\leq
be a fixed monomial ordering on
\mathrm{Mon}(R)
and
\mathbb{N}_{\circ}
be the set of non-negative integers. If
f=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{\circ}^{n}}a_{\alpha}\textbf{x}^{\alpha}
is a nonzero polynomial of
R
with each
a_{\alpha}\in\mathbb{K}
, then the initial monomial of
f
with respect to
\leq
is the biggest monomial with respect to
\leq
among the monomials belonging to
\mathrm{supp}(f):=\{\mathbf{x}^{\alpha}\colon\;a_{\alpha}\neq 0\}
. Let
\mathrm{in}_{\leq}(f)
be the initial monomial of
f
with respect to
\leq
. If
I
is a nonzero ideal of
R
, the initial ideal of
I
with respect to
\leq
is the monomial ideal of
R
generated by
\{\mathrm{in}_{\leq}(f)\colon\;0\neq f\in I\}
. Let
\mathrm{in}_{\leq}(I)
denote the initial ideal of
I
, i.e.

\mathrm{in}_{\leq}(I)=(\mathrm{in}_{\leq}(f)\colon\;0\neq f\in I).

Definition
.

Let
I
be a nonzero ideal of
R
. A finite set of nonzero polynomials
\{g_{1},\ldots,g_{s}\}
where
g_{i}\in I
is said to be a Gröbner basis of
I
with respect to
\leq
if the initial ideal
\mathrm{in}_{\leq}(I)
of
I
is generated by the monomials
\mathrm{in}_{\leq}(g_{1}),\ldots,\mathrm{in}_{\leq}(g_{s})
.

Let
\leq
be a monomial ordering on
R
. The following facts are well-known in the theory of Gröbner bases:

•

Gröbner bases always exist for any ideal
I
of
R
. Moreover, any Gröbner basis of
I
can be transformed into a reduced Gröbner basis
\{g_{1},\ldots,g_{m}\}
in the following sense:

–

the coefficient of
\mathrm{in}_{\leq}(g_{i})
in
g_{i}
is
1
, for all
1\leq i\leq m
,

–

If
i\neq j
, then none of the monomials belonging to
\mathrm{supp}(g_{j})
is divisible by
\mathrm{in}_{\leq}(g_{i})
.

•

If
\mathcal{G}
is a Gröbner basis of
I
then so is
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
for any
\mathcal{G}\subseteq\mathcal{G}^{\prime}\subseteq I
.

•

Any Gröbner basis of
I
is a set of generators for
I
.

•

If
\mathcal{G}=\{g_{1},\ldots,g_{s}\}
is a Gröbner basis of
I
and if
f_{1},\ldots,f_{s}\in I
are nonzero polynomials with
\mathrm{in}_{\leq}(f_{i})=\mathrm{in}_{\leq}(g_{i})
, then
\{f_{1},\ldots,f_{s}\}
is also a Gröbner basis of
I
.

•

If
\mathcal{G}=\{g_{1},\ldots,g_{s}\}
is a Gröbner basis of
I
, then every polynomial
f\in R
has a unique remainder
r
with respect to
g_{1},\ldots,g_{s}
(see
[
7
, Lemma 2.2.3]
). In this case, we say that
f
reduces to
r
with respect to
g_{1},\ldots,g_{s}
.

•

If
\mathcal{G}=\{g_{1},\ldots,g_{s}\}
is a Gröbner basis of
I
, then a nonzero polynomial
f\in R
belongs to
I
if and only if the unique remainder of
f
with respect to
g_{1},\ldots,g_{s}
is
0
.

•

If
f,g_{1},\ldots,g_{s}
are non-zero polynomials in
R
, then division algorithm (see e.g.
[
7
, Theorem 2.2.1]
) guarantees the existence of the following standard form

f=f_{1}g_{1}+\cdots+f_{s}g_{s}+r

for
f
, where

(a)

if
r\neq 0
and
u\in\mathrm{supp}(r)
, then none of the initial monomials
\mathrm{in}_{\leq}(g_{1}),\ldots,\mathrm{in}_{\leq}(g_{s})
divides
u
,

(b)

if
f_{i}\neq 0
, then
\mathrm{in}_{\leq}(f)\geq\mathrm{in}_{\leq}(f_{i}g_{i})
.

Let
\mathcal{G}=\{f_{1},\ldots,f_{s}\}
be a set of generators of
I
. The Buchberger’s criterion provides an efficient way to check whether
\mathcal{G}
is a Gröbner basis of
I
or not. Given nonzero polynomials
f,g\in R
, let
c_{f}
and
c_{g}
denote the coefficients of
\mathrm{in}_{\leq}(f)
and
\mathrm{in}_{\leq}(g)
in
f
and
g
, respectively. The polynomial

S(f,g)=\frac{\mathrm{lcm}(\mathrm{in}_{\leq}(f),\mathrm{in}_{\leq}(g))}{c_{f}\cdot\mathrm{in}_{\leq}(f)}f-\frac{\mathrm{lcm}(\mathrm{in}_{\leq}(f),\mathrm{in}_{\leq}(g))}{c_{g}\cdot\mathrm{in}_{\leq}(g)}g

is called the
S
-polynomial of
f
and
g
. We now come to the most important theorem in the theory of Gröbner bases.

Theorem 1.1

(Buchberger’s criterion)
.

Let
I
be a nonzero ideal of
R
and
\mathcal{G}=\{g_{1},\ldots,g_{s}\}
be a system of generators of
I
. Then
\mathcal{G}
is a Gröbner basis of
I
if and only if
S(g_{i},g_{j})
reduces to
0
with respect to
g_{1},\ldots,g_{s}
, for all
i\neq j
.

The Buchberger criterion (Theorem
1.1
) supplies an algorithm to compute a Gröbner basis starting from a system of generators of an ideal. This algorithm is so-called as Bughberger’s algorithm. In general, the complexity of this algorithm is of double exponential order. Finding an explicit and quick algorithm for computing a Gröbner basis of some families of ideals is very important in the context of computational commutative algebra. The aim of this paper is to compute explicitly a Gröbner basis of LSS-ideals associated to trees.

Let
\mathbb{K}
be a field,
n
be a positive integer, and set
[n]=\{1,\ldots,n\}
. Let
\Gamma=([n],E)
be a simple graph with vertex set
[n]
and edge set
E
. In
[
6
]
and later in
[
5
,
8
]
, the authors study the graded ideal

L_{\Gamma}^{\mathbb{K}}(d)=\left(x_{i,1}x_{j,1}+\cdots+x_{i,d}x_{j,d}\colon\quad\{i,j\}\in E({\Gamma})\right)

in the polynomial ring
\mathbb{K}[x_{i,k}\colon\;i=1,\ldots,n,\;k=1,\ldots,d]
. The ideal
L_{\Gamma}^{\mathbb{K}}(d)
is called the
Lovász-Saks-Schrijver ideal
,
LSS-ideal
for short, of
\Gamma
with respect to
\mathbb{K}
. For
d=1
, the ideal
L_{\Gamma}^{\mathbb{K}}(d)
is a square-free monomial ideal known as the
edge ideal
of
\Gamma
. The algebraic properties of LSS-ideal, such as being prime, radical, and complete intersection are studied in
[
5
,
6
]
. Also, in
[
6
]
the (minimal) primary decomposition of
L_{\Gamma}^{\mathbb{K}}(2)
has been presented in terms of combinatorial properties of
\Gamma
. Let
S=\mathbb{K}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}]
. In
[
8
, Corollary 3.6]
the author shows that
L_{\Gamma}^{\mathbb{K}}(2)
is a complete intersection if and only if the parity binomial edge ideal
\mathcal{I}_{\Gamma}\subseteq S
of
\Gamma
, defined as below, is a complete intersection:

\mathcal{I}_{\Gamma}=\left(x_{i}x_{j}-y_{i}y_{j}\colon\quad\{i,j\}\in E(\Gamma)\right).

Recall that an ideal
I\subseteq R
is called
complete intersection
if
I
is generated by a sequence
a_{1},\ldots,a_{m}
such that
a_{i}
is not a zero-divisor of
R/(a_{1},\ldots,a_{i-1})
, for
i=1,\ldots,m
. Note that
L_{\Gamma}^{\mathbb{K}}(2)=\mathcal{I}_{G}
if
\mathrm{char}(\mathbb{K})=2
. However, it is shown in
[
8
]
that the graded Betti numbers
\beta_{i,j}(S/L_{\Gamma}^{\mathbb{K}}(2))
and
\beta_{i,j}(S/\mathcal{I}_{\Gamma})
coincide in any characteristic. It turns out that the algebraic invariants of the LSS-ideal and parity binomial ideal (such as dimension, depth, regularity, projective dimension, etc.) are the same.

When
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{R}
the vanishing set of the ideal
L_{\Gamma}^{\mathbb{K}}(d)
coincides with the variety of orthogonal representations of complement of
\Gamma
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Orthogonal representations of graphs were introduced by Lovász in 1979
[
10
]
, and it is shown that they are intimately related to important combinatorial properties of graphs (see
[
9
, Chapter 10]
). An
orthogonal representation
of
\Gamma
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
assigns to each
i\in[n]
a vector
u_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
u^{T}_{i}u_{j}=0
, whenever
\{i,j\}\in\bar{E}=\binom{[n]}{2}\setminus E
. The variety of orthogonal representations was studied in the works of Lovász, Saks, and Schrijver
[
11
,
12
]
. The reference
[
10
]
is a nice reference for a comprehensive treatment of orthogonal representations, their varieties, and their relations to important combinatorial properties of graphs.

We find a Gröbner basis of
L_{T}^{\mathbb{K}}(2)
with respect to lexicographic ordering induced by
x_{1}>\cdots>x_{n}>y_{1}>\cdots>y_{n}
when
T
is a tree, that is a connected graph without any cycle. Theorem
2.1
shows that Gröbner basis of
L_{T}^{\mathbb{K}}(2)
can be described by paths of even and odd length in
T
. We show in Corollary
2.2
that Gröbner basis has a simple form if we restrict ourselves to a monomial ordering induced by an ascending labeling on vertices of
T
. It turns out that the Gröbner basis of
L_{T}^{\mathbb{K}}(2)
(and hence its Hilbert series and Krull dimension) with respect to this ordering is independent of the choice of the ground field
\mathbb{K}
. Also, in Theorem
3.2
and Proposition
3.4
, we present explicit formulas for the Krull dimension of
S/L_{T}^{\mathbb{K}}(2)
in terms of the combinatorics of
T
. Finally, in Corollary
3.3
, we show that the Krull dimension of
S/L_{T}^{\mathbb{K}}(2)
is bounded below by
n+1
and bounded above by
n-1+p(T)
where
p(T)
is the number of pendants of
T
. Recall that a pendant vertex in
T
is a vertex which has a unique neighbour in
T
. For the graph theoretical concepts in this paper, the reader may refer to
[
3
]
.

2.
Gröbner basis of LSS-ideal of trees

Let

Corollary 2.2
.

Let

be a tree with an ascending labeling. Then

\mathcal{G}:=\{g_{P}\colon\quad P\emph{isapathofoddlengthin}T\}\cup\{g^{\prime}_{P,\varnothing}\colon\quad P\emph{isapathofevenlengthin}T\}

with respect to the lexicographic ordering induced by

Proof.

First observe that for any path in

one of the end points has the maximum label among the other vertices of the path. From the proof of Theorem
2.1
one observes that

and

reduce to zero modulo

for all paths

and

and

,

reduces to zero modulo

and

3.
Krull dimension of LSS-ideals of trees

The aim of this section is to present a formula for the Hilbert series of LSS-ideals of trees and the Krull dimension of the quotient ring. To this end, we use the fact that
S/I
and
S/\mathrm{in}_{\leq}(I)
share the same Hilbert series for all monomial orderings
\leq
(see
[
7
, Proposition 2.2.5]
). In this regard, Corollary
2.2
provides us with a simple description of
\mathrm{in}_{\leq}(I)
. Since
\mathrm{in}_{\leq}(L_{T}^{\mathbb{K}}(2))
is a square-free monomial ideal, it is the Stanley-Reisner ideal of a simplicial complex.

A collection
\Delta
of subsets of a set
V=\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}\}
is a
simplicial complex
if

(a)

\{v_{i}\}\in\Delta
for all
i
;

(b)

if
F\in\Delta
, then all subsets of
F
are also in
\Delta
(including the empty set).

The elements of
\Delta
are called
faces
and the maximal faces under inclusion are called
facets
of
\Delta
. A subset
F\subseteq[n]
is called a
non-face
of
\Delta
if
F\notin\Delta
.

The
dimension
of a face
F
is
\dim F=|F|-1
, where
|F|
denotes the cardinality of
F
and the
dimension
of
\Delta
,
\dim(\Delta)
, is defined as

\dim(\Delta)=\max\{\dim F\colon F\in\Delta\}.

Let
\Delta
be a simplicial complex of dimension
d-1
on the vertex set
V
and
f_{i}=f_{i}(\Delta)
denote the number of faces of
\Delta
of dimension
i
. Thus, in particular
f_{-1}=1
and
f_{0}=n
. The sequence

\textbf{f}(\Delta):=\left(f_{0},\ldots,f_{d-1}\right)

is called the
f
-
vector
of
\Delta
.

To each simplicial complex
\Delta
on the vertex set
V
we associate a square-free monomial ideal
I_{\Delta}\subset\mathbb{K}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
, called the Stanley-Reisner ideal of
\Delta
, defined as follows:

I_{\Delta}=(\prod_{i\in F}x_{i}\colon\,F\notin\Delta).

Now, let
R:=\mathbb{K}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
and
J\subseteq(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})^{2}
be a square-free monomial ideal. Let
\mathrm{Supp}(u):=\{i\in[n]\colon x_{i}\mid u\}
for any monomial
u\in R
and let
\Delta(J)
be the set of all subsets
A
of
[n]
for which
\mathrm{Supp}(u)\nsubseteq A
for all
u\in\mathcal{G}(J)
. Then
\Delta(J)
is a simplicial complex and
I_{\Delta(J)}=J
, hence

\mathrm{Hilb}\left(\frac{R}{J},t\right)=\sum_{i=0}^{d}f_{i-1}\left(\frac{t}{1-t}\right)^{i},

(1)

where
d=\dim\Delta(J)+1
and
f_{i-1}=f_{i-1}(\Delta(J))
for
i=0,\ldots,d
(see
[
4
, Theorem 5.1.7]
). In order to compute
\mathrm{Hilb}(R/J,t)
, we note from the exact sequence

0\longrightarrow\frac{R}{J\colon u}(-d)\overset{\cdot u}{\longrightarrow}\frac{R}{J}\longrightarrow\frac{R}{J+(u)}\longrightarrow 0

that

\mathrm{Hilb}\left(\frac{R}{J},t\right)=t^{d}\mathrm{Hilb}\left(\frac{R}{J\colon u},t\right)+\mathrm{Hilb}\left(\frac{R}{J+(u)},t\right).

(2)

For any monomial
u\in R
, define

J\odot_{i}u:=\begin{cases}J\colon u,&i=0,\\
J+(u),&i=1.\end{cases}

Accordingly, for any
f\in\{0,1\}^{[n]}
we set

J_{f}:=J\odot_{f(1)}x_{1}\odot\cdots\odot_{f(n)}x_{n}

and put
A_{f}:=f^{-1}(0)
and
B_{f}:=f^{-1}(1)
. Clearly,

J_{f}=\begin{cases}(x_{i}\colon i\in B_{f}),&A_{f}\in\Delta(J),\\
(1),&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

that is
\Delta(J)=\{A_{f}\subseteq[n]\colon J_{f}\neq(1)\}
.

Remark 2
