Reverse PAC Codes: Look-ahead List Decoding
Xinyi Gu, Mohammad Rowshan, Member, IEEE , and Jinhong Yuan, Fellow, IEEE

Abstract
Convolutional precoding in polarization-adjusted convolutional (PAC) codes is a recently introduced variant of polar codes. It has demonstrated an effective reduction in the number of minimum weight codewords (a.k.a error coefficient) of polar codes. This reduction has the potential to significantly improve the error correction performance. From a codeword formation perspective, this reduction has limitations. Capitalizing on the understanding of the decomposition of minimum-weight codewords, this paper studies reverse precoding that can effectively reduce minimum-weight codewords more than in PAC codes. We propose a look-ahead list decoding for the reverse PAC codes, which has the same order of complexity as list decoding in PAC codes. Through numerical analysis, we demonstrate a notable reduction in error coefficients compared to PAC codes and polar codes, resulting in a remarkable improvement in the block error rate, in particular at high code rates.



I
INTRODUCTION

Polarization-adjusted convolutional (PAC) codes
[
1
]
are a variant of polar codes
[
2
]
resulting from the convolutional pretransformation before polar coding.
The pre-transformation in PAC coding can reduce the number of minimum weight codewords of underlying polar codes due to the impact on the formation of minimum weight codewords
[
3
]
and involvement of forzen coordinates carrying non-zero values. This reduction is expected to improve the performance of PAC codes under (near) ML decoders, such as the list decoder
[
4
,
5
,
6
]
, sequential decoders
[
5
]
and sphere decoder
[
7
]
, and could be considered for adaptation in 6G
[
8
]
.
The coset-wise study on the reduction of minimum weight codewords in PAC coding
[
9
]
revealed that there are limitations to this reduction, particularly, when the contribution of frozen rows in codeword formation is lacking, or they do not possess a special property. One approach to overcome this drawback is to design a pre-coder that breaks these limitations.
Different pre-transformations (or precoders) have been suggested in the literature such as the ones based on dynamic frozen bits or parity bits, the ones based on CRC bits, and finally, the ones based on the idea of pre-transformation in PAC coding
[
3
,
10
,
11
,
12
,
13
,
14
]
. However, they do not perform the precoding from the perspective of the minimum-weight codeword formation. A detailed introduction of polar codes, PAC codes, and their variations can be found in
[
8
, Section VII]
.
In our previous work
[
15
]
, we introduced a precoding scheme for polar codes that can reduce the number of minimum weight codewords, considerably more than the PAC coding. The main idea is to involve more frozen rows in the formation of codewords through row combinations, particularly in cosets that are characterized as incapable of reducing the minimum weight codewords through precoding
[
9
, Lemma 2]
.

In this work, we further elaborate on the proposed precoding scheme in
[
15
]
and name it reverse PAC (RPAC) codes. The shortcoming of
[
15
]
was the lack of a low-complexity decoding algorithm for these codes. Since de-mapping of outer code (which is the inverse of precoding) is performed in the oppoite direction of decoding of inner code, the conventional successive cancellation list (SCL) decoding
[
4
]
cannot be used for the RPAC codes. To address this issue, we propose a look-ahead list (LA-SCL) decoding for RPAC codes which employs two decoding trees: 1) a tree similar to the list decoding of polar codes representing pre-transformed information, and 2) a tree representing the information sequence before the pre-transformation. The latter tree, called a look-ahead tree, considers a few steps ahead of the current stage, taking into account the branch expansions corresponding to the reverse convolution.
Numerical results demonstrate that with the LA-SCL decoder, RPAC codes outperform CRC-Polar codes and PAC codes under conventional list decoding for high-rate short codes, while maintaining the same order of complexity.

II-A

Polar Codes and PAC Codes

Polar codes of length
N=2^{n}
are constructed based on the
n
-th Kronecker power of binary Walsh-Hadamard matrix

\mathbf{G}_{2}={\footnotesize\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
1&1\end{bmatrix}}
, that is,
\boldsymbol{G}_{N}=\mathbf{G}_{2}^{\otimes n}=[\mathbf{g}_{0}\;\;\mathbf{g}_{2}\;;\cdots\;\;\mathbf{g}_{N-1}]^{T}
which we call it
polar transform
throughout this paper. A generator matrix of the polar code is formed by selecting the rows
\mathbf{g}_{i},i\in\mathcal{I}
of
\boldsymbol{G}_{N}
. Then,
\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{I})
denotes such a linear code. Note that
\mathcal{I}\subseteq[0,N-1]=[0,2^{n}-1]
. The characterization of the information set
\mathcal{I}
for polar codes is based on the channel polarization theorem
[
2
]
and the concept of
bit-channel reliability
. The indices in
\mathcal{I}
are dedicated to information bits, while the indices in
\mathcal{I}^{c}\triangleq[0,N-1]\setminus\mathcal{I}
are used to transmit a known value, ’0’ by default, which are called
frozen bits
and the corresponding rows are frozen rows.

In PAC coding
[
1
]
, however, we have a pre-transformation stage before polar coding where the input vector
\mathbf{u}=[u_{0},\ldots,u_{N-1}]
for polar coding is obtained
by a convolutional transformation using the binary generator polynomial of degree
s
, with coefficients
\mathbf{p}=[p_{0},\ldots,p_{s}]
as follows:

u_{i}=\sum_{\ell=0}^{s}p_{\ell}v_{i-\ell},

(1)

where
\mathbf{v}=[v_{0},\ldots,v_{N-1}]
is the vector constructed based on
\mathcal{I}
.
This coding scheme is called
polarization-adjusted convolutional (PAC)
coding. The convolution operation can be represented in the form of an upper triangular matrix
[
3
]
where the rows of the
pre-transformation matrix

\mathbf{P}
are formed by shifting the vector
\mathbf{p}=(p_{0},p_{1},\ldots p_{s})
one element at a row.
Note that
p_{0}=p_{s}=1
by convention. Then, we can obtain
\mathbf{u}
by matrix multiplication as
\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{v}\mathbf{P}
.

Due to this precoding, we would have
u_{i}\in\{0,1\}
for
i\in\mathcal{I}^{c}
, indicating that
u_{i}
correpsoding to a frozen
v_{i}=0,i\in\mathcal{I}^{c}
may no longer be fixed.
Consequently, the vector
\mathbf{u}
is mapped to codeword
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{u}\mathbf{G}_{N}
. Overall, we obtain
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{v}\mathbf{P}\mathbf{G}_{N}
. It was analytically shown in
[
3
,
16
]
that by convolutional pre-transformation, the number of minimum weight codewords, a.k.a error coefficient which is denoted by
A_{\mathrm{w}_{\min}}
where
\mathrm{w}_{\min}
is the minimum weight, may significantly decrease relative to polar codes (without pre-transformation). Hence, from the union bound
[
17
, Sect. 10.1]
, we expect that this reduction potentially improves the block error rate (BLER) of a (near) maximum likelihood decoding for a binary input additive white Gaussian noise (BI-AWGN) channel, particularly at high signal-to-noise ratios (SNRs).

II-B

Minimum Weight Codewords in Cosets

In the conventional PAC coding, forward convolution as per (
1
) is performed. Although forward convolution can reduce the number of codewords of minimum weight relative to polar codes
[
3
]
, it has its own limitations. To show the limitations, we first partition all codewords, excluding the all-zero codeword, of a polar code
\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{I})
into
cosets
defined as:

Definition 1
.

Cosets: Given information set
\mathcal{I}\subseteq[0,N-1]
for a polar code, we define the set of codewords
\mathcal{C}_{i}(\mathcal{I})\subseteq\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{I})
for each
i\in\mathcal{I}
in a coset of the subcode
\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{I}\setminus[0,i])
of
\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{I})
as

\mathcal{C}_{i}(\mathcal{I})\triangleq\left\{\mathbf{g}_{i}+\sum_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\mathbf{g}_{h}\colon\mathcal{H}\subseteq\mathcal{I}\setminus[0,i]\right\}\subseteq\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{I}),

(2)

where
\mathbf{g}_{i}
is the
coset leader
. We denote the number of minimum weight codewords of the coset
\mathcal{C}_{i}
by
A_{i,\mathrm{w}_{\min}}(\mathcal{I})
. The total number of minimum weight codewords for a polar code
\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{I})
is
A_{\mathrm{w}_{\min}}=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}A_{i,\mathrm{w}_{\min}}(\mathcal{I})
.

Observe that the coordinate of the first non-zero element in vector
\mathbf{u}
,
i=\min\{\supp(\mathbf{u})\}
, while encoding by
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{u}\mathbf{G}_{N}
plays a key role in classifying the codewords into cosets. According to
[
9
, Lemma 1]
, this coordinate remains the same after precoding by
\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{v}\mathbf{P}
. That is,

\min\{\supp(\mathbf{u})\}=\min\{\supp(\mathbf{v})\}.

(3)

Nevertheless, the resulting
u_{j}
for
j>i,j\in\mathcal{I}^{c}
might be
u_{j}\not=0
, unlike in polar coding. This difference may impact the number of minimum weight codewords in the cosets due to the inclusion of rows
\mathbf{g}_{j}
for
j\in\mathcal{I}^{c}\cap[i,N-1]
in row combinations. Note that the weight of the codewords in a coset
\mathcal{C}_{i}
depends on the set of row indices
\mathcal{H}=\{h:h>i\}
in (
2
). Furthermore, according to
[
16
, Corollary 5]

\w(\mathbf{g}_{i}+\sum_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\mathbf{g}_{h})\geq\w(\mathbf{g}_{i}),

(4)

there exists minimum weight codeword
\mathbf{c}\in\mathcal{C}_{i}
, where
\w(\mathbf{c})=\mathrm{w}_{\min}
, only if
i\in\mathcal{B}
where set
\mathcal{B}
is defined as

\mathcal{B}=\{j:j\in\mathcal{I},w(\mathbf{g}_{j})=\mathrm{w}_{\min}\}.

(5)

Observe that we have
\mathcal{H}\subseteq[i+1,N-1]
in PAC coding whereas in polar coding, we have
\mathcal{H}\subseteq[i+1,N-1]\backslash\mathcal{I}^{c}
. The minimum distance of the PAC codes is
[
9
, Lemma 1]

d_{\min}=\mathrm{w}_{\min}=\min(\{\w(\mathbf{g}_{i}):i\in\mathcal{I}\}).

(6)

According to
[
16
, Theorem 1]
, the minimum weight codewords are uniquely formed by the following row combinations:

\w\big(\mathbf{g}_{i}+\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}\mathbf{g}_{j}+\sum_{m\in\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{J})}\mathbf{g}_{m}\big)=\mathrm{w}_{\min},

(7)

where
\w(\mathbf{g}_{i})=\mathrm{w}_{\min}
,
\mathcal{J}\subseteq\mathcal{K}_{i}
and
\mathcal{K}_{i}
is
[
16
, Lemma 2.a]

\mathcal{K}_{i}\triangleq\{j\in\mathcal{I}\backslash[0,i]\colon|\supp(j)\backslash\supp(i)|=1\}.

(8)

As a result, every subset of
\mathcal{K}_{i}
along with other rows in (
7
) form a minimum weight codeword.
The number of subsets of
\mathcal{K}_{i}
is given by
2^{|\mathcal{K}_{i}|}
. Given
\mathcal{B}\triangleq\{i\in\mathcal{I}:\w(\mathbf{g}_{i})=\mathrm{w}_{\min}\}
, the total number of minimum-weight codewords of the polar code will be
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}}2^{|\mathcal{K}_{i}|}
.
The set
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{J})
is a function of the set
\mathcal{J}
and every
m\in\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{J})
has the property (see
[
16
, (9),(10)]
for a detailed definition of
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{J})
):

\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{J})\!\subseteq\!\{m\!>\!i:|\supp(\bin(m))\backslash\supp(\bin(i))|\!>\!1\}.

(9)

The relation (
7
) can be extened such that the sets
\mathcal{J}
and
\mathcal{M}
also intersect with
\mathcal{I}^{c}
(see
[
9
, (18)]
). This is useful when considering the impact of precoding.
Now, let us see the main limitation of the forward convolution in PAC coding, that forward convolution cannot reduce their minimum weight codewords.

Lemma 1
.

(
[
9
, Lemma 2]
) For any coset
\mathcal{C}_{i}(\mathcal{I})
where

1.

\mathcal{I}^{c}\cap(i,N-1]=\emptyset
, or

2.

|\supp(\bin(f))\!\backslash\!\supp(\bin(i))|\!=\!1,\forall f\!\in\!\left(\mathcal{I}^{c}\!\cap\!(i,N\!-\!1]\right)
,

we have

A_{i,\mathrm{w}_{\min}}(\mathbf{G},\mathcal{I})=A_{i,\mathrm{w}_{\min}}(\mathbf{PG},\mathcal{I}).

In other words, any cosets
\mathcal{C}_{i}
where there is no frozen row
\mathbf{g}_{f}
for
f\in\mathcal{I}^{c}\cap(i,N-1]
such that
|\supp(\bin(f))\backslash\supp(\bin(i))|>1
, we get
A_{i,\mathrm{w}_{\min}}=

2^{\left|\mathcal{K}_{i}\right|}
in the PAC coding, independently of the choice of
\mathbf{p}
.

III
Reverse PAC (RPAC) Codes

To tackle the limitations described for the characterised cosets in Lemma
1
and further reduce the total number of minimum weight codewords
A_{\mathrm{w}_{\min}}
, the general idea is to change the coset leader
\mathbf{g}_{i}
in (
2
) through a different precoding scheme such that the coordinate of the first non-frozen element in
\mathbf{u}
is not preserved, unlike (
3
). That is, we need a pre-transformation matrix
\mathbf{P}^{\prime}
that gives
\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{v}\mathbf{P}^{\prime}
where

\min\{\supp(\mathbf{u})\}\not=\min\{\supp(\mathbf{v})\}.

To further refine the problem, for any pair of
(i,i^{\prime})
where
i^{\prime}=\min\{\supp(\mathbf{v})\}
and
i=\min\{\supp(\mathbf{u})\}
, we need to have

1.

w(\mathbf{g}_{i})\!\geq\!w(\mathbf{g}_{i^{\prime}})
. Observe that if we have
w(\mathbf{g}_{i})\!<\!w(\mathbf{g}_{i^{\prime}})
, according to (
4
), the minimum distance
d_{min}
after precoding might decrease. Note that the resulting coset may be led by a frozen row
\mathbf{g}_{i},i\in\mathcal{I}^{c}
.

2.

i<i^{\prime}
. Observe that if we have
i>i^{\prime}
, we may lose some of the information bits.

Remark 1
.

Letting
\min\{\supp(\mathbf{u})\}<\min\{\supp(\mathbf{v})\}
moves the coset leader from
\mathbf{g}_{i^{\prime}}
to
\mathbf{g}_{i}
where
i<i^{\prime}
. This will affect the minimum weight codewords in the coset
\mathcal{C}_{i}
as follows:

a)

If
\w(\mathbf{g}_{i})>\w(\mathbf{g}_{i^{\prime}})
, according to (
4
), we have
A_{i,\mathrm{w}_{\min}}=0
.

b)

If
\w(\mathbf{g}_{i})=\w(\mathbf{g}_{i^{\prime}})
and the conditions of Lemma
1
are violated, then
A_{i,\mathrm{w}_{\min}}(\mathbf{PG},\mathcal{I})<A_{i,\mathrm{w}_{\min}}(\mathbf{G},\mathcal{I})
.

III-A

Practical Approach for Designing the Pre-transform

There might be different ways to implement the general approach proposed above. In this section, we propose a practical scheme to realize the proposed approach.

Our scheme is based on designing a mapping function
\mathbf{v}\rightarrow\mathbf{u}
such that we get
\min\{\supp(\mathbf{u})\}<\min\{\supp(\mathbf{v})\}
, and then form the corresponding pre-transformation matrix
\mathbf{P}^{\prime}
. The requirements for the mapping are as follows:

•

u_{i}=1
for
i<i^{\prime}
where
i^{\prime}=\min\{\supp(\mathbf{v})\}
,

•

u_{j}\in\{0,1\}
for
j>i
,

•

u_{j}\leftarrow v_{j}+\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}}p_{k}v_{k}
where
\mathcal{K}\subset[i^{\prime},N-1]\backslash\{j\}
and
p_{k}\in\{0,1\}
.

To make the scheme tractable, we constrain the scope of
\mathcal{K}\subset[i^{\prime},N-1]\backslash\{j\}
by selecting
\mathcal{K}=[j+1,j+s]
for some
s
. Hence, we can write this mapping function in
\mathbb{F}_{2}
as

u_{i}=\sum_{\ell=0}^{s}p_{\ell}v_{i+\ell}.

(10)

The vector
\mathbf{p}=[p_{0},p_{1},\cdots,p_{s}]
is similar to the one used in the conventional precoding of PAC codes. However, the matrix form of the proposed precoder has a lower triangular shape; that is,
\mathbf{P}^{\prime}=\mathbf{P}^{T}
. From linear algebra, we know that the product of two lower (or upper) triangular matrices is a lower (or upper) triangular matrix. Hence, knowing
\mathbf{G}_{N}
is a lower triangular matrix, we can conclude that
\mathbf{P}^{\prime}\mathbf{G}_{N}
is also a lower triangular matrix.

Furthermore, to fulfill the first requirement mentioned in Section
III
as
\w(\mathbf{g}_{i})\geq\w(\mathbf{g}_{i^{\prime}})
and to avoid the reduction in
\mathrm{w}_{\min}
, we need to constrain the precoder as follows:

\text{$u_{i}$}=\begin{cases}\sum_{\ell=0}^{s}p_{\ell}v_{i+\ell}&if $\w(g_{i})\geq\mathrm{w}_{\min}$\\
v_{i}&otherwise\\
\end{cases}.

(11)

As can be seen in (
11
), the mapping to
u_{i}
is based on
[v_{i}\;v_{i+1}\;\cdots\;v_{i+s}]
which are placed ahead of coordinate
i
. Thus, the convolution is performed in
reverse
direction, the opposite of the forward direction in the PAC coding. Hence, this scheme is called reverse PAC or in short RPAC. We use the notion RPAC(
s+1
) to denote the constraint length,
s+1
, of the reverse precoder.
Now, let us see a simple example that illustrates the difference between precoding in PAC codes and the proposed RPAC codes.

Example 1
.

Consider the polar code (64,14) with
d_{min}=16
and
\mathcal{I}=\{31,46,47,51,53\!-\!55,57\!-\!63\}
where ’
-
’ indicates a range of integers. Given a vector
\mathbf{v}
with
v_{i}=0
for every
i\in\mathcal{I}
except for
v_{54}=1
, then the minimum weight of codewords obtained by row combinations for
polar coding where
\mathbf{p}=[1]
, PAC coding with
\mathbf{p}=[1\;1\;0\;1\;1\;0\;1\;1\;0\;1]
, and RPAC coding with the same
\mathbf{p}
are:

\text{Polar:}\w(\mathbf{v}\mathbf{P}\mathbf{G}_{N}=\mathbf{g}_{54})=16,\vskip-5.0pt

\text{PAC:}\w(\mathbf{v}\mathbf{P}\mathbf{G}_{N}=\mathbf{g}_{54}+\mathbf{g}_{56})=16,\vskip-5.0pt

\text{RPAC:}\w(\mathbf{v}\mathbf{P}^{\prime}\mathbf{G}_{N}=\mathbf{g}_{43}+\mathbf{g}_{54})=24.

Note that in the RPAC coding, the frozen row
\mathbf{g}_{43}
with the index
43<54
is involved in the row combination. This effectively increases the weight of the RPAC codeword to 24.

IV
Look-ahead List Decoding for RPAC

Reverse PAC codes have an advantage over PAC codes in terms of the number of minimum weight codewords. However, this advantage brings about challenges in decoding due to the opposite directions of pre-transformation and decoding.
A trivial solution is to employ generic decoders to guess the coordinate(s) of errors. Alternatively, sphere decoding can be considered, which is performed from the last bit towards the first bit. However, these candidate decoders exhibit high and variable computational complexity, and consequently high and non-constant latency and energy consumption.
Here, we propose a look-ahead list decoding algorithm that expands the
\mathbf{v}
sequence on each path by
s
bits ahead of the current stage
i
. We use
L
to denote the list size of the LA-SCL decoder.

The look-ahead list decoding can be considered as the expansion of search trees. We use two trees to represent the expansions: 1) a main tree for
\mathbf{u}
, corresponding to the tree in conventional list decoding, where the leaves represent stage
i
. 2) a parallel binary tree for
\mathbf{v}
, affiliated with the main tree, looks ahead for stage
i+s
. We refer to this tree as the
look-ahead tree
. This tree maintains the same number of paths as the main tree but extends
\mathbf{v}
-values for
s
stages ahead, corresponding to each path.
It’s important to note that the look-ahead tree only represents binary values of
\mathbf{v}
, while the
L
independent SC processes with different intermediate LLRs are retained on the main tree.
During decoding, at stage
i
of the main tree, the binary tree for
\mathbf{v}
expands first by examining stage
i+s
. If
i+s\in\mathcal{I}
, each path expands with two possibilities:
\hat{v}_{i+s}=0
and
\hat{v}_{i+s}=1
. Considering the sequences of:

[\hat{v}_{i}\;\;\hat{v}_{i+1}\;\;\cdots\;\;\hat{v}_{i+s}],

(12)

the estimated
\mathbf{v}
becomes the input to the convolutional transform in (
11
). The output
\hat{u}_{i}
corresponds to the paths on the main tree.
The path metrics are computed based on the values of
\hat{u}_{i}
, which are then fed back to the SC process to conduct the partial sums for intermediate LLRs.
As we move from stage
i
to stage
i+1
on the main tree, we extend the leaves of the look-ahead part of the tree by one branch from
i+s
to
i+s+1
and append the stage
i+1
to the main tree. The convolution in (
11
) for
\hat{u}_{i+1}
and partial sums for intermediate LLRs are computed accordingly.

For the LA-SCL decoder, at stage
i=0
, the affiliated tree needs to look ahead for
s
stages, requiring initialization.
The initialization of the trees is determined by the number of information bit coordinates within
v_{0}^{s}
, denoted by
\nu
. This initialization has two conditions: a) If the first information bit coordinate, denoted by
\mathcal{I}_{0}
, satisfies
\mathcal{I}_{0}\geq s
, the look-ahead tree undergoes an expansion process similar to the conventional expansion of the list decoder for PAC codes. When the looking-ahead sequence
\hat{v}_{i+s}
encounters
\mathcal{I}_{0}
, the look-ahead tree initiates expansion into two paths of
\hat{v}_{i+s}=0
and
\hat{v}_{i+s}=1
.
b) However, if
\mathcal{I}_{0}<s
in very high code rate conditions, the look-ahead tree needs to consider all the possible combinations of
[\hat{v}_{\mathcal{I}_{0}}\;\cdots\;\hat{v}_{s}]
. It will be initialized into a tree with a depth of
s-\mathcal{I}_{0}+1
, and the minimum required list size becomes
L_{min}=2^{\nu}
.

Note that the look-ahead list decoding does not increase the list size compared to the conventional list decoding.
Every path on the main tree for
\hat{u}_{i}
corresponds to the paths on the look-ahead tree for
\hat{v}_{i+s}
in a one-to-one manner. The SC process is performed based on the main tree and the intermediate and decision LLRs are calculated for the
L
paths up to stage
i
.
In stage
i+1
(corresponding to the main tree), we extend every path of the look-ahead tree by appending
\hat{v}_{i+s+1}\in\{0,1\}
. Then, the corresponding
\hat{u}_{i+1}
on the main tree are computed based on the expanded
\hat{v}_{i+s+1}
. The number of the paths on both trees becomes
2L
. When
L\geq L_{max}
, paths with the largest path metrics on the main tree, along with the corresponding paths on the look-ahead tree, are pruned.
Therefore, since the tree expansion of the main tree is similar to conventional list decoding and the look-ahead tree is expanded in parallel to keep track of
\mathbf{v}
-values ahead of stage
i
, the order of complexity remains the same, i.e.
O(L\cdot N\log_{2}N)
.

To clarify this matter, let’s consider an example.

Example 2
.

Given
s=3
and
\mathbf{p}=[1\;0\;\;1\;\;1]
, suppose that we are in stage
i=0
and performing look-ahead list decoding with list size
L=4
. We consider two initialization conditions with different information sets:
\mathcal{I}=\{3,\;4\}
and
\mathcal{I}=\{2,\;3,\;4\}
. The corresponding decoding trees are illustrated in Fig.
1
.

For
\mathcal{I}=\{3,\;4\}
, look-ahead tree initiates expansion until it reaches
\hat{v}_{3}
. An example of normal initializations of the trees are depicted in Fig.
1
(a). At stage
i=0
, the initialization of the look-ahead tree is marked in green.
While for
\mathcal{I}=\{2,\;3,\;4\}
, two information bit coordinates are included within the range of
\mathcal{I}_{0}
to
s
, i.e.
\nu=2
. Consequently,
all possible paths of
[\hat{v}_{2},\;\hat{v}_{3}]
needs to be listed. This special initialization, where
\mathcal{I}_{0}<s
, is shown in Fig.
1
(b).
The main tree for
\hat{u}_{i}
is expanded based on the look-ahead trees, resulting in the same number of paths. Moving forward to stage
i=1
,
\hat{v}_{4}
is expanded into two possible values of
\hat{v}_{4}\in\{0,\;1\}
for all existing paths, which is the same as the conventional SCL decoder, but with
s

VI
CONCLUSION

In this paper, we introduce the reverse PAC coding and its associated look-ahead list decoding.
The RPAC codes have a remarkably smaller number of minimum-weight codewords compared to polar codes and conventional PAC codes for high-rate codes.
The proposed LA-SCL decoder overcomes the challenge posed by the opposite directions of demapping and decoding in RPAC codes. With a similar complexity as conventional list decoding, the LA-SCL decoder for RPAC codes outperforms CRC-Polar codes and PAC codes under conventional list decoding for high-rate short codes.
