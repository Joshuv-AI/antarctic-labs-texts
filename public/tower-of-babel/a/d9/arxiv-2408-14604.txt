Co-factor analysis of citation networks
Alex Hayes

Abstract
One compelling use of citation networks is to characterize papers by their relationships to the surrounding literature. We propose a method to characterize papers by embedding them into two distinct “co-factor” spaces: one describing how papers send citations, and the other describing how papers receive citations. This approach presents several challenges. First, older documents cannot cite newer documents, and thus it is not clear that co-factors are even identifiable. We resolve this challenge by developing a co-factor model for asymmetric adjacency matrices with missing lower triangles and showing that identification is possible. We then frame estimation as a matrix completion problem and develop a specialized implementation of matrix completion because prior implementations are memory bound in our setting. Simulations show that our estimator has promising finite sample properties, and that naive approaches fail to recover latent co-factor structure. We leverage our estimator to investigate 237,794 papers published in statistics journals from 1898 to 2022, resulting in the most comprehensive topic model of the statistics literature to date. We find interpretable co-factors corresponding to many statistical subfields, including time series, variable selection, spatial methods, graphical models, GLM(M)s, causal inference, multiple testing, quantile regression, resampling, semi-parametrics, dimension reduction, and several more.



Keywords:
co-factor models, spectral network analysis, matrix completion, missing data, stochastic blockmodels

Introduction

Suppose we have a collection of written documents, and these documents cite each other. For example, the documents might be academic papers, judicial opinions, or patents, among other possibilities. One useful way to understand individual documents in the collection, and the collection as a whole, is to find documents that cite, and are cited, in similar ways. These documents are likely to be about the same subject, and can thus reveal information about important topics in the corpus.

In this paper, we develop a network-based approach to understanding the structure in citation corpora. We first represent the documents as a network, where each document corresponds to a node, and citations between documents correspond to directed edges. We then develop a network factorization technique to find groups of similar documents. In particular, we develop a spectral technique
(
Athreya et al. 2018
;
Ji and Jin 2016
;
Jin 2015
;
Lei and Rinaldo 2015
;
Le et al. 2016
;
Rohe et al. 2011
;
von Luxburg et al. 2008
;
von Luxburg 2007
)
that embeds each document into two distinct latent spaces, one characterize how papers cite, and the other characterizing how papers get cited.

Unlike prior approaches to citation analysis, we model citations from older documents to newer documents as structurally missing (the rationale behind this choice is discussed extensively in Section
2
). As a consequence, our algorithm must estimate singular subspaces via matrix completion methods. Existing matrix completion methods are computationally prohibitive in this setting, so we develop a singular subspace estimator with reasonable time and space complexity for upper triangular matrices (Section
3
).

After estimating singular subspaces, we use varimax rotation to identify latent factors in the network (as opposed to k-means of k-medians clustering). This allows each document to have a weighted membership in each cluster; with this generalization, we refer to
factors
rather than
clusters
. The overall procedure can be intuitively understood in the context of stochastic blockmodels, but is appropriate for a much broader class of models known as semi-parametric factor models.

We validate the new procedure with a simulation study, finding that the new estimator recovers latent factors under a partially observed stochastic blockmodel (Section
4
). Finally, we analyze 237,794 statistics papers and 2 million citations published in journals on statistics and probability (Section
5
), producing a comprehensive breakdown of statistical topics from 1898 to 2022. We present the keywords most associated with these topics in Table
1
(factors describing how papers get cited) and Table
2
(factors describing how papers cite).

Model

2.1
Semi-parametric co-factor model

We leverage the co-factor model of
Rohe and Zeng 2022
as a model for latent similarities between documents. The co-factor model is a broad semi-parametric generalization of the stochastic co-blockmodel
(
Holland et al. 1983
;
Rohe et al. 2016
)
, and includes sub-models such as stochastic blockmodels, degree-corrected stochastic blockmodels
(
Karrer and Newman 2011
)
, (degree-corrected) mixed membership stochastic blockmodels
(
Airoldi et al. 2008
;
Jin and Ke 2017
)
, latent dirichlet allocation
(
Blei et al. 2003
)
, and (generalized) random dot product graphs
(
Lyzinski et al. 2014
)
, many of are closely to related topic models from the natural language processing literature
(
Gerlach et al. 2018
)
.

In the co-factor model, we conceptualize each document
i
as possessing two co-factors. One co-factor
Z_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
controls outgoing citations, or the topics that a paper is likely to cite, and the other co-factor
Y_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
controls incoming citations, or the topics that a paper is likely to be cited by. The co-factor structure of the model operationalizes the fundamental difference between citing and being cited. Mathematically, co-factor models are generalizations of factor models, and there are compelling reasons to model full co-factor structure: co-factor structure is theoretically necessary to capture key features of real world network data
(
Chanpuriya et al. 2020
)
, an observation empirically verified by
Rohe et al. 2016
and
Qing and Wang 2021
, among others.

Example 2.1
.

Consider
Tibshirani 1996
, which introduced
LASSO
regression. The
LASSO
paper builds upon a small body of statistical work on variable selection and resampling, but itself forms the basis for a large body of applied work, primarily in genomics and biomedical settings. The directionality of citations is clear in the reference counts:
Tibshirani 1996
cites twenty papers, but is cited by tens of thousands of papers. If we do not distinguish between papers cited and citing papers, we might fail to distinguish between the genomics literature (incoming co-topic) and the variable selection literature (outgoing co-topic).

Under the co-factor model of
Rohe and Zeng 2022
, the factors
(Z_{i},Y_{i})
are randomly sampled from leptokurtic distributions on
\mathbb{R}^{k}
, where
k
is dimension of the latent factor spaces. Conditional on the latent factors, each element
A_{ij}
of the network is sampled independently from a distribution with expectation
\mathcal{A}\equiv\mathbb{E}(A\mid Z,B,Y)=ZBY^{T}
where
B\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k}
is a mixing matrix that controls how the outgoing and incoming latent factors interact. In the citation setting,
\mathcal{A}
represents the similarities between documents in the latent topic space. As the
B
-mediated similarity between the outgoing topic of document
i
and the incoming similarity of document
j
increases, (i.e.
\mathcal{A}_{ij}
gets larger), the probability of observing a citation
i\to j
goes up.

2.2
Chronological observation mechanism

Thus far, we have described a relatively standard factor model. To specialize the model to the citation setting, we incorporate an observation mechanism.

Definition 2.1
.

Given a corpus of documents
i=1,...,n
published at times
T_{1},...,T_{n}
, the partially observed adjacency matrix is

\displaystyle A_{ij}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if $T_{j}\leq T_{i}$ and $i$ cites $j$,}\\
0&\text{if $T_{j}\leq T_{i}$ and $i$ does not cite $j$, and }\\
\text{unobserved}&\text{if $T_{j}>T_{i}$.}\end{cases}

(2.1)

For convenience, we will always re-index the documents such that
T_{1}\geq...\geq T_{n}
. That is,
T_{1}
will always be the most recent publishing time, and
T_{n}
will always be the time that the first document was published. Under this indexing scheme, the observed portion of the network is nearly upper triangular, but elements can occur in the lower triangle when
T_{i}=T_{j}
.

Under the citation observation mechanism, citations from older papers to newer papers are missing. This is because the impossibility of citations from older papers to newer papers should be uninformative about the outgoing co-factor of the older paper and the incoming co-factor of the newer paper.

If we presume that the older paper definitively cites the newer paper, or definitively does not cite the newer paper, this will force the corresponding co-factors closer together or farther apart in the latent topic space, respectively. Our method thus treats citations forward-in-time as missing rather than precisely observed zeroes or ones. This allows the algorithm to spectrally infer co-factors without introducing chronological artifacts into the estimation process.

Example 2.2
.

Consider
Hoerl and Kennard 1970
, which introduced
RIDGE
regression. Since the
RIDGE
paper was published long before the
LASSO
paper,
Hoerl and Kennard 1970
does not cite
Tibshirani 1996
. But, since
RIDGE
regression and
LASSO
regression are closely related, it is plausible that the two papers are close to each other in outgoing topic space. The impossibility of citation forward-in-time is uninformative about the latent similarity between the two papers.

Remark 2.1
.

The observation mechanism is only relevant if citations are directed relationships. If there is no semantic information contained in the direction of a citation, we can impute the lower triangle of
A
based on the upper triangle of
A
by setting
A_{ij}=A_{ji}
for all
A_{ij}
that are missing.

2.3
Statistical identification of latent co-factors

The chronologically missing data approach to citation analysis presents several challenges. First, it is unclear if the co-factors
Z
and
Y
are identified based on the information observed in the upper triangle of
A
.

Later in this section, Proposition
2.1
states that outgoing community memberships
Z_{i\cdot}
are identified for all but the very earliest documents, and that the incoming community memberships
Y_{i\cdot}
are identified for all but the most recent documents. This is because the most recent documents have not been around long enough to possibly cite papers from all
k
topics. Similarly, the oldest documents were written too early to possibly cite papers from all
k
topics.

More precisely, Proposition
2.1
states that if the conditional expectation of a citation network
\mathcal{A}
is rank
k
and the
\ell\times\ell
submatrix in the top right of
\mathcal{A}
is rank
k
, then we can reconstruct all of
\mathcal{A}
except for the elements in the first
\ell
columns and the elements in the last
\ell
rows. In other words, observing a full rank matrix
M_{\ell}
in the top right of
\mathcal{A}
ensures that no information is hidden in the lower triangle. See Figure
1
for visual summary of this result. In practice, we recommend choosing
\ell
according to domain knowledge (see Section
5.2
for an example).

Figure 1:
A decomposition of a conditional expectation matrix
\mathcal{A}
. Elements in the upper triangle (blue) are observed in the citation setting. We would like to recover elements of the lower triangle based on the information in the upper triangle. When
\rank(\mathcal{A})=\rank(M_{\ell})
, a portion of the lower triangle (green) is identified, but some rows and columns cannot be recovered (red).

The statement of Proposition
2.1
requires some additional notation. Let
\mathscr{R}_{n,k}
be the set of rank
k
matrices contained in
\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
. Imagine that
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}\in\mathscr{R}_{n,k}
are the conditional expectations of two semi-parametric factor models.
P_{U}(\mathcal{A})
and
P_{U}(\mathcal{B})
are projections of
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
onto the space of upper triangular matrices.
P_{U}(\mathcal{A})
and
P_{U}(\mathcal{B})
represent the conditional expectations of the observed portion of
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
, respectively.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}\in\mathscr{R}_{n,k}
. If
P_{U}(\mathcal{A})=P_{U}(\mathcal{B})
and there exists
\ell\in\{k,...,n/2\}
such that
M_{\ell}=\mathcal{A}_{[1:\ell,(n-\ell):n]}
has rank
k
, then
\mathcal{A}_{ij}=\mathcal{B}_{ij}
for all
i,j\in\mathbb{Z}
satisfying
1<i\leq(n-\ell)
and
\ell<j\leq n
.

Remark 2.2
.

Proposition
2.1
can be understood constructively as approximating
\mathcal{A}
from
P_{U}(A)
using the Nyström method
(
Drineas and Mahoney 2005
;
Gittens and Mahoney 2016
)
. Because
\mathcal{A}
is exactly rank
k
, the Nyström method has zero approximation error.

When the conditions of Proposition
2.1
are violated, it is possible that no elements in the lower triangle of
\mathcal{A}
are recoverable. That is, if
M
is rank
k
only for some
\ell>n/2
, there is information hidden in the lower triangle of
\mathcal{A}
that we lose when projecting onto the upper triangle. A concrete example where recovery is impossible is given by the following matrix
\mathcal{A}
, which has rank two. Let
J_{n}
denote an
n\times n
matrix of ones and suppose

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}=\begin{bmatrix}a\,J_{n/2}&a\,J_{n/2}\\
b\,J_{n/2}&a\,J_{n/2}\end{bmatrix}.

Every element in the upper triangle of
\mathcal{A}
is
a
, and thus we have no way of estimating
b
when the lower triangle of
\mathcal{A}
is missing, no matter the amount of data. This
\mathcal{A}
corresponds to a two-block stochastic blockmodel where the first
n/2
documents are in one block and the last
n/2
documents are in a separate block. Because these blocks of documents do not overlap in time (here represented by node order) and they have asymmetric citation probabilities, all information about these asymmetric citation probabilities is lost. However, if
n
is large and the rows and columns of
\mathcal{A}
are permuted according to the same random permutation, then there will be an
\ell\ll n/2
that satisfies the conditions of Proposition
2.1
with high probability.

The follow proposition shows that, for our simulation test-bed model, which is a specific sub-model of the stochastic co-blockmodel, it is sufficient to take
\ell=2k\log k
to satisfy the conditions of Proposition
2.1
. Our goal here is not to characterize identifiability for a large class of models, but rather to demonstrate that identification with
\ell\ll n/2
is reasonable in blockmodel-like settings.

Proposition 2.2
.

Suppose
\mathcal{A}
characterizes the expected adjacency matrix of the simulation test-bed model (Definition
4.2
). Let
\ell=2k\log k
and let
M_{\ell}
be as defined in Proposition
2.1
. Then
\mathbb{P}(\rank(M_{\ell})=k)=1-2n^{-1}
.

2.4
Related work on citation networks

The semi-parametric factor model deviates from traditional models of citation networks such the Price model
(
Price 1976
)
and its undirected specialization of
Barabasi and Albert 1999
, which do not embed topic or community structure into the network. These models are most appropriate for investigating preferential attachment phenomena, and they focus on representing the growth process of citation networks rather than topic structures. The Price model has been extended to include stochastic blockmodel-like community structure in
Pollner et al. 2006
and
Hajek and Sankagiri 2019
, and it is potentially a fruitful avenue of future work to extend these models with missing data mechanisms, but we do not do so here.

While our estimator is appropriate for many common generative network and topic models, it is worth noting that it does not perform hierarchical or multi-resolution inference. Nor does it leverage the content of documents themselves. In cases where hierarchical structure is of direct interest, practitioners should consider alternative methods such as
Li et al. 2020
,
Peixoto 2014
,
Peixoto 2017
and
Lyzinski et al. 2017
, and in cases where document content itself matters, joint network-text models may be more appropriate
(
Liu et al. 2009
;
Nallapati and Cohen 2008
;
Zhu et al. 2013
)
.

3
Spectral estimation

Over the last several decades, many spectral estimators have been proposed for clustering fully observed graphs, and a smaller number for co-clustering full observed graphs. Typically, spectral estimation has three steps. First, represent the graph as a matrix. Often this is the adjacency matrix, but some estimators use normalized or regularized versions of the graph Laplacian. Second, compute the leading eigenvectors or singular vectors of this matrix, which has the consequence of associating each node in the graph with a point in Euclidean space. In the third step, we cluster, co-cluster, or factor analyze the node embeddings using standard methods for Euclidean data. While this estimation strategy may seem ad hoc, spectral estimators in fact performs statistical inference under network models that are identified by their singular subspaces, a large class of models that includes stochastic blockmodels and many generalizations thereof
(
von Luxburg 2007
;
Ji and Jin 2016
;
Jin 2015
;
Lei and Rinaldo 2015
;
Rohe et al. 2016
;
Lyzinski et al. 2017
;
Athreya et al. 2015
;
Athreya et al. 2018
;
Lyzinski et al. 2014
;
Priebe et al. 2019
)
.

The same three-stage estimation approach that works in the fully observed network case readily extends to the partially observed network case. In the first step we represent a network as an adjacency matrix, although this adjacency matrix is now only partially observed. In the second step, we use a singular subspace estimator that accommodates missing data. More precisely, we use the
AdaptiveImpute
algorithm of
Cho et al. 2019
, which can be thought of as a self-tuning variant of the
softImpute
algorithm of
Mazumder et al. 2010
, which itself falls into the larger class of nuclear norm regularizing spectral estimators. In the third stage of the procedure, we use varimax rotation to estimate the latent co-factor membership
(
Rohe and Zeng 2022
)
.

The algorithm

CitationImpute
accepts as input a network adjacency matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
where the lower triangle is assumed to be mostly missing, a desired number of co-factors
k\in\{2,...,n\}
, and a clipping parameter
\ell\in\{k,...,n/2\}
. It procceds as follows:

1.

Set all elements in the first
\ell
columns of
A
and last
\ell
rows of
A
to zero. That is, ignore edges corresponding to the unidentified rows of
Z
and
Y
. See Proposition
2.1
for details.

2.

Estimate the singular vectors and singular values of
A\approx\widehat{U}\widehat{D}\widehat{V}^{T}
using
AdaptiveImpute

(

)
. In Section
3.2
we describe why a naive implementation of
AdaptiveImpute
is computationally infeasible, and in Section
3.3
we outline our computational contributions and a practical implementation of
AdaptiveImpute
for upper triangular data.

3.

Compute the varimax rotations of
\widehat{U}
and
\widehat{V}
and construct rotated singular vector matrices
\widehat{Z}
and
\widehat{Y}
, respectively. We briefly review varimax rotation and the interpretation of the resulting varimax factors in Section
3.4
.

3.2
The computational problem

One contribution of this paper is a collection of algebraic identities (Propositions
3.1
and
3.2
) that allow for an efficient implementation of
AdaptiveImpute
on citation matrices with hundreds of thousands of documents. These identities are discussed in detail in Section
3.3
, but we explain why they are necessary here.

To begin, we must disambiguate between two senses of sparsity. A matrix is
sparse
if most of its elements are zero. These matrices can be represented very efficiently on a computer by recording only the small number of non-zero elements and their indices. On the other hand, a matrix is
sparsely observed
if only a few of its entries are observed, regardless of the value of those entries. These two notations of sparsity are often conflated, and sparsely observed matrices are often represented as sparse matrices, where implicit zeroes are considered missing, and the observed zeroes must be explicitly tracked.

In the citation setting, the data matrix
A
, as defined eq.
(
2.1
)
, is densely observed; at least half of the entries are defined by the data. However, in the portion of the network that is observed, the data is sparse, i.e., mostly zero-valued. Thus, the usual implementation-time conflation of sparse and sparsely observed matrices leads to issues: there are
n\,(n-1)/2
elements in the upper triangle of
A
that must be explicitly tracked even if they are zero. For even moderately sized corpora,
A
cannot be represented on standard machines in this way because they do not have enough memory. Beyond memory considerations, adding approximately
n\,(n-1)/2
explicit zeroes to a sparse matrix slows down basic matrix operations like matrix-vector multiplication.

This makes matrix completion algorithms infeasible in both time and space when using the naive sparse representation of
A
. In particular, both
AdaptiveImpute
and
softImpute
rely on iterated singular value decompositions of a running low rank approximation
\tilde{A}^{(t)}
to
A
. In the typical setting where the number of nodes is
n
, the rank of the decomposition is
k
, and
n\gg k
, naively taking a singular value decomposition of
\tilde{A}^{(t)}
has time complexity per iteration
\mathcal{O}\left(n^{2}\,k\right)
. This high computational complexity constrains researchers to inference on networks with at most thousands of nodes.

We are able to reduce the both the time and space complexity of the matrix completion problem. The solution requires leveraging the fact that
A
is sparse, even if it is not sparsely observed. In particular, there is no need to explicitly track zeroes in the upper triangle of
A
, and
A
may be represented as a sparse matrix that records only non-zero elements of
A
and zeroes in the lower triangle of
A
. Using this representation, with some algebraic tricks, all the operations necessary for
AdaptiveImpute
are computationally feasible. In brief, by representing
\tilde{A}^{(t)}
as the sum of four carefully constructed matrices, we can reduce the naive time complexity from
\mathcal{O}(n^{2}\,k)
down to
\mathcal{O}(|\tilde{\Omega}|\,k+n\,k^{2})
, where
|\tilde{\Omega}|
is the number of observed non-zero elements of
A
. In real world datasets
|\tilde{\Omega}|
represents the number of citations between documents, and empirical evidence suggests that each document in a citation network cites a fixed number of other documents, regardless of the overall size of the corpus. That is,
|\tilde{\Omega}|
is
\mathcal{O}(n)
. Thus the effective per-iteration runtime reduces from
\mathcal{O}(n^{2}\,k)
to
\mathcal{O}(n\,k^{2})
.

3.3.1
Feasible implementation

In practice, the runtime for each iteration of
AdaptiveImpute
and
softImpute
is dominated by the singular value decomposition, which is computed using an algorithm for sparse SVD such as the implicitly restarted Arnoldi method or the implicitly restarted Lanczos bidiagonalization algorithm. The time complexity of this sparse SVD in turn depends on cost of left and right matrix-multiplication of
\tilde{A}^{(t)}
with an appropriately sized vector.

When
A
is sparsely observed,
\tilde{A}^{(t)}
can be expressed as a sparse matrix plus a low rank matrix

\displaystyle\tilde{A}^{(t)}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{P_{\Omega}(A-Z^{(t)})}_{\text{sparse}}+\underbrace{Z^{(t)}}_{\text{low rank}},

sparsely observed setting

and matrix-vector multiplication has time complexity
\mathcal{O}(|\Omega|\,k)
for the sparse part and
\mathcal{O}(n\,k^{2})
for the low rank part. In the citation setting, naively re-using this decomposition in the Arnoldi method is inefficient since
|\Omega|\approx n^{2}/2
.

However, a similar trick can improve the time complexity of multiplication with
\tilde{A}^{(t)}
: we can drop observed zeroes from consideration if we partition
\tilde{A}^{(t)}
carefully. In particular
P_{\tilde{\Omega}}(A)=P_{\Omega}(A)
, and we can use this fact to compute only on
\tilde{\Omega}
. Let
U=\{(i,j):i<j\}
denote the indices of the upper triangle of
A
and
L
the indices of the observed elements of
A
on the lower triangle, such that
\Omega=U\cup L
. Then

\displaystyle\tilde{A}^{(t)}

\displaystyle=P_{\Omega}(A)+P_{\Omega}^{\perp}(Z^{(t)})

citation setting

\displaystyle=P_{\Omega}(A)-P_{\Omega}(Z^{(t)})+P_{\Omega}(Z^{(t)})+P_{\Omega}^{\perp}(Z^{(t)})

\displaystyle=P_{\tilde{\Omega}}(A)-P_{\Omega}(Z^{(t)})+Z^{(t)}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{P_{\tilde{\Omega}}(A)}_{\text{sparse}}-\underbrace{P_{L}(Z^{(t)})}_{\text{sparse}}-\underbrace{P_{U}(Z^{(t)})}_{\text{low rank until projection}}+\underbrace{Z^{(t)}}_{\text{low rank}}.

The intuition is now straightforward: efficient implementation strategies for matrix-vector multiplications with the sparse and low rank terms are well known. This leaves the
P_{U}(Z^{(t)})
term, which is low rank until it is projected onto the upper triangle. Here we can use the same implementation strategy as for the low rank component, but summing over fewer indices.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
Z^{(t)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be a rank
k
matrix with singular value decomposition
Z^{(t)}=UDV^{T}
. Let
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.
Then

\displaystyle\left[P_{U}(Z^{(t)})\,x\right]_{i}=\langle U_{i\cdot},\tilde{W}_{i}\rangle,

where
\tilde{W}_{ki}=\sum_{j=i+1}^{n}W_{kj}
and
W_{\cdot j}=(DV^{T})_{\cdot j}\cdot x_{j}

Proposition
3.1
is a straightforward algebraic result that suggests a computational scheme for evaluating the term
P_{U}(Z^{(t)})\,x
. In particular, it suggests constructing
W

\tilde{W}
, and then obtaining elements of
P_{U}(Z^{(t)})\,x
element by element. This procedure requires
\mathcal{O}(n\,k^{2})
flops as opposed to the
\mathcal{O}(n^{2}\,k)
flops of a naive implementation. We defer the proof to the appendix. The left-multiplication case is analogous.

The last requirement is a similarly efficient calculation of
\alpha^{(t)}
. To implement this computation we use the following expression.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
\tilde{A}^{(t)},Z^{(t)}
and
\alpha^{(t)}
be as defined in Algorithm

Z^{(t)}
is a low rank matrix of the form

with

\displaystyle\alpha^{(t)}=\frac{1}{n-k}\left[\left\|P_{\tilde{\Omega}}(A)\right\|_{F}^{2}+\left\|Z^{(t)}\right\|_{F}^{2}-\left\|P_{L}\left(Z^{(t)}\right)\right\|_{F}^{2}-\left\|P_{U}\left(Z^{(t)}\right)\right\|_{F}^{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{k}\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{i}^{2}\left(\tilde{A}^{(t)}\right)\right].

Additionally, define
U^{rq}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
V^{rq\triangle}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that

\displaystyle U^{rq}_{i}=U_{ir}\,U_{iq},\qquad\text{ and }\qquad V^{rq\triangle}_{i}=\sum_{j=i+1}^{n}DV^{T}_{rj}DV^{T}_{qj}\qquad\forall i=1,...,n.

Then

\displaystyle\left\|P_{U}\left(Z^{(t)}\right)\right\|_{F}^{2}=\sum_{r=1}^{k}\sum_{q=1}^{k}\left\langle U^{rq},V^{rq\triangle}\right\rangle.

To understand the computational complexity of this expression we proceed term by term. First, consider the
\sum_{i=1}^{k}\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{i}^{2}(\tilde{A}^{(t)})
term. Each iteration of
AdaptiveImpute
computes a truncated singular value decomposition of

of rank

and

are collectively

\mathcal{O}(|\tilde{\Omega}|\,k+n\,k^{2})

3.4
Varimax rotation

Once we have obtained an estimate of the singular value decomposition
A\approx\widehat{U}\widehat{D}\widehat{V}^{T}
from
AdaptiveImpute
, we varimax rotate these estimates to obtain latent factors for each node. Given an
n\times k
matrix orthonormal matrix
U
, varimax finds a
k\times k
orthogonal matrix
R
that maximizes

v(R,U)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left([UR]_{i\ell}^{4}-\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}[UR]_{j\ell}^{2}\right)^{2}\right)

over the set of
k\times k
orthonormal matrices. In particular, we compute
\widehat{R}_{U}
that maximizes
v(\cdot,\widehat{U})
and
\widehat{R}_{V}
that maximizes
v(\cdot,\widehat{V})
where
\widehat{R}_{U}
and
\widehat{R}_{V}
are
k\times k
orthonormal matrices. Calculating these rotation matrices is a routine operation available in many statistical packages. After the rotation matrices
\widehat{R}_{U}
and
\widehat{R}_{V}
have been found, the latent factors are estimated as

\displaystyle\widehat{Z}=\sqrt{n}\widehat{U}R_{\widehat{U}},\quad\widehat{Y}=\sqrt{n}\widehat{V}R_{\widehat{V}},\quad\text{ and }\quad\widehat{B}=R_{\widehat{U}}^{T}\widehat{D}R_{\widehat{V}}/n.

(3.1)

These factor loadings are identifiable up to column-wise sign flips and column reordering. We flip the signs of each column so that they are skew positive, for ease of interpretation, and then return the loadings. The rows of
\widehat{Z}
and
\widehat{Y}
can be roughly understood as estimates of (degree-corrected) mixed memberships from a degree-corrected mixed membership stochastic blockmodel
(
Airoldi et al. 2008
;
Jin and Ke 2017
)
, and
\widehat{B}
can be roughly thought of as an estimate of the mixing matrix in the mixed membership stochastic blockmodel. That is,
B_{ij}
describes the propensity for papers loadings on the
Z_{\cdot i}
factor to cite papers with loading on
Y_{\cdot j}
factor.

In forthcoming work,
Rohe and Zeng 2022
show that, for
\widehat{U},\widehat{V}
obtained from the singular value decomposition in the fully observed case, varimax rotated estimates
\widehat{Z},\widehat{B}
and
\widehat{Y}
are consistent for population factors
Z,B
and
Y
under some conditions on the factor model, where
\widehat{Z}
and
\widehat{Y}
are identified up to sign flips and column reordering. Stated informally, the conditions require that rows of the population factors
Z
and
Y
are independent, more skewed than a Gaussian, and not overly sparse or heavy-tailed.

3.5
Relation to existing work

Our goal in this paper is to co-factor a partially observed network, and as a result the closest existing work comes from the network co-clustering literature.
Choi and Wolfe 2014
and
Choi 2017
analyzed network co-clustering theoretically under a general graphon model, while
Rohe et al. 2016
proposed the stochastic co-blockmodel and a spectral co-clustering algorithm DI-SIM.
Rohe and Zeng 2022
extended DI-SIM to consider co-factor analysis using varimax rotation, and proposes the factor model that we consider here. None of these papers allow missing data in the observed network. The network co-clustering problem is more general than but closely related to community detection in bipartite networks
(
Larremore et al. 2014
;
Razaee et al. 2017
;
Yen and Larremore 2020
)
and some of these methods can be extended to handle missing data. For example,
Zhao et al. 2020
presented a purely heuristic approach, while
Peixoto 2018
considered missingness generated according to an i.i.d. observation mechanism.

There is a large literature on network clustering with missing data that is too extensive to detail here. Some work particularly relevant to our own includes
Chen et al. 2014
and
Vinayak et al. 2014
, which leveraged nuclear norm penalized singular subspace estimation to handle missing edge data in a clustering context, and
Li et al. 2020
, which used similar ideas for model selection. In both
Chen et al. 2014
and
Vinayak et al. 2014
the observation mechanism is assumed to be missing-at-random, while in our work the observation mechanism exhibits complex dependence.

Finally, our work is related to the matrix completion literature, in particular nuclear norm penalization approaches for estimating partially observed matrices
(
Kim and Choi 2013
;
Gu et al. 2014
;
Klopp 2014
;
Cui et al. 2015
;
Wang et al. 2015
;
Hosono et al. 2016
;
Gu et al. 2017
;
Zhang and Ng 2019
;
Yang et al. 2020
;
Shamir and Shalev-Shwartz 2014
;
Bhojanapalli and Jain 2014
;
Cho et al. 2019
;
Mazumder et al. 2010
)
. While this literature has recently made impressive inroads regarding the consistency of nuclear-norm regularization for spectral recovery in deterministic and non-uniform sampling settings
(
Foucart et al. 2021
;
Zhu et al. 2022
)
, we are unaware of consistency results for the upper triangular observation pattern present in citation data, which exhibits a complex dependence structure. Nonetheless, current results suggest that when matrix recovery is possible, it is possible using nuclear-norm penalization.

To summarize: current methods either co-cluster fully observed networks or cluster, rather than co-cluster, networks with missing edge data. We co-cluster with missing data, under an upper triangular observation mechanism. To handle missing data, our procedure incorporates nuclear norm regularization into well-established spectral network estimators. We cannot use an off-the-shelf nuclear norm regularizer for computational reasons, as demonstrated by the computational experiment summarized in Figure
2
and elaborated in Section
3.2
, so we develop a specialized implementation of
AdaptiveImpute
. This nuclear norm regularization is necessary to handle missing edge data; simulations in Section
4
show that it is inappropriate to handle missing edges via natural seeming imputation strategies.

4
Simulation study

To assess the performance of our estimator, we perform a simulation study, using a parametric sub-model of the semi-parametric factor model as a test-bed. In these simulations, we observe that our estimator recovers both singular subspaces of
\mathcal{A}
and the underlying latent factors
Z
and
Y
. We use clipping parameter
\ell=n/10
, such that we estimate latent factors for only 90% of the nodes in each simulated graph. We also compare our method with traditional approaches for fully observed networks combined with natural imputation strategies, to stress test whether a new method is genuinely needed in our setting. We find that it is, as combining existing tools with data imputation leads to incorrect estimates. As one would expect, the
AdaptiveImpute + varimax
estimator is less efficient that an oracle estimator that observes the full data, rather than just the upper triangle of the adjacency matrix.

4.3
Performance metrics

To measure how well various estimators recover the singular subspaces of
\mathcal{A}
, we measure the
\sin\Theta
distance between the subspaces spanned by
U
and
\widehat{U}
. This is a standard metric in subspace estimation problems
(
Vu and Lei 2013
;
Bhatia 1997
)
. Given two orthonormal bases
U\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}
and
\widehat{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}
, the singular values
\sigma_{1},...,\sigma_{k}
of
U^{T}\widehat{U}
are the cosines of the principal angles
\cos\theta_{1},...,\cos\theta_{k}
between the span of
U
and the span of
\widehat{U}
. Define
\sin\Theta(U,\widehat{U})
to be a diagonal matrix containing the sine of the principle angles of
U^{T}\widehat{U}
. Then the
\sin\Theta
distance between the subspaces spanned by
U
and
\widehat{U}
is given by

\displaystyle d(U,\widehat{U})=\|\sin\Theta(U,\widehat{U})\|_{F}.

In our simulations, we aggregate error across identified rows of the estimates
\widehat{U}
and
\widehat{V}
and report a single metric

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{subspace}}(U,\widehat{U},V,\widehat{V})=\|\sin\Theta(U,\widehat{U})\|_{F}+\|\sin\Theta(V,\widehat{V})\|_{F}.

To measure how well we recover the latent factors
Z
and
Y
, we report root mean squared error on individual elements of identified rows
\widehat{Z}
and
\widehat{Y}
. Since varimax estimates
\widehat{Z}
and
\widehat{Y}
are only determined up to sign-flips and column reordering, this requires us to align the columns and signs of
\widehat{Z}
and
Z
, and to similarly align
\widehat{Y}
and
Y
. Let
\mathscr{P}(k)
be the set of
k\times k
orthogonal matrices whose entries
P_{ij}
are elements of
\{-1,0,1\}
. Define

\displaystyle P_{Z}

\displaystyle=\argmin_{P\in\mathscr{P}(k)}\|Z-\widehat{Z}P\|_{F}

(4.1)

\displaystyle P_{Y}

\displaystyle=\argmin_{P\in\mathscr{P}(k)}\|Y-\widehat{Y}P\|_{F}.

(4.2)

After alignment, we can compute our elementwise factor root mean squared error as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{factor}}(Z,\widehat{Z},Y,\widehat{Y})=\sqrt{\frac{1}{n\,k}\left(\|Z-\widehat{Z}P_{Z}\|_{F}^{2}+\|Y-\widehat{Y}P_{Y}\|_{F}^{2}\right)}.

We find
P_{Z}
and
P_{Y}
heuristically: we first evaluate the skew of the columns in
Z,Y,\widehat{Z}
and
\widehat{Y}
, and sign-flip by column to force each column to have positive skew. This reduces the optimization problems (
4.1

4.2
) to a column matching problem which can be solved via the Hungarian algorithm. Our approach is not guaranteed to find
P_{Z}
and

exactly, so in a strict sense we only upper bound error in our simulations. In practice the heuristic is highly effective when
Z
and
Y
are generated according to leptokurtic distributions satisfying the varimax assumptions.

5
Analysis of the statistics literature

We now leverage our estimator for an analysis of the academic statistics literature.

5.1
Data

For our analysis we used proprietary Web of Science data that we obtained through an institutional agreement with Clarivate Analytics. The complete Web of Science corpus contains hundreds of millions of documents, which amount to nearly a terabyte of data. This was beyond our ability to handle computationally, so we considered only papers published in a subset of 125 journals focused on probability and statistics (see Appendix
D
for the list). The node-induced subgraph formed by considering only these papers and the citations between them had 262,958 nodes, 2,022,347 edges, and
23,168
weakly connected components (most of which were singletons). We considered the largest weakly connected component, which contained 237,794 nodes and 2,020,004 edges. From this point onward, when we refer to the “citation network” or “citation graph” we are referring exclusively to this largest connected component. For each document we additionally knew the authors, publication date, and the abstract text, although some of this information was missing.

Papers in the citation network were published between 1898 and 2022. The number of citations received from other papers in the largest connected component (i.e. in-degree) ranged from 0 to 4,504 and the number of citations sent to other papers in the largest connected component (i.e. out-degree) ranged from 0 to 608. There are several articles in the sample that cite hundreds of other papers; these articles are typically bibliographies or reviews. A small number of papers mutually cited each other.

5.2
Methods

First we constructed the partially observed adjacency matrix of the citation graph. To do this we ordered nodes chronologically and then ran
AdaptiveImpute
to obtain a low-rank decomposition
A\approx\widehat{U}\widehat{D}\widehat{V}^{T}
. Here we report the results for a rank
k=30
decomposition, but we repeated our analysis for
k\in\{5,10,20,30,40\}
, and obtained qualitatively consistent results across all
k
. After computing a low rank decomposition
A\approx\widehat{U}\widehat{D}\widehat{V}^{T}
, we performed varimax rotation of
\widehat{U}
and
\widehat{V}
to obtain a final low rank decomposition
A\approx\widehat{Z}\widehat{B}\widehat{Y}^{T}
, as described in Section
3
. The rows of
\widehat{Z}
and the rows of
\widehat{Y}
thus corresponding to document-level latent co-factors
(
Rohe and Zeng 2022
;
Rohe et al. 2016
)
. The rows of
\widehat{Z}
contained outgoing-citation factors, and the rows of
\widehat{Y}
contained incoming-citation factors. Both
\widehat{Z}
and
\widehat{Y}
were relatively sparse. Recall that
\widehat{Z},\widehat{B}
and
\widehat{Y}
can be roughly understood as estimates from a mixed membership stochastic blockmodel, as described in Section
3.4
.

Motivated by the identification result of Proposition
2.1
, for our analysis, we clipped the first
30,000
columns and the last
80,000
rows of
A
, such that we discarded incoming latent factors
Y
for papers published after 2019 and outgoing latent factors
Z
for papers published before 1999. By only estimating
Y
factors for papers published before 2019, we ensured that these papers had a chance to be cited, accounting for the time delay in the publication process. We clipped
Z
factors more aggressively, because some topics in modern statistics did not emerge until the 1990s. Papers published before this time cannot cite, for example, the
LASSO
literature, and thus their outgoing co-factors are likely unidentified. In several robustness experiments, we clipped fewer papers, and thus estimated outgoing co-factors for papers published prior to 1999. These embeddings lacked a clear interpretation and also failed to produce a diagonally dominant
\widehat{B}
matrix.

To interpret the co-factors
\widehat{Y}
and
\widehat{Z}
, we took several approaches. First, we found keywords most associated with each factor by examining the words in paper titles following the “best features” approach of
Zhang et al. 2021
;
Chen 2021
;
Wang and Rohe 2016
. To complement this keyword analysis, we found the papers with the largest loadings for each dimension of
\widehat{Y}
and
\widehat{Z}
, which we refer to as hub papers.

5.3
Results

Previous investigations of the statistical literature have repeatedly found fifteen or fewer meaningful communities within the statistics literature. In contrast, we find over thirty meaningful sub-fields in the statistical literature. We identify many of the same topics identified in previous work, but additionally find about fifteen more topics than previously detected in previous analyses of statistical citation networks such as
Ji and Jin 2016
,
Ji et al. 2022
, and
Wang and Rohe 2016
.

The
\widehat{Y}
factors generally corresponded to core statistical subtopics, including GLMM(s), multiple testing, survival analysis, MCMC, causal inference, mixture models, time series, missing data, model selection (both Bayesian and frequentist), longitudinal data analysis, and experimental design, with separate factors for adaptive clinical trial design and optimal treatment rules.

On particularly interesting feature of the
\widehat{Y}
factor was the presence of numerous incoming dimensions related to penalized regression. These factors covered the
LASSO

Another question was how to interpret co-factors exhibiting one-to-one incoming-to-outgoing correspondence. For example, what was the difference between the outgoing survival analysis factor (z10) and the incoming survival analysis factor (y10)? To answer this question, we looked at the hub papers for each co-factor. For the survival analysis factor, for example, the top incoming hub was
Cox 1972
, which introduced the proportional hazards model, and the top outgoing hub was
Guo and Zeng 2014
, a survey of semiparametric models in survival analysis. Incoming
\widehat{Y}
hub papers were typically highly cited, important papers in each sub-field. In contrast, the outgoing
\widehat{Z}
hub papers were typically review articles, retrospectives, tutorials, and papers with good literature reviews that summarized the past literature. Put differently, statistical papers tended to either: (1) perform important synthesis of past work but be cited very little, (2) cite a limited number of papers while receiving many citations, or (3) cite and be cited very little.

This distinct behavior from the
\widehat{Y}
paper hubs and
\widehat{Z}
paper hubs is evidence of co-factor structure in the statistics literature, and more broadly, evidence that papers do indeed cite and get cited in fundamentally different ways. Future analysis of citation networks should take the directed nature of citations and statistical topics into account.

5.4
How the past would cite the future

One of the interesting features of our missing data framework is that it allows us to impute latent similarities from older documents to newer documents, or, with conceptual abuse, citations forward in time. In particular, if a paper
i
was published before paper
j
, we can estimate the latent similarity from paper
i
to paper
j
via the real-valued imputation
\widehat{A}_{ij}\approx\widehat{Z}_{i\cdot}\,\widehat{B}\,\widehat{Y_{j\cdot}}^{T}
. We suggest interpreting these imputed similarities as you would interpret probability estimates from a linear probability model; as in the linear probability model, we have no guarantee that
\widehat{A}_{ij}\in[0,1]
, such that
\widehat{A}_{ij}
represents a valid probability of “citation”. However, we can still think of
\widehat{A}_{ij}
as indicative of probability of citation, had citation been possible.

In particular, for each paper, we calculated all of these imputed similarities from prior papers. Summing over these imputations, we obtained an estimate of the number of times papers from the past would have cited papers from the future on the basis of topical similarity, were they so able. We computed these estimates for each of the papers in our citation network and report the 15 papers with the highest imputed in-degree in Table
3
. Most of the papers with high imputed in-degree are related to feature screening, the graphical
LASSO
, or some form of high dimensional regression.

Discussion

We proposed a new method to co-factor documents in citation networks. The method is motivated by the observation that factors should be based on similarity measurements, and citations are only partially observed similarity measurements. Factoring a partially observed network complicated standard spectral clustering procedures and required use of matrix completion methods to estimate singular subspaces of the graph adjacency matrix. Here we found computational difficulties due to the precise observation pattern of citation data, which we resolved via a careful new implementation of the
AdaptiveImpute
algorithm. Because of dependence in the observation mechanism in the citation setting, existing theoretical results for
AdaptiveImpute
, and nuclear norm minimization more generally, were not applicable, and we validated our approach to matrix completion via a simulation study.

Our work suggests several avenues for methodological and theoretical exploration. Methodologically, it may be interesting to propose computationally efficient estimation procedures for other matrix completion methods in the upper triangular observation setting, or more generally in settings where sparse data is densely observed. Methods designed for independent but general sampling distributions, such as weighted nuclear norm minimization, may perform particularly well in the citation setting. Alternatively, further computational improvements would allow for larger scale bibliometric exploration of scientific citation networks. Current bibliometric databases contain hundreds of millions of papers and billions of references, more data than our method can handle on commodity hardware. While our analysis of the statistics literature is one of the most extensive to date, incorporating additional papers could illuminate the relationships between statistical methodology and scientific practice at large. Another open question is how to extend our approach to the tensor, or multi-layer, citation network case, which would be appropriate for data like U.S. Court Opinions, where there are several distinct and explicitly labelled types of citation that documents may use when referencing each other. Finally, it may be of significant practical use to develop a better theoretical understanding of how matrix completion methods perform in settings with dependent observation mechanisms.
