Waiting times for sea level variations in Port of Trieste: from scale-free to exponential-like distributions Journal: Physica A
Gabriel Tiberiu Pană, Alexandru Nicolin-Żaczek

Abstract
We report here a series of detailed statistical analyses on the sea level variations in the Port of Trieste using one of the largest existing catalogues that covers more than a century of measurements. We show that the distribution of waiting times, which are defined here akin to econophysics, namely the series of shortest time spans between a given sea level L L and the next sea level of at least L + δ L+\delta in the catalogue, exhibits a distinct scale-free character for small values of δ \delta , while for larger values of δ \delta the distribution is very similar to the exponential distribution. The distribution of waiting times for small values of δ \delta is typical for complex systems exhibiting criticality and is reported abundantly in the literature, while the exponential-like distribution observed for large values of δ \delta has been observed in contexts as diverse as magnetic systems and light sleep duration.

The unprecedented availability of observational data, coming both from historical sources, which are now digitized, as well as automatized natively digital systems, allows for unparalleled investigations into the world of complex systems. The topical coverage of complex systems is immense and a rapid literature survey will show that domains as diverse as linguistics

Many tools have been proposed and used to assess the dynamics of complex systems, but the so-called distribution of waiting times acquired a distinct position. The concept was initially introduced for financial markets as
investment horizon
,
i.e.
, the smallest time interval needed for an index to vary by a given amount, and – to give only two examples – it was successfully used to describe optimal investment strategies
[
7
]
and the gain-loss asymmetry for real and artificial stock indices
[
8

. This definition was adapted in the context of seismic studies to investigate by computational means the distributions of time intervals between earthquakes of specific properties. To this end, the waiting time was defined as the shortest time interval needed to find an earthquake of magnitude of at least
M+\delta
, with
\delta
a given constant (or threshold), after an earthquake of magnitude
M
was observed. Remarkably, we have shown using a series of open-source earthquake analysis tools
[
9
]
that the waiting times observed for earthquakes originating in Romania, Italy, United States of America (California region), and Japan
[
10
]
, as well as seismic events on the Moon
[
11

, exhibit a distinctive scale-free-like distribution. The aforementioned statistical results, as well as recent extensions on the distribution of motifs in earthquake networks
[
12
]
, are supported by simple mechanical models, like the celebrated Olami-Feder-Christensen model
[
13
]
, and suggest that seismic zones can be seen as self-organized critical systems. It should be noted that experimental data is not always supported by simple models and criticality is usually inferred through power-law distributions on some observables. In fact, there is a strong asymmetry between the large amount of statistical results obtained from the direct processing of empirical data and the substantially fewer results stemming from simplified structural models. This asymmetry reflects on one hand the large amounts of empirical data currently available, while on the other hand it shows how difficult it is to construct simplified models of reduced computational load that can be used for large-scale statistical studies.

Motivated by our work on the available data pertaining to The International Centre for Advanced Studies on River-Sea Systems DANUBIUS-RI, a pan-European distributed research infrastructure supporting interdisciplinary research on River-Sea Systems
[
14
]
, we report here a similar analysis on one of the most extensive sea level catalogues. This dataset, covering more than a century of sea level data in the Port of Trieste, is an excellent example of Open Data and allows a detailed investigation into the distribution of waiting times. For this article, a waiting time was defined as the time span between a given sea level
L
and the next sea level of at least
L+\delta
in the catalogue. Unlike earthquake databases, where we find seismic events recorded with varying degrees of precision on the position of the epicenters even for relatively recent events, the current catalogue offers very precise sea level data, with the imprecision on individual entries being less than 1 cm.

Finally, in 1961, the hut was enlarged and a new stilling well was built, the measurements continuing without interruption until the present day.

2
Method

The data catalogue detailed in the previous section was processed automatically, see Ref.
[
20
]
, to determine series of waiting times for given values of the sea level threshold
\delta
. For each value of
\delta
we sweep the data set and for each value of sea level
L
we determine the closest entry of value of at least
L+\delta
. The time span between these two sea levels is recorded in the waiting time series. Once the process is completed we determine the distribution of these numerically calculated waiting times, a process which is subject to some discussion in the specialized literature. As will be shown in Section
3
, these distributions have two distinct regimes, which are quite different: for small values of
\delta
the distributions of waiting times have a very prominent scale-free character, while for the large values of
\delta
the distributions are exponential-like. These two types of distributions are different on many levels, but for now, let us mention that fitting scale-free-like distributions is considerably more problematic than fitting exponential ones. Please also note that these distributions reflect the entire information available in the data set, a situation which is quite different from similar computations on earthquake databases where small-magnitude events are discarded from the statistics.

Finally, let us add that our results stem solely from the statistical processing of recorded data
[
21
]
, as we have not investigated the numerous (computationally demanding) models that can be used to describe – either partly or in full – the data, like those used for tides, storm surges, inverse barometric effect, river discharge, seasonal variability, and so on
[
22
,
23
,
24
]
. Please note that large-scale statistical analyses on simulated data are achievable only when there are simple models of reduced computational load, which is not the case here. Our approach is therefore data-driven with a focus on understanding to what extent one can predict certain events, particularly high sea-level fluctuations. In this context, the distribution of waiting times is seen both as a statistical indicator of critical behavior (through the scale-free-like distribution of small values of
\delta
) and as a potential predictability indicator.

2.1
Power-law distributed data

An interesting topic in the literature dedicated to fitting power-law distributed empirical data regards the distinction between different binning methods, on the one hand, and the maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) method
[
25
]
, on the other hand. The main problem is that binning methods can result in inaccurate estimates of the distribution parameters due to the (usually heavy) noise in the tail of the distribution (see, for instance, Ref.
[
26
]
). Moreover, fitting data on logarithmic plots can lead to spurious errors in the value of the power-law exponent. Instead of binning, an arguably better method is to represent the data via a cumulative distribution function and estimate the parameters using the MLE method.

If one suspects that a given set of empirical data might be distributed as a power law, it is useful to plot the data using the complementary cumulative distribution function (CCDF), that is

\displaystyle\hat{F}_{X}(x)=P(X>x)=1-F_{X}(x),

(1)

where the right side of the equation shows the probability that the variable
X

strictly exceeds
the value
x
.

Considering the probability distribution function (PDF) of a power law

\displaystyle p(x)=Cx^{-\alpha},

(2)

where
C
is the normalization constant, the probability
P(x)
that the variable has a value greater than
x
is

\displaystyle P(x)=\int_{x}^{\infty}C(x^{\prime})^{-\alpha}dx^{\prime}=\frac{C}{\alpha-1}x^{-(\alpha-1)}.

(3)

With the help of the MLE method, we compute the
\alpha
exponent following the recipe in Ref.
[
27
]
, which implements the
Python

powerlaw
package. The normalized expression for the power law is given by:

\displaystyle p(x)=\frac{\alpha-1}{x_{\mathrm{min}}}\left(\frac{x}{x_{\mathrm{min}}}\right)^{-\alpha}.

(4)

2.2
Goodness of fit

For data following non-normal distribution, one of the most common measures quantifying the quality of fit is the Kolmogorov-Smirnov (KS) distance
D

[
28
]
, as it measures the distance between two probability distributions,
i.e.
,

\displaystyle D={\max}_{x\geq x_{\mathrm{min}}}|S(x)-P(x)|,

(5)

which represents the maximum distance between the data and the fitted model CDFs.
S(x)
represents the CDF of the data for observations with values greater than or equal to
x_{\mathrm{min}}
and
P(X)
is the CDF for the power-law model that best fits the data considering
x\geq x_{\mathrm{min}}
. An appropriate
x_{\mathrm{min}}
is chosen such that the KS statistic is minimized.

The first result in our analysis is a comparison between fitting a power law on normal binning, logarithmic binning, a rank-frequency plot, and estimation using MLE, for a given
\delta
value of the waiting time threshold.

The next step of the analysis is to compute the parameter dependence of the
\alpha
exponent to the selection of the
\delta
threshold. We also show here the dependence of
x_{\mathrm{min}}
. To this end, we sweep numerically the
\delta
-interval from
1
to
15
in increments of
1
. For each value of
\delta
, we compute the waiting times, then estimate
\alpha
and
x_{\mathrm{min}}
using MLE, corresponding to each waiting times distribution. These results are then plotted against the corresponding
\delta
values, and the color gradient on the plot represents the quality of fit computed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov distance.

Lastly, for larger
\delta
values, we notice that the distribution of the waiting times resembles exponential distributions. We show a few of these plots, for
\delta
values larger than
100
.

In Fig.

Second, as scale-free-like distributions make it difficult to compute averages, predictions of sea level variations are also difficult and largely depend on the intrinsic (short and long waiting times) cut-offs of the aforementioned observed distributions. This is not the case for large values of
\delta

\delta
is large. Finally, we would like to mention that unlike the small
\delta

5

The overall observed distributions are still exponential-like but a close inspection of the depicted curves shows that the fine structure exhibits some differences, particularly for long waiting times.

4
Conclusion

We have reported a series of statistical results on the distribution of waiting times for sea level variations in the Port of Trieste using open-source data processing tools and a publicly available data set that covers more than a century of measurements. We find that the aforementioned distribution is highly impacted by the value of the
\delta
sea level threshold and we observe a clear transition from an effectively scale-free distribution for small values of
\delta
,
i.e.
, up to 15 cm, to an exponential-like distribution for large values of
\delta
,
i.e.
, 100 cm and more. This transition does not reflect a mere quantitative change in the shape of the distribution of waiting times, but a qualitative one, as the exponential distribution has the remarkable property of being memoryless. As we do not yet have simple models that describe the sea level dynamics we cannot offer a full picture of the underlying physical process behind the change in the distribution, but our results clearly show that there is a qualitative change from small to large values of
\delta
.

We have tested the robustness of our results against imprecision in the recorded sea level values, as well as against changes coming from the inclusion/exclusion of the historical measurements. Our results show that the reported distributions are very robust and remain effectively unchanged for simulated errors many times higher than the imprecision mentioned in the description of the data set. Please note that the error bars in the left panel of Fig.
4
come from the imprecision of the fit and are not related to the potential imprecision of the data. Finally, let us mention that the reported distribution of waiting times are almost identical across the individual main periods included in the data set,
i.e.
, 1905–1938 and 1939–2023, for small values of
\delta
, while for large values of
\delta
, when the observed distributions are exponential-like, one notices small differences in the fine structure of the distribution curves for long waiting times.
