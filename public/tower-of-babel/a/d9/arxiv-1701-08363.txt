Goodness-of-fit tests for the functional linear model based on randomly projected empirical processes
Juan A. Cuesta-Albertos, Eduardo García-Portugués, Manuel Febrero-Bande, Wenceslao González-Manteiga

Abstract
We consider marked empirical processes indexed by a randomly projected functional covariate to construct goodness-of-fit tests for the functional linear model with scalar response. The test statistics are built from continuous functionals over the projected process, resulting in computationally efficient tests that exhibit root- n n convergence rates and circumvent the curse of dimensionality. The weak convergence of the empirical process is obtained conditionally on a random direction, whilst the almost surely equivalence between the testing for significance expressed on the original and on the projected functional covariate is proved. The computation of the test in practice involves calibration by wild bootstrap resampling and the combination of several p p -values, arising from different projections, by means of the false discovery rate method. The finite sample properties of the tests are illustrated in a simulation study for a variety of linear models, underlying processes, and alternatives. The software provided implements the tests and allows the replication of simulations and data applications.

Introduction

The term “goodness-of-fit” was introduced at the beginning of the twentieth century by Karl Pearson, and, since then, there have been an enormous amount of papers devoted to this topic: first, concentrated on fitting a model for one distribution function, and, later, especially after the papers of
Bickel and Rosenblatt, 1973
and
Durbin, 1973
, on more general models related with the regression function. Considering a regression model with random design
Y=m(X)+\varepsilon
, the goal is to test the goodness-of-fit of a class of parametric regression functions
\mathcal{M}_{\Theta}:=\left\{m_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}:\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}^{q}\right\}
to the data. This is the testing of

H_{0}:m\in\mathcal{M}_{\Theta}\quad\text{vs.}\quad H_{1}:m\notin\mathcal{M}_{\Theta}

in an omnibus way from a sample
\left\{\left(X_{i},Y_{i}\right)\right\}_{i=1}^{n}
from
(X,Y)
. Here,
m(x)=\mathbb{E}\left[Y|X=x\right]
is the regression function of
Y
over
X
, and
\varepsilon
is a random error centred such that
\mathbb{E}\left[\varepsilon|{\bf X}\right]=0
. The literature of goodness-of-fit tests for the regression function is vast, and we refer to
González-Manteiga and Crujeiras, 2013
for an updated review of the topic.

Following the ideas on smoothing for testing the density function
(
Bickel and Rosenblatt, 1973
)
, the pilot estimators usually considered for
m
were nonparametric, for example, the Nadaraya–Watson estimator (
Nadaraya, 1964
,
Watson, 1964
):
\hat{m}_{h}(x):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}W_{ni}(x)Y_{i}
, with
W_{ni}(x):=K\big((x-X_{i})/h\big)\big/\allowbreak\sum_{j=1}^{n}K\big((x-X_{j})/h\big)
, where
K
is a kernel function and
h
is a bandwidth parameter. Using these kinds of pilot estimators, statistical tests were given by
T_{n}=d\left(\hat{m},m_{\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\right)
, with
d
some functional distance and
\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
an estimator of
\boldsymbol{\theta}
such that
\sqrt{n}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\boldsymbol{\theta})=\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}}(1)
under
H_{0}
. Alternatively, following the paper by
Durbin, 1973
for testing about the distribution, the pilot estimator in the regression case was given by
I_{n}(x):=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathds{1}_{\left\{X_{i}\leq x\right\}}Y_{i}
, and the empirical estimation of the integrated regression function was then
I(x):=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathds{1}_{\left\{X\leq x\right\}}Y\right]
.
Härdle and Mammen, 1993
, using
\hat{m}_{h}
, and
Stute, 1997
, using
I_{n}
, are key references for these two approximations in the literature, and were only the beginning of more than two hundred papers published in the last two decades
(
González-Manteiga and Crujeiras, 2013
)
.

More recently, there has been a growing interest in testing possible structures in a regression setting in the presence of functional covariates:

\displaystyle Y=m({\bf X})+\varepsilon,

(1)

with
{\bf X}
a random element in a functional space, for example, in the Hilbert space
\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$}=L^{2}[0,1]
, and
Y
a scalar response. This is the context of “Functional Data Analysis”, which has received increasing attention in the last decade (see, e.g.,
Ramsay and Silverman, 2005
,
Ferraty and Vieu, 2006
, and
Horváth and Kokoszka, 2012
) due to the practical need to analyse data generated by high-resolution measuring devices.

A simple null hypothesis
H_{0}
considered in the literature for model (
1
) is
H_{0}:m({\bf X})=c
, where
c\in\mathbb{R}
is a fixed constant, that is, the testing of significance of the covariate
{\bf X}
over
Y
. Following some of the ideas from
Ferraty and Vieu, 2006
on considering pseudometrics for performing smoothing with functional data, the test by
Härdle and Mammen, 1993
was adapted by
Delsol et al., 2011a
as

\displaystyle T_{n,h}^{\mathrm{D}}:=

\displaystyle\;\int\left(\hat{m}_{h}({\bf x})-\bar{Y}\right)^{2}\omega({\bf x})\,\mathrm{d}P_{{\bf X}}({\bf x})=d(\hat{m}_{h},\bar{Y}),

\displaystyle\hat{m}_{h}({\bf x}):=

\displaystyle\;\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigg[K\left(\frac{\bar{d}({\bf x},{\bf X}_{i})}{h}\right)Y_{i}\bigg/\sum_{j=1}^{n}K\left(\frac{\bar{d}({\bf X}_{i},{\bf X}_{j})}{h}\right)\bigg],

with
\bar{d}
a functional pseudometric,
K
a kernel function adapted to this situation,
h
a bandwidth parameter,
\omega
a weight function, and
P_{{\bf X}}
the probability measure induced by
{{\bf X}}
in
{\mathcal{H}}
. Testing
H_{0}
has also been considered by
Cardot et al., 2003
and
Hilgert et al., 2013
, not in an omnibus way, but inside a Functional Linear Model (FLM):
m({\bf X})=\left<{\bf X},\boldsymbol{\rho}\right>
, where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
represents the inner product in
{\mathcal{H}}
and
\boldsymbol{\rho}\in\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$}
is the FLM parameter. For both approximations, omnibus or not, there have also been other papers which consider the functional response case; see, for example,
Chiou and Müller, 2007
,
Kokoszka et al., 2008
, and
Bücher et al., 2011
.

The generalization of the hypothesis
H_{0}:m({\bf X})=c
to the general case

\displaystyle H_{0}:m\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{P}}=\left\{m_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}:\boldsymbol{\rho}\in\mathcal{P}\right\}\quad\text{vs.}\quad H_{1}:m\notin\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{P}},

(2)

where
\mathcal{P}
can be of either finite or infinite dimension, has been the focus of very few papers, particularly in the context of omnibus goodness-of-fit tests. In
Delsol et al., 2011b
, a discussion is given, without theoretical results, for the extension of the testing of a more complex null hypothesis, such as an FLM. Only one paper is known to us in which the FLM hypothesis is analysed with theoretical results. In
Patilea et al., 2012
, motivated by the smoothing test statistic considered by
Zheng, 1996
for finite dimensional covariates, a test based on

\displaystyle T_{n,h}^{\mathrm{P}}:=\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{1\leq i\neq j\leq n}

\displaystyle\left(Y_{i}-\hat{m}_{H_{0}}({\bf X}_{i})\right)\left(Y_{j}-\hat{m}_{H_{0}}({\bf X}_{j})\right)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\frac{1}{h}K\left(\frac{F_{n,{\bf h}}(\left<{\bf X}_{i},{\bf h}\right>)-F_{n,{\bf h}}(\left<{\bf X}_{j},{\bf h}\right>)}{h}\right),

is employed for checking the null hypothesis of linearity with
\hat{m}_{H_{0}}({\bf X}):=\left<{\bf X},\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\right>
,
\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}
a suitable estimator of
\boldsymbol{\rho}
, and
F_{n,{\bf h}}
the empirical distribution function of
\{\left<{\bf X}_{i},{\bf h}\right>\}_{i=1}^{n}
. In the same spirit,
Lavergne and Patilea, 2008
developed a test for the finite dimensional context, and
Patilea et al., 2016
provided a test for functional response. From a different perspective, and motivated by the test given by
Escanciano, 2006
for finite dimensional predictors,
García-Portugués et al., 2014
constructed a test from the marked empirical process
I_{n,{\bf h}}(x):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathds{1}_{\left\{\left<{\bf X}_{i},{\bf h}\right>\leq x\right\}}Y_{i}
, with
x\in\mathbb{R}
, and
{\bf h}\in\mathcal{H}
. The test statistic averages the Cramér–von Mises norm of
I_{n,{\bf h}}
over a finite-dimensional, estimation-driven space of random directions
{\bf h}
. Although this approach circumvents the technical difficulties that a marked empirical process indexed by
{\bf x}\in\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$}
would represent (a possible functional extension of the process given in
Stute, 1997
), no results on the convergence of the statistic are available.

In this paper, we consider marked empirical processes indexed by random projections of the functional covariate. The motivation stems from the almost surely characterization of the null hypothesis (
2
) via a
projected hypothesis
that arises from the conditional expectation on the projected functional covariate. This allows, conditionally on a randomly chosen
{\bf h}
, the study of the weak convergence of the process
I_{n,{\bf h}}(x)
for hypothesis testing with infinite-dimensional covariates and parameters. As a by-product, we obtain root-
n
goodness-of-fit tests that evade the curse of dimensionality and, contrary to smoothing-based tests, do not rely on a tuning parameter. In particular, we focus on the testing of the aforementioned hypothesis of functional linearity where, contrary to the finite dimensional situation, the functional estimator has a nontrivial effect on the limiting process and requires careful regularization. The test statistics are built by a continuous functional (Kolmogorov–Smirnov or Cramér–von Mises) over the empirical process and are effectively calibrated by a wild bootstrap on the residuals. To account for a higher power and less influence from
{\bf h}
, we consider a number
K
(not to be confused with a kernel function) of different random directions and merge the resulting
p
-values into a final
p
-value by means of the False Discovery Rate (FDR) of
Benjamini and Yekutieli, 2001
. The empirical analysis reports a competitive performance of the test in practice, with a low impact of the choice of
K
above a certain bound, and an expedient computational complexity of
\mathcal{O}(n)
that yields notable speed improvements over
García-Portugués et al., 2014
.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. The characterization of the null hypothesis through the projected predictor is addressed in Section
2
, together with an application for the testing of the null hypothesis
H_{0}:m=m_{0}
(Subsection
2.1
). Section
3
is devoted to testing the composite hypothesis
H_{0}:m\in\{\left<\cdot,\boldsymbol{\rho}\right>:\boldsymbol{\rho}\in\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$}\}
. To that aim, the regularized estimator for
\boldsymbol{\rho}
of
Cardot et al., 2007
,
\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}
, is reviewed in Subsection
3.1
. The pointwise asymptotic distribution of the projected process is studied in Subsection
3.2
, whereas Subsection
3.3
gives its weak convergence. Section
4
describes the implementation of the test and other practicalities. Section
5
illustrates the finite sample properties of the test through a simulation study and with some real data applications. Some final comments and possible extensions are given in Section
6
. Appendix
A
presents the main proofs, whereas the supplementary material contains the auxiliary lemmas and further results from the simulation study.

1.1
General setting and notation

Some of the general setting and notation considered in the paper are introduced now, while more specific notation will be introduced when required. The random variable (r.v.)
{\bf X}
belongs to a separable Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}
endowed with the inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
and associated norm
\|\cdot\|
. The space
{\mathcal{H}}
is a general real Hilbert space, but, for simplicity, it can be regarded as
\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$}=L^{2}[0,1]
.
Y
and
{\bf X}
are assumed to be centred r.v.’s providing an independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) sample
\{({\bf X}_{i},Y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}\subset\mathcal{H}\times\mathbb{R}
.
\varepsilon
is a centred r.v. with variance
\sigma^{2}_{\varepsilon}
that is independent from
{\bf X}
(the independence between
\varepsilon
and
{\bf X}
is a technical assumption required for proving Lemmas
A.4
and
A.5
, while for the rest of the paper it suffices that
\mathbb{E}\left[\varepsilon|{\bf X}\right]=0
). Given the
\mathcal{H}
-valued r.v.
{\bf X}
and
{\bf h}\in\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$}
, we denote by
{\bf X}^{\bf h}:=\langle{\bf X},{\bf h}\rangle
the projected
{\bf X}
in the direction
{\bf h}
, by
F_{{\bf h}}
the distribution function of
{\bf X}^{\bf h}
, and by
P_{\bf X}
the probability measure of
\mathbf{X}
in
{\mathcal{H}}
. Bold letters are used for vectors in
\mathcal{H}
(mainly) or column vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
(whose transposition is denoted by
′
), and the type is clearly determined by the context. Capital letters represent r.v.’s defined on the same probability space
(\Omega,\sigma,\nu)
and
\sim
denotes equality in distribution. Weak convergence is denoted by
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{L}}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}
and
D(\mathbb{R})
represents the Skorohod’s space of
càdlàg
functions defined on
\mathbb{R}
. Finally, we shall implicitly assume that the null hypotheses stated hold almost surely (a.s.).

2
Hypothesis projection

The pillar of the goodness-of-fit tests we present is the a.s. characterization of the null hypothesis (
2
), re-expressed as
H_{0}:\mathbb{E}[Y-m_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}({\bf X})|{\bf X}]=0
for some
\boldsymbol{\rho}\in\mathcal{P}
, by means of the associated
projected hypothesis on
{\bf h}\in\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$}
, defined as
H_{0}^{\bf h}:\mathbb{E}\left[Y-m_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}({\bf X})|{\bf X}^{{\bf h}}\right]=0
. In the following, we identify
Y-m_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}({\bf X})
by
Y
for the sake of simplicity in notation. In this section, we give two necessary and sufficient conditions based on the projections of
{\bf X}
such that
\mathbb{E}\left[Y|{\bf X}\right]=0
holds a.s.

The first condition only requires the integrability of
Y
, but the condition needs to be satisfied for every direction
{\bf h}
.

Proposition 2.1
.

Assume that
\mathbb{E}\left[|Y|\right]<\infty
. Then

\mathbb{E}\left[Y|{\bf X}\right]=0\text{ a.s.}\iff\mathbb{E}\big[Y|{\bf X}^{\bf h}\big]=0\text{ a.s. for every }{\bf h}\in\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$}.

The second condition, more adequate for application,
somehow
generalizes Proposition
2.1
, as it only needs to be satisfied for a randomly chosen
{\bf h}
. In exchange, it holds only under some additional conditions on the moments of
{\bf X}
and
Y
. Before stating it, we need some preliminary results, the first taken from
Cuesta-Albertos et al., 2007b
and included here for the sake of completeness.

Lemma 2.2

(Theorem 4.1 in
Cuesta-Albertos et al., 2007b
)
.

Let
\mu
be a nondegenerate Gaussian measure on
{\mathcal{H}}
and
{\bf X}_{1},{\bf X}_{2}
be two
{\mathcal{H}}
-valued r.v.’s defined on
(\Omega,\sigma,\nu)
. Assume that:

(a)

m_{k}:=\int\|{\bf X}_{1}\|^{k}\,\mathrm{d}\nu<\infty
, for all
k\geq 1
, and

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}m_{k}^{-1/k}=\infty
.

(b)

The set
\{{\bf h}\in\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$}:{\bf X}^{\bf h}_{1}\sim{\bf X}^{\bf h}_{2}\}
is of positive
\mu
-measure.

Then
{\bf X}_{1}\sim{\bf X}_{2}
.

Remark 2.2.1
.

The Gaussianity of
\mu
in Lemma
2.2
is not strictly required. It can be replaced by assuming a certain smoothness condition on
\mu
(see, for instance, Theorem 2.5 and Example 2.6 in
Cuesta-Albertos et al., 2007a
).

Remark 2.2.2
.

Assumption
(a)
in Lemma
2.2
is not of a technical nature. According to Theorem 3.6 in
Cuesta-Albertos et al., 2007b
, it becomes apparent that a similar condition is required. This assumption is satisfied if the tails of
P_{{\bf X}_{1}}
are light enough or if
{\bf X}_{1}
has a finite moment generating function in a neighbourhood of zero.

Lemma 2.3
.

If
\mathbb{E}\left[Y^{2}\right]<\infty
and
{\bf X}
satisfies
(a)
in Lemma
2.2
,
then
l_{k}:=\mathbb{E}\left[\|{\bf X}\|^{k}|Y|\right]\allowbreak<\infty
for all
k\geq 1
, and
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}l_{k}^{-1/k}=\infty
.

The second condition, and most important result in this section, is given as follows.

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
\mu
be a nondegenerate Gaussian measure on
{\mathcal{H}}
. Assume that
{\bf X}
satisfies
(a)
in Lemma
2.2
and that
\mathbb{E}\left[Y^{2}\right]<\infty
. If we denote
\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$}_{0}:=\big\{{\bf h}\in\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$}:\mathbb{E}\big[Y|{\bf X}^{\bf h}\big]=0\text{ a.s.}\big\}
, then

\mathbb{E}\left[Y|{\bf X}\right]=0\text{ a.s.}\iff\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$}_{0}\text{ has positive $\mu$-measure.}

Corollary 2.5
.

Under the assumptions of the previous theorem,

\mathbb{E}\left[Y|{\bf X}\right]=0\text{ a.s.}\iff\mu\big(\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$}_{0}\big)=1.

According to this corollary, if we are interested in testing the simple null hypothesis
H_{0}:\mathbb{E}\left[Y|{\bf X}\right]=0
, then we can do so as follows: (
i
) select, at random with
\mu
, a direction
{\bf h}\in\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$}
; (
ii
) conditionally on
{\bf h}
, test the projected null hypothesis
H_{0}^{\bf h}:\mathbb{E}\big[Y|{\bf X}^{\bf h}\big]=0
. The rationale is simple yet powerful: if
H_{0}
holds, then
H_{0}^{\bf h}
also holds; if
H_{0}
fails, then
H_{0}^{\bf h}
also fails
\mu
-a.s. In this case, with probability one, we have chosen a direction
{\bf h}
for which
H_{0}^{\bf h}
fails. Of course, the main advantage to testing
H_{0}^{\bf h}
over testing
H_{0}
directly is that in
H_{0}^{\bf h}
the conditioning r.v. is real, which simplifies the problem substantially.

Remark 2.5.1
.

The set of directions for which
H_{0}
is not congruent with
H_{0}^{\mathbf{h}}
has measure zero. A concrete example of this set is given as follows. Suppose we are interested in testing if a random
p
-vector
\mathbf{X}
is Gaussian. By the Crámer–Wold device,
\mathbf{X}
is Gaussian if and only if
\mathbf{a}^{\prime}\mathbf{X}
is Gaussian for any
\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
. However, by Theorem 3.6 in
Cuesta-Albertos et al., 2007a
, it suffices that
\mathbf{h}^{\prime}\mathbf{X}
is Gaussian for a
single
,
randomly chosen
direction
\mathbf{h}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
. Then the zero-measure set in which
H_{0}^{\mathbf{h}}
and
H_{0}
are incongruent (precisely,
H_{0}^{\mathbf{h}}
holds, but
H_{0}
does not) is the set of the
projection counterexamples

\{\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}:\mathbf{a}^{\prime}\mathbf{X}\text{ is Gaussian},\mathbf{X}\text{ is not Gaussian}\}
. For example, for
\mathbf{X}\sim(\mathrm{Exp}(1),\,\mathcal{N}(0,1))
, the set is
\{(0,\lambda):\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\}
. Obviously, if
{\bf h}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
is chosen at random with a nondegenerate measure
\mu
, it is impossible that
{\bf h}
lies exactly on this line.

2.1
Testing a simple null hypothesis

An immediate application of Corollary
2.5
is the testing of the simple null hypothesis
H_{0}:m=m_{0}
via the empirical process
I_{n}
of
Stute, 1997
. Recall that other testing alternatives can be considered on the projected covariate due to the
\mu
-a.s. characterization. We refer to
González-Manteiga and Crujeiras, 2013
for a review of alternatives.

For a random sample
\{({\bf X}_{i},Y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
from
({\bf X},Y)
, we consider the empirical process of the regression conditioned on the direction
{\bf h}
,

R_{n,{\bf h}}(x):=n^{1/2}I_{n,{\bf h}}(x)=n^{-1/2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathds{1}_{\left\{{\bf X}_{i}^{\bf h}\leq x\right\}}Y_{i},\quad x\in\mathbb{R}.

Then the following result is trivially satisfied using Theorem 1.1 in
Stute, 1997
.

Corollary 2.6
.

Under
H_{0}^{\bf h}
and
\mathbb{E}\left[Y^{2}\right]<\infty
,
R_{n,{\bf h}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{L}}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}_{1}
in
D(\mathbb{R})
, with
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}_{1}
a Gaussian process with zero mean and covariance function
K_{1}(s,t):=\int_{-\infty}^{s\wedge t}\allowbreak\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}\left[Y|{\bf X}^{\bf h}=u\right]\allowbreak\,\mathrm{d}F_{{\bf h}}(u)
.

Different statistics for the testing of
H_{0}^{\bf h}
can be built from continuous functionals on
R_{n,{\bf h}}(x)
. We shall cover this in more detail in Section
3
.

Example 2.7
.

Consider the FLM
Y=\left<{\bf X},\boldsymbol{\rho}\right>+\varepsilon
in
\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$}=L^{2}[0,1]
, with
{\bf X}
a Gaussian process with associated Karhunen–Loéve expansion (
5
) below, and
\varepsilon
independent from
{\bf X}
. Then
{\bf X}^{{\bf h}}
and
{\bf X}^{\boldsymbol{\rho}}
are centred Gaussians with variances
\sigma^{2}_{{\bf h}}
and
\sigma^{2}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}
, respectively, and
\mathbb{C}\mathrm{ov}[{\bf X}^{{\bf h}},{\bf X}^{\boldsymbol{\rho}}]=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}h_{j}\rho_{j}\lambda_{j}
, with
h_{j}:=\left<{\bf h},{\bf e}_{j}\right>
, and
\rho_{j}:=\left<\boldsymbol{\rho},{\bf e}_{j}\right>
. Hence,

\displaystyle K^{1}(s,t)=

\displaystyle\;\int_{-\infty}^{s\wedge t}\Bigg(\frac{\sigma^{2}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\sigma^{2}_{{\bf h}}-\big(\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}h_{j}\rho_{j}\lambda_{j}\big)^{2}}{\sigma^{2}_{{\bf h}}}+\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}\Bigg)\phi(u/\sigma_{{\bf h}})/\sigma_{{\bf h}}\,\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;\Bigg(\frac{\sigma^{2}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\sigma^{2}_{{\bf h}}-\big(\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}h_{j}\rho_{j}\lambda_{j}\big)^{2}}{\sigma^{2}_{{\bf h}}}+\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}\Bigg)\Phi((s\wedge t)/\sigma_{{\bf h}}),

where
\phi
and
\Phi
are the density and distribution functions of a
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
, respectively.

3
Testing the functional linear model

We focus now on testing the composite null hypothesis, expressed as

\displaystyle H_{0}:m({\bf X})=\langle{\bf X},\boldsymbol{\rho}\rangle={\bf X}^{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\text{ for some }\boldsymbol{\rho}\in\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$}.

(3)

According to Corollary
2.5
, testing (
3
) is
\mu
-a.s. equivalent to testing

H_{0}^{\bf h}:\mathbb{E}\left[\left(Y-{\bf X}^{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\right)\big|{\bf X}^{\bf h}\right]=0\text{ for some }\boldsymbol{\rho}\in\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$},

where
{\bf h}
is sampled from a nondegenerate Gaussian law
\mu
. Again, we construct the associated empirical regression process indexed by the projected covariate following
Stute, 1997
. Therefore, given an estimate
\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}
of
\boldsymbol{\rho}
under
H_{0}
, we consider

\displaystyle T_{n,{\bf h}}(x):=a_{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathds{1}_{\left\{{\bf X}_{i}^{\bf h}\leq x\right\}}\left(Y_{i}-{\bf X}_{i}^{\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}}\right)=a_{n}\left(T_{n,{\bf h}}^{1}(x)+T_{n,{\bf h}}^{2}(x)+T_{n,{\bf h}}^{3}(x)\right),

(4)

where
a_{n}\rightarrow 0
is a normalizing positive sequence to be determined later and

\displaystyle T_{n,{\bf h}}^{1}(x):=

\displaystyle\;\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathds{1}_{\left\{{\bf X}_{i}^{\bf h}\leq x\right\}}\left(Y_{i}-{\bf X}_{i}^{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\right),

\displaystyle T_{n,{\bf h}}^{2}(x):=

\displaystyle\;\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left<\mathds{1}_{\left\{{\bf X}_{i}^{\bf h}\leq x\right\}}{\bf X}_{i}-\mathbb{E}\left[\mathds{1}_{\left\{{\bf X}^{\bf h}\leq x\right\}}{\bf X}\right],\boldsymbol{\rho}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\right>,

\displaystyle T_{n,{\bf h}}^{3}(x):=

\displaystyle\;n\left<\mathbb{E}\left[\mathds{1}_{\left\{{\bf X}^{\bf h}\leq x\right\}}{\bf X}\right],\boldsymbol{\rho}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\right>.

The selection of the right estimator
\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}
has a crucial role in the weak convergence of
T^{3}_{n,{\bf h}}
, which requires a substantially more complex proof than that for the simple hypothesis. We consider the regularized estimate proposed in Sections 2 and 3 of
Cardot et al., 2007
(subsequently denoted by CMS), whose construction is sketched here for the sake of the exposition of our results.

3.1
Construction of the estimator of
\boldsymbol{\rho}

Consider the so-called Karhunen–Loéve expansion of
{\bf X}
:

\displaystyle{\bf X}=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{j}^{1/2}\xi_{j}{\bf e}_{j}.

(5)

Here,
\left\{\mathbf{e}_{j}\right\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
is a sequence of orthonormal eigenfunctions associated with the covariance operator of
{\bf X}
,
\Gamma{\bf z}:=\mathbb{E}\left[({\bf X}\otimes{\bf X})({\bf z})\right]
,
{\bf z}\in\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$}
, and the
\xi_{j}
’s are centred real r.v.’s (because
{\bf X}
is centred) such that
\mathbb{E}\left[\xi_{j}\xi_{j^{\prime}}\right]=\delta_{j,j^{\prime}}
, where
\delta_{j,j^{\prime}}
is the Kronecker’s delta. The Kronecker operator
\otimes
is such that
({\bf x}\otimes{\bf y}){\bf z}=\left<{\bf z},{\bf x}\right>{\bf y}
for
{\bf x},{\bf y},{\bf z}\in\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$}
. We assume that the multiplicity of each eigenvalue is one, so
\lambda_{1}>\lambda_{2}>\ldots>0
.

The functional coefficient
\boldsymbol{\rho}
is determined by the equation
\Delta=\Gamma\boldsymbol{\rho}
, with
\Delta
the cross-covariance operator of
{\bf X}
and
Y
,
\Delta{\bf z}:=\mathbb{E}\left[({\bf X}\otimes Y)({\bf z})\right]
,
{\bf z}\in\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$}
. To ensure the existence and uniqueness of a solution to
\Delta=\Gamma\boldsymbol{\rho}
, we require the next basic assumptions:

A1
.

{\bf X}
and
Y
satisfy
\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\lambda_{j}^{2}}\langle\mathbb{E}\left[{\bf X}Y\right],{\bf e}_{j}\rangle^{2}<\infty
.

A2
.

The kernel of
\Gamma
is
\{\mathbf{0}\}
.

The estimation of
\boldsymbol{\rho}
requires the inversion of
\Gamma_{n}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\bf X}_{i}\otimes{\bf X}_{i}
, but, since
\Gamma_{n}
is a.s. a finite rank operator, its inverse does not exist. CMS proposed a regularization yielding a family of continuous estimators for
\Gamma^{-1}
. Based on their Example 1, we define
\Gamma_{n}^{\dagger}
, an empirical finite rank estimate of
\Gamma^{-1}
:

\Gamma_{n}^{\dagger}:=\sum_{i=1}^{k_{n}}\frac{1}{\hat{\lambda}_{j}}\hat{{\bf e}}_{j}\otimes\hat{{\bf e}}_{j}.

The construction of
\Gamma_{n}^{\dagger}
(resp., the population version
\Gamma^{\dagger}:=\sum_{i=1}^{k_{n}}\frac{1}{\lambda_{j}}{\bf e}_{j}\otimes{\bf e}_{j}
) is done by considering a sequence of thresholds
c_{n}\in(0,\lambda_{1})
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, with
c_{n}\rightarrow 0
. The procedure is as follows: (
i
) compute the Functional Principal Components (FPC) of
{\bf X}_{1},\ldots,{\bf X}_{n}
, that is, calculate the eigenvalues
\{\hat{\lambda}_{j}\}
and eigenfunctions
\{\hat{\bf e}_{j}\}
of
\Gamma_{n}
; (
ii
) define the sequences
\left\{\delta_{j}\right\}
, with
\delta_{1}:=\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}
and
\delta_{j}:=\min(\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{j+1},\lambda_{j-1}-\lambda_{j})
for
j>1
, and set

k_{n}:=\sup\{j\in\mathbb{N}:\lambda_{j}+\delta_{j}/2\geq c_{n}\};

(
iii
) compute
\Gamma_{n}^{\dagger}
(resp.,
\Gamma^{\dagger}
) as the finite rank operator with the same eigenfunctions as
\Gamma_{n}
(resp.,
\Gamma
) and associated eigenvalues equal to
\hat{\lambda}_{j}^{-1}
(resp.,
\lambda_{j}^{-1}
) if
j\leq k_{n}
and
0
otherwise. The regularized estimator of
\boldsymbol{\rho}
is then

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}:=\Gamma_{n}^{\dagger}\Delta_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{k_{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\left<{\bf X}_{i}\otimes Y_{i},\hat{\bf e}_{j}\right>}{\hat{\lambda}_{j}}\hat{{\bf e}_{j}}.

(6)

Note that (
6
) is not readily computable in practice, since
\{\lambda_{j}\}
is usually unknown (and hence,
k_{n}
). As in CMS, we consider the (random) finite rank

d_{n}:=\sup\{j\in\mathbb{N}:\hat{\lambda}_{j}\geq c_{n}\}

as a replacement in practice for the deterministic
k_{n}
. As seen in Lemma
A.2
,
\nu[k_{n}=d_{n}]\rightarrow 1
. Hence the estimator (
6
) has the same asymptotic behaviour with either
k_{n}
or
d_{n}
. Therefore, we consider
k_{n}
in (
6
) due to the enhanced probabilistic tractability. The consideration of
k_{n}
in
\Gamma_{n}^{\dagger}
, instead of
d_{n}
, is the main difference between our definition of
\Gamma_{n}^{\dagger}
and the proposal given from Example 1 in CMS.

The following assumptions allow us to obtain meaningful asymptotic convergences involving
\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}
:

A3
.

\mathbb{E}\left[\|{\bf X}\|^{2}\right]<\infty
.

A4
.

\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}|\langle\boldsymbol{\rho},{\bf e}_{l}\rangle|<\infty
.

A5
.

For
j
large,
\lambda_{j}=\lambda(j)
, with
\lambda(\cdot)
a convex positive function.

A6
.

\frac{\lambda_{n}n^{4}}{\log n}=\mathcal{O}(1)
.

A7
.

\inf\left\{|\langle\boldsymbol{\rho},{\bf e}_{k_{n}}\rangle|,\frac{\lambda_{k_{n}}}{\sqrt{k_{n}\log k_{n}}}\right\}=\mathcal{O}(n^{-1/2})
.

A8
.

\sup_{j}\left\{\max\big(\mathbb{E}\big[\xi_{j}^{4}\big],\mathbb{E}\big[|\xi_{j}|^{5}\big]\big)\right\}\leq M<\infty
for
M\geq 1
.

A9
.

There exist
C_{1},C_{2}>0
such that
C_{1}n^{-1/2}<c_{n}<C_{2}n^{-1/2}
for every
n
.

A brief summary of these assumptions is given as follows.
A3
is standard for obtaining asymptotic distributions, allows decomposition (
5
), and implies
\mathbb{E}\left[Y^{2}\right]<\infty
, which is required in Theorem 1.1 of
Stute, 1997
.
A4
and
A5
are A.1 and A.2 in CMS.
A6
is very similar to an assumption in the second part of Theorem 2 in CMS.
A7
is the minimum requirement for controlling
\langle{\bf X},{\bf L}_{n}\rangle
(to be detailed in Section
A.2
) when Lemma 7 in CMS is used to prove Lemma
A.7
.
A8
is a reinforcement of A.3 in CMS, where only fourth-order moments are used. This is because we handle inner products of
\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}
times a nonindependent r.v., while in CMS the r.v. is not used to estimate
\boldsymbol{\rho}
.
A9
is useful, mainly (but also see the final part of Lemma
A.6
) to control the behaviour of
k_{n}
. We show this fact in Proposition
A.1
, with a conclusion very close to assumption (8) in CMS and coinciding with one of the conditions of their Theorem 3 if
\lim_{n}t_{n,\mathbf{E}_{x,{\bf h}}}<\infty
(the term
t_{n,\mathbf{E}_{x,{\bf h}}}
is defined in (
7
) below). Finally, we point out that in CMS the assumptions aim to control the behaviour of
k_{n}
while here we have sought to control the threshold
c_{n}
, as this can be modified by the statistician.

3.2
Pointwise asymptotic distribution of
T_{n,{\bf h}}

Corollary
2.6
shows the weak convergence of
n^{-1/2}T_{n,{\bf h}}^{1}
. We analyse now the pointwise behaviour of
T_{n,{\bf h}}^{2}(x)
and
T_{n,{\bf h}}^{3}(x)
for a fixed
x\in\mathbb{R}
. We will show that
T_{n,{\bf h}}^{2}(x)=o_{\mathbb{P}}(n^{1/2})
and that the rate of
T_{n,{\bf h}}^{3}(x)
depends on the key normalizing sequence
\{t_{n,\mathbf{E}_{x,{\bf h}}}\}
, where

\displaystyle t_{n,{\bf x}}:=\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{k_{n}}\frac{\langle{\bf x},{\bf e}_{j}\rangle^{2}}{\lambda_{j}}}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbf{E}_{x,{\bf h}}:=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathds{1}_{\left\{{\bf X}^{\bf h}\leq x\right\}}{\bf X}\right].

(7)

Theorem 3.1
.

Under
H_{0}^{\bf h}
and
A1
–
A9
, and for a fixed
x\in\mathbb{R}
, it follows that:

(a)

n^{-1/2}t_{n,\mathbf{E}_{x,{\bf h}}}^{-1}T_{n,{\bf h}}^{3}(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{L}}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}_{\varepsilon})
.

(b)

If
\lim_{n}t_{n,\mathbf{E}_{x,{\bf h}}}=\infty
, then with
a_{n}=n^{-1/2}t_{n,\mathbf{E}_{x,{\bf h}}}^{-1}
in (
4
), the asymptotic distribution of
T_{n,{\bf h}}(x)
is
n^{-1/2}t_{n,\mathbf{E}_{x,{\bf h}}}^{-1}T_{n,{\bf h}}^{3}(x)
.

(c)

If
\lim_{n}t_{n,\mathbf{E}_{x,{\bf h}}}<\infty
, then with
a_{n}=n^{-1/2}
in (
4
), the asymptotic distribution of
T_{n,{\bf h}}(x)
is
n^{-1/2}\big(T_{n,{\bf h}}^{1}(x)+T_{n,{\bf h}}^{3}(x)\big)
.

The behaviour of the sequence
\{t_{n,\mathbf{E}_{x,{\bf h}}}\}
, indexed by
n\in\mathbb{N}
and with arbitrary
{\bf h}\in\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$}
and
x\in\mathbb{R}
, is crucial for the convergence of
T_{n,{\bf h}}
. Since
\{t_{n,\mathbf{E}_{x,{\bf h}}}\}
is nondecreasing, it has always a limit (finite or infinite). Its asymptotic behaviour is described next.

Proposition 3.2
.

The sequence
\{t_{n,\mathbf{E}_{x,{\bf h}}}\}
has asymptotic order between
\mathcal{O}(1)
and
\mathcal{O}\big(k_{n}^{1/2}\big)
. In addition, if
{\bf X}
is Gaussian and satisfies
A3
, then
\sigma_{{\bf h}}^{2}:=\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}\left[{\bf X}^{{\bf h}}\right]<\infty
and
\lim_{n}t_{n,\mathbf{E}_{x,{\bf h}}}=\phi(x/\sigma_{{\bf h}})
.

3.3
Weak convergence of
T_{n,{\bf h}}
and the test statistics

The result given in Theorem
3.1
holds for every
x\in\mathbb{R}
. For case
(c)
of Theorem
3.1
(where the estimation of
\boldsymbol{\rho}
is not dominant) and under an additional assumption, the result can be generalized to functional weak convergence.

Theorem 3.3
.

Under
H_{0}^{\bf h}
,
A1
–
A9
, and
(c)
in Theorem
3.1
, it follows that:

(a)

The finite dimensional distributions of
T_{n,{\bf h}}
converge to a multivariate Gaussian with covariance function
K_{2}(s,t):=K_{1}(s,t)+C(s,t)+C(t,s)+V(s,t)
, where

\displaystyle C(s,t):=

\displaystyle\;\int_{\{{\bf u}^{\bf h}\leq s\}}\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}\left[Y|{\bf X}={\bf u}\right]\left<\mathbf{E}_{t,{\bf h}},\Gamma^{-1}{\bf u}\right>\,\mathrm{d}P_{\bf X}({\bf u}),

\displaystyle V(s,t):=

\displaystyle\;\int\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}\left[Y|{\bf X}={\bf u}\right]\left<\mathbf{E}_{s,{\bf h}},\Gamma^{-1}{\bf u}\right>\left<\mathbf{E}_{t,{\bf h}},\Gamma^{-1}{\bf u}\right>\,\mathrm{d}P_{\bf X}({\bf u}).

(b)

If
\mathbb{E}\big[\left|\left|\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}-\boldsymbol{\rho}\right|\right|^{4}\big]=\mathcal{O}(n^{-2})
, then

T_{n,{\bf h}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{L}}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}_{2}
in
D(\mathbb{R})
, with
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}_{2}
a Gaussian process with zero mean and covariance function
K_{2}
.

Remark 3.3.1
.

According to Theorem 1 in CMS, it is impossible for
\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}-\boldsymbol{\rho}
to converge to a nondegenerate random element in the topology of
{\mathcal{H}}
. To circumvent this issue and obtain the tightness of
T_{n,{\bf h}}
, we assume
\mathbb{E}\big[\left|\left|\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}-\boldsymbol{\rho}\right|\right|^{4}\big]=\mathcal{O}(n^{-2})
, which implies
\left|\left|\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}-\boldsymbol{\rho}\right|\right|=\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}}(n^{-1/2})
, and, thus, a finite-dimensional parametric convergence rate for
\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}
. For instance, this happens when
\boldsymbol{\rho}
is a linear combination of a finite number of the eigenfunctions of
\Gamma
. Notice that this is not needed for the convergence of the finite dimensional distributions of
T_{n,{\bf h}}
.

The next result gives the convergence of the Kolmogorov–Smirnov (KS) and Cramér–von Mises (CvM) statistics for testing the FLM.

Corollary 3.4
.

Under the assumptions in Theorem
3.3
and
\mathbb{E}\big[\left|\left|\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}-\boldsymbol{\rho}\right|\right|^{4}\big]=\mathcal{O}(n^{-2})
, if
\|T_{n,{\bf h}}\|_{\mathrm{KS}}:=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}|T_{n,{\bf h}}(x)|
and
\|T_{n,{\bf h}}\|_{\mathrm{CvM}}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}}T_{n,{\bf h}}(x)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}F_{n,{\bf h}}(x)
, then

\displaystyle\|T_{n,{\bf h}}\|_{\mathrm{KS}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{L}}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}\|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}_{2}\|_{\mathrm{KS}}\text{ and }\|T_{n,{\bf h}}\|_{\mathrm{CvM}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{L}}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}_{2}(x)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}F_{{\bf h}}(x).

Remark 3.4.1
.

An alternative to
(b)
and Corollary
3.4
is to consider a deterministic discretization of the statistics, for which the convergence in law is trivial from
(a)
. For example, if
\|T_{n,{\bf h}}\|_{\widetilde{\mathrm{KS}}}:=\max_{k=1,\ldots,G}|T_{n,{\bf h}}(x_{k})|
for a grid
\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{G}\}
, then

\|T_{n,{\bf h}}\|_{\widetilde{\mathrm{KS}}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{L}}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}\|\mathbf{Z}_{2}\|_{\widetilde{\mathrm{KS}}}
, where
\mathbf{Z}_{2}\sim\mathcal{N}_{G}(\mathbf{0},\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
,
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{ij}=K_{2}(x_{i},x_{j})
.

4
Testing in practice

The major advantage of testing
H_{0}^{\bf h}
over
H_{0}
is that in
H_{0}^{\bf h}
the conditioning r.v. is real. The potential drawbacks of this universal method are a possible loss of power and that the outcome of the test may vary for different projections. Both inconveniences can be alleviated by sampling several directions
{\bf h}_{1},\ldots,{\bf h}_{K}
, testing the projected hypotheses
H_{0}^{{\bf h}_{1}},\ldots,H_{0}^{{\bf h}_{K}}
, and selecting an appropriate way to mix the resulting
p
-values. For example, using the FDR method proposed in
Benjamini and Yekutieli, 2001
(see Section 2.2.2 of
Cuesta-Albertos and Febrero-Bande, 2010
), it is possible to control the final rejection rate to be
at most

\alpha
under
H_{0}
.

The drawing of random directions is clearly influential in the power of the test. For example, in the extreme case where the directions are orthogonal to the data, that is,
{\bf X}^{\bf h}=0
, then
T_{n,{\bf h}}(x)=(n^{-1/2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{\varepsilon}_{i})\mathds{1}_{\{0\leq x\}}
and
\|T_{n,{\bf h}}\|_{\mathrm{N}}=\|T^{*b}_{n,{\bf h}}\|_{\mathrm{N}}=0
under
H_{0}
. Therefore, Algorithm
4.2
would fail to calibrate the level of the test and potentially yield spurious results due to numerical inaccuracies in
\|T^{*b}_{n,{\bf h}}\|_{\mathrm{N}}\leq\|T_{n,{\bf h}}\|_{\mathrm{N}}
. A data-driven compromise to avoid drawing directions in subspaces
almost
orthogonal to the data is the following: (
i
) compute the FPC of
{\bf X}_{1},\ldots,{\bf X}_{n}
, that is, the eigenpairs
\{(\hat{\lambda}_{j},\hat{\bf e}_{j})\}
; (
ii
) choose
j_{n}:=\min\big\{k=1,\ldots,n-1:(\sum_{j=1}^{k}\hat{\lambda}_{j}^{2})/(\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\hat{\lambda}_{j}^{2})\geq r\big\}
for a variance threshold
r
, for example,
r=0.95
; (
iii
) generate the data-driven Gaussian process
{\bf h}_{j_{n}}:=\sum_{j=1}^{j_{n}}\eta_{j}\hat{\bf e}_{j}
, with
\eta_{j}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,s_{j}^{2})
and
s_{j}^{2}
the sample variance of the scores in the
j
th FPC. Without loss of generality, we will use this data-driven projecting process for drawing
{\bf h}
in the rest of the paper (see the supplement for the consideration of other data generating processes). Formally, the Gaussian measure
\mu
associated with
{\bf h}_{j_{n}}
does not respect the assumptions in Theorem
2.4
, since it is degenerate (but recall that
\mu
does not have to be independent from
{\bf X}
). A nondegenerate Gaussian process can be obtained as
{\bf h}_{j_{n}}+\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}
, with
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}
a Gaussian process tightly concentrated around zero, albeit employing
{\bf h}_{j_{n}}
or
{\bf h}_{j_{n}}+\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}
has negligible effects in practice.

The testing procedure is described in the following generic algorithm.

Algorithm 4.1

(Testing procedure for
H_{0}
)
.

Let
T_{n}
denote a test for checking
H_{0}^{{\bf h}}
with
{\bf h}
chosen by a nondegenerate Gaussian measure
\mu
on
{\mathcal{H}}
:

(i)

For
i=1,\ldots,K
, set by
p_{i}
the
p
-value of
H_{0}^{{\bf h}_{i}}
obtained with the test
T_{n}
.

(ii)

Set the final
p
-value of
H_{0}
as
\min_{i=1,\ldots,K}\frac{K}{i}p_{(i)}
, where
p_{(1)}\leq\ldots\leq p_{(K)}
.

The calibration of the test statistic for
H_{0}^{{\bf h}}
is done by a wild bootstrap resampling. The next algorithm states the steps for testing the FLM. The particular case of the simple null hypothesis corresponds to
\boldsymbol{\rho}=\mathbf{0}
, so its calibration corresponds to setting
\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}=\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}^{*}=\mathbf{0}
in the algorithm.

Algorithm 4.2

(Bootstrap calibration in FLM testing)
.

Let
\left\{\left({\bf X}_{i},Y_{i}\right)\right\}_{i=1}^{n}
be an i.i.d. sample from (
1
) and a given
{\bf h}\in\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$}
. To test (
3
), proceed as follows:

(i)

Estimate
\boldsymbol{\rho}
by FPC for a given
d_{n}
and obtain
\hat{\varepsilon}_{i}=Y_{i}-\left<{\bf X}_{i},\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\right>
.

(ii)

Compute
\|T_{n,{\bf h}}\|_{\mathrm{N}}=\left|\left|n^{-1/2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathds{1}_{\left\{{\bf X}_{i}^{\bf h}\leq x\right\}}\hat{\varepsilon}_{i}\right|\right|_{\mathrm{N}}
with
\mathrm{N}
either
\mathrm{KS}
or
\mathrm{CvM}
.

(iii)

Bootstrap resampling. For
b=1,\ldots,B
:

(a)

Draw binary i.i.d. r.v.’s
V_{1}^{*},\ldots,V_{n}^{*}
such that
\mathbb{P}\left\{V^{*}=(1-\sqrt{5})/2\right\}\allowbreak=(5+\sqrt{5})/10
and
\mathbb{P}\left\{V^{*}=(1+\sqrt{5})/2\right\}=(5-\sqrt{5})/10
.

(b)

Set
Y_{i}^{*}:=\left<{\bf X}_{i},\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\right>+\varepsilon_{i}^{*}
using the bootstrap residuals
\varepsilon^{*}_{i}:=V_{i}^{*}\hat{\varepsilon}_{i}
.

(c)

Estimate
\boldsymbol{\rho}^{*}
from
\left\{\left({\bf X}_{i},Y_{i}^{*}\right)\right\}_{i=1}^{n}
by FPC using the same
d_{n}
used in
(i)
.

(d)

Obtain the estimated bootstrap residuals
\hat{\varepsilon}_{i}^{*}:=Y_{i}^{*}-\left<{\bf X}_{i},\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}^{*}\right>
.

(e)

Compute
\|T_{n,{\bf h}}^{*b}\|_{\mathrm{N}}:=\left|\left|n^{-1/2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathds{1}_{\left\{{\bf X}_{i}^{\bf h}\leq x\right\}}\hat{\varepsilon}_{i}^{*}\right|\right|_{\mathrm{N}}
.

(iv)

Approximate the
p
-value by
\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\mathds{1}_{\left\{\|T_{n,{\bf h}}^{*b}\|_{\mathrm{N}}\leq\|T_{n,{\bf h}}\|_{\mathrm{N}}\right\}}
.

Notice that the role of
c_{n}
is the selection of
d_{n}
in the estimation of
\boldsymbol{\rho}
. The selection of
d_{n}
can be done in a data-driven way by selecting from among a set of candidate
d_{n}
’s the optimal one in terms of a model-selection criterion. For example, we consider the corrected Schwartz Information Criterion
(
McQuarrie, 1999
)
, defined as
\mathrm{SICc}(d_{n}):=\ell(\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{d_{n}})+\frac{\log(n)d_{n}}{n-d_{n}-2}
, in order to overpenalize large
d_{n}
’s that generate noisy estimates of
\boldsymbol{\rho}
, especially for low sample sizes. In the previous expression,
\ell(\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{d_{n}})
represents the log-likelihood of the FLM for
\boldsymbol{\rho}
estimated with
d_{n}
FPC’s. Of course, this selection could be done in terms of the
c_{n}
’s that determine the
d_{n}
’s but, since the latter are directly related to the model complexity, its analysis is more convenient in practice. Note that steps
(iii)(c)
and
(iii)(d)
can be easily computed using the properties of the linear model; see Section 3.3 of
García-Portugués et al., 2014
.

The bootstrap process we are considering is given by (we consider
a_{n}=n^{-1/2}
)

\displaystyle T_{n,{\bf h}}^{*}\left(x\right):=n^{-1/2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathds{1}_{\left\{{\bf X}_{i}^{\bf h}\leq x\right\}}\hat{\varepsilon}_{i}^{*}=n^{-1/2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathds{1}_{\left\{{\bf X}_{i}^{\bf h}\leq x\right\}}\hat{\varepsilon}_{i}V_{i}^{*}+n^{-1/2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathds{1}_{\left\{{\bf X}_{i}^{\bf h}\leq x\right\}}{\bf X}_{i}^{\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}^{*}},

which is estimating the distribution of

T_{n,{\bf h}}(x)=n^{-1/2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathds{1}_{\left\{{\bf X}_{i}^{\bf h}\leq x\right\}}\hat{\varepsilon}_{i}+n^{-1/2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathds{1}_{\left\{{\bf X}_{i}^{\bf h}\leq x\right\}}{\bf X}_{i}^{\boldsymbol{\rho}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}}.

The bootstrap consistency could be obtained as an adaptation of Lemma A.1 of
Stute et al., 1998
for the first term of
T_{n,{\bf h}}^{*}
, Lemma A.2
ibid
for the second term, and using the decomposition of
\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}-\boldsymbol{\rho}
given in (11) in CMS.

.

We explore first the dependence of the tests with respect to the number of projections

. Figure

bootstrap replicates. There is a clear L-shaped pattern in the empirical rejection rate curves, which is produced by the conservativeness of the FDR correction — under
H_{0}
, it ensures that the rejection rate is
at most

\alpha
— when dealing with the highly-dependent projected tests. For small
K

K\approx 3
), both tests calibrate the three levels for different sample sizes reasonably well, with the main exception being

are almost constant or exhibit mild decrements, except for certain bumps at lower values of

that provide a significant power gain. Both facts point towards choosing the number of projections

and particularly

The tests based on the KS and CvM norms are compared with the test presented in
García-Portugués et al., 2014
(denoted by PCvM), available in the
R
package
fda.usc

(
Febrero-Bande and Oviedo de la Fuente, 2017
)
, and whose test statistic can be regarded as the average of projected CvM statistics. The test was run with the same FPC estimation used in the new tests, the same number of components
d_{n}
, and
B=10000
(considered also for CvM and KS). Table
1
presents the empirical rejection rates of the different simulation scenarios with
K=1,3,5
for KS and CvM tests. The results show two consistent patterns. First, in our simulation scenarios, the CvM test consistently dominates over the KS test, with only one exception:
H_{9,1}
with
n=50
(see supplement for the latter). This is coherent with the fact that quadratic norms in goodness-of-fit tests are often more powerful than sup-norms (see, e.g., p. 110 of
D’Agostino and Stephens, 1986
for the distribution case). Second, PCvM tends to have a larger power than CvM for most of the situations, especially for small sample sizes and mild deviations. As an illustration, for
n=50

3
with respect to PCvM is
12.7\%
(
d=1
) and

(

and
1.3\%

5.2\%
and
0.2\%
, respectively. The drop in performance for CvM with respect to PCvM is expected due to the construction of CvM, which opts for exploring a set of random directions instead of averaging uniformly distributed finite-dimensional directions, as PCvM does. This also yields one the strongest points of the CvM test, which is its relatively short running times, especially for large
n
. Not surprisingly, the number of evaluations performed for computing the CvM statistic is
\mathcal{O}(n)
, a notable reduction from PCvM’s
\mathcal{O}((n^{3}-n^{2})/2)

\mathcal{O}(n)

\mathcal{O}((n^{2}-n-2)/2)
. The running times in Figure
4
is evidence of this improvement.

The new tests were also applied to the two data applications described in

fda.usc

Ferraty and Vieu, 2006
as a motivating example for introducing nonlinear regression models. The dataset contains

of finely chopped meat samples. Covariates giving the fat, water, and protein content of the meat are also available in the dataset. Typically, the goal is to predict the fat content of a meat sample using the spectrometric curve or any of its derivatives, and, for that, the FLM has been proposed as a candidate model. We test its adequacy for the dataset with the new goodness-of-fit tests proposed. The
p
-values obtained for
K=3
projections and
B=10000
are
0.020
and
0.022
for CvM and KS, respectively. Using
\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}
with

selected by SICc in PCvM gave a

. Employing the first or second derivatives of the absorbance curves provided null

-values. In addition, the tests for
H_{0}:\boldsymbol{\rho}=\textbf{0}
also had null

-values for all of tests. As a consequence, we conclude that, at level
\alpha=0.05

6
Discussion

We have presented a new way of building goodness-of-fit tests for regression models with functional covariates employing random projections. The methodology was illustrated using randomly projected empirical processes, which provided root-
n
consistent tests for testing functional linearity. The calibration of the tests was done by a wild bootstrap resampling and the FDR was used to combine
K

p
-values coming from different projections to account for a higher power. The empirical analysis of the tests, conducted in a fully data-driven way, showed that, in our simulation scenarios, CvM yields higher powers than KS and that a selection of
K\in\{1,\ldots,5\}
, in particular
K=3
, is a reasonable compromise between respecting size and increasing power. There is still a price to pay in terms of a moderate loss of power with respect to the PCvM test, which averages across a set of uniformly distributed finite-dimensional directions. However, the reduction in computational complexity of the new tests is more than notable.

We conclude the paper by sketching some promising extensions of the methodology for the testing of more complex models involving functional covariates:

(a)

Testing the significance of the functional covariate of
({\bf X},{\bf W})\in\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$}\times\mathbb{R}^{q}
in the functional partially linear model
(
Aneiros-Pérez and Vieu, 2006
)

Y=m({\bf X})+{\bf W}^{\prime}{\boldsymbol{\beta}}+\varepsilon
. The process to be considered for a sample
\{\left({\bf X}_{i},{\bf W}_{i},Y_{i}\right)\}_{i=1}^{n}
and an estimator
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
such that
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-\boldsymbol{\beta}=\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}}(n^{-1/2})
is

n^{-1/2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathds{1}_{\left\{{\bf X}_{i}^{\bf h}\leq x\right\}}\big(Y_{i}-{\bf W}_{i}^{\prime}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\big)
.

(b)

Testing a functional quadratic regression model
(
Horvath and Reeder, 2013
)
.

(c)

Testing the significance of a functional linear model with functional response:

H_{0}:\mathbb{E}\left[{\bf Y}|{\bf X}\right]=\mathbf{0}
, where now
\left({\bf X},{\bf Y}\right)\in\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$}_{1}\times\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$}_{2}
and the associated empirical process is
n^{-1/2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathds{1}_{\big\{{\bf X}_{i}^{{\bf h}_{1}}\leq x\big\}}{\bf Y}_{i}^{{\bf h}_{2}}
.

A.1
Hypothesis projection

Proof of Proposition
2.1
.

We denote by
{\bf X}_{p}
both the vectors
(X_{1},\ldots,X_{p})^{\prime}
and
(X_{1},\ldots,X_{p},0,\ldots)^{\prime}
containing the first
p
coefficients of
{\bf X}
in an orthonormal basis of
{\mathcal{H}}
. We prove first the result for the finite subspace of
{\mathcal{H}}
spanned by the first
p
elements of the orthonormal basis. We need to show that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[Y|{\bf X}_{p}\right]=0\text{ a.s.}\iff\mathbb{E}\left[Y|\langle{\bf X}_{p},{\bf h}\rangle\right]=0\text{ a.s. for every }{\bf h}\in\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$}.

(8)

To prove this, we make use of Theorem 1 in
Bierens, 1982
, which states that if
{\bf V}
and
{\bf Z}
are two
\mathbb{R}^{p}
-valued random vectors, then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{\bf V}|{\bf Z}\right]=0\text{ a.s.}\iff\mathbb{E}\big[{\bf V}e^{i\langle\mathbf{t},\mathbf{Z}\rangle}\big]=0\text{ for every }{\bf t}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}.

(9)

Assume that
\mathbb{E}\left[Y|{\bf X}_{p}\right]=0
and let
{\bf h}\in\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$}
. Since the
\sigma
-algebra generated by
\langle{\bf X}_{p},{\bf h}\rangle
,
\sigma(\langle{\bf X}_{p},{\bf h}\rangle)
, is contained in
\sigma({\bf X}_{p})
, we have that

\mathbb{E}\left[Y|\langle{\bf X}_{p},{\bf h}\rangle\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[Y|{\bf X}_{p}\right]|\langle{\bf X}_{p},{\bf h}\rangle\right]=0
a.s.,
which shows the
if
part. To obtain the
only if
part, let
{\bf h}\in\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$}
, and compute

\mathbb{E}\big[Ye^{it\left\langle\mathbf{X}_{p},{\bf h}\right\rangle}\big]=\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}\big[Y|\langle{\bf X}_{p},{\bf h}\rangle\big]e^{it\left\langle\mathbf{X}_{p},{\bf h}\right\rangle}\big]=0
, for every
t\in\mathbb{R}
. Then (
8
) follows from (
9
).

Now we are in position to prove the result for
{\mathcal{H}}
. As before, the
if
implication follows from
\sigma(\langle{\bf X},{\bf h}\rangle)\subset\sigma({\bf X})
. To prove the
only if
implication, given
p\in\mathbb{N}
and
{\bf h}\in\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$}
, since
{\bf h}_{p}\in\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$}
and
\langle{\bf X},{\bf h}_{p}\rangle=\langle{\bf X}_{p},{\bf h}\rangle
, then
\sigma(\langle{\bf X}_{p},{\bf h}\rangle)\subset\sigma(\langle{\bf X},{\bf h}\rangle)
, and we have that the assumption implies that
\mathbb{E}\left[Y|\langle{\bf X}_{p},{\bf h}\rangle\right]=0
a.s. Thus, from (
8
), we have that
\mathbb{E}\left[Y|{\bf X}_{p}\right]=0
a.s. for every
p
, and the result follows from the fact that
\sigma({\bf X}_{p})\uparrow\sigma({\bf X})
because of the integrability assumption on
Y
.
∎

Proof of Lemma
2.3
.

From the properties of the conditional expectation, the Cauchy–Schwartz, and Jensen inequalities, we have that

l_{k}=\mathbb{E}\left[\|{\bf X}\|^{k}\mathbb{E}\left[|Y||{\bf X}\right]\right]\leq\left(m_{2k}\right)^{1/2}\big(\mathbb{E}\left[Y^{2}\right]\big)^{1/2}.

Thus
l_{k}
is finite. By the convexity of the function
t\mapsto t^{(2k+1)/2k}
and Jensen’s inequality,
m_{2k}^{1/2k}\leq m_{2k+1}^{1/(2k+1)}
. Hence,
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}m_{2k}^{-1/2k}=\infty
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
2.4
.

The
only if
part is trivial because
\sigma({\bf X}^{{\bf h}})\subset\sigma({\bf X})
, and then
\mathbb{E}\left[Y|{\bf X}\right]=0
a.s. implies that
\mu(\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$}_{0})=1
. Concerning the
if
part, let us assume that
\mu(\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$}_{0})>0
. From the assumptions, we have that
\mathbb{E}\left[|Y||{\bf X}\right]<\infty
, and, if we take
{\bf h}\in\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$}_{0}
, then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[Y\right]=\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}\big[Y\big|{\bf X}^{{\bf h}}\big]\big]=0.

(10)

Let us assume that
\mathbb{E}\left[Y|{\bf X}\right]
is not zero a.s. Then the random variables

\displaystyle\Phi^{+}({\bf X}):=

\displaystyle\left(\mathbb{E}\left[Y|{\bf X}\right]\right)^{+}=\max\left\{\mathbb{E}\left[Y|{\bf X}\right],0\right\},

\displaystyle\Phi^{-}({\bf X}):=

\displaystyle\left(\mathbb{E}\left[Y|{\bf X}\right]\right)^{-}=\max\left\{-\mathbb{E}\left[Y|{\bf X}\right],0\right\},

are integrable and positive with positive probability. Thus, (
10
) implies that

V:=\int\Phi^{+}({\bf x})\,\mathrm{d}P_{\mathbf{X}}({\bf x})=\int\Phi^{-}({\bf x})\,\mathrm{d}P_{\mathbf{X}}({\bf x})>0.

Consider now the probability measures
\nu_{\Phi}^{+}
and
\nu_{\Phi}^{-}
, which are defined on
\mathcal{H}
and whose Radon–Nikodym derivatives with respect to
P_{\mathbf{X}}
are, respectively,

\frac{\mathrm{d}\nu_{\Phi}^{+}}{\mathrm{d}P_{\mathbf{X}}}({\bf x}):=V^{-1}\Phi^{+}({\bf x})\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{\mathrm{d}\nu_{\Phi}^{-}}{\mathrm{d}P_{\mathbf{X}}}({\bf x}):=V^{-1}\Phi^{-}({\bf x}).

For
k\in\mathbb{N}
, the moments of
\nu_{\Phi^{+}}
verify that (analogously for
\Phi^{-}
)

\int\|{\bf x}\|^{k}\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{\Phi^{+}}({\bf x})\leq V^{-1}\int\|{\bf x}\|^{k}\mathbb{E}\left[|Y||{\bf X}={\bf x}\right]\,\mathrm{d}P_{\mathbf{X}}({\bf x})=l_{k},

and then, due to Lemma
2.3
, they satisfy
(a)
in Lemma
2.2
. Given
{\bf h}\in\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$}_{0}
, the r.v.
{\bf X}^{{\bf h}}
is
{\bf X}
-measurable. Thus, a.s.

0=\mathbb{E}\big[Y\big|{\bf X}^{{\bf h}}\big]=\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}\left[Y|{\bf X}\right]\big|{\bf X}^{{\bf h}}\big]=\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}\left[Y|{\bf X}\right]^{+}\big|{\bf X}^{{\bf h}}\big]-\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}\left[Y|{\bf X}\right]^{-}\big|{\bf X}^{{\bf h}}\big].

From here, it is easy to prove that the marginal distributions of
\nu_{\Phi}^{+}
and
\nu_{\Phi}^{-}
on the one-dimensional subspace generated by
{\bf X}^{{\bf h}}
coincide if
{\bf h}\in\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$}_{0}
. Since
\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$}_{0}
has a positive
\mu
-measure, from Lemma
2.2
, we obtain that these probability measures indeed coincide and, as a consequence,
V^{-1}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[Y|{\bf X}\right]\right)^{+}=V^{-1}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[Y|{\bf X}\right]\right)^{-}
a.s., which trivially implies that
\mathbb{E}\left[Y|{\bf X}\right]=0
a.s.
∎

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

n^{-1/2}T_{n,{\bf h}}^{1}(x)\allowbreak\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{L}}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}\mathcal{N}(0,K_{1}(x,x))

a_{n}=o(n^{-1/2})

a_{n}T_{n,{\bf h}}^{1}(x)=o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)
. The following two lemmas give insights into the asymptotic behaviour of
k_{n}
and are required for the analysis of
T_{n,{\bf h}}^{2}
and
T_{n,{\bf h}}^{3}
.

Lemma A.1
.

Under
A6
and
A9
,
k_{n}^{3}(\log k_{n})^{2}=o(n^{1/2})
.

Lemma A.2
.

Under
A6

Proof of Proposition
3.2
.

By the definition of
t_{n,\mathbf{E}_{x,{\bf h}}}
and (
5
),

t_{n,\mathbf{E}_{x,{\bf h}}}^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{k_{n}}\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\mathds{1}_{\left\{{\bf X}^{\bf h}\leq x\right\}}\langle{{\bf X}},{\bf e}_{j}\rangle\right]^{2}}{\lambda_{j}}=\sum_{j=1}^{k_{n}}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathds{1}_{\left\{{\bf X}^{\bf h}\leq x\right\}}\xi_{j}\right]^{2}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{k_{n}}\mathbb{E}\left[\xi_{j}^{2}\right]=k_{n}.

We assume now that
{\bf X}
is Gaussian. Obviously, the two-dimensional random vector
(\xi_{j},{\bf X}^{\bf h})
,
j\in\mathbb{N}
, is centred normal. Moreover, the variance of
\xi_{j}
is one and, if
h_{j}=\langle{\bf h},{\bf e}_{j}\rangle
, then

\sigma^{2}_{{\bf h}}=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}h_{j}^{2}\lambda_{j}<\infty
(due to
\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}h_{j}^{2}<\infty
and
A3
) and
\mathbb{C}\mathrm{ov}[\xi_{j},{\bf X}^{\bf h}]=h_{j}\lambda_{j}^{1/2}
. Notice that, if
{\bf h}\neq{\bf 0}
,
\sigma_{{\bf h}}>0
since
\lambda_{j}>0
for all
j\in\mathbb{N}
. Denoting by
\phi_{{\bf h}}(u,v)
the joint density function of
(\xi_{j},{\bf X}^{\bf h})
and by
\phi_{{\bf h},2}(v)
its second marginal, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathds{1}_{\left\{{\bf X}^{\bf h}\leq x\right\}}\xi_{j}\right]=

\displaystyle\;\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}u\mathds{1}_{\{v\leq x\}}\frac{\phi_{{\bf h}}(u,v)}{\phi_{{\bf h},2}(v)}\,\mathrm{d}u\right)\phi_{{\bf h},2}(v)\,\mathrm{d}v

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;\int_{-\infty}^{x}\mathbb{E}\big[\xi_{j}|{\bf X}^{\bf h}=v\big]\phi_{{\bf h},2}(v)\,\mathrm{d}v

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;\int_{-\infty}^{x}\frac{h_{j}\sqrt{\lambda_{j}}v}{\sigma_{{\bf h}}^{2}}\phi_{{\bf h},2}(v)\,\mathrm{d}v=-\frac{h_{j}\sqrt{\lambda_{j}}}{\sigma_{{\bf h}}}{\phi(x/\sigma_{{\bf h}})}.

This, the initial development, and
A3
give us that

t_{n,\mathbf{E}_{x,{\bf h}}}^{2}=\frac{\phi^{2}(x/\sigma_{{\bf h}})}{\sigma^{2}_{{\bf h}}}\sum_{j=1}^{k_{n}}h_{j}^{2}\lambda_{j}\rightarrow{\phi^{2}(x/\sigma_{{\bf h}})}.

∎

Proof of Theorem
3.3
.

We first prove
(a)
. The joint asymptotic normality of
\big(T_{n,{\bf h}}(x_{1}),\allowbreak\ldots,T_{n,{\bf h}}(x_{k})\big)
for
\left(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
follows by the Cramér–Wold device and the same arguments used in Lemma
A.7
. Also, in the proof of that lemma it is shown that
n^{1/2}\left<\mathbf{E}_{x,{\bf h}},{\bf L}_{n}+{\bf Y}_{n}+{\bf S}_{n}\right>=o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)
. Then, due to (
11
) and (
5
),

\displaystyle T_{n,{\bf h}}(x)=

\displaystyle\;n^{-1/2}\big(T_{n,{\bf h}}^{1}(x)+T_{n,{\bf h}}^{3}(x)\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;n^{-1/2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\mathds{1}_{\left\{{\bf X}_{i}^{{\bf h}}\leq x\right\}}}\varepsilon_{i}+n^{-1/2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\left<\mathbf{E}_{x,{\bf h}},\Gamma^{\dagger}{\bf X}_{i}\right>\varepsilon_{i}}+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;n^{-1/2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\{A^{i}_{x}+B^{i}_{x}\}+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1),

with
A_{x}^{i}:=\mathds{1}_{\left\{{\bf X}_{i}^{{\bf h}}\leq x\right\}}\varepsilon_{i}
, and
B_{x}^{i}:=\sum_{j=1}^{k_{n}}\left<\mathbf{E}_{x,{\bf h}},{\bf e}_{j}\right>\lambda_{j}^{-1/2}\xi_{j}^{i}\varepsilon_{i}
. Since the
{\bf X}_{i}
’s and
\varepsilon_{i}
’s are i.i.d., and
\mathbb{E}\left[\varepsilon|{\bf X}\right]=0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{C}\mathrm{ov}\bigg[n^{-1/2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}

\displaystyle\{A^{i}_{s}+B^{i}_{s}\},n^{-1/2}\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{n}\{A^{i^{\prime}}_{t}+B^{i^{\prime}}_{t}\}\bigg]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;\mathbb{E}\left[A^{1}_{s}A^{1}_{t}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[A^{1}_{s}B^{1}_{t}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[B^{1}_{s}A^{1}_{t}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[B^{1}_{s}B^{1}_{t}\right].

Applying the tower property with the conditioning variables
{\bf X}^{\bf h}
(first expectation) and
{\bf X}
(second and third), it follows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[A^{1}_{s}A^{1}_{t}\right]=

\displaystyle\;K_{1}(s,t),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[A^{1}_{s}B^{1}_{t}\right]=

\displaystyle\;\int_{\{{\bf x}^{\bf h}\leq s\}}\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}\left[Y|{\bf X}={\bf x}\right]\left<\mathbf{E}_{t,{\bf h}},\Gamma^{\dagger}{\bf x}\right>\,\mathrm{d}P_{\bf X}({\bf x}),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[B^{1}_{s}B^{1}_{t}\right]=

\displaystyle\;\int\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}\left[Y|{\bf X}={\bf x}\right]\left<\mathbf{E}_{s,{\bf h}},\Gamma^{\dagger}{\bf x}\right>\left<\mathbf{E}_{t,{\bf h}},\Gamma^{\dagger}{\bf x}\right>\,\mathrm{d}P_{\bf X}({\bf x}).

Since
\Gamma^{\dagger}\rightarrow\Gamma^{-1}
in the operator norm
\left|\left|\cdot\right|\right|_{\infty}
, Cauchy–Schwartz and
\big|\big|(\Gamma^{\dagger}-\allowbreak\Gamma^{-1}){\bf x}\big|\big|\leq\left|\left|\Gamma^{\dagger}-\Gamma^{-1}\right|\right|_{\infty}\left|\left|{\bf x}\right|\right|
give that
\mathbb{E}\left[A^{1}_{s}B^{1}_{t}\right]-C_{1}(s,t)
and
\mathbb{E}\left[B^{1}_{s}B^{1}_{t}\right]-C_{2}(s,t)
converge to zero. The result then follows from Slutsky’s theorem.

We now prove
(b)
. The tightness of
n^{-1/2}T_{n,{\bf h}}^{1}
is obtained using the same arguments as in Theorem 1.1 of
Stute, 1997
. For the tightness of
n^{-1/2}T_{n,{\bf h}}^{3}
, define

\bar{T}_{n,{\bf h}}^{3}(u):=n\left<\mathbb{E}\left[\mathds{1}_{\left\{U_{\bf h}\leq u\right\}}{\bf X}\right],\boldsymbol{\rho}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\right>,\quad U_{\bf h}:=F_{{\bf h}}({\bf X}^{\bf h}),

as the time-changed version of
\bar{T}_{n,{\bf h}}^{3}
by
F_{{\bf h}}
, that is,

T_{n,{\bf h}}^{3}(x)=\bar{T}_{n,{\bf h}}^{3}(F_{{\bf h}}(x)).

Consider
0\leq u_{1}<u<u_{2}\leq 1
and the differences

\displaystyle n^{-1/2}\big(\bar{T}_{n,{\bf h}}^{3}(u)-\bar{T}_{n,{\bf h}}^{3}(u_{1})\big)

\displaystyle=n^{1/2}\left<\mathbb{E}\left[\mathds{1}_{\left\{u_{1}<U_{\bf h}\leq u\right\}}{\bf X}\right],\boldsymbol{\rho}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\right>,

\displaystyle n^{-1/2}\big(\bar{T}_{n,{\bf h}}^{3}(u_{2})-\bar{T}_{n,{\bf h}}^{3}(u)\big)

\displaystyle=n^{1/2}\left<\mathbb{E}\left[\mathds{1}_{\left\{u<U_{\bf h}\leq u_{2}\right\}}{\bf X}\right],\boldsymbol{\rho}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\right>.

Then, by the Cauchy–Schwartz and Jensen inequalities,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[n^{-2}\big|

\displaystyle\bar{T}_{n,{\bf h}}^{3}(u)-\bar{T}_{n,{\bf h}}^{3}(u_{1})\big|^{2}\left|\bar{T}_{n,{\bf h}}^{3}(u_{2})-\bar{T}_{n,{\bf h}}^{3}(u)\right|^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle\leq n^{2}\mathbb{E}\Big[\left|\left|\mathbb{E}\left[\mathds{1}_{\left\{u_{1}<U_{\bf h}\leq u\right\}}{\bf X}\right]\right|\right|^{2}\left|\left|\mathbb{E}\left[\mathds{1}_{\left\{u_{1}<U_{\bf h}\leq u\right\}}{\bf X}\right]\right|\right|^{4}\left|\left|\boldsymbol{\rho}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\right|\right|^{4}\Big]

\displaystyle=n^{2}\mathbb{E}\big[\left|\left|\boldsymbol{\rho}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\right|\right|^{4}\big]\int\mathbb{E}\left[{\bf X}(t)\mathds{1}_{\left\{u_{1}<U_{\bf h}\leq u\right\}}\right]^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\int\mathbb{E}\left[{\bf X}(t)\mathds{1}_{\left\{u<U_{\bf h}\leq u_{2}\right\}}\right]^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq n^{2}\mathbb{E}\big[\left|\left|\boldsymbol{\rho}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\right|\right|^{4}\big]\int\mathbb{E}\left[{\bf X}^{2}(t)\mathds{1}_{\left\{u_{1}<U_{\bf h}\leq u\right\}}\right]\,\mathrm{d}t\int\mathbb{E}\left[{\bf X}^{2}(t)\mathds{1}_{\left\{u<U_{\bf h}\leq u_{2}\right\}}\right]\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=n^{2}\mathbb{E}\big[\left|\left|\boldsymbol{\rho}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\right|\right|^{4}\big]\left[F(u)-F(u_{1})\right]\left[F(u_{2})-F(u)\right]

\displaystyle\leq n^{2}\mathbb{E}\big[\left|\left|\boldsymbol{\rho}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\right|\right|^{4}\big]\left[F(u_{2})-F(u_{1})\right]^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left[G(u_{2})-G(u_{1})\right]^{2},

where
F(u):=\int\mathbb{E}\left[{\bf X}^{2}(t)\mathds{1}_{\left\{U_{\bf h}\leq u\right\}}\right]\,\mathrm{d}t
and

are nondecreasing and continuous functions on
[0,1]
. This corresponds to employing
\gamma=2
and
\alpha=1
in Theorem 15.6 of

and, as a consequence of the Continuous Mapping Theorem (CMT),

in
D(\mathbb{R})
.

Finally, we prove that
n^{-1/2}T_{n,{\bf h}}^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\rightarrow}}\mathbf{0}
. Note first that, by Cauchy–Schwartz,

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\left|n^{-1/2}T_{n,{\bf h}}^{2}(x)\right|\leq

\displaystyle\;\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\left|\left|\mbox{$\overline{{\bf X}}_{x,{\bf h}}$}-\mathbf{E}_{x,{\bf h}}\right|\right|n^{1/2}\left|\left|\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}-\boldsymbol{\rho}\right|\right|.

Assumption
\mathbb{E}\big[\left|\left|\boldsymbol{\rho}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\right|\right|^{4}\big]=\mathcal{O}\big(n^{-2}\big)
implies
\left|\left|\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}-\boldsymbol{\rho}\right|\right|=\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}}(n^{-1/2})
. In addition, the weak law of large numbers in

(e.g.,

in

∎

\displaystyle\;\frac{\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}}{n^{3}}\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\bigg(\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{k_{n}}\xi_{j}^{1}\left(\xi_{j}^{i^{\prime}}\mathds{1}_{\left\{{\bf X}_{i^{\prime}}^{\bf h}\leq x\right\}}-\mathbb{E}\left[\xi_{j}^{1}\mathds{1}_{\left\{{\bf X}_{1}^{\bf h}\leq x\right\}}\right]\right)\bigg)^{2}\Bigg].

We need to compute the sum of the expectations in (
13
). This sum contains
n^{2}k_{n}^{2}
terms, and we analyse their possible behaviours next.

Let
i^{\prime},i^{\prime\prime}=1,\ldots,n
. If
i^{\prime}\neq i^{\prime\prime}
, then either
i^{\prime}\neq 1
or
i^{\prime\prime}\neq 1
. Assuming the first holds, we have that
\big(\xi_{j}^{i^{\prime}}\mathds{1}_{\left\{{\bf X}_{i^{\prime}}^{\bf h}\leq x\right\}}-\mathbb{E}\big[\xi_{j}^{1}\mathds{1}_{\left\{{\bf X}_{1}^{\bf h}\leq x\right\}}\big]\big)
is independent of
\big(\xi_{j}^{i^{\prime\prime}}\mathds{1}_{\left\{{\bf X}_{i^{\prime}}^{\bf h}\leq x\right\}}-\mathbb{E}\big[\xi_{j}^{1}\mathds{1}_{\left\{{\bf X}_{1}^{\bf h}\leq x\right\}}\big]\big)
,
\xi_{j}^{1}
, and
\xi_{j^{\prime}}^{1}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\xi_{j}^{1}

\displaystyle\left(\xi_{j}^{i^{\prime}}\mathds{1}_{\left\{{\bf X}_{i^{\prime}}^{\bf h}\leq x\right\}}-\mathbb{E}\left[\xi_{j}^{1}\mathds{1}_{\left\{{\bf X}_{1}^{\bf h}\leq x\right\}}\right]\right)

\displaystyle\times\xi_{j^{\prime}}^{1}\left(\xi_{j^{\prime}}^{i^{\prime\prime}}\mathds{1}_{\left\{{\bf X}_{i^{\prime\prime}}^{\bf h}\leq x\right\}}-\mathbb{E}\left[\xi_{j}^{1}\mathds{1}_{\left\{{\bf X}_{1}^{\bf h}\leq x\right\}}\right]\right)\Big]=0,

and we have
k_{n}^{2}n(n-1)
terms in (
13
) whose expectations are also zero.
With respect to the remaining terms, we can elaborate a bit more on (
13
) to obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\langle\mbox{$\overline{{\bf X}}_{x,{\bf h}}$}-\mathbf{E}_{x,{\bf h}},{\bf R}_{n}\rangle^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}}{n^{3}}\mathbb{E}\left[\bigg(\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{k_{n}}|\xi_{j}^{1}|\left(|\xi_{j}^{i^{\prime}}|+\mathbb{E}\left[|\xi_{j}^{1}|\right]\right)\bigg)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=:\frac{\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}}{n^{3}}\mathbb{E}\left[\bigg(\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{k_{n}}T(i^{\prime},j)\bigg)^{2}\right].

The expansion of the square in the last expression yields the terms
\mathbb{E}\big[T(i^{\prime},j)\allowbreak T(i^{\prime\prime},j^{\prime})\big]
. Each of these can be bounded by
M
in
A8
, since they are the sum of four terms of the form
\mathbb{E}\big[|\xi_{j}^{1}||\xi_{j}^{i^{\prime}}||\xi_{j^{\prime}}^{1}||\xi_{j^{\prime}}^{i^{\prime\prime}}|\big]
,
\mathbb{E}\big[|\xi_{j}^{1}||\xi_{j}^{i^{\prime}}||\xi_{j^{\prime}}^{1}|\big]\mathbb{E}\big[|\xi_{j}^{1}|\big]
, or
\mathbb{E}\big[|\xi_{j}^{1}||\xi_{j^{\prime}}^{1}|\big]\mathbb{E}\big[|\xi_{j}^{1}|\big]\mathbb{E}\big[|\xi_{j^{\prime}}^{1}|\big]
, with
i^{\prime},i^{\prime\prime}=1,\ldots,n
and
j,j^{\prime}=1,\ldots,k_{n}
. If we apply Cauchy–Schwartz’s and Jensen’s inequalities, we have that, for instance,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[|\xi_{j}^{1}||\xi_{j^{\prime}}^{1}|\right]\mathbb{E}\left[|\xi_{j}^{1}|\right]\mathbb{E}\left[|\xi_{j^{\prime}}^{1}|\right]

\displaystyle\leq\left(\mathbb{E}\left[|\xi_{j}^{1}|^{4}\right]\mathbb{E}\left[|\xi_{j^{\prime}}^{1}|^{4}\right]\right)^{1/4}\mathbb{E}\left[|\xi_{j}^{1}|^{4}\right]^{1/4}\mathbb{E}\left[|\xi_{j^{\prime}}^{1}|^{4}\right]^{1/4}

\displaystyle\leq M.

The remaining terms are handled similarly, and we can conclude that the non-null terms in (
13
) are bounded by
M
. Thus, we obtain that

\mathbb{E}\left[\langle\mbox{$\overline{{\bf X}}_{x,{\bf h}}$}-\mathbf{E}_{x,{\bf h}},{\bf R}_{n}\rangle\right]^{2}\leq\frac{\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}}{n^{3}}M\left(n^{2}k_{n}^{2}-k_{n}^{2}n(n-1)\right)=\frac{\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}}{n^{2}}Mk_{n}^{2}.

Consequently, Chebyshev’s inequality and Lemma
A.1
give the result.

\displaystyle\;C\frac{j\log j}{\sqrt{n}}

\displaystyle\langle\Gamma_{z}^{-1/2}{\bf X}_{r^{\prime}},\Gamma_{z}^{-1/2}{\bf X}_{s^{\prime}}\rangle\Big]\Big|

Proof of Lemma

.

According to Lemma 8 in CMS, we have that

Therefore, the second part of Proposition 3 in CMS (page 352) gives us that
\langle\mathbf{E}_{x,{\bf h}},{\bf Y}_{n}\rangle=o_{\mathbb{P}}(n^{-1/2})
.

Then, the result will be proven if we show that

\displaystyle n^{1/2}\left(\langle\mathbf{E}_{x,{\bf h}},{\bf L}_{n}\rangle+\langle\mathbf{E}_{x,{\bf h}},{\bf S}_{n}\rangle\right)=o_{\mathbb{P}}(1).

(31)

To prove (
31
), we analyse separately both terms on the left hand side of this expression. To do this, we follow the steps in some proofs in CMS. Before we do so, notice that, in the computation of
\mathbf{E}_{x,{\bf h}}
, we can take
{\bf X}
independent from all the remaining variables in the problem. In particular, for every

to be independent of
\textbf{W}_{n}
, where

denotes either

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\left\langle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathds{1}_{\left\{{\bf X}^{\bf h}\leq x\right\}}{\bf X}\right],\textbf{W}_{n}\right\rangle\right|\right]=

\displaystyle\;\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\mathbb{E}\left[\langle\mathds{1}_{\left\{{\bf X}^{\bf h}\leq x\right\}}{\bf X},\textbf{W}_{n}\rangle\Big|\textbf{W}_{n}\right]\right|\right]

31

Lemma 7 in CMS gives the following two bounds:

\mathbb{E}\left[|\langle{\bf X},{\bf L}_{n}\rangle|\right]\leq\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}|\langle\boldsymbol{\rho},{\bf e}_{k_{n}}\rangle|\sqrt{\sum_{j=k_{n}+1}^{\infty}\lambda_{j}},\\

Concerning the term

C.3
Dependence on the projection process

The goodness-of-fit tests depend clearly on the way random directions
{\bf h}
are chosen. In order to explore its practical influence, we replicated the results in Section
5
for two new processes. We have three different projecting processes in total: (
i
) the data-driven process described in Section
4
; (
ii
) the same process, but with constant variance coefficients
\eta_{j}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
; (
iii
) an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process with
\alpha=\tfrac{1}{2}
and
\sigma=1
that is completely independent from the sample. Note that process (
ii
) generates more noisy random directions than (
i
) since all the FPC’s of the sample are equally weighted.

C.4
Remarks on discrete
p
-values and FDR correction

The FDR correction of a set of
K

continuous

p
-values
p_{1},\ldots,p_{K}
, arising from
K
hypothesis tests, results in the FDR
p
-value
p_{\mathrm{FDR},K}:=\min_{i=1,\ldots,K}\frac{K}{i}p_{(i)}
. Under the null hypotheses for all the tests, the level of the test that rejects the null hypothesis if
p_{\mathrm{FDR},K}<\alpha
is
\alpha
at most
(
Benjamini and Yekutieli, 2001
)
. When using a resampling strategy for approximating the
p
-values
p_{1},\ldots,p_{K}
, for example, by considering
B
bootstrap replicates, we end up with a collection of
discrete

p
-values
\hat{p}_{1},\ldots,\hat{p}_{K}
. This has a notable influence on
p_{\mathrm{FDR},K}
, resulting in an increment of the type I error that is magnified for moderate and large
K
.

Under the null,
\hat{p}_{i}
is approximately distributed as a
\mathcal{U}(\{0,\frac{1}{B},\ldots,\frac{B}{B}\})
,
i=1,\ldots,K
. If independence between
\hat{p}_{1},\ldots,\hat{p}_{K}
is assumed, then the rejection rate of
p_{\mathrm{FDR},K}<\alpha
is at least
\mathbb{P}[\hat{p}_{(1)}=0]\approx 1-\big(\tfrac{B}{B+1}\big)^{K}

no matter what significance level
\alpha
is chosen
. For example, if
K=25
and
B=500
, under the null hypothesis,
p_{\mathrm{FDR},K}<\alpha
will reject the null at least
4.87\%
of the time for any
\alpha
. For
K=5
and
B=1000
, the lower bound for the rejection percentage drops to
0.499\%
. This simple argument illustrates the more demanding precision (larger
B
’s) required in the approximated
p
-values when
K
grows.

In order to gain more insights into the problematic dependence of
K
and
B
, we have conducted the following experiment, aimed at reproducing a comparable situation to our testing in practice.

1.

Simulate
K
discrete
p
-values independently:
\hat{p}_{i}\sim\mathcal{U}(\{0,\frac{1}{B},\ldots,\frac{B}{B}\})
and compute
p_{\mathrm{FDR},K}
.

2.

Repeat the previous step
M=10000
times and obtain the empirical rejection rates of
p_{\mathrm{FDR},K}<\alpha
for
\alpha=0.10,0.05,0.01
. Plot the rejection curves as a function of
K=1,\ldots,50
. Repeat this five times to account for variability.

3.

Repeat the above steps for different
B
’s.

The results of the experiment, namely the empirical rejection curves as a function of
K
for different
\alpha
’s and
B
’s, are shown in the left panel of Figure
8
. A sawtooth pattern of over-rejection appears for curves with
B=500,1000
(yellow and light green curves) and values of
K
larger than
7-10
, resulting in significant violations of the confidence intervals for the proportions
\alpha
for
K
’s in the range
[10,50]
. When
B
is larger (dark green and blue curves), the rejection rates remain more stable and inside the confidence intervals for
K
up to
50
. This highlights that, given the effect that both
K
and
B
have on the computation proficiency of the test, a reasonable compromise on the choice of
K
and
B
that respects the type I error is a low value for
K
, say
K\in\{1,\ldots,5\}
, and a relatively large value of
B
, such as
B\geq 1000
. The right panel of Figure
8
shows the resulting levels if the positive correction
\frac{\hat{p}B+1}{B+1}
is applied for avoiding null
p
-values. The same conclusions can be extracted, the main change being under-rejections instead of over-rejections.

p_{\mathrm{FDR},K}<\alpha
, as a function of
K
. Right: empirical levels employing a positive correction for the
p
-values.
