Precise asymptotics for the density and the upper tail of exponential functionals of subordinators
Bénédicte Haas

Abstract
We provide exact large-time equivalents of the density and upper tail distributions of the exponential functional of a subordinator in terms of its Laplace exponents. This improves previous results on the logarithmic asymptotic behaviour of the upper tail. Since the results in their general form remain rather abstract, we study some fairly common situations in details to see how the behavior near 0 of the Lévy measure of the subordinator influences the asymptotics of the density and upper tail distributions. Several examples of applications are then discussed.



1
Introduction

The exponential functional of a subordinator
\xi
is the random variable

I:=\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp(-\xi_{r})\mathrm{d}r.

To simplify we always assume in this paper that
\xi
starts from 0. Such random variables have been thoroughly investigated, since they play a key role in several facets of modern probabilities since they correspond to the extinction times of non-increasing self-similar Markov processes by Lamperti’s transformation
[
25
]
. As such, they are involved in the description of various processes ranging from the analysis of algorithms to coagulation or fragmentation processes
[
4
,
11
,
15
,
22
,
19
]
. They belong to the more general class of exponential functionals of Lévy processes drifting to
\infty
, which offers a wider range of applications. We refer to the survey of Bertoin and Yor
[
7
]
for a general overview on that topic.

A well-known result of Carmona, Petit and Yor
[
9
, Proposition 2.1]
, generalized to any subordinator by Pardo, Rivero and Schaik
[
28
, Theorem 2.3]
, is that the law of
I
has a density with respect to the Lebesgue measure on
[0,\infty)
, which satisfies an integral equation recalled in (
4.1
). Like most articles on that topic, we denote this density by
k
. This density, or the distribution of
I
, are known explicitly only in a few cases. For instance,
[
7
]
identifies subordinators that have an exponential functional
I
distributed as the power of an exponential distribution or as a generalized Mittag-Leffler distribution. Fine information is also known in some other specific cases, for instance when
\xi
is a standard Poisson process
[
5
,
11
]
or more generally a compound Poisson process with jumps having a fractional generating function
[
15
]
, a series representation of
k
is obtained explicitly using
q
-calculus techniques.

In this paper we obtain an equivalent of the density
k(t)
and the (upper) tail
\mathbb{P}(I>t)
as
t\rightarrow\infty
in a general framework. We also obtain the first order of convergence and give the tools to obtain further orders. It is well known that the tail of
I
is exponential or even lighter than exponential. More precisely a first order equivalent of the asymptotic behavior of the
logarithm
of the tail has been obtained by Rivero
[
33
]
when the Lévy measure of the subordinator is infinite whereas Maulik and Zwart
[
26
]
studied the asymptotic behavior of the tail in the compound Poisson case. Their results will be recalled below, once we have introduced the necessary notations. For comparison, the tail of the exponential functional of a non-monotonic Lévy process shows a drastically different asymptotic behavior since it is generally heavy tailed or even extremely heavy tailed (it may decrease as the power of the logarithm): see again Maulik and Zwart
[
26
]
and Rivero
[
34
,
35
]
for results in a general framework, and Kuznetsov and Pardo
[
23
,
24
]
, Patie
[
29
]
and the references therein for series representation of
k
for some specific subclasses of non-monotonic Lévy processes. To complete this picture we mention that some results on the small-time behavior of the density and the tail are also available in the literature, see e.g.
[
28
,
30
]
and the references therein.

Examples and applications.
The expressions of the equivalents of the density and tail of
I
remain quite abstract in their general form and it is interesting to see on examples what is the precise influence of the behavior of the Lévy measure near 0. Even when this Lévy measure is finite (compound Poisson cases), one observes a wide range of behaviors (see Case 1 in Section
2.2
). We detail in this paper several examples, typically when the lower and upper tails of the Lévy measure have asymptotic power expansions at 0, but not only, and then discuss several applications to non-negative non-decreasing self-similar Markov processes, and fragmentation and coalescence processes.

Organization of the paper.
Our main contribution on the asymptotic behavior of the tail and density of
I
is given in Section
2
, where we also present the examples and applications. Section
3
is devoted to some elementary properties of the functions
\phi
and
\psi
which will be needed for the proof of our main result, Theorem
1
, undertaken in Section
4
. Next, we focus on the applications: Section
5
details the cases where the Laplace exponent
\phi
has an asymptotic power expansion, while Section
6
provides a proof of Corollary
3
on the asymptotics of moments of non-increasing, non-negative self-similar Markov processes.

Note added.
While completing the writing of this manuscript, we became aware that the equivalents of the density and upper tail of
I
are also studied by Minchev and Savov in a paper posted recently on the arXiv
[
27
]
. Our approaches are different and each seems to have its advantages. Minchev and Savov apply the saddle point method to the Mellin transform of the exponential functional, while we exploit the proximity of an integral equation satisfied by the derivative of the logarithm of the tail of
I
with an integral equation satisfied by a function related to the Laplace exponent of the subordinator. This allows us to get immediately the first order of convergence and a method to give any further order by iterating our calculations. On the other hand, Minchev and Savov get an expression of a constant of multiplicity (noted
c_{I}
in the rest of this article) in terms of the Laplace exponent of the subordinator, which we do not have. They are also interested in the equivalents of the derivatives of the density. Here we also develop several examples and applications.

2
Main results and applications

2.1
Background and notation

Throughout we assume that the subordinator
\xi
is drift-free, unkilled and start from 0. We call
\pi
its Lévy measure (which is assumed to be non-zero) and
\phi
its Laplace exponent, so that
\mathbb{E}[\exp(-x\xi_{t})]=\exp\left(-t\phi(x)\right),\forall t,x\geq 0
with

\phi(x):=\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(1-\exp(-xv)\right)\pi(\mathrm{d}v),\qquad x\geq 0.

(2.1)

When the Lévy measure
\pi
is infinite and more precisely when the function
\phi
is regularly varying at
\infty
with some index
\gamma\in(0,1)
, Rivero
[
33
, Proposition 2]
proved that

\ln\mathbb{P}(I>t)\underset{t\rightarrow\infty}{\sim}-(1-\gamma)\psi(t),

where the function
\psi
is the inverse of the continuous and strictly increasing function
x\mapsto x/\phi(x)
:

\frac{\psi(x)}{\phi(\psi(x))}=x.

(2.2)

Note that
\psi
is well-defined on
(x_{\psi},\infty)
where

x_{\psi}:=\lim_{x\downarrow 0}x/\phi(x)=\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}v\pi(\mathrm{d}v)\right)^{-1}

is finite (possibly null), and that
\phi,\psi
are infinitely differentiable on
(0,\infty)
and
(x_{\psi},\infty)
respectively. In
[
21
, Proposition A.1]
an extension of Rivero’s result to the case where

\limsup_{x\rightarrow\infty}\frac{x\phi^{\prime}(x)}{\phi(x)}<1

(
\mathbf{H}
)

is given; then

\ln\mathbb{P}(I>t)\underset{t\rightarrow\infty}{\sim}-\int_{x_{\psi}}^{t}\frac{\psi(r)}{r}\mathrm{d}r.

(2.3)

In this paper we will always work under this assumption (

H

). We highlight that it is weaker than assuming that
\phi
is regularly varying at
\infty
, hence in particular that
\pi
is finite. We note also that without any hypothesis on the model,
\phi^{\prime}(x)\leq\phi(x)/x
for all
x\geq 0
since
\phi
is concave and thus the above limsup is always smaller or equal to 1. The assumption (

H

) is therefore not very restrictive and in fact verified in all applications we are interested in.

When
\pi
is finite Maulik and Zwart
[
26
, Sections 5 and 6]
obtained more precise results, which can be summarized as follows:

\bullet

when
\int_{0}^{\infty}u^{-1}\pi(\mathrm{d}u)<\infty
,

\mathbb{P}(I>t)\underset{t\rightarrow\infty}{\sim}\mathrm{c}_{I}\exp(-|\pi|t)

where
|\pi|=\pi(0,\infty)
,
\mathrm{c}_{I}=\mathbb{E}[e^{|\pi|e^{-X}I}]
is finite and positive, and
X
has the distribution of a jump of the subordinator (
\pi/|\pi|
) and is independent of
I

\bullet

when
\pi(0,u)=bu+o(u^{1+\delta})
as
u\downarrow 0
for some
b,\delta>0
,

\mathbb{P}(I>t)\underset{t\rightarrow\infty}{\sim}\mathrm{c}_{I}\penalty\ t^{\frac{b}{|\pi|}}\exp(-|\pi|t)

where
\mathrm{c}_{I}=|\pi|^{b}e^{b\gamma_{\mathrm{Euler}}}\left(\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}(1-\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-uk}\pi(\mathrm{d}u)/|\pi|)e^{b/k}\right)^{-1}
is finite and positive, and
\gamma_{\mathrm{Euler}}
is Euler’s constant.

Notation.
Throughout the paper, we will use the standard big-O and little-o Landau asymptotic notation, as well as the notation
f\lesssim g
on a domain
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, meaning that if
f,g
are two positive functions defined on
D
, there exists a positive constant
\mathrm{c}
such that
f(x)\leq\mathrm{c}g(x)
,
\forall x\in D
. Last, for two functions
f,g
defined on an interval
[a,\infty)
, we will write
f(x)\penalty\ \underset{x\rightarrow\infty}{\sim}\penalty\ g(x)
when
f(x)/g(x)\underset{x\rightarrow\infty}{\rightarrow}1
and more generally
f(x)\penalty\ \underset{x\rightarrow\infty}{\propto}\penalty\ g(x)
when
f(x)/g(x)\underset{x\rightarrow\infty}{\rightarrow}\mathrm{c}
for some
\mathrm{c}>0
.

2.2
Main results

Our main contribution is the following theorem and its corollary which improve Rivero’s result by giving an exact (non-logarithmic) asymptotic behavior of the tail of
I
, as well as its density
k
, and completes Maulik and Zwart’ results in the compound Poisson case.

Theorem 1
.

Assume
(

H

)
. Then there exists a constant
\mathrm{c}_{I}\in(0,\infty)
such that as
t\rightarrow\infty

\mathbb{P}(I>t)\penalty\ =\penalty\ \mathrm{c}_{I}\penalty\ \frac{t(\psi^{\prime}(t))^{1/2}}{\psi(t)}\penalty\ \exp\left(-\int_{x_{\psi}+1}^{t}\frac{\psi(r)}{r}\mathrm{d}r\right)\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{\psi(t)}\right)\right)

and

k(t)\penalty\ =\penalty\ \mathrm{c}_{I}\penalty\ \left(\psi^{\prime}(t)\right)^{1/2}\penalty\ \exp\left(-\int_{x_{\psi}+1}^{t}\frac{\psi(r)}{r}\mathrm{d}r\right)\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{\psi(t)}\right)\right).

We obtain in fact an asymptotic expansion of the quotient
k(t)/\mathbb{P}(I>t)
up to a
O(1/(\psi(t))^{2})
term in Proposition
8
, which is the key technical result of this paper and implies directly the above theorem. This is proved by using iteratively the integral equation (
4.1
) satisfied by
k
, which permits us to evaluate how close the quotients
k(t)/\mathbb{P}(I>t)
and
\psi(t)/t
are as
t\rightarrow\infty
. By pushing forward the iterative argument we could in fact improve the asymptotic expansion of Proposition
8
to any order.
This would allow us to develop the parentheses
(1+O(1/\psi(t)))
of Theorem
1
to any order
. However this does not seem to give easy-to-state expressions and we will be content with Proposition
8
and Theorem
1
here, and leave the additional calculations to the interested reader if they need further orders. Compared to previous works, we note that several results recalled at the beginning of this introduction were obtained by inverting the Mellin or Laplace transforms of the distribution of
I
, a technique that we do not use here. In spirit, we are closer to the approach of Maulik and Zwart
[
26
]
for finite measures
\pi
which relies on the fact that the distribution of
I
then satisfies the random affine equation
I=E+\exp(-X)\tilde{I}
where
E,X,\tilde{I}
are independent random variables,
E
having an exponential distribution,
X
being distributed according to
\pi/|\pi|
and
\tilde{I}
as
I
. However for infinite Lévy measure
\pi
there is no such explicit equation satisfied by
I
in general.

In the integral of Theorem
1
one can of course replace the bound
x_{\psi}+1
by any
x\in(x_{\psi},\infty)
, by multiplying the constant
\mathrm{c}_{I}
appropriately.
Our approach does not allow us, a priori, to get an explicit expression of
\mathrm{c}_{I}
. See the recent paper
[
27
]
for an expression of this constant involving an integral of a function of
\phi
and its first and second derivatives.

Note that if
I(\pi)
denotes the exponential functional of a subordinator with Lévy measure
\pi
then
I(c\pi)\overset{\mathrm{(d)}}{=}c^{-1}I(\pi)
for any
c>0
. So in the examples we will treat, we will always try to take the multiplicative version of the Lévy measure that gives the easiest to read asymptotic expressions of the tail and density of
I
.

There is one useful and immediate corollary of the above theorem. Indeed, when
\phi
is regularly varying with some index
\gamma\in[0,1)
one easily checks that
x\psi^{\prime}(x)/\psi(x)\rightarrow(1-\gamma)^{-1}
as
x\rightarrow\infty
(see Lemma
7
) and the above theorem then implies:

Corollary 2
(Regular variation).

Assume that
\phi
is regularly varying at
\infty
with some index
\gamma\in[0,1)
. Then

\mathbb{P}(I>t)\penalty\ \underset{t\rightarrow\infty}{\sim}\penalty\ \frac{\mathrm{c}_{I}}{(1-\gamma)^{1/2}}\penalty\ \left(\frac{t}{\psi(t)}\right)^{1/2}\exp\left(-\int_{x_{\psi}+1}^{t}\frac{\psi(r)}{r}\mathrm{d}r\right)

and

k(t)\penalty\ \penalty\ \underset{t\rightarrow\infty}{\sim}\penalty\ \frac{\mathrm{c}_{I}}{(1-\gamma)^{1/2}}\penalty\ \left(\frac{\psi(t)}{t}\right)^{1/2}\exp\left(-\int_{x_{\psi}+1}^{t}\frac{\psi(r)}{r}\mathrm{d}r\right),

where
\psi(t)/t
is regularly varying at
\infty
with index
\gamma/(1-\gamma)
.

In practice, to extract more explicit asymptotic expressions from those of Theorem
1
and Corollary
2
, the general strategy is of course to find a sufficiently precise asymptotic expansion for

and then deduce one for
\psi
. To illustrate, we finish this section by detailing cases where the lower or upper tails of

Case 1.
Assume that
\pi
is
finite
, say

is a probability. Then,
x^{-1}\psi(x)=\phi(\psi(x))=1-\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp(-\psi(x)u)\pi(\mathrm{d}u)

as

. We need more information on

to understand the behavior of the Big-O. Some examples:

\bullet

If
\int_{0}^{\infty}u^{-1}\pi(\mathrm{d}u)<\infty
, then the above remark shows that
\int_{x_{\psi}+1}^{t}r^{-1}\psi(r)\mathrm{d}r-t
converges as

and we get that

\mathbb{P}(I>t)\underset{t\rightarrow\infty}{\sim}k(t)\underset{t\rightarrow\infty}{\propto}\exp(-t).

\bullet

Now, assume that
\pi(0,u)=\sum_{i=1}^{p}c_{i}u^{\gamma_{i}}+O(u^{1+\varepsilon})
as

where
0<\gamma_{1}<\ldots<\gamma_{p-1}<\gamma_{p}=1
,

and the

\gamma_{1}>1/2

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(I>t)\underset{t\rightarrow\infty}{\sim}k(t)\underset{t\rightarrow\infty}{\propto}t^{c_{p}}\exp\left(-t+\sum_{i=1}^{p-1}\frac{c_{i}\Gamma(1+\gamma_{i})}{1-\gamma_{i}}t^{1-\gamma_{i}}\right).

If some of the
\gamma_{i}
s,
1\leq i\leq p-1
are smaller than or equal to
1/2
there will be additional power contributions in or in front of the exponential (in the exponential the powers of the additional terms will be smaller than
1

Case 2.
Assume that
\pi
is

-1<\gamma_{0}-1=\gamma_{p}<\gamma_{p-1}<\ldots<\gamma_{1}<\gamma_{0}<1

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(I>t)

\displaystyle\underset{t\rightarrow\infty}{\sim}

\displaystyle k(t)t^{-\frac{\gamma_{0}}{1-\gamma_{0}}}

\displaystyle\underset{t\rightarrow\infty}{\propto}

\displaystyle t^{\frac{-\gamma_{0}}{2(1-\gamma_{0})}-\frac{c_{p}\Gamma(1-\gamma_{p})}{1-\gamma_{0}}}\exp\left(-(1-\gamma_{0})t^{\frac{1}{1-\gamma_{0}}}-\sum_{i=1}^{p-1}\frac{c_{i}\Gamma(1-\gamma_{i})}{1-\gamma_{0}+\gamma_{i}}t^{\frac{1-\gamma_{0}+\gamma_{i}}{1-\gamma_{0}}}\right).

If some of the
\gamma_{i}
s,
1\leq i\leq p-1
are larger than or equal to
\gamma_{0}-1/2
, then there will be additional power contributions in or in front of the exponential.

(
a,b,c
)-Lévy measures.
A special case often encountered in practice are Lévy measures with which are proportional to

e^{-ax}(1-e^{-x/c})^{b-1}\mathrm{d}x\quad\text{ for some }a,c>0,b>-1

2.3
Some examples of applications

We start with two classical examples of subordinators, the Gamma and stable subordinators, and then turn to more general applications related to the connections between exponential functional of subordinators and non-negative self-similar Markov processes. To simplify the exposition, we will generally only give the asymptotic of the tail
\mathbb{P}(I>t)

\displaystyle\underset{t\rightarrow\infty}{\propto}\penalty\ \exp\left(-t\ln(t)-t\ln(\ln(t))+t+O\left(\frac{t\ln(\ln(t))}{\ln(t)}\right)\right)

Number of collisions in a

\Lambda-

We briefly recall that these are Markovian models for the coalescence of particules where at each step, starting from say

[0,1]
. These models where introduced by
[

,
36
]
and deeply studied since then. Let

denote the number of steps to go from

to 1 particules and set

,
u\in(0,1)
. From
[

,
19
]
we know that when

is regularly varying at 0 with index
-\gamma\in(-1,0)
and
\int_{0}^{1}x^{-1}\Lambda(\mathrm{d}x)<\infty

\frac{I_{n}}{s(1/n)}\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\overset{\mathrm{(d)}}{\longrightarrow}}I

where
I
is the exponential functional of a subordinator with Laplace exponent

\phi(x)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(2-\gamma)}\int_{0}^{1}\big(1-(1-x)^{\gamma\lambda}\big)x^{-2}\Lambda(\mathrm{d}x).

We can therefore obtain from Corollary
2
the asymptotic behavior of the tail and density of this limit according to the behavior of
s
at 0. More specifically, for the Beta
(\alpha,\beta)-
coalescent (i.e. when the measure
\Lambda
is the Beta
(\alpha,\beta)
distribution, with
1<\alpha<2,\beta>0
, the number of collisions

divided by

converge in distribution towards an exponential functional of a subordinator with Lévy measure

\pi(\mathrm{d}x)=\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\Gamma(a)}e^{-\beta x/(2-a)}\big(1-e^{-x/(2-a)}\big)^{3-a},

so we are in the
(a,b,c)
-Lévy measure case, with
a=\beta/(2-a)
and
b=a-2=-c
. Lemma

(in Section
5
) gives the behavior of the tail and density of the limiting exponential functional when

(equivalently

) and we can obtain further results for larger

by using Lemma

and adapting the proof of Lemma

.

Random walks with a barrier.
To illustrate the importance of the
(a,b,c)
-Lévy family, we finish this series of examples by recalling briefly that exponential functionals with such a Lévy measure also arise in the context of random walks with a barrier, which are random walks conditioned on the event that they will not exceed a given threshold
n
. Several conditionings are possible, we refer to
[

, Section 2.1]
for details. Fix such a walk and assume that it is non-decreasing, with a step distribution that varies regularly with index

. Then letting

(
[

,
19
]
) the absorbing time of such the walk appropriately rescaled converges in distribution to the exponential functional of a subordinator with a

-Lévy measure with some

A common case: Laplace exponents with asymptotic power expansions

In this section we give more concrete expressions of the equivalences of Corollary
2

is the exponential functional of a subordinator with Lévy measure
\pi

Lemma 19
.

Assume that
\phi
writes

\phi(x)=x^{\gamma}\left(1-\sum_{i=1}^{p}c_{i}{x^{-\gamma_{i}}}+O(x^{-1-\varepsilon})\right)\text{ as }x\rightarrow\infty

\gamma\in[0,1)

\frac{\psi(x)}{x}=x^{\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}}-\sum_{i=1}^{p}\frac{c_{i}}{(1-\gamma)}x^{\frac{\gamma-\gamma_{i}}{1-\gamma}}+O(x^{-1-\eta})

\mathbb{P}(I>t)\penalty\ \underset{t\rightarrow\infty}{\sim}\penalty\ k(t)t^{-\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}}\underset{t\rightarrow\infty}{\propto}t^{\frac{c_{p}-\frac{\gamma}{2}}{(1-\gamma)}}\exp\left(-(1-\gamma)t^{\frac{1}{1-\gamma}}+\sum_{i=1}^{p-1}\frac{c_{i}}{1-\gamma_{i}}t^{\frac{1-\gamma_{i}}{1-\gamma}}\right).

Proof.

By definition of
\psi
and using the assumption on
\phi
, we get for all
x>0

\psi(x)\underset{x\rightarrow\infty}{\sim}x^{\frac{1}{1-\gamma}}
and more precisely that

\displaystyle\psi(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle x^{\frac{1}{1-\gamma}}\left(1-\sum_{i=1}^{p}c_{i}\psi(x)^{-\gamma_{i}}+O\left(\psi(x)^{-1-\varepsilon}\right)\right)^{\frac{1}{1-\gamma}}

\displaystyle x^{\frac{1}{1-\gamma}}\left(1-\sum_{i=1}^{p}\frac{c_{i}}{(1-\gamma)}\psi(x)^{-\gamma_{i}}+O(\psi(x)^{-2\gamma_{1}})+O\left(\psi(x)^{-1-\varepsilon}\right)\right).

\displaystyle\psi(x)=x^{\frac{1}{1-\gamma}}-\sum_{i=1}^{p}\frac{c_{i}}{(1-\gamma)}x^{\frac{1-\gamma_{i}}{1-\gamma}}+O\Big(x^{\frac{1-2\gamma_{1}}{1-\gamma}}\Big)+O\Big(x^{-\frac{\varepsilon}{1-\gamma}}\Big).

\frac{\mathbb{P}\left(I_{X}>u\frac{\alpha t}{\psi(\alpha t)}+t\right)}{\mathbb{P}(I_{X}>t)}\underset{t\rightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}\exp(-u),\quad\forall u\geq 0

and we claim that for all
u>0
and all
t
large enough (independent of
u
)

\frac{\mathbb{P}(I_{X}>u\frac{\alpha t}{\psi(\alpha t)}+t)}{\mathbb{P}(I_{X}>t)}\leq\exp(-u).

Indeed, if we set
f(x):=-\ln(\mathbb{P}(I_{X}>x))
we know from Proposition
10
that
f^{\prime}(x)\geq\frac{\psi(\alpha x)}{\alpha x}
for all
x
large enough. Using that
x\mapsto\frac{\psi(x)}{x}
is non-decreasing, this implies that for
t
large enough and all
u>0

f\left(t+u\frac{\alpha t}{\psi(\alpha t)}\right)-f(t)=\int_{t}^{t+u\frac{\alpha t}{\psi(\alpha t)}}f^{\prime}(x)\mathrm{d}x\geq\frac{\psi(\alpha t)}{\alpha t}\left(t+u\frac{\alpha t}{\psi(\alpha t)}-t\right)=u

and therefore

\frac{\mathbb{P}(I_{X}>t+u\frac{\alpha t}{\psi(\alpha t)})}{\mathbb{P}(I_{X}>t)}=\exp\left(-f\left(t+u\frac{\alpha t}{\psi(\alpha t)}\right)+f(t)\right)\leq\exp(-u).

We can therefore apply the dominated convergence theorem to get from (
6.1
) that

\left(\frac{\psi(\alpha t)}{\alpha t}\right)^{\frac{a}{\alpha}}\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[X^{a}(t)\right]}{\mathbb{P}(I_{X}>t)}\underset{t\rightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}\frac{\frac{a}{\alpha}}{\mathbb{E}\Big[I_{X}^{\frac{a}{\alpha}}\Big]}\int_{0}^{\infty}u^{\frac{a}{\alpha}-1}\exp(-u)\mathrm{d}u=\mathbb{E}[R^{\frac{a}{\alpha}}].

Combined with Theorem
1
, this gives Corollary
3
.
