Introduction

Let
L^{m,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
denote the homogeneous Sobolev space of real-valued functions on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
whose (distributional) derivatives of order
m
belong to
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
for
1<p<\infty
. This space is equipped with the seminorm

||F||_{L^{m,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}=\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\nabla^{m}F(x)|^{p}\ dx\bigg)^{1/p}.

Provided
p>n/m
, any
F\in L^{m,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is a continuous function, and therefore can be restricted to an arbitrary subset
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We thus define the trace seminorm for functions
f:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
by

||f||_{L^{m,p}(\Omega)}=\inf\{\|F\|_{L^{m,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}:F\in L^{m,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),\;F|_{\Omega}=f\},

and we define the trace space
L^{m,p}(\Omega)
to be the set of all functions
f:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
with finite trace norm. We say that an operator
T:L^{m,p}(\Omega)\rightarrow L^{m,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is an
extension operator
if
Tf|_{\Omega}=f
for every
f\in L^{m,p}(\Omega)
.

In this article, we consider the
Sobolev extension problem
: Given a finite subset
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, does there exist a bounded linear extension operator
{T:L^{m,p}(\Omega)\rightarrow L^{m,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}
satisfying
||Tf||_{L^{m,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq C||f||_{L^{m,p}(\Omega)}
for some constant
C=C(m,n,p)
(in particular,
C
is independent of
\Omega
)?

When
p>n
and
m
is arbitrary, the second-named author, C. Fefferman, and G.K. Luli
[
5
]
completely resolved this problem in the affirmative.

When
n/m<p\leq n
, however, little is known. In this article, we consider the first nontrivial case in this parameter range – we study the Sobolev extension problem for the space
L^{2,p}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
when
1<p<2
. (Note that the problem is well-understood when
p=2
, because
L^{2,2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
is a Hilbert space.) We refer to this as the
planar Sobolev extension problem
.

For the remainder of this article, we assume that
1<p<2
. We now survey what is known about the planar Sobolev extension problem. (Our focus here is on the case in which the set
\Omega
is
finite
; for interesting results when
\Omega
is a bounded, simply connected domain, see
[
7
]
.)

Recently, M. Drake, C. Fefferman, K. Ren, and A. Skorobogatova
[
3
]
showed that there is a bounded linear extension operator
T:L^{2,p}(\Omega)\rightarrow L^{2,p}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
when
\Omega
is a finite subset of a line in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Moreover, the norm of their extension operator depends only on
p
, as desired.

In our previous paper
[
2
]
, we constructed a bounded linear extension operator
T:L^{2,p}(\Omega)\rightarrow L^{2,p}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
for
\Omega
belonging to a certain family of discrete subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with fractal geometry. We showed that the construction of such an operator could be reduced to an extension problem for a weighted Sobolev space on a tree. Thanks to a theorem of Fefferman-Klartag
[
6
]
, we were able to solve the extension problem on the tree, and thus construct a linear extension operator for the Sobolev space on the plane.

In this article, we continue to investigate the connection between the planar Sobolev extension problem and weighted Sobolev extension problem on trees. The main theorem of this paper establishes conditions under which these problems are equivalent.

Consider a rooted
N
-ary tree of depth
L\geq 1
with vertices
V
. By
N
-ary, we mean that every non-leaf node has
at most

N
children. In addition, to avoid degenerate branches, we require each non-leaf node to have at least 2 children. We’ll abuse notation and refer to
V
as the tree. We let
d(v)
denote the depth of
v\in V
.

We write
[N]=\{0,1,\dots,N-1\}
. We fix an ordering of the tree, i.e., an isomorphism from
V
to a subset of

\bigcup_{k=0}^{L}[N]^{k}

so that any
v\in V
is identified with a string of
d(v)
digits from the set
[N]
.

The root node of
V
is the empty string
\emptyset
of length zero. We write
V_{0}=V\backslash\{\emptyset\}
.

For
v\in V_{0}
and
1\leq k\leq d(v)
, let
v_{k}
denote the
k
-th entry of
v
and let
\pi_{k}(v)\in V_{0}
denote the prefix of
v
of length
k
. We define
\pi_{0}(v)=\emptyset
and write
\pi(v)=\pi_{d(v)-1}(v)
to denote the parent of
v\in V_{0}
. We denote the set of leaves of
V
by
\partial V
.

Given vertices
v_{0},v_{1}
in
V
, if
\pi_{d(v_{0})}(v_{1})=v_{0}
then we say that
v_{1}
is a
descendent
of
v_{0}
and that
v_{0}
is an
ancestor
of
v_{1}
. In particular, each vertex of
V
is both an ancestor and a descendent of itself. We let
\mathrm{lca}(x,y)
denote the
lowest common ancestor
of
x,y\in V
, namely, the ancestor of
x
and
y
of largest depth.

We suppose that we are given a set of
weights

\{W_{v}\}_{v\in V_{0}}
, where
W_{v}>0
for every
v\in V_{0}
. We define the
L^{1,p}(V)
-seminorm of
\Phi:V\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
by

||\Phi||_{L^{1,p}(V)}=\Big(\sum_{v\in V_{0}}|\Phi(v)-\Phi(\pi(v))|^{p}\cdot W_{v}^{2-p}\Big)^{1/p},

and the
L^{1,p}(\partial V)
trace seminorm of
\phi:\partial V\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
by

||\phi||_{L^{1,p}(\partial V)}=\inf\{||\Phi||_{L^{1,p}(V)}:\Phi|_{\partial V}=\phi\}.

We write
L^{1,p}(V)
,
L^{1,p}(\partial V)
to denote the spaces of real-valued functions on (respectively)
V
,
\partial V
, equipped with the relevant seminorm. We say that an operator
H:L^{1,p}(\partial V)\rightarrow L^{1,p}(V)
is an
extension operator
if
H\phi|_{\partial V}=\phi
for all
\phi:\partial V\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
.

We now state the
weighted Sobolev extension problem on trees
: For any
N
-ary tree
V
, as above, does there exist a bounded linear extension operator
H:L^{1,p}(\partial V)\rightarrow L^{1,p}(V)
satisfying

||H\phi||_{L^{1,p}(V)}\leq C||\phi||_{L^{1,p}(\partial V)}

for a constant
C=C(p,N)
(i.e.,
C
is independent of
V
and the weights
\{W_{v}\}_{v\in V_{0}}
)?

We say that an
N
-ary tree is
perfect
if each non-leaf node has exactly
N
children and all leaf nodes are at the same depth. We say that weights
\{W_{v}\}_{v\in V_{0}}
are
radially symmetric
if
W_{v}=W_{u}
for every
v,u\in V_{0}
with
d(v)=d(u)
.

Thanks to the work of Fefferman and Klartag
[
6
]
mentioned above, such an operator
H
is known to exist when
V
is a perfect, binary tree with radially symmetric weights. Additionally, in
[
1
]
, A. Björn, J. Björn, J. Gill, and N. Shanmugalingam show that
H
can be taken to be a simple averaging operator when
V
is a perfect tree with radially symmetric weights satisfying certain additional properties. These are the only results that we are aware of on the problem of weighted Sobolev extension on trees. We emphasize that, to our knowledge, nothing is known for finite trees when either (1) the tree
V
is not perfect or (2) the weights are not radially symmetric.

In this article, we make neither of these assumptions. Instead, we introduce a parameter
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and say that weights
\{W_{v}\}_{v\in V_{0}}
are
radially decaying
provided

W_{v}\leq\varepsilon W_{\pi(v)}\;\text{for all}\;v\in V_{0}.

(1)

Here and in the remainder of this paper, we adopt the convention that
W_{\emptyset}=1
. Clearly, for such radially decaying weights we have

W_{v_{1}}\leq\varepsilon^{d(v_{1})-d(v_{0})}W_{v_{0}}\mbox{ if }v_{1}\mbox{ is a descendent of }v_{0}\mbox{ in }V.

(2)

We then have the following theorem.

Theorem 1
.

There exists an absolute constant
k_{0}>0
so that the following holds. Fix
N\geq 2
. Let
V
be an
N
-ary tree, and let
\{W_{v}\}_{v\in V_{0}}
be radially decaying weights satisfying (
1
) for some
\varepsilon\leq k_{0}/N
. Then there exists a set
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that the following holds:

For any
1<p<2
, there exists a bounded linear extension operator
H:L^{1,p}(\partial V)\rightarrow L^{1,p}(V)
if and only if there exists a bounded linear extension operator
T:L^{2,p}(E)\rightarrow L^{2,p}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
.

In addition, if such operators exist, then

C^{-1}||T||_{L^{2,p}(E)\rightarrow L^{2,p}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\leq||H||_{L^{1,p}(\partial V)\rightarrow L^{1,p}(V)}\leq C||T||_{L^{2,p}(E)\rightarrow L^{2,p}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}

for a constant
C=C(p,N)
.

Thanks to Theorem
1
, a negative answer to the problem of Sobolev extension on trees with radially decaying weights would resolve the planar Sobolev extension problem in the negative. This would be the first known example of a negative answer to the general Sobolev extension problem.

Alternatively, a positive answer to the problem of Sobolev extension on trees with radially decaying weights would produce the first known example of a bounded linear extension operator
T:L^{2,p}(E)\rightarrow L^{2,p}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
for certain sets
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

We remark that in our previous paper
[
2
]
, we showed that for a certain set
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
there exists a bounded linear extension operator
L^{2,p}(E)\rightarrow L^{2,p}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
if there exists a bounded linear extension operator
L^{1,p}(\partial V)\rightarrow L^{1,p}(V)
for a certain full, binary, weighted tree. (Note that we did
not
show that the extension problems are equivalent.) Theorem
1
improves this result by (1) allowing for much more general trees and (2) establishing the equivalence of the extension problems.

For the remainder of this article we place ourselves in the setting of Theorem
1
: We let
k_{0}>0
be a small enough absolute constant, to be picked later, and we fix an integer
N\geq 2
, a rooted
N
-ary tree
V
(of which we fix some ordering), and radially decaying weights
\{W_{v}\}_{v\in V_{0}}
satisfying (
1
) for some
0<\varepsilon\leq k_{0}/N
.

We now construct the set
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
whose existence is asserted by Theorem
1
. Define

\Delta=\min_{v\in\partial V}W_{v}

(3)

and recursively define a map
\Psi:V\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
via

\Psi(v)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }v=\emptyset,\\
\Psi(\pi(v))+W_{\pi(v)}\cdot\frac{v_{d(v)}}{N-1}&\text{else}.\end{cases}

(4)

Observe that

\Psi(v)=\sum_{i=1}^{d(v)}W_{\pi_{i-1}(v)}\cdot\frac{v_{i}}{N-1}\;\text{for any}\;v\in V_{0}.

(5)

The set
E
is then of the form

E=E_{1}\cup E_{2},

where

\displaystyle E_{1}

\displaystyle=([0,2)\cap(\Delta\mathbb{Z}))\times\{0\},

(6)

\displaystyle E_{2}

\displaystyle=\{(\Psi(v),W_{v}):v\in\partial V\}.

(7)

See Figure
1
for an illustration of
E
corresponding to a specific weighted tree of depth 2.

This concludes the introduction; the remainder of this article is devoted to the proof of Theorem
1
.

Throughout this article, we will write
K,K^{\prime},k,k^{\prime},\dots
to denote positive absolute constants (independent of
p
and all other parameters), and
K_{X},K^{\prime}_{X},\dots
to denote positive constants depending on a parameter
X
. The value of these constants may change from line to line. For
A,B>0
we write
A\lesssim B
(resp.
A\lesssim_{X}B
) if there exists a constant
K
(resp.
K_{X}
) such that
A\leq KB
(resp.
A\leq K_{X}B
). We write
A\approx B
(resp.
A\approx_{X}
B) if
A\lesssim B
and
A\lesssim B
(resp.
A\lesssim_{X}B
and
B\lesssim_{X}A
).

Given
\delta>0
, we say a set
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
is
\delta
-separated
provided
|x-y|\geq\delta
for all distinct
x,y\in S
.

For a (Lebesgue) measurable function
F
defined on a measurable set
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
with
|S|>0
, we write
(F)_{S}:=|S|^{-1}\int_{S}F\ dx
.

Given an annulus
A=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:r\leq|x-x_{0}|\leq R\}
with inner radius
r
and outer radius
R
, the
thickness ratio
of
A
is defined to be the quantity
R/r
.

The following version of the Sobolev inequality is proved in
[
2
]
.

Lemma 1
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a square, a ball, or an annulus with thickness ratio at most
C_{0}\in[1,\infty)
and let
1<r<2
. For any
F\in L^{2,r}(\Omega)
and any
x\in\Omega
, we define an affine function
T_{x,\Omega}(F):\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
by

T_{x,\Omega}(F)(y)=F(x)+(\nabla F)_{\Omega}\cdot(y-x).

We then have, for any
y\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, that

|T_{x,\Omega}(F)(y)-T_{z,\Omega}(F)(y)|\lesssim_{r,C_{0}}\|F\|_{L^{2,r}(\Omega)}|x-z|^{2-2/r}\;\text{for any}\;x,z\in\Omega.

In particular,

\|F-T_{x,\Omega}(F)\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\lesssim_{r,C_{0}}\mathrm{diam}(\Omega)^{2-2/r}\|F\|_{L^{2,r}(\Omega)}.

Let
B(z,r)
denote the ball of radius
r>0
centered at
z\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, and let
\mathcal{M}
denote the uncentered Hardy-Littlewood maximal operator, i.e.,

(\mathcal{M}f)(x)=\sup_{B(z,r)\ni x}\frac{1}{|B(z,r)|}\int_{B(z,r)}f(y)\ dy\;\text{for any}\;f\in L^{1}_{\text{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{2}).

Recall that
\mathcal{M}
is a bounded operator from
L^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
to
L^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
for any
1<q\leq\infty
(see, e.g.,
[
8
]
).

3
Properties of the map
\Psi

Recall from Section
1
that the children of any vertex of
V
are ordered. Precisely, for children
x,y
of a common parent we say that
x<y
if
x_{d(x)}<y_{d(y)}
.

This induces an ordering on the leaves
\partial V
. Consider distinct
v,w\in\partial V
with
d(\mathrm{lca}(v,w))=m
, so that
v_{i}=w_{i}
for all
0\leq i\leq m
but
v_{m+1}\neq w_{m+1}
. Then we say that
v<w
if and only if
v_{m+1}<w_{m+1}
.

Lemma 2
.

Let
\Psi:V\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be the map defined in Section
1
. Then for any distinct
v,w\in\partial V
, the following hold:

•

If
v<w
, then
0\leq\Psi(v)<\Psi(w)<2
.

•

For an absolute constant
K>1
,

K^{-1}W_{\emph{lca}(v,w)}/N\leq|\Psi(w)-\Psi(v)|\leq KW_{\emph{lca}(v,w)}.

Proof.

We claim that
\Psi(w)\in[0,2)
for any
w\in V
. Indeed,
\Psi(w)\geq 0
is immediate from the representation (
5
). Because the weights are radially decaying and
W_{\emptyset}=1
, we have
W_{\tilde{v}}\leq\varepsilon^{d(\tilde{v})}
for all
\tilde{v}\in V
. Observe that
\varepsilon<1/2
, since we have assumed
\varepsilon<k_{0}/N
for small enough
k_{0}
. By (
5
) and the fact that
w_{i}\leq N-1
for all
i
, we deduce that

\Psi(w)=\sum_{i=1}^{d(v)}W_{\pi_{i-1}(w)}\cdot\frac{w_{i}}{N-1}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{d(w)}W_{\pi_{i-1}(w)}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{d(w)}\varepsilon^{i-1}<2.

We will show that the embedding
\Psi|_{\partial V}:\partial V\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is order preserving. To see this, we fix
v,w\in\partial V
with
d(\mathrm{lca}(v,w))=m
and
v<w
. By (
5
), we have

\Psi(w)-\Psi(v)\geq W_{\pi_{m}(w)}\cdot\frac{w_{m+1}-v_{m+1}}{N-1}-\mathbbm{1}_{d(v)\geq m+2}\sum_{i=m+2}^{d(v)}W_{\pi_{i-1}(v)}\cdot\frac{v_{i}}{N-1}.

(8)

Note that
w_{m+1}>v_{m+1}
, so
w_{m+1}-v_{m+1}\geq 1
. If
d(v)=m+1
, then (
8
) implies that

\Psi(w)-\Psi(v)>\frac{W_{\pi_{m}(w)}}{N}.

Assume instead that
d(v)\geq m+2
. From (
2
), and since
v_{i}\leq N-1
and
\varepsilon<1/2
, we get

\sum_{i=m+2}^{d(v)}W_{\pi_{i-1}(v)}\cdot\frac{v_{i}}{N-1}\leq W_{\pi_{m}(v)}\sum_{i=1}^{d(v)-m-1}\varepsilon^{i}\leq 2\varepsilon W_{\pi_{m}(v)}.

(9)

Combining this with (
8
) and using that
\pi_{m}(w)=\pi_{m}(v)=\mathrm{lca}(w,v)
, and
\varepsilon\leq k_{0}/N
for sufficiently small
k_{0}
, gives

\Psi(w)-\Psi(v)\geq W_{\mathrm{lca}(w,v)}\cdot\frac{1-2k_{0}}{N}>\frac{W_{\mathrm{lca}(w,v)}}{2N}.

(10)

In particular,
\Psi(w)>\Psi(v)
for any
v,w\in\partial V
with
w>v
. Therefore, the embedding
\Psi|_{\partial V}
of
\partial V
into
\mathbb{R}
is order preserving.

We now claim that

|\Psi(v)-\Psi(w)|\leq KW_{\mathrm{lca}(v,w)}\;\text{for any distinct}\;v,w\in\partial V.

(11)

Consider distinct
v,w\in\partial V
with
d(\mathrm{lca}(v,w))=m
. Combining (
5
) and the triangle inequality gives

|\Psi(v)-\Psi(w)|\leq\sum_{i=m+1}^{d(v)}W_{\pi_{i-1}(v)}\cdot\frac{v_{i}}{N-1}+\sum_{i=m+1}^{d(w)}W_{\pi_{i-1}(w)}\cdot\frac{w_{i}}{N-1}.

Arguing as in (
9
), we deduce (
11
). Together with (
10
), we have established the second bullet point of the lemma.

∎

Recall that the map
\Psi
is used to define the set
E_{2}
in (
7
), and
E_{1}\subset\mathbb{R}\times\{0\}
is defined in (
6
). We now establish some basic properties of the set
E
.

Lemma 3
.

The set
E
has the following properties:

1.

E\subset[0,2)\times[0,2)
,

2.

E
is
\Delta
-separated,

3.

Let
x\in E_{2}
. Then

(a)

\Delta\leq x^{(2)}\leq\mathrm{dist}(x,E_{1})\leq 2x^{(2)}
,

(b)

\Delta\leq x^{(2)}\leq\mathrm{dist}(x,E_{2}\backslash\{x\})
.

Proof.

Since the weights are radially decaying, we have
W_{v}<1
for any
v\in\partial V
. Combining this with (
6
) and Lemma
2
, we deduce Part
1
of the lemma.

Note that Part
2
of the lemma follows from Part
3
(recall that the points of
E_{1}
are
\Delta
-separated by definition.

It remains to prove Part
3
.

Let
x=(x^{(1)},x^{(2)})\in E_{2}
. Then
x=(\Psi(v),W_{v})
for some
v\in\partial V
. Therefore,

\mathrm{dist}(x,E_{1})\geq x^{(2)}\geq\min_{v\in\partial V}W_{v}=\Delta.

Since
\Psi(v)\in[0,2)
for
v\in\partial V
, and by definition of
E_{1}
in (
6
),

\mathrm{dist}(x,E_{1})\leq x^{(2)}+\mathrm{dist}((x^{(1)},0),E_{1})\leq x^{(2)}+\Delta\leq 2x^{(2)}.

By Lemma
2
, and since the weights are radially decaying,

\mathrm{dist}(x,E_{2}\backslash\{x\})\geq\frac{W_{\pi(v)}}{KN}\geq\frac{W_{v}}{KN\varepsilon}.

Recall that
\varepsilon\leq k_{0}/N
, and thus taking
k_{0}
sufficiently small gives

\mathrm{dist}(x,E_{2}\backslash\{x\})\geq\frac{x^{(2)}}{KN\varepsilon}\geq\frac{x^{(2)}}{Kk_{0}}\geq x^{(2)}\geq\Delta.

This concludes the proof of Part
3
.
∎

4
The Whitney decomposition

This section borrows heavily from Section 3 of our previous paper
[
2
]
.

We will work with squares in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
; by this we mean axis parallel squares of the form
Q=[a_{1},b_{1})\times[a_{2},b_{2})
. We let
\delta_{Q}
denote the sidelength of such a square
Q
. To
bisect
a square
Q
is to partition
Q
into squares
Q_{1},Q_{2},Q_{3},Q_{4}
, where
\delta_{Q_{i}}=\delta_{Q}/2
for each
i=1,2,3,4
. We refer to the
Q_{i}
as the
children
of
Q
.

We define a square
Q^{0}=[-3,5)\times[-3,5)
; note that
E\subset Q^{0}
. A
dyadic
square
Q
is one that arises from repeated bisection of
Q^{0}
. Every dyadic square
Q\neq Q^{0}
is the child of some square
Q^{\prime}
; we call
Q^{\prime}
the
parent
of
Q
and denote this by
(Q)^{+}=Q^{\prime}
.

We say that two dyadic square
Q,Q^{\prime}

touch
if
1.1Q\cap 1.1Q^{\prime}\neq\emptyset
. We write
Q\leftrightarrow Q^{\prime}
to denote that
Q
touches
Q^{\prime}
.

For any dyadic square
Q
, we define a collection
\mathcal{W}(Q)
, called the
Whitney decomposition of

Q
, by setting

\mathcal{W}(Q)=\{Q\}\;\text{if }\#(3Q\cap E)\leq 1,

and

\mathcal{W}(Q)=\bigcup\{\mathcal{W}(Q^{\prime}):(Q^{\prime})^{+}=Q\}\;\text{if}\;\#(3Q\cap E)\geq 2.

We write
\mathcal{W}=\mathcal{W}(Q^{0})
. Evidently,
\mathcal{W}
is a partition of
Q^{0}
by dyadic squares. Note that

Lemma 5
.

For any

\delta_{Q}\approx(\Delta+\mathrm{dist}(Q,E_{1}))

For

(15)

Since any

4.2
Whitney partition of unity

Let
\{\theta_{Q}\}_{Q\in\mathcal{W}}
be a partition of unity subordinate to
\mathcal{W}
constructed so that the following properties hold. For any
Q\in\mathcal{W}
,

(POU1)

\mathrm{supp}(\theta_{Q})\subset 1.1Q
.

(POU2)

For any
|\alpha|\leq 2
,
\|\partial^{\alpha}\theta_{Q}\|_{L^{\infty}}\lesssim\delta_{Q}^{-|\alpha|}.

(POU3)

0\leq\theta_{Q}\leq 1
.

For any
x\in Q^{0}
,

(POU4)

\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{W}}\theta_{Q}(x)=1.

The construction of such a partition of unity is a standard exercise and may be found in the literature; e.g., see
[
4
]
.

Lemma 7

(Patching Lemma)
.

Given affine polynomials
\{P_{Q}\}_{Q\in\mathcal{W}}
, define
{F:Q^{0}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}}
by

F(x)=\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{W}}\theta_{Q}(x)P_{Q}(x).

Then

||F||_{L^{2,p}(Q^{0})}^{p}\lesssim_{p}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Q,Q^{\prime}\in\mathcal{W}:\\
Q\leftrightarrow Q^{\prime}\end{subarray}}||P_{Q}-P_{Q^{\prime}}||_{L^{\infty}(Q)}^{p}\delta_{Q}^{2-2p}.

Proof.

Fix a square
Q^{\prime}\in\mathcal{W}
. Observe that

F(x)=\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{W}}\theta_{Q}(x)[P_{Q}(x)-P_{Q^{\prime}}(x)]+P_{Q^{\prime}}(x)\qquad(x\in Q^{0}).

By Property 4 of Lemma
4
, there are a bounded number of squares
Q\in\mathcal{W}
for which
x\in(1.1Q)\cap Q^{\prime}
. Therefore, by
(POU1)
, there are a bounded number of
Q\in\mathcal{W}
with
\mathrm{supp}(\theta_{Q})\cap Q^{\prime}\neq\emptyset
. Taking
2^{\text{nd}}
derivatives, using
(POU2)
, and integrating
p^{\text{th}}
powers then gives

\|F\|_{L^{2,p}(Q^{\prime})}^{p}\lesssim_{p}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Q\in\mathcal{W}:\\
Q\leftrightarrow Q^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\big\{\delta_{Q}^{2-2p}\|P_{Q}-P_{Q^{\prime}}\|^{p}_{L^{\infty}(Q)}+\delta_{Q}^{2-p}|\nabla(P_{Q}-P_{Q^{\prime}})|^{p}\big\}.

For any affine polynomial
P
and any square
Q
, we have
|\nabla P|\leq\delta_{Q}^{-1}||P||_{L^{\infty}(Q)}
, and thus

\|F\|_{L^{2,p}(Q^{\prime})}^{p}\lesssim_{p}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Q\in\mathcal{W}:\\
Q\leftrightarrow Q^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\delta_{Q}^{2-2p}\|P_{Q}-P_{Q^{\prime}}\|^{p}_{L^{\infty}(Q)}.

Since
\mathcal{W}
is partition of
Q^{0}
, summing over
Q^{\prime}\in\mathcal{W}
proves the lemma.
∎

5
Clusters of the set
E_{2}

For the remainder of this article we fix a sufficiently large absolute constant

so that the conclusion of Lemma
6
holds. All constants

etc. may depend on
K_{0}
.

For each
v\in V
we define the

S_{v}=\{u\in\partial V:\pi_{d(v)}(u)=v\}.

(19)

Each shadow is a subset of
\partial V
; we let
\mathcal{S}=\{S_{v}\}_{v\in V}
be the collection of shadows. Recall that we defined

E_{2}=\{(\Psi(v),W_{v}):v\in\partial V\},

and therefore the set of leaves

. This determines an injection
\mathcal{S}\rightarrow 2^{E_{2}}
(where

denotes the power set of

). We define the

to be the image of

(20)

The set of all clusters

\mathcal{C}:=\{C_{v}\}_{v\in V}

forms a tree under the relation of set inclusion, i.e.,
C\in\mathcal{C}
is an ancestor of
C^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}
if

. Observe that for any two clusters

exactly one of the following is true: (1)
C
is an ancestor or descendant of

, or (2)

. We identify this tree with the tree

via the isomorphism

. As with

, we denote the set of leaves of

(note that

is the root node of the tree
\mathcal{C}
).

We naturally associate to the tree
\mathcal{C}
a family of weights
\{W_{C}\}_{C\in\mathcal{C}}
by setting
W_{C_{v}}=W_{v}
for every

. We can then define the weighted Sobolev space
L^{1,p}(\mathcal{C})
and the analogous trace space
L^{1,p}(\partial\mathcal{C})
. Since the weighted trees
V
and
\mathcal{C}
are isomorphic, a bounded linear extension operator

induces a bounded linear extension operator
\mathcal{H}:L^{1,p}(\partial\mathcal{C})\rightarrow L^{1,p}(\mathcal{C})
, and vice versa. Moreover, such operators have equal operator norms. We will make use of these facts in Sections
6
and
7
.

We next detail some basic geometric properties of the clusters of
E_{2}
.

Note that the root of the tree
\mathcal{C}
is the set
C_{\emptyset}=E_{2}
, while the set of leaves

(21)

Using Lemma
2
, the definition of clusters (see (
19

\displaystyle N^{-1}W_{C}\lesssim\mathrm{diam}(C)\lesssim W_{C}\;\text{for every}\;C\in\mathcal{C}\backslash\partial\mathcal{C},

(22)

\displaystyle\mathrm{dist}(C,C^{\prime})\gtrsim N^{-1}(W_{\pi(C)}+W_{\pi(C^{\prime})})\;\text{for any}\;C,C^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}\;\text{with}\;C\cap C^{\prime}=\emptyset.

(23)

For each

. Observe that the singleton cluster

(24)

(26)

\displaystyle Q^{0}=[-3,5)\times[-3,5)\subset B_{E_{2}}

(45)

As in the previous section, we let

r=\frac{p+1}{2}

and claim that for any
Q,Q^{\prime}\in\mathcal{W}
with
Q\leftrightarrow Q^{\prime}
we have

\|L_{Q}-L_{Q^{\prime}}\|_{L^{\infty}(Q)}^{r}\lesssim_{p}\delta_{Q}^{2r-2}\|G\|_{L^{2,r}(5K_{0}Q)}^{r}.

(46)

This implies that

\|L_{Q}-L_{Q^{\prime}}\|_{L^{\infty}(Q)}^{r}\lesssim_{p}\delta_{Q}^{2r}\mathcal{M}(|\nabla^{2}G|^{r})(z)\;\text{for any}\;z\in Q,

where
\mathcal{M}
is the Hardy-Littlewood maximal operator (see Section
2
). Taking
(p/r)
-th powers and integrating gives

\|L_{Q}-L_{Q^{\prime}}\|_{L^{\infty}(Q)}^{p}\lesssim_{p}\delta_{Q}^{2p-2}\|\mathcal{M}(|\nabla^{2}G|^{r})\|_{L^{p/r}(Q)}^{p/r}.

By Property 3 of Lemma
4
, we deduce that

\sum_{Q\leftrightarrow Q^{\prime}}\|L_{Q}-L_{Q^{\prime}}\|_{L^{\infty}(Q)}^{p}\delta_{Q}^{2-2p}\lesssim_{p}\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{W}}\|\mathcal{M}(|\nabla^{2}G|^{r})\|_{L^{p/r}(Q)}^{p/r}.

Squares in
\mathcal{W}
have pairwise disjoint interiors (since
\mathcal{W}
is a partition of
Q^{0}
), and thus we have

\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{W}}\|\mathcal{M}(|\nabla^{2}G|^{r})\|_{L^{p/r}(Q)}^{p/r}\leq\|\mathcal{M}(|\nabla^{2}G|^{r})\|_{L^{p/r}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}^{p/r}.

Because the Hardy-Littlewood maximal operator is bounded from
L^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
to
L^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
for
1<q\leq\infty
, we deduce that

\sum_{Q\leftrightarrow Q^{\prime}}||L_{Q}-L_{Q^{\prime}}||^{p}_{L^{\infty}(Q)}\delta_{Q}^{2-2p}\lesssim_{p}||G||_{L^{2,p}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}^{p}\leq||\mathcal{H}||^{p}\cdot||G||_{L^{2,p}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}^{p}.

(47)

(The last inequality simply uses that
||\mathcal{H}||\geq 1
.) We now prove (
46
).

Fix
Q,Q^{\prime}\in\mathcal{W}
with
Q\leftrightarrow Q^{\prime}
and observe that for any
x\in Q
we have

L_{Q}(x)-L_{Q^{\prime}}(x)=f(z_{Q})+(\nabla L_{Q})\cdot(x-z_{Q})-f(z_{Q^{\prime}})-(\nabla L_{Q^{\prime}})\cdot(x-z_{Q^{\prime}}).

Since
G(z_{Q^{\prime}})=f(z_{Q^{\prime}})
, and since
z_{Q}^{(2)}=z_{Q^{\prime}}^{(2)}=0
, we have

T_{z_{Q^{\prime}},5K_{0}Q}(G)(z_{Q})=f(z_{Q^{\prime}})+(\partial_{1}G)_{5K_{0}Q}(z_{Q}^{(1)}-z_{Q^{\prime}}^{(1)}).

(See Section
2
for the definition of
T_{z_{Q^{\prime}},5K_{0}Q}
.) By Lemma
4
, we have
|x-z_{Q}|,|x-z_{Q^{\prime}}|,|z_{Q}-z_{Q^{\prime}}|\lesssim\delta_{Q}
for
x\in Q
. Therefore, since
\partial_{2}L_{Q}=\partial_{2}L_{Q^{\prime}}=0
, by the triangle inequality we have

\begin{split}\|L_{Q}-L_{Q^{\prime}}\|_{L^{\infty}(Q)}\leq\;&|f(z_{Q})-T_{z_{Q^{\prime}},5K_{0}Q}(G)(z_{Q})|\\
&+|(\partial_{1}G)_{5K_{0}Q}-\partial_{1}L_{Q}|\cdot\delta_{Q}\\
&+|(\partial_{1}G)_{5K_{0}Q}-\partial_{1}L_{Q^{\prime}}|\cdot\delta_{Q}.\end{split}

(48)

By Lemma
4
,
\frac{1}{2}\delta_{Q^{\prime}}\leq\delta_{Q}\leq 2\delta_{Q^{\prime}}
, and thus,
K_{0}Q^{\prime}\subset 5K_{0}Q
. In particular,

\{z_{Q},w_{Q},z_{Q^{\prime}},w_{Q^{\prime}}\}\subset 5K_{0}Q\cap E_{1}.

Since
G|_{E}=f
, Lemma
1
implies

\displaystyle|f(z_{Q})-T_{z_{Q^{\prime}},5K_{0}Q}(G)(z_{Q})|\lesssim_{p}\delta_{Q}^{2-2/r}\|G\|_{L^{2,r}(5K_{0}Q)},

\displaystyle|f(z_{Q})-f(w_{Q})-(\partial_{1}G)_{5K_{0}Q}\cdot(z_{Q}^{(1)}-w_{Q}^{(1)})|\lesssim_{p}\delta_{Q}^{2-2/r}\|G\|_{L^{2,r}(5K_{0}Q)},

\displaystyle|f(z_{Q^{\prime}})-f(w_{Q^{\prime}})-(\partial_{1}G)_{5K_{0}Q}\cdot(z_{Q^{\prime}}^{(1)}-w_{Q^{\prime}}^{(1)})|\lesssim_{p}\delta_{Q}^{2-2/r}\|G\|_{L^{2,r}(5K_{0}Q)}.

By definition of
L_{Q}
we have
\partial_{1}L_{Q}=\frac{f(z_{Q})-f(w_{Q})}{z_{Q}^{(1)}-w_{Q}^{(1)}}
and
\partial_{1}L_{Q^{\prime}}=\frac{f(z_{Q^{\prime}})-f(w_{Q^{\prime}})}{z_{Q^{\prime}}^{(1)}-w_{Q^{\prime}}^{(1)}}
. Thus, by combining the previous inequality with (
48
) we deduce (
46
).

Next, we claim that

\sum_{Q\leftrightarrow Q^{\prime}}|\Phi(C_{Q})-\Phi(C_{Q^{\prime}})|^{p}\cdot\delta_{Q}^{2-p}\lesssim_{p,N}||\mathcal{H}||^{p}\cdot||G||_{L^{2,p}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}^{p}.

(49)

Combining this with (
45
) and (
47
) proves (
44
), establishing
(F3)
. We now prove (
49
).

By Lemma
8
, if
Q\leftrightarrow Q^{\prime}
and
C_{Q}\neq C_{Q^{\prime}}
, then either
C_{Q}=\pi(C_{Q^{\prime}})
or
C_{Q^{\prime}}=\pi(C_{Q})
. Therefore,

\sum_{Q\leftrightarrow Q^{\prime}}|\Phi(C_{Q})-\Phi(C_{Q^{\prime}})|^{p}\cdot\delta_{Q}^{2-p}\lesssim\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}_{0}}|\Phi(C)-\Phi(\pi(C))|^{p}\sum_{(Q,Q^{\prime})\in\mathcal{Q}_{C}}\delta_{Q}^{2-p},

(50)

where

\mathcal{Q}_{C}=\{(Q,Q^{\prime})\in\mathcal{W}\times\mathcal{W}:Q\leftrightarrow Q^{\prime},C_{Q}=C,C_{Q^{\prime}}=\pi(C)\}

(as in Lemma
8
). Applying Lemma
8
gives

\sum_{Q\leftrightarrow Q^{\prime}}|\Phi(C_{Q})-\Phi(C_{Q^{\prime}})|^{p}\cdot\delta_{Q}^{2-p}\lesssim_{p}\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}_{0}}|\Phi(C)-\Phi(\pi(C))|^{p}\cdot W_{C}^{2-p}.

Since
\mathcal{H}
is a bounded linear extension operator and
\Phi=\mathcal{H}\phi
, we have that

\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}_{0}}|\Phi(C)-\Phi(\pi(C))|^{p}\cdot W_{C}^{2-p}\lesssim_{p}||\mathcal{H}||^{p}\cdot\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}_{0}}|\Xi(C)-\Xi(\pi(C))|^{p}\cdot W_{C}^{2-p}

for any
\Xi:\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
satisfying
\Xi|_{\partial\mathcal{C}}=\phi
. Taking
\Xi(C)=(\partial_{2}G)_{B_{C}}
for
C\in\mathcal{C}\backslash\partial\mathcal{C}
, we use (
42
) and apply the triangle inequality to get

\begin{split}\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}_{0}}|\Phi(C)-\Phi(\pi(C))|^{p}\cdot W_{C}^{2-p}&\\
\lesssim_{p}||\mathcal{H}||^{p}\cdot\bigg\{&\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}_{0}}|(\partial_{2}G)_{B_{C}}-(\partial_{2}G)_{B_{\pi(C)}}|^{p}\cdot W_{C}^{2-p}\\
&+\sum_{C\in\partial\mathcal{C}}|\partial_{2}P_{x_{C}}-(\partial_{2}G)_{B_{C}}|^{p}\cdot W_{C}^{2-p}\bigg\}.\end{split}

(51)

Applying Lemma
9
(note that by (
41
), (
31
), we have for every
x\in E_{2}
that
P_{x}=T_{x}(G)
) gives

\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}_{0}}|\Phi(C)-\Phi(\pi(C))|^{p}\cdot W_{C}^{2-p}\lesssim_{p,N}||\mathcal{H}||^{p}\cdot||G||_{L^{2,p}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}^{p};

(52)

we deduce (
49
).

We have thus constructed
F\in L^{2,p}(Q^{0})
and shown that it satisfies
(F1)
–
(F3)
. It remains to extend
F
to a function on all of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

Recall the set of boundary squares

\partial\mathcal{W}=\{Q\in\mathcal{W}:1.1Q\cap\partial Q^{0}\neq\emptyset\},

introduced in Section
4
.

By Lemma
6
, there exist points
z_{0},w_{0}
so that

z_{Q}=z_{0},\;w_{Q}=w_{0}\;\text{for all}\;Q\in\partial\mathcal{W}.

We define
L_{0}
to be the unique affine polynomial satisfying

L_{0}|_{\{z_{0},w_{0}\}}=f|_{\{z_{0},w_{0}\}}\quad\text{and}\quad\partial_{2}L_{0}=0.

We then have

L_{Q}=L_{0}\;\text{for all}\;Q\in\partial\mathcal{W}.

Similarly, by Lemma
8
we have for every
Q\in\partial\mathcal{W}
that
C_{Q}=E_{2}
and thus

\Phi(C_{Q})=\Phi(E_{2})\;\text{for all}\;Q\in\partial\mathcal{W}.

Invoking (
43
), we see that

P_{Q}=L_{0}+x^{(2)}\cdot\Phi(E_{2})\;\text{for all}\;Q\in\partial\mathcal{W}.

We define

\tilde{F}(x)=\begin{cases}F(x)&\text{if }x\in Q^{0},\\
L_{0}(x)+x^{(2)}\cdot\Phi(E_{2})&\text{if }x\notin Q^{0}.\end{cases}

Recall (see (
40
)) that
F
is of the form

F=\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{W}}P_{Q}\theta_{Q},

with
\mathrm{supp}(\theta_{Q})\subset 1.1Q
, and therefore
\tilde{F}\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
. Clearly, then,
||\tilde{F}||_{L^{2,p}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}=||F||_{L^{2,p}(Q^{0})}
and
\tilde{F}|_{E}=F|_{E}=f
. Moreover,
\tilde{F}
depends linearly on
f
thanks to
(F1)
, the definition of
L_{0}
, and the definition of
\Phi
.

For any
f\in L^{2,p}(E)
, we define

(Tf)(x)=\tilde{F}(x)\;(x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}).

Then
T:L^{2,p}(E)\rightarrow L^{2,p}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
is a bounded linear extension operator satisfying (
39
).

7
The extension operator for
L^{1,p}(V)

In this section, we assume the existence of a bounded linear extension operator
T:L^{2,p}(E)\rightarrow L^{2,p}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
as in Theorem
1
. Using
T
, we construct a bounded linear extension operator
\mathcal{H}:L^{1,p}(\partial\mathcal{C})\rightarrow L^{1,p}(\mathcal{C})
satisfying

||\mathcal{H}||_{L^{1,p}(\partial\mathcal{C})\rightarrow L^{1,p}(\mathcal{C})}\lesssim_{p,N}||T||_{L^{2,p}(E)\rightarrow L^{2,p}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}.

(53)

As in the previous section, we use that
V
and
\mathcal{C}
are isomorphic to observe that
\mathcal{H}
induces a bounded linear extension operator
H:L^{1,p}(\partial V)\rightarrow L^{1,p}(V)
satisfying

||H||_{L^{1,p}(\partial V)\rightarrow L^{1,p}(V)}=||\mathcal{H}||_{L^{1,p}(\partial\mathcal{C})\rightarrow L^{1,p}(\mathcal{C})}.

Combined with the results of the previous section, this proves Theorem
1
.

We write
||T||:=||T||_{L^{2,p}(E)\rightarrow L^{2,p}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}
.

Suppose that we are given a function
\phi:\partial\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. We must then construct a function
\Phi:\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
satisfying:

(
\Phi
1)

\Phi
is determined linearly by the data
\phi
,

(
\Phi
2)

,

(
\Phi
3)

||\Phi||_{L^{1,p}(\mathcal{C})}\lesssim_{p,N}||T||\cdot||\Xi||_{L^{1,p}(\mathcal{C})}
for any
\Xi:\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
with
\Xi|_{\partial\mathcal{C}}=\phi
.

Once we’ve constructed such a
\Phi
, we define

\mathcal{H}\phi(C)=\Phi(C)\;\text{for all}\;C\in\mathcal{C},

establishing (
53
). We prepare to construct the function
\Phi
.

First, recall that for every
x\in E_{2}
we have
\{x\}\in\partial\mathcal{C}
. We can thus define a function

by setting

\phi(\{x\})\cdot W_{\{x\}}&\text{if }x\in E_{2}.\end{cases}

We then apply the extension operator
T
to
f
and obtain a function
F\in L^{2,p}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, i.e., we define

F(x)=Tf(x)\;\text{for }x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}.

(54)

We now define the function
\Phi:\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
by

\Phi(C)=\begin{cases}\phi(C)&\text{if }C\in\partial\mathcal{C},\\
(\partial_{2}F)_{B_{C}}&\text{if }C\in\mathcal{C}\backslash\partial\mathcal{C}.\end{cases}

Property
(

Φ

2)
is an immediate consequence of the definition of
\Phi
, and Property
(

Φ

1)
follows easily from the linearity of
T
and the definition of

. It remains to prove that
\Phi
satisfies
(

Φ

Observe that

\displaystyle||\Phi||_{L^{1,p}(\mathcal{C})}^{p}=

\displaystyle\sum_{C\in\partial\mathcal{C}}|\phi(C)-(\partial_{2}F)_{B_{\pi(C)}}|^{p}\cdot W_{C}^{2-p}

\displaystyle+\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}_{0}\backslash\partial\mathcal{C}}|(\partial_{2}F)_{B_{C}}-(\partial_{2}F)_{B_{\pi(C)}}|^{p}\cdot W_{C}^{2-p}.

Applying the triangle inequality gives

\displaystyle||\Phi||_{L^{1,p}(\mathcal{C})}^{p}=

\displaystyle\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}_{0}}|(\partial_{2}F)_{B_{C}}-(\partial_{2}F)_{B_{\pi(C)}}|^{p}\cdot W_{C}^{2-p}

\displaystyle+\sum_{C\in\partial\mathcal{C}}|\phi(C)-(\partial_{2}F)_{B_{C}}|^{p}\cdot W_{C}^{2-p}.

Observe that
\phi(C)=\partial_{2}(T_{x_{C}}(F))
for

Since

||\Phi||_{L^{1,p}(\mathcal{C})}^{p}\lesssim_{p,N}||T||^{p}\cdot||G||_{L^{2,p}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}^{p}

for any

satisfying

.

We now assume that we are given
\Xi:\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
satisfying
\Xi|_{\partial\mathcal{C}}=\phi
. In a moment, we will define a function
\widetilde{G}\in L^{2,p}(\mathbb{R}^{2})

\widetilde{G}|_{E}=f

(55)

\Phi
satisfies Property
(

Φ

3)
.

We define the function
\widetilde{G}
introduced above using the Whitney Decomposition

(see Section
