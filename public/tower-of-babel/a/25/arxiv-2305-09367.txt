(Non)Null results of time-varying α \alpha in the meVSL
Seokcheon Lee

Abstract
In the expanding Universe, the dimensional quantities like the wavelength and the temperature of photons are cosmologically redshifted by the relative difference between the observed and emitted ones. Therefore, it can be physically meaningful to talk about the constancy or variability of any dimensional constant (not only of dimensionless one) when the Universe is expanding. It has been known that one can measure the temporal variation of the fine structure constant α \alpha in the emission and absorption lines of quasar spectra when the speed of light varies for cosmic time, even though this statement is model dependent. Current observations based on the alkali doublet method and on the many-multiplet one show superficially contradictory results. The former finds no statistically significant evidence for a time dependence of α \alpha , while the latter does. The so-called meVSL model can reconcile these results naturally without any contradiction.

II
Quasar spectra

One way to investigate the time variation of the
\alpha
at high redshifts using quasar spectra is by analyzing the absorption lines in the spectra of foreground gas clouds. Astronomers measure the wavelengths of these absorption lines determined by the energy levels of the atoms and ions in the gas clouds. Since the
\alpha
is related to the energy levels of atomic transitions, one might be able to detect any variation in
\alpha
over time in the observed wavelengths of the absorption lines. If
\alpha
varies over time, the spacing between the absorption lines would also change, producing a measurable shift in the observed wavelengths. There exist two popular methods.

III
Consequences

Observationally, the AD method only makes use of the difference and the ratio of wavelengths, while the MM method requires knowing accurately the individual values of the wavelengths for all the transitions. Results of the AD method show no cosmic evolution of
\alpha,
and those of the MM method show its cosmic evolution. This conclusion is considered a contradiction to each other and leads to different arguments for the time variation of
\alpha
. It may be due to systematics. However, we show that these two observation results can be consistent with the prediction of the meVSL model that supports both the time variation of the
\alpha
and those of other physical quantities. Due to the same scale factor dependence of the Rydberg energy unit and
\alpha^{2}
, we cannot obtain the redshift dependence by considering the wavelength difference proportional to
\alpha^{2}
only. However, if one can find the contribution from
\alpha^{4}
as proposed in the MM method, then one can observe the time variation of
\alpha
from QSO absorption lines.
q_{2}

q_{2}

Dzuba:1998au
. This fact might be the reason for the current null results for time-varying

Murphy:2021xhb

However, it is important to note that this technique is subject to various systematic uncertainties, such as uncertainties in the atomic data used to interpret the spectra, and the possibility of unknown systematic effects in the data. Therefore, multiple independent measurements and cross-checks are necessary to confirm any potential variation in
\alpha
.

IV
Conclusion

Even in standard cosmology, observations show that the dimensional quantities are cosmologically redshifted, and we can measure the cosmic time variation of those quantities. Thus, we can extend our standard cosmology into the model with the time-varying dimensional constants, including the speed of light, without violating any local law of physics. In the meVSL model, the Rydberg unit varies as
a^{-\frac{b}{2}}
and this variation is absorbed in the determination of the redshift parameter
z
. The cosmic variation of
\alpha^{2}
is proportional to the inverse of the wavelength, and one cannot measure any cosmic time variation from wavelengths generated from
\alpha^{2}
. One can measure the time variation of
\alpha
obtained from a higher order than
\alpha^{2}
, and that might be why one can measure it not from the Akali-doublet method but from the many-multiplet one. We need to use the atomic spectra with large
q_{2}
values with caution in their signs to measure the time-varying fine structure constant.
