Uncertainty quantification for iterative algorithms in linear models with application to early stopping
Pierre C. Bellec, Kai Tan

Abstract
This paper investigates the iterates 𝒃 ^ 1 , … , 𝒃 ^ T {\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{1},\dots,{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{T} obtained from iterative algorithms in high-dimensional linear regression problems, in the regime where the feature dimension p p is comparable with the sample size n n , i.e., p ≍ n p\asymp n . The analysis and proposed estimators are applicable to Gradient Descent (GD), proximal GD and their accelerated variants such as Fast Iterative Soft-Thresholding (FISTA). The paper proposes novel estimators for the generalization error of the iterate 𝒃 ^ t {\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t} for any fixed iteration t t along the trajectory. These estimators are proved to be n \sqrt{n} -consistent under Gaussian designs. Applications to early-stopping are provided: when the generalization error of the iterates is a U-shape function of the iteration t t , the estimates allow to select from the data an iteration t ^ \hat{t} that achieves the smallest generalization error along the trajectory. Additionally, we provide a technique for developing debiasing corrections and valid confidence intervals for the components of the true coefficient vector from the iterate 𝒃 ^ t {\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t} at any finite iteration t t . Extensive simulations on synthetic data illustrate the theoretical results.

1
Introduction

Consider the linear model

(1.1)

\bm{y}=\bm{X}\bm{b}^{*}+{\bm{\varepsilon}},

where
\bm{y}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is the response vector,
\bm{X}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times p}
is the design matrix with i.i.d. rows with
covariance
\bm{\Sigma}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p\times p}
,
and
{\bm{\varepsilon}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is the noise vector with i.i.d. entries from
\mathsf{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
.
To estimate the coefficient vector in the linear model with
dimension
p
larger or comparable to the sample size
n
,
a common strategy is to consider
the penalized least-squares estimator

(1.2)

{\widehat{\bm{b}}}\in\mathop{\rm arg\,min}_{\bm{b}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p}}\frac{1}{2n}\|\bm{y}-\bm{X}\bm{b}\|^{2}+\rho(\bm{b}).

This estimator could be ordinary least-squares estimate (if
\rho(\bm{b})=0
),
Ridge regression, Lasso
(
Tibshirani 1996
)
, Elastic Net
(
Zou and Hastie 2005
)
, group Lasso
(
Yuan and Lin 2006
)
, nuclear norm
penalization
(
Koltchinskii et al. 2011
)
or
Slope
(
Bogdan et al. 2015
)
, to name a few convex examples.
Concave penalty functions
\rho
in (
1.2
) have also been considered,
including
SCAD
(
Fan and Li 2001
)
and MCP
(
Zhang 2010
)
.
The properties of the penalized estimator
{\widehat{\bm{b}}}
in (
1.2
) are now well understood in high-dimensional regimes where the dimension

p
is larger than
n
.
For instance, optimal L1 and L2 bounds of Lasso estimator
{\widehat{\bm{b}}}
have been studied
extensively
(
Wainwright 2009
;
Bickel et al. 2009
;
Ye and Zhang 2010
;
Bayati and Montanari 2012
;
Dalalyan et al. 2017
;
Bellec et al. 2018
, among others)
.
Beyond risk bounds, confidence intervals
for single entries of
\bm{b}^{*}
have been developed in
Zhang and Zhang 2014b
;
Javanmard and Montanari 2014
;
van de Geer et al. 2014a
, and more recently in

Cai and Guo 2017
;
Javanmard and Montanari 2018
;
Bellec and
Zhang 2022b
.
We refer readers to
Bühlmann and Van De Geer 2011
;
van de Geer 2016
for a comprehensive review of such results.
In most results cited above,
p\gg n
and the L2 risk of (
1.2
)
typically converges to 0, i.e.,
{\widehat{\bm{b}}}
is consistent.
Consistency, on the other hand, is not granted in the regime
where dimension and sample size are of the same order, i.e.,

(1.3)

p\asymp n,\qquad\text{ or }\qquad\lim(p/n)\text{ exists and is finite}.

In this regime,
Bayati and Montanari 2012
;
Miolane and Montanari 2021

showed that the Lasso L2 risk,
under suitable assumptions, converges to a finite positive constant
that can be characterized by a system of nonlinear equations.
Regime (
1.3
) will be in force in the present paper.
Since the early works

(
Bayati and Montanari 2012
;
El Karoui et al. 2013
;
Donoho and Montanari 2016
)

on this proportional regime,
significant progress was made
to develop a unified theory explaining the behaviors of M-estimators
and penalized estimators when (
1.3
) holds, see for instance
Thrampoulidis et al. 2018
;
Celentano et al. 2023
;
Loureiro et al. 2021
.
Compared with the early debiasing literature
(
Zhang and Zhang 2014a
;
van de Geer et al. 2014a
;
Javanmard and Montanari 2014
)
,
new debiasing techniques are needed to construct confidence intervals
for components of
\bm{b}^{*}
in this regime, in particular with the need
to account for the degrees-of-freedom of the estimator (
1.2
)
for the debiasing correction

(
Bellec and Zhang 2022a
;
Celentano et al. 2023
;
Bellec and Zhang 2023
)
.

1.1
Iterative algorithms

As we have seen,
the properties of the estimator
{\widehat{\bm{b}}}
in (
1.2
)
are now well understood, both in the classical high-dimensional regime with
p\gg n

and in the proportional regime (
1.3
).
In practice, an exact minimizer
{\widehat{\bm{b}}}
of (
1.2
) is
typically unavailable in closed-form, and numerical approximations are used
instead.
To solve the minimization problem (
1.2
) numerically, the practitioner
resorts to iterative algorithms
developed in the optimization literature
to obtain an approximate solution to (
1.2
).

For smooth objective functions,
this includes first-order methods such as
Gradient Descent (GD),
its accelerated version known as Accelerated Gradient Descent (AGD)
(
Nesterov 1983
)
,
or
its randomized variants
(
Bottou 2010
)
.
For non-smooth objective functions, the practitioner typically
resorts to coordinate descent

(
Friedman et al. 2010
)
or iterative algorithms
involving the proximal operator of
\rho
such as
Iterative Soft-Thresholding Algorithm (ISTA)

(
Daubechies et al. 2004
)
and its accelerated version Fast Iterative Soft-Thresholding Algorithm (FISTA)

(
Beck and Teboulle 2009
)
.
Such iterative algorithm starts with an initializer
{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{1}
and then iteratively produces iterates
{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{2},{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{3},...
by applying a nonlinear
function to a weighted sum of the previous iterate and the gradient of the first term
in (
1.2
) (the smooth part of the objective function) at the previous iterate.
For instance,
for a convex penalty function
\rho
in (
1.2
)
the proximal gradient descent algorithm solves (
1.2
)
with the iterations

(1.4)

{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t+1}=\prox[\tfrac{1}{L}\rho]\bigl({\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t}+\tfrac{1}{nL}\bm{X}^{\top}(\bm{y}-\bm{X}{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t})\bigr){,}

where the proximal operator is defined as
\prox[\frac{1}{L}\rho](\bm{v})=\mathop{\rm arg\,min}_{\bm{b}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p}}\|\bm{b}-\bm{v}\|^{2}/2+\frac{1}{L}\rho(\bm{b})
and
the scalar
L>0
is usually taken
(
Beck and Teboulle 2009
)
as an upper bound
on the Lipschitz constant of the gradient of the first term in the objective
function (
1.2
), i.e., such that
L\geq\|\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{X}/n\|_{\rm op}
.
For the Lasso problem with
\rho(\bm{b})=\lambda\|\bm{b}\|_{1}
in (
1.2
),
the iterations (
1.4
) become the ISTA iterations

(1.5)

{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t+1}=\soft_{\lambda/L}\bigl({\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t}+\tfrac{1}{nL}\bm{X}^{\top}(\bm{y}-\bm{X}{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t})\bigr){,}

where
\soft_{\lambda/L}(\cdot)
applies the soft-thresholding
u\mapsto{\rm sign}(u)(|u|-\lambda/L)_{+}
componentwise.
For AGD and FISTA,
the recursion formula defining
{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t+1}
involves the iterates

({\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t},{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t-1})
as we will detail in

Sections

3.2
and
3.4
below.

More generally, the focus of the present paper is on iterative algorithm of
the form

(1.6)

{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t+1}=\bm{g}_{t+1}({\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t},{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t-1},\bm{v}^{t},\bm{v}^{t-1}),\quad\text{ where}\quad\bm{v}^{t}=\tfrac{1}{n}\bm{X}^{\top}(\bm{y}-\bm{X}{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t})

for nonlinear Lipschitz functions
\bm{g}_{t+1}
, in order to include
accelerated algorithms from the
optimization literature that
are of the form (
1.6
), i.e.,
using the previous two iterations,
including AGD
(
Nesterov 1983
)

and FISTA

(
Beck and Teboulle 2009
)
.
Our theory actually applies to recursions of the form

(1.7)

{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t+1}=\bm{g}_{t+1}({\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t},{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t-1},\dots,{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{2},{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{1},~\bm{v}^{t},\bm{v}^{t-1},\dots,\bm{v}^{2},\bm{v}^{1}),

although the typical optimization algorithms mentioned in the previous
paragraph only use the previous two iterates, as in
(
1.6
).

These iterative algorithms are typically stopped when
a stopping criterion is met.
Existing theories in the optimization literature on iterative algorithms examine their convergence properties regarding the training error, specifically how fast (in
t
) does
the objective function at the iterate
{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t}
approach the minimal
value in (
1.2
), or how fast does
the iterate
{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t}
approach an ideal minimizer

{\widehat{\bm{b}}}
of (
1.2
) as
t\to+\infty
.
For instance,
Beck and Teboulle 2009
showed that FISTA enjoys a faster rate of convergence
compared to ISTA.

1.2
Statistical properties of iterates for small
t
or when convergence fails

If
{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t}
converges to
{\widehat{\bm{b}}}
as
t
increases, it is reasonable to expect that the properties of
{\widehat{\bm{b}}}
(e.g., risk bounds, or debiased confidence intervals for components of
\bm{b}^{*}
) are also valid for
{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t}
provided
t
is sufficiently large.
One motivation of the present paper is the study of iterates
({\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t})_{t\geq 1}
for small values of
t
, or when convergence of these iterates
fail for the optimization problem (
1.2
).
In such cases, because
{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t}
and an ideal solution
{\widehat{\bm{b}}}
to (
1.2
) significantly differ from each other, we should not expect that
the statistical properties of
{\widehat{\bm{b}}}
are inherited by
{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t}
.
This calls for a statistical analysis of
{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t}
itself, rather
than ideal minimizers
{\widehat{\bm{b}}}
of the optimization problem (
1.2
).
Two concrete situations for which convergence fails are the following.

•

The dataset
(\bm{X},\bm{y})
is so large that a single iteration
of the recursion (
1.7
)
is slow and/or computationally costly.
Running the iterations (
1.7
)
until convergence (
t\to+\infty
) is then not realistic.
Still,
in such situation, the practitioner may only run a few iterations and use

{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t}
for a small
t
(e.g.,
t=5
or
t=10
) for statistical purposes,
including making predictions
at new test data points
\bm{x}_{new}
.

•

If the optimization problem (
1.2
) is non-convex,
as is the case with SCAD
(
Fan and Li 2001
)

or MCP
(
Zhang 2010
)
, convergence of the iterates
to a local or global solution to (
1.2
) is a subtle
problem. Iterative algorithms may converge to a local optima.
Specific algorithms have been developed to ensure good
statistical properties under
suitable assumptions, such as Restricted Eigenvalue conditions

(
Zhang 2010
;
Feng and Zhang 2019
)
.
However, such conditions
are often not verifiable in practice
(
Bandeira et al. 2013
)
.
Despite the absence of any convergence guarantees,
the algorithm used to solve (
1.2
) for
SCAD or MCP is still implementable, yielding iterates in the form of
(
1.7
).
Despite the lack of verifiable conditions to guarantee convergence,
at a given
t

the iterate
{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t}
can still be used for downstream tasks,
for instance predictions at test data points
\bm{x}_{new}
.

In both situations, convergence does not occur and statistical
properties of
{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t}
at a finite
t
are expected to significantly departs
from those of
{\widehat{\bm{b}}}
.
Using
{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t}
for statistical purposes, instead of waiting for
convergence, is also appealing in order to save computational resources.
This calls for a statistical theory of
{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t}
itself, rather
than ideal minimizers
{\widehat{\bm{b}}}
of (
1.2
).
The present makes contributions in this direction with two major applications on the
properties of the iterate
{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t}
at a fixed
t
:
assessing the predictive performance of
{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t}
, and constructing debiased confidence
intervals for components of
\bm{b}^{*}
based on
{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t}
.

1.3
Early stopping

It is important to recognize that the predictive performance of
{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t}
does not necessarily improve as
t
grows.
In fact, early-stopped gradient descent has been found to be effective in several machine learning applications, including non-parametric regression in a reproducing kernel Hilbert space
(
Raskutti et al. 2014
)
, boosting
Bühlmann and Yu 2003
;
Rosset et al. 2004

and ridge regression
(
Ali et al. 2019
)
, to name a few.
The intuition is that early stopping provides a form of regularization
that prevents overfitting
(see, e.g.,
Ali et al. 2019
and the references therein).
In these settings, the population risk curve versus the iteration number is U-shaped (first decreasing and then increasing); we will observe instances of this
phenomenon in Figures
1
-
5
.
For practical purposes, the major question in such situations is to
infer from the data if early stopping is beneficial, and when to stop
the iterations. The practitioner typically wishes to stop the iteration as soon
as the population risk starts to increase; this is only possible
if an estimator of the population risk of
{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t}
is available.
Our results below provide such an estimator that can be effectively computed
from the data
(\bm{X},\bm{y})
.

1.4
Contributions

The aforementioned situations motivate studying the properties of the iterates
{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t}
at each step
t
, in particular for smaller values of
t
where
{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t}
is not yet close to the optimizer
{\widehat{\bm{b}}}
.
Our focus centers on two key questions:

(Q1)

Can we quantify the predictive performance of
{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t}
for each
t
along the trajectory?

(Q2)

Can we use the iterates
{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t}
to construct confidence intervals for the entries of
\bm{b}^{*}
,
for instance by deriving asymptotic normality results around
{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t}
?

This paper provides affirmative answers to these questions.
Our contributions are summarized as follows:

i)

We introduce a reliable method for estimating the generalization error of each iterate
{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t}
obtained from widely-used algorithms that solve (
1.2
). This estimator takes the form

\frac{1}{n}\|\sum_{s\leq t}\hat{w}_{s,t}(\bm{y}-\bm{X}{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{s})\|^{2}
,
i.e.,
the mean square error of the weighted average of the residual vector
\{\bm{y}-\bm{X}{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{s}\}_{s\leq t}
with
carefully chosen data-driven weights
\hat{w}_{s,t}
defined in
Theorem

2.1
below.
These weights are algorithm-specific and are computed from the data.
The theory also allows us to derive estimators for the generalization
error of weighted averages of the iterates.
For instance, our approach enables the estimation of the generalization error for the average
\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t}
.
The corresponding theory is presented in
Theorem

2.2
.

ii)

The proposed estimator of the generalization error serves as a practical proxy for minimizing the true generalization error. To identify the iteration index
t
such that
{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t}
has the lowest generalization error, one can choose the
t
that minimizes the estimated generalization error. This approach offers a viable method for pinpointing the optimal stopping point in the algorithm’s trajectory. This application is presented in
Corollary

2.3
.

iii)

We develop a method for constructing valid confidence intervals for the elements of
\bm{b}^{*}
based on a given iteration
t
and
iterate
{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t}
. Consequently, one needs not wait for the convergence of the algorithm to construct valid confidence intervals for
\bm{b}^{*}
. In cases where early-stopping is beneficial for achieving smaller generalization error, confidence intervals based on the early-stopped iterate
{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{\hat{t}}
(where
\hat{t}
is chosen by minimizing the estimated risk) are shorter than the confidence interval based on the fully converged minimizer
{\widehat{\bm{b}}}
.
The theory on asymptotic normality
and confidence intervals
is provided in
Theorem

2.4
.

iv)

The proposed method is applicable to a broad spectrum of regression techniques and algorithms.
We provide detailed expressions of the weights
\hat{w}_{s,t}
for several popular algorithms in
Section

3
.
It offers a practical solution for determining the optimal stopping iteration of the algorithm to enjoy the smallest generalization error as
a function of
t
along the
trajectory.
Extensive simulation studies on synthetic data corroborate our theoretical findings.
These studies demonstrate the efficiency of our proposed risk estimator for
{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t}
, and confirm the validity of the asymptotic normality results for the entries of
\bm{b}^{*}
derived from
{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t}
.
The simulations are presented and discussed in
of
Section

3
.

1.5
Related literature

For regression problems in the proportional regime (
1.3
), the most extensively studied iterative algorithm is Approximate Message Passing (AMP)
(
Donoho et al. 2009
;
Bayati and Montanari 2011
;
Bayati and Montanari 2012
)
. In the current framework, AMP can be formulated as
{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t+1}=\bm{\eta}_{t+1}({\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t}+\sum_{s\leq t}w_{t,s}^{\textsc{amp}}\bm{v}^{s})
, where
w_{t,s}^{\textsc{amp}}
are specific scalar weights. These weights ensure that at each step, the input of
\bm{\eta}_{t+1}
is approximately normally distributed.
This construction enables tracking the evolution of the error of
{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t}
as a function of
t
using a simple recursion, referred to as state evolution.
AMP has found numerous applications, including for instance
in high-dimensional statistics
(
Bayati and Montanari 2012
;
Celentano and Montanari 2022
)
,
spiked models/low-rank matrix denoising

(
Montanari and Venkataramanan 2021
, and references therein)

or sampling

(
El Alaoui et al. 2022
)
.
A proof of the state evolution for non-separable

\bm{\eta}_{t}
is given in
Berthier et al. 2020
.
The focus of the present is to study algorithms from the optimization
literature such as
proximal gradient (
1.4
), AGD or ISTA/FISTA
that do not use the special AMP weights.

Celentano et al. 2020
;
Montanari and Wu 2022
exhibit
fundamental limits for the performance of iterations that encompass (
1.7
). These works show that
iterates can be viewed as a Lipschitz function
g_{*}
of some AMP algorithm. They
characterize the fundamental lower bound obtained by taking
the infimum over all
g_{*}
and corresponding AMP algorithms,
and show that the lower bound is attained by a specific AMP algorithm, termed Bayes-AMP.
Our goal in this paper is different: to develop a data-driven estimate of the error of the iterates
{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t}
.

Still in the proportional regime,

Chandrasekher et al. 2022
;
Chandrasekher et al. 2023
;
Lou et al. 2024

consider iterations that,
in order to compute the
(t+1)
-th iterate,
use a fresh random batch

(\bm{x}_{i}^{t+1},y_{i}^{t+1})_{i\in N^{t+1}}
independent of all the past
(here, the past refers to
{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t},{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t-1},...{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{1}
as well as the random samples used to compute
these).
These works characterize the evolution of the iterates’ performance (measured by mean square error, or inner product with the ground truth) by a sequence of deterministic scalars defined by
simple recursions, and they show that the iterates’ performance
is close to the deterministic scalars.
If samples are reused across iterations, as in the present paper,
it is still possible to characterize deterministic equivalents of the
performance of the iterates in some cases
(see
Celentano et al. 2021
for continuous-time iterates,

Gerbelot et al. 2022
for discrete-time ones).
The deterministic equivalents of the iterates’ performance are necessarily more complex than in the fresh random batch setting. This complexity is captured by limiting Gaussian processes indexed by
t\in[T]

(
Celentano et al. 2021
;
Gerbelot et al. 2022
)
.

For optimization of the least-squares problem, with
\rho(\cdot)=0

in (
1.2
),
Ali et al. 2020
;
Ali et al. 2019
;
Sheng and Ali 2022
(among others)
study equivalences between early stopping and L2 regularization:
stopping the iterations of GD or AGD
at a fixed
t
implicitly performs shrinkage
of a similar nature to Ridge regression.
For gradient descent iterations solving this least-squares problem,

Patil et al. 2024
develops estimates of the generalization error
of the iterates using leave-one-out cross-validation, and show
consistency of these leave-one-out estimates along the GD trajectory.
While leave-one-out estimates are close to the goal of the present paper,
i.e., to develop estimates of the generalization error along the
trajectory of the algorithm, they are
not computationally practical without further modifications since they require
to running
n
algorithms in parallel to obtain the leave-one-out
estimate.

Outside of the proportional regime (
1.3
),

Luo et al. 2023
develop an efficient approximation of leave-one-out
cross validation for
iterates
{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t}
solving empirical risk minimization problems with additive regularization.
The normalized Hessian of the objective function is assumed to be Lipschitz
and non-singular at the iterates, which precludes settings
of interest for the present paper with
p>n

and non-smooth penalty in (
1.2
).
For uncertainty quantification of the iterates,

Hoppe et al. 2023

extends the classical debiased lasso estimator
(
Zhang and Zhang 2014a
;
Javanmard and Montanari 2014
;
van de Geer et al. 2014b
)
to the iterates of a variant of ISTA.
The asymptotic normality in

Hoppe et al. 2023

requires a bound on the size of the supports of

{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t}
and
\bm{b}^{*}
, as well as
\lVert{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t}-\bm{b}^{*}\rVert
to vanish as
n\to\infty
.
This is not the case in the regime (
1.3
) of interest here where
p
and
n
are of the same order.
The present paper departs from this analysis of a variant ISTA
on several fronts. First, our analysis is grounded in a more general iteration formula (see
(
1.6
) and (
1.7
)), which encompasses a broader spectrum of algorithms.
Second, our results establish asymptotic normality of
{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t}
for
any
t
, and do not require
\lVert{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t}-\bm{b}^{*}\rVert
to vanish as
n\to\infty
.
In contrast to these studies, the present paper introduces new estimator
of the generalization error of algorithm iterates that solve the regression problem (
1.2
) for
p>n
and with non-smooth penalty functions.
Additionally, we propose a method to construct confidence intervals for the entries of
\bm{b}^{*}
by debiasing the iterate
{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t}
.

1.6
Notation

Regular variables like
a,b,...
refer to scalars, bold lowercase letters such as
\bm{a},\bm{b},...
represent vectors, and bold uppercase letters like
\bm{A},\bm{B},...
indicate matrices.
For an integer
n\geq 1
, we use the notation

[n]=\{1,\ldots,n\}
.
The vectors
\bm{e}_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n},\bm{e}_{j}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p},\bm{e}_{t}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{T}
denote the canonical basis vector of the corresponding index.
For clarity, we always use implicit index
i
to loop or sum over
[n]
,
index
j
to loop over
[p]
, and
indices
t,t^{\prime},s
to loop over
[T]
.
For a real vector
\bm{a}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p}
,

\left\lVert\bm{a}\right\rVert
denotes its Euclidean norm. For a matrix
\bm{A}
,
\left\lVert\bm{A}\right\rVert_{\rm{F}}
,
\left\lVert\bm{A}\right\rVert_{\rm{op}}
,
\left\lVert\bm{A}\right\rVert_{*}
denote its Frobenius, operator and nuclear norm, respectively.
For a matrix
\bm{A}
, we denote its maximum
and minimum singular values by
\sigma_{\max}(\bm{A})
and
\sigma_{\min}(\bm{A})
, respectively. If
\bm{A}
is symmetric,

\lambda_{\max}(\bm{A})
and
\lambda_{\min}(\bm{A})
are its maximum and minimum eigenvalues.
Let
\bm{A}\otimes\bm{B}
be the Kronecker product of matrices
\bm{A}
and
\bm{B}
.
Let
{\bf 1}_{n}
denote the all-ones vector in

2.1
Iterates and derivatives

Our first task is to establish some notation
(namely, matrices

As a warm-up, we will first introduce this notation for proximal gradient

2.1.1
Proximal gradient descent

Consider the proximal gradient descent iterates
(
1.4
).
With this notation and starting with
{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{1}=\bf 0
, the proximal gradient descent
(
1.4
) iterations can be rewritten as

(2.2)

\displaystyle{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t}=\bm{g}_{t}({\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t-1},\bm{v}^{t-1}),\quad\text{ where }\bm{g}_{t}(\bm{b}^{t},\bm{v}^{t})=\prox[\tfrac{1}{L}\rho](\bm{b}^{t}+\tfrac{1}{L}\bm{v}^{t}).

The Jacobian of the function
\bm{g}_{t}
is a matrix of size

p\times 2p
and can be partitioned into the two
p\times p
blocks

(2.3)

\bm{J}_{t,t-1}=\frac{\partial\bm{g}_{t}}{\partial{\bm{b}^{t-1}}}({\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t-1},\bm{v}^{t-1}),\quad\bm{D}_{t,t-1}=\frac{\partial\bm{g}_{t}}{\partial{\bm{v}^{t-1}}}({\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t-1},\bm{v}^{t-1}).

Next, define
{\mathcal{J}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{pT\times pT}
and
{\mathcal{D}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{pT\times pT}

with
T\times T
blocks of size
p\times p
as follows

(2.4)

{\mathcal{J}}=\begin{bmatrix}{\mathbf{0}}&{\mathbf{0}}&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&{\mathbf{0}}\\
\bm{J}_{2,1}&{\mathbf{0}}&\ddots&\cdots&\cdots&\vdots\\
{\mathbf{0}}&\bm{J}_{3,2}&{\mathbf{0}}&\ddots&\cdots&\vdots\\
{\mathbf{0}}&{\mathbf{0}}&\bm{J}_{4,3}&{\mathbf{0}}&\ddots&\vdots\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&{\mathbf{0}}\\
{\mathbf{0}}&\cdots&{\mathbf{0}}&{\mathbf{0}}&\bm{J}_{T,T-1}&{\mathbf{0}}\\
\end{bmatrix},~{\mathcal{D}}=\begin{bmatrix}{\mathbf{0}}&{\mathbf{0}}&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&{\mathbf{0}}\\
\bm{D}_{2,1}&{\mathbf{0}}&\ddots&\cdots&\cdots&\vdots\\
{\mathbf{0}}&\bm{D}_{3,2}&{\mathbf{0}}&\ddots&\cdots&\vdots\\
{\mathbf{0}}&{\mathbf{0}}&\bm{D}_{4,3}&{\mathbf{0}}&\ddots&\vdots\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&{\mathbf{0}}\\
{\mathbf{0}}&\cdots&{\mathbf{0}}&{\mathbf{0}}&\bm{D}_{T,T-1}&{\mathbf{0}}\end{bmatrix}.

Each
\mathbf{0}
block in the above

is the zero matrix of

p\times p

For iterations depending not only on
{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t-1},\bm{v}^{t-1}

also on previous iterates, the lower triangular blocks
of

will be filled with the corresponding non-zero

2.1.2
Combining two previous iterates

To incorporate more general algorithms beyond proximal gradient descent, we consider the iteration formula:

(2.5)

\displaystyle{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t}=\bm{g}_{t}({\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t-1},{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t-2},\bm{v}^{t-1},\bm{v}^{t-2})\qquad\text{ for }t\geq 2.

Here,
{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{1}
serves as an initial vector, and we set
{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{0}=\bm{v}^{0}={\mathbf{0}}_{p}
.
The iteration functions
\bm{g}_{t}(\cdot):{\mathbb{R}}^{4p}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{p}
are determined by user-specified algorithms.
We include
{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t-2}
and
\bm{v}^{t-2}
into the argument of (
2.5
) to encompass optimization algorithms that update iterates by considering information from the two preceding steps.
This general formulation accommodates popular algorithms such as Nesterov’s accelerated gradient (AGD) method
(
Nesterov 1983
;
Nesterov 2003
)
and the Fast Iterative Shrinkage-Thresholding Algorithm (FISTA)
(
Beck and Teboulle 2009
)
.
We provide detailed expressions of
\bm{g}_{t}
for several important algorithms in
Section

3
.

For algorithms with iteration function
\bm{g}_{t}
in (
2.5
)
depending on the previous two iterates,
the Jacobian of
\bm{g}_{t}
at a point where

\bm{g}_{t}
is differentiable is a matrix of size
{\mathbb{R}}^{p\times 4p}
.
This Jacobian matrix in
{\mathbb{R}}^{p\times 4p}
is naturally partitioned
into the four
p\times p
blocks:

(2.6)

\displaystyle\bm{J}_{t,t-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\bm{g}_{t}}{\partial{\bm{b}^{t-1}}}({\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t-1},{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t-2},\bm{v}^{t-1},\bm{v}^{t-2}),\quad\bm{D}_{t,t-1}=\frac{\partial\bm{g}_{t}}{\partial{\bm{v}^{t-1}}}({\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t-1},{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t-2},\bm{v}^{t-1},\bm{v}^{t-2}),

\displaystyle\bm{J}_{t,t-2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\bm{g}_{t}}{\partial{\bm{b}^{t-2}}}({\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t-1},{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t-2},\bm{v}^{t-1},\bm{v}^{t-2}),\quad\bm{D}_{t,t-2}=\frac{\partial\bm{g}_{t}}{\partial{\bm{v}^{t-2}}}({\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t-1},{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t-2},\bm{v}^{t-1},\bm{v}^{t-2}).

We further define larger matrices
{\mathcal{J}},{\mathcal{D}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{pT\times pT}
by setting the
(t,t^{\prime})
block of
{\mathcal{J}}
as
\bm{J}_{t,t^{\prime}}
and that of
{\mathcal{D}}
as
\bm{D}_{t,t^{\prime}}
for all
t,t^{\prime}\in[T]
for all
t,t^{\prime}
corresponding to matrices
in (
2.6
),
with zeros everywhere else:

(2.7)

{\mathcal{J}}=\begin{bmatrix}{\mathbf{0}}&{\mathbf{0}}&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&{\mathbf{0}}\\
\bm{J}_{2,1}&{\mathbf{0}}&\ddots&\cdots&\cdots&\vdots\\
\bm{J}_{3,1}&\bm{J}_{3,2}&{\mathbf{0}}&\ddots&\cdots&\vdots\\
{\mathbf{0}}&\bm{J}_{4,2}&\bm{J}_{4,3}&{\mathbf{0}}&\ddots&\vdots\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&{\mathbf{0}}\\
{\mathbf{0}}&\cdots&{\mathbf{0}}&\bm{J}_{T,T-2}&\bm{J}_{T,T-1}&{\mathbf{0}}\\
\end{bmatrix},~{\mathcal{D}}=\begin{bmatrix}{\mathbf{0}}&{\mathbf{0}}&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&{\mathbf{0}}\\
\bm{D}_{2,1}&{\mathbf{0}}&\ddots&\cdots&\cdots&\vdots\\
\bm{D}_{3,1}&\bm{D}_{3,2}&{\mathbf{0}}&\ddots&\cdots&\vdots\\
{\mathbf{0}}&\bm{D}_{4,2}&\bm{D}_{4,3}&{\mathbf{0}}&\ddots&\vdots\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&{\mathbf{0}}\\
{\mathbf{0}}&\cdots&{\mathbf{0}}&\bm{D}_{T,T-2}&\bm{D}_{T,T-1}&{\mathbf{0}}\end{bmatrix}.

An equivalent definition
of
{\mathcal{J}},{\mathcal{D}}
using Kronecker products is

{\mathcal{J}}=\sum_{t=2}^{T}\sum_{s=t-2}^{t-1}(\bm{e}_{t}\bm{e}_{s}^{\top})\otimes\bm{J}_{t,s},\qquad{\mathcal{D}}=\sum_{t=2}^{T}\sum_{s=t-2}^{t-1}(\bm{e}_{t}\bm{e}_{s}^{\top})\otimes\bm{D}_{t,s},

where
\bm{e}_{t},\bm{e}_{s}
are the
t
-th
and
s
-th canonical basis vectors
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{T}
.

2.1.3
General form: combining all previous iterates

More generally, our theory applies to iterations that depend on
all previous iterates, i.e.,
iterates of the form

(2.8)

{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t}=\bm{g}_{t}({\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t-1},{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t-2},\dots,{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{2},{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{1},~\bm{v}^{t-1},,\dots,\bm{v}^{2},\bm{v}^{1})

with
\bm{v}^{s}={\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{s}=\mathbf{0}_{p}
for all
s\leq 0
by convention.
With the more general iterations (
2.8
)
depending on all previous iterates, the Jacobian of
\bm{g}_{t}
in
(
2.8
) is now a matrix in
{\mathbb{R}}^{p\times 2p(t-1)}

partitioned into blocks

(2.9)

\bm{J}_{t,s}=\frac{\partial\bm{g}_{t}}{\partial\bm{b}^{s}}\Bigl({\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t-1},...,{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{1},\bm{v}^{t-1},...,\bm{v}^{1}\Bigr),\quad\bm{D}_{t,s}=\frac{\partial\bm{g}_{t}}{\partial\bm{v}^{s}}\Bigl({\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t-1},...,{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{1},\bm{v}^{t-1},...,\bm{v}^{1}\Bigr)

for all
s=1,...,t-1
.
The large matrices
{\mathcal{J}},{\mathcal{D}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{pT\times pT}
are then defined as

(2.10)

{\mathcal{J}}=\sum_{t=2}^{T}\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}(\bm{e}_{t}\bm{e}_{s}^{\top})\otimes\bm{J}_{t,s},\qquad{\mathcal{D}}=\sum_{t=2}^{T}\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}(\bm{e}_{t}\bm{e}_{s}^{\top})\otimes\bm{D}_{t,s}.

This generalizes (
2.7
)
when
{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t}
depends on all previous
iterates.
The matrices
{\mathcal{J}}
and
{\mathcal{D}}

are always lower triangular block matrices, as in (
2.7
).

2.2
Lipschitz assumption and the chain rule

Throughout, we assume the following.

Assumption 2.1
.

The algorithm starts with
{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{1}={\mathbf{0}}
.
The iteration function
\bm{g}_{t}
in (
2.8
)
is
\zeta
-Lipschitz and satisfies

\bm{g}_{t}({\mathbf{0}})={\mathbf{0}}
.

The notation in (
2.6
) and (
2.9
) naturally arises from the application of the chain rule in the recursive computation of derivatives for the iterates
(
2.5
)
and
(
2.8
).
While assuming that
\bm{g}_{t}
is Lipschitz (
2.1
) implies
that the iterates are differentiable almost everywhere
with respect to
(\bm{X},\bm{y})
by Rademacher’s theorem
(a locally Lipschitz function of
(\bm{y},\bm{X})
is differentiable
almost everywhere), the chain rule may fail for composition
of multivariate Lipschitz functions.
This technical issue is clearly apparent
with the example

(2.11)

\phi^{*}(u,v)=(u,u),\quad\phi^{**}(u,v)=\max(u,{v}),\quad\phi^{**}\circ\phi^{*}(u,v)=u.

Although
\frac{\partial}{\partial u}\bigl(\phi^{**}\circ\phi^{*}\bigr)(u,v)=1

clearly holds,
the chain rule is undefined because

\phi^{**}
is not differentiable
at
\phi^{*}(u,v)
for all
(u,v)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
.
A fix to this issue for the composition of locally Lipschitz functions
is given in Corollary 3.2 of

Ambrosio and Dal Maso 1990
: the chain rule for
for the composition of two Lipschitz functions
g:{\mathbb{R}}^{k}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{q},u:{\mathbb{R}}^{q}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{p}

holds almost surely in the modified
form
\nabla(g\circ u)(x)=\nabla(g|_{T_{x}^{u}})\nabla u(x)

where
g|_{T_{x}^{u}}
is the restriction of
g
to the affine space defined
in
(
Ambrosio and Dal Maso 1990
, Corollary 3.2)
.
Thus, in order to leverage the chain rule in our proofs, we will assume that
the matrices in (
2.6
) or (
2.9
) are all
modified, if necessary, in order to grant the chain rule as given in

(
Ambrosio and Dal Maso 1990
, Corollary 3.2)
.
In many practical examples,
the Lipschitz (but not-necessarily differentiable everywhere)
nonlinear function used to recursively obtain the iterates
is separable, i.e.,
\bm{g}^{sep}:{\mathbb{R}}^{p}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{p}
of the form

\bm{g}^{sep}(\bm{x})_{j}=g_{j}(x_{j})\quad\text{ for all }\bm{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p},j\in[p]\quad\text{for some functions }g_{j}:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}.

This is the case for the soft-thresholding in (
1.5
)
as well as its variants, FISTA and LQA, detailed in

Sections

3.4
and
3.5
. In this case
by
(
Ziemer 1989
, Theorem 2.1.11)
, the chain rule holds
almost everywhere for the composition of Lipschitz functions
of the form
g_{j}\circ h
for
h:{\mathbb{R}}^{k}\to{\mathbb{R}}
and
g_{j}:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
.
Consequently, the usual (unmodified) chain rule is granted almost everywhere
for compositions of Lipschitz functions
that are either separable or
everywhere differentiable.

2.3
Memory matrix

Equipped with the above notation in (
2.9
) and (
2.10
), we now introduce the memory matrix. The memory matrix
is a key ingredient in the construction of our estimator of the prediction
error of the iterate
{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t}
obtained from the iterations
(
2.5
) or (
2.8
).

Definition 2.1
.

For iterates
\{{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{1},{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{2},...,{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{T}\}
obtained from the iteration (
2.8
),
with
{\mathcal{D}},{\mathcal{J}}
defined in
(
2.10
),
define the memory matrix
\smash{\widehat{\mathbf{A}}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{T\times T}
as

(2.12)

\smash{\widehat{\mathbf{A}}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Bigl(\bm{I}_{T}\otimes(\bm{e}_{i}^{\top}\bm{X})\Bigr)\Bigl(\bm{I}_{pT}+{\mathcal{D}}(\bm{I}_{T}\otimes\tfrac{\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{X}}{n})-{\mathcal{J}}\Bigr)^{-1}{\mathcal{D}}\Bigl(\bm{I}_{T}\otimes(\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{e}_{i})\Bigr)

where
\bm{e}_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is the
i
-th
canonical basis vector.

By definition, the memory matrix
\smash{\widehat{\mathbf{A}}}
only depends on the data
(\bm{X},\bm{y})
, the iterates
{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t}
, the vectors
\bm{v}^{t}
in
(
2.1
), and the derivative matrices in (
2.9
)
evaluated at
({\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t-1},...,{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{1},\bm{v}^{t-1},...,\bm{v}^{1})
.
The specific computation of
\smash{\widehat{\mathbf{A}}}
thus depends on the iteration function
\bm{g}_{t}
through the derivative matrices in (
2.9
)
that appear as blocks of
{\mathcal{D}}
and
{\mathcal{J}}
.
The matrix
\smash{\widehat{\mathbf{A}}}
will differ significantly for different choices
of nonlinear functions
\bm{g}_{t}
.
For the same nonlinear functions
\bm{g}_{t}
, the matrix
\smash{\widehat{\mathbf{A}}}

will also be different for different realizations of the data
(\bm{X},\bm{y})
,
although we observe in practice that the entries of
\smash{\widehat{\mathbf{A}}}
concentrate
and have small variance with respect to the randomness of
(\bm{X},\bm{y})
.

In
Section

3
, we provide detailed expressions of
{\mathcal{J}}
and
{\mathcal{D}}
for several important algorithms in the optimization literature.
While definition (
2.12
) involves the inverse of a matrix
of size
{\mathbb{R}}^{pT\times pT}
, due to its specific structure,
computation of
\smash{\widehat{\mathbf{A}}}
requires
much fewer resources that performing a matrix inversion
or solving a linear system in
{\mathbb{R}}^{pT\times pT}
.
We provide efficient methods to compute
\smash{\widehat{\mathbf{A}}}
row by row in
Section

4
.

An equivalent definition of the matrix
\smash{\widehat{\mathbf{A}}}
is to consider the matrix

(2.13)

\frac{1}{n}\Bigl(\bm{I}_{T}\otimes\bm{X}\Bigr)\Bigl(\bm{I}_{pT}+{\mathcal{D}}(\bm{I}_{T}\otimes\tfrac{\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{X}}{n})-{\mathcal{J}}\Bigr)^{-1}{\mathcal{D}}\Bigl(\bm{I}_{T}\otimes(\bm{X}^{\top})\Bigr)

in
{\mathbb{R}}^{nT\times nT}
as
a matrix by block, with
T\times T

blocks of size
n\times n
. The matrix
\smash{\widehat{\mathbf{A}}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{T\times T}
is then
obtained by taking the trace of each
n\times n
block.

Since
{\mathcal{D}}
and
{\mathcal{J}}
are lower triangular matrices with diagonal
blocks equal to
\mathbf{0}_{p\times p}
, both

\bm{I}_{pT}+{\mathcal{D}}(\bm{I}_{T}\otimes\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{X}/n)-{\mathcal{J}}

and its inverse are lower triangular with diagonal blocks equal to
\bm{I}_{p}
.
Consequently, (
2.13
) is block lower triangular with
diagonal blocks equal to
\mathbf{0}_{n\times n}
. By taking the trace
of each
n\times n
block in (
2.13
), we see that

\smash{\widehat{\mathbf{A}}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{T\times T}
is lower triangular with
zero diagonal entries.
It thus always holds that the matrix
\bm{I}_{T}-\smash{\widehat{\mathbf{A}}}/n
is a lower triangular with all diagonal entries equal to 1, invertible, with

(\bm{I}_{T}-\smash{\widehat{\mathbf{A}}}/n)^{-1}
also lower triangular with all diagonal entries equal to 1.
The matrix

(\bm{I}_{T}-\smash{\widehat{\mathbf{A}}}/n)^{-1}

plays a critical role in our
estimator of the generalization error of each iteration
{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t}
in

Theorems

2.1
and
2.2
below.

The noise

is independent of

and

.

Assumption 2.4
.

The sample size

for a constant
\gamma\in(0,\infty)
.

Recall that
\zeta
is the Lipschitz constant in
2.1

In the results below,

2.5
Estimation of the generalization error of iterates

We are interested in quantifying the performance of each iterate
{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t}
.
We assess the performance of the iterate
{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t}
using the out-of-sample prediction error (also referred to as prediction risk or generalization error)

(2.14)

r_{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny def}}}{{=}}{\mathbb{E}}\Bigl[\bigl(y_{new}-\bm{x}_{new}^{\top}{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t}\bigr)^{2}\mid(\bm{X},\bm{y})\Bigr]=\|\bm{\Sigma}^{1/2}({\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t}-\bm{b}^{*})\|^{2}+\sigma^{2},

where
(\bm{x}_{new},y_{new})
is a new test sample that has the same distribution as
(\bm{x}_{1},y_{1})
, the first row of
(\bm{X},\bm{y})
.
Since
{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t}
is measurable with respect to
(\bm{X},\bm{y})
, the last equality
above follows from
2.2
and
2.3
.
Note that
(\bm{x}_{new},y_{new})
is only used as a mathematical
device to define
r_{t}
in (
2.14
); we do not assume that independent
test samples are available.
Our first result concerns the estimation of prediction risk
r_{t}
for each iterate
{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t}
.

Theorem 2.1

(Estimation of prediction risk)
.

Let
2.2
,
2.3
,
2.4
and
2.1
be fulfilled.
For each
t\in[T]
,
define the estimate
\hat{r}_{t}
of
r_{t}
by

(2.15)

\hat{r}_{t}=\frac{1}{n}\Big\|\sum_{s=1}^{t}\hat{w}_{t,s}\bigl(\bm{y}-\bm{X}{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{s}\bigr)\Big\|^{2},

where
\hat{w}_{t,s}=\bm{e}_{t}^{\top}(\bm{I}_{T}-\smash{\widehat{\mathbf{A}}}/n)^{-1}\bm{e}_{s}
.
We have for any
t\in[T]
,

(2.16)

{\mathbb{E}}\big|\hat{r}_{t}-r_{t}\big|\leq n^{-\frac{1}{2}}{\rm var}(y_{1})C(\zeta,T,\gamma,\kappa).

Here

{\rm var}(y_{1})=\lVert\bm{\Sigma}^{1/2}\bm{b}^{*}\rVert^{2}+\sigma^{2}
, and

C(\zeta,T,\gamma,\kappa)
is a constant depending only on

\zeta,T,\gamma,\kappa
.

Theorem

2.1
establishes that
\hat{r}_{t}
is
\sqrt{n}
-consistent when

{\rm var}(y_{1}),\zeta,T,\gamma,\kappa
are constant as
n,p\to+\infty
.
The risk estimate
\hat{r}_{t}
for
{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t}
is determined by both the current residual vector
\bm{y}-\bm{X}{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t}
and all preceding residual vectors
\{\bm{y}-\bm{X}{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{s}\}_{s=1,...,t-1}
through the weighted sum
inside the mean squared norm in (
2.15
).
These weights

(\hat{w}_{t,1},\ldots,\hat{w}_{t,t})
are the first
t
entries of the
t
-th row of the matrix
(\bm{I}_{T}-\smash{\widehat{\mathbf{A}}}/n)^{-1}
.
The proof of
Theorem

2.1
is given in
.

By construction of the matrices
\smash{\widehat{\mathbf{A}}}

and
(\bm{I}_{T}-\smash{\widehat{\mathbf{A}}}/n)^{-1}
,
the definition of these weights are not influenced by the total number of iterations
T
(e.g., the weights
(\hat{w}_{s,t})_{s\leq t}
for any
t\leq T

are the same if the total number of iterations is
T
or any
T^{\prime}>T
).
In other words, opting for a larger
T
simply enlarges the dimensions of the matrix
\smash{\widehat{\mathbf{A}}}
and the inverse matrix
(\bm{I}_{T}-\smash{\widehat{\mathbf{A}}}/n)^{-1}
, yet the first
t
entries in the
t
-th row of
(\bm{I}_{T}-\smash{\widehat{\mathbf{A}}}/n)^{-1}
remain unchanged.
The necessity of a total number of iteration,
T
, is arguably
artificial; an equivalent presentation would increase the size
of
\smash{\widehat{\mathbf{A}}}
and
(\bm{I}_{T}-\smash{\widehat{\mathbf{A}}}/n)^{-1}
by one row and one column
at every new iteration.
We opted for a fixed total number of iterations
T
with
matrices
\smash{\widehat{\mathbf{A}}}
and
(\bm{I}_{T}-\smash{\widehat{\mathbf{A}}}/n)^{-1}

2.8

This subsection focuses on asymptotic normality and

using iterate

The next theorem shows that the following debiased estimate,

{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t,\rm{debias}}_{j}:=\underbrace{{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t}_{j}}_{\text{iterate}}~+~\underbrace{n^{-1}\sum_{s=1}^{t}\hat{w}_{t,s}\bigl(\bm{y}-\bm{X}{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{s}\bigr)^{\top}\bm{X}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{e}_{j}}_{\text{bias correction}},

(

The following result is understood asymptotically as

.
Implicitly, we assume that a sequence of regression problems
(
1.1

in (

serves as the index of the sequence,

and may change values as

increases, as long as

2.1

and

Let

,
2.1

2.3
and

be fulfilled and assume that both

Asymptotic normality “on average” over coordinates,

While the estimates

3.1
Gradient descent

Consider estimating
\bm{b}^{*}
by minimizing the squared loss function

\bm{f}(\bm{b})=\frac{1}{2n}\|\bm{y}-\bm{X}\bm{b}\|^{2}.

The gradient descent (GD) method finds the minimizer of
\bm{f}(\bm{b})
by iterations

{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t}={\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t-1}-\eta\nabla\bm{f}({\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t-1})={\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t-1}+\frac{\eta}{n}\bm{X}^{\top}(\bm{y}-\bm{X}{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t-1}),

with an initialization
{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{1}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p}
.
Since the function

\bm{f}
is an
L
-smooth with
L=\lVert\bm{X}\rVert_{\rm{op}}^{2}/n
, one can take fixed step size
\eta=\frac{1}{L}
.
Using the definition
\bm{v}^{t}=\frac{1}{n}\bm{X}^{\top}(\bm{y}-\bm{X}{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t})
, the iteration of GD can be written as

(3.1)

{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t}=\bm{g}_{t}({\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t-1},\bm{v}^{t-1})={\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t-1}+\eta\bm{v}^{t-1}\text{ for }t\geq 2.

Therefore, the function
\bm{g}_{t}
is Lipschitz continuous, and the matrices in (
2.3
) are given by

(3.2)

\bm{J}_{t,t-1}=\bm{I}_{p},\quad\bm{D}_{t,t-1}=\eta\bm{I}_{p}.

Hence, for GD with iteration (
3.1
), the expressions of
{\mathcal{J}}
and
{\mathcal{D}}
in (
2.7
) become

{\mathcal{J}}=\begin{bmatrix}{\mathbf{0}}&{\mathbf{0}}&\cdots&\cdots&{\mathbf{0}}\\
\bm{I}_{p}&{\mathbf{0}}&\ddots&&\vdots\\
{\mathbf{0}}&\bm{I}_{p}&{\mathbf{0}}&\ddots&\vdots\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&{\mathbf{0}}\\
{\mathbf{0}}&\cdots&{\mathbf{0}}&\bm{I}_{p}&{\mathbf{0}}\\
\end{bmatrix}=\bm{L}\otimes\bm{I}_{p},\qquad{\mathcal{D}}=\eta{\mathcal{J}}=\eta(\bm{L}\otimes\bm{I}_{p}),

where
\bm{L}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{T\times T}
is the strictly lower triangular matrix

\bm{L}=\sum_{t=2}^{T}\bm{e}_{t}\bm{e}_{t-1}^{\top}
.
Now we proceed to derive the expression for each entry of
\smash{\widehat{\mathbf{A}}}
in (
2.12
) for GD.
First, note that
{\mathcal{D}}=\eta{\mathcal{J}}
, so that

\displaystyle{\mathcal{J}}-{\mathcal{D}}(\bm{I}_{T}\otimes\tfrac{\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{X}}{n})=\bm{L}\otimes(\bm{I}_{p}-\eta\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{X}/n).

Since
\bm{L}
is strictly lower triangular,
the above display is also lower triangular.
With
\bm{\Gamma}=\bm{I}_{p}-\tfrac{\eta\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{X}}{n}
,
we have

\displaystyle\Bigl(\bm{I}_{pT}+{\mathcal{D}}(\bm{I}_{T}\otimes\tfrac{\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{X}}{n})-{\mathcal{J}}\Bigr)^{-1}=\Bigl(\bm{I}_{pT}-(\bm{L}\otimes\bm{\Gamma})\Bigr)^{-1}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\bigl(\bm{L}\otimes\bm{\Gamma}\bigr)^{k}=\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}\bm{L}^{k}\otimes\bm{\Gamma}^{k}

thanks to
(\bm{L}\otimes\bm{\Gamma})^{k}=\bm{L}^{k}\otimes\bm{\Gamma}^{k}
by the mixed-product
property and since
\bm{L}^{k}={\mathbf{0}}
for
k\geq T
.
In this case, by the definition (
2.12
) of
\smash{\widehat{\mathbf{A}}}
, we get

\displaystyle\smash{\widehat{\mathbf{A}}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}\bm{L}^{k+1}\trace\Bigl[\frac{\eta}{n}\bm{X}\bm{\Gamma}^{k}\bm{X}^{\top}\Bigr]=\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}\bm{L}^{k+1}\trace\Bigl[(\bm{I}_{p}-\bm{\Gamma})\bm{\Gamma}^{k}\Bigr]=\sum_{k=0}^{T-2}\bm{L}^{k+1}\trace\Bigl[(\bm{I}_{p}-\bm{\Gamma})\bm{\Gamma}^{k}\Bigr].

More visually, since each incremental power of
\bm{L}
moves
the diagonal one step towards the bottom left, we have

\displaystyle\smash{\widehat{\mathbf{A}}}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&0&0&0&\\
\trace(\bm{I}_{p}-\bm{\Gamma})&0&0&0&0&\\
\trace((\bm{I}_{p}-\bm{\Gamma})\bm{\Gamma})&\trace(\bm{I}_{p}-\bm{\Gamma})&0&0&0&\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&0&\\
\trace((\bm{I}_{p}-\bm{\Gamma})\bm{\Gamma}^{T-2})&\ldots&\trace((\bm{I}_{p}-\bm{\Gamma})\bm{\Gamma})&\trace(\bm{I}_{p}-\bm{\Gamma})&0&\end{bmatrix}.

The above simplifications are specific to GD and will typically not be
possible for examples involving nonlinear transformations such as
soft-thresholding.
Computation of
\smash{\widehat{\mathbf{A}}}
is straightforward once the eigenvalues of
\bm{\Gamma}
is computed.

Remark 3.1
.

A closely related estimator to the GD iterates (
3.1
) is the minimum
\ell_{2}
norm least-squares estimator, is defined as

(3.3)

\displaystyle{\widetilde{\bm{b}}}=\mathop{\rm arg\,min}\big\{\|\bm{b}\|:\bm{b}\text{ minimizes }\|\bm{y}-\bm{X}\bm{b}\|^{2}\big\}.

For
n>p
,

, and for

)

3.2
Nesterov’s accelerated gradient descent (AGD)

The Nesterov’s accelerated gradient method
(
Nesterov 1983
)
is a remarkable extension of the gradient descent by utilizing the momentum from previous iterates.
It is well known that AGD enjoys quadratic convergence rates, which is faster than the linear convergence rate of the gradient descent.
To describe the iteration of AGD, define the sequence of scalars

(3.4)

a_{0}=0,\quad a_{t}=\tfrac{1+\sqrt{1+4a_{t-1}^{2}}}{2},\quad w_{t}=\tfrac{1-a_{t}}{a_{t+1}}\quad\text{ for all }t\geq 1.

From some initialization
{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{1}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p}
,
AGD iterates are defined as the weighted sum

(3.5)

\displaystyle{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t}=(1-w_{t-1})({\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t-1}+\eta\bm{v}^{t-1})+w_{t-1}({\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t-2}+\eta\bm{v}^{t-2}),\qquad\text{ for all }t\geq 2.

Hence, the Jacobian matrices defined in (
2.6
) are given by

\displaystyle\bm{J}_{t,t-1}

\displaystyle=(1-w_{t-1})\bm{I}_{p},\quad

\displaystyle\bm{D}_{t,t-1}

\displaystyle=\eta(1-w_{t-1})\bm{I}_{p},

\displaystyle\bm{J}_{t,t-2}

\displaystyle=w_{t-1}\bm{I}_{p},\quad

\displaystyle\bm{D}_{t,t-2}

\displaystyle=\eta w_{t-1}\bm{I}_{p}.

It follows that the expressions of
{\mathcal{J}}
and
{\mathcal{D}}
in (
2.7
) become

{\mathcal{J}}=\begin{bmatrix}{\mathbf{0}}&{\mathbf{0}}&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&{\mathbf{0}}\\
(1-w_{1})\bm{I}_{p}&{\mathbf{0}}&\ddots&\cdots&\cdots&\vdots\\
w_{2}\bm{I}_{p}&(1-w_{2})\bm{I}_{p}&{\mathbf{0}}&\ddots&\cdots&\vdots\\
{\mathbf{0}}&w_{3}\bm{I}_{p}&(1-w_{3})\bm{I}_{p}&{\mathbf{0}}&\ddots&\vdots\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&{\mathbf{0}}\\
{\mathbf{0}}&\cdots&{\mathbf{0}}&w_{T-1}\bm{I}_{p}&(1-w_{T-1})\bm{I}_{p}&{\mathbf{0}}\end{bmatrix},\quad{\mathcal{D}}=\eta{\mathcal{J}}.

Remark 3.2
.

By Proposition 1 in
Hastie et al. 2022
, the
AGD iterate
{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t}
in (
3.5
) also converges to the min-norm least-squares estimator
{\widetilde{\bm{b}}}
as
t\to\infty
.

3.3
Iterative Shrinkage-Thresholding Algorithm (ISTA)

For regression problems with high-dimensional features, penalized regression are useful to achieve sparse solutions.
Consider the Lasso regression

(3.6)

{\widehat{\bm{b}}}\in\mathop{\rm arg\,min}_{\bm{b}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p}}\frac{1}{2n}\|\bm{y}-\bm{X}\bm{b}\|^{2}+\lambda\|\bm{b}\|_{1}.

This objective function of the above optimization has two parts, one is the squared loss, the other is an
\ell_{1}
penalty.
ISTA
(
Daubechies et al. 2004
)
is a simple algorithm to solve (
3.6
) by imposing a soft-thresholding nonlinearity at each iteration.
Concretely,
let
\soft_{\theta}:{\mathbb{R}}^{p}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{p}
denote the elementwise soft-thresholding operator, i.e.
\soft_{\theta}(\bm{b})_{j}=(|\bm{b}_{j}-\theta|)_{+}\text{sgn}(\bm{b}_{j}).

ISTA can be viewed as the proximal gradient descent in
Section

2.1.1
, and its iteration function
\bm{g}_{t}
is given by

(3.7)

{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t}=\bm{g}_{t}({\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t-1},\bm{v}^{t-1})=\soft_{\lambda/L}({\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t-1}+L^{-1}\bm{v}^{t-1}).

The function
\bm{g}_{t}
is Lipschitz continuous since the soft-thresholding
is 1-Lipschitz.
Let
S^{t}=\big\{j\in[p]:|{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t}_{j}+L^{-1}\bm{v}^{t}_{j}|>\lambda/L\big\}
.
For ISTA, the expressions of
\bm{J}_{t,t-1}
and
\bm{D}_{t,t-1}
in (
2.3
) are the diagonal matrices

\displaystyle\bm{J}_{t,t-1}=\sum_{j\in S^{t-1}}\bm{e}_{j}\bm{e}_{j}^{\top}=\text{diag}\Bigl(\bigl(\mathbb{I}\{j\in S^{t-1}\}\bigr)_{j\in[p]}\Bigr),\qquad\bm{D}_{t,t-1}=L^{-1}\bm{J}_{t,t-1}.

Substituting the above into (
2.4
) gives the expressions of
{\mathcal{J}}
and
{\mathcal{D}}
for ISTA.

Simulation results.

3.6
) with two
regularization parameters
\lambda\in\{0.01,0.1\}

The ISTA iterates converge to the Lasso estimator (

)
(see, e.g.,

).

sklearn.linear_model.Lasso
and

r_{\infty}
.

) for ISTA.

3.4
Fast Iterative Shrinkage-Thresholding Algorithm (FISTA)

Similar to the extension from GD to AGD,
FISTA
(
Beck and Teboulle 2009
)
is an accelerated version of ISTA,
incorporating momentum with the weights (
3.4
).
One advantage of FISTA is that it enjoys faster convergence rate than ISTA
(
Beck and Teboulle 2009
)
.
Using the same definitions of
a_{t},w_{t}
in (
3.4
), FISTA iterates the following steps with some initialization
{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{1}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p}
:

\displaystyle{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t}

\displaystyle=\bm{g}_{t}({\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t-1},{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t-2},\bm{v}^{t-1},\bm{v}^{t-2})

\displaystyle=(1-w_{t-1})\soft_{\lambda/L}({\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t-1}+L^{-1}\bm{v}^{t-1})+w_{t-1}\soft_{\lambda/L}({\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t-2}+L^{-1}\bm{v}^{t-2})\text{ for }t\geq 2.

Using
S^{t}=\big\{j\in[p]:|{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t}_{j}+L^{-1}\bm{v}^{t}_{j}|>\lambda/L\big\}
,
the matrices in (
2.6
) are the diagonal matrices

\displaystyle\bm{J}_{t,t-1}

\displaystyle=(1-w_{t-1})\sum_{j\in S^{t-1}}\bm{e}_{j}\bm{e}_{j}^{\top},\quad

\displaystyle\bm{D}_{t,t-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{L}\bm{J}_{t,t-1},

\displaystyle\bm{J}_{t,t-2}

\displaystyle=w_{t-1}\sum_{j\in S^{t-2}}\bm{e}_{j}\bm{e}_{j}^{\top},\quad

\displaystyle\bm{D}_{t,t-2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{L}\bm{J}_{t,t-2}.

We obtain the expressions of
{\mathcal{D}}
and
{\mathcal{J}}
for FISTA by substituting the above into (
2.7
).

3.5
Local quadratic approximation in non-convex penalized regression

In this subsection, we consider the folded-concave penalized least-squares,

(3.8)

\min_{\bm{b}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p}}\frac{1}{2n}\|\bm{y}-\bm{X}\bm{b}\|^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{p}\rho(|\bm{b}_{j}|),

where
\rho:{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is a concave penalty function.
This encompasses SCAD
(
Fan and Li 2001
)
and MCP
(
Zhang 2010
)
.
For simulations, we set
\rho(\cdot)
to be the MCP penalty
with two positive tuning parameters
(\lambda,\tau)
, defined as

(3.9)

\displaystyle\rho(x;\lambda,\tau)=\begin{cases}\lambda x-\frac{1}{2\tau}x^{2}&\text{if~}x\leq\tau\lambda\\
\frac{1}{2}\tau\lambda^{2}&\text{if~}x>\tau\lambda\end{cases}.

For simplicity, we will omit the parameters
(\lambda,\tau)
in the notation, using
\rho(x)
instead of
\rho(x;\lambda,\tau)
. Consequently, the derivative of
\rho
becomes
\rho^{\prime}(x)=(\lambda-x/\tau){\mathbb{I}}(x\leq\tau\lambda)
.
As
\tau\to\infty
,
\rho(x)
becomes
\lambda x
, and the optimization problem (
3.8
) then coincides with the Lasso (
3.6
).

In order to solve the non-convex penalized regression (
3.8
), we consider the local isotropic quadratic approximation (LQA) to the least-squares loss

f(\bm{b}):=\frac{1}{2n}\|\bm{y}-\bm{X}\bm{b}\|^{2}
at a vector
{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t-1}
, namely

(3.10)

f(\bm{b})\approx f({\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t-1})+(\bm{b}-{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t-1})^{\top}\nabla f({\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t-1})+(L/2)\|\bm{b}-{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t-1}\|^{2},

where
\nabla f({\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t-1})
is the gradient of
f
evaluated at
{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t-1}
, and

L=n^{-1}\lVert\bm{X}\rVert_{\rm{op}}^{2}
as in previous sections.
Applying the above quadratic approximation (
3.10
) to the least-squares in (
3.8
)
and ignoring the constant term yields the optimization problem

(3.11)

{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t}=\mathop{\rm arg\,min}_{\bm{b}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p}}\frac{1}{2}\|\bm{b}-({\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t-1}+(nL)^{-1}\bm{X}^{\top}(\bm{y}-\bm{X}{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t-1}))\|^{2}+\frac{1}{L}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\rho(|\bm{b}_{j}|).

Let
{\widehat{\bm{b}}}_{j}^{t}
and
\bm{v}_{j}^{t}
be the
j
-th entry of
{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t}
and
\bm{v}^{t}
, respectively.
For
\tau\geq 1/L
, the optimization problem (
3.11
) admits the closed-form solution

(3.12)

\displaystyle{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t}_{j}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\soft_{\lambda/L}\Bigl({\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t-1}_{j}+\frac{1}{L}\bm{v}^{t-1}_{j}\Bigr)(1-\tfrac{1}{\tau L})^{-1}&\text{if }|{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t-1}_{j}+\tfrac{1}{L}\bm{v}^{t-1}_{j}|\leq\tau\lambda,\\
{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t-1}_{j}+\frac{1}{L}\bm{v}^{t-1}_{j}&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}.

In this case,
\bm{J}_{t,t-1}
and
\bm{D}_{t,t-1}
in (
2.3
)
are the diagonal matrices

\displaystyle\bm{J}_{t,t-1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\bm{e}_{j}\bm{e}_{j}^{\top}\Bigl[(1-\tfrac{1}{\tau L})^{-1}{\mathbb{I}}\Bigl(|{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t-1}_{j}+\tfrac{1}{L}\bm{v}^{t-1}_{j}|\in[\lambda/L,\tau\lambda]\Bigr)+{\mathbb{I}}\Bigl(|{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t-1}_{j}+\tfrac{1}{L}\bm{v}^{t-1}_{j}|>\tau\lambda\Bigr)\Bigr]

and
\bm{D}_{t,t-1}=\tfrac{1}{L}\bm{J}_{t,t-1}
.

Remark 3.3
.

If
\tau=\infty
, the MCP function reduces to

\sum_{j=1}^{p}\rho(|\bm{b}_{j}|)=\lambda\lVert\bm{b}\rVert_{1}
, so MCP is the same
as the Lasso.
In this case, the LQA iterations (
3.12
) become

{\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t}=\soft_{\lambda/L}({\widehat{\bm{b}}}^{t-1}+\frac{1}{L}\bm{v}^{t-1})
, which is the same as ISTA iterations (
3.7
) in
Section

3.3
.

), we consider

and

4
Efficient computation of the memory matrix

Recall that the memory matrix
\smash{\widehat{\mathbf{A}}}

is crucial in the formulae of proposed risk estimator
\hat{r}_{t}
in (
2.15
) and debiased estimator
{\widehat{\bm{b}}}_{j}^{t,\rm debias}
in (
2.21
).
We have provided specific expressions of
\smash{\widehat{\mathbf{A}}}
for various algorithms in
Section

3
.
In this section, we provide an efficient way to compute the memory matrix
\smash{\widehat{\mathbf{A}}}
in (
2.12
) for algorithms with the general iterations (
2.8
).

4.1
Iteratively computing rows of
\smash{\widehat{\mathbf{A}}}

Recall the definition of
\smash{\widehat{\mathbf{A}}}
in (
2.12
) is

\displaystyle\smash{\widehat{\mathbf{A}}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigl(\bm{I}_{T}\otimes(\bm{e}_{i}^{\top}\bm{X})\bigr)\Bigl(\bm{I}_{pT}+{\mathcal{D}}(\bm{I}_{T}\otimes\tfrac{\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{X}}{n})-{\mathcal{J}}\Bigr)^{-1}{\mathcal{D}}\bigl(\bm{I}_{T}\otimes(\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{e}_{i})\bigr).

The first apparent computational hurdle lies in inverting the large matrix
\bm{I}_{pT}+{\mathcal{D}}(\bm{I}_{T}\otimes\tfrac{\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{X}}{n})-{\mathcal{J}}
of size
{pT\times pT}
.
We now provide an efficient way to compute the memory matrix
\smash{\widehat{\mathbf{A}}}
without explicitly inverting this large matrix.
Recall that
{\mathcal{D}}
and
{\mathcal{J}}
are
T\times T
block lower triangular matrices given in (
2.7
) or (
2.10
),
where each block is of size
p\times p
.
It follows that
{\mathcal{J}}-{\mathcal{D}}(\bm{I}_{T}\otimes\tfrac{\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{X}}{n})
is also a
T\times T
block lower triangular matrix with zero diagonal blocks.
Let
\bm{M}_{t}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p\times(pT)}
be the
t
-th block row
of
\mathcal{D}
.
Consider the linear system with unknowns
\bm{R}_{1}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p\times pT},\dots,\bm{R}_{T}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p\times pT}
given by

\Bigl(\bm{I}_{pT}-{\mathcal{J}}+{\mathcal{D}}(\bm{I}_{T}\otimes\tfrac{\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{X}}{n})\Bigr)\begin{bmatrix}\bm{R}_{1}\\
\bm{R}_{2}\\
\vdots\\
\bm{R}_{T-1}\\
\bm{R}_{T}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{M}_{1}\\
\bm{M}_{2}\\
\vdots\\
\bm{M}_{T-1}\\
\bm{M}_{T}\end{bmatrix}={\mathcal{D}}

which can be rewritten with
\bm{P}_{t,s}=\bm{J}_{t,s}-\bm{D}_{t,s}\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{X}/n
as

(4.1)

\begin{bmatrix}\bm{I}_{p}&{\mathbf{0}}_{p\times p}&\dots&\dots&{\mathbf{0}}_{p\times p}\\
-\bm{P}_{2,1}&\bm{I}_{p}&{\mathbf{0}}_{p\times p}&&\vdots\\
-\bm{P}_{3,1}&-\bm{P}_{3,2}&\bm{I}_{p}&\ddots&\vdots\\
\ \vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&{\mathbf{0}}_{p\times p}\\
-\bm{P}_{T,1}&&\dots&-\bm{P}_{T,T-1}&\bm{I}_{p}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\bm{R}_{1}\\
\bm{R}_{2}\\
\vdots\\
\bm{R}_{T-1}\\
\bm{R}_{T}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{M}_{1}\\
\bm{M}_{2}\\
\vdots\\
\bm{M}_{T-1}\\
\bm{M}_{T}\end{bmatrix}={\mathcal{D}}.

The left-most matrix being lower-triangular with identity diagonal blocks,
forward substitution provides the unique solution: starting with

\bm{R}_{1}=\bm{M}_{1}
we obtain directly

\bm{R}_{2}=\bm{M}_{2}+\bm{P}_{2,1}\bm{R}_{1}
, and more generally for all
t\geq 1
,

(4.2)

\bm{R}_{t}=\bm{M}_{t}+\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}\bm{P}_{t,s}\bm{R}_{s}=\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}\bm{e}_{s}^{\top}\otimes\bm{D}_{t,s}+\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}\bm{P}_{t,s}\bm{R}_{s},

where the second equality follows from the observation that

{\mathcal{D}}
is block lower triangular with
t
-th row block

\bm{M}_{t}=\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}\bm{e}_{s}^{\top}\otimes\bm{D}_{t,s}
.
Given

(\bm{R}_{s})_{s\leq t-1}
, compute
\bm{R}_{t}
according to
(
4.2
) and set

(4.3)

\smash{\widehat{\mathbf{A}}}_{t,t^{\prime}}=\tfrac{1}{n}\trace\bigl[\bm{X}\bm{R}_{t}(\bm{e}_{t^{\prime}}\otimes\bm{X}^{\top})\bigr]\quad\text{ for }t^{\prime}<t,\quad\smash{\widehat{\mathbf{A}}}_{t,t^{\prime}}=0\quad\text{ for }t^{\prime}\geq t.

If only one or two blocks
\bm{P}_{t,s}
per row are nonzero
as in (
2.7
), which is satisfied for all examples of

Section

3
, the recursion
(
4.2
) simplifies in this case to

(4.4)

\bm{R}_{t}=\sum_{s=t-2}^{t-1}\bm{e}_{s}^{\top}\otimes\bm{D}_{t,s}+\sum_{s=t-2}^{t-1}\bigl(\bm{J}_{t,s}-\bm{D}_{t,s}\tfrac{\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{X}}{n}\bigr)\bm{R}_{s},

followed by (
4.3
).
Notably, we may compute each
\bm{R}_{t}
recursively while
only keeping in memory
\bm{R}_{t-1}
and
\bm{R}_{t-2}
,
both of size
p\times(pT)
, at each step.

4.2
Hutchinson’s trace approximation

The above computation of
\smash{\widehat{\mathbf{A}}}
using (
4.3
) can still be prohibitive, as it requires storing in memory
matrices
\bm{R}_{t},\bm{R}_{t-1}
of size at most
p\times(pT)
at each step, and perform
matrix-matrix products with dimensions
p\times p
and
p\times(pT)

in (
4.4
).
We now describe an efficient way to approximate the entries of
\smash{\widehat{\mathbf{A}}}

while avoiding to store intermediate matrices of size
p\times(pT)
.
Let
m\geq 1
be a small constant integer; in simulations
we have noticed that
m=1,2
or
3
already gives accurate estimates.
We propose to approximate the trace (
4.3
)
for the
(t,s)
-th entry of
\smash{\widehat{\mathbf{A}}}

using Hutchinson’s trace approximation
(
Hutchinson 1990
)

\trace(\bm{M})\approx\trace(\bm{W}^{\top}\bm{M}\bm{W})

for any matrix
\bm{M}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n}
,
where

\bm{W}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times m}
is a random matrix with i.i.d. entries uniformly
distributed in
\{\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}},\frac{-1}{\sqrt{m}}\}
.
The Hanson-Wright inequality ensures that the above approximation
holds with high-probability when
\|\bm{M}\|_{\rm F}
is negligible
compared to
\trace(\bm{M})
.
Using this approximation, the computation of the trace of a matrix
of size
n\times n
is reduced to that of a matrix of size
m\times m
.

In our case, we need to approximate the trace in (
4.3
) using Hutchinson’s approximation.
To this end,
let

\bm{W}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times m}
be a random matrix with i.i.d. entries uniformly
distributed in
\{\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}},\frac{-1}{\sqrt{m}}\}

independently of everything else.
The matrix
\bm{W}
is only sampled once, and fixed throughout
the following computation.

5
Discussion

This paper introduces a novel procedure to estimate the out-of-sample prediction error for iterates of gradient descent type algorithms, in the context of high-dimensional regression.
As illustrated in
Section

3
, this risk-estimation procedure is applicable to a wide range of algorithms commonly used in optimization, including gradient descent, Nesterov’s accelerated gradient descent, iterative shrinkage-thresholding algorithm, fast iterative shrinkage-thresholding algorithm
(
Beck and Teboulle 2009
)
, and local quadratic approximation.
The proposed procedure do not require the knowledge of the design
covariance
\bm{\Sigma}
or of the noise level
\sigma^{2}
.
The estimates allow the statistician to leverage
the benefits of early stopping,
by selecting an early iteration
\hat{t}\in[T]
that minimizes
the generalization error among the first
T
iterations
up to negligible error
(
Corollary

2.3
).
We have further established the asymptotic normality of the entries of the iterates after a debiasing correction, which can be used to construct confidence intervals for the
j
-th of the ground-truth
\bm{b}^{*}

when
\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{e}_{j}
is known or can be estimated
(
Theorem

2.4
).
Extensive numerical simulations in
Section

3
demonstrate that
the proposed estimate is accurate,
and that the z-scores defining the confidence intervals
are approximately standard normal.

Here we highlight several directions for further exploration.
A first avenue for future research is to improve the dependence
of the bounds on the number
T
of iterations. Currently,
upper bounds in
Theorem

2.2
and other main results
involve constants that worst than exponential in
T
, while we observe
in simulations that the risk estimate is still accurate over the
whole trajectory for
T\gg\log n
. Another direction of interest
is to generalize
the estimates of the present paper beyond the square loss
in (
1.2
), for instance
with the Huber or least-absolute deviation loss in robust regression,
or the logistic loss in classification problems.
Another generalization concerns randomized versions of GD such
as stochastic gradient descent.
Finally, our proofs rely crucially on the Gaussianity of the design,
and it would be of interest to extend the validity of the estimates
proposed here to different design distributions. Recent progress
has been made in this direction

(
Montanari and Saeed 2022
;
Hu and Lu 2022
;
Han and Shen 2023
;
Pesce et al. 2023
;
Dudeja et al. 2023
)
regarding the universality
of the training and generalization error of minimizers such as
(
1.2
). An extra challenge presented here is that the validity
of
\hat{r}_{t}
for estimating
r_{t}
requires not only universality of the training and generalization error, but also universality the weights
\hat{w}_{t,s}
.

B.2
Review of Kronecker product

In this section, we review the definitions and properties of the Kronecker product.
Let
\bm{A}
be an

matrix, and
\bm{B}
be a

matrix. The Kronecker product of
\bm{A}
and
\bm{B}
, denoted by

, is defined as:

\bm{A}\otimes\bm{B}=\begin{bmatrix}a_{11}\bm{B}&\cdots&a_{1n}\bm{B}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
a_{m1}\bm{B}&\cdots&a_{mn}\bm{B}\end{bmatrix},

where
a_{ij}
represents the entry in the
i
-th row and
j
-th column of matrix
\bm{A}
.
Below we list a few properties of the Kronecker product that will be useful in our proofs.

(i)

The mixed-product property: If
\bm{A},\bm{B},\bm{C}
and
\bm{D}
are matrices of such size that the matrix products
\bm{A}\bm{C}
and
\bm{B}\bm{D}
make sense, then

(B.1)

(\bm{A}\otimes\bm{B})(\bm{C}\otimes\bm{D})=(\bm{A}\bm{C})\otimes(\bm{B}\bm{D})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{mp\times nq}.

(ii)

Inverse property:

(B.2)

\displaystyle(\bm{A}\otimes\bm{B})^{-1}=\bm{A}^{-1}\otimes\bm{B}^{-1}\text{ and }(\bm{A}\otimes\bm{B})^{\dagger}=\bm{A}^{\dagger}\otimes\bm{B}^{\dagger},

where
\bm{A}^{\dagger}
means the Moore-Penrose inverse of
\bm{A}
.

(iii)

Trace property:

(B.3)

\displaystyle\trace(\bm{A}\otimes\bm{B})=\trace(\bm{A})\trace(\bm{B}).

(iv)

Norm property:

(B.4)

\displaystyle\lVert\bm{A}\otimes\bm{B}\rVert_{\rm{op}}=\lVert\bm{A}\rVert_{\rm{op}}\lVert\bm{B}\rVert_{\rm{op}}\text{ and }\lVert\bm{A}\otimes\bm{B}\rVert_{\rm{F}}=\lVert\bm{A}\rVert_{\rm{F}}\lVert\bm{B}\rVert_{\rm{F}}.

(v)

Relationship with vectorization operator: If the matrix product
\bm{A}\bm{B}\bm{C}
makes sense, then

(B.5)

\displaystyle\vec(\bm{A}\bm{B}\bm{C})=(\bm{C}^{\top}\otimes\bm{A})\vec(\bm{B}),
