Distributed Collaborative Safety-Critical Control for Networked Dynamic Systems
Brooks A. Butler, Philip E. Paré

Abstract
As modern systems become ever more connected with complex dynamic coupling relationships, the development of safe control methods for such networked systems becomes paramount. In this paper, we define a general networked model with coupled dynamics and local control and discuss the relationship of node-level safety definitions for individual agents with local neighborhood dynamics. We define a node-level barrier function (NBF), node-level control barrier function (NCBF), and collaborative node-level barrier function (cNCBF) and provide conditions under which sets defined by these functions will be forward invariant. We use collaborative node-level barrier functions to construct a novel distributed algorithm for the safe control of collaborating network agents and provide conditions under which the algorithm is guaranteed to converge to a viable set of safe control actions for all agents or a terminally infeasible state for at least one agent. We introduce the notion of non-compliance of network neighbors as a metric of robustness for collaborative safety for a given network state and chosen barrier function hyper-parameters. We illustrate these results on a networked susceptible-infected-susceptible (SIS) model.

Introduction

Networked dynamic systems have become ubiquitous in modern society, underpinning critical infrastructure, transportation, communication, and coupled processes. In many applications, effective and safe operation in networked systems is crucial, as disruptions in these interconnected systems can potentially have far-reaching societal and economic consequences. Some examples of critical network model applications include the mitigation of epidemic spreading processes
Paré et al. 2020
, smart grid management
Tuballa and Abundo 2016
, uncrewed aerial drone swarms
Tahir et al. 2019
;
Chung et al. 2018
, autonomous vehicle networks
Plathottam and Ranganathan 2018
, and multi-agent robot systems
Wang et al. 2017a
. One of the greatest challenges in the effective control of such networked systems is the rate at which complexity scales with each additional node, where the computational complexity for even relatively simple networked models can become exponentially intractable as these networks grow (i.e., as the number of nodes increases). In order to overcome this scaling challenge, distributed and decentralized controllers are developed to scale with network growth. Distributed control laws become especially important when nodes represent independent agents with individual goals.
Additionally, when network agents are considered to be independent actors with individual objectives, cooperative control schemes allow for coordination between networked agents via active communication
Lewis et al. 2013
;
Li and Duan 2017
;
Wang et al. 2017b
;
Yu et al. 2017
, further emphasizing the importance of a distributed control law in such systems.
In this work, we develop methods for the collaborative distributed safety-critical control of networked dynamic systems and discuss principles of safe control for networked systems with node-level definitions of safety.

1.1
Background

While much of the foundational work on safety for dynamical systems via set invariance was performed by Nagumo during the 1940s
Nagumo 1942
;
Blanchini 1999
, the study of safety-critical control has seen a significant resurgence in recent years, due largely to the introduction and refinement of
control barrier functions
(CBFs)
Ames et al. 2016
;
Ames et al. 2019
.
Since their introduction, control barrier functions have been used in numerous applications to provide safety guarantees in many dynamic system models including robotics
Wang et al. 2017a
;
Glotfelter et al. 2017
;
Chung et al. 2018
;
Chen et al. 2020a
and autonomous vehicles
Notomista et al. 2020
;
Ma et al. 2021
;
Alan et al. 2022
;
Lyu et al. 2022
. We do not attempt an exhaustive literature review on the general application of CBFs to cyber-physical systems in this paper; additional references may be found here
Hobbs et al. 2023
;
Xiao et al. 2023
.

We now provide some background on work relating to the safety-critical control of networked systems with coupled dynamics. While many of the initial applications of safety-critical control have been focused on multi-robot systems
Wang et al. 2016
;
Wang et al. 2017a
;
Glotfelter et al. 2017
;
Glotfelter et al. 2018
;
Chen et al. 2020a
;
Panagou et al. 2015
;
Hu et al. 2022
with an emphasis on collision avoidance, there is a key distinction to be made between systems with networked communication and mutually independent agent dynamics (e.g. a group of robots with a common goal and shared communication but mutually independent kinematics) and a networked system with coupled dynamics (e.g. a networked power grid, physically coupled robots, or epidemic processes) where neighboring states can be found directly in the first order dynamics of each node. Thus, this work focuses on the second case of coupled networked dynamics.

One common strategy used to tackle the high dimensionality and complexity of networked systems is to break down these potentially large systems into smaller, and therefore more readily solvable, components and provide methods for composing a solution for the entire system. This strategy has been applied in the compositional construction of barrier functions for networked systems
Nejati et al. 2020a
;
Nejati et al. 2020b
;
Nejati et al. 2022
;
Jahanshahi et al. 2022
;
Jagtap et al. 2020
where systems are interconnected via input-output connections. Oftentimes, the input of other systems to an interconnected component is treated as a bounded disturbance, and one may take advantage of the small-gain theory when composing multiple barrier certificates
Anand et al. 2022
. Other work on composing barrier functions, although not explicitly for networked systems, uses geometric methods to compose multiple safety constraints on second-order systems
Breeden and Panagou 2023
, which utilizes principles of higher-order barrier functions
Xiao and Belta 2019
;
Xiao and Belta 2021
. Work has also been presented on the composition of neural certificates for networked systems
Zhang et al. 2023a
, which uses deep learning to train scalable models with networked connections based on principles of control Lyapunov functions combined with a small-gain assumption.

Another method for computing safety certificates for systems involves the use of signal temporal logic (STL)
Donzé 2013
;
Raman et al. 2014
;
Deshmukh et al. 2017
;
Raman et al. 2015
;
Sadigh and Kapoor 2016
which provides a natural syntax for ensuring that a dynamic system meets certain safety requirements for all time. These methods have been applied to networked systems with coupled dynamics to formulate both centralized and decentralized controllers under STL tasks
Lindemann and Dimarogonas 2020
;
Lindemann and Dimarogonas 2019a
;
Lindemann and Dimarogonas 2019b
which can be applied to time-varying barrier functions. Additionally, work has been done using assume-guarantee contracts and parameterized signal temporal logic (pSTL) as an analogy for barrier certificates in networked systems with applications to power systems
Chen et al. 2020b
;
Chen et al. 2019
. In these works, input from neighboring nodes in the network is treated as a bounded disturbance, which in the case of
Chen et al. 2020b
, these disturbances are considered to be scalar and summable. Many of these works often focus on the definition of safety for the network as a whole and use compositional methods to efficiently compute centralized safety certificates
Song et al. 2022
. There is also recent work that uses machine learning to train distributed controllers for scalable networked systems via graph neural networks
Zhou et al. 2020
;
Wu et al. 2020
;
Scarselli et al. 2008

to implement safe multi-agent control
Fan et al. 2023
that can be tested by neighbors without knowledge of the control inputs.

The field of cooperative control for multi-agent systems provides a rich body of literature that examines scenarios where agents may share information over a communication network
Lewis et al. 2013
;
Li and Duan 2017
;
Wang et al. 2017b
;
Yu et al. 2017
. In such formulations, agents typically share and receive information via either direct communication or broadcast that enables cooperative control adjustments to be made
Huang et al. 2010
;
Li et al. 2021
;
Qu 2010
;
Qu and Simaan 2012
. However, in many formulations of cooperative control, a common assumption is that agent first-order dynamics are independent of each other, thus the networked element is mainly facilitated via virtual communication. Contrasting in this work, we wish to include the class of explicitly coupled systems and leverage any knowledge of the networked dynamic structure in the formulation of safety requests.

While the current literature has explored some approaches for distributed safety-critical control of networked dynamic systems, the use of explicit coupled network dynamics for safety-critical control of individual network agents and the implementation of active collaboration between coupled agents to achieve independent safety goals is, to the best of our knowledge, an open problem. Therefore, this work aims to contribute to the development of control theory for safety-critical systems with coupled dynamics by exploiting directly the dynamic structure of coupled systems.

2.3
Safety Definitions

While the language and syntax of set invariance and barrier functions provide a mathematically succinct grammar for describing theoretical safety, defining safety in practice requires careful delineation between desired safety goals and actual viable safety in the defined state space with respect to the system dynamics. Additionally, when discussing definitions of safety for networked dynamic systems, one must consider the possibly differing definitions of safety for each node in the network. These
node-level
safety definitions become even more relevant when considering networked models where nodes may be viewed as independent agents working to achieve individual or node-level goals. Thus, in the context of a networked model defined in Section
2.2
, we define a node-level safety constraint for node
i\in[n]
with the set

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{i}

\displaystyle=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}:h_{i}(x_{i})\geq 0\right\},

(7)

where
h_{i}\in C^{r},r\geq 1
and
h_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{N_{i}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a function whose zero-super-level set defines the region which node
i\in[n]
considers to be safe (i.e., if
h_{i}(x_{i})<0

i
is no longer safe). Note that
\mathcal{C}_{i}
also collects all other states in the network, which enables us to define the safety constraints of the entire networked system as

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}

\displaystyle=\bigcap_{i\in[n]}\mathcal{C}_{i}.\;

(8)

This notation also allows us to define the constraints of a particular 1-hop neighborhood centered on node
i
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{N}_{i}}=\bigcap_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}\cup\{i\}}\mathcal{C}_{j}.

(9)

Given the definitions of these safety constraints, we define the viable safe regions

A set

of (

for all
t\in\mathcal{T}

We define a similar notion for the entire networked system, where we compose the dynamics for the entire system as

\dot{x}=f(x)+g(x,u)

2.4
Safety in Networked Dynamic Systems

One unique challenge that comes from the definition of node-level safety constraints as defined in Section
2.3
, which depend only on the state of the given node, is that when evaluating the derivative of
h_{i}(x_{i})
we must incorporate the network influence of its neighbors
j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}^{+}
, where, with a slight abuse of notation, we define the derivative of
h_{i}
as

\dot{h}_{i}(x,u)=\mathcal{L}_{f_{i}}h_{i}(x)+\mathcal{L}_{g_{i}}h_{i}(x,u)=\frac{\partial h_{i}(x)}{\partial x}\big(f_{i}(x)+g_{i}(x,u)\big).

Notice that in this case, we can define the node-level constraint function as the mapping
h_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{N_{i}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
; however, the dynamics of node
i
, whose dynamics are a function of all neighboring nodes in
\mathcal{N}_{i}^{+}
, require that
\dot{h}_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{N_{i}+N_{i}^{+}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
.

Therefore, even though we define
h_{i}
with respect to only
x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{i}}
, when computing the Lie derivative we must compute the Jacobian with respect to all network states
\frac{\partial h_{i}(x)}{\partial x}
, which naturally zeros out all states of nodes
j\notin\mathcal{N}_{i}^{+}\cup\{i\}
when taking only the first derivative, but is relevant when we must consider higher-order derivatives of the constraint function
h_{i}
. In fact, although the initial definition of a node-level constraint function
h_{i}(x_{i})
may appear to be agnostic to other nodes’ dynamics and their respective constraint functions
h_{j}(x_{j}),j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}^{+}
, we see the
(k-1)
-hop neighborhood dynamics appear in
k
th derivative of
h_{i}
as follows.

Taking the first-order Lie derivative of the node-level constraint function
h_{i}(x_{i})
with respect to a general networked system

\dot{x}=f(x),

(11)

without control inputs for the sake of notational simplicity in this initial discussion, yields

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{f}h_{i}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial h_{i}(x_{i})}{\partial x}f(x)=\sum_{j\in[n]}\frac{\partial h_{i}(x)}{\partial x_{j}}f_{j}(x).

(12)

Note that since
h_{i}
is defined with respect to only the states at node
i
, we have
\frac{\partial h_{i}(x_{i})}{\partial x_{j}}=0
for all
j\in[n]\setminus\{i\}

. Therefore, we may express the first-order Lie derivative of such a constraint function as

\mathcal{L}_{f}h_{i}(x)=\frac{\partial h_{i}(x_{i})}{\partial x_{i}}f_{i}(x)=\mathcal{L}_{f_{i}}h_{i}(x).

(13)

Extending this computation to the second-order Lie derivative yields

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{f}^{2}h_{i}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{f}h_{i}(x)}{\partial x}f(x)=\sum_{j\in[n]}\mathcal{L}_{f_{j}}\mathcal{L}_{f_{i}}h_{i}(x)

(14)

where we now begin to see the dynamics of the 1-hop neighbors of node

\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}\left(\frac{\partial h_{i}(x_{i})}{\partial x_{i}}f_{i}(x)\right)=0,\forall j\in[n]\setminus(\mathcal{N}_{i}^{+}\cup\{i\})

(15)

making

\mathcal{L}_{f}^{2}h_{i}(x)=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}^{+}}\mathcal{L}_{f_{j}}\mathcal{L}_{f_{i}}h_{i}(x)+\mathcal{L}^{2}_{f_{i}}h_{i}(x).

Therefore, the second derivative of

includes the Lie derivatives along the vector fields generated by both

and its neighbors

for

.
Following this same pattern, the

\mathcal{L}_{f}^{k}h_{i}(x)=\sum_{\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{k-1}\in[n]}\mathcal{L}_{f_{\alpha_{k-1}}}\cdots\mathcal{L}_{f_{\alpha_{1}}}\mathcal{L}_{f_{i}}h_{i}(x),

where we can see that these

encode the dynamic relationships of the

\displaystyle\psi_{i}^{k}(x)

3
Safety Guarantees with Local Control

We now consider the safety of a controlled networked dynamic system with respect to node-level defined safety conditions and distributed control. We first present a result that allows for a worse-case analysis of the vulnerability of a given node depending on the definition of safety constraints by its neighbors. We then define a collaborative node-level barrier function that incorporates the 1-hop neighborhood dynamics into the evaluation of node-level safety.

For this analysis, we limit our control actions to the case when the dynamics of each node
i\in[n]
are defined by

\dot{x}_{i}=f_{i}(x_{i},x_{\mathcal{N}_{i}^{+}})+g_{i}(x_{i},u_{i}).

(20)

In other words, assume that the controller
u_{i}\in\mathcal{U}_{i}
for each node
i\in[n]
is incapable of changing the strength of the network influences from its neighbors
\mathcal{N}_{i}^{+}
, which are encoded in the networked dynamics of
f_{i}(x_{i},x_{\mathcal{N}_{i}^{+}})
.

3.1
Node Vulnerability

We define a conservative condition for safety with respect to a worst-case scenario for neighbors as follows. Given the system dynamics defined in (
20
), we make a best-case assumption on the capability of the controllers for nodes
j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}^{+}
.

Assumption 1
.

Let there exist an NBF
h_{j}:\mathbb{R}^{N_{j}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
for each
j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}^{+}
with a corresponding node-level viability domain
\mathcal{S}_{j}
as defined by (
19
).

Thus, we assume that all neighbors
j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}^{+}
are capable of satisfying their individual safety constraints within their node-level viability domain. Further, we assume that the relative safety of each neighbor
j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}^{+}
has an influence on the capability of node
i
to remain safe, which is defined in the following assumption.

Assumption 2
.

For a given node
i\in[n]
with neighbors
j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}^{+}
and
x,y\in\mathcal{S}_{i}\cap\mathcal{S}_{j}
, if
x_{\mathcal{N}_{i}^{+}}=y_{\mathcal{N}_{i}^{+}}
and

h_{j}(x)<h_{j}(y)

(21)

then

\mathcal{L}_{f_{i}}h_{i}(x)\leq\mathcal{L}_{f_{i}}h_{i}(y).

(22)

Conversely, one may interpret Assumption
2
as increasing the safety of the neighbors of node
i
also increases its ability to remain safe. This assumption restricts the use of the following results to specific applications
such as
networked epidemic spreading models, where typically the shared goal of all nodes is to keep the level of infection below some threshold, and an increase in infection levels for connected neighbors of a given node results in a higher total infection rate. Another example is power system control, where a typical goal for each node is to keep voltage frequency within a specified range, and deviation by neighbors for a given node increases the strain on its own controller.

Under Assumptions
1
and
2
, we consider a worst-case scenario that can identify if node
i\in[n]
is vulnerable to violating its safety conditions based on the safety constraints of its neighbors.

Definition 4
.

Let
\mathcal{C}_{i}
be defined by (
7
). We have that
h_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{N_{i}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a node-level control barrier function (NCBF) if
\forall x\in\partial\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{N}_{i}^{+}},\exists u_{i}\in\mathcal{U}_{i}
such that

\dot{h}_{i}(x,u_{i})\geq-\alpha_{i}(h_{i}(x)),

(23)

where
\alpha_{i}
is an extended class-
\mathcal{K}
function.

Note that the NCBF is defined using the boundary states of neighbors viability domains
\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{N}_{i}^{+}}
, which yields the following results on the forward invariance of
\mathcal{S}_{i}\subseteq\mathcal{C}_{i}
.

Theorem 1
.

Given a networked dynamic system defined by (
20
) and that Assumptions
1
and
2
hold,
\mathcal{S}_{i}\subseteq\mathcal{C}_{i}
is forward invariant if and only if
h_{i}

∎

We, therefore, have a result that can describe the safety of a single node in a networked system where distributed controllers are unable to change network connections and where the safety of a node is mutually dependent on the safety of its neighbors. Given that the assumptions for this result limits its application to specific networked models, we aim next to generalize these results to a more diverse set of network applications that allow collaboration between node

and its neighbors.

3.2
Collaborative Network Safety-Critical Control

In the case where we have knowledge of network dynamic influences and all nodes are able to cooperate and communicate freely, we work to leverage this collective power to achieve network safety in a distributed manner.

4
Collaborative Safety

In this section, we construct an algorithm that exploits the properties of the cNCBF to communicate and process safety requests to and from neighbors, respectively.
Typically, the goal of safety-critical control is to minimally alter nominal control commands such that the controlled system is always safe. For example, given some nominal command control policies
u_{i}^{n}:\mathbb{R}^{N}\rightarrow\mathcal{U}_{i}
and

and its neighbors

\displaystyle\argmin_{u_{i},u_{\mathcal{N}_{i}^{+}}\in\mathcal{U}_{i}\times\mathcal{U}_{\mathcal{N}_{i}^{+}}}

\displaystyle{\displaystyle||u_{i}-u_{i}^{n}(x)||}^{2}+{||u_{\mathcal{N}_{i}^{+}}-u_{\mathcal{N}_{i}^{+}}^{n}(x)||}^{2}

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle\psi_{i}^{2}(x,u_{i},u_{\mathcal{N}_{i}^{+}})\geq 0.

5

To illustrate the theoretical results from Sections

4

Butler and Paré 2023
;
Zhang et al. 2023b

Butler et al. 2021

We define a networked system with
n
nodes where
x_{i}
is the proportion of infected population at node
i\in[n]
, with the infection dynamics defined by

\dot{x}_{i}=-(\gamma_{i}+u_{i})x_{i}+(1-x_{i})\sum_{j\in[n]}\beta_{ij}x_{j}

(66)

where
\gamma_{i}>0
is the recovery rate at node
i

is a control input boosting the healing rate at node
i
, and
\beta_{ij}\geq 0
is the networked connection going from node
j
to node
i
(note
\beta_{ii}
is simply the infection rate occurring at node
i
). Note that we could also add a control term that reduces the infection rate
\beta_{ii}
at each node
i
; however, we only consider one control term on the healing rate for simplicity. We also define the full state vector as
x\in[0,1]^{n}
and the vector of all control inputs
u\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
. Let
\mathcal{U}_{i}=[0,\bar{u}_{i}]
, where
\bar{u}_{i}
is the upper limit on boosting the healing rate at node
i
. We let each node define its individual safety constraint set as

h_{i}(x_{i})=\bar{x}_{i}-x_{i}

(67)

where
\bar{x}_{i}\in(0,1]
is the defined safety threshold for the acceptable proportion of infected individuals at node

. Thus, the individual safe sets for each node are given by

\mathcal{C}_{i}=\{x\in[0,1]^{n}:h_{i}(x_{i})\geq 0\}.

(68)

In the remainder of this section, we will show how the analytical results of Section
3.1
may be used to determine the worst-case vulnerability of a node given the safety constraints of the neighboring population as well as how Algorithm
1
may be used to ensure network safety using distributed collaborative control.

5.1
Node Vulnerability Analysis

In this section, we apply the results from Section
3.1
to analyze when a particular node
i\in[n]
may fail to meet its definitions of safety given its control limitations and the safety thresholds of its influencing neighbors. Consider a node
i\in[n]

(70)

Therefore, given
\mathcal{C}_{j},\forall j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}^{+}

where dividing by
\bar{x}_{i}
yields

1.

If

\mathcal{C}_{i}
is not forward invariant, independent of network influences.

We may consider the worst-case scenario where each neighbor

6
Conclusion

In this work, we have presented methods for the distributed, collaborative safety-critical control of networked dynamic systems that exploit networked dynamics and structure to ensure individual safety goals are met. We have shown that the choice of barrier function hyperparameters (i.e., the choice of class-
\mathcal{K}
functions in the higher-order barrier function definition) has both a qualitative effect on agent behavior and can allow for robustness in the sense of neighbor error in fulfilling safety requests. This novel approach leverages knowledge of networked dynamics in order to achieve stronger runtime assurances that tractably scale with network growth, which is of utmost importance as the world becomes more interconnected and prone to widespread failures.

While some foundational theory on safety-critical control of networks has been presented in this work, many directions of exploration and development remain open. For example, providing conditions for the convergence of a collaborative safety algorithm that accounts for potentially conflicting safety requests is vital for many relevant applications. Additional directions for future work include considering network influences beyond the 1-hop neighborhood as well as considerations of edge control in the coupling dynamics of networked models. Finally, we have considered the case when neighbors readily and truthfully share information and attempt to satisfy requests by their neighbors to the best of their ability; however, one may also consider the case where neighbors are uncooperative, share faulty information, or are actively malicious.
