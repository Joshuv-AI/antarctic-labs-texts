Lift and drag in three-dimensional steady viscous and compressible flow Preprint: APS/123-QED
Luoqin Liu ID, Jiezhi Wu, Weidong Su, Linlin Kang

Abstract
In a recent paper, Liu, Zhu and Wu (2015, J. Fluid Mech. 784 : 304) present a force theory for a body in a two-dimensional, viscous, compressible and steady flow. In this companion paper we do the same for three-dimensional flow. Using the fundamental solution of the linearized Navier-Stokes equations, we improve the force formula for incompressible flow originally derived by Goldstein in 1931 and summarized by Milne-Thomson in 1968, both being far from complete, to its perfect final form, which is further proved to be universally true from subsonic to supersonic flows. We call this result the unified force theorem , which states that the forces are always determined by the vector circulation 𝚪 ϕ \mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma$}_{\phi} of longitudinal velocity and the scalar inflow Q ψ Q_{\psi} of transverse velocity. Since this theorem is not directly observable either experimentally or computationally, a testable version is also derived, which, however, holds only in the linear far field. We name this version the testable unified force formula . After that, a general principle to increase the lift-drag ratio is proposed.



I
Introduction

The object of this paper is to obtain formulae for the forces when a solid body of any shape is at rest in a steady stream of viscous and compressible fluid. Namely, we assume the body moves steadily through the physical space filled with restful fluid, let the reference frame fix on the body, and neglect the inherent flow unsteadiness at very far field
(
Liu et al. 2017
)
. In such a flow model we only work in the steady subspace
V_{\rm st}
of the whole physical space, which can be divided into a nonlinear near-field and a linear far-field. Correspondingly, the aerodynamic force theories can also be categorized into two groups. One is the
far-field force theories
, by which universal force formulae can be rigorously deduced. Its central task is to identify the key physical quantities responsible for the forces, of which the first and most classic example is the Kutta-Joukowski (K-J) theorem
(
Joukowski 1906
)
for inviscid and incompressible flow that focuses one’s attention to the circulation around an airfoil. The other is the
near-field theories
, which can determine the relationships between the forces and the detailed flow processes and structures behind those universally identified key quantities. It is guided (for steady flow) by the results of far-field theories and always the main body of aerodynamic theories, as given in all monographs and textbooks of low- and high-speed aerodynamics.

Historically, however, various far-field theories had long been limited to incompressible and/or inviscid flow and never reached their highest possible goal to be truly universal: to identify the key physical quantities responsible for aerodynamic forces within the general framework of the Navier-Stokes (N-S) equations. The first breakthrough was made only very recently by
Liu et al. 2015
, who obtained a unified far-field aerodynamic force theory for two-dimensional (2D) viscous and compressible flow, valid from low-speed to supersonic regimes. In this theoretical paper, we present an exactly the same kind of theory but for three-dimensional (3D) flow.

To explain the motivation and orientation of our study, it is appropriate here to make a brief account of previous investigations about the far-field steady force theories, for both 2D and 3D flows due to their close relations. In so doing we rely crucially on the so-called
Helmholtz decomposition

(
Stokes 1851
;
Helmholtz 1858
)
, which decomposes a vector field into a longitudinal part and a transverse part. Let
u
and
U
denote the local and incoming flow velocities, then the disturbance velocity
\textbf{\emph{u}}^{\prime}=\textbf{\emph{u}}-\textbf{\emph{U}}
can always be written formally as

\textbf{\emph{u}}^{\prime}=\textbf{\emph{u}}_{\phi}+\textbf{\emph{u}}_{\psi}\equiv\nabla\phi+\nabla\times\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$},\quad\nabla\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}=0,

(1)

where
\phi
and
\psi
are the
velocity potential
(scalar potential) of the longitudinal field and the
vortical stream function
(vector potential) of the transverse field, respectively.

Before proceeding, we remark that the existence and uniqueness of the Helmholtz decomposition (
1
) is not always ensured, which is strongly dependent on the boundary conditions as well as the flow domains. For the uniqueness problem,
Chorin and Marsden 2000
have obtained a positive answer under the assumption that the transverse velocity
\textbf{\emph{u}}_{\psi}
is parallel to the boundary of domain. This powerful theorem, however, is not applicable to our case since in general the parallelism assumption can not be satisfied at the body surface
(
Wu et al. 2015
, pp. 43-44)
. Fortunately, for the linearized flow, which is the concern of present paper,
Lagerstrom et al. 1949
have proved that the decomposition (
1
) is indeed unique such that the N-S equation can be thoroughly split into two parts (see (
8
) below). For the existence problem,
Serrin 1959
has shown that it can be satisfied for both finite domain with the normal vorticity being zero at the boundary and infinite domain with the velocity decaying fast enough at far field. For the latter case,
Gregory 1996
has proved that the result is still valid even if isolated singular points present. Therefore, the existence and uniqueness of (
1
) in the case of LZW is completely confirmed since the whole space is regular except the isolated singular point where the body locates. But Gregory’s proof is not applicable to our case as the result (see (
37
) below) shows that the positive
x
-axis itself (rather than isolated points) is singular. In this sense, further proofs of the existence of (
1
) under more general conditions are still needed, which is out of the scope of this paper. Nevertheless, the existence of (
1
) can be taken for granted as long as we can find the corresponding solution under specific boundary conditions
(
Liu et al. 2017
, say, far-field decaying condition,)
.

I.1
Far-field force theory in two dimensions

To make the notations unified with 3D flow in Cartesian coordinates
(x,y,z)
with
\textbf{\emph{e}}_{x},\textbf{\emph{e}}_{y}
and
\textbf{\emph{e}}_{z}
being the unit vectors along incoming-flow, spanwise and vertical-up directions, respectively (see Fig.
1
), a 2D flow is assumed to occur on a
(x,z)
-sectional plane, with vorticity
\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}=\omega\textbf{\emph{e}}_{y}
and
\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}=(0,\psi,0)
so that
\textbf{\emph{u}}_{\psi}=\nabla\psi\times\textbf{\emph{e}}_{y}
. A control surface
S
with unit normal vector
n
in three dimensions
reduces
to a closed loop, which has unit tangent vector
t
so that
\textbf{\emph{n}}\times\textbf{\emph{t}}=\textbf{\emph{e}}_{y}
. Then the lift
\textbf{\emph{L}}=L\textbf{\emph{e}}_{z}
on a solid body in a steady flow of incompressible and inviscid fluid reads

\textbf{\emph{L}}=\rho_{0}\textbf{\emph{U}}\times\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma$}_{\phi},

(2)

where
\rho_{0}
and
\textbf{\emph{U}}=U\textbf{\emph{e}}_{x}
are the fluid density and velocity, respectively, at infinity,

\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma$}_{\phi}\equiv\int_{S}\textbf{\emph{n}}\times\nabla\phi\mathrm{d}S=\textbf{\emph{e}}_{y}[\![\phi]\!]

(3)

is the vector circulation
\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma$}=\Gamma\textbf{\emph{e}}_{y}
of a bound vortex in the body,
\phi
is the velocity potential and
[\![\cdot]\!]
denotes the jump of the corresponding quantity as one goes around the loop once. Equation (
2
) is the well-known K-J theorem, which is completely independent of the size and geometry of
S
and has since served as the very basis of classic steady aerodynamics. For example, when the body is an airfoil with sharp trailing edge,
\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma$}_{\phi}
can be determined by the Kutta condition
(
Kutta 1902
)
. Meanwhile, for this inviscid flow the drag is zero, in consistent with the famous
d’Alembert paradox
.

Figure 1:
Cartesian coordinates fixed on the body and other notations used in this article

In contrast to inviscid flow, in a viscous flow the vortical wake must extend to downstream
all the way to the outside of the steady flow region
V_{\rm st}
so that any contour
S\in V_{\rm st}
surrounding the airfoil must cut through the wake, leaving some vorticity outside of
S
. Thus, one has to ask (i) whether (
2
) is still effective, and
if yes, (ii)
whether the lift is still independent of the choice of
S
.

These questions were first studied experimentally by
Bryant and Williams 1926
. They found that the lift calculated by (
2
), with
\nabla\phi
replaced by
\textbf{\emph{u}}^{\prime}

or
u
, is a good approximation to that of the real viscous flow for typical aerodynamic applications:

\textbf{\emph{L}}\cong\rho_{0}\textbf{\emph{U}}\times\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma$},

(4)

with
\cong
implying
asymptotic equality
throughout this paper and

\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma$}\equiv\int_{S}\textbf{\emph{n}}\times\textbf{\emph{u}}^{\prime}\mathrm{d}S=\int_{V}\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}\mathrm{d}V.

(5)

Here
\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}=\nabla\times\textbf{\emph{u}}^{\prime}
is the vorticity and

V
is the volume enclosed by
S
. The experiment
results also indicate
that
\Gamma
may still be independent on
S
.
Then
,
Taylor 1926
pointed out that these
two
positive answers require two conditions: (a) the intersect of
S
and the wake has to be a vertical plane (“wake plane”, denoted by
W
) with normal
\textbf{\emph{n}}=\textbf{\emph{e}}_{x}
; and (b) the net vorticity flux through
W
must vanish, which can be proven for steady flow at large Reynolds number

Re
(for an improved proof of this issue see Ref.
Wu et al. 2015
). We call these conditions the
first and second Taylor criteria

(
Liu et al. 2015
)
, and (
4
) the Taylor lift formula.

Independent of the work of
Taylor 1926
,
Filon 1926
made a thorough analysis of the lift and drag problem for 2D
incompressible and viscous
flow. He
confirmed
that to the leading order the disturbance flow satisfies the Oseen equation (see (
8b
) below), which is valid for an arbitrarily

Re
as long as the distance from the body is sufficiently large. After obtaining the complete solution of Oseen’s equation,
he finally
showed that the lift is the same as (
4
) at infinity,
while the drag is associated with a particular term in the solution, given by

\textbf{\emph{D}}=\rho_{0}\textbf{\emph{U}}Q_{\psi},

(6)

where

Q_{\psi}\equiv-\int_{S}(\textbf{\emph{n}}\times\nabla)\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}\mathrm{d}S=-[\![\psi]\!]

(7)

represents an
inflow
at infinity at the tail.

Now, what LZW has achieved is to extend the above lift and drag formulae
of incompressible flow to compressible and viscous flow.
Starting from (
1
) and to make the decomposition unique, the linearized compressible N-S equation has to be split into a longitudinal
part and a transverse part
(
Lagerstrom et al. 1949
, pp. 30-34)
,

\displaystyle\Pi+\rho_{0}U\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,

(8a)

\displaystyle\left(\nabla^{2}-2k\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\right)\textbf{\emph{u}}_{\psi}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bf 0,

(8b)

where
\Pi=p-\mu_{\theta}\vartheta
is the
modified pressure,
\vartheta=\nabla\cdot\textbf{\emph{u}}
is the dilatation,
k=\rho_{0}U/2\mu
, and
\mu_{\theta}
and
\mu
are the longitudinal and shear viscosities, respectively.
While (
8a
) is the linearized Bernoulli equation, (
8b
) is the transverse Oseen equation. Then,
using
the far-field analysis and the fundamental solution method, LZW
has
proven that the force
F
exerted on the body is

\textbf{\emph{F}}=\rho_{0}\textbf{\emph{U}}\times\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma$}_{\phi}+\rho_{0}\textbf{\emph{U}}Q_{\psi},

(9)

where
\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma$}_{\phi}
and
Q_{\psi}
are given by (
3
) and (
7
), respectively. Obviously, (
9
) is independent of the choice of
S
since both (
3
) and (
7
) are independent of
S
due to the generalized Stokes theorem
(e.g.,
Wu et al. 2006
, p. 700)
. Since no assumption of incompressibility
is used
in the derivation of (
9
), it is also true for compressible flow in a wide range of Mach number, from subsonic to supersonic flows. Due to this
universality
, LZW calls (
9
) the Joukowski-Filon
(J-F)
theorem, which states that the lift and drag are always determined by
\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma$}_{\phi}
and
Q_{\psi}
,
respectively,
no matter how complicated the near-field flow surrounding the body might be.

Unfortunately, velocity potentials

\phi
and
\psi
are not directly observable either experimentally or computationally, and hence neither are the integrands of the J-F theorem. This is why Filon’s drag formula has seldom been noticed in aerodynamics community. But except providing universal and exact force
formulae
, the far-field theories have another task, namely to give asymptotic
formulae that are expressed by observable variables
only. Thus, LZW also derived a testable version of the J-F formula:

\textbf{\emph{F}}\cong\rho_{0}\textbf{\emph{U}}\times\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma$}+\rho_{0}\textbf{\emph{U}}Q_{W},

(10)

where
\Gamma
is given by (
5
),

W
denotes the wake plane
and

Q_{W}\equiv\int_{W}z\omega\mathrm{d}S.

(11)

Evidently,

\Gamma
and
Q_{W}
depend linearly on the vorticity and thus (
10
)
should still hold
in the linear far field of time-averaged
steady
flow such as turbulence,
which has been confirmed by numerical studies (cf. LZW)
.

Owing to this progress, the far-field force theory has for the first time been rigorously extended to
compressible and viscous
flow. The J-F theorem is thus far the only force theory that has the same form in incompressible and compressible flows.
In this sense
, the far-field force theory in two dimensions
is complete
.

I.2
Previous works on far-field force theory in three dimensions

The corresponding incompressible problem in three dimensions has been treated in two papers by
Goldstein 1929
;
Goldstein 1931
, who followed
Filon 1926
to apply the Oseen approximation at a great distance from the solid. In his
1929
paper, Goldstein discussed two series of solutions of the
velocity field
, which corresponds exactly to

\textbf{\emph{u}}_{\phi}
and
\textbf{\emph{u}}_{\psi}
given by (
1
). The first series yields a set of particular integrals of (
8a
),
where
the velocities are associated with certain values of the pressure. In the second series, which is a set of particular integrals of (
8b
) and of the nature of a complementary function, the velocities are rotational while the pressure does not appear. Thus, Filon’s drag formula (
6
) is shown to still
hold. However,
as pointed out by Burgers in one of notes of
Goldstein 1931
, who was thus listed as a coauthor, the solution of
Goldstein 1929
is valid only when the solid body is of revolution, and thus they are not sufficiently general.

In his 1931 paper, Goldstein investigated some more particular integrals, with special emphasis on singular solutions. He showed that for certain values of the pressure, the
longitudinal velocity
\textbf{\emph{u}}_{\phi}
has
singularities, which have to be cancelled by the suitable component of the
transverse
velocity
\textbf{\emph{u}}_{\psi}
.
Furthermore
, he divided
\textbf{\emph{u}}_{\psi}
into three parts,
\textbf{\emph{v}}_{1},\textbf{\emph{v}}_{2}
and
\textbf{\emph{v}}_{3}
, each of which satisfies (
8b
). Then,
\textbf{\emph{v}}_{1}
cancels out the singularities in

\textbf{\emph{u}}_{\phi}
, the sum
\textbf{\emph{v}}_{1}+\textbf{\emph{v}}_{2}
satisfies the condition of continuity
\nabla\cdot(\textbf{\emph{v}}_{1}+\textbf{\emph{v}}_{2})=0
, and
\textbf{\emph{v}}_{3}
satisfies
both the continuity condition and the far-field decaying condition
. Based on these analyses,
Goldstein 1931
obtained three major results:

(i) Filon’s drag (
6
) is found still true in three dimensions.

(ii)
A simple expression for the lift at infinity
is obtained, which is the same as Taylor’s lift formula (
4
).

(iii)
The
force
F
can be expressed as

\textbf{\emph{F}}\cong-\rho_{0}U\int_{W}(\textbf{\emph{v}}_{2}+\textbf{\emph{v}}_{3})\mathrm{d}S,

(12)

so that
D
is the integral of
\rho_{0}\textbf{\emph{U}}\cdot(\textbf{\emph{v}}_{2}+\textbf{\emph{v}}_{3})
over
W
.

Subsequently,
Garstang 1936
obtained the complete
solutions
discussed by
Goldstein 1931
, and thereby proved
\textbf{\emph{v}}_{2}\cdot\textbf{\emph{U}}=0
.
Besides
, in describing the results of
Goldstein 1931
and
Garstang 1936
,
Milne-Thomson 1968
found that
\Gamma
defined by (
5
) can be further reduced to the circulation solely due to
\textbf{\emph{v}}_{2}
.
Namely,
in (
4
) and (
12
) one may set

\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma$}\cong\int_{S}\textbf{\emph{n}}\times\textbf{\emph{v}}_{2}\mathrm{d}S,\quad D\cong-\rho_{0}\int_{W}\textbf{\emph{U}}\cdot\textbf{\emph{v}}_{3}\mathrm{d}S.

(13)

In sharp contrast to 2D
flow, the above results have not yet been pursued to a mature
stage: (i)
There is no universal force formula yet except Filon’s formula (
6
);
(ii) The force formulae (
4
) and (
12
)
are valid only
asymptotically; (iii)
The separate appearance of
\textbf{\emph{v}}_{2}
and
\textbf{\emph{v}}_{3}
in lift and drag of (
13
), respectively, is physically quite strange.
In our view,
the main reason for this embarrassing situation is that these authors did not thoroughly utilize the process decoupling (
1
) and (
8
), as seen from their division of
\textbf{\emph{u}}^{\prime}
into four parts. Besides, nor did they find a simple method for solving far-field equations. Thus we may ask: if we persist the process decoupling and turn to the fundamental-solution method that has been proven for 2D flow to be much neater and more straightforward than those classic techniques, is there any force formula in three dimensions that is as neat and universal as the
J-F theorem (
9
)?
In this paper, we will give a positive answer to this question.

Naturally, the next relevant extension would be compressible flow. Toward this goal and within three dimensions, among others,
Finn and Gilbarg 1957
have proven rigorously for subsonic nonlinear potential flow that the fluid exerts no net force to the body, which may be termed a “d’Alembert-like paradox” but is of course not the case for viscous flow. Then, based on some plausible assumptions,
Lagerstrom 1964
has proposed that (
4
) should give the lift for viscous compressible flow.

Having reviewed these pieces of progress of 3D far-field analyses, we may conclude that so far no profound and universal force theory is available. In other words, the far-field force theory in three dimensions is still far from complete.

I.3
Our work and this paper

In the rest of this paper we extend
LZW’s theory
to three dimensions in the same way.
In Section
II
we observe that the velocity potentials
\phi
and
\psi
must be singular
, for otherwise the body would be force-free. We then confirm the effectiveness
of Taylor’s
lift formula (
4
), as well as a drag formula,
for 3D viscous and compressible flow
. In Section
III
we introduce the fundamental solutions of the linearized N-S equations and make a detailed analysis of the
transverse
far-field
so that the singularity of
\phi
and
\psi
is proved rigorously.
Then we arrive at a profound universal force formula, which we state as the
unified force theorem
(UF theorem for short). This neat theorem is however not yet a complete aerodynamic theory since it is not directly testable or measurable.
In Section
IV
, therefore, we propose a type of testable UF formula, which is expressed solely by observable variables.
In Section
V
we provide a simple physical explanation of the singularity in incompressible flow with a concrete model, which is the essence of the universality of the UF theorem. A basic principle to increase the lift-drag ratio of
2D and 3D flows
is proposed in Section
VI
.
Conclusions
are given in Section
VII
.

To be self-contained, the fundamental solution of 3D steady linearized N-S equations is given in Appendix

.

\displaystyle n=2:

\displaystyle\int_{W}u_{\psi x}\mathrm{d}S=-\int_{W}\frac{\partial u_{\psi x}}{\partial z}z\mathrm{d}z\approx-\int_{W}z\omega_{y}\mathrm{d}S,

(23a)

\displaystyle n=3:

\displaystyle\int_{W}u_{\psi x}\mathrm{d}S=\color[rgb]{0,0,0}-\int_{W}\frac{\partial u_{\psi x}}{\partial\sigma}\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\mathrm{d}\sigma\mathrm{d}\theta\approx\frac{1}{2}\int_{W}\sigma\omega_{\theta}\mathrm{d}S,

(23b)

where
(x,\sigma,\theta)
is the cylindrical coordinates, and
\sigma\omega_{\theta}=y\omega_{z}-z\omega_{y}
for
n=3
.
Thus by (
22
), (
21
)
reduces
to

\textbf{\emph{F}}\cong\rho_{0}\textbf{\emph{U}}\times\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma$}+\rho_{0}\textbf{\emph{U}}Q_{W},

(24)

where
\Gamma
is
still
given by (
5
) and

Q_{W}\equiv\frac{\rho_{0}}{n-1}\int_{W}(z\omega_{y}-y\omega_{z})\mathrm{d}S,

(25)

with
n=2,3
being the space dimensionality.
This result
obviously includes and extends the J-F formula (
10
),
indicating that
we arrive at a unified far-field asymptotic force formula for both 2D and 3D flows. Thus, we name (
24
) the
testable unified force formula
. While thus far
(
20
) and (
24
) are
derived under the assumed existence of linear far field,
this assumption will be rigorously proved in the next two sections from low-speed to supersonic flow.

III
Unified force theorem

In this section we consider the
viscous
flow over a
finite
body, for which
Lagerstrom 1964

pointed
out that the linearization is feasible but without proof. The first proof
is
given by LZW for two dimensions,
using the fundamental solution of
the linear far field
of viscous and compressible flow.
This method is now applied to three dimensions, which will lead to a force formula universally true for
both 2D and 3D flows
.

III.1
Fundamental solution method

For an observer in very far field, a body moving through a fluid appears as a
singular point
,
its
action on the fluid appears as an
impulse force
,
and
the far-field disturbance flow is sufficiently weak and
governed
by linearized N-S equations.
Then,
to calculate the impulse force there is no need to solve these equations under specified boundary conditions. Rather, it suffices to directly use the
corresponding

fundamental solution
in free space. This is the basic idea in the study of linear differential equations, which is called
fundamental solution method
and has been successfully demonstrated by LZW for two dimensions.

Following LZW, we introduce (primed) disturbance quantities by

\textbf{\emph{u}}=U\textbf{\emph{e}}_{x}+\textbf{\emph{u}}^{\prime},\quad\rho=\rho_{0}(1+\rho^{\prime}),

(26)

then the steady momentum and continuity equations are

\displaystyle\left(\nu_{\theta}\mathbf{T}_{\psi}-\nu\mathbf{T}_{\phi}-U\partial_{x}\mathbf{I}\right)\cdot\textbf{\emph{u}}^{\prime}-c^{2}\nabla\rho^{\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\textbf{\emph{f}},

(27a)

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\textbf{\emph{u}}^{\prime}+U\partial_{x}\rho^{\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,

(27b)

where

\displaystyle\mathbf{T}_{\phi}=\nabla\nabla,\quad\mathbf{T}_{\psi}=\nabla\nabla-\nabla^{2}\mathbf{I},\quad\mathbf{I}=\textrm{unit tensor},

(27c)

and
\nu=\mu/\rho_{0}
and
\nu_{\theta}=\mu_{\theta}/\rho_{0}
are the constant
shear
and longitudinal kinematic viscosities, respectively,
c
is the speed of sound, and
f
represents an external body force, which in our case is the force exerted to the fluid by the body. In near-field formulation
f
could have a compact distribution in
(\textbf{\emph{x}},t)
-space as used by
Saffman 1992
, but below it will be idealized as a
\delta
-function of
x
, i.e.,

\textbf{\emph{f}}=-\frac{\delta(\textbf{\emph{x}})}{\rho_{0}}\textbf{\emph{F}},

(28)

where
F
is the total force
experienced by
the body,

\rho_{0}
is the fluid density at infinity, and

\int\delta(\textbf{\emph{x}})\mathrm{d}\textbf{\emph{x}}=1.

(29)

Denote
\mathbf{G}
as the fundamental solution of (
27
) for
\textbf{\emph{u}}^{\prime}
,
then

\textbf{\emph{u}}^{\prime}(\textbf{\emph{x}})=\int\mathbf{G}(\textbf{\emph{x}},\textbf{\emph{x}}^{\prime})\cdot\textbf{\emph{f}}(\textbf{\emph{x}}^{\prime})\mathrm{d}\textbf{\emph{x}}^{\prime}.

(30)

Since

\textbf{\emph{u}}^{\prime}
can be split into a longitudinal part and a transverse
part, see (
1
), it can be verified that
\mathbf{G}
can also be split into longitudinal and
transverse
parts,

\mathbf{G}(\textbf{\emph{x}},\textbf{\emph{x}}^{\prime})=\mathbf{G}_{\phi}(\textbf{\emph{x}},\textbf{\emph{x}}^{\prime})+\mathbf{G}_{\psi}(\textbf{\emph{x}},\textbf{\emph{x}}^{\prime}),

(31)

so that the longitudinal and
transverse
velocities defined by (
1
) now read

\displaystyle\textbf{\emph{u}}_{\phi}=\nabla\phi(\textbf{\emph{x}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\mathbf{G}_{\phi}(\textbf{\emph{x}},\textbf{\emph{x}}^{\prime})\cdot\textbf{\emph{f}}(\textbf{\emph{x}}^{\prime})\mathrm{d}\textbf{\emph{x}}^{\prime},

(32a)

\displaystyle\textbf{\emph{u}}_{\psi}=\nabla\times\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\mathbf{G}_{\psi}(\textbf{\emph{x}},\textbf{\emph{x}}^{\prime})\cdot\textbf{\emph{f}}(\textbf{\emph{x}}^{\prime})\mathrm{d}\textbf{\emph{x}}^{\prime}.

(32b)

Here (see Appendix
A
),

\displaystyle\mathbf{G}_{\phi}(\textbf{\emph{x}},\textbf{\emph{x}}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathbf{T}_{\phi}\left[\frac{e^{i\xi x}}{i\xi U}K_{0}\left(\sigma\sqrt{\xi^{2}+\frac{i\xi U}{\nu_{\theta}+\frac{c^{2}}{i\xi U}}}\right)\right]\mathrm{d}\xi,

(33a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{G}_{\psi}(\textbf{\emph{x}},\textbf{\emph{x}}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathbf{T}_{\psi}\left[\frac{e^{i\xi x}}{i\xi U}K_{0}\left(\sigma\sqrt{\xi^{2}+\frac{i\xi U}{\nu}}\right)\right]\mathrm{d}\xi

(33b)

are the fundamental solutions for the longitudinal and
transverse
processes, respectively, and
K_{0}
is the modified Bessel function of the second kind.

Compared to dealing with

\textbf{\emph{u}}^{\prime}
directly, we find it sometimes more convenient to deal with

\phi
and
\psi
.
By substituting (
28
) and (
33
) into (
32
), we can obtain

\displaystyle\phi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{4\pi\rho_{0}U}\textbf{\emph{F}}\cdot\nabla\Phi,

(34a)

\psi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{4\pi\rho_{0}U}\textbf{\emph{F}}\times\nabla\Psi,

(34b)

where

\displaystyle\Phi

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\pi}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{e^{i\xi x}}{i\xi}K_{0}\left(\sigma\sqrt{\xi^{2}+\frac{i\xi U}{\nu_{\theta}+\frac{c^{2}}{i\xi U}}}\right)\mathrm{d}\xi,

(35a)

\displaystyle\Psi

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\pi}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{e^{i\xi x}}{i\xi}K_{0}\left(\sigma\sqrt{\xi^{2}+\frac{i\xi U}{\nu}}\right)\mathrm{d}\xi.

(35b)

Here, since the integrals of (
35a
) and (
35b
) are divergent in general, following
Hadamard 1928
the symbol
\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int
is used to denote the finite part of divergent
integral.

III.2
The transverse far-field

By differentiating (
35b
) with respect to
x
, there is
(
Gradshteyn and Ryzhik 2007
, p. 722)

\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial x}=\frac{e^{-k(r-x)}}{r}\equiv\chi,

(36)

where

k=U/2\nu
and
r=\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}}
.
Then from (
36
) we have

\Psi=\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int_{-\infty}^{x}\chi(t,y,z)\mathrm{d}t=\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int_{r-x}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-kt}}{t}\mathrm{d}t,

(37)

where we have used the upstream decaying condition
\Psi(-\infty,y,z)=0
. Of course, (
37
) is independent of Mach number and divergent at the positive
x
-axis
(r-x=0)
, where the singular transverse velocity must be canceled by the longitudinal velocity.

By substituting (
34b
) into (
32b
), the transverse velocity is

\displaystyle\textbf{\emph{u}}_{\psi}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\pi\rho_{0}U}[\nabla(\textbf{\emph{F}}\cdot\nabla\Psi)-\textbf{\emph{F}}\nabla^{2}\Psi].

(38)

Since

\nabla^{2}\Psi=2k\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial x}=2k\chi,

(39)

(
38
) yields

\displaystyle\textbf{\emph{u}}_{\psi}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\pi\rho_{0}U}\nabla(\textbf{\emph{F}}\cdot\nabla\Psi)+\textbf{\emph{v}},

(40)

where

\textbf{\emph{v}}\equiv-\frac{1}{4\pi\mu}\chi\textbf{\emph{F}}

(41)

is the purely rotational velocity.

Then, by (
40
) and (
41
) the vorticity is

\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}=\nabla\times\textbf{\emph{v}}=\frac{1}{4\pi\mu}\textbf{\emph{F}}\times\nabla\chi.

(42)

Because
\nabla\chi
is the only vorticity source term in (
42
),
\chi
is called the
vorticity potential
, which was first introduced by
Lamb 1911
for the linearized far-field of steady axis-symmetrical flow. We now see it does exist for the linearized far-field of
any
steady 3D flow.
In particular,

\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\int_{W}\chi\mathrm{d}S=\frac{2\pi}{k}=\rm const.,

(43)

so the wake-plane integral of vorticity vanishes:

\int_{W}\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}\mathrm{d}S=\frac{\textbf{\emph{F}}\times\textbf{\emph{e}}_{x}}{4\pi\mu}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}\int_{W}\chi\mathrm{d}S=\bf 0.

(44)

As a check of our algebra, we substitute (
36
) into (
42
) to obtain

\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}=\frac{k}{4\pi\mu}\frac{e^{-k(r-x)}}{r}\nabla(r-x)\times\textbf{\emph{F}}+O\left(\frac{e^{-k(r-x)}}{r^{2}}\right),

(45)

which agrees exactly the asymptotic vorticity expression obtained by
Babenko and Vasil’ev 1973
for incompressible flow
(
Mizumachi 1984
, see also)
. Then, in the
x
-axis there is

\omega_{x}(x,0,0)=0,\quad\omega_{y}(x,0,0)=-\frac{F_{z}}{4\pi\mu x^{2}},\quad\omega_{z}(x,0,0)=\frac{F_{y}}{4\pi\mu x^{2}}.

(46)

Thus the lift and side force can also be written as

F_{y}=4\pi\mu x^{2}\omega_{z}(x,0,0),\quad F_{z}=-4\pi\mu x^{2}\omega_{y}(x,0,0).

(47)

Furthermore,
from (
41
) and (
43
) we have

\textbf{\emph{F}}\cong-\rho_{0}U\int_{W}\textbf{\emph{v}}\mathrm{d}S,

(48)

which has
the same form as (
12
)
since
\textbf{\emph{v}}=\textbf{\emph{v}}_{2}+\textbf{\emph{v}}_{3}
. But now it is also valid for compressible flow.

Since

v
is the purely rotational part of
\textbf{\emph{u}}^{\prime}
, we may expect that
F
can be solely expressed by the vorticity. This will be discussed
thoroughly
in Section
IV
.

III.3
Unified force theorem

With the above preparations, we can now state the following innovative theorem:

Unified force theorem
.
For an
n
-dimensional steady flow of viscous and compressible fluid over a rigid body,
n=2,3
, the
forces
exerted to the body are solely determined by the multi-valueness and singularities of the velocity potential
\phi
in the circulation
\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma$}_{\phi}
and the vortical stream function
\psi
in the inflow
Q_{\psi}
:

\textbf{\emph{F}}=(n-1)\rho_{0}\textbf{\emph{U}}\times\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma$}_{\phi}+\rho_{0}\textbf{\emph{U}}Q_{\psi},

(49)

where
\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma$}_{\phi}
and
Q_{\psi}
are given by the first expressions of (
3
) and (
7
), respectively, and are independent of the choice of control surface
S
.

Proof.
First, as remarked previously, either
\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma$}_{\phi}
or
Q_{\psi}
vanishes due to the generalized Stokes theorem, unless
\phi
or
\psi
are multi-valued or singular somewhere. This multi-valueness or singularity is independent of the integral surface
S
, and hence so is (
49
). In fact,
S
can even be located in the nonlinear near field as long as the definition domain of
\phi
and
\psi
is properly extended; but the proof of the theorem can be made in the linearized far field where the formal solution (
34
) is valid.

Then, to prove (
49
), we only need to show that for
n=3
there is

\textbf{\emph{F}}=2\rho_{0}\textbf{\emph{U}}\times\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma$}_{\phi}+\rho_{0}\textbf{\emph{U}}Q_{\psi},

(50)

since then a comparison of (
50
) and (
20
) implies

\rho_{0}\int_{S}\textbf{\emph{n}}\times\nabla(\textbf{\emph{U}}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$})\mathrm{d}S=\rho_{0}\textbf{\emph{U}}\times\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma$}_{\phi},

(51)

and thus (
49
) follows at once. Note that after the existence of linear far field was proven in
Subsections

III.1
and
III.2
, (
20
) has become a rigorous result and can be cited here.

To prove (
50
),
we substitute (
40
) and (
34a
) into (
1
), such that

\textbf{\emph{u}}^{\prime}=-\frac{1}{4\pi\rho_{0}U}\nabla[\textbf{\emph{F}}\cdot\nabla(\Phi-\Psi)]+\textbf{\emph{v}}.

(52)

Since
\textbf{\emph{u}}^{\prime}
and
v
must be regular, so must
be

\textbf{\emph{F}}\cdot\nabla(\Phi-\Psi)
.
If we
rewrite (
34a
) as

\phi=-\frac{1}{4\pi\rho_{0}U}[\textbf{\emph{F}}\cdot\nabla(\Phi-\Psi)+\textbf{\emph{F}}\cdot\nabla\Psi],

(53)

then the first term makes
no contribution to
\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma$}_{\phi}
.
Thus
(
3
) reduces to (see
B
)

\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma$}_{\phi}=-\frac{1}{4\pi\rho_{0}U}\int_{S}(\textbf{\emph{n}}\times\nabla)(\textbf{\emph{F}}\cdot\nabla\Psi)\mathrm{d}S=\frac{\textbf{\emph{F}}\times\textbf{\emph{e}}_{x}}{2\rho_{0}U}.

(54)

Similarly, by substituting (
34b
)
into
(
7
), we find (
see

B
)

Q_{\psi}=\frac{1}{4\pi\rho_{0}U}\int_{S}(\textbf{\emph{n}}\times\nabla)\cdot(\textbf{\emph{F}}\times\nabla\Psi)\mathrm{d}S=\frac{\textbf{\emph{F}}\cdot\textbf{\emph{e}}_{x}}{\rho_{0}U}.

(55)

Obviously, both
\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma$}_{\phi}
and
Q_{\psi}
are indeed independent of
S
.
The proof is thus completed.

In the above proof we only used the specific behavior of
\Psi
. This approach makes the proof concise and general, but leaves the relevant physical mechanisms of lift and drag obscure. Thus, to explore the underlying physics we still have to directly analyse the disturbance velocity. We do this in the next section.

Before proceeding, we remark that the K-J theorem, the most famous prototype of far-field theory, was basically regarded as the neatest model of engineering theory in the past. Its significance was underestimated due to the ignorance of the normative role of the far-field theories. The reason of this ignorance might come from two facts: (1) There is no far-field theory for inviscid supersonic aerodynamics before and thus its power has not been totally realized; (2) One has now been used to relying heavily on computational fluid dynamics (CFD) but often overlooks its shortages. Therefore, the unified force theorem represents a breakthrough of classic inviscid high-speed aerodynamics, which has set a universal norm for all theories of aerodynamic forces in viscous and compressible steady external flow. To make this normative role indeed take effect in practices, some basic questions are still awaiting to be answered. For example, how does the flow evolve between nonlinear near field and linear far field? Or similarly, how do the near-field theories transform smoothly to the far-field theories? Only with this question being answered can the problem of far-field boundary conditions involved in CFD be completely rationalized. However, these important issues won’t be discussed any longer in the present paper.

IV
Testable unified force formula

In this section, we shall analyze the longitudinal far-field flow structures, estimate the distances of the linear far field from the body and clarify the far-field behavior of multiple circulations. After that, we further confirm that (
24
) is the far-field asymptotic approximation of (
49
), which is expressed solely in terms of observable vorticity field.

IV.1
The longitudinal far-field

By differentiating (
35a
) with respect to
x
, there is

\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial x}=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{i\xi x}K_{0}\left(\sigma\sqrt{Ae^{i\theta}}\right)\mathrm{d}\xi,

(56)

where

Ae^{i\theta}\equiv\xi^{2}+\frac{i\xi U}{\nu_{\theta}+\frac{c^{2}}{i\xi U}}=(1-M^{2})\xi^{2}+i\frac{\nu_{\theta}M^{4}}{U}\xi^{3}+O(\nu_{\theta}^{2}),\quad M=\frac{U}{c}.

(57)

Obviously, the longitudinal field described by
\Phi
depends explicitly on Mach number
M
, as seen from the key factors
1-M^{2}
and
M^{4}
in (
57
).

IV.1.1
Subsonic flow

For subsonic flow, there is

A\cong\beta^{2}\xi^{2},\quad\theta\cong 0,\quad\beta^{2}=1-M^{2}>0.

(58)

Then

\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial x}=\frac{1}{r_{\beta}},\quad r_{\beta}=\sqrt{x^{2}+\beta^{2}(y^{2}+z^{2})}.

(59)

Thus we have

\Phi=\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int_{r_{\beta}-x}^{\infty}\frac{1}{t}\mathrm{d}t=-\ln(r_{\beta}-x).

(60)

The longitudinal potential
\phi
is

\phi=\frac{1}{4\pi\rho_{0}U}\textbf{\emph{F}}\cdot\nabla\ln(r_{\beta}-x),

(61)

which is singular at the positive
x
-axis and of which the singular longitudinal velocity can just cancel out that of the transverse field.

IV.1.2
Supersonic flow

For supersonic flow, there is

A\cong B^{2}\xi^{2},\quad\theta\cong\pi-2\Lambda\xi,\quad B^{2}=M^{2}-1>0,\quad\Lambda=\frac{\nu_{\theta}M^{4}}{2B^{2}U}\ll 1.

(62)

Now, we need to find such a viscous solution that is significant only near the Mach cone and decays exponentially elsewhere except in wake region. Firstly, note that

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial x}\cong\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{i\xi x}K_{0}\left(\Lambda B\sigma\xi^{2}+iB\sigma\xi\right)\mathrm{d}\xi,

(63)

then the far-field decaying condition can be ensured since
\Lambda B\sigma\xi^{2}\geq 0
, indicating that the viscous effect has a vital role in supersonic flow. However, (
63
) is hard to integrate explicitly. Instead, we consider its approximation at
\sigma=\sqrt{y^{2}+z^{2}}\gg 1
by the asymptotic identity

\displaystyle K_{0}(z)\cong\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2z}}e^{-z},\quad z\rightarrow\infty.

(64)

Then (
63
) reduces to

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial x}\cong\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi B\sigma}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\cos\left[\xi(x-B\sigma)-\frac{\pi}{4}\right]e^{-\Lambda B\sigma\xi^{2}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\xi}{\sqrt{\xi}}.

(65)

Although the integral of (
65
) can be worked out explicitly, here we left it out since its form is somewhat complicated and inconvenient to analyze.
Instead, suppose
\textbf{\emph{F}}=D\textbf{\emph{e}}_{x}
and substitute it into (
34a
), we have

\displaystyle\phi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{D}{4\pi\rho_{0}U}\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial x}.

(66)

Then from (
65
) and (
66
) and denote
C_{D}=2D/\rho_{0}U^{2}
, there is

\displaystyle\frac{1}{U}\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x}

\displaystyle\cong

\displaystyle\frac{C_{D}}{8\pi}\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi B\sigma}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\sin\left[\xi(x-B\sigma)-\frac{\pi}{4}\right]e^{-\Lambda B\sigma\xi^{2}}\sqrt{\xi}\mathrm{d}\xi.

(67)

Since the disturbance must be largest along the Mach cone, there is

\displaystyle\left.\frac{1}{U}\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x}\right|_{x=B\sigma}\cong-\frac{G(3/4)C_{D}}{16\pi^{\frac{3}{2}}\Lambda^{\frac{3}{4}}B^{\frac{5}{4}}\sigma^{\frac{5}{4}}}.

(68)

where
G(\cdot)
is the Gamma function and
G(3/4)=1.22542\cdots
.

IV.1.3
Sonic flow

The viscosity is also necessary for sonic flow, where

\sqrt{Ae^{i\theta}}=\sqrt{\frac{|\xi|^{3}}{Re_{\theta}}}e^{i\frac{\pi}{4}\,\textrm{sgn\,}\xi},\quad Re_{\theta}=\frac{U}{\nu_{\theta}},

(69)

and

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial x}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left[K_{0}\left(\sigma\sqrt{\frac{\xi^{3}}{Re_{\theta}}}e^{-i\frac{\pi}{4}}\right)e^{-i\xi x}+K_{0}\left(\sigma\sqrt{\frac{\xi^{3}}{Re_{\theta}}}e^{i\frac{\pi}{4}}\right)e^{i\xi x}\right]\mathrm{d}\xi,

(70)

which for
\sigma\gg 1
reduces to

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial x}

\displaystyle\cong

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi\sigma}}Re_{\theta}^{\frac{1}{4}}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\sigma\sqrt{\frac{\xi^{3}}{2Re_{\theta}}}}\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{8}-\xi x+\sigma\sqrt{\frac{\xi^{3}}{2Re_{\theta}}}\right)\frac{\mathrm{d}\xi}{\xi^{\frac{3}{4}}}.

(71)

Furthermore, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}\Phi(0,\sigma)}{\partial x^{2}}\cong\sqrt{\frac{2}{3\pi}}Re_{\theta}^{\frac{2}{3}}\frac{G(5/6)}{\sigma^{\frac{4}{3}}},

(72)

where
G(5/6)=1.12879\cdots
.
Thus from (
66
) and (
72
), there is

\displaystyle\left.\frac{1}{U}\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x}\right|_{x=0}\cong-\frac{G(5/6)C_{D}Re_{\theta}^{\frac{2}{3}}}{4\sqrt{6}\pi^{\frac{3}{2}}\sigma^{\frac{4}{3}}}.

(73)

IV.2
Distance of linear far-field from the body

We now use the preceding solutions of linear equations to predict how large the
minimum distance

r_{m}=\sqrt{x^{2}_{m}+\sigma^{2}_{m}}
from the body should be for them to become valid. The estimate is based on a simple requirement that the magnitude of
\textbf{\emph{u}}^{\prime}
, after being non-dimensionalized, is not larger than unity. For simplicity, we may assume only drag exists. Meanwhile, the corresponding 2D estimates given by LZW are also listed for comparison.

Let the characteristic length scale be unity so that
C_{D}=2D/\rho_{0}U^{2}
,
Re=U/\nu
and
Re_{\theta}=U/\nu_{\theta}
. Then, the minimum streamwise distance
x_{m}
of the linear far field can be obtained directly from (
52
) by setting
\sigma=0
and requiring
|\textbf{\emph{v}}|/U=1
:

x_{m}=O\left(\frac{C_{D}Re}{8\pi}\right),

(74)

which is independent of Mach number and fully determined by transverse process. This is comparable with the 2D estimate
r_{m}=O(C^{2}_{D}Re/16\pi)
.

On the other hand, the lateral minimum distance
\sigma_{m}
is dominated by longitudinal process, which can be determined from (
61
), (
68
) and (
73
), respectively:

(1) Subsonic far-field:

\sigma_{m}=O\left(\sqrt{\frac{C_{D}}{8\pi\beta}}\right),

(75a)

which is comparable with the 2D estimate
r_{m}=O(C_{D}/4\pi\beta)

IV.4
Testable unified force formula

Since the

and

Now the preceding analyses of the flow behaviour have revealed that the possibility lies in the linear far field, of which the existence has been confirmed by the estimates made in Subsection

.
For an

, the force exerted on the body is given by (

.
1. One of the proofs of the TUF formula is given in Section

V
Physical carrier of singularity: Trailing vortex pair

From the TUF formula (
24
), we see that the forces are expressible solely in terms of vorticity integrals, from incompressible regime all the way to supersonic regime, no matter what complex processes and structures such as shocks, entropy gradient and curved-shock generated vorticity field may occur. In other words, only vorticity leaves signature in far field since it decays in the wake most slowly and, what is more remarkably, because vorticity is a transverse quantity, (
24
) is completely independent of Mach number (the specific
M
-dependence of vorticity can only be identified by near-field flow behavior).

This being the case, we may well use the familiar difference in the behaviours of
incompressible
vorticity field for
n=2
and 3 to interpret the distinction in (
20
) and (
81
). For
n=2
, as discussed in details by LZW, vorticity lines are all straight and along the spanwise direction, making the last term of (
20
) vanishes without any singularity. Nevertheless, the flow domain is doubly-connected, permitting multiple values of
\phi
and
\psi
. This makes it inevitable that a body experiencing a force must have nonzero
[\![\phi]\!]
and/or
[\![\psi]\!]
, which are responsible for the lift and drag, respectively, and surely independent of the choice of control surface. In contrast, for
n=3
, vorticity lines can be stretched and tilted, and eventually go to far field with
\omega_{x}=-\nabla^{2}\psi_{x}
being the dominating component there, as indicated by the first term of (
81
). This explains why there is an extra term in (
20
) for
n=3
: a nonzero
\textbf{\emph{U}}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}=U\psi_{x}
implies a nonzero
\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma$}_{\psi}
, which gives half of
\Gamma
(total vorticity in
V_{\rm st}
) at far field with
r\rightarrow\infty
. Now, the flow domain is singly-connected, permitting no multi-valueness of
\phi
and
\psi
. Thus, the only possible mechanism for providing nonzero forces and being independent of the choice of control surface is the singularity of
\phi
and
\psi
, of which the only physical carrier must be the trailing vortex pair for any lifting body.

While a complete analysis of the physical carrier of the singularity for compressible flow is too difficult to be done if not impossible, owing to the
M
-independency of (
81
) the interpretation first presented by
Goldstein 1931
for incompressible is sufficient and worth recapitulating. We do this by a concrete line-vortex doublet model, which is a physical idealization of a vortex filament bounding an infinitely small plane area and of which the strength is equal to the product of the area and the strength of the vortex filament. Suppose
\textbf{\emph{F}}=L\textbf{\emph{e}}_{z}
and set
\beta=1
, then (
61
) reduces to

\phi=\frac{L}{4\pi\rho_{0}U}\frac{z}{r(r-x)},

(82)

which must be the dominant term in (
1
) for
y^{2}\gg 1
since the transverse field decays exponentially. Then, in a footnote,
Goldstein 1931
asserted that
z/r(r-x)
gives the potential of a line doublet stretching from the origin along the
x
-axis to plus infinity. Obviously, Goldstein’s vortex doublet is precisely the far-field picture of the familiar trailing vortex pair, which can be most intuitively visualized with our fundamental-solution method where the body is shrunk to a singular point.

We now show that this vortex doublet is indeed the only possible source of singularity. Suppose that there is a vortex filament
C
with strength
\Gamma
and width
b
such that
\Gamma b
is fixed as
b\rightarrow 0
. Then the velocity
\textbf{\emph{u}}^{\prime}
induced by
C
is
(
Wu et al. 2006
, p. 81)

\textbf{\emph{u}}^{\prime}=\frac{\Gamma}{4\pi}\oint_{C}\frac{\textbf{\emph{t}}\times\textbf{\emph{r}}^{\prime}}{r^{\prime 3}}\mathrm{d}s=\frac{\Gamma b\textbf{\emph{e}}_{z}}{4\pi}\frac{1}{r(r-x)}.

(83)

Note that
\textbf{\emph{u}}^{\prime}
has only one component
u^{\prime}_{z}
since fluids are entrained from the outside of the doublet forming “upwash” and pumped into it forming “downwash”. But the downwash phenomenon can not be observed from (
83
) due to our assumption:
\Gamma\rightarrow\infty
and
b\rightarrow 0
but
\Gamma b
remains fixed. Now consider both
y^{2}\gg 1
and
z^{2}\ll 1

\phi\cong\frac{\Gamma b}{4\pi}\frac{z}{r(r-x)}.

(84)

Recall that in lifting-line theory there is

Actually, from the TUF formula (

VI
Basic principle to increase the lift-drag ratio

As is well-known that, one of the key concerns in aerodynamics is the lift-drag ratio
L/D
. It represents the efficiency of aircraft: the larger
L/D
implies the better aerodynamic performance and the longer flight range. Evidently,
L/D
depends on various parameters (e.g., the angle of attack and the flight speed). For typical airplanes, this ratio is
17\sim 18
for low-speed and subsonic flights and
10\sim 12
for transonic flight. While for the aforementioned
Concorde
at supersonic speed it is
4\sim 8
.

We now use the TUF formula (
24
) to give a basic estimate of
L/D
solely in terms of the far-field vorticity distribution, which is applicable to all aircrafts in steady flight at large range of Mach number. For 2D flow, it can be written as

\frac{L}{D}=\frac{\displaystyle\int_{V_{\rm st}}\omega\mathrm{d}V}{\displaystyle\int_{W}z\omega\mathrm{d}z}.

(86)

This means that, the basic principle to increase
L/D
should be:
to increase the total vorticity in the steady region that is directly related to the lift as large as possible, and to decrease the vorticity moment in the wake plane that is directly related to the drag as small as possible
. One of the typical examples is the supercritical airfoil: through special configuration design, the onset of shock waves in the transonic speed range is significantly delayed and the shock-induced boundary-layer separation is greatly suppressed. As a consequence of the former, the total vorticity in the steady region is larger than that of the common airfoil under the same flow conditions. Meanwhile, as a consequence of the latter, the vorticity moment in the wake plane is also greatly reduced. Thus,
L/D
is increased.

For 3D flow, the above principle should also be applicable. However, since the vorticity lines or tubes in three dimensions must be stretched and tilted, the corresponding physics behind
L/D
involves much more abundant mechanisms than that in two dimensions. For instance, from (
24
) and (
81
),
L/D
can be expressed as

\frac{L}{D}=\frac{\displaystyle\int_{V_{\rm st}}\omega_{y}\mathrm{d}V}{\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{W}(z\omega_{y}-y\omega_{z})\mathrm{d}S}=\frac{\displaystyle\int_{W}y\omega_{x}\mathrm{d}S}{\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{W}(z\omega_{y}-y\omega_{z})\mathrm{d}S}.

(87)

The first equality has the same structure as that in (
86
), confirming that the previous principle indeed applies to 3D flow, and thus needs no further discussion. The second equality is unique for 3D flow and desires more explanations. Take a steady incompressible or subsonic flow over a wing as an example. In the far field, as argued in Section
V
, trailing vortex pair forms where
\omega_{x}
dominates. Although the strength of the trailing vortex is almost constant, the magnitude of
\omega_{x}
itself decreases along the downstream direction through the viscous diffusion. Due to the divergence-free condition
\nabla\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}=0
, therefore, the vortex must be accompanied by relatively small but nonzero
\omega_{y}
and
\omega_{z}
. Now, suppose that the vorticity field in the wake plane
W
is similar for different aircrafts under the same flow conditions. Meanwhile, it is also proper to assume that the typical values of
y
and
z
in the integrands of (
87
) are proportional to the characteristic scales of the wingspan
b
and thickness
h
of the wing. Thus, we may conclude that:
the larger of the ratio of characteristic wingspan to characteristic thickness of the lifting-body, the better will be its performance
. This should be one of the design principle of aircraft for low-speed or subsonic flight.

It should be pointed out that for transonic and supersonic flights this simple principle of increasing
b/h
may not work well. This is because vorticities generated by shocks would have a very large spatial extension (especially along the vertical-up direction), making the typical value of
z
no longer be characterized by
h
although that of
y
is still characterized by
b
. For hypersonic flight, however, this principle should resume to be effective as shock waves become very close to the body surface, recovering the characteristic role of
h
.

VII
Conclusions

In this theoretical paper, we have studied the forces experienced by a body in a steady flow of viscous and compressible fluid. The major findings are summarized as follows:

1. A unified force theorem has been obtained and proved to hold universally for both two and three dimensions. It states that the forces exerted on the body are unified determined by the vector circulation
\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma$}_{\phi}
of longitudinal velocity and scalar inflow
Q_{\psi}
of transverse velocity, both being independent of the choice of control surface used to calculate the circulation and inflow.

2. The far-field asymptotic form of the exact unified force formula has also been obtained, which is solely expressed by vorticity integrals and valid if the control surface lies in the linear far field. Its form is also independent of the Reynolds number and Mach number. This result is a reflection of the inherent flow physics: no matter how many interacting processes could appear in the nonlinear near-field flow, only the vorticity field has the farthest downstream extension and leaves signature in the far field.

3. The unified force formula and its far-field asymptotics contain explicitly the space dimensionality, so the expressions of forces are never the same for two and three dimensions. This fact is a result of the intrinsic difference of flow patterns in two and three dimensions. Unlike two dimensions where the forces come solely from the multi-valueness of velocity potentials
\phi
and
\psi
in doubly-connected flow domain, now in three dimensions they come solely from the singularity of
\phi
and
\psi
in singly-connected flow domain. As the body shrinks to a point in the far field, the trailing vortex pair degenerates to a line-vortex doublet of vanishingly width, which is the only physical carrier of singularity in both incompressible and compressible flows.
