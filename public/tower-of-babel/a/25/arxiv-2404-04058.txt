Alternative Proof of the Determinant of Complete Non-Ambiguous Trees
D Chen

Abstract
. Complete non-ambiguous trees have been studied in various contexts. Recently, a conjecture was made about their determinants, and subsequently proved by Aval. An alternative proof is given here.



1.
Introduction

Complete non-ambiguous trees (CNATs) are a combinatorial object of recent interest
[
ABBS14
]
. They may be seen as the proper way to embed a complete binary tree into a grid (see
2.1
).
Recently, a study of these objects was launched in
[
CO24
]
, in which a conjecture was made that the determinant is evenly distributed for odd-size CNATs. This was subsequently proved bijectively in
[
Ava23
]
.

In this short article, first some self-contained context to the problem is given by stating the background definitions and conjecture. Then an alternative proof is given.

2.
Definitions

The definition of a (complete) non-ambiguous tree is recalled from
[
ABBS14
]
as follows:

Definition 2.1
.

A
non-ambiguous tree
is a filling of a rectangular grid in which each cell may or may not contain a dot, such that the following constraints are satisfied:

(
Existence of a root
) The top-left cell (the
root
) is dotted.

(
Non-ambiguity
) For every dotted cell (
vertex
) other than the root, there is another vertex either in the same column and above, or in the same row and to the left, but not both.

(
Minimality
) Every row and column contains at least one vertex.

By connecting each vertex to its unique precursor in the same row or column, a rooted binary tree is formed, thus motivating the name
non-ambiguous tree
. If the underlying binary tree is complete, then the object is referred to as a
complete non-ambiguous tree
(CNAT).

This implies that a CNAT with
2n-1
vertices has
n
leaves and
n-1
internal vertices. Furthermore, every row and column has exactly one leaf, thus a CNAT with
2n-1
vertices is contained in an
n\times n
grid. We refer to the CNAT as having size
n
, or dimensions
n\times n
.

Figure 2.1
.
A 4x4 CNAT with leaf vertices highlighted in blue

Definition 2.2

(Leaf Matrices)
.

A given CNAT,
M
, can be viewed as a square matrix by setting dotted cells to 1 and empty cells to 0. The
leaf matrix
of
M
is the matrix obtained when setting leaf vertices to 1, and all other cells to 0.

Since there is exactly one leaf in every row and column, a leaf matrix is always a permutation matrix, and thus has determinant
\pm 1
. In fact, it was proved in Proposition 2.5 of
[
CO24
]
that the determinant of any CNAT is equal to the determinant of its leaf matrix.

3.
Statement of the conjecture

Given the preliminary definitions, we may now study the number of CNATs with determinant 1 and -1. Define these to be
A_{n}
and
B_{n}
respectively. Their sum is the total number of CNATs of a given size,
T_{n}
. Also define the
defect
,
D_{n}=A_{n}-B_{n}

The numbers

[

T_{n}=\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}{n-1\choose k-1}{n-1\choose k}T_{k}T_{n-k}

From the table, the determinant seems to be evenly distributed for odd

4.
Proof

Finally, an inductive proof of the conjecture is given.

Definition 4.1
.

For convenience, let
e^{n}_{k}
be the number of
k
-subsets of
\{1,\dots,n\}
with an even sum. Similarly define
o^{n}_{k}
for the number of subsets with odd sum.

Proposition 4.2
.

\forall n\geq 2
,

\begin{split}A_{n}=\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}&\left(A_{k}A_{n-k}+B_{k}B_{n-k})(e^{n-1}_{k-1}e^{n-1}_{k}+o^{n-1}_{k-1}o^{n-1}_{k}\right)\\
&+\left(A_{k}B_{n-k}+B_{k}A_{n-k})(e^{n-1}_{k-1}o^{n-1}_{k}+o^{n-1}_{k-1}e^{n-1}_{k}\right)\end{split}

Proof.

Proposition 9 of
[
ABBS14
]
establishes a recurrence for
T_{n}
, derived by removing the root node of the CNAT to obtain two smaller CNATs. Let us extend their proof to formulate recurrences for
A_{n}
and
B_{n}
.

By removing the root node of a CNAT, we obtain two smaller CNATs after flattening, whose rows and columns are interweaved together. Also, one contains all non-root vertices in the top row, and the other contains all non-root vertices in the leftmost column.
Hence to construct a CNAT
T
of size
n
with determinant 1, we need to choose:

(1)

The size of the smaller CNAT, say
M
, that contains the top row. Call this size
k
(
1\leq k\leq n-1
). The other smaller CNAT, say
M^{\prime}
, contains the leftmost column and has size
n-k
.

(2)

The structures of both smaller CNATs. We can choose any combination of the two determinants, so long as in the next step we interweave the rows and columns so that the determinant is 1. We consider two cases; if
\det M\det M^{\prime}=1
then there are
A_{k}A_{n-k}+B_{k}B_{n-k}
ways since the determinants are both even or both odd, and similarly if
\det M\det M^{\prime}=-1
then there are
A_{k}B_{n-k}+B_{k}A_{n-k}
ways.

(3)

A way of interweaving the rows and columns so that the determinant becomes (or stays) 1. For this, we separately choose the rows of
M
out of the rows of
T
, and then the columns. For the rows, since
M
must contain the top row, we have
k-1
out of
n-1
rows left to pick. For the columns,
M
cannot contain the leftmost column because
M^{\prime}
does, so we have
k
columns to pick out of
n-1
.

Now, we must pick the rows and columns so that the determinant is 1. Given a valid such choice, we may perform a sequence of swaps of adjacent rows or columns to turn it into a block diagonal matrix whose submatrices are
M
and
M^{\prime}
(so that
M
is contained in the first
k
rows and first
k
columns;
T
need not still be a CNAT). The determinant of this block diagonal matrix will be
\det M\det M^{\prime}
. Note that we are only allowed to perform
adjacent
swaps because the rows and columns in
M
and
M^{\prime}
must stay in the same order. Each of these swaps will flip the sum of chosen rows and columns mod 2, and will also flip the determinant of
T
. Hence if
\det M\det M^{\prime}
is 1 then the valid choices of rows and columns are precisely those where the sum of the rows and columns is congruent to
(1+\dots+k)+(1+\dots+k)\equiv 0
mod 2. Thus there are
e^{n-1}_{k-1}e^{n-1}_{k}+o^{n-1}_{k-1}o^{n-1}_{k}
ways, because the sum of rows and sum of columns must both be odd, or both be even. Similarly if
\det M\det M^{\prime}
is -1 then there are
e^{n-1}_{k-1}o^{n-1}_{k}+o^{n-1}_{k-1}e^{n-1}_{k}
ways.

Summing the number of ways for each possibility of
\det M\det M^{\prime}
, then summing over
k
, the result follows.
∎

Lemma 4.3
.

\begin{split}D_{n}=\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}&D_{k}D_{n-k}(e^{n-1}_{k-1}-o^{n-1}_{k-1})(e^{n-1}_{k}-o^{n-1}_{k})\end{split}

Proof.

First note that
\forall n\geq 2
, the following formula holds:

\begin{split}B_{n}=\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}&\left(A_{k}B_{n-k}+B_{k}A_{n-k})(e^{n-1}_{k-1}e^{n-1}_{k}+o^{n-1}_{k-1}o^{n-1}_{k}\right)\\
&+\left(A_{k}A_{n-k}+B_{k}B_{n-k})(e^{n-1}_{k-1}o^{n-1}_{k}+o^{n-1}_{k-1}e^{n-1}_{k}\right)\end{split}

This can be seen in an identical way to the proof of
4.2
, except in step 3 where we now wish to interweave the rows and columns so that the resulting determinant is -1.

Now, subtract this expression for
B_{n}
from the expression for
A_{n}
in
4.2
. The resulting equation factorises, and then occurrences of
A-B
may be replaced with
D
.
∎

Lemma 4.4
.

\forall n\in\mathbb{N},k\in\mathbb{Z}
with
0\leq k\leq n-1
:

e^{2n-1}_{2k}-o^{2n-1}_{2k}=(-1)^{k}{n-1\choose k}

e^{2n-1}_{2k+1}-o^{2n-1}_{2k+1}=(-1)^{k+1}{n-1\choose k}

Proof.

Consider the group action of
C_{2}^{k}
on subsets of
{1,\dots,2n-1}
, with generators switching
1\leftrightarrow 2,3\leftrightarrow 4,\dots,2n-3\leftrightarrow 2n-2
. If for some pair
(2t-1,2t)
only one of them is in a subset, then the orbit of that subset has equally many subsets with even and odd sum because switching
2t-1\leftrightarrow 2t
changes the parity of the sum. The subsets left over are those in which both
2t-1
and
2t
are in the subset, or neither. Note that once we have chosen these pairs, there is no choice in whether to pick
2n-1
, because we must match the parity of the total number of items chosen. Thus there are
{n-1\choose k}
of the leftover subsets, and their sums all have the same parity. The factor of
(-1)^{k}
follows.
∎

Theorem 4.5
.

D_{n}=A_{n}-B_{n}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }n=1\\
0&\text{if }n>1\text{ is odd}\\
(-1)^{n/2}T_{n/2}&\text{if \begin{math}n\end{math} even}\end{cases}

Proof.

Induction on
n
. Note it is true for
n=1,2
, and true for
n=3
because
A_{3}=B_{3}=2
. Now suppose it is true up to
n-1
where
n>3
; we will show it for
n
.

If
n
is odd:
for all
2\leq k\leq n-2
, one of
k
and
n-k
is odd and larger than 1, because
n
is odd. Hence
D_{k}D_{n-k}=0
by inductive hypothesis. Thus in the expression given for
D_{n}
in
4.3
, all terms in the RHS sum vanish except
k=1
and
k=n-1
. However both of these terms still contribute zero, because
n
is odd so
e^{n-1}_{1}=o^{n-1}_{1}
(and also
e^{n-1}_{n-2}=o^{n-1}_{n-2}
by taking the complement of subsets of size 1). So
D_{n}=0
as required.

Otherwise,
n
is even: write
n=2j,j\in\mathbb{N}
. In the expression given for
D_{n}
in
4.3
, terms in the RHS sum vanish when
k
is odd because
D_{k}=D_{2j-k}=0
by inductive hypothesis. Hence only the even terms contribute to the sum. Write
k=2r,r\in\mathbb{N}
:

D_{2j}=\sum_{r=1}^{j-1}D_{2r}D_{2j-2r}(e^{2j-1}_{2r-1}-o^{2j-1}_{2r-1})(e^{2j-1}_{2r}-o^{2j-1}_{2r})

=\sum_{r=1}^{j-1}D_{2r}D_{2j-2r}{j-1\choose r-1}{j-1\choose r}\;\text{by \lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{lem:dets_evenminusodd}}

=(-1)^{j}\sum_{r=1}^{j-1}T_{r}T_{j-r}{j-1\choose r-1}{j-1\choose r}\;\text{by inductive hypothesis}

The RHS is
(-1)^{j}
multiplied by the recurrence given in
Section

3
for the number of CNATs of a given size. Hence it equals
(-1)^{j}T_{j}
as required.

∎
