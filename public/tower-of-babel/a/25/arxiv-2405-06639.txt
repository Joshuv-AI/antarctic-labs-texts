Value Augmented Sampling for Language Model Alignment and Personalization
Seungwook Han, Idan Shenfeld, Akash Srivastava, Yoon Kim, Pulkit Agrawal

Abstract
Aligning Large Language Models (LLMs) to cater to different human preferences, learning new skills, and unlearning harmful behavior is an important problem. Search-based methods, such as Best-of-N or Monte-Carlo Tree Search, are performant, but impractical for LLM adaptation due to their high inference cost. On the other hand, using Reinforcement Learning (RL) for adaptation is computationally efficient, but performs worse due to the optimization challenges in co-training the value function and the policy. We present a new framework for reward optimization, Value Augmented Sampling (VAS), that can maximize different reward functions using data sampled from only the initial, frozen LLM. VAS solves for the optimal reward-maximizing policy without co-training the policy and the value function, making the optimization stable, outperforming established baselines, such as PPO and DPO, on standard benchmarks, and achieving comparable results to Best-of-128 with lower inference cost. Unlike existing RL methods that require changing the weights of the LLM, VAS does not require access to the weights of the pre-trained LLM. Thus, it can even adapt LLMs (e.g., ChatGPT), which are available only as APIs. In addition, our algorithm unlocks the new capability of composing several rewards and controlling the extent of each one during deployment time, paving the road ahead for the future of aligned, personalized LLMs.

Introduction

Large Language Models (LLMs) trained on large amounts of Internet data are useful for many applications, such as programming and web-based search
(
Schick et al., 2023
;
Rozière et al., 2024
;
Chiang et al., 2023
)
. It is common practice to adapt pre-trained language models to improve their task-specific capabilities, suppress unwanted behavior, or change their response style.
A popular approach for adaptation is to represent a target task or behavior as a reward function and leverage one of many search/learning-based frameworks to maximize the reward.

Different reward optimization methods have their strengths and weaknesses. In our opinion, there are
five
essential characteristics:
(i)
High Performance
: The ability to effectively maximize the specified reward, while maintaining other desirable capabilities of the LLM
(
Askell et al., 2021
)
;
(ii)
Computational Efficiency
during inference is critical for real-world deployment;
(iii)
Fine-Grained Adaptation
: The ability to easily tune the extent of reward optimization during deployment according to the user’s preference. For example, providing the user with a knob to specify how much alignment should be performed for a particular reward function, such as conciseness;
(iv)
On-the-fly Composition
: The ability to align the LLM to new, multiple reward functions during deployment (i.e., on-the-fly) without modifying the initial LLM. Such ability will enable users to express new preferences continually without the computational burden of re-training the LLM to be adapted to the combination of existing and new preferences;
(v)
Black-Box Adaptation
:
As many state-of-the-art models are available only via API
(
OpenAI, 2024
;
Google, 2024
)
, we desire the ability to adapt both white and black-box models.

A straightforward way to maximize the reward is to generate
N
different responses and choose the one with the highest reward. This method, called Best-of-N (BoN), achieves high performance with a sufficiently large
N

(
Gao et al., 2023
)
. Moreover, since the search is conducted during deployment, BoN can be used for on-the-fly composition, adapting black-box models, and, in principle, fine-grained adaptation.
Despite their superior performance and other advantages, BoN and other search-based methods
(
Silver et al., 2017
)
are not used for real-world deployment due to their high computational expense (e.g., BoN requires
N
inferences from the LLM).

To mitigate the high inference-time cost, it is common practice to modify the weights of the initial LLM to directly generate the reward-maximizing response. In this view, the LLM can be considered as the “policy”, and actor-critic reinforcement learning (RL), such as Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)
(
Schulman et al., 2017
;
Wu et al., 2023
)
has emerged as the prominent method for learning the policy weights.
While such adaptation is computationally efficient to deploy, it lags behind the naive BoN search in terms of performance
(
Gao et al., 2023
)
.

We hypothesize that actor-critic RL performs worse than BoN because of challenges posed by bi-level optimization alternating between learning an estimator of the expected reward (i.e., the value function) of the current policy and improving the policy using the estimated value function. We conjecture that such circular dependence results in the convergence of actor-critic methods to sub-optimal solutions
because (1) training a value estimator for a non-stationary policy is known to be a challenge
(
Nikishin et al., 2022
;
Lyle et al., 2022
)
, (2) errors in value estimation can result in noisy policy updates
(
Fujimoto et al., 2018
)

, and (3) in LLM adaptation, it is common to constrain the learned policy to be close to the original LLM to preserve the LLM’s capabilities, such as reasoning
(
Lin et al., 2023
)
.
Solving constrained optimization problems with actor-critic RL is a well-known challenge
(
Liu et al., 2021
)
.
In addition to the performance gap, learning-based
methods require access to the models’ weights and cannot perform on-the-fly composition or fine-grained adaptation during deployment, which is necessary for user personalization.

In this paper, we present a reward-maximization method that aims to attain the five essential characteristics of LLM adaptation outlined above. Our key insight is that under the common assumption that the pre-trained LLM only needs to be modified by a small amount, alternating training of the policy and value function is unnecessary. Instead, we estimate the value function of the reward function using data collected from the base (i.e., pre-trained) LLM policy. Thus, we bypass the bi-level optimization process, which is the cause of sub-optimality in actor-critic RL methods.
Furthermore, unlike BoN, where the search is performed by evaluating entire sequences, we guide the search token-by-token using the value function as the search heuristic. Thereby, we achieve superior performance given the same computational budget. Finally, our formulation, which keeps the weights of the base LLM frozen, unlocks new capabilities – the ability to adapt LLMs to new and multiple user preferences during deployment without the need for any LLM re-training and adapting black-box LLMs whose weights are unavailable.

In summary, Value Augmented Sampling (VAS):

1.

Overcomes the instabilities of existing RL algorithms for LLM adaptation and consequently outperforms strong baselines, such as PPO and DPO
(
Rafailov et al., 2023
)
on the benchmark tasks of improving summarization
(
Clark et al., 2023
)
and multi-turn chat dialogue
(
Bai et al., 2022
)
.

2.

VAS is on par with Best-of-128 in both tasks while being at least x6 more computationally efficient in terms of FLOPS.

3.

VAS offers the ability to perform fine-grained control over the magnitude at which the model is being adapted towards a particular reward at inference time. We show how this capability can be used to personalize the LLM’s output for a specific user preference.

4.

VAS can adapt closed-source, proprietary models with only API access. We demonstrate that we can improve GPT-3.5 capability for new tasks without access to the model’s weights.

2
Related Work

Search-based algorithms for LLM decoding.

To maximize the reward, theoretically, one could conduct a full search of all possible responses. However, this is intractable, and various search algorithms adopt different sampling strategies to approximate the full search while staying close to the original model. BoN samples
N
sequences from the LLM and thereby implicitly maintains the constraint. Monte Carlo Tree Search
(
Liu et al., 2023
)
still uses the initial LLM to sample sequences, but uses a Value function to direct the search into high-reward responses. These algorithms and their variants have been shown to achieve remarkable performance but are expensive to compute
(
Nakano et al., 2021
;
Eisenstein et al., 2023
)
.

Another notable line of work
(
Dathathri et al., 2019
;
Krause et al., 2020
)
introduces a family of search algorithms that augment the token sampling with a classifier in order to find high-reward sequences. In each decoding step, they augment the LLM distribution with the classifier predictions and sample from it. The closest to our work among these algorithms is FUDGE
(
Yang & Klein, 2021
)
, which trains the classifier on partial responses from an offline dataset. In contrast to these prior methods, our work leverages the RL framework to train the augmented Value estimator so that it leads to the optimal solution to the constrained optimization problem.

Reinforcement Learning for Language Models.

The most commonly used RL algorithm for tuning LLMs is Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO,
(
Schulman et al., 2017
)
) and its variants
(
Ramamurthy et al., 2022
;
Wu et al., 2023
)
. Most of these are actor-critic algorithms
(
Sutton & Barto, 2018
)
, which iterate between learning a Value estimator for the current policy and using this estimator to improve the policy. This bi-level process can lead to a suboptimal policy, as has been observed empirically
(
Gao et al., 2023
;
Mudgal et al., 2023
)
. Unlike these methods, our algorithm utilizes a closed-form solution to extract the optimal constrained policy given a Value function, bypassing the policy optimization step. In addition, we learn the Value function of a fixed initial policy, thereby avoiding all of the aforementioned difficulties in optimization. Of most similarity to our work is ILQL
(
Snell et al., 2022
)
, which proposes a Q-learning algorithm for steering LLMs. However, their work focuses on the offline RL setting, while ours assumes online interactions with the environment (i.e., prompts and reward function). We further discuss these similarities and differences in Appendix
B
.

Concurrently and independently of our work, Controlled Decoding
(
Mudgal et al., 2023
)
explores a similar method to use the Q function to guide the decoding process.

3
Method

3.1
Problem Definition.

Consider the following finite-time Markov Decision Process (MDP) for a generative model
\pi
(“policy”): at time step
t
, the policy observes a state
s_{t}
, defined as the text prompt
y
concatenated with the response generated so far
x_{\leq t}
, and outputs an action
x_{t+1}\in\mathbb{V}
, where
\mathbb{V}
is the vocabulary of tokens,
x
. Once the action is chosen, the model deterministically transitions into the next state
s_{t+1}=s_{t}\oplus x_{t+1}
, where
\oplus
is the concatenation operation. The episode ends either after the policy generates T new tokens or an [EOS] token. At the end of the episode, a scalar reward is collected from the reward model
r(s_{T})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{V}^{T}\to\mathbb{R}
where
s_{T}
is the generated response. In the following Kullback-Leibler (KL)-regularized RL setting
(
Ng et al., 2000
;
Boularias et al., 2011
)
, we aim to learn a new policy that maximizes the expected reward while keeping the distance from the initial
\pi_{0}
under a threshold
\epsilon
:

\max_{\pi}{\mathbb{E}_{s_{T}\sim\pi}[r(s_{T})]}\quad s.t\quad D_{KL}(\pi||\pi_{0})<\epsilon

(1)

where
D_{KL}
is the KL divergence.

This work assumes access to the output distribution of
\pi_{0}
, but not necessarily to its weights. This assumption holds true in the case of open-source models and proprietary models with access to logits through API.

3.2
From Reward Optimization to Guided Decoding.

Prior works tackled the optimization problem in Equation
1
by directly optimizing a language model to be the solution
\pi
. As aforementioned, solving this constrained optimization problem under the actor-critic formulation can be complex and unstable.
As an alternative, we look into the following closed-form solution to the KL-regularized RL problem
(
Peters & Schaal, 2007
;
Peng et al., 2019
)
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let the expected reward of being in state
s_{t}
, and choosing token
x_{t+1}
be the Q-value
Q^{\pi_{0}}(x_{t+1}|s_{t})=\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{s_{T}}\sim\pi_{0}}[r(\mathsf{s_{T}})|\mathsf{s}_{t}=s_{t},\mathsf{x}_{t+1}=x_{t+1}]
. The solution to the optimization problem in Equation
1
is:

\pi(x_{t+1}|s_{t})\propto\pi_{0}(x_{t+1}|s_{t})e^{\beta Q^{\pi_{0}}(x_{t+1}|s_{t})}

(2)

\beta
is a scalar dependent on
\epsilon
and independent of
x_{t+1}
.

This proposition implies that instead of learning
\pi
, we can estimate the optimal solution
\pi
by learning
Q^{\pi_{0}}
. Oftentimes, learning
Q^{\pi_{0}}
for a fixed policy
\pi_{0}
is simpler and stable. Therefore, it allows us to solve the KL-regularized RL problem more easily without having an explicit model for
\pi
.

Figure 2:
The KL-performance trade-off for both reward and win rate over SFT, for different summarization objectives. VAS outperforms the baselines in both Attribution and Main Ideas. In Conciseness, PPO achieves a higher reward but a significantly lower win rate. These results show that VAS maximizes the reward better without reward hacking as much as PPO.

Learning a Q-value estimator for a given policy is a well-studied problem
(
Sutton & Barto, 2018
)
. However, in language modeling, there are unique opportunities and difficulties. Unlike most RL environments, where the action space is either continuous or low-dimensional discrete
(
Zhu et al., 2021
)
, the action space of language models is the set of all possible next tokens
\mathbb{V}
, which is usually in the tens of thousands. Existing algorithms for Q-value estimation have been shown to be ineffective in such large discrete action spaces
(
Dulac-Arnold et al., 2015
)
, thus presenting a significant challenge in applying RL algorithms to natural language tasks
(
He et al., 2015
;
Guo et al., 2021
;
Ramamurthy et al., 2022
)
. To overcome this challenge, we take advantage of another unique property of the language generation task:
deterministic transitions
. Given the current state and the next action, the next state is simply the concatenation of the generated tokens. This property allows us to draw the equivalence between the Q-value of a state-action pair and the value function:

Q^{\pi_{0}}(x_{t+1}|s_{t})=V^{\pi_{0}}(x_{t+1}\oplus s_{t})

(3)

This allows us to re-write Equation
2
as,

\pi(x_{t+1}|s_{t})\propto\pi_{0}(x_{t+1}|s_{t})e^{\beta V^{\pi_{0}}(x_{t+1}\oplus s_{t})}

(4)

As seen in Eq.
3
, the value function does not depend on the action space size. Therefore, when the action space is large, it can be learned more easily than a Q-value function. The trade-off of this lower learning complexity is the inference cost of predicting
V^{\pi_{0}}(x_{t+1}\oplus s_{t})
for all
x_{t+1}\in\mathbb{V}
. In other words, the value estimator needs to predict the expected return of all possible subsequent states. We discuss this trade-off in more detail in Appendix
B
.

3.3
Value Augmented Sampling

In the following section, we present Value Augmented Sampling (VAS). Equation
4
presents an elegant but computationally expensive solution to the KL-regularized RL problem.
To realize it into a practical algorithm, we propose the following approximation. At every decoding step, we first obtain
k
tokens with the highest output probabilities under our base policy
\pi_{0}
. Then, we calculate the value estimation for only these
k
tokens and sample the subsequent action according to the following distribution:

\pi(x_{t+1}|s_{t})\propto\begin{cases}\pi_{0}(x_{t+1}|s_{t})e^{\beta V^{\pi_{0}}(x_{t+1}\oplus s_{t})}&x_{t}\in\text{top-k}\\
\pi_{0}(x_{t+1}|s_{t})e^{\beta\bar{V}(s_{t})}&x_{t}\notin\text{top-k}\end{cases}

(5)

With
\bar{V}(s_{t})=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{x_{t}\in\text{top-k}}{V^{\pi_{0}}(x_{t+1}\oplus s_{t})}
. We have found that even when
k<<\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\mathbb{V}\right\rvert}
, our proposed approximation is effective while reducing computational complexity significantly.

Next, we will analyze the inference-time compute required by VAS. Let
m
be the compute (in FLOPS) required by an LLM to process a single token, and
n
the compute required by a secondary model (either value estimator or reward function) to do the same. Policy optimization methods directly optimize the base policy and, therefore, only require
o(T^{2}\cdot m)
FLOPS to generate a response of length
T
. BoN requires generating
N
responses and evaluating all of them with the reward model, requiring a total of
o(N\cdot T^{2}(n+m))
FLOPS. VAS requires one pass over the base policy and
k
token evaluation with our value estimator, requiring a total of
o(T^{2}(m+kn))
. When
n<<m
, our method can be significantly more efficient than BoN and approach the computational cost of using a single policy.

To train our Value function, we first collect a dataset
D=\{s^{i},y^{i},r^{i}\}_{i}^{N}
by sampling
s^{i}\sim\pi_{0}(s_{T}|y^{i})
and evaluating them with the reward function
r^{i}=r(s^{i})
. Then, we use TD(
\lambda
) algorithm
(
Sutton & Barto, 2018
;
Schulman et al., 2015
)
to train our value estimator. Our value estimator is parametrized as a separate neural network, decoupled from
\pi_{0}
. This parametrization removes both the need to have access to the initial policy’s weights and update them.

4
Experiments

We compare VAS against prior works on adapting LLMs and highlight the unique advantages of our method. We first compare VAS against baselines on two common text-summarization benchmarks (Sec.
4.1.1
) and multi-turn chat dialogue (Sec.
4.1.2
). We additionally demonstrate VAS ability to simultaneously maximize multiple reward functions
4.2
. Then, we showcase our algorithm’s ability to teach black-box models (GPT-3.5) new capabilities of tool-use (Sec.
4.4
). Finally, we empirically demonstrate the ability to perform fine-grained control over the specified task behavior during inference in the task of LLM personalization (Sec.
4.3
). Finally, Sec.
4.5
,
4.6
and
4.7
present ablation studies.

Experimental Setup

Ours and baseline methods are used to align a base LLM using a reward function after supervised fine-tuning of a pre-trained model on the respective datasets.
This model will serve as the initial LLM,
\pi_{0}
, and will be called the supervised fine-tuned (SFT) policy. For all of the models up until Section
4.3
, we use LLaMA 7B (either version 1 or 2, see Appendix
C
).
For the reward models, we use popular pre-trained models, and when not available, we train custom reward models with FLAN-T5-L (780M parameters)
(
Wei et al., 2021
)
. To maximize the performance of each method, we conducted independent hyperparameter searches for each method and reported the result for the best hyperparameter setting. See Appendix
C
for more details.

Evaluation

Following
(
Gao et al., 2023
;
Rafailov et al., 2023
;
Wu et al., 2023
)
, we conduct an evaluation using two different metrics. First, we analyze the trade-off between reward and KL divergence from the SFT model to study each algorithm’s ability to maximize the specified reward. Further, due to the known ‘reward hacking’ problem in many algorithms, we use GPT-4 as a proxy for human evaluators to conduct comparisons between models. Many works have shown that GPT-4 has a high correlation with human judgment
(
Dubois et al., 2023
;
Li et al., 2023
)
. We report win rates against responses generated by the SFT model and conduct direct comparisons with our baselines.

Results

We first analyze the results in Figure
2
, which compares the reward and win rates against SFT results. As illustrated in the left two columns, we outperform PPO and FUDGE on Attribution and Main Ideas. We maximize the rewards given any KL divergence constraint better and summarize better overall (as determined by GPT-4). It is important to mention that FUDGE does not try to maximize the reward directly, but instead trains a classifier directly on the labeled data, which can explain its considerably low reward scores. However, the GPT-4 win rate shows that VAS learns an overall better summarization ability. This highlights the benefits of using a value estimator over a classifier to augment the token-level distribution. Unlike FUDGE, PPO shares the same learning objective as VAS, but achieves worse results both in reward and win rate. We attribute the reason for the performance gap to the PPO training instabilities mentioned above (Sec.
2
), which often leads it to converge to a sub-optimal solution. On the last axis of Conciseness, although PPO achieved higher rewards than VAS, its generated summaries are judged to be worse by GPT-4. This is a tangible example of ’reward hacking’, a common problem with RL-based methods that directly optimize the policy.
We further evaluate the model’s summarization ability by directly comparing the generated responses from different models against each other in
Table

1
. The results show that VAS clearly outperforms the baselines, with an average 60% success rate. Performing MCTS on top of PPO improves the results a bit, but VAS still outperforms it. More importantly, we approximately match the performance of Best-of-128, a very strong but computationally expensive method.

4.1.2
Chat Dialogue

Task

The second task is to improve the LLM’s ability to engage in multi-turn dialogue as a chat assistant. To this end, we use Anthropic’s Helpfulness and Harmlessness (HH) dataset
(
Bai et al., 2022
)
, which contains
\sim
161K examples of conversations between a human and an assistant. Each conversation is paired with two responses, one of which is labeled as preferred. The preference is based on which is more informative, honest, and safe.

Baselines

On top of the previous baselines of PPO and FUDGE, we add another strong baseline, Direct Policy Optimization (DPO)
(
Rafailov et al., 2023
)
. DPO is an offline contextual bandit algorithm that learns a policy directly from preference data. Although being a strong baseline, DPO is designed specifically for a preference dataset with pairs of responses and, as such, is unsuitable for the other tasks considered in this work. Contrary, FUDGE is not designed for pair-wise preference learning. Therefore, we create a variation of FUDGE (“Online FUDGE”) as a baseline for this task. Instead of learning a prefix classifier from offline data, Online FUDGE learns a Value estimator from online data and reward function by regarding it as a regression task. Therefore, it can also be seen as a TD(1) version of VAS. For GPT-4 evaluations, we additionally evaluate against Best-of-128.

Results

As in the summarization task, we study the KL-reward frontier and evaluate the quality of the generated responses as a chat assistant with GPT-4. As illustrated in the left plot of Figure
3
, VAS attains the best KL-reward trade-off among all the models. DPO’s weak performance on this metric originates from the fact that it does not learn to maximize the reward, but instead learns directly from the data. On the right plot, we show head-to-head win rate comparisons against different models. Noticeably, we outperform DPO, a strong baseline tailored specifically for preference optimization, with a 55% win rate. In addition, we show that the responses generated by VAS are comparable, if not slightly better, to best-of-128. Regarding the poor performance of PPO, we report the best performance we were able to achieve. Although we experimented with three well-regarded codebase implementations
(
Taori et al., 2023
;
von Werra et al., 2020
;
Havrilla et al., 2023
)
and over 20 hyperparameter search, we found PPO to be unstable on this dataset. Prior work also reports similar difficulties in training PPO on this dataset
(
Rafailov et al., 2023
)
. This once again alludes to the algorithm’s optimization instability.

4.2
Composing multiple alignment objectives

Task

In the real world, alignment to multiple different axes is required to model the complex set of human preferences. Thus, we further extend the evaluation of our algorithm’s capabilities in solving multi-axis alignment. Specifically, we focus on the task of improving the three axes of Attribution, Main Ideas, and Conciseness simultaneously. Under the RL framework, multi-axis optimization is usually framed as maximizing a linear combination of different reward functions
(
Hayes et al., 2022
)
. To transform VAS into a multi-reward optimization algorithm, we can take advantage of the fact that the Value is a linear function of the reward
(
Sutton & Barto, 2018
)
. Therefore, maximizing a linear combination of rewards is equivalent to performing VAS with a linear combination of the respective Value estimators. It is important to mention that these estimators do not need to be jointly trained and are combined only during inference.

Baselines

For baselines, we compare against Multi-Objective PPO, a PPO training where the reward is the sum of the three separate reward functions. Moreover, we compare against Rewarded Soup
(
Rame et al., 2023
)
, an alternative approach to extend PPO for multi-alignment. Rewarded Soup trains a PPO model for each reward separately and averages the weights of these models during inference. We conducted the experiments under the setting that the three attributes should be weighted equally.

Results

As seen in Figure
4
, VAS effectively maximizes the rewards on each axis without sacrificing the performance in another and outperforms both MO-PPO and Rewarded Soup. We observe an interesting phenomenon with MO-PPO, where the different reward models compete with each other over the course of the training, and the policy exhibits mode-switching behavior, alternating between optimizing for one reward model over the other. At one point, as shown with MO-PPO (middle), it is able to learn a reasonably good balance, but at the end of the training, as seen with MO-PPO (last), the model collapses on one of the axes. This gives another example of the instabilities in PPO training.

VAS is able to maximize multiple rewards simultaneously without sacrificing performance in any axis. Mode-switching behavior can be observed during the Mo-PPO training (see the comparison between middle and last), which once again alludes to its inherent instability in optimization.

4.3
VAS enables fine-grained control for personalization

Task

Unlike in the previous two sets of experiments, for some behaviors, it is not always desirable to simply maximize the rewards but control it dynamically. This is important in the problem of personalization, where users have different preferences for the style of responses. Prompt engineering is a solution, but it can be time-consuming and computationally expensive to optimize the prompts to get the exact degree of an attribute
(
Pryzant et al., 2023
;
Deng et al., 2022
)
. In this experiment, we show that VAS is a complementary approach to prompting that allows easy tuning at inference time. This can be done by modulating the
\beta
parameter to control the weight between the original LLM and the Value augmentation. In contrast, control at inference-time is not possible with PPO, since a new policy needs to be trained for each different configuration. We show this fine-grained control capability over the axes of
Formality
and
Verbosity
.

Experimental Details

Conclusion

This work proposes an alternative approach to solving the KL-regularized RL problem to adapt LLMs via the closed-form solution. Thereby, not only does our method address the core problem of optimization instability with existing actor-critic-based RL algorithms and significantly improve the performance, but also it enables new capabilities of personalization and easy compositionality. We envision a future where people interact with models customized to their needs and preferences instead of a standardized, static one. VAS serves as an important step towards this future. However, important open challenges remain along the way. An important question would be to investigate whether a Q-value function can be learned in lieu of a Value function. We explored versions of learning this Q-value estimator and encountered challenges because of the complexity of the learning problem in environments with large action spaces, as shown in Appendix

A.1
Solution to the KL-regularized Rl problem

The following derivation is known as Reward-Weighted-Regression
(
Peters & Schaal, 2007
)
and is closely related to the Bayesian perspective on KL-regularized RL
(
Korbak et al., 2022
)
. As a reminder, the problem we aim to solve is:

\max_{\pi}{\mathbb{E}_{s_{T}\sim\pi}[r(s_{T})]}\quad s.t\quad D_{KL}(\pi||\pi_{0})<\epsilon

Where we We will start by adding a constant to the first part of the objective, transforming it into:

\max_{\pi}{\mathbb{E}_{s_{T}\sim\pi}[r(s_{T})]-\mathbb{E}_{s_{T}\sim\pi_{0}}[r(s_{T})]}\quad s.t\quad D_{KL}(\pi||\pi_{0})<\epsilon

Using the Performance Difference Lemma (Lemma 6.1 from
(
Kakade & Langford, 2002
)
) we can write:

\mathbb{E}_{s_{T}\sim\pi}[r(s_{T})]-\mathbb{E}_{s_{T}\sim\pi_{0}}[r(s_{T})]=\mathbb{E}_{s_{T}\sim\pi}[Q^{\pi_{0}}(x_{t+1}|s_{t})-V^{\pi_{0}}(s_{t})]

And so our optimization problem became:

\max_{\pi}{\mathbb{E}_{s_{T}\sim\pi}[Q^{\pi_{0}}(x_{t+1}|s_{t})-V^{\pi_{0}}(s_{t})]}\quad s.t\quad D_{KL}(\pi||\pi_{0})<\epsilon

With the additional constraint that
\pi(\cdot|s_{t})
is a probability distribution for all
t
. The constraints should hold for every state, and in particular under the state distribution induced by
\pi
. Therefore, the corresponding Lagrangian is:

\mathcal{L}(\pi,\vec{\lambda}_{1},\vec{\lambda}_{2})=\mathbb{E}_{s_{T}\sim\pi}\left[Q^{\pi_{0}}(x_{t+1}|s_{t})-V^{\pi_{0}}(s_{t})+\lambda_{1}(s_{t})\left(D_{KL}(\pi||\pi_{0})-\epsilon\right)+\lambda_{2}(s_{t})\left(\sum_{T}\pi(x_{t+1}|s_{t})-1\right)\right]

Where
\vec{\lambda}_{1},\vec{\lambda}_{2}
is state-dependent lagrange multipliers. Notice that if we consider only the decision at a specific timestep
\tau
, the expectation depends only on the policy’s distribution on that specific timestep, for example the first term:

\mathbb{E}_{s_{T}\sim\pi}[Q^{\pi_{0}}(x_{t+1}|s_{t})-V^{\pi_{0}}(s_{t})]=\sum_{x_{t+1}}\pi(x_{t+1}|s_{t})[Q^{\pi_{0}}(x_{t+1}|s_{t})-V^{\pi_{0}}(s_{t})]

Plugging it in and taking the derivative at specific timestep
t
with respect to the probability of taking a specific action
x_{t+1}
:

\frac{\partial}{\partial\pi(x_{t+1}|s_{t})}\mathcal{L}(\pi,\vec{\lambda}_{1},\vec{\lambda}_{2})=Q^{\pi_{0}}(x_{t+1}|s_{t})-V^{\pi_{0}}(s_{t})+\lambda_{1}(s_{t})\left(\log{\frac{\pi(x_{t+1}|s_{t})}{\pi_{0}(x_{t+1}|s_{t})}}+1\right)+\lambda_{2}(s_{t})

The corresponding fixed-point solution is:

\pi(x_{t+1}|s_{t})=\pi_{0}(x_{t+1}|s_{t})e^{\frac{Q^{\pi_{0}}(x_{t+1}|s_{t})-V^{\pi_{0}}(s_{t}}{\lambda_{1}(s_{t})}}e^{\frac{\lambda_{2}(s_{t})+1}{\lambda_{1}(s_{t})}}

We can simplify the expression by considering all the terms that are independent of the choice of
x_{t}
as part of the normalization constant, which leaves us with:

\pi(x_{t+1}|s_{t})\propto\pi_{0}(x_{t+1}|s_{t})e^{\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}(s_{t})}Q^{\pi_{0}}(x_{t+1}|s_{t})}

B.1
Training Q Estimator for Text Generation

In our initial formulation, as presented in Equation
2
, we propose augmenting the outputs of the baseline policy with the Q-values corresponding to each token. This step is helpful for enhancing the LLM’s decoding process by considering the expected rewards associated with each potential token. Traditionally in reinforcement learning literature, Q-function estimators for discrete action spaces are parameterized as mappings from the entire state space to the action space. This typically involves a neural network architecture that processes the state as input, followed by a linear layer that outputs values spanning the action space dimension. The state space in text generation encompasses all conceivable text sequences, which is astronomically large. This is further compounded by the vast number of actions (tokens) typically involved, often numbering in the tens of thousands. Such a vast state and action space presents formidable optimization challenges.

Given the finite size of the training dataset, rare tokens (tokens with low probability under the original LLM) are scarcely represented, resulting in minimal updates to their corresponding weights. Consequently, these rare tokens may yield arbitrary values during inference, undermining the estimator’s reliability. This is less of an issue for the state space since all input tokens share the same weights, and also, the backbone model is pretrained on a vast amount of data. To address and explore potential solutions to this challenge, we conducted a series of experiments. Through these investigations, we aim to provide insights into the complexities and practical challenges of applying Q-function estimators to text generation tasks, contributing to our decision to use a Value estimator instead.

B.2
Experiment Description

All the experiments described below were done on the SEAHORSE summarization dataset, specifically with property Q4 - attribution. However, our experiments indicate that all the conclusions also hold for the other two properties in this dataset. For the Q-value estimator, we use a Llama-2 7B backbone with a linear head on top of its last hidden layer. This linear head had an output dimension of 32k, which is the vocabulary size of the Llama-2 model family. The rest of the training details are exactly the same as described in section
4.1.1
.

Our first experiment compared two ways to compute the target Q-value during Temporal Difference (TD) learning. In our experiment, we used TD(
\lambda
), which constructs this value as a linear combination of several n-step bootstrap targets and the final reward
\hat{v}(s_{t})=\gamma^{T}\lambda^{T}r(s_{T})+\sum_{i=t}^{T-1}\gamma^{i}\lambda^{i}[\gamma V^{\pi_{0}}(s_{i+1})-V^{\pi_{0}}(s_{i})]
. Since we don’t have access to the true Values of the states, we need to estimate them using the Q function. The connection between Value and Q-values that come into hand here is
V^{\pi_{0}}(s_{i})=\mathbb{E}_{x_{i}\sim\pi}[Q^{\pi_{0}}(s_{i-1},x_{i})]
. Having access to the output distribution of
\pi_{0}
, we can calculate this expression exactly or regard the tokens in the data as samples from this expectation. Figure X (left) shows the results of these two options as ’
Vanilla Q (exact)
’ and ’
Vanilla Q (sampling)
’. The sampling method performs much better, and we hypothesize that this is because the sampled tokens are usually those with high probability. That way it disregards the rare tokens, which usually have bad Q estimators, in the bootstrapping process.

To investigate the effect of rare tokens on the performance further, we used the Q estimator trained with the sampling-based bootstrapping and, while decoding, evaluated only
k
tokens at each step. These
top-k
tokens were chosen as the ones with the highest probability under the base model. The results in Figure X (left) show that limiting the tokens to these with high probability helps a lot. We didn’t find large performance gaps between different values of
k
up to two thousand. After that, the performance starts to degrade. This experiment supports the conclusion that with a linear head, it is hard to learn good Q-value estimation for all tokens.

After realizing the source of the poor performance of the Q estimator, we investigated with alternative parameterization to the Q-value estimator. Since learning Q-values for rare tokens is hard, we looked for a solution that will learn a value for them that will not have an effect on the decoding.
Dueling Networks

(
Wang et al., 2016
)
does precisely that by decomposing the Q-value into a Value and Advantage. A dueling network has two heads, one to predict Values and one to predict Advantage, and during a forward pass it adds them together. The advantage head is initialized to zero, so rare tokens that do not change their prediction much will get an overall Q-value estimation which is just the Value. As can be seen from Figure X (right), this parameterization achieves significantly better results than the linear head. Therefore, to push the idea even further, we experimented with
Value Advantage Learning

(
Tang et al., 2023
)
, a recent learning objective that optimizes the Advantage head and the Value head separately. This represents the best results we were able to achieve using Q-value, and although they were significantly better than initial trials, they still didn’t achieve the same performance as with the Value estimator.

ILQL
(
Snell et al., 2022
)
is an offline RL algorithm designed for altering the behavior of LLMs given a fixed set of generated data (not necessarily from the initial LLM) annotated with a reward function. Unlike our algorithm, ILQL does not aim to learn a Q-value estimator for the current function but instead tries to learn a Q-value estimator for the optimal policy to the
unconstrained
RL problem. This is the policy that solely maximizes the reward, disregarding how far its output is from those of the initial policy
\pi_{0}
. After learning the Q-value estimator, ILQL uses during decoding a method similar to Equation
2
.
Although built for offline data, we experienced with an online version of ILQL to see if the algorithm implemented there could be useful for learning a Q-value estimator for text generation. The results in Figure
9
shows that ILQL can be used in the online setting, but is still underperformed compared to VAS.
