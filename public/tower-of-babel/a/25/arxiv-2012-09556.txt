Path Integral Approach Unveils the Role of Complex Energy Landscape for Activated Dynamics of Glassy Systems
Tommaso Rizzo

Abstract
The complex dynamics of an increasing number of systems is attributed to the emergence of a rugged energy landscape with an exponential number of metastable states. To develop this picture into a predictive dynamical theory I discuss how to compute the exponentially small probability of a jump from one metastable state to another. This is expressed as a path integral that can be evaluated by saddle-point methods in mean-field models, leading to a boundary value problem. The resulting dynamical equations are solved numerically by means of a Newton-Krylov algorithm in the paradigmatic spherical p p -spin glass model that is invoked in diverse contexts from supercooled liquids to machine-learning algorithms. I discuss the solutions in the asymptotic regime of large times and the physical implications on the nature of the ergodicity-restoring processes.



I
Introduction

The emergence of a rugged free energy landscape with many minima and saddles is a paradigm often invoked to explain complex dynamical phenomena ranging from supercooled liquids
Charbonneau et al. 2014
to the performance of widely used algorithm in machine-learning and inference
Ros et al. 2019a
;
Mannelli et al. 2020
.
Powerful methods developed originally in the spin-glass literature
Mézard et al. 1987
allow to obtain a rather complete description of the landscape in a growing number of statistical physics model. On the debit side the resulting picture is essentially limited to
static
properties of the landscape, like the energy of the metastable states, their free energy and notably their number, the so-called configurational entropy.
Thus understanding the exact way in which the landscape shapes the dynamics is largely an open problem.
The question is particularly urgent in the context of supercooled liquids where the rugged landscape paradigm is at the core of the Random-First-Order-Transition (RFOT) theory
Kirkpatrick et al. 1989
;
Wolynes and Lubchenko 2012
.

More than thirty years after its formulation RFOT is still one of the major competing theories in the ongoing debate on the nature of the Glass transition
Rizzo 2020a
.
In a nutshell the theory posits that
the physics of supercooled liquids is the same of Spin-Glass (SG) models displaying one-step of Parisi’s Replica-Symmetry-Breaking (1RSB)
Mézard et al. 1987
. The mean-field versions of these models display an ergodicity-breaking transition at a dynamical temperature
T_{d}
where the phase space splits into many metastable states that trap the dynamics; at lower temperatures, the configurational entropy,
i.e.
the log of the number of metastable states, decreases eventually vanishing at a static temperature
T_{s}
.
Ergodicity breaking between
T_{d}
and
T_{s}
is a mean-field artifact and one expects that in real systems ergodicity is restored through droplet-like excitations. Furthermore the size of these excitations must diverge as the configurational entropy vanishes leading eventually to a genuine ergodicity-breaking transition at
T_{s}
.
RFOT originated from the realization
Kirkpatrick and Thirumalai 1987
that similar features had been discussed in various unrelated (and themselves controversial) theories of supercooled liquids, most notably: 1) dynamics at the ergodicity-breaking transition is the same of the (avoided) Mode-Coupling-Theory (MCT) of supercooled liquids
Götze 2008
, 2) within the Adams-Gibbs-Di Marzio theory
Gibbs and DiMarzio 1958
;
Adam and Gibbs 1965
, the glass transition is driven by a correlation length that diverges at the Kauzmann temperature where the configurational entropy vanishes.

Efforts to validate the theory have been driving theoretical, numerical and experimental research for years.
At the theoretical level RFOT has been substantiated by a number of results arguing that mean-field models of supercooled liquids exhibit 1RSB
Mézard and Parisi 2012
;
Monasson 1995
including the solution of the limit of infinite physical dimensions
Charbonneau et al. 2014
;
Charbonneau et al. 2017
. Numerically, the MCT phenomenology is well documented
Kob and Andersen 1995
;
Kob 1999
as well as the increase of dynamic
Kob et al. 1997
;
Flenner et al. 2014
and static correlation lengths
Berthier et al. 2005
;
Biroli et al. 2008
. Experimentally, the observation of a decreasing configurational entropy dates back to the 40’s while more recently RFOT has inspired measurements of non-linear susceptibilities
Albert et al. 2016
.
Furthermore the analogy with supercooled liquids has also led to the discovery that off-equilibrium relaxational dynamics of mean-field Spin-glass models display aging
Cugliandolo and
Kurchan 1993
and it is an active line of research
Folena et al. 2020
;
Altieri et al. 2020
.

In spite of this huge body of work, consensus on the validity of the theory is still lacking. One of the problem is that RFOT-inspired literature often focuses on quantities, namely point-to-set correlation lengths and configurational entropy, whose actual relevance for the problem of the glass transition, that instead is dynamical in its essence, can also be questioned.
Besides, predictions are often merely qualitatively, which is a problem given that
e.g.
the observed static length increases are too modest to convince the community that they actually drive the slowing down of the dynamics.

.
The main challenge is that current theoretical knowledge is mostly limited to mean-field models that display ergodicity-breaking at

: in order to recover ergodicity between

and

and obtain realistic predictions we have to go beyond mean-field, intense efforts in this line of research are currently underway

;
Baity-Jesi

;
Carbone et al. 2020
;
Hartarsky et al. 2019
;
Ros et al. 2019b
;
Stariolo and

;

;

.
In recent years progress has been made for the temperature region
close
to the dynamical temperature
T_{d}

Rizzo 2014
;
Rizzo and Voigtmann 2015
;
Rizzo 2016a
. It is now possible to describe qualitatively and quantitatively how the ergodicity-breaking MCT transition is turned into a dynamical crossover in mean-field SG
Rizzo 2016b
(due to finite-size effects) and most importantly in some finite-dimensional models
Rizzo and Voigtmann 2020
.
In this paper I consider instead the region between

and
T_{s}
where metastable states are present and discuss how to compute the transition rate, that is
the exponentially small probability
of a jump from an equilibrium state to another occurring in a finite time.
To make contact with the phenomenology of supercooled liquids we have to remember that below the experimental MCT transition temperature a particle is trapped most of the time into a cage formed by the surrounding particles and diffusion occurs through
hopping

i.e.
sudden rare jumps from a cage to another (see fig.
1
).
Mean-field models capture

through the appearance of metastable states and the study of jumps in the free energy landscape initiated in this paper is essential to a quantitative description of

in real space.

I.1
Main Results

The main results to be discussed in the paper are: i) a path integral method to compute the transition rate ii) a Newton-Krylov algorithm yielding the numerical solution of the corresponding dynamical equations iii) an asymptotic analysis of the solutions in the regime of large times and iv) some non-trivial implications on the ergodicity-restoring processes.

At the methodological level the problem is formulated as a path integral over Langevin dynamic trajectories that can be computed though saddle-point methods in mean-field models.
The problem displays some important differences with respect to the standard relaxational dynamics
Sompolinsky and
Zippelius 1982
;
Crisanti et al. 1993
;
Cugliandolo and
Kurchan 1993
, namely the use of replicas and the need to explicitly handle the divergent path integral.
I have focused on the paradigmatic spherical
p
-spin SG model
Crisanti and Sommers 1992
;
Crisanti et al. 1993
but the method is fairly general and the equations can be derived with some effort for other mean-field systems
e.g.
supercooled liquids in large dimension
Kurchan et al. 2016
;
Maimbourg et al. 2016
;
Manacorda et al. 2020
.
Another more important difference follows from to the fact that while in ordinary relaxation dynamics one only fixes the
initial
condition, in order to study activated dynamics one has to fix both the
initial
and
final
conditions.
This difference manifests itself at the level of the dynamical equations: while relaxational equations display first-order time derivatives the equations obtained here are second-order. One must then solve a more difficult boundary value problem instead of a simpler initial value problem
1
1

1

This is also the main difference with the earlier method of
Lopatin and Ioffe 1999
;
Lopatin and Ioffe 2000

and more recently of
Ros et al. 2021

that allow to study some special activated processes through relaxational dynamics equations but cannot be used to study transition rates between generic equilibrium states.
.
Indeed while the relaxational dynamical equations can be solved at times
t+\Delta t
iteratively using the solution at times
t^{\prime}\leq t

the activated dynamical equations must be solved simultaneously at all times, something for which no standard algorithm exists.
A successful solution strategy has been developed
based on three elements: 1) Newton’s method on discretized equations, 2) Krylov methods with physical preconditioning to invert the Jacobian, 3) Richardson extrapolation to reach the continuum limit.
The whole technology can be again exported to other problems, with possible computational complexity issues due to the specific dynamical order parameter.

In the remaining of this section I will give a compact presentation of the main results, leaving the details for the body of the paper and the appendices.
In particular in section
I.1.1

the object I compute is introduced and its general properties discussed.
Later on I discuss the asymptotic behavior of the solutions that is interesting for a number of physical and technical reasons.
From the physical point of view the most interesting outcome of the computation is associated to the ergodicity restoring processes as discussed in sec.
I.1.3
.
The actual dynamical equations are given later in section (
II
), their solutions will be studied in various regimes (free, ergodic and activated). The numerical solution is challenging and will be discussed in sec.
II.6
. In section
III
I will give some concluding perspectives.

The object considered is the transition rate
T_{2\mathcal{T}}(\sigma|\tau)
defined as the probability that the system is in configuration
\sigma
at time
t_{fin}=\mathcal{T}
given that it was in configuration
\tau
at time
t_{in}=-\mathcal{T}
. It is convenient to actually consider the following object that, due to detailed balance, is symmetric with respect to the exchange of
\sigma
and
\tau
:

\hat{T}_{2\mathcal{T}}(\sigma,\tau)\equiv T_{2\mathcal{T}}(\sigma|\tau)e^{{\beta\over 2}H(\sigma)-{\beta\over 2}H(\tau)}\ .

(1)

An integral representation of Langevin dynamics is used and
\sigma
,
\tau
are chosen as generic
equilibrium
configurations. At low temperatures
\hat{T}(\sigma,\tau)
is exponentially small in the system size
N
in mean-field models, therefore an annealed average
P_{eq}(\sigma)P_{eq}(\tau)\hat{T}(\sigma,\tau)
would interfere with the equilibrium measure of
\sigma
and
\tau
and
the correct procedure is to consider the quenched average

[\ln\hat{T}]\equiv\sum_{\sigma,\tau}P_{eq}(\sigma)P_{eq}(\tau)\,\ln\hat{T}(\sigma,\tau)\ \ .

(2)

One can resort to the replica method to eliminate the logarithm, besides, if quenched disorder is present, the corresponding averages (represented by an overline in the following) require the introduction of additional replicas of the initial and final configurations.

One can argue that the rate is self-averaging, meaning that most couples
(\sigma,\tau)
display the same rate
2
2

2

This can be shown computing
O(n)
corrections to the rate where
n
is the replica number.
, thus, given an initial configuration
\sigma
, the total number of configurations with rate equal to the typical value given by the average
\overline{[\ln\hat{T}_{2\mathcal{T}}]}
is equal to the total number of equilibrium configurations
\tau
,
i.e.
the exponential of the entropy,
e^{S}
.
Neglecting exponentially small corrections, the total probability of jumping to one of the equilibrium configurations is thus
e^{[\ln\hat{T}]+S}
and must be smaller than one leading to the bound:

\overline{[\ln\hat{T}_{2\mathcal{T}}]}+S\leq 0\ .

(3)

Now, after a finite time a system in equilibrium will be in another equilibrium configuration
correlated
with the initial one, therefore the probability to be in a
generic
equilibrium configuration (that is uncorrelated to the initial one) must be smaller than one meaning that at any finite
\mathcal{T}
the above bound should not be saturated. This also implies that even for
T>T_{d}
if
\mathcal{T}<\infty
one must consider the logarithm of the rate (and thus resort to the replica method with replica number
n=0
) in order not to interfere with the equilibrium measure on the initial and final conditions. On the other hand ergodicity implies that when
\mathcal{T}
goes to infinity the probability measure will be flat over the
e^{S}
equilibrium configurations and the average rate should become equal to
e^{-S}
saturating the bound.

At the mean-field level the expression for the logarithm of the rate can be computed by the

\mathcal{A}\equiv\lim_{\mathcal{T}\rightarrow\infty}\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}{1\over N}\left(\overline{[\ln\hat{T}_{2\mathcal{T}}]}+S\right)\ .

(4)

In 1RSB models, as discussed in the introduction, the dynamical temperature

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0\,\ \ \mathrm{for}\ T\geq T_{d}

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle 0\,\ \ \mathrm{for}\ T<T_{d}

(6)

Let us now mentions for completeness a few technical features of the problem that will be further discussed in the following sections.
At all temperatures, even in the ergodic phase

I.1.3
Ergodicity Restoring Processes

This work is focused on the
exponentially small
probability that the system jumps to another equilibrium state in a
finite
time, which is complementary to the problem of determining the
exponentially large
time-scale
\tau_{erg}
over which the system finds itself into another equilibrium state with
finite
probability.

Given that, according to the discussion before eq. (
3
) the exponentially small probability to jump to an equilibrium state with typical rate is
p=e^{[\ln T]+S}
it is natural to expect that such a probability becomes finite on a time-scale of order
1/p
. This is indeed what happens for ferromagnetism,
e.g.
in the Curie-Weiss model at low temperatures where two metastable states are present.
The following discussion shows instead that
the connection does not hold in presence of an exponential number of metastable states, i.e. a finite configurational entropy
.
In order to see this it is convenient to introduce the average of the rate at inverse temperature
\beta
over initial and final configurations that are in equilibrium at different inverse temperatures
\beta_{in}
and
\beta_{fin}
:

[\ln T]_{\beta_{in},\beta,\beta_{fin}}\equiv\sum_{\sigma\,,\tau}P_{eq}^{(\beta_{fin})}(\sigma)P_{eq}^{(\beta_{in})}(\tau)\ln T^{(\beta)}_{2\,\mathcal{T}}(\sigma|\tau)\ ,

(20)

where
P_{eq}^{(\beta)}(\sigma)
is the Boltzmann distribution at inverse temperature
\beta
and
T^{(\beta)}_{2\,\mathcal{T}}(\sigma|\tau)
is the transition rate from configuration
\tau
to
\sigma
due to Langevin dynamics at inverse temperature
\beta
.
The quantity that generalizes
{\cal A}
as given in eq. (
4
) is then:

{\cal A}(\beta^{\prime}|\beta)\equiv\lim_{\mathcal{T}\rightarrow\infty}\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}{1\over N}\left(\overline{[\ln T_{2\mathcal{T}}]}_{\beta,\beta,\beta^{\prime}}+S(\beta^{\prime})\right)\ ,

(21)

An expression of the above quantity can be easily obtained in terms of second-order dynamical equations as a straightforward generalization of the case where both the initial and final configurations are at equilibrium, see appendix
E
.
One can thus compute the total probability of jumping from an equilibrium state to a state with a different energy
E^{\prime}\neq E_{eq}
, as

p(E^{\prime})\propto\exp N{\cal A}(\beta^{\prime}|\beta)

(22)

where naturally
\beta^{\prime}
is such that
E^{\prime}
is the equilibrium energy at the (inverse) temperature
\beta^{\prime}
.
An explicit computation (see appendix
E
) shows that

\displaystyle\left.\frac{dp(E^{\prime})}{dE^{\prime}}\right|_{E^{\prime}=E_{eq}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0\,\ \ \mathrm{for}\ T\geq T_{d}

\displaystyle\left.\frac{dp(E^{\prime})}{dE^{\prime}}\right|_{E^{\prime}=E_{eq}}

\displaystyle>

\displaystyle 0\,\ \ \mathrm{for}\ T<T_{d}

The behavior for
T>T_{d}
implies that the probability
p(E^{\prime})
has a maximum for
E^{\prime}=E_{eq}
, this was to be expected due to the fact that
p(E_{eq})=1
(see eq. (
5
)) saturates the bound
p(E^{\prime})\leq 1
.
Instead for
T<T_{d}
we have
p(E^{\prime})\gg p
for
E^{\prime}\geq E_{eq}
and the system has a finite probability to jump to one of these higher energy states on a time scale
1/p(E^{\prime})
exponentially smaller than
1/p(E_{eq})
:

1/p(E^{\prime})\ll 1/p(E_{eq})\,\ \ \mathrm{for}\ T<T_{d},E^{\prime}>E_{eq}

(23)

On the other hand the transition rate from a configuration with energy
E^{\prime}
back to a configuration with energy
E
obeys the detailed balance condition:

e^{-\beta H(\tau)}T_{\Delta t}(\tau^{\prime}|\tau)=e^{-\beta H(\tau^{\prime})}T_{\Delta t}(\tau|\tau^{\prime})\ ,

(24)

taking into account that there are
S
configurations with energy
E
and
S^{\prime}
configurations with energy
E^{\prime}

we find that the probability to jump from a state with energy
E^{\prime}

back
to an equilibrium state is

p(E^{\prime})e^{\Delta S-\beta\Delta E}\gg p(E^{\prime})\,,

(25)

i.e.
it is exponentially larger than
p(E^{\prime})
given that, by definition, the free energy
-S+\beta E
has its minimum on the equilibrium states. Therefore, after the system has jumped to a state with higher energy
E^{\prime}>E_{eq}
it will jump
back
to a
generic
equilibrium state on a scale exponentially smaller than
1/p(E^{\prime})
,
i.e.
instantaneously on that scale.
This implies that an intermediate jump to one of the exponentially many metastable states with
E^{\prime}>E_{eq}
provides a more efficient path for restoring ergodicity than a direct jump to another equilibrium state and thus the ergodic scale is smaller than
1/p(E_{eq})
, at variance with ferromagnetism where there are only two metastable states.

It is to be expected that
p(E^{\prime})
reaches a maximum
p(E_{max})\ll 1
at some
E_{max}>E_{eq}
for
T<T_{d}
. The true ergodic time
\tau_{erg}
should be identified with
1/p(E_{max})
but could be even smaller, a detailed analysis of
p(E^{\prime})
and of the correct
\tau_{erg}
is left for future work.

II
Analysis of The Saddle-Point equations of the Spherical
p
-Spin-Glass Model

II.1
The order parameter and its meaning

In the appendix I will derive the expression for the logarithm of the rate
\mathcal{A}
associated to Langevin dynamics of the Spherical
p
-SG model in terms on an order parameter determined through saddle-point equations.
The order parameter is a couple of
2\times 2
matrices of functions of two times
t
and
t^{\prime}
on the square
-\mathcal{T}\leq t,t^{\prime}\leq\mathcal{T}
:

C\equiv\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}C(t,t^{\prime})&\hat{R}_{2}(t,t^{\prime})\\
\hat{R}_{1}(t,t^{\prime})&\hat{X}(t,t^{\prime})\end{array}\right)\,

(26)

C^{dt}\equiv\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}C^{dt}(t,t^{\prime})&\hat{R}_{2}^{dt}(t,t^{\prime})\\
\hat{R}_{1}^{dt}(t,t^{\prime})&\hat{X}^{dt}(t,t^{\prime})\end{array}\right)\,

(27)

The physical meaning of
C(t,t^{\prime})
and
C^{dt}(t,t^{\prime})
has been discussed already:
C(t,t^{\prime})
is the correlation on the same trajectory (therefore
C(t,t)=1
at all times in the spherical and Ising model) while
C^{dt}(t,t^{\prime})
measures the correlations between the configurations visited by
different trajectories
. Since by definition all trajectories have the same initial and final condition we have

C^{dt}(\pm\mathcal{T},t)=C(\pm\mathcal{T},t)\ .

(28)

The correlations are obviously symmetric with respect to
(t,t^{\prime})\rightarrow(t^{\prime},t)
:

\displaystyle C(t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle C(t^{\prime},t)

(29)

\displaystyle C^{dt}(t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle C^{dt}(t^{\prime},t)\ .

(30)

Furthermore given that the measure over the trajectories is invariant under time reversal we have an additional symmetry with respect to the exchange of the initial and final configuration. Given that
t_{in}=-t_{fin}
this symmetry translates into:

\displaystyle C(t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle C(-t,-t^{\prime})

(31)

\displaystyle C^{dt}(t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle C^{dt}(-t,-t^{\prime})

(32)

For the
\hat{R}
components of the order parameter we have:

\displaystyle\hat{R}_{1}(t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\overline{[\langle\hat{x}_{i}(t)s_{i}(t^{\prime})\rangle]}

(33)

\displaystyle\hat{R}_{1}^{dt}(t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\overline{[\langle\hat{x}_{i}(t)\rangle\langle s_{i}(t^{\prime})\rangle]}

(34)

where
\hat{x}
is an auxiliary variable of the dynamics (see the appendix). They translate into:

\displaystyle\hat{R}_{1}(t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\overline{\left[{\delta\langle s_{i}(t^{\prime})\rangle\over\beta\,\delta h(t)}\right]}-{1\over 2}{d\over dt}C(t,t^{\prime})

(35)

\displaystyle\hat{R}_{1}^{dt}(t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\overline{\left[{\delta\ln D(\sigma,\tau)\over\beta\,\delta h(t)}\langle s_{i}(t^{\prime})\rangle\right]}-{1\over 2}{d\over dt}C^{dt}(t,t^{\prime})

(36)

Thus
\hat{R}_{1}(t,t^{\prime})
is connected to the response of the time average of the spin over trajectories to a field
h(t)
. The functions
\hat{R}_{2}(t,t^{\prime})
and
\hat{R}_{2}^{dt}(t,t^{\prime})
are equal to the l.h.s.’s of the above equations with the exchange
t\leftrightarrow t^{\prime}
. Thus while
neither
function is symmetric with respect to
t\leftrightarrow t^{\prime}
they are related through two functions
\hat{R}(t,t^{\prime})
and
\hat{R}^{dt}(t,t^{\prime})
such that

\hat{R}_{1}(t,t^{\prime})=\hat{R}_{2}(t^{\prime},t)=\hat{R}(t^{\prime},t)

(37)

\hat{R}_{1}^{dt}(t,t^{\prime})=\hat{R}_{2}^{dt}(t^{\prime},t)=\hat{R}^{dt}(t^{\prime},t)

(38)

which implies that the matrices (
26
) and (
27
) are symmetric.
On the other hand time-reversal invariance implies that
\hat{R}_{1}(t,t^{\prime})
,
\hat{R}_{2}(t,t^{\prime})
,
\hat{R}_{1}^{dt}(t,t^{\prime})
,
\hat{R}_{2}^{dt}(t,t^{\prime})
, are symmetric with respect to
(t,t^{\prime})\rightarrow(-t,-t^{\prime})
(because
t_{in}=-t_{fin}
as above).
For the
\hat{X}
components we have:

\displaystyle\hat{X}(t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\overline{[\langle\hat{x}_{i}(t)\hat{x}_{i}(t^{\prime})\rangle]}

(39)

\displaystyle\hat{X}^{dt}(t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\overline{[\langle\hat{x}_{i}(t)\rangle\langle\hat{x}_{i}(t^{\prime})\rangle]}

(40)

The physical meaning is also associated to particular responses that takes a simple form in the ergodic phase, see section
II.4
. The above formulas imply that both
\hat{X}(t,t^{\prime})
and
\hat{X}^{dt}(t,t^{\prime})
are symmetric with respect to
t\leftrightarrow t^{\prime}
and to time-reversal
(t,t^{\prime})\rightarrow(-t,-t^{\prime})
.

II.2
The Saddle Point Equations

The saddle-point equations can be written in a compact form which is also suitable for numerical integration considering the space of
2\times 2
matrices whose components are functions of two times
t
and
t^{\prime}
on the square
-\mathcal{T}\leq t,t^{\prime}\leq\mathcal{T}
.
The generic element of this space can be written as

A\equiv\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}C_{A}(t,t^{\prime})&\hat{R}_{2,A}(t,t^{\prime})\\
\hat{R}_{1,A}(t,t^{\prime})&\hat{X}_{A}(t,t^{\prime})\end{array}\right)\,

(41)

Given two elements
A
and
B
in the above space we have a natural definition of the product that generalizes the matrix product (it corresponds to exactly to ordinary matrix products if times are discretized).
For a real function
B(x)
we also define the element-wise function
B[A]

such that

\displaystyle C_{B[A]}(t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle B(C_{A}(t,t^{\prime}))\,,

\displaystyle\hat{R}_{2,B[A]}(t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle B^{\prime}(C_{A}(t,t^{\prime}))\,\hat{R}_{2,A}(t,t^{\prime})\,,

\displaystyle\hat{R}_{1,B[A]}(t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle B^{\prime}(C_{A}(t,t^{\prime}))\,\hat{R}_{1,A}(t,t^{\prime})\,,

\displaystyle\hat{X}_{B[A]}(t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle B^{\prime}(C_{A}(t,t^{\prime}))\,\hat{X}_{A}(t,t^{\prime})+

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle B^{\prime\prime}(C_{A}(t,t^{\prime}))\,\hat{R}_{1,A}(t,t^{\prime})\hat{R}_{2,A}(t,t^{\prime})\,.

We define also:

M\equiv\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}-{1\over 2\Gamma_{0}}\delta^{\prime\prime}(t,t^{\prime})+\hat{\mu}(t)\delta(t,t^{\prime})&\mu(t)\delta(t,t^{\prime})\\
\mu(t)\delta(t,t^{\prime})&-{2\over\Gamma_{0}}\delta(t,t^{\prime})\end{array}\right)\,

(42)

and

T\equiv\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&\delta(t,t^{\prime})\\
\delta(t,t^{\prime})&o\end{array}\right)\,\ .

Note that due to the spherical constraint the operator
M
depends on two additional quantities
\mu(t)
and
\hat{\mu}(t)
, see appendix
A.3
.
In order to write down the saddle-point equations it is useful to introduce two additional objects
\Lambda
and
\Lambda^{dt}
that are also
2\times 2
matrices of two-time functions.
Another useful quantity is:

\delta_{\mp}\equiv\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\hat{R}_{\Lambda,1}(t,\mp\mathcal{T})C(\mp\mathcal{T},t^{\prime})&\hat{R}_{\Lambda,1}(t,\mp\mathcal{T})\,\hat{R}_{2}(\mp\mathcal{T},t^{\prime})\\
C_{\Lambda}(t,\mp\mathcal{T})C(\mp\mathcal{T},t^{\prime})&C_{\Lambda}(t,\mp\mathcal{T})\hat{R}_{2}(\mp\mathcal{T},t^{\prime})\end{array}\right)\,\ .

(43)

With the above definitions the saddle-point equations of the spherical model derived in the appendix take the following compact expressions:

\Lambda=-{\beta^{2}\over 2}f^{\prime}[C]\,,\ \Lambda^{dt}=-{\beta^{2}\over 2}f^{\prime}[C^{dt}]

(44)

\displaystyle MC+\,T\,\Lambda\,T\,C

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle+(n-1)\,T\,\Lambda^{dt}\,T\,\,C^{dt}+\delta_{-}+\delta_{+}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle I

(45)

\displaystyle MC^{dt}+\,T\,\Lambda^{dt}\,T\,\,C+\,T\,\Lambda\,T\,C^{dt}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle+(n-2)\,T\,\Lambda^{dt}\,T\,C^{dt}+\delta_{-}+\delta_{+}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0

(46)

The quantities
\mu(t)
and
\hat{\mu}(t)
are unknown and must be determined self-consistently imposing the conditions:

C(t,t)=1\,,\ \ \hat{R}(t,t)={1\over 2}\ \ \ \forall t\ .

(47)

Due to the presence of the operator
M
that contains second-order derivatives the equations must be also supplemented with the following
boundary conditions
:

\displaystyle C(\pm\mathcal{T},t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle C(t^{\prime},\pm\mathcal{T})

(48)

\displaystyle\hat{R}_{2}(\pm\mathcal{T},t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hat{R}_{1}(t^{\prime},\pm\mathcal{T})

(49)

\displaystyle C^{dt}(\pm\mathcal{T},t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle C^{dt}(t^{\prime},\pm\mathcal{T})

(50)

\displaystyle\hat{R}_{2}^{dt}(\pm\mathcal{T},t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hat{R}^{dt}_{1}(t^{\prime},\pm\mathcal{T})

(51)

Additional boundary conditions follow from the properties of the initial and final configurations:

C(\pm\mathcal{T},\pm\mathcal{T})=1\,,\ \ C(\pm\mathcal{T},\mp\mathcal{T})=0

C^{dt}(\pm\mathcal{T},\pm\mathcal{T})=1\,,\ \ C^{dt}(\pm\mathcal{T},\mp\mathcal{T})=0

The above boundary conditions are sufficient to compute the r.h.s. of the saddle point equations (
45
,
46
) for a generic
C
and
C^{dt}
but given the structure of the equation and the physical meaning of the order parameter the actual solution verifies the additional symmetries discussed previously.
In particular the symmetry of the problem under the exchange between the initial and final configuration implies:

\mu(t)=\mu(-t)\,,\ \ \hat{\mu}(t)=\hat{\mu}(-t)\ .

(52)

In the numerical analysis times are discretized, thus
C
and
C^{dt}
become actual matrices and the above symmetries allow a four-fold reduction of the memory required to store them.

II.3
The free case

In the infinite temperature limit the interaction vanishes and the system performs a free Brownian motion on the
N-1
dimensional sphere.
In this case one can find the analytic solution of the saddle-point equations which, in turn, is useful to guess an initial solution to feed to the Newton’s algorithm at finite temperature.
Besides, as we discussed in the introduction, it allows to bypass the non-renormalizability of the expression for
\overline{[\ln\hat{T}]}
by integrating with respect to the temperature.
The saddle point equations (
45
,
46
) become in the free case:

\displaystyle MC=I\,,\ \ MC^{dt}=0

(53)

The first equation corresponds to:

\displaystyle-2\hat{R}_{1}(t,t^{\prime})+\mu(t)C(t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle-{1\over 2}{d^{2}\over dt^{2}}C(t,t^{\prime})+\mu(t)\hat{R}_{1}(t,t^{\prime})+\hat{\mu}(t)C(t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\delta(t-t^{\prime})

\displaystyle-2\hat{X}(t,t^{\prime})+\mu(t)\hat{R}_{2}(t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\delta(t-t^{\prime})

\displaystyle-{1\over 2}{d^{2}\over dt^{2}}\hat{R}_{2}(t,t^{\prime})+\mu(t)\hat{X}(t,t^{\prime})+\hat{\mu}(t)\hat{R}_{2}(t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0

Note that the equations for
C
do not depend on
C^{dt}
. To solve them one can start noticing that the conditions (
47
) plugged into the first equation lead to

\mu(t)=1\ \ \ \forall t\,.

(54)

Then the first equation and the symmetries
\hat{R}_{2}(t,t^{\prime})=\hat{R}_{1}(t^{\prime},t)
,
C(t,t^{\prime})=C(t^{\prime},t)
and the third equation lead to

\displaystyle\hat{R}_{1}(t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{1\over 2}C(t,t^{\prime})

(55)

\displaystyle\hat{R}_{2}(t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{1\over 2}C(t,t^{\prime})

(56)

\displaystyle\hat{X}(t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-{1\over 2}\delta(t-t^{\prime})+{1\over 4}C(t,t^{\prime})\ .

(57)

As a consequence the correlation
C(t,t^{\prime})
is determined by the following equation:

-{1\over 2}{d^{2}\over dt^{2}}C(t,t^{\prime})+\left({1\over 2}+\hat{\mu}(t)\right)C(t,t^{\prime})=\delta(t-t^{\prime})

To be solved with boundary conditions
C(\pm\mathcal{T},t)=C(t,\pm\mathcal{T})
and
C(\pm\mathcal{T},\pm\mathcal{T})=1
,
C(\pm\mathcal{T},\mp\mathcal{T})=0
.
The equation has the solution

C(t,t^{\prime})=C(|t-t^{\prime}|)\,,\ \ \hat{\mu}(t)=\hat{\mu}\,.

(58)

To determine the function
C(x)
it is convenient to define a quantity
-\infty<a<1
implicitly through the equation:

\mathcal{T}={1\over 2\sqrt{a}}\,{\rm arctanh}\sqrt{a}\ .

(59)

Figure 5:
Left: The function
C(t,t^{\prime})
in the free case for
a=0.999
corresponding to
\mathcal{T}=2.07442
(see text), Right: the function
C^{dt}(t,t^{\prime})
.

We then have, see fig. (
5
):

\displaystyle\hat{\mu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{a-1\over 2}

(60)

\displaystyle C(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\cosh\sqrt{a}x-{1\over\sqrt{a}}\sinh\sqrt{a}x\ .

(61)

Note that we also have
C(0)=1
and
\dot{C}(0)=-1
and these are general results that hold also at finite temperature being a consequence of the presence of the delta functions and of the symmetry
C(t,t^{\prime})=C(t^{\prime},t)
.
Let us discuss the limits of large and small
\mathcal{T}
. For large values of
\mathcal{T}
we have:

a\approx 1-4\,e^{-4\mathcal{T}}\,,\ \hat{\mu}\approx-2\,e^{-4\mathcal{T}}\ \ \mathrm{for}\ \ \mathcal{T}\rightarrow\infty\ .

(62)

Note that in the large
\mathcal{T}
limit
\hat{\mu}
tends to zero and the function
C(x)
tends to the equilibrium solution
C_{eq}(x)=e^{-x}
.
On the other hand
a
becomes negative for
\mathcal{T}<1/2
:

a\approx 6\left(\mathcal{T}-{1\over 2}\right)\ \mathrm{for}\ \ \mathcal{T}\approx{1\over 2}

(63)

therefore
a^{1/2}
is imaginary and the hyperbolic functions in (
61
) become ordinary trigonometric functions:

\displaystyle C(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\cos\sqrt{|a|}x-{1\over\sqrt{|a|}}\sin\sqrt{|a|}x\ .

(64)

In the small
\mathcal{T}
limit
a
tends to minus infinity as

a\approx-{\pi^{2}\over 16\mathcal{T}^{2}}\,,\ \hat{\mu}\approx-{\pi^{2}\over 32\mathcal{T}^{2}}\ \ \mathrm{for}\ \ \mathcal{T}\rightarrow 0

(65)

leading to:

C(x)\approx\cos{\pi x\over 4\mathcal{T}}\ \mathrm{for}\ \ \mathcal{T}\rightarrow 0

(66)

The above expression correctly vanishes at
x=2\mathcal{T}
but does not display the correct behavior
C(x)\approx 1-x
at small
x
. Indeed it is only valid for
x=O(\mathcal{T})
and the correct linear behavior is recovered for
x\ll\mathcal{T}
.
We now turn to the equations for
C^{dt}
:

\displaystyle-2\hat{R}_{1}(t,t^{\prime})+\mu(t)C^{dt}(t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle-{1\over 2}{d^{2}\over dt^{2}}C^{dt}(t,t^{\prime})+\mu(t)\hat{R}_{1}^{dt}(t,t^{\prime})+\hat{\mu}(t)C^{dt}(t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle-2\hat{X}^{dt}(t,t^{\prime})+\mu(t)\hat{R}_{2}^{dt}(t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle-{1\over 2}{d^{2}\over dt^{2}}\hat{R}_{2}^{dt}(t,t^{\prime})+\mu(t)\hat{X}^{dt}(t,t^{\prime})+\hat{\mu}(t)\hat{R}_{2}^{dt}(t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0

The condition
\mu(t)=1
derived earlier, the first equation, the symmetries
\hat{R}_{2}^{dt}(t,t^{\prime})=\hat{R}_{1}^{dt}(t^{\prime},t)
,
C^{dt}(t,t^{\prime})=C^{dt}(t^{\prime},t)
and the third equation lead to:

\displaystyle\hat{R}_{1}^{dt}(t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{1\over 2}\,C^{dt}(t,t^{\prime})

(67)

\displaystyle\hat{R}_{2}^{dt}(t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{1\over 2}\,C^{dt}(t,t^{\prime})

(68)

\displaystyle\hat{X}^{dt}(t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{1\over 4}\,C^{dt}(t,t^{\prime})

(69)

and
C^{dt}(t,t^{\prime})
obeys the equation

-{1\over 2}{d^{2}\over dt^{2}}C^{dt}(t,t^{\prime})+\left({1\over 2}+\hat{\mu}\right)C^{dt}(t,t^{\prime})=0

(70)

To be solved with boundary conditions
C^{dt}(\pm\mathcal{T},t)=C^{dt}(t,\pm\mathcal{T})
and
C^{dt}(\pm\mathcal{T},\pm\mathcal{T})=1
,
C^{dt}(\pm\mathcal{T},\mp\mathcal{T})=0
.
The solution reads:

\displaystyle C^{dt}(t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\sqrt{1-a}\over a}\cosh(\sqrt{a}(t+t^{\prime}))+

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle{1-a\over a}\cosh(\sqrt{a}(t-t^{\prime}))\,,\ \mathrm{for}\ \mathcal{T}>1/2

\displaystyle C^{dt}(t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\sqrt{1-a}\over a}\cos(\sqrt{|a|}(t+t^{\prime}))+

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle{1-a\over a}\cos(\sqrt{|a|}(t-t^{\prime}))\,\ \mathrm{for}\ 0<\mathcal{T}<1/2

In the limit
\mathcal{T}\rightarrow\infty
we have
C^{dt}(t,t^{\prime})=0
for any finite
t,t^{\prime}
. Close to the initial and finite time we have instead

C^{dt}(\pm\mathcal{T}+\delta t,\pm\mathcal{T}+\delta t^{\prime})=C_{eq}(|\delta t+\delta t^{\prime}|)\ \mathrm{for}\ \ \mathcal{T}\rightarrow\infty.

In the limit
\mathcal{T}\rightarrow 0
we obtain instead:

C^{dt}(t,t^{\prime})\approx\cos{\pi(t-t^{\prime})\over 4\mathcal{T}}\ \mathrm{for}\ \ \mathcal{T}\rightarrow 0

Note that in this limit we have also
C^{dt}(t,t^{\prime})\approx C(t,t^{\prime})
meaning that all trajectories tend to follow the same path.

The computation of the transition rate requires to treat carefully the divergences of the path integral and is given in the appendix. The final result is:

{1\over N}\overline{[\ln\hat{T}]}=-{1\over 2}\ln 2\pi-{1\over 2}+(1+\hat{\mu})\mathcal{T}+{1\over 4}\ln{-\hat{\mu}\over 2}\ .

From the above expression we see that the logarithm of the rate tends to minus infinity as
\mathcal{T}
goes to zero:

{1\over N}\overline{[\ln\hat{T}]}\approx-{\pi^{2}\over 32\mathcal{T}}\,,\ \ \ \mathcal{T}\approx 0\ .

For
\mathcal{T}
going to infinity one can see that although
\hat{\mu}\approx-2\,e^{-4\mathcal{T}}
the
O(\mathcal{T})
divergences in the last two terms cancel and the rate has a finite limit. This limit is exactly equal to minus the entropy
S(\beta)
of the spherical model at infinite temperature (
\beta=0
),
i.e.
the logarithm of the surface of the
N
-dimensional sphere of radius
\sqrt{N}
:

\lim_{\mathcal{T}\rightarrow\infty}{1\over N}\overline{[\ln\hat{T}]}=-{1\over 2}\ln 2\pi-{1\over 2}=-S(0)\ .

This is expected in the
\mathcal{T}\rightarrow\infty
limit at any finite
N
and implies that the two limits commute.
One can show that the approach to the
\mathcal{T}\rightarrow\infty
limit is exponential:

{1\over N}\overline{[\ln\hat{T}]}\approx-S(0)-{1\over 2}e^{-4\,\mathcal{T}}\,,\ \ \mathcal{T}\gg 1\ .

II.4
The Ergodic Phase

As explained in the introduction the ergodic phase defined by
T>T_{d}
is characterized by the fact that the
\mathcal{T}\rightarrow\infty
limit commutes with the
N\rightarrow\infty
limit.
In order to discuss the solutions in this regime it is convenient to analyze first the ergodic limit at finite
N
.
While the present formalism is fully invariant under time reversal to discuss this limit it is convenient to reintroduce the arrow of time. In the ergodic limit
\mathcal{T}\rightarrow\infty
dynamics looses any dependence on the initial configuration and the transition rate obeys

\lim_{\mathcal{T}\rightarrow\infty}T_{2\mathcal{T}}(\sigma|\tau)={e^{-\beta H(\sigma)}\over Z}

(71)

that, according to the previous definitions leads to:

\lim_{\mathcal{T}\rightarrow\infty}\hat{T}_{2\mathcal{T}}(\sigma,\tau)={1\over Z}e^{-{\beta\over 2}H(\sigma)-{\beta\over 2}H(\tau)}

(72)

and the following exact result:

\lim_{\mathcal{T}\rightarrow\infty}[\ln\hat{T}_{2\mathcal{T}}(\sigma,\tau)]=-\beta E-\ln Z=-S

(73)

In the ergodic limit we expect that trajectories close to the initial condition at time
-\mathcal{T}
are not influenced by the fact that we are fixing the final configuration at time
\mathcal{T}
. This implies that they are typical trajectories and since the initial configuration is weighted with the equilibrium weight we expect that correlations and response are those valid at equilibrium and in particular satisfy time-translational-invariance (TTI) and fluctuation-dissipation theorem (FDT).
To see the implications on the functions it is convenient to start from the expressions of the six functions as averages of
s_{i}(t)
and
\hat{x}_{i}(t)
obtained in subsection
II.1
.
It is then convenient to transform back to the variable
\hat{s}
introduced in the first steps of the integral represenation (see appendix
A.2
) by writing

\hat{x}_{i}=\hat{s}_{i}(t)-\dot{s}_{i}(t)\ .

(74)

It is well known the equilibrium averages of
\hat{s}_{i}(t)
are associated to responses to a field at time
t
and since according to (
71
) the distribution at
\sigma
is completely independent of what happens at any finite time
t\ll\mathcal{T}
we have for these times

\langle\hat{s}(t)\rangle=0\,,\ \ \langle\hat{s}(t)\hat{s}(t^{\prime})\rangle=0\ .

(75)

The above relationships plus FDT allow to derive the following expressions in the ergodic limit:

\displaystyle C(t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle C_{eq}(|t-t^{\prime}|)

(76)

\displaystyle\hat{R}_{2}(t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hat{R}_{1}(t^{\prime},t)={\rm sign}(t-t^{\prime})\,{1\over 2}\,{d\over dt^{\prime}}C(t,t^{\prime})

(77)

\displaystyle\hat{X}(t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-{1\over 4}{d^{2}\over dt\,dt^{\prime}}C(t,t^{\prime})

(78)

Note that
\hat{R}(t,t^{\prime})
is symmetric (which is not true in the activated phase) and it is equal to
1/2
on the diagonal according to the saddle-point equations.
The expression for
\hat{X}(t,t^{\prime})
is valid also for
t=t^{\prime}
and thus it may be integrated, this is consistent with the fact that
\hat{X}(t,t^{\prime})
has a term proportional
-\delta(t-t^{\prime})/2
on the diagonal.
The above properties have been derived for
t,t^{\prime}\ll\mathcal{T}
, in order to study the region of times close to
\mathcal{T}
it is better to reverse the arrow of time, following the same arguments we obtain the above equations with
(t,t^{\prime})\rightarrow(-t,-t^{\prime})
and since the functions are symmetric with respect to this transformation we conclude that they are valid at all times.

At large values of
\mathcal{T}
the correlation between different trajectories are different from zero only for times
t,t^{\prime}
that are both close to either
-\mathcal{T}
or
\mathcal{T}
. To determine them we can use the following relationship :

\sum_{\tau}{e^{-\beta H(\tau)}\over Z}T_{\Delta t}(\tau^{\prime}|\tau)T_{\Delta t^{\prime}}(\tau^{\prime\prime}|\tau)={e^{-\beta H(\tau^{\prime})}\over Z}T_{\Delta t+\Delta t^{\prime}}(\tau^{\prime\prime}|\tau^{\prime})

(79)

that follows from the detailed balance condition:

e^{-\beta H(\tau)}T_{\Delta t}(\tau^{\prime}|\tau)=e^{-\beta H(\tau^{\prime})}T_{\Delta t}(\tau|\tau^{\prime})

(80)

and from the general property:

\sum_{\tau}T_{\Delta t^{\prime}}(\tau^{\prime\prime}|\tau)\,T_{\Delta t}(\tau|\tau^{\prime})=T_{\Delta t+\Delta t^{\prime}}(\tau^{\prime\prime}|\tau^{\prime})\ .

(81)

Furthermore responses between different trajectories vanish because of
\langle\hat{s}(t)\rangle=0
.
In particular if we write times as
t=\mp\mathcal{T}\pm\Delta t
(
\Delta t\geq 0
) we have:

\displaystyle C^{dt}(t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle C_{eq}(\Delta t+\Delta t^{\prime})

(82)

\displaystyle\hat{R}_{2}^{dt}(t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hat{R}_{1}^{dt}(t^{\prime},t)=\mp{1\over 2}\,{d\over dt^{\prime}}C^{dt}(t,t^{\prime})

(83)

\displaystyle\hat{X}^{dt}(t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{1\over 4}{d^{2}\over dt\,dt^{\prime}}C^{dt}(t,t^{\prime})\ .

(84)

Note that the expression for
\hat{R}_{2}^{dt}(t,t^{\prime})
changes sign depending on weather we are close to
\mp\mathcal{T}
, indeed the second case (
+\mathcal{T}
) is obtained from the transformation
(t,t^{\prime})\rightarrow(-t,-t^{\prime})
applied to the first case (
-\mathcal{T}
).
A solution of the saddle-point equations with the above structure can be found only if
C_{eq}(\infty)=0
, thus the ergodic solution does not exist below
T_{d}
where
C_{eq}(\infty)=q
in the thermodynamic limit and the condition
C(\pm\mathcal{T},\mp\mathcal{T})=0
cannot be fulfilled.
The above expressions are valid for both Ising and spherical models, in addition for the spherical model we have:

\mu(t)=\mu_{eq}\,,\ \ \ \hat{\mu}(t)=0\,.

(85)

Note that all components are parameterized by the equilibrium correlation
C_{eq}(t)
, let us see how an equation for
C_{eq}(t)
can be derived in the present context. In the formulation of the problem discussed in the appendix one works with objects
\bm{Q(ab)}
. In the ergodic phase one can additionally introduce a special class of objects
\bm{A(ab)}
whose components
A(ab)
and
A^{dt}(ab)
obey formulas (
76
,
77
,
78
) and (
82
,
83
,
84
) with
C_{eq}(t)
replaced by some generic function
C_{A}(t)
.
In the following this kind of objects will be loosely referred as having an ‘equilibrium” structure. A simple computation shows that the equilibrium structure is preserved by the application of a function,
i.e.

f(\bm{A(ab)})
has also the equilibrium structure with:

f(\bm{A(ab)})\rightarrow C_{f[A]}(t)=f(C_{A}(t))

(86)

Similarly, given another equilibrium object
\bm{B(ab)}
parameterized by a function
C_{B}(t)
, one can show through a tedious computation that the product has also the equilibrium structure with:

\int\bm{A(ac)B(cb)dc}\rightarrow C_{AB}(t)

C_{AB}(t)=C_{A}(0)C_{B}(t)-\int_{0}^{t}C_{A}(t-s){dC_{B}(s)\over ds}ds

The above equation holds for
C_{A}(\infty)=C_{B}(\infty)=0
which is granted by the fact that we are working in zero field and
f^{\prime}(0)=0
so that the overlap between different equilibrium states is zero.
The last two equations applied to the
saddle-point equation (
136
)
lead immediately to the equilibrium equation:

{\dot{C}}(t)=-\,C(t)-{\beta^{2}\over 2}\int_{0}^{t}f^{\prime}(C(t-s))\,\dot{C}(s)\,ds

(87)

where we have used
\mu=1+{\beta^{2}\over 2}f^{\prime}(1)
that follows from the condition
C(0)=-\dot{C}(0)=1
.
The above equations is usually written as
Crisanti and Sommers 1992
;
Crisanti et al. 1993
;
Castellani and Cavagna 2005
:

\displaystyle\dot{C}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\,C(t)+{\beta^{2}\over 2}f^{\prime}(C(t))(1-C(t))+

(88)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle{\beta^{2}\over 2}\int_{0}^{t}(f^{\prime}(C(t-s))-f^{\prime}(C(t)))\,{dC\over ds}\,ds

Where
C(\infty)
is the solution of the equation

C={\beta^{2}\over 2}f^{\prime}(C)(1-C)\ .

(89)

This equation admits the solution
C=0
at all temperature but develops an additional non-zero solution at
T_{d}
specified by the condition:

1={\beta_{d}^{2}\over 2}(f^{\prime\prime}(C_{d})(1-C_{d})-f^{\prime}(C_{d}))

(90)

In the pure
p
-spin models
f(x)=x^{p}
we have

T_{d}=\sqrt{{p(p-2)^{p-2}\over 2(p-1)^{p-1}}}\,,\ \ C_{d}(\infty)={p-2\over p-1}\ \ .

(91)

To conclude the discussion of the ergodic phase we have to show that for
T>T_{d}
the
\mathcal{T}\rightarrow\infty
and
N\rightarrow\infty
limits commute and we have

\mathcal{A}=0\,\ \ \mathrm{for}\ \,T_{d}<T<\infty

(92)

In the previous section we have shown that the relationship is satisfied for
\beta=1/T=0
and thus it suffices to show that its derivative with respect to the
\beta
is also zero.
In appendix
E
we show that the derivative of the rate with respect to
\beta
is
{\beta\over 2}{d\over d\,n}\int d{\bf a}d{\bf b}f({\bf Q(a,b)})
. The above expression takes a very simple form in the ergodic phase, indeed using the above equilibrium formulas one can show that the generic formula (
191
) for the integrals takes a very simple form:

\displaystyle\int\bm{B(ab)dadb}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(m+m^{\prime})C_{B}(0)+n\left(4\int R_{2,B}(-\mathcal{T},t)dt+\int\hat{X}_{B}(t,t^{\prime})\,dt\,dt^{\prime}+(n-1)\int\hat{X}_{B^{dt}}(t,t^{\prime})\,dt\,dt^{\prime}\right)=

(93)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(m+m^{\prime}+n)C_{B}(0)

that leads to:

{d\over d\beta}\lim_{\mathcal{T}\rightarrow\infty}\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}{1\over N}\overline{[\ln\hat{T}]}={\beta\over 2}f(1)\,,

(94)

to be compared with

{1\over N}{dS(\beta)\over d\beta}=-{\beta\over 2}f(1)\ .

(95)

We thus see that eq. (
92
) is verified:

\lim_{\mathcal{T}\rightarrow\infty}\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}{1\over N}\overline{[\ln\hat{T}]}=-S(\beta)=-S(0)+{\beta^{2}\over 4}f(1)\ .

(96)

The above relationship is valid also for Ising systems.

II.6
Numerical Solution

II.6.1
Newton-Krylov Methods

To solve the equations numerically time was discretized in steps
\Delta t=\mathcal{T}/N_{t}
for integer
N_{t}
up to
N_{t}=2000
. The six functions can then be jointly represented as two real matrices
C
and
C^{dt}
of size
2(2N_{t}+1)
so that the total number of variables is
O(32N_{t}^{2})
.
Using standard formulas for discrete integrals and second-order derivatives (see below) one obtains expressions with an
O(\Delta t^{2})
error.

It turned out that the equations can be solved by Newton’s method. To initialize the algorithm an approximate solution not too far away from the correct solution can be obtained from the analytic solutions in the free case.
One can start from
\beta=0
at finite
\mathcal{T}
and switch on the temperature, then at fixed temperature,
\mathcal{T}
can be changed changing the discretization parameter
\Delta t
at fixed
N_{t}
by small amounts. At fixed
\mathcal{T}
and
\beta
,
\Delta t
can be reduced extrapolating the result of a coarser grid to a finer grid (larger
N_{t}
) and using it as a starting point for Newton’s method at the new
N_{t}
.
The main technical problem is that every iteration of Newton’s method requires to invert the Jacobian of the equations, a
(32N_{t}^{2})\times(32N_{t}^{2})
matrix. Even exploiting the symmetries of the problem, with current technology
exact
inversion of the Jacobian becomes unfeasible for
N_{t}
of the order
70-80
limiting the values of
\mathcal{T}
that can be studied.

N_{t}
could instead be increased up to
2000
using an approximate method for the well-studied problem
Saad 2003
of solving a very large linear system
A\,x=b
. Specifically I used the Generalized Minimal Residue (GMRES) algorithm
Saad and Schultz 1986
;
Saad 2003
that requires the computation of the Krylov subspace of order
k
, defined by the vectors
\{b,A\,b,A^{2}\,b,A^{3}\,b,\dots,A^{k}b\}
.
The advantage of Krylov methods is that one always work with vectors
v
and has to perform matrix multiplications
A\,v
without the need to store the full
(32N_{t}^{2})\times(32N_{t}^{2})
matrix
A
.
In GMRES one searches for an approximate solution in the Krylov sub-space of order
k
: an orthonormal basis is obtained using a numerically stable Gram-Schmidt orthogonalisation called Arnoldi iteration and the solution is then found by least squares minimization of the linear equations in this space.
The advantage of the method is that the error decreases systematically increasing
k
, the drawback is that it requires to generate and store all the
k
vectors of the basis and
k
cannot be too small to obtain accurate solutions.
Two important ingredients that are key to the efficiency of the procedure are
preconditioning and compression to be discussed later.
I wrote a code using Mathematica (retrievable in the ancillary files section of
Rizzo 2020b
) being able to reach values of
N_{t}=2000
with
k=400
using up to 90
\%
memory on a cluster with 256 Giga of RAM.

II.6.2
Richardson Extrapolation

Once the numerical solutions at fixed
\beta
and
\mathcal{T}
are obtained for various
N_{t}
, a polynomial (Richardson) extrapolation is essential to reach the
\Delta t=0
limit and remove a few pathologies of the finite
\Delta t
solutions.
Let us discuss first the order of the algorithm.
For the second derivatives appearing in the equations I have used the formula

f^{\prime\prime}(t)\approx{f(t+\Delta t)+f(t-\Delta t)-2f(t)\over\Delta t}

(98)

where

\Delta t=\mathcal{T}/N_{t}\,.

(99)

The above formula has a
O(\Delta t^{2})
error if
f(t)
has continuous derivatives up to the third order.
The integrals have been written as

\int_{-\mathcal{T}}^{+\mathcal{T}}f(t)dt\approx{\Delta t\over 2}(f(-\mathcal{T})+f(+\mathcal{T}))+\sum_{i=-N_{t}+1}^{N_{t}-1}f(i\,\Delta t)\Delta t\ .

(100)

The above trapezoidal rule also has a
O(\Delta t^{2})
error for a continuous function.
As mentioned before, the full algorithm has a
O(\Delta t^{2})
error, however this is
not
trivial because the functions
C(t,t^{\prime})
,
\hat{R}(t,t^{\prime})
and
\hat{X}(t,t^{\prime})
have discontinuous odd derivatives for
t=t^{\prime}
. While the discontinuity of the first derivative is canceled by the delta function the discontinuity of the third derivative leads to a
O(\Delta t)
error in expression (
98
) on the diagonal
t=t^{\prime}
.
However on the diagonal the order of the equation is not
O(1)
but
O(1/\Delta t)
due to the presence of the delta function in eq. (
167
) and of
\hat{X}(t,t^{\prime})
(that can be written as a delta function on the diagonal plus a regular part) in eq. (
168
), thus even if the absolute error on the diagonal is
O(\Delta t)
, the
relative
error is
O(\Delta t^{2})
.
In Fig. (
11
) it is demonstrated that the error is indeed
O(\Delta t^{2})
for
\mu(t)
and it is shown how that the
\Delta t\rightarrow 0
limit is safely reached by polynomial extrapolations.
The figures discussed in the previous sections were all obtained by means of polynomial extrapolations on the largest
N_{t}
set available (up to
N_{t}=2000
for
\mathcal{T}=32,40
) using in most cases a fourth order form
c_{0}+c_{2}\,\Delta t^{2}+c_{3}\,\Delta t^{3}+c_{4}\,\Delta t^{4}
where the vanishing of the linear term
\Delta t
was imposed.
One should note that the finite
N_{t}
curves often display pathologies due to the discretisation that tend to be less severe increasing
N_{t}
. For instance in the right of fig. (
11
) we see that
\mu(t)
at finite
N_{t}
displays cusps close to
t=\pm\mathcal{T}
that are absent for
N_{t}\rightarrow\infty
. It is impressing how a simple polynomial extrapolation over
N_{t}
leads to the disappearance of these spurious features and allows to obtain accurate predictions
with relatively small values of
N_{t}
that are indistinguishable from extrapolations obtained from considerably larger values of
N_{t}
.
The same features are also seen in fig. (
12
), in this case corrections higher that
O(\Delta t^{2})
are so small that too high-order interpolation functions overfit that data and it is convenient to use the form
c_{0}+c_{2}\,\Delta t^{2}
. Besides we see that, even if the finite
N_{t}
results for
\hat{\mu}(t)
have pronounced spurious cusps close to
t\pm\mathcal{T}
and may even have the wrong sign, the extrapolations are cuspless and negative for all
t
.

II.6.3
Preconditioning

Efficient Krylov methods often require preconditioning which amount to reduce the span of the eivengalues of
A
. The equations in the form (
45
) and (
46
) are ill-conditioned because the operator
M
contains second-order derivatives leading to an unbounded continuous spectrum.
To overcome this problem I have multiplied equations (
45
) and (
46
) times
M^{-1}
. The corresponding Jacobian turns out to have a discrete and bounded spectrum as can be seen numerically using the fact that the Arnoldi diagonalization allows to obtain an approximate set of eigenvalues and eigenvectors.
More details on the procedure can be found in the commented Mathematica codes provided in the ancillary files section of
Rizzo 2020b
.

II.6.4
Compression

To compute the equations and the matrix-times-vector products
J\,c
involving the Jacobian
J
we have to store huge matrices and perform multiplications and element-wise operations, for which many highly-optimized and parallelized libraries exist.
One can solve eq. (
45
) and (
46
) for generic
C
and
C^{dt}
and verify that as expected the algorithm converges to solutions with the required symmetries discussed in sec.
II.1
. However it is useful to work from the start in the subspace of solutions with the required symmetries obtaining a four-fold reductions of the memory required to store
C
and
C^{dt}
and thus the generic element of the Krylov subspace. Besides it turns out that this allows to consider a smaller Krylov subspace to obtain the same level of accuracy. Explicit use of the symmetry however requires an efficient procedure to quickly compress and decompress the large matrices
C
and
C^{dt}
and the elements of the Krylov subspace.
The choice depends on the specific linear algebra programming tool used.

III
Concluding Perspectives

In this work I have shown how the transition rate can be computed in mean-field models. As stated in the introduction, the main motivation to perform such a computation is to obtain dynamical quantitative predictions not accessible through static methods. Besides the most interesting physical outcome of the computation is to shed new light on the role of the complex landscape for ergodicity restoring processes as discussed in section
I.1.3
:
the conclusion is that to visit different equilibrium states it is more convenient to make intermediate jumps to off-equilibrium metastable states rather than direct jumps from one equilibrium state to another.

The methods discussed here can be applied to other mean-field systems, in the introduction we mentioned supercooled liquids models in high dimensions but the Langevin equation can be also viewed as an algorithm in the wider family of (stochastic) gradient-descent algorithms that are widely used in the context of machine-learning and statistical inference.
The analysis of the algorithm performance in the context of high-dimensional inference has been initiated in
Mannelli et al. 2020

unveiling a glassy phase that limits the efficiency of the algorithm. Those results where obtained in the thermodynamic limit where the evolution of the algorithm can be associated to relaxational dynamic equations similar to those of the spherical
p
-spin SG model
Crisanti et al. 1993
;
Cugliandolo and
Kurchan 1993
recently reconsidered in
Folena et al. 2020
. On the hand other realistic problems have
finite
size and thus the algorithm can overcome those thresholds albeit with larger convergence times that can be studied by the methods presented here switching from the initial value dynamical equations studied in
Mannelli et al. 2020
to boundary value dynamical equations.
Concerning the extension to supercooled liquid in high dimensions, the equations can be derived and solved in principle by the GMRES algorithm although the actual implementation is likely to be considerably more complex than for the spherical model
Manacorda et al. 2020
.
In this respect it is worth noticing that for more complex problems one can use a Jacobian-free method, in which the product
J\,v
required by Newton-Krylov methods is approximated by
E(c+\epsilon\,v)-E(c))/\epsilon
for small
\epsilon
and thus only the numerical computation of the equations
E(c)
is required.
Furthermore other algorithms exist that do not require to store the whole Krylov Space,
e.g.
Biconjugated Gradient
Saad 2003
; in general they are less safe and controlled than GMRES, but an efficient algorithm requiring less memory would be welcomed. Note that GMRES allows to have an approximation for the spectrum of the true Jacobian, which could be useful information to devise alternative algorithms.

We have mentioned that in the
asymptotic
regime
\mathcal{T}\rightarrow\infty
the solutions solve
\mathcal{T}
-independent scaling equations with the time derivatives dropped.
In the context of off-equilibrium dynamics the same thing happens and allows to establish a remarkable connection with the equations of the static/replica framework
Cugliandolo and
Kurchan 1993
. This grants that the phase diagram and many non-trivial off-equilibrium quantities can be determined without solving explicitly the dynamical equations and it would be interesting to determine if there is also some static potential from which the results of the
\mathcal{T}\rightarrow\infty
limit can be recovered without solving the dynamics. A natural question is weather such a static potential is the celebrated Franz-Parisi potential
Franz and Parisi 1997
that develops a secondary minimum at
T_{d}
. One should indeed remember that, while the minimum is associated to equilibrium states and configurational complexity, it is not known if the potential
difference
between the secondary minimum and the maximum is in fact associated to any quantity computed by actual dynamical methods as those considered here.

An interesting open question is how one should expect the solutions to look like at
T_{d}
, in particular in the asymptotic form. Some guidance could be offered by the fact that in the
T\rightarrow T_{d}
limit the results should match somehow those obtained in
Rizzo 2014
;
Rizzo and Voigtmann 2015
;
Rizzo 2016a
;
Rizzo 2016b
;
Rizzo and Voigtmann 2020
by considering finite-size/finite-dimensional systems directly at
T=T_{d}
(from a technical point of view the two approaches differ by which one of the limits
T\rightarrow T_{d}
or
N\rightarrow\infty
is taken first). This suggests for instance that the important physics occurs close to the plateau value of the overlap.

It should be noted that the dynamical equations have been solved by making a Replica-Symmetric ansatz on the
n
dynamical replicas introduced to compute the logarithm of the rate (see eq. (
139
) in appendix
B
) and one may ask if it yields correct results or a RSB ansatz should be used. While a full answer requires a complicated analysis of the stability of the saddle-point equations, an instability could nonetheless manifests itself in some inconsistent value of some physical quantity, much as the RS entropy of the Sherrington-Kirkpatrick model becomes negative at low temperatures
Mézard et al. 1987
. This is not the case as the numerical solution did not show any visible inconsistency suggesting that the RS ansatz is correct in the range of temperatures considered.

To conclude we recall that in section
I.1.3
it was shown that the presence of many metastable states implies that the
exponentially small
probability that the system jumps to another equilibrium state in a
finite
time is not trivially related to
the
exponentially large
time-scale
\tau_{erg}
over which the system jumps to another equilibrium state with
finite
probability. A question worth of further investigation is weather the latter could also be computed by similar methods.

Appendix A
Path Integral Expression of the Rate

A.1
Replicas

The logarithm in the quenched average can be eliminated by the Replica method

\sum_{\sigma,\tau}P_{eq}(\sigma)P_{eq}(\tau)\,\ln\hat{T}(\sigma,\tau)\ ,

(101)

furthermore we consider systems with quenched disorder whose equilibrium properties can also be studied by the replica method, thus we introduce the following object:

Z_{\hat{T}}(m,m^{\prime},n)\equiv\sum_{\sigma_{1}\dots\sigma_{m}}\sum_{\tau_{1}\dots\tau_{m^{\prime}}}\exp\left[-\beta\sum_{i=1}^{m}H_{J}(\sigma_{i})-\beta\sum_{i=1}^{m^{\prime}}H_{J}(\tau_{i})\right]\hat{T}(\sigma_{1},\tau_{1})^{n}

(102)

and we have:

\sum_{\sigma,\tau}P_{eq}(\sigma)P_{eq}(\tau)\,\ln\hat{T}(\sigma,\tau)=\lim_{m\rightarrow 0}\lim_{m^{\prime}\rightarrow 0}\lim_{n\rightarrow 0}{d\over dn}\ln Z_{\hat{T}}(m,m^{\prime},n)\ .

(103)

As usual in the context of mean-field models the thermodynamic limit is taken before the above limits. Note that, the quantity
\lim_{m\rightarrow 0,m^{\prime}\rightarrow 0}\,Z_{\hat{T}}(m,m^{\prime},n)
at finite
n
allows to study the large deviations of
\ln\hat{T}(\sigma,\tau)
and determine if it is self-averaging with respect to the equilibrium configurations
\sigma
and
\tau
.
As we will see in the next subsection it is possible to obtain an integral representation of the dynamics (see
Zinn-Justin 2002
, chapter 17):

\hat{T}(\sigma_{1},\tau_{1})=\int_{s(-\mathcal{T})=\sigma_{1},s(\mathcal{T})=\tau_{1}}ds\exp\left[{1\over 2}\int d1d2s(1)\Gamma(1,2)s(2)-\beta\int d1H_{J}(s(1))\right]\ .

(104)

Where the
1
and
2
are coordinates that collect a time variable and Fermionic variables introduced to obtain the integral representation.
The expression is made of two parts: a universal dynamic one encoded by the matrix
\Gamma(1,2)
and an interaction part that depends on the model encoded in the Hamiltonian
H_{J}
.
The above expressions leads to:

\displaystyle Z_{\hat{T}}(m,m^{\prime},n)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma_{1}\dots\sigma_{m}}\sum_{\tau_{1}\dots\tau_{m^{\prime}}}\prod_{a=1}^{n}\left[\int_{s_{a}(-\mathcal{T})=\sigma_{1},s_{a}(\mathcal{T})=\tau_{1}}[ds_{a}]\right]\exp\left[\sum_{a=1}^{n}{1\over 2}\int d1d2s_{a}(1)\Gamma(1,2)s_{a}(2)+\right.

(105)

\displaystyle\left.-\beta\sum_{i=1}^{m}H_{J}(\sigma_{i})-\beta\sum_{i=1}^{m^{\prime}}H_{J}(\tau_{i})-\beta\sum_{a=1}^{n}\int d1H_{J}(s_{a}(1))\right]\ ,

this in turn can be written in a compact form as:

Z_{\hat{T}}(m,m^{\prime},n)=\int[d{\bf s}]\exp\left[{1\over 2}\int{\bf d1d2s(1)\Gamma(1,2)s(2)}-\beta\int d{\bf 1}H_{J}({\bf s(1)})\right]

(106)

where the bold index
{\bf 1}
runs over the replicas and the dynamical indexes

{\bf s(1)}=\{\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{m},s_{1}(1),\dots,s_{n}(1),\tau_{1},\dots,\tau_{m^{\prime}}\}

(107)

and we have:

\int f({\bf s(1)})d{\bf 1}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}f(\sigma_{i})+\sum_{i=1}^{m^{\prime}}f(\tau_{i})+\sum_{a=1}^{n}\int d1f(s_{a}(1))\ .

(108)

The dynamical operator
{\bf\Gamma(1,2)}
is diagonal with respect to the replica indexes and associates each dynamical replica with the boundary conditions at
\sigma_{1}
and
\tau_{1}
. Its definition in the specific representation we will use is given in eq.
161
.
As usual the great advantage of having the above compact representation is that one can perform the average of the disorder and then perform standard manipulations yielding an expression formally identical to the one obtained in the case of a static replica computation.

A.2
Path Integral Representation of Langevin Dynamics

This section discusses the path integral representation of Langevin dynamics, given in terms of a single component
q
. The one-dimensional case is discussed for simplicity, the generalisation to
N
-dimensional vectors used in the paper is straightforward.
The Langevin equation reads

{1\over\Gamma_{0}}\dot{q}=-\beta{dH\over dq}+\xi\,,\ \langle\xi(t)\xi(t^{\prime})\rangle={2\over\Gamma_{0}}\delta(t-t^{\prime})

(109)

and we discretize it as:

{1\over\Gamma_{0}}{q_{i+i}-q_{i}\over\Delta t}=-\beta\left(c\,{dH\over dq_{i}}+(1-c){dH\over dq_{i+1}}\right)+\xi_{i}

(110)

where
0\leq c\leq 1
is an arbitrary constant: in Itô discretization with have
c=1
, in Stratonovich we have
c=1/2
.
Enforcing the equations through an integral representation we can write the average over trajectories at fixed initial and final conditions as an integral

T(\sigma|\tau)=\int\left(\prod_{i=1}^{N-1}dq_{i}\right)\left(\prod_{i=0}^{N-1}{d\hat{q}_{i}\over 2\pi}\right)\times

\times\exp\left[-\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\left(\hat{q}_{i}E_{i}\Delta t-\ln\left[{1\over\Gamma_{0}}-(1-c)\beta{d^{2}H\over dq_{i+1}^{2}}\,\Delta t\right]\right)\right]

where the interval between the initial and final time is divided in
N
sub-intervals of size
\Delta t
and:

E_{i}\equiv{1\over\Gamma_{0}}{q_{i+i}-q_{i}\over\Delta t}+\beta\left(c\,{dH\over dq_{i}}+(1-c){dH\over dq_{i+1}}\right)-\xi_{i}

(111)

The logarithm comes from the determinant of the Jacobian that gets contribution only from the diagonal since the Jacobian is a triangular matrix.
Expanding the Jacobian at first order in
\Delta t
we obtain:

T(\sigma|\tau)=\int\left(\prod_{i=1}^{N-1}dq_{i}\right)\left(\prod_{i=0}^{N-1}{d\hat{q}_{i}\over 2\pi\Gamma_{0}}\right)\exp[-\mathcal{L}]

(112)

The Lagrangian reads:

\mathcal{L}=\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\left(\hat{q}_{i}E_{i}\Delta t-(1-c)\beta{d^{2}H\over dq_{i+1}^{2}}\,\Gamma_{0}\,\Delta t\right)

(113)

and in the continuum limit we have

{\mathcal{L}}=\int dt\left[{1\over\Gamma_{0}}\left(\hat{q}\dot{q}-\hat{q}^{2}\right)+\hat{q}\beta{dH\over dq}-(1-c)\Gamma_{0}\beta{d^{2}H\over dq^{2}}\right]\ .

(114)

Note that unexpectedly the continuum limit expression depends on the microscopic parameter
c
of the discretization while one would expect it to be irrelevant. This is a well-known ambiguity of path integral representation of stochastic equations. One can choose to use the Itô discretization corresponding to
c=1
and neglect it but it will resurface later in the computation.
In the following we prefer to keep it also to remind us that the continuum limit of stochastic equations must be taken with care as the ordinary rules of calculus (integration by parts, differentiation, chain rules) are modified.
Beside we will use Hamiltonians where the interaction part is just linear (the
p
-spin interactions) and thus in the end we will go back to special Langevin equation for a single variable.
Let us consider the symmetric rate defined as

\hat{T}(\sigma,\tau)\equiv T(\sigma|\tau)e^{{\beta\over 2}\left(H(\sigma)-H(\tau)\right)}

(115)

In the continuum limit we would expect the following to be an equality

{\beta\over 2}\left(H(\sigma)-H(\tau)\right)\neq{\beta\over 2}\int_{-\mathcal{T}}^{+\mathcal{T}}dt\,\dot{q}{\partial H\over dq}\ .

(116)

Instead in order to get the correct expression we should go back to the discretized expression. We have:

H(q_{i+1})-H(q_{i})={dH\over dq_{i}}\Delta q_{i}+{1\over 2}{d^{2}H\over dq_{i}^{2}}\Delta q_{i}^{2}

(117)

then we have to use the fact that in the Lagrangian we use the following discretized definition of
dH/dq

c\,{dH\over dq_{i}}+(1-c){dH\over dq_{i+1}}\ .

(118)

By rewriting the differential as

{dH\over dq_{i}}=\left(c\,{dH\over dq_{i}}+(1-c){dH\over dq_{i+1}}\right)-(1-c){d^{2}H\over dq_{i}^{2}}\Delta q_{i}

(119)

we obtain

dH={dH\over dq}dq+\left(c-{1\over 2}\right){d^{2}H\over dq^{2}}dq^{2}\ .

(120)

We can see that for
c=1
we recover Itô’s lemma while for the Stratonovich prescription
c=1/2
we find that the ordinary chain rule applies.
The second term cannot be neglected because it gives an
O(dt)
contribution but we can make the replacement

dq^{2}=2\,\Gamma_{0}\,dt

(121)

and obtain

{\beta\over 2}\left(H(\sigma)-H(\tau)\right)={\beta\over 2}\int_{-\mathcal{T}}^{+\mathcal{T}}dt\left({dH\over dq}\dot{q}+\left(c-{1\over 2}\right){d^{2}H\over dq^{2}}2\,\Gamma_{0}\right)\ .

The same result can also be obtained reabsorbing the term
\Delta q^{2}
in the term
\dot{q}^{2}
below (see
e.g.

Zinn-Justin 2002
, section 4.6).
Making the following change of variable

\hat{q}=\hat{x}+{\dot{q}\over 2}

(122)

we finally obtain

\hat{\mathcal{L}}(t)={1\over\Gamma_{0}}\left({\dot{q}^{2}\over 4}-\hat{x}^{2}\right)+\hat{x}\beta{dH\over dq}-{1\over 2}\Gamma_{0}\beta{d^{2}H\over dq^{2}}\ .

(123)

We can further integrate out the
\hat{x}
:

\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}(t)={1\over\Gamma_{0}}{\dot{q}^{2}\over 4}+{\Gamma_{0}\over 4}\left(\beta{dH\over dq}\right)^{2}-{1\over 2}\Gamma_{0}\beta{d^{2}H\over dq^{2}}\ ,

(124)

see also
Zinn-Justin 2002
, pag. 70.
Note that the integration over
\hat{x}
leads to a divergent prefactor to the above path integral:

{1\over 2\pi\Gamma_{0}}\int d\hat{x}e^{\hat{x}^{2}\over\Gamma_{0}}dt={1\over 2\pi\Gamma_{0}}\left(2\pi{\Gamma_{0}\over 2\,dt}\right)^{1/2}\ .

(125)

which is usually buried into the expression
[dq]
defined as:

[dq]\equiv dq\,\left({1/(2\Gamma_{0})\over 2\pi dt}\right)^{N/2}

(126)

see eq. 2.20 in
Zinn-Justin 2002
.

A.3
Path Integral Representation for Models with Multi-Linear Interactions

Typical mean-field SG models have interactions that are multi-linear, while the non-linear part of the Hamiltonian is local and does not need to be decoupled in order to obtain a saddle point expression. This allows to use simplified integral representations of the dynamics in which fewer variables are introduced with respect to the general case discussed in
Zinn-Justin 2002
, chapter 17.
In the following we introduce a bosonic variable
\eta
that behaves as the product of two Grassmann variables, that is we have:

\eta^{2}=0\,,\ \int d\eta=0\,,\ \int\eta\,d\eta=1

(127)

with it we define a new coordinate
1\equiv(t,\eta)
and field
s(1)
:

s_{i}(1)\equiv s_{i}(t)+\hat{x}_{i}(t)\eta\ .

(128)

With the above definitions the interacting part of the multi-linear Hamiltonian can be written as:

\beta\int dt\sum_{i}\hat{x}_{i}(t){dH\over ds_{i}}=\beta\int d1H(s(1))\ .

(129)

A similar formulation is also useful in the spherical model. In this case however the non-linear part of the Hamiltonian is due to the spherical constraint which is not local and must be treated appropriately.
Let us consider the problem of the integral representation of Langevin dynamics of
N
real spins
s_{i}
constrained on a
(N-1)
-dimensional surface specified by some condition
G(s)=0
. The statics of the problem can be written as

\int d^{N}s\,\delta(G)|\nabla G|e^{-\beta H(s)}

(130)

A convenient way to define Langevin dynamics on the surface is to relax the delta function replacing it with a Gaussian of infinitesimal variance
\epsilon
. Then one have to compute the standard dynamical integral in presence of a Hamiltonian
H(x)=G^{2}(x)/(2N\epsilon)
. For the spherical constraint on
N
continuous spins
s_{i}
we have

G(s)\equiv\sum_{i=1}^{N}s_{i}^{2}-N

(131)

the term
|\nabla G|
is exactly equal to
N
and can be ignored, For
H(x)=G^{2}(x)/(2N\epsilon)
we have:

\sum_{i}\hat{x}_{i}\beta{dH\over dq_{i}}-{1\over 2}\Gamma_{0}\beta\sum_{i}{d^{2}H\over dq_{i}^{2}}={\beta\over N\epsilon}G(s)\left(2\sum_{i}\hat{x}_{i}s_{i}-\Gamma_{0}N\right)

(132)

plus
o(N)
terms. The expression can be decoupled through a Hubbard-Stratonovich transformation in terms of two additional fields
\mu(t)
and
\tilde{\mu}(t)
and taking the limit
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
the quadratic part disappears leading to the following contribution to the action:

-{1\over 2}\int dt\hat{\mu}(t)\left(\sum_{i}s_{i}^{2}-N\right)-{1\over 2}\int dt{\mu}(t)\left(2\sum_{i}\hat{x}_{i}s_{i}-\Gamma_{0}N\right)\ .

Introducing the variable
\mu(1)=\mu(t)+\hat{\mu}(t)\eta
we can write the above term as

-{1\over 2}\int d1\mu(1)\left(\sum_{i}s_{i}^{2}(1)-N\right)+{1\over 2}\Gamma_{0}\int dt\mu(t)

Note that presence on the second term that is essential to get the correct saddle-point equations and the correct value of the rate.

Appendix B
The
p
-spin Spherical Model: The Action and Saddle-Point Equations

In the standard treatment
Mézard et al. 1987
the expression of the free energy of the fully-connected spin-glass models is obtained through a number of steps. First
n
replicas of the model are introduced, then the disorder average over the partition function of the replicated system is performed and this leads to an expression in which different replicas are coupled. Then a Hubbard-Stratonovich transformation is performed leading to an action depending on a single local replicated spin variable and on two
n\times n
matrices
Q_{ab}
and
\Lambda_{ab}
. Finally the action has to be integrated over
Q_{ab}
and
\Lambda_{ab}
but the mean-field nature of the model allows to use the saddle-point method.
The very same manipulations can be applied to the interaction part of expression (
106
), the only difference is that instead of having spins
s_{a}
labeled by indexes
a=1,\dots,n
the spins depend on a more complex index (or coordinate)
{\bf 1}
specified in eq. (
107
).
As a result the objects
Q
and
\Lambda
are (formally) matrices in this more complex coordinate.
In spherical models the spin-variables can be also integrated out and
we obtain the following expression for
Z_{\hat{T}}
of the
p
-spin spherical model:

\exp\,N\left[{\beta^{2}\over 4}\int d{\bf a}d{\bf b}f({\bf Q(a,b)})+{1\over 2}{\bf\Lambda(a,b)Q(a,b)}-{1\over 2}{\rm Tr}\,\ln({\bf\Gamma}+{\bf\mu}+{\bf\Lambda})+const.+{1\over 2}\int d{\bf a}\,\mathbf{\mu(a)}+\sum_{a=1}^{n}{\Gamma_{0}\over 2}\int\mu_{a}(t)dt\right]

(133)

The expression
const.
above collects a number of terms coming from the explicit Gaussian integration, it is divergent and cancels the divergences associated to the expression
{\rm Tr}\,\ln({\bf\Gamma}+{\bf\mu}+{\bf\Lambda})
. These are pathologies of the path integral representation that are fixed going back to the discrete times and will be further discussed in appendix
G
.
Note that the application of the standard manipulations to the form (
106
) would lead to the above expression without the last term that appears instead if we want to use the simplified formulation which is suitable for multi-linear interactions.
The above expression has to be extremized with respect to
{\bf Q(ab)}
,
{\bf\Lambda(ab)}
and with respect to
\bm{\mu(a)}
. Extremization with respect to
{\bf Q(ab)}
,
\bm{\Lambda(ab)}
leads to the saddle point equations:

\displaystyle{\bf\Lambda(ab)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-{\beta^{2}\over 2}f^{\prime}({\bf Q(ab)})

(134)

\displaystyle{\bf Q}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{1\over\bm{\Gamma+\Lambda+\mu}}

(135)

The last equation can be rewritten as:

\bm{\Gamma(ac)Q(cb)+\bm{\mu(a)\,\delta(ac)Q(cb)}+\Lambda(ac)Q(cb)}=\bm{\delta(ab)}

(136)

where there integration over the variable
\bm{c}
is implicit.
The above expression is extremely compact, in the following we wil see that it encodes eight integro-differential equations.

From now on we specialize to the case of a 1RSB transition.
We start noticing that the initial and final configuration are weighted with the equilibrium Gibbs measure and their properties must not depend on the dynamics. This is granted by the fact that the terms depending on the dynamics in the equations for the replicas of the initial and final configurations are
O(n)
and disappear in the
n\rightarrow 0
limit (that must be taken first).
This means that
Q(ab)
with
a
and
b
corresponding to the
a,b=1,\dots,m+m^{\prime}
replicas associated to the equilibrium boundary conditions is the ordinary equilibrium replica matrix.
For simplicity we will work in zero field and zero random field meaning that:

f^{\prime}(0)=0\,,

(137)

in this way the overlap between different equilibrium configurations is zero. Therefore above
T_{d}
the solution is
Q(ab)=\delta(ab)
that plugged into the above equations leads to:

\mu=1+{\beta^{2}\over 2}f^{\prime}(1)\ .

(138)

Below
T_{d}
the solution is actually 1RSB. The replicas determining the boundary conditions should naturally belong to different RSB blocks ensuring that we are studying the transition between different states. On the other hand the equations for the dynamical part will have a non-vanishing correlation with the remaining
x-1
replicas in the block of the initial configuration and with the
x-1
replicas in the block of the final configuration.
This will lead to a correction of order
x-1
to the dynamical equations valid at high temperature. However for
T_{s}<T<T_{d}
we have exactly
x=1
and thus this contribution vanishes. Therefore we will safely use the high temperature Replica-Symmetric (RS) solution
Q(ab)=\delta(ab)
also in the region
T_{s}<T<T_{d}
. This is consistent with the known result that annealed and quenched averages are equivalent above
T_{s}
.

The global order parameter
\bm{Q(ab)}
can be divided into a static part corresponding to the
m+m^{\prime}
replicas, a dynamic part describing the
n
replicas of the dynamics and a mixed part. We have seen before that, as it should, the static part does not depend on the dynamics part due to the
n\rightarrow 0
limit.
We now focus on the dynamic part. We will make a RS ansatz on the
n
dynamical replicas, therefore the dynamical component of
\bm{Q(ab)}
will be characterized by two matrices

\bm{Q(ab)}=\delta_{\alpha\beta}Q(ab)+(1-\delta_{\alpha\beta})Q^{dt}(ab)

(139)

where we have moved from the full coordinates
\bm{(ab)}
to purely dynamical coordinates
(ab)
and replica coordinates
\alpha,\beta=1,\dots,n
. The RS ansatz implies also

\mu_{\alpha}(t)=\mu(t)\,,\ \hat{\mu}_{\alpha}(t)=\hat{\mu}(t)\,

(140)

and we have:

\displaystyle\bm{\Gamma(ab)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\delta_{\alpha\beta}\Gamma(ab)

(141)

\displaystyle\bm{\delta(ab)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\delta_{\alpha\beta}\delta(ab)\ .

(142)

The dynamical components of equations (
136
) can then be rewritten in a form that can be analitically continued to real values of the replica number

Appendix F
The energy

The instantaneous energy on the trajectory reads:

E(t)\equiv\sum_{p=1}^{\infty}\sum_{i_{1}<\dots<i_{p}}\overline{J_{i_{1}\dots i_{p}}[\langle s_{i_{1}}(t)\dots s_{i_{p}}(t)\rangle]}\ .

(192)

Exploiting the fact that the
J
’s are Gaussian random variables through an integration by part one easily obtains:

e(t)\equiv{E(t)\over N}=-{\beta\over 2}\left(C_{f[C]}(t,-\mathcal{T})+C_{f[C]}(t,\mathcal{T})+\int_{-\mathcal{T}}^{+\mathcal{T}}\hat{R}_{2,f[C]}(t,t^{\prime})dt^{\prime}+(n-1)\int_{-\mathcal{T}}^{+\mathcal{T}}\hat{R}_{2,f[C^{dt}]}(t,t^{\prime})dt^{\prime}\right)

\mu(t)

(194)
