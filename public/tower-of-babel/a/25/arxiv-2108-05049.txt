Representing polynomials by degenerate Bernoulli polynomials
Dae San Kim, Taekyun Kim

Abstract
. In this paper, we consider the problem of representing any polynomial in terms of the degenerate Bernoulli polynomials and more generally of the higher-order degenerate Bernoulli polynomials. We derive explicit formulas with the help of umbral calculus and illustrate our results with some examples.



2000 Mathematics Subject Classification
05A19; 05A40; 11B68; 11B83

1.
Introduction and preliminaries

In [11], the following identity is obtained from formulas (see Remark 3.2) expressing any polynomial in terms of Bernoulli polynomials
B_{n}(x)
after a slight modification:

(1.1)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\frac{1}{2k\left(2n-2k\right)}B_{2k}\left(x\right)B_{2n-2k}\left(x\right)+\frac{2}{2n-1}B_{1}\left(x\right)B_{2n-1}\left(x\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{1}{2k}\dbinom{2n}{2k}B_{2k}B_{2n-2k}\left(x\right)+\frac{1}{n}H_{2n-1}B_{2n}\left(x\right)+\frac{2}{2n-1}B_{1}\left(x\right)B_{2n-1},

where
n\geq 2
and
H_{n}=1+\frac{1}{2}+\cdots+\frac{1}{n}
.

Letting
x=0
in (
1.1
), we obtain a slight variant of the well-known Miki’s identity:

(1.2)

\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\frac{1}{2k\left(2n-2k\right)}B_{2k}B_{2n-2k}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{1}{2k}\dbinom{2n}{2k}B_{2k}B_{2n-2k}+\frac{1}{n}H_{2n-1}B_{2n}.

Letting
x=\frac{1}{2}
in (
1.1
), we get the Faber-Pandharipande-Zagier (FPZ) identity:

(1.3)

\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\frac{1}{2k\left(2n-2k\right)}\overline{B}_{2k}\overline{B}_{2n-2k}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{1}{2k}\dbinom{2n}{2k}B_{2k}\overline{B}_{2n-2k}+\frac{1}{n}H_{2n-1}\overline{B}_{2n},

where
\overline{B}_{n}=\left(2^{1-n}-1\right)B_{n}=B_{n}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)
,
for all
n=0,1,2,\dots.

It should be emphasized that our proof of Miki’s and Faber-Pandharipande-Zagier identities
follows from the simple formulas in Remark 3.2, involving only derivatives and integrals of the given polynomials.
However, the other proofs of Miki’s and FPZ identities are quite involved. Indeed, for the Miki’s identity, Miki [16] uses a formula for the Fermat quotient

\frac{a^{p}-a}{p}
modulo
p^{2}
, Shiratani-Yokoyama [20] utilizes
p
-adic analysis and Gessel [6] exploits two different expressions for Stirling numbers of the second kind
S_{2}\left(n,k\right)
.

In 1998, Faber and Pandharipande found that certain conjectural relations between Hodge integrals in Gromov-Witten theory require the FPZ identity. A proof of the FPZ identity was given by Zagier in the appendix of [5].
Dunne-Schubert [4] also proves the FPZ identity by making use of the asymptotic expansion
of some special polynomials coming from the quantum field theory computations.

Analogous formulas to Remark 3.2 can be obtained for the representations by Euler and Genocchi polynomials. Many interesting identities have been derived by using these formulas and the one in Remark 3.2 (see [7-12]). The list in the References are far from being exhaustive. The interested reader can easily find more related papers in the literature. Also, we should mention here that there are other ways of obtaining the same result as the one in (
1.1
). One of them is to use Fourier series expansion of the function obtained by extending by periodicity 1 of the polynomial function restricted to the interval
[0,1)
(see [13-15]).

The aim of this paper is to derive formulas (see Theorem 3.1) expressing any polynomial in terms of the degenerate Bernoulli polynomials (see [1]) with the help of umbral calculus (see [3,18,19,21]) and to illustrate our results with some examples. This can be generalized to the higher-order degenerate Bernoulli polynomials. Indeed, we deduce formulas of representing any polynomial in terms of the higher-order degenerate Bernoulli polynomials (see Theorems 4.1 and 4.2) again by using umbral calculus. The contribution of this paper is the derivation of such formulas which, we think, have many potential applications. We have obtained formulas for representing any polynomial by the higher-order degenerate Euler polynomials and the higher-order degenerate Genocchi polynomials which will appear elsewhere.

The outline of this paper is as follows. In Section 1, we recall some necessary facts that are needed throughout this paper. In Section 2, we go over umbral calculus briefly. In Section 3, we derive formulas expressing any polynomial in terms of the degenerate Bernoulli polynomials. In Section 4, we derive formulas representing any polynomial in terms of the higher-order degenerate Bernoulli polynomials. In Section 5, we illustrate our results with some examples. Finally, we conclude our paper in Section 6.

The Bernoulli polynomials
B_{n}(x)
are defined by

(1.4)

\frac{t}{e^{t}-1}e^{xt}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}B_{n}(x)\frac{t^{n}}{n!}.

When
x=0
,
B_{n}=B_{n}(0)
are called the Bernoulli numbers. We observe that
B_{n}(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\binom{n}{j}B_{n-j}x^{j},\,\frac{d}{dx}B_{n}(x)=nB_{n-1}(x)
.
The first few terms of
B_{n}
are given by:

\displaystyle B_{0}=1,\,B_{1}=-\frac{1}{2},\,B_{2}=\frac{1}{6},\,B_{4}=-\frac{1}{30},\,B_{6}=\frac{1}{42},\,B_{8}=-\frac{1}{30},\,B_{10}=\frac{5}{66},

\displaystyle\,B_{12}=-\frac{691}{2730},\dots;B_{2k+1}=0,\,\,(k\geq 1).

More generally, for any nonnegative integer
r
, the Bernoulli polynomials
B_{n}^{(r)}(x)
of order
r

are given by

(1.5)

\bigg(\frac{t}{e^{t}-1}\bigg)^{r}e^{xt}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}B_{n}^{(r)}(x)\frac{t^{n}}{n!}.

The Euler polynomials
E_{n}(x)
are defined by

(1.6)

\frac{2}{e^{t}+1}e^{xt}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}E_{n}(x)\frac{t^{n}}{n!}.

When
x=0
,
E_{n}=E_{n}(0)
are called the Euler numbers. We observe that
E_{n}(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\binom{n}{j}E_{n-j}x^{j},\,\frac{d}{dx}E_{n}(x)=nE_{n-1}(x)
.
The first few terms of
E_{n}
are given by:

\displaystyle E_{0}=1,\,E_{1}=-\frac{1}{2},\,E_{3}=\frac{1}{4},\,E_{5}=-\frac{1}{2},\,E_{7}=\frac{17}{8},\,B_{9}=-\frac{31}{2},\dots;

\displaystyle E_{2k}=0,\,\,(k\geq 1).

The Genocchi polynomials
G_{n}(x)
are defined by

(1.7)

\frac{2t}{e^{t}+1}e^{xt}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}G_{n}(x)\frac{t^{n}}{n!}.

When
x=0
,
G_{n}=G_{n}(0)
are called the Genocchi numbers. We observe that
G_{n}(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\binom{n}{j}G_{n-j}x^{j}
,
\frac{d}{dx}G_{n}(x)=nG_{n-1}(x)
, and
\mathrm{deg}\,G_{n}(x)=n-1
, for
n\geq 1
.
The first few terms of
G_{n}
are given by:

\displaystyle G_{0}=0,\,G_{1}=1,\,G_{2}=-1,\,G_{4}=1,\,G_{6}=-3,\,G_{8}=17,\,G_{10}=-155

\displaystyle G_{12}=2073,\dots;G_{2k+1}=0,\,\,(k\geq 1).

For any nonzero real number
\lambda
, the degenerate exponentials are given by

(1.8)

\displaystyle e_{\lambda}^{x}(t)=(1+\lambda t)^{\frac{x}{\lambda}}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(x)_{n,\lambda}\frac{t^{n}}{n!},

\displaystyle e_{\lambda}(t)=e_{\lambda}^{1}(t)=(1+\lambda t)^{\frac{1}{\lambda}}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(1)_{n,\lambda}\frac{t^{n}}{n!}.

Carlitz [1] introduced a degenerate version of the Bernoulli polynomials
B_{n}(x)
, called the degenerate Bernoulli polynomials and denoted by
\beta_{n,\lambda}(x)
, which is given by

(1.9)

\frac{t}{e_{\lambda}(t)-1}e_{\lambda}^{x}(t)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\beta_{n,\lambda}(x)\frac{t^{n}}{n!}.

For
x=0
,
\beta_{n,\lambda}=\beta_{n,\lambda}(0)
are called the degenerate Bernoulli numbers.

More generally, for any nonnegative integer
r
, the degenerate Bernoulli polynomials
\beta_{n,\lambda}^{(r)}(x)
of order
r

are given by

(1.10)

\bigg(\frac{t}{e_{\lambda}(t)-1}\bigg)^{r}e_{\lambda}^{x}(t)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\beta_{n,\lambda}^{(r)}(x)\frac{t^{n}}{n!}.

We remark that
\beta_{n,\lambda}(x)\rightarrow B_{n}(x)
, and
\beta_{n,\lambda}^{(r)}(x)\rightarrow B_{n}^{(r)}(x)
, as
\lambda
tends to
0
.

We recall some notations and facts about forward differences. Let
f
be any complex-valued function of the real variable
x
. Then, for any real number
a
, the forward difference
\Delta_{a}
is given by

(1.11)

\Delta_{a}f(x)=f(x+a)-f(x).

If
a=1
, then we let

(1.12)

\Delta f(x)=\Delta_{1}f(x)=f(x+1)-f(x).

In general, the
n
th oder forward differences are given by

(1.13)

\Delta_{a}^{n}f(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\binom{n}{i}(-1)^{n-i}f(x+ia).

For
a=1
, we have

(1.14)

\Delta^{n}f(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\binom{n}{i}(-1)^{n-i}f(x+i).

Finally, we recall that the Stirling numbers of the second kind
S_{2}(n,k)
can be given by means of

(1.15)

\frac{1}{k!}(e^{t}-1)^{k}=\sum_{n=k}^{\infty}S_{2}(n,k)\frac{t^{n}}{n!}.

2.
Review of umbral calculus

Here we will briefly go over very basic facts about umbral calculus. For more details on this, we recommend the reader to refer to [3,18,19,21].
Let
\mathbb{C}
be the field of complex numbers. Then
\mathcal{F}
denotes the algebra of formal power series in
t
over
\mathbb{C}
, given by

\mathcal{F}=\bigg\{f(t)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}\frac{t^{k}}{k!}~\bigg|~a_{k}\in\mathbb{C}\bigg\},

and
\mathbb{P}=\mathbb{C}[x]
indicates the algebra of polynomials in
x
with coefficients in
\mathbb{C}
.

Let
\mathbb{P}^{*}
be the vector space of all linear functionals on
\mathbb{P}
. If
\langle L|p(x)\rangle
denotes the action of the linear functional
L
on the polynomial
p(x)
, then the vector space operations on
\mathbb{P}^{*}
are defined by

\langle L+M|p(x)\rangle=\langle L|p(x)\rangle+\langle M|p(x)\rangle,\quad\langle cL|p(x)\rangle=c\langle L|p(x)\rangle,

where
c
is a complex number.

For
f(t)\in\mathcal{F}
with
\displaystyle f(t)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}\frac{t^{k}}{k!}\displaystyle
, we define the linear functional on
\mathbb{P}
by

(2.1)

\langle f(t)|x^{k}\rangle=a_{k}.

From (
2.1
), we note that

\langle t^{k}|x^{n}\rangle=n!\delta_{n,k},\quad(n,k\geq 0),

where
\delta_{n,k}
is the Kronecker’s symbol.

Some remarkable linear functionals are as follows:

\displaystyle\langle e^{yt}|p(x)\rangle=p(y),

(2.2)

\displaystyle\langle e^{yt}-1|p(x)\rangle=p(y)-p(0),

\displaystyle\bigg\langle\frac{e^{yt}-1}{t}\bigg|p(x)\bigg\rangle=\int_{0}^{y}p(u)du.

Let

(2.3)

f_{L}(t)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\langle L|x^{k}\rangle\frac{t^{k}}{k!}.

Then, by (
2.1
) and (
2.3
), we get

\langle f_{L}(t)|x^{n}\rangle=\langle L|x^{n}\rangle.

That is,
f_{L}(t)=L
. Additionally, the map
L\longmapsto f_{L}(t)
is a vector space isomorphism from
\mathbb{P}^{*}
onto
\mathcal{F}
.

Henceforth,
\mathcal{F}
denotes both the algebra of formal power series in
t
and the vector space of all linear functionals on
\mathbb{P}
.
\mathcal{F}
is called the umbral algebra and the umbral calculus is the study of umbral algebra.
For each nonnegative integer
k
, the differential operator
t^{k}
on
\mathbb{P}
is defined by

(2.4)

t^{k}x^{n}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}(n)_{k}x^{n-k},&\textrm{if $k\leq n$,}\\
0,&\textrm{if $k>n$.}\end{array}\right.

Extending (
2.4
) linearly, any power series

f(t)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{a_{k}}{k!}t^{k}\in\mathcal{F}

gives the differential operator on
\mathbb{P}
defined by

(2.5)

f(t)x^{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}a_{k}x^{n-k},\quad(n\geq 0).

It should be observed that, for any formal power series
f(t)
and any polynomial
p(x)
, we have

(2.6)

\langle f(t)|p(x)\rangle=\langle 1|f(t)p(x)\rangle=f(t)p(x)|_{x=0}.

Here we note that an element
f(t)
of
\mathcal{F}
is a formal power series, a linear functional and a differential operator. Some notable differential operators are as follows:

\displaystyle e^{yt}p(x)=p(x+y),

(2.7)

\displaystyle(e^{yt}-1)p(x)=p(x+y)-p(x),

\displaystyle\frac{e^{yt}-1}{t}p(x)=\int_{x}^{x+y}p(u)du.

The order
o(f(t))
of the power series
f(t)(\neq 0)
is the smallest integer for which
a_{k}
does not vanish. If
o(f(t))=0
, then
f(t)
is called an invertible series. If
o(f(t))=1
, then
f(t)
is called a delta series.

For
f(t),g(t)\in\mathcal{F}
with
o(f(t))=1
and
o(g(t))=0
, there exists a unique sequence
s_{n}(x)
(deg
s_{n}(x)=n
) of polynomials such that

(2.8)

\big\langle g(t)f(t)^{k}|s_{n}(x)\big\rangle=n!\delta_{n,k},\quad(n,k\geq 0).

The sequence
s_{n}(x)
is said to be the Sheffer sequence for
(g(t),f(t))
, which is denoted by
s_{n}(x)\sim(g(t),f(t))
. We observe from (
2.8
) that

(2.9)

s_{n}(x)=\frac{1}{g(t)}p_{n}(x),

where
p_{n}(x)=g(t)s_{n}(x)\sim(1,f(t))
.

In particular, if
s_{n}(x)\sim(g(t),t)
, then
p_{n}(x)=x^{n}
, and hence

(2.10)

s_{n}(x)=\frac{1}{g(t)}x^{n}.

It is well known that
s_{n}(x)\sim(g(t),f(t))
if and only if

(2.11)

\frac{1}{g\big(\overline{f}(t)\big)}e^{x\overline{f}(t)}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{s_{k}(x)}{k!}t^{k},

for all
x\in\mathbb{C}
, where
\overline{f}(t)
is the compositional inverse of
f(t)
such that
\overline{f}(f(t))=f(\overline{f}(t))=t
.

The following equations (
2.12
), (
2.13
), and (
2.14
) are equivalent to the fact that
s_{n}\left(x\right)
is Sheffer for
\left(g\left(t\right),f\left(t\right)\right)
, for some invertible
g(t)
:

(2.12)

\displaystyle f\left(t\right)s_{n}\left(x\right)

\displaystyle=ns_{n-1}\left(x\right),\quad\left(n\geq 0\right),

(2.13)

\displaystyle s_{n}\left(x+y\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\binom{n}{j}s_{j}\left(x\right)p_{n-j}\left(y\right),

with
p_{n}\left(x\right)=g\left(t\right)s_{n}\left(x\right),

(2.14)

s_{n}\left(x\right)=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\frac{1}{j!}\big\langle{g\left(\overline{f}\left(t\right)\right)^{-1}\overline{f}\left(t\right)^{j}}\big|{x^{n}\big\rangle}x^{j}.

If
s_{n}(x)\sim(g(t),f(t))
, then the following recurrence relation holds:

(2.15)

s_{n+1}(x)=\bigg(x-\frac{g^{\prime}(t)}{g(t)}\bigg)\frac{1}{f^{\prime}(t)}s_{n}(x).

For
s_{n}(x)\sim(g(t),f(t))
, and
r_{n}(x)\sim(h(t),l(t))
, we have

(2.16)

s_{n}\left(x\right)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}C_{n,k}r_{k}\left(x\right),\quad\left(n\geq 0\right),

where

(2.17)

C_{n,k}=\frac{1}{k!}\bigg\langle{\frac{h\left(\overline{f}\left(t\right)\right)}{g\left(\overline{f}\left(t\right)\right)}l\left(\overline{f}\left(t\right)\right)^{k}}\Big|{x^{n}}\bigg\rangle.

Let
p_{n}(x),\,q_{n}(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}q_{n,k}x^{k}
be sequences of polynomials. Then the umbral composition of
q_{n}(x)
with
p_{n}(x)
is defined to be the sequence

(2.18)

q_{n}({\bf{p}}(x))=\sum_{k=0}^{n}q_{n,k}p_{k}(x).

3.
Representation by degenerate Bernoulli polynomials

Our interest here is to derive formulas expressing any polynomial in terms of the degenerate Bernoulli polynomials.

From (
1.9
) and (
1.8
), we first observe that

(3.1)

\displaystyle\beta_{n,\lambda}(x)\sim\big(g(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{t}-1}{f(t)}=\frac{\lambda(e^{t}-1)}{e^{\lambda t}-1},f(t)=\frac{1}{\lambda}(e^{\lambda t}-1)\big),

(3.2)

\displaystyle(x)_{n,\lambda}\sim(1,f(t)=\frac{1}{\lambda}(e^{\lambda t}-1)).

From (
1.11
), (
2.2
), (
2.7
), (
2.12
), (
3.1
) and (
3.2
), we note that

(3.3)

\displaystyle f(t)\beta_{n,\lambda}(x)

\displaystyle=n\beta_{n-1}(x)=\frac{1}{\lambda}(e^{\lambda t}-1)\beta_{n}(x)=\frac{1}{\lambda}\Delta_{\lambda}\beta_{n}(x),

(3.4)

\displaystyle f(t)(x)_{n,\lambda}=n(x)_{n-1,\lambda}.

It is immediate to see from (
1.9
) that

(3.5)

\displaystyle\Delta\beta_{n,\lambda}(x)

\displaystyle=\beta_{n,\lambda}(x+1)-\beta_{n,\lambda}(x)=n(x)_{n-1,\lambda},

(3.6)

\displaystyle\beta_{n,\lambda}(1)-\beta_{n,\lambda}=\delta_{n,1},

where
\delta_{n,1}
is the Kronecker’s delta.

Now, we assume that
p(x)\in\mathbb{C}[x]
has degree
n
, and write
p(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}a_{k}\beta_{k,\lambda}(x)
.
Let
h(x)=p(x+1)-p(x)=\Delta p(x)
. Then, from (
3.4
) and (
3.5
), we have

\displaystyle h(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}a_{k}(\beta_{k,\lambda}(x+1)-\beta_{k,\lambda}(x))

(3.7)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}a_{k}k(x)_{k-1,\lambda}

\displaystyle=f(t)\sum_{k=0}^{n}a_{k}(x)_{k,\lambda}

For
r\geq 1
, from (
3.7
) and (
3.4
) we obtain

(3.8)

\displaystyle(f(t))^{r-1}h(x)

\displaystyle=(f(t))^{r}\sum_{k=0}^{n}a_{k}(x)_{k,\lambda}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=r}^{n}k(k-1)\cdots(k-r+1)a_{k}(x)_{k-r,\lambda}.

Letting
x=0
in (
3.8
), we finally get

(3.9)

a_{r}=\frac{1}{r!}(f(t))^{r-1}h(x)|_{x=0}=\frac{1}{r!}\langle(f(t))^{r-1}|h(x)\rangle,\,\,(r\geq 1),

An alternative expression of (
3.9
) is given by

As
f(t)h(x)=\frac{1}{\lambda}(e^{\lambda t}-1)h(x)=\frac{1}{\lambda}\Delta_{\lambda}h(x)
,

\displaystyle a_{r}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{r!\lambda^{r-1}}\Delta_{\lambda}^{r-1}h(x)|_{x=0}

(3.10)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{r!\lambda^{r-1}}(\Delta_{\lambda}^{r-1}p(1)-\Delta_{\lambda}^{r-1}p(0))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{r!\lambda^{r-1}}\Delta_{\lambda}^{r-1}\Delta p(0).

From (
1.13
), we have another alternative expression of (
3.9
) which is given by

\displaystyle a_{r}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{r!\lambda^{r-1}}\Delta_{\lambda}^{r-1}h(x)|_{x=0}

(3.11)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{r!\lambda^{r-1}}\sum_{k=0}^{r-1}\binom{r-1}{k}(-1)^{r-1-k}h(x+k\lambda)|_{x=0}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{r!\lambda^{r-1}}\sum_{k=0}^{r-1}\binom{r-1}{k}(-1)^{r-1-k}(p(1+k\lambda)-p(k\lambda)).

By using (
1.15
), we obtain yet another expression of (
3.9
) that is given by

\displaystyle a_{r}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{r!}\langle(f(t))^{r-1}|h(x)\rangle

(3.12)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{r\lambda^{r-1}}\bigg\langle\frac{1}{(r-1)!}(e^{\lambda t}-1)^{r-1}\bigg|h(x)\bigg\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{r\lambda^{r-1}}\bigg\langle\sum_{l=r-1}^{\infty}S_{2}(l,r-1)\frac{\lambda^{l}t^{l}}{l!}\bigg|h(x)\bigg\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{r}\sum_{l=r-1}^{n}S_{2}(l,r-1)\frac{\lambda^{l-r+1}}{l!}(p^{(l)}(1)-p^{(l)}(0)),

where
p^{(l)}(x)=(\frac{d}{dx})^{l}p(x)
.

Now, it remains to determine
a_{0}
. We first note from (
2.2
), (
2.6
), (
3.3
) and (
3.6
) that

\displaystyle g(t)p(x)|_{x=0}

\displaystyle=\langle g(t)|p(x)\rangle

\displaystyle=\bigg\langle\frac{e^{t}-1}{f(t)}\bigg|p(x)\bigg\rangle

(3.13)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}a_{k}\bigg\langle\frac{e^{t}-1}{f(t)}\bigg|\beta_{k,\lambda}(x)\bigg\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}a_{k}\bigg\langle\frac{e^{t}-1}{f(t)}\bigg|f(t)\frac{1}{k+1}\beta_{k+1,\lambda}(x)\bigg\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{a_{k}}{k+1}\bigg\langle e^{t}-1\bigg|\beta_{k+1,\lambda}(x)\bigg\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{a_{k}}{k+1}\delta_{k+1,1}=a_{0}.

We want to find more explicit expression for (
3.13
). As
\frac{\lambda t}{e^{\lambda t}-1}e^{xt}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\lambda^{n}B_{n}(\frac{x}{\lambda})\frac{t^{n}}{n!}
, we note from (
2.10
) that
\lambda^{n}B_{n}(\frac{x}{\lambda})=\frac{\lambda t}{e^{\lambda t}-1}x^{n}
.
To proceed further, we let
p(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{n}b_{i}x^{i}
.

From (
2.2
), (
3.1
) and (
3.13
), noting that
g(t)=\frac{e^{t}-1}{t}\frac{\lambda t}{e^{\lambda t}-1}
, we have

\displaystyle a_{0}

\displaystyle=\bigg\langle\frac{e^{t}-1}{t}\bigg|\frac{\lambda t}{e^{\lambda t}-1}p(x)\bigg\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{n}b_{i}\bigg\langle\frac{e^{t}-1}{t}\bigg|\lambda^{i}B_{i}\big(\frac{x}{\lambda}\big)\bigg\rangle

(3.14)

\displaystyle=\bigg\langle\frac{e^{t}-1}{t}\bigg|\sum_{i=0}^{n}b_{i}\lambda^{i}B_{i}\big(\frac{x}{\lambda}\big)\bigg\rangle

\displaystyle=\bigg\langle\frac{e^{t}-1}{t}\bigg|p\big(\lambda{\bf{B}}\big(\frac{x}{\lambda}\big)\big)\bigg\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}p\big(\lambda{\bf{B}}\big(\frac{u}{\lambda}\big)\big)\,du,

where
p\big(\lambda{\bf{B}}\big(\frac{x}{\lambda}\big)\big)
denotes the umbral composition of
p(x)
with
\lambda^{i}B_{i}\big(\frac{x}{\lambda}\big)
, that is, it is given by

.
Finally, from (
3.9
)–(
3.11
) and (
3.14
) we get the following theorem.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let

. Then we have

p(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}a_{k}\beta_{k,\lambda}(x)
,

where

\displaystyle a_{0}=g(t)p(x)|_{x=0}=\bigg\langle\frac{e^{t}-1}{t}\bigg|\frac{\lambda t}{e^{\lambda t}-1}p(x)\bigg\rangle=\int_{0}^{1}p\big(\lambda{\bf{B}}\big(\frac{u}{\lambda}\big)\big)du,

\displaystyle a_{k}=\frac{1}{k!}(f(t))^{k-1}(p(x+1)-p(x))|_{x=0}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k!\lambda^{k-1}}\big\langle\big(e^{\lambda t}-1\big)^{k-1}\big|p(x+1)-p(x)\big\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k!\lambda^{k-1}}\Delta_{\lambda}^{k-1}\Delta p(0)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k!\lambda^{k-1}}\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\binom{k-1}{j}(-1)^{k-1-j}(p(1+j\lambda)-p(j\lambda))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{l=k-1}^{n}S_{2}(l,k-1)\frac{\lambda^{l-k+1}}{l!}(p^{(l)}(1)-p^{(l)}(0)),\,\,\mathrm{for}\,\,k=1,2,\dots,n,

where
g(t)=\frac{\lambda(e^{t}-1)}{e^{\lambda t}-1},\,\,f(t)=\frac{1}{\lambda}(e^{\lambda t}-1)
,
and
p\big(\lambda{\bf{B}}\big(\frac{x}{\lambda}\big)\big)
denotes the umbral composition of
p(x)
with
\lambda^{i}B_{i}\big(\frac{x}{\lambda}\big)
.

Remark 3.2
.

Let

4.
Representation by higher-order degenerate Bernoulli polynomials

Our interest here is to derive formulas expressing any polynomial in terms of the higher-order degenerate Bernoulli polynomials.

With
g(t)=\frac{e^{t}-1}{f(t)}=\frac{\lambda(e^{t}-1)}{e^{\lambda t}-1},f(t)=\frac{1}{\lambda}(e^{\lambda t}-1)
, from (
1.10
) we note that

\beta_{n,\lambda}^{(r)}(x)\sim(g(t)^{r},f(t)).

Also, from (
2.12
), we have

(4.1)

f(t)\beta_{n,\lambda}^{(r)}(x)=n\beta_{n-1,\lambda}^{(r)}(x),

and from the generating function of the higher-order degenerate Bernoulli polynomials, it is immediate to see that

(4.2)

\Delta\beta_{n,\lambda}^{(r)}(x)=\beta_{n,\lambda}^{(r)}(x+1)-\beta_{n,\lambda}^{(r)}(x)=n\beta_{n-1,\lambda}^{(r-1)}(x).

Now, we assume that
p(x)\in\mathbb{C}[x]
has degree
n
, and write
p(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}a_{k}\beta_{k,\lambda}^{(r)}(x)
.
It is important to observe from (
4.1
) and (
4.2
) that

\displaystyle g(t)\beta_{n,\lambda}^{(r)}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{t}-1}{f(t)}\beta_{n,\lambda}^{(r)}(x)

(4.3)

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{t}-1}{f(t)}f(t)\frac{\beta_{n+1,\lambda}^{(r)}(x)}{n+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n+1}(\beta_{n+1,\lambda}^{(r)}(x+1)-\beta_{n+1,\lambda}^{(r)}(x))

\displaystyle=\beta_{n,\lambda}^{(r-1)}(x).

Thus, from (
4.3
) we have
g(t)^{r}\beta_{n,\lambda}^{(r)}(x)=\beta_{n,\lambda}^{(0)}(x)=(x)_{n,\lambda}
, and hence

(4.4)

g(t)^{r}p(x)=\sum_{l=0}^{n}a_{l}\,g(t)^{r}\beta_{l,\lambda}^{(r)}(x)=\sum_{l=0}^{n}a_{l}(x)_{l,\lambda}.

By using (
4.4
) and (
3.4
), we observe that

(4.5)

\displaystyle f(t)^{k}g(t)^{r}p(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=0}^{n}a_{l}f(t)^{k}(x)_{l,\lambda}

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=k}^{n}a_{l}\,l(l-1)\cdots(l-k+1)(x)_{l-k,\lambda}.

By evaluating (
4.5
) at
x=0
, we obtain

(4.6)

a_{k}=\frac{1}{k!}f(t)^{k}g(t)^{r}p(x)|_{x=0}=\frac{1}{k!}\langle f(t)^{k}g(t)^{r}|p(x)\rangle.

This also follows from the observation
\langle g(t)^{r}f(t)^{k}|\beta_{l,\lambda}^{(r)}(x)\rangle=l!\,\delta_{l,k}.

To proceed further, we note that
f(t)g(t)=f(t)\frac{e^{t}-1}{f(t)}=e^{t}-1
.

Assume first that
r>n
. Then
r>k
, for all
k=0,1,\dots,n
.

\displaystyle a_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k!}\langle f(t)^{k}g(t)^{r}|p(x)\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k!}\langle(e^{t}-1)^{k}g(t)^{r-k}|p(x)\rangle

(4.7)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k!}\sum_{j=0}^{k}(-1)^{k-j}\binom{k}{j}\langle e^{jt}|g(t)^{r-k}p(x)\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k!}\sum_{j=0}^{k}(-1)^{k-j}\binom{k}{j}g(t)^{r-k}p(x)|_{x=j}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k!}\sum_{j=0}^{k}(-1)^{k-j}\binom{k}{j}g(t)^{r-k}p(j).

Next, we assume that
r\leq n
. If further
0\leq k<r
, then
a_{k}
is the same as the expression in (
4.7
).
Let
r\leq k\leq n
. Then we have

\displaystyle a_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k!}\langle f(t)^{k}g(t)^{r}|p(x)\rangle

(4.8)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k!}\langle f(t)^{k-r}(e^{t}-1)^{r}|p(x)\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k!}\sum_{j=0}^{r}(-1)^{r-j}\binom{r}{j}\langle e^{jt}|f(t)^{k-r}p(x)\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k!}\sum_{j=0}^{r}(-1)^{r-j}\binom{r}{j}f(t)^{k-r}p(j).

Summarizing the results so far, from (
4.7
) and (
4.8
) we obtain the following theorem.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
p(x)\in\mathbb{C}[x],\mathrm{deg}\,p(x)=n
. Let
g(t)=\frac{\lambda(e^{t}-1)}{e^{\lambda t}-1},f(t)=\frac{1}{\lambda}(e^{\lambda t}-1)
. Then we have

(a) For
r>n
, we have

p(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{1}{k!}\sum_{j=0}^{k}(-1)^{k-j}\binom{k}{j}g(t)^{r-k}p(j)\beta_{k,\lambda}^{(r)}(x).

(b) For
r\leq n
, we have

\displaystyle p(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{r-1}\frac{1}{k!}\sum_{j=0}^{k}(-1)^{k-j}\binom{k}{j}g(t)^{r-k}p(j)\beta_{k,\lambda}^{(r)}(x)

\displaystyle+\sum_{k=r}^{n}\frac{1}{k!}\sum_{j=0}^{r}(-1)^{r-j}\binom{r}{j}f(t)^{k-r}p(j)\beta_{k,\lambda}^{(r)}(x).

We would like to find more explicit expressions for the results in Theorem 4.1.

First, we note from (
1.13
) and (
1.15
) that

\displaystyle f(t)^{k-r}p(j)

\displaystyle=f(t)^{k-r}p(x)|_{x=j}

(4.9)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\lambda^{k-r}}\Delta_{\lambda}^{k-r}p(x)|_{x=j}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\lambda^{k-r}}\sum_{l=0}^{k-r}\binom{k-r}{l}(-1)^{k-r-l}p(j+l\lambda).

\displaystyle=(k-r)!\sum_{l=k-r}^{n}S_{2}(l,k-r)\frac{\lambda^{l-k+r}}{l!}p^{(l)}(j).

Let
I
be the linear integral operator defined on
\mathbb{P}
, which is given by

(4.10)

Iq(x)=\frac{e^{t}-1}{t}q(x)=\int_{x}^{x+1}q(u)\,du.

From
\big(\frac{\lambda t}{e^{\lambda t}-1}\big)^{a}e^{xt}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\lambda^{n}B_{n}^{(a)}(\frac{x}{\lambda})\frac{t^{n}}{n!}
, we get

(4.11)

\lambda^{n}B_{n}^{(a)}\big(\frac{x}{\lambda}\big)=\bigg(\frac{\lambda t}{e^{\lambda t}-1}\bigg)^{a}x^{n},

and hence, from (
4.11
), we see that

(4.12)

\bigg(\frac{\lambda t}{e^{\lambda t}-1}\bigg)^{r-k}p(x)=p\big(\lambda{\bf{B}}^{(r-k)}\big(\frac{x}{\lambda}\big)\big),

where
p\big(\lambda{\bf{B}}^{(r-k)}\big(\frac{x}{\lambda}\big)\big)
denotes the umbral composition of
p(x)
with
\lambda^{i}B_{i}^{(r-k)}\big(\frac{x}{\lambda}\big)
.

Now, noting that
g(t)=\frac{e^{t}-1}{t}\frac{\lambda t}{e^{\lambda t}-1}
, from (
4.10
) and (
4.12
), we have

\displaystyle g(t)^{r-k}p(j)

\displaystyle=g(t)^{r-k}p(x)|_{x=j}

\displaystyle=\bigg(\frac{e^{t}-1}{t}\bigg)^{r-k}\bigg(\frac{\lambda t}{e^{\lambda t}-1}\bigg)^{r-k}p(x)\bigg|_{x=j}

(4.13)

\displaystyle=\bigg(\frac{e^{t}-1}{t}\bigg)^{r-k}p\big(\lambda{\bf{B}}^{(r-k)}\big(\frac{x}{\lambda}\big)\big)\bigg|_{x=j}

\displaystyle=I^{r-k}p\big(\lambda{\bf{B}}^{(r-k)}\big(\frac{x}{\lambda}\big)\big)\bigg|_{x=j}

\displaystyle=\bigg\langle e^{jt}\bigg(\frac{e^{t}-1}{t}\bigg)^{r-k}\bigg|\bigg(\frac{\lambda t}{e^{\lambda t}-1}\bigg)^{r-k}p(x)\bigg\rangle.

From (
1.15
), we see that
\big(\frac{e^{t}-1}{t}\big)^{m}=\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}S_{2}(l+m,m)\frac{m!}{(l+m)!}t^{l}
, and hence from (
4.12
) and (
4.13
) we get another expression for
g(t)^{r-k}p(j)
in the following:

\displaystyle g(t)^{r-k}p(j)

\displaystyle=\bigg(\frac{\lambda t}{e^{\lambda t}-1}\bigg)^{r-k}\bigg(\frac{e^{t}-1}{t}\bigg)^{r-k}p(x)\bigg|_{x=j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=0}^{n}S_{2}(l+r-k,r-k)\frac{(r-k)!}{(l+r-k)!}p^{(l)}\big(\lambda{\bf{B}}^{(r-k)}\big(\frac{x}{\lambda}\big)\big)\big|_{x=j}.

Now, from Theorem 4.1, (
4.9
) and (
4.12
), we finally arrive at the following theorem.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
p(x)\in\mathbb{C}[x],\mathrm{deg}\,p(x)=n
. Then we have

(a) For
r>n
, we have

\displaystyle p(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{1}{k!}\sum_{j=0}^{k}(-1)^{k-j}\binom{k}{j}I^{r-k}p\big(\lambda{\bf{B}}^{(r-k)}\big(\frac{x}{\lambda}\big)\big)\bigg|_{x=j}\beta_{k,\lambda}^{(r)}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{1}{k!}\sum_{j=0}^{k}\sum_{l=0}^{n}(-1)^{k-j}\binom{k}{j}\frac{(r-k)!}{(l+r-k)!}S_{2}(l+r-k,r-k)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\times p^{(l)}\big(\lambda{\bf{B}}^{(r-k)}\big(\frac{x}{\lambda}\big)\big)\big|_{x=j}\beta_{k,\lambda}^{(r)}(x).

(b) For
r\leq n
, we have

\displaystyle p(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{r-1}\frac{1}{k!}\sum_{j=0}^{k}(-1)^{k-j}\binom{k}{j}I^{r-k}p\big(\lambda{\bf{B}}^{(r-k)}\big(\frac{x}{\lambda}\big)\big)\bigg|_{x=j}\beta_{k,\lambda}^{(r)}(x)

\displaystyle+\sum_{k=r}^{n}\frac{1}{k!\lambda^{k-r}}\sum_{j=0}^{r}\sum_{l=0}^{k-r}(-1)^{k-j-l}\binom{r}{j}\binom{k-r}{l}p(j+l\lambda)\beta_{k,\lambda}^{(r)}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{r-1}\frac{1}{k!}\sum_{j=0}^{k}\sum_{l=0}^{n}(-1)^{k-j}\binom{k}{j}\frac{(r-k)!}{(l+r-k)!}S_{2}(l+r-k,r-k)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\times p^{(l)}\big(\lambda{\bf{B}}^{(r-k)}\big(\frac{x}{\lambda}\big)\big)\big|_{x=j}\beta_{k,\lambda}^{(r)}(x)

\displaystyle+\sum_{k=r}^{n}\frac{1}{k!}\sum_{j=0}^{r}\sum_{l=k-r}^{n}(-1)^{r-j}\binom{r}{j}(k-r)!\frac{\lambda^{l-k+r}}{l!}S_{2}(l,k-r)p^{(l)}(j)\beta_{k,\lambda}^{(r)}(x)

Here
I
is the linear operator given by
Iq(x)=\int_{x}^{x+1}q(u)\,du,
and
p\big(\lambda{\bf{B}}^{(r-k)}\big(\frac{x}{\lambda}\big)\big)
denotes the umbral composition of
p(x)
with
\lambda^{i}B_{i}^{(r-k)}\big(\frac{x}{\lambda}\big)
.

Remark 4.3
.

Let
p(x)\in\mathbb{C}[x],with\,\,\mathrm{deg}\,p(x)=n

(a) Here we illustrate Theorem 3.1, with

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{n}\sum_{l=1}^{n-2}\frac{1}{n-l}\binom{n}{l}B_{n-l}\big\langle\big(e^{\lambda t}-1\big)^{k-1}\big|B_{l}(x+1)-B_{l}(x)\big\rangle

Thus, from (

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\frac{1}{k(n-k)}B_{k}(x)B_{n-k}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{n}\bigg(\sum_{l=0}^{n-2}\frac{1}{n-l}\binom{n}{l}B_{n-l}\lambda^{l}B_{l}+H_{n-1}\lambda^{n}B_{n}\bigg)\beta_{0,\lambda}(x)

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\frac{2}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{1}{k!\lambda^{k-1}}\bigg(\sum_{l=1}^{n-2}\frac{l}{n-l}\binom{n}{l}B_{n-l}\Delta_{\lambda}^{k-1}0^{l-1}+nH_{n-1}\Delta_{\lambda}^{k-1}0^{n-1}\bigg)\beta_{k,\lambda}(x).

(c) In [11], it is shown that the following identity holds for
n\geq 2
:

\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\frac{1}{k(n-k)}E_{k}(x)E_{n-k}(x)=\frac{4E_{n+1}}{n^{2}(n+1)}-\frac{4}{n}\sum_{l=1}^{n}\frac{\binom{n}{l}(H_{n-1}-H_{n-l})}{n-l+1}E_{n-l+1}B_{l}(x),

where
H_{n}=1+\frac{1}{2}+\cdots+\frac{1}{n}
is the harmonic number.

Write
\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\frac{1}{k(n-k)}E_{k}(x)E_{n-k}(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}a_{k}\beta_{k,\lambda}(x)
.

By proceeding similarly to (b), we obtain the following identity:

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\frac{1}{k(n-k)}E_{k}(x)E_{n-k}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{n}\bigg(\frac{E_{n+1}}{n(n+1)}-\sum_{l=1}^{n}\frac{\binom{n}{l}(H_{n-1}-H_{n-l})}{n-l+1}E_{n-l+1}\lambda^{l}B_{l}\bigg)\beta_{0,\lambda}(x)

\displaystyle\quad\quad-\frac{4}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{1}{k!\lambda^{k-1}}\bigg(\sum_{l=1}^{n}\frac{l\,\binom{n}{l}(H_{n-1}-H_{n-l})}{n-l+1}E_{n-l+1}\Delta_{\lambda}^{k-1}0^{l-1}\bigg)\beta_{k,\lambda}(x).

(d) In [15], it is proved that the following identity is valid for

(5.7)

\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\frac{1}{k(n-k)}G_{k}(x)G_{n-k}(x)=-\frac{4}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-2}\binom{n}{k}\frac{G_{n-k}}{n-k}B_{k}(x).

Again, by proceeding analogously to (b), we get the following identity:

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\frac{1}{k(n-k)}G_{k}(x)G_{n-k}(x)

\displaystyle=-\frac{4}{n}\bigg(\sum_{l=0}^{n-2}\binom{n}{l}\frac{G_{n-l}}{n-l}\lambda^{l}B_{l}\bigg)\beta_{0,\lambda}(x)

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{4}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n-2}\frac{1}{k!\lambda^{k-1}}\bigg(\sum_{l=1}^{n-2}\binom{n}{l}\frac{l\,G_{n-l}}{n-l}\Delta_{\lambda}^{k-1}0^{l-1}\bigg)\beta_{k,\lambda}(x).

(e) Nielsen [17,2] expressed products of two Bernoulli polynomials in terms of Bernoulli polynomials. Namely, for positive integers
m
and
n

B_{m}(x)B_{n}(x)=\sum_{r}\left\{\binom{m}{2r}n+\binom{n}{2r}m\right\}\frac{B_{2r}B_{m+n-2r}(x)}{m+n-2r}+(-1)^{m+1}\frac{B_{m+n}}{\binom{m+n}{m}}.

Again, in a similar way to (b), we can show that

\displaystyle B_{m}(x)B_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=\bigg(\sum_{r}\left\{\binom{m}{2r}n+\binom{n}{2r}m\right\}\frac{B_{2r}B_{m+n-2r}}{m+n-2r}\lambda^{m+n-2r}+(-1)^{m+1}\frac{B_{m+n}}{\binom{m+n}{m}}\bigg)\beta_{0,\lambda}(x)

(f) Nielsen [17,2] also represented products of two Euler polynomials in terms of Bernoulli polynomials as follows:

\displaystyle E_{m}(x)E_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=-2\sum_{r=1}^{m}\binom{m}{r}E_{r}\frac{B_{m+n-r+1}(x)}{m+n-r+1}

\displaystyle\quad-2\sum_{s=1}^{n}\binom{n}{s}E_{s}\frac{B_{m+n-s+1}(x)}{m+n-s+1}

\displaystyle\quad+2(-1)^{n+1}\frac{m!\,n!}{(m+n+1)!}E_{m+n+1}.

In the same way as (b), we can show that

\displaystyle E_{m}(x)E_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=-2\bigg(\sum_{r=1}^{m}\binom{m}{r}E_{r}\frac{B_{m+n-r+1}\lambda^{m+n-r+1}}{m+n-r+1}+\sum_{s=1}^{n}\binom{n}{s}E_{s}\frac{B_{m+n-s+1}\lambda^{m+n-s+1}}{m+n-s+1}

\displaystyle\quad\quad+(-1)^{n}\frac{m!\,n!}{(m+n+1)!}E_{m+n+1}\bigg)\beta_{0,\lambda}(x)

\displaystyle\quad\quad-\sum_{k=1}^{m+n}\frac{2}{k!\lambda^{k-1}}\bigg(\sum_{r=1}^{m}\binom{m}{r}E_{r}\Delta_{\lambda}^{k-1}0^{m+n-r}+\sum_{s=1}^{n}\binom{n}{s}E_{s}\Delta_{\lambda}^{k-1}0^{m+n-s}\bigg)\beta_{k,\lambda}(x).

\displaystyle\times G_{n-m}(j+l\lambda)\bigg)\beta_{k,\lambda}^{(r)}(x).

6.
Conclusion

In this paper, we were interested in representing any polynomial in terms of the degenerate Bernoulli polynomials and of the higher-order degenerate Bernoulli polynomials. We were able to derive formulas for such representations with the help of umbral calculus. We showed that, by letting
\lambda
tends to zero, they agree with the previously found formulas for representations by the Bernoulli polynomials and by the higher-order Bernoulli polynomials. Further, we illustrated the formulas with some examples.

Even though the method adopted in this paper is elementary, they are very useful and powerful. Indeed, as we mentioned in the Section 1, both a variant of Miki’s identity and Faber-Pandharipande-Zagier (FPZ) identity follow from the one identity (see (
1.1
)) that can be derived from a formula (see Remark 3.2) involving only derivatives and integrals of the given polynomial, while all the other proofs are quite involved. We recall here that the FPZ identity was a conjectural relations between Hodge integrals in Gromov-Witten theory. In addition, this paper demonstrates that umbral calculus is a very convenient tool.

It is one of our future research projects to continue to find formulas representing polynomials in terms of some specific special polynomials and to apply those in discovering some interesting identities.
