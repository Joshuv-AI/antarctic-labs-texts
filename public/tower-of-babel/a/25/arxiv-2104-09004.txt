Irredundance Trees of Diameter 𝟑 \mathbf{3}
C.M. Mynhardt, A. Roux

Abstract
A set D D of vertices of a graph G = ( V , E ) G=(V,E) is irredundant if each non-isolated vertex of G ⁡ [ D ] G[D] has a neighbour in V − D V-D that is not adjacent to any other vertex in D D . The upper irredundance number IR ⁡ ( G ) \operatorname{IR}(G) is the largest cardinality of an irredundant set of G G ; an IR ⁡ ( G ) \operatorname{IR}(G) -set is an irredundant set of cardinality IR ⁡ ( G ) \operatorname{IR}(G) . The IR \operatorname{IR} -graph of G G has the IR ⁡ ( G ) \operatorname{IR}(G) -sets as vertex set, and sets D D and D ′ D^{\prime} are adjacent if and only if D ′ D^{\prime} can be obtained from D D by exchanging a single vertex of D D for an adjacent vertex in D ′ D^{\prime} . An IR \operatorname{IR} -tree is an IR \operatorname{IR} -graph that is a tree. We characterize IR \operatorname{IR} -trees of diameter 3 3 by showing that these graphs are precisely the double stars S ⁡ ( 2 ​ n , 2 ​ n ) S(2n,2n) , i.e., trees obtained by joining the central vertices of two disjoint stars K 1 , 2 ​ n K_{1,2n} .

Introduction

Reconfiguration problems are concerned with determining
conditions under which a feasible solution to a given problem can be
transformed into another one via a sequence of feasible solutions in such a
way that any two consecutive solutions are adjacent according to a specified
adjacency relation. The solutions form the vertex set of the associated
reconfiguration graph, two vertices being adjacent if one solution can be
obtained from the other in a single step. Typical questions about the
reconfiguration graph concern its structure (connectedness, Hamiltonicity,
diameter), realizability (which graphs can be realized as a specific type of
reconfiguration graph), and algorithmic properties (finding a shortest path
between two solutions).

Domination reconfiguration problems involving dominating sets of different
cardinalities were introduced by Haas and Seyffarth
[
9
]
and also
studied in, for example,
[
1
,
10
,
11
,
16
]
. Two
variations of domination reconfiguration problems involving only
minimum-cardinality dominating sets were introduced by Lakshmanan and
Vijayakumar
[
13
]
in 2010, and Fricke, Hedetniemi, Hedetniemi, and
Hutson
[
8
]
in 2011, respectively, and also studied in
[
2
,
5
,
6
,
7
,
13
,
17
,
18
,
19
,
20
]
. A survey of results
concerning the reconfiguration of colourings and dominating sets in graphs can
be found in
[
14
]
.

The study of upper irredundance graphs, or
\operatorname{IR}
-graphs, was
mentioned as an open problem in
[
17
]
and initiated by the current
authors in
[
15
]
. There we showed that all disconnected graphs,
but not all connected graphs, are realizable as
\operatorname{IR}
-graphs,
and that the smallest non-complete
\operatorname{IR}
-tree is the double star

S(2,2)
(see Figure
1
). Here we characterize
\operatorname{IR}
-trees of diameter
3
by showing that these graphs are precisely the

double stars

S(2n,2n)

vertices of two disjoint stars
K_{1,2n}

\operatorname{IR}(G_{n})\cong S(2n,2n)

Definitions

We follow the notation of
[
3
]
for general concepts, and
that of
[
12
]
for domination related concepts not defined here. For a
graph
G=(V,E)
and vertices
u,v\in V
, we use the notation
u\sim v

(

, respectively) to denote that

is adjacent (nonadjacent,
respectively) to
v

D
-
private neighbour

that is dominated
by
v
(i.e.,
v^{\prime}=v

v^{\prime}\sim v
) but by no vertex in

D-\{v\}
. The set of
D
-private neighbours of
v

private neighbourhood of

with respect to

and denoted by

\operatorname{PN}(v,D)

The concept of irredundance was introduced by Cockayne, Hedetniemi and Miller

[
4
]
in 1978. A set

is

if

\operatorname{PN}(v,D)\neq\varnothing
for each
v\in D
. The
upper
irredundance number
\operatorname{IR}(G)
is the largest cardinality of an
irredundant set of
G
. An
\operatorname{IR}
-
set
of
G
, or an

\operatorname{IR}(G)
-
set
, is an irredundant set of
cardinality
~\operatorname{IR}(G)
. Let
D
be an irredundant set of
G
. For

v\in D
, it is possible that
v\in\operatorname{PN}(v,D)
; this happens if
and only if
v
is isolated in the subgraph
G[D]
induced by
D
. If

u\in\operatorname{PN}(v,D)
and

, then

; in this case

is an
external
D
-
private neighbour of
v
. The set of external

D
-private neighbours of
v
is denoted by
\operatorname{EPN}(v,D)
. An
isolated vertex of
G[D]
may or may not have external
D
-private neighbours,
but if
v
has positive degree in
G[D]
, then
\operatorname{EPN}(v,D)\neq\varnothing
.

As defined in
[
15
]
, the
\operatorname{IR}
-graph

G(\operatorname{IR})

of

is the graph whose vertex set
consists of the
\operatorname{IR}(G)
-sets, where sets
D
and
D^{\prime}

are adjacent if and only if there exist vertices

. We
shorten the expression

to

to show that

and

are

G(\operatorname{IR})

is
swapped into

is
swapped out of
the

\operatorname{IR}(G)
-set, or simply that
u
and
v
are
swapped
. To
prove that a given graph

is an
\operatorname{IR}
-graph, one needs to
construct a graph
G
such that
G(\operatorname{IR})\cong H
. Figure

1
shows a graph
G^{\prime}
and and its
\operatorname{IR}
-graph

G^{\prime}(\operatorname{IR})\cong S(2,2)

-sets are given in Lemma

4.2
The upper irredundance number of

Since

are independent sets of

\operatorname{IR}(G_{n})\geq 2n+1

. We
prove that

(a)

|S_{i}\cap X|\leq 2

(b)

Proof of (a).
Note that if, without loss of
generality,

\operatorname{PN}(c_{i},X)=\varnothing

\blacklozenge

Proof of (b).
Assume that

.
Note that
\operatorname{PN}(u,\{u,v\})=A

\operatorname{PN}(v,\{u,v\})=B
. Hence, if

and

. Assume that

. Suppose

\operatorname{PN}(a_{i},X)\subseteq\{c_{i},d_{i}\}
. Moreover,

\operatorname{PN}(v,X)=\{b_{i}\}
. This implies that

. Therefore, the private

\blacklozenge
