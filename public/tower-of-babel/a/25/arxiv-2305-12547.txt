Natural coordinates and horizontal approximations in two-field cosmological models
Calin Iuliu Lazaroiu

Abstract
. We construct natural local coordinate systems on the phase space of two-field cosmological models with orientable target space, which allow for a description of cosmological flows through quantities of direct physical interest. Such coordinates are induced by the fundamental observables of the model, which we formulate geometrically using the tautological bundle of the tangent bundle of the scalar manifold. We also describe a large class of geometric dynamical approximations induced by the choice of an Ehresmann connection in the tangent bundle of the scalar manifold. Such approximations take a conceptually simple form in natural coordinates and we illustrate one of them as an application.

In the absence of cosmological perturbations, a multifield
cosmological model is defined by a nonlinear geometric second order
ODE associated to a
scalar triple

(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{G},\Phi)
, where

(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{G})
is a connected (but generally non-compact) smooth and
complete Riemannian manifold without boundary (called the

of the model) and

is a smooth real-valued

\mathcal{M}

strictly positive. To such data one associates the

of the scalar triple:

\nabla_{t}\dot{\varphi}(t)+\frac{1}{M_{0}}\left[||\dot{\varphi}(t)||^{2}+2\Phi(\varphi(t))\right]^{1/2}\dot{\varphi}(t)+(\mathrm{grad}\Phi)(\varphi(t))=0~~,

\mathcal{M}
defined on

\mathcal{G}
,

connection of
(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{G})
and
\nabla_{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\nabla_{\dot{\varphi}(t)}

rescaled Planck mass

a parameter which can be absorbed by redefining

more convenient for various arguments to treat it as a free
parameter. The problem of mathematical and physical interest is to
study the solutions of this geometric ODE for general scalar triples

(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{G},\Phi)
and to develop geometric methods for extracting the
qualitative features of solutions and for approximating them
efficiently
. Among other questions, one is interested in the
asymptotic behavior of solutions which (depending on the asymptotics of

\Phi
) might “escape towards infinity” at the Freudenthal ends of

\mathcal{M}
. We refer the reader to
[
13
,
21
,
22
]
for a
conceptual discussion of certain aspects of multifield cosmological
models with topologically non-trivial target spaces
\mathcal{M}
.

The first nontrivial case of interest arises for two-field
cosmological models, which comprise the case
\dim\mathcal{M}=2
– i.e. when

(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{G})
is a (generally non-compact) Riemann surface. Such models
can already display intricate behavior which depends strongly on the
topology of
\mathcal{M}
and on the asymptotic behavior of
\Phi
and
\mathcal{G}

towards its Freudenthal ends (see

[
13
,
14
,
15

). Despite being the focus of much
recent interest, little is known about the behavior of solutions of the
cosmological equation for such models, except for special examples
which are generally studied using naive approximations or by numerical
means. In particular, no systematic theory exists which provides
geometric approximation schemes that allow for efficient control of
the error terms. Moreover, no global geometric description of
quantities of physical interest was developed in the physics
literature.

In the present paper, we address some of these issues for two-field
models with orientable target space
\mathcal{M}
by developing the geometric
theory of natural cosmological observables and associated notions
while introducing a wide class of approximation schemes (which we call
“horizontal approximations”) for the cosmological equation, which
are defined by the choice of an Ehresmann connection on an open subset
of
T\mathcal{M}
. As applications, we write the first order form of the
cosmological equation in certain “universal” local coordinate
systems on
T\mathcal{M}
which have direct physical interpretation and
describe the horizontal approximation and some of its attending
objects defined by one of of these. This turns out to correspond to
a proposal made previously in the physics literature, some
aspects of which were partially clarified in
[
23
]
. Given space
limitations, we postpone the analysis of two-field cosmological
dynamics in the natural coordinates identified in this
paper (as well as an analysis of the error of horizontal
approximations) for further work.

Horizontal approximations for second order geometric ODEs

1.1.
Approximations of a geometric dynamical system induced by complementary distributions

Let
N
be a manifold and
S\in\mathcal{X}(N)
a vector field
defined on
N
. We view
(N,S)
as an autonomous dynamical system
whose flow curves
\gamma:I\rightarrow N
are the solutions of the
integral curve equation of
S
:

\dot{\gamma}(t)=S(\gamma(t))~~\forall t\in I~~.

Two Frobenius distributions
V,W\in TN
are called
complementary

if
TN=V\oplus W
. We think of
V
as being “vertical” and of
W
as
being “horizontal” and use notation justified by this for reasons
which will become clear in the next subsection. Given a pair of
complementary distributions
(V,W)
on
N
, any vector field
X\in\mathcal{X}(N)
decomposes uniquely as
X=X_{V}\oplus X_{H}
with
X_{V}\in\Gamma(V)
and
X_{H}\in\Gamma(W)
. This defines projectors
P_{V}\in\mathrm{Hom}(TN,V)
and
P_{H}\in\mathrm{Hom}(TN,W)
such that:

P_{V}X=X_{V}~~,~~P_{H}X=X_{H}~~.

We will use terminology which doesn’t refer to
V
explicitly, for
reasons which will become clear in our discussion of second order
geometric ODEs below.

Definition 1.1
.

The
W
-flow curves of
S
are the integral curves of the vector field

S_{H}
, i.e. the solutions
\lambda:I\rightarrow N
of the equation:

\dot{\lambda}(t)=S_{H}(\lambda(t))~~\forall t\in I~~.

In the situation above, the
W
-approximation
of the
dynamical system
(N,S)
consists of replacing it with the dynamical
system
(N,S_{H})
, which we call the
W
-approximant
of
(N,S)
.
Accordingly, one approximates the integral curves
\gamma
of
S
by
the integral curves
\lambda
of
S_{H}
. The approximation can be
accurate only for those integral curves
\gamma
of
S
which have the
property that
S_{V}(\gamma(t))
is “small” for all
t\in I
with
respect to some appropriate notion of smallness for sections of
W
;
this requires the choice of an appropriate topology on
\Gamma(W)

(for example, the Whitney topology or the distance topology induced by
a metric on
TN
). Intuitively, the approximation is good if
\gamma

“stays close” to the zero locus of
S_{V}
in
N
. One can of course
also consider the “complementary” approximation in which one
replaces
S
by
S_{V}
.

Definition 1.2
.

The
W
-mean field locus

\mathcal{Z}_{W}

\mathcal{Z}_{W}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\{n\in N~|~S_{V}(n)=0\}~~.

Notice that

is a manifold if
S_{V}
is transverse
to the zero section of
V
. Let

be a point of

\gamma:I\rightarrow N

0.1

An important special case arises if the distribution

1.2.
Formulation for second order geometric ODEs

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a connected manifold and
S\in\mathcal{X}(T\mathcal{M})
be a
second order vector field
[
24
]
(a.k.a. “semispray”) defined on
the total space of the tangent bundle
\pi:T\mathcal{M}\rightarrow\mathcal{M}
; this
means that
S
is invariant under the “flip” involution of the
double tangent bundle
TT\mathcal{M}
. It is well-known (see loc. cit.) that
such a vector field describes the first order formulation of an
autonomous
geometric
second order ODE defined on
\mathcal{M}
whose
second order term separates. In any local coordinate system
(x^{i})
on

\mathcal{M}
, this corresponds to a system of
n
second order ODEs of the
form:

\ddot{x}^{i}=f^{i}(x,\dot{x})~~.

The adjective “geometric” means that this second order system is
invariant under coordinate transformations on
\mathcal{M}
; equivalently, it
means that it can be formulated as the condition

j^{2}(\varphi)=\mathfrak{s}(j^{1}(\varphi))
for curves
\varphi:I\rightarrow\mathcal{M}
, where
j^{1}(\varphi)=\dot{\varphi}:I\rightarrow J^{1}(\mathcal{M})=T\mathcal{M}

and
j^{2}(\varphi):I\rightarrow J^{2}(\mathcal{M})
are the first and second jet
prolongations of
\varphi
and
\mathfrak{s}
is a section of the jet bundle
fibration
\pi_{21}:J^{2}(\mathcal{M})\rightarrow J^{1}(\mathcal{M})
. Here

J^{k}(\mathcal{M})\rightarrow\mathcal{M}
denotes the
k
-th jet bundle of curves in

\mathcal{M}
(see Subsection
2.3
).

By definition, a
flow curve
of
S
is an integral curve

\gamma:I\rightarrow T\mathcal{M}
of
S
while a
solution curve
of
S

is a solution
\varphi:I\rightarrow\mathcal{M}
of the corresponding second
order geometric ODE defined on
\mathcal{M}
. Let
C_{s}(\mathcal{M})\subset C(\mathcal{M})
be
the set of solution curves and
C_{f}(T\mathcal{M})\subset C(T\mathcal{M})
be the set
of flow curves, where
C(N)
denotes the set of smooth curves in a
manifold
N
. Since
S
is a semispray, the maps
c:C(\mathcal{M})\rightarrow C(T\mathcal{M})
and
p:C(T\mathcal{M})\rightarrow C(\mathcal{M})
defined through

c(\varphi):=\dot{\varphi}
and
p(\gamma):=\pi\circ\gamma
(see
Subsection
0.1
) restrict to mutually-inverse
bijections between
C_{s}(\mathcal{M})
and
C_{f}(T\mathcal{M})
.

Let
\pi:T\mathcal{M}\rightarrow\mathcal{M}
be the projection of
T\mathcal{M}
,
V:=\ker(\mathrm{d}\pi)\subset TT\mathcal{M}
be the vertical distribution of
T\mathcal{M}
and
W

be a complete Ehresmann connection on
T\mathcal{M}
, i.e. a distribution

W\subset TT\mathcal{M}
on
T\mathcal{M}
which is complementary to
V
in
TT\mathcal{M}
:

TT\mathcal{M}=V\oplus W

and which defines a horizontal lift of any curve in
\mathcal{M}
through any
point of
T\mathcal{M}
lying above that curve. We denote by
X_{V},X_{H}
the
vertical and horizontal projections of any vector field field
X\in\Gamma(TT\mathcal{M})
defined on
T\mathcal{M}
. For any pointed curve

\varphi:I\rightarrow\mathcal{M}
and any
u\in T\mathcal{M}
with

\pi(u)=\varphi(0)
, let
\bm{\varphi}_{u}:I\rightarrow T\mathcal{M}
be the
horizontal lift of
\varphi
through
u
at

t=0
, which is uniquely determined by the conditions:

\dot{\bm{\varphi}}_{u}(t)\in W_{\bm{\varphi}_{u}(t)}~~\forall t\in I~~,~~\bm{\varphi}_{u}(0)=u~~\mathrm{and}~~\pi\circ\bm{\varphi}_{u}=\varphi~~.

-curve. Equation (
2
)
for
\psi_{u}
takes the form:

\dot{\bm{\psi}}_{u}(t)=S_{H}(\bm{\psi}_{u}(t))~~\forall t\in I

(3)

and can be viewed as a first order geometric ODE for
\psi
with
initial condition
\psi(0)=m
and parameter
u\in T_{m}\mathcal{M}
. This ODE
has a unique maximal solution for each parameter
u\in T_{\psi(0)}\mathcal{M}

and
\psi
can be recovered by projecting that solution to
\mathcal{M}
. Let

I_{u}
be the interval of definition of the maximal solution of
(
3
) at a fixed value of
u
. Then the maximal solution of
(
3
) for parameter
u
projects to a
W
-curve

\psi^{(u)}:I_{u}\rightarrow\mathcal{M}
which satisfies
\psi^{(u)}(0)=m
.
This gives a family of
W
-curves passing through each point
m
of

\mathcal{M}
and parameterized by the vectors tangent to
\mathcal{M}
at that
point. Notice that this parameterization need not be single-valued. A
specific
W
-curve
\psi^{(u)}
which passes through
m
at

can
be specified by selecting a tangent vector
u\in T_{m}\mathcal{M}
. This
determines the speed of
\psi^{(u)}
at
t=0
through the relation:

\dot{\psi}^{(u)}(0)=(\mathrm{d}\pi)(S(u))~~.

Remark 1.7
.

Two phase space
W
-curves
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}:I\rightarrow T\mathcal{M}

determine the same
W
-curve
\psi:I\rightarrow\mathcal{M}
iff both of them
project to
\psi
, which requires
(\mathrm{d}\pi)\circ S\circ\lambda_{1}=(\mathrm{d}\pi)\circ S\circ\lambda_{2}=\dot{\psi}
.

The
horizontal approximation
defined by the Ehresmann connection

W
consists of approximating integral curves of
S
by phase space

W
-curves of
S
, i.e. approximating the flow of
S
on
T\mathcal{M}
by the
flow of
S_{H}
. As explained below, this amounts to approximating
solution curves
\varphi
of the second order geometric ODE defined by

S
on
\mathcal{M}
by
W
-curves of
S
whose speed approximates the
projection of the acceleration of
\varphi
. The approximation is accurate for those
integral curves
\gamma:I\rightarrow T\mathcal{M}
of
S
which are almost
horizontal in the sense that
S_{V}\circ\gamma
is close to zero (where
“closeness to zero” is measured, for example, by the fiberwise norm
induced on
TT\mathcal{M}
by a metric on
T\mathcal{M}
).

Let
\varphi:I\rightarrow\mathcal{M}
be a pointed solution curve such that
the vector
u:=\dot{\varphi}(0)
lies on the mean field manifold

\mathcal{Z}_{W}
. Then
S(u)\in W
and the acceleration of
\varphi
at

t=0
is horizontal:

\ddot{\varphi}(0)=S(\dot{\varphi}(0))=S(u)\in W~~\mathrm{in}~~TT\mathcal{M}~~.

After passing to a subinterval of
I
if necessary, let
\psi=\pi\circ\lambda:I\rightarrow\mathcal{M}
be the
W
-curve which is the projection of
the unique phase space
W
-curve
\lambda:I\rightarrow T\mathcal{M}
which
satisfies
\lambda(0)=\dot{\varphi}(0)=u
. The curve
\lambda
and the
canonical lift
\gamma=\dot{\varphi}
of
\varphi
satisfy:

\dot{\lambda}(t)=S_{H}(\lambda(t))~~,~~\dot{\gamma}(t)=S(\gamma(t))~~\forall t\in I

with the initial conditions:

\gamma(0)=\lambda(0)=u~~.

Thus:

\psi(0)=\pi(u)=\varphi(0)~~\mathrm{and}~~\dot{\psi}(0)=(\mathrm{d}\pi)(S(u))=(\mathrm{d}\pi)(\ddot{\varphi}(0))

and we have:

\dot{\psi}(t)=(\mathrm{d}\pi)(\dot{\lambda}(t))=(\mathrm{d}\pi)(S(\lambda(t)))~~\forall t\in I~~.

The approximation
\gamma(t)\approx\lambda(t)
implies

S(\lambda(t))\approx S(\gamma(t))=\dot{\gamma}(t)=\ddot{\varphi}(t)

and is equivalent with the joint approximation
\varphi(t)\approx\psi(t)~\&~\dot{\varphi}(t)\approx\dot{\lambda}(t)
, which implies

\varphi(t)\approx\psi(t)~~\&~(\mathrm{d}\pi)(\ddot{\varphi}(t))\approx\dot{\psi}(t)
. Notice that the “mean field vector”

u=\dot{\varphi}(0)\in\mathcal{Z}_{W}
determines the
W
-curve
\psi
which
passes through the point
\varphi(0)
at
t=0
and is used to
approximate the solution curve
\varphi
. Intuitively, the
approximation neglects the vertical component of the acceleration

\ddot{\varphi}
of
\varphi
. This approximation is exact at
t=0

and remains accurate on a small enough neighborhood of the origin
inside the interval
I
. In particular, the phase space

W
-approximation of flow curves is exact on the mean field manifold
for all flow curves which intersect
\mathcal{Z}_{W}
.

Definition 1.8
.

A
mean field
for
S
relative to
W
is a locally defined vector
field
X\in\Gamma(TU_{0})
such that:

X(m)\in\mathcal{Z}_{W}~~\forall m\in U_{0}~~,

(4)

where
U_{0}
is an open subset of
\mathcal{M}
.

If
U
is connected, then the image
X(U)
of a mean field

X\in\Gamma(U,T\mathcal{M})
is a subset of a “branch” of the mean field
manifold
\mathcal{Z}_{W}
(when the latter is viewed as a multisection of

\pi
). This allows one to give local parameterizations of that
branch of the mean field manifold by choosing local coordinates
defined on open subsets
U\subset\mathcal{M}
. The name “mean field” was
chosen by analogy with similar objects which appear in condensed
matter physics.

1.5.
The mean field approximation defined by
W

Let
S_{W}\in\Gamma(TW)
be the projection of

S|_{\mathcal{Z}_{W}}
to the integrable distribution
T\mathcal{Z}_{W}
, where the
projection is taken parallel to
V
.

Definition 1.9
.

A curve
\rho:I\rightarrow\mathcal{Z}_{W}
is called a
phase mean field
curve
of
S
relative to
W
if it is an integral curve of the
vector field
S_{W}\in\Gamma(TW)
, i.e. if it satisfies:

\dot{\rho}(t)=S_{W}(\rho(t))~~\forall t\in I~~.

(5)

Notice that any phase mean field curve lies on
\mathcal{Z}_{W}

The
mean field approximation
consists of approximating solution
curves of the geometric ODE defined by
S
by phase mean field curves
of
S
relative to
W
. This is a singular approximation in the sense
that it restricts the phase space of the dynamical system from
T\mathcal{M}

to
T\mathcal{Z}_{W}
. The approximation is accurate for those solution curves

\varphi
whose flow curve
\gamma=\dot{\varphi}
lies very close to
the mean field manifold
\mathcal{Z}_{W}
,
provided that

S|_{\mathcal{Z}_{W}}

is well-approximated by
S_{W}
.

Let
\varphi:I\rightarrow\mathcal{M}
be a pointed solution curve of
S
such that
the vector
u:=\dot{\varphi}(0)
lies on the mean field manifold

\mathcal{Z}_{W}
. Let
\gamma:=\dot{\varphi}:I\rightarrow T\mathcal{M}
be the canonical
lift of
\varphi
. Then:

\dot{\gamma}(0)=S(\gamma(0))=S(u)\in W_{u}~~.

After passing to a subinterval of
I
if necessary, let

\rho:I\rightarrow\mathcal{Z}_{W}
be the unique phase mean field curve which
satisfies:

\rho(0)=u~~.

Then:

\dot{\rho}(t)=S_{W}(\rho(t))~~\forall t\in I

and we have:

\dot{\rho}(0)=S_{W}(u)~~.

Let
\mu\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\pi\circ\rho:I\rightarrow\mathcal{M}
. The approximation

\gamma(t)\approx\rho(t)
implies
\varphi(t)\approx\mu(t)
and

(\mathrm{d}\pi)(S_{W}(\dot{\varphi}(t)))\approx\dot{\mu}(t)
and is accurate
for
t
close to zero only if
\dot{\gamma}(0)\approx\dot{\rho}(0)
,
i.e. only if the horizontal vector
S(u)
is almost tangent to
\mathcal{Z}_{W}

and hence is well approximated by
S_{W}(u)
. This approximation is much
coarser than the horizontal approximation and is not guaranteed to be
accurate for any flow curve or non-degenerate time interval.

Remark 1.10
.

The discussion above can be adapted easily to the restriction of

and
W
to a non-empty open subset of
T\mathcal{M}
. We leave the details of this
generalization to the reader.

1.6.
Fiberwise coordinates and special coordinate systems on
\dot{T}\mathcal{M}

Suppose for simplicity that
\dim\mathcal{M}=2
, which is the case
relevant for the remainder of the paper. Let
U
be a non-empty open
subset of
T\mathcal{M}
. Then
U_{0}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\pi(U)
is an open subset of
\mathcal{M}

since
\pi
is a submersion and hence an open map.

Definition 1.11
.

A
system of fiberwise coordinates
defined on
U
is a (generally
non-surjective) submersion
y=(y^{1},y^{2}):U\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
whose
restriction to every non-empty intersection of
U
with a fiber of

T\mathcal{M}
is a system of coordinates on that fiber. That is, the map:

y|_{\pi^{-1}(m)\cap U}:\pi^{-1}(m)\cap U\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}

is a diffeomorphism to its image
y(\pi^{-1}(m)\cap U)
for all
m\in U_{0}
.

A particular case arises when
y(\pi^{-1}(m)\cap U)=y(U)
for all
m\in U
.

Proposition 1.12
.

Let
y:U\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a smooth map. Then the
following statements are equivalent:

(a)

y
is a fiberwise coordinate system such that

y(\pi^{-1}(m)\cap U)=y(U)
for all
m\in U_{0}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\pi(U)
.

(b)

The restriction
\pi_{U}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\pi|_{U}:U\rightarrow U_{0}
is a
topologically trivial fiber bundle with typical fiber
y(U)
and the
map
(\pi_{U},y):U\rightarrow U_{0}\times y(U)
is a trivialization of
this fiber bundle, where
U_{0}\times y(U)
is viewed as trivial fiber
bundle over
U_{0}
using projection on the first factor.

In this case,
\pi_{U}
is fiber sub-bundle of the vector bundle
T\mathcal{U}_{0}
.

Proof.

To see that
(a)
implies
(b)
, notice that all fibers of the surjective
submersion
\pi_{U}
are diffeomorphic with
y(U)
and
(\pi_{U},y)

is an isomorphism of fiber bundles to the trivial
y(U)
-bundle over

U_{0}
. The converse implication follows from the definition of
trivializations of fiber bundles.
∎

An interesting class of examples arises from local frames of
T\mathcal{M}
.

Definition 1.13
.

Let
U_{0}
be a non-empty open subset of
\mathcal{M}
. A surjective system of fiberwise
coordinates
y=(y^{1},y^{2}):\pi^{-1}(U_{0})=TU_{0}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
is called

linear
if the restriction of
y
to every fiber of
TU_{0}
is a
linear isomorphism.

Proposition 1.14
.

The following statements are equivalent for a map
y:TU_{0}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

(a)

y
is a linear system of fiberwise coordinates.

(b)

There exists a frame
(e_{1},e_{2})
of
TU_{0}
such that:

u=y^{1}(u)e_{1}(\pi(u))+y^{2}(u)e_{2}(\pi(u))~~\forall u\in TU_{0}

Notice that the frame
(e_{1},e_{2})
need not be holonomic
(i.e. it need not be induced by a local coordinate system on
\mathcal{M}
).

Proof.

Suppose that
(a)
holds. For any
m\in U_{0}
, the restrictions
y^{i}_{m}:T_{m}\mathcal{M}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
give linear coordinates on
T_{m}\mathcal{M}
. If
e_{1}(m),e_{2}(m)\in T_{m}\mathcal{M}
are the unique vectors which satisfy

y(e_{1}(m))=(1,0)
and
y(e_{2}(m))=(0,1)
then any
u\in T_{m}\mathcal{M}
can be
written as:

u=y^{1}_{m}(u)e_{1}(m)+y^{2}_{m}(u)e_{2}(m)~~

and hence

(e_{1}(m),e_{2}(m))
forms a basis of the fiber
T_{m}\mathcal{M}
. This gives a
frame
(e_{1},e_{2})
of
TU_{0}
and hence
(b)
holds. Now suppose that

(b)
holds. Then we have
y^{i}_{m}(u)=u^{i}
for
m\in U_{0}
and
u\in T_{m}U_{0}
, where
u=u^{1}e_{1}(m)+u^{2}e_{2}(m)
is the frame expansion of

u
. It is clear that
y=(y^{1},y^{2})
is a linear system of fiberwise
coordinates.
∎

Definition 1.15
.

A coordinate system
x=(y^{1},y^{2},\xi^{1},\xi^{2}):U\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{4}

defined on an open subset
U\subset T\mathcal{M}
is called
special
if
it satisfies the following two conditions:

1.

(y^{1},y^{2}):U\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a system of fiberwise
coordinates defined on
U
.

2.

There exists a system of coordinates

x=(x^{1},x^{2}):U_{0}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\pi(U)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that

\xi^{1}=(x^{1})^{\mathrm{v}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}x^{1}\circ\pi_{U}
and
\xi^{2}=(x^{2})^{\mathrm{v}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}x^{2}\circ\pi_{U}
, where
\pi_{U}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\pi|_{U}
.

Given any system of fiberwise coordinates
(y^{1},y^{2})
on

U\subset T\mathcal{M}
and any system of coordinates
(x^{1},x^{2})
on

U_{0}=\pi(U)
, it is clear that
(y^{1},y^{2},\xi^{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}(x^{1})^{\mathrm{v}},\xi^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}(x^{2})^{\mathrm{v}})

.

1.7.
The horizontal approximation defined by a special system of coordinates

Let
(y^{1},y^{2},\xi^{1}=(x^{1})^{\mathrm{v}},\xi^{\mathrm{v}}=(x^{2})^{\mathrm{v}}):U\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{4}
be a special system of coordinates defined on the open subset

U\subset T\mathcal{M}
and let
U_{0}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\pi(U)
. The rank two integrable
distribution
W
spanned by the vector fields
\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi^{1}}

and
\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi^{2}}
on
U
is complementary to the vertical
distribution
V
of the submersion
\pi_{U}:U\rightarrow U_{0}
and
defines a complete
flat
Ehresmann connection on
U
.
As in the previous subsection, this gives a decomposition
S=S_{V}+S_{H}
of any
semispray
S
defined on
U
. Write:

S=S^{y^{i}}\frac{\partial}{\partial y^{i}}+S^{\xi^{i}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi^{i}}~~,

where we use implicit summation over repeated indices and
i\in\{1,2\}
. Since the vector fields
\frac{\partial}{\partial y^{i}},\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi^{i}}\in\mathcal{X}(U)
are respectively vertical and horizontal, we
have:

S_{V}=S^{y^{i}}\frac{\partial}{\partial y^{i}}~\mathrm{and}~~S_{H}=S^{\xi^{i}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi^{i}}~~.

To identify the horizontal part of
S
, let

\zeta=(\zeta^{1},\zeta^{2}):TU_{0}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
be the linear system of
fiberwise coordinates defined by the frame
\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{1}},\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{2}}\right)
of
TU_{0}
. This is known as the
tangent chart
of the chart
(x^{1},x^{2}):U_{0}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
of

\mathcal{M}
. The change of coordinates from
(\zeta,\xi)
to
(y,\xi)
and
its inverse have the forms:

y^{i}=\mathrm{y}^{i}(\zeta,\xi)~,~~\xi^{i}=\xi^{i}~~\mathrm{and}~~\zeta^{i}=\upzeta^{i}(y,\xi)~~,~~\xi^{i}=\xi^{i}~,

(6)

where
\mathrm{y}^{i}
and
\upzeta^{i}
are functions. In particular, we have
\frac{\partial\xi^{i}}{\partial y^{j}}=0
and hence:

\frac{\partial}{\partial y^{i}}=\frac{\partial\upzeta^{j}}{\partial y^{i}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\zeta^{j}}~~.

Since
S
is a semispray, its horizontal part is given by the
following formula in the tangent chart
(\zeta,\xi)
(see
[
24
]
):

S_{H}=\zeta^{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi^{i}}~~.

Thus:

S^{\xi^{i}}=\zeta^{i}~~.

For any curve
\gamma:I\rightarrow U
, we
have:

\dot{\gamma}(t)=\dot{y}^{i}(t)\frac{\partial}{\partial y^{i}}\Big|_{\gamma(t)}+\dot{\xi}^{i}(t)\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi^{i}}\Big|_{\gamma(t)}~~,

where
y^{i}(t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}y^{i}(\gamma(t))
and
\xi^{i}(t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\xi^{i}(\gamma(t))
. Hence:

\dot{\gamma}(t)_{V}=\dot{y}^{j}(t)\frac{\partial}{\partial y^{i}}\Big|_{\gamma(t)}~~,~~\dot{\gamma}(t)_{H}=\dot{\xi}^{i}(t)\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi^{i}}\Big|_{\gamma(t)}

and the integral curve equation of
S
amounts to
the following system of ODEs:

\dot{y}^{i}(t)=S^{y^{i}}(y(t),\xi(t))~~,~~\dot{\xi}^{i}(t)=S^{\xi^{i}}(y(t),\xi(t))=\upzeta^{i}(y(t),\xi(t))~~.

(7)

The mean field locus
\mathcal{Z}_{W}
of
S
relative to
W
has equations:

S^{y_{1}}(y,\xi)=S^{y_{2}}(y,\xi)=0~~.

As before, we assume that
S
is regular relative to
W
, i.e.
S_{V}

is transverse to the zero section of
V
. This amounts to the
condition that the map
(S^{y^{1}},S^{y^{2}}):U\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
restricts
to a submersion on a tubular neighborhood of
\mathcal{Z}_{W}
in
U
. Writing a vector
field
X\in\mathcal{X}(U_{0})
as
X=X^{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{i}}
with
X^{i}\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(U_{0})
, we have
\xi^{i}\circ X=x^{i}
and
\zeta^{i}\circ X=X^{i}
.
Hence the mean field equation (
4
) reads:

S^{y^{i}}(\mathrm{y}^{1}(X^{1}(x),X^{2}(x),x),\mathrm{y}^{2}(X^{1}(x),X^{2}(x),x),x)=0~~(i=1,2)~~,

where
\mathrm{y}^{i}
are the functions appearing in the coordinate
transformations (
6
). This system of functional
equations locally determines
X^{i}
as functions of
x
, though the
solution need not be globally unique.

Let
\lambda:I\rightarrow U
be a curve in
U
. Setting
y^{i}(t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}y^{i}(\lambda(t))
and
\xi^{i}(t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\xi^{i}(\lambda(t))
, the phase space

W
-curve equation (
2
) amounts to the system:

\dot{y}^{i}(t)=0~~\mathrm{and}~\dot{\xi}^{i}(t)=S^{\xi^{i}}(y(t),\xi(t))=\upzeta^{i}(y(t),\xi(t))~~\forall t\in I~~.

Intuitively, the horizontal approximation defined by the special
coordinates
(y,\xi)
amounts to treating the variables
y^{1}
and

y^{2}
as ‘slow’ in the sense that
\dot{y}^{1}
and
\dot{y}^{2}

are neglected. This corresponds to neglecting the following combinations:

\frac{\partial\mathrm{y}^{i}}{\partial\zeta^{j}}(\dot{x}(t),x(t))\ddot{x}^{j}(t)+\frac{\partial\mathrm{y}^{i}}{\partial\xi^{j}}(\dot{x}(t),x(t))\dot{x}^{j}(t)~~(i=1,2)

of the components of the speed and acceleration of a solution curve

\varphi
of the geometric second order ODE

\ddot{\varphi}(t)=S(\dot{\varphi})(t)
defined by
S
on
U_{0}
. Here

x^{i}(t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}x^{i}(\varphi(t))
and we noticed that

\xi^{i}(\dot{\varphi}(t))=x^{i}(t)
and

\zeta^{i}(\dot{\varphi}(t))=\dot{x}^{i}
.

2.
Two-field cosmological models

By a
two-field cosmological model
we mean a
classical cosmological model with two real scalar fields derived from the
following Lagrangian on a spacetime with topology
\mathbb{R}^{4}
:

\mathcal{L}[g,\varphi]=\left[\frac{M^{2}}{2}\mathrm{R}(g)-\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Tr}_{g}\varphi^{\ast}(\mathcal{G})-\Phi\circ\varphi\right]\mathrm{vol}_{g}~~.

(8)

Here
M
is the reduced Planck mass,
g
is the spacetime metric on

\mathbb{R}^{4}
(taken to be of “mostly plus” signature) and
\mathrm{vol}_{g}
and

\mathrm{R}(g)
are the volume form and scalar curvature of
g
. The
scalar fields are described by a smooth map
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{4}\rightarrow\mathcal{M}
, where
\mathcal{M}
is a (generally non-compact) connected, smooth and
paracompact surface without boundary which we assume to be oriented
and endowed with a smooth Riemannian metric
\mathcal{G}
, while

\Phi:\mathcal{M}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a smooth function which plays the role of
potential for the scalar fields. One usually requires that
\mathcal{G}
is
complete to ensure conservation of energy. For simplicity, we also
assume that
\Phi
is strictly positive and non-constant on
\mathcal{M}
. The
triplet
(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{G},\Phi)
is called the
scalar triple
of the
model, while
(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{G})
is called the
scalar manifold
. The
model is parameterized by the quadruplet
\mathfrak{M}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}(M_{0},\mathcal{M},\mathcal{G},\Phi)
, where:

M_{0}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}M\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}}

is the
rescaled Planck mass
. We denote by:

\mathrm{Crit}\Phi\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\{m\in\mathcal{M}~|~(\mathrm{d}\Phi)(m)=0\}~~\mathrm{and}~~\mathcal{M}_{0}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\mathcal{M}\setminus\mathrm{Crit}\Phi

the
critical and noncritical subsets
of
\mathcal{M}
. Notice that

\mathcal{M}_{0}
is open in
\mathcal{M}
. It is also non-empty since we assume that

\Phi
is not constant.

The two-field model parameterized by the quadruplet
\mathfrak{M}
is obtained
by assuming that
g
is an FLRW metric with flat spatial section:

\mathrm{d}s^{2}_{g}=-\mathrm{d}t^{2}+a(t)^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{3}\mathrm{d}x_{i}^{2}

(9)

(where
a
is a smooth and positive function of
t
) and that

\varphi
depends only on the
cosmological time

t\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}x^{0}
. Here
(x^{0},x^{1},x^{2},x^{3})
are the Cartesian coordinates on

\mathbb{R}^{4}
. Define the
Hubble parameter
through:

H(t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\frac{\dot{a}(t)}{a(t)}~~,

where the dot indicates derivation with respect to
t
.

2.1.
The cosmological equation

Let
\pi:T\mathcal{M}\rightarrow\mathcal{M}
be bundle projection. Let

\mathcal{H}:T\mathcal{M}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{>0}
be the
rescaled Hubble function
of the scalar triple

(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{G},\Phi)
, which is defined through:

\mathcal{H}(u)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{M_{0}}\left[||u||^{2}+2\Phi(\pi(u))\right]^{1/2}~~\forall u\in T\mathcal{M}~~

(10)

and let
\nabla
be the Levi-Civita connection of
(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{G})
. When
H>0

(which we assume for the remainder of the paper), the Euler-Lagrange
equations of (
8
) reduce (see
[
13
,
16
,
17
]
)
to the
cosmological equation
:

\nabla_{t}\dot{\varphi}(t)+\mathcal{H}(\dot{\varphi}(t))\dot{\varphi}(t)+(\mathrm{grad}\Phi)(\varphi(t))=0~~,

(11)

(where
\nabla_{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\nabla_{\dot{\varphi}(t)}

The smooth solutions

A cosmological curve

\displaystyle I_{\mathrm{sing}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\{t\in I~|~\dot{\varphi}(t)=0\}~~,~~I_{\mathrm{reg}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}I\setminus I_{\mathrm{sing}}=\{t\in I~|~\dot{\varphi}(t)\neq 0\}~~.

The sets of

2.2.
The cosmological dynamical system

The cosmological equation (
11
) is
geometric
in the sense that it can be written as the condition

j^{2}(\varphi)=\mathfrak{s}(j^{1}(\varphi))
, where
\mathfrak{s}
is a section of the
fiber bundle
J^{2}(\mathcal{M})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\pi_{21}}}{{\rightarrow}}J^{1}(\mathcal{M})
(see
Subsection
2.3
). This statement follows from the fact
that (
11
) is expressed in terms of
diffeomorphism-covariant operators and from the fact that the double
derivative term in (
11
) separates from the lower order
terms in any local system of coordinates.

The geometric character of (
11
) implies that the
cosmological equation is invariant under changes of local coordinates
on
\mathcal{M}
and hence its first order form defines a geometric dynamical
system on the total space of
T\mathcal{M}
in the sense of

[
19
,
20
]
, which we call the
cosmological dynamical
system
defined by the scalar triple
(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{G},\Phi)
. As explained
for example in
[
24
]
, the defining vector field
S\in\mathcal{X}(T\mathcal{M})

of this dynamical system is a second-order vector field which we call
the
cosmological semispray
. We have (see
[
21
]
):

S=\mathbb{S}-Q~~,

where
\mathbb{S}
is the geodesic spray of the Riemannian manifold

(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{G})
and
Q\in\mathcal{X}(T\mathcal{M})
is the
cosmological correction
,
which is the vertical vector field:

Q=\mathcal{H}C+(\mathrm{grad}\Phi)^{v}~~.

Here
C\in\mathcal{X}(T\mathcal{M})
is the Euler vector field of
T\mathcal{M}
and

(\mathrm{grad}\Phi)^{v}\in T\mathcal{M}
is the vertical lift of the vector field

\mathrm{grad}\Phi\in\mathcal{X}(\mathcal{M})
to a vector field defined on
T\mathcal{M}
. The
cosmological equation (
11
) is equivalent with the
integral curve equation of
S
in the following sense (see

[
21
]
):

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
\varphi:I\rightarrow\mathcal{M}
be a curve in
\mathcal{M}
. Then the following
statements are equivalent:

(a)

\varphi
satisfies the cosmological equation (
11
)

(b)

The canonical lift
\gamma\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\dot{\varphi}:I\rightarrow T\mathcal{M}
of

\varphi
is an integral curve of the vector field
S
, i.e. it
satisfies the equation:

\dot{\gamma}(t)=S(\gamma(t))~~\forall t\in I~~.

(13)

The flow of
S
on the total space of
T\mathcal{M}
is called the

cosmological flow
of the model. Following dynamical
system terminology, we will refer to
T\mathcal{M}
as the
phase space
of the cosmological dynamical system. The
integral curves of
S
are called
cosmological flow
curves
while their images in
T\mathcal{M}
are called
cosmological flow orbits
. It can be shown that any
cosmological
flow
orbit is aperiodic and without
self-intersections. This follows from the fact that the
cosmological dynamical system is dissipative (see
Proposition
2.12
below).

2.3.
Local cosmological observables

For any

k

\mathcal{M}

\mathcal{M}

\pi_{k}

k

Remark 2.2

The product

bundle of sections of the trivial bundle
\mathbb{R}\times\mathcal{M}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

where the projection to

is the first projection of the Cartesian

Let
\mathcal{E}
be a vector bundle over
\mathcal{M}
and set
\mathcal{E}^{k}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\pi_{k}^{\ast}(\mathcal{E})

Definition 2.3
.

A (continuous and off-shell)

-valued local cosmological

\pi^{\ast}(\mathcal{E})

The rescaled Hubble function

Proof.

4.
Fundamental basic observables

4.1.
The fundamental parameters of a smooth curve in
\mathcal{M}

Let
\varphi:I\rightarrow\mathcal{M}

4.1.1.
Fundamental first order parameters.

We start by considering first order parameters, whose value at
t\in I
depends only only the tangent vector
\dot{\varphi}(t)

Definition 4.1
.

The
fundamental first order parameters
of a curve

\varphi:I\rightarrow\mathcal{M}
are defined as follows:

•

The
first IR parameter
of
\varphi
at time
t\in I
is
the non-negative real number:

\kappa_{\varphi}(t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\frac{||\dot{\varphi}(t)||^{2}}{2\Phi(\varphi(t))}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}~~.

(56)

•

The
rescaled conservative parameter
of
\varphi

{\hat{c}}_{\varphi}(t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\frac{\mathcal{H}_{\varphi}(t)||\dot{\varphi}(t)||}{||(\mathrm{d}\Phi)(\varphi(t))||}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}~~.

(57)

The
signed characteristic angle
of

\varphi

relative to the unit gradient vector

3.12

\theta_{\varphi}(t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\theta(T_{\varphi}(t))=\uptheta_{n_{\varphi}(t)}(T_{\varphi}(t))\in(-\pi,\pi]~~.

The first IR parameter equals the ratio between the kinetic
and potential energy of
\varphi
and plays a crucial role in
reference
[
21
]
. As we recall in Appendix
B
, this
parameter carries the same information as the ordinary first slow roll
parameter used in the physics literature. The conservative parameter

the cosmological equation and controls what we call the
quasi-conservative and strongly dissipative approximations (see
Section
8

8.
The quasi-conservative and strongly dissipative regimes

Definition 8.1
.

The
quasi-conservative condition
on a vector
u\in T\mathcal{M}_{0}
is
the condition
c(u)\ll 1
. The
strong dissipation condition
on

u
is the condition
c(u)\gg 1
.

Definition 8.2
.

We say that a noncritical cosmological curve
\varphi:I\rightarrow\mathcal{M}_{0}
satisfies the quasi-conservative or strong dissipation
condition at time
t\in I
when
c_{\varphi}(t)=c(\dot{\varphi}(t))
is
much smaller (respectively much larger) than one.

The cosmological equation (
11
) can be written as:

\nabla_{t}\dot{\varphi}(t)+||\mathrm{d}\Phi||\left[c_{\varphi}(t)T_{\varphi}(t)+n_{\varphi}(t)\right]=0~~.

When
\varphi
satisfies the quasi-conservative condition at time
t_{0}
,
this can be approximated by the
conservative equation
:

\nabla_{t}\dot{\varphi}(t)+(\mathrm{grad}\Phi)(\varphi(t))=0

for
t
close to
t_{0}
. The conservative equation describes motion of
a particle in the Riemannian manifold
(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{G})
under the influence
of the potential
\Phi
(which corresponds to a conservative dynamical
system). When
\varphi
satisfies the strong dissipation condition at time

t_{0}
, the cosmological equation can be approximated by the
modified geodesic equation
:

\nabla_{t}\dot{\varphi}(t)+\mathcal{H}_{\varphi}(t)\dot{\varphi}(t)=0

for
t
close to
t_{0}
. The solutions of this equation are
reparameterized geodesics of
(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{G})
. Some more detail about the
quasi-conservative and strongly dissipative approximations can be
found in
[
23
]
.

Remark 8.3
.

Recall equation (
84
):

({\hat{\eta}}^{\parallel}-1)^{2}+\hat{\omega}^{2}=\frac{1}{{\hat{c}}^{2}}~~.

(113)

This shows that the
rapid turn condition

|\hat{\omega}|\gg 1
is
equivalent with
\frac{1}{{\hat{c}}}\gg\sqrt{1+({\hat{\eta}}^{\parallel}-1)^{2}}
, which
reduces to the quasi-conservative condition
{\hat{c}}\ll 1
in the second
slow roll regime
|{\hat{\eta}}^{\parallel}|\ll 1
. Hence the rapid turn and
quasi-conservative limits are equivalent within the second slow roll
regime. Recall that the condition
{\hat{\eta}}^{\parallel}\approx 1
defines the
adiabatic ultra slow roll regime. In this case, we have

\hat{\omega}\approx-\frac{\sigma}{{\hat{c}}}\neq 0
and
\theta\approx\sigma\frac{\pi}{2}
.

The first relation in (
83
) requires:

{\hat{\eta}}^{\parallel}\in\left[1-\frac{1}{{\hat{c}}},1+\frac{1}{{\hat{c}}}\right]

and can be written as (
104
). In particular, the interval
within which
{\hat{\eta}}^{\parallel}
can take values is centered at
1
and
constrained by the value of
{\hat{c}}
. This interval is very large in the
quasi-conservative regime
{\hat{c}}\ll 1
and becomes narrow in the
strongly dissipative regime
{\hat{c}}\gg 1
, when
{\hat{\eta}}^{\parallel}
is forced to be
close to one. In particular, the adiabatic ultra slow roll
approximation
{\hat{\eta}}^{\parallel}\approx 1
is accurate in the strongly
dissipative regime.

Appendix B
Relation between parameters used in this paper and parameters used in the physics literature

B.0.1.
The first slow roll parameter.

It is customary to define another second order parameter of a curve as follows:

Definition B.1
.

The
first slow roll parameter
of a curve

\varphi:I\rightarrow\mathcal{M}
is the function

\bm{\varepsilon}_{\varphi}:I\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
defined through:

\bm{\varepsilon}_{\varphi}(t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}-\frac{\dot{H_{\varphi}}(t)}{H_{\varphi}(t)^{2}}=-\frac{1}{H_{\varphi}(t)}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\log H_{\varphi}(t)=3{\hat{\bm{\varepsilon}}}_{\varphi}(t)~~,

where:

{\hat{\bm{\varepsilon}}}_{\varphi}(t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}-\frac{1}{\mathcal{H}_{\varphi}(t)}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\log\mathcal{H}_{\varphi}(t)

is the
rescaled
first slow roll parameter of
\varphi
.

For cosmological curves, the first slow roll parameter
reduces to a function of the first IR parameter and hence does not
give an independent basic observable.

Proposition B.2
.

Suppose that
\varphi:I\rightarrow\mathcal{M}
is a cosmological curve. Then we have:

{\hat{\bm{\varepsilon}}}_{\varphi}(t)=\frac{\kappa_{\varphi}(t)}{1+\kappa_{\varphi}(t)}~~\forall t\in I~~.

Proof.

Follows immediately from the cosmological equation (

∎
