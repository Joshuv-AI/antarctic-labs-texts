Mirror Descent Algorithms with Nearly Dimension-Independent Rates for Differentially- Private Stochastic Saddle-Point Problems
Tomás González, Cristóbal Guzmán, Courtney Paquette

Abstract
We study the problem of differentially-private (DP) stochastic (convex-concave) saddle-points in the ℓ 1 \ell_{1} setting. We propose ( ε , δ ) (\varepsilon,\delta) -DP algorithms based on stochastic mirror descent that attain nearly dimension-independent convergence rates for the expected duality gap, a type of guarantee that was known before only for bilinear objectives. For convex-concave and first-order-smooth stochastic objectives, our algorithms attain a rate of log ⁡ ( d ) / n + ( log ⁡ ( d ) 3 / 2 / [ n ​ ε ] ) 1 / 3 \sqrt{\log(d)/n}+(\log(d)^{3/2}/[n\varepsilon])^{1/3} , where d d is the dimension of the problem and n n the dataset size. Under an additional second-order-smoothness assumption, we show that the duality gap is bounded by log ⁡ ( d ) / n + log ⁡ ( d ) / n ​ ε \sqrt{\log(d)/n}+\log(d)/\sqrt{n\varepsilon} with high probability, by using bias-reduced gradient estimators. This rate provides evidence of the near-optimality of our approach, since a lower bound of log ⁡ ( d ) / n + log ⁡ ( d ) 3 / 4 / n ​ ε \sqrt{\log(d)/n}+\log(d)^{3/4}/\sqrt{n\varepsilon} exists. Finally, we show that combining our methods with acceleration techniques from online learning leads to the first algorithm for DP Stochastic Convex Optimization in the ℓ 1 \ell_{1} setting that is not based on Frank-Wolfe methods. For convex and first-order-smooth stochastic objectives, our algorithms attain an excess risk of log ⁡ ( d ) / n + log ⁡ ( d ) 7 / 10 / [ n ​ ε ] 2 / 5 \sqrt{\log(d)/n}+\log(d)^{7/10}/[n\varepsilon]^{2/5} , and when additionally assuming second-order-smoothness, we improve the rate to log ⁡ ( d ) / n + log ⁡ ( d ) / n ​ ε \sqrt{\log(d)/n}+\log(d)/\sqrt{n\varepsilon} . Instrumental to all of these results are various extensions of the classical Maurey Sparsification Lemma [ 34 ] , which may be of independent interest.

There is a major discrepancy in the literature between the achievable rates of convergence for the duality gap between some structured problems and more general convex-concave settings

1.2
Related Work

DP stochastic saddle-points

Stochastic (non-private) saddle-point problems have connections to optimization, economics, machine learning, and many other fields. One such algorithm for solving these problems is the stochastic entropic mirror-descent algorithm
[
31
]
, which is the basis for much of this work. Recently, a line of research has studied the differentially private (DP) version of stochastic saddle-point problems
[
13
,
44
,
43
,
27
,
8
]
. These works focus exclusively on Euclidean setups, which necessarily leads to algorithms that have a polynomial dependence on the dimension. Moreover, due to the Euclidean setup, the techniques used in these works do not necessarily apply to the setting considered here. In addition, with only limited exceptions
[
13
,
8
]
, most previous work provide guarantees on the
weak
duality gap. The (strong) duality gap (
2
), on the other hand, is more meaningful (see
[
8
]
for justification), but also more difficult to bound, most notably in the private setting. This is the convergence criterion that we use in this work.

Optimization in the
\ell_{1}
setting

Closest to this work is DP stochastic convex optimization (SCO) in the
\ell_{1}
setting
[
3
,
9
,
21
]
, as well as earlier work on private empirical risk minimization (ERM)

[
25
,
38
,
37
]
. These works attain nearly dimension-independent excess risk rates with private variants of the (stochastic) Frank-Wolfe method.
Even though this method leads to optimal rates for DP-SCO, it is not known to yield any nontrivial rates for saddle-point problems.
Even without DP constraints, it is not known how to solve SSP problems with Frank-Wolfe methods unless strong additional assumptions are made: namely, either simultaneously assuming smoothness and strong convexity/strong concavity
3
3

3

These assumptions are indeed strong: If

is an
L_{1}
-smooth and
\mu
-strongly convex function w.r.t.
\|\cdot\|_{1}
, then
L_{1}/\mu=\Omega(d)
. This follows by conjugate duality and the argument in
[
18

,
or strong convexity of the feasible sets
[
20
]
.
Clearly,
these assumptions are not suitable for this work.

Maurey Sparsification

This is a classical technique in high-dimensional convex geometry
[
34
]
. The idea is based on sampling vertices with the barycentric coordinates weights and taking the empirical average, as nicely illustrated in the introduction of the book
[
39
]
.
This approximation is established for norms with concentration estimates for independent random sums, e.g., quantified by their Rademacher type
[
35
]
. This technique has been used systematically as a compression-type argument to obtain faster rates in statistical estimation and learning (e.g.,
[
4
,
46
]
). More recently,
[
1
]
proved a result similar to Lemma
2
, a functional approximation for randomized sparsification, in the context of model compression for distributed learning of generalized linear models.
They also proposed a sparsified mirror descent algorithm that works in the
\ell_{1}/\ell_{q}
setting where
q<\infty
, resorting on smoothness properties of the Bregman divergence. Furthemore, they only sparsify the outputs of each machine. We do not follow this approach. Instead, we sparsify every iterate of mirror descent.
Furthermore, we show that entropic SMD (using a nonsmooth Bregman divergence) with iterate sparsification works for convex and smooth losses, which to our knowledge is an entirely new result, and does not follow from direct ex-post model compression.

Debiasing

This is a technique proposed in the stochastic simulation literature
[
11
]
, to leverage biased estimators whose accuracy improves with larger sample size. The main difference between the works on simulation and our approach is the finite sample size, which limits the amount of bias-reduction. The work

[
2
]
studied bias-reduction in regularization for SCO.
In concurrent work
[
19
]
, a gradient debiasing procedure is developed for Euclidean DP-SCO. Although our results do not depend on their techniques, we borrow some inspiration from them. Note futher that most of our results do not use bias-reduction.

Concurrent and follow up work

In independent and concurrent work, the paper
[
47
]
investigates empirical and population worst-group risk minimization under DP, with a focus on group fairness and robust learning. While the convex-linear loss functions of their work correspond to a particular case of ours (convex-concave), their use of Euclidean geometry makes their results incomparable to ours. Furthermore, their sequential regularization approach is independent from ours. The follow up work
[
6

considers DP-SSPs in
\ell_{p}/\ell_{q}

Preliminaries

Given
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we denote the
1
-norm and
\infty
-norm by
\|x\|_{1}=\sum_{j\in[d]}|x_{j}|
and
\|x\|_{\infty}=\max_{j\in[d]}|x_{j}|
, respectively. A differentiable function
F:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is
L_{0}
-Lipschitz w.r.t.
\|\cdot\|_{1}
over a set
\mathcal{X}
if
\|\nabla F(x)\|_{\infty}\leq L_{0}
for all

. It is
L_{1}
-smooth w.r.t.
\|\cdot\|_{1}
over
\mathcal{X}
if
\|\nabla F(x^{1})-\nabla F(x^{2})\|_{\infty}\leq L_{1}\|x^{1}-x^{2}\|_{1}
for all

. We say a function is first-order-smooth if it is
L_{0}
-Lipschitz and
L_{1}
-smooth w.r.t.
\|\cdot\|_{1}
. We say a function is second-order-smooth if it is first-order-smooth and its partial derivatives are
L_{2}
-smooth w.r.t
\|\cdot\|_{1}
.
4
4

4

Note that this assumption is milder than the typical second-order-smoothness assumption in optimization, and it is satisfied by the widely used quadratic and logistic losses.
For
a,b:\mathbb{R}^{p}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
, we say

if there exists an absolute constant
C>0
such that for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
,
a(x)\leq Cb(x)
. When
a\lesssim b
and
b\lesssim a
, we write
a\eqsim b
. We denote by

the
i
-th canonical vector (the dimension being clear from context). The standard simplex in

is the set
\Delta_{d}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\|x\|_{1}=1,x_{j}\geq 0\text{ for all }j\in[d]\}
. A vector
x\in\Delta_{d}
induces a probability distribution over the canonical vectors, which we denote by
P_{x}
. Hence,
\hat{x}\sim P_{x}
means that
\hat{x}
takes the value
e_{i}

x_{i}
. Note that if
\hat{x}\sim P_{x}
, then
\mathbb{E}[\hat{x}]=x
. Given

\Phi(x,y)=\big(\nabla_{x}f(x,y),-\nabla_{y}f(x,y)\big)
be saddle operator (a.k.a. the

)
[
36
]
. We denote
\ell=\log(d_{x})+\log(d_{y})

DP Synthetic Data Generation

(3)

.

Maurey Sparsification with Functional Value Approximation

The classical Maurey Sparsification Lemma
[
34
]
is an application of the probabilistic method that leads to an approximate and (nearly) dimension-independent version of Carathéodory’s Theorem
[
15
]
. While this method is well-known to apply with smooth norms, it appears that for norms such as
\|\cdot\|_{1}
such approximations are out of reach.
5
5

5

In fact,
\|\cdot\|_{1}
is as far as being smooth as possible, in the sense of having trivial Rademacher type
[
35
]
. Furthermore, polynomial in
d
mean estimation lower bounds with respect to
\|\cdot\|_{1}
are known
[
40
]
.
We circumvent this limitation by directly working with function value approximations. These results will be crucial for the analysis of our algorithms in the forthcoming sections.

We start with a simple adaptation of Maurey’s method for function approximation in the general smooth case. A similar proof
appears in
[
1
]
. Throughout this section we denote
\bar{x}^{T}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{x^{t}}{T}
and
\bar{a}^{T}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{a^{t}}{T}
unless explicitly said otherwise.

Lemma 2
(Function value approximation in expectation).

Let
(\mathbf{E},\|\cdot\|)
be a normed space, and let
F
be a
L_{1}
-smooth function w.r.t.
\|\cdot\|
. Let
x^{1},...,x^{T}
be a collection of deterministic vectors,
(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{T})\in\Delta_{T}
, and
a^{1},...,a^{T}
be random vectors such that
\mathbb{E}[a^{t}|a^{t-1},..,a^{1}]=x^{t}
. Let
\bar{x}^{T}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\lambda_{t}x^{t}
and
\bar{a}^{T}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\lambda_{t}a^{t}
. Then
\big|\mathbb{E}\left[F\left(\bar{a}^{T}\right)-F\left(\bar{x}^{T}\right)\right]\big|\leq\frac{L_{1}}{2}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\lambda_{t}^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|a^{t}-x^{t}\right\|^{2}\right]
.

Proof.

Let
\alpha_{t}=\Big\langle\nabla F\Big(\sum_{k=t}^{T}\lambda_{k}x^{k}+\sum_{k=1}^{t-1}\lambda_{k}a^{k}\Big),\lambda_{t}(a^{t}-x^{t})\Big\rangle
. Noting that
F(\bar{a}^{T})=F(\bar{x}^{T}+\bar{a}^{T}-\bar{x}^{T})
, and using repeatedly the
L_{1}
-smoothness of
F
, we have

\displaystyle F(\bar{a}^{T})

\displaystyle\leq F(\bar{x}^{T}+\bar{a}^{T-1}-\bar{x}^{T-1})+\alpha_{T}+\frac{L_{1}\lambda_{T}^{2}\left\|a^{T}-x^{T}\right\|^{2}}{2}

\displaystyle\ldots

\displaystyle\leq F\left(\bar{x}^{T}\right)+\sum_{t\in[T]}\alpha_{t}+\frac{L_{1}}{2}\sum_{t\in[T]}\lambda_{t}^{2}\|a^{t}-x^{t}\|^{2}.

Clearly
(\alpha_{t})_{t\in[T]}
is a martingale difference sequence. Hence,
\mathbb{E}\big[\sum_{t\in[T]}\alpha_{t}\big]=0

and consequently
\mathbb{E}[F(\bar{a}^{T})-F(\bar{x}^{T})]\leq\frac{L_{1}}{2}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\lambda_{t}^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|a^{t}-x^{t}\right\|^{2}\right]
. Since
-F
is also
L_{1}
-smooth, the same idea yields
\mathbb{E}[F(\bar{x}^{T})-F(\bar{a}^{T})]\leq\frac{L_{1}}{2}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\lambda_{t}^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|a^{t}-x^{t}\right\|^{2}\right]
.

We now instantiate Lemma
2
for the space
(\mathbb{R}^{d},\|\cdot\|_{1})
with
\lambda_{i}=\frac{1}{T}
,
i\in[T]
. In later sections we will see that
x^{t}\in\Delta_{d}
represents the
t
-th iterate of our algorithm and
a^{t}\sim P_{x^{t}}
a private, unbiased estimator of
x^{t}
.

\|\cdot\|_{1}

Let
F:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}

L_{1}
-smooth function w.r.t.
\|\cdot\|_{1}
. Let

a^{1},...,a^{T}\in\Delta_{d}

for all
t\in[T]
. Then

\left|\mathbb{E}\left[F\left(\bar{a}^{T}\right)-F\left(\bar{x}^{T}\right)\right]\right|\leq\frac{2L_{1}}{T}
.

The above result bounds the bias incurred by evaluating gradients at sampled vertices. More concretely, the following corollaries hold.

Corollary 4
(Gradient bias with second-order-smoothness).

Suppose
F:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
has partial derivates that are
L_{2}
-smooth w.r.t.
\|\cdot\|_{1}
. Let
x^{1},...,x^{T}\in\Delta_{d}
be deterministic vectors and
a^{1},...,a^{T}\in\Delta_{d}
be random vectors such that
\mathbb{E}[a^{t}|a^{t-1},..,a^{1}]=x^{t}
for all
t\in[T]
.
Then
\left\|\mathbb{E}\left[\nabla F\left(\bar{a}^{T}\right)-\nabla F\left(\bar{x}^{T}\right)\right]\right\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{2L_{2}}{T}
.

Proof.

This follows directly from applying Corollary
3
to each partial derivate: for all
j\in[d]
, we have
\left|\mathbb{E}\left[\nabla_{j}F\left(\bar{a}^{T}\right)-\nabla_{j}F\left(\bar{x}^{T}\right)\right]\right|\leq\frac{2L_{2}}{T}
.

Corollary 5
(Gradient bias with first-order-smoothness).

Suppose
F:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is
L_{1}
-smooth w.r.t.
\|\cdot\|_{1}
.Let
x^{1},...,x^{T}
be a collection of deterministic vectors in
\Delta_{d}
and
a^{1},...,a^{T}\in\Delta_{d}
be random vectors such that
\mathbb{E}[a^{t}|a^{t-1},..,a^{1}]=x^{t}
for all
t\in[T]
.
Then
\left\|\mathbb{E}\left[\nabla F\left(\bar{a}^{T}\right)-\nabla F\left(\bar{x}^{T}\right)\right]\right\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{4L_{1}}{\sqrt{T}}
.

Proof.

Denote
\bar{x}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t\in[T]}x^{t},\bar{a}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t\in[T]}a^{t}
. By
L_{1}
-smoothness of
F
, we have for all
j\in[d],r\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
:
F(\bar{x}+re_{j})\leq F(\bar{x})+\langle\nabla F(\bar{x}),re_{j}\rangle+\frac{L_{1}r^{2}}{2}\|e_{j}\|_{1}^{2}
.
Re-arranging this expression gives
-\langle\nabla F(\bar{x}),e_{j}\rangle\leq\frac{F(\bar{x})-F(\bar{x}+re_{j})}{r}+\frac{L_{1}r}{2}
.
By
L_{1}
-smoothness of
-F
, we can obtain
\langle\nabla F(\bar{a}),e_{j}\rangle\leq\frac{F(\bar{a}+re_{j})-F(\bar{a})}{r}+\frac{L_{1}r}{2}
. Adding these inequalities and taking expectations we get

\mathbb{E}[\langle\nabla F(\bar{a})-\nabla F(\bar{x}),e_{j}\rangle]\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[F(\bar{a}+re_{j})-F(\bar{x}+re_{j})+F(\bar{x})-F(\bar{a})\right]}{r}+L_{1}r.

By applying Corollary
3
to
F(\cdot)
and
F(\cdot+re_{j})
we obtain

\mathbb{E}\left[F(\bar{a}+re_{j})-F(\bar{x}+re_{j})+F(\bar{x})-F(\bar{a})\right]\leq 4L_{1}/T.

Hence, for all
j\in[d]
,
\mathbb{E}[\nabla_{j}F(\bar{a})-\nabla_{j}F(\bar{x})]=\mathbb{E}[\langle\nabla F(\bar{a})-\nabla F(\bar{x}),e_{j}\rangle]\leq\frac{4L_{1}}{rT}+L_{1}r.
Analogously we can prove the same bound for
\mathbb{E}[\langle\nabla F(\bar{x})-\nabla F(\bar{a}),e_{j}\rangle]
. Finally,
choosing
r=2/\sqrt{T}
gives the result.

We will also need to bound the second-moment of the error incurred by evaluating gradients at sampled vertices. Towards this, we first observe that the in-expectation guarantees obtained in Lemma
2
can be turned into high-probability guarantees by
controlling the increments of the martingale difference sequence
(\alpha_{t})_{t\in[T]}

with the bounded differences inequality.

Lemma 6
(Function value approximation with high probability).

Let
(\mathbf{E},\|\cdot\|)
be a normed space and
F
be an
L_{0}
-Lipschitz,
L_{1}
-smooth function w.r.t.
\|\cdot\|
. Let
x^{1},...,x^{T}
be a collection of deterministic vectors,
(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{T})\in\Delta_{T}
and
a^{1},...,a^{T}
be random vectors such that
\mathbb{E}[a^{t}|a^{t-1},..,a^{1}]=x^{t}
. Suppose

\|x^{t}-a^{t}\|\leq D
a.s. for all
t\in[T]
. Define
\bar{x}^{T}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\lambda_{t}x^{t}
and
\bar{a}^{T}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\lambda_{t}a^{t}
. Then, for any
\beta>0

\mathbb{P}\left[F\left(\bar{a}^{T}\right)-F\left(\bar{x}^{T}\right)\geq\frac{L_{1}D^{2}}{2}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\lambda_{t}^{2}+\beta\sqrt{2}L_{0}D\sqrt{\sum_{t=1}^{T}\lambda_{t}^{2}}\,\right]\leq\exp(-\beta^{2}).

Proof.

Let
\alpha_{t}=\lambda_{t}\left\langle\nabla F\left(\sum_{k=t}^{T}\lambda_{k}x^{k}+\sum_{k=1}^{t-1}\lambda_{k}a^{k}\right),a^{t}-x^{t}\right\rangle
as in the proof of Lemma
2
. From that proof we have that
F(\bar{a}^{T})-F\left(\bar{x}^{T}\right)\leq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\alpha_{t}+\frac{L_{1}D^{2}}{2}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\lambda_{t}^{2}
. Hence

\mathbb{P}[F(\bar{a}^{T})-F\left(\bar{x}^{T}\right)\geq\alpha]\leq\mathbb{P}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\alpha_{t}+\frac{L_{1}D^{2}}{2}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\lambda_{t}^{2}\geq\alpha\right]=\mathbb{P}\left[\sum_{t\in[T]}\alpha_{t}\geq\alpha^{\prime}\right],

(4)

where
\alpha=\frac{L_{1}D^{2}}{2}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\lambda_{t}^{2}+\alpha^{\prime}
. Next, we bound the probability on the right-hand side. Note that
(\alpha_{t})_{t\in[T]}
is a martingale difference sequence
and
|\alpha_{i}|\leq L_{0}D\lambda_{i}
, so the bounded differences inequality gives
\mathbb{P}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\alpha_{t}\geq\beta\sqrt{2}L_{0}D\sqrt{\sum_{t=1}^{T}\lambda_{t}^{2}}\right]\leq\exp(-\beta^{2})
, for any
\beta>0
. Making
\alpha^{\prime}=\beta\sqrt{2}L_{0}D\sqrt{\sum_{t=1}^{T}\lambda_{t}^{2}}
and replacing back into Equation
4
, we conclude the claimed result.

We now use Lemma
6
to give bounds on the expected second-moment error, with respect to
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
.

Lemma 7
(Second moment of maximum expected error).

Let
(\mathbf{E},\|\cdot\|)
be a normed space and
F_{1},...,F_{M}
a collection of
L_{0}
-Lipschitz and
L_{1}
-smooth functions w.r.t
\|\cdot\|
. Let
x^{1},\ldots,x^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{T})\in\Delta_{T}
and
a^{1},...,a^{T}
be a collection of random vectors such that
\mathbb{E}[a^{t}|a^{t-1},\ldots,a^{1}]=x^{t}
and
\|a^{t}-x^{t}\|\leq D
a.s. for all
t\in[T]
. Let
\bar{a}^{T}=\sum_{t\in[T]}\lambda_{t}a^{t}
and
\bar{x}^{T}=\sum_{t\in[T]}\lambda_{t}x^{t}
. Then

\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{j\in[M]}|F_{j}(\bar{a}^{T})-F_{j}(\bar{x}^{T})|^{2}\right]\leq\frac{L_{1}^{2}D^{4}}{2}\left(\sum_{t=1}^{T}\lambda_{t}^{2}\right)^{2}+2L_{0}^{2}D^{2}(4+\log(M))\sum_{t=1}^{T}\lambda_{t}^{2}.

Proof.

Define the constants
A=\frac{L_{1}D^{2}}{2}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\lambda_{t}^{2}
and
C=\sqrt{2}L_{0}D\sqrt{\sum_{t=1}^{T}\lambda_{t}^{2}}
. We will introduce a quantity,
B
, in the following computation, and later optimize over the choice of this
B
. We proceed as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[\max_{j\in[M]}|F_{j}(\bar{a}^{T})-F_{j}(\bar{x}^{T})|^{2}

\displaystyle\bigg]=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left[\max_{j\in[M]}|F_{j}(\bar{a}^{T})-F_{j}(\bar{x}^{T})|\geq\beta\right]\beta d\beta

\displaystyle\hskip-65.44142pt\leq\int_{0}^{A+B}\beta d\beta+\sum_{j\in[M]}\int_{A+B}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left[|F_{j}(\bar{a}^{T})-F_{j}(\bar{x}^{T})|\geq\beta\right]\beta d\beta

\displaystyle\hskip-65.44142pt=\frac{(A+B)^{2}}{2}+\sum_{j\in[M]}\int_{B/C}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left[|F_{j}(\bar{x})-F_{j}(\bar{a}^{T})|\geq A+C\beta\right](A+C\beta)Cd\beta

\displaystyle\hskip-65.44142pt\leq\frac{(A+B)^{2}}{2}+2MC\left[C\int_{B/C}^{\infty}\exp\left(-\beta^{2}\right)\beta d\beta+A\int_{B/C}^{\infty}\exp\left(-\beta^{2}\right)d\beta\right]

\displaystyle\hskip-65.44142pt=\frac{(A+B)^{2}}{2}+2MC\left[C\exp\left(-(B/C)^{2}\right)+A\sqrt{\pi}\mathbb{P}[X\geq B/C]\right],

where the first inequality comes from the union bound, the second from Lemma
6
, and in the last line, we use density of a normal random variable,
X\sim{\cal N}(0,1/2)
. From Mill’s inequality, we have
\mathbb{P}[X\geq B/C]\leq\frac{C}{B\sqrt{\pi}}\exp\left(-(B/C)^{2})\right)
. Hence,

\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{j\in[M]}|F_{j}(\bar{a}^{T})-F_{j}(\bar{x}^{T})|^{2}\right]\leq\frac{(A+B)^{2}}{2}+2MC(C+A)\exp\left(-(B/C)^{2}\right).

Finally, setting
B=C\sqrt{\log(M)}
if
M>1
and
B=C
if
M=1
gives

\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{j\in[M]}|F_{j}(\bar{a}^{T})-F_{j}(\bar{x}^{T})|^{2}\right]\leq 2A^{2}+(4+\log(M))C^{2}.

We conclude this section by presenting two corollaries of Lemma

.

Corollary 8
(Gradient approximation error with second-order-smoothness).

Let
F:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be a
L_{1}
-smooth function w.r.t.
\|\cdot\|_{1}
with
L_{2}
-smooth partial derivatives. Let

be a collection of deterministic vectors and

\mathbb{E}\left\|\nabla F\left(\bar{a}^{T}\right)-\nabla F\left(\bar{x}^{T}\right)\right\|^{2}_{\infty}\leq\frac{8L_{2}^{2}}{T^{2}}+\frac{8L_{1}^{2}(4+\log(d))}{T}.

Proof.

Apply Lemma
7
with
F_{j}=\nabla_{j}F
,
j\in[d]
.

Corollary 9
(Gradient approximation error with first-order-smoothness).

Let
F:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be a function that is
L_{0}
-Lipschitz and
L_{1}
-smooth w.r.t.
\|\cdot\|_{1}
. Let
x^{1},...,x^{T}\in\Delta_{d}
be a collection of deterministic vectors and
a^{1},...,a^{T}\in\Delta_{d}
be random vectors such that
\mathbb{E}[a^{t}|a^{t-1},..,a^{1}]=x^{t}
for every

. Then

\mathbb{E}\left\|\nabla F\left(\bar{a}^{T}\right)-\nabla F\left(\bar{x}^{T}\right)\right\|^{2}_{\infty}\leq\frac{8\sqrt{2}L_{1}^{2}}{T^{3/2}\sqrt{4+\log(d)}}+\frac{8\sqrt{2}(L_{0}^{2}+L_{1}^{2})\sqrt{(4+\log(d))}}{\sqrt{T}}.

Proof.

Similarly to the proof of Corollary
5
, using
L_{1}
-smoothness of

,

\displaystyle\langle\nabla F(\bar{a})-\nabla F(\bar{x}),e_{j}\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\frac{F(\bar{a}+re_{j})-F(\bar{x}+re_{j})+F(\bar{x})-F(\bar{a})}{r}+L_{1}r

\displaystyle\leq\frac{|F(\bar{a}+re_{j})-F(\bar{x}+re_{j})+F(\bar{x})-F(\bar{a})|}{r}+L_{1}r.

Since
-F
is also
L_{1}
-smooth, then

\langle\nabla F(\bar{x})-\nabla F(\bar{a}),e_{j}\rangle\leq\frac{|F(\bar{a}+re_{j})-F(\bar{x}+re_{j})+F(\bar{x})-F(\bar{a})|}{r}+L_{1}r.

We conclude that

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla F\left(\bar{a}\right)-\nabla F\left(\bar{x}\right)\right\|^{2}_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\max_{j\in[d]}\left|\nabla_{j}F\left(\bar{a}\right)-\nabla_{j}F\left(\bar{x}\right)\right|^{2}=\max_{j\in[d]}\left|\langle\nabla F(\bar{a})-\nabla F(\bar{x}),e_{j}\rangle\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\hskip-28.45274pt\leq 2\max_{j\in[d]}\frac{|F(\bar{a}+re_{j})-F(\bar{x}+re_{j})+F(\bar{x})-F(\bar{a})|^{2}}{r^{2}}+L_{1}^{2}r^{2}.

By Lemma

\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{j\in[d]}\frac{|F(\bar{a}+re_{j})-F(\bar{x}+re_{j})+F(\bar{x})-F(\bar{a})|^{2}}{r^{2}}\right]\leq\frac{32L_{1}^{2}}{T^{2}r^{2}}+\frac{32L_{0}^{2}(4+\log(d))}{Tr^{2}}.

Putting everything together and taking expectations

\mathbb{E}[\left\|\nabla F\left(\bar{a}\right)-\nabla F\left(\bar{x}\right)\right\|^{2}_{\infty}]\leq\frac{64L_{1}^{2}}{T^{2}r^{2}}+\frac{64L_{0}^{2}(4+\log(d))}{Tr^{2}}+2L_{1}^{2}r^{2}.

Choosing

3.2
A constant probability duality gap bound via Private Stochastic Mirror Descent with Bias-Reduced Sampling from Vertices

The limitation that prevents Algorithm
1
from attaining optimal rates is the bias of the stochastic gradient oracles. This is a consequence of sampling from vertices, and can only be mitigated by increasing the sampling rate. However, this eats up the privacy budget. In this section, we propose circumventing this limitation by combining our gradient oracles with a bias-reduction method; see Algorithm
3

3.3
A high probability duality gap bound via Private Stochastic Mirror Descent with Bias-Reduced Sampling from Vertices

In this section, we will prove that Theorem
19
can be turned into a high probability result. This is achieved with Algorithm
4
. The key idea is as follows. It is easy to see that by running Algorithm
3
multiple times on independent datasets of size of order
O(n/\log(1/\beta))
(for any

), we can ensure that with probability at least
1-\beta
, one of the output pairs has a duality gap of the same order as the gap obtained in Theorem
19
, up to
\operatorname{polylog}(1/\beta)
factors. The problem is how to find the pair with small duality gap among all the candidate pairs. The first idea would be to run a DP selection procedure, such as the exponential mechanism. This does not work directly, since the duality gap is not computable. Hence, in order to run the exponential mechanism, we need to create a score function that (1) resembles the duality gap and (2) is computable from samples and we can control its sensitivity. For each candidate pair, we propose a two step procedure to approximate the duality gap while satisfying the conditions (1) and (2). For a fixed pair
x,y
, we first use the fact that the duality gap is defined as a sum of maxima of concave functions, so we can use the Private Variance Reduced Frank-Wolfe from
[
3
]
to find an approximate maximizer, say
(\tilde{x},\tilde{y})
. The second step is to approximate the stochastic objective by samples. This way, we make two approximations:
\operatorname{Gap}(x,y)\approx F_{\mathcal{D}}(\tilde{x},y)-F_{\mathcal{D}}(x,\tilde{y})\approx F_{\tilde{S}}(\tilde{x},y)-F_{\tilde{S}}(x,\tilde{y})
, where
F_{\tilde{S}}(x,y)=\frac{1}{|{\tilde{S}}|}\sum_{z\in{\tilde{S}}}f(x,y;z)
and
{\tilde{S}}
is independent of
x,y,\tilde{x},\tilde{y}
. The error in the first approximation can be controlled with the convergence guarantees of Private Variance Reduced Frank-Wolfe while the second can be controlled by Hoeffding’s Inequality under the assumption that
f
is bounded. Furthermore,
F_{\tilde{S}}(\tilde{x},y)-F_{\tilde{S}}(x,\tilde{y})
is computable from samples. Now that we have approximated the duality gap by a computable quantity, we can run the exponential mechanism with this quantity as score function, which will select a candidate pair that approximately has the smallest duality gap among all the candidates. A detailed procedure can be found in Algorithm

Lemma 26
(Anytime online-to-batch
[
16
,
45
]
).

Let
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}
be a convex set. For any sequence
\beta_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{+},g^{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}
, suppose an online learner predicts
x^{t}
and receives
t
-th loss
\ell_{t}(x)=\langle g^{t},x\rangle
. Define
w^{t}=\sum_{i\in[t]}\frac{\beta_{i}x^{i}}{\sum_{j\in[t]}\beta_{j}}
and
\operatorname{Regret}_{T}(x)=\sum_{t\in[T]}\langle g_{t},x_{t}-x\rangle
. Then, for any convex, differentiable
F
over
\mathcal{X}
and any

F(w^{T})-F(x)\leq\frac{1}{\sum_{j\in[T]}\beta_{j}}\bigg(\operatorname{Regret}_{T}(x)+\sum_{t\in[T]}\langle\beta_{t}\nabla F(w^{t})-g_{t},x_{t}-x\rangle\bigg).

An advantage of this conversion is that the weighted averages

change slowly from one iteration to the next, which is very useful in the smooth case
[
45
]
. Using Lemma
26
with weights

to optimize over
\Delta_{x}
, the average iterates satisfy
\|w^{t}-w^{t-1}\|_{1}=\|x^{t}-w^{t-1}\|_{1}/t\leq 2/t
.
Consequently,
P_{w^{t-1}}
and
P_{w^{t}}
are progressively closer distributions.
We exploit this idea in Algorithm
5
by maintaining estimates of the private iterates for a large number of steps,
aggressively
reducing the number of sampled vertices and improving the privacy.
Theorem
27

provides a faster rate compared to the one we would get with the plain online-to-batch conversion.

4.1
Privacy Analysis of Algorithm
5

Now we make explicit the advantage of using the anytime online-to-batch approach in terms of privacy. Namely, due to the substantial sampling reduction, our privacy budget degrades much more nicely.

Proposition 28
.

Let
0<\delta<1
,
0<\varepsilon<8\log(1/\delta)
. Then, Algorithm
5
is
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP if
\tau\leq\frac{B\varepsilon}{8L_{0}\sqrt{2(TK/q+qK)\log(1/\delta)}}
.

Proof.

Note that
w^{t}=\frac{1}{t}\sum_{i\in[t]}x^{i}
. Hence, for any
j\in[d_{x}]

w^{t}_{j}=\frac{1}{t}\sum_{i\in[t]}x^{i}_{j}\propto\sum_{i\in[t]}\exp\left(-\tau\sum_{k\in[i-1]}g^{k}_{j}\right)=\exp\left(\log\left(\sum_{i\in[t]}\exp\left(-\tau\sum_{k\in[i]}g^{k}_{j}\right)\right)\right).

Denote by
G_{j}(S)=\big(\sum_{k\in[i-1]}g^{k}_{j}\big)_{i\in[t]}
the vector of the cumulative sum of the gradients that our algorithm produces when it has been fed with dataset
S
. Also, define the log-sum-exp function as
\operatorname{LSE}((x_{1},...,x_{k}))=\log(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\exp\{x_{i}\})
. With this new notation,
w^{t}_{j}\propto\exp(\operatorname{LSE}(-\tau G_{j}(S)))
. Two well-known properties of the
\operatorname{LSE}(x)
function are that it is convex in its argument and its gradient is the softmax function of
x
. As a consequence of the second property mentioned, it is clear that
\|\nabla\operatorname{LSE}(x)\|_{1}=1
for every
x
. By convexity of
\operatorname{LSE}
, we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{LSE}(-\tau G_{j}(S))

\displaystyle-\operatorname{LSE}(-\tau G_{j}(S^{\prime}))\leq\langle\nabla\operatorname{LSE}(-\tau G_{j}(S)),\tau G_{j}(S^{\prime})-\tau G_{j}(S)\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\tau\|\nabla\operatorname{LSE}(-\tau G(S))\|_{1}\|G_{j}(S)-G_{j}(S^{\prime})\|_{\infty}=\tau\|G_{j}(S)-G_{j}(S^{\prime})\|_{\infty}.

Furthermore,

\max_{j\in[d_{x}]}\tau\|G_{j}(S)-G_{j}(S^{\prime})\|_{\infty}=\tau\max_{j\in[d_{x}],i\in[t]}\left|\sum_{k\in[i-1]}(g^{k}_{j}-g^{\prime k}_{j})\right|\leq\frac{2\tau L_{0}}{B}
by
L_{0}
-Lipschitzness of
f
. Hence sampling vertices from
\Delta_{x}
according to the probabilities induced by
w^{t}
is equivalent to running the exponential mechanism with score function
s(S,j)=\operatorname{LSE}(-\tau G_{j}(S))
, whose sensitivity is bounded by
\frac{2\tau L_{0}}{B}
. Since we sample
K
times during the first
q
iterations, then every
q
iterations and then
K
times to sparsify the last iterate, the overall number of samplings will be
TK/q+qK+K
. Since each of them is
\frac{4\tau L_{0}}{B}
-DP, then by Advanced Composition of DP (Corollary
15
) we only need that

\frac{4\tau L_{0}}{B}\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2\sqrt{2(TK/q+(q+1)K)\log(1/\delta)}}
.

Proof of Theorem

.

Let

be a minimizer of

. From Lemma

The standard analysis of mirror descent yields,
\sum_{t\in[T]}\langle g_{t},x_{t}-x\rangle\leq\frac{\log(d_{x})}{\tau}+\frac{\tau}{2}\sum_{t\in[T]}\|g^{t}\|_{\infty}^{2}

[
31
]

Using that

and taking expectations, we obtain

\mathbb{E}[F_{\mathcal{D}}(w^{T})-F_{\mathcal{D}}(x)]\leq\frac{\log(d_{x})}{\tau T}+\frac{\tau L_{0}^{2}}{2}+\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t\in[T]}\mathbb{E}\left[\langle\nabla F_{\mathcal{D}}(w^{t})-g^{t},x^{t}-x\rangle\right].

Let us bound
\mathbb{E}\left[\langle\nabla F_{\mathcal{D}}(w^{t})-g^{t},x^{t}-x\rangle\right]
. For
t\leq q
, we have
g^{t}\!=\!\nabla f(\hat{w}^{t};B^{t})

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\langle\nabla F_{\mathcal{D}}(w^{t})-\nabla f(\hat{w}^{t};B^{t}),x^{t}-x\rangle]=\mathbb{E}[\langle\nabla f(w^{t};B^{t})-\nabla f(\hat{w}^{t};B^{t}),x^{t}-x\rangle]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x^{t}}[\langle\mathbb{E}_{\hat{w}^{t}}[\nabla f(w^{t};B^{t})-\nabla f(\hat{w}^{t};B^{t})\mid x^{t}],x^{t}-x\rangle]

\displaystyle\leq 2\|\mathbb{E}_{\hat{w}^{t}}[\nabla f(w^{t};B^{t})-\nabla f(\hat{w}^{t};B^{t})]\|_{\infty}\leq\begin{cases}\frac{8L_{1}}{\sqrt{K}}&\text{for first-order-smooth $f(\cdot;z)$}\\
\frac{4L_{2}}{K}&\text{for second-order-smooth $f(\cdot;z)$.}\end{cases}

where in the first equality we used that
\nabla f(w^{t};B^{t})
is an unbiased estimator of
\nabla F_{\mathcal{D}}(w^{t})
and for the second inequality we used Corollaries
5
and
4
.
Now consider the case
t>q
. First, denote by
t_{0}=q\lfloor t/q\rfloor
. That is,
t_{0}
was the last time that
\hat{w}
was updated by the algorithm before the
t
-th iteration. Hence,
g^{t}=\nabla f(\hat{w}^{t_{0}};B^{t})
. Now, we decompose the error into three terms and bound each of them individually:

\displaystyle\langle\nabla F_{\mathcal{D}}(w^{t})-\nabla f(\hat{w}^{t_{0}};B^{t}),x^{t}-x\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\nabla F_{\mathcal{D}}(w^{t})-\nabla f(w^{t};B^{t}),x^{t}-x\rangle

\displaystyle+\langle\nabla f(w^{t};B^{t})-\nabla f(w^{t_{0}};B^{t}),x^{t}-x\rangle

\displaystyle+\langle\nabla f(w^{t_{0}};B^{t})-\nabla f(\hat{w}^{t_{0}};B^{t}),x^{t}-x\rangle.

Clearly
\mathbb{E}[\langle\nabla F_{\mathcal{D}}(w^{t})-\nabla f(w^{t};B^{t}),x^{t}-x\rangle]=0
, since
\nabla f(w^{t};B^{t})
is an unbiased estimator of
\nabla F_{\mathcal{D}}(w^{t})
conditionally on the randomness up to iteration

\displaystyle\langle\nabla f(w^{t};B^{t})-\nabla f(w^{t_{0}};B^{t}),x^{t}-x\rangle\leq\|\nabla f(w^{t};B^{t})-\nabla f(w^{t_{0}};B^{t})\|_{\infty}\|x^{t}-x\|_{1}

where the first inequality follows from Hölder, the second from the

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\hat{w}^{t_{0}}}[\langle\widetilde{\Delta}^{t_{0}},\tilde{x}^{t}-x\rangle]=\langle\mathbb{E}_{\hat{w}^{t_{0}}}[\nabla f(w^{t_{0}};B^{t})-\nabla f(\hat{w}^{t_{0}};B^{t})],\tilde{x}^{t}-x\rangle

\frac{4L_{2}}{K}&\text{for second-order-smooth $f(\cdot;z)$,}\end{cases}

\displaystyle(x^{t}-\tilde{x}^{t})_{j}

\displaystyle=\frac{x^{t_{0}}_{j}\exp(\hat{G}_{j})}{\sum_{i\in[d_{x}]}x^{t_{0}}_{i}\exp(\hat{G}_{i})}-\frac{x^{t_{0}}_{j}\exp\left(G_{j}\right)}{\sum_{i\in[d_{x}]}x^{t_{0}}_{i}\exp\left(G_{i}\right)}

A_{2}

\displaystyle A_{2}=\langle\widetilde{\Delta}^{t_{0}},x^{t}-\tilde{x}^{t}\rangle=\langle\nabla f(w^{t_{0}};B^{t})-\nabla f(\hat{w}^{t_{0}};B^{t}),x^{t}-\tilde{x}^{t}\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\|\nabla f(w^{t_{0}};B^{t})-\nabla f(\hat{w}^{t_{0}};B^{t})\|_{\infty}\|x^{t}-\tilde{x}^{t}\|_{1}

\displaystyle\textstyle\leq 4\tau\sum_{k=t_{0}}^{t-1}\|\nabla f(w^{t_{0}};B^{t})-\nabla f(\hat{w}^{t_{0}};B^{t})\|_{\infty}\|\nabla f(\hat{w}^{t_{0}};B^{k})-\nabla f(w^{t_{0}};B^{k})\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\textstyle\leq 2\tau\sum_{k=t_{0}}^{t-1}\Big(\|\nabla f(w^{t_{0}};B^{t})-\nabla f(\hat{w}^{t_{0}};B^{t})\|_{\infty}^{2}+\|\nabla f(\hat{w}^{t_{0}};B^{k})-\nabla f(w^{t_{0}};B^{k})\|_{\infty}^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle\textstyle\leq 2\tau q\|\nabla f(w^{t_{0}};B^{t})-\nabla f(\hat{w}^{t_{0}};B^{t})\|_{\infty}^{2}+2\tau\sum_{k=t_{0}}^{t-1}\|\nabla f(\hat{w}^{t_{0}};B^{k})-\nabla f(w^{t_{0}};B^{k})\|_{\infty}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\begin{cases}q\tau\big[\frac{L_{1}^{2}}{\sqrt{\log(d_{x})}K^{3/2}}+\frac{(L_{0}^{2}+L_{1}^{2})\sqrt{\log(d_{x})}}{\sqrt{K}}\big]&\text{for first-order-smooth $f(\cdot;z)$}\\
q\tau\big[\frac{L_{2}^{2}}{K^{2}}+\frac{L_{1}^{2}\log(d_{x})}{K}\big]&\text{for second-order-smooth $f(\cdot;z)$,}\end{cases}

where we bounded the second moment of the estimation errors with the results in Corollaries
8
and
9
.
We conclude that for all
t>q

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\langle\nabla F_{\mathcal{D}}(w^{t})-\nabla f(\hat{w}^{t_{0}};B^{t}),x^{t}-x\rangle]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\big[\langle\nabla F_{\mathcal{D}}(w^{t})-\nabla f(w^{t};B^{t}),x^{t}-x\rangle\big]+\mathbb{E}\big[\langle\nabla f(w^{t};B^{t})-\nabla f(w^{t_{0}};B^{t}),x^{t}-x\rangle\big]

\displaystyle\quad+\mathbb{E}\big[\langle\nabla f(w^{t_{0}};B^{t})-\nabla f(\hat{w}^{t_{0}};B^{t}),x^{t}-x\rangle\big]

\displaystyle\leq 0+\frac{4L_{1}q}{q\lfloor t/q\rfloor+1}+2\|\mathbb{E}_{\hat{w}^{t_{0}}}[\nabla f(w^{t_{0}};B^{t})-\nabla f(\hat{w}^{t_{0}};B^{t})]\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\,+2\tau q\|\nabla f(w^{t_{0}};B^{t})-\nabla f(\hat{w}^{t_{0}};B^{t})\|_{\infty}^{2}+2\tau\sum_{k=t_{0}}^{t-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\nabla f(\hat{w}^{t_{0}};B^{k})-\nabla f(w^{t_{0}};B^{k})\|_{\infty}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim\begin{cases}\frac{L_{1}}{\sqrt{K}}+q\tau\big[\frac{L_{1}^{2}}{\sqrt{\log(d_{x})}K^{3/2}}+\frac{(L_{0}^{2}+L_{1}^{2})\sqrt{\log(d_{x})}}{\sqrt{K}}\big]&\text{for first-order-smooth $f(\cdot;z)$}\\
\frac{L_{2}}{K}+q\tau\big[\frac{L_{2}^{2}}{K^{2}}+\frac{L_{1}^{2}\log(d_{x})}{K}\big]&\text{for second-order-smooth $f(\cdot;z)$.}\end{cases}

And recall that for
t\leq q
,

\mathbb{E}[\langle\nabla F_{\mathcal{D}}(w^{t})-\nabla f(\hat{w}^{t};B^{t}),x^{t}-x\rangle]\leq\begin{cases}\frac{8L_{1}}{\sqrt{K}}&\text{for first-order-smooth $f(\cdot;z)$}\\
\frac{4L_{2}}{K}&\text{for second-order-smooth $f(\cdot;z)$.}\end{cases}

Finally, we plug this bounds into our excess risk bound. We start considering the case of second-order-smooth functions. We bound
\mathbb{E}[F_{\mathcal{D}}(w^{T})-F_{\mathcal{D}}(x)]
by

\displaystyle\frac{\log(d_{x})}{\tau T}+\frac{\tau L_{0}^{2}}{2}+\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t\in[T]}\mathbb{E}\left[\langle\nabla F_{\mathcal{D}}(w^{t})-g^{t},x^{t}-x\rangle\right]

\displaystyle\textstyle\lesssim\frac{\log(d_{x})}{\tau T}+\tau L_{0}^{2}+\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t\in[T]}\Big(\frac{L_{2}}{K}+\mathbbm{1}_{(t>q)}\Big[\frac{4L_{1}q}{q\lfloor t/q\rfloor+1}+q\frac{L_{2}^{2}\tau}{K^{2}}+q\frac{L_{1}^{2}\log(d_{x})\tau}{K}\Big]\Big)

\displaystyle\textstyle\lesssim\frac{\log(d_{x})}{\tau T}+\tau\Big[L_{0}^{2}+\frac{L_{1}^{2}q\log(d_{x})}{K}+\frac{L_{2}^{2}q}{K^{2}}\big]+\frac{L_{2}}{K}+\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=q+1}^{T}\frac{L_{1}q}{q\lfloor t/q\rfloor+1}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{\log(d_{x})}{\tau T}+\tau\Big[L_{0}^{2}+\frac{L_{1}^{2}q\log(d_{x})}{K}+\frac{L_{2}^{2}q}{K^{2}}\big]+\frac{L_{2}}{K}+\frac{L_{1}q\log(T/q)}{T},

(11)

where the last line follows from
\sum_{t=q+1}^{T}\left[\frac{1}{q\lfloor t/q\rfloor+1}\right]\leq q\left(\frac{1}{q}+\frac{1}{2q}+...+\frac{1}{(T/q)q}\right)\lesssim\log\big(\frac{T}{q}\big)
.
Now, by Theorem
28
, and recalling that the batch size is
B=n/T
, we have a constraint on
\tau>0
in order to satisfy privacy. We also have the constraint of
\psi\leq 1
, which holds true only when
\tau\leq 1/(8qL_{0})
. Optimizing (
11
) under these constraints, we have that
we should choose
\tau
of order

\displaystyle\min\bigg\{

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{\log(d_{x})}{(L_{0}^{2}+L_{1}^{2}q\log(d_{x})/K+L_{2}^{2}q/K^{2})T}},\frac{1}{L_{0}q},\frac{n\varepsilon}{TL_{0}\sqrt{(TK/q+qK)\log(1/\delta)}}\bigg\}.

With this choice we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[F_{\mathcal{D}}(w^{T})-F_{\mathcal{D}}(x)]

\displaystyle\lesssim\sqrt{\frac{(L_{0}^{2}+L_{1}^{2}q\log(d_{x})/K+L_{2}^{2}q/K^{2})\log(d_{x})}{T}}+\frac{L_{0}q\log(d_{x})}{T}

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{L_{0}\sqrt{(TK/q+qK)\log(1/\delta)}\log(d_{x})}{n\varepsilon}+\frac{L_{2}}{K}+\frac{L_{1}q\log(T/q)}{T}.

Note that the sparsification error is, by Corollary
3
,
\mathbb{E}[F_{\mathcal{D}}(\hat{w}^{T})-F_{\mathcal{D}}(w^{T})]\lesssim\frac{L_{1}}{K}
. Combining this with the previous inequality and setting
q=\sqrt{T/\log(d_{x})},K=T/q=\sqrt{T\log(d_{x})}
, leads to

\textstyle\mathbb{E}[F_{\mathcal{D}}(\hat{w}^{T})-F_{\mathcal{D}}(x)]\lesssim(L_{0}+L_{1})\sqrt{\frac{\log(d_{x})}{T}}\\
\textstyle+\frac{L_{2}}{T^{3/4}\log(d_{x})^{1/4}}+\frac{L_{1}+L_{2}}{\sqrt{T\log(d_{x})}}+\frac{L_{1}\log(\sqrt{n\log(d_{x})})}{\sqrt{T\log(d_{x})}}+\frac{L_{0}\sqrt{T\log(1/\delta)}\log^{3/2}(d_{x})}{n\varepsilon}\\
\textstyle\lesssim(L_{0}+L_{1})\sqrt{\frac{\log(d_{x})}{T}}+\frac{L_{2}}{\sqrt{T}\log(d_{x})^{1/4}}+\frac{L_{1}\log(\sqrt{n\log(d_{x})})}{\sqrt{T\log(d_{x})}}+\frac{L_{0}\sqrt{T\log(1/\delta)}\log^{3/2}(d_{x})}{n\varepsilon}.

Hence letting
T=\min\Big\{n,\frac{n\varepsilon}{\log(d_{x})\sqrt{\log(1/\delta)}}\Big\}
gives the claimed rate for second-order-smooth functions. Now, we look at the case where the partial derivates are not necessarily smooth. Now, we can bound
\mathbb{E}[F_{\mathcal{D}}(w^{T})-F_{\mathcal{D}}(x)]
by

\displaystyle\frac{\log(d_{x})}{\tau T}+\frac{\tau L_{0}^{2}}{2}+\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t\in[T]}\mathbb{E}\left[\langle\nabla F_{\mathcal{D}}(w^{t})-g^{t},x^{t}-x\rangle\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{\log(d_{x})}{\tau T}+\tau L_{0}^{2}+\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t\in[T]}\bigg(\frac{L_{1}}{\sqrt{K}}+\mathbbm{1}_{(t>q)}\bigg[\frac{4L_{1}q}{q\lfloor t/q\rfloor+1}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+\frac{L_{1}^{2}q\tau}{\sqrt{\log(d_{x})}K^{3/2}}+\frac{(L_{0}^{2}+L_{1}^{2})\sqrt{\log(d_{x})}q\tau}{\sqrt{K}}\bigg]\bigg)

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{\log(d_{x})}{\tau T}+\tau\Big[L_{0}^{2}+\frac{L_{1}^{2}q}{\sqrt{\log(d_{x})}K^{3/2}}+\frac{(L_{0}^{2}+L_{1}^{2})\sqrt{\log(d_{x})}q}{\sqrt{K}}\Big]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\frac{L_{1}}{\sqrt{K}}+\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=q+1}^{T}\left[\frac{L_{1}q}{q\lfloor t/q\rfloor+1}\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{\log(d_{x})}{\tau T}+\tau\Big[L_{0}^{2}+\frac{L_{1}^{2}q}{\sqrt{\log(d_{x})}K^{3/2}}+\frac{(L_{0}^{2}+L_{1}^{2})\sqrt{\log(d_{x})}q}{\sqrt{K}}\Big]+\frac{L_{1}}{\sqrt{K}}+\frac{L_{1}q\log(T/q)}{T}.

By Corollary
3
, the sparsification error is
\mathbb{E}[F_{\mathcal{D}}(\hat{w}^{T})-F_{\mathcal{D}}(w^{T})]\lesssim\frac{L_{1}}{K}
, which implies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[F_{\mathcal{D}}(\hat{w}^{T})-F_{\mathcal{D}}(x)]\lesssim

\displaystyle\frac{\log(d_{x})}{\tau T}+\tau\Big[L_{0}^{2}+\frac{L_{1}^{2}q}{\sqrt{\log(d_{x})}K^{3/2}}+\frac{(L_{0}^{2}+L_{1}^{2})\sqrt{\log(d_{x})}q}{\sqrt{K}}\Big]

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{L_{1}}{\sqrt{K}}+\frac{L_{1}q\log(T/q)}{T}.

Setting
T=\min\left\{n,\frac{(n\varepsilon)^{4/5}}{(\log(d_{x})\log(1/\delta))^{2/5}}\right\}
,
q=\sqrt{T}/\log(d_{x})
,
K=T/\log(d_{x})
and

\tau\!\eqsim\!\!\min\!\left\{\sqrt{\frac{\log(d_{x})}{(L_{0}^{2}+(L_{0}^{2}+L_{1}^{2})q\sqrt{\log(d_{x})/K}+L_{1}^{2}q/[\sqrt{\log(d_{x})}K^{3/2}])T}},\frac{1}{L_{0}q},\frac{n\varepsilon}{TL_{0}\sqrt{(TK/q+qK)\log(1/\delta)}}\right\}
leads to the convergence rate claimed for first-order-smooth functions.

5
Conclusion and Future Work

We presented mirror descent based algorithms for DP-SSP in the polyhedral setting.
Our algorithms are the first achieving polylogarithmic-in-the-dimension error beyond bilinear objectives. In fact, for
second-order-smooth objectives our rates are optimal up to logarithmic factors.
An important question remains: Are the optimal rates achievable in expectation for first-order-smooth
functions? We leave this question for future work. We also apply our ideas to DP-SCO in the polyhedral setting, where we also leave open the problem of achieving optimal rates with mirror descent based algorithms.
