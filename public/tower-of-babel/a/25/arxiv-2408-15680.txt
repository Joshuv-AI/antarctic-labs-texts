Abstract

In recent years, the study of biological transportation networks has attracted significant interest, focusing on their self-regulating, demand-driven nature. This paper examines a mathematical model for these networks, featuring nonlinear elliptic equations for pressure and an auxiliary variable, and a reaction-diffusion parabolic equation for the conductivity tensor, introduced in
[
19
]
. The model, based on an energy functional with diffusive and metabolic terms, allows for various entropy generating functions, facilitating its application to different biological scenarios. We proved a local well-posedness result for the problem in Hölder spaces employing Schauder and semigroup theory. Then, after a suitable parameter reduction through scaling, we computed the numerical solution for the proposed system using a recently developed ghost nodal finite element method
[
6
]
. An interesting aspect emerges when the solution is very articulated and the branches occupy a wide region of the domain.

1.
Introduction

In recent years, there has been growing interest in understanding the principles and mechanisms that underpin the formation of biological transportation networks. Many biologists believe these networks are demand-driven by nature, developing without centralized control
[
38
]
, and finely tuned through cycles of evolutionary selection pressure. Thus, they can be seen as emergent structures arising from self-regulating processes. These structures, particularly the organization of leaf venation networks, vascular networks, and neural networks, have been a focal point for the biophysical and mathematical research communities, as evidenced by recent studies such as
[
4
,
5
,
3
,
11
,
20
,
24
]
. Research in this area has significant implications, reaching beyond biology to fields like medicine, chemistry, and engineering.

In this work, we examine the elliptic-parabolic system introduced in
[
19
]
, which comprises two nonlinear elliptic equations for the pressure
p
and the auxiliary variable
\sigma
, along with a reaction-diffusion parabolic equation for the conductivity tensor
\mathbb{C}
. This system seeks to describe the formation of biological network structures and is derived as the gradient flow of an energy functional (see (
2.1
)), incorporating a diffusive term, an activation term—interpreted as entropy dissipation—and a metabolic cost term.
The energy expression generalizes the one introduced in
[
15
,
17
]
, where the convex entropy generating function
\Phi(p)
was quadratic. The energy functional is constrained by a Poisson equation for pressure, which describes Darcy’s law for slow flow in porous media, as discussed in
[
13
,
28
]
.
By specifying different forms of
\Phi(p)
, we can model various phenomena, such as Joule’s heating and Fisher information
[
19
]
. This flexibility is crucial, as comparing known networks with newly discovered ones is a natural approach to studying biological networks. The introduction of
\Phi(p)
is pivotal because it provides greater freedom in creating patterns that can be matched with empirical biological data. In future research, our model’s outcomes can be rigorously compared with real biological data, enabling a more comprehensive analysis.

A one-dimensional analysis of the proposed model was conducted in
[
8
]
, which demonstrated local-in-time existence for smooth solutions and provided numerical evidence that
\mathbb{C}
touches zero in finite time. In this paper, we focus on the multi-dimensional case, where the analysis is more challenging.

Furthermore, our work is dedicated to the mathematical modeling of biological transportation networks in porous media, specifically leaf venation in plants
[
14
,
27
]
. To this end, we define our equations in a leaf-shaped domain
\Omega
, contained within a rectangular region discretized by a regular square mesh. The mesh is intersected arbitrarily by
\Omega
. We propose a nodal ghost finite-element method, for a detailed explanation of the numerical scheme refer to
[
6
]
. The active mesh
\Omega_{h}
is the subset of the tessellation that intersects
\Omega
. The finite element space on
\Omega_{h}
is a restriction of the finite element space on the background mesh. The finite element method is based on a variational formulation over
\Omega
, with stabilization terms to handle instability from cut elements whose size is proportional to a power of the cell length. In
[
6
,
7
]
, the authors introduce a ”snapping back to grid” technique that aligns the snapping threshold with the penalization term, ensuring optimal convergence.

The paper is structured as follows: Section
2
introduces the mathematical model; Section
3
establishes the existence of solutions to the system (
2.2
–
2.4
) using Hölder theory. Sections
4
through
6
detail the numerical scheme, with special emphasis on space discretization in Section
5
. Section
7
presents various numerical tests in different geometries and with varying convex entropy generators. Finally, we conclude with some insights and future directions.

2.
Model

The recent study
[
19
]
introduced a novel category of self-regulatory processes. These processes are defined through the minimization of an entropy dissipation, expressed by the energy functional

(2.1)

\displaystyle E[\mathbb{C}]=\int_{\Omega}\left(D^{2}\frac{||\nabla\mathbb{C}||^{2}}{2}+c^{2}\Phi^{\prime\prime}(p)\nabla p\cdot(\mathbb{C}+r\mathbb{I})\nabla p+\frac{\nu}{\gamma}||\mathbb{C}||^{\gamma}\right)\mathrm{d}x.

This energy functional, denoted as
E=E[\mathbb{C}]
, undergoes minimization over all the
d
-dimensional symmetric and positive semidefinite conductivity tensor fields
\mathbb{C}=\mathbb{C}(x)\in\mathscr{S}^{d}(\mathbb{R})
.
The diffusion coefficient
D^{2}
controls the random effects in the transportation medium, while the activation parameter
c^{2}
describes the tendency of the network to align with the pressure gradient.
The function
\Phi:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
represents a convex entropy generator, whereas
p=p(x)
describes the scalar material pressure of the porous medium where the network arises.
On the other hand,
r:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
describes the isotropic background permeability of the medium.
The metabolic cost to sustain the network is given by
\frac{\nu}{\gamma}||\mathbb{C}||^{\gamma}
where
\nu>0
is a metabolic coefficient,
\gamma>0
is the metabolic exponent and
||\cdot||
denotes the Frobenius norm. For leaf venation in plants,
1/2\leq\gamma\leq 1
, as shown in
[
21
,
22
]
. For this range of values, there is no proof of convergence to a unique steady state, while such a proof exists for
\gamma>1
in
[
17
]
. Both cases have been numerically investigated in
[
4
,
5
]
. The domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
(with
d\geq 1
) is assumed to be bounded, open and with
C^{1}
boundary
\Gamma:=\partial\Omega
.

The above energy functional is constrained by the mass conservation law

(2.2)

\displaystyle-\mathrm{div}\,((\mathbb{C}+r\mathbb{I})\nabla p)=S\quad\text{in}\;\Omega,

where
S=S(x)
denotes the given distribution of sources and sinks within the system, assumed to be constant over time. We impose that the material flux,
described by the expression
{(\mathbb{C}+r\mathbb{I})\nabla p}
, is zero on
\Gamma
, i.e.,

(\mathbb{C}+r\mathbb{I})\nabla p\cdot\widehat{n}=0\quad\text{on}\;\Gamma.

Here,
\widehat{n}
denotes the outer unit normal vector to
\Gamma
. Compatibility with such a choice requires

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}S(x)\mathrm{d}x=0.

The formal
L^{2}
-gradient flow for the energy functional (
2.1
), constrained by the mass conservation law (
2.2
), reads

(2.3)

\frac{\partial\mathbb{C}}{\partial t}=D^{2}\Delta\mathbb{C}+c^{2}\left(\Phi^{\prime\prime}(p)\nabla p\otimes\nabla p+\frac{\nabla\sigma\otimes\nabla p+\nabla p\otimes\nabla\sigma}{2}\right)-\nu||\mathbb{C}||^{\gamma-2}\mathbb{C}\quad\text{in}\;(0,\infty)\times\Omega,

as detailed in Lemma 1 on
[
19
]
. The variable
t\geq 0
serves as a temporal parameter for the gradient flow, and
\sigma=\sigma(t,x)
solves the boundary value problem

(2.4)

-\mathrm{div}\,((\mathbb{C}+r\mathbb{I})\nabla\sigma)=\Phi^{\prime\prime\prime}(p)\nabla p\cdot(\mathbb{\mathbb{C}}+r\mathbb{I})\nabla p\quad\text{in}\,(0,\infty)\times\Omega,

with the no-flux boundary condition
(\mathbb{C}+r\mathbb{I})\nabla\sigma\cdot\widehat{n}=0
along
\Gamma
.
We choose homogeneous Neumann boundary conditions for all entries of
\mathbb{C}
and the same type of conditions was selected for the numerical simulations carried out in
[
23
]
.
At the end, the system is closed by taking a positive definite initial condition
\mathbb{C}_{0}>0
for the conductivity, i.e.,

(2.5)

\displaystyle\mathbb{C}(t=0,x)=\mathbb{C}_{0}(x)\quad\mathrm{in}\;\Omega.

Remark 1
.

We point out that in Lemma 1 of
[
19
]
, the boundary conditions for
p
and
\sigma
were specified as homogeneous Dirichlet, alongside a particular equilibrium condition for
\Phi(p)
on the boundary, i.e.,
\Phi^{\prime}(p)=0
on
\Gamma
. However, transitioning to no-flux boundary conditions does not alter the gradient flow structure, thereby obviating the need for the equilibrium condition assumption for
\Phi(p)
.

3.
Existence of solutions of the multi-dimensional problem in Hölder space

The goal of this section is to show existence of a solution of the
d
-dimensional system (
2.2
–
2.4
) with initial condition (
2.5
). For the sake of mathematical transparency, we restrict our analysis to the case of
r=0
, as regularization is not necessary for the subsequent theory. However, we mention that the following results can easily be carried over to the physical relevant case of positive background permeability
r
.
We shall work in the space of Hölder-continuous functions.

Theorem 1
.

Let
S\in C^{0,\alpha}(\bar{\Omega})
for some
\alpha\in(0,1)
,
D=0
and
\Phi\in C^{4}(\mathbb{R})
.
Then, the problem (
2.2
–
2.4
) subject to initial condition (
2.5
) - with
\mathbb{C}_{0}(x)\geq\beta
- admits a local in time solution
\mathbb{C}\in X_{T}
for some
T>0
, where the set
X_{T}
is defined as

\displaystyle X_{T}:=\left\{\mathbb{C}\in C\left([0,T];C^{1,\alpha}\left(\bar{\Omega};\mathscr{S}^{d}(\mathbb{R})\right)\right):\;\mathbb{C}\geq\frac{\beta}{2}>0,\|\mathbb{C}\|_{C([0,T];C^{1,\alpha}(\bar{\Omega};\mathscr{S}^{d}(\mathbb{R}))}\leq R\,\mathrm{for\;some}\,R>0\right\}.

Proof.

We shall employ the Banach fixed point theorem.
For technical simplicity of the proof, we shall assume that
\mathbb{C}
is a scalar quantity; however, the below calculations also hold in the tensor case with obvious modifications. Moreover, let
c^{2}=\nu=1
, as clearly, nothing changes in the more general case.

For
T>0
, to be specified later, we construct the operator
\mathcal{S}:X_{T}\rightarrow X_{T}
that maps
\bar{\mathbb{C}}\in X_{T}
to
\mathcal{S}(\bar{\mathbb{C}})=:\mathbb{C}
, whose fixed points are solutions of (
2.2
–
2.4
). Our goal is to demonstrate that
\mathcal{S}
is a contraction on
X_{T}
and that
\mathrm{Im}(\mathcal{S})\subset X_{T}
. Fix
\bar{\mathbb{C}}\in X_{T}
, then from (
2.2
) we have

\displaystyle-\bar{\mathbb{C}}\Delta p-\nabla\bar{\mathbb{C}}\cdot\nabla p=S,

which by the Schauder theory
[
16
]
admits a solution
p(t)\in C^{2,\alpha}(\bar{\Omega})
and there exists a constant
K(d,\alpha,\beta,R)=:K_{\beta,R}>0
such that

(3.1)

\displaystyle\|p(t)\|_{2,\alpha}\leq K_{\beta,R}\|S\|_{0,\alpha},

where
\|\cdot\|_{C^{k,\alpha}(\bar{\Omega})}=\|\cdot\|_{k,\alpha}
. For the sake of simplicity, we denote possibly different constants which depend on
\beta,R
by the same symbol
K_{\beta,R}
. Observe that
\Phi^{\prime\prime\prime}(p(t))\bar{\mathbb{C}}(t)|\nabla p(t)|^{2}\in C^{0,\alpha}(\bar{\Omega})
and there exists
\sigma(t)\in C^{2,\alpha}(\bar{\Omega})
satisfying

\displaystyle\|\sigma(t)\|_{2,\alpha}

\displaystyle\leq K_{\beta,R}\|\Phi^{\prime\prime\prime}(p)\|_{0,\alpha}\|\nabla p(t)\|_{0,\alpha}^{2}

where we used the Banach algebra structure of Hölder spaces. It is straightforward to show that
\|\Phi^{\prime\prime\prime}(p)\|_{0,\alpha}\leq\|\Phi^{\textrm{iv}}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathrm{Im}(p))}\|p\|_{0,\alpha}
where
\|\Phi^{\textrm{iv}}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathrm{Im}(p))}
just depends on
\beta,R
so it will be successively incorporated in the constant
K_{\beta,R}
. Therefore, using (
3.1
) we rewrite the Hölder estimate for
\sigma

(3.2)

\displaystyle\|\sigma(t)\|_{2,\alpha}\leq K_{\beta,R}\|S\|_{0,\alpha}^{3}.

In the course of the computations, we shall omit the temporal dependence when there is no risk of confusion. Next, let
\bar{\mathbb{C}_{1}},\bar{\mathbb{C}_{2}}\in X_{T}
,
p_{i}:=p[\bar{\mathbb{C}_{i}}]
and
\sigma_{i}:=\sigma[\bar{\mathbb{C}_{i}}],\,i=1,2
be the solutions of (
2.2
), (
2.4
), respectively. Then, integrating (
2.3
) on
t\in[0,T]
and subtracting we get

\displaystyle\|\mathbb{C}_{1}-\mathbb{C}_{2}\|_{1,\alpha}

\displaystyle\leq\bigg\|\int_{0}^{t}\bigg[\left(\Phi^{\prime\prime}(p_{1})|\nabla p_{1}|^{2}-\Phi^{\prime\prime}(p_{2})|\nabla p_{2}|^{2}\right)+\left(\nabla p_{1}\cdot\nabla\sigma_{1}-\nabla p_{2}\cdot\nabla\sigma_{2}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\qquad+\left(|\mathbb{C}_{1}|^{\gamma-1}-|\mathbb{C}_{2}|^{\gamma-1}\right)\bigg]\,\,\mathrm{d}s\bigg\|_{1,\alpha}

(3.3)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{t}\left(J_{1}(s)+J_{2}(s)+J_{3}(s)\right)\,\,\mathrm{d}s,

where

\displaystyle J_{1}(s)

\displaystyle:=\|\Phi^{\prime\prime}(p_{1})|\nabla p_{1}|^{2}-\Phi^{\prime\prime}(p_{2})|\nabla p_{2}|^{2}\|_{1,\alpha}

\displaystyle J_{2}(s)

\displaystyle:=\|\nabla p_{1}\cdot\nabla\sigma_{1}-\nabla p_{2}\cdot\nabla\sigma_{2}\|_{1,\alpha}

\displaystyle J_{3}(s)

\displaystyle:=\||\mathbb{C}_{1}|^{\gamma-1}-|\mathbb{C}_{2}|^{\gamma-1}\|_{1,\alpha}.

Firstly, adding and subtracting
\Phi^{\prime\prime}(p_{2})|\nabla p_{1}|^{2}
, using (
3.1
), the local Lipschitz continuity of
\Phi^{\prime\prime}
and the continuous embedding of
C^{2,\alpha}(\bar{\Omega})
into
C^{1,\alpha}(\bar{\Omega})
, we estimate
J_{1}(s)

\displaystyle J_{1}(s)

\displaystyle\leq\big\|\left(\Phi^{\prime\prime}(p_{1})-\Phi^{\prime\prime}(p_{2})\right)|\nabla p_{1}|^{2}\big\|_{1,\alpha}+\big\|\Phi^{\prime\prime}(p_{2})\left(|\nabla p_{1}|^{2}-|\nabla p_{2}|^{2}\right)\big\|_{1,\alpha}

\displaystyle\leq K_{\beta,R}\|S\|_{0,\alpha}^{2}\|p_{1}-p_{2}\|_{1,\alpha}+\|\Phi^{\prime\prime}(p_{2})\|_{1,\alpha}\|\nabla p_{1}+\nabla p_{2}\|_{1,\alpha}\|\nabla p_{1}-\nabla p_{2}\|_{1,\alpha}

(3.4)

\displaystyle\leq K_{\beta,R}\|S\|_{0,\alpha}^{2}\|p_{1}-p_{2}\|_{2,\alpha}.

To estimate
p_{1}-p_{2}
, we consider the elliptic equation (
2.2
) for both
p_{1},p_{2}
and subtract the two, obtaining

\displaystyle-\mathrm{div}\,\left(\bar{\mathbb{C}_{1}}(\nabla p_{1}-\nabla p_{2})\right)=\mathrm{div}\,\left(\left((\bar{\mathbb{C}_{1}}-\bar{\mathbb{C}_{2}}\right)\nabla p_{2}\right)\in C\left([0,T];C^{0,\alpha}(\bar{\Omega})\right).

Employing Schauder theory, estimate (
3.1
) and the continuous embedding of
C^{1,\alpha}(\bar{\Omega})
into
C^{0,\alpha}(\bar{\Omega})
, we can write

(3.5)

\displaystyle\|p_{1}-p_{2}\|_{2,\alpha}\leq K_{\beta,R}\|S\|_{0,\alpha}\|\bar{\mathbb{C}_{1}}-\bar{\mathbb{C}_{2}}\|_{1,\alpha},

which substituted into (
3.4
) yields

(3.6)

\displaystyle J_{1}(s)\leq K_{\beta,R}\|S\|_{0,\alpha}^{3}\|\bar{\mathbb{C}_{1}}-\bar{\mathbb{C}_{2}}\|_{1,\alpha}\quad\forall\;\bar{\mathbb{C}_{1}},\bar{\mathbb{C}_{2}}\in X_{T}.

Adding and subtracting
\nabla p_{1}\cdot\nabla\sigma_{2}
and using (
3.1
), (
3.2
), (
3.5
) we obtain the following estimate for
J_{2}(s)

\displaystyle J_{2}(s)

\displaystyle\leq\big\|\nabla p_{1}\cdot\left(\nabla\sigma_{1}-\nabla\sigma_{2}\right)\big\|_{1,\alpha}+\big\|\nabla\sigma_{2}\cdot\left(\nabla p_{1}-\nabla p_{2}\right)\big\|_{1,\alpha}

(3.7)

\displaystyle\leq K_{\beta,R}\|S\|_{0,\alpha}\left(\|\sigma_{1}-\sigma_{2}\|_{2,\alpha}+\|S\|_{0,\alpha}^{3}\|\bar{\mathbb{C}_{1}}-\bar{\mathbb{C}_{2}}\|_{1,\alpha}\right).

An estimate of
\sigma_{1}-\sigma_{2}
is obtained by considering the elliptic equation (
2.4
) for
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}
and subtracting the two equations to get

\displaystyle-\mathrm{div}\,\left(\bar{\mathbb{C}_{1}}\nabla\sigma_{1}-\bar{\mathbb{C}_{2}}\nabla\sigma_{2}\right)=\Phi^{\prime\prime\prime}(p_{1})\bar{\mathbb{C}_{1}}|\nabla p_{1}|^{2}-\Phi^{\prime\prime\prime}(p_{2})\bar{\mathbb{C}_{2}}|\nabla p_{2}|^{2},

which is recast, after some algebraic manipulation, into

\displaystyle-\mathrm{div}\,\left(\bar{\mathbb{C}_{1}}\left(\nabla\sigma_{1}-\nabla\sigma_{2}\right)\right)=

\displaystyle\mathrm{div}\,\left(\left(\bar{\mathbb{C}_{2}}-\bar{\mathbb{C}_{1}}\right)\nabla\sigma_{2}\right)+\left(\Phi^{\prime\prime\prime}(p_{1})-\Phi^{\prime\prime\prime}(p_{2})\right)\bar{\mathbb{C}_{1}}|\nabla p_{1}|^{2}

\displaystyle+\Phi^{\prime\prime\prime}(p_{2})\big[\left(\bar{\mathbb{C}_{1}}-\bar{\mathbb{C}_{2}}\right)|\nabla p_{1}|^{2}+\bar{\mathbb{C}_{2}}\left(|\nabla p_{1}|+|\nabla p_{2}|\right)\left(|\nabla p_{1}|-|\nabla p_{2}|\right)\big].

The right-hand side of the latter belongs to
C\left([0,T];C^{0,\alpha}(\bar{\Omega})\right)
. Therefore, the Schauder theory gives the desired estimate

(3.8)

\displaystyle\|\sigma_{1}-\sigma_{2}\|_{2,\alpha}\leq K_{\beta,R}\|S\|_{0,\alpha}^{3}\|\bar{\mathbb{C}_{1}}-\bar{\mathbb{C}_{2}}\|_{1,\alpha}.

Applying (
3.8
) to (
3.7
) we get

(3.9)

\displaystyle J_{2}(s)\leq K_{\beta,R}\|S\|_{0,\alpha}^{4}\|\bar{\mathbb{C}_{1}}-\bar{\mathbb{C}_{2}}\|_{1,\alpha}\quad\forall\;\bar{\mathbb{C}_{1}},\bar{\mathbb{C}_{2}}\in X_{T}.

Regarding
J_{3}(s)
, we observe that the derivative of the function
x\rightarrow|\bar{\mathbb{C}}(x)|^{\gamma-1}
is locally bounded away from the zero. Given that
\bar{\mathbb{C}}\in X_{T}
implies
\bar{\mathbb{C}}\geq\frac{\beta}{2}>0
, it is not feasible to consider a neighborhood around zero. This condition implies that the function is locally Lipschitz continuous, that is, there exists a constant
K_{\beta}>0
such that

(3.10)

\displaystyle J_{3}(s)\leq K_{\beta}\|\bar{\mathbb{C}_{1}}-\bar{\mathbb{C}_{2}}\|_{1,\alpha}\quad\forall\;\bar{\mathbb{C}_{1}},\bar{\mathbb{C}_{2}}\in X_{T}.

Substitution of (
3.6
), (
3.9
) and (
3.10
) into (
3.3
) yields

\displaystyle\|\mathbb{C}_{1}-\mathbb{C}_{2}\|_{1,\alpha}\leq K_{\beta,R}\left(\|S\|_{0,\alpha}^{4}+\|S\|_{0,\alpha}^{3}+1\right)\int_{0}^{t}\|\bar{\mathbb{C}_{1}}(s)-\bar{\mathbb{C}_{2}}(s)\|_{1,\alpha}\,\mathrm{d}s.

Subsequently, taking the maximum on
[0,T]
of the latter expression, we get

\displaystyle\|\mathbb{C}_{1}-\mathbb{C}_{2}\|_{X_{T}}\leq K_{\beta,R}\left(\|S\|_{0,\alpha}^{4}+\|S\|_{0,\alpha}^{3}+1\right)T\|\bar{\mathbb{C}_{1}}-\bar{\mathbb{C}_{2}}\|_{X_{T}},

and choosing
T
such that

\displaystyle T<\frac{1}{K_{\beta,R}\left(\|S\|_{0,\alpha}^{4}+\|S\|_{0,\alpha}^{3}+1\right)}

we prove the contraction property for
\mathcal{S}
.

To complete the proof, it remains to show that
\mathcal{S}(\bar{\mathbb{C}})=:\mathbb{C}\in X_{T}
for every
\bar{\mathbb{C}}\in X_{T}
.
Integrating (
2.3
) on
[0,t]
for some
t\in(0,T)
and using (
3.1
), (
3.2
) we obtain the following bound from below for
\mathbb{C}

\displaystyle\mathbb{C}

\displaystyle\geq\beta-\int_{0}^{t}\big|\Phi^{\prime\prime}(p)|\nabla p|^{2}+\nabla p\cdot\nabla\sigma-|\mathbb{C}|^{\gamma-1}\big|\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\geq\beta-\int_{0}^{t}\|\Phi^{\prime\prime}(p)\|_{0,\alpha}\|\nabla p\|^{2}_{0,\alpha}+\|\nabla p\|_{0,\alpha}\|\nabla\sigma\|_{0,\alpha}+\||\mathbb{C}|^{\gamma-1}\|_{0,\alpha}\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\geq\beta-K_{\beta,R}\left(\|S\|_{0,\alpha}^{4}+\|S\|_{0,\alpha}^{3}+1\right)T,

where for the last term we used the fact that
\|\mathbb{C}\|_{0,\alpha}\leq R
.
Designing
T
such that

\displaystyle T\leq\frac{\beta}{2K_{\beta,R}\left(\|S\|_{0,\alpha}^{4}+\|S\|_{0,\alpha}^{3}+1\right)},

we ensure
\mathbb{C}\geq\frac{\beta}{2}
, so that
\textrm{Im}(\mathcal{S})\subset X_{T}
.
Finally, existence and uniqueness follow from Banach’s fixed point theorem.

Remark 2
.

In Theorem
1
, we initially assumed
D=0
. However, this restriction can be relaxed to
D>0
by applying semigroup theory. Indeed, we notice from the above proof that the function

\displaystyle f(\mathbb{C}(t,x)):=c^{2}\left(\Phi^{\prime\prime}(p)\nabla p\otimes\nabla p+\frac{\nabla\sigma\otimes\nabla p+\nabla p\otimes\nabla\sigma}{2}\right)-\nu||\mathbb{C}||^{\gamma-2}\mathbb{C}

is locally Lipschitz with respect to
\mathbb{C}
. Moreover, the operator
\Delta
is the infinitesimal generator of a
C_{0}
semigroup
\mathscr{T}(t)
on
C^{1,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega};\mathscr{S}^{d}(\mathbb{R}))
. Then, by semigroup theory
[
32
, Section 6.1, Theorem 1.4]
, for every
\mathbb{C}_{0}(x)\in C^{1,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega};\mathscr{S}^{d}(\mathbb{R}))
there exists a maximal time
t_{\mathrm{max}}<\infty
for which the initial value problem (
2.3
-
2.5
) admits a unique mild solution
\mathbb{C}
on the time interval
[0,t_{\mathrm{max}}[
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathbb{C}(t,x)=\mathscr{T}(t)\mathbb{C}_{0}(x)+\int_{0}^{t}\mathscr{T}(t-s)f(\mathbb{C}(s,x))ds.

Furthermore, it is shown that
\lim_{t\rightarrow t_{\mathrm{max}}}\|\mathbb{C}(t)\|_{1,\alpha}=\infty
.

Notice that the energy expression defined in (
2.1
) is dependent on four parameters, namely,
E[\mathbb{C}]\equiv E_{D,c,\nu,\gamma}[\mathbb{C}]
. As the objective of this paper is to conduct several numerical experiments, we propose a parameter reduction through scaling. It is observed that
1/D
and
c^{2}
exert similar influences on the system dynamics. Specifically, as these parameters increase, the network number of branches increases
[
1
]
. Consequently, we introduce the following scaling aimed at simplifying the numerical experiments

\displaystyle E_{D,c,\nu,\gamma}[\mathbb{C}]

\displaystyle=c^{2}\int_{\Omega}\left(\frac{D^{2}}{c^{2}}\frac{||\nabla\mathbb{C}||^{2}}{2}+\Phi^{\prime\prime}(p)\nabla p\cdot\left(\mathbb{C}+r\mathbb{I}\right)\nabla p+\frac{\nu}{c^{2}\gamma}||\mathbb{C}||^{\gamma}\right)\mathrm{d}x

(3.11)

\displaystyle=:c^{2}E_{\widetilde{D},\widetilde{\nu},\gamma}[\mathbb{C}]

where
\widetilde{D}:=\frac{D}{c}
and
\widetilde{\nu}=\frac{\nu}{c^{2}}
. The rescaled energy
E_{\widetilde{D},\widetilde{\nu},\gamma}[\mathbb{C}]
modifies the original formulation such that the kinetic term is normalized. In contrast, the diffusion term becomes comparatively small (considering
D
as a small parameter and
c
as a large one), the metabolic term can be adjusted according to the specific experimental setup of interest, and the rescaled time variable is
\widetilde{t}=c^{2}t
, that we rename as
t
for simplicity.

4.
Variational formulation

Let
\Omega\subset R
be an open, bounded and sufficiently smooth domain in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, where
R
is a rectangular region. We now rewrite (
2.2
-
2.5
) according to the scaling defined in (
3.11
)

(4.1a)

\displaystyle-{\rm div}\left(\left(\mathbb{C}+r\mathbb{I}\right)\nabla p\right)=S\qquad

(4.1b)

For simplicity, in the equation we omit the dependence on the space and time variables of the functions.

We now discuss discretization in time. We consider a final time

\displaystyle+\Delta t\,\widetilde{\nu}\left(\,\left(||\mathbb{C}_{h}^{n}||+\varepsilon\right)^{\gamma-2}\mathbb{C}_{h}^{n+1},\mathbb{B}_{h}\right)=\left(\,\mathbb{C}_{h}^{n},\mathbb{B}_{h}\,\right),

7.
Numerical results

In this section we apply the ghost nodal FEM
[
6
]
with the dual purpose of testing the newly developed method by verifying its accuracy and independence on grid orientation, and exploring the behavior of the solution of the biological transportation model, for different geometries and various choices of the entropy generator function
\Phi(p)
.
By specifying
\Phi(p)
, which affects the entropy dissipation term,
we model various phenomena, as highlighted in
[
19
]
.
A common example is given by
\Phi(p)=p^{2}/2
, which corresponds to the minimization of Joule heating
[
19
,
25
]
.
In this case, the pumping energy is determined by Joule’s law, which states that the system power density is given by the scalar product of the current
J=\mathbb{C}\nabla p
and the potential gradient
\nabla p
. This scenario has been extensively studied in the literature, e.g.
[
4
,
5
,
18
]
.

Another interesting entropy generator is given by the Boltzmann entropy generator
\Phi(p)=(p+1)(\ln(p+1)-1)
, which converts the kinetic part of the energy (
3.11
) into the Fisher information
[
19
]
. This quantity plays an important role in various mathematical fields, ranging from information theory and statistical parameter estimation
[
33
]
, to statistical mechanics and fluctuations in thermodynamic equilibrium
[
12
]
, and even mesoscopic models in biological network formation
[
20
]
.
In
[
2
, Lemma 2.6]
, the sub- and super-entropies used in the relative entropy argument leading to a log-Sobolev inequality were the Boltzmann entropy and the quadratic entropy, respectively. In our context, there are no restrictions on the choice of entropy. Therefore, we can use a quartic entropy generating function
\Phi(p)={p^{4}}/{12}
to test the numerical stability of our scheme and explore beyond the quadratic threshold previously mentioned. This choice is noteworthy because nonlinear and non-local PDEs often exhibit poor behavior with higher powers, such as those seen in quartic entropy generating functions. For similar numerical reasons, a linear combination of the Fisher entropy and quartic entropy generators is also of interest.

In summary, the expressions we choose for the second derivative of the entropy generating function
\Phi(p)
are:

quartic:

\;\;\quad\qquad\Phi^{\prime\prime}(p)=p^{2}

Fisher:

\qquad\qquad\Phi^{\prime\prime}(p)=(p+1)^{-1}

combination:

\quad\Phi^{\prime\prime}(p)=0.5\,p^{2}+0.5\,(p+1)^{-1}

Regarding the domain
\Omega
, we start with a circular domain, to see if there are effects coming from the anisotropy
of the grid. The equations presented in (
6.1
-
6.2b
) are symmetric with respect to the
x
and
y
axis. Therefore, we expect that a symmetric domain would maintain their properties. In Fig.
5
, the time evolution of the solution at selected times is presented to illustrate the moment in which symmetry is lost. Our numerical evidences suggest that the loss of symmetry is entirely attributable to the metabolic term. There is no loss of symmetry for
\widetilde{\nu}=0
and
\widetilde{\nu}\neq 0,\,\gamma>1
. These tests led us to suspect that the loss of symmetry is mainly due to the small denominator in the term
||\mathbb{C}+\varepsilon||^{\gamma-2}
, when
\gamma<1
and
\mathbb{C}\approx 0
. For this reason, we conducted other tests that confirmed our theory. We also observe no loss of symmetry in the following tests:
\widetilde{\nu}\neq 0,\,\gamma<1
and

Conclusions

In this paper, we began by revisiting the
L^{2}

[
19
]
. We established a local existence and uniqueness theorem within Hölder spaces, employing Schauder and semigroup theory. The development of a comprehensive Sobolev theory remains an ongoing challenge due to the absence of sufficient regularity estimates and the lack of a minimum principle for the conductivity tensor.

A key objective of our research was to conduct extensive numerical experiments. We were interested in showing results in different geometries and for different sets of parameters.
To this purpose we adopted a newly developed nodal ghost method, which is particularly suited for evolutionary PDEs on arbitrary domains in two space dimensions
[
6
]
.
We began with a circular domain to establish that the numerical solution is barely affected by grid orientation. To further validate this, we conducted tests on a leaf-shaped domain, including showing the effects of a rotated domain.

The main discover of the paper is the following. As
r
decreases, the solution becomes increasingly intricate, with numerous small branches spreading across a larger area of the computational domain. When
\mathbb{C}<r
, isotropic permeability takes over, causing the branches to disappear. Moreover, as

r
gets smaller, finer scales emerge in the solution, making it more challenging to achieve accurate results due to the need for higher number of cells to capture these details. A parallel version of the code, which allows unprecedented high resolution computations, is currently in preparation. The new code should allow us to formulate conjectures about the fractal nature of the small branches for vanishingly small values of the isotropic conductivity, for the various models considered in this paper.
