Critical slowdown of non-equilibrium polaron dynamics
K. Knakkergaard Nielsen, L. A. Pe n ~ \tilde{\rm n} a Ardila, G. M. Bruun, T. Pohl

Abstract
We study the quantum dynamics of a single impurity following its sudden immersion into a Bose-Einstein condensate. The ensuing formation of the Bose polaron in this general setting can be seen as impurity decoherence driven by the condensate, which we describe within a master equation approach. We derive rigorous analytical results for this decoherence dynamics, and thereby reveal distinct stages of its evolution from a universal stretched exponential initial relaxation to the final approach to equilibrium. The associated polaron formation time exhibits a strong dependence on the impurity speed and is found to undergo a critical slowdown around the speed of sound of the condensate. This rich non-equilibrium behaviour of quantum impurities is of direct relevance to recent cold atom experiments, in which Bose polarons are created by a sudden quench of the impurity-bath interaction. Keywords : Quantum gases, Polarons, Non-equilibrium many-body systems, Impurity-bath decoherence

2
The System

We consider an impurity of mass
m
in a gas of bosons with a mass
{m_{\rm B}}
and a density
{n_{\text{B}}}
. The boson-boson and boson-impurity interactions are both of short range nature and characterized by the scattering lengths
{a_{\text{B}}}
and
a
, respectively. For weak interactions and close to zero temperature, the bosons form a BEC that is accurately described by Bogoliubov theory. The Hamiltonian,

H=H_{\rm B}+H_{\rm I}+H_{\rm IB},

(1)

of this system can be separated into three terms, where

H_{\rm B}=\sum_{{\bf k}}E_{\bf k}\beta^{\dagger}_{{\bf k}}\beta_{{\bf k}}

(2)

describes the BEC in terms of Bogoliubov modes with momenta
{\bf k}
and associated energies
E_{\bf k}=[\varepsilon^{\rm B}_{\bf k}(\varepsilon^{\rm B}_{\bf k}+2{n_{\text{B}}}{\mathcal{T}_{\rm B}})]^{1/2}
that are created by the operators
\beta_{{\bf k}}^{\dagger}
. Here,
{\mathcal{T}_{\rm B}}=4\pi{a_{\text{B}}}/{m_{\rm B}}
is the scattering matrix for the boson-boson interaction, and
\varepsilon^{\rm B}_{\bf k}=k^{2}/2{m_{\rm B}}
is the bare boson kinetic energy at momentum
{\bf k}
. We work in units where
\hbar=1
and the temperature is zero. The impurity Hamiltonian is given by

H_{\rm I}=\sum_{{\bf k}}\varepsilon_{\bf k}c^{\dagger}_{{\bf k}}c_{{\bf k}},

(3)

where
c^{\dagger}_{{\bf k}}
creates an impurity with momentum
{\bf k}
and energy

\varepsilon_{\bf k}=k^{2}/2m+\varepsilon_{\rm MF},

(4)

which includes the mean-field shift
\varepsilon_{\rm MF}={n_{\text{B}}}U_{0}
of the impurity energy due to its interaction with the BEC.
Here,
U_{\bf k}
is the impurity-boson interaction in momentum space. Accordingly, the term

H_{\rm IB}=\sum_{{\bf k},{\bf p}}U_{\bf k}\sqrt{\frac{{n_{\text{B}}}\varepsilon^{\rm B}_{\bf k}}{VE_{\bf k}}}c^{\dagger}_{{\bf p}-{\bf k}}c_{{\bf p}}\left(\beta^{\dagger}_{{\bf k}}+\beta_{-{\bf k}}\right)

(5)

describes the impurity-boson interaction. Equations (
2
)-(
5
) correspond to the so-called Fröhlich model
[
37
]
, originally put forth to describe the electrons coupled to optical phonons of a dielectric crystal. In the present case of an impurity in a BEC, there are interaction terms not included in (
5
), which describe the scattering of the impurity on bosons already excited out of the BEC. Their contribution is, however, suppressed by a factor
({n_{\text{B}}}a_{\rm B}^{3})^{1/2}

[
24
]
, which we assume to be small. Focussing our analysis on the regime of validity of the Fröhlich Hamiltonian, we consider all observables to second order in the impurity scattering length
a
. We note however, that central characteristics of the impurity dynamics, such as the stretched exponential initial coherence decay and the critical slowdown of polaron formation, shown in figure
1
, persist beyond the Fröhlich model.

Explicitly, we express the interaction as
U_{\bf k}=U_{0}g_{\bf k}
, with
g_{\bf k}
a rescaled coupling and
g_{0}=1
. We then solve the Lippmann-Schwinger equation to express
U_{0}
in terms of
a
to second order. This yields
U_{0}={\mathcal{T}}+{\mathcal{T}}^{2}/(2\pi)^{3}\int{\rm d}^{3}k\;g_{\bf k}^{2}\;2m_{r}/k^{2}
. Here
{\mathcal{T}}=2\pi a/m_{r}
is the zero energy impurity-boson scattering matrix and
m_{r}={m_{\rm B}}m/(m+{m_{\rm B}})
denotes the reduced mass. This allows to re-express our results in terms of the scattering length
a
, which yields well-defined result in the limit of zero range interactions.

In order to study decoherence, we consider a quench in which an impurity with momentum
{\bf p}
is suddenly immersed into the condensate at time
t=0
, creating the initial state

\ket{\psi_{0}}=\left(\cos\theta+\sin\theta c_{{\bf p}}^{\dagger}\right)\ket{{\rm BEC}},

(6)

where
\theta
is the mixing angle that determines the probability,
\sin^{2}\theta
, for initial impurity creation. The coherence between the vacuum and the single-impurity state can then be obtained from

\displaystyle C_{p}(t)=\bra{\psi_{0}}\!c_{{\bf p}}(t)\!\ket{\psi_{0}},

(7)

where
c_{{\bf p}}(t)=\exp(iHt)c_{{\bf p}}\exp(-iHt)
is the time-dependent impurity operator in the Heisenberg picture.
The evolution of
C_{p}(t)
closely traces the dynamical formation of the polaron. Substituting the initial state (
6
) into (
7
) yields

\displaystyle C_{p}(t)=

\displaystyle\cos\theta\sin\theta\!\bra{{\rm BEC}}\!c_{{\bf p}}(t)c^{\dagger}_{{\bf p}}(0)\!\ket{{\rm BEC}}=i\cos\theta\sin\theta G_{{\bf p}}(t).

(8)

This shows that the coherence is related to the time-dependent impurity Green’s function,
G_{\bf p}
, for
t>0
. For long times, it should thus approach the asymptotic Green’s function of the polaron

\displaystyle\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}C_{p}(t)\sim Z_{p}e^{-iE_{p}\cdot t-t/2\tau_{p}},

(9)

where
E_{p}
is the polaron energy,
\tau_{p}
its lifetime, and
Z_{p}
its quasiparticle residue. For an infinite polaron lifetime,
|C_{p}(t)|
therefore converges to the
quasiparticle weight
Z_{p}

[
31
]
.

In cold atom experiments, the initial state (
6
) can be prepared by driving a transition between internal atomic states of the impurity which feature different interactions. Indeed recent experimental demonstrations of the Bose polaron were based on RF spectroscopy on a hyperfine transition between a weakly and a strongly interacting state of a single-component
39
K BEC
[
16
]
or of
40
K impurity atoms immersed in a
87
Rb condensate
[
17
]
. In both cases, a direct measurement of the coherence dynamics is possible via Ramsey spectroscopy
[
38
,
39
,
40
]
.

3
Master equation description

We determine the dynamics of the coherence using the impurity density operator
{\rho_{\rm I}}
. First, we consider the density operator
\rho(t)
of the entire system, i.e. the impurity and the BEC. In the interaction picture, it obeys the von Neumann equation

i\partial_{t}\rho=[H_{\rm IB}(t),\rho(t)].

(10)

One can formally solve this equation by integrating both sides and resubstituting the result into the right hand side of (
10
) to obtain

\partial_{t}\rho=\!-i[H_{\rm IB}(t),\rho(0)]-\!\int_{0}^{t}\!\!{\rm d}s[H_{\rm IB}(t),[H_{\rm IB}(s),\rho(s)]].\!\!

(11)

We can now trace out the bosonic degrees of freedom on both sides of (
11
) to obtain an evolution equation for the reduced density operator,
{\rho_{\rm I}}={\rm Tr}_{\rm B}\rho
, of the impurity. For our chosen initial state (
6
), the initial density operator of the entire system factorizes according to
\rho(0)={\rho_{\rm I}}(0)\otimes{\rho_{\rm B}}(0)
, where
{\rho_{\rm B}}(0)=\ket{{\rm BEC}}\bra{{\rm BEC}}
is the density operator for the BEC. Taking the trace of the first term in (
11
) only yields terms proportional to
\braket{\beta}=\braket{\beta^{\dagger}}=0
, such that we obtain the following equation of motion for the impurity density operator

\partial_{t}{\rho_{\rm I}}=\!-\!\int_{0}^{t}\!\!{\rm d}s\;{\rm Tr}_{\rm B}\!\left[H_{\rm IB}(t),[H_{\rm IB}(s),{\rho_{\rm I}}(s)\otimes{\rho_{\rm B}}(0)]\right].\!

(12)

Here, we have made the Born approximation,
\rho(s)={\rho_{\rm I}}(s)\otimes{\rho_{\rm B}}(0)
assuming that the density matrix of the BEC is unaffected by the impurity, which is justified for small impurity interactions. In the same limit, we can also replace
{\rho_{\rm I}}(s)
by
{\rho_{\rm I}}(t)
to obtain a time-local equation that contains all relevant contributions up to second order in the interaction strength
[
41
,
42
]
. Altogether this makes it possible to evaluate the trace over the commutator in (
12
). Using further that
\bra{{\rm BEC}}\beta_{{\bf k}}\beta^{\dagger}_{{\bf k}^{\prime}}\ket{{\rm BEC}}=\delta_{{\bf k},{\bf k}^{\prime}}
and
\beta_{{\bf k}}(t)={\rm e}^{-iE_{\bf k}t}\beta_{{\bf k}}(0)
in
the interaction picture, we finally obtain

\displaystyle\partial_{t}{\rho_{\rm I}}=

\displaystyle-\frac{{n_{\text{B}}}{\mathcal{T}}^{2}}{V}\!\sum_{{\bf k},{\bf p}1,{\bf p}2}\!\!g_{\bf k}^{2}\frac{\varepsilon^{\rm B}_{\bf k}}{E_{\bf k}}\int_{0}^{t}{\rm d}s\left(e^{-iE_{\bf k}(t-s)}\right.

\displaystyle\times\left.\left[c_{{\bf p}1-{\bf k}}^{\dagger}(t)c_{{\bf p}1}(t),c_{{\bf p}2+{\bf k}}^{\dagger}(s)c_{{\bf p}2}(s){\rho_{\rm I}}(t)\right]+\text{h.c.}\right).

(13)

Further details of this derivation are given in
A
. Equation (
13
) constitutes an effective von Neumann equation for the impurity density operator, and will now be used to determine the dynamics of the impurity coherence.

4
Impurity Coherence

Knowing the time-dependent density operator of the impurity we can calculate its coherence from

\displaystyle C_{p}(t)

\displaystyle={\rm Tr}[c_{{\bf p}}(t){\rho_{\rm I}}(t)]=\bra{0}c_{{\bf p}}(t){\rho_{\rm I}}(t)\ket{0},

(14)

where we now trace over the impurity degrees of freedom, and have used that only the vacuum
\ket{0}
contributes to the trace for the initial state (
6
). It turns out to be convenient to introduce the coherence
\tilde{C}_{p}(t)={\rm e}^{i\varepsilon_{\bf p}t}C_{p}(t)
, stripped of the single particle and mean field phase rotation. Using (
14
) and
c_{{\bf p}}(t)={\rm e}^{-i\varepsilon_{\bf p}t}c_{{\bf p}}(0)
we can write for its time evolution
\partial_{t}\tilde{C}_{p}(t)=\bra{0}c_{{\bf p}}(0)\partial_{t}{\rho_{\rm I}}(t)\ket{0}
, which, upon substituting (
13
) and after some algebra, yields

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\tilde{C}_{p}(t)=i[\Gamma_{p}(t)-\Gamma_{p}(0)]\tilde{C}_{p}(t),

(15)

with the time-dependent rate coefficient

\displaystyle\Gamma_{p}(t)

\displaystyle={n_{\text{B}}}\mathcal{T}^{2}\int\frac{{\rm d}^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}g^{2}_{\bf k}\frac{\varepsilon^{\rm B}_{{\bf k}}}{E_{{\bf k}}}\frac{{\rm e}^{i(\varepsilon_{{\bf p}}-\varepsilon_{{\bf p}-{\bf k}}-E_{{\bf k}})t}}{\varepsilon_{{\bf p}}-\varepsilon_{{\bf p}-{\bf k}}-E_{{\bf k}}}.

(16)

Equation (
15
) implies a pure decay of the impurity coherence driven by its interaction with the surrounding BEC. The solution of (
15
) is readily obtained, and upon reintroducing the phase factor
{\rm e}^{i\varepsilon_{\bf p}t}
gives

C_{p}(t)={\rm e}^{-i(p^{2}/2m+{n_{\text{B}}}{\mathcal{T}}+\Sigma_{p})t}{\rm e}^{i\int_{0}^{t}\!{\rm d}s\Gamma_{p}(s)}C_{p}(0),

(17)

with

\hskip-0.56917pt\Sigma_{p}\!={n_{\text{B}}}{\mathcal{T}}^{2}\!\!\int\!\!\frac{{\rm d}^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}g^{2}_{\bf k}\!\left[\frac{\varepsilon^{\rm B}_{\bf k}}{E_{\bf k}}\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{\bf p}-\varepsilon_{{\bf p}-{\bf k}}-E_{\bf k}}\!+\!\frac{2m_{r}}{k^{2}}\right].\!\!

(18)

Here, we have used

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{{\bf p}}+\Gamma_{p}(0)

\displaystyle=p^{2}/2m+{n_{\text{B}}}U_{0}+\Gamma_{p}(0)=p^{2}/2m+{n_{\text{B}}}{\mathcal{T}}+\Sigma_{p}

(19)

which follows from the Lippmann-Schwinger equation, relating
U_{0}
to the zero-energy impurity-boson scattering matrix
{\mathcal{T}}
, as discussed in Sec.
2
. Equation (
17
) explicitly shows how the phase factor corresponding to the polaron energy
E_{p}=p^{2}/2m+{n_{\text{B}}}{\mathcal{T}}+\Sigma_{p}
naturally emerges from our formalism. Indeed, (
18
) coincides with the second order contribution to the ground state energy of the Bose polaron obtained in
[
21
,
24
]
in the limit of a zero range potential with
g_{\bf k}=1
. Comparing (
17
) with the
t\rightarrow\infty
limit given by (
9
), we see that the integral
\int_{0}^{t}\!{\rm d}s\;\Gamma_{p}(s)
determines the dynamical formation of the polaron state.

Upon evaluating the integral in (
16
), the expression for
\Gamma_{p}(t)
can be written as

\Gamma_{p}(t)=\Gamma\cdot\gamma(\nu,p\xi/\sqrt{2},t/t_{\rm B}),

(20)

where

\Gamma=\frac{m_{\rm B}^{2}}{m_{r}^{2}}\frac{\sqrt{2}a^{2}}{{a_{\text{B}}}\xi}t_{\rm B}^{-1},

(21)

is a rate constant, and
\gamma(\nu,p\xi/\sqrt{2},t/t_{\rm B})
is a time-dependent function that only depends on the mass ratio
\nu=m/{m_{\rm B}}
and the dimensionless impurity momentum
p\xi/\sqrt{2}
in units of the inverse of the BEC coherence length
\xi=1/\sqrt{8\pi{n_{\text{B}}}{a_{\text{B}}}}
. Moreover, (
21
) contains the characteristic timescale
{t_{\text{B}}}=\xi/\sqrt{2}c
for the impurity dynamics, determined by the speed of sound
c=\sqrt{4\pi{n_{\text{B}}}{a_{\text{B}}}}/{m_{\rm B}}
of the condensate. Physically, this timescale reflects how fast Bogoliubov modes can build up distortions on a length scale
\xi
, which corresponds to the typical size of the screening cloud surrounding the impurity.

5
Short-time dynamics

At very short times, the impurity dynamics depends on the actual shape of the impurity-boson interaction. To model a finite range, we consider a step function in momentum space,
g_{\bf k}=\Theta(\kappa-k)
. This choice provides a simplified model for the actual interaction between the atoms, whose typical range,
r_{0}
, relates to the characteristic momentum
\kappa\sim r_{0}^{-1}
. As shown in
B
, finite range effects are significant only at times shorter than
t_{i}/{t_{\text{B}}}\simeq 1/(\kappa\xi)^{2}\sim(r_{0}/\xi)^{2}
. The characteristic extent of interactions between alkaline atoms is below
1
nm and, thus, about two orders of magnitudes smaller than the typical coherence length
\xi\sim 0.1\mu
m of atomic BECs. It thus follows that the finite interaction range only affects the initial dynamics for extremely short times
t/{t_{\text{B}}}\lesssim 10^{-4}
, such that we can take the well defined zero-range limit,
\kappa\rightarrow\infty
, under typical experimental conditions.

Long-time dynamics

, the impurity dynamics starts to depend on the momentum whereby
|C_{p}(t)|
approaches a finite value for

[

(see

7
Polaron formation time

Equation (
26
) indicates that the timescale
t_{\rm f}
for the discussed approach to equilibrium is on the order of
t_{\rm B}
for equal masses
\nu=1
and zero momentum. In general, however, the polaron formation time
t_{\rm f}
can depend strongly on the impurity momentum, as we will now discuss.

Consider first the regime of momenta below the Landau critical value,
p<p_{c}
. Here, we can gain insights into the asymptotic coherence dynamics by expanding (
16
) in orders of
1/t
. As outlined in

Most strikingly, we find that the formation time diverges when the impurity momentum approaches the Landau critical momentum

According to the above discussion, the discovered critical slowdown is linked to forward emission of phonons and can be understood as follows.
As suggested by the form of (
16
), the formation time
t_{\rm f}
is generally determined by how fast the described off-shell scattering processes with energy costs
\Delta E_{{\bf p},{\bf k}}
dephase, whereby the dominant contributions arise from energy differences around
\Delta E_{{\bf p},{\bf k}}\approx 0
. As illustrated in figure
6
, for impurity momenta below
p_{c}
this corresponds to long-wavelength excitations with wave vectors around
k\approx 0
. As the impurity momentum approaches
p_{c}
, the linear slope of
\Delta E_{{\bf p},{\bf k}}
with respect to momentum of forward emitted phonons steadily decreases, such that the corresponding virtual scattering events continue to add in phase for longer and longer times. For
p=p_{c}
, the slope eventually vanishes, thus implying a divergence of the formation time, as seen in figure
1
(b).
Above
p_{c}
,
\Delta E_{{\bf p},{\bf k}}
also vanishes for finite phonon momenta, as shown in figure
6
. This enables the emission of Cherenkov radiation with
\Delta E_{{\bf p},{\bf k}}=0
and leads to finite steady state damping with the rate (
24
). The approach to this asymptotic behaviour, however, is again limited by the dephasing of virtual scattering events around
k\approx 0
and is, therefore, slowed down when the impurity momentum approaches
p_{c}
from above in the same way as for
p<p_{c}
.

An intuitive picture for the resulting critical slowdown derives from the developed notion that the polaron arises from decoherence driven by the dissipation of phonons. Hereby the propagation of generated phonons away from the impurity transmits information about its presence and thereby diminishes the coherence
C_{p}
. This effective measurement and decoherence process, however, becomes inefficient as the impurity speed approaches the speed of sound
c=p_{c}/m
. In fact, when the impurity moves precisely with
c
, forward emitted phonons propagate at the same speed and cannot be dissipated away from the impurity (see figure
1
(b)), such that decoherence becomes arrested. In this case, forward scattering of phonons turns into the bottleneck for impurity relaxation and ultimately causes a critical slowdown of polaron formation when approaching the Landau critical momentum
p_{c}=mc
.

8
Conclusion

In summary, we have studied the non-equilibrium dynamics of a quantum impurity immersed into a Bose-Einstein condensate. By tracing the dynamics of the impurity coherence via a master equation approach we could identify three distinct dynamical regimes from a stretched exponential initial coherence decay and a subsequent phonon-driven relaxation to the final formation of the polaron steady state (figure
1
(a)). While the underlying hierarchy of associated timescales was shown to hold for arbitrary impurity momenta, the ultimate formation of the polaron undergoes a critical slowdown when approaching the speed of sound of the condensate (figure
1
(b)).

This established link between the equilibrium properties of the condensate and the dynamical critical behaviour of the non-equilibrium impurity state raises a number of open questions. For example it suggest that a similar slowdown might occur in condensates at finite temperature
[
46
]
and cause extraordinary long polaron formation times close to the critical temperature for Bose-Einstein condensation. Clarifying this question would indeed be important for future experiments on the Bose polaron at finite temperatures and across the BEC phase transition.
For the parameters of a recent experiment
[
16
]
, in which Bose polarons have been created and studied by driving the
(F=1,M_{F}=-1)\rightarrow(F=1,m_{F}=0)
hyperfine transition of a
39
K BEC, we obtain for our relevant timescales
t_{\rm B}\approx 0.2
ms,
t_{0}\approx 14
ms and
t_{\rm f}\approx 1
ms for
a=20{a_{\text{B}}}
and
p=0
. It turns out that the shortest timescale,
t_{\rm B}=0.2
ms, is just about the duration of the microwave excitation pulse used in
[
16
]
, such that the predictions of the present work should be observable with present technology, enabling direct experimental access to the non-equilibrium properties of the Bose polaron.

The results of this work should also be relevant to other systems and similar problems, such as the decoherence of molecular rotational states in superfluid helium droplets
[
47
]
, whose equilibrium properties have been linked to quantum impurity physics in recent theoretical work
[
48
,
49
,
50
]
. Following these ideas, the theoretical framework described in the present work appears also applicable for the description of the rotational dynamics of such molecules
[
47
]
. While the corresponding decoherence timescales are expected to be much shorter than for cold atom systems, they can be well resolved using fs-laser spectroscopy
[
47
]
.

The process of impurity decoherence is intrinsic to the formation of the polaron. Therefore, understanding its interplay with coherent driving of internal impurity states is also important for the quality of light matter interfaces involving polaron physics. Examples include the storage
[
51
]
and propagation
[
52
]
of quantum light in strongly interacting nonlinear media
[
53
]
or atomic BECs coupled to an optical cavity
[
54
]
.

While the derivation of analytical results and scaling relations of this work has been possible in the limit of weak impurity interactions, the central results of these derivations are of more general applicability.
For example, the found universal short-time behaviour
|C_{p}(t)/C_{p}(0)|\approx 1-\sqrt{t/t_{0}}
applies to arbitrary strong impurity-boson interactions and remains valid beyond the Fröhlich model. Moreover, the presented theory indicates that in the limit of an ideal Bose gas, the stretched exponential decoherence given by (
22
) persists for all times of the impurity dynamics. Most importantly, the discovered divergence of the formation time for moving impurities around the Landau critical momentum could be traced back to the suppression of phonon emission when the impurity speed approaches the speed of sound. We note that this mechanism does neither rely on the presence of weak interactions nor on the specific simplified form of the Fröhlich model, (
5
). While higher-order interaction effects can shift the precise value of the corresponding impurity momentum, e.g. through the renormalization of the polaron mass
[
32
]
, the discovered critical slowdown of polaron formation should thus occur for any interaction strength including the regime of strong impurity coupling. Future cold atom experiments on the momentum-resolved impurity dynamics will make it possible to put these predictions to test.

\Gamma_{0}(\tilde{t})=-\frac{2\sqrt{2}}{{\pi}{t_{\text{B}}}}\frac{a^{2}}{{a_{\text{B}}}\xi}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}\Phi\frac{\Phi^{2}}{(1+2\Phi)^{5/2}}{\rm e}^{-i\Phi\tilde{t}}.

The problem has thus been reduced to calculating the above Fourier transform. A primitive to the integrand is found in Mathematica. With a bit of rewriting we then arrive at the result

\displaystyle\Gamma_{0}(\tilde{t})=

\displaystyle-\frac{2\sqrt{2}}{{\pi}{t_{\text{B}}}}\frac{a^{2}}{{a_{\text{B}}}\xi}\left[\frac{(1+i)\sqrt{\pi}}{24\sqrt{\tilde{t}}}\left(i\tilde{t}^{2}+6\tilde{t}-3i\right){\rm e}^{i\tilde{t}/2}\right.

\displaystyle\times\left.\left(1-{\rm erf}\left(\frac{1+i}{2}\sqrt{\tilde{t}}\right)\right)-\frac{5+i\tilde{t}}{12}\right],

(39)

with
{\rm erf}
the error function. This has also been checked numerically. It is also possible to achieve an analytical expression for the integral of
\Gamma_{0}(t)
. Integrating (
39
) we get

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}{\rm d}s\;\Gamma_{0}(s)=

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{2}}{3\pi}\frac{a^{2}}{{a_{\text{B}}}\xi}\left[\tilde{t}+i(i\tilde{t}+3)\frac{1+i}{2}\sqrt{\pi\tilde{t}}\;{\rm e}^{i\tilde{t}/2}\right.

\displaystyle\times\left.\left(1-{\rm erf}\left(\frac{1+i}{2}\sqrt{\tilde{t}}\right)\right)\right]

\displaystyle\simeq i\frac{2\sqrt{2}}{3\pi}\frac{a^{2}}{{a_{\text{B}}}\xi}\left(1+\frac{3}{\tilde{t}^{2}}\right),

(40)

where the last expression is accurate
only
asymptotically to order
\tilde{t}^{-2}=({t_{\text{B}}}/t)^{2}
.

Appendix E
Damping in the long time limit

We calculate the polaron life-time,
1/(2\tau_{p})=\lim_{t\to\infty}{\rm Im}\Gamma_{p}(t)
. We can write the damping as

{\rm Im}\Gamma_{p}(t)={n_{\text{B}}}{\mathcal{T}}^{2}\int\frac{{\rm d}^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{\varepsilon^{\rm B}_{\bf k}}{E_{\bf k}}{\rm Re}\int_{0}^{t}{\rm d}s\;{\rm e}^{+i(\varepsilon_{{\bf p}}-\varepsilon_{{\bf p}-{\bf k}}-E_{{\bf k}})s}.

For
t\!\!\to\!\!\infty
the temporal integral yields
\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}s\;{\rm e}^{+ixs}={\rm Pr}(-i/x)+\pi\delta(x)
, where Pr stands for the principal value and
x=\varepsilon_{{\bf p}}-\varepsilon_{{\bf p}-{\bf k}}-E_{{\bf k}}
. Taking the real part of this expression gives the damping rate at long times

{\rm Im}\Gamma_{p}(\infty)=\frac{1}{2\tau_{p}}={n_{\text{B}}}{\mathcal{T}}^{2}\int\frac{{\rm d}^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{\varepsilon^{\rm B}_{\bf k}}{E_{\bf k}}\pi\delta(\varepsilon_{{\bf p}}-\varepsilon_{{\bf p}+{\bf k}}-E_{{\bf k}}),

with
\tau_{p}
the polaron life-time. For momenta below the Landau critical momentum,
p<p_{c}=mc
, the integral is 0. For
p>p_{c}
the integral can be evaluated analytically to yield

\frac{{t_{\text{B}}}}{2\tau_{p}}=\frac{(1+1/\nu)^{2}}{4\sqrt{2}}\frac{a^{2}}{{a_{\text{B}}}\xi}\frac{{\tilde{k}}_{\rm max}\sqrt{1+{\tilde{k}}_{\rm max}^{2}}-{\rm arcsinh}({\tilde{k}}_{\rm max})}{p/p_{c}},

(41)

for
p>p_{c}
, where we use
{\tilde{p}}_{c}=p_{c}\xi/\sqrt{2}=\nu/2
and

{\tilde{k}}_{\max}=\frac{\nu^{2}}{\nu^{2}-1}\left(\sqrt{\left(\frac{p}{p_{c}}\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{\nu^{2}}-1}-\frac{1}{\nu}\frac{p}{p_{c}}\right),

(42)

with
{\tilde{k}}_{\max}=(p/p_{c}-p_{c}/p)/2
in the equal mass case
\nu=1
. We hereby have a closed form expression for the long time damping for all mass ratios.

Appendix F
Momentum dependent residue

We calculate the quasiparticle residue as given in (
25
)

Z_{p}={\rm e}^{-\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}t({\rm Im}\Gamma_{p}(t)-1/2\tau_{p})}.

(43)

Using that
\partial\Phi_{{\tilde{p}}}({\tilde{k}})/\partial\cos(\theta)=-2{\tilde{p}}{\tilde{k}}/\nu
we can perform the angle integral in (
38
) and get

\displaystyle\!\!{\rm Im}\Gamma_{p}(t)=

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{2}(1+1/\nu)^{2}}{4\pi{t_{\text{B}}}}\frac{a^{2}}{{a_{\text{B}}}\xi}\frac{\nu}{2{\tilde{p}}}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}{\tilde{k}}\frac{{\tilde{k}}^{2}}{\sqrt{1+{\tilde{k}}^{2}}}\left[{\rm Si}\left(\Phi^{+}_{{\tilde{p}}}({\tilde{k}})\tilde{t}\right)-{\rm Si}\left(\Phi^{-}_{{\tilde{p}}}({\tilde{k}})\tilde{t}\right)\right],\!\!

(44)

with
\tilde{t}=t/{t_{\text{B}}}
,
{\tilde{k}}=k\xi/\sqrt{2}
,
\nu=m/{m_{\rm B}}
,
{\rm Si}(x)=\int_{0}^{x}{\rm d}u\sin(u)/u
the sine integral, and
\Phi^{\pm}_{{\tilde{p}}}({\tilde{k}})={\tilde{k}}\sqrt{{\tilde{k}}^{2}+1}+({\tilde{k}}^{2}\pm 2{\tilde{k}}{\tilde{p}})/\nu
. Then integrating the sine integrals we get

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}{\rm d}s\;{\rm Im}\Gamma_{p}(s)=\frac{\sqrt{2}(1+1/\nu)^{2}}{4\pi}\frac{a^{2}}{{a_{\text{B}}}\xi}\frac{\nu}{2{\tilde{p}}}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}{\tilde{k}}\frac{{\tilde{k}}^{2}}{\sqrt{1+{\tilde{k}}^{2}}}

\displaystyle\times\left[\tilde{t}\left({\rm Si}\left(\Phi^{+}_{{\tilde{p}}}({\tilde{k}})\tilde{t}\right)-{\rm Si}\left(\Phi^{-}_{{\tilde{p}}}({\tilde{k}})\tilde{t}\right)\right)+\frac{\cos\left(\Phi^{+}_{{\tilde{p}}}({\tilde{k}})\tilde{t}\right)-1}{\Phi^{+}_{{\tilde{p}}}({\tilde{k}})}-\frac{\cos\left(\Phi^{-}_{{\tilde{p}}}({\tilde{k}})\tilde{t}\right)-1}{\Phi^{-}_{{\tilde{p}}}({\tilde{k}})}\right].

We recognize the first two terms in the integral as
{\rm Im}\Gamma_{p}(t)t
which approaches
t/(2\tau_{p})
for

. We therefore get

\displaystyle\ln Z_{p}=

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}s\left[{\rm Im}\Gamma_{p}(s)-\frac{1}{2\tau_{p}}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\lim_{\tilde{t}\to\infty}\frac{\sqrt{2}(1+1/\nu)^{2}}{4\pi}\frac{a^{2}}{{a_{\text{B}}}\xi}\frac{\nu}{2{\tilde{p}}}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}{\tilde{k}}\frac{{\tilde{k}}^{2}}{\sqrt{1+{\tilde{k}}^{2}}}

\displaystyle\times\left[\frac{\cos\left(\Phi^{+}_{{\tilde{p}}}({\tilde{k}})\tilde{t}\right)-1}{\Phi^{+}_{{\tilde{p}}}({\tilde{k}})}-\frac{\cos\left(\Phi^{-}_{{\tilde{p}}}({\tilde{k}})\tilde{t}\right)-1}{\Phi^{-}_{{\tilde{p}}}({\tilde{k}})}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\sqrt{2}(1+1/\nu)^{2}}{2\pi}\frac{a^{2}}{{a_{\text{B}}}\xi}\;{\rm Pr}\!\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!{\rm d}{\tilde{k}}\frac{{\tilde{k}}}{\sqrt{1+{\tilde{k}}^{2}}}\frac{1}{\left(\sqrt{1+{\tilde{k}}^{2}}+{\tilde{k}}/\nu\right)^{2}-\left(2{\tilde{p}}/\nu\right)^{2}}.

(45)

In the second equality we use that
(\cos(\Phi\tilde{t})-1)/\Phi=-\int_{0}^{\tilde{t}}{\rm d}\tilde{s}\sin(\Phi\tilde{t})={\rm Im}\int_{0}^{\tilde{t}}{\rm d}\tilde{s}\exp(-i\Phi\tilde{t})\to{\rm Pr}(-1/\Phi)
, for
\tilde{t}\to\infty
, with Pr the principal value. The resulting integral above can readily be solved in the equal mass case
\nu=1
. Here, using
x=\sqrt{1+{\tilde{k}}^{2}}+{\tilde{k}}
we get

\displaystyle\ln Z_{p}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\pi}\frac{a^{2}}{{a_{\text{B}}}\xi}\;{\rm Pr}\int_{1}^{\infty}{\rm d}x\frac{x^{2}-1}{x^{2}(x^{2}-(2{\tilde{p}})^{2})}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\pi}\frac{a^{2}}{{a_{\text{B}}}\xi}\frac{1}{(2{\tilde{p}})^{2}}\left[1+\frac{(2{\tilde{p}})^{2}-1}{2{\tilde{p}}}{\rm Re}\;{\rm arctanh}\left(\frac{1}{2{\tilde{p}}}\right)\right].

The integral is performed using the transformation
\tanh(\theta)=x/2{\tilde{p}}
. Note that
{\rm Re}\;{\rm arctanh}(1/x)={\rm Re}\;{\rm arctanh}(x)
. The final result is then

\displaystyle\ln Z_{p}=

\displaystyle-\!\!\frac{a^{2}}{{a_{\text{B}}}\xi}\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\pi}\!\left(\frac{p_{c}}{p}\right)^{\!\!2}\!\!\left[\!1+\frac{\left(\frac{p}{p_{c}}\right)^{\!2}-1}{p/p_{c}}{\rm Re}\;{\rm arctanh}\left(\frac{p}{p_{c}}\right)\!\!\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{a^{2}}{{a_{\text{B}}}\xi}\;A_{p}(1),

(46)

thus defining
A_{p}(\nu)
for
\nu=1
. At

and

a limiting process must be performed. We have thus obtained an analytical result for the residue as a function of momentum in the equal mass case. We plot the result for different values of the boson-impurity scattering length in figure

. This exhibits an intriguing non-monotonic behaviour around
p=p_{c}
.

Appendix G
Asymptotic dynamics

We calculate the asymptotic behaviour of the coherence for general mass ratios,
\nu
, and momenta,
p
. It turns out that there are two regimes in which this behaves quite differently:
p<p_{c}
and
p>p_{c}
, where
p_{c}=mc
is the Landau critical momentum of the condensate.

\bf{p<p_{c}}
: We calculate
\int_{0}^{t}{\rm d}s\,\Gamma_{p}(s)
to leading order in
1/t
. We will see that

\int_{0}^{t}{\rm d}s\;\Gamma_{p}(s)\simeq\frac{a^{2}}{{a_{\text{B}}}\xi}\left(A_{p}(\nu)+B_{p}(\nu)\left(\frac{{t_{\text{B}}}}{t}\right)^{2}\right),

(48)

where
A_{p}(\nu)
is defined in (
46
). This analysis is detailed below.

Defining
w_{{\tilde{p}}}({\tilde{k}})={\tilde{k}}^{3}/(\sqrt{{\tilde{k}}^{2}+1}\Phi_{{\tilde{p}}}({\tilde{k}}))
, we can write the time-dependent rate coefficient (
32
) as

\Gamma_{p}(t)=\!-\frac{\sqrt{2}(1+1/\nu)^{2}}{4\pi{t_{\text{B}}}}\frac{a^{2}}{{a_{\text{B}}}\xi}\!\int{\rm d}\cos\theta{\rm d}{\tilde{k}}\;w_{{\tilde{p}}}({\tilde{k}}){\rm e}^{-i\Phi_{{\tilde{p}}}({\tilde{k}})\tilde{t}},

(49)

We wish to expand this integral asymptotically for
\tilde{t}=t/{t_{\text{B}}}\gg 1
. Specifically we investigate the
{\tilde{k}}
-integral and write

I_{\theta}=\int{\rm d}{\tilde{k}}\;w_{{\tilde{p}}}({\tilde{k}}){\rm e}^{-i\Phi_{{\tilde{p}}}({\tilde{k}})\tilde{t}}=\frac{1}{-i\tilde{t}}\int{\rm d}{\tilde{k}}\frac{w_{{\tilde{p}}}({\tilde{k}})}{\partial_{{\tilde{k}}}\Phi_{{\tilde{p}}}}\;\partial_{{\tilde{k}}}{\rm e}^{-i\Phi_{{\tilde{p}}}({\tilde{k}})\tilde{t}},\\

which is a simple rewritting valid as long as
\partial_{{\tilde{k}}}\Phi_{{\tilde{p}}}\neq 0
for all
{\tilde{k}}
. This turns out to be the case as long as we are below the critical momentum,
p<p_{c}=mc
, or in units of
\xi
:
{\tilde{p}}=p\xi/\sqrt{2}<\nu/2
. Then since the integral is now written as a function times the derivate of another we can use integration by parts. Repeating this procedure successively gives a systematic asymptotic expansion in powers of
1/\tilde{t}
. It turns out that we have to go to order
1/\tilde{t}^{3}
to get a nonzero contribution

\displaystyle I_{\theta}

\displaystyle=-\frac{i}{\tilde{t}^{3}}\!\left.\frac{\partial_{{\tilde{k}}}\left(\frac{\partial_{{\tilde{k}}}(w_{{\tilde{p}}}/\partial_{{\tilde{k}}}\Phi_{{\tilde{p}}})}{\partial_{{\tilde{k}}}\Phi_{{\tilde{p}}}}\right)}{\partial_{{\tilde{k}}}\Phi_{{\tilde{p}}}}{\rm e}^{-i\Phi_{{\tilde{p}}}({\tilde{k}})\tilde{t}}\right|_{{\tilde{k}}=0}^{{\tilde{k}}=\infty}\!\!+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\tilde{t}^{4}}\right)=\frac{i}{t^{3}}\frac{2}{(1-2{\tilde{p}}\cos\theta/\nu)^{4}}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\tilde{t}^{4}}\right).

In the second equality we use that at low
{\tilde{k}}
the functions asymptotically behave like
w_{{\tilde{p}}}({\tilde{k}})\to k^{2}/(1-2{\tilde{p}}\cos\theta/\nu)
and
\partial_{{\tilde{k}}}\Phi_{{\tilde{p}}}\to 1-2{\tilde{p}}\cos\theta/\nu
.
Inserting this in (
49
), and performing the angle integral we then get the asymptote

\displaystyle{\rm Im}\Gamma_{p}(t){t_{\text{B}}}\simeq

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\tilde{t}^{3}}\frac{\sqrt{2}(1+1/\nu)^{2}}{6\pi}\frac{a^{2}}{{a_{\text{B}}}\xi}\frac{\nu}{2{\tilde{p}}}\left(\frac{1}{(1-2{\tilde{p}}/\nu)^{3}}-\frac{1}{(1+2{\tilde{p}}/\nu)^{3}}\right).

Integrating this and using
\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}s\;{\rm Im}\Gamma_{p}(s)=A_{p}(\nu)\,a^{2}/({a_{\text{B}}}\xi)
we get

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}{\rm d}s\;{\rm Im}\Gamma_{p}(s)=\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}s\;{\rm Im}\Gamma_{p}(s)-\int_{t}^{\infty}{\rm d}s\;{\rm Im}\Gamma_{p}(s)

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{a^{2}}{{a_{\text{B}}}\xi}\left[A_{p}(\nu)+\left(\frac{{t_{\text{B}}}}{t}\right)^{2}\frac{\sqrt{2}(1+1/\nu)^{2}}{12\pi}\frac{p_{c}}{p}\left(\frac{1}{(1-p/p_{c})^{3}}-\frac{1}{(1+p/p_{c})^{3}}\right)\right].

In the first line we use
\tilde{t}=t/{t_{\text{B}}}
,
{\tilde{p}}=p\xi/\sqrt{2}
, and
p_{c}\xi/\sqrt{2}=\nu/2
. Comparing with (
48
) we get

B_{p}(\nu)=\frac{\sqrt{2}(1+1/\nu)^{2}}{12\pi}\frac{p_{c}}{p}\left(\frac{1}{(1-p/p_{c})^{3}}-\frac{1}{(1+p/p_{c})^{3}}\right).

(50)

Further, taking the
p=0
limit in the above, we get
B_{0}(\nu=1)=2\sqrt{2}/\pi
. This fits perfectly with the asymptotic form derived from an analytical expression for the integral in (
40
), which serves as a good check of our current expression. We have also successfully checked the asymptote numerically. This asymptotic expansion is accurate for
t/{t_{\text{B}}}\gg\sqrt{B_{p}/A_{p}}
. Evaluating
I_{\theta}
to fourth order yields the dominant contribution to
{\rm Re}\Gamma_{p}
at long times

\displaystyle{\rm Re}\Gamma_{p}(t)\simeq

\displaystyle\left(\frac{{t_{\text{B}}}}{t}\right)^{\!\!4}\frac{3\sqrt{2}(1+1/\nu)^{2}}{2\pi\nu{t_{\text{B}}}}\frac{a^{2}}{{a_{\text{B}}}\xi}\frac{p_{c}}{p}\left(\frac{1}{(1-p/p_{c})^{5}}-\frac{1}{(1+p/p_{c})^{5}}\right).

(51)

\bf{p>p_{c}}
: A key feature for the above calculation is that the phase
\Phi_{{\tilde{p}}}({\tilde{k}})
has no
stationary
point, i.e.
\partial_{{\tilde{k}}}\Phi_{{\tilde{p}}}\neq 0
for all
{\tilde{k}}
. For
p>p_{c}
this is no longer true. As we shall now see, this leads to a different asymptotic behaviour of
\Gamma_{p}
.

The strategy in this case is slightly different. We take (
44
) and use the large argument asymptote of the sine integral
{\rm Si}(x)\simeq\pi/2-\cos(x)/x

\displaystyle\!\!{\rm Im}\Gamma_{p}(t){t_{\text{B}}}\sim

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{2}(1+1/\nu)^{2}}{4\pi}\frac{a^{2}}{{a_{\text{B}}}\xi}\frac{\nu}{2{\tilde{p}}}\int\!{\rm d}{\tilde{k}}\frac{{\tilde{k}}^{2}}{\sqrt{1+{\tilde{k}}^{2}}}\!\!\left[\frac{\cos(\Phi^{-}_{{\tilde{p}}}({\tilde{k}})\tilde{t})}{\Phi^{-}_{{\tilde{p}}}({\tilde{k}})\tilde{t}}-\frac{\cos(\Phi^{+}_{{\tilde{p}}}({\tilde{k}})\tilde{t})}{\Phi^{+}_{{\tilde{p}}}({\tilde{k}})\tilde{t}}\right].\!\!

(52)

The phase
\Phi^{-}_{{\tilde{p}}}({\tilde{k}})
has a stationary point
{\tilde{k}}_{0}
, i.e.
\partial_{{\tilde{k}}}\Phi^{-}_{{\tilde{p}}}|_{{\tilde{k}}={\tilde{k}}0}=0
, defined by the equation

0=\sqrt{{\tilde{k}}_{0}^{2}+1}+{\tilde{k}}_{0}^{2}/\sqrt{{\tilde{k}}_{0}^{2}+1}+\frac{2}{\nu}({\tilde{k}}_{0}-{\tilde{p}}).

(53)

At the end of this section we will calculate
{\tilde{k}}_{0}
in the limit
p\gtrsim p_{c}
. In general it turns out however, that this equation is quite involved to solve. Because of this stationary point the term in (
52
) corresponding to
\Phi^{-}
contains the leading order contribution at long times. We let
w^{-}_{{\tilde{p}}}({\tilde{k}})={\tilde{k}}^{2}/(\sqrt{1+{\tilde{k}}^{2}}\Phi^{-}_{{\tilde{p}}}({\tilde{k}}))
and calculate the integral

\displaystyle I_{-}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}{\tilde{k}}\;w^{-}_{{\tilde{p}}}({\tilde{k}}){\rm e}^{-i\Phi^{-}_{{\tilde{p}}}({\tilde{k}})\tilde{t}}\simeq{\rm e}^{-i\Phi^{-}_{{\tilde{p}}}({\tilde{k}}_{0})\tilde{t}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}{\rm d}{\tilde{k}}\;w^{-}_{{\tilde{p}}}({\tilde{k}}){\rm e}^{-i\Phi^{\prime\prime}_{{\tilde{p}}}/2\;(k-k_{0})^{2}\tilde{t}}

\displaystyle\simeq{\rm e}^{-i\Phi^{-}_{{\tilde{p}}}({\tilde{k}}_{0})\tilde{t}}w^{-}_{{\tilde{p}}}({\tilde{k}}_{0})\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{\Phi^{\prime\prime}_{p}\tilde{t}}}{\rm e}^{i\pi/4}.

First, we expand the phase to second order in
{\tilde{k}}-{\tilde{k}}_{0}
:
\Phi^{-}_{{\tilde{p}}}({\tilde{k}})\simeq\Phi^{-}_{{\tilde{p}}}({\tilde{k}}_{0})+\Phi^{\prime\prime}_{p}(k-k_{0})^{2}/2
. This is valid to do at long times, because away from
{\tilde{k}}_{0}
the heavy oscillations lead to rapid cancellation. We also use this to expand the integral to
-\infty
. We use the rapid cancellation simply to evaluate the function in front at
{\tilde{k}}={\tilde{k}}_{0}
. Finally, we evaluate the integral in the last step. Taking the real part of
I_{-}
and dividing by
\tilde{t}
we get the leading order contribution to the damping we were after

\displaystyle{\rm Im}\Gamma_{p}\simeq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\tau_{p}}+\frac{(1+1/\nu)^{2}}{2\sqrt{\pi}}\frac{a^{2}}{{a_{\text{B}}}\xi}\frac{t_{\rm B}^{1/2}}{t^{3/2}}\frac{p_{c}}{p}\frac{w^{-}_{{\tilde{p}}}({\tilde{k}}_{0})}{\sqrt{\Phi^{\prime\prime}_{{\tilde{p}}}}}\cos\left(\Phi^{-}_{{\tilde{p}}}({\tilde{k}}_{0})t/{t_{\text{B}}}+\pi/4\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\tau_{p}}+\frac{a^{2}}{{a_{\text{B}}}\xi}D_{p}(\nu)\frac{t_{\rm B}^{1/2}}{t^{3/2}}\cos\left(\omega_{p}t+\pi/4\right).

(54)

Here we have included the nonzero damping rate found in (
41
), which the stationary phase approximation fails to calculate. We also define the expansion coefficient,
D_{p}
and frequency of oscillation,
\omega_{p}
according to

\displaystyle D_{p}(\nu)

\displaystyle=\frac{(1+1/\nu)^{2}}{2\sqrt{\pi}}\frac{p_{c}}{p}\frac{w^{-}_{{\tilde{p}}}({\tilde{k}}_{0})}{\sqrt{\Phi^{\prime\prime}_{{\tilde{p}}}}},

\displaystyle\omega_{p}

\displaystyle=\Phi^{-}_{{\tilde{p}}}({\tilde{k}}_{0})/{t_{\text{B}}}.

(55)

Numerically, we have found that this expansion works very well after a few oscillations:
\omega_{p}t>1
. Remember that
\partial_{{\tilde{k}}}\Phi^{-}_{{\tilde{p}}}|_{{\tilde{k}}={\tilde{k}}_{0}}=0
and that
{\bf k}_{0}
is parallel to
{\bf p}
in
\Phi^{-}
. This explicitly shows that
\omega_{p}
is the energy difference of the impurity before and after it has emitted a phonon in the forward direction. Further, this is at the minimum of the energy difference. For
p\to p_{c}^{+}
,
k_{0}=\nu(p/p_{c}-1)/2
and in turn
\omega_{p}{t_{\text{B}}}=\nu(p/p_{c}-1)^{2}/4
.

Appendix H
Divergence of formation time for a light impurity

We analyze the mass dependence of the polaron formation time,
t_{\rm f}
. This is defined and calculated as explained in Sec.
7
of the main text. In figure
10
we show the result for an impurity at zero momentum,
p=0
. We clearly observe a divergence in the limit of a very light impurity,
\nu\to 0
. The underlying physical reason for this divergence is the enhanced recoil experienced by a light impurity. In terms of the dephasing picture developed in this article, see Sec.
7
, the increasing formation time can be understood from the low momentum behaviour of the energy cost of generating phonons,
\Delta E_{{\bf p},{\bf k}}=E_{\bf k}+\varepsilon_{{\bf p}-{\bf k}}-\varepsilon_{\bf p}
, which for zero impurity momentum,
p=0
, and in units of
{t_{\text{B}}}
reads

\Delta E_{p=0,{\bf k}}\cdot{t_{\text{B}}}={\tilde{k}}\left(\sqrt{{\tilde{k}}^{2}+1}+\frac{{\tilde{k}}}{\nu}\right)\simeq{\tilde{k}}=\frac{\nu}{2}\frac{k}{p_{c}},

(56)

valid for
k\ll p_{c}
. We hereby see, that the
slope
of
\Delta E
decreases with decreasing mass ratio
\nu=m/{m_{\rm B}}
. Much like the slowdown around the critical momentum studied in Sec.
7
, this diminished slope means that the scattering events for low momenta continue to add in phase for a longer time, thereby extending the period of time where damping takes place and increasing the formation time.

{a_{\text{B}}}

, as along as we operate in the weakly interacting regime. The formation time diverges in the limit of a very light impurity, and quickly saturates to a value on the order of

.
