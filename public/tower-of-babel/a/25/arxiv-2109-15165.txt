Euclidean numbers and numerosities
Vieri Benci, Lorenzo Luperi Baglini

Abstract
Several different versions of the theory of numerosities have been introduced in the literature. Here, we unify these approaches in a consistent frame through the notion of set of labels, relating numerosities with the Kiesler field of Euclidean numbers. This approach allows to easily introduce, by means of numerosities, ordinals and their natural operations, as well as the Lebesgue measure as a counting measure on the reals.

2.1
Non Archimedean fields

Here, we recall the basic definitions and some facts regarding non

{\mathbb{K}}

Definition 1

Let

•

\xi

An ordered field

If

2.3
Labelled sets

The notion of labelled set has been introduced in
[

]

[

to construct a numerosity theory for countable sets. Here we

Definition 8
.

We call
label set
a family of sets
\mathfrak{B}\subset\mathfrak{L}
such that

(i)

\forall\mathfrak{s,t}\in\mathfrak{B,}\ \mathfrak{s}\cap\mathfrak{t},\ \mathfrak{s}\cup\mathfrak{t}\in\mathfrak{B}
;

(ii)

\forall\mathfrak{s}\in\mathfrak{B},\ \mathfrak{s}\cap\mathfrak{L}=\varnothing;

(iii)

\bigcup_{\mathfrak{s}\in\mathfrak{B}}\mathbb{V}\left(\mathfrak{s}\right)=\Lambda
.

Requirement (i) gives to

a lattice structure, whilst
requirement (ii) entails that the elements of a label are either atoms or
infinite sets.

Having fixed the notion of ”labels”, we can now introduce the notion of
”labelling”:

Let
\mathfrak{B}

\ell(a)=\bigcap_{\mu\in I_{a}}\mu,

. For every

.

Roughly speaking, the label of an object

whose elements allow to define

There is plenty of sets of labels: just set

4
Ordinal numbers and numerosities

In this section we will select a subset of the numerosities which we will
call ordinal numerosities (or simply ordinals). This set, equipped with its
natural order relation
<
, is isomorphic to the set of ordinal numbers. In
Section
4.2
we will show that this correspondence is deeper than
expected since it preserves also the natural operations between ordinals.

4.1
The ordinal numerosities

Let
\mathbf{Num}
be the set of numerosities.

Definition 17
.

The set
\mathbf{Ord}\subset\mathbf{Num}
of ordinal
numerosities is defined as follows:
\tau\in\mathbf{Ord}
if and only if

\tau=\mathfrak{num}\left(\Omega_{\tau}\right),

where

\Omega_{\tau}=\left\{x\in\mathbf{Ord\ |\ }x<\tau\right\}.

It is easy to see by transfinite induction that this is a good definition.
In fact, it is immediate to check that

•

;

•

if

then

(and hence

).

Moreover, if
\tau_{k}=\mathfrak{num}\left(\Omega_{k}\right),

(
\left|K\right|<\kappa
) are ordinal numerosities, then

\tau:=\mathfrak{num}\left(\mathop{\displaystyle\bigcup}\limits_{k\in K}\Omega_{k}\right)\in\mathbf{Ord.}

In fact, this holds as
\mathop{\displaystyle\bigcup}\limits_{k\in K}\Omega_{k}=\{x\in\mathbf{Ord}\mid x<\tau\}
: the inclusion
\mathop{\displaystyle\bigcup}\limits_{k\in K}\Omega_{k}\subseteq\{x\in\mathbf{Ord}\mid x<\tau\}
holds trivially, as if
x\in\mathop{\displaystyle\bigcup}\limits_{k\in K}\Omega_{k}
then

and

for some

, and so

; conversely, if

is
such that

we would
have that
\Omega_{x}\supseteq\mathop{\displaystyle\bigcup}\limits_{k\in K}\Omega_{k}
, and so
by taking numerosities we would get

, which is absurd.

Definition 18

If
\tau_{k},

k\in K,
(
\left|K\right|<\kappa

we set

\underset{k\in K}{\sup}\tau_{k}=\mathfrak{num}\left(\mathop{\displaystyle\bigcup}\limits_{k\in K}\Omega_{\tau_{k}}\right),

where
\tau_{k}=\mathfrak{num}\left(\Omega_{\tau_{k}}\right).

equal or greater than every

namely

and

\forall k\in K,\ \tau\geq\tau_{k};

\forall k\in K,\ \forall\xi\geq\tau_{k}\Rightarrow\xi\geq\tau.

(11)

However

greater or equal
to every
\tau_{k}\mathbf{.}
In fact, as we have seen, if

is not a maximum, there are numerosities

greater that every

and smaller than

Our construction of the ordinal numbers is similar to the construction of

is the set of all
the Von Neumann ordinals contained in
\tau

is the numerosity of the set of ordinals smaller than

Obviously, not all numerosities are ordinals: for example,

is not an ordinal. In fact, if
\alpha=\mathfrak{num}\left(\mathbb{N}\right)
were an ordinal then:

\displaystyle\alpha

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathfrak{num(}\left\{x\in\mathbf{Ord}\ |\ x<\mathfrak{num}\left(\mathbb{N}\right)\right\})=\mathfrak{num}(\mathbb{N}_{0})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathfrak{num}(\mathbb{N}\cup\left\{0\right\})=\alpha+1.

In a similar way, one can prove that no infinite numerosity smaller than

\alpha+1=\mathfrak{num}\left(\mathbb{N}_{0}\right)=\mathfrak{num}\left(\{x\in\mathbf{Ord}\ |\ x<\alpha\}\right).

Actually

is the smallest infinite ordinal. From now on, we will
call it

As we expect,
\mathbf{Ord}
is a well ordered set; in fact is

the minimum is given by

\min\ E=\sup\left\{x\in\mathbf{Ord}\ |\ \forall a\in E,\ x\leq a\right\}.

4.2

In this section we will show that the set of ordinal numerosities is closed

sums and products of ordinal numerosities and the natural operations between
Cantor ordinals.

First, we start by showing that the operations between numerosities are

7.1

Given a numerosity theory and a set

where
\gamma\in\mathbb{N}^{\ast}

\mu_{\gamma}

. In this case we will say that

Theorem 40
.

The numerosity measure
\mu_{\gamma}
satisfies the following
properties:

(i)

it is finitely additive: for all sets

\mu_{\gamma}\left(A\cup B\right)=\mu_{\gamma}\left(A\right)+\mu_{\gamma}\left(B\right)-\mu_{\gamma}\left(A\cap B\right);

(ii)

of a set

\mu_{\gamma}\left(A\right)\geq\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\mu_{\gamma}\left(A_{n}\right).

Proof.

(i) This is a trivial consequence of the additivity of the
numerosity.

(ii) By Theorem
16
, we have that for all
N\in\mathbb{N}
,

\mathfrak{num}\left(A\right)\geq\mathfrak{num}\left(\mathop{\displaystyle\bigcup}\limits_{n=0}^{N}A_{n}\right)=\sum_{n=0}^{N}\mathfrak{num}\left(A_{n}\right),

hence

st\left(\frac{\mathfrak{num}\left(A\right)}{\gamma}\right)\geq st\left(\sum_{n=0}^{N}\frac{\mathfrak{num}\left(A_{n}\right)}{\gamma}\right)=\sum_{n=0}^{N}st\left(\frac{\mathfrak{num}\left(A_{n}\right)}{\gamma}\right);

therefore,

\mu_{\gamma}\left(A\right)\geq\sum_{n=0}^{N}\mu_{\gamma}\left(A_{n}\right).

The conclusion follows taking the Cauchy limit in the above inequality for

.

The arbitrariness of
\varepsilon
gives the desired inequality

\mu_{\beta}\left(E\right)\leq\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\mu_{\beta}\left(E_{j}\right).\enskip\hbox{\enskip\vrule width=4.0pt,height=6.0pt,depth=1.5pt\hskip 1.0pt}

We can now prove our desired final result:

Theorem 43
.

\mu_{\beta}(E)=\mu_{L}(E)
for all Lebesgue measurable
sets
E\subseteq\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

By Theorems
40
,
41
and by Proposition
42
we have that
\mu_{\beta}
, restricted to Lebesgue measurable sets, has the empty set property, it is countably additive (as it is both subadditive and superadditive), it is invariant under translation and it is normalized. Hence it must coincide with the Lebesgue measure.

The arguments above could be generalized to prove that for any measurable
set
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
we have that

m_{N}(A)=st\left(\frac{\mathfrak{num}\left(A\right)}{\beta^{N}}\right).

Similarly, we can define the ”fractal measure” any fractal set
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
as follows:

\mathfrak{m}_{d}(A)=st\left(\frac{\mathfrak{num}\left(A\right)}{\beta^{d}}\right),\ \ \ d\in\left[0,N\right].

We are not gonna study this fractal measure in detail here; however, it is
not difficult to chek that
\mathfrak{m}_{d}(A)
concides with the
normalized Hausdorff measure
H_{d}
.

7.3
Numerosity and nonstandard measures

It is well known that the Lebesgue measure can be realized using a counting
procedure based on hyperfinite sets: this is, e.g., at the core of the
construction of Loeb measures, which is the most known and used of such
constructions. Loeb measures were introduced in mid-70’s, see
[
20
]
;
see also
[
22
]
for an overview of Loeb methods and applications, and

[
23
]
for an overview of other applications of nonstandard analysis
in measure theory. To confront Loeb construction with our approach, here we
shortly recall Loeb construction following Goldblatt’s presentantion, see

[
18
]
, Section 16.8.

Let
N
be an infinite hypernatural number, and let
S=\{\frac{k}{N}\mid-N^{2}\leq k\leq N^{2},k
hyperinteger
\}
. Let
\wp_{I}(S)
the set of
internal subsets of
S
, and for every
A\in\wp_{I}(S)
let

m(A):=st\left(\frac{|A|}{N}\right),

where
|A|
denotes the internal cardinality of
A
. Then
m:\wp_{I}\left(S\right)\rightarrow[0,+\infty]
is a finitely additive measure on
\wp_{I}\left(S\right)
. The Loeb measure is obtained by means of the
usual Carathéodory extension procedure applied to
m
(we will denote
also the Loeb measure by
m
). What Loeb proved is that the Lebesgue measure
can be seen as a restriction of
m
, in the sense that for every Lebesgue
measurable set
X
the Lebesgue measure
m_{L}(X)
is equal to the Loeb
measure of the so-called pre-shadow
st^{-1}(X)
of
X
, namely

m_{L}(X)=st\left(m\left(st^{-1}\left(X\right)\right)\right),

where
st^{-1}(X)=\{\xi\in S\mid st(\xi)\in X\}
.

The similarity between our approach is that we have that, actually,
\mu_{\beta}
is obtained as the standard part of a quotient similar to Loeb’s
one. In fact,
\mu_{\beta}\left(A\right)=st\left(\frac{|A^{\ast}\cap\Gamma|}{|[0,1)^{\ast}\cap\Gamma|}\right)
, where:

1.

|\cdot|
denotes the internal cardinality of a set;

2.

\Gamma
is the hyperfinite set obtained by taking
\lim_{\lambda\uparrow\Lambda}\lambda\cap\mathbb{R}
.

However, in our approach the use of Carathéodory extension procedure, as
well as of pre-shadows, is substituted with the choice of a particular
labelling set, which can be equivalently seen as a particular choice of the
hyperfinite set used in the quotient. A similar result in a general
nonstandard setting was first obtained by Bernstein and Wattenberg (see
[
13
]
; see also
[
15
]
, Section 2, for a comparison of
Bernstein-Wattenberg’s result and Loeb measures), who in fact proved that
there exists hyperfinite subsets
S\subseteq[0,1]^{\ast}
such that
for all Lebesgue measurable
A\subseteq[0,1]

m_{L}(A)=st\left(\frac{|A^{\ast}\cap S|}{|S|}\right).

As we said before, Theorem
43
provides a new proof of the
above result by taking

S=\lim_{\lambda\uparrow\Lambda}\ \left(\lambda\cap\mathbb{R}\right).

Finally, the problem of the relationship between numerosities and Lebesgue
measure in general has been addressed in
[
7
,
8
]
. In these
papers, the authors introduced the notion of ”elementary numerosity” (see

[
7
]
, Definition 1.1), that we recall:

Definition
.

An elementary numerosity on a set
\Omega
is a function
n:\wp\left(\Omega\right)\rightarrow[0,+\infty)
defined on all subsets of
\Omega
, taking values in the non-negative part of an ordered field
\mathbb{F}\supseteq\mathbb{R}
, and such that the following two conditions are
satisfied:

1.

n({x})=1
for every point
x\in\Omega
;

2.

n(A\cup B)=n(A)+n(B)
whenever A and B are disjoint.

The main connection between the ”elementary numerosity” and Lebesgue measure
is given by the following result, which is one of the instances of Theorem
3.1 in
[
8
]
:

Theorem
.

There exists an elementary numerosity
\mathfrak{n}:\wp\left(\mathbb{R}\right)\rightarrow[0,+\infty)_{\mathbb{F}}
such that
m_{L}(X)=st\left(\frac{\mathfrak{n}\left(X\right)}{\mathfrak{n}\left([0,1)\right)}\right)
for every Lebesgue measurable set
X
.

Once again, Theorem
43
provides another proof of the above
result, as
\mathfrak{num}
, when restricted to
\wp\left(\mathbb{R}\right)
is, in fact, an elementary numerosity on
\mathbb{R}
.

The interest of Theorem
43
lies on the fact that it is based
on a numerosity theory which satisfies many other additional properties.
