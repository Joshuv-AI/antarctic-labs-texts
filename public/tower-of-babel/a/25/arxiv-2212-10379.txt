Strong Pedersen rigidity for coactions of compact groups
S. Kaliszewski, Tron Omland, John Quigg, Jonathan Turk

Abstract
. We prove a version of Pedersen’s outer conjugacy theorem for coactions of compact groups, which characterizes outer conjugate coactions of a compact group in terms of properties of the dual actions. In fact, we show that every isomorphism of a dual action comes from a unique outer conjugacy of a coaction, which in this context should be called strong Pedersen rigidity . We promote this to a category equivalence.

46L05, 46L55

1.
Introduction

Given an action
\alpha
of a locally compact group
G
on a
C^{*}
-algebra
A
,
one forms the crossed product
C^{*}
-algebra
A\rtimes_{\alpha}G
.
The question is then how much the crossed product remembers of the underlying dynamics,
assuming knowledge of the group
G
.
In general, if we only know the crossed product up to isomorphism,
we cannot say much about the underlying dynamics,
not even up to Morita equivalence.
Thus, we need some extra information about the crossed product,
chosen with an eye toward recovering the action up to some type of equivalence,
and this concept is often thought of as the rigidity of the dynamical system.

Pedersen
[
5
, Theorem 35]
proved that two actions
(A,\alpha)
and
(B,\beta)
of a locally compact abelian group
G

are outer conjugate
(he used the term “exterior equivalent”, which is inconsistent with current use)
if and only if there is an isomorphism between the crossed products that is equivariant for the dual actions

\widehat{\alpha}
and
\widehat{\beta}

and respects the embeddings of
A
and
B
.
We call the assumption on respecting the embeddings
Pedersen’s condition
.

In
[
2
, Theorem 3.1]
, the first three authors (to whom we refer here as “KOQ”)
extended Pedersen’s theorem to nonabelian groups,
using dual coactions,
and in
[
2
, Theorem 5.9]

gave a categorical context for Pedersen’s theorem,
establishing an equivalence between an
outer category
of actions
and a
fixed-point equivariant category
of coactions.

KOQ ran into difficulty when attempting to find examples
showing that Pedersen’s condition on the embeddings is necessary.
This would mean finding two actions of
G
that are not outer conjugate but nevertheless have isomorphic dual coactions.
Not only were no counterexamples found, KOQ proved that no such examples exist under a variety of circumstances, for instance (just to give a representative sampling)
G
discrete,
A
and
B
stable or commutative, or
\alpha
and
\beta
inner.
KOQ referred to such results as “no-go theorems”.
More accurately, in
[
3
]
the aforementioned no-go theorems were proved for abelian groups,
and in
[
4
]
this extra assumption was dropped.

KOQ called situations in which Pedersen’s condition is redundant
Pedersen rigid
(for actions) and
fixed-point rigid
(for the equivariant coactions). And this is implied by formally stronger properties called strongly Pedersen rigid and strongly fixed-point rigid, respectively.
More precisely, a coaction
(A,\delta)

was called strongly fixed-point rigid
if it has a unique generalized fixed-point algebra, independent of the choice of
\delta_{G}-\delta
equivariant unitary homomorphism
V:C^{*}(G)\to M(A)
,
and an action
(A,\alpha)
was called strongly Pedersen rigid if its dual coaction is strongly fixed-point rigid.
We now regard these definitions to be flawed.
See the end of Section
2
for corrected versions.

In view of the failure to find counterexamples, there still remains what KOQ called the
strong Pedersen rigidity problem
:
Is every action of
G
strongly Pedersen rigid?
Equivalently, is every equivariant coaction of
G
strongly fixed-point rigid?
(There were also slightly weaker versions, without the adjective “strong”.)

In this paper we begin the investigation of the dual problem,
involving coactions and their dual actions.
The first roadblock is that we need a fully working version of Pedersen’s theorem,
which can be formulated by routinely dualizing everything,
and would say:
“two coactions
(A,\delta)
and
(B,\varepsilon)
of
G
are outer conjugate
if and only if there is an isomorphism between the crossed products
that is equivariant for the dual actions
\widehat{\delta}
and
\widehat{\varepsilon}

and respects the embeddings of
A
and
B
.”
As KOQ point out in
[
2
]
, one direction of this desired theorem
is essentially recorded in
[
7
, Proposition 2.8]
,
where it was phrased for exterior equivalence, but a routine
modification shows that
if the coactions are outer conjugate then there is an isomorphism of the dual actions that preserves the embeddings.
But in the general case the converse is still open.
In the present paper, specializing to compact groups, we prove the converse direction of this Pedersen’s theorem,
which ultimately allows us to prove a no-go theorem
and a category equivalence.

The outer category of actions has also been considered in
[
8
, Section 1.3]
, where it is called the cocycle category,
and where isomorphisms are called cocycle conjugacies.
Pedersen rigidity for actions of discrete groups on commutative
C^{*}
-algebras was treated with a different approach,
using groupoids, and just called
C^{*}
-rigidity in
[
1
, Remark 7.6]
.

We thank the anonymous referee for comments that greatly improved our paper.

Pedersen rigidity (and its strong counterpart) for actions can be equivalently expressed in terms of the dual coactions, using Landstad duality and Pedersen’s theorem.

(1)

A coaction

(3)

A dual coaction

Also, by Landstad duality a coaction

An action

3.
Reduction of coconditions for compact groups

The following lemma shows that, when
G
is compact, Landstad’s coconditions can be greatly simplified.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
(A,\alpha,\mu)
be an equivariant action.
When
G
is compact,
Landstad’s coconditions
(L2)
–
(L3)

are redundant,
so that
A^{\alpha,\mu}
coincides with the fixed-point algebra
A^{\alpha}
.

Proof.

First note that, by Landstad duality, we have

A=\overline{\operatorname{span}}\{A^{\alpha,\mu}\mu(C(G))\},

and because
C(G)
is unital we must have
A^{\alpha,\mu}\subseteq A
.
Thus Landstad’s cocondition
(L1)

implies that
A^{\alpha,\mu}\subseteq A^{\alpha}
,
and for the opposite containment
it suffices to show that if
a\in A^{\alpha}
then
a
satisfies
(L2)
–
(L3)
.
Since

\mu(C(G))\subseteq M(A)

and
a\in A
,

(L2)

is trivially satisfied.

For the other part, the homomorphism
\mu
implements an inner coaction
\delta
of
G
on
A
, so
if
c\in C^{*}(G)
then,
because
a\in A
,
we have
\delta(a)(1\otimes c)\in A\otimes C^{*}(G)
.
Since
C^{*}(G)
is nuclear (because
G
is amenable) the tensor product
A^{\alpha}\otimes C^{*}(G)
is canonically identified with a
C^{*}
-subalgebra of
A\otimes C^{*}(G)
.
In fact, we have

A^{\alpha}\otimes C^{*}(G)=\bigl(A\otimes C^{*}(G)\bigr)^{\alpha\otimes\text{{id}}}.

Since
a\in A^{\alpha}
,
for all
s\in G

we have

\displaystyle(\alpha_{s}\otimes\text{{id}})\bigl(\delta(a)(1\otimes c)\bigr)

\displaystyle=\delta\bigl(\alpha_{s}(a)\bigr)(1\otimes c)

\displaystyle=\delta(a)(1\otimes c),

so

\delta(a)(1\otimes c)\in A^{\alpha}\otimes C^{*}(G).

Similarly for
(1\otimes c)\delta(a)
,
and we have verified
(L3)
.
∎

Proof.

4.
Pedersen’s theorem for coactions of compact groups

Recall that
G
denotes a compact group.

Theorem 4.1
.

(1)

Two coactions
\delta
and
\varepsilon
of
G
on
A
are
exterior equivalent if and only if
there is an isomorphism

\Theta:(A\rtimes_{\delta}G,\widehat{\delta})\xrightarrow{\simeq}(A\rtimes_{\varepsilon}G,\widehat{\varepsilon})

such that

(4.1)

\Theta\circ j_{A}^{\delta}=j_{A}^{\varepsilon}.

In fact, if
U
is an
\varepsilon
-cocycle such that
\delta=\operatorname{Ad}U\circ\varepsilon
,
then there is an isomorphism

\Phi_{U}:(A\rtimes_{\delta}G,\widehat{\delta})\xrightarrow{\simeq}(A\rtimes_{\varepsilon}G,\widehat{\varepsilon})

satisfying (
4.1
) such that

(4.2)

(\Phi_{U}\circ j_{G}^{\delta}\otimes\text{{id}})(w_{G})=(j_{A}^{\varepsilon}\otimes\text{{id}})(U)(j_{G}^{\varepsilon}\otimes\text{{id}})(w_{G}),

and conversely every isomorphism
\Theta
satisfying Equations
4.1
and
4.2

is of the form
\Phi_{U}
for a unique
\varepsilon
-cocycle
U
.

(2)

Two coactions
(A,\delta)
and
(B,\varepsilon)
of
G
are
outer conjugate if and only if
there is an isomorphism

\Theta:(A\rtimes_{\delta}G,\widehat{\delta})\xrightarrow{\simeq}(B\rtimes_{\varepsilon}G,\widehat{\varepsilon})
.

Note that in (2) we did not explicitly mention Pedersen’s condition

\Theta(j_{A}(A))=j_{B}(B)
, since that follows automatically from

3.1
because, for example,

j_{A}(A)=(A\rtimes_{\delta}G)^{\widehat{\delta},j_{G}^{\delta}}
by Landstad duality.

Proof.

(1)
Three of us proved in

\varepsilon

.
Conversely,
assume that we have an isomorphism

\Theta:(A\rtimes_{\delta}G,\widehat{\delta})\xrightarrow{\simeq}(A\rtimes_{\varepsilon}G,\widehat{\varepsilon})

Use this to identify

Note that

\displaystyle\delta(a)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Ad}V(a\otimes 1)

\displaystyle\varepsilon(a)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Ad}W(a\otimes 1).

\displaystyle\text{{id}}\otimes\delta_{G}(U)

\displaystyle=\text{{id}}\otimes\delta_{G}(VW^{*})

\displaystyle=V_{12}V_{13}W_{13}^{*}W_{12}^{*}

\displaystyle=V_{12}W_{12}^{*}W_{12}V_{13}W_{13}^{*}W_{12}^{*}

\displaystyle=U_{12}\operatorname{Ad}W_{12}(U_{13})

\displaystyle=U_{12}\varepsilon\otimes\text{{id}}(U).

\displaystyle\qquad=\mu\otimes\text{{id}}\bigl((\textup{rt}_{s}\otimes\text{{id}})(w_{G})\bigr)\nu\otimes\text{{id}}\bigl((\textup{rt}_{s}\otimes\text{{id}})(w_{G})\bigr)^{*}(1\otimes c)

\displaystyle\qquad=\mu\otimes\text{{id}}\bigl(w_{G}(1\otimes s)\bigr)\nu\otimes\text{{id}}\bigl((1\otimes s^{-1})w_{G}^{*}\bigr)(1\otimes c)

\displaystyle\qquad=\mu\otimes\text{{id}}(w_{G})\nu\otimes\text{{id}}(w_{G}^{*})(1\otimes c)

\displaystyle\qquad=U(1\otimes c).

then

\displaystyle\operatorname{Ad}U\circ\varepsilon(a)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Ad}VW^{*}\circ\operatorname{Ad}W(a\otimes 1)=\operatorname{Ad}V(a\otimes 1)=\delta(a).

For the other part, it suffices to note that the above arguments imply that for any such
isomorphism
\Theta
we have

\displaystyle(\Theta\circ j_{G}^{\delta}\otimes\text{{id}})(w_{G})

\displaystyle=\mu\otimes\text{{id}}(w_{G})

\displaystyle=V=VW^{*}W=UW

\displaystyle=j_{A}^{\varepsilon}\otimes\text{{id}}(U)j_{G}^{\varepsilon}\otimes\text{{id}}(w_{G}),

since
U\in M(A\otimes C^{*}(G))
and
j_{A}^{\varepsilon}=\text{{id}}_{A}
.

(2)
The forward direction is
[
7
, Proposition 2.8 (ii)]
.
Conversely,
assume that we have an isomorphism

\Theta:(A\rtimes_{\delta}G,\widehat{\delta})\xrightarrow{\simeq}(A\rtimes_{\varepsilon}G,\widehat{\varepsilon})
.
Note that, since by Landstad duality
j_{A}(A)
is the fixed-point algebra of
A\rtimes_{\delta}G
under the dual action
\widehat{\delta}
, and similarly for
j_{B}(B)
,
it follows from Lemma
3.1
that the isomorphism
\Theta
takes

j_{A}(A)
to
j_{B}(B)
.
We dualize the argument of
[
3
, Theorem 2.1]
:
Note that
(B\rtimes_{\varepsilon}G,\widehat{\varepsilon},j_{G}^{\delta})

is an equivariant action of
G
, with fixed-point algebra

(B\rtimes_{\varepsilon}G)^{\widehat{\varepsilon}}=\Theta\bigl((A\rtimes_{\delta}G)^{\widehat{\delta}}\bigr)=\Theta(j_{A}(A))=j_{B}(B),

and
j_{B}:B\to j_{B}(B)
is an isomorphism,
so by Landstad duality there are a coaction
\eta
of
G
on
B
and an isomorphism

\psi:(B\rtimes_{\eta}G,\widehat{\eta})\xrightarrow{\simeq}(B\rtimes_{\varepsilon}G,\widehat{\varepsilon})

such that

\psi\circ j_{G}^{\eta}=j_{G}^{\delta}\quad\text{and}\quad\psi\circ j_{B}^{\eta}=j_{B}^{\varepsilon}.

Thus by part (1) the coactions
\varepsilon
and
\eta
are exterior equivalent.

On the other hand, we have an isomorphism

\psi^{-1}\circ\Theta:(A\rtimes_{\delta}G,\widehat{\delta})\xrightarrow{\simeq}(B\rtimes_{\eta}G,\widehat{\eta}),

taking
j_{G}^{\delta}
to
j_{G}^{\eta}
,
so again by Landstad duality the coactions
\delta
and
\eta
are isomorphic.
Therefore
\delta
and
\varepsilon
are outer conjugate.
∎

5.
Category equivalence

Let
G
be a fixed compact group.

The
outer category
\mathcal{C}
of coactions
is defined as follows:
An object is a coaction of
G
, typically denoted by
(A,\delta)
,
and a morphism in the category is a pair
(\varphi,U):(A,\delta)\to(B,\varepsilon)
,
where
U
is an
\varepsilon
-cocycle and

\varphi:A\to M(B)
is a nondegenerate homomorphism
that is
\delta-\operatorname{Ad}U\circ\varepsilon
equivariant.
In
[
2
, Lemma 6.8]
it is shown that the category
\mathcal{C}
is well-defined.
Isomorphisms in the category are outer conjugacies of coactions.

Next, we define the

category
\mathcal{A}
of dual actions
,
as in Definition
2.3
,
where a morphism

\psi:(C,\alpha)\to(D,\beta)

is a nondegenerate

\alpha-\beta
equivariant
homomorphism
\psi:C\to M(D)
.
Note that,
by equivariance,

\psi
automatically takes
C^{\alpha}
to
M(D^{\beta})
.

Suppose
(\varphi,U):(A,\delta)\to(B,\varepsilon)
in
\mathcal{C}
.
Then
U
is an
\varepsilon
-cocycle,
giving an exterior-equivalent coaction
\zeta=\operatorname{Ad}U\circ\varepsilon
,
and
\varphi:(A,\delta)\to(B,\zeta)
is a nondegenerate
\delta-\zeta
equivariant homomorphism.
Taking crossed products gives a nondegenerate
\widehat{\delta}-\widehat{\zeta}
equivariant homomorphism

\varphi\rtimes G:A\rtimes_{\delta}G\to B\rtimes_{\zeta}G

taking
j_{G}^{\delta}
to
j_{G}^{\zeta}
.

On the other hand, Pedersen’s theorem
(Theorem
4.1
, but here we only need the “easy half”;
see
[
7
, Proposition 2.8]
)
gives a

\widehat{\zeta}-\widehat{\varepsilon}
equivariant
isomorphism

\Phi_{U}:B\rtimes_{\zeta}G\xrightarrow{\simeq}B\rtimes_{\varepsilon}G,

and then the composition gives a nondegenerate

\widehat{\delta}-\widehat{\varepsilon}
equivariant homomorphism

\Phi_{U}\circ(\varphi\rtimes G):A\rtimes_{\delta}G\to B\rtimes_{\varepsilon}G.

Theorem 5.1
.

With the above notation, the assignments

(5.1)

\displaystyle(A,\delta)

\displaystyle\mapsto(A\rtimes_{\delta}G,\widehat{\delta})

(5.2)

\displaystyle(\varphi,U)

\displaystyle\mapsto\Phi_{U}\circ(\varphi\rtimes G)

give a category equivalence

\operatorname{CP}:\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{A}
.

Proof.

We have to show that (
5.1
)–(
5.2
) give
a functor
that is faithful, full, and essentially surjective.
It follows immediately from the definitions that the object and morphism maps (
5.1
)–(
5.2
)
are well-defined and
that (
5.2
)
preserves identity morphisms.
To check that
\operatorname{CP}
preserves compositions,
given morphisms

\textstyle{(A,\delta)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{(\varphi,U)}
\textstyle{(B,\varepsilon)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{(\psi,V)}
\textstyle{(C,\zeta)}

in
\mathcal{C}
,
we have (similarly to
[
2
, Proposition 5.8]
)

\displaystyle\operatorname{CP}(\psi,V)\circ\operatorname{CP}(\varphi,U)\circ j_{A}^{\delta}

\displaystyle=\bigl(\Phi_{V}\circ(\psi\rtimes G)\bigr)\circ\bigl(\Phi_{U}\circ(\varphi\rtimes G)\bigr)\circ j_{A}^{\delta}

\displaystyle=\Phi_{V}\circ(\psi\rtimes G)\circ\Phi_{U}\circ j_{B}^{\operatorname{Ad}U\circ\varepsilon}\circ\varphi

\displaystyle=\Phi_{V}\circ(\psi\rtimes G)\circ j_{B}^{\varepsilon}\circ\varphi

\displaystyle=\Phi_{V}\circ j_{C}^{\operatorname{Ad}V\circ\zeta}\circ\psi\circ\varphi

\displaystyle=j_{C}^{\zeta}\circ\psi\circ\varphi

\displaystyle=\Phi_{(\psi\circ U)V}\circ j_{C}^{\operatorname{Ad}(\psi\circ U)V}\circ\psi\circ\varphi

\displaystyle=\Phi_{(\psi\circ U)V}\circ\bigl((\psi\circ\varphi)\rtimes G\bigr)\circ j_{A}^{\delta}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{CP}\bigl(\psi\circ\varphi,(\psi\circ U)V\bigr)\circ j_{A}^{\delta}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{CP}\bigl((\psi,V)\circ(\varphi,U)\bigr)\circ j_{A}^{\delta}.

Moreover
(exactly as in
[
2
, Theorem 6.12]
),

\displaystyle\bigl(\operatorname{CP}(\psi,V)\circ\operatorname{CP}(\varphi,U)\circ j_{G}^{\delta}\otimes\text{{id}}\bigr)(w_{G})

\displaystyle\quad=\Bigl(\operatorname{CP}\bigl((\psi,V)\circ(\varphi,U)\bigr)\circ j_{G}^{\delta}\otimes\text{{id}}\Bigr)(w_{G}),

which gives that

\displaystyle\operatorname{CP}(\psi,V)\circ\operatorname{CP}(\varphi,U)\circ j_{G}^{\delta}=\operatorname{CP}\bigl((\psi,V)\circ(\varphi,U)\bigr)\circ j_{G}^{\delta}.

It follows that
\operatorname{CP}:\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{A}
is a functor.

To check that
\operatorname{CP}
is essentially surjective,
choose an object
(C,\alpha)
in
\mathcal{A}
, so that by definition
there exists a coaction
(A,\delta)
and an isomorphism

(C,\alpha)\xrightarrow{\simeq}(A\rtimes_{\delta}G,\widehat{\delta}).

Finally, we show that for objects
(A,\delta)
and
(B,\varepsilon)
in
\mathcal{C}
,
the functor
\operatorname{CP}
gives a bijection:

(5.3)

\begin{split}\mor_{\mathcal{C}}\bigl((A,\delta),(B,\varepsilon)\bigr)&\longleftrightarrow\mor_{\mathcal{A}}\bigl(\operatorname{CP}(A,\delta),\operatorname{CP}(B,\varepsilon)\bigr).\end{split}

First, to see that
\operatorname{CP}
is faithful,
suppose that we have morphisms

(\varphi,U),(\rho,V):(A,\delta)\to(B,\varepsilon)

in
\mathcal{C}
such that

\operatorname{CP}(\varphi,U)=\operatorname{CP}(\rho,V):(A\rtimes_{\delta}G,\widehat{\delta})\to(B\rtimes_{\varepsilon}G,\widehat{\varepsilon})

in
\mathcal{A}
.
By construction, we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{CP}(\varphi,U)\circ j_{A}^{\delta}

\displaystyle=\Phi_{U}\circ(\varphi\rtimes G)\circ j_{A}^{\delta}

\displaystyle=\Phi_{U}\circ j_{B}^{\operatorname{Ad}U\circ\varepsilon}\circ\varphi

\displaystyle=j_{B}^{\varepsilon}\circ\varphi,

and similarly

\operatorname{CP}(\rho,V)\circ j_{A}^{\delta}=j_{B}^{\varepsilon}\circ\rho,

so
\varphi=\rho
since
j_{B}^{\varepsilon}
is injective.
Moreover,

\displaystyle\bigl(\operatorname{CP}(\varphi,U)\circ j_{G}^{\delta}\otimes\text{{id}}\bigr)(w_{G})

\displaystyle=\bigl(\Phi_{U}\circ(\varphi\rtimes G)\circ j_{G}^{\delta}\otimes\text{{id}}\bigr)(w_{G})

\displaystyle=(j_{B}^{\varepsilon}\otimes\text{{id}})(U)(j_{G}^{\varepsilon}\otimes\text{{id}})(w_{G}),

and similarly

\bigl(\operatorname{CP}(\rho,V)\circ j_{G}^{\delta}\otimes\text{{id}}\bigr)(w_{G})=(j_{B}^{\varepsilon}\otimes\text{{id}})(V)(j_{G}^{\varepsilon}\otimes\text{{id}})(w_{G}),

so
(j_{B}^{\varepsilon}\otimes\text{{id}})(U)=(j_{B}^{\varepsilon}\otimes\text{{id}})(V)
since
(j_{G}^{\varepsilon}\otimes\text{{id}})(w_{G})
is unitary.
Since
j_{B}^{\varepsilon}
is injective, so is

j_{B}^{\varepsilon}\otimes\text{{id}}\colon B\otimes C^{*}(G)\to M\bigl((B\rtimes_{\varepsilon}G)\otimes C^{*}(G)\bigr).

Thus
U=V
, and hence
(\varphi,U)=(\rho,V)
.

Suppose we are given a morphism

\psi\colon(A\rtimes_{\delta}G,\widehat{\delta})\to(B\rtimes_{\varepsilon}G,\widehat{\varepsilon})

in
\mathcal{A}
.
We have that
j_{A}^{\delta}(A)=(A\rtimes_{\delta}G)^{\widehat{\delta}}
and
j_{B}^{\varepsilon}(B)=(B\rtimes_{\varepsilon}G)^{\widehat{\varepsilon}}
.
By Landstad duality there are a coaction
(B,\zeta)
and an isomorphism

\Theta\colon(B\rtimes_{\zeta}G,\widehat{\zeta})\xrightarrow{\simeq}(B\rtimes_{\varepsilon}G,\widehat{\varepsilon})

taking
j_{G}^{\zeta}
to
\psi\circ j_{G}^{\delta}
such that

(5.4)

\Theta\circ j_{B}^{\zeta}=j_{B}^{\varepsilon}.

In particular, we have a
\widehat{\zeta}-\widehat{\varepsilon}
equivariant morphism

\Theta\colon B\rtimes_{\zeta}G\to B\rtimes_{\varepsilon}G

satisfying (
5.4
), so by Pedersen’s theorem (Theorem
4.1
)
there is a unique
\varepsilon
-cocycle
U

such that
\zeta=\operatorname{Ad}U\circ\varepsilon
and
\Theta=\Phi_{U}
.

Furthermore, the map

\Theta^{-1}\circ\psi:(A\rtimes_{\delta}G,\widehat{\delta})\to(B\rtimes_{\zeta}G,\widehat{\zeta})

takes
j_{G}^{\delta}
to
j_{G}^{\zeta}
,
so by Landstad duality there exists a map

\varphi:(A,\delta)\to(B,\zeta)

such that
\Theta^{-1}\circ\psi=\varphi\rtimes G
and thus

\psi=\Theta\circ(\varphi\rtimes G)=\Phi_{U}\circ(\varphi\rtimes G)=\operatorname{CP}(\varphi,U).

Hence, the map (
5.3
) is surjective.
∎

Remark 5.2
.

Concerning our definition of the functor
\operatorname{CP}
, in the discussion preceding Theorem
5.1
we only needed the easy half of Pedersen’s theorem, which is valid for any locally compact group
G
,
so the assignments (
5.1
)–(
5.2
)
make sense for arbitrary
G
.
Now, careful inspection of the above proof reveals that the only place where we required
G
to be compact
and not merely amenable
(note that amenability is required to ensure that
j_{B}^{\varepsilon}
is injective)
is the verification that the functor
\operatorname{CP}

is full.
Consequently, if we only assume that
G
is amenable
we still get an essentially surjective faithful functor
\operatorname{CP}:\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{A}
.
This highlights exactly where we need the full force of Pedersen’s theorem Theorem
4.1
.

Now, let
\mathcal{B}
be the nondegenerate category of
C^{*}
-algebras,
that is, the category whose objects are
C^{*}
-algebras and morphisms are nondegenerate
∗
-homomorphisms.

We define a forgetful functor
F:\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{B}
as usual,
by
(C,\alpha)\to C
on objects and on morphisms by taking

(C,\alpha)\to(D,\beta)

to the map
C\to M(D)
.
We define the
outer crossed-product functor
as the composition

\widetilde{\operatorname{CP}}:=F\circ\operatorname{CP}:\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{B}.

This setting describes an inversion of
\widetilde{\operatorname{CP}}
in the sense of
[
2
, Section 4]
that we call
outer Landstad duality for coactions
.

In
[
2
, Section 4]
we say that the inversion is
good
if the image of
F
is contained in the essential image of
\widetilde{\operatorname{CP}}
,
and
F
enjoys the following unique isomorphism lifting property:
For all objects
y\in\mathcal{B}
and
x\in F^{-1}(y)
and every isomorphism
\theta
in
\mathcal{B}
with domain
y
,
there exists a unique isomorphism
\eta
in
\mathcal{A}
such that
F(\eta)=\theta
.

Proposition 5.3
.

Outer Landstad duality for coactions is a good inversion.

Proof.

If
C=F(C,\alpha)

then by definition there is a coaction
(A,\delta)
such that
C\simeq A\rtimes_{\delta}G
.
Thus, the image of
F
is contained in the essential image of
\widetilde{\operatorname{CP}}
.

Moreover, given an object
(C,\alpha)
of
\mathcal{A}
and an isomorphism
\theta\colon C\xrightarrow{\simeq}D
in
\mathcal{B}
,
we can use
\theta
to carry the action
\alpha

over to an action
\beta
on
D
,
and then
\theta
gives an isomorphism

\widetilde{\theta}\colon(C,\alpha)\to(D,\beta)

in
\mathcal{A}
covering
\theta\colon C\to D
.
The forgetful functor is faithful, so
\widetilde{\theta}
is unique.
Thus, the unique isomorphism lifting property is satisfied.
∎
