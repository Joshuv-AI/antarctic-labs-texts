YEAST: Yet Another Sequential Test
Alexey Kurennoy, Majed Dodin, Tural Gurbanov, Ana Peleteiro Ramallo

Abstract
Online A/B experiments require timely detection of harmful changes while controlling false alarms. We introduce YEAST, a simple sequential test for continuous monitoring of count and real-valued metrics over a prespecified event horizon. It compares the cumulative difference between experiment groups with a constant boundary determined by an estimate of the variance of this difference at the end of monitoring. Under the strong null of no effect on the event-generation process, we establish asymptotic Type-I error control through a maximal inequality and a central limit theorem. The required conditions allow some forms of repeated-user dependence. We evaluate YEAST using synthetic simulations and a semi-synthetic study of fixed-duration monitoring and observe power gains over the tested martingale-based methods.



1
Introduction

Online A/B experiments have become a standard approach for evaluating the impact of machine learning models on the systems and products where they are deployed. Detecting harmful effects promptly can protect user experience and reduce financial losses. Repeatedly applying a fixed-sample test as data accumulate can, however, inflate the false positive rate. Sequential tests account for repeated significance checks, allowing experiments to stop early while controlling false alarms.

Continuous monitoring allows significance checks as new observations arrive, including after every event. Both martingale methods and continuous-time extensions of group sequential testing support this flexibility
[
15
,
12
,
3
]
. Our focus is on a procedure that combines continuous monitoring with simple calibration and high sensitivity over a prespecified finite horizon.

We propose YEAST (YEt Another Sequential Test), which tracks the cumulative difference in a metric between the experiment groups and signals when that difference crosses a constant boundary. The metric can count events or sum real-valued outcomes, such as revenue. The boundary has a closed-form expression based on a prespecified maximum number of events and an estimate of the variance of the cumulative difference at that horizon.

Our theoretical contribution is an asymptotic maximal inequality that connects boundary-crossing probabilities to the terminal distribution. Together with a terminal central limit theorem and conditions on future increments, it yields asymptotic Type-I error control under the null that treatment leaves the event-generation process unchanged. This approach allows some repeated-user dependence without requiring a Brownian approximation for the whole trajectory. In the canonical Brownian setting, YEAST’s one-sided boundary coincides with the continuous-monitoring O’Brien–Fleming boundary
[
25
]
.

We evaluate YEAST using synthetic simulations and a semi-synthetic study based on public online retail data. We observe power gains over the tested martingale-based methods, particularly at small and moderate effects.

To summarise, our
main contributions
are:

•

A sequential test for count and real-valued metrics, with a closed-form constant boundary that can be checked after every event up to a prespecified horizon.

•

An asymptotic maximal bound inspired by the generalised Lévy inequalities
[
22
, Paragraph 29.1, A]
, together with Type-I error and power theorems.

•

Synthetic and semi-synthetic evaluations of false detection rates, power, and sensitivity to variance and horizon calibration, with openly available code and simulation outputs
[
17
]
.

Related Work

Our work is related to a large literature on sequential decision-making
based on sequences of hypothesis tests, confidence sequences, or sequences of “always-valid” p-values. While early work in this field was primarily concerned with clinical trials and industrial quality control
[
7
,
5
,
2
]
, more recent work is typically motivated by the sequential nature of online experiments, where users arrive sequentially and generate outcomes that are observed quickly relative to the duration of the experiment
[
18
,
15
,
12
]
. Here, the goal of sequential testing is typically either to reduce the opportunity cost of longer experiments, as in the context of best-arm identification in multi-armed bandit problems
[
14
,
16
,
41
,
13
]
, or to manage risk by faster detection of harmful treatments
[
21
,
11
]
.

2.1
Martingale Methods and E-Processes

Building on the seminal work of Wald
[
38
]
and Doob
[
8
]
, most modern sequential procedures are based on maximal inequalities for appropriately constructed martingales that provide time-uniform statistical guarantees.
While Wald’s original sequential probability ratio test (SPRT) was developed from scratch, its construction is essentially of this “martingale-type”. SPRT sequentially evaluates the likelihood ratio test statistic at each sample size for the null hypothesis
H_{0}:\theta=\theta_{0}
against the simple alternative
H_{1}:\theta=\theta_{1}
. The test continues until the statistic crosses one of two predefined bounds, at which point the null hypothesis is either rejected or not rejected accordingly. SPRT requires specifying a single-point alternative, which limits its use for general-purpose experimental monitoring. To address this limitation, likelihood ratio methods using the method of mixtures (mSPRT) have been developed. These approaches replace the single-point alternative with a composite hypothesis and the standard likelihood ratio — with a mixture, which remains a martingale under the null
[
29
]
.

In addition to mSPRT, the class of martingale methods encompasses more sophisticated approaches constructed using other martingales and falling under the framework of generalised always-valid inference (GAVI)
[
15
,
12
,
11
,
20
,
19
,
4
]
.

Another recent branch of martingale-based methods is built upon the principle of testing by betting (see
[
27
]
and references therein).

The martingale-based approach was generalised through the concept of
e-processes

[
10
,
32
]
— stochastic processes characterised by a property that allows for straightforward construction of sequential tests. The roots of this perspective can be traced back to
[
37
]
, who introduced the use of test martingales within a game-theoretic framework as a foundation for statistical testing. This remains an active area of research, with a growing literature developing new constructions and applications of e-processes
[
33
,
34
]
. See
[
28
]
for a recent overview of the topic and further references.

2.2
Group Sequential Methods

A conceptually different approach is followed by methods based on group sequential tests (GST)
[
26
,
25
,
35
,
9
]
. GST methods rely on (asymptotic) characterisations of the joint distribution of a vector of test statistics across increments of data, which permits the computation of sequential boundaries that control the Type-I error rate across the scheduled analyses via multivariate (numerical) integration.
Earlier GST methods (such as
[
26
,
25
]
) required a fixed number and timing of interim analyses. In contrast, the later generalisation
[
9
]
permits flexible monitoring schedules without fixing the total number of analyses in advance. This flexibility is achieved using an alpha-spending function, which controls the allocation of Type-I error across analyses, based on a prespecified maximum information level.

Betensky
[
3
]
extends this perspective to continuous monitoring by constructing a continuous stopping boundary from a prescribed alpha-spending function. In the Brownian-motion formulation, the reflection principle gives an explicit crossing probability for a constant one-sided boundary
[
24
, Section 2.2]
, providing the connection to our proposed method as discussed in Section
3
.

2.3
Simple Sequential A/B Testing

Our approach has similarities to
simple sequential A/B testing

[
23
]
. However, the test from
[
23
]
only allows monitoring count-type metrics (for example, the number of clicks or purchases) whilst our proposed method can be used to monitor metrics of any type, including real-valued financial metrics such as revenue. One can view our work as a generalisation of
[
23
]
yet we emphasise that this generalisation is not at all trivial as the theory presented in
[
23
]
fundamentally supports only count-type metrics.

3
YEAST: YEt Another Sequential Test

In this section, we present a novel, alternative sequential testing method proposed in this paper.

3.1
Problem Setup and Notation

Suppose we are conducting an A/B test
and have a certain metric of interest.
We want to constantly track this metric and have a tool to check if the difference between the groups is at any point large enough to conclude that the treatment has an impact on the metric. The tool must have good control of the false detection rate despite the repeated checks. In other words, it must allow for peeking into the interim results of the experiment without inflating the false detection rate.

To develop a tool satisfying the above requirement, we consider the difference
S
in the total value of the metric of interest accumulated by the control
(W=0)
and the treatment
(W=1)
groups. Typically, this difference
S
can be represented as a sum of increments over a stream of events. For example, if the metric of interest is revenue then the tracked difference
S
can be decomposed into the sum of order values over checkout events where the order value is taken with a positive sign if it comes from the control group and with a negative sign if it comes from the treatment arm. Mathematically, the increments can be written as

X_{i}=\left(1-2W_{i}\right)Y_{i},

(1)

where
Y_{i}
is the value or outcome associated with the
i
-th event (such as the order value in the example above) and
W_{i}
is the event assignment indicator (it equals 0 if the event was generated by a subject from the control group and 1 if its subject belongs to the treatment group). With this notation at hand, the difference in the metric of interest after observing
n
events can be written as the running sum

S_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i},\quad n\geq 1,

(2)

as illustrated in the example data set displayed in Table
1
.

3.2
Assumptions and the Null Hypothesis

We will now state and discuss our assumptions and the null hypothesis.

Let
U_{i}
denote the subject generating event
i
, and let
A_{u}
be the assignment sign of subject
u
, with
A_{u}=1
for control and
A_{u}=-1
for treatment. Thus
1-2W_{i}=A_{U_{i}}
and
X_{i}=A_{U_{i}}Y_{i}
.

Throughout, the event stream denotes the sequence
\{(Y_{i},U_{i}):i\geq 1\}
of event values and subject identities, excluding the assignment indicators
W_{i}
.

Assumption 1 (Random Subject Assignment)

The subject signs
\{A_{u}\}
are independent, with
\Pr\{A_{u}=1\}=\Pr\{A_{u}=-1\}=1/2
. Each subject retains the same assignment throughout the experiment.

We work with the following strong null hypothesis.

\textup{H}_{0}
:
the treatment does not affect the event-generation process, including event values, subject identities, and event timing. In particular, the subject assignments are independent of the entire event stream:

\{A_{u}\}_{u}\perp\!\!\!\perp\{(Y_{i},U_{i}):i\geq 1\}.

(3)

Together with Assumption 1, this implies

\Pr\{W_{i}=1\mid(Y_{j},U_{j})_{j\geq 1}\}=1/2\quad\textup{a.s.},\quad i\geq 1.

(4)

It is important to distinguish between subjects (such as users) and events generated by subjects (such as orders).
The event outcomes may be dependent and need not be identically distributed.
The event assignment indicators are identical for events from the same subject.
Under the strong null, the collection of subject assignments is independent of the entire event stream.
The event-level sequences
\{Y_{i}\}
and
\{W_{i}\}
can nevertheless be dependent on one another:
event values can provide information about whether two events come from the same subject and hence have identical assignment indicators.

The null hypothesis stated above exhibits the difference between our setup and the classical testing for a zero average treatment effect. Specifically,

•

our testing procedure acts on the level of events generated by experiment subjects and not the experiment subjects themselves (for example, orders and not customers);

•

we assume that under the null hypothesis, the treatment does not affect the “event generation process” in any way.

In the revenue monitoring example, the latter implies that both the frequency of orders and the order value distribution stay the same in both groups under the null. We discuss the limitations of the considered setup in Section
5
.

The approach described so far allows us to derive a simple but effective sequential testing procedure we present next.

3.3
Proposed Sequential Testing Method

The proposed monitoring method consists of tracking the difference in the metric of interest between the experiment groups and (continuously) comparing it with a constant (alerting) boundary. Whenever the boundary is crossed, we reject the strong null at nominal significance level
\alpha
. The asymptotic guarantee requires the regularity conditions stated below.

The procedure requires setting the maximum number of events to be observed during the monitoring period that we denote by
N
. In other words, the boundary is computed assuming that the monitoring will not continue after
N
events have been collected. See Section
3.3.2
for how to set
N
in practice.

The proposed method has only one other parameter that needs to be estimated beforehand - the (scaled) variance of the difference in the metric of interest at the end of monitoring,
V_{N}=var(S_{N})/N
. The nonnegative estimator of
V_{N}
needs to be relatively consistent under the null hypothesis, i. e.
\hat{V}_{N}/V_{N}\to_{p}1
. Given the estimate
\hat{V}_{N}
(computed by such an estimator), the alerting boundary is set to

b^{*}=z_{1-\alpha/2}\cdot\sqrt{N\hat{V}_{N}},

(5)

where
z_{1-\alpha/2}
is the quantile of the standard normal distribution of level
1-\alpha/2
. The proposed simple sequential test proceeds by tracking
S_{n}
and comparing it against
b^{*}
.

The necessity to fix or estimate
N
and to provide an estimate of
V_{N}
beforehand can be perceived as a disadvantage of the proposed approach. However, we argue that
in many practical scenarios, those parameters can be reliably set based on pre-experiment data (in fact, the underlying data distribution has to have some level of stability or regularity in time for the A/B testing to be meaningful in the first place).

3.3.1
Informal Derivation of the Proposed Method

The derivation of the alerting boundary (
5
) follows two steps. First, under the regularity conditions in Section
3.3.3
, an asymptotic inequality bounds the false detection rate by twice the crossing probability at the end of monitoring. For
b=\gamma\sqrt{var(S_{N})}
and fixed
\gamma>0
,

FDR=\Pr\left\{\max_{n=1}^{N}S_{n}>b\right\}\leq 2\Pr\{S_{N}>b\}+o(1).

(6)

Here FDR denotes the probability of a false detection in one experiment.
In the second step we use the Central Limit Theorem to obtain

\Pr\{S_{N}>b\}=1-\Phi\left(\frac{b}{\sqrt{NV_{N}}}\right)+o(1),

(7)

where
\Phi
denotes the CDF of the standard normal distribution. Setting
b=z_{1-\alpha/2}\sqrt{NV_{N}}
gives
FDR\leq\alpha+o(1)
. Theorem
1
establishes this oracle result, and Corollary
1
justifies replacing
V_{N}
by a (relatively) consistent estimate.

The proposed testing procedure is defined in Algorithm
1
and will be referred to as
YEAST
(from YEt Another Sequential Test). The estimation of
V_{N}
is discussed in Section
3.3.2
below.

Mind that differently from the non-sequential testing, the significance level

In the remainder of this subsection we draw a comparison between YEAST and GST methods
[
26
,
25
,
9
]
, particularly focusing on their continuous-time variants
[
3
]
.

Continuous-time group sequential testing typically calibrates a stopping boundary using a Brownian-motion approximation to the test statistic, with time measured by accumulated statistical information. This approximation describes both the marginal distributions of the statistic and its dependence across monitoring times. Different boundary shapes can then be chosen to determine how readily the test rejects at different stages of the experiment
[
9
,
3
]
.

YEAST obtains an asymptotic bound on the probability of crossing its boundary using a terminal central limit theorem and conditions on the remaining increments. These conditions ensure that, conditional on the observed history, the remaining sum has probability at least approximately one-half of being nonnegative, except near the horizon, where its magnitude is negligible. This argument does not require a Brownian-motion approximation for the entire trajectory. It accommodates the repeated-customer dependence in our example, although its assumptions must still be verified for the event stream under consideration.

The resulting boundaries can coincide. In the canonical Brownian model, the reflection principle
[
24
, Section 2.2]
gives the one-sided YEAST threshold
z_{1-\alpha/2}\sigma_{N}
, which is also the continuous-monitoring O’Brien–Fleming boundary on the cumulative-sum scale
[
25
]
. Here,
\sigma_{N}
is the terminal standard deviation; YEAST permits its replacement by a consistent estimate. YEAST therefore provides an alternative justification for this simple boundary under its stated assumptions, including settings with dependent event contributions.

3.3.2
Setting the Input Parameters

As was described in the previous subsection, the computation of the alerting boundary of the proposed sequential testing procedure requires two inputs: the number of events
N
to be observed during the monitoring process and an estimate
\hat{V}_{N}
of the (scaled) variance of the tracked metric difference at the end of monitoring. In this subsection, we discuss how to provide those inputs in practice.

The formal results below use a deterministic event cap
N
and require monitoring to stop by that cap. In practice, to set
N
, we estimate the expected number of events to be collected over the experiment time frame. In the simplest form, this can amount to setting
N
to the number of events observed in a pre-experiment period of the same duration as the experiment we want to monitor.

For variance estimation, we suggest using pre-experiment data as well. Obtaining an accurate estimate this way requires the pre-experiment period to be representative of the experiment time. However, in cases where this cannot be assumed, the validity of fixed-duration randomised experiments is questionable in principle (as the effects measured during such experiments may not generalise beyond their time frames). So in the context of A/B testing, this is a natural assumption. Note that estimating variance from pre-experiment data is a standard procedure performed before an A/B test because it is used in computing the MDE (minimum detectable effect) and identifying the necessary sample size.

As was noted in Section
3.2
, the event outcomes
Y_{i}
,
i\geq 1
, can be dependent because some of the events are generated by the same user. The same holds for the event assignment indicators
W_{i}
,
i\geq 1
. Consequently, the increments
X_{i}
,
i\geq 1
, of the monitored trajectory can be serially correlated in which case the variance
var(S_{N})
does not equal the sum of variances
var(X_{i})
. Under the strong null, contributions from different users are independent conditional on the event stream and are uncorrelated unconditionally.
Therefore, we propose to use an estimator that is robust to clustered serial correlation
[
1
]
to estimate
V_{N}
. Implementations of such estimators are readily available, for example as part of the
sandwich
package in R
[
43
,
42
]
(see function
vcovCL
). The cluster variable in this case is the user identifier. As we will show in our experiments with real-world data in Section
4
, the use of a robust (clustered) variance estimator (instead of the simple sum of
var(X_{i})
) can be utterly important for effective control of the false detection rate.

While the need to set a finite horizon
N
can be viewed as a disadvantage of YEAST, we have not found it problematic in practice. When running A/B experiments it is typical to plan for a certain duration and the number of observations collected during the experiment time frame is usually predictable. In addition, the decision boundary of YEAST depends on the square root of
N
, which makes the method more robust to inaccuracies in the estimation of
N
. That said, we acknowledge that there are situations where it is difficult to reliably estimate
N
and in that case, it may indeed be better to resort to other methods (e.g.
[
12
]
) that do not require setting a finite horizon. In the empirical evaluations in Section
4
, we never assume that
N
is known and estimate it from pre-experiment data (as part of the method). We then monitor throughout the planned calendar period without enforcing the estimated event count as a stopping cap, to assess practical robustness to uncertainty in
N
.

3.3.3
Formal Statements

In this subsection, we establish asymptotic guarantees for the false detection rate and power of the proposed method.
The variables
Y_{i}
,
W_{i}
, and
U_{i}
,
i\geq 1
, are random variables on the probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\Pr)
, and
\mathrm{I}\!\mathrm{R}
denotes the real numbers.
All limits are as the deterministic event cap

Let
S_{0}=0

\mathcal{F}_{n}=\sigma\{(Y_{i},W_{i},U_{i}):1\leq i\leq n\},\qquad\sigma_{N}^{2}=var(S_{N}),\qquad V_{N}=\sigma_{N}^{2}/N.

(8)

Assumption 2 (Terminal CLT)

Let

(10)

Assumption 3 (Future Increments)

There exist deterministic integers
1\leq L_{N}\leq N

L_{N}\to\infty

(a)

There exist numbers

(11)

(b)

For

(Approximate Terminal Reduction)

Under Assumptions 2–3, for every fixed

\mathcal{U}_{k}=\{U_{i}:1\leq i\leq k\}

\mathcal{G}_{k,N}=\mathcal{F}_{k}\vee\sigma\{(Y_{i},U_{i}):1\leq i\leq N\}

s_{k,N}^{2}=\sum_{u\notin\mathcal{U}_{k}}\left(\sum_{i=k+1}^{N}Y_{i}\mathbf{1}\{U_{i}=u\}\right)^{2}.

Then Assumption 3(a) holds with
\delta_{N}=\rho_{N}
, for both signs of the running sum.

The last condition in (
21
) prevents the still-random sum from concentrating in an interval as small as the already-revealed contribution.
For example, suppose the conditional CDF of
R_{k,N}/s_{k,N}
given
\mathcal{G}_{k,N}
differs from
\Phi
by at most a deterministic
r_{N}\downarrow 0
, almost surely, uniformly over its argument and
1\leq k\leq N-L_{N}
. Then

\displaystyle\Pr\{|R_{k,N}|\leq\epsilon_{N}s_{k,N}\mid\mathcal{G}_{k,N}\}

\displaystyle\leq\Phi(\epsilon_{N})-\Phi(-\epsilon_{N})+2r_{N}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\epsilon_{N}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}+2r_{N}.

(22)

The first inequality uses the conditional CDF at
\epsilon_{N}
and its left limit at
-\epsilon_{N}
, so it also covers atoms. The second follows by integrating the standard normal density
\phi(x)=(2\pi)^{-1/2}e^{-x^{2}/2}\leq(2\pi)^{-1/2}
over an interval of length

. Thus the last condition in (

Example 1

(Daily Cohorts with Repeat Purchases)
.

Suppose that, starting with no customers, each day brings an independent, identically distributed number of first-time buyers in
\{0,\ldots,J\}
, with positive mean and fixed integer
J\geq 1
. Each customer makes an initial purchase and, independently on each of the following
D
days, another purchase with probability
p\in(0,1)
, for a fixed integer
D\geq 1
. No further purchases occur after that window. Order values lie in
[a,b]
, where
0<a<b<\infty
. Customer purchase histories, measured relative to the first-purchase day, are independent and identically distributed across customers and independent of the daily cohort sizes. Purchases are ordered chronologically, with any ties resolved independently of assignments. Each customer receives an independent fair assignment sign, retained for all their purchases, and the entire purchase process is independent of these signs. Thus Assumption 1 and the strong null hold, while customers can make several purchases on different days, interleaved with those of other customers. For example,
D=30
allows repeat purchases over a month.

We first verify Assumption 2. Let
Q_{u}
and
T_{u}
be customer
u
’s total number of purchases and total spending over their full purchase history, and let
T_{u,N}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}Y_{i}\mathbf{1}\{U_{i}=u\}
be their spending through event
N
. Let
\mathcal{H}=\sigma\{(Y_{i},U_{i}):i\geq 1\}
and
q_{N}=var(S_{N}\mid\mathcal{H})=\sum_{u}T_{u,N}^{2}
. After the
N
-th event, at most
J(D+1)
already-observed customers have purchases remaining beyond the observation horizon. Completing these histories and including any customers whose first purchase occurs later on the same day changes the purchase count and the sum of squared customer totals by bounded amounts. The law of large numbers for the independent daily cohorts therefore gives

\frac{q_{N}}{N}\to v:=\frac{\mathop{\textrm{E}}[T_{u}^{2}]}{\mathop{\textrm{E}}[Q_{u}]}>0\quad\textup{a.s.}

Since
a^{2}\leq q_{N}/N\leq(D+1)b^{2}
and
\mathop{\textrm{E}}[S_{N}\mid\mathcal{H}]=0
, bounded convergence also gives
\sigma_{N}^{2}/N=\mathop{\textrm{E}}[q_{N}]/N\to v
. Conditional on
\mathcal{H}
, the sum has independent signed customer contributions bounded by
(D+1)b
, with total variance at least
a^{2}N
. Their conditional CLT, together with
q_{N}/\sigma_{N}^{2}\to 1
almost surely, yields Assumption 2.

Next, to verify Assumption 3, choose
L_{N}=\lfloor N^{1/3}\rfloor
, so
L_{N}\to\infty
and
L_{N}=o(\sqrt{N})
. For part (b), simply note that each increment has absolute value at most
b
, so
H_{N}\leq bL_{N}
. Since
\sigma_{N}^{2}/N\to v>0
, the chosen
L_{N}
gives
H_{N}/\sigma_{N}\leq bL_{N}/\sigma_{N}\to 0
. It remains to verify part (a).

For all sufficiently large
N
such that
L_{N}>2M
, where
M=JD(D+1)
, and each
1\leq k\leq N-L_{N}
, consider the remainder
S_{N}-S_{k}
of the running sum. Among already-seen customers only those whose first purchase occurred within the last
D+1
calendar days can contribute to this remainder. There are at most
J(D+1)
such customers, each with at most
D
purchases remaining. We therefore have that
|B_{k,N}|\leq Mb
and at least
N-k-M
future purchases belong to unseen customers. For each unseen customer
u
, the total
T_{u,N}
includes only events after
k
and satisfies
0\leq T_{u,N}\leq T_{u}\leq(D+1)b
. Conditional on
\mathcal{G}_{k,N}
, the contributions of unseen customers are independent fair signs multiplied by these totals, and

s_{k,N}^{2}=\sum_{u\notin\mathcal{U}_{k}}T_{u,N}^{2}\geq a^{2}(N-k-M),\qquad\frac{\sum_{u\notin\mathcal{U}_{k}}T_{u,N}^{3}}{s_{k,N}^{3}}\leq\frac{(D+1)b}{s_{k,N}}.

For any
1\leq k\leq N-L_{N}
, the first inequality gives
s_{k,N}^{2}\geq a^{2}(L_{N}-M)\geq a^{2}L_{N}/2
for all sufficiently large
N
, since

is fixed. Let
F_{k,N}
be a regular conditional CDF of
R_{k,N}/s_{k,N}
given
\mathcal{G}_{k,N}
. The Berry–Esseen bound gives deterministic constants
C_{0}<\infty
and
N_{0}
such that, on a single event of probability one, for every
N\geq N_{0}
,

\max_{1\leq k\leq N-L_{N}}\sup_{x\in\mathrm{I}\!\mathrm{R}}|F_{k,N}(x)-\Phi(x)|\leq\frac{C_{0}}{\sqrt{L_{N}}}.

Set the deterministic quantity
d_{N}=\sqrt{2}\,Mb/(a\sqrt{L_{N}})
. Since
Mb/s_{k,N}\leq d_{N}

\pm d_{N}
and bounding the normal density as in (

N\geq N_{0}
and all

\displaystyle\Pr\{|R_{k,N}|\leq Mb\mid\mathcal{G}_{k,N}\}

\displaystyle\leq F_{k,N}(d_{N})-F_{k,N}(-d_{N}-)

\displaystyle\leq\Phi(d_{N})-\Phi(-d_{N})+\frac{2C_{0}}{\sqrt{L_{N}}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2d_{N}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}+\frac{2C_{0}}{\sqrt{L_{N}}}=\frac{C}{\sqrt{L_{N}}},

where

is deterministic. The left limit in the above formula includes any mass at the lower endpoint, and the conditional probabilities use the same regular conditional distributions.
Conditional symmetry and

This example relies on bounded daily inflow, bounded order values, and a finite purchase window per customer; it permits repeat purchases across days or weeks within that window.

A bounded number of events per user alone is insufficient for the sign-balance assumption. To see this, set

and construct successive blocks indexed by

Validation Using Real-World Data: a Semi-Synthetic Experiment

The best possible real-world validation would be using data from a very large number of A/B tests (some with statistically significant results and some with neutral outcomes under high power). Having those data, one could apply the compared sequential tests retrospectively and measure their false detection rates and sensitivity (power). 5
Limitations

The empirical comparisons in Section
4
show favourable calibration and power for YEAST despite its simplicity. Yet there are scenarios where the underlying null hypothesis of no effect on the data generation process is too strong, potentially leading to some “undesirable sensitivity”. In other words, sometimes the treatment can alter the data generation process but have a neutral average effect on the metric of interest. We can think of (at least) two such scenarios.

Situations where the treatment brings multiple changes to the data generation process and those changes counter-balance each other leading to a neutral average effect on the metric.
For example, the treatment can make users place fewer orders but increase the average order value at the same time so the two effects neutralise each other and the revenue per user stays intact.

Situations where the treatment changes the variance of the event outcome, leaving the average unaffected.
For example, the treatment can increase the variability in the order values without changing their average.

If in a given practical application, having sensitivity to one of the scenarios above is highly undesirable, it is safer to adhere to alternative sequential testing methods (e.g.
[
12
,
11
]
).

Note that none of the problematic two scenarios can materialise when the event outcome is constant (i. e. the metric of interest simply counts events). In addition, the first scenario cannot hold when the treatment cannot affect the number of events, for example, when the events correspond to incoming patients in a medical study.

We see studying the behaviour of our proposed method in situations mentioned in this section and seeking mitigation strategies as a topic for future research.

It is worth emphasising that in some situations it can be desirable to capture all relevant potential defects (beyond the effect on the mean)
[
21
]
. For example, an increased outcome variance can indicate that the effect of the treatment is heterogeneous and some important subgroups of the population can be negatively affected (even though the overall mean stays the same). In such scenarios, the extra sensitivity mentioned in this section is not really a limitation but an advantage instead.

6
Conclusion

This paper proposes a novel sequential test for continuous experiment monitoring. The proposed test supports both discrete and real-valued metrics. Its effectiveness is demonstrated in an empirical study (a semi-synthetic experiment based on real-world data).
We hope that the proposed method will enable a wider use of sequential testing for online evaluation.

Proof of Proposition
1
.

Fix
k\leq N-L_{N}
. Conditional on
\mathcal{G}_{k,N}
, the event stream through event
N
and the signs of seen users are fixed. Assumption 1 and (

R_{k,N}
is a weighted sum of independent fair signs and is symmetric about zero. In particular,

\Pr\{R_{k,N}\geq 0\mid\mathcal{G}_{k,N}\}\geq 1/2.

By (
21
),
B_{k,N}\geq-\epsilon_{N}s_{k,N}
, so

\displaystyle\Pr\{B_{k,N}+R_{k,N}\geq 0\mid\mathcal{G}_{k,N}\}

\displaystyle\geq\Pr\{R_{k,N}\geq\epsilon_{N}s_{k,N}\mid\mathcal{G}_{k,N}\}

\displaystyle\geq 1/2-\Pr\{|R_{k,N}|\leq\epsilon_{N}s_{k,N}\mid\mathcal{G}_{k,N}\}

\displaystyle\geq 1/2-\rho_{N}.

Taking conditional expectations given
\mathcal{F}_{k}
gives the same lower bound at time

. The hypotheses hold simultaneously at all such

, so this proves Assumption 3(a). Replacing

Proof of Theorem
2
.

Let
\xi_{N}=(S_{N}-N\mu)/\sqrt{var(S_{N})}
. By the assumed CLT,
\xi_{N}=O_{p}(1)
. Since the deterministic sequence
V_{N}=var(S_{N})/N
is bounded above,

\frac{S_{N}}{N}=\mu+\xi_{N}\sqrt{\frac{V_{N}}{N}}\to_{p}\mu>0.

(26)

For fixed
\alpha\in(0,1)
, write
z=z_{1-\alpha/2}>0
. The condition
\hat{V}_{N}=O_{p}(1)
under the alternative gives

\frac{z\sqrt{N\hat{V}_{N}}}{N}=z\sqrt{\frac{\hat{V}_{N}}{N}}\to_{p}0.

Consequently,

\displaystyle\Pr\left\{\max_{n\leq N}S_{n}>z\sqrt{N\hat{V}_{N}}\right\}

\displaystyle\geq\Pr\left\{S_{N}>z\sqrt{N\hat{V}_{N}}\right\}

\displaystyle=\Pr\left\{\frac{S_{N}}{N}-z\sqrt{\frac{\hat{V}_{N}}{N}}>0\right\}\to 1.

B.2
Non-Normal Data

The derivation of YEAST involves the application of the Central Limit Theorem to sums of
X_{i}
(increments in the metric difference between the two groups). For a fixed sample size, the quality of the normal approximation depends on the distribution of
X_{i}
. In this section, we explore the performance of YEAST in situations where the distribution of
Y_{i}^{c}
and
Y_{i}^{t}
is not normal. Specifically, we used two alternative distributions: Student’s t which has heavier tails than the normal distribution and Gamma which is asymmetric. Student’s t distribution had 3 degrees of freedom and was shifted by
\sqrt{3}
for the control and by
\sqrt{3}(1+\xi)
for the treatment. The shifting was done to maintain the same coefficient of variation as in Section
B.1
. The Gamma distribution had its shape parameter set to 1.0. The scale parameter equalled
2
for the control and
2(1+\xi)
for the treatment. The effect size
\xi
took the same values as in the first simulation experiment:
\xi=0.0,\,0.1,\,0.2,\,0.3,\,0.4
. The random seed was set to 2023 for the simulations with the Student’s t distribution and to 2024 for the simulations with the Gamma distribution. The two seeds were different to avoid dependence across the two sets of simulations.

The results are depicted in Figure
1
. The first data point on each line corresponds to the case of no treatment effect and therefore represents the false detection rate. The remaining points represent the power for different treatment effect sizes.
Similarly to the experiment with normal data, YEAST had a considerably higher power curve (both for the Gamma and the Student’s t distribution cases) than other methods that did not inflate the false detection rate.
