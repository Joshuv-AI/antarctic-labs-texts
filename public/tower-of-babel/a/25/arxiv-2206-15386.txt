Phase-Field Finite Deformation Fracture with an Effective Energy for Regularized Crack Face Contact Preprint: Accepted to appear in Journal of the Mechanics and Physics of Solids (DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jmps.2022.104994 )
Maryam Hakimzadeh, Vaibhav Agrawal, Kaushik Dayal, Carlos Mora-Corral

Abstract
Phase-field models are a leading approach for realistic fracture problems. They treat the crack as a second phase and use gradient terms to smear out the crack faces, enabling the use of standard numerical methods for simulations. This regularization causes cracks to occupy a finite volume in the reference, and leads to the inability to appropriately model the closing or contacting – without healing – of crack faces. Specifically, the classical idealized crack face tractions are that the shear component is zero, and that the normal component is zero when the crack opens and identical to the intact material when the crack closes. Phase-field fracture models do not replicate this behavior. This work addresses this shortcoming by introducing an effective crack energy density that endows the regularized (finite volume) phase-field crack with the effective properties of an idealized sharp crack. The approach is based on applying the QR (upper triangular) decomposition of the deformation gradient tensor in the basis of the crack, enabling a transparent identification of the crack deformation modes. By then relaxing over those modes that do not cost energy, an effective energy is obtained that has the intact response when the crack faces close and zero energy when the crack faces are open. The effective energy is often, but not always, consistent with the classical crack-face tractions; it is shown here that there generally does not exist a stored energy that is consistent both with the classical crack-face tractions and with reproducing the intact response when the crack closes. A highlight of this approach is that it lies completely in the setting of finite deformation, enabling potential application to soft materials and other settings with large deformation or rotations. The model is applied to numerically study representative complex loadings, including (1) cyclic loading on a cavity in a soft solid that shows the growth and closing of cracks in complex stress states; and (2) cyclic shear that shows a complex pattern of crack branching driven by the closure of cracks.

1
Introduction

Phase-field approaches to fracture regularize the singular crack and thereby enable easy numerical calculations for complex fracture problems.
The key idea is to introduce an additional scalar field
\displaystyle\phi
and then regularize the field through the introduction of gradient terms, of the form
\displaystyle|\nabla\phi|^{2}
, in the energy.
The field
\displaystyle\phi
tracks the level of damage in the domain, and the crack corresponds to regions that are completely damaged.
The regularization term
\displaystyle|\nabla\phi|^{2}
is consequently related to the energy associated with the formation of fracture surfaces.
This regularization enables the use of standard computational methods, specifically the finite element method (FEM), to approximate the evolution of cracks in a specimen under load.

The regularization, however, causes cracks to occupy a finite volume in the reference configuration, and leads to unphysical behavior when an existing crack is subject to loads that causes the crack faces to close
1
1

1

We use “close” to denote crack faces that come in contact and
not
that the crack heals; we do not allow cracks to heal in this work.

.
For instance, the earliest phase-field models of fracture used simply that the damaged region has zero elastic energy; however, this causes cracks in this model to grow even under compressive load as it provides a means of relieving elastic energy of all kinds, not just those that drive crack growth in real systems.
In addition, these models allow for interpenetration of the crack faces.
To address these issues,
miehe-ijnme
proposed a partition of the elastic energy into tensile and compressive parts, and allowed the damaged region to sustain only the compressive part.
Related to this,
amor-jmps
proposed a partition of the energy into compressive hydrostatic, tensile hydrostatic, and deviatoric parts, and allowed the damaged region to sustain only the compressive hydrostatic part.
However, this class of approaches has a key shortcoming: the energy partitioning does not consider the crack direction at all.
For instance, tension across the crack faces that drives crack growth is not distinguished from tension along the length of the crack that does not directly drive crack growth.
Appendix
A
describes specific instances where this class of approaches gives incorrect stresses; in turn, this can lead to incorrect driving forces for crack growth and affect the crack-parallel T-stress.

Based on the recognition that it is essential to consider the crack orientation in defining the energy of the damaged region,
strobl2015
;
strobl2016
;
agrawal-thesis
;
steinke2019phase
;
fei2020phase
proposed models, in the linear regime, for the damaged elastic energy that account for the crack orientation.
The current work builds on these approaches in that we account for the crack normal in defining the energy density of the damaged region.
Figure
1
summarizes our overall approach: we aim to derive an effective crack energy such that a regularized crack volume has the same response as an idealized sharp crack.
We achieve this by separating the kinematics associated with each of the deformation modes in Figure
1
, and then appropriately treating each mode.

Our technical strategy in brief is as follows.
Rather than starting with the typical polar decomposition of the deformation gradient
\displaystyle{\mathbfit F}
, we use the QR or Gram–Schmidt multiplicative decomposition of
\displaystyle{\mathbfit F}
into a rotation and an upper-triangular part in the frame of the crack.
The upper triangular representation in the frame of the crack provides a transparent and direct measure of the opening or closing of the crack face, the stretch along the length of the crack, and the crack face shear deformation.
We then obtain the damaged elastic energy by minimizing the intact energy over the crack face shear – using that the idealized crack cannot sustain shear tractions – and minimizing it over the crack opening stretch if the crack is opening.
This strategy provides a constructive approach to obtaining the effective damaged energy given the form of the intact energy.
We demonstrate the efficacy of this approach in numerical calculations: first, of the elastic response of cracks that do not evolve; and, second, of the response and growth of cracks in complex settings that include compressive and cyclic loadings that cause crack closure.

1.A
The Classical Phase-Field Model of Fracture

The starting point of phase-field modeling of brittle fracture is the influential variational model of quasistatic crack evolution due to Francfort and Marigo

In its simplest form, it consists in minimizing the energy

(1)

is the stored energy density of the material.
The evolution of the system is typically given by an external loading that depends on the time
\displaystyle t

1

The proposed evolution law postulates that at each time
\displaystyle t

\displaystyle({\mathbfit y}(t),\Gamma(t))

Motivation and heuristics

The term

This

\displaystyle\nabla{\mathbfit y}

3
The Classical Crack Face Traction Condition

The classical crack face traction conditions for a smooth frictionless crack are that the shear traction is zero and that the normal traction is either zero (when the crack is open) or compressive (when the crack is closed).
To this, we append the natural condition that the compressive normal traction, when the crack is closed, is identical to that in the intact material under the same deformation.
The key question in this section is to understand if the proposed energy
\displaystyle W_{\mathrm{d}}
satisfies these traction conditions.
In summary, we find that it does so for some but not all materials.

In terms of the (Piola–Kirchhoff) stress
\displaystyle{\mathbfit T}^{\mathrm{d}}
in the damaged material, and the stress
\displaystyle{\mathbfit T}
in the intact material, the classical crack face conditions can be written as

T_{{\mathbfit t}_{1}{\mathbfit n}}^{\mathrm{d}}=T_{{\mathbfit t}_{2}{\mathbfit n}}^{\mathrm{d}}=0\quad\text{and}\quad T_{{\mathbfit n}{\mathbfit n}}^{\mathrm{d}}=\min\{T_{{\mathbfit n}{\mathbfit n}},0\}.

(14)

The notation for subindices is so that
\displaystyle T^{\mathrm{d}}_{{\mathbfit t}_{i}{\mathbfit n}}
indicates the shear components of the traction, while
\displaystyle T^{\mathrm{d}}_{{\mathbfit n}{\mathbfit n}}
denotes the normal component of the traction, and analogously for
\displaystyle{\mathbfit T}
.
Of course, the stresses are the derivatives of the corresponding elastic energy densities.

A potential strategy to construct
\displaystyle W_{\mathrm{d}}
that satisfies (
14
) could be to simply use (
14
) as the starting point, and integrate appropriately to construct the corresponding stored energy density.
By QR decomposition and frame-indifference, in order for (
14
) to be satisfied for all deformation gradients, it is enough that it holds for those of the form (
6
).
Standard aguments based on the symmetry of the second derivative show that, given
\displaystyle W
(hence
\displaystyle{\mathbfit T}
), an energy
\displaystyle W_{\mathrm{d}}
exists satisfying (
14
) if and only if

\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial A_{{\mathbfit n}{\mathbfit n}}\,\partial A_{{\mathbfit t}_{i}{\mathbfit n}}}W\left({\mathbfit A}_{{\mathbfit t}_{1},{\mathbfit t}_{2},{\mathbfit n}}(A_{{\mathbfit n}{\mathbfit n}},A_{{\mathbfit t}_{1}{\mathbfit t}_{1}},A_{{\mathbfit t}_{2}{\mathbfit t}_{2}},A_{{\mathbfit t}_{1}{\mathbfit n}},A_{{\mathbfit t}_{2}{\mathbfit n}},A_{{\mathbfit t}_{1}{\mathbfit t}_{2}})\right)=0,\qquad i=1,2.

(15)

\displaystyle\{{\mathbfit t}_{1},{\mathbfit t}_{2},{\mathbfit n}\}
,

\displaystyle\displaystyle W\left({\mathbfit A}_{{\mathbfit t}_{1},{\mathbfit t}_{2},{\mathbfit n}}(A_{{\mathbfit n}{\mathbfit n}},A_{{\mathbfit t}_{1}{\mathbfit t}_{1}},A_{{\mathbfit t}_{2}{\mathbfit t}_{2}},A_{{\mathbfit t}_{1}{\mathbfit n}},A_{{\mathbfit t}_{2}{\mathbfit n}},A_{{\mathbfit t}_{1}{\mathbfit t}_{2}})\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=a\left(A_{{\mathbfit t}_{1}{\mathbfit t}_{1}}^{2}+A_{{\mathbfit t}_{2}{\mathbfit t}_{2}}^{2}+A_{{\mathbfit n}{\mathbfit n}}^{2}+A_{{\mathbfit t}_{1}{\mathbfit t}_{2}}^{2}+A_{{\mathbfit t}_{1}{\mathbfit n}}^{2}+A_{{\mathbfit t}_{2}{\mathbfit n}}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\quad+b\left(A_{{\mathbfit t}_{1}{\mathbfit t}_{1}}^{2}A_{{\mathbfit n}{\mathbfit n}}^{2}+A_{{\mathbfit t}_{2}{\mathbfit t}_{2}}^{2}A_{{\mathbfit n}{\mathbfit n}}^{2}+A_{{\mathbfit t}_{1}{\mathbfit t}_{1}}^{2}A_{{\mathbfit t}_{2}{\mathbfit t}_{2}}^{2}+A_{{\mathbfit t}_{1}{\mathbfit t}_{1}}^{2}A_{{\mathbfit t}_{2}{\mathbfit n}}^{2}+A_{{\mathbfit n}{\mathbfit n}}^{2}A_{{\mathbfit t}_{1}{\mathbfit t}_{2}}^{2}+\left(A_{{\mathbfit t}_{2}{\mathbfit t}_{2}}A_{{\mathbfit t}_{1}{\mathbfit n}}-A_{{\mathbfit t}_{2}{\mathbfit n}}A_{{\mathbfit t}_{1}{\mathbfit t}_{2}}\right)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\quad+h(A_{{\mathbfit t}_{1}{\mathbfit t}_{1}}A_{{\mathbfit t}_{2}{\mathbfit t}_{2}}A_{{\mathbfit n}{\mathbfit n}}),

which is readily seen to satisfy (
15
).

On the other hand, if we consider exponents
\displaystyle p,q>0
with
\displaystyle(p,q)\neq(2,2)
then it is easy to check that the energy

W({\mathbfit F})=a\left|{\mathbfit F}\right|^{p}+b\left|\cof{\mathbfit F}\right|^{q}+h(\det{\mathbfit F})

does not satisfy (
15
).
In summary, it shows that, for general energies, there do not exist effective crack energy densities that both satisfy the crack face traction conditions and have the correct intact response when the crack closes.

3.B
Traction on the crack face satisfied by the effective energy

In this subsection we calculate the tractions
\displaystyle T_{{\mathbfit t}_{i}{\mathbfit n}}^{\mathrm{d}}
and
\displaystyle T_{{\mathbfit n}{\mathbfit n}}^{\mathrm{d}}
for the effective energy constructed in Definition
2.1
, and compare them with (
14
).

Let
\displaystyle A_{{\mathbfit n}{\mathbfit n}},A_{{\mathbfit t}_{1}{\mathbfit t}_{1}},A_{{\mathbfit t}_{2}{\mathbfit t}_{2}}>0
and
\displaystyle A_{{\mathbfit t}_{1}{\mathbfit n}},A_{{\mathbfit t}_{2}{\mathbfit n}},A_{{\mathbfit t}_{1}{\mathbfit t}_{2}}\in\mathbb{R}
.
From Definition
2.1
we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle W_{\mathrm{d}}\left({\mathbfit A}_{{\mathbfit t}_{1},{\mathbfit t}_{2},{\mathbfit n}}(A_{{\mathbfit n}{\mathbfit n}},A_{{\mathbfit t}_{1}{\mathbfit t}_{1}},A_{{\mathbfit t}_{2}{\mathbfit t}_{2}},A_{{\mathbfit t}_{1}{\mathbfit n}},A_{{\mathbfit t}_{2}{\mathbfit n}},A_{{\mathbfit t}_{1}{\mathbfit t}_{2}}),{\mathbfit n}\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\begin{cases}W\left({\mathbfit A}_{{\mathbfit t}_{1},{\mathbfit t}_{2},{\mathbfit n}}(A^{*}_{{\mathbfit n}{\mathbfit n}},A_{{\mathbfit t}_{1}{\mathbfit t}_{1}},A_{{\mathbfit t}_{2}{\mathbfit t}_{2}},A^{*}_{{\mathbfit t}_{1}{\mathbfit n}},A^{*}_{{\mathbfit t}_{2}{\mathbfit n}},A_{{\mathbfit t}_{1}{\mathbfit t}_{2}})\right),&\text{if }A_{{\mathbfit n}{\mathbfit n}}\geq A_{{\mathbfit n}{\mathbfit n}}^{*},\\
W\left({\mathbfit A}_{{\mathbfit t}_{1},{\mathbfit t}_{2},{\mathbfit n}}(A_{{\mathbfit n}{\mathbfit n}},A_{{\mathbfit t}_{1}{\mathbfit t}_{1}},A_{{\mathbfit t}_{2}{\mathbfit t}_{2}},A^{**}_{{\mathbfit t}_{1}{\mathbfit n}},A^{**}_{{\mathbfit t}_{2}{\mathbfit n}},A_{{\mathbfit t}_{1}{\mathbfit t}_{2}})\right),&\text{if }A_{{\mathbfit n}{\mathbfit n}}<A_{{\mathbfit n}{\mathbfit n}}^{*},\end{cases}

for some functions

\displaystyle\displaystyle A^{*}_{{\mathbfit n}{\mathbfit n}}=A^{*}_{{\mathbfit n}{\mathbfit n}}(A_{{\mathbfit t}_{1}{\mathbfit t}_{1}},A_{{\mathbfit t}_{2}{\mathbfit t}_{2}},A_{{\mathbfit t}_{1}{\mathbfit t}_{2}}),\qquad A^{*}_{{\mathbfit t}_{i}{\mathbfit n}}=A^{*}_{{\mathbfit t}_{i}{\mathbfit n}}(A_{{\mathbfit t}_{1}{\mathbfit t}_{1}},A_{{\mathbfit t}_{2}{\mathbfit t}_{2}},A_{{\mathbfit t}_{1}{\mathbfit t}_{2}})\quad(i=1,2),

\displaystyle\displaystyle A^{**}_{{\mathbfit t}_{i}{\mathbfit n}}=A^{**}_{{\mathbfit t}_{i}{\mathbfit n}}(A_{{\mathbfit n}{\mathbfit n}},A_{{\mathbfit t}_{1}{\mathbfit t}_{1}},A_{{\mathbfit t}_{2}{\mathbfit t}_{2}},A_{{\mathbfit t}_{1}{\mathbfit t}_{2}})\quad(i=1,2).

Assume, for simplicity, that the functions
\displaystyle A^{*}_{{\mathbfit n}{\mathbfit n}},A^{*}_{{\mathbfit t}_{i}{\mathbfit n}},A^{**}_{{\mathbfit t}_{i}{\mathbfit n}}
can be defined uniquely, at least locally; we will see in Section

an example of this situation.
The derivatives of
\displaystyle W_{\mathrm{d}}
in the directions
\displaystyle({\mathbfit t}_{i},{\mathbfit n})
and
\displaystyle({\mathbfit n},{\mathbfit n})

\frac{\partial W_{\mathrm{d}}}{\partial A_{{\mathbfit t}_{i}{\mathbfit n}}}\left({\mathbfit A}_{{\mathbfit t}_{1},{\mathbfit t}_{2},{\mathbfit n}}(A_{{\mathbfit n}{\mathbfit n}},A_{{\mathbfit t}_{1}{\mathbfit t}_{1}},A_{{\mathbfit t}_{2}{\mathbfit t}_{2}},A_{{\mathbfit t}_{1}{\mathbfit n}},A_{{\mathbfit t}_{2}{\mathbfit n}},A_{{\mathbfit t}_{1}{\mathbfit t}_{2}}),{\mathbfit n}\right)=0,\qquad i=1,2

(16)

and

\begin{split}&\frac{\partial W_{\mathrm{d}}}{\partial A_{{\mathbfit n}{\mathbfit n}}}\left({\mathbfit A}_{{\mathbfit t}_{1},{\mathbfit t}_{2},{\mathbfit n}}(A_{{\mathbfit n}{\mathbfit n}},A_{{\mathbfit t}_{1}{\mathbfit t}_{1}},A_{{\mathbfit t}_{2}{\mathbfit t}_{2}},A_{{\mathbfit t}_{1}{\mathbfit n}},A_{{\mathbfit t}_{2}{\mathbfit n}},A_{{\mathbfit t}_{1}{\mathbfit t}_{2}}),{\mathbfit n}\right)\\
&=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }A_{{\mathbfit n}{\mathbfit n}}>A_{{\mathbfit n}{\mathbfit n}}^{*},\\
\frac{\partial W}{\partial A_{{\mathbfit n}{\mathbfit n}}}\left({\mathbfit A}_{{\mathbfit t}_{1},{\mathbfit t}_{2},{\mathbfit n}}(A_{{\mathbfit n}{\mathbfit n}},A_{{\mathbfit t}_{1}{\mathbfit t}_{1}},A_{{\mathbfit t}_{2}{\mathbfit t}_{2}},A^{**}_{{\mathbfit t}_{1}{\mathbfit n}},A^{**}_{{\mathbfit t}_{2}{\mathbfit n}},A_{{\mathbfit t}_{1}{\mathbfit t}_{2}})\right)&\text{if }A_{{\mathbfit n}{\mathbfit n}}<A_{{\mathbfit n}{\mathbfit n}}^{*},\end{cases}\end{split}

(17)

since

\frac{\partial W}{\partial A_{{\mathbfit t}_{i}{\mathbfit n}}}\left({\mathbfit A}_{{\mathbfit t}_{1},{\mathbfit t}_{2},{\mathbfit n}}(A_{{\mathbfit n}{\mathbfit n}},A_{{\mathbfit t}_{1}{\mathbfit t}_{1}},A_{{\mathbfit t}_{2}{\mathbfit t}_{2}},A^{**}_{{\mathbfit t}_{1}{\mathbfit n}},A^{**}_{{\mathbfit t}_{2}{\mathbfit n}},A_{{\mathbfit t}_{1}{\mathbfit t}_{2}})\right)=0,\qquad i=1,2

because
\displaystyle A^{**}_{{\mathbfit t}_{1}{\mathbfit n}},A^{**}_{{\mathbfit t}_{2}{\mathbfit n}}
are minimizers of
\displaystyle W\left({\mathbfit A}_{{\mathbfit t}_{1},{\mathbfit t}_{2},{\mathbfit n}}(A_{{\mathbfit n}{\mathbfit n}},A_{{\mathbfit t}_{1}{\mathbfit t}_{1}},A_{{\mathbfit t}_{2}{\mathbfit t}_{2}},A_{{\mathbfit t}_{1}{\mathbfit n}},A_{{\mathbfit t}_{2}{\mathbfit n}},A_{{\mathbfit t}_{1}{\mathbfit t}_{2}})\right)
.

We compare these results with the crack frace conditions (
14
).
Let
\displaystyle{\mathbfit T}
and
\displaystyle{\mathbfit T}^{\mathrm{d}}
be as in the beginning of this section.
Formulas (
16
) and (
17
) show that, for strains of the form
\displaystyle{\mathbfit A}_{{\mathbfit t}_{1},{\mathbfit t}_{2},{\mathbfit n}}
(see (

)),

T_{{\mathbfit t}_{1}{\mathbfit n}}^{\mathrm{d}}=T_{{\mathbfit t}_{2}{\mathbfit n}}^{\mathrm{d}}=0

(18)

and

T_{{\mathbfit n}{\mathbfit n}}^{\mathrm{d}}=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }A_{{\mathbfit n}{\mathbfit n}}>A_{{\mathbfit n}{\mathbfit n}}^{*},\\
T_{{\mathbfit n}{\mathbfit n}}\left({\mathbfit A}_{{\mathbfit t}_{1},{\mathbfit t}_{2},{\mathbfit n}}(A_{{\mathbfit n}{\mathbfit n}},A_{{\mathbfit t}_{1}{\mathbfit t}_{1}},A_{{\mathbfit t}_{2}{\mathbfit t}_{2}},A^{**}_{{\mathbfit t}_{1}{\mathbfit n}},A^{**}_{{\mathbfit t}_{2}{\mathbfit n}},A_{{\mathbfit t}_{1}{\mathbfit t}_{2}})\right)&\text{if }A_{{\mathbfit n}{\mathbfit n}}<A_{{\mathbfit n}{\mathbfit n}}^{*}.\end{cases}

(19)

Nevertheless, in the particular case that

\begin{cases}0\leq T_{{\mathbfit n}{\mathbfit n}}&\text{if }A_{{\mathbfit n}{\mathbfit n}}>A_{{\mathbfit n}{\mathbfit n}}^{*},\\
T_{{\mathbfit n}{\mathbfit n}}\left({\mathbfit A}_{{\mathbfit t}_{1},{\mathbfit t}_{2},{\mathbfit n}}(A_{{\mathbfit n}{\mathbfit n}},A_{{\mathbfit t}_{1}{\mathbfit t}_{1}},A_{{\mathbfit t}_{2}{\mathbfit t}_{2}},A^{**}_{{\mathbfit t}_{1}{\mathbfit n}},A^{**}_{{\mathbfit t}_{2}{\mathbfit n}},A_{{\mathbfit t}_{1}{\mathbfit t}_{2}})\right)=T_{{\mathbfit n}{\mathbfit n}}\leq 0&\text{if }A_{{\mathbfit n}{\mathbfit n}}<A_{{\mathbfit n}{\mathbfit n}}^{*},\end{cases}

(20)

we have that conditions (

) are equivalent to (

).

Let us have a closer look to (

).
As
\displaystyle A_{{\mathbfit n}{\mathbfit n}}^{*},A^{*}_{{\mathbfit t}_{1}{\mathbfit n}},A^{*}_{{\mathbfit t}_{2}{\mathbfit n}}
are minimizers of
\displaystyle W\left({\mathbfit A}_{{\mathbfit t}_{1},{\mathbfit t}_{2},{\mathbfit n}}\right)
(where
\displaystyle{\mathbfit A}_{{\mathbfit t}_{1},{\mathbfit t}_{2},{\mathbfit n}}
is as in (

)), we have that

T_{{\mathbfit n}{\mathbfit n}}\left({\mathbfit A}_{{\mathbfit t}_{1},{\mathbfit t}_{2},{\mathbfit n}}(A^{*}_{{\mathbfit n}{\mathbfit n}},A_{{\mathbfit t}_{1}{\mathbfit t}_{1}},A_{{\mathbfit t}_{2}{\mathbfit t}_{2}},A^{*}_{{\mathbfit t}_{1}{\mathbfit n}},A^{*}_{{\mathbfit t}_{2}{\mathbfit n}},A_{{\mathbfit t}_{1}{\mathbfit t}_{2}})\right)=0.

A natural condition would then be that

T_{{\mathbfit n}{\mathbfit n}}\left({\mathbfit A}_{{\mathbfit t}_{1},{\mathbfit t}_{2},{\mathbfit n}}(A_{{\mathbfit n}{\mathbfit n}},A_{{\mathbfit t}_{1}{\mathbfit t}_{1}},A_{{\mathbfit t}_{2}{\mathbfit t}_{2}},A^{*}_{{\mathbfit t}_{1}{\mathbfit n}},A^{*}_{{\mathbfit t}_{2}{\mathbfit n}},A_{{\mathbfit t}_{1}{\mathbfit t}_{2}})\right)\geq 0\quad\text{when }A_{{\mathbfit n}{\mathbfit n}}>A^{*}_{{\mathbfit n}{\mathbfit n}},

T_{{\mathbfit n}{\mathbfit n}}\left({\mathbfit A}_{{\mathbfit t}_{1},{\mathbfit t}_{2},{\mathbfit n}}(A_{{\mathbfit n}{\mathbfit n}},A_{{\mathbfit t}_{1}{\mathbfit t}_{1}},A_{{\mathbfit t}_{2}{\mathbfit t}_{2}},A^{*}_{{\mathbfit t}_{1}{\mathbfit n}},A^{*}_{{\mathbfit t}_{2}{\mathbfit n}},A_{{\mathbfit t}_{1}{\mathbfit t}_{2}})\right)\leq 0\quad\text{when }A_{{\mathbfit n}{\mathbfit n}}<A^{*}_{{\mathbfit n}{\mathbfit n}},

while only in the few situations where the expression of the energy
\displaystyle W
has an uncoupled dependence on the terms
\displaystyle A_{{\mathbfit n}{\mathbfit n}},A_{{\mathbfit t}_{1}{\mathbfit n}},A_{{\mathbfit t}_{2}{\mathbfit n}}
we additionally have that

T_{{\mathbfit n}{\mathbfit n}}\left({\mathbfit A}_{{\mathbfit t}_{1},{\mathbfit t}_{2},{\mathbfit n}}(A_{{\mathbfit n}{\mathbfit n}},A_{{\mathbfit t}_{1}{\mathbfit t}_{1}},A_{{\mathbfit t}_{2}{\mathbfit t}_{2}},A_{{\mathbfit t}_{1}{\mathbfit n}},A_{{\mathbfit t}_{2}{\mathbfit n}},A_{{\mathbfit t}_{1}{\mathbfit t}_{2}})\right)\geq 0\quad\text{when }A_{{\mathbfit n}{\mathbfit n}}>A^{*}_{{\mathbfit n}{\mathbfit n}},

and

\displaystyle\displaystyle T_{{\mathbfit n}{\mathbfit n}}\left({\mathbfit A}_{{\mathbfit t}_{1},{\mathbfit t}_{2},{\mathbfit n}}(A_{{\mathbfit n}{\mathbfit n}},A_{{\mathbfit t}_{1}{\mathbfit t}_{1}},A_{{\mathbfit t}_{2}{\mathbfit t}_{2}},A^{**}_{{\mathbfit t}_{1}{\mathbfit n}},A^{**}_{{\mathbfit t}_{2}{\mathbfit n}},A_{{\mathbfit t}_{1}{\mathbfit t}_{2}})\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=T_{{\mathbfit n}{\mathbfit n}}\left({\mathbfit A}_{{\mathbfit t}_{1},{\mathbfit t}_{2},{\mathbfit n}}(A_{{\mathbfit n}{\mathbfit n}},A_{{\mathbfit t}_{1}{\mathbfit t}_{1}},A_{{\mathbfit t}_{2}{\mathbfit t}_{2}},A_{{\mathbfit t}_{1}{\mathbfit n}},A_{{\mathbfit t}_{2}{\mathbfit n}},A_{{\mathbfit t}_{1}{\mathbfit t}_{2}})\right)\leq 0\quad\text{when }A_{{\mathbfit n}{\mathbfit n}}<A^{*}_{{\mathbfit n}{\mathbfit n}}.

We thus recover the interpretation that (

\displaystyle W
has an uncoupled dependence of
\displaystyle A_{{\mathbfit n}{\mathbfit n}}
and
\displaystyle A_{{\mathbfit t}_{i}{\mathbfit n}}
.

4
Example material: Mooney–Rivlin

In this section we compute the effective energy for a Mooney–Rivlin material, in both 2D and 3D.

4.A
Neo-Hookean material in 2D

We consider the neo-Hookean energy in 2D

W({\mathbfit F})=\frac{\mu}{2}\left(|{\mathbfit F}|^{2}-2-2\log\det{\mathbfit F}\right)+\frac{\lambda}{2}\left(\det{\mathbfit F}-1\right)^{2},

(21)

for
\displaystyle\mu,\lambda>0
.
Note that the minimum value of
\displaystyle W
is zero and is attained at
\displaystyle SO(2)
.
Moreover, it is easy to check that the elasticity tensor
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathsf{C}}=D^{2}W({\mathbfit I})
is given by

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{C}}\mathbfit{\varepsilon}:\mathbfit{\varepsilon}=2\mu|\mathbfit{\varepsilon}|^{2}+\lambda(\tr\mathbfit{\varepsilon})^{2}

(22)

for symmetric
\displaystyle\mathbfit{\varepsilon}
, so
\displaystyle\lambda
and
\displaystyle\mu
are the Lamé parameters.

We compute
\displaystyle W_{\mathrm{d}}
according to Definition
2.1
with the obvious modifications for 2D.
As a consequence of Proposition
2.4
, it is enough to calculate
\displaystyle W_{\mathrm{d}}({\mathbfit A},{\mathbfit e}_{2})
for upper triangular matrices
\displaystyle{\mathbfit A}
with positive diagonal elements.
The orthonormal basis chosen will be, of course,
\displaystyle\{{\mathbfit e}_{1},{\mathbfit e}_{2}\}
.
Since it is the canonical basis, we will use the usual triangular representation of a matrix, instead of the notation
\displaystyle A_{22}{\mathbfit e}_{2}\otimes{\mathbfit e}_{2}+A_{11}{\mathbfit e}_{1}\otimes{\mathbfit e}_{1}+A_{12}{\mathbfit e}_{1}\otimes{\mathbfit e}_{2}
.

For
\displaystyle A_{11},A_{22}>0
and
\displaystyle A_{12}\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

W\begin{pmatrix}A_{11}&A_{12}\\
0&A_{22}\end{pmatrix}=\frac{\mu}{2}\left(A_{11}^{2}+A_{12}^{2}+A_{22}^{2}-2-2\log(A_{11}A_{22})\right)+\frac{\lambda}{2}(A_{11}A_{22}-1)^{2}.

Given
\displaystyle A_{11}>0
the minimum of the above expression in
\displaystyle A_{22}>0
and
\displaystyle A_{12}\in\mathbb{R}
is easily seen to be attained at
\displaystyle A^{*}_{12}=0
and

A_{22}^{*}(A_{11}):=\frac{\lambda A_{11}+\sqrt{4\mu^{2}+4\mu\lambda A_{11}^{2}+\lambda^{2}A_{11}^{2}}}{2(\mu+\lambda A_{11}^{2})},

(23)

while given
\displaystyle A_{11},A_{22}>0
the minimum in
\displaystyle A_{12}\in\mathbb{R}
is easily seen to be attained at
\displaystyle A^{**}_{12}=0
.
For future reference, note that

A_{22}^{*}(A_{11})>1\quad\text{if and only if}\quad A_{11}<1.

(24)

The expression for
\displaystyle W_{\mathrm{d}}
is, therefore,

W_{\mathrm{d}}\left(\begin{pmatrix}A_{11}&A_{12}\\
0&A_{22}\end{pmatrix},{\mathbfit e}_{2}\right)=\begin{cases}W\begin{pmatrix}A_{11}&0\\
0&A_{22}^{*}\end{pmatrix},&\text{if }A_{22}>A_{22}^{*},\\
W\begin{pmatrix}A_{11}&0\\
0&A_{22}\end{pmatrix},&\text{if }A_{22}\leq A_{22}^{*}.\end{cases}

(25)

The values of
\displaystyle W_{\mathrm{d}}({\mathbfit F},{\mathbfit n})
for any
\displaystyle({\mathbfit F},{\mathbfit n})
can be calculated with the formula above and the relations

W_{\mathrm{d}}({\mathbfit Q}{\mathbfit F},{\mathbfit n})=W_{\mathrm{d}}({\mathbfit F},{\mathbfit n})=W_{\mathrm{d}}({\mathbfit F}{\mathbfit Q}^{T},{\mathbfit Q}{\mathbfit n}),\qquad{\mathbfit Q}\in SO(2).

(26)

(see Proposition
2.4
and note that
\displaystyle W
is isotropic).
More explicitly, we consider, for any unit vector
\displaystyle{\mathbfit n}
,

{\mathbfit Q}=\begin{pmatrix}n_{2}&-n_{1}\\
n_{1}&n_{2}\end{pmatrix},

(27)

which satisfies
\displaystyle{\mathbfit Q}\in SO(2)
and
\displaystyle{\mathbfit Q}{\mathbfit n}={\mathbfit e}_{2}
.
Then
\displaystyle W_{\mathrm{d}}({\mathbfit F},{\mathbfit n})=W_{\mathrm{d}}({\mathbfit F}{\mathbfit Q}^{T},{\mathbfit e}_{2})
.
We apply QR decomposition to
\displaystyle{\mathbfit G}:={\mathbfit F}{\mathbfit Q}^{T}
and obtain that
\displaystyle{\mathbfit G}={\mathbfit R}{\mathbfit A}
with
\displaystyle{\mathbfit R}\in SO(2)
and
\displaystyle{\mathbfit A}
upper triangular.
Elementary calculations show that

{\mathbfit A}=\begin{pmatrix}A_{11}&A_{12}\\
0&A_{22}\end{pmatrix}

with

A_{11}=\sqrt{G_{11}^{2}+G_{21}^{2}}=\sqrt{(F_{11}n_{2}-F_{12}n_{1})^{2}+(F_{21}n_{2}-F_{22}n_{1})^{2}},\qquad A_{22}=\frac{\det{\mathbfit F}}{A_{11}},

(28)

while the expression for
\displaystyle A_{12}
is not important.
Let
\displaystyle A_{22}^{*}
be as in (
23
).
Then

\boxed{W_{\mathrm{d}}({\mathbfit F},{\mathbfit n})=\begin{cases}\frac{\mu}{2}\left(A_{11}^{2}+(A_{22}^{*})^{2}-2-2\log(A_{11}A_{22}^{*})\right)+\frac{\lambda}{2}\left(A_{11}A_{22}^{*}-1\right)^{2}&\text{if }A_{22}>A_{22}^{*},\\
\frac{\mu}{2}\left(A_{11}^{2}+A_{22}^{2}-2-2\log(A_{11}A_{22})\right)+\frac{\lambda}{2}\left(A_{11}A_{22}-1\right)^{2}&\text{if }A_{22}\leq A_{22}^{*}.\end{cases}}

(29)

Using formulas (
23
) and (
25
), we compute the effective energy
\displaystyle W_{\mathrm{d}}
of the basic modes
(a)
–
(e)
described in Section
2.B
, with the obvious modifications for 2D, in the special case
\displaystyle{\mathbfit n}={\mathbfit e}_{2}
and
\displaystyle{\mathbfit t}={\mathbfit e}_{1}
:

(a)

\displaystyle{\mathbfit F}=A_{22}{\mathbfit e}_{2}\otimes{\mathbfit e}_{2}+{\mathbfit e}_{1}\otimes{\mathbfit e}_{1}
with
\displaystyle A_{22}>11
.
Then

W_{\mathrm{d}}\left(\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&A_{22}\end{pmatrix},{\mathbfit e}_{2}\right)=W\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}=0.

(b)

\displaystyle{\mathbfit F}={\mathbfit I}+A_{12}{\mathbfit e}_{1}\otimes{\mathbfit e}_{2}
with
\displaystyle A_{12}\in\mathbb{R}
.
Then

W_{\mathrm{d}}\left(\begin{pmatrix}1&A_{12}\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},{\mathbfit e}_{2}\right)=W\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}=0.

(c)

\displaystyle{\mathbfit F}={\mathbfit e}_{2}\otimes{\mathbfit e}_{2}+A_{11}{\mathbfit e}_{1}\otimes{\mathbfit e}_{1}
with
\displaystyle 0<A_{11}<1
.
Then, thanks to (
24
),

W_{\mathrm{d}}\left(\begin{pmatrix}A_{11}&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},{\mathbfit e}_{2}\right)=W\begin{pmatrix}A_{11}&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}.

(d)

\displaystyle{\mathbfit F}={\mathbfit e}_{2}\otimes{\mathbfit e}_{2}+A_{11}{\mathbfit e}_{1}\otimes{\mathbfit e}_{1}
with
\displaystyle A_{11}>1
.
Then, thanks to (
24
),

W_{\mathrm{d}}\left(\begin{pmatrix}A_{11}&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},{\mathbfit e}_{2}\right)=W\begin{pmatrix}A_{11}&0\\
0&A_{22}^{*}\end{pmatrix}.

It is interesting to note that, contrary to mode
(c)
, in this mode the energy of the cracked material is less than that of the intact material.
Under extension parallel to the crack, the damaged material undergoes – in addition to an extension of magnitude
\displaystyle A_{11}>1
parallel to the crack – a compression of magnitude
\displaystyle A_{22}^{*}<1
perpendicular to the crack due to the transverse shrinkage, because the material has a positive Poisson ratio.

(e)

\displaystyle{\mathbfit F}=A_{22}{\mathbfit e}_{2}\otimes{\mathbfit e}_{2}+{\mathbfit e}_{1}\otimes{\mathbfit e}_{1}
with
\displaystyle 0<A_{22}<1
.
Then

W_{\mathrm{d}}\left(\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&A_{22}\end{pmatrix},{\mathbfit e}_{2}\right)=W\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&A_{22}\end{pmatrix}.

These examples
(a)
–
(e)
show that the effective energy meet our expectations.
Now we calculate the effective energy of two other representative modes, for which an intermediate behavior is presented:
\displaystyle W_{\mathrm{d}}
will carry some energy but less than
\displaystyle W
, i.e.,
\displaystyle 0<W_{\mathrm{d}}<W
.

(f)

Isotropic compression with shear parallel to the crack:

\displaystyle{\mathbfit F}=\alpha{\mathbfit I}+A_{12}{\mathbfit e}_{1}\otimes{\mathbfit e}_{2}
with
\displaystyle 0<\alpha<1
and
\displaystyle A_{12}\in\mathbb{R}
.
Then

W_{\mathrm{d}}\left(\begin{pmatrix}\alpha&A_{12}\\
0&\alpha\end{pmatrix},{\mathbfit e}_{2}\right)=W\begin{pmatrix}\alpha&0\\
0&\alpha\end{pmatrix}.

(g)

Isotropic compression with shear perpendicular to the crack:

\displaystyle{\mathbfit F}=\alpha{\mathbfit I}+A_{21}{\mathbfit e}_{2}\otimes{\mathbfit e}_{1}
with
\displaystyle 0<\alpha<1
and
\displaystyle A_{21}\in\mathbb{R}
.
By QR decomposition,

\displaystyle\displaystyle W_{\mathrm{d}}\left(\begin{pmatrix}\alpha&0\\
A_{21}&\alpha\end{pmatrix},{\mathbfit e}_{2}\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=W_{\mathrm{d}}\left(\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{\alpha^{2}+A_{21}^{2}}&\frac{\alpha A_{21}}{\sqrt{\alpha^{2}+A_{21}^{2}}}\\
0&\frac{\alpha^{2}}{\sqrt{\alpha^{2}+A_{21}^{2}}}\end{pmatrix},{\mathbfit e}_{2}\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=W\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{\alpha^{2}+A_{21}^{2}}&0\\
0&\frac{\alpha^{2}}{\sqrt{\alpha^{2}+A_{21}^{2}}}\end{pmatrix}.

4.B
Mooney–Rivlin material in 3D

We consider the Mooney–Rivlin energy

W({\mathbfit F})=\frac{\mu_{1}}{2}\left(\left|{\mathbfit F}\right|^{2}-2\log\det{\mathbfit F}-3\right)+\frac{\mu_{2}}{2}\left(\left|\cof{\mathbfit F}\right|^{2}-4\log\det{\mathbfit F}-3\right)+\frac{\bar{\lambda}}{2}\left(\det{\mathbfit F}-1\right)^{2},

where
\displaystyle\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\bar{\lambda}>0
.
The minimum of
\displaystyle W
is
\displaystyle 0
and is attained at
\displaystyle SO(3)
.
The Lamé parameters of this material are
\displaystyle\lambda=\bar{\lambda}+2\mu_{2}
and
\displaystyle\mu=\mu_{1}+\mu_{2}
, since the elasticity tensor
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathsf{C}}
at the origin is given by (
22
).
They are related with Young’s modulus
\displaystyle E
and Poisson’s ratio
\displaystyle\nu
as

\mu=\frac{E}{2(1+\nu)},\qquad\lambda=\frac{E\nu}{(1+\nu)(1-2\nu)},\qquad\nu=\frac{\lambda}{2(\lambda+\mu)}.

In particular, this material has a positive Poisson ratio.

We do the calculations of
\displaystyle W_{\mathrm{d}}
corresponding to Definition
2.1
.
As in Section
4.A
, as a consequence of Proposition
2.4
and the isotropy of
\displaystyle W
, it is enough to calculate
\displaystyle W_{\mathrm{d}}({\mathbfit A},{\mathbfit e}_{3})
for triangular matrices
\displaystyle{\mathbfit A}
with positive diagonal elements.
We have

\displaystyle\displaystyle W

\displaystyle\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}A_{11}&A_{12}&A_{13}\\
0&A_{22}&A_{23}\\
0&0&A_{33}\end{pmatrix}=\frac{\mu_{1}}{2}\left(A_{11}^{2}+A_{22}^{2}+A_{33}^{2}+A_{12}^{2}+A_{13}^{2}+A_{23}^{2}-2\log(A_{11}A_{22}A_{33})-3\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+\frac{\mu_{2}}{2}\Big(A_{11}^{2}A_{22}^{2}+A_{11}^{2}A_{23}^{2}+(-A_{22}A_{13}+A_{23}A_{12})^{2}+A_{11}^{2}A_{33}^{2}+A_{33}^{2}A_{12}^{2}+A_{22}^{2}A_{33}^{2}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad-4\log(A_{11}A_{22}A_{33})-3\Big)+\frac{\bar{\lambda}}{2}\left(A_{11}A_{22}A_{33}-1\right)^{2}.

Given
\displaystyle A_{11},A_{22}>0
and
\displaystyle A_{12}\in\mathbb{R}
, the minimum in
\displaystyle A_{33}>0

8
Numerical Implementation

We use the finite element method to compute numerical solutions, implemented in the open source FEniCS library, which is now used widely for problems in mechanics, e.g.,
logg2012automated
;
barchiesi2021computation
; our implementation adapts the code from
natarajan2019phase
.
We use
\displaystyle{\mathbfit y}({\mathbfit x})
and
\displaystyle{\mathbfit d}({\mathbfit x})
as the primary unknown functions, and approximate them using standard piecewise linear elements.
We consider two classes of problems.

First, we compute the energy-minimizing deformation
\displaystyle{\mathbfit y}
, keeping
\displaystyle{\mathbfit d}
fixed at a prescribed configuration that corresponds roughly to a crack; we refer to this type of problem as a “frozen crack”.
For these problems, the energy is minimized under load using the FEniCS Dolfin Adjoint library
mitusch2019dolfin
.

Second, we compute the fully-coupled problem of crack growth, wherein both
\displaystyle{\mathbfit y}
and
\displaystyle{\mathbfit d}
evolve to minimize the energy under a time-dependent external loading.
To compute the minimization at each load step, we find that alternate minimization over
\displaystyle{\mathbfit y}
and
\displaystyle{\mathbfit d}
works well, following
natarajan2019phase
.
Specifically, at each load step, we perform first a minimization over
\displaystyle{\mathbfit y}
with
\displaystyle{\mathbfit d}
fixed, and then minimize over
\displaystyle{\mathbfit d}
with
\displaystyle{\mathbfit y}
fixed, and alternate this until we converge.
That is, given the iterates
\displaystyle{\mathbfit y}^{i}
and
\displaystyle{\mathbfit d}^{i}
, we obtain the next iterate using:

\begin{split}&{\mathbfit y}^{i+1}=\argmin_{{\mathbfit y}}E[{\mathbfit y},{\mathbfit d}^{i}],\\
&{\mathbfit d}^{i+1}=\argmin_{{\mathbfit d}}E[{\mathbfit y}^{i+1},{\mathbfit d}]\end{split}

(50)

and we repeat until
\displaystyle\max\{\lVert{\mathbfit y}^{i+1}-{\mathbfit y}^{i}\rVert,\lVert{\mathbfit d}^{i+1}-{\mathbfit d}^{i}\rVert\}<10^{-3}
.
Once we reach a converged solution, we update the time-dependent load.

An important aspect of crack growth is that the minimization over
\displaystyle{\mathbfit d}
must respect the physics that cracks do not heal even when the load is reversed.
Following standard approaches, e.g.
Bourdin07
, we implement this irreversibility as follows.
We first choose a critical value
\displaystyle d_{c}
, taken to be
\displaystyle 0.95
in this paper.
If the converged vector field
\displaystyle{\mathbfit d}_{0}
in a loading step satisfies
\displaystyle|{\mathbfit d}_{0}|\geq d_{c}
, we impose the pointwise constraint
\displaystyle{\mathbfit d}=\frac{{\mathbfit d}_{0}}{|{\mathbfit d}_{0}|}
for all future times, and minimize over
\displaystyle{\mathbfit d}
that respect this constraint.

In addition to preventing the magnitude of
\displaystyle{\mathbfit d}
from decreasing once it satisfies
\displaystyle|{\mathbfit d}|\geq d_{c}
, the constraint also prevents the crack normal from changing orientation.
This is important in the setting considered in this paper.
Specifically, if we did not prevent the orientation of the crack from changing, we could concieve of a sequence of loading steps that would lead to the unphysical result that a crack normal changes orientation even if the crack – defined by the magnitude of
\displaystyle{\mathbfit d}
– is fixed.

We use
\displaystyle\epsilon=0.015
in a nondimensionalized length scale for the phase-field regularization parameter introduced in (
5
).
Given this value of
\displaystyle\epsilon
, we use a mesh that is sufficiently refined as to resolve the interfaces.

9
Numerical Calculations

We solve the model numerically for three settings:
first, we look at the elastic response of frozen cracks in which
\displaystyle{\mathbfit d}
is held fixed to a given configuration, as a means of testing the efficacy of the model;
second, we look at a configuration with cyclic shear loading that leads to crack closure as well as crack branching;
and, third, we look at fracture in a cavity under cyclic loading that leads to crack closure and the growth of multiple cracks, motivated by recent experiments on similar settings.

All of the settings considered above use the large-deformation neo-Hookean model in 2D (Section
4.A
).
We choose material parameters corresponding roughly to PMMA, which is a common material for experimental research
sane2001interconversion
.
We use
\displaystyle\lambda=$\displaystyle 2.576\text{\,}\mathrm{GPa}$
,
\displaystyle\mu=$\displaystyle 1.104\text{\,}\mathrm{GPa}$
, and
\displaystyle G_{c}=$\displaystyle 285\text{\,}\mathrm{N}\text{\,}{\mathrm{m}}^{-1}$
for the frozen cracks.
For growing cracks, we set
\displaystyle\lambda=0
to avoid Poisson’s ratio effects.
All plots of energy density and stress are normalized by
\displaystyle\mu
.

9.B
Crack Growth under Cyclic Shear Loading with Crack Face Contact

We now consider an example in which cracks change direction, close, and branch due to a cyclic shear loading.
We use a specimen as in Figure
8
(a) that contains an initial crack.
Our boundary conditions are as follows: on the bottom face, we fix the displacement to zero; on the left and right faces, we fix the traction to zero; and on the top face, we fix the vertical displacement to zero and the horizontal displacement to cause shearing.

First, we shear the specimen to the right, and that causes the initial crack to kink, i.e., change direction, and grow towards the bottom-right (Figs.
8
(bc)).
This is consistent with the crack normal being aligned with the direction of maximum tension.
We notice 2 stress concentrations: one is from the crack tip, and the other is at the point that the crack kinks.
The stress concentration at the kink can be understood as, roughly, due to the re-entrant corner.

Next, we reverse the shear on the specimen to the left, and that causes a branch crack to nucleate at the kink – driven by the high stresses there – and grow towards the right (Figs.
8
(de)).
The direction of maximum tension would suggest that the crack grow towards the top-right, but we impose constraints – requiring that both components of
\displaystyle{\mathbfit d}
are non-negative – to drive the crack to grow horizontally after branching.
We do this to set up a situation without reentrant-corner-like geometries, enabling us to better understand the stresses at the branch point.

We highlight that the crack that grew under the shearing to the right now closes and the crack faces contact.
From Figure
8
(e), we see that the elastic energy shows only a very small signature of the presence of the closed crack, suggesting that the model works well in capturing the response of closed cracks.

We also highlight that are higher stresses near the branch point, which would not occur in an idealized Y-configuration crack pattern with a straight upper crack branch.
These stresses occur because
\displaystyle{\mathbfit d}
is a smooth field and it rotates as it transitions between crack branches.
That is, the second argument of the effective crack energy density
\displaystyle W_{\mathrm{d}}(\nabla{\mathbfit y},{\mathbfit n})
takes a range of values, and hence the response deviates from the idealized sharp crack response.

We consider the growth of fracture patterns in a cavity that is subject to a remote load.

\displaystyle{\mathbfit F}^{0}

Figure

Figures

We find that 4 of the 8 cracks grow while the others do not, and the elastic energy shows the expected regularized stress concentration at the crack tips.

10
Discussion

In this paper, we have presented an effective energy for phase-field cracks that provides the correct crack response when the crack is subject to complex loadings that cause contact across the crack faces.
Our approach is valid in the setting of finite deformations, which enables application to soft materials in complex configurations (e.g., to complement theoretical studies such as
mo2022finite
;
geubelle1994finite
;
knowles1983large
), as well as to stiff materials where strains are small but fractured pieces can have large rotations or where compression driving crack closure is significant (e.g.,
huang2022cohesive
).

While the numerical results presented above are promising, there remain several important directions for future research:

•

Quasiconvexity is a central property for well-posed problems in finite elasticity, e.g.,
Braides98
;
antman2005problems
;
DaFrTo05
, and it remains to check that
\displaystyle W_{\mathrm{d}}
is quasiconvex in the
\displaystyle{\mathbfit F}
variable.
While lack of convexity typically leads to instabilities or microstructure, our numerical computations have not shown that behavior.
In this context, we notice that an alternative definition of
\displaystyle W_{\mathrm{d}}
could have been

W_{\mathrm{d}}({\mathbfit F},{\mathbfit n})=\min_{{\mathbfit y}={\mathbfit F}{\mathbfit x}\text{ on }\partial Q_{{\mathbfit n}}}\int_{Q_{{\mathbfit n}}}W(\nabla{\mathbfit y})\ \mathrm{d}V_{{\mathbfit x}},

where:
\displaystyle Q_{{\mathbfit n}}
is the cube of volume one centered at the origin with edges parallel to the orthonormal basis
\displaystyle\{{\mathbfit t}_{1},{\mathbfit t}_{2},{\mathbfit n}\}
;
\displaystyle{\mathbfit y}
is Sobolev in the two half-cubes
\displaystyle Q_{{\mathbfit n}}^{\pm}=\{{\mathbfit x}\in Q_{{\mathbfit n}}:\pm{\mathbfit x}\cdot{\mathbfit n}>0\}
, but can have a jump in the interface
\displaystyle\{{\mathbfit x}\in Q_{{\mathbfit n}}:{\mathbfit x}\cdot{\mathbfit n}=0\}
;
\displaystyle{\mathbfit y}
does not interpenetrate.
This definition renders a quasiconvex
\displaystyle W_{\mathrm{d}}
and, in addition, simulates the effective response of two blocks under the affine deformation
\displaystyle{\mathbfit F}
applied on the boundary (Fig.
10
).

Figure 10:
Obtaining the effective crack energy through the homogenized effective response of two blocks.

However, explicitly carrying out the minimization is challenging.

•

The limiting sharp-interface model that appears as
\displaystyle\epsilon\to 0
, and the corresponding consequences on the crack face tractions, needs to be clarified.

•

The numerical studies on branched cracks show that the crack normal is not well defined at the branch point, which leads to undesired responses at the branch point when the crack is subject to complex loads. While it is not immediately clear to us as to how to eliminate this, possibly it is useful to borrow from damage theory in using a tensorial parameter
lemaitre1994mechanics
that can potentially represent a richer kinematics such as multiple normals at a spatial location.

•

We have considered the setting of idealized frictionless crack faces. A natural extension is to consider friction, which is relevant to a large class of materials. While relaxation of the energy is not applicable to this setting, the QR decomposition provides an approach to transparently separate the relative slip of the crack faces from other deformation modes. Therefore, we expect that the kinematics introduced in this paper will be useful in considering more realistic boundary conditions.

Appendix A
Shortcomings of the Energy Splitting Method

The simplest approach to model the energy density of the damaged volume is to set the damaged energy to
\displaystyle 0
.
However, this leads to unphysical behavior such as interpenetration of the crack faces and an incorrect mechanical response of a body with a crack.
The papers
miehe-ijnme
and
amor-jmps
proposed different ways of decomposing the energy, and associating only certain terms of the energy to the damaged region.
While the energy splitting method vastly improved the difficulties caused by simply using zero energy for the crack, it does not consider the crack orientation in the energetic decomposition.
Consequently, energy splitting leads to unphysical response in some settings; further, it is unclear how to extend it beyond the setting of linear isotropic elasticity.
We discuss below some simple examples where the splitting methods provide unexpected results.
We emphasize that the incorrect stress field at crack tips can affect the crack growth behavior significantly, even if the stresses are largely correct away from the crack tip.

In all examples, we consider a large specimen with a given far-field stress or strain, and a finite crack oriented with normal
\displaystyle{\mathbfit e}_{2}
.
We use
\displaystyle\mathbfit{\sigma}_{split}
to denote the response of the cracked material predicted by the energy splitting model.

We define the intact energy and the corresponding stress by

W_{intact}(\mathbfit{\varepsilon})=\frac{1}{2}\lambda(\tr\mathbfit{\varepsilon})^{2}+\mu|\mathbfit{\varepsilon}|^{2}\hskip 11.49994pt\Rightarrow\hskip 11.49994pt\mathbfit{\sigma}_{intact}=\lambda\left(\trace{\mathbfit{\varepsilon}}\right){\mathbfit I}+2\mu\mathbfit{\varepsilon}.

(51)

A value
\displaystyle\mu>0
is required for convexity of
\displaystyle W_{intact}
.
For simplicity we consider
\displaystyle\lambda>0
, and consequently the Poisson ratio
\displaystyle\nu
is positive.

A.1
Splitting Based on the Principal Strain

Following
miehe-ijnme
, the compressive (
\displaystyle\psi_{0}^{-}
) and tensile (
\displaystyle\psi_{0}^{+}
) energies are defined by

\psi_{0}^{\pm}:=\frac{1}{2}\lambda\langle{\varepsilon}_{1}+{\varepsilon}_{2}+{\varepsilon}_{3}\rangle_{\pm}^{2}+\mu\langle{\varepsilon}_{1}\rangle_{\pm}^{2}+\langle{\varepsilon}_{2}\rangle_{\pm}^{2}+\langle{\varepsilon}_{3}\rangle_{\pm}^{2},

where
\displaystyle{\varepsilon}_{1},{\varepsilon}_{2},{\varepsilon}_{3}
denote the principal strains, and the corresponding principal directions are
\displaystyle{\mathbfit n}_{1},{\mathbfit n}_{2},{\mathbfit n}_{3}
.
Also,
\displaystyle\langle x\rangle_{+}=\max\{0,x\}
and
\displaystyle\langle x\rangle_{-}=\min\{0,x\}
.
The effective energy of the crack is assumed to consist of only the compressive part
\displaystyle\psi_{0}^{-}
.
The corresponding stress is

\mathbfit{\sigma}_{split}=\sum_{a=1}^{3}\left(\lambda\langle{\varepsilon}_{1}+{\varepsilon}_{2}+{\varepsilon}_{3}\rangle_{-}+2\mu\langle{\varepsilon}_{a}\rangle_{-}\right){\mathbfit n}_{a}\otimes{\mathbfit n}_{a}.

(52)

We notice from this expression that
\displaystyle\mathbfit{\sigma}_{split}
is always non-tensile in every direction.

Uniaxial tension parallel to the crack.

Consider the specimen subject to a far-field stress
\displaystyle\sigma_{0}{\mathbfit e}_{1}\otimes{\mathbfit e}_{1}
with
\displaystyle\sigma_{0}>0
, giving a far-field strain of:

\mathbfit{\varepsilon}=\frac{\sigma_{0}}{\mu(3\lambda+2\mu)}\begin{pmatrix}\lambda+\mu&0&0\\
0&-\lambda/2&0\\
0&0&-\lambda/2\end{pmatrix}.

(53)

The corresponding stress response in the crack in this model is
\displaystyle\mathbfit{\sigma}_{split}=-\frac{\sigma_{0}}{3\lambda+2\mu}\left({\mathbfit e}_{2}\otimes{\mathbfit e}_{2}+{\mathbfit e}_{3}\otimes{\mathbfit e}_{3}\right)
.

We emphasize two aspects of this result.
First, there is an unexpected (since
\displaystyle\nu>0
) compressive stress along the
\displaystyle{\mathbfit e}_{2}
direction.
Second, the stress along the
\displaystyle{\mathbfit e}_{1}
direction is zero, while the expectation is that for far-field stress parallel to crack, the material should behave as an intact material and sustain the far-field stress as it is.
The incorrect stress field can potentially cause spurious crack growth: the effective zero stiffness for tension parallel to cracks can lead to spurious stress concentration around the tip as the intact material ahead of the tip can sustain far-field stress in
\displaystyle{\mathbfit e}_{1}
direction but the cracked phase cannot.
Further, this can have a significant spurious impact on the T-stress which is an important parameter for ductile crack growth
ravindran2021fracture
.

Shear traction across the crack.

Consider the specimen subject to a far-field strain given by

\mathbfit{\varepsilon}=\frac{\tau}{2\mu}\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\\
1&0&0\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix}

with
\displaystyle\tau>0
.
The corresponding stress response in the crack in this model is

\mathbfit{\sigma}_{split}=\frac{\tau}{2}\begin{pmatrix}-1&1&0\\
1&-1&0\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix}

(54)

which predicts a shear traction across the crack faces, which violates the classical crack face traction conditions.

A.2
The Hydrostatic-Deviatoric Split

amor-jmps
proposes a splitting that allows the crack to resist compressive hydrostatic stress, but not tensile hydrostatic and deviatoric stresses.
One starts by writing the isotropic linear elastic energy as

W_{intact}(\mathbfit{\varepsilon})=\frac{1}{2}\kappa\langle\trace\mathbfit{\varepsilon}\rangle_{-}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\kappa\langle\trace\mathbfit{\varepsilon}\rangle_{+}^{2}+\mu|\mathbfit{\varepsilon}_{D}|^{2}

where
\displaystyle\kappa=\lambda+2\mu/3>0
is the bulk modulus and
\displaystyle\mathbfit{\varepsilon}_{D}:=\mathbfit{\varepsilon}-\frac{1}{3}\left(\trace{\mathbfit{\varepsilon}}\right){\mathbfit I}
is the deviatoric component of the strain.
The effective energy of the crack is assumed to consist only of the term
\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\kappa\langle\trace\mathbfit{\varepsilon}\rangle_{-}^{2}
, giving the stress reponse

\mathbfit{\sigma}_{split}=\left(\kappa\langle\trace\mathbfit{\varepsilon}\rangle_{-}\right){\mathbfit I}.

We notice from this expression that the crack response is always non-tensile hydrostatic, regardless of the applied loading.

Uniaxial tension parallel to the crack.

Consider the specimen subject to a far-field stress
\displaystyle\sigma_{0}{\mathbfit e}_{1}\otimes{\mathbfit e}_{1}
with
\displaystyle\sigma_{0}>0
, and the corresponding far-field strain is in (
53
).

In this model, we find
\displaystyle\mathbfit{\sigma}_{split}={\bf 0}
.
As described in Section
A.1
, the expectation is that material sustains the far-field tensile stress parallel to crack faces as it is.
Further, the result that
\displaystyle\mathbfit{\sigma}_{split}=\bf 0
has similar implications: spurious stress concentrations at the crack tip that affects the crack driving force and the T-stress.

Uniaxial compression normal to the crack.

Consider the specimen subject to a far-field stress
\displaystyle-\sigma_{0}{\mathbfit e}_{2}\otimes{\mathbfit e}_{2}
with
\displaystyle\sigma_{0}>0
.
We expect the crack faces to contact and the response to be identical to the intact material.

The far-field strain corresponding to this stress is

\mathbfit{\varepsilon}=\frac{\sigma_{0}}{\mu(3\lambda+2\mu)}\begin{pmatrix}\lambda/2&0&0\\
0&-(\lambda+\mu)&0\\
0&0&\lambda/2\end{pmatrix},

which should also be response of the crack.
However, this model predicts the crack response
\displaystyle\mathbfit{\sigma}_{split}=-\frac{\sigma_{0}}{3}{\mathbfit I}
.
Similar results can be obtained in the general case of multiaxial compressive loading.
