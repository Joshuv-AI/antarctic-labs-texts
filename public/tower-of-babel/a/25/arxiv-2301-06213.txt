Robust and Sparse M-Estimation of DOA
Christoph F. Mecklenbräuker, Peter Gerstoft, Esa Ollila, Yongsung Park

Abstract
A robust and sparse Direction of Arrival (DOA) estimator is derived for array data that follows a Complex Elliptically Symmetric (CES) distribution with zero-mean and finite second-order moments. The derivation allows to choose the loss function and four loss functions are discussed in detail: the Gauss loss which is the Maximum-Likelihood (ML) loss for the circularly symmetric complex Gaussian distribution, the ML-loss for the complex multivariate t t -distribution (MVT) with ν \nu degrees of freedom, as well as Huber and Tyler loss functions. For Gauss loss, the method reduces to Sparse Bayesian Learning (SBL). The root mean square DOA error of the derived estimators is discussed for Gaussian, MVT, and ϵ \epsilon -contaminated data. The robust SBL estimators perform well for all cases and nearly identical with classical SBL for Gaussian noise.

Here, we solve the DOA estimation problem from multiple array data snapshots in the SBL framework

We assume a CES data model with unknown source variances for the formulation of the likelihood function.
To determine the unknown parameters, we maximize a Type-II likelihood (evidence) for this CES data model

The major contributions of this paper are:
(1) We formulate an SBL algorithm with a general loss function for DOA M-estimation which, given the number of sources, estimates the set of DOAs corresponding to non-zero source power.
The data model parametrization is independent of the number of snapshots.
(2) In addition to conventional Gaussian loss, we investigate the RMSE performance of DOA M-estimation for loss functions associated with data priors featuring potentially strong outliers (Huber, MVT and Tyler).
This leads to a robust and sparse DOA M-estimator.
(3) The robust and sparse DOA M-estimator is shown to be insensitive to heavy tails, outliers, and unknown correlations among the sources.

II
Complex elliptically symmetric data model

Narrowband waves are observed on
N
sensors for
L
snapshots
\bm{y}_{\ell}
and the array data is

\bm{Y}=[\bm{y}_{1}\ldots\bm{y}_{L}]\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times L}
.
We model the snapshots
\bm{y}_{\ell}
by a scale mixture of Gaussian distributions, which have a stochastic decomposition of the form

\displaystyle\bm{y}_{\ell}=\sqrt{\tau_{\ell}}\,\bm{v}_{\ell},\text{ with }\bm{v}_{\ell}=\bm{A}\bm{x}_{\ell}+\bm{n}_{\ell},\quad(\ell=1\ldots L)

(1)

where
\tau_{\ell}>0
is a random variable independent of
\bm{v}_{\ell}
.
The random variables
\tau_{\ell}
enable to accommodate various array data distributions in the model to be discussed further below.

The data model (
1
) has been used for modelling heavy-tailed non-Gaussian data, most notably clutter in radar
[
11
,
12
,
13
,
14
,
15
,
16
,
17
]
.
We use CES assumptions differently from

[
11
,
12
,
13
,
14
,
15
,
16
,
17
]
where the model has been used to model noise, clutter, or interference.
We assume that the snapshots follow a CES distribution.
The motivation for the data model (
1
) is to formulate a model which includes the additive white Gaussian noise (AWGN) model as a special case and is general enough to include cases with heavy-tailed data and data with outliers.

A possibility would be to estimate the
\tau_{\ell}
for each snapshot.
However, we formulate a robust statistical method that is insensitive to outliers and small departures from the model (
1
).

The unknown zero-mean complex source amplitudes are the elements of
\bm{X}=[\bm{x}_{1}\ldots\bm{x}_{L}]\in\mathbb{C}^{M\times L}
where

M
is the considered number of hypothetical DOAs on the given grid
\{\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{M}\}
. The source amplitudes are independent across sources and snapshots, i.e.,
x_{ml}
and
x_{m^{\prime}l^{\prime}}
are independent for
(m,l)\neq(m^{\prime},l^{\prime})
.
If
K
sources are present in the
\ell
th array data snapshot, the
\ell
th column of
\bm{X}
is
K
-sparse and we assume that the sparsity pattern is the same for all snapshots.
The sparsity pattern is modeled by the active set

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}

\displaystyle=\left\{m\in\{1,\ldots,M\}\,|\,x_{m\ell}\neq 0\right\}=\{m_{1},\ldots,m_{K}\},

(2)

and
x_{m\ell}=0
for all
m\not\in\mathcal{M}
.
The noise
\bm{N}=[\bm{n}_{1}\ldots\bm{n}_{L}]\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times L}
is assumed independent identically distributed (iid) across sensors and snapshots, zero-mean, with finite variance
\sigma^{2}
for all
n,\ell
.

The
M
columns of the dictionary
\bm{A}=[\bm{a}_{1}\ldots\bm{a}_{M}]\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times M}
are the replica vectors for all hypothetical DOAs. For a uniform linear array (ULA), the dictionary matrix elements are
A_{nm}=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j}(n-1)\frac{2\pi d}{\lambda}\sin\theta_{m}}
(
d
is the element spacing and
\lambda
the wavelength).
The
K
“active” replica vectors are aggregated in

\displaystyle\bm{A}_{\mathcal{M}}=[\bm{a}_{m_{1}}\ldots\bm{a}_{m_{K}}]\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times K},

(3)

with its
k
th column vector
\bm{a}_{m_{k}}
, where
m_{k}\in\mathcal{M}
.

The source and noise amplitudes are jointly Gaussian and independent of each other, i.e.
\bm{x}_{\ell}\sim\mathbb{C}\mathcal{N}_{M}(\bm{0},\bm{\Gamma})
and
\bm{n}_{\ell}\sim\mathbb{C}\mathcal{N}_{N}(\bm{0},\sigma^{2}\bm{I}_{N})
. It follows from (
1
), that
\bm{v}_{\ell}\sim\mathbb{C}\mathcal{N}_{N}(\bm{0},\bm{\Sigma})
with

\displaystyle\bm{\Sigma}

\displaystyle=\bm{A}\bm{\Gamma}\bm{A}^{\sf H}+\sigma^{2}\bm{I}_{N},

(4)

\displaystyle\bm{\Gamma}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{cov}(\bm{x}_{\ell})=\mathop{\mathrm{diag}}(\bm{\gamma})

(5)

where
\bm{\gamma}=[\gamma_{1}\ldots\gamma_{M}]^{\sf T}
is the
K
-sparse vector of unknown source powers.

The matrix
\bm{\Sigma}
can be interpreted as the covariance matrix
\mathrm{cov}(\bm{v}_{\ell})
of the Gaussian component
\bm{v}_{\ell}
, but this is not observable in this model and the sensor array only observes the scale mixture
\bm{y}_{\ell}
. The matrix
\bm{\Sigma}
is called the scatter matrix in the following.

Since
\bm{y}_{\ell}|\tau_{\ell}\sim\mathbb{C}\mathcal{N}_{N}(\bm{0},\tau_{\ell}\bm{\Sigma})
, the density of
\bm{y}_{\ell}
is

\displaystyle p_{\bm{y}}(\bm{y}_{\ell})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!\!p_{\bm{y},\tau}(\bm{y}_{\ell},\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau=\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!\!p_{\bm{y}|\tau}(\bm{y}_{\ell}|\tau)p_{\tau}(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau

(6)

\displaystyle=(\det\bm{\Sigma})^{-1}g(\bm{y}_{\ell}^{\sf H}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{y}_{\ell})

(7)

where the so-called
density generator

g(\cdot)

[
10
]
,
[
8
, Eq.(4.15)]
is evaluated by

g(t)=\pi^{-N}\int_{0}^{\infty}\tau^{-N}e^{-t/\tau}p_{\tau}(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau.

(8)

The form of (
7
) shows that the distribution of
\bm{y}_{\ell}
is CES with mean zero
[
44
,
45
,
46
]
.
The CES distribution generalizes the complex Gaussian by allowing higher or lower tail probabilities than the complex Gaussian while keeping the elliptical contours of equal density
[
10
]
.

If the random scaling
\sqrt{\tau_{\ell}}=1
in (
1
) for all
\ell
then the commonly assumed Gaussian data model is recovered,

\bm{y}_{\ell}=\bm{A}\bm{x}_{\ell}+\bm{n}_{\ell}~.

(9)

This model results in Gaussian data,
\bm{y}_{\ell}\sim\mathbb{C}\mathcal{N}_{N}(\bm{0},\bm{\Sigma})
.
Model (
9
) was assumed in
[
9
]
while here we assume the more general model (
1
).

In array processing applications, the complex Multi-Variate
t
-distribution (MVT distribution)
[
47
,
48
]
can be used as an alternative to the Gaussian distribution
in the presence of outliers because it has heavier tails than the Gaussian distribution.
The MVT-distribution is a suitable choice for such data and provides a parametric approach to robust statistics
[
8
,
43
]
.
The complex MVT distribution is a special case of the CES distribution, for details see Appendix
-A
.

For numerical performance evaluations of the derived M-estimator of DOA, three data models are used in Sec.
IV
: Gaussian, MVT, and
\epsilon
-contaminated. The Gaussian and MVT models are CES, whereas the
\epsilon
-contaminated model is not.

III
M-estimation based on CES distribution

III-A

Covariance matrix objective function

We follow a general approach based on loss functions and assume that the array data
\bm{Y}
has a CES distribution with zero mean
\bm{0}
and positive definite Hermitian
N\times N
covariance matrix parameter
\bm{\Sigma}

[
10
,
49
]
. Thus

\displaystyle p(\bm{Y}|\bm{0},\bm{\Sigma})

\displaystyle=\prod_{\ell=1}^{L}\det(\bm{\Sigma}^{-1})g(\bm{y}_{\ell}^{\sf H}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{y}_{\ell}).

(10)

An M-estimator of the covariance matrix
\bm{\Sigma}
is defined as a positive definite Hermitian
N\times N
matrix that minimizes the objective function
[
8
, (4.20)]
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\bm{\Sigma})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Lb}\ \sum\limits_{\ell=1}^{L}\rho(\bm{y}_{\ell}^{\sf H}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{y}_{\ell})-\log\det(\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}),

(11)

where
\bm{y}_{\ell}
is the
\ell
th array snapshot and
\rho:\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
, is called the loss function. The loss function is any continuous, non-decreasing function which satisfies that
\rho(e^{x})
is convex in
-\infty<x<\infty
, cf.
[
8
, Sec. 4.3]
.
A specific choice of loss function
\rho
renders (
11
) equal to the negative log-likelihood of
\bm{\Sigma}
when the array data are CES distributed with density generator
g(t)=\mathrm{e}^{-\rho(t)}

[
50
]
. If the loss function is chosen, e.g., as
\rho(t)=t
then (
11
) becomes the negative log-likelihood function for
\bm{\Sigma}
in the Gaussian model (
9
).

The term
b
is a fitting coefficient, called consistency factor, which
makes the minimizer of (
11
) consistent to
\bm{\Sigma}
when the array data are Gaussian
[
8
, Sec. 4.3]
. Thus,
\bm{y}\sim\mathbb{C}\mathcal{N}_{N}(\bm{0},\bm{I})
,

\displaystyle b

\displaystyle=\mathsf{E}[\psi(\|\bm{y}\|^{2})]/N,\quad

(12)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\int_{0}^{\infty}\psi(t/2)f_{\chi^{2}_{2N}}(t)\mathrm{d}t

(13)

where
\psi(t)=t\,\mathrm{d}\rho(t)/\mathrm{d}t
and
f_{\chi^{2}_{2N}}(t)
denotes the pdf of chi-squared distribution with
2N
degrees of freedom.
To arrive from (
12
) to (
13
) we used that
\|\bm{y}\|^{2}\sim(1/2)\chi^{2}_{2N}
.

Minimizing (
11
) with
b
according to (
13
) results in a consistent M-estimator of the covariance matrix
\bm{\Sigma}
when the objective function is derived under a given non-Gaussian array data assumption (as in Sec.
III-B
) but is in fact Gaussian (
\bm{y}_{\ell}\sim\mathbb{C}\mathcal{N}_{N}(\bm{0},\bm{\Sigma}))
.
A derivation of the consistency factor
b
for the loss functions used in this paper is given in Appendix
-B
.

III-C

Source Power Estimation

Similarly to Ref.
[
9
, Sec. III.D]
, we regard
(
11
) as a function of
\bm{\gamma}
and
\sigma^{2}
and compute the first order derivative

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\gamma_{m}}

\displaystyle=-\bm{a}_{m}^{\sf H}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{a}_{m}+\frac{1}{Lb}\sum\limits_{\ell=1}^{L}\|\bm{a}_{m}^{\sf H}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{y}_{\ell}\|_{2}^{2}u(\bm{y}_{\ell}^{\sf H}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{y}_{\ell};\cdot).

(24)

Equation (
24
) is identical to Ref.
[
9
, Eq.(21)]
except for the weight function
u(\bm{y}_{\ell}^{\sf H}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{y}_{\ell};\cdot)
. For the Gaussian array data model, the weight function is the constant function
u_{\mathrm{Gauss}}(t)\equiv 1
.

Setting (
24
) to zero gives

\displaystyle\bm{a}_{m}^{\sf H}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{a}_{m}

\displaystyle=\bm{a}_{m}^{\sf H}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{R}_{\bm{Y}}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{a}_{m},\;\forall m\in\{1,\ldots,M\},

(25)

where
\bm{R}_{\bm{Y}}
is the weighted SCM

\displaystyle\bm{R}_{\bm{Y}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Lb}\sum\limits_{\ell=1}^{L}u(\bm{y}_{\ell}^{\sf H}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{y}_{\ell};\cdot)\bm{y}_{\ell}\bm{y}_{\ell}^{\sf H}=\frac{1}{L}\bm{Y}\bm{D}\bm{Y}^{\sf H}

(26)

with
\bm{D}=\mathrm{diag}(u_{1},\ldots,u_{L})/b
and

.
Note that
\bm{R}_{\bm{Y}}
can be understood as an adaptively weighted SCM
[
8
, Sec. 4.3]
.

\bm{R}_{\bm{Y}}
is Fisher consistent for the covariance matrix when
\bm{y}_{\ell}
are Gaussian distributed,
i.e. ,
\mathsf{E}[\bm{R}_{\bm{Y}}]=\bm{\Sigma}
thanks to the consistency factor
b

[
8
, Sec. 4.4.1]
.
Although (
25
) is rather concise, it hides the parameters
\bm{\gamma},\sigma^{2}
in
\bm{\Sigma}
and
\bm{R}_{\bm{Y}}
and a closed-form solution for
\bm{\gamma},\sigma^{2}
is not known to the authors. Therefore, we formulate a fixed-point equation for each
\gamma_{m}
given
\sigma^{2}
and
\gamma_{p}
,
p\neq m\in\{1,\ldots,M\}
for solving (
25
) by iterations. Let
\gamma_{m}^{\mathrm{old}}
be a previous approximation for
\gamma_{m}
then (similarly to EM-based approaches
[
34
, Eqs. (18)–(19)]
and
[
53
]
) the gradient-based update is computed with stepsize

[
54

\displaystyle\gamma_{m}^{\mathrm{new}}

\displaystyle=(1-\mu)\gamma_{m}^{\mathrm{old}}+\mu\gamma_{m}^{\mathrm{old}}G_{m}(\bm{\gamma}^{\mathrm{old}}),\quad\forall m,

(27)

where
0<\mu\leq 1
and

\displaystyle G_{m}(\bm{\gamma})

\displaystyle=\frac{\bm{a}_{m}^{\sf H}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{R}_{\bm{Y}}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{a}_{m}}{\bm{a}_{m}^{\sf H}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{a}_{m}},\quad\forall m.

(28)

\displaystyle=\frac{\frac{1}{L}\sum\limits_{\ell=1}^{L}\left|\bm{a}_{m}^{\sf H}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{y}_{\ell}\sqrt{u_{\ell}/b}\right|^{2}}{\bm{a}_{m}^{\sf H}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{a}_{m}}.

(29)

The definition of
G_{m}(\bm{\gamma})
in (

) ensures that (

) is fulfilled at the fixed-point of (

).
In (
28
), both

III-D

Noise Variance Estimation

The original SBL algorithm exploits Jaffer’s necessary condition
[
40

which leads to the noise subspace based estimate
[
37
, Eq. (15)]
,
[
9

\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{S}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\left[{(\bm{I}_{N}-\bm{A}_{\mathcal{M}}\bm{A}^{+}_{\mathcal{M}})\bm{S}_{\bm{Y}}}\right]}{N-K},

(30)

where
(\cdot)^{+}
denotes the Moore-Penrose pseudo inverse.
This noise variance estimate works well with DOA estimation
[
9

53

55

without outliers in the array data.
The derivation of the robust noise variance estimate starts from the following robust formulation of Jaffer’s condition, namely

\displaystyle\bm{A}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\sf H}(\bm{R}_{\bm{Y}}-\bm{\Sigma})\bm{A}_{\mathcal{M}}=\bm{0}.

(31)

This results in the robust estimate

\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{R}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\left[{(\bm{I}_{N}-\bm{A}_{\mathcal{M}}\bm{A}^{+}_{\mathcal{M}})\bm{R}_{\bm{Y}}}\right]}{N-K},

(32)

for the full derivation see
[
43
]
. For Gauss loss function,
\bm{R}_{\bm{Y}}=\bm{S}_{\bm{Y}}
, and the expressions (
30

32

(33)

[

26

15

\bm{\Sigma}

\sigma^{2}_{\text{ceil}}={\rm tr}[\bm{S}_{\bm{Y}}]/N

As discussed in Appendix

III-E

Algorithm

The proposed DOA M-estimation algorithm using SBL is displayed in Table
II
with the following remarks:

III-E
1
DOA grid pruning

To reduce numerical complexity in the iterations, we introduce the pruned DOA grid
\mathcal{P}
by not wasting computational resources on those DOAs which are associated with source power estimates below a chosen threshold value
\gamma_{\text{floor}}
, i.e. , we introduce a thresholding operation on the
\bm{\gamma}^{\text{new}}
vector.
The pruned DOA grid is formally defined as an index set,

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}=\{p\in\{1,\ldots,M\}\;|\;\bm{\gamma}^{\text{new}}_{p}\geq\gamma_{\text{floor}}\}=\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{P}\}.

(34)

where
\gamma_{\text{floor}}=\gamma_{\text{range}}\max{\bm{\gamma}^{\text{new}}}
and we have chosen
\gamma_{\text{range}}=10^{-3}
.

III-E
2
Initialization

In our algorithm we need to give initial values of source signal powers
\bm{\gamma}
and the noise variance
\sigma^{2}
.
The initial estimates are computed via following steps:

a)

Compute
\bm{S}_{\bm{Y}}
and CBF output powers

\displaystyle\gamma_{m}^{\text{init}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\bm{a}^{\sf H}_{m}\mathbf{S}_{Y}\bm{a}_{m}}{\|\bm{a}_{m}\|^{4}},\quad\forall m=1,\ldots,M.

(35)

b)

Compute the initial active set by identifying
K
largest peaks in the CBF output powers,

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}

\displaystyle=\{m\in\mathbb{N}\;|\;K\;\text{largest peaks in }\bm{\gamma}^{\text{init}}\}

(36)

c)

Compute the initial noise variance

\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}^{2}

\displaystyle=\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{S}=\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\left[{(\bm{I}_{N}-\bm{A}_{\mathcal{M}}\bm{A}^{+}_{\mathcal{M}})\bm{S}_{\bm{Y}}}\right]}{N-K}

(37)

d)

Compute initial estimates of source powers:

\displaystyle\gamma_{m}^{\text{new}}

\displaystyle=\max(\delta,(\gamma_{m}^{\text{init}}-\hat{\sigma}^{2})),\quad\mbox{for $m=1,\ldots,M$}.

(38)

where
\delta>0
is a small number, guaranteeing that all initial
\gamma_{m}^{\text{new}}
are positive.

III-E
3
Convergence Criterion

The DOA Estimates returned by the iterative algorithm in Table
II
are obtained from the active set
\mathcal{M}
. Therefore, the active set is monitored for changes in its elements to determine whether the algorithm has converged. If
\mathcal{M}
has not changed during the last
z\in\mathbb{N}
iterations, then the repeat-until loop (lines 14–29 in Table
II
) terminates. Here
z
is a tuning parameter which allows to trade off computation time against DOA estimate accuracy. To ensure that the iterations always terminate, the maximum iteration count is defined as
j_{\mathrm{max}}
with

z<j_{\mathrm{max}}
.

Coherent sources

Coherent sources are often encountered in applications, e.g.,
ocean acoustics and radar array processing.
In contrast to subspace methods SBL performs well for coherent sources
[
61
,
62
,
23
]
.
To demonstrate this, we report results for a two-source scenario with DOAs at
\pm 10^{\circ}
with source correlation coefficient

\rho=\frac{\mathsf{E}[x_{8001,l}x_{10001,l}^{*}]}{\sqrt{\mathsf{E}[|x_{8001,l}|^{2}]\,\mathsf{E}[|x_{10001,l}|^{2}]}}.

(41)

The data model for coherent sources has a full source correlation matrix
\bm{\Gamma}

\bm{\Gamma}
is numerically costly

]

The Robust SBL algorithm in Table

\bm{\Gamma}

\bm{\Gamma}
model (

In Fig.

In Fig.

For source DOA on the dictionary grid, Fig.
6
(a), the RMSE performance curve resembles the behavior of an ML-estimator at low ASNR up to a certain threshold ASNR (dashed vertical lines) where the RMSE abruptly crosses the CRB and becomes zero.
The threshold ASNR is deduced from the following argument:
Let
\bm{a}_{m}
be the true DOA dictionary vector and
\bm{a}_{m+1}
be the dictionary vector for adjacent DOA on the angular grid. Comparing the corresponding Bartlett powers, we see that DOA errors become likely if the noise variance exceeds
2(|\bm{a}_{m}^{\sf H}\bm{a}_{m}|-|\bm{a}_{m}^{\sf H}\bm{a}_{m+1}|)/N=2-2|\bm{a}_{m}^{\sf H}\bm{a}_{m+1}|/N
.

For source DOA off the dictionary grid, Fig.

(b), the RMSE performance curve resembles the behavior of an ML-estimator at low ASNR up to a threshold ASNR. In the random DOA scenario, however, the RMSE flattens at increasing ASNR.
Since the variance of the uniformly distributed source DOA is
\delta^{2}/12
,
the limiting
\mathrm{RMSE}=\delta/\sqrt{12}
for
\mathrm{ASNR}\to\infty

The limiting RMSE (dashed horizontal lines) depends on the dictionary size
M
through the angular grid resolution
\delta
.
The asymptotic RMSE limits are shown as dashed horizontal lines in Fig.

\mathcal{M}
whose elements represent the estimated source DOAs. The required number of iterations for convergence of
\mathcal{M}

depends on the source scenario, array data model, ASNR, and stepsize
\mu
. We select
\mu=1
.

Figure

Figure

At

Peak number of iterations is near 160 at ASNR levels between 12 and 15 dB.

VI
Conclusion

Robust and sparse DOA M-estimation is derived based on array data following a zero-mean complex elliptically symmetric distribution with finite second-order moments. Our derivations allowed using different loss functions.
The DOA M-estimator is numerically evaluated by iterations
and available as a Matlab function on GitHub
[
2
]
.
A specific choice of loss function determines the RMSE performance and robustness of the DOA estimate.
Four choices for loss function are discussed and investigated in numerical simulations with synthetic array data: the ML-loss function for the circular complex multivariate
t
-distribution with
\nu
degrees of freedom, the loss functions for Huber and Tyler M-estimators. For Gauss loss, the method reduces to Sparse Bayesian Learning.
We discuss the robustness of these DOA M-estimators by evaluating the root mean square error for Gaussian, MVT, and
\epsilon
-contaminated array data. The robust and sparse M-estimators for DOA perform well in simulations for MVT and
\epsilon
-contaminated noise and nearly identical with classical SBL for Gaussian noise.
