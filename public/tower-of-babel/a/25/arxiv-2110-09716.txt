Speed Limits for Macroscopic Transitions
Ryusuke Hamazaki

Abstract
Speed of state transitions in macroscopic systems is a crucial concept for foundations of nonequilibrium statistical mechanics as well as various applications in quantum technology represented by optimal quantum control. While extensive studies have made efforts to obtain rigorous constraints on dynamical processes since Mandelstam and Tamm, speed limits that provide tight bounds for macroscopic transitions have remained elusive. Here, by employing the local conservation law of probability, the fundamental principle in physics, we develop a general framework for deriving qualitatively tighter speed limits for macroscopic systems than many conventional ones. We show for the first time that the speed of the expectation value of an observable defined on an arbitrary graph, which can describe general many-body systems, is bounded by the “gradient” of the observable, in contrast with conventional speed limits depending on the entire range of the observable. This framework enables us to derive novel quantum speed limits for macroscopic unitary dynamics. Unlike previous bounds, the speed limit decreases when the expectation value of the transition Hamiltonian increases; this intuitively describes a new tradeoff relation between time and quantum phase difference. Our bound is dependent on instantaneous quantum states and thus can achieve the equality condition, which is conceptually distinct from the Lieb-Robinson bound. We also find that, beyond expectation values of macroscopic observables, the speed of macroscopic quantum coherence can be bounded from above by our general approach. The newly obtained bounds are verified in transport phenomena in particle systems and nonequilibrium dynamics in many-body spin systems. We also demonstrate that our strategy can be applied for finding new speed limits for macroscopic transitions in stochastic systems, including quantum ones, where the bounds are expressed by the entropy production rate. Our work elucidates novel speed limits on the basis of local conservation law, providing fundamental limits to various types of nonequilibrium quantum macroscopic phenomena.

Summary of the results

I.1.1
General framework for deriving speed limits on macroscopic transitions

In this work, we develop a new, general, and rigorous

to obtain state-dependent speed limits applicable to processes with macroscopic transitions on the basis of the local conservation law of probability, the fundamental principles of physics (see Fig.

We first demonstrate for the first time that

(either classical or quantum) defined on vertices of a graph, which describes arbitrary systems including many-body ones, is bounded like

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}\braket{{A}}}{\mathrm{d}t}\right|\leq

(a term involving
\nabla A
)

\displaystyle\times\text{(a term involving local probability current)},

(3)

which takes place of, e.g., inequality (
2
).
Here, the gradient
\nabla A
mathematically corresponds to the derivative-like operation on a discrete graph (see Appendix
B
).
The appearance of the gradient can dramatically tighten the speed limit for the case

\displaystyle\nabla A\ll\Delta A\text{ or }\|A\|_{\mathrm{op}}.

(4)

For the example in Fig.
2
, while

in inequality (
2
) becomes large before saturating to

, the term involving
\nabla x
in inequality (
I.1.1
) is always

.
We also show that the inequality (
I.1.1
) also provides a reasonable timescale for the macroscopic transition.
Furthermore, going beyond the expectation value, we show that our method can be used for obtaining the speed limits for, e.g., the variance of the observable and entropy of the state.
We also discuss how our results on general graphs to connected to continuous systems. We also point out the new relation of our speed limits and the optimal transport problem, which indicates the connection
between nonequilibrium statistical mechanics and underlying mathematical structures.

Our theory only relies on the local probability conservation and thus are applied to any physically normal systems, as demonstrated for quantum unitary dynamics, nonlinear dynamics, classical stochastic dynamics, and quantum stochastic dynamics in this manuscript.
Furthermore, our results are even applicable to discrete many-body systems, which is concisely formulated with the language of the graph theory.
Note that related speed limits based on the local probability conservation were obtained for, e.g., the continuous classical Fokker-Planck equation
Dechant and Sasa 2018a
;
Ito and Dechant 2020
before; however, our work is fundamentally distinct from previous work by demonstrating that the local probability conservation generally provides useful and insightful speed limits even for quantum systems, discrete systems, and many-body systems, pointing out that it holds for any physical processes.

I.1.2
Speed limits in quantum unitary dynamics as a new tradeoff relation

As a primary application of our general framework, we derive a novel type of speed limits applicable to unitary quantum dynamics.
Unlike conventional quantum speed limits, we find the importance of the expectation value
E_{\mathrm{trans}}
of the transition Hamiltonian of the system (see Eq. (
44
)) for our speed limit.
In particular, instead of inequality (
2
), we show the following inequality

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}\braket{\hat{A}}}{\mathrm{d}t}\right|\leq

\displaystyle\text{\>(a term involving $\nabla{A}$)}\times\sqrt{R^{2}-E_{\mathrm{trans}}^{2}}

(5)

for an observable
\hat{A}
given in Eq. (
40

Here,

is bounded by the probability-distribution-dependent factor (see Eq. (

It is further bounded by the state-dependent constant that is easily known from the Hamiltonian structure, which is also equivalent to the maximum degree of the weighted graph (see Eq. (
48
)).
In addition, beyond conventional expectation values, we prove similar speed limits for dynamics of macroscopic coherence, a key quantity for quantum information, which previous literature seldom discussed.
We also discuss the relation with the continuous-space nonlinear Schrödinger equation and elucidate that the inequality (
5
) for a general discrete system reduces to a previously unknown speed limit (see inequality (
III.5
)) based on the kinetic energy in the continuous system.
We verify our speed limits for the transport in single-particle and interacting many-particle systems and the nonequilibrium process of
an interacting many-body spin system.

Interestingly, inequality (

can decrease the speed provided that

Thus, our inequality (

Let us briefly discuss the conceptual distinctions between previous works.
Our bound is in general state-dependent through
E_{\mathrm{trans}}
and can be tighter than the bound indicated by the Lieb-Robinson velocity
Lieb and Robinson 1972

Quite importantly, in contrast with the Lieb-Robinson bound,
our bound can satisfy the equality condition in some situations, as discussed in Sec.
IV

In addition, our bound is different from
Refs.
Bukov et al. 2019
;
Lam et al. 2021

Our theory instead derives fundamental and rigorous laws that govern speed limits of general macroscopic dynamics using the distinct principle of local conservation of probability.

I.1.3
Speed limits in stochastic dynamics by entropy production

To demonstrate the broad applicability of our general approach, we also prove speed limits for macroscopic systems involving Markovian dissipation.
We first derive a speed limit based on the irreversible entropy production; while the entropy production is recently found to play an important role for state transitions
Deffner and Lutz 2010
;
Deffner and Lutz 2011
;
Shiraishi et al. 2016
;
Barato and Seifert 2015
;
Gingrich et al. 2016
;
Horowitz and Gingrich 2020
;
Dechant and Sasa 2018b
;
Dechant and Sasa 2018a
;
Shiraishi et al. 2018
;
Funo et al. 2019
, we show that a related speed limit of macroscopic observables on a general graph is obtained from our framework.
The bound can be qualitatively better for macroscopic systems than that proposed in Ref.
Shiraishi et al. 2018
.
In addition, we obtain a modified speed limit, which is valuable even without the detailed balance condition, using the Hatano-Sasa entropy production
Hatano and Sasa 2001
.
We verify our bounds for the dynamics of the simple exclusion processes.
We also show that a similar speed limit is obtained even for macroscopic quantum open systems described by the Gorini-Kossakowski-Sudarshan-Lindblad equation
Gorini et al. 1976
;
Lindblad 1976
.

II
Macroscopic speed limit constrained by currents

We begin with a general formulation of our speed limit based on the local conservation law of probability for a given graph structure, which represents a general system including a many-body one.
We show that the instantaneous speed of the expectation value of an observable is bounded by the gradient of an observable on the graph and the sum of the magnitude of the local probability current.
We demonstrate that this bound can dramatically improve the speed limit for certain observables compared with the bound based on, e.g., the uncertainty relation in inequality (
2

\displaystyle|\braket{\dot{A}}|\leq\sqrt{\frac{[A^{\mathsf{T}}\nabla_{r}^{2}A]}{2}\cdot\sum_{n\sim_{A}m}\frac{J_{nm}^{2}}{r_{nm}}}

(18)

and

\displaystyle|\braket{\dot{A}}|\leq\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla A\|_{\infty}\cdot\sum_{n\sim_{A}m}|J_{nm}|.

(19)

II.2
Speed limit for other quantities

We can obtain Similar speed limits for other quantities not written as the expectation value of an observable.
For example, the general scalar function of the probability distribution written as
F(p)
has the following speed limit:

\displaystyle|\dot{F}|\leq\sqrt{\frac{[(\partial F)^{\mathsf{T}}\nabla_{r}^{2}(\partial F)]}{2}\cdot\sum_{n\sim_{\partial F}m}\frac{J_{nm}^{2}}{r_{nm}}}

(20)

and

\displaystyle|\dot{F}|\leq\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla(\partial F)\|_{\infty}\cdot\sum_{n\sim_{\partial F}m}|J_{nm}|,

(21)

where

\partial F(p)=\left(\frac{\partial F}{\partial p_{1}},\cdots,\frac{\partial F}{\partial p_{|\mathcal{V}|}}\right)^{\mathsf{T}}.

Taking
F=\sum_{n}a_{n}p_{n}
leads to the inequalities (
18
) and (
19
).

The general result is useful when we consider the speed limit of entropy-like quantity,

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}(p,X)=-\sum_{n}p_{n}\ln\frac{p_{n}}{X_{n}},

(22)

where
X=\{X_{n}\}
is independent of
p
and
t
.
When we take
X=p^{\mathrm{ref}}
, where
p^{\mathrm{ref}}
is some reference probability distribution,
\mathcal{S}(p,X)
reduces to the Kullback-Leibler divergence
-D(p||p^{\mathrm{ref}})
, which quantifies the difference between the two probability distributions
Cover 1999
.
Instead, if we take
X_{n}
as the dimension of the subspace
n
,
\mathcal{S}(p,X)
reduces to the observational entropy
S_{\mathrm{obs}}^{\mathcal{V}}(\hat{\rho})
, which is the promising candidate for entropy in isolated quantum systems
Strasberg and Winter 2021
.
In this case, we find the speed limit, e.g.,

\displaystyle|\dot{\mathcal{S}}|\leq\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\sum_{n\sim m}r_{nm}\left(\ln\frac{p_{n}X_{m}}{p_{m}X_{n}}\right)^{2}}\sqrt{\sum_{n\sim m}\frac{|J_{nm}|^{2}}{r_{nm}}}.

(23)

Moreover, as detailed in the following sections, the speed of more nontrivial quantities, such as macroscopic quantum coherence and variance of observables, is bounded by a similar quantity.
For example, let us consider a variance of
{A}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}[A]=\Delta A^{2}=\braket{A^{2}}-\braket{A}^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{nm}(a_{n}-a_{m})^{2}p_{n}p_{m}.

(24)

Using inequality (
20
), we have

\displaystyle|\dot{\mathbb{V}}[A]|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\sum_{n\sim_{A}m}(a_{n}-a_{m})^{2}(a_{n}+a_{m}-2\braket{A})^{2}r_{nm}}\sqrt{\sum_{n\sim_{A}m}\frac{|J_{nm}|^{2}}{r_{nm}}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\|\nabla A\|_{\infty}}{2}\sqrt{\sum_{n\sim_{A}m}(a_{n}+a_{m}-2\braket{A})^{2}r_{nm}}\sqrt{\sum_{n\sim_{A}m}\frac{|J_{nm}|^{2}}{r_{nm}}},

(25)

meaning that the speed of the variance is also bounded using the gradient of the observable.

II.3
Limit for transition time

Next, we discuss that our approach also enables us to bound the timescales for transition processes.
Let us consider a situation for which the expectation value of an observable
A
changes from
A_{\mathrm{ini}}
at
t=0
to

A_{\mathrm{fin}}
at
t=T
.
Since

|A_{\mathrm{fin}}-A_{\mathrm{ini}}|\leq\int_{0}^{T}dt\left|\braket{\dot{A}(t)}\right|
,
we readily have a lower bound for the transition time

\displaystyle T\geq\frac{|A_{\mathrm{fin}}-A_{\mathrm{ini}}|}{\overline{|\braket{\dot{A}}|}},

(26)

which can be evaluated with inequalities (
18
) and (
19
),
where

\overline{\cdots}=\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}dt\cdots

denotes the temporal average.
We also obtain a better lower limit by noticing

A_{\mathrm{fin}}-A_{\mathrm{ini}}=-\frac{T}{2}\sum_{(n,m)\in\mathcal{E}_{A}}(a_{n}-a_{m})\overline{J_{mn}},

from which we have

\displaystyle T\geq\frac{|A_{\mathrm{fin}}-A_{\mathrm{ini}}|}{\sqrt{\frac{[A^{\mathsf{T}}\nabla_{r}^{2}A]}{2}\cdot\sum_{n\sim_{A}m}\frac{\overline{J_{nm}}^{2}}{r_{nm}}}}

(27)

and

\displaystyle T\geq\frac{2|A_{\mathrm{fin}}-A_{\mathrm{ini}}|}{\|\nabla A\|_{\infty}\cdot\sum_{n\sim_{A}m}|\overline{J_{nm}}|}.

(28)

These inequalities are useful when

|A_{\mathrm{fin}}-A_{\mathrm{ini}}|
is much larger than
[A\nabla_{r}^{2}A]
or
\|\nabla A\|_{\infty}
.
For example, let us again consider a one-dimensional graph
1,\cdots,N\in\mathcal{V}
and
(n,n+1)\in\mathcal{E}\>(1\leq n\leq N-1)
.
For
a_{n}=n
, by choosing
A_{\mathrm{ini}}=1
and
A_{\mathrm{fin}}=N
, we have (from inequality (
28
))

T\geq\frac{2(N-1)}{\sum_{n\sim_{A}m}\left|\overline{J_{mn}}\right|}.

assuming that
\sum_{(n,m)\in\mathcal{E}_{A}}\left|\overline{J_{mn}}\right|=\mathrm{O}(T^{-\gamma})\>(\gamma\geq 0)
, we have a proper macroscopic timescale
T\gtrsim\mathrm{O}(N^{\frac{1}{1-\gamma}})
.
Note that
\gamma=0
and
\gamma=1/2
correspond to the ballistic and diffusive timescale, respectively
3
3

3

Assuming that the current fluctuates randomly in time with zero mean, we obtain
\gamma=1/2
.
.
We note that this macroscopic timescale cannot be attained by the speed limit similar to
inequality (
13
), i.e.,

\displaystyle T\geq\frac{2|A_{\mathrm{fin}}-A_{\mathrm{ini}}|}{D_{A}\cdot\sum_{n\in\mathcal{V}}\overline{|\dot{p}_{n}|}}.

(29)

Indeed, if we consider the above example for this inequality, the right-hand side is
\mathrm{O}(1)
when
\sum_{n\in\mathcal{V}}\overline{|\dot{p}_{n}|}=\mathrm{O}(1)
, which is quite loose.

II.4
Continuous case

We can consider a similar speed limit for continuous systems as the ones obtained for the discrete graph.
To see this, let us consider the continuous system whose space coordinate is denoted by
\mathbf{x}
.
We consider time evolution for the probability distribution
P(\mathbf{x},t)
,
which obeys the continuity equation

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}P(\mathbf{x},t)}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\nabla\cdot\mathbf{J}(\mathbf{x},t).

(30)

We assume that
\mathbf{J}(\mathbf{x})
becomes zero for
|\mathbf{x}|\rightarrow 0
.
Using the integration by parts, the instantaneous speed limit for
\braket{{A}(t)}=\int d\mathbf{x}P(\mathbf{x},t)A(\mathbf{x})
reads

\displaystyle|\braket{\dot{A}}|\leq\sqrt{\int d\mathbf{x}r(\mathbf{x})(\nabla A(\mathbf{x}))^{2}}\sqrt{\int d\mathbf{x}\frac{|\mathbf{J}(\mathbf{x})|^{2}}{r(\mathbf{x})}}

(31)

for
r(\mathbf{x})>0
and

\displaystyle|\braket{\dot{A}}|\leq\max_{\mathbf{x}}|\nabla A(\mathbf{x})|\int d\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{J}(\mathbf{x})|,

(32)

which are the continuous version of inequalities (
10
) and (
11
).
Note that this is generalized to arbitrary function written as
F=F[P(\mathbf{x},t)]
,
where
\nabla A(\mathbf{x})
is replaced by
\nabla\left(\frac{\delta F}{\delta P}\right)
.
We also note that, while Ref.
Dechant and Sasa 2018a
uses
inequality (
31
) for specific
r(\mathbf{x})
in classical Fokker-Planck equation, we consider more general systems, including, e.g., a system obeying the nonlinear Schrödinger equation (see Sec.
III
).

Moreover, when we consider a situation where the expectation value of the observable
A
changes from
A_{\mathrm{ini}}
at
t=0
to

A_{\mathrm{fin}}
at
t=T
,
we have a relation (cf. inequality (
28
))

\displaystyle|A_{\mathrm{fin}}-A_{\mathrm{ini}}|\leq\max_{\mathbf{x}}|\nabla A(\mathbf{x})|\int d\mathbf{x}T|\overline{\mathbf{J}(\mathbf{x})}|.

(33)

Here, we note that
\max|\nabla A(\mathbf{x})|
is the Lipschitz constant for a differentiable function
A(\mathbf{x})
.

We note that inequality (
33
) is closely tied to the order-one Wasserstein distance.
The Wasserstein distance plays a crucial role in characterizing the distance between two probability distributions
P(\mathbf{x})
and
Q(\mathbf{x})

by taking account of the underlying geometric structure, such as the Euclidean distance between
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
(see Appendix
A
for the definition).

As shown in Appendix
A
, in one dimension,
we can show that the order-one Wasserstein distance with respect to the Euclidean distance
|x-y|
becomes

\displaystyle W_{1}(P(0),P(T))=T\int d{x}\left|\overline{{J}}(x)\right|,

(34)

where we have assumed
\overline{{J}}(-\infty)=0
.
Thus, combined with inequality (
33
), we have

\displaystyle|A_{\mathrm{fin}}-A_{\mathrm{ini}}|\leq\max|\partial_{x}A({x})|\cdot W_{1}(P(0),P(T)).

(35)

Thus, in this case, our approach to derive the speed limit is directly connected to the optimal transport problem.

III
Unitary quantum dynamics: theory

In this section, we apply the general formalism obtained in the previous section to unitary quantum systems.
We introduce the decomposition of the Hilbert space for general quantum many-body systems with discrete states and show several speed limits for certain observables.
In particular, by bounding the probability current from above,
we show that the speed limit is bounded using the expectation value
E_{\mathrm{trans}}
of the transition Hamiltonian, which is a standard observable.
Interestingly, the speed limit can be tighter when the magnitude of
E_{\mathrm{trans}}
becomes increased because of the suppression of the quantum phase difference.
This intuitively means the novel tradeoff relation between time and quantum phase difference, instead of the tradeoff relation between time and energy fluctuation by Mandelstamm and Tamm.
We also show that, beyond expectation values, we can bound the speed of the change of entropy and macroscopic quantum coherence.
Furthermore, we discuss the case for continuous systems and elucidate the relation to speed limits for discrete systems.
The detailed derivation of each result is given in Appendices
C
-
F
.

III.1
General setting for discrete quantum systems

Let us consider the von Neumann equation

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\hat{\rho}(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}=-i[\hat{H}(t),\hat{\rho}(t)],

(36)

where we set the Planck constant as the unity in the following.
We assume that the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
is finite dimensional and decompose it as

\mathcal{H}=\bigoplus_{n=1}^{|\mathcal{V}|}\mathcal{H}_{n}

and define
\hat{P}_{n}
as the projection operator onto
\mathcal{H}_{n}
.
From the von Neumann equation, we find a continuity equation for

\displaystyle p_{n}(t)=\mathrm{Tr}[\hat{\rho}(t)\hat{P}_{n}]

(37)

as

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}p_{n}}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\sum_{m(\sim n)}J_{mn}^{q}(t),

(38)

where

\displaystyle J_{mn}^{q}(t)=i\mathrm{Tr}[\hat{H}(t)_{nm}\hat{\rho}(t)_{mn}-\hat{H}(t)_{mn}\hat{\rho}(t)_{nm}].

(39)

Here,
\hat{X}_{nm}=\hat{P}_{n}\hat{X}\hat{P}_{m}
for an operator
\hat{X}

and
(n,m)\in\mathcal{E}
if
n\neq m
and
\hat{H}(t)_{nm}\neq 0
for any
t
.

As discussed in Fig.
3
(a), two cases are particularly important.
One is the case where
n
labels the complete basis
{\left\{\ket{x}\right\}}\>(1\leq x\leq\dim[\mathcal{H}])
of the Hilbert space. In that case,
\hat{P}_{x}=\ket{x}\bra{x}
.
The other interesting case is that
n
labels a position in a one-dimensional graph, i.e.,
\mathcal{E}={\left\{(n,n+1)\>|\>1\leq n\leq N-1\right\}}
.

III.2
Speed limit for expectation values of observables

As a first target, we show the speed limit of the expectation value of an observable given by

\displaystyle\hat{A}=\sum_{n}a_{n}\hat{P}_{n}.

(40)

Following the general derivation in the previous section, we find

\displaystyle|\braket{\dot{\hat{A}}}|\leq\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{gL}}=\sqrt{\frac{[A^{\mathsf{T}}\nabla_{r}^{2}A]}{2}}\sqrt{\sum_{n\sim_{A}m}\frac{|J_{nm}^{q}|^{2}}{r_{nm}}}

(41)

and

\displaystyle|\braket{\dot{\hat{A}}}|\leq\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{Lip}}={\frac{\|\nabla A\|_{\infty}}{2}}{\sum_{n\sim_{A}m}{|J_{nm}^{q}|}},

(42)

where
A=(a_{1},\cdots,a_{|\mathcal{V}|})^{\mathsf{T}}
is regarded as a vector obtained from
\hat{A}
(“gL” and “Lip” represent the graph Laplacian and Lipshitz constant, respectively).

Although the speed limits above contain the sum of the functions of local probability currents, we find that they are further bounded from above using more physically relevant quantities.
Indeed, we obtain a hierarchy of the bounds

\displaystyle|\braket{\dot{\hat{A}}}|\leq\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{gL}}\leq\mathcal{B}_{p}\leq\mathcal{B}_{H},

(43)

where each of the bounds is explained in the following.
In particular, below we show the importance of the expectation value of the transition Hamiltonian defined as

\displaystyle E_{\mathrm{trans}}=\left\langle\sum_{n\sim_{A}m}\hat{H}_{nm}\right\rangle.

(44)

This quantity is just an expectation value of the globally defined observable
\sum_{n\sim_{A}m}\hat{H}_{nm}
, unlike
\sum_{n\sim_{A}m}|J_{nm}|
which involves information of local current.
We also note that, when
\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{E}^{A}
, we have
E_{\mathrm{trans}}=\mathrm{Tr}\left[\hat{\rho}\left(\hat{H}-\sum_{n}\hat{H}_{nn}\right)\right]
.

First,
\mathcal{B}_{p}

is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{p}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2\frac{[A^{\mathsf{T}}\nabla^{2}_{r^{p}}A]}{{R_{p}}}}\sqrt{R_{p}^{2}-{E_{\mathrm{trans}}^{2}}},

(45)

where

\displaystyle r^{p}_{nm}=\|\hat{H}_{nm}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\sqrt{p_{n}p_{m}}

(46)

for
n\sim m
(otherwise
r_{nm}^{p}=0
) and

\displaystyle R_{p}=\mathrm{Tr}[\nabla^{2}_{r^{p}}]=\sum_{n\sim_{A}m}\|\hat{H}_{nm}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\sqrt{p_{n}p_{m}}

(47)

are dependent on probability distribution
p(t)
.

The probability distribution
\{p_{n}\}
in
R_{p}
is in principle measured in experiments of artificial quantum systems, such as the quantum-microscope technique in ultracold atomic systems
Bakr et al. 2009
.
Since
E_{\mathrm{trans}}
is just a standard expectation value of the observable, the bound in inequality (
45
) is accessible in state-of-the-art experiments.

Figure 4:
Schematic illustration of the tradeoff relation between the current and the expectation value of the transition energy. The local current
|J_{nm}^{q}|
and the expectation value of the local transition Hamiltonian
\propto Y_{nm}
cannot be simultaneously large when
2|\mathrm{Tr}[\hat{H}_{nm}\hat{\rho}_{mn}]|
is fixed, since
|J_{nm}^{q}|=2|\mathrm{Tr}[\hat{H}_{nm}\hat{\rho}_{mn}]|\sin\theta_{nm}
and
Y_{nm}=2|\mathrm{Tr}[\hat{H}_{nm}\hat{\rho}_{mn}]|\cos\theta_{nm}
, where
\theta_{nm}
plays the role of difference of quantum phases. Summation over all of the edges
n\sim_{A}m
leads to the tradeoff relation between
\sum_{n\sim_{A}m}|J_{nm}^{q}|
,
E_{\mathrm{trans}}
, and
R_{p}
, as detailed in Appendix
C
.

This bound contains nontrivial information.
First, the speed limit becomes tighter when the absolute value of the expectation value of the transition Hamiltonian is larger, provided that
R_{p}
is almost the same.
It may sound counter-intuitive that large energy concerning the transition Hamiltonian suppresses the speed for transition.
Intuitively, this is attributed to the fact that, while the difference of quantum phases generates the current, such phase difference should be suppressed for large
E_{\mathrm{trans}}
.
To be more precise, the local current
J_{nm}^{q}
and the
local energy
Y_{nm}
concerning the transition Hamiltonian are respectively given by
2|\mathrm{Tr}[\hat{\rho}_{nm}\hat{H}_{mn}]|\sin\theta_{nm}
and
2|\mathrm{Tr}[\hat{\rho}_{nm}\hat{H}_{mn}]|\cos\theta_{nm}
, where
\theta_{nm}
plays the role of difference of quantum phases
4
4

4

When we consider a pure state and take the entire basis
\{\ket{i}\}
of the Hilbert space to describe each subspace,
\theta_{ij}
indeed becomes the standard phase difference,
\theta_{j}-\theta_{i}
. Here, we take
\braket{i|\psi}=|\braket{i|\psi}|e^{i\theta_{i}}
.
Thus, if
\sum_{n\sim_{A}m}|2\mathrm{Tr}[\hat{\rho}_{nm}\hat{H}_{mn}]|
(which can be bounded by
2R_{p}
) is given, the sum of the local current
\sum_{n\sim_{A}m}|J_{nm}^{q}|
and energy of the transition Hamiltonian
2E_{\mathrm{trans}}=\sum_{n\sim_{A}m}Y_{nm}
cannot be large simultaneously (see Fig.
4
and Appendix
C
).
Note that the correspondence between

\sqrt{R_{p}^{2}-E_{\mathrm{trans}}^{2}}
and the quantum phase difference becomes more evident by considering the continuous-space limit (see subsection
III.5
).

The above discussion indicates the novel tradeoff relation between time and quantum phase difference:
since small magnitude of the phase difference (i.e., the large expectation value of transition Hamiltonian) suppresses the speed,
we cannot simultaneously decrease the
time required for transitions and the magnitude of quantum phase difference.
This new tradeoff relation takes the place of the conventional tradeoff relation between time and energy fluctuation in inequalities (
1
) and (
2
).
In addition, as discussed in Sec.
VIII
,
the term
\sqrt{R_{p}^{2}-E_{\mathrm{trans}}^{2}}
in our bounds cannot be replaced with
\Delta H
.

Second, since
R_{p}=\sum_{n\sim_{A}m}\|\hat{H}_{nm}\|_{\mathrm{op}}p_{n}-\sum_{n\sim_{A}m}\|\hat{H}_{nm}\|_{\mathrm{op}}(\sqrt{p_{n}}-\sqrt{p_{m}})^{2}/2
, the bound can be tight if the gradient of the probability distribution (i.e., the difference between
p_{n}
and
p_{m}
) becomes larger.
This is distinct from typical classical systems, where the speed can increase when the gradient is larger.
The distinction again comes from the fact that the probability current for unitary time evolution can arise owing to the gradient of phases instead of probability distributions.

Next, the bound

\mathcal{B}_{H}

is simply given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{H}=\|\nabla A\|_{\infty}\sqrt{C_{H}^{2}-E_{\mathrm{trans}}^{2}},

(48)

where

\displaystyle C_{H}:=\max_{n\in\mathcal{V}}\sum_{m(\sim_{A}n)}\|\hat{H}_{nm}\|_{\mathrm{op}}

(49)

is the maximum degree of the graph with the edge
\mathcal{E}_{A}
and weight
\|\hat{H}_{nm}\|_{\mathrm{op}}
.
Equation (
48
) does not explicitly contain the probability distribution (it appears only through
E_{\mathrm{trans}}
).
Thus, given a Hamiltonian and an observable
\hat{A}
,
the bound is readily obtained if the expectation value of the transition Hamiltonian can be measured.

We stress that the bounds
Eqs. (
45
) and (
48
) universally hold for any isolated quantum systems for an observable given as Eq. (
40
).
As highlighted in Sec.
IV
, these bounds indeed turn out to be useful for macroscopic transitions.

We discuss the conceptual distinction between the Lieb-Robinson bound
Lieb and Robinson 1972
;
Nachtergaele and Sims 2006
and our results.
The Lieb-Robinson bound is the speed limit for the information propagation described by the operator norm of the commutator of
\hat{A}(t)
and
\hat{B}
, where
\hat{A}
and
\hat{B}
are spatially distant.
While the Lieb-Robinson bound provides a bound for general operators that may not be restricted to Eq. (
40
) and leads to various dynamical constraints
Bravyi et al. 2006
;
Lashkari et al. 2013
;
Abanin et al. 2015
;
Kuwahara et al. 2016
;
Abanin et al. 2017
;
Kukuljan et al. 2017
;
Gong et al. 2020a
;
Gong et al. 2020b
, it only gives a state-independent maximal velocity and cannot achieve the equality condition in general.
Our speed limit is state-dependent through, e.g.,
E_{\mathrm{trans}}
and thus can give a tighter bound than the Lieb-Robinson bound for an operator in the form of (
40
).
Furthermore, our bounds can achieve the equality condition for some situations, as discussed in Sec.
IV
.

We note that we can obtain a completely different type of inequalities by our approach, i.e., speed limits of acceleration, provided that
\hat{H}
is independent of time.
As shown in Appendix
D
, we find bounds in the form
|\braket{\ddot{\hat{A}}}|\leq\mathcal{Q}
, from which we have

|\braket{\dot{\hat{A}}}|\leq\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{acc}}:=\int_{0}^{t}d\tau\mathcal{Q}
.
Such bounds
\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{acc}}
become zero for short-time limit
t\rightarrow 0
, which may sometimes be better than
\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{gL}}
and

\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{Lip}}
since they can be nonzero for
t\rightarrow 0
.

III.3
Speed limit for macroscopic coherence

As represented by inequality (
20
), the bound is generalized to quantities that are not written as the standard expectation value of an observable.
Here, we show that a reasonable speed limit is obtained even for macroscopic quantum coherence.
While we can consider different measures for coherence
Baumgratz et al. 2014
;
Shimizu and Morimae 2005
;
Yadin and Vedral 2016
;
Streltsov et al. 2017
;
Fröwis et al. 2018
, we introduce the following measure to describe macroscopic quantum coherence:

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}=\sum_{n,m\in\mathcal{V}}c^{nm}||\hat{\rho}(t)_{nm}||_{2}^{2}

(50)

for given
c^{nm}\geq 0
,
where
\|\hat{X}\|_{2}=\sqrt{\mathrm{Tr}[\hat{X}^{\dagger}\hat{X}]}
.
While this measure reduces to (the square of)
l_{2}
-norm coherence for
c^{nm}=-\delta_{nm}+1
, we
here assume that
c^{nm}
is a more general symmetric and positive function of
n
and
m
.
For example, let us consider a single particle on a one-dimensional lattice and take
c^{nm}=|n-m|
(
1\leq n,m\leq N
), which is regarded as a distance between
n
and
m

5
5

5

To be precise,
\mathcal{C}
obtained from this specific choice of
c_{nm}=|n-m|
may not satisfy some criteria
Baumgratz et al. 2014
for quantum coherence. Nevertheless, the measure obtained by this choice can be useful for discussing the speed of certain transitions between states with different macroscopic coherence. We thus use this choice in this work as well as the coherence measure based on the variance.
.
Then, a state
\ket{\psi}=\frac{\ket{l_{1}}+\ket{l_{2}}}{\sqrt{2}}
has
\mathcal{C}=|l_{1}-l_{2}|/2
, whose macroscopicity is controlled by
|l_{1}-l_{2}|
.
Another state
|\psi\rangle=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\frac{|k\rangle}{\sqrt{N}}
has
\mathcal{C}=\frac{N^{2}-1}{3N}
, indicating the existence of the macroscopic coherence.

Instead, when we take
c_{nm}=(a_{n}-a_{m})^{2}
and assume pure states,
\mathcal{C}
essentially reduces to the variance of the macroscopic observable, i.e.,

\mathcal{C}=2\mathbb{V}[\hat{A}]=2(\braket{\hat{A}^{2}}-\braket{\hat{A}}^{2})
.
This provides a lower bound of the macroscopic coherence indicator proposed in Ref.
Shimizu and Morimae 2005
and satisfies some plausible criteria for macroscopic quantum coherence
Yadin and Vedral 2016
.

For simplicity, we consider pure states

\hat{\rho}=\ket{\psi}\bra{\psi}
to derive the speed limit of macroscopic coherence (we discuss the generalization to mixed states in Appendix
E
).
In that case,
\mathcal{C}=\sum_{n,m}c^{nm}p_{n}p_{m}
and we can use the framework in inequalities (
20
) and (
21
).
For example, using (
20
) and (
21
), we have

\displaystyle|\dot{\mathcal{C}}|

\displaystyle\leq 2\sqrt{\frac{\sum_{n\sim_{\mathcal{C}}l}(\tilde{c}^{n}-\tilde{c}^{l})^{2}r_{nl}^{p}}{R_{p}}}\sqrt{R_{p}^{2}-E_{\mathrm{trans}}^{2}}

(51)

and

\displaystyle|\dot{\mathcal{C}}|

\displaystyle\leq 2\max_{n\sim_{\mathcal{C}}l}|\tilde{c}^{n}-\tilde{c}^{l}|\sqrt{C_{H}^{2}-E_{\mathrm{trans}}^{2}},

(52)

where
\tilde{c}^{n}:=\sum_{m}c^{nm}p_{m}
and

\sim_{A}
in the previous subsection is replaced with
\sim_{\mathcal{C}}
, defined from the graph

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{C}}={\left\{(n,l)\>|\>((n,l)\in\mathcal{E})\cap(\tilde{c}^{n}\neq\tilde{c}^{l})\right\}}.

(53)

Interestingly, for pure quantum states,
the obtained bounds of macroscopic coherence contain the same factor with
E_{\mathrm{trans}}
as in the case for the expectation values of observables.

When
c^{nm}
satisfies
the triangle inequality, e.g.,
c^{nm}=|n-m|
, we can replace
\tilde{c}^{n}-\tilde{c}^{l}
above with
c^{nl}
. For example, we have (from, e.g., inequality (
52
))

\displaystyle|\dot{\mathcal{C}}|

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{\tilde{B}}_{p}:=2\max_{n\sim_{\mathcal{C}}l}|c^{nl}|\sqrt{R_{p}^{2}-E_{\mathrm{trans}}^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{\tilde{B}}_{H}:=2\max_{n\sim_{\mathcal{C}}l}|c^{nl}|\sqrt{C_{H}^{2}-E_{\mathrm{trans}}^{2}}.

(54)

The bound in inequality (
III.3
) becomes particularly useful when
\max_{n\sim_{\mathcal{C}}l}|c^{nl}|\ll\max_{n,l\in\mathcal{V}}|c^{nl}|
.
Indeed, for the example of a particle in the one-dimensional system,

\mathcal{C}=|l_{1}-l_{2}|/2
itself can be large when
|l_{1}-l_{2}|=\mathrm{O}(N)
,
whereas
\max_{n\sim_{\mathcal{C}}l}|c^{nl}|=1
and remains
\mathrm{O}(N^{0})
.

Instead, we can take
c_{nm}=(a_{n}-a_{m})^{2}
and obtain the speed limit for the variance, e.g.,

\displaystyle\left|\dot{\mathbb{V}}[\hat{A}]\right|\leq\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{var}}

(55)

\displaystyle:=\sqrt{\frac{\sum_{n\sim_{\mathcal{C}}l}(a_{n}-a_{l})^{2}(a_{n}+a_{l}-2\braket{\hat{A}})^{2}r_{nl}^{p}}{R_{p}}}\sqrt{R_{p}^{2}-E_{\mathrm{trans}}^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\|\nabla A\|_{\infty}\sqrt{\frac{\sum_{n\sim_{\mathcal{C}}l}(a_{n}+a_{l}-2\braket{\hat{A}})^{2}r_{nl}^{p}}{R_{p}}}\sqrt{R_{p}^{2}-E_{\mathrm{trans}}^{2}},

where we have used inequality (
II.2
).

We also note that, applying a similar type of the inequality,
we obtain the absolute bound for the square root of the variance (i.e., the standard deviation
\Delta A=\sqrt{\mathbb{V}[\hat{A}]}
) at finite times (see Appendix
F
),

\displaystyle\left|\Delta A(T)-\Delta A(0)\right|\leq\|\nabla A\|_{\infty}\overline{\sqrt{C_{H}^{2}-E_{\mathrm{trans}}^{2}}}T.

(56)

This means that the graph structure generated by the Hamiltonian can control even the growth of the standard deviation of the observable.

III.4
Speed limit for entropy-like quantities

Applying inequality (
23
) to unitary quantum dynamics, we can also derive the bound for the entropy-like quantities

\mathcal{S}(p,X),

which is defined in Eq. (
22
).
We have, e.g.,

\displaystyle|\dot{\mathcal{S}}|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{\max_{n\sim m}\|\hat{H}_{nm}\|_{\mathrm{op}}}{{R_{p}}}\sum_{n\sim m}p_{n}\left(\ln\frac{p_{n}X_{m}}{p_{m}X_{n}}\right)^{2}}\sqrt{R_{p}^{2}-{E_{\mathrm{trans}}^{2}}},

(57)

where we set
r=r^{p}
and use
\sqrt{p_{n}p_{m}}\leq(p_{n}+p_{m})/2
. Here,
R_{p}
and
E_{\mathrm{trans}}
are defined from the graph
\mathcal{E}
instead of
\mathcal{E}_{A}
.
For
X=p^{\mathrm{ref}}
, the first term in the right-hand side reduces to the square root of the (discrete version of) Fisher divergence
Lyu 2012
,

\tilde{\mathcal{F}}(p||p^{\mathrm{ref}})=\sum_{n\sim m}p_{n}(\ln(p_{n}/p_{m})-\ln(p_{n}^{\mathrm{ref}}/p_{m}^{\mathrm{ref}}))^{2}/2
(see inequality (
66
) for the continuous case).

When we consider the Shannon entropy,
S(p)=\mathcal{S}(p,\vec{1})=-\sum_{n}p_{n}\ln p_{n}
,
we have a different type of nontrivial bounds.
As shown in Appendix
G
, we find

\displaystyle|\dot{S}|\leq\sqrt{8\left(\frac{C_{H}}{R_{p}}-1\right)}\sqrt{{R_{p}^{2}-{E_{\mathrm{trans}}^{2}}}}.

(58)

This is further evaluated by the bound determined from only the graph structure and the expectation value of the transition Hamiltonian:

\displaystyle|\dot{S}|\leq\sqrt{2}\left(C_{H}-\frac{E_{\mathrm{trans}}^{2}}{C_{H}}\right).

(59)

It is an interesting future problem to compare our result with other speed limits for entropies
Deffner 2020
, such as
the Bremermann-Bekenstein bound
Bremermann 1967
;
Bekenstein 1981
for information transfer.

III.5
Bound for continuous case

Before ending this section, let us discuss a speed limit for continuous systems and its relation to the bound for discrete systems.
For simplicity, we here focus on the following nonlinear Schrödinger equation,

\displaystyle i\hbar\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t}=\left(-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\nabla^{2}+V_{\mathrm{ext}}+g|\psi|^{2}\right)\psi,

(60)

where we explicitly write the Planck constant in this subsection.
This equation can describe, e.g., the Gross-Pitaevskii equation
Dalfovo et al. 1999
for Bose-Einstein condensates by imposing the normalization condition
\int d\mathbf{x}|\psi(\mathbf{x})|^{2}=M
, where
M
is the number of Bosons.
In the following, we instead assume
\int d\mathbf{x}|\psi(\mathbf{x})|^{2}=1
without loss of generality, which makes it easier directly to compare with the discussion in the previous subsections.

In this model, the quantum current is given by

\displaystyle\mathbf{j}=\frac{i\hbar}{2m}\left(\psi\nabla\psi^{*}-\psi^{*}\nabla\psi\right):=|\psi(\mathbf{x},t)|^{2}\mathbf{v}=\rho\mathbf{v},

(61)

where
\rho=|\psi|^{2}
.
This satisfies the continuity equation

\frac{\partial|\psi|^{2}}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot\mathbf{j}=0.

Then, assuming that
\mathbf{j}(\mathbf{x})\rightarrow 0
for
|\mathbf{x}|\rightarrow\infty
, the expectation value of an on-site observable
A(\mathbf{x})
has the following speed limit:

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int d\mathbf{x}A(\mathbf{x})|\psi(\mathbf{x},t)|^{2}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\int d\mathbf{x}\nabla A\cdot\mathbf{j}\right|\leq\sqrt{\int d\mathbf{x}\rho|\nabla A|^{2}}\sqrt{2E_{\mathrm{kin}}}.

(62)

Here, we have introduced the kinetic energy

\displaystyle E_{\mathrm{kin}}=\int d\mathbf{x}\frac{\rho|\mathbf{v}|^{2}}{2}=\int d\mathbf{x}\frac{\rho|\nabla\theta|^{2}\hbar^{2}}{2m^{2}}

(63)

and the quantum phase
\theta
of the state (or the condensate), which satisfies
\nabla\theta=m\mathbf{v}/\hbar
.
Inequality (
III.5
) indicates that the speed should be small when the magnitude of the quantum phase difference is small, which represents the tradeoff relation between transition time and the quantum phase difference.
We also note that

E_{\mathrm{kin}}=\int d\mathbf{x}\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}|\nabla\psi|^{2}-\int d\mathbf{x}\frac{\hbar^{2}}{8m}\rho(\nabla\ln\rho)^{2},

where the second term is called the quantum pressure
Dalfovo et al. 1999
in atomic physics, which is also proportional to (one of the definitions of) the classical Fisher information
Villani 2009
known in statistics.
As far as we know, the quantum speed limit for the nonlinear Schrödinger equation in the form of inequality (
III.5
) has never been mentioned before.

We can also find the speed limits of the variance and the entropy-like quantities as in the inequalities (
56
) and (
III.4
).
For example, the variance of
A
satisfies

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{V}[A]}{\mathrm{d}t}\right|\leq 2\|\nabla A\|_{\infty}\sqrt{\mathbb{V}[A]}\sqrt{2E_{\mathrm{kin}}},

(64)

which leads to

\displaystyle|\Delta A(T)-\Delta A(0)|\leq\|\nabla A\|_{\infty}\overline{\sqrt{2E_{\mathrm{kin}}}}T.

(65)

In addition, the Kullback-Leibler divergence between
\rho(\mathbf{x},t)
and
\sigma(\mathbf{x})
has a speed limit

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int d\mathbf{x}\rho(\mathbf{x},t)\ln\frac{\rho(\mathbf{x},t)}{\sigma(\mathbf{x})}\right|\leq\sqrt{\mathcal{F}(\rho||\sigma)}\sqrt{2E_{\mathrm{kin}}},

(66)

where

\mathcal{F}(\rho||\sigma):=\int d\mathbf{x}\rho(\mathbf{x})\left|\nabla\ln\rho(\mathbf{x})-\nabla\ln\sigma(\mathbf{x})\right|^{2}
is the Fisher divergence
Lyu 2012
.

Notably, for a simple case, this speed limit corresponds to the continuous version of
\mathcal{B}_{p}
for a discrete nonlinear Schrödinger equation.
To see this, let us focus on the following equation of motion for a one-dimensional system

\displaystyle i\frac{\mathrm{d}\psi_{l}}{\mathrm{d}t}=-K(\psi_{l-1}+\psi_{l+1})+W\psi_{l}+\tilde{g}|\psi_{l}|^{2}\psi_{l}.

(67)

In this case, we have

E_{\mathrm{trans}}=\sum_{l}K(\psi_{l}^{*}\psi_{l-1}+\psi_{l-1}^{*}\psi_{l})

and
\|\hat{H}_{nm}\|_{\mathrm{op}}=K
.
With inserting
\psi_{l}=\sqrt{\rho_{l}}e^{i\theta_{l}}
and taking the continuous limit, we have (neglecting the boundary term)

\displaystyle{E_{\mathrm{trans}}}

\displaystyle=K\sum_{l}{\left\{\rho_{l}+\rho_{l-1}-(\sqrt{\rho_{l}}-\sqrt{\rho_{l-1}})^{2}\right\}}\cos(\theta_{l}-\theta_{l-1})

\displaystyle\rightarrow 2K-Ka^{2}\int dx\rho(\partial_{x}\theta)^{2}-Ka^{2}\int dx\frac{(\partial_{x}\rho)^{2}}{4\rho},

(68)

\displaystyle R_{p}\rightarrow 2K-Ka^{2}\int dx\frac{(\partial_{x}\rho)^{2}}{\rho},

(69)

\displaystyle R_{p}-\frac{E_{\mathrm{trans}}^{2}}{R_{p}}\rightarrow 2Ka^{2}\int dx\rho(\partial_{x}\theta)^{2},

(70)

and

\displaystyle 2[A^{\mathsf{T}}\nabla^{2}_{r^{p}}{A}]\rightarrow 2Ka^{2}\int dx(\partial_{x}A)^{2}\rho.

(71)

Here,
a
is the lattice constant for the discrete system, and we take the leading order of
a
.
Consequently, the transition-energy bound in Eq. (
45
) becomes

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int d{x}A({x})|\psi({x},t)|^{2}\right|\leq 2Ka^{2}\sqrt{\int d{x}\rho|\partial_{x}A|^{2}}\sqrt{2E_{\mathrm{kin}}},

(72)

which is equivalent to inequality (
III.5
) by setting

K=\frac{\hbar}{2ma^{2}}.

The comparison with the continuous and discrete systems also clarifies the close relationship between
R_{p}
and the Fisher information.
In fact, if
\|\hat{H}_{nm}\|_{\mathrm{op}}
is constant for all
n
and
m
,
R_{p}
involves the negative sign of the discrete version of Fisher information (see Eq. (
69
) for the continuous conterpart).
As a more rigorous statement, in this simple case, we can show that

\displaystyle\frac{R_{p}}{\|\hat{H}_{nm}\|_{\mathrm{op}}}=\sum_{n\sim_{A}m}p_{n}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n\sim_{A}m}(\sqrt{p_{n}}-\sqrt{p_{m}})^{2},

(73)

where the second term becomes proportional to the Fisher information when we take the continuous limit.

IV.0.2
Many-particle system

Next, we consider a one-dimensional system composed of

\displaystyle\hat{H}=-\sum_{l=1}^{L-1}K(\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{l+1}\hat{a}_{l}+\mathrm{h.c.})+\sum_{l=1}^{L-1}V\hat{n}_{l}\hat{n}_{l+1}+\sum_{l=1}^{L}W_{l}\hat{n}_{l},

\hat{a}_{l}

V.1
General setting for discrete systems

We introduce a finite-dimensional state space
\mathcal{S}
and consider the Markovian equation

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\rho_{x}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\sum_{y}\mathcal{W}_{xy}\rho_{y},

(94)

where
x,y\in\mathcal{S}
,
\rho
is the classical probability distribution and
\mathcal{W}_{xy}
is the transition rate matrix satisfying
\mathcal{W}_{xy}\geq 0
for
x\neq y
and
\sum_{x}\mathcal{W}_{xy}=0
.

We arbitrarily decompose
\mathcal{S}
as

\mathcal{S}=\bigoplus_{n}\mathcal{S}_{n}

and define the coarse-grained probability

\displaystyle p_{n}=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{S}_{n}}\rho_{x}

(95)

as well as the coarse-grained transition rate matrix

\displaystyle{W}_{nm}=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{S}_{n}}\frac{\sum_{y\in\mathcal{S}_{m}}\mathcal{W}_{xy}\rho_{y}}{\sum_{y\in\mathcal{S}_{m}}\rho_{y}}.

(96)

Then, the original Master equation becomes the coarse-grained one
Esposito 2012
,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}p_{n}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\sum_{m}{W}_{nm}p_{m}=-\sum_{m(\sim n)}J_{mn}^{c},

(97)

where

\displaystyle J_{mn}^{c}=-{W}_{nm}p_{m}+{W}_{mn}p_{n}.

(98)

and the graph
\mathcal{E}
is defined as
\mathcal{E}={\left\{(n,m)\>|\>(n\neq m)\text{ and }(W_{nm}\neq 0\text{ or }W_{mn}\neq 0)\right\}}
.
We note that
{W}
depends on
p(t)
in general and that
\sum_{n}W_{nm}=0
.

V.2
Bound for macroscopic observables

We consider a macroscopic observable that is given by

\displaystyle A=\sum_{n}a_{n}p_{n}.

(99)

Using the general method introduced in Sec.
II
, we obtain

\braket{\dot{A}}=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n\sim m}(a_{n}-a_{m})J_{mn}^{c}

and inequalities such as

\displaystyle|\braket{\dot{A}}|\leq\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{cur}}^{c}:=\frac{\|\nabla A\|_{\infty}}{2}\sum_{n\sim_{A}m}|J_{mn}^{c}|.

(100)

V.2.1
Standard entropy production bound

Let us first consider a bound based on the standard entropy production rate.
For simplicity, we start from the case where the system is attached to a single heat bath at temperature
\beta^{-1}
and
\mathcal{W}
satisfies the detailed balance condition, i.e.,
\mathcal{W}_{xy}/\mathcal{W}_{yx}=e^{-\beta(E_{x}-E_{y})}
, where
E_{x}
is the energy of the state
x
.
Denoting the Shannon entropy of
\rho
as
S(\rho)
, we can write down the entropy production rate of the system
\dot{\Sigma}:=\dot{S}(\rho_{x})+\sum_{x\neq y}\mathcal{W}_{yx}\rho_{x}\beta(E_{x}-E_{y})
as
Seifert 2012

\displaystyle\dot{\Sigma}=\sum_{x\neq y}\mathcal{W}_{xy}\rho_{y}\ln\frac{\mathcal{W}_{xy}\rho_{y}}{\mathcal{W}_{yx}\rho_{x}}.

(101)

Similarly, we can define the following entropy production rate defined from the coarse-grained Markovian dynamics
Esposito 2012

\displaystyle\dot{\Sigma}_{\mathrm{cg}}:=\sum_{n\sim_{A}m}W_{nm}p_{m}\ln\frac{W_{nm}p_{m}}{W_{mn}p_{n}}.

(102)

This quantity is smaller than the bare entropy production rate, i.e.,

\dot{\Sigma}_{\mathrm{cg}}\leq\dot{\Sigma}
,
which is shown via the log-sum inequality (see Appendix
I
).

Now, as proven in Appendix
I
, we find the following bound for the instantaneous speed of the expectation value of
A
:

\displaystyle~|\braket{\dot{A}}|\leq\sqrt{\frac{\Theta_{A}\dot{\Sigma}_{\mathrm{cg}}}{2}}\leq\|\nabla A\|_{\infty}\sqrt{\frac{\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{cg}}\dot{\Sigma}_{\mathrm{cg}}}{2}}

(103)

and

\displaystyle~|\braket{\dot{A}}|\leq\|\nabla A\|_{\infty}\sqrt{\frac{\mathcal{A}\dot{\Sigma}}{2}}

(104)

where
\Theta_{A}:=\sum_{n\sim m}(a_{n}-a_{m})^{2}W_{nm}p_{m}
is the second moment of the transition speed of
A
and we define

\displaystyle~\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{cg}}:=\sum_{n\sim_{A}m}W_{nm}

(105)

as the dynamical activity of the coarse-grained equation.
Note that
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{cg}}\leq\mathcal{A}:=\sum_{x\neq y}\mathcal{W}_{xy}
, where
\mathcal{A}
is the bare dynamical activity
Shiraishi et al. 2018
.
The proof of inequality (
103
) is made
by taking
r_{nm}=W_{nm}p_{m}+W_{mn}p_{n}
(note that
\Theta_{A}=[A^{\mathsf{T}}\nabla^{2}_{r}A]
) and using the inequalities introduced in Refs.
Shiraishi et al. 2018
;
Shiraishi et al. 2016
.
This inequality can also be obtained from the short-time version of the thermodynamic uncertainty relation
Otsubo et al. 2020
.
It explicitly shows that the entropy production rate can provide a useful bound for macroscopic systems because of the factor
\|\nabla A\|_{\infty}
, which is concretely
illustrated in Sec.
VI
.

We can use the instantaneous speed limit obtained above as the bound for the relaxation time. Integrating the inequality in (
104

T
,
we obtain

\displaystyle T\geq\frac{2|\braket{{A}}(t)-\braket{A}(0)|^{2}}{||\nabla A||_{\mathrm{\infty}}^{2}{{\overline{\mathcal{A}}\Sigma}}},

(106)

where we define the total entropy production

\Sigma=\int_{0}^{T}dt\dot{\Sigma}
, which is equal to
D(\rho(0)||\rho^{\mathrm{ss}})-D(\rho(t)||\rho^{\mathrm{ss}})
for time-independent
\mathcal{W}

8
8

8

This bound may be further tightened by using the recently found inequality for the Wasserstein distance
Dechant 2021
.
.

We note that the above inequalities can be much tighter than that proposed in Ref.
Shiraishi et al. 2018

Indeed, the reference considers the speed limit for total variation distance, from which we obtain

V.2.2
Hatano-Sasa entropy production bound

While the above bounds based on the standard entropy production rate is useful under the detailed balance condition, it may not provide a tight bound for long times without this condition.
This is because of the possible existence of a stationary current, which leads to the nonzero entropy production rate.

Here, we show that a modified speed of the transition of an observable is bounded using the Hatano-Sasa entropy production
Hatano and Sasa 2001
, which is
the entropy production where the entropy generated by the stationary dissipation is subtracted.
Assuming that the instantaneous stationary state
\rho^{\mathrm{ss}}
of
\mathcal{W}(t)
is unique, the Hatano-Sasa entropy production rate for general Markovian systems is given by

\displaystyle\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{HS}}=\sum_{x,y}\mathcal{W}_{xy}(t)\rho_{y}(t)\ln\frac{\mathcal{W}_{xy}(t)\rho_{y}(t)}{\tilde{\mathcal{W}}_{yx}(t)\rho_{x}(t)},

(108)

where

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{W}}_{yx}(t)=\frac{\mathcal{W}_{xy}(t)\rho_{y}^{\mathrm{ss}}(t)}{\rho_{x}^{\mathrm{ss}}(t)}

(109)

is the generator of the dual process
Shiraishi et al. 2018
.
The important property of
\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{HS}}
is that it vanishes for the stationary state unlike
\dot{\Sigma}
.
In addition,
\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{HS}}=-\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}D(\rho(t)||\rho^{\mathrm{ss}})
for time-independent
\mathcal{W}
.
To derive general inequalities, we also introduce the coarse-grained version of
\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{HS}}
,

\displaystyle\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{HS}}_{\mathrm{cg}}=\sum_{n,m}W_{nm}(t)p_{m}(t)\ln\frac{W_{nm}(t)p_{m}(t)}{\tilde{W}_{mn}(t)p_{n}(t)},

(110)

where

\tilde{W}_{mn}(t)=\frac{W_{nm}(t)p_{m}^{\mathrm{ss}}(t)}{p_{n}^{\mathrm{ss}}(t)}
.

Now, to derive a speed limit, we notice that

\displaystyle-J_{mn}^{c}=W_{nm}p_{m}-\tilde{W}_{mn}p_{n}-J_{mn}^{c,\mathrm{ss}}\frac{p_{n}}{p_{n}^{\mathrm{ss}}},

(111)

where

\displaystyle J_{mn}^{c,\mathrm{ss}}=-W_{nm}p_{m}^{\mathrm{ss}}+W_{mn}p_{n}^{\mathrm{ss}}

(112)

is the stationary probability current.
From this, we have

\displaystyle\braket{\dot{A}}=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{(n,m)\in\mathcal{E}^{c}}(a_{n}-a_{m})J_{mn}^{c,\mathrm{ss}}\frac{p_{n}}{p_{n}^{\mathrm{ss}}}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{(n,m)\in\mathcal{E}^{c}}(a_{n}-a_{m})(W_{nm}p_{m}-\tilde{W}_{mn}p_{n}).

(113)

Then, introducing

\displaystyle V_{A}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{(n,m)\in\mathcal{E}^{c}}a_{n}J_{mn}^{c,\mathrm{ss}}\left(\frac{p_{n}}{p_{n}^{\mathrm{ss}}}+\frac{p_{m}}{p_{m}^{\mathrm{ss}}}\right),

(114)

we have

\displaystyle|\braket{\dot{A}}-V_{A}|\leq\sqrt{{\frac{\tilde{\Theta}_{A}\dot{\Sigma}_{\mathrm{cg}}^{\mathrm{HS}}}{2}}}\leq\|\nabla A\|_{\infty}\sqrt{\frac{\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{cg}}\dot{\Sigma}_{\mathrm{cg}}^{\mathrm{HS}}}{2}},

(115)

where

\tilde{\Theta}_{A}=\max[\sum_{n\sim_{A}m}(a_{n}-a_{m})^{2}W_{nm}p_{m}
,
\sum_{n\sim_{A}m}(a_{n}-a_{m})^{2}\tilde{W}_{nm}p_{m}]
.
As a special case, we have the non-coarse-grained version of the inequality,

\displaystyle|\braket{\dot{A}}-V_{A}|\leq\|\nabla A\|_{\infty}\sqrt{\frac{\mathcal{A}\dot{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{HS}}}{2}}.

(116)

We can regard the (modified) speed limits in inequalities (
115
) and (
116
) as bounds for the speed where the contribution of the background current is subtracted.
In fact, in the stationary state,
V_{A}
becomes a stationary current for
{A}
.
Since the right-hand side of inequality (
116
) becomes zero for the stationary state, the inequality can be much tighter than the bound using the standard entropy production rate if the detailed balance condition is absent.

VII
Speed limit for macroscopic quantum systems with Markovian dissipation

In this section, we discuss speed limit for open quantum systems described the Gorini-Kossakowski-Sudarshan-Lindblad master equation
Gorini et al. 1976
;
Lindblad 1976
.
In this case, the quantum state obeys

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\hat{\rho}}{\mathrm{d}t}=-i[\hat{H},\hat{\rho}]+\sum_{\eta}\left(\hat{L}_{\eta}\hat{\rho}\hat{L}_{\eta}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}{\left\{\hat{L}_{\eta}^{\dagger}\hat{L}_{\eta},\hat{\rho}\right\}}\right),

(121)

where
\eta
labels the type of the dissipation.
We obtain the following continuity equation for this dynamics:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}p_{n}}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\sum_{m,n}J_{mn}^{q}(t)-\sum_{\eta}K^{\eta}_{n},

(122)

where
J_{mn}^{q}
is given by Eq. (
39
) and

\displaystyle K^{\eta}_{n}=

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}[\sum_{ml}(\hat{L}_{\eta})_{nm}\hat{\rho}_{ml}(\hat{L}_{\eta}^{\dagger})_{ln}-\frac{1}{2}(\hat{L}_{\eta}^{\dagger})_{nm}(\hat{L}_{\eta})_{ml}\hat{\rho}_{ln}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\hat{\rho}_{nm}(\hat{L}_{\eta}^{\dagger})_{ml}(\hat{L}_{\eta})_{ln}].

(123)

For an observable written as
\hat{A}=\sum_{n}a_{n}\hat{P}_{n}
, we have

\displaystyle|\braket{\dot{\hat{A}}}|\leq\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{Uni}}+\left|\sum_{\eta}\sum_{n}a_{n}K_{n}^{\eta}\right|,

(124)

where the first term in the right hand side,

\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{Uni}}=\mathrm{min}{\left\{\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{gL}},\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{Lip}}\right\}}
, is the contribution from the Hamiltonian part (unitary dynamics) which we have discussed in Sec.
III
.
Note that the graph
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{Uni}}
to evaluate

\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{Uni}}
is defined from the nonzero off-diagonal elements of the Hamiltonian as in Sec.
III
, which is different from

\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{D}}
in the following discussion.

To bound the second term in inequality (
124
), which comes from the dissipation operators
\hat{L}_{\eta}
,
we impose one assumption about the jump operator: we require that each jump
\eta
moves a state in the subspace
\mathcal{H}_{m}
to that in
\mathcal{H}_{n=f_{\eta}(m)}
with an injective function
f_{\eta}
.
In other words, when
(\hat{L}_{\eta})_{nm}
is nonzero for some
n
and
m
,

(\hat{L}_{\eta})_{n^{\prime}m}\>(n^{\prime}\neq n)
and

(\hat{L}_{\eta})_{nm^{\prime}}\>(m^{\prime}\neq m)
should be zero.
Under this assumption, we introduce the (coarse-grained) transition rate from
m
to
n
,

\displaystyle T_{m\rightarrow n}^{\mathrm{cg}}=\sum_{\eta}\mathrm{Tr}[(\hat{L}_{\eta}^{\dagger})_{mn}(\hat{L}_{\eta})_{nm}\hat{\rho}_{mm}],

(125)

and the edge set
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{D}}={\left\{(n,m)\>|\>T_{m\rightarrow n}^{\mathrm{cg}}\neq 0\right\}}
.

Then, we find (see Appendix
J
)

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{\eta}\sum_{n}a_{n}K_{n}^{\eta}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\max_{(n,m)\in\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{D}}}|a_{n}-a_{m}|\sqrt{\frac{\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{cg}}^{d}\dot{\Sigma}_{\mathrm{cg}}^{d}}{2}},

(126)

where we have defined

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{cg}}^{d}=\sum_{n\neq m}T_{m\rightarrow n}^{\mathrm{cg}},

(127)

and

\displaystyle\dot{\Sigma}_{\mathrm{cg}}^{d}=\sum_{n,m}T_{m\rightarrow n}^{\mathrm{cg}}\ln\frac{T_{m\rightarrow n}^{\mathrm{cg}}}{T_{n\rightarrow m}^{\mathrm{cg}}}\geq 0.

(128)

We note that the non-coarse-grained version of
\dot{\Sigma}_{\mathrm{cg}}^{d}
,

\displaystyle\dot{\Sigma}^{d}=\sum_{x,y}\sum_{\eta}|(\hat{L}_{\eta})_{xy}|^{2}p_{y}\ln\frac{\sum_{\eta}|(\hat{L}_{\eta})_{xy}|^{2}p_{y}}{\sum_{\eta}|(\hat{L}_{\eta})_{yx}|^{2}p_{x}},

(129)

reduces to Eq. (
101
) when

\hat{L}_{\eta}=\hat{L}_{(xy)}=\delta_{\eta,(xy)}\sqrt{\mathcal{W}_{xy}}\ket{x}\bra{y}.

In addition, we can regard
\dot{\Sigma}^{d}
as the physical entropy production of open quantum systems by assuming
\{\hat{L}_{\eta}\}
as the Lindblad operators driven by baths that satisfy the local detailed balance condition
Funo et al. 2019
.

To summarize, we have a bound (
124
) with inequality (
126
), which represents the bound based on
the gradient of
\hat{A}
.
Thus, our speed limit is again valuable for macroscopic transitions for which the coherent dynamics and incoherent dissipation process coexist.
One of the examples of such processes can be the transport process in an extended quantum system with the stochastic hopping
Eisler 2011
;
Temme et al. 2012
;
Yusipov et al. 2017
;
Haga et al. 2021
, which is proposed to be realized in a cold atomic system with engineered dissipation
Diehl et al. 2008
;
Haga et al. 2021
.

VIII.2
Concentration and distribution

Although we have focused on the speed of specific quantities such as the expectation value, we can also discuss the bound on the entire probability distribution
p(t)=\{p_{n}(t)\}
.
To see this, we first notice Chebyshev’s inequality,

\mathbb{P}_{t}[|A-\braket{A}|>\epsilon]\leq\frac{\mathbb{V}[A]}{\epsilon^{2}}

for
\epsilon>0
,
where
\mathbb{P}_{t}
is the probability with respect to
p(t)
.
To simplify the discussion, let us consider unitary quantum dynamics.

Then,
\mathbb{V}
is bounded as (see Eqs. (
55

56

\mathbb{V}[\hat{A}(t)]\leq\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{var}}(t^{\prime})dt^{\prime}+\mathbb{V}[\hat{A}(0)]

\mathbb{V}[\hat{A}(t)]\leq\|\nabla A\|_{\infty}^{2}(\Delta A(0)+\overline{\sqrt{C_{H}^{2}-E_{\mathrm{trans}}^{2}}}t)^{2}
.
Employing the latter inequality, for example, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{t}\left[\left|\frac{A-\braket{\hat{A}}}{\|\nabla A\|_{\infty}}\right|>\epsilon\right]\leq\frac{\left(\Delta A(0)+\overline{\sqrt{C_{H}^{2}-E_{\mathrm{trans}}^{2}}}t\right)^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}}.

(131)

To see an example of the physical implication of this inequality, let us consider a situation for which the underlying graph is one dimensional as in Fig.
3
(c). We also assume that
\Delta A(0)=0
for simplicity.
Then, taking

leads to

\displaystyle\sum_{|n-\braket{\hat{n}}|>\epsilon}p_{n}(t)\leq\frac{(\overline{\sqrt{C_{H}^{2}-E_{\mathrm{trans}}^{2}}}t)^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}}.

(132)

Thus, the probability of the tail for
|n-\braket{n}|\gg\overline{\sqrt{C_{H}^{2}-E_{\mathrm{trans}}^{2}}}t
is polynomially suppressed.

Furthermore, we find the following nontrivial exponential form of the inequality

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{t}\left[\left|\frac{A-A^{\prime}}{\|\nabla A\|_{\infty}}\right|>\epsilon\right]\leq 2\left\langle\cosh\frac{\lambda(\hat{A}-A^{\prime})}{\|\nabla A\|_{\infty}}\right\rangle_{0}e^{\overline{C_{H}}t-\lambda\epsilon}

(133)

for

and an arbitrary real value
A^{\prime}
(see Appendix

where
\braket{\cdots}_{0}
denotes the average with respect to
\hat{\rho}(0)

VIII.3
Other observables

In the previous sections concerning quantum dynamics, we have considered observables that are written as
\hat{A}=\sum_{n}a_{n}\hat{P}_{n}
.
Here we discuss the possibility of extending our results to other observables.

One direction is to diagonalize a general observable
\hat{B}
as
\hat{B}=\sum_{\nu}b_{\nu}\hat{P}_{\nu}
and consider a graph whose vertices are labeled by
\{\nu\}
.
In this case, the speed is given by
|\braket{\dot{\hat{B}}}|=\left|\sum_{\nu\mu}(b_{\nu}-b_{\mu})J_{\nu\mu}^{q}\right|
, where
J_{\nu\mu}^{q}=\mathrm{Tr}[\hat{H}_{\nu\mu}\hat{\rho}_{\mu\nu}-\hat{\rho}_{\nu\mu}\hat{H}_{\mu\nu}]
.
Thus, when
|J_{\nu\mu}^{q}|
is suppressed for
(\nu,\mu)
with large
|b_{\nu}-b_{\mu}|
, the speed limit can decrease.
We leave the detailed analysis of this condition as a future problem.

Another direction is to obtain the speed limit of certain observables
from the knowledge of the distribution
p(t)
, which was discussed in the previous subsection.
For example, let us assume the one-dimensional graph with the label
n=\cdots,-1,0,1,\cdots
and
p_{n}(0)=\delta_{n0}
(see the previous subsection).
We consider a general observable
\hat{B}
satisfying

\hat{P}_{n}\hat{B}\hat{P}_{n^{\prime}}=0
when
n,n^{\prime}\leq n_{\mathrm{th}}=\mathrm{O}(N)
.
In this case, using inequality (
134
), we can prove that the speed of
\braket{\hat{B}}
is small for
t\lesssim n_{\mathrm{th}}/v_{M}
as

\displaystyle|\braket{\dot{\hat{B}}}|\leq c\|\hat{B}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\|\hat{H}\|_{\mathrm{op}}e^{\overline{C_{H}}(t-n_{\mathrm{th}}/v_{M})},

(135)

where
c
is a constant.
Note that, even when
\|\hat{B}\|_{\mathrm{op}}
and
\|\hat{H}\|_{\mathrm{op}}
are
\mathrm{O}(N)
, the right-hand side is exponentially suppressed with
N
for
t\lesssim n_{\mathrm{th}}/v_{M}
.

VIII.4
Tightening quantum speed limit for mixed states

In the previous sections concerning quantum systems, we have mainly focused on pure quantum states.
On the other hand, the loss of quantum coherence can tighten the speed limit for a purity-decreasing process.

As a first example, let us consider the bound on the transition time for finite times in inequality (
28
).
Assuming time-independent unitary time dynamics, we obtain the bound for the averaged speed (using the discussion similar to Appendix
C
)

\displaystyle\frac{|A_{\mathrm{fin}}-A_{\mathrm{ini}}|}{T}\leq{\|\nabla A\|_{\infty}}\sqrt{R_{\mathrm{av}}^{2}-\overline{E_{\mathrm{trans}}}^{2}},

(136)

where
R_{\mathrm{av}}=\sum_{n\sim_{A}m}\mathrm{Tr}[\overline{\hat{\rho}}_{nm}\hat{H}_{mn}]
.
The right-hand side can be suppressed for finite
T
compared with the case for
T\simeq 0
when starting from a pure state, since
R_{\mathrm{av}}\leq\sum_{n\sim_{A}m}\|\overline{\hat{\rho}}_{nm}\|_{1}\|\hat{H}_{nm}\|_{\mathrm{op}}

and
\|\overline{\hat{\rho}}_{nm}\|_{1}
can be smaller than
\sqrt{p_{n}p_{m}}
for mixed states.

Another example is the case for the dissipative quantum dynamics discussed in Section
VII
.
In this case, we have, e.g.,

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{H}}\leq\sqrt{2\frac{[A^{\mathsf{T}}\nabla^{2}_{r^{\mathrm{coh}}}A]}{{R_{\mathrm{coh}}}}}\sqrt{R_{\mathrm{coh}}^{2}-{E_{\mathrm{trans}}^{2}}},

(137)

where
r_{nm}^{\mathrm{coh}}=\|\hat{\rho}_{nm}\|_{1}\|\hat{H}_{nm}\|_{\mathrm{op}}
(see Eq. (
181
)) and
R_{\mathrm{coh}}=\sum_{n\sim_{A}m}r_{nm}^{\mathrm{coh}}
.
Since
r_{nm}^{\mathrm{coh}}\leq r_{nm}^{p}
for mixed states
and a dissipative time evolution leads to the loss of purity,
the right-hand side of inequality (
137
) is in general smaller than
\mathcal{B}_{p}
for such a process.

IX
Conclusion and outlook

This paper has presented a fundamental framework for deriving speed limits in processes with macroscopic transitions.
Our strategy is to employ the local conservation law of probability
on a general graph, which describes general systems including many-body ones.
We prove the bound with the local probability current and the gradient of the quantity of interest,
which is concisely expressed by the notions of the graph theory.
Our rigorous bounds are qualitatively tighter for extended and many-body systems than conventional speed limits based on, e.g., the uncertainty relation.

Our framework applies to various dynamics since it relies only on the local conservation law of probability, leading to many novel and practical bounds.
Applying it to quantum systems, we obtain previously unknown bounds that become smaller when the expectation value of the transition Hamiltonian increases for unitary quantum dynamics.
This is intuitively understood as the novel tradeoff relation between time and quantum phase difference instead of that between time and energy fluctuation in the Mandelstam-Tamm bound.
Our results provide first general state-dependent speed limits useful for macroscopic systems;
it can achieve the equality condition for some situations, in stark contrast with the state-independent Lieb-Robinson bound.
In addition, we demonstrate that our speed limits can apply to quantities that are not written as the expectation value of an observable, such as macroscopic quantum coherence.
We have also discussed that speed limits are described by the entropy production rate for macroscopic classical stochastic processes.
In particular, we derive a valuable speed limit for long times even without the detailed balance condition.
Furthermore, our method enables us to derive a speed limit for macroscopic dissipative quantum systems.

There are many open questions for future studies.
First, while our starting point is to use the local conservation law of probability, which holds for generic systems, it is interesting to discuss how additional conservation laws may tighten the speed limit.
For example, a time-dependent Hamiltonian in an isolated system leads to the local conservation law of energy density, from which a different type of speed limits can arise.
Moreover, more nontrivial conservation laws (such as the dipolar moment
Pai et al. 2019
;
Sala et al. 2020
or the ones associated with integrability
Castro-Alvaredo et al. 2016
;
Bulchandani et al. 2017
) have recently been known to slow down the dynamics, and our approach may uncover corresponding speed limits.
We also note that our method can be helpful for a more coarse-grained level by starting from the continuity equation of the (possibly discrete) quantum/classical hydrodynamics
Spohn 2012
;
Tsubota et al. 2013
.

Second, it is important to thoroughly investigate the relationship with quantum thermalization in isolated many-body systems.
Timescale for thermalization for quantum chaotic systems is an active field of research
Goldstein et al. 2013
;
Goldstein et al. 2015
;
Reimann 2016
;
de Oliveira et al. 2018
;
Schiulaz et al. 2019
;
García-Pintos et al. 2017
, but it is not simple to obtain rigorous results on a relevant timescale for macroscopic transitions.
While our approach succeeds in obtaining rigorous bounds for such a macroscopic process,
we may obtain even tighter bounds by taking in the complexity of
many-body dynamics.
For this purpose, we may need to combine our method and the property of quantum chaotic systems, such as the eigenstate thermalization hypothesis
Jensen and Shankar 1985
;
Deutsch 1991
;
Srednicki 1994
;
Rigol et al. 2008
and the local random-matrix theory
Nahum et al. 2017
;
Nahum et al. 2018
;
von Keyserlingk et al. 2018
;
Khemani et al. 2018
;
Rakovszky et al. 2018
;
Hamazaki and Ueda 2018
;
Sugimoto et al. 2021
.

Third, it will be intriguing to compare our results with the knowledge in the field of statistics and mathematics
Villani 2009
;
Boucheron et al. 2013
;
Van Handel 2014
.
While our general approach evades the explicit introduction of the Wasserstein distance, which may complicate the discussion and concrete calculation, the obtained bounds can be tied to this distance as indicated by the case for one dimension (see inequality (
33
) and Appendix
A
).
It is a future outlook to relate our results with recent development of the notion of quantum Wasserstein distance
Carlen and Maas 2014
;
Datta and Rouzé 2020
;
Deffner 2017
;
Chow et al. 2019
;
Van Vu and Hasegawa 2021
;
De Palma et al. 2021
;
Hu et al. 2020
and the optimal-transport-based inequalities for stochastic systems
Dechant and Sakurai 2019
;
Van Vu and Hasegawa 2021
;
Nakazato and Ito 2021
;
Dechant et al. 2021
;
Dechant 2021
.
Another mathematical perspective is the connection with the spectral graph theory
Chung and Graham 1997
.
While our speed limits are described in the terminology of graph theory, the graph’s structure can also be uncovered from the spectra of the graph Laplacians.
It is thus interesting to discuss our speed limit in light of the spectral property of the graph.

Finally, it is a significant challenge to investigate the implications of our results to the engineering of quantum systems.
For example, it is interesting to discuss how our speed limits are related to the quantum sensing
Tóth and Apellaniz 2014
and shortcuts to adiabaticity
Guéry-Odelin et al. 2019
for many-body systems.
In addition, the problem of optimal quantum-state transfer
Bose 2003
;
Kay 2010
;
Yung 2006
;
Caneva et al. 2009
;
Murphy et al. 2010
;
Ashhab et al. 2012
is closely related to our setup, since one needs to transfer a quantum state from one site to another distant site.
Our speed limits may give a new useful bound on the transfer speed, which we leave for future research.
