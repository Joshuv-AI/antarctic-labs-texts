Impact of Isolation and Fiducial Cuts on q T q_{T} and N-Jettiness Subtractions
and Frank J. Tackmann

Abstract
Kinematic selection cuts and isolation requirements are a necessity in experimental measurements for identifying prompt leptons and photons that originate from the hard-interaction process of interest. We analyze how such cuts affect the application of the q T q_{T} and N N -jettiness subtraction methods for fixed-order calculations. We consider both fixed-cone and smooth-cone isolation methods. We find that kinematic selection and isolation cuts both induce parametrically enhanced power corrections with considerably slower convergence compared to the standard power corrections that are already present in inclusive cross sections without additional cuts. Using analytic arguments at next-to-leading order we derive their general scaling behavior as a function of the subtraction cutoff. We also study their numerical impact for the case of gluon-fusion Higgs production in the H → γ ​ γ H\to\gamma\gamma decay mode and for p ​ p → γ ​ γ pp\to\gamma\gamma direct diphoton production. We find that the relative enhancement of the additional cut-induced power corrections tends to be more severe for q T q_{T} , where it can reach an order of magnitude or more, depending on the choice of parameters and subtraction cutoffs. We discuss how all such cuts can be incorporated without causing additional power corrections by implementing the subtractions differentially rather than through a global slicing method. We also highlight the close relation of this formulation of the subtractions to the projection-to-Born method.

This resummation is based on the same factorization theorems underlying the subtraction methods,

In addition to the resummed singular cross section, one has to include the power corrections
in order to recover the full fixed-order result for the spectrum.
Thus, understanding the effect of selection and isolation cuts on the factorization

So far, studies of power corrections have only considered
inclusive processes, while the effect
of selection and isolation cuts have not yet been considered.
As we will see, these cuts are an additional source of power corrections.
Given their necessity for experimental measurements, it is important to study
the cut-induced power corrections, and in particular determine if and when they lead
to the dominant corrections or if they can even lead to a breakdown of the
factorization and thus the subtraction methods.

In this paper, we study the effect of kinematic selection and isolation cuts
on

{\mathcal{T}}_{0}

either in the direct process

We will therefore primarily talk about photons, but we stress that our
results and conclusions apply equally to leptons.
Using a simplified calculation at NLO, we determine the scaling of power corrections
induced by the cuts. In particular, we discuss the dependence on the isolation method
and parameters, considering both fixed-cone and smooth-cone isolations.
We will find that the cuts induce power corrections that are parametrically enhanced,
and which can thus be significantly larger than for the case without cuts.
This enhancement is particularly severe for the case of
q_{T}
subtractions with
smooth-cone isolation.
This has important ramifications for the numerical stability of the subtractions
in practical applications.
In fact, in refs.
[
77

78

it was already observed numerically that processes involving photon isolation
suffer from large enhanced power corrections, which is explained by our results.

Given the potentially significant size of the cut-induced power corrections,
it is essential to account for them. Since in general they are complicated and cut specific,
including them by an explicit analytic calculation (e.g. along the lines
of the inclusive ones discussed above) would be challenging and tedious.
Differential subtractions
[
39

offer a way to avoid
the power corrections because they do not require the finite cutoff
that is necessary in the slicing approach.
Exploiting this, we propose a strategy to incorporate the measurement cuts exactly
such that the additional cut-induced power corrections are avoided.
It uses the Born-like measurement that appears in the singular
subtractions to separate the cut-induced power corrections from the
inclusive, cut-independent ones, where the former can be kept exactly while the
latter can be treated in the standard way.
We also show that in this way the projection-to-Born method
[
31

naturally appears as the special case where the inclusive, cut-independent power corrections
are fully known.

and its dimensionless version as
\tau

For the case of color-singlet production (

the total transverse momentum of the color-singlet final state,

{\mathcal{T}}\equiv q_{T}^{2}
, which yields

subtractions
[

]

For
0

0
-jettiness
(aka beam thrust)
{\mathcal{T}}\equiv{\mathcal{T}}_{0}
.
In terms of the hadronic final-state momenta

, these are defined as
1

1

For
0
-jettiness or beam thrust, one can define more generic
measures
[

80

{\mathcal{T}}_{0}^{\rm lep}

\displaystyle{\mathcal{T}}

\displaystyle\equiv q_{T}^{2}=\Bigl(\sum_{i}\vec{k}_{T,i}\Bigr)^{2}\,,\qquad

\displaystyle\tau\equiv q_{T}^{2}/Q^{2}\,,

(2.1)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{T}}

\displaystyle\equiv{\mathcal{T}}_{0}^{\mathrm{lep}}=\sum_{i}{\rm min}\bigl\{k_{i}^{+}e^{Y}\,,\,k_{i}^{-}e^{-Y}\bigr\}\,,\qquad

\displaystyle\tau\equiv{\mathcal{T}}_{0}^{\rm lep}/Q\,.

(2.2)

Here, the sums over real emissions

A key property of

is that it is an IR-safe

-jet resolution variable,
i.e. it vanishes for the Born process and in the IR-singular limit where all real

become soft or collinear.
We can thus write the cross section
\sigma(X)

as an integral over the cross section differential in
\tau

\sigma(X)=\int\!\mathrm{d}\tau\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma(X)}{\mathrm{d}\tau}=\sigma(X,\tau_{\mathrm{cut}})+\int_{\tau_{\mathrm{cut}}}\!\mathrm{d}\tau\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma(X)}{\mathrm{d}\tau}\,,

(2.3)

where the cumulative cross section as a function of
\tau_{\mathrm{cut}}
is defined as

\sigma(X,\tau_{\mathrm{cut}})=\int^{\tau_{\mathrm{cut}}}\!\mathrm{d}\tau\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma(X)}{\mathrm{d}\tau}\,.

(2.4)

Here,
X
denotes all measurements. It includes the measurements performed on
the Born process, including any selection cuts on its constituents.
It also contains any additional cuts on the hadronic final state such as isolation cuts.

The slicing method is obtained by adding and subtracting a
global subtraction term
\sigma^{\mathrm{sub}}(X,\tau_{\mathrm{cut}})
,

\displaystyle\sigma(X)

\displaystyle=\sigma^{\mathrm{sub}}(X,\tau_{\mathrm{cut}})+\int_{\tau_{\mathrm{cut}}}\!\mathrm{d}\tau\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma(X)}{\mathrm{d}\tau}+\Delta\sigma(X,\tau_{\mathrm{cut}})\,,

\displaystyle\Delta\sigma(X,\tau_{\mathrm{cut}})

\displaystyle=\sigma(X,\tau_{\mathrm{cut}})-\sigma^{\mathrm{sub}}(X,\tau_{\mathrm{cut}})\,.

(2.5)

Since
\tau
vanishes by construction in the Born limit, the integral in eq. (
2.1
)
necessarily involves at least one resolved real emission, and hence
\mathrm{d}\sigma(X)/\mathrm{d}\tau

can be calculated from the corresponding Born+1-parton calculation at one lower order.
The key requirement on
\sigma^{\mathrm{sub}}(X,\tau_{\mathrm{cut}})
is that it must contain the leading
terms in the
\tau_{\mathrm{cut}}\to 0
limit. If that is the case,
then
\Delta\sigma(X,\tau_{\mathrm{cut}})
is a power correction of
\mathcal{O}(\tau_{\mathrm{cut}})
which vanishes as

\tau_{\mathrm{cut}}\to 0
and hence it can be neglected for sufficiently small
\tau_{\mathrm{cut}}
.

To construct
\sigma^{\mathrm{sub}}
and study the size of
\Delta\sigma
, it is useful
to expand the differential cross section and its cumulant for
\tau\ll 1

and correspondingly
\tau_{\mathrm{cut}}\ll 1

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma(X)}{\mathrm{d}\tau}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma^{(0)}(X)}{\mathrm{d}\tau}

\displaystyle+\sum_{m>0}\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma^{(2m)}(X)}{\mathrm{d}\tau}\,,

(2.6)

\displaystyle\sigma(X,\tau_{\mathrm{cut}})

\displaystyle=\sigma^{(0)}(X,\tau_{\mathrm{cut}})

\displaystyle+\sum_{m>0}\sigma^{(2m)}(X,\tau_{\mathrm{cut}})\,,

where the different contributions scale as

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma^{(0)}(X)}{\mathrm{d}\tau}

\displaystyle\sim\delta(\tau)+\sum_{j\geq 0}\biggl[\frac{\ln^{j}\tau}{\tau}\biggr]_{+}\,,\qquad\sigma^{(0)}(X,\tau_{\mathrm{cut}})

\displaystyle\sim 1+\sum_{j\geq 0}\ln^{1+j}\tau_{\mathrm{cut}}\,,

\displaystyle\tau\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma^{(2m)}(X)}{\mathrm{d}\tau}

\displaystyle\sim\sum_{j\geq 0}\tau^{m}\ln^{j}\tau\,,\qquad\qquad\quad\sigma^{(2m)}(X,\tau_{\mathrm{cut}})

\displaystyle\sim\sum_{j\geq 0}\tau_{\mathrm{cut}}^{m}\ln^{j}\tau_{\mathrm{cut}}\,.

(2.7)

The
\mathrm{d}\sigma^{(0)}/\mathrm{d}\tau
and
\sigma^{(0)}(\tau_{\mathrm{cut}})

are the leading-power (LP) or singular terms, as they diverge as

for
\tau\to 0

In particular, they fully capture the cancellation of virtual and real IR divergences,
which is encoded in the
\delta
and plus distributions.
The
\mathrm{d}\sigma^{(2m)}/\mathrm{d}\tau

contain at most integrable divergences for
\tau\to 0
,
and correspondingly
\sigma^{(2m)}(\tau_{\mathrm{cut}}\to 0)\to 0
.
They are thus referred to as nonsingular or power-suppressed corrections.

For eq. (
2.1
) to provide a viable subtraction,
\sigma^{\mathrm{sub}}(X,\tau_{\mathrm{cut}})

must at least contain the singular terms, i.e., we require

\displaystyle\sigma^{\mathrm{sub}}(X,\tau_{\mathrm{cut}})

\displaystyle=\sigma^{(0)}(X,\tau_{\mathrm{cut}})\bigl[1+{\mathcal{O}}(\tau_{\mathrm{cut}})\bigr]\,.

(2.8)

The correction term in eq. (
2.1
) then scales as a power correction

\displaystyle\Delta\sigma(X,\tau_{\mathrm{cut}})

\displaystyle=\sigma(\tau_{\mathrm{cut}})-\sigma^{\mathrm{sub}}(X,\tau_{\mathrm{cut}})={\mathcal{O}}\bigl(\tau^{m}_{\mathrm{cut}}\bigr)\,,

(2.9)

where
m
is determined by the first term in the sum in eq. (
2.6
)
that is not contained in
\sigma^{\mathrm{sub}}
.

For inclusive Higgs and Drell-Yan production, the sum in eq. (
2.6
)
starts with

for both
q_{T}

[
74
]

and
{\mathcal{T}}_{0}

[
39

70

71
,
72
]
.
In these cases, the full
{\mathcal{O}}(\tau_{\mathrm{cut}}^{1})
correction is known at NLO

and can be included

\sigma^{\mathrm{sub}}
such that
\Delta\sigma\sim{\mathcal{O}}(\tau_{\mathrm{cut}}^{2})

2.2
Review of photon isolation

Photon production at hadron colliders such as the LHC is dominated by
secondary
photons
arising from the decay of hadrons inside final-state jets, in particular
\pi^{0},\eta\to{\gamma\gamma}
,
whereas one is interested in
prompt
photons directly produced in hard interactions.
Experimentally, secondary photons can be efficiently suppressed using the shape
of the electromagnetic showers in the calorimeter, see e.g. ref.
[
81
]
.
This is supplemented by an additional cone isolation which restricts the transverse energy inside a fixed cone of radius
R
around the photon,

\displaystyle\sum_{i:\,d(i,\gamma)\leq R}E_{T}^{i}\leq E_{T}^{\rm iso}\,.

(2.10)

Here, the sum runs over all identified hadrons
i
with momenta
k_{i}
,
E_{T}^{i}\equiv E_{T}(k_{i})
is their transverse energy, and the distance measure between two particles
i
and
j

is as usual given in terms of their difference in azimuth and pseudorapidity,

\displaystyle d(i,j)=\sqrt{(\phi_{i}-\phi_{j})^{2}+(\eta_{i}-\eta_{j})^{2}}\,.

(2.11)

The isolation energy
E_{T}^{\rm iso}
is typically chosen as either a fixed value
or relative to the photon transverse energy,
E_{T}^{\rm iso}=\epsilon\,p_{T\gamma}
.

Theory predictions employing this fixed-cone isolation require the use
of photon fragmentation functions
D_{q}
to cancel collinear singularities
arising from collinear quark splittings
q\to q+\gamma
.
This is analogous to the absorption of collinear singularities
from initial-state splittings into parton distribution functions.
The fragmentation functions are nonperturbative objects and have been determined from data

[
82
,
83
,
84
,
85
]
.
After their inclusion, quark fragmentation factorizes into a nonperturbative
and perturbative piece, allowing for an infrared-safe calculation
[
86
,
87
]
.

Currently, the fragmentation functions
D_{q}
are only poorly constrained from data,
yielding large theory uncertainties.
Furthermore, for tight isolation cuts with small
{R\ll 1}
one encounters
large logarithms
\ln(R)
which can render the perturbative calculation unstable
[
87
]
.
Their resummation has been addressed e.g. in refs.
[
88
,
89
]
.

To avoid the added complications of nonperturbative fragmentation functions,
perturbative calculations often employ
the smooth-cone isolation proposed by Frixione
[
90
]
,
as used e.g. in the NNLO calculations of direct diphoton production
in refs.
[
91
,
92
,
93
]
.
2
2

2

One can also employ a hybrid approach by combining smooth-cone isolation with radius
R_{0}

with a fixed-cone isolation of larger radius
R\ll R_{0}
, as used e.g. in the NNLO calculation
of direct photon production in ref.
[
94
]
.

Frixione isolation modifies eq. (
2.10
) to

\displaystyle\sum_{i:\,d(i,\gamma)\leq r}E_{T}^{i}\leq E_{T}^{\rm iso}\,\chi(r)\qquad\forall r\leq R\,,

(2.12)

where
\chi(r)
is a function that vanishes as
\chi(r\to 0)\to 0
,
and
E_{T}^{\rm iso}
can again be chosen as a fixed value or relative to the photon momentum,

E_{T}^{\rm iso}=\epsilon\,p_{T\gamma}
.
This isolation constraint becomes stronger the closer the hadrons are to the photon.
In particular, it fully suppresses radiation exactly collinear to the photon,
and hence removes the collinear singularities from
q\to q+\gamma
splittings.
On the other hand, soft radiation with
E_{T}\to 0
is not vetoed,
which is crucial to not spoil the cancellation of soft divergences.
Thus, calculations employing Frixione isolation are infrared safe without the inclusion of fragmentation functions.
Due to finite detector resolution, this isolation cannot be implemented experimentally,
but it has been shown to yield results compatible (within theory uncertainties) to
fixed-cone isolation for sufficiently tight isolations
[
95
,
96
,
93
]
.

A common choice of
\chi(r)
is given by

\displaystyle\chi(r)=\biggl[\frac{1-\cos(r)}{1-\cos(R)}\biggr]^{n}\,,

(2.13)

with the parameter
n>0
, and we will use this implementation for our numerical results in
section
4
.
For the analytic study in
section
3
, we will instead use

\displaystyle\chi(r)=\Bigl(\frac{r}{R}\Bigr)^{2n}\,,

(2.14)

which is a good approximation of eq. (
2.13
) for
r,R\ll 1
.

For illustration purpose, we will also consider a harsh isolation criterion,
where one completely vetoes any radiation inside the isolation cone,
implemented by restricting the
total hadronic transverse energy in the isolation cones to vanish,

\displaystyle\sum_{i:\,d(i,\gamma)\leq R}E_{T}^{i}=0\,.

(2.15)

While this criterion is of course infrared unsafe, as even soft radiation is vetoed,
it will be useful to illustrate how factorization-violating effects can potentially arise.

Finally we note that recently a new isolation technique based
on jet substructure techniques was proposed in ref.
[
97
]
.
Here, one uses soft drop to identify “photon jets” that do not contain notable substructure
and defines these as isolated photons.
In the case of a single emission with momentum
k
and distance
r<R
from the photon,
this technique amounts to requiring that

\displaystyle k_{T}<p_{T\gamma}\frac{z_{\rm cut}(r/R)^{\beta}}{1-z_{\rm cut}(r/R)^{\beta}}\,,

(2.16)

where
R
is size of the isolation cone, and
z_{\rm cut}<1/2
and
\beta
are soft-drop parameters.
As discussed in ref.
[
97
]
, eq. (
2.16
) is equivalent
to the Frixione isolation in eqs. (
2.12
) and (
2.14
)
in the limit of small
z_{\rm cut}
or
r/R
if one identifies

E_{T}^{\rm iso}=z_{\rm cut}\,p_{T\gamma}
and
\beta=2n
.
Hence we will not discuss this technique separately.

\displaystyle=\frac{f_{a}(x_{a})f_{b}(x_{b})}{2x_{a}x_{b}E_{\mathrm{cm}}^{4}}A^{\mathrm{LO}}(Q,Y;X)\,\delta\bigl({\mathcal{T}}\bigr)\,

\displaystyle A^{\mathrm{LO}}(Q,Y;X)

\displaystyle=\int\!\mathrm{d}\Phi_{L}(p_{a}+p_{b})\,\bigl\lvert{\mathcal{M}}^{\mathrm{LO}}_{ab\to L}(p_{a},p_{b};\{p_{i}\})\bigr\rvert^{2}f_{X}(\{p_{i}\})\,,

(3.3)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\Phi_{L}(q)

\displaystyle=\biggl[\prod_{i}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{4}p_{i}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,\delta_{+}(p_{i}^{2}-m_{i}^{2})\biggr]\,(2\pi)^{4}\delta^{(4)}\Bigl(q-\sum_{i}p_{i}\Bigr)\,.

(3.4)

In eq. (
3.3
),
f_{X}(\{p_{i}\})
implements the cuts on the final state momenta
\{p_{i}\}
,
which are kept implicit in the phase-space integral
\mathrm{d}\Phi_{L}(q)
.
In eq. (
3.4
),
\delta_{+}(p^{2}-m^{2})=\theta(p^{0})\delta(p^{2}-m^{2})
are on-shell
\delta
functions.
Finally, the incoming momenta of the Born process are given by

\displaystyle p_{a}^{\mu}

\displaystyle=x_{a}E_{\mathrm{cm}}\frac{n^{\mu}}{2}=Qe^{+Y}\frac{n^{\mu}}{2}\,,\qquad p_{b}^{\mu}=x_{b}E_{\mathrm{cm}}\frac{{\bar{n}}^{\mu}}{2}=Qe^{-Y}\frac{{\bar{n}}^{\mu}}{2}\,,

(3.5)

where as before
n^{\mu}=(1,0,0,1)
and
{\bar{n}}^{\mu}=(1,0,0,-1)

are lightlike reference vectors along the beam directions.

Next, we consider the correction to eq. (
3.1
) from a single real emission,

\displaystyle a^{\prime}(p^{\prime}_{a})+b^{\prime}(p^{\prime}_{b})\to L(\{p_{i}^{\prime}\})+k(k)\,,

(3.6)

where
k^{\mu}
is the momentum of the emitted parton.
The resulting cross section is given by

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\rm real}(X)}{\mathrm{d}Q^{2}\mathrm{d}Y\mathrm{d}{\mathcal{T}}}

\displaystyle=\int\!\frac{\mathrm{d}^{d}k}{(2\pi)^{d}}(2\pi)\delta_{+}(k^{2})\,\frac{f_{a^{\prime}}(\zeta_{a})f_{b^{\prime}}(\zeta_{b})}{2\zeta_{a}\zeta_{b}E_{\mathrm{cm}}^{4}}\delta\bigl[{\mathcal{T}}-\hat{\mathcal{T}}(k)\bigr]

(3.7)

\displaystyle\quad\times\int\mathrm{d}\Phi_{L}(p^{\prime}_{a}+p^{\prime}_{b}-k)\,\bigl\lvert{\mathcal{M}}(p^{\prime}_{a},p^{\prime}_{b};k,\{p_{i}^{\prime}\})\bigr\rvert^{2}\,f_{X}(k,\{p_{i}^{\prime}\})\,.

Here,
{\mathcal{M}}
is the matrix element for the process in eq. (
3.6
),
including the relevant strong coupling constant
\alpha_{s}
and renormalization scale
\mu^{d-4}
,
and
\hat{\mathcal{T}}(k)
is the measurement operator that determines the value of
{\mathcal{T}}
as a function of
k
.
The measurement function
f_{X}
now acts on both
k
and
\{p_{i}^{\prime}\}
.
The incoming momenta are now fully determined in terms of
k
and the measurements
of
Q
and
Y
as

\displaystyle{p^{\prime}_{a}}^{\mu}

\displaystyle=\zeta_{a}E_{\mathrm{cm}}\frac{n^{\mu}}{2}=\Bigl(k^{-}+e^{+Y}\sqrt{Q^{2}+k_{T}^{2}}\Bigr)\frac{n^{\mu}}{2}\,,

\displaystyle{p^{\prime}_{b}}^{\mu}

\displaystyle=\zeta_{b}E_{\mathrm{cm}}\frac{{\bar{n}}^{\mu}}{2}=\Bigl(k^{+}+e^{-Y}\sqrt{Q^{2}+k_{T}^{2}}\Bigr)\frac{{\bar{n}}^{\mu}}{2}\,.

(3.8)

The restriction that
\zeta_{a,b}\in[0,1]
is kept implicit in the support of the PDFs.

Resolution variables
{\mathcal{T}}
sensitive to soft emission,
k^{\mu}\to 0
,
and collinear emissions,
n\cdot k\to 0
or
{\bar{n}}\cdot k\to 0
, become singular in these limits.
Following the strategy of refs.
[
74
,
75
]
, we can use
the SCET power expansion to organize the expansion of the cross section in the
{\mathcal{T}}\to 0
limit
by considering the relevant collinear and soft scalings of
k^{\mu}
.
Resolution variables insensitive to the transverse momentum
k_{T}
are described by SCET
I
,
where the appropriate modes are

\displaystyle\text{$n$-collinear}:\quad

\displaystyle k_{n}\sim Q\,(\lambda^{2},1,\lambda)

\displaystyle\quad\Rightarrow\quad n\cdot k\ll k_{T}\ll{\bar{n}}\cdot k\,,

(3.9)

\displaystyle\text{${\bar{n}}$-collinear}:\quad

\displaystyle k_{\bar{n}}\sim Q\,(1,\lambda^{2},\lambda)

\displaystyle\quad\Rightarrow\quad{\bar{n}}\cdot k\ll k_{T}\ll n\cdot k\,,

\displaystyle\text{ultrasoft}:\quad

\displaystyle k_{us}\sim Q\,(\lambda^{2},\lambda^{2},\lambda^{2})

\displaystyle\quad\Rightarrow\quad n\cdot k\,\sim k_{T}\,\sim{\bar{n}}\cdot k\,.

Here, we use the lightcone notation
k^{\mu}=(k^{+},k^{-},k_{T})=(n\cdot k,{\bar{n}}\cdot k,k_{T})
,
and
\lambda
is a power-counting parameter.
For example, for
0
-jettiness
{\mathcal{T}}_{0}
we have
\lambda\sim\sqrt{{\mathcal{T}}_{0}/Q}
.

Resolution variables resolving the transverse momentum
k_{T}
fall into the realm of SCET
II

and are characterized by the following modes,

\displaystyle\text{$n$-collinear}:\quad

\displaystyle k_{n}\sim Q\,(\lambda^{2},1,\lambda)

\displaystyle\quad\Rightarrow\quad n\cdot k\ll k_{T}\ll{\bar{n}}\cdot k\,,

(3.10)

\displaystyle\text{${\bar{n}}$-collinear}:\quad

\displaystyle k_{\bar{n}}\sim Q\,(1,\lambda^{2},\lambda)

\displaystyle\quad\Rightarrow\quad{\bar{n}}\cdot k\ll k_{T}\ll n\cdot k\,,

\displaystyle\text{soft}:\quad

\displaystyle k_{s}\sim Q\,(\lambda,\lambda,\lambda)

\displaystyle\quad\Rightarrow\quad n\cdot k\,\sim k_{T}\,\sim{\bar{n}}\cdot k\,.

For example, for
q_{T}
we have
\lambda\sim q_{T}/Q
.
Eqs. (
3.9
) and (
3.10
) only differ by the scaling of soft and ultrasoft modes,
which will not change the analytic calculations here, only the resulting scaling
of power corrections in
\lambda
. In contrast, it does have a significant impact on the singular limit
of the matrix element itself, and for SCET
II
it requires the use of rapidity regulators,
see refs.
[
74
,
75
]
for more details.

Inserting the appropriate scalings of eq. (
3.9
) or eq. (
3.10
) into eq. (
3.7
),
we can systematically expand the cross section in
\lambda
,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma(X)}{\mathrm{d}Q^{2}\mathrm{d}Y\mathrm{d}{\mathcal{T}}}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma^{(0)}(X)}{\mathrm{d}Q^{2}\mathrm{d}Y\mathrm{d}{\mathcal{T}}}}_{\sim\lambda^{-2}}+\sum_{m>0}\underbrace{\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma^{(2m)}(X)}{\mathrm{d}Q^{2}\mathrm{d}Y\mathrm{d}{\mathcal{T}}}}_{\sim\lambda^{2m-2}}\,.

(3.11)

As briefly reviewed in
section
2.1
,
\sigma^{(0)}
is referred to
as leading-power or singular limit and contains the cancellation of all IR divergences.

It is easy to see from eqs. (
3.7
) and (
3.1
) that the total momentum of
L

reduces to its Born value at leading power, i.e.

\displaystyle q=p^{\prime}_{a}+p^{\prime}_{b}-k=\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{Q^{2}+k_{T}^{2}}\cosh(Y)\\
{\vec{k}}_{T}\\
\sqrt{Q^{2}+k_{T}^{2}}\sinh(Y)\end{pmatrix}=p_{a}+p_{b}+{\mathcal{O}}(k_{T})\,.

(3.12)

Hence at leading power, the phase space
\mathrm{d}\Phi_{L}
in eq. (
3.7
)
reduces to the Born phase space. Note also that the light-cone
coordinates
q^{\pm}
only receive relative corrections of

\mathcal{O}(k_{T}^{2}/Q^{2})\sim\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{2})
.

For the cuts
X
to be infrared safe they must be insensitive to collinear splittings or
soft emissions, and hence reduce to their Born result at leading power. For the measurement
function
f_{X}
in eq. (
3.7
), this implies

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\Phi_{L}(p_{a}^{\prime}+p_{b}^{\prime}-k)\,f_{X}(k,\{p_{i}^{\prime}\})=\mathrm{d}\Phi_{L}(p_{a}+p_{b})\,f_{X}(\{p_{i}\})\times\bigl[1+{\mathcal{O}}(\lambda^{m})\bigr]\,.

(3.13)

Here, on the right hand side the total momentum
q
is replaced by its
Born value,
q\to p_{a}+p_{b}
, and the individual momenta
\{p_{i}^{\prime}\}
are
correspondingly evaluated in the Born limit
\{p_{i}^{\prime}\}\to\{p_{i}\}
.
Eqs. (
3.12
) and (
3.13
) are key ingredients in the derivation of the factorization theorem
that predicts the leading singular terms
\sigma^{(0)}
.
In particular, they imply that to all orders in
\alpha_{s}
, the singular cross section
is only sensitive to the Born kinematics of the final state
L
.
The corrections beyond the Born approximation crucially depend on the
precise definition of
X
, but are always suppressed by
{\mathcal{O}}(\lambda^{m}
),
where
m>0
encodes the fact that
X
is infrared safe.
For
m=0
,
X
would modify the leading singular behavior in
{\mathcal{T}}
and hence
break the factorization for
{\mathcal{T}}
and lead to a divergent result for the cross section.

The
\sigma^{(2m)}
with
m>0
in eq. (
3.11
) denote power corrections to
the singular cross section
\sigma^{(0)}
.
They can be systematically computed by expanding all ingredients in eq. (
3.7
)
to higher order in
\lambda
. The expansion of PDFs and matrix elements
in this approach has already been carried out for Higgs and Drell-Yan production
in refs.
[
74
,
75
]
, which found that these corrections scale as

\lambda^{0}
, i.e. the sum in eq. (
3.11
) starts indeed with
m=1
as expected
on general grounds.

Here, we extend these works by calculating the power corrections in eq. (
3.13
)
arising from the color-singlet phase space and additional measurement cuts.
They can be calculated by considering the cross section

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma^{(\mathrm{cuts})}(X)}{\mathrm{d}Q^{2}\mathrm{d}Y\mathrm{d}{\mathcal{T}}}

\displaystyle=\int\!\frac{\mathrm{d}^{4}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,\delta_{+}(k^{2})\,\frac{f_{a^{\prime}}(\zeta_{a})f_{b^{\prime}}(\zeta_{b})}{2\zeta_{a}\zeta_{b}E_{\mathrm{cm}}^{4}}\delta\bigl[{\mathcal{T}}-\hat{\mathcal{T}}(k)\bigr]

\displaystyle\quad\times\int\Bigl[\mathrm{d}\Phi_{L}(p^{\prime}_{a}+p^{\prime}_{b}-k)\,f_{X}(k,\{p_{i}^{\prime}\})-\mathrm{d}\Phi_{L}(p_{a}+p_{b})\,f_{X}(\{p_{i}\})\Bigr]

\displaystyle\quad\qquad\times\bigl\lvert{\mathcal{M}}(p^{\prime}_{a},p^{\prime}_{b};k,\{p_{i}^{\prime}\})\bigr\rvert^{2}\,,

(3.14)

and expanding it in the power-counting parameter
\lambda
.
The difference in square brackets is the
difference between the exact and LP limit on the left and right-hand sides of eq. (
3.13
).
Since it vanishes for
k\to 0
, the
k
integral is IR finite and can be evaluated
in
d=4
dimensions.

Since eq. (
3.1
) contains the process-dependent matrix elements,
it is not possible to give a general result for the cut-induced power corrections.
To obtain a generic analytic understanding of their size,
in the following we assume that the squared matrix element only depends
on the total momentum
q^{\mu}
of
L
but not the individual momenta
\{p_{i}^{\prime}\}
, i.e.,
we assume that

\bigl\lvert{\mathcal{M}}(p^{\prime}_{a},p^{\prime}_{b};k,\{p_{i}^{\prime}\})\bigr\rvert^{2}\equiv\bigl\lvert{\mathcal{M}}(p^{\prime}_{a},p^{\prime}_{b};k,q\bigr\rvert^{2}\,.

(3.15)

This holds for Higgs production,
where due to the isotropic decay all details of the decay are encapsulated in the branching ratio.
While this is a crude approximation for more complicated processes such as direct photon production,
it is completely sufficient to obtain a qualitative understanding of the cut effects,
since their power suppression is determined by the term in square brackets in eq. (
3.1
).

Using eq. (
3.15
) allows us to pull out the matrix element, so eq. (
3.1
) becomes

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma^{(\mathrm{cuts})}(X)}{\mathrm{d}Q^{2}\mathrm{d}Y\mathrm{d}{\mathcal{T}}}

\displaystyle=\int\!\frac{\mathrm{d}^{4}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\delta_{+}(k^{2})\frac{f_{a^{\prime}}(\zeta_{a})f_{b^{\prime}}(\zeta_{b})}{2\zeta_{a}\zeta_{b}E_{\mathrm{cm}}^{4}}\bigl\lvert{\mathcal{M}}(p^{\prime}_{a},p^{\prime}_{b};k,q)\bigr\rvert^{2}\delta\bigl[{\mathcal{T}}-\hat{\mathcal{T}}(k)\bigr]\times\Delta\Phi_{X}(Q,Y,k)

\displaystyle\Delta\Phi_{X}(Q,Y,k)

\displaystyle=\int\Bigl[\mathrm{d}\Phi_{L}(p_{a}^{\prime}+p_{b}^{\prime}-k)\,f_{X}\bigl(k,\{p_{i}^{\prime}\}\bigr)-\mathrm{d}\Phi_{L}(p_{a}+p_{b})\,f_{X}\bigl(\{p_{i}\}\bigr)\Bigr]\,,

(3.16)

where
\Delta\Phi_{X}(Q,Y,k)
fully contains the effect of the recoil due to the emission
k

on
\mathrm{d}\Phi_{L}
as well as the cuts
X
. Recall that
p_{a,b}^{\prime}
and
p_{a,b}
are
determined in terms of
Q
,
Y
, and
k
.

Using eq. (
3.1
), it is straightforward to deduce the scaling
of the cut-induced power corrections by expanding
\Delta\Phi_{X}
to
the first nonvanishing order in
\lambda
, while keeping the remaining
terms in eq. (
3.1
) in the singular limit.
If
\Delta\Phi_{X}
scales as
{\mathcal{O}}(\lambda^{2m})
, then the resulting
power correction scales as
\mathrm{d}\sigma^{(\mathrm{cuts})}(X)/\mathrm{d}{\mathcal{T}}\sim\lambda^{-2+2m}
.
More explicitly, for the two cases we are interested in we have

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma^{(\mathrm{cuts})}(X)}{\mathrm{d}Q^{2}\mathrm{d}Y\mathrm{d}q_{T}^{2}}\sim\frac{1}{q_{T}^{2}}\biggl(\frac{q_{T}^{2}}{Q^{2}}\biggr)^{m}\,,\qquad\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma^{(\mathrm{cuts})}(X)}{\mathrm{d}Q^{2}\mathrm{d}Y\mathrm{d}{\mathcal{T}}_{0}}\sim\frac{1}{{\mathcal{T}}_{0}}\biggl(\frac{{\mathcal{T}}_{0}}{Q}\biggr)^{m}\,.

(3.17)

This should be compared to the normal power corrections that arise from
expanding the matrix elements, etc. for which
m=1
.
If the kinematic cuts or isolation requirements yield a larger value,
m>1
,
then their effects are parametrically suppressed compared to the normal power corrections,
while for
m<1
they are parametrically enhanced, and for
m=0
they would violate the factorization,
as explained above.
In the remainder of this section, we will determine
m

for kinematic selection cuts and various photon isolation techniques.

3.2
Kinematic selection cuts

We begin by discussing the power corrections induced by kinematic selection cuts.
As an illustrative example, we consider a color-singlet final state
L
composed of two massless
particles with momenta
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
, and impose a minimum transverse momentum cut on both particles,

p_{T1}\,,p_{T2}\geq p_{T}^{\rm min}\,.

(3.18)

This is the most common selection cut, which is practically always applied.
In addition, in practice one also requires cuts on the rapidities
y_{1,2}
,
which we neglect here for simplicity as they do not lead to qualitatively new features.

We write the total momentum
q
and the individual momenta
p_{1,2}
as

\displaystyle q^{\mu}

\displaystyle=\Bigl(\sqrt{Q^{2}+q_{T}^{2}}\cosh(Y)\,,\,q_{T},\,0\,,\sqrt{Q^{2}+q_{T}^{2}}\sinh(Y)\Bigr)\,,

\displaystyle p_{1}^{\mu}

\displaystyle=p_{T}\bigl(\cosh(Y+\Delta y)\,,\,\cos\varphi\,,\,\sin\varphi\,,\,\sinh(Y+\Delta y)\bigr)\,,

\displaystyle p_{2}^{\mu}

\displaystyle=q^{\mu}-p_{1}^{\mu}\,.

(3.19)

Here,
q^{\mu}
is parameterized in terms of its invariant mass
Q
, rapidity
Y
,
and transverse momentum
q_{T}
, and using overall azimuthal symmetry
we choose to align the transverse momentum with the
x
axis.
The massless momentum
p_{1}^{\mu}
is expressed in terms of the angle
\varphi

between its transverse momentum
{\vec{p}}_{T}
and
{\vec{q}}_{T}
and the rapidity difference
\Delta y=y_{1}-Y
,
where
y_{1}
is the rapidity of
p_{1}^{\mu}
.
Note that using this parameterization, for
q_{T}=0
one has
y_{1,2}=Y\pm\Delta y
.
For simplicity of notation, we now identify
p_{T}\equiv p_{T1}
, while
p_{T2}
is defined implicitly through eq. (
3.2
).
Momentum conservation yields the relation

\displaystyle p_{T}=\frac{Q^{2}/2}{\sqrt{Q^{2}+q_{T}^{2}}\cosh(\Delta y)-q_{T}\cos\varphi}\,.

(3.20)

In terms of the above variables, the two-particle phase space in eq. (
3.4
) is given by

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\Phi_{L}(q)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{d}^{4}p_{1}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\delta_{+}(p_{1}^{2})\,\frac{\mathrm{d}^{4}p_{2}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\delta_{+}(p_{2}^{2})\,(2\pi)^{4}\delta^{(4)}(p_{1}+p_{2}-q)=\frac{p_{T}^{2}}{8\pi^{2}Q^{2}}\mathrm{d}\varphi\,\mathrm{d}\Delta y\,.

(3.21)

Integrating this differential phase space in the presence of the cut in eq. (
3.18
) yields

\displaystyle\Phi_{L}(q,p_{T}^{\rm min})

\displaystyle=\int\mathrm{d}\Phi_{L}(q)\,\theta\bigl(p_{T1}-p_{T}^{\rm min}\bigr)\theta\bigl(p_{T2}-p_{T}^{\rm min}\bigr)

(3.22)

\displaystyle=4\int_{0}^{\pi}\mathrm{d}\varphi\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\Delta y\,\frac{p_{T}^{2}}{8\pi^{2}Q^{2}}\theta\bigl[{\rm min}\bigl\{p_{T}^{2},p_{T}^{2}-2p_{T}q_{T}\cos\varphi+q_{T}^{2}\bigr\}-(p_{T}^{\rm min})^{2}\bigr]\,.

In the second line, we combined the two cuts into one
\theta
function,
and employed symmetry of the integrand.
Note that this integral is independent of the total rapidity
Y
.

Eq. (
3.22
) depends on the total transverse momentum
q_{T}
only
through the combinations
q_{T}^{2}
and
q_{T}\cos\varphi
.
Naively, one may thus expect that in the expansion of
\Phi_{L}(q,p_{T}^{\rm min})
,
all odd powers of
q_{T}
vanish due to the integral over the azimuthal angle
\varphi
,
which would imply that the first power correction arises at
{\mathcal{O}}(q_{T}^{2})
.
However, the minimum in eq. (
3.22
) explicitly breaks the azimuthal symmetry.
Concretely, in the limit
q_{T}\ll Q
, we have

\displaystyle{\rm min}\bigl\{p_{T}^{2},p_{T}^{2}-2p_{T}q_{T}\cos\varphi+q_{T}^{2}\bigr\}=\begin{cases}p_{T}^{2}\,,\hskip 89.626pt\cos\varphi<0\\
p_{T}^{2}-2p_{T}q_{T}\cos\varphi\,,\qquad\cos\varphi\geq 0\\
\end{cases}\,,

(3.23)

up to corrections of
\mathcal{O}(q_{T}^{2}/p_{T}^{2})
, and it is clear that this result breaks the azimuthal symmetry, such that the
\varphi
integral does not vanish.

Expanding eq. (
3.22
) correspondingly in
q_{T}\ll p_{T}\sim Q
, we obtain the result

\displaystyle\Phi_{L}(q,p_{T}^{\rm min})

\displaystyle=\Phi^{(0)}_{L}(q,p_{T}^{\rm min})+\Phi^{(1)}_{L}(q,p_{T}^{\rm min})+\mathcal{O}(q_{T}^{2}/Q^{2})\,,

(3.24)

where the LP and NLP results are given by

\displaystyle\Phi^{(0)}_{L}(q,p_{T}^{\rm min})

\displaystyle=\frac{\theta(Q-2p_{T}^{\rm min})}{8\pi}\sqrt{1-(2p_{T}^{\rm min}/Q)^{2}}\,,

(3.25)

\displaystyle\ \Phi^{(1)}_{L}(q,p_{T}^{\rm min})

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2\pi^{2}}\frac{q_{T}}{Q}\frac{p_{T}^{\rm min}}{Q}\frac{\theta(Q-2p_{T}^{\rm min})}{\sqrt{1-(2p_{T}^{\rm min}/Q)^{2}}}\,.

(3.26)

These scale as
{\mathcal{O}}[(q_{T}/Q)^{0}]
and
{\mathcal{O}}[(q_{T}/Q)^{1}]
, respectively.
These results can be easily verified by comparing against the numerical evaluation of the exact expression in eq. (
3.22
).

For illustration, we show in
figure
1
the relative difference
between the exact phase space
\Phi_{L}
and its Born approximation
\Phi_{L}^{(0)}

in the presence of three different cuts
p_{T}^{\rm min}
, namely
p_{T}^{\rm min}=25~\,\mathrm{GeV}
(red solid),

p_{T}^{\rm min}=40~\,\mathrm{GeV}
(blue dashed), and
p_{T}^{\rm min}=60~\,\mathrm{GeV}
(green dotted).
From the slope of each of the three curves, one can easily see the linear dependence on
q_{T}
,
and the slope is in perfect agreement with the result in eq. (
3.26
).

Figure 1
:
The two-particle phase space
\Phi_{L}(q_{T})
relative to its Born-level value
\Phi_{L}(0)

in the presence of a cut
p_{T}^{\rm min}
on the individual momenta,
as a function of the transverse momentum
q_{T}
.

The function
\Delta\Phi_{X}
that captures the power corrections induced by the
p_{T}^{\rm min}
cut
is easily obtained by combining eqs. (
3.1
) and (
3.24
),

\displaystyle\Delta\Phi_{p_{T}^{\rm min}}

\displaystyle=\Phi_{L}(q,p_{T}^{\rm min})-\Phi^{(0)}_{L}(q,p_{T}^{\rm min})={\mathcal{O}}\biggl(\frac{q_{T}}{Q}\frac{p_{T}^{\rm min}}{Q}\biggr)\,.

(3.27)

This linear dependence on
q_{T}
translates into a relative power suppression
of
{\mathcal{O}}(\lambda)
.
Thus the power corrections in eq. (
3.17
) for a
p_{T}^{\rm min}
cut
have
m=1/2
and scale as

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma^{(\mathrm{cuts})}(X)}{\mathrm{d}Q^{2}\mathrm{d}Y\mathrm{d}q_{T}^{2}}\sim\frac{1}{q_{T}^{2}}\frac{q_{T}}{Q}\,,\qquad\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma^{(\mathrm{cuts})}(X)}{\mathrm{d}Q^{2}\mathrm{d}Y\mathrm{d}{\mathcal{T}}_{0}}\sim\frac{1}{{\mathcal{T}}_{0}}\sqrt{\frac{{\mathcal{T}}_{0}}{Q}}\,.

(3.28)

Hence, compared to the normal case of
m=1
, where the power corrections
scale as
q_{T}^{2}/Q^{2}
and
{\mathcal{T}}_{0}/Q
, corresponding to
\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{2})
,
the power corrections induced by the kinematic selection
cuts are enhanced as
{\mathcal{O}}(q_{T}/Q)
and
{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{{\mathcal{T}}_{0}/Q})
.
Intuitively, this arises from breaking
the azimuthal symmetry that is present in the Born process, but which is
explicitly broken by the recoil of the color-singlet system against the real emission.
Hence, additional kinematic selection cuts will generically induce enhanced power corrections
of
\mathcal{O}(\lambda)
.

3.3
Photon isolation

Next, we study the impact of photon isolation cuts on the power corrections.
To disentangle this effect from the fiducial cuts considered
in the previous section, we do not impose any other cuts besides the isolation.
We define an isolation function
f_{\rm iso}(k,p_{\gamma})
to evaluate to
1

if the photon with momentum
p_{\gamma}
is isolated from the emission with momentum
k
,
and to evaluate to
0
otherwise.
The integrated phase space for diphoton production in the presence of such isolation,
as defined in eq. (
3.1
), is given by

\displaystyle\Delta\Phi_{\rm iso}(Q,Y,k)

\displaystyle=\int\Bigl[\mathrm{d}\Phi_{L}(p_{a}^{\prime}+p_{b}^{\prime}-k)\,f_{\rm iso}(k,p_{1}^{\prime})f_{\rm iso}(k,p_{2}^{\prime})-\mathrm{d}\Phi_{L}(p_{a}+p_{b})\Bigr]\,,

(3.29)

where as before
p_{1,2}^{\prime}
are the momenta of the two photons,

p_{a,b}^{\prime}
are the momenta of the incoming partons,
and
k
is the momentum of the real emission.
To calculate the leading power behavior of eq. (
3.29
),
it suffices to work in the singular limit of the phase space,
where the photons are back to back with total momentum
q^{\mu}=(Q\cosh Y,0,0,Q\sinh Y)
.
We parameterize their individual momenta by

\displaystyle p_{1}^{\mu}

\displaystyle=p_{T}\bigl(\cosh(Y+\Delta y)\,,\,+\cos(\varphi)\,,\,+\sin(\varphi)\,,\,\sinh(Y+\Delta y)\bigr)\,,

\displaystyle p_{2}^{\mu}

\displaystyle=p_{T}\bigl(\cosh(Y-\Delta y)\,,\,-\cos(\varphi)\,,\,-\sin(\varphi)\,,\,\sinh(Y-\Delta y)\bigr)\,,

(3.30)

where the rapidity difference
\Delta y
and the photon transverse momenta
p_{T}
are related by

\displaystyle\cosh(\Delta y)=\frac{Q}{2p_{T}}\,.

(3.31)

Using the expression eq. (
3.21
) for the diphoton phase space in the
q_{T}=0
limit, we obtain

\displaystyle\Delta\Phi_{\rm iso}(Q,Y,k)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{32\pi^{2}}\int\frac{\mathrm{d}\Delta y}{\cosh^{2}\Delta y}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\!\mathrm{d}\varphi\,\bigl[f_{\rm iso}(k,p_{1})f_{\rm iso}(k,p_{2})-1\bigr]\,.

(3.32)

The calculation can be further simplified by assuming that both photons are well separated,
such that their isolation cones never overlap with each other, and by assuming that the isolation
energies for both photons are identically chosen as
E_{T}^{\rm iso}
. Since we work in the Born limit here,
where
p_{T1}=p_{T2}\equiv p_{T}
, this assumption holds even if the isolation threshold is
chosen proportional to the photon momentum,
E_{T}^{\rm iso}=\epsilon p_{T}
.
This renders eq. (
3.32
) symmetric in
both momenta
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
, such that we obtain

\displaystyle\Delta\Phi_{\rm iso}(Q,Y,k)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{16\pi^{2}}\int\frac{\mathrm{d}\Delta y}{\cosh^{2}\Delta y}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\!\mathrm{d}\varphi\,\bigl[f_{\rm iso}(k,p_{1})-1\bigr]\,.

(3.33)

In the following, we evaluate eq. (
3.33
) for the different isolation techniques discussed in
section
2.2

to deduce the resulting power corrections.

3.3.1
Fixed-cone isolation

We first study the fixed-cone isolation as defined in eq. (
2.10
), for which we have

\displaystyle f_{\rm cone}(k,p_{\gamma})=1-\theta(k_{T}-E_{T}^{\rm iso})\theta[R-d(k,p_{\gamma})]\,,

(3.34)

such that the photon is considered isolated unless the parton is inside
the isolation cone of size
R
and its transverse momentum exceeds the isolation energy
E_{T}^{\rm iso}
.
Evaluating eq. (
3.33
) with eq. (
3.34
) gives

\displaystyle\Delta\Phi_{\rm cone}(Q,Y,k)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\theta(k_{T}-E_{T}^{\rm iso})}{16\pi^{2}}\int\frac{\mathrm{d}\Delta y}{\cosh^{2}\Delta y}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\mathrm{d}\varphi\,\theta\Bigl[R-\sqrt{\varphi^{2}+(Y+\Delta y-y_{k})^{2}}\Bigr]

\displaystyle=-\frac{\theta(k_{T}-E_{T}^{\rm iso})}{8\pi^{2}}\int\frac{\mathrm{d}\Delta y}{\cosh^{2}\Delta y}\sqrt{R^{2}-(Y+\Delta y-y_{k})^{2}}\,.

(3.35)

Here,
y_{k}
is the rapidity of
k
, and the range of the
\Delta y
integral
is kept implicit from the support of the square root.
Note that eq. (
3.3.1
) is always negative,
because it arises from an additional phase space restriction.
For small
R^{2}\ll 1
, it can be approximated by

\displaystyle\Delta\Phi_{\rm cone}(Q,Y,k)

\displaystyle=-\frac{R^{2}}{16\pi}\frac{\theta(k_{T}-E_{T}^{\rm iso})}{\cosh^{2}(Y-y_{k})}\times\bigl[1+{\mathcal{O}}(R^{2})\bigr]\,.

(3.36)

This correction vanishes as
R\to 0
, as in this limit the isolation turns off.

The nontrivial kinematic dependence of eq. (
3.36
) is entirely given by the denominator.
To understand the induced power corrections, we first rewrite it as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\cosh^{2}(Y-y_{k})}=\biggl(\frac{2e^{Y-y_{k}}}{1+e^{2(Y-y_{k})}}\biggr)^{2}=\frac{4k^{+}k^{-}}{(k^{-}e^{-Y}+k^{+}e^{Y})^{2}}\,.

(3.37)

Using the power counting from eqs. (
3.9
) and (
3.10
), we find in the

n
-collinear and soft limits the corrections

\displaystyle\text{$n$-collinear}:\quad

\displaystyle k_{n}\sim Q\,(\lambda^{2},1,\lambda)\,,

\displaystyle\quad\Rightarrow\quad\frac{1}{\cosh^{2}(Y-y_{k})}\quad\sim\quad{\mathcal{O}}(\lambda^{2})\,,

\displaystyle\text{soft}:\quad

\displaystyle k_{s}\sim Q\,(\lambda,\lambda,\lambda)\,.

\displaystyle\quad\Rightarrow\quad\frac{1}{\cosh^{2}(Y-y_{k})}\quad\sim\quad{\mathcal{O}}(\lambda^{0})\,,

(3.38)

and the corresponding
{\bar{n}}
-collinear and ultrasoft behavior follows trivially.
Eq. (
3.3.1
) implies that only the (ultra)soft limit of eq. (
3.36
) can yield power corrections
that are enhanced relative to the normal
{\mathcal{O}}(\lambda^{2})
corrections intrinsic to the factorization,
as the collinear corrections are always suppressed by (at least)
{\mathcal{O}}(\lambda^{2})
as well.

From eqs. (
3.36
) and (
3.3.1
), it follows immediately that the power
correction to the
q_{T}
factorization from fixed-cone isolation is given by

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\rm(cone)}(X)}{\mathrm{d}Q^{2}\mathrm{d}Y\mathrm{d}q_{T}^{2}}\sim\frac{R^{2}}{q_{T}^{2}}\theta(q_{T}-E_{T}^{\rm iso})\,.

(3.39)

Thus, while the scaling behavior is that of a leading-power term,
1/q_{T}^{2}\sim\lambda^{-2}
,
this correction only contributes to
q_{T}\geq E_{T}^{\rm iso}
, and hence is suppressed for sufficiently
large isolation energies. For a tight isolation, the effect can however become sizable.

The impact on the
{\mathcal{T}}_{0}
subtraction is more involved, as it remains to integrate over
k
against the
{\mathcal{T}}_{0}
measurement.
To do so, we first note that the effect of collinear emissions
is always suppressed at least as
{\mathcal{T}}_{0}
by virtue of eq. (
3.3.1
).
Thus, an enhanced power correction can only result from the soft limit,
which can be deduced by an explicit one-loop calculation.
The bare expression for the soft limit without isolation effect
is given by
[
74
]

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\rm soft}}{\mathrm{d}Q^{2}\mathrm{d}Y\mathrm{d}{\mathcal{T}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\mathrm{LO}}}{\mathrm{d}Q^{2}\mathrm{d}Y}\frac{\alpha_{s}\mathbf{C}}{\pi}\frac{e^{\epsilon\gamma_{E}}\mu^{2\epsilon}}{\Gamma(1-\epsilon)}\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!\frac{\mathrm{d}k^{+}\mathrm{d}k^{-}}{(k^{+}k^{-})^{1+\epsilon}}

(3.40)

\displaystyle\quad\times\,\Bigl[\theta(e^{-Y}k^{-}-e^{Y}k^{+})\delta({\mathcal{T}}_{0}-e^{Y}k^{+})+\theta(e^{Y}k^{+}-e^{-Y}k^{-})\delta({\mathcal{T}}_{0}-e^{-Y}k^{-})\Bigr]\,,

where
\mathbf{C}=C_{F},C_{A}
is the appropriate Casimir for quark annihilation and gluon fusion.
Eq. (
3.40
) is the leading-power limit of the first line in eq. (
3.1
)
without taking effects from
\Delta\Phi_{X}
into account.
By inserting eq. (
3.36
) into the integral in eq. (
3.40
),
we can thus calculate the leading correction from the isolation.
Letting
\epsilon\to 0
and rescaling
k^{\pm}\to e^{\mp Y}k^{\pm}
to remove any dependence on
Y
, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\rm(cone)}}{\mathrm{d}Q^{2}\mathrm{d}Y\mathrm{d}{\mathcal{T}}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\mathrm{LO}}}{\mathrm{d}Q^{2}\mathrm{d}Y}\times\frac{\alpha_{s}\mathbf{C}}{2\pi}\frac{R^{2}}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}k^{-}}{({\mathcal{T}}_{0}+k^{-})^{2}}\theta[{\mathcal{T}}_{0}k^{-}-(E_{T}^{\rm iso})^{2}]\,\theta(k^{-}-{\mathcal{T}}_{0})

\displaystyle=-\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\mathrm{LO}}}{\mathrm{d}Q^{2}\mathrm{d}Y}\times\frac{\alpha_{s}\mathbf{C}}{2\pi}\frac{R^{2}}{\pi}\biggl[\frac{2}{{\mathcal{T}}_{0}}\theta({\mathcal{T}}_{0}-E_{T}^{\rm iso})+\frac{{\mathcal{T}}_{0}}{{\mathcal{T}}_{0}^{2}+(E_{T}^{\rm iso})^{2}}\theta(E_{T}^{\rm iso}-{\mathcal{T}}_{0})\biggr]\,.

(3.41)

In summary, the correction from fixed-cone isolation for
{\mathcal{T}}_{0}
is given by

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\rm(cone)}(X)}{\mathrm{d}Q^{2}\mathrm{d}Y\mathrm{d}{\mathcal{T}}_{0}}\sim\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{R^{2}}{{\mathcal{T}}_{0}}\biggl(\frac{{\mathcal{T}}_{0}}{E_{T}^{\rm iso}}\biggr)^{2}\qquad,\quad{\mathcal{T}}_{0}\leq E_{T}^{\rm iso}\,,\\
\displaystyle\frac{R^{2}}{{\mathcal{T}}_{0}}\hskip 64.01869pt,\quad{\mathcal{T}}_{0}>E_{T}^{\rm iso}\,.\end{cases}

(3.42)

For
{\mathcal{T}}_{0}>E_{T}^{\rm iso}
, this yields the leading-power
1/{\mathcal{T}}_{0}
behavior,
albeit suppressed by
R^{2}
, while for
{\mathcal{T}}_{0}<E_{T}^{\rm iso}
this contribution
is highly suppressed as
({\mathcal{T}}_{0}/E_{T}^{\rm iso})^{2}
.

3.3.2
Smooth-cone isolation

Next, we consider the smooth-cone isolation, eq. (
2.12
),
using the definition of eq. (
2.14
) for
\chi(r)
.
In this case, we have

\displaystyle f_{\rm smooth}(k,p_{\gamma})

\displaystyle=1-\theta\bigl[k_{T}-E_{T}^{\rm iso}(d(k,p_{\gamma})/R)^{2n}\bigr]\theta[R-d(k,p_{\gamma})]

\displaystyle=1-\theta\bigl[d_{\rm min}-d(k,p_{\gamma})\bigr]\,,

(3.43)

where

\displaystyle d_{\rm min}^{2}={\rm min}\bigl\{R^{2},R^{2}(k_{T}/E_{T}^{\rm iso})^{1/n}\bigr\}\,.

(3.44)

According to eq. (
3.3.2
), the photon is considered isolated
unless the parton is inside the radiation cone and
its transverse energy exceeds the threshold value,
which itself depends on the distance between photon and parton.
Eq. (
3.33
) in the presence of the isolation function eq. (
3.3.2
) can be evaluated similar to eq. (
3.3.1
) and yields

\displaystyle\Delta\Phi_{\rm smooth}(Q,Y,k)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{16\pi^{2}}\int\frac{\mathrm{d}\Delta y}{\cosh^{2}(\Delta y)}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\mathrm{d}\varphi\,\theta\bigl[d_{\rm min}-d(k,\gamma)\bigr]

\displaystyle=-\frac{R^{2}}{16\pi}\frac{(k_{T}/E_{T}^{\rm iso})^{1/n}}{\cosh^{2}(Y-y_{k})}\times\bigl[1+{\mathcal{O}}(d_{\rm min}^{2})\bigr]\,,

(3.45)

where we expanded in small
d_{\rm min}
and used that in the singular limit
k_{T}\ll Q

the minimum in eq. (
3.44
) is always dominated by the second value.

From eqs. (
3.3.2
) and (
3.3.1
), it follows immediately that the power
correction to the
q_{T}
factorization from smooth-cone isolation is given by

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\rm(smooth)}(X)}{\mathrm{d}Q^{2}\mathrm{d}Y\mathrm{d}q_{T}^{2}}\sim\frac{R^{2}}{q_{T}^{2}}\Bigl(\frac{q_{T}}{Q}\Bigr)^{1/n}\biggl(\frac{Q}{E_{T}^{\rm iso}}\biggr)^{1/n}\,.

(3.46)

Here, the overall
1/q_{T}^{2}
arises from multiplying the leading-power singular with the isolation correction.
This result should be compared to the inclusive power corrections, which scale as
q_{T}^{2}/Q^{2}
.
Hence, while the absolute size of the isolation effect is suppressed by
R^{2}
, it is enhanced
because the isolation energy
E_{T}^{\rm iso}
is typically much smaller than the hard scale
Q
.
For
n>1/2
the scaling in
q_{T}
is also parametrically enhanced compared to the inclusive case,
and thus in practice the smooth-cone isolation can give sizable power corrections.

For
{\mathcal{T}}_{0}
, we have to distinguish that for collinear modes
k_{T}\sim\lambda Q\sim\sqrt{{\mathcal{T}}_{0}Q}
,
while for ultrasoft modes
k_{T}\sim\lambda^{2}Q\sim{\mathcal{T}}_{0}
. Taking eq. (
3.3.1
) into account,
we can deduce the dominant corrections depending on the isolation parameter
n
from eq. (
3.3.2
) as

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\rm(smooth)}(X)}{\mathrm{d}Q^{2}\mathrm{d}Y\mathrm{d}{\mathcal{T}}_{0}}\sim\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{R^{2}}{{\mathcal{T}}_{0}}\,\Bigl(\frac{{\mathcal{T}}_{0}}{Q}\Bigr)^{1+1/(2n)}\Bigl(\frac{Q}{E_{T}^{\rm iso}}\Bigr)^{1/n}\qquad,\quad n<1/2\,,\vskip 4.30554pt\\
\displaystyle\frac{R^{2}}{{\mathcal{T}}_{0}}\,\Bigl(\frac{{\mathcal{T}}_{0}}{Q}\Bigr)^{1/n}\Bigl(\frac{Q}{E_{T}^{\rm iso}}\Bigr)^{1/n}\hskip 44.10185pt,\quad n>1/2\,.\end{cases}

(3.47)

As for the
q_{T}
case, there is an enhancement in
Q/E_{T}^{\rm iso}

due to the typically small value for the isolation energy.
Furthermore, compared to the inclusive case where the correction scales as
{\mathcal{O}}({\mathcal{T}}^{1})
,
the scaling in
{\mathcal{T}}_{0}
is parametrically enhanced for
n>1
.
Hence, the relative parametric enhancement compared to the normal case
turns out to be more severe for
q_{T}
than
{\mathcal{T}}_{0}
.

The results in eqs. (
3.46
) and (
3.47
)
hold for
q_{T}<E_{T}^{\rm iso}
or
{\mathcal{T}}_{0}<E_{T}^{\rm iso}
, in which case the
minimum in eq. (
3.44
) is given by the second term,
which then induces the
k_{T}
dependence of eq. (
3.3.2
).
For the opposite case of
q_{T}>E_{T}^{\rm iso}
or
{\mathcal{T}}_{0}>E_{T}^{\rm iso}
,
the minimum in eq. (
3.44
) is instead given by
d_{\rm min}=R
,
such that smooth-cone isolation reduces to fixed-cone isolation.
Thus, we find that for
q_{T}>E_{T}^{\rm iso}
or
{\mathcal{T}}_{0}>E_{T}^{\rm iso}
, smooth-cone isolation
yields the same leading-power
1/q_{T}
or
1/{\mathcal{T}}_{0}
behavior as for fixed-cone isolation.

3.3.3
Harsh isolation.

Finally, we consider the harsh isolation defined in eq. (
2.15
), where

\displaystyle f_{\rm harsh}(k,p_{\gamma})=1-\theta\bigl[R-d(k,p_{\gamma})\bigr]\,,

(3.48)

which vetoes any radiation inside the isolation cone.
The corresponding result for eq. (
3.33
) is easily obtained from eq. (
3.36
) by setting
E_{T}^{\rm iso}=0
,

\displaystyle\Delta\Phi_{\rm harsh}(Q,Y,k)

\displaystyle=-\frac{R^{2}}{16\pi}\frac{\theta(k_{T})}{\cosh^{2}(Y-y_{k})}\times\bigl[1+{\mathcal{O}}(R^{2})\bigr]\,.

(3.49)

The induced correction then follows directly from eqs. (
3.39
) and (
3.42
) as

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\rm(harsh)}(X)}{\mathrm{d}Q^{2}\mathrm{d}Y\mathrm{d}q_{T}^{2}}\sim\frac{R^{2}}{q_{T}^{2}}\,,\qquad\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\rm(harsh)}(X)}{\mathrm{d}Q^{2}\mathrm{d}Y\mathrm{d}{\mathcal{T}}_{0}}\sim\frac{R^{2}}{{\mathcal{T}}_{0}}\,.

(3.50)

This is a leading power (singular) effect, as the harsh isolation completely removes
part of the real emission phase space, namely the vicinity of the two photons,
and thus immediately breaks both factorization theorems, which rely on an
analytic integration over the full emission phase space.

3.4
Factorization violation in photon isolation

In this section, we briefly discuss a
potential source for factorization violation for isolation methods
when not carefully applying the isolation procedure.
In general, one only keeps events that satisfy
the chosen isolation criterion. The remaining events can then
still contain jets, as defined by a suitable jet algorithm applied after the isolation,
that are inside or overlapping the isolation cones, e.g. if the jets are sufficiently
soft.

Since any jet inside the isolation cone will typically be quite soft,
as part of the overall isolation procedure one can in principle also remove any jets
inside the isolation cone from further consideration, i.e., the events are kept
but the jets are not further considered for the calculation of physical quantities,
e.g. jet selection cuts. This approach is for example proposed in the original definition of
smooth-cone isolation in ref.
[
90
]
.

For the purpose of the subtractions, it is however crucial to keep
all

reconstructed jets, or more generally all emissions, for the determination of the
resolution variable
{\mathcal{T}}
.
More generally, this applies to employing any factorization theorem,
irrespective of whether it is used for subtractions or resummation of large logarithms.
For example, recall the definition for
0
-jettiness, see eq. (
2.2
)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{T}}_{0}=\sum_{i}{\rm min}\bigl\{k_{i}^{+}e^{Y}\,,\,k_{i}^{-}e^{-Y}\bigr\}\,.

(3.51)

Here, the sum
i
runs over all particles
i
in the final state, only excluding
the color-singlet final state,
which is critical for the derivation of the
{\mathcal{T}}_{0}
factorization theorem.
Excluding any emissions inside the isolation cones from the sum in eq. (
3.51
)
would thus change the definition of
{\mathcal{T}}_{0}
and immediately violate
the
{\mathcal{T}}_{0}
factorization theorem.
For example, at one loop, where one has only one real emission, excluding jets
inside the isolation cones is equivalent to excluding the emission.
As far as calculating
{\mathcal{T}}_{0}
is concerned, this exactly corresponds to the harsh isolation
defined in eq. (
2.15
). As discussed in
section
3.3.3
,
this induces leading-power corrections, which exactly corresponds
to breaking the factorization.

For
q_{T}
subtraction, one can trivially avoid this problem by determining

q_{T}
directly from the color-singlet final state
L
, i.e.
q_{T}\equiv q_{T,L}
.
On the other hand, if
q_{T}
is obtained from the sum of all real emissions,

q_{T}=|\sum_{i}\vec{k}_{T,i}|
, then as for
{\mathcal{T}}_{0}
, the sum over
i
must not exclude
emissions inside the isolation cones to not violate the factorization.

Lastly, we point out that this leads to a trivial yet dangerous pitfall in the
calculation of power corrections.
For example, to calculate the NLO cross section for
pp\to H
using
{\mathcal{T}}_{0}
subtractions,
one would use
pp\to H+j
at LO to calculate the power corrections or the
above-cut contributions in the slicing approach.
Naively applying the smooth-cone isolation including the discussed treatment of jets to
the resulting
H+j
events, one would classify
all events where the emitted parton falls inside the isolation cone as
0
-jet events,
which depending on the used tool might be discarded in a
pp\to H+j
calculation,
where at least one jet is required at Born level.
We have explicitly checked that this is the case for MCFM8

[
98
,
99
,
100
,
101
]
.
To not violate the subtraction method, it is however mandatory to keep all such events,
and we have turned off this mechanism in MCFM8 to obtain the correct results
for our numerical studies in
section
4
.
(This does not impact the NLO calculations in MCFM8 itself, which keeps the

H+j
events that are otherwise classified as
0
-jet events.)

5
{\mathcal{T}}_{N}
subtractions including measurement cuts

In this section, we discuss how all cut-induced power corrections can be
accounted for exactly in the subtraction procedure. Our starting point are
differential
{\mathcal{T}}_{N}
subtractions
[
39
]
, using which
the cross section with a measurement
X
is given by

\displaystyle\sigma(X)

\displaystyle=\sigma^{\mathrm{sub}}(X,\tau_{\mathrm{off}})+\int\!\mathrm{d}\tau\,\biggl[\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma(X)}{\mathrm{d}\tau}-\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\mathrm{sub}}(X)}{\mathrm{d}\tau}\theta(\tau<\tau_{\mathrm{off}})\biggr]

\displaystyle=\sigma^{\mathrm{sub}}(X,\tau_{\mathrm{off}})+\int^{\tau_{\mathrm{off}}}\!\mathrm{d}\tau\,\biggl[\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma(X)}{\mathrm{d}\tau}-\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\mathrm{sub}}(X)}{\mathrm{d}\tau}\biggr]+\int_{\tau_{\mathrm{off}}}\!\mathrm{d}\tau\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma(X)}{\mathrm{d}\tau}\,.

(5.1)

As in
section
2.1
,

\tau
stands for any (dimensionless)
N
-jet resolution variable for which a LP
factorization theorem is known.
The differential subtraction term
\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\mathrm{sub}}(X)/\mathrm{d}\tau

captures the leading-power singularities for
\tau\to 0
, which means it satisfies

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\mathrm{sub}}(X)}{\mathrm{d}\tau}=\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma^{(0)}(X)}{\mathrm{d}\tau}\bigl[1+\mathcal{O}(\tau)\bigr]\,,

(5.2)

such that the integrand in square brackets in eq. (
5
) is a power
correction with at most integrable singularities for
\tau\to 0
, and so the integral
can be carried out numerically. Since the integral exists and is finite, the point
\tau=0

is irrelevant, which means the integrand is never evaluated at
\tau=0
. Hence,
the full result for
\mathrm{d}\sigma(X)/\mathrm{d}\tau
is only needed for nonzero
\tau>0

and thus reduces to performing the NLO Born+1-parton calculation. Similarly the
distributional structure of
\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\mathrm{sub}}(X)/\mathrm{d}\tau
at
\tau=0
is not needed for the differential
subtraction terms, which are fully known to N
3
LO for both
q_{T}
and
{\mathcal{T}}_{0}
subtractions
[
51
]
.
The first term in eq. (
5
)
is the cumulant of
\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\mathrm{sub}}(X)/\mathrm{d}\tau
up to
\tau_{\mathrm{off}}
. Its evaluation
does require the full distributional structure of
\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\mathrm{sub}}(X)/\mathrm{d}\tau
.

Note that in principle the integrand does need to be sampled arbitrarily close to

\tau=0
, but due to the subtraction the contribution from a region
\tau<\delta

is of
\mathcal{O}(\delta)
.
This is similar to the fact that even in a fully local subtraction method the
real-emission phase-space formally needs to be sampled arbitrarily close to the IR-singular
region, but the subtractions ensure that the total subtracted integrand
is well-behaved, so the contribution from a region of size
\delta
around the singularity
only contributes an amount of
\mathcal{O}(\delta)
. Letting
\delta\to 0
still requires
evaluating the real-emissions matrix elements arbitrarily close to the singularity,
and to avoid numerical instabilities due to arbitrarily large numerical cancellations
one always has a technical cutoff
\delta
that cuts out the actual singular points
of phase space.

The parameter
\tau_{\mathrm{off}}
determines the range over which the subtractions
act, and by taking
\tau_{\mathrm{off}}\sim 1
there are no large numerical cancellations
between the first and second term in eq. (
5
).
(In the context of resummation,
\tau_{\mathrm{off}}
corresponds to where the
\tau

resummation is turned off.)
The slicing method described in
section
2.1
is obtained from
eq. (
5
) by taking
\tau_{\mathrm{off}}=\tau_{\mathrm{cut}}
, see eq. (
2.1
).
In this case, the integral below
\tau_{\mathrm{off}}=\tau_{\mathrm{cut}}

corresponds to
\Delta\sigma(X,\tau_{\mathrm{cut}})
and is neglected, which induces the
power corrections.
In contrast, eq. (
5
) is exact
and involves no neglected power corrections.

The practical challenge in implementing eq. (
5
) is that the NLO
calculation for
\mathrm{d}\sigma(X)/\mathrm{d}\tau
has to be obtained as a function of

\tau
. In general this is not easy as it requires to organize the
integration over the real-emission phase space in such a way that
\tau

is preserved, which by default is not the case for standard NLO subtractions.
For a more detailed discussion we refer to ref.
[
39
]
.

To make the differential subtractions more viable in practice, we can follow
the same basic strategy as in
section
3
to separate the different sources
of power corrections.
We first note that the LP singular contribution only depends
on the Born phase space. That is, the factorization theorem for
\tau
is
always fully differential in the Born phase space, which involves choosing a
specific set of kinematic variables to parametrize the Born phase space.
The measurement
X
is then evaluated on this reference Born phase space.
In other words, constructing

\mathrm{d}\sigma^{(0)}(X)/\mathrm{d}\tau
involves choosing a Born projection
\hat{\Phi}_{N}(\Phi_{N+k})

from the real-emission phase-space with
k
additional emissions,
\Phi_{N+k}
,
to the Born phase space,
\Phi_{N}
.
For color-singlet production (
N=0
), a typical choice is to use
Q
and
Y

as the Born variables, as we did in
section
3
above.
The LP measurement function that actually enters
in
\mathrm{d}\sigma^{(0)}(X)/\mathrm{d}\tau
is then given by

f_{X}^{(0)}(\Phi_{N+k})=f_{X}[\hat{\Phi}_{N}(\Phi_{N+k})]\,.

(5.3)

For color-singlet production at NLO, this is precisely the LP
term on the right-hand side of eq. (
3.13
).
Denoting this LP measurement by
X^{(0)}
, we therefore have

\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\mathrm{sub}}(X)}{\mathrm{d}\tau}\equiv\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\mathrm{sub}}(X^{(0)})}{\mathrm{d}\tau}\,,\qquad\sigma^{\mathrm{sub}}(X,\tau_{\mathrm{off}})\equiv\sigma^{\mathrm{sub}}(X^{(0)},\tau_{\mathrm{off}})\,.

(5.4)

Next, we can consider the full cross section but with the measurement replaced by
this LP Born reference measurement,
\mathrm{d}\sigma(X^{(0)})/\mathrm{d}\tau
.
By adding and subtracting it, we can rewrite eq. (
5
) as

\displaystyle\sigma(X)

\displaystyle=\sigma^{\mathrm{sub}}(X^{(0)},\tau_{\mathrm{off}})+\int\!\mathrm{d}\tau\,\biggl[\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma(X^{(0)})}{\mathrm{d}\tau}-\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\mathrm{sub}}(X^{(0)})}{\mathrm{d}\tau}\theta(\tau<\tau_{\mathrm{off}})\biggr]+\int\!\mathrm{d}\tau\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma(X-X^{(0)})}{\mathrm{d}\tau}

\displaystyle\equiv\sigma(X^{(0)})+\sigma(X-X^{(0)})\,.

(5.5)

We have now isolated the two different sources of power corrections.
The sum of the first two terms in the first line of eq. (
5
)
is the calculation of the
reference cross section
\sigma(X^{(0)})
using differential
\tau
subtractions.
Since it involves the same reference measurement
X^{(0)}
everywhere,
the difference
\mathrm{d}\sigma(X^{(0)})-\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\mathrm{sub}}(X^{(0)})
does not
involve any cut-induced power corrections, hence reducing the problem
of power corrections to the normal and well-studied case, and for which
the power corrections can be systematically calculated if necessary
[
70
,
71
,
72
,
73
,
74
,
75
,
76
]
.
In particular, if the implementation of the differential
\tau
subtractions
proves too difficult in practice, this contribution could be calculated with the
slicing approach (see below).

The last term in eq. (
5
) amounts to measuring the difference
between
X
and
X^{(0)}
on the full cross section. Here we exploited that the
difference of the two cross sections can be combined into a single cross section,
as the only difference lies in the measurement,

\int\!\mathrm{d}\tau\biggl[\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma(X)}{\mathrm{d}\tau}-\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma(X^{(0)})}{\mathrm{d}\tau}\biggr]=\int\!\mathrm{d}\tau\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma(X-X^{(0)})}{\mathrm{d}\tau}=\sigma(X-X^{(0)})\,.

(5.6)

That is,
\sigma(X-X^{(0)})

contains the difference of the full and LP measurement functions,

f_{X}(\Phi_{N+k})-f_{X}^{(0)}(\Phi_{N+k})
. For example, for color-singlet
production at NLO,
\mathrm{d}\sigma(X-X^{(0)})
is precisely given by eq. (
3.1
).
Since for any infrared safe
X
this measurement difference vanishes in the singular
limit,
\sigma(X-X^{(0)})
still amounts to effectively performing a
Born+1-parton calculation at one lower order.
It contains all cut-induced power corrections, which as we discussed can be
potentially large, and it should therefore be treated exactly.
Since it can be formulated as a specific choice
of measurement, it can be implemented straightforwardly into
existing NLO calculations. Once this is done, the explicit dependence
on
\tau
disappears. (In general it might still be implicit through the choice of
X^{(0)}
.)
One might say that the reference cross section
\mathrm{d}\sigma(X^{(0)})
in eq. (
5.6
)
effectively acts as a fully local subtraction term. However, this is somewhat misleading,
since the IR singularities do not cancel in the difference of two singular contributions.
Rather, they are simply regulated by performing an IR-safe Born+1-parton
measurement.

When performing the calculation of
\sigma(X-X^{(0)})
one might still have
to integrate near the singular region of phase space, but only to the extent
to which the full measurement is sensitive to, which is the best one can hope for.
For example, if
X
contains isolation cuts, then
X^{(0)}
will contain no
isolation cuts. The difference
X-X^{(0)}
then measures the cross section
that is removed by the isolation, which is sensitive to real emissions with energies
down to
E_{T}^{\rm iso}
, while below that the difference of the two measurements explicitly vanishes.
For selection cuts, one can still get sensitive to arbitrarily soft emissions,
e.g., when measuring the
p_{T}
of the photons in
H\to\gamma\gamma
very close to the
Born limit
p_{T}=m_{H}/2
. However, this is a well-known feature of such cuts
and inherent to the measurement itself and not related the subtraction method.

From the above discussion, we can also see the connection to the projection-to-Born
method
[
31
]
. It amounts to the special case where the reference cross
section
\mathrm{d}\sigma(X^{(0)})
is known analytically or from some other calculation,
while the last term
is precisely the effective Born+1-parton calculation that also appears in the
projection-to-Born method. In other words, the projection-to-Born method is
simply the statement that
\sigma(X)
can be calculated as

\sigma(X)=\sigma(X^{(0)})+\sigma(X-X^{(0)})\,,

(5.7)

when the full cross section
\sigma(X^{(0)})
for some reference
measurement
X^{(0)}
is already known, and the correction term

\sigma(X-X^{(0)})
is calculated
by evaluating the
X-X^{(0)}
measurement
for the lower-order Born+1-parton calculation as described above.

To conclude, we note that if the reference cross section
\sigma(X^{(0)})
is
obtained via a global
\tau
slicing, one can of course combine both Born+1-parton
calculations into a single one,

\displaystyle\sigma(X)

\displaystyle=\sigma^{\mathrm{sub}}(X^{(0)},\tau_{\mathrm{cut}})+\sigma[X-X^{(0)}\theta(\tau<\tau_{\mathrm{cut}})]+\Delta\sigma(X^{(0)},\tau_{\mathrm{cut}})\,.

(5.8)

This makes it explicit that in contrast to eq. (
2.1
), here the power corrections

\Delta\sigma(X^{(0)},\tau_{\mathrm{cut}})
are only those for the chosen reference measurement. The cut-induced power
corrections are accounted for by the Born+1-parton calculation in the second term, because
it correctly captures the difference
X-X^{(0)}
below
\tau_{\mathrm{cut}}
.

6
Conclusions

We have studied the impact of kinematic selection cuts
and isolation requirements for leptons and photons on the
q_{T}
and
N
-jettiness
subtraction methods. Using a simplified one-loop calculation, we analytically determined
the scaling of power corrections induced by these cuts including their dependence
on the isolation method and its parameters.
We find that both selection cuts and isolation induce additional power corrections
that are parametrically enhanced relative to the usual, cut-independent power corrections
inherent to the
q_{T}
and
{\mathcal{T}}_{0}
factorization theorems.
We have also discussed how the cut effects can be fully incorporated into the subtraction,
thereby avoiding the additional power corrections, by employing
differential subtractions for them instead of a global slicing method.

To summarize our key findings, we expand the differential
q_{T}
and
{\mathcal{T}}_{0}
spectra as

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma(X)}{\mathrm{d}Q^{2}\mathrm{d}Y\mathrm{d}q_{T}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma^{(0)}(X)}{\mathrm{d}Q^{2}\mathrm{d}Y\mathrm{d}q_{T}^{2}}\times\Bigl[1+{\mathcal{O}}\bigl[(q_{T}^{2}/Q^{2})^{m}\bigr]\Bigr]\,,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma(X)}{\mathrm{d}Q^{2}\mathrm{d}Y\mathrm{d}{\mathcal{T}}_{0}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma^{(0)}(X)}{\mathrm{d}Q^{2}\mathrm{d}Y\mathrm{d}{\mathcal{T}}_{0}}\times\Bigl[1+{\mathcal{O}}\bigl[({\mathcal{T}}_{0}/Q)^{m}\bigr]\Bigr]\,,

(6.1)

where
\sigma^{(0)}
are the leading-power limits predicted by the factorization theorems.
We find the following power corrections
in the square brackets in eq. (
6
) for typical selection and isolation cuts:

•

For inclusive processes without any cuts, one has
m=1
.

•

A typical
p_{T}>p_{T}^{\rm min}
selection cut for photons or leptons yields enhanced power
corrections with
m=1/2
and proportional to
\sim p_{T}^{\rm min}/Q
.
Since this arises from breaking azimuthal symmetry that is only present in the
Born process, we expect a similar enhancement for generic fiducial cuts.

•

All photon isolation methods yield
leading-power
corrections (
m=0
)
for
q_{T}>E_{T}^{\rm iso}
and
{\mathcal{T}}_{0}>E_{T}^{\rm iso}
, respectively,
which are proportional to the size of the isolation cone
\sim{\mathcal{O}}(R^{2})
.

•

At one loop, fixed-cone isolation induces no corrections for
q_{T}<E_{T}^{\rm iso}

and highly suppressed corrections (
m=2
) for
{\mathcal{T}}_{0}<E_{T}^{\rm iso}
. At higher
orders one can expect nontrivial corrections also below
E_{T}^{\rm iso}
, which
should be power suppressed.

•

Smooth-cone isolation as defined in eq. (
2.14
) yields power corrections
scaling as
m=1/(2n)
for
q_{T}
and
m=1/n
for
{\mathcal{T}}_{0}
, respectively.
They are further enhanced by an overall factor
(Q/E_{T}^{\rm iso})^{1/n}
.

In general, tight cuts can thus yield significantly enhanced power corrections.
The enhancement is most severe for smooth-cone isolation with
q_{T}
subtractions.
We have numerically verified and studied these findings for the examples of

pp\to H\to{\gamma\gamma}
and
pp\to{\gamma\gamma}
.

While our analysis is based on an explicit one-loop study, we expect the
dominant qualitative behavior to persist at NNLO and beyond, since the same
kinematic effects will also appear at higher orders.
For example, our results immediately apply to real-virtual contributions
at higher orders involving a single real emission.
For contributions with two or more real emissions additional nontrivial
kinematic correlations among multiple emissions are likely to lead to
additional effects, e.g., one can expect the kinks at

q_{T}=E_{T}^{\rm iso}
and
{\mathcal{T}}_{0}=E_{T}^{\rm iso}
to get smeared out.
It seems extremely unlikely though that such effects from multiple emissions
could somehow improve the behavior that is already present for a single real
emission – one might hope that they do not make things worse.
Note that at order
\alpha_{s}^{n}
, the inclusive power corrections contain up to
2n-1

logarithms
\ln(Q/q_{T})
and
\ln(Q/{\mathcal{T}}_{0})
, respectively, and it would be
interesting to study in detail to what extent the enhanced power corrections
also receive such additional logarithmic factors, which would make them
numerically even more important.

Our results provide an important step for a better understanding of power corrections
whenever kinematic selection cuts or isolation cuts are applied.
This is crucial both for subtraction methods and the resummation of large logarithms in such processes.
In principle, our technique can be employed to exactly calculate the induced corrections.
In practice, it will however be more advantageous to account for all cut-induced corrections
within the subtraction method itself as discussed in
section
5
.
