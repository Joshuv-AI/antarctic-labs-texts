Subresultants and the Shape Lemma
David A. Cox, Carlos D’Andrea

Abstract
. In nice cases, a zero-dimensional complete intersection ideal over a field has a Shape Lemma. There are also cases where the ideal is generated by the resultant and first subresultant polynomials of the generators. This paper explores the relation between these representations and studies when the resultant generates the elimination ideal. We also prove a Poisson formula for resultants arising from the hidden variable method.

1.
Introduction

This paper will study the relation between the Shape Lemma and subresultants. We begin with an example that introduces some of the key players:

Example 1.1
.

In the polynomial ring
\mathbb{C}[x_{1},x_{2}]
, let

f_{1}=x_{1}^{2}-x_{2}-1,\ f_{2}=x_{1}^{2}+x_{1}x_{2}-2.

Computing a lex Gröbner basis of
I=\langle f_{1},f_{2}\rangle
with
x_{2}\prec x_{1}
gives

\displaystyle I=\langle r(x_{2}),\,

\displaystyle x_{1}-g_{1}(x_{2})\rangle,\quad r(x_{2})=x_{2}^{3}+2x_{2}-1,\ g_{1}(x_{2})=x_{2}^{2}+1.

The structure of this basis makes it easy to find the solutions and is encapsulated by saying that
I

has a Shape Lemma
. Notice also that
I\cap\mathbb{C}[x_{2}]=\langle r(x_{2})\rangle
.

A more classical approach to finding nice generators of
I
uses resultants and subresultant polynomials. Regarding
f_{1},f_{2}
as polynomials in
x_{1}
with coefficients in
A=\mathbb{C}[x_{2}]
, the zeroth subresultant polynomial is the resultant, and the first subresultant polynomial is linear in
x_{1}
. These polynomials will be denoted
R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{2})
and
p_{1}(x_{1},x_{2})
respectively in what follows. By standard determinantal formulas,

\displaystyle R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{2})

\displaystyle=-x_{2}^{3}-2x_{2}+1

\displaystyle p_{1}(x_{1},x_{2})

\displaystyle=x_{2}\cdot x_{1}+x_{2}-1.

(One could also use the
SubresultantPolynomials
command in
Mathematica

[
Math21
]
.) These polynomials always lie in
I
, and in this case, they actually generate. To see why, note that
r(x_{2})=-R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{2})
, and since
x_{2}
is relatively prime to
R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{2})=-x_{2}^{3}-2x_{2}+1
, we have a
Bézout identity

A\cdot R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}+B\cdot x_{2}=1,\quad A=1,\ B=x_{2}^{2}+2.

One computes without difficulty that

x_{1}+B\cdot(x_{2}-1)=Ax_{1}\cdot R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}+B\cdot p_{1}\in\langle R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt},p_{1}\rangle,

and also that

x_{1}-g_{1}(x_{2})=x_{1}-x_{2}^{2}-1=x_{1}+B\cdot(x_{2}-1)+R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}\in\langle R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt},p_{1}\rangle.

Since we know
\langle R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt},p_{1}\rangle\subseteq I=\langle r(x_{2}),x_{1}-g(x_{2})\rangle
, equality follows.

This example has two features that lead to interesting questions:

•

I
has a Shape Lemma representation
I=\langle r(x_{2}),x_{1}-g(x_{2})\rangle
and a subresultant representation
I=\langle R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{2}),p_{1}(x_{1},x_{2})\rangle
. How often does this happen?

•

r(x_{2})
generates the elimination ideal
I\cap\mathbb{C}[x_{2}]
, which implies that
R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{2})
also generates
I\cap\mathbb{C}[x_{2}]
. How often does this happen?

Our goal is to study these questions when
I=\langle f_{1},\dots,f_{n}\rangle\subseteq\mathbb{K}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
is a zero-dimensional complete intersection and
\mathbb{K}
is algebraically closed. We will always assume that
n\geq 2
.

The paper is structured as follows. Sections
2
and
3
provide background material on the Shape Lemma and resultants that will be used in Sections
4
and
5
, where the main theorems of the paper are proved. In the remainder of this introduction, we will describe the contents of Sections
2
–
5
in more detail and discuss how our results relate to previous work.

Section
2
studies zero-dimensional ideals
I\subseteq\mathbb{K}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
of the form

I=\langle r(x_{n}),x_{1}-g_{1}(x_{n}),\dots,x_{n-1}-g_{n-1}(x_{n})\rangle

for polynomials
r(x_{n}),g_{1}(x_{n}),\dots,g_{n-1}(x_{n})\in\mathbb{K}[x_{n}]
. We say that
I

has a Shape Lemma with respect to
x_{n}
when this happens. Since our results and examples will always be with respect to
x_{n}
, we will simply say “
I
has a Shape Lemma” for the rest of the paper. Section
2
will characterize when
I
has a Shape Lemma, following
[
BMMT94
]
for Lemmas
2.1
and
2.2
, and adding Lemma
2.3
as suggested by one of the reviewers.

Resultants take center stage in Section
3
. We will use the classical multivariable resultant

\mathrm{Res}_{d_{1},\ldots,d_{n}}(g_{1},\ldots,g_{n})\in A,

where
g_{1},\ldots,g_{n}
are homogeneous polynomials in
A[x_{0},\ldots,x_{n-1}]
of respective degrees
d_{1},\dots,d_{n}
, and
A
is an integral domain. We assume
d_{i}\geq 1
for all
i
.

Given an ideal
I=\langle f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}\rangle\subseteq\mathbb{K}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
as above, we want to think of the last variable
x_{n}
as a constant, similar to what we did in Example
1.1
.
So regard
f_{1},\dots,f_{n}
as lying in
A[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n-1}]
with
A=\mathbb{K}[x_{n}]
, and let
f_{1}^{h},\ldots,f_{n}^{h}\in A[x_{0},x_{1},\ldots,x_{n-1}]
be their homogenizations with a new variable
x_{0}
up to degrees
d_{1},\ldots,d_{n}
respectively, where
d_{i}=\deg_{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n-1}}(f_{i})
for
i=1,\dots,n
. Define

R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{n}):=\mathrm{Res}_{d_{1},\ldots,d_{n}}(f_{1}^{h},\ldots,f_{n}^{h})\in\mathbb{K}[x_{n}].

This resultant will appear in the theorems proved in Sections
3
,
4
and
5
.
Since the variable
x_{n}
is “hidden” in the coefficients, the resultant
R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{n})
is an instance of the so-called “hidden variable method” (see for example
[
CLO05
, Chapter 3, §5]
). Extra care must be taken because in some cases (like in
[
CLO05
]
), the degrees
d_{1},\ldots,d_{n}
used to compute a hidden variable resultant are the total degrees of
f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}
. Note that in our case, we use a smaller degree sequence that takes into account only the first
n-1
variables.

The main purpose of Section
3
is to give a Poisson-style formula for
R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{n}).
Since
f_{1}^{h},\ldots,f_{n}^{h}\in\mathbb{K}[x_{0},\ldots,x_{n-1},x_{n}]
are homogeneous with respect to
x_{0},\dots,x_{n-1}
, they define a variety

\mathbf{V}(f_{1}^{h},\ldots,f_{n}^{h})\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n-1}_{\mathbb{K}}\times_{\mathbb{K}}\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{K}}^{1}.

Here is the main result of Section
3
, which is of independent interest:

Theorem 1.1
.

If
\mathbf{V}(f_{1}^{h},\dots,f_{n}^{h})\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n-1}_{\mathbb{K}}\times_{\mathbb{K}}\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{K}}^{1}
is finite, then there is a nonzero constant
c\in\mathbb{K}
such that

(1.1)

R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{n})=c\!\!\prod_{\xi\in\mathbf{V}(f_{1}^{h},\dots,f_{n}^{h})}\!\!(x_{n}-\xi_{n})^{m_{\xi}},

where
\xi=([\xi_{0}\hskip 0.85pt{:}\hskip 0.85pt\ldots\hskip 0.85pt{:}\hskip 0.85pt\xi_{n-1}],\xi_{n})\in\mathbb{P}^{n-1}_{\mathbb{K}}\times_{\mathbb{K}}\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{K}}^{1}
and
m_{\xi}
is the Hilbert-Samuel multiplicity of
\xi
.

Properties of resultants guarantee that
R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{n})\in\langle f_{1},\dots,f_{n}\rangle\cap\mathbb{K}[x_{n}]=I\cap\mathbb{K}[x_{n}]
. Section
4
will study when
R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{n})
generates
I\cap\mathbb{K}[x_{n}]
. The ideal
I\subseteq\mathbb{K}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
gives
\mathbf{V}(I)=\mathbf{V}(f_{1},\dots,f_{n})\subseteq\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{K}}^{n}
, where the affine space
\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{K}}^{n}
has coordinates
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
. This lies in
\mathbb{P}^{n-1}_{\mathbb{K}}\times_{\mathbb{K}}\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{K}}^{1}
, with complement defined by
x_{0}=0
, the “points at
\infty
” in
\mathbb{P}^{n-1}_{\mathbb{K}}\times_{\mathbb{K}}\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{K}}^{1}
. From this point of view, elements of
\mathbf{V}(f_{1}^{h},\dots,f_{n}^{h})
with
x_{0}=0
will be regarded as “solutions at
\infty
” of
f_{1}=\cdots=f_{n}=0
.

The main result of Section
4
describes how solutions at
\infty
and the Shape Lemma interact with
R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{n})
and the elimination ideal
I\cap\mathbb{K}[x_{n}]
:

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
I=\langle f_{1},\dots,f_{n}\rangle
be a zero-dimensional ideal such that the map
\mathbf{V}(I)\rightarrow\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{K}}^{1}
given by projection onto the
n
th coordinate is injective as a map of sets. Then any two of the following three conditions imply the third
:

(1)

I
has a Shape Lemma.

(2)

f_{1},\dots,f_{n}
have no solutions at
\infty
.

(3)

I\cap\mathbb{K}[x_{n}]=\langle R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{n})\rangle
.

In Section
5
, subresultants enter the picture. The
critical degree
of the system
f_{1}=\dots=f_{n}=0
is
\rho=d_{1}+\cdots+d_{n}-n
, where
d_{i}=\deg_{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n-1}}(f_{i})
as above. For every monomial
x^{\alpha}
in
x_{0},\dots,x_{n-1}
of degree
\rho
, there is a
scalar subresultant

s_{\alpha}(x_{n})\in\mathbb{K}[x_{n}]
(it is scalar with respect to
x_{0},\dots,x_{n-1}
). Then, if
\rho\geq 1
, define

s_{i}(x_{n}):=s_{\alpha(i)}(x_{n}),\text{ where }x^{\alpha(i)}=x_{0}^{\rho-1}x_{i}\text{ for }i=0,\dots,n-1,

which leads to the
first subresultant polynomials

p_{i}(x_{i},x_{n}):=s_{0}(x_{n})x_{i}-s_{i}(x_{n})\in\mathbb{K}[x_{i},x_{n}]

for
i=1,\dots,n-1
. Note that the coefficient of
x_{i}
in
p_{i}(x_{i},x_{n})
is
s_{0}(x_{n})
, independent of
i
. This will be important in what follows. When
n=2
,
p_{1}(x_{1},x_{2})
agrees with the subresultant polynomial that appeared in Example
1.1
.

The first main result of Section
5
describes the optimal interaction between
the ideal
I
, the resultant
R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{n})
, and first subresultant polynomials
p_{i}(x_{i},x_{n})
:

Theorem 1.3
.

Assume that
I=\langle f_{1},\dots,f_{n}\rangle
is zero-dimensional with
\rho\geq 1
. Then the following are equivalent
:

(1)

I
has a Shape Lemma and no solutions at
\infty
.

(2)

I\cap\mathbb{K}[x_{n}]=\langle R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{n})\rangle
and
\gcd(R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{n}),s_{0}(x_{n}))=1
.

(3)

I=\langle R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{n}),p_{1}(x_{1},x_{n}),\ldots,p_{n-1}(x_{n-1},x_{n})\rangle
and

I\cap\mathbb{K}[x_{n}]=\langle R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{n})\rangle
.

Furthermore, when these conditions are all true, we have

I\cap\mathbb{K}[x_{i_{1}},\dots,x_{i_{\ell}},x_{n}]=\langle R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{n}),p_{i_{0}}(x_{i_{0}},x_{n}),\dots,p_{i_{\ell}}(x_{i_{\ell}},x_{n})\rangle

whenever
1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{\ell}<n
.

Notice that (3) is the nicest case: the ideal is generated by the resultant and first subresultant polynomials, and the elimination ideal is generated by the resultant. The miracle is that when this holds, explicit generators can be given for
all
elimination ideals that do not eliminate
x_{n}
. Also, as we did in Example
1.1
, in this situation one can recover the lexicographic Gröbner basis of all of these ideals by computing
x_{i_{j}}-s_{i_{j}}(x_{n})\cdot(s_{0}(x_{n})^{-1}\!\!\!\mod R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{n})),\,j=1,\ldots,\ell.

The second main theorem of Section
5
assumes only that
I
is generated by the resultant
R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{n})
and first subresultant polynomials
p_{i}(x_{i},x_{n})=s_{0}(x_{n})x_{i}-s_{i}(x_{n})
:

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
I=\langle f_{1},\dots,f_{n}\rangle
be zero-dimensional with
\rho\geq 1
and assume that
I=\langle R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{n}),p_{1}(x_{1},x_{n}),\dots,p_{n-1}(x_{n-1},x_{n})\rangle
. Then
:

(1)

\gcd(R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{n}),s_{0}(x_{n}),\dots,s_{n-1}(x_{n}))=1
.

(2)

I
has a Shape Lemma.

Furthermore, the following conditions are equivalent
:

(3)

I\cap\mathbb{K}[x_{n}]=\langle R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{n})\rangle
.

(4)

\gcd(R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{n}),s_{0}(x_{n}))=1
.

(5)

f_{1},\dots,f_{n}
have no solutions at
\infty
.

The case
n=2
has one special feature. If
f_{1},f_{2}\in\mathbb{K}[x_{1},x_{2}]
, then their leading coefficients with respect to
x_{1}
are polynomials in
x_{2}
. In Section
4
, we will show that
f_{1},f_{2}
have no solutions at
\infty
if and only if these leading coefficients are relatively prime in
\mathbb{K}[x_{2}]
. Thus solutions at
\infty
are easy to detect when
n=2
.

Let us revisit Example
1.1
in light of what we now know:

Example 1.2
.

For
f_{1}=x_{1}^{2}-x_{2}-1,\ f_{2}=x_{1}^{2}+x_{1}x_{2}-2\in\mathbb{C}[x_{1},x_{2}]
, we began Example
1.1
by computing that
I=\langle f_{1},f_{2}\rangle=\langle x_{1}^{2}-x_{2}-1,x_{1}^{2}+x_{1}x_{2}-2\rangle
.
Thus
I
has a Shape Lemma. Also, the leading coefficients of
f_{1},f_{2}
with respect to
x_{1}
are both equal to
1
, which implies that there are no solutions at
\infty
. By Theorem
1.3
, we immediately conclude that
I=\langle R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{2}),p_{1}(x_{1},x_{2})\rangle
and
I\cap\mathbb{C}[x_{2}]=\langle R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{2})\rangle
.

In closing, we mention that the results of this paper can be modified to apply when
\mathbb{K}
is an arbitrary field. For example, in Theorems
1.2
,
1.3
and
1.4
, injectivity means that projection induces an injection on points over
\bar{\mathbb{K}}
, and Theorem
1.1
needs to be formulated in terms of the irreducible polynomials that define the image of
\mathbf{V}(f_{1}^{h},\dots,f_{n}^{h})\rightarrow\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{K}}^{1}
. We prefer to assume that
\mathbb{K}
is algebraically closed since this makes the relation between the algebra and the geometry easier to see.

Previous Work

The representation of algebraic varieties by polynomials having a “Shape Lemma” has a long history in Computational Algebra. In 1826, Abel solved
\chi(x,y)=\theta(x,y)=0
by eliminating
y
via a Poisson formula and then expressing
y
as a rational function of
x
(see
[
Abel81
, p. 148]
). A more general version of this idea is due to Kronecker (see the introduction of
[
GLS01
]
for a reference and more history). In
[
Can88
]
,
u
-resultants are used to compute this representation, while
[
GLS01
]
introduces geometric resolutions of varieties to simplify the computations. In this representation, the focus is on equations for the variety
\mathbf{V}(I)
and not on the ideal
I
itself, so these results are limited to radical ideals with points in some kind of general position (see for instance
[
BU99
, Theorem 7.4]
). In
[
Rou99
]
, a “Rational Univariate Representation,” which also takes into account the multiplicities of the points, is introduced and studied from a computational point of view. A generalization to a sparse RUR can be found in
[
MST17
]
.

Our current conception of the Shape Lemma began with
[
GM89
]
for a radical zero-dimensional ideal, though the name “Shape Lemma” came later. The history of the Shape Lemma is discussed in
[
BMMT94
]
, which also characterizes ideals having a Shape Lemma in terms of the geometry of the points in
\mathbf{V}(I)
. We will review and use some of their results in Section
2
. Also, comparing the monic generator of
I\cap\mathbb{K}[x_{n}]
with
R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{n})
is a classical exercise in basic Computational Algebra, see for instance Exercise 3 of Chapter 3, §6 in the first three editions of
[
CLO07
]
. It is clear that both multiplicities and roots at infinity play a decisive role here. In
[
MRZ16
]
, directional multiplicities are used to explain the differences between the degrees of these two polynomials. In
[
GRZ13
]
, the connection between the elimination ideal and univariate resultants of two generators is explored.

The use of subresultants for the Shape Lemma has been done already by Habicht in
[
Hab48
]
. In
[
GV91
]
, this method is explained and used to produce another Gröbner-free/resultant-friendly computation of the Shape Lemma for a radical ideal with points in general position. In a different context (overdetermined
n
homogeneous polynomials in
n
variables),
[
Sza08
]
uses multivariable subresultants to describe the roots of a polynomial system.

As already noted, Poisson-type formulæ for resultants are important tools for solving polynomial systems. For the classical homogeneous case, this goes back to Poisson in 1802. See
[
Jou91
, Proposition 2.7]
for the presentation of this formula for generic polynomials. Whether or not one can apply Poisson to a given polynomial system depends on where the solutions are. For the classical resultant, such a formula is valid if there are no solutions at infinity. In
[
DS15
, Theorem 1.1]
, Poisson has been extended to sparse resultants and its validity has been shown for systems having all of their roots in
(\mathbb{K}^{\times})^{n}
(no solutions at any infinity of the associated toric variety).

2.
The Shape Lemma

As in Section
1
, a zero-dimensional ideal
I\subseteq\mathbb{K}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
has a Shape Lemma if it is of the form
I=\langle r(x_{n}),x_{1}-g_{1}(x_{n}),\dots,x_{n-1}-g_{n-1}(x_{n})\rangle
. We write the generators in this order because to find the solutions, one first solves
r(x_{n})=0
and then uses the roots
\xi_{n}
to find the coordinates
\xi_{1}=g_{1}(\xi_{n}),\dots,\xi_{n-1}=g_{n-1}(\xi_{n})
of the solution
\xi=(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n})
.

Here we recall some basic facts about the Shape Lemma, following
[
BMMT94
]
. (Although
[
BMMT94
]
assumes characteristic zero, this assumption is not used in the results we cite from their paper.). We say that a point
\xi
of a zero-dimensional scheme over
\mathbb{K}
is
curvilinear
if it is either smooth or has a one-dimensional Zariski tangent space. See
[
Cox05
]
for more on curvilinear singularities.

We begin with a lemma that combines several results from
[
BMMT94
]
:

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
I=\langle r(x_{n}),x_{1}-g_{1}(x_{n}),\dots,x_{n-1}-g_{n-1}(x_{n})\rangle\subseteq\mathbb{K}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
have a Shape Lemma. Then the map

\mathbf{V}(I)\subseteq\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{K}}^{n}\mathrel{\mathchoice{{}\hbox{$\displaystyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\textstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle{\meno}$}}}\mathrel{\mkern-3.0mu}\rightarrow\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{K}}^{1}

given by projection onto the
x_{n}
-axis is injective as a map of sets. Furthermore, for every point
\xi=(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n})\in\mathbf{V}(I)
, we have
:

(1)

\xi
is curvilinear.

(2)

The Hilbert-Samuel multiplicity of
\xi\in\mathbf{V}(I)
equals the length of the
local ring
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{V}(I),\xi}
.

(3)

The Hilbert-Samuel multiplicity of
\xi\in\mathbf{V}(I)

equals the multiplicity of
\xi_{n}
as a root of
r(x_{n})
.

(4)

The projection
\mathbf{V}(I)\rightarrow\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{K}}^{1}
induces an isomorphism of Zariski tangent spaces

T_{\xi}(\mathbf{V}(I))\xrightarrow{\,\sim\,}T_{\xi_{n}}(\mathbf{V}(r)).

Proof.

For the projection
\mathbf{V}(I)\rightarrow\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{K}}^{1}
, note that
x_{i}-g_{i}(x_{n})\in I
implies that for
i=1,\dots,n-1
, the
i
th coordinate of a point in
\mathbf{V}(I)
is determined by its
n
th coordinate. Injectivity follows immediately. Also note that (2) is true since
I
is a complete intersection. It remains to prove (1), (3) and (4).

Since
I\cap\mathbb{K}[x_{n}]=\langle r(x_{n})\rangle
, we have an injection

(2.1)

\mathbb{K}[x_{n}]/\langle r(x_{n})\rangle\lhook\mathrel{\mkern-3.0mu}\mathrel{\mathchoice{{}\hbox{$\displaystyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\textstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle{\meno}$}}}\mathrel{\mkern-3.0mu}\rightarrow\mathbb{K}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]/I,

which is onto since
[x_{i}]=[g_{i}(x_{n})]
in
\mathbb{K}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]/I
for
i=1,\dots,n-1
. Thus (
2.1
) is an isomorphism. Write
r(x_{n})=\prod_{j=1}^{N}(x_{n}-\xi_{n}^{(j)})^{e_{j}}
with distinct
\xi_{n}^{(j)}
(we may assume that
r(x_{n})
is monic). Then the points of
\mathbf{V}(I)
are given by
\xi^{(j)}=(\xi_{1}^{(j)},\xi_{2}^{(j)},\dots,\xi_{n}^{(j)})
for
j=1,\dots,N
, where
\xi_{i}^{(j)}=g_{i}(\xi_{n}^{(j)})
for
i=1,\dots,n-1
. Using the isomorphism (
2.1
), we obtain

\mathbb{K}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]/I\simeq\mathbb{K}[x_{n}]/\langle r(x_{n})\rangle\simeq\prod_{j=1}^{N}\mathbb{K}[x_{n}]/\langle(x_{n}-\xi_{n}^{(j)})^{e_{j}}\rangle.

This expresses
\mathbb{K}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]/I
as a product of local rings, and in particular, the local ring
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{V}(I),\xi^{(j)}}\simeq(\mathbb{K}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]/I)_{\xi^{(j)}}
is isomorphic to
\mathbb{K}[x_{n}]/\langle(x_{n}-\xi_{n}^{(j)})^{e_{j}}\rangle
via projection onto the
x_{n}
-axis. This implies that
\xi^{(j)}
is curvilinear and its Hilbert-Samuel multiplicity, which equals the length of
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{V}(I),\xi}
by (2), is simply the multiplicity of
\xi_{n}^{(j)}
as a root of
r(x_{n})
. This proves assertion (3).

Finally, since
(\mathbb{K}[x_{1}\dots,x_{n}]/I)_{\xi^{(j)}}\simeq\mathbb{K}[x_{n}]/\langle(x_{n}-\xi_{n}^{(j)})^{e_{j}}\rangle
is induced by the projection, we get an induced isomorphism on Zariski tangent spaces since the Zariski tangent space of a local ring with maximal ideal
\mathfrak{m}
is
\mathfrak{m}/\mathfrak{m}^{2}
. This proves assertions (1) and (4).
∎

The converse of Lemma
2.1
is true for a zero-dimensional ideal
I
, namely if the projection onto the
x_{n}
-axis is injective as a map of sets and conditions (1)–(4) of the lemma are satisfied for
I\cap\mathbb{K}[x_{n}]=\langle r(x_{n})\rangle
, then
I
has a Shape Lemma. In fact, we have a slightly stronger result as follows:

Lemma 2.2
.

Suppose that
I\subseteq\mathbb{K}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
is a zero-dimensional ideal and let
I\cap\mathbb{K}[x_{n}]=\langle r(x_{n})\rangle
. If the projection
\mathbf{V}(I)\rightarrow\mathbb{A}^{1}_{\mathbb{K}}
onto the
x_{n}
-axis is injective as a map of sets, then the following conditions are equivalent
:

(1)

For every
\xi=(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n})\in\mathbf{V}(I)
, the Hilbert-Samuel multiplicity of
\xi
equals the multiplicity of
\xi_{n}
as a root of
r(x_{n})
.

(2)

For every
\xi=(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n})\in\mathbf{V}(I)
, the length of
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{V}(I),\xi}
equals the multiplicity of
\xi_{n}
as a root of
r(x_{n})
.

(3)

For every
\xi=(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n})\in\mathbf{V}(I)
, the induced map on Zariski tangent spaces
T_{\xi}(\mathbf{V}(I))\rightarrow T_{\xi_{n}}(\mathbf{V}(r))
is an isomorphism.

(4)

I
has a Shape Lemma.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.1
, (4) implies (1), (2) and (3). It remains to prove that (1), (2) and (3) each imply (4).
Since
I\cap\mathbb{K}[x_{n}]=\langle r(x_{n})\rangle
, we get the injection (
2.1
). Thus, to prove (4), it suffices to show that (
2.1
) is an isomorphism since the elements of
\mathbb{K}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]/I
represented by
x_{1},\dots,x_{n-1}
would be in the image of (
2.1
). This would give the desired
g_{1}(x_{n}),\dots,g_{n-1}(x_{n})
.

Let
\mathbf{V}(I)=\{\xi^{(1)},\dots,\xi^{(N)}\}
with
\xi^{(j)}=(\xi_{1}^{(j)},\dots,\xi_{n}^{(j)})
, and write

(2.2)

\mathbb{K}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]/I=\prod_{j=1}^{N}\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{V}(I),\xi^{(j)}}.

Let
m_{j}
be the Hilbert-Samuel multiplicity of
\xi^{(j)}
and
k_{j}
be the length
\dim_{\mathbb{K}}\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{V}(I),\xi^{(j)}}
of
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{V}(I),\xi^{(j)}}
. Then
\dim_{\mathbb{K}}\mathbb{K}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]/I=\sum_{j=1}^{N}k_{j}
, and since
k_{j}\leq m_{j}
for all
j
, the injection (
2.1
) implies

(2.3)

\begin{array}[]{c}\begin{aligned} \deg(r(x_{n}))&=\dim_{\mathbb{K}}\mathbb{K}[x_{n}]/\langle r(x_{n})\rangle\\
&\leq\dim_{\mathbb{K}}\mathbb{K}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]/I=\sum_{j=1}^{N}k_{j}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{N}m_{j}.\end{aligned}\end{array}

Now assume that (1) is true. Since
r(x_{n})\in I
and
\xi^{(j)}=(\xi_{1}^{(j)},\dots,\xi_{n}^{(j)})
, we have
r(\xi_{n}^{(j)})=0
, and then assumption (1) implies that
\xi_{n}^{(j)}
is a root of multiplicity
m_{j}
of
r(x_{n})
. Thus
(x_{n}-\xi_{n}^{(j)})^{m_{j}}
divides
r(x_{n})
. But the
\xi_{n}^{(j)}
are distinct by our injectivity assumption, so that
\prod_{j=1}^{N}(x_{n}-\xi_{n}^{(j)})^{m_{j}}
divides
r(x_{n})
. Thus

\deg(r(x_{n}))\geq\sum_{j=1}^{N}m_{j},

which is an equality by virtue of (
2.3
). Thus (
2.1
) is an injection where both source and target have the same dimension, proving that it is an isomorphism.

Similarly, if (2) is true, then
\xi_{n}^{(j)}
is a root of multiplicity
k_{j}
of
r(x_{n})
, so that
\deg(r(x_{n}))\geq\sum_{j=1}^{N}k_{j}
. Using (
2.3
) as in the previous paragraph, we again see that (
2.1
) is an isomorphism.

Finally, assume that (3) is true. Then Proposition 5 of
[
BMMT94
]
implies that
I
has a Shape Lemma. For completeness, we sketch the proof. Write
I
as the intersection of primary ideals
Q_{j}
whose radicals are the maximal ideals of the
\xi^{(j)}
. The isomorphism on Zariski tangent spaces implies that
\xi^{(j)}
is curvilinear. Also, with
\ell_{1}=x_{n}-\xi_{n}^{(j)}
and
\ell_{i}=x_{i}
for
i=1,\dots,{n-1}
,
[
BMMT94
, Proposition 4(10)]
implies that

Q_{j}=\langle(x_{n}-\xi_{n}^{(j)})^{m_{j}},x_{i}-g_{ij}(x_{n}),\,i=1,\dots,n-1\rangle,\text{ where }g_{ij}(x_{n})\in\mathbb{K}[x_{n}].

It follows that
Q_{j}\cap\mathbb{K}[x_{n}]=\langle(x_{n}-\xi_{n}^{(j)})^{m_{j}}\rangle
. Since
r(x_{n})\in I\cap\mathbb{K}[x_{n}]\subseteq Q_{j}\cap\mathbb{K}[x_{n}]
, we see that
(x_{n}-\xi_{n}^{(j)})^{m_{j}}
divides
r(x_{n})
. From here, the proof of (1)
\Rightarrow
(3) shows that (
2.1
) is an isomorphism.
∎

Our final lemma uses the scheme-theoretic fibers of the projection morphism
\mathbf{V}(I)\rightarrow\mathbb{A}^{1}_{\mathbb{K}}
to characterize when
I
has a Shape Lemma. Since
\mathbb{K}
is algebraically closed, a closed point
\mathfrak{p}\in\mathrm{Spec}(A)=\mathrm{Spec}(\mathbb{K}[x_{n}])=\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{K}}^{1}
can be written as
\mathfrak{p}=\langle x_{n}-\lambda\rangle
for some
\lambda\in\mathbb{K}
. For simplicity, set
V=\mathbf{V}(I)
. Then the scheme-theoretic fiber
V_{\mathfrak{p}}
is the subscheme of
V=\mathrm{Spec}(\mathbb{K}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]/I)
defined as follows. When
V
is finite, the decomposition (
2.2
) can be written

\mathbb{K}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]/I\simeq\prod_{\xi\in V}\mathcal{O}_{V,\xi}.

Take
\xi=(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n})\in V
. If
\xi_{n}\neq\lambda
, then
x_{n}-\lambda
is nonvanishing at
\xi
and hence invertible in
\mathcal{O}_{V,\xi}
. Thus
\mathfrak{p}\mathcal{O}_{V,\xi}=\mathcal{O}_{V,\xi}
when
\xi_{n}\neq\lambda
. It follows that the fiber above
\mathfrak{p}
is given by

(2.4)

V_{\mathfrak{p}}=\mathrm{Spec}(A/\mathfrak{p}\otimes_{A}\mathbb{K}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]/I)=\mathrm{Spec}\bigg(\!\prod_{\xi\in V,\,\xi_{n}=\lambda}\!\!\mathcal{O}_{V,\xi}/\mathfrak{p}\mathcal{O}_{V,\xi}\bigg).

Lemma 2.3
.

The zero-dimensional ideal
I
has a Shape Lemma if an only if for all closed points
\mathfrak{p}\in\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{K}}^{1}
, the fiber
V_{\mathfrak{p}}
is either empty or consists of a single reduced point.

Proof.

First suppose that
I
has a Shape Lemma and take
\mathfrak{p}=\langle x_{n}-\lambda\rangle\subseteq\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{K}}^{1}
as above. If
\mathfrak{p}
is not in the image of
V\rightarrow\mathbb{A}^{1}_{\mathbb{K}}
, then
\lambda\neq\xi_{n}
for all
\xi\in V
, so that the fiber
V_{\mathfrak{p}}
is empty by (
2.4
). On the other hand, if
\mathfrak{p}
is in the image of
V\rightarrow\mathbb{A}^{1}_{\mathbb{K}}
, there is a unique point
\xi\in\mathbf{V}
with
\xi_{n}=\lambda
since
V\rightarrow\mathbb{A}^{1}_{\mathbb{K}}
is injective as a map of sets by Lemma
2.1
. Then the fiber (
2.4
) becomes
V_{\mathfrak{p}}=\mathrm{Spec}(\!\mathcal{O}_{V,\xi}/\mathfrak{p}\mathcal{O}_{V,\xi})
. The proof of Lemma
2.1
implies that

\mathcal{O}_{V,\xi}\simeq\mathbb{K}[x_{n}]/\langle(x_{n}-\lambda)^{m_{\xi}}\rangle.

The maximal ideal of
\mathbb{K}[x_{n}]/\langle(x_{n}-\lambda)^{m_{\xi}}\rangle
is generated by the image of
x_{n}-\lambda
, so the same is true for
\mathcal{O}_{V,\xi}
, so that
\mathfrak{p}\mathcal{O}_{V,\xi}
is the maximal ideal of
\mathcal{O}_{V,\xi}
. Thus
\!\mathcal{O}_{V,\xi}/\mathfrak{p}\mathcal{O}_{V,\xi}\simeq\mathbb{K}
, which proves that
V_{\mathfrak{p}}
consists of a single reduced point.

Conversely, suppose that every fiber
V_{\mathfrak{p}}
is either empty or consists of a single reduced point. If
V_{\mathfrak{p}}
is nonempty, there is
\xi\in V
with
x_{n}=\lambda
. Since
x_{n}-\lambda
vanishes at
\xi
, it gives an element of the maximal ideal
\mathfrak{m}_{\xi}\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{V,\xi}
. Hence
\mathfrak{p}\mathcal{O}_{V,\xi}\subseteq\mathfrak{m}_{\xi}
, which proves that
\mathcal{O}_{V,\xi}/\mathfrak{p}\mathcal{O}_{V,\xi}
is a nonzero ring. Since
V_{\mathfrak{p}}
consists of a single reduced point, it follows that there is a unique point
\xi\in V
that maps to
\mathfrak{p}
. We conclude that
V\rightarrow\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{K}}^{1}
is injective as map of sets.

Let us examine further the case when
V_{\mathfrak{p}}
consists of a single reduced point. This means
\mathcal{O}_{V,\xi}/\mathfrak{p}\mathcal{O}_{V,\xi}\simeq\mathbb{K}
, so that
\mathfrak{p}\mathcal{O}_{V,\xi}
equals the maximal ideal
\mathfrak{m}_{\xi}\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{V,\xi}
. Let
I\cap\mathbb{K}[x_{n}]=\langle r(x_{n})\rangle
with
r(x_{n})
monic, and write
r(x_{n})=\prod_{\xi\in V}(x_{n}-\xi_{n})^{e_{\xi}}
. Then for
\xi\in V
with
\xi_{n}=\lambda
the injection (
2.1
) gives an injection of local rings

\varphi:\mathbb{K}[x_{n}]/\langle(x_{n}-\lambda)^{e_{\xi}}\rangle\lhook\mathrel{\mkern-3.0mu}\mathrel{\mathchoice{{}\hbox{$\displaystyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\textstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle{\meno}$}}}\mathrel{\mkern-3.0mu}\rightarrow\mathcal{O}_{V,\xi}.

Once we prove that
\varphi
is an isomorphism for all
\xi\in V
, Lemma
2.2
will imply that
I
has a Shape Lemma. It suffices so show that
\varphi
is surjective. We will regard
\varphi
as an inclusion

\mathcal{R}_{\xi}:=\mathbb{K}[x_{n}]/\langle(x_{n}-\lambda)^{e_{\xi}}\rangle\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{V,\xi}.

Then
M=\mathcal{O}_{V,\xi}/\mathcal{R}_{\xi}
is a finitely generated
\mathcal{R}
-module, and we have
M/\mathfrak{p}M=0

since
\mathcal{R}_{\xi}/\mathfrak{p}\mathcal{R}_{\xi}\simeq\mathcal{O}_{V,\xi}/\mathfrak{p}\mathcal{O}_{V,\xi}\simeq\mathbb{K}
. Thus
M=0
by Nakayama’s Lemma. We conclude that
\mathcal{R}_{\xi}=\mathcal{O}_{V,\xi}
, which proves that
\varphi
is surjective.
∎

3.
A Resultant Formula

The goal of this section is to prove Theorem
1.1
. The resultant
R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{n})
featured in this theorem uses the classical multivariable resultant. Recall that if
A
is an integral domain and
g_{1},\ldots,g_{n}\in A[x_{0},\ldots,x_{n-1}]
are homogeneous of respective degrees
d_{1},\ldots,d_{n}
, their resultant
\mathrm{Res}_{d_{1},\ldots,d_{n}}(g_{1},\ldots,g_{n})\in A
vanishes if and only if the system
g_{1}=\ldots=g_{n}=0
has a solution in
\mathbb{P}_{\overline{k(A})}^{n-1}
. where
\overline{k(A)}
is the algebraic closure of the field of fractions of
A
. The general theory of resultants is developed in
[
Jou91
]
. See also
[
CLO05
, Chapter 3]
when
A=\mathbb{C}
.

As in Section
1
, we regard
f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}\in\mathbb{K}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
as lying in
A[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n-1}]
with
A=\mathbb{K}[x_{n}]
and let
f_{1}^{h},\ldots,f_{n}^{h}\in A[x_{0},x_{1},\ldots,x_{n-1}]
be their homogenizations with respect to the new variable
x_{0}
up to degrees
d_{1},\ldots,d_{n}
respectively, with
d_{i}=\deg_{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n-1}}(f_{i})
for
i=1,\dots,n
. Then

R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{n}):=\mathrm{Res}_{d_{1},\ldots,d_{n}}(f_{1}^{h},\ldots,f_{n}^{h})\in\mathbb{K}[x_{n}].

Being homogeneous with respect to
x_{0},\dots,x_{n-1}
, the polynomials
f_{1}^{h},\ldots,f_{n}^{h}
define

\mathbf{V}(f_{1}^{h},\ldots,f_{n}^{h})\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n-1}_{\mathbb{K}}\times_{\mathbb{K}}\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{K}}^{1}.

Theorem
1.1
states that when
\mathbf{V}(f_{1}^{h},\dots,f_{n}^{h})
is finite,

(3.1)

R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{n})=c\!\!\prod_{\xi\in\mathbf{V}(f_{1}^{h},\dots,f_{n}^{h})}\!\!(x_{n}-\xi_{n})^{m_{\xi}}

for some
c\in\mathbb{K}^{\times}
. Here,
\xi=([\xi_{0}\hskip 0.85pt{:}\hskip 0.85pt\ldots\hskip 0.85pt{:}\hskip 0.85pt\xi_{n-1}],\xi_{n})\in\mathbb{P}^{n-1}_{\mathbb{K}}\times_{\mathbb{K}}\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{K}}^{1}
, and
m_{\xi}
is the Hilbert-Samuel multiplicity of
\xi
.

Our proof of (
3.1
) will use some results from
[
SS96
]
. We will need the following algebraic characterization of when
\mathbf{V}(f_{1}^{h},\dots,f_{n}^{h})
is finite:

Proposition 3.1
.

V=\mathbf{V}(f_{1}^{h},\dots,f_{n}^{h})\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n-1}_{\mathbb{K}}\times_{\mathbb{K}}\mathbb{A}^{1}_{\mathbb{K}}
is finite if and only if
f_{1}^{h},\dots,f_{n}^{h}
form a regular sequence in
A[x_{0},x_{1},\dots,x_{n-1}]
.

Proof.

If
f_{1}^{h},\dots,f_{n}^{h}
form a regular sequence, then for each
i=0,\dots,n-1
, their dehomogenizations in
\mathbb{K}[x_{0},\dots,\hat{x}_{i},\dots,x_{n-1},x_{n}]
form a regular sequence and hence define a zero-dimensional subscheme of
U_{i}\times_{\mathbb{K}}\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{K}}^{1}
, where
U_{i}\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n-1}_{\mathbb{K}}
is the affine open defined by
x_{i}\neq 0
. The
U_{i}
cover
\mathbb{P}^{n-1}_{\mathbb{K}}
, which proves that
V
is finite.

Conversely, assume that
V
is finite. Let
A_{\mathfrak{m}}
be the localization of
A=\mathbb{K}[x_{n}]
at a maximal ideal
\mathfrak{m}\subseteq A
. It suffices to prove that the homogeneous elements
f_{1}^{h},\dots,f_{n}^{h}
form a regular sequence in
A_{\mathfrak{m}}[x_{0},\dots,x_{n-1}]
. To prove this, let
I_{\mathfrak{m}}\subseteq A_{\mathfrak{m}}[x_{0},\dots,x_{n-1}]
be generated by
f_{1}^{h},\dots,f_{n}^{h}
. Then
\mathbf{V}(I_{\mathfrak{m}})\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n-1}_{\mathbb{K}}\times_{\mathbb{K}}\mathrm{Spec}(A_{\mathfrak{m}})
. Since
V=\mathbf{V}(f_{1}^{h},\dots,f_{n}^{h})\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n-1}_{\mathbb{K}}\times_{\mathbb{K}}\mathbb{A}^{1}_{\mathbb{K}}
is finite,
\mathbf{V}(I_{\mathfrak{m}})
is either empty, or nonempty and finite. We consider each case separately.

First suppose that
\mathbf{V}(I_{\mathfrak{m}})=\emptyset
. We claim that
A_{\mathfrak{m}}[x_{0},\dots,x_{n-1}]/I_{\mathfrak{m}}
is finite over
A_{m}
. This is easy, since
\mathbf{V}(I_{\mathfrak{m}})\subseteq\mathrm{Proj}(A_{\mathfrak{m}}[x_{0},\dots,x_{n-1}])
satisfies

\mathbf{V}(I_{\mathfrak{m}})=\emptyset\iff\langle x_{0},\dots,x_{n-1}\rangle\subseteq\sqrt{I_{\mathfrak{m}}}\iff\langle x_{0},\dots,x_{n-1}\rangle^{N}\subseteq I_{\mathfrak{m}}\ \text{for some}\ N

by standard properties of
\mathrm{Proj}
. If follows that
A_{\mathfrak{m}}[x_{0},\dots,x_{n-1}]/I_{\mathfrak{m}}
is finite over
A_{m}
when
\mathbf{V}(I_{\mathfrak{m}})=\emptyset
. By (3)
\Rightarrow
(1) of Theorem 7.3 of
[
SS01
]
,
I_{\mathfrak{m}}
is a complete intersection, so that
f_{1}^{h},\dots,f_{n}^{h}
form a regular sequence in
A_{\mathfrak{m}}[x_{0},\dots,x_{n-1}]
.

Next suppose that
\mathbf{V}(I_{\mathfrak{m}})
is nonempty and finite. Here, we will use the theory of
∗
local rings from
[
BH93
, Section 1.5]
. A proper homogeneous ideal is a graded ring
R
is
∗
maximal
if the only strictly larger homogenous ideal is
R
itself, and
R
is
∗
local
if it has a unique
∗
maximal ideal. The basic idea is that
∗
local rings are the graded counterparts of local rings.

In our situation,
R=A_{\mathfrak{m}}[x_{0},\dots,x_{n-1}]
. Since
A_{\mathfrak{m}}
is a local ring with maximal ideal
\mathfrak{m}A_{\mathfrak{m}}
, it is easy to see that
R
is
∗
local with
∗
maximal ideal
\mathfrak{M}=\mathfrak{m}A_{\mathfrak{m}}+\langle x_{0},\dots,x_{n-1}\rangle
. The fact that
\mathfrak{M}
is maximal in the usual sense simplifies some features of the theory. For example, the
∗
dimension
of
R
is defined to be the height of the
∗
maximal ideal
\mathfrak{M}
. Since
\mathfrak{M}
is maximal, this is just the dimension of
R
, so that
{}^{*}\!\dim R=\dim R=n+1
.

The quotient
R/I_{\mathfrak{m}}
is also
∗
local, and its
∗
maximal ideal is again maximal in the usual sense. Thus
{}^{*}\!\dim R/I_{\mathfrak{m}}=\dim R/I_{\mathfrak{m}}=1
since
V(I_{\mathfrak{m}})\subseteq\mathrm{Proj}(R)
is finite and nonempty. Hence

(3.2)

{}^{*}\!\dim R/I_{\mathfrak{m}}=1=(n+1)-n={}^{*}\!\dim R-n.

However, we also know that
R
is Cohen-Macaulay and that
I_{\mathfrak{m}}
is generated by
n
elements. If we replace
R
with a Cohen-Macaulay local ring and
∗
\dim
by
\dim
, then it is well known that (
3.2
) implies that the
n
generators form a regular sequence (for example, this follows from Theorem 2.12 and Proposition A.4 of
[
BH93
]
). Since the same result holds in the
∗
local setting, we conclude that
f_{1}^{h},\dots,f_{n}^{h}
form a regular sequence in
R
, as desired.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

We need to prove (
3.1
). The finiteness of
V=\mathbf{V}(f_{1}^{h},\dots,f_{n}^{h})
implies that the resultant
R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{n})
is nonzero and hence can be written

R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{n})=c\!\!\!\!\prod_{\lambda\in\mathbf{V}(R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{n}))}\!\!\!\!(x_{n}-\lambda)^{e_{\lambda}}

for integers
e_{\lambda}\geq 1
and a nonzero constant
c\in\mathbb{K}
.

We know that
R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{n})
vanishes at every point of
V
, and since
\mathbb{K}
is algebraically closed, the universal property of the resultant implies that any root
\lambda
of
R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{n})
comes from a solution
\xi=([\xi_{0}\hskip 0.85pt{:}\hskip 0.85pt\ldots\hskip 0.85pt{:}\hskip 0.85pt\xi_{n-1}],\xi_{n})\in V
with
\xi_{n}=\lambda
. Thus (
3.1
) will follow once we prove that

(3.3)

e_{\lambda}=\!\!\sum_{\xi\in V,\,\xi_{n}=\lambda}\!\!m_{\xi},

where
m_{\xi}
is the Hilbert-Samuel multiplicity of
\xi
. The sum on the right is finite since
V
is finite by assumption.

Since
V
is finite, Proposition
3.1
implies that
f_{1}^{h},\dots,f_{n}^{h}
form a regular sequence in
A[x_{0},\dots,x_{n-1}]
. This allows us to use the results of
[
SS96
]
. The
resultant ideal

\mathfrak{R}\subseteq A
is generated by
R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{n})
. We also have the
A
-algebra of global sections

B=\Gamma(V,\mathcal{O}_{V})=\prod_{\xi\in V}\mathcal{O}_{V,\xi}.

Note that
V=\mathrm{Spec}(B)
.

A root
\lambda\in\mathbb{K}
of
R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{n})
gives a point
\mathfrak{p}\in\mathrm{Spec}(A)
for
\mathfrak{p}=\langle x_{n}-\lambda\rangle\subseteq A
. Localizing the
A
-module
B
at
\mathfrak{p}
gives

B_{\mathfrak{p}}=\!\!\prod_{\xi\in V,\,\xi_{n}=\lambda}\!\!\mathcal{O}_{V,\xi}.

In the local ring
A_{\mathfrak{p}}
, the resultant ideal
\mathfrak{R}=\langle R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{n})\rangle
localizes to
\mathfrak{R}_{\mathfrak{p}}=\langle(x_{n}-\lambda)^{e_{\lambda}}\rangle
. Thus

A_{\mathfrak{p}}/\mathfrak{R}_{\mathfrak{p}}=A_{\mathfrak{p}}/\langle(x_{n}-\lambda)^{e_{\lambda}}\rangle.

Since
\mathfrak{p}
has codimension 1,
[
SS96
, Theorem 2.1]
implies that
\mathrm{length}(B_{\mathfrak{p}})=\mathrm{length}(A_{\mathfrak{p}}/\mathfrak{R}_{\mathfrak{p}})
. Combining this with the previous paragraph, we obtain

\displaystyle\!\!\sum_{\xi\in V,\,\xi_{n}=\lambda}\!\!\mathrm{length}(\mathcal{O}_{V,\xi})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{length}(B_{\mathfrak{p}})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{length}(A_{\mathfrak{p}}/\mathfrak{R}_{\mathfrak{p}})=\mathrm{length}(A_{\mathfrak{p}}/\langle(x_{n}-\lambda)^{e_{\lambda}}\rangle)=e_{\lambda}.

Since
V=\mathbf{V}(f_{1}^{h},\dots,f_{n}^{h})
is a local complete intersection, the length of
\mathcal{O}_{V,\xi}
is the Hilbert-Samuel multiplicity
m_{\xi}
, and (
3.3
) follows immediately. This completes the proof of Theorem
1.1
.
∎

Remark 3.1
.

The proof just given uses the localization
B_{\mathfrak{p}}
to express the exponent
e_{\lambda}
as a sum of Hilbert-Samuel multiplicities. It is natural to ask how this relates to the fiber
V_{\mathfrak{p}}
of the projection
V\hookrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{n-1}_{\mathbb{K}}\times_{\mathbb{K}}\mathbb{A}^{1}_{\mathbb{K}}\rightarrow\mathbb{A}^{1}_{\mathbb{K}}
. Since
V=\mathrm{Spec}(B)
, the fiber above the maximal ideal
\mathfrak{p}=\langle x_{n}-\lambda\rangle\in\mathrm{Spec}(A)=\mathbb{A}^{1}_{\mathbb{K}}
is the affine scheme

V_{\mathfrak{p}}=\mathrm{Spec}(A/\mathfrak{p}\otimes_{A}B)=\mathrm{Spec}(B/\mathfrak{p}B)=\mathrm{Spec}\bigg(\!\prod_{\xi\in V,\,\xi_{n}=\lambda}\!\!\mathcal{O}_{V,\xi}/\mathfrak{p}\mathcal{O}_{V,\xi}\bigg)

since
\mathfrak{p}\mathcal{O}_{V,\xi}=\mathcal{O}_{V,\xi}
when
\xi_{n}\neq\lambda
. Thus

\deg V_{\mathfrak{p}}=\mathrm{length}(B/\mathfrak{p}B)=\!\!\sum_{\xi\in V,\,\xi_{n}=\lambda}\!\!\mathrm{length}(\mathcal{O}_{V,\xi}/\mathfrak{p}\mathcal{O}_{V,\xi})\leq\!\!\sum_{\xi\in V,\,\xi_{n}=\lambda}\!\!\mathrm{length}(\mathcal{O}_{V,\xi}).

Hence the degree of the fiber is bounded above by the sum of the lengths of the local rings, and it is easy to see that equality holds if and only if
\mathfrak{p}B_{\mathfrak{p}}=0
(see
[
SS96
, Theorem 2.6]
).

For an example where
\mathfrak{p}B_{\mathfrak{p}}\neq 0
, consider
f_{1}=x_{1}^{2}-x_{2}^{2}
and
f_{2}=x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}
in
\mathbb{C}[x_{1},x_{2}]
. These homogenize to
f_{1}^{h}=x_{1}^{2}-x_{0}^{2}x_{2}^{2}
and
f_{2}^{h}=x_{1}^{2}+x_{0}^{2}x_{2}^{2}
in
A[x_{0},x_{1}]
,
A=\mathbb{C}[x_{2}]
, with resultant
R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{2})=4x_{2}^{4}
. To analyze the scheme
V=\mathbf{V}(f_{1}^{h},f_{2}^{h})
, note that the equations
x_{1}^{2}-x_{0}^{2}x_{2}^{2}=x_{1}^{2}+x_{0}^{2}x_{2}^{2}=0
imply that
x_{1}=0
, so that
x_{0}\neq 0
since
x_{0},x_{1}
are homogeneous coordinates for
\mathbb{P}^{1}_{\mathbb{K}}
. Thus, there are no solutions at
\infty
, and since
\langle f_{1},f_{2}\rangle=\langle x_{1}^{2},x_{2}^{2}\rangle
, we obtain

V=\mathbf{V}(f_{1},f_{2})=\mathrm{Spec}(B),\quad B=\mathbb{C}[x_{1},x_{2}]/\langle x_{1}^{2},x_{2}^{2}\rangle.

Thus
V
consists of a single point of multiplicity
4
. However, for
\mathfrak{p}=\langle x_{2}\rangle\in\mathrm{Spec}(\mathbb{C}[x_{2}])
, we have
B_{\mathfrak{p}}=B
, and the fiber is

V_{\mathfrak{p}}=\mathrm{Spec}(B/\mathfrak{p}B),\quad B/\mathfrak{p}B=B/x_{2}B\simeq\mathbb{C}[x_{1},x_{2}]/\langle x_{1}^{2},x_{2}\rangle,

which consists of a single point of multiplicity
2\neq 4
. The discrepancy arises because
\mathfrak{p}B_{\mathfrak{p}}\neq 0
.

4.
The Elimination Ideal and Resultants

As in Section
1
,
f_{1},\dots,f_{n}\in\mathbb{K}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
give
R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{n})=\mathrm{Res}_{d_{1},\dots,d_{n}}(f_{1}^{h},\dots,f_{n}^{h})
. It is well known that
R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{n})
belongs to the elimination ideal
I\cap\mathbb{K}[x_{n}]
for
I=\langle f_{1},\dots,f_{n}\rangle
. Easy examples show that
R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{n})
does not always generate
I\cap\mathbb{K}[x_{n}]
. Even when the Shape Lemma holds,
R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{n})
may fail to generate
I\cap\mathbb{K}[x_{n}]
, though in this case, Theorem
1.2
to be proved below explains what goes wrong.

For the moment, assume only that
I=\langle f_{1},\dots,f_{n}\rangle
is zero-dimensional, so that
\mathbf{V}(f_{1},\dots,f_{n})\subseteq\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{K}}^{n}
is finite. Let
I\cap\mathbb{K}[x_{n}]=\langle r(x_{n})\rangle
. Then
r(x_{n})\mid R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{n})
, which implies that every root of
r(x_{n})
is also a root of
R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{n})
. It is natural to inquire about the converse, i.e., whether every root of
R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{n})
is also a root of
r(x_{n})
.

So suppose that
\xi_{n}
is a root of
R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{n})
. Then

(4.1)

0=R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(\xi_{n})=\mathrm{Res}_{d_{1},\dots,d_{n}}(f_{1}^{h}(x_{0},\dots,x_{n-1},\xi_{n}),\dots,f_{n}^{h}(x_{0},\dots,x_{n-1},\xi_{n})).

Recall that
d_{i}=\deg_{x_{1},\dots,x_{n-1}}(f_{i})
and
f_{i}^{h}(x_{0},\dots,x_{n})
is homogeneous of degree
d_{i}
in
x_{0},\dots,x_{n-1}
. By the universal property of the multivariable resultant, (
4.1
) implies that the equations

f_{1}^{h}(x_{0},\dots,x_{n-1},\xi_{n})=\cdots=f_{n}^{h}(x_{0},\dots,x_{n-1},\xi_{n})=0

have a nontrivial solutions
(\xi_{0},\dots,\xi_{n-1})\in\mathbb{P}^{n-1}_{\mathbb{K}}
. If
\xi_{0}\neq 0
, then we can assume that
\xi_{0}=1
, and it follows easily that
(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n})\in\mathbf{V}(f_{1},\dots,f_{n})
. Thus
r(\xi_{n})=0
since
r\in I
. On the other hand, if
\xi_{0}=0
, then this solution may cause
R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{n})
to relate poorly to
r(x_{n})
. Here are two simple examples with
n=2
:

Example 4.1
.

Let
f_{1}=1+2x_{1}+x_{2}+2x_{1}x_{2}
and
f_{2}=3+x_{1}+x_{2}+x_{1}x_{2}
in
\mathbb{C}[x_{1},x_{2}]
. One computes that
I=\langle f_{1},f_{2}\rangle=\langle x_{2}+5,x_{1}+\tfrac{1}{2}\rangle
, which has a Shape Lemma. Thus
I\cap\mathbb{K}[x_{2}]=\langle x_{2}+5\rangle
. However,

R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{2})=\mathrm{Res}_{1,1}(f_{1}^{h},f_{2}^{h})=(x_{2}+1)(x_{2}+5),

so that
R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{2})
does not generate
I\cap\mathbb{C}[x_{2}]
because of the extraneous factor of
x_{2}+1
. The reason for this factor is easy to see. Since
f_{1}^{h}(x_{0},x_{1},x_{2})=x_{0}+2x_{1}+x_{0}x_{2}+2x_{1}x_{2}
and
f_{2}^{h}(x_{0},x_{1},x_{2})=3x_{0}+x_{1}+x_{0}x_{2}+x_{1}x_{2}
, we have

\displaystyle f_{1}^{h}(0,x_{1},x_{2})=f_{2}^{h}(0,x_{1},x_{2})=0

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow 2x_{1}+2x_{1}x_{2}=x_{1}+x_{1}x_{2}=0

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow 2x_{1}(1+x_{2})=x_{1}(1+x_{2})=0.

But
x_{0},x_{1}
are homogeneous coordinates, so
x_{0}=0
implies
x_{1}\neq 0
, and then we can assume
x_{1}=1
.
The solution
(x_{0},x_{1},x_{2})=(0,1,-1)
“at
\infty
” accounts for the extraneous factor of
x_{2}+1
in the resultant.

Example 4.2
.

Let
f_{1}=x_{1}^{2}x_{2}+x_{1}+x_{2}+1
and
f_{2}=x_{1}^{3}x_{2}^{2}+x_{1}-x_{2}+1
in
\mathbb{C}[x_{1},x_{2}]
. One computes that

I=\langle f_{1},f_{2}\rangle=\langle x_{2}(x_{2}^{4}-3x_{2}^{3}-x_{2}^{2}-5x_{2}+6),4x_{1}+x_{2}^{4}+3x_{2}^{3}+x_{2}^{2}-x_{2}+4\rangle.

Thus
(-1,0)
is a solution of
f_{1}=f_{2}=0
, and since
x_{2}^{4}-3x_{2}^{3}-x_{2}^{2}-5x_{2}+6
is irreducible over
\mathbb{Q}
, its roots are distinct and give four more solutions. Since
I
has a Shape Lemma, all solutions have multiplicity one by Lemma
2.1
. However,

R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{3})=\mathrm{Res}_{2,3}(f_{1}^{h},f_{2}^{h})=x_{2}^{2}(x_{2}^{4}-3x_{2}^{3}-x_{2}^{2}-5x_{2}+6),

does not generate the elimination ideal
I\cap\mathbb{C}[x_{2}]=\langle x_{2}(x_{2}^{4}-3x_{2}^{3}-x_{2}^{2}-5x_{2}+6)\rangle
. One can check that
(x_{0},x_{1},x_{2})=(0,1,0)
is a solution “at
\infty
.” This explains why the exponent of
x_{2}
in the resultant is strictly bigger than the exponent that appears in the generator of the elimination ideal.

In the setting of this paper, here is precisely what we mean by “at
\infty
”:

Definition 4.1
.

Polynomials
f_{1},\dots,f_{n}\in\mathbb{K}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
have a
solution at
\infty
with respect to
x_{1},\dots,x_{n-1}
if there are
\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n}\in\mathbb{K}
with
(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n-1})\neq(0,\dots,0)
such that the homogenizations
f_{1}^{h},\dots,f_{n}^{h}\in\mathbb{K}[x_{0},\dots,x_{n}]
satisfy

f_{1}^{h}(0,\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n-1},\xi_{n})=\cdots=f_{n}^{h}(0,\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n-1},\xi_{n})=0.

We will write “solution at
\infty
” when the context is clear. Solutions at
\infty

are easy to understand from a geometric point of view. Being homogeneous with respect to
x_{0},x_{1},\dots,x_{n-1}
means that
f_{1}^{h},\dots,f_{n}^{h}
define
\mathbf{V}(f_{1}^{h},\dots,f_{n}^{h})\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n-1}_{\mathbb{K}}\times_{\mathbb{K}}\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{K}}^{1}
, and decomposing
\mathbb{P}^{n-1}_{\mathbb{K}}
into the affine space
\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{K}}^{n-1}
(where
x_{0}\neq 0
) and the hyperplane at
\infty
(where
x_{0}=0
) gives the disjoint union

(4.2)

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}(f_{1}^{h},\dots,f_{n}^{h})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{V}(f_{1},\dots,f_{n})\cup\{\text{solutions with }x_{0}=0\}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{V}(f_{1},\dots,f_{n})\cup\{\text{solutions at }\infty\}.

Recall that Theorem
1.2
states that if
I=\langle f_{1},\dots,f_{n}\rangle
is a zero-dimensional ideal such that projection
\mathbf{V}(I)\rightarrow\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{K}}^{1}
onto the
n
th coordinate is injective as a map of sets, then any two of the following three conditions imply the third:

(1)

I
has a Shape Lemma.

(2)

f_{1},\dots,f_{n}
have no solutions at
\infty
.

(3)

I\cap\mathbb{K}[x_{n}]=\langle R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{n})\rangle
.

In other words, if any one of the above conditions holds, then the other two are equivalent.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

First note that when (1) holds,
I
can be written in the form
\langle r(x_{n}),x_{1}-g_{1}(x_{n}),\dots,x_{n-1}-g_{n-1}(x_{n})\rangle
, which implies
I\cap\mathbb{K}[x_{n}]=\langle r(x_{n})\rangle
. By our injectivity hypothesis, the roots
\xi_{n}
of
r(x_{n})
are indexed by solutions
\xi=(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n})\in\mathbf{V}(I)=\mathbf{V}(f_{1},\dots,f_{n})
, and by Lemma
2.1
, the multiplicity of
\xi_{n}
as a root of
r(x_{n})
is the multiplicity
m_{\xi}
of
\xi
as a solution. Since
r(x_{n})
can be assumed to be monic, we see that (1) allows us to write

(4.3)

r(x_{n})=\prod_{\xi\in\mathbf{V}(f_{1},\dots,f_{n})}\!\!\ (x_{n}-\xi_{n})^{m_{\xi}}.

(1) & (2)
\Rightarrow
(3): By (1),
r(x_{n})
is given by (
4.3
).
Note that
\mathbf{V}(I)
is finite since
I
is zero-dimensional. Combining this with (2) and (
4.2
), we see that
\mathbf{V}(f_{1}^{h},\dots,f_{n}^{h})
is also finite. Then the resultant formula in Theorem
1.1
implies that for some nonzero constant
c
,

R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{n})=c\!\!\prod_{\xi\in\mathbf{V}(f_{1}^{h},\dots,f_{n}^{h})}\!\!\ (x_{n}-\xi_{n})^{m_{\xi}}=c\!\!\prod_{\xi\in\mathbf{V}(f_{1},\dots,f_{n})}\!\!(x_{n}-\xi_{n})^{m_{\xi}}

where the second equality follows since there are no solutions at
\infty
. By (
4.3
),
R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{n})=c\hskip 1.0ptr(x_{n})
, and (3) follows.

(2) & (3)
\Rightarrow
(1): As in the previous paragraph, our hypothesis and (2) imply that
R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{n})=c\prod_{\xi\in\mathbf{V}(f_{1},\dots,f_{n})}(x_{n}-\xi_{n})^{m_{\xi}}
. By (3), this is
r(x_{n})
up to a constant, so that by our injectivity hypothesis, for every
\xi=(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n})\in\mathbf{V}(I)
, the multiplicity of
\xi\in\mathbf{V}(I)
equals the multiplicity of
\xi_{n}
as a root of
r(x_{n})
. Then (1) follows from Lemma
2.2
.

(1) & (3)
\Rightarrow
(2): There are two cases where this implication is easy. First, if there are only finitely many solutions at
\infty
, then the product formula of Theorem
1.1
shows that solutions at infinity contribute factors of positive degree to the resultant, which makes it easy to see that
\deg(R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{n}))>\deg(r(x_{n}))
when there are solutions at
\infty
. A second easy case is when there is a solution at
\infty
that does not lie above the roots of
r(x_{n})
. Here, the universal property of the resultant implies that
R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{n})
has more distinct roots than
r(x_{n})
, so again the two cannot be equal.

However, when there are infinitely many points at
\infty
, all of which lie over roots of
r(x_{n})
(Example
4.3
shows that this can happen), the two previous cases do not apply. Hence we need to take a different approach. We will show that if (1) holds and there is a solution at
\infty
, then
\deg(R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{n}))>\deg(r(x_{n}))
, which implies that
I\cap\mathbb{K}[x_{n}]\neq\langle R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{n})\rangle
. Our proof will use a deformation argument to reduce to the case when
\mathbf{V}(f_{1}^{h},\dots,f_{n}^{h})
is finite

Suppose that there is a point
\xi^{*}=([0\hskip 0.85pt{:}\hskip 0.85pt\xi^{*}_{1}\hskip 0.85pt{:}\hskip 0.85pt\ldots\hskip 0.85pt{:}\hskip 0.85pt\xi^{*}_{n-1}],\xi^{*}_{n})\in\mathbf{V}(f_{1}^{h},\ldots,f_{n}^{h})
. Without loss of generality we may assume
\xi^{*}_{1}\neq 0
. Then define

\displaystyle g_{1}

\displaystyle=x_{0}^{d_{1}}

\displaystyle g_{2}

\displaystyle=(\xi^{*}_{2}x_{1}-\xi^{*}_{1}x_{2})^{d_{2}}

\displaystyle\ \,\vdots

\displaystyle g_{n-1}

\displaystyle=(\xi^{*}_{n-1}x_{1}-\xi^{*}_{1}x_{n-1})^{d_{n-1}}

\displaystyle g_{n}

\displaystyle=x_{1}^{d_{n}}(x_{n}-\xi^{*}_{n}).

Let
\varepsilon
be a new parameter, and consider the deformed system

(4.4)

f^{h}_{i,\varepsilon}:=f^{h}_{i}+\varepsilon\cdot g_{i},\quad i=1,\dots,n,

We will consider solutions of the deformed system over the algebraically closed field
\widehat{\mathbb{K}}
whose elements
\hat{h}
consist of formal series

\hat{h}=\sum_{e\in S}c_{e}\varepsilon^{e}

where
S\subset\mathbb{Q}
is a well-ordered subset depending on
\hat{h}
,
c_{e}\in\mathbb{K}
for all
e\in S
, and
S
has the property that for some positive integer
m
,

mS\subseteq\begin{cases}\mathbb{Z}&\mathrm{ch}(\mathbb{K})=0\\
\bigcup_{k=0}^{\infty}\tfrac{1}{p^{k}}\mathbb{Z}&\mathrm{ch}(\mathbb{K})=p>0.\end{cases}

When
\mathrm{ch}(\mathbb{K})=0
,
\widehat{\mathbb{K}}
is the field of Puiseux series
\bigcup_{m=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{K}((\varepsilon^{1/m}))
, which is known to be algebraically closed. When
\mathrm{ch}(\mathbb{K})=p>0
,
\widehat{\mathbb{K}}
is algebraically closed by
[
Ray68
]
. The paper
[
Ked01
]
describes a smaller algebraically closed field, but we prefer
\widehat{\mathbb{K}}
because it is easier to describe. (References to other proofs that
\widehat{\mathbb{K}}
is algebraically closed can be found in
[
Ked01
]
.)

The solutions of the deformed system give
\mathbf{V}(f_{1,\varepsilon}^{h},\ldots,f_{n,\varepsilon}^{h})\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n-1}_{\widehat{\mathbb{K}}}\times_{\widehat{\mathbb{K}}}\mathbb{A}_{\widehat{\mathbb{K}}}^{1}
. Note that
\xi^{*}=([0\hskip 0.85pt{:}\hskip 0.85pt\xi^{*}_{1}\hskip 0.85pt{:}\hskip 0.85pt\ldots\hskip 0.85pt{:}\hskip 0.85pt\xi^{*}_{n-1}],\xi^{*}_{n})\in\mathbf{V}(f_{1,\varepsilon}^{h},\ldots,f_{n,\varepsilon}^{h})
by the construction of the
f_{i,\varepsilon}^{h}
.

An element
\hat{h}=\sum_{s\in S}c_{s}\varepsilon^{s}\in\widehat{\mathbb{K}}
can be regarded as a generalized Puiseux series.
We often need to take the limit as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
. To explain what this means, suppose that
\hat{h}\neq 0
, so that
\hat{h}=c_{s}\hskip 1.0pt\varepsilon^{s}+\cdots
, where
s
is the minimal element of
S
such that
c_{s}\neq 0
(remember that
S
is well-ordered). Thus all other nonzero terms of the expansion are of the form
c_{s^{\prime}}\,\varepsilon^{s^{\prime}}
, with
c_{s^{\prime}}\in\mathbb{K}
and
s^{\prime}>s
in
S
. We define
\mathrm{val}(\hat{h})=s
. Note that
\mathrm{val}
is a discrete valuation on any subfield of
\widehat{\mathbb{K}}
that is finitely generated over
\mathbb{K}
. The limit
\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\hat{h}
means setting
\varepsilon=0
in
\hat{h}
. If we write
\hat{h}=c_{s}\hskip 1.0pt\varepsilon^{s}+\cdots
as above, then

\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\hat{h}=\begin{cases}\infty&s<0\\
c_{0}&s=0\\
0&s>0.\end{cases}

In particular,
\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\hat{h}\in\mathbb{K}^{\times}
if and only if
\mathrm{val}(\hat{h})=0
.

We claim that
\mathbf{V}(f_{1,\varepsilon}^{h},\ldots,f_{n,\varepsilon}^{h})\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n-1}_{\widehat{\mathbb{K}}}\times_{\widehat{\mathbb{K}}}\mathbb{A}_{\widehat{\mathbb{K}}}^{1}
is finite. To prove this, first note that
\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}R_{\text{\it f}_{\varepsilon}\hskip 0.8pt}(x_{n})=R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{n})\neq 0
. Thus
R_{\text{\it f}_{\varepsilon}\hskip 0.8pt}(x_{n})\neq 0
, so that only finitely many last coordinates occur in
\mathbf{V}(f_{1,\varepsilon}^{h},\ldots,f_{n,\varepsilon}^{h})
. If
\mathbf{V}(f_{1,\varepsilon}^{h},\ldots,f_{n,\varepsilon}^{h})
is infinite, there must thus be some
\hat{\xi}_{n}\in\widehat{\mathbb{K}}
such that

\mathbf{V}(f_{1,\varepsilon}^{h}|_{x_{n}=\hat{\xi}_{n}},\ldots,f_{n,\varepsilon}^{h}|_{x_{n}=\hat{\xi}_{n}})\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n-1}_{\widehat{\mathbb{K}}}

has positive dimension. In particular, this variety must meet the hyperplane
x_{1}=0
, so that ignoring
f_{n,\varepsilon}
for the moment, the
n-1
homogeneous equations

f_{1,\varepsilon}^{h}|_{x_{1}=0,x_{n}=\hat{\xi}_{n}}=\cdots=f_{n-1,\varepsilon}^{h}|_{x_{1}=0,x_{n}=\hat{\xi}_{n}}=0

in
n-1
variables
x_{0},x_{2},\dots,x_{n-1}
have a nontrivial solution in
\mathbb{P}^{n-2}_{\widehat{\mathbb{K}}}
. Hence

(4.5)

\mathrm{Res}_{d_{1},\ldots,d_{n-1}}(f_{1,\varepsilon}^{h}|_{x_{1}=0,x_{n}=\hat{\xi}_{n}},\ldots,f_{n-1,\varepsilon}^{h}|_{x_{1}=0,x_{n}=\hat{\xi}_{n}})=0.

However, it is easy to see that

\mathrm{Res}_{d_{1},\ldots,d_{n-1}}(f_{1,\varepsilon}^{h}|_{x_{1}=0,x_{n}=\hat{\xi}_{n}},\ldots,f_{n-1,\varepsilon}^{h}|_{x_{1}=0,x_{n}=\hat{\xi}_{n}})=(-\xi^{*}_{1})^{N}\varepsilon^{M}\!+\mbox{lower terms in}\,\varepsilon,

with
N,M\in\mathbb{N}
. This is clearly nonzero, contradicting (
4.5
). So
\mathbf{V}(f_{1,\varepsilon}^{h},\dots,f_{n,\varepsilon}^{h})
must be finite.

By Theorem
1.1
, it follows that there is a nonzero
c(\varepsilon)\in\widehat{\mathbb{K}}
such that

R_{\text{\it f}_{\varepsilon}\hskip 0.8pt}(x_{n})=c(\varepsilon)\!\!\prod_{\hat{\xi}\in\mathbf{V}(f_{1,\varepsilon}^{h},\ldots,f^{h}_{n,\varepsilon})}\!\!(x_{n}-\hat{\xi}_{n})^{m_{\hat{\xi}}}

The limit
\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\hat{\xi}=\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}([\hat{\xi}_{0}\hskip 0.85pt{:}\hskip 0.85pt\dots\hskip 0.85pt{:}\hskip 0.85pt\hat{\xi}_{n-1}],\hat{\xi}_{n})
is a point
\xi
in the complete variety
\mathbb{P}^{n-1}_{\mathbb{K}}\times\mathbb{P}^{1}_{\mathbb{K}}
. Let homogeneous coordinates be
x_{0},\dots,x_{n-1}
for
\mathbb{P}^{n-1}_{\mathbb{K}}
and
x_{n},x_{n+1}
for
\mathbb{P}^{1}_{\mathbb{K}}
, where
x_{n+1}
is a new variable. Recall that
\mathbf{V}(f_{1}^{h},\dots,f_{n}^{h})
is a subvariety of
\mathbb{P}^{n-1}_{\mathbb{K}}\times_{\mathbb{K}}\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{K}}^{1}
. Thus, if
\hat{\xi}\in\mathbf{V}(f_{1,\varepsilon}^{h},\ldots,f^{h}_{n,\varepsilon})
, then
\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\hat{\xi}=\xi
, where

\xi\in\mathbf{V}(f_{1}^{h},\dots,f_{n}^{h})\cup\{x_{n+1}=0\}=\underbrace{\mathbf{V}(f_{1},\dots,f_{n})}_{A}\cup\underbrace{\{x_{0}=0\}\cup\{x_{n+1}=0\}}_{B}.

In what follows, we often write
\hat{\xi}\rightarrow\xi
when
\hat{\xi}\in\mathbf{V}(f_{1,\varepsilon}^{h},\ldots,f^{h}_{n,\varepsilon})
and
\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\hat{\xi}=\xi
.

Using this notation, we can write
R_{\text{\it f}_{\varepsilon}\hskip 0.8pt}(x_{n})
as the product
R_{\text{\it f}_{\varepsilon}\hskip 0.8pt}(x_{n})=RS
, where

R=\!\!\prod_{\hat{\xi}\rightarrow\xi\in A}\!\!(x_{n}-\hat{\xi}_{n})^{m_{\hat{\xi}}}\ \text{ and }\ S=c(\varepsilon)\!\!\prod_{\hat{\xi}\rightarrow\xi\in B}\!\!(x_{n}-\hat{\xi}_{n})^{m_{\hat{\xi}}}.

Note that
x_{n}-\xi_{n}^{*}
is a factor of
S
since
\xi^{*}=([0\hskip 0.85pt{:}\hskip 0.85pt\xi_{1}^{*}\hskip 0.85pt{:}\hskip 0.85pt\dots\hskip 0.85pt{:}\hskip 0.85pt\xi_{n-1}^{*}],\xi_{n}^{*})\in\mathbf{V}(f_{1,\varepsilon}^{h},\ldots,f^{h}_{n,\varepsilon})
.

The next step is to apply
\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}
to the equation
R_{\text{\it f}_{\varepsilon}\hskip 0.8pt}(x_{n})=RS
. On the left-hand side of this equation, we already observed that
\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}R_{\text{\it f}_{\varepsilon}\hskip 0.8pt}(x_{n})=R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{n})
, which is a nonzero polynomial in
x_{n}
. To take the limit on the right-hand side, we first focus on
\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}R
.

All solutions
\xi\in A
arise as limits
\hat{\xi}\rightarrow\xi
for some
\hat{\xi}\in\mathbf{V}(f_{1,\varepsilon}^{h},\dots,f_{n,\varepsilon}^{h})
in a way that is compatible with multiplicities. Note also that
\xi=(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n})=([1\hskip 0.85pt{:}\hskip 0.85pt\xi_{1}\hskip 0.85pt{:}\hskip 0.85pt\dots\hskip 0.85pt{:}\hskip 0.85pt\xi_{n-1}],\xi_{n})\in\mathbb{P}^{n-1}_{\mathbb{K}}\times_{\mathbb{K}}\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{K}}^{1}
, and then
\hat{\xi}=([\hat{\xi}_{0}\hskip 0.85pt{:}\hskip 0.85pt\dots\hskip 0.85pt{:}\hskip 0.85pt\hat{\xi}_{n-1}],\hat{\xi}_{n})\rightarrow\xi
implies that
\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\hat{\xi}_{n}=\xi_{n}
.

Now fix
\xi\in\mathbf{V}(f_{1}^{h},\dots,f_{n}^{h})
and consider all
\hat{\xi}\in\mathbf{V}(\hat{f}_{1},\dots,\hat{f}_{n})
such that
\hat{\xi}\rightarrow\xi
. The previous paragraph implies that

(4.6)

\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\prod_{\hat{\xi}\rightarrow\xi\in A}(x_{n}-\hat{\xi}_{n})^{m_{\hat{\xi}}}=(x_{n}-\xi_{n})^{m_{\xi}},

and since
A=\mathbf{V}(f_{1},\dots,f_{n})
, it follows immediately that

\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}R=\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\prod_{\hat{\xi}\rightarrow\xi\in A}(x_{n}-\hat{\xi}_{n})^{m_{\hat{\xi}}}\ =\prod_{\xi\in\mathbf{V}(f_{1},\dots,f_{n})}\!\!\ (x_{n}-\xi_{n})^{m_{\xi}}=r(x_{n}),

where the last equality follows from (
4.3
). This is a nonzero polynomial, so that

R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{n})=\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}R_{\text{\it f}_{\varepsilon}\hskip 0.8pt}(x_{n})=\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}RS=r(x_{n})\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}S.

Since
S
is a polynomial in
x_{n}
, it follows that
\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}S
is also a polynomial in
x_{n}
. We noted above that
x_{n}-\xi_{n}^{*}
is a factor of
S
. Being independent of
\varepsilon
, it becomes a factor of
\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}S
. This shows that
\deg(R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{n}))>\deg(r(x_{n}))
, concluding the proof of (1) & (3)
\Rightarrow
(2).
∎

Example 4.3
.

Let
f_{1}=1+x_{1}+x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}
,
f_{2}=x_{2}+x_{1}^{2}x_{3}
and
f_{3}=1+x_{1}+2x_{2}+x_{2}^{2}x_{3}
in
\mathbb{C}[x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}]
. Then one computes that

I=\langle f_{1},f_{2},f_{3}\rangle=\langle x_{3}^{2}+6x_{3},x_{1}+\tfrac{2}{9}x_{3}+1,x_{2}+\tfrac{1}{9}x_{3}\rangle.

Thus
I
has a Shape Lemma with solutions
(-1,0,0)
and
(\tfrac{1}{3},\tfrac{2}{3},-6)
, both of multiplicity one. Note also that
I\cap\mathbb{C}[x_{3}]=\langle r(x_{3})\rangle
for
r(x_{3})=x_{3}(x_{3}+6)
.

However, when we homogenize, we get

f_{1}^{h}=x_{0}^{2}+x_{0}x_{1}+x_{1}x_{2}x_{3},\ f_{2}^{h}=x_{0}x_{2}+x_{1}^{2}x_{3},\ f_{3}^{h}=x_{0}^{2}+x_{0}x_{1}+2x_{0}x_{2}+x_{2}^{2}x_{3}

in
A[x_{0},x_{1},x_{2}]
,
A=\mathbb{C}[x_{3}]
. Using the classical formula for the resultant of three ternary quadrics (see, for example
[
CLO05
, (2.8)]
), one obtains

R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{3})=\mathrm{Res}_{2,2,2}(f_{1}^{h},f_{2}^{h},f_{3}^{h})=x_{3}^{7}(x_{3}+6).

By Theorem
1.2
, there must be at least one solution at
\infty
. In fact, there are a lot, since
f_{i}^{h}(0,x_{1},x_{2},0)=0
, giving a projective line of solutions at
\infty
, and it is easy to see that these are the only solutions at
\infty
.

In the elimination ideal
I\cap\mathbb{C}[x_{3}]=\langle x_{3}^{2}+6x_{3}\rangle=\langle x_{3}(x_{3}+6)\rangle
, the affine solution
(-1,0,0)
contributes the factor of
x_{3}
. But in the resultant, the solutions at
\infty
(all of which have
x_{3}=0
) cause the exponent of
x_{3}
to increase from
1
to
7
. This is mysterious. It would be nice to have a theoretical explanation of the exponent.

We conclude this section with the special case
n=2
, where solutions at
\infty
are easy to understand. Given
f_{1},f_{2}\in\mathbb{K}[x_{1},x_{2}]
, write them as

f_{i}(x_{1},x_{2})=\sum_{j=0}^{d_{i}}a_{ij}(x_{2})x_{1}^{j},\quad d_{i}=\deg_{x_{1}}(f_{i}),

and let
\mathrm{lc}_{x_{1}}(f_{i})=a_{id_{i}}(x_{2})
be the leading coefficient of
f_{i}
with respect to
x_{1}
. Note that
\mathrm{lc}_{x_{1}}(f_{i})\in\mathbb{K}[x_{2}]
is nonzero. Then

f_{i}^{h}(x_{0},x_{1},x_{2})=\sum_{j=0}^{d_{i}}a_{ij}(x_{2})x_{0}^{d_{i}-j}x_{1}^{j},

and
f_{i}^{h}(0,x_{1},x_{2})=\mathrm{lc}(f_{i},x_{1})x_{1}^{d_{i}}
. The solutions at
\infty
lie in
\mathbf{V}(f_{1}^{h},f_{2}^{h})\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{1}_{\mathbb{K}}\times_{\mathbb{K}}\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{K}}^{1}
, so that any solution with
x_{0}=0
must have
x_{1}\neq 0
. Hence we can assume
x_{1}=1
, and then solutions at
\infty
are
([0\hskip 0.85pt{:}\hskip 0.85pt1],\xi)
, where

(4.7)

\mathrm{lc}_{x_{1}}(f_{1})(\xi)=\mathrm{lc}_{x_{1}}(f_{2})(\xi)=0.

This leads to the following corollary of Theorem
1.2
when
n=2
:

Corollary 4.1
.

Let
I=\langle f_{1},f_{2}\rangle\subseteq\mathbb{K}[x_{1},x_{2}]
be a zero-dimensional ideal such that the map
\mathbf{V}(I)\rightarrow\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{K}}^{1}
given by projection onto the second coordinate is injective as a map of sets. Then any two of the following three conditions imply the third
:

(1)

I
has a Shape Lemma

(2)

\gcd(\mathrm{lc}_{x_{1}}(f_{1}),\mathrm{lc}_{x_{1}}(f_{2}))=1
.

(3)

I\cap\mathbb{K}[x_{2}]=\langle R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{2})\rangle

Proof.

This follows immediately from Theorem
1.2
since the analysis of solutions at
\infty
given in (
4.7
) shows that
\gcd(\mathrm{lc}_{x_{1}}(f_{1}),\mathrm{lc}_{x_{1}}(f_{2}))=1
if and only if there are no solutions at
\infty
.
∎

5.
The Shape Lemma and Subresultants

In this final section of the paper, we highlight the role of subresultants. The classical theory of subresultants of two univariate polynomials goes back to the work of Jacobi
[
Jac36
]
and Sylvester
[
Syl40
]
. Modern accounts can be found in
[
AJ06
,
vzGL03
]
, both of which contain references to many other papers on subresultants. In the multivariable case, we will follow the definition and presentation given in
[
Cha95
]
, where general statements and results are presented. For our purposes it will be enough to focus on the multivariable version of the “first subresultant polynomial”.

Let
A
be an integral domain with field of fractions
k(A)
. Let
g_{1},\ldots,g_{n}\in A[x_{0},\ldots,x_{n-1}]
be homogeneous polynomials of respective degrees
d_{1},\ldots,d_{n}
, and define
\rho:=d_{1}+\cdots+d_{n}-n
to be the
critical degree
of the system. If the multivariable resultant of these polynomials is nonzero, the degree
\rho
piece of the graded ring
k(A)[x_{0},\ldots,x_{n-1}]/\langle g_{1},\ldots,g_{n}\rangle
has dimension one. While these conditions are not equivalent (there are systems with nontrivial solutions that also satisfy this property), in general one expects this dimension to be equal to one.

For any monomial
x^{\alpha}=x_{0}^{\alpha_{0}}\ldots x_{n-1}^{\alpha_{n-1}}
of degree
\rho
, there exists
s_{\alpha}\in A
that is a polynomial in the coefficients of
g_{1},\ldots,g_{n}
and vanishes if and only if the class of
x^{\alpha}
fails to be a basis of
(k(A)[x_{0},\ldots,x_{n-1}]/\langle g_{1},\ldots,g_{n}\rangle)_{\rho}
. We call
s_{\alpha}
the
scalar subresultant
associated to
x^{\alpha}
.

Proposition 5.1

(Theorems
1
and
2
in
[
Cha95
]
)
.

With notation as above
:

(1)

s_{\alpha}x^{\beta}-s_{\beta}x^{\alpha}\in\langle g_{1},\ldots,g_{n}\rangle\subseteq A[x_{0},\ldots,x_{n-1}]
for all
\alpha,\beta
of degree
\rho
.

(2)

s_{\alpha}=0
for all
\alpha
of degree
\rho
if and only if

\dim\hskip 0.5pt(k(A)[x_{0},\ldots,x_{n-1}]/\langle g_{1},\ldots,g_{n}\rangle)_{\rho}>1.

Note that
\rho=0
if and only if
d_{1}=\cdots=d_{n}=1
, in which case the monomial
1
is the only one of critical degree. Its subresultant is then defined as
s_{(0,\ldots,0)}=1
, which fulfills the conditions of Proposition
5.1
. In what follows, we will always assume that
\rho\geq 1
, For readers interested in the computational aspects of multivariable subresultants, we recommend the treatment given in
[
GV91
,
Cha94
,
Cha95
]
.

In this paper, we are dealing with polynomials
f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}\in\mathbb{K}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
. Their homogenizations with respect to
x_{1},\dots,x_{n-1}
are
f_{1}^{h},\ldots,f_{n}^{h}\in A[x_{0},\ldots,x_{n-1}]
for
A=\mathbb{K}[x_{n}]
. Thus
s_{\alpha}=s_{\alpha}(x_{n})\in\mathbb{K}[x_{n}]
for all
\alpha
of degree
\rho
. In this situation, Proposition
5.1
gives the following useful result:

Proposition 5.2
.

Assume that
I=\langle f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}\rangle
is zero-dimensional with
\rho\geq 1
. Given
\xi_{n}\in\mathbb{K}
, the following are equivalent
:

(1)

R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(\xi_{n})=0
and
s_{\alpha}(\xi_{n})\neq 0

(2)

The fiber of
\mathbf{V}(f_{1}^{h},\dots,f_{n}^{h})\rightarrow\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{K}}^{1}
over
\xi_{n}
consists of a single reduced point given by
\xi=([\xi_{0}\hskip 0.85pt{:}\hskip 0.85pt\dots\hskip 0.85pt{:}\hskip 0.85pt\xi_{n-1}],\xi_{n})\in\mathbf{V}(f_{1}^{h},\dots,f_{n}^{h})
and
\xi^{\alpha}\neq 0
, where
\xi^{\alpha}=\xi_{0}^{\alpha_{0}}\dots\xi_{n-1}^{\alpha_{n-1}}
.

Proof.

(1)
\Rightarrow
(2): By the universal property of resultants,
R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(\xi_{n})=0
implies that the fiber has
at least one point
\xi
, and since
s_{\alpha}(\xi_{n})\neq 0
, (2) of Proposition
5.1
implies that the Hilbert function of the fiber above the specialized system in the critical degree coincides with the one of a complete intersection of a single point. It follows that the fiber consists of a single reduced point. To show that
\xi^{\alpha}\neq 0
, pick
x^{\beta}
of degree
\rho
such that
\xi^{\beta}\neq 0
. If
\beta=\alpha
we are done. Otherwise, by (1) of Proposition
5.1
, we have
s_{\alpha}(\xi_{n})\xi^{\beta}=s_{\beta}(\xi_{n})\xi^{\alpha}
. Since
s_{\alpha}(\xi_{n})\neq 0\neq\xi^{\beta},
the same is true for
\xi^{\alpha}
.

(2)
\Rightarrow
(1): If the fiber over
\xi_{n}
is nonempty, then
R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(\xi_{n})
must be zero. In addition, if it is a single reduced point, by
[
Cha94
, Corollaire 2]
, the dimension of the
\rho
-th degree part of
\mathbb{K}[x_{0},\ldots,x_{n-1}]/\langle f_{1}^{h}(x,\xi_{n}),\dots,f_{n}^{h}(x,\xi_{n})\rangle
must be equal to one. As
\xi^{\alpha}\neq 0,
the monomial
x_{0}^{\alpha_{0}}\ldots x_{n-1}^{\alpha_{n-1}}
is a basis of this
\mathbb{K}
-vector space, which implies that
s_{\alpha}(\xi_{n})\neq 0
because of the definition of
s_{\alpha}
given above.
∎

For our purposes, certain scalar subresultants are especially useful. Suppose that
\rho\geq 1
and consider the monomials
x^{\alpha(i)}=x_{0}^{\rho-1}x_{i}
,
i=0,\dots,n-1
, of degree
\rho
. For simplicity, the scalar subresultant
s_{\alpha(i)}(x_{n})
will be denoted
s_{i}(x_{n})
in what follows. Proposition
5.1
implies that the polynomials
s_{0}(x_{n})x_{0}^{\rho-1}x_{i}-s_{i}(x_{n})x_{0}^{\rho}
belong to the ideal
\langle f_{1}^{h},\ldots,f_{n}^{h}\rangle\subset\mathbb{K}[x_{0},\ldots,x_{n-1}]
for
i=1,\ldots,n-1
. Setting
x_{0}=1
gives the following polynomials:

(5.1)

p_{i}(x_{i},x_{n}):=s_{0}(x_{n})\,x_{i}-s_{i}(x_{n})\in\langle f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}\rangle\cap\mathbb{K}[x_{i},x_{n}],\quad i=1,\dots,n-1.

These are the
first subresultant polynomials
of
f_{1},\dots,f_{n}
.

The main theorems of this section involve the ideal

(5.2)

\langle R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{n}),p_{1}(x_{1},x_{n}),\ldots,p_{n-1}(x_{n-1},x_{n})\rangle\subseteq I

generated by the resultant and first subresultant polynomials. But before giving the proofs, we need the following general lemma about the inclusion (
5.2
):

Lemma 5.1
.

Given
d(x_{n}),d_{0}(x_{n}),\dots,d_{n-1}(x_{n})\in\mathbb{K}[x_{n}]
with
d(x_{n})\neq 0
, set

(5.3)

J:=\langle d(x_{n}),d_{0}(x_{n})x_{1}-d_{1}(x_{n}),\dots,d_{0}(x_{n})x_{n-1}-d_{n-1}(x_{n})\rangle.

Then
J
is zero-dimensional if and only if
\gcd(d(x_{n}),d_{0}(x_{n}),\dots,d_{n-1}(x_{n}))=1
.
Furthermore, if these conditions are satisfied and
J\subseteq I\subseteq\mathbb{K}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
, then
:

(1)

I
has a Shape Lemma.

(2)

If the generator of
I\cap\mathbb{K}[x_{n}]
lies in
J
, then
I=J
.

Proof.

First assume that
J
is zero-dimensional. Any nontrivial common divisor
h(x_{n})
of
d(x_{n}),d_{0}(x_{n}),\dots,d_{n-1}(x_{n})
divides
p_{i}(x_{i},x_{n})
for
i=1,\dots,n-1
, so that
J\subseteq\langle h(x_{n})\rangle
, which is impossible when
J
is zero-dimensional. Conversely, assume that the gcd condition is satisfied. It suffices to prove that
\mathbf{V}(J)
is finite. Take
\xi=(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n})\in\mathbf{V}(J)
. Then
d(\xi_{n})=0
, and since
d(x_{n})
is nonzero, there are only finitely many choices of
\xi_{n}
. If
d_{0}(\xi_{n})=0
, then for
i=1,\dots,n-1
, we have

0=d_{0}(\xi_{n})\xi_{i}-d_{i}(\xi_{n})=0\cdot\xi_{i}-d_{i}(\xi_{n})=-d_{i}(\xi_{n}),

which shows that
x_{n}-\xi_{n}
is a common divisor of
d(x_{n}),d_{0}(x_{n}),\dots,d_{n-1}(x_{n})
, a contradiction. Thus
d_{0}(\xi_{n})\neq 0
, and then
\xi_{i}=d_{i}(\xi_{n})/d_{0}(\xi_{n})
for
i=1,\dots,n-1
shows that
\xi
is uniquely determined by
\xi_{n}
. Hence
\mathbf{V}(J)
is finite.

For (1), consider all representations (
5.3
) of
J
that satisfy the gcd condition
\gcd(d(x_{n}),d_{0}(x_{n}),\dots,d_{n-1}(x_{n}))=1
, and pick one where
\deg(d(x_{n}))
is minimal. Let
e_{0}(x_{n}):=\gcd(d(x_{n}),d_{0}(x_{n}))
. Then we can write
d(x_{n})=e_{0}(x_{n})e(x_{n})
and
d_{0}(x_{n})=e_{0}(x_{n})E(x_{n})
, and we have a Bézout identity

(5.4)

A(x_{n})\,e(x_{n})+B(x_{n})\,E(x_{n})=1.

Also define
e_{i}(x_{n})=B(x_{n})d_{i}(x_{n})
for
i=1,\ldots,n-1
. We claim that

(5.5)

\gcd(e(x_{n}),e_{0}(x_{n}),\dots,e_{n-1}(x_{n}))=1.

If not, there is
\xi_{n}\in\mathbb{K}
that makes them all vanish. Then
e(\xi_{n})=0
and (
5.4
) imply that
B(\xi_{n})\neq 0
. But
e_{0}(x_{n})=\gcd(d(x_{n}),d_{0}(x_{n}))
implies

(5.6)

\gcd(e_{0}(x_{n}),d_{1}(x_{n}),\dots,d_{n-1}(x_{n}))=\gcd(d(x_{n}),d_{0}(x_{n}),\dots,d_{n-1}(x_{n}))=1.

Since
e_{0}(\xi_{n})=0
, we must have
d_{i}(\xi_{n})\neq 0
for some
1\leq i\leq n-1
, and then
e_{i}(\xi_{n})=B(\xi_{n})d_{i}(\xi_{n})\neq 0
, a contradiction. This proves (
5.5
).

We next claim that

(5.7)

J=\langle e(x_{n}),e_{0}(x_{n})x_{1}-e_{1}(x_{n}),\dots,e_{0}(x_{n})x_{n-1}-e_{n-1}(x_{n})\rangle.

In what follows, we will omit “
(x_{n})
” for simplicity. To prove (
5.7
), let
J^{\prime}
denote the ideal on the right. For the inclusion
J^{\prime}\subseteq J
, first note that (
5.4
) implies

e_{0}\hskip 1.0ptx_{i}-e_{i}=e_{0}(A\,e+B\,E)x_{i}-Bd_{i}=Ax_{i}\cdot d+B\cdot(d_{0}x_{i}-d_{i})\in J

since
d=e_{0}e
,
d_{0}=e_{0}E
, and
e_{i}=Bd_{i}
. Showing that
e\in J
will take more work. First observe that

dx_{i}-e(e_{0}x_{i}-e_{i})=ee_{i},\ i=1,\dots,n-1.

The left-hand side lies in
J
by what we just proved, so
ee_{i}=eBd_{i}\in J
. Then

(5.8)

d_{0}x_{i}-d_{i}-E(e_{0}x_{i}-e_{i})=-d_{i}+EBd_{i}=(-1+EB)d_{i}=-Aed_{i}

proves that
Aed_{i}\in J
. We showed above that
Bed_{i}\in J
, and since
A
and
B
are relatively prime by (
5.4
), we get
ed_{i}\in J
for
i=1,\dots,n-1
. However, we also have
ee_{0}\in J
. Since
e_{0},d_{1},\dots,d_{n-1}
are relatively prime by (
5.6
), it follows that
e\in J
, completing the proof of
J^{\prime}\subseteq J
.

For
J\subseteq J^{\prime}
, note that
d=e_{0}e\in J^{\prime}
. Also, for
i=1,\ldots,n-1
,
J^{\prime}
contains
e
and
e_{0}x_{i}-e_{i}
, so that
d_{0}x_{i}-d_{i}\in J^{\prime}
by (
5.8
). Thus
J\subseteq J^{\prime}
, and (
5.7
) is proved.

When we combine (
5.6
) and (
5.7
) with the minimality of
\deg(d(x_{n}))
in (
5.3
), we see that
\deg(e(x_{n}))\geq\deg(d(x_{n}))
. Since
d(x_{n})=e_{0}(x_{n})e(x_{n})
, it follows that
e_{0}(x_{n})=\gcd(d(x_{n}),d_{0}(x_{n}))
is constant, i.e.,
e_{0}(x_{n})=1
. Then (
5.7
) becomes

(5.9)

J=\langle e(x_{n}),x_{1}-e_{1}(x_{n}),\dots,x_{n-1}-e_{n-1}(x_{n})\rangle.

so that
x_{i}-e_{i}(x_{n})\in J
for
i=1,\dots,n-1
.

Now let
I
be an ideal containing
J
as in the statement of the lemma. Then
I
is also zero-dimensional, so that
I\cap\mathbb{K}[x_{n}]=\langle r(x_{n})\rangle
for some nonzero
r(x_{n})\in\mathbb{K}[x_{n}]
. It is straighforward to show that
G=\{r(x_{n}),\,x_{1}-e_{1}(x_{n}),\ldots,x_{n-1}-e_{n-1}(x_{n})\}
is a Gröbner basis of
I
for lex order with
x_{n}\prec x_{n-1}\prec\ldots\prec x_{1}
. Since a Gröbner basis is a basis, it follows that
I
has a Shape Lemma, proving (1).

For (2), let
I\cap\mathbb{K}[x_{n}]=\langle r(x_{n})\rangle
with
r(x_{n})
monic. The hypothesis of (2) implies
r(x_{n})\in J
, so that the Gröbner basis of
I
constructed in the previous paragraph lies in
J
. The equality
I=J
follows immediately.
∎

Remark 5.1
.

Implicit in the proof of Lemma
5.1
is an algorithm that constructs a Shape Lemma basis of the ideal (
5.3
) when
\gcd(d(x_{n}),d_{0}(x_{n}),\dots,d_{n-1}(x_{n}))=1
. Note also that the algorithm takes only one step when
\gcd(d(x_{n}),d_{0}(x_{n}))=1
.

Lemma
5.1
gives a nice consequence of (
5.2
):

Corollary 5.1
.

I
has a Shape Lemma when
\gcd(R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{n}),s_{0}(x_{n}),\dots,s_{n-1}(x_{n}))=1
.

Proof.

Since
p_{i}(x_{i},x_{n})=s_{0}(x_{n})x_{i}-s_{i}(x_{n})
, (
5.2
) can be written

\langle R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{n}),s_{0}(x_{n})x_{1}-s_{1}(x_{n}),\ldots,s_{0}(x_{n})x_{n-1}-s_{n-1}(x_{n})\rangle\subseteq I.

Then our gcd hypothesis and Lemma
5.1
imply that
I
has a Shape Lemma.
∎

The first main result of Section
5
is Theorem
1.3
, which says that if
I=\langle f_{1},\dots,f_{n}\rangle
is zero-dimensional and
\rho\geq 1
, then the conditions

(1)

I
has a Shape Lemma and
f_{1},\dots,f_{n}
have no solutions at
\infty
.

(2)

I\cap\mathbb{K}[x_{n}]=\langle R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{n})\rangle
and
\gcd(R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{n}),s_{0}(x_{n}))=1
.

(3)

I=\langle R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{n}),p_{1}(x_{1},x_{n}),\ldots,p_{n-1}(x_{n-1},x_{n})\rangle
and
I\cap\mathbb{K}[x_{n}]=\langle R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{n})\rangle
.

are equivalent, and when these conditions are all true, we also have

I\cap\mathbb{K}[x_{i_{1}},\dots,x_{i_{\ell}},x_{n}]=\langle R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{n}),p_{i_{0}}(x_{i_{0}},x_{n}),\dots,p_{i_{\ell}}(x_{i_{\ell}},x_{n})\rangle

whenever
1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{\ell}<n
.

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

(1)
\Rightarrow
(2): Assume that
I
has a Shape Lemma with no solutions at
\infty
.
The implication (1) & (2)
\Rightarrow
(3) of Theorem
1.2
implies that
I\cap\mathbb{K}[x_{n}]=\langle R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{n})\rangle
(the injectivity hypothesis of Theorem
1.2
is satisfied since
I
has a Shape Lemma).

Now suppose that
\xi_{n}
is a root of
R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{n})
. Since there are no solutions at
\infty
, it follows that there is a solution
\xi=([1\hskip 0.85pt{:}\hskip 0.85pt\xi_{1}\hskip 0.85pt{:}\hskip 0.85pt\dots\hskip 0.85pt{:}\hskip 0.85pt\xi_{n-1}],\xi_{n})
. Since
I
has a Shape Lemma, the fiber over
\xi_{n}
consists of a single smooth point by Lemma
2.3
. For
x^{\alpha(0)}=x_{0}^{\rho}
, we clearly have
\xi^{\alpha(0)}\neq 0
, so that
s_{\alpha(0)}(\xi_{n})\neq 0
by Proposition
5.2
. But by definiton,
s_{0}(\xi_{n})=s_{\alpha(0)}(x_{n})
. Thus
s_{0}(\xi_{n})\neq 0
whenever
R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(\xi_{n})=0
, which proves that
\gcd(R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{n}),s_{0}(x_{n}))=1
.

(2)
\Rightarrow
(3): Assume
I\cap\mathbb{K}[x_{n}]=\langle R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{n})\rangle
and
\gcd(R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{n}),s_{0}(x_{n}))=1
. Then
J=\langle R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{n}),p_{1}(x_{1},x_{n}),\dots,p_{n-1}(x_{n-1},x_{n})\rangle
satisfies the the gcd condition of Lemma
5.1
. Since
J\subset I
and the generator of
I\cap\mathbb{K}[x_{n}]
lies in
J
,
the lemma implies
I=J
.

(3)
\Rightarrow
(1): Here, we assume that
I=\langle R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{n}),p_{1}(x_{1},x_{n}),\ldots,p_{n-1}(x_{n-1},x_{n})\rangle
and
I\cap\mathbb{K}[x_{n}]=\langle R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{n})\rangle
. Since
I
is zero-dimensional, Lemma
5.1
implies that
I
has a Shape Lemma.

Since
I
has a Shape Lemma, Lemma
2.1
implies that the injectivity hypothesis of Theorem
1.2
is satisfied. Then this theorem and
I\cap\mathbb{K}[x_{n}]=\langle R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{n})\rangle
imply that there are no solutions at
\infty
, completing the proof that (3)
\Rightarrow
(1).

Finally, suppose that the conditions of Theorem
1.3
are all true. Then
R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{n})
generates
I\cap\mathbb{K}[x_{n}]
and is relatively prime with
s_{0}(x_{n})
. Fix
1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{\ell}<n
and observe that
\langle R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{n}),p_{i_{0}}(x_{i_{0}},x_{n}),\dots,p_{i_{\ell}}(x_{i_{\ell}},x_{n})\rangle
can be written as

(5.10)

\langle R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{n}),s_{0}(x_{n})x_{i_{0}}-s_{i_{0}}(x_{n}),\dots,s_{o}(x_{n})x_{i_{\ell}}-s_{i_{\ell}}(x_{n})\rangle

This lies in
I\cap\mathbb{K}[x_{i_{1}},\dots,x_{i_{\ell}},x_{n}]
. Since
\gcd(R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{n}),s_{0}(x_{n}))=1
, we have

\gcd(R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{n}),s_{0}(x_{n}),s_{i_{1}}(x_{n}),\dots,s_{i_{\ell}}(x_{n}))=1,

and the generator of
(I\cap\mathbb{K}[x_{i_{1}},\dots,x_{i_{\ell}},x_{n}])\cap\mathbb{K}[x_{n}]=I\cap\mathbb{K}[x_{n}]=\langle R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{n})\rangle
is in (
5.10
). Then (2) of Lemma
5.1
gives the desired equality

I\cap\mathbb{K}[x_{i_{1}},\dots,x_{i_{\ell}},x_{n}]=\langle R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{n}),p_{i_{0}}(x_{i_{0}},x_{n}),\dots,p_{i_{\ell}}(x_{i_{\ell}},x_{n})\rangle.\qed

Example 5.1
.

Let
f_{1}=1+x_{1}x_{3}
,
f_{2}=1+x_{2}x_{3}
, and
f_{3}=1+x_{1}^{2}x_{3}+x_{2}^{2}x_{3}+x_{3}^{2}
in
\mathbb{C}[x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}]
. One checks that
f_{i}^{h}(0,x_{1},x_{2},0)=0
, so there are solutions at
\infty
(in fact, infinitely many). We compute a Gröbner basis for
I
to get a Shape Lemma

I=\langle f_{1},f_{2},f_{3}\rangle=\langle 2+x_{3}+x_{3}^{3},\,x_{1}-\tfrac{1}{2}-\tfrac{1}{2}x_{3}^{2},\,x_{2}-\tfrac{1}{2}-\tfrac{1}{2}x_{3}^{2}\rangle.

On the other hand,
\rho=1
, and computing resultants and subresultants gives

\displaystyle R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{3})

\displaystyle=x_{3}^{3}(2+x_{3}+x_{3}^{3})

\displaystyle s_{0}(x_{3})

\displaystyle=s_{(1,0,0)}(x_{3})=x_{3}^{2}

\displaystyle s_{1}(x_{3})

\displaystyle=s_{(0,1,0)}(x_{3})=-x_{3}

\displaystyle s_{2}(x_{3})

\displaystyle=s_{(0,0,1)}(x_{3})=-x_{3},

so that
p_{1}(x_{1},x_{3})=x_{3}f_{1}
and
p_{2}(x_{2},x_{3})=x_{3}f_{2}.
In this case,
x_{3}
is a factor of all the scalar subresultants.
It is fun to see how all three conditions of Theorem 1.3 fail in this case.

The second main result of this section is Theorem
1.4
, which assumes only that the ideal is generated by the resultant and first subresultant polynomials. More precisely, for a zero-dimensional ideal
I=\langle f_{1},\dots,f_{n}\rangle
with
\rho\geq 1
, Theorem
1.4
asserts that if
I=\langle R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{n}),p_{1}(x_{1},x_{n}),\dots,p_{n-1}(x_{n-1},x_{n})\rangle
, then

(1)

\gcd(R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{n}),s_{0}(x_{n}),\dots,s_{n-1}(x_{n}))=1
.

(2)

I
has a Shape Lemma.

In addition, the following are equivalent:

(3)

I\cap\mathbb{K}[x_{n}]=\langle R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{n})\rangle

(4)

\gcd(R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{n}),s_{0}(x_{n}))=1
, where
p_{i}(x_{i},x_{n})=s_{0}(x_{n})x_{i}-s_{i}(x_{n})
.

(5)

f_{1},\dots,f_{n}
have no solutions at
\infty
.

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.

As in the proof of Corollary
5.1
,
I
can be written

(5.11)

I=\langle R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{n}),s_{0}(x_{n})x_{1}-s_{1}(x_{n}),\ldots,s_{0}(x_{n})x_{n-1}-s_{n-1}(x_{n})\rangle.

Then Lemma
5.1
implies that
\gcd(R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{n}),s_{0}(x_{n}),\dots,s_{n-1}(x_{n}))=1
since
I
has dimension zero. This proves (1), and then (2) follows from Corollary
5.1
.

It remains to show that our hypothesis on
I
implies the equivalence of (3), (4), and (5). Given Theorem
1.3
, this is easy:

•

(3)
\Rightarrow
(4) follows from (3)
\Rightarrow
(2) of Theorem
1.3
.

•

(4)
\Rightarrow
(3) follows since the Bézout identity for
\gcd(R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{n}),s_{0}(x_{n}))=1
and (
5.11
) imply that
I=\langle R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{n}),x_{1}-g_{1}(x_{n}),\ldots,x_{n-1}-g_{n-1}(x_{n})\rangle
for some
g_{1}(x_{n}),\dots,g_{n-1}(x_{n})\in\mathbb{K}[x_{n}]
. This easily implies (3).

•

(3)
\Rightarrow
(5) follows from (3)
\Rightarrow
(1) of Theorem
1.3
.

•

(5)
\Rightarrow
(3) follows from (1)
\Rightarrow
(2) of Theorem
1.3
since
I
has a Shape Lemma.∎

We conclude this section with a discussion of the case
n=2
. In Section
4
, we noted that
f_{1},f_{2}
have no solution at
\infty
if and only if
\gcd(\mathrm{lc}_{x_{1}}(f_{1}),\mathrm{lc}_{x_{1}}(f_{2}))=1
. In particular, this allows us to replace the condition “no solution at
\infty
” with the easier-to-check condition “
\gcd(\mathrm{lc}_{x_{1}}(f_{1}),\mathrm{lc}_{x_{1}}(f_{2}))=1
” in Theorems
1.3
and
1.4
.

Example 5.2
.

Let
f_{1}=x_{2}x_{1}^{2}+x_{1}+x_{2}^{2}+x_{2}
and
f_{2}=x_{2}x_{1}+1
in
\mathbb{C}[x_{1},x_{2}]
. Since
\mathrm{lc}_{x_{1}}(f_{1})=\mathrm{lc}_{x_{1}}(f_{2})=x_{2}
, there is a solution at
\infty
. Hence (1) in Theorem
1.3
is false, so that (2) and (3) are also false.

It is instructive to see exactly how (1), (2) and (3) fail in this case. A Gröbner basis calculation shows that
I=\langle f_{1},f_{2}\rangle=\langle x_{2}+1,x_{1}-1\rangle
has a Shape Lemma, so one part of (1) is true while the other part is false. For (2), we have

R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{2})=x_{2}^{3}(x_{2}+1)\ \text{ and }\ p_{1}(x_{1},x_{2})=x_{2}x_{1}+1,

so that
s_{0}(x_{2})=x_{2}
. Hence
\gcd(R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{2}),s_{0}(x_{2}))=x_{2}\neq 1
, and

I\cap\mathbb{C}[x_{2}]=\langle x_{2}+1,x_{1}-1\rangle\cap\mathbb{C}[x_{2}]=\langle x_{2}+1\rangle\neq\langle R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{2})\rangle

because
R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{2})=x_{2}^{3}(x_{2}+1)
has an extraneous factor of
x_{2}^{3}
. It follows that both parts of (2) are false. As for (3), a Gröbner basis calculation reveals that

\langle R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{2}),p_{1}(x_{1},x_{2})\rangle=\langle x_{2}^{3}(x_{2}+1),x_{2}x_{1}+1\rangle=\langle x_{2}+1,x_{1}-1\rangle.

Hence
I=\langle R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{2}),p_{1}(x_{1},x_{2})\rangle
is true in this case. Thus one part of (3) is true and the other part is false. Theorem
1.4
explains what is going on:

•

I=\langle R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{2}),p_{1}(x_{1},x_{2})\rangle
implies that
I
has a Shape Lemma. This accounts for the parts above that are true.

•

I=\langle R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{2}),p_{1}(x_{1},x_{2})\rangle
implies that (3), (4), and (5) of Theorem
1.4
are equivalent. So
I\cap\mathbb{C}[x_{2}]\neq\langle R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{2})\rangle
guarantees that there are solutions at
\infty
and that
\gcd(R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{2}),s_{0}(x_{2}))\neq 1
. This explains the parts above that are false.

Our final result shows that when
n=2
, sometimes just knowing the resultant is enough to guarantee that the conditions of Theorem
1.3
are all true:

Proposition 5.3
.

If
f_{1},f_{2}\in\mathbb{K}[x_{1},x_{2}]
satisfy
\rho\geq 1
and the resultant
R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{2})
has degree
\deg(f_{1})\cdot\deg(f_{2})
with distinct roots, then the conditions of Theorem
1.3
are all true. In particular,
I=\langle f_{1},f_{2}\rangle
has a Shape Lemma,
I=\langle R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{2}),p_{1}(x_{1},x_{2})\rangle
, and
I\cap\mathbb{K}[x_{2}]=\langle R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{2})\rangle
.

Proof.

We first show that
f_{1},f_{2}
are relatively prime. The nonvanishing of
R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{2})
implies that
f_{1},f_{2}
cannot have a common factor that involves
x_{1}
. But a common factor involving only
x_{2}
would appear with exponent
d_{1}+d_{2}=\rho+2\geq 3
in the resultant, which is impossible since
R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{2})
has distinct roots. It follows that
I=\langle f_{1},f_{2}\rangle
is zero-dimensional.

Let
M_{i}=\deg(f_{i})
and recall that
d_{i}=\deg_{x_{1}}(f_{i})
. In this case, Bézout
[
Bez64
]
proved in 1764 that

\deg(R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{2}))\leq M_{1}d_{2}+M_{2}d_{1}-d_{1}d_{2}=M_{1}M_{2}-(M_{1}-d_{1})(M_{2}-d_{2}).

Since the degree in the left is
M_{1}M_{2}
by hypothesis, we must have either
M_{1}=d_{1}
or
M_{2}=d_{2}
. If
M_{1}=d_{1}
, then
\mathrm{lc}(f_{1},x_{1})
is a nonzero constant, which implies that
\gcd(\mathrm{lc}(f_{1},x_{1}),\mathrm{lc}(f_{2},x_{1}))=1
. Similarly,
\gcd(\mathrm{lc}(f_{1},x_{1}),\mathrm{lc}(f_{2},x_{1}))=1
when
M_{2}=d_{2}
. It follows that there are no solutions at
\infty
.

The universal property of the resultant implies that the
M_{1}M_{2}
distinct roots of
R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{2})
extend to solutions in
\mathbb{P}^{1}\times_{\mathbb{K}}\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{K}}^{1}
. There are no solutions at
\infty
since
\gcd(\mathrm{lc}(f_{1},x_{1}),\mathrm{lc}(f_{2},x_{1}))=1
, so that the
M_{1}M_{2}
distinct roots of
R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{2})
extend to solutions of
f_{1}=f_{2}=0
. Thus we have
M_{1}M_{2}
elements of
\mathbf{V}(f_{1},f_{2})
with distinct
x_{2}
-coordinates. If we homogenize
f_{i}(x_{1},x_{2})
to
F_{i}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})
, Bézout’s theorem for
\mathbb{P}^{2}_{\mathbb{K}}
implies that
F_{1}=F_{2}=0
has
M_{1}M_{2}
solutions in
\mathbb{P}^{2}
, counting multiplicity. Yet we just constructed
M_{1}M_{2}
affine solutions in
\mathbb{K}^{2}
with distinct
x_{2}
-coordinates. Thus:

•

All solutions of
F_{1}=F_{2}=0
lie in
\mathbb{K}^{2}
and have multiplicity one.

•

The solutions in
\mathbf{V}(f_{1},f_{2})
have distinct
x_{2}
-coordinates.

The second bullet shows that the injectivity hypothesis of Lemma
2.2
is satisfied. Furthermore, if we let
I\cap\mathbb{K}[x_{2}]=\langle r(x_{2})\rangle
, then
r(x_{2})
divides
R_{\text{\it f}\hskip 0.9pt}(x_{2})
. Since the latter has distinct roots, the same is true for
r(x_{2})
, so its roots all have multiplicity one. All solutions of
f_{1}=f_{2}=0
have multiplicity one by the first bullet, so that
I
has a Shape Lemma by Lemma
2.2
. We showed above that there are no solutions at
\infty
, so condition (1) of Theorem
1.3
is satisfied, and we are done.
∎
