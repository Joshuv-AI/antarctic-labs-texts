PnP Restoration with Domain Adaptation for SANS
Shirin Shoushtari, Edward P. Chandler, Jialiang Zhang, Manjula Senanayake

Abstract
Small Angle Neutron Scattering (SANS) is a non-destructive technique utilized to probe the nano- to mesoscale structure of materials by analyzing the scattering pattern of neutrons. Accelerating SANS acquisition for in-situ analysis is essential, but it often reduces the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), highlighting the need for methods to enhance SNR even with short acquisition times. While deep learning (DL) can be used for enhancing SNR of low quality SANS, the amount of experimental data available for training is usually severely limited. We address this issue by proposing a P lug-and-play R estoration for SANS ( PR-SANS ) that uses domain-adapted priors. The prior in PR-SANS is initially trained on a set of generic images and subsequently fine-tuned using a limited amount of experimental SANS data. We present a theoretical convergence analysis of PR-SANS by focusing on the error resulting from using inexact domain-adapted priors instead of the ideal ones. We demonstrate with experimentally collected SANS data that PR-SANS can recover high-SNR 2D SANS detector images from low-SNR detector images, effectively increasing the SNR. This advancement enables a reduction in acquisition times by a factor of 12 while maintaining the original signal quality.

1
Introduction

Small angle neutron scattering (SANS) is widely used technique for investigating material structure at mesoscopic scale
[
1
]
.
Long exposure to the neutron beam is often necessary for detailed structural information from the SANS experiments.
There has been an increased demand for insights into the dynamic behaviors of mesoscale structures, motivating the development of in-situ and operando SANS. Traditionally, a limitation of SANS has been its lower neutron flux compared to x-ray scattering, leading to longer acquisition times and diminished temporal resolution. Recent advancements have seen the introduction of SANS instruments equipped with larger and faster detectors, enhanced guide coating technologies, and high-flux neutron sources, aiming to overcome these challenges
[
2
,
3
]
. Despite these improvements, obtaining detailed structural information often requires significant signal averaging and prolonged acquisitions times, complicating high-quality data acquisition for applications like in-situ SANS. Consequently, attempts to expedite data collection frequently compromise the temporal resolution or SNR of the measurements
[
4
]
.

The recovery of high-SNR SANS detector images from low-SNR SANS measurements can be formulated as an
image restoration problem. Deep learning (DL) has been extensively used as a data-driven strategy to address a wide range of imaging inverse problems
[
5
,
6
]
. Traditional DL approaches are based on training a neural network to map low-quality observations to high-quality images
[
7
,
8
,
9
,
10
,
11
]
. There is an increasing interest in leveraging DL for enhancing the quality of SANS data
[
12
]
, predicting structural geometry of sample material
[
13
]
, and performing data analysis and feature extraction from SANS
[
14
,
15
]
.
Despite the promising performance of DL in computationally enhancing quality of SANS data
[
12
]
, the lack of experimentally collected SANS data poses a challenge for training effective DL models. Domain adaptation is a promising strategy to address the issue of limited data availability for training DL models, a challenge often faced in SANS experiments where acquiring large-scale datasets is impractical. Domain adaptation involves pre-training a DL model on one dataset (domain) and adapting it to perform effectively on a different domain
[
16
,
17
,
18
]
.

Plug-and-play (PnP) priors
[
19
,
20
]
is a computational framework widely-used for designing DL methods for solving imaging inverse problems. PnP methods leverage powerful DL denoisers as priors by combining them with the forward model of the imaging instruments. PnP has been successfully used in various imaging inverse problems including super-resolution, phase retrieval, microscopy, and medical imaging
[
7
,
9
,
21
,
22
]
(see recent reviews
[
23
,
24
]
).

Despite the success of PnP on a number of imaging inverse problems, it has never been applied for restoring high-SNR SANS data. In this paper, we present the first investigation into PnP for restoring high-SNR SANS data collected in an accelerated fashion that results in low-SNR SANS detector images. Our PnP restoration method, called PR-SANS, restores high-SNR detector images from low-SNR measurements with shorter acquisition time, thereby mitigating the need for long acquisition times in SANS experiments. To address the limited amount of experimentally collected SANS datasets for training deep priors for PnP, we propose a domain adaptation strategy that adapts a generic image prior (trained on natural grayscale images) into a dedicated SANS prior. We present a theoretical convergence analysis of PR-SANS that considers the errors introduced by using adapted deep priors instead of ideal ones. We show on experimentally collected SANS data that PR-SANS can restore high-SNR SANS detector images while significantly reducing the acquisition times for detector image of equivalent SNR (
12\times
faster data acquisition). Our results numerically show the relationship between the amount of training SANS data and the final restoration performance.

Proposed Method

Image restoration.
We propose to formulate the recovery of the clean image
{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
from the noisy SANS measurement
{\bm{y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
as an image restoration problem of form

{\bm{y}}={\bm{x}}+{\bm{e}},

(1)

where
{\bm{e}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the noise. It is common to formulate restoration problems as an optimization problem

{\widehat{\bm{x}}}=\mathop{\mathsf{arg\,min}}_{{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}f({\bm{x}})\quad\text{with}\quad f({\bm{x}})=g({\bm{x}})+h({\bm{x}}),

(2)

where
g
denotes the data-fidelity term that measures the consistency of recovered signal with the measurement
{\bm{y}}
and
h
is a regularizer that imposes prior knowledge on the recovered signal. Widely-used data-fidelity and regularization terms are least-squares

g({\bm{x}})=\frac{1}{2}\|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}\|_{2}^{2}
and total variation (TV) regularizer
h({\bm{x}})=\tau\|\bm{D}({\bm{x}})\|_{1}
, where
\bm{D}
is image gradient and
\tau>0
is the regularization parameter
[
25
]
.

PnP.
PnP is one of the most popular approaches for solving imaging inverse problems where image priors are specified using pre-trained image denoisers
[
24
]
. Our proposed PR-SANS is a variant of PnP with the update rule

{\bm{x}}^{k}={\mathsf{prox}}_{\gamma g}({\bm{x}}^{k-1}-\gamma\tau{\mathsf{\widehat{R}}}({\bm{x}}^{k-1}))\quad\text{with}\quad{\mathsf{\widehat{R}}}={\mathsf{I}}-{\widehat{\mathsf{D}}}_{\sigma},

(3)

where
{\widehat{\mathsf{D}}}_{\sigma}
is the domain-adapted deep prior with parameter
\sigma>0
for controlling the noise strength,
\tau>0
is the regularization parameter, and
\gamma>0
is the penalty parameter. We denote the ideal SANS prior as mapping
{\mathsf{D}}_{\sigma}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
pre-trained to solve the following restoration problem

{\bm{z}}={\bm{x}}+{\bm{n}}\quad\text{with}\quad{\bm{n}}\sim{\mathcal{N}}(0,\sigma^{2}{\bm{I}}),

(4)

where
{\bm{n}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is additive white Gaussian noise (AWGN) of variance
\sigma^{2}

[
26
,
27
]
.
As common in PnP methods
[
24
]
, we assume that
{\mathsf{D}}_{\sigma}
performs MMSE estimation of
{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
for the problem (
4
)

{\mathsf{D}}_{\sigma}({\bm{z}})=\mathbb{E}[{\bm{x}}|{\bm{z}}]=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}{\bm{x}}p_{{\bm{x}}|{\bm{z}}}({\bm{x}};{\bm{z}}){\mathsf{d}}{\bm{x}},

(5)

where
p_{\bm{x}}
represent the distribution of target dataset.
When the PnP method converges, it converges to
{\bm{x}^{\ast}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
that satisfies

\nabla g({\bm{x}^{\ast}})+\tau{\mathsf{R}}({\bm{x}^{\ast}})=\bm{0},

(6)

where
{\bm{x}^{\ast}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the fixed point.
We discuss the connection of fixed points with stationary points of an objective function
f=g+h
for some regularizer
h
in Section
6
.

Domain adaptation.

In traditional DL frameworks for addressing image restoration tasks, it is common
to train convolutional neural networks (CNNs) to map observations to desired images. In this approach, the training and testing data are often drawn from the same distribution
[
28
,
29
,
30
,
31
]
.
However, domain shift can occur when training and testing data originate from different distributions
[
32
,
33
,
34
,
35
,
36
]
.
Domain adaptation techniques can address this issue by generalizing a DL model trained on a source domain to a domain of interest (target domain)
[
17
,
37
,
38
]
. Domain adaptation is particularly beneficial in scenarios where the available training data is insufficient
[
39
]
. Among adaptation techniques, a simple strategy involves fine-tuning a pre-trained neural network using limited available data from the target domain. This method relies on the premise that image features can be transferred across different domains
[
39
,
16
,
18
]
.

Our domain adaptation relies on a dataset of natural images
{\mathcal{D}}_{s}=\{{\bm{x}}_{i}^{s},{\bm{z}}_{i}^{s}\}_{i=1}^{N}
as the source dataset.
{\mathcal{D}}_{s}
is used to train the initial denoiser
{\mathsf{D}}^{s}_{\sigma}
by minimizing the MSE loss

{\mathcal{L}}({\mathsf{D}}^{s}_{\sigma})=\mathbb{E}\left[\|{\bm{x}}^{s}-{\mathsf{D}}^{s}_{\sigma}({\bm{z}}^{s})\|_{2}^{2}\right].

(7)

The parameters of this initial denoiser, denoted by
{\bm{\theta}}^{s}

, are stored for the subsequent adaptation phase. The target dataset
{\mathcal{D}}_{t}=\{{\bm{x}}_{i}^{t},{\bm{z}}_{i}^{t}\}_{i=1}^{K}
(where
K\ll N
) includes a small number of SANS detector images.
The adaptation involves fine-tuning the initial denoiser using the target dataset
{\mathcal{D}}_{t}
with the MSE loss

{\mathcal{L}}({\mathsf{\widehat{D}}}_{\sigma})=\mathbb{E}\left[\|{\bm{x}}^{t}-{\mathsf{\widehat{D}}}_{\sigma}({\bm{z}}^{t})\|_{2}^{2}\right],

(8)

starting from the initial parameters
{\bm{\theta}}^{s}
of the denoiser pre-trained on source data.

3
Theoretical Analysis

In this section, we present the theoretical convergence of the iterates generated by PR-SANS with adapted priors. Our analysis will require several assumptions that act as sufficient conditions for our theoretical results.

Assumption 1
.

The prior density
p_{{\bm{x}}}
is non-degenerate over
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

If the support of the probability density
p_{{\bm{x}}}
is confined to space with a lower dimension than
n
, it is degenerate over
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We use the assumption of non-degenerative prior density to establish a link between the operator (
5
) and the following regularizer

h({\bm{x}})=-\tau\sigma^{2}\log p_{{\bm{z}}}({\bm{x}}),\quad{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

(9)

where
\tau
is the regularization parameter,
p_{\bm{z}}
is the density of observation (
4
), and
\sigma^{2}
is the AWGN level used for training
{\mathsf{D}}_{\sigma}
(the derivation is in Lemma
3
).
The link between MMSE operators and and regularization beyond non-degenerate priors can be found in
[
40
]
. It can be proven that function
h
is infinitely continuously differentiable
[
41
,
40
]
.

Assumption 2
.

The adapted restoration operator
{\mathsf{\widehat{D}}}_{\sigma}
satisfies

\|{\mathsf{\widehat{D}}}_{\sigma}({\bm{x}}^{k})-{\mathsf{D}}_{\sigma}({\bm{x}}^{k})\|_{2}\leq\varepsilon_{k},\quad k=0,1,2,3,\ldots

where
{\mathsf{D}}_{\sigma}
is given in (
5
).

This assumption serves as a bounding constraint on the discrepancy between the domain adapted
{\widehat{\mathsf{D}}}_{\sigma}
and ideal MMSE restoration operator
{\mathsf{D}}_{\sigma}
for SANS data at each iteration of PR-SANS algorithm.

Our analysis assumes that at every iteration, PR-SANS uses the adapted MMSE restoration operator, where the ideal MMSE restoration operator
{\mathsf{D}}_{\sigma}
is unavailable. We consider the case where at iteration
k
of PR-SANS, the distance of the outputs of
{\mathsf{D}}_{\sigma}
and
{\mathsf{\widehat{D}}}_{\sigma}
is bounded by a constant
\varepsilon_{k}
.

Assumption 3
.

The function
g
is continuously differentiable. Additionally,
\nabla g
and
\nabla h
are Lipschitz continuous with constants
L>0
and
M>0
, respectively.

Lipschitz continuity is a standard assumption in the context of imaging inverse problems
[
42
]
.

Assumption 4
.

The data-fidelity term
g
and the implicit regularizer
h
are bounded from below.

This assumption implies that there exists
f^{\ast}=f({\bm{x}}^{*})>-\infty
such that
f({\bm{x}})\geq f^{\ast}
for all
{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Theorem 1
.

Run PR-SANS with the adapted MMSE restoration operator
{\mathsf{\widehat{D}}}_{\sigma}
for
t\geq 1
iterations under Assumptions
1
-
4
using a step-size
\gamma>0
. Then, for each iteration
1\leq k\leq t
, we have

\mathop{\mathsf{min}}_{1\leq k\leq t}\|\nabla f({\bm{x}}^{k})\|^{2}_{2}\leq\frac{1}{t}\sum_{k=1}^{t}\|\nabla f({\bm{x}}^{k})\|^{2}_{2}\leq\frac{B_{1}}{t}\left(f({\bm{x}}^{0})-f({\bm{x}}^{*})\right)+B_{2}{\overline{\varepsilon}}^{2}_{t},

where
B_{1}>0
and
B_{2}>0
are iteration independent constants, and
{\overline{\varepsilon}}_{t}^{2}\,\coloneqq\,(1/t)\sum_{k=1}^{t}\varepsilon^{2}_{k-1}
. Additionally, if the sequence of error terms
\{\varepsilon_{i}\}_{i\geq 0}
is square-summable, we have that
\|\nabla f({\bm{x}}^{k})\|\to 0
as
k\to\infty
.

The expressions for constant
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
are given in the proof. Theorem 1 shows that the iterates generated by PR-SANS with adapted priors satisfy
\nabla f({\bm{x}}^{k})\to\bm{0}
as
k\to\infty
when the sequence of error term
\varepsilon_{k}
is square-summable. The theorem shows that if the sequence of error is square-summable, PR-SANS asymptotically achieves a stationary point of
f
. Additionally, if the sequence of error is
not
square-summable, one can still precisely characterize the error due to the discrepancy between adapted and ideal MMSE denoisers.

Experimental Results

We perform numerical validation of PR-SANS for restoring experimentally collected SANS detector images. Our experiments are designed to evaluate the effectiveness of the PR-SANS algorithm by comparing its performance against baselines, such as the total variation (TV) denoising algorithm and end-to-end CNNs. Furthermore, we highlight how the complexity of the sample in the domain adaptation of deep priors influences the performance of PR-SANS.

We compare PR-SANS performance with several established methods including end-to-end CNNs and TV denoising algorithm, which represents a non-learning-based approach to noise reduction

DnCNN models that are trained with a limited amount of data are labeled as "zero-start," while those trained with domain adaptation strategy are referred to as "adapted". Additionally, to investigate the effect of sample complexity on the domain adaptation of deep image priors, we evaluate the performance of PR-SANS with multiple adapted priors that differ in the quantity of target domain samples used for adaptation. Our evaluation metrics include SNR and root-square-mean-error (RMSE) for the recovery of 2D detector images, alongside normalized-mean-square-error (NMSE) and mean absolute error (MAE) for performance analysis of 1D intensity plots. We used a pre-trained DnCNN model, initially trained for reducing AWGN with a noise level parameter of
\sigma=5

For the training of both adapted and zero-start priors, 100 pairs of low- and high-SNR SANS detector images were used, with an extra set of 10 SANS detector image pairs reserved for validation and 10 pairs reserved for testing. The models were trained for 300 epoch and the model with the best performance on validation set was chosen. The number of iteration for PR-SANS was set to 20. The penalty parameter is set to
\gamma=0.7
for all the experiments. The regularization parameter
\tau
was optimized for the best performance. The data collection procedure for SANS experiments is detailed in section
6.1

5
Conclusion

In this paper, we introduce PR-SANS, a PnP algorithm leveraging deep image priors to recover high-SNR SANS data.
PR-SANS enhances the acquisition process by restoring high-SNR detector images from low-SNR SANS data with shorter acquisition time. Our findings reveal that domain adaptation successfully addresses the challenge of limited training datasets, with a combination of 400 natural images and 100 SANS datasets proving adequate for obtaining adapted priors that successfully restore SANS detector images through PR-SANS. Comparative visual, empirical, and numerical analyses indicate that PR-SANS surpasses both traditional restoration algorithms, such as Total Variation (TV), and Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) in the restoration of SANS data.

Theorem
.

Run PR-SANS with the adapted MMSE restoration operator
{\mathsf{\widehat{D}}}_{\sigma}
for
t\geq 1
iterations under Assumptions
1
-
4
using a step-size
0<\gamma\leq\mathop{\mathsf{min}}(1/M,1/L)
. Then, for each iteration

\mathop{\mathsf{min}}_{1\leq k\leq t}\|\nabla f({\bm{x}}^{k})\|^{2}_{2}\leq\frac{1}{t}\sum_{k=1}^{t}\|\nabla f({\bm{x}}^{k})\|^{2}_{2}\leq\frac{B_{1}}{t}\left(f({\bm{x}}^{0})-f({\bm{x}}^{*})\right)+B_{2}{\overline{\varepsilon}}_{t}^{2},

.

Proof.

The update rule for adapted operator

and ideal operator
{\mathsf{D}}_{\sigma}

From the optimality conditions for the update rule of ideal operator, we have

\frac{1}{\gamma}({\overline{\bm{x}}}^{k}-{\bm{x}}^{k-1})+\nabla h({\bm{x}}^{k-1})+\nabla g({\overline{\bm{x}}}^{k})=0.

By using this equation, the gradient of the objective function

\displaystyle\|\nabla f({\bm{x}}^{k})\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\|\nabla g({\bm{x}}^{k})+\nabla h({\bm{x}}^{k})\|_{2}=\|\nabla g({\bm{x}}^{k})-\nabla g({\overline{\bm{x}}}^{k})+\nabla h({\bm{x}}^{k})-\nabla h({\bm{x}}^{k-1})+\frac{1}{\gamma}({\bm{x}}^{k-1}-{\bm{x}}^{k})+\frac{1}{\gamma}({\bm{x}}^{k}-{\overline{\bm{x}}}^{k})\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left(L+\frac{1}{\gamma}\right)\alpha\varepsilon_{k-1}+\left(M+\frac{1}{\gamma}\right)\|{\bm{x}}^{k}-{\bm{x}}^{k-1}\|_{2},

where we used Lemma

\nabla h
from Assumptions

(a+b)^{2}\leq 2a^{2}+2b^{2}
, we have

\|\nabla f({\bm{x}}^{k})\|^{2}_{2}\leq A_{1}\|{\bm{x}}^{k}-{\bm{x}}^{k-1}\|_{2}^{2}+A_{2}\varepsilon^{2}_{k-1},

where
A_{1}\,\coloneqq\,2(M+1/\gamma)^{2}
and
A_{2}\,\coloneqq\,2\alpha^{2}(L+1/\gamma)^{2}
. By using the result from Lemma

and averaging both sides of the bound over
