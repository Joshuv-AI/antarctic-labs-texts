Criteria for the (in)stability of planar interfaces in singularly perturbed 2-component reaction-diffusion equations
Paul Carter, Arjen Doelman, Kaitlynn Lilly, Erin Obermayer, & Shreyas Rao

Abstract
We consider a class of singularly perturbed 2-component reaction-diffusion equations which admit bistable traveling front solutions, manifesting as sharp, slow-fast-slow, interfaces between stable homogeneous rest states. In many example systems, such as models of desertification fronts in dryland ecosystems, such fronts can exhibit an instability by which the interface destabilizes into fingering patterns. Motivated by the appearance of such patterns, we propose two versions of a 2D stability criterion for (transversal) long wavelength perturbations along the interface of these traveling slow-fast-slow fronts. The fronts are constructed using geometric singular perturbation techniques by connecting slow orbits on two distinct normally hyperbolic slow manifolds through a heteroclinic orbit in the fast problem. The associated stability criteria are expressed in terms of the nonlinearities of the system and the slow-fast-slow structure of the fronts. We illustrate and further elaborate the general set-up by explicitly working out the existence and transversal (in)stability of traveling fronts in a number of example systems/models. We analytically establish the instability of invading bare soil/vegetation interfaces against transversal long wavelength perturbations in several dryland ecosystem models and numerically recover fingering vegetation patterns counter-invading an invading desertification front.

Finally, we briefly remark on the writing style of the paper. Based on the present state of the art literature on geometric singularly perturbation theory (for the existence problem) and the analysis of singularly perturbed spectral operators (for the stability), all our findings can be established rigorously. However, for transparency of presentation we have chosen not to formulate our main results as theorems, and thus not to provide all details of the proofs of these theorems. Instead, we have chosen to present our work in the present hands-on setting, with a central role for section
4
in which we explicitly show how our methods work in the context of a number of diverse and explicit problems. In a way, our paper can be seen as a ‘user guide’: we have written the paper such that it can directly be used for the analysis of (the stability of) planar interfaces in explicit systems of the type (
1.1

for some more details. However, the precise nature of the conditions imposed in the literature are for a large part for practical reasons, the methods used (and developed) can typically also be applied to more general systems (see again section
4.3.1
). We also refer to the concluding paragraph of (upcoming) section
2.1
and to Remark
2.1
for an extended discussion on the assumptions under which slow-fast-slow fronts can be constructed.

There is one crucial additional assumption for the subsequent stability analysis:

\bullet

(S-I)
The constructed slow-fast-slow heteroclinic traveling front is stable against longitudinal perturbations, i.e. perturbations that only depend on
x
.

We refer to section
2.2
for a more precise statement and a brief discussion.

2.1
The construction of slow-fast-slow interfaces

There are 2 equivalent ways to write (
1.3
) as a 4-dimensional spatial dynamical system,

({\rm fast})\;\;\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}u_{\xi}&=&p\\
p_{\xi}&=&-F(u,v)-c\tau p\\
v_{\xi}&=&\varepsilon q\\
q_{\xi}&=&\varepsilon[-G(u,v)-\varepsilon cq]\end{array}\right.\;\;({\rm slow})\;\;\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}\varepsilon u_{X}&=&p\\
\varepsilon p_{X}&=&-F(u,v)-c\tau p\\
v_{X}&=&q\\
q_{X}&=&-G(u,v)-\varepsilon cq\end{array}\right.

(2.1)

with
X=\varepsilon\xi
the slow (spatial) coordinate. The fronts/interfaces of interest correspond to heteroclinic orbits
\gamma_{h}(\xi)
in (
2.1
) that connect its critical points
(\bar{U}^{-},0,\bar{V}^{-},0)
and
(\bar{U}^{+},0,\bar{V}^{+},0)
for a critical value
c_{\ast}
of the speed
c
. The orbits
\gamma_{h}(\xi)
can be constructed explicitly by the methods of geometric singular perturbation theory. To do so, we reintroduce the two slow manifolds
{\cal M}^{\pm}_{0}
,

{\cal M}^{\pm}_{0}=\{(u,p,v,q)\in\mathbb{R}^{4}:u=f^{\pm}(v),p=0,v^{\pm}_{-}<v<v^{\pm}_{+}\}

(2.2)

with
F(f^{\pm}(v),v)\equiv 0
and ‘endpoints’
v^{\pm}_{\pm}
at which
F_{u}(f^{\pm}(v^{\pm}_{\pm}),v^{\pm}_{\pm})=0
(where we note that
{\cal M}^{\pm}_{0}
may be unbounded, i.e. that
v^{\pm}_{+}
may be
\infty
and/or
v^{\pm}_{-}
may be
-\infty
). As stated in
(E-III)
, it is necessary for the construction of
\gamma_{h}(\xi)
to assume that the projections of
{\cal M}^{\pm}_{0}
on the
v
-axis overlap, i.e. that
(v^{-}_{-},v^{-}_{+})\cap(v^{+}_{-},v^{+}_{+})\neq\emptyset
. Naturally,
(\bar{U}^{\pm},0,\bar{V}^{\pm},0)\in{\cal M}^{\pm}_{0}
are critical points of the full system (
2.1
) and
(\bar{V}^{\pm},0)
of the reduced slow systems,

v_{XX}+G(f^{\pm}(v),v)=0,

(2.3)

where we note that these (planar) systems are integrable (Hamiltonian) under the implicit assumption that
\varepsilon c\to 0
as
\varepsilon\to 0
(cf. (
3.2
) for
\tau={\cal O}(\varepsilon)
where
\varepsilon c=\tilde{c}={\cal O}(1)
). The assumption that both
(\bar{U}^{\pm},\bar{V}^{\pm})
are stable as homogeneous backgrounds states of (
1.1
) has a direct impact on the nature of the critical points
(\bar{U}^{\pm},0,\bar{V}^{\pm},0)
of (
2.1
), of the critical points
(\bar{V}^{\pm},0)
of (
2.3
) and of the slow manifolds
{\cal M}^{\pm}_{0}
. To see this, we note that the states
(\bar{U}^{\pm},\bar{V}^{\pm})
are stable if the eigenvalues
\lambda^{\pm}_{1,2}(k,\ell)
of

\tau\lambda^{2}-\left[(\bar{F}_{u}^{\pm}+\tau\bar{G}_{v}^{\pm})-\frac{(\tau+\varepsilon^{2})(k^{2}+\ell^{2})}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right]\lambda+\left[(\bar{F}_{u}^{\pm}\bar{G}_{v}^{\pm}-\bar{F}_{v}^{\pm}\bar{G}_{u}^{\pm})-\frac{(\bar{F}_{u}^{\pm}+\varepsilon^{2}\bar{G}_{v}^{\pm})(k^{2}+\ell^{2})}{\varepsilon^{2}}+\frac{(k^{2}+\ell^{2})^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right]=0

(2.4)

satisfy Re
\left(\lambda^{\pm}_{1,2}(k,\ell)\right)<0
for all
k,\ell\in\mathbb{R}
(where
\bar{F}_{u}^{\pm}=\frac{\partial F}{\partial U}(\bar{U}^{\pm},\bar{V}^{\pm})
, etc., and
k,\ell
are the (Fourier) wavenumbers in the
x,y
-directions)
[
10
]
.
For
0<\varepsilon\ll 1
, this is the case if (and only if),

\bar{F}_{u}^{\pm}+\tau\bar{G}_{v}^{\pm}<0,\;\bar{F}_{u}^{\pm}\bar{G}_{v}^{\pm}-\bar{F}_{v}^{\pm}\bar{G}_{u}^{\pm}>0,\;\bar{F}_{u}^{\pm}<0

(2.5)

[
11
]
, where we note that the last 2 conditions come from the observation that the
\lambda
-independent term in (
2.4
) must be positive for all
k,\ell\in\mathbb{R}
(consider
k=\ell=0
and
k^{2}+\ell^{2}={\cal O}(\varepsilon)
). Together,
\bar{F}_{u}^{\pm}\bar{G}_{v}^{\pm}-\bar{F}_{v}^{\pm}\bar{G}_{u}^{\pm}>0
and
\bar{F}_{u}^{\pm}<0
imply that
(\bar{V}^{\pm},0)
is a saddle of the slow flow (
2.3
) – where we note that
(f^{\pm})^{\prime}(\bar{V}^{\pm})=-\bar{F}_{v}^{\pm}/\bar{F}_{u}^{\pm}
. Moreover, the critical point
(\bar{U}^{\pm},0)
of the fast reduced system (
1.4
) at the level
v_{0}=\bar{V}^{\pm}
also must be a saddle (
\bar{F}_{u}^{\pm}<0
). Since
0<\varepsilon\ll 1
this implies that the eigenvalues
\lambda^{\pm}_{j}
,
j=1,...,4
associated to the critical points
(\bar{U}^{\pm},0,\bar{V}^{\pm},0)
of (
2.1
) satisfy
\lambda^{\pm}_{1}<\lambda^{\pm}_{2}<0<\lambda^{\pm}_{3}<\lambda^{\pm}_{4}
(with
\lambda^{\pm}_{1,4}={\cal O}(1)
determined by (
1.4
) and
\lambda^{\pm}_{2,3}={\cal O}(\varepsilon)
by (
2.3
)). Moreover, the various branches
u=f^{j}(v)
of the manifold
F(u,v)=0
are separated from each other by transition points at which
F_{u}(u,v)=0
(Fig.
1
(a)), i.e.
F_{u}(f^{\pm}(v^{\pm}_{\pm}),v^{\pm}_{\pm})=0
in the case of the curves
f^{\pm}(v)
. As a consequence, the sign of
F_{u}(f^{\pm}(v),v)
does not change along
{\cal M}^{\pm}_{0}
: the stability of
(\bar{U}^{-},0,\bar{V}^{-},0)
implies that
F_{u}(f^{\pm}(v),v)<0
on
{\cal M}^{\pm}_{0}
. This means that
{\cal M}^{\pm}_{0}
are both
normally hyperbolic
, so that they persist as invariant manifolds
{\cal M}^{\pm}
for the flow of (
2.1
) (for
\varepsilon
sufficiently small
[
18
,
28
]
) – see also Remark
2.1
.

The ‘skeleton structure’ of the heteroclinic orbit
\gamma_{h}(\xi)
can now be built by combining the reduced slow flows (
2.3
) with an intermediate fast jump governed by the fast reduced system (
1.4
). During its first slow part,
\gamma_{h}(\xi)
will follow, i.e. will be exponentially close to, the unstable manifold
{\cal W}^{\rm u}((\bar{V}^{-},0))
of the saddle
(\bar{V}^{-},0)
on
{\cal M}^{-}_{0}
(Fig.
3
). Likewise, it will follow the stable manifold
{\cal W}^{\rm s}((\bar{V}^{+},0))
of the saddle
(\bar{V}^{+},0)
on
{\cal M}^{+}_{0}
during its second slow part. Neither the
v

-component of
\gamma_{h}(\xi)
will change (at leading order in

) during the fast jump (
1.4
). Thus, the
\gamma_{h}
-skeleton can be constructed if the unstable manifold
{\cal W}^{\rm u}((\bar{V}^{-},0))
on
{\cal M}^{-}_{0}
intersects the stable manifold
{\cal W}^{\rm s}((\bar{V}^{+},0))
on
{\cal M}^{+}_{0}
in the combined projections of the reduced slow flows on
{\cal M}^{\pm}_{0}
(Fig.
3
): (the skeleton of)
\gamma_{h}(\xi)
‘takes off’ from
{\cal M}^{-}_{0}
at an intersection
(v_{\ast},q_{\ast})\in{\cal W}^{\rm u}((\bar{V}^{-},0))\cap{\cal W}^{\rm s}((\bar{V}^{+},0))
, follows the fast field, and ‘touches down’ on
{\cal M}^{+}_{0}
again at
(v_{\ast},q_{\ast})
. Or more precisely,
\gamma_{h}(\xi)
follows the fast flow from the point
(u^{-}_{\ast},0,v_{\ast},q_{\ast})\in{\cal M}^{-}_{0}
to
(u^{+}_{\ast},0,v_{\ast},q_{\ast})\in{\cal M}^{+}_{0}
, with
u^{\pm}_{\ast}=f^{\pm}(v_{\ast})
, so that
(u^{\pm}_{\ast},0)
are critical points of (
1.4
) with
v_{0}=v_{\ast}
– see Fig.
1
(a) and upcoming Fig.
5
(a) with a 3-dimensional sketch in the context of ecosystem model (
4.1
).

The speed
c_{\ast}
of the traveling interface is determined by the passage through the fast field (at
v=v_{\ast}
,
q=q_{\ast}
). In general, i.e. for general
c
, the unstable manifold of the saddle
(u^{-}_{\ast},0)
of (
1.4
) will not be connected to the stable manifold of
(u^{-}_{\ast},0)
– where we recall that the assumed stability of background states
(\bar{U}^{\pm},\bar{V}^{\pm})
implies that the critical points of (
1.4
) are saddles. The situation is now completely similar to that of constructing heteroclinic traveling fronts for scalar equation (
1.2
) – in fact, by (re)defining
F(u,v_{\ast})
as
H(u)
, (
1.4
) is identical to the (spatial) traveling wave planar dynamical system associated to (
1.2
). Thus, for a given
v_{\ast}
we can (by phase plane techniques) determine a unique value
c_{\ast}
of
c
for which there is a (planar) heteroclinic connection
(u_{\ast}(\xi),p_{\ast}(\xi))
between the saddles
(u^{\pm}_{\ast},0)
. By construction,
p_{\ast}(\xi)=u_{\ast,\xi}(\xi)
is either strictly positive or strictly negative:
(u_{\ast},p_{\ast})
forms the (monotonic) fast component of the above skeleton structure (Fig.
1
). (It should be noted that although the fast jump
u_{\ast}(\xi)
is monotonic, the
u
-component
u_{h}(\xi)
of the full slow-fast-slow connecting front typically is not monotonic – see Fig.
1
.)

In the setting of a given model it may be a technical undertaking to explicitly set up the construction of the skeleton of the heteroclinic orbit
\gamma_{h}(\xi)=(u_{h}(\xi),p_{h}(\xi),v_{h}(\xi),q_{h}(\xi))
– see section
4
. Here, in the general setting, we assume that this has been done. By definition, the piecewise approximations of
v_{h}(\xi)
are given by
v^{-}_{u}(X)
near
{\cal M}^{-}_{0}
and
v^{+}_{s}(X)
near
{\cal M}^{+}_{0}
, while
v_{h}(\xi)=v_{\ast}
, i.e. constant, over the fast jump; by defining
u^{-}_{u}(X)=f^{-}(v^{-}_{u}(X))
and
u^{+}_{s}(X)=f^{+}(v^{+}_{s}(X))
we obtain similar leading order approximations of
u_{h}(\xi)
in the slow fields. Naturally,
u_{h}(\xi)
is approximated by
u_{\ast}(\xi)
during the fast jump – see Figs.
1
and
3
. Note that
p_{h}(\xi)=u_{h,\xi}(\xi)
and
q_{h}(X)=v_{h,X}(X)
by (
2.1
). From the geometrical point of view, the construction of the full slow-fast-slow front is now based on a transversal intersection of the (3-dimensional) stable and unstable manifolds
{\cal W}^{\rm u}({\cal M}^{-}_{0})
and
{\cal W}^{\rm s}({\cal M}^{+}_{0})
(in the
\varepsilon\to 0
limit) and it thus follows by the methods of geometric singular perturbation theory that asymptotically close to this skeleton there indeed is an orbit – the orbit
\gamma_{h}(\xi)
– that is a heteroclinic connection between the critical points
(\bar{U}^{-},0,\bar{V}^{-},0)
and
(\bar{U}^{+},0,\bar{V}^{+},0)
. This orbit corresponds by construction to the (planar) traveling interface
(U(x,y,t),V(x,y,t))=(U_{h}(x-c_{\ast}t),V_{h}(x-c_{\ast}t))=(u_{h}(\xi),v_{h}(\xi))
– see Fig.
1
.

Wrapping up the existence analysis, we return to the conditions under which the traveling interface
(U_{h}(\xi),V_{h}(\xi))
can be constructed. Additional to
(E-I)
-
(E-III)
stated at the beginning of this section, that did not consider the slow flows on
{\cal M}^{\pm}_{0}
, we now add the fourth condition:

\bullet

(E-IV)
The unstable manifold
{\cal W}^{\rm u}((\bar{V}^{-},0))
of the slow flow (
2.3
) on
{\cal M}^{-}_{0}
intersects the stable manifold
{\cal W}^{\rm s}((\bar{V}^{+},0))
of the slow flow (
2.3
) on
{\cal M}^{+}_{0}
in their combined projection.

Note that this certainly is not ‘automatically’ the case, on the other hand,
{\cal W}^{\rm u}((\bar{V}^{-},0))\cap{\cal W}^{\rm s}((\bar{V}^{+},0))
may consist of several points
(v_{\ast,i},q_{\ast,i})
– see Fig.
3
– so that there may exist several distinct connecting fronts for a given choice of parameters
\vec{\mu}
. For a given explicit system, there also is a ‘hidden’ condition under which the skeleton structure can be built: the take off/touch down points
(u^{\pm}_{\ast},0,v_{\ast},q_{\ast})
must be on
{\cal M}^{\pm}_{0}
, i.e.
v_{\ast}\in(v^{-}_{-},v^{-}_{+})\cap(v^{+}_{-},v^{+}_{+})
. Although this must be the case by construction, in a given system a manifold
{\cal M}^{\pm}_{0}
may extend beyond one of its end/transition points
(f^{\pm}(v^{\pm}_{\pm}),v^{\pm}_{\pm})
as non-normally hyperbolic manifold – see for instance (
4.13
) in section
4.1.2
– and then it may happen that (the projections of)
{\cal W}^{\rm u}((\bar{V}^{-},0))
and
{\cal W}^{\rm s}((\bar{V}^{+},0))
intersect at a
v
-coordinate
v_{\ast}\notin(v^{-}_{-},v^{-}_{+})\cap(v^{+}_{-},v^{+}_{+})
. In that case at least one of the points
(u^{\pm}_{\ast},0,v_{\ast},q_{\ast})
will not be a saddle for the fast flow (
1.4
) so that the fast jump between
{\cal M}^{\pm}_{0}
cannot be made (by a monotonic fast connection
u_{\ast}(\xi)
– see Remark
2.1
).

We conclude that the existence problem for (
1.1
) differs from that of (
1.2
) in the sense that there always is a uniquely determined traveling connecting (monotonic, planar) interface in (
1.2
) (when there are two stable background states), while we found that this certainly is not the case for singularly perturbed 2-component model (
1.1
). Nevertheless, the conditions on
F(U,V)
and
G(U,V)
are quite mild and the slow-fast-slow interfaces
(U_{h}(\xi),U_{h}(\xi))
can be constructed for many models – as we shall see in section
4
.

Remark 2.1
.

As in
[
11
]
, we consider a slow manifold of (
2.1
) to be normally hyperbolic if it is normally hyperbolic for all
c
with
c\tau={\cal O}(1)
. The dashed parts of
\{F(U,V)=0\}
in Fig.
1
(a) are normally hyperbolic for
c\neq 0
, but elliptic for
c=0
, and thus not considered as normally hyperbolic in the present analysis. Orbits that (for

that are not normally hyperbolic in the present sense, i.e. that are elliptic for

(\bar{U}^{\pm},\bar{V}^{\pm})
necessarily must be on invariant manifolds that are normally hyperbolic for all

.

2.2
Transversal instability of the interface

To determine the (spectral) stability of the traveling interface
(U_{h}(x-c_{\ast}t),V_{h}(x-c_{\ast}t))
as solution of (
1.1
), we consider perturbations of the form,

(U(x,y,t),V(x,y,t))=(U_{h}(\xi)+\bar{u}(\xi)e^{\lambda t+i\ell y},V_{h}(\xi)+\bar{v}(\xi)e^{\lambda t+i\ell y}),\;\;\lambda=\lambda(\ell)\in\mathbb{C},\ell\in\mathbb{R},

(2.6)

and derive the linearized system,

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcrclcccccc}\tau\lambda\bar{u}&=&\bar{u}_{\xi\xi}&-&\ell^{2}\bar{u}&+&c_{\ast}\tau\bar{u}_{\xi}&+&F_{u}(u_{h}(\xi),v_{h}(\xi))\bar{u}&+&F_{v}(u_{h}(\xi),v_{h}(\xi))\bar{v}\\
\lambda\bar{v}&=&\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\bar{v}_{\xi\xi}&-&\frac{\ell^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\bar{v}&+&c_{\ast}\bar{v}_{\xi}&+&G_{u}(u_{h}(\xi),v_{h}(\xi))\bar{u}&+&G_{v}(u_{h}(\xi),v_{h}(\xi))\bar{v}\end{array}\right.

(2.7)

which can equivalently be written as

\mathbb{L}(\xi)\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\bar{u}\\
\bar{v}\end{array}\right)=\lambda\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\bar{u}\\
\bar{v}\end{array}\right)+\ell^{2}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\frac{1}{\tau}\bar{u}\\[2.84526pt]
\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\bar{v}\end{array}\right)

(2.8)

where the
2\times 2
matrix operator
\mathbb{L}(\xi)
is given by

\mathbb{L}(\xi)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}{\cal L}_{u}(\xi)&\frac{1}{\tau}F_{v}(u_{h}(\xi),v_{h}(\xi))\\
G_{u}(u_{h}(\xi),v_{h}(\xi))&{\cal L}_{v}(\xi)\end{array}\right)

(2.9)

with Sturm-Liouville operators

{\cal L}_{u}(\xi)=\frac{1}{\tau}\left(\frac{d^{2}}{d\xi^{2}}+c_{\ast}\tau\frac{d}{d\xi}+F_{u}(u_{h}(\xi),v_{h}(\xi))\right),\;{\cal L}_{v}(\xi)=\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\frac{d^{2}}{d\xi^{2}}+c_{\ast}\frac{d}{d\xi}+G_{v}(u_{h}(\xi),v_{h}(\xi)).

(2.10)

We know by assumption
(E-I)
on the stability of the endstates
(\bar{U}^{\pm},\bar{V}^{\pm})
of the interface that the essential spectrum of
\mathbb{L}(\xi)
is in the stable complex half-plane, moreover we know by the translational invariance of (
1.1
) that
\lambda(0)=0
is an eigenvalue of (
2.8
) at
\ell=0
with eigenfunction
(\bar{u}(\xi),\bar{v}(\xi))=(u_{h,\xi}(\xi),v_{h,\xi}(\xi))
, the ‘derivative of the wave’. The interface
(U_{h}(\xi),V_{h}(\xi))
is spectrally stable if (
2.7
)/(
2.8
) only has integrable (nontrivial) solutions
(\bar{u}(X),\bar{v}(X))
for Re
(\lambda(\ell))\leq 0

[
29
]
.

As stated in the beginning of this section (and in the Introduction), we also impose assumption
(S-I)
: the interface is stable against 1-dimensional perturbations, i.e. longitudinal perturbations that do not depend on
y
. In other words, we assume that all elements of the discrete spectrum of
\mathbb{L}(\xi)
have Re
(\lambda(0))<0
, except for the – under this assumption – critical translational eigenvalue
\lambda_{c}(0)=0
. Moreover, we additionally impose the (generic) assumption that the dimension of the kernel of
\mathbb{L}
is equal to 1, i.e. that the parameters
\vec{\mu}
of (
1.1
) are chosen away from their (co-dimension 1) bifurcation sets – see also section
5
. Naturally, these assumptions need to be validated in the setting of an explicit model – something that typically can be done by exploiting the singularly perturbed nature of (
1.1
) by the methods of
[
1
,
6
,
12
,
14
,
35
,
36
,
49
]
and the references therein (although one should not underestimate the technicalities involved).

To analyze the impact of the
y
-direction, and thus the possibility of transversal instabilities, we consider the
\ell
-dependence of the critical eigenvalue
\lambda_{c}(\ell)
. As explained in the Introduction, we only consider long wavelength perturbations along the interface, i.e.
0<\ell\ll 1
. Recalling that the spectral problem (
2.7
)/(
2.8
) varies in
\ell^{2}
instead of
\ell
, we expand

\lambda_{c}(\ell)=\lambda_{c,2}\ell^{2}+{\cal O}(\ell^{4}),\;\;\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\bar{u}_{c}(\xi;\ell^{2})\\
\bar{v}_{c}(\xi;\ell^{2})\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}u_{h,\xi}(\xi)\\
v_{h,\xi}(\xi)\end{array}\right)+\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\bar{u}_{c,2}(\xi)\\
\bar{v}_{c,2}(\xi)\end{array}\right)\ell^{2}+{\cal O}(\ell^{4}).

(2.11)

By substitution of (
2.11
) into (
2.8
) we find at
{\cal O}(\ell^{2})
the inhomogeneous equation,

\mathbb{L}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\bar{u}_{c,2}(\xi)\\
\bar{v}_{c,2}(\xi)\end{array}\right)=\lambda_{c,2}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}u_{h,\xi}(\xi)\\
v_{h,\xi}(\xi)\end{array}\right)+\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\frac{1}{\tau}u_{h,\xi}(\xi)\\[2.84526pt]
\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}v_{h,\xi}(\xi)\end{array}\right)

Since
\mathbb{L}(\xi)
has a nontrivial kernel, the inhomogeneous term needs to satisfy a solvability/Fredholm condition
[
29
]
. Clearly
\mathbb{L}
is not self-adjoint (and neither are its diagonal elements
{\cal L}_{u}
and
{\cal L}_{v}
(
2.10
)). We therefore define the adjoint operator
\mathbb{L}^{A}(\xi)
,

\mathbb{L}^{A}(\xi)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\frac{1}{\tau}\left(\frac{d^{2}}{d\xi^{2}}-c_{\ast}\tau\frac{d}{d\xi}+F_{u}(u_{h}(\xi),v_{h}(\xi))\right)&G_{u}(u_{h}(\xi),v_{h}(\xi))\\
\frac{1}{\tau}F_{v}(u_{h}(\xi),v_{h}(\xi))&\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\frac{d^{2}}{d\xi^{2}}-c_{\ast}\frac{d}{d\xi}+G_{v}(u_{h}(\xi),v_{h}(\xi))\end{array}\right)

(2.12)

and its eigenfunction
(\bar{u}_{c}^{A}(\xi),\bar{v}_{c}^{A}(\xi))
at
\lambda_{c}^{A}=0
, i.e. the integrable (nontrivial) solution of
\mathbb{L}^{A}(\bar{u},\bar{v})=(0,0)
. It follows that,

\left<\lambda_{c,2}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}u_{h,\xi}(\xi)\\
v_{h,\xi}(\xi)\end{array}\right)+\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\frac{1}{\tau}u_{h,\xi}(\xi)\\[2.84526pt]
\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}v_{h,\xi}(\xi)\end{array}\right),\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\bar{u}^{A}_{c}(\xi)\\
\bar{v}^{A}_{c}(\xi)\end{array}\right)\right>=0,

(2.13)

which yields an explicit expression for
\lambda_{2,c}
,

\lambda_{2,c}=-\frac{\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\frac{1}{\tau}u_{h,\xi}(\xi)\bar{u}^{A}_{c}(\xi)+\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}v_{h,\xi}(\xi)\bar{v}^{A}_{c}(\xi)\right)\,d\xi}{\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(u_{h,\xi}(\xi)\bar{u}^{A}_{c}(\xi)+v_{h,\xi}(\xi)\bar{v}^{A}_{c}(\xi)\right)\,d\xi}.

(2.14)

The planar interface
(U_{h}(\xi),V_{h}(\xi))
is unstable with respond to long wavelength perturbations along the interface if
\lambda_{2,c}>0
. It follows from (

) that we can explicitly characterize this potential destabilization mechanism by determining (a leading order approximation in
\varepsilon

.

To do so, we rewrite
\mathbb{L}^{A}(\bar{u},\bar{v})=(0,0)

) and split this equation up into two slow parts and one fast part. The slow parts are at leading order given by,

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcccrcr}\varepsilon^{2}\bar{u}_{XX}&-&\varepsilon c_{\ast}\tau\bar{u}_{X}&+&F_{u}(u^{\pm}(X),v^{\pm}(X))\bar{u}&=&-\tau G_{u}(u^{\pm}(X),v^{\pm}(X))\bar{v}\\
\bar{v}_{XX}&-&\varepsilon c_{\ast}\bar{v}_{X}&+&G_{v}(u^{\pm}(X),v^{\pm}(X))\bar{v}&=&-\frac{1}{\tau}F_{v}(u^{\pm}(X),v^{\pm}(X))\bar{u}\end{array}\right.

(2.15)

where we have approximated the slow parts
(u_{h}(X),v_{h}(X)
by
(u^{\pm}(X),v^{\pm}(X))
and suppressed the
u
-,
s
-subscripts (i.e.
u^{\pm}(X)
is either
=u^{-}_{u}(X)
or
u^{+}_{s}(X)
, etc.). The fast part is (at leading order) given by

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcccrcr}\bar{u}_{\xi\xi}&-&c_{\ast}\tau\bar{u}_{\xi}&+&F_{u}(u_{\ast}(\xi),v_{\ast})\bar{u}&=&-\tau G_{u}(u_{\ast}(\xi),v_{\ast})\bar{v}\\
\bar{v}_{\xi\xi}&=&\varepsilon^{2}\left[c_{\ast}\bar{v}_{X}\right.&-&G_{v}(u_{\ast}(\xi),v_{\ast})\bar{v}&-&\left.\frac{1}{\tau}F_{v}(u_{\ast}(\xi),v_{\ast})\bar{u}\right]\end{array}\right.

(2.16)

now with
u_{h}(X)=u_{\ast}(\xi)
,
v_{h}(X)\equiv v_{\ast}
as leading order approximations during the fast jump. By taking
\varepsilon\to 0
in the
\bar{u}
-equation of (

\bar{u}
is determined by
\bar{v}
in the slow fields,

\bar{u}=-\tau\frac{G_{u}(u^{\pm},v^{\pm})}{F_{u}(u^{\pm},v^{\pm})}\bar{v}

(at leading order). By
(f^{\pm})^{\prime}(v)=-F_{v}(f^{\pm}(v),v)/F_{u}(f^{\pm}(v),v)

\bar{v}_{XX}+\left[G_{u}(u^{\pm},v^{\pm})+G_{v}(u^{\pm},v^{\pm})(f^{\pm})^{\prime}(v^{\pm})\right]\bar{v}=0

in the slow fields (as
\varepsilon\to 0
), which implies by (
2.3
) that
\bar{v}^{\pm}(X)=\alpha^{\pm}v^{\pm}_{X}(X)
for some constants
\alpha^{\pm}
. Taking
\varepsilon\to 0
in the fast field yields that
\bar{v}(\xi)\equiv\bar{v}_{\ast}
, a constant, and that

\bar{u}_{\xi\xi}-c_{\ast}\tau\bar{u}_{\xi}+F_{u}(u_{\ast}(\xi),v_{\ast})\bar{u}={\cal L}_{u}^{A}\bar{u}=-\tau G_{u}(u_{\ast}(\xi),v_{\ast})\bar{v}_{\ast}

(

{\cal L}_{u}
is spanned by
u_{\ast,\xi}
this implies that

\tau\bar{v}_{\ast}\int_{\mathbb{R}}G_{u}(u_{\ast}(\xi),v_{\ast})u_{\ast,\xi}\,d\xi=\tau\bar{v}_{\ast}\left[G(u_{\ast}^{+},v_{\ast})-G(u_{\ast}^{-},v_{\ast})\right]=0

(at leading order in
\varepsilon)
. Thus, under the (generic) assumption that
G(u_{\ast}^{+},v_{\ast})\neq G(u_{\ast}^{-},v_{\ast})
, we conclude that
\bar{v}
must be
{\cal O}(\varepsilon)
, so that
\bar{v}^{\pm}=\varepsilon\bar{\alpha}^{\pm}v^{\pm}_{X}=\bar{\alpha}^{\pm}v^{\pm}_{\xi}

{\cal L}_{u}^{A}\bar{u}=0
(at leading order), which implies that

\bar{u}(\xi)=\alpha_{\ast}u_{\ast,\xi}(\xi)\,e^{c_{\ast}\tau\xi}

(2.18)

for some
\alpha_{\ast}
. By matching over the fast field, we can eliminate 2 of the 3 free factors
\bar{\alpha}^{\pm}
and
\alpha_{\ast}
. First we note that
\bar{v}
must be continuous over the fast field so that
\bar{\alpha}^{+}=\bar{\alpha}^{-}=\bar{\alpha}
. Since
v^{-}_{X}(0)=q_{\ast}=v^{+}_{X}(0)
(Fig.
3

\bar{v}_{\ast}=\varepsilon\bar{\alpha}q_{\ast}
. Recalling that
\bar{v}={\cal O}(\varepsilon)
, we measure the accumulated change in
\bar{v}_{\xi}
over the fast field by integrating
\bar{v}_{\xi\xi}

\Delta_{f}\bar{v}_{\xi}=-\varepsilon^{2}\frac{\alpha_{\ast}}{\tau}\int_{\mathbb{R}}F_{v}(u_{\ast}(\xi),v_{\ast})u_{\ast,\xi}(\xi)e^{c_{\ast}\tau\xi}\,d\xi

(
2.16
), (
2.18
), which must bridge the jump in the
\bar{v}_{\xi}

\Delta_{s}\bar{v}_{\xi}=\lim_{X\downarrow 0}\bar{v}_{\xi}-\lim_{X\downarrow 0}\bar{v}_{\xi}=\bar{\alpha}\left[\lim_{X\downarrow 0}v^{+}_{\xi\xi}-\lim_{X\uparrow 0}v^{-}_{\xi\xi}\right]=\varepsilon^{2}\bar{\alpha}\left[\lim_{X\downarrow 0}v^{+}_{XX}-\lim_{X\uparrow 0}v^{-}_{XX}\right]=-\varepsilon^{2}\bar{\alpha}\left[G(u_{\ast}^{+},v_{\ast})-G(u_{\ast}^{-},v_{\ast})\right]

(
2.3

\frac{\bar{\alpha}}{\alpha_{\ast}}=\frac{1}{\tau}\frac{\int_{\mathbb{R}}F_{v}(u_{\ast},v_{\ast})u_{\ast,\xi}\,e^{c_{\ast}\tau\xi}\,d\xi}{G(u_{\ast}^{+},v_{\ast})-G(u_{\ast}^{-},v_{\ast})}.

(2.19)

(again assuming that
G(u_{\ast}^{+},v_{\ast})\neq G(u_{\ast}^{-},v_{\ast})
). Now we can evaluate (
2.14
), although we need to be a bit more precise about our choice for the fast field,

\int_{\mathbb{R}}v_{h,\xi}\bar{v}^{A}_{c}\,d\xi=\left(\int_{-\infty}^{-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}}+\int_{-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}}^{\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}}+\int_{-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}}^{\infty}\right)v_{h,\xi}\bar{v}^{A}_{c}\,d\xi=\bar{\alpha}\int_{-\infty}^{-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}}(v^{-}_{\xi})^{2}\,d\xi+\varepsilon^{2}\bar{\alpha}\int_{-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}}^{\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}}q_{\ast}^{2}\,d\xi+\bar{\alpha}\int_{\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}}^{\infty}(v^{+}_{\xi})^{2}\,d\xi

i.e.

\int_{\mathbb{R}}v_{h,\xi}\bar{v}^{A}_{c}\,d\xi=\varepsilon\bar{\alpha}\left(\int_{-\infty}^{0}(v^{-}_{X})^{2}\,dX+\int_{0}^{\infty}(v^{+}_{X})^{2}\,dX\right)+{\cal O}(\varepsilon\sqrt{\varepsilon}).

(2.20)

In contrast, the integral over
u_{h,\xi}\bar{u}^{A}_{c}
is dominated by its fast part and is of
{\cal O}(1)

\int_{\mathbb{R}}u_{h,\xi}\bar{u}^{A}_{c}\,d\xi=\alpha_{\ast}\int_{\mathbb{R}}(u_{\ast,\xi})^{2}e^{c_{\ast}\tau\xi}\,d\xi+{\cal O}(\varepsilon)

(2.21)

(
2.18
). Thus, we find by (
2.14
) and (
2.19

\lambda_{2,c}=-\frac{1}{\varepsilon\tau}\,\frac{\int_{\mathbb{R}}F_{v}(u_{\ast},v_{\ast})u_{\ast,\xi}\,e^{c_{\ast}\tau\xi}\,d\xi}{G(u_{\ast}^{+},v_{\ast})-G(u_{\ast}^{-},v_{\ast})}\,\frac{\int_{-\infty}^{0}(v^{-}_{X})^{2}\,dX+\int_{0}^{\infty}(v^{+}_{X})^{2}\,dX}{\int_{\mathbb{R}}(u_{\ast,\xi})^{2}e^{c_{\ast}\tau\xi}\,d\xi}

(2.22)

(at leading order in
\varepsilon
), which indeed yields (

F_{\ast}
and
G_{\ast}
as in (

Remark 3.1
.

The three subconditions of condition (

3.2
Bifurcation into traveling waves

As was already noted in the Introduction, the expression
\tilde{M}_{\ast}(\vec{\mu};\tilde{\tau})
(

\tilde{c}_{\ast}=0
(see (

3.9
)). Therefore, it is natural to expect that a zero of
\tilde{M}_{\ast}(\vec{\mu};\tilde{\tau})
may be related to a ‘structural geometrical change’ in the existence analysis. In this subsection we consider the special case of stationary fronts, i.e. fronts for which the existence problem does not depend on
\tilde{\tau}
. We show that in this case a change of the sign of
\tilde{M}_{\ast}(\vec{\mu};\tilde{\tau})
indeed has a direct impact on the existence problem: it initiates a bifurcation into traveling waves, i.e. as
\tilde{M}_{\ast}(\vec{\mu};\tilde{\tau})
crosses through 0 – for instance as function of
\tilde{\tau}
– a pair of counter-moving traveling fronts branches off from the stationary fronts.

Existence problem (
2.1
) with
\tau=\varepsilon\tilde{\tau}
and
c=\tilde{c}/\varepsilon
has a stationary front solution if (
3.1
) has a heteroclinic orbit for
\tilde{c}=0
for a value of

which also is the
v
-coordinate of the intersection of the unstable manifold
{\cal W}^{\rm u}((\bar{V}^{-},0))
and the stable manifold
{\cal W}^{\rm s}((\bar{V}^{+},0))
of the projected flows (
3.2
) on
{\cal M}^{\pm}_{0}
with
\tilde{c}=0
. In other words,
{\cal C}(\tilde{v})
and
{\cal V}(\tilde{c})
of (

{\cal C}\left({\cal V}(0)\right)=0
, which imposes a co-dimension 1 condition on the parameters
\vec{\mu}
of (

\vec{\mu}
indeed has values so that
{\cal C}\left({\cal V}(0)\right)=0
and thus use the fact that (
1.1
) has a stationary front solution for any
\tau=\varepsilon\tilde{\tau}>0
. Given this set-up, we ask the question: under which additional condition can there be slowly traveling fronts, i.e. fronts traveling with speed
\tilde{c}=\hat{c}\delta
with (artificial) small parameter
0<\varepsilon\ll\delta\ll 1
(that is independent of
\varepsilon
), such that these fronts merge with the stationary front as

We first consider the fast problem (
3.1

\tilde{u}_{\ast}(\xi)
between the critical points
(\tilde{u}_{\ast}^{-},0)
and
(\tilde{u}_{\ast}^{+},0)

\tilde{u}_{\ast}^{\pm}=f^{\pm}(\tilde{v}_{\ast})

\hat{c}=0
. Thus, we define the family of Hamiltonians,

(3.12)

parameterized by
v_{0}

\tilde{{\cal H}}_{f}(\tilde{u}_{\ast}^{-},0;v_{\ast})=\tilde{{\cal H}}_{f}^{\ast}(\tilde{u}_{\ast}^{+},0;v_{\ast})=\int_{\tilde{u}_{\ast}^{-}}^{\tilde{u}_{\ast}^{+}}F(s,\tilde{v}_{\ast})\,ds=0,

(3.13)

see also
[
35
,
45
]
and note that (
3.13
) is the explicit version of the expression
{\cal C}(\tilde{v}_{\ast})=0
(
3.3
). Naturally, one expects that if a traveling front appears from the stationary front, it will not make its fast jump at exactly the same value of
\tilde{v}_{\ast}
, therefore we introduce
\hat{v}_{1}
by
\tilde{v}_{\ast}(\tilde{c})=\tilde{v}_{\ast}+\delta\hat{v}_{1}
(with
\hat{v}_{1}=\hat{v}_{1}(\hat{c})
and recall that
\tilde{c}=\delta\hat{c}
). As a consequence, the
u
-coordinates of the 2 saddle points
(\tilde{u}^{\pm}_{\ast}(\tilde{c}),0)=(f^{\pm}(\tilde{v}_{\ast}(\tilde{c})),0)
of (

{\cal O}(\delta)

Invasion fronts in a Klausmeier-type dryland ecosystem model

On flat terrains, the ecosystem model proposed and studied in

(4.1)

i.e.

represents vegetation or biomass and

[

[

[

[

\mu_{3}>0

\mu_{2}>0

(1-\mu_{2}U)

From the ecological point of view it is natural to consider invasion fronts of the bare soil state, i.e.
(U(x,y,t),V(x,y,t))\equiv(\bar{U}^{-},\bar{V}^{-})=(0,\mu_{3})

(U(x,y,t),V(x,y,t))\equiv(\bar{U}^{+},\bar{V}^{+})

1.4

u_{\xi\xi}+cu_{\xi}-\mu_{1}u+v_{0}u^{2}(1-\mu_{2}u)=0,\;\;v=v_{0},q=q_{0}

(4.2)

that defines two normally hyperbolic slow manifolds,

{\cal M}^{-}_{0}=\{u=f^{-}(v)\equiv 0,p=0\},\;\;{\cal M}^{+}_{0}=\{u=f^{+}(v)=u^{+}(v),p=0,v>4\mu_{1}\mu_{2}\}

(4.3)

with

,

(4.4)

Clearly, the (reduced) slow flow on
{\cal M}^{-}_{0}
is linear,
v_{XX}+\mu_{3}-v=0
, while the flow on
{\cal M}^{+}_{0}
is given by

v_{XX}+\mu_{3}+\frac{\mu_{1}}{\mu_{2}}=\left(1+\frac{1}{2\mu_{2}^{2}}\right)v+\frac{1}{2\mu_{2}^{2}}\sqrt{v^{2}-4\mu_{1}\mu 2v}=0.

(4.5)

Since the right hand side of (
4.5
) is an increasing function of

4.5

4.1.2
Reversing desertification by front instabilities

In
[
19
]
a novel mechanism is proposed through which desertification may be reversed. It is shown that an invasion front of a bare soil state into a homogeneously vegetated that is stable with respect to longitudinal perturbations may be unstable with respect to transversal perturbations as planar interface. The vegetation fingers driven by this instability grow back into the bare soil state. Thus, instead of the collapse of the savanna state into a desert state that is predicted by the 1-dimensional point of view, (realistically) allowing for 2-dimensional perturbations along the interface predicts the counter-invasion of vegetation patterns into the desert, with as endstate a fully vegetated patterned domain (see
[
19
]
and Fig.
2
).

The model considered in
[
19
]
reads,

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}U_{t}&=&\,\,\,\,\,\,\Delta U-U+U(1+\mu_{1}U)^{2}(1-U)V\\
V_{t}&=&\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\Delta V+\mu_{2}-\frac{\mu_{3}V}{1+\mu_{4}U}-\mu_{5}U(1+\mu_{1}U)^{2}V\end{array}\right.

(4.11)

(cf.
[
50
]
). Note that model (
4.1
) can be seen as a simplification of this (somewhat) more realistic model. The evaporation rate is scaled to
-1
in (
4.1
), while here it is unscaled (parameter
\mu_{3}
) and takes into account the effect vegetation has on evaporation (
\mu_{4}
). Moreover, following
[
31
]
, the nonlinear mechanism by which plants increase water infiltration is modeled (and scaled) as
U^{2}V
in (
4.1
), while in (
4.11
) the fact that plants extend spatially through their roots is included in the infiltration term, which yields the extended term
U(1+\mu_{1}U)^{2}V

[
33
]
. Apart from that the systems are only scaled in slightly different ways. As we shall see, the upcoming analysis is also quite similar – but slightly more technical and less explicit – to that of the simplified Klausmeier-type model (
4.1
). Moreover, the outcome is in essence identical.

The fast reduced system for traveling waves is now given by,

u_{\xi\xi}+cu_{\xi}-u+v_{0}u(1+\mu_{1}u)^{2}(1-u)=0,\;\;v=v_{0},q=q_{0}

(4.12)

and the associated two normally hyperbolic slow manifolds of interest are thus given by,

{\cal M}^{-}_{0}=\{u=f^{-}(v)\equiv 0,p=0,v<1\},\;\;{\cal M}^{+}_{0}=\{u=f^{+}(v),p=0,v>v_{\rm m}\},

(4.13)

with
u=f^{+}(v)
the largest solution of

v_{F}(u)=v=\frac{1}{(1-u)(1+\mu_{1}u)^{2}},

(4.14)

that intersects the
\{u=0\}
-axis at
v=1
and has a minimum at,

(u_{\rm m},v_{\rm m})=\left(\frac{2\mu_{1}-1}{3\mu_{1}},\frac{27\mu_{1}}{4(1+\mu_{1})^{3}}\right).

(4.15)

Note that in order to have heteroclinic front solutions jumping from
{\cal M}^{-}_{0}
to
{\cal M}^{+}_{0}
we need to impose that

and
v_{\rm m}<1
, i.e. that
\mu_{1}>\frac{1}{2}
– see Fig.

(\bar{U}^{\pm},\bar{V}^{\pm})
are on
{\cal M}^{\pm}_{0}

are saddles – see section

(4.16)

either with
u=0
or with

(

). The former yields the bare soil state

, and we thus impose the condition

u>0

must have a unique zero and

has a maximum at

has at most 2 elements that both correspond to homogeneously vegetated savanna states (see also Remark

). Although the soil moisture level below a bare soil state can both be higher or lower than that below a vegetated state

38

, model (

). Therefore, we need to impose the condition
\bar{V}^{+}<\bar{V}^{-}
, i.e.

\mu_{3}\mu_{4}-\mu_{5}(1+\mu_{4}\bar{U}^{+})(1+\mu_{1}\bar{U}^{+})^{2}<0.

As for (

{\cal M}^{-}_{0}
is linear,

{\cal M}^{+}_{0}

of (

on

must intersect the stable manifold
{\cal W}^{\rm s}((\bar{V}^{+},0))
on
{\cal M}^{+}_{0}
(in their combined projection) – exactly as for model (

(with

for which there is a heteroclinic connection

in (

Therefore, we again conclude by the methods of geometric singular perturbation theory that a traveling planar invasion front
(U_{h}(\xi),V_{h}(\xi))
between the desert state
(\bar{U}^{-},\bar{V}^{-})
and savanna state

\lambda_{c}(\ell)
has

4.3
Example systems with

4.3.1
On cylindrical domains

The stability of planar interfaces in a general class of singularly perturbed 2-component reaction-diffusion systems has
also been considered in
[
44
,
45
]
. The class of models considered in
[
44
,
45
]
is more restricted than the models considered here. Several simplifying assumptions are formulated on the functions
F(U,V)
and
G(U,V)
, it is for instance assumed that the slow flows on
{\cal M}^{\pm}_{0}
do not have additional critical points between the saddles at
(\bar{V}^{\pm},0)
and the intermediate ‘jumping line’
\{v=v_{\ast}\}
. Especially in the setting of ecological models this indeed is a restriction
[
11
,
27
]
. Moreover, ecological models also typically have
\tau={\cal O}(1)
– in fact
\tau=1
– while in
[
44
,
45
]
only the case
\tau=\varepsilon\tilde{\tau}
is considered. However, we have seen that
\tau={\cal O}(1)
is more simple than
\tau={\cal O}(\varepsilon)
and the nature and strength of the methods of
[
44
,
45
]
– that are based on
[
35
,
36
]
– strongly suggest that the more analytical approach of these works (compared to the present more geometrical set-up) can also be successfully applied beyond the class of models considered in
[
44
,
45
]
. In fact, we expect that any model that can be studied along the lines of this paper can also be analyzed by the methods of
[
35
,
36
,
44
,
45
]
.

A much more important difference between our present study and
[
44
,
45
]
is that in the latter works the spatial domain is considered to be cylindrical: instead of
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
considered here,
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}\times\Omega_{y}
with
\Omega_{y}=(0,L)
bounded (in fact, in
[
45
]
,
(x,y)\in\Omega_{x}\times\Omega_{y}
with also
\Omega_{x}
bounded (as in
[
35
]
), while
\Omega_{y}\subset\mathbb{R}^{N-1}
in
[
44
]
– these differences can be overcome though). The (in)stability criteria obtained here are against long wavelength transversal perturbations: if an interface is unstable in this sense, it will only be unstable on a cylindrical domain
\mathbb{R}\times(0,L)
for
L
sufficiently large. So, in a way
[
44
,
45
]
consider the more complex issue of the destabilization of the interface for a given size
L
of the domain
\Omega_{y}
– in fact, a large part of the analytical focus of
[
44
,
45
]
is on the nature of the fastest growing unstable ‘eigenmode’, a topic we do not consider here.

A clear advantage of the more simple setting of the present work is that it enables us to also obtain simple instability results for the models considered in
[
44
,
45
]
under the assumption that
L
is sufficiently large. Instead of considering the models of
[
44
,
45
]
in their full generality, we focus on an example model also considered in
[
35
,
44
,
45
]
,

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}\tau U_{t}&=&\,\,\,\,\,\,\Delta U+U^{2}(1-U)-UV\\
V_{t}&=&\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\Delta V+\mu_{1}UV+\mu_{2}V-\mu_{3}V^{2}\end{array}\right.

(4.24)

Clearly, the normally hyperbolic slow manifolds
{\cal M}^{\pm}_{0}
are given by,

{\cal M}^{-}_{0}=\{u=p=0,v>0\},\;\;{\cal M}^{+}_{0}=\{u=u^{+}(v)=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{1-4v},p=0,v<\frac{1}{4}\},

and the reduced slow flows on
{\cal M}^{\pm}_{0}
by,

{\cal M}^{-}_{0}:v_{XX}+\mu_{2}v-\mu_{3}v^{2}=0,\;\;{\cal M}^{+}_{0}:v_{XX}+\mu_{1}vu^{+}(v)+\mu_{2}v-\mu_{3}v^{2}=0.

Both flows have
(0,0)
as critical point and we want this to be a center in both cases:
\mu_{2}>0
and
\mu_{1}+\mu_{2}>0
. We also want the slow flows to both have exactly one additional critical point on
{\cal M}^{\pm}_{0}
, i.e.
\mu_{3}>{\rm max}\{0,2(\mu_{1}+2\mu_{2})\}

(\bar{V}^{\pm},0)
with
\bar{V}^{\pm}>0

{\cal W}^{\rm u}((\bar{V}^{-},0))
must intersect
{\cal W}^{\rm s}((\bar{V}^{+},0))
at
(v_{\ast},q_{\ast})
with

. Since

for
\mu_{1}>0
– the case considered
[
44
,
45
]
– the existence of the traveling front follows immediately; for
\mu_{1}<0
, there may be 3 critical points on
{\cal M}^{-}_{0}
in which case a fast heteroclinic jump may not always be possible so that additional (technical) conditions need to be added to ensure the existence of a traveling front. Since by construction
u_{\ast}(\xi)>0
,
u_{\ast,\xi}(\xi)>0
and,

\mu_{1}>0

[

,
45

However, we so far only considered

, while
\tau=\varepsilon\tilde{\tau}={\cal O}(\varepsilon)
in
[

45
]
. Nevertheless, this does impact neither the instability result itself nor its simplicity (for

[

\tilde{v}_{\ast}=\frac{2}{9},\;\tilde{u}_{\ast}^{+}=\frac{2}{3},\;\tilde{F}_{\ast}=-\frac{2}{9},\;\tilde{G}_{\ast}=\frac{4}{27},\;\tilde{I}_{f}=\frac{2}{81}\sqrt{2}

4.9

at which it undergoes a bifurcation into traveling waves (by section

). Thus, also for

, the straightforward observations (

must be unstable as planar interface on

up to the critical value

Finally, we note that for

5
Discussion

In
[
2
]
, predictions of model studies of vegetation patterns in dryland ecosystems have been validated by observational evidence. These model studies go back to
[
42
,
43
]
in which the persistence of Turing patterns in a (generalized Klausmeier) model of type (
1.1
) under slowly varying parameters was investigated. The main conclusion of
[
2
]
was that the multistability induced by the richness of spatial (Turing) patterns increases the resilience of an ecosystem: instead of a catastrophic collapse, a patterned state may adapt under worsening external circumstances to a nearby adjacent (vegetated and patterned) state. It is postulated in
[
37
]
that this is not special for dryland ecosystems: any ecosystem that exhibits spatial patterns is expected to have an increased resilience. In fact, it is argued in
[
37
]
that spatial patterning may provide the ecosystem with a mechanism to evade tipping, and thus to circumvent catastrophic collapse.

Although the patterns of
[
2
,
42
,
43
]
that initiated the insights of
[
37
]
are all of Turing pattern type, it is observed in
[
37
]
that there are other types of patterns – with backgrounds that are not of Turing type – that will have the same positive impact on the resilience of an ecosystem. An important role is given to the patterns that originate from the interfaces between homogeneous states. These coexistence states occur naturally in various kinds of ecosystems, moreover, these ecosystems are typically modeled by systems of reaction-diffusion equations like (
1.1
) – see
[
3
,
16
,
17
,
19
,
48
,
50
]
and the references therein. Thus, the conditions obtained here on the instability of planar interfaces and the associated onset of fingering patterns – and especially the insight of section
4.1
on the natural occurrence of this instability in ecosystem models – have, by
[
37
]
, a direct interpretation in the context of the resilience of ecosystems.

Nevertheless, the present results mostly only are a first step towards understanding the relevance of transversal instabilities in ecosystems.

\bullet

Non-planar fronts.
The present focus on straight, flat interfaces orthogonal to the longitudinal direction of a 1-dimensional front is quite restricted – especially from the ecological point of view. In the companion paper
[
5
]
, radially symmetric curved fronts are considered – in the explicit context of ecosystem model (
4.1
). Using geometric blow-up methods, first a spot pattern – i.e. a localized (circular) vegetated area surrounded by bare soil – is constructed rigorously. Gap patterns can be constructed in a very similar fashion and we find that parameter condition (
4.6
) on the existence of stationary planar fronts determines the transition between the existence of spots and gaps (in parameter space). It is additionally indicated in
[
5
]
how the same geometric approach yields the existence of ring and target patterns. Moreover, the stability of the gap and spot patterns is considered. It is shown that spots and gaps with a sufficiently large radius are unstable with respect to the same (fingering) instability as encountered here: simulations show that the evolving fingers merge the vegetated and bare soil patches into a fully mixed labyrinthine state.

\bullet

Multiple fronts and non-flat terrains.
Existence conditions
(E-I)
-
(E-IV)
allow for the construction of double-front homoclinic stripe patterns – either of gap or of spot type. Moreover, these localized stripes are the ‘endpoints’ of a 1-parameter family of periodic stripe patterns – see
[
27
]
for the construction of these patterns in a model of intermediate complexity between (
4.1
) and (
4.11
) (Remark
4.2
). Stripe patterns are typically observed on sloped terrains
[
9
,
21
]
, therefore it is natural to study these patterns in an extended version of model (
1.1
) that includes an advection term ‘
+\mu_{m+1}V_{x}
’ in the
V
-equation – which models the downslope flow of water in an ecosystem setting
[
1
,
31
,
40
,
41
]
. Central questions to consider are: will the multiple-front patterns (also) be unstable with respect to transversal long wavelength perturbations, and what is the impact of parameter
\mu_{m+1}=\mu_{m+1}(\varepsilon)
? (This is the subject of work in progress.)

\bullet

Multi-component systems.
Ecosystem models typically contain more than one type of vegetation and often also separate equations for soil water and overland water – see for instance
[
3
,
16
,
30
,
33
,
37
]
and the references therein. The 2-component ecosystem models of
[
1
,
2
,
17
,
19
,
27
,
31
,
48
,
50
]
are either obtained by reduction from a more extended model or postulated as conceptual, simplified, model. To achieve a next level in ecological validity, the present analysis needs to be extended to multi-component systems. The observation that many ecosystem models have various ordered small parameters opens up the possibility for setting up a geometric multi-scale analysis – as initial explorations indicate.

The present analysis also triggers a number of mathematical research questions, of which we mention a few.

\bullet

Bifurcations of fronts and interfaces.
In section
2.2
, stability assumption
(S-I)
was formulated more precisely: apart from assuming that the interface is stable against longitudinal perturbations, it was also imposed that the dimension of the kernel of operator
\mathbb{L}
(
2.9
) is equal to 1. In other words, it is assumed that the parameters
(\tau,\vec{\mu})
of (
1.1
) are chosen away from potential (local) bifurcation sets. This assumption underlies the approach of this section – and the paper – since the leading order approximations (
2.22
) and (
3.11
) – that underlie criteria (
1.6
) and (
1.7
) – can now be derived by approximating the 1-dimensional kernel of the adjoint operator
\mathbb{L}^{A}
(
2.12
). As
(\tau,\vec{\mu})
or
(\tilde{\tau},\vec{\mu})
passes through a bifurcational (co-dimension 1) set
(\tau_{\ast},\vec{\mu}_{\ast})
/
(\tilde{\tau}_{\ast},\vec{\mu}_{\ast})
– for instance as in section
3.2
– then there is a second eigenvalue curve
\lambda_{b}(\ell)
that passes through
\lambda_{c}(\ell)
. Naturally,
\lambda_{b}(0)=\lambda_{c}(0)=0
: the dimension of the kernel of
\mathbb{L}
is (at least) 2 at the bifurcation set. Thus, one has to extend the approach that led to (
2.22
) and (
3.11
) to determine the local character – as function of
|\ell|\ll 1
– of
\lambda_{b}(\ell)
and
\lambda_{c}(\ell)
as the bifurcational set is crossed. This is an interesting line of research, especially since the results of section
4.3.2
suggest that there may be a relation between the signs of
\lambda_{2,c}
of the longitudinally stable interfaces before and after the bifurcation.

\bullet

Breakdown of existence conditions.
Although we write at the end of section
2.1
‘the conditions on
F(U,V)
and
G(U,V)
are quite mild and the slow-fast-slow interfaces
(U_{h}(\xi),U_{h}(\xi))
can be constructed for many models’, it is certainly not always possible to construct a traveling front solution that corresponds to a heteroclinic orbit of (
2.1
) if only condition
(E-I)
holds – unlike in the scalar case (
1.2
). Conditions
(E-III)
and
(E-IV)
may be violated for generic classes within the full family of systems (
1.1
) (see Remark
2.2
and
[
11
]
for
(E-II)
). It is natural to expect, under the fundamental assumption
(E-I)
, that (
1.1
) ‘must’ exhibit some kind of localized patterns that approach
(\bar{U}^{\pm},\bar{V}^{\pm})
for
x\to\pm\infty
. We are not aware of any systematic studies of this issue. We note that such a study could start with considering the implications of varying
\vec{\mu}
such that the intersection
{\cal W}^{\rm u}((\bar{V}^{-},0))\cap{\cal W}^{\rm s}((\bar{V}^{+},0))
vanishes (i.e. the parameters are varied beyond the validity
(E-IV)
). The example of
[
12
]
shows that this may lead to (spatially localized) finite-time blow-up behavior.

Finally, we note that (viscous) fingering has already been identified as ‘archetype for growth patterns’ in fluid mechanics
[
7
]
, and has for example also been observed in experiments/observations and model simulations of expanding bacterial colonies
[
22
]
, tumor growth
[
4
]
and wound healing
[
32
]
. At the same time, our understanding of the nonlinear dynamics of fingering – or non-fingering (Fig.
6
) – interfaces is limited. Due to the relative simplicity of 2-component model (
1.1
) and its suitability for mathematical analysis – by its singularly perturbed nature – a mathematical study of the evolution of ‘sharp’ slow-fast-slow interfaces in (
1.1
) that are unstable under ‘sideband conditions’ (
1.6
) or (
1.7
) may be a promising way to gain novel fundamental insights in the fingering phenomenon.
