Asymptotic linear convergence of fully–corrective generalized conditional gradient methods
Kristian Bredies, Marcello Carioni, Silvio Fanzon, Daniel Walter

Abstract
. We propose a fully-corrective generalized conditional gradient method (FC-GCG) for the minimization of the sum of a smooth, convex loss function and a convex one-homogeneous regularizer over a Banach space. The algorithm relies on the mutual update of a finite set 𝒜 k \mathcal{A}_{k} of extremal points of the unit ball of the regularizer and of an iterate u k ∈ cone ⁡ ( 𝒜 k ) u_{k}\in\operatorname{cone}(\mathcal{A}_{k}) . Each iteration requires the solution of one linear problem to update 𝒜 k \mathcal{A}_{k} and of one finite dimensional convex minimization problem to update the iterate. Under standard hypotheses on the minimization problem we show that the algorithm converges sublinearly to a solution. Subsequently, imposing additional assumptions on the associated dual variables, this is improved to a linear rate of convergence. The proof of both results relies on two key observations: First, we prove the equivalence of the considered problem to the minimization of a lifted functional over a particular space of Radon measures using Choquet’s theorem. Second, the FC-GCG algorithm is connected to a Primal-Dual-Active-point Method (PDAP) on the lifted problem for which we finally derive the desired convergence rates. Key words: non-smooth optimization, conditional gradient method, sparsity, Choquet’s theorem. 2020 Mathematics Subject Classification: 65J22, 65K05, 52A41, 46G12, 46A55.

1.
Introduction

This paper is concerned with the analysis of an efficient solution algorithm for minimization problems in composite form

(
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{M}}
)

\displaystyle\inf_{u\in\mathcal{M}}J(u),\quad J(u)\coloneqq F(Ku)+\mathcal{G}(u)

over a Banach space
\mathcal{M}
. Here, the forward operator
K
maps continuously from
\mathcal{M}
into a Hilbert space
Y
of observations, not necessarily finite dimensional, and
F
denotes a smooth convex loss function. The second part of the objective functional is constituted by a convex but possibly non-smooth functional
\mathcal{G}
which promotes desired structural properties. We refer, e.g., to the sparsifying property of total variation regularization or the staircasing effect of bounded variation penalties. The observation that certain structural features of minimizers can be brought forth by a suitable choice of the functional
\mathcal{G}
and of the space
\mathcal{M}
, has made the analysis of problems of the form (

P
M

) a flourishing topic in the context of optimal control, inverse problems, compressed sensing and machine learning. As a consequence, the interest for such models has also sparked the demand for efficient solution algorithms. Since many of the structural features of (

P
M

) are tightly linked to properties of the underlying, possibly infinite dimensional Banach space, a particular focus in this context lies on
function space methods
, i.e., algorithms solving (

P
M

) without discretizing
\mathcal{M}
. This is a challenging task for a variety of reasons: On the one hand, it requires algorithms that can handle the non-smoothness of the objective functional
J
. On the other hand, it forces to work directly on the Banach space
\mathcal{M}
which usually lacks “nice” properties such as reflexivity or uniform convexity. Efficient algorithms have been developed, for instance, for inverse problems regularized with
\ell^{p}
penalties
[
18
,
31
]
, inverse problems in the space of measures regularized with the total variation
[
11
,
21
,
33
]
and dynamic inverse problems with optimal transport regularizers
[
16
,
17
,
14
,
15
]
.

1.1.
Contribution & related work

In many interesting applications it is meaningful to assume that
\mathcal{M}
is given as the topological dual of a separable Banach space
\mathcal{C}
, and
K
is the adjoint of a “predual” operator
K_{*}\colon Y\to\mathcal{C}
,
(K_{*})^{*}=K
. We refer to Section
4
for a few examples. In this case a simple approach to computing a minimizer to (

P
M

) is constituted by
generalized conditional gradient
(GCG) algorithms. Assuming that the solution set of (

P
M

) is bounded by some
M>0
, this method updates the iterate
u_{k}
by computing the dual variable
p_{k}=-K^{*}\nabla F(Ku_{k})
and setting

\displaystyle v_{k}\in\argmin_{\|v\|_{\mathcal{M}}\leq M}\left[-\langle p_{k},v\rangle+\mathcal{G}(v)\right],\penalty\ u_{k+1}=u_{k}+s_{k}(v_{k}-u_{k})\,,

where
s_{k}\in(0,1)
is chosen according to some stepsize rule. If
\mathcal{G}(u)=I_{A}(u)
is the indicator function of a compact convex set
A\subset\mathcal{M}
, then the iteration scheme reduces to the
Frank-Wolfe
(FW) algorithm for constrained minimization
[
40
,
32
,
37
]
.

In the present paper we focus on convex positively homogeneous functionals
\mathcal{G}
with compact sublevel sets, where compactness is intended with respect to the weak* topology induced by
\mathcal{C}
. The compactness assumption is equivalent to

\mathcal{G}
having weak* closed and norm bounded sublevels. In particular, these assumptions on
\mathcal{G}
encompass the important case of norm regularization
\mathcal{G}(u)=\|u\|_{\mathcal{M}}
, and also allow for seminorm penalties if the problem is posed in a suitable quotient space
[
13
]
. More in general, our setting covers the case of all
gauge functions
of the form

\kappa_{\mathcal{A}}(u):=\inf\left\{\rho\geq 0\;|\;u\in\rho A\right\}\quad\text{where }\,\,\rho A=\{\rho v\;|\;v\in A\}\,,

and
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathcal{M}
is a weak* compact and convex set. This type of functional is of particular relevance in machine learning applications
[
28
,
62
]
.

In this general setting, GCG methods benefit from several desirable properties. For example, they only rely on the repeated solution of (partially) linearized problems that, in some interesting cases, can be solved analytically. Additionally, the descent direction can be chosen to satisfy
v_{k}=M_{k}\hat{v}_{k}
where
M_{k}\geq 0
is a scaling factor and
\widehat{v}_{k}
is an
extremal point
of the “unit ball”
B=\{\,u\;|\;\mathcal{G}(u)\leq 1\,\}
of the regularizer. We denote by
\operatorname{Ext}(B)
the set of such points. Thus, the above mentioned partially linearized problems can be solved, equivalently, in the set
\operatorname{Ext}(B)
, reducing considerably the complexity of each iteration of the algorithm. As a further consequence, initializing the algorithm by
u_{0}=0
and selecting descent directions
v_{k}
that are extremal points of
B
, the iterate
u_{k}
exhibits
k-sparsity
, i.e., it is contained in the conic hull of at most
k
points in
\operatorname{Ext}(B)
. The connection between the structure enhancing properties of a functional
\mathcal{G}
and the set of extremal points of
B
has been recently studied in
[
13
]
and
[
12
]
. In this context, a central result is given by
convex representer theorems
. Loosely speaking, these state that problems of the form (

P
M

) admit solutions
\bar{u}
which are contained in the conic hull of at most
\operatorname{dim}Y
extremal points.

While the FW method
[
35
,
36
,
41
,
47
]
, and its many variants such as
away-step
FW
[
43
,
49
]
, or
fully-corrective
FW
[
44
,
58
,
59
]
, have received a lot of attention, GCG algorithms for general non-smooth functionals
\mathcal{G}
are less frequently studied. We refer, e.g., to
[
16
,
20
,
57
,
62
]
as well as
[
60
, Chapter 6]
which all prove a global sublinear
\mathcal{O}(1/k)
rate of convergence of
J(u_{k})
towards the minimum value. Note that this rate is known to be optimal
[
24
]
. Moreover, the absence of steps that remove extremal points from
u_{k}
often leads to clustering phenomena in practice. Thus, despite its various advantages, these shortcomings of GCG methods limit their practical utility.

The main contribution of the present work is the analysis of a
fully-corrective generalized conditional gradient method
(FC-GCG) for (

P
M

). This relies on the mutual update of a sequence of sparse iterates
u_{k}
and of a sequence of finite, ordered
active sets

\mathcal{A}^{u}_{k}=\left\{\,u^{k}_{i}\in\operatorname{Ext}(B)\;|\;i=1,\dots,N_{k}\,\right\}\,,\,\,N_{k}\in\mathbb{N}\,,

of extremal points, which constitute the
atoms
representing the sparse iterate
u_{k}
. Given the current iterate
u_{k}
and active set
\mathcal{A}_{k}
, the proposed method first enlarges
\mathcal{A}_{k}
by setting
N^{+}_{k}=N_{k}+1
and

(1.1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}^{u,+}_{k}=\mathcal{A}^{u}_{k}\cup\{\widehat{v}_{k}\}\,,\quad\penalty\ \widehat{v}_{k}\in\argmax_{v\in\operatorname{Ext}(B)}\,\langle p_{k},v\rangle\,.

Subsequently, the new iterate iterate
u_{k+1}
is found by solving the subproblem

(1.2)

\displaystyle\min_{u\in\operatorname{cone}(\mathcal{A}^{u,+}_{k})}\left[F(Ku)+\kappa_{\mathcal{A}^{u,+}_{k}}(u)\right],

where the minimization occurs over the cone spanned by
\mathcal{A}^{u,+}_{k}
, and
\mathcal{G}
is replaced by the gauge function associated with
\mathcal{A}^{u,+}_{k}
, which in this case
[
10
,
28
]
simplifies to

\displaystyle\kappa_{\mathcal{A}^{u,+}_{k}}(u)=\min_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N^{+}_{k}}}\left.\left\{\,\sum^{N^{+}_{k}}_{i=1}\lambda_{i}\;\right|\;u=\lambda_{N^{+}_{k}}\widehat{v}_{k}+\sum^{N_{k}}_{i=1}\lambda_{i}u^{k}_{i}\,\right\}.

Finally,
\mathcal{A}^{u,+}_{k}
is pruned by removing the extremal points for which the weight is set to zero by (
1.2
), obtaining the next active set
\mathcal{A}^{u}_{k+1}
.

As we will see, the proposed method combines the advantages of GCG methods, e.g., its global convergence, with an improved convergence behavior and sparser iterates.
More in detail, we first prove a global sublinear convergence rate
\mathcal{O}(1/k)
for
J(u_{k})
towards the minimum of (

P
M

), see Theorem
3.3
. This is achieved
under mild assumptions, see
(A1)
-
(A3)
discussed in Section
2
. These are quite standard and are, in particular, sufficient for the well-posedness of (

P
M

), as shown in Proposition
2.3
.
Subsequently, in Theorem
3.8
,
we show that
J(u_{k})
converges asymptotically at a linear rate of
\mathcal{O}(\zeta^{k})
,
\zeta\in(0,1)
, provided that the optimal dual variable and the loss in (

P
M

) meet certain structural requirements, see Assumptions
(B1)
-
(B5)
in Section
3.3
. Specifically, these assumptions imply that the minimizer to (

P
M

) is unique and sparse, i.e., of the form
\bar{u}=\sum^{N}_{i=1}\bar{\lambda}_{i}\bar{u}_{i}
for a finite number of extremal points
\bar{u}_{i}\in\operatorname{Ext}(B)
and coefficients
\bar{\lambda}_{i}>0
. More crucially, we also require the existence of a “distance function”
g
defined on
\operatorname{Ext}(B)
such that the forward operator
K
and the optimal dual variable
\bar{p}=-K_{*}\nabla F(K\bar{u})
associated to
\bar{u}
satisfy, respectively, the following Lipschitz and quadratic growth conditions

(1.3)

\|K(u-\bar{u}_{i})\|_{Y}\lesssim g(u,\bar{u}_{i}),\qquad 1-\langle\bar{p},u\rangle\gtrsim g(u,\bar{u}_{i})^{2}\,,

for all
i=1,\dots,N
, and extremal points
u
in the weak* vicinity of
\bar{u}_{i}
, respectively.

The proofs of the convergence results of Theorems
3.3
,
3.8
rely on a novel lifting strategy, discussed in Section
5
, which allows us to
recast (

P
M

) into an equivalent minimization
problem on
M^{+}(\mathcal{B})
, the space of positive Radon measures on the set
\mathcal{B}:=\overline{\operatorname{Ext}(B)}^{*}
.
Specifically, this is achieved by making three key
observations:
First, using Choquet’s Theorem
[
51
, Page 14]
, we argue the existence of a surjective mapping
\mathcal{I}
from
M^{+}(\mathcal{B})
to the domain of
\mathcal{G}
with

\displaystyle\langle p,\mathcal{I}(\mu)\rangle=\int_{\mathcal{B}}\langle p,v\rangle\penalty\ \mathrm{d}\mu(v)\,,\,\,\text{ for all }\,p\in\mathcal{C},\penalty\ \mu\in M^{+}(\mathcal{B})\,.

Second, we consider the auxiliary problem

(
\mathcal{P}_{M^{+}}
)

\displaystyle\inf_{\mu\in M^{+}(\mathcal{B})}\left[F(\mathcal{K}\mu)+\|\mu\|_{M(\mathcal{B})}\right]\,,

where the forward operator
K
is replaced by a lifted model
\mathcal{K}\colon M^{+}(\mathcal{B})\to Y
satisfying
\mathcal{K}\mu=K\mathcal{I}(\mu)
for all
\mu\in M^{+}(\mathcal{B})
.
As it turns out, see Theorem
5.4
, the above minimization problems are equivalent, in the sense that
\bar{u}=\mathcal{I}(\bar{\mu})
solves (

P
M

) if and only if
\bar{\mu}
minimizes in (

P

M
+

).
As a third key ingredient, such equivalence allows to interpret the iteration scheme introduced in (
1.1
)–(
1.2
) as one step of an
exchange algorithm
or a
Primal-Dual-Active-Point method
(PDAP), cf. Algorithm
2
, applied to (

P

M
+

).
These methods are fully-corrective variants of the generalized conditional gradient method for minimization problems over spaces of Borel measures introduced in
[
21
]
, and their linear convergence has been recently proven in the finite-dimensional case, i.e., for measures supported on a compact subset of the euclidean space, see
[
39
,
52
]
.
Combining these three key observations, and carefully extending the techniques and results of
[
52
]
to the present setting,
we are able to conclude the asymptotic linear convergence of our algorithm.

If we interpret (

P
M

) as a
sparse dictionary learning problem
, in which the dictionary is given by
\operatorname{Ext}(B)
, our FC-GCG method can also be linked to a
fully-corrective greedy selection
method,
[
56
]
, or an
accelerated gradient boosting
algorithm,
[
61
]
. In this context, a similar lifting approach was implicitly used in
[

]
to derive a fully-corrective GCG method for matrix-regularization problems which eventually converges at a global linear rate, albeit under very restrictive conditions. More in detail, expressed in the notation of the present paper, the authors require the strong convexity of

, as well as that
\operatorname{Ext}(B)
is a finite set. For a related result in settings with finitely many atoms we also point out

In this paper, we mainly focus on the theoretical aspects of the FC-GCG method and, in particular, its convergence. Naturally, from the practical standpoint, the applicability of our method relies on the knowledge
of the set

To illustrate the working of our method and the role of assumptions

For this example we also demonstrate numerically the expected linear convergence of Algorithm

consider the trace regularization of linear operators from a Hilbert space into itself, which favours the reconstruction of rank-one operators. In Section

Finally, in Section

2.
The minimization problem

In this section we introduce the minimization problem we are concerned with solving, and prove its well-posedness under suitable assumptions. We start by establishing some notations. Throughout the paper
\mathcal{C}
denotes a separable Banach space with norm
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{C}}
and topological dual space
\mathcal{M}\simeq\mathcal{C}^{*}
. We denote the duality pairing between
p\in\mathcal{C}
and
u\in\mathcal{M}
by
\langle p,u\rangle
. The space
\mathcal{M}
is equipped with the canonical dual norm

\displaystyle\|u\|_{\mathcal{M}}:=\sup_{\|p\|_{\mathcal{C}}\leq 1}\langle p,u\rangle\quad\text{ for all }\,\,u\in\mathcal{M}\,.

Let
\mathcal{G}\colon\mathcal{M}\to[0,\infty]
be a convex, weak* lower semi-continuous and positively one-homogeneous functional, that is
\mathcal{G}(\lambda u)=\lambda\mathcal{G}(u)
for all
\lambda\geq 0
. Let
K\colon\mathcal{M}\to Y
be a linear weak*-to-weak continuous operator, mapping to a given Hilbert space
Y
, and
F\colon Y\to\mathbb{R}
be a convex mapping. The inner product and induced norm on
Y
will be denoted by
(\cdot,\cdot)_{Y}
and
\|\cdot\|_{Y}
, respectively. Our interest lies in efficient solution algorithms for problems of the form (

P
M

), which we remind the reader is of the form

\inf_{u\in\mathcal{M}}J(u),\quad J(u):=F(Ku)+\mathcal{G}(u)\,.

Remark 2.1
.

Notice that the weak*-to-weak continuity of the linear operator
K:\mathcal{M}\rightarrow Y
implies existence of a linear continuous operator
K_{*}:Y\rightarrow\mathcal{C}
that is the pre-adjoint of
K
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\langle K_{*}y,u\rangle=(Ku,y)_{Y}\quad\text{ for all }\,\,y\in Y,\penalty\ u\in\mathcal{M}\,.

See, for example,
[
21
, Remark 3.2]
. Moreover, the existence of a continuous pre-adjoint
K_{*}
implies the strong-to-strong continuity of the operator
K
.

Throughout the paper we moreover require the following basic assumptions.

Assumption 2.2
.

Assume that:

(A1)

F
is bounded from below, strictly convex and Fréchet differentiable on
Y
. Its gradient
\nabla F\colon Y\to Y
is Lipschitz continuous on compact sets.

(A2)

The sublevel set

\displaystyle S^{-}(\mathcal{G},\alpha):=\left\{\,u\in\mathcal{M}\;|\;\mathcal{G}(u)\leq\alpha\,\right\}

is weak* compact for every
\alpha\geq 0
.

(A3)

The operator
K:\mathcal{M}\rightarrow Y
is sequentially weak*-to-strong continuous in

{\rm dom}(\mathcal{G}):=\{u\in\mathcal{M}\;|\;\mathcal{G}(u)<\infty\,\}\,,

namely, for every sequence
\{u_{k}\}_{k}
in
{\rm dom}(\mathcal{G})
such that
u_{k}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle*}}{{\rightharpoonup}}u
for some
u\in\mathcal{M}
, it holds that
Ku_{k}\to Ku
in
Y
.

Note that Assumption
(A2)
is equivalent to ask that the sublevel set
S^{-}(\mathcal{G},\alpha)
is closed and norm bounded for every
\alpha\geq 0
.
The above conditions guarantee the existence of minimizers to (

P
M

).

Proposition 2.3
.

Assume
(A1)
-
(A3)
. Then there exists at least one minimizer to (

P
M

). Moreover
\bar{u}\in\mathcal{M}
is a solution to (

P
M

) if and only if
\bar{p}=-K_{*}\nabla F(K\bar{u})\in\mathcal{C}
satisfies

(2.1)

\displaystyle\langle\bar{p},\bar{u}\rangle=\mathcal{G}(\bar{u})\,,\quad\penalty\ \max_{v\in S^{-}(\mathcal{G},1)}\langle\bar{p},v\rangle\leq 1\,.

Finally, if
\bar{u}_{1},\bar{u}_{2}\in\mathcal{M}
are two solutions to (

P
M

), then
K\bar{u}_{1}=K\bar{u}_{2}
.

Proof.

The existence of a minimizer follows by the direct method of calculus of variations. Indeed, the sublevel sets of
J
are weak* compact thanks to Assumption
(A1)
and Assumption
(A2)
. Moreover
J
is weak* lower semicontinuous, since
\mathcal{G}
is weak* lower semicontinuous,
K
is weak*-to-weak continuous and
F
is convex and continuous (Assumption
(A1)
).
In order to show (
2.1
), notice
that the function
f(u)=F(Ku)
is Gâteaux-differentiable with

f^{\prime}(u)(v)=\langle K_{*}\nabla F(Ku),v\rangle
for all
v\in\mathcal{M}
.
Hence, see e.g.
[
60
, Proposition 6.3]
,
\bar{u}\in\mathcal{M}
is a solution to (

P
M

) if and only if

\displaystyle\langle\bar{p},u-\bar{u}\rangle+\mathcal{G}(\bar{u})\leq\mathcal{G}(u)\,\,\text{ for all }\,u\in\mathcal{M}.

This variational inequality holds if and only if

\displaystyle\langle\bar{p},\bar{u}\rangle=\mathcal{G}(\bar{u}),\quad\langle\bar{p},v\rangle\leq\mathcal{G}(v)\,\,\text{ for all }\,v\in\mathcal{M}\,,

which is equivalent to (
2.1
), thanks to the one-homogeneity of
\mathcal{G}
. Last, the identity
K\bar{u}_{1}=K\bar{u}_{2}
for two solutions
\bar{u}_{1},\bar{u}_{2}
of (

P
M

) follows from the strict convexity of
F
.
∎

For the remainder of the paper we refer to

(2.2)

\bar{y}\coloneqq K\bar{u}\in Y\,,\quad\bar{p}:=-K_{*}\nabla F(K\bar{u})\in\mathcal{C}\,,

as the (unique) optimal observation and dual variable for (

P
M

), respectively, where
\bar{u}
is any minimizer to (

P
M

). Set
B=S^{-}(\mathcal{G},1)
. In the following we further require the notion of an

of

.

Definition 2.4
.

An element

is called an extremal point of

if there are
no

u_{1},u_{2}\in B
and

with

. The set of all extremal points of

Since

is weak* compact, non empty and convex thanks to Assumptions

-

and for every

. Set

. Since the predual space

In particular, we have that

Remark 2.5

Let us mention that all the results in this paper still hold under slightly weaker conditions on the functional

for which
\dom(F)
is open. In this case,
F
is required to be smooth on
\dom(F)
and
\nabla F\colon\dom(F)\to Y
is assumed to be Lipschitz continuous on compact subsets of
\dom(F)
. For more details, we refer the interested reader to
[
52
,
60
]
.

3.
A numerical minimization algorithm

This section concerns the development of an implementable and efficient solution algorithm for the minimization problem (

P
M

), namely, the
fully-corrective generalized conditional gradient method
(FC-GCG). As anticipated in the introduction, FC-GCG
comprises the two basic steps at (
1.1
) and (
1.2
), which we
now describe in more detail.
For this purpose, recall that every
u\in\dom(\mathcal{G})
can be approximated, in the weak* topology of
\mathcal{M}
, by a sequence in
\operatorname{cone}(\operatorname{Ext}(B))
thanks to Krein-Milman’s Theorem. The considered method exploits this observation by alternating between the update of a finite set

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}^{u}_{k}=\{u^{k}_{i}\}^{N_{k}}_{i=1}\subset\operatorname{Ext}(B),

the so-called
active set
, and of an iterate

\displaystyle u_{k}\in\operatorname{cone}(\mathcal{A}^{u}_{k})=\left\{\,u=\sum^{N_{k}}_{i=1}\lambda^{k}_{i}u^{k}_{i}\;|\;\lambda^{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{k}}_{+}\,\right\}\,,

where
\mathbb{R}_{+}=[0,+\infty)
. Given the current active set
\mathcal{A}^{u}_{k}=\{u^{k}_{i}\}^{N_{k}}_{i=1}
with
u^{k}_{i}\in\operatorname{Ext}(B)
and the iterate
u_{k}\in\operatorname{cone}(\mathcal{A}^{u}_{k})
, we compute the dual variable
p_{k}=-K_{*}\nabla F(Ku_{k})\in\mathcal{C}
and update

(3.1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}^{u,+}_{k}=\mathcal{A}^{u}_{k}\cup\{\widehat{v}^{u}_{k}\},\quad\penalty\ \widehat{v}^{u}_{k}\in\argmax_{v\in\operatorname{Ext}(B)}\,\langle p_{k},v\rangle\,.

The above step requires the minimization of a linear functional over the not necessarily compact set of extremal points.
Remarkably, this problem is well-posed, as shown in Lemma
A.1
in Appendix
A
.
Setting
N^{+}_{k}=N_{k}+1
and
u^{k}_{N^{+}_{k}}=\widehat{v}^{u}_{k}
, we introduce the gauge function associated with
\mathcal{A}^{u,+}_{k}
as

\displaystyle\kappa_{\mathcal{A}^{u,+}_{k}}(u):=\min_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N^{+}_{k}}}\left\{\left.\,\sum^{N^{+}_{k}}_{i=1}\lambda_{i}\;\right|\;u=\sum^{N^{+}_{k}}_{i=1}\lambda_{i}u_{i}^{k}\,\right\}.

Note that
\kappa_{\mathcal{A}^{u,+}_{k}}(u)
is well-defined on
\operatorname{cone}(\mathcal{A}^{u,+}_{k})
, as the minimum is
achieved due to lower semicontinuity of the
\ell^{1}
norm and closedness of
\mathbb{R}_{+}
.
Subsequently, the next iterate
u_{k+1}
is found by solving the subproblem

(3.2)

\displaystyle\min_{u\in\operatorname{cone}(\mathcal{A}^{u,+}_{k})}\left[F(Ku)+\kappa_{\mathcal{A}^{u,+}_{k}}(u)\right],

where the search for the minimizer is restricted to the cone spanned by
\mathcal{A}^{u,+}_{k}
and the possibly complicated regularizer
\mathcal{G}
is replaced by the easier-to-handle gauge function of the finite set
\mathcal{A}^{u,+}_{k}
. We point out that the objective functional in (
3.2
) constitutes an upper bound on
J
, i.e., there holds

\displaystyle J(u)\leq F(Ku)+\kappa_{\mathcal{A}^{u,+}_{k}}(u)\quad\text{for all}\penalty\ u\in\operatorname{cone}(\mathcal{A}^{u,+}_{k})

due to the convexity and one-homogeneity of
\mathcal{G}
. Apart from that, Problems (

P
M

) and (
3.2
) share the same basic structure of minimizing the sum of a smooth fidelity term and a gauge-like regularizer. In particular, the existence result and the necessary first-order conditions in Proposition
2.3
also apply to (
3.2
). However, as shown in Proposition
A.2
in Appendix
A
, the big advantage of (
3.2
) is that a solution can be computed as

(3.3)

u_{k+1}=\sum^{N^{+}_{k}}_{i=1}\lambda^{k+1}_{i}u^{k}_{i}\,,

where
\lambda^{k+1}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N^{+}_{k}}
solves the following finite dimensional optimization problem:

(3.4)

\displaystyle\min_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N^{+}_{k}}}\left[F\left(\sum^{N^{+}_{k}}_{i=1}\lambda_{i}Ku^{k}_{i}\right)+\sum^{N^{+}_{k}}_{i=1}\lambda_{i}\right]\,.

Thus, in practice, we first determine a minimizer
\lambda^{k+1}\in\mathbb{R}^{N^{+}_{k}}
to (
3.4
). We remark that (
3.4
) constitutes a finite dimensional non-smooth convex minimization problem, which can be efficiently solved by proximal methods or generalized Newton algorithms provided that
F
is sufficiently smooth.
Once this is accomplished, the new iterate
u_{k+1}
is defined according to (
3.3
). As a final step, we truncate the active set
\mathcal{A}^{u,+}_{k}
by removing all extremal points that were assigned a zero weight by the optimization
procedure (
3.4
), i.e., we set

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}^{u}_{k+1}=\mathcal{A}^{u,+}_{k}\setminus\{\,u^{k}_{i}\;|\;\lambda^{k+1}_{i}=0,\penalty\ i=1,\dots,N^{+}_{k}\,\}

and increment
k
by one. The method is summarized in Algorithm
1
below.

FC-GCG for (

P

,
\lambda^{0}_{i}>0
,

.

for

and

and
\widehat{v}^{u}_{k}
with

\displaystyle p_{k}=-K_{*}\nabla F(Ku_{k})\,,\quad\widehat{v}^{u}_{k}\in\argmax_{v\in\operatorname{Ext}(B)}\,\langle p_{k},v\rangle\,.

3.1.
Stopping condition

Note that Algorithm
1
terminates at iteration
k\geq 1
if

(3.5)

\max_{v\in\operatorname{Ext}(B)}\langle p_{k},v\rangle\leq 1\,.

This is justified by the fact that, in this case, the current iterate
u^{k}
is a minimizer to (

P
M

), as shown
in Proposition
3.1
below. In particular, in this situation, the algorithm converges in a finite number
of iterations.

Proposition 3.1
.

Suppose
(A1)
-
(A3)
in Assumption
2.2
hold.
Let
u_{k}\in\operatorname{cone}(\mathcal{A}^{u}_{k})
be generated by Algorithm
1
, with
k\geq 1
. Set
p_{k}=-K_{*}\nabla F(Ku_{k})
and assume (
3.5
). Then
u_{k}
solves (

P
M

).

Proof.

Thanks to Proposition
2.3
and Lemma
A.1
,
u_{k}
solves (

P
M

)
if and only if

(3.6)

\displaystyle\langle p_{k},u_{k}\rangle=\mathcal{G}(u_{k})\,,\quad\max_{v\in B}\langle p_{k},v\rangle=\max_{v\in\operatorname{Ext}(B)}\langle p_{k},v\rangle\leq 1\,.

The second condition in (
3.6
) is satisfied by assumption, so we are only left to check the first.
If
u_{k}=0
this is trivial, since
\mathcal{G}(0)=0
.
Therefore assume
u_{k}\neq 0
. This implies

\mathcal{G}(u_{k})\neq 0
. Indeed, if by contradiction

\mathcal{G}(u_{k})=0
, then by one-homogeneity of
\mathcal{G}
we would obtain

\lambda u_{k}\in S^{-}(\mathcal{G},0)
for all
\lambda\geq 0
. But then

S^{-}(\mathcal{G},0)
would not be norm bounded, contradicting
(A2)
. Thus
\mathcal{G}(u_{k})\neq 0
. Since
u_{k}/\mathcal{G}(u_{k})\in B
, from the second condition in
(
3.6
) we get

(3.7)

\displaystyle\langle p_{k},u_{k}\rangle\leq\mathcal{G}(u_{k}).

By construction
u_{k}=\sum^{N_{k}}_{i=1}\lambda^{k}_{i}u^{k}_{i}
, with
u_{i}^{k}\in\operatorname{Ext}(B)
and
\lambda^{k}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N_{k}}
a minimizer of

\displaystyle\min_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N_{k}}}\left[F\left(\sum^{N_{k}}_{i=1}\lambda_{i}Ku^{k}_{i}\right)+\sum^{N_{k}}_{i=1}\lambda_{i}\right].

Note that since
\lambda^{k}
is a minimizer to the above problem, we have

(3.8)

\displaystyle\langle p_{k},u_{k}\rangle=\sum^{N_{k}}_{i=1}\lambda^{k}_{i}\langle p_{k},u^{k}_{i}\rangle=\sum^{N_{k}}_{i=1}\lambda^{k}_{i}\,,\quad\langle p_{k},u^{k}_{i}\rangle\leq 1\,,

for
i=1,\dots,N_{k}
.
As
u_{k}=\sum^{N_{k}}_{i=1}\lambda^{k}_{i}u^{k}_{i}
with
u_{i}^{k}\in\operatorname{Ext}(B)
, by convexity and one-homogeneity of
\mathcal{G}
we estimate

\mathcal{G}(u_{k})\leq\sum^{N_{k}}_{i=1}\lambda^{k}_{i}
.
Therefore
\langle p_{k},u_{k}\rangle=\mathcal{G}(u_{k})
by (
3.7
)-(
3.8
), ending the proof.
∎

Remark 3.2
.

Note that Algorithm

Indeed in this situation we have

3.2.
Worst-case convergence rates

The main contribution of the present manuscript is the derivation of convergence results for the sequence of
residuals

(3.9)

\displaystyle r_{J}(u_{k})=J(u_{k})-\min_{u\in\mathcal{M}}J(u)

associated to the iterates generated by Algorithm
1
. This is a challenging task for a variety of reasons. For example the space
\mathcal{M}
is, in general, not reflexive. Moreover the functional
J
lacks useful properties, such as smoothness or strict convexity.
Classical approaches
[
36
,
35
,
5
]
provide sublinear convergence rates for GCG methods defined in Banach spaces, however very few linear convergence results are available in the literature and these results are usually limited to specific cases.
In this section, we state the convergence results anticipated in the introduction, and we detail the additional assumptions which are needed to prove them. Their proofs, which are rather technical, are then postponed to Sections
6.1
and
6.2.10
.
We start with the following sublinear convergence result, which holds under the basic Assumptions
(A1)
-
(A3)
.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
(A1)
-
(A3)
in Assumption
2.2
hold. Then, Algorithm
1
either terminates after a finite number of steps, with
\bar{u}=u_{k}
a minimizer to (

P
M

), or there is a constant
c>0
such that

(3.10)

r_{J}(u_{k})\leq c\,\frac{1}{k+1}\quad\text{ for all }\,\,k\in\mathbb{N}\,,

where
r_{J}
is defined at (
3.9
). Moreover, in this case, the sequence
\{u_{k}\}_{k}
admits at least one weak* accumulation point and each of such point is a solution to (

P
M

). If the solution
\bar{u}
to (

P
M

) is unique, then we have
u_{k}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle*}}{{\rightharpoonup}}\bar{u}
in
\mathcal{M}
for the whole sequence.

3.3.
Non-degeneracy and fast convergence

While Theorem
3.3
proves the convergence of Algorithm
1
, the provided, slow, sublinear rate of convergence does not match the computed results of Section
4.1
. Motivated by this gap between theory and numerical observations, we argue the asymptotic linear convergence of
r_{J}(u_{k})
provided that certain structural assumptions on problem (

P
M

) are satisfied. First, in addition to Assumptions
(A1)
-
(A3)
, we require that the solution set of the linear problem

\max_{v\in\mathcal{B}}\,\langle\bar{p},v\rangle

consists of a finite number of extremal points
\bar{u}_{1},\ldots,\bar{u}_{N}\in\ext(B)
, where
\bar{p}
denotes the unique dual variable of (

P
M

), see (
2.2
). Moreover, we ask that the restriction of the operator
K
into the span of
\bar{\mathcal{A}}\coloneqq\{\bar{u}_{i}\}^{N}_{i=1}
is injective. Such assumptions ensure the uniqueness of the minimizer to (

P
M

), denoted by
\bar{u}
, see Proposition
3.5
below. Additionally, we ask that
F
is strongly convex around the unique optimal observation
\bar{y}
, see (
2.2
). This set of assumptions is summarized below.

Assumption 3.4
.

(Uniqueness and strong convexity)

(B1)

The map
F\colon Y\to\mathbb{R}
is strictly convex and strongly convex around the unique optimal observation
\bar{y}
, i.e., there exists a neighborhood
\mathcal{N}(\bar{y})
of
\bar{y}
and
\theta>0
such that

\displaystyle(\nabla F(y_{1})-\nabla F(y_{2}),y_{1}-y_{2})_{Y}\geq\theta\|y_{1}-y_{2}\|_{Y}^{2}\,,\,\,\text{ for all }\,\,y_{1},y_{2}\in\mathcal{N}(\bar{y})\,,

(B2)

There is
N>0
and a finite collection of extremal points
\bar{\mathcal{A}}:=\{\bar{u}_{i}\}^{N}_{i=1}\subset\operatorname{Ext}(B)
such that the unique dual variable
\bar{p}=-K_{*}\nabla F(\bar{y})\in\mathcal{C}
satisfies

\argmax_{v\in\mathcal{B}}\,\langle\bar{p},v\rangle=\left\{\,v\in\mathcal{B}\;|\;\langle\bar{p},v\rangle=1\,\right\}=\{\bar{u}_{i}\}^{N}_{i=1}\,,

(B3)

The set
\{K\bar{u}_{i}\}^{N}_{i=1}\subset Y

Proposition 3.5

Let Assumptions
(A1)
-
(A3)

Remark 3.9
.

Before ending this section, we briefly comment on Assumptions
(B1)
-
(B5)
. First, we note that Assumption
(B3)
is trivially fulfilled if
K
is injective, as seen for example in Section
4.1
. Moreover, Assumptions
(B3)
,
(B4)
, as well as the uniqueness of the minimizer to (

P
M

), are not fully independent. Indeed, if
\{K\bar{u}_{i}\}^{N}_{i=1}
is linearly dependent and there is a solution
\bar{u}=\sum^{N}_{i=1}\bar{\lambda}_{i}\bar{u}_{i}
with
\bar{\lambda}_{i}>0
, then there exists a second solution
\tilde{u}\neq\bar{u}
with
\tilde{u}=\sum^{N}_{i=1}\tilde{\lambda}_{i}\bar{u}_{i}
,
\tilde{\lambda}_{i}\geq 0
, and equality holds for at least one index, cf. also
[
52
, Section 3.2]
.
Second, it is worthwhile to further discuss the quadratic growth condition in Assumption
(B5)
and relate it to more well-known concepts in the literature. For this purpose, recall that the necessary and sufficient optimality conditions for (

P
M

) are given by the variational subgradient inequality

\displaystyle\langle\bar{p},u-\bar{u}\rangle+\mathcal{G}(\bar{u})\leq\mathcal{G}({u})\quad\text{for all}\penalty\ u\in\mathcal{M}\,.

Due to the one-homogeneity of
\mathcal{G}
, this can be equivalently reformulated as

\displaystyle\langle\bar{p},\bar{u}\rangle=\mathcal{G}(\bar{u}),\quad 1\geq\langle\bar{p},u\rangle\quad\text{for all}\penalty\ u\in B\,,

see Proposition
2.3
. By applying Lemma
A.1
, this is equivalent to

\displaystyle\langle\bar{p},\bar{u}\rangle=\mathcal{G}(\bar{u}),\quad 1\geq\langle\bar{p},u\rangle\quad\text{for all}\penalty\ u\in\operatorname{Ext}(B).

Finally, due to Assumption
(B2)
, we arrive at

\displaystyle\langle\bar{p},\bar{u}\rangle=\mathcal{G}(\bar{u}),\quad 0\geq\langle\bar{p},u-\bar{u}_{i}\rangle\quad\text{for all}\penalty\ u\in\operatorname{Ext}(B).

In particular, this implies that
\bar{p}\in\partial I_{\operatorname{Ext}(B)}(\bar{u}_{i})
where
I_{\operatorname{Ext}(B)}(\bar{u}_{i})
denotes the subdifferential of the nonconvex indicator function of
\operatorname{Ext}(B)
at
\bar{u}_{i}
. In this context, Assumption
(B5)
implies the locally strengthened condition

\displaystyle\langle\bar{p},\bar{u}\rangle=\mathcal{G}(\bar{u}),\quad 0\geq\langle\bar{p},u-\bar{u}_{i}\rangle+\kappa g(u,\bar{u}_{i})^{2}\quad\text{whenever}\penalty\ u\in U_{i}\,.

This is very reminiscent of the concept of
strongly metric subregular subdifferentials
in convex optimization which plays a vital role for the derivation of fast convergence rates for proximal point methods
[
4
]
, and “vanilla” generalized conditional gradient methods
[
48
]
.

We point out that, in the general case, we are not aware of possibly stronger, but more intuitive, structural assumptions on
\bar{p}
as well as
\operatorname{Ext}(B)
which eventually ensure Assumptions
(B2)
and
(B5)
. However, for particular instances such a characterization is indeed possible, see Section
4
and the examples and references therein.

4.
Examples

Summarizing the previous sections, we see that the practical application and the convergence analysis of Algorithm

rest on three pillars: First, a characterization of the set of extremal points

and, third, the derivation of sufficient structural assumptions to ensure Assumptions
(B1)
-
(B5)
. In this section, we outline this program for three examples, namely, sparse initial value identification, trace regularization, and minimum effort problems, see Sections
4.1
,
4.2
,
4.3
, respectively. In all these cases, a particular focus is put on the computation of
\widehat{v}^{u}_{k}
and the verification of Assumption
(B5)
. For the sake of brevity, we decided to strike a balance between practically interesting settings and problems for which the characterization of
\operatorname{Ext}(B)
and the derivation of Assumption
(B5)
can be done in a concise manner. As a main take-away message, these examples suggest that there is no general “recipe” for the resolution of Step 2 in Algorithm
1
. Quite the reverse, the method of choice for computing
\widehat{v}^{u}_{k}
as well as the associated computational burden strongly depend on the example at hand.

We also stress that Algorithm
1
is applicable to far more complex problems, in which characterizing extremal points and deriving quadratic growth conditions could also get much more convoluted. One such problem, namely the optimal transport regularization of dynamic inverse problems, is briefly teased in Section
4.4
, and will be the subject of a follow-up work. Other examples are given by works
[
25
,
45
]
, in which the authors apply the program outlined above to certain regularizers given by certain infimal convolutions.

4.1.
Sparse source identification

Let us consider the inverse problem of identifying the initial source of a heat equation on a convex polygonal spatial domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
from distributed temperature measurements
y_{d}
at a given final time
T>0
. Our particular interest lies in the recovery of sparse sources

u^{\dagger}=\sum^{N}_{i=1}\lambda^{\dagger}_{i}\delta_{x^{\dagger}_{i}}\,,

given as a linear combination of finitely many point measures, where the coefficients
\lambda^{\dagger}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
, the positions
x^{\dagger}_{i}\in\Omega
, and the number
N\in\mathbb{N}
of points are all assumed to be unknown. Taking the ill-posedness of the described inverse problem into account we follow
[
21
,
50
]
and consider the convex Tikhonov-regularized problem

(4.1)

\displaystyle\min_{u\in M(\Omega),y}\left[\frac{1}{2}\|y(T)-y_{d}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\beta\|u\|_{M(\Omega)}\right]\,.

Here
M(\Omega)
denotes the space of Borel measures on the open set
\Omega
,
y
is a scalar function defined on
[0,1]\times\Omega
with
y(t):=y(t)(\cdot)
,
y_{d}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
is a given desired state, and the pair
(y,u)
satisfies, in the sense of distributions, the heat equation

(4.2)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}y-\bigtriangleup y=0\penalty\ \text{in}\penalty\ (0,T)\times\Omega,\penalty\ y=0\penalty\ \text{in}\penalty\ (0,T)\times\partial\Omega,\penalty\ y(0)=u\penalty\ \text{in}\penalty\ \Omega\,.

The
a priori
assumption on the sparsity of the unknown source is encoded in the choice of the regularizer, defined as the total variation norm of
u
with
\beta>0
.
To fit (
4.1
) into the setting of (

P
M

) we set
\mathcal{C}=C_{0}(\Omega)
, the space of continuous functions vanishing on
\partial\Omega
, and we equip it with the canonical supremum norm

\displaystyle\|p\|_{\mathcal{C}}=\max_{x\in\Omega}|p(x)|\quad\text{for all}\penalty\ p\in C_{0}(\Omega)\,.

This makes
\mathcal{C}
a Banach space. According to the Riesz-Markov-Kakutani theorem we have
\mathcal{C}^{*}\simeq\mathcal{M}
for
\mathcal{M}=M(\Omega)
. Moreover, define
Y=L^{2}(\Omega)
,
F=(1/2)\|\cdot-y_{d}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
and
\mathcal{G}=\beta\|\cdot\|_{M(\Omega)}
. Of course, these functionals satisfy
(A1)
and
(A2)
in Assumption
2.2
. Finally, we replace the PDE constraint by introducing a
source-to-observation
operator
K\colon M(\Omega)\to L^{2}(\Omega)
mapping a measure
u\in M(\Omega)
to
y(T)
, where
y
solves (
4.2
). It is readily verified that
K
is injective, thanks to a priori estimates for weak solutions to (
4.2
)
[
26
, Lemma 2.2]
, as well as weak*-to-strong continuous
[
26
, Lemma 2.3]
. Hence, (
4.1
) admits a unique solution and
(A3)
in Assumption
2.2
is satisfied. Moreover,
K
is the adjoint of the operator
K_{*}\colon L^{2}(\Omega)\to C_{0}(\Omega)
defined by
K_{*}\varphi:=z(0)
, where the pair
(z,\varphi)
satisfies, in the sense of distributions, the backwards heat equation

(4.3)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}z+\bigtriangleup z=0\penalty\ \text{in}\penalty\ (0,T)\times\Omega,\penalty\ z=0\penalty\ \text{in}\penalty\ (0,T)\times\partial\Omega,\penalty\ z(T)=\varphi\penalty\ \text{in}\penalty\ \Omega\,.

For more details we refer to
[
50
,
26
]
. Note that
K_{*}
is well-defined as
z(0)\in C_{0}(\Omega)
, due to parabolic regularity estimates. For sake of completeness, this is justified in Lemma
B.1
in the appendix.

The next lemma characterizes the set of extremal points of the unit ball of the regularizer
\mathcal{G}
, that is, of the set
B=S^{-}(\beta\|\cdot\|_{M(\Omega)},1)
.

Lemma 4.1
.

We have

(4.4)

\ext(B)=\{\sigma\beta^{-1}\delta_{x}\,\colon\,x\in\Omega,\,\sigma\in\{-1,1\}\}\,.

Moreover,
\overline{\ext(B)}^{*}=\ext(B)\cup\{0\}
.

Proof.

The characterization of
\ext(B)
is well-known
[
13
, Proposition 4.1]
.
As for the second claim, note that
0\in\overline{\ext(B)}^{*}
: indeed one can take
\{x_{k}\}_{k}
in
\Omega
such that
x_{k}\to x
with
x\in\partial\Omega
, so that
u_{k}:=\beta^{-1}\delta_{x_{k}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle*}}{{\rightharpoonup}}0
. Thus
\ext(B)\cup\{0\}\subset\overline{\ext(B)}^{*}
. For the opposite inclusion, assume given a sequence
u_{k}=\sigma_{k}\beta^{-1}\delta_{x_{k}}
in
\ext(B)
, such that
u_{k}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle*}}{{\rightharpoonup}}u
. Then, up to subsequences,
\sigma_{k}\rightarrow\sigma\in\{-1,1\}
and
x_{k}\rightarrow x\in\overline{\Omega}
. Hence, if
x\in\partial\Omega
then
u=0
, while if
x\in\Omega
then
u=\sigma\beta^{-1}\delta_{x}\in\ext(B)
.
∎

Moreover, applying Proposition
2.3
and the characterization of
K_{*}
, we immediately deduce the optimality conditions for (
4.1
).

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
\bar{u}\in M(\Omega)
be given and denote by
\bar{z}
the solution to (
4.3
) for
\varphi=y_{d}-\bar{y}
,
\bar{y}=K\bar{u}
.
Then
\bar{u}
is a minimizer of (
4.1
) if and only if

\displaystyle\langle\bar{z}(0),\bar{u}\rangle=\beta\|\bar{u}\|_{M(\Omega)},\quad\|\bar{z}(0)\|_{\mathcal{C}}\leq\beta\,.

This implies

\displaystyle\supp\bar{u}\subset\left\{\,x\in\Omega\;|\;|\bar{z}(0)(x)|=\beta\,\right\}.

Thanks to the characterization of extremal points presented in Lemma
4.1
, the FC-GCG method presented in Algorithm
1
for solving (
4.1
) generates a sequence of iterates

u_{k}=\sum^{N_{k}}_{i=1}\lambda^{k}_{i}u_{i}^{k}\,,\quad u_{i}^{k}=\sigma^{k}_{i}\beta^{-1}\delta_{x^{k}_{i}}\,,\quad\sigma^{k}_{i}\in\{-1,1\}\,,\quad x^{k}_{i}\in\Omega\,,\quad\lambda_{i}^{k}\geq 0\,,

as well as an associated sequence of active sets
\mathcal{A}^{u}_{k}=\{u^{k}_{i}\}^{N_{k}}_{i=1}
. Moreover, let
z_{k}
denote the solution of (
4.3
) for
\varphi=y_{d}-y_{k}(T)
,
y_{k}(T)=Ku_{k}
. We now claim that the new candidate extremal point in the iteration
k
of Algorithm
1
can be chosen as

(4.5)

\displaystyle\widehat{v}^{u}_{k}=\operatorname{sign}(z_{k}(0)(\hat{x}_{k}))\beta^{-1}\delta_{\hat{x}_{k}}\penalty\ \text{ where }\penalty\ \hat{x}_{k}\in\argmax_{x\in\Omega}|z_{k}(0)(x)|,

i.e., Step 2 in Algorithm
1
is equivalent to computing a global extremum of a continuous function.
This is verified in the following proposition.

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
\widehat{v}^{u}_{k}
be defined as in (
4.5
).
There holds
z_{k}(0)=-K_{*}\nabla F(Ku_{k})
as well as

\displaystyle\langle z_{k}(0),\widehat{v}^{u}_{k}\rangle=\max_{v\in\mathcal{B}}\,\langle z_{k}(0),v\rangle\,.

Proof.

We directly get
z_{k}(0)=K_{*}(y_{k}(T)-y_{d})=-K_{*}\nabla F(Ku_{k})
from the characterization of
K_{*}
. The remaining statement follows directly from

\displaystyle\langle z_{k}(0),v\rangle\leq\|z_{k}(0)\|_{\mathcal{C}}\|v\|_{\mathcal{M}}\leq\|z_{k}(0)\|_{\mathcal{C}}/\beta\quad\text{for all}\penalty\ v\in\mathcal{B}

as well as

\langle z_{k}(0),\widehat{v}^{u}_{k}\rangle=\operatorname{sign}(z_{k}(0)(\hat{x}_{k}))\beta^{-1}z_{k}(0)(\hat{x}_{k})=\|z_{k}(0)\|_{\mathcal{C}}/\beta\,.\qed

Thus, for sparsity examples, solving (
3.1
) amounts to computing an extremum of the continuous function
z_{k}(0)=-K_{*}\nabla F(Ku_{k})
, where
u_{k}
is the iterate generated by Algorithm
1
. Since
|z_{k}(0)|
is, in general, non-concave, this optimization task could be non-trivial. However, since the spatial domain
\Omega
is low dimensional in this example, it is possible to resort to heuristic strategies to approximate the extremum of
z_{k}(0)
. In particular, a standard widely accepted strategy
[
21
,
11
]
, consists in discretizing the domain
\Omega
using a uniform grid
\{x^{h}\}_{h=1,\ldots N^{2}}
and performing local searches around
x^{h}
using gradient descent methods. Then the extremum of
z_{k}(0)
can be estimated as

(4.6)

\displaystyle\argmax_{y^{h}:\,h=1,\ldots,N^{2}}z_{k}(0)(y^{h})\,,

where
y^{h}
is the outcome of the local search around the point of the grid
x^{h}
. Moreover, a practical implementation of Algorithm
1
for this particular problem also entails a discretization of the heat equation, e.g. by piecewise polynomial and continuous finite elements. In this case, the computation of the new point
\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{x}_{k}
becomes trivial, see Section
4.1.1
.

We now discuss the non-degeneracy conditions. Denoting by
\bar{u}\in M(\Omega)
the minimizer of (
4.1
), we propose a natural and easy to verify set of assumptions for
\bar{z}(0)
that implies our general non-degeneracy assumptions from Section
3.3
for a suitable choice of
g
. More precisely, this new set of assumptions on
\bar{z}(0)
will imply Assumption
(B2)
,
(B3)
and
(B5)
. We remark that Assumption
(B4)
still needs to be assumed to ensure the fast convergence; however we decide not to state it in the following set of assumptions as, for this specific example, it would be formulated exactly as in
(B4)
. Moreover, its verification can be done straightforwardly looking at the structure of the unique minimizer of (
4.1
).
We finally remind that
\bar{z}
is the solution to (
4.3
) for
\varphi=y_{d}-\bar{y}
,
\bar{y}=K\bar{u}
and therefore by the characterization of
K_{*}
it holds that
\bar{z}(0)=-K_{*}\nabla F(K\bar{u})
.
The following structural assumptions are made, see also
[
50
,
52
]
.

(C1)

There are
\bar{x}_{i}\in\Omega
,
i=1,\dots,N
,
N>0
, such that

(4.7)

\displaystyle\left\{\,x\in\Omega\;|\;|\bar{z}(0)(x)|=\beta\,\right\}=\{\bar{x}_{i}\}^{N}_{i=1}.

(C2)

There exists
\gamma>0
such that for all
i=1,\dots,N
we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{sign}(\bar{z}(0)(\bar{x}_{i}))(\delta x,\nabla^{2}\bar{z}(0)(\bar{x}_{i})\delta x)_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\leq-\gamma|\delta x|^{2}\,\,\text{ for all }\,\delta x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}.

Regarding
(C2)
recall that
\bar{z}(0)
is at least two times continuously differentiable in the vicinity of
\bar{x}_{i},\penalty\ i=1,\dots,N
, see Lemma
B.1
.
Loosely speaking, the additional requirements in Assumptions
(C1)
-
(C2)
state that
\bar{z}(0)
only admits a finite number of global minima/maxima and its curvature around them does not degenerate. The latter corresponds to a second order sufficient optimality condition for the global extrema of
\bar{z}(0)
.
We now prove that
(C1)
and
(C2)
imply
(B2)
,
(B3)
and
(B5)
.
First we show that
(C1)
guarantees Assumptions
(B2)
-
(B3)
.
For this purpose, set
\bar{u}_{i}=\operatorname{sign}(\bar{z}(0)(\bar{x}_{i}))\beta^{-1}\delta_{\bar{x}_{i}}\in\ext(B)
for every
i=1,\dots,N
.

Proposition 4.4
.

Let Assumption
(C1)
hold.
Then, we have

\displaystyle\argmax_{v\in\overline{\ext(B)}^{*}}\langle\bar{z}(0),v\rangle=\left\{\bar{u}_{i}\right\}^{N}_{i=1}.

Moreover, the set
\{K\bar{u}_{i}\}^{N}_{i=1}
is linearly independent.

Proof.

First recall that every
v\in\operatorname{Ext}(B)
is of the form
v=\sigma\beta^{-1}\delta_{x}
for some
\sigma\in\{-1,1\},\penalty\ x\in\Omega
. Moreover, we have

\displaystyle\langle\bar{z}(0),\sigma\beta^{-1}\delta_{x}\rangle=(\sigma/\beta)\bar{z}(0)(x)\leq\|\bar{z}(0)\|_{\mathcal{C}}/\beta=1,

see Proposition
4.2
and (
4.7
),
with equality if and only if
|\bar{z}(0)(x)|=\|\bar{z}(0)\|_{\mathcal{C}}
,
\sigma=\operatorname{sign}(\bar{z}(0)(x))
. Hence, the claimed statement follows from (
4.7
) and Lemma
4.1
. Finally, the linear independence of
\{K\bar{u}_{i}\}^{N}_{i=1}
follows from the injectivity of
K
.
∎

Next we address Assumption
(B5)
. For every subdomain
\Omega_{0}
with
\bar{\Omega}_{0}\subset\Omega
define the quantities

(4.8)

\|\psi\|_{\operatorname{Lip}(\Omega_{0})}:=\sup_{x,y\in\Omega_{0},x\neq y}\frac{|\psi(x)-\psi(y)|}{|x-y|}

for
\psi\in C_{0}(\Omega)
and

(4.9)

\|K_{*}\|_{Y,\operatorname{Lip}(\Omega_{0})}:=\sup_{\|y\|_{Y}\leq 1}\|K_{*}y\|_{\operatorname{Lip}(\Omega_{0})}\,.

Note that
\|K_{*}\|_{Y,\operatorname{Lip}(\Omega_{0})}<\infty
, due to Lemma
B.1
. The next lemma shows that Assumptions
(C1)
-
(C2)
imply the quadratic growth of
\beta-|\bar{z}(0)|
around
\{\bar{x}_{i}\}^{N}_{i=1}
. The proof can be found in Section
B.1.1
in the appendix.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let Assumption
(C1)
-
(C2)
hold and fix an index
i=1,\dots,N
. Then, there exists
R>0
such that
\bar{B}_{R}(\bar{x}_{i})\subset\Omega
,
\operatorname{sign}(\bar{z}(0)(x))=\operatorname{sign}(\bar{z}(0)(\bar{x}_{i}))
for all

and

(4.10)

\displaystyle\beta-|\bar{z}(0)(x)|\geq(\gamma/4)|x-\bar{x}_{i}|^{2}\quad\text{for all}\penalty\ x\in B_{R}(\bar{x}_{i})\,,

(4.11)

\displaystyle\|K(\delta_{x}-\delta_{\bar{x}_{i}})\|_{Y}\leq\|K_{*}\|_{Y,\operatorname{Lip}(B_{R}(\bar{x}_{i}))}|x-\bar{x}_{i}|,\quad\text{for all}\penalty\ x\in B_{R}(\bar{x}_{i})\,.

Now, given arbitrary extremal points
\sigma_{1}\beta^{-1}\delta_{x_{1}},\sigma_{2}\beta^{-1}\delta_{x_{2}}\in\operatorname{Ext}(B)
, with
\penalty\ \sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}\in\{-1,+1\}
and
x_{1},x_{2}\in\Omega
, define the distance function
g\colon\ext(B)\times\ext(B)\to[0,\infty)
by

(4.12)

\displaystyle g(u_{1},u_{2})=|\sigma_{1}-\sigma_{2}|+|x_{1}-x_{2}|\,.

Such
g
will be the one verifying Assumption
(B5)
.
In the next lemma we show that the weak* convergence in
M(\Omega)
of a sequence of extremal points to
\bar{u}_{i}
is equivalent to convergence with respect to
g
. The proof can be found in Section
B.1.2
in the appendix.

Lemma 4.6
.

Consider a sequence
\{u_{k}\}_{k}
in
\operatorname{Ext}(B)
, i.e.,
u_{k}=\sigma_{k}\beta^{-1}\delta_{x_{k}}
, for
\sigma_{k}\in\{-1,+1\}
and
x_{k}\in\Omega
. Then, there holds

\displaystyle u_{k}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle*}}{{\rightharpoonup}}\bar{u}_{i}\penalty\ \text{if and only if}\penalty\ \lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}g(u_{k},\bar{u}_{i})=0\,.

In particular, if
u_{k}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle*}}{{\rightharpoonup}}\bar{u}_{i}
, then
\sigma_{k}=\operatorname{sign}(\bar{z}(0)(\bar{x}_{i}))
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
large enough.

Finally we combine the previous observations to conclude Assumption
(B5)
.

Proposition 4.7
.

Let
R>0
be chosen according to Lemma
4.5
. Then, there is a
d_{\mathcal{B}}
-neighbourhood
\bar{U}_{i}
of
\bar{u}_{i}
which satisfies

\displaystyle U_{i}\coloneqq\bar{U}_{i}\cap\operatorname{Ext}(B)\subset\left\{\,\sigma\beta^{-1}\delta_{x}\;|\;\sigma=\operatorname{sign}(\bar{z}(0)(\bar{x}_{i})),\penalty\ x\in B_{R}(\bar{x}_{i})\,\right\}

as well as

\begin{gathered}\|K(u-\bar{u}_{i})\|_{L^{2}}\leq(\|K_{*}\|_{Y,\operatorname{Lip}(B_{R}(\bar{x}_{i}))}/\beta)\,g(u,\bar{u}_{i})\\
\langle\bar{z}(0),\bar{u}_{i}-u\rangle\geq(\gamma/(4\beta))\,g(u,\bar{u}_{i})^{2}\end{gathered}

for every
u\in U_{i}
and for every
i=1,\ldots,N
.

Proof.

The statement on the existence of a

-neighbourhood
\bar{U}_{i}
with the stated properties follows immediately from Lemma
4.6
. In fact, if such a neighbourhood does not exist, then there exists a sequence
\{u_{k}\}_{k}
in
\operatorname{Ext}(B)
, i.e.,

.
∎

4.2.
Rank-one matrix reconstruction by trace regularization

Let
H
be a separable Hilbert space with norm
\|\cdot\|_{H}
induced by an inner product
(\cdot,\cdot)_{H}
. In the following, to simplify notation, we will focus on infinite dimensional Hilbert spaces, the finite dimensional case, i.e.
H\simeq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, follows by the same arguments. Denote by
K(H)
the space of bounded, linear, compact and self-adjoint operators from
H
into itself which we equip with the standard operator norm. An operator
\mathcal{U}\in K(H)
is called positive, denoted by
\mathcal{U}\geq 0
, if

\displaystyle(h,\mathcal{U}h)_{H}\geq 0\quad\text{for all}\penalty\ h\in H\,.

Moreover, let
\{h_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
be an orthonormal basis (ONB) of

\mathcal{U}\in K(H)
we formally define its trace as

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}(\mathcal{U})=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}(h_{i},\mathcal{U}h_{i})_{H}\,.

An operator
\mathcal{U}\in K(H)
is called

if
\operatorname{Tr}(|\mathcal{U}|)<\infty
, where
|\mathcal{U}|
is the unique positive square root of
\mathcal{U}^{2}
, and

(HS) if
\operatorname{Tr}(\mathcal{U}^{2})<\infty

\|\mathcal{U}\|_{\text{HS}}=(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{U})^{1/2}_{\text{HS}}
induced by the inner product

\displaystyle(\mathcal{U}_{1},\mathcal{U}_{2})_{\text{HS}}=\operatorname{Tr}(\mathcal{U}_{1}\mathcal{U}_{2})\quad\text{for all}\penalty\ \mathcal{U}_{1},\mathcal{U}_{2}\in\operatorname{HS}(H)

\text{HS}(H)
. Moreover,
the space of all trace-class operators, denoted by

, forms a Banach space when equipped with the nuclear norm
\|\mathcal{U}\|_{\text{T}}=\operatorname{Tr}(|\mathcal{U}|)
. In this case, we also have
K(H)^{*}\simeq T(H)
where the duality pairing is realized by

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{U}_{1},\mathcal{U}_{2}\rangle=\operatorname{Tr}(\mathcal{U}_{1}\mathcal{U}_{2})\quad\text{for all}\penalty\ \mathcal{U}_{1}\in K(H),\penalty\ \mathcal{U}_{2}\in T(H)\,.

With these prerequisites, consider

(4.13)

\displaystyle\min_{\mathcal{U}\in T(H),\penalty\ \mathcal{U}\geq 0}\ \left[F(K\mathcal{U})+\beta\operatorname{Tr}(\mathcal{U})\right]

[

7
]
, since they are known to favour solutions with rank one. As before, we start by computing the extremal points of

\displaystyle B=\left\{\,\mathcal{U}\in T(H)\;|\;\beta\operatorname{Tr}(\mathcal{U})\leq 1,\penalty\ \mathcal{U}\geq 0\,\right\}\,.

For this purpose, given

by
\mathcal{U}_{h}h_{1}=(h,h_{1})_{H}h
for all

with
\operatorname{Tr}(\mathcal{U}_{h})=\|h\|^{2}_{H}
. Moreover, we recall that for every
\mathcal{U}\in K(H)
,
\mathcal{U}\geq 0
, there exists an ONB

as well as

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}h^{\mathcal{U}}_{i}=\sigma^{\mathcal{U}}_{i}h^{\mathcal{U}}_{i},\quad\mathcal{U}=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\sigma^{\mathcal{U}}_{i}h^{\mathcal{U}}_{i}\otimes h^{\mathcal{U}}_{i}\,.

Lemma 4.8

(4.14)

\ext(B)=\{\,\beta^{-1}h\otimes h\;|\;\|h\|_{H}=1\,\}\cup\{0\},

4.3.
Minimum effort problems

\displaystyle\min_{u\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)}J(u)\,,\,\,J(u):=F(Ku)+\alpha\|u\|_{\infty}\,,

\displaystyle\langle p,u\rangle=\int_{\Omega}p(x)u(x)\penalty\ \mathrm{d}x\quad\text{for all}\penalty\ p\in L^{1}(\Omega),\penalty\ u\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)\,.

-
(A3)

As in the previous examples, we now characterize the extremal points of the unit ball of the regularizer, i.e., the set

Subsequently, we use this characterization to describe minimizers of (

4.4.
Dynamic inverse problems regularized with the Benamou-Brenier energy

Consider the space
M(\Omega)
of Radon measures on the open bounded domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
n\geq 1
, and denote by

X:=[0,1]\times\Omega
the time-space domain.
The aim of dynamic inverse problems is to reconstruct a time-dependent curve of measures
t\mapsto\rho_{t}
with

\rho_{t}\in M(\Omega)
and
t\in[0,1]
, starting
from noisy ill-posed measurements
K\rho
, where
\rho=\mathrm{d}t\otimes\rho_{t}\in M(X)
and
K\colon M(X)\to Y
is a linear operator, with
Y
a Hilbert space. In
[
17
]
it has been proposed to reconstruct the dynamic data
\rho
by solving a variational inverse problem regularized with a coercive version of the Benamou-Brenier energy
J_{\alpha,\beta}
, allowing for a correlation between the measurements at different time instants (see also
[
22
]
). Such variational models have been applied, for instance, to dynamic cell imaging in PET
[
55
]
, to 4d image reconstruction in nanoscopy
[
22
]
and to particle image velocimetry methods
[
54
]
. A general variant of the problem considered in
[
17
]
can be formulated in our setting by considering

(4.27)

\displaystyle\min_{(\rho,m)\in\mathcal{M}}\ F(K\rho)+J_{\alpha,\beta}(\rho,m)\,,

where
\mathcal{M}:=M(X)\times M(X;\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
K:M(X)\rightarrow Y
is weak*-to-weak continuous and such that
(A3)
holds, while
F:Y\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a convex fidelity term satisfying
(A1)
.
The Benamou-Brenier energy
[
9
]
can be regarded as a dynamic version of optimal transport, and is defined as follows. Setting

\Phi\colon\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to[0,\infty]
as

(4.28)

\Phi(t,x):=\frac{|x|^{2}}{2t}\,\,\,\text{ if }\,t>0\,,\quad\Phi(t,x):=0\,\,\,\text{ if }\,t=|x|=0\,,

and
\Phi:=\infty
otherwise,
the Benamou-Brenier energy is defined for
(\rho,m)\in\mathcal{M}
by the formula

(4.29)

J(\rho,m):=\int_{X}\Phi\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}\rho}{\mathrm{d}\sigma},\frac{\mathrm{d}m}{\mathrm{d}\sigma}\right)\,\mathrm{d}\sigma\,,

where
\sigma\in M^{+}(X)
is an arbitrary measure such that
\rho,|m|\ll\sigma
. We refer the reader to
[
3
,
53
]
for more details. Following
[
17
]
, the regularizer
J_{\alpha,\beta}
is then defined by

(4.30)

J_{\alpha,\beta}(\rho,m):=\beta J(\rho,m)+\alpha\left\|\rho\right\|_{M(X)}+I_{\mathcal{D}}(\rho,m)\,,

where
\alpha,\beta>0
, and
\mathcal{D}
is the set of pairs
(\rho,m)\in\mathcal{M}
satisfying the continuity equation
\partial_{t}\rho+\div m=0
in
X
in the weak sense.
The functional
J_{\alpha,\beta}
is convex, weak* lower semicontinuous, positively one-homogeneous and satisfies
(A2)
, see
[
15
, Lemma 4]
. Denote by
B
the unit ball of
J_{\alpha,\beta}
. In
[
15
, Theorem 6]
it has been shown that

\operatorname{Ext}(B)=\{(0,0)\}\cup\mathfrak{C}_{\alpha,\beta}\,,

where
\mathfrak{C}_{\alpha,\beta}
is the set of measures concentrated on absolutely continuous curves in
\Omega
, i.e., pairs
(\rho_{\gamma},m_{\gamma})
satisfying

(4.31)

\displaystyle\rho_{\gamma}=a_{\gamma}\,\mathrm{d}t\otimes\delta_{\gamma(t)},\quad m_{\gamma}=\dot{\gamma}(t)\rho_{\gamma}\,,\quad a_{\gamma}=\left(\frac{\beta}{2}\int_{0}^{1}|\dot{\gamma}(t)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t+\alpha\right)^{-1}\,,

for some
\gamma\colon[0,1]\rightarrow\Omega
an absolutely continuous curve with weak derivative in
L^{2}
.
Therefore, it is possible to apply the FC-GCG method of Algorithm
1
for computing solutions to (
4.27
). In this setting the iterates are of the form
u_{k}=(\rho_{k},m_{k})
with

\rho_{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{k}}\lambda_{i}^{k}\rho_{\gamma_{i}^{k}}\,,\quad m_{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{k}}\lambda_{i}^{k}m_{\gamma_{i}^{k}}\,,

with
\lambda_{i}^{k}\geq 0
and
\gamma_{i}^{k}\colon[0,1]\to\Omega
absolutely continuous.
Moreover it can be shown, see
[
16
]
, that Step
2
in Algorithm
1
is equivalent to solving

(4.32)

\displaystyle\hat{\gamma}\in\argmax_{\gamma\colon[0,1]\to\Omega}\ a_{\gamma}\int_{0}^{1}p_{k}(t,\gamma(t))\,\mathrm{d}t\,,

where
p_{k}=-K_{*}\nabla F(K\rho_{k})\in C(X)
is the dual variable at the
k
-th iteration. The new point inserted is then
\widehat{v}_{k}=(\rho_{\hat{\gamma}},m_{\hat{\gamma}})
.
Thanks to the validity of Assumption
(A1)
-
(A3)
, Theorem
3.3
guarantees the sublinear convergence of Algorithm
1
. On the other hand, the verification of hypotheses
(B1)
-
(B5)
, necessary for ensuring fast convergence of Algorithm
1
, is non-trivial and is left to future work.
We remark that an implementable version of Algorithm
1
for solving (
4.27
) under specific choices of the fidelity term
F
and the operator
K
has been recently proposed in

[
16
]
(see also
[
38
]
). We refer to these papers for more details about the practical implementation and the modifications needed to deal with time dependent measurement operators.
Similarly to
[
16
]
, solving (
3.1
) amounts to computing an absolutely continuous curve
\gamma
by solving (
4.32
) at every iteration of the algorithm. This is a challenging non-concave variational problem in the space of curves. In
[
16
]
, the authors proposed to solve (
4.32
) by a multistart gradient descent approach in the space of curves. The initialization curves are chosen to be a linear interpolation of randomly generated points
\{x^{h}\}
in
\Omega
. Moreover several heuristic rules are employed to optimally select
\{x^{h}\}
and to reduce the computational time of the multistart gradient descent routine. Together with further acceleration strategies, this method is shown to be computationally feasible and very accurate for the task of tracking several dynamic sources in presence of high noise and severe spatial undersampling.
In
[
38
]
, the authors proposed to speed up the algorithm in
[
16
]
by considering inexact subproblems (

) and solving them using known algorithms for computing shortest paths on directed acyclic graphs.

, which we equip with the supremum norm

\|P\|_{C(\mathcal{B})}:=\max_{u\in\mathcal{B}}|P(u)|\,,

making it a Banach space. Following the definitions of

, we denote by

the
\sigma
-algebra of Borel sets on
\mathcal{B}
formed with respect to the topology induced by

. A finite Radon measure on

is a
\sigma
-additive map

. We call
\mu

\mu(\Pi)\subset[0,\infty)
. Given a finite Radon measure

is defined by

|\mu|(E):=\sup\left.\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}|\mu(E_{i})|\,\right|\,\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}E_{i}=E,\,\,E_{i}\in\Pi\,\text{ pairwise disjoint}\right\}\,,

for all
E\in\Pi
.
Note that
|\mu|
is always a positive finite Radon measure. The set of finite Radon measures over
\mathcal{B}
is a vector space denoted by

, which becomes a Banach space when endowed with the total variation norm

\|\mu\|_{M(\mathcal{B})}:=|\mu|(\mathcal{B})\,.

We recall that
M(\mathcal{B})
is the dual space of

with respect to
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{C(\mathcal{B})}
. The duality pairing will be denoted by
\llangle\cdot,\cdot\rrangle
.
The set
M^{+}(\mathcal{B})
of all positive finite Radon measures on
\mathcal{B}
forms a weak* closed cone. For
\mu\in M(\mathcal{B})
and

, we say that
\mu
is concentrated on

if
\mu(\mathcal{B}\setminus E)=0
. For a set

we define the restriction of
\mu
to

as the measure
\mu\mathbin{\vrule height=6.88889pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}E^{\prime}
such that
\mu\mathbin{\vrule height=6.88889pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}E^{\prime}(E)=\mu(E^{\prime}\cap E)
for all

. Finally, the support of a measure

, denoted by

such that

for all neighbourhoods

of

.

5.2.
An equivalent problem

We require the following definition.

Definition 5.1
.

We say that a measure
\mu\in M^{+}(\mathcal{B})

represents

if

(5.2)

\langle p,u\rangle=\int_{\mathcal{B}}\langle p,v\rangle\,\mathrm{d}\mu(v)\,,\,\,\,\,\text{ for all }\,\,\,p\in\mathcal{C}\,.

An element

such that (

of

in
\mathcal{B}
.

It turns out that each

is the barycenter of at least one measure
\mu\in M^{+}(\mathcal{B})
.

Proposition 5.2
.

There exists a surjective linear map
\mathcal{I}\colon M^{+}(\mathcal{B})\to\operatorname{dom}(\mathcal{G})
such that

\langle p,\mathcal{I}(\mu)\rangle=\int_{\mathcal{B}}\langle p,v\rangle\penalty\ \mathrm{d}\mu(v)\,,\,\,\,\,\text{ for all }\,\,\,p\in\mathcal{C}\,.

In particular,
\mu
represents
\mathcal{I}(\mu)
in the sense of (

) and it holds:

i)

For any
\mu\in M^{+}(\mathcal{B})
we have
\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{I}(\mu))\leq\left\|\mu\right\|_{M(\mathcal{B})}
.

ii)

For any

there exists

concentrated on

such that

u=\mathcal{I}(\mu)\quad\text{ and }\quad\mathcal{G}(u)=\left\|\mu\right\|_{M(\mathcal{B})}.

iii)

\mathcal{I}
is weak*-to-weak* continuous.

For a proof of the above statement we refer the reader to Appendix

. Here we only mention that existence of the map

is easily obtained by the theory of weak* integration, while surjectivity is a consequence of the classic Choquet’s Theorem
C.1

Thus, instead of solving (

M

) directly we can equivalently determine a measure
\bar{\mu}\in M^{+}(\mathcal{B})
which represents any of its minimizers. Again, this can be done by solving a suitable minimization problem. To make this idea more rigorous we argue the existence
of a linear continuous operator
\mathcal{K}\colon M(\mathcal{B})\to Y
which agrees with

on measures representing points of
\mathcal{M}
. Again, we postpone the proof of such statement to Appendix

Proposition 5.3
.

There exists a linear continuous operator
\mathcal{K}\colon M(\mathcal{B})\to Y
such that

(5.4)

(\mathcal{K}\mu,y)_{Y}=\int_{\mathcal{B}}(Kv,y)_{Y}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(v)\,,\,\,\,\,\text{ for all }\,\,\,y\in Y\,,\,\,\mu\in M(\mathcal{B})\,.

Moreover
\mathcal{K}
satisfies the following properties:

i)

The norm of
\mathcal{K}
is such that

\left\|\mathcal{K}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}(M(\mathcal{B}),Y)}\leq C\|K\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{M},Y)}\,,\quad C:=\sup_{v\in\mathcal{B}}\,\lVert v\rVert_{\mathcal{M}}\,.

ii)

For every
\mu\in M^{+}(\mathcal{B})
representing
u\in\mathcal{M}
, there holds
\mathcal{K}\mu=Ku
.

iii)

\mathcal{K}
is weak*-to-strong continuous.

iv)

\mathcal{K}
is the adjoint operator of
\mathcal{K}_{*}\colon Y\to C(\mathcal{B})
, where

[\mathcal{K}_{*}y](v):=\langle K_{*}y,v\rangle\quad\text{ for all }\quad v\in\mathcal{B}\,.

If in addition
Y
is separable, then (
5.4
) holds in the strong sense, that is,

(5.5)

\mathcal{K}\mu=\int_{\mathcal{B}}Kv\,\mathrm{d}\mu(v)\,,

for all
\mu\in M(\mathcal{B})
, where the right hand side integral is in the Bochner sense.

We are now in position to investigate the announced equivalence of (

P
M

) and the sparse minimization problem (

P

M
+

). To this end, define

\displaystyle j(\mu):=F(\mathcal{K}\mu)+\|\mu\|_{M(\mathcal{B})}\,,

where

is the operator from Proposition

) and (

M
+

Theorem 5.4
.

The functional

is weak* lower semicontinuous, has weak* compact sublevels and (

+

) admits at least a solution. In addition, minimizers of (

M

) and (

P

The final part of the statement follows from (

Proposition 5.5

Theorem 5.6

A measure

Remark 6.3

In the next section, devoted to the proof of the linear convergence of Algorithm

Remark 6.4
.

The proof strategy described above is inspired by

There exists

\displaystyle P_{k}(v)\leq 1-\sigma/2\quad\text{ for all }\,\,v\in\mathcal{B}\setminus\bigcup^{N}_{i=1}\bar{U}_{i}\,,

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{k}\subset\bigcup^{N}_{i=1}U_{i}\,,

Proof.

As

uniformly by

such that, for all

\displaystyle=\bar{P}(v)+P_{k}(v)-\bar{P}(v)

where we used (

This proves (

by Proposition

Conclusions

We have introduced a fully-corrective generalized conditional gradient method (FC-GCG) to solve a class of non-smooth minimization problems in Banach spaces. For such algorithm we provided a global sublinear rate of convergence under the mild Assumptions
(A1)
-
(A3)
, as well as a local linear convergence rate under the Assumptions
(B1)
-
(B5)
. Several examples of applications were considered, showing that it is possible to formulate a problem-dependent natural set of assumptions which is easy to verify and implies Assumptions
(B1)
-
(B5)
, thus ensuring linear convergence of our method. We demonstrate numerically the fast convergence for our first example, showing that, compared to standard conditional gradient methods (GCG), our algorithm exhibits a vastly improved rate of convergence. Additionally, we have discussed in details the computational burden of each presented example, and we have described viable strategies to solve the (partially) linearized problem (
1.1
).

As in the euclidean case of
[
52
]
, we believe that all results in the present paper are transferable to non-smooth regularizers of the form
\Phi(\mathcal{G}(\cdot))
where
\mathcal{G}
is as in Assumptions
(A1)
-
(A3)
, and
\Phi
is a suitable convex, monotone increasing function, e.g.,
\Phi(\mathcal{G}(u))=(1/2)\mathcal{G}(u)^{2}
. This generalization was omitted in the present paper for the sake of readability.

Lemma A.1
.

Let
p\in\mathcal{C}
be given. Then, there exists
\bar{v}\in\operatorname{Ext}(B)
with

\displaystyle\langle p,\bar{v}\rangle=\max_{v\in\mathcal{B}}\,\langle p,v\rangle=\max_{v\in B}\,\langle p,v\rangle\,.

Proof.

In order to show the claimed result we first prove that the maximization problem

(A.1)

\max_{v\in B}\,\langle p,v\rangle

admits a solution
\bar{v}\in\operatorname{Ext}(B)
. This statement is classical, but for the reader’s convenience we produce a proof. Let
v^{*}
be a maximizer of (
A.1
), which exists since
v\mapsto\langle p,v\rangle
is weak* continuous and
B
is weak* compact. Consider the set

H:=\{\,v\in B\;|\;\langle p,v\rangle=\langle p,v^{*}\rangle\,\}
. Then
\operatorname{Ext}(H)\neq\emptyset
by Krein-Milman’s Theorem, since
H\subset B
is convex, non-empty, weak* closed and thus weak* compact. Thus, let
\bar{v}\in\ext(H)
be given. We claim that
\bar{v}\in\operatorname{Ext}(B)
. Indeed, assume that
\bar{v}=sv_{1}+(1-s)v_{2}
for
v_{1},v_{2}\in B
,
s\in(0,1)
. Notice that
v_{1},v_{2}\in H
, otherwise we would have
\langle p,\bar{v}\rangle<\langle p,v^{*}\rangle
, contradicting the maximality of
\bar{v}
. As
\bar{v}\in\operatorname{Ext}(H)
we then have
\bar{v}=v_{1}=v_{2}
, showing that
\bar{v}\in\operatorname{Ext}(B)
. The claimed statement now follows from
\operatorname{Ext}(B)\subset\mathcal{B}\subset B
ending the proof.
∎

Proposition A.2
.

Let
\mathcal{A}^{u,+}_{k}=\{u^{k}_{i}\}^{N^{+}_{k}}_{i=1}\subset\ext(B)
be given.
If
\widehat{\lambda}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N^{+}_{k}}
solves (
3.4
), then

\widehat{u}=\sum^{N^{+}_{k}}_{i=1}\widehat{\lambda}_{i}u^{k}_{i}

solves (
3.2
). Conversely, if
\widehat{u}
solves (
3.2
), then there exists a solution
\widehat{\lambda}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N^{+}_{k}}
to (
3.4
) such that

\widehat{u}=\sum^{N^{+}_{k}}_{i=1}\widehat{\lambda}_{i}u^{k}_{i}
.

Proof.

Let
\widehat{\lambda}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N^{+}_{k}}
be a solution to (
3.4
). Set
\widehat{u}=\sum^{N^{+}_{k}}_{i=1}\widehat{\lambda}_{i}u^{k}_{i}
and fix an arbitrary
u\in\operatorname{cone}(\mathcal{A}^{u,+}_{k})
. Then there exists
\lambda^{u}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N^{+}_{k}}
such that

u=\sum^{N^{+}_{k}}_{i=1}\lambda^{u}_{i}u^{k}_{i}
with
\sum^{N^{+}_{k}}_{i=1}\lambda^{u}_{i}=\kappa_{\mathcal{A}^{+}_{k}}(u)
.
We estimate

\displaystyle F(K\widehat{u})+\kappa_{\mathcal{A}^{+}_{k}}(\widehat{u})

\displaystyle\leq F\left(\sum^{N^{+}_{k}}_{i=1}\widehat{\lambda}_{i}Ku^{k}_{i}\right)+\sum^{N^{+}_{k}}_{i=1}\widehat{\lambda}_{i}

\displaystyle\leq F\left(\sum^{N^{+}_{k}}_{i=1}\lambda^{u}_{i}Ku^{k}_{i}\right)+\sum^{N^{+}_{k}}_{i=1}\lambda^{u}_{i}=F(Ku)+\kappa_{\mathcal{A}^{+}_{k}}(u)\,,

showing that
\widehat{u}
is a solution to (
3.2
).
Conversely, let
\widehat{u}
be a minimizer to (
3.2
). Since
\widehat{u}\in\operatorname{cone}(\mathcal{A}^{u,+}_{k})

\widehat{\lambda}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N^{+}_{k}}

\widehat{u}=\sum^{N^{+}_{k}}_{i=1}\widehat{\lambda}_{i}u^{k}_{i}

\sum^{N^{+}_{k}}_{i=1}\widehat{\lambda}_{i}=\kappa_{\mathcal{A}^{u,+}_{k}}(\widehat{u})

\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N^{+}_{k}}

\widehat{u}

\displaystyle F\left(\sum^{N^{+}_{k}}_{i=1}\widehat{\lambda}_{i}Ku^{k}_{i}\right)+\sum^{N^{+}_{k}}_{i=1}\widehat{\lambda}_{i}

\displaystyle\leq F(Ku^{\lambda})+\kappa_{\mathcal{A}^{u,+}_{k}}(u^{\lambda})

\displaystyle\leq F\left(\sum^{N^{+}_{k}}_{i=1}\lambda_{i}Ku^{k}_{i}\right)+\sum^{N^{+}_{k}}_{i=1}\lambda_{i}\,.

Thus,
\widehat{\lambda}
is a minimizer of (
3.4
).
∎
