Correlation length in random MPS and PEPS
Cécilia Lancien, David Pérez-García

Abstract
. Tensor network states are used extensively as a mathematically convenient description of physically relevant states of many-body quantum systems. Those built on regular lattices, i.e. matrix product states (MPS) in dimension 1 1 and projected entangled pair states (PEPS) in dimension 2 2 or higher, are of particular interest in condensed matter physics. The general goal of this work is to characterize which features of MPS and PEPS are generic and which are, on the contrary, exceptional. This problem can be rephrased as follows: given an MPS or PEPS sampled at random, what are the features that it displays with either high or low probability? One property which we are particularly interested in is that of having either rapidly decaying or long-range correlations. In a nutshell, our main result is that translation-invariant MPS and PEPS typically exhibit exponential decay of correlations at a high rate. We have two distinct ways of getting to this conclusion, depending on the dimensional regime under consideration. Both yield intermediate results which are of independent interest, namely: the parent Hamiltonian and the transfer operator of such MPS and PEPS typically have a large spectral gap. In all these statements, our aim is to get a quantitative estimate of the considered quantity (generic correlation length or spectral gap), which has the best possible dependency on the physical and bond dimensions of the random MPS or PEPS.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Motivations

One of the main practical problems when dealing with many-body quantum systems is the curse of dimensionality: a system composed of
N

d
-dimensional particles has dimension
d^{N}
, a number of degrees of freedom too large to handle in most computations as soon as more than a few particles are involved. However, it is known that, in many contexts, physically relevant states of many-body quantum systems are actually well approximated by states living in a very small subset of the whole exponentially large state space, namely the one of tensor network states. Intuitively, these should be a mathematically convenient way of representing states of systems composed of many sub-systems having a certain geometry and subject to interactions respecting this geometry.

Tensor network states are constructed as follows: Given a non-oriented graph
G
with vertex set
V
and edge set

|\hat{\chi}_{G}\rangle\in(\mathbf{C}^{d})^{\otimes|V|}\otimes(\mathbf{C}^{D})^{\otimes 2|E|}
. Then, we contract together the indices of

corresponding to a same edge to obtain a tensor
|\chi_{G}\rangle\in(\mathbf{C}^{d})^{\otimes|V|}

Often the underlying graph
G
is taken to be a regular lattice. The corresponding tensor network state
|\chi_{G}\rangle
is then usually referred to as a matrix product state (MPS) in dimension
1
and a projected entangled pair state (PEPS) in dimension
2
or higher. MPS and PEPS are especially interesting in the context of condensed matter physics. Indeed, it is rigorously proven in some cases and conjectured in others that they are good approximations of ground states of gapped local many-body Hamiltonians
[
28
,
29
,
3
,
37
]
. They are therefore used (amongst other) as Ansatz in ground energy computations, allowing for optimization over a tractable number of parameters, even when a large number of particles are involved. They have also been used very successfully to obtain analytical results concerning the understanding and classification of quantum phases of matter (see
[
17
]
for a recent review on this topic).

This brings us to the general problem we are interested in, which is, very broadly speaking, the following: are common beliefs about MPS and PEPS at least true generically? Or to rephrase it a bit more precisely: which features of MPS and PEPS are typical and which are exceptional? The kind of features that we have in mind include: being the ground state of a parent Hamiltonian which is either gapped or gapless, exhibiting either rapidly decaying or long-range correlations etc. One possible route to tackle this question is to sample MPS and PEPS at random (in a way which should be as physically relevant as possible) and study what are the characteristics that these generically display.

Note that random tensor network states have already been successfully studied in the context of holography. Indeed, tensor network states also provide a natural framework for studying AdS/CFT correspondence. And it turns out that random ones actually reproduce several conjectured properties in this theory
[
34
]
(see also
[
32
]
for a pioneer work in this direction). Finally, let us point out that the formalism of tensor networks has also been recently applied in several areas beyond quantum physics: machine learning
[
14
,
15
]
, PDEs
[
7
,
21
]
, finance
[
42
]
or signal processing
[
21
]
. The underlying reason is again the fact that tensor networks can parametrize efficiently highly complex multidimensional tensors.

In this work, we will be looking at a Gaussian model of random tensor network states (described rigorously below). The main reason for this is that it is arguably the first model that one should look at. Indeed, the most important take-home message from standard random matrix theory is that all ‘sufficiently well-behaved’ random matrix models exhibit features which are somehow similar to that of their Gaussian counterpart. It is thus not unreasonable to conjecture that the same should be true for at least some of the properties of random tensor network states that we study. The Gaussian setting has the advantage of allowing for the use of powerful tools to compute averages explicitly and of often displaying provably strong concentration around these averages. So it really serves as a benchmark for other settings, where we might expect averages to be of the same order of magnitude (but where concentration is usually much weaker). Even in terms of proof techniques, it is often the case that results on a given random matrix/tensor model are established by first showing that it can be suitably compared to a Gaussian model, and then using the known result for the latter. Hence, as a first investigation of the question of typical spectral gaps and correlation lengths in random tensor network states, it does make sense to start with a Gaussian model.

An important property of MPS and PEPS is injectivity, and more generally normality. Let us start by recalling the definition of these terms.

Definition 1.1

(Injectivity and normality)
.

An MPS, resp. a PEPS, is called injective if its
1
-site tensor, viewed as a linear map from the bond space to the physical space, i.e. from
(\mathbf{C}^{D})^{\otimes 2}

\mathbf{C}^{d}

Our random MPS and PEPS, with

Proof.

The first general statement follows from

The second more specific statement is immediate once noticed that the

\widetilde{\chi}^{1}=\sum_{x=1}^{d}\sum_{l,r=1}^{D}g_{xlr}|x\rangle\!\langle lr|\ \ \text{and}\ \ \widetilde{\chi}_{1}^{1}=\sum_{x=1}^{d}\sum_{l,r,a,b=1}^{D}g_{xlrab}|x\rangle\!\langle lrab|\,.

Hence they are almost surely injective as soon as their output dimension is larger than their input dimension.

1.3.
Summary of our main results and connections with previous works

As already said, our goal in this work is to study what are the features that our models of random translation-invariant MPS and PEPS, with
1
-site random tensors respectively defined by equations (
1
) and (
3
), typically exhibit. We are particularly interested in understanding what is their typical correlation length. We try to tackle this question through two distinct strategies. Both ultimately boil down to determining what is the typical spectral gap of a random operator associated to our random tensor network states: either their parent Hamiltonian or their transfer operator. The first approach, through the study of the parent Hamiltonian has the advantage of yielding results which are true for any system size for both MPS and PEPS, but the disadvantage of applying only to injective MPS and PEPS. On the contrary, the approach through the transfer operator has the advantage of being valid beyond the injectivity regime, but the disadvantage of giving results which depend on the system size in the PEPS case. So they somehow complement each other.

The overall merit of our various results could perhaps be summarized as follows: In the
1
-dimensional case, none of them is qualitatively surprising. What is interesting is that we are able to prove quantitative lower bounds on the generic spectral gap of the random parent Hamiltonian and transfer operator, in terms of the physical and bond dimensions (which translate into quantitative upper bounds on the generic correlation length of the random MPS). In the
2
-dimensional case though, nothing was known (and there was not even a consensus on what should be expected). So already the qualitative result that, in some range of physical and bond dimensions, random PEPS generically exhibit exponential decay of correlation is relevant in its own.

First, in Section
2
, we place ourselves in the injectivity regime of our random tensor networks, i.e.
d>D^{2}
in the case of MPS and
d>D^{4}
in the case of PEPS, where it makes sense to talk about their canonical parent Hamiltonian. The latter is precisely defined in Section
2
. For now, let us just say that it is a
2
-local (nearest-neighbour interaction) Hamiltonian which is frustration-free and has the MPS or PEPS as unique ground state. We are able to show that, at least in a ‘super-injectivity’ regime, this parent Hamiltonian has with high probability a large spectral gap. More precisely, we obtain the following result, which appears as Theorems
2.12
(for the case of MPS) and
2.19
(for the case of PEPS).

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
d,D,N\in\mathbf{N}
. Denote by
H_{MPS}
, resp.
H_{PEPS}
, the parent Hamiltonian of our random MPS, resp. PEPS. If
d\geqslant D^{10+\epsilon}
for some
\epsilon>0
, then

\mathbf{P}\left(\Delta\left(H_{MPS}\right)\geqslant 1-\frac{C}{D^{\epsilon/2}}\right)\geqslant 1-e^{-cD^{2}}\,,

and if
d\geqslant D^{26+\epsilon}
for some
\epsilon>0
, then

\mathbf{P}\left(\Delta\left(H_{PEPS}\right)\geqslant 1-\frac{C}{D^{\epsilon/2}}\right)\geqslant 1-e^{-cD^{4}}\,,

where
C,c>0
are universal constants.

In words, Theorem
1.3
tells us the following: as
d,D
grow, with
d>D^{10}
, resp.
d>D^{26}
, it holds that, with probability going to
1
, the spectral gap of
H_{MPS}
, resp.
H_{PEPS}
, is going to (at least)
1
. Let us point out immediately that the constraints on the scaling of
d
with respect to
D
are likely to be far from optimal. It is indeed clear when looking at the whole reasoning in Section
2
that, in several steps, we use general bounds that might be rough in our particular case. It seems however that getting better ones could require a very careful analysis, that we do not pursue here. We build our whole reasoning upon the well-known fact that, in some range of parameters, Wishart matrices (suitably rescaled) can be approximated by the identity in a strong sense.

Let us also mention that the question of whether random local Hamiltonians are generically gapped or gapless has recently been studied in
[
41
]
and
[
38
]
, with a quite different perspective than ours. In both works, the local terms composing the Hamiltonian are picked at random, while what we pick at random is the ground state of the Hamiltonian. In
[
41
]
local terms are sampled independently, and it is shown that the obtained Hamiltonian is gapless with probability
1
in the thermodynamic limit. In
[
38
]
, on the contrary, only one local term is sampled and repeated, hence imposing translation-invariance of the obtained Hamiltonian, which is shown to be gapped with strictly positive probability in the thermodynamic limit. This latter setting is in fact very close to ours: in addition to being translation-invariant, the random local Hamiltonian which is studied is frustration-free (a characteristics that the parent Hamiltonian of an MPS or PEPS has by definition). And it indeed leads to a similar conclusion.

As a consequence of Theorem
1.3
we have that, in this same regime, our random MPS and PEPS exhibit, with probability going to
1
as
d,D
grow, exponential decay of correlations. This result appears as Theorems
3.1
and
3.2
in Section
3
. Even though already interesting, it still has two weaknesses. First of all, it only applies to the injectivity regime. Indeed, even if the constraints
d>D^{10}
for MPS and
d>D^{26}
for PEPS could probably be improved, there is no way that we can say anything about the range
d<D^{2}
for MPS and
d<D^{4}
for PEPS via this approach (which consists in showing exponential decay of correlations in a tensor network state through showing that its parent Hamiltonian is gapped). Second of all, it cannot give anything stronger than a constant correlation length. In our specific case, we can actually improve the latter point by using the powerful recent result of
[
26
]
. Indeed, we have more than a lower bound on the typical spectral gap of the parent Hamiltonian, namely an upper bound on the typical commutator of the local terms composing it. And this enables us to prove that, in fact, the correlation length typically decays as
1/\log D
(see Theorems
3.5
and
3.8
in Section
3
).

However, to remedy the first problem it is necessary to look for another possible way to prove typical exponential decay of correlations in our random MPS and PEPS. A very natural one is through showing that their associated transfer operator is typically gapped, a result which is moreover of independent interest. This is what we do in Section
4
, ultimately obtaining Theorems
4.15
(for the case of MPS) and
4.23
(for the case of PEPS), which are summarized below.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
d,D\in\mathbf{N}
. Denote by
T_{MPS}
the transfer operator of our random MPS. Then,

\mathbf{P}\left(\Delta\left(T_{MPS}\right)\geqslant 1-\frac{C}{d^{1/2}}\right)\geqslant 1-e^{-cD}\,,

where
C,c>0
are universal constants.

Let
d,D,N\in\mathbf{N}
. Denote by
T_{PEPS}
the transfer operator of our random PEPS. If
d\simeq N^{\alpha}
and
D\simeq N^{\beta}
with
\alpha>8
and
(\alpha+1)/3<\beta<(\alpha-2)/2
, then

\mathbf{P}\left(\Delta\left(T_{PEPS}\right)\geqslant 1-\frac{C}{N^{\alpha/2-\beta-1}}\right)\geqslant 1-e^{-cN^{3\beta-\alpha}}\,,

where
C,c>0
are universal constants.

Let us comment first on the MPS case, where the result is valid for any
d
and
D
. The spectral gap question that we investigate has already been studied in
[
30
,
44
]
and
[
23
]
on different random models (not all of them motivated by the study of random MPS), involving unitary and truncated unitary rather than Gaussian operators. In these three pieces of work the emphasis is put on having as tight as possible average results, while we mostly care about the order of magnitude but want to show that it is generic. It however remains that all approaches yield one similar result, namely an expected spectral gap of the considered random transfer operator larger than
1-C/\sqrt{d}
. Our proof strategy, to first lower bound the expected spectral gap of
T_{MPS}
, follows closely that of
[
44
]
and
[
23
]
. An additional difficulty in our case comes from the fact that we are dealing with a non-normal random matrix model, which makes the analysis of its spectrum significantly more delicate. In order to then show that this lower bound is actually also typical we make use of a slightly refined version of the standard Gaussian concentration inequality.

Concerning the PEPS case, we see that we are able to prove that the transfer operator is generically gapped only in the regime where
d,D
grow polynomially with
N
. While this is to be expected for
D
, it seems much less natural for
d
though. This scaling can however easily be enforced by a so-called blocking procedure, namely: We start from a square lattice with
\underline{N}\times\underline{N}
sites, where
\underline{N}:=N\sqrt{\log N}
, each having physical dimension
\underline{d}
and bond dimension
\underline{D}:=\underline{D}^{\prime\sqrt{\log N}}
. We then redefine
1
site as being a square of
\sqrt{\log N}\times\sqrt{\log N}
sites. We thus obtain a square lattice with
N\times N
sites, each having physical dimension
d:=\underline{d}^{\log N}
and bond dimension
D:=\underline{D}^{\sqrt{\log N}}=\underline{D}^{\prime\log N}
. Hence indeed, setting
\alpha:=\log\underline{d}
and
\beta:=\log\underline{D}^{\prime}
, we have
d=N^{\alpha}
and
D=N^{\beta}
. Finally, for the parameters
\alpha,\beta
to be in the valid range, we just have to impose on the parameters
\underline{d},\underline{D}^{\prime}
that they satisfy
\underline{d}>e^{8}
and
(e\underline{d})^{1/3}<\underline{D}^{\prime}<\underline{d}^{1/2}/e
. Let us emphasize here that the proof techniques to prove a lower bound on the typical spectral gap of
T_{PEPS}
are, as far as we are aware of, essentially new. The basic idea is some kind of recursion procedure that uses the MPS results as building blocks.

As a quite straightforward consequence of Theorem
1.4
we obtain that our random MPS and PEPS typically exhibit exponential decay of correlations at a provably high rate, and in a dimension regime that goes beyond the injectivity one. This result appears as Theorems
5.7
and
5.9
in Section
5
.

Theorem
1.4
has several other implications, some of which are studied in Section
6
. In particular, we draw the path towards constructions of random quantum expanders and random dissipative evolutions.

Let us make some final comments on the extra technical difficulties when trying to extend
1
-dimensional results to
2
-dimensional ones. Our first approach, based on looking at the parent Hamiltonian, has the great advantage of not being that much more complicated for PEPS than for MPS. The reason behind this is that, in both cases, the terms composing the parent Hamiltonian have the same locality (namely they just involve
2
sites). The only thing that makes the PEPS case slightly more subtle is that the
4
terms which act non-trivially on a given site are of
2
different kinds (
2
identical ‘horizontal’ terms and
2
identical ‘vertical’ terms), while in the MPS case there are only
2
identical terms which act non-trivially on a given site. On the other hand, our second approach, based on looking at the transfer operator, is way more difficult to go through for PEPS than for MPS. Indeed, the transfer operator of a PEPS, contrary to that of an MPS, depends on the system size (since it is not constructed from its
1
-site tensor but from its
N
-site column tensor). Hence, while in the MPS case any statement about the transfer operator is automatically valid for any system size, this is not true anymore in the PEPS case.

1.4.
A few key results in Gaussian concentration

Let us conclude this introductory part with two technical results that we will be using in multiple occasions throughout this paper. The first one is the celebrated concentration inequality for Lipschitz functions on Gaussian space, which was proved independently in
[
9
]
and
[
45
]
. The second one is a local version of this concentration inequality, which is useful when the considered function does not have a small Lipschitz constant on the whole Gaussian space but only on a large measure subset, and which was established in
[

.

Theorem 1.5

(Gaussian concentration inequality, global version
[
9
,
45
]
)
.

Let
f:\mathbf{C}^{n}\longrightarrow\mathbf{R}
be
L
-Lipschitz (with respect to the Euclidean norm). For
g\in\mathbf{C}^{n}
a Gaussian vector with mean
0
and variance
\sigma^{2}
, we have

\forall\ \epsilon>0,\ \mathbf{P}(f(g)\gtrless\E f\pm\epsilon)\leqslant e^{-\epsilon^{2}/\sigma^{2}L^{2}}\,.

Theorem 1.6

(Gaussian concentration inequality, local version
[
6
]
)
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbf{C}^{n}
and let
f:\mathbf{C}^{n}\longrightarrow\mathbf{R}
be
L
-Lipschitz on
\Omega
(with respect to the Euclidean norm). For
g\in\mathbf{C}^{n}
a Gaussian vector with mean
0
and variance
\sigma^{2}
, we have

\forall\ \epsilon>0,\ \mathbf{P}(f(g)\gtrless\E f\pm\epsilon)\leqslant e^{-\epsilon^{2}/\sigma^{2}L^{2}}+\mathbf{P}(g\notin\Omega)\,.

Finally, we will also use several times the fact that (suitably rescaled) large Wishart matrices of large enough parameter are with high probability close to the identity. More precisely, we will need the result below, which can be found in
[
5
, Proposition 6.33]
.

Theorem 1.7

(Strong convergence of Wishart matrices
[
5
]
)
.

Fix

. Let

be an

Wishart matrix of parameter

where

\mathbf{P}\left(\left\|\frac{1}{s}W-\mathrm{Id}\right\|_{\infty}>6\sqrt{\frac{n}{s}}\right)\leqslant 2e^{-n/4}\,.

2.
Typical spectral gap of the parent Hamiltonian of random MPS and PEPS

In this section we want to show that, in the dimensional regime where our random MPS and PEPS are injective, their canonical parent Hamiltonians are typically gapped. We are actually only able to establish this in a ‘super-injective’ regime, which might be an artefact of our proof techniques. We first study the MPS case in Section
2.1
and then follow step by step the same reasoning for the PEPS case in Section
2.2
. The final results appear as Theorems
2.12
and
2.19
respectively.

Let us just briefly emphasize again a point that we have already raised in the introduction. The corollaries of these results in terms of typical correlation length (see Section
3
) are very powerful in the PEPS case but not as much as they could be in the MPS case. Indeed, for the latter, our second approach through characterizing the typical spectral gap of the transfer operator (see Section
4
) yields stronger typical correlation length consequences (see Section
5
). The interest of nevertheless carrying through the MPS analysis here is two-fold: First, the result on the typical spectral gap of the parent Hamiltonian is interesting in its own. Second, the overall reasoning can really be seen as a ‘warm up’ before getting into the slightly more intricate to follow, but in the end entirely analogous, PEPS reasoning.

Before we move on, let us just state here one quite straightforward fact concerning the maximum norm increase of a matrix under realignment, which we will make use of several times later on. We recall that the realignment (in the canonical tensor orthonormal bases) of an
nm\times nm
matrix
M
is the
n^{2}\times m^{2}
matrix
\mathcal{R}(M)
defined by

\forall\ 1\leqslant i,j\leqslant n,\ 1\leqslant k,l\leqslant m,\ \mathcal{R}(M)_{ij,kl}:=M_{ik,jl}\,.

Fact 2.1
.

Let
n,m\in\mathbf{N}
. For any
nm\times nm
matrix
M
,

\|\mathcal{R}(M)\|_{\infty}\leqslant\min(n,m)\|M\|_{\infty}\,.

2.1.
The case of MPS

We assume here that
d>D^{2}
, so that our random MPS
|\chi^{N}\rangle
is injective with probability
1
(see Fact
1.2
). We know from
[
18
]
that, in this case, there exists a canonical way of constructing a
2
-local frustration-free Hamiltonian on
(\mathbf{C}^{d})^{\otimes N}
whose unique ground state is
|\chi^{N}\rangle
, with ground energy
0
. We call it the parent Hamiltonian of
|\chi^{N}\rangle
and denote it by
H_{\chi}
. We now want to show that this random Hamiltonian
H_{\chi}
typically has a large (lower) spectral gap
\Delta(H_{\chi})
. Note that since the smallest eigenvalue of
H_{\chi}
is
0
,
\Delta(H_{\chi})
is actually nothing else than the second smallest eigenvalue of
H_{\chi}
.

Let us first recall how the Hamiltonian
H_{\chi}
is constructed. Define
V_{\chi}\subset\mathbf{C}^{d}\otimes\mathbf{C}^{d}
as

V_{\chi}:=\mathrm{Span}\left\{\sum_{x_{1},x_{2}=1}^{d}\Tr(G_{x_{1}}G_{x_{2}}M)|x_{1}x_{2}\rangle:M\ D\times D\ \text{matrix}\right\}\,,

where the
G_{x}
’s are the
D\times D
matrices appearing in equation (
2
) defining the transfer operator
T
associated to
|\chi\rangle
.
Equivalently,

(6)

V_{\chi}:=\mathrm{Span}\left\{|\chi_{\upsilon}\rangle:|\upsilon\rangle\in\mathbf{C}^{D}\otimes\mathbf{C}^{D}\right\}\,,

where
|\chi_{\upsilon}\rangle\in\mathbf{C}^{d}\otimes\mathbf{C}^{d}
is the
2
-site MPS having
|\chi\rangle
as
1
-site tensor and
|\upsilon\rangle
as boundary condition.
By construction we always have
\mathrm{dim}(V_{\chi})\leqslant D^{2}<d^{2}
, so that
\mathrm{dim}(V_{\chi}^{\perp})\geqslant d^{2}-D^{2}>0
. And in our case we actually have
\mathrm{dim}(V_{\chi})=D^{2}
with probability
1
. Denoting by
\Pi
the projector on
V_{\chi}
, the parent Hamiltonian
H_{\chi}
of
|\chi^{N}\rangle
is then defined as

(7)

H_{\chi}:=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Pi^{\perp}_{i,i+1}\otimes\mathrm{Id}_{1,\ldots,i-1,i+2,\ldots,N}\,.

2.1.1.
Approximating the local ground space projectors

The following operator
W
will appear repeatedly in our subsequent computations:
W:\mathbf{C}^{D}\otimes\mathbf{C}^{D}\longrightarrow\mathbf{C}^{D}\otimes\mathbf{C}^{D}

What will be crucial for us is that, in the range
d>D^{2}
that we are interested in,
W
(suitably renormalized) is generically close to the identity. Indeed, as immediate corollary of Theorem
1.7
(applied with
n=D^{2}
and
s=d
) we have the result below.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
d\geqslant D^{2\tau}
, for some
\tau>1
. Then, there exist universal constants
c,C>0
such that

\mathbf{P}\left(\left\|DW-\mathrm{Id}\right\|_{\infty}\leqslant\frac{C}{D^{\tau-1}}\right)\geqslant 1-e^{-cD^{2}}\,.

Let us now define the operators
Q,P,M
as
Q:\mathbf{C}^{D}\otimes\mathbf{C}^{D}\longrightarrow\mathbf{C}^{d}\otimes\mathbf{C}^{d}
P=QQ^{*}:\mathbf{C}^{d}\otimes\mathbf{C}^{d}\longrightarrow\mathbf{C}^{d}\otimes\mathbf{C}^{d}
M=Q^{*}Q:\mathbf{C}^{D}\otimes\mathbf{C}^{D}\longrightarrow\mathbf{C}^{D}\otimes\mathbf{C}^{D}

We will first show that, in the range
d>D^{6}
, just as
W
,
M
(suitably renormalized) is close to the identity.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
d\geqslant D^{2\tau}
, for some
\tau>3
. Then, there exist universal constants
c,C>0
such that

\mathbf{P}\left(\|DM-\mathrm{Id}\|_{\infty}\leqslant\frac{C}{D^{\tau-3}}\right)\geqslant 1-e^{-cD^{2}}\,.

Proof.

Observe that
M=\mathcal{R}\left(\mathcal{R}(W)\mathcal{R}(W)\right)
, while
\mathrm{Id}=D\mathcal{R}(|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|)
, i.e. equivalently
|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|=\mathcal{R}(\mathrm{Id})/D
. Thus,

\displaystyle\|DM-\mathrm{Id}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=D\left\|\mathcal{R}\left(\mathcal{R}(W)\mathcal{R}(W)-|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|\right)\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leqslant D^{2}\left\|\mathcal{R}(W)\mathcal{R}(W)-|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=D^{2}\left\|\left(\mathcal{R}(W)-|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|\right)|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|+\mathcal{R}(W)\left(\mathcal{R}(W)-|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|\right)\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leqslant D^{2}\left(\left\||\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|\right\|_{\infty}+\left\|\mathcal{R}(W)\right\|_{\infty}\right)\left\|\mathcal{R}(W)-|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=D\left(1+\left\|\mathcal{R}(W)\right\|_{\infty}\right)\left\|\mathcal{R}(DW-\mathrm{Id})\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leqslant D^{2}\left(1+D\left\|W\right\|_{\infty}\right)\left\|DW-\mathrm{Id}\right\|_{\infty}\,,

where the first and last inequalities are by Fact
2.1
. Now, we know by Proposition
2.2
that

\mathbf{P}\left(\left\|DW-\mathrm{Id}\right\|_{\infty}>\frac{C}{D^{\tau-1}}\right)\leqslant e^{-cD^{2}}\,,

and therefore also that

\mathbf{P}\left(\left\|W\right\|_{\infty}>\frac{2}{D}\right)\leqslant e^{-cD^{2}}\,.

Hence putting everything together, we eventually get

\mathbf{P}\left(\|DM-\mathrm{Id}\|_{\infty}>\frac{3C}{D^{\tau-3}}\right)\leqslant 2e^{-cD^{2}}\,,

which (suitably re-labelling
c,C
) is exactly the announced result.
∎

As a direct consequence of Lemma
2.3
we get the following upper bound on the operator norm of
Q
.

Corollary 2.4
.

Let
d\geqslant D^{2\tau}
, for some
\tau>3
. Then, there exists a universal constant
c>0
such that

\mathbf{P}\left(\|Q\|_{\infty}\leqslant\frac{2}{D^{1/2}}\right)\geqslant 1-e^{-cD^{2}}\,.

Proof.

We deduce from Lemma
2.3
that

\mathbf{P}\left(\|M\|_{\infty}>\frac{4}{D}\right)\leqslant e^{-cD^{2}}\,.

Just recalling that
M=Q^{*}Q
, so that
\|Q\|_{\infty}=\|M\|_{\infty}^{1/2}
, we get precisely the claimed result.
∎

From now on we set
\widetilde{P}:=DP
.

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
d\geqslant D^{2\tau}
, for some
\tau>3
. Let
V_{\chi}\subset{\mathbf{C}^{d}\otimes\mathbf{C}^{d}}
be defined as in equation (
6
). Then, there exist universal constants
c,C>0
such that

\mathbf{P}\left(\forall\ |\varphi\rangle\in V_{\chi},\ \left\|\widetilde{P}|\varphi\rangle-|\varphi\rangle\right\|\leqslant\frac{C}{D^{\tau-3}}\||\varphi\rangle\|\right)\geqslant 1-e^{-cD^{2}}\,.

Proof.

Define
\Sigma_{\chi}:=\left\{|\upsilon\rangle\in\mathbf{C}^{D}\otimes\mathbf{C}^{D},\ \left\||\chi_{\upsilon}\rangle\right\|=1\right\}
, and let
|\upsilon\rangle\in\Sigma_{\chi}
. Note that
|\chi_{\upsilon}\rangle=Q|\upsilon\rangle
and
P|\chi_{\upsilon}\rangle=QM|\upsilon\rangle
. Hence,

\left\|\widetilde{P}|\chi_{\upsilon}\rangle-|\chi_{\upsilon}\rangle\right\|=\left\|DQM|\upsilon\rangle-Q|\upsilon\rangle\right\|=\left\|Q(DM-\mathrm{Id})|\upsilon\rangle\right\|\leqslant\|Q\|_{\infty}\|DM-\mathrm{Id}\|_{\infty}\||\upsilon\rangle\|\,.

What is more, observe that

1=\langle\chi_{\upsilon}|\chi_{\upsilon}\rangle=\langle\upsilon|M|\upsilon\rangle=\frac{1}{D}\left(\langle\upsilon|\mathrm{Id}|\upsilon\rangle+\langle\upsilon|DM-\mathrm{Id}|\upsilon\rangle\right)\geqslant\frac{1}{D}(1-\|DM-\mathrm{Id}\|_{\infty})\langle\upsilon|\upsilon\rangle\,.

We thus actually have that, for all
|\upsilon\rangle\in\Sigma_{\chi}
,

\left\|\widetilde{P}|\chi_{\upsilon}\rangle-|\chi_{\upsilon}\rangle\right\|\leqslant\|Q\|_{\infty}\|DM-\mathrm{Id}\|_{\infty}\left(\frac{D}{1-\|DM-\mathrm{Id}\|_{\infty}}\right)^{1/2}\,.

Now, we know by Lemma
2.3
that

\mathbf{P}\left(\|DM-\mathrm{Id}\|_{\infty}>\frac{C}{D^{\tau-3}}\right)\leqslant e^{-cD^{2}}\,,

while we know by Corollary
2.4
that

\mathbf{P}\left(\|Q\|_{\infty}>\frac{2}{D^{1/2}}\right)\leqslant e^{-cD^{2}}\,.

Therefore putting everything together,

\mathbf{P}\left(\exists\ |\upsilon\rangle\in\Sigma_{\chi}:\left\|\widetilde{P}|\chi_{\upsilon}\rangle-|\chi_{\upsilon}\rangle\right\|>\frac{2C}{(1-C/D^{\tau-3})D^{\tau-3}}\right)\leqslant 2e^{-cD^{2}}\,,

which (suitably re-labelling
c,C
) is exactly the announced result.
∎

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
G,H
be two independent
D\times d
matrices whose entries are independent complex Gaussians with mean
0
and variance
1
. Then,

\forall\ \epsilon>0,\ \mathbf{P}\left(\Tr(GG^{*})>dD(1+\epsilon)^{2}\right)\leqslant e^{-dD\epsilon^{2}}\ \text{and}\ \mathbf{P}\left(\Tr(GH^{*})>dD\epsilon\right)\leqslant e^{-dD\epsilon^{2}/8}+2e^{-dD}\,.

Proof.

The first deviation inequality is a direct application of the Gaussian concentration inequality, recalled in Theorem
1.5
. The function that we are looking at here is
f:G\mapsto(\Tr(GG^{*}))^{1/2}=\|G\|_{2}
, which is such that
\E f\leqslant\sqrt{dD}
and clearly
1
-Lipschitz. Hence,

\mathbf{P}\left(\Tr(GG^{*})>dD(1+\epsilon)^{2}\right)=\mathbf{P}\left((\Tr(GG^{*}))^{1/2}>\sqrt{dD}(1+\epsilon)\right)\leqslant e^{-dD\epsilon^{2}}\,.

The second deviation inequality is easily obtained from the first one and the local version of the Gaussian concentration inequality, recalled in Theorem
1.6
. The function that we are looking at here is
f:(G,H)\mapsto\Tr(GH^{*})
, which is such that
\E f=0
. Moreover

\displaystyle|f(G,H)-f(G^{\prime},H^{\prime})|

\displaystyle=|\Tr(GH^{*}-G^{\prime}H^{\prime*})|

\displaystyle\leqslant\|G\|_{2}\|H-H^{\prime}\|_{2}+\|H^{\prime}\|_{2}\|G-G^{\prime}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\left(\|G\|_{2}^{2}+\|H^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2}\right)^{1/2}\left(\|G-G^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2}+\|H-H^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2}\right)^{1/2}\,.

So setting
\Omega=\left\{(G,H):\|G\|_{2},\|H\|_{2}\leqslant 2\sqrt{dD}\right\}
, we have that
f
is
\sqrt{8dD}
-Lipschitz on
\Omega
. And by the first deviation inequality we also know that
\mathbf{P}((G,H)\notin\Omega)\leqslant 2e^{-dD}
. Hence,

\mathbf{P}\left(\Tr(GH^{*})>dD\epsilon\right)\leqslant e^{-dD\epsilon^{2}/8}+2e^{-dD}\,.

The proof is thus complete.
∎

Corollary 2.7
.

Let
G_{1},\ldots,G_{D}
be independent
D\times d
matrices whose entries are independent complex Gaussians with mean
0
and variance
1
. Then,

\forall\ 0<\epsilon<1/16,\ \mathbf{P}\left(\sum_{a,b=1}^{D}(\Tr(G_{a}G_{b}^{*}))^{2}>d^{2}D^{3}(1+\epsilon)\right)\leqslant e^{-cd\epsilon}\,,

where
c>0
is a universal constant.

Proof.

Denote by
G,H
two independent
D\times d
matrices whose entries are independent complex Gaussians with mean
0
and variance
1
. Then, we have by the union bound

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\left(\sum_{a,b=1}^{D}(\Tr(G_{a}G_{b}^{*}))^{2}>D\times d^{2}D^{2}(1+\epsilon)^{4}+(D^{2}-D)\times d^{2}D\epsilon\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{1\leqslant a\leqslant D}\mathbf{P}\left((\Tr(G_{a}G_{a}^{*}))^{2}>d^{2}D^{2}(1+\epsilon)^{4}\right)

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ +\sum_{1\leqslant a\neq b\leqslant D}\mathbf{P}\left((\Tr(G_{a}G_{b}^{*}))^{2}>d^{2}D\epsilon\right)

\displaystyle=D\times\mathbf{P}\left(\Tr(GG^{*})>dD(1+\epsilon)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant e^{-cd\epsilon}\,,

where the next-to-last inequality is by Lemma
2.6
. Just noticing that

D\times d^{2}D^{2}(1+\epsilon)^{4}+(D^{2}-D)\times d^{2}D\epsilon\leqslant d^{2}D^{3}(1+16\epsilon)

(and re-labelling

by

.

Let

And this clearly implies that

Proposition 2.10
.

Let

, for some
\tau>3

\mathbf{P}\left(\left\|\left[\widetilde{P}_{12}\otimes\mathrm{Id}_{3}\,,\,\mathrm{Id}_{1}\otimes\widetilde{P}_{23}\right]\right\|_{\infty}\leqslant\frac{C}{D^{\tau-1}}\right)\geqslant 1-e^{-cD^{2}}\,.

In order to prove Proposition

(\widetilde{P}_{12}\otimes\mathrm{Id}_{3})(\mathrm{Id}_{1}\otimes\widetilde{P}_{23})
and
(\mathrm{Id}_{1}\otimes\widetilde{P}_{23})(\widetilde{P}_{12}\otimes\mathrm{Id}_{3})

P_{123}:\mathbf{C}^{d}\otimes\mathbf{C}^{d}\otimes\mathbf{C}^{d}\longrightarrow\mathbf{C}^{d}\otimes\mathbf{C}^{d}\otimes\mathbf{C}^{d}

Before doing so, there are still two operators

Proof.

Note that
P_{123}=(N_{12}\otimes\mathrm{Id}_{3})(\mathrm{Id}_{1}\otimes N^{\prime*}_{23})

\displaystyle\left\|\left(\widetilde{P}_{12}\otimes\mathrm{Id}_{3}\right)\left(\mathrm{Id}_{1}\otimes\widetilde{P}_{23}\right)-\widetilde{P}_{123}\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=D\left\|(N_{12}\otimes\mathrm{Id}_{3})(\mathrm{Id}_{1}\otimes(DW_{2}-\mathrm{Id}_{2})\otimes\mathrm{Id}_{3})(\mathrm{Id}_{1}\otimes N^{\prime*}_{23})\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leqslant D\|N_{12}\otimes\mathrm{Id}_{3}\|_{\infty}\|\mathrm{Id}_{1}\otimes N^{\prime*}_{23}\|_{\infty}\|\mathrm{Id}_{1}\otimes(DW_{2}-\mathrm{Id}_{2})\otimes\mathrm{Id}_{3}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=D\|N\|_{\infty}\|N^{\prime*}\|_{\infty}\|DW-\mathrm{Id}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=D\|NN^{*}\|_{\infty}\|DW-\mathrm{Id}\|_{\infty}\,.

Next, observe that

, where
\hat{W}_{1}:=\Tr_{2}(W_{12})
. Consequently,

\|NN^{*}\|_{\infty}\leqslant\|Q\|_{\infty}\|Q^{*}\|_{\infty}\|\hat{W}\|_{\infty}\leqslant D\|Q\|_{\infty}^{2}\|W\|_{\infty}\,.

Now, we know by Corollary
2.4
that

\mathbf{P}\left(\|Q\|_{\infty}>\frac{2}{D^{1/2}}\right)\leqslant e^{-cD^{2}}\,,

while we know by Proposition
2.2
that

\mathbf{P}\left(\|DW-\mathrm{Id}\|_{\infty}>\frac{C}{D^{\tau-1}}\right)\leqslant e^{-c^{\prime}D^{2}}\,,

and therefore also that

\mathbf{P}\left(\|W\|_{\infty}>\frac{2}{D}\right)\leqslant e^{-c^{\prime}D^{2}}\,.

Hence putting everything together, we eventually get

\mathbf{P}\left(\left\|\left(\widetilde{P}_{12}\otimes\mathrm{Id}_{3}\right)\left(\mathrm{Id}_{1}\otimes\widetilde{P}_{23}\right)-\widetilde{P}_{123}\right\|_{\infty}>\frac{8C}{D^{\tau-1}}\right)\leqslant e^{-cD^{2}}+2e^{-c^{\prime}D^{2}}\,.

Similarly, we can show that

\mathbf{P}\left(\left\|\left(\mathrm{Id}_{1}\otimes\widetilde{P}_{23}\right)\left(\widetilde{P}_{12}\otimes\mathrm{Id}_{3}\right)-\widetilde{P}_{123}\right\|_{\infty}>\frac{8C}{D^{\tau-1}}\right)\leqslant e^{-cD^{2}}+2e^{-c^{\prime}D^{2}}\,.

As a consequence, we have

\mathbf{P}\left(\left\|\left(\widetilde{P}_{12}\otimes\mathrm{Id}_{3}\right)\left(\mathrm{Id}_{1}\otimes\widetilde{P}_{23}\right)-\left(\mathrm{Id}_{1}\otimes\widetilde{P}_{23}\right)\left(\widetilde{P}_{12}\otimes\mathrm{Id}_{3}\right)\right\|_{\infty}>\frac{16C}{D^{\tau-1}}\right)\leqslant 2\left(e^{-cD^{2}}+2e^{-c^{\prime}D^{2}}\right)\,,

which (suitably re-labelling
c,C
) implies precisely the announced result.
∎

Theorem 2.11
.

Let

Proof.

Setting

\mathbf{P}\left(\left\|\left[\widetilde{P}_{12}\otimes\mathrm{Id}_{3}\,,\,\mathrm{Id}_{1}\otimes\widetilde{P}_{23}\right]\right\|_{\infty}>\frac{C}{D^{\tau-1}}\right)\leqslant e^{-cD^{2}}\,.

While on the other hand, we know by Proposition
2.9

\mathbf{P}\left(\|R\|_{\infty}>\frac{C^{\prime}}{D^{\tau-5}}\right)\leqslant e^{-c^{\prime}D^{2}}\,,

and therefore also that

\mathbf{P}\left(\|\widetilde{P}\|_{\infty}>1+\frac{C^{\prime}}{D^{\tau-5}}\right)\leqslant e^{-c^{\prime}D^{2}}\,.

We thus get that

\mathbf{P}\left(\left\|\left[\Pi_{12}\otimes\mathrm{Id}_{3}\,,\,\mathrm{Id}_{1}\otimes\Pi_{23}\right]\right\|_{\infty}>\frac{C}{D^{\tau-1}}+\frac{10C^{\prime}}{D^{\tau-5}}\right)\leqslant e^{-cD^{2}}+2e^{-c^{\prime}D^{2}}\,,

which (suitably re-labelling
c,C
) is exactly the announced result.
∎

We are now in position to prove that the parent Hamiltonian
H_{\chi}
of
|\chi^{N}\rangle
, as defined by equation (

Theorem 2.12
.

Let
d\geqslant D^{2\tau}
, for some
\tau>5
. Let
H_{\chi}
be the parent Hamiltonian of
|\chi^{N}\rangle
, as defined by equation (
7

such that

\mathbf{P}\left(\Delta(H_{\chi})\geqslant 1-\frac{C}{D^{\tau-5}}\right)\geqslant 1-e^{-cD^{2}}\,.

Proof.

In order to prove Theorem
2.12
we will use a now standard strategy, first introduced in
[
20
, Theorem 6.4]
. Namely, we will actually show that, with probability larger than
1-Ne^{-cD^{2}}
,
H_{\chi}^{2}\geqslant(1-C/D^{\tau-5})H_{\chi}
. For the sake of simplifying notation we set
\hat{\Pi}_{i}:=\Pi_{i,i+1}^{\perp}\otimes\mathrm{Id}_{1,\ldots,i-1,i+2,\ldots,N}
,
1\leqslant i\leqslant N
, so that

H_{\chi}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat{\Pi}_{i}\,.

And thus,

H_{\chi}^{2}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\hat{\Pi}_{i}\hat{\Pi}_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat{\Pi}_{i}^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\hat{\Pi}_{i}\hat{\Pi}_{i+1}+\hat{\Pi}_{i}\hat{\Pi}_{i-1}\right)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j=1\\
|i-j|>1\end{subarray}}^{N}\hat{\Pi}_{i}\hat{\Pi}_{j}\,.

Now first, since the
\hat{\Pi}_{i}
’s are projectors,

\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat{\Pi}_{i}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat{\Pi}_{i}=H_{\chi}\,.

Then, since
\hat{\Pi}_{i}
and
\hat{\Pi}_{j}
commute for
|i-j|>1

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j=1\\
|i-j|>1\end{subarray}}^{N}\hat{\Pi}_{i}\hat{\Pi}_{j}\geqslant 0\,.

And finally, we know by Theorem
2.11
that, with probability larger than
1-e^{-cD^{2}}
,
\hat{\Pi}_{i}\hat{\Pi}_{i\pm 1}\geqslant-C/D^{\tau-5}(\hat{\Pi}_{i}+\hat{\Pi}_{i\pm 1})

\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\hat{\Pi}_{i}\hat{\Pi}_{i+1}+\hat{\Pi}_{i}\hat{\Pi}_{i-1}\right)\geqslant-\frac{4C}{D^{\tau-5}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat{\Pi}_{i}=-\frac{4C}{D^{\tau-5}}H_{\chi}\,.

Hence putting everything together, we eventually get that, with probability larger than
1-2e^{-cD^{2}}
,

H_{\chi}^{2}\geqslant\left(1-\frac{4C}{D^{\tau-5}}\right)H_{\chi}\,,

which (suitably re-labelling
c,C
) is indeed what we wanted to show.
∎

2.2.
The case of PEPS

To study the case of PEPS we will follow step by step the strategy adopted in the case of MPS. We may thus skip several details.

We assume here that

To streamline notation we use the following shorthand for ‘vertical’ and ‘horizontal’

2.2.1.

3.
Consequence: Typical correlation length in random MPS and PEPS

In the previous section we showed that the parent Hamiltonians of our random MPS and PEPS are typically gapped, at least in a ‘super-injectivity’ dimensional regime. In this section we derive from the latter result that our random MPS and PEPS typically exhibit exponential decay of correlations.

Let us begin with explaining precisely what we mean when we talk about correlations in an MPS or a PEPS. Set
\tilde{N}:=N
in the case of MPS and
\tilde{N}:=N^{2}
in the case of PEPS. Let
|\chi^{N}\rangle\in(\mathbf{C}^{d})^{\otimes\tilde{N}}
be an
\tilde{N}
-site translation-invariant MPS or PEPS. Let
R,R^{\prime}\subset[\tilde{N}]
be such that
R\cap R^{\prime}=\emptyset
and let
A,A^{\prime}
be Hermitian operators on
(\mathbf{C}^{d})^{\otimes|R|},(\mathbf{C}^{d})^{\otimes|R^{\prime}|}
. We would like to compare the value of the observable
A_{R}\otimes A^{\prime}_{R^{\prime}}\otimes\mathrm{Id}_{[\tilde{N}]\setminus R\cup R^{\prime}}
on
|\chi^{N}\rangle
to the product of the values of
A_{R}\otimes\mathrm{Id}_{[\tilde{N}]\setminus R}
and
A^{\prime}_{R^{\prime}}\otimes\mathrm{Id}_{[\tilde{N}]\setminus R^{\prime}}
on
|\chi^{N}\rangle

d(R,R^{\prime})
that we have to plug in Theorem
3.8
. Indeed, site
(i,j)
in the new lattice actually corresponds to the square of sites
\{(i-1)\sqrt{\log N},\ldots,i\sqrt{\log N}-1\}\times\{(j-1)\sqrt{\log N},\ldots,j\sqrt{\log N}-1\}
in the old lattice. Hence, regions
R,R^{\prime}
in the new lattice correspond to regions
\underline{R},\underline{R}^{\prime}
in the old lattice which are such that
|\underline{R}|=(\log N)|R|,|\underline{R}^{\prime}|=(\log N)|R^{\prime}|
and
d(\underline{R},\underline{R}^{\prime})=\sqrt{\log N}d(R,R^{\prime})
. We thus get the result below.

Theorem 3.10
.

Fix
\underline{d},\underline{D}\in\mathbf{N}
with
\underline{d}>\underline{D}^{26}
and let
d=\underline{d}^{\log N}
. Set also
\underline{N}=N\sqrt{\log N}
. Let
|\chi^{\underline{N}}\rangle\in(\mathbf{C}^{\hat{d}})^{\otimes\underline{N}^{2}}
be the random
\underline{N}^{2}
-site translation-invariant PEPS whose random
1
-site tensor

is defined as in equation (
3
). Then, with probability larger than
1-e^{-c\underline{D}^{4}}
, for any

such that

and any Hermitian operators
A,A^{\prime}
on
(\mathbf{C}^{d})^{\otimes|R|},(\mathbf{C}^{d})^{\otimes|R^{\prime}|}

\gamma_{\chi}(A,A^{\prime},R,R^{\prime})\leqslant(\log N)\min(|R|,|R^{\prime}|)e^{-c^{\prime}(\log\underline{d})\sqrt{\log N}d(R,R^{\prime})}\|A_{R}\|_{\infty}\|A^{\prime}_{R^{\prime}}\|_{\infty}\,,

where

are universal constants.

Let us just make one last comment about the parent Hamiltonian
H_{\chi}
of
|\chi^{N}\rangle
in this latter case. It takes the same form as before, i.e.

H_{\chi}:=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat{\Pi}^{v}_{(i,j),(i+1,j)}\right)+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{N}\hat{\Pi}^{h}_{(i,j),(i,j+1)}\right)\,,

except that, now, site
(i,j)
is the square of sites
\{(i-1)\sqrt{\log N},\ldots,i\sqrt{\log N}-1\}\times\{(j-1)\sqrt{\log N},\ldots,j\sqrt{\log N}-1\}
. The interesting fact to point out is that this grouping makes the new

commute more than the old ones
\underline{\hat{\Pi}}
. This intuitive statement is made quantitative by

, which upper bounds the commutator of the new projectors
\hat{\Pi}
in terms of the spectral gap of the old parent Hamiltonian
\underline{H}_{\chi}
. More precisely, we thus get: If
|i-i^{\prime}|>1
or

\left\|\left[\hat{\Pi}^{v}_{(i,j),(i+1,j)},\hat{\Pi}^{v}_{(i^{\prime},j^{\prime}),(i^{\prime}+1,j^{\prime})}\right]\right\|_{\infty}=0\,,

if

or

, then

\left\|\left[\hat{\Pi}^{h}_{(i,j),(i,j+1)},\hat{\Pi}^{h}_{(i^{\prime},j^{\prime}),(i^{\prime},j^{\prime}+1)}\right]\right\|_{\infty}=0\,,

and if
i^{\prime}\notin\{i,i+1\}
or
j^{\prime}\notin\{j,j-1\}

\left\|\left[\hat{\Pi}^{v}_{(i,j),(i+1,j)},\hat{\Pi}^{h}_{(i^{\prime},j^{\prime}),(i^{\prime},j^{\prime}+1)}\right]\right\|_{\infty}=0\,.

While we know by Theorem
2.19
that, with probability larger than
1-e^{-c\underline{D}^{4}}
,
\Delta(\underline{H}_{\chi})\geqslant 1-C/\underline{D}^{2\tau-13}
, so that by

\displaystyle\left\|\left[\hat{\Pi}^{v}_{(i-1,j),(i,j)},\hat{\Pi}^{v}_{(i,j),(i+1,j)}\right]\right\|_{\infty}\leqslant 2\left(\frac{1}{1+\kappa\big(1-C/\underline{D}^{2\tau-13}\big)}\right)^{(\log N)/2}\leqslant 2\left(\frac{1}{1+\kappa^{\prime}}\right)^{\log N}\,,

\displaystyle\left\|\left[\hat{\Pi}^{h}_{(i,j-1),(i,j)},\hat{\Pi}^{h}_{(i,j),(i,j+1)}\right]\right\|_{\infty}\leqslant 2\left(\frac{1}{1+\kappa\big(1-C/\underline{D}^{2\tau-13}\big)}\right)^{(\log N)/2}\leqslant 2\left(\frac{1}{1+\kappa^{\prime}}\right)^{\log N}\,,

\displaystyle\left\|\left[\hat{\Pi}^{v}_{(i,j),(i+1,j)},\hat{\Pi}^{h}_{(i^{\prime},j^{\prime}),(i^{\prime},j^{\prime}+1)}\right]\right\|_{\infty}\leqslant 2\left(\frac{1}{1+\kappa\left(1-C/\underline{D}^{2\tau-13}\right)}\right)^{(\log N)/2}\leqslant 2\big(\frac{1}{1+\kappa^{\prime}}\big)^{\log N}\text{ for }\begin{cases}i^{\prime}\in\{i,i+1\}\\
j^{\prime}\in\{j,j-1\}\end{cases}.

Comparing Theorems
3.9
and
3.10
, we see that they yield a typical correlation length of order
1/\log N
for the former and
1/\sqrt{\log N}
for the latter. The result of Theorem

is absolutely not surprising: blocking before sampling the random tensor simply means that the physical and bond dimensions of
1
site have been scaled up, so that the correlation decay rate is expected to scale up accordingly. In contrast, the result of Theorem

is slightly more subtle. Also, since in this second case the random tensor is sampled on a site having physical and bond dimensions
\underline{d}
and
\underline{D}
, these need to be large for the result to actually hold with probability close to

. While in the first case the random tensor is sampled on a site having physical and bond dimensions
\underline{d}^{\log N}
and
\underline{D}^{\sqrt{\log N}}

grows.

4.
Typical spectral gap of the transfer operator of random MPS and PEPS

In this section, in contrast to the two previous ones, we do not constrain our random MPS and PEPS to be injective. What we want to show here is that their associated transfer operators are typically gapped. In the MPS case, treated in Section
4.2
, we can prove this, in a quantitative way, for any physical and bond dimensions (see Theorem
4.15
). On the contrary, in the PEPS case, treated in Section
4.3
, we need to impose that the physical and bond dimensions grow polynomially with the number of particles and scale in a specific way with respect to one another (see Theorem
4.23
).

4.1.
Toolbox and strategy

Our goal here will be to show that the random transfer operators
T
and
T_{N}
, as defined by equations (
2
) and (
5
), typically have a large (upper) spectral gap. For this we will make use of two technical results, providing variational formulas for the singular values of a matrix (see e.g.
[
10
, Problem III.6.1]
) and a majorization result between the eigenvalues and the singular values of a matrix (see e.g.
[
10
, Theorem II.3.6]
).

Before stating them, let us fix some notation. Given an
n\times n
complex matrix
M
we denote by
\lambda_{1}(M),\ldots,\lambda_{n}(M)
its eigenvalues, ordered so that
|\lambda_{1}(M)|\geqslant\cdots\geqslant|\lambda_{n}(M)|
, and by
s_{1}(M)\geqslant\cdots\geqslant s_{n}(M)\geqslant 0
its singular values. We furthermore define its upper spectral gap as
\Delta(M):=|\lambda_{1}(M)|-|\lambda_{2}(M)|
. This is the same notation as the one we were using in Sections
2
and
3
for the lower spectral gap, but there should be no possible confusion.

Also, given an
n^{2}\times n^{2}
complex matrix
M
, we will denote by
\mathcal{M}
its corresponding map on
n\times n
complex matrices. Formally, we identify

M=\sum_{x=1}^{r}K_{x}\otimes\bar{L}_{x}\ \ \text{and}\ \ \mathcal{M}:X\mapsto\sum_{x=1}^{r}K_{x}XL_{x}^{*}\,.

What is important for us is that this identification preserves the spectrum.

Theorem 4.1

(Minimax principle for singular values
[
10
]
)
.

Let
M
be an
n\times n
complex matrix. Then, for any
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
,

s_{i}(M)=\min_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{i}}\|MP\|_{\infty}\,,

where
\mathcal{P}_{i}
denotes the set of rank
n-i+1
projectors on
\mathbf{C}^{n}
.

As an immediate consequence of Theorem
4.1
we see that, for any unit vector
|\varphi\rangle\in\mathbf{C}^{n}
,

s_{2}(M)\leqslant\left\|M\left(\mathrm{Id}-|\varphi\rangle\!\langle\varphi|\right)\right\|_{\infty}\,.

Indeed, the minimax principle applied to the case
i=2
tells us that
s_{2}(M)
is equal to the infimum over unit vectors
|\phi\rangle\in\mathbf{C}^{n}
of
\left\|M\left(\mathrm{Id}-|\phi\rangle\!\langle\phi|\right)\right\|_{\infty}
.

Theorem 4.2

(Weyl’s majorant theorem
[
10
]
)
.

Let
M
be an
n\times n
complex matrix. Then, for any
1\leqslant k\leqslant n
,

\sum_{i=1}^{k}|\lambda_{i}(M)|\leqslant\sum_{i=1}^{k}s_{i}(M)\,.

In particular, applying Theorem
4.2
to the case
k=2
, we get

|\lambda_{1}(M)|+|\lambda_{2}(M)|\leqslant s_{1}(M)+s_{2}(M)\,.

Theorem 4.3

(Perron-Froebenius theorem for irreducible positive maps
[
19
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be an irreducible positive map on
n\times n
matrices. Then,

|\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{M})|=\underset{X\geqslant 0}{\sup}\sup\{\lambda\in\mathbf{R}\ :\ \mathcal{M}(X)\geqslant\lambda X\}\,.

As a consequence of Theorem
4.3
we have that, if there exists
X\geqslant 0
such that
\mathcal{M}(X)\geqslant\lambda X
for some
\lambda\in\mathbf{R}
, then
|\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{M})|\geqslant\lambda
.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
M
be an
n\times n
complex matrix satisfying the following: there exists a unit vector
|\varphi\rangle\in\mathbf{C}^{n}
such that
|\langle\varphi|M|\varphi\rangle|\leqslant\lambda
and
\left\|M\left(\mathrm{Id}-|\varphi\rangle\!\langle\varphi|\right)\right\|_{\infty},\left\|\left(\mathrm{Id}-|\varphi\rangle\!\langle\varphi|\right)M\right\|_{\infty}\leqslant\lambda^{\prime}
for some
\lambda>\lambda^{\prime}>0
. Then,
s_{1}(M)\leqslant\lambda+\lambda^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Let
|\phi_{1}\rangle,|\phi_{2}\rangle\in\mathbf{C}^{n}
be unit vectors, which we write as
|\phi_{i}\rangle=\alpha_{i}|\varphi\rangle+\beta_{i}|\varphi^{\prime}\rangle
, where
|\varphi^{\prime}\rangle\in\mathbf{C}^{n}
is a unit vector orthogonal to
|\varphi\rangle
and
\alpha_{i},\beta_{i}\in\mathbf{C}
are such that
|\alpha_{i}|^{2}+|\beta_{i}|^{2}=1
, for
i=1,2
.
We then have

\langle\phi_{1}|M|\phi_{2}\rangle=\bar{\alpha}_{1}\alpha_{2}\langle\varphi|M|\varphi\rangle+\bar{\alpha}_{1}\beta_{2}\langle\varphi|M|\varphi^{\prime}\rangle+\bar{\beta}_{1}\alpha_{2}\langle\varphi^{\prime}|M|\varphi\rangle+\bar{\beta}_{1}\beta_{2}\langle\varphi^{\prime}|M|\varphi^{\prime}\rangle\,.

Now, by assumption,
|\langle\varphi|M|\varphi\rangle|\leqslant\lambda
, while
\left|\langle\varphi^{\prime}|M|\varphi^{\prime}\rangle\right|,\left|\langle\varphi|M|\varphi^{\prime}\rangle\right|,\left|\langle\varphi^{\prime}|M|\varphi\rangle\right|\leqslant\lambda^{\prime}
. Also,

\left|\bar{\alpha}_{1}\alpha_{2}\right|+\left|\bar{\alpha}_{1}\beta_{2}\right|+\left|\bar{\beta}_{1}\alpha_{2}\right|+\left|\bar{\beta}_{1}\beta_{2}\right|=\left(\left|\alpha_{1}\right|+\left|\beta_{1}\right|\right)\left(\left|\alpha_{2}\right|+\left|\beta_{2}\right|\right)\leqslant 2\left(\left|\alpha_{1}\right|^{2}+\left|\beta_{1}\right|^{2}\right)^{1/2}\left(\left|\alpha_{2}\right|^{2}+\left|\beta_{2}\right|^{2}\right)^{1/2}=2\,.

Hence,
\left|\bar{\alpha}_{1}\beta_{2}\right|+\left|\bar{\beta}_{1}\alpha_{2}\right|+\left|\bar{\beta}_{1}\beta_{2}\right|\leqslant 2-\left|\bar{\alpha}_{1}\alpha_{2}\right|
. And we therefore get, by the triangle inequality and the fact that
\left|\bar{\alpha}_{1}\alpha_{2}\right|\leqslant 1
,

\left|\langle\phi_{1}|M|\phi_{2}\rangle\right|\leqslant\left|\bar{\alpha}_{1}\alpha_{2}\right|\lambda+\left(\left|\bar{\alpha}_{1}\beta_{2}\right|+\left|\bar{\beta}_{1}\alpha_{2}\right|+\left|\bar{\beta}_{1}\beta_{2}\right|\right)\lambda^{\prime}\leqslant\left|\bar{\alpha}_{1}\alpha_{2}\right|(\lambda-\lambda^{\prime})+2\lambda^{\prime}\leqslant\lambda+\lambda^{\prime}\,.

Since the latter upper bound holds for any unit vectors
|\phi_{1}\rangle,|\phi_{2}\rangle\in\mathbf{C}^{n}
, it indeed proves that
s_{1}(M)=\|M\|_{\infty}\leqslant\lambda+\lambda^{\prime}
.
∎

Proposition 4.5
.

Let
M
be an
n^{2}\times n^{2}
complex matrix and
\mathcal{M}
be its corresponding map on
n\times n
complex matrices. Assume that the following holds: (i)
\mathcal{M}
is positive irreducible and there exists a positive semidefinite matrix
X
on
\mathbf{C}^{n}
such that
\mathcal{M}(X)\geqslant(1-\delta)X
, (ii) there exists a unit vector
|\varphi\rangle
in
\mathbf{C}^{n^{2}}
such that
|\langle\varphi|M|\varphi\rangle|\leqslant 1+\epsilon
and
\left\|M\left(\mathrm{Id}-|\varphi\rangle\!\langle\varphi|\right)\right\|_{\infty},\left\|\left(\mathrm{Id}-|\varphi\rangle\!\langle\varphi|\right)M\right\|_{\infty}\leqslant\eta
, where
0<\delta,\epsilon,\eta<1/5
. Then,

|\lambda_{1}(M)|\geqslant 1-\delta\ \ \text{and}\ \ |\lambda_{2}(M)|\leqslant\delta+\epsilon+2\eta\,,

so that in particular

\Delta(M)\geqslant 1-2\delta-\epsilon-2\eta\,.

Proof.

To begin with, condition (i) implies, by Theorem
4.3
, that

|\lambda_{1}(M)|=|\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{M})|\geqslant 1-\delta\,.

Next, Theorem
4.2
tells us that

|\lambda_{2}(M)|\leqslant s_{1}(M)+s_{2}(M)-|\lambda_{1}(M)|\,.

Now, condition (ii) implies, first of all by Lemma
4.4
that
s_{1}(M)\leqslant 1+\epsilon+\eta
, and second of all by Theorem
4.1

s_{2}(M)\leqslant\left\|M\left(\mathrm{Id}-|\varphi\rangle\!\langle\varphi|\right)\right\|_{\infty}\leqslant\eta
. Hence,

|\lambda_{2}(M)|\leqslant(1+\epsilon+\eta)+\eta-(1-\delta)=\delta+\epsilon+2\eta\,.

And the proof is thus complete.
∎

With the result of Proposition
4.5
in mind, we can now explain what is our strategy in order to show that the random MPS transfer operator
T
, as defined by equation (
2
), typically has a spectral gap
\Delta(T)>0
. We know by Fact
1.2
that, with probability
1
, the positive map
\mathcal{T}
corresponding to
T
is irreducible. Our goal is thus to find a positive semidefinite matrix
X
on
\mathbf{C}^{D}
and a unit vector
|\varphi\rangle
in
\mathbf{C}^{D}\otimes\mathbf{C}^{D}
such that, with high probability

(11)

\mathcal{T}(X)\geqslant(1-\delta)X\,,

(12)

|\langle\varphi|T|\varphi\rangle|\leqslant 1+\epsilon\ \ \text{and}\ \ \|T(\mathrm{Id}-|\varphi\rangle\!\langle\varphi|)\|_{\infty},\|(\mathrm{Id}-|\varphi\rangle\!\langle\varphi|)T\|_{\infty}\leqslant\eta\,,

for some
0<\delta,\epsilon,\eta<1/5
. Indeed, we know by Proposition
4.5
that if equations (
11
) and (
12
) hold it guarantees that, with high probability

\Delta(T)=|\lambda_{1}(T)|-|\lambda_{2}(T)|\geqslant 1-2\delta-\epsilon-2\eta>0\,.

We proceed similarly for the random PEPS transfer operator
T_{N}
, as defined by equation (
5
), with a positive semidefinite matrix
X_{N}
on
(\mathbf{C}^{D})^{\otimes N}
and a unit vector
|\varphi_{N}\rangle
in
(\mathbf{C}^{D}\otimes\mathbf{C}^{D})^{\otimes N}
.

Before proceeding, we shall make one last simple observation on the spectrum of the random MPS transfer operator
T
, which straightforwardly follows from noticing that
T
and
\bar{T}
have the same spectrum. The latter claim is in turn a consequence of the fact that
\bar{T}=FTF^{*}
, where
F
denotes the flip unitary on
\mathbf{C}^{D}\otimes\mathbf{C}^{D}
(which is defined by
F|\alpha\beta\rangle=|\beta\alpha\rangle
, for any
1\leqslant\alpha,\beta\leqslant D
).

Fact 4.6
.

Let
T
be defined as in equation (
2
). If
\lambda\in\mathrm{spec}(T)
then
\bar{\lambda}\in\mathrm{spec}(T)
. And therefore, for any
n\in\mathbf{N}
,
\Tr(T^{n})\in\mathbf{R}
.

4.2.
The case of MPS

Our candidate semidefinite positive matrix on
\mathbf{C}^{D}
satisfying with high probability equation (
11
) will be the identity matrix
\mathrm{Id}
, while our candidate unit vector in
\mathbf{C}^{D}\otimes\mathbf{C}^{D}
satisfying with high probability equation (
12
) will be the maximally entangled unit vector
|\psi\rangle
. Before launching into proofs, let us briefly explain what is the intuition behind such choice. First, it is easy to check (cf. subsequent computations) that
\E T=|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|
. It is thus natural to expect that the largest eigenvalue of
T
should be close to
1
and that the corresponding eigenvector should be close to
|\psi\rangle
. Second, we know from observations in Section
2.1
that
T=\mathcal{R}(W)/Dd
for
W
a
D^{2}\times D^{2}
Wishart matrix with parameter
d
. And it was proved in
[
4
]
that the singular value distribution of
\sqrt{d}(\mathcal{R}(W)/Dd-|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|)
, i.e. of
\sqrt{d}(T-|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|)
, converges in moments to the quarter-circle distribution. This means that the singular values of
T-|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|
are at least almost all of order at most
1/\sqrt{d}
. However, this result does not tell us anything about potential isolated singular values (or in fact eigenvalues), which is what would truly matter for our purposes. What is more, even the statement about so-called weak convergence of the singular value distribution of
\sqrt{d}(T-|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|)
was proved only in the regime where
d
is of order
D^{2}
. While, as we will later see, our results are valid for any respective scaling of
d
and
D
.

4.2.1.
Computing the typical value of the transfer CP map on the identity

Proposition 4.7
.

Let
T
be defined as in equation (
2
) and let
\mathcal{T}
be its corresponding CP map. Then,

\mathbf{P}\left(\left\|\mathcal{T}(\mathrm{Id})-\mathrm{Id}\right\|_{\infty}\leqslant\frac{6}{\sqrt{d}}\right)\geqslant 1-2e^{-D/4}\,,

which implies that

\mathbf{P}\left(\mathcal{T}(\mathrm{Id})\geqslant\left(1-\frac{6}{\sqrt{d}}\right)\mathrm{Id}\right)\geqslant 1-2e^{-D/4}\,.

Proof.

Recall that

\mathcal{T}(\mathrm{Id})=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{x=1}^{d}G_{x}G_{x}^{*}\,,

where the
G_{x}
’s are independent
D\times D
matrices whose entries are independent complex Gaussians with mean
0
and variance
1/D
. This means that
\mathcal{T}(\mathrm{Id})
is distributed as
GG^{*}/dD
, for
G
a
D\times dD
matrix whose entries are independent complex Gaussians with mean
0
and variance
1
, i.e. for
GG^{*}
a
D\times D
Wishart matrix of parameter
dD
. Hence, by Theorem
1.7
(applied with
n=D
and
s=dD
), we know that

\mathbf{P}\left(\left\|\mathcal{T}(\mathrm{Id})-\mathrm{Id}\right\|_{\infty}>\frac{6}{\sqrt{d}}\right)\leqslant 2e^{-D/4}\,,

as claimed.
∎

4.2.2.
Computing the expected overlap of the transfer operator with the maximally entangled state

Proposition 4.8
.

Let
T
be defined as in equation (
2
). Then,
\langle\psi|T|\psi\rangle\in\mathbf{R}
and

\E\langle\psi|T|\psi\rangle=1\,.

Proof.

The claimed result easily follows from a direct computation. Indeed,

\displaystyle\langle\psi|T|\psi\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{dD}\sum_{x=1}^{d}\sum_{\alpha,\beta=1}^{D}\langle\alpha|G_{x}|\beta\rangle\langle\alpha|\bar{G}_{x}|\beta\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{dD}\sum_{x=1}^{d}\sum_{\alpha,\beta=1}^{D}|\langle\alpha|G_{x}|\beta\rangle|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{dD}\sum_{x=1}^{d}\Tr(G_{x}G_{x}^{*})\,.

So first it is clear that
\langle\psi|T|\psi\rangle\in\mathbf{R}
. And second,

\E\langle\psi|T|\psi\rangle=\frac{1}{dD}\sum_{x=1}^{d}\E\Tr(G_{x}G_{x}^{*})=1\,,

where the last equality is because, for each
1\leqslant x\leqslant d
,
\E\Tr(G_{x}G_{x}^{*})=D
.
∎

4.2.3.
Upper bounding the expected norm of the projection of the transfer operator on the orthogonal of the maximally entangled state

Lemma 4.9
.

Let
G
be a
D\times D
matrix whose entries are independent complex Gaussians with mean
0
and variance
1/D
. Then, for any even
p\in\mathbf{N}
such that
(2D^{2})^{1/5}\leqslant p/2\leqslant D^{2/3}
, we have

\E\Tr|G|^{p}\leqslant 2^{p}\times\frac{p^{5}}{128D}\,.

Proof.

Given
q\in\mathbf{N}
, we denote by
\mathcal{S}_{q}
the set of permutations of
\{1,\ldots,q\}
, by
\gamma\in\mathcal{S}_{q}
the full cycle
(1\cdots q)
and, for any
\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{q}
, by
\sharp(\pi)
the number of cycles in the cycle decomposition of
\pi
. Then, it is well-known that we can write

\E\Tr|G|^{2q}=D\sum_{\delta=0}^{\lfloor q/2\rfloor}S(q,\delta)D^{-2\delta}\,,

where
S(q,\delta)=|\{\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{q}:\sharp(\gamma\pi^{-1})+\sharp(\pi)=q+1-2\delta\}|
(see e.g.
[
36
, Appendix B.2]
for further details). Now, we know from
[
40
, Lemma 12]
that
S(q,0)\leqslant 4^{q-1}
and, for each
1\leqslant\delta\leqslant\lfloor q/2\rfloor
,
S(q,\delta)\leqslant 4^{q-1}q^{3\delta+1}
. Hence,

\E\Tr|G|^{2q}\leqslant 4^{q-1}D\left(1+q\sum_{\delta=1}^{\lfloor q/2\rfloor}\left(\frac{q^{3}}{D^{2}}\right)^{\delta}\right)\,.

Consequently, if
(2D^{2})^{1/5}\leqslant q\leqslant D^{2/3}
, we have

\E\Tr|G|^{2q}\leqslant 4^{q-1}D\left(1+q\times\frac{q}{2}\times\frac{q^{3}}{D^{2}}\right)\leqslant 4^{q}\times\frac{q^{5}}{4D}\,,

where the first inequality is because
q^{3}/D^{2}\leqslant 1
and the second inequality is because
1\leqslant q^{5}/2D^{2}
. And the advertised result follows, simply replacing
q
by
p/2
.
∎

Lemma 4.10
.

Let
G_{1},\ldots,G_{d},H_{1},\ldots,H_{d}
be independent
D\times D
matrices whose entries are independent complex Gaussians with mean
0
and variance
1/D
. Then,

\E\left\|\sum_{x=1}^{d}\left(G_{x}\otimes\bar{G}_{x}-H_{x}\otimes\bar{H}_{x}\right)\right\|_{\infty}\leqslant 20\sqrt{d}\,.

Proof.

The reasoning is directly inspired from the one in the proofs of
[
44
, Lemma 4.1 and Theorem 4.2]
and
[
43
, Theorem 16.6]
.
First observe that

\displaystyle\E\left\|\sum_{x=1}^{d}\left(G_{x}\otimes\bar{G}_{x}-H_{x}\otimes\bar{H}_{x}\right)\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\E\left\|\sum_{x=1}^{d}\left(\frac{G_{x}+H_{x}}{\sqrt{2}}\otimes\frac{\bar{G}_{x}+\bar{H}_{x}}{\sqrt{2}}-\frac{G_{x}-H_{x}}{\sqrt{2}}\otimes\frac{\bar{G}_{x}-\bar{H}_{x}}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\E\left\|\sum_{x=1}^{d}\left(G_{x}\otimes\bar{H}_{x}+H_{x}\otimes\bar{G}_{x}\right)\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\E\left\|\sum_{x=1}^{d}G_{x}\otimes\bar{H}_{x}\right\|_{\infty}

Next, for any
p\in\mathbf{N}
, we know that
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}\leqslant\|\cdot\|_{p}
, so that by Jensen inequality

\E\left\|\sum_{x=1}^{d}G_{x}\otimes\bar{H}_{x}\right\|_{\infty}\leqslant\left(\E\left\|\sum_{x=1}^{d}G_{x}\otimes\bar{H}_{x}\right\|^{p}_{p}\right)^{1/p}\,.

Now, for any even
p\in\mathbf{N}
, writing
p=2q
, we have

\displaystyle\E\left\|\sum_{x=1}^{d}G_{x}\otimes\bar{H}_{x}\right\|^{p}_{p}

\displaystyle=\E\Tr\left|\left(\sum_{x=1}^{d}G_{x}\otimes\bar{H}_{x}\right)\left(\sum_{x=1}^{d}G_{x}^{*}\otimes\bar{H}_{x}^{*}\right)\right|^{q}

\displaystyle=\E\left(\sum_{x_{1},\ldots,x_{q},y_{1},\ldots,y_{q}=1}^{d}\Tr\left(G_{x_{1}}G^{*}_{y_{1}}\cdots G_{x_{q}}G^{*}_{y_{q}}\right)\Tr\left(\bar{H}_{x_{1}}\bar{H}^{*}_{y_{1}}\cdots\bar{H}_{x_{q}}\bar{H}^{*}_{y_{q}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x_{1},\ldots,x_{q},y_{1},\ldots,y_{q}=1}^{d}\left(\E\Tr\left(G_{x_{1}}G^{*}_{y_{1}}\cdots G_{x_{q}}G^{*}_{y_{q}}\right)\right)^{2}\,.

Yet, for each
1\leqslant x_{1},\ldots,x_{q},y_{1},\ldots,y_{q}\leqslant d
, we know by Hölder inequality that

\left|\Tr\left(G_{x_{1}}G^{*}_{y_{1}}\cdots G_{x_{q}}G^{*}_{y_{q}}\right)\right|\leqslant\left(\Tr\left|G_{x_{1}}\right|^{p}\right)^{1/p}\left(\Tr\left|G_{y_{1}}\right|^{p}\right)^{1/p}\cdots\left(\Tr\left|G_{x_{q}}\right|^{p}\right)^{1/p}\left(\Tr\left|G_{y_{q}}\right|^{p}\right)^{1/p}\,,

which implies, since
\E|X_{1}\cdots X_{p}|\leqslant\E|X|^{p}
for identically distributed random variables
X,X_{1},\ldots,X_{p}
, that

\displaystyle\left|\E\Tr\left(G_{x_{1}}G^{*}_{y_{1}}\cdots G_{x_{q}}G^{*}_{y_{q}}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\E\left|\Tr\left(G_{x_{1}}G^{*}_{y_{1}}\cdots G_{x_{q}}G^{*}_{y_{q}}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\E\left(\left(\Tr\left|G_{x_{1}}\right|^{p}\right)^{1/p}\left(\Tr\left|G_{y_{1}}\right|^{p}\right)^{1/p}\cdots\left(\Tr\left|G_{x_{q}}\right|^{p}\right)^{1/p}\left(\Tr\left|G_{y_{q}}\right|^{p}\right)^{1/p}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\E\Tr|G|^{p}\,.

We thus have shown that

\displaystyle\E\left\|\sum_{x=1}^{d}G_{x}\otimes\bar{H}_{x}\right\|^{p}_{p}

\displaystyle\leqslant\E\Tr|G|^{p}\sum_{x_{1},\ldots,x_{q},y_{1},\ldots,y_{q}=1}^{d}\left|\E\Tr\left(G_{x_{1}}G^{*}_{y_{1}}\cdots G_{x_{q}}G^{*}_{y_{q}}\right)\right|

\displaystyle=\E\Tr|G|^{p}\sum_{x_{1},\ldots,x_{q},y_{1},\ldots,y_{q}=1}^{d}\E\Tr\left(G_{x_{1}}G^{*}_{y_{1}}\cdots G_{x_{q}}G^{*}_{y_{q}}\right)

\displaystyle=\E\Tr|G|^{p}\E\Tr\left|\sum_{x=1}^{d}G_{x}\right|^{p}

\displaystyle=(\E\Tr|G|^{p})^{2}\sqrt{d}^{p}\,,

where the first equality is because the only non vanishing terms in the sum are non negative and the last equality is because
\sum_{x=1}^{d}G_{x}\sim\sqrt{d}G
, where
G
is a
D\times D
matrix whose entries are independent complex Gaussians with mean
0
and variance
1/D
. Now, we know from Lemma
4.9
that, for any even
p\in\mathbf{N}
such that
(2D^{2})^{1/5}\leqslant p/2\leqslant D^{2/3}
,

\E\Tr|G|^{p}\leqslant 2^{p}\times\frac{p^{5}}{128D}\,.

Hence putting everything together, we finally get that, for such
p\in\mathbf{N}
,

\displaystyle\E\left\|\sum_{x=1}^{d}\left(G_{x}\otimes\bar{G}_{x}-H_{x}\otimes\bar{H}_{x}\right)\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\E\left\|\sum_{x=1}^{d}G_{x}\otimes\bar{H}_{x}\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\left(\E\left\|\sum_{x=1}^{d}G_{x}\otimes\bar{H}_{x}\right\|^{p}_{p}\right)^{1/p}

\displaystyle\leqslant 2(\E\Tr|G|^{p})^{2/p}\sqrt{d}

\displaystyle\leqslant 8\left(\frac{p^{5}}{128D}\right)^{2/p}\sqrt{d}\,.

Choosing
p=2\lfloor D^{2/3}\rfloor
in the above inequality, we see that

\E\left\|\sum_{x=1}^{d}\left(G_{x}\otimes\bar{G}_{x}-H_{x}\otimes\bar{H}_{x}\right)\right\|_{\infty}\leqslant 8\left(\frac{D^{7/3}}{4}\right)^{1/D^{2/3}}\sqrt{d}\,.

Since
\left(D^{7/3}/4\right)^{1/D^{2/3}}\leqslant 5/2
for all

, the claimed result follows.
∎

Lemma 4.12
.

Let
P
be a projector on
\mathbf{C}^{D}\otimes\mathbf{C}^{D}
, and define the function
\hat{f}
, of
d
-uples of
D\times D
matrices, as

\hat{f}:(A_{1},\ldots,A_{d})\mapsto\left\|\left(\frac{1}{d}\sum_{x=1}^{d}A_{x}\otimes\bar{A}_{x}\right)P\right\|_{\infty}\,.

Then, for
G_{1},\ldots,G_{d}
independent
D\times D
matrices whose entries are independent complex Gaussians with mean
0
and variance
1/D
, we have

\forall\ \epsilon>0,\ \mathbf{P}\left(\hat{f}(G_{1},\ldots,G_{d})\gtrless\E\hat{f}\pm\epsilon\right)\leqslant e^{-Dd\epsilon^{2}/72}+e^{-Dd}\,.

Proof.

Define the following subset of the set of
d
-uples of
D\times D
matrices:

\hat{\Omega}:=\left\{(A_{1},\ldots,A_{d}):\left(\sum_{x=1}^{d}\|A_{x}\|_{\infty}^{2}\right)^{1/2}\leqslant 3\sqrt{d}\right\}\,.

We will first show that, for
G_{1},\ldots,G_{d}
independent
D\times D
matrices whose entries are independent complex Gaussians with mean
0
and variance
1/D
, we have

\mathbf{P}\left((G_{1},\ldots,G_{d})\notin\hat{\Omega}\right)\leqslant e^{-Dd}\,.

For this, we will use the Gaussian concentration inequality, recalled in Theorem
1.5
. Let us start with showing that the average of the function we are interested in is upper bounded by
2\sqrt{d}
. Indeed, by Jensen inequality

\E\left(\sum_{x=1}^{d}\|G_{x}\|_{\infty}^{2}\right)^{1/2}\leqslant\left(\sum_{x=1}^{d}\E\|G_{x}\|_{\infty}^{2}\right)^{1/2}\,.

Yet, for
G
a
D\times D
matrix whose entries are independent complex Gaussians with mean
0
and variance
1/D
, it is well known that
\E\|G\|_{\infty}^{2}\leqslant 4
. And therefore,

\E\left(\sum_{x=1}^{d}\|G_{x}\|_{\infty}^{2}\right)^{1/2}\leqslant 2\sqrt{d}\,.

Let us now turn to showing that the Lipschitz constant of the function we are interested in is upper bounded by
1
. Indeed, by the triangle inequality (twice)

\displaystyle\left|\left(\sum_{x=1}^{d}\|G_{x}\|_{\infty}^{2}\right)^{1/2}-\left(\sum_{x=1}^{d}\|G_{x}^{\prime}\|_{\infty}^{2}\right)^{1/2}\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\left(\sum_{x=1}^{d}\left(\|G_{x}\|_{\infty}-\|G_{x}^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\right)^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\left(\sum_{x=1}^{d}\|G_{x}-G_{x}^{\prime}\|_{\infty}^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\left(\sum_{x=1}^{d}\|G_{x}-G_{x}^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2}\right)^{1/2}\,.

With these two estimates at hand, we can conclude that

\forall\ \epsilon>0,\ \mathbf{P}\left(\left(\sum_{x=1}^{d}\|G_{x}\|_{\infty}^{2}\right)^{1/2}>2\sqrt{d}+\epsilon\right)\leqslant e^{-D\epsilon^{2}}\,,

which, taking
\epsilon=\sqrt{d}
, is exactly what we wanted to prove.

We will now make us of the local version of the Gaussian concentration inequality, recalled in Theorem
1.6
. In the case of our function
\hat{f}
and our subset
\hat{\Omega}
, we have that, if
(G_{1},\ldots,G_{d}),(G_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,G_{d}^{\prime})\in\hat{\Omega}
, then

\displaystyle\left|\hat{f}(G_{1},\ldots,G_{d})-\hat{f}(G_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,G_{d}^{\prime})\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\left\|\left(\frac{1}{d}\sum_{x=1}^{d}G_{x}\otimes\bar{G}_{x}\right)P\right\|_{\infty}-\left\|\left(\frac{1}{d}\sum_{x=1}^{d}G_{x}^{\prime}\otimes\bar{G}_{x}^{\prime}\right)P\right\|_{\infty}\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\left\|\left(\frac{1}{d}\sum_{x=1}^{d}(G_{x}\otimes\bar{G}_{x}-G_{x}^{\prime}\otimes\bar{G}_{x}^{\prime})\right)P\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leqslant\left\|\frac{1}{d}\sum_{x=1}^{d}(G_{x}\otimes\bar{G}_{x}-G_{x}^{\prime}\otimes\bar{G}_{x}^{\prime})\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{d}\sum_{x=1}^{d}\left(\|G_{x}\|_{\infty}+\|G_{x}^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\right)\|G_{x}-G_{x}^{\prime}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{d}\left(\sum_{x=1}^{d}\left(\|G_{x}\|_{\infty}+\|G_{x}^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\right)^{2}\right)^{1/2}\left(\sum_{x=1}^{d}\|G_{x}-G_{x}^{\prime}\|_{\infty}^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{\sqrt{2}}{d}\left(\left(\sum_{x=1}^{d}\|G_{x}\|_{\infty}^{2}\right)^{1/2}+\left(\sum_{x=1}^{d}\|G_{x}^{\prime}\|_{\infty}^{2}\right)^{1/2}\right)\left(\sum_{x=1}^{d}\|G_{x}-G_{x}^{\prime}\|_{\infty}^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{6\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{d}}\left(\sum_{x=1}^{d}\|G_{x}-G_{x}^{\prime}\|_{\infty}^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{6\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{d}}\left(\sum_{x=1}^{d}\|G_{x}-G_{x}^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2}\right)^{1/2}\,,

where the first inequality is by the triangle inequality, the third inequality is also by the triangle inequality (after noticing that
G_{x}\otimes\bar{G}_{x}-G_{x}^{\prime}\otimes\bar{G}_{x}^{\prime}=G_{x}\otimes(\bar{G}_{x}-\bar{G}_{x}^{\prime})+(G_{x}-G_{x}^{\prime})\otimes\bar{G}_{x}^{\prime}
), the fourth inequality is by Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, the fifth inequality is because
(a+b)^{2}\leqslant 2(a^{2}+b^{2})
and
\sqrt{a+b}\leqslant\sqrt{a}+\sqrt{b}
for any
a,b\geqslant 0
, and the sixth inequality is by assumption on
\hat{\Omega}
. Putting together this upper bound on the Lipschitz constant of
\hat{f}
on
\hat{\Omega}
with the upper bound on the probability of the complement of
\hat{\Omega}
, we eventually get

\forall\ \epsilon>0,\ \mathbf{P}\left(\hat{f}(G_{1},\ldots,G_{d})\gtrless\E\hat{f}\pm\epsilon\right)\leqslant e^{-Dd\epsilon^{2}/72}+e^{-Dd}\,,

which is exactly the announced result.
∎

which implies that

\mathbf{P}\left(\mathcal{T}_{N}(\mathrm{Id})\geqslant\left(1-\left(1+\frac{28D}{\sqrt{d}}\right)^{N}\frac{28}{\sqrt{d}}\right)\mathrm{Id}\right)\geqslant 1-(D+1)^{2N}(N+2)e^{-cD}\,,

where
c>0
is a universal constant.

Proof.

Recall that

\mathcal{T}_{N}(\mathrm{Id})=\frac{1}{D^{N}d^{N}}\sum_{a_{1},b_{1},\ldots,a_{N},b_{N}=1}^{D}\sum_{x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}=1}^{d}G_{a_{N}a_{1}x_{1}}G^{*}_{b_{N}b_{1}x_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes G_{a_{N-1}a_{N}x_{N}}G^{*}_{b_{N-1}b_{N}x_{N}}\,,

where the
G_{a_{i-1}a_{i}x_{i}}
’s are independent
D\times D
matrices whose entries are independent complex Gaussians with mean
0
and variance
1/D
. This means that
\mathcal{T}_{N}(\mathrm{Id})
is distributed as

(15)

\frac{1}{D^{2N}d^{N}}\sum_{a_{1},b_{1},\ldots,a_{N},b_{N}=1}^{D}G_{a_{N}a_{1}}G^{*}_{b_{N}b_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes G_{a_{N-1}a_{N}}G^{*}_{b_{N-1}b_{N}}\,,

where the
G_{a_{i-1}a_{i}}
’s are independent
D\times dD
matrices whose entries are independent complex Gaussians with mean
0
and variance
1
.

Let us first look at the term corresponding to
b_{1}=a_{1},\ldots,b_{N}=a_{N}
in expression (
15
), i.e.

W:=\frac{1}{D^{N}}\sum_{a_{1},\ldots,a_{N}=1}^{D}\left(\frac{1}{dD}G_{a_{N}a_{1}}G^{*}_{a_{N}a_{1}}\right)\otimes\cdots\otimes\left(\frac{1}{dD}G_{a_{N-1}a_{N}}G^{*}_{a_{N-1}a_{N}}\right)\,.

For each
1\leqslant a,a^{\prime}\leqslant D
,
G_{aa^{\prime}}G_{aa^{\prime}}^{*}
is a
D\times D
Wishart matrix of parameter
dD
. So by Theorem
1.7
(applied with
n=D
and
s=dD
), we know that

\mathbf{P}\left(\left\|\frac{1}{dD}G_{aa^{\prime}}G_{aa^{\prime}}^{*}-\mathrm{Id}\right\|_{\infty}>\frac{6}{\sqrt{d}}\right)\leqslant 2e^{-D/4}\,.

Hence by the union bound,

(16)

\mathbf{P}\left(\exists\ 1\leqslant a,a^{\prime}\leqslant D:\left\|\frac{1}{dD}G_{aa^{\prime}}G_{aa^{\prime}}^{*}-\mathrm{Id}\right\|_{\infty}>\frac{6}{\sqrt{d}}\right)\leqslant 2D^{2}e^{-D/4}\,.

Now, assume that
A_{1},\ldots,A_{N}
are
D\times D
matrices satisfying
\|A_{i}-\mathrm{Id}\|_{\infty}\leqslant\epsilon
,
1\leqslant i\leqslant N
. Then,

(17)

\left\|A_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes A_{N}-\mathrm{Id}^{\otimes N}\right\|_{\infty}\leqslant\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}(1+\epsilon)^{i-1}\right)\epsilon\leqslant(1+\epsilon)^{N}\epsilon\,.

The first inequality can easily be shown by induction, after noticing that

A_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes A_{N}-\mathrm{Id}^{\otimes N}=\left(A_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes A_{N-1}-\mathrm{Id}^{\otimes(N-1)}\right)\otimes A_{N}+\mathrm{Id}^{\otimes(N-1)}\otimes(A_{N}-I)\,,

so that

\displaystyle\left\|A_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes A_{N}-\mathrm{Id}^{\otimes N}\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leqslant\left\|A_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes A_{N-1}-\mathrm{Id}^{\otimes(N-1)}\right\|_{\infty}\|A_{N}\|_{\infty}+\|\mathrm{Id}\|^{N-1}_{\infty}\|A_{N}-\mathrm{Id}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leqslant\left\|A_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes A_{N-1}-\mathrm{Id}^{\otimes(N-1)}\right\|_{\infty}(1+\epsilon)+\epsilon\,.

Putting together equations (
16
) and (
17
), we thus get that the probability that

\exists\ 1\leqslant a_{1},\ldots,a_{N}\leqslant D:\left\|\left(\frac{1}{dD}G_{a_{N}a_{1}}G^{*}_{a_{N}a_{1}}\right)\otimes\cdots\otimes\left(\frac{1}{dD}G_{a_{N-1}a_{N}}G^{*}_{a_{N-1}a_{N}}\right)-\mathrm{Id}\right\|_{\infty}>\left(1+\frac{6}{\sqrt{d}}\right)^{N}\frac{6}{\sqrt{d}}

is smaller than
2D^{2}e^{-D/4}
.
And consequently, just noticing that

W-\mathrm{Id}=\frac{1}{D^{N}}\sum_{a_{1},\ldots,a_{N}=1}^{D}\left(\left(\frac{1}{dD}G_{a_{N}a_{1}}G^{*}_{a_{N}a_{1}}\right)\otimes\cdots\otimes\left(\frac{1}{dD}G_{a_{N-1}a_{N}}G^{*}_{a_{N-1}a_{N}}\right)-\mathrm{Id}\right)\,,

we get by the triangle inequality that

\mathbf{P}\left(\left\|W-\mathrm{Id}\right\|_{\infty}>\left(1+\frac{6}{\sqrt{d}}\right)^{N}\frac{6}{\sqrt{d}}\right)\leqslant 2D^{2}e^{-D/4}\,.

Let us now look at the other terms in expression (
15
). For each
1\leqslant a_{1},\ldots,a_{N}\leqslant D
, define

W_{a_{1},\ldots,a_{N}}:=\sum_{q=1}^{N}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\subset[N]\\
|I|=q\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}b_{i}\neq a_{i},\,i\in I\\
b_{i}=a_{i},\,i\notin I\end{subarray}}\left(\frac{1}{dD}G_{a_{N}a_{1}}G^{*}_{b_{N}b_{1}}\right)\otimes\cdots\otimes\left(\frac{1}{dD}G_{a_{N-1}a_{N}}G^{*}_{b_{N-1}b_{N}}\right)\,,

so that
W^{\prime}:=\mathcal{T}_{N}(\mathrm{Id})-W
can be written as

W^{\prime}=\frac{1}{D^{N}}\sum_{a_{1},\ldots,a_{N}=1}^{D}W_{a_{1},\ldots,a_{N}}\,.

Fix
1\leqslant a_{1},\ldots,a_{N}\leqslant D
. By the triangle inequality, we have

\left\|W_{a_{1},\ldots,a_{N}}\right\|_{\infty}\leqslant\sum_{q=1}^{N}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\subset[N]\\
|I|=q\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}b_{i}\neq a_{i},\,i\in I\\
b_{i}=a_{i},\,i\notin I\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{N}\left\|\frac{1}{dD}G_{a_{i-1}a_{i}}G^{*}_{b_{i-1}b_{i}}\right\|_{\infty}\,.

Now, given
1\leqslant q\leqslant N
and
I\subset[N]
such that
|I|=q
, set
\bar{I}:=\cup_{i\in I}\{i,i+1\}
. We then have that, for
i\in\bar{I}
,
G_{b_{i-1}b_{i}}
is independent from
G_{a_{i-1}a_{i}}
, while for
i\notin\bar{I}
,
G_{b_{i-1}b_{i}}=G_{a_{i-1}a_{i}}
. Hence, for
i\in\bar{I}
, we know from Lemma
4.16
that

\mathbf{P}\left(\left\|\frac{1}{dD}G_{a_{i-1}a_{i}}G^{*}_{b_{i-1}b_{i}}\right\|_{\infty}>\frac{16\sqrt{\ln 6}}{\sqrt{d}}\right)\leqslant e^{-cD}\,.

While for
i\notin\bar{I}
, we know from Theorem
1.7
that

\mathbf{P}\left(\left\|\frac{1}{dD}G_{a_{i-1}a_{i}}G^{*}_{b_{i-1}b_{i}}\right\|_{\infty}=\left\|\frac{1}{dD}G_{a_{i-1}a_{i}}G^{*}_{a_{i-1}a_{i}}\right\|_{\infty}>1+\frac{6}{\sqrt{d}}\right)\leqslant 2e^{-D/4}\,.

And therefore, by the union bound,

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{N}\left\|\frac{1}{dD}G_{a_{i-1}a_{i}}G^{*}_{b_{i-1}b_{i}}\right\|_{\infty}>\left(\frac{16\sqrt{\ln 6}}{\sqrt{d}}\right)^{|\bar{I}|}\left(1+\frac{6}{\sqrt{d}}\right)^{N-|\bar{I}|}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{i\in\bar{I}}\mathbf{P}\left(\left\|\frac{1}{dD}G_{a_{i-1}a_{i}}G^{*}_{b_{i-1}b_{i}}\right\|_{\infty}>\frac{16\sqrt{\ln 6}}{\sqrt{d}}\right)

\displaystyle+\sum_{i\notin\bar{I}}\mathbf{P}\left(\left\|\frac{1}{dD}G_{a_{i-1}a_{i}}G^{*}_{b_{i-1}b_{i}}\right\|_{\infty}>1+\frac{6}{\sqrt{d}}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant|\bar{I}|e^{-cD}+2(N-|\bar{I}|)e^{-D/4}

\displaystyle\leqslant Ne^{-c^{\prime}D}\,.

Since
|\bar{I}|\geqslant|I|+1=q+1
, we thus have by the union bound again that

\mathbf{P}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}b_{i}\neq a_{i},\,i\in I\\
b_{i}=a_{i},\,i\notin I\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{N}\left\|\frac{1}{dD}G_{a_{i-1}a_{i}}G^{*}_{b_{i-1}b_{i}}\right\|_{\infty}>D^{q}\left(\frac{16\sqrt{\ln 6}}{\sqrt{d}}\right)^{q+1}\left(1+\frac{6}{\sqrt{d}}\right)^{N-q-1}\right)\leqslant D^{q}Ne^{-c^{\prime}D}\,.

And consequently, once more by the union bound, the probability that

\sum_{q=1}^{N}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\subset[N]\\
|I|=q\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}b_{i}\neq a_{i},\,i\in I\\
b_{i}=a_{i},\,i\notin I\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{N}\left\|\frac{1}{dD}G_{a_{i-1}a_{i}}G^{*}_{b_{i-1}b_{i}}\right\|_{\infty}>\sum_{q=1}^{N}{N\choose q}D^{q}\left(\frac{16\sqrt{\ln 6}}{\sqrt{d}}\right)^{q+1}\left(1+\frac{6}{\sqrt{d}}\right)^{N-q-1}

is smaller than

\left(\sum_{q=1}^{N}{N\choose q}D^{q}\right)Ne^{-c^{\prime}D}\leqslant(D+1)^{N}Ne^{-c^{\prime}D}\,.

Hence, just noticing that

\sum_{q=1}^{N}{N\choose q}D^{q}\left(\frac{16\sqrt{\ln 6}}{\sqrt{d}}\right)^{q+1}\left(1+\frac{6}{\sqrt{d}}\right)^{N-q-1}\leqslant\left(1+\frac{6}{\sqrt{d}}+\frac{16\sqrt{\ln 6}D}{\sqrt{d}}\right)^{N}\frac{16\sqrt{\ln 6}}{\sqrt{d}}\leqslant\left(1+\frac{28D}{\sqrt{d}}\right)^{N}\frac{22}{\sqrt{d}}\,,

we eventually get that

\mathbf{P}\left(\left\|W_{a_{1},\ldots,a_{N}}\right\|_{\infty}>\left(1+\frac{28D}{\sqrt{d}}\right)^{N}\frac{22}{\sqrt{d}}\right)\leqslant(D+1)^{N}Ne^{-c^{\prime}D}\,.

And therefore, by the triangle inequality and the union bound,

\mathbf{P}\left(\|W^{\prime}\|_{\infty}>\left(1+\frac{28D}{\sqrt{d}}\right)^{N}\frac{22}{\sqrt{d}}\right)\leqslant D^{N}(D+1)^{N}Ne^{-c^{\prime}D}\,.

We are now left with combining the results of the two parts of the proof. Indeed, since
\|\mathcal{T}_{N}(\mathrm{Id})-\mathrm{Id}\|_{\infty}\leqslant\|W-\mathrm{Id}\|_{\infty}+\|W^{\prime}\|_{\infty}
, we eventually obtain that

\mathbf{P}\left(\left\|\mathcal{T}_{N}(\mathrm{Id})-\mathrm{Id}\right\|_{\infty}>\left(1+\frac{6}{\sqrt{d}}\right)^{N}\frac{6}{\sqrt{d}}+\left(1+\frac{28D}{\sqrt{d}}\right)^{N}\frac{22}{\sqrt{d}}\right)\leqslant 2D^{2}e^{-D/4}+(D+1)^{2N}Ne^{-c^{\prime}D}\,,

which yields the announced result.
∎

4.3.2.
Computing the typical overlap of the transfer operator with the maximally entangled state

Proposition 4.18
.

Let
T_{N}
be defined as in equation (
5
). Then,
\langle\psi^{\otimes N}|T_{N}|\psi^{\otimes N}\rangle\in\mathbf{R}
and

\mathbf{P}\left(\left|\langle\psi^{\otimes N}|T_{N}|\psi^{\otimes N}\rangle-1\right|\leqslant\frac{42N}{\sqrt{d}}+D^{2}\left(\frac{84}{\sqrt{d}}\right)^{N}\right)\geqslant 1-6e^{-D^{3}/72}\,.

Proof.

Let
T
be defined as in equation (
2
). Observe that

(18)

\langle\psi^{\otimes N}|T_{N}|\psi^{\otimes N}\rangle=\Tr\left(\tilde{T}^{N}\right)=\lambda_{1}(\tilde{T})^{N}+\cdots+\lambda_{D^{2}}(\tilde{T})^{N}\,,

where
\tilde{T}
is distributed as
T
with
\tilde{d}=D^{2}d
,
\tilde{D}=D
. Hence, we first know by Fact
4.6
that
\langle\psi^{\otimes N}|T_{N}|\psi^{\otimes N}\rangle\in\mathbf{R}
. Second, we know from Propositions
4.13
and
4.14
(combined with observations from Proposition
4.5
) that

\forall\ 0<\epsilon<\sqrt{d},\ \mathbf{P}\left(\left|\lambda_{1}(T)-1\right|\leqslant\frac{40+2\epsilon}{\sqrt{d}}\ \text{and}\ |\lambda_{2}(T)|,\ldots,|\lambda_{D^{2}}(T)|\leqslant\frac{80+4\epsilon}{\sqrt{d}}\right)\geqslant 1-6e^{-D\epsilon^{2}/72}\,.

Consequently,

\forall\ 0<\epsilon<D\sqrt{d},\ \mathbf{P}\left(\left|\lambda_{1}(\tilde{T})-1\right|\leqslant\frac{40+2\epsilon}{D\sqrt{d}}\ \text{and}\ \left|\lambda_{2}(\tilde{T})\right|,\ldots,\left|\lambda_{D^{2}}(\tilde{T})\right|\leqslant\frac{80+4\epsilon}{D\sqrt{d}}\right)\geqslant 1-6e^{-D\epsilon^{2}/72}\,.

And therefore, taking
\epsilon=D
, we get that

\mathbf{P}\left(\left|\lambda_{1}(\tilde{T})-1\right|\leqslant\frac{42}{\sqrt{d}}\ \text{and}\ \left|\lambda_{2}(\tilde{T})\right|,\ldots,\left|\lambda_{D^{2}}(\tilde{T})\right|\leqslant\frac{84}{\sqrt{d}}\right)\geqslant 1-6e^{-D^{3}/72}\,.

By equation (
18
), this implies that, with probability greater than
1-6e^{-D^{3}/72}
, the two following hold

\displaystyle\langle\psi^{\otimes N}|T_{N}|\psi^{\otimes N}\rangle\geqslant\left(1-\frac{42}{\sqrt{d}}\right)^{N}-(D^{2}-1)\left(\frac{84}{\sqrt{d}}\right)^{N}\geqslant 1-\frac{42N}{\sqrt{d}}-D^{2}\left(\frac{84}{\sqrt{d}}\right)^{N}

\displaystyle\langle\psi^{\otimes N}|T_{N}|\psi^{\otimes N}\rangle\leqslant\left(1+\frac{42}{\sqrt{d}}\right)^{N}+(D^{2}-1)\left(\frac{84}{\sqrt{d}}\right)^{N}\leqslant 1+\frac{42N}{\sqrt{d}}+D^{2}\left(\frac{84}{\sqrt{d}}\right)^{N}\,,

which is precisely what we wanted to show.
∎

4.3.3.
Upper bounding the typical norm of the projection of the transfer operator on the orthogonal of the maximally entangled state

In the sequel, we will make extensive use of the following simple observation: given positive random variables
X,Y
and positive numbers
x,y
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}(X+Y>x+y)\leqslant\mathbf{P}(X>x\ \text{or}\ Y>y)\leqslant\mathbf{P}(X>x)+\mathbf{P}(Y>y)\,,

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}(XY>xy)\leqslant\mathbf{P}(X>x\ \text{or}\ Y>y)\leqslant\mathbf{P}(X>x)+\mathbf{P}(Y>y)\,.

Indeed, if
X+Y>x+y
, resp.
XY>xy
, then necessarily either
X>x
or
Y>y
.

We now gather three deviation inequalities that we will also use repeatedly later on.

Lemma 4.19
.

Let
G_{1},\ldots,G_{d},H_{1},\ldots,H_{d}
be independent
D\times D
matrices whose entries are independent complex Gaussians with mean
0
and variance
1/D
. Then,

\displaystyle\forall\ \epsilon>0,

\displaystyle\ \mathbf{P}\left(\left\|\frac{1}{d}\sum_{x=1}^{d}G_{x}\otimes\bar{G}_{x}\right\|_{\infty}>(1+\epsilon)\left(1+\frac{41}{\sqrt{d}}\right)\right)\leqslant e^{-cDd\min(\epsilon,\epsilon^{2})}\,,

\displaystyle\ \mathbf{P}\left(\left\|\frac{1}{d}\sum_{x=1}^{d}G_{x}\otimes\bar{H}_{x}\right\|_{\infty}>(1+\epsilon)\frac{10}{\sqrt{d}}\right)\leqslant e^{-cDd\min(\epsilon/\sqrt{d},\epsilon^{2}/d)}\,,

\displaystyle\ \mathbf{P}\left(\left\|\frac{1}{d}\sum_{x=1}^{d}(G_{x}\otimes\bar{G}_{x}-H_{x}\otimes\bar{H}_{x})\right\|_{\infty}>(1+\epsilon)\frac{20}{\sqrt{d}}\right)\leqslant e^{-cDd\min(\epsilon/\sqrt{d},\epsilon^{2}/d)}\,,

where
c>0
is a universal constant.

Proof.

The proof follows step by step that of Lemma
4.12
, just slightly generalizing it in one point, so some details are skipped here.
We first fix
\delta>0
and define the following subset of the set of
d
-uples of
D\times D
matrices:

\hat{\Omega}_{\delta}:=\left\{(A_{1},\ldots,A_{d}):\left(\sum_{x=1}^{d}\|A_{x}\|_{\infty}^{2}\right)^{1/2}\leqslant(2+\delta)\sqrt{d}\right\}\,.

Reasoning as in the proof of Lemma
4.12
we obtain that, for
G_{1},\ldots,G_{d}
independent
D\times D
matrices whose entries are independent complex Gaussians with mean
0
and variance
1/D
,

\mathbf{P}\left((G_{1},\ldots,G_{d})\notin\hat{\Omega}_{\delta}\right)\leqslant e^{-Dd\delta^{2}}\,.

We then use the local version of the Gaussian concentration inequality, recalled in Theorem
1.6
.
In our case, the functions that we are looking at are

\displaystyle\hat{f}_{1}:(G_{1},\ldots,G_{d})\mapsto\left\|\frac{1}{d}\sum_{x=1}^{d}G_{x}\otimes\bar{G}_{x}\right\|_{\infty}\,,

\displaystyle\hat{f}_{2}:(G_{1},\ldots,G_{d},H_{1},\ldots,H_{d})\mapsto\left\|\frac{1}{d}\sum_{x=1}^{d}G_{x}\otimes\bar{H}_{x}\right\|_{\infty}\,.

Arguing again exactly as in the proof of Lemma
4.12
it can be shown that, for
(G_{1},\ldots,G_{d}),(G_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,G_{d}^{\prime})\in\hat{\Omega}_{\delta}

\left|\hat{f}_{1}(G_{1},\ldots,G_{d})-\hat{f}_{1}(G_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,G_{d}^{\prime})\right|\leqslant\frac{2\sqrt{2}(2+\delta)}{\sqrt{d}}\left(\sum_{x=1}^{d}\|G_{x}-G_{x}^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2}\right)^{1/2}\,,

while for
(G_{1},\ldots,G_{d},H_{1},\ldots,H_{d}),(G_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,G_{d}^{\prime},H_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,H_{d}^{\prime})\in\hat{\Omega}_{\delta}\times\hat{\Omega}_{\delta}

\left|\hat{f}_{2}(G_{1},\ldots,G_{d},H_{1},\ldots,H_{d})-\hat{f}_{1}(G_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,G_{d}^{\prime},H_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,H_{d}^{\prime})\right|\leqslant\frac{\sqrt{2}(2+\delta)}{\sqrt{d}}\left(\sum_{x=1}^{d}\|G_{x}-G_{x}^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2}+\sum_{x=1}^{d}\|H_{x}-H_{x}^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2}\right)^{1/2}\,.

Finally we know that
\E\hat{f}_{1}\leqslant 1+41/\sqrt{d}
and
\E\hat{f}_{2}\leqslant 10/\sqrt{d}
. Putting everything together, we thus get that

\displaystyle\forall\ \epsilon>0,

\displaystyle\ \mathbf{P}\left(\left\|\frac{1}{d}\sum_{x=1}^{d}G_{x}\otimes\bar{G}_{x}\right\|_{\infty}>(1+\epsilon)\left(1+\frac{41}{\sqrt{d}}\right)\right)\leqslant e^{-Dd\epsilon^{2}/8(2+\delta)^{2}}+e^{-Dd\delta^{2}}\,,

\displaystyle\ \mathbf{P}\left(\left\|\frac{1}{d}\sum_{x=1}^{d}G_{x}\otimes\bar{H}_{x}\right\|_{\infty}>(1+\epsilon)\frac{10}{\sqrt{d}}\right)\leqslant e^{-32D\epsilon^{2}/(2+\delta)^{2}}+2e^{-Dd\delta^{2}}\,.

Choosing
\delta_{1},\delta_{2}>0
in the two deviation probabilities above satisfying, respectively,
e^{-Dd\epsilon^{2}/8(2+\delta_{1})^{2}}=e^{-Dd\delta_{1}^{2}}
and
e^{-32D\epsilon^{2}/(2+\delta_{2})^{2}}=2e^{-Dd\delta_{2}^{2}}
, we eventually obtain that there exist universal constants
c_{1},c_{2}>0
such that

\displaystyle\forall\ \epsilon>0,

\displaystyle\ \mathbf{P}\left(\left\|\frac{1}{d}\sum_{x=1}^{d}G_{x}\otimes\bar{G}_{x}\right\|_{\infty}>(1+\epsilon)\left(1+\frac{41}{\sqrt{d}}\right)\right)\leqslant e^{-c_{1}Dd\min(\epsilon,\epsilon^{2})}\,,

\displaystyle\ \mathbf{P}\left(\left\|\frac{1}{d}\sum_{x=1}^{d}G_{x}\otimes\bar{H}_{x}\right\|_{\infty}>(1+\epsilon)\frac{10}{\sqrt{d}}\right)\leqslant e^{-c_{2}Dd\min(\epsilon/\sqrt{d},\epsilon^{2}/d)}\,,

which are precisely the first two deviation inequalities. As for the third one, we simply have to recall that, for each
1\leqslant x\leqslant d
,
G_{x}\otimes\bar{G}_{x}-H_{x}\otimes\bar{H}_{x}\sim G_{x}\otimes\bar{H}_{x}-H_{x}\otimes\bar{G}_{x}
. Therefore, for all
\epsilon>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\left(\left\|\frac{1}{d}\sum_{x=1}^{d}(G_{x}\otimes\bar{G}_{x}-H_{x}\otimes\bar{H}_{x})\right\|_{\infty}>(1+\epsilon)\frac{20}{\sqrt{d}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{P}\left(\left\|\frac{1}{d}\sum_{x=1}^{d}(G_{x}\otimes\bar{H}_{x}-H_{x}\otimes\bar{G}_{x})\right\|_{\infty}>(1+\epsilon)\frac{20}{\sqrt{d}}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathbf{P}\left(\left\|\frac{1}{d}\sum_{x=1}^{d}G_{x}\otimes\bar{H}_{x}\right\|_{\infty}+\left\|\frac{1}{d}\sum_{x=1}^{d}H_{x}\otimes\bar{G}_{x}\right\|_{\infty}>(1+\epsilon)\frac{20}{\sqrt{d}}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathbf{P}\left(\left\|\frac{1}{d}\sum_{x=1}^{d}G_{x}\otimes\bar{H}_{x}\right\|_{\infty}>(1+\epsilon)\frac{10}{\sqrt{d}}\right)

\displaystyle\ +\mathbf{P}\left(\left\|\frac{1}{d}\sum_{x=1}^{d}H_{x}\otimes\bar{G}_{x}\right\|_{\infty}>(1+\epsilon)\frac{10}{\sqrt{d}}\right)\,.

And since we know by what precedes that the latter sum of deviation probabilities is upper bounded by

2e^{-c_{2}Dd\min(\epsilon/\sqrt{d},\epsilon^{2}/d)}\leqslant e^{-c_{3}Dd\min(\epsilon/\sqrt{d},\epsilon^{2}/d)}\,,

the third deviation inequality is proved as well.
∎

Lemma 4.20
.

Let
G_{1},\ldots,G_{d}
and
H_{1}^{i},\ldots,H_{d}^{i}
,
1\leqslant i\leqslant N
, be independent
D\times D
matrices whose entries are independent complex Gaussians with mean
0
and variance
1/D
. Then, for all
\epsilon>0
, the probability that

\left\|\frac{1}{d^{N}}\sum_{x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}=1}^{d}\left(\underset{i=1}{\overset{N}{\bigotimes}}\,G_{x_{i}}\otimes\bar{G}_{x_{i}}-\underset{i=1}{\overset{N}{\bigotimes}}\,H_{x_{i}}^{i}\otimes\bar{H}_{x_{i}}^{i}\right)\right\|_{\infty}>(1+\epsilon)^{N}\left(1+\frac{41}{\sqrt{d}}\right)^{N-1}\frac{20N}{\sqrt{d}}

is smaller than
3Ne^{-cDd\min(\epsilon/\sqrt{d},\epsilon^{2}/d)}
, where
c>0
is a universal constant.

Proof.

We will show by induction that the result is true for
N
being any
n\in\mathbf{N}
. To simplify notation, we will set

Y:=\left\|\frac{1}{d}\sum_{x=1}^{d}G_{x}\otimes\bar{G}_{x}\right\|_{\infty}\,,

and for each
n\in\mathbf{N}
,

X_{n}:=\left\|\frac{1}{d^{n}}\sum_{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}=1}^{d}\left(\underset{i=1}{\overset{n}{\bigotimes}}\,G_{x_{i}}\otimes\bar{G}_{x_{i}}-\underset{i=1}{\overset{n}{\bigotimes}}\,H_{x_{i}}^{i}\otimes\bar{H}_{x_{i}}^{i}\right)\right\|_{\infty}\,.

We know from Lemma
4.19
that

\forall\ \epsilon>0,\ \mathbf{P}\left(X_{1}>(1+\epsilon)\frac{20}{\sqrt{d}}\right)\leqslant e^{-cDd\min(\epsilon/\sqrt{d},\epsilon^{2}/d)}\leqslant 3e^{-cDd\min(\epsilon/\sqrt{d},\epsilon^{2}/d)}\,.

So the statement is true for
n=1
.

Let us now assume that the statement is true for some
n\in\mathbf{N}
and show that this implies that it is true also for
n+1
.
Observe that, setting for each
1\leqslant i\leqslant n+1
and
1\leqslant x_{i}\leqslant d
,
M_{x_{i}}:=G_{x_{i}}\otimes\bar{G}_{x_{i}}
and
N_{x_{i}}^{i}:=H_{x_{i}}^{i}\otimes\bar{H}_{x_{i}}^{i}
, we have for each
1\leqslant x_{1},\ldots,x_{n+1}\leqslant d
,

\underset{i=1}{\overset{n+1}{\bigotimes}}\,M_{x_{i}}-\underset{i=1}{\overset{n+1}{\bigotimes}}\,N_{x_{i}}^{i}=\left(\underset{i=1}{\overset{n}{\bigotimes}}\,M_{x_{i}}\right)\otimes\left(M_{x_{n+1}}-N_{x_{n+1}}^{n+1}\right)+\left(\underset{i=1}{\overset{n}{\bigotimes}}\,M_{x_{i}}-\underset{i=1}{\overset{n}{\bigotimes}}\,N_{x_{i}}^{i}\right)\otimes N_{x_{n+1}}^{n+1}\,.

So by the triangle inequality,

(19)

X_{n+1}\leqslant Y^{n}X_{1}+X_{n}^{\prime}Y^{\prime}\,.

Now, we know from Lemma
4.19
that

\forall\ \epsilon>0,\ \mathbf{P}\left(Y>(1+\epsilon)\left(1+\frac{41}{\sqrt{d}}\right)\right)\leqslant e^{-cDd\min(\epsilon,\epsilon^{2})}\,,

and therefore also that

\forall\ \epsilon>0,\ \mathbf{P}\left(Y^{n}>(1+\epsilon)^{n}\left(1+\frac{41}{\sqrt{d}}\right)^{n}\right)\leqslant e^{-cDd\min(\epsilon,\epsilon^{2})}\,.

What is more, we know by the initialisation step and by the recursion hypothesis that

\displaystyle\forall\ \epsilon>0,

\displaystyle\ \mathbf{P}\left(X_{1}>(1+\epsilon)\frac{20}{\sqrt{d}}\right)\leqslant e^{-cDd\min(\epsilon/\sqrt{d},\epsilon^{2}/d)}\,,

\displaystyle\ \mathbf{P}\left(X_{n}>(1+\epsilon)^{n}\left(1+\frac{41}{\sqrt{d}}\right)^{n-1}\frac{20n}{\sqrt{d}}\right)\leqslant 3ne^{-cDd\min(\epsilon/\sqrt{d},\epsilon^{2}/d)}\,.

Consequently, for all
\epsilon>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\left(X_{n+1}>(1+\epsilon)^{n+1}\left(1+\frac{41}{\sqrt{d}}\right)^{n}\frac{20(n+1)}{\sqrt{d}}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathbf{P}\left(Y^{n}X_{1}+X_{n}Y^{\prime}>(1+\epsilon)^{n+1}\left(1+\frac{41}{\sqrt{d}}\right)^{n}\frac{20(n+1)}{\sqrt{d}}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathbf{P}\left(Y^{\prime}>(1+\epsilon)\left(1+\frac{41}{\sqrt{d}}\right)\right)+\mathbf{P}\left(Y^{n}>(1+\epsilon)^{n}\left(1+\frac{41}{\sqrt{d}}\right)^{n}\right)

\displaystyle\ +\mathbf{P}\left(X_{1}>(1+\epsilon)\frac{20}{\sqrt{d}}\right)+\mathbf{P}\left(X_{n}>(1+\epsilon)^{n}\left(1+\frac{41}{\sqrt{d}}\right)^{n-1}\frac{20n}{\sqrt{d}}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant 2e^{-cDd\min(\epsilon,\epsilon^{2})}+(3n+1)e^{-cDd\min(\epsilon/\sqrt{d},\epsilon^{2}/d)}

\displaystyle\leqslant 3(n+1)e^{-cDd\min(\epsilon/\sqrt{d},\epsilon^{2}/d)}\,.

So the statement is indeed true for
n+1
, which concludes the proof.
∎

Corollary 4.21
.

Let
G_{1},\ldots,G_{d}
and
H_{1}^{i},\ldots,H_{d}^{i}
,
1\leqslant i\leqslant N
, be independent
D\times D
matrices whose entries are independent complex Gaussians with mean
0
and variance
1/D
. And define the random variables
G:=(G_{1},\ldots,G_{d})
and
H:=(H_{1}^{1},\ldots,H_{d}^{1},\ldots,H_{1}^{N},\ldots,H_{d}^{N})
. Set also

(20)

\eta\equiv\eta(N,d,D):=(4\sqrt{d}N)^{2(N+1)}\left(1+\frac{41}{\sqrt{d}}\right)^{N-1}\frac{20N}{\sqrt{d}}e^{-cD^{3}/d}\,.

Then, for all
d^{3/4}\geqslant D
, the probability over
G
that

\E_{H}\left\|\frac{1}{d^{N}}\sum_{x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}=1}^{d}\left(\underset{i=1}{\overset{N}{\bigotimes}}\,G_{x_{i}}\otimes\bar{G}_{x_{i}}-\underset{i=1}{\overset{N}{\bigotimes}}\,H_{x_{i}}^{i}\otimes\bar{H}_{x_{i}}^{i}\right)\right\|_{\infty}>(1+\eta)\left(1+\frac{D}{\sqrt{d}}\right)^{N}\left(1+\frac{41}{\sqrt{d}}\right)^{N-1}\frac{20N}{\sqrt{d}}

is smaller than
3Ne^{-cD^{3}/d}
, where
c>0
is a universal constant.

Proof.

We know from Lemma
4.20
that, for all
\epsilon>0
, the probability over
G,H
that

\left\|\frac{1}{d^{N}}\sum_{x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}=1}^{d}\left(\underset{i=1}{\overset{N}{\bigotimes}}\,G_{x_{i}}\otimes\bar{G}_{x_{i}}-\underset{i=1}{\overset{N}{\bigotimes}}\,H_{x_{i}}^{i}\otimes\bar{H}_{x_{i}}^{i}\right)\right\|_{\infty}>\left(1+\epsilon\right)^{N}\left(1+\frac{41}{\sqrt{d}}\right)^{N-1}\frac{20N}{\sqrt{d}}

is smaller than
3Ne^{-cDd\min(\epsilon/\sqrt{d},\epsilon^{2}/d)}
. Taking
\epsilon=D/\sqrt{d}
, this implies that, for all
d\geqslant D
(so that
\epsilon/\sqrt{d}\leqslant 1
), there exists a set
\Omega
of
G
’s of measure larger than
1-3Ne^{-cD^{3}/d}
such that, for all fixed
G\in\Omega
, the random variable

X_{H}:=\left\|\frac{1}{d^{N}}\sum_{x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}=1}^{d}\left(\underset{i=1}{\overset{N}{\bigotimes}}\,G_{x_{i}}\otimes\bar{G}_{x_{i}}-\underset{i=1}{\overset{N}{\bigotimes}}\,H_{x_{i}}^{i}\otimes\bar{H}_{x_{i}}^{i}\right)\right\|_{\infty}\,

satisfies

\forall\ \delta>0,\ \mathbf{P}\left(X_{H}>\left(1+\delta\right)^{N}\left(1+\frac{41}{\sqrt{d}}\right)^{N-1}\frac{20N}{\sqrt{d}}\right)\leqslant 3Ne^{-cDd\min(\delta/\sqrt{d},\delta^{2}/d)}\,.

Assume that the latter holds. Then, setting
M:=(1+41/\sqrt{d})^{N-1}20N/\sqrt{d}
, we can re-write

\E X_{H}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbf{P}(X_{H}=u)udu=\int_{0}^{(1+D/\sqrt{d})^{N}M}\mathbf{P}(X_{H}=u)udu+\int_{(1+D/\sqrt{d})^{N}M}^{\infty}\mathbf{P}(X_{H}=u)udu\,.

Now on the one hand,

\int_{0}^{(1+D/\sqrt{d})^{N}M}\mathbf{P}(X_{H}=u)udu\leqslant\left(1+\frac{D}{\sqrt{d}}\right)^{N}M\,.

While on the other hand,

\displaystyle\int_{(1+D/\sqrt{d})^{N}M}^{\infty}\mathbf{P}(X_{H}=u)udu

\displaystyle=NM^{2}\int_{D/\sqrt{d}}^{\infty}\mathbf{P}\left(X_{H}=(1+t)^{N}M\right)(1+t)^{2N-1}dt

\displaystyle\leqslant NM^{2}\int_{D/\sqrt{d}}^{\infty}\mathbf{P}\left(X_{H}>(1+t)^{N}M\right)(1+t)^{2N-1}dt

\displaystyle\leqslant 3N^{2}M^{2}\left(\int_{D/\sqrt{d}}^{\sqrt{d}}e^{-cDt^{2}}(1+t)^{2N-1}dt+\int_{\sqrt{d}}^{\infty}e^{-cD\sqrt{d}t}(1+t)^{2N-1}dt\right)\,,

where the first equality is by change of variables and the last inequality is by assumption on
X_{H}
.
Yet, we clearly have

\int_{D/\sqrt{d}}^{\sqrt{d}}e^{-cDt^{2}}(1+t)^{2N-1}dt\leqslant\frac{(1+\sqrt{d})^{2N}}{2N}e^{-cD^{3}/d}\leqslant(2\sqrt{d})^{2N}e^{-cD^{3}/d}\,.

And it can easily be shown by successive integrations by parts that, as soon as
D\sqrt{d}\geqslant 1/c
,

\int_{\sqrt{d}}^{\infty}e^{-cD\sqrt{d}t}(1+t)^{2N-1}dt\leqslant(1+\sqrt{d})^{2N-1}\left(\sum_{q=0}^{2N-1}(2N-1)^{q}\right)\frac{e^{-cD\sqrt{d}}}{cD\sqrt{d}}\leqslant(4\sqrt{d}N)^{2N}e^{-cD\sqrt{d}}\,.

Hence in the end, for all
d^{3/4}\geqslant D\geqslant 28
,

\int_{(1+D/\sqrt{d})^{N}M}^{\infty}\mathbf{P}(X_{H}=u)udu\leqslant 3N^{2}M^{2}\left((2\sqrt{d})^{2N}e^{-cD^{3}/d}+(4\sqrt{d}N)^{2N}e^{-cD\sqrt{d}}\right)\leqslant(4\sqrt{d}N)^{2(N+1)}M^{2}e^{-cD^{3}/d}\,.

Putting everything together, we eventually obtain that, for all
d^{3/4}\geqslant D\geqslant 28
,

\E X_{H}\leqslant\left(1+\eta\right)\left(1+\frac{D}{\sqrt{d}}\right)^{N}M\,,

which is exactly the announced result.
∎

Proposition 4.22
.

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\left(\left\|T_{N}(\mathrm{Id}-|\psi^{\otimes N}\rangle\!\langle\psi^{\otimes N}|)\right\|_{\infty}>(1+\eta)\left(1+\frac{93D}{\sqrt{d}}\right)^{N}\frac{60N}{\sqrt{d}}\right)\leqslant 4N(D+1)^{N}e^{-cD^{3}/d}\,,

5.
Consequence: Typical correlation length in random MPS and PEPS

In the previous section we showed that the transfer operators of our random MPS and PEPS are typically gapped. In this section we derive from the latter result that our random MPS and PEPS typically exhibit exponential decay of correlations. Compared to the statements in Section
3
, those in the current section have one main advantage: they apply to a dimensional regime that goes beyond the one of injectivity.

5.1.
Preliminary facts

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
M
be an
n\times n
complex matrix satisfying the following:
\lambda_{1}(M)=\lambda
for some
\lambda\in\mathbf{C}
and, for all
2\leqslant i\leqslant n
,
\lambda_{i}(M)=\lambda\epsilon_{i}
with
|\epsilon_{i}|\leqslant\epsilon
for some
0<\epsilon<1
. Then, there exists a unit vector
|\varphi\rangle\in\mathbf{C}^{n}
such that, for any
k,k^{\prime}\in\mathbf{N}
and any
n\times n
complex matrices
A,A^{\prime}
,

\displaystyle\Tr(M^{k})=\lambda^{k}\left(1+\epsilon^{(k)}\right)\ \text{with}\ |\epsilon^{(k)}|\leqslant n\epsilon^{k}\,,

\displaystyle\Tr(AM^{k})=\lambda^{k}\left(\langle\varphi|A|\varphi\rangle+\epsilon^{(k)}_{A}\right)\ \text{with}\ |\epsilon^{(k)}_{A}|\leqslant n\epsilon^{k}\|A\|_{\infty}\,,

\displaystyle\Tr(AM^{k}A^{\prime}M^{k^{\prime}})=\lambda^{k+k^{\prime}}\left(\langle\varphi|A|\varphi\rangle\langle\varphi|A^{\prime}|\varphi\rangle+\epsilon^{(k,k^{\prime})}_{A,A^{\prime}}\right)\ \text{with}\ |\epsilon^{(k,k^{\prime})}_{A,A^{\prime}}|\leqslant(\epsilon^{k}+\epsilon^{k^{\prime}}+n\epsilon^{k+k^{\prime}})\|A\|_{\infty}\|A^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\,.

Proof.

By the Schur decomposition, we know that
M
can be written in triangular form as
M=\lambda(|\varphi_{1}\rangle\!\langle\varphi_{1}|+R)
, with
\langle\varphi_{1}|R|\varphi_{1}\rangle=0
,
\langle\varphi_{j}|R|\varphi_{i}\rangle=0
for all
1\leqslant i<j\leqslant n
, and
\|R\|_{\infty}\leqslant\epsilon
. Hence, for any
\ell\in\mathbf{N}
,
M^{\ell}=\lambda^{\ell}(|\varphi_{1}\rangle\!\langle\varphi_{1}|+R^{(\ell)})
, with
R^{(\ell)}
having the same form as
R
and
\|R^{(\ell)}\|_{\infty}\leqslant\epsilon^{\ell}
. As a consequence, we have

\displaystyle\Tr(M^{k})=\lambda^{k}\left(1+\Tr(R^{(k)})\right)\,,

\displaystyle\Tr(AM^{k})=\lambda^{k}\left(\langle\varphi_{1}|A|\varphi_{1}\rangle+\Tr(AR^{(k)})\right)\,,

\displaystyle\Tr(AM^{k}A^{\prime}M^{k^{\prime}})=\lambda^{k+k^{\prime}}\left(\langle\varphi_{1}|A|\varphi_{1}\rangle\langle\varphi_{1}|A^{\prime}|\varphi_{1}\rangle+\langle\varphi_{1}|AR^{(k)}A^{\prime}|\varphi_{1}\rangle+\langle\varphi_{1}|A^{\prime}R^{(k^{\prime})}A|\varphi_{1}\rangle+\Tr(AR^{(k)}A^{\prime}R^{(k^{\prime})})\right)\,.

We now just need to upper bound the error terms in the three expressions above. For the first one we clearly have

|\Tr(R^{(k)})|\leqslant(n-1)\epsilon^{k}\,.

For the second one we get by Hölder inequality that

|\Tr(AR^{(k)})|\leqslant\|R^{(k)}\|_{1}\|A\|_{\infty}\leqslant(n-1)\epsilon^{k}\|A\|_{\infty}\,.

And for the third one we get again by Hölder inequality that

\displaystyle|\langle\varphi_{1}|AR^{(k)}A^{\prime}|\varphi_{1}\rangle|\leqslant\|AR^{(k)}A^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\leqslant\|R^{(k)}\|_{\infty}\|A\|_{\infty}\|A^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\leqslant\epsilon^{k}\|A\|_{\infty}\|A^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\,,

\displaystyle|\langle\varphi_{1}|A^{\prime}R^{(k^{\prime})}A|\varphi_{1}\rangle|\leqslant\|A^{\prime}R^{(k^{\prime})}A\|_{\infty}\leqslant\|R^{(k^{\prime})}\|_{\infty}\|A\|_{\infty}\|A^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\leqslant\epsilon^{k^{\prime}}\|A\|_{\infty}\|A^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\,,

\displaystyle|\Tr(AR^{(k)}A^{\prime}R^{(k^{\prime})})|\leqslant\|R^{(k)}\|_{2}\|R^{(k^{\prime})}\|_{2}\|A\|_{\infty}\|A^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\leqslant(n-1)\epsilon^{k+k^{\prime}}\|A\|_{\infty}\|A^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\,.

And we thus get precisely the announced result.
∎

Corollary 5.2
.

Let
M
be an
n\times n
complex matrix satisfying the following:
\lambda_{1}(M)=\lambda
for some
\lambda\in\mathbf{C}
and, for all
2\leqslant i\leqslant n

\lambda_{i}(M)=\lambda\epsilon_{i}
with
|\epsilon_{i}|\leqslant\epsilon
for some
0<\epsilon<1

be such that

n\times n

\left|\frac{\Tr(AM^{k}A^{\prime}M^{k^{\prime}})}{\Tr(M^{k+k^{\prime}+2})}-\frac{\Tr(AM^{k+k^{\prime}+1})\Tr(A^{\prime}M^{k+k^{\prime}+1})}{\left(\Tr(M^{k+k^{\prime}+2})\right)^{2}}\right|\leqslant\frac{10}{|\lambda|^{2}(1-\epsilon^{k})^{2}}\epsilon^{k}\|A\|_{\infty}\|A^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\,.

Proof.

We know by Lemma
5.1
(and using the notation introduced there) that

\displaystyle\Tr(M^{k+k^{\prime}+2})=\lambda^{k+k^{\prime}+2}\left(1+\epsilon^{(k+k^{\prime}+2)}\right)\,,

\displaystyle\Tr(AM^{k+k^{\prime}+1})=\lambda^{k+k^{\prime}+1}\left(\langle\varphi|A|\varphi\rangle+\epsilon^{(k+k^{\prime}+1)}_{A}\right)\,,

\displaystyle\Tr(A^{\prime}M^{k+k^{\prime}+1})=\lambda^{k+k^{\prime}+1}\left(\langle\varphi|A^{\prime}|\varphi\rangle+\epsilon^{(k+k^{\prime}+1)}_{A^{\prime}}\right)\,,

\displaystyle\Tr(AM^{k}A^{\prime}M^{k^{\prime}})=\lambda^{k+k^{\prime}}\left(\langle\varphi|A|\varphi\rangle\langle\varphi|A^{\prime}|\varphi\rangle+\epsilon^{(k,k^{\prime})}_{A,A^{\prime}}\right)\,.

Set
\alpha:=\langle\varphi|A|\varphi\rangle
and
\alpha^{\prime}:=\langle\varphi|A^{\prime}|\varphi\rangle
, which are clearly such that
|\alpha|\leqslant\|A\|_{\infty}
and
|\alpha^{\prime}|\leqslant\|A^{\prime}\|_{\infty}
. Set also

\gamma:=\left|\frac{\Tr(AM^{k}A^{\prime}M^{k^{\prime}})}{\Tr(M^{k+k^{\prime}+2})}-\frac{\Tr(AM^{k+k^{\prime}+1})\Tr(A^{\prime}M^{k+k^{\prime}+1})}{\left(\Tr(M^{k+k^{\prime}+2})\right)^{2}}\right|\,.

We then have

\displaystyle\gamma

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\lambda|^{2}|1+\epsilon^{(k+k^{\prime}+2)}|^{2}}\left|\left(\alpha\alpha^{\prime}+\epsilon^{(k,k^{\prime})}_{A,A^{\prime}}\right)\left(1+\epsilon^{(k+k^{\prime}+2)}\right)-\left(\alpha+\epsilon^{(k+k^{\prime}+1)}_{A}\right)\left(\alpha^{\prime}+\epsilon^{(k+k^{\prime}+1)}_{A^{\prime}}\right)\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\lambda|^{2}|1+\epsilon^{(k+k^{\prime}+2)}|^{2}}\left|\left(1+\epsilon^{(k+k^{\prime}+2)}\right)\epsilon^{(k,k^{\prime})}_{A,A^{\prime}}+\epsilon^{(k+k^{\prime}+2)}\alpha\alpha^{\prime}-\alpha\epsilon^{(k+k^{\prime}+1)}_{A^{\prime}}-\alpha^{\prime}\epsilon^{(k+k^{\prime}+1)}_{A}-\epsilon^{(k+k^{\prime}+1)}_{A}\epsilon^{(k+k^{\prime}+1)}_{A^{\prime}}\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{|\lambda|^{2}(1-n\epsilon^{k+k^{\prime}+2})^{2}}\left((1+n\epsilon^{k+k^{\prime}+2})(\epsilon^{k}+\epsilon^{k^{\prime}}+n\epsilon^{k+k^{\prime}})+n\epsilon^{k+k^{\prime}+2}+2n\epsilon^{k+k^{\prime}+1}+n^{2}\epsilon^{2(k+k^{\prime}+1)}\right)\|A\|_{\infty}\|A^{\prime}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{10}{|\lambda|^{2}(1-\epsilon^{k})^{2}}\epsilon^{k}\|A\|_{\infty}\|A^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\,,

which is exactly what we wanted to show.
∎

Lemma 5.3
.

Let

M

The first two equalities are simply by definition of

\displaystyle\left|\langle\varphi_{E}|\tilde{B}_{E}|\varphi_{E}\rangle\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\Tr_{E}\left[\Tr_{H}\left[(B_{H}\otimes\mathrm{Id}_{E})|\chi\rangle\!\langle\chi|_{HE}\right]|\varphi\rangle\!\langle\varphi|_{E}\right]\right|

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
g
be a Gaussian vector in
\mathbf{C}^{n}
with mean
0
and variance

. Then,

\forall\ \epsilon>0,\ \mathbf{P}\left(\|g\|>1+\epsilon\right)\leqslant e^{-n\epsilon^{2}}\,.

Lemma

\E\|g\|\leqslant\left(\E\|g\|^{2}\right)^{1/2}=1\,.

1

\forall\ \epsilon>0,\ \mathbf{P}\left(\|g\|>1+\epsilon\right)\leqslant e^{-n\epsilon^{2}}\,,

which is exactly the announced result.
∎

Lemma 5.5
.

Let

be independent Gaussian vectors in

\forall\ \epsilon>0,\ \mathbf{P}\left(|g_{0}|>\epsilon\right)\leqslant 2e^{-n\epsilon^{2}}\,,

which concludes the proof.
∎

Lemma 5.8
.

Let
T_{N}
be defined as in equation (
5
). Then,

\forall\ \epsilon>0,\ \mathbf{P}\left(\Tr(T_{N})\leqslant(1+4D\epsilon)^{2N}\right)\geqslant 1-2N(D+1)^{N}e^{-d\epsilon^{2}}\,.

The proof technique is largely inspired from that of Proposition
4.22
. So we might skip a few details here.

To begin with, observe that
\Tr(T_{N})
is distributed as

\frac{1}{D^{N}}\sum_{a_{1},b_{1},\ldots,a_{N},b_{N}=1}^{D}\underset{i=1}{\overset{N}{\prod}}\,\langle g_{a_{i-1}a_{i}}|g_{b_{i-1}b_{i}}\rangle\,,

where the

’s are independent Gaussian vectors in
\mathbf{C}^{d}
with mean
0
and variance
1/d
. Indeed,

\displaystyle\Tr(T_{N})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{D^{N}}\sum_{a_{1},b_{1},\ldots,a_{N},b_{N}=1}^{D}\frac{1}{d^{N}}\sum_{x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}=1}^{d}\underset{i=1}{\overset{N}{\prod}}\left(\Tr(G_{a_{i-1}a_{i}x_{i}})\Tr(\bar{G}_{b_{i-1}b_{i}x_{i}})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{D^{N}}\sum_{a_{1},b_{1},\ldots,a_{N},b_{N}=1}^{D}\underset{i=1}{\overset{N}{\prod}}\left(\sum_{x_{i}=1}^{d}g_{a_{i-1}a_{i}x_{i}}\bar{g}_{b_{i-1}b_{i}x_{i}}\right)\,,

where we have set, for each
1\leqslant i\leqslant N
and each
1\leqslant a_{i-1},a_{i}\leqslant D
,
1\leqslant x_{i}\leqslant d
,
g_{a_{i-1}a_{i}x_{i}}:=\Tr(G_{a_{i-1}a_{i}x_{i}})/\sqrt{d}
, so that the
g_{a_{i-1}a_{i}x_{i}}
’s are independent complex Gaussians with mean
0
and variance
1/d
.

Now, given
1\leqslant a_{1},\ldots,a_{N}\leqslant D
, we can re-write

\sum_{b_{1},\ldots,b_{N}=1}^{D}\underset{i=1}{\overset{N}{\prod}}\,\langle g_{a_{i-1}a_{i}}|g_{b_{i-1}b_{i}}\rangle=\sum_{q=0}^{N}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\subset[N]\\
|I|=q\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}b_{i}\neq a_{i},\,i\in I\\
b_{i}=a_{i},\,i\notin I\end{subarray}}\underset{i=1}{\overset{N}{\prod}}\,Z_{a_{i-1}a_{i}b_{i-1}b_{i}}\,,

where we introduced the notation, for each
1\leqslant a,a^{\prime},b,b^{\prime}\leqslant D
,

Z_{aa^{\prime}bb^{\prime}}:=\langle g_{aa^{\prime}}|g_{bb^{\prime}}\rangle\,.

Given
1\leqslant q\leqslant N
and
I\subset[N]
such that
|I|=q
, we define
\bar{I}:=\cup_{i\in I}\{i,i+1\}
. We then have that, for
i\in\bar{I}
,
g_{b_{i-1}b_{i}}
is independent from
g_{a_{i-1}a_{i}}
, while for
i\notin\bar{I}
,
g_{b_{i-1}b_{i}}=g_{a_{i-1}a_{i}}
. Hence, for
i\in\bar{I}
, we know from Lemma
5.5
that

\mathbf{P}\left(\left|Z_{a_{i-1}a_{i}b_{i-1}b_{i}}\right|>\epsilon\right)\leqslant 2e^{-d\epsilon^{2}}\,,

while for
i\notin\bar{I}
, we know from Lemma
5.4
that

\mathbf{P}\left(\left|Z_{a_{i-1}a_{i}b_{i-1}b_{i}}\right|>(1+\epsilon)^{2}\right)\leqslant e^{-d\epsilon^{2}}\,.

And therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\left(\underset{i=1}{\overset{N}{\prod}}\,\left|Z_{a_{i-1}a_{i}b_{i-1}b_{i}}\right|>\epsilon^{|\bar{I}|}(1+\epsilon)^{2(N-|\bar{I}|)}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{i\in\bar{I}}\mathbf{P}\left(\left|Z_{a_{i-1}a_{i}b_{i-1}b_{i}}\right|>\epsilon\right)+\sum_{i\in\bar{I}}\mathbf{P}\left(\left|Z_{a_{i-1}a_{i}b_{i-1}b_{i}}\right|>(1+\epsilon)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant|\bar{I}|\times 2e^{-d\epsilon^{2}}+(N-|\bar{I}|)\times e^{-d\epsilon^{2}}

\displaystyle\leqslant 2Ne^{-d\epsilon^{2}}\,.

Since
|\bar{I}|\geqslant|I|+1=q+1
, we thus have

\mathbf{P}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}b_{i}\neq a_{i},\,i\in I\\
b_{i}=a_{i},\,i\notin I\end{subarray}}\underset{i=1}{\overset{N}{\prod}}\,\left|Z_{a_{i-1}a_{i}b_{i-1}b_{i}}\right|>D^{q}\epsilon^{q+1}(1+\epsilon)^{2(N-q-1)}\right)\leqslant D^{q}\times 2Ne^{-d\epsilon^{2}}\,.

\sum_{q=0}^{N}{N\choose q}D^{q}\epsilon^{q+1}(1+\epsilon)^{2(N-q-1)}=\epsilon(1+\epsilon)^{2(N-1)}\left(D\epsilon+(1+\epsilon)^{2}\right)^{N}\leqslant(1+4D\epsilon)^{2N}\,,

while on the other hand,

2Ne^{-d\epsilon^{2}}\sum_{q=0}^{N}{N\choose q}D^{q}=2N(D+1)^{N}e^{-d\epsilon^{2}}\,.

And consequently, we get in the end that

\mathbf{P}\left(\sum_{q=0}^{N}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\subset[N]\\
|I|=q\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}b_{i}\neq a_{i},\,i\in I\\
b_{i}=a_{i},\,i\notin I\end{subarray}}\underset{i=1}{\overset{N}{\prod}}\,\left|Z_{a_{i-1}a_{i}b_{i-1}b_{i}}\right|>(1+4D\epsilon)^{2N}\right)\leqslant 2N(D+1)^{N}e^{-d\epsilon^{2}}\,,

which implies precisely the result we wanted to show.
∎

|\lambda_{1}(T_{M})|\geqslant 1-\frac{\hat{C}}{M^{\alpha/2-\beta-1}}\ \ \text{and}\ \ \forall\ 2\leqslant i\leqslant D^{2M},\ |\lambda_{i}(T_{M})|\leqslant|\lambda_{1}(T_{M})|\times\frac{\hat{C}}{M^{\alpha/2-\beta-1}}\,.

Next, we know by Lemma
5.8
(applied with
\epsilon=\tilde{C}/M^{(\alpha-1)/2}
) that, with probability larger than
1-e^{-\tilde{c}M^{1/2}}
,

\Tr(T_{M})\leqslant\left(1+\frac{\tilde{C}}{M^{\alpha/2-\beta-3/2}}\right)^{2}\,.

Now, combining Corollary
5.2
and Lemma
5.3
we know that, setting
\epsilon(T_{M}):=|\lambda_{1}(T_{M})|/|\lambda_{2}(T_{M})|
, we have, for any
k\leqslant N-C_{0}M
and any Hermitian operators
A,A^{\prime}
on
(\mathbf{C}^{d})^{\otimes M}
,

\gamma_{\chi}(A,A^{\prime},k)\leqslant\frac{10(\Tr(T_{M}))^{2}}{|\lambda_{1}(T_{M})|^{2}\left(1-\epsilon(T_{M})^{k}\right)^{2}}\epsilon(T_{M})^{k}\|A\|_{\infty}\|A^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\,.

We now just have to observe that, for
\alpha>11
,
(\alpha+1)/3<(\alpha-3)/2
(so that the range of possible values for
\beta
is not empty), and that with the assumptions we made on
\alpha,\beta
, we have
\left(1+\tilde{C}/M^{\alpha/2-\beta-3/2}\right)^{2}\leqslant\tilde{C}^{\prime}
and
M^{3\beta-\alpha}\geqslant M>M^{1/2}
. Hence putting everything together, we eventually get that there exist universal constants
c,C,C^{\prime}>0
such that, with probability larger than
1-e^{-cM^{1/2}}
, for any
k\leqslant N-C_{0}M
and any Hermitian operators
A,A^{\prime}
on
(\mathbf{C}^{d})^{\otimes M}
,

\gamma_{\chi}(A,A^{\prime},k)\leqslant C^{\prime}\times\left(\frac{C}{M^{\alpha/2-\beta-1}}\right)^{k}\|A\|_{\infty}\|A^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\,,

exactly as announced. ∎

Note that the setting of Theorem
5.9
is more or less the same as the one of Theorem
3.9
, with

having to grow with
N
. The only difference is that in Theorem
5.9

and
D
are both polynomial in
N
, while in Theorem
3.9

d
is polynomial in
N
and
D
is sub-polynomial in
N
. Anyway, the obtained typical correlation length is of the same order in both cases, namely
1/\log N
.

6.1.
New random constructions of quantum expanders

Let
S
be an MPS transfer operator, of the form

S=\sum_{x=1}^{d}K_{x}\otimes\bar{K}_{x}\,,

where the
K_{x}
’s are
D\times D
matrices.
S
can equivalently be seen as a completely positive (CP) map on the set of
D\times D
matrices, having
d
Kraus operators, defined by

\mathcal{S}(X):=\sum_{x=1}^{d}K_{x}XK_{x}^{*}\,.

Given a unit vector
|\varphi\rangle\in\mathbf{C}^{D}\otimes\mathbf{C}^{D}
, written as

|\varphi\rangle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{D}\varphi_{ij}|ij\rangle\,,

define
M_{\varphi}
,
D\times D
matrix with unit Hilbert–Schmidt norm, as

M_{\varphi}:\,=\sum_{i,j=1}^{D}\varphi_{ij}|i\rangle\!\langle j|\,.

It is then easy to see that

S|\varphi\rangle=\lambda|\varphi\rangle\ \Longleftrightarrow\ \mathcal{S}(M_{\varphi})=\lambda M_{\varphi}\,.

So
S
and
\mathcal{S}
have the same eigenvalues and associated eigenvectors in one-to-one correspondence.

The notion of quantum expander was introduced in
[
8
]
. We here adopt the slightly generalized definition of
[
30
]
, which applies to any completely positive trace-preserving (CPTP) map (not necessarily unital, i.e. having the maximally mixed state as fixed state). A CPTP map on
n\times n
matrices is called
k
-regular if it has Kraus rank at most
k
, and it is called
(1-\varepsilon)
-expanding if its second largest eigenvalue (in modulus) is at most
\varepsilon
. Such a CPTP map is called a quantum expander with parameters
(m,k,\varepsilon)
if, in addition, its fixed state has entropy at least
\log m
. A ‘good’ quantum expander should have
m
as large at possible and
k,\varepsilon
as small as possible.

Let
T
be the random MPS transfer operator, as defined by equation (
1
), and let
\mathcal{T}
be its associated random CP map, which is thus defined by

(26)

\mathcal{T}(X):=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{x=1}^{d}G_{x}XG_{x}^{*}\,.

We know by Theorem
4.15
that

\mathbf{P}\left(1-\frac{C}{\sqrt{d}}\leqslant\lambda_{1}(T)\leqslant 1+\frac{C}{\sqrt{d}}\ \text{and}\ |\lambda_{2}(T)|\leqslant\frac{C}{\sqrt{d}}\right)\geqslant 1-e^{-cD}\,,

which by the preceding discussion is equivalent to

\mathbf{P}\left(1-\frac{C}{\sqrt{d}}\leqslant\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{T})\leqslant 1+\frac{C}{\sqrt{d}}\ \text{and}\ |\lambda_{2}(\mathcal{T})|\leqslant\frac{C}{\sqrt{d}}\right)\geqslant 1-e^{-cD}\,.

Furthermore, denoting by
|\phi\rangle
the eigenvector of
T
with associated eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}(T)
, we also know by Theorem
4.15
that, with probability larger than
1-e^{-cD}
, there exists a vector
|\phi^{\prime}\rangle
with norm at most
1
such that

|\phi\rangle=|\psi\rangle+\frac{C}{\sqrt{d}}|\phi^{\prime}\rangle\,.

This implies that the eigenvector of
\mathcal{T}
with associated eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{T})
is
M_{\phi}
, which, with probability larger than
1-e^{-cD}
, is of the form

M_{\phi}=M_{\psi}+\frac{C}{\sqrt{d}}M_{\phi^{\prime}}=\frac{\mathrm{Id}}{\sqrt{D}}+\frac{C}{\sqrt{d}}M_{\phi^{\prime}}\,,

where
M_{\phi^{\prime}}
has Hilbert–Schmidt norm at most
1
.

\mathcal{T}
is a priori not TP. Nevertheless, we will show that we can construct a random CPTP map
\hat{\mathcal{T}}
which is with high probability a good approximation of
\mathcal{T}
(at least as soon as
d\geqslant C_{0}D
for some universal constant
C_{0}>0
). With this aim in view set

(27)

\Sigma:=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{x=1}^{d}G_{x}^{*}G_{x}\,.

Saying that
\mathcal{T}
is close to being TP is equivalent to saying that
\Sigma
is close to the identity, which is what we prove below.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
\Sigma
be the random
D\times D
matrix defined by equation (
27
). Then,

\mathbf{P}\left(\|\Sigma-\mathrm{Id}\|_{\infty}\leqslant\frac{C}{\sqrt{d}}\right)\geqslant 1-e^{-cD}\,,

where
c,C>0
are universal constants.

Proof.

Observe that, re-scaled by a factor
dD
,
\Sigma
is a
D\times D
Wishart matrix of parameter
dD
. The result thus immediately follows from Theorem
1.7
, applied with
n=D
and
s=dD
.
∎

By Lemma
6.1
we get in particular that
\Sigma
is invertible with probability larger than
1-e^{-cD}
. If this is the case, then we can define the map
\hat{\mathcal{T}}
by

(28)

\hat{\mathcal{T}}(X):=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{x=1}^{d}\Sigma^{-1/2}G_{x}XG_{x}^{*}\Sigma^{-1/2}=\Sigma^{-1/2}\mathcal{T}(X)\Sigma^{-1/2}\,.

\hat{\mathcal{T}}
is by construction a CPTP map. Let us now show that it is with high probability a good approximation of
\mathcal{T}
.

Corollary 6.2
.

Let
\mathcal{T}
and
\hat{\mathcal{T}}
be the random CP and CPTP maps defined by equations (
26
) and (
28
). Then,

\mathbf{P}\left(\left\|\hat{\mathcal{T}}-\mathcal{T}\right\|_{2\rightarrow 2}\leqslant\frac{C}{\sqrt{d}}\right)\geqslant 1-e^{-cD}\,,

where
c,C>0
are universal constants.

Proof.

For any
X
such that
\|X\|_{2}\leqslant 1
, we have

\displaystyle\|\hat{\mathcal{T}}(X)-\mathcal{T}(X)\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\|\Sigma^{-1/2}\mathcal{T}(X)\Sigma^{-1/2}-\mathcal{T}(X)\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\|(\Sigma^{-1/2}-\mathrm{Id})\mathcal{T}(X)\Sigma^{-1/2}\|_{2}+\|\mathcal{T}(X)(\Sigma^{-1/2}-\mathrm{Id})\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\left(\|\Sigma^{-1/2}\|_{\infty}+1\right)\left\|\mathcal{T}(X)\right\|_{2}\|\Sigma^{-1/2}-\mathrm{Id}\|_{\infty}\,,

where the first inequality is by the triangle inequality, after noticing that
AYA-Y=(A-\mathrm{Id})YA+Y(A-\mathrm{Id})
, while the second inequality is by Hölder inequality.

Now first of all, we know by Lemma
6.1
that, with probability larger than
1-e^{-cD}
,
\|\Sigma^{-1/2}-\mathrm{Id}\|_{\infty}\leqslant C/\sqrt{d}
, so a fortiori
\|\Sigma^{-1/2}\|_{\infty}\leqslant 1+C/\sqrt{d}
. What is more, with probability larger than
1-e^{-cD}
, for any
X
such that
\|X\|_{2}\leqslant 1
,

\left\|\mathcal{T}(X)\right\|_{2}\leqslant|\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{T})|\|X\|_{2}\leqslant 1+\frac{C}{\sqrt{d}}\,.

Putting everything together, we thus eventually get that, with probability larger than
1-2e^{-cD}
, for any
X
such that
\|X\|_{2}\leqslant 1
,

\|\hat{\mathcal{T}}(X)-\mathcal{T}(X)\|_{2}\leqslant\left(1+\frac{C}{\sqrt{d}}\right)^{2}\frac{C}{\sqrt{d}}\leqslant\frac{4C}{\sqrt{d}}\,.

which (suitably re-labelling
c,C
) is precisely the advertised result.
∎

Proposition 6.3
.

Let
\hat{\mathcal{T}}
be the random CPTP map defined by equation (
28
), and denote by
\hat{\rho}
its fixed state. Then,

\mathbf{P}\left(\left\|\hat{\rho}\right\|_{2}\leqslant\left(1+\frac{C}{\sqrt{d}}\right)\frac{1}{\sqrt{D}}\right)\geqslant 1-e^{-cD}\,,

where
c,C>0
are universal constants.

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{T}
is CP, we can assume without loss of generality that
M_{\phi}\geqslant 0
. We can thus define
\rho_{\phi}:=M_{\phi}/\|M_{\phi}\|_{1}
, which is the state such that
\mathcal{T}(\rho_{\phi})=\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{T})\rho_{\phi}
. Then,

\displaystyle\left\|\hat{\rho}-\rho_{\phi}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|\hat{\mathcal{T}}(\hat{\rho})-\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{T})}\mathcal{T}(\rho_{\phi})\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\left\|\hat{\mathcal{T}}(\hat{\rho})-\hat{\mathcal{T}}(\rho_{\phi})\right\|_{2}+\left\|\hat{\mathcal{T}}(\rho_{\phi})-\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{T})}\mathcal{T}(\rho_{\phi})\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant|\lambda_{2}(\hat{\mathcal{T}})|\left\|\hat{\rho}-\rho_{\phi}\right\|_{2}+\left\|\hat{\mathcal{T}}-\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{T})}\mathcal{T}\right\|_{2\rightarrow 2}\left\|\rho_{\phi}\right\|_{2}\,.

This means that

\left\|\hat{\rho}-\rho_{\phi}\right\|_{2}\leqslant\frac{1}{1-|\lambda_{2}(\hat{\mathcal{T}})|}\left\|\hat{\mathcal{T}}-\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{T})}\mathcal{T}\right\|_{2\rightarrow 2}\left\|\rho_{\phi}\right\|_{2}\,,

and therefore, by the triangle inequality, that

\left\|\hat{\rho}\right\|_{2}\leqslant\left\|\rho_{\phi}\right\|_{2}+\left\|\hat{\rho}-\rho_{\phi}\right\|_{2}\leqslant\left(1+\frac{1}{1-|\lambda_{2}(\hat{\mathcal{T}})|}\left\|\hat{\mathcal{T}}-\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{T})}\mathcal{T}\right\|_{2\rightarrow 2}\right)\left\|\rho_{\phi}\right\|_{2}\,.

Now, first of all we know that, with probability larger than
1-e^{-cD}
,
|\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{T})|\leqslant 1+C/\sqrt{d}
, so that

\left\|\hat{\mathcal{T}}-\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{T})}\mathcal{T}\right\|_{2\rightarrow 2}\leqslant\left\|\hat{\mathcal{T}}-\mathcal{T}\right\|_{2\rightarrow 2}+\frac{2C}{\sqrt{d}}\left\|\mathcal{T}\right\|_{2\rightarrow 2}\leqslant\left\|\hat{\mathcal{T}}-\mathcal{T}\right\|_{2\rightarrow 2}+\frac{2C}{\sqrt{d}}\left(1+\frac{C}{\sqrt{d}}\right)\,.

Next, we know by Corollary
6.2
that, with probability larger than
1-e^{-cD}
,

\left\|\hat{\mathcal{T}}-\mathcal{T}\right\|_{2\rightarrow 2}\leqslant\frac{C}{\sqrt{d}}\,.

Since we also know that, with probability larger than
1-e^{-cD}
,
|\lambda_{2}(\mathcal{T})|\leqslant C/\sqrt{d}
, this implies that, with probability larger than
1-2e^{-cD}
,

|\lambda_{2}(\hat{\mathcal{T}})|\leqslant\frac{2C}{\sqrt{d}}\,.

Putting everything together we thus get that, with probability larger than
1-3e^{-cD}
,

\left\|\hat{\rho}\right\|_{2}\leqslant\left(1+\frac{C^{\prime}}{\sqrt{d}}\right)\left\|\rho_{\phi}\right\|_{2}\,.

The only thing that now remains to be done is to upper bound the typical value of
\|\rho_{\phi}\|_{2}
. Since
\|\rho_{\phi}\|_{2}=1/\|M_{\phi}\|_{1}
, we actually have to lower bound the typical value of
\|M_{\phi}\|_{1}
. Yet, we know that, with probability larger than
1-e^{-cD}
,

\left\|M_{\phi}\right\|_{1}=\left\|\frac{\mathrm{Id}}{\sqrt{D}}+\frac{C}{\sqrt{d}}M_{\phi^{\prime}}\right\|_{1}\geqslant\left\|\frac{\mathrm{Id}}{\sqrt{D}}\right\|_{1}-\frac{C}{\sqrt{d}}\left\|M_{\phi^{\prime}}\right\|_{1}\geqslant\left(1-\frac{C}{\sqrt{d}}\right)\sqrt{D}\,,

where the last inequality is because
\left\|M_{\phi^{\prime}}\right\|_{1}\leqslant\sqrt{D}\left\|M_{\phi^{\prime}}\right\|_{2}\leqslant\sqrt{D}
.

So in the end we have shown that, with probability larger than
1-4e^{-cD}
,

\left\|\hat{\rho}\right\|_{2}\leqslant\left(1+\frac{C^{\prime\prime}}{\sqrt{d}}\right)\frac{1}{\sqrt{D}}\,,

which (suitably re-labelling
c,C
) is precisely the advertised result.
∎

Theorem 6.4
.

Let
\hat{\mathcal{T}}
be the random CPTP map defined by equation (
28
). Then, with probability larger than
1-e^{-cD}
,
\hat{\mathcal{T}}
is a quantum expander with parameters
((1-C/\sqrt{d})D,d,C/\sqrt{d})
, where
c,C>0
are universal constants.

Proof.

The fact that
\hat{\mathcal{T}}
is
d
-regular is clear by definition.
The fact that, with probability larger than
1-e^{-cD}
,
|\lambda_{2}(\hat{\mathcal{T}})|\leqslant C/\sqrt{d}
, so that
\hat{\mathcal{T}}
is
(1-C/\sqrt{d})
-expanding, is established in the proof of Proposition
6.3
. So it only remains to lower bound the typical entropy of
\hat{\rho}
. By concavity of
\log
we have

S(\hat{\rho})=-\Tr(\hat{\rho}\log\hat{\rho})\geqslant-\log\Tr(\hat{\rho}^{2})\,.

Combining this observation with Proposition
6.3
we get that, with probability larger than
1-e^{-cD}
,

S(\hat{\rho})\geqslant-\log\left(\left(1+\frac{C}{\sqrt{d}}\right)^{2}\frac{1}{D}\right)\geqslant\log\left(\left(1-\frac{6C}{\sqrt{d}}\right)D\right)\,,

which concludes the proof (after suitably re-labelling
c,C
).
∎

6.2.
A model of random dissipative evolution

In the recent past, the analysis of local random circuits has triggered great interest. Very broadly speaking, the main question that one tries to answer in this field is: after which depth does the action of a circuit composed of local random unitaries ‘resembles’ that of a global random unitary (on any input many-body state)? Typical features of a global Haar-distributed unitary, that one would like to reproduce in a more economical way, include: scrambling
[
12
,
11
,
27
]
, decoupling
[
13
]
, entanglement spreading
[
24
,
25
]
etc. Such models for random reversible evolutions have now been studied quite extensively. But what about similar models for random dissipative evolutions?

Our random PEPS transfer operator, as defined by equation (
5
), actually provides one such model of random evolution in the open system picture. Indeed, as explained in Section
6.1
in the case of MPS, a random transfer operator can equivalently be seen (after renormalization) as a random quantum channel, i.e. a random evolution of a system coupled to an environment. What is more, the quantum channel corresponding to a PEPS transfer operator is by construction acting on its input many-body state with some locality constraints, as a local circuit in the case of isolated systems. The only issue is that, in this particular context of chaotic quantum dynamics, a non translation-invariant model is usually more relevant than a translation-invariant one.

So let us start with explaining how the results of Section
4.3
can be extended to the case of non translation-invariant random PEPS. The model that we are now considering is constructed in a very similar way to the one we have been looking at up to here: the only difference is that we sample the
N^{2}

1
-site tensors independently from one another, each being distributed as the
1
-site tensor defined by equation (
3
). Let us denote by
T_{N}^{\prime}
the corresponding transfer operator, i.e.

(29)

T_{N}^{\prime}:=\frac{1}{D^{N}}\sum_{a_{1},b_{1},\ldots,a_{N},b_{N}=1}^{D}\frac{1}{d^{N}}\sum_{x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}=1}^{d}G_{a_{N}a_{1}x_{1}}^{1}\otimes\bar{G}_{b_{N}b_{1}x_{1}}^{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes G_{a_{N-1}a_{N}x_{N}}^{N}\otimes\bar{G}_{b_{N-1}b_{N}x_{N}}^{N}\,,

where the
G_{a_{i-1}a_{i}x_{i}}^{i}
’s are independent
D\times D
matrices whose entries are independent complex Gaussians with mean
0
and variance
1/D
.

The analogues of Propositions
4.18
and
4.22
now read as follows.

Proposition 6.5
.

Let
T_{N}^{\prime}
be defined as in equation (
29
). Then,
\langle\psi^{\otimes N}|T_{N}^{\prime}|\psi^{\otimes N}\rangle\in\mathbf{R}
and there exist universal constants
C,c>0
such that

\mathbf{P}\left(\left|\langle\psi^{\otimes N}|T_{N}^{\prime}|\psi^{\otimes N}\rangle-1\right|\leqslant\frac{CN}{\sqrt{d}}+D^{2}\left(\frac{C}{\sqrt{d}}\right)^{N}\right)\geqslant 1-Ne^{-cD^{3}}\,.

Proposition 6.6
.

Let
T_{N}^{\prime}
be defined as in equation (
29
). Then, there exist universal constants
C,c>0
such that, for all
\sqrt{d}\geqslant D
,

\mathbf{P}\left(\left\|T_{N}^{\prime}(\mathrm{Id}-|\psi^{\otimes N}\rangle\!\langle\psi^{\otimes N}|)\right\|_{\infty}\leqslant(1+\eta)\left(1+\frac{CD}{\sqrt{d}}\right)^{N}\frac{CN}{\sqrt{d}}\right)\geqslant 1-N^{2}D^{N}e^{-cD^{3}/d}\,,

where
\eta\equiv\eta(N,d,D)=d^{N}N^{2N}(1+C/\sqrt{d})^{N}e^{-cD^{3}/d}
.

Note that the only difference with Propositions
4.18
and
4.22
is an extra
N
factor in the deviation probabilities. This is quite intuitive to understand: contrary to
T_{N}
which is made out of one single random tensor repeated
N
times,
T_{N}^{\prime}
is made of
N
independent random tensors, so that deviation probabilities for each of them have to somehow add up. We will not fully redo the proofs in this non translation-invariant case, but simply explain how the proofs in the translation-invariant case have to be modified.

Sketch of proof of Proposition
6.5
.

Similarly to the proof of Proposition
4.18
, we begin with observing that

\langle\psi^{\otimes N}|T_{N}^{\prime}|\psi^{\otimes N}\rangle=\Tr\left(\tilde{T}_{1}\cdots\tilde{T}_{N}\right)\,,

where the
\tilde{T}_{i}
’s are independent and distributed as
T
with
\tilde{d}=D^{2}d
and
\tilde{D}=D
. Next, for each
1\leqslant i\leqslant N
, write
\tilde{T}_{i}
as
\tilde{T}_{i}=\lambda_{i}|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|+\epsilon_{i}M_{i}
with
\langle\psi|M_{i}|\psi\rangle=0
and
\|M_{i}\|_{\infty}\leqslant 1
. We thus have

\Tr\left(\tilde{T}_{1}\cdots\tilde{T}_{N}\right)=\lambda_{1}\cdots\lambda_{N}+\epsilon_{1}\cdots\epsilon_{N}\Tr(M_{1}\cdots M_{N})\,.

Now, we know from Propositions
4.13
and
4.14
that, for each
1\leqslant i\leqslant N
, with probability larger than
1-e^{-cD^{3}}
,
|\lambda_{i}-1|\leqslant C/\sqrt{d}
and
|\epsilon_{i}|\leqslant C/\sqrt{d}
. Therefore, with probability larger than
1-Ne^{-cD^{3}}
,
the two following hold

\langle\psi^{\otimes N}|T_{N}^{\prime}|\psi^{\otimes N}\rangle\geqslant\left(1-\frac{C}{\sqrt{d}}\right)^{N}-D^{2}\left(\frac{C}{\sqrt{d}}\right)^{N}\ \text{and}\ \langle\psi^{\otimes N}|T_{N}^{\prime}|\psi^{\otimes N}\rangle\leqslant\left(1+\frac{C}{\sqrt{d}}\right)^{N}+D^{2}\left(\frac{C}{\sqrt{d}}\right)^{N}\,,

which is precisely what we wanted to show.
∎

Sketch of proof of Proposition
6.6
.

Our first claim is: Lemma
4.20
holds exactly the same when, instead of having only
d
independent matrices
G_{x}
’s, one has
dN
independent matrices
G_{x}^{i}
’s, only replacing
3N
by
N^{2}
in the deviation probability. Indeed, the induction proof works exactly alike. The only thing that changes is that we now have to define

Y:=\left\|\frac{1}{d}\sum_{x=1}^{d}G_{x}\otimes\bar{G}_{x}\right\|_{\infty}\ \text{and}\ X_{n}:=\left\|\frac{1}{d^{n}}\sum_{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}=1}^{d}\left(\underset{i=1}{\overset{n}{\bigotimes}}\,G_{x_{i}}^{i}\otimes\bar{G}_{x_{i}}^{i}-\underset{i=1}{\overset{n}{\bigotimes}}\,H_{x_{i}}^{i}\otimes\bar{H}_{x_{i}}^{i}\right)\right\|_{\infty}\,,

and the upper bound (
19
) becomes

X_{n+1}\leqslant Y_{1}\cdots Y_{n}X_{1}+X_{n}^{\prime}Y_{1}^{\prime}\,.

Then, we know on the one hand that
Y>(1+\epsilon)(1+C/\sqrt{d})
with probability at most
e^{-cDd\min(\epsilon,\epsilon^{2})}
, so that also, clearly,
Y_{1}\cdots Y_{n}>(1+\epsilon)^{n}(1+C/\sqrt{d})^{n}
with probability at most
ne^{-cDd\min(\epsilon,\epsilon^{2})}
. While we know on the other hand that, by recursion hypothesis,
X_{1}>(1+\epsilon)C/\sqrt{d}
with probability at most
e^{-cDd\min(\epsilon/\sqrt{d},\epsilon^{2}/d)}
and
X_{n}>(1+\epsilon)^{n}(1+C/\sqrt{d})^{n}Cn/\sqrt{d}
with probability at most
n^{2}e^{-cDd\min(\epsilon/\sqrt{d},\epsilon^{2}/d)}
. This implies, exactly as wanted, that
X_{n+1}>(1+\epsilon)^{n+1}(1+C/\sqrt{d})^{n+1}C(n+1)/\sqrt{d}
with probability at most
(n+1)^{2}e^{-cDd\min(\epsilon/\sqrt{d},\epsilon^{2}/d)}
.

It then immediately follows that also Corollary
4.21
holds exactly the same when, instead of having only
d
independent matrices
G_{x}
’s, one has
dN
independent matrices
G_{x}^{i}
’s, only replacing
3N
by
N^{2}
in the deviation probability. And from there we deduce that Proposition
4.22
as well holds exactly the same for
T_{N}^{\prime}
instead of
T_{N}
, only replacing
4N(D+1)^{N}
by, say,
2N^{2}(D+1)^{N}
in the deviation probability.
∎

From Propositions
6.5
and
6.6
, one can then straightforwardly derive the analogue of Theorem
4.23
in this independent case, as stated below.

Theorem 6.7
.

There exist universal constants
C,c>0
such that, for all
\sqrt{d}\geqslant D
,

\mathbf{P}\left(\Delta(T_{N}^{\prime})\geqslant 1-(1+\eta)\left(1+\frac{CD}{\sqrt{d}}\right)^{N}\frac{CN}{\sqrt{d}}+D^{2}\left(\frac{C}{\sqrt{d}}\right)^{N}\right)\geqslant 1-N^{2}D^{N}e^{-cD^{3}/d}\,,

where
\eta\equiv\eta(N,d,D)=d^{N}N^{2N}(1+C/\sqrt{d})^{N}e^{-cD^{3}/d}
.

In particular, there exist universal constants
C^{\prime},c^{\prime}>0
such that, if

and

with

and

, then

\mathbf{P}\left(\Delta(T_{N}^{\prime})\geqslant 1-\frac{C^{\prime}}{N^{\alpha/2-\beta-1}}\right)\geqslant 1-e^{-c^{\prime}N^{3\beta-\alpha}}\,.

This lower bound on the typical spectral gap of the PEPS transfer operator
T_{N}^{\prime}
, together with the knowledge that
|\psi^{\otimes N}\rangle
is typically close to its largest eigenvalue eigenvector, would now allow for an analysis quite similar to that carried on in Section
6.1
for the MPS transfer operator
T
. In particular, one could study the following questions: How close typically is the fixed point of the quantum channel
\hat{\mathcal{T}}_{N}^{\prime}
, corresponding to

, to the maximally mixed state? And when iteratively applying
\hat{\mathcal{T}}_{N}^{\prime}
to an input state, how fast does the latter typically converge towards this fixed point?

Miscellaneous final comments

Let us start with a few comments on the results of Section
4
, about the typical spectral gap of random transfer operators. In the MPS case, we know that the scaling we obtain for the spectral gap is optimal. In the PEPS case though, the statements that we are able to make remain not fully satisfying. The main open question clearly is: could these results be improved so that
d,D
growing polynomially with
N
is not needed? With our current proof techniques, the exponents in this polynomial dependence could be optimized. But getting rid of this limitation would require totally different arguments.
Hence for now, the transfer operator approach does not seem to be the most suited to tackle the case of higher dimensional regular lattices. Indeed, even though it is extremely powerful in dimension
1
, it is doomed to yield results which are not independent of the system size in dimension
2
or higher.

Concerning the results of Section
2
, they are likely to be sub-optimal in both the MPS and PEPS cases. Indeed, there is no a priori obstruction for the validity regime
d>D^{\theta}
to be improved to
\theta=2
for MPS and
\theta=4
for PEPS. So it would be nice to be able to get closer to this regime. It is indeed clear what are the two points in the proofs where we probably lose something. First it is when upper bounding the operator norm of realigned random matrices (Proposition
2.3
in the case of MPS): we pick up local dimension factors which are quite likely not to be necessary. Second it is when upper bounding the operator norm of approximate ground space projectors on the complement of the ground space by their trace norm (Proposition
2.8
in the case of MPS). In both cases, getting upper bounds with the optimal order of magnitude would require a careful analysis of the specific random matrix models under consideration. Using similar techniques as those used in the proofs of Section
4.2
, this does not seem out of reach.
What is more, a nice feature of the parent Hamiltonian approach is that it can a priori be easily generalized to any regular lattice.

Let us also say, about this parent Hamiltonian analysis, that it could in principle be generalized to non-injective random MPS and PEPS. For instance, if our random MPS is in a dimensional regime where it is almost surely injective only after blocking together
K
sites (with
K>1
), then its associated canonical parent Hamiltonian is not
2
-local but
(K+1)
-local. However, its construction remains entirely similar, and the strategy to try and lower bound its spectral gap as well. So we believe that the reasoning would carry through. It would just be more cumbersome, which is why we have restricted ourselves to writing it down properly only under the injectivity assumption. Of course, this guess that our results would generalize in a quite straightforward way to
K
-injective random MPS is only for
K
a fixed constant. Instead, if
K
were to depend on other parameters involved (such as
d,D,N
), then the analysis could become much more subtle.

These results on random parent Hamiltonians being typically gapped obviously trigger a new question: instead of constructing a translation-invariant ground state at random and then studying the spectral properties of the corresponding local translation-invariant Hamiltonian, what about directly constructing the Hamiltonian at random? This viewpoint is the one adopted in
[
38
]
. It would be interesting to see if the results in the latter could be extended to non frustration-free situations (i.e. to situations which are outside of the parent Hamiltonian picture).

Another, very different, route that one could explore is how to change our model of random MPS and PEPS in a meaningful (but manageable) manner? A natural idea would be to sample the
1
-site tensors in a non unitarily-invariant way. Indeed, in a model where either physical or bond indices would be favoured, or even some bond indices compared to others, interesting phenomena might arise. But being able to attack such problem seems to be quite challenging.
It would also be useful to understand what happens when some symmetry is imposed on the random
1
-site tensors (i.e. when the latter are sampled under the constraint that they are invariant under the action of some group). This situation of MPS and PEPS exhibiting a local symmetry is indeed very important in practice
[
17
]
.
