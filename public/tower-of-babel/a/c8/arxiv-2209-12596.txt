Convergence guarantees for coefficient reconstruction in PDEs from boundary measurements by variational and Newton type methods via range invariance
Barbara Kaltenbacher*

Abstract
A key observation underlying this paper is the fact that the range invariance condition for convergence of regularization methods for nonlinear ill-posed operator equations – such as coefficient identification in partial differential equations PDEs from boundary observations – can often be achieved by extending the searched for parameter in the sense of allowing it to depend on additional variables. This clearly counteracts unique identifiability of the parameter, though. The second key idea of this paper is now to restore the original restricted dependency of the parameter by penalization. This is shown to lead to convergence of variational (Tikhonov type) and iterative (Newton type) regularization methods. We concretize the abstract convergence analysis in a framework typical of parameter identification in PDEs in a reduced and an all-at-once setting. This is further illustrated by three examples of coefficient identification from boundary observations in elliptic and time dependent PDEs.

Introduction

A proof of convergence of iterative methods for parameter identification problems in partial differential equations PDEs from boundary measurements, as relevant, e.g., in tomographic applications, has been a long-standing open problem due to the fact that the convergence analysis of these methods can only be carried out under restrictions on the nonlinearity of the forward operator that could not be verified for such PDE coefficient identification problems so far. Likewise, although at a first glance not burdened with such restrictive assumptions on the forward operator, Tikhonov regularization requires the computation of a global minimizer of a functional whose (local) convexity can only be verified under similar restricions on the nonlinearity.

The goal of this paper is to revisit one of these conditions -- range invariance of the linearized forward operator -- and show that an
introduction of additional/artificial degrees of freedom in
the searched for parameter

1
1

1

we will shortly call this “extension”

often allows for its verification. Since this counteracts unique identifiability of the parameter, we restore the original restricted dependency of the parameter by a proper penalization within the reconstruction method.

\forall x,\tilde{x}\in U\,\exists R(x,\tilde{x})\in L(X,X)\,:\ R(x,\tilde{x})^{-1}\in L(X,X)\mbox{ and }F^{\prime}(x)=F^{\prime}(\tilde{x})R(x,\tilde{x}).

The validity of range invariance for arbitrary observation operator

1.1
Examples

For further motivation, we provide three classical examples of parameter identification problems where range invariance of the linearization of the forward operator can be verified.
All the computations will be completely formal; for details we refer to Appendix
A
.

As can be clearly seen there, in the all-at-once setting we have much more freedom in choosing the function spaces as compared to the reduced case, where we are bound to PDE solution theory in order to establish the parameter-to-state map
S

Example 1

(identification of a potential in an elliptic PDE)
.

(For the reduced case (
1.2
), see, e.g.,
[
31
, Example 3.2]
).
Consider identification of the spatially varying potential
q\in L^{2}(\Omega)
in the elliptic boundary value problem

\displaystyle-\Delta u+q(x)u

\displaystyle=f\mbox{ in }\Omega

(1.11)

\displaystyle\partial_{\nu}u

\displaystyle=h\mbox{ on }\partial\Omega

with given
f
,
h
,
from observations
y=Cu
of

, for example measurements at the boundary of the domain
\Omega
.

With an extension
\bar{h}
of the boundary data according to (
A.3
),
we can write the inverse problem in a reduced form (
1.2
) as
\mathbf{F}(q)=y
, or in an all-at-once form (
1.1
) as
\mathbb{F}(q,u)=(0,y)^{T}
with the respective reduced and all-at-once forward operators being defined by

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}(q)=CS(q)=-C(-\Delta_{N}+q\cdot)^{-1}[f+\bar{h}],

(1.12)

\displaystyle\mathbb{F}(q,u)=(-\Delta_{N}u+q\cdot u-f-\bar{h},\,Cu)^{T}.

where
\Delta_{N}
denotes the Neumann Laplacian (
A.2
).

The linearizations of
\mathbf{F}
and
\mathbb{F}
are formally given by

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}^{\prime}(q)\underline{dq}=-C(-\Delta_{N}+q\cdot)^{-1}\Bigl[\underline{dq}\cdot S(q)\Bigr],

(1.13)

\displaystyle\mathbb{F}^{\prime}(q,u)(\underline{dq},\underline{du})=(-\Delta_{N}\underline{du}+q\cdot\underline{du}+\underline{dq}\cdot u,\,C\underline{du})^{T},

and can easily be verified to be Fréchet derivatives in the function space setting given in (
A.5
), (
A.6
).
For any fixed

, setting

\displaystyle\mathbf{R}(q)\underline{dq}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{S(q_{0})}\cdot\bigl((-\Delta_{N}+q_{0}\cdot)\,(-\Delta_{N}+q\cdot)^{-1}[\underline{dq}\cdot S(q)]\bigr)

(1.14)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{S(q_{0})}\cdot\bigl(\underline{dq}\cdot S(q)+(q-q_{0})\cdot S^{\prime}(q)\underline{dq}\bigr)

\displaystyle\mathbb{R}(q,u)(\underline{dq},\underline{du})

\displaystyle=\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{u_{0}}\bigl(\underline{dq}\cdot u+(q-q_{0})\cdot\underline{du}\bigr),\,\underline{du}\Bigr)

we get

(1.15)

where boundedness away from zero of the denominators can be guaranteed by maximum principles for
S(q_{0})
and simply by a proper choice for

.
One can also prove (see
(
A.7
)) that

\|\mathbf{R}(q)-\textup{id}\|_{Q\to Q}\leq C\|q-q_{0}\|_{Q},\quad\|\mathbb{R}(q,u)-\textup{id}\|_{Q\times V\to Q\times V}\leq C\|(q,u)-(q_{0},u_{0})\|_{Q\times V},

so that in a sufficiently small neighborhood

of

(or of
(q_{0},u_{0})
, respectively), the
R
operators are bounded and boundedly invertible, which implies range invariance of the linearizations

\forall q\in U\,:\ \textup{rng}(\mathbf{F}^{\prime}(q))=\textup{rng}(\mathbf{F}^{\prime}(q_{0})),\quad\forall(q,u)\in U\,:\ \textup{rng}(\mathbb{F}^{\prime}(q,u))=\textup{rng}(\mathbb{F}^{\prime}(q_{0},u_{0})),

no matter which observation operator
C
is contained in the definition of
\mathbf{F}
,
\mathbb{F}
.
Note that here, besides linearity and boundedness
C\in L(V,Y)
, we do not make any assumptions on the observation operator
C
.
This example has also been shown to satisfy the tangential cone condition in the reduced setting (
1.2
)
[
21
]
, however only with full measurements, that is,

being the embedding operator

Thus, range invariance is easy to verify here, even in case of restricted measurements, as opposed to the tangential cone condition or adjoint range invariance. Still, keeping also unique identifiability of

in mind, the operator

should not be too restrictive either.
For example by a straightforward dimension count, uniqueness from a single boundary observation cannot be expected to hold here and in fact the full Neumann-to-Dirichlet map (see (
4.1
) below) assigning to any prescribed boundary flux

the corresponding Dirichlet data

can be shown to yield uniqueness (see
[
28
]
for

,
[
45
]
for
d\geq 3

Example 2

(identification of a potential in a
time-dependent

.

We add a time dimension by considering the

version of Example
1

\displaystyle D_{t}^{M}u-\Delta u+q(x)u

\displaystyle=f\mbox{ in }\Omega\times(0,T)

\displaystyle\partial_{\nu}u

\displaystyle=h\mbox{ on }\partial\Omega\times(0,T)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}^{m}u(x,0)

\displaystyle=u_{m}(x)\ x\in\Omega\quad m\in\{0,\ldots,m-1\}

Here besides the parabolic case with

\displaystyle\mathbb{F}(q,u)=(u^{\prime}-\Delta_{N}u+q\cdot(u+\bar{h}),\,Cu)^{T}\,.

Analogously to the above elliptic example (more or less just substituting

Note that even if

In this example, uniqueness of

from a single time trace on the boundary or final time measurements of

C=\textup{tr}_{\Gamma\times(0,T)}\ \textup{ or }\ C=\textup{tr}_{\Omega\times\{T\}}

can be shown

,

]
.
However, as we have just seen, establishing range invariance requires extension of the parameter space, which leads to a loss of uniqueness.
Therefore, we will introduce a restriction procedure by penalization, that allows to show convergence to the unique solution of the original inverse problem (that is, with the original smaller parameter space).
In this example, this can be done, e.g., by means of the functional

\mathcal{P}(q):=\|Pq\|_{Z}^{2}

Example 3

(identification of a diffusion coefficient in an elliptic PDE)
.

We now consider identification of the coefficient
a(x)
in

\displaystyle-\nabla\cdot(a(x)\nabla u)

\displaystyle=f\mbox{ in }\Omega

(1.21)

\displaystyle a(x)\partial_{\nu}u

\displaystyle=h\mbox{ on }\partial\Omega.

with given
f
,
h
.
In case
f=0
and with the physical interpretation of

being an electric potential,

the conductivity, and the measurements defined by all voltage-current pairs achievable by excitation at electrodes on the boundary, this is the well-known and well-investigated Calderón problem with application in electric impedance tomography EIT.

With general measurements defined by a linear operator
C
, this can be written in a reduced (
1.2
) or in an all-at-once form (
1.1
) with

and

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}(a)=CS(a)=-C(-\Delta_{a,N})^{-1}[f+\bar{h}],

(1.22)

\displaystyle\mathbb{F}(a,u)=(-\Delta_{a,N}u-f-\bar{h},\,Cu)^{T},

where

\langle-\Delta_{a,N}u,v\rangle_{H^{1}(\Omega)^{*},H^{1}(\Omega)}:=\int_{\Omega}a\,\nabla u\cdot\nabla v\,dx\quad\forall u,v\in H^{1}(\Omega)

(1.23)

and
\bar{h}
is defined by (

).

Here, verification of (

) (and likewise of (

)) only works out in one space dimension. To see this, we consider the derivatives

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}^{\prime}(a)\underline{da}=-C(-\Delta_{a,N})^{-1}\Bigl[\Delta_{\underline{da},N}S(a)\Bigr],

\displaystyle\mathbb{F}^{\prime}(a,u)(\underline{da},\underline{du})=(-\Delta_{a,N}\underline{du}-\Delta_{\underline{da},N}u,\,C\underline{du})^{T},

and write the sufficient condition for (

vary in space and differ from each other, an estimate of the

\|\nabla\underline{da}\|_{L^{t}(\Omega)}\|\nabla S(a)\|_{L^{s}(\Omega)}

) only provides directional derivatives of

2
Structure exploiting reconstruction methods and their convergence

We return to the general inverse problem (

Rather than working with (

Conclusion and Outlook

While the use of range invariance conditions for proving convergence of regularization methods is known in the literature, their use has so far largely been limited to relatively simple model problems – as is the case for other restrictions on the nonlinearity of the forward operator (such as the tangential cone condition) used in the analysis of solution methods for ill-posed problems.
In this paper, we suggest to overcome this limitation by possibly augmenting the dimensions of variability of the searched for parameter, thus allowing to establish validity of this condition for a much larger class of inverse problems, which indeed contains practically relevant examples.
The loss of uniqueness, which also impacts convergence of these methods (since the initial guess needs to be orthogonal to the nullspace of the linearized forward operator) is overcome by adding a penalty that in the limit enforces the original lower dimensional variability.

The analysis is carried out in a general framework (Section
2
) that covers both reduced and all-at-once formulations of inverse problems as demonstrated in Section
3
and contains some practically relevant inverse problems of reconstruction from boundary observations as detailed in Section
4
.

While – for clarity of exposition – we stayed with norm based misfit functionals in most of this paper, extension to more general functional should be considered in view of their practical usefulness for, e.g., modeling different types of data noise.

Also convergence rates under source conditions will be subject of future research.

As above we can avoid imposing conditions for existence of a minimizer and computing it exactly by adding a tolerance

Comparison with

(C.2)

\displaystyle+\beta_{n}\Bigl(\|r^{\prime}(x_{n}^{\delta})(x_{n+1}^{\delta}-x^{\dagger})+\textup{res}_{\textup{Taylor}}+r(x^{\dagger})-\hat{r}_{n+1}^{\delta}\|_{\widetilde{X}}^{b}-\|\textup{res}_{\textup{Taylor}}\|_{\widetilde{X}}^{b}\Bigr)+\mathcal{P}(x_{n+1}^{\delta})

\displaystyle\leq\delta^{p}+\eta_{n},

where we have used

\displaystyle\textup{res}_{\textup{Taylor}}:=

\displaystyle r(x_{n}^{\delta})-r(x^{\dagger})+r^{\prime}(x_{n}^{\delta})(x^{\dagger}-x_{n}^{\delta}),

\displaystyle\textup{res}_{\textup{Newton}}:=

\displaystyle r(x_{n}^{\delta})-r(x^{\dagger})+r^{\prime}(x_{n}^{\delta})(x_{n+1}^{\delta}-x_{n}^{\delta})=r^{\prime}(x_{n}^{\delta})(x_{n+1}^{\delta}-x^{\dagger})+\textup{res}_{\textup{Taylor}}.

However, the connection between

and

sufficiently large, enforces the corresponding constraint

into the data misfit term and the regularization term, the definition of the Newton iterate reduces to

\displaystyle x_{n+1}^{\delta}\in\mbox{argmin}_{x\in\widetilde{X}\times U}J_{n,1}^{\delta}(x)

(C.3)

\displaystyle\mbox{where }J_{n,1}^{\delta}(x):=\|K(r(x_{n}^{\delta})+r^{\prime}(x_{n}^{\delta})(x-x_{n}^{\delta}))+F(x_{0})-y^{\delta}\|_{Y}^{p}

\displaystyle+\alpha_{n}\mathcal{R}(r(x_{n}^{\delta})+r^{\prime}(x_{n}^{\delta})(x-x_{n}^{\delta}))+\mathcal{P}(x),

where the data misfit term can be rewritten as

Thus, as compared to (

\tilde{X}
.
As compared to the Levenberg-Marquardt Method (or the iteratively regularized Gauss-Newton method), see, e.g.
[

\displaystyle+\alpha_{n}\Bigl(\mathcal{R}(r(x^{\dagger})+r^{\prime}(x_{n}^{\delta})(x_{n+1}^{\delta}-x^{\dagger})+\textup{res}_{\textup{Taylor}})-\mathcal{R}(r(x^{\dagger})+\textup{res}_{\textup{Taylor}})\Bigr)+\mathcal{P}(x_{n+1}^{\delta})\leq\delta^{p}.

Deriving a contractive recursion on

\displaystyle\leq\|B_{0}^{-1}\|_{W^{*},\widetilde{Q}^{(0)}_{J}}\|[(B^{\prime}(u^{\dagger})-B^{\prime}(u))\underline{du}]q^{\dagger}+[B^{\prime}(u)\underline{du}](q^{\dagger}-q)+[B(u^{\dagger})-B(u)]\underline{dq}\|_{W^{*}_{J}}.

Appendix D
Linearized uniqueness proofs

D.1
Nullspace condition (
3.6
) for the elliptic case in Example
5

We use the fact that
\mathbf{F}^{\prime}(q_{0})\underline{dq}=0
with
\underline{du}^{j}=(S^{j})^{\prime}(q_{0})\underline{dq}
,
u_{0}^{j}=S^{j}(q_{0})
reads as

\int_{\Omega}\Bigl(\nabla\underline{du}^{j}\cdot\nabla w+q_{0}\underline{du}^{j}w+\underline{dq}u_{0}^{j}w\Bigr)\,dx=0\,,\forall w\in W:=H^{1}(\Omega),\quad\textup{tr}_{\partial\Omega}\underline{du}^{j}=0\qquad j\in\mathbb{N}.

From this, using integration by parts, we conclude that

\forall j\in\mathbb{N}\,\forall w\in M_{q_{0}}:=\{v\in H^{2}(\Omega)\,:\,-\Delta v+q_{0}\cdot v=0\}\,:\int_{\Omega}\underline{dq}u^{j}w\,dx=0.

(Note that no boundary conditions are prescribed on
v\in M_{q_{0}}
.)
Since the boundary values
\varphi^{j}
of
u_{0}^{j}
run over a basis of
H^{-1/2}(\partial\Omega)
, this is equivalent to

\forall w_{1},\,w_{2}\,\in M_{q_{0}}:\int_{\Omega}\underline{dq}\,w_{1}\,w_{2}\,dx=0,

which means that
\underline{dq}
is orthogonal to the set of products of functions in
M_{q_{0}}
. This set has been shown to be dense in
L^{2}(\Omega)
, see, e.g.,
[
41
, Theorem 6.25]
, which implies
\underline{dq}=0
.

D.2
Nullspace condition (
3.6
) for the transient case in Example
5

\mathbb{F}^{\prime}(q_{0},u_{0})(\underline{dq},\underline{du})=0
is equivalent to
(D+q_{0}\cdot)\underline{du}+\underline{dq}\cdot(u_{0}+\bar{h})=0
and
C\underline{du}=0
, where we assume
(u_{0}+\bar{h})(x,t)=\phi(x)\psi(t)
.
Taking the
L^{2}_{w}
inner product with
\varphi_{i}^{k}
as well as the Laplace transform with respect to time, and defining the complex functions
\omega_{\lambda_{i}}(z):=\sum_{m=1}^{M}z^{m}\widehat{\mathcal{M}}_{m}(z)+\sum_{n=0}^{N}z^{n}\widehat{\mathcal{N}}_{n}(z)*\lambda_{i}^{\beta_{n}}
yields

\displaystyle\omega_{\lambda_{i}}(z)\,\widehat{\underline{du}}_{i}^{k}(z)=-\langle\underline{dq}\cdot\phi,\varphi_{i}^{k}\rangle_{L^{2}_{w}(\Omega)}\widehat{\psi}(z),\ \ i\in\mathbb{N},\ k\in K^{i},\quad\textup{ and }\ \sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\sum_{k\in K^{j}}\widehat{\underline{du}}_{j}^{k}(z)\,C\varphi_{j}^{k}=0

for
\underline{du}_{i}^{k}:=\langle\underline{du},\varphi_{i}^{k}\rangle_{L^{2}_{w}(\Omega)}
, and all
z\in\mathbb{C}
, which can be rewritten as a system

\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{\widehat{\psi}(z)}{\omega_{\lambda_{j}}(z)}\sum_{k\in K^{j}}\,a_{j}^{k}\,C\varphi_{j}^{k}\,=0

(D.1)

(valid for all
z\in\mathbb{C}
except for the roots of
\omega_{\lambda_{j}}
,
j\in\mathbb{N}
)
for the coefficients
a_{i}^{k}:=\langle\underline{dq}\cdot\phi,\varphi_{i}^{k}\rangle_{L^{2}_{w}(\Omega)}
.
In order to first of all disentangle the sum over
j\in\mathbb{N}
, we assume that the poles of
\frac{1}{\omega_{\lambda}}
differ for different
\lambda
, that is

\forall i\in\mathbb{N}\,\exists p_{i}\in\mathbb{C}\,:\ \omega_{\lambda_{i}}(p_{i})=0\text{ and }\forall j\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{i\}\,:\ \omega_{\lambda_{j}}(p_{i})\not=0.

(D.2)

Taking the residues of (
D.1
) we can thus use the fact that

\textup{Res}(\frac{1}{\omega_{\lambda_{i}}};p_{i})\not=0
and

\textup{Res}(\frac{1}{\omega_{\lambda_{j}}};p_{i})=0
for
j\not=i
, which yields

\forall i\in\mathbb{N}\,:\widehat{\psi}(p_{i})\sum_{k\in K^{i}}\,a_{i}^{k}\,C\varphi_{i}^{k}\,=0

(D.3)

We additionally suppose that
\psi
was chosen such that
\widehat{\psi}(p_{i})\not=0
for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
and make the linear independence assumption on the individual eigenspaces

\forall i\in\mathbb{N}\,\Bigl(\forall\vec{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{K^{i}}\,:\ \sum_{k\in K^{i}}\,b^{k}\,C\varphi_{i}^{k}\,=0\ \Rightarrow\ \vec{b}=0\Bigr).

(In the context of
C:=\textup{tr}_{\Gamma}
this means that the eigenfunctions should not lose their linear independence when restricted to the observation set
\Gamma
; see
[
38
, Remark 4.1]

for some examples and comments.)
This allows to conclude that for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
,
k\in K^{i}
, we have
a_{i}^{k}=0
and thus
\underline{dq}\cdot\phi=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\sum_{k\in K^{j}}\,a_{j}^{k}\,C\varphi_{j}^{k}\,=0
. Having chosen
\phi
nonzero almost everywhere, we can conclude
\underline{dq}=0
.

Condition (
D.2
) can be easily verified in the parabolic and hyperbolic cases, but also in certain fractional damping models, cf., e.g,
[
38
]
.

D.3

\mathbf{F}^{\prime}(q_{0})\underline{dq}=0
