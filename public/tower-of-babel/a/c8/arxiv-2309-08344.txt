Complete embedded constant mean curvature surfaces in euclidean three space with prescribed topologies
Stephen J. Kleene

Abstract
. In this article, we construct complete embedded constant mean curvature surfaces in ℝ 3 \mathbb{R}^{3} with freely prescribed genus and any number of ends greater than or equal to four. Heuristically, the surfaces are obtained by resolving finitely many points of tangency between collections of spheres. The construction relies a family of constant mean curvature surfaces constructed in [ 14 ] , constructed as graphs over catenoidal necks of small scale.

[

[
3

More precisely, they construct embedded surfaces with prescribed numbers of ends, and for each fixed number of ends embedded surfaces of finitely many distinct topological types are constructed. [

[
4

are constrained by the fact that they are modeled on families of weighted graphs, in which the nodes of the graph are replaced by spheres and edges are modeled on Delaunay ends with length approximately equal to an integer weight. The topology of the surface is then inherited from that of the underlying graph. The graphs must be carefully constructed in order to ensure embeddness of the resulting surfaces. In
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, the surfaces constructed in
[
3
]
have genus
2k-5

[
4

[
3

can be modified to obtain additional topological types for each

, although the specifics are not mentioned. In contrast, the geometry of the surfaces we construct is comparatively simple and is easy to describe in terms of their limiting configuration. In the case of four ends and maximal symmetry, the surfaces depend on a single parameter, and as the parameter tends to zero they converge to a singular limit comprising two tangent Delaunay ends differing by a translation. The surfaces can thought of has having been obtained by removing finitely many of the points of tangency and replacing them with catenoidal bridges. A basic modification of this idea introduces more ends into the construction. Since the ends of the limiting configuration are not separated, it is not immediately obvious that the surfaces we construct are in fact embedded, and to establish this a careful treatment of the linearized problem is needed. Although we mention Delaunay ends above, they do not appear directly in our construction. Rather, we construct our surfaces by resolving the points of tangency of configurations of tangentially touching spheres with catenoidal necks of small scale. Also, we remark that, although the surfaces we construct have a planar symmetry, no symmetry is required by our method.

1.1.
General comments on the proof

The general approach of our construction has its origins in the work of Mazzeo-Pacard in
[
17
]
. A novel feature of their approach, at least in the context of singular perturbations, is the use of the Dirichlet to Neumann operator in matching the Cauchy data of various component surfaces. This is in contrast with the approach developed by Kapouleas, in which families of globally defined approximate solutions are constructed and the mapping properties of the jacobi operator are understood globally. Both types of constructions are carried out subject to one or more parameters, which we refer to here as the
scale
of the construction and denote by
\tau
, and which is assumed to small subject to various constraints. For small values of
\tau
, the relevant error term is shown to be sufficiently small, and the linearized problem sufficiently regular, so that a version of Newton’s Method can be employed to find exact solutions. A general feature of these constructions is that the problem does not extend
\tau=0
, which has to do with the fact that surfaces tend to singular limits as the scale
\tau
tends to zero, and uniform
C^{1}
estimates for the problem do not hold at small scales. Thus, perturbing away from
\tau=0
using methods requiring differentiability in
\tau
are not available, and instead arguments relying on continuity, and their attendant technical estimates, must be substituted.

The principal advance offered by our construction is a formulation of the problem that extends differentiably to
\tau=0
. This allows for a direct use of the implict function theorem and abstract appeals to differentiabilty, and obviates the need for many of the technical estimates that are required when only continuity is available. This allows us to perturb the problem differentiably away from scale zero and obtain improved estimates for solutions in terms of the scale. These improved estimates are fundamental to our construction, which relies on the ability to linearize in
\tau
at
\tau=0
, and would not be possible otherwise. Our formulation relies on a family of constant mean curvature necks constructed in
[
14
]
, whose relavant properties are discussed in detail in Section
2
.

1.2.
Notation, terminology and conventions

In this article, we mostly follow generally established notation that is widely in use, and thus we will avoid commenting extensively on it. So, for example,
\mathbb{R}^{n}
denotes

as canonically included in

to mean that the quantities

and

are equal up to a fixed positive constant. We use

to mean that the ratio

tends

f
and
g

We take the mean curvature of an oriented surface

in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
to be the sum of the principal curvatures. Thus, the sphere of radius
\frac{1}{2}
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
has mean curvature

stability operator

of a surface is given by
L_{S}=\Delta_{S}+|A_{S}|^{2}
, where here

denotes the square length of the second fundamental form of

and
\Delta_{S}
the Laplace-Beltrami operator. If
X
is a vector field on
S
, the linear change
\dot{H}
at
t=0
in the mean curvature
H_{t}
of the one parameter family of surfaces
S_{t}:=S+tX
is given by

(1.1)

\displaystyle\dot{H}=L_{S}X^{\perp}+\nabla_{X^{\top}}H.

Above,
X^{\perp}:=X\cdot N
denotes the normal part of
X
along
S
,
X^{\top}:=X-X^{\perp}N
denotes the tangential part and
\nabla_{X^{\top}}H:=\nabla H\cdot X^{\top}
denotes direction derivative of
H
in the direction
X
. A
jacobi field
on
S
is a function

on
S
satisfying
L_{S}u=0
. For a constant mean curvature surface, the gradient of the mean curvature vanishes. Thus, since the mean curvature is invariant under rigid motions, the normal part of the translational and rotational vector fields along
S
are jacobi fields. We refer to jacobi fields of this form as
geometric
. A geometric jacobi field generated by a translation is said to be
translational
and one generated by a rotation is said to be
rotational
. When
S
is non-degenerate–meaning that the Dirichlet kernel of the stability operator of
S
is trivial–we let
\dot{\mathcal{h}}_{S,f}
denote the unique jacobi field on
S
with trace
f
.

Throughout, we rely on the language of disjoint unions, which in places we use somewhat informally. If
I
is an index set
F=(F_{i})_{i\in I}
is a family of sets, then the
disjoint union
of the family is given by
\coprod_{i\in I}F_{i}=\bigcup_{i}\{i\}\times F_{i}
. Although this is standard, we record this precisely as
disjoint union
is frequently used informally to mean the union of disjoint sets. In this article, we will frequently consider disjoint unions in which the index set is only implicitly mentioned, if at all, since the existence of an indexing is trivial and distinct indexings give rise to isomorphic objects. We define the
canonical projection

\pi:\coprod_{i\in I}F_{i}\rightarrow\bigcup_{i}F_{i}
the mapping given by
(i,p)\in\{i\}\times F_{i}\mapsto p
. When the sets
F_{i}
are subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
–or more generally, belong to a common space–we will sometimes say that the canonical projection
projects into
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. When the sets
F_{i}
are mutually disjoint, the disjoint union and the union are isomorphic under the canonical projection. When this is the case, we will not carefully distinguish between the two unless it is important to do so.

A one parameter family of surfaces
S_{t}
is smooth if there is smooth family
V_{t}
of vector fields such that
S_{t}=S_{0}+V_{t}
. Let
S_{t}
be a smooth one parameter family of oriented surfaces and assume that
S_{0}
is smooth. The
normal variation field
on
S_{0}
generated by the family

is given by
\dot{S}^{\perp}:=\left(\left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{t=0}V_{t}\right)\cdot N_{S_{0}}
where
N_{S_{0}}
denotes the unit normal to the surface
S_{0}
. Suppose that
V_{t}
and
W_{t}
are smooth families of vector fields on
S_{0}
satisfying
S_{t}=S_{0}+V_{t}=S_{0}+W_{t}
. Then there is a smooth family of a diffeomorphisms
\phi_{t}
of
S_{0}
such that
p+V_{t}(p)=\phi_{t}(p)+W_{t}(\phi_{t}(p))
for all

. Differentiating in

at

gives

\dot{V}=\dot{W}+\dot{\phi}

and thus the normal parts
\dot{V}^{\perp}
and
\dot{W}^{\perp}
agree, and the normal variation field is well-defined. When
S_{t}
is a smooth one parameter family of constant mean curvature surfaces, we have from (

) that

L_{S_{0}}\dot{S}^{\perp}=0,

and thus the normal variation field of a smooth one parameter family of CMC surfaces is a jacobi field on

.

1.3.
Outline of the proof

In Section
2
, we record the properties of the family of constant mean curvature necks constructed in
[
14
]
that are relevant to our construction. In Section
3
, we define the family of surfaces
\mathcal{C}
that we will use to construct complete embedded CMC surfaces with prescribed topologies and describe the parameters of the family. The surfaces are derived from collections
\mathbb{S}
of spheres that touch tangentially, by taking the disjoint union of the components of the collection that are within a fixed small distance from the points of tangency with the components of the closed complement. The collection
\mathcal{S}
of components that are far from the points of tangency are regular subsets of spheres, and nearby CMC surfaces are freely prescribed by perturbations of the boundary. Each element in the collection
\mathcal{N}
of components near the points of tangency lies at the extremal limit of the family of constant mean curvature necks described in Section
3
. The family of surfaces
\mathcal{C}
is defined to be the disjoint union of the components in
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{N}
and is controlled by parameters
\xi\in\underline{\xi}
which parametrize the family of constant mean curvature necks, as well as boundary perturbations of the components of
\mathcal{S}
. We group the parameters into two types, according to the jacobi fields they generate in
\mathcal{C}
. The type II parameters can be roughly thought of as dirichlet parameters with
C^{2,\alpha}
boundary data, and generate
C^{2,\alpha}
, non-trivial jacobi fields on
\mathcal{C}
. The full complement of jacobi fields is not generated by type II variations, as those that are generated by
\mathcal{N}
through variations of the family of CMC necks are orthogonal to the space of lower modes. The type I parameters behave differently, either generating trivial jacobi fields or else singular ones with logarithmic poles at the points of tangency. These parameters modify the scale and rotate the axes of the necks in
\mathcal{N}
. The type I and type II parameters will play distinct roles in the construction. We also define a pairing
*
–an order two diffeomorphism–of the boundary
\partial\mathcal{C}
by the condition that the paired components project to the same curve under the canonical projection into
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. This gives rise to a notion of even and odd functions on the boundary of
\mathcal{C}
, which we use to formulate the Cauchy data matching operator. In Section
4
, we define an operator
\Lambda
, which we call the
defect operator
, associated to the family
\mathcal{C}
which we use to match the Cauchy data of components of
\mathcal{C}

up to translations
. That is, the zeroes of
\Lambda
correspond to surfaces in the family whose paired boundary components differ by a translation and whose co-normals are opposing. Roughly speaking, the operator
\Lambda
measures the angle between conormals across paired boundary components. It maps from the parameter space
\underline{\xi}
into the error space
\underline{e}_{*}
which is equal to the space of even
C^{1,\alpha}
functions on
\partial\mathcal{C}
. We also prove differentiability and defined-ness of
\Lambda
on a ball about the origin in the parameter space
\underline{\xi}
. Since the components of the nonsingular part
\mathcal{S}
of
\mathcal{C}
are regular, we can restrict the family
\mathcal{C}
apriori to variations in which paired boundary components differ by translations. In other words, we define the family of surfaces
\mathcal{C}
so that at each parameter value
\xi
, the dirichlet data of
\mathcal{C}_{\xi}
on paired boundary components is matched up to a translation. This reduces the Cauchy data matching problem to finding surfaces with opposing conormals and to resolving the translational errors separately. In Section
5
, we study the linearization of
\Lambda
in the type II parameters of the family. The operator is shown to be self-adjoint, and thus index zero, and the kernel–which we denote by
\underline{e}_{*,I}
– corresponds to the space of bounded jacobi fields on
\mathbb{S}
, with each sphere in
\mathbb{S}
contributing a three dimensional space of translational jacobi fields in the absence of imposed symmetries. Thus, the orthogonal complement
\underline{e}_{*,II}
of kernel the
\underline{e}_{*,I}
is generated by the type II parameters. In Section
6
, we specialize to the case that the collection
\mathbb{S}
of spheres, from which the family
\mathcal{C}
is derived, is arranged on a regular integer lattice in the plane. The collection is then invariant under a group
\mathcal{G}
of symmetries, which is large enough so that the subspace of
\mathcal{G}
-invariant parameters of the family
\mathcal{C}
avoids the co-kernel of the Type II variations. Thus,
\mathcal{G}
-invariant perturbations of
\mathcal{C}
through zeroes of
\Lambda
are freely prescribable through variations of
\mathcal{G}
-invariant Type
I
parameters. Moreover, the symmetries of the
\mathcal{G}
-invariant perturbations imply that the remaining translational error is trivially resolved. In Section
7
, as a prelude to addressing symmetry breaking variations, we record the
L^{2}
projections of the variations of
\Lambda
generated by type I and type II parameters at small
\mathcal{G}
-invariant perturbations of
\mathcal{C}
. The symmetries of
\mathcal{C}
, and an imposed orthogonality condition on the necks, together imply that the type II variations preserve the orthogonal complement of
\underline{e}_{*,I}
, and the type I and type II together generate the error space
\underline{e}_{*}
. In fact, to generate the co-kernel, only type I variations of the
vertical necks
in
\mathcal{N}
–necks that connect spheres belonging to the same column of
\mathbb{S}
–are needed. We denote by
\underline{e}
the space all type II parameters, together with the type I parameters supported on the vertical necks. The entire parameter space
\underline{\xi}
is then the direct sum of
\underline{e}
with the space
\underline{v}
of type I parameters supported on the horizontal necks. In Section
8
, we develop the linear theory needed for symmetry breaking perturbations. Of note here is our use of
graded norms
, in which terms are measured not by their absolute size, but by their differences across fundamental domains. We then show that
\Lambda
is defined and differentiable in a ball about zero in the parameter space equipped with an exponentially decaying graded norm, and that the linearization in
\underline{e}
is a bounded isomorphism onto
\underline{e}_{*}
. By appealing directly to the implicit function theorem, we are then able to construct families
\mathcal{C}_{v}
of zeroes of
\Lambda
parametrized by the remaining free parameters
v\in\underline{v}
of the construction. In Section
9
, we show that the translational errors between components of the surfaces
\mathcal{C}_{v}
are resolvable with the remaining free parameters. This is done by arranging
\mathcal{C}
into sub-collections we call
branches
, in which translational errors are trivially resolvable. Basically, a branch comprises the components of
\mathcal{S}
belonging to a single column, along with all incident half necks. The surfaces in each branch can then be translated independently from each other so that paired boundary components are matched exactly, not just up to translations. Since the half necks are translated independently from each other, this process introduces new translational errors–namely, the differences between the waists of the half necks belonging to the same CMC neck–which need to be resolved. This error is measured by an operator which we denote by
\mathcal{E}
which at each horizontal neck takes values on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
–due to the imposed symmetries. We then show that the remaining free parameters generate the error space of
\mathcal{E}
at each neck. To match the surfaces, we restrict our attention to the finite dimensional subspace
\underline{v}_{0}
of
\underline{v}
comprising parameters
v\in\underline{v}
that vanish away from a fixed compact subset
\mathbb{F}
of horizontal necks. The operator
\mathcal{E}
is then a bounded isomorphism near zero. By translating the branches independently from each other, and correcting the error with
v
, we then obtain families of smoothly immersed CMC surfaces prescribed by the relative translations
d
of the branches. Finally, in Section
10
, we exhibit explicit choices of
\mathbb{F}
and
d
that give rise to embedded surfaces with prescribed genus. Since the construction just described only gives rise to surfaces with prescribed even numbers of ends greater than three, we explain a modification that yields any number of ends greater than four.

CMC necks

The central tool used in our construction is a family of CMC annuli constructed in

2.1.
Catenoidal necks

The standard catenoid is given implicitly by the equation
r=\cosh(z)
in cylindrical coordinates on
\mathbb{R}^{3}

\mathcal{N}_{\tau}
denote the intersection of the standard catenoid the tube of radius

as a catenoidal neck, and we refer to

comprises the circles
\partial^{\pm}\mathcal{N}_{\tau}:=\mathbb{S}^{2}\pm l_{\tau}e_{z}
, where here

. The waist

of the catenoidal neck

with the plane

\mathbb{R}^{2}

\int_{\partial^{+}\mathcal{N}_{\tau}}\eta=2\pi\sin(\tau)e_{z},

where above

\mathcal{N}_{\tau}
. Thus, we will alternatively refer to

\mathcal{N}_{\tau}

2.2.
The separation parameter

It will be useful in places to parametrize the family of necks

.

2.4.
CMC necks

The family of surfaces constructed in
[

]
extend the properties of catenoidal necks discussed above. They are constructed as normal perturbations of catenoidal necks by functions solving a constant mean curvature dirichlet problem on catenoidal necks of small scale. We restate the main theorem of

]
below

.

There is
\epsilon>0
and
C>0
such that: Given
\tau>0
with
\tau<\epsilon

f\in C^{2,\alpha}(\partial\mathcal{N}_{\tau})
with
\|f\|<\epsilon

Here, the term

at unit speed, and such that the slice

2.5.
Criteria for embeddedness of CMC necks

Since the functions
\mathcal{h}_{\tau,\delta,f}
defining

are normalized, it follows from the uniqueness assertion in Theorem

that the surface

is invariant under reflections through the plane

when

is, and is invariant under rotations about the

are rotationally symmetric about the

\delta

by the ‘downward pointing’ unit normal, for negative values of

the surfaces lie in immersed family of

and are thus non-embedded for small values of

relative to

\delta

and

Let

The family of half necks

2.8.
Conventions in this article

In this section we describe some minor modifications to the parameters that describe the family of CMC necks that will be more convenient for our use in this article. The basic problem is that the surfaces
\mathcal{N}_{\tau,\delta,f}
as introduced are described as graphs over the family of minimal catenoidal necks
\mathcal{N}_{\tau}
, which is slightly inconvenient in the context of constant mean curvature surfaces. We thus take some time to modify the definitions of these parameters so that certain quantities, and their geometric interpretations, are defined relative to the constant mean curvature neck
\mathcal{N}_{0,\delta,0}
instead.

2.8.1.
Rescaling of the surfaces

We will, throughout this article regard
\delta>0
as a small but fixed constant and suppress it from our notation. Thus, for example, we will write
\mathcal{N}_{\tau,f}
to mean
\mathcal{N}_{\tau,\delta,f}
and
\mathcal{D}_{f}:=\mathcal{D}_{\delta,f}
. We will also set
\mathcal{N}_{\tau}:=\mathcal{N}_{\tau,\delta}
and
\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{D}_{\delta}
. This can potentially cause confusion, and to avoid this we observe that for example
\mathcal{N}_{\tau}
now longer refers to the family of minimal surfaces defined above, but rather the CMC annulus of rotation

. Similarly,
\mathcal{D}
no longer refers to the unit disk in the plane, but rather the rotational CMC graph over
\mathcal{D}
with constant curvature
\delta
and tangent to the origin. We will also assume that the surface
\mathcal{N}_{\tau,\delta,f}
has been scaled so that the mean curvature is
1
. Precisely, we will identify
\mathcal{N}_{\tau,f}
with the scaled surface
\delta\cdot\mathcal{N}_{\tau,\delta,f}
. Observe that, when

and
\tau
vanish, the surface

is the intersection of two spheres of radius

centered at the points

with a ball of small radius about the origin. Thus, it is the union of the two constant mean curvature disks

.

2.8.2.
Modifying the Dirichlet parameter

Let

Fix a constant

of

.

Remark 3.1
.

Observe that here
\delta
differs slightly from the

, and that we have fixed as a background constant throughout the article. Denoting it here by

.

We refer to the closed complement of

as the

of
\mathbb{S}
. Each component of
\mathcal{S}
up to translations is a sphere of radius

with finitely many extrinsic disks of small radius

removed. We will refer to any such surface as a

. Since the collection
\mathbb{S}
is pre-embedded, the number such disks, as well the pairwise distance from their centers, is bounded by a universal constant, and the components of

lie in a fixed compact subset of the moduli space of compact CMC surfaces with smooth boundary, independent of the collection
\mathbb{S}
. As a consequence, taking

smaller if necessary, and since the stability operator on the sphere has kernel–the three dimensional space spanned by the translational killing fields–we can ensure by strict monotonicity of eigenvalues that each spherical domain is non-degenerate, meaning that the Dirichlet kernel of the stability operator is trivial. We will assume throughout that
\delta>0
has been chosen sufficiently small so that this is the case

3.1.
Pairings

Let
\mathcal{C}
denote the disjoint union of the components of the collections
\mathcal{N}
and
\mathcal{S}
, and let
\pi:\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3}

\pi\mathcal{C}=\mathbb{S}
and the projection restricts to a diffeomorphism on the interior of

Alternatively, since the canonical projection restricts to a

.
We use

4.2.
The linearized operator

Let

5.
Type II variations

In this section we study the linearization
\dot{\Lambda}
of
\Lambda
in the type II parameters by relating it to a Dirichlet to Neumann operator.

5.1.
The Dirichlet to Neumann operator

Definition 5.1
.

We define the operator
\not{\partial}:C^{2,\alpha}(\partial\mathcal{C})\rightarrow C^{1,\alpha}(\partial\mathcal{C})
to be the operator given by:

\not{\partial}g=\frac{\dot{\mathcal{h}}_{\mathcal{C},g}}{\partial\eta}:=\nabla\dot{\mathcal{h}}_{\mathcal{C}^{\prime},g}\cdot\eta.

We refer to the operator
\not{\partial}
as the
Dirichlet to Neumann operator
of the collection
\mathcal{C}
.

It is well-known that
\not{\partial}
is an elliptic first order self adjoint pseudo-differential operator with principle symbol
-|\xi|
. Moreover it is straightforward to verify that
\not{\partial}
is self adjoint with respect to
L^{2}
inner product on
\partial\mathcal{C}
: Let
u
and
v
be jacobi fields on
\mathcal{C}
, then

\int_{\mathcal{C}^{\prime}}uLv-vLu=\int_{\partial\mathcal{C}^{\prime}}uv_{\eta}-vu_{\eta}=0.

When
\mathcal{tr}(u)=f
and
\mathcal{tr}(v)=g
, the second equality above gives
\int_{\partial\mathcal{C}^{\prime}}f\not{\partial}g-g\not{\partial}f=0
. It then follows directly that:

Proposition 5.2
.

The even part
\not{\partial}^{(e)}
of
\not{\partial}
is self adjoint on the subspace of even functions on
\partial\mathcal{C}

Proof.

Since
\not{\partial}
is self adjoint, we have

\langle f,\not{\partial}g\rangle=\langle\not{\partial}f,g\rangle.

Applying
*
to both sides above gives

\langle f^{*},\left(\not{\partial}g\right)^{*}\rangle=\langle\left(\not{\partial}f\right)^{*},g^{*}\rangle.

Since we are assuming
f
and
g
are even, we have
f=f^{*}
and
g=g^{*}
and thus summing the two equations above gives

\langle f,\not{\partial}g+\left(\not{\partial}g\right)^{*}\rangle=\langle\not{\partial}f+\left(\not{\partial}f\right)^{*},g\rangle.

which gives the claim.
∎

Thus,
\not{\partial}
is index zero and the obstruction to inverting the operator arises from its kernel. The following lemma relates
\dot{\Lambda}
to the operator
\not{\partial}
.

Lemma 5.3
.

It holds that
\dot{\Lambda}(f,\rho)=\not{\partial}^{(e)}(f+\rho^{\perp})
, where here we are identifying the piecewise translation
\rho
of
\partial\mathcal{S}
with its even extension to
\partial\mathcal{C}
.

Proof.

Fix a component
\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}
in
\mathcal{S}
and let
\mathcal{N}_{\alpha}=\mathcal{N}_{\alpha,1}\coprod\ldots\mathcal{N}_{\alpha,k}
denote the components of
\mathcal{N}
that are incident to
\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}
. That is, for each component
\partial
of the boundary
\partial\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}
, there is a unique
i\in\{1,\ldots k\}
such that
\partial^{*}
is in
\mathcal{N}_{\alpha,i}
. After applying a piecewise translation to
\mathcal{N}_{\alpha}
, we can assume that the boundaries are matched, so that
\partial=\partial^{*}
for all components
\partial
of
\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}
. Since the boundary components of
\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}
are matched the normal variation field generated by
(\dot{\rho},\dot{f})
at
\xi=0
has even trace and on
\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}
is given by
\dot{\mathcal{h}}_{\mathcal{S}_{\alpha},\dot{\rho}^{\perp}+f}
. By expression (
4.3
) in Lemma

, we then have
\dot{\Lambda}_{\dot{\rho},\dot{f}}=\left(\left(\dot{\mathcal{h}}_{C,f+\rho^{\perp}}\right)_{\eta}\right)^{(e)}=\not{\partial}^{(e)}(f+\rho^{\perp})
.

∎

5.2.
The kernel of
\not{\partial}^{(e)}

The kernel of
\not{\partial}^{(e)}
in the space of even functions can be characterized as the space of jacobi fields on
\mathbb{S}
. To do this, it will be helpful to have a few definitions first:

Definition 5.4

A function

is said to be Dirichlet matched if its trace

is even. It is Neumann matched if the deriviative
\frac{\partial u}{\partial\eta}
of

with respect to the boundary conormal is odd. It is Cauchy matched if it is both Dirichlet and Neumann matched.

An obvious consequence of Definition

is that Cauchy matched functions descend as

functions under the canonical projection into

. Moreover, by standard elliptic regularity, if

is a Cauchy matched jacobi field, then it descends to a smooth function on

under the canonical projection. We record this observation in Lemma

Lemma 5.5

Let

be a Dirichlet matched function. Then the projection
\widehat{u}:\mathbb{S}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, given by
\widehat{u}(\pi^{\prime}(p))
= u (p) is a well defined
C^{0}
function on
\mathbb{S}
. When

is Cauchy matched
\widehat{u}
, is
C^{1}
.

We can define lifts of functions on
\mathbb{S}
as an inverse operator to projections of Dirichlet matched functions on
\mathcal{C}
.

Definition 5.6
.

Given a function
u
on
\mathbb{S}
, its
lift

\widecheck{u}:\mathcal{C}^{\prime}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
to
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
is given by
\widecheck{u}(p)=u(\pi^{\prime}(p))

Clearly, we have:

Lemma 5.7
.

The lift
\widecheck{u}
of a smooth function
u
on
\mathbb{S}
is a smooth function on
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
. Moreover,
\widecheck{u}
is Cauchy matched and it holds that
\widehat{\widecheck{u}}=u=\widecheck{\widehat{u}}
.

5.3.
The kernel of

We are ready to characterize the kernel of

We let

The kernel of the stability operator

It is a standard fact that the kernel of the stability operator on a sphere is three dimensional and is spanned by the translational jacobi fields. Thus, if

We let

Proposition 5.10

Proof.

Pick

As a direct consequence we have:

Corollary 5.11

The map
\not{\partial}^{(e)}
is a bounded isomorphism of

onto

.

5.4.
Resolving solutions

We now show that the type

Lemma 6.10
.

The space of bounded jacobi fields on the catenoid is precisely the space of translational jacobi fields.

In other words, the space of bounded jacobi fields on the catenoid is spanned by the components of the unit normal.

Proof of Proposition
6.9
.

Suppose that a branch

is not rotationally symmetric. Then the rotational vector field

has non-trivial normal part, which we denote by

. Observe that

is a periodic jacobi field and moreover the symmetries of
\mathcal{B}
imply that

is odd with respect to coordinate plane reflections. We normalize

so that
\sup u=1
. Now, take a sequence
\tau_{0,j}
of base flux assignments tending to

and consider the corresponding sequences
\mathcal{B}_{j}
of branches and
u_{j}
of periodic jacobi fields on
\mathcal{B}_{j}
. Observe that as
\tau_{0,j}\rightarrow 0
the branch
\mathcal{B}_{j}
converges to a limit
\mathcal{B}_{0}
, which is a collection of spheres of radius

touching at the north and south poles–that is, the points where the unit normal coincides with
\pm e_{z}
. Pick a sequence
p_{j}
of points realizing the supremum of
u_{j}
, so

. There are then several possibilities. If

remains in a fixed compact subset away from the poles, then

converges to a non-trivial bounded jacobi field on the sphere away from the north and south poles. Standard removable singularity theory then implies that it extends smoothy across the puncture to a non-trivial jacobi field on the sphere, and is thus a translational jacobi field. However, this is a contradiction, since we assumed that
u
is orthogonal to the translational jacobi fields over each fundamental domain. Thus, we must have that

Corollary 7.2

,
N_{y}
and
N_{z}
denote components of the unit normal to
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}_{\tau_{0}+e_{\mathcal{G},\tau_{0}}}
. Observe the symmetries of
\mathcal{L}_{i,j}
give that along
\partial_{t}
and
\partial_{b}
,
\mathcal{k}^{y}_{i,j}
and
N_{y}
are proportional up to a positive constant, so
\mathcal{k}^{y}_{i,j}=\alpha N_{y}
for some
\alpha>0
on
\partial_{b}
and
\partial_{t}
. Thus, in order to estimate the
L_{2}
-projection
\langle\frac{\partial\Lambda}{\partial\phi}(\dot{\phi}),\mathcal{k}^{y}_{i,j}\rangle
it suffices to estimate
\langle\frac{\partial\Lambda}{\partial\phi}(\dot{\phi}),N_{y}\rangle_{\partial\mathcal{S}_{i,j}}
.

The parameter
\phi
rotates
\partial_{t}
about the origin through the positive angle
\phi^{v}_{i,j+1}
and thus on
\mathcal{N}^{v,-}_{i,j+1}
the parameter
\phi
also acts as the same rotation. Let
u
denote the normal variation field generated by
\phi^{v}_{i,j+1}
at
\phi=0
. Then

\left.\dot{\Lambda}\right|_{\partial\mathcal{S}_{i,j}}=\left(u_{\eta}\right)^{e}.

Integrating over the leaf
\mathcal{L}_{i,j}
we have

0=\int_{\mathcal{L}_{i,j}}N_{y}Lu=\int_{\partial\mathcal{L}_{i,j}}u_{\eta}N_{y}-uN_{y,\eta},

where above
L
denotes the stability operator of
\mathcal{C}
.
Since
N_{y}
is smooth on
\pi\mathcal{L}
it is Cauchy matched and we have
\left.N_{y,\eta}\right|_{\partial_{t}}=-\left.N_{y,\eta}\right|_{\partial_{t}^{*}}
. This then gives

\int_{\partial\mathcal{S}_{i,j}}\dot{\Lambda}N_{y}+\int_{w_{i,j}}u_{\eta}N_{y}-uN_{y,\eta}=0.

Thus, it remains to compute the second integral in the left hand side above.
On
\mathcal{N}^{v}_{i,j+1}
,
u
is the rotational field given by

u=yN_{z}-zN_{y}.

Let
w_{t}
denote the top boundary component of
\pi\mathcal{L}_{i,j}
. Thus,
w_{t}
is the waist of the top half neck in
\mathcal{L}_{i,j}
. Then
w_{t}
is a circle of radius
\delta
, invariant under rotations about the
z
-axis. Let
\theta
denote the standard cylindrical coordinate on
w_{t}
. On
w_{t}
, we have
N_{z}=0
and
N_{y,\eta}=0
, and
z_{\eta}=1
. Moreover, we have
N_{y}=\sin(\theta)
and thus
y=r_{0}N_{y}=r_{0}\sin(\theta)
, where
r_{0}
denotes the radius of
w_{t}
. Finally, since the mean curvature of the Delaunay end is
1
with respect to the outward unit normal, we have that
N_{z,\eta}=\left(1-\frac{1}{r_{0}}\right)
. Using the expression for
u
above we then have

\displaystyle u_{\eta}

\displaystyle=\left(yN_{z}-zN_{y}.\right)_{\eta}

\displaystyle=yN_{z,\eta}-z_{\eta}N_{y}

\displaystyle=y\left(1-\frac{1}{r_{0}}\right)-N_{y}

\displaystyle=\sin(\theta)(r_{0}-1)-\sin(\theta)

\displaystyle=(r_{0}-2)\sin(\theta)

Thus we have

\displaystyle\int_{w_{t}}u_{\eta}N_{y}-uN_{y,\eta}

\displaystyle=\int_{w_{t}}u_{\eta}N_{y}ds

\displaystyle=(r_{0}-2)\int_{0}^{2\pi}\sin^{2}(\theta)d\theta

\displaystyle=\frac{r_{0}-2}{2}

Thus, we have

\left\langle\frac{\partial\Lambda}{\partial\phi}(\dot{\phi}),\mathcal{k}^{y}_{i,j}\right\rangle=\frac{2-r_{0}}{2}\phi^{v,\Delta}_{i,j}

as claimed. This completes the proof.
∎

8.
Symmetry breaking perturbations

In this section we record the mapping properties of the operator
\Lambda
as a map from the space
\underline{\xi}
into
\underline{e}_{*}
in the vertically graded norms with exponentially decaying weights. The main results of the section are Proposition
8.1
, which says that
\Lambda
is defined and bounded in
C^{1}
on a ball about the origin and Proposition
8.4
, which says the linearization is an isomorphism from
\underline{e}
onto
\underline{e}_{*}
. As a direct consequence, in Corollary
8.5
we construct a differentiable map
v\mapsto e_{v}
parametrizing the zeroes of
\Lambda
near
\xi=0
, so
\Lambda_{v+e_{v}}=0
.

Proposition 8.1
.

There is
\epsilon>0
such that
\Lambda:\underline{\xi}\rightarrow\underline{e}_{*}
is defined and differentiable on the ball
B_{\epsilon}\left(0;\underline{\xi}\right)
in the norm vertically graded norm
\|-\|^{\sim}_{-\mu}
.

Proof.

By Lemma
6.4
we have
\|\xi\|\leq^{C}\|\xi\|^{\sim}_{-\mu}
. Moreover, since
\Lambda^{\Delta}(\xi)=\Lambda(\xi^{\Delta})
, we have

\left\|\Lambda(\xi)\right\|^{\sim}_{-\mu}\leq^{C}\left\|\xi\right\|^{\sim}_{-\mu},

so that
\Lambda
is bounded in
C^{0}
on the ball of radius
\epsilon
about zero in
\underline{\xi}
in the form
\|-\|^{\sim}_{-\mu}
.
The derivative estimate follows similarly.
∎

The following proposition records an a-priori estimate for the operator
\Lambda
in the norms
\|-\|^{\sim}_{-\mu}

Proposition 8.2
.

There is
\epsilon>0
such that: For each
\tau<\epsilon
, there is a constant
C>0
such that: Given
e\in\underline{e}
it holds that

\left\|e\right\|^{\sim}_{-\mu}\leq^{C}\left\|\dot{\Lambda}(e)\right\|^{\sim}_{-\mu}.

Proof.

We will first show that

(8.1)

\displaystyle\left\|e^{\Delta}\right\|_{-\mu}\leq^{C}\left\|\dot{\Lambda}^{\Delta}(e)\right\|_{-\mu}.

If this doesn’t hold, then for arbitrarily small base flux
\tau_{0}
, there is a sequence
e_{k}
with
\left\|\dot{\Lambda}^{\Delta}(e_{k})\right\|_{-\mu}\rightarrow 0
and
\left\|e_{k}^{\Delta}\right\|_{-\mu}=1
. Pick integers
i_{k}
and
j_{k}
such that

1=\left\|e^{\Delta}_{k}\right\|_{-\mu}=\cosh^{\mu}(j_{k})\left\|\left(e^{\Delta}_{k}\right)_{(i_{k},j_{k})}\right\|.

Let
\overline{e}_{k}
denote the element of
\underline{e}
such that
\left(\overline{e}_{k}\right)_{i,j}=\left(e_{k}\right)_{i_{k},j_{k}}

for arbitrary
i
and
j
and thus
\overline{e}_{k}^{\Delta}=0
. Without loss of generality we can assume that
i_{k}=0
and that
j_{k}\geq 0
. We will suppress
i
from the notation throughout the rest of the proof. We then define the renormalized sequence
\tilde{e}_{k}
as follows:

\left(\tilde{e}_{k}\right)_{j}:=\cosh^{\mu}(j_{k})\left(\left(e_{k}\right)_{j_{k}+j}-\left(\overline{e}_{k}\right)_{j+j_{k}}\right).

After passing to a subsequence we can assume that the sequence
j_{k}
is convergent with limit
j_{\infty}
in the extended integers
\mathbb{Z}\cup\{\infty\}
. Observe that by construction we have
\left(\tilde{e}_{k}\right)_{0}=0
and that

\displaystyle\left\|\left(\tilde{e}^{\Delta}_{k}\right)_{j}\right\|

\displaystyle=\cosh^{\mu}(j_{k})\left\|\left(e^{\Delta}_{k}\right)_{j+j_{k}}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\cosh^{-\mu}_{j_{k}}(j),

where above we have used that
\left\|e_{k}^{\Delta}\right\|_{-\mu}=1
, and by definition we have
\left\|\left(\tilde{e}^{\Delta}_{k}\right)_{0}\right\|=1
. After passing to a subsequence, we have that
\tilde{e}_{k}
converges to a non-trivial limit
e\in\underline{e}
. The limit
e
then satisfies
\left(e\right)_{0}=0
and the estimate
\left\|\left(e^{\Delta}\right)\right\|\leq\cosh_{j_{\infty}}(j)
. We have:

\displaystyle\left\{\dot{\Lambda}(\tilde{e}_{k})\right\}_{j}

\displaystyle=\cosh^{\mu}(j_{k})\left\{\dot{\Lambda}\left(e_{k}\right)\right\}_{j+j_{k}}-\cosh^{\mu}(j_{k})\left\{\dot{\Lambda}\left(\overline{e}_{k}\right)\right\}_{j+j_{k}}.

Since
\overline{e}^{\Delta}_{k}=0
we have
\left(\dot{\Lambda}\left(\overline{e}_{k}\right)\right)^{\Delta}=0
and thus

\displaystyle\left\|\left\{\left(\dot{\Lambda}(\tilde{e}_{k})\right)^{\Delta}\right\}_{j}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\cosh^{\mu}(j_{k})\left\|\dot{\Lambda}(e_{k})\right\|_{-\mu}\cosh^{-\mu}(j+j_{k})

\displaystyle\leq\cosh^{-\mu}_{j_{k}}(j)\left\|\dot{\Lambda}(e_{k})\right\|_{-\mu}.

Thus,
\left\{\left(\dot{\Lambda}(\tilde{e}_{k})\right)^{\Delta}\right\}_{j}\rightarrow 0
as
k\rightarrow\infty
. Taking the limit as
k\rightarrow\infty
gives

\left(\dot{\Lambda}(e)\right)^{\Delta}=0.

Let
e_{I}
and
e_{II}
denote the projections of
e
into the spaces
\underline{e}_{I}
and
\underline{e}_{II}
, respectively.
By Lemmas
7.3
and
7.4
and
7.1
we have that

\left\|\left\{\left(\dot{\Lambda}(\dot{e})\right)_{j}\right\}_{I}\right\|\leq^{C}\left\|\left\{e^{\Delta}\right\}_{j}\right\|\leq\cosh_{j_{\infty}}(j).

Since
\left\{\dot{\Lambda}(e)\right\}_{j}
is independent of
j
, the decay estimate above forces
\left(\dot{\Lambda}(e)\right)_{I}
to vanish. Applying Lemmas
7.3
and
7.4
and
7.1
again and the fact that
\left\{e_{I}\right\}_{0}=0
gives that
e_{I}\equiv 0
and thus
e
is in
\underline{e}_{II}
.

Now, take a sequence
\tau_{0,n}
of base flux assignments tending to
0
, and let
e_{n}
denote the corresponding solutions constructed above. The solutions then converge to a non-trivial limit
e
satisfying
\dot{\Lambda}_{0}^{\Delta}(e)=0
, where here
\dot{\Lambda}_{0}
denotes the linearization of
\Lambda
at
\xi_{total}=0
. Let
u
denote the normal variation field generated by
e
. Then
u
is a jacobi field on the branch
\mathcal{B}_{0}
containing
\mathcal{S}_{0,0}
. Since the background flux
\tau_{0}
is now zero,
\mathcal{B}_{0}
is a collection of spheres, and thus
u
is a collection of translational jacobi fields on the spheres
\mathbb{S}_{j}
comprising the leaves of
\mathcal{B}_{0}
. Since
e_{0}=0
, we have that
u
vanishes on
\mathbb{S}_{0}
, from which it follows that
u\equiv 0
and thus
e=0
. This establishes the estimate (
8.1
).

Now, suppose that for small
\tau_{0}
, there is a sequence
e_{k}
with
\left\|e_{k}\right\|^{\sim}_{-\mu}=1
and
\left\|\dot{\Lambda}(e_{k})\right\|^{\sim}_{-\mu}\rightarrow 0
. By the estimate (
8.1
), we have
\left\|e^{\Delta}_{k}\right\|_{-\mu}\rightarrow 0
and thus the sequence
e_{k}
converges to a non-trivial limit
e\in\underline{e}
with
e^{\Delta}=0
and satisfying
\dot{\Lambda}(e)=0
. Since
e^{\Delta}=0
, the symmetries imply that
\left(\dot{e}_{I}\right)_{0}=0
and thus
e_{I}=0
. Taking the base flux
\tau_{0}\rightarrow 0
as above we obtain a contradiction as above. This completes the proof.
∎

In the following lemma we show that the linear operator
\dot{\Lambda}
is an isomorphism in the graded norms with exponentially growing weights. This is needed for a perturbation argument in the proof of Lemma
8.4
, which records that the operator is an isomorphism in the vertically graded norms with exponentially decaying weight.

In order to simplify our presentation, we will assume throughout that the collection
\mathcal{C}
derived from
\mathbb{S}
, and the corresponding parameter spaces, are invariant under reflections through the plane
\{y=0\}
. Thus, given
\xi\in\underline{\xi}
, the entries of
\xi_{i,j}
of
\xi
are determined by their values for
j\geq 0
. Observe additionally that the symmetries imply that if
e_{*}\in\underline{e}_{*}
then
e_{*,i,0}
is orthogonal to translational jacobi field
\mathcal{k}^{z}
. Also, given
e_{I}\in\underline{e}_{I}
, we have
e_{I,i,j}=-e_{I,i,-j+1}
. Since
e_{*,i,0}
is a-priori orthogonal to
\mathcal{k}^{z}
, we will assume throughout that
\tau^{v}_{i,1}=\tau^{v}_{i,0}=0
.

Lemma 8.3
.

The mapping
\dot{\Lambda}:\underline{e}\rightarrow\underline{e}_{*}
is a bounded isomorphism in the weighted norms
\|-\|_{\mu}
for
\mu>0
.

Proof.

Clearly, we have that
\dot{\Lambda}
is bounded in the norms
\|-\|_{\mu}
for arbitrary
\mu
. To see that it has a bounded inverse in the norms
\|-\|_{\mu}
for
\mu>0
, pick
e_{*}\in\underline{e}_{*}
with
\|e_{*}\|_{\mu}=1
. By Lemma’s
7.3
and
7.4
there is a unique
e_{I}\in\underline{e}_{I}
such that
\dot{\Lambda}(e_{I})+e_{*}\in\underline{e}_{II}
. Moreover, we have the estimate
\left\|\left(e^{\Delta}_{I}\right)_{i,j}\right\|\cong\left\|e_{*i,j}\right\|
and thus

\|\left(\dot{e}_{I}\right)_{i,j}\|\leq\sum_{j^{\prime}=0}^{j}\left\|\left(\dot{e}_{I}^{\Delta}\right)_{i,j^{\prime}}\right\|\leq^{C}\sum_{j^{\prime}=0}^{j}\cosh^{\mu}(j^{\prime})\leq^{C}\cosh(j).

This then gives the estimate
\left\|\dot{\Lambda}(\dot{e}_{I})+e_{*,I}\right\|\leq^{C}\cosh^{\mu}(j)
. We have thus reduced to the case of showing that
\dot{\Lambda}
is a bounded isomorphism in the norm
\|-\|_{\mu}
from
\underline{e}_{II}
into
\underline{e}_{*,II}
, which is Corollary
7.2
.
∎

As a direct corollary to the apriori estimate recorded in Proposition
8.2
and the existence result in Lemma
8.3
, we have:

Proposition 8.4
.

The mapping
\dot{\Lambda}:\underline{e}\rightarrow\underline{e}_{*}
is a bounded isomorphism in the weighted norms
\|-\|^{\sim}_{-\mu}
.

Proof.

Fix
e_{*}\in\underline{e}_{*}
with
\|e\|^{\sim}_{-\mu}=1
. By Lemma
6.4
, we have
\|e_{*}\|\leq^{C}\|e\|^{\sim}_{-\mu}\leq C
and thus in particular
\|e_{*}\|_{\mu}\leq C
. By Lemma
8.3
, there is
e\in\underline{e}
with
\|e\|_{\mu}\leq{C^{\prime}}
and such that
\dot{\Lambda}(e)=e_{*}
. By Proposition
8.2
, we have the estimate

\left\|e\right\|^{\sim}_{-\mu}\leq^{C}\|e_{*}\|^{\sim}_{-\mu}.

This completes the proof.
∎

Thus, we have shown that the operator is bounded in
C^{1}
on a ball about the origin in
\underline{\xi}
as a map into
\underline{e}_{*}
and that the linearization at the origin is an isomorphism of
\underline{e}
onto
\underline{e}_{*}
. As a direct application of the implicit function theorem we then have that the zeroes of
\Lambda
near the origin are differentiably parametrized by the free variables
v\in\underline{v}
.

Corollary 8.5
.

There is
\epsilon>0
and
C>0
such that: Given
v\in\underline{v}
with
\|v\|^{\sim}_{\mu}\leq\epsilon
, there is a unique
e\in\underline{e}
with
\|e\|^{\sim}_{-\mu}\leq^{C}\epsilon
and such that

\Lambda_{v+e}=0.

Moreover, the mapping
v\mapsto e
is differentiable from
\underline{v}
into
\underline{e}
.

Proof.

This is a direct consequence of Propositions
8.1
,
8.4
and the implicit function theorem.
∎

Remark 8.6
.

For the remainder of the article, we will regard
e\in\underline{e}
as a differentiable function of
v\in\underline{v}
using Corollary
8.5
, so that
\Lambda_{v}=\Lambda(v+e)=0
for each small value of
v
. Thus, each branch in
\mathcal{C}_{v}
is a smooth surface with boundary comprising the waists of the incident half necks.

9.
Cancelling the translational error

In this section we resolve the translational differences between the half necks. The result is a smooth immersed
CMC
surface without boundary.

9.1.
Regular parameter assigments

It will be convenient to paramatrize the horizontal necks in
\mathcal{C}
by their separation
\sigma^{h}_{i,j}
parameters, rather than the flux
\tau^{h}_{i,j}
, since they are regular near zero. Thus, a
regular parameter assignment
in
\mathcal{N}^{h}
is a tuple
r=(r_{i,j})
where
r_{i,j}=\left(\sigma^{h}_{i,j},\phi^{h}_{i,j}\right)
and where
\sigma^{h}_{i,j}
denotes the separation of the horizontal neck
\mathcal{N}^{h}_{i,j}
and
\phi^{h}_{i,j}
the phase. We denote by
\underline{r}
the space of regular parameter assignments in
\mathcal{N}^{h}
. Observe that since the separation and flux parameters are related by (
2.2
), we have that
\underline{r}
and the space
\underline{v}
are isomorphic under the mapping

(9.1)

\displaystyle l:\left(\tau^{h}_{i,j},\phi^{h}_{i,j}\right)\in\underline{v}\mapsto\left(l_{\tau^{h}_{i,j}},\phi^{h}_{i,j}\right)\in\underline{r}

9.2.
The translational error operator
\mathcal{E}

We let
\underline{r}_{*}
denote the space of tuples in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
indexed by pairs of integers
(i,j)\in\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}
. We define an operator
\mathcal{E}:\underline{r}\rightarrow\underline{r}_{*}
as follows: Given
(i,j)\in\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}
, we let
\mathcal{N}^{h,+}_{i,j}
and
\mathcal{N}^{h,-}_{i,j}
denote the half necks in
\mathcal{N}^{h}_{i,j}
that are incident to
\mathcal{S}_{i,j+1}
and
\mathcal{S}_{i,j}
, respectively, and we let
w^{h,+}_{i,j}
and
w^{h,-}_{i,j}
denote the waists of each. Then
w^{h,+}_{i,j}
and
w^{h,-}_{i,j}
differ by a translation, which we denote by
\mathcal{E}_{i,j}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Thus we have

w^{h,+}_{i,j}=w^{h,-}_{i,j}+\mathcal{E}_{i,j}.

This defines a mapping
\mathcal{E}:\underline{r}\rightarrow\underline{r}_{*}
, which is locally differentiable in the sense that for each
(i,j)
the mapping
\mathcal{E}_{i,j}
is differentiable from
\underline{r}
into
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

9.3.
The restriction of
\mathcal{E}

We restrict the domain and codomains
\underline{r}
and
\underline{r}_{*}
as follows: Fix a finite subset
\mathbb{F}
of
\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}
. We will assume throughout that
r\in\underline{r}
satisfies
r_{i,j}=0
unless
(i,j)\in\mathbb{F}
, and simillarly for
r_{*}\in\underline{r}_{*}
. Let
\mathbb{F}_{i}\subset\mathbb{Z}
be defined by the condition that
j\in\mathbb{F}_{i}
if and only if
(i,j)\in\mathbb{F}
. We will assume throughout that
\mathbb{F}_{i}
is empty for
i\leq 0
and
i>n
and is otherwise nonempty.
Since the parameter
r_{i,j}
vanishes for
i
outside of the range
\{1,\ldots n\}
, so too does
v_{i,j}=l^{-1}_{r_{i,j}}
and thus the branches
\mathcal{B}_{i}
,
i\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0,\ldots,n\}
are smooth Delaunay surfaces.

Remark 9.1
.

For the remainder of the article, we will restrict
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}_{v}
to the sub-collection comprising the branches
\mathcal{B}_{0}
through
\mathcal{B}_{n}
.

9.4.
Regular variables generate the error space
\underline{r}_{*}

When
r=0
, the branches of
\mathcal{C}_{r}
are rotationally symmetric Delaunay ends, differing from each other by a translation. We can assume that a fixed translation has been applied separately to the branches so that
\mathcal{E}
vanishes at
r=0
. Thus, the collection
\mathcal{C}_{r}
at
r=0
is a collection of Delaunay ends touching tangentially.

We wish to will apply the implicit function theorem, with
r
serving as the regular variable, and with families of translations of the branches serving as the free parameters. We show now that the linearization of
\mathcal{E}
at
r=0
in
r
is an isomorphism onto the error space
\underline{r}_{*}
. In order to prove embeddedness of some the surfaces we construct, we will need to precisely understand the effect of the parameters
\phi^{h}
and
\sigma^{h}
. In the following, we let
\dot{\mathcal{E}}
denote the linearization of
\mathcal{E}
at
r=0
.

Proposition 9.2
.

The mapping
\dot{\mathcal{E}}:\underline{r}\rightarrow\underline{r}_{*}
is an isomorphism. Precisely, it holds that

\left\{\dot{\mathcal{E}}\left(\dot{\sigma}^{h}\right)\right\}_{i,j}=-\dot{\sigma}^{h}_{i,j}e_{y},\quad\left\{\dot{\mathcal{E}}(\dot{\phi}^{h})\right\}_{i,j}=\dot{\phi}_{i,j}^{h}e_{z}+O(\tau_{0}).

Proof.

In the following, we will fix a pair
(i,j)
in
\mathbb{F}
and suppress it from the notation.
We assume that the coordinate axes are positioned so that the waists
w^{-}:=w^{h,-}
and
w^{+}:=w^{h,+}
, which are points when
v=0
, coincide with the origin. Recall that throughout we have fixed a base flux assignment
\tau_{0}\in\underline{\tau}_{0}
and suppressed
\xi_{0}:=\tau_{0}+e_{\mathcal{G},\tau_{0}}
from our notation. Throughout, we have then written
\mathcal{C}_{\xi}
to mean
\mathcal{C}_{\xi+\xi_{0}}
. Thus, making the dependence on all parameters explicit again, we have
\mathcal{C}_{r}=\mathcal{C}\left(v_{r}+e_{v_{r}}+\xi_{0}\right)
, where we have put
v_{r}=l^{-1}_{r}
and where
l
is the mapping defined in Section
9.1
. Thus, when
\tau_{0}=r=0
, we have that the total parameter
\xi_{\text{total}}
vanishes and the branches of
\mathcal{C}
coincide with the collection
\mathbb{S}
of spheres of radius
1/2
on the integer lattice
(0,i,j)
. The mapping
v\mapsto e_{v}
is not defined here–it is only defined for positive base flux
\tau_{0}
–but we can compute the partial derivative of
\mathcal{E}
in
\phi^{h}
by fixing the remaining parameter values at
0
. The necks
w^{-}
and
w^{+}
are then given by

w^{-}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\cos(\phi^{h})e_{y}+\sin(\phi^{h})e_{z}\right)+\left(0,-\frac{1}{2},0\right)

and

w^{+}=-\frac{1}{2}\left(\cos(\phi^{h})e_{y}+\sin(\phi^{h})e_{z}\right)+\left(0,\frac{1}{2},0\right)

We then have

\mathcal{E}_{i,j}=w^{+}-w^{-}=\left(\cos(\phi^{h})e_{y}+\sin(\phi^{h})e_{z}\right)+\left(0,1,0\right),

From which the partial derivative
\frac{\partial\mathcal{E}}{\partial\phi^{h}}
can be computed.

Now, we compute the total derivative
\mathcal{E}_{,\phi^{h}}
for non-vanishing
\tau_{0}
and vanishing
v
, where we are again regarding
e=e_{v}
as a function of
v
. Since
\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{E}_{\xi}
is a differentiable function of
\xi=v+e
and
e=e_{v}
is a function of
v
, the total derivative of
\mathcal{E}
in
\phi^{h}
is given by

\mathcal{E}_{,\phi^{h}}=\frac{\partial\mathcal{E}}{\partial\phi^{h}}+\frac{\partial\mathcal{E}}{\partial e}\frac{\partial e}{\partial\phi^{h}}.

Picking a variation
\dot{\phi}^{h}
of
\phi^{h}
at
\phi^{h}=0
and letting
\dot{e}=\left.De\right|_{v=0}(\dot{\phi}^{h})
denote the induced variation of
e
, we have.

\dot{\Lambda}\left(\dot{\phi}^{h}+\dot{e}\right)=0.

Observe that
\left.\frac{\partial\Lambda}{\partial\phi}\right|_{\xi_{total}=0}=0
. By the differentiability properties of the mapping
\Lambda
we then have the estimate
\left\|\dot{\Lambda}\left(\dot{\phi}^{h}\right)\right\|\leq^{C}\tau_{0}
. Proposition
8.2
then gives
\left\|\dot{e}\right\|^{\sim}_{-\mu}\leq^{C}\left\|\dot{\Lambda}\left(\dot{\phi}^{h}\right)\right\|^{\sim}_{-\mu}\leq^{C}\tau_{0}\left\|\dot{\phi}^{h}\right\|
. Thus, we have

10.
Embeddedness of solutions

The surfaces

Notational conventions in this section

in the proof of Proposition

recorded at the end of the section. We will set
\tilde{\sigma}_{i,j}=d_{i}
and we let
\tilde{\tau}_{i,j}
be defined by the relation (

). We put
\tilde{r}=\left(\tilde{\sigma}^{h}_{i,j},0\right)
and
\tilde{v}=\left(\tilde{\tau}^{h}_{i,j},0\right)
. Since
\tilde{\sigma}_{i,j}
, and consequently

10.2.
Existence of embedded surfaces

The main theorem of this section is:

Proposition 10.1
.

There exist relative translations
d_{i}
such that the surface
\mathcal{C}_{d}
is embedded.

We establish embeddedness in two steps. The first step, which is relatively simple, establishes embeddedness on compact subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. The only conditions required in this step on the relative separations
d_{i}
are that they are positive, and embeddedness follows from the fact that
\tilde{\tau}_{i,j}
is much smaller than
d
. Since the solution
e=e_{v}
depends differentiably on
v
, which is much smaller than
d
, the resulting correction is small relative to
d
and embeddedness is thus preserved locally. For asymptotic embeddedness, additional conditions are required. To understand these, we need to understand carefully the asymptotics of the ends of
\mathcal{C}_{d}
for a fixed choice of
d
.

To do this, we extract asymptotic values of the solution
e=e_{v}
. By Corollary
8.5
, we have that
\|e\|^{\sim}_{-\mu}\leq^{C}\left\|v\right\|
. Thus, for fixed
i
, we have
e^{\Delta}_{i,j}\cong\left\|v\right\|\cosh^{-\mu}(j)
, and thus
e_{i,j}
is a cauchy sequence in
j
. We let
\overline{e}_{i}=\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}e_{i,j}
denote its limit, which we regard as periodic in
j
by setting
\overline{e}_{i,j}=\overline{e}_{i}
. By continuity, we have
\Lambda(\overline{e})=0
, and thus the corresponding limit
\overline{\mathcal{B}}_{i}
of the branch
\mathcal{B}_{i}
is a smooth periodic CMC surface in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Such a surface is necessarily a Delaunay end, which we prove with tools internal to this paper. Once it is established that
\overline{\mathcal{B}}_{i}
is symmetric about an axis, the asymptotics of
\overline{\mathcal{B}}_{i}
can be determined precisely from the limit data
\overline{e}=\left(\overline{\tau},\overline{\phi},\overline{\rho},\overline{f}\right)
. Precisely, it follows that the axis of
\overline{\mathcal{B}}_{i}
is parallel to
-\sin(\overline{\phi}_{i})e_{y}+\cos(\overline{\phi}_{i})e_{z}
. It then follows that the surface
\mathcal{C}_{d}
is asymptotically embedded if
\overline{\phi}^{\vartriangleright}<0
. We first record, in Lemma

below, bounds for the exact solutions
r
in terms of the approximate solution

.

Lemma 10.2
.

It holds that
\|r-\tilde{r}\|\leq^{C}\|\tilde{\tau}\|
.

Proof.

In the following, we will make the dependence of
\mathcal{E}
on

\mathcal{E}^{d}_{r}=\mathcal{E}(r+e_{r})
. Observe that
\mathcal{E}^{d}(\tilde{r})=0
. Too see this, observe that, when

and
e
vanish, the branches
\mathcal{B}_{i}
and
\mathcal{B}_{i+1}
of
\mathcal{C}
differ from each other by the translation by

and are symmetric through their axes, respectively. Thus, the horizontal necks
\mathcal{N}^{h}_{i,j}
are symmetric through vertical lines passing through their centers. When the parameter values

and

are zero, the variations induced by
\tau^{h}
are supported on the horizontal catenoids and preserve the symmetries listed above. Thus, the boundary components
\partial^{\pm}\mathcal{N}^{h}_{i,j}
of
\mathcal{N}^{h}_{i,j}

\mathcal{t}^{\pm}=\pm\sigma^{h}_{i,j}e_{y}

we can apply the branchwise translation

so that

Now, since

\mathcal{E}_{\tilde{r}}=\mathcal{E}^{d}(\tilde{r}+e_{\tilde{v}})=O(e_{\tilde{v}})=O\left(\left\|\tilde{v}\right\|\right)=O\left(\|\tilde{\tau}\|\right).

Since

we have

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\mathcal{E}^{d}_{r}-\mathcal{E}^{d}_{\tilde{r}}+O\left(\|\tilde{\tau}\|\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\int_{0}^{1}\left.\frac{\partial\mathcal{E}}{\partial r}\right|_{r(t)}dt\right)\left(r-\tilde{r}\right)+O(\|\tilde{\tau}\|),

where above we have set

As a direct consequence of Lemma

\displaystyle\left\|v\right\|\leq^{C}\left\|\tilde{\tau}\right\|.

In order to determine precisely the asymptotics of the ends in terms of their limits, we need to know that the ends are rotationally invariant about an axis, and not just that they are singly periodic. This is recorded in Lemma

Lemma 10.3
.

Each asymptotic end
\overline{\mathcal{B}}_{i}
is invariant under rotations about an axis.

Proof.

We fix

\overline{\mathcal{B}}

. Observe that, since the limit
\overline{\mathcal{B}}

minimizing the

distance over a fundamental domain. For the purposes of the proof, this is not necessary and we take, for the time being, the axis to be the line parallel to
\overline{\mathcal{t}}
and passing through the origin.

Now, suppose the claim is false. Then the normal variation field generated by rotations about the axis has a non-zero part
u
orthogonal to the jacobi fields generated by translations over fundamental domains of
\overline{\mathcal{B}}
. The function
u
is then a periodic jacobi field on
\overline{\mathcal{B}}
. We can assume that
u
normalized so that the supremum is
1
. Taking

, the asymptotic solution
\overline{e}
tends to zero and thus the asymptotic end
\overline{\mathcal{B}}
converges on compact subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
to a smooth CMC surface of rotation with axis parallel to

(Thus, it is a Delaunay surface). After possibly applying a translation, we can assume that the axis of rotation coincides the
z

u
on a Delaunay surface with small parameter value
\tau_{0}
, which is orthogonal to the translational jacobi fields over fundamental domains. The proof is finished by arguing as in the proof of Proposition
6.9
. Though the overall strategy is the same, there are enough differences that we present the argument completely. We normalize
u
so that
\sup u=1
. We can decompose
u
into its higher and lower parts
\mathring{u}
and
\overline{u}
. The lower part lies in the six dimensional space of geometric jacobi fields generated by rotations, translations, and the variation of the Delaunay parameter. Since the Delaunay variation and rotations generate linearly growing jacobi fields, it follows that
\overline{u}
is generated by a translation. However, since
u
is orthogonal to the translational jacobi fields over fundamental domains of
\overline{\mathcal{B}}
, we have that
\overline{u}
must vanish and thus

. Now, take a sequence
\tau_{0,j}
of base flux assignments tending to

and consider the corresponding sequences
\overline{\mathcal{B}}_{j}
of branches and

of periodic jacobi fields on
\underline{\mathcal{B}}_{j}
. Observe that as
\tau_{0,j}\rightarrow 0
the branch
\overline{\mathcal{B}}_{j}
converges to a limit
\overline{\mathcal{B}}_{0}
, which is a collection of spheres of radius

touching at the north and south poles–that is, the points where the unit normal coincides with

. Pick a sequence

of points realizing the supremum of

, so

. There are then several possibilities. If

remains in a fixed compact subset away from the poles, then

converges to a non-trivial bounded jacobi field on the sphere away from the north and south poles. Standard removable singularity theory then implies that it extends smoothy across the puncture to a non-trivial jacobi field on the sphere, and is thus a translational jacobi fields. However, this is a contradiction, since we assumed that
u
is orthogonal to the translational jacobi fields over each fundamental domain. Thus, we must have that

converges to a pole, which without loss of generality we can take to be the origin. After applying a small translation in the

direction, we can assume that

lies in the plane

. The rescaled Delaunay surface

then converges to a limit

that is either a flat plane or else the standard catenoid. In both cases, define the function

by
\tilde{u}_{j}(p)=u(|p_{j}|p)
. Then

is a jacobi field on
\tilde{\mathcal{B}}_{j}
that achieves its supremum, which is equal to

and converges to a nontrivial bounded jacobi field

\widetilde{\mathcal{B}}

is the punctured plane,

is the limit of functions with vanishing lower part, the lower part of
