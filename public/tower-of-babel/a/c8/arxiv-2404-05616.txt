Tomography of the spatial structure of light
M. Gil de Oliveira, A. L. S. Santos Junior, P. M. R. Lima, A. C. Barbosa, B. Pinheiro da Silva, S. Pádua, A. Z. Khoury

Abstract
In this work we study, both theoretically and experimentally, the tomography of the spatial structure of light. We consider a a spatially structured light mode that is specified by coefficients with respect to some basis, such as the Laguerre-Gaussian or Hermite-Gaussian modes. Our aim is to retrieve these coefficients given only spatially resolved intensity measurements. We explore a mathematical analogy between this problem and quantum state tomography, which allows us to readily utilize many techniques of this well established field. In order to demonstrate the robustness of our approach, we apply it to several different experimental scenarios: intense light beams in a variety of basis, the photocount regime, and beams obstructed by obstacles. The method is also extremely fast, being able to perform the tomography of a 6-dimensional state at around 50 μ \mu s. The work here presented is expected to help the development of characterization techniques in the field of structured light and in its application for both classical and quantum information protocols.



I
Introduction

The field of structured light has found promising applications in areas such as optical communication
Cozzolino et al. 2019
;
Gibson et al. 2004
;
Wang et al. 2012
, optical tweezers
Melo et al. 2020
;
Almeida et al. 2023
;
Padgett and Bowman 2011
and quantum cryptography
Mirhosseini et al. 2015
;
Sit et al. 2018
;
Vallone et al. 2014
;
D’ambrosio et al. 2012
;
Souza et al. 2008
. Central to them is the transverse spatial structure of light, and, therefore, its characterization is extremely important, as reflected by the multitude of approaches that have been proposed in recent years. Methods in which a Spatial Light Modulator (SLM) is part of the measurement apparatus are commonly used
Hiekkamäki et al. 2019
;
Jia et al. 2023
;
Bent et al. 2015
. Machine learning techniques have also been employed
da Silva et al. 2021
;
Zia et al. 2023
;
Guo et al. 2022
. These techniques have been successfully applied to the classification of structures through atmospheric turbulence
Krenn et al. 2014
;
Xiang et al. 2022
. In
Zhou et al. 2021
, a technique based on using the polarization to find the density matrix in position basis was also introduced while, in
Rambach et al. 2021
, self-guided tomography was used to perform accurate tomography in high dimensional spaces.

In this work, we intend to perform the characterization using only spatially resolved intensity measurements over our light field, which are easily implemented by cameras or a fiber coupled detector which scans the transverse plane. In order to achieve this, we explore a complete mathematical correspondence between this problem
Ježek and Hradil 2004
and the one of quantum state tomography
Paris and Řeháček 2004
;
Heinosaari and Ziman 2011
. Such correspondence provides us with a versatile method, as will be demonstrated by the multitude of different experimental settings for which we apply it. We start with intense, partially coherent light beams spanned by a basis containing only Laguerre-Gaussian modes of positive topological charge. For this kind of basis, we show that a single direct image is sufficient to perform the tomography. Next, we consider the spaces formed by fixed order modes, which contain both positive and negative topological charges. It is clear from the start that a single image will not suffice in this case, but once again we utilize the correspondence to analyze the action of mode converters
Beijersbergen et al. 1993
;
Vaity et al. 2013
;
Buono et al. 2022
;
Raymer et al. 1994
;
da Silva et al. 2021
and generalize them to our needs. We then turn to the photocount regime, where we attenuate a coherent source in such a way that individual photocounts may be identified, and study how the quality of our reconstruction technique depends on the number of detections. Finally, we go back to the intense regime but now we study the effects of obstructions on our light source, which are experimentally implemented by a blade and an iris. In particular, we study how the Fisher information for the corresponding measurement behaves as we change the amount of blockage.

Our proposal has several advantages when compared with the previous methods: first, it allows us to readily apply the complete toolbox of (quantum) information theory and quantum tomography to this problem. As we will show, we are able to achieve extremely high tomography speeds, up to an order of magnitude faster than neural network based algorithms, and there is no need to perform a training step, which is costly both computationally and experimentally. Our approach is also efficient in the sense of imposing no power loss during the measurement stage, being only restricted by the efficiency of the detectors. This stands in contrast with SLM based tomography methods, in which each passage through the SLM incurs at a considerable losses, which is undesirable, specially when working with very faint sources.

IV

V

II
Theory

II.1
Formulation of the problem

Our aim is to characterize a spatially structured light field in a sense that will be made more precise in what follows. Our light field has associated with it a photocount probability density
p(\mathbf{r})
, in such a way that a detector, which covers a region
\mathcal{R}
, has a probability to make a detection given by

p(\mathcal{R})=\int_{\mathcal{R}}p(\mathbf{r})d\mathbf{r}.

(1)

We also assume that the detections are independent from one another. The detector could be a fiber coupled photocounter, in which case the
\mathcal{R}
would be the region covered by the objective, or more simply a camera, in which case
\mathcal{R}
would be the area covered by a pixel. The relation of
p(\mathbf{r})
with our field is somewhat dependent on its regime: for classical light, be it coherent or not, the probability density is proportional to the intensity. More generally, to take quantum properties into account, we have that
p(\mathbf{r})\propto\expectationvalue{E^-(\mathbf{r}) E^+(\mathbf{r})}
, where
E^{+}(\mathbf{r})
is the positive frequency part of the electric field operator, which contains only annihilation operators, and
E^{-}(\mathbf{r})
is its Hermitian conjugate
Loudon 2000
. Furthermore,
\expectationvalue{ \bullet}
denotes the expectation value over the quantum field state.

Our problem is the following: we will assume that our field is in a state such that
p(\mathbf{r})
assumes the form

p(\mathbf{r})=\sum_{j,k=1}^{d}\rho_{jk}u_{j}(\mathbf{r})u_{k}^{*}(\mathbf{r})

(2)

for a certain finite set
\mathcal{B}=\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{d}\}
of basis functions (e.g. Laguerre-Gaussian or Hermite-Gaussian modes). We will then perform detections in a series of regions
\mathcal{R}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{R}_{M}
. We ask if, from these measurement statistics, we are able to recover the entries matrix elements
\rho_{jk}
, and, if so, how.

In order to solve this question, it will be useful to define an operator
\rho
in the space of square integrable functions, in such a way that

p(\mathbf{r})=\matrixelement{\mathbf{r}}{\rho}{\mathbf{r}}=\Tr\left(\rho\outerproduct{\mathbf{r}}{\mathbf{r}}\right).

(3)

This property is satisfied if we define
\rho
through the relation

\matrixelement{\mathbf{r}}{\rho}{\mathbf{r}^\prime}\propto\expectationvalue{E^-(\mathbf{r}) E^+(\mathbf{r}^\prime)},

(4)

where the missing normalization constant enforces

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}p(\mathbf{r})d\mathbf{r}=\Tr\rho=1.

(5)

The definition (
4
) also ensures that
\rho
is Hermitian. We may also check that it is positive: let
\ket{v}
be any vector. Define
v(\mathbf{r})=\innerproduct{\mathbf{r}}{v}
and

a_{v}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}v^{*}(\mathbf{r})E^{+}(\mathbf{r})d\mathbf{r},

(6)

which can be interpreted as the annihilation operator for a spatial mode
v(\mathbf{r})
, if
v
is normalized to
1

Fabre and Treps 2020
. We then have that

\matrixelement{v}{\rho}{v}=\expectationvalue{a^\dagger_v a_v}\geq 0,

(7)

because
a^{\dagger}_{v}a_{v}
is positive. This shows that
\rho
is also positive, so it has all the properties that characterize a density operator. It may then appear that our problem is one of quantum state tomography in disguise. In fact, there is a complete mathematical correspondence, which will form the basis of our solving strategy, as explained in a following section. Nonetheless, one must stress that
\rho

is not
the density operator of our field state, as it does not provide its complete characterization. This is because, in general, a field can not be specified only by its two point correlation function, even classically
de Oliveira et al. 2024
. To have a specific example in mind, consider a Fock state
\ket{n,u}
in a spatial mode
u(\mathbf{r})
and a coherent state
\ket{\alpha,u}
in the same spatial mode. These states are clearly different, but share the same
\rho
. The difference may be revealed in a Hanburry-Brown and Twiss type experiment, where one would observe an anti bunching effect for the Fock state.

II.2
Quantum State Tomography

We now briefly review the theory of quantum state tomography. Let
\her(\mathcal{H})\subset\mathcal{H}
be the set of Hermitian operators acting on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
. The state of a quantum system is represented by an element
\rho
of the set of positive semi-definite operators
\pos(\mathcal{H})\subset\her(\mathcal{H})
such that
\Tr\rho=1
. Observables are represented by elements
A\in\her(\mathcal{H})
so that its expectation value is given by
\expectationvalue{A}=\Tr\left(\rho A\right)
. A Positive Operator Valued Measure (POVM) is a set of observables
\{\Pi_{m}\}\subset\pos(\mathcal{H})
with the property that
\sum_{m}\Pi_{m}=\mathbbm{1}
, where
\mathbbm{1}
is the identity operator. These operators model the possible outcomes of an experiment: outcome
m
happens with probability
p_{m}=\Tr\left(\Pi_{m}\rho\right)
. This is Born’s rule. The POVM conditions ensure that
p_{m}\geq 0
and
\sum_{m}p_{m}=1
. The POVM formalism has been used for minimum quantum tomography of polarization states
Ling et al. 2006
, qubits and qudits in path states
Pimenta et al. 2010
;
Pimenta et al. 2013
;
Cardoso et al. 2019
or in vector vortex beams
Al Khafaji et al. 2022
.

To solve the tomography problem, we first choose an orthonormal set of traceless operators
\{\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{d^{2}-1}\}\subset\her(\mathcal{H})
. For definiteness, we set the
\omega
’s as the generalized Gell-Mann matrices
Bertlmann and Krammer 2008
, which come in three types:

\displaystyle X_{jk}

\displaystyle=\frac{\outerproduct{u_j}{u_k}+\outerproduct{u_k}{u_j}}{\sqrt{2}},

(8)

\displaystyle Y_{jk}

\displaystyle=\frac{i(\outerproduct{u_j}{u_k}-\outerproduct{u_k}{u_j})}{\sqrt{2}},

\displaystyle Z_{j}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{j+j^{2}}}\left(\sum_{r=1}^{j}\outerproduct{u_r}{u_r}-j\outerproduct{u_{j+1}}{u_{j+1}}\right).

In the above equations,
j
assumes the values
1,\ldots d-1
, while
k
assumes the values
j+1,\ldots,d
. Furthermore,
\{\ket{u_1},\ldots,\ket{u_d}\}
is our chosen basis set of
\mathcal{H}
.

We can then specify an arbitrary state
\rho
by a list of coefficients
\boldsymbol{\theta}=(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{d^{2}-1})
such that

\rho=\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\frac{\mathbbm{1}}{d}+\sum_{n=1}^{d^{2}-1}\theta_{n}\omega_{n}.

(9)

We call
\boldsymbol{\theta}
a generalized Bloch vector. By taking the Hilbert-Schmidt inner product of both sides with respect to the POVM elements, we arrive at

\mathbf{q}=T\boldsymbol{\theta}.

(10)

Here,
q_{m}=p_{m}-\Tr\Pi_{m}/d
and
T
is a matrix with entries
T_{mn}=\Tr\left(\Pi_{m}\omega_{n}\right)
. If
T
is injective, the linear system of equations (
10
) has a unique solution given by

\boldsymbol{\theta}=(T^{\dagger}T)^{-1}T^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{q}.

(11)

In this case, the POVM is said to be informationally complete. Otherwise, if
T
is not injective, the solution is no longer unique and the POVM is said informationally incomplete. In physical terms, this means that our measurements are not sufficient to uniquely determine the state, as there will be at least two states that give the same experimental statistics.

One should observe, nonetheless, that the probabilities that figure in (
11
) are not directly measurable, and can only be
estimated
. One simple estimation method is to substitute them by the observed experimental frequencies
\hat{p}_{m}=N_{m}/N
, where
N_{m}
is the number of times that outcome
m
was observed and
N=\sum_{m}N_{m}
is the total number of observations. Then, by applying (
11
), we get estimators
\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{LI}
and
\hat{\rho}_{LI}
for the coefficients
\boldsymbol{\theta}
and for the state
\rho
. This is the so called Linear Inversion Estimator, and is the simplest solution to the tomography problem. We utilize this estimator for a large part of the work, but it has some shortcomings: first, it may produce density matrices that are not positive semi definite. Therefore, we always project the output of this estimator to the closest valid density operator, utilizing the algorithm of Ref.
Smolin et al. 2012
. It is also only reliable for a large number of observations, otherwise the experimental frequencies may strongly deviate from the true probabilities. When necessary, we will discuss other estimators that circumvent these issues.

II.3
Fisher Information

The fisher information matrix allows us to give a quantitative information about the quality of our POVM
Řeháček et al. 2015
. Given a probability distribution
p(\xi|\boldsymbol{\theta})
for some random variable
\xi
, depending on the parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}=(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{n})
, the entries of its Fisher information matrix
I
are defined by

I_{jk}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\partial\ln p}{\partial\theta_{j}}\frac{\partial\ln p}{\partial\theta_{k}}\middle|\boldsymbol{\theta}\right],

(12)

where
\mathbb{E}\left[\bullet\middle|\boldsymbol{\theta}\right]
denotes the expectation value with respect to
p(\xi|\boldsymbol{\theta})
.

One of the main results concerning the Fisher information is the following: consider an unbiased estimator
\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
, that is, one that satisfies

\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\middle|\boldsymbol{\theta}\right]=\boldsymbol{\theta},

(13)

and define its covariance matrix
\mathcal{C}
as

\mathcal{C}_{jk}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\hat{\theta}_{j}-\theta_{j}\right)\left(\hat{\theta}_{k}-\theta_{k}\right)\middle|\boldsymbol{\theta}\right].

(14)

Then, the inequality

\mathcal{C}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\geq I^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\theta})

(15)

holds, which is the so called Crammer-Rao bound
Zhu 2014
. The above equation should be understood in the sense that
\mathcal{C}-I^{-1}
is a positive semi definite matrix. We can put this matrix inequality in a form that will be more useful for our needs by taking its trace:

\mathbb{E}\left[\norm{\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}} - \boldsymbol{\theta}}^{2}\middle|\boldsymbol{\theta}\right]\geq\Tr I^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\theta}).

(16)

Here,
\norm{\bullet}
denotes the Euclidean norm. This shows that the trace of the inverse of the Fisher information bounds the average error in the determination of the parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}
. When multiple observations of the random variable
\xi
are independent, the Fisher information for
N
observations
I^{(N)}
satisfies
I^{(N)}=NI^{(1)}
, so that the above equation reads

\mathbb{E}\left[\norm{\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}} - \boldsymbol{\theta}}^{2}\middle|\boldsymbol{\theta}\right]\geq\frac{B(\boldsymbol{\theta})}{N},

(17)

where we have defined

B(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\Tr\left\{\left[I^{(1)}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\right]^{-1}\right\}.

(18)

When
\boldsymbol{\theta}
is a generalized Bloch vector describing a density matrix
\rho
, we may yet rewrite (
17
) as

\mathbb{E}\left[\Tr\left(\hat{\rho}-\rho\right)^{2}\middle|\boldsymbol{\theta}\right]\geq\frac{B(\boldsymbol{\theta})}{N}.

(19)

In this case, the probability of a single outcome
m
is
p(m|\boldsymbol{\theta})=\Tr\left[\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta})\Pi_{m}\right]
so that

I^{(1)}_{jk}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\sum_{m}\frac{\Tr\left(\omega_{j}\Pi_{m}\right)\Tr\left(\omega_{k}\Pi_{m}\right)}{p(m|\boldsymbol{\theta})}=\left(T^{\dagger}DT\right)_{jk}\,,

(20)

where
D
is a diagonal matrix with entries
D_{mm}=1/p(m|\boldsymbol{\theta})
. We may see that, if our POVM is informationally incomplete, that is, if
T
has a zero eigenvalue, then
I
also has a zero eigenvalue, with the same eigenvector. Then, the bound (
19
) diverges, meaning that there are states for which we can get no reliable estimation.

II.4
Tomography of the Spatial Structure

In the case of spatially structured light, the POVM is the set of operators
\{\Pi(\mathcal{R}_{1}),\ldots,\Pi(\mathcal{R}_{M})\}
, where
Ježek and Hradil 2004

\Pi(\mathcal{R})=\int_{\mathcal{R}}\outerproduct{\mathbf{r}}{\mathbf{r}}d\mathbf{r},

(21)

and
\{\mathcal{R}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{R}_{M}\}
is a set of disjoint regions such that
\cup\mathcal{R}_{m}=\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Then,
\sum\Pi(\mathcal{R}_{m})=I
as a consequence of the completeness relation for the kets
\ket{\mathbf{r}}
. The matrix elements of
\Pi(\mathcal{R})
are given simply by

\Pi(\mathcal{R})_{jk}=\matrixelement{u_j}{\Pi(\mathcal{R})}{u_k}=\int_{\mathcal{R}}u_{j}^{*}(\mathbf{r})u_{k}(\mathbf{r})d\mathbf{r},

(22)

so that one may check that Born’s rule
p(\mathcal{R})=\Tr\left[\rho\Pi(\mathcal{R})\right]
agrees with (
1
) and (
2
)
if one expands
\rho
as

\rho=\sum_{j,k=1}^{d}\rho_{jk}\outerproduct{u_j}{u_k}.

(23)

The matrix elements (
22
) are all that one needs for the application of the linear inversion estimator and the calculation of the Fisher information.

II.5
Determination of the beam’s center and waist

In what follows, we will work with basis sets formed by Hermite Gaussian modes

HG_{mn}(\mathbf{r};w)=N_{mn}H_{m}\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}x}{w}\right)H_{n}\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}y}{w}\right)e^{-r^{2}/w^{2}},

(24)

or Laguerre Gaussian modes

LG_{pl}(\mathbf{r};w)=N_{pl}\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}r}{w}\right)^{\absolutevalue{l}}L_{p}^{\absolutevalue{l}}\left(\frac{2r^{2}}{w^{2}}\right)e^{-r^{2}/w^{2}+il\phi}.

(25)

Here,
H_{n}
and
L_{p}^{\absolutevalue{l}}
are Hermite and Laguerre polynomials, respectively,
w
is the beam’s waist and the
N
’s are constants that ensure that the modes are normalized to
1
.

Observe that these formulas assume a frame of reference in which the beams are centered at its origin, but that usually will not be true when one performs actual measurements: the beam could be further displaced by a vector
\mathbf{r}_{0}=(x_{0},y_{0})
. One must then find an estimator for this displacement, as well as for the beam’s waist, in order to properly apply our tomography method.

One simple solution is to, before starting the tomography, send a known calibration beam from which we can perform least squares fitting and retrieve the desired parameters. For simplicity, we send a Gaussian beam, and perform the fitting of the captured intensity utilizing the model

f(\mathbf{r};\mathbf{r}_{0},w,A,b)=b+A\exp(-2(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}_0)^2/w^2).

(26)

Here,
A
is an amplitude and
b
is a background that will also be removed from the subsequent images.

Although it is simple, this method requires a calibration beam which is known to have the same waist and center as the beam for which we will perform the tomography. This might be unavailable or it might be experimentally difficult to obtain. A second proposed solution that circumvents this issue can be found if we restrict ourselves to a subspace of a known order. The order is the parameter
N=m+n
for the Hermite-Gaussian modes and
N=2p+\absolutevalue{l}
for the Laguerre-Gaussian ones. In this case, one can estimate
x_{0},y_{0}
and
w
independently from the Bloch vector, as we discuss as follows.

The Hermite and Laguerre-Gaussian modes can be described by an operator formalism, borrowed from quantum mechanics
Andrews 2011
. We define
\hat{x},\hat{y}
as operators that act on a square integrable function
f
through the formulas
\hat{x}f(x,y)=xf(x,y)
and
\hat{y}f(x,y)=yf(x,y)
. In an analogous manner, we define
\hat{p}_{x}=-i\partial_{x}
and
\hat{p}_{y}=-i\partial_{y}
. These operators follow the standard commutation relations
\left[\hat{x},\hat{p}_{x}\right]=\left[\hat{y},\hat{p}_{y}\right]=i
. We can then define the lowering operators

a_{j}=\frac{\hat{j}}{w}+\frac{iw\hat{p}_{j}}{2},\ \ \ j=x,y,

(27)

where
w
is the waist of our modes.

The fundamental Gaussian mode
\ket{0}=HG_{00}=LG_{00}
is the simultaneous solution of the eigenvalue equations
a_{x}\ket{0}=0
and
a_{y}\ket{0}=0
. The Hermite-Gaussian modes can then be defined as

HG_{mn}=\left(a^{\dagger}_{x}\right)^{m}\left(a^{\dagger}_{y}\right)^{n}\ket{0}.

(28)

A field in a fixed order state may be expanded in the basis
u_{n}=HG_{n-1,N-n+1}(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}_{0};w),\ n=1,\ldots,N+1
where
N
is the order. Then, using the standard properties of the raising and lowering operators, one shows that, for any such field, the corresponding probability density function satisfies

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\mathbf{r}p(\mathbf{r})d\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{r}_{0},

(29a)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}r^{2}p(\mathbf{r})d\mathbf{r}=r_{0}^{2}+\frac{(N+1)w^{2}}{2}.

(29b)

By performing measurements with a camera, or similar device, and recording the frequencies
\hat{p}_{mn}
of photocounts in the pixel with coordinates
(m,n)
, one may easily estimate the desired parameters through the formulas

\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{0}=\sum_{m,n}(m,n)\hat{p}_{mn},

(30a)

\hat{w}=\sqrt{\frac{2}{N+1}\left(\sum_{m,n}(m^{2}+n^{2})\hat{p}_{mn}-\hat{r}_{0}^{2}\right)}.

(30b)

As mentioned, in this case, one needs no calibration beam, and the complete tomography may be performed using only the image of the mode of interest.

III.2
Fixed order

We next analyze the tomography of modes spanned by the basis
\mathcal{B}=\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{d}\}
where
u_{n}=HG_{n-1,d-n}
and
HG_{mn}
are the Hermite-Gaussian modes. We see that this basis now spans the entire space of order
d-1
. One can already anticipate that direct intensity measurements do not form an informationally complete POVM, as one cannot distinguish two Laguerre-Gaussian modes with opposite topological charges. More generally, modes whose field is complex conjugate to one another are indistinguishable. To see how to arrive at this conclusion within our formalism, notice that the elements of our basis are real valued, so we may conclude that

\Tr\left[Y_{jk}\Pi(\mathcal{R})\right]=\sqrt{2}\imaginary\int_{\mathcal{R}}u_{j}^{*}(\mathbf{r})u_{k}(\mathbf{r})d\mathbf{r}=0

(31)

for any region
\mathcal{R}
. Then, for a Bloch vector
\boldsymbol{\theta}
for which the only nonzero entries are those corresponding to the
Y_{jk}
, we have that
T\boldsymbol{\theta}=0
, so that
T
is not injective and the corresponding Fisher information matrix is singular.

To remedy this incompleteness problem, one may augment a POVM
\{\Pi_{m}\}
by performing an unitary transformation
U
on the state before measuring it, which generates the larger POVM
\{\Pi_{m}/2,U^{\dagger}\Pi_{m}U/2\}
. This is a standard practice, for example, in polarization tomography, where the actual measurements are projections on the horizontal and vertical polarization basis
\{\ket{H},\ket{V}\}\,
, but the informationally complete POVM can be obtained by preceding the polarization projection with unitary transformations performed by half and quarter wave plates.

Of particular interest to the tomography of the spatial structure of light is the unitary

U_{\theta}=\sum_{m,n=0}^{\infty}e^{i(m-n)\theta}\outerproduct{HG_{mn}}{HG_{mn}},

(32)

which is physically realized by an astigmatic mode converter made of cylindrical or tilted spherical lenses
Beijersbergen et al. 1993
;
Vaity et al. 2013
;
Buono et al. 2022
. The parameter
\theta
can be adjusted experimentally by tuning the propagation distance and the tilt angle of the lens
sup
. Denoting
\Pi_{\theta}(\mathcal{R})=U^{\dagger}_{\theta}\Pi(\mathcal{R})U_{\theta}\,
, we have that

\displaystyle\Tr\left[\Pi_{\theta}(\mathcal{R})X_{jk}\right]

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2}\cos\left[(j-k)\theta\right]\Tr\left[\Pi(\mathcal{R})X_{jk}\right],

(33)

\displaystyle\Tr\left[\Pi_{\theta}(\mathcal{R})Y_{jk}\right]

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2}\sin\left[(j-k)\theta\right]\Tr\left[\Pi(\mathcal{R})X_{jk}\right],

\displaystyle\Tr\left[\Pi_{\theta}(\mathcal{R})Z_{j}\right]

\displaystyle=\Tr\left[\Pi(\mathcal{R})Z_{j}\right].

Therefore, we can avoid having
\Tr\left[\Pi_{\theta}(\mathcal{R})Y_{jk}\right]=0
by properly choosing a value for
\theta
.

The tomography of
pure
states of fixed order using this technique was demonstrated in
da Silva et al. 2021
, where the standard value
\theta=\pi/2
was used. In that work, the tomography was performed using machine-learning, but there was no explanation of why the measurements performed do indeed carry enough information. The theory presented here explains why: let us consider a pure state
\ket{\psi}=\sum_{j}c_{j}\ket{u_j}
. The knowledge of the main diagonal elements
\rho_{jj}
(determined from the coefficients corresponding to the
Z_{j}
) already allows the specification of
\absolutevalue{c_j}
, while the first secondary diagonal
\rho_{j,j+1}=\left[\Tr\left(\rho X_{j,j+1}\right)+i\Tr\left(\rho Y_{j,j+1}\right)\right]/\sqrt{2}
allows the determination of the relative phases between each successive
c_{j}
, which completely characterizes the state. Then, the direct measurement gives us access to
\rho_{jj}
and
\real\rho_{j,j+1}
, while the the measurement preceded by the astigmatic transformation allows us do determine
\imaginary\rho_{j,j+1}
. The possibility of recovering
pure
states from measurements is called unique determinedness
Zhang et al. 2024
.

Note, nonetheless, that
\Tr\left[\Pi_{\pi/2}(\mathcal{R})Y_{jk}\right]
will still be zero when
j-k
is even, so that this augmented POVM is still informationally incomplete, except in the case of order
N=1
, when the only possible value for
j-k
is
1\,
. In order to achieve informational completeness for any order strictly smaller then
N
, we may choose an angle
\theta=\pi/N\,
. Then, one can verify that
\Tr\left[\Pi_{\theta}(\mathcal{R})Y_{jk}\right]\neq 0
for every allowed value of
j,k
, which allows the POVM to be informationally complete. Therefore, from direct and astigmatic imaging, one can perform the tomography of our light beam, as demonstrated by the experiment we describe next.

\lambda/2
) and a polarizing beam splitter (PBS), in order to control the intensity of each arm. Then, one arm is sent through a tilted spherical lens
(L_{T})
, which implements our mode converter, while the other arm passes through a usual spherical lens (
L_{D}
). The mode converter is set to an angle
\pi/6
, as we work with orders up to
5
. The setup is shown in Fig.

.

IV
Experiment: Photocount Regime

Up until here, we restricted ourselves to intense light beams that were measured by a standard CCD camera. This allowed us to use the simple Linear Inversion estimator, which proved itself to be quite reliable. Now, we wish to turn to a regime were the light source is extremely faint, in such a way that one can now identify the individual photocounts. In particular, we wish to study how the quality of our estimate of the state behaves as we increase the observed number of photocounts.

We can no longer utilize the Linear Inversion method because, now, there will not be a sufficient amount of observations to reliably estimate the probabilities of each outcome. This fact is taken into account by some tomographic techniques, such as Bayesian inference
Granade et al. 2016
;
Lukens et al. 2020
;
Landa et al. 2022
;
Lohani et al. 2023
;
Blume-Kohout 2010
, the variational tomography method
Maciel and Vianna 2009
;
Maciel et al. 2011
or some of the methods based on neural networks
Koutný et al. 2022
. Here, we will employ the maximum likelihood estimator
Smolin et al. 2012

\hat{\rho}_{ML}=\argmax\mathcal{L}(N_{1},\ldots,N_{M}|\rho)

(34)

where the likelihood function is defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(N_{1},\ldots,N_{M}|\rho)

\displaystyle=\prod_{m=1}^{M}p_{m}^{N_{m}}

(35)

\displaystyle=\prod_{m=1}^{M}\left[\Tr\left(\Pi_{m}\rho\right)\right]^{N_{m}}

and
N_{m}
is the number of times that outcome
m
was observed. In Eq. (
34
),
\rho
should be constrained to be a valid density operator. In order to perform the maximization, we utilized the Accelerated Projected Gradient (APG) with adaptive restart algorithm, which is described in
Shang et al. 2017
.

We use a similar experimental setup as shown in Fig.
3
, the only differences being the use of an ICCD camera, which is sensitive to individual photocounts, and a filter, in order to not saturate the measurement. Some experimental images are shown in Fig.
5
.

V
Obstructed Measurements

Suppose now that we have a set of non negative operators
\{\Pi_{1},\ldots,\Pi_{M}\}
such that the probability
p_{m}
of an outcome
m
of a measurement on the state
\rho
only satisfies

p_{m}=\Tr\left(\rho\Pi_{m}\right)/\mathcal{N}(\rho),

(36)

where
\mathcal{N}
is a normalization constant that may depend on the state, but not on the outcome. To find it we impose that the probabilities add up to one, and we get

\mathcal{N}(\rho)=\Tr\left(\rho g\right)

(37)

where we have defined

g=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\Pi_{m}.

(38)

This situation is trivial if
\{\Pi_{1},\ldots,\Pi_{M}\}
is an non normalized POVM, that is,
g=\mathcal{N}I
. Then, one may simply define
\tilde{\Pi}_{m}=\Pi_{m}/\mathcal{N}
and the new set
\{\tilde{\Pi}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{\Pi}_{M}\}
is a POVM. A more interesting situation is when
g
is no longer proportional do the identity. This may rise when
\{\Pi_{1},\ldots,\Pi_{M}\}
is actually a subset of a POVM, and, in the experiment, one only measures this subset
Maciel and Vianna 2009
;
Maciel et al. 2011
. This may happen because of experimental ease, such as in
James et al. 2001
, in which the tomography of an entangled polarization state generated by spontaneous parametric down conversion is performed. The measurements are coincidence counts on the photon pairs, which are preceded by wave plates and polarizers. One then adjusts the wave plates in order to perform projections in different states. The use of polarizers, instead of a polarizing beam splitter and extra detectors, means that there are unobserved photons, even when losses are neglected. The reference
James et al. 2001
proposes a set of
16
projective measurements which are shown to be enough to reconstruct any state, despite not being a POVM, not even a non normalized one.

In the case of the transverse structure of light, this may arise when the measurement regions
\mathcal{R}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{R}_{M}
are still disjoint but do not cover the entire plane, as was actually assumed previously. Then, denoting
\tilde{\mathcal{R}}=\cup_{m}\mathcal{R}_{m}
, we have that

g=\int_{\tilde{\mathcal{R}}}\outerproduct{\mathbf{r}}{\mathbf{r}}d\mathbf{r}.

(39)

This will actually always be the case, because it is impossible to have detectors with infinite spacial extent. What made us be able to neglect this fact was that
g
was
approximately
the identity operator
restricted
to our basis functions. This might not be the case when one is performing measurements with a fiber that scans the transverse plane, or as we will also investigate experimentally, when there are objects blocking our beam. More specifically, we want to evaluate how the introduction of obstacles affects the Fisher information, and how can we still perform the tomography despite of these obstacles. This study has practical relevance for free space communication with structured light. In this scenario, the beam propagates through large distances and may arrive at the detection apparatus with a large transversal area. It is therefore important to understand what are the consequences of a partial detection in our ability to recognize structured light modes.

In order to answer these questions, let us perform the Cholesky factorization
g=A^{\dagger}A
, where
A
is an upper triangular matrix. We assume that
g
, and therefore,
A
are invertible. If it was not the case, there would be a state
\ket{\psi}
such that
\expectationvalue{g}{\psi}=\sum\expectationvalue{\Pi_m}{\psi}=0
. As
\expectationvalue{\Pi_m}{\psi}\geq 0
, we could conclude that
\expectationvalue{\Pi_m}{\psi}=0
for every
m
, and, therefore,
\ket{\psi}
would be
undetectable
. With that assumption in mind, we define a new set of operators

\tilde{\Pi}_{m}=(A^{-1})^{\dagger}\Pi_{m}A^{-1},

(40)

which form a POVM, because

\sum_{m=1}^{M}\tilde{\Pi}_{m}=(A^{-1})^{\dagger}gA^{-1}=I.

(41)

If we also introduce the effective state

\tilde{\rho}=\frac{A\rho A^{\dagger}}{\Tr\left(A\rho A^{\dagger}\right)}=\frac{A\rho A^{\dagger}}{\Tr\left(\rho g\right)},

(42)

we have that

\Tr\left(\tilde{\rho}\tilde{\Pi}_{m}\right)=\frac{\Tr\left(\rho\Pi_{m}\right)}{\Tr\left(\rho g\right)}=p_{m}.

(43)

Then, our measurement probabilities are the same as if we were measuring the state
\tilde{\rho}
with respect to the POVM
\{\tilde{\Pi}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{\Pi}_{M}\}
. We can then use all of the techniques described previously to perform the tomography, which will reconstruct
\tilde{\rho}
. Finally, in order to recover
\rho
, we invert relation (
43
), obtaining

\rho=\frac{A^{-1}\tilde{\rho}(A^{-1})^{\dagger}}{\Tr\left[A^{-1}\tilde{\rho}(A^{-1})^{\dagger}\right]}.

(44)

When the operators
\Pi_{1},\ldots,\Pi_{M}
are a subset of a POVM, we may give a nice interpretation to formula (
42
). We may assume that we first perform a measurement consisting of determining if the state is in the measured set or not. The probability of a positive result is then
\Tr\left(\rho g\right)
and (
42
) is the post measurement state, according to the measurement postulate.

We also wish to calculate the Fisher information in this new case. This can be done by plugging (
36
) into the definition (
12
), and one arrives at

I^{(1)}_{jk}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\frac{\Tr\left(\tilde{\omega}_{j}\tilde{\Pi}_{m}\right)\Tr\left(\tilde{\omega}_{k}\tilde{\Pi}_{m}\right)}{p_{m}}-\Tr\tilde{\omega}_{j}\Tr\tilde{\omega}_{k},

(45)

where we have defined

\tilde{\omega}_{j}=\frac{A\omega_{j}A^{\dagger}}{\Tr\left(\rho g\right)}.

(46)

\ket{u_1}

B
for the mode

if we make the same number of observations

VI
Conclusion

In this work, we explored an approach to the recognition of the spatial structure of light based on the formalism of quantum state tomography. As demonstrated by the experimental results here presented, the method is extremely versatile, working with a variety of basis, and being straightforwardly applied to both the intense and photocount regimes, as well as to obstructed beams. We are also able to immediately apply the extensive toolbox of quantum state tomography to this problem, as evidenced by our analysis of the Fisher information relative to the estimation of the states. Nonetheless, there are still areas in which we believe that our formalism could be readily applied, such as mode classification, as opposed to tomography, the modeling of turbulence as a quantum channel and its subsequent tomography, and the recognition of vortex beams, just to name a few. The method is also extremely fast, as discussed in the Appendix
A
. The results and methods reported here will also be useful for optical communication protocols both in classical and quantum domains, as well as for the characterization of spatially structured light modes in general.
