The QAOA gets stuck starting from a good classical string
Madelyn Cain, Edward Farhi, Sam Gutmann, Daniel Ranard, Eugene Tang

Abstract
The Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA) is designed to maximize a cost function over bit strings. While the initial state is traditionally a uniform superposition over all strings, it is natural to try expediting the QAOA: first use a classical algorithm to produce some good string, and then run the standard QAOA starting in the computational basis state associated with that string. Here we report numerical experiments that show this method of initializing the QAOA fails dramatically, exhibiting little to no improvement of the cost function. We provide multiple analytical arguments for this lack of improvement, each of which can be made rigorous under different regimes or assumptions, including at nearly linear depths. We emphasize that our negative results only apply to our simple incarnation of the warm-start QAOA and may not apply to other approaches in the literature. We hope that our theoretical analysis will inform future algorithm design.



1
Introduction

The Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA)
[
FGG14
]
is designed to find good approximate solutions to the problem of maximizing a cost function defined on bit strings. The standard QAOA starts in the uniform superposition of all classical bit string and applies cost function-dependent unitaries, with the goal of improving the cost function beyond its expected value in the initial state. It is natural to imagine running a classical algorithm to produce a good string then initializing the QAOA in the associated computational basis state
and looking for improvement. Variations of this idea have been explored in existing literature under the name of
warm-start QAOA
.

Here we seek to theoretically understand a counter-intuitive phenomenon: in extensive numerical experiments involving small and large instances at low and high depths, the QAOA makes effectively
zero
progress when starting from a single warm start string. That is, nearly every good classical string appears to be a local maximum that cannot be improved by
any
QAOA circuit at any numerically accessible depth.
The consistency of our numerics suggests there is an underlying theoretical reason why the QAOA with a warm start gets stuck. In this work, we provide several analytic arguments to explain this phenomenon. Each argument has a different flavor and can be made rigorous in different regimes or under different assumptions. Section
5
presents an argument for small angles and low depths, Section
6
presents an argument for constant depths that rests on a thermality assumption, and Section
7
presents an argument for sub-linear depths that rests on an assumption about how the number of strings decreases at higher cost.

We emphasize that our results apply to the case where the QAOA is initialized in a single classical string and the unitary operators comprising the QAOA do not depend explicitly on the initial string; they do not necessarily apply to possibly more viable approaches to the warm-start QAOA in the literature
[
Tat+20
,
EMW21
]
.

2
Standard and single-string warm-start QAOA

2.1
A quick review of the standard QAOA

The standard QAOA starts in the uniform superposition of all
n
-bit strings
\ket{s}=\frac{1}{2^{n/2}}\sum_{z}\ket{z}
, and applies a sequence of
2p
unitaries depending on the
2p
parameters
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
and
\boldsymbol{\beta}
, producing

\ket{\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta}}=e^{-i\beta_{p}B}e^{-i\gamma_{p}C}\dots e^{-i\beta_{1}B}e^{-i\gamma_{1}C}\ket{s},

(1)

where
B=\sum_{i}X_{i}
is the sum of the single-qubit
X
operators, and
C
is the diagonal cost function operator. The goal is then to find the parameters
\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta}
to make
\bra{\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta}}C\ket{\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta}}
as large as possible. We focus on graph-based problems such as MaxCut, where the cost function can be written as

C(z)=\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle\in E}C_{\langle i,j\rangle}(z_{i},z_{j}),

(2)

where
\langle i,j\rangle
ranges over all edges of a graph
G=(V,E)
. For example, the MaxCut cost function is

\displaystyle C_{\mathrm{MC}}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle\in E}(1-Z_{i}Z_{j}),

(3)

which counts the number of cut edges. The cut fraction is defined as the number of cut edges divided by the total number of edges.
It will often be convenient to consider the simpler MaxCut cost function operator,

\displaystyle C_{Z}=-\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle\in E}Z_{i}Z_{j}.

(4)

2.2
The QAOA starting from a good classical string

In this paper, we focus on the QAOA starting at a computational basis state
\ket{w}
, where
w
is a good string, with operators in the same form as the previous section. Note that when we start in
\ket{w}
, the first unitary
e^{-i\gamma_{1}C}
in equation (
1
) only introduces a phase, so effectively the state becomes

\ket{\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta},w}=U(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta})|w\rangle=e^{-i\beta_{k}B}e^{-i\gamma_{k-1}C}\dots e^{-i\gamma_{1}C}e^{-i\beta_{1}B}\ket{w},

(5)

which depend on the
2k-1
parameters
\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{k}
and
\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{k-1}
. In keeping with the convention of the standard QAOA, where there are
2p
parameters, we define the QAOA starting on a classical string with a fractional value of
p
so that
2p=2k-1
.

Given
w
, the goal is to maximize
\bra{\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta},w}C\ket{\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta},w}
by finding the optimal parameters

\displaystyle(\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{w},\boldsymbol{\beta}_{w})=\argmax_{(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta})}\,\langle w|U^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta})CU(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta})|w\rangle.

(6)

We then define the optimal QAOA unitary for the string
w
,

\displaystyle U_{w}=U(\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{w},\boldsymbol{\beta}_{w}),

(7)

which depends on
w
only through the optimal parameters
(\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{w},\boldsymbol{\beta}_{w})
.

2.3
Cost function as a sum over neighborhoods

We now review how the expected cost function may be organized as a sum over subgraphs, as in
[
FGG14
]
. This is instructive for analyzing large graphs at constant
p
in Sections
3.2
and
6
. We consider the warm-start QAOA with initial classical string
w
and expected cost
\bra{w}U^{\dagger}CU\ket{w}
, where
U=U(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta})
is the QAOA unitary.
In this regime, the operator
U^{\dagger}C_{\langle i,j\rangle}U
only acts non-trivially on the “edge neighborhood” of
\langle i,j\rangle
: vertices within graph distance
r
of
\langle i,j\rangle
, where the radius
r=p-\frac{1}{2}
is the number of applications of
e^{-i\gamma C}
in the QAOA circuit. Because
U^{\dagger}C_{\langle i,j\rangle}U
only acts non-trivially on the edge neighborhood of
\langle i,j\rangle
, we can write

\displaystyle\langle w|U^{\dagger}CU|w\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle\in E}\langle w|U^{\dagger}C_{\langle i,j\rangle}U|w\rangle.

(8)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle\in E}\langle w_{\langle i,j\rangle}|U^{\dagger}C_{\langle i,j\rangle}U|w_{\langle i,j\rangle}\rangle.

(9)

where
w_{\langle i,j\rangle}
refer to the subset of bits in the corresponding edge neighborhood.
For constant
p
on a bounded-degree graph, this sum can be handled with low computational cost: each term can be computed using only the Hilbert space of the qubits in the edge neighborhood and the values
w_{\langle i,j\rangle}
.

B

16

14
vertices, albeit with rare exceptions discussed in Appendix
C.1
.

. We prepare good classical strings using two classical algorithms: simulated annealing and Goemans-Williamson

1000n

,

4
Failure on a Decoupled
Problem

A simple intuition for why starting from a good string might fail is as follows: consider the cost function
C_{\langle i,j\rangle}
on edge
\langle i,j\rangle
for MaxCut. For a given string
w
, the clause is either satisfied or not, so we can suggestively write the expectation of
C
after applying the QAOA unitary
U
as

\displaystyle\bra{w}U^{\dagger}C_{\langle i,j\rangle}U\ket{w}=1-\varepsilon_{\langle i,j\rangle}

(10)

in the satisfied case, and

\displaystyle\bra{w}U^{\dagger}C_{\langle i,j\rangle}U\ket{w}=\delta_{\langle i,j\rangle}

(11)

in the unsatisfied case (note that if
U=1
then
\varepsilon_{\langle i,j\rangle}
and
\delta_{\langle i,j\rangle}
are zero). Now imagine that the average of
\varepsilon_{\langle i,j\rangle}
over the satisfied edges is equal to the average of
\delta_{\langle i,j\rangle}
over the unsatisfied edges. In a good string, there are more satisfied edges than unsatisfied, so we have

\displaystyle\bra{w}U^{\dagger}CU\ket{w}\leq\bra{w}C\ket{w},

(12)

where
C
is the total cost over all edges.
Therefore, no improvement would be possible.

We now give a simple example where exactly this happens. Consider a graph of
2m
vertices consisting of
m
pairs of vertices, each pair connected by an edge. Focus on the first copy which has edge
\langle 1,2\rangle
. At any depth, the QAOA consists of applications of the unitaries that are exponentials of phases times
X_{1}+X_{2}
or
Z_{1}Z_{2}
. Note that these operators commute with the swap operator on 1 and 2 as well as the operator
X_{1}X_{2}
. The eigenstates and eigenvalues of these two operators are given in Table
3
.

Note that
\ket{a},\ket{b}
have unique quantum numbers so under any QAOA unitary they can each only pick up a phase, but
\ket{c},\ket{d}
can mix as (up to an overall phase)

\displaystyle U\ket{c}

\displaystyle=\cos\theta\ket{c}+e^{i\phi}\sin\theta\ket{d},

(13)

\displaystyle U\ket{d}

\displaystyle=e^{-i\phi}\sin\theta\ket{c}-\cos\theta\ket{d}.

(14)

Then the QAOA initialized in the unsatisfied string
\ket{+1,+1}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{c}+\ket{a})
evolves as

\displaystyle U\ket{+1,+1}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\cos\theta\ket{c}+e^{i\phi}\sin\theta\ket{d}+e^{i\eta_{1}}\ket{a})

(15)

where
\eta_{1}
is another phase.
Therefore, the expected value of
C
goes from 0 to
\frac{1}{2}\sin^{2}\theta
. Similarly, initializing the QAOA in the satisfied string
\ket{+1,-1}
, we find that the expected value of
C
goes from 1 to
1-\frac{1}{2}\sin^{2}\theta
.
So for the
m
-edge problem starting in any string
w
, every unsatisfied clause is improved by
\frac{1}{2}\sin^{2}\theta
and every satisfied edge degrades by the same amount. Therefore, no matter the depth, the QAOA starting in a good string cannot improve upon its initial cost.

We contrast this with how the usual QAOA performs. Starting in the uniform superposition at
p=1
with
\gamma=\pi/2
and
\beta=\pi/8
, we go to the perfect cut with probability
1
. Note that for the standard QAOA, there is an asymptotic performance guarantee in that the maximum expectation value is guaranteed to reach the optimum as
p\rightarrow\infty

[
FGG14
]
. This example is notable in showing that no such guarantee is present for the case of warm-start QAOA.

5
Small angles and low depth

When improvements are seen in the numerical experiments, they are always small. One explanation for this could be that the optimal
\beta
’s, which drive the mixing, are also small. Therefore, in this section we examine when improvements are possible for small
\beta
’s. First we show that for MaxCut at
p=1/2
, no improvement is possible for any
\beta_{1}
. Next we give two necessary and sufficient conditions for improvement at
p=3/2
when
\beta_{1},\beta_{2}
are small. We find that in our MaxCut numerical experiments at
p=3/2
, improvement was seen if and only if one of our conditions were satisfied. Consistent with our intuition, improvement is possible only if it is possible for small
\beta
’s.

5.1
Quantum walk starting on a classical string (
p=1/2
)

The expected cost for
p=1/2
after evolving under
B
is given by

\displaystyle\bra{w}e^{i\beta_{1}B}C_{Z}e^{-i\beta_{1}B}\ket{w}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle}\bra{w}e^{i\beta_{1}B}Z_{i}Z_{j}e^{-i\beta_{1}B}\ket{w}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle}\bra{w}(\cos(2\beta_{1})Z_{i}+\sin(2\beta_{1})Y_{i})(\cos(2\beta_{1})Z_{j}+\sin(2\beta_{1})Y_{j})\ket{w}

\displaystyle=\cos^{2}(2\beta_{1})C_{Z}(w).

(16)

Therefore, if
w
is a good starting string, meaning that
C_{Z}(w)>0
,
then
\beta_{1}=0
is optimal and the optimized expected cost remains
C_{Z}(w)
.

5.2
Warm starting at
p=3/2
for MaxCut on
3
-regular graphs

We now look at the
p=3/2
QAOA warm starting in
|w\rangle
with both
\beta
’s small but
\gamma
arbitrary. We focus on MaxCut for 3-regular graphs. We state a necessary and sufficient condition for improvement here, and give the detailed derivation in Appendix
D
.

First, let us define the quantity

\displaystyle\delta_{i}=(C_{i}-C(w))/2,

(17)

where
C_{i}=\bra{w}X_{i}CX_{i}\ket{w}
measures the cost of
w
after flipping bit
i
. For MaxCut on 3-regular graphs,
|\delta_{i}|=
1 or 3. At
p=3/2
, we find that improvement of the cost is possible for small
\beta
’s if and only if

\displaystyle\sum_{|\delta_{i}|=1}\delta_{i}>0\qquad\text{or}\qquad\sum_{i}\delta_{i}^{3}>0.

(18)

If
w
is a local maximum with respect to bit flips, then all of the
\delta_{i}
are negative and neither condition can be met. We expect that for most good strings, neither of these two conditions can be met. We make use of the identity

\displaystyle\sum_{i}\delta_{i}=-2C_{Z}(w),

(19)

which is straightforward to establish. Because
C_{Z}(w)>0
for a good starting string, the
\delta_{i}
are usually negative, meaning that both conditions (
18
) are unlikely. As the goodness of the starting string increases there will be fewer positive
\delta_{i}
, so the conditions are even less likely to be met. This is consistent with the numerics on small MaxCut instances at
p=3/2
in Section
3.1
where there are fewer improvements on the better strings. Further, we expect that as
n
increases, fluctuations that allow the conditions to be met will become even more rare. This is consistent with the findings in Section
3.2
on large MaxCut instances, where the condition is never met and improvement is never seen for thousands of initial strings. In all of our numerics in Section
3
, improvement was seen at
p=3/2
if and only if equation (
18
) was satisfied.

6
Thermal argument for getting stuck

In this section we give two upper bounds for the progress made by the single-string warm-start QAOA at constant depth on large bounded-degree graphs. The first bound shows that strings chosen uniformly at random are likely to get stuck. The second bound instead considers strings chosen from a finite-temperature thermal ensemble, and the argument uses the
principle of minimum energy from thermodynamics in equation (
97
).

6.1
Starting from a uniformly random string

We first consider the case of starting the QAOA with a classical bit string chosen uniformly at random. Our results apply to generic cost functions for general bounded-degree graphs,

C(z)=\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle\in E}C_{\langle i,j\rangle}(z_{i},z_{j}),

(20)

with
m
edges at any constant depth. We define the average value per edge of the cost function,

\displaystyle\overline{c}=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{z\in\{-1,1\}^{n}}\frac{1}{m}C(z).

(21)

Using the fact that
C
is a sum of terms, most of which are uncorrelated because of the bounded degree, one can show that for any
\epsilon>0
, and
w
chosen uniformly at random,

\displaystyle\Pr_{w}\left(\left|\frac{1}{m}C(w)-\bar{c}\right|\geq\frac{1}{m^{1/2-\varepsilon}}\right)\longrightarrow 0,\quad\text{as }m\rightarrow\infty.

(22)

This implies that in the limit of large graph size
m
, almost all strings have cost function values extremely close to the average value
\overline{c}
.

In Appendix
E.1
we prove the following theorem, which states that typical strings cannot be improved by constant depth QAOA.

Theorem 6.1
.

For any
\epsilon>0
, and
w
chosen uniformly at random,

\displaystyle\Pr_{w}\left(\frac{1}{m}\langle w|U_{w}^{\dagger}CU_{w}|w\rangle-\frac{1}{m}C(w)\geq\frac{1}{m^{1/2-\varepsilon}}\right)\rightarrow 0,\qquad\text{as }m\rightarrow\infty,

(23)

where
U_{w}
is the optimal constant-depth QAOA unitary for each
w
.

Therefore, we have that for nearly all initial strings, the constant-depth QAOA can only improve the cost function (per edge) by an amount that vanishes in the limit of large graphs. While this result does not apply to warm starts, whose starting cost value per edge
C(w)/m
is substantially larger than
\bar{c}
, it is very surprising that even starting from a mediocre string, the QAOA fails to make progress.

Equation (
22
) implies that warm starts form a vanishingly small fraction of all strings. Next, we give results on these rare warm-start strings.

6.2
Starting from a typical good string

We work in the setting of
d
-regular graphs with a cost function of the form of equation (
20
).
For typical large random
d
-regular graphs, almost all of the local neighborhoods are trees. Fixing a neighborhood radius
r
(corresponding to the QAOA with
p=r+\frac{1}{2}
, see Section
2.3
), we define the fraction
\delta
of edges whose neighborhoods are
not
trees as

\displaystyle\delta=1-\frac{|E_{T}|}{m},

(24)

where
E_{T}
denotes the set of edges whose neighborhood of radius
r
is a tree. Our argument will be useful in the case that
\delta
is small. For random
d
-regular graphs with
n\to\infty
with
d
and
r
fixed,
\delta=O\left(1/n\right)
(
[
JLR00
]
, Theorem 9.5) is vanishingly small.

We introduce two ensembles associated with the warm-start string
w
. For any classical string
w\in\{-1,1\}^{n}
and a mostly locally tree-like
G
we define its
local ensemble
by

\displaystyle\rho_{w,\mathrm{tree}}=\frac{1}{|E_{T}|}\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle\in E_{T}}|w_{\langle i,j\rangle}\rangle\langle w_{\langle i,j\rangle}|,

(25)

where
w_{\langle i,j\rangle}
denotes the restriction of
w
onto the local tree neighborhood
T_{\langle i,j\rangle}
, and
T_{\langle i,j\rangle}
denotes the tree neighborhood centered around edge
\langle i,j\rangle\in E_{T}
. Each tree neighborhood has a fixed number of vertices, so
w_{\langle i,j\rangle}
is a string of that length, and
\rho_{w,\mathrm{tree}}
is a density matrix over the corresponding number of qubits.

Let

\displaystyle\rho_{\beta}=\frac{1}{\mathrm{Tr}\big(e^{-\beta(-C)}\big)}e^{-\beta(-C)}

(26)

be the global thermal density matrix with respect to the Hamiltonian
H=-C
, at inverse temperature
\beta
chosen so that
\mathrm{Tr}(C\rho_{\beta})=C(w)
. Here we introduce a minus sign because in thermodynamics we associate low temperatures with low energies, whereas for us good strings have high
C
.

We will consider the reduced density matrix of
\rho_{\beta}
onto the tree neighborhood
T_{\langle i,j\rangle}
, denoted
\mathrm{Tr}_{G\backslash T_{\langle i,j\rangle}}\left(\rho_{\beta}\right)
.
Because all the tree neighborhoods
T_{\langle i,j\rangle}
are isomorphic subgraphs, we can identify their associated Hilbert spaces. We consider each reduced operator
\mathrm{Tr}_{G\backslash T_{\langle i,j\rangle}}\left(\rho_{\beta}\right)
to act on the same abstract Hilbert space (the same Hilbert space implicit in the definition of
\rho_{w,\mathrm{tree}}
). With this identification, we can define the average over neighborhoods,

\displaystyle\rho_{\beta,\mathrm{tree}}=\frac{1}{|E_{T}|}\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle\in E_{T}}\mathrm{Tr}_{G\backslash T_{\langle i,j\rangle}}\left(\rho_{\beta}\right).

(27)

Again, both
\rho_{w,\mathrm{tree}}
and
\rho_{\beta,\mathrm{tree}}
act on the Hilbert space of a single tree neighborhood: although their definitions involve the sum of terms associated to different neighborhoods, these neighborhoods are all isomorphic trees, and we implicitly identify the neighborhoods when summing the terms.

We now give our main result, an upper bound on how much the cost function of a warm-start string
w
can improve under the QAOA at fixed depth. The upper bound depends on a quantity
\varepsilon_{w}
, which we call the “thermality coefficient”, that measures the distance between the ensembles
\rho_{w,\mathrm{tree}}
and
\rho_{\beta,\mathrm{tree}}
:

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{w}=\|\rho_{\beta,\mathrm{tree}}-\rho_{w,\mathrm{tree}}\|_{1},

(28)

where
\beta
is the inverse temperature chosen so that

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}(C\rho_{\beta})=C(w)

(29)

and where

\|\rho-\sigma\|_{1}\equiv\mathrm{Tr}\Big(\sqrt{(\rho-\sigma)^{\dagger}(\rho-\sigma)}\Big)

denotes the trace norm. Both
\rho_{\beta,\mathrm{tree}}
and
\rho_{w,\mathrm{tree}}
are diagonal in the computational basis, i.e., they are classical probability distributions over strings, in which case
\varepsilon_{w}
is also equal to twice the total variation distance. When
\varepsilon_{w}
is small, we refer to
w
as being “locally thermal”. Note that
\varepsilon_{w}
depends on the QAOA depth
p
which defines the neighborhood size. Our main result, stated next and proven in Appendix
E.2
, shows that if
w
is locally thermal, i.e., if
\varepsilon_{w}
is small, then
w
can only improve by a small amount.

Theorem 6.2
.

Consider the single-string warm-start QAOA initialized in a string
w
with thermality coefficient
\varepsilon_{w}
, and let
\delta
be the fraction of neighborhoods whose edges are not trees. Then

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m}\langle w|U^{\dagger}_{w}CU_{w}|w\rangle\leq c(w)+2\varepsilon_{w}+4\delta,

(30)

where
c(w)=C(w)/m
is the cut fraction of the original string
w
.

Because
\delta
is small for random graphs, the heart of the result is the dependence on
\varepsilon_{w}
. If
w
is locally thermal, then
w
can only improve by a small amount. In Appendices
E.3
and
E.4
, we give theoretical and numerical evidence that
\varepsilon_{w}
scales as
1/\sqrt{m}
for typical good strings
w
.

7
Compression argument for getting stuck

In this section we develop another argument to upper bound the progress made by the QAOA starting from a good string. This result is complementary to the previous results and uses a different assumption. We imagine that the initial good string is chosen uniformly at random from the set of all strings with cost
C_{0}
. We seek to improve the cost above some target
C_{1}
. Our argument will produce a useful bound whenever the number of strings
d_{0}
at cost
C_{0}
is much larger than the number of strings
d_{1}
with cost at least
C_{1}
. In particular, if the ratio
d_{1}/d_{0}
is suppressed exponentially in the size of the graph, then with high probability, the randomly chosen initial string cannot be improved beyond
C_{1}
using depth
p=O(n^{q})
for
q<1
.

We refer to this argument as a “compression” argument because of the following intuition. Consider a fixed QAOA unitary
U
. If there are many more strings at cost
C_{0}
than at cost above
C_{1}
, then due to unitarity,
U
cannot map all (or even most) of the strings from cost
C_{0}
to above
C_{1}
. That is, the unitary cannot “compress” a larger space (of strings at
C_{0}
) to a smaller space (of strings above
C_{1}
). This is the core of the argument. Of course, there is more than just one QAOA unitary, so there is no need for a single unitary
U
to map all the strings at
C_{0}
to above
C_{1}
. Instead, one can use different QAOA unitaries
U_{w}
that depend on the initial string
w
, hoping that for each
w
at cost
C_{0}
there is
some

U_{w}
such that
U_{w}|w\rangle
has cost at least
C_{1}
. However, this is impossible when the depth
p
is not too large, because the space of unitaries that can be generated by the QAOA at depth
p
is small in the appropriate sense.

Our results prior to this section only characterize the improvement in expected cost, without explicitly bounding the probability of obtaining a good string after measurement. However, for the case of states produced by constant-depth circuits, one can show the distribution of measured costs is concentrated about the expected cost
[
FGG14
]
. In contrast, here we will consider circuits of super-constant depth, so we must take care with the distinction between the expected cost and the distribution of measured costs.
To this end, we introduce the following notion of
improvable strings
.

Definition 7.1
.

A string
w
is
p
-improvable to cost
C_{1}
, with a selection probability at least
\Delta
, if there exists a depth-
p
QAOA unitary
U_{w}
such that a measurement of
U_{w}|w\rangle
in the computational basis has a probability at least
\Delta
of yielding a string with cost at least
C_{1}
.

Theorem 7.2
.

Consider warm-start QAOA at depth
p
, starting with a string chosen uniformly at random among strings with cost
C_{0}
. Let
d_{0}
be the number of strings at cost
C_{0}
, and let
d_{1}
be the number of strings with cost at least
C_{1}
. Then with probability at least
1-\epsilon
, the initial string will not be improvable to cost
C_{1}
using selection probability at least
\epsilon
, where

\displaystyle\epsilon=\left(\frac{2d_{1}}{d_{0}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2p+2}}(16\pi pn^{2})^{\frac{p}{p+1}}.

(31)

This theorem is most useful when
d_{1}/d_{0}
is exponentially suppressed in
n
, i.e.  when there are exponentially more strings near the starting cost than above the target cost. Then
\epsilon
will be small for
p
sub-linear in
n
, and we conclude that with high probability, the initial string will not be improvable.

More concretely, suppose that there are exponentially (in
n
) more strings at cost
C_{0}
than there are strings with cost above
C_{1}
. This is often the case when
C_{1}-C_{0}
scales with
n
, and we provide further justification below. In that case we have

\displaystyle\frac{d_{1}}{d_{0}}=O(e^{-an})

(32)

for some constant
a>0
. Consider a depth
p=O(n^{q})
for
q<1
. Then Theorem
7.2
holds with

\displaystyle\epsilon=O(e^{-bn^{1-q}}),

(33)

for some constant
b>0
, decaying faster than any polynomial in
n
. Thus the probability of improvement is very small at large
n
.

To further justify the scaling of (
32
), we consider the MaxCut cost function of the decoupled graph in Section
4
, which has
m=n/2
pairs of vertices, each pair connected by an edge. The number of strings as a function of cost (i.e., the density of states) is binomial, with variance
m
. For
C_{1}-C_{0}\propto m
, one obtains (
32
). More generally, one also expects (
32
) whenever the free energy associated to
C
is extensive since
S=-\partial F/\partial T
then implies an extensive entropy. The free energy of MaxCut on
d
-regular graphs exhibits this behavior, as shown (at least above the phase transition) in
[
Coj+22
]
. For other classes of problems with random local terms, also see Proposition 4 of
[
Dal+22
]
, where their upper bound on the number of solutions as a function of cost indicates similar exponential scaling.

8
Conclusion

This work was motivated by numerical experiments on the single-string warm-start QAOA showing zero improvement for almost all good initial strings from the starting cost value. We presented several theoretical results to explain these consistent observations. This behavior stands in stark contrast to the standard QAOA initialized in the uniform superposition over all bit strings, which appears to make consistent progress toward the optimum.

A.1
Starting from a bad string

A bad string is one whose cost function per edge

Appendix B
Maximum Independent Set

In this section, we show using another example that the QAOA gets stuck starting on a good string. We look at the Maximum Independent Set problem: given a graph
G=(V,E)
, the goal is to find a maximum size subset of vertices which have no edge connecting any pair of them. Formally, we want to find a string of bits
b
such that the Hamming weight
W(b)=\sum_{i}b_{i}
is maximized, subject to the constraint that
\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle\in E}b_{i}b_{j}=0
. Note that in this section we are using bits
b_{i}
that are
\{0,1\}
-valued, with corresponding quantum operators
\hat{b}_{i}=(I-Z_{i})/2
. To make this problem amenable to the usual QAOA, where any bit string is a valid input, we use the relaxed cost function

\displaystyle C(b)=\sum_{i\in V}b_{i}-\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle\in E}b_{i}b_{j}\equiv W(b)-K(b),

(35)

which we aim to maximize. Given a bit string
b
with cost
C(b)
, we can prune it, fixing violations
b_{i}b_{j}=1
one at a time by setting either
b_{i}
or
b_{j}
equal to 0. Fixing one violation decreases
W
by
1
but also decreases
K
by at least
1
, so
C
does not decrease. Continuing to prune until there are no violations, we obtain an independent set of size at least
C(b)
. This also shows that the maximum of
C
is the size of the largest independent set.
Note that for a
d
-regular graph we can write the cost function as

\displaystyle C(b)=W(b)-K(b)=\sum_{\langle j,k\rangle\in E}\left[\frac{1}{d}(b_{j}+b_{k})-b_{j}b_{k}\right],

(36)

which shows that this cost function is a sum of terms which act the same way on each edge, satisfying the uniformity assumption required for our statistical argument in Section
6.2
.

We now provide numerical and analytic evidence that the single-string warm-start QAOA fails for Maximum Independent Set. We first see numerically at high
p
that the warm-start QAOA fails at low bit number. Then, we show analytically that improvement is impossible at
p=1/2
if the QAOA is initialized in an independent set obtained by running a standard greedy algorithm.

We now analyze the performance of the

), for all
\beta_{1}

\displaystyle\bra{w}e^{i\beta_{1}B}Ce^{-i\beta_{1}B}\ket{w}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in V}\bra{w}e^{i\beta_{1}X_{i}}\hat{b}_{i}e^{-i\beta_{1}X_{i}}\ket{w}-\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle\in E}\bra{w}e^{i\beta_{1}X_{i}}\hat{b}_{i}e^{-i\beta_{1}X_{i}}e^{i\beta_{1}X_{j}}\hat{b}_{j}e^{-i\beta_{1}X_{j}}\ket{w}

(37)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in V}\bra{w}\left(\sin^{2}\beta_{1}+[1-2\sin^{2}\beta_{1}]\hat{b}_{i}\right)\ket{w}

\displaystyle\quad-\sum_{\langle ij\rangle\in E}\bra{w}\left(\sin^{2}\beta_{1}+[1-2\sin^{2}\beta_{1}]\hat{b}_{i}\right)\left(\sin^{2}\beta_{1}+[1-2\sin^{2}\beta_{1}]\hat{b}_{j}\right)\ket{w}

(38)

(39)

w

\beta_{1}

\displaystyle\sin^{2}\beta_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d}\frac{n-W(w)(d+2)}{n-4W(w)}.

(40)

Substituting this value back into equation (

) to get the optimal value of the cost function, we find

\displaystyle\bra{w}e^{i\beta_{1}B}Ce^{-i\beta_{1}B}\ket{w}

C.1
Magic angles for the Sherrington-Kirkpatrick model

We now discuss the exceptions. For the SK model on an even number of sites, with any set of couplings
\{J_{i,j}\}
, there are parameters at
p=3/2
which creates a cat state starting on any computational basis state. The “magic angle” unitary which creates the cat state is given by:

\displaystyle U_{\text{magic}}=e^{-i\frac{\pi}{4}B}e^{i\frac{\pi}{4}C_{\text{SK}}}e^{i\frac{\pi}{4}B}.

(43)

In this section we show that when
n
is even, this unitary takes any initial string to a cat state of two strings given (up to global phase) by

\displaystyle U_{\text{magic}}\ket{w}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\ket{w^{\prime}}+e^{i\theta}\ket{-w^{\prime}}\right),

(44)

where
\theta
is a known phase, and where

\displaystyle\ket{w^{\prime}}=\prod_{J_{i,j}=-1}(-iY_{i}Y_{j})\ket{w}.

(45)

In other words, evolving with the QAOA unitary at these parameters takes
\ket{w}
to a cat state involving
\ket{w^{\prime}}
and
\ket{-w^{\prime}}
, where
w^{\prime}
is obtained from
w
by flipping the
i
th bit if the product over
j
of
J_{i,j}
is
-1
. For a typical
\{J_{i,j}\}
this makes it seem that
C(w^{\prime})
is essentially random, not different in distribution from
C(w^{\prime\prime})
where
w^{\prime\prime}
is chosen at random. If
w
is a good string, it is unlikely that
w^{\prime}
will be better. On rare occasions, this transformation can improve the cost function. However, improvement becomes less likely for better initial strings. We see this in our numerics: when starting from strings with energy
17
, none of the 50 warm start samples improve under the magic angle unitary, whereas four do improve starting at the lower energy of
11
.

To show this result, let us first consider the case where all
J_{i,j}=1
. Using the fact that
e^{i\frac{\pi}{4}X}Ze^{-i\frac{\pi}{4}X}=Y
, we can simplify the unitary to

\displaystyle e^{-i\frac{\pi}{4}B}e^{i\frac{\pi}{4}\sum_{i,j}Z_{i}Z_{j}}e^{i\frac{\pi}{4}B}\ket{w}=e^{i\frac{\pi}{4}\sum_{i,j}Y_{i}Y_{j}}\ket{w}=e^{-i\frac{\pi}{8}n}e^{i\frac{\pi}{8}(\sum_{i}Y_{i})^{2}}\ket{w}.

(46)

We will evaluate (
46
) in the
y
-basis by writing the
Y_{i}
operators in terms of their eigenvalues
y_{i}
, which are
\pm 1
valued bits. In particular, we will show by induction that for
n
even, we have

\displaystyle\exp\bigg({i\frac{\pi}{8}\Big(\sum_{i}y_{i}\Big)^{2}}\bigg)=\frac{e^{i\frac{\pi}{4}}}{\sqrt{2}}\left(1-i^{n+1}y_{1}y_{2}\cdots y_{n}\right).

(47)

The
n=2
base case is immediate:

\displaystyle\exp\left({i\frac{\pi}{8}(y_{1}+y_{2})^{2}}\right)=\frac{e^{i\frac{\pi}{4}}}{\sqrt{2}}(1-i^{3}y_{1}y_{2}).

(48)

Now to go from the
n-2
case to
n
we have

\displaystyle\exp\bigg({i\frac{\pi}{8}\Big(\sum_{i}y_{i}\Big)^{2}}\bigg)

\displaystyle=\exp\left({i\frac{\pi}{8}(y_{1}+\dots+y_{n-2})^{2}+i\frac{\pi}{4}(y_{1}+\dots+y_{n-2})(y_{n-1}+y_{n})+i\frac{\pi}{8}(y_{n-1}+y_{n})^{2}}\right)

(49)

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{i\frac{\pi}{4}}}{\sqrt{2}}\left(1-i^{n-1}y_{1}y_{2}\cdots y_{n-2}\right)\exp\left({i\frac{\pi}{4}(y_{1}+\dots+y_{n-2})(y_{n-1}+y_{n})+i\frac{\pi}{8}(y_{n-1}+y_{n})^{2}}\right).

(50)

We now split the analysis into two cases: when
y_{n-1}=1
and
y_{n}=-1
, and when
y_{n-1}=1
and
y_{n}=1.
For the first case, we can simplify (
50
) to

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{i\frac{\pi}{4}}}{\sqrt{2}}\left(1-i^{n-1}y_{1}\dots y_{n-2}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{i\frac{\pi}{4}}}{\sqrt{2}}\left(1+i^{n-1}y_{1}\dots y_{n}\right)=\frac{e^{i\frac{\pi}{4}}}{\sqrt{2}}\left(1-i^{n+1}y_{1}\dots y_{n}\right),

(51)

which satisfies the inductive hypothesis.
For the second case, we have

\displaystyle\frac{e^{i\frac{\pi}{4}}}{\sqrt{2}}(1-i^{n-1}y_{1}\dots y_{n-2})e^{i\frac{\pi}{2}(y_{1}+\dots+y_{n-2})}e^{i\frac{\pi}{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{i\frac{\pi}{4}}}{\sqrt{2}}(1-i^{n-1}y_{1}\dots y_{n-2})i^{n-2}(y_{1}\dots y_{n-2})i

(52)

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{i\frac{\pi}{4}}}{\sqrt{2}}(1-i^{n+1}y_{1}\dots y_{n}),

(53)

where in the last line we have used the fact that
n
is even. This agrees with the inductive hypothesis as well, including all phases, so we have shown that equation (
47
) is true.

So the action of
U_{\text{magic}}
on
\ket{w}
is

\displaystyle U_{\text{magic}}\ket{w}=\frac{e^{-i\frac{\pi}{8}(n-2)}}{\sqrt{2}}\left(1-i^{n+1}Y_{1}Y_{2}\cdots Y_{n}\right)\ket{w}.

(54)

This operator creates a superposition of
\ket{w}
and its complement
\ket{-w}
when all
J_{i,j}=1
.

Now consider what happens if we change a particular
J_{i,j}
to
-1
. The new unitary
U_{\text{magic}}^{\prime}
is related to
U_{\text{magic}}
as

\displaystyle U_{\text{magic}}^{\prime}=e^{-i\frac{\pi}{2}Y_{i}Y_{j}}U_{\text{magic}}=-iY_{i}Y_{j}U_{\text{magic}},

(55)

so the net effect of flipping
J_{i,j}
is to flip the corresponding bits in the cat state. Flipping additional
J
’s repeats this effect, so we always end up with a state (up to global phase) of the form

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\big(\ket{w^{\prime}}+e^{i\theta}\ket{-w^{\prime}}\big),

(56)

where
w^{\prime}
is obtained from the original string
w
by flipping the sequence of bits associated with the negative
J
’s as described in equation (
45
).

Appendix D
Condition for MaxCut improvement at small angles for
p=3/2

Here we give conditions for the
p=3/2
QAOA for
\beta_{1},\beta_{2}\ll 1
to improve upon its initial string. Our final result is stated in equation
18
in the main text.

The expected cost produced by the
p=3/2
warm started QAOA is, for the MaxCut cost
C
,

\displaystyle\langle w|e^{i\beta_{1}B}e^{i\gamma C}e^{i\beta_{2}B}Ce^{-i\beta_{2}B}e^{-i\gamma C}e^{-i\beta_{1}B}|w\rangle.

(57)

We now expand to order
\beta^{2}
. The zeroth-order term is
C(w)
and the higher order terms are the change from this value. Terms of order
\beta
cancel, and the terms of order
\beta^{2}
are

\displaystyle-\beta_{1}\beta_{2}\langle w|Be^{i\gamma C}BCe^{-i\gamma C}|w\rangle+\mathrm{c.c.}+\beta_{1}\beta_{2}\langle w|Be^{i\gamma C}CBe^{-i\gamma C}|w\rangle+\mathrm{c.c.}

\displaystyle-\frac{\beta_{1}^{2}}{2}\langle w|B^{2}e^{i\gamma C}Ce^{-i\gamma C}|w\rangle+\mathrm{c.c.}-\frac{\beta_{2}^{2}}{2}\langle w|e^{i\gamma C}B^{2}Ce^{-i\gamma C}|w\rangle+\mathrm{c.c.}

\displaystyle+\beta_{1}^{2}\langle w|Be^{i\gamma C}Ce^{-i\gamma C}B|w\rangle+\beta_{2}^{2}\langle w|e^{i\gamma C}BCBe^{-i\gamma C}|w\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad=-2\beta_{1}\beta_{2}C(w)\sum_{i}\cos[\gamma(C_{i}-C(w))]+2\beta_{1}\beta_{2}\sum_{i}C_{i}\cos[\gamma(C_{i}-C(w))]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad-\beta_{1}^{2}nC(w)-\beta_{2}^{2}nC(w)+\beta_{1}^{2}\sum_{i}C_{i}+\beta_{2}^{2}\sum_{i}C_{i},

(58)

where
C_{i}=\bra{w}X_{i}CX_{i}\ket{w}
. We define the quantity

\displaystyle\delta_{i}=(C_{i}-C(w))/2.

(59)

Then we can replace each factor of
n
with a sum over
i
, and substitute the definition of
\delta_{i}
to obtain

\displaystyle 2(\beta_{1}^{2}+\beta_{2}^{2})\sum_{i}\delta_{i}+4\beta_{1}\beta_{2}\sum_{i}\delta_{i}\cos 2\gamma\delta_{i}=2(\beta_{1}+\beta_{2})^{2}\sum_{i}\delta_{i}-8\beta_{1}\beta_{2}\sum_{i}\delta_{i}\sin^{2}\gamma\delta_{i}.

(60)

Therefore we have improvement if we can find parameters for which

\displaystyle 2(\beta_{1}+\beta_{2})^{2}\sum_{i}\delta_{i}-8\beta_{1}\beta_{2}\sum_{i}\delta_{i}\sin^{2}\gamma\delta_{i}>0.

(61)

We restrict our attention to
3
-regular graphs, for which
|\delta_{i}|=1
or
3
.
We want conditions on
\{\delta_{i}\}
under which the inequality (
61
) can be met for small
\beta_{1},\beta_{2}
, and some
\gamma
. Let us split the analysis into two cases, depending on whether
\sum_{i}\delta_{i}\sin^{2}(\gamma\delta_{i})
is positive or negative.

If
\sum_{i}\delta_{i}\sin^{2}(\gamma\delta_{i})
is positive, then we can always meet (
61
), for example, by taking
\beta_{2}=-\beta_{1}
. So we seek conditions on
\{\delta_{i}\}
that guarantee that
\sum_{i}\delta_{i}\sin^{2}(\gamma\delta_{i})
is positive for some
\gamma
. Now,

\displaystyle\sum_{i}\delta_{i}\sin^{2}(\gamma\delta_{i})

\displaystyle=\sum_{|\delta_{i}|=1}\delta_{i}\sin^{2}\gamma+\sum_{|\delta_{i}|=3}\delta_{i}\sin^{2}(3\gamma)

(62)

\displaystyle=\sum_{|\delta_{i}|=1}\delta_{i}\sin^{2}\gamma+\sum_{|\delta_{i}|=3}\delta_{i}(3\sin\gamma-4\sin^{3}\gamma)^{2}

(63)

\displaystyle=\sin^{2}\gamma\bigg[\sum_{|\delta_{i}|=1}\delta_{i}+(3-4\sin^{2}\gamma)^{2}\sum_{|\delta_{i}|=3}\delta_{i}\bigg].

(64)

We have that
0\leq(3-4\sin^{2}\gamma)^{2}\leq 9
, so we see that the term in brackets ranges from the first sum to the first sum plus
9
times the second sum as
\gamma
varies. This means that (
64
) is positive for some
\gamma
if and only if

\displaystyle\qquad\sum_{|\delta_{i}|=1}\delta_{i}>0\qquad\text{or}\qquad\sum_{|\delta_{i}|=1}\delta_{i}+9\sum_{|\delta_{i}|=3}\delta_{i}>0.

(65)

The second condition is exactly equal to
\sum_{i}\delta_{i}^{3}>0
.

Therefore
\sum_{i}\delta_{i}\sin^{2}(\gamma\delta_{i})
can be positive if and only if

\displaystyle\sum_{|\delta_{i}|=1}\delta_{i}>0\qquad\text{or}\qquad\sum_{i}\delta_{i}^{3}>0.

(66)

Now let us examine the case where
\sum_{i}\delta_{i}\sin^{2}(\gamma\delta_{i})
is negative. In this case, we can succeed in satisfying (
61
) as long as

\displaystyle\sum_{i}\delta_{i}>\frac{4\beta_{1}\beta_{2}}{(\beta_{1}+\beta_{2})^{2}}\sum_{i}\delta_{i}\sin^{2}(\gamma\delta_{i})

(67)

for some
\beta_{1},\beta_{2}
. The right-hand side is minimized at
\beta_{1}=\beta_{2}
, so this condition can hold if and only if

\displaystyle\sum_{i}\delta_{i}>\sum_{i}\delta_{i}\sin^{2}(\gamma\delta_{i}),

(68)

or equivalently, if for some
\gamma
,

\displaystyle\sum_{i}\delta_{i}\cos^{2}(\gamma\delta_{i})>0.

(69)

Repeating now the analysis done for the
\sum_{i}\delta_{i}\sin^{2}(\gamma\delta_{i})>0
case, we obtain the same conditions for success.
In summary, at
p=3/2
improvement of the cost is possible for small
\beta
’s if and only if

\displaystyle\sum_{|\delta_{i}|=1}\delta_{i}>0\qquad\text{or}\qquad\sum_{i}\delta_{i}^{3}>0

(70)

which proves equation (
18
) in the main text.

Appendix E
Thermality argument proofs

E.1
Proof that typical strings cannot be improved

In this section we will show for any
\epsilon>0
, with
w
chosen uniformly at random,

\displaystyle\Pr_{w}\left(\frac{1}{m}\langle w|U_{w}^{\dagger}CU_{w}|w\rangle-\frac{1}{m}C(w)\geq\frac{1}{m^{1/2-\varepsilon}}\right)\rightarrow 0,\qquad\text{as }m\rightarrow\infty,

(71)

where
U_{w}
is the optimal QAOA unitary for each
w
.

To streamline the presentation, we restrict ourselves to the
p=3/2
QAOA on triangle-free
d
-regular graphs, although the argument can be generalized to any constant depth, and to general bounded-degree graphs. Let
G=(V,E)
be a triangle-free
d
-regular graph with
|V|=n
vertices and
|E|=m=nd/2
edges. We consider cost functions of the generic form

\displaystyle C(z)=\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle\in E}C_{\langle i,j\rangle}(z_{i},z_{j}).

(72)

We can use the observations of Section
2.3
to reorganize the QAOA expectation: at
p=3/2
, the QAOA involves only a small tree around each edge consisting of
2d
vertices.
That is, we can write the expected value of the cost operator (normalized by the number of edges) as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m}\langle w|U^{\dagger}_{w}CU_{w}|w\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle\in E}\langle w|U_{w}^{\dagger}C_{\langle i,j\rangle}U_{w}|w\rangle,

(73)

where each matrix element on the right-hand side only depends on the
2d
bits of
w
which lie on the tree whose central edge is
\langle i,j\rangle
. We call the restriction of
w
to these bits
w_{\langle i,j\rangle}
. So we can write

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m}\langle w|U^{\dagger}_{w}CU_{w}|w\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle\in E}\langle w_{\langle i,j\rangle}|U_{w}^{\dagger}C_{\langle i,j\rangle}U_{w}|w_{\langle i,j\rangle}\rangle,

(74)

where each
w_{\langle i,j\rangle}
is a bit string on
2d
bits. Summing over the possible values of such bit strings, we can rearrange the sum as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m}\langle w|U^{\dagger}_{w}CU_{w}|w\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{\ell\in\{-1,1\}^{2d}}\#_{w}(\ell)\langle\ell|U_{w}^{\dagger}C_{\mathrm{edge}}U_{w}|\ell\rangle,

(75)

where
\#_{w}(\ell)
is the number of times the
2d
-bit string
\ell
appears within the collection of
w_{\langle i,j\rangle}
, and where
C_{\mathrm{edge}}
denotes a generic edge term (for MaxCut,
C_{\mathrm{edge}}=-Z_{a}Z_{b}
, where
\langle a,b\rangle
labels the central edge of a tree neighborhood). We consider
U^{\dagger}_{w}C_{\mathrm{edge}}U_{w}
as an operator on the
2d
-qubit Hilbert space associated with the
2d
vertices in the radius-1 tree neighborhood of an edge.

Now we consider the case where the string
w\in\{-1,1\}^{n}
is chosen uniformly at random. We can think of
\#_{w}(\ell)
as a random variable whose average value is
m/2^{2d}
. We conveniently partition the sum as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m}\langle w|U^{\dagger}_{w}CU_{w}|w\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{2d}}\sum_{\ell\in\{-1,1\}^{2d}}\langle\ell|U_{w}^{\dagger}C_{\mathrm{edge}}U_{w}|\ell\rangle+\frac{1}{m}\sum_{\ell\in\{-1,1\}^{2d}}\left(\#_{w}(\ell)-\frac{m}{2^{2d}}\right)\langle\ell|U_{w}^{\dagger}C_{\mathrm{edge}}U_{w}|\ell\rangle.

(76)

The first term of the right-hand side of equation (
76
) turns out to be a simple constant,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2^{2d}}\sum_{\ell\in\{-1,1\}^{2d}}\langle\ell|U_{w}^{\dagger}C_{\mathrm{edge}}U_{w}|\ell\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{2d}}\sum_{\ell\in\{-1,1\}^{2d}}\mathrm{Tr}_{\textrm{tree}}\left(U_{w}^{\dagger}C_{\mathrm{edge}}U_{w}|\ell\rangle\langle\ell|\right)

(77)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{2d}}\mathrm{Tr}_{\textrm{tree}}\left(U_{w}^{\dagger}C_{\mathrm{edge}}U_{w}I\right)

(78)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{2d}}\mathrm{Tr}_{\textrm{tree}}(C_{\mathrm{edge}})

(79)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{z\in\{-1,1\}^{n}}\frac{1}{m}\langle z|C|z\rangle

(80)

\displaystyle=\bar{c},

(81)

where
\overline{c}
is the average cost value per edge as defined in equation (
21
), and where
\mathrm{Tr}_{\textrm{tree}}
refers to the trace over the Hilbert space of an abstract tree neighborhood.

The second term in (
76
) is then the deviation of the optimized cost function
\frac{1}{m}\langle w|U^{\dagger}_{w}CU_{w}|w\rangle
from the average cost function
\bar{c}
. We want to upper bound this deviation, which we denote by
\chi
, defined as

\displaystyle\chi

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m}\langle w|U^{\dagger}_{w}CU_{w}|w\rangle-\bar{c}

(82)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{\ell\in\{-1,1\}^{2d}}\left(\#_{w}(\ell)-\frac{m}{2^{2d}}\right)\langle\ell|U^{\dagger}_{w}C_{\mathrm{edge}}U_{w}|\ell\rangle.

(83)

We can bound
\chi^{2}
using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality as

\displaystyle\chi^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{m^{2}}\bigg[\sum_{\ell\in\{-1,1\}^{2d}}\left(\#_{w}(\ell)-\frac{m}{2^{2d}}\right)^{2}\bigg]\bigg[\sum_{\ell\in\{-1,1\}^{2d}}\left|\langle\ell|U_{w}^{\dagger}C_{\mathrm{edge}}U_{w}|\ell\rangle\right|^{2}\bigg]

(84)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{m^{2}}2^{2d}\sum_{\ell\in\{-1,1\}^{2d}}\left(\#_{w}(\ell)-\frac{m}{2^{2d}}\right)^{2},

(85)

where we use the fact that
|C_{\mathrm{edge}}|\leq 1
in the second inequality. For each
\ell\in\{-1,1\}^{2d}
, we can imagine
\#_{w}(\ell)
as a sum of indicator random variables
B_{\langle i,j\rangle}
. Each
B_{\langle i,j\rangle}
is
1
if
w_{\langle i,j\rangle}=\ell
, where we recall that
w_{\langle i,j\rangle}
is the restriction of
w
to the edge neighborhood centered at edge
\langle i,j\rangle
, and
0
otherwise:

\displaystyle\#_{w}(\ell)=\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle\in E}B_{\langle i,j\rangle}.

(86)

The mean of each
B_{\langle i,j\rangle}
is
2^{-2d}
because the string
w
is chosen uniformly at random. Note
B_{\langle i,j\rangle}
and
B_{\langle i^{\prime},j^{\prime}\rangle}
are independent when the tree neighborhoods of the corresponding edges do not overlap. For each
B_{\langle i,j\rangle}
, there are at most
D=2d^{3}
distinct edge neighborhoods with which it has non-trivial overlap. Averaging over the
w
we get

\displaystyle\Ex_{w}\left[\left(\#_{w}(\ell)-\frac{m}{2^{2d}}\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle\in E}\sum_{\langle i^{\prime},j^{\prime}\rangle\in E}\Ex_{w}\left[\left(B_{\langle i,j\rangle}-\frac{1}{2^{2d}}\right)\left(B_{\langle i^{\prime},j^{\prime}\rangle}-\frac{1}{2^{2d}}\right)\right]

(87)

\displaystyle\leq mD\frac{1}{2^{2d}}\left(1-\frac{1}{2^{2d}}\right),

(88)

because there are at most
mD
pairs of overlapping neighborhoods in the double sum (the other terms vanish), and for these nonzero terms, by Cauchy-Schwarz, the covariance
\Ex_{w}\left[\cdots\right]
is upper bounded by the variance
\mu(1-\mu)
of the indicator random variables
B_{\langle i,j\rangle}
with mean
\mu=2^{-2d}
.

Altogether,
\Ex_{w}[\chi^{2}]
is bounded above as

\displaystyle\Ex_{w}\left[\chi^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{m^{2}}2^{2d}mD\frac{1}{2^{2d}}\left(1-\frac{1}{2^{2d}}\right)\leq\frac{D}{m}.

(89)

By the Chebyshev inequality, for any
a>0
, we have

\displaystyle\Pr_{w}\left(\left|\chi\right|\geq a\right)\leq\frac{\Ex_{w}[\chi^{2}]}{a^{2}}\leq\frac{D}{ma^{2}}.

(90)

Choosing
a=m^{-1/2+\varepsilon}
for any
\varepsilon>0
, and plugging in the definition of
\chi
in equation (
82
), we get

\displaystyle\Pr_{w}\left(\left|\frac{1}{m}\langle w|U^{\dagger}_{w}CU_{w}|w\rangle-\bar{c}\right|\geq\frac{1}{m^{1/2-\varepsilon}}\right)\longrightarrow 0,\quad\text{as }m\rightarrow\infty..

(91)

It follows that as the number of edges
m
tends to infinity, the probability of any non-negligible deviation from
\bar{c}
tends to zero. Equation (
91
), combined with the law of large numbers (equation (
22
), main text), finally yields the bound

\displaystyle\Pr_{w}\left(\frac{1}{m}\langle w|U_{w}^{\dagger}CU_{w}|w\rangle-\frac{1}{m}C(w)\geq\frac{1}{m^{1/2-\varepsilon}}\right)\rightarrow 0,\qquad\text{as }m\rightarrow\infty.

(92)

We conclude that for nearly all initial strings, the constant-depth QAOA can only improve the cost function (per edge) by an amount that vanishes in the limit of large graphs.

E.2
Proof that good strings cannot be improved

In this section we develop a statistical argument to upper bound the progress made by the QAOA starting from a good string. The bound applies to the QAOA at constant depth on large bounded-degree graphs. In the previous section, we proved the QAOA makes little progress when starting from a string chosen uniformly at random. Because such strings are not generally “good” strings, we extend the argument to good strings we describe as “locally thermal.”

E.2.1
Intuition for the thermal argument

We begin with an intuitive explanation of our argument, then state the main Theorem
E.1
and its derivation.
Our argument relies on the locality and the uniformity of the operators appearing in the QAOA circuit. Recall that we consider cost functions that are a sum of terms for each edge of a graph, as in equation (
2
). Let
U
be the QAOA operator from equation (
5
) at depth
p
. The QAOA operator for a given edge is
U^{\dagger}C_{\langle i,j\rangle}U
, which acts non-trivially only on the edge neighborhood of
\langle i,j\rangle
with maximal radius
r=p-1/2
(see Section
2.3
). By uniformity, we mean that the reduced operator on any subgraph depends only on the isomorphism type of the underlying subgraph (i.e., the reduced operators on different subgraphs are the same when the subgraphs are isomorphic). Note that the operators of the standard QAOA satisfy this uniformity property as long as the
C_{\langle i,j\rangle}
do not depend on the edge
\langle i,j\rangle
. Our statistical argument also relies on bounded degree, so it does not apply directly to problems like the Sherrington-Kirpatrick model in Appendix
C
.

For MaxCut on
d
-regular graphs as
n\to\infty
and at constant depth, all but a vanishing fraction of edge neighborhoods are trees. Following the main text, we define the fraction
\delta
of edges whose neighborhoods are
not
trees as

\displaystyle\delta=1-\frac{|E_{T}|}{m},

(93)

where
E_{T}
denotes the set of edges whose neighborhood of radius
r
is a tree.

Recall the ensemble
\rho_{w,\mathrm{tree}}
associated with the warm-start string
w
. For any classical string
w\in\{-1,1\}^{n}
and a mostly locally tree-like
G
we define its
local ensemble
by

\displaystyle\rho_{w,\mathrm{tree}}=\frac{1}{|E_{T}|}\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle\in E_{T}}|w_{\langle i,j\rangle}\rangle\langle w_{\langle i,j\rangle}|,

(94)

where
w_{\langle i,j\rangle}
denotes the restriction of
w
onto the local tree neighborhood
T_{\langle i,j\rangle}
, and
T_{\langle i,j\rangle}
denotes the tree neighborhood centered around edge
\langle i,j\rangle\in E_{T}
. Each tree neighborhood has a fixed number of vertices, so
w_{\langle i,j\rangle}
is a string of that length, and
\rho_{w,\mathrm{tree}}
is a density matrix over the corresponding number of qubits.

We now use the global thermal density matrix
\rho_{\beta}
with respect to
H=-C
, (see equation
26
in the main text) to define the reduced density matrix onto the tree neighborhood
T_{\langle i,j\rangle}
, denoted
\mathrm{Tr}_{G\backslash T_{\langle i,j\rangle}}\left(\rho_{\beta}\right)
, and the ensemble

\displaystyle\rho_{\beta,\mathrm{tree}}=\frac{1}{|E_{T}|}\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle\in E_{T}}\mathrm{Tr}_{G\backslash T_{\langle i,j\rangle}}\left(\rho_{\beta}\right).

(95)

Again, both
\rho_{w,\mathrm{tree}}
and
\rho_{\beta,\mathrm{tree}}
act on the Hilbert space of a single tree neighborhood.

Consider two strings
w
and
w^{\prime}
drawn from the thermal ensemble
\rho_{\beta}
. While the strings may look totally different on any particular neighborhood, you might expect that
\rho_{w,\mathrm{tree}}
and
\rho_{w^{\prime},\textrm{tree}}
are nonetheless similar: both strings look the same locally when averaged over neighborhoods.
In that case, typical strings from the thermal ensemble should all have the same expected cost function under
U
, since the expected cost is completely determined by
\rho_{w,\mathrm{tree}}
up to small corrections in
\delta
. In particular, the cost function should behave as if the thermal state
\rho_{\beta}
were itself the input to the QAOA. But a key property of the thermal state is that it has the minimum expected energy of all states with the same entropy. That is, fixing some
\beta>0
, we have

\displaystyle\rho_{\beta}=\argmin_{\rho\,:\,S(\rho)=S(\rho_{\beta})}\mathrm{Tr}(H\rho).

(96)

In particular, since unitaries preserve entropy, we have

\displaystyle\min_{U}\mathrm{Tr}(HU\rho_{\beta}U^{\dagger})=\mathrm{Tr}(H\rho_{\beta}).

(97)

This means that no unitary applied to
\rho_{\beta}
can lower the energy.

Suppose
\rho_{w,\mathrm{tree}}
is well-approximated by the density matrix
\rho_{\beta,\mathrm{tree}}
.
Then
w
gives the same expectation for the cost-function as the associated
\rho_{\beta}
under any QAOA unitary, so no such unitary applied to
w
can reduce the energy. We call such strings
w
“locally thermal,” and we quantify this property with the “thermality coefficient”
\varepsilon_{w}
,

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{w}=\|\rho_{\beta,\mathrm{tree}}-\rho_{w,\mathrm{tree}}\|_{1},

(98)

where
\beta
is the inverse temperature chosen so that

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}(C\rho_{\beta})=C(w).

(99)

Note that
\varepsilon_{w}
depends on the QAOA depth
p
which defines the neighborhood radius. Our main result, stated next (Theorem
6.2
in the main text), shows that if
w
is locally thermal, i.e., if
\varepsilon_{w}
is small, then
w
can only improve by a small amount.
Locally thermal strings may be viewed as exhibiting a form of ergodicity: the average over local neighborhoods for the fixed string looks like the thermal average over different global strings.

E.2.2
Proof of the thermal argument

We now prove our main result, which bounds the improvement of the single-string warm-start QAOA for locally thermal strings.

Theorem E.1
.

Consider the single-string warm-start QAOA initialized in a string
w
with thermality coefficient
\varepsilon_{w}
, and let
\delta
be the fraction of neighborhoods whose edges are not trees. Then

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m}\langle w|U^{\dagger}_{w}CU_{w}|w\rangle\leq c(w)+2\varepsilon_{w}+4\delta,

(100)

where
c(w)=C(w)/m
is the cut fraction of the original string
w
.

Recalling equation (
73
) and the observations of Section
2.3
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m}\langle w|U^{\dagger}_{w}CU_{w}|w\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle\in E}\langle w|U_{w}^{\dagger}C_{\langle i,j\rangle}U_{w}|w\rangle

(101)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{m}\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle\in E_{T}}\langle w|U_{w}^{\dagger}C_{\langle i,j\rangle}U_{w}|w\rangle+\delta

(102)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle\in E_{T}}\langle w_{\langle i,j\rangle}|U_{w}^{\dagger}C_{\langle i,j\rangle}U_{w}|w_{\langle i,j\rangle}\rangle+\delta

(103)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m}\mathrm{Tr}_{\textrm{tree}}\big(U_{w}^{\dagger}C_{\mathrm{edge}}U_{w}\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle\in E_{T}}|w_{\langle i,j\rangle}\rangle\langle w_{\langle i,j\rangle}|\big)+\delta

(104)

\displaystyle=\frac{|E_{T}|}{m}\mathrm{Tr}_{\textrm{tree}}\big(U_{w}^{\dagger}C_{\mathrm{edge}}U_{w}\rho_{w,\mathrm{tree}}\big)+\delta.

(105)

The second line drops the contribution from edges whose neighborhoods are not trees. The
\mathrm{Tr}_{\textrm{tree}}
refers to the trace over the Hilbert space associated to an abstract tree neighborhood. The final line uses the definition of
\rho_{w,\mathrm{tree}}
in (
94
).

Next, we replace
\rho_{w,\mathrm{tree}}
with
\rho_{\beta,\mathrm{tree}}
, picking up an error proportional to
\varepsilon_{w}
, which measures the local thermality of
w
. We use the property of the trace norm that

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}(A^{\dagger}B)\leq\|A\|_{\infty}\cdot\|B\|_{1},

(106)

where
\left\lVert A\right\rVert_{\infty}
is the operator norm (the largest singular value). Recall that we assume
C
is normalized so that
|C_{\langle i,j\rangle}|\leq 1
, or equivalently,
||C_{\textrm{edge}}||_{\infty}\leq 1
. Then we find

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m}\langle w|U^{\dagger}_{w}CU_{w}|w\rangle\leq\frac{|E_{T}|}{m}\mathrm{Tr}_{\textrm{tree}}\left(U_{w}^{\dagger}C_{\mathrm{edge}}U_{w}\rho_{\beta,\mathrm{tree}}\right)+\varepsilon_{w}+\delta.

(107)

Now we substitute (
95
), replace the partial trace
\mathrm{Tr}_{G\backslash T_{\langle i,j\rangle}}(\rho_{\beta})
with the full thermal operator
\rho_{\beta}
, and re-sum the cost Hamiltonian
C
:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m}\langle w|U^{\dagger}_{w}CU_{w}|w\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{m}\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle\in E_{T}}\mathrm{Tr}_{\textrm{tree}}\left(U_{w}^{\dagger}C_{\langle i,j\rangle}U_{w}\mathrm{Tr}_{G\backslash T_{\langle i,j\rangle}}(\rho_{\beta})\right)+\varepsilon_{w}+\delta

(108)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{m}\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle\in E}\mathrm{Tr}\left(U_{w}^{\dagger}C_{\langle i,j\rangle}U_{w}\rho_{\beta}\right)+\varepsilon_{w}+2\delta

(109)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m}\mathrm{Tr}\left(U_{w}^{\dagger}CU_{w}\rho_{\beta}\right)+\varepsilon_{w}+2\delta.

(110)

Finally, we apply the principle of minimum energy in thermodynamics (equation (
97
)) to the Hamiltonian
H=-C
with the thermal state
\rho_{\beta}
of (
26
), we get

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m}\langle w|U^{\dagger}_{w}CU_{w}|w\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{m}\mathrm{Tr}\left(U_{w}^{\dagger}CU_{w}\rho_{\beta}\right)+\varepsilon_{w}+2\delta

(111)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{m}\mathrm{Tr}(C\rho_{\beta})+\varepsilon_{w}+2\delta

(112)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{|E_{T}|}{m}\mathrm{Tr}_{\textrm{tree}}(C_{\textrm{edge}}\rho_{\beta,\mathrm{tree}})+\varepsilon_{w}+3\delta

(113)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{|E_{T}|}{m}\mathrm{Tr}_{\textrm{tree}}(C_{\textrm{edge}}\rho_{w,\mathrm{tree}})+2\varepsilon_{w}+3\delta

(114)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{m}\langle w|C|w\rangle+2\varepsilon_{w}+4\delta

(115)

or equivalently,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m}\langle w|U^{\dagger}_{w}CU_{w}|w\rangle\leq c(w)+2\varepsilon_{w}+4\delta

(116)

where
c(w)
is the cut fraction of the original string
w
, reproducing the desired result of Theorem (
E.1
). Since
\delta=O(1/n)
is vanishingly small for large graphs, we conclude that the improvement is controlled by
\varepsilon_{w}
, which measures how close
w
is to being locally thermal.

E.3
When are initial strings locally thermal?

Here we ask about the values of
\varepsilon_{w}
for typical good strings
w
drawn from the thermal ensemble: how close is
\rho_{w,\mathrm{tree}}
to
\rho_{\beta,\mathrm{tree}}
? While
\rho_{w,\mathrm{tree}}
is not immediately guaranteed to be close to
\rho_{\beta,\mathrm{tree}}
for all
w
, the relation holds on average over thermally sampled strings
w
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{w\sim\rho_{\beta}}[\rho_{w,\mathrm{tree}}]=\rho_{\beta,\mathrm{tree}},

(117)

where
\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{w\sim\rho_{\beta}}
denotes expectation with respect to samples of
w
from the thermal distribution. This follows by commuting the thermal average with the average over neighborhoods:

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{w\sim\rho_{\beta}}[\rho_{w,\mathrm{tree}}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Z}\sum_{w}e^{-\beta(-C(w))}\rho_{w,\mathrm{tree}}

(118)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Z}\sum_{w}e^{-\beta(-C(w))}\frac{1}{|E_{T}|}\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle\in E_{T}}|w_{\langle i,j\rangle}\rangle\langle w_{\langle i,j\rangle}|

(119)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|E_{T}|}\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle\in E_{T}}\frac{1}{Z}\sum_{w}e^{-\beta(-C(w))}|w_{\langle i,j\rangle}\rangle\langle w_{\langle i,j\rangle}|

(120)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|E_{T}|}\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle\in E_{T}}\mathrm{Tr}_{G\backslash T_{\langle i,j\rangle}}\left(\rho_{\beta}\right)

(121)

\displaystyle=\rho_{\beta,\mathrm{tree}},

(122)

where
Z=\mathrm{Tr}\big(e^{-\beta(-C)}\big)
. In going to the second and fourth lines above, we use the definitions of
\rho_{w,\mathrm{tree}}
and
\rho_{\beta,\mathrm{tree}}
in (
94
) and (
95
), respectively.

Since the average of
\rho_{w,\mathrm{tree}}
over
w
matches
\rho_{\beta,\mathrm{tree}}
, to show that
\rho_{w,\mathrm{tree}}
is close to
\rho_{\beta,\mathrm{tree}}
for typical
w
, it suffices to show that
\rho_{w,\mathrm{tree}}
has small variance with respect to choice of
w
. Treating
w
as a random variable distributed according to the thermal ensemble,
\rho_{w,\mathrm{tree}}
defined in (
94
) becomes an average over random variables
|w_{\langle i,j\rangle}\rangle\langle w_{\langle i,j\rangle}|
defined on each neighborhood. If these variables have small correlation across different neighborhoods, then
\rho_{w,\mathrm{tree}}
has small variance. So
\varepsilon_{w}
is small for typical strings sampled from the thermal distribution, assuming the thermal distribution has only small spatial correlations across the graph. At fixed temperature, we therefore expect the variance of
\rho_{w,\mathrm{tree}}
shrinks with graph size as
m^{-1}
, and
\varepsilon_{w}\propto m^{-1/2}
.

When is it reasonable to assume that
\rho_{\beta}
has small spatial correlations across the graph? At infinite temperature, the case discussed in Section
6.1
, the correlation is zero, so there
\varepsilon_{w}\propto m^{-1/2}
, consistent with our conclusion there. In the high temperature regime (where the strings are “good,” i.e. better than random guesses, but not too good), we similarly expect
\rho_{\beta}
to have small correlations.
At low temperatures, depending on the specific combinatorial problem, there may be thermal phase transitions and development of long-range correlations. However, since we are interested in warm starts which can be efficiently generated by classical algorithms, this low temperature regime may be less relevant in practice.

E.4
Numerical evidence for the thermality of typical good strings

In Section
E.2
, we proved that when using a warm start with a classical string
w
, the cost function cannot improve much when
w
is “locally thermal.” More precisely, we showed the improvement of the cost function per edge is controlled by thermality coefficient

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{w}=\|\rho_{\beta,\mathrm{tree}}-\rho_{w,\mathrm{tree}}\|_{1}.

(123)

When
w
is locally thermal, meaning
\varepsilon_{w}
is small, the average of
w
over local neighborhoods looks like the thermal average over different global strings. In this section, we present numerical evidence that, for a given cost function, typical good strings
w
are indeed approximately locally thermal. Moreover, it appears that the magnitude of the thermality coefficient diminishes with larger graph sizes. In our investigations below, we only perform numerical experiments for MaxCut, and we only analyze typical good strings associated with one fixed temperature. We leave the analysis of other problems besides MaxCut to future investigations.

We study MaxCut on random 3-regular graphs of
n=10000
to
50000
vertices. The local thermality of typical strings is expected to hold with high accuracy only in the limit of large graphs because it involves the concentration of averages over neighborhoods. Fixing a temperature
T=1.75
, corresponding to a cut fraction of
\sim 0.64
, we use simulated annealing to sample classical strings
w
from the thermal distribution
\rho_{\beta}
of (
26
). That is, we sample strings
w
with probabilities proportional to
e^{-\beta(-C(w))}
.

We consider
k=10
samples,
\{w_{\alpha}\}
for
\alpha=1,\ldots,k
. For each string
w_{\alpha}
, it is straightforward to compute the associated density matrix
\rho_{w_{\alpha},\textrm{tree}}
of
(
94
)
. We consider tree neighborhoods of radius
r=2
, i.e., all vertices within distance
2
of a given edge
\langle i,j\rangle
. For
d=3
, these have 14 vertices, so
\rho_{w_{\alpha},\textrm{tree}}
lives on a Hilbert space of 14 qubits.

To verify that the sampled
w_{\alpha}
are locally thermal, ideally we would calculate
\left\lVert\rho_{w_{\alpha},\textrm{tree}}-\rho_{\beta,\mathrm{tree}}\right\rVert_{1}=\varepsilon_{w}
. Unfortunately, for these large graphs with
\geq 10000
vertices, direct computation of
\rho_{\beta}
and
\rho_{\beta,\mathrm{tree}}
is impossible in practice. To proceed, we restate (
117
), which says that

\displaystyle\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{w\sim\rho_{\beta}}[\rho_{w,\mathrm{tree}}]=\rho_{\beta,\mathrm{tree}},

(124)

where the expectation is with respect to samples of
w
from the thermal distribution. That is, the average of
\rho_{w,\mathrm{tree}}
over thermal samples matches
\rho_{\beta,\mathrm{tree}}
. In fact we find that for our numerically generated samples

, all of the associated
\rho_{w_{\alpha},\textrm{tree}}
are approximately equal. Thus even with our few
k=10

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{k}\left\lVert\rho_{w_{\alpha},\textrm{tree}}-\overline{\rho_{w,\textrm{tree}}}\right\rVert_{1},

(125)

where

\displaystyle\overline{\rho_{w,\textrm{tree}}}=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{k}\rho_{w_{\alpha},\textrm{tree}}.

(126)

The value of
\mathcal{E}
measures the average deviation of
\rho_{w_{\alpha},\textrm{tree}}
over samples. When
\mathcal{E}
is small, it follows that typical samples

are approximately locally thermal. We plot the average deviation

as a function of the number of vertices
n
in Figure

, with
10
samples for each

\mathcal{E}\propto n^{-1/2}
. The figure suggests that typical strings
w
at this temperature

are approximately locally thermal with
\varepsilon_{w}
shrinking as
n^{-1/2}

E.3
.

We aim to prove Theorem

F.1
Preliminary lemmas

First we review some general ideas about
\epsilon
-nets and
\epsilon
-packings on metric spaces.

Given a set
S
on a metric space, a subset
S_{c}\subset S
is called an
\epsilon
-covering
when every point in
S
is within distance
\epsilon
of some point in
S_{c}
. That is, the
\epsilon
-balls around each point in
S_{c}
together cover
S
.

A subset
S_{p}\subset S
is called an
\epsilon
-packing
when all points in
S_{p}
are distance at least
\epsilon
apart. That is, the
\epsilon/2
-balls around each point in
S_{p}
are disjoint.

A subset which is both an
\epsilon
-net and an
\epsilon
-packing is called an
\epsilon
-net
. We first collect a few useful facts about
\varepsilon
-nets.

Lemma F.1
.

Every finite set has at least one
\epsilon
-net, for any
\epsilon>0
.

Proof.

To construct the
\epsilon
-net, choose any point in the set and put it in the net. Throw away all points within an
\epsilon
-ball of the chosen point. If there are no remaining points then we are done. Otherwise, pick a remaining point, put it into the net, and throw away all the points within an
\epsilon
-ball of the point. Continue this process until the set is exhausted. Since the set is finite, this process must terminate.
∎

Lemma F.2
.

A
2\epsilon
-packing cannot have larger cardinality than an
\epsilon
-covering.

Proof.

We show, if
S
has an
\epsilon
-covering
S_{c}\subset S
and a
2\epsilon
-packing
S_{p}\subset S
, then
|S_{p}|\leq|S_{c}|
. To see this, note that all the points in
S_{p}
must be within distance
\epsilon
from some point in
S_{c}
. And by triangle inequality, no two distinct points in
S_{p}
can be within distance
\epsilon
of the same point in
S_{c}
(otherwise they would be within
2\epsilon
of each other). So there’s a surjection from
S_{p}
to
S_{c}
, i.e. every point in the packing must be covered by a different point in
S_{c}
.
∎

We can show that there is an
\epsilon
-covering of the space of depth-
p
QAOA unitaries with the following parameters.

Lemma F.3
.

For any
\delta\in(0,1)
, the set of QAOA unitaries produced by using
2p
parameters has an
\epsilon
-covering of size
\left(\frac{2\pi}{\delta}\right)^{2p}
for
\epsilon=p\delta n^{2}
, using the operator norm distance.

Proof.

Consider the set
S
of QAOA unitaries produced by using
2p
parameters, each in
[0,2\pi]
. Let
S_{0}\subset S
be the set of QAOA unitaries obtained by using discretized parameters

\displaystyle|S_{0}|=\left(\frac{2\pi}{\delta}\right)^{2p}.

(127)

We want to show that any QAOA unitary is close (in operator norm) to one of these unitaries with discretized parameters. That is, every point in
S

S_{0}

Consider the rotation
e^{i\gamma C}

\displaystyle\left\lVert e^{i\gamma_{1}C}-e^{i\gamma_{2}C}\right\rVert\leq|\gamma_{1}-\gamma_{2}|\left\lVert C\right\rVert,

\displaystyle\left\lVert e^{i\beta_{1}B}-e^{i\beta_{2}B}\right\rVert\leq|\beta_{1}-\beta_{2}|\left\lVert B\right\rVert\leq|\beta_{1}-\beta_{2}|n,

\displaystyle\left\lVert e^{i\gamma_{1}C}-e^{i\gamma_{2}C}\right\rVert

(131)

\displaystyle\left\lVert U_{1}\ldots U_{m}-V_{1}\ldots V_{m}\right\rVert\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left\lVert U_{i}-V_{i}\right\rVert.

\displaystyle\left\lVert U_{1}U_{2}-V_{1}V_{2}\right\rVert

\displaystyle=\left\lVert U_{1}(U_{2}-V_{2})+U_{1}V_{2}-V_{1}V_{2}\right\rVert

(133)

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert U_{1}(U_{2}-V_{2})\right\rVert+\left\lVert U_{1}V_{2}-V_{1}V_{2}\right\rVert

(134)

\displaystyle=\left\lVert(U_{2}-V_{2})\right\rVert+\left\lVert(U_{1}-V_{1})V_{2}+V_{1}V_{2}-V_{1}V_{2}\right\rVert

(135)

\displaystyle=\left\lVert(U_{2}-V_{2})\right\rVert+\left\lVert(U_{1}-V_{1})\right\rVert.

(136)

Now consider two QAOA unitaries

\displaystyle\left\lVert U-V\right\rVert\leq p\delta n^{2}.

\epsilon

Lemma F.4

Take any
\epsilon>2\gamma>0.
