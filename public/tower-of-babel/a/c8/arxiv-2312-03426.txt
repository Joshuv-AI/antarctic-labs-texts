Internal and External Calculi: Ordering the Jungle without Being Lost in Translations
Tim S. Lyon ORCID:0000-0003-3214-0828, Agata Ciabattoni, Didier Galmiche, Dominique Larchey-Wendling, Daniel Méry, Nicola Olivetti, Revantha Ramanayake

Abstract
. This paper gives a broad account of the various sequent-based proof formalisms in the proof-theoretic literature. We consider formalisms for various modal and tense logics, intuitionistic logic, conditional logics, and bunched logics. After providing an overview of the logics and proof formalisms under consideration, we show how these sequent-based formalisms can be placed in a hierarchy in terms of the underlying data structure of the sequents. We then discuss how this hierarchy can be traversed using translations. Translating proofs up this hierarchy is found to be relatively easy while translating proofs down the hierarchy is substantially more difficult. Finally, we inspect the prevalent distinction in structural proof theory between ‘internal calculi’ and ‘external calculi’. It is observed that these classes resist a rigorous separation, and we critically assess the properties that (calculi from) these classes are purported to possess.

Introduction

The widespread application of logical methods in computer science, epistemology, and artificial intelligence has resulted in an explosion of new logics. These logics are more expressive than classical logic, allowing finer distinctions and a direct representation of notions that cannot be well stated in classical logic. For instance, they are used to express different modes of truth (e.g. modal logics
[
BdRV01
]
) and to study different types of reasoning, e.g. hypothetical/plausible reasoning (e.g. conditional logics
[
Lew73
]
) or reasoning about the separation and sharing of resources (e.g. bunched implication logics
[
Pym02
]
). Apart from formalizing reasoning, these logics are also used to model systems and to prove properties about them, leading, for example, to applications in software verification
[
McM18
]
.

These applications require the existence of analytic calculi. Analytic calculi consist solely of rules that compose (decompose, in the case of tableau calculi) the formulae to be proved in a stepwise manner, and in particular, the key rule of cut—used to simulate
modus ponens
—is not needed. As a result, the proofs from an analytic calculus possess the subformula property: every formula that appears (anywhere) in the proof is a subformula of the formulae proved. This is a powerful restriction on the form of proofs, which can be exploited to develop automated reasoning methods
[
Sla97
]
as well as prove important (meta-)logical properties of the formalized logics such as consistency
[
Gen35a
,
Gen35b
]
, decidability
[
Dyc92
]
, and interpolation
[
Mae60
]
.

Since its introduction by Gentzen in the 1930’s and his seminal proof of the
Hauptsatz
(see
[
Gen35a
,
Gen35b
]
), the sequent calculus has become the preferred formalism for constructing analytic calculi.
This is because the sequent calculus is relatively simple and it does not require much technical machinery (i.e. ‘bureaucracy’) to enable analyticity. The downside of this simplicity is that the sequent calculus is not expressive enough to construct analytic calculi for many logics of interest, thus necessitating the discovery of new formalisms. In response,
many proof-theoretic formalisms extending the sequent calculus have been proposed over the last 50 years to define analytic calculi for families of logics; these include hypersequent calculi
[
Avr96
,
Min68
]
, nested sequent calculi
[
Bul92
,
Kas94
]
, bunched sequent calculi
[
Pym02
]
, display calculi
[
Bel82
,
Wan94
]
, and labeled sequent calculi
[
Sim94
,
Vig00
]
. Each of these proof-theoretic formalisms (or,
formalisms
for short) is characterized in terms of the standard notation it uses, the data structures employed in sequents, the types of inference rules that normally appear, and the types of properties ordinarily shared by the proof calculi thereof (which serve as
instances
of a formalism). As the notion is central to this paper, we further remark that a proof-theoretic formalism is a
paradigm
in which calculi are built or defined, i.e. a formalism constitutes the
way
in which calculi are constructed, giving rise to a family resemblance shared by systems within the same formalism.

In the literature, proof-theoretic formalisms and calculi have often been classified into
internal
or
external
. There is no formal definition of these properties and the proof-theoretic community lacks consensus on how each term should be precisely defined. Nevertheless, the literature abounds with informal definitions of internal and external calculi. Typically, internal calculi are described along multiple (sometimes intersecting, sometimes conflicting) lines; e.g. internal calculi have been qualified as proof systems omitting semantic elements from their syntax, or proof systems where every sequent is translatable into an equivalent logical formula. Often times, external calculi are defined as the opposite of internal calculi, and therefore, have also been qualified in multiple ways. It has been claimed that distinguishing internal and external calculi is useful because each type of calculus has distinct properties making one type better suited for certain applications than the other. For example, it has been claimed that internal calculi are better suited for establishing properties such as termination, interpolation, and optimal complexity, while external calculi are better suited for counter-model generation and permit easier proofs of cut admissibility and completeness
[
Vig00
,
Neg05
]
.

Due to the diverse number of proof-theoretic formalisms and the categorization of calculi into internal and external, a large body of work has been dedicated to investigating the relationships between calculi within distinct formalisms by means of
translations
. A translation is a function from one proof formalism into another which extends in a natural way to yield structure-preserving maps between derivations in the concrete calculi instantiating the formalisms. The goal of this paper is twofold: (1) we map out the relationships between various proof-theoretic formalisms by means of translations and (2) we investigate the internal and external distinction in light of these relationships.

We consider sequent-based formalisms in this paper for generality as other types of proof systems, e.g. tableaux and natural deduction systems, can be transformed into sequent calculi. Moreover, sequent-based systems are easier to mechanize, meaning the systems we consider have a higher degree of practical use.
What we find is that proof-theoretic formalisms sit within a hierarchy that increases in complexity from Gentzen-style sequents up to labeled sequents, and is based upon the underlying data structure of the sequents used in the system. We will argue that it is ‘easier’ to translate proofs up this hierarchy than down this hierarchy. Furthermore, we will dispel myths about internal and external calculi, explaining that the terms ‘internal’ and ‘external’ resist rigorous definition, and clarifying what properties can actually be expected of proof systems that are often qualified as ‘internal’ or ‘external’. To provide a broad account of sequent-based formalisms and internal/external calculi we consider a large number of formalisms and systems constructed for a wide array of logics, including modal and tense logics, intuitionistic logic, conditional logics, and bunched implication logic.

We identify five desiderata for proof-theoretic formalisms (see e.g.,
[
Wan94
,
Avr96
]
):
(1) Generality: the formalism covers a sizable class of logics with proof systems sharing desirable properties, (2) Uniformity: the formalism need not be enriched to obtain a system for a logic within a given class, (3) Modularity: a system for one logic within the considered class can be transformed into a system for another, with properties preserved, by adding/deleting rules or modifying the functionality of rules, (4) Constructibility: a method is known for constructing a calculus for a given logic in the considered class, and (5) Syntactic Parsimony: the data structures used are as simple as required by the logic or purpose of the proof systems.

When the desiderata (1)-(4) are satisfied, a proof formalism is expected to generate large classes of proof calculi for logics on demand without requiring substantial work on the side of the logician. The fifth desideratum is meant to enforce that a formalism should produce calculi which are as simple as required to improve their interpretability and the ease with which they may be applied. However, the
purpose
of a proof system, i.e. its use in a particular application, may require the data structure of the sequent to be enriched, and so, we note that it is sensible at times to trade syntactic parsimony for complexity so the proof system is better adapted for a particular application. For example, although nested sequents employ trees of Gentzen-style sequents, they are better suited for counter-model extraction than Gentzen-style sequent calculi, and so, if we aim to use our systems to extract counter-models of formulae, then it is sensible to trade the simple structure of Gentzen-style sequents for nested sequents.

It is not to be taken for granted that a single proof formalism can fulfill all of the above requirements, which justifies the study of alternative proof systems and formalisms with different properties, used for different purposes. We will, throughout the course of the paper, discuss which formalisms satisfy which desiderata to pinpoint distinctions between formalisms and motivate the usage of one formalism relative to another.

This paper is organized as follows: In Section
1
, we introduce the various families of logics we will consider and their semantics, including modal and tense logics, intuitionistic logic, conditional logics, and bunched logics. In Section
2
, we will explain the various sequent-based formalisms and specific systems that have been introduced for these logics, giving a broad account of the types of sequent systems that appear in the literature. In the subsequent section (Section
3
), we organize these proof-theoretic formalisms and systems into a hierarchy and explain how to traverse this hierarchy by means of translations. Lastly, in Section
4
, we consider the internal and external distinction, discuss the issues with defining these terms, and set the record straight regarding what properties calculi deemed ‘internal’ or ‘external’ can be expected to satisfy.

1.
Logical Preliminaries

To keep the paper self contained, we introduce the various logics whose proof systems will be considered throughout the paper. In particular, we define the languages and model-theoretic semantics for modal and tense logics, intuitionistic logic, conditional logics, and bunched implication logics.

1.1.
Modal Logics

The origin of modal logic is often attributed to the work of C.I. Lewis
[
Lew18
]
, who formulated a notion of strict implication in an attempt to resolve certain paradoxes of material implication. Since then various modal logics have been defined by expanding a base logic (e.g. classical or intuitionistic logic) with
modalities
, that is, logical operators that qualify the truth of a proposition.
Normal modal logics
extend classical propositional logic through the incorporation of
alethic
modalities, namely, “it is possible that” (denoted by
\Diamond
) and “it is necessary that” (denoted by
\Box
). For an in-depth treatment and presentation of such logics, see Blackburn et al.
[
BdRV01
]
.

Let us formally define the normal modal logics we consider. We let
\mathsf{Prop}:=\{p,q,r,\ldots\}
be a set of
propositional atoms
(which are occasionally annotated), and note that normal modal logics are extensions of classical propositional logic, which makes use of formulae generated by the following grammar in BNF:

A::=p\ |\ \neg A\ |\ A\lor A\ |\ A\land A\ |\ A\rightarrow A

where
p
ranges over
\mathsf{Prop}
. We mention this language as classical propositional logic will be discussed later on. It is well known that disjunction and conjunction are definable in terms of
\neg
and
\rightarrow
, i.e.
A\lor B:=\neg A\rightarrow B
and
A\land B:=\neg(A\rightarrow\neg B)
. We mention this because the modal logics we consider in this paper omit the use of
\lor
and
\land
as such connectives are definable in terms of the others, and therefore, extraneous. To obtain modal logics, we define the language
\mathcal{L}_{M}
to be the set of formulae generated via the following grammar in BNF:

A::=p\ |\ \neg A\ |\ A\rightarrow A\ |\ \Box A

where
p
ranges over
\mathsf{Prop}
. Such formulae are interpreted over
modal Kripke models
. We define such models below, and afterward, define how formulae are interpreted over them.

1.2.
Tense Logics

Tense logics were invented by Prior in the 1950’s
[
Pri57
]
, and are types of normal modal logics that not only include the
\Diamond
and
\Box
modalities, but the

\blacklozenge
and
\blacksquare

\Diamond
is read as “in some future moment,”
\Box

\blacklozenge
is read as “in some past moment,” and
\blacksquare
is read as “in every past moment.” In this paper, we consider the

are in negation normal form as this will simplify the sequent systems we consider later on. Note that negation

[

]

1.3.
Intuitionistic Logic

Intuitionistic logic aims to capture the notion of constructive proof, something which classical logic fails to do
[
Hey30
]
. For this reason intuitionistic logic does not contain familiar classical axioms such as the law of the excluded middle (
p\lor\lnot p
) and double negation elimination (
\lnot\lnot p\supset p
).

The language of intuitionistic logic
\mathcal{L}_{I}
is the same as for classical propositional logic, with the exception that implication
\supset
must be included in the language as it is not definable in terms of the other connectives. We define
\mathcal{L}_{I}
by means of the following grammar in BNF:

A::=p\ |\ \bot\ |\ A\lor A\ |\ A\land A\ |\ A\supset A

where
p
ranges over
\mathsf{Prop}
. As usual, we define
\lnot A:=A\supset\bot
. Intuitionistic formulae are interpreted over
intuitionistic Kripke models
, defined accordingly.

{defi}

[Intuitionistic Kripke Model] An
intuitionistic Kripke frame
is defined to be an ordered pair
F:=(W,\leq)
such that
W
is a non-empty set of points, called
worlds
, and the
accessibility relation

\leq\ \subseteq W\times W
is reflexive and transitive. A
intuitionistic Kripke model
is defined to be a tuple
M=(F,V)
such that
F
is an intuitionistic Kripke frame and
V:\mathsf{Prop}\to 2^{W}
is a
valuation function
satisfying the
persistence
condition, that is, if
w\in V(p)
and
wRu
, then
u\in V(p)
.

{defi}

[Semantic Clauses] Given an intuitionistic Kripke model
M=(W,\leq,V)
, we define a forcing relation
\models
such that

•

M,w\not\models\bot
;

•

M,w\models p

iff

w\in V(p)
;

•

M,w\models A\lor B

iff

M,w\models A
or
M,w\models B
;

•

M,w\models A\land B

iff

M,w\models A
and
M,w\models B
;

•

M,w\models A\supset B

iff
for every
u\in W
, if
w\leq u
and
M,u\models A
, then
M,u\models B
;

•

M\models A

iff
for every

,
M,w\models A
.

We define a formula

to be
intuitionistically-valid

iff
for all intuitionistic Kripke models

,

. We define

\mathsf{IL}
to be the set of intuitionistically-valid formulas.

A basic fact of intuitionistic logic is that it forms a proper subset of classical logic. Conversely, via the double negation translation, classical logic can be embedded into
\mathsf{IL}

[

]
. Moreover, there is also a natural embedding of (axiomatic extensions of) intuitionistic logic (called
intermediate logics
) into (axiomatic extensions of) the modal logic
\mathsf{S4}

[
Kri65
]
.

1.4.
Conditional Logics

Conditional logic deals with a hypothetical notion of implication that cannot be represented by classical/material implication or by intuitionistic/strict (modal) implication. Rather, implication is interpreted either
counterfactually
, e.g. “if A were the case, then B would be the case,” or
typically
, e.g. “Normally, if A then B.” Although the family of conditional logics contains over 50 systems, we concentrate on the conditional logic of counterfactual reasoning
V
defined by Lewis
[
Lew73
]
. We choose to focus our attention on this conditional logic as its proof-theoretical treatment requires and illustrates special techniques.

In Lewis’s account, the conditional operator is defined in terms of another operator, referred to as
comparative plausibility
and denoted
\preceq
. The formula
A\preceq B
states that “
A
is at least as plausible as
B
.” The conditional operator
A>B
may then be defined as
(\bot\preceq A)\lor\neg(A\land\neg B\preceq(A\land B))
, and is interpreted as, “
A
is impossible or
A\land\neg B
is less plausible than
A\land B
.” We note that this definition of
A>B
can be simplified by replacing
A\land B
by
A
in the second disjunct, yielding the official definition:

A>B:=(\bot\preceq A)\lor\neg(A\land\neg B\preceq A)

Conversely, we note that
\preceq
can be defined in terms of the conditional operator
>
. Our full language is defined via the following grammar in BNF:

A::=p\ |\ \neg A\ |\ A\lor A\ |\ A\land A\ |\ A\rightarrow B\ |\ A\preceq A

where
p
ranges over
\mathsf{Prop}
.

An axiomatization of
V
in terms of
\preceq
is given by extending an axiomatization of classical propositional logic including
modus ponens
(MP) with the following set of axioms and rules:

•

(TR)
(A\preceq B)\land(B\preceq C)\rightarrow(A\preceq C)

•

(CO)
(A\preceq B)\lor(B\preceq A)

•

(CPA)
(A\preceq A\lor B)\lor(B\preceq A\lor B)

•

(CPR) the rule

\frac{A\rightarrow B}{B\preceq A}

From a semantic point of view,
V
is characterized by special kinds of neighborhood models, introduced by Lewis and called
sphere models
. In this semantics, each world is assigned a system of spheres, i.e. a set of nested neighborhoods. The intuition is that spheres represent degrees of plausibility, so that worlds in smaller spheres are considered more plausible than worlds contained solely in larger spheres.

{defi}

[Sphere Model] A sphere model
M=(W,S,V)
is a triple such that
W
is a non-empty set of worlds,
S:W\to 2^{2^{W}}
,

is a valuation function, and the following conditions are satisfied, for every

•

for every
\alpha\in S(w)
,
\alpha\not=\emptyset

•

for every
\alpha,\beta\in S(w)

\alpha\subseteq\beta
or
\beta\subseteq\alpha
.

The elements of

{defi}

[Semantic Clauses] Given a sphere model

such that

•

M,w\models p

iff

w\in V(p)
;

•

M,w\models\neg A

iff

M,w\not\models A
;

•

M,w\models A\lor B

iff

M,w\models A

M,w\models B

iff

M,w\models A

M,w\models B

We define a formula

Given a sphere model

\alpha\models^{\exists}A

With this notation, the semantic clauses for the comparative plausibility operator and the conditional operator are equivalent to the clauses shown below top and below bottom, respectively.

•

iff
for all

\alpha\models^{\exists}A

•

iff

, or there is an

\alpha\models^{\exists}A
and
\alpha\models^{\forall}A\to B

By imposing additional properties on sphere models, we obtain a family of conditional logics. Later on, we will define calculi for conditional logics that incorporate inference rules for the plausibility operator, taking the conditional operator as defined.

1.5.
Bunched Logics

2.1.

The sequent calculi by Gentzen

The calculus
\mathsf{S(CP)}
has some important properties that set it as an “ideal” proof system, which we enumerate and elaborate on below. We note that the following terminology will also be applied to the other kinds of sequent-style systems we consider later on.

•

Analyticity
: the premises of each rule only contain subformulae of the conclusion. Thus, if we do not consider multiple occurrences of the same formulae (i.e. we consider a sequent as a pair of sets), given a sequent
S
at the root of a derivation
\mathcal{D}
, there are only finitely many different sequents that can occur in
\mathcal{D}
. This follow from the redundancy of the cut rule, i.e.
cut-elimination
, meaning every proof containing applications of
(cut)
can be transformed into a
(cut)
-free proof of the same conclusion
[
Gen35a
,
Gen35b
]
.

•

Termination
: the premises of each rule are less complex than the conclusion. We can see that this property holds in
\mathsf{S(CP)}
(without structural rules) since the auxiliary formulae are always less complex than the principal formulae. This property together with analyticity ensures that proof-search always terminates, that is, every branch of a derivation
\mathcal{D}
terminates with an axiom or an unprovable sequent (usually containing only atoms).

•

Invertibility
: if the conclusion of a rule is derivable, then its premises are derivable. By this property, the order of bottom-up applications of rules during proof-search does not matter: either we obtain a proof of the root sequent, or we get a (finite) derivation containing an unprovable sequent as a leaf. When this property is present, backtracking (i.e. searching for alternative proofs) is unnecessary during proof-search.

•

Counter-model generation
: if proof-search yields a derivation
\mathcal{D}
that is not a proof of the conclusion, then there exists an unprovable sequent as a leaf which can be used to define a counter-model of the conclusion. In the case of
\mathsf{S(CP)}

\Gamma\Rightarrow\Delta
is such a leaf in a derivation

, then
\Gamma\cap\Delta=\emptyset
, and one can define a propositional evaluation ‘

iff

\mathcal{D}
.

The calculus

Gentzen’s sequent systems are also flexible enough to capture other logics. For example, intuitionistic logic can be provided a sequent calculus by making simple modifications to
\mathsf{S(CP)}
. The calculus
\mathsf{S(IL)}
, shown in Figure
5

\Gamma\Rightarrow\Delta
such that
|\Delta|\leq 1
) could be used in derivations. However, Gentzen’s restriction invalidates certain admissibility and invertibility properties, which can be regained by swapping the
(\rightarrow_{l})
and
(\rightarrow_{l})
rules with the
(\supset_{l})
and
(\supset_{r})
rules shown in Figure
5

\mathsf{S(IL)}
is a variant of Gentzen’s sequent calculus for intuitionistic logic that has all structural rules, including
(cut)
, admissible.

The calculus
\mathsf{S(IL)}
is analytic, though not terminating as the premises of a rule may be as complex as the conclusion as witnessed by the premises of (
\supset_{l}
). Additionally, the (
\supset_{r}
) rule is not invertible, which is an impediment to proof-search. In particular, if proof-search constructs a derivation that is not a proof, then a proof may still exist and the constructed derivation may not provide a counter-model of the conclusion. Due to analyticity a decision procedure can still be obtained in principle, however, the procedure will also require loop checking which diminishes its efficiency. We find that although Gentzen’s sequent formalism satisfies syntactic parsimony to a high degree, the formalism lacks uniformity and modularity since simple modifications to a calculus can nullify important properties. In addition, although Gentzen-style sequent calculi have been provided for many logics, the formalism is not general enough to yield cut-free systems for many logics of interest (e.g.
\mathsf{S5}
and bi-intuitionistic logics
[
BG07
]
). These facts motivate the search for more expressive formalisms that satisfy our important desiderata.

Hypersequents

Introduced independently by Mints
[

[
Pot83
]

[

]

“

hypersequent
is a multiset of the form:

\Gamma_{1}\Rightarrow\Delta_{1}\;\mid\;\dots\;\mid\;\Gamma_{n}\Rightarrow\Delta_{n}

where, for each
1\leq i\leq n

is a Gentzen-style sequent, called a
component
of the hypersequent.

As with ordinary sequent calculi, the inference rules of hypersequent calculi consist of initial hypersequents (i.e. axioms), logical rules, and structural rules, which includes a cut rule. These inference rules are usually presented as
rule schemata. A concrete instance of a rule is obtained by instantiating the
schematic variables with concrete formulae. In the case of hypersequents,
the rule schema may also contain schematic context variables for side hypersequents, denoted by
G
or

H
, representing (possibly empty) hypersequents.
The logical and structural rules are divided into internal and external rules.
The internal rules deal with formulae within one component of the conclusion. Examples of internal structural rules are the ordinary rules of weakening and contraction of the sequent calculus (augmented with the side hypersequents). Examples of external structural rules are external weakening (
ew

ec

hypersequents. The hypersequent structure opens the possibility to
define new external structural rules that allow for the “exchange of information” between different sequents. It is this possibility which increases the expressive power of hypersequent calculi compared to ordinary, Gentzen-style sequent calculi.

We end this subsection with a brief discussion concerning the relationship between propagation/reachability rules and the property of
modularity
, that is, the ease with which a calculus for one logic may be transformed into a calculus for another logic within a given class. An interesting feature of propagation and reachability rules concerns the means by which they introduce modularity into a proof calculus. It has been argued—most notably by Avron
[
Avr96
, Section 1]
and Wansing
[
Wan94
, Section 3.3]

[T]he rules for the logical operations are never changed: all changes are made in the structural rules
[
Doš88
, p. 352]

Although we agree that modularity is an important feature of a proof formalism, we argue that Došen’s principle is
too strict
. This perspective is supported by the formulation of propagation/reachability rules within nested systems, which attain modularity by a different means. Since these types of rules generalize the functionality of logical rules by permitting data to be shifted or consumed along paths within a nested sequent, systems which include such rules possess a high degree of modularity, obtained by simply changing the paths considered, irrespective of structural rules.

3.
Navigating the Proof-Theoretic Jungle

As discussed in Section
2
and shown in Figure
2
, the data structure underlying sequents naturally imposes a hierarchy on the various types of proof systems we have been considering. At the base of this hierarchy sits Gentzen-style sequents, which are incrementally generalized at each level of the hierarchy by defining ever more complex data structures, until labeled sequents are reached at the top. As we are interested in exploring this hierarchy, we present translations of proofs between systems in different proof-theoretic formalisms, thus letting us ‘shift’ derivations up and down the hierarchy. The lesson we learn is that translating proofs down the hierarchy requires significantly more work than translating proofs up the hierarchy.

3.2.
From Hypersequents to Sequents with Cuts

Hypersequent calculi are more expressive than sequent calculi since the latter are a particular case of the former. There are logics that possess a cut-free hypersequent calculus
but for which no cut-free sequent calculus is known. This is the case, for instance, of the modal logic S5. A syntactic transformation from cut-free hypersequent calculi to sequent calculi with cuts whose cut-formulas have a certain bounded shape has been introduced in
[
CLR21
]
.
The transformation works for large classes of substructural and modal logics.

Given a proof of a sequent in the hypersequent calculus, the high-level idea is to progressively convert hypersequents to sequents (with the addition of cuts), starting from each “peculiar” hypersequent rule working downwards towards the root.
The key observation here is that we can extract a sequent calculus from a hypersequent calculus that does not make use of hypersequent rules, other than
(EW)
and
(EC)
, that operates on more than one components.
The subformula property in the hypersequent calculus is used to bound the form of the sequent cuts that need to be introduced.

3.5.2.
From Labeled Sequents to Structured Sequents.

The translation from the labeled calculus
G_{lV}
to the structured calculus
IG({V})
is more difficult because not every sequent of the labeled calculus can be translated into a sequent of the structured calculus, and consequently a derivation in
G_{lV}
contains steps that cannot be simulated in the calculus
IG({V})
.
In this short description we limit to describe the approach and difficulties in order to define such a translation, for a formal treatment we refer to
[
GON18
]
.

(1)

The translation only applies to labeled sequents of the form
t(\Gamma\Rightarrow\Delta)^{x}
that are the image of the translation of a sequent
\Gamma\Rightarrow\Delta
of the structured calculus
IG({V})
.

(2)

However a proof of
t(\Gamma\Rightarrow\Delta)^{x}
in
G_{lV}
may involve sequents that are not translatable, whence rules that cannot be simulated in the internal calculus.

(3)

To deal with this problem we rearrange a proof in a specific
normal form
in which rules are applied in a certain order.

(4)

We then show that the ”main” steps (that is sequents) in a normal proof can be translated in the internal calculus and are linked by rules of the internal calculus.

(5)

Thus, the rules of the structured calculus act as “macro” rules of the labeled calculus that skip untranslatable sequents.

We illustrate the translation of a sequent by an example: let
S
be the sequent:

\displaystyle a_{1}\subseteq a_{2},a_{2}\subseteq a_{3},a_{1}\subseteq a_{3},a_{1}\in S(x),a_{2}\in S(x),a_{3}\in S(x),

\displaystyle a_{1}\Vdash^{\exists}A_{1},a_{2}\Vdash^{\exists}A_{2},a_{3}\Vdash^{\exists}A_{3},x:\Gamma\Rightarrow x:\Delta,a_{1}\Vdash^{\exists}\Sigma_{1},a_{2}\Vdash^{\exists}\Sigma_{2},a_{3}\Vdash^{\exists}\Sigma_{3}

where
\Gamma,\Delta
only contain formulas of the language, then its translation is:

\Gamma\Rightarrow\Delta,\left[\Sigma_{1},\Sigma_{2},\Sigma_{3}\vartriangleleft A_{1}\right],\left[\Sigma_{2},\Sigma_{3}\vartriangleleft A_{2}\right],\left[\Sigma_{3}\vartriangleleft A_{3}\right]

we add to the corresponding block also formulas

such that

occurs in the consequent of the labeled sequent.
Thus, each block in the internal calculus consists of
\preceq

a

Intuitively a sequent
\Gamma\Rightarrow\Delta
has a tree-like structure if for every label
x
occurring in
\Gamma

reachable from
x
by the transitive closure of the relation
x\rightarrow y
forms a tree.
As matter of fact only a subset of sequents with a tree-like structure can be translated in sequents of the language of
IG({V})
.

By exploiting the tree structure, a derivable untranslatable sequent
\Gamma\Rightarrow\Delta
can be
replaced
by a derivable translatable sequent
\Gamma_{1}\Rightarrow\Delta_{1}
obtained by a decomposition of
\Gamma\Rightarrow\Delta
determined by the tree-like structure associated to every label
x
occurring in
\Gamma
: either
\Gamma_{1}\Rightarrow\Delta_{1}
is the subsequent containing only the labels in the tree rooted in
x
and the formulas/relation involving these labels, or it is the subsequent obtained by
removing
from
\Gamma\Rightarrow\Delta
the labels and formulas of the tree of
x
, or it is obtained from the latter by iterating the process (on another label). If
\Gamma\Rightarrow\Delta
is derivable, there exists a translatable subsequent
\Gamma_{1}\Rightarrow\Delta_{1}
of
\Gamma\Rightarrow\Delta
which is derivable too (with the same height).

Thus, in order to define the translation of the whole derivation
\mathcal{D}
, when an untranslatable sequent
\Gamma\Rightarrow\Delta
is reached, we consider then the translation of the sub-derivation
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
of a subsequent
\Gamma_{1}\Rightarrow\Delta_{1}
obtained by decomposition of
\Gamma\Rightarrow\Delta
.
Since the sequent
\Gamma_{1}\Rightarrow\Delta_{1}
is not determined in advance and it is not necessarily unique, the translation of
\mathcal{D}
is not entirely
deterministic
.

3.6.3.
Lost in Translation: why it fails when it fails.

Bunched (and resource) logics exhibit a first notable difference with intuitionistic logic and modal logics like
\mathsf{K}
in that the corresponding semantics do not rely only on properties of an accessibility relation in a Kripke model, but also on world (resource) composition.
In particular, since
\mathsf{BI}
admits both an additive and a multiplicative composition, the relational atoms
u\mathcal{R}w
are generalised into relations of the form
{\mathfrak{r}(\ell_{1},\ell_{2})\mathrel{\leqslant}\ell}
where
\mathfrak{r}
is one of the binary functors
\mathfrak{a}
or
\mathfrak{m}
.
Moreover, in intuitionistic logic or modal logics like
\mathsf{K}
,
\mathsf{S4}
,
\mathsf{S5}
, the semantic and the syntactic readings of a relational atom
u\mathcal{R}w
coincide when interpreted in terms of ordering relations “successor” and “expanded after”.
More precisely, consider the rule for right implication in intuitionistic logic depicted in Fig.
13
.
The semantic reading is that, when interpreted in a Kripke structure,
u
should be the successor of
w
w.r.t. the accessibility relation, which can be written as
w\leqslant_{\text{succ}}u
.
The syntactic reading of
u\mathcal{R}w
is that since
A
and
B
are labeled with
w
and
A\mathbin{\makebox[9.68747pt]{$\supset$}}B
is labeled with
u
,
A
and
B
are subformulas of
A\mathbin{\makebox[9.68747pt]{$\supset$}}B
and should therefore necessarily appear (and be expanded) after
A\mathbin{\makebox[9.68747pt]{$\supset$}}B
in a (shallow) proof system.
In other words, the subformula interpretation induces a rule application order in a syntactic proof system, which could be written as

(the formulas labeled with
u
must be expanded after the ones labeled with
w
).
Notice that
\leqslant_{\text{succ}}
and
\leqslant_{\text{after}}
are covariant (
w
and
u
occur on the same side in both orders).

However, a key problem in
\mathsf{BI}
(and in resource logics more generally) is that the syntactic and the semantic readings are contravariant and sometimes even fully lost.
Indeed, if we consider the rule for the left multiplicative conjunction
\mathbin{\makebox[6.45831pt]{$\ast$}}
given in Fig.

, it is clearly seen that since
A
and
B
are subformulas of

, we syntactically have

(for

, but we semantically have (reading

as

)

(with
\ell_{1}
and
\ell_{2}
occurring on the opposite side compared with
\leqslant_{\text{after}}
).
Moreover, we do not even get any relation of the form
\ell\leqslant_{\text{succ}}\ell_{i}
or
\ell_{i}\leqslant_{\text{succ}}\ell
at all.

The immediate consequence of losing the general connection between the syntactic subformula ordering and the semantic successor ordering is that finding an extension of (the translation in) Definition
3.6.2
that could work for unrestricted labeled proofs is not at all trivial and might even be impossible to achieve.

3.7.
Some Remarks on Translations

The above translations substantiate our claim that translating up the proof-theoretic hierarchy tends to be ‘easier’ than translating down. In particular, we found that cuts needed to be introduced to proofs when translating hypersequents to sequents for
\mathsf{S5}
(Section
3.2
) and that structural rule elimination was needed to translate labeled proofs into nested proofs for intuitionistic logic (Section
3.4
). Moreover, translating labeled proofs to structured sequent proofs for conditional logics introduced non-determinism (Section
3.5
) and translating labeled proofs into bunched proofs (Section
3.6
) was only possible given that the labeled proof was of a ‘treelike’ shape. Converse translations were far simpler to obtain, e.g. translating sequent proofs into labeled proofs for intuitionistic logic (Section
3.4
) and translating display proofs into labeled proofs for the tense logic
\mathsf{Kt}
(Section
3.3
). The sophistication required in translating proofs down the hierarchy supports the claim that formalisms higher up in the hierarchy are more expressive than those below them.

4.
The Internal and External Distinction

In the literature, proof formalisms and calculi are often classified into
internal
or
external
. Typically, a formalism or calculus is placed into one of these two classes based on (1) the syntactic elements present within the types of sequents used and/or (2) the interpretability of sequents as logical formulae. Various definitions have been stipulated to categorize a formalism or calculus as internal or external, and tend to be expressed along one of the following two lines, which we analyze and explore subsequently:

(1)

Internal calculi omit semantic elements from the syntax of their sequents, whereas external calculi explicitly include semantic elements.

(2)

Internal calculi are those where every sequent is interpretable as a formula in the language of the logic, whereas external calculi are those without a formula translation.

For example, hypersequent and nested calculi are often considered internal as such calculi tend to employ sequents which are immediately interpretable as logical formulae
[
MMS21
]
. On the other hand, labeled calculi are often classified as external as they incorporate semantic information in their syntax and labeled sequents exist which resist interpretation as logical formulae
[
CLR18
]
. We remark that sometimes extra machinery is inserted into a proof calculus for ‘bureaucratic’ reasons (e.g. to simplify proof-search algorithms); such machinery should be ignored when considering a calculus internal or external.

A core motivation for separating formalisms/calculi into these two categories, is that internal and external formalisms/calculi are
claimed
to possess distinct advantages over one another. It has been argued that internal calculi are better suited for establishing properties such as termination, interpolation, and optimal complexity, while external calculi are more easily constructed and permit simpler proofs of completeness, cut-admissibility, and counter-model generation (from terminating proof-search). However, we will argue that a large number of such claims are false.

In this section we delve into the internal and external distinction, and discuss two main themes. First, we attempt to formally define the notions of internal and external, arguing that each candidate definition comes with certain drawbacks, or fails to satisfy our intuitions concerning internal and external systems in some way. Second, we aim to dispel myths about the claimed properties of internal and external systems, while pointing out which attributes of such systems are genuinely useful for certain applications.

4.1.
Analyzing Definitions of Internal and External

We begin by investigating definition (1) above, where external calculi are those which incorporate ‘semantic elements’ into the syntax of their sequents while internal calculi are those which do not. An immediate issue that arises with this definition is that it relies on an inherently vague notion: what do we take to be a ‘semantic element’? Admittedly, it seems clear that the labels and relational atoms used in labeled sequents should qualify as ‘semantic elements’ as such syntactic objects encode features of relational models. Yet, via the translation from labeled to nested sequents (see Definition
3.4.2
in Section
3

Similarly, the components of linear nested sequents and hypersequents directly correspond to worlds in relational models with the linear nested structure ‘
\sslash

The issue with the first proposed definition is that it is too vague to properly distinguish between internal and external systems as the concept of a ‘semantic element’ is too vague. Thus, we find that definition (1) is unsuitable for distinguishing internality and externality.

Let us now investigate definition (2) above, where internal calculi are qualified as those with sequents interpretable as formulae in the language of the logic, and external calculi are those for which this property does not hold. A couple of questions come to the fore when we consider this definition: What does it mean for a sequent to be
interpretable
as a formula? What
properties
should such an interpretation possess? We begin investigating these questions by considering a few examples of ‘internal’ systems from the literature. Our aim is to extract general underlying patterns from the examples with the goal of supplying a formal definition of ‘internality’. What we will find is that regardless of how we attempt to rigorously specify a definition, calculi recognized as ‘internal’ and ‘external’ exist which respectively fall outside or within the definition, thus witnessing the formal definition’s inadequacy.

Gentzen-style calculi, nested sequent calculi, and hypersequent calculi are normally characterized as internal systems. Typically, what is meant by an ‘interpretation of a sequent as a formula’ is a

Based on the discussion above, we could identify calculi as internal or external if the sequent language of the calculus is internal or external, respectively. Nevertheless, two practical issues arise: first, confirming that a language is external is subject to the difficulty that one must confirm the non-existence of any translation mapping sequents to ‘structurally similar’ and ‘logical equivalent’ formulae. However, it must be conceded that the above notion of ‘internal’ is aligned with our intuition concerning what an internal system ought to be, and can be taken as a sufficient (but not necessary) criterion for applying the term ‘internal’ to a proof system. What we find to be important however, is less about whether a proof system satisfies our intuitions concerning ‘internality,’ and more about the existence of translations from sequents to ‘structurally similar’ and ‘logically equivalent’ formulae–a fact that will be discussed in more detail below.

4.2.
Analyzing Properties of Internal and External Systems

Here we consider various properties attributed to internal and external calculi, and clarify how such claims are (in)correct. For ease of presentation, we first present each claim in italics, and after, provide our perspective of the claim. Although this section is intended to dispel myths about internal and external systems, we do put forth positive applications of ‘internal’ calculi at the end of the section. In particular, we explain how the existence of a translation from sequents to ‘structurally similar’ and ‘logically equivalent’ formulae can be practically leveraged in a few different ways.

Internal calculi are better suited than external calculi for decidability
. There are two standard methods in which decidability is obtained via proof-search in a sequent calculus, which we call (1) the syntactic method, and (2) the semantic method. In the syntactic method, one establishes that every theorem has a proof of a certain form, and shows that only a finite number of such proofs exist. Decidability is then obtained by searching this finite space, and if a proof is found, the input is known to be valid; otherwise, the input is known to be invalid. In the semantic method, one attempts to construct a proof of the input, and shows that if a proof-search fails, then a counter-model of the input can be extracted. It does seem that the syntactic method is more easily carried out with Gentzen-style systems, which are internal systems, due to the minimality of the underlying data structure used in sequents. Nevertheless, external systems–such as those of Simpson
[
Sim94
]
–also admit decidability via the syntactic method.

When it comes to applying the semantic method, there appears to be a trade-off between using internal and external calculi. Note that the semantic method consists of two components: (1) one must establish the termination of the proof-search procedure, and (2) one must extract a counter-model if proof-search fails. We point out that ‘internal’ calculi seem better for securing termination while ‘external’ calculi appear better suited for extracting a counter-model. First, since the sequents in internal systems tend to utilize simpler data structures, establishing the termination of proof-search tends to be more easily obtained than for external systems (which utilize more complex and difficult to control data structures). Second, extracting a counter-model from failed proof-search tends to be easier in external systems than internal systems as the former tend to encode model-theoretic information. Nevertheless, this observation merely points out that there are trade-offs in using one type of system as opposed to another, and does not outright prove that one type of system is more advantageous than another in establishing decidability. Indeed, there are many examples of decision/proof-search algorithms for wide classes of logics based on internal systems
[
LGA22
,
TIG12
,
Sla97
]
and external systems
[
HS04
,
Sim94
]
, so we find that this claim is not warranted.

Internal calculi are better suited than external calculi for interpolation
. The method of establishing interpolation via sequent-style systems is due to Maehara
[
Mae60
]
, and was originally introduced in the context of Gentzen-style sequent systems. This method has been adapted to linear nested and hypersequent systems
[

]
, nested systems
[

,

]
, display systems
[

]
, and labeled systems
[

. If one compares such works on proof-theoretic interpolation, they will find that both internal and external systems alike are used in securing interpolation properties for a logics; e.g. truly sizable classes of logics have been shown to exhibit Craig and Lyndon interpolation with both internal (viz. nested) systems
[

]
and external (viz. labeled) systems
[
Kuz16
]
. Therefore, the claim that internal calculi are better suited for establishing interpolation does not appear warranted.

Internal calculi are harder to find/construct then external calculi
. We somewhat agree with the claim that internal calculi are more difficult to find/construct in contrast to external calculi. First, we note that it is rather straightforward to generate labeled calculi for diverse classes of logics
[
CMS13
,
Sim94
]
. Nevertheless, techniques do exist for generating internal calculi as well. For example, numerous logics have been provided (internal) display calculi
[

,
Wan02
]
, algorithms exist for producing sequent and hypersequent calculi from suitable Hilbert systems
[

]
, and it is now understood how to transform certain semantic properties into nested sequent systems
[
Lyo21a
]
or hypersequents
[
Lah13
]
. Even though such methods yield sizable classes of internal calculi, they are more involved than the method of generating labeled systems.

Cut-admissibility is more difficult to establish for internal calculi
. The claim that cut-admissibility is more difficult to shown with internal calculi does not appear to be warranted. Both the labeled and display formalisms yield uniform and modular calculi for extensive classes of logics, yet, general cut-admissibility results exist for labeled calculi
[
Sim94
]
and a general cut-elimination theorem holds for display calculi
[
Bel82
]
.

In spite of the various properties attributed to ‘internal’ and ‘external’ systems, we have identified three ways in which ‘internal’ systems (i.e. sequent-style systems with a ‘structurally similar’ and ‘logically equivalent’ formula translation) are useful. First, if the rules of an ‘internal’ system are invertible and the system is shown to be weakly complete (i.e. any theorem of the logic is derivable), then one should be able to establish the strong completeness of the calculus (i.e. any valid sequent is derivable). It is straightforward to establish this property: if we assume a sequent is valid, then its formula translation is valid, meaning the formula translation is derivable by weak completeness. One can then apply the invertibility of the inference rules to the formula translation to derive the original sequent. Although we do not claim that this property holds of any system that might be reasonably deemed ‘internal’ we do note that this method of lifting weak completeness to strong completeness appears to work in a variety of cases; e.g. (linear) nested sequents
[
KL18
,
Lyo21a
]
. A second favorable property of ‘internal’ calculi concerns the lack of a ‘meta-semantics’. Since sequents are interpreted via their ‘structurally similar’ and ‘logically equivalent’ formula translations, there is no need to define a more general semantics–as is done with labeled systems–to interpret sequents. Third, it has been shown that ‘internal’ (viz. nested) systems can be used to derive Hilbert systems, i.e. axiomatizations, for logics
[
IK07
]
, and it seems that Hilbert proofs can be extracted as well. This is obviously beneficial for anyone interested in characterizing a logic purely in terms of its formulae with simple inference rules. Last, another interesting feature of ‘internal’ calculi is that such systems usually do not make any
semantic commitments
since they utilize a language as expressive as the logic’s and tend to omit semantic elements.
