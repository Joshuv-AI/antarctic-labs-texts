Abstract
. We construct unique martingale solutions to the damped stochastic wave equation μ ​ ∂ 2 u ∂ t 2 ​ ( t , x ) = Δ ​ u ​ ( t , x ) − ∂ u ∂ t ​ ( t , x ) + b ⁡ ( t , x , u ⁡ ( t , x ) ) + σ ⁡ ( t , x , u ⁡ ( t , x ) ) ​ d ​ W t d ​ t , \mu\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial t^{2}}(t,x)=\Delta u(t,x)-\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}(t,x)+b(t,x,u(t,x))+\sigma(t,x,u(t,x))\frac{dW_{t}}{dt}, where Δ \Delta is the Laplacian on [ 0 , 1 ] [0,1] with Dirichlet boundary condition, W W is space-time white noise, σ \sigma is 3 4 + ϵ \frac{3}{4}+\epsilon -Hölder continuous in u u and uniformly non-degenerate, and b b has linear growth. 1.
Introduction

In this paper we are interested in the damped stochastic wave equation with irregularity in both the drift and the diffusion coefficients. On the compact interval
D=[0,1]\subset\mathbb{R}
the equation can be formulated as

(1.1)

\displaystyle\mu\frac{\partial^{2}u^{\mu}}{\partial t^{2}}=\Delta u^{\mu}(t,x)-\frac{\partial u^{\mu}}{\partial t}(t,x)+b(t,x,u^{\mu}(t,x))+g(t,x,u^{\mu}(t,x))dW(t,x),

\displaystyle u^{\mu}(t,x)=0,\quad x\in\partial D,

\displaystyle u^{\mu}(0,x)=u_{0}(x),\quad\frac{\partial u^{\mu}}{\partial t}(0,x)=v_{0}(x),\quad x\in D,

with
\mu>0
. The equation models a material with elasticity, experiencing friction, as well as deterministic and random forcing. The
-\frac{\partial u^{\mu}}{\partial t}
term models elasticity and
\mu>0
is the mass density.

We will also consider in parallel the stochastic wave equation without damping, formulated as

(1.2)

\displaystyle\mu\frac{\partial^{2}u^{\mu}}{\partial t^{2}}=\Delta u^{\mu}(t,x)+b(t,x,u^{\mu}(t,x))+g(t,x,u^{\mu}(t,x))dW(t,x),

\displaystyle u^{\mu}(t,x)=0,\quad x\in\partial D,

\displaystyle u^{\mu}(0,x)=u_{0}(x),\quad\frac{\partial u^{\mu}}{\partial t}(0,x)=v_{0}(x),\quad x\in D.

When the coefficients
b
and
g
are Lipschitz continuous in
u^{\mu}
, these SPDEs have been well studied. The well-posedness of a strong solution follows from a fixed point theorem, and as
\mu\to 0
, the damped stochastic wave equation (
1.1
) converges to the corresponding stochastic heat equation. Motivated by finite dimensional Langevin systems, it is natural to ask whether we can deduce the same results when the coefficients
b
and
g
are not locally Lipschitz continuous. We will give a positive answer in this paper.

In this paper, we will work with equations (
1.1
) and (
1.2
) in their abstract forms, formulated as evolution equations on an infinite dimensional, separable Hilbert space
H
. In the literature, (
1.1
) and (
1.2
) are usually called random field solutions of SPDEs, which can be regraded as particular examples as abstract evolution equations on Hilbert space. Our abstract treatment not only makes the proof clearer but also shows the result has much wider applicability. We will rewrite (
1.1
) and (
1.2
) as the following evolution equation defined on
H\times H^{-1}:
(in the setting of (
1.1
), (
1.2
) we take
H=L^{2}([0,1];\mathbb{R})
, and for a general separable Hilbert space
H

)

(1.3)

\mu\frac{\partial^{2}u_{\mu}(t)}{\partial t^{2}}=Au_{\mu}(t)-\zeta\frac{\partial u_{\mu}(t)}{\partial t}+B(t,u_{\mu}(t))+G(t,u_{\mu}(t))\frac{dW_{t}}{dt},

where

In this paper we are interested in the well-posedness issue of (

This paper provides, to our best knowledge, currently the most general results on stochastic wave equation with irregular coefficients. We cover irregular drift and diffusion coefficients, and irregularity in the diffusion coefficient has never been considered before in this context. We work on a separable Hilbert space so that random field solutions are contained as special cases but our theory is much more general. We provide the first proof of Smoluchowski-Kramers approximation for an infinite dimensional system with merely Hölder coefficients. Due to the complexity of the wave equation semigroup (which is not analytic, for example), even the weak existence part of Theorem
1.2
seems novel and is not covered by standard methods (see Section
3.1
for discussions). Before we discuss the method and plan of this paper, we first give a brief literature review of related results.

1.1.
Related literature

1.1.1.
Works on regularization by noise

In the broadest scope, this work contributes to investigating the regularization by noise phenomenon for stochastic differential equations with irregular coefficients. The simplest case one may think of is a finite dimensional SDE

dX_{t}=b(X_{t})dt+\sigma(X_{t})dW_{t}

defined on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
When
\sigma=I
is the identity and
b
is bounded measurable, one can construct a unique weak solution via Girsanov transform, and prove strong uniqueness via Zvonkin’s transform
[
38
]
. Extensions to unbounded, time dependent coefficients can be found in
[
27
]
. Concerning an irregular diffusion coefficient
\sigma
, when
\sigma
is non-degenerate and continuous, one can always construct a unique weak solution via representing the solution as a time changed Brownian motion (see
[
35
]
, Section 6.5), and under some extra Sobolev regularity assumptions on
\sigma
one can show the solution is strong. The two claims concerning
\sigma

are available primarily because we work in finite dimensions, so that the solution to the SDE without drift is a time-changed Brownian motion, and the analytical theory of parabolic PDEs can be applied to derive quantitative estimates.

For an infinite dimensional stochastic system, consider the stochastic heat equation

(1.14)

dX_{t}=AX_{t}dt+b(X_{t})dt+\sigma(X_{t})dW_{t}

where we may as well take
A
the Laplacian on
[0,1]
as a motivating example. There has been much recent investigation of well-posedness with an irregular drift
b
in the case of additive noise
\sigma=I
: pathwise uniqueness is proved for Hölder continuous drifts
b
in
[
10
]
in the context of evolution equations on Hilbert space, and for bounded measurable drifts
[
22
]
and distributional drifts
[
1
]
in the context of random field solutions (but not for evolution equations on infinite dimensional Hilbert space). The investigation of an irregular diffusion coefficient in the infinite dimensional setting turns out to be much more difficult. Notable technical challenges appear in infinite dimensions, as we may no longer represent the solution as a time changed Brownian motion and no analytical PDE theory is available.
In
[
30
]
the authors made a great achievement in proving pathwise uniqueness of random field solution to the stochastic heat equation when the drift
b
is Lipschitz and diffusion coefficient
\sigma
is
\frac{3}{4}+\epsilon
-Hölder continuous in the function argument. The proof is fairly technical and does not seem to extend to Hilbert space valued abstract SPDEs. Recently in
[
24
]
the author introduced a direct method to prove uniqueness of martingale solutions of (
1.14
) with irregular
\sigma
and
b
for (
1.14
) defined on infinite dimensional separable Hilbert space, allowing an additional drift
(-A)^{1/2}F(X_{t})
and studied the long time behavior of solutions.

For the stochastic wave equation (
1.1
), there are a few papers considering irregular drift coefficients and additive white noise, see for example
[
29
]
, and papers on a related problem of martingale solutions with reflection, see
[
25
]
. Very recently, in
[
2
]
the authors proved pathwise uniqueness and Malliavin differentiability for the random field solution to the hyperbolic SPDE
X_{s,t},(s,t)\in[0,T]^{2}:

(1.15)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}X(s,t)}{\partial s\partial t}=b(s,t,X(s,t))+\frac{\partial^{2}W(s,t)}{\partial s\partial t},\quad s,t\in[0,T]^{2},

\displaystyle X(s,0)=X(0,t)=x

where
W_{s,t}
is the Wiener sheet and
b:[0,T]^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is bounded measurable. Their method is based on an adaptation of Davie’s pathwise argument
[
13
]
and does not extend to the case of an irregular diffusion coefficient
\sigma
. Moreover, the stochastic wave equation with a damping term
\partial_{t}u
is not covered by the results in
[
2
]
, as they consider the SDE (
1.15
) and then recover the wave equation via a
\frac{\pi}{4}
rotation. Thus Theorem
1.2
in this paper is somewhat orthogonal to
[
2
]
: we consider (damped) semilinear stochastic wave equation with irregular noise coefficients and solve SPDEs on an infinite dimensional Hilbert space, which has much wider generality and contains the random field SPDEs as special examples. Due to this infinite dimensional feature, establishing pathwise uniqueness is particularly difficult (for the stochastic wave equation with additive noise, pathwise uniqueness of the Hilbert space valued abstract SPDE is currently only established when the drift
B
is
\beta>\frac{2}{3}
-Hölder continuous, see
[
29
]
). Meanwhile, weak well-posedness and convergence in distribution are already quite useful in many applications, so we do not discuss the proof of pathwise uniqueness in this paper.

1.1.2.
Works on stochastic wave equation

This paper is the first, to our best knowledge, to consider stochastic wave equations with non-Lipschitz noise coefficients. Earlier studies on the stochastic wave equation focus on Lipschitz continuous

[

[

[

1.1.3.
Works on Smoluchowski-Kramers approximation

The second component of this paper, Theorem
1.5
, concerns the small noise limit of stochastic wave equations. This phenomenon was first studied in finite dimensions for the Langevin equation in
[
26
]
and
[
34
]
and is then called the Smoluchowski-Kramers approximation. More results on the convergence in finite dimensions can be found in
[

]

[
5
]
,
[
6
]
, and later extended in
[
33
]
to show that the approximation works for multiplicative noise in any spatial dimension provided that the covariance of the noise is suitably chosen. Some more recent investigations of Smoluchowski-Kramers approximation can be found in
[

]

[
7
]
,
[
8
]

[
37
]
the authors proved Smoluchowski-Kramers approximation for 2D stochastic wave equation with additive space-time white noise and polynomial and sine nonlinearities.

In a recent work
[
36

the authors considered a finite dimensional system with Hölder continuous drift and non-degenerate noise, and proved Smoluchowski-Kramers approximation via an application of Zvonkin’s transform. In this paper Theorem

we show that Smoluchowski-Kramers approximation is also valid for irregular coefficients in infinite dimensions, widely extending the scope of the said topic to cases where analytical techniques are not available.

1.2.
Plan of this paper

The key technique that we will use to treat irregular coefficients is
a generalized coupling technique introduced in
[
28
]
in the setting of stochastic delay equations. This technique makes use of the non-degeneracy of noise coefficient
G
and a combination of both pathwise and probabilistic techniques. Suppose we want to measure the distance between two solutions with same initial condition but different coefficients,

(1.16)

\mu\frac{\partial^{2}u(t)}{\partial t^{2}}=Au(t)-\zeta\frac{\partial u(t)}{\partial t}+G(t,u(t))\frac{dW_{t}}{dt},

(1.17)

\mu\frac{\partial^{2}u^{n}(t)}{\partial t^{2}}=Au^{n}(t)-\zeta\frac{\partial u^{n}(t)}{\partial t}+G^{n}(t,u^{n}(t))\frac{dW_{t}}{dt},

we introduce a control parameter
\lambda
, a stopping time
\tau
and consider the controlled process

(1.18)

\mu\frac{\partial^{2}\widetilde{u}^{n}(t)}{\partial t^{2}}=A\widetilde{u}^{n}(t)-\zeta\frac{\partial\widetilde{u}^{n}(t)}{\partial t}+\lambda(u(t)-\widetilde{u}^{n}(t))1_{t\leq\tau}+G^{n}(t,\widetilde{u}^{n}(t))\frac{dW_{t}}{dt},

In the probabilistic step we estimate the total variation distance between
u^{n}|_{[0,T]}
and
\widetilde{u}^{n}|_{[0,T]}
via Girsanov transform and relative entropy computations, which is standard. In the analytic step we compare the distance between
u(t)
and
\widetilde{u}^{n}(t)
via pathwise estimates. The pathwise estimate is somewhat more involved in infinite dimensions. In the parabolic literature
[
24
]
this estimate was achieved via a careful use of the stochastic factorization theorem. In the hyperbolic case of this paper, we further develop the ideas in
[
24
]
, combining more refined estimates of second order semigroups in
[
33
]
. The proof of Theorem
1.5
goes through similar lines, yet a further refined semigroup analysis is needed to make the leading numerical constants independent of the small parameter
\mu
.

This article is organised as follows. In Section
2
we develop the necessary functional analytic tools and maximal inequality estimates for stochastic integrals. In section
3
we prove existence and uniqueness of a weak-mild solutions, Theorem
1.2
. In Section
4
we prove the small mass limit, Theorem
1.5
.

2.
Stochastic estimates of the semigroup

We now summarize the functional analytic background of this paper, and give a precise definition to the solutions of the (damped) stochastic wave equation.

2.1.
Functional analytic preliminaries

For any
\delta\in\mathbb{R}
,
define the interpolation spaces
H^{\delta}
(see for example
[
23
]
, Section 4.4) as the completion of the vector space
\operatorname{Vect}(e_{1},e_{2},\cdots)
of finite linear sums of
e_{k},k\in\mathbb{N}
with respect to the norm

|f|_{H^{\delta}}^{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{k}^{\delta}\langle f,e_{k}\rangle_{H}^{2},

and define the phase space
\mathcal{H}_{\delta}:=H^{\delta}\times H^{\delta-1}
. We will mostly consider
\mathcal{H}:=\mathcal{H}_{0}
.

To make the notations more compact, we keep the undetermined constant
\zeta
that take two values: 0 and 1. The choice
\zeta=1
corresponds to the damped stochastic wave equation, and the choice
\zeta=0
corresponds to the one without damping. We define a linear operator, for both
\zeta=0
and
\zeta=1
:

\mathcal{A}^{\lambda}_{\mu}:D(\mathcal{A}_{\mu}^{\lambda}):=\mathcal{H}_{\delta+1}\to\mathcal{H}_{\delta}
via

(2.1)

\mathcal{A}_{\mu}^{\lambda}(u,v)=(v,(A-\lambda)u/\mu-\zeta v/\mu).

In the following discussions, whenever
\lambda
is omitted in the notation, it means that we take
\lambda=0
.

We give some clarifications for the constants
\lambda,\mu,\zeta
: the constant
\lambda
is an intermediate parameter and we will set it very large in the proof. It does not appear in the final statements of our theorems. The constant
\mu
is the parameter that we will tune to zero in the small mass limit. The value of
\mu\neq 0
is irrelevant when we prove the well-posedness of the wave equation. In the proof of well-posedness, we will consider both cases
\zeta=0,1
, while in the proof of small mass limit only the case
\zeta=1
matters.

Now we denote by
S_{\mu}^{\lambda}(t):\mathcal{H}_{\delta}\to\mathcal{H}_{\delta}
the
C_{0}
semigroup generated by
\mathcal{A}_{\mu}^{\lambda}
. This semigroup is continuous but not analytic, thus constituting the major analytical difficulty of this paper.

Now define
\Pi_{1}:\mathcal{H}_{\delta}\to H^{\delta}
to be the projection to the first component and
\Pi_{2}:\mathcal{H}_{\delta}\to H^{\delta-1}
projection to the second component. Now set
\mathcal{I}_{\mu}:H^{\delta}\to H_{\delta}
via

(2.2)

\mathcal{I}_{\mu}u=(0,u/\mu).

Then for each
\lambda\geq 0,
the solution to the abstract stochastic wave equation with
\zeta=0,1

(2.3)

\mu\frac{\partial^{2}u_{\mu}^{\lambda}(t)}{\partial t^{2}}=(A-\lambda)u_{\mu}^{\lambda}(t)-\zeta\frac{\partial u_{\mu}^{\lambda}(t)}{\partial t}+B(t,u_{\mu}^{\lambda}(t))+G(t,u_{\mu}^{\lambda}(t))\frac{dW_{t}}{dt},

can be formulated into the following abstract evolution equation. Denote the two coordinates by
Z_{\mu}^{\lambda}(t)=(u_{\mu}^{\lambda}(t),v_{\mu}^{\lambda}(t))
with initial value
Z_{\mu}^{\lambda}(0)=Z_{0}=(u_{0},v_{0})\in\mathcal{H}_{1}
, we can rewrite (
2.3
) as

(2.4)

dZ_{\mu}^{\lambda}(t)=\mathcal{A}_{\mu}^{\lambda}Z_{\mu}^{\lambda}(t)dt+\mathcal{I}_{\mu}B(t,\Pi_{1}Z_{\mu}^{\lambda}(t))dt+\mathcal{I}_{\mu}G(t,\Pi_{1}Z_{\mu}^{\lambda}(t))dW_{t}.

The mild solutions to the stochastic wave equations are defined as follows:

Definition 2.1
.

The mild solution to the abstract stochastic wave equation (
1.3
) is defined as the adapted process
Z_{\mu}
on
\mathcal{H}_{0}
that satisfies, for each
t>0
,

(2.5)

\displaystyle Z_{\mu}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{S}_{\mu}(t)z_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\Pi_{1}\mathcal{S}_{\mu}(t-s)\mathcal{I}_{\mu}B(s,\Pi_{1}Z_{\mu}(s))ds

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}\Pi_{1}\mathcal{S}_{\mu}(t-s)\mathcal{I}_{\mu}G(s,\Pi_{1}Z_{\mu}(s))dW_{s}.

More generally, the mild solution to the abstract stochastic wave equation (
2.3
) is defined as the adapted process
Z_{\mu}^{\lambda}
on
\mathcal{H}_{0}
that satisfies, for each
t>0
,

(2.6)

\displaystyle Z_{\mu}^{\lambda}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{S}_{\mu}^{\lambda}(t)z_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\Pi_{1}\mathcal{S}_{\mu}^{\lambda}(t-s)\mathcal{I}_{\mu}B(s,\Pi_{1}Z_{\mu}^{\lambda}(s))ds

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}\Pi_{1}\mathcal{S}_{\mu}^{\lambda}(t-s)\mathcal{I}_{\mu}G(s,\Pi_{1}Z_{\mu}^{\lambda}(s))dW_{s}.

The mild solution to the stochastic heat equation (
1.11
) is defined as the adapted process
u(t)
on
H
that satisfies, for each
t>0
,

(2.7)

u(t)=\mathcal{S}(t)u_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{S}(t-s)B(s,u(s))ds+\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{S}(t-s)G(s,u(s))dW_{s}.

Since
A-\lambda
has eigenvectors
\{e_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
for each
\lambda\geq 0
, the operator
\mathcal{A}_{\mu}^{\lambda}
leaves invariant the linear subspace of
\mathcal{H}
with the form
\{(u_{k}e_{k},v_{k}e_{k}):u_{k},v_{k}\in\mathbb{R}\}
.
Given
u\in H,v\in H^{-1}
, define
u_{k}=\langle u,e_{k}\rangle_{H}
and
v_{k}=\langle v,e_{k}\rangle_{H^{-1}}
, and consider

f_{\mu}^{\lambda}(k)(t,u_{k},v_{k}):=\langle e_{k},\Pi_{1}\mathcal{S}_{\mu}^{\lambda}(t)(u_{k}e_{k},v_{k}e_{k})\rangle_{H}.

Then
f_{\mu}^{\lambda}(k)(t,u_{k},v_{k})
solves, in the case
\zeta=1
,

(2.8)

\mu{f_{\mu}^{\lambda}(k)}^{\prime\prime}+{f_{\mu}^{\lambda}(k)}^{\prime}+(\alpha_{k}+\lambda)f_{\mu}^{\lambda}(k)=0,\quad f_{\mu}^{\lambda}(k)(0)=u_{k},f_{\mu}^{\lambda}(k)^{\prime}(0)=v_{k},

and solves, in the case
\zeta=0,

(2.9)

\mu{f_{\mu}^{\lambda}(k)}^{\prime\prime}+(\alpha_{k}+\lambda)f_{\mu}^{\lambda}(k)=0,\quad f_{\mu}^{\lambda}(k)(0)=u_{k},f_{\mu}^{\lambda}(k)^{\prime}(0)=v_{k}.

We will use the following estimate from
[
33
]
:

Lemma 2.2
.

Assume
f_{\mu}^{\lambda}(k)(t,u,v)
is a solution to (
2.8
). Assume
\zeta=1.
Then

•

assume
u=0
and
1-4\mu(\alpha_{k}+\lambda)\geq 0
. Then

(2.10)

|f_{\mu}^{\lambda}(k)(t,0,v)|\leq 4\mu|v|e^{-(\alpha_{k}+\lambda)t},

and

(2.11)

|f_{\mu}^{\lambda}(k)^{\prime}(t,0,v)|\leq 2|v|e^{-(\alpha_{k}+\lambda)t}.

•

Assume
u=0
and
1-4\mu(\alpha_{k}+\lambda)\leq 0
, then

(2.12)

|f_{\mu}^{\lambda}(k)(t,0,v)|\leq\frac{\sqrt{4\mu}|v|}{\sqrt{\alpha_{k}+\lambda}}e^{-\frac{t}{4\mu}}

and

(2.13)

|f_{\mu}^{\lambda}(k)^{\prime}(t,0,v)|\leq 2|v|e^{-\frac{t}{4\mu}},

•

In general, for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
\mu>0
and arbitrary
u,v\in\mathbb{R}
,

(2.14)

\mu|f_{\mu}^{\lambda}(k)^{\prime}(t,u,v)|^{2}+(\alpha_{k}+\lambda)|f_{\mu}^{\lambda}(k)(t,u,v)|^{2}\leq\mu|v|^{2}+(\alpha_{k}+\lambda)|u|^{2}.

We have the following
\zeta=0
version of the above estimate:

Lemma 2.3
.

Assume
f_{\mu}^{\lambda}(k)(t,u,v)
is a solution to (
2.9
). Assume
\zeta=0.
Then

(2.15)

|f_{\mu}^{\lambda}(k)(t,0,v)|\leq\frac{\sqrt{\mu}|v|}{\sqrt{\alpha_{k}+\lambda}}

and

(2.16)

|f_{\mu}^{\lambda}(k)^{\prime}(t,0,v)|\leq|v|.

Proof.

The solutions to (
2.9
) are explicitly given by

(2.17)

f_{\mu}^{\lambda}(k)(t,u,v)=u\cos(\sqrt{\frac{\alpha_{k}+\lambda}{\mu}}t)+v\sin(\sqrt{\frac{\alpha_{k}+\lambda}{\mu}}t)\sqrt{\frac{\mu}{\alpha_{k}+\lambda}}.

∎

Now we outline some simple consequences of these estimates.

Lemma 2.4
.

For any
t\geq 0
,
\mu>0
,
\lambda\geq 0
and
\zeta=\{0,1\}
, we have that

(2.18)

\|\Pi_{1}\mathcal{S}_{\mu}^{\lambda}(t)\mathcal{I}_{\mu}\|_{\mathcal{L}(H)}\leq 4,\quad\zeta=1,

and

(2.19)

\|\Pi_{1}\mathcal{S}_{\mu}^{\lambda}(t)\mathcal{I}_{\mu}\|_{\mathcal{L}(H)}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_{1}\mu}},\quad\zeta=0,

Proof.

The case
\zeta=1
and

is proved in

]
, Lemma 5.3. By definition,

\Pi_{1}\mathcal{S}_{\mu}^{\lambda}(t)\mathcal{I}_{\mu}e_{k}=f_{\mu}^{\lambda}(k)(t,0,1/\mu)e_{k}.

As

forms an orthogonal basis of

,

\|\Pi_{1}\mathcal{S}_{\mu}^{\lambda}(t)\mathcal{I}_{\mu}\|_{\mathcal{L}(H)}\leq\sup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}|f_{\mu}^{\lambda}(k)(t,0,1/\mu)|.

Assume
\zeta=1
, for
k
with

use (
2.10
) , and for the case

use (

). Assume
\zeta=0

∎

Lemma 2.5
.

For any
\mu>0
,
t\geq 0
,
\zeta=\{0,1\}
and

,

(2.20)

\|\Pi_{1}\mathcal{S}_{\mu}^{\lambda}(t)\begin{pmatrix}I\\

Proof.

In both cases

, we have

In the case

this term is less than or equal to 1 thanks to (
2.14
). In the case
\zeta=0
this is less than or equal to 1 thanks to (

).
∎

Lemma 2.6
.

In the case
\zeta=1
,
consider
N_{\mu}^{\lambda}=\max\{k\in\mathbb{N}:1-4\mu(\alpha_{k}+\lambda)\geq 0\}

We also have the following

and

,

(2.24)

\|\Pi_{1}\mathcal{S}_{\mu}^{\lambda}(t)\begin{pmatrix}0\\
I\end{pmatrix}\|_{\mathcal{L}(H^{-1},H)}\leq\sqrt{\mu},\quad\zeta=0.

Proof.

The first two claims are proved in Lemma 5.5 and 5.6 of

∎

Lemma 2.7
.

For any
\mu\in(0,1)
, we have

(2.25)

\|\mathcal{S}_{\mu}^{\lambda}(t)\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})}\leq\mu^{-1/2}\sup_{k}\sqrt{\frac{\alpha_{k}+\lambda}{\alpha_{k}}},\quad\zeta=0,1.

Proof.

The result is proved for
\zeta=1
and
\lambda=0
in

By definition of

, for

\mu|\mathcal{S}_{\mu}^{\lambda}(t)(u,v)\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\leq\mu\|\Pi_{2}\mathcal{S}_{\mu}^{\lambda}(t)(u,v)|_{H^{-1}}^{2}+|\Pi_{1}\mathcal{S}_{\mu}^{\lambda}(t)(u,v)|_{H}^{2}.

From the Fourier decomposition it suffices to bound

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{\mu}{\alpha_{k}}|f_{\mu}^{\lambda}(k)^{\prime}(t,u_{k},v_{k})|^{2}+|f_{\mu}^{\lambda}(k)(t,u_{k},v_{k})|^{2}\right).

First consider the case

Then consider the case
\zeta=1
. It suffices to use (

∎

2.2.
Estimates in shifted norm

As will be made clear afterwards, for general
\lambda>0
it is more convenient to re-define the norm of the interpolation space
H^{\delta}
. Now we define, for each fixed
\lambda>0
,
H^{\delta}(\lambda)
as the completion of the vector space
\operatorname{Vect}(e_{1},\cdots,e_{k},\cdots)
with respect to the norm

|f|_{H^{\delta}(\lambda)}^{2}:=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(\alpha_{k}+\lambda)^{\delta}\langle f,e_{k}\rangle_{H}^{2}.

This new norm of
H^{\delta}(\lambda)
is equivalent to the norm of
H^{\delta}
for any
\lambda>0
, but it will be more convenient to use.

We now consider the phase space

\mathcal{H}(\lambda):=H^{0}(\lambda)\times H^{-1}(\lambda)=H\times H^{-1}(\lambda).

Note that
H=H^{0}(\lambda)
so the norm in the first component of
\mathcal{H}
is the same as that of
\mathcal{H}(\lambda)
. Now we restate many of our previous estimates in the new norms
H^{-1}(\lambda)
and
\mathcal{H}_{\lambda}
:

Lemma 2.8
.

With the same notation as Lemma
2.6
and
2.7
, we have

(2.26)

\|\Pi_{1}\mathcal{S}_{\mu}^{\lambda}(t)\begin{pmatrix}0\\
I-P_{N_{\mu}^{\lambda}}\end{pmatrix}\|_{\mathcal{L}(H^{-1}(\lambda),H)}\leq\sqrt{4\mu},\quad\zeta=1.

and

(2.27)

\|\Pi_{1}\mathcal{S}_{\mu}^{\lambda}(t)\begin{pmatrix}0\\
I\end{pmatrix}\|_{\mathcal{L}(H^{-1}(\lambda),H)}\leq\sqrt{\mu},\quad\zeta=0.

Moreover, for any
\mu\in(0,1),
we have

(2.28)

\|\mathcal{S}_{\mu}^{\lambda}(t)\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}(\lambda))}\leq\mu^{-1/2},\quad\zeta=0,1.

Proof.

The proof is exactly the same. Thanks to (
2.12
) and (
2.15
), we have

\sup_{k>N_{\mu}^{\lambda}}\sqrt{\alpha_{k}+\lambda}\left|f_{\mu}^{\lambda}(k)(t,0,1)\right|\leq\sqrt{4\mu},\quad\zeta=1

and

\sup_{k\geq 1}\sqrt{\alpha_{k}+\lambda}\left|f_{\mu}^{\lambda}(k)(t,0,1)\right|\leq\sqrt{\mu},\quad\zeta=0.

The last claim follows again from (
2.14
) when
\zeta=1
and (
2.17
) when
\zeta=0,
while the fact that we have
\alpha_{k}+\lambda
instead of
\alpha_{k}
in the definition of the norm of
\mathcal{H}(\lambda)
allows us to get rid of the
\sup_{k}\sqrt{\frac{\alpha_{k}+\lambda}{\alpha_{k}}}
factor in (
2.25
).
∎

2.3.
Maximal inequalities of stochastic integrals

In this paper we will extensively use maximal inequalities of stochastic integrals of the following form

(2.29)

\Gamma^{\mu,\lambda}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{S}_{\mu}^{\lambda}(t-s)\mathcal{I}_{\mu}\Phi(s)dW_{s}

for some adapted, self adjoint process
\Phi\in L^{p}(\Omega;L^{\infty}([0,T];\mathcal{L}(H_{0},H)))
. When we apply the estimates related to
\Gamma^{\mu,\lambda}
, we will often take
\Phi(s)=G(s,u(s))
or
\Phi(s)=G(s,u(s))-G^{n}(s,u^{n}(s))
for some approximations
G^{n}
of
G
. By assumption (
1.7
) and our approximating procedure, these choices will make
\Phi(s)
a self-adjoint adapted processes. In the rest of this paper we will assume self adjointness of
\Phi
without mentioning.

We will use the factorization formula (
[
11
]
,Section 5.3.1) for stochastic convolutions which gives, for
0<\alpha<1
(which we will later choose to be sufficiently small),

(2.30)

\Gamma^{\mu,\lambda}(t)=\frac{\sin(\alpha\pi)}{\pi}\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{\alpha-1}\mathcal{S}_{\mu}^{\lambda}(t-s)\Gamma_{\alpha}^{\mu,\lambda}(s)ds,

where

(2.31)

\Gamma_{\alpha}^{\mu,\lambda}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{-\alpha}\mathcal{S}_{\mu}^{\lambda}(t-s)\mathcal{I}_{\mu}\Phi(s)dW_{s}.

We first prove maximal inequality estimates for
\Gamma_{\alpha}^{\mu,\lambda}
. The proof is similar to
[
33
]
, Lemma 6.1 but we make a better use of the summability of
\sum_{n}\frac{1}{\lambda_{n}^{\sigma}}
,
\sigma<1
, and our result can be re-interpreted as a maximal inequality of Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process when
\lambda
is very large.

Notation 2.9
.

We abbreviate, for any
T>0
,

\|\Phi\|_{T}:=\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}|\Phi(t)|_{\mathcal{L}(H_{0},H)}.

Proposition 2.10
.

Fix
T>0
.
Assume that there is some
\eta_{0}\in(0,1)
such that

(2.32)

\sum_{n}\frac{1}{\alpha_{n}^{1-\eta}}<\infty

for each
\eta\in(0,\eta_{0})
. Then for each
p\geq 2
and each
\eta\in(0,\eta_{0}),
for both
\zeta=0
and
\zeta=1,

(2.33)

\mathbb{E}[|\Pi_{1}\Gamma_{\alpha}^{\mu,\lambda}(t)\|_{H}^{p}]\leq C_{\eta,p,\mu}\|\Phi\|_{t}^{p}\lambda^{-\frac{\eta p}{2}},\quad t\in[0,T].

The constant
c_{\eta,p,\mu}<\infty
depends on
\eta
,
p
,
\mu
and
T
.

Proof.

Applying the Burkholder-Davis-Gundy inequality to
\Gamma_{\alpha}^{\mu,\lambda}
:

(2.34)

\mathbb{E}[\|\Pi_{1}\Gamma_{\alpha}^{\mu,\lambda}(t)\|_{H}^{p}]\leq C\mathbb{E}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{-2\alpha}|\Pi_{1}\mathcal{S}_{\mu}^{\lambda}(t-s)\mathcal{I}_{\mu}\Phi(s)e_{j}|_{H}^{2}ds\right)^{p/2}.

The quadratic variation

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\alpha}^{\mu,\lambda}(t):

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{-2\alpha}|\Pi_{1}\mathcal{S}_{\mu}^{\lambda}(t-s)\mathcal{I}_{\mu}\Phi(s)e_{j}|_{H}^{2}ds

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{-2\alpha}\langle\Pi_{1}\mathcal{S}_{\mu}^{\lambda}(t-s)\mathcal{I}_{\mu}\Phi(s)e_{j},e_{k}\rangle_{H}^{2}ds

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{-2\alpha}\langle\Phi(s)e_{j},\mathcal{I}_{\mu}^{*}S_{\mu}^{\lambda*}(t-s)\Pi_{1}^{*}e_{k}\rangle_{H}^{2}ds

Since

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{I}_{\mu}^{*}\mathcal{S}_{\mu}^{\lambda*}(t)\Pi_{1}^{*}e_{k},e_{j}\rangle_{H}

\displaystyle=\langle\Pi_{1}\mathcal{S}_{\mu}^{\lambda}(t)\mathcal{I}_{\mu}e_{j},e_{k}\rangle_{H}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}f_{\mu}^{\lambda}(k)(t,0,1/\mu)\quad j=k\\
0\quad j\neq k,\end{cases}

we further simplify
\Lambda_{\alpha}^{\mu,\lambda}(t)
as, abbreviating
f_{\mu}^{\lambda}(k)(t):=f_{\mu}^{\lambda}(k)(t,0,1/\mu)
,

\Lambda_{\alpha}^{\mu,\lambda}(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{-2\alpha}(f_{\mu}^{\lambda}(k)(t-s))^{2}\langle\Phi(s)e_{j},e_{k}\rangle_{H}^{2}ds.

By self-adjointness of
\Phi
we have, using
e_{k}\in H_{0}
with
|e_{k}|_{H_{0}}\leq M
,

\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\langle\Phi(s)e_{j},e_{k}\rangle_{H}^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\langle\Phi(s)e_{k},e_{j}\rangle_{H}^{2}=|\Phi(s)e_{k}|_{H}^{2}\leq M|\Phi(s)|^{2}_{\mathcal{L}(H_{0},H)}.

Thus we bound
\Lambda_{\alpha}^{\mu,\lambda}(t)
as follows:

(2.35)

\Lambda_{\alpha}^{\mu,\lambda}(t)\leq\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{-2\alpha}\|\Phi(s)\|_{t}^{2}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}f_{\mu}^{\lambda}(k)^{2}(t-s,0,1/\mu)\right)ds.

In the following we find effective upper bounds for

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}f_{\mu}^{\lambda}(k)^{2}(t,0,1/\mu).

For the case
\zeta=1
, thanks to (
2.10
), (
2.12
), this is bounded by

\sum_{k=1}^{N_{\mu}^{\lambda}}e^{-2(\alpha_{k}+\lambda)t}+\sum_{k=N_{\mu}^{\lambda}+1}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-\frac{t}{2\mu}}}{\mu(\alpha_{k}+\lambda)}:=J_{1}(t)+J_{2}(t).

For the case
\zeta=0
, thanks to (
2.15
), this is bounded by

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\mu(\alpha_{k}+\lambda)}:=J_{3}.

For any
\eta\in(0,1)
, by Young’s inequality we may find
c_{\eta}>0
such that

(2.36)

\alpha_{k}+\lambda\geq c_{\eta}{\alpha_{k}}^{1-\eta}\lambda^{\eta}.

Thus for any
\eta\in(0,\eta_{0})
, by (
2.32
) we may find
c_{\eta}>0
such that

(2.37)

J_{3}\leq\frac{c_{\eta}}{\mu}\lambda^{-\eta},\quad J_{2}(t)\leq\frac{c_{\eta}}{\mu}\lambda^{-\eta}e^{-\frac{t}{2\mu}}.

In the case
\zeta=0
, the proof is already complete by plugging the bound of
J_{3}
into (
2.35
).

In the following we focus on the case
\zeta=1
. We first compute the stochastic integral relating to
J_{1}(t)
. The analysis of
J_{1}(t)
is similar to that of the heat semigroup in
[
24
]
. For any
r<1
we can find positive constants
c_{r}
,
c_{r,\eta}
such that, by using (
2.36
) in the second inequality,

(2.38)

e^{-2(\alpha_{k}+\lambda)t}\leq c_{r}\frac{1}{((\alpha_{k}+\lambda)t)^{r}}\leq c_{r,\eta}\frac{1}{\alpha_{k}^{(1-\eta)r}}\lambda^{-\eta r}\frac{1}{t^{r}}.

Since

(2.39)

\int_{0}^{t}s^{-2\alpha-r}ds<\infty

by choosing
\alpha>0
sufficiently small followed by choosing
r<1
sufficiently close to 1, one can ensure that
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\alpha_{k}^{(1-\eta)r}}<\infty
for
r
sufficiently close to 1, and then
r\eta
, the exponent of
\lambda^{-1},
can achieve any value in

3.5.
Alternative proof of existence and uniqueness assuming uniform approximation

In this section we give an alternative, shorter proof of weak existence and uniqueness under the following stronger assumption

\mathbf{H}7
: There exists a sequence of mappings

that satisfy assumptions
\mathbf{H}3,\mathbf{H}4,\mathbf{H}5
with uniform in

is Lipschitz in

with Lipschitz constant uniform in

\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{t>0}\sup_{x\in H}|G^{n}(t,x)-G(t,x)|_{\mathcal{L}(H_{0},H)}=0.

Though assumption
\mathbf{H}7
looks much stronger, it indeed covers many interesting examples of random field solutions to the stochastic wave equation. Say we consider

and

for any

. Assume that for some

we have

so the only place the noise coefficient

is non-Lipschitz is at

. We can find a sequence of
g_{n}
approximating

, each

being Lipschitz continuous in

, and

(3.22)

\sup_{t\geq 0}\sup_{u\in\mathbb{R}}|g_{n}(t,u)-g(t,u)|\to 0,\quad n\to\infty.

Defining

for any

, so that the approximating sequence

satisfies
\mathbf{H}7
. More generally, one could consider diffusion coefficients

such that we can find a sequence

uniformly approximating

We follow roughly the same steps as in the previous section: we may assume

\mu\frac{\partial^{2}u(t)}{\partial t^{2}}=Au(t)-\zeta\frac{\partial u(t)}{\partial t}+G(t,u(t))\frac{dW_{t}}{dt},

(3.24)

\mu\frac{\partial^{2}u^{n}(t)}{\partial t^{2}}=Au^{n}(t)-\zeta\frac{\partial u^{n}(t)}{\partial t}+G^{n}(t,u^{n}(t))\frac{dW_{t}}{dt},

(3.25)

\mu\frac{\partial^{2}\widetilde{u}^{n}(t)}{\partial t^{2}}=A\widetilde{u}^{n}(t)-\zeta\frac{\partial\widetilde{u}^{n}(t)}{\partial t}+\lambda(u(t)-\widetilde{u}^{n}(t))1_{t\leq\tau}+G^{n}(t,\widetilde{u}^{n}(t))\frac{dW_{t}}{dt},

Under assumption
\mathbf{H}7
we no longer need to consider the compact subset

given in

Denote by

4.1.
Mass-independent estimates of stochastic integrals

In this section we only focus on the

In proving the small-mass limit of the damped stochastic wave equation, the maximal inequality estimate in Proposition (

All the estimates in this section are modifications to those in

In all the following estimates, the constant

with the same numerical constant

Proposition 4.1

Assume that there is some
\eta_{0}\in(0,1)

(4.1)

\sum_{n}\frac{1}{\alpha_{n}^{1-\eta}}<\infty,\quad\frac{1}{C}k^{\frac{1}{1-\eta_{0}}}\leq\alpha_{k}\leq Ck^{\frac{1}{1-\eta_{0}}}\quad\forall k\in\mathbb{N}_{+}

for each
\eta\in(0,\eta_{0})
. Then for each fixed
\lambda>0
we can find some

as a function of
\lambda
such that for any
\mu\in(0,\mu_{0}(\lambda)]
,
for each
p\geq 2
and each
\eta\in(0,\eta_{0}),

(4.2)

\mathbb{E}[|\Pi_{1}\Gamma_{\alpha}^{\mu,\lambda}(t)|_{H}^{p}]\leq C_{\eta,p}\mathbb{E}\|\Phi\|_{t}^{p}(\lambda^{-\frac{\eta p}{2}}+\mu^{(\eta_{0}-2\alpha)p/2}),\quad\text{ for any }t\in[0,T].

The constant
c_{\eta,p}
depends on
\eta

but does not depend on
\lambda
and
\mu

Proof.

Recall from the proof of Proposition

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\alpha}^{\mu,\lambda}(t):

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{-2\alpha}|\Pi_{1}\mathcal{S}_{\mu}^{\lambda}(t-s)\mathcal{I}_{\mu}\Phi(s)e_{j}|_{H}^{2}ds

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{-2\alpha}\|\Phi\|_{s}^{2}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}f_{\mu}^{\lambda}(k)^{2}(t-s,0,1/\mu)\right)ds,

and

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}f_{\mu}^{\lambda}(k)^{2}(t,0,1/\,u)\leq\sum_{k=1}^{N_{\mu}^{\lambda}}e^{-2(\alpha_{k}+\lambda)t}+\sum_{k=N_{\mu}^{\lambda}+1}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-\frac{t}{2\mu}}}{\mu(\alpha_{k}+\lambda)}:=J_{1}(t)+J_{2}(t).

and that

N_{\mu}^{\lambda}:=\max\{k\in\mathbb{N}:1-4\mu(\alpha_{k}+\lambda)\geq 0\},

we assume that for the fixed
\lambda>0
,
\mu>0
is chosen sufficiently small such that
\mu\lambda\leq\frac{1}{40}
, that is, we set
\mu_{0}(\lambda)=\frac{1}{40\lambda}
. Under this choice for
k>N_{\mu}^{\lambda}
one must have
\mu\alpha_{k}\geq\frac{9}{40}
and hence
\alpha_{k}\geq 9\lambda
for all

. Thus we have

J_{2}(t)\leq e^{-\frac{t}{2\mu}}\frac{9}{10}\sum_{k=N_{\mu}^{\lambda}+1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\mu\alpha_{k}}.

To bound this summation, we need to make use of the asymptotic growth of

. By the second assumption in (
4.1
),
we have, for some universal constant

and constant

depending only on

(4.3)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=N_{\mu}^{\lambda}+1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\mu\alpha_{k}}

\displaystyle\leq C\sum_{k=C^{-1}\mu^{-(1-\eta_{0})}}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\mu k^{\frac{1}{1-\eta_{0}}}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{\eta_{0}}\mu^{-1}\int_{C^{-1}\mu^{-(1-\eta_{0})}}^{\infty}x^{-\frac{1}{1-\eta_{0}}}dx\leq C_{\eta_{0}}\mu^{\eta_{0}-1}.

Note also the elementary calculation

(4.4)

\int_{0}^{\infty}s^{-2\alpha}e^{-\frac{s}{2\mu}}ds=(2\mu)^{1-2\alpha}\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{-2\alpha}e^{-t}dt=C\mu^{1-2\alpha},

so that

\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{-2\alpha}J_{2}(t-s)ds\leq\int_{0}^{t}s^{-2\alpha}e^{-\frac{s}{2\mu}}\mu^{\eta_{0}-1}ds\leq\mu^{\eta_{0}-2\alpha}.

For the term involving
J_{1}(t)
, we follow exactly the proof of Proposition
2.10
. Combining everything, we now have

\Lambda_{\alpha}^{\mu,\lambda}(t)\leq C\|\Phi\|_{t}^{2}(\lambda^{-\eta}+\mu^{\eta_{0}-2\alpha})

and then the Proposition follows from application of the BDG inequality.
