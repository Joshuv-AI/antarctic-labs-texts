Abstract
. Let 𝒞 {\mathcal{C}} be a small category. We investigate ringed sites ( 𝐂 , ℜ ) (\C,\mathfrak{R}) on 𝒞 {\mathcal{C}} and the resulting module categories 𝔐 ​ od − ⁡ ℜ \Mod\mathfrak{R} . When 𝒞 {\mathcal{C}} is finite, based on Grothendieck and Verdier’s classification of finite topoi, we prove that each 𝔐 ​ od − ⁡ ℜ \Mod\mathfrak{R} is equivalent to Mod- ⁡ ℜ | 𝒟 ​ [ 𝒟 ] \rMod\mathfrak{R}|_{{\mathcal{D}}}[{\mathcal{D}}] , where ℜ | 𝒟 ​ [ 𝒟 ] \mathfrak{R}|_{{\mathcal{D}}}[{\mathcal{D}}] is the skew category algebra, canonically defined on ( 𝐂 , ℜ ) (\C,\mathfrak{R}) , for a uniquely determined full subcategory 𝒟 ⊂ 𝒞 {\mathcal{D}}\subset{\mathcal{C}} and the restriction ℜ | 𝒟 \mathfrak{R}|_{{\mathcal{D}}} of ℜ \mathfrak{R} to 𝒟 {\mathcal{D}} .



1.
Introduction

Let
{\mathcal{C}}
be a small category. It is said to be
finite
if Mor
{\mathcal{C}}
is finite, and it is
object-finite
if
\mathop{\rm Ob}\nolimits{\mathcal{C}}
is finite. One may put a Grothendieck topology
{\mathcal{J}}
on
{\mathcal{C}}
to obtain a site
\C=({\mathcal{C}},{\mathcal{J}})
, which is said to be finite if the underlying category
{\mathcal{C}}
is. If
\mathfrak{R}
is a sheaf of rings on
\C
, one can consider the
right
modules on the ringed site
(\C,\mathfrak{R})
. They form an Abelian category, written as
\Mod\mathfrak{R}
, which is our major subject of investigation. In this paper, we mainly focus on finite categories, because all of their Grothendieck topologies, as well as the resulting topoi, are classified in
[
1
]
. The classification enables us to characterize module categories on ringed finite sites.

Our motivation comes from the representation theory of finite groups and finite-dimensional algebras. This is the main reason that we want to focus on sites rather than topoi. On the one hand, in group representations, one often looks into various finite categories associated to the group in question, and consider their representations (i.e. presheaves) and cohomology. Recent progresses showed that sheaves on these categories are interesting
[
4
,
15
]
. On the other hand, representations of quivers, posets and general small categories have been under intensive investigations, in both algebra and topology, see for instance
[
2
,
3
,
11
]
. This work might shed new light on these subjects.

Suppose
k
is a commutative ring with identity. We shall denote by
k
-Alg the category of unital associative algebras and unital
k
-algebra homomorphisms. Let
\mathfrak{R}
be a presheaf of unital
k
-algebras on
{\mathcal{C}}
. Motivated by the
Grothendieck construction

Gr_{{\mathcal{C}}}\mathfrak{R}
of
\mathfrak{R}
on
{\mathcal{C}}

[
6
, VI.8]
, we introduce the
skew category algebra

\mathfrak{R}[{\mathcal{C}}]
, as a “linearization” of
Gr_{{\mathcal{C}}}\mathfrak{R}
, which includes skew group algebras and category algebras as special cases. The right modules of
\mathfrak{R}[{\mathcal{C}}]
form the module category
{\rm Mod}\text{-}\mathfrak{R}[{\mathcal{C}}]
. When
{\mathcal{C}}
is a poset, the above construction has been studied by Gerstenhaber and Schack
[
5
]
, in disguise, as what they called a diagram ring
\mathfrak{R}!
.

We establish in Section 3 the following category equivalence.

Theorem A

(Theorem
3.5
)
.

Let
{\mathcal{C}}
be a small category and let
\mathfrak{R}:{\mathcal{C}}^{op}\to k\mbox{\rm-Alg}
be a presheaf of unital
k
-algebras on
\C
. If
\mathop{\rm Ob}\nolimits{\mathcal{C}}
is finite, then we have a category equivalence

\Mod\mathfrak{R}\simeq{\rm Mod\text{-}}\mathfrak{R}[{\mathcal{C}}].

One can continue to put a Grothendieck topology
{\mathcal{J}}
on
{\mathcal{C}}
. If it is the minimal topology
{\mathcal{J}}_{\min}
, then sheaves on
({\mathcal{C}},{\mathcal{J}}_{\min})
and presheaves on
{\mathcal{C}}
are the same. When we consider another topology
{\mathcal{J}}
on
{\mathcal{C}}
, we have to employ theories of sheaves and topoi. Under the circumstance, the sheaf category (a Grothendieck topos)
\mathop{\rm Sh}\nolimits({\mathcal{C}},{\mathcal{J}})
becomes a subtopos of the presheaf topos
\mathop{\rm PSh}\nolimits({\mathcal{C}})=\mathop{\rm Sh}\nolimits({\mathcal{C}},{\mathcal{J}}_{\min})
. Grothendieck and Verdier
[
1
, IV.9]
pointed out that
{\mathcal{J}}={\mathcal{J}}^{{\mathcal{D}}}
is uniquely determined by a strictly full Karoubian subcategory
{\mathcal{D}}
of
{\mathcal{C}}
. Moreover
\mathop{\rm Sh}\nolimits({\mathcal{C}},{\mathcal{J}}^{{\mathcal{D}}})\simeq\mathop{\rm PSh}\nolimits({\mathcal{D}})
. Here being Karoubian means that the category is idempotent-complete
[
9
]
or Cauchy-complete
[
7
]
. It allows us to deduce the following result, when
{\mathcal{C}}
is finite.

Theorem B

(Theorem
4.5
)
.

Let
\mathbf{C}=({\mathcal{C}},\mathcal{J})
be a finite site and
\mathfrak{R}:{\mathcal{C}}^{op}\to k\mbox{\rm-Alg}
be a sheaf of unital
k
-algebras on
\C
. Then
{\mathcal{J}}={\mathcal{J}}^{{\mathcal{D}}}
is determined by a strictly full subcategory
{\mathcal{D}}
, and there is a category equivalence

\Mod\mathfrak{R}\simeq\rMod\mathfrak{R}|_{{\mathcal{D}}}[{\mathcal{D}}],

where
\mathfrak{R}|_{{\mathcal{D}}}
is the restriction of
\mathfrak{R}
to
{\mathcal{D}}
.

If, for each
x\in\mathop{\rm Ob}\nolimits{\mathcal{D}}
,
\mathfrak{R}(x)
is Noetherian, then
\mathfrak{R}|_{{\mathcal{D}}}[{\mathcal{D}}]
is finite-dimensional and the coherent
\mathfrak{R}|_{{\mathcal{D}}}[{\mathcal{D}}]
-modules are the finitely generated ones.

Theorem C

(Corollary
4.6
)
.

Let
\mathbf{C}=({\mathcal{C}},\mathcal{J}^{{\mathcal{D}}})
be a finite site and
\mathfrak{R}:{\mathcal{C}}^{op}\to k\mbox{\rm-Alg}
be a sheaf of
k
-algebras on
\C
. If for each
x\in\mathop{\rm Ob}\nolimits{\mathcal{D}}
,
\mathfrak{R}(x)
is Noetherian, then there is a category equivalence

{\mathop{\rm coh}\nolimits}\text{-}\mathfrak{R}\simeq\rmod\mathfrak{R}|_{{\mathcal{D}}}[{\mathcal{D}}].

The paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we recall basics on Grothendieck topologies, topoi, ringed sites and their modules, and present some concrete examples. Then we characterize the modules on object-finite categories in Section 3. We recall Grothendieck and Verdier’s classification of finite topoi in Section 4, which allows us to reduce the characterizations of general module categories to the situation in the preceding section. Afterwards, we can present the category equivalences that we have stated above. We end in Section 5 with several remarks.

2.
Prelimenaries

In this section, we will recall the definitions of a Grothendieck topology, a sheaf, as well as the concepts of a ringed site, a ringed topos and their modules.

Convention
: We shall use curly upper-case letters
{\mathcal{C}},{\mathcal{D}},{\mathcal{E}}
etc for categories (underlying sites); lower-case letters
w,x,y,z
etc for the objects of categories; scan Serif letters
\textsf{f},\textsf{g},\textsf{h},\textsf{u},\textsf{v}
etc for the morphisms of categories. Functors among these categories will be denoted by Greek letters. Presheaves and sheaves are written as Fraktur letters
\mathfrak{F},\mathfrak{G},\mathfrak{M},\mathfrak{N},\mathfrak{R}
etc.

If
f
is a morphism in
{\mathcal{C}}
, we denote by
{\rm dom}(\textsf{f})
and
{\rm cod}(\textsf{f})
its domain and codomain.

In this paper, we shall let
k
to be a commutative ring with identity, and denote by
k
-Alg the category of unital associative algebras and unital
k
-algebra homomorphisms. All algebras and algebra morphisms will be unital. All modules, either over an algebra or a ringed site, are
right
modules.

2.1.
Grothendieck topologies and sheaves

Our main references on topos and sheaf theories are
[
1
,
8
,
9
,
12
]
. Here we only recall necessary ingredients.

Let
{\mathcal{C}}
be a small category. We shall denote by
\mathop{\rm PSh}\nolimits({\mathcal{C}})
the category of
presheaves of sets, or equivalently the contra-variant functors from
{\mathcal{C}}
to Set (the category of sets). Given the coefficient ring
k
, we write
\mathop{\rm PSh}\nolimits({\mathcal{C}},k)
for the category of presheaves of
k
-modules (contra-variant functors from
{\mathcal{C}}
to Mod-
k
). If
k=\mathbb{Z}
, this is often dubbed as
\text{PSh}({\mathcal{C}},\mathbb{Z})=\text{PSh}({\mathcal{C}},{\rm Ab})
. If
G
is a group, regarded as a category with a single object,
\text{PSh}(G)
and
\text{PSh}(G,k)
are canonically identified
\rSet G
and Mod-
kG
, the categories of right
G
-sets and of right
kG
-modules, respectively.

2.1.1.
Grothendieck topologies

One may put a Grothendieck topology
\mathcal{J}_{{\mathcal{C}}}
on
{\mathcal{C}}
, via the concept of a sieve. By definition a
sieve

S
on
x\in\text{Ob}{\mathcal{C}}
is simply a subfunctor of the representable functor
\text{Hom}_{{\mathcal{C}}}(-,x)
. It can be identified with a set (still written as
S
) of morphisms with codomain
x
, satisfying the condition that if
\textsf{u}\in S
and
uv
exists then
\textsf{uv}\in S
. For instance the
maximal sieve

\text{Hom}_{{\mathcal{C}}}(-,x)
is given by the set of all morphisms with codomain
x
. Meanwhile there is always an empty sieve on
x
.

Definition 2.1
.

A
(Grothendick) topology
on a category
{\mathcal{C}}
is a function
\mathcal{J}
which assigns to each object
x
of
{\mathcal{C}}
a non-empty collection
\mathcal{J}(x)
of sieves on
{\mathcal{C}}
, in such a way that

(1)

the maximal sieve
\text{\rm Hom}_{{\mathcal{C}}}(-,x)
is in
\mathcal{J}(x)
;

(2)

(stability axiom) if
S\in\mathcal{J}(x)
, then
\textsf{f}^{*}(S)=\{\textsf{g}~|~\textsf{fg}\in S\}\in\mathcal{J}(y)
for any arrow
\textsf{f}:y\rightarrow x
;

(3)

(transitivity axiom) if
S_{1}\in\mathcal{J}(x)
, and
S_{2}
is any sieve on
x
such that
\textsf{f}^{*}(S_{2})\in\mathcal{J}(y)
for all
\textsf{f}:y\rightarrow x
in
S_{1}
, then
S_{2}\in\mathcal{J}(x)
.

Any sieve in
\mathcal{J}(x)
is called a
covering sieve
on
x
. A small category
{\mathcal{C}}

equipped with a Grothendieck topology
\mathcal{J}
is called a
site

\textbf{C}=({\mathcal{C}},\mathcal{J})
.

Here are some examples of Grothendieck topologies that we will encounter.

Example 2.2
.

(1)

(
The minimal topology
) Given any category
{\mathcal{C}}
, one can define the minimal topology
\mathcal{J}_{\rm min}
such that
\mathcal{J}_{\rm min}(x)=\{\text{\rm Hom}_{{\mathcal{C}}}(-,x)\}
, for each
x\in\mathop{\rm Ob}\nolimits{\mathcal{C}}
.

(2)

(
The dense topology
)
The dense topology can be defined for an arbitrary category
{\mathcal{C}}
: for a sieve
S
, let
S\in\mathcal{J}_{\rm den}(x)
if and only if for any
\textsf{f}:y\rightarrow x
there is a morphism
\textsf{g}:z\rightarrow y
such that
\textsf{fg}\in S
.

It can be seen from the definition that, the covering sieves of a dense topology are always non-empty.

(3)

(
The maximal topology
)
Let
{\mathcal{C}}
be any category. To define the maximal topology
{\mathcal{J}}_{\rm max}
, we declare all sieves to be covering sieves.

2.1.2.
Sheaves on sites

Given a Grothendieck topology, one can consider sheaves on
{\mathcal{C}}
. Roughly speaking, a sheaf of sets on
{\mathcal{C}}
is a presheaf of sets on
{\mathcal{C}}
, satisfying
certain gluing properties mandated by
\mathcal{J}
. A concise definition
of a sheaf is the following.

Definition 2.3
.

A presheaf
\mathfrak{F}\in\mathop{\rm PSh}\nolimits({\mathcal{C}})
is a (
\mathcal{J}
-)
sheaf
of sets if, for every

x\in\mathop{\rm Ob}\nolimits{\mathcal{C}}
and every
S\in{\mathcal{J}}(x)
, the inclusion
S\hookrightarrow\mathop{\rm Hom}\nolimits_{{\mathcal{C}}}(-,x)
induces an isomorphism

\mathop{\rm Nat}\nolimits(\mathop{\rm Hom}\nolimits_{{\mathcal{C}}}(-,x),\mathfrak{F})\overset{\cong}{\longrightarrow}\mathop{\rm Nat}\nolimits(S,\mathfrak{F}).

The category of sheaves of sets on
\mathbf{C}=({\mathcal{C}},\mathcal{J})
is the full subcategory of
\mathop{\rm PSh}\nolimits({\mathcal{C}})
, consisting of all (
\mathcal{J}
-)sheaves, denoted by
\mathop{\rm Sh}\nolimits(\mathbf{C})
.

Let
{\mathfrak{F}}_{\bullet}
be the presheaf that sends every object
x
to a fixed singleton set
\bullet
, and every morphism to the identity. It is a terminal object of
\mathop{\rm PSh}\nolimits({\mathcal{C}})
, and one quickly verifies that it is always a sheaf, under any topology.

Given a presheaf, there is a natural way to build a sheaf. By standard procedures of construction (see for instance
[
12
, III.5]
or
[
8
, 7.10]
), given a presheaf
\mathfrak{F}
, we shall first construct the half-sheafification (terminology by P. Johnstone)
\mathfrak{F}^{\dagger}\in\text{PSh}({\mathcal{C}})
which, for each
x\in\text{Ob}{\mathcal{C}}
, we define

\mathfrak{F}^{\dagger}(x)=\mathop{\varinjlim}\nolimits_{{\mathcal{J}}(x)}\text{Nat}(-,\mathfrak{F}),

where
{\mathcal{J}}(x)
is deemed as a poset by inclusion. If
\mathfrak{F}^{\dagger}
is not a sheaf, then we repeat the construction and
\mathfrak{F}^{a}=(\mathfrak{F}^{\dagger})^{\dagger}\in\text{Sh}(\mathbf{C})
is the
sheafification
of
\mathfrak{F}
or the sheaf associated to
\mathfrak{F}
. The sheafification functor is exact and is left adjoint to the forgetful functor
\text{Sh}(\mathbf{C})\rightarrow\text{PSh}({\mathcal{C}})
, which is left exact.

Remark 2.4
.

From the adjunction between the forgetful functor and the sheafification functor, one realizes that
\mathop{\rm Sh}\nolimits(\mathbf{C})
is a localization of
\mathop{\rm PSh}\nolimits({\mathcal{C}})

[
1
,
7
,
9
]
. In fact the morphisms that become isomorphisms after sheafification form a multiplicative system
\Xi
in
\mathop{\rm PSh}\nolimits({\mathcal{C}})
. The morphisms in
\Xi
are called local isomorphisms
[
9
]
or morphismes bicouvrant
[
1
]
, giving a category equivalence

\mathop{\rm Sh}\nolimits(\C)\simeq\Xi^{-1}\mathop{\rm PSh}\nolimits({\mathcal{C}}).

Example 2.5
.

(1)

If
{\mathcal{C}}
is given the minimal topology, then all presheaves are sheaves. The local isomorphisms are exactly all the natural isomorphisms.

(2)

If
{\mathcal{C}}
is given the maximal topology, then the only sheaf is
{\mathfrak{F}}_{\bullet}
. The local isomorphisms are all the morphisms. (Be aware of set-theoretic issues!)

Let
k
be the coefficient ring. A presheaf of
k
-modules is a contra-covariant functor
{\mathfrak{M}}
from
{\mathcal{C}}
to
\rMod k
. It is a sheaf of
k
-modules if the composite
{\mathcal{C}}{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\to}\limits^{{\mathfrak{M}}}}}\rMod k\hookrightarrow
Set is a sheaf of sets. The resulting sheaf category
\mathop{\rm Sh}\nolimits(\C,k)
is Abelian, while the zero object
0
(=
{\mathfrak{F}}_{\bullet}
) maps every object to
0
. The forgetful and sheafification functors restrict to a pair of adjoint functors between
\mathop{\rm Sh}\nolimits(\C,k)
and
\mathop{\rm PSh}\nolimits({\mathcal{C}},k)
. It follows that

\mathop{\rm Sh}\nolimits(\C,k)\simeq\mathop{\rm PSh}\nolimits({\mathcal{C}},k)/\mathcal{S},

where
\mathcal{S}
is the Serre subcategory consisting of
{\mathfrak{M}}\in\mathop{\rm PSh}\nolimits({\mathcal{C}},k)
satisfying
{\mathfrak{M}}^{a}=0
.

In the Abelian category
\mathop{\rm Sh}\nolimits(\C,k)
, one may consider its coherent objects. They form an Abelian full subcategory
{\mathop{\rm coh}\nolimits}(\C,k)
, see
[
14
]
, as well as
[
1
,
8
]
.

Example 2.6
.

On a group
G
, considered as a category with one object
\bullet
, there are only two topologies. The minimal topology(=dense topology) contains only one sieve
\text{\rm Hom}_{{\mathcal{C}}}(-,\bullet)
, while the maximal topology has two sieves
\{\emptyset,\text{\rm Hom}_{{\mathcal{C}}}(-,\bullet)\}
.

Under the minimal topology, we have
\mathop{\rm Sh}\nolimits(\G)=\rSet G
and
\mathop{\rm Sh}\nolimits(\G,k)=\rMod kG
. When
k
is Noetherian,
{\mathop{\rm coh}\nolimits}(\G,k)=\rmod kG
, the category of finitely generated
kG
-modules.

2.2.
Geometric morphisms and the Comparison Lemma

To compare sheaves on two sites, we need the concepts of continuous functors
[
1
]
. Let
\alpha:{\mathcal{C}}\to{\mathcal{D}}
be a (covariant) functor. Then it induces a restriction along
\alpha
,
Res_{\alpha}:\mathop{\rm PSh}\nolimits({\mathcal{D}})\to\mathop{\rm PSh}\nolimits({\mathcal{C}})
, which has two adjoint functors
LK_{\alpha},RK_{\alpha}:\mathop{\rm PSh}\nolimits({\mathcal{C}})\to\mathop{\rm PSh}\nolimits({\mathcal{D}})
, called the left and right Kan extensions along
\alpha
.

Definition 2.7
.

Let
\C=({\mathcal{C}},{\mathcal{J}})
and
\D=({\mathcal{D}},{\mathcal{J}}^{\prime})
be two sites. A functor
\alpha:{\mathcal{C}}\to{\mathcal{D}}
is called
continuous
if the restriction
Res_{\alpha}:\mathop{\rm PSh}\nolimits({\mathcal{D}})\to\mathop{\rm PSh}\nolimits({\mathcal{C}})
preserves sheaves.

When
\alpha
is continuous, it gives rise to a functor
Res_{\alpha}:\mathop{\rm Sh}\nolimits(\D)\to\mathop{\rm Sh}\nolimits(\C)
.

Definition 2.8
.

Let
\C=({\mathcal{C}},{\mathcal{J}})
and
\D=({\mathcal{D}},{\mathcal{J}}^{\prime})
be two sites. A
morphism of sites

\Lambda:\D\to\C
is given by a continuous functor
\alpha:{\mathcal{C}}\to{\mathcal{D}}
such that

LK_{\alpha}^{a}=(-)^{a}\circ LK_{\alpha}:\mathop{\rm Sh}\nolimits(\C)\to\mathop{\rm Sh}\nolimits(\D)

is left exact.

In topos theory, the notion of a topos is more important than that of a site. Although we would like to focus on sites, certain relevant key results are formulated in terms of topoi.

Definition 2.9
.

A
(Grothendieck) topos
is a category that is equivalent to some
\mathop{\rm Sh}\nolimits(\C)
, a category of sheaves of sets on a site
\C
. A
(geometric) morphism of topoi

\Psi=(\Psi^{-1},\Psi_{*}):\mathop{\rm Sh}\nolimits(\D)\to\mathop{\rm Sh}\nolimits(\C)

consists of a pair of functors
\Psi_{*}:\mathop{\rm Sh}\nolimits(\D)\to\mathop{\rm Sh}\nolimits(\C)
and
\Psi^{-1}:\mathop{\rm Sh}\nolimits(\C)\to\mathop{\rm Sh}\nolimits(\D)
such that
\Psi^{-1}
is left exact and is left adjoint to
\Psi_{*}
.

If
\Psi_{*}
happens to be an inclusion which is fully faithful, then
\mathop{\rm Sh}\nolimits(\D)
is said to be a
subtopos
of
\mathop{\rm Sh}\nolimits(\C)
.

A morphism of sites
\Lambda:\D\to\C
always induces a morphism of topoi
\Lambda=(\Lambda^{-1},\Lambda_{*})
, where
\Lambda_{*}=Res_{\alpha}:\mathop{\rm Sh}\nolimits(\D)\to\mathop{\rm Sh}\nolimits(\C)
and
\Lambda^{-1}=LK_{\alpha}^{a}:\mathop{\rm Sh}\nolimits(\C)\to\mathop{\rm Sh}\nolimits(\D)
.

The Comparison Lemma will be used in Section 4, in order to establish our main results.

Definition 2.10
.

Let
({\mathcal{C}},{\mathcal{J}})
be a site. A full subcategory
{\mathcal{D}}
of
{\mathcal{C}}
is said to be
{\mathcal{J}}
-dense
if for every object
x
of
{\mathcal{C}}
the sieve generated by the family of arrows from objects in
{\mathcal{D}}
to
x
is
{\mathcal{J}}
-covering.

Given such a subcategory
{\mathcal{D}}
, the Grothendieck topology
{\mathcal{J}}|_{{\mathcal{D}}}
on
{\mathcal{D}}
induced by
{\mathcal{J}}
is defined as follows: for any sieve
S
in
{\mathcal{D}}
on an object
x
,
S\in{\mathcal{J}}|_{{\mathcal{D}}}(x)
if and only if
\bar{S}\in{\mathcal{J}}(x)
, where
\bar{S}
is the sieve in
{\mathcal{C}}
generated by the arrows in
S
.

Theorem 2.11

(Comparison Lemma)
.

Let
({\mathcal{C}},{\mathcal{J}})
be a site and
{\mathcal{D}}
be a full subcategory of
{\mathcal{C}}
which is
{\mathcal{J}}
-dense. Then the restriction
Res_{\iota}
, along
\iota:{\mathcal{D}}\hookrightarrow{\mathcal{C}}
, induce a functor
Res_{\iota}:\mathop{\rm Sh}\nolimits({\mathcal{C}},{\mathcal{J}})\to\mathop{\rm Sh}\nolimits({\mathcal{D}},{\mathcal{J}}|_{{\mathcal{D}}})
, which is an equivalence. The quasi-inverse is the right Kan extension
RK_{\iota}
along
\iota
.

It is illuminating to have a concrete yet more sophiscated example other than groups.

2.4.

A full subcategory

A category is

if and only if

and

3.1.
Grothendieck construction

Let
{\mathcal{C}}
be a small category and
\mathfrak{R}:{\mathcal{C}}^{op}\to k\text{-Alg}
be a presheaf of
k
-algebras. Since every unital ring can be regarded as a small additive category with a single object
[
13
]
,
\mathfrak{R}
becomes a contravariant functor from
{\mathcal{C}}
to
{\rm Cat}
, the category of small categories.

There is a device, called the Grothendieck construction (see
[
6
, VI.8]
), to build a fibred category over a small category
{\mathcal{C}}
equipped with a pseudo-functor to
{\rm Cat}
. Since strict functors are pseudo, we can examine the construction for
\mathfrak{R}
.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
\mathfrak{F}:{\mathcal{C}}^{op}\to{\rm Cat}
be a (strict) functor. The
Grothendieck construction

Gr_{{\mathcal{C}}}\mathfrak{F}
is a small category whose objects are
\{(x,c)~|~x\in\mathop{\rm Ob}\nolimits{\mathcal{C}},c\in\mathop{\rm Ob}\nolimits\mathfrak{F}(x)\}
, and whose morphisms are

{\rm Hom}_{Gr_{{\mathcal{C}}}\mathfrak{F}}((x,c),(y,d))=\{(\textsf{f},\textsf{u})~|~\textsf{f}:x\to y\in{\rm Mor}{\mathcal{C}},\textsf{u}:c\to\mathfrak{F}(\textsf{f})(d)\in{\rm Mor}~\mathfrak{F}(x)\}.

The composition of morphisms is given by
(\textsf{g},\textsf{v})\circ(\textsf{f},\textsf{u})=(\textsf{g}\textsf{f},\mathfrak{F}(\textsf{f})(\textsf{v})\circ\textsf{u})
.

Since
\mathfrak{R}
admits an “action” by
{\mathcal{C}}
, our category
Gr_{{\mathcal{C}}}\mathfrak{R}
carries certain algebraic structures on its morphism sets. To explain, we present
Gr_{{\mathcal{C}}}\mathfrak{R}
as follows

(1)

Objects:
\{(x,\bullet_{x})~|~x\in\mathop{\rm Ob}\nolimits{\mathcal{C}}\;\mbox{and}\,\bullet_{x}\,\mbox{is the unique object of}\;\mathfrak{R}(x)\}
,

(2)

Morphisms:

{\rm Hom}_{Gr_{{\mathcal{C}}}\mathfrak{R}}((x,\bullet_{x}),(y,\bullet_{y}))=\{(\textsf{f},r)~|~\textsf{f}:x\to y,r:\bullet_{x}\to\mathfrak{R}(\textsf{f})(\bullet_{y}),r\in\mathfrak{R}(x)\},

(3)

Composition of morphisms:
(\textsf{g},s)\circ(\textsf{f},r)=(\textsf{g}\textsf{f},\mathfrak{R}(\textsf{f})(s)r)
.

Note that we always have
\mathfrak{R}(\textsf{f})(\bullet_{y})=\bullet_{x}
for any morphism
\textsf{f}:x\to y
.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
Gr_{{\mathcal{C}}}\mathfrak{R}
be the above Grothendieck construction. Suppose
\textsf{f}:x\to y
is a morphism in
{\mathcal{C}}
. Then

(1)

The following subset, of
{\rm Hom}_{Gr_{{\mathcal{C}}}\mathfrak{R}}((x,\bullet_{x}),(y,\bullet_{y}))
,

\mathop{\rm Hom}\nolimits^{\textsf{f}}((x,\bullet_{x}),(y,\bullet_{y}))=\{(\textsf{f},r)~|~r:\bullet_{x}\to\mathfrak{R}(\textsf{f})(\bullet_{y}),r\in\mathfrak{R}(x)\}

has a natural
k
-module structure.

(2)

The set
\mathop{\rm Aut}\nolimits^{\textsf{1}_{x}}(x,\bullet_{x})=\{(\textsf{1}_{x},r)~|~r:\bullet_{x}\to\mathfrak{R}(\textsf{1}_{x})(\bullet_{x}),r\in\mathfrak{R}(x)\}
has a natural ring structure and is isomorphic to
\mathfrak{R}(x)
.

(3)

The set
\mathop{\rm Hom}\nolimits^{\textsf{f}}((x,\bullet_{x}),(y,\bullet_{y}))
is a rank one free right
\mathop{\rm Aut}\nolimits^{\textsf{1}_{x}}(x,\bullet_{x})
-module, with
(\textsf{f},1_{\mathfrak{R}(x)})
as a base element.

Proof.

On
\mathop{\rm Hom}\nolimits^{\textsf{f}}((x,\bullet_{x}),(y,\bullet_{y}))
, one can define an addition by asking
(\textsf{f},r)+(\textsf{f},r^{\prime})
to be
(\textsf{f},r+r^{\prime})
. For
l\in k
, one sets
l(\textsf{f},r)=(\textsf{f},lr)
and then the operations do give a
k
-module structure.

Specializing to
\mathop{\rm Aut}\nolimits^{\textsf{1}_{x}}(x,\bullet_{x})=\mathop{\rm Hom}\nolimits^{\textsf{1}_{x}}((x,\bullet_{x}),(x,\bullet_{x}))
, it has an multiplicative structure on top of the
k
-module structure. In fact, we can set
(1_{x},r)(1_{x},r^{\prime})=(1_{x},rr^{\prime})
and verify that it is compatible with the
k
-module structure. It is easy to see that there exists a natural isomorphism
\mathop{\rm Aut}\nolimits^{\textsf{1}_{x}}(x,\bullet_{x})\cong\mathfrak{R}(x)
.

Finally, from
(\textsf{f},1_{\mathfrak{R}(x)})(1_{x},r)=(\textsf{f},r)
, we can readily establish the third statement.
∎

Since there is no reasonable way to define a “sum” of
(\textsf{f},r)
and
(\textsf{f}^{\prime},r^{\prime})
, the set
{\rm Hom}_{Gr_{{\mathcal{C}}}\mathfrak{R}}((x,\bullet_{x}),(y,\bullet_{y}))
is not an Abelian group itself. Nonetheless, motivated by the above analyse and the work of Mitchell
[
13
]
, we introduce an algebra on the Grothendieck construction.

3.2.
Skew category algebras

The idea is to consider the coproduct of all
k
-modules constructed in Proposition 3.2 (1). However for future applications, we shall slightly simplify the notations.

Definition 3.3

Let

be a (non-empty) small category. Let

be a presheaf of

\mathfrak{R}[{\mathcal{C}}]

is a

\textsf{f}\in\mathop{\rm Mor}\nolimits{\mathcal{C}}

\displaystyle s\textsf{g}\ast r\textsf{f}=\begin{cases}(\mathfrak{R}(\textsf{f})(s)r)\textsf{g}\textsf{f},&\text{if}~{\rm dom}(\textsf{g})={\rm cod}(\textsf{f});\\
0,&{\rm otherwise}.\end{cases}

Extending this product linearly to two arbitrary elements,
\mathfrak{R}[{\mathcal{C}}]
becomes an associative
k
-algebra.

The algebra
\mathfrak{R}[{\mathcal{C}}]
has an identity
\Sigma_{x\in\mathop{\rm Ob}\nolimits{\mathcal{C}}}1_{\mathfrak{R}(x)}\textsf{1}_{x}
if
{\mathcal{C}}
is object-finite.

If
{\mathcal{C}}
is empty, the skew category algebra is understood to be the null ring. The above construction will be used to characterize the category of right
\mathfrak{R}
-modules over a presheaf
\mathfrak{R}
of
k
-algebras.

Remark 3.4
.

(1)

If

is the constant presheaf such that

, then the skew category algebra
\underline{k}[{\mathcal{C}}]
on
{\mathcal{C}}
with respect to

is just the category algebra
k{\mathcal{C}}

[
16
]
.

(2)

If
{\mathcal{C}}
be a group
G
, then
\mathfrak{R}=\mathfrak{R}(\bullet)
(
\bullet
being the unique object of
G

k
-algebra with a designated
G
-action, and the skew category algebra is just a skew group algebra.

(3)

If
{\mathcal{C}}={\mathcal{P}}
is a poset, we recover the construction

of Gerstenhaber and Schack
[
5
]
.

We note that due to our convention the above constructions are slightly different from their common forms in the literature. For example, in (2) the group

on the right, while one usually sets

to be a left

[

3.3.
Presheaves of modules

For a presheaf of
k
-algebras
\mathfrak{R}
on an object-finite small category
{\mathcal{C}}
, the following theorem characterizes the category
\Mod\mathfrak{R}
of right
\mathfrak{R}
-modules.

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
{\mathcal{C}}
be a small category and
\mathfrak{R}:{\mathcal{C}}^{op}\to k\mbox{-}{\rm Alg}
be a presheaf of
k
-algebras on
\C
. If
\mathop{\rm Ob}\nolimits{\mathcal{C}}
is finite, then we have the following equivalence

\Mod\mathfrak{R}\simeq\rMod\mathfrak{R}[{\mathcal{C}}].

Proof.

We will prove by constructing a pair of functors, and then show that they give rise to an equivalence between the categories. Recall that the category
\Mod\mathfrak{R}
is just the category of preasheaves of
\mathfrak{R}
-modules.

Firstly, let us define a functor
\Theta:\Mod\mathfrak{R}\to{\rm Mod\text{-}}\mathfrak{R}[{\mathcal{C}}]
by

\mathfrak{M}\mapsto\bigoplus_{y\in{\mathcal{C}}}\mathfrak{M}(y).

Here
\bigoplus_{y\in{\mathcal{C}}}\mathfrak{M}(y)
is a right
\mathfrak{R}[{\mathcal{C}}]
-module: for any
m\in\mathfrak{M}(y)
and any
r\textsf{f}\in\mathfrak{R}[{\mathcal{C}}]
, we can define an action

\displaystyle m\cdot r\textsf{f}=\begin{cases}\mathfrak{M}(\textsf{f})(m)\cdot r,&\text{if}~\mathop{\rm cod}(\textsf{f})=y;\\
0,&\text{otherwise.}\\
\end{cases}

We are now at the position to verify that the operation defined above does give
{\mathfrak{M}}
a right
\mathfrak{R}[{\mathcal{C}}]
-module structure. It is clear that the operation is closed. For the identity
1_{\mathfrak{R}[{\mathcal{C}}]}\in\mathfrak{R}[{\mathcal{C}}]
, since

m\cdot 1_{\mathfrak{R}(y)}\textsf{1}_{y}=\mathfrak{M}(\textsf{1}_{y})(m)\cdot 1_{\mathfrak{R}(y)}=m,

it follows that

m\cdot 1_{\mathfrak{R}[{\mathcal{C}}]}=m\cdot\sum_{y}1_{\mathfrak{R}(y)}\textsf{1}_{y}=m.

It remains to show that

(m\cdot s\textsf{g})\cdot r\textsf{f}=m\cdot(s\textsf{g}\ast r\textsf{f})

for any two elements
s\textsf{g},r\textsf{f}\in\mathfrak{R}[{\mathcal{C}}]
. We only need to consider the case that
x\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textsf{f}}}{{\to}}y\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textsf{g}}}{{\to}}z
are composable in
{\mathcal{C}}
,
m\in\mathfrak{M}(z)
,
s\in\mathfrak{R}(y)
and
r\in\mathfrak{R}(x)
.
Then we have

\displaystyle(m\cdot s\textsf{g})\cdot r\textsf{f}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\mathfrak{M}(\textsf{g})(m)\cdot s)\cdot r\textsf{f}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathfrak{M}(\textsf{f})(\mathfrak{M}(\textsf{g})(m)\cdot s)\cdot r,

and

\displaystyle m\cdot(s\textsf{g}\ast r\textsf{f})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle m\cdot(\mathfrak{R}(\textsf{f})(s)r)\textsf{g}\textsf{f}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathfrak{M}(\textsf{g}\textsf{f})(m)\cdot(\mathfrak{R}(\textsf{f})(s)r)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathfrak{M}(\textsf{f})(\mathfrak{M}(\textsf{g})(m))\cdot(\mathfrak{R}(\textsf{f})(s)r)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\mathfrak{M}(\textsf{f})(\mathfrak{M}(\textsf{g})(m))\cdot\mathfrak{R}(\textsf{f})(s))\cdot r.

Due to the right
\mathfrak{R}
-module structure on
\mathfrak{M}
, it follows that

\mathfrak{M}(\textsf{f})(\mathfrak{M}(\textsf{g})(m)\cdot s)=\mathfrak{M}(\textsf{f})(\mathfrak{M}(\textsf{g})(m))\cdot\mathfrak{R}(\textsf{f})(s).

Hence

(m\cdot s\textsf{g})\cdot r\textsf{f}=m\cdot(s\textsf{g}\ast r\textsf{f}).

With obvious map on morphisms, it is not difficult to see that
\Theta
is a functor.

Secondly, let us define another functor

\Omega:{\rm Mod\text{-}}\mathfrak{R}[{\mathcal{C}}]\to\Mod\mathfrak{R}

by
N\mapsto\mathfrak{N}
. The presheaf
\mathfrak{N}:{\mathcal{C}}^{op}\to\rMod k
is defined as follows

{\mathfrak{N}}(y)=N\cdot 1_{\mathfrak{R}(y)}\textsf{1}_{y},\forall y\in\mathop{\rm Ob}\nolimits{\mathcal{C}}

and

{\mathfrak{N}}(\textsf{f})=-\cdot 1_{\mathfrak{R}(x)}\textsf{f}:N\cdot 1_{\mathfrak{R}(y)}\textsf{1}_{y}\to N\cdot 1_{\mathfrak{R}(x)}\textsf{1}_{x},\forall\textsf{f}:x\to y.

Our task now is to verify that
\mathfrak{N}
is a right
\mathfrak{R}
-module. Recall that, a presheaf of
\mathfrak{R}
-module
\mathfrak{N}
is a presheaf
\mathfrak{N}:{\mathcal{C}}^{op}\to\rMod k
such that, for each
x\in\mathop{\rm Ob}\nolimits{\mathcal{C}}
,
\mathfrak{N}(x)
has a right
\mathfrak{R}(x)
action.

Let us first check that
\mathfrak{N}
is a functor. For any
n\cdot 1_{\mathfrak{R}(y)}\textsf{1}_{y}\in N\cdot 1_{\mathfrak{R}(y)}\textsf{1}_{y}
, observe that

(n\cdot 1_{\mathfrak{R}(y)}\textsf{1}_{y})\cdot 1_{\mathfrak{R}(x)}\textsf{f}=n\cdot(1_{\mathfrak{R}(x)}\textsf{f}\ast 1_{\mathfrak{R}(x)}\textsf{1}_{x})=(n\cdot 1_{\mathfrak{R}(x)}\textsf{f})\cdot 1_{\mathfrak{R}(x)}\textsf{1}_{x}\in N\cdot 1_{\mathfrak{R}(x)}\textsf{1}_{x},

so
\mathfrak{N}(\textsf{f})=-\cdot 1_{\mathfrak{R}(x)}\textsf{f}
is well defined. Obviously,

\mathfrak{N}(\textsf{1}_{y})=-\cdot 1_{\mathfrak{R}(y)}\textsf{1}_{y}={\rm Id}_{N\cdot 1_{\mathfrak{R}(y)}\textsf{1}_{y}},

and

\displaystyle\mathfrak{N}(\textsf{f})\circ\mathfrak{N}(\textsf{g})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\cdot 1_{\mathfrak{R}(y)}\textsf{g}\cdot 1_{\mathfrak{R}(x)}\textsf{f}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\cdot(1_{\mathfrak{R}(y)}\textsf{g}\ast 1_{\mathfrak{R}(x)}\textsf{f})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\cdot(\mathfrak{R}(\textsf{f})(1_{\mathfrak{R}(y)})1_{\mathfrak{R}(x)})\textsf{g}\textsf{f}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\cdot 1_{\mathfrak{R}(x)}\textsf{g}\textsf{f}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathfrak{N}(\textsf{g}\textsf{f}).

According to the fact that
\mathfrak{R}(\textsf{f})
is a unital algebra homomorphism, the fourth equality above holds. Thus we have checked that
\mathfrak{N}
is a functor.

The next thing to do is to check that
\mathfrak{N}
has a right
\mathfrak{R}
-module structure. For each
y\in{\rm Ob}{\mathcal{C}}
, there is a right action of
\mathfrak{R}(y)
on
N\cdot 1_{\mathfrak{R}(y)}\textsf{1}_{y}
as follows. For any
n\cdot 1_{\mathfrak{R}(y)}\textsf{1}_{y}\in N\cdot 1_{\mathfrak{R}(y)}\textsf{1}_{y}
and
r\in\mathfrak{R}(y)
,
r
acts on
n\cdot 1_{\mathfrak{R}(y)}\textsf{1}_{y}
by
r\textsf{1}_{y}
from the right:
(n\cdot 1_{\mathfrak{R}(y)}\textsf{1}_{y})\cdot r\textsf{1}_{y}=(n\cdot r\textsf{1}_{y})\cdot 1_{\mathfrak{R}(y)}\textsf{1}_{y}\in N\cdot 1_{\mathfrak{R}(y)}\textsf{1}_{y}
. It is straightforward to verify that this makes
\mathfrak{N}
a right
\mathfrak{R}
-module.

From the definitions of
\Theta
and
\Omega
, it is not difficult to see that

\Omega\Theta\cong{\rm Id}_{\Mod\mathfrak{R}},

and, moreover if
{\rm Ob}{\mathcal{C}}
is finite,

\Theta\Omega\cong{\rm Id}_{{\rm Mod\text{-}}\mathfrak{R}[{\mathcal{C}}]}.

Thus we arrive at the conclusion that if
{\mathcal{C}}
is object-finite then

\Mod\mathfrak{R}\simeq{\rm Mod\text{-}}\mathfrak{R}[{\mathcal{C}}].

∎

Remark 3.6
.

If
{\mathcal{C}}
is not object-finite, then we only have a fully faithful functor
\Theta:\Mod\mathfrak{R}\hookrightarrow\rMod\mathfrak{R}[{\mathcal{C}}]
. For example, if
X
is a topological space and
{\mathcal{P}}_{X}
is the poset of open subsets of
X
, then the classical sheaf theory can be recovered as
\mathop{\rm Sh}\nolimits({\mathcal{P}}_{X})
, by giving
{\mathcal{P}}_{X}
a canonical Grothendieck topology. In this case,
{\mathcal{P}}_{X}
is not object-finite in general.

4.
Subcategory topologies and module categories

In
[
1
, Exposé IV]
, Grothendieck and Verdier outlined a classification of “topos finis”. A topos is said to be finite, if it is equivalent to the presheaf topos
\mathop{\rm PSh}\nolimits({\mathcal{C}})
over a finite category
{\mathcal{C}}
.

Proposition 4.1

(
[
1
]
IV, Exercise 9.1.12)
.

Let
{\mathcal{C}}
be a finite category.

(1)

Every subtopos of a finite topos is again a finite topos.

(2)

There is an order-reversing bijection between the poset of Grothendieck topologies on
{\mathcal{C}}
, and that of the strictly full Karoubian subcategories of
{\mathcal{C}}
.

(3)

There is an order-reversing bijection between the poset of Grothendieck topologies on
{\mathcal{C}}
, and that of the subtopoi of
\mathop{\rm PSh}\nolimits({\mathcal{C}})
.

Proof.

The proof is a combination of several exercises in
[
1
, Exposé IV]
, in Sections 7 and 9. It relies on considering the points of topoi, which are geometric morphisms from Set to the presheaf topos in our case. Since it is involved and is far from our purposes, we will reorganize the existing approaches and describe another way to partially demonstrate the facts, which can be extended to ringed finite sites and their module categories.
∎

The reader can revisit Examples 2.12 and 2.15, where every subcategory is a strictly full Karoubian subcategory, to better understand the statements.

To establish the above statements,
[
1
, IV Exercise 9.1.12]
introduced a topology on
{\mathcal{C}}
, for each strictly full subcategory
{\mathcal{D}}
. For reference, we recode it below and give the topology a name. Suppose
x\in\mathop{\rm Ob}\nolimits{\mathcal{C}}
and
\{x_{i}\to x\}_{i}
is a set of morphisms in
{\mathcal{C}}
. For any
w\in\mathop{\rm Ob}\nolimits{\mathcal{C}}
, there is a natural set map

\coprod_{x_{i}\to x}\mathop{\rm Hom}\nolimits_{{\mathcal{C}}}(w,x_{i})\to\mathop{\rm Hom}\nolimits_{{\mathcal{C}}}(w,x).

Definition 4.2
.

Let
{\mathcal{C}}
be a finite category and
{\mathcal{D}}
be a strictly full subcategory. The
subcategory topology

{\mathcal{J}}^{{\mathcal{D}}}
is given by defining
{\mathcal{J}}^{{\mathcal{D}}}(x)
,
\forall x\in\mathop{\rm Ob}\nolimits{\mathcal{C}}
, to be the following

\{\mbox{sieves}\ S\ \mbox{on}\ x\bigm|\coprod_{y\to x\in S}\mathop{\rm Hom}\nolimits_{{\mathcal{C}}}(w,y)\to\mathop{\rm Hom}\nolimits_{{\mathcal{C}}}(w,x)\ \mbox{is surjective for all}\ w\in\mathop{\rm Ob}\nolimits{\mathcal{D}}\}.

To verify that the above is truly a Grothendieck topology on
{\mathcal{C}}
, one can check that the three conditions in Definition 2.1 are satisfied. This is tedious but routine.

Remark 4.3
.

Let
{\mathcal{C}}
be a finite category and
{\mathcal{D}}
be a strictly full subcategory. For each object
x\in\mathop{\rm Ob}\nolimits{\mathcal{C}}
, the minimal covering sieve (possibly empty) in
{\mathcal{J}}^{{\mathcal{D}}}(x)
is the one generated by all morphisms from objects of
{\mathcal{D}}
to
x
.

We are interested in the following result, stated in
[
1
, Exposé IV, Exercise 9.1.12 (e)]
, which makes it possible to classify module categories over ringed finite sites.

Proposition 4.4
.

[
1
, Exposé IV, Exercise 9.1.12 (e)]
Let
{\mathcal{C}}
be a finite category.

(1)

The subcategory topologies exhausts all possible topologies on a finite category
{\mathcal{C}}
.

(2)

Let
{\mathcal{D}}
be a strictly full subcategory. Under the subcategory topology
{\mathcal{J}}^{{\mathcal{D}}}
, there is an equivalence

\mathop{\rm Sh}\nolimits({\mathcal{C}},{\mathcal{J}}^{{\mathcal{D}}})\simeq\mathop{\rm PSh}\nolimits({\mathcal{D}}).

Proof.

The first part depends on a discussion on points of topoi, which we shall not dwell into. A relatively elementary proof for Karoubian categories in one direction can be found in
[
7
, C 2.2 Lemma 2.2.21]
. As to the second part, by Definition 2.10, the full subcategory
{\mathcal{D}}
equipped with
{\mathcal{J}}^{{\mathcal{D}}}|_{{\mathcal{D}}}
is a dense subsite of
({\mathcal{C}},{\mathcal{J}}^{{\mathcal{D}}})
. Moreover, since we must have
1_{w}\in S
for any
S\in{\mathcal{J}}^{{\mathcal{D}}}(w)
,
\forall w\in\mathop{\rm Ob}\nolimits{\mathcal{D}}
,
{\mathcal{J}}^{{\mathcal{D}}}|_{{\mathcal{D}}}
is the minimal topology on
{\mathcal{D}}
. Therefore we have an equivalence
\mathop{\rm Sh}\nolimits({\mathcal{C}},{\mathcal{J}}^{{\mathcal{D}}})\simeq\mathop{\rm PSh}\nolimits({\mathcal{D}})
, by the Comparison Lemma.
∎

The above equivalence can be extended to one between suitable ringed topoi. The later will give us a classification of module categories on ringed finite sites, together with the main result in the preceding section on presheaf topoi.

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
{\mathcal{C}}
be a finite category and
{\mathcal{D}}
be a strictly full subcategory. Let
\mathfrak{R}
be a sheaf of
k
-algebras on the site
({\mathcal{C}},{\mathcal{J}}^{{\mathcal{D}}})
. Then we have category equivalences

\Mod\mathfrak{R}\simeq\rMod\mathfrak{R}|_{{\mathcal{D}}}[{\mathcal{D}}].

Here
\mathfrak{R}|_{{\mathcal{D}}}
is the restriction of
\mathfrak{R}
to
{\mathcal{D}}
.

Proof.

Given the equivalence of topoi

\Psi=(\Psi^{-1},\Psi_{*})=(RK_{\iota},Res_{\iota}):\mathop{\rm Sh}\nolimits({\mathcal{C}},{\mathcal{J}}^{{\mathcal{D}}}){\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\to}\limits^{\simeq}}}\mathop{\rm PSh}\nolimits({\mathcal{D}}),

and a structure sheaf
\mathfrak{R}
on
\mathop{\rm Sh}\nolimits({\mathcal{C}},{\mathcal{J}}^{{\mathcal{D}}})
, we introduce a morphism of ringed topoi

(\Psi,\Psi^{\sharp}):(\mathop{\rm Sh}\nolimits({\mathcal{C}},{\mathcal{J}}^{{\mathcal{D}}}),\mathfrak{R})\to(\mathop{\rm PSh}\nolimits({\mathcal{D}}),\mathfrak{R}^{\prime}),

where
\mathfrak{R}^{\prime}=\Psi_{*}(\mathfrak{R})
and
\Psi^{\sharp}
is the identity. This is an equivalence of ringed topoi. Hence we obtain an equivalence of module categories

\Mod\mathfrak{R}\simeq\Mod\mathfrak{R}^{\prime}.

But
\mathfrak{R}^{\prime}=\Psi_{*}(\mathfrak{R})=Res_{\iota}\mathfrak{R}
is the restriction of
\mathfrak{R}
along the inclusion functor. Thus
\mathfrak{R}^{\prime}=\mathfrak{R}|_{{\mathcal{D}}}
, and we have desired equivalences

\Mod\mathfrak{R}\simeq\Mod\mathfrak{R}|_{{\mathcal{D}}}\simeq\rMod\mathfrak{R}|_{{\mathcal{D}}}[{\mathcal{D}}],

by Theorem 3.5.
∎

If, for each
x\in\mathop{\rm Ob}\nolimits{\mathcal{D}}
,
\mathfrak{R}(x)
is Noetherian, then
\mathfrak{R}|_{{\mathcal{D}}}[{\mathcal{D}}]
is finite-dimensional and the coherent
\mathfrak{R}|_{{\mathcal{D}}}[{\mathcal{D}}]
-modules are the finitely generated ones
[
14
]
.

Corollary 4.6
.

Let
\mathbf{C}=({\mathcal{C}},\mathcal{J})
be a finite site and
\mathfrak{R}:{\mathcal{C}}^{op}\to k\mbox{\rm-Alg}
be a sheaf of
k
-algebras on
\C
. If for each
x\in\mathop{\rm Ob}\nolimits{\mathcal{D}}
,
\mathfrak{R}(x)
is Noetherian, then there is a category equivalence

{\mathop{\rm coh}\nolimits}\text{-}\mathfrak{R}\simeq\rmod\mathfrak{R}|_{{\mathcal{D}}}[{\mathcal{D}}],

for some full subcategory
{\mathcal{D}}
uniquely determined by
{\mathcal{J}}
.

5.
Further remarks

From our perspective, it is important to understand the role of various finite EI-categories in finite group representations. We end this paper with several remarks, demonstrating promising connections between the present paper and some other works.

5.1.
On dense EI-sites

Finite EI-categories frequently occur in group representations and cohomology. We consider sheaves on finite EI-categories under the dense topology, as an example to illustrate methods developed earlier. Moreover, we will see that the sheafification may be used to replace the right Kan extension in Proposition 4.4(2) and Theorem 4.5. The following lemma will help us to compute the sheafifications of presheaves.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
{\mathcal{C}}
be a finite
EI
-category and
{\mathcal{J}}_{\rm den}
the dense topology. Then, for each object
x
, the minimal non-empty covering sieve
S^{\min}_{x}
is given by all the morphisms from the minimal objects to
x
, namely

S^{\min}_{x}=\{\textsf{f}\in{\rm Hom}_{{\mathcal{C}}}(y,x)~|~y\in\mathop{\rm Ob}\nolimits({\mathcal{C}}_{\leq x})_{\min}\}.

Subsequently
{\mathcal{J}}_{\rm den}={\mathcal{J}}^{{\mathcal{C}}_{\min}}
.

Proof.

Firstly, we verify that
S_{x}
is a sieve. For any morphism
\textsf{f}:y\to x\in S_{x}
and any morphism
\textsf{g}:z\to y
with
{\rm cod}(\textsf{g})={\rm dom}(\textsf{f})
, we have to show that
\textsf{fg}\in S_{x}
. Note that
z\leq y
and
y
is minimal, we infer that
z\in\mathop{\rm Ob}\nolimits({\mathcal{C}}_{\leq x})_{\min}
. It follows that
\textsf{fg}\in S_{x}
.

Secondly, we shall show that
S_{x}
is a covering sieve. For any
\textsf{f}:y\to x\in{\rm Hom}_{{\mathcal{C}}}(-,x)
, we have to prove that there exists a morphism
\textsf{g}:z\to y
such that
\textsf{fg}\in S_{x}
. Since
{\mathcal{C}}
is finite, we can always find such a morphism
\textsf{g}:z\to y
.

Finally, we will show that
S_{x}
is minimal in
{\mathcal{J}}_{\rm den}(x)
. That is, we have to check that
S_{x}\subseteq T
for any
T\in{\mathcal{J}}_{\rm den}(x)
. For any
\textsf{f}:y\to x\in S_{x}
, by definition,
y\in\mathop{\rm Ob}\nolimits({\mathcal{C}}_{\leq x})_{\min}
. Since
T\in{\mathcal{J}}_{\rm den}(x)
, according to the definition of the dense topology, there exists a morphism
\textsf{g}:z\to y
such that
\textsf{fg}:z\to x\in T
. Now
y
is minimal and clearly
[z]\leq[y]
, it follows that
z\cong y
. Thus there exists a morphism
\textsf{h}:y\to z
. It follows immediately that
\textsf{fgh}:y\to x\in T
as
T
is a sieve. Evidently,
\textsf{gh}\in{\rm Aut}_{{\mathcal{C}}}(y)
, therefore
\textsf{f}=\textsf{fgh}(\textsf{gh})^{-1}\in T
. This proves that
S_{x}\subseteq T
. The non-emptiness of sieves follows from the definition of the dense topology.
∎

Equivalently, the unique minimal covering sieve of
{\mathcal{J}}_{\rm den}(x)
can be characterized as a functor:

\displaystyle S_{x}^{\rm min}(y)=\begin{cases}\text{Hom}_{{\mathcal{C}}}(y,x),&\text{if}~y\in\mathop{\rm Ob}\nolimits({\mathcal{C}}_{\leq x})_{\min};\\
\emptyset,&{\rm otherwise}.\end{cases}

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
\mathbf{C}=({\mathcal{C}},\mathcal{J}_{\rm den})
be the dense site on a finite
EI
-category. Then, for each presheaf
\mathfrak{F}:{\mathcal{C}}^{op}\rightarrow{\rm Set}
and each
x\in{\rm Ob}{\mathcal{C}}
, the half-sheafification of
\mathfrak{F}
can be obtained as follows (with obvious maps)

\mathfrak{F}^{\dagger}(x)\cong\underset{[y]\subset\mathop{\rm Ob}\nolimits({\mathcal{C}}_{\leq x})_{\min}}{\prod}\underset{i}{\biguplus}~~\mathfrak{F}(y)^{H_{i}}.

where
H_{i}
’s are the stabilizers of the transitive
\mathop{\rm Aut}\nolimits_{{\mathcal{C}}}(y)
-sets in the morphism set

{\rm Hom}_{{\mathcal{C}}}(y,x)=\underset{i}{\biguplus}~~H_{i}\backslash\text{\rm Aut}_{{\mathcal{C}}}(y).

Moreover,
\mathfrak{F}^{a}\cong\mathfrak{F}^{\dagger}
. Consequently,
\mathfrak{F}^{\dagger}
is a sheaf.

Let
\iota:{\mathcal{C}}_{\min}\hookrightarrow{\mathcal{C}}
be the inclusion. Any presheaf
{\mathfrak{G}}
on
{\mathcal{C}}_{\min}
can be naturally extended to a presheaf
{\mathfrak{G}}^{\prime}
on
{\mathcal{C}}
satisfying
({\mathfrak{G}}^{\prime})^{a}\cong RK_{\iota}{\mathfrak{G}}
.

Proof.

We proceed to prove this proposition by direct computation. Given a presheaf
\mathfrak{F}
, let us first compute its half-sheafification. On an object
x\in{\rm Ob}{\mathcal{C}}
,

\displaystyle\mathfrak{F}^{\dagger}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathop{\varinjlim}\nolimits_{S\in\mathcal{J}(x)}\text{Nat}(S,\mathfrak{F})

\displaystyle\cong

\displaystyle\text{Nat}(S_{x}^{\rm min},\mathfrak{F})

\displaystyle\cong

\displaystyle\underset{[y]\subset\mathop{\rm Ob}\nolimits({\mathcal{C}}_{\leq x})_{\min}}{\prod}\text{Hom}(S_{x}^{\rm min}(y),\mathfrak{F}(y))

\displaystyle\cong

\displaystyle\underset{[y]\subset\mathop{\rm Ob}\nolimits({\mathcal{C}}_{\leq x})_{\min}}{\prod}\text{Hom}_{\text{Aut}_{{\mathcal{C}}}(y)}(\text{Hom}_{{\mathcal{C}}}(y,x),\mathfrak{F}(y))

\displaystyle\cong

\displaystyle\underset{[y]\subset\mathop{\rm Ob}\nolimits({\mathcal{C}}_{\leq x})_{\min}}{\prod}\text{Hom}_{\text{Aut}_{{\mathcal{C}}}(y)}\left(\underset{i}{\biguplus}~~H_{i}\backslash\text{Aut}_{{\mathcal{C}}}(y),\mathfrak{F}(y)\right)

\displaystyle\cong

\displaystyle\underset{[y]\subset\mathop{\rm Ob}\nolimits({\mathcal{C}}_{\leq x})_{\min}}{\prod}\underset{i}{\biguplus}~~\mathfrak{F}(y)^{H_{i}}.

Here
[y]
is the isomorphism class of the object
y
in
{\mathcal{C}}
. The third isomorphism above follows from the characterization of the minimal sieve. Particularly if
y\in\mathop{\rm Ob}\nolimits{\mathcal{C}}_{\min}
, then
S_{y}^{\rm min}(y)=\text{Aut}_{{\mathcal{C}}}(y)
. Thus

\mathfrak{F}^{\dagger}(y)\cong\text{Hom}_{\text{Aut}_{{\mathcal{C}}}(y)}(\text{Aut}_{{\mathcal{C}}}(y),\mathfrak{F}(y))\cong\mathfrak{F}(y).

For a morphism
\textsf{f}:w\to x
, one can see that
{\mathfrak{F}}^{\dagger}(\textsf{f}):{\mathfrak{F}}^{\dagger}(x)\to{\mathfrak{F}}^{\dagger}(w)
is given by the restriction on fixed points, because
f
induces an
\mathop{\rm Aut}\nolimits_{{\mathcal{C}}}(y)
-map

\mathop{\rm Hom}\nolimits_{{\mathcal{C}}}(y,w)\to\mathop{\rm Hom}\nolimits_{{\mathcal{C}}}(y,x)

and subsequently an injective group homomorphism between their stabilizers in
\mathop{\rm Aut}\nolimits_{{\mathcal{C}}}(y)
.

Repeating the above steps, we get the sheafification of
\mathfrak{F}
:

\displaystyle\mathfrak{F}^{a}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\mathfrak{F}^{\dagger})^{\dagger}(x)

\displaystyle\cong

\displaystyle\text{Nat}(S_{x}^{\rm min},\mathfrak{F}^{\dagger})

\displaystyle\cong

\displaystyle\underset{[y]\subset\mathop{\rm Ob}\nolimits({\mathcal{C}}_{\leq x})_{\min}}{\prod}\text{Hom}_{\text{Aut}_{{\mathcal{C}}}(y)}(S_{x}^{\rm min}(y),\mathfrak{F}^{\dagger}(y))

\displaystyle\cong

\displaystyle\underset{[y]\subset\mathop{\rm Ob}\nolimits({\mathcal{C}}_{\leq x})_{\min}}{\prod}\text{Hom}_{\text{Aut}_{{\mathcal{C}}}(y)}(S_{x}^{\rm min}(y),\mathfrak{F}(y))

\displaystyle\cong

\displaystyle\mathfrak{F}^{\dagger}(x).

One readily checks that
\mathfrak{F}^{\dagger}
and
\mathfrak{F}^{a}
coincide on morphisms as well. Therefore
\mathfrak{F}^{a}\cong\mathfrak{F}^{\dagger}
, it follows that
\mathfrak{F}^{\dagger}
is a sheaf.

In the end, given a presheaf
{\mathfrak{G}}
on
{\mathcal{C}}_{\min}
, one can define a presheaf
{\mathfrak{G}}^{\prime}
on
{\mathcal{C}}
by asking
{\mathfrak{G}}^{\prime}(y)={\mathfrak{G}}(y)
if
y
is minimal, and
{\mathfrak{G}}^{\prime}(y)=\emptyset
otherwise. Now by our previous discussions, both
({\mathfrak{G}}^{\prime})^{a}
and
RK_{\iota}{\mathfrak{G}}
are sheaves on
\C_{\rm den}=({\mathcal{C}},{\mathcal{J}}_{\rm den})
. By Theorem 4.5, we must have
({\mathfrak{G}}^{\prime})^{a}\cong RK_{\iota}{\mathfrak{G}}
because they agree on
{\mathcal{C}}_{\min}
.
∎

Remark 5.3
.

Let
\mathbf{C}=({\mathcal{C}},\mathcal{J}_{\rm den})
be the dense site on a finite
EI
-category.

(1)

We emphasize that the last statement of Proposition 5.2 works for an arbitrary co-ideal
{\mathcal{D}}
of
{\mathcal{C}}
. For such a strictly full subcategory, any presheaf on
{\mathcal{D}}
can be extended to a presheaf on
{\mathcal{C}}
, as in the above proof. Therefore, the right Kan extension may be computed by the sheafification, which provide another way to compare two sheaf categories.

(2)

From Proposition 5.2, one can quickly deduce the following equivalence

\mathop{\rm Sh}\nolimits(\C_{\rm den})\simeq\underset{[y]\subset\mathop{\rm Ob}\nolimits{\mathcal{C}}_{\min}}{\prod}\rSet{\rm Aut}_{{\mathcal{C}}}(y),

which matches with Proposition 4.4(2). Let
\mathfrak{R}
be a sheaf of
k
-algebras on
\C_{\rm den}
. Passing to the module categories, we obtain an equivalence
\Mod\mathfrak{R}\simeq\prod_{[y]\subset\mathop{\rm Ob}\nolimits{\mathcal{C}}_{\min}}\rMod\mathfrak{R}(y)[\mathop{\rm Aut}\nolimits_{{\mathcal{C}}}(y)]\simeq\rMod\prod_{[y]\subset\mathop{\rm Ob}\nolimits{\mathcal{C}}_{\min}}\mathfrak{R}(y)[\mathop{\rm Aut}\nolimits_{{\mathcal{C}}}(y)]
.

The preceding result provides some kind of “block decomposition” of
\Mod\mathfrak{R}
.

Example 5.4
.

Let
G
be a finite group and
p
be a prime number such that
p\bigm||G|
. Consider
\mathbf{O}_{p}^{\circ}(G)=({\mathcal{O}}_{p}^{\circ}(G),\mathcal{J}_{\rm den})
(see Section 2.4). Let
\underline{k}
be the constant sheaf given by
k
. Then

\mathop{\rm Sh}\nolimits(\mathbf{O}_{p}^{\circ}(G),k)=\Mod\underline{k}\simeq\prod_{P}\rMod k(N_{G}(P)/P),

where
P
runs over the set of conjugacy classes of order
p
-subgroups.

5.2.
Vertices and sources

In
[
16
]
, given a finite EI-category
{\mathcal{C}}
, one can introduce a vertex (which is a unique full subcategory of
{\mathcal{C}}
) and a source for each finite-dimensional indecomposable
k{\mathcal{C}}
-module (covariant functor from
{\mathcal{C}}
to
\rMod k
)
{\mathfrak{M}}
. Let
{\mathfrak{N}}
be a presheaf on
{\mathcal{C}}
. In light of sheaf theory, there is a (unique) finest topology
{\mathcal{J}}_{{\mathfrak{N}}}
, for which
{\mathfrak{N}}:{\mathcal{C}}^{op}\to\rMod k
becomes a sheaf on
({\mathcal{C}},{\mathcal{J}}_{{\mathfrak{N}}})
. Now we know that
{\mathcal{J}}_{{\mathfrak{N}}}={\mathcal{J}}^{{\mathcal{D}}}
for a unique strictly full subcategory
{\mathcal{D}}
. It is interesting to compare
{\mathcal{D}}
with the vertex of
{\mathfrak{N}}
(when it is indecomposable), because they seem to be closely related. Plus, the construction of
{\mathcal{D}}
for an indecomposable module works for any finite category, not just the finite EI-categories. Moreover it is defined for arbitrary modules, even the infinite-dimensional ones.

5.3.
Sipp-topology on
G
-Set

There is a sipp-topology introduced on
G
-Set by Balmer
[
4
]
. It may be restricted to the finite EI-category
{\mathcal{O}}(G)
, the orbit category of
G
. One can verify that the sipp-topology is a subcategory topology on
{\mathcal{O}}(G)
, given by the strictly full subcategory
{\mathcal{O}}_{p}(G)
, the
